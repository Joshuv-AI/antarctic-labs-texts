Loading a relativistic kappa distribution in particle simulations
Seiji Zenitani, Shin’ya Nakano

Abstract
A procedure for loading particle velocities from a relativistic kappa distribution in particle-in-cell (PIC) and Monte Carlo simulations is presented. It is based on the rejection method and the beta prime distribution. The rejection part extends earlier method for the Maxwell–Jüttner distribution, and then the acceptance rate reaches ≳ 95 % \gtrsim 95\% . Utilizing the generalized beta prime distributions, we successfully reproduce the relativistic kappa distribution, including the power-law tail. The derivation of the procedure, mathematical preparations, comparison with other procedures, and numerical tests are presented.



I
Introduction

The kappa distribution is one of the most fundamental velocity distributions
in kinetic studies in space and solar-wind plasmas.
(
Livadiotis 2017
)

Since it was introduced in 1960s,
(
Vasyliunas 1968
;
Olbert 1968
)

the kappa distribution has been widely used to study
plasmas with suprathermal populations,
because it seamlessly contains both the thermal Maxwellian component and
the nonthermal power-law component in the high-energy part.
It is also noteworthy that
the kappa distribution is connected with Tsallis statistical mechanics,
as it maximizes a non-extensive entropy.
(
Livadiotis & McComas 2009
;
Tsallis 1988
;
Livadiotis 2017
)

For the theory and applications of the kappa distribution,
the readers may refer to a recent monograph
(
Livadiotis 2017
)
and references therein.
Kinetic plasma processes in a kappa-distributed space plasma
have been investigated
by using particle-in-cell (PIC) simulations.
(
Lu et al. 2010
;
Koen et al. 2012
;
Park et al. 2013
;
Abdul & Mace 2015
;
Adbul et al. 2021
)

To simulate a kappa-distributed plasma,
one often has to initialize particle velocities according to the kappa distribution,
however, numerical procedures to load kappa distributions may not be well-documented.
Among them,
Abdul & Mace 2015
recognized that the kappa distribution is
equivalent to the multivariate
t
-distribution,
which can be generated from a normal distribution and
a chi-squared distribution.
(
Kroese et al. 2011
)

To deal with energetic electrons of
\gtrsim 0.5
MeV and energetic ions of
\gtrsim 1
GeV,
special relativity needs to be considered.
Velocity distributions need to be modified accordingly.
A relativistic Maxwell distribution,
often referred to as a Maxwell–Jüttner distribution,
(
Jüttner 1911
)

has been used in Monte Carlo simulations
as well as in PIC simulations in high-energy astrophysics.
It is not straightforward to generate a Maxwell–Jüttner distribution,
because the relativistic Lorentz factor
\gamma=[1-(v/c)^{2}]^{1/2}

makes the problem difficult.
To load Maxwell–Jüttner distributions, several rejection-based algorithms have been proposed.
(
Sobol 1976
;
Pozdnyakov et al. 1983
;
Canfield et al. 1987
;
Schnittman & Krolik 2013
;
Swisdak 2013
;
Zenitani 2015
)

For example,
Sobol 1976
has proposed a simple rejection method,
based on the gamma distribution.

Canfield et al. 1987
have utilized four gamma distributions.
Their method achieves good acceptance rate of
\gtrsim 70\%
for nearly arbitrary initial conditions.
Another option is the inverse transform method,
which refers to a numerical table of the cumulative distribution function.
In such a case, one needs to carefully adjust the size of the table,
because the code often becomes inefficient.

A relativistic kappa distribution will be useful
in modeling energetic processes in laser, space, and astrophysical plasmas.
Its mathematical form including the normalization constant was
provided by
Xiao 2006
and by
Han-Thanh et al. 2022
.
Tsallis-type statistics in relativistic collisionless plasmas
has gained attention very recently.
(
Zhdankin 2022
)

Meanwhile, to the best of our knowledge, no one has presented
a numerical procedure to load a relativistic kappa distribution
in particle simulations.
One can similarly consider the inverse transform method,
but a much larger table will be required,
because the kappa distribution has a power-law tail that extends nearly infinitely.
We desire a reliable algorithm that is free from tables.

In this article, we propose a numerical algorithm to load
a relativistic kappa distribution in PIC and Monte Carlo simulations.
The rest of this manuscript is organized as follows.
As starting points,
Sections
II
and
III
discuss
algorithms to generate Maxwell and kappa distributions.
Section
IV
presents our extention of
Canfield et al. 1987
’s algorithm
to generate a Maxwell–Jüttner distribution.
Section
V
introduces
a new procedure to generate a relativistic kappa distribution,
based on beta prime distributions.
Section
VI
presents numerical tests of the proposed methods for relativistic distributions.
The efficiency of the rejection method are evaluated.
Section
VII
contains discussions and summary.

II
Maxwell distribution

A Maxwell distribution is a multivariate normal distribution.

\displaystyle f_{\rm M}(\boldsymbol{v})d^{3}{v}

\displaystyle=N_{M}\Big(\frac{1}{\pi v_{M}^{2}}\Big)^{\frac{3}{2}}\exp\Big(-\frac{\boldsymbol{v}^{2}}{v_{M}^{2}}\Big)d^{3}{v}

(1)

Here,
N_{M}
is the number density for a Maxwellian plasma,
v_{M}
is the most probable velocity, and the other symbols have their standard meanings. In this case, plasma temperature is given by
T_{M}=(1/2)mv_{M}^{2}
. Throughout the paper, we focus on isotropic distributions.

The three components of the Maxwellian can be obtained by

\displaystyle{v}_{x}=\sigma n_{1},~~{v}_{y}=\sigma n_{2},~~{v}_{z}=\sigma n_{3},

(2)

where
\sigma^{2}=(1/2)v^{2}_{\rm M}
is the variance and

n_{1},n_{2},
and
n_{3}
are the normal random variates.
The normal variates can be generated by the Box–Muller method
(
Box & Muller 1958
)
or other methods.
(
Devroye 1986
;
Kroese et al. 2011
;
Yotsuji 2010
)

We examine the Maxwellian from another angle for discussions in later sections.
We move to spherical coordinates
via
d^{3}v=4\pi v^{2}dv
.
We use a normalized parameter

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{v^{2}}{v^{2}_{M}},

(3)

and then we obtain

\displaystyle f_{\rm M}(x)dx=\frac{2N_{M}}{\sqrt{\pi}}x^{1/2}e^{-x}dx=N_{M}{\rm Ga}\left(x;\frac{3}{2},1\right)dx

(4)

where
\Gamma(x)
is the gamma function and

\displaystyle{\rm Ga}(x;k,\lambda)=\frac{x^{k-1}e^{-x/\lambda}}{\Gamma(k)\lambda^{k}}

(5)

is the gamma distribution with a shape parameter
k
and a scale parameter
\lambda
.
There are several procedures to generate the gamma distributions.
(
Devroye 1986
;
Marsaglia & Tsang 2000
;
Kroese et al. 2011
;
Yotsuji 2010
)

We present some of them in Appendix
A
.
From a gamma-distributed random variate
X_{{\rm Ga}(\alpha,\beta)}
,
we recover the velocity

\displaystyle v=v_{M}\sqrt{X_{{\rm Ga}(3/2,1)}}=\sigma\sqrt{X_{{\rm Ga}(3/2,2)}}.

(6)

We can also rewrite this with
T_{M}
,

\displaystyle v=\sqrt{\frac{2T_{M}}{m}X_{{\rm Ga}(3/2,1)}}=\sqrt{X_{{\rm Ga}(3/2,2T_{M}/m)}}.

(7)

Finally, we obtain the
\boldsymbol{v}
vector,
by randomly scattering
v
onto the spherical surface,
using two uniform random variates
X_{1},X_{2}\sim U(0,1)
.

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}v_{x}&=&v~(2X_{1}-1)\\
v_{y}&=&2v\sqrt{X_{1}(1-X_{1})}\cos(2\pi X_{2})\\
v_{z}&=&2v\sqrt{X_{1}(1-X_{1})}\sin(2\pi X_{2})\end{array}\right.

III
Kappa distribution

A kappa distribution is defined by

\displaystyle f_{\rm\kappa}(\boldsymbol{v})d^{3}v=\frac{N_{\kappa}}{(\pi\kappa\theta^{2})^{3/2}}\frac{\Gamma(\kappa+1)}{\Gamma(\kappa-1/2)}\Big(1+\frac{\boldsymbol{v}^{2}}{\kappa\theta^{2}}\Big)^{-(\kappa+1)}d^{3}v

(12)

where
\theta
is the most probable speed and
\kappa
is the kappa parameter.
The
\kappa
parameter controls a power-law index in the high-energy part.
It ranges
\kappa>3/2
, so that the effective plasma temperature
T=[\kappa/(2\kappa-3)]m\theta^{2}
remains finite.
Other properties of the kappa distribution are discussed in
Livadiotis 2017
and references therein.
Assuming isotropy, we obtain

\displaystyle f_{\rm\kappa}(v)dv

\displaystyle=N_{\kappa}\frac{4}{\pi^{1/2}(\kappa\theta^{2})^{3/2}}\frac{\Gamma(\kappa+1)}{\Gamma(\kappa-1/2)}\Big(1+\frac{{v}^{2}}{\kappa\theta^{2}}\Big)^{-(\kappa+1)}v^{2}dv

(13)

\displaystyle=N_{\kappa}{\rm B^{\prime}}\left(v;\frac{3}{2},\frac{\nu}{2},2,({\kappa}\theta^{2})^{1/2}\right)dv.

(14)

Here,
\nu=2\kappa-1
and

{\rm B^{\prime}}
is the generalized beta prime distribution
with a shape parameter
p
and a scale parameter
q
,

\displaystyle{\rm B^{\prime}}(x;\alpha,\beta,p,q)

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{qB(\alpha,\beta)}\left(\frac{x}{q}\right)^{\alpha p-1}\left(1+\left(\frac{x}{q}\right)^{p}\right)^{-(\alpha+\beta)}

\displaystyle=\frac{p\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{q^{\alpha p}\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}\left(1+\left(\frac{x}{q}\right)^{p}\right)^{-(\alpha+\beta)}x^{\alpha p-1}.

(15)

B(\alpha,\beta)
is the beta function.
If we redefine
x^{\prime}\equiv(x/q)^{p}
, with help from
(dx^{\prime}/x^{\prime})=p(dx/x)
,
then
x^{\prime}
follows the (standard) beta prime distribution,

\displaystyle{\rm B^{\prime}}(x^{\prime};\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle={\rm B^{\prime}}(x^{\prime};\alpha,\beta,1,1)=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}\left(1+{x^{\prime}}\right)^{-(\alpha+\beta)}(x^{\prime})^{\alpha-1}.

(16)

A random variate according to the beta prime distribution,

X_{{\rm B^{\prime}}(\alpha,\beta)}
, is generated by

\displaystyle X_{{\rm B^{\prime}}(\alpha,\beta)}=\frac{X_{{\rm Ga}(\alpha,\delta)}}{X_{{\rm Ga}(\beta,\delta)}}

(17)

where
X_{{\rm Ga}(\alpha,\delta)}
is a random variate
that follows the gamma distribution
{\rm Ga}(\alpha,\delta)
.
(
Forbes 2010
)

Here Eq. (
17
) is independent of choice of
\delta
.
Since the relation (Eq. (
17
)) is not well known,
we provide a brief proof in Appendix
B
.
Then, considering the scaling factors, we obtain

\displaystyle X_{{\rm B^{\prime}}(\alpha,\beta,p,q)}=q\left(X_{{\rm B^{\prime}}(\alpha,\beta)}\right)^{1/p}=q\left(\frac{X_{{\rm Ga}(\alpha,\delta)}}{X_{{\rm Ga}(\beta,\delta)}}\right)^{1/p}

(18)

Setting
\delta=2
,
the kappa-distributed velocity
v
is given by

\displaystyle v=\left(\kappa\theta^{2}\frac{X_{{\rm Ga}(3/2,2)}}{X_{{\rm Ga}(\nu/2,2)}}\right)^{1/2}

(19)

Then we generate two gamma distributions.
We note that
the shape parameter
\nu/2
in the denominator
can be an integer, half-integer, or floating-point number,
as long as it satisfies
\nu/2=\kappa-1/2>1
.
Gamma generators for arbitrary
k>1
can be found
in Appendix
A
and references therein
(
Devroye 1986
;
Marsaglia & Tsang 2000
;
Kroese et al. 2011
;
Yotsuji 2010
)
.
After this, we spherically scatter
\boldsymbol{v}
to the
v_{x},v_{y}
, and
v_{z}
components
by Eq. (
II
).
The entire steps to generate the kappa distribution are presented in Algorithm 1-1 in Table
1
.

In Section
II
, we have already seen that
the gamma distribution with
k=3/2
and the spherical scattering provide the Maxwell distribution (Eqs. (
2
), (
6
), and (
II
)).
From Eqs. (
19
) and (
II
),
we similarly obtain the three components of the kappa distribution:

\displaystyle{v}_{x}=\sqrt{\kappa\theta^{2}}~\frac{n_{1}}{\sqrt{\chi^{2}_{\nu}}},~~{v}_{y}=\sqrt{\kappa\theta^{2}}~\frac{n_{2}}{\sqrt{\chi^{2}_{\nu}}},~~{v}_{z}=\sqrt{\kappa\theta^{2}}~\frac{n_{3}}{\sqrt{\chi^{2}_{\nu}}},

(20)

where
n_{1},n_{2},
and
n_{3}
are the normal random variates and

{\chi^{2}_{\nu}}=X_{{\rm Ga}(\nu/2,2)}
is a random variate
according to the chi-squared distribution with
\nu
degrees of freedom.
The procedure is presented in Algorithm 1-2 in Table
1
.
Mathematically, the kappa distribution is identical to a multivariate
t
-distribution in three dimensions.
(
Abdul & Mace 2015
)

It is known that a multivariate
t
-distribution can be generated from a multivariate normal distribution divided by a chi-squared distribution with
\nu
degrees of freedom.
(
Kroese et al. 2011
)

Algorithm 1-2 is equivalent to this well-known procedure.

IV
Relativistic Maxwell distribution

We describe an algorithm to load
a relativistic Maxwell distribution, also known as a Maxwell–Jüttner distribution.
Since the procedure extends an earlier work by
Canfield et al. 1987
,
we call it the modified Canfield method.

The phase-space density of a Maxwell–Jüttner distribution is given by

f_{\rm MJ}(\boldsymbol{p})d^{3}{p}=\frac{N_{M}}{4\pi m^{2}cT_{M}K_{2}(mc^{2}/T_{M})}\exp\Big(-\frac{\gamma mc^{2}}{T_{M}}\Big)d^{3}{p}

(21)

where
\gamma=[1-(v/c)^{2}]^{-1/2}
is the Lorentz factor,
\boldsymbol{p}=m\gamma\boldsymbol{v}
is the momentum, and
K_{n}(x)
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind.
Hereafter we set
m=c=1
and write
t\equiv{T_{M}}/{mc^{2}}
for brevity.
In the spherical coordinates, we obtain

\displaystyle f_{\rm MJ}(p)dp=\frac{N_{M}}{tK_{2}(1/t)}\exp\Big(-\frac{\sqrt{1+p^{2}}}{t}\Big)p^{2}dp

(22)

Using a kinetic energy parameter
x

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{{E}}{mc^{2}}={\gamma-1},

(23)

we rewrite Eq. (
22
) into

\displaystyle f_{\rm MJ}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-1/t}}{tK_{2}(1/t)}e^{-x/t}~(1+x)\sqrt{(x+1)^{2}-1}~dx

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-1/t}}{tK_{2}(1/t)}e^{-x/t}~(\sqrt{2}x^{1/2}+ax+b\sqrt{2}x^{3/2}+x^{2})~\frac{(1+x)\sqrt{x+2}}{\sqrt{2}+ax^{1/2}+b\sqrt{2}x+x^{3/2}}~dx

(24)

where we have introduced two hyperparameters:
a
(
0<a\leq 1
) and
b
(
0<b\leq 1
).
Unless stated otherwise, we employ
a=0.56
and
b=0.35
throughout this paper.

Following
Canfield et al. 1987
,
we define weight functions
w_{i}(t)
,
their sum
S(t)
, normalized weights
\pi_{i}(t)
, and
a rejection function
R(x)
,

\displaystyle w_{3}(t)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\pi},~~w_{4}(t)=a\sqrt{2t},~~w_{5}(t)=\frac{3b\sqrt{\pi}t}{2},~~w_{6}(t)={(2t)^{3/2}},

(25)

\displaystyle S(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=3}^{6}w_{i}(t)=\sqrt{\pi}+a\sqrt{2t}+\frac{3b\sqrt{\pi}t}{2}+{(2t)^{3/2}}

(26)

\displaystyle\pi_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{w_{i}(t)}{S(t)}~~~(i=3,4,5,6),~~~\sum_{i=3}^{6}\pi_{i}(t)=1,

(27)

\displaystyle R(x;a,b)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{(1+x)\sqrt{x+2}}{\sqrt{2}+a{x}^{1/2}+b\sqrt{2}x+x^{3/2}}

(28)

This rejection function ranges
0.957\lesssim R(x)<1
.
It is asymptotic to
1

in the
x\rightarrow 0
and
x\rightarrow\infty
limits.

We also remind the readers of the gamma distribution with shape
k/2
and scale
\lambda
,

\displaystyle Ga(x;k/2,\lambda)=\frac{1}{\lambda^{k/2}\Gamma(k/2)}x^{k/2-1}e^{-x/\lambda}

(29)

In particular, we take advantage of the following four distributions,

\displaystyle Ga\Big(x;\frac{3}{2},t\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}t^{3/2}}x^{1/2}e^{-x/t},~~~Ga(x;2,t)=\frac{1}{t^{2}}xe^{-x/t},

\displaystyle Ga\Big(x;\frac{5}{2},t\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{3\sqrt{\pi}t^{5/2}}x^{3/2}e^{-x/t},~~Ga(x;3,t)=\frac{1}{2t^{3}}x^{2}e^{-x/t},

(30)

Using Eqs. (
25
)–(
28
) and (
IV
),
Eq. (
24
) yields

\displaystyle f_{\rm MJ}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{t}e^{-1/t}S(t)}{\sqrt{2}K_{2}(1/t)}\left(\sum_{i=3}^{6}\pi_{i}(t)~Ga\Big(x;\frac{i}{2},t\Big)\right)R(x)~dx

(31)

We also define an auxiliary distribution
F(x)
without
R(x)

\displaystyle F_{\rm MJ}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{t}e^{-1/t}S(t)}{\sqrt{2}K_{2}(1/t)}\left(\sum_{i=3}^{6}\pi_{i}(t)~Ga\Big(x;\frac{i}{2},t\Big)\right)~dx

(32)

Since
\sum_{i=3}^{6}\pi_{i}(t)=1
,
the bracketed term in Eq. (
32
) stands for a superposition of four gamma distributions at the probability of
\pi_{i}(t)
.
Using a uniform random variate
X_{1}\sim U(0,1)
and the probability
\pi_{i}(t)
,
one can choose one gamma distribution from the four.
A random variate for the gamma distribution
{\rm Ga}(x;i/2,t)

can be easily generated, for example, by the procedure in Appendix
A
.
Superimposing four distributions,
we obtain the auxiliary distribution
F_{\rm MJ}(x)
.
Then we apply a rejection method.
Using another uniform random
X_{2}\sim U(0,1)
,
we accept
x
when the following condition is met:

\displaystyle X_{2}<R(x)

(33)

If this condition is not met, we reject the particle, and
then we regenerate the random variate.
We further use the squeeze technique to evaluate Eq. (
33
).
As will be shown, the rejection function is greater than 0.95.
Considering this, we rewrite the condition,

\displaystyle X_{2}<0.95~~{\bf or}~~X_{2}<R(x)

(34)

Then, the code immediately returns true without calling the rejection function in 95% of the cases,
and it calls the rejection function only in the remaining 5% cases.
This usually makes the code faster.

After the rejection, we obtain
x
according to the distribution of Eq. (
31
).
We translate
x
into
p

\displaystyle p=\sqrt{x(x+2)}

(35)

and then we further convert it to
\boldsymbol{p}
via Eq. (
II
).
The obtained
\boldsymbol{p}
follows the Maxwell–Jüttner distribution in 3-D momentum space (Eq. (
21
)).
Algorithm 2 in Table
2
displays
the entire procedure in the pseudo code.
The normalization constant in Eq. (
21
) does not appear in the procedure.

Behavior of this algorithm depends on
the plasma temperature,
because the probability
\pi_{i}(t)
(Eq. (
27
)) is a function of
t=T_{M}/mc^{2}
.
Fig.
3
shows
the
t
-dependence of the probability densities.
The thick solid lines indicate
\pi_{i}
in color.
The four components gradually change their percentages.
In the nonrelativistic temperature limit of
t\ll 1
,
the
\pi_{3}
component is dominant.
In such a case, from Eqs. (
IV
) and (
35
),
we estimate
v\simeq(2x)^{1/2}\approx\sqrt{2X_{{\rm Ga}(3/2,t)}}=\sqrt{X_{{\rm Ga}(3/2,2t)}}
.
This recovers the nonrelativistic Maxwell distribution,
as discussed in Eq. (
7
) in Section

.
As the temperature increases,
the
\pi_{3}
component decreases.
Instead, the
\pi_{4}
and
\pi_{5}
components appear successively.
The three components are replaced by the
\pi_{6}
component, which is dominant in the
t\gg 1
limit.
In this limit, the distribution (Eq. (
22
)) approaches
f_{\rm MJ}(p)dp\approx\exp(-p/t)p^{2}dp
, and
p\approx x
(Eq. (
35
)). This is consistent with the
Ga(x;3,t)
term in Eq. (
IV
).
Compared with the
\pi_{3}
and
\pi_{6}
components,
the
\pi_{4}
and
\pi_{5}
components are localized in the medium temperature range.

The original method by
Canfield et al. 1987
corresponds to the
a=b=1
case without the squeeze method.
Quantities in Eqs. (
25
)–(
28
) need to be recalculated accordingly.
The probabilities
\pi_{i}
for
a=b=1
are shown by the thin dashed lines in Fig.

V
Relativistic kappa distribution

A relativistic kappa distribution is defined by
(
Han-Thanh et al. 2022
)

\displaystyle f_{\rm RK}(\boldsymbol{p})d^{3}p

\displaystyle\equiv A\left(1+\frac{(\gamma-1)mc^{2}}{\kappa T_{\kappa}}\right)^{-(\kappa+1)}d^{3}p

(37)

\displaystyle A(\kappa,T_{\kappa})

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{\kappa}\Gamma\left(\kappa+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{{(2\pi m\kappa T_{\kappa})^{3/2}}(\kappa+1)~\Gamma(\kappa-2)~{}_{2}F_{1}\Big(-\frac{3}{2},\frac{5}{2};\kappa+\frac{1}{2};1-\frac{\kappa T_{\kappa}}{2mc^{2}}\Big)}

(38)

where
A
is the normalization constant,

N_{\kappa}
is the plasma density,

T_{\kappa}
is a characteristic temperature,
and
{}_{2}F_{1}
is the hypergeometric function.
The
\kappa
parameter should satisfy
\kappa>3
,
so that the energy density remains finite.
We set
m=c=1
and write
t\equiv T_{\kappa}/mc^{2}
for brevity.
The black solid line in Fig.
5
shows
a 1-D cut of a relativistic kappa distribution with
\kappa=3.5
and
t=1
.
The blue dashed line indicates a Maxwell–Jüttner distribution
with
T_{M^{\prime}}=T_{\kappa}
and
N_{M^{\prime}}=0.40N_{\kappa}
.
This distribution is inscribed to the relativistic kappa distribution
at the energy of
{E}=(\gamma-1)mc^{2}=T_{\kappa}

(at
p_{x}=\pm\sqrt{3}
; the gray vertical lines),
as described in Appendix
D
.
Compared with the Maxwell–Jüttner distribution,
the relativistic kappa distribution has broader high-energy tails.

Moving to the spherical coordinates, we obtain

\displaystyle f_{\rm RK}({p})d{p}

\displaystyle=A(\kappa,t)\left(1+\frac{\gamma-1}{\kappa t}\right)^{-(\kappa+1)}4\pi p^{2}~dp

(39)

We use the normalized kinetic energy
x
,

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{E}{mc^{2}}={\gamma-1}

(40)

and then Eq. (
39
) yields

\displaystyle f_{\rm RK}(x)dx

\displaystyle=4\pi A(\kappa,t)\left(1+\frac{x}{\kappa t}\right)^{-(\kappa+1)}~\Big(1+{x}\Big)\sqrt{\Big(1+{x}\Big)^{2}-1}~dx

(41)

\displaystyle=4\pi A(\kappa,t)\left(1+\frac{x}{\kappa t}\right)^{-(\kappa+1)}\Big(\sqrt{2}{x}^{1/2}+a{x}+b\sqrt{2}{x}^{3/2}+{x}^{2}\Big)\frac{(1+x)\sqrt{x+2}}{\sqrt{2}+ax^{1/2}+b\sqrt{2}x+x^{3/2}}~dx

(42)

We set the hyperparameters to
a=0.56
and
b=0.35
.

We define weight functions
w_{i}(\kappa,t)
,
their sum
S(\kappa,t)
, normalized weights
\pi_{i}(\kappa,t)
, and
a rejection function
R(x)
,

\displaystyle w_{3}(\kappa,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{2}{\Gamma\Big(\kappa-\frac{1}{2}\Big)},~~~~~~~~~~~~~w_{4}(\kappa,t)=a\sqrt{\kappa t}~\Gamma(\kappa-1),

\displaystyle w_{5}(\kappa,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{3b\sqrt{2\pi}}{4}{(\kappa t)~\Gamma\Big(\kappa-\frac{3}{2}\Big)},~~~~w_{6}(\kappa,t)=2{(\kappa t)^{3/2}~\Gamma(\kappa-2)},

(43)

\displaystyle S(\kappa,t)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{i=3}^{6}w_{i}(\kappa,t),

(44)

\displaystyle\pi_{i}(\kappa,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{w_{i}(\kappa,t)}{S(\kappa,t)}~~~(i=3,4,5,6),~~~\sum_{i=3}^{6}\pi_{i}(\kappa,t)=1,

(45)

\displaystyle R(x;a,b)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{(1+x)\sqrt{x+2}}{\sqrt{2}+a{x}^{1/2}+b\sqrt{2}x+x^{3/2}}.

(46)

In this section, we use the generalized beta prime distributions,

\displaystyle{\rm B^{\prime}}\Big(x;\frac{3}{2},\kappa-\frac{1}{2},1,\kappa t\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\kappa+1)}{(\kappa t)^{3/2}~\Gamma(3/2)\Gamma(\kappa-1/2)}\left(1+\frac{x}{\kappa t}\right)^{-(\kappa+1)}x^{1/2},

(47)

\displaystyle{\rm B^{\prime}}\Big(x;2,\kappa-1,1,\kappa t\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\kappa+1)}{(\kappa t)^{2}~\Gamma(2)\Gamma(\kappa-1)}\left(1+\frac{x}{\kappa t}\right)^{-(\kappa+1)}x,

(48)

\displaystyle{\rm B^{\prime}}\Big(x;\frac{5}{2},\kappa-\frac{3}{2},1,\kappa t\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\kappa+1)}{(\kappa t)^{5/2}~\Gamma(5/2)\Gamma(\kappa-3/2)}\left(1+\frac{x}{\kappa t}\right)^{-(\kappa+1)}x^{3/2},

(49)

\displaystyle{\rm B^{\prime}}\Big(x;3,\kappa-2,1,\kappa t\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\kappa+1)}{(\kappa t)^{3}~\Gamma(3)\Gamma(\kappa-2)}\left(1+\frac{x}{\kappa t}\right)^{-(\kappa+1)}x^{2}.

(50)

Using Eqs. (
43
)–(
50
),
Eq. (
42
) yields,

\displaystyle f_{\rm RK}(x)dx=\frac{4\pi A(\kappa,t)S(\kappa,t)}{\Gamma(\kappa+1)}(\kappa t)^{3/2}\left(\sum_{i=3}^{6}\pi_{i}(\kappa,t)~{\rm B^{\prime}}\Big(x;\frac{i}{2},\kappa+1-\frac{i}{2},1,\kappa t\Big)\right)R(x)~dx

(51)

We also define an auxiliary distribution
F(x)
without
R(x)
,

\displaystyle F_{\rm RK}(x)dx=\frac{4\pi A(\kappa,t)S(\kappa,t)}{\Gamma(\kappa+1)}(\kappa t)^{3/2}\left(\sum_{i=3}^{6}\pi_{i}(\kappa,t)~{\rm B^{\prime}}\Big(x;\frac{i}{2},\kappa+1-\frac{i}{2},1,\kappa t\Big)\right)~dx.

(52)

From Eq. (
52
), it is fairly easy to generate the auxiliary distribution
F_{\rm RK}(x)
.
Using a uniform random variate
X_{1}\sim U(0,1)
and the probability
\pi_{i}(\kappa,t)
,
we choose one of the four beta prime distributions.
Then we obtain
x
from a random variate according to
the generalized beta prime distribution,
X_{{\rm B^{\prime}}(\cdots)}
.
Using two gamma-distributed random variates and an appropriate scaling (Appendix
B
),
we obtain

\displaystyle x=X_{{\rm B^{\prime}}(\frac{i}{2},\kappa+1-i/2,1,\kappa t)}=\kappa t\times\frac{X_{{\rm Ga}(i/2,1)}}{X_{{\rm Ga}(\kappa+1-i/2,1)}}.

(53)

Next, we apply the rejection method to
x
.
Using another uniform random
X_{2}\sim U(0,1)

if
X_{2}<R(x)

if not, we repeat the procedure from the beginning.
We similarly use the squeeze technique (Eq. (
34

as discussed in Section
IV
.
After this, the accepted variates
x
are distributed by Eq. (
51

We convert

\displaystyle p=\sqrt{x(x+2)}

(54)

and then we further use the spherical scattering (Eq. (

VII
Discussion and summary

The proposed methods will be used at startup in PIC or Monte Carlo simulations.
The algorithms do not need to be the fastest,
but it is important to know their computational cost.
As an indicator of the cost,
we estimate the number of random variates per particle in the procedures.
To generate a nonrelativistic Maxwellian,
we need 3 normal random variates per particle.
For the Maxwell–Jüttner distribution,
according to Tables
2
and
4
,
the modified Canfield method uses 3–5 uniform or normal variates in the rejection loop and another 2 uniform variates outside the loop.
Considering
{\rm eff}_{\rm MJ}\approx 1
,
it typically uses
\lesssim 7
variates per particle.
For the relativistic kappa distribution, the number depends on
\kappa
.
Judging from Tables
3
and
4
,
our method uses
(\kappa+4)
random variates in the loop in the worst case,
and another 2 outside the loop.
Considering
{\rm eff}_{\rm RK}\approx 1
,
the number is
\lesssim(\kappa+6)
per particle.
Though the numerical cost will increase for higher
\kappa
,
it is still predictable.
For another examples,
if we employ another gamma generator in Table
5
,
and if we assume its acceptance efficiency to be
\approx 1
,
the modified Canfield method uses
\approx 6
uniform or normal variates per particle
for the Maxwell–Jüttner distribution.
Similarly, the new kappa method uses
\approx 8
variates,
regardless of
\kappa
.
The numerical cost can be smaller.
Considering these issues,
the numerical cost is unlikely to be a big problem.

We remark that
the
\kappa
parameter should satisfy
\kappa>3
in our method.
Even though the nonrelativistic kappa distribution is defined for

\kappa>3/2
,
our method does not permit
3/2<\kappa\leq 3

in the nonrelativistic limit.
This is because our method uses
the
{\rm B^{\prime}}(3,\kappa-2,1,\kappa t)
component (Eq. (
50
)),
whose energy increases to infinity at
\kappa=3
.
To relax this constraint,
one option is to drop the
{\rm B^{\prime}}(x;3,\kappa-2,1,\kappa t)
component in the nonrelativistic regime.
In such a case, the next
{\rm B^{\prime}}(x;5/2,\kappa-3/2,1,\kappa t)
component
requires
\kappa>5/2
.
To justify this, the probability
\pi_{6}
(Eq. (
45
)) needs to be small enough, however, it is non-negligible (Fig.
6
).
For example,
\pi_{6}(3.5,10^{-2})\approx 4\times 10^{-3}
and

\pi_{6}(3.5,10^{-3})\approx 10^{-4}
.
Therefore, this option will be useful only in a limited case of
t\ll 1
.

Although it is unlikely,
when we use a very large number of particles,
the denominators in Eqs. (
53
) or Eq. (
19
) may be zero.
To avoid the division by zero, we have three options.
First, we can simply check the denominator, and then
regenerate the variate if necessary.
Second, we may employ a gamma generator that never return zero.
For example, if uniform variates are defined in the interval
(0,1)
, then the gamma generator in Table
4
does not return zero.
For another example, at least theoretically,
the gamma generator in Table
5
does not return zero.
Third, we may add a very small number
\epsilon

to the denominators in Eqs. (
53
) and (
19
).
The gamma distribution
{\rm Ga}(x;k,\lambda)
starts at the origin when
k>1
, and the mode (the most frequent value) of the distribution
x_{m}\equiv\lambda(k-1)
can be regarded as a typical value of
X_{\rm Ga(k,\lambda)}
.
In such a case, it would be plausible to set

\epsilon
to an order of a machine epsilon of
x_{m}
, i.e.,

10^{-12}\times\mathcal{O}(x_{m})
in double precision and

10^{-7}\times\mathcal{O}(x_{m})
in single precision.
When
k\rightarrow 1
or
k=1
,
population near
x=0
is non-negligible.
This occurs when
\kappa\rightarrow 3
in the relativistic kappa distribution and

\kappa\rightarrow 3/2
in the nonrelativistic kappa distribution.
Simulation settings need to be reconsidered,
because the kappa distributions cannot be defined in these limits.

When plasmas are drifting, we need to consider the Lorentz transformation.
In order to transform a velocity distribution function,
it is necessary to transform the volume element,
as well as the particle energy and momentum.
This often requires another rejection process.
In such a case, we recommend the readers to combine
the procedures in this paper and
the volume transform methods in Section III in Ref.
Zenitani 2015
.
Finally, we remark that
our methods essentially take advantage of
the following transform of an isotropic distribution,

\displaystyle p^{2}dp\rightarrow\Big(\sqrt{2}{x}^{1/2}+a{x}+b\sqrt{2}{x}^{3/2}+{x}^{2}\Big)R(x;a,b)~dx,

and we show
R(x;a,b)\gtrsim 95\%
for
(a,b)=(0.56,0.35)
in Fig.
4
.
Thus, as long as isotropic,
it might be possible to apply our efficient rejection function (
\gtrsim 95\%
)
to other relativistic velocity distributions.

In summary, we have presented a rejection-based algorithm
for generating the relativistic kappa distributions.
As preparation, we have briefly reviewed
algorithms for the nonrelativistic distributions.
Then we have presented
the modified Canfield method for the Maxwell–Jüttner distribution.
We have improved its rejection function,
by introducing the two hyperparameters.
Using this and generalized beta prime distributions,
we have constructed a rejection-based algorithm,
presented in Table
3
.
These procedures are numerically tested.
The new method excellently generates the distribution,
including its power-law tail.
For Maxwell–Jüttner distribution, we have improved the acceptance rate
from
\gtrsim 72\%
to
\gtrsim 95\%
.
The new method for the relativistic kappa distribution
takes advantage of the efficient rejection function.
Its efficiency is
\gtrsim 95\%

from the nonrelativistic to ultrarelativistic temperature regimes.
The algorithms use a predictable number of random variates.
The entire procedure is simple enough to avoid bugs.
Based on these facts, our algorithm is practically useful.
We hope that these algorithms are useful in PIC and Monte Carlo simulations in relativistic plasma physics.

For the latter purpose, we outline algorithms to load the gamma distributions.

U_{i}\sim U(0,1)

\displaystyle=-\lambda\ln\prod_{i=1}^{k}U_{i}

(59)

k

(

\displaystyle X_{{\rm Ga}(k,\lambda)}

\displaystyle=-\lambda\ln\prod_{i=1}^{[k]}U_{i}+\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)n_{1}^{2},

(60)

where
[x]
is the floor function.
The procedure is listed in Table.

.
Using Box–Muller transform, one can replace the last term
(\lambda/2)n^{2}=-\lambda(\ln u_{1})\cos^{2}2\pi u_{2}

u_{1}
and
u_{2}
.
Practically, when the uniform random variate
U_{i}
is defined in the interval
[0,1)
,
it is useful to redefine
U_{i}=1-U_{i}
to avoid
\log 0

Appendix B
Generating beta prime distributions

We consider two independent random variates
X\sim{\rm Ga}(\alpha,\delta)
and
Y\sim{\rm Ga}(\beta,\delta)
.
Using them, we consider the following variables,

\displaystyle U=\frac{X}{Y},~~~V=X+Y

(61)

X
and
Y
are expressed as follows

\displaystyle X=\frac{UV}{1+U},~~~Y=\frac{V}{1+U}

(62)

The joint probability distribution function of
X,Y
is

\displaystyle f_{X,Y}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)\delta^{\alpha}}x^{\alpha-1}e^{-x/\delta}\frac{1}{\Gamma(\beta)\delta^{\beta}}y^{\beta-1}e^{-y/\delta}

(63)

We rewrite this function in terms of
(u,v
)

\displaystyle f_{U,V}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)\delta^{\alpha}}x^{\alpha-1}e^{-x/\delta}\frac{1}{\Gamma(\beta)\delta^{\beta}}y^{\beta-1}e^{-y/\delta}\left\|\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\right\|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)\delta^{\alpha+\beta}}\left(\frac{uv}{1+u}\right)^{\alpha-1}\left(\frac{v}{1+u}\right)^{\beta-1}e^{-v/\delta}\frac{v}{(1+u)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left\{\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}u^{\alpha-1}(1+u)^{-(\alpha+\beta)}\right\}\times\left\{\frac{v^{(\alpha+\beta)-1}e^{-v/\delta}}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)\delta^{\alpha+\beta}}\right\}

(64)

This means that

u
and
v
follow a beta prime distribution
U\sim{\rm B^{\prime}}(\alpha,\beta)
and a gamma distribution
V\sim{\rm Ga}(\alpha+\beta,\delta)
, respectively.
This also indicates that
U
and
V
are independent.
In other words, the beta prime distribution can be generated from the two gamma distributions.

Appendix C
Inscribed Maxwell distributions for the nonrelativistic kappa distribution

We derive a Maxwell distribution which is inscribed to the kappa distribution.
We consider the density ratio of the Maxwellian to the kappa distribution
a(v)
as a function of
v
. In addition, we consider its two derivatives.

\displaystyle a(v)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{f_{\rm M}(v)}{f_{\rm\kappa}(v)}=\frac{N_{M}\Gamma{\left(\kappa-1/2\right)}}{N_{\kappa}\Gamma{\left(\kappa+1\right)}}\left(\frac{\kappa\theta^{2}}{v_{M}^{2}}\right)^{3/2}\left(1+\frac{v^{2}}{\kappa\theta^{2}}\right)^{\kappa+1}\exp\left(-\frac{v^{2}}{v_{M}^{2}}\right)

(65)

\displaystyle a^{\prime}(v)

\displaystyle=2a(v)\left\{v\left[\frac{\kappa+1}{v^{2}+\kappa\theta^{2}}-\frac{1}{v_{M}^{2}}\right]\right\}

(66)

\displaystyle a^{\prime\prime}(v)

\displaystyle=2\left\{\Big(a^{\prime}(v)v+a(v)\Big)\left[\frac{\kappa+1}{v^{2}+\kappa\theta^{2}}-\frac{1}{v_{M}^{2}}\right]-a(v)\frac{2(\kappa+1)v^{2}}{(v^{2}+\kappa\theta^{2})^{2}}\right\}

(67)

When the Maxwellian core (Eq. (
1
)) is inscribed to the kappa distribution at
v=v_{0}
,
the following conditions need to be satisfied:

\displaystyle a(v_{0})

\displaystyle=1,~~~a^{\prime}(v_{0})=0,~~~a^{\prime\prime}(v_{0})\leq 0,

(68)

Since Eq. (
66
) changes its sign only once,

v=v_{0}
is the only contact point.
The conditions can be simplified to

\displaystyle v_{0}\left[\frac{\kappa+1}{v_{0}^{2}+\kappa\theta^{2}}-\frac{1}{v_{M}^{2}}\right]=0,~~\left(\left[\frac{\kappa+1}{v_{0}^{2}+\kappa\theta^{2}}-\frac{1}{v_{M}^{2}}\right]-\frac{2(\kappa+1)v_{0}^{2}}{(v_{0}^{2}+\kappa\theta^{2})^{2}}\right)\leq 0,

(69)

and then we obtain the following solution
v_{M}

\displaystyle v_{M}^{2}=\frac{1}{\kappa+1}\left(v_{0}^{2}+\kappa\theta^{2}\right).

(70)

There can be several choices for
v_{0}
.
If we employ
v_{0}=0
, then Maxwellian parameters are given by

\displaystyle v_{M}^{2}=\frac{\kappa}{\kappa+1}\theta^{2},~~~N_{M}=\left(\frac{1}{\kappa+1}\right)^{3/2}\frac{\Gamma(\kappa+1)}{\Gamma(\kappa-1/2)}N_{\kappa}.

(71)

This is identical to the asymptotic Maxwellian core in the
v\rightarrow 0
limit
[for example, Eq. (12) in
Livadiotis & McComas 2009
].
If we employ the most probable speed
v_{0}=\theta
,
then we obtain
Oka et al. 2013
’s adjusted Maxwellian

\displaystyle v_{M}=\theta,~~~N_{M}=e\frac{\Gamma(\kappa+1)}{\Gamma(\kappa-1/2)}\kappa^{-\frac{3}{2}}\bigg(1+\frac{1}{\kappa}\bigg)^{-(\kappa+1)}N_{\kappa},

(72)

where
e
is Euler’s number.
This adjusted Maxwellian is useful to evaluate
the thermal and nonthermal parts of the kappa distribution.
(
Oka et al. 2013
)
