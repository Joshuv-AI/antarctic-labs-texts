Abstract
. We prove four results towards a description, in terms of the null support function, of the set of isometric embeddings of the hyperbolic plane into Minkowski 3-space. We show that for sufficiently tame null support function, the corresponding entire surface of constant curvature -1 is complete, and for sufficiently sharp null support function, it is incomplete. Our results apply also to entire surfaces whose curvature is merely bounded.



1.
Introduction

The purpose of this paper is to study complete spacelike surfaces of constant intrinsic curvature
-1
(which we call hyperbolic), or more generally surfaces whose curvature is non-positive and is bounded above or below by negative constants, in Minkowski three-space
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
.

Examples and statement of the problem

It is easily shown that every complete spacelike surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
is entire, meaning that it is the graph of a function globally defined on the horizontal plane. Moreover, if the intrinsic curvature is non-positive, then (up to a reflection in a horizontal plane) such function is convex. The very first example is the well-known hyperboloid model of the hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, namely the future unit sphere in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
, see Figure
1
.

Figure 1.
The hyperboloid model of the hyperbolic plane (in red). In this and the following figures, the blue surface is the boundary of the domain of dependence of the red surface.

The first non-umbilic examples of complete hyperbolic surfaces in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
have been obtained by Hano and Nomizu in 1983 (
[
HN83
]
). By considering “surfaces of revolution” with spacelike axis, they reduced the problem of finding a function whose graph has constant intrinsic curvature
-1
to an ordinary differential equation, whose maximal solutions give a one-parameter family of non-equivalent surfaces. It turns out that these surfaces are complete, and thus intrinsically isometric to
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. See Figures
2
and
3
(on the left).

Another large source of examples arose from the work
[
BBZ11
]
of Barbot-Béguin-Zeghib: they showed that for every
g\geq 2
and every representation
\rho:\pi_{1}(S_{g})\to\mathrm{Isom}(\mathbb{H}^{3})\cong\mathrm{O}(2,1)\ltimes{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
(
S_{g}
being a closed orientable surface of genus
g
) whose linear part is Fuchsian, there exists a unique
\rho
-equivariant embedding of constant curvature
-1
. By cocompactness, the induced metric is complete and therefore isometric to
\mathbb{H}^{2}
; these surfaces are non-equivalent to the hyperboloid unless
\rho
has a global fixed point.

Figure 2.
A complete hyperbolic surfaces in the Hano-Nomizu family, obtained as surface of revolution with spacelike axis. Its null support is a continuous piecewise affine function on the circle.

Figure 3.
Another example in the Hano-Nomizu family, called the
semitrough
. In this case the null support function equals zero on a closed arc, and
+\infty
on its complement.

A third source of examples arises from the study of the asymptotics of entire hyperbolic surfaces. To explain this, let us introduce a fundamental object for the present work, namely the
null support function
. Given an entire convex spacelike surface
\Sigma
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
, expressed as the graph of a convex, 1-Lipschitz function
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, we define the function
\phi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
by

\phi(\theta)=\sup_{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}(x\cos\theta+y\sin\theta-f(x,y))=\sup_{p\in\Sigma}\langle p,\vec{\theta}\rangle~,

which is easily seen to be well-defined and lower-semicontinuous as a consequence of convexity of
f
. Here and in the following, we use the notation
\vec{\theta}:=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,1)
.

The geometric interpretation of the null support function is the following. Given
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
, the null affine plane
P
defined by the equation
\langle\vec{\theta},\cdot\rangle=\phi(\theta)
is a support plane for
\Sigma
, meaning that every translate of
P
in the future intersects
\Sigma
, while every translate in the past is disjoint from
\Sigma
. We also remark that the intersection of the future half-spaces bounded by the null support planes of equation
\langle\vec{\theta},\cdot\rangle=\phi(\theta)
, as
\theta
varies in
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, is called
domain of dependence

\mathcal{D}
of
\Sigma
, and is pictured in blue in Figures
1
,
2
and
3
.
Some concrete examples: the null support function of the hyperboloid of Figure
1
is identically zero and its domain of dependence is the future cone over the origin; the null support functions of some of the Hano-Nomizu examples are pictured in Figures
2
and
3
(on the right).

In
[
Li95
]
, Li showed that for every smooth function
\phi
on the circle, there exists a hyperbolic surface having null support function
\phi
, which is moreover complete. The results of
[
Li95
]
actually hold in higher dimension, for hypersurfaces of constant Gauss-Kronecker curvature. In dimension three, the existence part of Li’s results has been improved in
[
GJS06
]
, for Lipschitz continuous
\phi
, and in
[
BS17
]
for
\phi
lower semicontinuous and bounded. Furthermore, in
[
BS17
]
the first two authors proved that this construction gives a bijection between the set of (convex, which we always assume here) entire hyperbolic surfaces in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
with bounded second fundamental form and the (infinite-dimensional!) vector space of functions
\phi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
having the Zygmund regularity. Since an entire hyperbolic surface with bounded second fundamental form is necessarily complete, the aforementioned results provide another large class of isometrically embedded copies of the hyperbolic plane, non-equivalent to one another.

Finally, in
[
BSS19
]
, we characterized
all
entire hyperbolic surfaces in terms of their null support functions, by showing that the same construction gives
a bijection between the set of
entire
hyperbolic surfaces in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
and the set of lower semicontinuous functions
\phi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
which are finite on at least three points. In the present paper we address the question of characterizing all smooth isometric embeddings of the hyperbolic plane into
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
. Equivalently, our aim is to determine those lower semicontinuous functions
\phi
which correspond to
complete
hyperbolic surfaces.

It is worth pausing to point out, once more, that an entire spacelike surface may be incomplete. Roughly speaking, this may happen if the surface approaches a null direction quickly enough. For instance, in Figure
4
we see an entire hyperbolic surface, obtained as a surface of revolution with respect to a lightlike axis, which is incomplete, being intrinsically isometric to a half-plane in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
(
[
BS17
, Appendix A]
). Its null support function is equal to minus the characteristic function of the point in
\mathbb{S}^{1}
corresponding to the lightlike axis.

Figure 4.
The entire hyperbolic surface of revolution with lightlike axis, and its null support function. The surface is incomplete, because it has a Cauchy sequence escaping to the right.

We remark that in
[
BSS22
, Corollary E]
, we completely described the possible intrinsic geometry of entire hyperbolic surfaces, showing that they are isometric to the interior of the convex hull in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
of a subset of
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{2}
, and moreover any such metric can be realized. Let us now state our results.

Main results

Our first results are two conditions on the null support function which guarantee completeness of a convex entire spacelike surface. Although in the above discussion we focused on
hyperbolic
surfaces, our results hold more generally for non-positively curved surfaces satisfying certain curvature bounds.

Theorem A

(Sequentially sublinear condition)
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
be lower semicontinuous and finite on at least three points. Suppose that for each
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
at which
\phi
is finite, there exists
M>0
and a sequence
\theta_{i}\to\theta_{0}
such that

(Comp)

\phi(\theta_{i})<\phi(\theta_{0})+M|\theta_{i}-\theta_{0}|~.

If
\Sigma
is a convex entire spacelike surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
with curvature bounded below and null support function
\phi
, then
\Sigma
is complete.

Throughout, we identify
\mathbb{S}^{1}
with
{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}
in the standard way, and define
|\theta-\theta^{\prime}|
to be distance in
{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}
. This is not an essential point, as the statement remains true for any reasonable notion of distance in the circle.

We remark the condition (
Comp
) has a simple geometric interpretation in terms of the domain of dependence
\mathcal{D}
of the surface
\Sigma
which, as explained above, is entirely determined by the function
\phi
on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Namely, (
Comp
) is equivalent to the condition that, whenever
\mathcal{D}
has a null support plane
P
(which is of the form
\langle\vec{\theta},\cdot\rangle=\phi(\theta)
for some
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
), there exists a null line in
P
which does not intersect
\partial\mathcal{D}
. See Proposition
4.6
and Corollary
4.7
for more details.

If we restrict to constant curvature
-1
, Theorem
A
implies that for any function
\phi
satisfying (
Comp
), the entire hyperbolic surface with null support function
\phi
(which exists and is unique by the results in
[
BSS19
]
) is complete, and hence gives an isometric embeddings of the hyperbolic plane in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
.

See Figure
5
for a schematic picture of the condition (
Comp
) of Theorem
A
. We stress that a function
\phi
satisfying the condition (
Comp
) at every point can be highly discontinuous, and can take value
+\infty
on large portions of the circle. For instance, by virtue of Theorem
A
the function taking value
0
on any Cantor set in
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, and
+\infty
elsewhere, is the null support function of a complete hyperbolic surface.

Figure 5.
The condition (
Comp
) in Theorem
A
.

Hence Theorem
A
is a remarkable improvement with respect to the state-of-the-art, since so far the most general result in this direction, which follows from
[
BS17
]
, is that an entire hyperbolic surface with
Zygmund continuous
null support function is complete.
Nevertheless, Theorem
A
is not sharp, as showed by the next statement, which gives another criterion for completeness.

Theorem B

(Subloglogarithmic condition)
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
be lower semicontinuous and finite on at least three points, and let
\lambda>0
. Suppose that that for each
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
at which
\phi
is finite, there is a one-sided neighbourhood
U
of
\theta_{0}
and such that

(Comp’)

\phi(\theta)\leq\phi(\theta_{0})+\frac{\lambda}{4}|\theta-\theta_{0}|\log(-\log|\theta-\theta_{0}|)

for every
\theta\in U
. If
\Sigma
is a convex entire spacelike surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
with curvature bounded below by
-\lambda^{2}
and null support function
\phi
, then
\Sigma
is complete.

By one-sided neighbourhood of
\theta_{0}
, we mean that
U
contains an interval either of the form
(\theta_{0}-\epsilon,\theta_{0}]
or
[\theta_{0},\theta_{0}+\epsilon)
for
\epsilon>0
.

In the other direction, we state now two conditions which are sufficient to guarantee incompleteness.

Theorem C

(Power function condition)
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
be lower semicontinuous and finite on at least three points. Suppose that there exist
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
at which
\phi
is finite, a neighborhood
U
of
\theta_{0}
, and constants
\epsilon>0
and
0<\alpha<1
such that

(Inc)

\phi(\theta)-\phi(\theta_{0})>\epsilon|\theta-\theta_{0}|^{\alpha}

for every
\theta\in U
. If
\Sigma
is a convex entire spacelike surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
with null support function
\phi
and curvature bounded above by a negative constant, then
\Sigma
is incomplete.

For example, taking any
\alpha
, the conclusion holds if
\phi
has a
two-sided jump
at
\theta_{0}
, meaning that
\phi(\theta_{0})<\liminf_{\theta\to\theta_{0}}\phi(\theta)
, which covers the example of Figure
4
.

Theorem D

(One-sided superlogarithmic condition)
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
be lower semicontinuous and finite on at least three points. Suppose that there exist
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
at which
\phi
is finite, a neighbourhood
U
of
\theta_{0}
, and
\epsilon>0
such that

(Inc’)

\begin{cases}\phi(\theta)=+\infty&\text{ if }\theta\text{ is on one side of }\theta_{0}\\
\phi(\theta)\geq\phi(\theta_{0})+\epsilon|(\theta-\theta_{0})\log|\theta-\theta_{0}||&\text{ if }\theta\text{ is on the other one side of }\theta_{0}.\end{cases}

for every
\theta\in U
. If
\Sigma
is a convex entire spacelike surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
with null support function
\phi
and curvature bounded above by a negative constant, then
\Sigma
is incomplete.

Inc’
) in Theorem
D
.

for a schematic picture of the condition (
Inc’
) of Theorem
D
.

Taking together our completeness and incompleteness theorems, we have a narrow window of local behaviors of
\phi
for which we cannot yet determine completeness, such as
\phi(\theta)=|\theta\log|\theta||
near
\theta=0
. From our perspective, the still-open problem of classifying isometric immersions of the hyperbolic plane by their asymptotic behavior amounts to closing this window.

Let us now briefly outline the methods involved in the proofs.

Directional completeness

The first important step in our investigation consists in establishing a notion of “directional completeness". We say a convex entire surface
\Sigma
with null support function
\phi
is
incomplete at
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
(Definition
3.10
) if there is a proper finite-length geodesic
\gamma(t)
on
\Sigma
with
\langle\gamma(t),\vec{\theta}\rangle
converging to
\phi(\theta)
, where we recall that
\vec{\theta}
is the null vector
(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,1)
. Geometrically, this means that
\gamma(t)
is asymptotic to a null support plane which is defined by the equation
\langle\vec{\theta},\cdot\rangle=\phi(\theta)
. Otherwise, we say that
\Sigma
is
complete at

\theta
.

A first fundamental result about this notion is the following:

Proposition
E

(Rough version)
.

Let
\Sigma
be a convex entire spacelike surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
. If
\gamma:[0,T)\to\Sigma
is a proper geodesic with finite length, then there exists a unique direction
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
such that

\langle\gamma(t),\vec{\theta_{0}}\rangle
converges to
\phi(\theta_{0})
, and
\phi(\theta_{0})
is finite.

In other words,
\gamma(t)
is asymptotic to a unique null support plane. For the most complete version of Proposition
E
, see Section
3.2
. An immediate consequence of Proposition
E
, together with the Hopf-Rinow theorem, is the following.

Corollary
F
.

Let
\Sigma
be a convex entire spacelike surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
. If
\Sigma
is complete at every
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
, then
\Sigma
is complete.

In fact, all the theorems stated above are proved in the paper in a stronger version, in terms of directional completeness. Namely, Theorems
A
and
B
actually state that, if the null support function
\phi
satisfies the conditions (
Comp
) or (
Comp’
)
for a fixed
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
, then the convex entire surface
\Sigma
(satisfying some curvature bounds) is complete
at
\theta_{0}
. The statements of Theorems
A
and
B
are then an immediate consequence using Corollary
F
. Similarly, the conclusion of the stronger version of Theorems
C
and
D
is that the surface
\Sigma
is incomplete
at
\theta_{0}
.

Having established such a well-behaved notion of directional completeness, we now explain the main tools in the proofs of our results, keeping in mind that, based on Corollary
F
above, it now suffices to deal with completeness and incompleteness
in a fixed direction
\theta_{0}
.

Completeness criteria: “finding long segments"

In order to prove Theorems
A
and
B
, the rough strategy is the following. Assume
\gamma(t)
is a proper finite-length geodesic on
\Sigma
which is asymptotic to a null plane
P
in the direction of
\theta_{0}
. We need to use this geodesic in order to find lower bounds for the null support function
\phi
around
\theta_{0}
, thus showing the contrapositive of Theorems
A
and
B
.

Those lower bounds will be achieved by producing certain surfaces contained in the future of the surface
\Sigma
. Indeed, if a surface
\Sigma^{\prime}
is in the future of
\Sigma
, then the null support function of
\Sigma^{\prime}
is smaller than that of
\Sigma
. Those surfaces
\Sigma^{\prime}
that we consider need not be spacelike, though: roughly speaking, they contain “lightlike parts".
For instance, in the proof of Theorem
B
, these surfaces are the boundary of the future of a spacelike segments, and look exactly like the blue surface that appears in Figure
2
.

More concretely, the strategy involves two main steps:

(1)

If
\gamma(t)
is a proper finite-length geodesic on
\Sigma
asymptotic to a null support plane
P
, and
L
is a spacelike line in
P
, then the timelike distance of
\gamma(t)
from
L
tends to zero. (See Lemma
3.8
.)

(2)

If
L
is a spacelike line containing
\Sigma
in its future and
\Sigma
contains a point
p
whose timelike distance from
L
is smaller than
\delta
, then one can find a “long” spacelike segment parallel to
L
in the future of
\Sigma
, whose length is roughly
-\log\delta
. (See Proposition
4.3
.)

The proof of Theorem
A
essentially puts together these two ingredients: we produce a spacelike strip in the future of
\Sigma
which is “swept-out” by the segments of step (2), as the point
p
varies in the tail of the finite-length geodesic
\gamma
. This spacelike strip is very “wide” since the distance of
p
by the line
L
goes to zero by step (1).

Theorem
B
is proved by applying similar ideas, and by making those estimates more quantitative. The price to pay is that the “long segments” from item (2) that we consider here are constructed over a sequence of points
\gamma(t_{n})
in
\Sigma
, not over the entire tail of
\gamma
. Taking the boundary of the future of these segments provides entire surfaces in the future of
\Sigma
and leads to a better bound (indeed, the function
|\theta-\theta_{0}|\log(-\log|\theta-\theta_{0}|)
that appears in the condition (
Comp’
) is much larger than the linear function
M|\theta-\theta_{0}|
that appears in (
Comp
)), but at the price that condition (
Comp’
) must be supposed to hold in a whole one-sided neighbourhood of
\theta_{0}
, not just along a subsequence as in Theorem
B
.

Incompleteness criteria: “finding barriers"

Let us finally move on to an outline of the proofs of Theorems
C
and
D
. In this setting, we need to
assume
a lower bound on the null support function
\phi
of
\Sigma
near
\theta_{0}
, and infer the incompleteness of
\Sigma
in the direction of
\theta_{0}
. For both theorems, this is done by means of “barriers”.

More precisely, the general tool that we use is the following “comparison” statement, that allows to infer, given two surfaces, incompleteness of one from incompleteness of the other.

Corollary
G
.

Let
\Sigma_{+}
and
\Sigma_{-}
be entire spacelike surfaces in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
with null support functions
\phi_{+}
and
\phi_{-}
, and let
{\theta}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Suppose that the curvature functions of
\Sigma_{+}
and
\Sigma_{-}
satisfy:

(1)

K_{\Sigma_{-}}\leq-C\leq K_{\Sigma_{+}}\leq 0

for some constant
C>0
. If
\phi_{+}
touches
\phi_{-}
from below at
{\theta}
and
\Sigma_{+}
is incomplete at
\theta
, then
\Sigma_{-}
is also incomplete at
\theta
.

We say that
\phi_{+}

touches
\phi_{-}
from below at
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
if
\phi_{+}\leq\phi_{-}
and
\phi_{+}(\theta)=\phi_{-}(\theta)<\infty
.
Corollary
G
is essentially a consequence of:

(1)

The comparison principle for Gaussian curvature, saying that if the curvature functions of the surfaces
\Sigma_{+}
and
\Sigma_{-}
satisfy the inequality (
1
) and
\phi_{+}\leq\phi_{-}
, then
\Sigma_{+}
is in the future of
\Sigma_{-}
(Proposition
5.2
);

(2)

The statement that one can find a 1-Lipschitz map from
\Sigma_{+}
to
\Sigma_{-}
, and that, since
\Sigma_{+}
and
\Sigma_{-}
have the same null support plane
P
in the direction
\theta
, this 1-Lipschitz map sends a diverging Cauchy surface of
\Sigma_{+}
asymptotic to
P
to a diverging Cauchy surface of
\Sigma_{-}
, again asymptotic to
P
(Lemma
5.3
).

Based on Corollary
G
, we then prove Theorems
C
and
D
using explicit “barriers". As a first remark, using the hyperbolic surface of Figure
4
, one can immediately deduce that if a convex entire surface
\Sigma
has curvature bounded above by a negative constant and its null support function has a two-sided jump at a point
\theta_{0}
, then
\Sigma
is incomplete at
\theta_{0}
(see Corollary
7.1
). Theorem
C
is a much stronger statement: it shows that the condition (
Inc
), which is much weaker than having a two-sided jump, implies incompleteness at
\theta_{0}
.

To prove Theorem
C
we produce a “barrier", namely an entire spacelike surface whose curvature is non-constant, but is non-positive and bounded below. Moreover, this surface is incomplete in a direction
\theta_{0}
, around which the null support function behaves like
\epsilon|\theta-\theta_{0}|^{\alpha}
. This is done by constructing an explicit support function in the so-called
parabolic coordinates
. For Theorem
D
, instead, the “barrier” that we use has constant curvature and is obtained by imposing the invariance by a one-parameter group of isometries with hyperbolic linear part, similarly to the examples of Hano and Nomizu, except that in this case the translation part is allowed to be non-trivial. The surfaces obtained by this construction are incomplete (except when the trivial part vanishes, thus recovering the Hano and Nomizu surfaces), and therefore can be successfully applied as the “barrier"
\Sigma_{+}
in Corollary
G
.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
be the vector space
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
endowed with the standard Lorentzian metric

\langle(x,y,z),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\rangle=xx^{\prime}+yy^{\prime}-zz^{\prime}.

A vector
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
is said to be:

•

spacelike
if
\langle v,v\rangle>0
;

•

lightlike
, or
null
, if
\langle v,v\rangle=0
and
v\neq 0
;

•

timelike
if
\langle v,v\rangle<0
.

and
causal
if it is either timelike, lightlike, or zero. A causal vector
v
is called
future-directed
if the
z
-coordinate of
v
is non-negative.

The three-dimensional Minkowski space is the underlying oriented and time-oriented Lorentzian manifold of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
.
Its group of isometries is the semidirect product
\mathrm{O}(2,1)\ltimes{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
, and its identity component
\mathrm{O}_{0}(2,1)\ltimes{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
consists of the isometries that preserve both orientation and time-orientation.
We will not distinguish notationally between Minkowski space and the vector space
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
.

2.1.
Causality for curves and surfaces

A curve or surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
is a locally Lipschitz regular submanifold. Most of the time, our surfaces will be
C^{2}
. A curve in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
is called timelike (resp. causal) if every pair of points in it is timelike (resp. causally) separated. We say that a surface
\Sigma
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
is:

•

achronal
if it meets every timelike curve at most once;

•

acausal
if it meets every causal curve at most once;

•

spacelike
if it is
C^{1}
and its tangent plane at each point is acausal.

Clearly acausal is stronger than achronal. We will see below that if
\Sigma
is either convex or closed, then spacelike does imply acausal. However, since spacelike is a local condition, it does not in general imply acausal or achronal.

If
P
is an affine plane, let
v
be a nonzero vector orthogonal to the linear plane parallel to
P
. Then
P
is spacelike if and only if it is acausal, if and only if
v
is timelike. It is achronal if and only if
v
is causal.
An affine plane which is achronal but not acausal (that is, such that
v
is lightlike) will be called a
null plane
.

In the following, let
\pi:{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be the orthogonal projection to the plane
z=0
.

Remark 2.1
.

If
\Sigma
is an achronal surface, then
\pi|_{\Sigma}:\Sigma\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
is a homeomorphism onto its image. Indeed
\pi|_{\Sigma}
is locally injective because
\Sigma
meets every vertical line at most once, so the conclusion follows from invariance of domain. Hence
\Sigma
is the graph of a continuous function over a domain in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.
We can thus give the following characterization:

•

\Sigma
is achronal if and only if it is the graph of a
1
-Lipschitz function from a subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
to
{\mathbb{R}}
;

•

\Sigma
is acausal if and only if it is the graph of a contractive function (that is, it satisfies
|f(x,y)-f(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})|<\|(x-x^{\prime},y-y^{\prime})\|
);

•

\Sigma
is spacelike if and only if it is locally the graph of a
C^{1}
function with
\|Df\|<1
.

Remark 2.2
.

If
\Sigma
is spacelike, then its first fundamental form defines a
C^{0}
Riemannian metric on
\Sigma
.

Definition 2.3
.

We will denote by
J^{+}
be the cone of future-directed causal vectors (which includes the zero vector), and by
I^{+}
the open cone of future-directed timelike vectors. If
X
is a subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
, we will call the set

J^{+}(X)=\bigcup_{p\in X}\{q\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}|q-p\in J^{+}\}

the
causal future
of
X
, or simply the
future
of
X
. We will call the analogous set
I^{+}(X)
the
timelike future
of
X
. The pasts
J^{-}
,
I^{-}
,
J^{-}(X)
, and
I^{-}(X)
are defined analogously.

Note that according to our conventions,
X\subseteq J^{+}(X)
.

We also introduce the notion of
domain of dependence
, which we use primarily in section
4.4
. Since it gives a useful tool for visualizing achronal surfaces, we also draw the domain of dependence, shaded in blue, in Figures
1
,
2
,
3
.

Definition 2.4
.

The
domain of dependence
of an achronal surface
\Sigma\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
is the set of points
p\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
such that every causal line through
p
meets
\Sigma
.

2.2.
Convexity and support planes

We now turn our attention to notions concerning convexity, with particular interest in achronal surfaces. The definitions below take advantage of the splitting
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}\cong{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\times{\mathbb{R}}
, but in fact the notions are all invariant under the group of isometries of Minkowski space that preserve the time-orientation.

Definition 2.5
.

An achronal surface
\Sigma
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
is
locally (strictly) convex
it is locally the graph of a (strictly) convex function from a subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
to
{\mathbb{R}}
. It is
(strictly) convex
if it is the graph of a (strictly) convex function (which is therefore defined on a convex subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
).

Some authors define a surface as in Definition
2.5
above to be
future-convex
, since it is convex with respect to the choice of the future-directed vertical vector (or any other future-directed vector). We will simply say (locally) convex to simplify the terminology.

Let us now introduce support planes and study them in relation with the causality of Minkowski space.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
\Sigma
be a convex achronal surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
. A spacelike or null plane
P\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
is a
support plane
for
\Sigma
if
\Sigma
is contained in the (causal) future of
P
, but for every
\epsilon>0
,
\Sigma
is not contained in the future of
P+(0,0,\epsilon)
.

Remark 2.7
.

From the definition, it follows that a convex achronal surface
\Sigma
does not admit two distinct parallel support planes. That is, if
P
is a support plane, then every plane of the form
P+v
and distinct from
P
is not a support plane.

We remark that a support plane
P
may or may not intersect
\Sigma
. We say that a support plane
P

touches

\Sigma
at a point
p
if
p\in\Sigma\cap P
. By convexity, if
\Sigma
is a convex surface, for every
p\in\Sigma
there exists at least one support plane
P
that touches
\Sigma
at
p
. Let us now develop this point of view further.

Proposition 2.8
.

A convex achronal surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
can only be touched by spacelike or null support planes. A convex acausal surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
can only be touched by spacelike support planes.

Proof.

Let
\Sigma
be an achronal convex surface, and let
p\in\Sigma
. Since
\Sigma
is achronal, it does not intersect
I^{+}(p)
. Since
\Sigma
is the graph of a convex function on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, it is touched from below by the graph of some affine function
f(x,y)
at
p
. To prove the first statement, it suffices to show that the graph of
f
is not timelike.

Suppose by contradiction that the graph of
f
is timelike, and let
R
be the open ray in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
starting from
p
in the direction of the gradient of
f
. If
\pi:{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
denotes the vertical projection, then every point of
\pi^{-1}(R)
is either in
I^{+}(p)
or lies below the graph of
f
. Therefore, the restriction
\pi_{\Sigma}
of
\pi
to
\Sigma
cannot meet
R
. But by Remark
2.1
,
\pi_{\Sigma}
is a local homeomorphism at
p
. This contradiction shows that
p
is touched by a spacelike or null support plane.

If
\Sigma
is moreover acausal, then it does not intersect
J^{+}(p)
except at
p
, and the same argument with
I^{+}
replaced by
J^{+}
shows that it can have no null support planes.
∎

We immediately conclude:

Corollary 2.9
.

Every
C^{1}
convex acausal surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
is spacelike.

Proof.

Since
\Sigma
is
C^{1}
and convex, the tangent planes are precisely the support planes that touch
\Sigma
. By Proposition
2.8
, each tangent plane must be spacelike, that is,
\Sigma
is spacelike.
∎

Finally, let us relate the local convexity with the Gauss map and the curvature of the first fundamental form. Recall that the
Gauss map
of a spacelike surface
\Sigma
is the map
G:\Sigma\to\mathbb{H}^{2}
, where
\mathbb{H}^{2}
is the one-sheeted hyperboloid consisting of all those future-directed vectors
v
with
\langle v,v\rangle=-1
, sending
p\in\Sigma
to the unique
v\in\mathbb{H}^{2}
normal to
\Sigma
at
p
. The
shape operator
of
\Sigma
is the section
B
of the bundle
\mathrm{End}(T\Sigma)
defined by
B=P\circ dG
where
P:T_{G(p)}\mathbb{H}^{2}\to T_{p}\Sigma
is the parallel transport in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
. The
Gauss equation
in Minkoswki space is the following identity:

K_{\mathrm{I}}=-\det B~,

where
\mathrm{I}
is the first fundamental form of
\Sigma
and
K_{\mathrm{I}}
its curvature.

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
\Sigma
be a spacelike surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
.

•

If
\Sigma
is locally strictly convex, then
G
is a local homeomorphism.

•

If
\Sigma
is
C^{2}
, then it is locally convex (up to a reflection in the plane
z=0
) if and only if the first fundamental form is non-positively curved.

Proof.

The first item follows from the definition of locally strictly convex, since in a small neighbourhood
U
, no two points can have the same normal vector. Hence
G
is locally injective, and a local homeomorphism by the invariance of domain. The second item is a consequence of the Gauss equation, since local convexity/concavity is equivalent to
B
being non-negative definite. Applying a reflection in the horizontal plane, we can transform a locally concave map into a locally convex one.
∎

2.3.
Entire surfaces

In this paper we will study
entire
surfaces in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
, which we now define.

Definition 2.11
.

An achronal surface
\Sigma
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
is
entire
if it meets every timelike line exactly once.

Equivalently, an achronal surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
is entire if and only if it is the graph of a (globally defined) 1-Lipschitz function from
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
to
{\mathbb{R}}
. The following lemma shows that it suffices for
\Sigma
to be closed, and moreover that we can replace achronal by locally achronal (i.e. covered by open achronal sets), which is in general weaker than both achronal and spacelike.

Lemma 2.12

(
[
BSS19
, Proposition 1.10]
)
.

Let
\Sigma
be a closed locally achronal surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
. Then
\Sigma
is an entire achronal surface.

Another easy criterion that ensures entireness is the following:

Proposition 2.13

(
[
Bon05
, Lemma 3.1]
)
.

Let
\Sigma
be a spacelike surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
such that the first fundamental form is complete. Then
\Sigma
is an entire acausal surface.

The converse of Proposition
2.13
does not hold, that is, there exist examples of entire spacelike surfaces which are not complete. Those examples can also be produced of constant negative curvature, see Example
2.30
. This is exactly the starting point of the present work. Our results address necessary and sufficient conditions for an entire spacelike surface satisfying certain curvature bounds (as a particular case, when it has constant negative curvature) to be complete.

Remark 2.14
.

Recall that, from Section
2.1
, a spacelike surface is supposed to be
C^{1}
. When
\Sigma
is assumed to satisfy certain hypotheses on the curvature, like in the statements of all our main theorems, then we implicitly assume without further mention that
\Sigma
is
C^{2}
.

Moreover, all our surfaces will always be convex and entire. From Proposition
2.10
, an entire spacelike surface with non-positive curvature is convex up to reflection across a spacelike plane. By our convention of choosing the future-directed normal vector, the second fundamental form, which is defined as
\mathrm{I}\hskip-1.13791pt\mathrm{I}(\cdot,\cdot)=\mathrm{I}(B(\cdot),\cdot)
, will always be non-negative definite.

2.4.
Support functions

We now focus on convex entire achronal surfaces, which can be described dually by its
homogeneous support function

s
.

Definition 2.15
.

Given a convex entire achronal surface
\Sigma
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
, the
homogeneous support function
of
\Sigma
is the function
s:J^{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
defined by:

s({v})=\sup_{{p}\in\Sigma}\langle{p},{v}\rangle~.

The homogeneous support function is a proper closed convex function, which means that the supergraph
\{({v},z)\in J^{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}\,|\,z\geq s({v})\}
is nonempty, closed, and convex. (We remark that this meaning of proper is unrelated to the topological condition of properness as in Section
3
.) It is also homogeneous in the sense that
s(\lambda{v})=\lambda s({v})
for any
\lambda\geq 0
.

Remark 2.16
.

Fix
v\in J^{+}\setminus\{0\}
. Then
s(v)=c\in{\mathbb{R}}
if and only if the affine plane defined by the equation
\langle\cdot,v\rangle=c
is a support plane of
\Sigma
. In this case, it is the unique support plane parallel to
v^{\perp}
, by Remark
2.7
. On the other hand,
s(v)=+\infty
means that there exists no support plane parallel to
v^{\perp}
.

A standard fact in the theory of convex functions is the following.

Proposition 2.17
.

[
BSS19
, Proposition 2.4]
The map sending
\Sigma
to its homogeneous support function
s
gives a bijection between the set
of convex entire achronal surfaces in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
and the set
of homogeneous proper closed convex functions on
J^{+}
.

It is often more convenient to work with a dehomogenized support function. We will use two different dehomogenizations in this paper, either restricting to an affine spacelike plane, which we call an elliptic dehomogenization, or restricting to an affine null plane, which we call a parabolic dehomogenization.

Let us first introduce the elliptic dehomogenization. Denote by
\Xi:\overline{\mathbb{D}}\to J^{+}
be the inclusion of the closed unit disc at height one, namely:

(2)

\Xi(x,y)=(x,y,1)~,

for
x^{2}+y^{2}\leq 1
.

Definition 2.18
.

The
elliptic dehomogenization

s_{\mathrm{ell}}:\overline{\mathbb{D}}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
of the homogeneous support function
s
is the map:

s_{\mathrm{ell}}(x,y)=s\circ\Xi(x,y)=\sup_{{p}\in\Sigma}\langle{p},\Xi(x,y)\rangle~,

and is called the
elliptic support function
, or simply the
support function
. Its restriction to the unit circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}=\partial\mathbb{D}
, that we will always denote by
\phi
, is called
(elliptic) null support function
.

Clearly the homogeneous support function
s
is uniquely determined by
s_{\mathrm{ell}}
, and it is recovered as the homogeneous extension of
s_{\mathrm{ell}}
to
J^{+}
. As a consequence of Proposition
2.17
, we have:

Corollary 2.19
.

The map sending
\Sigma
to its (elliptic) support function
s_{\mathrm{ell}}
gives a bijection between the set of convex entire achronal surfaces in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
and the set
of proper closed convex functions on
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
.

We remark that the unit circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}=\partial\mathbb{D}
contains exactly one representative for each null direction in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
.
To simplify the notation, we will identify the unit circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
in the standard way by
\mathbb{S}^{1}={\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}
, and write the null support function of
\Sigma
as
\phi(\theta)
(where by a small abuse of notation, we write a real number
\theta
to actually mean the class of
\theta
modulo
2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}
). This choice of notation and terminology is consistent with the paper
[
BSS19
]
.

We will also occasionally find it convenient to use the alternative notation

(3)

\vec{\theta}:=\Xi(\cos(\theta),\sin(\theta))=(\cos(\theta),\sin(\theta),1)~,

so that

(4)

\phi(\theta)=\sup_{p\in\Sigma}\langle p,\vec{\theta}\rangle~.

The null support function at a point
\theta
encodes the null support plane of
\Sigma

in a given direction
, a notion that we now define formally and that will be of fundamental importance.

Definition 2.20
.

Let
\Sigma
be a convex entire achronal surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
, and let
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
. We say that
P
is a null support plane
in the direction

\theta
if
P
is a null support plane and the translate of
P
through the origin is
\vec{\theta}^{\perp}
.

Remark 2.21
.

By Remark
2.16
,
\Sigma
has a (necessarily unique) support plane in the direction of
\theta
exactly when
\phi(\theta)<+\infty
, and in this case it is the plane defined by the equation
\langle\cdot,\vec{\theta}\rangle=\phi(\theta)
.

2.5.
Parabolic support functions

The parabolic dehomogenization of the homogeneous support function, depends on a choice of a null direction. In most of the paper, we will choose the direction of the null vector
\vec{\pi}=(-1,0,1)
, but in Section
8
we will have to make different choices too.

The idea is to consider the restriction of the homogeneous support function to a null plane which is a translate of the linear plane
\vec{\pi}^{\perp}
. However, we choose a particular parametrization of this plane, that we now introduce.
Let
\overline{\mathcal{H}}=\{(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y}){\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\,|\,\mathsf{y}\geq 0\}
be the closed upper half-plane, and let
\zeta:\overline{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
as follows:

(5)

\begin{split}{\zeta}(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})=\begin{bmatrix}1-\mathsf{x}^{2}-\mathsf{y}^{2}\\
2\mathsf{x}\\
1+\mathsf{x}^{2}+\mathsf{y}^{2}\end{bmatrix}~.\end{split}

Remark 2.22
.

We will use everywhere the symbols
\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y}
for the coordinates on
\overline{\mathcal{H}}
, to distinguish them from the coordinates
(x,y)
on
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
and
(x,y,z)
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
.

Note that the image of
{\zeta}
is the intersection of
J^{+}
with the null plane
x+z=2
. This intersection meets every causal line through the origin except for the single null direction
\vec{\pi}
. The map
\zeta
is well-behaved with respect to the group of linear parabolic isometries fixing
\vec{\pi}
. This is formulated more precisely in the next two remarks.

Remark 2.23
.

The map
\zeta
above has the useful property that the action of the one-parameter group of parabolic isometries of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
fixing
\vec{\pi}=(-1,0,1)
corresponds to the action by translation in the
\mathsf{x}
-coordinate in the upper half-plane
\overline{\mathcal{H}}
.
Indeed, let
\{L_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
the one-parameter parabolic group defined by:

(6)

L_{t}\begin{bmatrix}-1\\
0\\
1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-1\\
0\\
1\end{bmatrix}\qquad L_{t}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\\
0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}t\\
1\\
-t\end{bmatrix}\qquad L_{t}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\\
1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1-t^{2}\\
2t\\
1+t^{2}\end{bmatrix}~.

Then it is immediate to check that

L_{t}\circ\zeta(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})=\zeta(\mathsf{x}+t,\mathsf{y})
.

Remark 2.24
.

Moreover, our choice of coordinates
{\zeta}
has the property that the projection

(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})\mapsto{\zeta}(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})/|{\zeta}(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})|={\zeta}(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})/2\mathsf{y}\in\mathbb{H}^{2,1}

from the upper half-plane to the unit hyperboloid
\mathbb{H}^{2}
is conformal (by a direct computation we have
|{\zeta}|:=\sqrt{-\langle{\zeta},{\zeta}\rangle}=2\mathsf{y}
). Indeed the pull-back of the hyperbolic metric of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
by this map is the standard metric
(d\mathsf{x}^{2}+d\mathsf{y}^{2})/\mathsf{y}^{2}
on the upper half-plane
\mathcal{H}
.

We are now ready to introduce the parabolic (null) support function.

Definition 2.25
.

The
parabolic dehomogenization

s_{\mathrm{par}}:\overline{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
of the homogeneous support function
s
is the map :

(7)

\begin{split}s_{\mathrm{par}}(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})=s\circ\zeta(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})=\sup_{{p}\in\Sigma}\langle{p},{\zeta}(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})\rangle~,\end{split}

and is called the
parabolic support function
. Its restriction to
{\mathbb{R}}=\partial\mathcal{H}=\{\mathsf{y}=0\}
, which we will always denote by
\psi
, is called the
parabolic null support function
.

Similarly to Equation (
4
), we thus have the following identity for the parabolic null support function:

(8)

\psi(\mathsf{x})=\sup_{{p}\in\Sigma}\langle{p},{\zeta}(\mathsf{x},0)\rangle~.

Remark 2.26
.

We will sometimes need to specify the direction at infinity, we will say that
s_{\mathrm{par}}
and
\psi
are the parabolic (null) support functions
with point at infinity
\vec{\pi}
. We will occasionally use the terminology “parabolic (null) support function with point at infinity
\vec{\theta}_{0}
" to mean the functions obtained as in (
7
) and (
8
), having modified the map
\zeta
by post-composition with an element of the group
\mathrm{O}(2)
of time-orientation isometries fixing the
z
-axis, sending
\vec{\pi}
to
\vec{\theta}
.

The following proposition collects formulas relating the elliptic and parabolic support functions.

Proposition 2.27
.

Let
s_{\mathrm{ell}}
and
s_{\mathrm{par}}
be the elliptic and parabolic dehomogenizations of the homogeneous support function
s
. Then

(9)

s_{\mathrm{ell}}\left(\frac{1-\mathsf{x}^{2}-\mathsf{y}^{2}}{1+\mathsf{x}^{2}+\mathsf{y}^{2}},\frac{2\mathsf{x}}{1+\mathsf{x}^{2}+\mathsf{y}^{2}}\right)=\frac{s_{\mathrm{par}}(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})}{1+\mathsf{x}^{2}+\mathsf{y}^{2}}~.

In particular, if
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{1}-\{\pi\}
, the elliptic and parabolic null support functions satisfy:

(10)

\phi(\theta)=\frac{\psi(\mathsf{x})}{1+\mathsf{x}^{2}}

where
\mathsf{x}=\tan(\theta/2)
. Moreover,

(11)

\phi(\pi)=\liminf_{\mathsf{x}^{2}+\mathsf{y}^{2}\to\infty}\frac{s_{\mathrm{par}}(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})}{1+\mathsf{x}^{2}+\mathsf{y}^{2}}~.

Proof.

The proof of (
9
) is immediate by observing that

\zeta(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})=(1+\mathsf{x}^{2}+\mathsf{y}^{2})\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1-\mathsf{x}^{2}-\mathsf{y}^{2}}{1+\mathsf{x}^{2}+\mathsf{y}^{2}}\\
\frac{2\mathsf{x}}{1+\mathsf{x}^{2}+\mathsf{y}^{2}}\\
1\end{bmatrix}

and applying the homogeneity of
s
and the definitions of
s_{\mathrm{ell}}
and
s_{\mathrm{par}}
. The proof of (
10
) then follows from setting
\mathsf{y}=0
and noting that

\vec{\theta}=\begin{bmatrix}\cos(\theta)\\
\sin(\theta)\\
1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1-\mathsf{x}^{2}}{1+\mathsf{x}^{2}}\\
\frac{2\mathsf{x}}{1+\mathsf{x}^{2}}\\
1\end{bmatrix}

for
\mathsf{x}=\tan(\theta/2)
. Finally, to show (
11
), observe that the radial projection from
\zeta(\overline{\mathcal{H}})
to the closed unit disc
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
at height one induces a homeomorphism between the one-point compactification
\overline{\mathcal{H}}\cup\{\infty\}
and
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. Moreover, since
s_{\mathrm{ell}}
is a closed convex function, its value at any boundary point equals the liminf there:

s_{\mathrm{ell}}(\cos(\theta),\sin(\theta))=\liminf_{(x,y)\to(\cos(\theta),\sin(\theta))}s_{\mathrm{ell}}(x,y)~.

Together with (
9
), we deduce

\phi(\pi)=s_{\mathrm{ell}}(-1,0)=\liminf_{\mathsf{x}^{2}+\mathsf{y}^{2}\to\infty}\frac{s_{\mathrm{par}}(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})}{1+\mathsf{x}^{2}+\mathsf{y}^{2}}

as claimed.
∎

Since the map
\zeta
in the definition of the parabolic support function omits the null direction spanned by
\vec{\pi}
, the parabolic null support function
\psi
does not quite contain as much information as the (elliptic) null support function
\phi
. However, the previous proposition shows that the missing information, namely the value of
\phi
at
\pi
, can be recovered from the value of
s_{\mathrm{par}}
in the interior of
\mathcal{H}
. In particular, we recover an analog of convex duality for parabolic support functions:

Corollary 2.28
.

The map sending
\Sigma
to its parabolic support function
s_{\mathrm{par}}
gives a bijection between the set of convex entire achronal surfaces in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
and the set
of proper closed functions on
\overline{\mathcal{H}}
convex with respect to the affine structure of the plane
x+z=2
.

We say that a function
u:\overline{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
is convex with respect to the affine structure of the plane
x+z=2
if
u\circ\zeta^{-1}:J^{+}\cap\{x+z=2\}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
is convex.

Proof.

We have to show that, given
u:\overline{\mathcal{H}}\to\{{\mathbb{R}}\cup+\infty\}
proper, closed and convex with respect to the affine structure of the plane
x+z=2
, there is a unique convex entire achronal surface
\Sigma
with parabolic support function
u
.
To see this, recall that the affine plane
x+z=2
meets every line through the origin in
J^{+}
except for
\mathrm{Span}(\vec{\pi})
. Hence we can extend
u
to a homogeneous function
s
on
J^{+}\setminus\mathrm{Span}(\vec{\pi})
. The 1-homogeneous extension
s
is still convex.
Finally, the function
s
has a unique closed convex extension to
\langle\vec{\pi}\rangle
, which is defined at the point
(-1,0,1)
by the liminf as in (
11
).
The existence and uniqueness of
\Sigma
then follow from Proposition
2.17
.
∎

2.6.
Examples

We conclude this preliminary section by two examples of entire spacelike surfaces of constant curvature
-1
, which will be used in the following.

Example 2.29
.

The first surface we consider is the so-called
semitrough
, which is pictured in Figure
3
. It can be parameterized by the map
X:(0,+\infty)\times{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
:

X(t,s)=\begin{bmatrix}t-\coth(t)\\
{\sinh(s)}/{\sinh(t)}\\
{\cosh(s)}/{\sinh(t)}\end{bmatrix}~.

One can see immediately that
X
is invariant under the one-parameter linear
hyperbolic
group
M_{s}
fixing
(1,0,0)
and translating by
s
along the geodesic
\{z^{2}-y^{2}=1\,,y=0\}=\mathbb{H}^{2}\cap\{x=0\}
. More precisely,
X(t,s+s_{0})=M_{s_{0}}X(t,s)
. It can be shown that
X
parameterizes an entire spacelike hyperbolic surface
\Sigma
. This surface
\Sigma
has been first studied by Hano and Nomizu in
[
HN83
]
, where it was also shown that it is complete. More precisely, Hano and Nomizu studied a one-parameter family of complete hyperbolic surfaces in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
which are invariant under the same linear hyperbolic group, which includes
\Sigma
; see also Figure
2
.

We omit the proofs of our claims here, since in Section
8
we will recover
X
as part of a different one-parameter family of surfaces, invariant under one-parameter groups of isometries now with non-zero translation parts.

The null support function of this entire surface is very simple, namely:

\phi(\theta)=\begin{cases}0&\textrm{if }\theta\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]\\
+\infty&\textrm{otherwise}\end{cases}~.

See again Figure

. Moreover, the surface is
complete
, which shows that the null support function of complete hyperbolic surfaces in

need not be finite.

It is instructive to consider another example, which is easily expressed in terms of the parabolic support function.

Example 2.30
.

We now consider a family of surfaces which are invariant under the linear
parabolic
group fixing
\vec{\pi}=(-1,0,1)
, which is defined in (
6
). These surfaces have been studied in detail in
[

, Appendix A]
. Here we will describe such a surface by means of its parabolic support function, using Corollary
2.28
. By Remark
2.23
, we should consider a parabolic support function of the form

u(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})=f(\mathsf{y})~.

It turns out that the choice

f(\mathsf{y})=\int_{0}^{\mathsf{y}}g(\mathsf{t})d\mathsf{t}\qquad g(\mathsf{y})=-2\sqrt{1+\epsilon^{2}\mathsf{y}^{2}}~,

for any
\epsilon\geq 0
,
gives rise to an entire spacelike surface of curvature

. For more details, see Section

.
By applying Lemma

, the corresponding entire spacelike surface provided by Corollary

is incomplete unless
\epsilon=0
. In fact, one sees readily that the case

corresponds to the hyperboloid
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, by definition of parabolic support function applied to
\Sigma=\mathbb{H}^{2}
:

s_{\mathrm{par}}^{\mathbb{H}^{2}}(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})=\sup_{p\in\mathbb{H}^{2}}\langle p,\zeta(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})\rangle=\langle\zeta(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y}),\zeta(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})/|\zeta(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})|\rangle=-|\zeta(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})|=-2\mathsf{y}~.

Finally, we can compute explicitly the elliptic or parabolic null support function, using Proposition

. Indeed, for
\mathsf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we immediately get
\psi(\mathsf{x})=u(\mathsf{x},0)=0
, and therefore

for all

f(\mathsf{y})=-\mathsf{y}\sqrt{1+\epsilon^{2}\mathsf{y}^{2}}-(1/\epsilon)\mathrm{arcsinh}(\epsilon\mathsf{y})

and therefore

by (

is a function that is constantly zero on the complement of

.

3.
Proper finite-length geodesics

In this section, we begin to develop some tools to understand the geodesic completeness of convex entire spacelike surfaces. Without further mention, we will work with
C^{2}
surfaces. The most important result is Proposition
E
, where we associate a unique null direction to each proper finite-length geodesic on any convex entire spacelike surface. We remark that the results in this section use the convexity assumption, but no further assumption on the curvature of the surface (e.g. curvature bounded above or below by negative constants, which are instead assumed in the main results of this paper).

3.1.
Geodesic convexity

In this subsection we prove that convex entire spacelike surfaces
\Sigma
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
are geodesically convex, in the sense that there is a unique geodesic in
\Sigma
between any two points.

Remark 3.1
.

When the curvature of
\Sigma
is a negative constant, the result follows (up to applying a homothety) by
[
BSS22
, Corollary E]
, where it has been proved that an entire spacelike surface of curvature
-1
is isometric to the interior of a domain in the hyperbolic plane bounded by complete geodesics.

As a tool to study the behavior of geodesics in
\Sigma
, we will use the extrinsic distance.
Given
p\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
and
q
in the complement of
I^{+}(p)\cup I^{-}(p)
, we define

d^{\mathrm{ext}}_{p}(q)=\sqrt{\langle q-p,q-p\rangle}~.

To simplify the notation, we will sometimes write
d^{\mathrm{ext}}_{p}(q)=|q-p|
.
We first establish some important properties of this function.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\Sigma
be an entire spacelike surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
. Fix any point
p\in\Sigma
. Then
d^{\mathrm{ext}}_{p}:\Sigma\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a well-defined proper function on
\Sigma
.

Proof.

This is Proposition 1 of
[
CY76
]
. Here is a recap of the proof. Write
\Sigma
as the graph of an entire 1-Lipschitz function
f
, as assume after an isometry that
p
is the origin and
df(0,0)=0
. We will write
x
for a point in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. Using only that
f(x)\leq|x|
, we see that
d^{\mathrm{ext}}_{p}
is well-defined. By Taylor’s theorem, there is an
\epsilon
such that
|f(x)|\leq\epsilon/2
on the circle of radius
\epsilon
. Since
f
is 1-Lipschitz, it follows that
|f(x)|\leq|x|-\epsilon/2
outside the circle of radius
\epsilon
. Therefore for
|x|\geq\epsilon
,
d^{\mathrm{ext}}_{p}((x,f(x)))=\sqrt{|x|^{2}-f(x)^{2}}\geq\sqrt{\epsilon|x|-\epsilon^{2}/4}
, and so
d^{\mathrm{ext}}_{p}
is proper.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\Sigma
be a convex spacelike surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
and let
\gamma(t)
be any geodesic of
\Sigma
. For any future-directed causal vector
v
, the function

\Phi_{v}(t)=\langle\gamma(t),v\rangle~.

is concave in
t
.

Proof.

The acceleration
\ddot{\gamma}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
is normal to
\Sigma
because
\gamma
is a geodesic. Moreover (recalling our conventions from Remark
2.14
) since
\Sigma
is convex,
\ddot{\gamma}
must be a nonnegative multiple of the future normal vector to
\Sigma
. Since the inner product of any two future-directed causal vectors is nonpositive, we have
\ddot{\Phi}_{v}(t)=\langle\ddot{\gamma}(t),v\rangle\leq 0
.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\Sigma
be a convex spacelike surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
, and let
\gamma(t)
be any unit speed geodesic of
\Sigma
with
\gamma(0)=p
. Then for every
t\geq 0
,

\frac{d}{dt}d^{\mathrm{ext}}_{p}(\gamma(t))\geq 1~.

Proof.

By the chain rule,

\frac{d}{dt}d^{\mathrm{ext}}_{p}(\gamma(t))=\langle\mathrm{grad}~d^{\mathrm{ext}}_{p}(\gamma(t)),\dot{\gamma}(t)\rangle~.

The gradient in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
of
d^{\mathrm{ext}}_{p}
at
q
is just the unit vector in the direction of
q-p
, so the right hand side is the inner product of two unit spacelike vectors in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
.
If
u
is a unit spacelike vector, define

(12)

\begin{split}I_{u}:=\{\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{1}|\langle u,\vec{\theta}\rangle\geq 0\}~,\end{split}

which a nonempty closed interval.

If
{u}
and
{v}
are two unit spacelike vectors, one can check that
\langle{u},{v}\rangle\geq 1
exactly when either
I_{u}\subseteq I_{v}
or
I_{v}\subseteq I_{u}
. We will prove the lemma by showing that
I_{\dot{\gamma}(t)}\subseteq I_{\mathrm{grad}\,d^{\mathrm{ext}}_{p}(\gamma(t))}
for all
t
.

For a given
\theta
, define

(13)

\begin{split}\Phi_{\theta}(t)=\langle\gamma(t),\vec{\theta}\rangle~,\end{split}

which is concave in
t
by Lemma
3.3
.

Now, suppose
\theta\in I_{\dot{\gamma}(t)}
. This implies
\dot{\Phi}_{\theta}(t)\geq 0
.
By concavity of
\Phi_{\theta}
, we have:

\Phi_{\theta}(t)-\Phi_{\theta}(0)\geq t\dot{\Phi}_{\theta}(t)\geq 0~.

This implies that
\theta\in I_{\mathrm{grad}\,d^{\mathrm{ext}}_{p}(\gamma(t))}
because

\langle\mathrm{grad}~d^{\mathrm{ext}}_{p}(\gamma(t)),\vec{\theta}\rangle=\langle\frac{\gamma(t)-p}{|\gamma(t)-p|},\vec{\theta}\rangle=\frac{\Phi_{\theta}(t)-\Phi_{\theta}(0)}{|\gamma(t)-p|}\geq 0~.

Hence,
I_{\dot{\gamma}(t)}\subseteq I_{\mathrm{grad}\,d^{\mathrm{ext}}_{p}(\gamma(t))}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

For the next lemma, we suppose that
\Sigma
is both convex and entire. By convexity, its Gauss curvature is non-positive, so no geodesic on
\Sigma
can have conjugate points, which is to say that the exponential map of
\Sigma
from any point
p
is a local diffeomorphism. The next lemma shows that it is also proper.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\Sigma
be a convex entire spacelike surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
. For any
p\in\Sigma
, the exponential map at
p
is a proper map from its domain of definition in
T_{p}\Sigma
to
\Sigma
.

Proof.

Let
U\subset T_{p}\Sigma
be the domain of definition of the exponential map, meaning the star-shaped domain consisting of vectors
v
in
T_{p}\Sigma
such that the geodesic through
p
with derivative
v
exists for time 1. It suffices to show that the composition
r(v):=d^{\mathrm{ext}}_{p}\circ\mathrm{exp}_{p}
is proper on
U
.

First, let
u
be an arbitrary unit vector in
U
and consider the restriction of
r
to the maximal ray
\{tu:t\in[0,t_{\mathrm{max}})\}
. We will show that this is a diffeomorphism from
[0,t_{\mathrm{max}})
to
[0,\infty)
. Obviously, its value at
t=0
is 0. According to Lemma
3.4
,
r(tu)
is a strictly increasing function of
t
and, integrating from 0,
r(tu)\geq t
. In the case that
t_{\mathrm{max}}=\infty
, it follows that
r(tu)
tends to infinity as
t\to t_{\mathrm{max}}
, and hence that
r(tu)
is a diffeomorphism from
[0,t_{\mathrm{max}})
to
[0,\infty)
. On the other hand, if
t_{\mathrm{max}}<\infty
, then the geodesic
\mathrm{exp}_{p}(tu)
must be proper in
\Sigma
since finite length geodesics that stay in a compact set are always extendable. Hence the properness of
d^{\mathrm{ext}}_{p}
on
\Sigma
(Lemma
3.2
) also implies in this case that
r(tu)
tends to infinity as
t\to t_{\mathrm{max}}
, and the claim follows.

To complete the proof, for any
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we need to show that the sublevel set
\{r(v)\leq a\}\subset U
is compact in
U
. According to the previous paragraph, there is a unique function
t_{a}(u)
on the unit sphere in
T_{p}\Sigma
such that
r(t_{a}u)=a
, and the sublevel set of
r
is the radial subgraph
\{tu\,|\,t\leq t_{a}(u)\}
. Moreover, since
r
is a smooth function on
U\setminus\{0\}
and its radial derivative is nonzero by Lemma
3.4
, the implicit function theorem shows that
t_{a}
is a smooth — in particular, continuous — function of
u
. Therefore the sublevel set of
r
is a compact subset of
U
, completing the proof.
∎

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\Sigma
be a convex entire spacelike surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
. Then
\Sigma
is geodesically convex.

Proof.

For any point
p\in\Sigma
, the exponential map
\mathrm{exp}_{p}
at
p
is a local diffeomorphism since
\Sigma
is non-positively curved, and proper by Lemma
3.5
, hence it is a covering map. Since
\Sigma
is simply connected,
\mathrm{exp}_{p}
is in fact a diffeomorphism. Therefore, there is a unique geodesic from
p
to any point
q
of
\Sigma
. Since
p
was arbitrary, we conclude that there is a unique geodesic between any two points of
\Sigma
, in other words that
\Sigma
is geodesically convex.
∎

3.2.
Asymptotics of finite-length geodesics

In the following, recall that
\vec{\theta}
is the null vector in the direction
\theta
as defined in (
3
). We now prove the following result that describes the asymptotic behaviour of incomplete proper geodesics on an entire convex spacelike surface.

Definition 3.7
.

Let
V
be an affine subspace of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
, and let
TV
be the parallel linear subspace. We say that a curve or a sequence in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
is asymptotic to
V
if it converges in the quotient
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}/TV
to the point corresponding to
V
.

Proposition E
.

Let
\Sigma
be a convex entire spacelike surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
. If
\gamma:[0,T)\to\Sigma
is a proper geodesic with finite length, then

(1)

There is a unique direction
{\theta}_{+}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
such that
\dot{\gamma}
converges projectively to
\vec{\theta}_{+}
as
t\to T
.

(2)

There is a unique null line
L=\{x+r\vec{\theta}_{+},r\in{\mathbb{R}}\}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
such that
\gamma
is asymptotic to
L
in the sense of Definition
3.7
.

(3)

\langle\gamma(t),\vec{\theta}_{+}\rangle\to\phi(\theta_{+})<\infty
as
t\to T
, where
\phi
is the support function of
\Sigma
.

(4)

\langle\gamma(t),\vec{\theta}\rangle\to-\infty
for all
\theta\neq\theta_{+}
.

Proof.

It is harmless to assume that
\gamma
is parameterized by unit speed. For
t\in[0,T)
and
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
, let

\Phi_{\theta}(t)=\langle\gamma(t),\vec{\theta}\rangle

as in equation (
13
). By Lemma
3.3
,
\Phi_{\theta}(t)
is concave in
t
.

Since
\dot{\gamma}(t)
is a spacelike vector, at every time
t
the set (defined similarly to (
12
) in the proof of Lemma
3.4
)

(14)

\begin{split}I_{t}:=\{\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{1}|\dot{\Phi}_{\theta}(t)\geq 0\}=\{\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{1}|\langle\dot{\gamma}(t),\vec{\theta}\rangle\geq 0\}\end{split}

is a nonempty closed interval. The concavity in
t
of
\Phi_{\theta}
says that
\dot{\Phi}_{\theta}
is non-increasing in time, hence for
t^{\prime}>t
we have
I_{t^{\prime}}\subseteq I_{t}
. Since the intervals
I_{t}
are closed, their intersection is nonempty. We claim that the intersection is a single point. Indeed, if it contained two different points
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
, then
\vec{\theta_{1}}+\vec{\theta_{2}}
is a timelike vector whose inner product with
\dot{\gamma}
is non-negative and decreasing with
t
, hence is bounded for all
t
. Then the unit vector
\dot{\gamma}
must lie between two spacelike planes perpendicular to
\vec{\theta_{1}}+\vec{\theta_{2}}
— in particular, in a compact subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
— so the finite-length curve
\gamma
must stay inside a compact subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
, contradicting properness. We conclude that the intersection of the intervals
I_{t}
over all
t
must be a single point. Let
{\theta}_{+}
be this point. Since
\dot{\gamma}^{\perp}\cap(\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\{1\})
consists of two vectors that converge to
\vec{\theta}_{+}
as
t\to T
, it follows that
\dot{\gamma}
converges projectively to
\vec{\theta}_{+}
, which proves the first statement.

For the second statement, we first observe that since
\Phi_{\theta_{+}}(t)
is non-decreasing for all
t\in[0,T)
and concave, it has a finite limit as
t\to T
. This already implies convergence to some null plane in the direction of
{\theta_{+}}
. To show that
\gamma
converges to some line in that plane, choose another point
{\theta}_{-}
which lies outside all of the intervals
I_{t}
. Next, let
e_{-}
be the positive multiple of
\vec{\theta}_{-}
with
\langle e_{-},\vec{\theta}_{+}\rangle=-1
; let
e_{+}=\frac{1}{2}\vec{\theta}_{+}
; and third let
e_{0}
be a (spacelike) unit vector perpendicular to both
e_{+}
and
e_{-}
. Using the basis
\{e_{-},{e}_{0},e_{+}\}
, we write
\gamma
as

(15)

\gamma(t)=a(t)e_{-}+b(t)e_{0}+c(t)e_{+}~.

By our choice of basis, we have
a(t)=-\Phi_{\theta_{+}}(t)
and
c(t)
is a negative multiple of
\Phi_{\theta_{-}}
, hence
\dot{a}(t)\leq 0<\dot{c}(t)
for all
t
. By our hypotheses on the basis
\{e_{-},{e}_{0},e_{+}\}
,

|\dot{\gamma}(t)|^{2}=-\dot{a}(t)\dot{c}(t)+\dot{b}(t)^{2}~,

which is equal to one since we have taken
\gamma
to be unit speed. Hence
\dot{b}(t)^{2}\leq 1
for all
t
, so the coordinate
b(t)
has a finite limit as
t\to T
. Therefore,
\gamma
converges to a null line parallel to
\vec{\theta}_{+}
.

To prove the third statement, we just need to show that the unique null plane containing the line
L
is a support plane for
\Sigma
, since we have shown that
\gamma
lies in the future of this plane and is asymptotic to it. For each time
t
, the spacelike surface
\Sigma
lies in the future of the past null cone
J^{-}(\gamma(t))
. Since
\gamma
is asymptotic to
L
and
c(t)
tends to infinity, the null cones
J^{-}(\gamma(t))
converge in compact subsets to
J^{-}(L)
, which is a half-space whose boundary is the null plane containing
L
. Hence
\Sigma
lies in the future of this null plane. But it cannot lie in the future of any parallel null plane in the future of
L
since
\gamma
is asymptotic to
L
. Therefore, the null plane containing
L
is the null support plane for
\Sigma
in the direction of
\theta_{+}
.

Finally, to prove the fourth statement, note that since
\gamma
is proper and the first two coordinates are bounded, the third coordinate,
c(t)
must diverge, and since it is increasing, it diverges to positive infinity. Since
c(t)
is a negative multiple of
\Phi_{\theta_{-}}=\langle\gamma(t),\vec{\theta}_{-}\rangle
, this is equivalent to

(16)

\lim_{t\to T}\langle\gamma(t),{\vec{\theta}_{-}}\rangle=-\infty~.

Now observe that the only assumption we made on
\theta_{-}
was that it is contained in the complement of all the intervals
I_{t}
. But since the intersection of the intervals
I_{t}
equals
\{\theta_{+}\}
, every
\theta\neq\theta_{+}
satisfies this assumption up to restricting
\gamma
to
(T-\epsilon,T)
. Hence (
16
) holds for any choice of
\theta_{-}\neq\theta_{+}
.

∎

In the same setting as Proposition
E
, we now show that the timelike distance from
\gamma
to any spacelike line contained in its asymptotic null plane converges to zero. This will be important in the proof of Theorem
A
, in the next section.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
\Sigma
be a convex entire spacelike surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
, and suppose
\gamma:[-T,0)\to\Sigma
is a proper finite length geodesic in
\Sigma
asymptotic to a null support plane
P
of
\Sigma
in the sense of Definition
3.7
. Let
L
be any spacelike line in
P
. Then as
t\to 0
, the timelike distance from
L
to
\gamma(t)
tends to zero.

Proof.

Choose a basis
\{e_{-},e_{0},e_{+}\}
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
such that
e_{+}
is a future null vector tangent to
P
,
e_{0}
is tangent to
L
, and
e_{-}
is a future null vector with

\displaystyle\langle e_{+},e_{-}\rangle

\displaystyle=-1/2

\displaystyle\langle e_{0},e_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle\langle e_{\pm},e_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=0.

Suppose furthermore that
L
goes through the origin, up to applying a translation. Write

(17)

\begin{split}\gamma(t)=a(t)e_{-}+b(t)e_{0}+c(t)e_{+}.\end{split}

Observe that
a(t)=-2\langle\gamma(t),e_{+}\rangle=-2\Phi_{e_{+}}(t)
and similarly
a(t)=-2\Phi_{e_{-}}(t)
, hence by Lemma
3.3

a(t)
and
c(t)
are convex functions.
As in the proof of Proposition
E
,
the function
a(t)
is positive, nonincreasing and (by the assumption that
L
contains the origin) converges to 0 as
t\to 0
.
Since the graph of
a
on the interval
[t,0]
lies below the linear function interpolating its values at the endpoints, we have that

(18)

\begin{split}\dot{a}(t)\leq-a(t)/|t|\end{split}

for all
t<0
.

Since
a(t)
is bounded as
t\to 0
and
b(t)
is bounded by Proposition
E
, in order for
\gamma
to be proper we must have
\lim_{t\to 0}c(t)=+\infty
. Assume from here on that
t
is close enough to zero such that

(19)

\begin{split}c(t)>0\textrm{ and }\dot{c}(t)>0.\end{split}

Then
\gamma(t)
is in the future of
L
, and the timelike distance to
L
is
\sqrt{a(t)c(t)}
.

Assuming without loss of generality that
\gamma
is unit speed,

1=|\dot{\gamma}(t)|^{2}=-\dot{a}(t)\dot{c}(t)+\dot{b}(t)^{2}~,

and in particular
-1\leq\dot{a}\dot{c}
.
Therefore

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(|t|+\frac{a(t)c(t)}{|t|}\right)

\displaystyle=-1+\frac{\dot{a}c}{|t|}+\frac{a\dot{c}}{|t|}+\frac{ac}{t^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\dot{a}\dot{c}+\frac{\dot{a}c}{|t|}+\frac{a\dot{c}}{|t|}+\frac{ac}{t^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(\dot{a}+\frac{a}{|t|}\right)\left(\dot{c}+\frac{c}{|t|}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 0

where the last inequality follows from (
18
) and (
19
). Hence
|t|+ac/|t|
is bounded as
t\to 0
, and so the timelike distance
\sqrt{a(t)c(t)}
tends to zero.
∎

Remark 3.9
.

Although we will not use a more quantitative version of Lemma
3.8
, the proof shows that if the proper geodesic
\gamma:[-T,0)\to\Sigma
is parameterized by unit speed, then the timelike distance from
L
to
\gamma(t)
is
O(\sqrt{|t|})
.

3.3.
Directional completeness

In light of Proposition
E
, it is natural to give the following definition (recall also Definition
2.20
).

Definition 3.10
.

A convex entire spacelike surface
\Sigma
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
is
incomplete at
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
if there is a proper finite-length geodesic on
\Sigma
asymptotic to a null support plane in the direction of
\theta
. Otherwise, we say that
\Sigma
is
complete at

\theta
.

Remark 3.11
.

According to our definition, if
\Sigma
admits no null support plane in the direction of
\theta
, then
\Sigma
is complete at
\theta
.

The following is an immediate consequence of Proposition
E
and the Hopf-Rinow theorem.

Corollary F
.

Let
\Sigma
be a convex entire spacelike surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
. If
\Sigma
is complete at every
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
, then
\Sigma
is complete.

Proof.

The Hopf-Rinow theorem says that if
\Sigma
is incomplete, it has a proper finite length geodesic. By statements (2) and (3) of Proposition
E
, this geodesic must be asymptotic to some null support plane of
\Sigma
. Hence
\Sigma
is incomplete at some direction
\theta
.
∎

In the following lemma, we show that one could alternatively define completeness at
\theta
in terms of Cauchy sequences. As a corollary, it is not necessary to require that the proper finite-length curve is a geodesic in Definition
3.10
. This will be useful below when we want to show (see the proof of Proposition
5.1
in Section
5
) that a surface is incomplete using a short map from another incomplete surface.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
\Sigma
be a convex entire spacelike surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
and let
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Suppose that there exists a diverging Cauchy sequence on
\Sigma
asymptotic to a null support plane in the direction of
\theta
. Then
\Sigma
is incomplete at
\theta
.

Proof.

Suppose that
p_{n}
is a diverging Cauchy sequence asymptotic to a null support plane
P
. We need to show that there exists a proper finite-length geodesic asymptotic to
P
. Let
\Phi_{{\theta}}(p)=\langle\vec{\theta},p\rangle
. By the last statement of Proposition
E
, it is sufficient to find a proper finite-length geodesic on which
\Phi_{{\theta}}
is bounded below.

Fix a point
p_{0}
in
\Sigma
and let
\gamma_{n}:[0,t_{n}]\to\Sigma
be the sequence of unit speed geodesic segments from
p_{0}
to
p_{n}
. Let
T_{n}
be their tangent vectors at
p_{0}
, and let
T_{0}
be a subsequential limit of the
T_{n}
. Let
\gamma:[0,t_{0})\to\Sigma
be the maximal geodesic segment starting at
p_{0}
with tangent vector
T_{0}
. The main point is to prove that
t_{0}\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}t_{n}
.

Let
\Omega\subset T_{p_{0}}\Sigma
be the domain of the exponential map at
p_{0}
. Since
\exp_{p_{0}}:\Omega\to\Sigma
is a diffeomorphism by Proposition
3.6
and
p_{n}=\exp(t_{n}T_{n})
is proper in
\Sigma
, the sequence
t_{n}T_{n}
is proper in
\Omega
, so any limit point will lie outside of
\Omega
. Hence the maximal existence time of the geodesic in the limiting direction is less than or equal to the norm of this limit point.

It follows that
\gamma
is finite length, and proper. To show that
\Phi_{{\theta}}
is bounded on
\gamma
, first note that since
p_{n}
converges to
P
, there is a constant
C
such that
\Phi_{{\theta}}(p_{n})\geq-C
for all
n
. Next, the concavity of
\Phi_{{\theta}}\circ\gamma_{n}
(Lemma
3.3
) implies that
\Phi_{{\theta}}\circ\exp_{p_{0}}
is bounded below by
\min(\Phi_{{\theta}}(p_{0}),-C)
on each segment
[0,t_{n}T_{n}]
. Since
\Phi_{{\theta}}\circ\exp_{p_{0}}
is continuous on
\Omega
, it has the same bound on the segment
[0,t_{0}T_{0}]
. This completes the proof.
∎

4.
Completeness I: sequentially sublinear condition

In this section we will prove the first completeness criterion, namely Theorem
A
.
We state it here in a local form, namely in terms of completeness in a given direction
\theta_{0}
. The statement given in the introduction then follows immediately by Corollary
F
.

Theorem
A

(Sequentially sublinear condition – local version)
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
be lower semicontinuous and finite on at least three points. Suppose
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
is such that
\phi(\theta_{0})<+\infty
and there exists
M>0
and a sequence
\theta_{i}\to\theta_{0}
such that

(Comp)

\phi(\theta_{i})<\phi(\theta_{0})+M|\theta_{i}-\theta_{0}|~.

If
\Sigma
is a convex entire spacelike surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
with curvature bounded below and null support function
\phi
, then
\Sigma
is complete at
\theta_{0}
.

In particular, if condition (
Comp
) holds for every
\theta_{0}
at which
\phi
is finite, then
\Sigma
is complete.

The two fundamental properties that we will use in the proof are Lemma
3.8
and Proposition
4.3
, which is proved in Section
4.2
and in turn relies on Lemma
4.2
below. The proof of Theorem
A
is then completed in Section
4.3
.

4.1.
An application of the comparison principle

Let us first quickly recall the (finite) comparison principle.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be an open domain with compact closure, and let
\Sigma_{\pm}=\mathrm{graph}(f_{\pm})
be spacelike surfaces in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
, for
f_{\pm}\in C^{2}(\Omega)\cap C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})
. Suppose that the curvature functions of
\Sigma_{+}
and
\Sigma_{-}
satisfy:

K_{\Sigma_{-}}\leq-C\leq K_{\Sigma_{+}}\leq 0

for some constant
C>0
. If
f_{-}<f_{+}
on
\partial\Omega
, then
f_{-}<f_{+}
on
\Omega
.

Proof.

Let us first assume that the curvature of
\Sigma_{+}
is strictly larger than
-C
. Assume that
f_{+}-f_{-}
takes nonpositive values on
\Omega
, and let
x_{\min}
be its minimum point, which is in
\Omega
since
f_{+}-f_{-}
is positive on
\partial\Omega
. Translating
\Sigma_{+}
vertically by
f_{+}(x_{\min})-f_{-}(x_{\min})
, we obtain a new surface
\Sigma_{+}^{\prime}
which is tangent to
\Sigma_{-}
over
x_{\min}
and contained in the future of
\Sigma_{-}
. But this contradicts the condition that the curvature of
\Sigma_{-}
is strictly larger in absolute value than that of
\Sigma_{+}
.

In the general case, namely when we only assume
K_{\Sigma_{+}}\geq-C
, take
\delta>0
so that
f_{-}+\delta\leq f_{+}
on
\partial\Omega
.
Then applying a homothety of a factor
1+\epsilon
to
\Sigma_{+}
, the previous case applies. Take the limit as
\epsilon\to 0
, we conclude that
f_{-}+\delta\leq f_{+}
on
\Omega
, so that

f_{-}<f_{+}
as claimed.
∎

By applying the comparison principle, we now prove a fundamental estimate.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\Sigma=\mathrm{graph}(f)
be an entire spacelike surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
of curvature
\geq-1
. Suppose that
f(x,y)\geq h>0
on the boundary of the half-infinite strip
[0,\infty)\times(-h,h)
. Then for
r
sufficiently large (depending on
h
),

(20)

f(r,0)\geq\frac{he^{-2r}}{4}~.

Proof.

For each value of
r
, we will find a semitrough
\Sigma_{-}
whose height on the boundary of the the half-infinite strip (and therefore on the entire half-infinite strip, by convexity) is at most
h
, and whose height above the point
(r,0)
is approximately
he^{-2r}/2
up to lower order terms. We would like to conclude by the comparison principle (Proposition
4.1
) on the half-infinite strip that
\Sigma
lies above this semitrough, but we cannot apply it directly since the strip is not bounded.

Fortunately, there is an easy fix. Apply a
\delta
-Lorentz boost (for
\delta>0
small) around the
y
-axis, namely the linear isometry

L_{\delta}=\begin{pmatrix}\cosh(\delta)&0&\sinh(\delta)\\
0&1&0\\
\sinh(\delta)&0&\cosh(\delta)\end{pmatrix}~,

that sends
\Sigma
to a surface
\Sigma^{\delta}=L_{\delta}(\Sigma)
. Let us call
f_{\delta}
the function whose graph is
\Sigma^{\delta}
. Since
L_{\delta}
does not decrease the height of all points whose first coordinate is non-negative,
f_{\delta}\geq f
on the half-infinite strip, and therefore
f_{\delta}\geq h
on the boundary of the half-infinite strip.

Moreover, observe that
f>0
on the half-infinite strip because it is at least
h
on its boundary and 1-Lipschitz. Hence the portion of the surface
\Sigma^{\delta}
over the half-infinite strip is in the future of the plane of equation
x\sinh(\delta)-z\cosh(\delta)=0
, and therefore
f_{\delta}>h
on the segment of the half-infinite strip defined by
x=h/\tanh(\delta)
. We can then apply the standard comparison principle on the truncation
x\leq h/\tanh(\delta)
of the half-infinite strip to see that
\Sigma^{\delta}
lies above the semitrough, and take the limit as
\delta\to 0
to conclude that
\Sigma
does as well.

Given a point
(r,0)
along the positive
x
axis, it remains only to find a semitrough satisfying the two conditions above. For some
d,\epsilon
to be given as functions of
r
, let
X_{d,\epsilon}(s,t)
be a parametrized semitrough given by

X_{d,\epsilon}(s,t)=\begin{bmatrix}t-\coth(t)-d+1\\
\sinh(s)/\sinh(t)\\
\cosh(s)/\sinh(t)-\epsilon\end{bmatrix}

for
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
t\in(0,+\infty)
. (Observe that
X_{d,\epsilon}(s,t)
is simply a translate of the semitrough introduced in Example
2.29
, where
d
and
\epsilon
are the parameters of horizontal and vertical translation.)
In order to guarantee that the height of the semitrough along the boundary of the half-infinite strip is at most
h
, it is sufficient for the surface
X_{d,\epsilon}
to contain the point
(0,h,h)
. Indeed, since the semitrough is symmetric by reflection in the plane
y=0
, if it contains the point
(0,h,h)
then it also contains
(0,-h,h)
, hence by convexity its height over
\{0\}\times(-h,h)
is
\leq h
. Moreover, by direct inspection one sees that the height function of the semitrough is decreasing on
\{(x,h)\,|\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}\}
as a function of
x
. Observe also that for any
d\in{\mathbb{R}}
there exists a unique
\epsilon
such that
X_{d,\epsilon}
contains the point
(0,h,h)
, since the vertical translates of an entire graph foliate
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
. Moreover
\epsilon
is positive because
X_{d,0}
is always contained in the future of the
x
-axis. We begin by giving an asymptotic formula for this
\epsilon
as a function of
d
, valid for large
d
.

By assumption, there is a point
(s_{0},t_{0})
such that
X_{d,\epsilon}(s_{0},t_{0})=(0,h,h)
. Comparing
x
-coordinates, we have

d=t_{0}-\coth{t_{0}}+1

which we can invert to find

t_{0}=d+O(e^{-2d})~.

We can eliminate
s_{0}
from the two equations for the
y
- and
z
- coordinates using
\cosh^{2}-\sinh^{2}=1
to find

(h+\epsilon(d))^{2}-h^{2}=1/\sinh^{2}(t_{0})~.

Next, taking the positive solution of the quadratic equation for
\epsilon
and Taylor approximating
\sinh
yields

(21)

\epsilon(d)=2e^{-2t_{0}}/h+O(e^{-4t_{0}})=2e^{-2d}/h+O(e^{-4d})~.

Here, and for the remainder of the proof, the implicit constant in
O()
is allowed to depend on
h
.

The next step is to choose
d(r)
in order that the height of
X_{d(r),\epsilon(d(r))}
above the point
(r,0)
is rather large, approximately
he^{-2r}/2
. For any
d
and
r
, let
t_{1}
be such that
X_{d,\epsilon(d)}(0,t_{1})=(r,0,z_{1})
for some height
z_{1}
. We now use the same Taylor approximations as above. Comparing
x
-coordinates gives

t_{1}=d+r+O(e^{-2(d+r)})~,

and comparing
z
-coordinates, plugging in the formula (
21
) for
\epsilon(d)
, gives

z_{1}=2e^{-t_{1}}+O(e^{-3t_{1}})-2e^{-2d}/h+O(e^{-4d})

and hence

z_{1}=2e^{-(d+r)}-2e^{-2d}/h+O(e^{-3(d+r)})+O(e^{-4d})

To make sense of the big-O notation here, recall that we will take
d
to be a function of
r
. Specifically, we choose
d(r)
to maximize the highest-order part of
z_{1}
(that is to say, everything except for the big-O terms). Doing so, we find that we should take
d(r)=r-\log(h/2)
, which yields

z_{1}=he^{-2r}/2+O(e^{-4r})~.

It follows that for
r
large enough,
z_{1}\geq he^{-2r}/4
. The lemma then follows by the comparison principle.
∎

4.2.
Finding long segments in the future

The following proposition, roughly speaking, serves to show that if a convex entire spacelike surface
\Sigma
with curvature bounded below is close to a spacelike line

and contained in the future of
L
, then the future of
\Sigma
contains a long segment parallel to
L
.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\Sigma
be a convex entire spacelike surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
of curvature
\geq-1
, and
L
a spacelike line containing
\Sigma
in its future. For any
h>0
, there exists
\epsilon>0
such that for any
\delta<\epsilon
, if there is a pair of points

and

with timelike distance

\tilde{L}
of length
\frac{1}{2}\log(h/4\delta)
parallel to

at a distance

bisected by the ray from

to

is contained in the future of
\Sigma
(Figure

).

Proof.

Fix

. Let us choose

in such a way that inequality (

for
r\geq r_{0}
. We will take
\epsilon=he^{-2r_{1}}/4
, for some

for some

. We need to prove, assuming

, that the the spacelike segment
\tilde{L}
of length
r
parallel to
L
at a distance
h
bisected by the ray from
p
to

is contained in the future of
\Sigma
.

Up to an isometry of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
, we may suppose that
L
is the
x
-axis,
p
is the point
(r,0,0)
, and
q=(r,0,z)
for

. Then
\tilde{L}
is the segment from
(r/2,0,h)
to
(3r/2,0,h)
. Let
R
be the intersection of
J^{+}(\Sigma)
with the spacelike plane

, or equivalently the convex hull of the intersection of

with this plane. Since
\Sigma
is contained in the future of

,

is contained in the infinite strip

were contained in the half-infinite strip

would be at least

on the boundary of this strip, and we would satisfy the conditions of Lemma

Therefore, there must be a point

and

.

Next, we use the fact that

to show that, for

sufficiently large, half of the line
\tilde{L}
is also in

. Since
\Sigma
is spacelike and contains the point

, the disk

I^{+}(q)\cap\{z=h\}

must also be contained in

. This horizontal disk is centered at
(r,0,h)
with radius at least
h(1-e^{-2r}/4)
. We shall show that, for

sufficiently large, the convex hull of this disk together with the point

contains the half of the segment
\tilde{L}
between
(r/2,0,h)

Proof of Theorem
A

Now we are ready to prove Theorem

Proof of Theorem
A
.

It clearly suffices to prove the theorem in the case that
\theta_{0}=0
and
\phi(0)=0
. We will prove the contrapositive, namely we will suppose we have an entire hyperbolic surface
\Sigma
that is incomplete at 0, and then we will show that for any slope

Let

be the null support plane of
\Sigma
in the direction

be a geodesic ray of finite length in

. Also fix a second null support plane

of
\Sigma
, and let

be the spacelike line
P\cap Q
. Up to an isometry of

we may suppose that

is the

to which

converges (by Proposition

{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
. We furthermore suppose, by chopping off the beginning of

and

For any value of

there is a time after which the tail of

from

. For each point of this tail of

with

to find a segment parallel to

of length

, lying in the future of
\Sigma
. By the choice of

, each of these segments is contained in the hyperbolic cylinder

of this cylinder with bounds

|y|\leq l(\epsilon)/4

over which we have a priori no control. To explain the bound (

), observe that if the curve
\gamma
was actually contained in the plane

; in general, since the

It remains to find the null support function

be the doubly infinite strip we obtain by dropping the cutoff on the

as the intersection of the hyperbolic cylinder

is the

. Moreover, in those null directions sufficiently close to 0, every support plane of

is also a support plane of

, and so in a neighborhood of zero (whose size depends on

is exactly

Using the approximation

, there is a neighborhood of zero on which

, where

. This is exactly the negation of the condition (

) in the case

.

.

.

It is easy to check using the relationship in Equation (

4.4.
A geometric condition

In this section, we translate the condition (
Comp
) into a condition on the domain of dependence of
\Sigma
(Definition
2.4
). For context, we remark that the domain of dependence of a convex entire spacelike surface is always a

, which is by definition an open subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
that can be written as the intersection of the futures of some number — possibly infinite, but at least 2 — of non-parallel null planes
[
Bon05
]
. Its boundary is a convex entire achronal surface that is nowhere spacelike. The null support function of (the boundary of) the domain of dependence of
\Sigma
is the same as the null support function of
\Sigma
, and conversely the domain of dependence of
\Sigma
depends only on the null support function.

For reference, we state the negation of the condition (
Comp
) at a point
\theta_{0}
: for all
M>0
, there exists
\epsilon>0
such that
\phi(\theta)\geq\phi(\theta_{0})+M|\theta-\theta_{0}|
for all
\theta
with
|\theta-\theta_{0}|<\epsilon
. By Remark
3.11
and Theorem
A
, if
\Sigma
is incomplete at
\theta_{0}
then
\phi(\theta_{0})
is finite and
\phi
satisfies the negation of condition (
Comp
) at
\theta_{0}
.

Proposition 4.6
.

Suppose
\Sigma
has a support plane
P
in the direction
\theta_{0}
. Condition (

) is equivalent to the condition that there is a null line in

that does not intersect the boundary
\partial\mathcal{D}
of the domain of dependence

of
\Sigma
.

Proof.

Since both conditions are invariant by the isometry group of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
, it suffices to prove the proposition in the case that
\theta_{0}=0
and
\phi(\theta_{0})=0
. Having done so, it is convenient to use Remark
4.5
to substitute the parabolic null support function

for the elliptic null support function
\phi

corresponds to

.

We begin with an observation about the parabolic null support function of points. From Equations (
5
) (with
\mathsf{y}=0
) and (

) for the parabolic null support function, we see that the parabolic null support function of a single point
p=[a,b,c]
(or equivalently, of its future null cone) is the polynomial

(23)

f_{p}(\mathsf{x})=(a-c)+2b\mathsf{x}-(a+c)\mathsf{x}^{2}

of degree at most 2 in
\mathsf{x}
. Clearly this construction provides a vector space isomorphism between
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
and the space of polynomials of degree at most 2. Moreover, observe that
p
is in the null plane
P
exactly when
\langle p,[1,0,1]\rangle=a-c=0
, which is equivalent to
f_{p}(0)=0
. In this case, the linear coefficient
f_{p}^{\prime}(0)
measures the horizontal displacement in
P
, meaning that the sets
\{p\in P\,|\,f_{p}^{\prime}(0)=m\}
are precisely the null lines
L
in
P
. This provides a bijective correspondence between slopes
m\in{\mathbb{R}}
and null lines
L\subset P
.

With that in mind, we may now prove the proposition. First suppose that every null line in
P
meets
\partial\mathcal{D}
somewhere. Then for any positive number
M
, let
L=\{p\in P\,|\,f_{p}^{\prime}(0)=M+1\}
be the null line corresponding to the slope
m=M+1
. By assumption, there is a point
p\in L\cap\partial\mathcal{D}
. Since
\psi
is the parabolic null support function of
\mathcal{D}
— defined as a supremum over points of
\mathcal{D}
— it follows that
\psi\geq f_{p}
. But since
f_{p}(0)=0
and
f_{p}^{\prime}(0)>M
, there is an
\epsilon>0
such that
f_{p}(\mathsf{x})\geq M\mathsf{x}
for
0\leq\mathsf{x}<\epsilon
. Repeating the argument for negative
M
and taking the minimum of the two values of
\epsilon
, we conclude that
\psi
satisfies the negation of condition (
Comp
) at 0.

Conversely, suppose that the negation of condition (
Comp
) holds for
\psi
at
0
, and let
L
be any null line in
P
, and
M
be its corresponding slope. By assumption, there is an
\epsilon
such that for all
\mathsf{x}
with
|\mathsf{x}|<\epsilon
, there holds
\psi(\mathsf{x})\geq M|\mathsf{x}|
. Now let
q
be a point in the future of
\partial\mathcal{D}
, so that
f_{q}
is a lower bound for
\psi
. Choose
p\in L
sufficiently far in the future — thus making its quadatic term sufficiently negative — that
f_{p}(\mathsf{x})<f_{q}(\mathsf{x})
for all
\mathsf{x}
with
|\mathsf{x}|\geq\epsilon
. If we require furthermore that the quadratic term of
f_{p}
is at least less than zero, then
f_{p}(\mathsf{x})\leq M|\mathsf{x}|
for
|\mathsf{x}|<\epsilon
as well. Hence, whether
\mathsf{x}
is small or large, we have
f_{p}(\mathsf{x})\leq\psi(\mathsf{x})
, and so
p
is in
J^{+}(\partial\mathcal{D})
. Since
p
is also in
P
, a support plane for
\partial\mathcal{D}
, it follows that
p
must actually be contained in
\partial\mathcal{D}
. This completes the proof.
∎

We immediately conclude the following:

Corollary 4.7
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a regular domain in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
. Suppose that every support plane
P
of
\mathcal{D}
contains a null line disjoint from
\partial{\mathcal{D}}
. Then any
convex entire spacelike surface with domain of dependence
\mathcal{D}
and curvature bounded below is complete.

In this section we prove a tool to deduce incompleteness in a given direction

.

and

5.1.
An important corollary

Before proving the lemma, we note that in the case of hyperbolic surfaces, or more generally under certain bounds on the curvature, we can restate our assumptions purely in terms of support functions. Given two functions
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}
on the circle, we say that

from below at

and

First, observe that if conditions (1) and (2) in Proposition

of

everywhere; the condition (2) is then equivalent to the equality
\phi_{+}(\theta)=\phi_{-}(\theta)
.
The converse holds under a suitable curvature assumption, based on an application of the maximum principle. The proof follows immediately from
[

, Proposition 3.11]
.

Corollary G

5.2.
Proof of Proposition
5.1

Let us now provide the proof of Proposition
5.1
. We first need to show that we can find a 1-Lipschitz map from
\Sigma_{+}
to
\Sigma_{-}
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\Sigma_{+}
and
\Sigma_{-}
be entire spacelike surfaces in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
with
\Sigma_{+}
convex. Then there exists a 1-Lipschitz map
\Pi:\Sigma_{+}\cap J^{+}(\Sigma_{-})\to\Sigma_{-}
. Moreover, if
\Sigma_{+}
and
\Sigma_{-}
have the same null support plane
P
and
\Sigma_{+}
has a diverging Cauchy sequence
p_{n}
asymptotic to
P
which is in the future of
\Sigma_{-}
, then
\Pi(p_{n})
is a diverging Cauchy sequence in
\Sigma_{-}
asymptotic to
P
.

Proof.

We essentially use the Lorentzian analog of the fact that closest point projection onto a convex body in Euclidean space is distance decreasing, but we must be slightly careful because the sign reversal means that we need to project from the wrong side, where the closest point may not be unique. Instead, we start with any foliation
\mathcal{F}=\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
by convex entire spacelike surfaces, including
\Sigma_{+}=\mathcal{F}_{0}
as a leaf. For concreteness, take
\mathcal{F}
to be the foliation by vertical translates of
\Sigma_{+}
. Let
V
be the unique past timelike vector field such that

(1)

V
is normal to
\mathcal{F}
, and

(2)

\mathcal{F}
is invariant by the flow of
V
, meaning that the time
t_{0}
flow of
V
maps
\mathcal{F}_{t}
to
\mathcal{F}_{t+t_{0}}
.

If
\mathcal{F}_{0}
is the graph of the function
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, then the leaves of
\mathcal{F}
are the level sets of the function
F(x,y,z)=z-f(x,y)
, and then
V=-\operatorname{grad}F/\langle\operatorname{grad}F,\operatorname{grad}F\rangle
. Starting from any point in
\Sigma_{+}\cap J^{+}(\Sigma_{-})
, if we flow by the vector field
V
we will eventually hit
\Sigma_{-}
because
\Sigma_{-}
is convex and entire (this is essentially the observation that the domain of dependence of a convex entire surface contains its future). Moreover, each flow line will intersect
\Sigma_{-}
exactly once, and transversally, since
V
is timelike and
\Sigma_{-}
is spacelike. Let
\Phi(x,t):\Sigma_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
be the flow of
V
, and
T:\Sigma_{+}\cap J^{+}(\Sigma_{-})\to{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
be the intersection time with
\Sigma_{-}
, which is smooth by transversality. We want to show that if
x
is in
\Sigma_{+}\cap J^{+}(\Sigma_{-})
, then the smooth map
\Pi:\Sigma_{+}\cap J^{+}(\Sigma_{-})\to\Sigma_{-}
defined by
\Pi(x)=\Phi(x,T(x))
is 1-Lipschitz. It is harmless to assume that
x
is in
\Sigma_{+}\cap I^{+}(\Sigma_{-})
, so that a small neighbourhood of
x
in
\Sigma_{+}
is contained in the future of
\Sigma_{-}
. If
W
is tangent to
\Sigma_{+}
at
x
, then by the chain rule,

d\Pi(W)=\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial x}(W)+\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial t}dT(W)

Since
\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial x}(W)
is tangent to the leaves of the foliation, and
\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial t}=V
is orthogonal to the leaves of the foliation and timelike, we conclude

|d\Pi(W)|^{2}\leq\bigg|\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial x}(W)\bigg|^{2}

It remains to show that the right hand side is less than or equal to
|W|^{2}
, which will follow from convexity of the foliation. If we extend
W
to a vector field
\tilde{W}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
which is invariant by the flow of
V
, then the derivative of the square length of
\tilde{W}
with respect to
t
is

\mathcal{L}_{V}\langle\tilde{W},\tilde{W}\rangle=(\mathcal{L}_{V}g)(\tilde{W},\tilde{W})=-2|V|\mathrm{I}\hskip-1.13791pt\mathrm{I}(\tilde{W},\tilde{W})\leq 0

where
g
is the Minkowski metric,
\mathcal{L}
the Lie derivative,
\mathrm{I}\hskip-1.13791pt\mathrm{I}=(1/2)\mathcal{L}_{N}g
the second fundamental form of the foliation with respect to the future unit normal
N
, and
|V|
the positive length defined by
V=-|V|N
. The final inequality is the convexity of the foliation. Integrating this inequality gives

\bigg|\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial x}(0,T)(W)\bigg|^{2}\leq\bigg|\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial x}(0,0)(W)\bigg|^{2}=|W|^{2}

Putting this together, we find that
\Pi
is 1-Lipschitz.

For the “moreover" part, let
p_{n}
a diverging Cauchy sequence in
\Sigma_{+}\cap J^{+}(\Sigma_{-})
such that

(24)

\langle p_{n},\vec{\theta}\rangle\to\phi_{+}({\theta}).

Since
\Pi
is Lipschitz, the sequence
\Pi(p_{n})\subset\Sigma_{-}
is still Cauchy. Let us show that the sequence
\langle\Pi(p_{n}),\vec{\theta}\rangle
converges to
\phi_{-}({\theta})
. Since
\Pi(p_{n})
lies in the timelike past of
p_{n}
and
\vec{\theta}
is future-directed, we have

(25)

\langle\Pi(p_{n}),\vec{\theta}\rangle\geq\langle p_{n},\vec{\theta}\rangle.

But since
\Sigma_{-}
lies in the future of the null support plane of
\Sigma_{+}
at
\theta
, we also have

(26)

\langle\Pi(p_{n}),\vec{\theta}\rangle\leq\phi_{+}({\theta})=\phi_{-}(\theta).

Putting together (
24
), (
25
) and (
26
), we conclude that

(27)

\langle\Pi(p_{n}),\vec{\theta}\rangle\to\phi_{-}({\theta})~.

To conclude the proof, it only remains to show that the Cauchy sequence
\Pi(p_{n})
is diverging. This is easily done by a contradiction argument: if
\Pi(p_{n})
had a converging subsequence, then the limit point
q_{\infty}
would be in
\Sigma_{-}
because
\Sigma_{-}
is closed, and in
P
because
\langle q_{\infty},\vec{\theta}\rangle=\phi_{-}(\theta)
by (
27
). But then the null support plane
P
would touch
\Sigma_{-}
at
q_{\infty}
, contradicting Proposition
2.8
.
In conclusion, we have shown that
\Pi(p_{n})
is a diverging Cauchy sequence in
\Sigma_{-}
asymptotic to
P
.
∎

We are now ready to conclude the proof of Proposition
5.1
.

Proof of Proposition
5.1
.

Suppose
\Sigma_{+}
is in the future of
\Sigma_{-}
. Let
\Pi:\Sigma_{+}\to\Sigma_{-}
the 1-Lipschitz map from Lemma
5.3
. Since
\Sigma_{+}
is incomplete at
{\theta}
, there is a Cauchy sequence
p_{n}
in
\Sigma_{+}
asymptotic to a null support plane
P
in the direction of
\theta
.
By the second part of Lemma
5.3
,
\Pi(p_{n})
is a diverging Cauchy sequence in
\Sigma_{-}
asymptotic to
P
.
By Lemma
3.12
,
\Sigma_{-}
is incomplete at
{\theta}
.
∎

6.
Completeness II: subloglogarithmic condition

The main result of this section, namely Theorem
B
below, provides another sufficient condition for an entire spacelike surface of curvature bounded below to be complete.

Let us give the statement of Theorem
B
, which again we give in a local form; the statement that appears in the introduction follows again by Corollary
F
.

Theorem
B

(Subloglogarithmic condition – local version)
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
be lower semicontinuous and finite on at least three points, and let
\lambda>0
. Suppose
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
is such that
\phi(\theta_{0})<+\infty
and there is a one-sided neighbourhood
U
of
\theta_{0}
with

(Comp’)

\phi(\theta)\leq\phi(\theta_{0})+\frac{\lambda}{4}|\theta-\theta_{0}|\log(-\log|\theta-\theta_{0}|)

for every
\theta\in U
.
If
\Sigma
is a convex entire spacelike surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
with curvature bounded below by
-\lambda^{2}
and null support function
\phi
, then
\Sigma
is complete at
\theta_{0}
.

In particular, if condition (
Comp’
) holds for every
\theta_{0}
at which
\phi
is finite, then
\Sigma
is complete.

Remark 6.1
.

Note that in the statement of Theorem
B
, we need an explicit lower bound on the curvature to conclude that the surface is complete, whereas in Theorem
A
we only needed that the curvature had some lower bound. A priori, it might be possible that there exist two convex entire spacelike surfaces with curvature bounded below, having the same null support function
\phi
, one of which is complete and the other incomplete.

For instance, by the results in
[
BSS19
]
, for every
\kappa>0
there is a convex entire spacelike surface of constant negative curvature
-\kappa
with a given null support function
\phi
, and these surfaces foliate a regular domain in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
. We are unable to rule out the possibility that some of these surfaces become incomplete
\kappa
sufficiently large, for instance for
\phi
a function as in Theorem
B
.

Remark 6.2
.

Theorem
B
also shows that the sufficient condition for completeness from Theorem
A
is not a necessary condition.
The point is that the function
|\theta-\theta_{0}|\log|\log|\theta-\theta_{0}||
does not satisfy the sequentially sublinear condition (
Comp
) of the previous section. Neither though does the sequentially sublinear condition (
Comp
) imply the subloglogarithmic condition (
Comp’
), since the first condition applies even to subsequences.

6.1.
Preliminary steps

To prove Theorem
B
, we can assume after an isometry that
\theta_{0}=0
and
\phi(0)=0
, in which case the condition (
Comp’
) becomes:

\phi(\theta)\leq\frac{\lambda|\theta|\log(-\log|\theta|)}{4}~.

We will find the computations easier if we work with parabolic null support functions, so as a preliminary step we show this condition essentially doesn’t change when the null support function is replaced by the parabolic null support function, for the point
\theta_{0}=0
.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
s
be a homogeneous function, and let
\phi
and
\psi
be respectively its elliptic dehomogenization and parabolic dehomogenization, with point at infinity
\pi
.
If

\phi(\theta)\leq\frac{\lambda|\theta|\log(-\log|\theta|)}{4}

for all
\theta
in some (one-sided) neighbourhood
U
of 0, then

(28)

\begin{split}\psi(\mathsf{x})\leq\frac{\lambda|\mathsf{x}|\log(-\log|\mathsf{x}|)}{2}\end{split}

for all
\mathsf{x}
in some (one-sided) neighbourhood
U^{\prime}
of 0.

Proof.

The second statement of Proposition
2.27
, gives us the relationships
\mathsf{x}=\tan(\theta/2)
and

\phi(\theta)=\frac{\psi(\mathsf{x})}{1+\mathsf{x}^{2}}~.

Assume
\mathsf{x}
is small enough that
3|\mathsf{x}|/2\leq|\theta|\leq 2|\mathsf{x}|

\displaystyle\psi(\mathsf{x})

\displaystyle=(1+\mathsf{x}^{2})\phi(\theta)

\displaystyle\leq(1+\mathsf{x}^{2})\frac{\lambda|\theta|\log(-\log|\theta|)}{4}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\mathsf{x}^{2})\frac{\lambda|\mathsf{x}|\log(-\log|\mathsf{x}|-\log(3/2))}{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda|\mathsf{x}|}{2}(1+\mathsf{x}^{2})\left(\log(-\log|\mathsf{x}|)+\frac{\log(3/2)}{\log|\mathsf{x}|}\right)

where in the last line we used that
\log(a+b)=\log a+\log(1+b/a)\leq\log a+(b/a)
for any
a>0
and
b\in(-a,+\infty)
.
Developing the cross terms in the product, we obtain:

\psi(\mathsf{x})\leq\frac{\lambda|\mathsf{x}|\log(-\log|\mathsf{x}|)}{2}+\frac{\lambda|\mathsf{x}|}{2}\left(\mathsf{x}^{2}\log(-\log|\mathsf{x}|)+\frac{\log(3/2)}{\log|\mathsf{x}|}+\frac{\log(3/2)\mathsf{x}^{2}}{\log|\mathsf{x}|}\right)~.

We claim that the second term on the right hand side is negative for
|x|
small, which concludes the proof. To see this more easily, set
|\mathsf{x}|=e^{-t}
. Then the sum in the last bracket equals

e^{-2t}\log(t)-\log(3/2)(1+e^{-2t})/t
, which is clearly negative for
t
large.
∎

As a last preliminary step, we observe that it suffices to prove Theorem
B
assuming
\lambda=1
. This follows from the following lemma, whose proof is straightforward from the definition of support function.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
\phi
and
\phi^{\prime}
be the null support functions of two convex spacelike surfaces
\Sigma
and
\Sigma^{\prime}
. If
\Sigma^{\prime}=(1/\lambda)\Sigma
, then
\phi^{\prime}=\phi/\lambda
. The same holds for the parabolic null support functions.

Indeed, from Lemma
6.4
, if
\Sigma
has curvature bounded below by
-\lambda^{2}
and satisfies (
28
), then
\Sigma^{\prime}=(1/\lambda)\Sigma
has curvature bounded below by
-1
and satisfies (

) with

. Clearly
\Sigma
is complete in a given direction

if and only if
\Sigma^{\prime}
is. Hence it is harmless to assume

in Theorem

.

6.2.
Proof of Theorem
B

In the proof of Theorem
A
, we used two fundamental qualitative observations:

(1)

the timelike distance to a spacelike line contained in a null support plane
P
tends to zero along incomplete geodesics asymptotic to

(Lemma

), and

(2)

to each point in
\Sigma
at which this timelike distance is small, there corresponds a segment in the future of

with large spacelike length (Proposition

We will achieve Theorem

by turning the qualitative observations (1) and (2) into more quantitative estimates.

Proof of Theorem
B
.

In Section

we observed already that we can assume
\theta_{0}=0
, which corresponds to
\mathsf{x}=0
in parabolic coordinates, that the parabolic null support function
\psi
satisfies (

), and that
\lambda=1
. Moreover, up to applying a reflection in the plane

to the surface
\Sigma
, we can assume that the one-sided neighbourhood

contains an interval of the form

. Hence we assume that (

) holds for small positive

.

Suppose that
\Sigma
is incomplete at
\mathsf{x}=0
, and let
\gamma
be a proper geodesic ray asymptotic to the support plane of
\Sigma
in the direction of
\mathsf{x}=0
, which we will denote

. Again allowing ourselves the freedom of an isometry of

, we may suppose that the null line in

to which

is asymptotic (Proposition

) goes through the origin. We moreover assume that the

with some other null support plane of

\Sigma
is contained in the future of the

-axis.

The next step is to define a certain spacelike surface with

as a null support plane and which is complete at

. It is easiest to describe this surface in Lorentzian-cylindrical coordinates

defined on the future of the

(29)

\begin{split}\begin{bmatrix}x\\

\rho\cosh(\alpha)\end{bmatrix}~.\end{split}

Observe that
\rho

\alpha\to+\infty
as you approach

. Let
\Sigma_{\epsilon}
be the spacelike surface defined by
\rho(\alpha)=\epsilon(1+\alpha^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}

.

Since

and we see that this is spacelike for all

, so integrating its square root to infinity, we see that the length is infinite.

We will now use the completeness of
\Sigma_{\epsilon}
at

to show that there is a divergent sequence of points

on the curve

\Sigma_{\epsilon}

at

.

Let us now consider the points

We now apply Proposition

, the proposition gives a segment
\tilde{L}_{n}
of length

r=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{\alpha_{n}}{4\epsilon}\right)~.

Setting
C=-\log(4\epsilon)/4
, this implies
r/2=\log(\alpha_{n})/4+C
. In summary, the endpoints of the segment
\tilde{L}_{n}
have
\rho
-coordinate 1,
\alpha
-coordinate
\alpha_{n}
, and
y
-coordinate
y_{n}\pm(\log(\alpha_{n})/4+C)
. Let us call
w_{n}
the endpoint with
y
-coordinate
y_{n}+\log(\alpha_{n})/4+C
.

These segments
\tilde{L}_{n}
lie in the future of
\Sigma
, and therefore give lower bounds on the (parabolic) null support function
\psi
of
\Sigma
. To calculate these lower bounds, we need to get our hands just a little bit dirty with parabolic coordinates.

Recall from Equation (
23
) that the parabolic null support function of the point
p=[a,b,c]
(equivalently, of its future null cone) equals

f_{p}(\mathsf{x})=(a-c)+2b\mathsf{x}-(a+c)\mathsf{x}^{2}~.

Hence the parabolic null support function of the point
w_{n}
with cylindrical coordinates
(1,\alpha_{n},y_{n}+\log(\alpha_{n})/4{+C})
is

f_{w_{n}}(\mathsf{x})=-e^{-\alpha_{n}}+2\left(y_{n}+\frac{\log(\alpha_{n})}{4}+C\right)\mathsf{x}-e^{\alpha_{n}}\mathsf{x}^{2}~.

For each
n
, we have
f_{w_{n}}(\mathsf{x})\leq\psi(\mathsf{x})
for all
\mathsf{x}
, but we only use this inequality at the point
\mathsf{x}_{n}=e^{-\alpha_{n}}
, for which we have

\psi(\mathsf{x}_{n})\geq f_{w_{n}}(\mathsf{x}_{n})=2\mathsf{x}_{n}\left(-1+y_{n}+\frac{\log(-\log\mathsf{x}_{n})}{4}+C\right)~.

Since the
y_{n}
tend to zero, we can take
\epsilon
small enough so that
C>\max_{n}y_{n}+1
, and conclude

\psi(\mathsf{x}_{n})>\frac{\mathsf{x}_{n}\log(-\log\mathsf{x}_{n})}{2}~,

and this gives a contradiction. Hence the theorem is proved.
∎

7.
Incompleteness I: power function condition

The purpose of this section is to prove Theorem
C
, which we restate below with a slightly strengthened conclusion with respect to the introduction, namely that
\Sigma
is incomplete
at
\theta_{0}
.

Theorem
C

(Power function condition – local version)
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
be lower semicontinuous and finite on at least three points. Suppose
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
is such that
\phi(\theta_{0})<+\infty
and there exists a neighborhood
U
of
\theta_{0}
and constants
\epsilon>0
,
0<\alpha<1
with

(Inc)

\phi(\theta)-\phi(\theta_{0})>\epsilon|\theta-\theta_{0}|^{\alpha}

for every
\theta\in U
.
If
\Sigma
is a convex entire spacelike surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
with null support function
\phi
and curvature bounded above by a negative constant, then
\Sigma
is incomplete at
\theta_{0}
.

7.1.
Outline of the strategy

Before entering the details of the proof of Theorem
C
, we remark that an easier incompleteness criterion can be immediately deduced from Corollary
G
. That is, we prove the following statement.

Corollary 7.1
.

Suppose
\phi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
is a lower semicontinuous function, finite on at least three points. Suppose that there exist
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
at which
\phi
has a
two-sided jump
: that is,
\phi(\theta_{0})<+\infty
and there are a neighborhood
U
of
\theta_{0}
and a constant
\epsilon>0
such that

(30)

\phi(\theta)-\phi(\theta_{0})>\epsilon

for every
\theta\in U
. If
\Sigma
is a convex entire spacelike surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
with null support function
\phi
and curvature bounded above by a negative constant, then
\Sigma
is incomplete at
\theta_{0}
.

Remark 7.2
.

Theorem
C
, which is the main goal of this section, is a much stronger result in this direction. In fact, it infers incompleteness of
\Sigma
under the condition (

), which is much weaker than (
30

As a warm-up towards Theorem

, let us prove Corollary
7.1
.

Proof of Corollary
7.1
.

We can assume (up to an isometry and a rescaling) that
\theta_{0}=\pi
,
\phi(\theta_{0})=-\epsilon
(which implies
\phi>0
in a punctured neighbourhood of
\pi
) and that the surface
\Sigma
has curvature bounded above by
-1
. The strategy of the proof consists in applying Corollary
G
to
\Sigma_{-}=\Sigma
and to
\Sigma_{+}
a parabolic-invariant entire surface of curvature
-1
as in Example
2.30
, which is incomplete at
\pi
, and has null support function
\phi_{+}
equal to
-\epsilon
at
\pi
and identically zero elsewhere. Hence in particular, we have
\phi_{+}(\pi)=\phi(\pi)=-\epsilon
and
\phi_{+}\leq\phi
in a neighbourhood of

\psi_{+}
of
\Sigma_{+}
is also identically zero.

We are however not yet in the conditions to apply Corollary

, since for that we need the inequality
\phi_{+}\leq\phi
to hold globally on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. This is easily fixed by replacing
\Sigma_{+}
with a translate of
\Sigma_{+}
in the direction
\vec{\pi}
. Indeed, the parabolic null support function
\psi
of
\Sigma
is lower semi-continuous and positive near infinity, so it is bounded below by some constant
-2c
. By Equations (
5
) (with

by the vector

adds the constant

everywhere. So, we apply corollary

to

and

and conclude that
\Sigma
is incomplete at

.
∎

To achieve Theorem
C
, we will apply a very similar strategy. However, we construct a more complicated surface to serve as a “barrier”

, a role that in the proof of Corollary

above is played by the surface of Example

. Let us outline the strategy that we will implement.

The idea of our proof is to construct, for any choice of constants
\epsilon,\alpha
and

, an explicit entire surface

, which:

(1)

is spacelike

(2)

has curvature between
-C
and 0,

(3)

is incomplete at
\theta_{0}
, and

(4)

has elliptic null support function

satisfying

(a)

\phi_{+}(\pi)=0

(b)

\phi_{+}(\theta)-\phi_{+}(\theta_{0})\leq\epsilon|\theta-\theta_{0}|^{\alpha}
on some neighborhood of

(c)

\phi_{+}
is bounded above.

and to invoke the comparison principle Corollary

.
Indeed, as long as

satisfies these four conditions, then if

is any surface with null support function

satisfying the inequality (

) on some neighborhood of

, then a suitable translate of

will have null support function less than

and will still be incomplete at

(as in the proof of Corollary

that

is incomplete.

This approach will require a few preliminary steps, which are proved in Subsection

,
in order to reformulate the conditions on

into conditions on its parabolic support function. For the impatient reader, we point out that the explicit expression for the parabolic support function

7.2.
Preliminary steps

In this section we will establish some standard relationships between geometric properties of a surface and its parabolic support functions.
In particular we will explicitly compute the curvature of a surface at the pre-image of the Gauss map in terms of the parabolic support function under some natural regularity assumptions.
Most of the content of this section is known in the literature (see for instance
[
BF17
]
),
but we give a self contained exposition since the computations in terms of the parabolic support function
are less common.

Recall from Corollary
2.28
that a convex entire achronal surface is associated to a proper closed convex function
u
on the upper half-space.
The first lemma of this section gives a sufficient condition in terms of
u
for the corresponding surface
\Sigma_{+}
to be acausal instead of merely achronal. For the surface obtained from our specific function
u
defined in (
44
), we will then apply Corollary
2.9
to ensure that
\Sigma_{+}
is spacelike, which is the first condition in the list of Section
7.1
.

To simplify the notation, we will associate to a proper closed function
u:\overline{\mathcal{H}}\cup\{\infty\}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
, which is convex with respect to the affine structure of the plane
x+z=2
(see Corollary
2.28
for this definition), an “extension”
\overline{u}:\overline{\mathcal{H}}\cup\{\infty\}
to the one-point compactification
\overline{\mathcal{H}}\cup\{\infty\}
of
\overline{\mathcal{H}}
, defined by:

(31)

\overline{u}(\infty)=\liminf_{\mathsf{x}^{2}+\mathsf{y}^{2}\to\infty}\frac{u(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})}{1+\mathsf{x}^{2}+\mathsf{y}^{2}}~.

Remark 7.3
.

The definition of
\overline{u}(\infty)
in (
31
) is of course motivated by Proposition
2.27
(see (
11
)): if
u=s_{\mathrm{par}}
is the parabolic support function of a convex entire achronal surface
\Sigma
, then
\overline{u}(\infty)
equals the value of the (elliptic) null support function of
\Sigma
at
\pi
. We remark that in the remainder of this section we will assume (up to applying an isometry of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
) that
\theta_{0}=\pi
and
\phi(\theta_{0})=0
in the statement of Theorem
C
; hence we will be particularly interested in the behaviour of surfaces
\Sigma
in the direction
\pi
in relation with the null support function around
\pi
.

Lemma 7.4
.

Let
\Sigma
be a convex entire achronal surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
and let

u=s_{\mathrm{par}}:\overline{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}

be its parabolic support function. Suppose that for every function
f
of the form

(32)

f(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})=a+b\mathsf{x}+c(\mathsf{x}^{2}+\mathsf{y}^{2})

such that
f\leq u
on
\overline{\mathcal{H}}
, it holds that
\overline{f}<\overline{u}
on
{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
. Then
\Sigma
is acausal.

Proof.

Suppose
\Sigma
were not acausal. Then we claim that it is touched by a null support plane at some point
q
. Indeed, it contains points
p
and
q
such that the segment from
p
to
q
is lightlike. Notice that
p
is contained in the past of every spacelike plane through
q
, so
\Sigma
can have no spacelike support plane containing
q
. Therefore the support plane through
q
must be lightlike (and indeed its tangent space is the orthogonal to the vector
q-p
).

Let
f
be the parabolic support function of the point
q
(equivalently, of its future null cone). From Equation (
5
) and (
7
),
f
is a quadratic polynomial of the form (
32
) for some
a,b,c\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Since
\Sigma
is achronal, the future null cone of
q
lies in its (causal) future so the elliptic support function of
q
is less than or equal to the elliptic support function of
\Sigma
. But, since both are supported by the null plane
P
, their support functions are equal in the null direction corresponding to
P
. Translated into the parabolic support functions using Proposition
2.27
, this says that
f\leq u
on
\overline{\mathcal{H}}
but
f=u
at the point of
{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
corresponding to
P
.
∎

Following the list in Section
7.1
, we then need to be able to show that
\Sigma_{+}
is
C^{2}
and to verify property (2) of the surface
\Sigma_{+}
, namely that its curvature is bounded below. In order to do this, we will need to parametrize
\Sigma_{+}
by the inverse of its Gauss map. More precisely, under a mild regularity assumption on the support function,
we will point out a continuous map
\Gamma:\mathcal{H}\to\Sigma
which has the property that the plane though
\Gamma(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})
orthogonal to
\zeta(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})
is a support plane for
\Sigma
. (Recall that the map

has been introduced in (

Lemma 7.5
.

Let
\Sigma
be a convex entire acausal surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
and let

u=s_{\mathrm{par}}:\overline{\mathcal{H}}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}

be its parabolic support function.
Assume that
u
is

in a neighbourhood

of a point
(\mathsf{x}_{0},\mathsf{y}_{0})\in\mathcal{H}
. Then

(1)

There exists a unique map
\Gamma:U\to\mathbb{R}^{2,1}
which satisfies the following equations:

(33)

\displaystyle\langle\Gamma,\zeta\rangle

\displaystyle=u

(34)

\displaystyle\langle\Gamma,\zeta_{\mathsf{x}}\rangle

\displaystyle=u_{\mathsf{x}}

(35)

\displaystyle\langle\Gamma,\zeta_{\mathsf{y}}\rangle

\displaystyle=u_{\mathsf{y}}

(2)

\Gamma
is continuous and
\Gamma(\mathsf{x}_{0},\mathsf{y}_{0})
is a point of
\Sigma
and the affine plane through
\Gamma(\mathsf{x}_{0},\mathsf{y}_{0})

\zeta(\mathsf{x}_{0},\mathsf{y}_{0})

\Sigma
only
at
\Gamma(\mathsf{x}_{0},\mathsf{y}_{0})
.

(3)

If moreover

\Gamma
is a homeomorphism onto an open subset
V
of
\Sigma
.
In this case

is
C^{1}
and spacelike on

, and

(36)

G\circ\Gamma=\bar{\zeta}

where

is the Gauss map and
\bar{\zeta}:=\frac{\zeta}{|\zeta|}

Proof.

The fact that
\Gamma
is well-defined and continuous follows from the observation that
\zeta(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y}),\zeta_{\mathsf{x}}(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y}),\zeta_{\mathsf{y}}(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})
form a basis of
\mathbb{R}^{2,1}
for every

Let
\Gamma_{0}=\Gamma(\mathsf{x}_{0},\mathsf{y}_{0})
and consider the function
F:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

F(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})=u(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})-\langle\Gamma_{0},\zeta(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})\rangle~,

Notice that
F\circ\zeta^{-1}
is convex and
F
vanishes at
(\mathsf{x}_{0},\mathsf{y}_{0})
by (
33
). Moreover by (
34
) and (
35
),
F
has a critical point at

(\mathsf{x}_{0},\mathsf{y}_{0})
. It follows that
F(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})\geq 0
for all
(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})\in\mathcal{H}
, that is
\langle\Gamma_{0},\zeta(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})\rangle\leq u(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})
, with equality at
\zeta(\mathsf{x}_{0},\mathsf{y}_{0})
. Now, recall from Proposition
2.17
and Corollary
2.28
that the entire surface
\Sigma
is entirely determined by its parabolic support function
u
: more precisely,
\Sigma
is the boundary of the convex domain in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
consisting of the points
p
such that
\langle p,\zeta(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})\rangle\leq u(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})
for all
(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})\in\mathcal{H}
. Hence
\Gamma_{0}\in\Sigma
.

Let now
p\in\mathbb{R}^{2,1}
be a point in the affine plane defined by
u(\mathsf{x}_{0},\mathsf{y}_{0})=\langle p,\zeta(\mathsf{x}_{0},\mathsf{y}_{0})\rangle
, with
p\neq\Gamma_{0}
.
Then at least one among (
34
) and (
35
) does not hold so there is a vector
W\in T_{(\mathsf{x}_{0},\mathsf{y}_{0})}\mathcal{H}
such that

du(W)<\langle p,d\zeta(W)\rangle

and this implies that there is some point
(\mathsf{x}_{1},\mathsf{y}_{1})\in\mathcal{H}
such that

u(\mathsf{x}_{1},\mathsf{y}_{1})<\langle p,\zeta(\mathsf{x}_{1},\mathsf{y}_{1})\rangle

showing that
p\notin\Sigma
.

Assume now that
u\circ\zeta^{-1}
is strictly convex on
U
. Hence the function
F\circ\zeta^{-1}
is strictly convex, since the difference between
u\circ\zeta^{-1}
and
F\circ\zeta^{-1}
is an affine function. We have already observed that
F(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})\geq 0
and
F(\mathsf{x}_{0},\mathsf{y}_{0})=0
. Hence strict convexity of

implies that the zero set of

only contains
(\mathsf{x}_{0},\mathsf{y}_{0})
.
Since the zero set of

equals the set of
(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})\in\mathcal{H}
such that
\zeta(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})
is orthogonal to a support plane through
\Gamma_{0}
, this proves that
\Gamma
is an injective function, and by the Invariance of domain Theorem,

is an open subset of
\Sigma
.
Moreover each point in
V
admits only one support plane, hence the surface
\Sigma
is

on
\Gamma(U)
.
Since those support planes are spacelike,

is a spacelike surface.
Finally using that the plane through
\Gamma(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})
orthogonal to
\zeta(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})
is a support plane for

we conclude that

.
∎

Now for a

Let

, then

Consider for every

In order to relate a higher regularity of the support function to a higher regularity of the surface

, the following standard observation will be useful.

Lemma 7.7
.

Let
\Sigma
be a spacelike surface in
\mathbb{R}^{2,1}

furnishes a

\begin{split}\langle\Gamma_{\mathsf{x}},\zeta_{\mathsf{x}}\rangle+\langle\Gamma,\zeta_{{\mathsf{x}}{\mathsf{x}}}\rangle&=u_{{\mathsf{x}}{\mathsf{x}}}\\
\langle\Gamma_{\mathsf{x}},\zeta_{\mathsf{y}}\rangle+\langle\Gamma,\zeta_{{\mathsf{x}}{\mathsf{y}}}\rangle&=u_{{\mathsf{x}}{\mathsf{y}}}\\
\langle\Gamma_{\mathsf{y}},\zeta_{\mathsf{x}}\rangle+\langle\Gamma,\zeta_{{\mathsf{y}}{\mathsf{x}}}\rangle&=u_{{\mathsf{y}}{\mathsf{x}}}\\
\langle\Gamma_{\mathsf{y}},\zeta_{\mathsf{y}}\rangle+\langle\Gamma,\zeta_{{\mathsf{y}}{\mathsf{y}}}\rangle&=u_{{\mathsf{y}}{\mathsf{y}}}\\
\end{split}

Verifying from the definition of
\zeta
that
\zeta_{{\mathsf{x}}{\mathsf{y}}}=0
and
\zeta_{\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}}=\zeta_{{\mathsf{y}}{\mathsf{y}}}=\zeta_{\mathsf{y}}/{\mathsf{y}}
and using (

(40)

\begin{split}\begin{bmatrix}\langle\Gamma_{\mathsf{x}},\zeta_{\mathsf{x}}\rangle&\langle\Gamma_{\mathsf{x}},\zeta_{\mathsf{y}}\rangle\\
\langle\Gamma_{\mathsf{y}},\zeta_{\mathsf{x}}\rangle&\langle\Gamma_{\mathsf{y}},\zeta_{\mathsf{y}}\rangle\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}u_{{\mathsf{x}}{\mathsf{x}}}-u_{\mathsf{y}}/{\mathsf{y}}&u_{{\mathsf{x}}{\mathsf{y}}}\\
u_{{\mathsf{y}}{\mathsf{x}}}&u_{{\mathsf{y}}{\mathsf{y}}}-u_{\mathsf{y}}/{\mathsf{y}}\end{bmatrix}=\frac{2}{\mathsf{y}}A=\frac{4}{|\zeta|}A\end{split}

Now we use that
\bar{\zeta}_{\mathsf{x}}\in\frac{\zeta_{\mathsf{x}}}{|\zeta|}+\mathrm{Span}(\zeta)
,
\bar{\zeta}_{\mathsf{y}}\in\frac{\zeta_{\mathsf{y}}}{|\zeta|}+\mathrm{Span}(\zeta)
, and that
\langle\Gamma_{x},\zeta\rangle=\langle\Gamma_{y},\zeta\rangle=0
(which is obtained by combining (
33
), (
34
) and (
35
)), to deduce that:

(41)

\begin{split}\begin{bmatrix}\langle\Gamma_{\mathsf{x}},\bar{\zeta}_{\mathsf{x}}\rangle&\langle\Gamma_{\mathsf{x}},\bar{\zeta}_{\mathsf{y}}\rangle\\
\langle\Gamma_{\mathsf{y}},\bar{\zeta}_{\mathsf{x}}\rangle&\langle\Gamma_{\mathsf{y}},\bar{\zeta}_{\mathsf{y}}\rangle\end{bmatrix}=\frac{4}{|\zeta|^{2}}A\end{split}

Since
\bar{\zeta}_{\mathsf{x}},\bar{\zeta}_{\mathsf{y}}
is an orthogonal basis of
T_{\bar{\zeta}}\mathbb{H}^{2}
and
||\bar{\zeta}_{\mathsf{x}}||^{2}=||\bar{\zeta}_{\mathsf{y}}||^{2}=4/|\zeta|^{2}
,
the coordinates of a vector of
T_{\bar{\zeta}}\mathbb{H}^{2}
with respect to this basis are given by the scalar product with the elements of the basis multiplied by
|\zeta|^{2}/4
.
Since
d\Gamma\circ(d\bar{\zeta})^{-1}
sends
\bar{\zeta}_{\mathsf{x}}
to
\Gamma_{\mathsf{x}}
and
\bar{\zeta}_{\mathsf{y}}
to
\Gamma_{\mathsf{y}}
, from (
41
)
we see that its representative matrix is given by
A
.

A consequence of this computation is that the differential of
\Gamma
is injective under the hypothesis that
A
is invertible, so
\Gamma
realizes a
C^{1}
-diffeomorphism onto its image.
Since
G\circ\Gamma=\bar{\zeta}
we see that

, so that the restriction of
G
to
\Gamma(U)
is
C^{1}
. We deduce that
\Gamma(U)
is
C^{2}
by Lemma
7.7
.

Finally using that
dG=d\bar{\zeta}\circ(d\Gamma)^{-1}=(d\Gamma\circ(d\bar{\zeta})^{-1})^{-1}
we conclude that the representative matrix of

with respect to the basis
\bar{\zeta}_{\mathsf{x}},\bar{\zeta}_{\mathsf{y}}
is

A^{-1}
.
By the Gauss equation
K(\Gamma(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y}))=\det A^{-1}
and the result follows.
∎

Before finally giving the formula for our function

Lemma 7.9
.

Suppose that
u:\mathcal{H}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is the parabolic support function of a convex entire spacelike surface
\Sigma
, and that
u(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})=u(-\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})
. Suppose furthermore that the restriction of
u
to the
\mathsf{y}
axis is
C^{2}
and strictly convex, and satisfies

\int_{1}^{\infty}\left(u_{\mathsf{y}\mathsf{y}}-\frac{u_{\mathsf{y}}}{\mathsf{y}}\right)d\mathsf{y}<\infty~.

Then
\Sigma
is incomplete at
\theta_{0}=\pi
and

\overline{u}(\infty)=\sup_{(0,\mathsf{y})\in\mathcal{H}}\frac{u_{\mathsf{y}}(0,\mathsf{y})}{2\mathsf{y}}~.

Proof.

Because of the bijection established in Corollary
2.28
,
\Sigma
also has the corresponding reflection symmetry, across the timelike plane
y=0
spanned by the vectors
\vec{0}
and
\vec{\pi}
. Since
\Sigma
is convex, it follows that its null support planes in these directions contains the null support lines of its intersection with this timelike plane. Since
u
is
C^{2}
and strictly convex on the
\mathsf{y}
axis, we can parametrize the intersection of
\Sigma
with this plane by
\Gamma(0,\mathsf{y})
defined by equations
(\ref{eqn: def v 1},~\ref{eqn: def v 2},~\ref{eqn: def v 3})
. Then since
\overline{u}(\infty)
is the value of the elliptic support function of
\Sigma
at
{{\theta_{0}}=}{\pi}
by Proposition
2.27
and since
\vec{\pi}=\zeta_{\mathsf{y}}/2\mathsf{y}
, we have

(42)

\begin{split}\overline{u}(\infty)&=\sup_{\mathsf{y}}\langle\Gamma(0,\mathsf{y}),\vec{\pi}\rangle\\
&=\sup_{\mathsf{y}}\langle\Gamma(0,\mathsf{y}),\frac{\zeta_{\mathsf{y}}}{2\mathsf{y}}\rangle\\
&=\sup_{\mathsf{y}}\frac{u_{\mathsf{y}}(0,\mathsf{y})}{2\mathsf{y}}~.\end{split}

For the incompleteness part of the lemma, it suffices to show that the length of
\Gamma(0,\mathsf{y})
is finite as
\mathsf{y}
approaches infinity, since we have already argued that this curve approaches the null support plane of
\Sigma
in the direction
\vec{\pi}
. By the reflection symmetry,
\Gamma_{\mathsf{y}}
is a multiple of
\overline{\zeta}_{\mathsf{y}}
, so its length is
\langle\Gamma_{\mathsf{y}},\overline{\zeta}_{\mathsf{y}}\rangle/|\overline{\zeta}_{\mathsf{y}}|
. From equation (
41
), which clearly remains true in the
\mathsf{y}
direction even though
u
is not differentiable in the
\mathsf{x}
direction, this is equal to

\frac{1}{2}\left(u_{\mathsf{yy}}-\frac{u_{\mathsf{y}}}{\mathsf{y}}\right).

Its integral is the length of the curve
\Gamma(0,\mathsf{y})
, so if the integral from one to infinity is finite, then the surface
\Sigma
is incomplete at
\pi
.
∎

Lemma 7.10
.

Let

7.3.
The explicit surface
\Sigma_{+}

We are now ready to present the formula for the parabolic support function

\Sigma_{+}
. For constants

u(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})=-M\mathsf{y}^{\beta}+\epsilon|\mathsf{x}|^{2-\alpha}(1+\mathsf{y})^{-\gamma}~.

Clearly
u(\mathsf{x},0)
satisfies (
43

is symmetric about the
\mathsf{y}
axis and its restriction to the

and strictly convex, we can use Lemma
7.9
to check incompleteness and the value of
\overline{u}(\infty)
, once we know that

is convex. We also remark that

is, inconveniently, not
C^{2}
on the
\mathsf{y}
-axis.

Let
F
be the operator

(45)

\begin{split}F(u)=-4K^{-1}=(\mathsf{y}u_{\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}}-u_{\mathsf{y}})(\mathsf{y}u_{\mathsf{y}\mathsf{y}}-u_{\mathsf{y}})-\mathsf{y}^{2}u^{2}_{\mathsf{x}\mathsf{y}}\end{split}

Lemma 7.11
.

Let
u
be the function defined in (
44
), and
F
the operator defined in (
45
).

•

If
\gamma<1-\alpha
, then
F(u)>0
for all points
(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})
with
\mathsf{x}\neq 0
.

•

If
\gamma\leq\beta(1-\alpha)
, then for any
C>0
there exists
M>0
such that
F(u)\geq 4/C
for all points
(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})
with
\mathsf{x}\neq 0
.

Proof.

Let us compute

(46)

u_{\mathsf{x}}=\epsilon(2-\alpha)\mathsf{x}|\mathsf{x}|^{-\alpha}(1+\mathsf{y})^{-\gamma}\qquad u_{\mathsf{y}}=-M\beta\mathsf{y}^{-1+\beta}-\epsilon\gamma|\mathsf{x}|^{2-\alpha}(1+\mathsf{y})^{-1-\gamma}

and

(47)

\begin{split}u_{\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}}&=\epsilon(2-\alpha)(1-\alpha)|\mathsf{x}|^{-\alpha}(1+\mathsf{y})^{-\gamma}\\
u_{\mathsf{x}\mathsf{y}}&=-\epsilon(2-\alpha)\gamma\mathsf{x}|\mathsf{x}|^{-\alpha}(1+\mathsf{y})^{-1-\gamma}\\
u_{\mathsf{y}\mathsf{y}}&=M\beta(1-\beta)\mathsf{y}^{-2+\beta}+\epsilon\gamma(1+\gamma)|\mathsf{x}|^{2-\alpha}(1+\mathsf{y})^{-2-\gamma}~.\end{split}

Hence we have:

\begin{split}F(u)=&(\mathsf{y}{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}u_{\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}}}-{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}u_{\mathsf{y}}})(\mathsf{y}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}u_{\mathsf{y}\mathsf{y}}}-{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}u_{\mathsf{y}}})-\mathsf{y}^{2}{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}u_{\mathsf{x}\mathsf{y}}}^{2}\\
=&\left(\mathsf{y}{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\epsilon(2-\alpha)(1-\alpha)|\mathsf{x}|^{-\alpha}(1+\mathsf{y})^{-\gamma}}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}M\beta\mathsf{y}^{-1+\beta}+\epsilon\gamma|\mathsf{x}|^{2-\alpha}(1+\mathsf{y})^{-1-\gamma}}\right)\cdot\\
&\left(\mathsf{y}({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}M\beta(1-\beta)\mathsf{y}^{-2+\beta}+\epsilon\gamma(1+\gamma)|\mathsf{x}|^{2-\alpha}(1+\mathsf{y})^{-2-\gamma}})+\right.\\
&\qquad\left.{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}M\beta\mathsf{y}^{-1+\beta}+\epsilon\gamma|\mathsf{x}|^{2-\alpha}(\mathsf{y}+1)^{-1-\gamma}}\right)\\
&-\mathsf{y}^{2}\left({\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}-\epsilon(2-\alpha)\gamma\mathsf{x}|\mathsf{x}|^{-\alpha}(1+\mathsf{y})^{-1-\gamma}}\right)^{2}\end{split}

We drop the final term in each of the two parenthetical expressions and combine two equivalent terms in the second, and recolor:

\begin{split}F(u)\geq&\left({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathsf{y}\epsilon(2-\alpha)(1-\alpha)|\mathsf{x}|^{-\alpha}(\mathsf{y}+1)^{-\gamma}}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}M\beta\mathsf{y}^{-1+\beta}}\right)\cdot\\
&\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}M\beta(2-\beta)\mathsf{y}^{-1+\beta}}+{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathsf{y}\epsilon\gamma(1+\gamma)|\mathsf{x}|^{2-\alpha}(1+\mathsf{y})^{-2-\gamma}}\right)\\
&-{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathsf{y}^{2}\left(\epsilon(2-\alpha)\gamma|\mathsf{x}|^{1-\alpha}(1+\mathsf{y})^{-1-\gamma}\right)^{2}}\end{split}

and now expand, dropping one of the four terms of the product:

\begin{split}F(u)\geq&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}((1-\alpha)(1+\gamma)-(2-\alpha)\gamma)\left(\mathsf{y}^{2}\epsilon^{2}(2-\alpha)\gamma|\mathsf{x}|^{2-2\alpha}(1+\mathsf{y})^{-2-2\gamma}\right)}\\
&+{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\epsilon(2-\alpha)(1-\alpha)M\beta(2-\beta)|\mathsf{x}|^{-\alpha}(1+\mathsf{y})^{-\gamma}\mathsf{y}^{\beta}}\\
&+{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}M^{2}\beta^{2}(2-\beta)\mathsf{y}^{-2+2\beta}}\end{split}

As long as
\gamma<1-\alpha
, the first term is positive, and hence
F(u)
is strictly positive. Assuming this, we now try to refine our choice of constants so that
F(u)\geq 4/C
. First of all, if
\mathsf{y}\leq 1
, the last term is at least
M^{2}\beta^{2}
, so for any
\beta
we can choose
M
to make this at least
4/C
. Hence, so long as we are free to choose
M
sufficiently large, we need only bound
F(u)
from below under the assumption that
\mathsf{y}\geq 1
. In this case,
(1+\mathsf{y})^{-1}\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{y}^{-1}
, and so for some
\delta(\epsilon,\alpha,\gamma)
small we have

\begin{split}F(u)\geq&\delta\left(\frac{\alpha}{2-\alpha}|\mathsf{x}|^{2-2\alpha}\mathsf{y}^{-2\gamma}+\frac{2-2\alpha}{2-\alpha}M\beta|\mathsf{x}|^{-\alpha}\mathsf{y}^{\beta-\gamma}\right)\\
\geq&\delta\mathsf{y}^{(-2\gamma\alpha+2(1-\alpha)(\beta-\gamma))/(2-\alpha)}(M\beta)^{(2-2\alpha)/(2-\alpha)}\end{split}

where we have used the weighted arithmetic-geometric inequality
\lambda a+(1-\lambda)b\geq a^{\lambda}b^{1-\lambda}
in the last line. If we choose
\gamma
such that

-2\gamma\alpha+2(1-\alpha)(\beta-\gamma)\geq 0

then for

sufficiently large,

for all
\mathsf{y}\geq 1
. A little algebra shows that it suffices to take
\gamma\leq\beta(1-\alpha)
.
∎

away from its intersection with the
y=0
plane, and thus it is greater than or equal to
-C
everywhere by continuity.
∎

7.4.
Proof of Theorem
C

At this point, we have assembled all the necessary components for a proof of the main theorem of this section, and so we end by simply putting them in order.

Proof of Theorem
C
.

As already observed at the beginning of Section
7.1
(Remark
7.3
), we can assume
\theta_{0}=\pi
and
\phi(\pi)=0
.
Let
\Sigma_{+}
be the surface whose existence is given by Lemma
7.15
, where
\gamma\leq\beta(1-\alpha)
and the constant
M
is chosen as in the second point of Lemma
7.11
.
From (
46
) and (
47
) we find
u_{\mathsf{y}\mathsf{y}}(0,\mathsf{y})-u_{\mathsf{y}}(0,\mathsf{y})/\mathsf{y}=M\beta(2-\beta)\mathsf{y}^{-2+\beta}
; moreover we have
u_{\mathsf{y}}(0,\mathsf{y})/2\mathsf{y}=-M\beta\mathsf{y}^{-2+\beta}/2
. Hence Lemma
7.9
shows that
\Sigma_{+}
is incomplete at
\pi
and its elliptic null support function
\phi_{+}
has value 0 at
\pi
. Moreover, since its parabolic null support function is

\psi_{+}(\mathsf{x})=\epsilon|\mathsf{x}|^{2-\alpha}~,

by Lemma
7.10
and the condition (
Inc
), we have that
\phi_{+}(\theta)\leq\epsilon|\theta-\pi|^{\alpha}<\phi(\theta)
for
\theta
in a neighbourhood
U
of
\pi
. In addition, we have
\phi_{+}(\pi)=\phi(\pi)=0
.

Analogously to the proof of Corollary
7.1
, we remark that we are not yet in the hypothesis of Corollary
G
, since we need the inequality
\phi_{+}\leq\phi
to hold globally on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. This issue is solved by replacing
\Sigma_{+}
with a translate in the direction of
\vec{\pi}
, which has the effect of changing its parabolic null support function

by adding the constant
-2c
and leaving the value of the elliptic null support function

at

\psi_{+}

∎

8.
Incompleteness II: one-sided superlogarithmic condition

The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
D
, which we state here as usual in a slightly stronger version with respect to the introduction, using directional incompleteness.

Theorem
D

(One-sided superlogarithmic condition – local version)
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
be lower semicontinuous and finite on at least three points. Suppose
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
is such that
\phi(\theta_{0})<+\infty
and there exist a neighborhood
U
of
\theta_{0}
and a constant
\epsilon>0
such that

(Inc’)

\begin{cases}\phi(\theta)=+\infty&\text{ if }\theta\text{ is on one side of }\theta_{0}\\
\phi(\theta)\geq\phi(\theta_{0})+\epsilon|(\theta-\theta_{0})\log|\theta-\theta_{0}||&\text{ if }\theta\text{ is on the other one side of }\theta_{0}.\end{cases}

for every
\theta\in U
.

If
\Sigma
is a convex entire spacelike surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
with null support function
\phi
and curvature bounded above by a negative constant, then
\Sigma
is incomplete at
\theta_{0}
.

To prove the incompleteness criterion given in Theorem
D
,
we will construct an incomplete entire hyperbolic surface
\Sigma_{\lambda}
(depending on a real parameter
\lambda)
invariant under a one-parameter glide hyperbolic group, study its null support function, and apply the comparison principle (Corollary
G
).

8.1.
The invariant surface
\Sigma_{\lambda}

Let us fix
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
, and consider the one-parameter group
A^{\lambda}=\{A^{\lambda}_{s}\}_{s\in\mathbb{R}}
of affine isometries of Minkowski space defined by

A^{\lambda}_{s}\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\\
x_{3}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}+\lambda s\\
\cosh(s)x_{2}+\sinh(s)x_{3}\\
\sinh(s)x_{2}+\cosh(s)x_{3}\end{bmatrix}~.

Let us define the map
X^{\lambda}:(0,+\infty)\times{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
given by

X^{\lambda}(t,s)=\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}(t-\coth(t))+\lambda s\\
\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}{\sinh(s)}/{\sinh(t)}\\
\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}{\cosh(s)}/{\sinh(t)}\end{bmatrix}

We have that

A^{\lambda}_{s_{0}}X^{\lambda}(t,s)=X^{\lambda}(t,s+s_{0})~.

Remark 8.1
.

When
\lambda=0
in the above expression, we recover the embedding
X=X^{0}
of the semitrough that we described in Example
2.29
. We have already observed there that the semitrough is complete. We will see that
X^{\lambda}
is a proper spacelike immersion, whose image
\Sigma_{\lambda}
is an entire spacelike surface of constant curvature
-1
which is complete if and only if
\lambda=0
. The incompleteness of
\Sigma_{\lambda}
for
\lambda\neq 0
is the fundamental property that we will apply to prove Theorem
D
.

Remark 8.2
.

Observe that
\Sigma_{-\lambda}=R(\Sigma_{\lambda})
, where
R=\mathrm{diag}(1,-1,1)
is the reflection in the plane
y=0
. Indeed, we immediately see from the definition that
X^{-\lambda}(t,s)=R\circ X^{\lambda}(t,-s)
.

Lemma 8.3
.

For any
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the image of
X^{\lambda}
is an entire spacelike surface.

Proof.

An explicit computation shows that

\partial_{t}X^{\lambda}(t,s)=\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}\frac{\cosh(t)}{\sinh^{2}(t)}\begin{bmatrix}\cosh(t)\\
-\sinh(s)\\
-\cosh(s)\end{bmatrix}\qquad\partial_{s}X^{\lambda}(t,s)=\frac{1}{\sinh(t)}\begin{bmatrix}\lambda\sinh(t)\\
\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}\cosh(s)\\
\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}\sinh(s)\end{bmatrix}

so that the coefficients of the pull-back of the Minkowski product are

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=\langle\partial_{t}X^{\lambda},\partial_{t}X^{\lambda}\rangle=(1+\lambda^{2})\coth^{2}(t)

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=\langle\partial_{t}X^{\lambda},\partial_{s}X^{\lambda}\rangle=\lambda\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}\coth^{2}(t)

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=\langle\partial_{s}X^{\lambda},\partial_{s}X^{\lambda}\rangle=\lambda^{2}\coth^{2}(t)+\frac{1}{\sinh^{2}(t)}\,.

Hence we get

(49)

EG-F^{2}=(1+\lambda^{2})\frac{\coth^{2}(t)}{\sinh^{2}(t)}>0

which shows that the pull-back metric is Riemannian, and the map
X^{\lambda}
is a spacelike immersion.

Let us prove that
X^{\lambda}
is proper. By contradiction let us assume there exists a diverging sequence
(t_{n},s_{n})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
such that
X^{\lambda}(t_{n},s_{n})
is converging in
\mathbb{R}^{2,1}
.
First let us assume that
s_{n}\to+\infty
.
Since
X^{\lambda}(t_{n},s_{n})
stays in a compact region of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
, from boundedness of the second or third coordinate we deduce that
e^{s_{n}-t_{n}}
is bounded. That is, that there exists
C>0
such that
t_{n}>s_{n}-C
. Therefore
\coth(t_{n})
is bounded above by some constant
C^{\prime}
, and the first component of
X_{\lambda}(t_{n},s_{n})
is larger than
(\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}+\lambda)s_{n}-(C+C^{\prime})\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}
for
n
sufficiently large. Since this quantity tends to
+\infty
, we get a contradiction.

Similarly, if
s_{n}\to-\infty
, from the third component we obtain that
e^{-s_{n}-t_{n}}
is bounded, hence
t_{n}>-s_{n}-C
, and the third component would be larger than
(\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}-\lambda)(-s_{n})-(C+C^{\prime})\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}
, which is impossible.

Finally let us consider the case where
s_{n}
is bounded and
t_{n}
is diverging in
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.
If
t_{n}\to 0
then the third component of
X(t_{n},s_{n})
diverges.
On the other hand, if
t_{n}\to+\infty
then the first component diverges.

We have thus shown that
X^{\lambda}
is a proper spacelike immersion. By
[
BSS19
, Proposition 1.10]
,
X^{\lambda}
is a proper embedding, and its image is an entire spacelike surface.
This concludes the proof.
∎

Let us call
\Sigma_{\lambda}
the image of the embedding
X^{\lambda}
, which is an entire spacelike surface. We now show that it is hyperbolic:

Lemma 8.4
.

For any
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the surface
\Sigma_{\lambda}
has constant curvature
-1
.

Proof.

A direct computation shows that the future pointing unit normal vector of
\Sigma_{\lambda}
at
X^{\lambda}(t,s)
is

(50)

N(t,s)=\begin{bmatrix}{-\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}}/{\sinh(t)}\\
\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}\coth(t)\sinh(s)+\lambda\cosh(s)\\
\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}\coth(t)\cosh(s)+\lambda\sinh(s)\end{bmatrix}

On the other hand the Hessian of
X^{\lambda}
is the following
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
-valued symmetric form:

\begin{split}D^{2}X_{\lambda}&=(\partial_{tt}X_{\lambda})dt^{2}+2(\partial_{ts}X_{\lambda})dtds+(\partial_{ss}X_{\lambda})ds^{2}=\\
&=\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}\left(\begin{bmatrix}-2\frac{\coth(t)}{\sinh^{2}(t)}\\
\frac{1+\cosh^{2}(t)}{\sinh^{3}(t)}\sinh(s)\\
\frac{1+\cosh^{2}(t)}{\sinh^{3}(t)}\cosh(s)\end{bmatrix}dt^{2}-2\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\frac{\coth(t)}{\sinh(t)}\cosh(s)\\
\frac{\coth(t)}{\sinh(t)}\sinh(s)\end{bmatrix}dtds+\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\frac{1}{\sinh(t)}\sinh(s)\\
\frac{1}{\sinh(t)}\cosh(s)\end{bmatrix}ds^{2}\right)\end{split}

Recall that the second fundamental form is defined as
\mathrm{I}\hskip-1.13791pt\mathrm{I}=-\langle D^{2}X_{\lambda},N\rangle
. It follows that the coefficients of the second fundamental form are given by

\displaystyle e

\displaystyle=g=(1+\lambda^{2})\frac{\coth(t)}{\sinh(t)}

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle=\lambda\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}\frac{\coth(t)}{\sinh(t)}~.

We have

eg-f^{2}=\frac{\coth^{2}(t)}{\sinh^{2}(t)}[(1+\lambda^{2})^{2}-\lambda^{2}(1+\lambda^{2})]=(1+\lambda^{2})\frac{\coth^{2}(t)}{\sinh^{2}(t)}

By the Gauss equation and Equation (
49
) we have that the curvature of
\Sigma
is given by

K=-\frac{eg-f^{2}}{EG-F^{2}}=-1

as claimed.
∎

By Proposition
2.10
,
\Sigma_{\lambda}
is either convex or concave; the proof of Lemma
8.4
actually showed that the second fundamental form is positive definite, hence
\Sigma_{\lambda}
is convex (Remark
2.14
).

Lemma 8.5
.

The surface
\Sigma_{\lambda}
is incomplete at
\theta_{0}=\pi/2
if
\lambda<0
, and at
\theta_{0}=-\pi/2
if
\lambda>0
.

Proof.

By Remark
8.2
, it suffices to prove the statement for
\lambda>0
.
Let us consider the proper path
\gamma:[1,+\infty)\to\Sigma
given by

\gamma(\tau)=X^{\lambda}(\lambda\tau,-\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}\tau)

We claim that
\gamma
has finite length.
Indeed, a direct computation shows that

\dot{\gamma}(\tau)=\lambda\partial_{t}X^{\lambda}(\lambda\tau,-\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}\tau)-\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}\partial_{s}X_{\lambda}(\lambda\tau,-\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}\tau)~.

Using the coefficients of the first fundamental form that we derived in the proof of Lemma
8.3
, we have

\displaystyle||\dot{\gamma}(\tau)||^{2}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2}E(\lambda\tau,-\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}\tau)+(1+\lambda^{2})G(\lambda\tau,-\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}\tau)-2\lambda\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}F(\lambda\tau,-\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}\tau)

\displaystyle=2\lambda^{2}(1+\lambda^{2})\coth^{2}(\lambda\tau)+\frac{1+\lambda^{2}}{\sinh^{2}(\lambda\tau)}-2\lambda^{2}(1+\lambda^{2})\coth^{2}(\lambda\tau)=\frac{1+\lambda^{2}}{\sinh^{2}(\lambda\tau)}~.

This shows that

\int_{1}^{+\infty}||\dot{\gamma}(\tau)||d\tau<+\infty~.

It remains to show that the direction of incompleteness is
\theta_{0}=-\pi/2
. One sees directly from the definition of
X^{\lambda}
that the null planes
z=\pm y
are support planes for
\Sigma^{\lambda}
(for all
\lambda
). Moreover, we can check that

\langle\gamma(\tau),(0,-1,1)\rangle=-\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}\frac{e^{-\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}\tau}}{\sinh(\lambda\tau)}\to 0

as
\tau\to+\infty
. Hence we can apply
Lemma
3.12
, and conclude that for
\lambda>0
,
\Sigma_{\lambda}
is incomplete at
-\pi/2
.
∎

8.2.
Null support function

We will now compute the null support function of the surface
\Sigma_{\lambda}
. We will use again the parabolic null support function. However, since we have shown in Lemma
8.2
that
\Sigma_{\lambda}
is incomplete at
-\pi/2
, we will use a variation of the map
\zeta
defined in Equation (
5
), so as to make the point
\theta_{0}=-\pi/2
correspond to
\mathsf{x}_{0}=0
, and the point
\pi/2
correspond to the direction at infinity. Namely, we just compose the map
\zeta:\overline{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
with a rotation of angle
-\pi/2
. That is, let us define:

(51)

{\xi}(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y}):=\begin{bmatrix}2\mathsf{x}\\
-1+\mathsf{x}^{2}+\mathsf{y}^{2}\\
1+\mathsf{x}^{2}+\mathsf{y}^{2}\end{bmatrix}

We then define the parabolic null support function
with point at infinity
\pi/2
analogously to (
8
) with respect to
\xi
instead of
\zeta
, and we will denote it by
\varphi
hereafter:

(52)

\varphi(\mathsf{x}):=\sup_{{p}\in\Sigma}\langle{p},{\xi}(\mathsf{x},0)\rangle~.

Notice that
\xi(\mathsf{x},0)
parameterizes all the lightlike directions
\vec{\theta}
except
(0,1,1)
, which is however the projective limit of
{\xi}(\mathsf{x},0)
as
\mathsf{x}\to\infty
. Observe that with this convention,
\mathsf{x}=\tan(\theta+\pi/2)
.

We are interested in the parabolic null support function of
\Sigma_{\lambda}
near the point
\mathsf{x}_{0}=0
, which corresponds to
\theta_{0}=-\pi/2
and to
\xi(0,0)=(0,-1,1)
. The fundamental result we have to achieve is the following, which holds for any
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
, although we are mostly concerned with the case where
\Sigma_{\lambda}
is incomplete at
-\pi/2
, namely (by Lemma
8.2
) when
\lambda>0
.

Proposition 8.6
.

For any
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
, let
\varphi_{\lambda}
be the parabolic null support function of the surface
\Sigma_{\lambda}
, with point at infinity
\pi/2
. Then

\varphi_{\lambda}(\mathsf{x})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-2\lambda|\mathsf{x}|\log|\mathsf{x}|&\textrm{if }\mathsf{x}<0\\
0&\textrm{if }\mathsf{x}=0\\
+\infty&\textrm{if }\mathsf{x}>0.\end{array}\right.

Before proving Proposition
8.6
we notice that the invariance of
\Sigma^{\lambda}
under the action of the one-parameter group
A^{\lambda}=\{A^{\lambda}_{s}\}_{s\in\mathbb{R}}
gives a strong constraint on its null support function.

Lemma 8.7
.

Let
\varphi_{\lambda}
be the parabolic null support function of any convex entire achronal surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
invariant under the one-parameter group
A^{\lambda}
, with point at infinity
\pi/2
. Then for every
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
\mathsf{x}\in\mathbb{R}
the following identity holds:

\varphi_{\lambda}(e^{s}\mathsf{x})=e^{s}(\varphi_{\lambda}(\mathsf{x})+2\lambda s\mathsf{x})

Proof.

Let us call
\Sigma
the invariant surface. Denote by
L_{s}
and
\tau_{s}
the linear and the translational part of
A_{s}
respectively, namely

L_{s}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&\cosh(s)&\sinh(s)\\
0&\sinh(s)&\cosh(s)\end{bmatrix}~,\qquad\tau_{s}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda s\\
0\\
0\end{bmatrix}~.

By the invariance under the action of the group
A^{\lambda}
, we have

\displaystyle\varphi_{\lambda}(\mathsf{x})

\displaystyle=\sup_{p\in\Sigma}\langle\xi(\mathsf{x},0),p\rangle

\displaystyle=\sup_{p\in\Sigma}\langle\xi(\mathsf{x},0),A_{s}(p)\rangle

\displaystyle=\sup_{p\in\Sigma}\langle\xi(\mathsf{x},0),L_{s}(p)+\tau_{s}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sup_{p\in\Sigma}\langle\xi(\mathsf{x},0),L_{s}(p)\rangle+\langle\xi(\mathsf{x},0),\tau_{s}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sup_{p\in\Sigma}\langle L_{-s}(\xi(\mathsf{x},0)),p\rangle+2\lambda\mathsf{x}s

By a direct computation,

L_{-s}(\xi(\mathsf{x},0))=e^{s}\xi(e^{-s}\mathsf{x},0)~.

Hence we obtain

\varphi_{\lambda}(\mathsf{x})=e^{s}\varphi_{\lambda}(e^{-s}\mathsf{x})+2\lambda\mathsf{x}s\,

and the result follows.
∎

Proof of Proposition
8.6
.

We have already observed in the proof of Lemma that the null planes
z=\pm y
are support planes of
\Sigma_{\lambda}
. This implies that the elliptic null support function of
\Sigma^{\lambda}
at
\theta_{0}=\pm\pi/2
equals
0
. Comparing the elliptic and parabolic null support functions as in Proposition
2.27
, one has
\varphi_{\lambda}(0)=0
.

Moreover, from Equation (
50
), the image of the Gauss map is contained in the half plane defined by
x\geq 0
in the hyperboloid model of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, and therefore the elliptic support function takes the value
+\infty
on the half-disc
\{(x,y,1)\,|\,x\geq 0\}
.
By convexity, the elliptic null support function equals
+\infty
at every
\theta\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
, that is
\varphi_{\lambda}(\mathsf{x})=+\infty
for every
\mathsf{x}>0
.

Finally, we claim that
\varphi_{\lambda}(-1)=0
. From Lemma
8.7
, this will imply that

\varphi_{\lambda}(-e^{s})=-2\lambda se^{s}

and the statement will be proved.

By definition of
\varphi_{\lambda}
, since
\xi(-1,0)=(-2,0,2)
, we have

(53)

\varphi_{\lambda}(-1)=\sup_{p\in\Sigma_{\lambda}}\langle p,(-2,0,2)\rangle~.

As an additional preliminary remark, we observe that it suffices to show that
\varphi_{\lambda}(-1)=0
for
\lambda\geq 0
. Indeed, by Remark
8.2
,
\Sigma_{-\lambda}=R(\Sigma_{\lambda})
where
R
is the reflection in the plane
y=0
. Hence

\varphi_{-\lambda}(-1)=\sup_{p\in\Sigma_{\lambda}}\langle R(p),(-2,0,2)\rangle=\sup_{p\in\Sigma_{\lambda}}\langle p,(-2,0,2)\rangle=\varphi_{\lambda}(-1)

where we have used that
R
fixes
(-2,0,2)
.

We are now ready to provide the final computation. From (
53
) and the parameterization of
\Sigma_{\lambda}
, we have

\displaystyle\varphi_{\lambda}(-1)

\displaystyle=\sup_{(t,s)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}}\langle X^{\lambda}(t,s),(-2,0,2)\rangle

\displaystyle=2\sup_{(t,s)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}}\left(\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}\left(\coth(t)-\frac{\cosh(s)}{\sinh(t)}-t\right)-\lambda s\right)\,.

For every
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
let us consider the function
F_{t}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

F_{t}(s)=\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}\left(\coth(t)-\frac{\cosh(s)}{\sinh(t)}-t\right)-\lambda s~.

This function has a global maximum at the point
s_{\max}=s_{\max}(t)
such that

(54)

\sinh(s_{\max})=-({\lambda}/{\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}})\sinh(t)~.

The corresponding maximum value is

\displaystyle G(t):

\displaystyle=\sup_{s}F_{t}(s)=F(s_{\max}(t))

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}\left(\coth(t)-\frac{\cosh(s_{\max}(t))}{\sinh(t)}-t\right)-\lambda s_{\max}(t)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}(\coth(t)-t)+\lambda(\coth(s_{\max}(t))-s_{\max}(t))

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}g(t)+\lambda g(s_{\max}(t))~,

where we have set
g(t):=\coth(t)-t
. We will show that
G^{\prime}(t)<0
. For this, let us first observe that
g^{\prime}(t)=-\coth^{2}(t)
and, differentiating (
54
), we have the relation

\cosh(s_{\max}(t))s_{\max}^{\prime}(t)=-\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}}\cosh(t)~.

Using these two relations, we obtain:

\displaystyle G^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}\frac{\cosh^{2}(t)}{\sinh^{2}(t)}-\lambda\frac{\cosh^{2}(s_{\max}(t))}{\sinh^{2}(s_{\max}(t))}(s_{\max}^{\prime}(t))

\displaystyle={\cosh(t)}\left(-{\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}}\frac{\cosh(t)}{\sinh^{2}(t)}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{{\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}}}\frac{\cosh(s_{\max}(t))}{\sinh^{2}(s_{\max}(t))}\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}\frac{\cosh(t)}{\sinh^{2}(t)}(-\cosh(t)+\cosh(s_{\max}(t)))<0

where in the last inequality we have used that, from (
54
),
|s_{\max}(t)|<t
.

This shows that
\psi_{\lambda}(-1)=2\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}G(t)
. To compute this limit, observe that, by (
54
),

s_{\max}(t)=-\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}}t+O(t^{2})~.

From the expression of
G(t)
, we then have:

\displaystyle G(t)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}\left(\frac{\cosh(t)-\cosh(s_{\max}(t))}{\sinh(t)}\right)-\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}t-\lambda s_{\max}(t)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}\left(\frac{ct^{2}+O(t^{3})}{t+O(t^{3})}\right)+O(t)~.

This shows that
\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}G(t)=0
and thus concludes the proof.
∎

8.3.
The surface
\Sigma_{+}

By Proposition
8.6
, the invariant surface has the correct local behaviour in order to prove Theorem
D
, using the comparison result of Corollary
G
. The first step is, analogously to Lemmas
6.3
and
7.10
, comparing the elliptic and parabolic null support functions.

Lemma 8.8
.

Let
s
be a homogeneous function, and let
\phi
and
\varphi
be respectively its elliptic dehomogenization and parabolic dehomogenization, the latter with point at infinity
\pi/2
. If

\varphi(\mathsf{x})\leq\epsilon|\mathsf{x}\log|\mathsf{x}||

for all
\mathsf{x}
in some (one-sided) neighbourhood of
\mathsf{x}_{0}=0
, then

\phi(\theta)\leq\epsilon|(\theta-\theta_{0})\log|\theta-\theta_{0}||

for all
\theta
in some (one-sided) neighbourhood
\theta_{0}=-\pi/2
.

Proof.

The proof is very similar to Lemma
6.3
, except that we consider the point at infinity
\pi/2
. Analogously to Proposition
2.27
, we have
\mathsf{x}=\tan((1/2)(\theta-\theta_{0}))
, hence for
\mathsf{x}
close to
\mathsf{x}_{0}=0
, the linear bound
|\mathsf{x}|\leq|\theta-\theta_{0}|
holds. Thus

\phi(\theta)=\frac{\varphi(\mathsf{x})}{1+\mathsf{x}^{2}}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{1+\mathsf{x}^{2}}|\mathsf{x}\log|\mathsf{x}||\leq\epsilon|(\theta-\theta_{0})\log|\theta-\theta_{0}||~.

This concludes the proof.
∎

So, let
\phi
be a function as in the hypothesis of Theorem
D
. Of course, up to applying an isometry of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
, we can assume
\theta_{0}=-\pi/2
and
\phi(\theta_{0})=0
. Let
\phi_{\lambda}
the elliptic null support function of the invariant surface
\Sigma_{\lambda}
, for
\lambda=\epsilon/2
. We thus have
\phi_{\lambda}(\theta_{0})=\phi(\theta_{0})
and
\phi_{\lambda}\leq\phi
in a neighbourhood of
\theta_{0}=-\pi/2
. However, in order to apply Corollary
G
, with
\phi_{\lambda}
playing the role of
\phi_{+}
, we would need the inequality
\phi_{\lambda}\leq\phi
to hold globally.

This is an issue that we have already taken care of in the proof of Theorem
C
, by a translation of the surface. In this setting, however, there is one more step to do. Indeed, we have that
\phi_{\lambda}
equals
+\infty
in the whole interval
(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
, while the function
\phi
is by hypothesis equal to
+\infty
only in a one-sided neighbourhood (we can assume it is a right neighbourhood) of
\theta_{0}=-\pi/2
. Translating the surface has an effect of adding a bounded function to its null support function, hence will not change this situation. We will instead modify the surface
\Sigma_{\lambda}
by applying a parabolic isometry fixing the direction
\vec{\theta_{0}}
, which has the effect of “shrinking” the interval on which
\phi_{\lambda}
is infinite. We provide the details of this argument in the following proof.

Proposition 8.9
.

For any
\epsilon,\kappa>0
and
\theta_{0},\theta_{1}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
, there exists a convex entire spacelike surface
\Sigma_{+}
of constant curvature
-\kappa
which is incomplete at
\theta_{0}
and whose null support function
\phi_{+}
satisfies

•

\phi_{+}(\theta_{0})=0
;

•

the set
\{\phi_{+}=+\infty\}
equals the right open interval between
\theta_{0}
and
\theta_{1}
;

•

\phi_{+}
is continuous on the subset
\{\phi_{+}<+\infty\}
where it is finite;

•

\phi_{+}(\theta)\leq\epsilon|(\theta-\theta_{0})\log|\theta-\theta_{0}||
in a left one-sided neighbourhood of
\theta_{0}
.

Proof.

First, observe that it suffices to prove the statement for
\kappa=1
, since applying a dilatation has the effect of multiplying the support function by a constant (Lemma
6.4
).

Now, assuming
\theta_{0}=-\pi/2
, we will prove the proposition by applying to the surface
\Sigma_{\lambda}
studied above (for
\lambda>0
to be chosen later) a parabolic linear isometry
L
that fixes the direction
\vec{\theta_{0}}=(0,-1,1)
. Let
\Sigma_{\lambda}^{\prime}=L(\Sigma_{\lambda})
and
\phi_{\lambda}^{\prime}
be the elliptic null support function of
\Sigma_{\lambda}^{\prime}
. Clearly
L
fixes the plane
z=-y
, which is a null support plane of
\Sigma_{\lambda}
, and it is therefore also a null support plane of
\Sigma_{\lambda}^{\prime}
. Hence
\phi_{\lambda}^{\prime}(\theta_{0})=\phi_{\lambda}(\theta_{0})=0
. Moreover, the action induced by
L
on the unit circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
clearly maps
\{\theta\,|\,\phi_{\lambda}(\theta)=+\infty\}=(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
to
\{\theta\,|\,\phi_{\lambda}^{\prime}(\theta)=+\infty\}
. Choosing
L
suitably, we can therefore achieve the latter set to be equal to the right interval between
\theta_{0}=-\pi/2
and
\theta_{1}
, for any
\theta_{1}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
.

Observe that by Lemma
8.8
, the null support function
\phi_{\lambda}
is smaller than
\lambda|(\theta+\pi/2)\log|\theta+\pi/2||
on the left of
-\pi/2
. It remains to show that
\phi_{\lambda}^{\prime}
still has the same behaviour in a left one-sided neighbourhood of
-\pi/2
. This is done, avoiding unnecessary computations, by considering the parabolic null support function
with point at infinity
-\pi/2
.

For this purpose, let us introduce yet another map
\widehat{\xi}:\overline{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
by:

(55)

{\widehat{\xi}}(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})=\begin{bmatrix}2\mathsf{x}\\
1-\mathsf{x}^{2}-\mathsf{y}^{2}\\
1+\mathsf{x}^{2}+\mathsf{y}^{2}\end{bmatrix}~.

This map parameterizes all timelike and lightlike directions in Minkowski space, except
(0,-1,1)
, which corresponds to
\vec{\theta_{0}}
. Observe that
\widehat{\xi}=R\circ\xi
, where
\xi
is the map we have used in this section (Equation (
51
)) and
R
is the reflection in the plane
y=0
. Hence, defining the parabolic null support function
\widehat{\psi}

with respect to
-\pi/2
as

\widehat{\varphi}_{\lambda}(\mathsf{x})=\sup_{{p}\in\Sigma_{\lambda}}\langle{p},\widehat{\xi}(\mathsf{x},0)\rangle~,

we immediately obtain from the identity
\Sigma_{-\lambda}=R(\Sigma_{\lambda})
(Remark
8.2
) that

\widehat{\varphi}_{\lambda}(\mathsf{x})=\sup_{{p}\in\Sigma_{\lambda}}\langle{p},\widehat{\xi}(\mathsf{x},0)\rangle=\sup_{{p}\in\Sigma_{\lambda}}\langle{p},R\circ{\xi}(\mathsf{x},0)\rangle=\sup_{{p}\in\Sigma_{\lambda}}\langle R(p),{\xi}(\mathsf{x},0)\rangle=\varphi_{-\lambda}(\mathsf{x})~.

Therefore from Proposition
8.6
, we have:

\widehat{\varphi}_{\lambda}(\mathsf{x})=2\lambda|x|\log|x|

for
\mathsf{x}<0
. Similarly to Remark
2.23
(or by a direct check using (
55
)), we have
L\circ\widehat{\xi}(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})=\widehat{\xi}(\mathsf{x}+c,\mathsf{y})
.
Hence applying a parabolic isometry fixing the direction
\vec{\theta}_{0}
to a surface has the effect of precomposing the parabolic null support function with point at infinity
\theta_{0}
by a translation:

\widehat{\varphi}^{\prime}_{\lambda}(\mathsf{x}):=\sup_{{p}\in\Sigma^{\prime}_{\lambda}=L(\Sigma_{\lambda})}\langle{p},\widehat{\xi}(\mathsf{x},0)\rangle=\sup_{{p}\in\Sigma_{\lambda}}\langle{p},\widehat{\xi}(\mathsf{x}+c,0)\rangle=\widehat{\varphi}_{\lambda}(\mathsf{x}+c)~.

In particular,

\widehat{\varphi}^{\prime}_{\lambda}(\mathsf{x})=2\lambda|\mathsf{x}+c|\log|\mathsf{x}+c|~.

Since

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{(t+c)\log(t+c)}{t\log t}=1~,

we have that, for any
\epsilon>2\lambda
and for
|\mathsf{x}|
large,

\widehat{\varphi}^{\prime}_{\lambda}(\mathsf{x})\leq\epsilon|\mathsf{x}|\log|\mathsf{x}|

This shows the null support function of
L(\Sigma_{\lambda})
is smaller than that of
\Sigma_{\epsilon/2}
in a one-sided neighbourhood of
\theta_{0}=-\pi/2
. Going back to the parabolic null support function with point at infinity
-\pi/2
, this means (from Proposition
8.6
) that
\varphi^{\prime}_{\lambda}(\mathsf{x})\leq-\epsilon|\mathsf{x}|\log|\mathsf{x}|
for
\mathsf{x}
in a left neighbourhood of
0
. Using Lemma
8.8
, the proof is complete.
∎

8.4.
Proof of Theorem
D

We are finally ready to conclude the proof of Theorem
D
.

Proof of Theorem
D
.

Let
\phi
be the null support function as in the hypothesis of the theorem. Assume as usual that
\phi(\theta_{0})=0
, and let’s say that the curvature of
\Sigma
is bounded above by
-\kappa
. By Proposition
8.9
(possibly applying a reflection) let be
\phi_{+}
the null support function of a convex entire spacelike surface
\Sigma_{+}
of curvature
-\kappa
, with the following properties:

•

\Sigma_{+}
is incomplete at
\theta_{0}
,

•

\phi_{+}(\theta_{0})=\phi(\theta_{0})=0
,

•

the set where
\phi_{+}=+\infty
is a proper subset of the set where
\phi=+\infty
,

•

\phi_{+}
is continuous on the subset of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
where it is finite,

•

\phi_{+}\leq\phi
in a one-sided neighbourhood of
\theta_{0}
, on the side of
\theta_{0}
where both are finite.

Exactly as in the proof of Theorem
C
, we must now modify
\Sigma_{+}
further so that
\phi_{+}\leq\phi
holds globally, so that by Corollary
G
we will deduce that
\Sigma
is incomplete at
\theta_{0}
. To achieve this, it now suffices to translate
\Sigma_{+}
in the direction of
\vec{\theta}_{0}
. Indeed,
\phi_{+}
is continuous on the complement of the open interval where it is equal to
+\infty
, whereas
\phi
is lower-semicontinuous, hence bounded below. The same of course holds for the parabolic null support functions
\widehat{\psi}_{+}
and
\widehat{\psi}
with point at infinity
\theta_{0}
. This implies that
\widehat{\psi}-\widehat{\psi}_{+}
is bounded below on
\{\widehat{\psi}_{+}<+\infty\}
. Recall from the proof of Theorem
C
that translating
\Sigma_{+}
in the direction
c\vec{\theta}_{0}
has the effect of adding the constant
-2c
to
\widehat{\psi}_{+}
, and does not change the elliptic null support function at
\theta_{0}
. Hence choosing
c
large enough, one can make sure that
\widehat{\psi}_{+}\leq\widehat{\psi}
, and therefore also
\phi_{+}\leq\phi
on the whole
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. The proof is then concluded by Corollary
G
.
∎
