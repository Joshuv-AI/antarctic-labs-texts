Proof strategy

As previously indicated, we develop and apply a nonabelian delta method to give a convenient analytic expression isolating the
\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{O}_{D}^{n}
satisfying
P(\gamma^{\prime})=0.

The delta method is initiated in §
3
.
In §
4
, we then apply Poisson summation in
\gamma^{\prime}.

After Poisson summation,
we obtain a dual sum over
\gamma\in D^{n}
in which
there are two flavors of error terms we must treat.

The first flavor of error term is a new feature of our nonabelian setting. After Poisson summation, we require a spectral expansion of a sum over
\delta\in D^{\times}
to further refine the
\gamma=0
term. This leads naturally to a spectral expansion in terms of automorphic representations of
D^{\times}_{\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}}}.

The relevant local spectral estimates are established in §§
5
and
6
.

The second flavor of error term comes, as in the abelian case, from
\gamma\neq 0.
However the behavior of these terms is markedly different. The reduced norm scales quadratically, i.e. with degree
d:=\sqrt{\dim{D}}=2.
Thus Poisson summation leads to longer dual sums over
\gamma
than in the abelian case
d=1
.
Usual heuristics suggest that the dual sum (i.e. the sum after applying Poisson summation) is of the same complexity as the original.
However, we discover new, nonabelian
vanishing phenomena
in §
8
, which allow for progress over
\gamma\neq 0
.
The situation is still complicated by the fact that our complete exponential sums fail square-root cancellation, asymptotically in
n

[
AHH+24
, Appendix B]
.
Nonetheless, by identifying various geometric sources of vanishing, cancellation, and sparsity, we eventually obtain
an error term that has an exponent
\frac{3}{4}
times the exponent of the trivial bound
X^{4n}
.
2
2

2

This “slope”
\frac{3}{4}
also appears in work on cubics, such as
[
Dav05
,
HB83
,
Vau86
,
Hoo86
,
Hoo97
,
HB98
]
.

The tools involved are diverse, featuring
Cartan decomposition, matrix identities, iterative Gauss sum calculations, the geometry of quadric fibrations, and lattice-point methods (geometry of numbers).

Our work on
\gamma\neq 0
appears in §§
7
–
9
.
General Hessian-based analysis is done in §
7
, using a rank estimate proven via the Cayley–Hamilton theorem.
Deeper local integral estimates are proven in §
8
.
A final global application of the geometry of numbers appears in §
9
, based on some fortuitous bounds on the successive minima of a relevant family of lattices.

We leave Kloosterman-type averaging as an interesting open question.
By this, we mean
the use of nontrivial averaging over the Fourier inversion variable
in our local integrals.
This would be analogous to Kloosterman’s idea of averaging over numerators in the abelian case, as is done in
[
HB96
,
Get18
]
, explicitly or implicitly.
We expect the prime-case formulas in
[
AHH+24
, Appendix B]
to be useful for this purpose.
Alternatively, one could improve on Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
by sharpening our geometry of numbers estimates in §

on average.
We also suspect that a secondary term of order
X^{3n-2}
may exist, and may be detectable by methods of
[
HB96
,

,

,

We also leave the case

open; it is unclear what bounds to expect.
Extrapolating from

, a tentative guess is that after accounting for nonabelian vanishing phenomena,
we might morally have sums of length
\ll(X^{d^{2}(d-1)})^{(d-1)/(d^{2}-d)}

and local cancellations of quality

,
all raised to the

for a total saving of roughly

If so, then we might hope for an asymptotic point count with main term

Yet by the methods of

Finally, we mention that our nonabelian delta method

It is also possible that “nonabelian exponential sums” on these arcs could be bounded without using Poisson summation as we do.

However, a key difference is that the equations discussed in

2.
Conventions

2.1.
Local fields

Let
F
be a global field and let
v
be a place of
F.

We let
|\cdot|_{v}
be the number theorist’s norm on
F_{v}
.
If
F_{v}\cong\mathbb{R}
then
|\cdot|_{v}
is the absolute value, and if
F\cong\mathbb{C}
then
|z|_{v}=z\overline{z}
.
If
F_{v}
is non-Archimedean, let
\mathcal{O}_{F_{v}}
be its ring of integers and
\varpi_{v}
be a uniformizer. Then
|\varpi^{-1}_{v}|_{v}
is the cardinality
q_{v}
of the residue field
\mathcal{O}_{F_{v}}/\varpi_{v}\mathcal{O}_{F_{v}}
. The valuation associated to the place
v
is denoted by the same symbol:
v:F_{v}\to\mathbb{Z}\cup\{\infty\}.
Thus
v(\varpi_{v})=1
.

The usual norm
z\mapsto(z\overline{z})^{1/2}
on
\mathbb{C}
is denoted by
|\cdot|.

Fix a place
v
of
F
and drop it from notation, writing
F:=F_{v},

|\cdot|:=|\cdot|_{v},
etc. This creates the possibility of confusion when
F\cong\mathbb{C}.
To alleviate this, we do not identify
F
and
\mathbb{C}
and we take the standard convention that
|x|=x\overline{x}
if
x\in F
and
|y|=(y\overline{y})^{1/2}
if
y\in\mathbb{C}.
In other words, the meaning of
|\cdot|
changes depending on whether we use the symbol
F
or
\mathbb{C}
for the domain.

If
F
is a finite extension of
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
,
the
standard additive character

\psi_{F}:F\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}
is defined by the formula
x\mapsto e^{2\pi i(\operatorname{Tr}_{F/\mathbb{Q}_{p}}(x)\bmod{\mathbb{Z}_{p}})}
, where
\operatorname{Tr}_{F/\mathbb{Q}_{p}}(x)\bmod{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}
denotes the image of
\operatorname{Tr}_{F/\mathbb{Q}_{p}}(x)
in
\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}\cong\mathbb{Z}[1/p]/\mathbb{Z}\subset\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
. Here the first isomorphism is induced by the inclusion of
\mathbb{Z}[1/p]
into
\mathbb{Q}_{p}.
If
F
is Archimedean, then the standard additive character is defined by
x\mapsto e^{-2\pi i\operatorname{Tr}_{F/\mathbb{R}}(x)}
. In general, a nontrivial additive character on
F
is given by
x\mapsto\psi_{F}(\alpha x)
for some
\alpha\in F^{\times}
.

Let
F
be a finite extension of
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
. For a nontrivial additive character
\psi
on
F
, its
conductor

c(\psi)
is the integer such that
\psi
is trivial on
\varpi^{c(\psi)}\mathcal{O}_{F}
but not on
\varpi^{c(\psi)-1}\mathcal{O}_{F}
. We say
\psi
is
unramified
if
c(\psi)=0
.

2.2.
Central simple algebras

For a central simple algebra
D
over a field
F,
let
d:=\sqrt{\dim_{F}D}
.
We let
\mathrm{trd}:D\to F
and
\mathrm{nrd}:D\to F
be the reduced trace and the reduced norm.
When
d=2
,
we let
\dagger
denote the unique standard involution on
D

[
Voi21
, Definition 3.2.4 and Corollary 3.4.4]
.
Then
\mathrm{trd}(A)=A+A^{\dagger}
and
\mathrm{nrd}(A)=A^{\dagger}A=AA^{\dagger}
.
When
F
is a number field, we use the same notation for the adelic and local versions of these maps.
At all split places
v
, we have
D_{v}:=D\otimes_{F}F_{v}\cong M_{d}(F_{v})
by definition.
By the Skolem-Noether theorem, for any such isomorphism

\mathrm{trd}
is sent to
\mathrm{tr}
and
\mathrm{nrd}
is sent to
\det.

Moreover, if
d=2
, then
\dagger
is sent to
the map sending an element of
M_{2}(F_{v})
to its adjugate,
because the adjugate is the unique standard involution on
M_{2}(F_{v})

[
Voi21
, Example 3.2.8 and Corollary 3.4.4]
.
Any maximal order of
M_{d}(F_{v})
over
\mathcal{O}_{F_{v}}
is isomorphic to
M_{d}(\mathcal{O}_{F_{v}})
by an inner automorphism of
M_{d}(F_{v})

[
Voi21
, Chapter 10]
.

Let
F
be a local field.
For any integer
m\geq 0
, the
box norm
on
F^{m}
is given by

(2.2.1)

\displaystyle\left\lVert(v_{1},\ldots,v_{m})\right\rVert:=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}|v_{i}|.

Often we will make an identification of
D
with
F^{d^{2}}
as
F
-vector spaces and let
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
be the induced box norm on
D
.
We sometimes call
\left\lVert x\right\rVert
the
magnitude
of
x
, to avoid linguistic confusion with the reduced norm
\mathrm{nrd}(x)
for
x\in D
.

Remark
.

If
F\cong\mathbb{C}
, then
\lvert\cdot\rvert
and
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert

(they do not satisfy the triangle inequality).

2.3.
Measures and Fourier transforms

Let
F
be a local field. If
F
is non-Archimedean, the Schwarz space
\mathcal{S}(D)=C_{c}^{\infty}(D)
is the space of compactly supported smooth functions. If
F
is Archimedean
\mathcal{S}(D)
is the usual Schwartz space on
D
(viewed as an
F
-vector space). Fix a nontrivial additive character
\psi:F\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}
. For a central simple
F
-algebra
D
, let

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{D}(f)(y):=\int_{D}f(x)\psi(\mathrm{trd}(xy))\,dx

be the Fourier transform on the Schwartz space
\mathcal{S}(D).
Here the measure
dx
on
D
is always normalized to be self-dual with respect to
\psi.
In other words, it is normalized so that the Fourier inversion holds:

(2.3.1)

\displaystyle f(y)=\int_{D}\mathcal{F}_{D}(f)(x)\psi(-\mathrm{trd}(xy))\,dx.

We will often drop the subscript and write
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{D}
whenever the context is clear. Any Haar measure on
D^{\times}
is a
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
multiple of
|\mathrm{nrd}|^{-d}dx.
We will specify the constant when necessary.

Suppose
F
is non-Archimedean. Let
\mathcal{O}_{D}
be a maximal order of
D
over
\mathcal{O}_{F}
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{D}^{\#}:=\big\{y\in D:\mathrm{trd}(xy)\in\mathcal{O}_{F}\,\forall x\in\mathcal{O}_{D}\big\}

be its dual. Note that
\mathcal{O}_{D}^{\#\#}=\mathcal{O}_{D}
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{O}_{D}})=dx(\mathcal{O}_{D})\mathbf{1}_{\varpi^{c(\psi)}\mathcal{O}_{D}^{\#}}.

If
D
is split and
\psi
is unramified, then our normalizations ensure that the function
\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{O}_{D}}
is self-dual, i.e.,
\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{O}_{D}})=\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{O}_{D}}
and
\mathrm{vol}(\mathcal{O}_{D})=1
.

2.4.
Gauss sum

Generalized Gauss sums will be repeatedly used in our local estimates throughout the paper.
Assume
F
is non-Archimedean
and define for
(a,t,\xi)\in F\times F^{\times}\times F
, the
generalized Gauss sum

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(a,t,\xi):=\int_{\mathcal{O}_{F}}\psi\left(\frac{ay^{2}+\xi y}{t}\right)\,dy.

We emphasize that our Gauss sums are normalized so that the trivial bound is
\lvert\mathcal{G}\rvert\leq\mathrm{vol}(\mathcal{O}_{F})
; in classical settings, Gauss sums are often normalized differently.

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose
F
is a finite extension of
\mathbb{Q}_{p}

and
\psi
is unramified.
In general
there is a constant
c_{F}>0
, with
c_{F}=1
if
p\neq 2
,
such that

(2.4.1)

\displaystyle c_{F}\,|\mathcal{G}(a,t,\xi)|_{\infty}\leq\min(1,|t/a|)^{1/2}=q^{\min(v(a)-v(t),0)/2},

which is an equality if

v(\xi)\geq\min(v(a),v(t))

and
p\neq 2
.
If
|t/a|\geq 1
, i.e.
v(a)\geq v(t)
, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(a,t,\xi)=\mathcal{G}(t,t,\xi)=\mathcal{G}(0,t,\xi)=\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{O}_{F}}(\xi/t).

Suppose
v(a)\leq v(t)
, i.e.
|t/a|\leq 1
.
Then
\mathcal{G}(a,t,\xi)=0
unless

(2.4.2)

\xi/a\in\mathcal{O}_{F},

i.e.
|\xi/a|\leq 1
.
In addition, if
\xi/a\in 2\mathcal{O}_{F}
, then

(2.4.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(a,t,\xi)=\psi\left(-\frac{\xi^{2}}{4at}\right)\mathcal{G}(a,t,0).

Proof.

Everything is clear if
v(a)\geq v(t)
, so we may assume
v(a)\leq v(t)
.
Dividing
(a,t,\xi)
by
a\neq 0
, we may assume
a=1
.
All but the bound for
p=2
follow from
[
Get18
, Lemma 6.1]
and its proof.
For
p=2
, it follows from the Van der Corput Lemma
[
Clu11
, Proposition 3.3]

∎

2.5.
Adelic measure and Fourier transform

Let
D
be a central simple algebra over a number field
F
. Fix a maximal order
\mathcal{O}_{D}\subset D
over
\mathcal{O}_{F}
.
At every finite place
v
, the localization
\mathcal{O}_{D_{v}}\subset D_{F_{v}}
is a maximal order over
\mathcal{O}_{F_{v}}
by
[
Voi21
, Lemma 10.4.3]
.

The Schwartz space of
D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}):=\mathcal{S}(D_{F_{\infty}})\otimes\mathcal{S}(D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}^{\infty}}).

Here

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}^{\infty}}):=\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\bigotimes}_{v\nmid\infty}\mathcal{S}(D_{F_{v}})=\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\bigotimes}_{v\nmid\infty}C^{\infty}_{c}(D_{F_{v}})

where the restricted tensor product is taken with respect to the basic functions
\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{O}_{D_{v}}}
, and

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(D_{F_{\infty}}):=\widehat{\bigotimes}_{v|\infty}\mathcal{S}(D_{F_{v}})

where
\mathcal{S}(D_{F_{\infty}})
and
\mathcal{S}(D_{F_{v}})
are the usual Schwartz spaces on the
\mathbb{R}
-vector spaces
D_{F_{\infty}}
(resp.
D_{F_{v}}
) with the natural Fréchet topology, and
\widehat{\bigotimes}
is the completion of the (projective) tensor product.
Concretely, this means that any element of
\mathcal{S}(D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}})
is a finite sum of functions of the form
f_{\infty}\otimes\left(\otimes_{v\nmid\infty}f_{v}\right)
with
f_{\infty}\in\mathcal{S}(D_{F_{\infty}})
and
f_{v}\in\mathcal{S}(D_{F_{v}})
where
f_{v}=\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{O}_{D_{v}}}
for all but finitely many
v.

Let
\psi:F\backslash\mathbb{A}_{F}\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}
be a nontrivial additive character. Then
\psi=\otimes_{v}\psi_{v}
and we have an adelic Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}_{D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}}:=\otimes_{v}\mathcal{F}_{D_{v}}
. Explicitly, it is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{D}:\mathcal{S}(D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}})

\displaystyle\tilde{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{S}(D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}})

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\longmapsto\left(y\mapsto\int_{D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}}f(x)\psi\left(\mathrm{trd}(yx)\right)dx\right)

where
dx=\prod_{v}dx_{v}
.

On
\mathcal{S}(D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}^{m})
we let
\mathcal{F}_{i}
be the Fourier transform in the
i
th entry. For instance if
\Phi\in\mathcal{S}(D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}^{2})
, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{2}(\Phi)(x,y)=\int_{D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}}\Phi(x,z)\psi\left(\mathrm{trd}(yz)\right)dz.

is the Fourier transform in the second variable. We use the same notation over local fields.

3.
The
\delta
-symbol method

Let
D
be a central division algebra over a number field
F
.
For
\alpha\in D
let

\displaystyle\delta_{\alpha}:=\mathbf{1}_{\alpha=0}:=\begin{cases}1&\textrm{ if }\alpha=0,\\
0&\textrm{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

The following is a nonabelian expansion of the
\delta
-symbol:

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\Phi\in\mathcal{S}(D^{2}_{\mathbb{A}_{F}})
. Assume
\Phi(t,0)=0
for all
t\in D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}

and
\mathcal{F}_{2}(\Phi)(0,0)\neq 0
.
For all
Q\in A_{\mathbb{G}_{m}}
,

(3.0.1)

b_{\Phi,Q}\,\delta_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{|Q|^{\dim_{F}D}}\sum_{\delta\in D^{\times}}\left(\Phi\left(\frac{\alpha\delta^{-1}}{Q},\frac{\delta}{Q}\right)-\Phi\left(\frac{\delta}{Q},\frac{\delta^{-1}\alpha}{Q}\right)\right),

where

b_{\Phi,Q}:=\sum_{\delta\in D}\mathcal{F}_{2}(\Phi)(0,Q\delta)=\mathcal{F}_{2}(\Phi)(0,0)+O_{N,\Phi}(|Q|^{-N})

for all
N\geq 0
.
Moreover, for
|Q|
sufficiently large

(3.0.2)

\delta_{\alpha}=\frac{c_{\Phi,Q}}{|Q|^{\dim_{F}D}}\sum_{\delta\in D^{\times}}\left(\Phi\left(\frac{\alpha\delta^{-1}}{Q},\frac{\delta}{Q}\right)-\Phi\left(\frac{\delta}{Q},\frac{\delta^{-1}\alpha}{Q}\right)\right),

where
c_{\Phi,Q}:=b_{\Phi,Q}^{-1}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{F}_{2}(\Phi)(0,0)}+O_{N,\Phi}(|Q|^{-N})
.

Proof.

If
\alpha=0
then by our vanishing assumption
\Phi(t,0)=0
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\delta\in D^{\times}}\left(\Phi\left(\frac{\alpha\delta^{-1}}{Q},\frac{\delta}{Q}\right)-\Phi\left(\frac{\delta}{Q},\frac{\delta^{-1}\alpha}{Q}\right)\right)=\sum_{\delta\in D^{\times}}\Phi\left(0,\frac{\delta}{Q}\right)=\sum_{\delta\in D}\Phi\left(0,\frac{\delta}{Q}\right).

By Poisson summation this is

\displaystyle|Q|^{\dim_{F}D}\sum_{\delta\in D}\mathcal{F}_{2}(\Phi)(0,Q\delta)=|Q|^{\dim_{F}D}\mathcal{F}_{2}(\Phi)(0,0)+O_{N,\Phi}(|Q|^{-N}).

This establishes the equality (
3.0.1
) in the lemma when
\alpha=0
.

If
\alpha\neq 0,
by changing variables

\delta\mapsto\delta^{-1}\alpha

we see that

\displaystyle\sum_{\delta\in D^{\times}}\Phi\left(\frac{\alpha\delta^{-1}}{Q},\frac{\delta}{Q}\right)=\sum_{\delta\in D^{\times}}\Phi\left(\frac{\delta}{Q},\frac{\delta^{-1}\alpha}{Q}\right)

and hence the sum on the right in (
3.0.1
) vanishes.
Of course, (
3.0.2
) is immediate from (
3.0.1
).
∎

Remark
.

Lemma
3.1
can be shown to hold even if
D
is an
alternative
non-associative division algebra, e.g. an octonion algebra. This is because in such an algebra unique two-sided inverses of nonzero elements exist, and if
x
and
y
are both nonzero one has
(xy)^{-1}=y^{-1}x^{-1}
and
y=x^{-1}(xy).

Since
D
is a division algebra, for
\alpha\in D,

\alpha=0
holds if and only if
\mathrm{nrd}(\alpha)=0
.
If we replace
D
by the split central simple algebra
M_{n}(F),
then the idea behind Lemma
3.1
naturally produces an identity where the condition
\alpha=0
is replaced by the weaker condition
\det(\alpha)=0.
This observation leads to the generalization we explore in the next subsection.

3.1.
A remark on generalizations

Let
G
be a linear algebraic group over
F
acting on a quasi-affine scheme
X
over
F:

(3.1.1)

\displaystyle X\times G\longrightarrow X.

Assume that
X
admits a unique open
G
-orbit
O,
that
b_{0}\in O(F),
and that the stabilizer of
b_{0}
in
G
is trivial. Thus the action map induces a bijection
G(F)\tilde{\to}O(F).

Finally assume that there are automorphisms (of schemes, not group schemes)
\iota:X\to X
and
\iota:G\to G
of order
2
such that
\iota(gh)=\iota(h)\iota(g)
and
\iota(b_{0}.g)=b_{0}.\iota(g).

Let
\Phi:X(\mathbb{A}_{F})\to\mathbb{C}
be a function and let
x\in X(F).
Consider

(3.1.2)

\displaystyle f_{\Phi}(x):=\sum_{g\in G(F)}\Phi\left(x.g^{-1},b_{0}.\iota(g)\right)-\sum_{g\in G(F)}\Phi\left(b_{0}.g,\iota(x).\iota(g^{-1})\right).

Here we assume that
\Phi
is sufficiently nice that both sums converge absolutely. For example, if
X
is smooth we can take
\Phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(X(\mathbb{A}_{F}))=C_{c}^{\infty}(X(F_{\infty}))\otimes C^{\infty}_{c}(X(\mathbb{A}_{F}^{\infty})).

If
x\in O(F)
we can choose an
h\in G(F)
such that
b_{0}.h=x.
We then change variables
g\mapsto g^{-1}h
in the first sum in the definition of
f_{\Phi}(x)
to see that
f_{\Phi}(x)=0
for
x\in O(F).
In other words,
f_{\Phi}(x)
vanishes on the open orbit
O(F).
We hasten to point out that
f_{\Phi}
is not necessarily constant away from
O(F)
, unless
X(F)-O(F)
consists of a single point.

Expressions of the type (
3.1.2
) could be useful for counting points of schemes that do not lie in an open orbit under a group action.
The
\delta
-symbol in Lemma
3.1
is essentially the special case where
X
and
G
are the scheme and group with points in an
F
-algebra
R
given by
X(R):=D\otimes R,

G(R):=(D\otimes R)^{\times}.
In this case
X(F)-O(F)=\{0\},
we can take
\iota
to be induced by
the standard involution
\dagger
on
D,
and
b_{0}=I_{D}.
In order to simplify the presentation we did not incorporate the involution into Lemma
3.1
.

Another natural case is when
G=\mathrm{GL}_{n}
and
X=M_{n\times n}
(
n\times n
matrices) equipped with the usual right action. In this case
X(F)-O(F)=\{Z\in M_{n\times n}(F):\det(Z)=0\}
and we can take
\iota
to be the transpose. In unpublished work Myerson and Vishe considered this setting when
n=2.

One could also work more generally with reductive monoids, and make use of the Poisson summation formula for these objects conjectured to exist by Braverman and Kazhdan
[
BK00
,
Ngô14
]
. The conjectural Poisson summation formula has been established in certain cases, see
[
BK99
,
BK02
,
CG21
,
GH20
,
GHL21
,
GL19a
,
GL19b
]
.

4.
Poisson summation

Let
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}^{n})
and
X\in A_{\mathbb{G}_{m}}.
In this section we study the analytic behavior as
|X|\to\infty
of

\displaystyle\Sigma(X):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma^{\prime}\in D^{n}\\
P(\gamma^{\prime})=0\end{subarray}}f\left(\frac{\gamma^{\prime}}{X}\right).

Assume henceforth that
P
is homogeneous of degree
2

and that
D
is a quaternion division algebra over
F.
This is the setting relevant for Theorem
1.2
and making this assumption now simplifies the notation required below.

Choose a pure tensor
\Phi=\otimes_{v}\Phi_{v}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}^{2})
such that the assumption of Lemma
3.1
is satisfied, i.e.
\Phi(t,0)=0
for all
t\in D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}
and
\int_{D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}}\Phi(0,t)dt\neq 0.
Applying the
\delta
-symbol expansion of Lemma
3.1
(with
Q=X\gg_{\Phi}1
), we have

(4.0.1)

\displaystyle\Sigma(X)=\frac{c_{\Phi,X}}{|X|^{4}}\sum_{\gamma\in D^{n}}f\left(\frac{\gamma^{\prime}}{X}\right)\sum_{\delta\in D^{\times}}\Phi^{\operatorname{diff}}\left(\frac{\gamma^{\prime}}{X},\frac{\delta}{X}\right),

where for convenience

(4.0.2)

\displaystyle\Phi^{\operatorname{diff}}(\gamma^{\prime},\delta):=\Phi\left(P(\gamma^{\prime})\delta^{-1},\delta\right)-\Phi\left(\delta,\delta^{-1}P(\gamma^{\prime})\right)

Here we have used the fact that
P(\gamma^{\prime}/X)=P(\gamma^{\prime})/X^{2}
.

Writing
\gamma^{\prime}=(\gamma_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\gamma_{n}^{\prime})
, we define pairings

(4.0.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\cdot:D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}^{n}\times D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}^{n}&\longrightarrow D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}\\
(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})&\longmapsto\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}\gamma_{i}^{\prime},\\
\langle\,,\,\rangle:D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}^{n}\times D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}^{n}&\longrightarrow\mathbb{A}_{F}\\
(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})&\longmapsto\mathrm{trd}(\gamma\cdot\gamma^{\prime})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{trd}\,\gamma_{i}\gamma_{i}^{\prime}.\end{split}

Applying Poisson summation in
\gamma^{\prime}\in D^{n}
to (
4.0.1
), we have

\displaystyle\Sigma(X)=\frac{c_{\Phi,X}}{|X|^{4}}\sum_{\gamma\in D^{n}}\int_{D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}^{n}}f\left(\frac{t}{X}\right)\sum_{\delta\in D^{\times}}\Phi^{\operatorname{diff}}\left(\frac{t}{X},\frac{\delta}{X}\right)\psi\left(\langle\gamma,t\rangle\right)\,dt.

By the change of variables
\gamma\mapsto\frac{\gamma}{\mathrm{nrd}(\delta)}
, we arrive at

\displaystyle\Sigma(X)=\frac{c_{\Phi,X}}{|X|^{4}}\sum_{\gamma\in D^{n}}\int_{D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}^{n}}f\left(\frac{t}{X}\right)\sum_{\delta\in D^{\times}}\Phi^{\operatorname{diff}}\left(\frac{t}{X},\frac{\delta}{X}\right)\psi\left(\frac{\langle\gamma,t\rangle}{\mathrm{nrd}(\delta)}\right)\,dt.

Changing variables
t\mapsto Xt
and using the fact that
\mathrm{nrd}(X)=X^{2}
, we obtain

(4.0.4)

\displaystyle\Sigma(X)=c_{\Phi,X}|X|^{4(n-1)}\sum_{\gamma\in D^{n}}\int_{D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}^{n}}f(t)\sum_{\delta\in D^{\times}}\Phi^{\operatorname{diff}}\left(t,\frac{\delta}{X}\right)\psi\left(\frac{\langle\gamma/X,t\rangle}{\mathrm{nrd}(\delta/X)}\right)\,dt.

For
(\delta,\gamma)\in D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}^{\times}\times D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}^{n},
define

(4.0.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}I_{0}(\delta,\gamma):=I_{0}(f,\Phi,\delta,\gamma)&:=\int_{D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}^{n}}f(t)\Phi(\delta,\delta^{-1}P(t))\psi\left(\frac{\langle\gamma,t\rangle}{\mathrm{nrd}(\delta)}\right)\,dt,\\
I_{1}(\delta,\gamma):=I_{1}(f,\Phi,\delta,\gamma)&:=\int_{D^{n}_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}}f(t)\Phi(P(t)\delta^{-1},\delta)\psi\left(\frac{\langle\gamma,t\rangle}{\mathrm{nrd}(\delta)}\right)\,dt.\end{split}

Then (
4.0.4
) can be written as

(4.0.6)

\begin{split}\Sigma(X)&=c_{\Phi,X}X^{4(n-1)}\sum_{\gamma\in D^{n}}\sum_{\delta\in D^{\times}}\left(I_{1}\left(\frac{\delta}{X},\frac{\gamma}{X}\right)-I_{0}\left(\frac{\delta}{X},\frac{\gamma}{X}\right)\right)\end{split}

Defining (
4.0.3
) and (
4.0.5
) analogously over local fields, one has for pure tensors
f=\otimes_{v}f

\displaystyle I_{i}(f,\Phi,\delta,\gamma)=\prod_{v}I_{i,v}(f_{v},\Phi_{v},\delta,\gamma).

To avoid potential confusion, we point out that since
X\in A_{\mathbb{G}_{m}}\cong\mathbb{R}_{>0},
if
f
and
\Phi
are pure tensors then
I_{i}\left(\frac{\delta}{X},\frac{\gamma}{X}\right)=I_{i}\left(\delta,\gamma\right)
for all
v\nmid\infty.
In other words only the Archimedean factors
I_{i,\infty}
(i.e. those with
v\mid\infty
) in (
4.0.6
) depend on the parameter
X.

Moreover, by the change of variables
t\mapsto t^{\dagger}
on
D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}^{n}
, and the cyclicity of
\mathrm{trd}
, we have

(4.0.7)

I_{0}(f,\Phi,\delta,\gamma)=I_{1}(f\circ\dagger,\Phi^{\operatorname{sw}}\circ\dagger,\delta^{\dagger},\gamma^{\dagger}),

where
f\circ\dagger
is the map
t\mapsto f(t^{\dagger})
,
and
\Phi^{\operatorname{sw}}\circ\dagger
is the map
(x,y)\mapsto\Phi(y^{\dagger},x^{\dagger})
.
So we may concentrate on either
I_{0}
or
I_{1}
for the most part.

Write
\Sigma(X)=\Sigma_{0}(X)+E_{1}(X),
where

(4.0.8)

\Sigma_{0}(X):=c_{\Phi,X}X^{4(n-1)}\sum_{\delta\in D^{\times}}\left(I_{1}\left(\frac{\delta}{X},0\right)-I_{0}\left(\frac{\delta}{X},0\right)\right),

(4.0.9)

E_{1}(X):=c_{\Phi,X}X^{4(n-1)}\sum_{\gamma\in D^{n}-\{0\}}\sum_{\delta\in D^{\times}}\left(I_{1}\left(\frac{\delta}{X},\frac{\gamma}{X}\right)-I_{0}\left(\frac{\delta}{X},\frac{\gamma}{X}\right)\right).

We expect that
E_{1}(X)\ll_{f,\Phi,\epsilon}X^{3n+\epsilon}
for all
\epsilon>0.
In Theorem
9.1
we prove this when
F=\mathbb{Q},

D
is nonsplit at
\infty
and
2,
and
f=\otimes_{v}f_{v}
,
\Phi:=\otimes_{v}\Phi_{v}
with
f_{v}:=\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{O}_{D_{v}}^{n}}
and
\Phi_{v}=\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{O}_{D_{v}}^{2}}

for all
v
where
D_{v}
is split.
This suffices for the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

4.1.
Contribution of
\Sigma_{0}

For the remainder of this section we focus on
\Sigma_{0}(X).
For this contribution we do not assume that
F=\mathbb{Q}
because we do not require this assumption.
For functions
\phi:D^{\times}\backslash D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}^{\times}\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}
and
s\in\mathbb{C}
let
\phi_{s}(x):=\phi(x)|\mathrm{nrd}(x)|^{s}.

In order to state a lemma let us discuss measures on
A_{\mathbb{G}_{m}}
and
D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}^{\times}.

Let

(D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}^{\times})^{1}:=\ker(|\mathrm{nrd}|:D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}^{\times}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}).

Endow
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
with the usual measure
\frac{dt}{t}.
Choose a Haar measure on
D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}^{\times}.
We assume that the map
|\mathrm{nrd}|:A_{\mathbb{G}_{m}}\tilde{\to}\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is measure preserving and that the natural isomorphisms
D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}^{\times}=A_{\mathbb{G}_{m}}\times(D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}^{\times})^{1}
and
(D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}^{\times})^{1}\tilde{\to}A_{\mathbb{G}_{m}}\backslash D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}^{\times}
are measure preserving. We then obtain a measure on
A_{\mathbb{G}_{m}}D^{\times}\backslash D^{\times}_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}
in the usual manner since
D^{\times}<A_{\mathbb{G}_{m}}\backslash D^{\times}_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}
is discrete. We define orthonormal bases on subspaces of
L^{2}(A_{\mathbb{G}_{m}}D^{\times}\backslash D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}^{\times})
using this measure.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
h\in C_{c}^{\infty}(D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}).
Then

(4.1.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{\delta\in D^{\times}}h(\delta g)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathrm{Re}(s)=\sigma}\sum_{\pi}\sum_{\phi\in B_{\pi}}\left(\int_{D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}^{\times}}h(x)\overline{\phi}_{s}(x)\,d^{\times}x\right)\phi_{-s}(g)\,ds,

for all
g\in D_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}^{\times}
and
\sigma\gg 1
, where the sum over
\pi
is taken over isomorphic classes of automorphic representations of
A_{\mathbb{G}_{m}}D^{\times}\backslash D^{\times}_{\mathbb{A}_{F}}
and
B_{\pi}
is an orthonormal basis of
\pi
.

Proof.

By Mellin inversion we have

Lemma 5.2

We can and do assume that

According to (

∎

Lemma 5.4
.

\displaystyle\frac{I(f,\mathbf{1}_{M_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F})^{2}},m_{s})}{L(s+3/2,\pi)}

5.1.

Lemma 5.5
.

Suppose

\displaystyle=|\mathrm{tr}(Z)|^{-1/2}\max(|\mathrm{tr}(Z)\det(Z)|,\left\lVert Z\right\rVert^{2})^{-1/2}

Proof.

Write
Y=(y_{ij})
and
Z=(z_{ij})
. Below we identify
M_{2}(F)
with

whose associated matrix is given by

\displaystyle J=\left(\begin{matrix}2z_{11}&z_{21}&z_{12}&0\\
z_{21}&0&z_{11}+z_{22}&z_{21}\\
z_{12}&z_{11}+z_{22}&0&z_{12}\\
0&z_{21}&z_{12}&2z_{22}\end{matrix}\right).

That is

1&1&0&z_{12}\\
1&-1&0&z_{21}\\
0&0&-1&-z_{11}\end{matrix}\right).

Let

One has

\displaystyle=2r\left(\begin{matrix}1&&&\\
&-1&&\\
&&1&\\
&&&\det(Z)\end{matrix}\right),

\displaystyle\det(R)

\displaystyle=2r,

\displaystyle R^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{2r}\left(\begin{matrix}-r_{+}&-r&-r&-r_{+}\\
-r_{-}&-r&r&-r_{-}\\
-2z_{11}&0&0&2z_{22}\\
2&0&0&2\end{matrix}\right).

Since

\displaystyle R^{-1}\mathcal{O}_{F}^{4}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}_{F}e_{1}+\mathcal{O}_{F}e_{2}+\mathcal{O}_{F}e_{3}+r^{-1}\mathcal{O}_{F}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}r_{+}\\
r_{-}\\
2z_{11}\\

\displaystyle=|r|\int_{r^{-1}\mathcal{O}_{F}}\int_{\mathcal{O}_{F}^{3}}\psi(r(w+r_{+}z)^{2}-r(x+r_{-}z)^{2}+r(y+2z_{11}z)^{2}+r\det(Z)4z^{2})\,dwdxdydz

\displaystyle=|r|\left|\int_{r^{-1}\mathcal{O}_{F}}\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{O}}(r_{+}z)\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{O}}(r_{-}z)\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{O}}(2z_{11}z)\psi(4r\det(Z)z^{2})\,dz\right|\left|\int_{\mathcal{O}_{F}}\psi(rw^{2}-rx^{2}+ry^{2})\,dwdxdy\right|

where

5.2.
Analytic continuation

Lemma 5.7
.

Suppose
n\geq 4
. For
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
, consider the series

\displaystyle\sum_{Z}|\det(Z)|^{-(\sigma+2)}|\mathrm{tr}(Z)|^{-n/2}\left\lVert Z\right\rVert^{-n},

where the sum in
Z
is taken over a set of representatives of cosets in
(M_{2}(F)-M_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F}))/M_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F})
such that
\det(Z)\geq\left\lVert Z\right\rVert
and
|\mathrm{tr}(Z)|\geq|2|.
The series converges when
\sigma>-\frac{n+1}{2}
and is bounded above by

\displaystyle c_{F}q^{-(\sigma+\frac{n+1}{2})}\zeta(n-3)\zeta(n/2-1)\zeta\left(\sigma+\frac{n+1}{2}\right),

for some positive constant
c_{F},
which is
1
if
2\nmid q
.

Proof.

We prove only for the case we need, i.e.
2\nmid q
, and leave the case
2|q
to the reader. We rewrite the series as

(5.2.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}q^{-m(\sigma+2)}\sum_{Z:|\det(Z)|=q^{m}}|\mathrm{tr}(Z)|^{-\frac{n}{2}}\left\lVert Z\right\rVert^{-n}.

For a fixed
m\geq 1
, we first study the inner sum. We define for integers
0\leq r\leq a
the set

\displaystyle E_{a,r}:=\{Z:\left\lVert Z\right\rVert=q^{a},|\mathrm{tr}(Z)|=q^{r}\}.

Note that
|E_{a,r}|\leq q^{3a+r}
. Since
\left\lVert Z\right\rVert\leq|\det(Z)|\leq\left\lVert Z\right\rVert^{2},
the inner sum in (
5.2.1
) is bounded by

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq a\leq m\leq 2a\\
0\leq r\leq a\end{subarray}}q^{3a+r}q^{-\frac{rn}{2}}q^{-na}

\displaystyle\leq\zeta(n/2-1)\sum_{0\leq\lceil\frac{m}{2}\rceil\leq a\leq m}q^{(3-n)a}

\displaystyle\leq q^{-m(n-3)/2}\zeta(n-3)\zeta(n/2-1).

Thus (
5.2.1
) is convergent when
\sigma>-\frac{n+1}{2}
and is bounded above by

\displaystyle\zeta(n-3)\zeta(n/2-1)\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}q^{-m(\sigma+\frac{n+1}{2})}=q^{-(\sigma+\frac{n+1}{2})}\zeta(n-3)\zeta(n/2-1)\zeta\left(\sigma+\frac{n+1}{2}\right).

∎

By Lemma

if

. The assertion then follows from Lemma

.
∎

For the remainder of this section we continue to assume that the coefficients of

lie in
\mathcal{O}_{F}^{\times}.

Recall for the trivial representation,
L(s+3/2,1\circ\mathrm{nrd})=\zeta_{D}(s+3/2)=\zeta(s+2)\zeta(s+1).
Motivated by global considerations, we define the local density to be

(5.2.3)

\displaystyle c\left(\mathbf{1}_{M_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F})^{n}}\right):=\left.\frac{I(\mathbf{1}_{M_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F})^{n}},\mathbf{1}_{M_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F})^{2}},1_{s})}{\zeta(s+2)\zeta(s+1)}\right|_{s=-2},

which is well defined for

By the proof of Proposition

,

\displaystyle c\left(\mathbf{1}_{M_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F})^{n}}\right)=\sum_{Z}\int_{M_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F})^{n}}\psi(\langle P(Y),Z\rangle)\,dY

is an absolutely convergent series. As the integral on the right is invariant under

we have

\displaystyle c\left(\mathbf{1}_{M_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F})^{n}}\right)=\int_{M_{2}(F)}\int_{M_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F})^{n}}\psi(\langle P(Y),Z\rangle)\,dY.

Corollary 5.9
.

Suppose

and

.

Proof.

By Proposition

\displaystyle 1>q^{-\frac{n-3}{2}}\zeta(n-3)\zeta(n/2-1)\zeta\left(\frac{n-3}{2}\right)

when

which is straightforward.
∎

We can alternatively write

\displaystyle c\left(\mathbf{1}_{M_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F})^{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{M_{2}(F)}\left(\int_{M_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F})^{n}}\psi(\langle P(Y),Z\rangle)dY\right)\,dZ

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\to\infty}\int_{M_{2}(F)}\mathbf{1}_{\varpi^{-m}M_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F})}(Z)\left(\int_{M_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F})^{n}}\psi(\langle P(Y),Z\rangle)dY\right)\,dZ

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\to\infty}q^{4m}\int_{M_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F})^{n}}\mathbf{1}_{\varpi^{m}M_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F})}(P(Y))\,dY

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\to\infty}q^{4m(1-n)}\sum_{Y\in(M_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F})/\varpi^{m}M_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F}))^{n}}\mathbf{1}_{\varpi^{m}M_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F})}(P(Y))>0

∎

6.
Spectral bounds for nonsplit cases

Let
F
be any local field of characteristic zero and assume
D
is nonsplit (hence a division algebra). Then we have an exact sequence

\displaystyle 1\longrightarrow(D^{\times})^{1}\longrightarrow D^{\times}\longrightarrow\mathrm{nrd}(D^{\times})\longrightarrow 1,

where
(D^{\times})^{1}
is the subgroup of norm
1,
which is also the maximal compact subgroup of
D^{\times}
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Suppose
\Phi
is (bi-)
(D^{\times})^{1}
-invariant in both entries. Then
I(f,\Phi,m_{s})=0
unless
\pi
is unramified.

Proof.

For
\mathrm{Re}(s)>0,
we have for
k_{1},k_{2}\in(D^{\times})^{1}

\displaystyle I(f,\Phi,m_{s})

\displaystyle=\int_{D^{\times}}\int_{D^{n}}f(Y)\Phi\left(P(Y)k_{2}^{-1}g^{-1}k_{1}^{-1},k_{1}gk_{2}\right)m_{s}(k_{1}gk_{2})\,dYdg

\displaystyle=\int_{D^{\times}}\int_{D^{n}}f(Y)\Phi\left(P(Y)g^{-1}gk_{2}^{-1}g^{-1},g\right)m_{s}(k_{1}gk_{2})\,dYdg.

Since
(D^{\times})^{1}
is a normal subgroup of
D^{\times},
by our assumptions on
\Phi
the integral equals

\displaystyle\int_{D^{\times}}\int_{D^{n}}f(Y)\Phi\left(P(Y)g^{-1},g\right)m_{s}(k_{1}gk_{2})\,dYdg.

∎

As in the split case we assume
\Phi
is (bi-)
(D^{\times})^{1}
-invariant in both entries, so
I(f,\Phi,m_{s})
is zero unless
\pi
is unramified by Lemma
6.1
.
The only unitary unramified representations
\pi
of
D^{\times}
are characters
|\mathrm{nrd}|^{it}
where
t\in\mathbb{R}.
Thus for our purposes in this section it is no loss of generality to assume that
\pi
is the trivial representation.

In view of this, we henceforth assume
\pi
is the trivial representation and the matrix coefficient
m
is identically
1.
For
\mathrm{Re}(s)>0
, we have by Fourier inversion

\displaystyle I(f,\Phi,1_{s})=\int_{D}\left(\int_{D^{n}}f(Y)\psi(\langle P(Y),Z\rangle)\,dY\right)\left(\int_{D^{\times}}\mathcal{F}_{1}(\Phi)(-gZ,g)|\mathrm{nrd}(g)|^{s+2}\,dg\right)\,dZ.

We will follow the strategy employed in the split case to prove the analytic continuation of
I(f,\Phi,1_{s}).
Recall the zeta integral

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{Z}(\Phi,s):=\mathcal{Z}_{Z}(\Phi,s,1)=\int_{D^{\times}}\mathcal{F}_{1}(\Phi)(-gZ,g)|\mathrm{nrd}(g)|^{s+2}\,dg,

and

\displaystyle H_{Z}(\Phi,s):=H_{Z}(\Phi,s,1)=\frac{\mathcal{Z}_{Z}(\Phi,s,1)}{\zeta_{D}(s+3/2)}.

Thus

(6.0.1)

\displaystyle\frac{I(f,\Phi,1_{s})}{\zeta_{D}(s+3/2)}=\int_{D}\left(\int_{D^{n}}f(Y)\psi\left(\left\langle P(Y),Z\right\rangle\right)\,dY\right)H_{Z}(\Phi,s)\,dZ.

6.1.

Suppose

\psi

Let

\varpi_{D}\mathcal{O}_{D}

\displaystyle\varpi_{D}\alpha=\overline{\alpha}\varpi_{D}

Let

\displaystyle D=K\oplus K\varpi_{D}=F\oplus F\sqrt{u}\oplus F\varpi_{D}\oplus F\sqrt{u}\varpi_{D},

\varpi_{D}\sqrt{u}=-\sqrt{u}\varpi_{D}

with

as a vector space via the

\displaystyle F^{4}

\displaystyle\tilde{\longrightarrow}D

\displaystyle(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},z_{4})

\displaystyle\longmapsto z_{1}+z_{2}\sqrt{u}+z_{3}\varpi_{D}+z_{4}\sqrt{u}\varpi_{D}.

and define a vector norm on

\displaystyle\|Z\|:=\max(|z_{1}|,|z_{2}|,|z_{3}|,|z_{4}|).

Note that

\displaystyle 1\ll\frac{|\mathrm{nrd}(Z)|^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\left\lVert Z\right\rVert}\leq 1.

Indeed, the right inequality is obvious. To prove the left inequality we may assume that

and some

The set of all

is a compact subset of

Since
\mathrm{nrd}
is anisotropic,

does not vanish on this compact set and hence is bounded below.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let

By changing variables

Write

, for

\displaystyle=2\varpi\left(z_{1}y_{1}^{2}-uz_{1}y_{2}^{2}+\varpi z_{1}y_{3}^{2}+u\varpi z_{1}y_{4}^{2}+2\varpi z_{3}y_{1}y_{3}+2u\varpi z_{4}y_{2}y_{3}-2u\varpi z_{2}y_{3}y_{4}\right)

∎

Lemma 6.3
.

\displaystyle|H_{Z}(\Phi,s)|\ll\max(|\mathrm{nrd}(Z)|,1)^{-(\mathrm{Re}(s)+2)}J(q^{-\mathrm{Re}(s)}).

. Then there exists

∎

Proposition 6.4

Recall that

6.2.

In this subsection

Lemma 6.6

For

Write

with

\mathrm{nrd}(Z)=z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2}+z_{3}^{2}+z_{4}^{2}.
We have

\displaystyle 2^{-1}\mathrm{trd}(Y^{2}Z)=(y_{1}^{2}-y_{2}^{2}-y_{3}^{2}-y_{4}^{2})z_{1}-2y_{1}y_{2}z_{2}-2y_{1}y_{3}z_{3}-2y_{1}y_{4}z_{4}.

After replacing
\psi(x)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}f(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},y_{4})\psi((y_{1}^{2}-y_{2}^{2}-y_{3}^{2}-y_{4}^{2})z_{1}-2y_{1}y_{2}z_{2}-2y_{1}y_{3}z_{3}-2y_{1}y_{4}z_{4})\,dy_{1}dy_{2}dy_{3}dy_{4}.

Lemma 9.3

Proposition 9.4
.

Assume
F=\mathbb{Q}
. Let
\eta\in\mathcal{O}_{D}\cap D^{\times}
be primitive and let
K\geq 1
be such that
K\mid\mathrm{nrd}(\eta)
,
where

is split at all primes dividing

(1)

Suppose that

and
A\eta\equiv 0\bmod{K\mathcal{O}_{D}}

(2)

One has

(3)

\eta\theta\equiv 0\bmod{K\mathcal{O}_{D}}

(4)

Proof.

For (1) and (2), we observe that, by factoring
K
into prime powers, and using the assumption that
D
splits at all prime divisors of
K
, an application of the Chinese Remainder Theorem
renders it sufficient to prove the following local statements, in both of which we assume that
\eta\in M_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F_{v}})\cap\mathrm{GL}_{2}(F_{v})
is primitive, and
K\in\mathcal{O}_{F_{v}}
is such that
K\mid\mathrm{nrd}(\eta)
:

(1’)

Suppose that
A\in M_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F_{v}})
is such that
A\eta\equiv 0\bmod{K}
. Then
K\mid\mathrm{nrd}(A)
.

(2’)

The congruence

\eta\theta\equiv 0\bmod{K}\text{,}

to be solved in
\theta\in M_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F_{v}})
, has exactly
K^{2}
solutions modulo
K
.

To prove (1’), by a Cartan decomposition argument we may assume that
\eta=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\varpi^{k}&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
for some integer
k\geq v(K)
.
If
A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}s&u\\
t&v\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, then the condition
A\eta\equiv 0\bmod{K}
becomes
K\mid u,v
, whence
K\mid sv-tu=\mathrm{nrd}(A)
.
To prove (2’), again by a Cartan decomposition argument we may assume that
\eta=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\varpi^{k}&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, whence if
\theta=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, the congruence to be solved becomes
c\equiv d\equiv 0\bmod{K}
, after which the result is clear. This finishes the proofs of (1’) and (2’), and hence of (1) and (2).

To prove (3), consider the lattice

L=\{\theta\in\mathcal{O}_{D}:\eta\theta\equiv 0\bmod{K}\}\subseteq D\text{.}

Note that
K\mathcal{O}_{D}\subseteq L\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{D}
, and by (2) we have
[L:K\mathcal{O}_{D}]=K^{2}
. Therefore,

\mathrm{covol}(L)\asymp[\mathcal{O}_{D}:L]=\frac{[\mathcal{O}_{D}:K\mathcal{O}_{D}]}{[L:K\mathcal{O}_{D}]}=\frac{K^{4}}{K^{2}}=K^{2}\text{.}

Hence, by Minkowski’s Theorem,
L
contains a nonzero vector of magnitude at most
O(\mathrm{covol}(L)^{1/4})=O(K^{1/2})
, which translates into the desired result.

Finally, to prove (4), consider the lattice

R=\eta\mathcal{O}_{D}+K\mathcal{O}_{D}\text{.}

Note that
K\mathcal{O}_{D}\subseteq R\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{D}
, and

(9.2.1)

\mathrm{covol}(R)\asymp[\mathcal{O}_{D}:R]=\frac{[\mathcal{O}_{D}:K\mathcal{O}_{D}]}{[R:K\mathcal{O}_{D}]}=\frac{K^{4}}{[R:K\mathcal{O}_{D}]}\text{.}

Consider the
\mathbb{Z}
-module homomorphism

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\varphi:&\mathcal{O}_{D}/K\mathcal{O}_{D}&\longrightarrow\mathcal{O}_{D}/K\mathcal{O}_{D}\\
&\theta+K\mathcal{O}_{D}&\longmapsto\eta\theta+K\mathcal{O}_{D}\end{array}

and observe that
[R:K\mathcal{O}_{D}]=|\mathrm{im}(\varphi)|
.
Note that
|\ker(\varphi)|=K^{2}
by (2).
By the First Isomorphism Theorem it follows that

[R:K\mathcal{O}_{D}]=\frac{|\mathcal{O}_{D}/K\mathcal{O}_{D}|}{|\ker(\varphi)|}=\frac{K^{4}}{K^{2}}=K^{2}\text{.}

Inserting this into (
9.2.1
), it follows that

\mathrm{covol}(R)\asymp\frac{K^{4}}{K^{2}}=K^{2}\text{.}

Now Minkowski’s Theorem implies that
R
contains a nonzero vector of magnitude at most
O(\mathrm{covol}(R)^{1/4})=O(K^{1/2})
, as desired.
∎

\left\lVert\eta^{\prime}\right\rVert\ll(m^{\prime})^{1/2}
.

We are almost done, but
\eta^{\prime}\in\mathcal{O}_{D}\cap D^{\times}
need not be primitive.
Write
\eta^{\prime}=z\sigma
, where
z\geq 1
and
\sigma\in\mathcal{O}_{D}\cap D^{\times}
is primitive.
Then
\gcd(m^{\prime},z)\mid\eta\alpha
, since
\eta^{\prime}\in\eta\alpha+m^{\prime}\mathcal{O}_{D}
.
So by Proposition
9.4
(1) with “
(\eta,K,A):=(\eta^{\dagger},\gcd(m^{\prime},z),\alpha^{\dagger})
”, we have
\gcd(m^{\prime},z)\mid\mathrm{nrd}(\alpha)
.
Thus

\begin{split}\mathrm{nrd}(\eta^{\prime})=(\eta^{\prime})(\eta^{\prime})^{\dagger}&\in(\eta\alpha+m^{\prime}\mathcal{O}_{D})((\eta\alpha)^{\dagger}+m^{\prime}\mathcal{O}_{D})\\
&\subseteq\mathrm{nrd}(\eta\alpha)+\gcd(m^{\prime},z)m^{\prime}\mathcal{O}_{D}=\gcd(m^{\prime},z)m^{\prime}\mathcal{O}_{D},\end{split}

since
m^{\prime}\mid\mathrm{nrd}(\eta)
.
But
\mathrm{nrd}(\eta^{\prime})=z^{2}\mathrm{nrd}(\sigma)
, so it follows that

\gcd(m^{\prime},z)m^{\prime}\mid\gcd(z^{2},\gcd(m^{\prime},z)m^{\prime})\mathrm{nrd}(\sigma)=\gcd(m^{\prime},z)^{2}\mathrm{nrd}(\sigma),

whence
m^{\prime}\mid\gcd(m^{\prime},z)\mathrm{nrd}(\sigma)\mid z\,\mathrm{nrd}(\sigma)
.
Yet
\left\lVert\sigma\right\rVert=\left\lVert\eta^{\prime}\right\rVert/z\asymp(m^{\prime})^{1/2}/z
, so we conclude that

m^{\prime}\leq z\,\lvert\mathrm{nrd}(\sigma)\rvert\ll z\left\lVert\sigma\right\rVert^{2}\asymp z(m^{\prime}/z^{2})=m^{\prime}/z.

Thus
z\ll 1
.
In other words,
\eta^{\prime}
is either primitive, or nearly primitive.

Let
K^{\star}:=K^{\prime}/\gcd(z^{2},K^{\prime})
and
m^{\star}:=m^{\prime}/\gcd(z^{2},m^{\prime})
.
Since
z\ll 1
, we have

K^{\star}\asymp K^{\prime},\quad\lvert\mathrm{nrd}(\sigma)\rvert\asymp\lvert\mathrm{nrd}(\eta^{\prime})\rvert\asymp m^{\prime}\asymp m^{\star},\quad\left\lVert\sigma\right\rVert\asymp\left\lVert\eta^{\prime}\right\rVert\asymp(m^{\prime})^{1/2}\asymp(m^{\star})^{1/2}.

Also, since
K^{\prime}\mid m^{\prime}\mid\mathrm{nrd}(\eta^{\prime})=z^{2}\mathrm{nrd}(\sigma)
,
we have
K^{\prime}\mid m^{\prime}\mid\gcd(z^{2},m^{\prime})\mathrm{nrd}(\sigma)
,
whence
K^{\star}\mid m^{\star}\mid\mathrm{nrd}(\sigma)
.
Moreover, since
\eta^{\prime}=z\sigma
, it is clear that

\Lambda(1,K^{\prime},m^{\prime},\eta^{\prime},M_{3})\subseteq\Lambda(1,K^{\star},m^{\star},\sigma,M_{3}),

by (
9.1.9
).
Let
Y:=M_{3}\in\mathcal{O}_{D}
, and take
\phi
to be the composition

\Lambda(H,K,m,\eta,M_{0})\to\Lambda(1,K^{\prime},m^{\prime},\eta,M_{3})\to\Lambda(1,K^{\prime},m^{\prime},\eta^{\prime},M_{3})\to\Lambda(1,K^{\star},m^{\star},\sigma,M_{3}),

where the first arrow is
M\mapsto M/H

and the second and third arrows are inclusion.
This finishes the job, with conditions (1)–(4) all satisfied.
∎

Proof of Lemma
9.3
.

In the notation of Lemma
9.5
, we have

\#\{M\in\Lambda(H,K,m,\eta,M_{0}):\left\lVert M\right\rVert\leq R\}\leq\#\{M\in\Lambda(1,K^{\star},m^{\star},\sigma,Y):\left\lVert M\right\rVert\leq R/H\}.

If (
9.1.10
) holds for
\Lambda(1,K^{\star},m^{\star},\sigma,Y)

\ll 1+\frac{R}{H}+\frac{(R/H)^{2}}{(K^{\star})^{1/2}}+\frac{(R/H)^{3}}{(K^{\star}m^{\star})^{1/2}}+\frac{(R/H)^{4}}{K^{\star}m^{\star}}.

This would imply (
9.1.10
) for
\Lambda(H,K,m,\eta,M_{0})
, because
K^{\star}\asymp K^{\prime}
and
m^{\star}\asymp m^{\prime}
.

Therefore, we are reduced to proving (
9.1.10
) for
\Lambda(1,K^{\star},m^{\star},\sigma,Y)
.
In other words, by Lemma
9.5
,
it suffices to prove Lemma
9.3
under the conditions

(9.2.2)

H=1,\quad\left\lVert\eta\right\rVert\asymp m^{1/2},\quad\lvert\mathrm{nrd}(\eta)\rvert\asymp m,

which we henceforth assume.
Then
(K^{\prime},m^{\prime})=(K,m)
, since
H=1
.

For the rest of the proof, we abbreviate
\Lambda(H,K,m,\eta,M_{0})
to
\Lambda
.
Trivially,

(9.2.3)

m\mathcal{O}_{D}\subseteq\Lambda\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{D},

by (
9.1.9
) and the condition
K\mid m
.
In particular,
\Lambda
is a full lattice in
D_{\infty}\cong\mathbb{R}^{4}
.
We claim that its successive minima
\lambda_{i}>0
(for
1\leq i\leq 4
) satisfy

(1)

\lambda_{1}\gg 1
;

(2)

\lambda_{2}\gg K^{1/2}
;

(3)

\lambda_{4}\ll(Km)^{1/2}
;

(4)

\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}\lambda_{4}\asymp\mathrm{covol}(\Lambda)\gg Km

Assuming the claim, the inequality
(
9.1.10
)
follows from
the classical fact,
recorded in
[
BLBS23
, Lemma 3.6 in the published version; Lemma 3.5 in
arXiv:2006.02356v1

and based on

, Lemma 2]

that if

is a full lattice and

denote its successive minima, and

(9.2.4)

|S\cap\Lambda|\ll_{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{R^{j}}{\lambda_{1}\cdots\lambda_{j}}.

Indeed,
\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}\gg(Km)^{1/2}

Thus we are reduced to proving the claim.

The proof of (1) is immediate from the containment
\Lambda\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{D}
.
For (2) we begin by observing that if

(1), which implies that under these assumptions

, where

Now if

.

We moreover have
\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}\lambda_{4}\asymp\mathrm{covol}(\Lambda)

by Minkowski’s second theorem, as is conveniently recorded in

\mathrm{covol}(\Lambda)\asymp[\mathcal{O}_{D}:\Lambda]=\frac{[\mathcal{O}_{D}:m\mathcal{O}_{D}]}{[\Lambda:m\mathcal{O}_{D}]}=\frac{m^{4}}{[\Lambda:m\mathcal{O}_{D}]}=\frac{m^{4}}{[\tau\Lambda:\mathrm{nrd}(\eta)\mathcal{O}_{D}]},

Appendix A
Birch singular locus

Recall that (
0.0.1
) in
\mathbb{Z}[1/2]

is equivalent to

\gamma_{1}^{2}+\dots+\gamma_{n}^{2}=0
in
\mathbb{H}\otimes\mathbb{Z}[1/2]
.

Proposition A.1
.

Assume
n\geq 6
.
Then the system (
0.0.1
)
defines an affine complete intersection of dimension
4n-4
over
\mathbb{C}
,
with a singular locus of dimension
2n+1
.
Moreover, the Birch singular locus has dimension
2n+2
.

Proof.

Write
\mathbb{H}\otimes\mathbb{C}\cong M_{2}(\mathbb{C})
.
For
A\in M_{2}(\mathbb{C})^{n}
, let
P(A):=A_{1}^{2}+\dots+A_{n}^{2}
.
For any
W\in M_{2}(\mathbb{C})
, view
\mathrm{trd}(WP)
as a polynomial in the
4n
scalar entries of
A
.
By definition, the Birch singular locus
consists of
A\in M_{2}(\mathbb{C})^{n}
for which there exists
W\in M_{2}(\mathbb{C})-\{0\}
with
\nabla{\mathrm{trd}(WP)}=0
.
Moreover, by the Jacobian criterion, the (non-Birch) singular locus
is the intersection of the Birch singular locus with the zero locus
P(A)=0
.
But
P
is diagonal, so

\nabla{\mathrm{trd}(WP)}=(\nabla{\mathrm{trd}(WA_{1}^{2})},\dots,\nabla{\mathrm{trd}(WA_{n}^{2})}).

Therefore, the Birch singular locus equals
\bigcup_{W\in M_{2}(\mathbb{C})-\{0\}}L(W)^{n}
, where

L(W):=\{A\in M_{2}(\mathbb{C}):\nabla{\mathrm{trd}(WA^{2})}=0\}=\{A\in M_{2}(\mathbb{C}):WA+AW=0\}.

The relation
A\in L(W)
is symmetric, i.e.
A\in L(W)\Leftrightarrow W\in L(A)
.

We claim that
\dim(L(W))=\max(2\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{trd}(W)=0},\mathbf{1}_{\det(W)=0})
for all
W\neq 0
.
We check this by casework on Jordan normal form, up to scaling, as follows.
If
W=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\lambda&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, then
L(W)=\{A:\lambda a_{11}=(\lambda+1)a_{12}=(1+\lambda)a_{21}=2a_{22}=0\}
, which has dimension
2\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\lambda=-1}+\mathbf{1}_{\lambda=0}
.
If
W=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&k\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, then
L(W)=\{A:2a_{11}+ka_{21}=2a_{12}+k(a_{11}+a_{22})=2a_{21}=2a_{22}+ka_{21}=0\}=0
.
If
W=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, then
L(W)=\{A:a_{21}=a_{22}+a_{11}=0\}
, which has dimension
2
.

For the eventual (non-Birch) singular locus estimate, we note that the previous paragraph also shows that if
\mathrm{trd}(W)=0
, then
L(W)\subseteq\mathrm{trd}^{-1}(0)
,
even if
\det(W)=0
.
Moreover, if
\mathrm{trd}(W)=0
, then
\det|_{L(W)}\neq 0
, i.e. there exists
A\in L(W)
with
\det(A)\neq 0
.

Next, we claim that if
W_{1},W_{2}\neq 0
and
L(W_{1})\neq L(W_{2})
, then
\dim(L(W_{1})\cap L(W_{2}))\leq 1
.
Indeed,
\dim(L(W_{i}))\leq 2
, so if
\dim(L(W_{1})\cap L(W_{2}))\geq 2
, then necessarily
L(W_{1})=L(W_{2})
.

Finally, we claim that if
W_{1},W_{2}\neq 0
and
L(W_{1})=L(W_{2})\cong\mathbb{C}^{2}
, then
W_{1}\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}W_{2}
.
Indeed, suppose not.
If
L(W_{1})
is spanned by
A_{1},A_{2}\neq 0
, then
W_{1},W_{2}\in L(A_{1})\cap L(A_{2})
, whence
L(A_{1})=L(A_{2})=\mathbb{C}W_{1}+\mathbb{C}W_{2}
.
But
\dim(L(W_{1}))=\dim(L(A_{1}))=2
implies
\mathrm{trd}(W_{1})=\mathrm{trd}(A_{1})=0
, so
L(W_{1}),L(A_{1})\subseteq\mathrm{trd}^{-1}(0)\cong\mathbb{C}^{3}
, whence
L(W_{1})\cap L(A_{1})\ni B\neq 0
, say.
Then
W_{1},W_{2},A_{1},A_{2}\in L(B)
.
Since
\dim(L(B))\leq 2
, we conclude that
\mathbb{C}A_{1}+\mathbb{C}A_{2}=L(B)=\mathbb{C}W_{1}+\mathbb{C}W_{2}
.
By definition of
A_{1},A_{2}
, this means
W_{1},W_{2}\in L(W_{1})=L(W_{2})
.
So for all
A\in L(W_{1})
, we have
A^{2}=0
, whence
\det(A)=0
.
This contradicts a previous observation.

It follows that if
W_{1}\neq W_{2}\in\mathbb{P}^{3}
, then
\dim(L(W_{1})\cap L(W_{2}))\leq 1
.
So
\bigcup_{W_{1}\neq W_{2}\in\mathbb{P}^{3}}(L(W_{1})^{n}\cap L(W_{2})^{n})
has dimension
\leq 3\cdot 2+n\leq 2n
, since
n\geq 6
.
Therefore, the Birch singular locus has dimension exactly

\dim(V_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(\mathrm{trd}))+2n=2n+2
,
by inclusion-exclusion.
Moreover, the intersection of the (non-Birch) singular locus with
L(W)^{n}
is precisely the set
\{A\in L(W)^{n}:\sum_{i}A_{i}^{2}=0\}=\{A\in L(W)^{n}:\sum_{i}\det(A_{i})=0\}
, whenever
\mathrm{trd}(W)=0
.
So the singular locus has dimension exactly

\dim(V_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(\mathrm{trd}))+(2n-1)=2n+1
,
by inclusion-exclusion.
The full system (
0.0.1
) is the union of its singular part, of dimension
2n+1
, and a smooth part, of dimension
4n-4
.
Thus each irreducible component must have dimension exactly
4n-4
.
∎

\begin{split}\bm{1}_{(u,v)\neq 0}=\bm{1}_{\tilde{\gamma}\delta\neq 0},\quad\bm{1}_{u=v=0}=\mathbf{1}_{\tilde{\gamma}\delta=0},\quad\bm{1}_{u=0}\bm{1}_{v\neq 0}=\mathbf{1}_{\delta^{\dagger}\tilde{\gamma}\delta=0}\mathbf{1}_{\tilde{\gamma}\delta\neq 0},\\
