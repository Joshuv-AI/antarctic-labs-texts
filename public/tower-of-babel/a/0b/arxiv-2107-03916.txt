Balanced Allocations with Incomplete Information: The Power of Two Queries
Dimitrios Los, Thomas Sauerwald

Abstract
We consider the allocation of m m balls into n n bins with incomplete information. In the classical Two-Choice process a ball first queries the load of two randomly chosen bins and is then placed in the least loaded bin. In our setting, each ball also samples two random bins but can only estimate a bin’s load by sending binary queries of the form “Is the load at least the median?” or “Is the load at least 100 100 ?”. For the lightly loaded case m = 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ) m=\mathcal{O}(n) , Feldheim and Gurel-Gurevich (2021) showed that with one query it is possible to achieve a maximum load of 𝒪 ⁡ ( log ⁡ n / log ⁡ log ​ n ) \mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\log n/\log\log n}) , and they also pose the question whether a maximum load of m / n + 𝒪 ⁡ ( log ⁡ n / log ⁡ log ​ n ) m/n+\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\log n/\log\log n}) is possible for any m = Ω ⁡ ( n ) m=\Omega(n) . In this work, we resolve this open problem by proving a lower bound of m / n + Ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) m/n+\Omega(\sqrt{\log n}) for a fixed m = Θ ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ n ) m=\Theta(n\sqrt{\log n}) , and a lower bound of m / n + Ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ n / log ⁡ log ⁡ n ) m/n+\Omega(\log n/\log\log n) for some m m depending on the used strategy. We complement this negative result by proving a positive result for multiple queries. In particular, we show that with only two binary queries per chosen bin, there is an oblivious strategy which ensures a maximum load of m / n + 𝒪 ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) m/n+\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\log n}) for any m ⩾ 1 m\geqslant 1 . Further, for any number of k = 𝒪 ⁡ ( log ⁡ log ⁡ n ) k=\mathcal{O}(\log\log n) binary queries, the upper bound on the maximum load improves to m / n + 𝒪 ⁡ ( k ​ ( log ⁡ n ) 1 / k ) m/n+\mathcal{O}(k(\log n)^{1/k}) for any m ⩾ 1 m\geqslant 1 . This result for k k queries has several interesting consequences: ( i ) (i) it implies new bounds for the ( 1 + β ) (1+\beta) -process introduced by Peres, Talwar and Wieder (2015), ( i ​ i ) (ii) it leads to new bounds for the graphical balanced allocation process on dense expander graphs, and ( i ​ i ​ i ) (iii) it recovers and generalizes the bound of m / n + 𝒪 ⁡ ( log ⁡ log ⁡ n ) m/n+\mathcal{O}(\log\log n) on the maximum load achieved by the Two-Choice process, including the heavily loaded case m = Ω ⁡ ( n ) m=\Omega(n) which was derived in previous works by Berenbrink, Czumaj, Steger and Vöcking (2006) as well as Talwar and Wieder (2014). One novel aspect of our proofs is the use of multiple super-exponential potential functions, which might be of use in future work.

Introduction

We study balls-and-bins processes where the goal is to allocate
m
balls (jobs) sequentially into
n
bins (servers). The balls-and-bins framework a.k.a. balanced allocations
[
6
]
is a very popular and simple framework for various resource allocation and storage problems such as load balancing, scheduling or hashing (see surveys
[
30
,
39
]
for more details). In most of these settings, the goal is to find a simple allocation strategy that results in an as balanced allocation as possible.

It is a classical result that if each ball is placed in a random bin chosen independently and uniformly (called
One-Choice
), then the maximum load is
\Theta(\log n/\log\log n)
w.h.p.

1

In general, with high probability refers to probability of at least
1-n^{-c}
for some constant
c>0
.
for
m=n
, and
m/n+\Theta(\sqrt{(m/n)\log n})
w.h.p. for
m\gg n
.
Azar et al.
[
6
]
(and implicitly Karp et al.
[
24
]
) proved that if each ball is placed in the lesser loaded of
two
randomly chosen bins, then the maximum load drops to
\log_{2}\log n+\mathcal{O}(1)
w.h.p., if
m=n
. This dramatic improvement of
Two-Choice
is widely known as “power of two choices”, and similar ideas have been applied to other problems including routing, hashing and randomized rounding
[
30
]
.

While for
m=n
a wide range of different proof techniques
have been employed, the heavily loaded case
m\gg n
turns out to be much more challenging. In a seminal paper
[
10
]
, Berenbrink et al. proved a maximum load of
m/n+\log_{2}\log n+\mathcal{O}(1)
w.h.p. using a sophisticated Markov chain analysis. A simpler and more self-contained proof was recently found by Talwar and Wieder
[
37
]
, giving a slightly weaker upper bound of
m/n+\log_{2}\log n+\mathcal{O}(\log\log\log n)
for the maximum load and at the cost of a larger error probability.

In light of the dramatic improvement of
Two-Choice
(or
d
-Choice
) over
One-Choice
, it is important to understand the robustness of these processes. For example, in a concurrent environment, information about the load of a bin might quickly become outdated or communication with bins might be restricted. Also, acquiring always
d\geqslant 2
uncorrelated choices might be costly in practice. Motivated by this, Peres et al.
[
32
]
introduced the
(1+\beta)
-process, in which two choices are available with probability
\beta
, and otherwise only one. Thus, the
(1+\beta)
-process interpolates nicely between
Two-Choice
and
One-Choice
, and surprisingly, a bound on the gap between maximum and average load
of
\mathcal{O}(\log n/\beta)
w.h.p. was shown, which also holds in the heavily loaded case where
m=\Omega(n)
. The
(1+\beta)
-process has been also connected to other processes, including population protocols
[
3
]
, balls-and-bins with weights
[
36
,
37
]
and, most notably, graphical balanced allocation
[
25
,
32
,
4
,
7
]
. In this graphical model, bins correspond to vertices of a graph, and for each ball we sample an edge uniformly at random and place the ball in the lesser loaded bin of the two endpoints.
Also the results and analysis techniques of the
(1+\beta)
-process have found important connections to population protocols
[
3
]
and balls-and-bins with weights
[
36
,
37
]
.

Our Model.
In this work, we will investigate the following model. At each step, a ball is allowed to sample two random bins chosen independently and uniformly, however, the load comparison between the two bins will be performed under incomplete information. This may capture scenarios in which it is costly to communicate or maintain the exact load of a bin.

Specifically, we assume that each ball is allowed to send up to
k
binary queries to each of the two bins, inquiring about their current load. These queries can either be about the absolute load (i.e., is the load at least
100
?), which we call
threshold processes
, or about the relative load (i.e., is the load at least the median?), which we call
quantile processes
.

We will distinguish between
oblivious
and
adaptive
allocation strategies. For an
adaptive
strategy, the queries may depend on the current load configuration (i.e., the full history of the process), whereas in the
oblivious
setting, queries may depend only on the current time-step.

Our Results.

For the case of
k=1
query, Feldheim and Gurel-Gurevich
[
18
]
proved a bound of
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\log n/\log\log n})
on the gap (between the maximum and average load) in the lightly loaded case
m=\mathcal{O}(n)
. In the same work, the authors suggest that the same bound might be also true in the heavily loaded case
[
18
, Problem 1.3]
. In this work, we disprove this by showing a lower bound of
\Omega(\sqrt{\log n})
on the gap for
m=\Theta(n\sqrt{\log n})
(
4.2
). We also prove a lower bound of
\Omega(\log n/\log\log n)
on the gap, which holds for at least
\Omega(n\log n/\log\log n)
of the time-steps in
[1,n\log^{2}n]
(
4.6
). These two lower bounds hold even for the more general class of adaptive strategies. The basic idea behind all these lower bounds is that, as
m\gg n
, one query is not enough to prevent the process from emulating the
One-Choice
process on a small scale.

It is natural to ask whether we can get an improved performance by allowing more, say
two
queries per bin. We prove that this is indeed the case, establishing a “power of two-queries” result. Specifically, we show in
6.1
that for any
k=\mathcal{O}(\log\log n)
, there is an allocation process with
k
uniform quantiles (i.e., queries only depend on
n
, but not on the time
t
) that achieves for any
m\geqslant 1
:

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gap(m)=\mathcal{O}\Big(k\cdot(\log n)^{1/k}\Big)\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-3}.

Comparing this for
k=2
to the lower bounds for
k=1
, we indeed observe a “power of two-queries” effect. For
k=\Theta(\log\log n)
, the gap even becomes
\mathcal{O}(\log\log n)
, which matches the
Two-Choice
result up to a multiplicative constant
[
10
,
37
]
. Hence, for large values of
k
, the process approximates
Two-Choice
, whereas for
k=1
it resembles the
(1+\beta)
-process. Indeed, the same upper bound of
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
follows from the analysis of the
(1+\beta)
-process (
5.2
).

We also prove new upper bounds on the gap of the
(1+\beta)
process with
\beta
close to
1
by relating it to a relaxed quantile process (
7.1
). We show that these in turn imply new upper bounds on the graphical balanced allocation on dense expander graphs, making progress towards Open Question 2 in
[
32
]
(
7.2
).

Applications and Implications on other Models.

A direct implementation of the
k
-quantile protocol in practice requires to maintain some
global
information about the load configuration (that is, the exact, or at least, the approximate, values of the quantiles). If this can be achieved, then the results of
k
-quantile for
k\geqslant 2
demonstrate that a sub-logarithmic gap is possible – even with very limited
local
information about the individual bin loads.

In addition, our study of the
k
-quantile process also leads to new results for some previously studied allocation processes. We demonstrate that a
(1+\beta)
-process for
\beta
close to
1
is majorized by a (relaxed version of the)
k
-quantile process. For any
\beta=1-o(1)
, this leads to a sub-logarithmic bound on the gap, and if
\beta=1-1/\operatorname{poly}(n)
, we recover the
\mathcal{O}(\log\log n)
gap from the
Two-Choice
process.
Secondly, we use a similar majorization argument to analyze graphical balanced allocation, which has been studied in several works on different graphs
[
32
,
25
,
4
,
7
]
.
Specifically, we prove for dense and strong expander graphs (including random
d
-regular graphs for
d=\operatorname{poly}(n)
) a gap of
\mathcal{O}(\log\log n)
.
To the best of our knowledge, these are the first sub-logarithmic gap bounds in the heavily loaded case for the
(1+\beta)
-process and graphical balanced allocation (apart from
\beta=1
or the graph being a clique, both equivalent to
Two-Choice
).

Further Related Work.

Our model for
k=1
is equivalent to the
d
-
Thinning
process for
d=2
, where for each ball, a random bin is “suggested” and based on the bin’s load, the ball is either allocated there or it is allocated to a second bin chosen uniformly and independently.
Generalizing the results of
[
18
]
for
d=2
, Feldheim and Li
[
20
]
also analyzed an extension of
2
-
Thinning
, called
d
-
Thinning
. For
m=\mathcal{O}(n)
, they proved tight lower and upper bounds, resulting into an achievable gap of
(d+o(1))\cdot\left(d\log n/\log\log n\right)^{1/d}
. Iwama and Kawachi
[
22
]
analyzed a special case of the threshold process for
m=n
and for
k
equally-spaced thresholds, proving a gap of

(k+\mathcal{O}(1))\sqrt[k+1]{(k+1)\frac{\log{n}}{\log\left((k+1)\log{n}\right)}}.

Mitzenmacher
[
29
, Section 5]
coined the term
weak threshold process
for the two threshold process in a queuing setting, where a customer chooses two queues uniformly at random and enters the first one iff it is shorter than
T
. This and previous work
[
15
,
23
,
40
]
analyze the case of a fixed threshold for queues and they do not directly imply results for the heavily loaded case.

In another related work, Alon et al.
[
5
]
established for the case
m=\Theta(n)
a trade-off between the number of bits used for the representation of the load and the number of
d
bin choices. This is a more restricted case of having a fixed number of non-adaptive queries. For
d=2
, Benjamini and Makarychev
[
8
]
obtained tight results for the gap, using a process very similar to the threshold process, but considering the case
m=\Theta(n)
only.

Czumaj and Stemann
[
14
]
investigated general allocation processes, in which the decision whether to take a second (or further) sample depends on the load of the lightest sampled bin. They obtained strong and tight guarantees, but they assume the full information model and also
m=\mathcal{O}(n)
(see
[
11
]
for some results for
m\geqslant n
).
Other processes with inaccurate (or outdated) information about the load of a bin have been studied in an asynchronous environment
[
2
]
or a batch-based allocation
[
9
]
.
However, the obtained bounds on the gap are only
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
. Other protocols that study the communication between balls and bins in more detail are
[
27
,
26
,
17
,
35
]
, but they assume that a ball can sample more than two bins.

After an earlier version of this paper went online, Feldheim, Gurel-Gurevich and Li
[
19
]
extended the lower bounds for
2
-
Thinning
when
m=\mathcal{O}(n\log^{2}n)
and also provided an adaptive thinning process that matches the
\Omega(\log n/\log\log n)
lower bound proved in this paper. Also, Los, Sauerwald and Sylvester
[
28
]
proved that
\textsc{Threshold}(m/n)
achieves w.h.p. a
\Theta(\log n)
gap.

Notation, Definitions and Preliminaries

We sequentially allocate
m
balls (jobs) into
n
bins (servers).
The load vector at step
t
is
x^{(t)}=(x_{1}^{(t)},x_{2}^{(t)},\ldots,x_{n}^{(t)})
and in the beginning,
x_{i}^{(0)}=0
for
i\in[n]
.
Also
y^{(t)}=(y_{1}^{(t)},y_{2}^{(t)},\ldots,y_{n}^{(t)})
will be the permuted load vector, sorted decreasingly in load.
This can be described by
ranks
, which form a permutation of
[n]
that satisfies

r=\Rank^{(t)}(i)\Rightarrow y_{r}^{(t)}=x_{i}^{(t)}.

Following previous work, we analyze allocation processes in terms of the

\Gap(t):=\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}x_{i}^{(t)}-\frac{t}{n}=y_{1}^{(t)}-\frac{t}{n},

i.e., the difference between maximum and average load at time
t\geqslant 0
. It is well-known that even for
Two-Choice
, the gap between maximum and minimum load is
\Omega(\log n)
for large
m
(e.g.
[
32
]
). Here our focus is on sequential allocation processes based on binary queries. That is, at each step
t
:

1.

Sample
two
bins independently and uniformly at random (with replacement).

2.

Send the same
k
binary queries to each of the two bins about their load.

3.

Allocate the ball in the lesser loaded one of the two bins (based on the answers to the queries), breaking ties randomly.

i_{1}=2

. (

We first describe
threshold-based processes
, where queries to each bin
j
are of the type “Is
x_{j}^{(t)}\geqslant f(t)
” for some function
f
that maps into
\mathbb{N}
. For example, we could ask whether the load of a bin is at least the average load. Formally, we denote such a process with two choices and
k
queries by
\textsc{Threshold}(f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{k})
, where
f_{1}>f_{2}>\ldots>f_{k}
are
k
different load thresholds, that may depend on the time
t
, in which case we write
f_{i}(t)
. After sending all
k
queries to a bin
j
, we receive the correct answers to all these queries and then we determine the
i
(
0\leqslant i\leqslant k
) for which,

x_{j}^{(t)}\in(f_{i+1}(t),f_{i}(t)],

where
f_{0}(t)=+\infty
and
f_{k+1}(t)=-\infty
(see
Fig.

1
). After having obtained two such numbers
i_{1},i_{2}\in\{0,1,\ldots,k\}
, one for each bin
j_{1}
and
j_{2}
, we will allocate the ball “greedily”, i.e., into
j_{1}
if
i_{1}<i_{2}
and into
j_{2}
if
i_{1}>i_{2}
. If
i_{1}=i_{2}
, then we will break ties randomly.

We proceed to define
quantile-based processes
. In this process, queries to a bin
j
are of the type “Is
x_{j}^{(t)}\geqslant y_{\delta(t)\cdot n}^{(t)}
?”, for some function
\delta
that maps
t
into
\{1/n,2/n,\ldots,1\}
. For example if
\delta=1/2
, we are querying whether the load of a bin is at most the median load. We denote such a process with two choices and
k
queries by
\textsc{Quantile}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\ldots,\delta_{k})
, where
\delta_{1}<\delta_{2}<\ldots<\delta_{k}
are
k
different quantiles, which may depend on the time
t
. After sending all
k
queries to a bin
j
in step
t
, we receive the correct answers and then we determine the
i
(
0\leqslant i\leqslant k
) for which,

\Rank^{(t)}(j)\in(\delta_{i}(t)\cdot n,\delta_{i+1}(t)\cdot n],

where
\delta_{0}(t)=0
and
\delta_{k+1}(t)=1
. As before, we allocate the ball to the bin with smaller
i
-value and break ties randomly.

Quantile
and
Threshold
processes can be classified into oblivious processes and adaptive processes, depending on the type of queries. In an
oblivious process
, the queries
f_{1},f_{2},\ldots
(or
\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\ldots
) may only depend on
t
(as well as
n
) —a special case is a
uniform process
where
\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\ldots
are constants (independent of
t
), and the
f_{i}
’s are of the form
t/n+f_{i}(n)
.
In an
adaptive process
, queries in step
t
may depend on the full history of the process, i.e., the load vector
x^{(t-1)}
, so each query
i
involves a function
f_{i}(x^{(t-1)})
, but this must be specified before receiving any answers. In the adaptive setting, a
k
-quantile process can simulate any
k
-threshold process, by setting the quantile to the largest
\delta_{i}(t)
such that
y_{\delta_{i}(t)\cdot n}\leqslant f_{i}(t)
(
3.7
).

The
d
-
Thinning
process

[
18
]
works as follows. For each ball to be allocated, an overseer can inspect up to
d
randomly sampled bins in an online fashion, and based on all previous history, can accept or reject each bin (however, one of the
d
proposed bins must be accepted).

The
d
-Choice
process

[
6
]
(sometimes also called
\textsc{Greedy}[d]
) is the process where, for each ball,
d
bins are chosen uniformly at random and the ball is placed in the least loaded bin. We will refer to the special case
d=1
as the
One-Choice
process
, and
d=2
as the
Two-Choice
process
. The
(1+\beta)
-process

[
32
]
is the process where each ball is placed with probability
\beta
according to
Two-Choice
and with probability
1-\beta
according to
One-Choice
.

Finally, in
graphical balanced allocation

[
25
,
32
]
, we are given an undirected graph
G
with
n
vertices corresponding to
n
bins. For each ball to be allocated, we select an edge
\{u,v\}\in E(G)
uniformly at random, and place the ball in the lesser loaded bin among
\{u,v\}
. As pointed out in
[
32
]
, this setting can be generalized even to hypergraphs.

Following
[
32
]
and generalizing the processes above,
an allocation process
can be described by a
probability vector

p^{(t)}=(p_{1}^{(t)},p_{2}^{(t)},\ldots,p_{n}^{(t)})
for step
t
, where
p_{i}^{(t)}
is the probability for incrementing the load of the
i
-th most loaded bin. Following the idea of
majorization
(see Lemma
A.5
), if two processes with (time-invariant) probability vectors
p
and
q
, for all
i\in[n]
satisfy
\sum_{j\leqslant i}p_{j}\leqslant\sum_{j\leqslant i}q_{j}
, then there is a coupling between the allocation processes with sorted load vectors
y(p)
and
y(q)
such that
\sum_{j\leqslant i}y_{i}^{(t)}(p)\leqslant\sum_{j\leqslant i}y_{i}^{(t)}(q)
for all
i\in[n]
(
q
majorizes
p
).

Finally, we define the
height
of a ball as
i\geqslant 1
if it is the
i
th
ball added to the bin.

Many statements in this work hold only for sufficiently large
n
, and several constants are chosen generously with the intention of making it easier to verify some technical inequalities.

:

\displaystyle p_{i}=\begin{cases}\frac{\delta_{1}}{n}&1\leqslant i\leqslant\delta_{1}\cdot n,\\
\frac{\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}}{n}&\delta_{1}\cdot n<i\leqslant\delta_{2}\cdot n,\\
\ \ \ \vdots&\\
\frac{\delta_{k-1}+\delta_{k}}{n}&\delta_{k-1}\cdot n<i\leqslant\delta_{k}\cdot n,\\
\frac{1+\delta_{k}}{n}&\delta_{k}\cdot n<i.\end{cases}

(3.1)

The next lemma shows that we can always execute the
\textsc{Quantile}(\delta)
in the same way as
2
-
Thinning
:

Lemma 3.1
.

Consider a quantile process
\textsc{Quantile}(\delta)
with one query. This process can be always transformed into an equivalent instance of
2
-
Thinning
: Sample a bin, if its rank is greater than
n\cdot\delta(t)
, then place the ball there; otherwise, place the ball in a randomly chosen bin.

Proof.

Let
p
be the probability vector of the
\textsc{Quantile}(\delta)
process and let
q
be the probability vector of the
2
-
Thinning
process described in the statement of the lemma. We will show that
p=q
. Let
R
denote the set of bins with rank
>n\cdot\delta(t)
. Let
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
be the two bin choices at some time step. We consider two cases, based on the rank of bin
i\in[n]
.

Case 1 (light bin):

i
has rank
>n\cdot\delta(t)
, then

\displaystyle p_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,B_{1}=i,B_{2}\in R\,\right]+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,B_{1}\in R,B_{2}=i\,\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,B_{1}=i,B_{2}\notin R\,\right]+\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,B_{1}\notin R,B_{2}=i\,\right]

\displaystyle=\left(1-\delta(t)\right)\cdot\frac{1}{n}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\cdot 2+2\cdot\frac{1}{n}\cdot\delta(t)=\frac{\delta(t)+1}{n},

\displaystyle q_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,B_{1}=i\,\right]+\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,B_{1}\notin R,B_{2}=i\,\right]=\frac{1}{n}+\delta(t)\cdot\frac{1}{n}=\frac{\delta(t)+1}{n}.

Case 2 (heavy bin):

i
has rank
\leqslant n\cdot\delta(t)
, then

\displaystyle p_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,B_{1}=i,B_{2}\notin R\,\right]+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,B_{1}\notin R,B_{2}=i\,\right]=\frac{1}{2}\cdot 2\cdot\frac{1}{n}\cdot\delta(t)=\frac{\delta(t)}{n},

q_{i}=\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,B_{1}\in R,B_{2}=i\,\right]=\delta(t)\cdot\frac{1}{n}.

∎

Similarly, for
\textsc{Threshold}{(f)}

Lemma 3.2
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to the one of

to be the set of bins with load less than
f(t)
.

Observation 3.3
.

For any
n\geqslant 0
, the
\textsc{Quantile}{(\frac{1}{n},\frac{2}{n}\ldots,\frac{n-1}{n})}

Two-Choice

Proof.

The probability vector of the
\textsc{Quantile}{(\frac{1}{n},\frac{2}{n}\ldots,\frac{n-1}{n})}

Two-Choice

p_{i}=\frac{\delta_{i-1}+\delta_{i}}{n}=\frac{i-1+i}{n^{2}}=\frac{2i-1}{n^{2}},

where we have used
\delta_{0}=0
and
\delta_{n}=1
for convenience.
∎

Observation 3.4
.

For
k<n-1
, for any
\delta^{\prime},\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{k}
quantiles, the
\textsc{Quantile}{(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{k})}

\textsc{Quantile}{(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{i},\delta^{\prime},\allowbreak\delta_{i+1},\ldots,\delta_{k})}

Proof.

Consider the
\textsc{Quantile}{(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{i},\delta^{\prime},\delta_{i+1},\ldots,\delta_{k})}

\textsc{Quantile}{(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{k})}

∎

By combining

Corollary 3.5
.

Any
\textsc{Quantile}{(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{k})}

Given any
\textsc{Quantile}{(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{k})}

, by

.
∎

Lemma 3.6

Lemma 3.7
.

Lemma 3.8
.

Any step
t
of a
\textsc{Quantile}{(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{k})}
process can be simulated by first choosing
f_{1}(t),f_{2}(t),\ldots,f_{k}(t)
randomly (from a suitable distribution depending on
x^{(t)}
and
\delta_{1}(t),\ldots,\delta_{k}(t)
) and then running
\textsc{Threshold}(f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{k})
.

In other words, there is a reduction from
Quantile
to adaptive
Threshold
, but the
Threshold
process must have the ability to randomize between different instances of
Threshold
.

Proof.

Figure 4
:
The threshold process which uses a threshold of
y_{n\cdot\delta}
probability
\alpha

y_{n\cdot\delta}+1

1-\alpha
, corresponds to mixing the probability vectors of
\textsc{Quantile}{(\delta^{1})}

\textsc{Quantile}{(\delta^{2})}

\textsc{Quantile}{(\delta)}
only in the region
(n\cdot\delta_{1},n\cdot\delta_{2}]

. Hence, the effect of the two processes is indistinguishable.

Let us first prove the claim for
k=1
, that is,

\textsc{Quantile}{(\delta)}
can be simulated by an adaptive randomized threshold process with one threshold. Since we only analyze one time-step
t
, we will for simplicity omit this dependency and write
\delta=\delta(t)
.

Let
\delta^{1}
be the quantile where the values equal to
y_{n\cdot\delta}
start and
\delta^{2}
, where they end (so
\delta^{1}\leqslant\delta\leqslant\delta^{2}
). Sampling between a threshold of
y_{n\cdot\delta}
and
y_{n\cdot\delta}+1
with probability
\alpha\in[0,1]
interpolates between the
\textsc{Quantile}{(\delta^{1})}
and
\textsc{Quantile}{(\delta^{2})}

p^{1}
and
p^{2}
be the probability vectors for
\textsc{Quantile}{(\delta^{1})}
and
\textsc{Quantile}{(\delta^{2})}

for this adaptive randomized threshold process is given by,

q_{i}=\alpha\cdot p_{i}^{1}+(1-\alpha)\cdot p_{i}^{2}.

At
i\leqslant n\cdot\delta^{1}\leqslant n\cdot\delta^{2}
, we have,

q_{i}=\alpha\cdot\frac{\delta^{1}}{n}+(1-\alpha)\cdot\frac{\delta^{2}}{n}.

We pick
\alpha=\frac{\delta^{2}-\delta}{\delta^{2}-\delta^{1}}\in[0,1]
so that

for
i\leqslant n\cdot\delta^{1}
. Then for
i\geqslant n\cdot\delta^{2}\geqslant n\cdot\delta^{1}
, we get

q_{i}=\alpha\cdot\frac{1+\delta^{1}}{n}+(1-\alpha)\cdot\frac{1+\delta^{2}}{n}=\frac{\alpha+(1-\alpha)}{n}+\frac{\alpha\cdot\delta^{1}+(1-\alpha)\cdot\delta^{2}}{n}=\frac{1+\delta}{n},

by the choice of
\alpha
. So at the indices
i\in[n]\setminus(n\delta^{1},n\delta^{2}]
agree with
\textsc{Quantile}{(\delta)}
.

At the indices
n\cdot\delta^{1}<i\leqslant n\cdot\delta^{2}
, the probability is shared between bins with the same load, so the effect is indistinguishable (see
Fig.

4

We will extend this idea to
k>1

\delta_{j}
with a mixture of two thresholds
y_{n\cdot\delta_{j}}
and
y_{n\cdot\delta_{j}}+1

\alpha_{j}
. For this, we define
\delta_{j}^{1}
and
\delta_{j}^{2}

To argue that there exist coefficients
\alpha_{j}
such that the two processes are equivalent, we start with the probability vector
q
of the
\textsc{Quantile}{(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{k})}

In each step we create probability vectors
p^{1j}
and
p^{2j}

\delta_{j}^{1}
and
\delta_{j}^{2}
respectively to
q^{j-1}

(n\cdot\delta_{j-1},n\cdot\delta_{j+1}]

\alpha_{j}:=\frac{\delta_{j}^{2}-\delta_{j}}{\delta_{j}^{2}-\delta_{j}^{1}}

q_{i}^{j}=\alpha_{j}\cdot\left(\frac{\delta_{j-1}+\delta_{j}^{1}}{n}\right)+(1-\alpha_{j})\cdot\left(\frac{\delta_{j-1}+\delta_{j}^{2}}{n}\right)=\frac{\delta_{j-1}}{n}+\alpha_{j}\cdot\frac{\delta_{j}^{1}}{n}+(1-\alpha_{j})\cdot\frac{\delta_{j}^{2}}{n}=\frac{\delta_{j-1}+\delta_{j}}{n}=q_{i},

and for

q_{i}^{j}=\alpha_{j}\cdot\left(\frac{\delta_{j}^{1}+\delta_{j+1}}{n}\right)+(1-\alpha_{j})\cdot\left(\frac{\delta_{j}^{2}+\delta_{j+1}}{n}\right)=\frac{\delta_{j+1}}{n}+\frac{\alpha_{j}\delta_{j}^{1}+(1-\alpha_{j})\delta_{j}^{2}}{n}=\frac{\delta_{j-1}+\delta_{j}}{n}=q_{i}.

The linear weighting preserves the following property: Let
B
be a set of bins, then if
\sum_{b\in B}p_{b}^{1j}=\sum_{b\in B}p_{b}^{2j}
then
\sum_{b\in B}q_{b}^{j}=\sum_{b\in B}p_{b}^{1j}=\sum_{b\in B}p_{b}^{2j}
. This implies that:

1.

If
p_{i}^{1j}=p_{i}^{2j}
, then
q_{i}^{j}=p_{i}^{1j}=p_{i}^{2j}
.

2.

Let
B_{x}
be the set of bins in
[1,\delta_{j-1}\cdot n]
with equal load

q^{j}
the probability of allocating a ball to

will be the same as in that of
q
.

Hence, this ensures that each step extends the agreement of probability vector
q^{j}
and
q
to each bin

. The only possible exceptions are bins with equal load, where the probability mass is just rearranged among them. Hence,
q^{k}
will be equivalent to
q
for the given load vector.∎

.

For any

, a

(2k)
-

Proof.

We may assume that

, then send the largest to bin

, then send the second largest to bin

Further, let

and
i_{2}
be two chosen bins, and
\widetilde{i}

Note that

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Rank^{(t)}(\widetilde{i})\leqslant n\cdot\gamma_{j}\,\right]=\gamma_{j}\cdot\gamma_{j}.

since

if and only if both bins

and

4
Lower Bounds for One Quantile and One Threshold

In the lightly loaded case (i.e.,
m=n
),
[
18
]
proved an upper bound of
(2+o(1))\cdot(\sqrt{2\log n/\log\log n})
on the maximum load for a uniform
Threshold
(f)
-process with
f=\sqrt{2\log n/\log\log n}
(
[
20
]
extended this to
d>2
). They also proved that this strategy is asymptotically optimal. In
[
18
, Problem 1.3]
, the authors suggest that the
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\log n/\log\log n})
bound on the gap extends to the heavily loaded case. Here we will disprove this, establishing a slightly larger lower bound of
\Omega(\sqrt{\log n})
(
4.2
). We also derive additional lower bounds (
4.1
and
4.6
) that demonstrate that any
Quantile
or
Threshold
process will “frequently” attain a gap which is even as large as
\Omega(\log n/\log\log n)
.

Let us describe the intuition behind this bound in case of uniform quantiles, neglecting some technicalities. Consider
\textsc{Quantile}(\delta)
and the equivalent
2
-
Thinning
instance where a ball is placed in the first bin if its load is among the
(1-\delta)\cdot n
lightest bins, and otherwise it is placed in a new (second) bin chosen uniformly at random (Lemma
3.1
). We have two cases:

Case 1:
We choose most times a “large”
\delta
. Then we allocate approximately
m\cdot\delta
balls to their second bin choice which is uniform over all
n
bins. This will lead to a behavior close to
One-Choice
(
4.3
).

Case 2:
We choose most times a “small”
\delta
. Then we allocate approximately
m\cdot(1-\delta)
balls with the first bin choice, which is a
One-Choice
process over the
n\cdot(1-\delta)
lightest bins. As we establish in
4.4
, for small
\delta
there are simply “too many” light bins that will reach a high load level, so the process is again close to
One-Choice
.

Theorem 4.1
.

For any adaptive
\textsc{Quantile}(\delta)
(or
Threshold
(f)
) process,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\max_{t\in[1,n\log^{2}n]}\Gap(t)\geqslant\frac{1}{8}\cdot\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}\,\right]\geqslant 1-o(n^{-2}).

In fact, as shown in
4.6
, this lower bound holds for a significant proportion of time-steps.
We also show a lower bound for fixed
m
, which is derived in a similar way
as
4.1
, but with a different parameterization of “large” and “small” quantiles:

Theorem 4.2
.

For any adaptive
\textsc{Quantile}(\delta)
(or
Threshold
(f)
) process, with
m=K\cdot n\sqrt{\log n}
balls for
K=1/10
, it holds that

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gap(m)\geqslant\frac{1}{20}\sqrt{\log n}\,\right]\geqslant 1-o(n^{-2}).

4.1
Preliminaries for Lower Bounds

Let us first formalize the intuition of the lower bound. Recall that we will analyze the adaptive case, which means that the quantiles at each step
t
may depend on the full history of the process, or, equivalently, on the load vector
(x_{1}^{(t-1)},x_{2}^{(t-1)},\ldots,x_{n}^{(t-1)})
. We also remind the reader that any adaptive
Threshold
(f)
process can be simulated by
\textsc{Quantile}(\delta)
(
3.7
), which is why we will do the analysis below for
\textsc{Quantile}(\delta)
only.

The next lemma proves that if within
n
consecutive allocations a large quantile is used too often, then
\textsc{Quantile}(\delta)
restricted to the heavily loaded bins generates a high maximum load, similar to
One-Choice
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Consider any adaptive
\textsc{Quantile}(\delta)
process during the time-interval
[t,t+n)
. If
\textsc{Quantile}(\delta)
allocates at least
n/(\log n)^{2}
balls with a quantile larger than
(\log n)^{-2}
in
[t,t+n)
, then

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gap(t+n)\geqslant\frac{1}{8}\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}\,\right]\geqslant 1-o(n^{-4}).

Proof.

Assume there are at least
n/(\log n)^{2}
allocations with quantile larger than
(\log n)^{-2}
. Then, using
A.1
, w. p. at least
1-o(n^{-4})
, at least
\frac{1}{e}\frac{n}{\log^{2}n}\cdot\frac{1}{\log^{2}{n}}\geqslant\frac{n}{\log^{5}n}
balls are thrown using
One-Choice
.

Consider now the load configuration before the
batch
, i.e. the next
n
balls are allocated. If
\Gap(t)\geqslant\log n
, then
\Gap(t+n)\geqslant\frac{1}{8}\log n/\log\log n
, as a load can decrease by at most
1
in
n
steps. So we can assume
\Gap(t)<\log n
. Let
B
be the set of bins whose load is at least the average load at time
t
, then
|B|\geqslant n/\log n
. Using
A.1
, w. p. at least
1-o(n^{-4})
the batch will allocate at least
n/(\log n)^{6}
balls to the bins of
B
. Hence, by
A.7
, at least one bin in
B
will increase its load by an additive factor of
\frac{1}{7}\log n/\log\log n
w. p. at least
1-o(n^{-4})
. Since the average load only increases by one during the batch, there will be a gap of
\frac{1}{8}\log n/\log\log n
w.h.p., and our claim is established.
∎

The next lemma implies that if for most allocations the largest quantile is too small, then the allocations on the lightest bins follows that of
One-Choice
, and we end up with a high maximum load.

Lemma 4.4
.

Consider any adaptive
\textsc{Quantile}(\delta)
process with
m=n\log^{2}n
balls that allocates at most
n
balls with a quantile larger than
(\log n)^{-2}
. Then,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gap(m)\geqslant 0.2\log n\,\right]\geqslant 1-o(n^{-2}).

The proof of this Lemma is similar to
4.3
, but a bit more complex. We define a coupling between the
\textsc{Quantile}(\delta)
process and the
One-Choice
process. We couple the allocation of balls whose first sample is among the
(1-\delta(t))\cdot n
-lightest bins with a
One-Choice
process.
The balls whose first sample is among the
\delta(t)\cdot n
-heaviest bins are allocated differently, and cause our process to diverge from an original
One-Choice
process. However, we prove that the number of different allocations is too small to change the order of the gap.

Proof.

We will use the following coupling between the allocations of
\textsc{Quantile}{(\delta)}
and
One-Choice
. At each step

Theorem 5.2

\textsc{Quantile}(\delta)
with
\delta\in[1/3,2/3]

37
]
derives the same gap bound up to an additive
\mathcal{O}(\log\log\log n)
term, but the error probability is much larger, i.e.,
\Theta((\log\log n)^{-4})
.
In comparison to their bound, our result achieves a much smaller error probability of
\mathcal{O}(n^{-3})
, but it comes at the cost of a multiplicative constant in the gap bound.

6.2
Relaxed Quantile Process and Outline of the Inductive Step

We now define a class of processes
\textsc{Relaxed-Quantile}_{\gamma}(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{k})
, which relaxes the definition of
\textsc{Quantile}{(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{k})}
, with
1\leqslant k\leqslant\kappa\log\log n
and a relaxation factor
\gamma\geqslant 1
. The probability vector
p
of such a process satisfies four conditions:
(i)
, for each
i\in[n]
,

p_{i}\leqslant\begin{cases}\gamma\cdot\frac{\delta_{1}}{n}&\text{for }1\leqslant i\leqslant\delta_{1}\cdot n,\\
\gamma\cdot\frac{\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}}{n}&\text{for }\delta_{1}\cdot n<i\leqslant\delta_{2}\cdot n,\\
\ \ \ \vdots&\\
\gamma\cdot\frac{\delta_{k-1}+\delta_{k}}{n}&\text{for }\delta_{k-1}\cdot n<i\leqslant\delta_{k}\cdot n,\\
\end{cases}

(ii)
the probability vector
p
is non-decreasing in
i
,
(iii)

p_{n/3}\leqslant\frac{1-4\varepsilon}{n}
and,
(iv)

p_{2n/3}\geqslant\frac{1+4\varepsilon}{n}
for some
0<\varepsilon<1/4
. Note that the process
\textsc{Quantile}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\ldots,\delta_{k})
with the
\delta_{i}
’s as defined above falls into this class with
\gamma=1
and
\varepsilon=1/12
(cf.
Eq.

3.1
).

Theorem 6.6

(generalization of Theorem
6.1
)
.

Consider a
\textsc{Relaxed-Quantile}_{\gamma}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\ldots,\delta_{k})
process with the
\delta_{i}

2\leqslant k\leqslant\kappa\log\log n
and
1\leqslant\gamma\leqslant 6
. Then for any

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gap(m)\leqslant 1000\cdot k\cdot(\log n)^{1/k}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-3}.

Reduction of
6.6
to
6.7
.

The proof of
6.6
employs some type of layered induction over
k
different, super-exponential potential functions. Generalizing the definition of
\Phi_{0}^{(s)}
from Section
5
, for any
0\leqslant j\leqslant k-1
:

\Phi_{j}^{(s)}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\exp\Big(\alpha_{2}\cdot(\log n)^{j/k}\cdot\Big(x_{i}^{(s)}-\frac{s}{n}-\frac{2}{\alpha_{2}}j(\log n)^{1/k}\Big)^{+}\Big),

where
\alpha_{2}=0.0002
(recall
z^{+}=\max\{z,0\}
).
We will then employ this series of potential functions

to analyze the process over the time-interval
s\in[m-n\log^{5}n,m]

The next lemma (
6.7
) formalizes this inductive argument. It shows that if for all steps

within some suitable time-interval, the number of balls of height at least
\frac{s}{n}+\frac{2}{\alpha_{2}}j(\log n)^{1/k}

\Phi_{j}

\Theta(n\log^{3}n)
term. Finally, for

, we can conclude that at step

, there are no balls of height
\frac{s}{n}+\frac{2}{\alpha_{2}}k(\log n)^{1/k}
. Hence we can infer that the gap is
\mathcal{O}(k\cdot(\log n)^{1/k})
, and
6.1

Section

6.6
for the complete proof of this step).

Lemma 6.7

(

Assume that for some

6.4.1
Analysis of the Drop of the Potentials
\Phi_{j}
and
\Psi_{j}

We define
\alpha_{j}^{(s)}:=\frac{s}{n}+\frac{2}{\alpha_{2}}\cdot j(\log n)^{1/k}
, so that when
\mathcal{E}_{j-1}^{(s)}
holds, then
y_{n\cdot\delta_{k-j}}^{(s)}\leqslant\alpha_{j}^{(s)}-1
; this will be established in the next lemma below.

Lemma 6.8
.

For any step
s\geqslant 1
, if
\mathcal{E}_{j-1}^{(s)}
holds then
y_{n\cdot\delta_{k-j}}^{(s)}\leqslant\alpha_{j}^{(s)}-1
.

Proof.

Assuming the opposite
y_{n\cdot\delta_{k-j}}^{(s)}>\alpha_{j}^{(s)}-1
, we conclude

\displaystyle\Phi_{j-1}^{(s)}

\displaystyle\geqslant\sum_{i=1}^{n\cdot\delta_{k-j}}\exp\Big(\alpha_{2}(\log n)^{(j-1)/k}\cdot\Big(\alpha_{j}^{(s)}-\frac{s}{n}-\frac{2}{\alpha_{2}}\cdot(j-1)(\log n)^{1/k}\Big)^{+}\Big)

\displaystyle\geqslant n\cdot 2^{-0.5(\log n)^{j/k}}\cdot e^{\alpha_{2}\cdot(\log{n})^{(j-1)/k}\cdot\frac{2}{\alpha_{2}}(\log n)^{1/k}}

\displaystyle\geqslant n\cdot 2^{-0.5(\log n)^{j/k}}\cdot e^{2\cdot(\log{n})^{j/k}}

\displaystyle>2cn,

since
(e^{2}\cdot 2^{-0.5})^{2\cdot(\log{n})^{j/k}}>(e^{2}\cdot 2^{-0.5})^{10^{4}}>2c
for sufficiently large
n
, which contradicts
\mathcal{E}_{j-1}^{(s)}
.
∎

Lemma 6.9
.

For any step
s\geqslant\beta_{j-1}=t+2jn\log^{3}n
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Phi_{j}^{(s+1)}\,\big|\,\mathcal{E}_{j-1}^{(s)},\Phi_{j}^{(s)}\,\right]\leqslant\Big(1-\frac{1}{n}\Big)\cdot\Phi_{j}^{(s)}+2,

and

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Psi_{j}^{(s+1)}\,\big|\,\mathcal{E}_{j-1}^{(s)},\Psi_{j}^{(s)}\,\right]\leqslant\Big(1-\frac{1}{n}\Big)\cdot\Psi_{j}^{(s)}+2.

Proof.

We will prove the statement for the potential function
\Psi_{j}
. The same proof holds for
\Phi_{j}
, since the only steps dependent on the coefficients (
\alpha_{1}
vs.
\alpha_{2}
) are
6.8
to obtain the bound
y_{n\cdot\delta_{k-j}}<\alpha_{j}^{(t)}-1
and the facts that
2^{0.5}>e^{0.01}>e^{\alpha_{2}}
and
0.5\cdot\alpha_{1}\cdot(\log n)^{j/k}>0.5\cdot\alpha_{2}\cdot(\log n)^{j/k}>1.2
(which also hold for
\alpha_{2}
).

In the following part of the proof, we will break down
\Psi_{j}^{(s+1)}
(and, similarly,
\Psi_{j}^{(s)}
) as follows:

\displaystyle\Psi_{j}^{(s+1)}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Psi_{j,i}^{(s+1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\exp\left(0.01(\log n)^{j/k}\cdot(x_{i}^{(s+1)}-\alpha_{j}^{(s+1)})^{+}\right).

Then we will split this sum into bins that have load at least (or less than)
\alpha_{j}^{(s)}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\Psi_{j}^{(s+1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\colon x_{i}^{(s)}\geqslant\alpha_{j}^{(s)}}\Psi_{j,i}^{(s+1)}+\sum_{i\colon x_{i}^{(s)}<\alpha_{j}^{(s)}}\Psi_{j,i}^{(s+1)}.

After that we will apply linearity of expectation to bound the expected value of the potential at step
s+1
.

Case 1
: First, consider the contribution of a bin
i
with
x_{i}^{(s)}\geqslant\alpha_{j}^{(s)}
to
\Psi_{j}^{(s+1)}
,

\Psi_{j,i}^{(s+1)}=\begin{cases}\exp{\left(0.01(\log{n})^{j/k}(1-\frac{1}{n})\right)}\Psi_{j,i}^{(s)}&\quad\text{if }x_{i}^{(s+1)}=x_{i}^{(s)}+1,\\
\exp{\left(0.01(\log{n})^{j/k}\left(-\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)}\Psi_{j,i}^{(s)}&\quad\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Define
u_{i}:=\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,x_{i}^{(s+1)}=x_{i}^{(s)}+1\,\big|\,\mathcal{E}_{j-1}^{(s)},\Psi_{j}^{(s)}\,\right]
. We have:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Psi_{j,i}^{(s+1)}\,\big|\,\mathcal{E}_{j-1}^{(s)},\Psi_{j}^{(s)}\,\right]=e^{0.01(\log{n})^{j/k}(1-\frac{1}{n})}\cdot\Psi_{j,i}^{(s)}\cdot u_{i}+e^{0.01(\log{n})^{j/k}(-\frac{1}{n})}\cdot\Psi_{j,i}^{(s)}\cdot(1-u_{i})

\displaystyle\quad=\left(e^{0.01(\log{n})^{j/k}(1-\frac{1}{n})}-e^{0.01(\log{n})^{j/k}(-\frac{1}{n})}\right)\cdot\Psi_{j,i}^{(s)}\cdot u_{i}+e^{0.01(\log{n})^{j/k}(-\frac{1}{n})}\cdot\Psi_{j,i}^{(s)}.

Note that since
y_{n\cdot\delta_{k-j}}^{(s)}\leqslant\alpha_{j}^{(s)}-1
(see
6.8
), bin
i
must be among the
(\delta_{k-j})
-th heaviest bins. To increment the load of bin
i
,
i
has to be one of the two randomly chosen bins, and the other choice must be a bin whose load is at most
y_{n\cdot\delta_{k-j}}^{(s)}
, hence

u_{i}\leqslant\frac{2}{n}\delta_{k-j}\leqslant\frac{3\gamma}{n}\widetilde{\delta}_{k-j}\leqslant\frac{18}{n}\widetilde{\delta}_{k-j}
(see
6.10
below this lemma for details), which yields

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Psi_{j,i}^{(s+1)}\,\big|\,\mathcal{E}_{j-1}^{(s)},\Psi_{j}^{(s)}\,\right]

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant e^{0.01(\log{n})^{j/k}(1-\frac{1}{n})}\cdot\Psi_{j,i}^{(s)}\cdot\frac{18}{n}\widetilde{\delta}_{k-j}+e^{0.01(\log{n})^{j/k}(-\frac{1}{n})}\cdot\Psi_{j,i}^{(s)}

\displaystyle\quad=e^{-0.01(\log{n})^{j/k}\frac{1}{n}}\cdot\Psi_{j,i}^{(s)}\cdot\left(1+\frac{18}{n}\cdot\frac{e^{0.01(\log{n})^{j/k}}}{2^{0.5(\log{n})^{j/k}}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\Psi_{j,i}^{(s)}\cdot\left(1-0.6\cdot\frac{0.01}{n}\cdot(\log n)^{j/k}\right)\cdot\left(1+\frac{18}{n}\cdot\left(\frac{e^{0.01}}{2^{0.5}}\right)^{(\log{n})^{j/k}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad<\Psi_{j,i}^{(s)}\cdot\left(1-\frac{1.2}{n}\right)\cdot\left(1+\frac{0.06}{n}\right)

\displaystyle\quad<\Psi_{j,i}^{(s)}\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right),

where we have used that
e^{x}<1+0.6\cdot x
for any
-1.1\leqslant x<0
and
(\log n)^{j/k}>\exp(\frac{1}{k}\log\log n)>\exp(\frac{\log(10^{4})}{\log\log n}\cdot\log\log n)=10^{4}
for sufficiently large
n
. In conclusion, we have shown that for any bin
i
with load
x_{i}^{(s)}\geqslant\alpha_{j}^{(s)}
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Psi_{j,i}^{(s+1)}\,\big|\,\mathcal{E}_{j-1}^{(s)},\Psi_{j}^{(s)}\,\right]\leqslant\Psi_{j,i}^{(s)}\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right).

Case 2
: Let us now consider the contributions of a bin
i
with
x_{i}^{(s)}<\alpha_{j}^{(s)}
to
\Psi_{j}^{(s+1)}
. Note that out of those bins, only bins
i
with
x_{i}^{(s)}\in[\alpha_{j}^{(s)}-1,\alpha_{j}^{(s)})
can change the potential.
Hence,

\Psi_{j,i}^{(s+1)}\leqslant\begin{cases}\exp{\left(0.01(\log{n})^{j/k}(1-\frac{1}{n})\right)}&\quad\text{if }x_{i}^{(s+1)}=x_{i}^{(s)}+1,\\
1&\quad\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Since
y_{n\cdot\delta_{k-j}}\leqslant\alpha_{j}^{(s)}-1
, we can conclude as in the previous case that such a bin
i
is incremented with probability at most
u_{i}\leqslant\frac{18}{n}\delta_{k-j}
, so

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Psi_{j,i}^{(s+1)}\,\big|\,\mathcal{E}_{j-1}^{(s)},\Psi_{j}^{(s)}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant(1-u_{i})\cdot\Psi_{j,i}^{(s)}+u_{i}\cdot\exp\left(0.01(\log n)^{j/k}(1-\frac{1}{n})\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant 1\cdot 1+\frac{18}{n}\widetilde{\delta}_{k-j}\cdot\exp\left(0.01(\log n)^{j/k}(1-\frac{1}{n})\right).

Combining the two cases, we find that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Psi_{j}^{(s+1)}\,\big|\,\mathcal{E}_{j-1}^{(s)},\Psi_{j}^{(s)}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\colon x_{i}^{(s)}\geqslant\alpha_{j}^{(s)}}\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Psi_{j,i}^{(s+1)}\,\big|\,\mathcal{E}_{j-1}^{(s)},\Psi_{j}^{(s)}\,\right]+\sum_{i\colon x_{i}^{(s)}<\alpha_{j}^{(s)}}\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Psi_{j,i}^{(s+1)}\,\big|\,\mathcal{E}_{j-1}^{(s)},\Psi_{j}^{(s)}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i\colon x_{i}^{(s)}\geqslant\alpha_{j}^{(s)}}\Psi_{j,i}^{(s)}\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+\sum_{i\colon x_{i}^{(s)}<\alpha_{j}^{(s)}}\Psi_{j,i}^{(s)}+\frac{18}{n}\delta_{k-j}\cdot e^{0.01(\log{n})^{j/k}(1-\frac{1}{n})}

\displaystyle\leqslant\Psi_{j}^{(s)}\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)+n\cdot\frac{18}{n}\cdot 2^{-0.5(\log n)^{j/k}}e^{0.01(\log n)^{j/k}}+1

\displaystyle\leqslant\Psi_{j}^{(s)}\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)+2,

where the second inequality used the fact that
\Psi_{j,i}^{(s)}=1
for
x_{i}^{(j)}<\alpha_{j}^{(s)}
.
∎

Claim 6.10
.

Let
\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}^{(s)}
,
\mathcal{E}_{j-1}^{(s)}
and
\alpha_{j}^{(s)}
be defined as in
6.9
. Then for any bin
i\in[n]
with
x_{i}^{(s)}\geqslant\alpha_{j}^{(s)}
, we get

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,x_{i}^{(s+1)}=x_{i}^{(s)}+1\,\big|\,\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}^{(s)},\mathcal{E}_{j-1}^{(s)},x_{i}^{(s)}\geqslant\alpha_{j}^{(s)}\,\right]\leqslant\frac{\gamma\delta}{n}.

Proof.

By
6.8
we get,
x_{i}^{(s)}\geqslant\alpha_{j}^{(s)}\geqslant y_{n\cdot\delta}^{(s)}
. By the definition of the process, incrementing bin
i
depends only on
\Rank^{(s)}(i)
.

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,x_{i}^{(s+1)}=x_{i}^{(s)}+1\,\big|\,\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}^{(s)},\mathcal{E}_{j-1}^{(s)},\Rank^{(s)}(i)=r\,\right]=\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,x_{i}^{(s+1)}=x_{i}^{(s)}+1\,\big|\,\Rank^{(s)}(i)=r\,\right]

So,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,x_{i}^{(s+1)}=x_{i}^{(s)}+1\,\big|\,\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}^{(s)},\mathcal{E}_{j-1}^{(s)},x_{i}^{(s)}\geqslant\alpha_{j}^{(s)}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,x_{i}^{(s+1)}=x_{i}^{(s)}+1\,\big|\,\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}^{(s)},\mathcal{E}_{j-1}^{(s)},x_{i}^{(s)}\geqslant\alpha_{j}^{(s)},\Rank^{(s)}(i)\leqslant n\cdot\delta\,\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{u}\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,x_{i}^{(s+1)}=x_{i}^{(s)}+1\,\big|\,\Rank^{(s)}(i)=u,\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}^{(s)},\mathcal{E}_{j-1}^{(s)},x_{i}^{(s)}\geqslant\alpha_{j}^{(s)},\Rank^{(s)}(i)\leqslant n\cdot\delta\,\right]

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Rank^{(s)}(i)=u\,\big|\,\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}^{(s)},\mathcal{E}_{j-1}^{(s)},x_{i}^{(s)}\geqslant\alpha_{j}^{(s)},\Rank^{(s)}(i)\leqslant n\cdot\delta\,\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{u:y_{u}^{(s)}\geqslant\alpha_{j}^{(s)}}\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,x_{i}^{(s+1)}=x_{i}^{(s)}+1\,\big|\,\Rank^{(s)}(i)=u\,\right]

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Rank^{(s)}(i)=u\,\big|\,\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}^{(s)},\mathcal{E}_{j-1}^{(s)},x_{i}^{(s)}\geqslant\alpha_{j}^{(s)},\Rank^{(s)}(i)\leqslant n\cdot\delta\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{u:y_{u}^{(s)}\geqslant\alpha_{j}^{(s)}}\frac{\gamma\delta}{n}\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Rank^{(s)}(i)=u\,\big|\,\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}^{(s)},\mathcal{E}_{j-1}^{(s)},x_{i}^{(s)}\geqslant\alpha_{j}^{(s)},\Rank^{(s)}(i)\leqslant n\cdot\delta\,\right]

The information in the previous potential functions,
\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}^{(s)}
and
\mathcal{E}_{j-1}^{(s)}
, does not allow to distinguish bins, so each one is equally likely to have any of the loads,

\displaystyle=\sum_{u:y_{u}^{(s)}\geqslant\alpha_{j}^{(s)}}\frac{\gamma\delta}{n}\cdot\frac{1}{\left|u:y_{u}^{(s)}\geqslant\alpha_{j}^{(s)}\right|}=\frac{\gamma\delta}{n}.

∎

The first lemma proves that
\widetilde{\Psi}_{j}^{(s)}
is small in expectation for at
at least one
time-step. It relies on the multiplicative drop (
6.9
), and the fact that precondition
\cap_{r\in[\beta_{j-1},s]}\mathcal{E}_{j-1}^{(r)}
holds due to the definition of the killed potential
\widetilde{\Psi}_{j-1}
.

Lemma 6.11
.

There exists
s\in[\beta_{j-1},\beta_{j-1}+n\log^{3}n]
such that
\mathbf{E}[\,\widetilde{\Psi}_{j}^{(s)}\,]\leqslant cn
.

Proof.

In this lemma, we analyze

\widetilde{\Psi}_{j}^{(s)}=\Psi_{j}^{(s)}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\cap_{r\in[\beta_{j-1},s]}\mathcal{E}_{j-1}^{(r)}}
, so we will implicitly only deal with the case where
\mathcal{E}_{j-1}^{(r)}
holds for all
r\in[\beta_{j-1},\beta_{j-1}+n\log^{3}n]
.

Since
\mathcal{E}_{j-1}^{(\beta_{j-1})}
holds,
\Phi_{j-1}^{(\beta_{j-1})}\leqslant 2cn
holds, so using
6.14
, we have
\Psi_{j}^{(\beta_{j-1})}\leqslant\exp(0.01(\log n)^{3})
. Note that if at step
r\in[\beta_{j-1},\beta_{j-1}+n\log^{3}n]
,
\widetilde{\Psi}_{j}^{(r)}\geqslant cn
, then the second inequality from
6.9
implies,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\widetilde{\Psi}_{j}^{(r+1)}\,\big|\,\widetilde{\Psi}_{j}^{(r)}\,\right]\leqslant\left(1-\frac{1}{2n}\right)\cdot\widetilde{\Psi}_{j}^{(r)},

(6.1)

since whenever
\widetilde{\Psi}_{j}^{(s)}=0
, the inequality holds trivially. We define the killed potential function,

\Lambda_{j}^{(r)}:=\widetilde{\Psi}_{j}^{(r)}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\bigcap_{\widetilde{r}\in[\beta_{j-1},r]}\widetilde{\Psi}_{j}^{(\widetilde{r})}\geqslant cn},

for
r\in[\beta_{j-1},\beta_{j-1}+n\log^{3}n]
. This satisfies the multiplicative drop in (
6.1
), but regardless of how large
\Lambda_{j}^{(r)}
is. By inductively applying the inequality for
\Delta=n\log^{3}n
steps, we have

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Lambda_{j}^{(\beta_{j-1}+\Delta)}\,\right]=\mathbf{E}\left[\,\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Lambda_{j}^{(\beta_{j-1}+\Delta)}\,\big|\,\Lambda_{j}^{(\beta_{j-1})}\,\right]\,\right]\leqslant e^{-\frac{\Delta}{2n}}\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,\widetilde{\Psi}_{j}^{(\beta_{j-1})}\,\right]\leqslant 1\leqslant c.

Hence, there exists
s\in[\beta_{j-1},\beta_{j-1}+\Delta]
, such that
\mathbf{E}\left[\,\widetilde{\Psi}_{j}^{(s)}\,\right]\leqslant cn
.
∎

Generalizing the previous lemma, and again exploiting the conditioning on
\cap_{r\in[\beta_{j-1},s]}\mathcal{E}_{j-1}^{(r)}
of
\Psi_{j}^{(s)}
, we know prove that
\widetilde{\Psi}_{j}^{(s)}
is small in expectation for the entire time interval.

Lemma 6.12
.

For all
s\in[\beta_{j-1}+n\log^{3}n,t+n\log^{5}n]
,
\mathbf{E}[\,\widetilde{\Psi}_{j}^{(s)}\,]\leqslant cn
.

Proof.

Again, note that this lemma analyzes

\widetilde{\Psi}_{j}^{(s)}=\Psi_{j}^{(s)}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\cap_{r\in[\beta_{j-1},s]}\mathcal{E}_{j-1}^{(r)}}
, and thus we will implicitly only deal with the case where
\mathcal{E}_{j-1}^{(r)}
holds for all
r\in[\beta_{j-1},\beta_{j-1}+n\log^{3}n]
.

By using
6.11
, we get that
\mathbf{E}[\,\widetilde{\Psi}_{j}^{(r_{0})}\,]\leqslant cn
for some
r_{0}\in[\beta_{j-1},\beta_{j-1}+n\log^{3}n]
.
Further, by using the inequality of
6.9
and the fact that
2\leqslant c
, we have for any
r\in[\beta_{j-1},t+n\log^{5}n]
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\widetilde{\Psi}_{j}^{(r+1)}\,\big|\,\widetilde{\Psi}_{j}^{(r)}\,\right]\leqslant\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)\cdot\widetilde{\Psi}_{j}^{(r)}+c.

For all
r\in[r_{0},t+n\log^{5}n]
such that
\mathbf{E}[\,\widetilde{\Psi}_{j}^{(r)}\,]\leqslant cn
holds, it follows that,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\widetilde{\Psi}_{j}^{(r+1)}\,\right]=\mathbf{E}\left[\,\mathbf{E}\left[\,\widetilde{\Psi}_{j}^{(r+1)}\,\big|\,\widetilde{\Psi}_{j}^{(r)}\,\right]\,\right]\leqslant\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,\widetilde{\Psi}_{j}^{(r)}\,\right]+c\leqslant\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)cn+c=cn.

Hence, starting from
\mathbf{E}[\,\widetilde{\Psi}_{j}^{(r_{0})}\,]\leqslant cn
, it follows inductively that for any
s\in[r_{0},t+n\log^{5}n]
,
\mathbf{E}[\,\widetilde{\Psi}_{j}^{(s)}\,]\leqslant cn
. Since
r_{0}\leqslant\beta_{j-1}+n\log^{3}n
, the claim follows.
∎

We now switch to the other potential function
\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}^{(s)}
, and prove that if it is polynomial in
at least one step
, then it is also linear in
at least one
step (not much later).

Lemma 6.13
.

For all
1\leqslant j<k
it holds that,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcup_{s\in[\beta_{j-1},\beta_{j}]}\{\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}^{(s)}\leqslant cn\}~\,\Big|\,~\bigcup_{r\in[\beta_{j-1},\beta_{j-1}+n\log^{3}n]}\{\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}^{(r)}\leqslant n^{4/3}\}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-5}.

Proof.

Note that if at step
\widetilde{r}
,
\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}^{(\widetilde{r})}>cn
, then the first inequality from
6.9
implies,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}^{(\widetilde{r}+1)}\,\big|\,\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}^{(\widetilde{r})},\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}^{(\widetilde{r})}\geqslant cn\,\right]\leqslant\left(1-\frac{1}{2n}\right)\cdot\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}^{(\widetilde{r})}.

(6.2)

We define the killed potential function

\Lambda_{j}^{(\widetilde{r})}:=\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}^{(\widetilde{r})}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\bigcap_{\widetilde{s}\in[r,\widetilde{r})}\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}^{(\widetilde{s})}\geqslant cn},

for
\widetilde{r}\in[\beta_{j-1},\beta_{j-1}+n\log^{3}n]
. This satisfies inequality
6.2
for all
\widetilde{r}\in[\beta_{j-1},\beta_{j-1}+n\log^{3}n]
, regardless of the value of
\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}^{(\widetilde{r})}
.

Let
r\geqslant\beta_{j-1}
be the smallest time-step such that
\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}^{(r)}\leqslant n^{4/3}
holds.
By inductively applying
6.2
for
\Delta=n\log^{2}n
steps, we have

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Lambda_{j}^{(r+\Delta)}\,\big|\,\Lambda_{j}^{(r)},\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}^{(r)}\leqslant n^{4/3}\,\right]\leqslant e^{-\frac{\Delta}{2n}}\cdot\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}^{(r)}\leqslant e^{-\frac{1}{2}\log^{2}n}\cdot n^{4/3}\leqslant n^{-4},

for sufficiently large
n
. By Markov’s inequality,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Lambda_{j}^{(r+\Delta)}>cn\,\big|\,\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}^{(r)}\leqslant n^{4/3}\,\right]\leqslant n^{-4}\cdot\frac{1}{cn}\leqslant n^{-5}.

Since,
\Lambda_{j}^{(r+\Delta)}\leqslant cn\Rightarrow\exists s\in[r,r+\Delta]\subseteq[\beta_{j-1},\beta_{j}]\colon\,\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}^{(s)}\leqslant cn
, we get the conclusion.
∎

6.4.3
Deterministic Relations between the Potential Functions

We collect several basic facts about the potential functions
\Phi_{j}^{(s)}
and
\Psi_{j}^{(s)}
.

Claim 6.14
.

For any
s\geqslant 0
,

\Phi_{j}^{(s)}\leqslant 2cn\Rightarrow\Psi_{j+1}^{(s)}\leqslant\exp(0.01\cdot\log^{3}n).

Proof.

Assuming
\Phi_{j}^{(s)}\leqslant 2cn
, implies that for any bin
i\in[n]
,

\displaystyle\exp\left(\alpha_{2}\cdot(\log n)^{j/k}\cdot\left(x^{(s)}_{i}-\frac{s}{n}-\frac{2}{\alpha_{2}}j(\log n)^{1/k}\right)\right)\leqslant 2cn

\displaystyle\Rightarrow~~x^{(s)}_{i}\leqslant\frac{s}{n}+\log(2c)+\frac{1}{\alpha_{2}}(\log n)^{\frac{k-j}{k}}+\frac{2}{\alpha_{2}}j(\log n)^{1/k}\leqslant\frac{s}{n}+0.5\cdot(\log n)^{2},

for sufficiently large
n
. Hence,

\Psi_{j+1}^{(s)}\leqslant n\cdot\exp(0.01\cdot(\log n)^{\frac{j+1}{k}}\cdot 0.5\cdot(\log n)^{2})\leqslant\exp(0.01\cdot\log^{3}n).

∎

The next claim is crucial for applying the concentration inequality, since the third statement bounds the maximum additive change of
\Phi^{(s)}
(assuming
\Psi^{(s)}
is small enough):

Claim 6.15
.

For any
s\geqslant 0
, if
\Psi_{j}^{(s)}\leqslant cn^{12}
, then (i)
x_{i}^{(s)}\leqslant\frac{s}{n}+\frac{12.1}{\alpha_{1}}\cdot(\log n)^{\frac{k-j}{k}}+\frac{2}{\alpha_{2}}j(\log n)^{1/k}
for all
i\in[n]
, (ii)
\Phi_{j}^{(s)}\leqslant n^{4/3}
and (iii)
|\Phi_{j}^{(s+1)}-\Phi_{j}^{(s)}|\leqslant n^{1/3}
.

Proof.

Let
s
be some time-step with
\Psi_{j}^{(s)}\leqslant cn^{12}
. For
(i)
, assuming that
x_{i}^{(s)}>\frac{s}{n}+\frac{12.1}{\alpha_{1}}\cdot(\log n)^{\frac{k-j}{k}}+\frac{2}{\alpha_{2}}j(\log n)^{1/k}
, then we get
\Psi_{j}^{(s)}>\exp(\alpha_{1}\cdot\frac{12.1}{\alpha_{1}}\cdot\log n)=n^{12.1}
, which is a contradiction.
For
(ii)
, it suffices to prove
\Phi_{j,i}^{(s)}\leqslant n^{1/3}
, so,

\displaystyle\Phi_{j,i}^{(s)}

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\bigl(\alpha_{2}\cdot(\log n)^{j/k}\cdot\bigl(x_{i}^{(s)}-\frac{s}{n}-\frac{2}{\alpha_{2}}j(\log n)^{1/k}\bigr)^{+}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\bigl(\alpha_{2}\cdot(\log n)^{j/k}\cdot\frac{12.1}{\alpha_{1}}\cdot(\log n)^{\frac{k-j}{k}}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\exp\bigl(\frac{12.1\cdot 0.0002}{0.01}\cdot\log n\bigr)\leqslant n^{1/3}.

For
(iii)
, following the argument in the proof of
6.15
, because the potential function is convex, the maximum change is upper bounded by the hypothetical scenario of placing two balls in the heaviest bin, i.e. by
n^{1/3}
.
∎

The next claim is a simple “smoothness” argument showing that the potential cannot decrease quickly within
n/\log^{2}n
steps. The derivation is elementary and relies on the fact that average load does not change by more than
1/\log^{2}n
.

Claim 6.16
.

For any
s\geqslant 0
and any
r\in[s,s+n/\log^{2}n]
, we have
\Phi_{j}^{(r)}\geqslant 0.99\cdot\Phi_{j}^{(s)}
.

Proof.

The normalized load after
r-s
steps can decrease by at most
\frac{r-s}{n}\leqslant\frac{1}{\log^{2}n}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\Phi_{j,i}^{(r)}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(0.01\cdot(\log n)^{j/k}\cdot(x_{i}^{(s)}-\frac{r-s}{n}-\frac{s}{n}-\frac{2}{\alpha_{2}}j(\log n)^{1/k})^{+}\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant\exp\left(0.01\cdot(\log n)^{j/k}\cdot(x_{i}^{(s)}-\frac{s}{n}-\frac{2}{\alpha_{2}}j(\log n)^{1/k})^{+}-\frac{0.01\cdot(\log n)^{j/k}\frac{n}{\log^{2}n}}{n}\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant\Phi_{j,i}^{(s)}\cdot e^{-\frac{0.01\cdot(\log n)^{j/k}}{\log^{2}n}}\geqslant 0.99\cdot\Phi_{j,i}^{(s)},

for sufficiently large
n
.
∎

6.5
Completing the Proof of Key Lemma (
6.7
)

The proof of
6.7
shares some of the ideas from the proof of
5.3
. However, there we could more generously take a union bound over the entire time-interval to ensure that the potential is indeed small everywhere with high probability. Here we cannot afford to lose a polynomial factor in the error probability, as the inductive step has to be applied
k=\omega(1)
times. To overcome this, we will partition the time-interval into consecutive intervals of length
n/\log^{2}n
. Then, we will prove that at the end of each such interval the potential is small w.h.p., and finally use a simple smoothness argument of the potential to show that the potential is small w.h.p. for
all
time steps.

Proof of
6.7
.

The first and second statements in
6.15
, imply that if
\Psi_{j}^{(s)}\leqslant cn^{12}
holds, then

\left|\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}^{(s+1)}-\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}^{(s)}\right|\leqslant n^{1/3},

and
\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}^{(s)}\leqslant n^{4/3}
.

Thus we will next establish that
\Psi_{j}^{(s)}\leqslant cn^{12}
occurs with high probability.

By
6.12
, for all
s\in[\beta_{j-1}+n\log^{3}n,t+n\log^{5}n]
,
\mathbf{E}[\,\widetilde{\Psi}_{j}^{(s)}\,]\leqslant cn
. Using Markov’s inequality, we have with probability at least
1-n^{-11}
that
\widetilde{\Psi}_{j}^{(s)}\leqslant cn^{12}
. Hence by the union bound it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcap_{s\in[\beta_{j-1}+n\log^{3}n,t+n\log^{5}n]}\{\widetilde{\Psi}_{j}^{(s)}\leqslant cn^{12}\}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-9}.

(6.3)

We now define the intervals

\mathcal{I}_{1}:=(t_{0},t_{0}+\Delta],\mathcal{I}_{2}=(t_{0}+\Delta,t_{0}+2\Delta],~\ldots~,\mathcal{I}_{q}:=(t_{0}+(q-1)\Delta,t+n\log^{5}n],

where
t_{0}\in[\beta_{j-1}+n\log^{3}n,\beta_{j}]
is arbitrary (but will be chosen later),
\Delta:=n/\log^{2}n
and
q:=\lfloor\frac{t+n\log^{5}n-t_{0}}{\Delta}\rfloor\leqslant\log^{7}n
. In order to prove that
\Phi_{j}
is at most
2cn
over all these intervals, we will use our auxiliary lemmas and the supermartingale concentration inequality (
A.4
)
to establish that
\Phi_{j}
is at most
(c+1)\cdot n
at the points
t_{0}+\Delta,t_{0}+2\Delta,\ldots t_{0}\cdot q,t+n\log^{5}n
. By using a smoothness argument (
6.16
), this will establish
that
\Phi
is at most
2cn
at all points in
[t_{0},t+n\log^{5}n]
, which is the conclusion of the lemma.

For each interval
i\in[q]
, we define for
s\in(t_{0}+(i-1)\Delta,t_{0}+i\Delta]
,

X_{i}^{(s)}:=\begin{cases}\Phi_{j}^{(s)}&\text{if }\exists u\in(t_{0}+(i-1)\cdot\Delta,s)$ such that $\Phi_{j}^{(u)}>5\cdot c\cdot n,\\
5cn+n^{1/3}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Note that whenever the first condition in the definition of
X_{i}^{(s)}
is satisfied, it remains satisfied until
t_{0}+i\cdot\Delta
, again by
6.16
.

Following the notation of
A.4
, define the event

\overline{B_{i}^{(s)}}:=\left(\bigcap_{u\in[t_{0}+(i-1)\cdot\Delta,s)}\{\widetilde{\Psi}_{j}^{(u)}\leqslant cn^{12}\}\right)\bigcap\left(\bigcap_{u\in[t_{0}+(i-1)\cdot\Delta,s)}\mathcal{E}_{j-1}^{(u)}\right).

By the inductive hypothesis of
6.7
for
j-1
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcap_{u\in[\beta_{j-1},t+n\log^{5}n]}\mathcal{E}_{j-1}^{(u)}\,\right]\geqslant 1-\frac{(\log n)^{8(j-1)}}{n^{4}},

and hence by the union bound over this and
Eq.

6.3
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\overline{B}_{i}^{(s)}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-9}-\frac{(\log n)^{8(j-1)}}{n^{4}}\geqslant 1-\frac{2(\log n)^{8(j-1)}}{n^{4}}.

Claim 6.17
.

Fix any interval
i\in[q]
. Then the sequence of random variable
X_{i}^{(s)}
with filtration
\mathcal{F}_{i}^{(s)}
, for
s\in[t_{0}+(i-1)\Delta,t_{0}+i\Delta]
and
B_{i}^{(s)}
being the bad set associated, satisfies for all
s
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,X_{i}^{(s)}\,\mid\,\mathcal{F}_{i}^{(s-1)}\,\right]\leqslant X_{i}^{(s-1)},

and

\left|\left(X_{i}^{(s)}-X_{i}^{(s-1)}\right)\,\mid\,\mathcal{F}_{i}^{(s-1)}\right|\leqslant 2n^{1/3}.

Proof of
6.17
.

We begin by noting that, step
s\in[t_{0}+(i-1)\Delta,t_{0}+i\Delta]
with
X_{i}^{(s)}\geqslant 4n
, the first inequality of
6.9
can be relaxed to,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Phi_{j}^{(s+1)}\,\mid\,\mathcal{E}_{j-1}^{(s)},\Phi_{j}^{(s)}\,\right]\leqslant\Big(1-\frac{1}{2n}\Big)\cdot\Phi_{j}^{(s)}.

(6.4)

We consider the following three cases:

•

Case 1:
Assume that
\Phi_{j}^{(s)}<5\cdot c\cdot n
and this was the case for all previous time steps. Then, the
X_{i}^{(s+1)}=X_{i}^{(s)}
, so the two statements hold trivially.

•

Case 2:
Assume that
\Phi_{j}^{(u)}>5\cdot c\cdot n
for some
u\leqslant s-2
, then by
6.16
, since
s-u\leqslant n/\log^{2}n
,

\Phi_{j}^{(s-1)}\geqslant 0.99\cdot\Phi_{j}^{(u)}>2cn,

and thus by
Eq.

6.4
, the first statement follows. The second statement follows, since conditional on
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{i}^{(s)}
, the precondition of
6.15
(iii) holds.

•

Case 3:
Assume that
\Phi_{j}^{(u)}<5cn
for
u\leqslant s-2
and
\Phi_{j}^{(s-1)}>5cn
.
First, we obtain that
\Phi_{j}^{(s-1)}\leqslant\Phi_{j}^{(s-2)}+n^{1/3}<5\cdot c\cdot n+n^{1/3}
(by
6.15
(ii), using again that
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{i}^{(s)}
holds). Further, by definition,

X_{i}^{(s-1)}=5cn+n^{1/3}
, so
\mathbf{E}[\,X_{i}^{(s)}\,\mid\,\Phi_{i}^{(s-1)}\,]=\mathbf{E}[\,\Phi_{j}^{(s)}\,\mid\,\Phi_{i}^{(s-1)}\,]\leqslant\Phi_{j}^{(s-1)}<X_{i}^{(s-1)}
by
Eq.

6.4
. The second inequality follows by
6.15
(iii), since
|X_{i}^{(s-1)}-X_{i}^{(s)}|\leqslant n^{1/3}+|\Phi_{j}^{(s)}-\Phi_{j}^{(s-1)}|\leqslant 2n^{1/3}
.

∎

Next we claim that
X_{i}^{(s)}
satisfies the following conditions of
A.4
(where
\mathcal{F}_{i}
are the filtrations associated with the balls allocated at
\mathcal{I}_{i}
and
B_{i}^{(s)}
is the bad set associated):

1.

\mathbf{E}[\,X_{i}^{(s)}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i}^{(s-1)}\,]\leqslant X_{i}^{(s-1)}
by the first statement of
6.17
.

2.

\mathbf{Var}[\,X_{i}^{(s)}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i}^{(s-1)}\,]\leqslant n^{2/3}
. This holds, since

\displaystyle\mathbf{Var}\left[\,X_{i}^{(s)}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i}^{(s-1)}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{4}\left|\left(\max X_{i}^{(s)}-\min X_{i}^{(s)}\right)\,\mid\,\mathcal{F}_{i}^{(s-1)}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{4}\left(2\cdot\left|X_{i}^{(s)}-X_{i}^{(s-1)}\right|\,\mid\,\mathcal{F}_{i}^{(s-1)}\right)^{2}\leqslant 4n^{2/3},

where the first inequality follows by Popovicius’ inequality, the second by the triangle inequality and the third by
6.17
.

3.

X_{i}^{(s)}-\mathbf{E}[\,X_{i}^{(s)}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i}^{(s-1)}\,]\leqslant 2\cdot\bigl(\bigl|X_{i}^{(s)}-X_{i}^{(s-1)}\bigr|\,\mid\,\mathcal{F}_{i}^{(s-1)}\bigr)\leqslant 4n^{1/3}
which follows by the second statement of
6.17
.

Now applying
A.4
for
i\in[q]
with
\lambda=\frac{n}{\log^{7}n}
,
a_{i}=4n^{1/3}
, and
M=0
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X_{i}^{(t_{0}+i\Delta)}\geqslant X_{i}^{(t_{0}+(i-1)\Delta)}+\lambda\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{n^{2}/\log^{14}n}{2(\Delta\cdot(16n^{2/3}+4n^{2/3}))}\right)+\frac{2(\log n)^{8(j-1)}}{n^{4}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{3(\log n)^{8(j-1)}}{n^{4}}.

Taking the union bound over the
\log^{7}n
intervals
i\in[q]
, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcup_{i\in[q]}\{X_{i}^{(t_{0}+i\Delta)}\geqslant X_{1}^{(t_{0})}+i\cdot\lambda\}\,\right]\leqslant\log^{7}n\cdot\frac{3(\log n)^{8(j-1)}}{n^{4}}.

(6.5)

It remains to show the existence of a
t_{0}\in[\beta_{j-1},\beta_{j-1}+n\log^{3}n]
for which
X_{1}^{(t_{0})}
is small.

Since
\Psi_{j}^{(s)}\leqslant\widetilde{\Psi}_{j}^{(s)}
, we can conclude from
Eq.

6.3
that with probability at least
1-n^{-9}
, for
s=\beta_{j-1}+n\log^{3}n
we have
\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}^{(\beta_{j-1}+n\log^{3}n)}\leqslant n^{4/3}
.

Assuming this occurs, then by
6.13
, there exists a time step
t_{0}\in[\beta_{j-1},\beta_{j}]
such that
\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}^{(t_{0})}\leqslant cn
w.p. at least
1-n^{-4}
. Thus by the union bound over this and
Eq.

6.3
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcup_{t_{0}\in[\beta_{j-1},\beta_{j}]}\{\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}^{(t_{0})}\leqslant cn\}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-4}-n^{-9}.

As

\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}^{(t_{0})}=\Phi_{j}^{(t_{0})}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\cap_{s\in[\beta_{j-1},t_{0}]}\Phi_{j-1}^{(s)}\leqslant 2cn},

and

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcap_{s=\beta_{j-1}}^{t_{0}}\{\Phi_{j-1}^{(s)}\leqslant 2cn\}\,\right]\geqslant\frac{(\log n)^{8(j-1)}}{n^{4}}
by the inductive hypothesis,
a union bound yields

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcup_{t_{0}\in[\beta_{j-1},\beta_{j}]}\{\Phi_{j}^{(t_{0})}\leqslant cn\}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-4}-n^{-9}-\frac{(\log n)^{8(j-1)}}{n^{4}}\geqslant 1-\frac{2(\log n)^{8(j-1)}}{n^{4}}.

Since
c>4
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcup_{t_{0}\in[\beta_{j-1},\beta_{j}]}\{X_{1}^{(t_{0})}\leqslant cn\}\,\right]=\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcup_{t_{0}\in[\beta_{j-1},\beta_{j}]}\{X_{1}^{(t_{0})}\leqslant\max\{cn,4n+n^{1/3}\}\}\,\right]\geqslant 1-\frac{2(\log n)^{8(j-1)}}{n^{4}}.

Taking the union bound over this and
Eq.

6.5
, we conclude

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcup_{i\in[q]}\{X_{i}^{(t_{0}+i\Delta)}\geqslant cn+\log^{7}n\cdot\frac{n}{\log^{7}n}\}\,\right]\leqslant\log^{7}n\cdot\frac{4(\log n)^{8(j-1)}}{n^{4}}.

(6.6)

For the time-step
u=u(i):=t_{0}+i\Delta
at the end of the interval
u
, we cannot deduce anything about
\Phi_{j}
from
X_{i}
because of the shift-by-one in time-steps. To fix this, recall that by
6.15
(third statement),
\Psi_{j}^{(u)}\leqslant cn^{12}
implies
\left|\Phi_{j}^{(u+1)}-\Phi_{j}^{(u)}\right|\leqslant 2n^{1/3}
. Hence
Eq.

6.3
(together with the inductive hypothesis) implies that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X_{i}^{(u)}+2n^{1/3}\geqslant\Phi_{j}^{(u)}\,\right]\geqslant 1-\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left|\Phi_{j}^{(u+1)}-\Phi_{j}^{(u)}\right|\leqslant 2n^{1/3}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-9}-\frac{(\log n)^{8(j-1)}}{n^{4}}.

Using this,
Eq.

6.6
and then applying a union bound over
i\in[q]

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcup_{i\in[q]}\{\Phi_{j}^{(u(i))}\geqslant cn+\log^{7}n\cdot\frac{n}{\log^{7}n}+2n^{1/3}\}\,\right]\leqslant\log^{7}n\cdot\frac{6(\log n)^{8(j-1)}}{n^{4}}.

Finally, by
6.16

the above statement extends to
all
time-steps at the cost of a slightly larger threshold:

Remark 7.4
.

Consider a

n

7.2
Graphical Balanced Allocation

We now analyze the
graphical balanced allocation process
, with a focus on dense expander graphs.
To this end, we first recall some basic notation of spectral graph theory and expansion.
For an undirected graph
G
, the normalized Laplacian Matrix of
G
is an
n\times n
-matrix defined by

\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\cdot\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{D}^{1/2},

where
\mathbf{I}
is the identity matrix,
\mathbf{A}
is the adjacency matrix and
\mathbf{D}
is the diagonal matrix where
D_{u,u}=\operatorname{deg}(u)
for any vertex
u\in V
.
Further, let
\lambda_{1}\leqslant\lambda_{2}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant\lambda_{n}
be the
n
eigenvalues of
\mathbf{L}
, and let
\lambda:=\max_{i\in[2,n]}\left|1-\lambda_{i}\right|
be the spectral expansion of
G
.
Further, for any set
U\subseteq V
define
\operatorname{vol}(U):=\sum_{v\in U}\operatorname{deg}(v)
.
Note that for a
d
-regular graph, we have
\operatorname{vol}(U)=d\cdot|U|
and
\operatorname{vol}(V)=dn
.

We now recall the following (stronger) version of the Expander Mixing Lemma (cf.
[
13
]
):

Lemma 7.5

(Expander Mixing Lemma)
.

For any subsets
X,Y\subseteq V
,

\left||E(X,Y)|-\frac{\operatorname{vol}(X)\cdot\operatorname{vol}(Y)}{\operatorname{vol}(V)}\right|\leqslant\lambda\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\operatorname{vol}(X)\cdot\operatorname{vol}(\overline{X})\cdot\operatorname{vol}(Y)\cdot\operatorname{vol}(\overline{Y})}}{\operatorname{vol}(V)},

where
\operatorname{vol}(\overline{X})=\operatorname{vol}(V\setminus X)
.

In the following, we consider
G
to be a
d
-regular graph.

Proposition 7.6
.

Consider the probability vector
p_{i}^{t}
,
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
of a graphical balanced allocation process on a
d
-regular graph
G
with spectral expansion
\lambda
. Then this vector satisfies for any load configuration at any time
t
the following three inequalities.

1.

For any
1\leqslant j\leqslant\lambda\cdot n
,

\sum_{i=1}^{j}p_{i}^{t}\leqslant 2\lambda\cdot\frac{j}{n}.

2.

For any
\lambda\cdot n\leqslant j
,

\sum_{i=1}^{j}p_{i}^{t}\leqslant 2\cdot\left(\frac{j}{n}\right)^{2}.

3.

For any
1\leqslant j\leqslant n
,

\sum_{i=1}^{j}p_{i}^{t}\leqslant\frac{j}{n}\cdot\left(1-(1-\lambda)\cdot\frac{n-j}{n}\right).

Proof.

Fix
1\leqslant j\leqslant\lambda\cdot n
. and let
S
be the subset of vertices corresponding to the
j
-th most heavily loaded bins; so in particular,
|S|=j
. Using Lemma
7.5
for
X=Y=S
:

|E(S,S)|\leqslant d\cdot\frac{|S|\cdot|S|}{n}+\lambda\cdot\frac{d|S|(n-|S|)}{n}\leqslant d\cdot\frac{|S|\cdot|S|}{n}+\lambda d\cdot|S|.

Since
|S|\leqslant\lambda\cdot n
, we conclude that

|E(S,S)|\leqslant 2\lambda d\cdot|S|.

Note that the for the graphical balanced allocation process, we place a ball in one of the
|S|
-th most heavily loaded bins if and only if we pick an edge in
E(S,S)
. Using this and the upper bound on
|E(S,S)|
from above, it follows that

\sum_{i=1}^{|S|}p_{i}^{t}=\frac{|E(S,S)|}{2|E|}\leqslant\frac{2\lambda d|S|}{nd}=2\lambda\cdot\frac{|S|}{n},

and the first statement follows.

Consider now the case where
\lambda n\leqslant|S|
. Then,

\displaystyle|E(S,S)|

\displaystyle\leqslant d\cdot\frac{|S|\cdot|S|}{n}+\lambda d\cdot\frac{|S|\cdot(n-|S|)}{n}

\displaystyle\leqslant d\cdot\frac{|S|\cdot|S|}{n}+d\cdot\frac{|S|\cdot|S|}{n}\cdot\frac{n-|S|}{n}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2d\cdot\frac{|S|\cdot|S|}{n}.

and therefore,

\sum_{i=1}^{|S|}p_{i}^{t}=\frac{|E(S,S)|}{2|E|}\leqslant\frac{2d\cdot\frac{|S|\cdot|S|}{n}}{nd}=2\cdot\left(\frac{|S|}{n}\right)^{2},

which establishes the second statement.

Finally, consider the general case where
1\leqslant|S|\leqslant n
.
Then using Lemma
7.5
,

\displaystyle|E(S,S)|

\displaystyle\leqslant d\cdot\frac{|S|\cdot|S|}{n}+d\cdot\lambda\cdot\frac{|S|(n-|S|)}{n}

\displaystyle=d\cdot|S|\cdot\left(\frac{|S|}{n}+\lambda\cdot\frac{n-|S|}{n}\right)

\displaystyle=d\cdot|S|\cdot\left(\frac{|S|}{n}+\frac{n-|S|}{n}-1\cdot\frac{n-|S|}{n}+\lambda\cdot\frac{n-|S|}{n}\right)

\displaystyle=d\cdot|S|\cdot\left(1-(1-\lambda)\cdot\frac{n-|S|}{n}\right).

and therefore,

\sum_{i=1}^{|S|}p_{i}=\frac{|E(S,S)|}{2|E|}\leqslant\frac{d|S|\cdot\left(1-(1-\lambda)\cdot\frac{n-|S|}{n}\right)}{nd}=\frac{|S|}{n}\cdot\left(1-(1-\lambda)\cdot\frac{n-|S|}{n}\right).

∎

Lemma 7.7
.

Consider a graphical balanced allocation process on a connected,
d
-regular graph on
G
with spectral expansion
\lambda\leqslant 1/2
. Further, let
2^{-0.5(\log n)^{(k-1)/k}}\geqslant\lambda

for an integer

Then there exists a process in the class

\delta_{i}

\widetilde{\delta}_{i}

\gamma=2

Proof.

For the graphical balanced allocation process as defined in the statement, for any
1\leqslant j\leqslant n\delta_{1}
, it follows by

\sum_{i=1}^{j}p_{i}^{t}\leqslant\frac{j}{n}\cdot 2\lambda\leqslant 2\cdot\frac{j}{n}\cdot\delta_{1},

and it is majorized by a

\gamma\geqslant 2
.

Consider now any prefix sum over
p
, where
n\delta_{1}<j

7.6
(second statement)

\sum_{i=1}^{j}p_{i}^{t}\leqslant 2\cdot\left(\frac{j}{n}\right)^{2}.

Let
q
be the probability vector of the (original)

-quantile. If we take the corresponding sum in the definition of the relaxed quantile process, then, as this is at least the probability that
Two-Choice

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{j}\gamma\cdot q_{i}

Hence if

is smaller than that of a relaxed quantile process.

First, consider any prefix sum until

8
Conclusions

In this work, we introduced a new framework of balls-and-bins with incomplete information. The main contributions are as follows (see also Table
2
for a comparison with related work):

1.

A lower bound of
\Omega(\sqrt{\log n})
for a fixed
m=\Theta(n\sqrt{\log n})
for one adaptive query (
4.2
), disproving Problem 1.3 in
[
18
]
. Also, a stronger lower bound of
\Omega(\log n/\log\log n)
for “many” time-steps in
[1,n\log^{2}n]
(
4.6
), again for one adaptive query.

2.

Design and analysis of an instance of the
k
-quantile process for any
k\geqslant 1
. This process achieves w.h.p. an
\mathcal{O}(k\cdot(\log n)^{1/k})
gap for any
m\geqslant 1
and
k=\mathcal{O}(\log\log n)
(Theorem
6.1
). This process has several theoretical applications:

•

A “power of two-queries” phenomenon: reduction of the gap from
\Omega(\log n/\log\log n)
to
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\log n})

Probabilistic Tools

A.1
Concentration Inequalities

Lemma A.1

(Multiplicative factor Chernoff Binomial Bound
[
30
]
)
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be independent binary random variables with
\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X_{i}=1\,\right]=p
. Then,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\geqslant npe\,\right]\leqslant e^{-np},

and

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\leqslant\frac{np}{e}\,\right]\leqslant e^{\left(\frac{2}{e}-1\right)np}.

Theorem A.2

(Berry-Esseen
[
16
]
)
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be a sequence of i.i.d random variables with mean
\mu
, variance
\sigma^{2}
and central moment
\rho=\mathbf{E}[\,|X_{i}-\mu|^{3}\,]
. Then there exists a constant
C>0
such that for
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}

\left\lvert\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\frac{\overline{X}_{n}-\mu}{\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}}\leqslant\alpha\,\right]-\widetilde{\Phi}(\alpha)\right\rvert\leqslant C\cdot\frac{\rho}{\sigma^{3}\sqrt{n}},

where
\widetilde{\Phi}
is the cumulative distribution of the standard normal distribution.

Lemma A.3

(Berry-Esseen for Poisson r.vs)
.

Let
X\sim\mathrm{Po}(m)
, where
m\in\mathbb{N}
, then

\left|\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X\leqslant m+\alpha\sqrt{m}\,\right]-\widetilde{\Phi}(\alpha)\right|\leqslant C\cdot\frac{\rho}{\sigma^{3}\sqrt{m}}.

Proof.

The sum of
n
independent Poisson r.vs. with parameters
(k_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
is a Poisson r.v. with parameter
\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}
(e.g.
[
31
, Lemma 5.2]
). Hence, we can write
X
as the sum of
m
r.vs.
X_{i}\sim\mathrm{Po}(1)
. Then, applying
A.2
gives,

\left|\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\frac{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}}{m}-\mu}{\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{m}}}\leqslant\alpha\,\right]-\widetilde{\Phi}(\alpha)\right|=\left|\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X\leqslant m+\alpha\sqrt{m}\,\right]-\widetilde{\Phi}(\alpha)\right|\leqslant C\cdot\frac{\rho}{\sigma^{3}\sqrt{m}}.

∎

In order to state the concentration inequality for supermartingales conditional on a bad event not occurring, we introduce the following definitions from
[
12
]
. Consider any r.v.
X
(in our case it will be the potential functions
\Phi_{j}
and the
\Gamma_{1}
) that can be evaluated by a sequence of decisions
Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{N}
of finitely many outputs (the allocated balls). We can describe the process by a
decision tree

T
, a complete rooted tree with depth
n
with vertex set
V(T)
. Each edge
uv
of
T
is associated with a probability
p_{uv}
depending on the decision made from
u
to
v
.

We say
f:V(T)\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies an
admissible condition

P
if
P=\{P_{v}\}
holds for every vertex
v
. For an admissible condition
P
, the associated bad set
B_{i}
over the
X_{i}
is defined to be

B_{i}=\{v\mid\text{the depth of $v$ is $i$, and $P_{u}$ does not hold for some ancestor $u$ of $v$}\}.

Theorem A.4

(Theorem 8.5 from
[
12
]
)
.

For a filter
\mathcal{F}
,
\{\emptyset,\Omega\}=\mathcal{F}^{(0)}\subset\mathcal{F}^{(1)}\subset\ldots\subset\mathcal{F}^{(N)}=\mathcal{F}
,
suppose that a random variable
X^{(s)}
is
\mathcal{F}^{(s)}
-measurable, for
0\leqslant s\leqslant N
. Let
B
be the
bad set associated with the following admissible conditions:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{(s)}\mid\mathcal{F}^{(s-1)}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant X^{(s-1)},

\displaystyle\mathbf{Var}\left[\,X^{(s)}\mid\mathcal{F}^{(s-1)}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\sigma_{s}^{2},

\displaystyle X^{(s)}-\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{(s)}\mid\mathcal{F}^{(s-1)}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant a_{s}+M,

for fixed
\sigma_{s}>0
and
a_{s}>0
. Then, we have for any
\lambda>0
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X^{(N)}\geqslant X^{(0)}+\lambda\,\right]\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2(\sum_{s=1}^{N}(\sigma_{s}^{2}+a_{s}^{2})+M\lambda/3)}\right)+\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,B\,\right].

A.3
Facts about the One-Choice Process

The following facts about the (very) lightly loaded region of
One-Choice
, follow from the concentration inequalities stated before. The results by Raab and Steger
[
33
]
do not cover the region
m\ll n/\operatorname{polylog}(n)
, do not provide an estimate for the number of balls with height at least
k
and also the bounds are not derived for at least
1-n^{-c}
probability.

Lemma A.7
.

Consider the
One-Choice
process with
m=\frac{n}{\log^{c}{n}}
balls into
n
bins, where
c>0
is an arbitrary constant. Then, for any constant
\alpha>0
and for sufficiently large
n
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gap(m)>\frac{1}{c+1}\cdot\frac{\log{n}}{\log{\log{n}}}\,\right]\geqslant 1-\frac{2}{n^{\alpha}}.

Proof.

We will bound the probability of event
\mathcal{E}
, that the maximum load is less than
M=\frac{1}{c+1}\cdot\log{n}/\log{\log{n}}
. The maximum load is a function that is increasing with the number of balls.

The technique of Poissonization
[
1
, Theorem 12]
states that for
One-Choice
, the probability of a monotonically increasing event (in this case
\mathcal{E}
) is bounded by twice the probability that the event holds for independent Poisson r.vs. in place of the load r.vs.

We define
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
to be the event that the maximum load is less than
M
, for
n
Poisson r.vs. Thus,
\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{E}\,\right]\leqslant 2\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\,\right]
. We bound
\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\,\right]
by bounding the probability that no bin has load exactly
M
. We want

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\,\right]\leqslant\left(1-\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{\log^{c}{n}}}\left(\frac{1}{\log^{c}{n}}\right)^{M}}{M!}\right)^{n}\leqslant\exp{\left(-n\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{\log^{c}{n}}}\left(\frac{1}{\log^{c}{n}}\right)^{M}}{M!}\right)}\leqslant\frac{1}{n^{\alpha}}.

This is equivalent to showing that

\displaystyle-n\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{\log^{c}{n}}}\left(\frac{1}{\log^{c}{n}}\right)^{M}}{M!}<-\alpha\log{n}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\log{n}-\frac{1}{\log^{c}{n}}-Mc\log{\log{n}}-\log{M!}>\log{(\alpha\log{n})}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\log{n}-\log{(\alpha\log{n})}-\frac{1}{\log^{c}{n}}>Mc\log{\log{n}}+\log{M!}.

Using Stirling’s upper bound
[
21
, Equation 9.1]
,

\displaystyle Mc\log{\log{n}}+\log{M!}

\displaystyle<Mc\log{\log{n}}+M(\log{M}-1)+\log{M}

\displaystyle=M(c\log{\log{n}}-\log{(c+1)}+\log{\log{n}}-\log{\log{\log{n}}}-1)+\log{M}

\displaystyle=M(c+1)\log{\log{n}}-CM-M\log{\log{\log{n}}}+\log{M}

\displaystyle=\log{n}-CM-M\log{\log{\log{n}}}+\log{M}

\displaystyle<\log{n}-\log{(\alpha\log{n})}-\frac{1}{\log^{c}{n}},

for sufficiently large
n
, since
\log{(\alpha\log{n})}+\frac{1}{\log^{c}{n}}=o(M\log{\log{\log{n}}}-M)
for any constant
\alpha>0
. Hence, we get the desired lower bound.
∎

We now extend
A.7
to a case with fewer balls.

Lemma A.8
.

(cf.
A.7
) Consider the
One-Choice
process with
m=\frac{n}{e^{u\log^{c}{n}}}
(for constants
0<c<1
and
u>0
) balls into
n
bins. Then, for any constant
k>0
with
u\cdot k<1
, for any constant
\alpha>0
and for sufficiently large
n
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gap(m)\geqslant k\cdot(\log n)^{1-c}\,\right]\geqslant 1-\frac{2}{n^{\alpha}}.

Proof.

We define
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
as in
A.7
. We bound
\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\,\right]
by bounding the probability that no bin has load exactly
M=k\cdot(\log n)^{1-c}
. We claim

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\,\right]\leqslant\left(1-\frac{e^{-e^{-u\log^{c}{n}}}\left(e^{-u\log^{c}{n}}\right)^{M}}{M!}\right)^{n}\leqslant\exp{\left(-n\frac{e^{-e^{-u\log^{c}{n}}}\left(e^{-u\log^{c}{n}}\right)^{M}}{M!}\right)}<\frac{1}{n^{\alpha}},

which is equivalent to showing that

\displaystyle-n\frac{e^{-e^{-u\log^{c}{n}}}\left(e^{-u\log^{c}{n}}\right)^{M}}{M!}<-\alpha\log{n}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\log{n}-e^{-u\log^{c}{n}}-Mu\log^{c}{n}-\log{M!}>\log{(\alpha\log{n})}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\log{n}-\log{(\alpha\log{n})}-e^{-u\log^{c}{n}}>Mu\log^{c}{n}+\log{M!}.

Using Stirling’s upper bound
[
21
, Equation 9.1]
,

\displaystyle Mu\log^{c}{n}+\log{M!}

\displaystyle<Mu\log^{c}{n}+M(\log{M}-1)+\log{M}

\displaystyle=M(u\log^{c}{n}+\log{k}+(1-c)\log{\log{n}}-1)+\log{M}

\displaystyle=ku\cdot\log{n}+M(\log{k}+(1-c)\log{\log{n}}-1)+\log{M}

\displaystyle<\log{n}-\log{(\alpha\log{n})}-e^{-u\log^{c}{n}},

for sufficiently large
n
, since
\log{(\alpha\log{n})}+e^{-u\log^{c}{n}}+M(\log{k}+(1-c)\log{\log{n}}-1)+\log{M}=o((1-u\cdot k)\log{n})
for any constant
\alpha>0
and
u\cdot k<1
. Hence, we get the desired lower bound.
∎

Lemma A.9
.

Consider the
One-Choice
process for
m=n\log^{2}n
. With probability at least
1-o(n^{-2})
, there are at least
cn\log n
balls with at least
\frac{m}{n}+\frac{a}{2}\log n
height for
a=0.4
and
c=0.25
.

Proof.

Consider the event
\mathcal{E}
that the number of balls with load above
\frac{a}{2}\log n
is at most
\frac{1}{5}\log n
. Since
\mathcal{E}
is monotonically increasing in the number of balls, its probability is bounded by twice the probability of the event occurring for independent Poisson random variables
[
1
, Theorem 12]
.

By Berry-Esseen inequality for Poisson random variables (
A.3
), for sufficiently large
n
and since
\varepsilon=(\log n)^{-4}
,

\lvert\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,Y\geqslant a\,\right]-\widetilde{\Phi}(a)\rvert\leqslant\varepsilon\Rightarrow\widetilde{\Phi}(a)-\varepsilon\leqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X\geqslant\log^{2}n+a\log n\,\right]\leqslant\widetilde{\Phi}(a)+\varepsilon.

For
a=0.4
, we get
\widetilde{\Phi}(a)\leqslant 0.35
. Let
X_{i}:=\mathbf{1}(Y_{i}\geqslant\log^{2}n+a\log n)
and let
X:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
, then
X
is a Binomial distribution with
p\leqslant 0.35
. Using the lower tail Chernoff bound for the Binomial distribution (
A.1
),

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\leqslant\frac{np}{e}\,\right]\leqslant e^{-\Omega(n)}.

For sufficiently large
n
, the RHS can be made
o(1/n^{2})
, hence there are at least
np/e
bins with load at least
\frac{m}{n}+a\log n
w. p.
1-o(1/n^{2})
. This means that w.h.p. at least
np/e\cdot a\log n=\frac{npa}{e}\log n\leqslant 0.26\cdot n\log n
balls have height
\frac{m}{n}+\frac{a}{2}\log n=\frac{m}{n}+0.4\log n
.
∎

Lemma A.10
.

(cf.
A.9
) In the
One-Choice
process, with
m=Kn\sqrt{\log n}-\mathcal{O}(Kn\sqrt{\log n}\cdot e^{-\sqrt{\log n}})
with probability at least
1-o(n^{-2})
, for sufficiently large
n
, there are at least
e^{-0.21\sqrt{\log n}}\cdot Cn\sqrt{\log{n}}
balls with height at least
(K+C)\cdot\sqrt{\log n}
, for
K=1/10
and for
C=1/20
.

Proof.

Note that
m=K(1-o(1))n\sqrt{\log n}
. Using Poissonization
[
1
, Theorem 12]
, the probability that the statement of the lemma does not hold is upper bounded by twice the probability for the corresponding event with
n
independent Poisson random variables
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}
with parameter
\lambda=\frac{m}{n}=K(1-o(1))\sqrt{\log n}
. For a single Poisson random variable
X
, we lower bound the probability that
X\geqslant u
for
u=(K+2\cdot C)\sqrt{\log n}
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X\geqslant u\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X=u\,\right]=\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^{u}}{u!}\geqslant\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^{u}}{eu(u/e)^{u}}=e^{-\lambda+u-1-\log{u}}\left(\frac{\lambda}{u}\right)^{u}

\displaystyle\geqslant\exp{\left((K+2\cdot C)\sqrt{\log n}\cdot\log{\left(\frac{K(1-o(1))}{K+2\cdot C}\right)}\right)}

\displaystyle\geqslant\exp{(-0.8(K+2\cdot C)\sqrt{\log n})}>\exp{(-0.2\sqrt{\log n})},

where the penultimate inequality used
\log{\left(\frac{K(1-o(1))}{(K+2\cdot C)}\right)}>-0.8
. Using
A.1
, this implies that w.p.
1-o(n^{-2})
at least
ne^{-0.20\sqrt{\log n}-1}\geqslant ne^{-0.21\sqrt{\log n}}
bins have load at least
(K+2\cdot C)\sqrt{\log{n}}
, so at least
e^{-0.21\sqrt{\log n}}\cdot Cn\sqrt{\log{n}}
balls have height at least
(K+C)\sqrt{\log{n}}
.
∎
