Learning-to-Optimize with PAC-Bayesian Guarantees: Theoretical Considerations and Practical Implementation
Michael Sucker michael.sucker@math.uni-tuebingen.de, Jalal Fadili jalal.fadili@ensicaen.fr, Peter Ochs ochs@cs.uni-saarland.de

Abstract
We use the PAC-Bayesian theory for the setting of learning-to-optimize. To the best of our knowledge, we present the first framework to learn optimization algorithms with provable generalization guarantees (PAC-Bayesian bounds) and explicit trade-off between convergence guarantees and convergence speed, which contrasts with the typical worst-case analysis. Our learned optimization algorithms provably outperform related ones derived from a worst-case analysis. The results rely on PAC-Bayesian bounds for general, possibly unbounded loss-functions based on exponential families. Further, we provide a concrete algorithmic realization of the framework and new methodologies for learning-to-optimize. Finally, we conduct four practically relevant experiments to support our theory. With this, we showcase that the provided learning framework yields optimization algorithms that provably outperform the state-of-the-art by orders of magnitude.



Michael Sucker, Jalal Fadili and Peter Ochs

Introduction

Typically, optimization algorithms are derived by performing a worst-case analysis on a specific class of problems. Doing so one obtains theoretical convergence guarantees for any instance inside the class. However, the abstract class of problems contains an enormous number beyond the concrete problem of interest, often including also pathological functions that do not occur in practical applications. Furthermore, since the derivation is done “on pen and paper”, all modeling steps have to be analytically tractable. This limits the design of algorithms. Both of these restrictions can impair the performance of the resulting method on a concrete problem instance. Nevertheless, both restrictions can be alleviated through learning: Given a concrete application and performance-measure, the algorithm is trained on examples (data) to improve its performance. This enables the automatic adaptation of sophisticated algorithms to this particular setting. However, there is no free lunch: If the algorithm is explicitly based on quantities that are not analytically tractable, one cannot expect to obtain the same theoretical guarantees as for the worst-case analysis discussed above. Since the practical usefulness of an optimization algorithm without convergence guarantees is at least questionable, this is a major problem and poses the first central question:

What kind of theoretical guarantees can be given for a learned optimization algorithm? Are we able to ensure its usefulness?

One possible alternative to the common guarantees is of statistical nature: Even if we do not know exactly what the algorithm does, we can still observe its performance during training. However, this begs the question whether its performance on the training data is actually representative for the performance on unseen data.
Therefore, in the first part of this paper we provide a theoretical framework for learning an optimization algorithm based on its performance on a training set, that is, based on the empirical risk together with a generalization bound for the (true) risk. A popular framework that provides such generalization bounds is the PAC-Bayesian approach to learning, which we apply to the setting of learning-to-optimize. This yields the following informal version of our main theoretical result (compare Example
28
). It states that, with high probability we will observe a (training) data set
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}
for which the given bound on the risk of the algorithm’s output will hold uniformly in a so-called index
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma}
and distribution
\mathbb{Q}
:

Theorem 1

(Informal)
.

Under mild boundedness assumptions on the optimization algorithm, the
\mathbb{Q}
-average population loss
\mathcal{R}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}}
of the algorithm’s output can be bounded by the
\mathbb{Q}
-average empirical loss
\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}}
of the algorithm’s output plus some remainder term
r_{N}
that vanishes with the size
N
of the data set, that is, for all
\varepsilon>0
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}}\left\{\forall{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma},\ \forall\mathbb{Q}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}_{1}}:\mathbb{Q}[\mathcal{R}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}}]\leq\mathbb{Q}[\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}}]+r_{N}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma})\right\}\geq 1-\varepsilon\,.

Especially, the uniformity in
\mathbb{Q}
allows for
learning
such a distribution.
This provides one possible answer to the question about theoretical guarantees for learning-to-optimize. However, while being a generalization bound, such a guarantee is a statement about
relative
and not absolute values, that is, how the true risk
compares to
the empirical risk. Thus, one still has to train the optimization algorithm in such a way that the empirical risk is indeed small enough to be worth the effort. This is particularly important in the area of learning-to-optimize, as there are already algorithms that can provably solve the given problems in a rather short amount of time. Hence, the second central question that arises is of a more practical nature and pertains to the actual training of such an algorithm:

How do we learn an optimization algorithm, such that its performance is clearly superior to the one achieved by a worst-case analysis?

Therefore, in the second part of this work, we develop a concrete algorithmic realization, which allows for learning an optimization algorithm and evaluating the corresponding theoretical guarantee. This involves several key design choices that have not been used before and which are of interest in their own right. Furthermore, as empirical evaluation of our claims, we conduct four practically relevant experiments, all dealing with very different classes of functions, thereby demonstrating the wide applicability and strong practical performance of our approach. 1.1
Related Work

The literature on both learning-to-optimize and the PAC-Bayes learning approach is vast. Hence, for learning-to-optimize we will mainly focus on approaches that provide some theoretical guarantees. Especially, this excludes many model-free approaches, which replace the whole update step with a learnable mapping such as a neural network.
Chen et al. (2021)
provide a good overview about the variety of approaches in learning-to-optimize, and good introductory references for the PAC-Bayesian approach are given by
Guedj (2019)
,
Hellström et al. (2023)
, and
Alquier (2024)
.

1.1.1
Broader Context of Learning-to-Optimize

Optimization is an integral part of machine learning. Thus, learning-to-optimize has significant overlap with the areas of meta-learning (or “learning-to-learn”) and AutoML. The first one is a subset of learning-to-optimize, because it is mostly concerned with determining parameters of machine learning models
(
Vilalta and Drissi, 2002
;
Hospedales et al., 2021
)
. AutoML, however, more broadly refers to automating all steps necessary to create a machine learning application, which also includes the choice of an optimization algorithm and its hyperparameters
(
Yao et al., 2018
;
Hutter et al., 2019
;
He et al., 2021
)
.

1.1.2
Learning-to-Optimize with Guarantees

Learned optimization methods may lack theoretical guarantees for the sake of convergence speed
(
Chen et al., 2021
)
. Yet, there are applications where a convergence guarantee is of highest priority:
Moeller et al. (2019)
provide an example where a purely learning-based approach fails to reconstruct the crucial details in a medical image. Also, they prove convergence of their method by restricting the output to descent directions, for which mathematical guarantees exist. The basic idea is to trace the learned object back to, or constrain it to, a mathematical object with convergence guarantees. Similarly,
Sreehari et al. (2016)
provide sufficient conditions under which the learned mapping is a proximal mapping. Related schemes, under different assumptions and guarantees, are given by
Chan et al. (2016)
,
Teodoro et al. (2017)
,
Tirer and Giryes (2018)
,
Buzzard et al. (2018)
,
Ryu et al. (2019)
,
Sun et al. (2019)
,
Terris et al. (2021)
and
Cohen et al. (2021)
. A major advantage of these methods is the fact that the number of iterations is not restricted a priori. However, a major drawback is their restriction to specific algorithms and problems. This contrasts with the approach of unrolling, pioneered by
Gregor and LeCun (2010)
, which limits the number of iterations, yet in principle can be applied to every iterative optimization algorithm. Here,
Xin et al. (2016)
study the convergence properties of the IHT algorithm, while
Chen et al. (2018)
consider the unrolled ISTA. However, a difficulty in the theoretical analysis of unrolled algorithms is actually the notion of convergence itself, such that one rather has to consider the generalization performance. Only few works have addressed this: Either directly by means of Rademacher complexity
(
Chen et al., 2020b
)
, or indirectly in form of a stability analysis
(
Kobler et al., 2020
)
, as algorithmic stability is linked to generalization
(
Bousquet and Elisseeff, 2000
;
Bousquet and Elisseeff, 2002
;
Shalev-Shwartz et al., 2010
)
.
Our theoretical analysis corresponds to the approach of unrolling, that is, a fixed number of iterations. However, in the experiments we stay more closely to the iterative approach of learning an update step that can be applied for an arbitrary number of iterations.

1.1.3
Design-Choices in Learning-to-Optimize

A major problem of many learned optimization algorithms, especially the ones based on recurrent neural networks (RNN), is their restriction to a certain number of iterations: They cannot be trained for an arbitrary number of iterations due to instabilities or memory bottlenecks. Further, often they do not generalize well to more iterations than they were trained for
(
Andrychowicz et al., 2016
;
Chen et al., 2017
;
Lv et al., 2017
;
Chen et al., 2021
)
. A typical way to mitigate this problem is to split the whole trajectory into smaller parts
(
Andrychowicz et al., 2016
;
Chen et al., 2017
;
Metz et al., 2019
)
. However, often this does not lead to fully satisfactory results either, such that other approaches have been proposed: To improve generalization,
Lv et al. (2017)
introduce random scaling of the coordinates and the addition of a convex function to the objective.
Wichrowska et al. (2017)
introduce a hierarchical RNN architecture, and additionally draw the number of unrollings and the unrolling length from a heavy-tailed exponential distribution. While achieving the needed generalization, this approach does not achieve the same wall-clock time as simple optimization algorithms.
Metz et al. (2019)
replace the recurrent neural network with a multilayer perceptron (MLP), and they use two unbiased gradient estimators instead of one. Doing so they manage to train algorithms that are faster in wall-clock time than standard ones like Adam.

Chen et al. (2020a)
consider training techniques in general, and introduce a progressive scheme that gradually increases the unrolling length, as well as an imitation learning approach to learn to mimic analytic optimizers.

Besides the optimizer, a crucial design choice in learning-to-optimize is that of the loss function. Typically, either the final loss or a weighted sum of the losses along the iterations is used
(
Chen et al., 2021
)
.

We introduce a new loss function for training optimization algorithms, motivated by an intuitive theoretical argument. Further, we use a single learned update based on MLPs instead of an RNN, and we split the trajectory into subtrajectories and randomize its total length, however, in a new way.

1.1.4
PAC-Bayesian Bounds through Change-of-Measure

PAC is an acronym for
Probably Approximately Correct
, and PAC-
Bayes
refers to the fact that one considers distributions instead of points
(
Alquier, 2024
)
. This framework allows for giving high probability bounds on the risk, either as an oracle or as an empirical bound. The key ingredient is a change-of-measure inequality, the choice of which strongly influences the corresponding bound. The one used most often is based on a variational representation of the Kullback–Leibler divergence due to
Donsker and Varadhan (1975)
, employed, for example, by
Catoni (2004)
;
Catoni (2007)
. Yet, not all bounds are based on a variational representation, that is, holding uniformly over all posterior distributions
(
Rivasplata et al., 2020
)
. While many bounds involve the Kullback–Leibler divergence as measure of proximity
(
McAllester, 2003a
;
McAllester, 2003b
;
Seeger, 2002
;
Langford and Shawe-Taylor, 2002
;
Germain et al., 2009
)
, other divergences have been used:
Honorio and Jaakkola (2014)
prove an inequality for the
\chi^{2}
-divergence, which is also used by
London (2017)
.
Bégin et al. (2016)
and
Alquier and Guedj (2018)
use the Renyi-divergence (
\alpha
-divergence).
Ohnishi and Honorio (2021)
propose PAC-bounds based on f-divergences, which include the Kullback–Leibler-,
\alpha
- and
\chi^{2}
-divergences. More recently,
Amit et al. (2022)
propose to replace the Kullback-Leibler divergence by so-called “integral probability metrics”, which encompass, for example, the Wasserstein distance that obeys many favorable properties and also captures the geometry of the underlying space
(
Villani et al., 2009
, see)
. Motivated by this,
Haddouche and Guedj (2023)
also investigate PAC-Bayesian generalization bounds for the Wasserstein distance and their interplay with the output of optimization algorithms. A major advantage of using the Wasserstein distances instead of the Kullback-Leibler divergence is the fact that it does not constrain the support of the distribution a-priori through the choice of the prior. On the other hand, it demands assumptions on the loss function, which are not necessarily satisfied in learning-to-optimize.
We give a general PAC-Bayesian theorem based on exponential families. Here, prior, posterior, divergence and data dependence are given naturally. Further, it allows for implementing an abstract learning framework that can be applied to a wide variety of algorithms.

1.1.5
Boundedness of the Loss Function

A major drawback of many of the existing PAC-Bayes bounds is the assumption of a bounded loss-function. This assumption is mainly used to apply some exponential-moment inequality like the Hoeffding- or Bernstein-inequality
(
Rivasplata et al., 2020
;
Alquier, 2024
)
and several ways have been developed to circumvent this problem:
Germain et al. (2009)
explicitly include the exponential-moment in the bound,
Alquier et al. (2016)
use so-called Hoeffding- and Bernstein-assumptions,
Catoni (2004)
restricts to the sub-Gaussian or sub-Gamma case. Another possibility to ensure the generalization or exponential-moment bounds is to use properties of the algorithm:
London (2017)
uses algorithmic stability to provide PAC-Bayes bounds for SGD.
We consider suitable properties of optimization algorithms aside from algorithmic stability to ensure the exponential-moment bounds.

1.1.6
Minimization of the PAC-Bound

PAC-bounds relate the true risk to other terms such as the empirical risk. Yet, they do not directly say anything about the absolute numbers. Thus, learning procedures based on the PAC-Bayesian theory typically aim to minimize this bound:
Langford and Caruana (2001)
compute non-vacuous generalization bounds through a combination of PAC-bounds with a sensitivity analysis.
Dziugaite and Roy (2017)
extend this by minimizing the PAC-bound directly.
Pérez-Ortiz et al. (2021)
also consider learning as minimization of the PAC-Bayes bound and provide tight generalization bounds.
Thiemann et al. (2017)
are able to solve the minimization problem resulting from their PAC-bound by alternating minimization.
We follow this approach and consider learning as minimization of the PAC-Bayesian bound.

1.1.7
Choice of the Prior

A common difficulty in learning with PAC-Bayesian bounds is the choice of the prior distribution, as it heavily influences the performance of the learned models and the generalization bound
(
Catoni, 2004
;
Dziugaite et al., 2021
;
Pérez-Ortiz et al., 2021
)
. In part, and especially for the Kullback-Leibler divergence, this is due to the fact that the divergence term can dominate the bound, keeping the posterior close to the prior. This leads to the idea of choosing a data- or distribution-dependent prior
(
Seeger, 2002
;
Parrado-Hernández et al., 2012
;
Lever et al., 2013
;
Dziugaite and Roy, 2018
;
Pérez-Ortiz et al., 2021
)
, which, by using an independent subset of the data set, gets optimized to yield a good performance.
The prior distribution strongly influences the performance of our learned algorithms. Thus, we use a data-dependent prior. Further, we show how the prior can be used for preserving essential properties during learning: It is key to control the trade-off between convergence guarantee and convergence speed.

1.1.8
More Generalization Bounds

There are more areas of machine learning research that study generalization bounds. Importantly, the field of “stochastic optimization” (SO) provides generalization bounds for specific algorithms. The main differences to our setting are the learning approach and the assumptions made:

•

Instead of a distribution over hpyerparameters, the algorithms in SO generate a point estimate, and one studies the properties of this point in terms of the stationarity measure of the true risk functional
(
Bottou et al., 2018
;
Davis and Drusvyatskiy, 2022
;
Bianchi et al., 2022
)
.

•

Instead of an abstract algorithm, the setting in SO is more explicit. Thus, more assumptions have to be made. Typical assumptions are (weak) convexity
(
Shalev-Shwartz et al., 2009
;
Davis and Drusvyatskiy, 2019
)
, bounded gradients
(
Défossez et al., 2022
)
, bounded noise
(
Davis and Drusvyatskiy, 2022
)
, or smoothness
(
Kavis et al., 2022
)
.

We provide a principled way to learn a distribution over general hyperparameters of an abstract algorithm under weak assumptions and go explicitly beyond analytically tractable quantities. Therefore, the methodology is independent of the chosen implementation.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows: In Section
2
we introduce the notation and provide a formal description of the setting. In Section
3
, we derive the general PAC-Bayesian theorem and relate it to other existing bounds. In Section
4
, we identify properties of optimization algorithms that allow to apply the PAC-Bayesian theorem. As this strongly relies on assumptions on the prior distribution, we provide a probabilistic constraining procedure that allows to enforce such constraints in Section
5
. Then, in Section

we describe the learning procedure and our design choices for learning-to-optimize, and in Section
7
we conduct the experiments.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}U},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}V}})]=\int_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{U}}}\int_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{V}}}f({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}u}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}v}})\ \mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}V}|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}U}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}u}}}(d{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}v}})\ \mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}U}}}(d{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}u}})=\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}U}}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}V}|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}U}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}u}}}\left[f({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}u}},\cdot)\right]\right]\,.

Note that changing the order of integration is not allowed in this case. However, if
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}U}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}V}}
are independent, their joint distribution is given by the product
\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}U}}}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}V}}}
for which Fubini’s theorem is applicable, and the iterated integration is clarified by the subscripts
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}U},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}V}}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[f({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}U},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}V}})\right]=\int_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{U}}}\int_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{V}}}f({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}u}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}v}})\ \mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}V}}}(d{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}v}})\ \mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}U}}}(d{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}u}})=\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}U}}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}V}}}\left[f({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}u}},\cdot)\right]|_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}u}}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}U}}}\right]\,.

Finally, our theoretical results rely on the notions of probability kernels and exponential families, whose definitions are recalled in Appendix
A
.

2.2
Main Assumptions and Definitions for Learning Optimization Algorithms

We assume that we are given a distribution over loss-functions with a specific structure, which is modelled by a random variable:

Assumption 2
.

We are given a Polish space
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{P}}}
(separable and complete metrizable topological space) and a non-negative and measurable loss-function
\ell:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{P}}}\to[0,+\infty]
. Further, for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we are given i.i.d. random variables
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}_{1},...,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}_{N}}:\bigl(\Omega,\mathfrak{F},\mathbb{P}\bigr)\to{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{P}}}
.

Then, ideally, we would like to find a solution to each realization of the random objective:

Find
x^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}*}}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{P}}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, s.t.
x^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}*}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}})\in\argmin_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\ell(x,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}})\quad\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}-a.s.

(1)

However, we will only solve a relaxed version of (
1
) and provide
generalization bounds
for the
average performance
after training on a data set.

Definition 3
.

The measurable function

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}:\bigl(\Omega,\mathfrak{F},\mathbb{P}\bigr)\longrightarrow{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{P}}^{N}},\quad\omega\mapsto\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}_{1},...,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}_{N}}\right)(\omega)

is called a
data set
, and if the random variables
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}_{1},...,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}_{N}
are i.i.d., that is,
\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{i}}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}}
, it is called an
i.i.d. data set
. Further,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{P}}^{N}}
is called the
data-space
.

PAC-Bayesian generalization bounds involve a so-called posterior distribution, which usually is a “data-dependent distribution”. As also pointed out by
Rivasplata et al. (2020)
, this is an instance of a probability kernel (also called a “stochastic-” or “Markov kernel”):

Definition 4
.

Let
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}
be a data set with data-space
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{P}}^{N}}
, and let
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{U}}
be a measurable space. A probability kernel from
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{P}}^{N}}
to
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{U}}
is called a
data-dependent distribution
on
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{U}}
.

For solving problem (
1
), for every realization
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}
of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}
, we apply an optimization algorithm
\mathcal{A}
to
\ell(\cdot,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}})
. For this, we consider a similar setting as
London (2017)
, that is, randomized algorithms are considered as deterministic algorithms with randomized hyperparameters:

Definition 5
.

Let
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{H}}}
be a Polish space and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. A measurable function

\mathcal{A}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{H}}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{P}}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}},x^{(0)},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}})\mapsto\mathcal{A}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}},x^{(0)},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}})\,,

is called a
parametric algorithm
.
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the space of the optimization variable,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{P}}}
the space of the parameters of the loss function, and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{H}}}
the space of the hyperparameters of the algorithm.

Please note that
\mathcal{A}
corresponds to the
whole
algorithm, that is, for an iterative algorithm its output is the final iterate. In the PAC-Bayesian approach, learning
\mathcal{A}
refers to finding a distribution
\mathbb{Q}
on
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{H}}}
based on its performance on a data set
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}
. For this, one needs a reference distribution, called the
prior
, which can (and should) encode prior knowledge about suitable choices of hyperparameters:

Assumption 6
.

We are given a parametric algorithm
\mathcal{A}
with Polish hyperparameter space
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{H}}}
, and a (prior) distribution
\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}}
on
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{H}}}
that is induced by a random variable
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}:\bigl(\Omega,\mathfrak{F},\mathbb{P}\bigr)\longrightarrow{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{H}}}
, which is independent of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}
. Further, the initialization
x^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is given and fixed.

Notation 7
.

To simplify the notation, we use the short-hand
\ell({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}):=\ell(\mathcal{A}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}),{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}})
. Furthermore, if not needed explicitly,
x^{(0)}
and
\bigl(\Omega,\mathfrak{F},\mathbb{P}\bigr)
will not be mentioned in the following.

Definition 8
.

Suppose
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}
and
\ell
satisfy Assumption
2
, and
\mathcal{A}
satisfies Assumption
6
. The
risk
of
\mathcal{A}
is defined as the measurable function:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{H}}}\longrightarrow[0,+\infty],\ {\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}\mapsto\mathbb{E}\left[\ell\left(\mathcal{A}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}),{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}[\ell({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}})]=\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}}[\ell({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}},\cdot)]\,.

Similarly, for an i.i.d. dataset
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}=({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}_{1},...,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}_{N}})
the
empirical risk
is defined as:

\hat{\mathcal{R}}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{H}}}\times{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{P}}^{N}}\longrightarrow[0,+\infty],\quad({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}})\mapsto\hat{\mathcal{R}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}_{i}}\right)\,.

The following theory is based on exponential families, which is a very flexible class of distributions. We highlight the data-dependency in the following adjusted definition:

Definition 9
.

Let
\emptyset\neq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma}
be an index set,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}
a data set with data-space
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{P}}^{N}}
, and let
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{U}}
be a measurable space. A family of probability kernels
(\mathbb{Q}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}})_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma}}
from
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{P}}^{N}}
to
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{U}}
is called a
data-dependent exponential family
(in
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}
), if there is a probability measure
\mu
on
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{U}}
, functions
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma}\times{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{P}}^{N}\to(0,+\infty)
, and measurable functions
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{U}}\times{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{P}}^{N}}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}b}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{U}}\to(0,+\infty)
, such that

\mathbb{Q}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}})={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}b}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s})\exp\left(\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}),{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}(\cdot,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}})\rangle\right)\cdot\mu
for every
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma}
,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{P}}^{N}}
, that is,
\mathbb{Q}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}},\mathsf{B})=\int_{\mathsf{B}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}b}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}u}){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s})\exp(\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}),{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}u},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}})\rangle)\ \mu(d{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}u})
,
\mathsf{B}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathfrak{B}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{U}})
.

We introduce data-dependency through
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}
, since it strongly affects the shape of the distribution and, contrary to
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}
, is defined on the underlying space
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{U}}
. Since we want to learn a distribution over hyperparameters
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{H}}}
, we make the following assumption:

Assumption 10
.

On the hyperparameter space
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{H}}}
, we are given a data-dependent exponential family
(\mathbb{Q}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}})_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma}}
in
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}
with dominating probability measure
\mu=\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}}
, such that the map
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}\mapsto{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}b}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}})\exp(\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}),{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}})\rangle)
is non-trivial and integrable w.r.t.
\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}}
for every
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma}
,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{P}}^{N}}
, that is,
\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}b}\exp(\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}),{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}(\cdot,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}})\rangle)\right]\in(0,\infty)
.

Then, as shown in Lemma
38
in Appendix
B
, every member of the data-dependent exponential family is indeed a data-dependent distribution on
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{H}}}
. In the following, the last integral in Assumption
10
will be of great interest. Here, we will use a similar notation as in
Barndorff–Nielsen (2014)
and denote

\begin{split}&c({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}):=\int_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{H}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}b}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}})\exp(\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}),{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}})\rangle)\ \mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}}(d{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}})=\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}b}\exp(\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}),{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}(\cdot,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}})\rangle)\right]\,,\\
&\kappa({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}):=\log\left(c({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}})\right)=\log\left(\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}b}\exp\left(\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}),{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}(\cdot,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}})\rangle\right)\right]\right)\,.\end{split}

(2)

With this notation, it holds that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s})=c({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s})^{-1}
.

Remark 11
.

(i)

If
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}
describes a lower-dimensional manifold in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
,
(\mathbb{Q}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}})_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma}}
is called a
curved
exponential family
(
Efron, 1975
)
, whose properties might differ from the ones for
linear
exponential families, for example, convexity of the map
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}\mapsto{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s})
.

(ii)

In PAC-Bayes, the dominating measure
\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}}
is usually referred to as
prior
and every distribution
\mathbb{Q}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}_{1}}(\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}})
is referred to as a
posterior
. This deviates from the standard definitions of prior and posterior in Bayesian statistics.

(iii)

In general, the integrability assumption is restrictive, as it affects the choice of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}b},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}
. However, in Section
4
we will
construct

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}
such that this holds anyway.

(iv)

In the special case
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}b}\equiv 1
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma})\equiv{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}
, the map
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}\mapsto c({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s})
is the moment-generating function of the random variable
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}})
. Similarly, in this case
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}\mapsto\kappa({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s})
is the corresponding cumulant-generating function.

Finally, we will restrict
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma}
to a compact set. This is needed to get a uniform bound in
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}

(
Langford and Caruana, 2001
;
Catoni, 2007
;
Alquier, 2024
, see)
.

Assumption 12
.

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma}
is a compact set with finite covering
\mathcal{O}:=\{\mathsf{O}_{1},...,\mathsf{O}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{K}}}\}
, that is,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma}\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{K}}}\mathsf{O}_{i}
, such that there is a constant
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{O}}}
, which, for every
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{P}}^{N}}
, allows for the bound
\max_{i=1,...,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{K}}}\ \sup_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}^{\prime}\in\mathsf{O}_{i}}\kappa({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}})-\kappa({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}^{\prime},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}})\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{O}}}
.

Remark 13
.

The non-trivial part of this assumption is the existence of the constant
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{O}}}
for the given finite covering. It does hold, for example, if
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma}
is a finite set (
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{K}}=|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma}|
,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{O}}}=0
), or, if
\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma},\rho\right)
is a compact metric space and
\kappa
is Lipschitz-continuous in
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}
(uniformly in
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}
) with Lipschitz constant
L
, such that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{O}}}=L\cdot\max_{i=1,...,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{K}}}\mathrm{diam}\ \mathsf{O}_{i}
, where the diameter of a set
\mathsf{A}
is given by
\mathrm{diam}\ \mathsf{A}=\sup_{x,y\in\mathsf{A}}\rho(x,y)
.

3
General PAC-Bayesian Theorem

In this section we prove the general PAC-Bayesian bound for data-dependent exponential families, which then can be specialized into a generalization bound of the learned parametric optimization algorithm
\mathcal{A}
. It is based on the following two lemmas, whose proofs can be found in Appendix
C
and
D
, respectively. The first lemma is a form of the Donsker–Varadhan variational formulation and yields uniformity in the distributions
\mathbb{Q}
, while the second lemma yields uniformity in
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma}
by controlling
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}\mapsto\kappa({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}})
for every
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{P}}^{N}}
.

Lemma 14
.

Lemma 15
.

Suppose that Assumption

\mathbb{P}\{\kappa({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}})>{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t}\}\leq\exp(-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t})

Theorem 16
.

Suppose that Assumptions
10
and
12
hold, and assume that
\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}}[c({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma},\cdot)]\leq 1
for all
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma}
. Then, it holds that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Bigl\{

\displaystyle\forall{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma},\ \forall\mathbb{Q}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}_{1}}(\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}}):

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}\left[\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}),{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}(\cdot,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}})\rangle+\log({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}b})\right]|_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}}\leq D_{\rm{KL}}(\mathbb{Q}\ \|\ \mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}})+\log\left(\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{K}}}{\varepsilon}\right)+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{O}}}\Bigr\}\geq 1-\varepsilon\,.

Proof.

Applying Markov’s inequality to the non-negative random variable
c({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}})
yields for
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma}
:

\mathbb{P}\left\{c({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}})>\exp({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t})\right\}\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[c({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}})]}{\exp({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t})}\leq\exp(-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t})\,.

This implies that
\mathbb{P}\left\{\kappa({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}})>{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t}\right\}\leq\exp(-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t})
. Hence, Lemma
15
is applicable and gives:

\mathbb{P}\left\{\sup_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma}}\kappa({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}})\leq\log\left(\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{K}}}{\varepsilon}\right)+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{O}}}\right\}\geq 1-\varepsilon\,.

Using Lemma
14
gives:

\mathbb{P}\left\{\sup_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma}}\sup_{\mathbb{Q}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}_{1}}(\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}})}\mathbb{Q}[\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}),{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}(\cdot,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}})\rangle+\log({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}b})]|_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}}-D_{\rm{KL}}(\mathbb{Q}\ \|\ \mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}})\leq\log\left(\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{K}}}{\varepsilon}\right)+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{O}}}\right\}\geq 1-\varepsilon\,.

Simply rearranging and reformulating yields the result.
∎

Remark 17
.

(i)

Note that the statement is still true for a data-dependent prior
\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}}
: Given another independent data set
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}^{\prime}}
, one needs to assume that
\mathbb{E}\left[c({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma},({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}^{\prime}}))\right]\leq 1
.

(ii)

In Section
4
we provide sufficient conditions s.t.
\mathbb{E}[c({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}})]\leq 1
holds for all
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}>0
.

(iii)

Typically,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{K}}
is (related to) the covering-number of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma}
, and
\log({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{K}})
bears the intrinsic dimension of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma}
. Thus, in full generality, it might be large. For us, however, it only has a minor influence, since
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma}\subset\mathbb{R}
, and the empirical risk is typically much larger.

(iv)

Thanks to the reviewers we became aware of the monograph by
Hellström et al. (2023)
, which proposes a similar general PAC-Bayesian theorem. On first sight, it seems like theirs is more general than ours. However, Example
19
clarifies this. Furthermore, we want to remark that the first version of our Theorem
16
appeared in 2022.

For the rest of the paper, we set
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}b}\equiv 1
, such that
\log({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}b})\equiv 0
. The following corollary shows an example of how to transform Theorem
16
into a high-probability bound on the risk. The proof is given in Appendix
E
.

Corollary 18

(PAC-Bayesian Generalization Bound)
.

Denote
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}
by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}=({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}^{(1)},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}^{(r)})
with
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}^{(r)}:=({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}^{(2)},...,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}^{(k)})
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}
by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}=({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}^{(1)},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}^{(r)})
with
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}^{(r)}:=({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}^{(2)},...,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}^{(k)})
. If
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}^{(1)}=\mathcal{R}-\hat{\mathcal{R}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}^{(1)}>0
, the following are equivalent for any
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma}
,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{P}}^{N}}
,
\mathbb{Q}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}_{1}}(\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}})
:

(i)

\mathbb{Q}\left[\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}),{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}(\cdot,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}})\rangle\right]\leq D_{\rm{KL}}(\mathbb{Q}\ \|\ \mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}})+\log\left(\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{K}}}{\varepsilon}\right)+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{O}}}
,

(ii)

\mathbb{Q}[\mathcal{R}]\leq\mathbb{Q}[\hat{\mathcal{R}}(\cdot,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}})]+\frac{1}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}^{(1)}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma})}\left(D_{\rm{KL}}(\mathbb{Q}\ \|\ \mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}})+\log\left(\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{K}}}{\varepsilon}\right)+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{O}}}-\mathbb{Q}\left[\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}^{(r)}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}),{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}^{(r)}(\cdot,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}})\rangle\right]\right)\,.

In particular, if Theorem
16
applies, we can replace (i) with (ii).

Using similar rearrangements, the following example relates Theorem
16
to other known PAC-Bayesian bounds:

Example 19
.

(i)

Assume that the loss-function is bounded, that is,
0\leq\ell\leq C
, and define
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma}=\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}\}
,
b\equiv 1
,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{O}}}=0
,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}):=\left(\mathcal{R}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h})-\hat{\mathcal{R}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}),\ C^{2}\right)
, and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}):=\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma},-\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}^{2}}{8}\right)
. Then we recover Catoni’s bound
(
Catoni, 2003
;
Alquier, 2024
)
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Bigl\{

\displaystyle\forall\mathbb{Q}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}_{1}}(\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}}):

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}[\mathcal{R}]\leq\mathbb{Q}[\hat{\mathcal{R}}(\cdot,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s})]|_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(D_{\rm{KL}}(\mathbb{Q}\ \|\ \mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}})+\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)+\frac{\lambda^{2}C^{2}}{8N}\right)\Bigr\}\geq 1-\varepsilon\,.

(ii)

Assume that
\ell
takes values in
[0,1]
and let
D:[0,1]^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
be convex. Further, define
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma}=\{1\}
,
b\equiv 1
,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{O}}}=0
,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}):=\left(nD(\mathcal{R}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}),\hat{\mathcal{R}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}})),\ \log(\mathbb{E}_{({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H})}[\exp(nD(\mathcal{R},\hat{\mathcal{R}}))])\right)
, and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}):=\left(1,-1\right)
.
Then we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Bigl\{

\displaystyle\forall\mathbb{Q}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}_{1}}(\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}}):\mathbb{Q}[D(\mathcal{R},\hat{\mathcal{R}}(\cdot,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}))]|_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\left(D_{\rm{KL}}(\mathbb{Q}\ \|\ \mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}})+\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)+\log(\mathbb{E}_{({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H})}[\exp(nD(\mathcal{R},\hat{\mathcal{R}}))])\right)\Bigr\}\geq 1-\varepsilon\,.

Applying Jensen’s inequality to the left term, we get the bound of
Germain et al. (2009)
. Similarly, one can obtain the bound of
Bégin et al. (2014)
.

(iii)

Consider two measurable functions
f,g:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{H}}\times{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{P}}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
, and define
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma}=\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}\}
,
b\equiv 1
,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{O}}}=0
,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}):=(f({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}),g({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}))
, and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}):=({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma},-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma})
.
Then our assumption
\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}}[c({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma},\cdot)]\leq 1
reads

\mathbb{E}_{({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H})}\left[\exp({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}(f-g))\right]\leq 1
,
which is the same assumption as in
Hellström et al. (2023, Thm. 5.1)
. Similarly, defining
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}):=(1,-1)
and
T({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}):=(f({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}),\ \log(\mathbb{E}_{({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H})}[\exp(f)]))
,
we also get a similar bound as
Hellström et al. (2023, Proposition 5.2)
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Bigl\{

\displaystyle\forall\mathbb{Q}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}_{1}}(\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}}):\mathbb{Q}[f(\cdot,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s})]|_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}}

\displaystyle\leq D_{\rm{KL}}(\mathbb{Q}\ \|\ \mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}})+\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)+\log(\mathbb{E}_{({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H})}[\exp(f)])\Bigr\}\geq 1-\varepsilon\,.

4
Learning-to-Optimize with Guarantees

Here, for our setting in Subsection
2.2
, we consider properties of optimization algorithms that assert the necessary condition of Theorem
16
, namely
\mathbb{E}[c({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}})]\leq 1
for all
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma}
, with
c
defined as in Equation
2
, to employ the PAC-Bayesian bound from Section
3
.

4.1
Worst-Case Bounds

In the next theorem, the additional assumption on
\mathcal{A}
is sufficient to ensure the conditions of Theorem
16
. Essentially, it requires the loss of the algorithm’s output to be bounded. It can be used, for example, if one wants to combine the learning procedure with existing worst-case guarantees. Yet, as shown in Section
4.2
, it is too restrictive to achieve a significant acceleration compared to the standard choices from a worst-case analysis. For this, please recall our short-hand notation
\ell({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}})=\ell(\mathcal{A}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}),{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}})=\ell(\mathcal{A}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}},x^{(0)},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}),{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}})
, that is,
\ell(x^{(0)},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}})
evaluates the loss function at
x^{(0)}
, while
\ell({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}})
evaluates the loss function at the output of the algorithm with hyperparameters
h
and starting from
x^{(0)}
.

Theorem 20
.

Suppose that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}
and
\ell
satisfy Assumption
2
, and suppose that
\mathcal{A}
satisfies Assumption
6
. Further, assume that there is a measurable function
\rho:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{H}}}\longrightarrow[0,\infty)
, such that for every
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{H}}}
it holds that
\ell({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}},\cdot)\leq\rho({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}})\ell(x^{(0)},\cdot)

\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}}
-a.s. Furthermore, let
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}
be a corresponding i.i.d. data set of size
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Finally, assume that
\mathbb{E}\bigl[\ell(x^{(0)},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}})^{2}\bigr]<\infty
, and define
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}:(0,\infty)\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{H}}}\times{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{P}}^{N}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
through:

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}):=\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma},\ -\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}^{2}}{2}\right)\,,\ \ \ {\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}):=\left(\mathcal{R}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}})-\hat{\mathcal{R}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}),\ \frac{\rho^{2}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}})}{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\ell(x^{(0)},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}})^{2}\right]\right)\,.

Then it holds that
\mathbb{E}[c({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}})]\leq 1
for all
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}>0
.

Proof.

Since
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}
are independent, their joint distribution is given by the product measure
\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}}
. Thus, by Fubini’s theorem we get:

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}(\mathcal{R}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}})-\hat{\mathcal{R}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}))\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}(\mathcal{R}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}})-\hat{\mathcal{R}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}))\right)\right]\Bigl|_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}}\right]\,.

Hence, first consider the inner integral for a fixed
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{H}}}
. Then, by definition and the i.i.d. assumption one gets:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}(\mathcal{R}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}})-\hat{\mathcal{R}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}))\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\ell({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}_{i}})-\mathbb{E}[\ell({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}})]\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}}{N}\left(\ell({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}},\cdot)-\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}}[\ell({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}},\cdot)]\right)\right)\right]\,.

The loss-function is non-negative and, by assumption on
\mathcal{A}
, can be bounded
\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}}
-a.s. Thus, for every
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{H}}}
,
\ell({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}})
is a non-negative random variable with finite second-moment, as
\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}}[\ell({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}},\cdot)^{2}]\leq\rho({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}})^{2}\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}}\left[\ell(x^{(0)},\cdot)^{2}\right]<\infty
. Hence, by Lemma
39
, we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}}{N}\left(\ell({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}},\cdot)-\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}}[\ell({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}},\cdot)]\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Bigl(\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}^{2}}{2N^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}}[\ell({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}},\cdot)^{2}]\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Bigl(\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}^{2}}{2N^{2}}\rho({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}})^{2}\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}}[\ell(x^{(0)},\cdot)^{2}]\Bigr)\,.

Therefore we have the following bound:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}(\mathcal{R}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}})-\hat{\mathcal{R}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}))\right)\right]\leq\exp\Bigl(\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}^{2}}{2N}\rho({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}})^{2}\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}}\left[\ell(x^{(0)},\cdot)^{2}\right]\Bigr)\,.

This can be rearranged into
\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}(\mathcal{R}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}})-\hat{\mathcal{R}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}))-\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}^{2}}{2N}\rho({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}})^{2}\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}}[\ell(x^{(0)},\cdot)^{2}]\right)\right]\leq 1
, as the right-hand side does not depend on
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}
.
Since
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}
are independent, and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{H}}}
was arbitrary, this inequality does hold
\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}}
-a.s. Therefore, one directly gets the bound

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}(\mathcal{R}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}})-\hat{\mathcal{R}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}))-\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}^{2}}{2N}\rho({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}})^{2}\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}}[\ell(x^{(0)},\cdot)^{2}]\right)\right]\leq 1
.
Now, again by Fubini’s theorem, one can also switch the order of integration to get:

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}(\mathcal{R}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}})-\hat{\mathcal{R}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}))-\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}^{2}}{2N}\rho({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}})^{2}\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}}[\ell(x^{(0)},\cdot)^{2}]\right)\right]\Bigl|_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}}\right]\leq 1\,.

Inserting the definition of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}
gives
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}),{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}})\rangle\right)\right]|_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}}\right]\leq 1
. Here, the inner term is the same as

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}),{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}})\rangle\right)\right]=\int_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{H}}}}\exp\left(\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}),{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}))\rangle\right)\ \mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}}(d{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}})=c({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}})
. Hence, this is the same as
\mathbb{E}[c({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}})]\leq 1
.
∎

Remark 21
.

The argument still works for a data-dependent prior, if the corresponding data sets
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}^{\prime}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}
are independent: While interchanging the integration w.r.t.
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}^{\prime}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}
is not allowed, an interchange w.r.t.
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}
is still valid (under the integral), that is, for a function
f
it would hold

\mathbb{E}\left[f({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}^{\prime}})\right]=\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}^{\prime}}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}^{\prime}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}^{\prime}}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}}\left[f({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}},\cdot,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}^{\prime}})\right]|_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}}\right]\right]=\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}^{\prime}}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}^{\prime}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}^{\prime}}}\left[f(\cdot,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}^{\prime}})\right]|_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}}\right]\right]
, and the inner term is
\leq 1
in any case.

4.2
Conditional Boundedness

Typically, the previous approach is too restrictive, because the boundedness assumption on
\mathcal{A}
already requires theoretical worst-case estimates
almost surely
. For example, if
(\ell(\cdot,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}))_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{P}}}}
is a family of quadratic functions, and one tries to learn the step-size of gradient descent, the boundedness prevents step-size parameters that lie outside the worst-case convergence regime, as they would lead to a diverging behaviour, which increases the incurred empirical risk dramatically. Thus, to motivate the upcoming discussion, consider the following thought-experiment:

Example 22
.

Consider
\ell(x,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}):=\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}}{2}x^{2}
and assume that the chosen algorithm is gradient descent, that is
x^{(k+1)}=x^{(k)}-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}\ell^{\prime}(x^{(k)},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}})
. For a given
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}
, the optimal step-size is
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}=\frac{1}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}}
, which gives convergence in one step. Then, if
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}
is given by samples from the distribution
\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}}=0.99\delta_{1}+0.01\delta_{100}
, a worst-case analysis would suggest to take
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}_{w}=\frac{1}{100}
. In this case, we would have an algorithm that converges in a single step for 1% of the problem instances, while having a linear convergence rate of
(\frac{99}{100})^{k}
for the other 99%. Another choice is to take
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}_{d}=1
, which leads to an algorithm that does converge in a single step for 99% of the problem instances, but diverges in 1% of the cases. By restricting to the 99% of the cases where convergence does occur, the overall difference in speed is drastic.

Hence, in this section, a different approach is taken: We actually allow for divergence, if it only occurs in rare cases with a controllable probability, that is, “almost surely” is relaxed to “with a sufficiently large probability”. Essentially, we only consider the loss for all those hyperparameters, where the loss is bounded by a certain constant, as well as the probability for that to occur. Then, in Section
5
, we develop a technique that allows the user to actually control this probability. Clearly, a stronger guarantee trades for convergence speed.

Definition 23
.

Given a measurable function
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{P}}}\to\mathbb{R}
, the (parametric)
sublevel set

\mathsf{L}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}}\subset{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{H}}}\times{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{P}}}
is defined as
\mathsf{L}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}}:=\{({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}})\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{H}}}\times{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{P}}}\ :\ \ell({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}})\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}})\}
. The sections of
\mathsf{L}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}}
for fixed
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{H}}}
will be denoted by
\mathsf{L}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}}
.

In Lemma
40
we show that
\mathsf{L}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}}
is indeed a measurable set. This is not obvious, as the loss function and the algorithm are composed in a non-standard way. This result further implies that the sections
\mathsf{L}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}}
are measurable, too. Since
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{H}}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{P}}}
are Polish spaces, the product
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{H}}}\times{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{P}}}
is again Polish. Hence, there exists a regular version of the conditional probability of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}
, given
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}
, that is, a kernel
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{H}}}\to{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{P}}},\ ({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}},\mathsf{B})\mapsto\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}}[\mathsf{B}]
. By
Witting (2013, Thm. 1.122, p.124)
, this determines a regular version of the conditional probability of
({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}})
, given
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}
, through
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{H}}}\to{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{H}}}\times{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{P}}},\ ({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}},\mathsf{B})\mapsto\mathbb{P}_{({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}})|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}}[\mathsf{B}]:=\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}}[\mathsf{B}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}}]
, and we have
\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}}
-a.s. the equality
\mathbb{P}\{({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}})\in\mathsf{B}\ |\ {\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}\}=\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}}[\mathsf{B}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}}]
. In particular, this applies to the sublevel set
\mathsf{L}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}}
, and the map
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}\mapsto\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}}[\mathsf{L}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}}]
is measurable.

Definition 24
.

Let
\mathsf{L}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}}
be a parametric sublevel set. Define the
sublevel probability
as the measurable function
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}\mapsto\rho({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}):=\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}}[\mathsf{L}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}}]
.

This construction allows us to give a more fine-grained analysis of the algorithm, as it allows to trade the boundedness assumption for the sublevel probability. This basically extends a worst-case analysis, which would correspond to an uniform upper bound. Motivated by Lemma
41
, we define the
sublevel risk
as the expect loss
conditioned
on the sublevel set:

Definition 25
.

Let
\mathsf{L}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}}
be a parametric sublevel set. Then the
sublevel risk

\mathcal{R}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{H}}}\to[0,+\infty]
is defined as the conditional expectation of the loss given
\mathsf{L}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}}
:

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}\mapsto\mathcal{R}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}):=\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}}\left[\ell({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}},\cdot)\ |\ \mathsf{L}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}}\right]=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\rho({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}})}\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}}[\ell({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}},\cdot)\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{L}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}}}],&\text{if $\rho({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}})>0$}\,;\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\,.\end{cases}

Given a data set
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}=({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}_{1},...,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}_{N}})
, the
empirical sublevel risk

\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{H}}}\times{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{P}}^{N}}\to[0,+\infty]
is defined as

({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}})\mapsto\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}):=\frac{1}{\rho({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}})}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{L}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}_{i}})\ell({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}_{i}})
.

The following theorem is a direct generalization of Theorem
20
. Especially, note that the additional assumption on
\mathcal{A}
is not needed anymore.

Theorem 26
.

Suppose that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}
and
\ell
satisfy Assumption
2
, and suppose that
\mathcal{A}
satisfies Assumption
6
. Further, let
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}
be a corresponding i.i.d. data set of size
N\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\mathsf{L}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}}
be a parametric sublevel set with sublevel probability
\rho
. Assume that
\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}}\{\rho>0\}=1
and
\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}^{2}]<\infty
. Define
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}:(0,\infty)\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{H}}}\times{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{P}}^{N}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
as

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}):=\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma},\ -\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}^{2}}{2}\right)\,,\ \ \ {\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}):=\left(\mathcal{R}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}})-\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}),\ \frac{1}{\rho({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}})^{2}N}\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}^{2}\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{L}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}}}\right]\right)\,.

Then, for all
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}>0
, it holds that
\mathbb{E}[c({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}})]\leq 1
.

Proof.

The proof is very similar to the proof of Theorem
20
and basically uses the same reasoning. Let
\ell_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}):=\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{L}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}})\ell({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}})
. Since
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}
are independent, one gets from Fubini’s theorem:

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}(\mathcal{R}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}})-\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}})))\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\exp({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}(\mathcal{R}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}})-\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}})))\right]\Bigl|_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}}\right]\,.

Thus, first consider a fixed
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{H}}}
with
p({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}})>0
. Then, by definition and the i.i.d. assumption, it holds that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}(\mathcal{R}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}})-\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}})))\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}}{N\rho({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}})}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\ell_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}_{i}})-\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}}[\ell_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}},\cdot)]\right)\right)\right]

5
Implementing the Non-divergence – Speed Trade-Off

In Subsection
4.2
, care has to be taken in the choice of the prior
\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}}
: Just minimizing the upper bound as much as possible can lead to a neglect of a high sublevel probability, that is, the algorithm is especially fast on a small subset of the parameters, while it diverges for the rest. This is due to the fact that the term
\frac{1}{\rho({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}})}
might not compensate for the smaller sublevel risk. Thus, if a certain sublevel probability
\varepsilon_{\rm{conv}}\in[0,1]
has to be ensured, one has to enforce it. In the case of PAC-Bayesian learning with absolutely continuous distributions, it suffices to have this property for the prior:

Lemma 29
.

Let
\varepsilon_{\rm{conv}}\in[0,1]
and assume that
\rho({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}})\geq\varepsilon_{\rm{conv}}
a.s. Then, for every
\mathbb{Q}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}_{1}}(\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}})
we have
\mathbb{Q}\{\rho<\varepsilon_{\rm{conv}}\}=0
.

Proof.

By assumption we have
\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}}\{\rho<\varepsilon_{\rm{conv}}\}=0
. Thus, the result follows directly by definition of absolute continuity.
∎

Though the proof is trivial, this lemma has a very important consequence, which we want to stress: If one can guarantee that a required property is satisfied for the prior, it will be
preserved
during the PAC-Bayesian learning process, that is, if the prior only puts mass on hyperparameters that ensure a certain sublevel probability, the posterior will do the same. How to enforce such constraints during construction of the prior is discussed next.

\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}U}}}
, if the restriction to
\mathsf{A}
can be satisfied differently.
Thus, we have to integrate this constraint into a sampling procedure for
\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}U}}}
. Because we do not have any geometrical or topological information about the set
\mathsf{C}
, we resort to statistical information: Given i.i.d. samples
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}V}_{1},...,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}V}_{n}}\sim\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}V}}}
, for a given
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}u}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{U}}
, we are able to evaluate the Bernoulli random variables
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}I_{n}}:=\mathds{1}\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}V}_{n}}\in\mathsf{C}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}u}}\}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. These have the parameter
\mathbb{P}\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}I_{n}}=1\}=\mathbb{P}\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}V}_{n}}\in\mathsf{C}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}u}}\}=\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}V}}}[\mathsf{C}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}u}}]=\rho({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}u})
. Thus, by estimating
\rho({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}u})
with an estimator
\hat{\rho}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}u})
, we approximate the constraint
\mathsf{A}
with
\hat{\mathsf{A}}
:

\mathsf{A}=\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}u}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{U}}\ :\ \rho({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}u})\in[\rho_{l},\rho_{u}]\}\approx\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}u}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{U}}\ :\ \hat{\rho}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}u})\in[\rho_{l},\rho_{u}]\}=:\hat{\mathsf{A}}\,.

To decide whether a given sample
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}U}_{i}}\sim\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}U}}
does lie in
\mathsf{A}
, that is, whether
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}U}_{i}}
can actually be regarded as a sample from
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}U}}}
, we resort to a simple accept-reject mechanism as in
Metropolis-Hastings
-type algorithms
(
Robert and Casella, 2004
)
. Note that this allows to keep an algorithm
inside

\hat{\mathsf{A}}
. However, it does not provide a way
into

\hat{\mathsf{A}}
, let alone
\mathsf{A}
.

We estimate
\rho({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}u})
in a Bayesian way, as it allows us to balance accuracy against computational complexity through uncertainty-quantification, which we use as a stopping criterion: We place a Beta-prior
\mathrm{Beta}(a^{(0)},b^{(0)})
over the interval
[0,1]
. As we do not have prior knowledge, and the map
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}u}\mapsto\rho({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}u})
can be discontinuous
3
3

3

Consider learning the step-size parameter
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}>0
for gradient descent on quadratic functions with largest eigenvalue
L
: The algorithm converges for
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}<\frac{2}{L}
(
\rho({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}})=1
) and diverges for
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}>\frac{2}{L}
(
\rho({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}})=0
) .
, we use a noninformative prior
(
Berger, 1985
, Ch. 3.3)
, that is,
a_{0}=b_{0}=1
. Since the Beta distribution is the conjugate prior for the Bernoulli distribution
(
Berger, 1985
, p.130)
, that is, the posterior is again a Beta-distribution, after observing a sample
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}I_{k+1}}
, the parameters
a^{(k)},b^{(k)}
get updated as:

a^{(k+1)}=a^{(k)}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}I_{k+1}},\qquad b^{(k+1)}=b^{(k)}+(1-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}I_{k+1}})\,.

This allows us to do the estimation iteratively: We only draw a new sample
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}I_{n+1}}
as long as
Q^{(n)}(q_{u})-Q^{(n)}(q_{l})\geq\varepsilon
, where
Q^{(n)}
denotes the quantile-function of
\mathrm{Beta}(a^{(n)},b^{(n)})
, and
q_{u},q_{l},\varepsilon\in[0,1]
are parameters that specify the accuracy of the estimation. Finally, one can use the posterior mean
\frac{a^{(n)}}{a^{(n)}+b^{(n)}}
or posterior mode
\frac{a^{(n)}-1}{a^{(n)}+b^{(n)}-2}
(provided
a^{(n)},b^{(n)}>1
) as point estimate
\hat{\rho}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}u}}
. By adjusting
q_{l},q_{u}
or
\varepsilon
, one can balance between accuracy and computational complexity. However, the number of iterations needed also depends on the true probability: For
\rho({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}u})\approx 0
or
\rho({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}u})\approx 1
, the uncertainty decreases significantly faster than for
\rho({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}u})\approx 0.5
. This procedure is summarized in Algorithm

and depicted in Figure

5.1.2
Broader Context

Different, yet conceptually similar ideas for how to cut the computational cost of Bayesian Markov-Chain-Monte-Carlo algorithms through subsampling have been proposed:
Korattikara et al. (2014)
use sequential hypothesis tests to reach the binary accept-reject decision in the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm.
Bardenet et al. (2014)
estimate the accept-reject step in such a way that it coincides with the true accept-reject step with a user-specified probability.
Maclaurin and Adams (2014)
introduce an auxiliary binary variable
z_{n}\in\{0,1\}
, which allows for querying only a subset of the data for the computation of the exact likelihood. And
Quiroz et al. (2018)
combine subsampling with a bias-correction strategy to speed-up the sampling procedure. A summary of different approaches is given by
Bardenet et al. (2017)
. We leave the analysis for our proposed approximation to future work.

Minimization of the PAC-Bound

Learning is phrased as minimizing the PAC-Bayesian upper-bound. Hence, in this subsection we consider
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}
,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}
and item
(ii)
from Corollary

, and we seek for
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma}

\inf_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma}}\ \inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}_{1}}(\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}})}\ \mathbb{Q}[\hat{\mathcal{R}}(\cdot,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}})]+\frac{\left(D_{\rm{KL}}(\mathbb{Q}\ \|\ \mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}})+\log\left(\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{K}}}{\varepsilon}\right)+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{O}}}-\mathbb{Q}\left[\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}^{(r)}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}),{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}^{(r)}(\cdot,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}})\rangle\right]\right)}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}^{(1)}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma})}\,.

By factoring out

Empirically, the performance of the learned algorithm is significantly improved by the following two design choices. The motivation is to prevent overfitting and to learn a scale-independent contraction of the loss:

6.3.1
Ratio of Losses

The canonical loss function to be minimized is the empirical risk
\hat{\mathcal{R}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}_{i}})
, and, if
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{H}}}
is high-dimensional or if
N
is large, one resorts to stochastic empirical risk minimization. While this kind of loss was used extensively before, for learning-to-optimize it has a strong disadvantage: Only the overall outcome after
n_{\rm{max}}
iterations gets penalized. Thus, it does not take the performance along the trajectory into account. Further, often it is hard to minimize (due to training instabilities) and does not lead to the desired performance. To circumvent this,
Andrychowicz et al. (2016)
proposed to use
\tilde{\ell}_{\rm{train}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}},x^{(0)}):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(x^{(i)},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}})
. Again, this formulation has a decisive flaw: Under most objectives, if the algorithm performs reasonably well, the loss at the beginning is
several orders
of magnitude larger than the loss at the end. Hence,
\tilde{\ell}_{\rm{train}}
mainly penalizes the loss at the beginning, leading to an algorithm that minimizes the loss very fast in early iterations, yet slows down a lot in later iterations. This is due to
\tilde{\ell}_{\rm{train}}
being
scale-sensitive
. Additionally, the incurred loss might vary strongly with the initialization
x^{(0)}
alone, thereby introducing ambiguity into the incurred losses. We propose to use the
ratio of consecutive losses
:

\ell_{\rm{train}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}},x^{(0)},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t}):=\sum_{i=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t}}\mathds{1}_{\{\ell(x^{(i-1)},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}})>0\}}\frac{\ell(x^{(i)},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}})}{\ell(x^{(i-1)},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}})}\,,\qquad{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t}\in\mathbb{N},\ {\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t}\leq n\,.

This has several advantages: First, the loss is not scale-sensitive anymore, such that it favors hyperparameters that yield a good performance in each iteration. Second, there is no ambiguity in the observed loss through the initialization, as the only criterion is a strong contraction of the loss (instead of a small loss). Third, the incurred losses do not vary too much, which empirically makes it easier to choose hyperparameters of the learning procedure. However, it also has a disadvantage: If the function values do indeed converge in a setting where the optimal loss is strictly greater than zero, this gets fully penalized, as then
\frac{\ell(x^{(i)},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}})}{\ell(x^{(i-1)},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}})}\equiv 1
. For now, we do not know how to avoid this problem (apart from just stopping the iterations in case of convergence) while keeping the advantages.

The yellow algorithm was trained with our design choice and the orange one without. Besides that, everything else was kept the same. In the left plot we can see that using the ratio of consecutive losses strongly improves the performance, and in the right plot we can see that the randomization procedure yields generalization beyond
n_{\rm{train}}

and
an overall better performance.

6.3.2
Randomized Trajectory Length

Training
\mathcal{A}
with fixed initialization
x^{(0)}
and fixed trajectory length leads to overfitting: Applying it at another starting point
\tilde{x}^{(0)}
or applying it for more iterations typically does not work, or even leads to divergence. To avoid this, we propose the following randomization: Fix
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t}\leq n_{\rm{train}}
and set
y:=x^{(0)}
.

0)

Sample a parameter
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}
uniformly at random from
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}
.

1)

Compute
x^{(1)},...,x^{({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t})}
with
\mathcal{A}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}},y)
and the loss
\ell_{\rm{train}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}},y,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t})
, and update
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}
.

2)

Sample
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}R^{(k)}}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t}}{n_{\rm{train}}})
. If
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}R^{(k)}}=0
, set
y:=x^{({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t})}
and go to step 1). If
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}R^{(k)}}=1
, set
y:=x^{(0)}
and go to step 0).

The random variable
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}R^{(k)}}
decides whether the algorithm gets restarted from
x^{(0)}
with a new parameter
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}}
, or if one continuous the current trajectory. The choice
\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t}}{n_{\rm{train}}}
ensures that the expected trajectory-length equals
n_{\rm{train}}
: Define
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Z}:=\inf\{k\in\mathbb{N}\ :\ {\color[rgb]{0,0,0}R^{(k)}}=1\}
.
Then,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Z}\sim\mathrm{Geo}(\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t}}{n_{\rm{train}}})
is a geometrically distributed with expectation
\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Z}]=\frac{n_{\rm{train}}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t}}
. Therefore, for the actual length
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Z}
of the trajectory we get
\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}]={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t}\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Z}]=n_{\rm{train}}
.

Remark 33
.

Similarly to
Andrychowicz et al. (2016)
, we omit the computation of second-order derivatives during training. Additionally, and surprisingly, it usually suffices to consider single iterates, that is
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t}=1
. That amounts to learning an update step that is agnostic to the recurrent nature of the optimization algorithm and just learns to adapt to the local geometry of the loss function
along the iterations
.

Everything else is kept the same, that is, both were trained with Algorithm

\varphi_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}})=\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}),\tilde{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}})\rangle

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}_{1},...,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n_{\rm{sam}}}}}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{H}}}

\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}}\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}\}:=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{i=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n_{\rm{sam}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}w}_{i}\delta_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}_{i}}}\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}}\}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}}\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h}_{j}}\}

with the potential

Thus, the parameters are given by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}=(A,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}+n}=:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{P}}}

m

L\in[L_{-},L_{+}]
and

. Hence, assuming that it is not feasible to compute the smoothness and strong-convexity constants for each problem separately, the given class of functions is
L_{+}
-smooth and
m_{-}

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\alpha}=\Bigl(\frac{2}{\sqrt{L_{+}}+\sqrt{\mu_{-}}}\Bigr)^{2}
and
\beta=\Bigl(\frac{\sqrt{L_{+}}-\sqrt{\mu_{-}}}{\sqrt{L_{+}}+\sqrt{\mu_{-}}}\Bigr)^{2}

)
. Further details can be found in Appendix

7.2
Image Processing

We consider (gray-scale)
image denoising/deblurring
with a regularizer given as smooth approximation to the
L_{1}
-norm of the image derivative, that is, problems of the form:

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{1}{2}\|Ax-b\|^{2}+\lambda\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\sqrt{(D_{h}x)_{i,j}^{2}+(D_{w}x)_{i,j}^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}\qquad\lambda\in\mathbb{R},\ A,D_{h},D_{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},\ b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,.

The matrix
A
describes the “blurring” of the image, while
D_{h}
and
D_{w}
are the discrete image derivatives in h- and w-direction, respectively, which are used to penalize local changes in the image. We use images of height
N_{h}=250
and width
N_{w}=\texttt{int}(0.75\cdot N_{h})=187
. Thus, the dimension
n
of the optimization space is given by
n=46750
. Further, as parameters
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}
we use the observed image and the regularization parameter, that is,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}=(b,\lambda)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}=:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{P}}}
.
Since the problem is smooth and convex, yet not strongly convex, the baseline algorithm is given by the
accelerated gradient descent
(NAG) algorithm due to
Nesterov (1983)
. Its update is given by first computing
y^{(k+1)}=x^{(k)}+\frac{t_{k}-1}{t_{k+1}}(x^{(k)}-x^{(k-1)})
followed by setting
x^{(k+1)}=y^{(k)}-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\alpha}\nabla f(y^{(k+1)})
. We use the optimal choices
t_{k+1}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\sqrt{1+4t_{k}^{2}}\right)
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\alpha}=\frac{1}{L}
. Here, the smoothness constant
L
is given by the largest eigenvalue of
A^{T}A+\frac{\lambda}{\varepsilon}D^{T}D
, where
D\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times n}
is given by “stacking”
D_{h}
and
D_{w}
, that is,
D=\begin{pmatrix}D_{h}&D_{w}\end{pmatrix}^{T}
. Further details can be found in Appendix
F.2
.
The results of this experiment are summarized in Figure

The upper right plot confirms this finding also in terms of computation time. Yet, one can observe a strong increase in computation time for the dotted line (loss per pixel of about
\frac{1}{46750}
), indicating that the learned algorithm often is not able to reach this accuracy. In the lower left plot, one can observe that the predicted PAC-bound is not perfectly tight, yet provides the guarantee to outperform NAG. Finally, the lower right plot shows that, while the algorithm did not reach the sublevel set in all of the test cases, the probabilistically constraint optimization/sampling procedure did work correctly.

7.3
Lasso-Problem

Here we consider the Lasso problem
(
Tibshirani, 1996
)
, that is, a non-smooth problem of the form:

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{1}{2}\|Ax-b\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|x\|_{1}\qquad A\in\mathbb{R}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}m}\times n},\ b\in\mathbb{R}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}m}}\,,

with
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}m}\leq n
. Thus, we are solving an underdetermined system of linear equations with an additional
\ell_{1}
-regularization term, which promotes sparsity in the solution
(
Hastie et al., 2009
, see)
. Hence, the optimization variable is given by
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
As baseline we use the
fast iterative shrinkage-thresholding algorithm
(FISTA) by
Beck and Teboulle (2009)
, which performs an extrapolation step followed by a proximal gradient step, that is, abbreviating
h(x):=\frac{1}{2}\|Ax-b\|^{2}
and
g(x):=\lambda\|x\|_{1}
, the update is given by first computing
y^{(k)}=x^{(k)}+\beta^{(k)}\left(x^{(k)}-x^{(k-1)}\right)
followed by setting
x^{(k+1)}=\text{prox}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\alpha}g}\left(y^{(k)}-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\alpha}\nabla h(y^{(k)})\right)
. Here, the proximal mapping is defined as
\hat{x}=\text{prox}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\alpha}g}(\bar{x})=\argmin_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\ \lambda\|x\|_{1}+\frac{1}{2{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\alpha}}\|x-\bar{x}\|_{2}^{2}
, and can be computed efficiently in closed-form yielding the so-called
soft-thresholding operator
:

\hat{x}_{i}=\begin{cases}\bar{x}_{i}-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\alpha}\lambda\frac{\bar{x}_{i}}{|\bar{x}_{i}|}\,,&|\bar{x}_{i}|>{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\alpha}\lambda\,;\\
0\,,&\text{otherwise}\,,\end{cases}\,,\quad i=1,...,n\,.

We choose
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\alpha}=1/L
, where
L
is the largest eigenvalue of
A^{T}A
, that is, the smoothness parameter of
h
, while
\beta^{(k)}
is set to
\beta^{(k)}:=(t_{k}-1)/t_{k+1}
with
t_{k+1}=(1+\sqrt{1+4t_{k}^{2}})/2
. Further details about the experiment can be found in Appendix
F.3
.

8
Discussion and Limitations

The motivation for this paper was to use more structure in a given problem than is analytically tractable. For this, we considered a distribution over parametric loss functions and formulated the (ultimate) goal in (
1
), that is, to find a solution to each realization from this distribution. Under reasonable assumptions, this problem is too general to be solved. This led to the formulation of the performance of an algorithm in terms of its risk. However, since this is intractable, we derived PAC-Bayesian generalization bounds relating the risk to the empirically observable performance on a data set. This resulted in the formulation of a training objective, which relies heavily on the existence of a prior distribution satisfying our assumptions and yielding a good performance. As such a distribution is typically not known, we derived a procedure to construct it. This involved several key design choices, such as the loss-function, specific randomization steps, and, especially, the probabilistic constraints. Finally, we validated the resulting learning procedure on four practically relevant problems and showed that it yields a superior performance. While these experimental results are promising, we nevertheless see five main limitations of our work. First, the only guarantee that is provided by the PAC-Bayesian bound is an upper bound on the function value after a specified number of iterations. In particular, it does not guarantee that the function values, the iterates, or the gradient norm actually do converge. Second, our learning procedure is
not guaranteed to work
and still involves many design choices. Third, one still has to find a good architecture for each given problem, which can be time-consuming. Fourth, the presented algorithmic procedure has a high computational cost (offline), which however, at least in part, is due to the nature of learning-to-optimize. Finally, the procedure only applies to deterministic algorithms. All these are promising directions of research that we leave to a future work.

Lemma 38
.

Under Assumption
10

\mathbb{Q}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}}
is a data-dependent distribution for every

by

. The map

is
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathfrak{B}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{P}}^{N}})
measurable, as
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}
is measurable w.r.t. the product-
\sigma
-algebra and
\mathbb{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}}
is a finite measure
(
Kallenberg, 2021
, Lemma 1.28, p.25)
. Hence,

As we contrast the learned algorithm to first-order methods, in each iteration
\mathcal{A}
has access to iterates, (sub)gradients, and function values, and the update is solely based on these. Here, we perform preprocessing: The (sub)gradient is split into its norm

and the corresponding unit vector

. Further, the norm is transformed to

to be less scale-sensitive. The iterates
x^{(k)},x^{(k-1)}
are combined into the momentum term
m^{(k)}:=x^{(k)}-x^{(k-1)}

d^{(k)}_{2}

F.3.2
Algorithm

The solutions of the Lasso problem are typically sparse. To achieve this, the algorithm has to identify the coordinates which are non-zero. Therefore, in each iteration, we treat the zero and non-zero entries of
x^{(k)}
(and derived quantities) separately. Here, non-zero entries are written with a
\neq

0

x^{(k)}_{\neq}
and
x^{(k)}_{0}
.Then, first, we compute weights
w_{1},...,w_{8}
with a fully-connected block with ReLU-activation functions, where we use the features
n^{(k)}_{1}=\log(1+\|\nabla\ell(x^{(k)},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p})\|)
,
n^{(k)}_{2}=\log(1+\|x^{(k)}-x^{(k-1)}\|)
,
n^{(k)}_{3}=\log(1+\|p^{(k)}\|)
, where
p^{(k)}=\mathrm{prox}_{\beta g}\left(x^{(k)}-\beta\nabla\ell(x^{(k)},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p})\right)
,
\Delta\ell^{(k)}:=\ell(x^{(k)},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p})-\ell(x^{(k-1)},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p})
,
\Delta g^{(k)}:=g(x^{(k)})-g(x^{(k-1)})
,
\Delta h^{(k)}:=h(x^{(k)},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p})-h(x^{(k-1)},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p})
, the scalar products
s^{(k)}_{\neq}
and
s^{(k)}_{0}
between the (normalized) gradient and (normalized) momentum, and the regularization parameter
\lambda
. Then, we use these weights to perform a reweighting of the directions
d^{(k)}_{1},...,d^{(k)}_{4}
, which are the normalized gradient, the normalized momentum, the normalized residual
x^{(k)}-p^{(k)}
, and the coordinate-wise product between (normalized) gradient and (normalized) momentum. Then, these reweighted directions get fed into a 1x1-convolutional block, which predicts the two directions
d^{(k)}_{out,1}
and
d^{(k)}_{out,2}
, which we use to compute the final update with the proximal mapping, given by

F.4.2
Loss Function and Architecture

Since the mean square error is the standard choice for training models on regression tasks, the loss is given by
\ell(\beta,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}_{i}}):=c(\mathtt{N}(\beta,X_{i}),Y_{i}):=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}(\mathtt{N}_{\beta}(x_{i,j})-y_{i,j})^{2}
.

As model we use a fully-connected two layer neural network with
ReLU
-activation functions. To have more features in the input layer, the input
x
is transformed into the vector
(x,x^{2},...,x^{5})

\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}m}}

and biases
b_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{50},b_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
of the two fully-connected layers. Therefore, the optimization space is of dimension
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}m}=(5\cdot 50)+(1\cdot 50)+50+1=351
.

Remark 43

Note that, as the theory does not apply to stochastic algorithms, we have to compute full gradients. This limits the amount of images (20x20) per data set to 250.

As loss-function, we use a penalized cross-entropy loss to enforce higher classification-accuracy, as parameters
