Shortcuts for adiabatic and variational algorithms in molecular simulation
Julián Ferreiro-Vélez, Iñaki Iriarte-Zendoia, Yue Ban, Xi Chen

Abstract
Quantum algorithms offer a promising route toward computational advantage, but current implementations remain constrained by limited coherence and gate errors, making circuit complexity and noise sensitivity critical considerations. Here, we develop a shortcuts-to-adiabaticity strategy for molecular ground-state preparation and apply it to both digitized adiabatic quantum computing and the variational quantum eigensolver. In the adiabatic framework, we construct an approximate counterdiabatic (CD) Hamiltonian using a nested-commutator expansion. For the molecular systems considered, the CD correction accelerates state preparation and improves energy convergence in the fast-evolution regime, enabling a target accuracy to be reached with a lower implemented-operator count. In the variational setting, we introduce the adiabatic gauge ansatz ( AGA ), obtained by promoting the Pauli-string components generated by the approximate adiabatic gauge potential (AGP) to independent variational generators. We further propose a reduced variant, AGAR , that retains only Pauli generators with weight not larger than two, substantially reducing the ansatz size while maintaining competitive accuracy over the regimes considered. Benchmarks on LiH \mathrm{LiH} and BeH 2 \mathrm{BeH}_{2} show that the proposed ansätze achieve accuracies competitive with established approaches such as UCCSD. Experiments on the IBM_basquecountry superconducting quantum processor, together with simulations under depolarizing noise, show that the reduced CD-inspired ansatz yields smaller energy discrepancies than UCCSD under the noise conditions considered. Overall, our results demonstrate how AGP-inspired operator structures can be used to balance accuracy and implementation cost in molecular simulations on current noise-limited quantum hardware.

II
PRELIMINARIES

II.1
Molecular Hamiltonian and Qubit Encoding

Quantum chemistry aims to determine molecular properties from first principles by solving the electronic Schrödinger equation. Since exact solutions are limited to simple systems, practical calculations rely on controlled approximations and efficient representations of the molecular Hamiltonian. Within the nonrelativistic approximation, the molecular Hamiltonian is written as
[
35
,
8
,
58
]

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{mol}}=

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m_{e}}\nabla_{i}^{2}-\sum_{I=1}^{K}\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2M_{I}}\nabla_{I}^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{I=1}^{K}\frac{e^{2}Z_{I}}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}|\vec{r}_{i}-\vec{R}_{I}|}

(1)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\neq j}^{N}\frac{e^{2}}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}|\vec{r}_{i}-\vec{r}_{j}|}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{I\neq J}^{K}\frac{e^{2}Z_{I}Z_{J}}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}|\vec{R}_{I}-\vec{R}_{J}|}.

Here
N
and
K
denote the number of electrons and nuclei,
m_{e}
and
M_{I}
are the electron and nuclear masses,
\vec{r}_{i}
and
\vec{R}_{I}
represent their spatial coordinates, and
Z_{I}
is the atomic number of nucleus
I
. The first two terms describe the kinetic energies of electrons and nuclei, while the remaining terms account for Coulomb interactions.

The Born-Oppenheimer approximation exploits the large mass difference between nuclei and electrons, allowing the nuclei to be treated as fixed classical point charges.
Under this approximation, the molecular wavefunction can be approximated as
\Psi(\vec{r},\vec{R})\simeq\psi_{e}(\vec{r};\vec{R})\,\chi_{n}(\vec{R})
, where
\psi_{e}
and
\chi_{n}
describe the electronic and nuclear components, respectively.
By treating the nuclei as fixed sources of electrostatic potential, the electronic Hamiltonian (in atomic units
\hbar=m_{e}=e=4\pi\epsilon_{0}=1
) becomes

\hat{H}_{e}=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{i}^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{I=1}^{K}\frac{Z_{I}}{r_{iI}}+\sum_{i<j}^{N}\frac{1}{r_{ij}},

(2)

where
r_{iI}=|\vec{r}_{i}-\vec{R}_{I}|
and

r_{ij}=|\vec{r}_{i}-\vec{r}_{j}|
. The nuclear-nuclear repulsion term can be treated as a geometry-dependent energy shift and is therefore not included in the electronic Hamiltonian.

To facilitate both analytical treatments and numerical simulations, we express the electronic Hamiltonian in the second-quantization form:

\hat{H}_{e}=\sum_{p,q}h_{pq}\hat{a}_{p}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{q}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p,q,r,s}h_{pqrs}\hat{a}_{p}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{q}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{r}\hat{a}_{s}.

(3)

Here,
\hat{a}_{p}^{\dagger}
and
\hat{a}_{p}
are fermionic creation and annihilation operators, respectively, and the electronic Hamiltonian is represented in a basis of antisymmetric spin orbitals
\{\phi_{p}\}
. The coefficients
h_{pq}
and
h_{pqrs}
denote the one- and two-electron integrals, respectively, which encode the one- and two-electron contributions of the molecular Hamiltonian
[
58
,
60
]
. The electronic states are represented in the occupation-number basis, where each spin orbital
\phi_{p}
has occupation
f_{p}\in\{0,1\}
.

To implement Eq. (
3
) on a gate-based quantum computer, fermionic operators are mapped to qubit operators through transformations that express them as combinations of tensor products of Pauli matrices,
\{\hat{a}_{p}^{\dagger},\hat{a}_{p}\}\rightarrow\{\hat{X},\hat{Y},\hat{Z}\}
. Common mappings include the Jordan-Wigner (JW) transformation
[
50
]
, the parity mapping
[
70
]
, and the Bravyi-Kitaev (BK) transformation
[
13
]
. In this work, we adopt the BK transformation, which combines occupation and parity information and can reduce the support of fermionic operators relative to the JW mapping, potentially lowering the implementation cost.

II.2
Counterdiabatic Driving

Throughout this work, we develop CD strategies to accelerate molecular ground-state preparation on quantum devices. As a starting point, we consider an adiabatic interpolation of the molecular Hamiltonian in Eq. (
3
),

\hat{H}_{\lambda}(t)=\sum_{p,q}h_{pq}\,\hat{a}_{p}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{q}+\frac{\lambda(t)}{2}\sum_{p,q,r,s}h_{pqrs}\,\hat{a}_{p}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{q}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{r}\hat{a}_{s},

(4)

where
\lambda(t)\in[0,1]
controls the strength of the electron–electron interaction. This interpolation connects the non-interacting reference Hamiltonian to the fully interacting molecular Hamiltonian. As
\lambda(t)
increases, electronic correlations are progressively introduced, driving the system toward the correlated ground state.

In practice, the complex spectral structure of molecular Hamiltonians and finite evolution times lead to diabatic excitations during the evolution.
To mitigate these effects, we incorporate CD control by augmenting the Hamiltonian as

\hat{H}(t)=\hat{H}_{\lambda}(t)+\hat{H}_{CD}(t),\qquad\hat{H}_{CD}(t)=\dot{\lambda}(t)\hat{A}_{\lambda},

(5)

where
\hat{A}_{\lambda}
is the adiabatic gauge potential (AGP). We adopt a smooth schedule
\lambda(t)=\sin^{2}(\pi t/2T)
, which ensures vanishing time derivatives at boundaries
t=0
and
t=T
, where
T
is the total evolution time.
The AGP generates infinitesimal unitary transformations along the adiabatic path,
suppressing diabatic transitions
[
49
]
. However, computing the exact AGP requires full knowledge of instantaneous eigenstates, making it as difficult as solving the original problem.

To address this challenge, variational CD approaches construct approximate AGPs within a restricted operator space
[
71
]
. In particular, the AGP can be approximated using a truncated nested-commutator expansion
[
22
]
,

\hat{A}_{\lambda}^{(l)}=i\sum_{k=1}^{l}\alpha_{k}\underbrace{[\hat{H}_{\lambda},[\hat{H}_{\lambda},...[\hat{H}_{\lambda},}_{2k-1}\partial_{\lambda}\hat{H}_{\lambda}]]],

(6)

where
k=1,\ldots,l
indexes the terms of the nested-commutator
expansion, and
l
denotes the truncation order. The
k
-th term
contains
2k-1
nested commutators.
Increasing
l
enlarges the AGP operator space and can generate more nonlocal and higher-body terms. These contributions become particularly relevant near gap-closing points, where long-range CD corrections may be required
[
26
]
. Away from such critical regimes, low-order AGP approximations can capture the dominant CD contributions in gapped systems. We therefore focus on
l=1
as a practical compromise between expressivity and implementation cost.
The coefficients
\vec{\alpha}
are determined by minimizing the action functional

S_{l}(\vec{\alpha})=\mathrm{Tr}\!\left[\hat{G}_{l}(\vec{\alpha})^{2}\right],\qquad\hat{G}_{l}=\partial_{\lambda}\hat{H}_{\lambda}-i[\hat{H}_{\lambda},\hat{A}_{\lambda}^{(l)}].

(7)

This procedure yields the variationally optimal approximation of the AGP within the chosen operator manifold. For larger systems, the operator space can be further optimized using more scalable approximation strategies
[
84
,
74
,
17
]
.

III
Speedup of adiabatic state preparation

In this section, we investigate molecular ground-state preparation within the AQC framework, where the solution is obtained by following the ground state of a slowly varying Hamiltonian. In our setting, the Hamiltonian evolution interpolates from an easily
prepared noninteracting reference Hamiltonian, whose ground state
\ket{\Psi_{0}}
is represented by a single Slater determinant, to the fully interacting electronic Hamiltonian in Eq. (
3
). The electron–electron interaction is gradually introduced through a control schedule
\lambda(t)
. The ideal continuous-time evolution is given by

\ket{\Psi(T)}\equiv\hat{U}(T)\ket{\Psi_{\rm 0}}=\mathcal{T}\exp\!\left(-i\int_{0}^{T}\hat{H}_{\lambda}(t)\,dt\right)\ket{\Psi_{\rm 0}},

(8)

where
\mathcal{T}
denotes time ordering.

To implement this evolution on gate-based hardware, we employ DAQC
[
6
]
, which approximates the time-ordered exponential using a first-order Trotter decomposition. After mapping the fermionic Hamiltonian to qubits, the Hamiltonian is expressed as a sum of Pauli strings,
\hat{H}_{\lambda}(t)=\sum_{j}\hat{H}_{j}(t)
, and the evolution operator is approximated as

\mathcal{T}\exp\left(-i\int_{0}^{T}\hat{H}_{\lambda}(t)\,dt\right)\approx\prod_{n=1}^{N}\prod_{j}\exp\!\left[-i\delta t\,\hat{H}_{j}(n\delta t)\right],

(9)

where
N
is the number of Trotter steps and
\delta t=T/N
.

We now augment the molecular Hamiltonian with a CD correction
\hat{H}_{CD}(t)=\dot{\lambda}(t)\hat{A}_{\lambda}
[Eq. (
5
)], where the AGP
\hat{A}_{\lambda}
is approximated using the first-order nested-commutator expansion (
l=1
) in Eq. (
6
). In practice, we construct
\hat{H}_{CD}(t)
for the
\mathrm{LiH}
and
\mathrm{BeH}_{2}

(b). The resulting AGP contains multiple Pauli operators with different supports. The corresponding AGP-derived operator sets used in the variational construction are detailed in Appendix
B
. Including
\hat{H}_{CD}(t)
can improve ground-state preparation at shorter evolution times
T
by suppressing diabatic transitions.
To quantify this advantage, we compare the energy convergence for different
\delta t
and
N
, with and without CD correction, for
\mathrm{LiH}
and
\mathrm{BeH}_{2}
(Fig.

We quantify convergence through the energy error

\delta E=E(T;{R}_{b})-E_{0}({R}_{b}),

(10)

where

E(T;{R}_{b})=\langle\Psi(T;{R}_{b})|\hat{H}(T)|\Psi(T;{R}_{b})\rangle

is the final energy obtained from the digitized evolution generated by
\hat{H}(t)=\hat{H}_{\lambda}(t)+\hat{H}_{CD}(t)
.
At the end of the evolution,
\dot{\lambda}(T)=0
, so that
\hat{H}_{CD}(T)=0
and
\hat{H}(T)
reduces to the target molecular Hamiltonian. Here,
{R}_{b}
denotes the interatomic distance and
E_{0}({R}_{b})
is the exact ground-state energy obtained by diagonalization. In our simulation, the energy is evaluated at fixed bond distances
{R}_{b}=1.55\AA
for the
\rm{LiH}
and
{R}_{b}=1.33\AA
for the
\rm{BeH}_{2}
.

Figure
2
shows that including
\hat{H}_{CD}(t)
improves convergence over a broad range of
\delta t
and
N
. While the inclusion of
\hat{H}_{CD}(t)
increases the number of Hamiltonian terms, and hence the implementation cost per Trotter step, it allows for shorter total evolution times. For the molecular systems considered here, this trade-off results in a net reduction of the total implemented-operator count required to reach a given accuracy in the fast-evolution regime; see Appendix
A
. Fig.
2
further illustrates the interplay between digitization errors and finite-time diabatic errors. For a first-order Trotter decomposition, the leading splitting error arises from the noncommutativity of the Hamiltonian terms within each Trotter slice and scales as

\epsilon_{\mathrm{tr}}^{(1)}\sim\mathcal{O}\,\left(T\delta t\,\max_{t\in[0,T]}\sum_{j<j^{\prime}}\left\|[\hat{H}_{j}(t),\hat{H}_{j^{\prime}}(t)]\right\|\right).

(11)

Finite protocol duration introduces an additional diabatic error associated with transitions between instantaneous eigenstates. Writing the evolution in terms of the dimensionless parameter
s=t/T
, the leading adiabatic contribution can be schematically estimated as

\epsilon_{\mathrm{ad}}\sim\mathcal{O}\,\left(\frac{1}{T}\max_{s\in[0,1]}\frac{\|\partial_{s}\hat{H}_{\lambda}(s)\|}{\Delta_{\lambda}(s)^{2}}\right),

(12)

where
\Delta_{\lambda}(s)
denotes the relevant instantaneous energy gap of the reference Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\lambda}(s)
.

By construction, the CD term suppresses finite-time diabatic transitions. Once digitized, however, the additional CD terms also modify the commutator structure of the Hamiltonian and therefore do not necessarily reduce the Trotter error itself. For
\mathrm{LiH}
[Fig.
2
(e)], the CD advantage persists at relatively large
\delta t
and small
N
, where digitization effects are more pronounced, indicating that the improvement remains robust in a regime with significant Trotter error. For
\mathrm{BeH}_{2}
[Fig.
2
(f)], the largest improvements occur at short total evolution times, consistent with a stronger role of finite-time diabatic effects. Overall, the net benefit of CD driving reflects a system-dependent competition between the suppression of diabatic transitions and the additional cost of digitizing the CD-assisted Hamiltonian.

Motivated by these results, we next incorporate the same CD/AGP structure into a variational framework. While AQC and DAQC provide a direct route to state preparation, implementing time-dependent evolutions and CD corrections can be resource intensive as the system size increases. In contrast, VQE transfers part of this complexity to a parameterized ansatz and a classical optimization loop. The CD-inspired ansatz, namely the
AGA
, therefore provides a variational route to exploit the AGP operator structure without explicitly implementing the full time-dependent CD evolution, allowing a favorable trade-off between accuracy and implementation cost.

IV
VQE with CD-inspired ansatz

We now turn to the VQE
[
65
,
58
]
for calculating the electronic ground state of molecular systems. VQE is a hybrid quantum–classical algorithm designed to estimate the ground-state energy of many-body Hamiltonians, such as those arising in quantum chemistry. The algorithm employs a parametrized quantum circuit acting on a reference state (
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
) to generate a variational state

|\Psi(\vec{\theta})\rangle=\hat{U}(\vec{\theta})|\Psi_{0}\rangle,

(13)

where
\hat{U}(\vec{\theta})
denotes the parametrized variational circuit and
\vec{\theta}
is the set of variational parameters. The circuit parameters are optimized classically by minimizing

C(\vec{\theta})=\langle\Psi(\vec{\theta})|\hat{H}|\Psi(\vec{\theta})\rangle.

(14)

By the variational principle, minimizing
C(\vec{\theta})
provides an upper bound to the ground-state energy and drives the variational state toward the ground state within the expressible manifold of the ansatz.
Although the present work focuses on ground-state preparation, extensions of the VQE framework allow access to excited states and spectral properties through modified cost functions and ansatz constructions
[
47
,
61
]
.

The performance of VQE critically depends on the design of the variational ansatz in
\hat{U}(\vec{\theta})
of Eq. (
13
). An effective ansatz should be expressive enough to capture the relevant correlations of the target state while avoiding unnecessary parameters and circuit complexity that can hinder optimization and amplify the effects of hardware noise
[
18
,
48
,
55
]
.
Inspired by Hamiltonian-variational approaches
[
63
,
81
]
and CD-inspired circuits
[
19
,
45
]
, we introduce an ansatz based on the operator structure generated by the approximate AGP for the molecule ground-state preparation. This approach, termed the
AGA
, uses the AGP-derived Pauli operators as a physically motivated variational basis, as illustrated in Fig.
1
(c). Building upon Eq. (
6
), the Pauli-string components generated by the nested-commutator expansion are promoted to independent variational generators. The corresponding
AGA
circuit is implemented as

\hat{U}_{\mathrm{AGA}}^{(l)}(\vec{\theta})=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\exp\!\left(-i\theta_{j}\hat{A}_{j}\right),

(15)

where
\{\hat{A}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
are the Pauli-string generators obtained
from the nested-commutator expansion truncated at order
l
,
m
is the
number of generated operators, and
\vec{\theta}=\{\theta_{j}\}
are
independent variational parameters. The explicit Pauli-generator sets
used for
\mathrm{LiH}
and
\mathrm{BeH}_{2}
are listed in
Appendix
B
.

The
AGA
is a Hamiltonian-informed variational ansatz that incorporates dynamical information from CD driving by using the AGP-generated operators as a physically motivated variational basis.
The approximate AGP identifies physically relevant directions associated with changes along the adiabatic path, motivating their use as variational generators for ground-state preparation.
Within the VQE framework, the coefficients of the individual AGP-derived Pauli generators are treated as independent variational parameters, rather than being constrained by the coefficients
\{\alpha_{k}\}
of the approximate AGP in Eq. (
6
)
[
46
,
84
,
74
,
83
]
. This reparameterization increases the variational flexibility within a given AGP-generated operator space and can reduce the need for higher-order nested-commutator expansions when the dominant variational directions are already captured at low order. For
\mathrm{LiH}
, increasing the truncation order from
l=1
to
l=2
does not lead to a noticeable improvement, indicating that the first-order operator space already captures the dominant variational directions for this system. Nevertheless, for
\mathrm{BeH}_{2}
the inclusion of
l=2
operators slightly improves the convergence. This system-dependent trade-off between expressivity and implementation cost is analyzed in Appendix
C
.

For the
\mathrm{LiH}
and
\mathrm{BeH}_{2}
molecules considered here, even the first-order expansion (
l=1
) generates Pauli operators with support on more than two qubits, which can increase the implementation cost on current quantum hardware. To reduce this complexity, we introduce a simplified variant, the reduced adiabatic gauge ansatz (
AGAR
), which retains only Pauli generators of weight not larger than two,
i.e., Pauli strings containing at most two non-identity operators.
This restriction reduces the number of variational generators and the corresponding implementation cost, while preserving competitive accuracy over the regimes analyzed below. To benchmark the proposed
AGA
and
AGAR
ansätze, we compare them with widely used ansätze, including UCCSD
[
3
]
, 3-UpCCGSD
[
56
]
, and ADAPT-Givens VQE
[
38
,
4
]
(see Appendix
D
). Starting from the Hartree–Fock reference state
|\Psi_{0}\rangle=|\Psi_{\rm HF}\rangle
, we evaluate the ground-state energy and its deviation from exact diagonalization for
\mathrm{LiH}
and
\mathrm{BeH}_{2}
across a range of bond distances. Fig.
3
shows the ground-state energy [panels (a, b)] and corresponding errors [panels (c, d)]. Both
\mbox{AGA}^{(1)}
and
\mbox{AGAR}^{(1)}
reach chemical accuracy (below
\sim 1
kcal/mol) over substantial parts of the bond-distance ranges considered. In particular,
\mbox{AGA}^{(1)}

VI
Conclusion and Outlook

In summary, we have investigated CD driving, a central STA technique, for molecular ground-state preparation within both AQC and VQE frameworks. Our results show that CD driving provides a common physical framework for improving finite-time state preparation and for constructing variational ansätze from the operator structure of the approximate adiabatic gauge potential. In the molecular examples considered here, these CD-inspired constructions provide favorable trade-offs between accuracy and implementation cost.

In the AQC paradigm, the inclusion of approximate CD terms suppresses finite-time diabatic excitations and improves convergence to the target ground state, particularly in the fast-evolution regime. Although the CD correction increases the implementation cost of each Trotter step, it can reduce the total evolution resources required to reach a given target accuracy. Its net advantage therefore results from a competition between improved adiabatic following and the additional digitization cost associated with the CD-assisted Hamiltonian.

In the variational setting, we introduced the
AGA
, which promotes the Pauli-string components generated by the approximate AGP to independent variational generators. This construction achieves competitive or improved energy accuracy with respect to established ansätze such as UCCSD and 3-UpCCGSD for the molecular systems studied here. We further introduced the reduced
AGAR
ansatz, which retains AGP-derived Pauli generators with weight not larger than two. This reduction substantially decreases the ansatz size while preserving competitive accuracy over the relevant bond-distance regimes.

Beyond noiseless simulations, we evaluated the proposed ansätze on the
IBM_basquecountry
quantum processor and through controlled depolarizing-noise simulations. Under the noise conditions considered,
AGA
and particularly
AGAR
yield smaller energy discrepancies than UCCSD. These results illustrate the practical importance of balancing variational expressivity with implementation cost when designing ansätze for current noise-limited quantum hardware.

Overall, our results establish the AGP as a useful bridge between dynamical and variational approaches to molecular ground-state preparation. A natural next step is to combine AGP-derived operator spaces with adaptive selection strategies, in which only the most relevant generators are incorporated during the optimization. Recently proposed adaptive CD VQE approaches provide a complementary realization of this idea
[
78
]
, trading a more compact problem-dependent circuit for the additional measurement and optimization overhead associated with adaptive operator selection. Further extensions to Krylov-subspace methods
[
75
]
and excited-state preparation also represent promising directions.

From an experimental perspective, CD driving has already been experimentally demonstrated across multiple quantum platforms, including NMR
[
87
]
, photonic systems
[
72
,
2
]
, and superconducting qubits
[
85
,
40
]
. These developments provide an experimental basis for exploring AGP- and CD-inspired algorithmic constructions on current and future quantum processors.

Appendix C
AGP reparameterization and higher-order nested expansions

The construction of variational quantum ansätze requires balancing expressivity against implementation cost. Enlarging the operator space, increasing the number of variational parameters, or adding circuit layers can improve the accessible state manifold, but also increases the optimization burden and the cost of implementing the resulting circuit. In this Appendix, we analyze the role of AGP reparameterization and the effect of increasing the nested-commutator truncation order.

As a reference, we consider a layered AGP-inspired variational ansatz constructed from the operator structures generated by the nested-commutator expansion,

U_{\mathrm{layer}}^{(l)}(\vec{\theta})=\small{\prod_{p=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{l}\exp\!\left[-i\theta_{pk}\left(i\underbrace{[\hat{H}_{\lambda},[\hat{H}_{\lambda},\ldots[\hat{H}_{\lambda},}_{2k-1}\partial_{\lambda}\hat{H}_{\lambda}]]\right)\right]}.

(17)

Where
N
is the number of layers, and
l
the nested-commutator truncation order. Here, the original AGP coefficients
\alpha_{k}
are not retained. Instead, the overall amplitude of each nested-commutator contribution is promoted to an independent variational parameter
\theta_{pk}
, while the relative weights of the Pauli-string components within that contribution remain fixed.

Reference ansätze for VQE

In this Appendix, we briefly summarize the reference ansätze used for comparison with the proposed

and

D.1
UCCSD

The unitary coupled-cluster ansatz with single and double excitations (UCCSD) is defined from the excitation operators

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,a}t_{i}^{a}\hat{a}_{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i},

(18)

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i,j,a,b}t_{ij}^{ab}\hat{a}_{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{b}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i}\hat{a}_{j},

(19)

where
i,j
label occupied orbitals and
a,b
unoccupied orbitals. The amplitudes
t_{i}^{a}
and
t_{ij}^{ab}
are treated as variational parameters. Defining
\hat{T}=\hat{T}_{1}+\hat{T}_{2}
, the formal unitary ansatz is

\hat{U}_{\mathrm{UCCSD}}(\vec{\theta})=\exp\!\left[\hat{T}(\vec{\theta})-\hat{T}^{\dagger}(\vec{\theta})\right].

(20)

The anti-Hermitian generator makes the ansatz unitary. In practice, implementation on a gate-based quantum processor requires a suitable decomposition, typically in Trotterized or disentangled form.

D.2
k
-UpCCGSD

The
k
-unitary pair coupled-cluster generalized singles and doubles (
k
-UpCCGSD) ansatz reduces the excitation space by combining generalized single excitations with paired double excitations
[
56
]
. The corresponding excitation operators are

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{p,q}t_{p}^{q}\hat{a}_{p}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{q},

(21)

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,a}t_{i_{\alpha}i_{\beta}}^{a_{\alpha}a_{\beta}}\hat{a}_{a_{\alpha}}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{a_{\beta}}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i_{\alpha}}\hat{a}_{i_{\beta}},

(22)

where the generalized singles connect arbitrary orbitals, while the paired doubles transfer electron pairs between spatial orbitals. The
k
-layer ansatz is written as

\hat{U}_{k\text{-UpCCGSD}}=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\exp\!\left[\hat{T}^{(j)}(\vec{\theta}^{(j)})-\hat{T}^{(j)\dagger}(\vec{\theta}^{(j)})\right],

(23)

where each repetition has its own set of variational parameters. In this work, we use
k=3
.

D.3
ADAPT-Givens

ADAPT-VQE
[
38
,
4
]
is an adaptive variational protocol rather than a fixed ansatz. It iteratively constructs the ansatz by selecting operators from a predefined pool based on their contribution to the energy gradient. At iteration
n
, the gradient with respect to operator
\hat{A}_{k}
is given by

\frac{\partial E^{(n)}}{\partial\theta_{k}}=\langle\Psi^{(n)}|[\hat{H},\hat{A}_{k}]|\Psi^{(n)}\rangle.

(24)

The operator with the largest gradient magnitude is added to the ansatz, and the procedure is repeated until the gradient norm falls below a threshold
\epsilon
.

In the ADAPT-Givens implementation considered here, the operator pool is constructed from particle-number-conserving Givens rotations. In the single-excitation subspace, their action can be represented as

\displaystyle U_{\mathrm{GR}}(\theta)|01\rangle

\displaystyle=\cos\theta\,|01\rangle-\sin\theta\,|10\rangle,

(25)

\displaystyle U_{\mathrm{GR}}(\theta)|10\rangle

\displaystyle=\sin\theta\,|01\rangle+\cos\theta\,|10\rangle.

These rotations preserve particle number and provide the operator pool used by the adaptive optimization in our benchmark calculations.
