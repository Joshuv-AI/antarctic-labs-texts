Stability Implies Robust Convergence of a Class of Preconditioned Parallel-in-time Iterative Algorithms
Shu-Lin Wu, Tao Zhou, Zhi Zhou

Abstract
Solving evolutionary equations in a parallel-in-time manner is an attractive topic and many algorithms are proposed in recent two decades. The algorithm based on the block α \alpha -circulant preconditioning technique has shown promising advantages, especially for wave propagation problems. By fast Fourier transform for factorizing the involved circulant matrices, the preconditioned iteration can be computed efficiently via the so-called diagonalization technique, which yields a direct parallel implementation across all time levels. In recent years, considerable efforts have been devoted to exploring the convergence of the preconditioned iteration by studying the spectral radius of the iteration matrix, and this leads to many case-by-case studies depending on the used time-integrator. In this paper, we propose a unified convergence analysis for the algorithm applied to u ′ + A ​ u = f u^{\prime}+Au=f , where σ ⁡ ( A ) ⊂ ℂ + \sigma(A)\subset\mathbb{C}^{+} with σ ⁡ ( A ) \sigma(A) being the spectrum of A ∈ ℂ m × m A\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m} . For any one-step method (such as the Runge-Kutta methods) with stability function ℛ ⁡ ( z ) \mathcal{R}(z) , we prove that the decay rate of the global error is bounded by α / ( 1 − α ) \alpha/(1-\alpha) , provided the method is stable, i.e., max λ ∈ σ ⁡ ( A ) ⁡ | ℛ ⁡ ( Δ ​ t ​ λ ) | ≤ 1 \max_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)}|\mathcal{R}(\Delta t\lambda)|\leq 1 . For any linear multistep method, such a bound becomes c ​ α / ( 1 − c ​ α ) c\alpha/(1-c\alpha) , where c ≥ 1 c\geq 1 is a constant specified by the multistep method itself. Our proof only relies on the stability of the time-integrator and the estimate is independent of the step size Δ ​ t \Delta t and the spectrum σ ⁡ ( A ) \sigma(A) .

Introduction

We are interested in solving the following evolutionary equation parallel-in-time (PinT):

y^{\prime}+Ay=g,~A\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m},

(1)

with initial value
y(0)=y_{0}
. The spectrum of the matrix
A
is supposed to lay on the right hand-side of the complex plane, i.e.,

\sigma(A)\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{+}=\{z=\delta+i\omega:\delta\geq 0,\omega\in\mathbb{R}\}.

Note that the system (
1
) is widely used as an spatially semi-discrete scheme
of many evolutionary partial differential equations, e.g.:

•

Diffusion equation:
u_{t}-\Delta u=f
,where
A=-\Delta_{h}\approx\Delta
denotes the discrete Laplacian,
and we have
\sigma(A)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

•

Acoustic wave equation:
u_{tt}-\Delta u=f
. In this case,
we can make an order reduction to yield the first-order system (
1
) with

y=\begin{bmatrix}u\\
v\end{bmatrix},~A=\begin{bmatrix}&-I_{x}\\
-\Delta_{h}&\end{bmatrix},~g=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
f\end{bmatrix},

(2)

Then we have
\sigma(A)\subseteq{\rm i}\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

•

Schrodinger equation:
{\rm i}u_{t}-\Delta u+V(x)u=f
with a positive potential term
V(x)>0
. Then we have
\sigma(A)\subseteq{\rm i}\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

In the recent two decades, the research toward reducing the computation time
via time parallelization is a hot topic (see
[
10
]
for a comprehensive review).
In this paper, we will focus on the approach
that uses the diagonalization technique proposed by Maday and Ronquist in
[
29
]
.
In that pioneer work, they formulated the time-stepping system into a space-time
all-at-once
system. Then they
diagonalize the time stepping matrix and solve all time steps in parallel.
Such a technique was first used in
[
15
]
for wave equations.
The concrete algorithm lies in using the geometrically increasing time step-sizes

\{\Delta t_{n}=\mu^{n-1}\Delta t_{1}\}_{n=1}^{N_{t}}
with some parameter
\mu>1
, in order
to make the time discretization matrix diagonalizable. The algorithm is
directly parallel, but the roundoff error arising from the diagonalization
procedure increases dramatically for large
N_{t}
.
In order to balance the roundoff error and the discretization error,
the quantity
N_{t}
can not be too large (in practice, people choose
N_{t}=20\sim 25
).

To overcome the restriction on
N_{t}
, the diagonalization technique was used in an iterative fashion

[
28
,
23
,
21
,
26
,
25
,
19
]
, that will be investigated in this paper.
The idea is briefly explained as follows. First, we partition the time domain
(0,T)
by
N_{t}
equally spaced time points

\{t_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N_{t}}
with
step size
\Delta t=T/N_{t}
. Then we apply a time-integrator to (
1
) (such as Runge-Kutta (RK) methods)

y_{n+1}+\mathcal{R}(\Delta tA)y_{n}=\eta_{n},\quad n=0,1,\dots,N_{t}-1,

(3)

with a given initial value
y_{0}
, where
\eta_{n}
is a known quantity specified by the source term at
t_{n}
. The increment matrix
\mathcal{R}(\Delta tA)

is deduced from the stability function of the time-integrator. Instead of solving the
N_{t}
difference equations (
3
) level-by-level, we form these equations into an
all-at-once
system

\mathcal{K}{\bm{u}}={\bm{b}},

(4)

where
{\bm{u}}=(y_{1}^{\top},y_{2}^{\top},\dots,y_{N_{t}}^{\top})^{\top}
,
{\bm{b}}=((\eta_{0}-\mathcal{R}(\Delta tA)y_{0})^{\top},\eta_{1}^{\top},\dots,\eta_{N_{t}}^{\top})^{\top}
and

\mathcal{K}=I_{t}\otimes I_{x}+B\otimes\mathcal{R}(\Delta tA).

(5)

Here and hereafter
I_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{t}\times N_{t}}
and
I_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}

denotes the identity matrices in time and space respectively and the Toeplitz matrix
B
is

B=\begin{bmatrix}0&&&\\
1&0&&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&1&0\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{t}\times N_{t}}.

(6)

To solve the all-at-once system (
4
), we propose a block
\alpha
-circulant preconditioner:

\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}=I_{t}\otimes I_{x}+C(\alpha)\otimes\mathcal{R}(\Delta tA),

(7)

where
C(\alpha)
denotes the following
\alpha
-circulant matrix

C(\alpha)=\begin{bmatrix}0&&&\alpha\\
1&0&&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&1&0\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{t}\times N_{t}},

(8)

and apply the preconditioned iteration:

\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}\Delta{\bm{u}}^{k}={\bm{r}}^{k},{\bm{u}}^{k+1}={\bm{u}}^{k}+\Delta{\bm{u}}^{k},~{\bm{r}}^{k}:={\bm{b}}-\mathcal{K}{\bm{u}}^{k},~k=0,1,\dots.

(9)

Thanks to the property of the
\alpha
-circulant matrices (see, e.g.,
[
1
, Lemma 2.1]
),
we can make a block Fourier diagonalization for the preconditioner
\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}
and this allows to compute

\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}^{-1}(\mathcal{K}{\bm{u}}^{k}-{\bm{b}})
in (
9
) highly parallel for
all time levels
[
21
,
26
,
25
,
23
,
28
,
19
]
.

In this paper, we aim to answer the question:
under what conditions
the iterative algorithm (
9
) converges rapidly and robustly?

In existing references, people answered this question by examining the radius
of the spectrum of the iteration matrix
\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}^{-1}\mathcal{K}
, see e.g.,

[
28
,
19
,
26
,
25
,
37
]
for
parabolic problems and
[
16
,
23
]
for the wave equations.
For some special
time-integrators (e.g., the implicit Euler method
[
26
,
25
]
,
the implicit leap-frog method
[
23
]
,
and the two-stage singly diagonal implicit RK method
[
37
]
) it was shown

\rho(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}^{-1}\mathcal{K})\leq\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha},~\alpha\in(0,1).

(10)

The analysis in these works is rather technical and heavily depends on the special
property of the time-integrator, e.g., the sparseity, Toeplitz structure and diagonal dominance
of the time-discretization matrix.

Instead of exploring the spectrum of the iterative matrix,
we shall examine the error of the preconditioned iteration directly, and prove that for
any
one-step time stepping method (
3
)
the error
{\bm{err}}^{k}={\bm{u}}^{k}-{\bm{u}}
of the algorithm (
9
) satisfies (Theorem
2.1
)

\|(I_{t}\otimes P){\bm{err}}^{k+1}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\|(I_{t}\otimes P){\bm{err}}^{k}\|_{\infty},

(11)

where
P

is the eigenvector matrix of
A
, provided the method solving (
1
) is stable, i.e.,

\max_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)}|\mathcal{R}(\Delta t\lambda)|\leq 1
. If
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
, the precondtioned iteration (
9
)
converges linearly with the convergence factor
\alpha/(1-\alpha)
.
The result (
11
) well explains the robust convergence observed in
[
26
,
25
,
17
,
16
]
,
because all the time-integrators used there are unconditionally stable.

Similarly, for
any
stable linear multistep method, we can also develop a preconditioned iterative solver,
and show that the iteration converges linearly (for suitable parameter
\alpha
) and satisfies (Theorem
3.1
)

\|(I_{t}\otimes P){\bm{err}}^{k+1}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{c\alpha}{1-c\alpha}\|(I_{t}\otimes P){\bm{err}}^{k}\|_{\infty},

(12)

where
c\geq 1
is a generic constant only depending on the stability property of the multistep method.
For parabolic equations and subdiffusion models with memory effects,
such an iterative algorithm for
r
-step backward differentiation formula up to
r=6
has
been analyzed in a most recent work
[
35
]
.
The current work provides a more systematic argument showing the relation between
stability of time-integrators and the convergence of the iterative solver (
9
).

These estimates imply that the convergence of the
iterative algorithm (
9
) is very fast when we choose a very small
\alpha
.
However, in practice, the parameter
\alpha
can not be arbitrarily small, because the roundoff error
arising from the block diagonalization of
\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}

will increase dramatically when
\alpha\rightarrow 0
. Such a roundoff error is explained as follows. For any diagonalizable square matrix

Q
with eigenvalue matrix
D
and eigenvector matrix
V
, due to the floating point operations
Q
can not precisely equal to

VDV^{-1}
and it only holds
Q\approx VDV^{-1}
. In our case, for the
\alpha
-circulant matrix
C(\alpha)
in (
8
),
the difference between
C(\alpha)
and
VDV^{-1}

continuously increase as
\alpha
decreases. See
[
16
,
35
]
for some detailed studies
on the roundoff error of the diagonalization
procedure, where the authors proved

\texttt{roundoff error}=O(\epsilon\alpha^{-2}),

(13)

where
\epsilon
is the machine precision (
\epsilon=2.2204\times 10^{-16}
for a 32-bit computer).

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we prove (
11
) for the one-step time-integrators. The proof lies in representing the preconditioned iteration (
9
) as a special difference equations with head-tail coupled condition and relays on the stability function only. We also consider the linear multistep methods in Section
3
, for which we prove (
12
) and the stability of the numerical method plays a central role in the proof as well. We conclude this paper in Section
4
by listing some important issues that need to be addressed in the future.

2
Convergence of iteration (
9
) for one-step methods

In this section, we prove the convergence of the iterative solver (
9
).
To this end, we assume that the one-step time-integrator (
3
) is stable in the following sense.

Assumption 1
.

The matrix
A
in the linear system (
1
) is diagonalizable as
A=PD_{A}P^{-1}
with
\sigma(A)\subset\mathbb{C}^{+}
. For such a system, it holds

|\mathcal{R}(\Delta t\lambda)|\leq 1\qquad{\forall~\lambda\in\sigma(A)},

(14)

where
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)
denotes the stability function of the one-step method (
3
).

Then we are ready to state our main theorem.

Theorem 2.1
.

Under Assumption 1, the error at the
k
-th iteration in (
9
), denoted by
{\bm{err}}^{k}={\bm{u}}^{k}-{\bm{u}}
,
satisfies

\|(I_{t}\otimes P){\bm{err}}^{k+1}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\|(I_{t}\otimes P){\bm{err}}^{k}\|_{\infty}\qquad\forall~k\geq 1.

(15)

Therefore, the iteration (
9
) converges linearly if
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
.

Proof.

From (
9
),
we observe that the error
{\bm{err}}^{k}
satisfies

{\bm{err}}^{k+1}={\bm{err}}^{k}-(\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}^{-1}\mathcal{K}){\bm{err}}^{k},~k=0,1,\dots.

Now we define

{\bm{\zeta}}^{k}=(({\bm{\xi}}^{k}_{1})^{\top},({\bm{\xi}}^{k}_{2})^{\top},\dots,({\bm{\xi}}^{k}_{m})^{\top})^{\top}=(I_{t}\otimes P){\bm{err}}^{k}.

Let
z
be an arbitrary eigenvalue of
\Delta tA
and
{\bm{\xi}}^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{t}}
be the corresponding subvector of
{\bm{\zeta}}^{k}
. Then, it is clear that

{\bm{\xi}}^{k+1}={\bm{\xi}}^{k}-(P_{\alpha}^{-1}(z)K(z)){\bm{\xi}}^{k},~k=0,1,\dots.

(16)

with

K(z)=I_{t}+\mathcal{R}(z)C_{t},\quad P_{\alpha}(z)=I_{t}+\mathcal{R}(z)C(\alpha).

By applying
\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}
on (
16
), we derive that for
k\geq 1

\begin{split}P_{\alpha}(z){\bm{\xi}}^{k+1}=(P_{\alpha}(z)-K(z)){\bm{\xi}}^{k}.\end{split}

(17)

Let

{\bm{\xi}}^{k}=(\xi^{k}_{1},\xi^{k}_{2},\dots,\xi^{k}_{N_{t}})^{\top}.

Then the relation (
17
) implies

{\begin{split}\left(I_{t}+\mathcal{R}(z)C_{t}\right){\bm{\xi}}^{k+1}=\alpha\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{R}(z)(\xi^{k+1}_{N_{t}}-\xi^{k}_{N_{t}})\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0\end{bmatrix}.\end{split}}

Therefore, (
16
) is equivalent to the following difference equations with a head-tail coupled condition

\begin{cases}\xi^{k+1}_{n+1}+\mathcal{R}(z)\xi^{k+1}_{n}=0,~n=0,1,\dots,N_{t}-1,\\
\xi^{k+1}_{0}=\alpha(\xi^{k+1}_{N_{t}}-\xi^{k}_{N_{t}}).\end{cases}

(18)

By Assumption 1, we know that the time-integrator is stable, i.e.,
|\mathcal{R}(z)|\leq 1
.
This together with the first relation in (
18
) immediately implies for all
k\geq 0

\left|\xi^{k+1}_{n+1}\right|=|\mathcal{R}(z)|\left|\xi^{k+1}_{n}\right|\leq\left|\xi^{k+1}_{n}\right|.

Hence, the the second relation in (
18
) leads to

\left|\xi^{k+1}_{n}\right|\leq\left|\xi^{k+1}_{0}\right|=\alpha\left|\xi^{k+1}_{N_{t}}-\xi^{k}_{N_{t}}\right|\leq\alpha\left|\xi^{k+1}_{N_{t}}\right|+\alpha\left|\xi^{k}_{N_{t}}\right|\leq\alpha\left|\xi^{k+1}_{n}\right|+\alpha\left|\xi^{k}_{n}\right|,

where
n=0,1,\dots,N_{t}
. That is,

\left|\xi^{k+1}_{n}\right|\leq\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\left|\xi^{k}_{n}\right|.

To summarize, it holds

\|{\bm{\xi}}^{k+1}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\|{\bm{\xi}}^{k}\|_{\infty}.

(19)

For
{\bm{\zeta}}^{k}=(({\bm{\xi}}^{k}_{1})^{\top},({\bm{\xi}}^{k}_{2})^{\top},\dots,({\bm{\xi}}^{k}_{m})^{\top})^{\top}
with any index
k
, it is clear that

\|{\bm{\zeta}}^{k}\|_{\infty}=\max_{j=1,2,\dots,m}\|{\bm{\xi}}_{j}^{k}\|_{\infty}.

Since
{\bm{\xi}}^{k}
is an arbitrary subvector of
{\bm{\zeta}}^{k}
,
it follows from (
19
) that

\|{\bm{\zeta}}^{k+1}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\|{\bm{\zeta}}^{k}\|_{\infty}
. This,
together with
{\bm{\zeta}}^{k}=(I_{t}\otimes P){\bm{err}}^{k}
, gives the desired result.
∎

Remark 2.1
.

Theorem
2.1
reveals the relation between the stability
of the time stepping scheme (
3
) and the convergence
of preconditioned iterative solver (
9
).
The proof only utilizes the stability of the time stepping scheme
instead of any matrix structure of the all-at-once system explicitly,
and can be extended to other numerical schemes,
e.g. linear multistep methods, which will be introduced in the
next section.

To test the sharpness of the estimate (
15
), we consider the following advection-diffusion equation

\begin{cases}u_{t}-\nu u_{xx}+u_{x}=0,&(x,t)\in(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})\times(0,T),\\

(20)

with the periodic boundary condition,
where

\nu

with

\begin{split}A=\frac{\nu}{\Delta x^{2}}\begin{bmatrix}2&-1&&&-1\\
-1&2&-1&&\\

(21)

For time-discretization, we consider the following two-stage SDIRK method

3
Convergence of iteration (
9
) for linear multistep methods

In this section, we consider the linear multistep methods.
For parabolic equations, the parallel algorithms for BDF methods (with initial corrections) up to order six
has been proposed and analyzed in
[
35
]
,
and has been generalized to the diffusion models with historical memory effects.
In the present work, we propose a systematic
approach, showing the convergence of the preconditioned iteration,
which works for all stable linear multistep methods.

We consider the
r
-step method (
r\geq 1
) with
time levels
t_{n}=n\Delta t
(
n=1,\ldots,N_{t}
): for given
y_{0}
,
y_{1}
, …,
y_{r-1}
,
we find
y_{n+r}
such that

{\sum}_{j=0}^{r}a_{j}y_{n+r-j}=\Delta t{\sum}_{j=0}^{r}b_{j}(-Ay_{n+r-j}+g_{n+r-j}),\quad n=0,1,\ldots,N_{t}-r.

(25)

Without loss of generality, we assume that
a_{0}=1
. Then
we define the characteristic polynomials for
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
:

p(s;z)={\sum}_{j=0}^{r}a_{j}s^{r-j}+zb_{j}s^{r-j}.

(26)

We assume that the
r
-step method is stable (Assumption 2).
In principle, for any given angle
\theta\in(0,\pi/2)
, there exist
A(\theta)
-stable
r
-step methods of
order
r
for every
r\geq 1
.

Assumption 2
.

The matrix
A
in the linear system (
1
) is diagonalizable as
A=PD_{A}P^{-1}
with
\sigma(A)\subset\mathbb{C}^{+}
.
Then for
z=\Delta t\lambda
with
\lambda\in\sigma(A)
, the characteristic polynomial satisfies
the following root condition:

p(s;z)=0~~\Longrightarrow~~\begin{cases}\text{either}~~|s|<1,\\
\text{or}~~|s|=1~\text{and it is a root of multiplicity 1}.\end{cases}

(27)

Assumption
2
immediately implies a stability estimate of the following discrete initial value problem:
for given
\xi_{0}
,
\xi_{1}
, …,
\xi_{r-1}
, find
\xi_{r+n}
such that

\sum_{j=0}^{r}a_{j}\xi_{n+r-j}=\sum_{j=0}^{r}b_{j}(-z\xi_{n+r-j}+\Delta tf_{n+r-j}),\quad n=0,1,\ldots,N_{t}-r,

where
z=\lambda\Delta t
and
\lambda\in\sigma(A)
. Then Assumption
2
leads to the following
stability result
[
18
, Theorem 6.3.2]

|\xi_{n}|\leq c\Big(\max_{0\leq j\leq r-1}|\xi_{j}|+\Delta t\sum_{j=r}^{n}|f_{j}|\Big),

where the constant
c
is independent of
n
,
\Delta t
and
N_{t}
.

Next, we describe the parallel-in-time solver of the time-stepping scheme (
25
).
Similar to the one-step methods, we can also form (
25
) into an
all-at-once
system (
4
) with

\mathcal{K}=B_{1}\otimes I_{x}+\Delta tB_{2}\otimes A,

(28)

where
B_{1},B_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{t}\times N_{t}}
are Toeplitz matrices

B_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{0}&&&&&\\
a_{1}&a_{0}&&&&\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&&&\\
a_{r}&&\ddots&\ddots&&\\
&\ddots&&a_{1}&a_{0}&\\
&&a_{r}&\dots&a_{1}&a_{0}\end{bmatrix},~B_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}b_{0}&&&&&\\
b_{1}&b_{0}&&&&\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&&&\\
b_{r}&&\ddots&\ddots&&\\
&\ddots&&b_{1}&b_{0}&\\
&&b_{r}&\dots&b_{1}&b_{0}\end{bmatrix}.

Again, we solve the all-at-once system (
4
) by the preconditioned iteration (
9
) with preconditioner

\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}=C_{1}(\alpha)\otimes I_{x}+C_{2}(\alpha)\otimes A,

(29)

where
C_{1}(\alpha)
and
C_{2}(\alpha)
are
\alpha
-circulant matrices

C_{1}(\alpha)=\begin{bmatrix}a_{0}&&\alpha a_{r}&\dots&\alpha a_{2}&\alpha a_{1}\\
a_{1}&a_{0}&&&&\alpha a_{2}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{r}&&~\ddots&\ddots&&\alpha a_{r}\\
&\ddots&&a_{1}&a_{0}&\\
&&a_{r}&\dots&a_{1}&a_{0}\end{bmatrix},~C_{2}(\alpha)=\begin{bmatrix}b_{0}&&\alpha b_{r}&\dots&\alpha b_{2}&\alpha b_{1}\\
b_{1}&b_{0}&&&&\alpha b_{2}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&&\ddots&\vdots\\
b_{r}&&\ddots&\ddots&&\alpha b_{r}\\
&\ddots&&b_{1}&~b_{0}&\\
&&b_{r}&\dots&b_{1}&b_{0}\end{bmatrix}.

Then we are ready to show the convergence of the preconditioned iteration (
9
) for the linear multistep methods.

Theorem 3.1
.

Under Assumption 2, the error at the
k
-th iteration in (
9
), denoted by
{\bm{err}}^{k}={\bm{u}}^{k}-{\bm{u}}
,
satisfies

\|(I_{t}\otimes P){\bm{err}}^{k+1}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{c\alpha}{1-c\alpha}\|(I_{t}\otimes P){\bm{err}}^{k}\|_{\infty},

(30)

where
c>0
denotes a generic constant independent of
\Delta t
and
N_{t}
.
Therefore, the iteration (
9
) converges linearly if
\alpha\in(0,1/2c)
.

Proof.

From
(
9
), the error
{\bm{err}}^{k}
satisfies

{\bm{err}}^{k+1}={\bm{err}}^{k}-(\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}^{-1}\mathcal{K}){\bm{err}}^{k}\qquad\forall~k\geq 0.

We apply
I_{t}\otimes P
on both sides of the above equation and define

{\bm{\zeta}}^{k}=(({\bm{\xi}}^{k}_{1})^{\top},({\bm{\xi}}^{k}_{2})^{\top},\dots,({\bm{\xi}}^{k}_{m})^{\top})^{\top}=(I_{t}\otimes P){\bm{err}}^{k}.

Let
z
be an arbitrary eigenvalue of
\Delta tA
,

{\bm{\xi}}^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{t}}
be the corresponding subvector of
{\bm{\zeta}}^{k}
. Define

K(z)=C_{1}+zC_{2},~P_{\alpha}(z)=C_{1}(\alpha)+zC_{2}(\alpha).

Then, it is clear that

{\bm{\xi}}^{k+1}={\bm{\xi}}^{k}-(P_{\alpha}^{-1}(z)K(z)){\bm{\xi}}^{k},~\forall k\geq 0.

(31)

By applying
P_{\alpha}
on both sides of (
31
), we derive

\begin{split}P_{\alpha}(z){\bm{\xi}}^{k+1}=(P_{\alpha}(z)-K(z)){\bm{\xi}}^{k}.\end{split}

(32)

Let

{\bm{\xi}}^{k}=(\xi^{k}_{r},\xi^{k}_{r+1},\dots,\xi^{k}_{N_{t}})^{\top}.

Then a routine calculation yields

{\begin{split}\left(C_{1}+zC_{2}\right){\bm{\xi}}^{k+1}=\alpha\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{j=1}^{r}(a_{j}+zb_{j})(\xi^{k+1}_{N_{t}-j+1}-\xi^{k}_{N_{t}-j+1})\\
\sum_{j=2}^{r}(a_{j}+zb_{j})(\xi^{k+1}_{N_{t}-j+2}-\xi^{k}_{N_{t}-j+2})\\
\vdots\\
(a_{r}+zb_{r})(\xi^{k+1}_{N_{t}}-\xi^{k}_{N_{t}})\\
\vdots\\
0\end{bmatrix}.\end{split}}

Since
P_{\alpha}(z)-K(z)=C_{1}+zC_{2}
, (
32
) is equivalent to the following difference equations

\begin{cases}\sum_{j=0}^{r}a_{j}\xi_{n-j}^{k+1}=-z\sum_{j=0}^{r}b_{j}\xi_{n-j}^{k+1},&n=r,r+1,\ldots,N_{t}-r,\\
\xi^{k+1}_{r-n}=\alpha(\xi^{k+1}_{N_{t}+1-n}-\xi^{k}_{N_{t}+1-n}),&n=1,\ldots,r.\end{cases}

(33)

By Assumption
2
, we have the stability estimate

\max_{0\leq n\leq N_{t}}|\xi_{n}^{k+1}|\leq c_{0}\max_{0\leq n\leq r-1}|\xi^{k+1}_{n}|\leq c_{0}\alpha\max_{0\leq n\leq r-1}|\xi^{k+1}_{N_{t}-n}-\xi^{k}_{N_{t}-n}|,

(34)

with a generic constant
c_{0}>1
.
This implies

\max_{0\leq n\leq r-1}|\xi^{k+1}_{N_{t}-n}|\leq c_{0}\alpha\Big(\max_{0\leq n\leq r-1}|\xi^{k+1}_{N_{t}-n}|+\max_{0\leq n\leq r-1}|\xi^{k}_{N_{t}-n}|\Big).

Rearranging terms in the above inequality, we arrive at

\max_{0\leq n\leq r-1}|\xi^{k+1}_{N_{t}-n}|\leq\frac{c_{0}\alpha}{1-c_{0}\alpha}\max_{0\leq n\leq r-1}|\xi^{k}_{N_{t}-n}|.

Then, applying the stability estimate (
34
) again leads to

\begin{split}\max_{r\leq n\leq N_{t}-r}|\xi_{n}^{k+1}|&\leq c_{0}\alpha\Big(\max_{0\leq n\leq r-1}|\xi^{k+1}_{N_{t}-n}|+\max_{0\leq n\leq r-1}|\xi^{k}_{N_{t}-n}|\Big)\\
&\leq c_{0}\alpha\Big(c_{0}\max_{r\leq n\leq N_{t}-r}|\xi_{n}^{k+1}|+c_{0}\max_{r\leq n\leq N_{t}-r}|\xi^{k}_{n}|\Big).\end{split}

(35)

We arrange the inequality and obtain

\max_{r\leq n\leq N_{t}-r}|\xi^{k+1}_{n}|\leq\frac{c_{0}^{2}\alpha}{1-c_{0}^{2}\alpha}\max_{r\leq n\leq N_{t}-r}|\xi^{k}_{n}|.

Letting
c=c_{0}^{2}
, we derive

\max_{r\leq n\leq N_{t}}|\xi^{k+1}_{n}|\leq\frac{c\alpha}{1-c\alpha}\max_{r\leq n\leq N_{t}}|\xi^{k}_{n}|,

which implies that

\|{\bm{\xi}}^{k+1}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{c\alpha}{1-c\alpha}\|{\bm{\xi}}^{k}\|_{\infty}.

(36)

Now, substituting
{\bm{\zeta}}^{k}=(I_{t}\otimes P){\bm{err}}^{k}
into (
36
) gives the desired result (
30
).
∎

c\geq 1
.
It depends on the stability of the time stepping scheme, but it is always independent of the

\Delta t

and the total number of steps

4
Conclusion and discussion

We gave a uniformed proof for a class of PinT algorithm, which is based on formulating the time discretization into an all-at-once system
\mathcal{K}{\bm{u}}={\bm{b}}
and then solving this system iteratively by using a block
\alpha
-circulant matrix
\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}
as the preconditioner. Existing work focus on examining the spectrum of the iterative matrix
\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}^{-1}\mathcal{K}
and this leads to many case-by-case studies depending on the underlying time-integrator. In this short paper, we proved that the error of the algorithm decays with a rate only depending on the parameter
\alpha
for all
stable
one-step methods. We also considered the linear multistep methods, for which we proved similar result provided the method is stable as well. The error decaying rate is slightly different from that of the one-step methods and in practical computation we indeed observed such a difference. The proof given in this paper is completely different from existing work. It lies in representing the preconditioned iteration as a special difference equations with head-tail coupled condition and relays on the stability function only.

An important work that we do not concern is how to get the increment
\Delta{\bm{u}}^{k}=\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}^{-1}{\bm{r}}^{k}
in (
9
). For one-step methods, we can factorize
\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}
in (
7
) as

\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}=(V\otimes I_{x})(I_{t}\otimes I_{x}+D\otimes\mathcal{R}(\Delta tA))(V^{-1}\otimes I_{x}),

where
V
and
D
are the eigenvector and eigenvalue matrices of the circulant matrix
C
, i.e.,
C=VDV^{-1}
. Then, we can compute
\Delta{\bm{u}}^{k}
via the following steps

\begin{cases}{\bm{p}}=(V^{-1}\otimes I_{x}){\bm{r}}^{k},&\text{Step-(a)}\\
\left(I_{x}+\lambda_{n}\mathcal{R}(\Delta tA)\right)q_{n}=p_{n},~n=1,2,\dots,N_{t},&\text{Step-(b)}\\
\Delta{\bm{u}}^{k}=(V\otimes I_{x}){\bm{q}},&\text{Step-(c)}\\
\end{cases}

(38)

where
{\bm{p}}=(p_{1}^{\top},\dots,p_{N_{t}}^{\top})^{\top}
and
{\bm{q}}=(q_{1}^{\top},\dots,q_{N_{t}}^{\top})^{\top}
. For Step-(a) and Step-(c), we only need to do matrix-vector multiplications and the major computation burden is Step-(b). These are completely decoupled linear systems and can be solved in parallel, but there are still many concrete issues that need to be explored.

The first issue is about the general implicit RK method of stage
s
specified
by the Butcher tableau

\begin{array}[]{r|l}c&{{\Theta}}\\
\hline\cr&b^{\top}\end{array},

for which
\mathcal{R}(\Delta tA)=I_{x}-b^{\top}\otimes(\Delta tA)(I_{s}\otimes I_{x}+\Theta\otimes(\Delta tA))^{-1}({\bm{1}}\otimes I_{x})
, where
I_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times s}
is an identity matrix and
{\bm{1}}=(1,1,\dots,1)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
. Then how to efficiently solve the linear system in Step-(b) is an very important topic. Some preliminary experiences are as follows. For the two-stage RK methods, by an
elimination
operation for the stage variables it can be shown that such a linear system is equivalent to the following

(a_{0}I_{x}+a_{1}(\Delta tA)+a_{2}(\Delta tA)^{2})\tilde{p}=\tilde{q},

(39)

where
a_{0}
,
a_{1}
,
a_{2}
depend on
\lambda_{n}
and the RK method. The quadratic term
(\Delta tA)^{2}
will be a serious problem for both the memory storage and the computation cost. The approach for this issue is to notice that equation (
39
) can be represented as

\left(a_{0}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1&\\
&1\end{bmatrix}}_{=I_{s}}\otimes I_{x}+\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}-\mu&\mu\\
a_{1}-\mu-\frac{a_{0}a_{2}}{\mu}&\mu\end{bmatrix}}_{:=W_{s}}\otimes(\Delta tA)\right)\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{p}\\
p_{\dagger}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{q}\\
\tilde{q}\end{bmatrix},

(40)

where
p_{\dagger}
is an auxiliary variable and
\mu\neq 0
is a free parameter. Then, we can factorize
W_{s}
as
W_{s}=V_{s}D_{s}V_{s}^{-1}
and solve (
40
) by using the diagonalization technique again. In this way, we only need to solve two independent linear systems of the form

(\eta I_{x}+\Delta tA)v=b,

(41)

where
\eta=a_{0}/D_{s}(1,1)
(or
\eta=a_{0}/D_{s}(2,2)
). (Here we assume
D_{s}(1,1)\neq 0
, since otherwise it is trivial to treat the linear system.) The above idea was used in
[
36
]
for the two-stage Lobatto IIIC method and in
[
37
]
for the two-stage SDIRK method. Similar to (
39
), for a general
s
-stage RK method we believe that the linear system in Step-(b) of (
38
) can be equivalently represented as

(a_{0}I_{x}+a_{1}(\Delta tA)+\cdots+a_{s}(\Delta tA)^{s})\tilde{p}=\tilde{q}.

However, it is unclear how to further represent it as (
40
) with a suitable matrix
W_{s}
. Such a
W_{s}
should be diagonalizable with small condition number of the eigenvector matrix
V_{s}
.

The second issue is about the computation of the complex-shift problem (
41
). It seems this is a very fundamental problem arising in various fields, such as the Laplace inversion approach, preconditioning technique for the Helmholtz equations and the diagonalization technique here. For ODEs with first-order temporal derivative, in most cases we found that the quantity
\eta
arising from the diagonalization technique has non-negative real part, i.e.,
\Re(\eta)\geq 0
, and thus it is not difficult to solve (
41
). This issue was carefully addressed for the multigrid method in
[
27
,
31
]
.
For second-order ODEs, it happens
\Re(\eta)<0
for some
\lambda_{n}
in (
38
) and therefor (
41
) is a Helmholtz-like problem, a typical hard problem in numerics when
|\Re(\eta)|
is large and the ratio
r:=|\Im(\eta)/\Re(\eta)|\ll 1
. Cocquet and Gander made an interesting analysis for the multigrid method in
[
4
]
and the main conclusion is that the multigrid method always converges if
|\Im(\eta)|=\mathcal{O}(|\Re(\eta)|)
. However, numerical results indicate that the multigrid method could converge arbitrarily slow and the convergence rate actually heavily depends on the ratio
r
. A local Fourier analysis will reveal how the convergence rate depends on
r
and this is one of our ongoing work. Besides the multigrid method, there are also other work concerning the complex-shift problem (
41
) and to name a few we mention the preconditioned GMRES method
[
14
]
, the domain decomposition method
[
20
]
and the approach based on representing the real and imaginary parts separately,
leading to a real symmetric linear system, which can be solved
by an Uzawa-type method (see
[
34
, Section 5]
for this approach).
