Abstract
We study and propose schemes that map messages onto constant-weight codewords using variable-length prefixes. We provide polynomial-time computable formulas that estimate the average number of redundant bits incurred by our schemes. In addition to the exact formulas, we also perform an asymptotic analysis and demonstrate that our scheme uses 1 2 ​ log ⁡ n + O ⁡ ( 1 ) \frac{1}{2}\log n+O(1) redundant bits to encode messages into length- n n words with weight ( n / 2 ) + 𝗊 (n/2)+{\sf q} for constant 𝗊 {\sf q} . We also propose schemes that map messages into balanced codebooks with error-correcting capabilities. For such schemes, we provide methods to enumerate the average number of redundant bits.

We first present Scheme A and outline a strategy to analyze the average redundancy of Scheme A. As mentioned earlier, the main obstacle in the direct application of Knuth’s technique is the existence of bad words.
Next, we define bad and good words and classify good words into two types.

Definition 2

A word

{\mathbfsl{x}}

In Section

Lemma 4

If

We now analyze the average redundancy of Scheme A.

. In other words,
{\mathbfsl c}\in{\mathcal{B}}(n,{\sf q})
. Surprisingly, this modification simplifies the analysis of average redundancy and allows us to prove that the average redundancy of Scheme B is within an additive constant of

from the optimal value in (
3
) (when

As before, for a word

, we define the set of indices

and use it to classify

as good or bad.

Definition 6
.

Let
{\mathbfsl{x}}\in\{0,1\}^{n-1}
. Then we set

T_{B}({\mathbfsl{x}},{\sf q})\triangleq\{j\in\left\llbracket n-1\right\rrbracket:{\mathsf{wt}}({\mathsf{Flip}}({\mathbfsl{x}},j))\in\{m+{\sf q}-1,m+{\sf q}\}\}.

(20)

Then
{\mathbfsl{x}}
is
bad
if
T_{B}({\mathbfsl{x}},{\sf q})=T_{B}(\overline{{\mathbfsl{x}}},{\sf q})=\varnothing
.
Otherwise,
{\mathbfsl{x}}
is
Type-
1
-good
if
T_{B}({\mathbfsl{x}},{\sf q})\neq\varnothing
;
and
{\mathbfsl{x}}
is
Type-
0
-good
if
T_{B}({\mathbfsl{x}},{\sf q})=\varnothing
and
T_{B}(\overline{{\mathbfsl{x}}},{\sf q})\neq\varnothing
.

Using similar methods, we obtain the analogue of Lemma
5
.

Lemma 15
.

Suppose that
{\mathbfsl{x}}
is bad.
If
{\mathbfsl{x}}^{\prime}={\mathbfsl{x}}^{[n-2{\sf q}-3]}
, then either
{\mathbfsl{x}}^{\prime}0^{2{\sf q}-2}
or
{\mathbfsl{x}}^{\prime}1^{2{\sf q}-2}
is Type-
1
-good. We say that
{\mathbfsl{x}}
is Type-

{\mathbfsl{x}}^{\prime}i^{2{\sf q}-1}
is Type-
1
-good.

We now formally describe Scheme B.

Scheme B
: An
(n,n-1,\rho_{B};{\sf q})
-balancing scheme

Input
:
{\mathbfsl{x}}\in\{0,1\}^{n-1}

Output
:
{\mathbfsl c}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{B}}(n,{\sf q})
,
{\mathbfsl p}\in\{0,1\}^{*}

(I)

{\mathbfsl{x}}
is Type-
i

•

\hat{{\mathbfsl{x}}}={\mathbfsl{x}}
.

•

.

•

{\mathbfsl{x}}

•

{\mathbfsl{x}}

\hat{{\mathbfsl{x}}}={\mathbfsl{x}}^{[n-2q-3]}0^{2q-2}
.

Hence, we have that

.

(II)

Determine the
{\sf q}
-balanced word
{\mathbfsl c}^{\prime}
.

•

\tau\leftarrow\min T_{B}(\hat{{\mathbfsl{x}}},{\sf q})

•

{\mathbfsl c}\leftarrow{\mathsf{Flip}}(\hat{{\mathbfsl{x}}},j)

•

If
{\mathsf{wt}}({\mathbfsl c})=m+{\sf q}-1
, we append 1 to
{\mathbfsl c}
to obtain
{\mathbfsl c}^{\prime}
.

•

If
{\mathsf{wt}}({\mathbfsl c})=m+{\sf q}
, we append 0 to
{\mathbfsl c}
to obtain
{\mathbfsl c}^{\prime}
.

(III)

Determine the prefix
{\mathbfsl p}
.

•

Compute
\Gamma_{\sf q}^{(B)}({\mathbfsl c})
using Proposition
18

•

{\mathbfsl{z}}\leftarrow
length-
r
binary representation of the index of
\tau
in
\Gamma^{(B)}_{\sf q}({\mathbfsl c})
. Here,
r=\log|\Gamma^{(B)}_{\sf q}({\mathbfsl c})|
.

•

If
{\mathbfsl{x}}
is Type-
i
-good, we set
{\mathbfsl p}\leftarrow 0i{\mathbfsl{z}}
.

•

If
{\mathbfsl{x}}
is Type-
i
-bad, we set
{\mathbfsl p}\leftarrow 1i{\mathbfsl{z}}{\mathbfsl{x}}_{[2q-2]}
.

As before, to estimate the average redundancy
\rho_{B}
, we determine the number of bad words and the average redundancy of good words. Specifically, let
{\mathbfsl{x}}
be a message and set
{\mathbfsl{z}}({\mathbfsl{x}})
and
{\mathbfsl p}({\mathbfsl{x}})
to be the resulting index representation and prefix, respectively.
If we denote the number of bad words in
\{0,1\}^{n-1}
by
D_{B}(n-1,{\sf q})
, we have that

\rho_{B}\leqslant 3+\frac{1}{2^{n-1}}\left(\sum_{{\mathbfsl{x}}\text{ is good}}|{\mathbfsl{z}}({\mathbfsl{x}})|\right)+\frac{D_{B}(n-1,{\sf q})}{2^{n-1}}(2{\sf q}-2+\log_{2}n).

(21)

To determine

, we use the following characterization.

Lemma 16
.

Fix
{\sf q}>0
and let
{\mathbfsl{x}}\in\{0,1\}^{n-1}
.
Then
{\mathbfsl{x}}
is bad if and only if
\Pi({\mathbfsl{x}})
is strictly between
Y=X+{\sf q}-1
and
Y=X-{\sf q}+1
.

Then we have the following corollary.

Corollary 17
.

For fixed
{\sf q}
,
2D_{B}(n-1,{\sf q})\leqslant D(n,{\sf q})=o(2^{n})
.

Proof.

Let
{\mathbfsl{x}}
be a bad word of length
n-1
. Then
\Pi({\mathbfsl{x}})
is a lattice path bounded strictly between
Y=X+{\sf q}-1
and
Y=X-{\sf q}+1
. Now, append either 0 or 1 to
{\mathbfsl{x}}
to obtain
{\mathbfsl{x}}^{\prime}
. Clearly,
\Pi({\mathbfsl{x}}^{\prime})
is now bounded strictly between
Y=X+{\sf q}
and
Y=X-{\sf q}
and so,
{\mathbfsl{x}}^{\prime}
is bad. Hence,
2D_{B}(n-1,{\sf q})\leqslant D(n,{\sf q})
as desired.
∎

Next, we estimate
\sum_{{\mathbfsl{x}}\text{ is good}}|{\mathbfsl{z}}({\mathbfsl{x}})|
.
To do so, we set
\tau_{\sf q}({\mathbfsl{x}})\triangleq\min{T_{B}({\mathbfsl{x}},{\sf q})}
and

\Gamma_{\sf q}^{(B)}({\mathbfsl c})\triangleq\{j\in\left\llbracket n-1\right\rrbracket:{\mathsf{Flip}}({\mathbfsl{x}},j)={\mathbfsl c}\,,\,\tau_{\sf q}({\mathbfsl{x}})=j\text{ for }{\mathbfsl{x}}\in\{0,1\}^{n-1}\}\,.

As before, we characterize
\Gamma_{\sf q}^{(B)}({\mathbfsl c})
using its running sum.

Proposition 18
.

Suppose that
{\mathsf{wt}}({\mathbfsl c})\in\{m+{\sf q}-1,m+{\sf q}\}
.

\Gamma^{(B)}_{\sf q}({\mathbfsl c})=\begin{cases}\{i\in\left\llbracket n-1\right\rrbracket:R({\mathbfsl c})_{i}\geqslant 0,~R({\mathbfsl c})_{i}\neq R({\mathbfsl c})_{j}\text{ for }j<i\},&\text{ if }{\mathsf{wt}}({\mathbfsl c})=n/2+{\sf q}-1,\\
\{i\in\left\llbracket n-1\right\rrbracket:R({\mathbfsl c})_{i}\leqslant 0,~R({\mathbfsl c})_{i}\neq R({\mathbfsl c})_{j}\text{ for }j<i\},&\text{ if }{\mathsf{wt}}({\mathbfsl c})=n/2+{\sf q}.\end{cases}

Proof.

We consider the case where
{\mathsf{wt}}({\mathbfsl c})=m+{\sf q}-1
. The proof for
{\mathsf{wt}}({\mathbfsl c})=m+{\sf q}
is similar.
Proceeding as in the proof of Proposition
12
, we have that
i\notin\Gamma_{\sf q}^{(B)}({\mathbfsl c})
if there exists
j<i
such that
R({\mathbfsl c})_{i}=R({\mathbfsl c})_{j}
.

Next, we claim that if
R({\mathbfsl c})_{i}<0
, then
i\notin\Gamma^{(B)}_{\sf q}({\mathbfsl c})
.
Now, let
{\mathbfsl{x}}={\mathsf{Flip}}({\mathbfsl c},i)
. Since
R({\mathbfsl c})_{i}<0
, we have that
c_{i}=0
and so,
x_{i}=1
. Then
{\mathsf{Flip}}({\mathbfsl{x}},i-1)
has weight
m+{\sf q}
and so, the balancing index for
{\mathbfsl{x}}
is at most
i-1
. Therefore,
i\notin\Gamma_{\sf q}^{(B)}({\mathbfsl c})
.
∎

As before, for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
, we consider the following set of length-
(n-1)
words:

{\mathcal{E}}^{(B)}_{\sf q}(i,n-1)=\{{\mathsf{wt}}({\mathbfsl c})\in\{m+{\sf q}-1,m+{\sf q}\}:|\Gamma^{(B)}_{\sf q}({\mathbfsl c})|=i\}

(22)

and determine its size
\gamma_{\sf q}^{(B)}(i,n-1)\triangleq|{\mathcal{E}}^{(B)}_{\sf q}(i,n-1)|
.

Lemma 19
.

Fix
{\sf q}
and
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
.
Then
{\mathbfsl{x}}\in{\mathcal{E}}^{(B)}_{\sf q}(i,n-1)
if and only if

(i)

the path
\Pi({\mathbfsl{x}})
starts from
(0,{\sf q}-1)
and is above and touching
Y=X+{\sf q}-i
, or,

(ii)

the path
\Pi({\mathbfsl{x}})
starts from
(0,{\sf q})
and is below and touching
Y=X+{\sf q}+i-1
.

Then using principles of inclusion and exclusion with Theorem
8
, we have a surprisingly clean expression for
\gamma_{\sf q}(i,n-1)
.

Theorem 20
.

Fix
{\sf q}>0
. Then

\displaystyle\gamma_{\sf q}^{(B)}(i,n-1)=\begin{cases}\frac{2i-2{\sf q}}{n}\binom{n}{m+i-q},&\text{ if }1\leqslant i\leqslant 2{\sf q}-1,\\
\frac{2i-2{\sf q}}{n}\binom{n}{m+i-q}+\frac{2i+2{\sf q}}{n}\binom{n}{m+i+q},&\text{ if }2{\sf q}\leqslant i\leqslant m-{\sf q},\\
\frac{2i+2{\sf q}}{n}\binom{n}{m+i+q},&\text{ if }m-{\sf q}+1\leqslant i\leqslant m+{\sf q}.\\
\end{cases}

When
{\sf q}=0
, we have

\gamma_{\sf q}^{(B)}(i,n-1)=\frac{4i}{n}\binom{n}{m+i}\,.

Proof.

Let
{\mathbfsl c}\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\sf q}(i,n-1)
. First, suppose that
{\mathbfsl c}
has weight
m+{\sf q}-1
.
Then Lemma
19
states that
\Pi({\mathbfsl c})
is above and touching the line
Y=X+{\sf q}-i
.
By shifting all the paths down by
{\sf q}-i
units, we obtain the following equivalence.
The number of such paths is the same as the number of simple paths that start from
(0,i-1)
, end at
(m+{\sf q}-1,m-{\sf q}+i+1)
and are above and touching the line
Y=X
.

Proceeding as in the proof of Theorem
14
, we apply the principle of inclusion and exclusion with (
13
), and compute the number of such paths to be
\left(\binom{2m-1}{m-{\sf q}}-\binom{2m-1}{m+i-{\sf q}}\right)-\left(\binom{2m-1}{m-{\sf q}}-\binom{2m-1}{m+i-1-{\sf q}}\right)
, which is

\binom{2m-1}{m+i-1-{\sf q}}-\binom{2m-1}{m+i-{\sf q}}\\
=\frac{2i-2{\sf q}}{2m}\binom{2m}{m+i-{\sf q}}\,.

Since we require both
(0,i-1)
and
(m+{\sf q}-1,m-{\sf q}+i+1)
to be above the line
Y=X
, we have that
\max(1,2{\sf q})\leqslant i\leqslant m+{\sf q}
.

Now, when
{\mathbfsl c}
has weight
m+{\sf q}-1
, Lemma
19
states that
\Pi({\mathbfsl c})
is below and touching the line
Y=X+{\sf q}+i-1
. Proceeding as before, the number of such paths is also
\frac{2i+2{\sf q}}{2m}\binom{2m}{m+i+{\sf q}}
.
Therefore, by summing these two quantities, we obtain the result.
∎

Therefore, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{{\mathbfsl{x}}\text{ is good}}|{\mathbfsl{z}}({\mathbfsl{x}})|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=\max(1,2{\sf q})}^{m+q}\frac{2i-2{\sf q}}{2m}\binom{2m}{m+i-q}(i\log{i})\sum_{i=\max(1,-2{\sf q})}^{m-q}\frac{2i+2{\sf q}}{2m}\binom{2m}{m+i+q}(i\log{i})\,.

(23)

In what follows, we make use of the following result to obtain an asymptotically sharp estimate of (
23
).

Theorem 21

(
[
22
, Theorem 4.9]
)
.

Let
F(x)
be a polynomially bounded function, that is,
F(x)=O(x^{d})
for some integer
d>0
. Then

\sum_{k=1}^{m}F(k)\frac{\binom{2m}{m-k}}{\binom{2m}{m}}\sim\int_{x=0}^{\infty}e^{-x^{2}/m}F(x)dx\,.

We present the main result of this section.

Theorem 22
.

Fix
{\sf q}
. Then

\frac{1}{2^{n-1}}\sum_{{\mathbfsl{x}}\text{ is good}}|{\mathbfsl{z}}({\mathbfsl{x}})|\lesssim\frac{1}{2}\log n+\beta\,,

(24)

where
\beta=\frac{2-\ln 4-\gamma}{\ln 4}-\frac{1}{2}\approx-0.474\ldots
and
\gamma
is the Euler-Mascheroni constant.

Proof.

Let
Z_{1}
be the first summand of (
23
) and we estimate this quantity.
First, we set
k=i-{\sf q}
on the right-hand side and extend the range of
k
to obtain

\displaystyle Z_{1}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2^{2m-1}}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac{2k(k+{\sf q})\log(k+{\sf q})}{2m}\binom{2m}{m+k}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{m}\frac{\binom{2m}{m}}{2^{2m}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}k(k+{\sf q})\log(k+{\sf q})\frac{\binom{2m}{m+k}}{\binom{2m}{m}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{m}\frac{\binom{2m}{m}}{2^{2m}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}(k+{\sf q})^{2}\log(k+{\sf q})\frac{\binom{2m}{m+k}}{\binom{2m}{m}}\,.

(25)

Next, we have Stirling’s estimate of the central binomial
[
22
]
:

\frac{\binom{2m}{m}}{2^{2m}}\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi m}}\,.

Then setting
F(x)=(x+{\sf q})^{2}\log(x+{\sf q})
in Theorem
21
, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}(k+{\sf q})^{2}\log(k+2+{\sf q})\frac{\binom{2m}{m+k}}{\binom{2m}{m}}\sim\int_{x=0}^{\infty}e^{-x^{2}/m}(x+{\sf q})^{2}\log(x+{\sf q})dx.

We do a change in the variable and set
z=x/\sqrt{m}
to obtain the expression

\int_{z=0}^{\infty}e^{-z^{2}}(\sqrt{m}z+{\sf q})^{2}\log(\sqrt{m}z+{\sf q})(\sqrt{m}dz)
.
Since
(\sqrt{m}z+{\sf q})^{2}\sim mz^{2}
and
\log(\sqrt{m}z+{\sf q})\sim\log(\sqrt{m}z)
, we have

Z_{1}\lesssim m^{3/2}\int_{z=0}^{\infty}e^{-z^{2}}z^{2}\log zdz+\frac{m^{3/2}\log m}{2}\int_{z=0}^{\infty}e^{-z^{2}}z^{2}dz\,.

Similarly, for the second summand in (
23
), we obtain the exact same expression.

Now, we have
\int_{z=0}^{\infty}e^{-z^{2}}z^{2}\log zdz=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}(2-\ln 4-\gamma)}{4\ln 4}
and

\int_{z=0}^{\infty}e^{-z^{2}}z^{2}dz=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{4}
.
So, combining everything, we have that the right-hand side of (
25
) tends to

\displaystyle\left(\frac{2}{m}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi m}}\right)m^{3/2}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\pi}(2-\ln 4-\gamma)}{4\ln 4}+\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{4}\left(\frac{1}{2}\log m\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\Bigg(\frac{1}{2}\log m+\frac{2-\ln 4-\gamma}{\ln 4}\Bigg)/2

\displaystyle=\Bigg(\frac{1}{2}\log n+\left(\frac{2-\ln 4-\gamma}{\ln 4}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\Bigg)/2

\displaystyle\approx\Big(\frac{1}{2}\log n-0.474\ldots\Big)/2.

Hence, by combining two summands, we obtain (
24
) as required.
∎

Together with (
21
), we obtain the desired upper bound for
\rho_{B}
and obtain Theorem
3
.
Moreover, when
{\sf q}=0
, we have that all words are good. In this case, we need not prepend
{\mathbfsl{z}}
with the two bits and thus, the average redundancy is simply given by
1+\frac{1}{2}\log{n}+\beta\approx\frac{1}{2}\log{n}+0.526
.

VI
Balancing Schemes with Error-Correcting Capabilities

In this section, we set
{\sf q}=0
and consider variable-length balancing schemes that have certain error-correcting capabilities. Specifically, we propose Scheme C and study methods to determine its average redundancy.

Now, the main ingredient of Scheme C is what we termed as
cyclic-balancing technique
, proposed by Chee
et al.
in
[
5
, Sec III-A]
.
Here we provide a high-level description.
For a vector
{\mathbfsl{x}}\in\{0,1\}^{n-1}
, let
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\mathbfsl{x}},i)
be the vector obtained by cyclically shifting the components of
{\mathbfsl{x}}
to the right
i
times.
Similarly,
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\mathbfsl{x}},-i)
is the vector obtained by cyclically shifts to the left
i
times.
To preserve certain distance properties, we always flip the first “half” of a message
{\mathbfsl{x}}
, in other words, the first
m
bits.
So, for simplicity, we denote
{\mathsf{Flip}}({\mathbfsl{x}},m)
as
{\mathsf{Flip}}_{c}({\mathbfsl{x}})
.
Clearly,
{\mathsf{Flip}}_{c}({\mathbfsl{x}})
may not have weight in
\{m-1,m\}
.
Nevertheless, a key observation in
[
5
]
is the following:
if we consider all cyclic shifts of
{\mathbfsl{x}}
, i.e.,
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\mathbfsl{x}},i)
for
i\in\left\llbracket n\right\rrbracket
,
then flipping the first
m
bits of one of these shifts must yield a balanced word.
In other words,
{\mathsf{Flip}}_{c}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\mathbfsl{x}},i))
has weight in
\{m-1,m\}
for some
i\in\left\llbracket n\right\rrbracket
.

With this observation, we can proceed in a similar fashion as Schemes A and B to design a variable-length balancing scheme.
Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be an
[n-1,k,d]_{2}
-cyclic code.
In contrast with previous sections, the set of messages in this section belongs to
{\mathcal{C}}
and we introduce the following notation:

\displaystyle T_{\mathcal{C}}({\mathbfsl{x}})

\displaystyle=\Big\{\,j\in\left\llbracket n-1\right\rrbracket:{\mathsf{Flip}}_{c}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\mathbfsl{x}},j))\text{ has weight in }\{m-1,m\}\,\Big\}\text{ for }{\mathbfsl{x}}\in{\mathcal{C}}\,.

Scheme C

Input
:
{\mathbfsl{x}}\in{\mathcal{C}}

Output
:
{\mathbfsl c}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{B}}(n)
,
{\mathbfsl p}\in\{0,1\}^{*}

(I)

Determine the balanced word
{\mathbfsl c}^{\prime}
.

•

\tau\leftarrow\min T_{\mathcal{C}}({\mathbfsl{x}})

•

{\mathbfsl c}\leftarrow{\mathsf{Flip}}_{c}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\mathbfsl{x}},\tau))

•

If
{\mathsf{wt}}({\mathbfsl c})=m-1
, we append 1 to
{\mathbfsl c}
to obtain
{\mathbfsl c}^{\prime}
.

•

If
{\mathsf{wt}}({\mathbfsl c})=m
, we append 0 to
{\mathbfsl c}
to obtain
{\mathbfsl c}^{\prime}
.

(II)

Determine the prefix
{\mathbfsl p}
.

•

Compute
\Gamma_{\mathcal{C}}({\mathbfsl c})
using Proposition
25
.

•

{\mathbfsl{z}}\leftarrow
length-
r
binary representation of the index of
\tau
in
\Gamma_{\mathcal{C}}({\mathbfsl c})
. Here,

from
[

.

Proposition 23
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be an
[n-1,k,d]

\textsc{Enc}({\mathbfsl{x}})\triangleq({\mathbfsl c}^{\prime},{\mathbfsl p})

Since weight of
{\mathbfsl c}^{\prime}

.

we detail the steps to encode a data stream
0001011\ldots
into balanced blocks of length
n=8
.

•

We pick the first three bits as the first packet:
000
.
We encode to simplex codeword
{\mathbfsl{x}}=0000000
and Scheme C sets
{\mathbfsl c}^{\prime}=11110000
with
{\mathbfsl p}
being the empty string.

•

For the second packet, we pick the next three bits:
101
.
Repeating the procedure, we have
{\mathbfsl{x}}=1011100
with
{\mathbfsl c}^{\prime}=10101100
and
{\mathbfsl p}=(01)
.
In this case, we prepend the next packet with
(01)
. Here, we place parenthesis to emphasize that the bits
(01)
are redundant.

•

In other words, the third packet is
(01)1
. Then we have
{\mathbfsl{x}}=0111001
with
{\mathbfsl c}^{\prime}=10101100
and
{\mathbfsl p}=(10)
. Again,
(10)
will be prepended to the fourth packet.

Therefore, the transmitted codewords are:
11110000
,
10101100
,
10101100
.

Decoding:

Observe that the transmitted codewords belong the extended simplex code which is a
[8,3,4]_{2}
-cyclic code.
Therefore, assuming that each block has at most one error, we are able to correct the error and so the balanced codewords are received correctly.

We invert the encoding process and attempt to recover the data stream.
Here, we assume that we have decoded the fourth codeword and obtained the fourth data packet.

•

The third codeword is
{\mathbfsl c}^{\prime}=10101100
or
{\mathbfsl c}=1010110
.
Since
\Gamma_{\mathcal{C}}({\mathbfsl c})
has four indices, we pick the first two bits of the fourth packet which is
(10)
.
As
(10)
corresponds to shift index two, we set
{\mathbfsl{x}}={\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\mathsf{Flip}}_{c}({\mathbfsl c}),-2)=0111001
. Hence, the third packet is
101
.

•

The second codeword is
{\mathbfsl c}^{\prime}=1010110
. Hence,
{\mathbfsl c}=1010110
and
|\Gamma_{\mathcal{C}}({\mathbfsl c})|=4
.
So, we pick the first two bits of the third packet
(01)
.
Then
{\mathbfsl{x}}={\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\mathsf{Flip}}_{c}({\mathbfsl c}),-1)=1011100
and the second packet is
101
.

•

Finally, the first codeword is
{\mathbfsl c}=11110000
and so,
{\mathbfsl c}=11110000
and
|\Gamma_{\mathcal{C}}({\mathbfsl c})|=1
.
Therefore, the shift index is zero and
{\mathbfsl{x}}=0000000
and the first packet
000
.

•

Hence, the packets are
000
,
101
,
(01)1
, …. Recall that when we decoded the second codeword, we figured out the corresponding prefix is
(01)
. Therefore, we remove the prefix and so, the data stream is
0001011\ldots
.

VI-B

Average Redundancy Analysis

In this subsection, we determine the average redundancy
\rho_{\mathcal{C}}
.
As with previous sections, we consider the set of balancing indices that can be received with a codeword
{\mathbfsl c}
.
Specifically, we consider the following set of indices:

\Gamma_{\mathcal{C}}({\mathbfsl c})\triangleq\Big\{j\in\left\llbracket n-1\right\rrbracket:{\mathsf{Flip}}_{c}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\mathbfsl{x}},j))={\mathbfsl c}\,,\,\min T_{\mathcal{C}}({\mathbfsl{x}})=j\text{ for some }{\mathbfsl{x}}\in{\mathcal{C}}\Big\}\,.

Then we have two tasks. First, we need a simple characterization of
\Gamma_{\mathcal{C}}({\mathbfsl c})
and we do so in Proposition
25
.
Unlike previous schemes, as our messages are obtained from a cyclic code
{\mathcal{C}}
, we have an additional task to determine the possible received words
{\mathbfsl c}
.
Specifically, we need to determine

{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{C}})\triangleq\Big\{{\mathsf{Flip}}_{c}({\mathbfsl{x}}):{\mathbfsl{x}}\in{\mathcal{C}}\text{ and }{\mathsf{Flip}}_{c}({\mathbfsl{x}})\text{ has weight in }\{m-1,m\}\Big\}\,.

Then proceeding as before, we define
\gamma_{\mathcal{C}}(i,n-1)\triangleq|\{{\mathbfsl c}\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{C}}):|\Gamma_{\mathcal{C}}({\mathbfsl c})|=i\}|
and
we have that the average redundancy to be

\rho_{\mathcal{C}}=1+\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{C}}|}\sum_{i=1}^{m}i\gamma_{\mathcal{C}}(i,n-1)\log i.

Observe that the domain of
i
is between 1 and
m
. This is valid because the indices from
\Gamma_{\mathcal{C}}({\mathbfsl c})
belong to
\left\llbracket m-1\right\rrbracket
. This follows from a result in Chee
et al.

[
5
]
.
For completeness, we restate the lemma and provide a simple proof.

Lemma 24
.

Let
{\mathbfsl{x}}\in{\mathcal{C}}
and set
\tau=\min T_{\mathcal{C}}({\mathbfsl{x}})
. Then
\tau\leqslant m-1
.

Proof.

Suppose otherwise that
\tau\geqslant m
.
We claim that
\tau-m
belongs to
T_{\mathcal{C}}({\mathbfsl{x}})
.
Let
{\mathbfsl c}={\mathsf{Flip}}_{c}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\mathbfsl{x}},\tau))
and so,
{\mathsf{wt}}({\mathbfsl c})\in\{m-1,m\}
.
Then
{\mathsf{Flip}}_{c}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\mathbfsl{x}},\tau-m))={\mathsf{Flip}}({\mathbfsl c},n)
has weight in
\{m-1,m\}
.
Therefore,
\tau-m
belongs to
\min\{T_{\mathcal{C}}({\mathbfsl{x}})\}
but
\tau-m<\tau
, contradicting the minimality of
\tau
.
∎

For our first task of characterizing
\Gamma_{\mathcal{C}}({\mathbfsl c})
, we introduce the notion of cyclic running sum.

Definition 7
.

Let
{\mathbfsl c}=c_{1}c_{2}\ldots c_{n-1}\in\{0,1\}^{n-1}
. The
cyclic running sum
of
{\mathbfsl c}
, denoted by
CR({\mathbfsl c})
, is a length-
m
integer-valued vector indexed by
\left\llbracket m-1\right\rrbracket
defined by

CR({\mathbfsl c})_{i}=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }i=0,\\
CR({\mathbfsl c})_{i-1}+(-1)^{c_{i}+1}+(-1)^{c_{i+m}+1},&\text{if }i>0.\end{cases}

Then we obtain the following characterization.

Proposition 25
.

Let
{\mathbfsl c}
belong to
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{C}})
.

\Gamma_{\mathcal{C}}({\mathbfsl c})=\{0\}\cup\{i\in\{1,\ldots,m-1\}:CR({\mathbfsl c},j)\neq 0\text{ for all }1\leqslant j\leqslant i\}.

(26)

Proof.

Let
i
belong to the set on the RHS of (
26
).
Set
{\mathbfsl{x}}={\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\mathsf{Flip}}_{c}({\mathbfsl c}),-i)
and we claim that
i=\min T_{\mathcal{C}}({\mathbfsl{x}})
.
Clearly,
i\in T_{\mathcal{C}}({\mathbfsl{x}})
and so, it remains to show that
i
is the smallest index.

For
j<i
, we consider the weight of the word
{\mathsf{Flip}}_{c}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\mathbfsl{x}},j))
.
Observe that
{\mathsf{Flip}}_{c}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\mathbfsl{x}},j))={\mathsf{Flip}}_{c}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\mathsf{Flip}}_{c}({\mathbfsl c}),-t))
where
t=i-j
.
Since
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\mathsf{Flip}}_{c}({\mathbfsl c}),-t)=\overline{c_{t+1}\cdots c_{m}}c_{m+1}\cdots c_{2m-1}\overline{c_{1}\cdots c_{t}}
,
we have that

{\mathsf{Flip}}_{c}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\mathsf{Flip}}_{c}({\mathbfsl c}),-t))=c_{t+1}\cdots c_{m}\overline{c_{m+1}\cdots c_{t+m}}c_{t+m+1}\cdots c_{2m-1}\overline{c_{1}\cdots c_{t}}.

Therefore,
{\mathsf{wt}}({\mathsf{Flip}}_{c}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\mathsf{Flip}}_{c}({\mathbfsl c}),-t)))=\sum_{i=1}^{2m-1}c_{i}-2CR({\mathbfsl c})_{t}={\mathsf{wt}}({\mathbfsl c})-2CR({\mathbfsl c})_{t}
.
Since
0<t<i
, we have that
2CR({\mathbfsl c})_{t}\neq 0
and so,
{\mathsf{wt}}({\mathsf{Flip}}_{c}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\mathsf{Flip}}_{c}({\mathbfsl c}),-t)))\notin\{m-1,m\}
.
In other words,
i=\min T_{\mathcal{C}}({\mathbfsl{x}})
.
∎

In the next two subsections, we proceed to our next task of determining
\gamma_{\mathcal{C}}(i,n-1)
.
Unfortunately, in this case, we are unable to obtain a closed formula for this quantity for general cyclic code
{\mathcal{C}}
.
Nevertheless, in the case when
{\mathcal{C}}=\{0,1\}^{n-1}
, that is,
{\mathcal{C}}
comprise all binary words of length
n-1
, we have a closed formula.
On the other hand, for a general cyclic code
{\mathcal{C}}
, we provide a method that computes
\gamma_{\mathcal{C}}(i,n-1)
in polynomial time under some conditions.

VI-C

Average Redundancy Analysis when
{\mathcal{C}}=\{0,1\}^{n-1}

In this subsection, we set
{\mathcal{C}}=\{0,1\}^{n-1}
and we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 26
.

For
1\leqslant i\leqslant m
, we have that

\gamma_{\mathcal{C}}(i,n)=\frac{2}{i}\binom{2i-2}{i-1}\binom{2m-2i}{m-i}\,.

Proof.

We first consider the case where
i<m
.
Suppose that
|\Gamma_{\mathcal{C}}({\mathbfsl c})|=i
and we set
CR({\mathbfsl c})=(s_{0},s_{1},\ldots,s_{m-1})
.
Then Proposition
25
states that
CR({\mathbfsl c})
satisfy the following properties:

(S1)

s_{0}=s_{i}=0
.

(S2)

s_{j}>0
for all
0<j<i
or
s_{j}>0
for all
0<j<i
.

Therefore,
\gamma_{\mathcal{C}}(i,n)
is the number of binary length
n-1
words whose cyclic running sums satisfy (S1) to (S3).

First, we observe that the values of
c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{i},c_{m+1},c_{m+2}\ldots,c_{m+i}
completely determines the values of
s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{i}
. So, our first claim is the following:

(Claim 1)
. The number of choices for
c_{1},\ldots,c_{i},c_{m+1},\ldots,c_{m+i}
that result in
s_{1},\ldots,s_{i}
satisfying (S1) and (S2) is
\frac{2}{i}\binom{2i-2}{i-1}
.

To prove Claim 1, we use the lattice-path results in Section
IV
.
Specifically, we consider the length-
2i
word
{\mathbfsl p}=c_{1}c_{m+1}c_{2}c_{m+2}\ldots c_{i}c_{m+i}
and the corresponding lattice path
\Pi({\mathbfsl p})
. Since
s_{i}=0
, we have that
{\mathsf{wt}}({\mathbfsl p})=i
and
\Pi({\mathbfsl p})
starts from
(0,0)
and ends at
(i,i)
.

When
c_{1}=1
, we have that
s_{j}>0
for all
0<j<i
. Hence,
\Pi({\mathbfsl p})
travels from
(0,0)
to
(0,1)
, stays above the line
Y=X+1
until it reaches
(i-1,i)
, and finally ends
(i,i)
. Applying Theorem
8
with
\Delta_{x}=\Delta_{y}=i-1
and
d-a=i-1
, we have that the number of such paths is
\frac{1}{i}\binom{2i-2}{i-1}
. When
c_{1}=0
, a similar argument applies that we have the number of such paths is also
\frac{1}{i}\binom{2i-2}{i-1}
.

Next, we determine the number of choices for
c_{i+1},\ldots,c_{m-1},c_{m},c_{m+i+1},\ldots,c_{2m-1}
.
Here, the number of remaining bits is
2m-1-2i
. Among them, the number of ones is
m-i-1
or
m-i
. Therefore, the number of choices is
\binom{2m-1-2i}{m-i}+\binom{2m-1-2i}{m-i-1}=\binom{2m-2i}{m-i}
.
Therefore, combining with Claim 1, we have the theorem.

When
i=m
, we append
c_{2m}
so that the length-
2m
word
{\mathbfsl c}^{\prime}
has weight
m
.
Then we proceed as before and consider the lattice path
\Pi({\mathbfsl c}^{\prime})
.
We can argue that the lattice path
\Pi({\mathbfsl c}^{\prime})
starts from
(0,0)
and ends at
(m,m)
.
Furthermore,
\Pi({\mathbfsl c}^{\prime})
either always stays above
Y=X
or below
Y=X
. In other words, the number of words/paths is exactly
\frac{2}{i}\binom{2i-2}{i-1}
, as required.
∎

Remark 8
.

In this proof, for the sake of consistency, we use lattice-path combinatorics to obtain Claim 1.
Here, we sketch an alternative (and essentially, equivalent) derivation that makes use of the combinatorial object, Dyck words.
A
Dyck word

{\mathbfsl{x}}
of length
2n
is a binary word with weight
n
where all prefixes of
{\mathbfsl{x}}
have more ones than zeroes.
It is well-known that the number of length-
2n
Dyck word is given by the
n
-th Catalan number
C_{n}=\frac{1}{n+1}\binom{2n}{n}

(see for example,
[
22
, Chapter 6]
).
We can then argue that
c_{2}c_{m+2}\cdots c_{i}
is a Dyck word of length
2i-2
and this recovers Claim 1.

Therefore, we have the following expression for the average redundancy.

Corollary 27
.

\rho_{\mathcal{C}}=1+\frac{1}{2^{2m-1}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}2\binom{2i-2}{i-1}\binom{2m-2i}{m-i}\log i
.

The next proposition provides an asymptotic estimate for
\rho_{\mathcal{C}}
.

Proposition 28
.

If
{\mathcal{C}}=\{0,1\}^{n-1}
, then
\rho_{\mathcal{C}}\sim\log n
.

Proof.

First, since
\log i\leqslant\log m
for all
i
, we have that
\rho_{\mathcal{C}}\leqslant 1+\log m=\log n
.

Next, we fix
0<\epsilon,\delta<1
and show that

\rho_{\mathcal{C}}\gtrsim 1+(1-\epsilon)\left(-2+(\log n-1)\left(1-\frac{2\sin^{-1}\sqrt{\delta}}{\pi}\right)\right)

(27)

In other words, we have that
\rho_{\mathcal{C}}\gtrsim\log n-2\sim\log n
, as required.

To show (
27
), we use Stirling’s approximation
\binom{2N}{N}\sim\frac{2^{2N}}{\sqrt{\pi N}}
(see for example,
[
22
]
).
In other words, for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
N_{0}
such that

\binom{2N}{N}\geqslant(1-\epsilon)\frac{2^{2N}}{\sqrt{\pi N}}\text{ for $N\geqslant N_{0}$}\,.

(28)

Given
\delta>0
, we then choose
m_{0}
such that
\delta m_{0}\geqslant N_{0}+1
.
Hence, for all
m\geqslant m_{0}
and
\delta m\leqslant i\leqslant(1-\delta)m
, we have that
i-1\geqslant\delta m_{0}-1\geqslant N_{0}
, and so,

\binom{2i-2}{i-1}\geqslant(1-\epsilon)\frac{2^{2i-2}}{\sqrt{\pi(i-1)}}
.
Similarly,
m-i\geqslant\delta m\geqslant\delta m_{0}\geqslant N_{0}
and so,

\binom{2m-2i}{m-i}\geqslant(1-\epsilon)\frac{2^{2m-2i}}{\sqrt{\pi(m-i)}}
.
Therefore, we have that

\sum_{i=1}^{m}2\log(i)\binom{2i-2}{i-1}\binom{2m-2i}{m-i}\geqslant\sum_{i=\delta m}^{(1-\delta)m}2\log(i)\Big(1-\epsilon\Big)\frac{2^{2m-2}}{\pi\sqrt{(i-1)(m-i)}}=\frac{2^{2m-1}(1-\epsilon)}{\pi}\sum_{i=\delta m}^{(1-\delta)m}\frac{\log i}{\sqrt{(i-1)(m-i)}}\,.

Now, we estimate the sum using an integral. That is,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=\delta m}^{(1-\delta)m}\frac{\log i}{\sqrt{(i-1)(m-i)}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=\delta m}^{(1-\delta)m}\frac{\log i}{m\sqrt{(i-1)/m(1-i/m)}}

\displaystyle\sim\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}\frac{\log(\lambda m)}{\sqrt{\lambda(1-\lambda)}}\,d\lambda

\displaystyle=\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}\frac{\log m}{\sqrt{\lambda(1-\lambda)}}\,d\lambda+\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}\frac{\log\lambda}{\sqrt{\lambda(1-\lambda)}}\,d\lambda

\displaystyle\geqslant\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}\frac{\log m}{\sqrt{\lambda(1-\lambda)}}\,d\lambda+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\log\lambda}{\sqrt{\lambda(1-\lambda)}}\,d\lambda\,.

Now, the last inequality follows from the fact that
\log\lambda
is negative in the interval
(0,1)
and we have that
\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\log\lambda}{\sqrt{\lambda(1-\lambda)}}\,d\lambda=-2\pi
.
Moreover,
\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}\frac{\log m}{\sqrt{\lambda(1-\lambda)}}\,d\lambda=(\pi-2\sin^{-1}\sqrt{\delta})\log m
, we can now complete our estimate for
\rho_{\mathcal{C}}
.

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathcal{C}}

\displaystyle\geqslant 1+\frac{1}{2^{2m-1}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}2\log(i)\binom{2i-2}{i-1}\binom{2m-2i}{m-i}

\displaystyle\gtrsim 1+\frac{2^{2m-1}(1-\epsilon)}{2^{2m-1}\pi}(-2\pi+(\pi-2\sin^{-1}\sqrt{\delta})\log m)

\displaystyle\geqslant 1+(1-\epsilon)\left(-2+(\log n-1)\left(1-\frac{2\sin^{-1}\sqrt{\delta}}{\pi}\right)\right)\,.

This completes the proof of (
27
).
∎

VI-D

Average Redundancy Analysis for General Cyclic Codes
{\mathcal{C}}

For a general
[n-1,k,d]
-cyclic
{\mathcal{C}}
, we propose a trellis that determines whether a word
{\mathbfsl c}
belongs to
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{C}})
.
Moreover, it provides a polynomial-time method to compute the average redundancy. Let
{\mathbfsl c}=c_{1}c_{2}\ldots c_{n-1}
be a length-
(n-1)
string.
To determine if
{\mathbfsl c}
belongs to
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{C}})
, we start from the root and traverse the trellis according the bits at the
i
-th and
(i+m)
-th positions, where
1\leqslant i\leqslant m-1
.
Specifically, at the
i
th step, we look at the label
c_{i}c_{i+m}
and transition accordingly. In the final step, we move according to
m
-th position, that is,
c_{m}
.

We provide a formal description of the trellis.
Let
{\mathbfsl H}=[{\mathbfsl{h}}_{1},{\mathbfsl{h}}_{2},\ldots,{\mathbfsl{h}}_{n}]
the
(n-k)\times n
-parity check matrix of the cyclic code
{\mathcal{C}}
. Here,
{\mathbfsl{h}}_{i}
denotes the
i
-th column of
{\mathbfsl H}
.
Then each vertex or state
{\mathbfsl u}
of the trellis stores five values.
Specifically, we describe the state
{\mathbfsl u}\triangleq({\mathsf{level}}_{u},CR_{\mathbfsl u},{\mathsf{wt}}_{\mathbfsl u},{\mathsf{syn}}_{\mathbfsl u},{\mathsf{index}}_{\mathbfsl u})
as follow. Here, the five values correspond to the level, cyclic running sum, weight, syndrome, and number of balancing indexes, respectively.
To describe the transition rules, let
{\mathbfsl v}=({\mathsf{level}}_{\mathbfsl v},CR_{\mathbfsl v},{\mathsf{wt}}_{\mathbfsl v},{\mathsf{syn}}_{\mathbfsl v},{\mathsf{index}}_{\mathbfsl v})
be a state following
{\mathbfsl u}
, or
{\mathbfsl u}\rightarrow{\mathbfsl v}
, with the edge labelled by
c_{i}c_{i+m}
or
c_{m}
.

•

The machine has
m+1
levels. The
{\mathsf{level}}
keeps track of the current level. Hence,
{\mathsf{level}}_{\mathbfsl v}={\mathsf{level}}_{\mathbfsl u}+1
.

•

At level
0
,
CR
is initialized to be
0
. For
1\leqslant i\leqslant m-1
, transition from level
i-1
to
i
increases
CR
by
(-1)^{c_{i}+1}+(-1)^{c_{i+m}+1}
. From level
m-1
to
m
, the value
CR
increases by
(-1)^{c_{m}+1}
.

•

At level
0
,
{\mathsf{wt}}
is initialized to be
0
. For
1\leqslant i\leqslant m-1
, transition from level
i-1
to
i
increases
{\mathsf{wt}}
by
c_{i}+c_{i+m}
. From level
m-1
to
m
, the value
{\mathsf{wt}}
increases by
{c_{m}}
.

•

At level
0
,
{\mathsf{syn}}
is initialized to be zero-vector
\boldsymbol{0}
of length
n-k
. For
1\leqslant i\leqslant m-1
, transition from level
i-1
to
i
modifies
{\mathsf{syn}}
by setting
{\mathsf{syn}}\leftarrow{\mathsf{syn}}+(c_{i}+1){\mathbfsl{h}}_{i}+c_{i+m}{\mathbfsl{h}}_{i+m}
. From level
m-1
to
m
, we have
{\mathsf{syn}}\leftarrow{\mathsf{syn}}+(c_{m}+1){\mathbfsl{h}}_{m}
.

•

The
{\mathsf{index}}
keeps track of the number of balancing indices. Here,
{\mathsf{index}}
can be either take a integer value from
1
to
m
or the symbol
\mathord{?}
. The starting state has
{\mathsf{index}}=0
. The transition rules are as follow.

–

If
{\mathsf{index}}_{\mathbfsl u}=0
and

, then
{\mathsf{index}}_{\mathbfsl v}=\mathord{?}
. If
{\mathsf{index}}_{\mathbfsl u}=0
and

, then
{\mathsf{index}}_{\mathbfsl v}=1
.

–

If
{\mathsf{index}}_{\mathbfsl u}\neq\mathord{?}
, then
{\mathsf{index}}_{\mathbfsl v}={\mathsf{index}}_{\mathbfsl v}
.

–

If
{\mathsf{index}}_{\mathbfsl u}=\mathord{?}
and
CR_{\mathbfsl v}\neq 0
, then
{\mathsf{index}}_{\mathbfsl v}=\mathord{?}
. If
{\mathsf{index}}_{\mathbfsl u}=\mathord{?}
and
CR_{\mathbfsl v}=0
, then
{\mathsf{index}}_{\mathbfsl v}={\mathsf{level}}_{\mathbfsl v}
.

Let
\boldsymbol{0}_{n-k}
be the zero vector of length
n-k
.
Then the root of the trellis or the starting state is labelled with
(0,0,0,\boldsymbol{0}_{n-k},0)
. At level
m
, there are
2m
final states with labels:

(m,0,m,\boldsymbol{0}_{n-k},j)
and
(m,0,m-1,\boldsymbol{0}_{n-k},j)
for
1\leqslant j\leqslant m
. We observe that a sequence of states or a path from starting state to one of the final states uniquely determines a codeword in
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{C}})
, and vice versa.
Moreover, for
1\leqslant j\leqslant m
, a path from
(0,0,0,\boldsymbol{0}_{n-k},0)
to

corresponds to a word
{\mathbfsl c}
in
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{C}})
of weight

with

. Therefore, the total number of paths from

or

.

Finally, we have the following theorem that provides a practical method to compute the average redundancy of Scheme C.

Theorem 32
.

If
{\mathcal{C}}
is an
[n,k,d]
-cyclic code, then
\gamma_{\mathcal{C}}(i,n)
can be computed in
O(2^{n-k}n^{4})
time.
Hence, if
n-k
is a constant, then
\gamma_{\mathcal{C}}(i,n)
can be computed in
O(n^{4})
time.

Proof.

Since each recursion formula in Propositions
30
and
31
involve at most four summands,
the time complexity is
O(V)
where
V
is the total number of states.
Here, we provide a simple upper bound for
V
.
Recall that each state is labelled by five values.
The first value corresponds to its level and this has
m+1
possibilities.
Since the second value, the cyclic running sum, is between
-m
and
m
, we have
2m+1
possibilities.
The third value, weight, is from
0
to
m
, while
the fifth value, index, takes values from
1
to
m
and also an extra symbol
\mathord{?}
.
In both cases, we have
m+1
possibilities.
Finally, the fourth value is the possible realizations of the syndrome, which is a binary vector of length
n-k
.
Hence, there are at most
2^{n-k}
possible syndromes.
In summary,
V=O(2^{n-k}n^{4})
and we have the required running time.
∎

Corollary 33
.

If
{\mathcal{C}}
is
[n,k,d]
-cyclic code, then
\rho_{\mathcal{C}}
can be computed in
O(2^{n-k}n^{5})
.
Suppose further that
n-k
is constant, then
\rho_{\mathcal{C}}
can be computed in
O(n^{5})
.

VII
Conclusion

We studied schemes that use variable-length prefixes to encode messages onto length-
n
codewords of weight
n/2+{\sf q}
. Of significance, we obtained the following results for average redundancy of our schemes.

(i)

When
{\sf q}
is a constant, we prove that our schemes uses at most
\frac{1}{2}\log{n}+2.526
redundant bits.

(ii)

In particular, when
{\sf q}=0
, we obtained a closed form formula for the average redundancy. Moreover, in this case, the number of redundant bits asymptotically differs from the optimal redundancy by a factor of
0.2
.

(iii)

We extended our work to the balancing scheme with error correcting capabilities for
{\sf q}=0
. Although the average redundancy
\rho_{C}
depends on the choice of the cyclic code
{\mathcal{C}}
as the set of messages, we showed that
\rho_{C}\sim\log{n}
when
{\mathcal{C}}=\{0,1\}^{n-1}
.

(iv)

We provided polynomial-time computable formulas to estimate the average redundancy of all our schemes.

Some open problems arise from this work.

(i)

How to construct balancing schemes when
{\sf q}
grows with
n
?
This question has been considered in literature
[
24
]
. Since our schemes inherits the simplicity of Knuth’s technique, it seems unable to deal with bad words when
{\sf q}
is large. However, Lemmas
5
and
15
are elementary, and it is likely that more sophisticated techniques (like those in
[
24
]
) are able to balance bad words with less redundant bits. This may then reduce the average redundancy.

(ii)

Do our schemes work with non-binary alphabet?
This question was
prompted by a reviewer in our conference paper. Specifically, are we able to adapt our schemes and the accompanying analysis for balanced
q
-ary words (as defined in
[
19
,
25
]
)?
However, it is beyond the scope of this work and we defer.

(iii)

Can we obtain closed formulas for
\rho_{\mathcal{C}}
when
{\mathcal{C}}
are well-known cyclic codes?
In Section
VI-D
, we gave a general algorithm to determine
\rho_{\mathcal{C}}
for any given cyclic code. However, for certain classes of cyclic codes, like Hamming and BCH codes (see for example,
[
14
]
), it is plausible that we can exploit certain algebraic properties to obtain closed formulas.

(iv)

Can the complexity
O(n^{4})
in Theorem
32
be reduced?
We noted that the estimates in Theorem
32
are loose since we consider all states. In fact, we can ignore many states, which are either not connected to any final state or not reachable from the root as in Figure
2
. Moreover, our trellis construction simply makes use any parity check matrix
{\mathbfsl H}
. It is plausible that a column-permutation of
{\mathbfsl H}
may yield a smaller number of states. This is somewhat similar to the classic problem in the study of trellises (see for example,
[
30
]
).
