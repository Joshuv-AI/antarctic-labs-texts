Rank One Perturbations Supported by Hybrid Geometries and Their Deformations
Fatih Erman, Sema Seymen, O. Teoman Turgut

Abstract
We study the hybrid type of rank one perturbations in ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} and ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} , where the perturbation supported by a circle/sphere is considered together with the delta potential supported by a point outside of the circle/sphere. The construction of the self-adjoint Hamiltonian operator associated with the formal expressions for the rank one perturbation supported by a circle and by a point is explicitly given. The bound state energies and scattering properties for each problem are also studied. Finally, we consider the rank one perturbation supported by a deformed circle/sphere and show that the first order change in the bound state energies under small deformations of the circle/sphere has a simple geometric interpretation.



Keywords: Rank one perturbation supported by curves and surfaces, resolvent, Krein’s resolvent formula, bound states, scattering.

Introduction

Dirac delta potentials, also known as point interactions in general, are among the exactly solvable classes of potentials studied from both physical and mathematical points of view. A detailed review of the subject together with their mathematically rigorous constructions as well as spectral properties are given in the monographs

Extension of point delta potentials to the ones whose support is a sphere - known as the delta shell potentials - is formally presented in some quantum mechanics textbooks

[
7

In addition to these specific geometrical objects as the supports of delta function, more complicated contact type interactions are studied by considering some regular curves or surfaces. The Schrödinger operators or Hamiltonians for such class of singular interactions are formally given by

\displaystyle H_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H_{0}-\lambda\delta(\cdot-\Gamma)\;,

\displaystyle H_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla\psi|^{2}d^{2}x-\lambda\int_{\Gamma}|\psi|^{2}ds\;,

The main physical motivation for studying such systems is to give a realistic model for trapped electrons due to interfaces between two different semiconductor materials, which are known as leaky graphs, curves or surfaces in the literature

The aim of this paper is to address the issue of the bound state and scattering spectrum of the free Hamiltonian operators perturbed by the following rank one perturbations:

(i)

Rank one perturbation supported by a circle and by a point outside of the circle in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

(ii)

Rank one perturbation supported by a sphere and by a point outside of the sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
,

(iii)

Rank one perturbation supported by a small deformation in the normal direction of a circle in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

(iv)

Rank one perturbation supported by a small deformation in the normal direction of a sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Actually, such perturbations are singular rank one perturbations in the sense described in
[
2
]
but we will simply call them rank one perturbations throughout this paper. For instance, the Hamiltonian for the first problem is formally given by

\displaystyle H_{0}-\lambda_{1}|\mathbf{a}\rangle\langle\mathbf{a}|-\lambda_{2}|\Gamma\rangle\langle\Gamma|\;,

(1.3)

where
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
\Gamma
is the circle centered at the origin with radius
R
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0
.

In this paper, we shall call discrete eigenvalues of the Hamiltonian below its essential spectrum the bound state energies, which is a common terminology in physics.

It is well known that the resolvent of such singular interactions (delta potential supported by a point, a curve, or a surface) can be expressed by some explicit formulae involving the resolvent of the free Hamiltonian. These expressions are commonly known as Krein’s formula in the literature
[
1
,
2
,
20
]
. To find the resolvent of the above hybrid type of potentials, we essentially follow an approach given in
[
24
]
. For this, we first regularize the ill-defined interaction terms by finite rank projections acting on Hilbert space and then find the resolvent associated with these regularized Hamiltonians. Then, considering the strong limit of these resolvents, as we remove the regularization parameter, allows us to define a self-adjoint operator corresponding to their limits thanks to the Trotter-Kato theorem. If the support of the interaction is co-dimension two or three, then it is well-known that we need to renormalize the problem (see e.g.,
[
25
,
26
]
for the point interactions in two and three dimensions). In this case, we need to choose the coupling constants or strengths as functions of the regularization parameter such that the limit converges. It is important to emphasize that the second term in the above Hamiltonian (
1.3
) defines a (singular) rank one perturbation supported on the circle. It turns out, the Hamiltonian in equation (
1.3
) corresponds to the zero angular momentum sector of delta potentials supported on a circle (Similar models together with some regular potentials have been studied in
[
27
]
using quasi boundary triples. For an alternative treatment of scattering applied to co-existing point and line defects, see also the recent work
[
28
]
).

For the sake of brevity, we only present the construction of the self-adjoint operator associated with the first system (i) and skip the technical details of the construction of the self-adjoint Hamiltonian associated with the other systems (ii)-(iv) since the idea of
construction is essentially the same. One of the main results of the paper is to give explicitly the bound state energies and differential cross sections for systems (i)-(ii). The physical motivation of considering systems in (i) and (ii) is based on the extension of previously leaky curves/surfaces described in
[
20
]
to the case where impurities in the semiconductor can be modeled by point delta interactions in the low energy approximation (we restrict the problem to the
l=0
sector) for simplicity. Moreover, we show that the change in the bound state energies under small deformations in the normal directions of the circle/sphere can be similarly studied to the first order in the deformation.
An interesting observation here is that the first order perturbative calculation of the bound state energy gives the same result as the rank one perturbation supported by a circle/sphere with a radius increased by the average of the deformation. The method developed in this paper is in fact rather general and can be applied also to rank one perturbation supported by curves and surfaces in principle.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we explicitly show that there exists a self-adjoint operator associated with the initial formal Hamiltonian where the interaction contains a rank one perturbation supported by a circle centered at the origin and a point outside of this circle (point being inside does not present any difficulties, it can equally be considered). Then, we briefly discuss the bound state analysis as well as scattering solutions. Section
3
deals with the bound state spectrum and scattering states for the rank one perturbation supported by a sphere centered at the origin and a point outside of the sphere. Moreover, we study how the small deformations of the circle and sphere in the normal directions change the bound state spectrum and scattering properties in Sections
4
and
5
. Finally, Appendix is devoted to the Trotter-Kato theorem, which is needed to prove the self-adjointness of the Hamiltonian.

Notation
.

In our formulae we often use the Dirac notation for the inner products, however this notation is particularly designed for self-adjoint operators (often the distinction between symmetric and self-adjoint is ignored in physics literature), here we need to keep in mind that the operators appearing in our expressions always act on the right, unless specified otherwise. We shall also use the notation
*
and
\dagger
for the complex conjugation of a complex number and adjoint of an operator, respectively.
Dirac delta function supported by a point
\mathbf{a}
is defined on the test functions
\psi
by
\langle\delta_{\mathbf{a}}|\psi\rangle=\langle\mathbf{a}|\psi\rangle:=\psi(\mathbf{a})
. Similarly, the Dirac delta function
\delta_{\Gamma}
supported by the curve
\Gamma
and the Dirac delta function
\delta_{\Sigma}
supported by the surface
\Sigma
are defined by their action on
\psi

[
29
]

\displaystyle\langle\delta_{\Gamma}|\psi\rangle=\langle\Gamma|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L(\Gamma)}\int_{\Gamma}\psi\;ds\;,

(1.4)

\displaystyle\langle\delta_{\Sigma}|\psi\rangle=\langle\Sigma|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{A(\Sigma)}\int_{\Sigma}\psi\;dA\;,

(1.5)

where
ds
is the integration element over the curve
\Gamma
and
dA
is the integration element over the surface. For the circle
\Gamma=S^{1}
,
ds=Rd\theta
and
L(\Gamma)=2\pi R
. For the sphere
\Sigma=S^{2}
,
dA=R^{2}\sin\theta d\theta d\phi
and
A(\Sigma)=4\pi R^{2}
. The volume elements in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are denoted by
d^{n}r
.The formal expressions
|\mathbf{a}\rangle\langle\mathbf{a}|
,
|\Gamma\rangle\langle\Gamma|
, and
|\Sigma\rangle\langle\Sigma|
are also written as
\langle\delta_{\mathbf{a}},\cdot\rangle\delta_{\mathbf{a}}
,
\langle\delta_{\Gamma},\cdot\rangle\delta_{\Gamma}
, and
\langle\delta_{\Sigma},\cdot\rangle\delta_{\Sigma}
, respectively in the literature.

2
Rank One Perturbation Supported by a Circle and a Point in
\mathbb{R}^{2}

We first consider the rank one perturbation supported by a circle and a point, given formally in Dirac’s notation by

\displaystyle H_{a\Gamma}=H_{0}-\lambda_{1}|\mathbf{a}\rangle\langle\mathbf{a}|-\lambda_{2}|\Gamma\rangle\langle\Gamma|\;,

(2.1)

where
\Gamma
is the circle centered at the origin with radius
R
and
D(H_{0})=H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. We shall use units such that
\hbar=2m=1
for simplicity. In order to make sense of the above expression, we first regularize the Hamiltonian
H_{a\Gamma}
by heat kernel
K_{\epsilon/2}
in the following way:

\displaystyle H_{a\Gamma,\,\epsilon}=H_{0}-\lambda_{1}(\epsilon)|\mathbf{a}^{\epsilon}\rangle\langle\mathbf{a}^{\epsilon}|-\lambda_{2}|\Gamma^{\epsilon}\rangle\langle\Gamma^{\epsilon}|\;,

(2.2)

where

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{a}^{\epsilon}|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}K_{\epsilon/2}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{a})\psi(\mathbf{r})\;d^{2}r\;,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\langle\Gamma^{\epsilon}|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L(S^{1})}\int_{S^{1}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}K_{\epsilon/2}(\mathbf{r},\bm{\gamma}(s))\psi(\mathbf{r})\;d^{2}r\right)\;ds\;.

(2.4)

Here
\epsilon>0
is the regularization parameter or cut-off and
\bm{\gamma}(s)=(R\cos(s/R),R\sin(s/R))
is the parametrization of the circle
S^{1}
. The explicit form of the heat kernel in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is given by
[
30
]

\displaystyle K_{\epsilon/2}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{(2\pi\epsilon)^{n/2}}\;e^{-|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|^{2}/2\epsilon}\;.

(2.5)

The strength or coupling constant of the point Dirac delta interaction, denoted by
\lambda_{1}
, is considered to be a function of
\epsilon>0
, whose explicit form will be determined later.
In this way,
H_{a\Gamma,\,\epsilon}
becomes a finite rank perturbation of the free Hamiltonian so that it is self-adjoint on the domain of
H_{0}
thanks to the Kato-Rellich theorem
[
31
]
. This choice of the regularization is based on the fact that the heat kernel converges to the Dirac delta function as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
in the distributional sense. This is an especially natural choice if we consider delta potentials in manifolds
[
32
,
33
]
and here it is not only useful for the regularization but also allows us to approximate a singular interaction supported by a circle with a more regular one. It is important to emphasize that the expressions (
2.3
) and (
2.4
) are well defined for functions
\psi
in
H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
using Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and reproducing property of the heat kernel
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}K_{\epsilon/2}(\mathbf{r_{1}},\mathbf{r})K_{\epsilon/2}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r_{2}})d^{2}r=K_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2})
. For simplicity, Let us define
|f_{1}(\epsilon)\rangle=|\mathbf{a}^{\epsilon}\rangle
and
|f_{2}(\epsilon)\rangle=|\Gamma^{\epsilon}\rangle
. Then, we have the following first result:

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\lambda_{1}(\epsilon)
be a continuous function of
\epsilon
, which converges to zero as
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
and
\lambda_{2}>0
be an arbitrary positive real number. The resolvent of the regularized Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{a\Gamma,\,\epsilon}=H_{0}-\sum_{i=1}^{2}\lambda_{i}(\epsilon)|f_{i}(\epsilon)\rangle\langle f_{i}(\epsilon)|

(2.6)

is given by

\displaystyle R(\epsilon,E)=R_{0}(E)+R_{0}(E)\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}|f_{i}(\epsilon)\rangle\left(\Phi^{-1}(\epsilon,E)\right)_{ij}\langle f_{j}(\epsilon)|R_{0}(E)\;,

(2.7)

where

\displaystyle\Phi_{ij}(\epsilon,E)=\frac{\delta_{ij}}{\lambda_{i}(\epsilon)}-\langle f_{i}(\epsilon)|R_{0}(E)|f_{j}(\epsilon)\rangle\;,

(2.8)

and its resolvent set is given by
\rho(H_{a\Gamma,\,\epsilon})=\{E\in\rho(H_{0}):\det(\Phi(\epsilon,E))\neq 0\;\text{for}\;\text{all}\;\epsilon>0\}
.

Remark 2.2
.

As emphasized in the introduction, here the Dirac’s notation
\langle\tilde{f}_{i}(\epsilon)|R_{0}(E)|\tilde{f}_{i}(\epsilon)\rangle
should be interpreted as
\langle\tilde{f}_{i}(\epsilon)|R_{0}(E)\tilde{f}_{i}(\epsilon)\rangle
.

Proof.

To find the resolvent of the regularized Hamiltonian (
2.2
), we need to solve the following inhomogenous Schrödinger equation

\displaystyle(H_{a\Gamma,\,\epsilon}-E)|\psi\rangle=|\rho\rangle\;,

(2.9)

for a given function
\langle\mathbf{r}|\rho\rangle=\rho(\mathbf{r})\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. The existence of the solution is guaranteed by the basic self-adjointness criteria
\Ran(H_{a\Gamma,\,\epsilon}-E)=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for at least one
E
in the upper half-plane and one in the lower half-plane
[
31
]
. It is useful to express the interaction as the sum of the rescaled projection operators:

\displaystyle H_{a\Gamma,\,\epsilon}=H_{0}-\sum_{j=1}^{2}|\tilde{f}_{j}(\epsilon)\rangle\langle\tilde{f}_{j}(\epsilon)|\;,

(2.10)

where
|\tilde{f}_{i}(\epsilon)\rangle=\sqrt{\lambda_{i}(\epsilon)}|f_{i}(\epsilon)\rangle
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
Then, applying the free resolvent
R_{0}(E)=(H_{0}-E)^{-1}
defined on the resolvent set
\rho(H_{0})=\mathbb{C}\setminus[0,\infty)
to the equation (
2.9
), we find

\displaystyle|\psi\rangle=R_{0}(E)|\rho\rangle+R_{0}(E)\sum_{j=1}^{2}|\tilde{f}_{j}(\epsilon)\rangle\langle\tilde{f}_{j}(\epsilon)|\psi\rangle\;.

(2.11)

The right hand side of this expression involves unknown complex numbers
\langle\tilde{f}_{j}(\epsilon)|\psi\rangle
. In order to find them, we project this equation onto the
\langle\tilde{f}_{i}(\epsilon)|
and isolate the
j=i
th term in the summation to get the following matrix equation

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{2}\tilde{\Phi}_{ij}(\epsilon,E)\langle\tilde{f}_{j}(\epsilon)|\psi\rangle=\langle\tilde{f}_{i}(\epsilon)|R_{0}(E)|\rho\rangle\;,

(2.12)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{\Phi}_{ij}(\epsilon,E)=\begin{cases}1-\langle\tilde{f}_{i}(\epsilon)|R_{0}(E)|\tilde{f}_{i}(\epsilon)\rangle&i=j\\
-\langle\tilde{f}_{i}(\epsilon)|R_{0}(E)|\tilde{f}_{j}(\epsilon)\rangle&i\neq j\;.\end{cases}

(2.13)

Assume that the matrix
\tilde{\Phi}
is invertible for some subset of the free resolvent set to be determined below. Then, the solution of (
2.12
) exists and is unique. Substituting this solution into (
2.11
), we get

\displaystyle|\psi\rangle=R_{0}(E)|\rho\rangle+R_{0}(E)\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}|\tilde{f}_{i}\rangle\left(\tilde{\Phi}^{-1}(\epsilon,E)\right)_{ij}\langle\tilde{f}_{j}|R_{0}(E)|\rho\rangle\;.

(2.14)

Then, the resolvent of the regularized Hamiltonian can be directly read from the above result

\displaystyle R(\epsilon,E)=R_{0}(E)+R_{0}(E)\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}|\tilde{f}_{i}(\epsilon)\rangle\left(\tilde{\Phi}^{-1}(\epsilon,E)\right)_{ij}\langle\tilde{f}_{j}(\epsilon)|R_{0}(E)\;.

(2.15)

It is convenient to express the above sum in the following way

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}|\tilde{f}_{i}(\epsilon)\rangle\left(\tilde{\Phi}^{-1}(\epsilon,E)\right)_{ij}\langle\tilde{f}_{j}(\epsilon)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Tr\left(\tilde{F}(\epsilon)\tilde{\Phi}^{-1}(\epsilon,E)\right)\;,

(2.16)

where we have defined the matrix
\tilde{F}_{ij}:=|\tilde{f}_{i}\rangle\langle\tilde{f}_{j}|
. If we define the diagonal matrix
D_{ij}(\epsilon):=\sqrt{\lambda_{i}(\epsilon)}\delta_{ij}
we can decompose
\tilde{F}=DFD
, where
F_{ij}=|f_{i}\rangle\langle f_{j}|
. This helps us to write the summation term (
2.16
) as
\Tr\left(\tilde{F}\tilde{\Phi}^{-1}\right)=\Tr(DFD\tilde{\Phi}^{-1})=\Tr(FD\tilde{\Phi}^{-1}D)=\Tr\left(F\Phi^{-1}\right)
, where
\Phi
is related to
\tilde{\Phi}
by a similarity transformation
\Phi=D^{-1}\tilde{\Phi}D^{-1}
and given by

\displaystyle\Phi_{ij}(\epsilon,E)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}(\epsilon)}-\langle f_{i}(\epsilon)|R_{0}(E)|f_{i}(\epsilon)\rangle&i=j\\
-\langle f_{i}(\epsilon)|R_{0}(E)|f_{j}(\epsilon)\rangle&i\neq j\;.\end{cases}

(2.17)

Hence, we explicitly find the resolvent formula for the regularized Hamiltonian (
2.7
).

We now show that
E\in\rho(H_{0})
lies in the resolvent set for
(H_{a\Gamma,\,\epsilon}-E)
if and only if the matrix
\Phi(\epsilon,E)
is invertible. To prove this, we first assume that
\Phi(\epsilon,E)
is invertible for some values of
E\in\rho(H_{0})
. From the triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle||R(\epsilon,E)|\psi\rangle||\leq||R_{0}(E)|\psi\rangle||+4\max_{1\leq i,j\leq 2}|\left(\Phi^{-1}(\epsilon,E)\right)_{ij}|\;|\langle f_{j}(\epsilon)|R_{0}(E)|\psi\rangle|\;||R_{0}(E)|f_{i}(\epsilon)\rangle||\;.

(2.18)

We need to show that the right hand side of this inequality is a bounded function of
E
where
E
must lie in
\rho(H_{0})
and satisfy
\det\Phi(\epsilon,E)\neq 0
. Moreover, this bound must also be a regular function of
\epsilon
since we will consider the limiting case as
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
by appropriately choosing
\lambda_{1}(\epsilon)
, as we will show later on.

A direct application of Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to the inner product in the right hand side of the above inequality does not yield a regular estimate in
\epsilon
since the norm of the function
f_{i}(\epsilon)
is not regular. For this reason, we may think that the adjoint of the bounded free resolvent operator acts on the first entry in the inner product and then apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

\displaystyle|\langle f_{i}(\epsilon)|R_{0}(E)|\psi\rangle|\leq||R_{0}(E^{*})|f_{i}(\epsilon)\rangle||\,||\psi||<\infty\;,

(2.19)

where we have used the fact that
R_{0}^{\dagger}(E)=R_{0}(E^{*})
. Since
E
is inside the resolvent set of
H_{0}
, the expression
||R_{0}(E)|f_{i}(\epsilon)\rangle||
and the inner product on the right hand side of the inequality (
2.18
) is finite as long as
|f_{i}(\epsilon)\rangle
lies in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. However, we must also show that their bounds must be regular in
\epsilon
as
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
. It is easy to see that

\displaystyle||R_{0}(E^{*})|\mathbf{a}^{\epsilon}\rangle||^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{|\langle\mathbf{p}|\mathbf{a}^{\epsilon}\rangle|^{2}}{(p^{2}-E)(p^{2}-E^{*})}\;\frac{d^{2}p}{(2\pi)^{2}}\;.

(2.20)

Using (
2.3
) and the explicit form of the heat kernel (
2.5
), we find

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{p}|\mathbf{a}^{\epsilon}\rangle=\frac{e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{a}}}{2\pi\epsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{a})}e^{-\frac{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{a}|^{2}}{2\epsilon}}d^{2}r\;.

(2.21)

By writing the integral in polar coordinates and using the integral representation of the Bessel function of the first kind
J_{0}(x)

\displaystyle J_{0}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{-ix\cos\theta}\;d\theta

(2.22)

and the formula
[
34
]

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{\nu+1}e^{-\alpha x^{2}}J_{\nu}(\beta x)\;\;dx=\frac{\beta^{\nu}}{(2\alpha)^{\nu+1}}\;e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{4\alpha}}\;,

(2.23)

we get

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{p}|\mathbf{a}^{\epsilon}\rangle=e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{a}}\;e^{-\epsilon p^{2}/2}\;.

(2.24)

Substituting this result into (
2.20
) yields the following bound

\displaystyle||R_{0}(E^{*})|\mathbf{a}^{\epsilon}\rangle||^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\epsilon p^{2}}p}{|p^{4}-2p^{2}\Real(E)+(\Real(E)^{2}+\Imaginary(E)^{2})|}\;dp\;.

(2.25)

Except for the positive real
E
axis, the above integral converges and one can estimate its upper bound if
\Real(E)<0
by

\displaystyle||R_{0}(E^{*})|\mathbf{a}^{\epsilon}\rangle||^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\epsilon p^{2}}p}{p^{4}+A}\;dp\;,

(2.26)

where
A=\Real(E)^{2}+\Imaginary(E)^{2}
. Thanks to the result (3.354) given in
[
34
]
, we can evaluate the above integral so that

\displaystyle||R_{0}(E^{*})|\mathbf{a}^{\epsilon}\rangle||^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4\pi\sqrt{A}}\left(\ci(\epsilon\sqrt{A})\sin(\epsilon\sqrt{A})-\si(\epsilon\sqrt{A})\cos(\epsilon\sqrt{A})\right)\;,

(2.27)

where
\si(x)=-\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{\sin t}{t}dt
is the sine integral function, and
\ci(x)=-\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{\cos t}{t}dt
is the cosine integral function. It is easy to see that this bound is a regular function of
\epsilon
for all
A\neq 0
.

If
\Real(E)>0
,

\displaystyle||R_{0}(E^{*})|\mathbf{a}^{\epsilon}\rangle||^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\epsilon u}}{(u-\Real(E))^{2}+\Imaginary(E)^{2}}\;du

(2.28)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{-\Real(E)}^{0}\frac{e^{-\epsilon(v+\Real(E))}}{v^{2}+\Imaginary(E)^{2}}\;dv+\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\epsilon(v+\Real(E))}}{v^{2}+\Imaginary(E)^{2}}\;dv

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{-\Real(E)}^{0}\frac{1}{v^{2}+\Imaginary(E)^{2}}\;dv+\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\epsilon v}}{v^{2}+\Imaginary(E)^{2}}\;dv\;,

which are finite and regular in
\epsilon
by the same reason given above. We can similarly show that the following norm

\displaystyle||R_{0}(E^{*})|\Gamma^{\epsilon}\rangle||^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{|\langle\mathbf{p}|\Gamma^{\epsilon}\rangle|^{2}}{(p^{2}-E)(p^{2}-E^{*})}\;\frac{d^{2}p}{(2\pi)^{2}}\;,

(2.29)

is a bounded function of
E
on
\rho(H_{0})
and regular in
\epsilon
. For this, we need to find

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{p}|\Gamma^{\epsilon}\rangle=\frac{1}{L}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\left(\int_{S^{1}}K_{\epsilon/2}(\mathbf{r},\bm{\gamma}(s))\;ds\right)\;d^{2}r\;.

(2.30)

Using the explicit expression of the heat kernel (
2.5
) and the integral representation of the modified Bessel function of the first kind
[
35
]

\displaystyle I_{0}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{x\cos\theta}d\theta\;,

(2.31)

we get

\displaystyle\int_{S^{1}}K_{\epsilon/2}(\mathbf{r},\bm{\gamma}(s))\;ds=\frac{R}{\epsilon}\;e^{-\frac{(r^{2}+R^{2})}{2\epsilon}}\;I_{0}\left(\frac{R}{\epsilon}r\right)\;.

(2.32)

Then, from the result (6.633) in
[
34
]

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}x\,e^{-\alpha x^{2}}I_{\nu}(\beta x)J_{\nu}(\gamma x)\;dx=\frac{1}{2\alpha}e^{\frac{(\beta^{2}-\gamma^{2})}{4\alpha}}J_{\nu}\left(\frac{\beta\gamma}{2\alpha}\right)\;,

(2.33)

we obtain

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{p}|\Gamma^{\epsilon}\rangle=e^{-\frac{\epsilon p^{2}}{2}}\,J_{0}(pR)\;.

(2.34)

Combining all these results yield

\displaystyle||R_{0}(E^{*})|\Gamma^{\epsilon}\rangle||^{2}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{p\,e^{-\epsilon p^{2}}J_{0}^{2}(pR)}{(p^{2}-E)(p^{2}-E^{*})}\;dp\;.

(2.35)

Since

, we obtain the same form of the estimate (
2.27
) and (
2.28
) as above.
All these imply that
R(\epsilon,E)
is bounded if
E\in\rho(H_{0})
and satisfies
\det(\Phi(\epsilon,E))\neq 0
, hence
E\in\rho(H_{a\Gamma,\,\epsilon})
.

Conversely, if
E\in\rho(H_{a\Gamma,\,\epsilon})
, then
\det(\Phi(\epsilon,E))\neq 0
. For this, suppose that
E\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[0,\infty)
satisfies
\det(\Phi(\epsilon,E))=0
. We need to show that
E\notin\rho(H_{a\Gamma,\,\epsilon})
or
E
lies in the spectrum of
H_{a\Gamma,\,\epsilon}
, or in particular
E
is an eigenvalue of
H_{a\Gamma,\,\epsilon}
:

\displaystyle H_{a\Gamma,\,\epsilon}|\psi\rangle=E|\psi\rangle\;,

(2.36)

for some non-zero
|\psi\rangle\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. The above eigenvalue problem for the regularized Hamiltonian is equivalent to the problem of finding non-trivial solution
\langle\tilde{f}_{j}(\epsilon)|\psi\rangle
of the equation (
2.12
) with
|\rho\rangle=|0\rangle
:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{2}\tilde{\Phi}_{ij}(\epsilon,E)\langle\tilde{f}_{j}(\epsilon)|\psi\rangle=0\;.

(2.37)

Since the equation (
2.37
) is derived from the eigenvalue equation (
2.36
) of the regularized Hamiltonian, the set of
E
in (
2.36
) must also satisfy the equation (
2.37
). To prove the converse, we first need to show that
\langle f_{i}(\epsilon)|R_{0}(E)|f_{j}(\epsilon)\rangle\neq 0
for all
i,j
. Otherwise,
\tilde{\Phi}
would be an identity matrix, which is clearly invertible. Then, the equation (
2.37
) implies that
\langle\tilde{f}_{j}(\epsilon)|\psi\rangle=0
for all
j
. Expanding explicitly the form of the matrix
\tilde{\Phi}
in (
2.37
) and using the above fact, it follows that
E
must satisfy the eigenvalue equation for the regularized Hamiltonian. Hence, we have a non-trivial solution of the above linear equation (
2.37
) for
\langle\tilde{f}_{j}(\epsilon)|\psi\rangle
with some
|\psi\rangle\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
if and only if
\det\tilde{\Phi}(\epsilon,E)=\det\Phi(\epsilon,E)=0
.

∎

Now, we consider the limiting case as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
to properly define the initial formal Hamiltonian. For this reason, we choose
\lambda_{1}(\epsilon)
in such a way that the regularized Hamiltonian has a reasonable and non-trivial limit as we remove the cut-off parameter, that is, as

Proposition 2.3

for

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
E
be a real negative number that satisfies
\det\Phi(E)\neq 0

is invertible.

Proof.

We first show that the limit operator

for the real negative values of

\Ker(R(E))=\{|0\rangle\}

for some

given by (

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\mathbf{p})=-\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}\frac{\langle\mathbf{p}|f_{i}\rangle}{p^{2}-E}\left[\Phi^{-1}(E)\right]_{ij}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\langle f_{j}|R_{0}(E)|\mathbf{q}\rangle\langle\mathbf{q}|f\rangle\frac{d^{2}q}{(2\pi)^{2}}\;.

(2.54)

By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\langle\mathbf{a}|R_{0}(E)|\mathbf{q}\rangle\langle\mathbf{q}|\psi\rangle\frac{d^{2}q}{(2\pi)^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{a}}}{q^{2}-E}f(\mathbf{q})\frac{d^{2}q}{(2\pi)^{2}}

(2.55)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{q}{(q^{2}-E)^{2}}\;\frac{dq}{2\pi}\right)^{1/2}||f||<\infty\;,

and

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\langle\Gamma|R_{0}(E)|\mathbf{q}\rangle\langle\mathbf{q}|\psi\rangle\frac{d^{2}q}{(2\pi)^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{J_{0}(qR)}{q^{2}-E}f(\mathbf{q})\frac{d^{2}q}{(2\pi)^{2}}

(2.56)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{q}{(q^{2}-E)^{2}}\;\frac{dq}{2\pi}\right)^{1/2}||f||<\infty\;.

With the above bounds (
2.55
) and (
2.56
), we show that

\displaystyle\hskip-56.9055pt\hat{f}(\mathbf{p})=-\Bigg[e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{a}}\left[\Phi^{-1}(E)\right]_{11}C_{1}+e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{a}}\left[\Phi^{-1}(E)\right]_{12}C_{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt+\,J_{0}(pR)\left[\Phi^{-1}(E)\right]_{21}C_{2}+J_{0}(pR)\left[\Phi^{-1}(E)\right]_{22}C_{1}\Bigg]\;,

(2.57)

where

are finite real numbers and

is a negative real number that satisfies

. However, this solution

can not be in

unless

. ∎

depending on the parameter

(2.58)

for the above values of

. For the case
\mathbf{a}\in U
, the continuity of the function
\phi_{k}
at
\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{a}
from the equation (
2.70
) implies that
\left[\Phi^{-1}(k^{2})\right]_{11}\phi_{k}(\mathbf{a})+\left[\Phi^{-1}(k^{2})\right]_{12}\bigg(\frac{1}{L}\int_{S^{1}}\phi_{k}(\bm{\gamma}(s))\;ds\bigg)=0
. Similarly, if
\Gamma\in U
, the term
\left[\Phi^{-1}(k^{2})\right]_{21}\phi_{k}(\mathbf{a})+\left[\Phi^{-1}(k^{2})\right]_{22}\bigg(\frac{1}{L}\int_{S^{1}}\phi_{k}(\bm{\gamma}(s))\;ds\bigg)
must vanish. Hence, we obtain
H_{a\Gamma}\psi(\mathbf{r})=0
in
U
.

∎

2.1
Bound State Analysis

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\Gamma
be the circle centered at the origin with radius
R<a
. Then, the essential spectrum of
H_{a\Gamma}
associated with the point delta and the rank one perturbation supported by
\Gamma
coincides with the essential spectrum of the free Hamiltonian, i.e.,
\sigma_{ess}(H_{a\Gamma})=\sigma_{ess}(H_{0})=[0,\infty)
. Furthermore, the point spectrum
\sigma_{p}(H_{a\Gamma})
of
H_{a\Gamma}
lies in the negative real axis and
H_{a\Gamma}
has at least one and at most two negative eigenvalues (counting multiplicity). Let
\Real(k)=0
and
\Imaginary(k)>0
, then
k^{2}\in\sigma_{p}(H_{a\Gamma})
if and only if
\det\Phi(k^{2})=0
and multiplicity (degeneracy) of the eigenvalue
k^{2}
is the same as the multiplicity of this zero eigenvalue of the matrix
\Phi(k^{2})
. Moreover, let
E=-\nu_{*}^{2}<0
be an eigenvalue of
H_{a\Gamma}
, then the eigenfunction
|\psi_{ev}\rangle
associated with this eigenvalue is given by

\displaystyle\psi_{ev}(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\langle\mathbf{r}|R_{0}(-\nu_{*}^{2})|f_{i}\rangle A_{i}\;,

where
(A_{1},A_{2})
is an eigenvector corresponding to a zero eigenvalue of
\Phi(-\nu_{*}^{2})
.

Proof.

As it is well-known that the point spectrum
\sigma_{p}
of an operator
H_{a\Gamma}
consists of the set of complex numbers
E
such that
\Ker(H_{a\Gamma}-E)\neq\{|0\rangle\}
. From the explicit expression of the resolvent
R(k^{2})
given by (
2.38
) for
E=k^{2}
, the poles of the resolvent for
k^{2}<0
can only appear if the matrix
\Phi(k^{2})
is singular, that is, if

\displaystyle\det\Phi(k^{2})=0\;.

(2.73)

Let
|\psi_{ev}\rangle
be an eigenvector of
H_{a\Gamma}
with corresponding eigenvalue
E_{ev}=k_{ev}^{2}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle H_{a\Gamma}|\psi_{ev}\rangle=E_{ev}|\psi_{ev}\rangle\;,

(2.74)

where
|\psi_{ev}\rangle\in D(H_{a\Gamma})
. Since any function in the domain of
H_{a\Gamma}
can be decomposed according to Theorem
2.7
, we have

\displaystyle|\psi_{ev}\rangle=|\phi_{k}\rangle+\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}R_{0}(k^{2})|f_{i}\rangle\left[\Phi^{-1}(k^{2})\right]_{ij}\langle f_{j}|\phi_{k}\rangle\;,

(2.75)

for some
k^{2}\in\rho(H_{a\Gamma})
with
\Imaginary(k)>0
and
|\phi_{k}\rangle\in D(H_{0})
. Actually, Theorem
2.7
provides us another relation between
|\psi_{ev}\rangle
and
|\phi_{k}\rangle
:

\displaystyle|\phi_{k}\rangle=(k_{ev}^{2}-k^{2})R_{0}(k^{2})|\psi_{ev}\rangle\;.

(2.76)

Substituting equation (
2.75
) into (
2.76
), we find

\displaystyle|\phi_{k}\rangle=(k_{ev}^{2}-k^{2})\Bigg(R_{0}(k^{2})|\phi_{k}\rangle+\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}R_{0}(k^{2})R_{0}(k^{2})|f_{i}\rangle\left[\Phi^{-1}(k^{2})\right]_{ij}\langle f_{j}|\phi_{k}\rangle\Bigg)\;.

(2.77)

By acting
H_{0}-k^{2}
on this vector, it yields

\displaystyle(H_{0}-k_{ev}^{2})|\phi_{k}\rangle=(k_{ev}^{2}-k^{2})\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}R_{0}(k^{2})|f_{i}\rangle\left[\Phi^{-1}(k^{2})\right]_{ij}\langle f_{j}|\phi_{k}\rangle\;.

(2.78)

The solution of this in momentum representation is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{k}(\mathbf{p})=\frac{(k_{ev}^{2}-k^{2})}{p^{2}-k_{ev}^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}\frac{\langle\mathbf{p}|f_{i}\rangle}{p^{2}-k^{2}}\left[\Phi^{-1}(k^{2})\right]_{ij}\langle f_{j}|\phi_{k}\rangle\;.

(2.79)

If
E_{ev}=k_{ev}^{2}\geq 0
, then this equation has no nontrivial solution since
\hat{\phi}_{k}(\mathbf{p})
cannot lie in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
unless it is identically zero. This implies that
|\phi_{k}\rangle\notin L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
thanks to the Plancherel theorem. Hence,
\psi_{ev}(\mathbf{r})=0
for all
\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, which proves that there is no nonnegative eigenvalue of
H_{a\Gamma}
.

However, if
E_{ev}=k_{ev}^{2}=-\nu_{*}^{2}<0
with
\nu>0
, it is legitimate to apply
R_{0}(-\nu_{*}^{2})
on each side of equation (
2.78
) and get

\displaystyle|\phi_{k}\rangle=\left(R_{0}(-\nu_{*}^{2})-R_{0}(k^{2})\right)\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}|f_{i}\rangle\left[\Phi^{-1}(k^{2})\right]_{ij}\langle f_{j}|\phi_{k}\rangle\;.

(2.80)

Inserting this solution into (
2.75
), we formally find the eigenfunctions of
H_{a\Gamma}

\displaystyle\psi_{ev}(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}\langle\mathbf{r}|R_{0}(-\nu_{*}^{2})|f_{i}\rangle\left[\Phi^{-1}(k^{2})\right]_{ij}\langle f_{j}|\phi_{k}\rangle\;.

(2.81)

This formal solution includes unknown factors
\langle f_{j}|\phi_{k}\rangle
. In order to find them, we first note that the principal matrix
\Phi
can also be expressed purely in terms of the free resolvent kernels, that is,

\displaystyle\Phi(k^{2})=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}\langle f_{1}|\left(R_{0}(-\mu^{2})-R_{0}(k^{2})\right)|f_{1}\rangle&&-\langle f_{1}|R_{0}(k^{2})|f_{2}\rangle\\
\\
-\langle f_{2}|R_{0}(k^{2})|f_{1}\rangle&&\frac{1}{\lambda_{2}}-\langle f_{2}|R_{0}(k^{2})|f_{2}\rangle\end{array}\right)\;.

Then, it is easy to check that

\displaystyle\langle f_{i}|\left(R_{0}(-\nu_{*}^{2})-R_{0}(k^{2})\right)|f_{j}\rangle=\Phi_{ij}(k^{2})-\Phi_{ij}(-\nu_{*}^{2})\;.

(2.86)

Using this result in (
2.80
) after taking the projection onto
\langle f_{j^{\prime}}|
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{2}\Phi_{ij}(-\nu_{*}^{2})A_{j}=0\;,

(2.87)

where
A_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left[\Phi^{-1}(k^{2})\right]_{ji}\langle f_{i}|\phi_{k}\rangle
. This equation tells us that
A_{i}
is an eigenvector of the matrix
\Phi(-\nu_{*}^{2})
with a zero eigenvalue.

Conversely, let us suppose that

\displaystyle|\psi_{ev}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{2}R_{0}(-\nu_{*}^{2})|f_{i}\rangle A_{i}\;,

(2.88)

where
A_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{2}\left[\Phi^{-1}(k^{2})\right]_{ij}\langle f_{j}|\phi_{k}\rangle
is an eigenvector of
\Phi(-\nu_{*}^{2})
with eigenvalue zero. We will show that
|\psi_{ev}\rangle\in D(H_{a\Gamma})
and
H_{a\Gamma}|\psi_{ev}\rangle=-\nu_{*}^{2}|\psi_{ev}\rangle
. First, we need to show that
|\psi_{ev}\rangle\in D(H_{a\Gamma})
. For this, we define

\displaystyle|\phi_{k}\rangle=(k_{ev}^{2}-k^{2})R_{0}(k^{2})|\psi_{ev}\rangle

(2.89)

for some
k^{2}\in\rho(H_{a\Gamma})
with
\Imaginary(k)>0
. Then, it follows easily that
|\phi_{k}\rangle\in D(H_{0})
and inserting (
2.88
) into (
2.89
) and using the first resolvent identity for the free resolvent we obtain

\displaystyle|\phi_{k}\rangle=\left(R_{0}(-\nu_{*}^{2})-R_{0}(k^{2})\right)\sum_{i=1}^{2}|f_{i}\rangle A_{i}\;

(2.90)

or

\displaystyle|\phi_{k}\rangle+R_{0}(k^{2})\sum_{i=1}^{2}|f_{i}\rangle A_{i}=|\psi_{ev}\rangle\;.

(2.91)

Moreover, by taking the projection of (
2.89
) onto
\langle f_{j}|
and using the above result (
2.86
),
|\psi_{ev}\rangle\in D(H_{a\Gamma})
by Theorem
2.7
. Finally, using the result
(H_{0}-k^{2})|\phi_{k}\rangle=(H_{a\Gamma}-k^{2})|\psi_{ev}\rangle
in Theorem
2.7
for the eigenstate
|\psi_{ev}\rangle
, and equation (
2.89
) we deduce that

\displaystyle H_{a\Gamma}|\psi_{ev}\rangle=(H_{0}-k^{2})|\phi_{k}\rangle+k^{2}|\psi_{ev}\rangle=-\nu_{*}^{2}|\psi_{ev}\rangle\;.

(2.92)

It is useful to express the condition (
2.73
) in terms of a real positive parameter
\nu
, defined by
\nu=-ik>0
. Then, the solutions of the equation
\det\Phi(-\nu^{2})=0
determine the point spectrum of
H_{a\Gamma}
or bound state spectrum of
H_{a\Gamma}
.
However, finding the roots of equation (
2.73
) is analytically not possible. Nevertheless, we may obtain some information about the bound states as follows. First, suppose that the principal matrix
\Phi
has an eigenvector
A
associated with the eigenvalue
\omega
,

\displaystyle\Phi A=\omega A\;.

(2.93)

The eigenvalues can be explicitly calculated

\displaystyle\omega_{1}(\nu)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi\lambda}\Bigg\{2\pi+\lambda\log\left(\frac{\nu}{\mu}\right)-\lambda I_{0}(\nu R)K_{0}(\nu R)-\Bigg[\lambda^{2}I_{0}^{2}(\nu R)\left(4K_{0}^{2}(\nu a)+K_{0}^{2}(\nu R)\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\left(\lambda\log\left(\frac{\nu}{\mu}\right)-2\pi\right)^{2}+2\lambda I_{0}(\nu R)K_{0}(\nu R)\left(\lambda\log\left(\frac{\nu}{\mu}\right)-2\pi\right)\Bigg]^{1/2}\Bigg\}\;,

(2.94)

and

\displaystyle\omega_{2}(\nu)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi\lambda}\Bigg\{2\pi+\lambda\log\left(\frac{\nu}{\mu}\right)-\lambda I_{0}(\nu R)K_{0}(\nu R)+\Bigg[\lambda^{2}I_{0}^{2}(\nu R)\left(4K_{0}^{2}(\nu a)+K_{0}^{2}(\nu R)\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\left(\lambda\log\left(\frac{\nu}{\mu}\right)-2\pi\right)^{2}+2\lambda I_{0}(\nu R)K_{0}(\nu R)\left(\lambda\log\left(\frac{\nu}{\mu}\right)-2\pi\right)\Bigg]^{1/2}\Bigg\}\;.

(2.95)

Finding zeroes of the determinant of the matrix
\Phi
is equivalent to finding the zeroes of its eigenvalues. We now show that these are increasing functions of
\nu
by expressing the principal matrix
\Phi
in its closed form. Suppose for simplicity that the eigenvectors
A
are normalized. We can determine how the eigenvalues change with respect to
\nu
according to the Feynman-Hellman theorem
[
38
]

\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\nu}=A^{*T}\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\nu}A\;,

(2.96)

where
*
and
T
denote the complex conjugation and transpose, respectively. Here, it is convenient to express the derivative of the principal matrix in the following form,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Phi_{11}}{\partial\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\nu}\;,

(2.97)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Phi_{12}}{\partial\nu}=\frac{\partial\Phi_{21}^{*}}{\partial\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(2\nu)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{a}}}{(p^{2}+\nu^{2})^{2}}J_{0}(pR)\frac{d^{2}p}{(2\pi)^{2}}\;,

(2.98)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Phi_{22}}{\partial\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(2\nu)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{J_{0}^{2}(pR)}{(p^{2}+\nu^{2})^{2}}\frac{d^{2}p}{(2\pi)^{2}}\;,

(2.99)

by taking the derivative of
\Phi
under the integral sign thanks to the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem. Then, one can show that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\nu}=(2\nu)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bigg|A_{1}e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{a}}+A_{2}J_{0}(pR)\bigg|^{2}\;\frac{1}{(p^{2}+\nu^{2})^{2}}\;\frac{d^{2}p}{(2\pi)^{2}}>0\;,

(2.100)

for all
\nu>0
, that is, all the eigenvalues
\omega
of the principal matrix
\Phi
are strictly increasing functions of
\nu
.

The solutions of (
2.73
) can also be considered as the zeroes of the eigenvalues of the principal matrix
\Phi
so all the bound state energies can be found from the zeroes of the eigenvalues, say
\nu_{*}
, for which

\displaystyle E=-\nu_{*}^{2}\;.

(2.101)

The positivity condition (
2.100
) implies that there are at most two bound state energies since each eigenvalue can cross the
\nu
axis only once. The zero of the eigenvalue
\omega_{1}
corresponds to the ground state energy.
This bound state always exists for all values of the parameter since
\lim_{\nu\rightarrow 0^{+}}\omega_{1}=-\infty
and it is an increasing function of
\nu
and positive for sufficiently large values of
\nu
. However, the second eigenvalue
\omega_{2}
may not have any zeroes if it is not negative around
\nu=0
.

It follows from Weyl’s theorem
[
39
]
that the essential spectra of
H_{a\Gamma}
and
H_{0}
coincide, that is,
\sigma_{ess}(H_{a\Gamma})=\sigma_{ess}(H_{0})=[0,\infty)
if we show that
R(E)-R_{0}(E)
is compact for some
E\in\rho(H_{a\Gamma})\cap\rho(H_{0})
.
Note that we have
R(E)-R_{0}(E)
given by an explicit formula (
2.38
). Here
\Phi_{ij}
is invertible for a sufficiently negative
E_{*}
on the real axis and all its eigenvalues become positive.
Therefore

R(E_{*})-R_{0}(E_{*})=R_{0}(E_{*})\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}|f_{i}\rangle\Phi^{-1}_{ij}\langle f_{j}|R_{0}(E_{*})\;

(2.102)

indeed becomes a finite rank operator. For this, note that the principal matrix has a spectral decomposition
\Phi^{-1}(E_{*})=\sum_{k}\omega_{k}^{-1}(E_{*})A^{(k)}(E_{*})A^{(k)*}(E_{*})
with
A^{(k)}
representing the
k
th eigenvector of
\Phi(E)
and
\omega_{k}
is the corresponding eigenvalue. All the eigenvalues become positive for
E_{*}
. We therefore need to observe that all the vectors

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2}\omega_{k}^{-1/2}(E_{*})A_{i}^{(k)}R_{0}(E_{*})|f_{i}\rangle\;

(2.103)

for
k=1,2
have a finite norm, as can be seen as follows;

\displaystyle||\sum_{i=1}^{2}\omega_{k}^{-1/2}(E_{*})A^{(k)}_{i}R_{0}(E_{*})|f_{i}\rangle||\leq\sum_{i=1}^{2}|\omega_{k}^{-1/2}(E_{*})A_{i}^{(k)}|||R_{0}(E_{*})|f_{i}\rangle||\;.

(2.104)

Hence, we have shown that
R(E)-R_{0}(E)
is a trace class operator, which is compact.

2.2
Stationary Scattering Problem

Stationary scattering problem for such singular potentials is well-defined, that is, the wave operators
\Omega_{\pm}

exist and are complete
thanks to Birman-Kuroda theorem
[
40
]
. This theorem states that if the difference between the resolvent
(H-E)^{-1}
of the self-adjoint operator
H
and the resolvent of the free self-adjoint Hamiltonian
(H_{0}-E)^{-1}
, this difference being defined on their common resolvent set, is trace class, then wave operators exist and are complete. We have already shown above that
R(E)-R_{0}(E)
is trace class. Therefore, the wave operators for defining scattering phenomena exist. Once we have well-defined wave operators, we can study physically measurable quantities (e.g., cross section) of a scattering experiment by finding the scattering amplitudes. For this reason, we first need to determine the boundary values of the operator
T(E)
as
E
approaches to the positive real axis from above. This is accomplished from the explicit formula of the resolvent written on the complex plane. For convenience, let
E=E_{k}+i\epsilon
where
E_{k}=k^{2}
with
k>0
. The relation between the resolvent and operator
T
is given by
[
41
]

\displaystyle R(E)=R_{0}(E)-R_{0}(E)T(E)R_{0}(E)\;.

(2.105)

Then, we have the following result for the differential cross section.

Theorem 2.10
.

The differential cross section for the rank one perturbation supported by a circle of radius
R
centered at the origin and by a point at
\mathbf{a}
outside of the circle is given by

\displaystyle\frac{d\sigma}{d\theta}=|f(\mathbf{k}\rightarrow\mathbf{k}^{\prime})|^{2}=\frac{1}{8\pi k}\Bigg|e^{i(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{a}}\left(\Phi^{-1}(E_{k}+i0)\right)_{11}+J_{0}(kR)\left(e^{-i\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{a}}+e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{a}}\right)\left(\Phi^{-1}(E_{k}+i0)\right)_{12}

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt+\,J_{0}^{2}(kR)\left(\Phi^{-1}(E_{k}+i0)\right)_{22}\Bigg|^{2}\;,

(2.106)

where
\Phi(E_{k}+i0)
is defined by the analytic continuation of (
2
).

Proof.

Since we have the explicit expression for resolvent (
2.38
) extended onto the complex plane, we can read off the boundary values of operator
T(E)
on the positive real axis:

\displaystyle T(E_{k}+i0)=-\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}|f_{i}\rangle\left(\Phi^{-1}(E_{k}+i0)\right)_{ij}\langle f_{j}|\;,

(2.107)

where

\displaystyle\Phi(E_{k}+i0)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(-\frac{i\pi}{2}+\log\left(\frac{k}{\mu}\right)\right)&&-\frac{i}{4}H_{0}^{(1)}\left(ka\right)J_{0}\left(kR\right)\\
\\
-\frac{i}{4}H_{0}^{(1)}\left(ka\right)J_{0}\left(kR\right)&&\frac{1}{\lambda_{2}}-\frac{i}{4}H_{0}^{(1)}\left(kR\right)J_{0}\left(kR\right)\end{array}\right)\;.

Here we have used
K_{0}(z)=\frac{i\pi}{2}H_{0}^{(1)}(e^{i\pi/2}z)
and
I_{0}(z)=e^{-i\pi/2}J_{0}(e^{i\pi/2}z)
for
-\pi<arg(z)<\pi/2

[
35
]
. The scattering amplitude denoted by
f
and the boundary values of the operator
T
in two dimensions is related by

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{k}\rightarrow\mathbf{k}^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi k}}\langle\mathbf{k}^{\prime}|T(E_{k}+i0)|\mathbf{k}\rangle\;,

(2.112)

where
|\mathbf{k}\rangle
is the generalized Dirac ket vector and
|\mathbf{k}^{\prime}|=|\mathbf{k}|
. (We note that there is another choice for the scattering amplitude by ignoring the factor
\sqrt{i/k}
to get some desirable properties
[
42
]
, here we use the conventional version). Substituting the result (
2.107
) into

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{k}^{\prime}|T(E_{k}+i0)|\mathbf{k}\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}-i\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}\langle\mathbf{x}^{\prime}|T(E_{k}+i0)|\mathbf{x}\rangle\;d^{2}x\,d^{2}x^{\prime}

(2.113)

and using the integral representation of the Bessel function
J_{0}(x)
given in (
2.22
) we find

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{k}^{\prime}|T(E_{k}+i0)|\mathbf{k}\rangle=e^{i(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{a}}\left(\Phi^{-1}(E_{k}+i0)\right)_{11}+J_{0}(kR)\left(e^{-i\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{a}}+e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{a}}\right)\left(\Phi^{-1}(E_{k}+i0)\right)_{12}

\displaystyle+\,J_{0}^{2}(kR)\left(\Phi^{-1}(E_{k}+i0)\right)_{22}\;,

(2.114)

where
\left(\Phi^{-1}(E_{k}+i0)\right)_{ij}
is the
ij
th element of the inverse of the matrix
\Phi(E_{k}+i0)
given in equation (
2.2
).

Rank One Perturbation Supported by a Sphere and a Point in
\mathbb{R}^{3}

In this section, we will consider the rank one perturbation supported by a sphere and by a point in three dimensions. Since all the techniques and results are similar to the case discussed in the previous section, we will summarize some results without giving detailed proofs.

The regularized Hamiltonian for this model is given by

\displaystyle H_{a\Sigma,\,\epsilon}=H_{0}-\lambda_{1}(\epsilon)|\mathbf{a}^{\epsilon}\rangle\langle\mathbf{a}^{\epsilon}|-\lambda_{2}|\Sigma^{\epsilon}\rangle\langle\Sigma^{\epsilon}|\;,

(3.1)

where
\Sigma
is the sphere centered at the origin with radius
R
and

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{a}^{\epsilon}|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}K_{\epsilon/2}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{a})\psi(\mathbf{r})\;d^{3}r\;,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\langle\Sigma^{\epsilon}|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{A(S^{2})}\int_{S^{2}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}K_{\epsilon/2}(\mathbf{r},\bm{\sigma}(\theta,\phi))\psi(\mathbf{r})\;d^{3}r\right)dA\;.

(3.3)

Here
\bm{\sigma}:(0,2\pi)\times(0,\pi)\rightarrow S^{2}
is the local parametrization given by

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}(\theta,\phi):=(R\sin\theta\cos\phi,R\sin\theta\sin\phi,R\cos\theta)\;.

(3.4)

Proceeding analogously to the previous construction of the resolvent for the circular defect accompanied by a point defect problem, we obtain the resolvent essentially in the same form (
2.38
). In this case, the first diagonal element of the matrix
\Phi
for
E=-\nu^{2}
can be calculated similarly:

\displaystyle\Phi_{11}(-\nu^{2})=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{t+\epsilon}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})\left(e^{-t\mu^{2}}-e^{-t\nu^{2}}\right)dt=\frac{(\nu-\mu)}{4\pi}\;,

(3.5)

by choosing the bare coupling constant
\lambda_{1}(\epsilon)
of the point interaction to be of the same type as (
2.43
) except the heat kernel here is written in three dimensions. Choosing the support of the point defect along the
z
axis, we find the off-diagaonal matrix elements of
\Phi
by going to spherical coordinates and evaluating the radial part of the integral by the residue theorem,

4
Small Deformations of a Circle in
\mathbb{R}^{2}

It would be interesting to ask how the bound state spectrum and scattering cross section for the above (or similar) problems change under small deformation of the support of the rank one perturbations. Let us first briefly define the normal deformations of a general curve in two dimensions. We consider a regular planar curve
\Gamma
parametrized with its arc length
s
with finite length. The Serret-Frenet equations for this curve are given by

\mathbf{t}=\frac{d\bm{\gamma}}{ds}
,
\frac{d\mathbf{t}}{ds}=\kappa\mathbf{n}
, and
\frac{d\mathbf{n}}{ds}=-\kappa\mathbf{t}
, where
\mathbf{t}
,
\mathbf{n}
are the tangent and normal vectors and
\kappa
is the curvature of the curve
\Gamma

[
44
]
.
The small deformation along a normal direction of the curve
\Gamma
is defined by

\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}(s)=\bm{\gamma}(s)+\epsilon h(s)\mathbf{n}(s)\;,

(4.1)

where
h
is assumed to be a smooth function of
s
. It is worth pointing out that
\epsilon
here is a small deformation parameter,
not the same parameter used for regularization
.

The length of the deformed curve
\tilde{\Gamma}
up to order
\epsilon
is given by

\displaystyle L(\tilde{\Gamma})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{L}\frac{d\tilde{s}}{ds}\;ds=\int_{0}^{L}\left(\frac{d\bm{\tilde{\gamma}}}{ds}\cdot\frac{d\bm{\tilde{\gamma}}}{ds}\right)^{1/2}ds=\int_{0}^{L}\left(\left(1-\epsilon\kappa(s)h(s)\right)^{2}+\epsilon^{2}\left(\frac{dh(s)}{ds}\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\;ds

(4.2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{L}\left(1-\epsilon\kappa(s)h(s)+O(\epsilon^{2})\right)\;ds=L(\Gamma)-\epsilon\int_{0}^{L}\kappa(s)h(s)ds+O(\epsilon^{2})\;.

If
\Gamma
is a circle of radius
R
,
\kappa=1/R
so that

\displaystyle L(\tilde{\Gamma})=2\pi R-\frac{\epsilon}{R}\int_{0}^{L}h(s)ds+O(\epsilon^{2})\;.

(4.3)

When we do reparametrization of the curve by the angle
\theta
, we will use the same notation for the functions
h
,
\bm{\gamma}
, and
\mathbf{n}
.

4.1
Perturbative First Order Calculation of the Bound State Energy

We consider here that the interaction is formally represented by
\lambda|\tilde{\Gamma}\rangle\langle\tilde{\Gamma}|
- rank one perturbation supported on a deformed circle.
Since the support of the defect has co-dimension one, the renormalization is not required for this model and the resolvent of the Hamiltonian
H
associated with a deformed circular defect can be found by using similar arguments summarized previously, as a result we find

\displaystyle R(E)=R_{0}(E)+R_{0}(E)|\tilde{\Gamma}\rangle\frac{1}{\tilde{\Phi}(E)}\langle\tilde{\Gamma}|R_{0}(E)\;,

(4.4)

where we denote the deformation of the circle
\tilde{S^{1}}
by
\tilde{\Gamma}
for notational simplicity. For the bound state, we need to calculate

\displaystyle\tilde{\Phi}(-\nu^{2})=\frac{1}{\lambda}-\langle\tilde{\Gamma}|R_{0}(-\nu^{2})|\tilde{\Gamma}\rangle=\frac{1}{\lambda}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{|\langle\tilde{\Gamma}|\mathbf{p}\rangle|^{2}}{p^{2}+\nu^{2}}\;\frac{d^{2}p}{(2\pi)^{2}}\;.

(4.5)

Using

\displaystyle\langle\tilde{\Gamma}|\mathbf{p}\rangle=\frac{1}{L(\tilde{\Gamma})}\int_{0}^{L}e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot\bm{\tilde{\gamma}}(s)}\;|\bm{\tilde{\gamma}^{\prime}}(s)|\,ds\;,

(4.6)

and expanding the exponential
e^{i\epsilon h(\theta)\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{n}(\theta)}
in
\epsilon
and the fact
|\bm{\tilde{\gamma}^{\prime}}(s)|=1-\frac{\epsilon}{R}h(s)+O(\epsilon^{2})
, it is easy to show that

\displaystyle\hskip-56.9055pt\tilde{\Phi}(-\nu^{2})=\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\left(1+\frac{\epsilon}{\pi R}\int_{0}^{2\pi}h(\theta)d\theta\right)\Bigg[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bigg(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\bm{\gamma}(\theta_{1})-\bm{\gamma}(\theta_{2}))}}{p^{2}+\nu^{2}}\;

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt\times\bigg(1-\frac{\epsilon}{R}(h(\theta_{1})+h(\theta_{2}))+i\epsilon((\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{n}(\theta_{1}))h(\theta_{1})-(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{n}(\theta_{2}))h(\theta_{2}))\bigg)d\theta_{1}d\theta_{2}\bigg)\Bigg]\frac{d^{2}p}{(2\pi)^{2}}+O(\epsilon^{2})\;.

(4.7)

Let us consider the first integral in the square bracket above:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\bm{\gamma}(\theta_{1})-\bm{\gamma}(\theta_{2}))}}{p^{2}+\nu^{2}}d\theta_{1}d\theta_{2}\right)\frac{d^{2}p}{(2\pi)^{2}}\;.

(4.8)

The uniformly convergent plane wave expansion in two dimensions
[
42
]

\displaystyle e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{r}}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\varepsilon_{m}i^{m}J_{m}(pr)\cos(m\theta)\;,

(4.9)

with
\varepsilon_{0}=1
,
\varepsilon_{m}=2
if
m>0
, and
\theta
being the angle between
\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{r}
helps us to compute the above integral with respect to the angle variables easily and left with the integration over the variable
p
only:

\displaystyle(2\pi)\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{J_{0}^{2}(pR)}{p^{2}+\nu^{2}}\;p\,dp\;,

(4.10)

where we have used
\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos(m(\theta-\theta_{k}))d\theta=2\pi\delta_{m0}
. Thanks to the integral representation
[
34
]

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x}{x^{2}+a^{2}}J_{0}^{2}(x)dx=I_{0}(a)K_{0}(a)\;,

(4.11)

we find

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\bm{\gamma}(\theta_{1})-\bm{\gamma}(\theta_{2}))}}{p^{2}+\nu^{2}}d\theta_{1}d\theta_{2}\right)\frac{d^{2}p}{(2\pi)^{2}}=(2\pi)I_{0}(\nu R)K_{0}(\nu R)\;.

(4.12)

For the second integral in equation (
4.7
), it is sufficient to consider

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\bm{\gamma}(\theta_{1})-\bm{\gamma}(\theta_{2}))}}{p^{2}+\nu^{2}}h(\theta_{1})d\theta_{1}d\theta_{2}\right)\frac{d^{2}p}{(2\pi)^{2}}\;.

(4.13)

With the help of the plane wave expansion (
4.9
) and the formula (
4.11
), the above integral becomes

\displaystyle I_{0}(\nu R)K_{0}(\nu R)\left(\int_{S^{1}}h(\theta)d\theta\right)\;.

(4.14)

The last integral in (
4.7
) can be computed similarly by first rewriting the expression
i(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{n}(\theta))e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot\bm{\gamma}(\theta)}=\frac{\partial}{\partial R}(e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot\bm{\gamma}(\theta)})
and
\frac{dJ_{0}(x)}{dx}=-J_{1}(x)
we find

\displaystyle\hskip-56.9055pt\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bigg(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\bm{\gamma}(\theta_{1})-\bm{\gamma}(\theta_{2}))}}{p^{2}+\nu^{2}}\;i\,(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{n}(\theta_{1}))h(\theta_{1})d\theta_{1}d\theta_{2}\bigg)\frac{d^{2}p}{(2\pi)^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\left(\int_{S^{1}}h(\theta)d\theta\right)\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{0}(pR)J_{1}(pR)\,\frac{p^{2}}{p^{2}+\nu^{2}}\;dp\;.

(4.15)

Rewriting
\frac{p^{2}}{p^{2}+\nu^{2}}
as
1-\frac{\nu^{2}}{p^{2}+\nu^{2}}
, and using the formula (6.512) in
[
34
]
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{\nu}(\alpha x)J_{\nu-1}(\alpha x)dx=\frac{1}{2\alpha}\;,

(4.16)

and formula (6.577) in
[
34
]
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{J_{0}(pR)J_{1}(pR)}{p^{2}+\nu^{2}}dp=\frac{1}{\nu}I_{1}(\nu R)K_{0}(\nu R)\;,

(4.17)

it follows that

\displaystyle\hskip-85.35826pt\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bigg(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\bm{\gamma}(\theta_{1})-\bm{\gamma}(\theta_{2}))}}{p^{2}+\nu^{2}}\;i\,(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{n}(\theta_{1}))h(\theta_{1})d\theta_{1}d\theta_{2}\bigg)\frac{d^{2}p}{(2\pi)^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{1}{2R}-\nu I_{1}(\nu R)K_{0}(\nu R)\right)\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}h(\theta)d\theta\right)\;.

(4.18)

After combining all the above results (
4.12
), (
4.14
), and (
4.18
), we finally obtain

\displaystyle\hskip-42.67912pt\tilde{\Phi}(-\nu^{2})=\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{2\pi}I_{0}(\nu R)K_{0}(\nu R)+\frac{\epsilon}{2\pi^{2}}\left(-\frac{1}{2R}+\nu I_{0}(\nu R)K_{1}(\nu R)\right)\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}h(\theta)d\theta\right)+O(\epsilon^{2})\;,

(4.19)

where we have used
I_{1}(x)K_{0}(x)+I_{0}(x)K_{1}(x)=1/x
.

When there is no deformation (
\epsilon=0
), we have only one bound state. This can be seen by simply expressing the second term
I_{0}(\nu R)K_{0}(\nu R)
using its integral representation (
4.11
):

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda}=\frac{1}{2\pi}I_{0}(\nu R)K_{0}(\nu R)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x}{x^{2}+\nu^{2}R^{2}}J_{0}^{2}(x)dx\;.

(4.20)

Then, by taking the derivative of the right hand side with respect to
\nu
under the integral sign, it is easy to see that the right hand side of the above equation is a decreasing function of
\nu
for given parameters
\lambda
and
R
. Therefore, there is a unique solution, say
\nu_{0}
, to the above equation.

It is important to notice that deformations satisfying
\int_{0}^{2\pi}h(\theta)d\theta=0
do not change the bound state energies up to first order in
\epsilon
. Since we evaluate the deformation to order
\epsilon
we can actually solve the bound state energy for the deformed curve to the same order. In
[
33
,
45
]
we derived a general formula for perturbations of eigenvalues for small perturbations of the principal matrix
\Phi
, here we have a one-dimensional version of this formula so we can directly use the expansion above. Let
\nu=\nu_{0}+\epsilon\nu_{1}+O(\epsilon^{2})
, where
\nu_{0}
denotes the solution to the original unperturbed circle case. Then, the bound state energy
E_{B}=-(\nu_{0}+\epsilon\nu_{1})^{2}
for the deformed circular defect can be found by the zeroes of
\tilde{\Phi}
. This is achieved up to order
\epsilon
by simply expanding its first term around
\nu_{0}
,

\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{2\pi}I_{0}((\nu_{0}+\epsilon\nu_{1})R)K_{0}((\nu_{0}+\epsilon\nu_{1})R)+\frac{\epsilon}{2\pi^{2}}\left(-\frac{1}{2R}+\nu_{0}I_{0}(\nu_{0}R)K_{1}(\nu_{0}R)\right)\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}h(\theta)d\theta\right)=0\;,

(4.21)

and using the fact that the zeroth order term cancels out
\frac{1}{\lambda}
above to get the solution
\nu_{1}
. Hence, we obtain an explicit formula for the bound state energy up to order
\epsilon

\displaystyle E_{B}=-\nu_{0}^{2}-\epsilon\,\frac{2\nu_{0}}{\pi R}\;\left(\frac{\left(\frac{1}{2R}-\nu_{0}I_{0}(\nu_{0}R)K_{1}(\nu_{0}R)\right)}{I_{1}(\nu_{0}R)K_{0}(\nu_{0}R)-I_{0}(\nu_{0}R)K_{1}(\nu_{0}R)}\right)\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}h(\theta)d\theta\right)+O(\epsilon^{2})\;,

(4.22)

which can be further simplified into the following.

Theorem 4.1
.

Under the small deformation of the circle described by (
4.1
), the bound state energy of the system up to first order in
\epsilon
is given by

\displaystyle E_{B}=-\nu_{0}^{2}-\epsilon\,\frac{\nu^{2}_{0}}{\pi R}\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}h(\theta)d\theta\right)+O(\epsilon^{2})\;.

(4.23)

The simplicity of the first order result is remarkable, and hints at a geometric interpretation.
Suppose that instead of the original circle with radius
R
we replace the circle with a circle of radius
R-\epsilon\langle h\rangle
where

\langle h\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi R}\int_{0}^{2\pi}h(\theta)Rd\theta
(note that the normal in the curvature description is inward). Because we are now using a delta function supported by a circle, we do have the same eigenvalue equation,

\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{2\pi}I_{0}\big((\nu_{0}+\epsilon\nu_{1})(R-\epsilon\langle h\rangle)\big)K_{0}\big((\nu_{0}+\epsilon\nu_{1})(R-\epsilon\langle h\rangle)\big)=0\;.

(4.24)

If we expand all the terms to order
\epsilon
, we find the relation

R\nu_{1}=\nu_{0}\langle h\rangle
.
By using
E_{B}=-(\nu_{0}+\epsilon\nu_{1})^{2}=-\nu_{0}^{2}-2\epsilon\nu_{0}\nu_{1}
we find exactly the above result.
So we state this observation as follows.

Corollary 4.2
.

A small deformation in the normal direction of a given circle, which supports an attractive delta function, leads to a perturbation of the original bound state energy, to first order the resulting change can be obtained as follows: increase the initial radius by an amount equal to the average of the deformation over the given circle, then compute the first order perturbation of the bound state energy corresponding to this new circle with the same coupling constant.

Remark 4.3
.

It is tempting to push this to the second order and search for, if there is any, a geometric interpretation of the result. But the calculations are rather involved so we postpone it for future work. Note that the circle problem per se can be solved by elementary methods, that is by choosing polar coordinates at the center and writing the delta function along the radial direction. However, a general curve cannot be solved by this approach as there is no natural coordinate system to choose. In the case of small deformation, one can think of rank one perturbation supported on this curve as a rank one perturbation supported on the original circle plus a series of perturbations. This idea leads to, even to first order, a term of the form of
\epsilon\frac{d\delta(r-R)}{dr}\int_{0}^{2\pi}h(\theta)d\theta
and some additional ones coming from the change of arc-length as well as the change of total length.
Here the derivative of delta function term is important since the wave function is of the form (disregarding the normalization)

I_{0}(r\nu_{0})K_{0}(R\nu_{0})\theta(R-r)+I_{0}(R\nu_{0})K_{0}(r\nu_{0})\theta(r-R),

and the usual first order perturbation of energy, which is found by evaluating the expectation value in the state of interest, leads to a
divergence (here we need to use the symmetric choice for the theta function as often used in distribution theory).

The single bound state energy
E_{B}
for the original circular defect can numerically be plotted as a function of
R
with fixed values of
\lambda
. For a particular deformation
h(\theta)=h_{0}\sin^{2}\theta
with
h_{0}=1
unit, we can plot how the bound state energy
E_{B}
changes with respect to
R
numerically with the help of Mathematica, as shown in Fig.

.

with

, it is easy to see that the function
\tilde{\Phi}

Remark 4.4
.

). However, this expression has a rather different purpose: Given any function in
L^{\infty}(-1,1)
, one defines a family of scaled potentials in the straightened strip conveniently expressed in the local coordinates given above. Then, resolvents of the Hamiltonians associated with these scaled potentials are constructed. Finally, one shows that Hamiltonians associated with the scaled potentials converge to the formal Hamiltonian associated with a delta potential supported on
\Gamma
in the norm resolvent sense. Our aim here is to study how the deformation of the rank one potential supported on the circle changes the spectrum of the problem.

Remark 4.5
.

A slightly different version of our deformed circle problem, in which the discrete spectrum of delta potential supported by a circle with a varying coupling constant, in particular a step function on the circle, has been studied in
[
15
]
. The problem of the ring delta potential with variable coupling constant can be considered as an equivalent problem of delta function supported by a deformed circle at first glance. Notice, for instance, that given

Remark 4.6
.

There are also works on more general curves chosen for the support of the delta functions. For instance, the asymptotic behaviour of the bound state energies of the attractive delta potentials supported by any closed Jordan curve in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
as the coupling constant tends to infinity has been studied in
[

and the asymptotic expansions of the bound state energies are found for two attractive delta potentials supported by two concentric circles as their distance tends to zero as well as to infinity in

5.1
Perturbative First Order Calculation of the Bound State Energy

We consider here that the interaction is formally represented by
\lambda|\tilde{\Sigma}\rangle\langle\tilde{\Sigma}|
- rank one perturbation supported on a deformed sphere.
The resolvent can be similarly constructed for the deformed spherical defect by following the same line of arguments discussed above. The explicit form of the resolvent operator is given by

\displaystyle R(E)=R_{0}(E)+R_{0}(E)|\tilde{\Sigma}\rangle\tilde{\Phi}^{-1}(E)\langle\tilde{\Sigma}|R_{0}(E)\;,

(5.3)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{\Phi}(E)=\frac{1}{\lambda}-\langle\tilde{\Sigma}|R_{0}(E)|\tilde{\Sigma}\rangle\;.

(5.4)

For this part, we assume that the sphere problem has a bound state solution.
We will choose

, as we will be interested in a bound state to begin with.
If we use the realization in the Fourier domain, the resolvent kernel is given by

\displaystyle R_{0}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}}|-\nu^{2})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r^{\prime}})}}{p^{2}+\nu^{2}}\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}

(5.5)

Our aim is to calculate the function
\tilde{\Phi}(-\nu^{2})
up to order
\epsilon
. Using (
5.2
) and expanding the terms up to order

, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\Phi}(-\nu^{2})=\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{(4\pi)^{2}}\left(1+\frac{\epsilon}{\pi R}\int_{S^{2}}h(\Omega)d\Omega\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt\times\int_{S^{2}\times S^{2}}R_{0}\left(\bm{\tilde{\sigma}}(\Omega),\bm{\tilde{\sigma}}(\Omega^{\prime})|-\nu^{2}\right)\left(1-\frac{2\epsilon}{R}\left(h(\Omega)+h(\Omega^{\prime})\right)\right)d\Omega d\Omega^{\prime}+O(\epsilon^{2})\;.

The resolvent kernel up to order

can be calculated using (

\displaystyle R_{0}\left(\bm{\tilde{\sigma}}(\Omega),\bm{\tilde{\sigma}}(\Omega^{\prime})|-\nu^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{\bm{\sigma}}(\Omega)}e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{\bm{\sigma}}(\Omega^{\prime})}\frac{(1+\epsilon(i\mathbf{p}\cdot(h(\Omega)\mathbf{N}(\Omega)-h(\Omega^{\prime})\mathbf{N}(\Omega^{\prime})))}{p^{2}+\nu^{2}}\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}+O(\epsilon^{2})\;.

Substituting this into (

\displaystyle\tilde{\Phi}(-\nu^{2})=\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{(4\pi)^{2}}\left(1+\frac{\epsilon}{\pi R}\int_{S^{2}}h(\Omega)d\Omega\right)\Bigg[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\bigg(\int_{S^{2}\times S^{2}}e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\mathbf{\bm{\sigma}}(\Omega)-\mathbf{\bm{\sigma}}(\Omega^{\prime}))}d\Omega d\Omega^{\prime}\bigg)

\displaystyle\times\,\frac{1}{p^{2}+\nu^{2}}\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}+\epsilon\bigg(2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\bigg(\int_{S^{2}\times S^{2}}e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\mathbf{\bm{\sigma}}(\Omega)-\mathbf{\bm{\sigma}}(\Omega^{\prime}))}(i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{N}(\Omega))\,h(\Omega)\;d\Omega d\Omega^{\prime}\bigg)\frac{1}{p^{2}+\nu^{2}}\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}

\displaystyle\langle\Sigma|R_{0}(-\nu^{2})|\Sigma\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(4\pi)^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\bigg(\int_{S^{2}\times S^{2}}e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\mathbf{\bm{\sigma}}(\Omega)-\mathbf{\bm{\sigma}}(\Omega^{\prime}))}d\Omega d\Omega^{\prime}\bigg)\frac{1}{p^{2}+\nu^{2}}\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi R}K_{1/2}(\nu R)I_{1/2}(\nu R)\;.

Appendix: Trotter-Kato Theorem

This is a slightly different version of Trotter-Kato theorem than typically found in the literature
[
48
]
, as stated also in
[
24
]
:

Theorem
.

Suppose that
H_{n}
be a sequence of self-adjoint operators with resolvents
R_{n}(E)=(H_{n}-E)^{-1}
defined for all complex numbers
E
except a closed proper subset
U
of
\mathbb{R}
. Furthermore, assume that
R_{n}(E)
converges strongly for some
E\notin U
and this limit is invertible. Then, there exists a self-adjoint operator
H
with resolvents
R(E)=(H-E)^{-1}
such that
R_{n}(E)
converges strongly to
R(E)
for all complex numbers
E\notin U

The idea of the proof is essentially the same as the original Trotter-Kato theorem. In our problem, we choose a sequence
\epsilon_{n}=1/n
. If
n
is sufficiently large
\det\Phi(\epsilon,E)\neq 0
if
E
satisfies
\det\Phi(E)\neq 0
. Then,
R(\epsilon_{n},E)
is defined for all complex
E
except a closed proper subset
U
of
\mathbb{R}
, namely
U=\{E\in[0,\infty):\det\Phi(E)=0\}
. Since we have shown that
R(\epsilon_{n},E)
converges strongly for some
E\notin U
(e.g., choose
E
to be a sufficiently large negative real number) and the limit is invertible, we conclude that there exists a self-adjoint operator
H
with resolvents
R(E)=(H-E)^{-1}
such that
R(\epsilon_{n},E)
converges strongly to
R(E)
for all complex numbers
E\notin U
thanks to the above theorem.
