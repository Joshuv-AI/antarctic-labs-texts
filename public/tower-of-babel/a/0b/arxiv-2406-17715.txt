Scattering for the one dimensional Hartree Fock equation
Cyril Malézé

Abstract
. We consider the Hartree-Fock equation in 1D, for a small and localised initial data and a finite measure potential. We show that there is no long range scattering due to a nonlinear cancellation between the direct term and the exchange term for plane waves. We employ the framework of space-time resonances that enables us to single out precisely this cancellation and to obtain scattering to linear waves as a consequence.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
The Hartree-Fock equation and the framework of random fields

We consider the following time-dependent Hartree-Fock equation for fermions

(1)

i\partial_{t}\gamma=[-\Delta+w*\rho+\mathcal{X},\gamma]

for a nonnegative self-adjoint operator
\gamma
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with kernel
k
, density
\rho(x)=k(x,x)
and where
\mathcal{X}
denotes the exchange term operator with kernel
-w(x-y)k(x,y)
, where
w
is an even pairwise finite measure and represents the interaction potential.

Equation (
1
) is the standard Hartree-Fock equation for density matrices, and can be reformulated in the probability framework:

(2)

i\partial_{t}X=-\Delta X+(w*\mathbb{E}[|X|^{2}])X-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}w(x-y)\mathbb{E}[\overline{X(y)}X(x)]X(y)\,dy,

where
X:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega\mapsto\mathbb{C}
is a random field defined over a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
,
\mathbb{E}
denotes the expectancty on
\Omega
. The introduction of the probability framework for the Hartree and the Hartree-Fock equation was already presented and motivated for instance in
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
40
]
.

The time-dependent Hartree-Fock equation is a mean-field equation for the dynamics of large Fermi systems. The derivation of (
1
) in the mean field limit was first done in
[
2
]
, and was extended to the case of unbounded interaction potentials, as the Coulomb one, in
[
21
]
. Estimates for the convergence in the semi-classical limit that arises for confined Fermi systems were proved in
[
5
,
20
]
, and were extended recently to mixed states, and conditionally to more singular interaction potentials, in
[
3
,
44
]
. Another derivation by different techniques, and including another large volume regime for long-range potentials, was given in
[
1
,
43
]
. The Cauchy problem for localised solutions to (
1
) was studied in
[
6
,
7
,
10
,
11
,
49
]
. One can check
[
14
]
for a quick formal derivation of (
2
).

Equations (
1
) and (
2
) have received less attention than the static Hartree-Fock equation or the reduced Hartree-Fock equation

(3)

i\partial_{t}X=-\Delta X+(w*\mathbb{E}[|X|^{2}])X,

in the probability framework, and

(4)

i\partial_{t}\gamma=[-\Delta+w*\rho,\gamma],

for density matrices. We mention that the exchange term in the Hartree equation does not appear in the case of bosonic systems, but always appear for fermions. It is due to the form of the canonical wave functions considered for each system. The exchange term for fermionic system is often negligible compared to the direct term (see
[
43
]
for example), but in some cases it can be relevant to study the influence of the exchange term on the dynamics of the system.

Most of the results describing the dynamics have so far been obtained for the equations without exchange term (
3
) and (
4
). Indeed, the contribution of the exchange term is negligible in certain regimes. This is the case in the semi-classical limit to the Vlasov equation, which was studied in
[
22
]
for a system with a finite number of particles, and in
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
in the limit of number of particles going to infinity. However, we observe in this paper some cancellation phenomena when considering the exchange terms, see Subsection
1.3
.

The exchange term can also be approximated as a function of the density
\mathbb{E}(|X|^{2})=\rho
, as in Density Functional Theory (see e.g.
[
8
]
for a review and
[
35
,
46
]
for the Cauchy problem of time-dependent Kohn-Sham equations).

The reduced Hartree equations (
3
) and (
4
) admit nonlocalised equilibria that models a space-homogeneous electron gas. The stability of such equilibria has been studied in
[
12
,
13
,
37
,
38
]
for Equation (
4
) and
[
15
,
16
,
30
]
for Equation (
3
). The stability of the zero solution for the time-dependent Kohn-Sham equation was showed in
[
45
]
. In
[
31
]
, Hadama proved the stability of steady states for the reduced Hartree equation in a wide class, which includes Fermi gas at zero temperature in dimension greater than
3
, with smallness asumption on the potential function. In a recent work
[
42
]
, Nguyen and You proved that the symbol of the linearised problem could not be inverted in the case of the Coulomb interaction potential. However, they were still able to describe and to prove some time decay for the linearised dynamics. The Hartree-Fock equation (
2
) also admits nonlocalised equilibria studied in
[

]
.

1.2.
Long range scattering for NLS and Hartree-Fock equations

Long range scattering is a way to describe the long-time dynamics of a solution of a dispersive equation. In some cases, the solution has a different asymptotic behavior than the one of a linear solution of the underlying equation, hence the solution does not enjoy scattering, and we need to incorporate a logarithmic phase correction to describe the asymptotic. In this case, we say that the solution enjoy long range scattering. For equation (
1
), we do not expect scattering in dimension 1 in
L^{2}
. As explained in
[
47
]
(principle 3.1), the critical regularity for scattering for a solution of

(5)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}i\partial_{t}u+\Delta u&=&\pm\lvert u\rvert^{p-1}u\\
u(0)&=&u_{0}\end{array}\right.,

is
s_{c}=\frac{d}{2}-\frac{2}{p-1}
, where
d
is the dimension and
p
the order of the non-linearity. In the case of (
1
), with
w
a finite measure, we expect a behaviour similar to the behaviour of the solution of (
5
) with
d=1
and
p=2
, that does not scatter in
L^{2}
.

The long range scattering for NLS has been widely investigated. Many methods have been introduced, we mention some of them. One can found argument relying on a bootstrap estimate involving
the energy-type norm in the work
[
32
]
. We also mention the work
[
39
]
, where the author introduce a new variable similar to the profile of the solution and then using energy estimates on this new variable. Another method used in
[
33
]
, consists in a wave packet decomposition, using a factorization of the Schrödinger linear group as seen in
[
26
]
. Another approach in
[
19
]
capitalizes on the complete integrability of the 1d cubic NLS through the use of inverse scattering. In
[
36
]
, the authors use the theory of space time resonances to show long range scattering for small initial data, for the non-linear Schrödinger (NLS) equation in dimension 1, and for the reduced Hartree-Fock equation with a Coulomb potential in dimension greater than 2.
This two cases are critical from the point of view of long time asymptotic behavior. For details on long range scattering for NLS and on the aforementioned papers, one can check the review
[
41
]
.

Like the previous work of Kato and Pusateri
[
36
]
, the main ideas in this paper come from the theory of space time resonances, and are mostly inspired by the works of Germain, Masmoudi and Shatah
[
23
,
24
,
25
]
. This theory was used for instance to study scattering for the Gross-Pitaevskii equation in three dimensions
[
29
]
, or to show global asymptotic stability of solitary waves of the nonlinear Schrödinger equation in space dimension 1
[
17
]
.

In
[
36
]
, the authors introduced a new functional framework that allows a stationary phase argument, that we extend in this paper to the case of the Hartree-Fock equation in dimension 1, for potentials that are finite measures. Using the point of view of
[
36
]
, allows us to make appear some cancellation for the Hartree-Fock equation that suggests that the solution of (
2
) scatters, which contradicts our first expectation. The results in
[
36
]
are similar to the ones in
[
32
]
, but the approach is different and more insightful as it can be extended to other cases as the one treated here.

Concerning long-range scattering for the Hartree-Fock equation, we can mention the works of Wada
[
48
]
and Ikeda
[
34
]
, where the authors obtain long-range scattering for the Hartree-Fock equation with a potential of the form
w(x)=\lambda\lvert x\rvert^{-1}
. The method used in those works are derived from
[
26
]
.

1.3.
Cancellation for plane waves solutions

The results we mentioned in subsection
1.2
on long time dynamics for Hartree equations and NLS in dimension 1, suggest that we could expect long range scattering for solutions of (
2
) with
d=1
, for small and localised initial data. However, we claim that in fact we have the scattering result (
8
). To explain this phenomenon, one can notice that plane wave functions of the form:

(6)

X(t,x,\omega)=f(\omega)e^{ix\xi-it\xi^{2}},

for any function
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
are solutions of
(
2
)
for which the non-linearity cancels out. This is because the direct and exchange terms cancel one another for such solutions. Then, because a small and localised solution of (
2
) is expected for large time to undergo either long range scattering or standard scattering, it should be close to a superposition of localised plane wave solutions of the form (
6
), and the non-linearity in (
2
) would vanish to leading order because of the cancellation. Thus, the solution would be in this case can be approximated by a solution of the linear part of (
2
), and the solution should enjoy scattering.

We mention that when studying nonlocalised equilibria of (
2
) (see
[
14
]
), the authors did not use this cancellation to prove the scattering results, as they studied non-localised equilibria, and they might have not expected a cancellation of this type. Moreover, in
[
34
,
48
]
, results of long range scattering are obtained in dimension greater than 2 for the Coulomb potential for the Hartree-Fock equation. In this case, the interactions of plane wave functions in the solutions are non-localised and thus the cancellation that we highlight in the present framework does not appear.

1.4.
Main result

In this paper, we prove the following, recalling that
\mathfrak{S}^{1}
is the standard Schatten space 1-norm for operators on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
w
be a finite measure and
\gamma_{0}
an operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, such that

\text{Tr}(\langle\nabla\rangle\gamma_{0})+\text{Tr}(\langle x\rangle\gamma_{0})\leq\varepsilon,

for
\varepsilon>0
small enough (depending on
w
), there exists a unique global solution
\gamma\in L_{t}^{\infty},\mathfrak{S}^{1}
of (
1
) with initial condition
\gamma(t=0)=\gamma_{0}
, that scatters at infinity, in the sense that there exists
\gamma_{\infty}\in\mathfrak{S}^{1}
such that for
t\geq 1
:

(7)

\|\gamma(t)-e^{it\Delta}\gamma_{\infty}\|_{\mathfrak{S}^{1}}\leq t^{-\delta}.

Remark 1.1
.

A unique local solution is provided by
[
6
,
7
]
, we prove in this paper that it is global and scatters at infinity in
\mathfrak{S}^{1}
.

Remark 1.2
.

The equation is time reversible, so the same results for negative times holds.

We prove Theorem
1.1
as a consequence of the following scattering theorem in the probality framework (for the notations of the functional spaces and their related norms, see subsection
1.5
)

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
w
be a finite measure and
\theta\in[0,1)
. There exists
\varepsilon>0
such that for some
0<\delta<\frac{1}{4}
and for any
X_{0}\in H^{1,0},L_{\omega}^{2}\cap H^{0,1},L_{\omega}^{2}
, with
\|X_{0}\|_{H^{1,0},L_{\omega}^{2}\cap H^{0,1},L_{\omega}^{2}}\leq\varepsilon
, there exists a unique global solution
X\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R},H^{1,0},L_{\omega}^{2}\cap H^{0,1},L_{\omega}^{2})
of (
2
) with initial condition
X(t=1)=e^{i\Delta}X_{0}
, which scatters at infinity, in the sense that there exists
\hat{W}\in H^{\theta,0}_{\xi},L_{\omega}^{2}\cap H^{0,\theta}_{\xi},L_{\omega}^{2}\cap L_{x}^{\infty},L_{\omega}^{2}
such that for
t\geq 1

(8)

\|\hat{X}(t)-e^{-it\xi^{2}}\hat{W}\|_{H^{\theta,0}_{\xi}\cap H^{0,\theta}_{\xi},L_{\omega}^{2}\cap L_{\xi}^{\infty},L_{\omega}^{2}}\leq t^{-\delta}.

Remark 1.3
.

The connections between the two equations is explained in subsection
1.1
, and is made rigorous in
[
18
]
and section
5
.

Remark 1.4
.

We almost reach scattering in
H^{1,0}\cap H^{0,1}
which is the set for the initial data. This slightly improves results in
[
36
]
, and we mention that in
[
48
]
, the author obtains a similar almost optimal result.

Proof of Proposition
2.1

3.1.
Setting the stationary phase argument

In this subsection, we set a stationary phase argument.

Writing Duhamel’s formula for the Cauchy problem (
2
) gives

(12)

X(t)=e^{i(t-1)\Delta}X_{0}+e^{it\Delta}C(X,X,X),

with

C(X,X,X)=-i\int_{1}^{t}e^{-is\Delta}\Big(\big(w*\mathbb{E}[\lvert X(s)\rvert^{2}]\big)X(s)-\int w(x-y)\mathbb{E}[\overline{X(s,y)}X(s,x)]X(s,y)dy\Big)ds.

We set
Z=e^{-it\Delta}X
the profile of the solution, then it verifies

(13)

\hat{Z}(t,\xi)=\hat{X_{0}}(\xi)+\hat{C}(X,X,X)(\xi).

To understand the dynamic in long time, we compute
\hat{C}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

The function
C(X,X,X)
verifies

(14)

\hat{C}(X,X,X)=I_{1}+I_{2},

with

I_{1}=-i\mathcal{F}_{x}\Bigg(\int_{1}^{t}ds\ e^{-is\Delta}(w*\mathbb{E}[\lvert X(s)\rvert^{2}])X(s))\Bigg),

and

I_{2}=i\mathcal{F}_{x}\Bigg(\int_{1}^{t}ds\ e^{-is\Delta}\int dy\ w(x-y)\mathbb{E}[\overline{X(s,y)}X(s,x)]X(s,y)dy\Bigg).

Proof.

We compute
I_{1}
first. Using the Fourier transform of a product

I_{1}=\frac{-i}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{1}^{t}ds\ e^{is\xi^{2}}\big(\mathcal{F}_{x}(w*\mathbb{E}[\lvert X(s)\rvert^{2}]\big)*\mathcal{F}_{x}(X(s)),

and then the Fourier transform of a convolution

I_{1}=-i\int_{1}^{t}ds\ e^{is\xi^{2}}\int d\eta\ \hat{w}(\eta)\mathcal{F}_{x}\big(\mathbb{E}[\lvert X(s)\rvert^{2}]\big)(\eta)\hat{X}(s,\xi-\eta).

Finally, we get

I_{1}=\frac{-i}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{1}^{t}ds\ e^{is\xi^{2}}\int d\eta\ \hat{w}(\eta)\mathbb{E}[\int d\sigma\overline{\hat{X}(s,\sigma-\eta)}\hat{X}(s,\sigma)]\hat{X}(s,\xi-\eta).

Recalling that:
\hat{X}(s,\xi)=e^{-is\xi^{2}}\hat{Z}(s,\xi)
, we obtain

I_{1}=\frac{-i}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{1}^{t}ds\ e^{is\xi^{2}}\int d\eta d\sigma\ \hat{w}(\eta)\mathbb{E}[e^{is(\eta-\sigma)^{2}}\overline{\hat{Z}(s,\sigma-\eta)}e^{-is\sigma^{2}}\hat{Z}(\sigma)]e^{-is(\xi-\eta)^{2}}\hat{Z}(\xi-\eta).

Thus

(15)

I_{1}=\frac{-i}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{1}^{t}ds\int d\eta d\sigma\ e^{2is\eta(\xi-\sigma)}\hat{w}(\eta)\mathbb{E}[\overline{\hat{Z}(s,\sigma-\eta)}\hat{Z}(\sigma)]\hat{Z}(\xi-\eta).

Following the same ideas, we compute
I_{2}
.

First we write

I_{2}=i\int_{0}^{t}ds\ e^{-is\xi^{2}}\mathcal{F}_{x}\bigg(w*\big(\mathbb{E}[\overline{X(s,\cdot)}X(s,x)]X(s,\cdot)\big)\bigg).

We compute that

\mathcal{F}_{x}\big(w*(\mathbb{E}[\overline{X(s,\cdot)}X(s,x)]X(s,\cdot))\big)=\int dx\ e^{-i\xi x}\int dy\ w(x-y)\mathbb{E}[\overline{X(s,y)}X(s,x)]X(s,y).

Using the formula of the inverse Fourier transform, we have

\mathcal{F}_{x}\big(w*(\mathbb{E}[\overline{X(s,\cdot)}X(s,x)]X(s,\cdot))\big)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int dydx\ e^{-i\xi x}w(x-y)\mathbb{E}\Big[\overline{X(s,y)}\int d\eta\ e^{i\eta x}\hat{X}(s,\eta)\Big]X(s,y).

And then, proceeding as for
I_{1}
, we obtain

(16)

I_{2}=\frac{i}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{1}^{t}ds\int d\eta d\sigma\ e^{2is\eta(\xi-\sigma)}\hat{w}(\eta)\mathbb{E}[\overline{\hat{Z}(s,\sigma-\eta)}\hat{Z}(\xi-\eta)]\hat{Z}(\sigma).

Combining (
15
) and (
16
) gives

(17)

\hat{C}=\frac{-i}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{1}^{t}ds\int d\eta d\sigma\ e^{2is\eta(\xi-\sigma)}\hat{w}(\eta)\Bigg(\mathbb{E}[\overline{\hat{Z}(s,\sigma-\eta)}\hat{Z}(\sigma)]\hat{Z}(\xi-\eta)-\mathbb{E}[\overline{\hat{Z}(s,\sigma-\eta)}\hat{Z}(\xi-\eta)]\hat{Z}(\sigma)\Bigg),

which is the desired result (
14
).
∎

Proposition 3.2
.

Z
is a solution of

\hat{Z}(t,\xi)=\hat{X}_{*}(\xi)+\int_{1}^{s}R(s,\xi)ds,

where

(18)

R(s,\xi)=\frac{-i}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int dxdy\ \frac{1}{2s}(e^{\frac{ixy}{2s}}-1)\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\eta,\sigma}(F)(s,x,y,\xi),

and

Proof.

Using a change of variable and using Plancherel’s formula we get, using (
17
)

(19)

\hat{C}(X,X,X)=\frac{-i}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{1}^{t}ds\int dxdy\ \mathcal{F}_{\eta,\sigma}\big(e^{2is\eta\sigma}\big)(x,y)\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\eta,\sigma}(F)(s,x,y,\xi),

And thus

\hat{C}(X,X,X)=\frac{-i}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{1}^{t}ds\int dxdy\ \frac{1}{2s}e^{\frac{ixy}{2s}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\eta,\sigma}(F)(s,x,y,\xi).

Noticing that

we get

\int dxdy\ \mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\eta,\sigma}(F)(s,x,y,\xi)=0,

and then we can write

\hat{C}(X,X,X)=\frac{-i}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{1}^{t}ds\int dxdy\ \frac{1}{2s}(e^{\frac{i\eta\sigma}{2s}}-1)\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\eta,\sigma}(F)(s,x,y,\xi).

Finally, we obtain

(20)

\hat{Z}=\hat{X}_{*}+\int_{1}^{t}R(s,\xi)ds,

where

(21)

R(s,\xi)=\frac{-i}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int dxdy\ \frac{1}{2s}(e^{\frac{i\eta\sigma}{2s}}-1)\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\eta,\sigma}(F(s,\eta,\sigma,\xi))(x,y),

which is the desired identity (

3.2.
Estimate on
R

Setting a stationary phase argument to the oscillatory integral with respect to the variables
\eta
and
\sigma

\hat{Z}(t,\xi)=\hat{X}_{*}(\xi)+\int_{1}^{s}R(s,\xi)ds.

Combining the continuity of the application
