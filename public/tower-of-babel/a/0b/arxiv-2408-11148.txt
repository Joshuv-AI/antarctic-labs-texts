On the logarithmic energy of solutions to the polynomial eigenvalue problem
Diego Armentano, Federico Carrasco, Marcelo Fiori

Abstract
In this paper, we compute the expected logarithmic energy of solutions to the polynomial eigenvalue problem for random matrices. We generalize some known results for the Shub-Smale polynomials, and the spherical ensemble. These two processes are the two extremal particular cases of the polynomial eigenvalue problem, and we prove that the logarithmic energy lies between these two cases. In particular, the roots of the Shub-Smale polynomials are the ones with the lowest logarithmic energy of the family.



Keywords:
Logarithmic Energy, Elliptic Fekete points, Polynomial eigenvalue problem, Random matrices.

MSC Classification:
60B20, 60G55, 31C20, 60J45.

1
Introduction and Main Result

The problem of finding configurations of points in the 2-dimensional sphere with small logarithmic energy is a very challenging problem, with several applications. It is one of the problems listed by Smale for the XXI Century
[
17
]
, and there have been several advances in different directions related to this problem.

Given
N
points in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, the logarithmic energy of the configuration is defined as

V(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})=-\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\ln\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|.

The problem of minimizing this energy in the unit sphere
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
is considered a very hard optimization problem, also known as the Fekete problem. Not only are the configurations of points that minimize the energy not completely understood even for a small number of points (for instance,
N=7
), but also the asymptotic value of the minimum is not known with enough precision. More precisely, let

V_{N}=\min_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}V(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})

be the minimum of the energy in the sphere. The 7th Smale problem consists of finding a configuration of points
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}
in the sphere, in polynomial time in
N
, such that its logarithmic energy
V(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})
is close enough to the minimum, namely
V(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})-V_{N}\leq c\ln N
, for a universal constant
c
.

One of the major obstacles is that the value of
V_{N}
itself is not known with precision up to the
\ln N
term, and therefore problem number 7 of Smale’s list is still far from being solved.

Indeed, the value of
V_{N}
is
[
8
]

V_{N}=\frac{\kappa}{2}N^{2}-\frac{N\ln N}{4}+CN+o(N),

(1)

where
\kappa=\frac{1}{2}-\ln 2
and
C
is an unknown constant. As far as it is known, this constant
C
is bounded (lower bound by
[
14
,
7
]
, upper bound by
[
10
,
8
]
)

-0.028426\ldots=\frac{\ln(2)}{2}-\frac{3}{8}\leq C\leq\ln 2+\frac{1}{4}\ln\frac{2}{3}+\frac{3}{2}\ln\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{\Gamma(1/3)}=-0.027802\ldots,

and the upper bound is conjectured to be actually the value for
C

[
10
,
8
]
.

Despite the intrinsic difficulties of finding these optimal configurations of points, or even the value of the minimal energy, there have been some very exciting advances throughout the last decades. For instance, the diamond ensemble proposed by Beltrán and Etayo
[
6
]
, which achieves configurations of points with logarithmic energy very close to the conjectured minimum, and two other random processes, which we describe in more detail in what follows.

The first one, proposed by Armentano, Beltrán, and Shub in
[
3
]
, consists of taking the roots of a random polynomial. Specifically, let

p_{N}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}a_{k}\binom{N}{k}^{1/2}z^{k}

(2)

with
a_{k}
i.i.d. complex standard Gaussian coefficients
\mathcal{N}_{{\mathbb{C}}}(0,1)
, i.e., each coefficient is
\alpha+i\beta
, with
\alpha,\beta
real independent zero-mean and
1/2
variance Gaussian. Now compute the roots of
p_{N}
in
{\mathbb{C}}
, and project them to
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
through the stereographic projection.
The authors prove that the expected logarithmic energy of the resulting ensemble in
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
is

\frac{\kappa}{2}N^{2}-\frac{N\ln N}{4}-\frac{\kappa}{2}N.

(3)

Observe that the expression coincides up to the first two terms with (
1
), and the constant for the linear term is
-\frac{\kappa}{2}\approx 0.096\ldots
.

More recently, in
[
15
]
the authors prove a central limit theorem for the logarithmic energy resulting from this random process, where they show that the fluctuations are of order
\sqrt{N}
, and therefore a typical realization of this process will have energy close to the expression in (
3
).

The second approach consists of taking the eigenvalues of a random matrix. Specifically, let
A
and
B
be two random matrices with i.i.d. complex standard Gaussian entries
\mathcal{N}_{{\mathbb{C}}}(0,1)
. Now compute the eigenvalues of the matrix
AB^{-1}
, and project them to
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
through the stereographic projection. Alishahi and Zamani
[
1
]
proved that the expected value of the logarithmic energy for this configuration, so-called
spherical ensemble
, is

\frac{\kappa}{2}N^{2}-\frac{N\ln N}{4}+\left(\frac{\ln 2}{2}-\frac{\gamma}{4}\right)N-\frac{1}{8}+O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right),

(4)

where
\gamma=0.57721\ldots
is the Euler-Mascheroni constant. Observe that the first two terms coincide with the known expression in (
1
), and the constant for the linear term is
\left(\frac{\ln 2}{2}-\frac{\gamma}{4}\right)\approx 0.2022\ldots
.

In this paper, we study a strategy for producing configurations of points, for which the last two examples (zeros of random polynomials and eigenvalues of random matrices) are particular cases. Namely, let us consider the following polynomial in
{\mathbb{C}}
,

F(z)=\det\left(\sum_{i=0}^{d}G_{i}{d\choose i}^{1/2}z^{i}\right),

where each
G_{i}
is an
r\times r
random matrix with independent entries distributed as
\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(0,1)
.
The problem of finding the zeros of this function is known as the
polynomial eigenvalue problem
(PEVP). Observe that
F(z)
has, generically,
N=dr
roots in
{\mathbb{C}}
, which can be projected to the unit sphere through the stereographic projection as before. We will denote the PEVP-ensemble to this configuration of points in
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
.

Now, for a given number of points
N
, one can choose different pairs of its divisors
(d,r)
forming
N=dr
. Notably, for
r=1
and
d=N
, we obtain exactly the random polynomials as in (
2
). In the other extreme, for
r=N
and
d=1
, we obtain
F(z)=\det(G_{0}+G_{1}z)
, whose roots coincide with the eigenvalues of
-G_{0}G_{1}^{-1}
, and therefore we recover the spherical ensemble.

Some numerical experiments suggest that the expected logarithmic energy of intermediate instances (meaning
1<d<N
) lies between the energy of the two extremal cases and decreases linearly with
d
. The main result of this paper, which we state below, gives a precise computation of the expected logarithmic energy for the PEVP-ensemble. The numerical experiments, along with the analysis of this dependence on
d
, are presented in Section
3
.

Theorem 1.1

(Main Theorem)
.

Let
F(z)
be the random complex polynomial of degree
N
defined as

F(z)=\det\left(\sum_{i=0}^{d}G_{i}{d\choose i}^{1/2}z^{i}\right),

where
G_{i}
are
r\times r
matrices with i.i.d. entries following
\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(0,1)
. Then, with the definitions above, we have

{\mathbb{E}}(V(x_{1},\cdots,x_{N}))=\frac{\kappa}{2}N^{2}-\frac{N\log d}{4}-\frac{N}{4}\bigg(1+\psi(r+1)-\psi(2)-2\ln 2\bigg)

where
\psi(n)=\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}(n)}{\Gamma(n)}
is the digamma function, i.e., the logarithmic derivative of the gamma function
\Gamma(n)
.

(See Section
2.3
for the proof.)

Observe that this result generalizes the computed expected logarithmic energies of the ensembles by
[
1
]
and
[
3
]
.
Moreover, for
r=N,d=1
, we get

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(V(x_{1},\cdots,x_{N}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa}{2}N^{2}-\frac{N\psi(N+1)}{4}+N\left(\frac{\ln 2}{2}-\frac{\gamma}{4}\right).

This is actually the exact value for the expected value of the spherical ensemble, which, to the best of our knowledge, had not been computed before. Using the usual approximation of
\psi(N+1)
, we obtain the same asymptotic expression as in (
4
). A more detailed asymptotic analysis of this expression is given in Section
3
.

Remark 1.2
.

Observe that in the matrix
\sum_{i=0}^{d}G_{i}{d\choose i}^{1/2}z^{i}
, each entry is a Shub-Smale polynomial, like the ones in (
2
). The distribution of the roots of these polynomials, projected to
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
, are invariant under the orthogonal group. Now, since the determinant is a homogeneous polynomial in the entries of the matrix, the zeros of the resulting process
F(z)
are also invariant under the same group of isometries (Proposition 2.1.1 of
[
13
]
). This invariance in
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
is a desirable property if we want the resulting points of a process in which every configuration is possible to be well distributed in the sphere.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. The proof of the main theorem reduces to compute the expectation of the three terms in (
6
), which present uneven levels of difficulties. Indeed, the results of the first and last terms follow from relatively simple random matrix computations, while the second one requires more tools.

In order to ease the notation, and also to state more general results that can be useful in other contexts, we first state and prove some tools in the framework of random matrices in Section
2.1
. Then, in Section
2.2
, we instantiate some of these results to obtain the expectation of the two most straightforward terms and use the weighted Kac-Rice formula, along with other tools, to compute the remaining expectation. We aggregate these computations in Section
2.3
to complete the proof of the main theorem. We conclude in Section
3
with some experimental results and a discussion on the dependence on
d
of the expected logarithmic energy.

2
Technical tools and proof of Main Theorem

In what follows, we compute the expected logarithmic energy of the PEVP-ensemble, but instead of doing so in the unit sphere, we will derive the expression through the Riemann sphere (i.e., the sphere of radius
1/2
centered at
(0,0,1/2)
as in
[
16
]
). Throughout the paper, we will denote as
z_{1},\ldots,z_{N}
the roots of
F(z)
in
{\mathbb{C}}
, and as
\hat{z}_{1},\ldots,\hat{z}_{N}
their stereographic projections onto the Riemann sphere. We can then transform this configuration to the unit sphere
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
via

(a,b,c)\to(2a,2b,2c-1).

Given a configuration of points
\hat{z}_{1},\ldots,\hat{z}_{N}
in the Riemann sphere, it is easy to see that the logarithmic energies of this configuration and the corresponding configuration in
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
are related as

V(x_{1},\cdots,x_{N})=V(\hat{z}_{1},\cdots,\hat{z}_{N})-\frac{N(N-1)}{2}\ln 2.

(5)

The goal is then to compute
{\mathbb{E}}(V(\hat{z}_{1},\cdots,\hat{z}_{N}))
. Following Armentano et al.
[
3
, Proposition 1]
, since
F(z)
is a polynomial, the logarithmic energy can be decomposed as follows:

V(\hat{z}_{1},\cdots,\hat{z}_{N})=(N-1)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\sqrt{1+|z_{i}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log|F^{\prime}(z_{i})|+\frac{N}{2}\log|a_{N}|,

where
a_{N}
is the leading coefficient of
F(z)
. It is easy to see that this leading coefficient is
\det(G_{d})
.

Then, taking expectation we get that
\mathbb{E}(V(\hat{z}_{1},\cdots,\hat{z}_{N}))
is equal to

(N-1)\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\sqrt{1+|z_{i}|^{2}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log|F^{\prime}(z_{i})|\right)+\frac{N}{2}\mathbb{E}(\log|\det(G_{d})|).

(6)

By computing the expectation of the three terms in the previous expression, we obtain the main result of the paper.

In order to do so, we will first prove some useful results in the following part, and then compute the mentioned expectations in Section
2.2
.

2.1
Technical tools

This section is dedicated to presenting (and proving) the technical tools (and results) necessary for the computations. For those eager to understand the underlying concepts without delving into the technical details, we refer them to Section
2.2
.

2.1.1
Geometric and probabilistic tools

Firstly, let us recall the coarea formula and derive some known results for random variables.

Suppose
\varphi:M\to N
is a surjective map from a Riemannian manifold
M
to a Riemannian manifold
N
, whose derivative
D\varphi(x):T_{x}M\to T_{\varphi(x)}N
is surjective for almost all
x\in M
.
The horizontal space
H_{x}\subset T_{x}M
is defined as the orthogonal complement of
\Ker D\varphi(x)
.
The horizontal derivative of
\varphi
at
x
is the restriction of
D\varphi(x)
to
H_{x}
.
The
Normal Jacobian

NJ_{\varphi}(x)
is the absolute value of the determinant of the horizontal derivative, defined almost everywhere on
M
.

Theorem

(
[
9
]
p. 241 The coarea formula)
.

Let
M,N
be Riemannian manifolds of respective dimension
m\geq n
, and let
\varphi:M\to N
be a smooth surjective map, whose derivative
D\varphi(x):T_{x}M\to T_{\varphi(x)}N
is surjective for almost all
x\in M
.
Then, for any positive measurable function,
\Phi:M\to[0,+\infty)
we have

\int_{x\in M}\Phi(x)dM=\int_{y\in N}\int_{x\in\varphi^{-1}(y)}\frac{\Phi(x)}{NJ_{\varphi}(x)}d\varphi^{-1}(y)dN

and

\int_{x\in M}NJ_{\varphi}(x)\Phi(x)dM=\int_{y\in N}\int_{x\in\varphi^{-1}(y)}\Phi(x)d\varphi^{-1}(y)dN,

where
d\varphi^{-1}(y)
is the induced volume measure in the manifold
\varphi^{-1}(y)
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
X(\cdot):{\mathbb{C}}^{D}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
be a random field with density
\rho_{X}(x)
, and
\varphi:{\mathbb{C}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{l}
be a smooth surjective map, whose derivative
D\varphi(x):{\mathbb{C}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{l}
is surjective for almost all
x\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
.
Then, the pointwise density of the random field
Y(\cdot):{\mathbb{C}}^{D}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{l}
defined by
Y(\cdot)=\varphi(X(\cdot))
is

\rho_{Y}(y)=\int_{\varphi^{-1}(y)}\rho_{X}(x)\frac{1}{NJ_{\varphi}(x)}d\varphi^{-1}(y).

Proof.

It follows from the coarea formula, applied to the fact that

\mathbb{P}(Y\in B)=\mathbb{P}\left(X\in\varphi^{-1}(B)\right)=\int_{\varphi^{-1}(B)}\rho_{X}(x)dx,

for every
B\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{l}
Borel subset.
∎

As a direct application, we get the following result.

Corolary 2.2
.

Let
A\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r\times r}
be a random matrix with i.i.d. centered Gaussian entries
\mathcal{N}_{{\mathbb{C}}}(0,\sigma^{2})
.
Then, the density at
0
of the random variable
\det(A)
is equal to

\rho_{\det(A)}(0)=\frac{1}{\pi\sigma^{2r}\Gamma(r)}.

Proof.

Using the previous lemma, it follows that

\rho_{\det(A)}(0)=\frac{1}{\pi^{r^{2}}\sigma^{2r^{2}}}\int_{\Sigma}e^{\frac{-\|A\|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}}\frac{1}{NJ_{\det}(A)}d\Sigma

where
\Sigma=\{A\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r\times r}\ :\ \det(A)=0\}
.

Following the Jacobi’s formula, the derivative of the determinant at
A
in the direction
\dot{A}
is equal to
\mathop{{\rm tr}}({\rm adj}\,(A)\dot{A})
, where the adjugate matrix of
A
,
{\rm adj}\,(A)
, is the transpose of the cofactor matrix.

After a routine computation, it follows that

NJ_{\det}(A)=\|{\rm adj}\,(A)\|_{F}^{2},

where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
stands for the
Frobenius norm.

Let
\hat{\Sigma}
be the set of rank
r-1
matrices of
{\mathbb{C}}^{r\times r}
. It is clear that
\hat{\Sigma}
is the smooth part of the algebraic variety
\Sigma
.
It follows that integrating over
\Sigma
is the same as integrating over
\hat{\Sigma}
, then

\rho_{\det(A)}(0)=\frac{1}{\pi^{r^{2}}\sigma^{2r^{2}}}\int_{\hat{\Sigma}}e^{\frac{-\|A\|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}}\frac{1}{\|{\rm adj}\,(A)\|_{F}^{2}}d\hat{\Sigma}.

(7)

Given a matrix
A\in\hat{\Sigma}
, the kernel is a one dimensional subspace of
{\mathbb{C}}^{r}
given by the orthogonal complement of the column space of
A
. Furthermore, given a direction
v\in\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{r})
, it is easy to check that there exist a matrix
A\in\hat{\Sigma}
such that the column space is orthogonal to
v
. Therefore, there is a well defined smooth surjective submersion

\varphi:\hat{\Sigma}\to\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{r}),\qquad\varphi(A)=v,\qquad\text{such that \ }Av=0,

(8)

where
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{r})
is the projective space, i.e., the space of one dimensional subspaces of
{\mathbb{C}}^{r}
.

A straightforward computation, using the derivative of an implicit function, shows that the differential map
D\varphi(A):T_{A}\hat{\Sigma}\to T_{v}\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{r})
is defined by

D\varphi(A)\dot{A}=-A^{\dagger}\dot{A}v,

where
A^{\dagger}
is the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse of
A
.
The tangent space to
\hat{\Sigma}
at
A
, can be parametrized as
\{\dot{X}A+A\dot{Y}\ :\dot{X},\dot{Y}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r\times r}\}
(see Arnold et al.
[
4
]
).

It is easy to check that the hermitian complement to
\ker D\varphi(A)
is the image of
\{(0,\dot{w}v^{*})\ :\ \dot{w}^{*}v=0\}
by the map
(\dot{X},\dot{Y})\mapsto\dot{X}A+A\dot{Y}
.
So, applying
D\varphi(A)
, we get the folowing composition

\{(0,\dot{w}v^{*})\ :\ \dot{w}^{*}v=0\}\mapsto A\dot{w}v^{*}\mapsto\dot{w},

which is the identity.

Then, the normal jacobian of the map
\varphi
is the inverse of the normal jacobian of the first map, which is in fact the square of the product of the non-zero singular values of
A
.
In conclusion, the normal jacobian of
\varphi
satisfies,

NJ_{\varphi}(A)=\frac{1}{\|{\rm adj}\,(A)\|^{2}_{F}}.

Applying the coarea formula to
\varphi:\hat{\Sigma}\to\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{r})
, equation (
7
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\rho_{\det(A)}(0)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi^{r^{2}}\sigma^{2r^{2}}}\int_{v\in\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{r})}\int_{A\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r\times(r-1)}}e^{\frac{-\|A\|_{F}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}}dAd\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{r}).

Recall that the volume of
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{r})
is
\frac{\pi^{r-1}}{\Gamma(r)}
.
Noting that the inner intergal does not depend on
v
, it follows that

\displaystyle\rho_{\det(A)}(0)=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi^{r-1}}{\pi^{r^{2}}\sigma^{2r^{2}}\Gamma(r)}\int_{A\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r\times(r-1)}}e^{\frac{-\|A\|_{F}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}}dA.

Finally, applying Fubini’s theorem and taking polar coordinates, we get

\rho_{\det(A)}(0)=\frac{1}{\pi\sigma^{2r}\Gamma(r)}.

∎

Remark 2.3
.

Observe that in this case, where the matrix A has Gaussian entries, this can also be proven using Kostlan’s theorem, as in
[
12
, Thm. 4.7.3]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
A\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r\times r}
be a random matrix with i.i.d. centered Gaussian entries
\mathcal{N}_{{\mathbb{C}}}(0,\sigma^{2})
.
If
\phi:{\mathbb{C}}^{r\times r}\to[0,+\infty)
is a measurable function such that
\phi(MU)=\phi(M)
for any unitary matrix
U
, then

\underset{A\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r\times r}}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\phi(A)\big|\det(A)=0\right)=\underset{A\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r\times r}}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\phi(A)\big|A_{r}=0\right)

where
A_{r}
is the
r
-th column of
A
.

Proof.

By definition, the conditional expectation of
\phi(A)
conditional to
\{\det(A)=0\}
is equal to the integral of
\phi(A)
with respect to the conditional density, i.e.,

\mathbb{E}\left(\phi(A)\big|\det(A)=0\right)=\frac{1}{\rho_{\det(A)}(0)}\int_{\Sigma}\frac{\phi(A)}{NJ_{\det}(A)}\frac{e^{\frac{-\|A\|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}}}{\pi^{r^{2}}\sigma^{2r^{2}}}d\Sigma.

Since
\rho_{\det(A)}(0)=\frac{1}{\pi\sigma^{2r}\Gamma(r)}
,
\phi(AU)=\phi(A)
for every unitary matrix
U
, and applying the coarea formula to the same map
\varphi
in (
8
) as in the previous corollary, we get

\mathbb{E}\left(\phi(A)\big|\det(A)=0\right)=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{r\times(r-1)}}\phi(A)\frac{e^{\frac{-\|A\|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}}}{\pi^{r(r-1)}\sigma^{2r(r-1)}}dA=\mathbb{E}\left(\phi(A)\big|A_{r}=0\right).

∎

2.1.2
Random matrices computations

The proofs of the following lemmas follow the ideas of Azaïs-Wschebor
[
5
]
, where
|\det A|
is considered as the volume of the parallelotope in
{\mathbb{C}}^{r}
generated by the columns
A_{1},\cdots,A_{r}
of
A
.

Observe that
\mathop{\rm vol}(A_{1},\cdots,A_{k})=\mathop{\rm vol}(A_{1},\cdots,A_{k-1})\|\Pi_{V_{k-1}^{\perp}}A_{k}\|
, where
V_{k-1}
is the subspace generated by
A_{1},\cdots,A_{k-1}
, and
\|\Pi_{V_{k-1}^{\perp}}A_{k}\|
is the Euclidean norm in
{\mathbb{C}}^{r}
of the orthogonal projection of the vector
A_{k}
onto the hermitian complement subspace
V_{k-1}
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
A
be a
r\times r
random complex matrix with standard Gaussian i.i.d. entries.
Then

\mathbb{E}(\log|\det A|)=\frac{r(\psi(r+1)-1)}{2},

where
\psi(n)=\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}(n)}{\Gamma(n)}
is the digamma function.

Proof.

Using the linearity of the expectation and the properties of the logarithm, iterating this process, we conclude that

\mathbb{E}(\log(\mathop{\rm vol}(A_{1},\cdots,A_{r})))=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{r}\underset{v_{i}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{i}}{\mathbb{E}}(\log(\|v_{i}\|)),

(9)

where
v_{i}
is a standard Gaussian complex vector in
{\mathbb{C}}^{i}
, and abusing notation,
\|v_{i}\|
stands for the Euclidean norm of
v_{i}
in its corresponding space.

Using polar coordinates,

\underset{v_{i}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{i}}{\mathbb{E}}(\log(\|v_{i}\|))=\frac{2}{\Gamma(i)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho^{2i-1}e^{-\rho^{2}}\log\rho d\rho=\frac{\psi(i)}{2},

where the last equality follows from taking
t=\rho^{2}
and differentiating the integral expression of
\Gamma(i)
with respect to
i
, or see directly Gradshteyn and Ryzhik
[
11
, 4.352.4]
.

Using that
\psi(n)=\left(\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{i}\right)-\gamma
, and that
n\psi(n+1)=n\psi(n)+1
, it follows

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\psi(i)=r(\psi(r+1)-1).

Then, we conclude

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\log(|\det A|))

\displaystyle=\frac{r(\psi(r+1)-1)}{2}.

∎

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
A
be a
r\times r
random complex matrix with standard Gaussian i.i.d. entries.

Then,
\underset{A\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r\times r}}{\mathbb{E}}\left(|\det A|^{2}\log|\det A|\right)
is equal to

\frac{r!}{2}\bigg((r+1)\psi(r+2)-r-\psi(2)\bigg).

Proof.

Let us define,

I_{r}(k)\coloneqq\underset{A_{i}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r}}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\mathop{\rm vol}(A_{1},\cdots,A_{k})^{2}\log\left(\mathop{\rm vol}(A_{1},\cdots,A_{k})\right)\right)

V_{r}(k)\coloneqq\underset{A_{i}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r}}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\mathop{\rm vol}(A_{1},\cdots,A_{k})^{2}\right)

where the
A_{i}
’s are independent standard Gaussian random vectors in
{\mathbb{C}}^{r}
.

Observe that

\mathop{\rm vol}(A_{1},\cdots,A_{k})=\mathop{\rm vol}(A_{1},\cdots,A_{k-1})\|\Pi_{V_{k-1}^{\perp}}A_{k}\|,

(10)

where
V_{k-1}
is the subspace generated by
A_{1},\cdots,A_{k-1}
.
Now, using the independence of the random vector, and proceeding iteratively, it is easy to check that

\displaystyle I_{r}(k)=

\displaystyle I_{r}(k-1)\underset{v\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r-k+1}}{\mathbb{E}}\|v\|^{2}+V_{r}(k-1)\underset{v\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r-k+1}}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\|v\|^{2}\log\|v\|\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I_{r}(1)\prod_{i=r-k+1}^{r}\underset{v\in{\mathbb{C}}^{i}}{\mathbb{E}}\|v\|^{2}+\frac{\Gamma(r+1)}{\Gamma(r-k+1)}\sum_{i=r-k+1}^{r-1}\frac{1}{i}\underset{v\in{\mathbb{C}}^{i}}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\|v\|^{2}\log\|v\|\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(r+1)}{\Gamma(r-k+1)}\sum_{i=r-k+1}^{r}\frac{1}{i}\underset{v\in{\mathbb{C}}^{i}}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\|v\|^{2}\log\|v\|\right).

Using the iterative formula for
I_{r}(r)
, we get that the expectation of
|\det A|^{2}\log|\det A|
is equal to

\displaystyle\underset{A\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r\times r}}{\mathbb{E}}\left(|\det A|^{2}\log|\det A|\right)=\frac{r!}{2}\left((r+1)\psi(r+2)-r-\psi(2)\right).

∎

Corolary 2.7
.

Let
A
be a
r\times r
random complex matrix with standard Gaussian i.i.d. entries and
M
be a fixed
r\times r
matrix.

Then,
\underset{A\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r\times r}}{\mathbb{E}}\left(|\det AM|^{2}\log|\det AM|\right)
is equal to

|\det M|^{2}\frac{r!}{2}\bigg((r+1)\psi(r+2)-r-\psi(2)+\log|\det M|^{2}\bigg).

Proof.

Using the multiplicative nature of the determinant, the properties of the logarithm, and the linearity of the expectation, one gets that
\underset{A\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r\times r}}{\mathbb{E}}\left(|\det AM|^{2}\log|\det AM|\right)
is equal to

|\det M|^{2}\left(\underset{A\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r\times r}}{\mathbb{E}}\left(|\det A|^{2}\log|\det A|\right)+\log|\det M|\underset{A\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r\times r}}{\mathbb{E}}|\det A|^{2}\right).

Now observe that using the same recursive argument as in (
10
), one gets that
\displaystyle{\underset{A\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r\times r}}{\mathbb{E}}|\det A|^{2}=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mathbb{E}_{v_{i}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{i}}\|v_{i}\|^{2}}=r!
, where again
v_{i}
is a standard gaussian vector in
{\mathbb{C}}^{i}
. The result follows.
∎

2.2
Three little terms

We are now in conditions to compute the three terms of (
6
).

Let us focus now on the first and last terms, which follow from a straightforward computation.

Proposition 2.8
.

In the conditions of Theorem
1.1
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\sqrt{1+|z_{i}|^{2}}\right)=\frac{N}{2}.

Proof.

Observe that the function
F(z)
is in fact a
polygaf
(see for example Krishnapur
[
13
]
).
Then, following Krishnapur
[
13
, section 2.3]
, we have that the first intensity function is

\rho_{1}(z)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\Delta_{z}\log(K(z,z)),\qquad z\in{\mathbb{C}},

where
\frac{1}{4}\Delta_{z}u=\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial z\partial\overline{z}}
is the complex laplacian of
u
, and
K(z,w)=(1+z\overline{w})^{d}
is the covariance kernel of each polynomial in the entries of
A
.
Then,

\rho_{1}(z)=\frac{N}{\pi}\frac{1}{(1+|z|^{2})^{2}},\qquad z\in{\mathbb{C}}.

It follows,

\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\sqrt{1+|z_{i}|^{2}}\right)=\frac{N}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\log\sqrt{1+|z|^{2}}}{(1+|z|^{2})^{2}}d{\mathbb{C}}=\frac{N}{2},

where the last equality follows from taking polar coordinates.
∎

Remark 2.9
.

The previous result can be also proved by stating that the roots are invariant under the action of the unitary matrices (cf. Remark
1.2
).

Proposition 2.10
.

In the conditions of Theorem
1.1
, we have

\mathbb{E}(\log|\det(G_{d})|)=\frac{r(\psi(r+1)-1)}{2}.

Proof.

The result follows from recalling that
G_{d}
is an
r\times r
random matrix with i.i.d. standard Gaussian entries and applying Lemma
2.5
.
∎

Now we will focus on the second term of (
6
).

The random complex polynomial
F(z)
, can be seen as a stochastic process from
{\mathbb{C}}
to
{\mathbb{C}}
.
Applying the weighted Kac-Rice formula to this particular process (see for example
[
5
, Thm. 6.10]
or
[
2
, Thm. 6.1]
) one gets the following expresion,

\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log|F^{\prime}(z_{i})|\right)=\int_{z\in{\mathbb{C}}}\mathbb{E}\left(|F^{\prime}(z)|^{2}\log|F^{\prime}(z)|\bigg|F(z)=0\right)P_{F(z)}(0)dz,

(11)

where
P_{F(z)}(0)
is the density at
0
of the random variable
F(z)
, and
\mathbb{E}\left(\cdot\big|F(z)=0\right)
is the conditional expectation, conditioned on the event
\{F(z)=0\}
.

Let us define
A(z)\coloneqq\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{d}G_{i}{d\choose i}^{1/2}z^{i}
, where each
G_{i}
is a
r\times r
random matrix with i.i.d. standard Gaussian entries
\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(0,1)
.

As a direct application of Corollary
2.2
we get the following.

Proposition 2.11
.

In the conditions of Theorem
1.1
, the density
P_{F(z)}(0)
satisfies

P_{F(z)}(0)=\frac{1}{\pi(1+|z|^{2})^{N}\Gamma(r)}.

Proof.

It follows from the fact that
A(z)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r\times r}
is a random matrix with i.i.d. centered Gaussian entries with variance
(1+|z|^{2})^{d}
, and
F(z)=\det(A(z))
.
∎

Proposition 2.12
.

In the conditions of Theorem
1.1
, the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}\left(|F^{\prime}(z)|^{2}\log|F^{\prime}(z)|\big|F(z)=0\right)
is equal to

\frac{N(1+|z|^{2})^{N-2}\Gamma(r)}{2}\bigg((r+1)\psi(r+2)-r-\psi(2)+\log\left(d(1+|z|^{2})^{N-2}\right)\bigg).

Proof.

Taking
\varphi(A(z))=|F^{\prime}(z)|^{2}\log|F^{\prime}(z)|
, which is in fact invariant, and applying Lemma
2.4
, we have the following equality of conditional expectations,

\mathbb{E}\left(|F^{\prime}(z)|^{2}\log|F^{\prime}(z)|\big|F(z)=0\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(|F^{\prime}(z)|^{2}\log|F^{\prime}(z)|\big|A_{r}(z)=0\right)

(12)

where
A_{r}(z)
is the
r
-th column of
A(z)
.

Since
F(z)=\det(A(z))
, we have that
F^{\prime}(z)=\mathop{{\rm tr}}({\rm adj}\,(A(z))A^{\prime}(z))
, where
A^{\prime}(z)
is the derivative of
A(z)
.
Using the definition of the adjugate matrix, it follows from a straightforward computation that

\mathop{{\rm tr}}({\rm adj}\,(A(z))A^{\prime}(z))=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\det\left(A(z)\overset{i}{\leftarrow}A^{\prime}(z)\right)

where the matrix
\left(A(z)\overset{i}{\leftarrow}A^{\prime}(z)\right)
is obtained from
A(z)
replacing its
i
-th column with the
i
-th column of
A^{\prime}(z)
. If we expand this matrix in terms of its columns, we get the expression

\left(A(z)\overset{i}{\leftarrow}A^{\prime}(z)\right)=\left(A_{1}(z)\big|\cdots\big|A_{i-1}(z)\big|A^{\prime}_{i}(z)\big|A_{i+1}(z)\big|\cdots\big|A_{r}(z)\right).

In the case where
A_{r}(z)=0
, we get

F^{\prime}(z)=\det\left(A(z)\overset{r}{\leftarrow}A^{\prime}(z)\right)=\det\left(A_{1}(z)\big|\cdots\big|A_{r-1}(z)\big|A^{\prime}_{r}(z)\right).

In conclusion, the right-hand side of (
12
) is

\mathbb{E}\left(\left|\det\left(A(z)\overset{r}{\leftarrow}A^{\prime}(z)\right)\right|^{2}\log\left|\det\left(A(z)\overset{r}{\leftarrow}A^{\prime}(z)\right)\right|\big|A_{r}(z)=0\right).

Let us do a Gaussian regression, as in Proposition 1 of Wschebor
[
18
]
.
Write
A^{\prime}_{r}(z)=\eta(z)+\beta A_{r}(z)
such that
\eta(z)
is a Gaussian random vector independent of
A_{r}(z)
.

It is easy to check that
\mathbb{E}(\eta(z)\eta(z)^{*})=d(1+|z|^{2})^{d-2}{\rm Id}_{r}=\sigma_{\eta}^{2}{\rm Id}_{r}
and we have that the last conditional expectation is equal to,

\mathbb{E}\left(\left|\det\left(A_{1}(z)\big|\cdots\big|A_{r-1}(z)\big|\eta(z)\right)\right|^{2}\log\left|\det\left(A_{1}(z)\big|\cdots\big|A_{r-1}(z)\big|\eta(z)\right)\right|\right).

Lastly, the last expectation is equal to

\mathbb{E}\left(|\det AM|^{2}\log|\det AM|\right),

where
A
is a
r\times r
with i.i.d. complex standard Gaussian entries, and
M
is the diagonal matrix whose first
(r-1)
entries are
(1+|z|^{2})
the last one is
\sigma_{\eta}
.

The result follows from applying Corollary
2.7
.
∎

Now that we have computed successfully the density
P_{F(z)}(0)
and the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}\left(|F^{\prime}(z)|^{2}\log|F^{\prime}(z)|\bigg|F(z)=0\right)
, let us compute the remaining term of (

Proposition 2.13
.

In the conditions of Theorem
1.1
,

\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log|F^{\prime}(z_{i})|\right)=\frac{N}{2}\bigg(N+\log d+(r+1)\psi(r+2)-r-\psi(2)-2\bigg).

Proof.

Applying the Kac-Rice formula (
11
), we get that the left-hand side is equal to

\int_{z\in{\mathbb{C}}}\mathbb{E}\left(|F^{\prime}(z)|^{2}\log|F^{\prime}(z)|\bigg|F(z)=0\right)P_{F(z)}(0)dz.

Furthermore, applying Proposition
2.12
and Proposition
2.11
we have that it is equal to

\frac{N}{2\pi}\left(\int_{z\in{\mathbb{C}}}\frac{(r+1)\psi(r+2)-r-\psi(2)+\log d}{(1+|z|^{2})^{2}}dz+(N-2)\int_{z\in{\mathbb{C}}}\frac{\log(1+|z|^{2})}{(1+|z|^{2})^{2}}dz\right).

Then, the result follows from changing to polar coordinates.
∎

2.3
Proof of Main Theorem

Now, we will combine everything done up to now, to prove Theorem
1.1
.

Let
F(z)
be the complex polynomial of degree
N=rd
, defined as

F(z)=\det\left(\sum_{i=0}^{d}G_{i}{d\choose i}^{1/2}z^{i}\right),

where
G_{i}
are
r\times r
complex random matrices with i.i.d. standard Gaussian entries
\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(0,1)
.

We want to compute
\mathbb{E}(V(\hat{z}_{1},\cdots,\hat{z}_{N}))
, the expected value of the logarithmic energy of the projection of the roots of
F(z)
onto the Riemmann sphere.

Recall (
6
), we have that
\mathbb{E}(V(\hat{z}_{1},\cdots,\hat{z}_{N}))
is equal to

(N-1)\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\sqrt{1+|z_{i}|^{2}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log|F^{\prime}(z_{i})|\right)+\frac{N}{2}\mathbb{E}(\log|\det(G_{d})|),

which, thanks to the computations of Section
2.2
, is in turn equal to

\frac{(N-1)N}{2}-\frac{N}{4}\bigg(N+\log d+(r+1)\psi(r+2)-r-\psi(2)-2\bigg)+\frac{Nr(\psi(r+1)-1)}{4}.

In conclusion,

\mathbb{E}(V(\hat{z}_{1},\cdots,\hat{z}_{N}))=\frac{N^{2}}{4}-\frac{N\log d}{4}-\frac{N}{4}\bigg(1+\psi(r+1)-\psi(2)\bigg).

The result in
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
follows by substracting
\frac{N(N-1)}{2}\ln 2
, see (
5
).

∎

3
Discussion and experimental examples

As observed above, the PEVP ensemble contains the spherical ensemble
[
1
]
and the random polynomial roots
[
3
]
as particular examples. Remember that the constant for the linear term in the energy was
\left(\frac{\ln 2}{2}-\frac{\gamma}{4}\right)\approx 0.2022\ldots
for the spherical ensemble, and
-\frac{\kappa}{2}\approx 0.096\ldots
for the random polynomial roots. This means that the random polynomial roots are better distributed than the spherical ensemble in terms of the logarithmic energy.

In this section, we study the dependence on
d
of the logarithmic energy of the PEVP ensemble in order to see if for an intermediate
(d,r)
, the logarithmic energy lies between the two extremes described above. From Theorem
1.1
, we have that the expectation of the logarithmic energy is

{\mathbb{E}}(V(x_{1},\cdots,x_{N}))=\frac{\kappa}{2}N^{2}-\frac{N\log d}{4}-\frac{N}{4}\bigg(1+\psi(r+1)-\psi(2)-2\ln 2\bigg).

Now, it is well known that

\psi(r+1)=\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}(r+1)}{\Gamma(r+1)}=-\gamma+\sum_{j=1}^{r}\frac{1}{j},

(13)

and using the Euler-Maclaurin formula as in
[
1
]
we have

\sum_{j=1}^{r}\frac{1}{j}=\ln r+\gamma+\frac{1}{2r}-\frac{1}{12r^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{r^{4}}\right).

(14)

Combining (
13
) and (
14
) we have

\psi(r+1)=\ln r+\frac{1}{2r}-\frac{1}{12r^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{r^{4}}\right).

(15)

Using (
15
) and the equality
\gamma=1-\psi(2)
in the logarithmic energy expression, we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(V(x_{1},\cdots,x_{N}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa}{2}N^{2}-\frac{N\ln N}{4}+N\left(\frac{\ln 2}{2}-\frac{\gamma}{4}\right)-\frac{d}{8}+\frac{d^{2}}{48N}+NO\left(\frac{1}{r^{4}}\right).

Let us make two observations. Firstly, for
d=1
we recover the expression for the logarithmic energy of the spherical ensemble. Secondly, for a given
N
, the logarithmic energy of the PEVP ensemble decreases linearly with
d
. Indeed, the term
\frac{d^{2}}{48N}
is comparable with
\frac{d}{8}
only for
d\geq 6N
, which is obviously not the case if
d
is a factor of
N
.

In what follows, we present some experimental results which illustrate this dependence with
d
, and the comparison of the computed expected value of the logarithmic energy with the empirical values.

We take two values for the number of points,
N=60
and
N=120
, which have several divisors, namely
12
and
16
, respectively. For each
N
and for each pair
(d,r)
such that
N=dr
, we sort random matrices and solve numerically the corresponding PEVP. The obtained eigenvalues are projected to
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
, and we compute the resulting logarithmic energy. This process is repeated
100.000
times for each pair
(d,n)
.

In Figure
1
, we present a violin plot (a kernel density estimation) for each
d
, on top of the expected value computed in Theorem
1.1
. Notice that for
d=N
, the logarithmic energy corresponds to the roots of random polynomials
[
3
]
.

In Figure
2
, we show, with the same experimental data, the linear dependence of the logarithmic energy on
d
.

In Figure
3
, we show the difference between the expected value computed in Theorem
1.1
and the density estimated in the
100.000
problem samples for each
d
. Observe that the distributions are centered at zero, and the variance seems to decrease with
d
.
