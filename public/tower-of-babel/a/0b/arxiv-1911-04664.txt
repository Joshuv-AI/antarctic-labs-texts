Quantum Even-Dimensional Balls
Colin MacLaurin

Abstract
. The quantum even-dimensional balls are defined as the C ∗ C^{*} -algebras generated by certain graphs. We exhibit a polynomial algebra for each even-dimensional quantum ball, and classify the irreducible representations of it.

1.
Preliminaries

1.1.
C^{*}
-algebras

1.1.1.
Preliminaries

A
C^{*}
-algebra
is a Banach
*
-algebra
A
which satisfies the
C^{*}
-identity

\left\lVert a^{*}a\right\rVert=\left\lVert a\right\rVert^{2}\,\forall a\in A.

For example, the set
B(\mathcal{H})
of bounded operators on a Hilbert space is a
C^{*}
-algebra, as is the set
C(X)
of continuous
\mathbb{C}
-valued functions on a compact Hausdorff topological space
X
.

The
Gelfand theorem
states that the category of Hausdorff topological spaces with continuous functions is dual to the category of abelian unital
C^{*}
-algebras with
*
-homomorphisms between them.

1.1.2.
Representations

A
*
-representation
of a
C^{*}
-algebra
A
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is a
*
-homomorphism
\pi\colon A\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. We say
\pi
is
nondegenerate
if

\left\{\pi(a)h\colon a\in A,h\in\mathcal{H}\right\}

is dense in
\mathcal{H}
. By representation we will mean a nondegenerate
*
-representation.

We say
\pi
is
faithful
if
\ker(\pi)=\left\{0\right\}
. Also
\pi
is
irreducible
if there are no nontrivial invariant subspaces of
\mathcal{H}
, which is a stronger condition than non-degeneracy. Suppose
\pi\colon A\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{1})
and
\rho\colon A\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2})
are two representations of
A
. We say
\pi
is
unitarily equivalent
to
\rho
if there exists a unitary operator
u\colon\mathcal{H}_{1}\to\mathcal{H}_{2}
s.t.
\rho(a)u=u\pi(a)\,\forall a\in A
.

Suppose we have an exact sequence
0\to J\to A\xrightarrow{\pi}B\to 0
where
J
is an ideal of
A
. Every representation of
J
or
B
extends to a representation of
A
as follows. Suppose
\rho\colon B\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is a representation of
B
. Then we can compose with the quotient map
\pi
to get a representation
\rho\circ\pi\colon A\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
of
A
. On the other hand, suppose
\rho\colon J\to\mathcal{B}(H_{i})
is a representation of
J
. Then
\left\{\rho(x)h\colon x\in J,h\in H\right\}
is dense in
\mathcal{H}
, so any element of
\mathcal{H}
can be approximated by
\rho(x)h
. We can extend
\rho
to a representation of
A
by defining

\rho(a)\left(\rho(x)h\right):=\rho(ax).

Moreover, irreducible representations extend, by this procedure, to irreducible ones. It is a theorem that these are all the irreducible representations of
A
:

\Irr(A)=\Irr(J)\sqcup\Irr(B),

where
\sqcup
means disjoint union.
If the ideal
J
is essential, then a faithful representation on
J
extends to a faithful representation on
A
. A theorem due to Gelfand-Naimark states that every
C^{*}
-algebra has a faithful nondegenerate representation.

1.1.3.
Projections and Isometries

A
projection
in a
C^{*}
-algebra is an element
p
satisfying
p=p^{*}=p^{2}
. In any representation
\pi
,
\pi(p)
is the orthogonal projection onto a subspace. We say an element
v
is an
isometry
if
v^{*}v=1
. In any representation
\pi
,
\left\lVert\pi(v)h\right\rVert=\left\lVert h\right\rVert\,\forall h
. A
proper isometry
is an isometry which is not unitary.

A
partial isometry
is an element
s
such that
s^{*}s
is a projection. Then
ss^{*}
is also a projection and
s=ss^{*}s
. We say
s
has
domain projection

s^{*}s
and
range projection

ss^{*}
. A
partial unitary
is a partial isometry for which the domain and range projections coincide.

1.1.4.
Matrix Units and
\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{H})

A
system of matrix units
indexed by a set
X
is a collection
\left\{u_{ij}\colon i,j\in X\right\}
of linearly independent partial isometries in a
C^{*}
-algebra which satisfy the relations

\centerdot

u_{ij}u_{mn}=\begin{cases}u_{in}&\text{if }j=m,\\
0&\text{if }j\neq m.\end{cases}

\centerdot

u_{ij}^{*}=u_{ji}
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be an infinite-dimensional separable Hilbert space, and
R
be the set of finite-rank operators on
\mathcal{H}
. Then the set of
compact operators

\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{H})
is defined to be the norm-closure of
R
.
\mathcal{K}
has only one irreducible representation up to unitary equivalence.

It can be shown that the closed span of a countably infinite system of matrix units in a
C^{*}
-algebra is isomorphic to
\mathcal{K}
.

1.1.5.
Polar Decomposition

Recall that we can write any complex number
a+bi
in polar form
re^{i\theta}
, where
r\in\mathbb{R}
is positive and
\left\lvert e^{i\theta}\right\rvert=1
. There is an analogous procedure for operators on a Hilbert space:

Proposition 1.1
.

Every operator
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
has a
polar decomposition

T=V\left\lvert T\right\rvert,

where
\left\lvert T\right\rvert:=(T^{*}T)^{1/2}
is positive and
V
is a partial isometry. If we require that
V^{*}V=(kerT)^{\perp}
for the domain projection of
V
, then this decomposition is unique. If
T
is invertible then
V
is unitary.

Note that
T^{*}T
is positive, so its spectrum is contained in
[0,\infty)
. So we can apply the functional calculus to the function
\sqrt{\cdot}
which is well defined on
[0,\infty)
. However we can’t always find a polar decomposition of an element in a general
C^{*}
-algebra. A sufficient condition that an element
x
of a unital
C^{*}
-algebra
A
has a polar decomposition is that
\left\lvert x\right\rvert
is invertible, in which case

x=u\left\lvert x\right\rvert,\qquad\text{where }u:=x\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{-1}.

More generally, suppose that
x=pxp
where
p\in A
is a projection. Then
x
belongs to the
C^{*}
-subalgebra
pAp
, which is a unital
C^{*}
-algebra with unit
p
. If
\left\lvert x\right\rvert
is invertible in
pAp
, then
x
has a polar decomposition
x=\alpha\left\lvert x\right\rvert
where the phase
\alpha
satisfies
\alpha^{*}\alpha=p
. In the examples we will see, we will also have
\alpha\alpha^{*}<p
.

When an element
x
of a unital
C^{*}
-algebra has a polar decomposition
x=u\left\lvert x\right\rvert
we say
\left\lvert x\right\rvert
is the
modulus
of
x
and
u
is the
phase
of
x
, by analogy with the complex numbers.

1.1.6.
The Busby invariant

The Busby invariant combines most of the information in an extension
0\to A\to B\to C\to 0
into a single morphism.

A
unitisation
of a
C^{*}
-algebra
A
is a unital
C^{*}
-algebra in which
A
can be embedded as an essential ideal. The smallest unitisation of
A
is written
A^{\sim}
, and the largest unitisation is called the
multiplier algebra

\mathcal{M}(A)
. For example,
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is the multiplier algebra for
\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{H})
.

The
corona algebra
of
A
is the
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{M}(A)/A
. The corona algebra is a generalisation of the Calkin algebra
\mathcal{B}/\mathcal{K}
.

The
Busby invariant
for an extension
E\colon 0\to A\xrightarrow{\alpha}B\xrightarrow{\beta}C\to 0
is the morphism
\tau_{E}\colon C\to\mathcal{M}(A)/A
defined by

\tau_{E}(c):=\pi\circ\sigma(b),

where
b\in B
is a lift of
c
through
\beta
,
\pi
is the quotient map
\colon\mathcal{M}(A)\to\mathcal{M}(A)/A
, and
\sigma
is the unique
*
-homomorphism
\colon B\to\mathcal{M}(A)
which makes the following diagram commutative:

A

B

M(A)

1.1.7.
Universal
C^{*}
-algebras

[
B
]

A universal
C^{*}
-algebra is defined on a set
G
of generators and a set
R
of relations. A representation of
(G,R)
is a set of operators on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
which satisfy the relations. We say that
(G,R)
is
admissible
if there exists a representation, and the representations sum together nicely. If
(G,R)
is admissible then

\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{0}:=\sup\left\{\left\lVert\rho(z)\right\rVert\colon\rho\text{ a representation of $(G,R)$}\right\}

is a well-defined finite number, and
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{0}
is a
C^{*}
-semiform. Then
J_{0}:=\left\{z\colon\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{0}=0\right\}
is a two-sided ideal of
\mathcal{F}(G)
, and the completion of the quotient
\mathcal{F}(G)/J_{0}
under
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{0}
is called the
universal
C^{*}
-algebra of
(G,R)
.

It has the property that there is a surjective homomorphism from the universal algebra to any given
C^{*}
-algebra with generators which satisfy the relations.

Examples
.

The quantum disc is the universal
C^{*}
-algebra generated by the relation
x^{*}x-qxx^{*}=(1-q)1
. Also a graph algebra is a universal
C^{*}
-algebra with generators and relations determined from a graph. We will see both of these examples shortly.

Universal algebras have convenient properties for showing isomorphism. Suppose we have two universal
C^{*}
-algebras
A
and
B
. It is enough to find maps
\phi\colon A\to B
and
\psi\colon B\to A
defined on generators which preserve the relations (so they extend to homomorphisms), composition gives the identity i.e.
\phi\circ\psi=\id_{B}
,
\psi\circ\phi=\id_{A}
, and such that the images of the generators are nonzero. Then
\phi
and
\psi
are isomorphisms.

1.2.
Graph Algebras

[
R
]

1.2.1.
Definitions

A
directed graph

E=(E^{0},E^{1},r,s)
consists of countable sets
E^{0}
and
E^{1}
of
vertices
and
edges
respectively, and maps
r,s\colon E^{1}\to E^{0}
describing the
range
and
source
of the edges. We represent a graph by putting points for vertices and arrows for the edges connecting the vertices.

Example
.

E^{0}=\left\{v,w\right\}
,
E^{1}=\left\{b,e\right\}
in the following graph:

\textstyle{v}
\scriptstyle{b}
\textstyle{w}
\scriptstyle{e}

Then
r(b)=v
,
r(e)=w
and
s(b)=s(e)=v
.

A
sink
is a vertex which emits no edges, i.e.
s^{-1}(v)=\emptyset
, whereas a
source
is a vertex which receives no edges, i.e.
r^{-1}(v)=\emptyset
. We will consider
row-finite graphs
, in which every vertex emits at most finitely many edges.

Definition
.

Let
E
be a row-finite directed graph. A
Cuntz-Krieger
E
-family
consists of families of projections
\left\{P_{v}\colon v\in E^{0}\right\}
and partial isometries
\left\{S_{e}\colon e\in E^{1}\right\}
on some Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
s.t.

(G1)

P_{v}P_{w}=0
for
v,w\in E^{0}
with
v\neq w

(G2)

S_{e}^{*}S_{e}=P_{r(e)}
for
e\in E^{1}

(G3)

P_{v}=\sum_{e\in E^{1}\colon s(e)=v}S_{e}S_{e}^{*}
for
v\in E^{0}
, provided
v
is not a sink.

The relation (G1) says that the projections are mutually orthogonal. The relation (G3) is known as the Cuntz-Krieger relation.

Proposition 1.2
.

For any row-finite directed graph
E
, there is a
C^{*}
-algebra
C^{*}(E)
generated by a Cuntz-Krieger
E
-family
\left\{P_{v},S_{e}\right\}
s.t. for every other Cuntz-Krieger
E
-family
\left\{Q_{v},R_{e}\right\}
in a
C^{*}
-algebra
B
, there is a surjective
*
-homomorphism
\pi\colon C^{*}(E)

\to B
s.t.
\pi(P_{v})=Q_{v}
and
\pi(S_{e})=R_{e}
for all
v\in E^{0}
,
e\in E^{1}
.

In other words,
C^{*}(E)
is the
universal

C^{*}
-algebra for these relations.
C^{*}(E)
is called the
C^{*}
-algebra of
E
, and is described as a
graph
C^{*}
-algebra
. It is also possible to generalise to the case of graphs which are not row-finite
[
FLR
]
.

There is a continuous action
\gamma\colon S^{1}\to\Aut(C^{*}(E))
called the
gauge action
which satisfies
\gamma_{t}(s_{e})=ts_{e}
,
\gamma_{t}(p_{v})=p_{v}
. We say an ideal
J
of
C^{*}(E)
is
gauge-invariant
if
\gamma_{t}J=J
for all
t\in S^{1}
. Existence of the gauge action is equivalent to universality of the
C^{*}
-algebra
C^{*}(E)
.

1.2.2.
Paths in
E

A
path
in
E
is a sequence of edges
\mu=(\mu_{1},\dotsc,\mu_{n})
s.t.
r(\mu_{i})=s(\mu_{i+1})
for all
i
.
E^{*}
is the set of all finite paths in
E
, including the zero-length paths, which are just vertices. If we define
S_{\mu}:=S_{\mu_{1}}S_{\mu_{2}}\dotsb S_{\mu_{n}}
, we see that
S_{\mu}\neq 0
only if
\mu
is a path, in which case
S_{\mu}
is a partial isometry.

Proposition 1.3
.

C^{*}(E)=\overline{\operatorname{span}}\left\{S_{\mu}S_{\nu}^{*}\colon\mu,\nu\in E^{*},\,r(\mu)=r(\nu)\right\}
.

1.2.3.
Ideals

For
v,w\in E^{0}
, we write
v\geq w
if there is a path from
v
to
w
. We say a subset
H
of
E^{0}
is

\centerdot

hereditary
if
w\in H
,
w\geq v

\Rightarrow

v\in H
,

\centerdot

saturated
if for
v\in E^{0}
not a sink,
\left\{r(e)\colon s(e)=v\right\}\subset H

\Rightarrow

v\in H
.

For a row-finite graph
E
the gauge-invariant ideals in
C^{*}(E)
are in a one-to-one correspondence with the saturated hereditary sets of
E^{0}
. If
I
is an ideal in
C^{*}(E)
then the set
H_{I}:=\left\{v\in E^{0}\colon P_{v}\in I\right\}
is hereditary and saturated. Conversely, if
H\subset E^{0}
is hereditary and saturated, then the ideal
I_{H}
generated by
\left\{P_{v}\colon v\in H\right\}
is gauge-invariant.

Furthermore, the quotient
C^{*}(E)/I_{H}
is the graph algebra
C^{*}(E\backslash H)
, where
I_{H}
is the ideal corresponding to some hereditary and saturated set
H
.
E\backslash H
is the graph obtained by removing all vertices in
H
and all edges with source or range in
H
.

1.3.
Toeplitz Algebra

1.3.1.
Definition

Recall
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
is the Hilbert space of square-summable sequences, where it will be assumed that
\mathbb{N}
includes
0
. Let
\left\{\zeta_{0},\zeta_{1},\dotsc\right\}
be the standard orthonormal basis of
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
. The
shift operator
or
unilateral shift

S
is defined on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
by

S\zeta_{i}:=\zeta_{i+1}.

S
is a bounded linear operator with adjoint

S^{*}\zeta_{i}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }i=0\\
\zeta_{i-1}&\text{if }i\geq 1.\end{cases}

The shift operator is a proper isometry, since
S^{*}S=1
but
SS^{*}\neq 1
.

Definition
.

The
Toeplitz algebra
is the
C^{*}
-algebra generated by the unilateral shift:

\mathcal{T}:=C^{*}(S).

Coburn’s Uniqueness Theorem
states that if
u\in A
,
v\in B
are proper isometries in
C^{*}
-algebras
A
and
B
then there is an isomorphism
\phi\colon C^{*}(u)\to C^{*}(v)
s.t.
\phi(u)=v
. In particular, any
C^{*}
-algebra generated by a proper isometry is isomorphic to
\mathcal{T}
.

\mathcal{K}
is an ideal of
\mathcal{T}
generated by
1-SS^{*}
, and
\mathcal{T}
fits into the short exact sequence

1.4.
Quantum Disc

[
KL
,
HMS
,
HMS2
,
CM
]

Definition
.

The
polynomial algebra
of the
quantum disc
is the
*
-algebra

P(\mathbb{D}_{q}):=\mathbb{C}\langle x,x^{*}\rangle/J_{q},\qquad 0<q<1,

where
\mathbb{C}\langle x,x^{*}\rangle
is the unital free
*
-algebra generated by
x
and
x^{*}
, and
J_{q}
is the
*
-ideal generated by the relation

x^{*}x-qxx^{*}=(1-q)1.

(1)

Every
*
-representation
\pi\colon P(\mathbb{D}_{q})\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
satisfies
\left\lVert\pi(x)\right\rVert=1
, since

\left\lVert\pi(x)\right\rVert^{2}=\left\lVert\pi(x)^{*}\pi(x)\right\rVert=\left\lVert q\pi(x)\pi(x)^{*}+(1-q)1\right\rVert=q\left\lVert\pi(x)\right\rVert^{2}+(1-q),

so
\left\lVert\pi(x)\right\rVert^{2}=1
and hence
\left\lVert\pi(x)\right\rVert=1
. Hence
\sup_{\pi}\left\lVert\pi(a)\right\rVert<\infty
for all
a\in P(\mathbb{D}_{q})
, and so we can define

\left\lVert a\right\rVert:=\sup_{\pi}\left\lVert\pi(a)\right\rVert.

This is a
C^{*}
-algebraic norm, but it is not complete.

Definition
.

The
C^{*}
-algebra

C(\mathbb{D}_{q})
of the
quantum disc
, for
0<q<1
, is the
C^{*}
-completion of
P(\mathbb{D}_{q})
.

This completion is the universal
C^{*}
-algebra generated by the relation
x^{*}x-qxx^{*}=(1-q)1
.

Proposition 1.4
.

Every irreducible
*
-representation of
C(\mathbb{D}_{q})
is unitarily equivalent to one of the following representations:

(1)

a one-dimensional representation
\rho_{\theta}
defined by
\rho_{\theta}(x)=e^{i\theta}
,
\rho_{\theta}(x^{*})=e^{-i\theta}
, for
0\leq\theta<2\pi
, or

(2)

an infinite-dimensional representation
\pi_{q}
defined on a Hilbert space with orthonormal basis
\left\{e_{i}\right\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
by

\displaystyle\pi_{q}(x)e_{i}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-q^{i+1}}e_{i+1},\quad i\geq 0,

\displaystyle\pi_{q}(x^{*})e_{i}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&i=0,\\
\sqrt{1-q^{i}}e_{i-1}&i\geq 1.\end{cases}

The infinite-dimensional representation
\pi_{q}
is faithful. The one-dimensional representations correspond to the classical points, forming a circle, of the quantum disc. If we think of
C(S^{1})
as the
C^{*}
-algebra generated by
a
and
a^{*}
subject to the relations
aa^{*}=a^{*}a=1
, then this classical circle can be embedded into the quantum disc via the homomorphism
\phi_{q}\colon C(\mathbb{D}_{q})\to C(S^{1})
defined by
x\mapsto a
.

There are also unbounded representations of
C(\mathbb{D}_{q})
. For
0<q<1
,
C(\mathbb{D}_{q})
is isomorphic to the Toeplitz algebra
\mathcal{T}
, as shown next.

1.4.1.
Isomorphism to the Toeplitz Algebra

Define an operator
z
on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
by:

z:=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\sqrt{1-q^{k+1}}-\sqrt{1-q^{k}}\right)S^{k+1}(S^{*})^{k},

(2)

with the convention
(S^{*})^{0}=1
. This sum converges since the sequence of partial sums
S_{n}
is Cauchy. For
n>m
,

\left\lVert S_{n}-S_{m}\right\rVert=\left\lVert\sum_{k=m+1}^{n}\lambda_{k}S^{k+1}(S^{*})^{k}\right\rVert\leq\sum_{k=m+1}^{n}\lambda_{k}=\sqrt{1-q^{n+1}}-\sqrt{1-q^{m+1}},

where
\lambda_{k}:=\sqrt{1-q^{k+1}}-\sqrt{1-q^{k}}
. As
n,m\to\infty
,
\sqrt{1-q^{n+1}}-\sqrt{1-q^{m+1}}\to 0
, and so the sum converges. Clearly
z\in\mathcal{T}
. To see how
z
acts on the basis elements
\zeta_{i}
, we evaluate

\displaystyle(S^{*})^{k}\zeta_{i}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }i<k\\
\zeta_{i-k}&\text{if }i\geq k,\end{cases}

\displaystyle S^{k+1}\zeta_{i}

\displaystyle=\zeta_{i+k+1},

and so

S^{k+1}(S^{*})^{k}\zeta_{i}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }i<k\\
\zeta_{i+1}&\text{if }i\geq k.\end{cases}

Hence in the sum defining
z(\zeta_{i})
, the terms are all zero for
k>i
. Hence

z(\zeta_{i})=\sum_{k=0}^{i}\lambda_{k}S^{k+1}(S^{*})^{k}\zeta_{i}=\sqrt{1-q^{i+1}}\zeta_{i+1},

since
\sum_{k=0}^{i}\lambda_{k}=\sqrt{1-q^{i+1}}
. So
z
is a
weighted shift
and has adjoint

z^{*}(\zeta_{i})=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }i=0,\\
\sqrt{1-q^{i}}\zeta_{i-1}&\text{if }i\geq 1.\end{cases}

Thus one may show that

z^{*}z-qzz^{*}=(1-q)1,

which is the defining relation for the quantum disc. Also note that we can recover
S
from
z
by considering the polar decomposition of
z
. The operator
\left\lvert z\right\rvert=(z^{*}z)^{1/2}
satisfies
\left\lvert z\right\rvert(\zeta_{i})=\sqrt{1-q^{i+1}}\zeta_{i}
which is invertible, so
z
has a unique polar decomposition with phase

z\left\lvert z\right\rvert^{-1}\colon\zeta_{i}\overset{\left\lvert z\right\rvert^{-1}}{\mapsto}(1-q^{i+1})^{-1/2}\zeta_{i}\overset{z}{\mapsto}\zeta_{i+1},

which is just
S
. In particular we can recover
S
from
z
, and so
C^{*}(S)=C^{*}(z)
.

To show
\mathcal{T}\cong C(\mathbb{D}_{q})
, define maps

\textstyle{C^{*}(S)}
\textstyle{C(\mathbb{D}_{q})}
\scriptstyle{\phi}
\scriptstyle{\psi}

by
\phi(S):=x\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{-1}
and
\psi(x):=z
on generators. To show these maps extend to homomorphisms we must show they preserve the relations. We have already shown that
\psi
preserves the quantum relation. To see that
x\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{-1}
is a partial isometry, consider the faithful representation
\pi
of
C(\mathbb{D}_{q})
above. By Proposition
1.4
,
\pi(x)e_{i}=\sqrt{1-q^{i+1}}e_{i+1}
, so by the same working as for
z
we have
\pi(x\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{-1})e_{i}=e_{i+1}
and this operator is a partial isometry.

Now
\psi\circ\phi=\id
since
(\psi\circ\phi)(S)=\psi(x\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{-1})=\psi(x)\left\lvert\psi(x)\right\rvert^{-1}=z\left\lvert z\right\rvert^{-1}=S
. Also
(\phi\circ\psi)(x)=\phi(z)=\phi(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}S^{k+1}(S^{*})^{k})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}(x\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{-1})^{k+1}(x\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{-1})^{*k}=x
, which can be seen by computing in the representation
\pi
and repeating calculations similar to those above. Hence
\phi\circ\psi=\id_{C(\mathbb{D}_{q})}
and
\psi\circ\phi=\id_{\mathcal{T}}
, which implies that
\phi
and
\psi
are injective and surjective and hence isomorphisms. This demonstrates the convenience of using universal properties to show isomorphism.

We will use a similar procedure in the case of

.

(1)

Follows from the definition.

(2)

Notice that the hereditary saturated subsets of

Lemma 3.3
.

C^{*}(E_{n})=C^{*}(S_{1},\dotsc,S_{n})
.

Proof.

By the Cuntz-Krieger relations,

S_{i}^{*}S_{i}=S_{i0}^{*}S_{i0}+\dotsb+S_{ii}^{*}S_{ii}=P_{0}+\dotsb+P_{i}.

Also

S_{i}S_{i}^{*}=\sum_{j,k=0}^{i}S_{ij}S_{ik}^{*}=\sum_{j=0}^{i}S_{ij}S_{ij}^{*}=P_{i}

for
i=1,\dotsc,n
and we also have
P_{0}=(P_{0}+P_{1})-P_{1}=S_{1}^{*}S_{1}-S_{1}S_{1}^{*}
. So we can recover the projections. Then

S_{ij}=S_{ij}P_{j}=S_{i}P_{j},

so we can recover the partial isometries as well.
∎

The above results show that
S_{i}
is a partial isometry for

and that
S_{n}
is a proper isometry. Now define

by

We again make computations in a representation

ending at

on

adds the edge

, if possible.

3.1.1.
Paths in
E_{n}

We can list all the finite paths in
E_{n}
ending at
v_{0}
by a recursive procedure. Let
\Gamma_{i}
be the set of such paths starting at
v_{i}
:

\Gamma_{i}:=\left\{\mu\in E_{n}^{*}\colon s(\mu)=v_{i},\,r(\mu)=v_{0}\right\}.

Then
\Gamma_{n}:=\bigcup_{i=0}^{n}\Gamma_{i}
is the set of all paths ending at
v_{0}
. There is only one such path starting at
v_{0}
, the
0
-length path consisting of the vertex only, so
\Gamma_{0}=\left\{v_{0}\right\}
. Paths starting at
v_{1}
have the form
e_{11}^{m_{1}}e_{10}\in\Gamma_{1}
for some
m_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\mathbb{N}
includes
0
. To list the set of all paths starting at
v_{2}
, note that such paths have the form
e_{22}^{m_{2}}e_{20}
or
e_{22}^{m_{2}}e_{10}\Gamma_{1}
, where the operation is concatenation, i.e. follow
e_{22}^{m_{2}}e_{10}
by any path starting at
v_{1}
. In general,

\Gamma_{i}=\bigcup_{j=0}^{i-1}e_{ii}^{m_{i}}e_{ij}\Gamma_{j},

where
m_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
.

We can categorise the paths as follows. Say two paths are equivalent if they differ only by the number of loops around a vertex, that is, once the loops are removed the paths are identical. The number of equivalence classes of paths starting from
v_{i}
for
i\geq 1
is
2^{i-1}
. Hence the total number of classes of paths is
2^{n}
.

3.1.2.
Representation
\pi
of
C^{*}(E)

We know
\pi(S_{ij})
acts by adding the edge
e_{ij}
, if possible. So
\pi(S_{ij})
is nonzero precisely on paths which start at
v_{j}
. Such paths have the form
e_{jj}^{m_{j}}\cdots
and

\pi(S_{ij})\zeta_{e_{jj}^{m_{j}}\cdots}=\begin{cases}\zeta_{e_{ii}^{m_{i}+1}\cdots}&\text{if }i=j\\
\zeta_{e_{ii}^{0}e_{ij}e_{jj}^{m_{j}}\cdots}&\text{if }i\neq j.\end{cases}

Now
\pi(S_{i})
acts by adding an edge from the set
\left\{e_{ij}\colon j\leq i\right\}
. There is at most one such edge from this set which can be added to a path. In fact
\pi(S_{i})
is nonzero precisely on paths starting at
v_{j}
where
j\leq i
, in which case

\pi(S_{i})\zeta_{e_{jj}^{m_{j}}\cdots}=\begin{cases}\zeta_{e_{ii}^{m_{i}+1}\cdots}&\text{if }i=j\\
\zeta_{e_{ii}^{0}e_{ij}e_{jj}^{m_{j}}\cdots}&\text{if }i>j.\end{cases}

So

\pi(S_{i})^{k+1}\zeta_{e_{jj}^{m_{j}}\cdots}=\begin{cases}\zeta_{e_{ii}^{m_{i}+k+1}\cdots}&\text{if }i=j\\
\zeta_{e_{ii}^{k}e_{ij}e_{jj}^{m_{j}}\cdots}&\text{if }i>j.\end{cases}

acts by removing an edge starting at
v_{i}
, if possible. Hence it is nonzero precisely on paths

starting at
v_{i}
, where

is the tail of the path, and then

\pi(S_{i})^{*}\zeta_{e_{ii}^{m_{i}}e_{ij}\mu}=\begin{cases}\zeta_{\mu}&\text{if }m_{i}=0\\

and on all other basis elements. So the composition satisfies

will be finite on any given basis element

.

Separating the

, we have

and so

\sqrt{1-q^{m_{i}+2}}\zeta_{e_{ii}^{m_{i}+1}\cdots}&\text{if }j=i\\

by similar calculations to those done for

. Hence the adjoint
\pi(z_{i})^{*}
is nonzero only on paths starting with

, and satisfies

\pi(z_{i})^{*}\zeta_{e_{ii}^{m_{i}}\mu}=\begin{cases}\sqrt{1-q}\zeta_{\mu}&\text{if }m_{i}=0\\
\sqrt{1-q^{m_{i}+1}}\zeta_{e_{ii}^{m_{i}-1}\mu}&\text{if }m_{i}\geq 1,\end{cases}

on such paths, where
\mu
is the tail of the path. One may then show the results

\displaystyle x_{i}x_{j}

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{for }i<j

(15)

\displaystyle x_{i}^{*}x_{j}

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{for }i\neq j

(16)

\displaystyle x_{i}^{*}x_{i}-qx_{i}x_{i}^{*}

\displaystyle=(1-q)(P_{0}+P_{1}+\dotsb+P_{i})

(17)

where the last relation follows from:

\pi(x_{i}^{*}x_{i})\zeta_{e_{jj}^{m_{j}}\cdots}=\begin{cases}(1-q^{m_{i}+2})\zeta_{e_{ii}^{m_{i}}\cdots}&\text{if }i=j\\

and

Intuitively,

for

since
\pi(x_{j})
adds an edge starting from
v_{j}
, if possible. But then we cannot add on an edge starting from
v_{i}
, as there is no path from
v_{i}
to
v_{j}
if

. Intuitively,

for

since

adds an edge starting from
v_{j}

subtracts an edge starting from

.

Lemma 3.4
.

C^{*}(E_{n})=C^{*}(z_{1},\dotsc,z_{n})
.

Proof.

We will show that

\left\lvert z_{n}\right\rvert
is invertible, so
z_{n}
has a unique polar decomposition with phase

which we can readily show is
S_{n}
by computing in the representation. For
i\leq n-1
,
\left\lvert z_{i}\right\rvert
is invertible in the unital

-subalgebra
(P_{0}+\dotsb+P_{i})C^{*}(E)(P_{0}+\dotsb+P_{i})
. Write

for the inverse of
\left\lvert z_{i}\right\rvert
in the

. Calculating using the representation, we may show that this phase is

. So we can recover

from

.

(\psi\circ\phi)(S_{i})=S_{i}
and so
\psi\circ\phi=\id

\psi\circ\phi=\id_{C^{*}(E_{2})}

\phi\circ\psi=\id_{C^{*}(T_{1},T_{2})}

∎

C^{*}(S_{1},\dotsc,S_{n})

We can also regard
C^{*}(S_{1},\dotsc,S_{n})
as a universal algebra generated by
S_{1},\dotsc,S_{n}
with certain relations between them.

Definition
.

Let
C^{*}(T_{1},\dotsc,T_{n})
be the universal
C^{*}
-algebra generated by
T_{1},\dotsc,T_{n}
s.t.

\displaystyle T_{i}T_{j}

\displaystyle=0\qquad\text{if }i<j

\displaystyle T_{i}^{*}T_{j}

\displaystyle=0\qquad\text{if }i\neq j

\displaystyle T_{n}^{*}T_{n}

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle T_{i-1}^{*}T_{i-1}

\displaystyle=T_{i}^{*}T_{i}-T_{i}T_{i}^{*},\qquad i=2,\dotsc,n

\displaystyle T_{1}T_{1}^{*}

\displaystyle<T_{1}^{*}T_{1}.

The relations imply that the
T_{i}
are partial isometries, and that
T_{n}
is a proper isometry. Also we must have
T_{i}T_{i}^{*}<T_{i}^{*}T_{i}
since
\pi\colon C^{*}(E_{2})\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is a representation of these relations in which
T_{i}\neq 0
.

Theorem 5.1
.

C^{*}(S_{1},\dotsc,S_{n})\cong C^{*}(T_{1},\dotsc,T_{n})
.

Proof.

As before we may exhibit isomorphisms

\textstyle{C^{*}(E_{n})}
\textstyle{\qquad\quad\;C^{*}(T_{1},\dotsc,T_{n})}
\scriptstyle{\phi}
\scriptstyle{\psi}

\phi

3.3

\psi(T_{i}):=S_{i}

i=1,\dotsc,n

\phi(S_{i})=T_{i}
for all

\phi\circ\psi=\id
and
\psi\circ\phi=\id
, and so we have isomorphism.
∎
