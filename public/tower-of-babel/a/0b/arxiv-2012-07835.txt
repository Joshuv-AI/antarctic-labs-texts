The Liouville line element and the energy of the diagonals
Cǎlin–Şerban Bǎrbat

Abstract
. In this work I show that in each rectangle formed by the parameter curves on a Liouville surface the energies of the main diagonals are equal. This result extends naturally to n n -dimensional Liouville manifolds.



1.
Introduction

In this work I show that in each rectangle formed by the parameter lines on a
Liouville
surface the diagonals have the same energy (see the main theorem
2.6
).
This is valid, when the surface has what I call an orthogonal
Liouville
line element (including all special cases, like the isothermal one for
U_{2}=U_{1}
and
V_{2}=V_{1}
which is known as
Liouville
line element in the literature) of the form:

\displaystyle{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s}^{2}=(U_{1}(u)+V_{1}(v)){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}u}^{2}+(U_{2}(u)+V_{2}(v)){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}v}^{2}

The diagonals are, in general, not geodesics on the surface; they are the image curves of the diagonals in a rectangle formed by two pairs of parameter lines in the definition domain. If the line element is isothermal
then the parametrization of the surface is conformal and preserves angles between curves and therefore the diagonals on the surface are isogonal trajectories with respect to the parameter curves of the surface.

1.1.
Plane isothermal
Liouville
maps

In the plane there are only four distinct isothermal
Liouville
maps (up to similarity transforms), see figure
1.1
.

Figure 1.1.
Isothermal
Liouville
coordinates on the plane: the two diagonals in each rectangle have the same energy

The diagonals of each rectangle formed by parameter lines in the domain of definition are mapped to isogonal trajectories with respect to the parameter lines in the image plane because the map is isothermal and therefore conformal, see figure
1.1
.

Because the line element of these maps is isothermal
Liouville
, the diagonals in each rectangle formed by pairs of parameter lines (belonging each to the two different families of parameter lines) have the same energy (see theorem
2.4
for the proof).

If we take the straight line diagonals – these are geodesics in the plane – then we have the theorem of
Ivory
:

Theorem 1.1

(
Ivory)
.

The geodesic diagonals in a rectangle formed by parameter lines have the same length if and only if the map has a
Stäckel
line element.

Remark 1.2
.

This theorem was proved by
W. Blaschke

[
3
]
and his student
K. Zwirner

[
17
]
for the
2
and
3
dimensional case and it is valid in higher dimensions too. A proof is also found in
[
10
]
and
[
8
]
where they say that
A. Thimm
filled a gap in
W. Blaschke
’s proof.

The isothermal
Liouville
map is of
Stäckel
type and therefore the straight line diagonals have the same length, see figure
1.2
.

Figure 1.2.
Isothermal
Liouville
coordinates on the plane: the two (straight line) geodesic diagonals in each rectangle have the same length, according to
Ivory
’s theorem

If we take a parameter line rectangle in one of the four isothermal
Liouville
coordinates, then we can prove a statement about the energies of the discrete diagonals of the rectangle, in the sense of discrete differential geometry. We take the common interval of definition of the diagonals and partition it uniformly in
k
pieces. Then we construct polygons approximating the diagonals. We can show that for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
the two polygons have the same energy, see figure
1.3
.

Figure 1.3.
For each
k
the two (discrete) diagonals have the same energy

Here
k=1
corresponds to the theorem of
Ivory
in the plane because the two diagonals are geodesics and then their length and energy are related by the
Schwarz
inequality. They not only have the same energy (the energy of a segment equals the squared length) in this case, but also the same length.

The limiting case
k\to\infty
corresponds to the main theorem
2.6
of this work. All this can be viewed as a generalization of
Ivory
’s theorem (see
[
1
]
).

2.
Energy of the diagonals

2.1.
Definitions

The length
L(p)
and the energy
E(p)
of a curve
p:[a,b]\rightarrow M
in a Riemannian manifold
M
are given by the following expressions (see
[
6
]
, (Chapter 9, p. 194)) and they are related by the
Schwarz
inequality (with equality if and only if
\left|\dot{p}(t)\right|
is constant, that means
p(t)
is parametrized proportionally to arc length):

\displaystyle L(p)

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}{\left|\dot{p}(t)\right|}{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}t}=\int_{a}^{b}{\sqrt{\dot{p}(t)\cdot\dot{p}(t)}}{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}t}

\displaystyle E(p)

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}{\left|\dot{p}(t)\right|^{2}}{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}t}=\int_{a}^{b}{\dot{p}(t)\cdot\dot{p}(t)}{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}t}

\displaystyle L^{2}(p)

\displaystyle\leq(b-a)E(p)

where
\dot{p}(t)
is the tangent vector of the curve and
\_\cdot\_
is the scalar product in
TM
.

Remark 2.1
.

Let’s assume that
\left|\dot{p}(t)\right|=q=\text{const.}
Then we have:

\displaystyle L^{2}(p)=\left(\int_{a}^{b}q{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}t}\right)^{2}=(b-a)^{2}q^{2}=(b-a)\int_{a}^{b}q^{2}{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}t}=(b-a)E(p)

The length and energy have the expressions:

\displaystyle L(p)=(b-a)q\quad\text{and}\quad E(p)=(b-a)q^{2}

If
0<q<1
then
L(p)>E(p)
. If
1<q
then
L(p)<E(p)
.

For an arc length parametrized geodesic
\left|\dot{\gamma}(t)\right|=q=1
we have equality:

\displaystyle L(\gamma)=(b-a)=E(\gamma)

Thus the geodesic
\gamma(t)
is (at the same time!) energy and length minimizing.

If the manifold
M
is the
Euclid
ean plane, we can construct a discretization as follows: we partition the interval
[a,b]
uniformly in
m
pieces:

\displaystyle a<a+1\frac{b-a}{m}<a+2\frac{b-a}{m}<\cdots<a+(m-1)\frac{b-a}{m}<b

The discrete length is then given by:

\displaystyle L(p,m)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left|p\left(a+k\frac{b-a}{m}\right)-p\left(a+(k-1)\frac{b-a}{m}\right)\right|

By taking a limit for
m\to\infty
we get:

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}L(p,m)

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{\left|p\left(a+k\frac{b-a}{m}\right)-p\left(a+(k-1)\frac{b-a}{m}\right)\right|}{\frac{b-a}{m}}\cdot\frac{b-a}{m}

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left|\frac{p\left(t_{k-1}+\Delta t\right)-p\left(t_{k-1}\right)}{\Delta t}\right|\cdot\Delta t=\int_{a}^{b}{\left|\dot{p}(t)\right|}{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}t}=L(p)

The discrete energy is given by:

\displaystyle E(p,m)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{\left(p\left(a+k\frac{b-a}{m}\right)-p\left(a+(k-1)\frac{b-a}{m}\right)\right)^{2}}{\frac{b-a}{m}}

By taking a limit for
m\to\infty
we get:

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}E(p,m)

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left(\frac{p\left(a+k\frac{b-a}{m}\right)-p\left(a+(k-1)\frac{b-a}{m}\right)}{\frac{b-a}{m}}\right)^{2}\cdot\frac{b-a}{m}

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left(\frac{p\left(t_{k-1}+\Delta t\right)-p\left(t_{k-1}\right)}{\Delta t}\right)^{2}\cdot\Delta t=\int_{a}^{b}{\left|\dot{p}(t)\right|^{2}}{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}t}=E(p)

This way we see that the discrete length and energy are consistent with the length and energy defined at the beginning of this section.

Let’s compute the length
L
and energy
E
of a curve
p(t)
on a surface
S
with parametrization
{\bf x}(u,v)
.

The curve is given by
p(t)={\bf x}(u(t),v(t)):[a,b]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathbb{R}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\bf x}}}{{\rightarrow}}S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
(here
n\in\{2,3\}
). First we compute
\dot{p}(t)
:

\displaystyle\dot{p}(t)={\bf x}_{u}(u(t),v(t))\dot{u}(t)+{\bf x}_{v}(u(t),v(t))\dot{v}(t)

The scalar product
\dot{p}(t)\cdot\dot{p}(t)
with itself is:

\displaystyle\dot{p}(t)\cdot\dot{p}(t)

\displaystyle={\bf x}_{u}(u(t),v(t))\cdot{\bf x}_{u}(u(t),v(t))(\dot{u}(t))^{2}

\displaystyle+2{\bf x}_{u}(u(t),v(t))\cdot{\bf x}_{v}(u(t),v(t))\dot{u}(t)\dot{v}(t)

\displaystyle+{\bf x}_{v}(u(t),v(t))\cdot{\bf x}_{v}(u(t),v(t))(\dot{v}(t))^{2}

Now we see the connection with the metric tensor (line element) of
S
:

\displaystyle\dot{p}(t)\cdot\dot{p}(t)=g_{11}(u(t),v(t))(\dot{u}(t))^{2}+2g_{12}(u(t),v(t))\dot{u}(t)\dot{v}(t)+g_{22}(u(t),v(t))(\dot{v}(t))^{2}

With
c(t)=\begin{pmatrix}u(t),v(t)\end{pmatrix}^{t}
and the matrix
G(c(t))
of the metric tensor, we can write:

\displaystyle\dot{p}(t)\cdot\dot{p}(t)=\dot{c}(t)^{t}G(c(t))\dot{c}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}\dot{u}(t),\dot{v}(t)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}g_{11}(u(t),v(t))&g_{12}(u(t),v(t))\\
g_{21}(u(t),v(t))&g_{22}(u(t),v(t))\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\dot{u}(t)\\
\dot{v}(t)\end{pmatrix}

Now we can define the length and energy of a curve on a surface:

Definition 2.2

(Length of a curve on a surface)
.

The length of a curve
p(t)={\bf x}(c(t))
on the surface
{\bf x}(u,v)
is given by:

\displaystyle L({\bf x}(c(t)))=\int_{a}^{b}\sqrt{\dot{c}(t)^{t}G(c(t))\dot{c}(t)}{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}t}

Definition 2.3

(Energy of a curve on a surface)
.

The energy of a curve
p(t)={\bf x}(c(t))
on the surface
{\bf x}(u,v)
is given by:

\displaystyle E({\bf x}(c(t)))=\int_{a}^{b}\dot{c}(t)^{t}G(c(t))\dot{c}(t){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}t}

2.2.
Main theorem

Here we prove two theorems which together give the main theorem
2.6
of this work:

Theorem 2.4
.

If a surface
{\bf x}(u,v)
has the following orthogonal
Liouville
line element:

\displaystyle{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s}^{2}=(U_{1}(u)+V_{1}(v)){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}u}^{2}+(U_{2}(u)+V_{2}(v)){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}v}^{2}

then the diagonals in each rectangle formed by parameter lines on the surface have the same energy.

Proof.

We proceed in several steps:

(1)

We construct a rectangle
ABCD
in the parameter domain
\left]u_{min},u_{max}\right[\times\left]v_{min},v_{max}\right[
of the surface
{\bf x}(u,v)
. First we take a point
M=(u_{0},v_{0})^{t}
as the center of the rectangle. Second, we use a vector
\delta=(\alpha,\beta)^{t}
(with
\alpha\leq 0
,
\beta\leq 0
) and set
A=M+\delta
. Then the first diagonal
CA
of rectangle
ABCD
has the parametrization
d_{1}(t)=M+t\delta
with
t\in[-1,1]
: for
t=-1
we get the point
C
, for
t=0
the point
M
and for
t=1
the point
A
.
For the second diagonal we use the vector
{\overline{\delta}}=(-\alpha,\beta)^{t}
. The second diagonal
DB
of rectangle
ABCD
has the parametrization
d_{2}(t)=M+t{\overline{\delta}}
with
t\in[-1,1]
: for
t=-1
we get the point
D
, for
t=0
the point
M
and for
t=1
the point
B
.
In choosing
M
and
\delta
, we must be careful to ensure that
M
,
A
and
C
stay inside the definition domain
\left]u_{min},u_{max}\right[\times\left]v_{min},v_{max}\right[
.
Then the sides of this rectangle
ABCD
are parameter lines in the definition domain.

(2)

For the diagonals on the surface we have:

\displaystyle q_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=\dot{d_{1}}(t)^{t}G(d_{1}(t))\dot{d_{1}}(t)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha,\beta\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}U_{1}(u_{0}+t\alpha)+V_{1}(v_{0}+t\beta)&0\\
0&U_{2}(u_{0}+t\alpha)+V_{2}(v_{0}+t\beta)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\
\beta\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}(U_{1}(u_{0}+t\alpha)+V_{1}(v_{0}+t\beta))+\beta^{2}(U_{2}(u_{0}+t\alpha)+V_{2}(v_{0}+t\beta))

and

\displaystyle q_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=\dot{d_{2}}(t)^{t}G(d_{2}(t))\dot{d_{2}}(t)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-\alpha,\beta\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}U_{1}(u_{0}-t\alpha)+V_{1}(v_{0}+t\beta)&0\\
0&U_{2}(u_{0}-t\alpha)+V_{2}(v_{0}+t\beta)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}-\alpha\\
\beta\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}(U_{1}(u_{0}-t\alpha)+V_{1}(v_{0}+t\beta))+\beta^{2}(U_{2}(u_{0}-t\alpha)+V_{2}(v_{0}+t\beta))

(3)

We show that the function
f(t)=q_{1}(t)-q_{2}(t)
is an odd function of
t
, that means
f(-t)=-f(t)
:

\displaystyle f(-t)=

\displaystyle q_{1}(-t)-q_{2}(-t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}(U_{1}(u_{0}-t\alpha)+V_{1}(v_{0}-t\beta))+\beta^{2}(U_{2}(u_{0}-t\alpha)+V_{2}(v_{0}-t\beta))

\displaystyle-\alpha^{2}(U_{1}(u_{0}+t\alpha)+V_{1}(v_{0}-t\beta))-\beta^{2}(U_{2}(u_{0}+t\alpha)+V_{2}(v_{0}-t\beta))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}U_{1}(u_{0}-t\alpha)+\beta^{2}U_{2}(u_{0}-t\alpha)-\alpha^{2}U_{1}(u_{0}+t\alpha)-\beta^{2}U_{2}(u_{0}+t\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}(U_{1}(u_{0}-t\alpha)+V_{1}(v_{0}+t\beta))+\beta^{2}(U_{2}(u_{0}-t\alpha)+V_{2}(v_{0}+t\beta))

\displaystyle-\alpha^{2}(U_{1}(u_{0}+t\alpha)+V_{1}(v_{0}+t\beta))+\beta^{2}(U_{2}(u_{0}+t\alpha)+V_{2}(v_{0}+t\beta))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle q_{2}(t)-q_{1}(t)=-f(t)

(4)

For the energies of the diagonals
{\bf x}(d_{1}(t))
and
{\bf x}(d_{2}(t))
on the surface we have:

\displaystyle E({\bf x}(d_{1}(t)))-E({\bf x}(d_{2}(t)))

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{+1}q_{1}(t){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}t}-\int_{-1}^{+1}q_{2}(t){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}t}

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{+1}q_{1}(t)-q_{2}(t){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}t}=\int_{-1}^{+1}f(t){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}t}=0

We see that
E({\bf x}(d_{1}(t)))=E({\bf x}(d_{2}(t)))
and we are done.
∎

Theorem 2.5
.

If the diagonals in each rectangle formed by parameter lines on a surface
{\bf x}(u,v)
have the same energy, then the surface has the following orthogonal
Liouville
line element:

\displaystyle{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s}^{2}=(U_{1}(u)+V_{1}(v)){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}u}^{2}+(U_{2}(u)+V_{2}(v)){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}v}^{2}

Proof.

We need several steps:

(1)

We construct a square
A_{1}B_{1}C_{1}D_{1}
in the parameter domain
\left]u_{min},u_{max}\right[\times\left]v_{min},v_{max}\right[
of the surface
{\bf x}(u,v)
. First we take the point
M=(u_{0},v_{0})^{t}
as the center of the square. Second, we use a vector
\delta=(\alpha,\alpha)^{t}
(with
\alpha<0
) and set
A_{1}=M+\delta
. Then the first diagonal
C_{1}A_{1}
of the square
A_{1}B_{1}C_{1}D_{1}
has the parametrization
d_{1}(t)=M+t\delta
with
t\in[-1,1]
.
For the second diagonal we use the vector
{\overline{\delta}}=(-\alpha,\alpha)^{t}
. The second diagonal
D_{1}B_{1}
of square
A_{1}B_{1}C_{1}D_{1}
has the parametrization
d_{2}(t)=M+t{\overline{\delta}}
with
t\in[-1,1]
.
In choosing
M
and
\delta
, we must be careful to ensure that
M
,
A_{1}
and
C_{1}
stay inside the definition domain
\left]u_{min},u_{max}\right[\times\left]v_{min},v_{max}\right[
.
Then the sides of this square
A_{1}B_{1}C_{1}D_{1}
are parameter lines in the definition domain.

(2)

The metric tensor of the surface has the general form:

\displaystyle G(u,v)=\begin{pmatrix}g_{11}(u,v)&g_{12}(u,v)\\
g_{12}(u,v)&g_{22}(u,v)\end{pmatrix}

and we compute
q_{1}(t)
and
q_{2}(t)
:

\displaystyle q_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=\dot{d_{1}}(t)^{t}G(d_{1}(t))\dot{d_{1}}(t)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha,\alpha\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}g_{11}(u_{0}+t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha)&g_{12}(u_{0}+t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha)\\
g_{12}(u_{0}+t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha)&g_{22}(u_{0}+t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\
\alpha\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}(g_{11}(u_{0}+t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha)+2g_{12}(u_{0}+t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha)+g_{22}(u_{0}+t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha))

and

\displaystyle q_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=\dot{d_{2}}(t)^{t}G(d_{2}(t))\dot{d_{2}}(t)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-\alpha,\alpha\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}g_{11}(u_{0}-t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha)&g_{12}(u_{0}-t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha)\\
g_{12}(u_{0}-t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha)&g_{22}(u_{0}-t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}-\alpha\\
\alpha\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}(g_{11}(u_{0}-t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha)-2g_{12}(u_{0}-t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha)+g_{22}(u_{0}-t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha))

(3)

We know that the energies of the diagonals are equal and this implies that the function
f_{1}(t)=q_{1}(t)-q_{2}(t)
is odd. Then the expression
f_{1}(-t)+f_{1}(t)=0
is the constant zero function.
For the particular value
t=0
,
f_{1}(0)=q_{1}(0)-q_{2}(0)=0
.

\displaystyle 0=f_{1}(0)=

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}(g_{11}(u_{0},v_{0})+2g_{12}(u_{0},v_{0})+g_{22}(u_{0},v_{0}))

\displaystyle-\alpha^{2}(g_{11}(u_{0},v_{0})-2g_{12}(u_{0},v_{0})+g_{22}(u_{0},v_{0}))=4\alpha^{2}g_{12}(u_{0},v_{0})

Therefore
g_{12}(u_{0},v_{0})=0
on the whole domain of definition, because
\alpha\neq 0
and we can choose the point
(u_{0},v_{0})^{t}
freely on the domain of definition. In what follows, we will use
g_{12}(u,v)=0
(the line element is orthogonal).

(4)

For
f_{1}(t)
we now have (with
g_{12}(u,v)=0
):

\displaystyle f_{1}(t)=

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}(g_{11}(u_{0}+t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha)+g_{22}(u_{0}+t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha)

\displaystyle-g_{11}(u_{0}-t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha)-g_{22}(u_{0}-t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha))

And we know that
c_{1}(t):=f_{1}(-t)+f_{1}(t)=0
is the constant zero function:

\displaystyle f_{1}(-t)+f_{1}(t)=

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}(g_{11}(u_{0}-t\alpha,v_{0}-t\alpha)+g_{22}(u_{0}-t\alpha,v_{0}-t\alpha)

\displaystyle-g_{11}(u_{0}+t\alpha,v_{0}-t\alpha)-g_{22}(u_{0}+t\alpha,v_{0}-t\alpha))

\displaystyle+\alpha^{2}(g_{11}(u_{0}+t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha)+g_{22}(u_{0}+t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha)

\displaystyle-g_{11}(u_{0}-t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha)-g_{22}(u_{0}-t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha))

Therefore also the derivative
\frac{{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}c_{1}}(t)}{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}t}=0
with respect to
t
is the constant zero function.
We can take another derivative
\frac{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}^{2}c_{1}(t)}{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}t^{2}}=0
and know that it still is the constant zero function for all values of
t
. For
t=0
we get

\displaystyle 0=\frac{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}^{2}c_{1}(0)}{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}t^{2}}=8\alpha^{4}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}g_{11}\left(u_{0},v_{0}\right)}{\partial u\,\partial v}+\frac{\partial^{2}g_{22}\left(u_{0},v_{0}\right)}{\partial u\,\partial v}\right)

Because
\alpha\neq 0
we have the following relation between
g_{11}(u,v)
and
g_{22}(u,v)
:

(2.1)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}g_{22}\left(u,v\right)}{\partial u\,\partial v}=-\frac{\partial^{2}g_{11}\left(u,v\right)}{\partial u\,\partial v}

(5)

We construct a rectangle
A_{2}B_{2}C_{2}D_{2}
in the parameter domain
\left]u_{min},u_{max}\right[\times\left]v_{min},v_{max}\right[
of the surface
{\bf x}(u,v)
. First we take the point
M=(u_{0},v_{0})^{t}
as the center of the rectangle. Second, we use a vector
\delta=(\alpha,\alpha\varepsilon)^{t}
(with
\alpha<0
,
0<\varepsilon<1
) and set
A_{2}=M+\delta
. Then the first diagonal
C_{2}A_{2}
of rectangle
A_{2}B_{2}C_{2}D_{2}
has the parametrization
d_{3}(t)=M+t\delta
with
t\in[-1,1]
.
For the second diagonal we use the vector
{\overline{\delta}}=(-\alpha,\alpha\varepsilon)^{t}
. The second diagonal
D_{2}B_{2}
of rectangle
A_{2}B_{2}C_{2}D_{2}
has the parametrization
d_{4}(t)=M+t{\overline{\delta}}
with
t\in[-1,1]
.
In choosing
M
and
\delta
, we must be careful to ensure that
M
,
A_{2}
and
C_{2}
stay inside the definition domain
\left]u_{min},u_{max}\right[\times\left]v_{min},v_{max}\right[
.
Then the sides of this rectangle
A_{2}B_{2}C_{2}D_{2}
are parameter lines in the definition domain.

(6)

The metric tensor of the surface has the form (remember that
g_{12}(u,v)=0
):

\displaystyle G(u,v)=\begin{pmatrix}g_{11}(u,v)&0\\
0&g_{22}(u,v)\end{pmatrix}

and we compute
q_{3}(t)
and
q_{4}(t)
:

\displaystyle q_{3}(t)

\displaystyle=\dot{d_{3}}(t)^{t}G(d_{3}(t))\dot{d_{3}}(t)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha,\alpha\varepsilon\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}g_{11}(u_{0}+t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha\varepsilon)&0\\
0&g_{22}(u_{0}+t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha\varepsilon)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\
\alpha\varepsilon\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}(g_{11}(u_{0}+t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha\varepsilon)+\varepsilon^{2}g_{22}(u_{0}+t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha\varepsilon))

and

\displaystyle q_{4}(t)

\displaystyle=\dot{d_{4}}(t)^{t}G(d_{4}(t))\dot{d_{4}}(t)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-\alpha,\alpha\varepsilon\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}g_{11}(u_{0}-t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha\varepsilon)&0\\
0&g_{22}(u_{0}-t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha\varepsilon)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}-\alpha\\
\alpha\varepsilon\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}(g_{11}(u_{0}-t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha\varepsilon)+\varepsilon^{2}g_{22}(u_{0}-t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha\varepsilon))

(7)

We know that the energies of the diagonals are equal and this implies that the function
f_{2}(t)=q_{3}(t)-q_{4}(t)
is odd. Then the expression
f_{2}(-t)+f_{2}(t)=0
is the constant zero function.

(8)

For
f_{2}(t)
we have:

\displaystyle f_{2}(t)=

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}(g_{11}(u_{0}+t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha\varepsilon)+\varepsilon^{2}g_{22}(u_{0}+t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha\varepsilon))

\displaystyle-\alpha^{2}(g_{11}(u_{0}-t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha\varepsilon)+\varepsilon^{2}g_{22}(u_{0}-t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha\varepsilon))

And we know that
c_{2}(t):=f_{2}(-t)+f_{2}(t)=0
.

\displaystyle c_{2}(t)=

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}(\varepsilon^{2}g_{22}\left(u_{0}-t\alpha,v_{0}-t\alpha\varepsilon\right)-\varepsilon^{2}g_{22}\left(u_{0}-t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle-\varepsilon^{2}g_{22}\left(u_{0}+t\alpha,v_{0}-t\alpha\varepsilon\right)+\varepsilon^{2}g_{22}\left(u_{0}+t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle+g_{11}\left(u_{0}-t\alpha,v_{0}-t\alpha\varepsilon\right)-g_{11}\left(u_{0}-t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle-g_{11}\left(u_{0}+t\alpha,v_{0}-t\alpha\varepsilon\right)+g_{11}\left(u_{0}+t\alpha,v_{0}+t\alpha\varepsilon\right))

Therefore also the derivative
\frac{{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}c_{2}}(t)}{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}t}=0
with respect to
t
is the constant zero function.
We can take another derivative
\frac{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}^{2}c_{2}(t)}{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}t^{2}}=0
and know that it still is the constant zero function for all values of
t
. We use relation (
2.1
) and we get for
t=0
:

\displaystyle 0=\frac{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}^{2}c_{2}(0)}{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}t^{2}}=8\alpha^{4}\varepsilon\left(1-\varepsilon^{2}\right)\frac{\partial^{2}g_{11}\left(u_{0},v_{0}\right)}{\partial u\,\partial v}

Because
\alpha\neq 0
and
0<\varepsilon<1
we have (combined with (
2.1
)):

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}g_{11}\left(u,v\right)}{\partial u\,\partial v}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\partial^{2}g_{22}\left(u,v\right)}{\partial u\,\partial v}=0

(9)

By solving each of these differential equations separately with respect to
u
and
v
we get:

\displaystyle g_{11}(u,v)=U_{1}(u)+V_{1}(v)\quad\text{and}\quad g_{22}(u,v)=U_{2}(u)+V_{2}(v)

And we are done.
∎

The previous two theorems combined give together the following main theorem of this work:

Theorem 2.6

(Main theorem)
.

If and only if a surface
{\bf x}(u,v)
has the following orthogonal
Liouville
line element:

\displaystyle{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s}^{2}=(U_{1}(u)+V_{1}(v)){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}u}^{2}+(U_{2}(u)+V_{2}(v)){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}v}^{2}

the diagonals in each rectangle formed by parameter lines on the surface have the same energy.

Corollary 2.7
.

It immediately follows that for all special cases of this line element the diagonals have the same energy.

3.
Liouville
surfaces

In this section we define and name some special line elements or parametrizations of surfaces and give examples of
Liouville
surfaces.

3.1.
Definitions

Let a surface be given as a parametrization
{\bf x}(u,v):\left]u_{min},u_{max}\right[\times\left]v_{min},v_{max}\right[\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{n}
(in this work we have
n\in\{2,3\}
but the theory is valid in any dimension).

Definition 3.1

(Line element and first fundamental form)
.

We define the line element
{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s}
of the surface
{\bf x}(u,v)
by the first fundamental form
ds^{2}
:

(3.1)

\displaystyle{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s}^{2}=g_{11}(u,v){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}u}^{2}+2g_{12}(u,v){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}u}{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}v}+g_{22}(u,v){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}v}^{2}

where

g_{11}(u,v)={\bf x}_{u}(u,v)\boldsymbol{\cdot}{\bf x}_{u}(u,v)
,

g_{12}(u,v)=g_{21}(u,v)={\bf x}_{u}(u,v)\boldsymbol{\cdot}{\bf x}_{v}(u,v)

and

g_{22}(u,v)={\bf x}_{v}(u,v)\boldsymbol{\cdot}{\bf x}_{v}(u,v)
.

When
g_{12}=0
, the
u
-lines
v=\rm const.
and
v
-lines
u=\rm const.
are orthogonal to each other on the surface and we speak of an orthogonal parametrization:

Definition 3.2

(Orthogonal parametrization)
.

When the line element
{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s}
of the surface
{\bf x}(u,v)
has the following form:

(3.2)

\displaystyle{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s}^{2}=g_{11}(u,v){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}u}^{2}+g_{22}(u,v){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}v}^{2}

with

g_{12}(u,v)=g_{21}(u,v)=0
, the parametrization of the surface is called orthogonal.

When
g_{11}=g_{22}
,
g_{12}=0
the parametrization is orthogonal and locally conformal (preserves angles between curves (their tangent vectors)):

Definition 3.3

(Isothermal (or conformal) parametrization)
.

When the line element
{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s}
of the surface
{\bf x}(u,v)
has the following form:

(3.3)

\displaystyle{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s}^{2}=g_{11}(u,v)({\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}u}^{2}+{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}v}^{2})

with

g_{11}(u,v)=g_{22}(u,v)
and

g_{12}(u,v)=g_{21}(u,v)=0
, the parametrization of the surface is called isothermal.

Now we can define
Liouville
and
Clairaut
parametrizations:

Definition 3.4

(Orthogonal
Liouville
parametrization)
.

When the line element
{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s}
of the surface
{\bf x}(u,v)
has the following form:

(3.4)

\displaystyle{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s}^{2}=(U_{1}(u)+V_{1}(v)){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}u}^{2}+(U_{2}(u)+V_{2}(v)){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}v}^{2}

with

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial u\partial v}g_{11}(u,v)=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial u\partial v}g_{22}(u,v)=0
and

g_{12}(u,v)=g_{21}(u,v)=0
, the parametrization of the surface is called orthogonal
Liouville
parametrization.

A special case is (when
g_{11}=g_{22}
):

Definition 3.5

(Isothermal (or classical)
Liouville
parametrization)
.

When the line element
{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s}
of the surface
{\bf x}(u,v)
has the following form:

(3.5)

\displaystyle{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s}^{2}=(U(u)+V(v))({\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}u}^{2}+{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}v}^{2})

with

g_{11}(u,v)=g_{22}(u,v)=U(u)+V(v)
and

g_{12}(u,v)=g_{21}(u,v)=0
, the parametrization of the surface is called isothermal
Liouville
parametrization.

The
Liouville
parametrizations specialize further to
Clairaut
parametrizations, when
g_{11}
and
g_{22}
depend only on
u
(or on
v
):

Definition 3.6

(Orthogonal
Clairaut
parametrization in
u
)
.

When the line element
{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s}
of the surface
{\bf x}(u,v)
has the following form:

(3.6)

\displaystyle{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s}^{2}=U_{1}(u){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}u}^{2}+U_{2}(u){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}v}^{2}

with

\frac{\partial}{\partial v}g_{11}(u,v)=\frac{\partial}{\partial v}g_{22}(u,v)=0
and

g_{12}(u,v)=g_{21}(u,v)=0
, the parametrization of the surface is called orthogonal
Clairaut
parametrization in
u
. (The other case is that of the orthogonal
Clairaut
parametrization in
v
.)

A special case is (when
g_{11}=g_{22}=U(u)
or
g_{11}=g_{22}=V(v)
):

Definition 3.7

(Isothermal
Clairaut
parametrization in
u
)
.

When the line element
{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s}
of the surface
{\bf x}(u,v)
has the following form:

(3.7)

\displaystyle{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s}^{2}=U(u)({\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}u}^{2}+{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}v}^{2})

with

g_{11}(u,v)=g_{22}(u,v)=U(u)
and

g_{12}(u,v)=g_{21}(u,v)=0
, the parametrization of the surface is called isothermal
Clairaut
parametrization in
u
. (The other case is that of the isothermal
Clairaut
parametrization in
v
.)

Other two special cases worth mentioning are:

Definition 3.8

(Orthogonal
Liouville
parametrization in
U_{1}(u)
and
V_{2}(v)
)
.

When the line element
{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s}
of the surface
{\bf x}(u,v)
has the following form:

(3.8)

\displaystyle{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s}^{2}=U_{1}(u){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}u}^{2}+V_{2}(v){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}v}^{2}

with

\frac{\partial}{\partial v}g_{11}(u,v)=\frac{\partial}{\partial u}g_{22}(u,v)=0
and

g_{12}(u,v)=g_{21}(u,v)=0
, the parametrization of the surface is called orthogonal
Liouville
parametrization in
U_{1}(u)
and
V_{2}(v)
.
(The other case is that of the orthogonal
Liouville
parametrization in
V_{1}(v)
and
U_{2}(u)
.)

The next line element is relevant for the
Ivory
–property of the geodesic diagonals:

Definition 3.9

(
Stäckel
parametrization)
.

When the line element
{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s}
of the surface
{\bf x}(u,v)
has the following form:

(3.9)

\displaystyle{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s}^{2}=\begin{vmatrix}U(u)&V(v)\\
U_{1}(u)&V_{1}(v)\end{vmatrix}\left(\frac{{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}u}^{2}}{V_{1}(v)}-\frac{{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}v}^{2}}{U_{1}(u)}\right)

with

g_{12}(u,v)=g_{21}(u,v)=0
, the parametrization of the surface is called
Stäckel
parametrization.

Remark 3.10
.

The isothermal
Liouville
parametrization is a
Stäckel
parametrization because we can write it as follows:

\displaystyle{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s}^{2}=\begin{vmatrix}U(u)&V(v)\\
-1&1\end{vmatrix}\left({\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}u}^{2}+{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}v}^{2}\right)=\left(U(u)+V(v)\right)\left({\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}u}^{2}+{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}v}^{2}\right)

Remark 3.11
.

The isothermal
Clairaut
parametrization in
u
is a
Stäckel
parametrization:

\displaystyle{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s}^{2}=\begin{vmatrix}U(u)&0\\
-1&1\end{vmatrix}\left({\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}u}^{2}+{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}v}^{2}\right)=U(u)\left({\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}u}^{2}+{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}v}^{2}\right)

The isothermal
Clairaut
parametrization in
v
is a
Stäckel
parametrization:

\displaystyle{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s}^{2}=\begin{vmatrix}0&V(v)\\
-1&1\end{vmatrix}\left({\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}u}^{2}+{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}v}^{2}\right)=V(v)\left({\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}u}^{2}+{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}v}^{2}\right)

Remark 3.12
.

The orthogonal
Clairaut
parametrization in
u
is a
Stäckel
parametrization:

\displaystyle{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s}^{2}=\begin{vmatrix}U_{1}(u)&0\\
\frac{U_{1}(u)}{U_{2}(u)}&-1\end{vmatrix}\left(-{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}u}^{2}-\frac{U_{2}(u)}{U_{1}(u)}{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}v}^{2}\right)=U_{1}(u){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}u}^{2}+U_{2}(u){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}v}^{2}

The orthogonal
Clairaut
parametrization in
v
is a
Stäckel
parametrization:

\displaystyle{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s}^{2}=\begin{vmatrix}0&V_{2}(v)\\
-1&\frac{V_{2}(v)}{V_{1}(v)}\end{vmatrix}\left(\frac{V_{1}(v)}{V_{2}(v)}{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}u}^{2}+{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}v}^{2}\right)=V_{1}(v){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}u}^{2}+V_{2}(v){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}v}^{2}

Remark 3.13
.

The orthogonal
Liouville
parametrization in
U_{1}(u)
and
V_{2}(v)
is a
Stäckel
parametrization:

\displaystyle{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s}^{2}=\begin{vmatrix}U_{1}(u)&0\\
U_{1}(u)&-V_{2}(v)\end{vmatrix}\left(-\frac{{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}u}^{2}}{V_{2}(v)}-\frac{{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}v}^{2}}{U_{1}(u)}\right)=U_{1}(u){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}u}^{2}+V_{2}(v){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}v}^{2}

The orthogonal
Liouville
parametrization in
V_{1}(v)
and
U_{2}(u)
is in general not a
Stäckel
parametrization. We can write:

\displaystyle{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s}^{2}=\begin{vmatrix}U(u)&V(v)\\
-\frac{1}{U_{2}(u)}&\frac{1}{V_{1}(v)}\end{vmatrix}\left(V_{1}(v){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}u}^{2}+U_{2}(u){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}v}^{2}\right)

and we see that this is only then an orthogonal
Liouville
parametrization in
V_{1}(v)
and
U_{2}(u)
when the following determinant is a non-zero constant:

\displaystyle\begin{vmatrix}U(u)&V(v)\\
-\frac{1}{U_{2}(u)}&\frac{1}{V_{1}(v)}\end{vmatrix}=\frac{U(u)}{V_{1}(v)}+\frac{V(v)}{U_{2}(u)}=\text{const.}

Remark 3.14
.

The orthogonal
Liouville
parametrization is in general not a
Stäckel
parametrization. We only get special cases when both
U_{1}(u)
and
V_{1}(v)
in the
Stäckel
parametrization are non-zero constants.

Examples of
Liouville

Liouville

Liouville

2.4

The first three examples are surfaces of constant

Example 3.20

(Parabolic cylinder)
.

4.1.
Standard curvature line parametrization of the triaxial ellipsoid

In the literature (see

because it actually computes (making use of elliptic integrals) the integrals already given by
Jacobi
in his “Lectures on Dynamics”. In this article we want to go in the opposite direction and map a plane rectangle conformally to a triaxial ellipsoid in such a way that the map has an isothermal
Liouville
line element. The result can be seen in the right image of figure

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\includegraphics[scale]{ellipsoid_cl.pdf}&\includegraphics[scale]{ellipsoid_li.pdf}\end{array}

Figure 4.1.

Liouville

We will start here with the standard curvature line parametrization of the triaxial ellipsoid with semi-axes
0<c<b<a
:

(4.1)

\displaystyle{\bf x}(u,v)=\left(\sqrt{\frac{a^{2}(a^{2}-u)(a^{2}-v)}{(a^{2}-b^{2})(a^{2}-c^{2})}},\sqrt{\frac{b^{2}(b^{2}-u)(b^{2}-v)}{(b^{2}-c^{2})(b^{2}-a^{2})}},\sqrt{\frac{c^{2}(c^{2}-u)(c^{2}-v)}{(c^{2}-a^{2})(c^{2}-b^{2})}}\right)^{t}

where
0<c^{2}<v<b^{2}<u<a^{2}
(see left image of figure
4.1
).

The coefficients of the first fundamental form are computed as follows:

\displaystyle g_{11}(u,v)

\displaystyle={\bf x}_{u}(u,v)\cdot{\bf x}_{u}(u,v)=(u-v)f(u)

\displaystyle g_{12}(u,v)

\displaystyle={\bf x}_{u}(u,v)\cdot{\bf x}_{v}(u,v)=g_{21}(u,v)=0

\displaystyle g_{22}(u,v)

\displaystyle={\bf x}_{v}(u,v)\cdot{\bf x}_{v}(u,v)=(u-v)(-f(v))

with the function
f
defined as:

\displaystyle f(t)=\frac{1}{4}\frac{t}{(a^{2}-t)(b^{2}-t)(c^{2}-t)}

The line element of the ellipsoid is:

(4.2)

\displaystyle{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s}^{2}=g_{11}(u,v){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}u}^{2}+g_{22}(u,v){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}v}^{2}=(u-v)(f(u){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}u}^{2}-f(v){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}v}^{2})

Remark 4.1
.

This line element is an orthogonal
Stäckel
line element, because it can be written as:

\displaystyle{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s}^{2}=(u-v)(f(u){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}u}^{2}-f(v){\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}v}^{2})=\begin{vmatrix}uf(u)&vf(v)\\
f(u)&f(v)\end{vmatrix}\left(\frac{{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}u}^{2}}{f(v)}-\frac{{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}v}^{2}}{f(u)}\right)

4.2.
Conformal map from ellipsoid to plane

What we want to achieve is the following isothermal
Liouville
form of this line element (
4.2
):

(4.3)

\displaystyle{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s}^{2}

\displaystyle=(U(x)-V(y))({\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}x}^{2}+{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}y}^{2})

If formulas (
4.2

\displaystyle{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}x}=\sqrt{+f(u)}{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}u}\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}y}=\sqrt{-f(v)}{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}v}

By integrating we get formulas corresponding to (7) and (8) from

\displaystyle X(u)

\displaystyle=\int_{b^{2}}^{u}\sqrt{+f(t)}{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}t}=F_{1}(u)-F_{1}(b^{2})=F_{1}(u)

\displaystyle Y(v)

\displaystyle=\int_{c^{2}}^{v}\sqrt{-f(t)}{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}t}=F_{2}(v)-F_{2}(c^{2})=F_{2}(v)

\displaystyle F_{1}\left(t\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{b^{2}i}{c\sqrt{a^{2}-b^{2}}}\Pi\left(n_{1};\varphi_{1}(t)|m_{1}\right)

\displaystyle F_{2}\left(t\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{c^{2}}{b\sqrt{a^{2}-c^{2}}}\Pi\left(n_{2};\varphi_{2}(t)|m_{2}\right)

where

\displaystyle n_{1}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{b^{2}}{c^{2}}

\displaystyle\varphi_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=\arcsin\left({-ic\sqrt{\frac{t-b^{2}}{\left(b^{2}-c^{2}\right)t}}}\right)

\displaystyle m_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{{a}^{2}\left(c^{2}-b^{2}\right)}{{c}^{2}\left(a^{2}-b^{2}\right)}

\displaystyle n_{2}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{c^{2}}{b^{2}}

\displaystyle\varphi_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=\arcsin\left({b\sqrt{\frac{t-c^{2}}{\left(b^{2}-c^{2}\right)t}}}\right)

\displaystyle m_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{{a}^{2}\left(b^{2}-c^{2}\right)}{{b}^{2}\left(a^{2}-c^{2}\right)}

and the incomplete elliptic integral of the third kind is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\Pi(n;\varphi|m)=\int_{0}^{\varphi}\frac{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}\theta}{(1-n\sin^{2}\theta)\sqrt{1-m\sin^{2}\theta}}

4.3.

If we plug

\displaystyle{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s}^{2}=\left(U(x)-V(y)\right)\left(f(U(x))\left(\frac{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}U(x)}{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}x}\right)^{2}{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}x}^{2}-f(V(y))\left(\frac{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}V(y)}{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}y}\right)^{2}{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}y}^{2}\right)

4.5.2.

Here we use the method outlined in

(4.5)

\displaystyle=y^{2}-(1-nx^{2})^{2}(1-x^{2})(1-mx^{2})

\displaystyle\varphi(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{y}

\displaystyle D=\frac{1}{\varphi}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}-\frac{1}{\varphi}\frac{f_{x}}{f_{y}}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}=y\frac{\partial}{\partial x}-\frac{f_{x}}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}

\displaystyle e^{uD}x=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{u^{j}\overbrace{D(D(\cdots D}^{j}(x)))}{j!}
