Minimizing the externalities variance in a LCFS-PR M / G / 1 M/G/1 queue under various constraints
Royi Jacobovic, Nikki Levering

Abstract
Consider a LCFS-PR M / G / 1 M/G/1 queue and assume that at time t = 0 t=0 , there are n + 2 n+2 customers c 1 , c 2 , … , c n + 1 , c c_{1},c_{2},...,c_{n+1},c who arrived in that order such that t = 0 t=0 is the arrival time of c c . Then, the externalities which are generated by c c is the total waiting time that would be saved by c 1 , c 2 , … , c n + 1 c_{1},c_{2},...,c_{n+1} if c c reduced his service requirement to zero. Motivated by some applications, this work is about the minimization of the externalities variance under various constraints.



Keywords:
M/G/1, Externalities, Convex piecewise-linear programming, Explicit optimal solution

AMS Subject Classification (MSC2010):
60K25, 60K30, 90C08, 90C25, 90C27.

1
Introduction

1.1
Externalities in queues

Congestion is a phenomenon which characterizes storage systems such as
telecommunication networks, production-lines, road-traffic, etc. In practice,
excessive congestion leads to reduced system efficiency. Motivated by
this issue, queueing theory is the study of mathematical modeling and
optimization of congested systems which are operated in various stochastic
environments.

An observation which dates back to classical works, e.g.,
[
11
,
28
,
32
]
, is as follows: Each customer who joins the queue will cause a potential delay to the customers who will be waiting during his service periods. Moreover, the individual utility maximization of any joining customer does not take into account the misery which he will cause to those who will have to wait because of him. Therefore, a plausible remedy to this market-failure is to impose a toll on customers who are joining the queue.

Basically, the social-planner problem is how to determine this toll in order to maximize the long-run average total welfare of the customers (for simplicity, assume that there are no costs due to service provision). One natural pricing policy to think about is called
internalization of externalities
, i.e., make each joining customer pay the expected loss which he will cause to others during his sojourn in the system, when all other customers are getting their service according to the socially optimal resource allocation. Some studies about this idea and its variations to queues in which the customers have no influence on their service times are, e.g.,
[
6
,
9
,
15
,
16
,
31
]
. In addition, there are also some works about this idea in the context of queues in which customers have influence on their service times, e.g.,
[
14
,
17
,
21
,
22
]
. Another genre of problems which are related to the notion of externalities concerns server-allocation (scheduling) problems in multi-class queues (especially with dynamic class types)
[
7
,
19
,
20
,
30
]
. For a more detailed discussion about the relation between these works and the notion of externalities, see
[
23
, Section 1.1]
.

Clearly, the externalities which are generated by a tagged customer, i.e., the total waiting time which would be saved by the other customers if the tagged customer reduced his service requirement to zero and left the system, is a random variable. Notably, since this random variable is very applicable, it makes sense to study its distribution. The prominent works in this direction are
[
18
,
23
,
24
]
, and, as to be explained in the sequel, the current work adds to this strand of literature.

1.2
Externalities in LCFS-PR M/G/1 queue

Consider a single-server queue with customers who arrive
with respect to
a Poisson process with rate
\lambda>0
. Their service demands are iid nonnegative random variables which are distributed according to
F(\cdot)
and independent from the arrival process. For simplicity of notation, for each
i\geq 1
, let
\mu_{i}
be the
i
-th moment of
F(\cdot)
and assume that the system is stable, i.e.,
\rho\equiv\lambda\mu_{1}<1
. Also, let the system
be operated according to a preemptive last-come, first-served (LCFS-PR) discipline. In addition, assume that at time
t=0
, there exist
n+1\geq 1
customers
c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{n+1}
in the system such that for each
1\leq i\leq n+1
, the remaining service time of
c_{i}
is equal to
v_{i}
. For clarity and without loss of generality, for every
1\leq i<j\leq n+1
we shall assume that
c_{i}
arrived before
c_{j}
.

Now, assume that there is an additional customer
c
who arrives at time
t=0
and whose service demand is equal to
x>0
. The
externalities
which are created by
c
is the total waiting time that
c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{n+1}
would save if
c
reduced his service demand to zero and left the system immediately. Importantly, the externalities are expressed via a random variable
\mathcal{E}
with a distribution which is determined by
\lambda,n,v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n+1},x
and
F(\cdot)
.

In a recent study
[
24
, Eq. (19)]
the mean and variance of
\mathcal{E}
were derived and their formulae are as follows:

E\mathcal{E}=\frac{nx}{1-\rho}\,,

(1)

\mbox{Var}\left(\mathcal{E}\right)=\frac{\lambda\mu_{2}}{(1-\rho)^{3}}\left[nx+2\sum_{1\leq k<\ell\leq n}\left(x-\sum_{i=k}^{\ell}v_{i}\right)^{+}\right]\,.

(2)

It is very eminent that both of the above-mentioned formulae are independent of
v_{n+1}
. In addition, it is also a bit surprising that the expected externalities are invariant with respect to the values of
v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n+1}
.

1.3
Problem statement

Assume that the system has a manager who knows the statistical assumptions of the model. In addition, assume that, at time
t=0
, he observes the vector

\left(x,v_{n+1},x+\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}v_{i},n\!+\!1\right)\,,

(3)

which includes four coordinates:

1.

x
which is the remaining service time of the customer
c
who has just entered the service position.

2.

v_{n+1}
which is the remaining service time of
c_{n+1}
who has just left the service position.

3.

x+\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}v_{i}
which equals the total workload in the system at
t=0
.

4.

n+1
which is the number of customers who exist in the system at time
t=0
.

In particular, this information scheme looks reasonable in settings in
which the manager has limited memory capacity, and he is unable to store the remaining
service times of all customers.

With this information, the manager may compute
E\mathcal{E}
via (
1
) but he is unable to compute
\text{Var}\left(\mathcal{E}\right)
via (
2
). Thus, the manager might be willing to compute the range of possible variance values given his available information at
t=0
, i.e., he needs to derive the infimum and supremum of
\text{Var}\left(\mathcal{E}\right)
over all parameterizations which are consistent with his available information at time
t=0
. Note that we refer to an infimum (resp. a supremum) problem and not to a minimum (resp. maximum) problem because the manager knows that there are
n+2
customers in the system. Thus, any parameterization which is consistent with this knowledge must be such that
v_{i}>0
for every
1\leq i\leq n
. In this work we focus on the infimum problem, as the supremum is evident. That is,
since
v_{1}
and
v_{n}
are both contributing
to the least number of sums in (
2
),
it is clear that
\mbox{Var}\left(\mathcal{E}\right)
reaches
its supremum if
\left(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}\right)
is either
(w,0,0,\ldots,0)
or
(0,0,\ldots,0,w)
where
w\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}
.

Eminently, when
w\geq nx
the infimum problem is simple and hence we consider the next setup: Let
n\in\mathbb{N},\ x>0
, and
w\in(0,nx)
be fixed parameters and define a function

f(\boldsymbol{v})\equiv\sum_{1\leq k\leq\ell\leq n}\left(x-\sum_{i=k}^{\ell}v_{i}\right)^{+}\ \ ,\ \ \boldsymbol{v}=(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\,.

(4)

Then, the infimum problem is translated to the next minimization:

\min_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\Lambda}f(\boldsymbol{v})\,,

(5)

where

\Lambda\equiv\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\ ;\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}\leq w\right\}\,.

(6)

1.4
Combinatorial version

Now, we present a combinatorial version of the continuous minimization (
5
). Specifically, it is stated as follows: With the parameters of the original problem, denote

m\equiv\left\lfloor\frac{w}{x}\right\rfloor
as well as
r\equiv w-mx
. Then, define the domain

\Upsilon\equiv\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\ ;\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}\leq w\ ,\ \left|\left\{i;v_{i}=x\right\}\right|=m\ ,\ \left|\left\{i;v_{i}=r\right\}\right|\geq 1\right\}\,.

(7)

Verbally, if
r>0
, then
\Upsilon
is the collection of all real
n
-dimensional vectors having
m
components which equal
x
, one component equals
r
, and all other components equal zero. Otherwise,
w=mx
(i.e.,
r=0
), and
\Upsilon
is the collection of all real
n
-dimensional vectors which have
m
components which equal
x
, and all other components equal zero. Notice that
\Upsilon
is a finite discrete set in both cases.

The combinatorial minimization to be studied in this paper is:

\min_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\Upsilon}f(\boldsymbol{v})\,.

(8)

Furthermore, we will explore its interplay with the continuous minimization (
5
). Also, besides being a straightforward combinatorial version of the continuous minimization (
5
), we note in passing that (
8
) might be associated with three additional interpretations which are explained below. We believe that each of these interpretations sheds some light on a different aspect of the combinatorial minimization (
8
). Especially, notice that the third interpretation is also relevant for the continuous minimization (
5
).

1.4.1
Card collection problem

Consider a collection of
n
cards
\mathcal{C}\equiv\left\{C_{1},C_{2},\ldots,C_{n}\right\}
. Let
m
be an integer which is less than
n
and define

\mathcal{C}_{m}\equiv\left\{\{C_{i_{1}},C_{i_{2}},\ldots,C_{i_{m}}\};1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\ldots<i_{m}\leq n\right\}\,.

(9)

Any subset of successive cards is called a
series
, i.e., the set of all series is

\mathcal{S}\equiv\left\{\left\{C_{k},C_{k+1},\ldots,C_{\ell}\right\};1\leq k\leq\ell\leq n\right\}\,.

(10)

In addition, there is a single player whose task is to choose a sub-collection
S
of
m
cards which participates in the maximal number of series, i.e., his needs to choose
S\in\mathcal{C}_{m}
which maximizes

\sum_{S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\textbf{1}\left(S\cap S^{\prime}\neq\emptyset\right)\ \ ,\ \ S\in\mathcal{C}_{m}\,.

(11)

Notably, this problem coincides with (
8
) parameterized by
x=1
,
m=w
and
r=0
.

Observe that when
r\in(0,1)
, (
8
) is describing the following stochastic version of the deterministic card collection problem: The player is required to choose
m
cards and an additional card. The sub-collection of cards which the player possesses after making his choice is random. It will include the
m
cards for sure, but the additional card will be included with probability
r
. Then, the purpose of the player is to choose the
m
cards and the additional card in order to maximize the expected number of series which have nonempty intersection with his cards sub-collection.

1.4.2
Location problem

Consider the triangular lattice

L\equiv\left\{(k,\ell)\in\mathbb{N}^{2};\ k+\ell\leq n+1\right\}\,,

(12)

and assume that each point belonging to
L
represents a site location. In addition, assume there is a planner who should allocate
1\leq m<n
stations on the hypotenuse of the lattice. Assume that for each
1\leq k\leq n
, the station which is located on
(k,\ell)
provides service to all sites on

\left\{(k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime})\in L;k^{\prime}\leq k\,,\ell^{\prime}\leq\ell\right\}\,.

(13)

Spatially, this means that every station provides service to all sites which are neither higher than it nor right to it (see e.g. Figure
1
). Then, under this assumption, the planner’s objective is to pick the allocation of the stations in order to maximize the number of sites where service is available. Note that this problem is a special case of (
8
) with the parameters
x=1
,
w=m
and
r=0
.

Figure 1:
Example of a triangular lattice
L
with
n=5
.
Station (2,4) is activated, and serves all sites in the lower left rectangle.

For more maximal covering location problems, see a recent overview
[
13
]
. In addition, a slightly different class of covering problems is
of the budgeted maximal coverage, see, e.g.,
[
26
]
.

1.4.3
Minimization of the excess demand for public goods

In economic theory, a
public good
is any good which has the following two properties:

1.

It is
non-excludable
, i.e., there is no way to prevent its consumption by customers who are willing to do so.

2.

It is
non-rivalrous
, i.e., its consumption by one customer does not limit the ability of other customers to consume it.

Some traditional examples of public goods are, e.g., national security, law enforcement, and clean air. In general, public goods are usually supplied by the government, since the private sector has very limited incentives to produce them (see, e.g.,
[
36
]
). For more information about the theory and applications of public goods, see, e.g.,
[
4
]
.

Now, consider an economy with
n
public goods
G_{1},G_{2},\ldots,G_{n}
, and
n(n+1)/2
customers who are doubly-indexed by
(k,\ell)
such that
1\leq k\leq\ell\leq n
. The government has a budget of
w=mx
monetary units and the cost of producing the allocation
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
, in which for each
1\leq i\leq n
there are
v_{i}
units of the
i
-th good is
x\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}
. In addition, for each
1\leq k\leq\ell\leq n
, the
(k,\ell)
-th customer demands to consume one unit of goods from the set
\{G_{k},G_{k+1},\ldots,G_{\ell}\}
. In particular, this means that the
(k,\ell)
-th customer is indifferent between the goods
G_{k},G_{k+1},\ldots,G_{\ell}
, i.e., he considers these goods as perfect substitutes. In order to demonstrate the market structure, one may think about
G_{1},G_{2},\ldots,G_{n}
as food products (potentially supplied by the government) which are ordered according to their nutritional value. At the same time,
G_{1},G_{2},\ldots,G_{n}
are reversely ordered with respect to their taste. Thus, the above-described market is such that the demand for the intermediate food products is higher than the demand for either healthy (but non-tasty) or tasty (but unhealthy) food products.

The government’s objective is to minimize the total excess-demand of the customers in the economy given its budget constraint. Formally,
if
G_{1},G_{2},\ldots,G_{n}
are indivisible (resp. divisible)
this problem is translated into (
8
) (resp. (
5
)).

Notice that, in both cases, the government’s problem is how to choose the variety of products which are supplied to its citizens. For some other product variety management problems which appear in the existing literature, see, e.g.,
[
27
,
34
]
and the references therein.

1.5
Additional related literature

Technically,
f(\cdot)
is a piecewise-linear convex function on
the compact domain
\Lambda
, i.e., there exists an optimal solution of (
5
). Furthermore, this means that for any given parameterization of (
5
) standard subgradient methods yield a sequence which converges to the numerical value of an optimal solution with adequate convergence rates. A survey about subgradient methods is given in
[
5
]
.

In addition, observe that by using the epigraph of the convex piecewise-linear function
f(\cdot)
, the continuous minimization (
5
) can be rephrased as a linear program (LP). Therefore, it is possible to solve the continuous minimization (
5
) in polynomial time, see, e.g.,
[
25
]
. In fact, it is clear that adding the constraints
v_{i}\leq x
for each
1\leq i\leq n
will not cause any difference in the resulting minimization. As a result, (
5
) may also be phrased as a special case of an
interval linear programming
. Thus, the analysis in
[
2
,
3
,
39
,
40
]
includes an explicit expression of each optimal solution of (
5
) in terms of some projection matrix and the generalized inverse of the constraints matrix in the resulting linear program.

When
r=0
, the combinatorial problem (
8
) may be considered as a version of the 0-1 knapsack problem
with dependencies
. Notably, even when there are no dependencies between the items, the classic 0-1 knapsack problem is
NP-hard
[
12
]
. To the best of our knowledge, there
is not much work about knapsack problems with dependencies. This strand of literature includes the more traditional quadratic knapsack problem. A survey of that topic is given in
[
33
]
. In addition, consider the recent paper
[
1
]
about knapsack problems with dependencies through non-additive measures and Choquet integral. Other non-standard versions of the knapsack problem are summarized in
[
29
]
.

Recall the interpretation of the combinatorial minimization (
8
) as a card collection problem, and assume that
r=0
. Then, there is also a relation to the
interval stabbing problems
which appear in
[
35
]
. This reference
contains a discussion about the computational complexity of finding the number of series which have nonempty intersection with a given subset of
m
cards. However, to the best of our understanding, there has been no discussion about the derivation of the optimal subset of
m
cards, i.e., the subset which has a nonempty intersection with the maximal number of series.

1.6
Contribution and paper organization

The present work includes a characterization of a set of highly-structured optimal solutions of (
8
). This yields an applicable numerical procedure to construct such an optimal solution (
8
).
In addition, it is shown that for some families of parameterizations, e.g., when
r=0
but not only, the continuous minimization (
5
) is reduced to the combinatorial one (
8
). Finally, we put forth a conjecture that this reduction is true for every parameterization.

The organization of this work is as follows: Section
2

considers the combinatorial minimization (
8
).
Results regarding the continuous minimization (
5
)
are described in Section
3
. Finally, in Section
4
we put forth and discuss a conjecture about an extension of the results of Section
3
.

2
Combinatorial minimization

The main result of this section is stated in the forthcoming Theorem
1
. For its statement, we need some definitions:

Definition 1

Let
0<a<b
be some integers and
I_{a,b}\equiv\{a,a+1,\ldots,b\}
.
Then,
j\in I_{a,b}
is a middle point of the set
I_{a,b}
if and only if

\left\lfloor\frac{a+b}{2}\right\rfloor\leq j\leq\left\lceil\frac{a+b}{2}\right\rceil\,.

(14)

Remark 1

Note that once
b-a
is odd, there is a unique middle point of
I_{a,b}
. In addition, if
b-a
is even, there are two middle points of
I_{a,b}
.

Definition 2

Assume that
w\in(0,nx)
,
m\equiv\left\lfloor\frac{w}{x}\right\rfloor
and
r\equiv w-mx
.
Then, for every positive integer
\delta\leq n+1
, let
\Gamma_{\delta}\equiv\Gamma_{\delta}(x,w)
be the set of solutions
\boldsymbol{v}=(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n})
with

v_{i}\equiv\begin{cases}x&i\in\{\Delta_{0},\Delta_{0}+\Delta_{1},\ldots,\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\Delta_{k}\}\\
r&i=j\\
0&\text{o.w.}\end{cases}

(15)

such that:

•

\Delta_{0},\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{m}
are positive integers for which:

1.

\sum_{k=0}^{m}\Delta_{k}=n+1
.

2.

\max\left\{\Delta_{k};0\leq k\leq m\right\}=\delta
.

3.

t
is some element of
\argmax\left\{\Delta_{k};0\leq k\leq m\right\}
and

\left|\Delta_{k_{1}}-\Delta_{k_{2}}\right|\leq 1
, for every
k_{1},k_{2}\in\{0,1,\ldots,m\}\setminus\{t\}
.

•

j
is a middle element of the set

\left\{\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}\Delta_{k}+1,\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}\Delta_{k}+2,\ldots,\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}\Delta_{k}+\delta-1\right\}\,.

(16)

Remark 2

When
w<x
,
m=0
, and
\Gamma_{\delta}
includes all solutions of the form (
15
) which are parameterized with

\Delta_{0}=\delta=n+1
,
t=0
, and
j
which is a middle point
of
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
.

Note that the conditions
w\in(0,nx)
and
x>0
imply that
n\geq 1
. In particular, when
n=1
we know that
w<x
, such that there is a unique optimal solution of (
8
) which equals
\boldsymbol{v}=w
. Observe that,
as explained in Remark
2
,
it is the only element of
\Gamma_{2}
.
Theorem
1
below refers to the case
n\geq 2
.

Definition 3

Let
a<b
be two positive integers. In addition, assume that
\Delta_{0}(a,b),\Delta_{1}(a,b),\ldots,\Delta_{a-1}(a,b)
are positive integers whose sum is
b
and satisfy the condition:

\max_{0\leq k_{1},k_{2}\leq a-1}\left|\Delta_{k_{1}}(a,b)-\Delta_{k_{2}}(a,b)\right|\leq 1\,.

(17)

Then, define

\displaystyle h(a,b)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{a-1}\Delta_{k}(a,b)\left[\Delta_{k}(a,b)-1\right]

(18)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\{\left(b\ \text{mod}\ a\right)\left(\left\lfloor\frac{b}{a}\right\rfloor+1\right)\left\lfloor\frac{b}{a}\right\rfloor+\left[a-\left(b\ \text{mod}\ a\right)\right]\left\lfloor\frac{b}{a}\right\rfloor\left(\left\lfloor\frac{b}{a}\right\rfloor-1\right)\right\}\,.

(19)

Now we are ready to state the main result:

Theorem 1

Assume that
w<nx
and
n\geq 2
. In addition, denote
m\equiv\left\lfloor\frac{w}{x}\right\rfloor
and
r\equiv w-mx
.

1.

If
m=0
, the set of optimal solutions of (
8
) is
\text{Conv}(\Gamma_{n+1})

(see also Remark
2
).

2.

If
m\geq 1
, define two functions:

A(\delta)\equiv A(\delta;r)\equiv x\left[h\left(m,n\!+\!1\!-\!\delta\right)+\frac{1}{2}\delta(\delta\!-\!1)\right]-r\left\lfloor\frac{\delta}{2}\right\rfloor\left\lceil\frac{\delta}{2}\right\rceil\,,

(20)

\phi(\delta)\equiv\left(2\delta+1\right)\left(x-\frac{r}{2}\right)-x\left(\left\lfloor\frac{n-\delta-1}{m}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{n-\delta}{m}\right\rfloor\right)-\frac{r}{2}\,.

(21)

In addition, denote

\displaystyle\delta_{1}

\displaystyle\equiv\min\{\delta\in\{1,3,5,\ldots\}\,;\,\phi(\delta)>0\}\,,

(22)

\displaystyle\delta_{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\min\{\delta\in\{2,4,6,\ldots\}\,;\,\phi(\delta)>0\}\,.

(23)

and set

Then,

\text{Conv}\left(\Gamma_{\delta^{*}}\right)\subseteq\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\Upsilon}f(\boldsymbol{v})\,.

2.1
Implementation of Theorem
1

When either
m=0
or
r=0
, Theorem
1
includes a precise construction of an optimal solution of the combinatorial minimization (
8
) which is straightforward to implement. Otherwise,
when
m\geq 1
and
r>0
, Theorem
1
implies that the derivation of an optimal solution of (
8
) boils down to the computations of
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
which are respectively given in
(
22
) and (
23
). In what follows, it is explained how to execute these computations.

To begin with, observe that
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
are
bounded by the solutions of the next two equations in
\delta
:

\left(2\delta+1\right)\left(x-\frac{r}{2}\right)-x\left(\frac{n-\delta-1}{m}+\frac{n-\delta}{m}\right)-\frac{r}{2}=\pm 2\,,

(26)

i.e., if we apply the notations

\delta_{-}\equiv\frac{\frac{r}{2}+x\frac{2n-1-m}{2m}-1}{x\left(1+\frac{1}{m}\right)-\frac{r}{2}}\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \delta_{+}\equiv\frac{\frac{r}{2}+x\frac{2n-1-m}{2m}+1}{x\left(1+\frac{1}{m}\right)-\frac{r}{2}}\,,

(27)

then we shall deduce that
\delta_{-}\leq\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\leq\delta_{+}
.
This means that the search for
\delta_{1}
(resp.
\delta_{2}
) may be limited to a set of integers

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{1}\equiv\left\{\left\lceil\delta_{-}\right\rceil,\left\lceil\delta_{-}\right\rceil+1,\ldots,\left\lfloor\delta_{+}\right\rfloor\right\}\cap\left\{1,3,5,\ldots\right\}\,,

(28)

\displaystyle\left(\text{resp. }\mathcal{D}_{2}\equiv\left\{\left\lceil\delta_{-}\right\rceil,\left\lceil\delta_{-}\right\rceil+1,\ldots,\left\lfloor\delta_{+}\right\rfloor\right\}\cap\left\{2,6,8,\ldots\right\}\right)\,.

Lastly, once

3
Continuous minimization

3.1
When
r=0

The forthcoming Theorem
2
reveals that once
r=0
, the class of optimal solutions of (

) which is stated in Theorem
1
is actually optimal in the broader (compact) minimization domain
\Lambda
defined in (

). Before providing its statement, we present the following useful notations:

\tau_{u}\equiv\tau_{u}(n,m)\equiv\left\lceil\frac{n+1}{m+1}\right\rceil\ \ ,\ \ \tau_{l}\equiv\tau_{l}(n,m)\equiv\left\lfloor\frac{n+1}{m+1}\right\rfloor\,.

(43)

Theorem 2

If
r=0
, i.e.,
w=mx
for some positive integer

, then,

\text{Conv}\left(\Gamma_{\tau_{u}}\right)\subseteq\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\Lambda}f(\boldsymbol{v})

(44)

and

\min_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\Lambda}f(\boldsymbol{v})=\left(\tau_{u}-1\right)\left(x(n+1)-\frac{1}{2}(w+x)\tau_{u}\right)

(45)

Remark 3

Note that construction of some vector in
\Gamma_{\tau_{u}}
is not computationally demanding. Therefore, when
r=0
, Theorem
2
yields a natural numerical procedure which is easy to implement in order to solve the continuous minimization (
5
).

Figure 3:
Representing
f(\cdot)

as the sum of the values of the individual balls when the parameters are
n=9
,
x=1.1
and
w=2.2
. For each

, the function

, and a maximum gap of

Proof:

Let

\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
be an element of
\Gamma_{\tau_{u}}
. In addition, for any
1\leq j\leq n
and
\boldsymbol{v}\in\Lambda
define

L_{j}\equiv\left\{(k,\ell)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}\ ;\ k\leq\ell\leq n\ ,\ \ell-k+1=j\right\}\,,

(46)

and

\displaystyle f_{j}(\boldsymbol{v})\equiv\sum_{(k,\ell)\in L_{j}}\left(x-\sum_{i=k}^{\ell}v_{i}\right)^{+}\,,

(47)

such that

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{v})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}f_{j}(\boldsymbol{v})\,.

(48)

In what follows, we will prove that

f_{j}(\boldsymbol{v})\geq f_{j}(\boldsymbol{v}^{*})
for any

1\leq j\leq n
and
\boldsymbol{v}\in\Lambda
, thereby
providing an explicit expression
for
f_{j}(\boldsymbol{v^{*}})
.
This will end the proof since
f(\cdot)
is convex,
and the minimum objective function is found through
(
48
) with input
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
.

To begin with, as illustrated in in Figure
3
,
observe that

\max\left\{i_{2}-i_{1};1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}\leq n\ \ \text{s.t.}\ v_{i_{1}}^{*}=v_{i_{2}}^{*}=x,v_{i_{1}+1}^{*}=\ldots=v_{i_{2}-1}^{*}=0\right\}

(49)

is bounded from above by
\tau_{u}
. Consequently,
f_{j}(\boldsymbol{v}^{*})=0
for every
j\geq\tau_{u}
, i.e., it is left to consider
1\leq j\leq\tau_{u}-1
. To this end, consider some
1\leq j\leq\tau_{u}-1
and recall that

\min\left\{i_{2}-i_{1};1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}\leq n\ \ \text{s.t.}\ v_{i_{1}}^{*}=v_{i_{2}}^{*}=x\right\}=\tau_{l}\geq\tau_{u}-1\,.

(50)

Thus, since
\sum_{i=k}^{\ell}v_{i}^{*}\leq x
for every
(k,\ell)\in L_{j}
, deduce that

\left|\left\{(k,\ell)\in L_{j}\ ;\ \sum_{i=k}^{\ell}v_{i}^{*}=x\right\}\right|=jm

(51)

and hence

\displaystyle f_{j}(\boldsymbol{v}^{*})

\displaystyle=\sum_{(k,\ell)\in L_{j}}\left(x-\sum_{i=k}^{\ell}v_{i}^{*}\right)

(52)

\displaystyle=(n\!+\!1\!-\!j)x-\sum_{(k,\ell)\in L_{j}}\sum_{i=k}^{\ell}v_{i}^{*}

\displaystyle=(n\!+\!1\!-\!j)x-jmx

\displaystyle=(n\!+\!1\!-\!j)x-jw\,.

\boldsymbol{v}\in\Lambda(m)\cap[0,x]^{n}
and observe that

\displaystyle f_{j}(\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{(k,\ell)\in L_{j}}\left(x-\sum_{i=k}^{\ell}v_{i}\right)=(n+1-j)x-\sum_{(k,\ell)\in L_{j}}\sum_{i=k}^{\ell}v_{i}\,.

(53)

\left|\left\{(k,\ell)\in L_{j};k\leq i\leq\ell\right\}\right|\leq j

and hence

\sum_{(k,\ell)\in L_{j}}\sum_{i=k}^{\ell}v_{i}\leq jw\,,

(55)

from which the result follows.

4
Conjecture

It is natural to look for a unifying generalization of Theorem
2
and
3
. The following Conjecture
1
has been verified in an enormous number of numerical examples using Mathematica.

Conjecture 1

Denote
y\equiv y(x,w)\equiv x-r
,

\tau_{1}\equiv\tau_{u}(n,m)
,
\tau_{2}\equiv\tau_{u}(n,m+1)
,
and
consider any two vectors
\boldsymbol{v}_{r}\in\Gamma_{\tau_{1}}\left(r,\left(m+1\right)r\right)
and
\boldsymbol{v}_{y}\in\Gamma_{\tau_{2}}\left(y,my\right)
.
Then,

\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\equiv\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\left(\boldsymbol{v}_{y},\boldsymbol{v}_{r}\right)\equiv\boldsymbol{v}_{y}+\boldsymbol{v}_{r}

(62)

is an optimal solution of (
5
).

Remark 5

Observe that when
r=0
, Conjecture
1
becomes Theorem
2
.
Moreover, note that the case
m\geq\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
is trivial,
as
f_{j}(\boldsymbol{v}^{*})=0
for any
j\geq 0
, and
f_{1}(\boldsymbol{v})

has the same value for each
\boldsymbol{v}\in\Lambda\cap[0,x]^{n}
.
Therefore, Theorem
3
implies that it is left to prove Conjecture
1
under the assumptions
0<r<x
,

m<\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
, and
\tau_{u}(n,m)\neq\tau_{u}(n,m+1)
.

Remark 6

Note that if Conjecture
1
is true, the convexity of
f(\cdot)
and the convexity of
\Lambda
imply that the resulting convex hull is a subset of optimal solutions.

Remark 7

Observe that the construction of
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
is based on optimal solutions of the combinatorial minimization (
8
). Therefore, we conjecture that the reduction of the continuous minimization (
5
) to the combinatorial minimization (
8
) is valid for any parameterization.

Prominently, the incomplete part of this research is the proof of Conjecture
1
. We anticipate that its proof should follow from (at least) one of the following directions: (1) Phrasing the problem as a dynamic program which can be solved by an application of Theorem
2
, or (2) applying the theory about Lagrange multipliers in the context of sub-differential calculus (see, e.g.,
[
37
]
). Our experience teaches that while both of these approaches look reasonable, they are not straightforward to apply. Therefore, we consider providing the proof of Conjecture
1
as an open problem of different magnitude
that goes beyond the scope of the current research. Hopefully, it will be solved in the future.
