On the Complexity of Rational Verification
Julian Gutierrez, Muhammad Najib, Giuseppe Perelli, Michael Wooldridge

Abstract
Rational verification refers to the problem of checking which temporal logic properties hold of a concurrent/multiagent system, under the assumption that agents in the system choose strategies that form a game theoretic equilibrium. Rational verification can be understood as a counterpart to model checking for multiagent systems, but while classical model checking can be done in polynomial time for some temporal logic specification languages such as 𝖢𝖳𝖫 {\mathsf{CTL}} , and polynomial space with 𝖫𝖳𝖫 {\mathsf{LTL}} specifications, rational verification is much harder: the key decision problems for rational verification are 2 𝖤𝖷𝖯𝖳𝖨𝖬𝖤 {\mathsf{EXPTIME}} -complete with 𝖫𝖳𝖫 {\mathsf{LTL}} specifications, even when using explicit-state system representations. Against this background, our contributions in this paper are threefold. First, we show that the complexity of rational verification can be greatly reduced by restricting specifications to 𝖦𝖱 ⁡ ( 𝟣 ) {\mathsf{GR(1)}} , a fragment of 𝖫𝖳𝖫 {\mathsf{LTL}} that can represent a broad and practically useful class of response properties of reactive systems. In particular, we show that for a number of relevant settings, rational verification can be done in polynomial space and even in polynomial time. Second, we provide improved complexity results for rational verification when considering players’ goals given by mean-payoff utility functions—arguably the most widely used approach for quantitative objectives in concurrent and multiagent systems. Finally, we consider the problem of computing outcomes that satisfy social welfare constraints. To this end, we consider both utilitarian and egalitarian social welfare and show that computing such outcomes is either 𝖯𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤 {\mathsf{PSPACE}} -complete or 𝖭𝖯 {\mathsf{NP}} -complete.

Introduction

The formal verification of computer systems has been a major research
area in computer science for the past 60 years. Verification is the
problem of checking program correctness: the key decision problem
relating to verification is that of establishing whether or not a
given system
P

contemporary approach to formal verification is model checking, in

is
represented as a Kripke structure

(a labelled transition
system), and the specification is represented as a temporal logic
formula
\varphi

“correct” behaviours of the system

. The
verification process then reduces to establishing whether the
specification formula
\varphi
is satisfied in the Kripke structure

K_{P}
(notation:
K_{P}\models\varphi
), a process that can be
efficiently automated in many settings of interest

example, model checking Linear Temporal Logic (
{\mathsf{LTL}}
) specifications
can be done in polynomial space, and for specifications in
Computation Tree Logic (
{\mathsf{CTL}}
) it can be done in polynomial
time

.

In the context of multiagent systems,
rational verification

forms a natural counterpart of model

;
WooldridgeGHMPT16
;
GutierrezHW17-aij
.
This is the problem of checking whether a given property

expressed as a temporal logic formula, is satisfied in a computation
of a system that might be generated
if agents within the system
choose strategies for selecting actions that form a game-theoretic
equilibrium
. This game theoretic aspect of rational verification
adds a new ingredient to the verification problem, as it becomes
necessary to take into account the

{\mathsf{LTL}}

with each player

In this setting, rational verification with respect to a specification

Our aim in this work is to improve this state of affairs: we present a range of

Related Work

The rational verification problem has been studied for a number of different settings, including iterated Boolean games, reactive modules games, and concurrent game structures

;
GutierrezHW17-aij
;
GHW15
;

.
In each of these settings, the main rational verification problems are 2
{\mathsf{EXPTIME}}
-complete, and hence highly intractable. Rational verification is closely related to rational synthesis, which is also 2
{\mathsf{EXPTIME}}
-complete both in the Boolean case

;

and turn-based setting

;

.

Our work also relates to
{\mathsf{LTL}}
and mean-payoff (

) games in

-complete even for two-player

and in

Linear Temporal Logic.

{\mathsf{LTL}}
extends propositional
logic with two operators,
\mathbf{X}
(“next”) and
\mathbf{U}

(‘‘until’’), for expressing properties of
paths

;

The syntax of

is defined with respect to a set

of atomic propositions as follows:

\phi::=\mathop{\top}\mid p\mid\neg\phi\mid\phi\vee\phi\mid\mbox{\small\boldmath$\mathbf{X}$}\phi\mid\phi\,\mbox{\small\boldmath$\mathbf{U}$}\,\phi

where

.
As usual, we define
\phi_{1}\wedge\phi_{2}\equiv\neg(\neg\phi_{1}\vee\neg\phi_{2})
,
\phi_{1}\to\phi_{2}\equiv\neg\phi_{1}\vee\phi_{2}
,
\mbox{\small\boldmath$\mathbf{F}$}\phi\equiv\mathop{\top}\,\mbox{\small\boldmath$\mathbf{U}$}\,\phi
, and
\mbox{\small\boldmath$\mathbf{G}$}\phi\equiv\neg\mbox{\small\boldmath$\mathbf{F}$}\neg\phi
.
We interpret
{\mathsf{LTL}}
formulae with respect to pairs
(\alpha,t)
, where
\alpha\in(2^{{{\mathrm{AP}}}})^{\omega}
is an infinite sequence of sets of atomic proposition that indicates which propositional variables are true in every time point and

is a temporal index into
\alpha
.
As usual, by
\alpha_{t}\in 2^{{{\mathrm{AP}}}}
we denote the
t
-th element of the infinite sequence
\alpha
.
Formally, the semantics of
{\mathsf{LTL}}
is given by the following rules:

\begin{array}[]{lcl}(\alpha,t)\models\mathop{\top}\\
(\alpha,t)\models p&\text{ iff }&p\in\alpha_{t}\\
(\alpha,t)\models\neg\phi&\text{ iff }&\text{it is not the case that $(\alpha,t)\models\phi$}\\
(\alpha,t)\models\phi\vee\psi&\text{ iff }&\text{$(\alpha,t)\models\phi$ or $(\alpha,t)\models\psi$}\\
(\alpha,t)\models\mbox{\small\boldmath$\mathbf{X}$}\phi&\text{ iff }&\text{$(\alpha,t+1)\models\phi$}\\
(\alpha,t)\models\phi\,\mbox{\small\boldmath$\mathbf{U}$}\,\psi&\text{ iff }&\text{for some $t^{\prime}\geq t:\ \big((\alpha,t^{\prime})\models\psi$ and }\\
&&\quad\text{for all $t\leq t^{\prime\prime}<t^{\prime}:\ (\alpha,t^{\prime\prime})\models\phi\big)$.}\\
\end{array}

If
(\alpha,0)\models\phi
, we write
\alpha\models\phi
and say that

satisfies

.

The language of

The special case of
{\mathsf{GR(1)}}
specifications.

As in the case of
{\mathsf{GR(1)}}
games, here we show that restricting the specification language to
{\mathsf{GR(1)}}
also lowers the complexity for
mp
games. The reason for this is that the path finding problem for
{\mathsf{GR(1)}}
specifications can be done while avoiding model-checking an
{{\mathsf{LTL}}}^{{{\mathsf{Lim}}}}
formula.
In order to do this, we follow a different approach. Using an
mp
game
{{\mathcal{G}}}
and a
{\mathsf{GR(1)}}
specification
\phi
we define a linear program such that the linear program has a solution if and only if the pair
({{\mathcal{G}}},\phi)
is an instance of
{\mathsf{E\mbox{-}Nash}}
. In particular, this approach is similar to the technique used in
(
GMPRW17
, Theorem 2)
, where Linear Programming is used to find the complexity of solving a variant of
{\mathsf{E\mbox{-}Nash}}
.
Formally, we have the following result.

Theorem 4.2

The
{\mathsf{E\mbox{-}Nash}}
problem for
mp
games with a
{\mathsf{GR(1)}}
specification
\varphi
is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete.

Proof

We will define a linear program of size polynomial in
{{\mathcal{G}}}
having a solution if and only if there exists an ultimately periodic path whose payoff for every player
i
is at least a minimum threshold
z_{i}
and satisfies the
{\mathsf{GR(1)}}
specification.

In order to do that, first recall that
\varphi
has the following form

\varphi=\bigwedge_{l=1}^{m}\mbox{\small\boldmath$\mathbf{G}$}\mbox{\small\boldmath$\mathbf{F}$}\psi_{l}\to\bigwedge_{r=1}^{n}\mbox{\small\boldmath$\mathbf{G}$}\mbox{\small\boldmath$\mathbf{F}$}\theta_{r}\text{,}

and let
V(\psi_{l})
and
V(\theta_{r})
be the subset of states in
{{\mathcal{G}}}
that satisfy the Boolean combinations
\psi_{l}
and
\theta_{r}
, respectively.
Observe that property
\varphi
is satisfied over a path
\pi
if, and only if, either
\pi
visits every
V(\theta_{r})
infinitely many times or visits some of the
V(\psi_{l})
only a finite number of times.

For the game
{{\mathcal{G}}}{[z]}
, let
\!\langle V,E,({{\mathsf{w}}}_{i}^{\prime})_{i\in{{\mathrm{N}}}}\rangle
be the underlying graph, where
{{\mathsf{w}}}_{i}^{\prime}(v)={{\mathsf{w}}}_{i}(v)-z_{i}
for every
i\in{{\mathrm{N}}}
, and
v\in V\subseteq{{\mathrm{St}}}
.
Furthermore, for every edge
e\in E
, we introduce a variable
x_{e}
.
Informally, the value
x_{e}
is the number of times that the edge
e
is used on a cycle.
Formally, let:

•

{\sf src}(e)=\{v\in V:\exists w\,e=(v,w)\in E\}
;

•

{\sf trg}(e)=\{v\in V:\exists w\,e=(w,v)\in E\}
;

•

{\sf out}(v)=\{e\in E:{\sf src}(e)=v\}
;

•

{\sf in}(v)=\{e\in E:{\sf trg}(e)=v\}
.

Consider
\psi_{l}
for some
1\leq l\leq m
, and define the linear program
{{\mathsf{LP}}}(\psi_{l})
with the following inequalities and equations:

Eq1:

x_{e}\geq 0
for each edge
e

a basic consistency criterion;

Eq2:

\Sigma_{e\in E}x_{e}\geq 1

ensures that at least one edge is chosen;

Eq3:

for each
i\in{{\mathrm{N}}}
,
\Sigma_{e\in E}{{\mathsf{w}}}_{i}^{\prime}({\sf src}(e))x_{e}\geq 0

ensures that the total sum of any solution is positive;

Eq4:

\Sigma_{{\sf src}(e)\cap V(\psi_{l})\neq\emptyset}x_{e}=0

ensures that no state in
V(\psi_{l})
is in the cycle associated with the solution;

Eq5:

for each
v\in V
,
\Sigma_{e\in{\sf out}(v)}x_{e}=\Sigma_{e\in{\sf in}(v)}x_{e}

says that the number of times one enters a vertex is equal to the number of times one leaves that vertex.

By construction, it follows that
{{\mathsf{LP}}}(\psi_{l})
admits a solution if and only if there exists a path
\pi
in
{{\mathcal{G}}}
such that
z_{i}\leq{{\mathsf{pay}}}_{i}(\pi)
for every player
i
and visits
V(\psi_{l})
only
finitely many times
.
Note that the condition
z_{i}\leq{{\mathsf{pay}}}_{i}(\pi)
is ensured by Eq3.
Indeed, the value of a path
\pi
in
{{\mathcal{G}}}{[z]}
that is represented in a solution to
{{\mathsf{LP}}}(\psi_{l})
, and thus satisfying Eq3, is such that
0\leq{{\mathsf{pay}}}^{{{\mathcal{G}}}{[z]}}_{i}(\pi)
, with
pay^{{{\mathcal{G}}}{[z]}}_{i}
representing the payoff function for agent
i
in the game
{{\mathcal{G}}}{[z]}
.
Now observe that, as the weights in
{{\mathcal{G}}}{[z]}
are all downshifted by a value
z_{i}
for every agent

{{\mathsf{pay}}}_{i}(\pi)={{\mathsf{pay}}}^{{{\mathcal{G}}}{[z]}}_{i}(\pi)+z_{i}
, which in turns implies that
z_{i}\leq{{\mathsf{pay}}}_{i}(\pi)
.

Now, consider also the linear program
{{\mathsf{LP}}}(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n})
defined with the following inequalities and equations:

Eq1:

x_{e}\geq 0
for each edge
e

a basic consistency criterion;

Eq2:

\Sigma_{e\in E}x_{e}\geq 1

ensures that at least one edge is chosen;

Eq3:

for each
i\in{{\mathrm{N}}}
,
\Sigma_{e\in E}{{\mathsf{w}}}_{i}^{\prime}({\sf src}(e))x_{e}\geq 0

ensures that the total sum of any solution is positive;

Eq4:

for all

,
\Sigma_{{\sf src}(e)\cap V(\theta_{r})\neq\emptyset}x_{e}\geq 1

ensures that for every
V(\theta_{r})
at least one state is in the cycle;

Eq5:

for each
v\in V
,
\Sigma_{e\in{\sf out}(v)}x_{e}=\Sigma_{e\in{\sf in}(v)}x_{e}

says that the number of times one enters a vertex is equal to the number of times one leaves that vertex.

In this case,
{{\mathsf{LP}}}(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n})
admits a solution if and only if there exists a path

for every player

and visits every
V(\theta_{r})

infinitely many times
.

Since the constructions above are polynomial in the size of both
{{\mathcal{G}}}
and
\phi
, we can conclude it is possible to check in
{\mathsf{NP}}

\varphi
such that

for every player

in the game if and only if one of the two linear programs defined above has a solution. For the lower bound, we use
UW11
and observe that if
\phi

mp

5
Social welfare verification

Until this point, the problems considered primarily concerned about
the satisfaction of a temporal logic property
\varphi
over the game

{{\mathcal{G}}}
. However, one might be interested in achieving an outcome
that is somehow best also for the agent society. To capture this
setting, we introduce
social welfare
measures. Social welfare
measures are
aggregate
measures of utility. Thus, a social welfare
measure takes as input a profile of utilities, one for each player in
the game, and somehow aggregates these into an overall measure, indicating how
good the outcome is for society as a whole. Note that since social welfare is
inherently a quantitative measure, in this section we restrict our attention to
mp
games.

Formally, for a game
{{\mathcal{G}}}
with a set
N
of agents, a
social
welfare function

{{\mathsf{sw}}}
takes the form

{{\mathsf{sw}}}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}

Thus, a social welfare function maps a
N
-tuple of real numbers into a real number which represents the
aggregated payoff
. More specifically, for a
strategy profile
{{\mathnormal{\vec{\sigma}}}}
, the social welfare of
{{\mathnormal{\vec{\sigma}}}}
is given
by
{{\mathsf{sw}}}({{\mathsf{pay}}}_{1}({{\mathnormal{\vec{\sigma}}}}),\ldots,{{\mathsf{pay}}}_{N}({{\mathnormal{\vec{\sigma}}}}))
. With an
abuse of notation, we denote
{{\mathsf{sw}}}({{\mathnormal{\vec{\sigma}}}})
the social welfare of

{{\mathnormal{\vec{\sigma}}}}
. Many different social welfare functions have been
proposed in the literature of economic theory. Here, we confine out
attention to the two best known:
utilitarian
and

egalitarian
social welfare. These functions are defined as
follows:

•

The
utilitarian
social welfare function is given by
{{\mathsf{usw}}}({{\mathnormal{\vec{\sigma}}}})=\sum_{i\in N}{{\mathsf{pay}}}_{i}({{\mathnormal{\vec{\sigma}}}})
.

•

The
egalitarian
social welfare function is given by
{{\mathsf{esw}}}({{\mathnormal{\vec{\sigma}}}})=\min_{i\in N}\{{{\mathsf{pay}}}_{i}({{\mathnormal{\vec{\sigma}}}})\}
.

For simplicity, for a given game
{{\mathcal{G}}}
and a formula
\varphi
, by
{{\mathsf{E\mbox{-}Nash}}}_{{{\mathcal{G}}}}(\varphi)=\{{{\mathnormal{\vec{\sigma}}}}\in{{\mathrm{NE}}}\allowbreak:\allowbreak\pi({{\mathnormal{\vec{\sigma}}}})\models\varphi\}
we denote the set of Nash equilibria that satisfy
\varphi
, that is, that are a solution to the
{\mathsf{E\mbox{-}Nash}}
problem of
({{\mathcal{G}}},\varphi)
.
For a fixed social welfare function
{{\mathsf{sw}}}
on a game
{{\mathcal{G}}}
, by:

•

{{\mathsf{MaxNE}}}_{{{\mathsf{sw}}}}({{\mathcal{G}}},\varphi)=\max_{{{\mathnormal{\vec{\sigma}}}}\in{{\mathsf{E\mbox{-}Nash}}}_{{{\mathcal{G}}}}(\varphi)}\{{{\mathsf{sw}}}({{\mathnormal{\vec{\sigma}}}})\}
, and

•

{{\mathsf{MinNE}}}_{{{\mathsf{sw}}}}({{\mathcal{G}}},\varphi)=\min_{{{\mathnormal{\vec{\sigma}}}}\in{{\mathsf{E\mbox{-}Nash}}}_{{{\mathcal{G}}}}(\varphi)}\{{{\mathsf{sw}}}({{\mathnormal{\vec{\sigma}}}})\}

we denote the maximal and minimal social welfare achieved over a Nash equilibrium profile, respectively, satisfying a given specification
\varphi
.

The values of
{{\mathsf{MaxNE}}}
and
{{\mathsf{MinNE}}}
determine how good or bad
the
{\mathsf{E\mbox{-}Nash}}
solutions are from the perspective of the agents in the
game collectively. Here, we consider both the decision and function
problem.

Definition 2
(Threshold social welfare)

For a given
mp
game
{{\mathcal{G}}}_{{\small{\sf mp}}}
, a social welfare function
{{\mathsf{sw}}}
, and a threshold value
t
, decide whether there exists a strategy profile
{{\mathnormal{\vec{\sigma}}}}
in
{{\mathsf{E\mbox{-}Nash}}}_{{{\mathcal{G}}}}(\varphi)
such that
t\leq{{\mathsf{sw}}}({{\mathnormal{\vec{\sigma}}}})
.
In case of a positive answer to this decision question, the pair
({{\mathcal{G}}},\varphi)
is called
t
-increase
.

Analogously, decide whether there exists a strategy profile
{{\mathnormal{\vec{\sigma}}}}
in
{{\mathsf{E\mbox{-}Nash}}}_{{{\mathcal{G}}}}(\varphi)
such that
t\geq{{\mathsf{sw}}}({{\mathnormal{\vec{\sigma}}}})
.
In case of a positive answer to this decision question, the pair
({{\mathcal{G}}},\varphi)
is called
t
-decrease
.

Definition 3
(Max and Min social welfare)

For a given
mp
game
{{\mathcal{G}}}_{{\small{\sf mp}}}
and a social welfare function
{{\mathsf{sw}}}
, compute
{{\mathsf{MaxNE}}}_{{{\mathsf{sw}}}}({{\mathcal{G}}},\varphi)
and
{{\mathsf{MinNE}}}_{{{\mathsf{sw}}}}({{\mathcal{G}}},\varphi)
.

The two definitions above can be instantiated with many different social welfare functions. In the following two subsections, we consider them in the context of the utilitarian and egalitarian welfare measures defined above.

5.1
Social welfare computation with
{\mathsf{LTL}}

We first show how to check that a given

For the utilitarian function, we have the following.

Proof

It is enough to show the case
t\leq{{\mathsf{usw}}}({{\mathnormal{\vec{\sigma}}}})
as the other one is similar.
The solution is a slight modification of the
{\mathsf{E\mbox{-}Nash}}
problem for
mp
games with
{\mathsf{LTL}}
specifications.
Consider the arena
A^{\prime}=\!\langle{{\mathrm{N}}}\cup\{n+1\},{{\mathrm{Ac}}},{{\mathrm{St}}},s_{0},{{\mathsf{tr}}}^{\prime},\lambda\rangle

defined as

for every

Indeed, observe that, for every strategy profile
{{\mathnormal{\vec{\sigma}}}}
in
{{\mathcal{G}}}_{{\small{\sf mp}}}^{\prime}

{\mathsf{LTL}}

It suffices to replace the
{\mathsf{LTL^{Lim}}}

\varphi_{{{\mathsf{E\mbox{-}Nash}}}}^{{{\mathsf{usw}}},t}:=\varphi_{{{\mathsf{E\mbox{-}Nash}}}}\wedge{\small{\sf mp}}(n+1)\geq t\text{.}

{\mathsf{PSPACE}}
as for
{\mathsf{E\mbox{-}Nash}}
.
The lower bound easily follows from the model checking of
{\mathsf{LTL}}
. ∎

For the case of egalitarian social welfare, we have the following.

Theorem 5.2

For a given
mp
game
{{\mathcal{G}}}_{{\small{\sf mp}}}=\!\langle A,({{\mathsf{w}}}_{i})_{i\in{{\mathrm{N}}}}\rangle

{\mathsf{LTL}}

-complete.
Analogously, deciding whether there exists a strategy profile
{{\mathnormal{\vec{\sigma}}}}\in{{\mathsf{E\mbox{-}Nash}}}_{{{\mathcal{G}}}}(\varphi)

is
{\mathsf{PSPACE}}
-complete.

Proof

It is enough to show the case
t\leq{{\mathsf{esw}}}({{\mathnormal{\vec{\sigma}}}})
as the other one is similar.
As for the case of utilitarian social welfare functions, the solution is a slight modification of the
{\mathsf{E\mbox{-}Nash}}
problem for
mp
games with
{\mathsf{LTL}}
specifications.
Indeed, observe that we can specify that the payoff of agent

is greater than the threshold
t

{\mathsf{LTL^{Lim}}}

{\small{\sf mp}}(i)\geq t

Therefore, specifying that the egalitarian social welfare is at least

\bigwedge_{i\in{{\mathrm{N}}}}{\small{\sf mp}}(i)\geq t

Thus, it suffice to replace the
{\mathsf{LTL^{Lim}}}

\varphi_{{{\mathsf{E\mbox{-}Nash}}}}

\varphi_{{{\mathsf{E\mbox{-}Nash}}}}^{{{\mathsf{esw}}},t}:=\varphi_{{{\mathsf{E\mbox{-}Nash}}}}\wedge\bigwedge_{i\in{{\mathrm{N}}}}{\small{\sf mp}}(i)\geq t\text{.}

Again, the computational complexity of the procedure is
{\mathsf{PSPACE}}

5.2
Social welfare computation with
{\mathsf{GR(1)}}
specifications

In this section, we address social welfare threshold problems with
{\mathsf{GR(1)}}
specifications. The techniques are similar to the ones used in the case of
{\mathsf{LTL}}
specifications. Firstly, we consider the utilitarian social welfare function. For a given
mp
game
{{\mathcal{G}}}_{{\small{\sf mp}}}=\!\langle A,({{\mathsf{w}}}_{i})_{i\in{{\mathrm{N}}}}\rangle
, we build the arena
A^{\prime}
and the game
{{\mathcal{G}}}_{{\small{\sf mp}}}^{\prime}
analogous to the way it is done in the proof of Theorem
5.1
. Now, to solve the case
t\leq{{\mathsf{usw}}}({{\mathnormal{\vec{\sigma}}}})
, we adapt the procedure for solving
{\mathsf{E\mbox{-}Nash}}
for
mp
games with
{\mathsf{GR(1)}}
specifications (Theorem
4.2
) as follows. We construct the corresponding multi-weighted graph
W=\!\langle V,E,({{\mathsf{w}}}_{i}^{\prime})_{i\in{{\mathrm{N}}}\cup{n+1}}\rangle
where
{{\mathsf{w}}}_{n+1}^{\prime}(v)={{\mathsf{w}}}_{n+1}(s)-t
. Then, solving
{\mathsf{E\mbox{-}Nash}}
problem for such an instance corresponds exactly to the threshold social welfare problem
t\leq{{\mathsf{usw}}}({{\mathnormal{\vec{\sigma}}}})
. For the case

, we simply define
{{\mathsf{w}}}_{n+1}^{\prime}(v)=t-{{\mathsf{w}}}_{n+1}(s)
. To obtain the lower bounds, we reduce from the
{\mathsf{E\mbox{-}Nash}}

{\mathsf{GR(1)}}
specifications. For the case

, we set

, and the other case, we fix

. Thus, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 5.3

For a given
mp
game
{{\mathcal{G}}}_{{\small{\sf mp}}}=\!\langle A,({{\mathsf{w}}}_{i})_{i\in{{\mathrm{N}}}}\rangle
, a
{\mathsf{GR(1)}}
specification
\varphi
, and a threshold value

, deciding whether there exists a strategy profile

such that

{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete.
Analogously, deciding whether there exists a strategy profile
{{\mathnormal{\vec{\sigma}}}}\in{{\mathsf{E\mbox{-}Nash}}}_{{{\mathcal{G}}}}(\varphi)
such that
t\geq{{\mathsf{usw}}}({{\mathnormal{\vec{\sigma}}}})
is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete.

Now we turn our attention to the egalitarian social welfare function. To solve the social threshold problem
t\leq{{\mathsf{esw}}}({{\mathnormal{\vec{\sigma}}}})
, we directly adapt from the procedure for solving
{\mathsf{E\mbox{-}Nash}}
for
mp
games with
{\mathsf{GR(1)}}
specifications (Theorem
4.2
). For the game
{{\mathcal{G}}}{[z]}
, we build the underlying graph
\!\langle V,E,({{\mathsf{w}}}_{i}^{\prime})_{i\in{{\mathrm{N}}}}\rangle
where
{{\mathsf{w}}}_{i}^{\prime}(v)={{\mathsf{w}}}_{i}(s)-(\max\{z_{i},t\})
. Then we define the linear programs
{{\mathsf{LP}}}(\psi_{l})
and
{{\mathsf{LP}}}(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n})
in the same way. Observe that, one of the two linear programs has a solution if and only if there is a path

satisfying

such that for every player

z_{i}\leq{{\mathsf{pay}}}_{i}(\pi)
and
t\leq{{\mathsf{pay}}}_{i}(\pi)
. To obtain the lower bound, again, we reduce from the
{\mathsf{E\mbox{-}Nash}}

{\mathsf{GR(1)}}
specifications. The reduction simply follows from the fact that by fixing

, we can encode

Theorem 5.4

For a given

{{\mathcal{G}}}_{{\small{\sf mp}}}=\!\langle A,({{\mathsf{w}}}_{i})_{i\in{{\mathrm{N}}}}\rangle
, a
{\mathsf{GR(1)}}

\varphi
, and a threshold value

, deciding whether there exists a strategy profile
{{\mathnormal{\vec{\sigma}}}}\in{{\mathsf{E\mbox{-}Nash}}}_{{{\mathcal{G}}}}(\varphi)
such that
t\leq{{\mathsf{esw}}}({{\mathnormal{\vec{\sigma}}}})

{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete.
Analogously, deciding whether there exists a strategy profile
{{\mathnormal{\vec{\sigma}}}}\in{{\mathsf{E\mbox{-}Nash}}}_{{{\mathcal{G}}}}(\varphi)
such that
t\geq{{\mathsf{esw}}}({{\mathnormal{\vec{\sigma}}}})
is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete.

The threshold social welfare calculation can be used to approximate the
{\mathsf{MaxNE}}
and
{\mathsf{MinNE}}
values of a game, be it either utilitarian or egalitarian.
Note that, for every agent
i\in{{\mathrm{N}}}
and every strategy profile
{{\mathnormal{\vec{\sigma}}}}
in the game, it holds that

\min({{\mathsf{w}}}_{i})=\min_{s\in{{\mathrm{St}}}}\{{{\mathsf{w}}}_{i}(s)\}\leq{{\mathsf{pay}}}_{i}({{\mathnormal{\vec{\sigma}}}})\leq\max_{s\in{{\mathrm{St}}}}\{{{\mathsf{w}}}_{i}(s)\}=\max({{\mathsf{w}}}_{i})\text{.}

This establishes a bound also on the social welfare function, which is given by

\sum_{i\in{{\mathrm{N}}}}\min({{\mathsf{w}}}_{i})\leq{{\mathsf{MinNE}}}_{{{\mathsf{sw}}}}({{\mathcal{G}}},\varphi)\leq{{\mathsf{MaxNE}}}_{{{\mathsf{sw}}}}({{\mathcal{G}}},\varphi)\leq\sum_{i\in{{\mathrm{N}}}}\max({{\mathsf{w}}}_{i})\text{.}

Moreover, observe that, for two values

, if
({{\mathcal{G}}},\varphi)
is
t
-increase but not

-increase, then it holds that
t\leq{{\mathsf{MaxNE}}}_{{{\mathsf{sw}}}}({{\mathcal{G}}},\varphi)<t^{\prime}
.
Analogously, if
({{\mathcal{G}}},\varphi)
is
t^{\prime}
-decrease, but not
t
-decrease, then it holds that
t\leq{{\mathsf{MinNE}}}_{{{\mathsf{sw}}}}({{\mathcal{G}}},\varphi)<t^{\prime}
.

These observations allow to apply a bisection-like method to approximate
{{\mathsf{MaxNE}}}
and
{{\mathsf{MinNE}}}
.
Moreover, note that at each iteration of the method, the absolute error is halved, which ensures linear convergence of the method
Sik82
.
Particularly, we obtain an approximation of the values within a fixed tolerance
\epsilon>0
in a number
n
of iterations bounded by
n_{\epsilon}=\lceil\log_{2}(\frac{b-a}{\epsilon})\rceil
, with
a=\sum_{i\in{{\mathrm{N}}}}\min({{\mathsf{w}}}_{i})
and
b=\sum_{i\in{{\mathrm{N}}}}\max({{\mathsf{w}}}_{i})
.

6
Other Rational Verification Problems

{\mathsf{E\mbox{-}Nash}}
is, we believe, the most fundamental problem in the rational
verification framework, but it is not the only one. The two other key
problems are
{\mathsf{A\mbox{-}Nash}}
and
{\mathsf{Non\mbox{-}emptiness}}
. The former is the dual
problem of
{\mathsf{E\mbox{-}Nash}}
, which asks, given a game
{{\mathcal{G}}}
and a
specification
\phi
, whether
\phi
is satisfied in
all
Nash
equilibria of
{{\mathcal{G}}}
. The latter simply asks whether the game

{{\mathcal{G}}}
has at least one Nash equilibrium, and it can be thought of as the special case of
{\mathsf{E\mbox{-}Nash}}
where the specification
\phi
is any tautology.

We can conclude from (the proofs of) the results presented so far, which are summarised in Table
1
, that while
{\mathsf{A\mbox{-}Nash}}
for
{\mathsf{GR(1)}}
games is also
{\mathsf{PSPACE}}

7
Concluding Remarks

We have presented improved complexity results for rational verification problems in three different settings: in the analysis of response properties of reactive systems modelled as multiagent systems; verification of mean-payoff games; and verification of collective properties of multiagent systems through the analysis of social welfare properties. The first scenario mostly concerns the verification of qualitative properties of reactive systems; the second the verification of quantitative properties; and the third the verification of “community” properties, as opposed to individual properties of agents in a system. In the remainder of this article, we discuss further the impact and relevance of our results in these three areas.

Reactive systems

The logical analysis of reactive systems is typically carried out using either linear temporal logics, such as
{\mathsf{LTL}}
, or branching time temporal logics, such as
{\mathsf{CTL}}
and
{\mathsf{CTL^{\ast}}}
. Such analysis may involve verifying that a temporal logic property holds in a given system (model checking) or automatically constructing the system from a temporal logic specification (automated synthesis). Rational verification subsumes both problems, and applies to systems modelled in a distributed way as a collection of semi-autonomous agents (a multiagent system). Despite the greater scope of rational verification with respect to both model checking and automated synthesis, previous work has shown that the overall complexity of rational verification is typically not higher/worse than the combined complexity of the associated synthesis problem. This connection also transfers when considering goals expressed in the
{\mathsf{GR(1)}}
fragment of LTL, where an initial solution in 2
{\mathsf{EXPTIME}}
is reduced to complexities lying in the polynomial hierarchy. However, to do so, careful attention must be paid to how the additional game-theoretic analysis that rational verification entails must be done without blowing up the combined computational complexity. This is particularly important since, in rational verification, strategies for multiple agents must be synthesised, rather than a single model for a reactive system.

Mean-Payoff games

In the computer science literature, mean-payoff games have been considered as a way of understanding the long-term behaviour (the average performance) of a system—the most common setting is that of a two-player game in which one of the players model the system and the other player models the environment. From a game-theoretic point of view, these are two-player games, which in a perfect information setting can be solved in NP
{}\cup{}
coNP, thus without a known polynomial time algorithm to solve them. In case of rational verification with mean-payoff objectives, the problem is definitely harder, (unless P=NP, which is unlikely). We have shown that if the principal has an LTL goal, the problem matches the complexity of
{\mathsf{LTL}}
model checking, a complexity gap that cannot be avoided since
{\mathsf{LTL}}
model checking is a particular case. But, even with
{\mathsf{GR(1)}}
specifications, the problem is very likely to be strictly harder than solving (two-player perfect-information) mean-payoff games since we have shown that with mean-payoff objectives the problem is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-Complete.

Social Welfare

While rational verification tends to privilege the preferences of individual agents in a system, social welfare measures focus, instead, on what is considered to be best for a society of agents. Because of this, our results regarding social welfare outcomes may complement nicely the analysis performed in rational verification as originally defined, where the perfromance of society as a whole was irrelevant. We have shown that even in this scenario, better complexity results can be achieved with respect to the complexity of the problem when only individual preferences are considered, as in a Nash equilibrium. In the specific scenario that we considered in the paper, we have shown that the problem is
{\mathsf{PSPACE}}
-complete, and therefore still efficient with respect to the space complexity of the problem.

Future Work

A limitation in adopting widely the use of rational verification instead of other reasoning techniques is its combined complexity, which is closely related to the complexity of associated automated synthesis problems.
Our results are important because they show that for several significant settings, rational verification can be done with polynomial space algorithms.
These results are much more attractive than in the general case, and hold out the hope of efficient practical tools (
c.f.
the
Equilibrium Verification Environment
(EVE)
GutierrezNPW18
;
GutierrezNPW20
, a tool for the automated analysis of temporal equilibrium properties).
Further practical implementations thus seem to be a natural step forward towards the deployment of rational verification in more realistic scenarios.
