Travelling waves in a PDE–ODE coupled system with nonlinear diffusion
K. Mitra, J.M. Hughes, S. Sonner, H.J. Eberl, J.D. Dockery

Abstract
We analyze travelling wave (TW) solutions for nonlinear systems consisting of an ODE coupled to a degenerate PDE with a diffusion coefficient that vanishes as the solution tends to zero and blows up as it approaches its maximum value. Stable TW solutions for such systems have previously been observed numerically as well as in biological experiments on the growth of cellulolytic biofilms. In this work, we provide an analytical justification for these observations and prove existence and stability results for TW solutions of such models. Using the TW ansatz and a first integral, the system is reduced to an autonomous dynamical system with two unknowns. Analysing the system in the corresponding phase–plane, the existence of a unique TW is shown, which possesses a sharp front and a diffusive tail, and is moving with a constant speed. The linear stability of the TW in two space dimensions is proven under suitable assumptions on the initial data. Finally, numerical simulations are presented that affirm the theoretical predictions on the existence, stability and parametric dependence of the TWs.



Keywords:
travelling waves, stability, degenerate diffusion, PDE–ODE system, biofilm

Introduction

Motivated by mathematical models for biofilm growth, we investigate
travelling wave (TW) solutions for coupled PDE–ODE systems of the form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}M=\partial_{x}[D(M)\,\partial_{x}M]+\left(f(S)-\lambda\right)M,

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}S=-\gamma\,f(S)\,M,

(1.1b)

where

and

represent the space and time coordinates respectively.
The functions
M
and

are normalized,

takes values in the interval
[0,\infty)
and

in
[0,1)
.
The growth and decay characteristics of the system are represented by the constants
\lambda,\,\gamma\in(0,1)
.
The diffusion coefficient

has a singularity as well as a degeneracy. More specifically, it satisfies
D(m)\nearrow\infty
as
m\nearrow 1
, and
D(0)=0
. The source function

is Lipschitz continuous and increasing. The exact assumptions on

and

are stated in
Section

For

we consider the following boundary conditions

\displaystyle M(\pm\infty,t)=0,\quad(D(M)\,\partial_{x}M)(\pm\infty,t)=0,

(1.2a)

and initial conditions that are consistent with the above. In particular, we assume that

\displaystyle\begin{cases}M(x,0)\in[0,1),\quad S(x,0)\in[0,\infty)&\text{ for all }x<0,\\
M(x,0)=0,\quad S(x,0)=1&\text{ for all }x\geq 0.\end{cases}

(1.2b)

Standard approaches to prove the existence and stability of TW solutions
for equations such as the Fischer-KPP equation do not generalize to the system (
1.1
).
Difficulties arise through the degeneracy and singularity of the diffusion coefficient, and the nonlinear coupling between the PDE and the ODE, which leads to a non-monotone profile for
M
. In this paper, we use ordering of orbits in the
(M,S)
phase–plane to prove the existence and uniqueness of a TW solution for the system (
1.1
) satisfying suitable boundary conditions. We further derive stability results using asymptotic expansions. Moreover, we present numerical simulations that affirm the theoretical predictions on the existence, stability and parametric dependence of the TWs.

Several examples of semilinear evolution equations coupled to an ODE
through the source–term can be found in

[
18
]
as models of reactive transport through the subsurface. In mathematical biology, examples of similar systems are found in
[
23
, Chapter 13]
, as well as a discussion on their TW solutions.
PDE–ODE coupled systems of various type with nonlinear diffusion coefficients are used to model variety of other physical or biological processes, ranging from hysteretic flow through porous media
[

]
, to

[

, and wound healing
[

.
The particular motivation for our analysis is the model for cellulolytic biofilm growth in
[

for which TW solutions have been observed numerically.
Cellulolytic biofilms play an important role in the production of cellulosic ethanol, a renewable biofuel that can be implemented in the existing transportation infrastructure.
In contrast to more traditional biofilms that form on mostly abiotic surfaces and develop colonies that grow into the surrounding aqueous phase,
many cellulolytic biofilms degrade and consume the biological material that supports them and form crater like depressions, a phenomenon known as inverted colony formation. Since the nutrients are immobile, whereas, the biofilm expands spatially, a PDE–ODE model was proposed for cellulolytic biofilms in
[
11
]
which is a special case of (
1.1
). In the model,
M
represented the biomass density and

the nutrient concentration.
The bacteria consume nutrients and degrade the biological material which results in the production of biomass. This was described in
[
11
]
using the Monod reaction function

, with the corresponding expressions being

\displaystyle D(m)

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta\,m^{a}}{(1-m)^{b}},

\displaystyle\delta>0,\ a,\,b>1,

(1.3a)

\displaystyle f(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{s}{\kappa+s},

\displaystyle 0<\kappa\ll 1.

Cellulolytic biofilm formation has been studied both numerically and experimentally. In
[
31
]
, an agent based stochastic discrete cellular automaton model was used to study the system. The model in
[

]
, on the other hand, is the deterministic continuum model (

) with
the specific functions

in (

). Extensions of this model that account for attachment of cells from the aqueous phase to the biofilm were presented in

,

using either Itô stochastic differential equations or random differential equations.
Numerical simulations of the original deterministic model in
[
11
]
suggest the existence of TW solutions, which describe a constant rate of degradation of the cellulosic material that is utilised by the bacteria. Additional numerical evidence for this is given in
[
17
]
where a different time integration method is used, along with an independent implementation. Celluloytic biofilm systems are very difficult to observe experimentally with time-lapse microscopy techniques. Nevertheless, in
[

experiments were reported that suggest degradation of paper chads by cellulosic biofilms at a constant speed, which gives indirect evidence for TW like degradation in the biological system.
Furthermore, experimental observations in

indicate that the width of the microbially

A rigorous proof of the existence and features of TW solutions of the model in
[

]

[

. We emphasize that, although our study is motivated and prompted by the cellulosic biofilm system, our results are valid for a significantly wider class of problems.

The existence of TWs for the (scalar) porous medium equation with a nonlinear source term was investigated in
[
5
]
. It was shown that under certain conditions on the coefficients, there exists a minimum speed for which TW solutions exist. The results were extended to include nonlinear advection terms in
[
3
]
. Furthermore, in
[
1
]
the stability of such TW solutions was shown in one space dimension. For the porous medium equation with Fischer type reaction term,
the existence of TWs was shown in
[
25
]
, again for wave-speeds larger than a minimum value. In
[
4
]
, further qualitative properties of the TW solutions were analysed. These results were generalized in
[
12
]
for equations with the biofilm diffusion coefficient
D
in (
1.3a
). However, the aforementioned results are limited to scalar equations with Fisher-type nonlinear reaction term, and thus, exclude the complex interplay between the ODE and the PDE solutions. While
the TW profile is a monotone function with respect to
x
in all the mentioned results, this is not the case for the TW profile of

in our system.

TWs for PDE–ODE coupled systems have been studied for multiphase flow through porous media in
[
28
,
22
,
20
]
, where non-monotone profiles of

have been observed. The ordering of orbits in the phase–plane is also used in these papers to predict the behaviour of the TWs. The existence of TWs in two-dimensions for a PDE–ODE model was investigated in
[
21
]
in the context of hysteretic flow through porous media, and non-planar TWs were shown to exist. However, TWs in these cases originate from the advection term, rather than the source term. Non-monotone profiles have also been observed for the TWs of PDE–ODE systems arising in biology, see
[
15
,
16
]
for examples. TWs for a PDE–PDE coupled model of bacteria spreading in an aqueous phase were analysed in
[
26
]
. Nevertheless, these systems differ fundamentally in their structure from (
1.1
). In our setting, as will be observed later, the TWs have
distinctive features which distinguish them from the examples above. They inherit a sharp front and a minimum speed of propagation like TWs of the porous medium equation. However, due to the coupling with the ODE, they exhibit a non-monotone profile with a diffusive tail.

The outline of our paper is as follows:
In Section
2
, we state the assumptions on the associated functions, and using the TW ansatz, the system (
1.1
) is reduced to a dynamical system with two unknowns. The existence result for the TWs is also stated, see
Theorem

2.1
. In
Section

3
, using phase–plane analysis we develop the auxiliary results which are then used to prove
Theorem

2.1
.
Section

4
is dedicated to proving a linear stability result for the TWs in two space dimensions using asymptotic expansion. In
Section

5
, numerical results are presented for a discretization of the full PDE–ODE system, an ODE approach inspired by the TW analysis, and a numerical continuation approach. All three different approaches concur about the existence/non-existence of TWs in a parametric regime indicated by our theory. Furthermore, numerical results showing the influence of the parameters, and the stability of the TWs are presented. In
Section

6
, we interpret the analytical and numerical results in the context of cellulolytic biofilms, and discuss possible generalizations and future applications.

Remark 3.2

(Consistency of the TW solution)
.

Let
(M,S)
be the TW solution described in
Theorem

2.1
and let
\mathfrak{M}
denote its
\mathfrak{M}
-mapping. Then
\ell(s)/\penalty\mathfrak{M}\to 0
as

from (

one has

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-D(M)\partial_{x}M=-D(M)\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\xi}M=v(M-\ell(S))\to 0,\\

Remark 3.3

.

Consequently, a sharp front exists at

4.1

Let

\displaystyle\partial_{t}M=\partial_{x}[D(M)\,\partial_{x}M]+\partial_{y}[D(M)\,\partial_{y}M]+\left(f(S)-\lambda\right)M,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}S=-\gamma\,f(S)\,M.

(4.1b)

We consider the following boundary conditions for (

\displaystyle M(\pm\infty,y,t)=0,\;\;(D(M)\,\partial_{x}M)(\pm\infty,y,t)=0

\displaystyle\text{ for all }y\in(0,L)\text{ and }t>0,

(4.2a)

\displaystyle(D(M)\,\partial_{y}M)(x,0,t)=(D(M)\,\partial_{y}M)(x,L,t)=0

\displaystyle\text{ for all }x\in{\mathbb{R}}\text{ and }t>0.

\displaystyle M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n,0}(0)=S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n,0}(0)=0,\text{ and }\lim\limits_{x\searrow-\infty}M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n,0}(x)=\lim\limits_{x\searrow-\infty}S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n,0}(x)=0\text{ for all }n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}.

(4.3b)

Remark 4.1

(Generality of the initial condition)

The initial condition (

contributions are added in (

components here. Observe that the initial condition (

We assume that the solution of (

\displaystyle\begin{cases}M(x,y,t)=M^{\langle{0}\rangle}(x-vt)+\varepsilon M^{\langle{1}\rangle}(x,y,t)+\dots,\\
S(x,y,t)=S^{\langle{0}\rangle}(x-vt)+\varepsilon S^{\langle{1}\rangle}(x,y,t)+\dots,\end{cases}

(4.4)

where

represents the wave speed, and

,

are differentiable functions such that the boundary conditions (

) and initial conditions (

) are satisfied.
Then by inserting the expansion (

) in (

) and equating the

\displaystyle\partial_{t}M^{\langle{1}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\partial_{xx}(D(M^{\langle{0}\rangle})M^{\langle{1}\rangle})+\partial_{yy}(D(M^{\langle{0}\rangle})M^{\langle{1}\rangle})

\displaystyle\quad+(f(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})-\lambda)M^{\langle{1}\rangle}+f^{\prime}(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})M^{\langle{0}\rangle}S^{\langle{1}\rangle},

(4.5a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}S^{\langle{1}\rangle}

\displaystyle=-\gamma[f(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})M^{\langle{1}\rangle}+f^{\prime}(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})M^{\langle{0}\rangle}S^{\langle{1}\rangle}].

Since the TW coordinate,

, is a more natural space-coordinate compared to

to analyse this problem, we use the coordinate transform

(4.6)

System (

) is modified accordingly. For simplicity, referring to
(y^{\prime},t^{\prime})
as

, we then have the following problem

\displaystyle\partial_{t}M^{\langle{1}\rangle}-v\,\partial_{\xi}M^{\langle{1}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\xi\xi}(D(M^{\langle{0}\rangle})M^{\langle{1}\rangle})+\partial_{yy}(D(M^{\langle{0}\rangle})M^{\langle{1}\rangle})

\displaystyle\quad+(f(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})-\lambda)M^{\langle{1}\rangle}+f^{\prime}(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})M^{\langle{0}\rangle}S^{\langle{1}\rangle},

(4.7a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}S^{\langle{1}\rangle}-v\,\partial_{\xi}S^{\langle{1}\rangle}

\displaystyle=-\gamma[f(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})M^{\langle{1}\rangle}+f^{\prime}(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})M^{\langle{0}\rangle}S^{\langle{1}\rangle}].

(4.7b)

Due to the form of the initial condition prescribed, we look for

and

of the following form

\displaystyle\begin{cases}M^{\langle{1}\rangle}(x,y,t)&=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}(\xi,t)\cos\left(\Lambda_{n}\,y\right),\\[5.0pt]

(4.8)

Substituting (
4.8
) in (

), observing that

only depends on

\displaystyle\partial_{t}M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}-v\,\partial_{\xi}M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\xi\xi}(D(M^{\langle{0}\rangle})M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n})-\Lambda_{n}^{2}M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}

\displaystyle\quad+(f(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})-\lambda)M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}+f^{\prime}(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})M^{\langle{0}\rangle}S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n},

(4.9a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}-v\,\partial_{\xi}S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}

\displaystyle=-\gamma[f(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}+f^{\prime}(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})M^{\langle{0}\rangle}S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}].

(4.9b)

In order to satisfy the boundary and initial conditions (

)–(

) for each

\displaystyle M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}(0,t)

\displaystyle=S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}(0,t)=0

\displaystyle\text{ for all }t>0,

(4.10a)

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\xi\searrow-\infty}M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{\xi\searrow-\infty}S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}(\xi)=0,

(4.10b)

\displaystyle M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}(\xi,0)

\displaystyle=M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n,0}(\xi),\quad S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}(\xi,0)=S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n,0}(\xi)

\displaystyle\text{ for all }\xi<0.

(4.10c)

Remark 4.2

(Choice of boundary conditions (

))
.

The boundary conditions at

prescribed in (

Hence, (

4.2
Stability in
L^{1}
-norm

Theorem 4.1

(Stability of the travelling waves)
.

Let
(M^{\langle{0}\rangle},S^{\langle{0}\rangle}):{\mathbb{R}}^{-}\to[0,1]^{2}
be a travelling wave solution satisfying (
2.11
)–(
2.12
) with a given wave-speed
v>0
.
Assume that for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, a continuously differentiable and absolutely integrable solution
(M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n},S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}):{\mathbb{R}}^{-}\times[0,\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
exists of the problem (
4.9
) which satisfies the initial and boundary conditions (
4.10
). Then, for any given
t>0
, one has

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{-}}\left[|M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}(t)|+\tfrac{1}{\gamma}|S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}(t)|\right]+\left(\lambda+\Lambda_{n}^{2}\right)\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{-}}|M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{-}}\left[|M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n,0}|+\tfrac{1}{\gamma}|S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n,0}|\right]+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\{M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}\cdot S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}\leq 0\}}[2f(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})|M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}|-f^{\prime}(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})M^{\langle{0}\rangle}|S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}|].

(4.11)

Consequently, if
\Lambda_{n}^{2}>2f(1)-\lambda>0
, then
\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{-}}|M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}|<\infty
, and

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{-}}\left[|M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}(t)|+\tfrac{1}{\gamma}|S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}(t)|\right]\text{ is strictly decreasing with respect to }t>0.

(4.12)

Remark 4.3

(Stability of the travelling waves)
.

\text{If }\;L<\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{2f(1)-\lambda}},\text{ then \eqref{eq:Decay} holds for all }n\in{\mathbb{N}},

thus proving stability of the TWs in two dimensions. However, for
n=0
,
\Lambda_{n}=0
and the stability of the TW is not guaranteed. In practice, this means that perturbations to the TW that have fast transverse variations decay. The case
n=0
represents no perturbation in the transverse direction but only in the direction of the TW. Unfortunately, only conditional stability can be proven for this case using our analysis. Increased stability due to transverse variations, similar to
Theorem

4.1
, has been studied earlier, for example in
[
29
]
. Using numerical simulations we show in
Section

5.1.1
that the TWs are also stable in terms of large longitudinal perturbations.

Proof.

The proof uses the well-known
L^{1}
-contraction principle. We reproduce a formal version here for the sake of brevity.

Let
{\rm sign}_{\varepsilon}:{\mathbb{R}}\to[-1,1]
denote a regularised version of the signum function for
\varepsilon>0
with
\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}
as its primitive. More precisely,

\displaystyle{\rm sign}_{\varepsilon}(u):=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }u>\varepsilon,\\
u/\penalty\varepsilon&\text{ if }|u|\leq\varepsilon,\\
-1&\text{ if }u<-\varepsilon,\\
\end{cases}\text{ and }\;\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(u)=\int_{0}^{u}{\rm sign}_{\varepsilon}.

(4.13)

Note the following properties of these functions for future use

\displaystyle|u\,{{\rm sign}_{\varepsilon}}^{\prime}(u)|\begin{cases}<1\text{ for }|u|\leq\varepsilon,\\
=0\text{ for }|u|>\varepsilon,\\
\end{cases}\text{ and }\;\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(u)\geq 0\text{ with equality only for }u=0,

(4.14a)

\displaystyle{\rm sign}_{\varepsilon}(u)\to{\rm sign}(u),\;u\,{\rm sign}_{\varepsilon}(u)\to|u|,\text{ and }\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(u)\to|u|\text{ pointwise as }\varepsilon\searrow 0.

(4.14b)

We use
{\rm sign}_{\varepsilon}(M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n})
as a test function in (
4.9a
). Multiplying (
4.9a
) by
{\rm sign}_{\varepsilon}(M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n})
and integrating in
{\mathbb{R}}^{-}
one has
term by term

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{-}}{\rm sign}_{\varepsilon}(M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n})\,\partial_{t}M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}=\partial_{t}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{-}}\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n})\right)\overset{\eqref{eq:propSignB}}{\to}\partial_{t}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{-}}|M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}|\right)\text{ as }\varepsilon\searrow 0,

(4.15a)

\displaystyle-v

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{-}}{\rm sign}_{\varepsilon}(M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n})\,\partial_{\xi}M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}=-v\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{-}}\partial_{\xi}\,\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n})=v[\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}(-\infty,t))-\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(0)]\overset{\eqref{eq:stabBCpde}}{=}0.

(4.15b)

From the second order term, one has using integration by parts, and the boundary conditions in (
4.10
) that

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{-}}{\rm sign}_{\varepsilon}(M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n})\,\partial_{\xi\xi}(D(M^{\langle{0}\rangle})M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n})=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{-}}\partial_{\xi}\left({\rm sign}_{\varepsilon}(M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n})\right)\partial_{\xi}(D(M^{\langle{0}\rangle})M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n})

\displaystyle=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{-}}{{\rm sign}_{\varepsilon}}^{\prime}(M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n})D(M^{\langle{0}\rangle})|\partial_{\xi}M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}|^{2}-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{-}}{{\rm sign}_{\varepsilon}}^{\prime}(M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n})\,M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}\,\partial_{\xi}\left(D(M^{\langle{0}\rangle})\right)\partial_{\xi}M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:propSignA}}{\leq}\int_{\{|M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}|<\varepsilon\}}|\partial_{\xi}D(M^{\langle{0}\rangle})\,\partial_{\xi}M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}|\to 0\qquad\text{ as }\varepsilon\searrow 0.

(4.15c)

Finally, for the source terms, one has as
\varepsilon\to 0
,

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{-}}{\rm sign}_{\varepsilon}(M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n})\,[(f(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})-\lambda-\Lambda_{n}^{2})M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}+f^{\prime}(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})M^{\langle{0}\rangle}S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}]

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:propSignB}}{\to}-(\lambda+\Lambda_{n}^{2})\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{-}}|M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}|+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{-}}{\rm sign}(M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n})\,[f(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}+f^{\prime}(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})M^{\langle{0}\rangle}S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}].

(4.15d)

Similarly, multiplying (
4.9b
) by
{\rm sign}_{\varepsilon}(S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n})
, integrating in
{\mathbb{R}}^{-}
and following the steps of (
4.15
) one has

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{-}}|S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}|\right)\leq-\gamma\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{-}}{\rm sign}(S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n})\,[f(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}+f^{\prime}(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})M^{\langle{0}\rangle}S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}].

(4.16)

Adding (
4.15
)–(
4.16
) one thus obtains

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{-}}\left[|M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}|+\tfrac{1}{\gamma}|S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}|\right]\right)+\left(\lambda+\Lambda_{n}^{2}\right)\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{-}}|M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{-}}({\rm sign}(M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n})-{\rm sign}(S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}))\,[f(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}+f^{\prime}(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})M^{\langle{0}\rangle}S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}].

(4.17)

Note that if both
M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n},\,S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}>0
or
M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n},\,S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}<0
then the right hand side vanishes. On the other hand, if
M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}>0
and
S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}<0
then
{\rm sign}(M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n})-{\rm sign}(S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n})=2
and
f(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}+f^{\prime}(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})M^{\langle{0}\rangle}S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}=f(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})|M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}|-f^{\prime}(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})M^{\langle{0}\rangle}|S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}|
. Hence, the integrand on the right hand side becomes

2(f(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})|M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}|-f^{\prime}(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})M^{\langle{0}\rangle}|S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}|)<2f(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})|M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}|-f^{\prime}(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})M^{\langle{0}\rangle}|S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}|.

By symmetry, we have the same inequality when
M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}<0
and
S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}>0
. Including the trivial cases of
M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}=0
and/or
S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}=0
which in both cases yield

({\rm sign}(M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n})-{\rm sign}(S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}))\,[f(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}+f^{\prime}(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})M^{\langle{0}\rangle}S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}]\leq 2f(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})|M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}|-f^{\prime}(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})M^{\langle{0}\rangle}|S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}|,

we have (
4.11
) by integrating (
4.2
) in time.

Since
f(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})<f(1)
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{-}
, we have
\lambda+\Lambda_{n}^{2}>2f(S^{\langle{0}\rangle})
in (
4.12
). This implies that there exists a constant
c_{1}>0
such that for any
0<t_{1}<t_{2}
,

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{-}}\left[|M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}(t_{2})|+\tfrac{1}{\gamma}|S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}(t_{2})|\right]+c_{1}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{-}}|M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}|\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{-}}\left[|M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}(t_{1})|+\tfrac{1}{\gamma}|S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}(t_{1})|\right].

Hence,
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{-}}\left[|M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}(t)|+\tfrac{1}{\gamma}|S^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}(t)|\right]
is a decreasing function having a limit, and
\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{-}}|M^{\langle{1}\rangle}_{n}|
is bounded uniformly for all
t>0
.
∎

5.2.1
TW algorithm

To directly find the TW profile and the corresponding wave-speed
v>0
for a given set of parameters, we use a semi-analytic approach that utilizes the monotonicity property described in
Theorem

3.5
with respect to
v>0
. We solve the dynamical system (
3.5
) for a given
v>0
, except we solve the negation of the vector field, i.e.

\displaystyle\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}M^{v}=-v\,[\ell(S^{v})-M^{v}],

(5.5a)

\displaystyle\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}S^{v}=-\tfrac{\gamma}{v}{f(S^{v})M^{v}D(M^{v})},

(5.5b)

with
D
and
f
as in (
1.3
). The above system is solved numerically for
\tau>0
using the 4
th
-order Runge-Kutta method
[
27
]
and the following initial condition is used for the computation:

\displaystyle(M^{v},S^{v})(0)=(\varepsilon,1),

\displaystyle\text{ for }\varepsilon=10^{-3}.

(5.6)

This avoids the problem of starting from the degenerate equilibrium point
(0,1)
and gives a close approximation to the TW profile as indicated by
Theorem

3.3
.

Then, the
bisection method
is used to determine the wave-speed for which
(M^{v},S^{v})
connects to the other equilibrium point
(0,s_{-\infty})
. Let
\bar{v}>0
be large enough so that
(M^{\bar{v}},S^{\bar{v}})
exits the region
\mathfrak{R}:=[0,1)\times[s_{-\infty},1]
through the line
\{s=s_{-\infty}\}
. The existence of such
\bar{v}>0
is ensured by
Theorem

3.5
. Similarly, let
\underline{v}\in(0,\bar{v})
be such that
(M^{\underline{v}},S^{\underline{v}})
exits
\mathfrak{R}
through the line
\{m=0\}
. Then we use the following algorithm to determine
v>0
:

Algorithm 5.1

(Bisection iteration to determine the travelling wave)
.

1.

Set
v=\frac{1}{2}|\bar{v}+\underline{v}|
. Solve
(M^{v},S^{v})
satisfying (
5.5
) and (
5.6
) numerically.

2.

If
(M^{v},S^{v})
exits

5.3
Numerical continuation method

Another approach to study parameter regimes for which the system
(
3.5
) has a connecting orbit is to use numerical continuation techniques
[
6
]
.
The approach is to find a connecting orbit on the center unstable manifold of the rest state

(0,s_{-\infty})
to the strong stable manifold of the rest state
(0,1)
.

Following
[
6
]
, we set this up as a two point boundary value problem:

\displaystyle\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}M=v\,T\,[\ell(S)-M],

\displaystyle\mbox{~~~for $\tau\in(0,1)$},

(5.7a)

\displaystyle\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}S=\tfrac{\gamma}{v}\,T\,\,f(S)\,MD(M),

\displaystyle\mbox{~~~for $\tau\in(0,1)$},

(5.7b)

\displaystyle(M(0),S(0))=(\varepsilon_{0},s_{\varepsilon_{0}}),

(5.7c)

\displaystyle(M(1),S(1)=(\varepsilon_{1},1),

(5.7d)

where
s_{\varepsilon_{0}}
is the smallest root of the equation

\ell(s)=\varepsilon_{0},

(5.8)

i.e.,
\ell(s_{\varepsilon_{0}})=\varepsilon_{0}
.
Note that (
5.7d
) is a point on the linearized strong stable manifold for the rest state

(0,1)
. The center unstable manifold of the rest state
(0,s_{-\infty})
is well approximated
by
(\ell(s),s)
for
s
near
s_{-\infty}
since the
homological equation for the center manifold is given by

M\,D(M)\,\dfrac{\mathrm{d}M}{\mathrm{d}S}=\frac{v^{2}}{\gamma f(S)}\,[\ell(S)-M],

and
MD(M)={\cal O}(M^{1+a})
for
M
near zero.
This is why we use the boundary condition (
5.7c
). The parameter
T
is the time of travel and is taken to be very large in order to approximate the heteroclinic
orbit. An integral condition (see, e.g.
[
6
,
7
]
) is added to (
5.7
) to
facilitate adaptive mesh selection when computing a branch of solutions.
We use AUTO-07P
[
8
]
for these computations.

To further illustrate the conclusions of Theorem
2.1
, and to investigate the limiting cases where the condition
\mathcal{G}(s)>0
for all
s\in(s_{-\infty},1)
is violated, we compute the heteroclinic connections using the parameter
values

\kappa=1

and

6
Interpretation of the results

The analysis in
Section

3
shows that TWs for the system (
1.1
) exist as expected from the numerical experiments in
[
11
]
and observations on cellulolytic biofilms made in
[
31
]
. Moreover, we predicted theoretically and verified numerically that they exist for a wide range of parameters. The resulting TWs are found to be stable even for large perturbations (see
Figures

6
and
8
) which agrees with the numerical observations in
[
11
,
17
]
.
In the context of biofilm growth, the TWs describe the formation of crater like structures (inverted colonies), i.e. the invasion and degradation of the undisturbed cellulosic environment by microbes. At the leading edge of this invading front is a microbially active layer that consumes the substrate. In the wake of this layer, decay terms dominate over growth terms, and thus, this region is dominated by the dynamics of how fast substrates degrade and how fast bacteria decay if growth cannot be sustained. This complex interplay gives the TW its distinct shape.

Our analysis also suggests that the TWs do not exist if for example, either the cell-loss rate
\lambda\in(0,1)
is too small or too large (
Proposition

3.6
). From the numerical experiments in
Section

5.2
, we can postulate the reason behind the non-existence of TWs. We see from
Figure

12
that as
\lambda
decreases, the TW profile becomes wider very quickly, and the biofilm concentration
M
approaches a value close to 1 in a large interval. This is expected since small
\lambda
implies less decay of
M
, and for very small
\lambda
we expect that the biofilm concentration would grow monotonically with time and reach 1 in every point of the domain, implying that a TW solution cannot exist. On the other hand, for
\lambda
large, the profiles become narrower and their amplitudes decrease as seen from
Figure

11
. Hence, one expects that for
\lambda
large enough, the initial biofilm profile would decay to 0 monotonically with time.

The variation of the wave-speed can also be explained through these observations. If
\lambda
is small, then the profile is wider. Hence, the bacteria consume the substrate faster, which results in a higher wave-speed. For
\lambda
large the effect is reversed.

Similarly, the effects of the consumption rate
\gamma>0
on the existence, profile-width and wave-speed of the TW can be explained, see
Figure

13
. Higher values of
\gamma
result in a faster consumption of the substrate, which leads to a decrease in the production of biomass. Hence, the effects of increasing
\gamma
are analogous to the effects of increasing
\lambda
.

Lastly, we remark that our approach can also be applied to PDE–ODE systems with a porous media type diffusion coefficient (i.e.
D(M)=M^{a}
), although boundedness of
M\in[0,1)
cannot be expected in this case. Furthermore, we expect that the results in this paper can be extended to study TW solutions of PDE–ODE systems with multiple substrates, see the system in
[
13
]
for instance. Due to the structure of the TW, as a pulse with sharp front and diffusive tail travelling at a constant speed, systems of the form (
1.1
) can likely be used to model several other biological and physical processes with immobile substrates, such as tumor growth, fungal growth, and the spreading of wildfire.
