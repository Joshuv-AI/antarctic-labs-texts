On the distribution of Sudler products and Birkhoff sums for the irrational rotation


Abstract
We study the value distribution of the Sudler product ∏ n = 1 N | 2 ​ sin ⁡ ( π ​ n ​ α ) | \prod_{n=1}^{N}|2\sin(\pi n\alpha)| and the Diophantine product ∏ n = 1 N ( 2 ​ e ​ ‖ n ​ α ‖ ) \prod_{n=1}^{N}(2e\|n\alpha\|) for various irrational α \alpha , as N N ranges in a long interval of integers. At badly approximable irrationals these products exhibit strong concentration around N 1 / 2 N^{1/2} , and at certain quadratic irrationals they even satisfy a central limit theorem. In contrast, at almost every α \alpha we observe an interesting anticoncentration phenomenon when the typical and the extreme values are of the same order of magnitude. Our methods are equally suited for the value distribution of Birkhoff sums ∑ n = 1 N f ⁡ ( n ​ α ) \sum_{n=1}^{N}f(n\alpha) for circle rotations. Using Diophantine approximation and Fourier analysis, we find the first and second moment for an arbitrary periodic f f of bounded variation, and (almost) prove a conjecture of Bromberg and Ulcigrai on the appropriate scaling factor in a so-called temporal limit theorem. Birkhoff sums also satisfy a central limit theorem at certain quadratic irrationals.

Introduction

The so-called Sudler product

P_{N}(\alpha):=\prod_{n=1}^{N}|2\sin(\pi n\alpha)|,\qquad\alpha\in\mathbb{R}

was first studied by Erdős, Szekeres
[
24
]
and Sudler
[
37
]
in the context of restricted partition functions. Sudler products later appeared in several areas of mathematics including
q
-series
[
33
]
, dynamical systems and KAM theory
[
30
]
, Zagier’s quantum modular forms
[
38
]
and hyperbolic knots in algebraic topology
[
12
]
. They also play an important role in a counterexample to the Baker–Gammel–Wills conjecture on Padé approximants
[
32
]
and the solution of the Ten Martini Problem
[
5
]
. The ubiquity of the Sudler product is explained by the large number of its different representations. While defined as a trigonometric product,
P_{N}(\alpha)
is also the modulus of the
q
-product
(1-q)(1-q^{2})\cdots(1-q^{N})
with
q=e^{2\pi i\alpha}
on the unit circle. In addition, its logarithm
\log P_{N}(\alpha)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(n\alpha)
is a Birkhoff sum for the irrational rotation with
f(x)=\log|2\sin(\pi x)|
. For a general overview of Birkhoff sums for toral translations we refer to the survey
[
19
]
. In particular, our results fit into the category of Birkhoff sums with a logarithmic singularity. The main goal of this paper is to study the value distribution of
P_{N}(\alpha)
as
N
ranges over a long interval of integers.

It is not surprising that the asymptotics of
P_{N}(\alpha)
as
N\to\infty
depends sensitively on the Diophantine properties of the irrational
\alpha
. Refining results of Lubinsky
[
33
]
, in a series of recent papers Aistleitner, Grepstad et al.
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
25
,
26
,
27
]
gave precise estimates for the extreme values of
P_{N}(\alpha)
in terms of the continued fraction of
\alpha
. Solving a long-standing open problem of Erdős and Szekeres, in
[
25
]
the golden ratio was shown to satisfy

1\ll P_{N}\bigg(\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}\bigg)\ll N.

(1)

The same holds for some (but not all) quadratic irrationals. Both the upper and the lower bound in (
1
) are sharp; in fact, for an arbitrary irrational
\alpha
there exist positive constants
C_{1}(\alpha),C_{2}(\alpha)
such that
P_{N}(\alpha)\leq C_{1}(\alpha)
and
P_{N}(\alpha)\geq C_{2}(\alpha)N
for infinitely many
N
. The range of oscillations of
P_{N}(\alpha)
thus span at least a factor of
N
, with the golden ratio (and certain other irrationals with small partial quotients) minimizing this range. In contrast, our first result shows that
P_{N}(\alpha)
concentrates around
N^{1/2}
for the majority of integers
N
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\alpha
be a badly approximable irrational. For any
M\geq 1
and
t>0
,

\frac{1}{M}\left|\left\{1\leq N\leq M\,:\,N^{1/2}e^{-t\sqrt{\log 2N}}\leq P_{N}(\alpha)\leq N^{1/2}e^{t\sqrt{\log 2N}}\right\}\right|=1-O\left(t^{-2}\right)

with an implied constant depending only on
\alpha
. In particular, for any sequence
t_{N}\to\infty
the set

\left\{N\in\mathbb{N}\,:\,N^{1/2}e^{-t_{N}\sqrt{\log N}}\leq P_{N}(\alpha)\leq N^{1/2}e^{t_{N}\sqrt{\log N}}\right\}

has asymptotic density
1
.

Recall that an irrational
\alpha
is called badly approximable if
\inf_{q\geq 1}q\|q\alpha\|>0
, where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the distance from the nearest integer. What we actually show is that for any badly approximable
\alpha
,

\frac{1}{M}\sum_{N=1}^{M}\log P_{N}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{2}\log M+O(\log\log M)

(2)

and

\log M\ll\frac{1}{M}\sum_{N=1}^{M}\left(\log P_{N}(\alpha)-\frac{1}{2}\log M\right)^{2}\ll\log M

(3)

with implied constants depending only on
\alpha
. From a probabilistic point of view the previous two formulas represent the expected value and the variance of
\log P_{N}(\alpha)
when
N
is chosen randomly from
[1,M]
, and Theorem
1
follows from the Chebyshev inequality. Lubinsky
[
33
]
proved that for a badly approximable
\alpha
the extreme values are

\log M\ll\max_{1\leq N\leq M}|\log P_{N}(\alpha)|\ll\log M.

Thus the square root of the variance is negligible compared to the extreme values, explaining the higher concentration in Theorem
1
compared to (
1

The estimates for the variance and the extreme values in the previous two formulas are optimal in the sense that both quantities can oscillate between two different positive constants times
\log M
for a suitable badly approximable
\alpha
. For quadratic irrational
\alpha
, however, we can say more:

\frac{1}{M}\sum_{N=1}^{M}\left(\log P_{N}(\alpha)-\frac{1}{2}\log M\right)^{2}=\sigma(\alpha)^{2}\log M+O((\log\log M)^{4})

(4)

with an explicitly computable constant
\sigma(\alpha)>0
, and
[
1
]

\max_{1\leq N\leq M}\log P_{N}(\alpha)=c(\alpha)\log M+O(1),\qquad\min_{1\leq N\leq M}\log P_{N}(\alpha)=(1-c(\alpha))\log M+O(1)

with some constant
c(\alpha)\geq 1
and implied constants depending only on
\alpha
. Here e.g.
c((1+\sqrt{5})/2)=1
(cf. formula (
1
)), but the precise value of
c(\alpha)
is not known in general. For certain simple quadratic irrationals including the golden ratio and
\sqrt{2}
, we also prove a central limit theorem with an estimate for the rate of convergence in the Kolmogorov metric.

Theorem 2
.

Let
a\geq 1
be an integer, and consider the quadratic irrational

\alpha=[0;a,a,a,\ldots]=\frac{\sqrt{a^{2}+4}-a}{2}.

For any
M\geq 3
and

\frac{1}{M}\bigg|\bigg\{1\leq N\leq M\,:\,\frac{\log P_{N}(\alpha)-\frac{1}{2}\log N}{\sigma(\alpha)\sqrt{\log N}}\leq t\bigg\}\bigg|=\int_{-\infty}^{t}\frac{e^{-x^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\mathrm{d}x+O\left(\frac{(\log\log M)^{2}}{(\log M)^{1/6}}\right)

with an implied constant depending only on
\alpha
.

Our approach is to consider
\log P_{N}(\alpha)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(n\alpha)
as a Birkhoff sum for the irrational rotation with
f(x)=\log|2\sin(\pi x)|
, and to exploit the fact that

has the particularly simple Fourier series expansion

\log|2\sin(\pi x)|=-\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{\cos(2\pi mx)}{m}.

(5)

The similar Birkhoff sum

S_{N}(\alpha):=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(\{n\alpha\}-1/2\right),

9

are harmonic conjugates: they are the real and imaginary parts of
\log(1-z)
(holomorphic on
\mathbb{C}\backslash[1,\infty)
and defined using the principal branch of the logarithm) on the unit circle

. Beck proved that for a quadratic irrational
\alpha
the expected value and the variance
1

Theorem 3

(Beck

.

Let

M\geq 3

\frac{1}{M}\bigg|\bigg\{1\leq N\leq M\,:\,\frac{S_{N}(\alpha)-E(\alpha)\log N}{(\sigma(\alpha)/\pi)\sqrt{\log N}}\leq t\bigg\}\bigg|=\int_{-\infty}^{t}\frac{e^{-x^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\mathrm{d}x+O\left(\frac{\log\log M}{(\log M)^{1/10}}\right)

with an implied constant depending only on

Here

is defined the following way: given the continued fraction expansion
\alpha=[a_{0};a_{1},a_{2},\dots]

Note that the limit exists by the periodicity of the partial quotients. For instance,

The variances of
S_{N}(\alpha)
and
\log P_{N}(\alpha)

\sigma(\alpha)
. The extra factor of
\pi^{2}
is explained by the harmonic conjugate property mentioned above. The constant
\sigma(\alpha)
also appears in the variance of certain lattice point counting problems

[

\sigma\bigg(\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}\bigg)^{2}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{60\sqrt{5}\log\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\sigma(\sqrt{3})^{2}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{24\sqrt{3}\log(2+\sqrt{3})}.

Using the harmonic conjugate property, Theorems

Theorem 4
.

Let
a\geq 1
be an integer, and consider the quadratic irrational

\alpha=[0;a,a,a,\ldots]=\frac{\sqrt{a^{2}+4}-a}{2}.

For any
M\geq 3
and any convex set

,

\frac{1}{M}\bigg|\bigg\{1\leq N\leq M\,:\,\left(\frac{\log P_{N}(\alpha)-\frac{1}{2}\log N}{\sigma(\alpha)\sqrt{\log N}},\frac{S_{N}(\alpha)-E(\alpha)\log N}{(\sigma(\alpha)/\pi)\sqrt{\log N}}\right)\in C\bigg\}\bigg|\\
=\int_{C}\frac{e^{-|x|^{2}/2}}{2\pi}\,\mathrm{d}x+O\left(\frac{(\log\log M)^{2}}{(\log M)^{1/6}}\right)

with an implied constant depending only on
\alpha
.

Our proof of Theorems
2
and
4
follows closely the rather long proof of Theorem

because for these some of the technical complications can be avoided, resulting in a proof of reasonable length. Nevertheless, we believe that Beck’s methods are fully applicable to Sudler products, and in particular that Theorems

.

Beck

Theorem 5
.

Let
\alpha
be a badly approximable irrational, and let

2.1
Euler’s number

Our methods generalize to other types of irrationals. As an illustration, consider Euler’s number
e
. It has been known since Euler himself that the continued fraction of
e
is

e=[2;1,2,1,1,4,1,\dots,1,2n,1,\dots].

In particular,
e
is only logarithmic factors away from being badly approximable in the sense that
\inf_{q\geq 3}\frac{q\log q}{\log\log q}\|qe\|>0
. We have a concentration result similar to the case of badly approximable irrationals.

Theorem 7
.

For any
M\geq 1
and
t>0
,

\frac{1}{M}\left|\left\{1\leq N\leq M\,:\,\exp\bigg(-t\left(\frac{\log 3N}{\log\log 3N}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}\bigg)\leq P_{N}(e)\leq\exp\left(t\left(\frac{\log 3N}{\log\log 3N}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}\right)\right\}\right|=1-O\left(t^{-2}\right)

with a universal implied constant. In particular, for any sequence
t_{N}\to\infty
the set

\bigg\{N\in\mathbb{N}\,:\,\exp\bigg(-t_{N}\left(\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}\bigg)\leq P_{N}(e)\leq\exp\bigg(t_{N}\left(\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}\bigg)\bigg\}

has asymptotic density
1
.

What we actually prove is that

\left|\frac{1}{M}\sum_{N=1}^{M}\log P_{N}(e)\right|\ll\log M

(7)

and

\frac{1}{M}\sum_{N=1}^{M}(\log P_{N}(e))^{2}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{540}\left(\frac{\log M}{\log\log M}\right)^{3}\bigg(1+O\left(\frac{\log\log\log M}{\log\log M}\right)\bigg).

(8)

Observe that now the expected value is negligible compared to the square root of the variance, which explains why
P_{N}(e)
oscillates around
1
instead of
N^{1/2}

a_{k}/(a_{1}^{2}+\cdots+a_{k}^{2})^{1/2}\to 0
of Beck, we conjecture that

satisfies the central limit theorem.

Conjecture
.

For any
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

\frac{1}{M}\Big|\Big\{1\leq N\leq M\,:\,\frac{\log P_{N}(e)}{\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{540}}\left(\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}\right)^{3/2}}\leq t\Big\}\Big|\to\int_{-\infty}^{t}\frac{e^{-x^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\mathrm{d}x\quad\textrm{as }M\to\infty.

From general results in

it follows that once again the extreme values are of larger order of magnitude than the square root of the variance:

\begin{split}\max_{1\leq N\leq M}\log P_{N}(e)&=V\left(\frac{\log M}{\log\log M}\right)^{2}\bigg(1+O\left(\frac{\log\log\log M}{\log\log M}\right)\bigg),\\

(9)

with

(10)

The constant

originally appeared in asymptotic results on Sudler products in
[

,

]

]

2.2
Almost every irrational

It is an unfortunate fact that the only “concrete” irrationals whose continued fraction expansion is explicitly known are the quadratic irrationals and the infinite family of irrationals related to
e
mentioned in the previous section. Very little is known about the continued fraction of other classical irrationals such as
\pi
,
\sqrt[3]{2}
or
\log 2
, although numerical experiments suggest they exhibit random behavior. We do know, however, the precise statistics of the partial quotients

of almost every real
\alpha

The extreme values of
\log P_{N}(\alpha)
for a.e.
\alpha
are as follows. Let
\varphi(x)
be a nondecreasing, positive real-valued function on
(0,\infty)
. If
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}1/\varphi(k)<\infty
, then for a.e.
\alpha
,

|\log P_{N}(\alpha)|\leq\varphi(\log N)+O(\log N\log\log N)

(11)

with an implied constant depending only on
\alpha
and
\varphi
. If
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}1/\varphi(k)=\infty
, then for a.e.
\alpha
,

\log P_{N}(\alpha)\geq\varphi(\log N)\quad\textrm{and}\quad\log P_{N}(\alpha)\leq-\varphi(\log N)\quad\textrm{for infinitely many }N.

(12)

Thus e.g. for any
\varepsilon>0
, for a.e.
\alpha
there exists a constant
C=C(\alpha,\varepsilon)>0
such that
|\log P_{N}(\alpha)|\leq C\log N(\log\log N)^{1+\varepsilon}
, but this fails with
\varepsilon=0
. Such a convergence/divergence criterion was first proved by Lubinsky
[
33
]
, who in fact explicitly constructed an exponentially increasing sequence of
N
’s (each having a single nonzero digit in its Ostrowski expansion) satisfying the second inequality in (
12
). For the sake of completeness, we explain in Section
3.4
how to derive (
11
) and (
12
) from general results in
[
1
,
12
]
. In this paper we improve the lower bound (
12
): we show that both inequalities hold on a set of positive upper asymptotic density.

Theorem 8
.

Let
\varphi(x)
be a nondecreasing, positive real-valued function on
(0,\infty)
. If
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}1/\varphi(k)=\infty
, then for a.e.
\alpha
the sets

\left\{N\in\mathbb{N}\,:\,\log P_{N}(\alpha)\geq\varphi(\log N)\right\}

and

\left\{N\in\mathbb{N}\,:\,\log P_{N}(\alpha)\leq-\varphi(\log N)\right\}

have upper asymptotic density at least
\pi^{2}/(1440V^{2})\approx 0.2627
, where
V
is as in (
10
).

What we actually prove is that for a.e.
\alpha
there exists a sequence
\psi_{\alpha}(M)\to 0
such that

\sqrt{\frac{1}{M}\sum_{N=1}^{M}(\log P_{N}(\alpha))^{2}}=\left(\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{720}V}+\psi_{\alpha}(M)\right)\max_{1\leq N\leq M}|\log P_{N}(\alpha)|\quad\textrm{for infinitely many }M.

(13)

This means that the typical and the extreme oscillations of
\log P_{N}(\alpha)
are of the same order of magnitude; we call it an anticoncentration result. We believe that the value
\pi/(\sqrt{720}V)
is sharp, i.e. for a.e.
\alpha
,

\limsup_{M\to\infty}\sqrt{\frac{1}{M}\sum_{N=1}^{M}(\log P_{N}(\alpha))^{2}}\left(\max_{1\leq N\leq M}|\log P_{N}(\alpha)|\right)^{-1}=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{720}V},

but this remains open. On the other hand,
\pi^{2}/(1440V^{2})
in Theorem
8
is likely not optimal; perhaps
\{N\in\mathbb{N}\,:\,|\log P_{N}(\alpha)|\geq\varphi(\log N)\}
even has upper asymptotic density
1
. Note that (
13
) immediately implies that
\log P_{N}(\alpha)
does not satisfy the central limit theorem (with any centering and scaling) for a.e.
\alpha
. See Dolgopyat and Sarig
[
20
,
21
]
for why the central limit theorem fails also for
S_{N}(\alpha,f)
with certain functions of bounded variation for a.e.
\alpha
.

The convergence/divergence criterion (
11
), (
12
) can be restated as follows. Under a suitable regularity condition on
\varphi(x)
in the case of convergence (e.g.
\varphi(x)/(x\log x)\to\infty
), for a.e.
\alpha
,

\limsup_{M\to\infty}\frac{\displaystyle\max_{1\leq N\leq M}\log P_{N}(\alpha)}{\varphi(\log M)}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\textrm{if }\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}1/\varphi(k)<\infty,\\
\infty&\textrm{if }\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}1/\varphi(k)=\infty,\end{array}\right.

and the same holds with
\max
replaced by
-\min
. Our next result establishes the liminf behavior of the same sequences.

Theorem 9
.

For a.e.
\alpha
,

\liminf_{M\to\infty}\frac{\displaystyle\max_{1\leq N\leq M}\log P_{N}(\alpha)}{\log M\log\log M}=\frac{12V}{\pi^{2}}.

The same holds with
\max
replaced by
-\min
.

It easily follows that for a.e.
\alpha
the set

\left\{N\in\mathbb{N}\,:\,|\log P_{N}(\alpha)|\geq\left(\frac{12V}{\pi^{2}}+\varepsilon\right)\log N\log\log N\right\},\qquad\varepsilon>0

has zero lower asymptotic density. In particular, in Theorem
8
upper asymptotic density cannot be replaced by lower asymptotic density.

In addition to the a.e. asymptotics, we also find the extreme values and the variance of
\log P_{N}(\alpha)
in a distributional sense. Since
P_{N}(\alpha)
is
1
-periodic in the variable
\alpha
, we will choose
\alpha
randomly from
[0,1]
. The main message of the next two results is that typically
\max_{1\leq N\leq M}|\log P_{N}(\alpha)|
is greater than
(M^{-1}\sum_{N=1}^{M}(\log P_{N}(\alpha))^{2})^{1/2}
by a factor of
\log\log M
. In particular, in (
13
) “for infinitely many
M
” cannot be replaced by “for all
M
”.

Theorem 10
.

We have

\frac{\displaystyle\max_{1\leq N\leq M}\log P_{N}(\alpha)}{\log M\log\log M}\to\frac{12V}{\pi^{2}}\qquad\textrm{in measure, as }M\to\infty.

The same holds with
\max
replaced by
-\min
.

Convergence in measure follows with respect to any probability measure
\mu
on
[0,1]
that is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. In contrast to the extreme values, the suitably normalized variance converges to the standard Lévy distribution.

Theorem 11
.

We have

\frac{1}{M}\sum_{N=1}^{M}\log P_{N}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{2}\log M+o\left(\sqrt{\log M}(\log\log M)^{2}\right)\qquad\textrm{in measure, as }M\to\infty.

Further, let
\mu
be a probability measure on
[0,1]
that is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. For any
t\geq 0
,

\mu\bigg(\bigg\{\alpha\in[0,1]\,:\,\frac{10\pi}{M(\log M)^{2}}\sum_{N=1}^{M}\left(\log P_{N}(\alpha)-\frac{1}{2}\log M\right)^{2}\leq t\bigg\}\bigg)\to\int_{0}^{t}\frac{e^{-1/(2x)}}{\sqrt{2\pi}x^{3/2}}\,\mathrm{d}x\quad\textrm{as }M\to\infty.

The different behavior of a.e.
\alpha
compared to badly approximable irrationals and Euler’s number — anticoncentration instead of concentration, the failure of the central limit theorem and the difference between limsup and liminf — might come as a surprise, considering that a.e.
\alpha
is only logarithmic factors away from being badly approximable: we have
\inf_{q\geq 1}\varphi(q)\|q\alpha\|>0
if and only if
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}1/\varphi(k)<\infty
. The explanation lies in the continued fraction
\alpha=[a_{0};a_{1},a_{2},\dots]
. As we will see,
\max_{1\leq N<q_{k}}\log P_{N}(\alpha)
is comparable to
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{k}
, while the variance is comparable to
a_{1}^{2}+\cdots+a_{k}^{2}
. For badly approximable irrationals and Euler’s number each term in these sums is negligible compared to the whole sum; in contrast, for a.e.
\alpha
the maximal term can dominate. Our proofs rely on asymptotic results on (trimmed) sums of partial quotients, see
[
6
,
18
,
34
,
36
]
and references therein.

2.3
A Diophantine product

The reader might wonder what happens if in the definition of the Sudler product we replace
|\sin(\pi x)|
by a similar function such as the distance from the nearest integer function
\|x\|
. The behavior of the Diophantine product
\prod_{n=1}^{N}\|n\alpha\|
can actually be reduced to that of the Sudler product.

Proposition 12
.

Assume that
\inf_{q\geq 1}q^{c}\|q\alpha\|>0
with some constant
1\leq c<2
. Then

P_{N}(\alpha)\ll\prod_{n=1}^{N}\left(2e\|n\alpha\|\right)\ll P_{N}(\alpha)

with implied constants depending only on
\alpha
.

The Diophantine condition is satisfied by all irrationals mentioned above: with
c=1
by a badly approximable
\alpha
, and with
c=1+\varepsilon
by Euler’s number
e
and by a.e.
\alpha
. All results for
P_{N}(\alpha)
stated above including the concentration inequalities and the central limit theorems thus have an analogue for the Diophantine product. For instance, (
1
) reads

1\ll\prod_{n=1}^{N}\bigg(2e\bigg\|n\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}\bigg\|\bigg)\ll N,

where both the upper and the lower bound are sharp.

Proof of Proposition
12
.

Consider the even
1
-periodic functions
f(x)=\log|2\sin(\pi x)|
and
g(x)=\log\|x\|+1+\log 2
. Note that
g
has zero mean, and its Fourier coefficients are easily computable using integration by parts:

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{1}g(x)\cos(2\pi mx)\,\mathrm{d}x&=2\int_{0}^{1/2}(\log x)\cos(2\pi mx)\,\mathrm{d}x=-2\int_{0}^{1/2}\frac{\sin(2\pi mx)}{2\pi mx}\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&=-\frac{1}{\pi m}\int_{0}^{\pi m}\frac{\sin x}{x}\,\mathrm{d}x=-\frac{1}{2m}+c_{m}\end{split}

with
c_{m}=1/(\pi m)\int_{\pi m}^{\infty}(\sin x)/x\,\mathrm{d}x\ll 1/m^{2}
. In the last step we used
\int_{0}^{\infty}(\sin x)/x\,\mathrm{d}x=\pi/2
. By sufficient smoothness, the Fourier series of
f(x)
and
g(x)
converge pointwise at nonintegral reals, hence

g(x)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{1}{m}+2c_{m}\right)\cos(2\pi mx)=f(x)+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}2c_{m}\cos(2\pi mx).

Therefore

\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N}g(n\alpha)-\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(n\alpha)\right|=\left|\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}c_{m}\left(1-\frac{\sin(\pi(2N+1)m\alpha)}{\sin(\pi m\alpha)}\right)\right|\ll\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m^{2}\|m\alpha\|}.

The last series is easily seen to converge under the assumption
\inf_{q\geq 1}|q|^{c}\|q\alpha\|>0
,
c<2
(cf.
[
31
, Chapter 2.3]
and Lemma
14
below), which proves the claim.
∎

3
Distribution of Sudler products

3.1
Expected value and variance

Our results on the expected value and the variance of Sudler products will be based on the explicit formulas

\log P_{N}(\alpha)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2m}\left(1-\frac{\sin(\pi(2N+1)m\alpha)}{\sin(\pi m\alpha)}\right)

(14)

and

\frac{1}{M}\sum_{N=0}^{M-1}\log P_{N}(\alpha)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2m}\left(1-\frac{\sin^{2}(\pi Mm\alpha)}{M\sin^{2}(\pi m\alpha)}\right),

(15)

which follow directly from the Fourier series expansion (
5
) for all irrational
\alpha
. We sum over
0\leq N\leq M-1
instead of
1\leq N\leq M
purely for aesthetic reasons, and to emphasize the connection to the Dirichlet kernel
\frac{\sin(\pi(2N+1)x)}{\sin(\pi x)}
and the Fejér kernel
\frac{\sin^{2}(\pi Mx)}{M\sin^{2}(\pi x)}
.

Let
\alpha=[a_{0};a_{1},a_{2},\dots]
be irrational with convergents
p_{k}/q_{k}=[a_{0};a_{1},\dots,a_{k}]
. For the sake of simplicity, we will assume that the partial quotients increase at most polynomially fast, i.e.
a_{k}\ll k^{d}
with some constant
d\geq 0
. This covers all types of irrationals mentioned before: badly approximable irrationals with
d=0
, Euler’s number with
d=1
and a.e. irrational with
d=1+\varepsilon
. This condition is closely related to the irrationality measure of
\alpha
. For instance, we have the implications

a_{k}\ll k^{d}\,\,\Longrightarrow\,\,\inf_{q\geq 2}\|q\alpha\|q(\log q)^{d}>0\,\,\Longrightarrow\,\,a_{k}\ll(k\log k)^{d}.

Obvious modifications of the proof yield similar results for more general irrationals.

Proposition 13
.

Assume that
a_{k}\leq ck^{d}
for all
k\geq 1
with some constants
c,d\geq 0
. For any
q_{k}\leq M<q_{k+1}
we have

\frac{1}{M}\sum_{N=0}^{M-1}\log P_{N}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{2}\log M+O\left(\max_{|\ell-k|\ll\log k}a_{\ell}\cdot\log\log M\right)

(16)

and

\frac{1}{M}\sum_{N=0}^{M-1}\left(\log P_{N}(\alpha)-\frac{1}{2}\log M\right)^{2}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}m^{2}\|m\alpha\|^{2}}+O\left(\max_{|\ell-k|\ll\log k}a_{\ell}^{2}\cdot(\log\log M)^{4}\right)

(17)

with implied constants depending only on
c
and
d
.

It remains to analyze the Diophantine sum on the right hand side of (
17
), which we do for various types of irrationals in Sections
3.2
and
3.4
. Following the methods of Beck
[
7
, Proposition 3.1, p. 171]
, we first prove several simple Diophantine sum estimates, and then give the proof of Proposition
13
.

Lemma 14
.

Assume that
a_{k}\leq ck^{d}
for all
k\geq 1
with some constants
c,d\geq 0
. For any
q_{k}\leq M<q_{k+1}
,

\begin{split}\sum_{1\leq m\leq M}\frac{1}{\|m\alpha\|}&\ll M(\log M)^{d+1},\\
\sum_{1\leq m\leq M(\log M)^{2d+2}}\frac{1}{m}\min\left\{\frac{1}{M\|m\alpha\|^{2}},M\right\}&\ll\max_{|\ell-k|\ll\log k}a_{\ell}\cdot\log\log M,\\
\sum_{1\leq m\leq M(\log M)^{2d+2}}\frac{1}{m^{2}\|m\alpha\|^{2}}\min\left\{\frac{1}{M\|2m\alpha\|},1\right\}&\ll\max_{|\ell-k|\ll\log k}a_{\ell}^{2}\cdot\log\log M,\\
\sum_{M<m\leq M(\log M)^{2d+2}}\frac{1}{m^{2}\|m\alpha\|^{2}}&\ll\max_{|\ell-k|\ll\log k}a_{\ell}^{2}\cdot\log\log M.\end{split}

Further,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq m_{1},m_{2}\leq M(\log M)^{2d+2}\\
m_{1}\neq m_{2}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{m_{1}\|m_{1}\alpha\|m_{2}\|m_{2}\alpha\|}\min\left\{\frac{1}{M\|(m_{1}-m_{2})\alpha\|},1\right\}\ll\max_{|\ell-k|\ll\log k}a_{\ell}^{2}\cdot(\log\log M)^{4},

and the same holds with
m_{1}-m_{2}
replaced by
m_{1}+m_{2}
.

Proof.

Since
q_{k}
increases at least as fast as the sequence of Fibonacci numbers, we have
k\ll\log q_{k}\ll\log M
. We will also use the simple fact
q_{0}+q_{1}+\cdots+q_{\ell}\leq 3q_{\ell}
for all
\ell\geq 0
. This follows e.g. from the recursion satisfied by
q_{\ell}
via

q_{1}+q_{2}+\cdots+q_{\ell-1}\leq a_{2}q_{1}+a_{3}q_{2}+\cdots+a_{\ell}q_{\ell-1}=q_{\ell}+q_{\ell-1}-q_{1}-q_{0}\leq 2q_{\ell}-q_{0}.

All five claims follow from a classical method based on the pigeonhole principle, see
[
31
, Chapter 2.3]
. More specifically, let
\ell\geq 0
, and consider the points
m\alpha\pmod{1}
,
q_{\ell}\leq m<q_{\ell+1}
. By the best rational approximation property,
\|m\alpha\|\geq\|q_{\ell}\alpha\|\geq 1/(2q_{\ell+1})
. In particular, none of these points lie in the open interval
(-1/(2q_{\ell+1}),1/(2q_{\ell+1}))
, and each interval
(j/(2q_{\ell+1}),(j+1)/(2q_{\ell+1}))
,
j\neq-1,0
contains at most one point.

To see the first claim of the lemma, observe that for any
0\leq\ell\leq k
,

\sum_{q_{\ell}\leq m<\min\{q_{\ell+1},M+1\}}\frac{1}{\|m\alpha\|}\ll\sum_{j=1}^{M}\frac{1}{j/q_{\ell+1}}\ll q_{\ell+1}\log M.

Summing over
0\leq\ell\leq k
leads to

\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{1}{\|m\alpha\|}\ll q_{k+1}\log M\ll k^{d}q_{k}\log M\ll M(\log M)^{d+1}.

We now prove the second claim of the lemma. For any
\ell\geq 0
with
q_{\ell}\leq M/(\log M)^{2d}
,

\sum_{q_{\ell}\leq m<q_{\ell+1}}\frac{1}{m}\min\left\{\frac{1}{M\|m\alpha\|^{2}},M\right\}\leq\frac{1}{q_{\ell}M}\sum_{q_{\ell}\leq m<q_{\ell+1}}\frac{1}{\|m\alpha\|^{2}}\ll\frac{1}{q_{\ell}M}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j^{2}/q_{\ell+1}^{2}}\ll\frac{a_{\ell+1}^{2}q_{\ell}}{M}.

By the growth assumption on the partial quotients, this is
\ll q_{\ell}(\log M)^{2d}/M
. Next, note that there are
\ll\log\log M
indices
\ell
with
M/(\log M)^{2d}<q_{\ell}\leq M(\log M)^{2d+2}
, and all of these indices satisfy
|\ell-k|\ll\log\log M\ll\log k
. For any such
\ell
we have

\sum_{q_{\ell}\leq m<q_{\ell+1}}\frac{1}{m}\min\left\{\frac{1}{M\|m\alpha\|^{2}},M\right\}\ll\frac{1}{q_{\ell}}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\min\left\{\frac{1}{Mj^{2}/q_{\ell+1}^{2}},M\right\}\ll\frac{q_{\ell+1}}{q_{\ell}}\ll a_{\ell+1}.

Therefore

\begin{split}\sum_{1\leq m\leq M(\log M)^{2d+2}}&\frac{1}{m}\min\left\{\frac{1}{M\|m\alpha\|^{2}},M\right\}\\
&\leq\Big(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\geq 0\\
q_{\ell}\leq M/(\log M)^{2d}\end{subarray}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\geq 0\\
M/(\log M)^{2d}<q_{\ell}\leq M(\log M)^{2d+2}\end{subarray}}\Big)\sum_{q_{\ell}\leq m<q_{\ell+1}}\frac{1}{m}\min\left\{\frac{1}{M\|m\alpha\|^{2}},M\right\}\\
&\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\geq 0\\
q_{\ell}\leq M/(\log M)^{2d}\end{subarray}}\frac{q_{\ell}(\log M)^{2d}}{M}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\geq 0\\
M/(\log M)^{2d}<q_{\ell}\leq M(\log M)^{2d+2}\end{subarray}}a_{\ell+1}\\
&\ll 1+\max_{|\ell-k|\ll\log k}a_{\ell}\cdot\log\log M,\end{split}

as claimed.

Let us now prove the third claim of the lemma. By the subadditivity of the function
\|\cdot\|
, we have
\|m\alpha\|\geq\frac{1}{2}\|2m\alpha\|
. Hence

\begin{split}\sum_{1\leq m\leq M(\log M)^{2d+2}}\frac{1}{m^{2}\|m\alpha\|^{2}}&\min\left\{\frac{1}{M\|2m\alpha\|},1\right\}\\
&\ll\sum_{1\leq m\leq M(\log M)^{2d+2}}\frac{1}{(2m)^{2}\|2m\alpha\|^{2}}\min\left\{\frac{1}{M\|2m\alpha\|},1\right\}\\
&\ll\sum_{1\leq m\leq 2M(\log M)^{2d+2}}\frac{1}{m^{2}\|m\alpha\|^{2}}\min\left\{\frac{1}{M\|m\alpha\|},1\right\},\end{split}

and it will be enough to estimate the last line of the previous formula. For any
\ell\geq 0
with
q_{\ell}\leq M/(\log M)^{3d}
,

\sum_{q_{\ell}\leq m<q_{\ell+1}}\frac{1}{m^{2}\|m\alpha\|^{2}}\min\left\{\frac{1}{M\|m\alpha\|},1\right\}\leq\frac{1}{q_{\ell}^{2}M}\sum_{q_{\ell}\leq m<q_{\ell+1}}\frac{1}{\|m\alpha\|^{3}}\ll\frac{1}{q_{\ell}^{2}M}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j^{3}/q_{\ell+1}^{3}}\ll\frac{a_{\ell+1}^{3}q_{\ell}}{M}.

By the growth assumption on the partial quotients, this is
\ll q_{\ell}(\log M)^{3d}/M
. Next, note that there are
\ll\log\log M
indices
\ell
with
M/(\log M)^{3d}<q_{\ell}\leq 2M(\log M)^{2d+2}
, and all of these indices satisfy
|\ell-k|\ll\log\log M\ll\log k
. For any such
\ell
we have

\sum_{q_{\ell}\leq m<q_{\ell+1}}\frac{1}{m^{2}\|m\alpha\|^{2}}\min\left\{\frac{1}{M\|m\alpha\|},1\right\}\ll\frac{1}{q_{\ell}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j^{2}/q_{\ell+1}^{2}}\ll\frac{q_{\ell+1}^{2}}{q_{\ell}^{2}}\ll a_{\ell+1}^{2}.

Therefore

\begin{split}\sum_{1\leq m\leq 2M(\log M)^{2d+2}}&\frac{1}{m^{2}\|m\alpha\|^{2}}\min\left\{\frac{1}{M\|m\alpha\|},1\right\}\\
&\leq\Big(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\geq 0\\
q_{\ell}\leq M/(\log M)^{3d}\end{subarray}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\geq 0\\
M/(\log M)^{3d}<q_{\ell}\leq 2M(\log M)^{2d+2}\end{subarray}}\Big)\frac{1}{m^{2}\|m\alpha\|^{2}}\min\left\{\frac{1}{M\|m\alpha\|},1\right\}\\
&\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\geq 0\\
q_{\ell}\leq M/(\log M)^{3d}\end{subarray}}\frac{q_{\ell}(\log M)^{3d}}{M}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\geq 0\\
M/(\log M)^{3d}<q_{\ell}\leq 2M(\log M)^{2d+2}\end{subarray}}a_{\ell+1}^{2}\\
&\ll 1+\max_{|\ell-k|\ll\log k}a_{\ell}^{2}\cdot\log\log M,\end{split}

as claimed.

To see the fourth claim of the lemma, observe that for any
\ell\geq 0
,

\sum_{q_{\ell}\leq m<q_{\ell+1}}\frac{1}{m^{2}\|m\alpha\|^{2}}\ll\frac{1}{q_{\ell}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j^{2}/q_{\ell+1}^{2}}\ll\frac{q_{\ell+1}^{2}}{q_{\ell}^{2}}\ll a_{\ell+1}^{2}.

There are
\ll\log\log M
indices
\ell
for which the intervals
[q_{\ell},q_{\ell+1})
and
[M,M(\log M)^{2d+2}]
intersect, and all of these indices satisfy
|\ell-k|\ll\log\log M\ll\log k
. Summing over all such indices
\ell
leads to

\sum_{M\leq m\leq M(\log M)^{2d+2}}\frac{1}{m^{2}\|m\alpha\|^{2}}\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\geq 0\\
[q_{\ell},q_{\ell+1})\cap[M,M(\log M)^{2d+2}]\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}a_{\ell+1}^{2}\ll\max_{|\ell-k|\ll\log k}a_{\ell}^{2}\cdot\log\log M.

We now prove the final claim. Here the cases
m_{1}-m_{2}
and
m_{1}+m_{2}
are analogous, and we will only consider the
m_{1}-m_{2}
case. Let

R_{M}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq m_{1},m_{2}\leq M(\log M)^{2d+2}\\
m_{1}\neq m_{2}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{m_{1}\|m_{1}\alpha\|m_{2}\|m_{2}\alpha\|}\min\left\{\frac{1}{M\|(m_{1}-m_{2})\alpha\|},1\right\}

denote the sum to be estimated. Using similar methods as before, we deduce

\sum_{1\leq m\leq M(\log M)^{2d+2}}\frac{1}{m\|m\alpha\|}\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\geq 0\\
q_{\ell}\leq M(\log M)^{2d+2}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{q_{\ell}}\sum_{1\leq j\leq M(\log M)^{2d+2}}\frac{1}{j/q_{\ell+1}}\ll(\log M)^{d+2}.

We will also need the fact that
\|m\alpha\|\gg 1/(m(\log m)^{d})
for all
m>1
, which follows from the assumption on the growth rate of the partial quotients.

Let
S=\{m\geq 1\,:\,m\|m\alpha\|\geq(\log M)^{4d+5}\}
. We first observe that the terms with
m_{1}\in S
are negligible:

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq m_{1},m_{2}\leq M(\log M)^{2d+2}\\
m_{1}\neq m_{2}\\
m_{1}\in S\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{m_{1}\|m_{1}\alpha\|m_{2}\|m_{2}\alpha\|}\min\left\{\frac{1}{M\|(m_{1}-m_{2})\alpha\|},1\right\}\\
\ll\frac{1}{(\log M)^{4d+5}}\sum_{1\leq m_{2}\leq M(\log M)^{2d+2}}\frac{1}{m_{2}\|m_{2}\alpha\|}\sum_{1\leq m^{\prime}\leq M(\log M)^{2d+2}}\frac{1}{M\|m^{\prime}\alpha\|}\ll 1.

Note that instead of
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
, we summed over
m^{\prime}:=|m_{1}-m_{2}|
and
m_{2}
. By symmetry, the same holds for the terms with
m_{2}\in S
. We similarly see that the terms with
\|(m_{1}-m_{2})\alpha\|\geq(\log M)^{2d+4}/M
are also negligible:

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq m_{1},m_{2}\leq M(\log M)^{2d+2}\\
\|(m_{1}-m_{2})\alpha\|\geq(\log M)^{2d+4}/M\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{m_{1}\|m_{1}\alpha\|m_{2}\|m_{2}\alpha\|}\min\left\{\frac{1}{M\|(m_{1}-m_{2})\alpha\|},1\right\}\\
\ll\frac{1}{(\log M)^{2d+4}}\sum_{1\leq m_{1}\leq M(\log M)^{2d+2}}\frac{1}{m_{1}\|m_{1}\alpha\|}\sum_{1\leq m_{2}\leq M(\log M)^{2d+2}}\frac{1}{m_{2}\|m_{2}\alpha\|}\ll 1.

By the previous two formulas,

R_{M}\ll 1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq m_{1},m_{2}\leq M(\log M)^{2d+2}\\
0<\|(m_{1}-m_{2})\alpha\|\leq(\log M)^{2d+4}/M\\
m_{1},m_{2}\not\in S\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{m_{1}\|m_{1}\alpha\|m_{2}\|m_{2}\alpha\|}.

We now claim that in the remaining sum there is no term with
1\leq m_{1}\leq M/(\log M)^{8d+10}
. Indeed, such an
m_{1}
would satisfy
\|m_{1}\alpha\|\gg(\log M)^{7d+10}/M
. On the other hand, the condition
0<\|(m_{1}-m_{2})\alpha\|\leq(\log M)^{2d+4}/M
forces
|m_{1}-m_{2}|\gg M/(\log M)^{3d+4}
. This in turn implies
m_{2}\gg M/(\log M)^{3d+4}
and, since
m_{2}\not\in S
,

\|m_{2}\alpha\|\leq\frac{(\log M)^{4d+5}}{m_{2}}\ll\frac{(\log M)^{7d+9}}{M}.

In particular, for large enough
M
we have, say,
\|m_{1}\alpha\|\geq 2\|m_{2}\alpha\|
and hence

\|(m_{1}-m_{2})\alpha\|\geq\|m_{1}\alpha\|-\|m_{2}\alpha\|\geq\frac{1}{2}\|m_{1}\alpha\|\gg\frac{(\log M)^{7d+10}}{M},

giving a contradiction. By symmetry, there are no terms with
1\leq m_{2}\leq M/(\log M)^{8d+10}
either, therefore

R_{M}\ll 1+\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M/(\log M)^{8d+10}\leq m\leq M(\log M)^{2d+2}\\
m\not\in S\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{m\|m\alpha\|}\bigg)^{2}.

To estimate the remaining sum, let
\ell\geq 0
be an index such that the intervals
[q_{\ell},q_{\ell+1})
and
[M/(\log M)^{8d+10},M(\log M)^{2d+2}]
intersect. Note that there are
\ll\log\log M
such indices
\ell
, each of which satisfies
|\ell-k|\ll\log k
. For any such
\ell
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q_{\ell}\leq m<q_{\ell+1}\\
m\not\in S\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{m\|m\alpha\|}\leq\frac{1}{q_{\ell}}\sum_{1\leq j\ll(\log M)^{5d+5}}\frac{1}{j/q_{\ell+1}}\ll a_{\ell+1}\log\log M.

Note that
m\not\in S
implies that

\|m\alpha\|\leq\frac{(\log M)^{4d+5}}{m}\leq\frac{(\log M)^{4d+5}}{q_{\ell}},

explaining why we only sum over
j
for which
j/q_{\ell+1}\leq(\log M)^{4d+5}/q_{\ell}
, that is, for which
j\ll(q_{\ell+1}/q_{\ell})(\log M)^{4d+5}\ll(\log M)^{5d+5}
. Hence

\begin{split}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M/(\log M)^{8d+10}\leq m\leq M(\log M)^{2d+2}\\
m\not\in S\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{m\|m\alpha\|}&\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\geq 0\\
[q_{\ell},q_{\ell+1})\cap[M/(\log M)^{8d+10},M(\log M)^{2d+2}]\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}a_{\ell+1}\log\log M\\
&\ll\max_{|\ell-k|\ll\log k}a_{\ell}\cdot(\log\log M)^{2},\end{split}

and the claim follows.
∎

Proof of Proposition
13
.

Fix
q_{k}\leq M<q_{k+1}
, and let
0\leq N\leq M-1
. We start by estimating the tails in the explicit formulas (
14
) and (
15
). Summation by parts gives that the tails of the Fourier series expansion (
5
) decay at the rate

\left|\sum_{m=H}^{\infty}\frac{\cos(2\pi mx)}{m}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2H\|x\|},\qquad H\geq 1.

(18)

Choosing
H=\lceil M(\log M)^{2d+2}\rceil
we deduce

\begin{split}\log P_{N}(\alpha)&=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{1\leq m\leq M(\log M)^{2d+2}}\frac{-\cos(2\pi mn\alpha)}{m}+O\left(\frac{1}{M(\log M)^{2d+2}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\|n\alpha\|}\right)\\
&=\sum_{1\leq m\leq M(\log M)^{2d+2}}\frac{1}{2m}\left(1-\frac{\sin(\pi(2N+1)m\alpha)}{\sin(\pi m\alpha)}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{(\log M)^{d+1}}\right),\end{split}

(19)

and by averaging over
0\leq N\leq M-1
,

\frac{1}{M}\sum_{N=0}^{M-1}\log P_{N}(\alpha)=\sum_{1\leq m\leq M(\log M)^{2d+2}}\frac{1}{2m}\left(1-\frac{\sin^{2}(\pi Mm\alpha)}{M\sin^{2}(\pi m\alpha)}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{(\log M)^{d+1}}\right).

(20)

Note that in (
19
) we used the first Diophantine sum estimate from Lemma
14
.

Using the estimate

\frac{\sin^{2}(\pi Mm\alpha)}{M\sin^{2}(\pi m\alpha)}\leq\min\left\{\frac{1}{M\|m\alpha\|^{2}},M\right\}

in (
20
), we deduce

\frac{1}{M}\sum_{N=0}^{M-1}\log P_{N}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{2}\log M+O\bigg(\log\log M+\sum_{1\leq m\leq M(\log M)^{2d+2}}\frac{1}{m}\min\left\{\frac{1}{M\|m\alpha\|^{2}},M\right\}\bigg).

Claim (
16
) thus follows from the second Diophantine sum estimate in Lemma
14
.

Next, we prove (
17
). Formula (
19
) yields

\frac{1}{M}\sum_{N=0}^{M-1}\left(\log P_{N}(\alpha)-H_{M}\right)^{2}=V_{M}+O\left(\frac{\sqrt{V_{M}}}{(\log M)^{d+1}}+\frac{1}{(\log M)^{2d+2}}\right)

(21)

with

H_{M}:=\sum_{1\leq m\leq M(\log M)^{2d+2}}\frac{1}{2m}=\frac{1}{2}\log M+O(\log\log M)

and

V_{M}:=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{N=0}^{M-1}\left(\sum_{1\leq m\leq M(\log M)^{2d+2}}\frac{\sin(\pi(2N+1)m\alpha)}{2m\sin(\pi m\alpha)}\right)^{2}.

Let us now expand the square in the previous formula. The diagonal terms satisfy

\begin{split}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{N=0}^{M-1}\sum_{1\leq m\leq M(\log M)^{2d+2}}&\frac{\sin^{2}(\pi(2N+1)m\alpha)}{4m^{2}\sin^{2}(\pi m\alpha)}\\
&=\sum_{1\leq m\leq M(\log M)^{2d+2}}\frac{1}{4m^{2}\sin^{2}(\pi m\alpha)}\left(\frac{1}{2}+O\left(\min\left\{\frac{1}{M\|2m\alpha\|},1\right\}\right)\right)\\
&=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}m^{2}\|m\alpha\|^{2}}+O\left(\max_{|\ell-k|\ll\log k}a_{\ell}^{2}\cdot\log\log M\right),\end{split}

where we used the third claim in Lemma
14
and the fact that
1/\sin^{2}(\pi x)=1/(\pi^{2}\|x\|^{2})+O(1)
. The last claim in Lemma
14
also shows that the contribution of the off-diagonal terms is negligible:

\begin{split}\bigg|\frac{1}{M}\sum_{N=0}^{M-1}&\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq m_{1},m_{2}\leq M(\log M)^{2d+2}\\
m_{1}\neq m_{2}\end{subarray}}\frac{\sin(\pi(2N+1)m_{1}\alpha)\sin(\pi(2N+1)m_{2}\alpha)}{2m_{1}\sin(\pi m_{1}\alpha)2m_{2}\sin(\pi m_{2}\alpha)}\bigg|\\
\ll&\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq m_{1},m_{2}\leq M(\log M)^{2d+2}\\
m_{1}\neq m_{2}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{m_{1}\|m_{1}\alpha\|m_{2}\|m_{2}\alpha\|}\min\left\{\frac{1}{M\|(m_{1}-m_{2})\alpha\|},1\right\}\\
&+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq m_{1},m_{2}\leq M(\log M)^{2d+2}\\
m_{1}\neq m_{2}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{m_{1}\|m_{1}\alpha\|m_{2}\|m_{2}\alpha\|}\min\left\{\frac{1}{M\|(m_{1}+m_{2})\alpha\|},1\right\}\\
\ll&\max_{|\ell-k|\ll\log k}a_{\ell}^{2}\cdot(\log\log M)^{4}.\end{split}

By the previous two formulas,

V_{M}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}m^{2}\|m\alpha\|^{2}}+O\left(\max_{|\ell-k|\ll\log k}a_{\ell}^{2}\cdot(\log\log M)^{4}\right).

In particular,
V_{M}\ll(\log M)^{2d+1}
(cf. formula (
37
) below), hence the error term on the right hand side of (
21
) is
O(1)
. The first claim (
16
) shows that the error of replacing
H_{M}
by
(1/2)\log M
in (
21
) is also negligible, and the claim (
17
) follows.
∎

3.2
Badly approximable irrationals

Fix a badly approximable
\alpha
. We first note that (
2
) and (
3
) follow easily from Proposition
13
. Indeed, the only missing piece is the observation

\log M\ll\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}m^{2}\|m\alpha\|^{2}}\ll\log M.

(22)

Here the upper bound follows from the pigeonhole principle as in Lemma
14
(cf. formula (
37
) below and
[
14
, Theorem 3]
). To see the lower bound, simply keep the convergent denominators
m=q_{k}\leq M
: each such term contributes
\gg 1
, and there are
\gg\log M
denominators
q_{k}\leq M
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

From (
2
), (
3
) and the Chebyshev inequality we obtain

\frac{1}{M}\left|\left\{1\leq N\leq M\,:\,\left|\log P_{N}(\alpha)-\frac{1}{2}\log M\right|\geq t\sqrt{\log M}\right\}\right|\ll\frac{1}{t^{2}}

with an implied constant depending only on
\alpha
. It remains to replace
\log M
by
\log N
in the previous formula. For any
M/(\log M)^{2}\leq N\leq M
we have
\log N=\log M+O(\log\log M)
, and so one readily checks that

\frac{1}{M}\left|\left\{1\leq N\leq M\,:\,\left|\log P_{N}(\alpha)-\frac{1}{2}\log N\right|\geq t\sqrt{\log N}\right\}\right|\ll\frac{1}{(\log M)^{2}}+\frac{1}{t^{2}}.

Note that
1/(\log M)^{2}
is the “probability” of
1\leq N\leq M/(\log M)^{2}
. The claim thus follows once

. However, the claim also trivially holds for

|\log P_{N}(\alpha)|\ll\log N
for all
N
.
∎

Next, assume in addition that

3.3
Central limit theorem for quadratic irrationals

Theorem
2
is obviously a special case of Theorem
4
, so it will be enough to prove the latter. We will actually work with
0\leq N<M
instead of
1\leq N\leq M
, but this of course does not affect the result. The reason is simply notational convenience, as the former interval is better suited for Ostrowski expansions.

We follow the “Fourier series approach” of Beck
[
7
, Chapter 4.4]
. There it is shown that for a quadratic irrational
\alpha
, the Birkhoff sum
S_{N}(\alpha)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}(\{n\alpha\}-1/2)
can be written in the form

S_{N}(\alpha)-E(\alpha)\log M=\sum_{1\leq m\leq M\log M}\frac{\cos(\pi(2N+1)m\alpha)}{2\pi m\sin(\pi m\alpha)}+O(\log\log M),\quad 0\leq N<M.

Using the tail estimate (
18
) with
H=\lceil M\log M\rceil
and following the steps in (
19
), we obtain the similar formula

\log P_{N}(\alpha)-\frac{1}{2}\log M=\sum_{1\leq m\leq M\log M}\frac{-\sin(\pi(2N+1)m\alpha)}{2m\sin(\pi m\alpha)}+O(\log\log M),\quad 0\leq N<M.

For the sake of readability, let us use the natural identification
(x,y)\leftrightarrow x+iy
between
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\mathbb{C}
. Then, for
0\leq N<M
,

\left(\log P_{N}(\alpha)-\frac{1}{2}\log M,\pi(S_{N}(\alpha)-E(\alpha)\log M)\right)\leftrightarrow\sum_{1\leq m\leq M\log M}\frac{ie^{2\pi i(N+1/2)m\alpha}}{2m\sin(\pi m\alpha)}+O(\log\log M).

(24)

Now let
\alpha=[0;a,a,a,\ldots]
. We will prove that given any
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
, with
M=q_{K}
a convergent denominator and any
q_{K}\ll Q_{K}\ll q_{K}
with implied constants depending only on
\alpha
,

T_{N}:=\sum_{1\leq m\leq Q_{K}\log Q_{K}}\frac{e^{2\pi i(N+\xi)m\alpha}}{2m\sin(\pi m\alpha)},\quad 0\leq N<q_{K}

satisfies the central limit theorem: for any convex set
C\subseteq\mathbb{C}
,

\frac{1}{q_{K}}\left|\left\{0\leq N<q_{K}\,:\,\frac{T_{N}}{\sigma(\alpha)\sqrt{\log q_{K}}}\in C\right\}\right|=\int_{C}\frac{e^{-|y|^{2}/2}}{2\pi}\,\mathrm{d}y+O\left(\frac{(\log K)^{2}}{K^{1/6}}\right)

(25)

with an implied constant depending only on
\alpha
.

We prove (
25
) in Sections
3.3.1
–
3.3.5
. Throughout the proof, constants and implied constants depend only on
\alpha
; in particular, every estimate is uniform in
\xi
and
Q_{K}
. We show how (
25
) implies Theorem
4
in Section
3.3.6
. We mention in advance that the parameters
\xi
and
Q_{K}
will play a technical role in extending the result from
M=q_{K}
(a convergent denominator) to general
M
.

3.3.1
Ostrowski expansion and
\alpha
-expansion

Fix an integer
a\geq 1
, and let
\alpha=[0;a,a,a,\ldots]
. We have

\alpha=\frac{\sqrt{a^{2}+4}-a}{2}\in(0,1)\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\frac{1}{\alpha}=\frac{\sqrt{a^{2}+4}+a}{2}\in(a,a+1).

The minimal polynomial of
\alpha
gives the useful relation
\alpha^{2}+a\alpha-1=0
. By solving the recursions, we deduce that the convergents
p_{k}/q_{k}
to
\alpha
are

p_{k}=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha^{2}+1}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha^{k}}-(-\alpha)^{k}\right)\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad q_{k}=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha^{2}+1}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha^{k+1}}-(-\alpha)^{k+1}\right).

Hence
q_{k}\alpha-p_{k}=(-1)^{k}\alpha^{k+1}
.

The so-called Ostrowski expansion of an integer
0\leq N<q_{K}
is the unique representation of the form
N=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}b_{k}q_{k}
, where
0\leq b_{0}\leq a-1
and
0\leq b_{k}\leq a\,\,(1\leq k\leq K-1)
are integers which satisfy the extra rule that
b_{k}=a
implies
b_{k-1}=0
. The Ostrowski expansion can be found with a greedy algorithm: define
b_{K-1}
as the largest integer
b
such that
N\geq bq_{K-1}
; this is obviously an integer in
[0,a]
. Then
0\leq N-b_{K-1}q_{K-1}<q_{K-1}
, and we iterate the same procedure. Note that if
b_{K-1}=a
, then
0\leq N-b_{K-1}q_{K-1}<q_{K}-aq_{K-1}=q_{K-2}
, hence
b_{K-2}=0
, explaining the extra rule.

The so-called
\alpha
-expansion of a real number
0\leq x<\alpha^{-K}
is entirely analogous: it is the (almost) unique representation of the form
x=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{K-1}c_{k}\alpha^{-k}
, where
0\leq c_{k}\leq a
are integers which satisfy the extra rule that
c_{k}=a
implies
c_{k-1}=0
. The
\alpha
-expansion can also be found with a greedy algorithm: define
c_{K-1}
as the largest integer
c
such that
x\geq c\alpha^{-(K-1)}
; this is obviously an integer in
[0,a]
. Then
0\leq x-c_{K-1}\alpha^{-(K-1)}<\alpha^{-(K-1)}
, and we iterate the same procedure. Note that if
c_{K-1}=a
, then
0\leq x-c_{K-1}\alpha^{-(K-1)}<\alpha^{-K}-a\alpha^{-(K-1)}=\alpha^{-(K-2)}
, hence
c_{K-2}=0
, explaining the extra rule. Exponentially fast convergence ensures that the infinite series
\sum_{k=-\infty}^{K-1}c_{k}\alpha^{-k}
indeed represents
x
.

The
\alpha
-expansion can be visualized by partitioning
[0,\alpha^{-K})
into
a+1
intervals corresponding to the possible values of
c_{K-1}
: the first
a
intervals are
[c\alpha^{-(K-1)},(c+1)\alpha^{-(K-1)})
,
0\leq c\leq a-1
, each of length
\alpha^{-(K-1)}
, and the last interval is
[a\alpha^{-(K-1)},\alpha^{-K})
, which is of length
\alpha^{-K}-a\alpha^{-(K-1)}=\alpha^{-(K-2)}
. The special length of the last interval represents self-similarity, and is related to the fact that the continued fraction of
\alpha
has period
1
. Iterating the procedure thus corresponds to a partitioning system of nested intervals. Note that the
\alpha
-expansion is not unique at the endpoints of these nested intervals, but it is in fact unique everywhere else. Since we will eventually work with a real number chosen from an interval uniformly at random, we can ignore this effect.

The following lemma constructs a simple coupling between these two expansions.

Lemma 15
.

For any real
x\in[0,\alpha^{-K})
, consider the
\alpha
-expansion
x=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{K-1}c_{k}\alpha^{-k}
, and the Ostrowski expansion
\lfloor x/(1+\alpha^{2})\rfloor=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}b_{k}q_{k}
. There exists a set
E\subset\mathbb{R}
of Lebesgue measure
\lambda(E)\ll K^{-10}\alpha^{-K}
such that for any
x\in[0,\alpha^{-K})\backslash E
, we have
b_{k}=c_{k}
for all
\lfloor\frac{10\log K}{\log(1/\alpha)}\rfloor\leq k\leq K-1
.

Remark
.

Note that
0\leq x<\alpha^{-K}
implies
0\leq x/(1+\alpha^{2})<\alpha^{-K}/(1+\alpha^{2})=q_{K}+O(\alpha^{K})
. By adding an interval of length
\ll\alpha^{K}
to
E
, we may assume that
0\leq\lfloor x/(1+\alpha^{2})\rfloor<q_{K}
.

Proof of Lemma
15
.

Set
k_{0}=\lfloor\frac{10\log K}{\log(1/\alpha)}\rfloor
. Let
A
be the set of all integers
N\in[0,q_{K})
whose Ostrowski expansion is of the form
N=\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{K-1}d_{k}q_{k}
, i.e. the first
k_{0}
digits are all zero. We will show that

E=(1+\alpha^{2})\bigcup_{N\in A}\left(N-1,N+1\right)\cup\left(N+q_{k_{0}-1}-1,N+q_{k_{0}-1}+1\right)

satisfies the claim of the lemma, where
(1+\alpha^{2})H=\{(1+\alpha^{2})y\,:\,y\in H\}
. The cardinality of
A
is the number of legitimate Ostrowski sequences
(d_{K-1},d_{K-2},\ldots,d_{k_{0}})
of length
K-k_{0}
, hence
|A|\ll q_{K-k_{0}}\ll\alpha^{k_{0}-K}\ll K^{-10}\alpha^{-K}
. Consequently,
\lambda(E)\ll K^{-10}\alpha^{-K}
.

Now let
x\in[0,\alpha^{-K})\backslash E
. Assume first that
0\leq c_{k_{0}}<a
. By the construction of the
\alpha
-expansion,

\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{K-1}c_{k}\frac{\alpha^{-k}}{1+\alpha^{2}}\leq\frac{x}{1+\alpha^{2}}<(c_{k_{0}}+1)\frac{\alpha^{-k_{0}}}{1+\alpha^{2}}+\sum_{k=k_{0}+1}^{K-1}c_{k}\frac{\alpha^{-k}}{1+\alpha^{2}}.

Replacing
\frac{\alpha^{-k}}{1+\alpha^{2}}
by
q_{k}=\frac{\alpha^{-k}}{1+\alpha^{2}}+O(\alpha^{k})
introduces an error
O(\alpha^{k_{0}})
, therefore very roughly,

\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{K-1}c_{k}q_{k}-\frac{1}{100}\leq\frac{x}{1+\alpha^{2}}<(c_{k_{0}}+1)q_{k_{0}}+\sum_{k=k_{0}+1}^{K-1}c_{k}q_{k}+\frac{1}{100}.

By the assumption
x\not\in E
, this can be improved to

\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{K-1}c_{k}q_{k}\leq\frac{x}{1+\alpha^{2}}<(c_{k_{0}}+1)q_{k_{0}}+\sum_{k=k_{0}+1}^{K-1}c_{k}q_{k}.

The integer part thus also satisfies

\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{K-1}c_{k}q_{k}\leq\left\lfloor\frac{x}{1+\alpha^{2}}\right\rfloor<(c_{k_{0}}+1)q_{k_{0}}+\sum_{k=k_{0}+1}^{K-1}c_{k}q_{k},

showing that its Ostrowski expansion is of the form
\lfloor x/(1+\alpha^{2})\rfloor=\sum_{k=0}^{k_{0}-1}b_{k}q_{k}+\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{K-1}c_{k}q_{k}
, as claimed.

Assume next that
c_{k_{0}}=a
. By the construction of the
\alpha
-expansion, we now have

\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{K-1}c_{k}\frac{\alpha^{-k}}{1+\alpha^{2}}\leq\frac{x}{1+\alpha^{2}}<\frac{\alpha^{-k_{0}+1}}{1+\alpha^{2}}+\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{K-1}c_{k}\frac{\alpha^{-k}}{1+\alpha^{2}}.

We similarly deduce that

\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{K-1}c_{k}q_{k}-\frac{1}{100}\leq\frac{x}{1+\alpha^{2}}<q_{k_{0}-1}+\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{K-1}c_{k}q_{k}+\frac{1}{100},

and by the assumption
x\not\in E
, that

\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{K-1}c_{k}q_{k}\leq\left\lfloor\frac{x}{1+\alpha^{2}}\right\rfloor<q_{k_{0}-1}+\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{K-1}c_{k}q_{k}.

Therefore the Ostrowski expansion is of the form
\lfloor x/(1+\alpha^{2})\rfloor=\sum_{k=0}^{k_{0}-2}b_{k}q_{k}+\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{K-1}c_{k}q_{k}
, as claimed.
∎

The main advantage of the
\alpha
-expansion over the Ostrowski expansion is that the sequence of digits forms a Markov chain, which in turn leads to independence; a crucial property in the proof of the central limit theorem. More precisely, let us choose a real number
x
from the interval
[0,\alpha^{-K})
uniformly at random, and consider the
\alpha
-expansion
x=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{K-1}c_{k}\alpha^{-k}
. The digits
c_{k}
are thus random variables. Using the geometric interpretation of the
\alpha
-expansion in terms of a partitioning system of nested intervals, it is not difficult to see that the sequence
c_{k}
is in fact a Markov chain: the state space is the finite set
\{0,1,\ldots,a\}
, and the chain is in the state
c_{K-t}
at time
t\in\mathbb{N}
. The distribution of the initial state, corresponding to the lengths of the partitioning intervals, is

\alpha^{K}\lambda\left(\left\{0\leq x<\alpha^{-K}\,:\,c_{K-1}=c\right\}\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\alpha&\textrm{if }0\leq c<a,\\
\alpha^{2}&\textrm{if }c=a.\end{array}\right.

The transition probabilities are described by the
(a+1)\times(a+1)
matrix

\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\alpha&\alpha&\cdots&\alpha&\alpha^{2}\\
\alpha&\alpha&\cdots&\alpha&\alpha^{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\alpha&\alpha&\cdots&\alpha&\alpha^{2}\\
1&0&\cdots&0&0\end{array}\right).

In other words, given any
0\leq c<a
, the probability of transitioning to a state
c^{\prime}
is
\alpha
if
0\leq c^{\prime}<a
, and
\alpha^{2}
if
c^{\prime}=a
. The last line of the matrix corresponds to the extra rule of
\alpha
-expansions: after the state
a
, we transition to the state
0
with probability
1
. One readily checks that this Markov chain is irreducible and aperiodic, and that its (unique) stationary distribution vector is
(\frac{\alpha+\alpha^{2}}{1+\alpha^{2}},\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha^{2}},\ldots,\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha^{2}},\frac{\alpha^{2}}{1+\alpha^{2}})
.

3.3.2
The covariance matrix

The covariance matrix of a complex-valued random variable
X=Y+iZ
is defined as

\mathrm{Cov}(X)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbb{E}(Y^{2})&\mathbb{E}(YZ)\\
\mathbb{E}(YZ)&\mathbb{E}(Z^{2})\end{array}\right)-\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}(\mathbb{E}Y)^{2}&\mathbb{E}Y\mathbb{E}Z\\
\mathbb{E}Y\mathbb{E}Z&(\mathbb{E}Z)^{2}\end{array}\right).

Given two matrices
A,B
, the notation
A=B+O(f(K))
means that
A_{mn}=B_{mn}+O(f(K))
for all entries.

Lemma 16
.

For any
1\leq M^{\prime}\leq M
,

\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{1}{m\|m\alpha\|}\min\left\{\frac{1}{M^{\prime}\|m\alpha\|},1\right\}\ll\left(1+\log\frac{M}{M^{\prime}}\right)^{2}.

Proof.

This is a slight modification of the third claim in Lemma
14
. We have

\sum_{q_{\ell}\leq m<q_{\ell+1}}\frac{1}{m\|m\alpha\|}\cdot\frac{1}{M^{\prime}\|m\alpha\|}\ll\frac{1}{q_{\ell}M^{\prime}}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j^{2}/q_{\ell+1}^{2}}\ll\frac{q_{\ell}}{M^{\prime}},

and by summing over all
\ell
such that
q_{\ell}\leq M^{\prime}
, we obtain
\sum_{m=1}^{M^{\prime}}\frac{1}{m\|m\alpha\|}\cdot\frac{1}{M^{\prime}\|m\alpha\|}\ll 1
. There are
\ll 1+\log(M/M^{\prime})
convergent denominators
M^{\prime}<q_{\ell}\leq M
, and they all satisfy

\sum_{q_{\ell}\leq m<q_{\ell+1}}\frac{1}{m\|m\alpha\|}\min\left\{\frac{1}{M^{\prime}\|m\alpha\|},1\right\}\ll\frac{1}{q_{\ell}}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j/q_{\ell+1}}\min\left\{\frac{1}{M^{\prime}j/q_{\ell+1}},1\right\}\ll\log\frac{q_{\ell+1}}{M^{\prime}}.

Hence

\sum_{m=M^{\prime}+1}^{M}\frac{1}{m\|m\alpha\|}\min\left\{\frac{1}{M^{\prime}\|m\alpha\|},1\right\}\ll\left(1+\log\frac{M}{M^{\prime}}\right)^{2},

and the claim follows.
∎

Lemma 17
.

If the integer
N
is chosen from
[0,q_{K})
uniformly at random, then
\mathbb{E}T_{N}=O((\log K)^{2})
, and

\mathrm{Cov}(T_{N})=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\sigma(\alpha)^{2}\log q_{K}&0\\
0&\sigma(\alpha)^{2}\log q_{K}\end{array}\right)+O((\log K)^{4}).

Proof.

An application of Lemma
16
shows that the expected value is

\begin{split}|\mathbb{E}T_{N}|&=\left|\frac{1}{q_{K}}\sum_{N=0}^{q_{K}-1}\sum_{1\leq m\leq Q_{K}\log Q_{K}}\frac{e^{2\pi i(N+\xi)m\alpha}}{2m\sin(\pi m\alpha)}\right|\ll\sum_{1\leq m\leq Q_{K}\log Q_{K}}\frac{1}{m\|m\alpha\|}\min\left\{\frac{1}{q_{K}\|m\alpha\|},1\right\}\\
&\ll(\log K)^{2},\end{split}

as claimed.

Repeating the steps in the estimate of
V_{M}
in the proof of Proposition
13
with obvious modifications, we deduce that
\mathbb{E}(\mathrm{Re}\,T_{N})^{2}
and
\mathbb{E}(\mathrm{Im}\,T_{N})^{2}
are both

\sum_{m=1}^{q_{K}}\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}m^{2}\|m\alpha\|^{2}}+O((\log K)^{4}).

Formula (
23
) shows that this is
\sigma(\alpha)^{2}\log q_{K}+O((\log K)^{4})
. Therefore the diagonal entries of
\mathrm{Cov}(T_{N})
are
\sigma(\alpha)^{2}\log q_{K}+O((\log K)^{4})
, as claimed. Next, consider

\mathbb{E}(\mathrm{Re}\,T_{N}\mathrm{Im}\,T_{N})=\frac{1}{q_{K}}\sum_{N=0}^{q_{K}-1}\sum_{1\leq m_{1},m_{2}\leq Q_{K}\log Q_{K}}\frac{\sin(2\pi(N+\xi)m_{1}\alpha)\cos(2\pi(N+\xi)m_{2}\alpha)}{4m_{1}m_{2}\sin(\pi m_{1}\alpha)\sin(\pi m_{2}\alpha)}.

The contribution of the terms
m_{1}=m_{2}
is

\frac{1}{q_{K}}\sum_{N=0}^{q_{K}-1}\sum_{1\leq m\leq Q_{K}\log Q_{K}}\frac{\sin(4\pi(N+\xi)m\alpha)}{8m^{2}\sin^{2}(\pi m\alpha)}\ll\sum_{1\leq m\leq Q_{K}\log Q_{K}}\frac{1}{m^{2}\|m\alpha\|^{2}}\min\left\{\frac{1}{q_{K}\|2m\alpha\|},1\right\}\ll\log K,

where we used the third claim in Lemma
14
. The contribution of the terms
m_{1}\neq m_{2}
is

\begin{split}\frac{1}{q_{K}}\sum_{N=0}^{q_{K}-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq m_{1},m_{2}\leq Q_{K}\log Q_{K}\\
m_{1}\neq m_{2}\end{subarray}}&\frac{\sin(2\pi(N+\xi)(m_{1}-m_{2})\alpha)+\sin(2\pi(N+\xi)(m_{1}+m_{2})\alpha)}{8m_{1}m_{2}\sin(\pi m_{1}\alpha)\sin(\pi m_{2}\alpha)}\\
\ll&\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq m_{1},m_{2}\leq Q_{K}\log Q_{K}\\
m_{1}\neq m_{2}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{m_{1}\|m_{1}\alpha\|m_{2}\|m_{2}\alpha\|}\min\left\{\frac{1}{q_{K}\|(m_{1}-m_{2})\alpha\|},1\right\}\\
&+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq m_{1},m_{2}\leq Q_{K}\log Q_{K}\\
m_{1}\neq m_{2}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{m_{1}\|m_{1}\alpha\|m_{2}\|m_{2}\alpha\|}\min\left\{\frac{1}{q_{K}\|(m_{1}+m_{2})\alpha\|},1\right\}\\
\ll&(\log K)^{4},\end{split}

where we used the last claim in Lemma
14
. The off-diagonal entries of
\mathrm{Cov}(T_{N})
are thus
O((\log K)^{4})
, as claimed.
∎

3.3.3
Approximation in
L^{2}

Any
1\leq m\leq Q_{K}\log Q_{K}
has a unique Ostrowski expansion of the form
m=\sum_{k=h}^{H}d_{k}q_{k}
, where
0\leq h=h(m)\leq H=H(m)
are integers, and
d_{h},d_{H}\neq 0
.

Lemma 18
.

We have
\alpha^{h(m)-H(m)}\ll m\|m\alpha\|\ll\alpha^{h(m)-H(m)}
.

Proof.

Note that
q_{H}\leq m<q_{H+1}
, in particular,
\alpha^{-H}\ll m\ll\alpha^{-H}
. It remains to show that
\alpha^{h}\ll\|m\alpha\|\ll\alpha^{h}
. Observe that

m\alpha=\sum_{k=h}^{H}d_{k}q_{k}\alpha\equiv\sum_{k=h}^{H}d_{k}(q_{k}\alpha-p_{k})=\sum_{k=h}^{H}(-1)^{k}d_{k}\alpha^{k+1}\pmod{1}.

Since
0<d_{h}\leq a
and
0\leq d_{k}\leq a
for all
k
, we have

d_{h}\alpha^{h+1}-d_{h+1}\alpha^{h+2}+d_{h+2}\alpha^{h+3}-\cdots\geq\alpha^{h+1}-a\alpha^{h+2}-a\alpha^{h+4}-\cdots=\alpha^{h+1}\left(1-\frac{a\alpha}{1+\alpha^{2}}\right),

and similarly,

d_{h}\alpha^{h+1}-d_{h+1}\alpha^{h+2}+d_{h+2}\alpha^{h+3}-\cdots\leq a\alpha^{h+1}+a\alpha^{h+3}+a\alpha^{h+5}+\cdots=\frac{a\alpha^{h+1}}{1+\alpha^{2}}.

Therefore

\alpha^{h}\ll\alpha^{h+1}\left(1-\frac{a\alpha}{1+\alpha^{2}}\right)\leq\|m\alpha\|\leq\frac{a\alpha^{h+1}}{1+\alpha^{2}}\ll\alpha^{h},

as claimed.
∎

For the rest of the proof, let

k_{0}:=\left\lfloor\frac{10\log K}{\log(1/\alpha)}\right\rfloor\textrm{ and }S:=\left\{1\leq m\leq Q_{K}\log Q_{K}\,:\,H(m)-h(m)\leq k_{0}\right\}.

Lemma
18
yields the implication
m\not\in S\,\,\Rightarrow\,\,m\|m\alpha\|\gg K^{10}
. We start by showing that the terms in
T_{N}
with
m\not\in S
have a negligible contribution.

Lemma 19
.

We have

\frac{1}{q_{K}}\sum_{N=0}^{q_{K}-1}\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq m\leq Q_{K}\log Q_{K}\\
m\not\in S\end{subarray}}\frac{e^{2\pi i(N+\xi)m\alpha}}{2m\sin(\pi m\alpha)}\bigg|^{2}\ll 1.

Proof.

Expanding the square and considering the diagonal and off-diagonal terms separately leads to

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{q_{K}}\sum_{N=0}^{q_{K}-1}\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq m\leq Q_{K}\log Q_{K}\\
m\not\in S\end{subarray}}\frac{e^{2\pi i(N+\xi)m\alpha}}{2m\sin(\pi m\alpha)}\bigg|^{2}\\
&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq m\leq Q_{K}\log Q_{K}\\
m\not\in S\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{4m^{2}\sin^{2}(\pi m\alpha)}+\frac{1}{q_{K}}\sum_{N=0}^{q_{K}-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq m_{1},m_{2}\leq Q_{K}\log Q_{K}\\
m_{1},m_{2}\not\in S\\
m_{1}\neq m_{2}\end{subarray}}\frac{e^{2\pi i(N+\xi)(m_{1}-m_{2})\alpha}}{4m_{1}m_{2}\sin(\pi m_{1}\alpha)\sin(\pi m_{2}\alpha)}\\
&\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq m\leq Q_{K}\log Q_{K}\\
m\not\in S\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{m^{2}\|m\alpha\|^{2}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq m_{1},m_{2}\leq Q_{K}\log Q_{K}\\
m_{1},m_{2}\not\in S\\
m_{1}\neq m_{2}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{m_{1}\|m_{1}\alpha\|m_{2}\|m_{2}\alpha\|}\min\left\{\frac{1}{q_{K}\|(m_{1}-m_{2})\alpha\|},1\right\}\\
&\ll\frac{1}{K^{10}}\sum_{1\leq m\leq Q_{K}\log Q_{K}}\frac{1}{m\|m\alpha\|}+\frac{1}{K^{10}}\sum_{1\leq m_{1}\leq Q_{K}\log Q_{K}}\frac{1}{m_{1}\|m_{1}\alpha\|}\sum_{1\leq m^{\prime}\leq Q_{K}\log Q_{K}}\frac{1}{q_{K}\|m^{\prime}\alpha\|}\\
&\ll 1.\end{split}

Note that instead of
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
, we summed over
m_{1}
and
m^{\prime}:=|m_{1}-m_{2}|
, and used the first claim in Lemma
14
combined with summation by parts.
∎

We now show that the dependence of
T_{N}
on the first
k_{0}
Ostrowski digits of
N
is very weak.

Lemma 20
.

For any integer
0\leq N<q_{K}
with Ostrowski expansion
N=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}b_{k}q_{k}
, let
g(N)=\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{K-1}b_{k}q_{k}
. We have

T_{N}=\sum_{m\in S}\frac{e^{2\pi i(g(N)+\xi)m\alpha}}{2m\sin(\pi m\alpha)}+Z_{N},\qquad 0\leq N<q_{K}

with some
Z_{N}
that satisfies
q_{K}^{-1}\sum_{N=0}^{q_{K}-1}|Z_{N}|^{2}\ll(\log K)^{4}
.

Proof.

The error term
Z_{N}
in the lemma can be written in the form

\begin{split}Z_{N}:=&T_{N}-\sum_{m\in S}\frac{e^{2\pi i(g(N)+\xi)m\alpha}}{2m\sin(\pi m\alpha)}\\
=&\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq m\leq Q_{K}\log Q_{K}\\
m\not\in S\end{subarray}}\frac{e^{2\pi i(N+\xi)m\alpha}}{2m\sin(\pi m\alpha)}+\sum_{m\in S}\frac{(e^{2\pi ir(N)m\alpha}-1)e^{2\pi i(g(N)+\xi)m\alpha}}{2m\sin(\pi m\alpha)},\end{split}

where
r(N):=N-g(N)
. By the triangle inequality for the
L^{2}
norm and Lemma
19
, it will be enough to estimate the second moment of the second term, that is,

\frac{1}{q_{K}}\sum_{N=0}^{q_{K}-1}\bigg|\sum_{m\in S}\frac{(e^{2\pi ir(N)m\alpha}-1)e^{2\pi i(g(N)+\xi)m\alpha}}{2m\sin(\pi m\alpha)}\bigg|^{2}=R_{\mathrm{diag}}+R_{\mathrm{off-diag}},

where

\begin{split}R_{\mathrm{diag}}&=\frac{1}{q_{K}}\sum_{N=0}^{q_{K}-1}\sum_{m\in S}\frac{|e^{2\pi ir(N)m\alpha}-1|^{2}}{4m^{2}\sin^{2}(\pi m\alpha)},\\
R_{\mathrm{off-diag}}&=\frac{1}{q_{K}}\sum_{N=0}^{q_{K}-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},m_{2}\in S\\
m_{1}\neq m_{2}\end{subarray}}\frac{(e^{2\pi ir(N)m_{1}\alpha}-1)(e^{-2\pi ir(N)m_{2}\alpha}-1)e^{2\pi i(g(N)+\xi)(m_{1}-m_{2})\alpha}}{4m_{1}m_{2}\sin(\pi m_{1}\alpha)\sin(\pi m_{2}\alpha)}.\end{split}

We thus need to show that
R_{\mathrm{diag}}+R_{\mathrm{off-diag}}\ll(\log K)^{4}
. Clearly,
r(N)=\sum_{k=0}^{k_{0}-1}b_{k}q_{k}<q_{k_{0}}\ll K^{10}
. Therefore

\sum_{m\in S}\frac{|e^{2\pi ir(N)m\alpha}-1|^{2}}{4m^{2}\sin^{2}(\pi m\alpha)}\ll\sum_{1\leq m\leq K^{20}}\frac{1}{m^{2}\|m\alpha\|^{2}}+\sum_{K^{20}<m\leq Q_{K}\log Q_{K}}\frac{r(N)^{2}}{m^{2}}\ll\log K

uniformly in
0\leq N<q_{K}
, where we used formula (
22
). This shows that
R_{\mathrm{diag}}\ll\log K
.

We now estimate
R_{\mathrm{off-diag}}
. Let
A(N_{0})=\{0\leq N<q_{K}\,:\,r(N)=N_{0}\}
. Note that
g(N)=N-N_{0}
for all
N\in A(N_{0})
. For the sake of readability, set

W(N_{0},m_{1},m_{2}):=(e^{2\pi iN_{0}m_{1}\alpha}-1)(e^{-2\pi iN_{0}m_{2}\alpha}-1)e^{2\pi i(-N_{0}+\xi)(m_{1}-m_{2})\alpha}.

Then
R_{\mathrm{off-diag}}
can be written in the form

R_{\mathrm{off-diag}}=\frac{1}{q_{K}}\sum_{0\leq N_{0}\ll K^{10}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},m_{2}\in S\\
m_{1}\neq m_{2}\end{subarray}}\frac{W(N_{0},m_{1},m_{2})}{4m_{1}m_{2}\sin(\pi m_{1}\alpha)\sin(\pi m_{2}\alpha)}\sum_{N\in A(N_{0})}e^{2\pi iN(m_{1}-m_{2})\alpha}.

Clearly
|W(N_{0},m_{1},m_{2})|\leq 4
, and also
|W(N_{0},m_{1},m_{2})|\ll\|m_{1}\alpha\|\|m_{2}\alpha\|N_{0}^{2}
. Let

D(N_{0})=\bigg\{1\leq d\leq Q_{K}\log Q_{K}\,:\,\bigg|\sum_{N\in A(N_{0})}e^{2\pi iNd\alpha}\bigg|\geq\frac{q_{K}}{K^{14}}\bigg\}.

The contribution of the terms for which
|m_{1}-m_{2}|\not\in D(N_{0})
is negligible:

\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},m_{2}\in S\\
0<|m_{1}-m_{2}|\not\in D(N_{0})\end{subarray}}\frac{W(N_{0},m_{1},m_{2})}{4m_{1}m_{2}\sin(\pi m_{1}\alpha)\sin(\pi m_{2}\alpha)}\sum_{N\in A(N_{0})}e^{2\pi iN(m_{1}-m_{2})\alpha}\bigg|\\
\ll\sum_{1\leq m_{1},m_{2}\leq Q_{K}\log Q_{K}}\frac{1}{m_{1}\|m_{1}\alpha\|m_{2}\|m_{2}\alpha\|}\cdot\frac{q_{K}}{K^{14}}\ll\frac{q_{K}}{K^{10}}.

The contribution of the terms for which
|m_{1}-m_{2}|=d\in D(N_{0})
is

\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},m_{2}\in S\\
|m_{1}-m_{2}|=d\end{subarray}}\frac{W(N_{0},m_{1},m_{2})}{4m_{1}m_{2}\sin(\pi m_{1}\alpha)\sin(\pi m_{2}\alpha)}\sum_{N\in A(N_{0})}e^{2\pi iN(m_{1}-m_{2})\alpha}\bigg|\\
\ll|A(N_{0})|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq m_{1},m_{2}\leq Q_{K}\log Q_{K}\\
|m_{1}-m_{2}|=d\end{subarray}}\frac{|W(N_{0},m_{1},m_{2})|}{m_{1}\|m_{1}\alpha\|m_{2}\|m_{2}\alpha\|}.

By symmetry it is enough to sum over
m_{1}<m_{2}
, in which case
m_{2}=m_{1}+d
. Hence

R_{\mathrm{off-diag}}\ll\frac{1}{q_{K}}\sum_{0\leq N_{0}\ll K^{10}}\left(\frac{q_{K}}{K^{10}}+|A(N_{0})|\sum_{d\in D(N_{0})}\sum_{1\leq m\leq Q_{K}\log Q_{K}}\frac{|W(N_{0},m,m+d)|}{m\|m\alpha\|(m+d)\|(m+d)\alpha\|}\right).

Here

\sum_{1\leq m\leq Q_{K}\log Q_{K}}\frac{|W(N_{0},m,m+d)|}{m\|m\alpha\|(m+d)\|(m+d)\alpha\|}\ll\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{N_{0}^{2}}{m(m+d)}\ll K^{20}\frac{\log d}{d},

which is negligible if, say,
d>K^{50}
. For smaller
d
, the contribution of all
m>K^{50}
is also negligible:

\sum_{K^{50}<m\leq Q_{K}\log Q_{K}}\frac{|W(N_{0},m,m+d)|}{m\|m\alpha\|(m+d)\|(m+d)\alpha\|}\ll\sum_{K^{50}<m\leq Q_{K}\log Q_{K}}\frac{N_{0}^{2}}{m(m+d)}\ll\frac{1}{K^{30}}.

Therefore our estimate for
R_{\mathrm{off-diag}}
simplifies to

\begin{split}R_{\mathrm{off-diag}}&\ll 1+\frac{1}{q_{K}}\sum_{0\leq N_{0}\ll K^{10}}|A(N_{0})|\left(\frac{\log K}{K^{30}}|D(N_{0})|+\sum_{1\leq m,d\leq K^{50}}\frac{1}{m\|m\alpha\|(m+d)\|(m+d)\alpha\|}\right)\\
&\ll 1+\frac{1}{q_{K}}\sum_{0\leq N_{0}\ll K^{10}}|A(N_{0})|\left(\frac{\log K}{K^{30}}|D(N_{0})|+(\log K)^{4}\right)\\
&\ll\frac{\log K}{K^{30}}\max_{0\leq N_{0}\ll K^{10}}|D(N_{0})|+(\log K)^{4}.\end{split}

In the last step we used
\sum_{N_{0}\geq 0}|A(N_{0})|=q_{K}
. It remains to estimate
|D(N_{0})|
. For any
N_{0}\geq 0
,

\begin{split}\sum_{1\leq d\leq Q_{K}\log Q_{K}}\bigg|\sum_{N\in A(N_{0})}e^{2\pi iNd\alpha}\bigg|^{2}&=\sum_{1\leq d\leq Q_{K}\log Q_{K}}\bigg(|A(N_{0})|+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N,N^{\prime}\in A(N_{0})\\
N\neq N^{\prime}\end{subarray}}e^{2\pi i(N-N^{\prime})d\alpha}\bigg)\\
&\ll|A(N_{0})|q_{K}\log q_{K}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N,N^{\prime}\in A(N_{0})\\
N\neq N^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\|(N-N^{\prime})\alpha\|}\\
&\ll q_{K}^{2}K+q_{K}\sum_{\ell=1}^{q_{K}-1}\frac{1}{\|\ell\alpha\|}\\
&\ll q_{K}^{2}K.\end{split}

An application of the Chebyshev inequality thus yields
|D(N_{0})|\ll K^{29}
. Therefore
R_{\mathrm{off-diag}}\ll(\log K)^{4}
, as claimed.
∎

3.3.4
Approximation by a Markov chain

The main goal of this section is to approximate
T_{N}
in terms of the Markov chain formed by the digits in the
\alpha
-expansion of a real number. Throughout, the integer
N
is chosen from
[0,q_{K})
uniformly at random, whereas the real
x
is chosen from
[0,\alpha^{-K})
uniformly at random and has
\alpha
-expansion
x=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{K-1}c_{k}\alpha^{-k}
.

The Ostrowski expansion of any
1\leq m\leq Q_{K}\log Q_{K}
is of the form
m=\sum_{k=h}^{H}d_{k}q_{k}
with some integers
0\leq h=h(m)\leq H=H(m)
and some digits
d_{k}=d_{k}(m)
, where
d_{h},d_{H}\neq 0
. We use the convention
d_{k}=d_{k}(m)=0
whenever
k\not\in[h,H]
. Let

G(x,m)=\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha^{2}}\left(\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{K-1}(-1)^{k}c_{k}d_{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{k_{0}}\alpha^{j}\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{K-1}(-1)^{k+j}c_{k}d_{k+j}+\sum_{j=1}^{k_{0}}\alpha^{j}\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{K-1}(-1)^{k}c_{k}d_{k-j}\right),

and define

U(x)=\sum_{m\in S}\frac{e^{2\pi i(G(x,m)+\xi m\alpha)}}{2m\sin(\pi m\alpha)}.

The point is that for a given
m\in S
, the function
G(x,m)
depends only on
c_{k}
,
k\in[h-k_{0},H+k_{0}]
, that is, only on
\ll k_{0}\ll\log K
of all the variables
c_{k}
,
k_{0}\leq k\leq K-1
.

For the sake of readability, from now on we use fully probabilistic notation. We thus write

\begin{split}\Pr\left(T_{N}\in A\right)&=\frac{1}{q_{K}}\left|\left\{0\leq N<q_{K}\,:\,T_{N}\in A\right\}\right|,\\
\Pr\left(U(x)\in A\right)&=\alpha^{K}\lambda\left(\left\{0\leq x<\alpha^{-K}\,:\,U(x)\in A\right\}\right)\end{split}

for a Lebesgue measurable set
A\subseteq\mathbb{C}
. Further, let

\Sigma_{K}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\sigma(\alpha)^{2}\log q_{K}&0\\
0&\sigma(\alpha)^{2}\log q_{K}\end{array}\right).

The following lemma reduces the central limit theorem for
T_{N}
to that for
U(x)
.

Lemma 21
.

We have
\mathbb{E}U(x)=O((\log K)^{2})
and
\mathrm{Cov}(U(x))=\Sigma_{K}+O(K^{1/2}(\log K)^{2})
. Further,

\sup_{C\subseteq\mathbb{C}}\left|\Pr\left(\frac{T_{N}}{\sigma(\alpha)\sqrt{\log q_{K}}}\in C\right)-\Phi(C)\right|=\sup_{C\subseteq\mathbb{C}}\left|\Pr\left(\frac{U(x)}{\sigma(\alpha)\sqrt{\log q_{K}}}\in C\right)-\Phi(C)\right|+O\left(\frac{(\log K)^{4/3}}{K^{1/3}}\right),

where the supremum is over all convex sets
C\subseteq\mathbb{C}
, and
\Phi(C)=\int_{C}\frac{e^{-|y|^{2}/2}}{2\pi}\,\mathrm{d}y
.

Proof.

Let
N_{x}=\lfloor x/(1+\alpha^{2})\rfloor
. Since
0\leq x/(1+\alpha^{2})<\alpha^{-K}/(1+\alpha^{2})=q_{K}+O(\alpha^{K})
, the random variable
N_{x}
is supported on either
[0,q_{K})
or
[0,q_{K}]
. For any
0\leq n\leq q_{K}-2
we have

\Pr(N_{x}=n)=\alpha^{K}(1+\alpha^{2})=q_{K}^{-1}+O(\alpha^{3K})=\Pr(N=n)+O(\alpha^{3K}),

and for
n\in\{q_{K}-1,q_{K}\}
we still have
\Pr(N_{x}=n)=\Pr(N=n)+O(\alpha^{2K})
. Therefore the distributions of
N_{x}
and
N
have exponentially small distance in total variation:

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|\Pr(N_{x}=n)-\Pr(N=n)|\ll\alpha^{2K}.

(26)

Using the pointwise bound

|T_{N}|\ll\sum_{1\leq m\leq Q_{K}\log Q_{K}}\frac{1}{m\|m\alpha\|}\ll(\log Q_{K})^{2}\ll K^{2}

(27)

and the estimate (
26
), we deduce
\mathbb{E}T_{N_{x}}=\mathbb{E}T_{N}+O(K^{2}\alpha^{2K})
and
\mathrm{Cov}(T_{N_{x}})=\mathrm{Cov}(T_{N})+O(K^{4}\alpha^{2K})
. Hence by Lemma
17
,
\mathbb{E}T_{N_{x}}=O((\log K)^{2})
and
\mathrm{Cov}(T_{N_{x}})=\Sigma_{K}+O((\log K)^{4})
.

Recall the definition of
g
and
Z_{N}
from Lemma
20
. We similarly deduce
\mathbb{E}|Z_{N_{x}}|^{2}=\mathbb{E}|Z_{N}|^{2}+O(K^{4}\alpha^{2K})=O((\log K)^{4})
. Therefore

U_{1}(x):=\sum_{m\in S}\frac{\exp\left(2\pi i\left(g(N_{x})+\xi\right)m\alpha\right)}{2m\sin(\pi m\alpha)}=T_{N_{x}}-Z_{N_{x}}

satisfies
\mathbb{E}U_{1}(x)=O((\log K)^{2})
and
\mathrm{Cov}(U_{1}(x))=\Sigma_{K}+O(K^{1/2}(\log K)^{2})
. Using the coupling between the Ostrowski expansion and the
\alpha
-expansion constructed in Lemma
15
, here
g(N_{x})=\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{K-1}c_{k}q_{k}
with probability
\geq 1-O(K^{-10})
. This together with a pointwise bound of the form (
27
) yields that

U_{2}(x):=\sum_{m\in S}\frac{\exp\left(2\pi i\left(\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{K-1}c_{k}q_{k}+\xi\right)m\alpha\right)}{2m\sin(\pi m\alpha)}

also satisfies
\mathbb{E}U_{2}(x)=O((\log K)^{2})
and
\mathrm{Cov}(U_{2}(x))=\Sigma_{K}+O(K^{1/2}(\log K)^{2})
.

Observe that

\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{K-1}c_{k}q_{k}m\alpha=\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{K-1}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}c_{k}q_{k}d_{\ell}q_{\ell}\alpha=\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{K-1}c_{k}\left(d_{k}q_{k}^{2}\alpha+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}d_{k+j}q_{k}q_{k+j}\alpha+\sum_{j=1}^{k}d_{k-j}q_{k}q_{k-j}\alpha\right).

Working modulo
1
, here

\begin{split}q_{k}^{2}\alpha&\equiv q_{k}(-1)^{k}\alpha^{k+1}=\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha^{2}}(-1)^{k}+O(\alpha^{2k})\pmod{1},\\
q_{k}q_{k+j}\alpha&\equiv q_{k}(-1)^{k+j}\alpha^{k+j+1}=\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha^{2}}(-1)^{k+j}\alpha^{j}+O(\alpha^{2k+j})\pmod{1},\\
q_{k}q_{k-j}\alpha&\equiv q_{k-j}(-1)^{k}\alpha^{k+1}=\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha^{2}}(-1)^{k}\alpha^{j}+O(\alpha^{2k-j})\pmod{1},\end{split}

hence

\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{K-1}c_{k}q_{k}m\alpha\equiv\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha^{2}}\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{K-1}(-1)^{k}c_{k}\left(d_{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(-\alpha)^{j}d_{k+j}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\alpha^{j}d_{k-j}\right)+O(\alpha^{k_{0}})\pmod{1}.

Discarding the terms
j>k_{0}
from both sums in the previous formula introduces the error
\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{K-1}\sum_{j=k_{0}+1}^{\infty}\alpha^{j}\ll K\alpha^{k_{0}}\ll K^{-9}
, thus
\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{K-1}c_{k}q_{k}m\alpha\equiv G(x,m)+O(K^{-9})\pmod{1}
. In particular,

|U_{2}(x)-U(x)|\ll\sum_{1\leq m\leq Q_{K}\log Q_{K}}\frac{K^{-9}}{m\|m\alpha\|}\ll K^{-7}.

(28)

Therefore
\mathbb{E}U(x)=O((\log K)^{2})
and
\mathrm{Cov}(U(x))=\Sigma_{K}+O(K^{1/2}(\log K)^{2})
, as claimed.

An application of the Chebyshev inequality yields

\Pr\left(\frac{|Z_{N_{x}}|}{\sigma(\alpha)\sqrt{\log q_{K}}}\geq\frac{(\log K)^{4/3}}{K^{1/3}}\right)\ll\frac{(\log K)^{4/3}}{K^{1/3}}.

Formulas (
26
) and (
28
) together with
\Pr(U_{1}(x)\neq U_{2}(x))\ll K^{-10}
show that for any of the random variables
Y\in\{T_{N},T_{N_{x}},U_{1}(x),U_{2}(x),U(x)\}
,

\sup_{C\subseteq\mathbb{C}}\left|\Pr\left(\frac{Y}{\sigma(\alpha)\sqrt{\log q_{K}}}\in C\right)-\Phi(C)\right|

is the same up to an error
O(K^{-1/3}(\log K)^{4/3})
. Note that we tacitly used the fact that with the notation
C^{\varepsilon}:=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,:\,\mathrm{dist}(y,\partial C)\leq\varepsilon\}
, we have
\Phi(C^{\varepsilon})\ll\varepsilon
uniformly in convex sets
C\subseteq\mathbb{C}

[
13
, p. 24]
. This finishes the proof of the second claim of the lemma.
∎

From now on we can thus work with the random variable
U(x)
, which is expressed in terms of the Markov chain
c_{K-1},c_{K-2},\ldots
, discussed in Section
3.3.1
. By classical results on finite state space irreducible, aperiodic Markov chains,
c_{K-j}
converges to the stationary distribution exponentially fast as
j\to\infty
. It is also not difficult to see that the sequence
c_{K-1},c_{K-2},\ldots
is
\psi
-mixing with exponential rate. That is, for any integers
j,\ell\geq 1
, letting
\mathcal{B}_{j}
denote the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
c_{K-1},c_{K-2},\ldots,c_{K-j}
and
\mathcal{F}_{j+\ell}
the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
c_{K-j-\ell},c_{K-j-\ell-1},\ldots
, the
\psi
-mixing coefficients satisfy

\psi(\ell):=\sup_{j\geq 1}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in\mathcal{B}_{j},\,\,\,\Pr(B)>0\\
F\in\mathcal{F}_{j+\ell},\,\,\,\Pr(F)>0\end{subarray}}\bigg|\frac{\Pr(B\cap F)}{\Pr(B)\Pr(F)}-1\bigg|\ll e^{-\tau\ell}

(29)

with some constant
\tau>0
. In particular (cf.
\rho
-mixing), if
f(x)
is a
\mathcal{B}_{j}
-measurable and
g(x)
is an
\mathcal{F}_{j+\ell}
-measurable complex-valued function, then

\mathbb{E}(f(x)g(x))=\mathbb{E}f(x)\mathbb{E}g(x)+O\left(\left(\mathbb{E}|f(x)|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\mathbb{E}|g(x)|^{2}\right)^{1/2}e^{-\tau\ell}\right).

(30)

We refer to
[
15
]
for a survey on mixing properties of Markov chains.

Since
\psi
-mixing is usually stated only for stationary Markov chains, but our chain
c_{K-1},c_{K-2},\ldots
is not started from the stationary distribution, we include a short proof of (
29
). Fix
j,\ell\geq 1
, and assume first that
B\in\mathcal{B}_{j}
and
F\in\mathcal{F}_{j+\ell}
are the events

\begin{split}B&=\{c_{K-1}=x_{K-1},\,\,c_{K-2}=x_{K-2},\ldots,c_{K-j}=x_{K-j}\},\\
F&=\{c_{K-j-\ell}=x_{K-j-\ell},\,\,c_{K-j-\ell-1}=x_{K-j-\ell-1},\ldots\}\end{split}

with some
x_{K-1},x_{K-2},\ldots\in\{0,1,\ldots,a\}
. Using the Markov property,

\begin{split}\Pr(B\cap F)&=\Pr(F\mid c_{K-j}=x_{K-j})\Pr(B)\\
&=\Pr(F\mid c_{K-j-\ell}=x_{K-j-\ell})\Pr(c_{K-j-\ell}=x_{K-j-\ell}\mid c_{K-j}=x_{K-j})\Pr(B)\\
&=\frac{\Pr(F)}{\Pr(c_{K-j-\ell}=x_{K-j-\ell})}\Pr(c_{K-j-\ell}=x_{K-j-\ell}\mid c_{K-j}=x_{K-j})\Pr(B).\end{split}

Hence

\left|\frac{\Pr(B\cap F)}{\Pr(B)\Pr(F)}-1\right|=\left|\frac{\Pr(c_{K-j-\ell}=x_{K-j-\ell}\mid c_{K-j}=x_{K-j})}{\Pr(c_{K-j-\ell}=x_{K-j-\ell})}-1\right|.

Here
\Pr(c_{K-j-\ell}=x_{K-j-\ell}\mid c_{K-j}=x_{K-j})
and
\Pr(c_{K-j-\ell}=x_{K-j-\ell})
are both exponentially close in
\ell
to the stationary distribution at
x_{K-j-\ell}
, thus

|\Pr(B\cap F)-\Pr(B)\Pr(F)|\ll e^{-\tau\ell}\Pr(B)\Pr(F)

uniformly in
j\geq 1
, with some constant
\tau>0
. The same holds for any
B\in\mathcal{B}_{j}
and
F\in\mathcal{F}_{j+\ell}
by
\sigma
-additivity. This finishes the proof of (
29
).

3.3.5
Approximation by independent variables

In this section we approximate
U(x)
by a sum of independent random variables, and thus establish (
25
).

Let
1\leq R\leq K^{0.99}
be an integer, to be chosen, and let us divide
[0,K]
into
R
subintervals
I_{j}=[(j-1)K/R,jK/R]
,
1\leq j\leq R
of equal length
K/R\geq K^{1/100}
. Set also
I_{0}=(-\infty,0]
and
I_{R+1}=[K,\infty)
. Let
b>1
be a large constant, to be chosen, and for any
1\leq j\leq R+1
, define

\begin{split}S_{j}&=\left\{m\in S\,:\,h(m)-bk_{0}\in I_{j},\,\,\,H(m)+bk_{0}\in I_{j}\right\},\\
S_{j}^{\prime}&=\left\{m\in S\,:\,h(m)-bk_{0}\in I_{j-1},\,\,\,H(m)+bk_{0}\in I_{j}\right\}.\end{split}

Note that the length of the interval
[h(m)-bk_{0},H(m)+bk_{0}]
is negligible compared to
K/R
, therefore the sets
S_{j},S_{j}^{\prime}
partition
S
. Since
h(m)\leq K+O(\log K)
for all
1\leq m\leq Q_{K}\log Q_{K}
, we actually have
S_{R+1}=\emptyset
provided that
b>1
is chosen large enough. Consequently, we can express
U(x)
as
U(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{R}X_{j}(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{R+1}Y_{j}(x)
, and in particular,

U(x)-\mathbb{E}U(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{R}(X_{j}(x)-\mathbb{E}X_{j}(x))+\sum_{j=1}^{R+1}(Y_{j}(x)-\mathbb{E}Y_{j}(x)),

with the random variables

X_{j}(x):=\sum_{m\in S_{j}}\frac{e^{2\pi i(G(x,m)+\xi m\alpha)}}{2m\sin(\pi m\alpha)}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad Y_{j}(x):=\sum_{m\in S_{j}^{\prime}}\frac{e^{2\pi i(G(x,m)+\xi m\alpha)}}{2m\sin(\pi m\alpha)}.

We first show that for all
j
,

|X_{j}(x)|\ll\frac{K\log K}{R}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad|Y_{j}(x)|\ll(\log K)^{2}.

(31)

Observe that for any given
0\leq h\leq H
,

\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in S\\
h(m)=h,\,\,\,H(m)=H\end{subarray}}\frac{e^{2\pi i(G(x,m)+\xi m\alpha)}}{2m\sin(\pi m\alpha)}\bigg|\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in S\\
h(m)=h,\,\,\,H(m)=H\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{m\|m\alpha\|}\ll 1.

Indeed, by Lemma
18
here each term is
\ll\alpha^{H-h}
, and the number of terms is equal to the number of legitimate Ostrowski sequences
(d_{h},d_{h+1},\ldots,d_{H})
, which is
\ll q_{H-h}\ll\alpha^{h-H}
. Summing over
h,H
in the appropriate range leads to (
31
).

The main observation is that
X_{j}(x)
depends only on
c_{k}
,
\min I_{j}+(b-1)k_{0}\leq k\leq\max I_{j}-(b-1)k_{0}
. Similarly,
Y_{j}(x)
depends only on
c_{k}
,
\min I_{j}-(b+2)k_{0}\leq k\leq\min I_{j}+(b+2)k_{0}
.

For any
1\leq j<j^{\prime}\leq R+1
, the functions
Y_{j}(x)
resp.
Y_{j^{\prime}}(x)
depend on sets of variables
c_{k}
with the indices
k
separated by at least
K/R-2(b+2)k_{0}\geq K^{1/100}/2
. By (
30
) and (
31
), we have

\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{j}(x)-\mathbb{E}Y_{j}(x)\right)\left(\overline{Y_{j^{\prime}}(x)}-\mathbb{E}\overline{Y_{j^{\prime}}(x)}\right)\ll(\log K)^{4}e^{-\tau K^{1/100}/2}\ll K^{-2}.

Hence

\mathbb{E}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{R+1}(Y_{j}(x)-\mathbb{E}Y_{j}(x))\right|^{2}\ll\sum_{j=1}^{R+1}\mathbb{E}|Y_{j}(x)-\mathbb{E}Y_{j}(x)|^{2}+1\ll R(\log K)^{4},

(32)

and by Lemma
21
,

\begin{split}\mathrm{Cov}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{R}(X_{j}(x)-\mathbb{E}X_{j}(x))\right)&=\mathrm{Cov}(U(x))+O\left(K^{1/2}R^{1/2}(\log K)^{2}\right)\\
&=\Sigma_{K}+O\left(K^{1/2}R^{1/2}(\log K)^{2}\right).\end{split}

(33)

The main contribution to
U(x)
is the sum of
X_{j}(x)
. For any
2\leq j\leq R
, the vector
(X_{1}(x),X_{2}(x),\ldots,X_{j-1}(x))
resp. the variable
X_{j}(x)
depend on sets of variables
c_{k}
with the indices
k
separated by at least
(2b-2)k_{0}
. By an approximation theorem of Berkes and Philipp
[
11
, Theorem 2]
, on a suitable probability space there exist complex-valued random variables
X_{j}^{*}
and
\zeta_{j}
,
1\leq j\leq R
, with
\zeta_{j}
,
1\leq j\leq R
independent, such that the vectors
(X_{1}(x),X_{2}(x),\ldots,X_{R}(x))
and
(X_{1}^{*},X_{2}^{*},\ldots,X_{R}^{*})
are identically distributed,
X_{j}^{*}
and
\zeta_{j}
are also identically distributed for any given
1\leq j\leq R
, and
\Pr(|X_{j}^{*}-\zeta_{j}|\geq 6\psi((2b-2)k_{0}))\leq 6\psi((2b-2)k_{0})
, where
\psi
denotes the
\psi
-mixing coefficients from (
29
). Here
\psi((2b-2)k_{0})\ll K^{-100}
provided that
b>1
is chosen large enough, thus

\Pr\left(\sum_{j=1}^{R}|X_{j}^{*}-\zeta_{j}|\geq K^{-99}\right)\ll K^{-99}.

By (
31
), we have
|X_{j}^{*}|,|\zeta_{j}|\ll(K\log K)/R
, and the previous formula yields in particular that
\mathbb{E}|\sum_{j=1}^{R}(X_{j}^{*}-\zeta_{j})|^{2}\ll K^{-96}
. Formula (
33
) thus leads to

\mathrm{Cov}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{R}(\zeta_{j}-\mathbb{E}\zeta_{j})\right)=\Sigma_{K}+O(K^{1/2}R^{1/2}(\log K)^{2}).

The random variables
(\zeta_{j}-\mathbb{E}\zeta_{j})/(\sigma(\alpha)\sqrt{\log q_{K}})
,
1\leq j\leq R
are thus independent, mean zero, and

\Sigma_{*}:=\mathrm{Cov}\left(\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{R}(\zeta_{j}-\mathbb{E}\zeta_{j})}{\sigma(\alpha)\sqrt{\log q_{K}}}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right)+O\left(K^{-1/2}R^{1/2}(\log K)^{2}\right).

Further,

\sum_{j=1}^{R}\mathbb{E}\left|\frac{\zeta_{j}-\mathbb{E}\zeta_{j}}{\sigma(\alpha)\sqrt{\log q_{K}}}\right|^{3}\ll\frac{K^{1/2}\log K}{R}\sum_{j=1}^{R}\mathbb{E}\left|\frac{\zeta_{j}-\mathbb{E}\zeta_{j}}{\sigma(\alpha)\sqrt{\log q_{K}}}\right|^{2}\ll\frac{K^{1/2}\log K}{R}.

The multidimensional central limit theorem with explicit rate
[
13
, p. 166]
thus gives

\sup_{C\subseteq\mathbb{C}}\left|\Pr\left(\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{R}(\zeta_{j}-\mathbb{E}\zeta_{j})}{\sigma(\alpha)\sqrt{\log q_{K}}}\in C\right)-\Phi_{\Sigma_{*}}(C)\right|\ll\sum_{j=1}^{R}\mathbb{E}\left|\frac{\zeta_{j}-\mathbb{E}\zeta_{j}}{\sigma(\alpha)\sqrt{\log q_{K}}}\right|^{3}\ll\frac{K^{1/2}\log K}{R},

where
\Phi_{\Sigma_{*}}(C)=\int_{C}e^{-\langle y,\Sigma_{*}^{-1}y\rangle/2}/(2\pi\det(\Sigma_{*})^{1/2})\,\mathrm{d}y
is the mean zero Gaussian with covariance matrix
\Sigma_{*}
. It is easy to see that
\Phi_{\Sigma_{*}}(C)=\Phi(C)+O(K^{-1/2}R^{1/2}(\log K)^{2})
uniformly in convex sets
C\subseteq\mathbb{C}
, hence

\sup_{C\subseteq\mathbb{C}}\left|\Pr\left(\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{R}(X_{j}(x)-\mathbb{E}X_{j}(x))}{\sigma(\alpha)\sqrt{\log q_{K}}}\in C\right)-\Phi(C)\right|\ll\frac{K^{1/2}\log K}{R}+\frac{R^{1/2}(\log K)^{2}}{K^{1/2}}.

Similarly,
Y_{j}(x)
,
1\leq j\leq R+1
can be extremely well approximated by independent random variables
\zeta_{j}^{\prime}
,
1\leq j\leq R+1
which, by (
31
), satisfy
|\zeta_{j}^{\prime}|\ll(\log K)^{2}
. Applying an exponential bound (e.g. Hoeffding’s inequality) to
\zeta_{j}^{\prime}
with a large enough constant
\rho>1
leads to

\Pr\bigg(\bigg|\sum_{j=1}^{R+1}(Y_{j}(x)-\mathbb{E}Y_{j}(x))\bigg|\geq\rho R^{1/2}(\log K)^{5/2}\bigg)\\
\ll\Pr\left(\left|\sum_{j=1}^{R+1}(\zeta_{j}^{\prime}-\mathbb{E}\zeta_{j}^{\prime})\right|\geq\frac{\rho}{2}R^{1/2}(\log K)^{5/2}\right)+K^{-100}\ll K^{-100}.

The previous two formulas, together with the fact that
\mathbb{E}U(x)=O((\log K)^{2})
is negligible, give

\sup_{C\subseteq\mathbb{C}}\left|\Pr\left(\frac{U(x)}{\sigma(\alpha)\sqrt{\log q_{K}}}\in C\right)-\Phi(C)\right|\ll\frac{K^{1/2}\log K}{R}+\frac{R^{1/2}(\log K)^{5/2}}{K^{1/2}}.

The optimal choice is
R=\lfloor K^{2/3}/\log K\rfloor
, in which case the error term is
K^{-1/6}(\log K)^{2}
. This finishes the proof of (
25
).

3.3.6
Completing the proof

Proof of Theorem
4
.

Let
M\geq 3
be an integer with Ostrowski expansion
M=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}b_{k}q_{k}
,
b_{K-1}\neq 0
, and let
M^{*}=\sum_{k=K-k_{0}}^{K-1}b_{k}q_{k}
, where
k_{0}=\lfloor\frac{10\log K}{\log(1/\alpha)}\rfloor
, as before. Consider a partition
[0,M^{*})=\bigcup_{\ell=K-k_{0}}^{K-1}\bigcup_{b=0}^{b_{\ell}-1}[M_{\ell,b},M_{\ell,b}+q_{\ell})
with integers
0\leq M_{\ell,b}\ll q_{\ell}
. The central limit theorem (
25
) applied to
T_{N}
,
0\leq N<q_{\ell}
defined with
\xi=M_{\ell,b}+1/2
and
Q_{\ell}=M_{\ell,b}+q_{\ell}
gives that for any convex set
C\subseteq\mathbb{C}
,

\frac{1}{q_{\ell}}\left|\left\{0\leq N<q_{\ell}\,:\,\frac{T_{N}}{\sigma(\alpha)\sqrt{\log q_{\ell}}}\in C\right\}\right|=\Phi(C)+O\left(\frac{(\log K)^{2}}{K^{1/6}}\right).

Setting
\Gamma_{N}=(\log P_{N}(\alpha),\pi S_{N}(\alpha))
and
E_{N}=(\frac{1}{2}\log N,\pi E(\alpha)\log N)
, formula (
24
) states that
\Gamma_{M_{\ell,b}+N}-E_{M_{\ell,b}+q_{\ell}}
is identified with
iT_{N}
,
0\leq N<q_{\ell}
up to a negligible error
O(\log K)
. Note that the standard Gaussian is invariant under the rotation corresponding to multiplication by
i
. Hence for any convex set
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

\left|\left\{0\leq N<q_{\ell}\,:\,\frac{\Gamma_{M_{\ell,b}+N}-E_{M_{\ell,b}+q_{\ell}}}{\sigma(\alpha)\sqrt{\log q_{\ell}}}\in C\right\}\right|=\Phi(C)q_{\ell}+O\left(q_{\ell}\frac{(\log K)^{2}}{K^{1/6}}\right).

Here
E_{M_{\ell,b}+q_{\ell}}
resp.
\sqrt{\log q_{\ell}}
can be replaced by
E_{M_{\ell,b}+N}
resp.
\sqrt{\log(M_{\ell,b}+N)}
up to a negligible error
O(q_{\ell}(\log K)/K)
. We tacitly used the fact that
\Phi((1+\varepsilon)C)=\Phi(C)+O(|\varepsilon|)
uniformly in convex sets
C
. Hence summing over
\ell,b
leads to

\left|\left\{0\leq N<M^{*}\,:\,\frac{\Gamma_{N}-E_{N}}{\sigma(\alpha)\sqrt{\log N}}\in C\right\}\right|=\Phi(C)M^{*}+O\left(M^{*}\frac{(\log K)^{2}}{K^{1/6}}\right).

Since
M-M^{*}\ll q_{K-k_{0}}\ll M/K^{10}
, we finally deduce

\frac{1}{M}\left|\left\{0\leq N<M\,:\,\frac{\Gamma_{N}-E_{N}}{\sigma(\alpha)\sqrt{\log N}}\in C\right\}\right|=\Phi(C)+O\left(\frac{(\log\log M)^{2}}{(\log M)^{1/6}}\right).

∎

3.4
Non-badly approximable irrationals

Fix an arbitrary irrational
\alpha=[a_{0};a_{1},a_{2},\dots]
with convergents
p_{k}/q_{k}=[a_{0};a_{1},\dots,a_{k}]
. Using a reciprocity formula of Bettin and Drappeau
[
12
]
, in our recent paper
[
1
, Equation (14) and Proposition 3]
we observed that

\max_{0\leq N<q_{k}}\log P_{N}(\alpha)=V(a_{1}+\cdots+a_{k})+O(A_{k}+(\log A_{k})k)

(34)

with
V
as in (
10
),
A_{k}=\max_{1\leq\ell\leq k}a_{\ell}
and a universal implied constant. We also record a variant for future reference, see
[
1
, Equation (5) and Proposition 3]
:

\max_{0\leq N<q_{k}}\log P_{N}(\alpha)=(V+o(1))(a_{1}+\cdots+a_{k})+O\left(\frac{\log A_{k}}{a_{k+1}}\right)\quad\textrm{as }k\to\infty,

(35)

provided that
(a_{1}+\cdots+a_{k})/k\to\infty
. We also proved
[
1
, Proposition 2]
that for any
0\leq N<q_{k}
,

\log P_{N}(\alpha)+\log P_{q_{k}-N-1}(\alpha)=\log q_{k}+O\left(\frac{\log A_{k}}{a_{k+1}}\right)

(36)

with a universal implied constant. In particular, the distribution of
\log P_{N}(\alpha)
when
N
is chosen randomly from
[0,q_{k})
is approximately symmetric about the center
\frac{1}{2}\log q_{k}
. Concerning the variance, the Diophantine sum in (
17
) has the evaluation
[
14
]

\sqrt{\sum_{1\leq m<q_{k}}\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}m^{2}\|m\alpha\|^{2}}}=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{720}}\sqrt{a_{1}^{2}+\cdots+a_{k}^{2}}+O(\sqrt{k})

(37)

with a universal implied constant. Although formulas (
34
)–(
37
) hold for arbitrary irrationals, all but (
36
) are useless for badly approximable ones; indeed, in that case the main terms and the error terms are of the same order of magnitude. Note also that (
37
) gives a wrong value of
\sigma(\alpha)
for quadratic irrationals. They give precise results, however, for certain non-badly approximable irrationals.

Consider first Euler’s number
e
. Then (
34
) and (
37
) read

\begin{split}\max_{0\leq N<q_{k}}\log P_{N}(e)&=\frac{V}{9}k^{2}+O(k\log k),\\
\sum_{1\leq m<q_{k}}\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}m^{2}\|me\|^{2}}&=\frac{\pi^{2}}{14580}k^{3}+O(k^{2}).\end{split}

Since
q_{k}
increases at the rate
\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}a_{\ell}\leq q_{k}\leq\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}(a_{\ell}+1)
, we have

3.5
Asymptotics almost everywhere

Let us recall the precise statistics of the partial quotients of almost every irrational. Fix a nondecreasing positive real-valued function
\varphi(x)
on
(0,\infty)
. The two most fundamental properties are that for a.e.
\alpha

(i)

the inequality

has finitely many solutions

if and only if
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}1/\varphi(k)<\infty
;

(ii)

\log q_{k}\sim\frac{\pi^{2}}{12\log 2}k
as

.

As for the sum of the partial quotients, a classical result of Khinchin
[

\frac{a_{1}+\cdots+a_{k}}{k\log k}\to\frac{1}{\log 2}\qquad\textrm{in measure.}

The same asymptotics only holds a.e. if the sum is “trimmed”, i.e. the largest term is removed. More precisely, Diamond and Vaaler
[

proved that for a.e.
\alpha

\frac{a_{1}+\cdots+a_{k}-\vartheta(\alpha,k)A_{k}}{k\log k}\to\frac{1}{\log 2}\quad\textrm{as }k\to\infty

with some
2
2

2

Their proof actually gives that the same holds with
\vartheta(\alpha,k)=1

0\leq\vartheta(\alpha,k)\leq 1

as before. In particular, the arithmetic mean of the partial quotients diverges, and from (

\alpha

Proof of Theorem

.

We shall need a further result of Diamond and Vaaler

3.6
Asymptotics in measure

Recall that the Gauss measure
\nu(A):=\frac{1}{\log 2}\int_{A}\frac{1}{1+x}\,\mathrm{d}x
(

Borel) is an invariant measure under the shift

. The partial quotients are thus identically distributed:

\nu\left(\left\{\alpha\in[0,1]\,:\,a_{k}=j\right\}\right)=\frac{1}{\log 2}\log\left(1+\frac{1}{j(j+2)}\right),\quad k,j\geq 1.

The terms “a.e.” and “convergence in measure” are of course equivalent for the Gauss and the Lebesgue measures, and we will use them in this sense.

Proof of Theorem

The proof of Theorem

is based on results of Samur
[
36
]
on the asymptotics of sums of the form
\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}f(a_{\ell})
. Let
\mu
be a probability measure on

that is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. A special case of
[

states that for any

\mu\left(\left\{\alpha\in[0,1]\,:\,\frac{a_{1}^{2}+\cdots+a_{k}^{2}}{k^{2}}\leq t\right\}\right)\to F(t)\qquad\textrm{as }k\to\infty,

where Samur defines the limit distribution via the Lévy–Khinchin representation of its characteristic function as

\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\,\mathrm{d}F(t)=\exp\left(i\frac{x}{\log 2}+\frac{1}{2\log 2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(e^{ixy}-1-ixyI_{(0,1]}(y)\right)y^{-3/2}\,\mathrm{d}y\right).

Elementary calculations and the values of the Fresnel-type integrals

. Using the appropriate normalization, in the special case of sums of squares of the partial quotients the theorem of Samur thus states that for any

\mu\left(\left\{\alpha\in[0,1]\,:\,\frac{2(\log 2)^{2}}{\pi}\cdot\frac{a_{1}^{2}+\cdots+a_{k}^{2}}{k^{2}}\leq t\right\}\right)\to\int_{0}^{t}\frac{e^{-1/(2x)}}{\sqrt{2\pi}x^{3/2}}\,\mathrm{d}x\qquad\textrm{as }k\to\infty.

(42)

We shall also need a more sophisticated form of

[

\mu\left(\left\{\alpha\in[0,1]\,:\,\frac{\log q_{k}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{12\log 2}k}{\tau\sqrt{k}}\leq t\right\}\right)\to\int_{-\infty}^{t}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^{2}/2}\,\mathrm{d}x\quad\textrm{as }k\to\infty,

. For general
f
of bounded variation, we use an approximation formula for sums of the form
\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(x_{n})
instead: if the points
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{N}\in\mathbb{R}
satisfy
\|x_{n}-x_{\ell}\|\geq r>0
for all
n\neq\ell
with some
r>0
, then for any integer
H>1
,

\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(x_{n})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathbb{Z}\\
0<|m|<H\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{|m|}{H}\right)\widehat{f}(m)\sum_{n=1}^{N}e^{2\pi imx_{n}}+O\left(V(f)\left(\frac{\log H}{rH}+1\right)\right)

(46)

with a universal implied constant. The proof is based on smoothing with the Fejér kernel of order
H
and Koksma’s inequality, see
[
10
]
.

Proof of Proposition
22
.

We may assume that
V(f)=1
. Fix
1\leq N\leq M
, and note that the points
x_{n}=n\alpha
,
1\leq n\leq N
satisfy
\|n\alpha-\ell\alpha\|\geq\|q_{k}\alpha\|\geq 1/(2q_{k+1})\gg 1/(M(\log M)^{d})
for all
n\neq\ell
by the assumption
a_{k}\leq ck^{d}
. Applying (
46
) with a suitable
r\gg 1/(M(\log M)^{d})
and
H=\lfloor M(\log M)^{2d+1}\rfloor
, we thus get

S_{N}(\alpha,f)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathbb{Z}\\
0<|m|<H\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{|m|}{H}\right)\widehat{f}(m)\frac{e^{2\pi im\alpha}-e^{2\pi iNm\alpha}}{1-e^{2\pi im\alpha}}+O(1).

Taking the average over
N\in[1,M]
and introducing

E_{M}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathbb{Z}\\
0<|m|<H\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{|m|}{H}\right)\widehat{f}(m)\frac{e^{2\pi im\alpha}}{1-e^{2\pi im\alpha}},

we obtain

\begin{split}A_{M}&=E_{M}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathbb{Z}\\
0<|m|<H\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{|m|}{H}\right)\widehat{f}(m)\frac{e^{2\pi im\alpha}-e^{2\pi im(M+1)\alpha}}{M(1-e^{2\pi im\alpha})^{2}}+O(1)\\
&=E_{M}+O\left(1+\sum_{m=1}^{H-1}\frac{1}{m}\min\left\{\frac{1}{M\|m\alpha\|^{2}},\frac{1}{\|m\alpha\|}\right\}\right)\\
&=E_{M}+O\left(\max_{|\ell-k|\ll\log k}a_{\ell}\cdot(\log\log M)^{2}\right),\end{split}

where the last step follows from an obvious modification of the second claim in Lemma
14
. This proves (
44
).

Next, we prove (
45
). By the previous three formulas,

S_{N}(\alpha,f)-E_{M}=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathbb{Z}\\
0<|m|<H\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{|m|}{H}\right)\widehat{f}(m)\frac{e^{2\pi iNm\alpha}}{1-e^{2\pi im\alpha}}+O(1).

Squaring both sides, and then taking the average over
N\in[1,M]
yields

\frac{1}{M}\sum_{N=1}^{M}\left(S_{N}(\alpha,f)-E_{M}\right)^{2}=V_{M}+O\left(\sqrt{V_{M}}+1\right),

where

V_{M}:=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{N=1}^{M}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathbb{Z}\\
0<|m|<H\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{|m|}{H}\right)\widehat{f}(m)\frac{e^{2\pi iNm\alpha}}{1-e^{2\pi im\alpha}}\right|^{2}.

Following the steps in the proof of Proposition
13
shows that the contribution of the off-diagonal terms is negligible, hence

V_{M}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathbb{Z}\\
0<|m|<H\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{|m|}{H}\right)^{2}\frac{|\widehat{f}(m)|^{2}}{|1-e^{2\pi im\alpha}|^{2}}+O\left(\max_{|\ell-k|\ll\log k}a_{\ell}^{2}\cdot(\log\log M)^{4}\right).

We can clean up the main term by noting that the contribution of the terms
M<|m|<H
is negligible, and that for all
0<|m|\leq M
the error of replacing
(1-|m|/H)^{2}
by
1
is also negligible:

\begin{split}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathbb{Z}\\
M<|m|<H\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{m^{2}\|m\alpha\|^{2}}&\ll\max_{|\ell-k|\ll\log k}a_{\ell}^{2}\cdot\log\log M,\\
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathbb{Z}\\
0<|m|\leq M\end{subarray}}\frac{|m|}{H}\cdot\frac{1}{m^{2}\|m\alpha\|^{2}}&\ll\frac{M}{H}(\log M)^{2d+1}\ll 1,\end{split}

see the fourth claim in Lemma
14
. Therefore

V_{M}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{|\widehat{f}(m)|^{2}}{2\sin^{2}(\pi m\alpha)}+O\left(\max_{|\ell-k|\ll\log k}a_{\ell}^{2}\cdot(\log\log M)^{4}\right),

and using
1/\sin^{2}(\pi m\alpha)=1/(\pi^{2}\|m\alpha\|^{2})+O(1)
we deduce

\frac{1}{M}\sum_{N=1}^{M}\left(S_{N}(\alpha,f)-E_{M}\right)^{2}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{|\widehat{f}(m)|^{2}}{2\pi^{2}\|m\alpha\|^{2}}+O\left(\sqrt{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{|\widehat{f}(m)|^{2}}{\|m\alpha\|^{2}}}+\max_{|\ell-k|\ll\log k}a_{\ell}^{2}\cdot(\log\log M)^{4}\right).

Finally, the first claim (
44
) shows that the error of replacing
E_{M}
by
A_{M}
on the left-hand side is negligible, and (
45
) follows.
∎

4.2
Indicators of intervals

In the special case
f(x)=I_{[a,b]}(\{x\})-(b-a)
with some
[a,b]\subset[0,1]
and a badly approximable
\alpha
, Proposition
22
gives

B_{M}^{2}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{\sin^{2}(\pi m(b-a))}{2\pi^{4}m^{2}\|m\alpha\|^{2}}+O\left(\sqrt{\log M}\right)

with an implied constant depending only on
\alpha
. The order of magnitude of
B_{M}^{2}
thus depends on the Diophantine approximation properties of the vector
(\alpha,b-a)
. As we observed in the previous section,
B_{M}^{2}\ll\log M
holds for any
[a,b]\subset[0,1]
. To establish a lower bound, we need to find positive integers
m
for which
\|m\alpha\|
is small, but
\|m(b-a)\|
is bounded away from zero. In other words, we want the sequence
(m\alpha,m(b-a))\pmod{1}
to visit a narrow but long rectangle in
\mathbb{R}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}^{2}
many times. Our proof of Theorem
5
is based on a quantitative form of Kronecker’s theorem due to Cassels
[
17
, p. 97–99]
.

Lemma 23

(Cassels)
.

Let
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n},\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
, and let
C_{1},\dots,C_{n},Q>0
be reals. Assume that for any integral vector
(m_{1},\dots,m_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
,

\|m_{1}\beta_{1}+\cdots+m_{n}\beta_{n}\|\leq\frac{2^{n}}{((n+1)!)^{2}}\max\left\{C_{1}|m_{1}|,\dots,C_{n}|m_{n}|,Q\|m_{1}\alpha_{1}+\cdots+m_{n}\alpha_{n}\|\right\}.

Then the system of
n+1
inequalities

\begin{split}\|q\alpha_{k}-\beta_{k}\|&\leq C_{k}\quad(1\leq k\leq n),\\
|q|&\leq Q\end{split}

has an integral solution
q\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof of Theorem
5
.

We give separate proofs for rational and irrational values of
b-a
. First, assume that
b-a
is irrational. Fix a constant
K>0
such that

\|q\alpha\|\geq\frac{K}{|q|},\qquad\|k(b-a)\|\geq K,\qquad\|q\alpha-k(b-a)\|\geq K/|q|

for all integers
q\neq 0
and all
k\in\{1,3,5,7,9\}
. Let us apply Lemma
23
with
n=2
and

\alpha_{1}=\alpha,\qquad\alpha_{2}=b-a,\qquad\beta_{1}=0,\qquad\beta_{2}=\frac{1}{2},\qquad C_{1}=\frac{100}{KQ},\qquad C_{2}=0.49,

and a large real number
Q>5/K
. We need to check that for all
(m_{1},m_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
,

\|m_{2}/2\|\leq\frac{1}{9}\max\left\{\frac{100|m_{1}|}{KQ},0.49|m_{2}|,Q\|m_{1}\alpha+m_{2}(b-a)\|\right\}.

(47)

This trivially holds if
m_{2}
is even, and also if
|m_{2}|\geq 11
. We may thus assume that
|m_{2}|\in\{1,3,5,7,9\}
, in which case the left hand side is
1/2
. If
m_{1}=0
, then the last term in the maximum satisfies
Q\|m_{2}(b-a)\|\geq QK>5
, and (
47
) follows. If
m_{1}\neq 0
, then

\max\left\{\frac{100|m_{1}|}{KQ},Q\|m_{1}\alpha+m_{2}(b-a)\|\right\}\geq\max\left\{\frac{100|m_{1}|}{KQ},\frac{KQ}{|m_{1}|}\right\}\geq 10,

and (
47
) follows once again.

Hence for any
Q>5/K
there exists an integer
|q|\leq Q
with
\|q\alpha\|\leq 100/(KQ)
and
\|q(b-a)-1/2\|\leq 0.49
; the latter inequality is equivalent to
\|q(b-a)\|\geq 1/100
. Since
\|q\alpha\|\geq K/|q|
, the integer solution
q
also satisfies
|q|\geq(K^{2}/100)Q
. Note that
-q
is a solution if and only if
q
is. In particular, there exist
\gg\log M
integers
m\in[1,M]
such that
\|m\alpha\|\ll 1/m
and
\|m(b-a)\|\geq 1/100
. Therefore

\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{\sin^{2}(\pi m(b-a))}{2\pi^{4}m^{2}\|m\alpha\|^{2}}\gg\log M,

and the claim follows.

Next, assume that
0<b-a<1
is rational, say
b-a=s/r
with relatively prime positive integers
s,r
. Then
\sin^{2}(\pi m(b-a))\gg 1
for any integer
m
not divisible by
r
. Applying Lemma
23
with
n=1
;
\alpha_{1}=r\alpha
;
\beta_{1}=-\alpha
and a large real
Q>0
, we similarly deduce that there exists an integer
Q\ll|q|\leq Q
such that
\|(qr+1)\alpha\|\ll 1/Q
. In particular, there exist
\gg\log M
integers
m\in[1,M]
such that
\|m\alpha\|\ll 1/m
and
m\equiv\pm 1\pmod{r}
, and the claim follows.
∎

Finally, we give an alternative proof of Theorem
5
for rational
b-a=s/r
. As observed, it is enough to show that there exist
\gg\log M
integers
m\in[1,M]
such that
\|m\alpha\|\ll 1/m
, and
m
is not divisible by
r
. The convergents to
\alpha
satisfy the identity
q_{\ell}p_{\ell-1}-q_{\ell-1}p_{\ell}=(-1)^{\ell}
. Therefore among any two consecutive convergent denominators, at least one is not divisible by
r
. This yields
\gg\log M
integers
m
with the desired properties.

4.3
Central limit theorem for functions of bounded variation

Proof of Theorem
6
.

This is very similar to the proof of Theorem
4
, so we only indicate the necessary changes. Throughout, constants and implied constants depend only on
\alpha
.

We may assume that
V(f)=1
, and that
B_{M}\gg(\log M)^{1/3}(\log\log M)^{2}
. We saw in the proof of Proposition
22
(cf. (
24
)) that, for
1\leq N\leq M
,

S_{N}(\alpha,f)-A_{M}=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathbb{Z}\\
0<|m|<M\log M\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{|m|}{\lfloor M\log M\rfloor}\right)\widehat{f}(m)\frac{e^{2\pi iNm\alpha}}{1-e^{2\pi im\alpha}}+O((\log\log M)^{2}).

(48)

We will prove that given any
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
, with
M=q_{K}
a convergent denominator and any
q_{K}\ll Q_{K}\ll q_{K}
,

T_{N}(f):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathbb{Z}\\
0<|m|<Q_{K}\log Q_{K}\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{|m|}{\lfloor Q_{K}\log Q_{K}\rfloor}\right)\widehat{f}(m)\frac{e^{2\pi i(N+\xi)m\alpha}}{1-e^{2\pi im\alpha}},\quad 0\leq N<q_{K}

satisfies the central limit theorem: for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

\frac{1}{q_{K}}\left|\left\{0\leq N<q_{K}\,:\,\frac{T_{N}(f)}{B_{q_{K}}}\leq t\right\}\right|=\int_{-\infty}^{t}\frac{e^{-y^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\mathrm{d}y+O\left(\frac{K^{1/3}(\log K)^{2}}{B_{q_{K}}}\right).

(49)

The main difference compared to the proof of Theorem
4
is that
T_{N}(f)
is real-valued, as can be seen by combining the
m
and
-m
terms, thus the limit distribution is now a
1
-dimensional standard Gaussian.

Observe that the coefficients in
T_{N}(f)
satisfy the same decay as those in
T_{N}
:

\left|\left(1-\frac{|m|}{\lfloor Q_{K}\log Q_{K}\rfloor}\right)\widehat{f}(m)\frac{1}{1-e^{2\pi im\alpha}}\right|\ll\frac{1}{|m|\cdot\|m\alpha\|}.

Hence if
N
is chosen from
[0,q_{K})
uniformly at random, then
\mathbb{E}T_{N}(f)=O((\log K)^{2})
and
\mathbb{E}|T_{N}(f)|^{2}=B_{q_{K}}^{2}+O(B_{q_{K}}(\log K)^{2})
, see
V_{M}
in the proof of Proposition
22
; an analogue of Lemma
17
. Let

k_{0}=\left\lfloor\frac{10\log K}{\log(1/\alpha)}\right\rfloor\quad\textrm{and}\quad S=\left\{0<|m|<Q_{K}\log Q_{K}\,:\,H(|m|)-h(|m|)\leq k_{0}\right\}.

As an analogue of Lemma
20
, we deduce that

T_{N}(f)=\sum_{m\in S}\left(1-\frac{|m|}{\lfloor Q_{K}\log Q_{K}\rfloor}\right)\widehat{f}(m)\frac{e^{2\pi i(g(N)+\xi)m\alpha}}{1-e^{2\pi im\alpha}}+Z_{N}(f),\quad 0\leq N<q_{K}

with some
Z_{N}(f)
,
0\leq N<q_{K}
that satisfies
q_{K}^{-1}\sum_{N=0}^{q_{K}-1}|Z_{N}(f)|^{2}\ll(\log K)^{4}
.

Let us choose
x
from the interval
[0,\alpha^{-K})
uniformly at random, and define

U(x,f)=\sum_{m\in S}\left(1-\frac{|m|}{\lfloor Q_{K}\log Q_{K}\rfloor}\right)\widehat{f}(m)\frac{e^{2\pi i(G(x,m)+\xi m\alpha)}}{1-e^{2\pi im\alpha}},

where
G(x,m)
,
m>0
is as in Section
3.3.4
, extended via
G(x,-m):=-G(x,m)
for negative integers. Similarly to Lemma
21
, we reduce the central limit theorem for
T_{N}(f)
to that for
U(x,f)
: we have
\mathbb{E}U(x,f)=O((\log K)^{2})
and
\mathbb{E}|U(x,f)|^{2}=B_{q_{K}}^{2}+O(B_{q_{K}}(\log K)^{2})
. Further,

\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\Pr\left(\frac{T_{N}(f)}{B_{q_{K}}}\leq t\right)-\Phi(t)\right|=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\Pr\left(\frac{U(x,f)}{B_{q_{K}}}\leq t\right)-\Phi(t)\right|+O\left(\frac{(\log K)^{4/3}}{B_{q_{K}}^{2/3}}\right),

where
\Phi(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}e^{-y^{2}/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}\,\mathrm{d}y
.

Following the steps in Section
3.3.5
, we can approximate
U(x,f)
by a sum of independent random variables, leading to

\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\Pr\left(\frac{U(x,f)}{B_{q_{K}}}\leq t\right)-\Phi(t)\right|\ll\frac{K\log K}{RB_{q_{K}}}+\frac{R^{1/2}(\log K)^{5/2}}{B_{q_{K}}}.

The optimal choice is once again
R=\lfloor K^{2/3}/\log K\rfloor
, in which case the error term is
O(K^{1/3}(\log K)^{2}/B_{q_{K}})
. This establishes (
49
), and in particular Theorem
6
for
M=q_{K}
a convergent denominator.

Taking the average in (
48
) over
N\in[1,M^{\prime}]
and applying Lemma
16
, we deduce that for any
3\leq M^{\prime}\leq M
,

\begin{split}|A_{M}-A_{M^{\prime}}|&=\left|\frac{1}{M^{\prime}}\sum_{N=1}^{M^{\prime}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathbb{Z}\\
0<|m|<M\log M\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{|m|}{\lfloor M\log M\rfloor}\right)\widehat{f}(m)\frac{e^{2\pi iNm\alpha}}{1-e^{2\pi im\alpha}}\right|+O((\log\log M)^{2})\\
&\ll\sum_{1\leq m<M\log M}\frac{1}{m\|m\alpha\|}\min\left\{\frac{1}{M^{\prime}\|m\alpha\|},1\right\}+(\log\log M)^{2}\\
&\ll\left(1+\log\frac{M}{M^{\prime}}\right)^{2}+(\log\log M)^{2}.\end{split}

By (
45
) and the fourth claim in Lemma
14
, for any
3\leq M^{\prime}\leq M
,

|B_{M}^{2}-B_{M^{\prime}}^{2}|=\sum_{m=M^{\prime}+1}^{M}\frac{|\widehat{f}(m)|^{2}}{2\pi^{2}\|m\alpha\|^{2}}+O(B_{M})\ll\sum_{m=M^{\prime}+1}^{M}\frac{1}{m^{2}\|m\alpha\|^{2}}+B_{M}\ll\log\frac{M}{M^{\prime}}+B_{M}.

We follow the steps in Section
3.3.6
to extend the result from
M=q_{K}
(a convergent denominator) to general
M
. With the notation of that section, formula (
49
) gives

\frac{1}{q_{\ell}}\left|\left\{0\leq N<q_{\ell}\,:\,\frac{S_{M_{\ell,b}+N}(\alpha,f)-A_{M_{\ell,b}+q_{\ell}}}{B_{q_{\ell}}}\leq t\right\}\right|=\Phi(t)+O\left(\frac{(\log M)^{1/3}(\log\log M)^{2}}{B_{q_{\ell}}}\right).

Here

|A_{M_{\ell,b}+q_{\ell}}-A_{M}|\ll\left(1+\log\frac{q_{K}}{q_{K-k_{0}}}\right)^{2}+(\log\log M)^{2}\ll(\log\log M)^{2},

and

\frac{B_{q_{\ell}}}{B_{M}}=1+O\left(\frac{\log\frac{q_{K}}{q_{K-k_{0}}}}{B_{M}^{2}}+\frac{1}{B_{M}}\right)=1+O\left(\frac{1}{B_{M}}\right),

hence
A_{M_{\ell,b}+q_{\ell}}
resp.
B_{q_{\ell}}
can be replaced by
A_{M}
resp.
B_{M}
up to a negligible error
O((\log\log M)^{2}/B_{M})
. Summing over
\ell,b
leads to

\frac{1}{M^{*}}\left|\left\{0\leq N<M^{*}\,:\,\frac{S_{N}(\alpha,f)-A_{M}}{B_{M}}\leq t\right\}\right|=\Phi(t)+O\left(\frac{(\log M)^{1/3}(\log\log M)^{2}}{B_{M}}\right).

Here
M-M^{*}\ll M/(\log M)^{10}
, therefore we can replace
M^{*}
by
M
. This finishes the proof of Theorem
6
for general
M\geq 3
.
∎
