Abstract
. We first study Clarke’s tangent cones at infinity to unbounded subsets of ℝ n . \mathbb{R}^{n}. We prove that these cones are closed convex and show a characterization of their interiors. We then study subgradients at infinity for extended real value functions on ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} and derive necessary optimality conditions at infinity for optimization problems. We also give a number of rules for the computing of subgradients at infinity and provide some characterizations of the Lipschitz continuity at infinity for lower semi-continuous functions.

1.
Introduction

The theory of subgradients (or generalized gradients) is concerned with the differential properties of functions, which do not have derivatives in the usual sense. This theory, in the setting of finite dimensional spaces, associates with a function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}:=\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
and a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a (possibly empty) closed subset
\partial f(x)
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
whose elements are called
subgradients
of
f
at
x.
The theory plays an important role in many areas such as optimization, optimal control and differential equations; for more details, we refer the reader to
[

,

,

,

,

]
.

Rockafellar
[
17
]
defined
\partial f(x)
for convex functions,
showed how to characterize
\partial f(x)
in terms of one-sided directional derivatives, and proved that under certain assumptions, rules such as the necessary optimality condition
0\in\partial f(x)
are valid.

Clarke
[
1
]
showed how the definition of
\partial f(x)
could be extended to arbitrary lower semi-continuous functions
f
in such a way that
\partial f(x)
is the set of subgradients in the sense of convex analysis when
f
is convex, and
\partial f(x)
reduces to the gradient vector
\nabla f(x)
when
f
is smooth (continuously differentiable).
He expressed
\partial f(x)
by way of a generalized directional derivative when
f
is Lipschitz continuous in a neighborhood of
x.

He used it to derive necessary optimality conditions for non-smooth, non-convex problems in optimal control and mathematical programming. For locally Lipschitz functions, he proved that
\partial f(x)
is nonempty compact and provided a number of rules for subgradient calculation that generalize the ones previously known for convex functions.

Rockafellar
[
19
,
20
,
21
]
supplied an alternative development of Clarke’s ideas that includes a direct definition of
\partial f(x)
in terms of a still more general directional derivative function. He expressed
\partial f(x)
by way of the normal cone of the epigraph of
f
at
(x,f(x)).
He proved that a lower semi-continuous function
f
is Lipschitz in a neighborhood of a point
x
if and only if
\partial f(x)
is nonempty compact. He also extended Clarke’s results to functions that are not necessarily locally Lipschitz.

Following the ideas of Clarke and Rockafellar, we define and study
subgradients at infinity
of extended real value functions.

Motivations

Our first motivation comes from optimality conditions in mathematical programming. Namely, let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be bounded below, i.e.,

\displaystyle f_{*}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\ >\ -\infty.

If
f
has a global minumum at a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},
then certainly
0\in\partial f(x).
In general, the function
f
may not attain its infimum, and so there exists a sequence
x_{k}
tending to infinity such that
f(x_{k})
tends to
f_{*};
then it is natural to find necessary optimality conditions
at infinity
for such cases.

Our second motivation is to characterize the Lipschitzness at infinity for lower semi-continuous functions.

Contributions

With notation and definitions given in the next sections, our main contributions are as follows:

•

Given an unbounded set
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and an index set
I\subset\{1,\ldots,n\},
we define the Clarke tangent cone
T_{C}(\infty_{I})
and normal cone
N_{C}(\infty_{I})
to
C
at infinity. We show that
T_{C}(\infty_{I})
is a closed convex cone and give a characterization of its interior
(see Corollary
3.5
and Theorem
3.6
).

•

Given a function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}},
we define the set
\partial f(\infty)
of
subgradients of
f
at infinity
in terms of the normal cone to the epigraph of
f
at infinity. Then we present a number of rules for the computing of subgradients at infinity. In particular, we demonstrate in Theorem
4.14
that under mild assumptions, if the restriction of
f
on an unbounded closed set
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
does not attain its infimum, then the following
necessary optimality condition at infinity
holds true:
0\in\partial f(\infty)+N_{C}(\infty_{I}),
where
I:=\{1,\ldots,n\}.

•

We show in Theorem
5.8
that a lower semi-continuous function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is
Lipschitz at infinity
if and only if
\partial f(\infty)
is nonempty compact.

It should be emphasized, due to the unboundedness of neighborhoods at infinity, that smooth functions are not necessarily Lipschitz at infinity and that the set of subgradients of
f
at infinity may not be a singleton set when
f
is smooth and Lipschitz at infinity (see Example
5.9
below).

We also note that some results given in this paper can be extended to infinite dimensional spaces. However, to lighten the exposition, we do not pursue this extension here.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, some notation and definitions are given.
In Section
3
we study Clarke’s tangent and normal cones at infinity to unbounded subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}.

In Section
4
we give the definition of subgradients at infinity for extended real valued functions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and establish some properties of them. In particular, we provide necessary optimality conditions at infinity for optimization problems.
In Section
5
, some characterizations of the Lipschitz continuity at infinity for lower semi-continuous functions are given.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Notation

In this paper we deal with the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
equipped with the usual scalar product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and the corresponding norm
\|\cdot\|.
We denote by
\mathbb{B}_{r}(x)
the closed ball centered at
x
with radius
r;
when
{x}
is the origin of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
we write
\mathbb{B}_{r}
instead of
\mathbb{B}_{r}({x}),
and when
r=1
we write
\mathbb{B}
instead of
\mathbb{B}_{1}.
For any two different points
x
and
x^{\prime}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n},
the
open line segment
joining
x
and
x^{\prime}
is the set

\displaystyle(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{(1-t)x+tx^{\prime}\mid 0<t<1\}.

We will adopt the convention that

\inf\emptyset=+\infty
and
\sup\emptyset=-\infty,
and that
\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}=+\infty
if either
\lambda_{1}
or
\lambda_{2}
is
+\infty
(even if the other is
-\infty
). It also is expedient to set

\displaystyle(\pm\infty)+\lambda

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda+(\pm\infty)\ =\ \pm\infty\quad\textrm{ for any real }\lambda,

\displaystyle\lambda\cdot(\pm\infty)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\pm\infty)\cdot\lambda\ =\ \pm\infty\quad\textrm{ for all }\lambda>0,

\displaystyle\lambda\cdot(\pm\infty)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\pm\infty)\cdot\lambda\ =\ \mp\infty\quad\textrm{ for all }\lambda<0.

The order on
\mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
is extended in the natural way to

-\infty<r<+\infty

for all
r\in\mathbb{R}.

Let
C
be a nonempty subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}.
The closure, interior, boundary, and convex hull of the set
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
will be written as
\mathrm{cl}{C},

\mathrm{int}C,

\partial C,
and
\mathrm{co}C,
respectively. The distance from a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
to
C
is defined by
d_{C}(x):=\inf_{y\in C}\|x-y\|.
By definition, it is easy to see that the distance function

d_{C}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R},\quad x\mapsto d_{C}(x),

is nonnegative and
1
-Lipschitz.

2.2.
Clarke’s tangent cones and generalized gradients

In this subsection we recall some definitions, which can be found in
[
7
,
18
,
19
,
22
]
.

Let
C
be a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}.
For each
x\in C,
the
tangent cone

T_{C}(x)
in the sense of Clarke consists of all
vectors
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that, whenever we have sequences
x_{k}\in C
with
x_{k}\to x
and
t_{k}\searrow 0,
there exists a sequence
v_{k}\to v
such that
x_{k}+t_{k}v_{k}\in C
for all
k.
It is well-known that
T_{C}(x)
is a closed convex cone in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
containing the origin
0\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
(see
[
7
,
19
,
21
]
). Its polar

\displaystyle N_{C}(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ |\ \langle v,w\rangle\leq 0\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad v\in T_{C}(x)\}

is called the
normal cone
to
C
at
x.
Clearly,
N_{C}(x)
is also a closed convex cone.

Let
f
be an extended real valued function on
\mathbb{R}^{n}.
As usual,
the effective domain and
epigraph
of
f
are denoted by, respectively,

\displaystyle\mathrm{dom}f

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid f(x)<\infty\},

\displaystyle\mathrm{epi}f

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\mid f(x)\leq y\}.

The
generalized (Clarke) gradient
of
f
at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
f(x)
finite is defined by

\displaystyle\partial f(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ |\ (\xi,-1)\in N_{\textrm{epi}f}(x,f(x))\}.

If
f(x)=\pm\infty,
we set
\partial f(x):=\emptyset.
It is well-known that
(see
[
7
, Propositions 2.2.4 and 2.2.7]
and
[
21
, Theorem 5]
)
when
f
is continuously differentiable,
\partial f(x)
reduces to the singleton set
\{\nabla f(x)\},
and when
f
is convex and is finite at
x,
then
\partial f(x)
coincides with what is called the
subgradient set
of convex analysis; that is, the set of vectors
\xi
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying

\displaystyle f(x^{\prime})-f(x)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\langle\xi,x^{\prime}-x\rangle\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

3.
Clarke’s tangent cones at infinity

Let
C
be a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
I
be a nonempty subset of
\{1,\ldots,n\}.
Consider the projection
\pi\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{\#I},x:=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\mapsto(x_{i})_{i\in I}.
For each
R>0
let

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{R,I}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ |\ \|\pi(x)\|\leq R\}.

Assume that the set
\pi(C)
is unbounded.

Definition 3.1
.

(i) By the
Clarke tangent cone of
C
at infinity (with respect to the index set
I
)
, denoted
T_{C}(\infty_{I}),
we mean the set of all vectors
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that, whenever we have sequences
x_{k}\in C
with
\pi(x_{k})\to\infty
and
t_{k}\searrow 0,
there exists a sequence
v_{k}\to v
such that
x_{k}+t_{k}v_{k}\in C
for all
k.

(ii) By the
normal cone to
C
at infinity (with respect to the index set
I
)
, we mean the set

\displaystyle N_{C}(\infty_{I})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ |\ \langle v,w\rangle\leq 0\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad v\in T_{C}(\infty_{I})\}.

Remark 3.2
.

For an extended real valued function
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{n},
the definition of
\partial f(\infty),
which relates to the variation of
f(x)
as
x
tends to infinity, will be given in terms of the normal cone to the epigraph of
f
at infinity. Hence, we will be only interested in points
(x,y)\in\mathrm{epi}f\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}
as
x
goes to infinity, i.e., points in the epigraph of
f
whose
i
-th coordinates tend to infinity for some
i
in the index set
\{1,\ldots,n\}\subset\{1,\ldots,n,n+1\}.
This explains why the index set
I
appears in the above definition of tangent and normal cones at infinity.

Lemma 3.3
.

For a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n},
the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

v\in T_{C}(\infty_{I}).

(ii)

For any sequences
x_{k}\in C
with
\pi(x_{k})\to\infty
and
t_{k}\searrow 0,
there exists a sequence
v_{k}\to v
such that
x_{k}+t_{k}v_{k}\in C
for infinitely many
k.

(iii)

For every
\epsilon>0
there exist constants
R>0
and
\lambda>0
such that

(1)

\displaystyle C\cap[{x}+t\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(v)]

\displaystyle\neq

\displaystyle\emptyset\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad{x}\in C\setminus\mathbb{B}_{R,I}\ \textrm{ and }\ t\in[0,\lambda].

(iv)

\lim_{\pi(x)\rightarrow\infty,\,x\in C,\,t\searrow 0}\dfrac{d_{C}(x+tv)}{t}=0.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii). This is clear from the definition of
T_{C}(\infty_{I}).

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii). Suppose the implication were false. Then we could find a real number
\epsilon>0
and sequences
x_{k}\in C
with
\pi(x_{k})\to\infty
and
t_{k}\searrow 0,
such that

\displaystyle C\cap[{x_{k}}+t_{k}\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(v)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\emptyset\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad k.

This contradicts our assumption that there is a sequence
v_{k}\to v
such that
x_{k}+t_{k}v_{k}\in C
for infinitely many
k.

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(iv). Take an arbitrary
\epsilon>0.
There exist constants
R>0
and
\lambda>0
such that for each
{x}\in C\setminus\mathbb{B}_{R,I}
and
t\in[0,\lambda]
we have
C\cap[{x}+t\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(v)]\neq\emptyset.
For such
x
and
t,
there is

\overline{x}\in C\cap[{x}+t\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(v)],
and so

\displaystyle\dfrac{d_{C}(x+tv)}{t}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\dfrac{\|x+tv-\overline{x}\|}{t}\ \leq\ \epsilon.

Since
\epsilon>0
is chosen arbitrarily small, it holds that

\displaystyle\lim_{\pi(x)\rightarrow\infty,\,x\in C,\,t\searrow 0}\dfrac{d_{C}(x+tv)}{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

(iv)
\Rightarrow
(i). Take any sequences
x_{k}\in C
with
\pi(x_{k})\rightarrow\infty
and
t_{k}\searrow 0.
We have

\displaystyle\epsilon_{k}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\dfrac{d_{C}(x_{k}+t_{k}v)}{t_{k}}\ \to\ 0

and there is
\overline{x}_{k}\in C
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\|x_{k}+t_{k}v-\overline{x}_{k}\|}{t_{k}}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\epsilon_{k}+\dfrac{1}{k}.

Setting
v_{k}:=\dfrac{\overline{x}_{k}-x_{k}}{t_{k}},
we get
\|v_{k}-v\|<\epsilon_{k}+\dfrac{1}{k}
. Hence,
v_{k}\to v
and
x_{k}+t_{k}v_{k}=\overline{x}_{k}\in C
for all
k.
By definition,
v\in T_{C}(\infty).

∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
D
be a nonempty compact subset of
T_{C}(\infty_{I}).
Then for every
\epsilon>0
there exist constants
R>0
and
\lambda>0
such that (
1
) holds simultaneously for all
v\in\mathrm{co}D.

Proof.

Take arbitrary
\epsilon>0.
Since
D
is compact, it can be covered by a finite family of balls
\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(v_{i}),
where
v_{i}\in D
for
i=1,\ldots,m.
Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{co}D

\displaystyle\subset

\displaystyle\mathrm{co}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(v_{i})\right)\ =\ \mathrm{co}\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\}+\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}.

Hence, it will suffice to show (
1
) holds for all
v
in
\mathrm{co}\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\},
because this will imply that for all
v\in\mathrm{co}D
we have

\displaystyle C\cap[{x}+t\mathbb{B}_{2\epsilon}(v)]

\displaystyle\neq

\displaystyle\emptyset\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad{x}\in C\setminus\mathbb{B}_{R,I}\ \textrm{ and }\ t\in[0,\lambda],

which is equivalent to the desired conclusion since
\epsilon>0
is arbitrary anyway.

For
i=1,\ldots,m,
we have that (
1
) holds for
v_{i}
and certain positive constants
R_{i}
and
\lambda_{i}.
Letting

\overline{R}:=\max_{i=1,\ldots,m}R_{i}\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\overline{\lambda}:=\min_{i=1,\ldots,m}\lambda_{i},

we have

(2)

\displaystyle C\cap[{x}+t\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(v_{i})]

\displaystyle\neq

\displaystyle\emptyset\quad\textrm{ for all }{x}\in C\setminus\mathbb{B}_{\overline{R},I},\ t\in[0,\overline{\lambda}],\ \textrm{ and }\ i=1,\ldots,m.

Let
R\geq\overline{R}
and
\lambda\in(0,\overline{\lambda}]
be such that

\displaystyle R-\lambda(\rho+\epsilon)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\overline{R},

where
\rho:=\max_{i=1,\ldots,m}\|v_{i}\|.
The assertion

(3)

\displaystyle C\cap[{x}+t\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(v)]

\displaystyle\neq

\displaystyle\emptyset\quad\textrm{ for all }{x}\in C\setminus\mathbb{B}_{R,I},\ t\in[0,\lambda],\textrm{ and }\ v\in\mathrm{co}\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\}

holds trivially for
k=1,
in view of (
2
). Make the induction hypothesis that it holds for
k=m-1.

Take arbitrary
{x}\in C\setminus\mathbb{B}_{R,I},

t\in[0,\lambda],
and
v\in\mathrm{co}\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\}.
We can write

v=\alpha v^{\prime}+(1-\alpha)v_{m},

where
\alpha\in[0,1]
and
v^{\prime}\in\mathrm{co}\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{m-1}\}.
Since
\alpha t\in[0,\lambda],
we have by induction that
C
meets
{x}+\alpha t\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(v^{\prime}).
Let
{x}^{\prime}
be any point in the intersection. Then
{x}^{\prime}\in{x}+\alpha t\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(v^{\prime}),
and so,

\displaystyle\|\pi({x}^{\prime})\|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\|\pi({x})\|-\alpha t(\|v^{\prime}\|+\epsilon)

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle R-\lambda(\rho+\epsilon)\ \geq\ \overline{R}.

Thus,
{x}^{\prime}\in C\setminus\mathbb{B}_{\overline{R},I}.
Since
(1-\alpha)t\leq\lambda\leq\overline{\lambda},
it follows from (
2
) that
C
meets
{x}^{\prime}+(1-\alpha)t\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(v_{m}).
Hence
C
meets

{x}+\alpha t\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(v^{\prime})+(1-\alpha)t\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(v_{m})={x}+t\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(v).

This proves (
3
) for
k=m
and completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 3.5
.

The set
T_{C}(\infty_{I})
is a closed convex cone in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
containing the origin.

Proof.

By definition, it is easy to check that
T_{C}(\infty_{I})
is a closed cone containing the origin. Moreover, it is convex in view of Lemma
3.4
.
∎

We are ready to prove the first of our main theorems, which gives a characterization of interior points of Clarke’s tangent cones at infinity.

Theorem 3.6
.

Assume that the set
C
is closed. Then
v\in\mathrm{int}T_{C}(\infty_{I})
if and only if there exist constants
\epsilon>0,R>0,
and
\lambda>0
such that

(4)

\displaystyle{x}+tv^{\prime}\in C\quad\textrm{ for all }{x}\in C\setminus\mathbb{B}_{R,I},\ t\in[0,\lambda],\ \textrm{ and }\ v^{\prime}\in\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(v).

Proof.

Sufficiency.
Assume that the assertion (
4
) holds, and take arbitrary
v^{\prime}\in\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(v).
For any sequences
x_{k}\in C
with

\pi(x_{k})\to\infty
and
t_{k}\searrow 0,
we have
x_{k}\not\in\mathbb{B}_{R,I}
and
t_{k}\in(0,\lambda)
for all
k
sufficiently large, and so
x_{k}+t_{k}v_{k}\in C
for
v_{k}:=v^{\prime}.
By definition, then
v^{\prime}\in T_{C}(\infty_{I}).
Therefore,
\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(v)\subset T_{C}(\infty_{I}).
Consequently,
v\in\mathrm{int}T_{C}(\infty_{I}).

Necessity.
Given
v\in\mathrm{int}T_{C}(\infty_{I}),
choose
\epsilon>0
small enough that
\mathbb{B}_{3\epsilon}(v)\subset T_{C}(\infty_{I}).
Apply Lemma
3.4
to the compact set
D:=\mathbb{B}_{3\epsilon}(v)
to obtain
R^{\prime}>0
and
\lambda^{\prime}>0
such that

(5)

\displaystyle C\cap[{x}+t\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(v^{\prime})]\neq\emptyset\quad\textrm{ whenever }\ {x}\in C\setminus\mathbb{B}_{R,I},\ t\in[0,\lambda^{\prime}],\textrm{ and }\ v^{\prime}\in\mathbb{B}_{3\epsilon}(v).

Next choose constants
R
and
\lambda
so that

(6)

\displaystyle R-2\lambda(\|v\|+\epsilon)\geq R^{\prime}\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\lambda\in(0,\lambda^{\prime}].

We will show that (
4
) holds for this choice of
\epsilon,R,
and
\lambda.

By contradiction, suppose that (
4
) does not hold. Then there exist

(7)

\displaystyle\overline{x}\in C\setminus\mathbb{B}_{R,I},\quad\overline{\lambda}\in[0,\lambda],\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\overline{v}\in\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(v)

such that
\overline{x}+\overline{\lambda}\overline{v}\not\in C.
Since
C
is closed, we can choose
\rho>0
small enough that

(8)

\displaystyle C\cap\mathbb{B}_{\rho}(\overline{x}+\overline{\lambda}\overline{v})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\emptyset.

In particular,
\rho<\|\overline{x}-(\overline{x}+\overline{\lambda}\overline{v})\|=\overline{\lambda}\|\overline{v}\|.

Let

(9)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\lambda}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\max\{s\in[0,\overline{\lambda}]\ |\ C\cap\mathbb{B}_{\rho}(\overline{x}+s\overline{v})\neq\emptyset\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max\{s\in[0,\overline{\lambda}]\ |\ C\cap[\overline{x}+s\overline{v}+\mathbb{B}_{\rho}]\neq\emptyset\};

this maximum is attached because
C
is closed and
\mathbb{B}_{\rho}
is compact. Since
\overline{x}\in C
and (
8
) holds, we have

(10)

\displaystyle 0<\widetilde{\lambda}<\overline{\lambda}\leq\lambda\leq\lambda^{\prime}.

Select any
\widetilde{x}\in C\cap\mathbb{B}_{\rho}(\overline{x}+\widetilde{\lambda}\overline{v}),
as exists by (
9
). The interior of the ball
\mathbb{B}_{\rho}(\overline{x}+\widetilde{\lambda}\overline{v})
cannot meet
C,
in view of (
9
). Thus,

\displaystyle\widetilde{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{x}+\widetilde{\lambda}\overline{v}+\rho e\quad\textrm{ with}\quad\|e\|=1.

We deduce from (
7
) and (
10
) that

\displaystyle\|\pi(\widetilde{x})\|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\|\pi(\overline{x})\|-\|\pi(\widetilde{x}-\overline{x})\|

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle R-\|\pi(\widetilde{\lambda}\overline{v}+\rho e)\|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle R-\|\widetilde{\lambda}\overline{v}+\rho e\|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle R-\widetilde{\lambda}\|\overline{v}\|-\rho

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle R-\widetilde{\lambda}\|\overline{v}\|-\overline{\lambda}\|\overline{v}\|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle R-2{\lambda}\|\overline{v}\|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle R-2{\lambda}(\|{v}\|+\epsilon).

It follows from (
6
) that for this
\widetilde{x}
and for
\widetilde{v}:=\overline{v}-2\epsilon e
we have

\widetilde{x}\in C\setminus\mathbb{B}_{R^{\prime},I}\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\widetilde{v}\in\mathbb{B}_{3\epsilon}(v).

Therefore, by (
5
),

(11)

\displaystyle C\cap[\widetilde{x}+t\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(\widetilde{v})]

\displaystyle\neq

\displaystyle\emptyset\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad t\in[0,\lambda^{\prime}].

However, take any
t>0
small enough that

(12)

\displaystyle 0<t<\min\{\frac{\rho}{2\epsilon},\overline{\lambda}-\widetilde{\lambda}\}.

(Hence
t<\overline{\lambda}\leq\lambda^{\prime}.)
It will be shown that

(13)

\displaystyle C\cap[\widetilde{x}+t\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(\widetilde{v})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\emptyset.

The contradiction between this and (
11
) will finish the proof.

Since
t<\frac{\rho}{2\epsilon}
in (
12
), we have

0<\rho-2\epsilon t<\rho-\epsilon t<\rho,

so that

\displaystyle\widetilde{x}+t\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(\widetilde{v})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\overline{x}+\widetilde{\lambda}\overline{v}+\rho e)+t(\overline{v}-2\epsilon e)+t\epsilon\mathbb{B}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{x}+(\widetilde{\lambda}+t)\overline{v}+(\rho-2t\epsilon)e+t\epsilon\mathbb{B}

\displaystyle\subset

\displaystyle\overline{x}+(\widetilde{\lambda}+t)\overline{v}+\mathbb{B}_{\rho}.

This yields (
13
), because

\displaystyle C\cap[\overline{x}+(\widetilde{\lambda}+t)\overline{v}+\mathbb{B}_{\rho}]=\emptyset

by the definition of
\widetilde{\lambda}
and for
t>0
small enough.
∎

Corollary 3.7
.

Assume that the set
C
is closed. If the interior of
T_{C}(\infty_{I})
is nonempty, then the multifunction
N_{C}\colon C\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
is closed at infinity, in the sense that

\displaystyle x_{k}\in C,\ \pi(x_{k})\to\infty,\ z_{k}\in N_{C}(x_{k}),\ z_{k}\to z\quad\Longrightarrow\quad z\in N_{C}(\infty_{I}).

Proof.

Take arbitrary
v\in\mathrm{int}T_{C}(\infty_{I}).
In view of Theorem
3.6
, there exists a constant
R>0
such that
v\in T_{C}(x)
for all
x\in C\setminus\mathbb{B}_{R,I},
and hence
v\in T_{C}(x_{k})
for all
k
sufficiently large. By the definition of
N_{C}(x_{k}),
then
\langle v,z_{k}\rangle\leq 0.
Consequently,

\langle v,z\rangle=\lim_{k\to\infty}\langle v,z_{k}\rangle\leq 0.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\langle v,z\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 0\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad v\in\mathrm{int}T_{C}(\infty_{I}).

Since
T_{C}(\infty_{I})
is convex with nonempty interior, it is the closure of its interior, and so the inequality

\langle v,z\rangle\leq 0
holds for all
v\in T_{C}(\infty_{I}).
Thus,
z\in N_{C}(\infty_{I}).

∎

Remark 3.8
.

The interior of
T_{C}(\infty_{I})
is nonempty if and only if
N_{C}(\infty_{I})
is a pointed cone in the sense that
v\in N_{C}(\infty_{I})\setminus\{0\}
implies
-v\not\in N_{C}(\infty_{I})
(see
[
22
, Exercise 6.22]
).

An analytical characterization of Clarke’s tangent cones is as follows.

Proposition 3.9
.

A vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
belongs to
T_{C}(\infty_{I})
if and only if the following equality holds:

\displaystyle\limsup_{\pi(x)\to\infty,\ d_{C}(x)\to 0,\ t\searrow 0}\frac{d_{C}(x+tv)-d_{C}(x)}{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

Proof.

Let
\gamma(v)
be the left-hand side of the above expression. Then
\gamma(v)\geq 0.

Let
v\in T_{C}(\infty_{I}).
Choose sequences
x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t_{k}\in(0,+\infty)
such that

\pi(x_{k})\to\infty,\quad t_{k}\searrow 0,\quad d_{C}(x_{k})\to 0,\quad\gamma(v)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{d_{C}(x_{k}+t_{k}v)-d_{C}(x_{k})}{t_{k}}.

Let
\overline{x}_{k}
in
C
satisfy

\displaystyle\|x_{k}-\overline{x}_{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d_{C}(x_{k})+t_{k}^{2}.

Then
\pi(\overline{x}_{k})\to\infty.
By the definition of
T_{C}(\infty_{I}),
there exists a sequence
v_{k}
converging to
v
such that
\overline{x}_{k}+t_{k}v_{k}\in C
for all
k.
Since the distance function
d_{C}(\cdot)
is 1-Lipschitz, it follows that

\displaystyle d_{C}(x_{k}+t_{k}v)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d_{C}(\overline{x}_{k}+t_{k}v_{k})+\|x_{k}-\overline{x}_{k}\|+t_{k}\|v-v_{k}\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|x_{k}-\overline{x}_{k}\|+t_{k}\|v-v_{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d_{C}(x_{k})+t_{k}^{2}+t_{k}\|v-v_{k}\|.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\gamma(v)\ =\ \lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{d_{C}(x_{k}+t_{k}v)-d_{C}(x_{k})}{t_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 0,

and so
\gamma(v)=0.

Conversely, assume that
\gamma(v)=0
and that sequences
x_{k}\in C
with
\pi(x_{k})\to\infty
and
t_{k}\searrow 0
are given.
Then

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{d_{C}(x_{k}+t_{k}v)}{t_{k}}\ =\ \lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{d_{C}(x_{k}+t_{k}v)-d_{C}(x_{k})}{t_{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

Choose any
\widetilde{x}_{k}\in C
so that

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{x}_{k}-(x_{k}+t_{k}v)\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d_{C}(x_{k}+t_{k}v)+t_{k}^{2}

and let

\displaystyle v_{k}:=\frac{\widetilde{x}_{k}-x_{k}}{t_{k}}.

Then
x_{k}+t_{k}v=\widetilde{x}_{k}\in C.
Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\|v_{k}-v\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\|\widetilde{x}_{k}-(x_{k}+t_{k}v)\|}{t_{k}}\ \leq\ \frac{d_{C}(x_{k}+t_{k}v)}{t_{k}}+t_{k},

which yields
v_{k}\to v.
By definition, therefore,
v\in T_{C}(\infty_{I}).

∎

4.
Subgradients at infinity and optimality conditions at infinity

In this section, let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}:=\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be an extended real valued function.
Fix the index set
I:=\{1,\ldots,n\}\subset\{1,\ldots,n,n+1\}
and consider the projection
\pi\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{n},(x,y)\mapsto x.
By definition, then
\pi(\mathrm{epi}f)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid f(x)<\infty\}.

4.1.
Subgradients at infinity

Definition 4.1
.

We define the set of
subgradients
(or
generalized gradients
)
of
f
at infinity
by

\displaystyle\partial f(\infty)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ |\ (\xi,-1)\in N_{\textrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I})\}.

Observe that
\partial f(\infty)
is a (possibly empty) closed convex set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
because the cone
N_{\textrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I})
is closed convex. The following simple examples illustrate our definition.

Example 4.2
.

(i) Let
n:=1
and consider the function
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

f(x):=\begin{cases}0&\textrm{ if }x\leq 0,\\
-x&\textrm{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

A direct calculation shows that

\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(v_{1},v_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\ |\ v_{2}\geq 0\textrm{ and }v_{1}+v_{2}\geq 0\},

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\ |\ w_{1}\leq 0,w_{2}\leq 0,\textrm{ and }w_{2}-w_{1}\leq 0\},

\displaystyle\partial f(\infty)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[-1,0].

(ii) Consider the function
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},x\mapsto e^{x}.
We have

\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(v_{1},v_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\ |\ v_{1}\leq 0\textrm{ and }v_{2}\geq 0\},

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\ |\ w_{1}\geq 0\textrm{ and }w_{2}\leq 0\},

\displaystyle\partial f(\infty)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[0,+\infty).

(iii) Consider the function
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},x\mapsto x^{3}.
We have

\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(v_{1},v_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\ |\ v_{1}\leq 0\},

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\ |\ w_{1}\geq 0\textrm{ and }w_{2}=0\},

\displaystyle\partial f(\infty)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\emptyset.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
f
be lower semi-continuous. Suppose
\partial f(\infty)\neq\emptyset
and it does not contain any entire line. Then the multifunction
\partial f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\cup\{\infty\}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
is closed at infinity, in the sense that

\displaystyle x_{k}\to\infty,\ \xi_{k}\in\partial f(x_{k}),\ \textrm{ and }\ \xi_{k}\to\xi\quad\implies\quad\xi\in\partial f(\infty).

Proof.

The set
\mathrm{epi}f
is closed because
f
is lower semi-continuous. In view of Corollary
3.7
and Remark
3.8
, it suffices to show that the cone
N_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I})
is pointed.
To see this, we first observe that
(0,r)\in T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I})
for all
r\geq 0,
and so

N_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I})\subset\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\mid y\leq 0\}.

Consequently, the cone
N_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I})
fails to be pointed if and only if for some
u\neq 0
it contains both
(u,0)
and
(-u,0)
. For an arbitrary element of
N_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I})
of the form
(\xi,-1)
, this property of
u
is equivalent to having

(\xi,-1)+t(u,0)\in N_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I})\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Thus, if
\partial f(\infty)
contains an element
\xi,
the property of
u
is equivalent to the line
\{\xi+tu\mid t\in\mathbb{R}\}
being contained in
\partial f(\infty).

∎

Proposition 4.4

(A version at infinity of the Fermat theorem)
.

Let
f
be bounded below. If there exists a sequence
x_{k}\to\infty
such that
f(x_{k})\to f_{*}:=\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x),
then
0\in\partial f(\infty).

Proof.

We will show that
(0,-1)\in N_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I}),
or equivalently, that

\displaystyle r\geq 0\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad(v,r)\in T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I}).

To this end, take any
(v,r)\in T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I}).
Let

t_{k}:=\begin{cases}\sqrt{f(x_{k})-f_{*}}&\textrm{ if }f(x_{k})>f_{*},\\
\frac{1}{k}&\textrm{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Then
t_{k}>0
and
t_{k}\to 0.
Passing to a subsequence if necessary, we can suppose that the sequence
t_{k}
is decreasing. By the definition of
T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I}),
there exists a sequence
(v_{k},r_{k})
converging to
(v,r)
such that

(x_{k},f(x_{k}))+t_{k}(v_{k},r_{k})\in\mathrm{epi}f.
But then

\displaystyle f(x_{k})+t_{k}r_{k}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle f(x_{k}+t_{k}v_{k})\ \geq\ f_{*}.

It follows that either
r_{k}\geq-\sqrt{f(x_{k})-f_{*}}
or
r_{k}\geq 0.
Consequently,
r=\lim_{k\to\infty}r_{k}\geq 0,
which completes the proof.
∎

4.2.
Subderivatives at infinity

The set
\partial f(\infty)
of subgradients at infinity corresponds to a kind of directional derivative at infinity that for general functions has a complicated
description but can be reduced to simpler formulas in many important cases (see Proposition
4.12
below).

The
(upper) subderivative of
f
at infinity with respect to a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined by

\displaystyle f^{\uparrow}(\infty;v)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0}\limsup_{x\xrightarrow{\mathrm{dom}f}\infty,\ t\searrow 0}\inf_{\ w\in\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(v)}\frac{f(x+tw)-f(x)}{t},

where the notation
x\xrightarrow{\mathrm{dom}f}\infty
means that
x\to\infty
with
x\in\mathrm{dom}f.
Observe that
f^{\uparrow}(\infty;0)\leq 0.
Furthermore, we have the following relationship between Clarke’s tangent cones and subderivatives at infinity.

Proposition 4.5
.

The tangent cone
T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I})
is the epigraph of the function
f^{\uparrow}(\infty;\cdot);
that is, a point
(v,r)
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}
belongs to
T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I})
if and only if
f^{\uparrow}(\infty;v)\leq r.

Proof.

Necessity.
Let
(v,r)\in T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I})
and let any
\epsilon>0
be given. It suffices to show

(14)

\displaystyle\limsup_{x\xrightarrow{\mathrm{dom}f}\infty,\ t\searrow 0}\inf_{w\in\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(v)}\frac{f(x+tw)-f(x)}{t}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle r.

To see this, let
x_{k}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{dom}f}\infty
and
t_{k}\searrow 0.
Since
(v,r)\in T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I}),
there exists a sequence
(v_{k},r_{k})
converging to
(v,r)
such that
(x_{k},f(x_{k}))+t_{k}(v_{k},r_{k})\in\mathrm{epi}f
for all
k.
Then

\displaystyle f(x_{k}+t_{k}v_{k})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle f(x_{k})+t_{k}r_{k}.

Consequently, for all
k
sufficiently large,

\displaystyle\inf_{w\in\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(v)}\frac{f(x_{k}+t_{k}w)-f(x_{k})}{t_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{f(x_{k}+t_{k}v_{k})-f(x_{k})}{t_{k}}\ \leq\ r_{k},

and the inequality (
14
) follows.

Sufficiency.
Suppose
f^{\uparrow}(\infty;v)\leq r
and let
(x_{k},y_{k})
be any sequence in
\mathrm{epi}f
with
x_{k}\to\infty
and
t_{k}
any sequence decreasing to
0.
In view of Lemma
3.3
, in order to show that
(v,r)
belongs to
T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I})
it suffices to construct a sequence
(v_{k},r_{k})
converging to
(v,r)
such that

\displaystyle(x_{k},y_{k})+t_{k}(v_{k},r_{k})

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle\mathrm{epi}f\quad\textrm{ infinitely time,}

that is,

(15)

\displaystyle f(x_{k}+t_{k}v_{k})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle y_{k}+t_{k}r_{k}\quad\textrm{ infinitely time.}

Since
f^{\uparrow}(\infty;v)\leq r,
for each positive integer
\ell,
there is a real number
\epsilon:=\epsilon({\ell})\in(0,\frac{1}{\ell})
such that

\displaystyle\limsup_{x\xrightarrow{\mathrm{dom}f}\infty,\ t\searrow 0}\inf_{\ w\in\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(v)}\frac{f(x+tw)-f(x)}{t}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle r+\frac{1}{\ell}.

Since
x_{k}\to\infty
with
f(x_{k})\leq y_{k}<\infty
and
t_{k}\searrow 0,
there exists an index
k=k(\ell)>\ell
such that

\displaystyle\inf_{w\in\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(v)}\frac{f(x_{k}+t_{k}w)-f(x_{k})}{t_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle r+\frac{2}{\ell},

and therefore a point
v_{k}\in\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(v)
such that

\displaystyle\frac{f(x_{k}+t_{k}v_{k})-f(x_{k})}{t_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle r+\frac{3}{\ell}.

Let us define, for each index
k
in the subsequence
k(1),k(2),\ldots,

\displaystyle r_{k}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\max\left\{r,\frac{f(x_{k}+t_{k}v_{k})-f(x_{k})}{t_{k}}\right\}.

Clearly,
r_{k}
converges to
r
and the inequality in (
15
) is satisfied.
∎

Corollary 4.6
.

The function
f^{\uparrow}(\infty;\cdot)
is convex, positively homogeneous, and lower semi-continuous.

Proof.

By Corollary
3.5
,
T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I})
is a closed convex cone. This fact, together with Proposition
4.5
, implies the desired conclusion.
∎

An analytical characterization of
\partial f(\infty)
is given in the following corollary.

Corollary 4.7
.

We have
\partial f(\infty)=\emptyset
if and only if
f^{\uparrow}(\infty;0)=-\infty.
Otherwise, we have

\displaystyle\partial f(\infty)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ |\ \langle\xi,v\rangle\leq f^{\uparrow}(\infty;v)\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\},

\displaystyle f^{\uparrow}(\infty;v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup\{\langle\xi,v\rangle\ |\ \xi\in\partial f(\infty)\},

\displaystyle\partial f^{\uparrow}(\infty;\cdot)(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial f(\infty).

Proof.

By definition,
\xi\in\partial f(\infty)
if and only if
(\xi,-1)\in N_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I}).
Equivalently,

\displaystyle\langle(\xi,-1),(v,r)\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 0\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad(v,r)\in T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I}).

This, together with Proposition
4.5
, implies that
\xi\in\partial f(\infty)
if and only if

\displaystyle\langle\xi,v\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle r\quad\textrm{ for all }v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\textrm{ and all }r\geq f^{\uparrow}(\infty;v).

If
f^{\uparrow}(\infty;v)=-\infty
for some
v,
there can be no such
\xi,
that is,
\partial f(\infty)=\emptyset.
On the other hand, we have
f^{\uparrow}(\infty;\lambda v)=\lambda f^{\uparrow}(\infty;v)
for all
v
and
\lambda>0
because the function
f^{\uparrow}(\infty;\cdot)
is positively homogeneous (we use the convention
\lambda(-\infty)=-\infty).
Therefore,
f^{\uparrow}(\infty;\cdot)=-\infty
somewhere if and only if it is
-\infty
at
0.

To complete the proof of the corollary we assume that
f^{\uparrow}(\infty;0)>-\infty.
Then
f^{\uparrow}(\infty;v)>-\infty
for all
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.
Moreover, by definition,
f^{\uparrow}(\infty;0)\leq 0.
It follows easily that
f^{\uparrow}(\infty;0)=0.

Since the function
f^{\uparrow}(\infty;\cdot)
is convex (see Corollary
4.6
), its subgradient set (in the sense of convex analysis) at
v=0
is nonempty (see
[
18
, Theorem 23.4]
) and agrees with
\partial f^{\uparrow}(\infty;\cdot)(0)
(see
[
7
, Proposition 2.2.7]
and
[
21
, Theorem 5]
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\emptyset\ \neq\ \{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ |\ \langle\xi,v\rangle\leq f^{\uparrow}(\infty;v)\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial f^{\uparrow}(\infty;\cdot)(0).

Consequently, the set
\partial f(\infty)
is nonempty because of the second assertion, as we have seen in the beginning of the proof.
The last assertion follows immediately. Finally, by Corollary
4.6
and
[
18
, Corollary 13.2.1]
(see also
[
22
, Theorem 8.24]
),
f^{\uparrow}(\infty;\cdot)
is the support function of
\partial f(\infty),
which is the third assertion. The proof is complete.
∎

Example 4.8
.

Let
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an unbounded set. Define the
indicator
function
\delta_{C}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
of the set
C
by

\delta_{C}(x):=\begin{cases}0&\textrm{ if }x\in C,\\
+\infty&\textrm{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

By definition,
\mathrm{epi}\delta_{C}=C\times[0,+\infty).
Hence

T_{\mathrm{epi}\delta_{C}}(\infty_{I})=T_{C}(\infty_{I})\times[0,+\infty)
and
N_{\mathrm{epi}\delta_{C}}(\infty_{I})=N_{C}(\infty_{I})\times(-\infty,0].
Therefore,
\partial\delta_{C}(\infty)=N_{C}(\infty_{I})
and

\delta_{C}^{\uparrow}(\infty;v)=\begin{cases}0&\textrm{ if }v\in T_{C}(\infty_{I}),\\
+\infty&\textrm{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

4.3.
Directionally Lipschitz functions at infinity

We will say that
f
is
directionally Lipschitz at infinity with respect to a vector

v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
if the quantity

\displaystyle f^{\dagger}(\infty;v)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\limsup_{x\xrightarrow{\mathrm{dom}f}\infty,\ t\searrow 0,\ w\to v}\frac{f(x+tw)-f(x)}{t}

is not
+\infty.
The function
f
is said to be
directionally Lipschitz at infinity
if it is directionally Lipschitz at infinity with respect to at least one
v
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
f
be lower semi-continuous. Then,
f
is directionally Lipschitz at infinity with respect to
v
if and only if there exists a real number
r
such that
(v,r)\in{\rm int}T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I}).

Proof.

Sufficiency.
Let
(v,r)\in{\rm int}T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I}).
Since
f
is lower semi-continuous,
{\mathrm{epi}f}
is a closed set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}.
In light of Theorem
3.6
, there exist constants
R>0
and
\epsilon>0
such that

\displaystyle({x},{y})+t(v^{\prime},r^{\prime})\in\mathrm{epi}f\ \textrm{ for all }\ ({x},{y})\in\mathrm{epi}f\setminus\mathbb{B}_{R,I},\ (v^{\prime},r^{\prime})\in\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(v,r),\ t\in(0,\epsilon).

Letting
{y}:=f({x})
and
r^{\prime}:=r,
we get
f({x}+tv^{\prime})\leq f({x})+tr,
and so

\displaystyle\dfrac{f({x}+tv^{\prime})-f({x})}{t}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle r.

It follows that
f
is directionally Lipschitz at infinity with respect to
v.

Necessity.
Assume that
f
is directionally Lipschitz at infinity with respect to
v,
i.e.,
f^{\dagger}(\infty;v)<+\infty.
Then we can find a real number
L>0
satisfying

\displaystyle\limsup_{x\xrightarrow{\mathrm{dom}f}\infty,\ t\searrow 0,\ w\to v}\frac{f(x+tw)-f(x)}{t}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle L.

Consequently, there are constants
R>0
and
\epsilon_{1}>0
such that for all
{x}\in\mathrm{dom}f\setminus\mathbb{B}_{R}
,
v^{\prime}\in\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon_{1}}(v)
and
t\in(0,\epsilon_{1}),
we have

\displaystyle\dfrac{f({x}+tv^{\prime})-f({x})}{t}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle L,

and hence

\displaystyle f({x}+tv^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle f({x})+tL\ \leq\ {y}+tr^{\prime},

where
{y}\geq f({x})
and
r^{\prime}\geq L.
Letting
r:=2L
and
\epsilon:=\min\{\epsilon_{1},\frac{L}{2}\},
we obtain

\displaystyle({x},{y})+t(v^{\prime},r^{\prime})\in\mathrm{epi}f\ \textrm{ for all }\ ({x},{y})\in\mathrm{epi}f\setminus\mathbb{B}_{R,I},\ (v^{\prime},r^{\prime})\in\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(v,r),\ t\in(0,\epsilon),

which yields
(v,r)\in{\rm int}T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I})
by Theorem
3.6
.
∎

Example 4.10
.

Let
n:=2
and consider the function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R},(x_{1},x_{2})\mapsto e^{x_{1}}+x_{2}.
A simple calculation shows that

\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(v_{1},v_{2},r)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}\ |\ v_{1}\leq 0,v_{2}\leq r\}.

In view of Proposition
4.9
,
f
is directionally Lipschitz at infinity with respect to
(v_{1},v_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
if and only if
v_{1}<0.

To simplify notation in what follows, let

\displaystyle D^{\uparrow}_{f}(\infty)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid f^{\uparrow}(\infty;v)<+\infty\},

\displaystyle D^{\dagger}_{f}(\infty)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid f^{\dagger}(\infty;v)<+\infty\}.

Corollary 4.11
.

Let
f
be lower semi-continuous. Then for every
v\in\mathrm{int}D^{\uparrow}_{f}(\infty),
the function
f
is directionally Lipschitz at infinity with respect to
v.

Proof.

The set
D^{\uparrow}_{f}(\infty)
is a convex cone as the function
f^{\uparrow}(\infty;\cdot)
is convex and positively homogeneous (by Corollary
4.6
). Take arbitrary
v\in\mathrm{int}D^{\uparrow}_{f}(\infty)
and let
r>f^{\uparrow}(\infty;v).
In light of
[
18
, Theorem 6.8]
,
(v,r)\in\mathrm{int}\,\mathrm{epi}f^{\uparrow}(\infty;\cdot).
This, together with Propositions
4.5
and
4.9
, implies that
f
is directionally Lipschitz at infinity with respect to the vector
v.

∎

One of the situations where a considerable simplification is possible in analyzing subderivatives at infinity is the case where there exists at least one
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
f^{\dagger}(\infty;v)<+\infty,
i.e., when the set
D^{\dagger}_{f}(\infty)
is nonempty.

Proposition 4.12
.

Let
f
be lower semi-continuous such that
D^{\dagger}_{f}(\infty)\neq\emptyset.
Then

\displaystyle D^{\dagger}_{f}(\infty)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm int}D^{\uparrow}_{f}(\infty).

Furthermore,
f^{\uparrow}(\infty;\cdot)
is continuous at each
v\in D^{\dagger}_{f}(\infty)
and agrees there with
f^{\dagger}(\infty;\cdot)
.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.9
, we have

\displaystyle D^{\dagger}_{f}(\infty)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi({\rm int}T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I})).

(Recall that
\pi\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{n},(v,r)\mapsto v,
denotes the projection on the first component.) Hence, in order to prove the first assertion of the theorem, it suffices to establish

(16)

\displaystyle\pi(\mathrm{int}T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm int}\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid f^{\uparrow}(\infty;v)<+\infty\}.

Let
v\in\pi(\mathrm{int}T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I})).
Then
(v,r)\in\mathrm{int}T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I})
for some
r\in\mathbb{R}.
In view of Proposition
4.5
,
(v,r)\in\mathrm{int}\,\mathrm{epi}f^{\uparrow}(\infty;\cdot).
Consequently,
f^{\uparrow}(\infty;w)<+\infty
for all
w
near
v,
and so
v
lies in the right-hand side of (
16
).

Conversely, let
v
be in the right-hand side of (
16
). Then, there are real numbers
\epsilon>0
and
r
such that
f^{\uparrow}(\infty;v^{\prime})<r-1
for all
v^{\prime}\in\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(v).
It follows that
(v^{\prime},r^{\prime})\in{\rm epi}f^{\uparrow}(\infty;\cdot)=T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I})
for all
(v^{\prime},r^{\prime})
close to
(v,r),
equivalently,
(v,r)\in\mathrm{int}T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I}),
and so
v\in\pi({\rm int}T_{{\rm epi}f}(\infty_{I})).
Therefore, the equation (
16
) holds, which proves the first assertion of the theorem.

Since the function
f^{\uparrow}(\infty;\cdot)
is convex, it is continuous on the interior of the set
D^{\uparrow}_{f}(\infty),
provided it is bounded above in a neighborhood of one point (see
[
18
, Theorem 10.1]
); this is the case precisely because
D^{\dagger}_{f}(\infty)\neq\emptyset.
The second assertion of the theorem is proved.

The last assertion of the theorem follows directly from the following expressions:

\displaystyle f^{\uparrow}(\infty;v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm inf}\{r\in\mathbb{R}\mid(v,r)\in T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I})\},

\displaystyle f^{\dagger}(\infty;v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm inf}\{r\in\mathbb{R}\mid(v,r)\in{\rm int}T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I})\},

where
v
belongs to
D^{\dagger}_{f}(\infty).

∎

We next give a formula for the subgradients at infinity of a sum of two functions.

Proposition 4.13

(sum rule)
.

Let
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
be lower semi-continuous such that
D^{\uparrow}_{f_{1}}(\infty)\cap\mathrm{int}D^{\uparrow}_{f_{2}}(\infty)\neq\emptyset.
Then

(17)

\displaystyle(f_{1}+f_{2})^{\uparrow}(\infty;v)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle f_{1}^{\uparrow}(\infty;v)+f_{2}^{\uparrow}(\infty;v)\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad v\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(18)

\displaystyle\partial(f_{1}+f_{2})(\infty)

\displaystyle\subset

\displaystyle\partial f_{1}(\infty)+\partial f_{2}(\infty).

Proof.

For simplicity of notation, we let
f_{0}:=f_{1}+f_{2}
and
\ell_{i}(v):=f_{i}^{\uparrow}(\infty;v)
for
i=0,1,2.

Our assumption implies that
\mathrm{int}D^{\uparrow}_{f_{2}}(\infty)\neq\emptyset.
By Corollary
4.11
, the function
f_{2}
is directionally Lipschitz at infinity. Take any
v\in D^{\uparrow}_{f_{1}}(\infty)\cap{\rm int}D^{\uparrow}_{f_{2}}(\infty)
and let
r>\ell_{2}(v)=f_{2}^{\uparrow}(\infty;v).
In view of Proposition
4.12
,
f_{2}^{\dagger}(\infty;v)=f_{2}^{\uparrow}(\infty;v)<r.
Hence, there exist
R>0
and
\epsilon>0
such that for all
x\in\mathrm{dom}f_{2}\setminus\mathbb{B}_{R}
,
w\in\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(v)
and
t\in(0,\epsilon),
we have

\displaystyle\dfrac{f_{2}(x+tw)-f_{2}(x)}{t}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle r,

and so

\displaystyle\frac{f_{0}(x+tw)-f_{0}(x)}{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{f_{1}(x+tw)-f_{1}(x)}{t}+\frac{f_{2}(x+tw)-f_{2}(x)}{t}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{f_{1}(x+tw)-f_{1}(x)}{t}+r.

Note that
\mathrm{dom}f_{0}\subset\mathrm{dom}f_{1}\cap\mathrm{dom}f_{2}.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\ell_{0}(v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0}\limsup_{x\xrightarrow{\mathrm{dom}f_{0}}\infty,\ t\searrow 0}\inf_{w\in\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(v)}\frac{f_{0}(x+tw)-f_{0}(x)}{t}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0}\limsup_{x\xrightarrow{\mathrm{dom}f_{1}}\infty,\ t\searrow 0}\inf_{w\in\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(v)}\left\{\frac{f_{1}(x+tw)-f_{1}(x)}{t}+r\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0}\limsup_{x\xrightarrow{\mathrm{dom}f_{1}}\infty,\ t\searrow 0}\inf_{w\in\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(v)}\left\{\frac{f_{1}(x+tw)-f_{1}(x)}{t}\right\}+r

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ell_{1}(v)+r.

Since this inequality is true for all
r>\ell_{2}(v)=f_{2}^{\uparrow}(\infty;v),
we derive the inequality (
17
), which we still need to establish for general
v.

If either
\ell_{1}(v)=+\infty
or
\ell_{2}(v)=+\infty
then (
17
) is trivial, so assume that
v
belongs to the set
D^{\uparrow}_{f_{1}}(\infty)\cap D^{\uparrow}_{f_{2}}(\infty).
By assumption, there exists a vector
\overline{v}
in
D^{\uparrow}_{f_{1}}(\infty)\cap{\rm int}D^{\uparrow}_{f_{2}}(\infty).
Since
the sets
D^{\uparrow}_{f_{1}}(\infty)
and
D^{\uparrow}_{f_{2}}(\infty)
are convex,
v_{t}:=(1-t)v+t\overline{v}\in D^{\uparrow}_{f_{1}}(\infty)\cap{\rm int}D^{\uparrow}_{f_{2}}(\infty)
for all
t\in(0,1).
By the case already treated, then

\displaystyle\ell_{0}({v}_{t})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ell_{1}({v}_{t})+\ell_{2}({v}_{t}).

The functions
\ell_{i}
are convex and lower semi-continuous, so
\lim_{t\to 0}\ell_{i}({v}_{t})=\ell_{i}({v})
(see
[
18
, Corollary 7.5.1]
). Therefore, the inequality (
17
) holds.

We now prove the inclusion (
18
). There are two cases to be considered:

Case 1:
\ell_{1}(0)=-\infty
or
\ell_{2}(0)=-\infty

Note that
\ell_{i}(0)=f_{i}^{\uparrow}(\infty;0)\leq 0
(by definition). Hence the inequality (
17
) implies
\ell_{0}(0)=-\infty,
and so
\partial f_{0}(\infty)=\emptyset
by Corollary
4.7
. Then the inclusion (
18
) is trivial.

Case 2:
\ell_{1}(0)\neq-\infty
and
\ell_{2}(0)\neq-\infty

Assume that
\partial f_{0}(\infty)\neq\emptyset
(otherwise the inclusion (
18
) is trivial). Then
\ell_{0}(0)>-\infty
in view of Corollary
4.7
. Moreover, we have

(19)

\displaystyle\partial f_{0}(\infty)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\langle\xi,v\rangle\leq\ell_{0}(v)\ \textrm{ for all }\ v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}

\displaystyle\subset

\displaystyle\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\langle\xi,v\rangle\leq(\ell_{1}+\ell_{2})(v)\ \textrm{ for all }\ v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial(\ell_{1}+\ell_{2})(0),

where the last equality is valid because
\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}
is a convex function satisfying
(\ell_{1}+\ell_{2})(0)=0.
The directionally Lipschitz property at infinity of
f_{2}
implies that the function
f_{2}^{\uparrow}(\infty;\cdot)
is continuous on the interior of the set
D_{f_{2}}^{\uparrow}(\infty)
(see Proposition
4.12
). Our assumption thus provides the existence of a vector
v
such that
\ell_{1}(v)<\infty
and
\ell_{2}
is bounded above on a neighborhood of
v.
In view of
[
18
, Theorem 23.8]
, we get

\displaystyle\partial(\ell_{1}+\ell_{2})(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial\ell_{1}(0)+\partial\ell_{2}(0)\ =\ \partial f_{1}(\infty)+\partial f_{2}(\infty),

where the second equality holds true because
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
are convex functions satisfying
\ell_{1}(0)=\ell_{2}(0)=0.
Now
the desired inclusion (
18
) follows directly from (
19
).
∎

4.4.
Optimality conditions at infinity

We are now in a position to prove the second of our main theorems, which provides necessary optimality conditions at infinity for optimization problems.

Theorem 4.14
.

Let
f
be lower semi-continuous and
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an unbounded closed set.
Assume that one of the following conditions holds:

(i)

D^{\uparrow}_{f}(\infty)\cap\mathrm{int}T_{C}(\infty_{I})\neq\emptyset.

(ii)

\mathrm{int}D^{\uparrow}_{f}(\infty)\cap T_{C}(\infty_{I})\neq\emptyset.

If there exists a sequence
x_{k}\in C
such that
x_{k}\to\infty
and
f(x_{k})\to\inf_{x\in C}f(x)>-\infty,
then the following optimality conditions hold:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle\partial f(\infty)+N_{C}(\infty_{I}),

\displaystyle f^{\uparrow}(\infty;v)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 0\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad v\in T_{C}(\infty_{I}).

Proof.

Recall that
\delta_{C}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
denotes the indicator function of the set
C,
which is
lower semi-continuous (because
C
is closed). By definition, then

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}(f+\delta_{C})(x_{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(f+\delta_{C}\right)(x).

Hence
0\in\partial\left(f+\delta_{C}\right)(\infty)
in light of Proposition
4.4
. Furthermore, we know from Example
4.8
that

D^{\uparrow}_{\delta_{C}}(\infty)=T_{C}(\infty_{I})\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\partial\delta_{C}(\infty)=N_{C}(\infty_{I}).

Therefore, either (i) or (ii) is sufficient for Proposition
4.13
to be applicable and yield the inclusion

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle\partial\left(f+\delta_{C}\right)(\infty)\ \subset\ \partial f(\infty)+\partial\delta_{C}(\infty)\ =\ \partial f(\infty)+N_{C}(\infty_{I}),

which gives the first statement. Moreover, by Corollary
4.7
, we have

\displaystyle(f+\delta_{C})^{\uparrow}(\infty;v)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 0\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

It follows from Proposition
4.13
that

\displaystyle f^{\uparrow}(\infty;v)+\delta_{C}^{\uparrow}(\infty;v)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 0\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Combining this inequality with Example
4.8
, we obtain the second statement.
∎

Example 4.15
.

Let
n:=2,

f(x_{1},x_{2}):=x_{1},
and
C:=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\ |\ x_{1}\geq 0,x_{2}\geq 0,x_{1}x_{2}\geq 1\}.
Clearly
\inf_{(x_{1},x_{2})\in C}f(x_{1},x_{2})=0
and
f
does not attain its infimum on
C.
A direct calculation shows that
\partial f(\infty)=\{(1,0)\}
and
N_{C}(\infty_{I})=(-\infty,0]\times(-\infty,0],
and so the necessary optimality condition at infinity
(0,0)\in\partial f(\infty)+N_{C}(\infty_{I})
holds.

5.
Lipschitz functions at infinity

In this section, we will present some characterizations of the Lipschitz continuity at infinity for lower semi-continuous functions.
So let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a real valued function. As before, we fix
I:=\{1,\ldots,n\}\subset\{1,\ldots,n,n+1\}
and consider the projection
\pi\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{n},(x,y)\mapsto x.

Definition 5.1
.

We say that
f
is
Lipschitz at infinity
if there exist constants
L>0
and
R>0
such that

\displaystyle|f(x)-f(x^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle L\|x-x^{\prime}\|\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathbb{B}_{R}.

The
generalized Clarke derivative of
f
at infinity with respect to

v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined by

\displaystyle f^{0}(\infty;v)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\limsup_{x\to\infty,\ t\searrow 0}\frac{f(x+tv)-f(x)}{t}.

By definition, if
f
is Lipschitz at infinity, then
f^{0}(\infty;v)
is finite for all
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.
Furthermore, we have the following.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
f
be Lipschitz at infinity. Then the epigraph of
f^{0}(\infty;\cdot)
is
T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I});
that is,
(v,r)\in T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I})
if and only if
f^{0}(\infty;v)\leq r.

Proof.

Let
(v,r)\in T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I}).
By the definition of
f^{0}(\infty;v),
there exist sequences
x_{k}\to\infty
and
t_{k}\searrow 0
such that

\displaystyle f^{0}(\infty;v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{f(x_{k}+t_{k}v)-f(x_{k})}{t_{k}}.

By the definition of
T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I}),
there exists a sequence
(v_{k},r_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}
tending to
(v,r)
such that
(x_{k},f(x_{k}))+t_{k}(v_{k},r_{k})\in\mathrm{epi}f
for all
k.
Hence

\displaystyle f(x_{k}+t_{k}v_{k})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle f(x_{k})+t_{k}r_{k}.

Equivalently,

\displaystyle\frac{f(x_{k}+t_{k}v_{k})-f(x_{k})}{t_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle r_{k}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\frac{f(x_{k}+t_{k}v)-f(x_{k})}{t_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{f(x_{k}+t_{k}v_{k})-f(x_{k})}{t_{k}}+L\|v_{k}-v\|\ \leq\ r_{k}+L\|v_{k}-v\|.

Letting
k\to\infty,
we get
f^{0}(\infty;v)\leq r.

Conversely, take any
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\delta\geq 0.
We will show that
(v,f^{0}(\infty;v)+\delta)\in T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I}).

To see this, let
(x_{k},y_{k})
be a sequence in
\mathrm{epi}f
with
x_{k}\to\infty
and let
t_{k}\searrow 0.
Define

\displaystyle r_{k}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\max\left\{f^{0}(\infty;v)+\delta,\frac{f(x_{k}+t_{k}v)-f(x_{k})}{t_{k}}\right\}.

Observe that
r_{k}
tends to
f^{0}(\infty;v)+\delta
because we know that

\displaystyle\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{f(x_{k}+t_{k}v)-f(x_{k})}{t_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle f^{0}(\infty;v).

Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle y_{k}+t_{k}r_{k}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle y_{k}+\big(f(x_{k}+t_{k}v)-f(x_{k})\big)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle f(x_{k})+\big(f(x_{k}+t_{k}v)-f(x_{k})\big)\ =\ f(x_{k}+t_{k}v).

Therefore,
(x_{k},y_{k})+t_{k}(v_{k},r_{k})\in\mathrm{epi}f,
where
v_{k}:=v,
and so
(v,f^{0}(\infty;v)+\delta)\in T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I}).

∎

We denote by
\Omega_{f}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
the set of points where
f
is not differentiable. By Rademacher’s theorem (see, for example,
[
22
]
), if
f
is Lipschitz at infinity, then there exists a real number
R>0
such that the set
\Omega_{f}\setminus\mathbb{B}_{R}
has measure zero in
\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
f
be Lipschitz at infinity. Then

\displaystyle\partial f(\infty)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{co}\{\lim\nabla f(x)\ |\ x\to\infty\textrm{ and }x\not\in\Omega_{f}\}.

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{\lim\nabla f(x)\ |\ x\to\infty\textrm{ and }x\not\in\Omega_{f}\}.

We first show that
\partial f(\infty)\supset\mathrm{co}A.
Since
\partial f(\infty)
is a convex set, it suffices to prove
\partial f(\infty)\supset A.

To see this, take any
\xi\in A.
By definition, there exists a sequence
x_{k}\to\infty
with
x_{k}\not\in\Omega_{f}
such that
\nabla f(x_{k})\to\xi.

We will show that
\xi\in\partial f(\infty).
In view of Proposition
5.2
, it suffices to show that
\langle\xi,v\rangle\leq f^{0}(\infty;v)
for all
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.
To do this, take any
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.
By
[
7
, Propositions 2.1.5 and 2.2.2]
, we know that

\displaystyle\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),v\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle f^{0}(x_{k};v)\ :=\ \limsup_{x^{\prime}\to x_{k},\ t\searrow 0}\frac{f(x^{\prime}+tv)-f(x^{\prime})}{t}.

On the other hand, by definition, there exist
\overline{x}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
with
\|x_{k}-\overline{x}_{k}\|\leq\frac{1}{k}
and
0<t_{k}<\frac{1}{k}
such that

\displaystyle f^{0}(x_{k};v)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{f(\overline{x}_{k}+t_{k}v)-f(\overline{x}_{k})}{t_{k}}+\frac{1}{k}.

Observe that
\overline{x}_{k}\to\infty
and
t_{k}\to 0.
Moreover,

\displaystyle\langle\xi,v\rangle\ =\ \lim_{k\to\infty}\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),v\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\limsup_{k\to\infty}f^{0}(x_{k};v)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\limsup_{k\to\infty}\left[\frac{f(\overline{x}_{k}+t_{k}v)-f(\overline{x}_{k})}{t_{k}}+\frac{1}{k}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\limsup_{x\to\infty,\ t\searrow 0}\frac{f(x+tv)-f(x)}{t}\ =\ f^{0}(\infty;v).

We have proved that
\partial f(\infty)\supset\mathrm{co}A.
Since
\mathrm{co}A
is a compact convex set in
\mathbb{R}^{n},
in view of Proposition
5.2
, in order to complete the proof it suffices to show that for all
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\},
we have

\displaystyle f^{0}(\infty;v)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\limsup\{\langle\nabla f(x),v\rangle\ |\ x\to\infty\textrm{ and }x\not\in\Omega_{f}\}.

To see this, let
r
be the right-hand side and take arbitrary
\epsilon>0.
By definition, there exists a constant
R>0
such that

\displaystyle\langle\nabla f(x),v\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle r+\epsilon\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus(\mathbb{B}_{R}\cup\Omega_{f}).

For each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},
we define
L_{x}:=\{x+tv\ |\ 0<t<\frac{R}{\|v\|}\}.
Since
\Omega_{f}
has measure zero in
\mathbb{R}^{n},
it follows from Fubini’s theorem that for almost
x
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathbb{B}_{R},
the set
L_{x}\cap\Omega_{f}
has measure zero in
L_{x}.
Let
x
be any point in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathbb{B}_{2R}
having this property and let
t
lie in
(0,\frac{R}{\|v\|}),
and then

\displaystyle f(x+tv)-f(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\langle\nabla f(x+sv),v\rangle ds,

because
\nabla f
exists almost everywhere on
L_{x}.
Since we have
\|x+sv\|\geq\|x\|-s\|v\|>R
for
0<s<t,
it follows that

\displaystyle\langle\nabla f(x+sv),v\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle r+\epsilon,

whence

\displaystyle f(x+tv)-f(x)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle t(r+\epsilon).

Since this inequality is true for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathbb{B}_{2R}
except those in a set of measure zero and for all
t
in
(0,\frac{R}{\|v\|})
and since
f
is continuous, it is in fact true for all such
x
and
t.
Therefore,

\displaystyle f^{0}(\infty;v)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle r+\epsilon.

Since
\epsilon>0
is arbitrary, we get
f^{0}(\infty;v)\leq r,
which completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 5.4
.

If
f
is Lipschitz at infinity, then
\partial(-f)(\infty)=-\partial f(\infty)
and

\displaystyle f^{0}(\infty;v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\limsup\{\langle\nabla f(x),v\rangle\ |\ x\to\infty\textrm{ and }x\not\in\Omega_{f}\}.

Example 5.5
.

The function
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},x\mapsto-|x|,
is 1-Lipschitz and so is Lipschitz at infinity. By Proposition
5.3
, we have

\displaystyle\partial(-f)(\infty)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\partial f(\infty)\ =\ \mathrm{co}\{-1,1\}\ =\ [-1,1].

A nonzero vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
perpendicular
to a set
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
at a point
x
in
\mathrm{cl}C
(this is denoted
v\perp C
at
x
) if
v=x^{\prime}-x,
where the point
x^{\prime}
has unique closest point
x
in
\mathrm{cl}C.
Equivalently,
v=x^{\prime}-x,
where there is a closed ball centered at
x^{\prime}
which meets
\mathrm{cl}C
only at
x.
(Note that
x^{\prime}\not\in\mathrm{cl}C
because
v\neq 0
.) With this notation, the subgradients at infinity of the distance function
d_{C}(\cdot)
can be characterized as follows.

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a nonempty set. Then

\displaystyle\partial d_{C}(\infty)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathbb{B}&\textrm{ if }C\textrm{ is bounded},\\
\mathrm{co}\left(\{0\}\cup A\right)&\textrm{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

where

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\left\{v=\lim\dfrac{v_{k}}{\|v_{k}\|}\mid v_{k}=x_{k}^{\prime}-x_{k}\perp C\ {\rm at}\ x_{k},\ x_{k}^{\prime}\to\infty\right\}.

Proof.

In view of
[
7
, Proposition 2.5.4]
, for each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},
if
\nabla d_{C}(x)
exists, then it is either zero or the unit vector
\dfrac{x-c}{\|x-c\|}
, where
c
is the unique closest point in
\mathrm{cl}C
to
x.
It follows from Proposition
5.3
that

\displaystyle\partial d_{C}(\infty)

\displaystyle\subset

\displaystyle\mathrm{co}\left(\{0\}\cup A\right)\ \subset\ \mathbb{B}.

We now prove the desired formula. There are two cases to be considered.

Case 1: The set
C
is bounded.

Since
\partial d_{C}(\infty)
is a convex set and contained in the ball
\mathbb{B},
it suffices to show that
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\subset\partial d_{C}(\infty).
To this end, take any
v\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}.
By the classical Weierstrass theorem, there exists a point
x\in\mathrm{cl}C
such that

\displaystyle\langle v,{x}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\langle v,c\rangle\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad c\in\mathrm{cl}C.

It follows that

\displaystyle d_{C}({x}+\lambda v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|{x}+\lambda v-{x}\|\ =\ \lambda\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad\lambda\geq 0.

Let
0<\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}.
Applying the mean value theorem (see
[
13
, Theorem 1.7]
or
[
7
, Theorem 2.3.7]
) to the function
d_{C}(\cdot)
, we find
u\in(x+\lambda_{1}v,x+\lambda_{2}v)
and
\xi\in\partial d_{C}(u)
such that

\displaystyle d_{C}(x+\lambda_{2}v)-d_{C}(x+\lambda_{1}v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\xi,(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})v\rangle.

Therefore,

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\xi,v\rangle.

Since
d_{C}(\cdot)
is 1-Lipschitz,
\|\xi\|\leq 1.
Consequently,
v=\xi\in\partial d_{C}(u).
Letting
\lambda_{1}\to\infty,
we see that
u\to\infty,
and so
v\in\partial d_{C}(\infty)
due to Corollary
4.3
. Since
v
was arbitrary in
\mathbb{S}^{n-1},
we conclude that
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\subset\partial d_{\Omega}(\infty),
as required.

Case 2: The set
C
is unbounded.

In this case there exists a sequence
c_{k}\in C
tending to infinity. Then
d_{C}(c_{k})=0
for all
k
. By Proposition
4.4
,
0\in\partial d_{C}(\infty).
Since
\partial d_{C}(\infty)\subset\mathrm{co}\left(\{0\}\cup A\right),
as we have seen above, it remains only to verify that
v\in\partial d_{C}(\infty)
for all
v\in A.
To see this, let
v_{k}:=x_{k}^{\prime}-x_{k}\neq 0,
where
x_{k}^{\prime}
has the closest point
x_{k}
in
\mathrm{cl}C
and
x_{k}^{\prime}\to\infty.
Choose a sequence
t_{k}\in(0,1)
such that
t_{k}\|x_{k}^{\prime}-x_{k}\|\to 0
and let
x_{k}^{\prime\prime}:=t_{k}x_{k}+(1-t_{k})x_{k}^{\prime}.
Then
d_{C}(x_{k}^{\prime\prime})=\|x_{k}^{\prime\prime}-x_{k}\|
and
\|x_{k}^{\prime}-x_{k}^{\prime\prime}\| =t_{k}\|x_{k}^{\prime}-x_{k}\|\to 0.
Applying the mean value theorem (see
[
13
, Theorem 1.7]
or
[
7
, Theorem 2.3.7]
) to the function
d_{C}(\cdot)
, we find
u_{k}\in(x_{k}^{\prime},x_{k}^{\prime\prime})
and
\xi_{k}\in\partial d_{C}(u_{k})
such that

\displaystyle d_{C}(x_{k}^{\prime})-d_{C}(x_{k}^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\xi_{k},x_{k}^{\prime}-x_{k}^{\prime\prime}\rangle.

As
d_{C}(x_{k}^{\prime})-d_{C}(x_{k}^{\prime\prime}) =\|x_{k}^{\prime}-x_{k}^{\prime\prime}\|,
we get

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle\xi_{k},\dfrac{x_{k}^{\prime}-x_{k}^{\prime\prime}}{\|x_{k}^{\prime}-x_{k}^{\prime\prime}\|} \right\rangle\ =\ \left\langle \xi_{k},\dfrac{v_{k}}{\|v_{k}\|}\right\rangle.

Since
d_{C}(\cdot)
is 1-Lipschitz,
\|\xi_{k}\|\leq 1.
Therefore,
\xi_{k}=\dfrac{v_{k}}{\|v_{k}\|} \in\partial d_{C}(u_{k}).
Noting that
u_{k}\to\infty
(since
x_{k}^{\prime}\to\infty
and
\|x_{k}^{\prime}-x_{k}^{\prime\prime}\|\to 0
), we deduce from Corollary
4.3
that

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim\dfrac{v_{k}}{\|v_{k}\|}\ \in\ \partial d_{C}(\infty),

which completes the proof.
∎

Remark 5.7
.

The proof of the above proposition also shows that if
C
is unbounded, then a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
belongs to
\partial d_{C}(\infty)
if and only if there exist
sequences
x_{k}\in\mathrm{cl}C
and
x_{k}^{\prime}\not\in\mathrm{cl}C
with
d_{C}(x_{k}^{\prime})=\|x_{k}^{\prime}-x_{k}\|
such that
x_{k}^{\prime}\to\infty
and

\dfrac{x_{k}^{\prime}-x_{k}}{\|x_{k}^{\prime}-x_{k}\|}\to v.

The third of our main theorems provides conditions guaranteeing that a lower semi-continuous function is Lipschitz at infinity.

Theorem 5.8
.

Let
f
be lower semi-continuous. The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

f
is Lipschitz at infinity.

(ii)

\partial f(\infty)
is nonempty compact.

(iii)

f^{\uparrow}(\infty;v)
is finite for all
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(iv)

f^{\dagger}(\infty;v)
is finite for all
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(v)

f^{0}(\infty;v)
is finite for all
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(vi)

\partial^{\infty}f(\infty)=\{0\}
, where
\partial^{\infty}f(\infty):=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ |\ (\xi,0)\in N_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I})\}.

Proof.

We will prove the implications (i)
\Rightarrow
(v)
\Rightarrow
(vi)
\Rightarrow
(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii)
\Rightarrow
(iv)
\Rightarrow
(i).

(i)
\Rightarrow
(v). This follows directly from the definition of
f^{0}(\infty;\cdot)
.

(v)
\Rightarrow
(vi). Take any
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.
We have
f^{\uparrow}(\infty;v)\leq f^{0}(\infty;v)<+\infty,
and so
(v,f^{0}(\infty;v))\in T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I})
in view of Proposition
4.5
. Consequently,
\pi(T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I}))=\mathbb{R}^{n}.

We now let
\xi\in\partial^{\infty}f(\infty),
that is
(\xi,0)\in N_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I}).
By definition, then

\displaystyle\langle\xi,v\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 0\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad v\in\pi(T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I}))=\mathbb{R}^{n},

which yields
\xi=0.
Therefore,
\partial^{\infty}f(\infty)=\{0\}.

(vi)
\Rightarrow
(ii). We first claim that
N_{{\rm epi}f}(\infty_{I})
contains a non-zero vector.

Indeed, if the interior of
T_{{\rm epi}f}(\infty_{I})
is empty, then there exists a vector
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
which does not lie in
T_{{\rm epi}f}(\infty_{I}).
Applying the separating hyperplane theorem (see, for example,
[
18
]
) for
w
and the convex cone
T_{{\rm epi}f}(\infty_{I}),
we get a non-zero vector
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
such that

\displaystyle\langle z,w^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 0\ \leq\ \langle z,w\rangle\quad\textrm{for all }\quad w^{\prime}\in T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I}).

This implies
z\in N_{{\rm epi}f}(\infty_{I}).

We now assume that the interior of
T_{{\rm epi}f}(\infty_{I})
is nonempty. Let
x_{k}
be a sequence tending to infinity. Since
(x_{k},f(x_{k}))
belongs to the boundary of
\mathrm{epi}f,
it follows from
[
7
, Corollary 2, p. 67]
that
N_{{\rm epi}f}((x_{k},f(x_{k}))
contains a unit vector, say,
z_{k}.
Passing to a subsequence if necessary, we may assume that the sequence
z_{k}
converges to a unit vector
z.
Note that
\mathrm{epi}f
is closed because
f
is lower semi-continuous. Hence, by Corollary
3.7
,
z\in N_{{\rm epi}f}(\infty_{I}).

We have proved that
N_{{\rm epi}f}(\infty_{I})
contains a non-zero vector. Therefore,

\partial f(\infty)\cup(\partial^{\infty}f(\infty)\setminus\{0\})\neq\emptyset.

By assumption, hence
\partial f(\infty)
is non-empty.

Finally, we show the boundedness of
\partial f(\infty).
By contradiction, suppose that there is a sequence
\xi_{k}\in\partial f(\infty)
tending to infinity. By definition,
(\xi_{k},-1)\in N_{{\rm epi}f}(\infty_{I})
, and hence

\dfrac{1}{\|\xi_{k}\|}(\xi_{k},-1)\in N_{{\rm epi}f}(\infty_{I}).

Passing to a subsequence if necessary, we may assume that
\dfrac{\xi_{k}}{\|\xi_{k}\|}\to\xi.
Then
\xi\neq 0
and
(\xi,0)\in N_{{\rm epi}f}(\infty_{I}),
which contradicts the assumption that
\partial^{\infty}f(\infty)=\{0\}.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii). This follows directly from Corollary
4.7
.

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(iv). The function
f^{\uparrow}(\infty;\cdot)
is convex and finite on
\mathbb{R}^{n},
so it must be continuous (see
[
18
, Corollary 10.1.1]
). Hence, every point
(v,r)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}
with
f^{\uparrow}(\infty;v)<r
belongs to
\mathrm{epi}f^{\uparrow}(\infty;\cdot)
and hence belongs also to
T_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty_{I})
in view of Proposition
4.5
. This, together with Propositions
4.9
and
4.12
, gives
f^{\dagger}(\infty;v)=f^{\uparrow}(\infty;v)
for all
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(iv)
\Rightarrow
(i). We first show that there are constants
L>0,

R>0,
and
\delta>0
such that for all
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathbb{B}_{R}
with
\|x-x^{\prime}\|<\delta,

\displaystyle|f(x)-f(x^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle L\|x-x^{\prime}\|.

Indeed, if this is not true, then we can find sequences
x_{k},x_{k}^{\prime}
tending to infinity such that
t_{k}:=\|x_{k}-x_{k}^{\prime}\|
tends to zero and that

\displaystyle[f(x_{k})-f(x_{k}^{\prime})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|f(x_{k})-f(x_{k}^{\prime})|\ >\ k\|x_{k}-x_{k}^{\prime}\|.

Passing to subsequences if necessary, we may assume that
v_{k}:=\frac{x_{k}-x_{k}^{\prime}}{\|x_{k}-x_{k}^{\prime}\|}
converges to a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.
Note that
t_{k}>0
and

\displaystyle\frac{[f(x_{k}^{\prime}+t_{k}v_{k})-f(x_{k}^{\prime})]}{t_{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{[f(x_{k})-f(x_{k}^{\prime})]}{t_{k}}\ >\ k,

which yields
f^{\dagger}(\infty;v)=+\infty,
a contradiction.

There is a
C^{\infty}
-function
\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,1]
such that

\varphi(x)=\begin{cases}1&\textrm{ if }\ \|x\|\geq 3R,\\
0&\textrm{ if }\ \|x\|\leq 2R.\end{cases}

Define the function
\overline{f}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
by
\overline{f}(x):=f(x)\varphi(x).
Then
\overline{f}\equiv f
on

\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathbb{B}_{3R}.
Furthermore, it is not hard to see that
\overline{f}
is locally Lipschitz and so it is globally Lipschitz on the compact set
\mathbb{B}_{3R}.
Increasing
L
if necessary, we may assume that
\overline{f}
is locally Lipschitz with constant
L.

By the property of the Clarke subdifferential, we have
\|\xi\|\leq L
for all
\xi\in\partial\overline{f}(x)
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

We now show that
f
is Lipschitz with constant
L
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathbb{B}_{3R}.

To see this, take any
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathbb{B}_{3R}.
By the mean value theorem (see
[
13
, Theorem 1.7]
or
[
7
, Theorem 2.3.7]
), we find a point
u\in(x,x^{\prime})
and a vector
\xi\in\partial\overline{f}(u)
such that
\overline{f}(x)-\overline{f}(x^{\prime})=\langle\xi,x-x^{\prime}\rangle.
Hence

\displaystyle|f(x)-f(x^{\prime})|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\overline{f}(x)-\overline{f}(x^{\prime})|\ =\ |\langle\xi,x-x^{\prime}\rangle|\ \leq\ L\|x-x^{\prime}\|,

which yields (i).
∎

The following examples show that smooth functions are not necessarily Lipschitz at infinity and that the set of subgradients of
f
at infinity may not be a singleton set when
f
is smooth and Lipschitz at infinity.

Example 5.9
.

(i) The function
f(x):=e^{x}
is of class
C^{\infty}
on
\mathbb{R}.
We have
\partial f(\infty)=[0,+\infty),
which is unbounded. By Theorem
5.8
,
f
is not Lipschitz at infinity. (We can also check directly that there is no constant
L>0
so that the inequality

|e^{k}-e^{k-1}|\leq L|k-(k-1)|

holds for all sufficient large
k.)

(ii) Let
n:=1
and consider the continuously differentiable function

f(x):=\begin{cases}x&\textrm{ if }x\geq 1,\\
\frac{1}{2}x^{2}+\frac{1}{2}&\textrm{ if }-1\leq x\leq 1,\\
-x&\textrm{ if }x\leq-1.\end{cases}

Clearly,
f
is Lipschitz at infinity. In view of Proposition
5.3
,
\partial f(\infty)=[-1,1],
which is not a singleton set.
