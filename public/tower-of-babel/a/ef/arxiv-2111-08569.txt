Isotropic vectors over global fields
Przemysław Koprowski

Abstract
. An isotropic vector of a given quadratic form is a nonzero vector where the form vanishes. Geometrically, it is a vector that is self-orthogonal with respect to this form. On the other hand, from the arithmetical point of view, an isotropic vector forms a solution to a multivariate quadratic equation. The problem of constructing isotropic vectors is one of the leading forces in the computational theory of quadratic forms. In this paper, we present algorithms for finding isotropic vectors of quadratic forms (of any dimension) over an arbitrary global field of characteristic distinct from 2 2 .

1.
Introduction

The notion of isotropy is fundamental for the algebraic theory of quadratic forms. Given a quadratic form
q
, a nonzero vector
v
is called
isotropic
when
q(v)=0
. The problem of finding an isotropic vector of a quadratic form has quite a long history with several ramifications. The first effective method (in fact, an algorithm, although it was not called as such when it was invented) is a descent method for ternary quadratic forms over the rationals. It dates back to the 18th century. The reader can find its description in an explicit algorithmic form in, e.g.,
[
39
, Chapter IV]
, where it is attributed to Lagrange, or in
[
12
, Algorithm I]
, where it is attributed to Legendre. For a long time, it was the standard method for finding isotropic vectors of ternary quadratic forms over
\mathbb{Q}
. The advent of computational algebra in the second half of the last century brought the problem back to the spotlight and stipulated the development of new, more efficient algorithms. It is well known (and recalled in Section
4.1
) that finding a solution of a ternary form is equivalent to solving a norm equation in a quadratic field extension. The latter problem can be considered for arbitrary (i.e., not only quadratic) field extensions and has been a subject of separate studies. We shall refer the reader to
[
11
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
27
,
37
]
for some known results. In particular, it should be pointed out that Simon’s solution based on
S
-units (see
[
37
]
) served as an original inspiration to our algorithm for finding isotropic vectors of quaternary quadratic forms (see Algorithm
1
).

The main disadvantage of the Lagrange/Legendre method is that it requires multiple factorizations of integers. While for low-dimensional forms (i.e., ternary and quaternary forms), it is necessary to factor the discriminant of the form, no other factorizations are necessary. In 1998, Cochrane and Mitchel
[
9
]
and in 2003, Cremona and Rusin
[
12
]
proposed algorithms for ternary quadratic forms over
\mathbb{Q}
that need no other factorization except the factorization of the discriminant. For forms over
\mathbb{Q}
that are not diagonal, Simon
[
38
]
devised an algorithm that finds an isotropic vector without first diagonalizing the form and also using only the factorization of the discriminant. For forms over the rationals of dimensions greater than
4
, it is possible altogether to avoid
any
factorization of integers as it was shown by Castel
[
8
]
. The key idea of Castel’s solution is first to find an indefinite subform of dimension
5
and then extend it to a
6
-dimensional isotropic form of a prescribed discriminant.

Far less is known for forms over fields different from
\mathbb{Q}
. Schicho
[
35
,
36
]
generalized the descent method to quadratic forms over the field
\mathbb{R}(t)
of rational functions over the reals. The algorithm was further generalized to an arbitrary rational function field
K(t)
of characteristic distinct from
2
by van Hoeij and Cremona in
[
41
]
. A different algorithm that works with quadratic forms over
\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
for odd
q
was given by Ivanyos et al. in
[
18
]
. Recently, Kutas et al.
[
23
]
presented a method for finding an isotropic vector of a quaternary quadratic form over rational function fields in characteristic
2
. Finally, we should also mention that it is possible to generalize the descent method to ternary quadratic forms over number fields. A corresponding algorithm is implicit in
[
34
]
and was implemented in Magma (see
[
7
, §127.5]
) by Steve Donelly. To the best of our knowledge, it has never been published in an explicit algorithmic form in any research paper.

In general, the methods listed above that work with forms over the rationals heavily depend on the idiosyncrasies of the arithmetic of the (euclidean) ring of integers. Consequently, they do not directly generalize to number fields. Here, the problem of constructing an isotropic vector is equivalent to solving a degree
2
homogeneous multivariate Diophantine equation over a ring of algebraic integers, where the arithmetic of such ring can be quite… weird. That is probably the reason why the problem of finding isotropic vectors of general quadratic forms over number fields has—to the best of our knowledge—remained mostly unsolved. The sole purpose of the present paper is to remedy this situation to a certain degree. We present explicit algorithms that construct isotopic vectors for quadratic forms over arbitrary global fields of characteristic distinct from
2
. The algorithms described in this paper have been implemented in Magma
[
5
]
and can be downloaded from the author’s homepage at
http://www.pkoprowski.eu/cqf
. In Appendix
B
, we report how they perform in practice.

2.
Notation

Throughout this paper, we use the following notation and terminology. Since the theory of quadratic forms in characteristic
2
is very different from all other characteristics, we exclude the case of characteristic
2
from all our considerations. For this reason, in the rest of the paper, the term “global field” actually means “global field of characteristic distinct from
2
”. By
K
, we will denote our base field, which is either a number field or an algebraic function field over some finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, where
q
is a power of an odd prime. If
L/K
is a finite field extension, then
N_{L/K}:L\to K
is the associated norm. A diagonal quadratic form
a_{1}x_{1}^{2}+\dotsb+a_{n}x_{n}^{2}
is denoted
\langle a_{1},\dotsc,a_{n}\rangle
. Since the problem of constructing isotropic vectors of degenerate forms is trivial and so totally uninteresting, all forms are always assumed to be non-degenerate, meaning that every
a_{i}
is nonzero.

A
place
of
K
is an equivalence class of valuations. We denote the places using fraktur letters
\mathfrak{p}
,
\mathfrak{q}
,
\mathfrak{r}
. A place of a number field can be either Archimedean or non-archimedean. Over global function fields, all places are non-archimedean. We write
K_{\mathfrak{p}}
for the completion of
K
at
\mathfrak{p}
and
K(\mathfrak{p})
for its residue field. In addition, if
\mathfrak{p}
is non-archimedean, we write
\ord_{\mathfrak{p}}:K^{\times}\to\mathbb{Z}
for the associated normalized discrete valuation. It is well known that instead of valuations, one can alternatively use
norms
. Given a place
\mathfrak{p}
of
K
, we will write
|\cdot|_{\mathfrak{p}}:K\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
for the associated norm. In fact, in this paper, we will use norms as more convenient objects exclusively in the case of real places. If
K
is a number field,
\mathfrak{r}
its real place, and
\sigma_{\mathfrak{r}}:K\rightarrowtail\mathbb{R}
the associated real embedding, then for any
a\in K
its norm
|a|_{\mathfrak{r}}
is nothing else but the standard absolute value
|\sigma_{\mathfrak{r}}(a)|
of the image of
a
under
\sigma_{\mathfrak{r}}
.

The non-archimedean places correspond to prime ideals in a maximal order of
K
. For this reason, we will use the terms “non-archimedean places” and “primes” interchangeably. We will sometimes write “finite primes” to emphasize the fact that they are non-archimedean. A prime
\mathfrak{p}
of a number field is called
dyadic
if
\ord_{\mathfrak{p}}2\neq 0
. Otherwise, it is called
non-dyadic
. In global function fields, all primes are non-dyadic. If
\mathfrak{p}
is a finite prime and
a,b\in K^{\times}
, then
(a,b)_{\mathfrak{p}}
denotes the
Hilbert symbol
of
a
and
b
at
\mathfrak{p}
. If further
q=\langle a_{1},\dotsc,a_{n}\rangle
is a quadratic form, then following
[
25
]
we define the
Hasse invariant
of
q
at
\mathfrak{p}
by the formula

s_{\mathfrak{p}}(q):=\prod_{i<j}(a_{i},a_{j})_{\mathfrak{p}}.

Recall that a finite set
S
of places of a global field is called a
Hasse set
if it contains all Archimedean places. In particular, every finite set of places of a global function field is a Hasse set. With a quadratic form
q=\langle a_{1},\dotsc,a_{n}\rangle
we associate a Hasse set, denoted
\mathfrak{P}(q)
, consisting of:

•

all Archimedean places and all dyadic primes (provided that
K
is a number field),

•

all these primes
\mathfrak{p}
such that
\ord_{\mathfrak{p}}a_{i}
is odd for at least one
i\leq n
.

Let
S
be a Hasse set, an element
a\in K^{\times}
is said to be
S
-singular
if
\ord_{\mathfrak{p}}a
is even for every prime
\mathfrak{p}
, not in
S
. It is called an
S
-unit
if
\ord_{\mathfrak{p}}a=0
for every
\mathfrak{p}\notin S
. The group of all
S
-singular elements is denoted
E_{S}
, and the group of all
S
-units by
U_{S}
. Observe that
K^{\times 2}
is a subgroup of
E_{S}
, hence we may consider the quotient group
\mathbb{E}_{S}:=\nicefrac{{E_{S}}}{{K^{\times 2}}}
of
S
-singular square classes. It is a subgroup of the square class group
\nicefrac{{K^{\times}}}{{K^{\times 2}}}
of
K
. Moreover, we will denote
\mathbb{U}_{S}:=\nicefrac{{U_{S}}}{{U_{S}\cap K^{\times 2}}}
the group of
S
-units modulo squares. It can be treated as a subgroup of
\mathbb{E}_{S}
. Observe that
\mathbb{E}_{S}
is an elementary
2
-group. Hence, it is an
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-linear space.

Algorithmic prerequisites

The algorithms presented in this paper depend on the number of already existing tools. First of all, we need routines for solving systems of linear equations (basically over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
) and for basic arithmetic in a global field (see, e.g.,
[
10
]
). As far as more advanced tools are concerned, we need:

(1)

Factorization of ideals in global fields (see, e.g.,
[
10
,
17
]
).

(2)

Solution to a norm equation of a form
N_{L/K}\left(x\right)=b
, where
b\in K^{\times}
and
L/K
is a finite field extension. As was mentioned in the introduction, this problem is discussed in
[
11
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
27
,
37
]
.

(3)

Construction of a basis (over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
) of the group
\mathbb{E}_{S}
of
S
-singular square classes. The author is aware of three existing methods. One is described in
[
21
]
. Another one is outlined in the Magma manual
[
7
]
. In this method, one constructs a set
T
containing
S
, such that the
T
-class number is odd. Then, it is known that the groups
\mathbb{E}_{T}
and
\mathbb{U}_{T}
coincide. Hence, the sought group
\mathbb{E}_{S}
can be constructed as a subspace of
\mathbb{U}_{T}
. A third method, due to A. Czogała, was recently described in
[
22
]
.

4.
Algorithms

In this section, we present the main results of the paper, namely the algorithms for constructing an isotropic vector of a given quadratic form. Our general strategy is as follows. For forms of dimensions
2
or
3
, the corresponding vector is constructed directly. On the other hand, for higher dimensional forms, we will reduce the problem to the one for forms of lower dimension. Hence, we will recursively push it down the ladder till we reach ternary forms. Unfortunately, the strategy differs significantly from one dimension to the other. Consequently, we present dedicated algorithms for forms of dimensions
4
and
5
and a general algorithm that deals with forms of dimension
6
and above.

4.1.
Dimension 3 and below

First, let us deal with forms of dimensions
3
and below. Unary forms cannot be isotropic, and so we ignore them. The construction of an isotropic vector of a binary form is trivial and boils down to computing the square root of its discriminant. As explained earlier, for ternary forms, the problem is equivalent to the well-studied problem of solving the norm equation. Indeed, let
q=\langle a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\rangle
be an isotropic ternary form and
v=(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})\in K^{3}
be its isotropic vector, we are looking for. Without loss of generality, we may assume that all three binary subforms
\langle a_{i},a_{j}\rangle
, with
1\leq i<j\leq 3
are anisotropic. In particular, this means that
-\nicefrac{{a_{2}}}{{a_{1}}}
is not a square in
K
and that
v_{3}
is non-zero. We have
a_{1}v_{1}^{2}+a_{2}v_{2}^{2}+a_{3}v_{3}^{2}=0
. Rearranging the terms we write

-\frac{a_{3}}{a_{1}}=\left(\frac{v_{1}}{v_{3}}\right)^{2}+\frac{a_{2}}{a_{1}}\cdot\left(\frac{v_{2}}{v_{3}}\right)^{2}.

Now, the right-hand side is the norm of an element

\xi=\frac{v_{1}}{v_{3}}+\frac{v_{2}}{v_{3}}\cdot\sqrt{-\frac{a_{2}}{a_{1}}}

in the quadratic field extension
K\bigl(\sqrt{-\nicefrac{{a_{2}}}{{a_{1}}}}\bigr)/K
. Therefore, as said, the problem of constructing an isotropic vector of a ternary form is equivalent to the one of solving the norm equation.

4.2.
Dimension 4

We shall now deal with quaternary forms. This case requires some preparations. Fix an isotropic quaternary quadratic form
q=\langle a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4}\rangle
over
K
. Without loss of generality we can assume that no
3
-dimensional subform
\langle a_{i},a_{j},a_{k}\rangle
of
q
(for some
1\leq i\leq j\leq l\leq 4
) is isotropic. Split the form into two subforms
q_{1}:=\langle a_{1},a_{2}\rangle
and
q_{2}:=\langle a_{3},a_{4}\rangle
. Let us begin with the following elementary observation determining our strategy for constructing an isotropic vector.

Observation 4.1
.

The form
q
is isotropic if and only if there is an element
b\in K^{\times}
such that the forms
\tilde{q}_{1}:=\langle-b\rangle\perp q_{1}
and
\tilde{q}_{2}:=\langle b\rangle\perp q_{2}
are both isotropic. Moreover, if
u=(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})
is an isotropic vector for
\tilde{q}_{1}
and
w=(w_{1},w_{2},w_{3})
is an isotropic vector for
\tilde{q}_{2}
, then
v:=(\nicefrac{{u_{2}}}{{u_{1}}},\nicefrac{{u_{3}}}{{u_{1}}},\nicefrac{{w_{2}}}{{w_{1}}},\nicefrac{{w_{3}}}{{w_{1}}})
is an isotropic vector for
q
.

Consequently, our goal is to find a method for constructing an element
b
as above. We shall find it among representatives of
S
-singular square classes for a certain Hasse set
S
. Let
S_{\infty}=\{\mathfrak{r}_{1},\dotsc,\mathfrak{r}_{r}\}
be the set (possibly empty) of all these real places
\mathfrak{r}
of
K
where either
\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}}a_{1}=\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}}a_{2}
(hence
q_{1}
is anisotropic at
\mathfrak{r}
) or
\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}}a_{3}=\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}}a_{4}
(hence
q_{2}
is anisotropic at
\mathfrak{r}
).

Lemma 4.2
.

For every
\mathfrak{r}\in S_{\infty}
the forms
\tilde{q}_{1}
and
\tilde{q}_{2}
are isotropic at
\mathfrak{r}
if and only if

(
\spadesuit
)

\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}}b=\begin{cases}\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}}a_{1},&\text{if $q_{1}\otimes K_{\mathfrak{r}}$ is anisotropic,}\\
-\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}}a_{3},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proof.

First, suppose that
q_{1}
is anisotropic at
\mathfrak{r}
. For
\tilde{q}_{1}\otimes K_{\mathfrak{r}}
to be isotropic, we must have
\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}}(-b)\neq\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}}a_{1}
. On the other hand, if
q_{1}\otimes K_{\mathfrak{r}}
is isotropic, it is
q_{2}\otimes K_{\mathfrak{r}}
that is anisotropic. Now, for
\tilde{q}_{2}\otimes K_{\mathfrak{r}}
to be isotropic we must have
\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}}b\neq\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}}a_{3}
.

Conversely, suppose that condition (

4.2
) holds. If
q_{1}\otimes K_{\mathfrak{r}}
is anisotropic, then it is clear that
\langle-b\rangle\perp q_{1}
is isotropic at
\mathfrak{r}
. We must show that
\tilde{q}_{2}\otimes K_{\mathfrak{r}}
is also isotropic. If already the form
q_{2}\otimes K_{\mathfrak{r}}
is isotropic, then so is
\tilde{q}_{2}\otimes K_{\mathfrak{r}}
. Hence, suppose that
q_{2}\otimes K_{\mathfrak{r}}
y is anisotropic. The form
q
is isotropic by assumption. In particular, it is isotropic at
\mathfrak{r}
. Therefore, all four coefficients cannot have the same sign. It follows that

\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}}b=\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}}a_{1}=\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}}a_{2}\neq\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}}a_{3}=\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}}a_{4}

and so
\tilde{q}_{2}\otimes K_{\mathfrak{r}}
is isotropic as expected. When
q_{1}\otimes K_{\mathfrak{r}}
is isotropic, we need only to show that
\tilde{q}_{2}
is isotropic at
\mathfrak{r}
. This fact, however, follows immediately from the second part of condition (

The next lemma is an analog of Lemma
4.2
but for finite primes.

Lemma 4.3
.

For every finite prime
\mathfrak{p}
:

(1)

The form
\tilde{q}_{1}
is locally isotropic at
\mathfrak{p}
if and only if
(a_{1},a_{2})_{\mathfrak{p}}=(-a_{1}a_{2},b)_{\mathfrak{p}}
.

(2)

The form
\tilde{q}_{2}
is locally isotropic at
\mathfrak{p}
if and only if
(-a_{3},-a_{4})_{\mathfrak{p}}=(-a_{3}a_{4},b)_{\mathfrak{p}}
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove just one of the two assertions. The other one is fully analogous. Now,
[
25
, Proposition V.3.22]
asserts that a ternary form
\tilde{q}_{1}
is isotropic over a local field
K_{\mathfrak{p}}
if and only if its Hasse invariant equals
(-1,-\det\tilde{q}_{1})_{\mathfrak{p}}
. Therefore, the isotropy of
\tilde{q}_{1}\otimes K_{\mathfrak{p}}
is equivalent to the condition

s_{\mathfrak{p}}(\tilde{q}_{1})=(-b,a_{1})_{\mathfrak{p}}(-b,a_{2})_{\mathfrak{p}}(a_{1},a_{2})_{\mathfrak{p}}=(-1,a_{1}a_{2}b)_{\mathfrak{p}}.

The Hilbert symbol is symmetric and multiplicative with respect to each of its arguments. Thus, the above condition can be rewritten in the form
(a_{1},a_{2})_{\mathfrak{p}}=(-a_{1}a_{2},b)_{\mathfrak{p}}
.
∎

It follows from the Local-Global Principle (see, e.g.,
[
25

) that
\tilde{q}_{1}
and
\tilde{q}_{2}
are isotropic (over
K
) if and only if the conditions of Lemmas

and
4.3
hold for all places of
K
. Now, for complex places, real places not in
S_{\infty}
and finite primes that do not divide any of the elements
a_{1},\dotsc,a_{4},b
, these two conditions are trivially satisfied. Thus, we have only finitely many places to deal with. Fix a Hasse set
S
containing
\mathfrak{P}(q)
and such that
b
is
S
-singular. Let
\mathscr{B}=\{\kappa_{1},\dotsc,\kappa_{k}\}

. Write the square class of

\xi_{1},\dotsc,\xi_{k}\in\{0,1\}

(-1)^{u_{i}}=\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}_{i}}a_{1}

Analogously, for every finite prime
\mathfrak{p}_{i}\in S

.

Use the notation introduced above. The coordinates

We have
b=\kappa_{1}^{\xi_{1}}\dotsm\kappa_{k}^{\xi_{k}}\cdot c^{2}

\sum_{j\leq k}\beta_{ij}\xi_{j}=v_{i}.

Analogously, we obtain the last equation:

\sum_{j\leq k}\gamma_{ij}\xi_{j}=w_{i}.

Writing all three equations in a matrix form for all possible indices

b\cdot K_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\times 2}
and
c\cdot K_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\times 2}
coincide for every place
\mathfrak{p}\in S
. We claim that
c
is represented over
K
by the forms
\langle a_{1},a_{2}\rangle
and
\langle-a_{3},-a_{4}\rangle
.

Denote
\hat{q}_{1}:=\langle-c,a_{1},a_{2}\rangle
and
\hat{q}_{2}:=\langle c,a_{3},a_{4}\rangle
. Evidently, the forms
\hat{q}_{1}
and
\hat{q}_{2}
are locally isotropic at all complex places of
K
. Fix any place
\mathfrak{p}\in S
, be it Archimedean or non-archimedean. We know that the forms
\langle-b,a_{1},a_{2}\rangle
and
\langle b,a_{3},a_{4}\rangle
are isotropic, so are their localizations
\langle-b,a_{1},a_{2}\rangle\otimes K_{\mathfrak{p}}
and
\langle b,a_{3},a_{4}\rangle\otimes K_{\mathfrak{p}}
. Now, the local square classes
b\cdot K_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\times 2}
and
c\cdot K_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\times 2}
are equal. Hence,

\langle-b,a_{1},a_{2}\rangle\otimes K_{\mathfrak{p}}\cong\hat{q}_{1}\otimes K_{\mathfrak{p}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\langle b,a_{3},a_{3}\rangle\otimes K_{\mathfrak{p}}\cong\hat{q}_{2}\otimes K_{\mathfrak{p}}.

This means that
\hat{q}_{1}\otimes K_{\mathfrak{p}}
and
\hat{q}_{2}\otimes K_{\mathfrak{p}}
are both isotropic, as desired.

Next, take a finite prime
\mathfrak{p}
not in
S
but distinct from
\mathfrak{q}
. In particular,
\mathfrak{p}
is non-dyadic since all the dyadic primes are in
\mathfrak{P}(q)\subseteq S
. Then all the elements
a_{1},\dots,a_{4}
and
c
have even valuations at
\mathfrak{p}
, consequently the forms
\hat{q}_{1}\otimes K_{\mathfrak{p}}
and
\hat{q}_{2}\otimes K_{\mathfrak{p}}
are isotropic by
[
25
, Corollary VI.2.5]
.

Finally, consider the singled-out prime
\mathfrak{q}
. By the previous paragraph and
[
25
, Proposition V.3.22]
for every prime
\mathfrak{p}\neq\mathfrak{q}
the Hasse invariant
s_{\mathfrak{p}}(\hat{q}_{1})
equals the Hilbert symbol
(-1,-\det\hat{q}_{1})_{\mathfrak{p}}
. Thus, we have

1=s_{\mathfrak{p}}(\hat{q}_{1})\cdot(-1-\det\hat{q}_{1})_{\mathfrak{p}}=(c,-a_{1}a_{2})_{\mathfrak{p}}\cdot(a_{1},a_{2})_{\mathfrak{p}}.

Using Hilbert reciprocity law (see e.g.,
[
32
, Chapter VII]
) we can write

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=(c,-a_{1}a_{2})_{\mathfrak{q}}\cdot(a_{1},a_{2})_{\mathfrak{q}}\cdot\prod_{\mathfrak{p}\neq\mathfrak{q}}(c,-a_{1}a_{2})_{\mathfrak{p}}\cdot\prod_{\mathfrak{p}\neq\mathfrak{q}}(a_{1},a_{2})_{\mathfrak{p}}

\displaystyle=(c,-a_{1}a_{2})_{\mathfrak{q}}\cdot(a_{1},a_{2})_{\mathfrak{q}}

\displaystyle=s_{\mathfrak{q}}(\hat{q}_{1})\cdot(-1,-\det\hat{q}_{1})_{\mathfrak{q}}.

Hence, again by
[
25
, Proposition V.3.22]
we obtain that
\hat{q}_{1}\otimes K_{\mathfrak{q}}
is isotopic. A similar argument shows that
\hat{q}_{2}\otimes K_{\mathfrak{q}}
is isotropic, as well. We have shown that the forms
\hat{q}_{1}
and
\hat{q}_{2}
are isotropic at every completion of
K
. Therefore, by the Local-Global Principle, they are isotropic over
K
, as well. This proves the claim. It now follows from the previous lemma that system (

♣

‣
4.4
) is solvable in
\mathbb{E}_{S\cup\{\mathfrak{q}\}}
.
∎

We are now ready to present the main algorithm of this paper.

Algorithm 1
.

Given an isotropic quadratic form
q=\langle a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4}\rangle
of dimension
4
over a global field
K
, this algorithm outputs a vector
v\in K^{4}
such that
q(v)=0
.

(1)

If there are indices
1\leq i<j<k\leq 4
such that
\langle a_{i},a_{j},a_{k}\rangle
is isotropic, then construct its isotropic vector
v\in K^{3}
. Output
v
with zero inserted at the omitted spot and quit.

(2)

Initialize
S:=\mathfrak{P}(q)
, and denote
q_{1}:=\langle a_{1},a_{2}\rangle
and
q_{2}:=\langle a_{3},a_{4}\rangle
.

(3)

Let
S_{\infty}:=\{\mathfrak{r}_{1},\dotsc,\mathfrak{r}_{r}\}\subset S
be the set of all the real places of
K
at which at least one of the forms
q_{1}
,
q_{2}
is anisotropic:

S_{\infty}:=\bigl\{\mathfrak{r}\mathrel{\mid}\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}}a_{1}=\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}}a_{2}\text{ or }\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}}a_{3}=\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}}a_{4}\bigr\}.

(4)

Let
u=(u_{i})
be the
(
column
)
vector defined by the formula

(-1)^{u_{i}}=\begin{cases}\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}_{i}}a_{1}&\text{if $q_{1}\otimes K_{{\mathfrak{r}_{i}}}$ is anisotropic}\\
-\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}_{i}}a_{3}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(5)

Repeat the following steps:

(a)

Construct a basis
\mathscr{B}=\{\kappa_{1},\dotsc,\kappa_{k}\}
of
\mathbb{E}_{S}
.

(b)

Construct a
(
column
)
vector
v=(v_{i})
determined by the condition

(-1)^{v_{i}}=(a_{1},a_{2})_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}.

(c)

Construct a
(
column
)
vector
w=(w_{i})
given by the formula

(-1)^{w_{i}}=(-a_{3},-a_{4})_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}.

(d)

Construct a matrix
A=(\alpha_{ij})
defined by the condition

(-1)^{\alpha_{ij}}=\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}_{i}}\kappa_{j}.

(e)

Construct a matrix
B=(\beta_{ij})
determined by the formula

(-1)^{\beta_{ij}}=(-a_{1}a_{2},\kappa_{j})_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}.

(f)

Construct a matrix
C=(\gamma_{ij})
given by the condition

(-1)^{\gamma_{ij}}=(-a_{3}a_{4},\kappa_{j})_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}.

(g)

Form system (

♣

‣
4.4
), by concatenating matrices
A
,
B
,
C
and vectors
u
,
v
,
w
. Check if it is solvable.

(h)

If it is, denote a solution by
(\xi_{1},\dotsc,\xi_{k})
and exit the loop.

(i)

Otherwise, if system (

♣

‣
4.4
) has no solution, find a new prime
\mathfrak{q}\notin S
. Append it to
S
and reiterate the loop.

(6)

Set

b:=\kappa_{1}^{\xi_{1}}\dotsm\kappa_{k}^{\xi_{k}},\qquad\hat{q}_{1}:=\langle-b,a_{1},a_{2}\rangle,\qquad\hat{q}_{2}:=\langle b,a_{3},a_{4}\rangle.

(7)

Find isotropic vectors of ternary forms
\hat{q}_{1}
and
\hat{q}_{2}
. Denote them
u
and
w
, respectively.

(8)

Output
v:=(\nicefrac{{u_{2}}}{{u_{1}}},\nicefrac{{u_{3}}}{{u_{1}}},\nicefrac{{w_{2}}}{{w_{1}}},\nicefrac{{w_{3}}}{{w_{1}}})
.

Remark 1
.

If one uses an incremental algorithm for constructing a basis of a group
\mathbb{E}_{S}
of
S
-singular square classes (as described, e.g., in
[
21
]
), then it is not necessary to compute the basis
\mathscr{B}
in step (
5a
) from scratch every time. Instead, one may just update the basis computed in the previous iteration of the loop.

Remark 2
.

In the proof of Proposition
4.5
, we used
[
28
, Lemma 2.1]
to prove the existence of a point
\mathfrak{q}
such that system (

♣

‣
4.4
) is solvable in
\mathbb{E}_{S\cup\{\mathfrak{q}\}}
. The proof of this lemma relies on the Chebotarev Density Theorem to show that the set of such points is not empty. In particular, this set have a positive density. It means that the corresponding primes
\mathfrak{q}
are (in a certain sense) relatively common.

Remark 3
.

In order to rigorously prove that after finitely many iterations, we will come across a prime
\mathfrak{q}
satisfying assertions of Proposition
4.5
, we should exhaustively iterate over all the primes. If
K
is a number field, we may, for example, start with a prime number
p=3
and loop over all the primes of
K
dividing
p
. Then take
p=5
and again loop over all the primes of
K
over
p
, and so on. A similar exhaustive search can be applied to global function fields, as well. In practice, a much more efficient solution is just to pick
\mathfrak{q}
at random, using the fact that the set of such primes has a positive density (see the previous remark). Thus, depending on the selected strategy for finding primes in step (
5i
) the above algorithm can be viewed either as a deterministic or a randomized one.

We comment on the complexity of Algorithm
1
in Appendix
A
.

4.3.
Dimension 5 and above

We shall now deal with forms of dimensions
5
and higher. Such forms are necessarily locally isotropic at all finite primes. The general strategy we will use is to reduce the dimension of the form step-by-step till we drop it to
4
, where we may use Algorithm
1
. Essentially we will use a variant of the
2
-linkage of quadratic forms (see, e.g.,
[
40
, Chapter 8]
) Recall that for any nonzero
a,b,s,t\in K^{\times}
such that
a+b\neq 0
we have the following isometries of binary quadratic forms (see, e.g.,
[
40
, Corollary 6.4.1]
):

\langle a,b\rangle\cong\langle as^{2},bt^{2}\rangle\qquad\text{and}\qquad\langle a,b\rangle\cong\langle a+b,(a+b)\cdot ab\rangle.

The next observation explicitly relates the isotropic vectors of these forms.

Observation 4.6
.

Let
a,b,s,t\in K^{\times}
and assume that
a+b
and
as^{2}+bt^{2}
are nonzero.

•

(x,y)
is an isotropic vector for
\langle as^{2},bt^{2}\rangle
if and only if
(sx,ty)
is an isotropic vector for
\langle a,b\rangle
.

•

(x,y)
is an isotropic vector for
\langle a+b,(a+b)\cdot ab\rangle
if and only
(x-by,x+ay)
is an isotropic vector for
\langle a,b\rangle
.

•

(x,y)
is an isotropic vector for
\langle as^{2}+bt^{2},(as^{2}+bt^{2})\cdot abs^{2}t^{2}\rangle
if and only if
(sx-bst^{2}y,tx-as^{2}ty)
is an isotropic vector for
\langle a,b\rangle
.

The first assertion is trivial. The second one follows by a direct calculation. The last one is an immediate consequence of the previous two. For convenience, we present one more observation that will be subsequently used in Algorithms
2
and
4
.

Observation 4.7
.

Let
a,b\in K^{\times}
be two nonzero elements and
\mathfrak{r}
be a real place of
K
. Set

s:=\bigl\lceil|\nicefrac{{b}}{{a}}|_{\mathfrak{r}}\bigr\rceil+1.

Then
\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}}(as^{2}+b)=\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}}a
.

First, we shall focus on forms of dimension
5
. Let
q=\langle a_{1},\dotsc,a_{5}\rangle
be an isotropic form. Without loss of generality, we can assume that no
4
-dimensional subform of
q
obtained by omitting one
a_{i}
is isotropic. Split
q
into two subforms (see also Remark
5
below):

q_{1}:=\langle a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\rangle\qquad\text{and}\qquad q_{2}:=\langle a_{4},a_{5}\rangle.

Our goal is to find an element
c\in K^{\times}
represented by
q_{2}
and such that
q_{1}\perp\langle c\rangle
is isotropic. To this end, we will use Observation
4.6
together with the Weak Approximation Theorem. The key ingredient is the following proposition.

Proposition 4.8
.

For every prime
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{P}(q_{1})
let
s_{\mathfrak{p}},t_{\mathfrak{p}}\in K^{\times}
be such that the form
\langle a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4}s_{\mathfrak{p}}^{2}+a_{5}t_{\mathfrak{p}}^{2}\rangle
is locally isotropic at
\mathfrak{p}
. Denote the set of all these real places of
K
where
q_{2}
is anisotropic by
S_{\infty}
. Assume that for every
\mathfrak{r}\in S_{\infty}
we have
s_{\mathfrak{r}},t_{\mathfrak{r}}\in K^{\times}
such that
\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}}(a_{4}s_{\mathfrak{r}}^{2}+a_{5}t_{\mathfrak{r}}^{2})\neq\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}}a_{3}
. Let
s,t\in K^{\times}
be elements of
K
satisfying the following set of conditions:

(1)

s\equiv s_{\mathfrak{p}}\pmod{\mathfrak{p}^{v}}
and
t\equiv t_{\mathfrak{p}}\pmod{\mathfrak{p}^{v}}
for all
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{P}(q_{1})
, where
v=1+\ord_{\mathfrak{p}}(4a_{4}s_{\mathfrak{p}}^{2}+4a_{5}t_{\mathfrak{p}}^{2})
,

(2)

|s-s_{\mathfrak{r}}|_{\mathfrak{r}},|t-t_{\mathfrak{r}}|_{\mathfrak{r}}<\varepsilon_{\mathfrak{r}}
for all
\mathfrak{r}\in S_{\infty}
, where

\varepsilon_{\mathfrak{r}}:=\min\Bigl\{\frac{1}{2},\frac{|a_{4}s_{\mathfrak{r}}^{2}+a_{5}t_{\mathfrak{r}}^{2}|_{\mathfrak{r}}}{2\cdot|a_{4}|_{\mathfrak{r}}\cdot(1+2|s_{\mathfrak{r}}|_{\mathfrak{r}})},\frac{|a_{4}s_{\mathfrak{r}}^{2}+a_{5}t_{\mathfrak{r}}^{2}|_{\mathfrak{r}}}{2\cdot|a_{5}|_{\mathfrak{r}}\cdot(1+2|t_{\mathfrak{r}}|_{\mathfrak{r}})}\Bigr\}.

Then the form
\langle a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4}s^{2}+a_{5}t^{2}\rangle
is isotropic.

Proof.

Denote
c:=a_{4}s^{2}+a_{5}t^{2}
and
q_{*}:=q_{1}\perp\langle c\rangle
. We shall check that
q_{*}
is locally isotropic everywhere. It is trivially isotropic at all the complex places of
K
. Further, if
\mathfrak{r}
is a real place not in
S_{\infty}
, then already
q_{1}\otimes K_{\mathfrak{r}}
is isotropic and more so
q_{*}\otimes K_{\mathfrak{r}}
. Now, fix a place
\mathfrak{r}\in S_{\infty}
. We claim that the signs (with respect to
\mathfrak{r}
) of
c
and
c_{\mathfrak{r}}:=a_{4}s_{\mathfrak{r}}^{2}+a_{5}t_{\mathfrak{r}}^{2}
coincide. Indeed, we have

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle=a_{4}\cdot(s-s_{\mathfrak{r}}+s_{\mathfrak{r}})^{2}+a_{5}\cdot(t-t_{\mathfrak{r}}+t_{\mathfrak{r}})^{2}

\displaystyle=c_{\mathfrak{r}}+a_{4}\cdot(s-s_{\mathfrak{r}})^{2}+a_{5}\cdot(t-t_{\mathfrak{r}})^{2}+2a_{4}\cdot(s-s_{\mathfrak{r}})\cdot s_{\mathfrak{r}}+2a_{5}\cdot(t-t_{\mathfrak{r}})\cdot t_{\mathfrak{r}}.

Observe that
|s-s_{\mathfrak{r}}|_{\mathfrak{r}}^{2}<|s-s_{\mathfrak{r}}|_{\mathfrak{r}}<\varepsilon_{\mathfrak{r}}
, since
\varepsilon_{\mathfrak{r}}<1
. Therefore, we have

|a_{4}\cdot(s-s_{\mathfrak{r}})^{2}+2a_{4}\cdot(s-s_{\mathfrak{r}})\cdot s_{\mathfrak{r}}|_{\mathfrak{r}}<|a_{4}|_{\mathfrak{r}}\cdot\varepsilon_{\mathfrak{r}}+2|a_{4}|_{\mathfrak{r}}\cdot\varepsilon_{\mathfrak{r}}\cdot|s_{\mathfrak{r}}|_{\mathfrak{r}}\\
\leq|a_{4}|_{\mathfrak{r}}\cdot\frac{|c_{\mathfrak{r}}|_{\mathfrak{r}}}{2|a_{4}|_{\mathfrak{r}}\cdot(1+2|s_{\mathfrak{r}}|_{\mathfrak{r}})}\cdot(1+2|s_{\mathfrak{r}}|_{\mathfrak{r}})=\frac{|c_{\mathfrak{r}}|_{\mathfrak{r}}}{2}.

Analogously, we show that

|a_{5}\cdot(t-t_{\mathfrak{r}})^{2}+2a_{5}\cdot(t-t_{\mathfrak{r}})\cdot t_{\mathfrak{r}}|_{\mathfrak{r}}<\frac{|c_{\mathfrak{r}}|_{\mathfrak{r}}}{2}.

It follows that
|c-c_{\mathfrak{r}}|_{\mathfrak{r}}<|c_{\mathfrak{r}}|_{\mathfrak{r}}
, which implies that
c
and
c_{\mathfrak{r}}
have equal signs, proving our claim. Now, the sign of
c_{\mathfrak{r}}
differs from that of
a_{3}
. Consequently
\bigl(q_{1}\perp\langle c_{\mathfrak{r}}\rangle\bigr)\otimes K_{\mathfrak{r}}
is isotropic and so is
\bigl(q_{1}\perp\langle c\rangle\bigr)\otimes K_{\mathfrak{r}}
.

We shall now turn our attention to non-archimedean places. If
\mathfrak{p}
is a finite prime not in
\mathfrak{P}(q_{1})
, then already
q_{1}\otimes K_{\mathfrak{p}}
is isotropic being a
3
-dimensional form (see, e.g.,
[
25
, Corollary VI.2.5]
). Hence,
\bigl(q_{1}\perp\langle c\rangle\bigr)\otimes K_{\mathfrak{p}}
is also isotropic. Thus, the only primes we must consider are those in
\mathfrak{P}(q_{1})
. Fix some
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{P}(q_{1})
and denote
c_{\mathfrak{p}}:=a_{4}s_{\mathfrak{p}}^{2}+a_{5}t_{\mathfrak{p}}^{2}
. From the congruences
s\equiv s_{\mathfrak{p}}\pmod{\mathfrak{p}^{v}}
and
t\equiv t_{\mathfrak{p}}\pmod{\mathfrak{p}^{v}}
we infer that

c=a_{4}s^{2}+a_{5}t^{2}\equiv c_{\mathfrak{p}}\pmod{\mathfrak{p}^{v}}.

It follows from the Local Square Theorem (see, e.g.,
[
25
, Theorem VI.2.19]
) that the local square classes
cK_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\times 2}
and
c_{\mathfrak{p}}K_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\times 2}
coincide. Therefore, the forms
\bigl(q_{1}\perp\langle c\rangle\bigr)\otimes K_{\mathfrak{p}}
and
\bigl(q_{1}\perp\langle c_{\mathfrak{p}}\rangle\bigr)\otimes K_{\mathfrak{p}}
are isomorphic. In particular,
q_{1}\perp\langle c\rangle
is locally isotropic at
\mathfrak{p}
, as desired.

Now, it follows from the Strong Hasse Principle (see, e.g.,
[
25
, Theorem VI.3.1]
) that
q_{1}\perp\langle c\rangle
is isotropic over
K
.
∎

The existence of the elements

and

in the previous proposition follows from the Weak Approximation Theorem. We can now write an explicit algorithm for finding isotropic vectors of

-dimensional forms.

Proof of correctness.

The correctness of the output of step (

and

in step (

) can be found. Thus, the form

is isotropic by Proposition

. Observation

shows that the vector

∎

It is possible to find elements
s_{\mathfrak{p}},t_{\mathfrak{p}}\in K^{\times}
in step (
5
) of the above algorithm in a purely deterministic way. In Appendix
C
, we present one possible method to do it. However, a randomized approach is much easier to implement, and it offers a superior performance in practical tests. Unfortunately, a rigorous probabilistic analysis seems to be very difficult.

The general idea is completely straightforward. Just keep picking elements of
K^{\times}
at random until revealing a pair
(s_{\mathfrak{p}},t_{\mathfrak{p}})
such that
(q_{1}\perp\langle a_{4}s_{\mathfrak{p}}^{2}+a_{5}t_{\mathfrak{p}}^{2}\rangle)\otimes K_{\mathfrak{p}}
is isotropic. If
q_{1}\otimes K_{\mathfrak{p}}
is anisotropic, then by
[
25
, Corollary VI.2.15]
it represents all local square-classes in
\nicefrac{{K_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\times}}}{{K_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\times 2}}}
except
-a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}\cdot K_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\times 2}
. The group
\nicefrac{{K_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\times}}}{{K_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\times 2}}}
consists of
2^{n}=4\cdot\bigl|K(\mathfrak{p})\bigr|^{\ord_{\mathfrak{p}}2}
elements (see, e.g.,
[
25
, Theorem VI.2.22]
). In particular, if
\mathfrak{p}
is non-dyadic we have
|\nicefrac{{K_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\times}}}{{K_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\times 2}}}|=4
. Therefore, if the function that maps a pair
(s,t)\in K^{\times}\times K^{\times}
to the local square-class
(as^{2}+bt^{2})\cdot K_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\times 2}
had a uniform distribution (for some random generator of
s
and
t
), the probability of failure would be
2^{-n}
. Unfortunately, it is not always the case. The reason is two-fold. For one, the form
\langle a,b\rangle
does not have to represent all the local square classes. It is possible to construct examples where it is the probability of
success
that is as low as
2^{-n}
(it will still be nonzero since
q
is isotropic by assumption) provided that all local square classes are represented by
s
and
t
with the same probability. However, here comes the second obstacle, the above assumption of equidistribution of local square classes is hard for a random generator to fulfill. Just to give the reader a glimpse of the problem, let us look at probably the simplest example when
K=\mathbb{Q}
and
\mathfrak{p}
is the place associated with some odd prime number
p\in\mathbb{Z}
. The group
\nicefrac{{\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{\times}}}{{\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{\times 2}}}
consists of four square classes represented by
1
,
u
,
p
and
up
, where
(\frac{u}{p})=-1
. Fix some
N>0
and a rational number
s=\nicefrac{{m}}{{n}}
, where
m
and
n
are picked at random from the set
\{1,\dotsc,N\}
with uniform distribution (for simplicity we ignore the sign here). Then, the probability that
s
has an odd
p
-adic valuation is bounded from above by
2P_{1}\cdot(1-P_{1})
, with an equality holding for
N
divisible by
p
, where

P_{1}=\frac{1}{p+1}\cdot\biggl(1-\Bigl(-\frac{1}{p}\Bigr)^{k}\biggr)\qquad\text{and}\qquad k=\lfloor\log_{p}N\rfloor.

Hence, in general, the two square classes of odd valuation (i.e.,
p\cdot\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{\times 2}
and
up\cdot\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{\times 2}
) appear far less frequently than the two others. This phenomenon is even more noticeable in general number fields. A construction of a random generator of elements of a global field uniformly distributed over local square classes exceeds the scope of this paper. A quick and dirty solution that works reasonably well in practice is to multiply with probability
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
elements of the field by a pre-computed uniformizer of the place in question.

It is known that algorithms relying on weak approximation can easily suffer from undesirable expression swell. This phenomenon is observed even if the approximation is limited just to finite primes (see, e.g.,
[
11
, §1.3.3]
). It is even more pronounced when we simultaneously approximate with respect to Archimedean and non-archimedean places as we do in Algorithm
2
. This fact stipulates the use of randomized algorithms. This approach is used in Algorithm
3
presented below. The idea is to try to find elements
s
and
t
at random instead of stitching them from local pieces using weak approximation.

Algorithm 3
.

Given an isotropic quadratic form
q=\langle a_{1},\dotsc,a_{5}\rangle
of dimension
5
over a global field
K
, and the maximal allowable number of iterations
N
, this algorithm outputs a vector
v\in K^{5}
such that
q(v)=0
or reports a failure.

(1)

If the
4
-dimensional form
q_{i}
, obtained from
q
by omitting
a_{i}
for some
i\leq 5
, is isotropic, then execute Algorithm
1
to construct its isotropic vector
w
. Expand
w
to a
5
-dimensional vector
v
, by inserting
0
at
i
-th coordinate. Output
v
and quit.

(2)

Denote
q_{1}:=\langle a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\rangle
.

(3)

For
j\in\{1,\dotsc,N\}
do:

(a)

Pick two random elements
s,t\in K^{\times 2}
.

(b)

Denote
q_{*}:=q_{1}\perp\langle a_{4}s^{2}+a_{5}t^{2}\rangle
.

(c)

If
q_{*}
is degenerate
(
i.e.
a_{4}s^{2}+a_{5}t^{2}=0
)
or anisotropic, then reiterate the loop.

(d)

Using Algorithm
1
find an isotropic vector
w=(w_{1},w_{2},w_{3},w_{4})
for
q_{*}

(e)

Output
v:=(w_{1},w_{2},w_{3},sw_{4},tw_{5})
and quit.

(4)

Report a failure.

The correctness of the above algorithm is evident. The average number of tries (hence the average running time) seems very hard to estimate for the reasons highlighted earlier. As a partial remedy in Appendix
B
, we report the running times of (an implementation of) the algorithms presented in this paper.

We can finally turn our attention to forms of dimension
6
and higher. Algorithm
4
presented below is very similar to Algorithm
2
. The only difference is that now, we may completely ignore finite primes. The form
q_{*}
constructed by the algorithm has dimension
d-1\geq 5
. Therefore, it is trivially isotropic at all the non-archimedean completions of
K
. In particular, the next algorithm is relevant only for formally real number fields. For non-real fields (either number fields or global function fields), it suffices to take any
5
-dimensional subform of
q
. It will automatically be isotropic.

Algorithm 4
.

Let
q=\langle a_{1},\dotsc,a_{d}\rangle
be an isotropic form of dimension
d\geq 6
over a global field
K
. This algorithm outputs a vector
v\in K^{d}
such that
q(v)=0
.

(1)

If the
(d-1)
-dimensional form
q_{i}
, obtained from
q
by omitting
a_{i}
for some index
i\leq d
, is isotropic, then construct its isotropic vector
w
. Expand
w
to a
d
-dimensional vector
v
, by inserting
0
at
i
-th coordinate. Output
v
and quit.

(2)

Denote
q_{1}:=\langle a_{1},\dotsc,a_{d-2}\rangle
.

(3)

Let
S_{\infty}
the set of all the real places
\mathfrak{r}
of
K
, at which
q_{1}
is locally anisotropic
(
i.e.,
a_{1},\dotsc,a_{d-2}
have the same sign in the completion
K_{\mathfrak{r}}
)
.

(4)

For every
\mathfrak{r}\in S_{\infty}
:

(a)

If
\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}}a_{d-2}\neq\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}}a_{d-1}
, then find
(
see Observation
4.7

)
a positive integer
s_{\mathfrak{r}}
such that
\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}}(a_{d-1}s_{\mathfrak{r}}^{2}+a_{d})=\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}}a_{d-1}
and set
t_{\mathfrak{r}}:=1
.

(b)

Otherwise, set
s_{\mathfrak{r}}:=1
and find a positive integer
t_{\mathfrak{r}}
satisfying the condition
\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}}(a_{d-1}+a_{d}t_{\mathfrak{r}}^{2})=\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}}a_{d}
.

(c)

Set

\varepsilon_{\mathfrak{r}}:=\min\Bigl\{\frac{1}{2},\frac{|a_{d-1}s_{\mathfrak{r}}^{2}+a_{d}t_{\mathfrak{r}}^{2}|_{\mathfrak{r}}}{2\cdot|a_{d-1}|_{\mathfrak{r}}\cdot(1+2|s_{\mathfrak{r}}|_{\mathfrak{r}})},\frac{|a_{d-1}s_{\mathfrak{r}}^{2}+a_{d}t_{\mathfrak{r}}^{2}|_{\mathfrak{r}}}{2\cdot|a_{d}|_{\mathfrak{r}}\cdot(1+2|t_{\mathfrak{r}}|_{\mathfrak{r}})}\Bigr\}.

(5)

Using weak approximation find some
s,t\in K^{\times}
such that

|s-s_{\mathfrak{r}}|_{\mathfrak{r}}<\varepsilon_{\mathfrak{r}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad|t-t_{\mathfrak{r}}|_{\mathfrak{r}}<\varepsilon_{\mathfrak{r}}

for all real places
\mathfrak{r}
of

.

(6)

Set
q_{*}:=\langle a_{1},\dotsc,a_{d-2},a_{d-1}s^{2}+a_{d}t^{2}\rangle
. Find an isotropic vector
w=(w_{1},\dotsc,w_{d-1})
of
q_{*}
.

(7)

Output

.

Remark 4
.

Observe that the elements

and

constructed in step (

) of Algorithm

and step (

) of Algorithm

are nonzero, since the corresponding

’s and

’s are nonzero integers and
|s-s_{\mathfrak{r}}|_{\mathfrak{r}},|t-t_{\mathfrak{r}}|_{\mathfrak{r}}<\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
. This fact guarantees that the form

Remark 5
.

Algorithms
2
–
4
rely on finding an element

represented by a binary subform of

and such that

is isotropic. All three algorithms construct the binary form from the last two coefficients of

. The only reason for such an arbitrary partitioning of the list of coefficients is the clarity of presentation. Obviously, we can take any binary subform
\langle a_{i},a_{j}\rangle
of

is then given by the remaining

Appendix A
Complexity of Algorithm
1

A detailed complexity analysis of algorithms presented in this paper seems to be very difficult, and definitely exceeds the competence of the author. One of the reasons is that to the best of our knowledge not all of the external subroutines, we rely on, offer such a rigorous complexity analysis. In this appendix we provide a crude estimate for the time complexity of Algorithm
1
.

Let
K
be a global field represented either in the form
K=\mathbb{Q}(\theta)
, if
K
is a number field, or
K=\mathbb{F}_{p^{k}}(x,\theta)
, if it is a function field. Denote the monic minimal polynomial of
\theta
by
f
and let
d
be the degree of
f
. An element of
K
can be expressed as a linear combination of successive powers of
\theta
, say
a=a_{0}+a_{1}\theta+\dotsb+a_{d-1}\theta^{d-1}
. Define the size of
a
to be the sum of bit-sizes of
a_{0},\dotsc,a_{d-1}
. For a quadratic form
q
over
K
, define its size as a sum of sizes of its coefficients.

In order to construct the set
\mathfrak{P}(q)
in step (
2
) of the algorithm, one has to factor the principal ideals generated by
K
-integral representatives of square classes of coefficients of
q
. If
K
is a number field, this problem can be reduced in polynomial time to the problem of factoring integers (see, e.g.,
[
11
, §2.3.5]
). The best known algorithms for the latter task are sub-exponential in size of the input. GNFS (see, e.g.,
[
6
]
,
[
26
]
) has complexity
L_{n}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}},(\nicefrac{{64}}{{9}})^{1/3})
, while ECM (see, e.g.,
[
29
]
,
[
19
]
) has complexity
L_{p}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},\sqrt{2})
, where
p
is the smallest prime dividing the integer to be factored. Now, the computation of a norm increases the size of the input by the factor
|K:\mathbb{Q}|
and so the complexity of step (
2
) for number fields is sub-exponential in the size of the form and the degree of the field.

If
K
is a global function field, a finite extension of
\mathbb{F}_{p^{k}}(x)
, then the problem of factoring the ideals can be analogously reduced to factoring polynomials over a finite field. The later problem is well-known to be polynomial-time in the size of the input (see, e.g.,
[
43
, Section 14.8]
) Hence, for function fields step (
2
) has polynomial time in size of the input and the degree
d
.

Now, steps (
3
–
4
) are meaningful only for formally real fields (hence, necessarily number fields). They boil down to performing a real root isolation of polynomials with rational coefficients. This can be done in polynomial time with respect to the degree
|K:\mathbb{Q}|
and the logarithm of the root distance. For details we refer the reader to
[
1
, Section 10.2]
(see also
[
2
, Section 3]
).

As expected, it is the analysis of the loop in step (
5
) which is most involving. The construction in step (
5a
) of the basis
\mathscr{B}
from scratch during the first execution of the loop can be reduced in polynomial time (with respect to the size of
S
) to the problem of computing the
S
-unit group. For a general number field, the author is not aware of any algorithm for a latter task that would offer a complexity better than exponential in the size of
S
and the bit-size of the discriminant of
K
. Related sub-exponential algorithms (see, e.g.,
[
3
]
) rely on some heuristics. On the other hand, as explained in Remark
1
, in subsequent iterations of the loop, after expanding
S
, the basis
\mathscr{B}
can be updated incrementally in time polynomial with respect to the size of
S
and the class number of
K
. The corresponding algorithm for function fields is described in
[
21
]
. It is likely that one can use a similar incremental algorithm also for number fields. However, to the best of our knowledge no such algorithm has ever been published.

Computations of Hilbert symbols in steps (
5b
,
5c
) and (
5e
,
5f
) can be done using Voight’s algorithm (see
[
42
]
) which works in the polynomial time. The previous comments on the complexity of steps (
3
–
4
) apply also to step (
5d
). All the remaining steps of the loop use only linear algebra over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, and so they run in the polynomial time with respect to the size of
S\cup S_{\infty}
.

It remains to estimate the needed number of iterations of loop (
5
). Denote by
\mathfrak{Q}
the set of all these primes
\mathfrak{q}
for which system (

♣

‣
4.4
) has a solution in
\mathbb{E}_{\mathfrak{P}(q)\cup\{\mathfrak{q}\}}
. As explained in Remark
2
after Algorithm
1
, the set
\mathfrak{Q}
has a positive density. In fact, if one repeats all the steps of the proof of
[
28
, Lemma 2.1]
, keeping track of the index of the used congruence subgroup, one can actually show that this density is bounded from below by

\prod_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{P}(q)\cup\{\mathfrak{p}_{0}\}}\hskip-5.69054pt(N\mathfrak{p})^{-1-\ord_{\mathfrak{p}}4},

where
\mathfrak{p}_{0}
is some fixed (arbitrarily chosen) prime not in
\mathfrak{P}(q)
.

As mentioned in Remark
3
, Algorithm
1
can be viewed either as a randomized or a deterministic one. In the former case, the average number of iterations of loop (
5
) is bounded by the inverse of the above product. Consequently, it is polynomial in the degree of the field and the size of coefficients. Summarizing, if Algorithm
1
is treated as a randomized one, then loop (
5
) has an exponential time but only due to the first execution of step (
5a
). If the first construction of the basis
\mathscr{B}
is moved to the initialization phase before the loop (which would simplify the complexity analysis, but obscure the presentation of the algorithm), then step (
5
) would execute in a randomized polynomial time.

Alternatively, we may treat Algorithm
1
as a deterministic one, exhaustively iterating over the primes of
K
. Although it may be tempting to base our analysis in this setting on an effective version of the Chebotarev Density Theorem (see, e.g.,
[
33
]
and the citations wherein), it is not an optimal strategy. The reason is that we are not interested in the number of primes in
\mathfrak{Q}
, whose norms sit in a given interval. What we need is to estimate how quickly we will find the first prime (with respect to the order in which we scan them) sitting in
\mathfrak{Q}
. This is sometimes referred to as the “first prime problem”. It is known to work in polynomial time with respect to the discriminant of
K
by
[
24
]
(see also
[
20
]
and citations within). Consequently, step (
5
) runs in the deterministic polynomial time with respect to the the degree of
K
, its discriminant and size of the coefficients, provided that the first construction of the basis
\mathscr{B}
is moved to the initialization phase (see the comments of the end of the previous paragraph).

Finally, let us look at step (
7
). Here, we need to find isotropic vectors of two ternary forms. As explained in Section
4.1
, this problem is equivalent to solving two norm equations. Algorithms devoted to this task has been recalled in the introduction. For number fields, Simon’s algorithm
[
37
]
seems to be the most popular one. (It can be also adapted to function fields.) The author is not aware of any complete rigorous complexity analysis of Simon’s algorithm. It is claimed (without proof) in
[
4
]
that over cyclotomic fields a solution of the norm equation can be computed in probabilistic polynomial time with respect to the bit-size of the input, provided that the factorization of the input is known. The latter condition can be safely assumed in our case since the set
S
is given and we are interested only in solutions modulo squares. Yet still, this claim refers only to cyclotomic fields. Conversely, in
[
31
]
there is a complete complexity analysis of a stripped down version of Simon’s algorithm, showing that the computation time is polynomial with respect to the size of the representation for the system of fundamental units of the unit group of the upper field, which in turn is exponential with respect to the bit-size of the coefficients (and also the degree of the extension, but in our case it is always
2
). On the other hand, for global function fields, Leem, Jacobson and Scheidler (see
[
27
]
) presented recently a new algorithm (in fact two new algorithms) for solving norm equations. Their algorithm works in polynomial time with respect to the size of the underlying finite field and the bit-size of coefficients, and in sub-exponential time with respect to the genus of the field. (It is also exponential with respect to the degree of the field extension but again it is always
2
in our case.)

Appendix C
Deterministic construction of elements
s
and
t

As explained in the comments following Algorithm
2
, an efficient and recommended method to construct elements
s_{\mathfrak{p}},t_{\mathfrak{p}}\in K^{\times}
needed in step (
5
) of Algorithm
2
is by a random search. Nonetheless, in the author’s opinion, it is in good taste to show that we are not limited to stochastic arguments but can construct such elements also using a purely deterministic method. Below, we will treat dyadic and non-dyadic primes uniformly here. However, if one is interested exclusively in the non-dyadic case (e.g., if one works over global function fields), then there are some possible optimizations that are not covered here. We begin with an auxiliary algorithm.

Algorithm 5
.

Let
\mathfrak{p}
be a prime of a number field
K
. Given three nonzero elements
a
,
b
and
d
of
K
of valuations zero, this algorithm outputs
s,t\in K^{\times}
such that
as^{2}+bt^{2}\not\equiv d\pmod{K_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\times 2}}
.

(1)

If
a\cdot d
is not a local square at
\mathfrak{p}
, then:

(a)

Find a uniformizer
\pi
of
\mathfrak{p}
.

(b)

Output
s:=1
,
t:=2\pi
and quit.

(2)

If
b\cdot d
is not a local square at
\mathfrak{p}
, then

(a)

Find a uniformizer
\pi
of
\mathfrak{p}
.

(b)

Output
s:=2\pi
,
t:=1
and quit.

(3)

If all three elements
a
,
b
and
d
belong to the same local-square class, then:

(a)

Find
u
such that
a=bu^{2}
.

(b)

Find
s_{*},t_{*}\in K^{\times}
such that
s_{*}^{2}+t_{*}^{2}
is not a square in
K_{\mathfrak{p}}

(
use, e.g.,
[
13
]
)
.

(c)

Output
s:=s_{*}
and
t:=ut_{*}
.

Proof of correctness.

First, suppose that
a\cdot d\notin K_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\times 2}
. Thus,

as^{2}+bt^{2}=a+b\cdot 4\pi^{2}\equiv a\pmod{\mathfrak{p}^{1+\ord_{\mathfrak{p}}4}}.

It follows from the Local Square Theorem that
as^{2}+bt^{2}
sits in the local square class of
a
, which differs from the local square class of
d
since
a\cdot d
is not a local square. It proves the correctness of step (
1
). The correctness of step (
2
) is completely analogous. Finally, consider the case when
a
,
b
, and
d
belong to the same local square class. Since
s_{*}^{2}+t_{*}^{2}
is not a square, we have

as^{2}+bt^{2}=a\cdot(s_{*}^{2}+t_{*}^{2})\not\equiv a\equiv d\pmod{K_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\times 2}}.\qed

Now, we can present a deterministic algorithm for constructing
s
and
t
.

Algorithm 6
.

Let
\mathfrak{p}
be a prime of a number field
K
. Given three nonzero elements
a
,
b
and
d
of
K
, this algorithm outputs
s,t\in K^{\times}
such the
as^{2}+bt^{2}\not\equiv d\pmod{K_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\times 2}}
.

(1)

If all three elements
a
,
b
and
d
have the same parity of valuation, then:

(a)

Find a uniformizer
\pi
of
\mathfrak{p}
.

(b)

Write
a
,
b
and
d
as

a=a_{*}\cdot\pi^{\ord_{\mathfrak{p}}a},\quad b=b_{*}\cdot\pi^{\ord_{\mathfrak{p}}b},\quad d=d_{*}\cdot\pi^{\ord_{\mathfrak{p}}d},

for some
\mathfrak{p}
-units
a_{*},b_{*},d_{*}\in K^{\times}
.

(c)

Execute Algorithm
5
with input
a_{*}
,
b_{*}
,
d_{*}
, and denote its output by
s_{*}
and
t_{*}
.

(d)

Output
s:=s_{*}\cdot\pi^{\lceil\nicefrac{{\ord_{\mathfrak{p}}d}}{{2}}\rceil-\lceil\nicefrac{{\ord_{\mathfrak{p}}a}}{{2}}\rceil}
,
t:=t_{*}\cdot\pi^{\lceil\nicefrac{{\ord_{\mathfrak{p}}d}}{{2}}\rceil-\lceil\nicefrac{{\ord_{\mathfrak{p}}b}}{{2}}\rceil}
and quit.

(2)

If
\ord_{\mathfrak{p}}d\not\equiv\ord_{\mathfrak{p}}a\pmod{2}
, then set
s:=1
and let
t:=\pi^{k}
be such that
2k>\ord_{\mathfrak{p}}a+\ord_{\mathfrak{p}}b
.

(3)

Otherwise, if
\ord_{\mathfrak{p}}d\not\equiv\ord_{\mathfrak{p}}b\pmod{2}
, then set
t:=1
and let
s:=\pi^{k}
be such that
2k>ord_{\mathfrak{p}}a+\ord_{\mathfrak{p}}b
.

(4)

Output
s
and
t
.

Proof of correctness.

If all three elements have the same parity of valuations, then the correctness of the result outputted in step (
1d
) follows from the correctness of Algorithm
5
. Conversely, suppose that the valuations of
a
and
d
have different parity. Then for
s
and
t
constructed in step (
2
) we have

\ord_{\mathfrak{p}}(as^{2}+bt^{2})=\ord_{\mathfrak{p}}as^{2}\equiv\ord_{\mathfrak{p}}a\not\equiv\ord_{\mathfrak{p}}d\pmod{2}.

It follows that local square classes of
d
and
as^{2}+bt^{2}
differ. One proceeds analogously when the parity of
\ord_{\mathfrak{p}}b
and
\ord_{\mathfrak{p}}d
differ.
∎
