Fast Query of Biharmonic Distance in Networks
Changan Liu, Ahad N. Zehmakan, Zhongzhi Zhang

Abstract
. The biharmonic distance (BD) is a fundamental metric that measures the distance of two nodes in a graph. It has found applications in network coherence, machine learning, and computational graphics, among others. In spite of BD’s importance, efficient algorithms for the exact computation or approximation of this metric on large graphs remain notably absent. In this work, we provide several algorithms to estimate BD, building on a novel formulation of this metric. These algorithms enjoy locality property (that is, they only read a small portion of the input graph) and at the same time possess provable performance guarantees. In particular, our main algorithms approximate the BD between any node pair with an arbitrarily small additive error ϵ \epsilon in time O ⁡ ( 1 ϵ 2 ​ poly ​ ( log ⁡ n ϵ ) ) O(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\text{poly}(\log\frac{n}{\epsilon})) . Furthermore, we perform an extensive empirical study on several benchmark networks, validating the performance and accuracy of our algorithms.

1.
Introduction

Graph distance metrics have long been a cornerstone in the realm of network analysis, with a plethora of measures being introduced over the years, finding applications across diverse domains
(
Yang, 2022
;
Lü and Zhou, 2011
;
Shimada et al., 2016
;
Tsitsulin et al., 2020
;
Jin et al., 2019
;
Tran et al., 2019
;
Zhang et al., 2019
)
. A prominent example is the geodesic distance
(
Newman, 2018
)
, which, intuitively speaking, gauges distance based on the shortest path between node pairs. Another notable metric is the resistance distance, rooted in electrical circuit theory, which has found significance in areas ranging from circuit theory
(
Doyle and Snell, 1984
)
and chemistry
(
Klein and Randić, 1993
;
Peng and Li, 2017
)
to combinatorial matrix
(
Bapat, 2010
;
Yang and Klein, 2013
)
and spectral graph theory
(
Chen and Zhang, 2007
)
.

One of the most fundamental and important distance measures which has gained substantial attention, due to its various applications
(
Yi et al., 2018b
;
Yi et al., 2022
;
Verma and Zhang, 2017
;
Kreuzer et al., 2021
;
Black et al., 2023
;
Yi et al., 2018a
;
Tyloo et al., 2017
;
Fan et al., 2020
;
Fitch and Leonard, 2014
;
Xu et al., 2022
)
, is the
biharmonic distance
(BD). Introduced by Lipman et al.
(
Lipman et al., 2010
)
, BD was originally conceived as a measure to gauge distances on curved surfaces, a pivotal challenge in computer graphics and geometric processing.
The connection between BD and other measures and its different variants have been studied in the prior work. For example,
Yi et al.
(
Yi et al., 2018a
)
established a connection between BD and edge centrality. Wei et al.
(
Wei et al., 2021
)
introduced the notation of biharmonic graph distance and explored its relationship with the Kirchhoff index
(
Klein and Randić, 1993
)
.

The BD offers a unique blend of both local and global graph structure properties, making it superior to the geodesic and resistance distance in various applications. The versatility of BD is evident, for example, in its application to measure the robustness of second-order noisy consensus problems
(
Bamieh et al., 2012
;
Yi et al., 2022
)
. BD has also found applications in other areas such as graph learning
(
Kreuzer et al., 2021
;
Black et al., 2023
)
, physics
(
Tyloo et al., 2017
)
, and other network related fields
(
Fan et al., 2020
;
Fitch and Leonard, 2014
;
Tyloo et al., 2019
)
.

Despite its importance, the computational landscape of BD, especially for expansive graphs, remains challenging. While Yi et al.
(
Yi et al., 2018a
)
proposed a promising algorithm for approximating all-pairs BD, the algorithm’s dependency on the entire graph input and the construction of a large dense matrix in the preprocessing step makes it inapplicable to modern, rapidly expanding networks. Recognizing this gap, our paper delves deeper into devising fast algorithms for computing and approximating BD. Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

We first develop a novel formula of BD utilizing the expansion of the Laplacian pseudoinverse.

•

Leveraging this formula, we present two novel local algorithms,
Push
and
Push+
, for estimating pairwise BD. To reduce computational complexity, we introduce a random walk-based algorithm,
STW
. Our pinnacle contribution is the
SWF
algorithm, which employs empirical estimates of the variance to reduce the number of random walks used by
STW
.

•

Based on the pairwise BD estimation algorithms, we also present
SNB
, and its more efficient version
SNB+
by integrating the fast summation estimation techniques, for estimating nodal BD (which is the sum of the BD between a specific node and other nodes).

•

Our empirical studies, spanning real networks, underscore the superiority of our algorithms, often outperforming state-of-the-art solutions by significant margins.

Preliminary

2.1.
Notations

Throughout this paper, we utilize bold lowercase (e.g.,
\bm{\mathit{x}}
) for vectors and uppercase (e.g.,
\bm{\mathit{M}}
) for matrices. Elements are indicated by subscripts, e.g.,
\bm{\mathit{x}}_{i}
or
\bm{\mathit{M}}_{i,j}
. For matrices,
\bm{\mathit{M}}[i,:]
and
\bm{\mathit{M}}[:,j]
represent the
i
-th row and
j
-th column, respectively. We use
\bm{\mathit{e}}_{i}
to denote
i
-th standard basis vector of appropriate dimension, with
i
-th element being 1 and other elements being 0. We use
\mathbf{1}
(resp.
\bm{\mathit{J}}
) to denote the all-ones (column) vector (resp. matrix) of proper dimension. We use
\bm{\mathit{x}}^{\top}
to denote the transpose of vector
\bm{\mathit{x}}
. Furthermore, let
\mathbb{I}_{x=y}
be an indicator function, which equals 1 if
x=y
and otherwise 0.

Consider a connected undirected graph (network)
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
with nodes
V
and edges
E\subseteq V\times V
. Let
n:=|V|
and
m:=|E|
denote the number of nodes and the number of edges, respectively. We use
\mathcal{N}\left(i\right)
to denote the set of neighbors of
i
, where the degree is
\bm{\mathit{d}}_{i}=\left|\mathcal{N}\left(i\right)\right|
. The Laplacian matrix of
\mathcal{G}
is the symmetric matrix
\bm{\mathit{L}}=\bm{\mathit{D}}-\bm{\mathit{A}}
, where
\bm{\mathit{A}}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
is the adjacency matrix whose entry
\bm{\mathit{A}}_{i,j}=1
if node
i
and node
j
are adjacent, and
\bm{\mathit{A}}_{i,j}=0
otherwise, and
\bm{\mathit{D}}
is the diagonal matrix
\bm{\mathit{D}}=\text{diag}(\bm{\mathit{d}}_{1},\cdots,\bm{\mathit{d}}_{n})
. For any pair of distinct nodes
u,v\in V
, we define
\bm{\mathit{b}}_{uv}=\bm{\mathit{e}}_{u}-\bm{\mathit{e}}_{v}
. Let
\gamma_{1}\leq\gamma_{2}\leq

\gamma_{3}\leq\cdots\leq\gamma_{n}
be the eigenvalues of
\bm{\mathit{L}}
. It is well-known that
\gamma_{1}=0
, and
\gamma_{2}
is the algebraic connectivity of
\mathcal{G}
. Matrix
\bm{\mathit{L}}
is positive semi-definite. Its Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse is
\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}=\big(\bm{\mathit{L}}+\frac{1}{n}\bm{\mathit{J}}\big)^{-1}-\frac{1}{n}\bm{\mathit{J}}
. We use
\widetilde{\bm{\mathit{L}}}=\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathit{L}}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-1/2}
to denote the normalized Laplacian matrix.

Random walks underpin our proposed algorithms. Let
\bm{\mathit{P}}=\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-1}\bm{\mathit{A}}
be the random walk matrix (i.e., transition matrix) of
\mathcal{G}
, in which
\bm{\mathit{P}}_{i,j}=\frac{1}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{i}}
if
e_{i,j}\in E
and
\bm{\mathit{P}}_{i,j}=0
otherwise. Correspondingly, we denote
p_{\ell}\left(i,j\right)=\bm{\mathit{P}}^{\ell}_{i,j}
, which can be interpreted as the probability of a random walk from node
i
visits node
j
at the
\ell
-th hop. In this paper, following the convention, we assume
\mathcal{G}
is not bipartite (which is the case for most real-world networks). According to
(
Motwani and Raghavan, 1995
)
, the random walks over
\mathcal{G}
are ergodic, i.e.,
\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\bm{\mathit{P}}^{\ell}_{i,j}=\bm{\pi}_{j}=\frac{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{j}}{2m}
for any
i,j\in V
, where
\bm{\pi}
denotes the stationary distribution of a random walk starting from any node. Let
\bm{\mathit{Q}}=\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathit{A}}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-1/2}=\bm{\mathit{D}}^{1/2}\bm{\mathit{P}}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-1/2}
. Recall that
\bm{\mathit{L}}=\bm{\mathit{D}}-\bm{\mathit{A}}=\bm{\mathit{D}}^{1/2}(\bm{\mathit{I}}-\bm{\mathit{Q}})\bm{\mathit{D}}^{1/2}
. Note that
\bm{\mathit{Q}}
is symmetric and is similar to
\bm{\mathit{P}}
. Let
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq

\lambda_{3}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}
be the eigenvalues of
\bm{\mathit{Q}}
(and also
\bm{\mathit{P}}
by the similarity of
\bm{\mathit{P}}
and
\bm{\mathit{Q}}
), with corresponding (row) orthonormal eigenvectors
\bm{\mathit{u}}_{1},\bm{\mathit{u}}_{2},\ldots,\bm{\mathit{u}}_{n}
, i.e.,
\bm{\mathit{u}}_{j}\bm{\mathit{Q}}=\lambda_{j}\bm{\mathit{u}}_{j}
. Let
\bm{\mathit{w}}_{1},\bm{\mathit{w}}_{2},\ldots,\bm{\mathit{w}}_{n}
be the column eigenvectors of
\bm{\mathit{P}}
, i.e.,
\bm{\mathit{P}}\bm{\mathit{w}}_{j}=\lambda_{j}\bm{\mathit{w}}_{j}
. Let
\lambda=\max\left\{\left|\lambda_{2}\right|,\left|\lambda_{n}\right|\right\}
. Notably,
\lambda_{1}=1
,
\bm{\mathit{u}}_{1}=\frac{\mathbf{1}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{1/2}}{\sqrt{2m}}
and
\bm{\mathit{w}}_{1}=\mathbf{1}

(
Haveliwala and Kamvar, 2003
)
.

2.2.
Problem Definition

Since its introduction in
(
Lipman et al., 2010
)
, biharmonic distance in graphs has been extensively studied
(
Fitch and Leonard, 2014
;
Yi et al., 2018b
;
Yi et al., 2018a
;
Yi et al., 2022
;
Zhang et al., 2020
;
Black et al., 2023
;
Chen and Zhang, 2007
;
Wei et al., 2021
)
. Smaller BD implies a closer node connection, while a larger distance suggests a more indirect connection. Its formal definition is as follows.

Definition 2.1.

(Pairwise BD
(
Yi et al., 2018a
)
)
For a graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
with the Laplacian matrix
\bm{\mathit{L}}
, the biharmonic distance
b(s,t)
between any pair of distinct nodes
s,t\in V
is defined by

(1)

\displaystyle b^{2}(s,t)=\bm{\mathit{b}}_{st}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{2{\dagger}}\bm{\mathit{b}}_{st}=\|\bm{\mathit{L}}^{{\dagger}}\bm{\mathit{b}}_{st}\|^{2}=\bm{\mathit{L}}_{s,s}^{2{\dagger}}+\bm{\mathit{L}}_{t,t}^{2{\dagger}}-2\bm{\mathit{L}}_{s,t}^{2{\dagger}}.

Hereafter, we will refer to the square of BD
b^{2}(s,t)
as
\beta(s,t)
. To avoid redundancy, we will sometimes refer to
\beta(s,t)
as BD instead of the square of BD, when it is clear from the context.

According to
(
Wei et al., 2021
)
, the BD between any distinct nodes
s
and
t
satisfies
2\gamma_{n}^{-2}\leq\beta(s,t)\leq 2\gamma_{2}^{-2}
, implying that the more well-connected the network is, the smaller the difference in BD between distinct node pairs.

Based on the pairwise BD, we can derive the nodal BD as follows.

Definition 2.2.

(Nodal BD
(
Yi et al., 2018b
;
Yi et al., 2022
)
)
For a graph
\mathcal{G}
, the biharmonic distance of a single node
s\in V
is defined as:

(2)

\displaystyle\beta(s)=\sum_{t\in V\backslash\{s\}}\beta(s,t)=n\bm{\mathit{L}}_{s,s}^{2{\dagger}}+\mathrm{Tr}\left(\bm{\mathit{L}}^{2{\dagger}}\right).

The exact computation of BD involves inverting the Laplacian matrix
\bm{\mathit{L}}
of
\mathcal{G}
with a time complexity of
O(n^{2.3727})

(
Leiserson et al., 1994
)
. A natural resort is to attempt to approximate the BD values. In this paper, we focus on approximating BD values and study both the pairwise biharmonic distance query and the nodal biharmonic distance query problems, which are formally defined as follows.

Problem 1.

(Pairwise BD query) Given a graph
\mathcal{G}
, a pair of nodes
(s,t)
with
s\neq t
, and an arbitrarily small additive error
\epsilon
, the problem of pairwise BD query is to find
\beta^{\prime}(s,t)
such that:

(3)

\displaystyle|\beta(s,t)-\beta^{\prime}(s,t)|\leq\epsilon.

Problem 2.

(Nodal BD query) Given a graph
\mathcal{G}
, a source node
s
, and an arbitrarily small additive error
\epsilon
, the nodal BD query problem is to find
\beta^{\prime}(s)
such that:

(4)

\displaystyle|\beta(s)-\beta^{\prime}(s)|\leq n\epsilon.

Biharmonic distance aligns with effective resistance on a related signed graph
(
Shi et al., 2019
)
, given that
\bm{\mathit{L}}^{2}
is the “repelling” signed Laplacian matrix
(
Chen et al., 2020
)
. Thus, calculating biharmonic distancemirrors computing effective resistance on this signed graph. This suggests that efficient biharmonic distance computation can enhance understanding of effective resistance on signed graphs
(
Shi et al., 2019
)
.

2.3.
Existing Algorithms

Approximating BD involves solving the linear Laplacian system
\bm{\mathit{L}}\bm{\mathit{x}}=\bm{\mathit{b}}
, which has been extensively explored in the theoretical computer science. Despite advancements, the most efficient Laplacian solver takes
\tilde{O}(m)
time
(
Spielman and Teng, 2014
;
Cohen et al., 2014
;
Gao et al., 2023
)
, proving expensive for large-scale graphs (with millions of nodes and edges). Based on the Laplacian solver, the authors of
(
Yi et al., 2022
)
introduced a random projection-based method that permits an approximate biharmonic distance query for any node pair in
\mathcal{G}
within
O(\log n)
time. Yet, its preprocessing step, constructing a
(24\log n/\epsilon^{2})\times n
matrix for a given
\epsilon
, requires
\tilde{O}(m/\epsilon^{2})
time, making it unsuitable for large graphs. In many real-world scenarios, we are only concerned with the biharmonic distances for a limited set of critical node pairs, which makes this preprocessing overhead undesirable. Furthermore, their algorithm requires full knowledge of the underlying graph, which is not feasible in many real-world examples. To overcome these issues, we propose some novel algorithms that only need local information (not the entire graph) and could efficiently answer biharmonic distance queries for a set of node pairs, without unnecessary preprocessing overhead.

3.
The push algorithm

In this section, we first present a novel formula of BD and subsequently provide a local algorithm
Push
which relies on approximating the Laplacian pseudo-inverse
\bm{\mathit{L}}^{{\dagger}}
of the graph.

3.1.
New Formula for Biharmonic Distance

Lemma 3.1.

Let
\bm{\mathit{h}}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\bm{\mathit{b}}_{st}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{P}}^{i}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-1}
where
s,t\in V
are any two distinct nodes, then

(5)

\displaystyle\beta(s,t)=\|\bm{\mathit{h}}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{n}(\bm{\mathit{h}}\mathbf{1})^{2}.

Proof.
To prove this lemma, we need two materials. The first is that the pseudoinverse of matrix
\bm{\mathit{L}}
is related to the normalized Laplacian matrix
(
Bozzo, 2013
)
and can be written as

\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}=\left(\bm{\mathit{I}}-\frac{1}{n}\bm{\mathit{J}}\right)\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-1/2}\widetilde{\bm{\mathit{L}}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-1/2}\left(\bm{\mathit{I}}-\frac{1}{n}\bm{\mathit{J}}\right).

Second, we have

\displaystyle\quad\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\widetilde{\bm{\mathit{L}}}^{{\dagger}}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{j=2}^{n}\frac{1}{1-\lambda_{j}}\bm{\mathit{u}}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{u}}_{j}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}=\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{j=2}^{n}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}^{i}\bm{\mathit{u}}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{u}}_{j}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=2}^{n}\lambda_{j}^{i}\bm{\mathit{u}}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{u}}_{j}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}=\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\left(\bm{\mathit{Q}}^{i}-\bm{\mathit{u}}_{1}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{u}}_{1}\right)\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

Therefore, with the above two materials in hand,

\displaystyle\quad\beta(s,t)=b^{2}(s,t)=\bm{\mathit{b}}_{st}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{2{\dagger}}\bm{\mathit{b}}_{st}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathit{b}}_{st}^{\top}(\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\widetilde{\bm{\mathit{L}}}^{{\dagger}}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-1}\widetilde{\bm{\mathit{L}}}^{{\dagger}}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}-\frac{1}{n}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\widetilde{\bm{\mathit{L}}}^{{\dagger}}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{\mathit{J}}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\widetilde{\bm{\mathit{L}}}^{{\dagger}}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})\bm{\mathit{b}}_{st}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathit{b}}_{st}^{\top}\Big(\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}(\bm{\mathit{Q}}^{i}-\bm{\mathit{u}}_{1}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{u}}_{1})\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-1}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}(\bm{\mathit{Q}}^{i}-\bm{\mathit{u}}_{1}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{u}}_{1})\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}-

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{n}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}(\bm{\mathit{Q}}^{i}-\bm{\mathit{u}}_{1}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{u}}_{1})\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{\mathit{J}}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}(\bm{\mathit{Q}}^{i}-\bm{\mathit{u}}_{1}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{u}}_{1})\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Big)\bm{\mathit{b}}_{st}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}\bm{\mathit{b}}_{st}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{\mathit{Q}}^{i}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-1}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}\bm{\mathit{Q}}^{i}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{\mathit{b}}_{st}-

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}\bm{\mathit{b}}_{st}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{\mathit{Q}}^{i}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{\mathit{J}}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}\bm{\mathit{Q}}^{i}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{\mathit{b}}_{st}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}\bm{\mathit{b}}_{st}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{P}}^{i}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-1}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}\bm{\mathit{P}}^{i}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-1}\bm{\mathit{b}}_{st}-\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}\bm{\mathit{b}}_{st}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{P}}^{i}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-1}\mathbf{1}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{P}}^{i}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-1}\bm{\mathit{b}}_{st},

which concludes the proof.

\Box

3.2.
A Universal Bound of Truncated Length

Given an integer
\ell\in[0,\infty]
, for any two distinct nodes
s,t\in V
, let
\bm{\mathit{h}}^{\ell}=\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\bm{\mathit{b}}_{st}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{P}}^{i}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-1}
, then we define

(6)

\displaystyle\beta^{\ell}(s,t)=\|\bm{\mathit{h}}^{\ell}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{n}(\bm{\mathit{h}}^{\ell}\mathbf{1})^{2}.

We aim to choose an appropriate
\ell
to approximate
\beta(s,t)
by
\beta^{\ell}(s,t)
with bounded error. In the lemma below, we provide a universal bound of length for any pair of nodes.

Lemma 3.2.

For any additive error
\epsilon>0
, it holds that
|\beta(s,t)-\beta^{\ell}(s,t)|\leq\epsilon/2
, where the truncated length
\ell
satisfies

(7)

\displaystyle\ell=\left\lceil\frac{\log(12n/(\epsilon(1-\lambda)^{2}))}{\log(1/\lambda)}\right\rceil.

Proof.
To aid our analysis, we write
\bm{\mathit{b}}_{st}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-1/2}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}\bm{\mathit{u}}_{j}
. Note that
\alpha_{1}=\bm{\mathit{b}}_{st}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathit{u}}_{1}^{\top}=0
. Thus
\bm{\mathit{b}}_{st}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-1/2}=\sum_{j=2}^{n}\alpha_{j}\bm{\mathit{u}}_{j}
. Next, we write
\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathit{e}}_{v}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\beta_{j}\bm{\mathit{u}}_{j}^{\top}
. Then, we have
\bm{\mathit{b}}_{st}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathit{Q}}^{i}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathit{e}}_{v}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}^{i}\alpha_{j}\beta_{j}
and

|\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}\beta_{j}|
=|\bm{\mathit{b}}_{st}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathit{e}}_{v}|=|\bm{\mathit{b}}_{st}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-1}\bm{\mathit{e}}_{v}|\leq|\bm{\mathit{b}}_{st}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{e}}_{v}|\leq 1
.

Now, we first analyze the error of every element
\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}
for
v\in V
:

\displaystyle\quad|\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}-\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}^{\ell}|=\Big|\sum\limits_{i=\ell}^{\infty}\bm{\mathit{b}}_{st}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathit{Q}}^{i}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathit{e}}_{v}\Big|

\displaystyle=\Big|\sum\limits_{i=\ell}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}\bm{\mathit{u}}_{j}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}^{i}\bm{\mathit{u}}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{u}}_{j}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\beta_{j}\bm{\mathit{u}}_{j}^{\top}\Big|

\displaystyle=\Big|\sum\limits_{i=\ell}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}^{i}\alpha_{j}\beta_{j}|\leq\sum\limits_{i=\ell}^{\infty}\lambda^{i}|\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}\beta_{j}\Big|\leq\frac{\lambda^{\ell}}{1-\lambda}.

The above result shows that the larger the
\ell
, the smaller the approximation error is.

Using the similar method, we can also obtain that
\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}\leq\frac{1}{1-\lambda}
.
Then, we can obtain that

\displaystyle\quad\Big|\|\bm{\mathit{h}}\|_{2}^{2}-\|\bm{\mathit{h}}^{\ell}\|_{2}^{2}\Big|=\Big|\sum_{v=1}^{n}(\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}^{2}-(\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}^{\ell})^{2})\Big|\leq\sum_{v=1}^{n}|\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}^{2}-(\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}^{\ell})^{2}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{v=1}^{n}|\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}-\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}^{\ell}||2\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}+\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}^{\ell}-\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}|\leq\sum_{v=1}^{n}|\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}-\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}^{\ell}|^{2}+|2\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}(\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}^{\ell}-\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v})|

(8)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda^{2\ell}}{(1-\lambda)^{2}}+\frac{2\lambda^{\ell}}{(1-\lambda)^{2}}\leq\frac{3n\lambda^{\ell}}{(1-\lambda)^{2}},

and

\displaystyle|\bm{\mathit{h}}\mathbf{1}-\bm{\mathit{h}}^{\ell}\mathbf{1}|\leq\sum_{v=1}^{n}|\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}-\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}^{\ell}|\leq\frac{n\lambda^{\ell}}{1-\lambda},

and subsequently, we have

\displaystyle\quad|(\bm{\mathit{h}}\mathbf{1})^{2}-(\bm{\mathit{h}}^{\ell}\mathbf{1})^{2}|=|\bm{\mathit{h}}\mathbf{1}-\bm{\mathit{h}}^{\ell}\mathbf{1}||2\bm{\mathit{h}}\mathbf{1}+\bm{\mathit{h}}^{\ell}\mathbf{1}-\bm{\mathit{h}}\mathbf{1}|

(9)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n^{2}\lambda^{2\ell}}{(1-\lambda)^{2}}+\frac{2n^{2}\lambda^{\ell}}{(1-\lambda)^{2}}\leq\frac{3n^{2}\lambda^{\ell}}{(1-\lambda)^{2}}.

Finally, combining Eq. (
8
) and Eq. (
9
) yields

\displaystyle\big|\|\bm{\mathit{h}}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{n}(\bm{\mathit{h}}\mathbf{1})^{2}-\big(\|\bm{\mathit{h}}^{\ell}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{n}(\bm{\mathit{h}}^{\ell}\mathbf{1})^{2}\big)\big|\leq

\displaystyle\big|\|\bm{\mathit{h}}\|_{2}^{2}-\|\bm{\mathit{h}}^{\ell}\|_{2}^{2}|+\frac{1}{n}|(\bm{\mathit{h}}^{\ell}\mathbf{1})^{2}-(\bm{\mathit{h}}\mathbf{1})^{2}\big|\leq\frac{6n\lambda^{\ell}}{(1-\lambda)^{2}}\leq\epsilon/2,

where the last inequality follows from

\displaystyle\ell=\frac{\log(12n/(\epsilon(1-\lambda)^{2}))}{\log(1/\lambda)},

concluding the proof.

\Box

4.
Refining Maximum Length

In this section, we introduce an improved estimation for the truncated length, factoring in the structural nuances around the node pair in question. The node-specific
mixing time
, represented as
\zeta_{s}
for a source node
s
, reflects the steps a random walk from
s
needs to stabilize to the graph’s stationary distribution,
\bm{\pi}

(
Lang and Engelbrecht, 2020
)
. By setting
\ell=max\{\zeta_{s},\zeta_{t}\}
, we can equate
\beta(s,t)
to
\beta^{\ell}(s,t)
. Essentially, for a fixed
\ell
, node pairs with lower mixing times tend to approximate
\beta(s,t)
more accurately. In simpler terms, shorter mixing times for nodes
s,t
allow a reduced
\ell
while preserving accuracy. To optimize large
\ell
values, we propose a costumed
\ell
for distinct node pairs in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1.

For any additive error
\epsilon>0
, it holds that
|\beta(s,t)-\beta^{\ell}(s,t)|\leq\epsilon/2
, where the truncated length
\ell
satisfies

(10)

\displaystyle\ell=\left\lceil\frac{\log\bigg(\frac{6\sum_{v=1}^{n}(\frac{1}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{s}}+\frac{1}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{t}}+\frac{2}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{v}})^{2}+\frac{6}{n}(\frac{n}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{s}}+\frac{n}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{t}}+\sum_{v=1}^{n}\frac{2}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{v}})^{2}}{\epsilon(1-\lambda)^{2}}\bigg)}{\log(1/\lambda)}\right\rceil.

Proof.
Let us first provide the lemma below.

Lemma 4.2.

(
Yang and Tang, 2023
)

Given an undirected graph
\mathcal{G}
, for any two nodes
s,t\in V
and any integer
i\geq 1
, the transition matrix
\bm{\mathit{P}}
can be decomposed as

(11)

\displaystyle p_{i}(s,t)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bm{\mathit{w}}_{j,s}\bm{\mathit{w}}_{j,t}\bm{\pi}_{t}\lambda_{j}^{i},\text{and }\frac{1}{\bm{\pi}_{t}}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bm{\mathit{w}}_{j,t}^{2}.

According to Eq. (
11
), the following equations hold

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bm{\mathit{w}}_{j,s}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2m}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{s}},\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bm{\mathit{w}}_{j,t}^{2}=\frac{2m}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{t}},\text{ and }

\displaystyle\bm{\mathit{b}}_{st}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{P}}^{i}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-1}\bm{\mathit{e}}_{v}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{i}(s,v)}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{v}}-\frac{p_{i}(t,v)}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{v}}=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\bm{\mathit{w}}_{j,s}-\bm{\mathit{w}}_{j,t})\bm{\mathit{w}}_{j,v}\lambda_{j}^{i}.

Now, we analyze the approximation error of every element
\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}
for
v\in V
:

\displaystyle|\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}-\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}^{\ell}|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\sum\limits_{i=\ell}^{\infty}\bm{\mathit{b}}_{st}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{P}}^{i}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-1}\bm{\mathit{e}}_{v}\bigg|=\bigg|\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\bm{\mathit{w}}_{j,s}-\bm{\mathit{w}}_{j,t})\bm{\mathit{w}}_{j,v}\sum\limits_{i=\ell}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}^{i}\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left|(\bm{\mathit{w}}_{j,s}-\bm{\mathit{w}}_{j,t})\bm{\mathit{w}}_{j,v}\right|\sum\limits_{i=\ell}^{\infty}\lambda^{i}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda^{\ell}}{1-\lambda}\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(|\bm{\mathit{w}}_{j,s}\bm{\mathit{w}}_{j,v}|+|\bm{\mathit{w}}_{j,t}\bm{\mathit{w}}_{j,v}|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda^{\ell}}{1-\lambda}\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\bm{\mathit{w}}_{j,s}^{2}+\bm{\mathit{w}}_{j,v}^{2}+\bm{\mathit{w}}_{j,t}^{2}+\bm{\mathit{w}}_{j,v}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda^{\ell}}{1-\lambda}\frac{1}{2m}\left(\frac{2m}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{s}}+\frac{2m}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{t}}+\frac{4m}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{v}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda^{\ell}}{1-\lambda}\left(\frac{1}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{s}}+\frac{1}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{t}}+\frac{2}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{v}}\right).

Using similar method, we can derive that

\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}\leq\frac{1}{1-\lambda}\left(\frac{1}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{s}}+\frac{1}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{t}}+\frac{2}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{v}}\right).

Subsequently, we derive that

\displaystyle\quad\Big|\|\bm{\mathit{h}}\|_{2}^{2}-\|\bm{\mathit{h}}^{\ell}\|_{2}^{2}\Big|=\Big|\sum_{v=1}^{n}(\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}^{2}-(\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}^{\ell})^{2})\Big|\leq\sum_{v=1}^{n}|\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}^{2}-(\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}^{\ell})^{2}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{v=1}^{n}|\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}-\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}^{\ell}||2\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}+\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}^{\ell}-\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}|\leq\sum_{v=1}^{n}|\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}-\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}^{\ell}|^{2}+|2\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}(\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}^{\ell}-\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3\lambda^{\ell}}{(1-\lambda)^{2}}\sum_{v=1}^{n}\Big(\frac{1}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{s}}+\frac{1}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{t}}+\frac{2}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{v}}\Big)^{2}.

For the second component, we first note that

\displaystyle|\bm{\mathit{h}}\mathbf{1}-\bm{\mathit{h}}^{\ell}\mathbf{1}|\leq\sum_{v=1}^{n}|\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}-\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}^{\ell}|\leq\frac{\lambda^{\ell}}{1-\lambda}\sum_{v=1}^{n}\Big(\frac{1}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{s}}+\frac{1}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{t}}+\frac{2}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{v}}\Big),

and
|\bm{\mathit{h}}\mathbf{1}|\leq\frac{1}{1-\lambda}\sum_{v=1}^{n}\Big(\frac{1}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{s}}+\frac{1}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{t}}+\frac{2}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{v}}\Big).

Subsequently, we have

\displaystyle|(\bm{\mathit{h}}\mathbf{1})^{2}-(\bm{\mathit{h}}^{\ell}\mathbf{1})^{2}|\leq\frac{3\lambda^{\ell}}{(1-\lambda)^{2}}\Big(\frac{n}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{s}}+\frac{n}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{t}}+\sum_{v=1}^{n}\frac{2}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{v}}\Big)^{2}.

Finally,

\displaystyle\Big|\|\bm{\mathit{h}}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{n}(\bm{\mathit{h}}\mathbf{1})^{2}-(\|\bm{\mathit{h}}^{\ell}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{n}(\bm{\mathit{h}}^{\ell}\mathbf{1})^{2})\Big|\leq

\displaystyle\frac{3\lambda^{\ell}}{(1-\lambda)^{2}}\Big(\sum_{v=1}^{n}\Big(\frac{1}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{s}}+\frac{1}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{t}}+\frac{2}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{v}}\Big)^{2}+\frac{1}{n}\Big(\frac{n}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{s}}+\frac{n}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{t}}+\sum_{v=1}^{n}\frac{2}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{v}}\Big)^{2}\Big)\leq\epsilon/2,

where the last inequality follows from

\displaystyle\ell=\frac{\log\bigg(\frac{6\sum_{v=1}^{n}(\frac{1}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{s}}+\frac{1}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{t}}+\frac{2}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{v}})^{2}+\frac{6}{n}(\frac{n}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{s}}+\frac{n}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{t}}+\sum_{v=1}^{n}\frac{2}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{v}})^{2}}{\epsilon(1-\lambda)^{2}}\bigg)}{\log(1/\lambda)},

concluding the proof.

\Box

5.
Approximating by sampling random walks

Push
and
Push+
conduct deterministic graph traversal over
\mathcal{G}
by sequentially visiting all nodes reachable from
s
and
t
. Given the small-world property common to social and information networks, most nodes can be quickly reached from one another. As an illustration, by 2012, 92% of reachable pairs on Facebook were within five steps
(
Backstrom et al., 2011
)
. Thus, when
\ell
is large and nodes
s,t
have multiple neighbors, vectors
\bm{\mathit{P}}^{i-1}[s,:]
and
\bm{\mathit{P}}^{i-1}[t,:]
become dense quickly. Consequently, each round of pushing can take up to
O(m)
time, making this approach inefficient for large networks.

To alleviate the efficiency and scalability issue of
Push
and
Push+
, while maintaining their superiority in effectiveness, we propose estimating biharmonic distance by sampling random walks.

5.1.
Approximating by Sampling Random Walks

Recall that
\beta^{\ell}(s,t)=\|\bm{\mathit{h}}^{\ell}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{(\bm{\mathit{h}}^{\ell}\mathbf{1})^{2}}{n}
where
\bm{\mathit{h}}^{\ell}=\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\bm{\mathit{b}}_{st}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{P}}^{i}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-1}
. We next resort to random samples to estimate the first and second part of
\beta^{\ell}(s,t)
. Let’s first analyze how to approximate the first part. Define
\bm{\mathit{h}}^{\ell,x}=\bm{\mathit{b}}_{st}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{P}}^{x}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-1}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\quad\|\bm{\mathit{h}}^{\ell}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{v=1}^{n}\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}^{\ell,i}\sum_{j=0}^{\ell-1}\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}^{\ell,j}=\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\sum_{j=0}^{\ell-1}\sum_{v=1}^{n}\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}^{\ell,i}\bm{\mathit{h}}_{v}^{\ell,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\sum_{j=0}^{\ell-1}\sum_{v=1}^{n}\Big(\frac{\bm{\mathit{P}}_{s,v}^{i}\bm{\mathit{P}}_{s,v}^{j}}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{v}^{2}}+\frac{\bm{\mathit{P}}_{t,v}^{i}\bm{\mathit{P}}_{t,v}^{j}}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{v}^{2}}-\frac{\bm{\mathit{P}}_{t,v}^{i}\bm{\mathit{P}}_{s,v}^{j}}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{v}^{2}}-\frac{\bm{\mathit{P}}_{s,v}^{i}\bm{\mathit{P}}_{t,v}^{j}}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{v}^{2}}\Big).

The quantity
\sum_{v=1}^{n}\frac{\bm{\mathit{P}}^{i}_{s,v}\bm{\mathit{P}}_{s,v}^{j}}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{v}^{2}}
represents the degree-normalized probability of two random walks with length
i
and
j
respectively, both starting from
s
, ending at the same node. Define
W_{i,j}=\sum_{1\leq t\leq r}W_{t}
, where
W_{t}=1
if two walks of length
i
and
j
from
s
end at the same node and this is accepted with a probability of the inverse square of the end node’s degree; otherwise,
W_{t}=0
. In the same manner,
X_{i,j}
pertains to two walks from
t
,
Y_{i,j}
to two walks from
s
and
t
, and
Z_{i,j}
to two walks from
t
and
s
.

We make use of the following Chernoff-Hoeffding’s inequality.

Theorem 5.1.

(
Chernoff-Hoeffding’s Inequality

(
Hoeffding, 1963
)
). Let
Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots,Z_{n_{z}}
be i.i.d. random variables with
Z_{j}\left(\forall 1\leq j\leq n_{z}\right)
being strictly bounded by the interval
\left[a_{j},b_{j}\right]
. We define the mean of these variables by
Z=\frac{1}{n_{z}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{z}}Z_{j}
. Then, we have

\mathbb{P}[|Z-\mathbb{E}[Z]|\geq\varepsilon]\leq 2\exp\bigg(-\frac{2n_{z}^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n_{z}}\left(b_{j}-a_{j}\right)^{2}}\bigg).

It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg[\bigg|\frac{W_{i,j}}{r}-\sum_{v=1}^{n}\frac{\bm{\mathit{P}}_{s,v}^{i}\bm{\mathit{P}}_{s,v}^{j}}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{v}^{2}}\bigg|\geq\frac{\epsilon}{16\ell^{2}}\bigg]=\mathbb{P}\bigg[\bigg|\frac{W_{i,j}}{r}-\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[W_{i,j}\right]}{r}\bigg|\geq\frac{\epsilon}{16\ell^{2}}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp(-2\epsilon^{2}r^{2}/(256\ell^{4}r))\leq\frac{\delta}{4\ell^{2}},

with the last inequality due to
r=128\ell^{4}\log(\frac{8\ell^{2}}{\delta})/\epsilon^{2}
. Similarly,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg[\bigg|\frac{X_{i,j}}{r}-\sum_{v=1}^{n}\frac{\bm{\mathit{P}}_{t,v}^{i}\bm{\mathit{P}}^{j}_{t,v}}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{v}^{2}}\bigg|\geq\frac{\epsilon}{16\ell^{2}}\bigg]\leq\frac{\delta}{4\ell^{2}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg[\bigg|\frac{Y_{i,j}}{r}-\sum_{v=1}^{n}\frac{\bm{\mathit{P}}^{i}_{t,v}\bm{\mathit{P}}^{j}_{s,v}}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{v}^{2}}\bigg|\geq\frac{\epsilon}{16\ell^{2}}\bigg]\leq\frac{\delta}{4\ell^{2}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg[\bigg|\frac{Z_{i,j}}{r}-\sum_{v=1}^{n}\frac{\bm{\mathit{P}}^{i}_{s,v}\bm{\mathit{P}}^{j}_{t,v}}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{v}^{2}}\bigg|\geq\frac{\epsilon}{16\ell^{2}}\bigg]\leq\frac{\delta}{4\ell^{2}},

Thus, by a union bound, it holds with a success probability of at least
1-4*\ell^{2}*\frac{\delta}{4\ell^{2}}=1-\delta
for some
\delta\in(0,1)
that

\displaystyle\bigg|\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\sum_{j=0}^{\ell-1}\Big(\frac{W_{i,j}}{r}+\frac{X_{i,j}}{r}-\frac{Y_{i,j}}{r}-\frac{Z_{i,j}}{r}\Big)-\|\bm{\mathit{h}}^{\ell}\|_{2}^{2}\bigg|\leq\frac{4\epsilon\ell^{2}}{16\ell^{2}}=\frac{\epsilon}{4}.

Let’s explore how random walk samples can be employed to approximate the second part which could easily be rewritten as:

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{n}(\bm{\mathit{h}}^{\ell}\mathbf{1})^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{u=1}^{n}\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\bm{\mathit{b}}_{st}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{P}}^{i}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-1}\bm{\mathit{e}}_{u}\sum_{v=1}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{\ell-1}\bm{\mathit{b}}_{st}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{P}}^{j}\bm{\mathit{D}}^{-1}\bm{\mathit{e}}_{v}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\sum_{j=0}^{\ell-1}\sum_{u=1}^{n}\sum_{v=1}^{n}\Big(\frac{\bm{\mathit{P}}^{i}_{s,u}\bm{\mathit{P}}^{j}_{s,v}}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{u}\bm{\mathit{d}}_{v}}+\frac{\bm{\mathit{P}}^{i}_{t,u}\bm{\mathit{P}}^{j}_{t,v}}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{u}\bm{\mathit{d}}_{v}}-\frac{\bm{\mathit{P}}^{i}_{s,u}\bm{\mathit{P}}^{j}_{t,v}}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{u}\bm{\mathit{d}}_{v}}-\frac{\bm{\mathit{P}}^{i}_{t,u}\bm{\mathit{P}}^{j}_{s,v}}{\bm{\mathit{d}}_{u}\bm{\mathit{d}}_{v}}\Big).

Let
\bar{W}_{i,j}=\sum_{1\leq t\leq r}\bar{W}_{t}
, where
\bar{W}_{t}
is an indicator random variable and
\bar{W}_{t}=1
if two walks of length
i
and
j
start from
s
and this is accepted with a probability of the inverse of the degree product of the end nodes; otherwise,
\bar{W}_{t}=0
. In the same manner,
\bar{X}_{i,j}
pertains to two walks from
t
,
\bar{Y}_{i,j}
to two walks from
s
and
t
, and
\bar{Z}_{i,j}
to two walks from
t
and
s
.

Based on the Chernoff-Hoeffding inequality,

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{P}\bigg[\bigg|\frac{\bar{W}_{i,j}}{nr}-\sum_{u=1}^{n}\sum_{v=1}^{n}\frac{\bm{\mathit{P}}^{i}_{s,u}\bm{\mathit{P}}^{j}_{s,v}}{n\bm{\mathit{d}}_{u}\bm{\mathit{d}}_{v}}\bigg|\geq\frac{\epsilon}{16\ell^{2}}\bigg]=\mathbb{P}\bigg[\bigg|\frac{\bar{W}_{i,j}}{nr}-\frac{\mathbb{E}[\bar{W}_{i,j}]}{nr}\bigg|\geq\frac{\epsilon}{16\ell^{2}}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp(-2\epsilon^{2}r^{2}n^{2}/(256\ell^{4}r))\leq\frac{\delta}{4\ell^{2}},

where
r=128\ell^{4}\log(\frac{8\ell^{2}}{\delta})/(n^{2}\epsilon^{2})
. Similarly,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg[\bigg|\frac{\bar{X}_{i,j}}{nr}-\sum_{u=1}^{n}\sum_{v=1}^{n}\frac{\bm{\mathit{P}}^{i}_{t,u}\bm{\mathit{P}}^{j}_{t,v}}{n\bm{\mathit{d}}_{u}\bm{\mathit{d}}_{v}}\bigg|\geq\frac{\epsilon}{16\ell^{2}}\bigg]\leq\frac{\delta}{4\ell^{2}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg[\bigg|\frac{\bar{Y}_{i,j}}{nr}-\sum_{u=1}^{n}\sum_{v=1}^{n}\frac{\bm{\mathit{P}}^{i}_{s,u}\bm{\mathit{P}}^{j}_{t,v}}{n\bm{\mathit{d}}_{u}\bm{\mathit{d}}_{v}}\bigg|\geq\frac{\epsilon}{16\ell^{2}}\bigg]\leq\frac{\delta}{4\ell^{2}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg[\bigg|\frac{\bar{Z}_{i,j}}{nr}-\sum_{u=1}^{n}\sum_{v=1}^{n}\frac{\bm{\mathit{P}}^{i}_{t,u}\bm{\mathit{P}}^{j}_{s,v}}{n\bm{\mathit{d}}_{u}\bm{\mathit{d}}_{v}}\bigg|\geq\frac{\epsilon}{16\ell^{2}}\bigg]\leq\frac{\delta}{4\ell^{2}}.

1-\delta

\displaystyle\bigg|\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\sum_{j=0}^{\ell-1}\Big(\frac{\bar{W}_{i,j}}{nr}+\frac{\bar{X}_{i,j}}{nr}-\frac{\bar{Y}_{i,j}}{nr}-\frac{\bar{Z}_{i,j}}{nr}\Big)-\frac{(\bm{\mathit{h}}^{\ell}\mathbf{1})^{2}}{n}\bigg|\leq\frac{4\epsilon\ell^{2}}{16\ell^{2}}=\frac{\epsilon}{4}.

6.
Sampling with Feedback

Utilizing Chernoff-Hoeffding’s inequality, the preceding section pre-determined the maximum necessary random walks. However, due to the actual variance of random variables often being lower than estimated, many walks become unnecessary. Ideally, an efficient method would utilize the fewest random walks, determined by the actual, albeit unknown, variance.

Inspired by the preceding discussion, we suggest modifying the sampling phase to sample with feedback. Specifically, rather than executing all random walks at once, we progressively conduct Monte Carlo random walks to estimate
\beta(s,t)
, terminating based on the empirical error from observed samples, calculable via the empirical Bernstein inequality in the following lemma.

Lemma 6.1.

(Empirical Bernstein Inequality
(
Audibert et al., 2007
)
). Let
Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots,Z_{n_{z}}
be real-valued i.i.d. random variables, such that
0\leq Z_{j}\leq\psi
. We denote by
Z=\frac{1}{n_{z}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{z}}Z_{j}
the empirical mean of these variables and
\hat{\sigma}^{2}=\frac{1}{n_{z}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{z}}(Z_{j}-\frac{Z}{n_{z}})^{2}
their empirical variance. Then, for some
\delta\in(0,1)
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left[|Z-\mathbb{E}[Z]|\geq f\left(n_{z},\hat{\sigma}^{2},\psi,\delta\right)\right]\leq\delta,

where
f\left(n_{z},\hat{\sigma}^{2},\psi,\delta\right)=\sqrt{\frac{2\hat{\sigma}^{2}\log(3/\delta)}{n_{z}}}+\frac{3\psi\log(3/\delta)}{n_{z}}.

We next show how we utilize the empirical Bernstein inequality to devise a new algorithm. We first present the following lemma.

Lemma 6.2.

For two length-
\ell

9.
Related Work

In this section, we discuss some prior work on biharmonic distance and explain how they are related to ours.

Biharmonic distance has found applications in different fields. In
(
Yi et al., 2018b
;
Yi et al., 2022
)
, the authors established a connection between the biharmonic distance of a graph and its second-order network coherence. In
(
Yi et al., 2018a
)
, the biharmonic distance was used to measure the edge centrality in a network. Furthermore, biharmonic distance has also been utilized in computational graphics
(
Lipman et al., 2010
;
Verma and Zhang, 2017
)
, machine learning
(
Kreuzer et al., 2021
;
Black et al., 2023
)
, and physics
(
Tyloo et al., 2017
)
, as well as graph matching
(
Fan et al., 2020
)
and leader selection in noisy networks
(
Fitch and Leonard, 2014
)
.

Despite its importance, the prior work has made little progress on devising efficient and effective practical algorithms for computing BD. In
(
Zhang et al., 2020
;
Yi et al., 2022
)
, the authors developed an algorithm to compute BD whose run time is linear in the number of edges in the network. However, this time complexity is not desirable when dealing with large networks and also their algorithm leads to large hidden memory load. The present work proposes local algorithms (reading only a small portion of the input graph) to approximate biharmonic distance with small space load.

There also exist several local algorithms for other random walk based quantities, such as the stationary distribution
(
Lee et al., 2013
)
, PageRank
(
Bressan et al., 2018
)
, Personalized PageRank (PPR)
(
Wang et al., 2017
)
and transition probabilities
(
Banerjee and Lofgren, 2015
)
. At first glance, it might seem that we could simply adapt and extend these techniques for BD computation. However, computing BD is much more involved. This is because they are defined according to inherently different types of random walks. More concretely, PPR leverages the one called random walk with restart (RWR)
(
Tong et al., 2006
)
, which would stop at each visited node with a certain probability during the walk. ER relies on simple random walks of various fixed lengths (from 0 to
\infty
)
(
Peng et al., 2021
)
. In contrast, BD relies on comparing the end nodes of pairs of walks with various lengths, indicating that the sampling process in BD is more complex.

10.
Conclusion

We provided a novel formula of biharmonic distance (BD) and subsequently proposed using deterministic truncated graph traversal to approximate BD queries. Specifically, we presented
Push
and
Push+
, where the former is with a universal truncated length for any pair of nodes while the latter is with a customized length for each query. These two choices suit different scenarios and are adopted according to the setup in hand. For the purpose of practical efficiency, we further developed
STW
and
SWF
, which are built on the idea of sampling random walks to estimate the desired probabilities.
STW
directly samples walks to approximate related probabilities while
SWF
observes whether in the process of random walk sampling, the predefined error criterion has been met, thus enabling an early termination of the simulation. Based on the algorithms for approximating pairwise BD, we propose
SNB
for computing nodal BD, and further propose
SNB+
to speed up its run time. The experimental results on real-world graph data demonstrate that our algorithms consistently and significantly outperform the existing competitors.
In the future, we aim to study the computation of biharmonic distance with relative error guarantees as well as under distributed and multithreading setups. Furthermore, it would be interesting to speed up our proposed algorithms leveraging novel heuristic and approximation techniques.
