Shock waves in nonlinear transmission lines
Eugene Kogan

Abstract
In the first half of the paper we consider interaction between the small amplitude travelling waves (”sound”) and the shock waves in the transmission line containing both nonlinear capacitors and nonlinear inductors. We calculate the ”sound” wave coefficient of reflection from (coefficient of transmission through) the shock wave. These coefficients are expressed in terms of the speeds of the ”sound” waves relative to the shock and the wave impedances. In the second half of the paper we explicitly include into consideration the dissipation in the system, introducing ohmic resistors shunting the inductors and also in series with the capacitors. This allows us to justify the conditions on the shocks, postulated in the first half of the paper. This also allows us to describe the shocks as physical objects of finite width and study their profiles, same as the profiles of the waves closely connected with the shocks - the kinks. The profiles of the latter, and in some particular cases the profiles of the former, were obtained in terms of elementary functions.

Introduction

The nonlinear electrical transmission lines are of much interest both due to their applications, and as the laboratories to study nonlinear waves
[
1
]

A very interesting particular type of signals which can propagate along such
lines - the shock waves - is attracting interest since long ago

We published a series of papers on the travelling waves in Josephson transmission lines (JTL): the kinks, the solitons and the shocks
[

(see the last publication of that series and the references therein). The cited publication is especially relevant for what will be presented below. In that publication we considered among other problems, the small amplitude wave (”sound”) reflection from and transmission through the shock wave.

Note that from the point of view of an electrical engineer, Josephson junctions are just the nonlinear inductors, and this is how they were described in all our papers on JTL. On the other hand, the capacitors in all these papers were assumed to be linear. On the other hand,
very recently we published the paper on the travelling waves in the transmission

In the present paper we consider the travelling waves in the transmission line where, in the general case, both the inductors and the capacitors are nonlinear.
In the first part of the paper, generalizing our previous results

V
Lossy transmission line

As it is well known
[
6
]
, the shock waves exist only in the presence of dissipation. (In the absence of the dissipation, the localized travelling waves in the systems we consider are kinks and solitons
[
10
,
11
,
4
]
.) On the other hand, many properties of the shocks in fluids were studied beginning from the XIX century without taking the dissipation explicitly into account, but just postulating the existence of discontinuous solutions and the jump conditions
[
12
]
.
This is exactly what we did in the previous Sections.

However in this Section, to be consistent, we assume the existence of ohmic resistors in the transmission line, shunting the inductors and in series with the capacitors. Such transmission line is shown on Fig.
4
. We’ll resolve the zero width discontinuities from the previous Sections, which will turn out to be narrow (for weak dissipation) crossover regions and rigorously prove the jump conditions.

Figure 4:
Lossy nonlinear transmission line

The corrected Eqs. (

\displaystyle\frac{dQ_{n}}{dt}

\displaystyle=I_{n}-I_{n+1}+\frac{1}{R_{L}}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\Phi_{n}-\Phi_{n+1}\right),

\displaystyle\frac{d\Phi_{n+1}}{dt}

(36b)

In the continuum approximation (

Since we are considering localized travelling waves,

, defined by the equations

\displaystyle\lim_{\tau\to-\infty}Q(\tau)

\displaystyle=Q_{1};\hskip 14.22636pt\lim_{\tau\to+\infty}Q(\tau)=Q_{2};

\displaystyle\lim_{\tau\to-\infty}\Phi(\tau)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{1};\hskip 14.22636pt\lim_{\tau\to+\infty}\Phi(\tau)=\Phi_{2}.

Equating the r.h.s. of (

The boundary conditions show that

Calculating the determinant and recalling the result for the ”sound” speed we obtain

\displaystyle U_{21}^{2}<u_{2}^{2}.

(47)

Similarly for
the point

\displaystyle u_{1}^{2}<U_{21}^{2}.

VIII.1
The shocks vs. the kinks

The main subject of the present paper is the shock waves.
However, Eq. (
49
) can describe both the shocks and the kinks
[
4
]
.
To understand the difference between two types of waves note that, strictly speaking,
the boundary conditions (
39
) tell us that the fictitious particle ends its motion at
\tau=+\infty
at the stationary point
Q_{2}
, which can be either potential minimum or potential maximum. For former case corresponds to the shocks, the latter - to the kinks
[
4
]
. Equation (
55
) can describe only the shocks. Equation (
60
), when the point
-Q_{3}
is between
Q_{2}
and
Q_{1}
, describes the kink, when
the point is outside of that interval - the shocks.

Equations (
78
) and (
79
) are valid both for the kinks and for the shocks, but
in these two cases the condition (
78
) has different meanings. For the kinks it’s the condition of their existence. For the shocks it’s the condition for them to be expressed in elementary functions (actually the condition that the fictitious particle doesn’t oscillate in the vicinity of the stable equilibrium). But, of course, the shocks exist for the general boundary conditions. The same can be said about the shocks described by Eq. (
55
).

Appendix B
Solitary wave solution of a generalized KdV equation

Consider a generalized KdV equation
[
15
]

\displaystyle\phi_{t}+(n+1)(n+2)\phi^{n}\phi_{x}+\phi_{xxx}=0.

(87)

For a travelling wave solution

\displaystyle\phi(t,x)=\phi(\tau),\hskip 28.45274pt\tau=t-x/U,

(88)

the equation in partial derivatives (
87
) is reduced to the ODE

\displaystyle U^{3}\frac{d\phi}{d\tau}-U^{2}(n+1)(n+2)\phi^{n}\frac{d\phi}{d\tau}-\frac{d^{3}\phi}{d\tau^{3}}=0.

(89)

Equation (
89
) doesn’t contain explicitly the independent variable
\tau
. This prompts the idea to
consider
\phi
as the new independent variable and

\displaystyle E=\frac{d\phi}{d\tau}

(90)

as the new dependent variable.
As the result, we can present the nonlinear third order ODE (
89
) as the linear second order ODE for
E^{2}

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}E^{2}}{d\phi^{2}}=2U^{3}-2(n+1)(n+2)U^{2}\phi^{n}.

(91)

A solitary wave corresponds to the boundary conditions

\displaystyle\lim_{\tau\to\pm\infty}\phi(\tau)=0,

(92)

which in the new variables are
[
5
]

\displaystyle E^{2}(0)=0,\hskip 28.45274pt\left.\frac{dE^{2}}{d\phi}\right|_{\phi=0}=0.

(93)

Solving (
91
) with these boundary conditions we obtain

\displaystyle E^{2}=U^{3}\phi^{2}-2U^{2}\phi^{n+2}.

(94)

Substituting thus obtained
E(\phi)
into (
90
) and integrating we obtain the well known result
[
15
]

\displaystyle\phi^{n}=\frac{U}{2}\sech^{2}\left[\frac{n\sqrt{U}}{2}\left(x-Ut\right)\right].

(95)
