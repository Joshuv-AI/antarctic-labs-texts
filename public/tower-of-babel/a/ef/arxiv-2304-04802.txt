Kasner-like description of spacelike singularities in spherically symmetric spacetimes with scalar matter
Warren Li

Abstract
We study the properties of spacelike singularities in spherically symmetric spacetimes obeying the Einstein equations, in the presence of matter. Building upon previous work of An–Zhang [ 4 ] , we consider matter described by a scalar field, both in the presence of an electromagnetic field and without. We prove that, if a spacelike singularity obeying several reasonable assumptions is formed, then the Hawking mass, the Kretschmann scalar, and the matter fields have inverse polynomial blow-up rates near the singularity that may be described precisely. Furthermore, one may view the resulting spacetime in the context of the BKL heuristics regarding spacelike singularities in relativistic cosmology. In particular, near any point p p on the singular boundary in our spherically symmetric spacetime, we obtain a leading order BKL-type expansion, including a description of Kasner exponents associated to p p . This confirms heuristics of Buonanno–Damour–Veneziano [ 14 ] . As a result, we provide a rigorous description of a detailed, quantitative correspondence between Kasner-like singularities most often associated to the cosmological setting, and the singularities observed in (spherically symmetric) gravitational collapse. Moreover, we outline a program concerning the study of the stability and instability of spacelike singularities in the latter picture, both outside of spherical symmetry and within (where the electromagnetic field acts as a proxy for angular momentum).

Introduction

1.1
Background

One of the monumental predictions of Einstein’s theory of general relativity is that of “singularities”. Indeed, celebrated theorems of Penrose
[
50
]
and Hawking
[
32
]
prove that globally hyperbolic spacetimes
(\mathcal{M},g)
satisfying the Einstein equations

G_{\mu\nu}[g]\coloneqq\mathrm{Ric}_{\mu\nu}[g]-\frac{1}{2}R[g]g_{\mu\nu}=2\,T_{\mu\nu},

(1.1)

as well as several physically reasonable conditions regarding the spatial topology and the matter fields, will contain “singularities”, so long as either of the following are present: (a) a codimension
2
trapped surface, or (b) a Cauchy hypersurface whose mean curvature is bounded strictly away from
0
.

In both cases, however, the phenomenon captured by these theorems is not actually singularity formation as this term is usually understood – suggesting some type of blow-up – but rather geodesic incompleteness of the spacetime
(\mathcal{M},g)
.
Indeed, even in the absence of matter, there are known examples of globally hyperbolic spacetimes, for instance the Kerr family or the Taub-NUT spacetimes (see, e.g.
[
33
]
), satisfying the assumptions of the Hawking-Penrose theorems, yet exhibiting no coordinate-independent blowup.

In fact, the above examples can be smoothly and isometrically embedded into a larger spacetime
(\tilde{\mathcal{M}},\tilde{g})
in such a way that all previously incomplete geodesics can be extended into
(\tilde{\mathcal{M}},\tilde{g})
. Hence, from the point of view of observers travelling along such geodesics, there is nothing ‘singular’ about the predictions of these theorems, and their geodesic incompleteness is instead due to the presence of so-called Cauchy horizons. In view of the strong cosmic censorship conjecture, one could ask about the non-genericity of such Cauchy horizons, and whether the presence of singularities is a true prediction of Einstein’s equation (
1.1
).

There are, of course, known solutions to the Einstein equations where genuine singularities are present. The celebrated Schwarzschild family of spacetimes are vacuum solutions to (
1.1
) depending on a single parameter
M>0
. They each contain a black hole interior region, described by the metric
g_{Schw}
on
(0,2M)\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}
:

g_{Schw}=-\left(\frac{2M}{r}-1\right)^{-1}\,dr^{2}+\left(\frac{2M}{r}-1\right)\,dt^{2}+r^{2}\left(d\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta\,d\varphi^{2}\right).

(1.2)

Treating
r
as a (past-directed) timelike coordinate, the interior region is bounded to the future by a
spacelike singularity
at
r=0
. The truly singular nature of this boundary is reflected in the fact that the
Kretschmann scalar
can be computed to be

\mathrm{Riem}[g_{Schw}]_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}\,\mathrm{Riem}[g_{Schw}]^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}=\frac{48M^{2}}{r^{6}}

and thus blows up towards the singularity at
r=0
. In fact, there does not even exist a
C^{0}
extension of (
1.2
) across the
r=0
boundary
[
58
]
.

Another example of a singular vacuum solution to (
1.1
) is the Kasner solution
[
36
]
, defined on the manifold
\mathcal{M}=(0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{3}
and given by the metric
g_{Kas}
:

g_{Kas}=-dt^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{3}t^{2p_{i}}(dx^{i})^{2}.

(1.3)

In order for (
1.3
) to satisfy Einstein’s equations (
1.1
) with
T_{\mu\nu}=0
, the
Kasner exponents

p_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
must be chosen to satisfy the two relations
\sum_{i=1}^{3}p_{i}=1
,
\sum_{i=1}^{3}p_{i}^{2}=1
. These relations imply that
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}
are constrained to lie on the so-called
Kasner circle
, parameterized by
u\in(-\infty,+\infty]
as follows:

p_{1}(u)=-\frac{u}{1+u+u^{2}},\quad p_{2}(u)=\frac{1+u}{1+u+u^{2}},\quad p_{3}(u)=\frac{u(1+u)}{1+u+u^{2}}.

(1.4)

Like Schwarzschild, the Kasner spacetimes possess a singular boundary at
t=0
, and the singular nature of the boundary is manifested by the following blow-up of the Kretschmann scalar as
t\to 0^{+}
:

\mathrm{Riem}[g_{Kas}]_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}\,\mathrm{Riem}[g_{Kas}]^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}=\frac{4}{t^{4}}\cdot\left(\sum_{i=1}^{3}p_{i}^{2}(p_{i}-1)^{2}+\sum_{i<j}p_{i}^{2}p_{j}^{2}\right)=\frac{16u^{2}(1+u)^{2}}{(1+u+u^{2})^{3}}\cdot\frac{1}{t^{4}}.

Here
u\in\{-1,0,+\infty\}
, which correspond to
(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3})=(1,0,0)
and permutations thereof, give rise to exceptional Kasner solutions that are flat and thus locally isometric to Minkowski space. These can be locally extended beyond the
t=0
boundary, and we exclude these solutions (so-called ‘special points’ of the Kasner circle) from the subsequent discussion.

The metrics (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) provide evidence that genuine singularities may arise in solutions to Einstein’s equation (
1.1
). But both are highly symmetric solutions, and to argue that singularities are a genuine prediction of general relativity, one must argue that generic, non-symmetric initial data would also produce such singularities in evolution.

In a highly influential paper, Khalatnikov and Lifshitz
[
39
]
propose an ansatz for the asymptotic form of a metric solving the vacuum Einstein equation near a candidate spacelike
singularity, of which (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) are special examples. However, via a function counting argument, the authors of
[
39
]
argue that their singular solutions are exceptional among solutions to Einstein’s vacuum equations arising from general initial data, and further provide a heuristic instability mechanism; loosely speaking, the instability is sourced by spatial curvature terms. This will be elaborated upon in Section
1.4
.

In this article, we study spherically symmetric solutions to Einstein’s equation (
1.1
). By Birkhoff’s theorem
[
13
]
, spherically symmetric solutions to the Einstein
vacuum
equations are non-dynamical and are (locally) isometric to some region of the Schwarzschild solution with mass
M\in\mathbb{R}
, where
M=0
corresponds to Minkowski space. We thus introduce dynamics into the problem via the addition of matter, namely a (massless and uncharged) scalar field
\phi
, whose energy-momentum tensor is given by

T_{\mu\nu}[\phi]=\nabla_{\mu}\phi\nabla_{\nu}\phi-\frac{1}{2}g_{\mu\nu}\nabla^{\rho}\phi\nabla_{\rho}\phi.

(1.5)

The scalar field
\phi
evolves according to the scalar wave equation on
(\mathcal{M},g)
:

\square_{g}\phi=g^{\mu\nu}\nabla_{\mu}\nabla_{\nu}\phi=0.

(1.6)

In studying the coupled system (
1.1
), (
1.5
), (
1.6
) in spherical symmetry, the aforementioned instability mechanism of
[
39
]
will be suppressed in two different ways. Firstly, we observe (see Section
1.4
) that spherical symmetry causes the spatial curvature term sourcing the instability to vanish by assumption. Secondly, as observed by Belinskii and Khalatnikov in
[
8
]
, the presence of scalar field matter allows for a more general class of asymptotic ansatz near the singularity, including so-called
subcritical
regimes where the spatial curvature no longer provides an instability.

For these reasons, it is expected that the formation and asymptotics of general spacelike singularities can be understood in this setting.
This is the objective of the present article, where we prove that spacelike singularities in the spherically symmetric Einstein-scalar field system can be described precisely, in the sense that in the neighborhood of a point
p
on the singular boundary, we can describe the leading order blow-up rates of all geometric quantities (including the Kretschmann scalar and the Hawking mass) and matter quantities such as
\phi
and its derivatives, in terms of the area-radius
r
(see Section
1.2
for a definition).

We further provide a precise correspondence between such spacelike singularities and the heuristics of
[
8
]
– in particular we associate to each point
p
on the singular boundary a triplet of Kasner exponents. This can be viewed as a rigorous version of the correspondence previously found in the physics literature
[
14
]
. See Section
1.3
for a rough version of our main theorem, or Section
3
for the detailed version.

Our work builds upon that of
[
4
]
, where the authors provide upper bound estimates (corresponding exactly to those in Section
4
) for various geometric and matter quantities. The improvement in the present paper is that such upper bounds are upgraded to precise leading-order asymptotics, thus allowing us to make the correspondence to Kasner-like singularities.

Another novel aspect of our work is that in order to provide an avenue within which one can study stable (i.e. subcritical) and unstable regimes, we allow also an electromagnetic field. This additional matter field is represented by a
2
-form
F\in\Omega^{2}(\mathcal{M})
, and has the energy-momentum tensor:

T_{\mu\nu}[F]=F_{\mu\rho}F_{\nu}^{\phantom{\nu}\rho}-\frac{1}{4}g_{\mu\nu}F^{\rho\sigma}F_{\rho\sigma}.

(1.7)

The equations of motion associated to
F
are given by Maxwell’s equations:

\nabla^{\nu}F_{\mu\nu}=0,\qquad\nabla_{[\lambda}F_{\mu\nu]}=0.

(1.8)

As we discuss in Section
1.5
, an electromagnetic field sources a similar instability to gravitational perturbations outside of symmetry, and the study of spacelike singularity formation becomes heavily linked to the subcritical regimes of
[
8
]
. It is of great interest to extend the ideas of
[
14
,
4
]
to the Einstein-Maxwell-scalar field model. In particular, the addition of the electromagnetic field will be a crucial component of future work, to be introduced in Section
1.7
.

We now outline the remainder of the introduction. In Section
1.2
, we provide a more thorough overview of the spherically symmetric system under investigation, with the aim of being able to state a rough version of our main theorems in Section
1.3
. In the following Sections
1.4
and
1.5
, we relate our main theorem to the heuristics of BKL
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
39
]
, where Section
1.5
emphasizes in particular the role played by the electromagnetic field. In Section
1.6
, we outline several of the important features of the proof, while in the remaining Sections
1.7
and
1.8
we provide mathematical context and propose several further directions of study, in particular we mention the expected stability properties of our singular spacetimes in Section
1.7
.

1.2
Einstein-scalar field in spherical symmetry

We now introduce the spherically symmetric Einstein-scalar field model in question. In a series of works
[
15
,
16
,
19
]
, Christodoulou studied this system (without electromagnetism) as a toy model for understanding the weak and strong cosmic censorship conjectures of Penrose
[
51
]
(or see
[
18
]
for a more modern treatment). After defining the key concepts associated to spherically symmetric spacetimes, we give a brief overview of Christodoulou’s conclusions.

A spherically symmetric spacetime
(\mathcal{M},g)
is such that there exists a group of isometries acting on
(\mathcal{M},g)
isomorphic to
SO(3)
, whose orbits are each copies of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
smoothly embedded in
\mathcal{M}
, outside of some orbits given by single points whose union we call the
center

\Gamma
.

Defining
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{M}/SO(3)
to be the quotient manifold, we can write
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{Q}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}
where
\mathcal{Q}
is a
1+1
-dimensional manifold with (possibly empty) boundary
\Gamma
, and
g
may be written as the warped product:

g=g_{\mathcal{Q}}+r^{2}d\sigma_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}=g_{\mathcal{Q}}+r^{2}(d\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta\,d{{\varphi^{2}}}).

Here
r\geq 0
is a function invariant under the
SO(3)
action known as the
area-radius
, vanishing precisely at
\Gamma
, while
g_{\mathcal{Q}}
is a
{{(1+1)}}
-dimensional Lorentzian metric on
\mathcal{Q}
.

Such a Lorentzian manifold
\mathcal{Q}
has local coordinate charts given by two null coordinates
(u,v)
, with respect to which the four dimensional metric
g
may be written as

g=-\Omega^{2}(u,v)\,dudv+r^{2}(u,v)\,d\sigma_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}.

(1.9)

We assume (as is the case for asymptotically flat one- or two-ended spacetimes) that this coordinate system on
\mathcal{Q}
is global. Hence, the geometry is described by two functions of
u
and
v
, the area-radius
r(u,v)
and the
null lapse

\Omega^{2}(u,v)
.

There is gauge freedom regarding the choice of the null coordinates
u
and
v
; given smooth and monotonically increasing
U=U(u)
and
V=V(v)
, we may alternatively write

g=-\tilde{\Omega}^{2}(U,V)\,dUdV+r^{2}(U,V)\,d\sigma_{\mathbb{S}^{2}},

so long as
\tilde{\Omega}^{2}(U(u),V(v))\cdot\frac{dU}{du}\frac{dV}{dv}=\Omega^{2}(u,v)
. It will often be useful to consider quantities that are
gauge-invariant
, such as the area-radius
r(u,v)
or the
Hawking mass

m(u,v)
, given by

1-\frac{2m}{r}\coloneqq g(\nabla r,\nabla r)=-4\Omega^{-2}\partial_{u}r\,\partial_{v}r.

(1.10)

We study the coupled system (
1.1
) and (
1.5
) for spherically symmetric metrics of the form (
1.9
). The system of evolution equations for
r(u,v)
,
\Omega^{2}(u,v)
and the scalar field
\phi(u,v)
with respect to these coordinates will be described in Section

.

Another new feature of the present article is the introduction of electromagnetism to the study of spacelike singularities in spherical symmetry. As mentioned previously, an electromagnetic field is represented by its Maxwell tensor, the

, and an energy-momentum tensor given by (

One then couples electromagnetism to gravity and scalar matter by studying the system (

. These results, however, are largely concerned with the existence and properties of the Cauchy horizon in the interior of black holes. Indeed, the groundbreaking result of

On the other hand, the objective of this present article is to study the behaviour of the scalar field and geometry when the singularity is no longer

spacelike
. This is more subtle than the model without electromagnetism, for reasons to be explained in Section

.

1.3
Precise asymptotics – rough version of our main theorems

We now present a rough version of our main theorems. The setup is as follows: we pose characteristic initial data for the spherically symmetric Einstein-Maxwell-scalar field equations (
1.1
), (
1.6
), (
1.8
) on the bifurcate null hypersurface
C_{0}\cup\underline{C}_{0}
, where
C_{0}
and
\underline{C}_{0}
are respectively outgoing and ingoing null cones.

By standard theory, we obtain a unique solution
(r,\Omega^{2},\phi,F)
to the above system of equations on a
maximal future domain of development

\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, attaining the prescribed data on
C_{0}\cup\underline{C}_{0}
, and such that
r,r^{-1},\log\Omega^{2},\phi
and
F
remain bounded and regular in compact subregions of
\mathcal{D}
. The domain
\mathcal{D}
is
globally hyperbolic
in the sense that any inextendible timelike curve in
\mathcal{D}
has a past endpoint on the initial data hypersurface
C_{0}\cup\underline{C}_{0}
. See already the Penrose diagram in Figure
3
.

Since we are interested in the
local
properties of spacelike singularities, we assume already that
C_{0}
and
\underline{C}_{0}
are foliated by trapped
2
-spheres i.e. that
\partial_{u}r<0
and
\partial_{v}r<0
on
C_{0}\cup\underline{C}_{0}
. By Raychaudhuri’s equations, this means that the maximal future development
\mathcal{D}
will be entirely foliated by trapped
2
-spheres.

For now, we suppose that
C_{0}
and
\underline{C}_{0}
are
regular
and
compact
; in particular, the area-radius
r(u,v)
restricted to
C_{0}\cup\underline{C}_{0}
remains bounded away from
0
.
We will, however, assume the initial data on
C_{0}\cup\underline{C}_{0}
to be such that the maximal future development
\mathcal{D}
will possess a future boundary which is an
\{r=0\}
singularity. That is, the entire future boundary will consist of the union of two regular null hypersurfaces (emanating from the future endpoints of
C_{0}
and
\underline{C}_{0}
) and a spacelike singularity
\mathcal{S}
at which
r
extends continuously to
0
.
We call such a maximal future development a

. This is shown pictorially in Figure

, and will be described in greater detail in Section

.

1.4
The BKL ansatz

We now briefly describe the ansatz for the near-singularity solutions of Einstein’s equations, proposed by Khalatnikov and Lifshitz in
[
39
]
and developed in the celebrated work of BKL
[
10
,
11
]
. Starting with vacuum,
[
39
]
considers the following
1+3
-decomposition of spacetime:

\mathcal{M}=(0,T)\times\Sigma,\qquad g=-dt^{2}+{}^{(3)}h(t).

(1.14)

Here
{}^{(3)}h(t)
is a time-dependent smooth Riemannian metric on the
3
-manifold
\Sigma
.

Influenced by the Kasner metric (
1.3
),
[
39
]
proposes the following ansatz for
g
in the neighborhood of a singularity at
\{t=0\}
: for
(t,x)\in\mathcal{M}
they write

g(t,x)\approx-dt^{2}+\sum_{I=1}^{3}t^{2p_{I}(x)}\omega^{I}(x)\otimes\omega^{I}(x).

(1.15)

In (
1.15
),
\omega^{I}(x)
are smooth
1
-forms on
\Sigma
spanning the cotangent space
T_{x}^{*}\Sigma
, while the exponents
p_{I}(x)
are smooth functions on
\Sigma
satisfying the two
Kasner relations
:

\displaystyle\sum_{I=1}^{3}p_{I}(x)=1,\qquad\sum_{I=1}^{3}p_{I}^{2}(x)=1.

(1.16)

The time coordinate
t
is chosen such that the singularity is synchronized at
t=0
.

Assume for now that
p_{1}(x)<p_{2}(x)<p_{3}(x)
. We define
e_{I}(x)
to be the vector fields dual to
\omega^{I}(x)
, and use the following notational convention: for any
(p,q)
-tensor field
T
defined on
\Sigma
we use lower case indices
T_{i_{1}\cdots i_{p}}^{\phantom{i_{1}\cdots i_{p}}j_{1}\cdots j_{q}}
to denote the tensor field evaluated using the coordinate vector fields
\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}
and their respective coordinate
1
-forms
dx^{i}
, while upper case indices
T_{I_{1}\cdots I_{p}}^{\phantom{I_{1}\cdots I_{p}}J_{1}\cdots J_{q}}
denote the tensor field evaluated using the frame fields
e_{I}(x),\omega^{I}(x)
. Indices are raised and lowered with respect to the Riemannian metric
{}^{(3)}h={}^{(3)}h(t)
.

In the gauge (
1.14
), the evolutionary components of Einstein’s equation (
1.1
) yield the following:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}{}^{(3)}h_{ij}=-2k_{ij},

(1.17)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det({}^{(3)}h_{pq})}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\sqrt{\det({}^{(3)} h_{pq})}k_{i}^{\phantom{i}j}\right)={}^{(3)}\mathrm{Ric}[{}^{(3)}h]_{i}^{\phantom{i}j}-2T_{i}^{\phantom{i}j}+\delta_{i}^{\phantom{i}j}\left(T_{0}^{\phantom{0}0}+T_{k}^{\phantom{k}k}\right).

(1.18)

The metric on the right hand side of (
1.15
) solves (
1.17
) and (
1.18
), if one assumes the right hand side of (
1.18
) to be absent. Hence a necessary condition for (
1.15
) to be self-consistent in vacuum (with
T_{\mu\nu}=0
) is that the expression
{}^{(3)}\mathrm{Ric}[{}^{(3)}h]
is suitably small. In
[
39
]
, the authors argue that the required smallness is that for some
\epsilon>0
,

|{}^{(3)}\mathrm{Ric}[{}^{(3)}h]_{IJ}|=O(t^{p_{I}+p_{J}-2+\epsilon}).

(1.19)

On the other hand, a computation of spatial Ricci curvature with respect to the (approximate) orthonormal frame
t^{-p_{I}(x)}e_{I}(x)
allows one to obtain the following asymptotics for
{}^{(3)}\mathrm{Ric}[{}^{(3)}h]_{IJ}
, where
\lambda_{IJ}^{K}
are the structure coefficients of the frame,
d\omega^{K}=\lambda_{IJ}^{K}\,\omega^{I}\wedge\omega^{J},\;\lambda^{K}_{IJ}=-\lambda^{K}_{JI}
.

\left|t^{-p_{I}-p_{J}+2}\cdot{}^{(3)}\mathrm{Ric}[{}^{(3)}h]_{IJ}\right|=(\lambda^{1}_{23})^{2}\cdot t^{2(1+p_{1}-p_{2}-p_{3})}\cdot\delta_{IJ}+O(t^{\epsilon})

(1.20)

Since
1+p_{1}-p_{2}-p_{3}=2p_{1}<0
(see for instance (
1.4
) with
u\geq 1
),
{}^{(3)}\mathrm{Ric}[{}^{(3)}h]
obeys (
1.19
) if and only if the structure coefficient
\lambda^{1}_{23}(x)
vanishes identically on
\Sigma
.
[
39
]
then argues that this prohibits the ansatz (
1.15
) from having the correct number of physical degrees of freedom. Note that
\lambda^{1}_{23}(x)=0
holds if and only if the one-form
\omega^{1}
is hypersurface orthogonal, and in particular holds in spacetimes with certain symmetries, including spherical symmetry.

In
[
10
]
, BKL further provide heuristics arguing that if the term (
1.20
) is present, then there exists some critical time
t_{c}
, such that the metric will dynamically “bounce” from the approximate form (
1.15
) for times
t\gg t_{c}
, to another approximate form (
1.15
) for times
t\ll t_{c}
, but with modified Kasner exponents

\tilde{p}_{1}(x)=-\frac{p_{1}(x)}{1+2p_{1}(x)},\quad\tilde{p}_{2}(x)=\frac{p_{2}(x)+2p_{1}(x)}{1+2p_{1}(x)},\quad\tilde{p}_{3}(x)=\frac{p_{3}(x)+2p_{1}(x)}{1+2p_{1}(x)}.

(1.21)

The new exponents
\tilde{p}_{I}
still obey (
1.16
), hence this new Kasner regime is subject to a similar inconsistency.
[
10
,
11
]
then propose that the generic eventual approach to a spacelike singularity involves an infinite cascade of such
Kasner bounces
, and is known in the literature as the
BKL oscillatory approach to singularity
.

Rigorous work concerning the proposed oscillatory dynamics will, in practise, involve controlling wildly behaved error terms. It is thus desirable to find regimes, possibly involving matter, where the instability mechanism can be suppressed. One such regime appears in
[
8
]
, where the authors find that matter described by a massless scalar field indeed has this property.

In
[
8
]
, the authors propose the same near-singularity ansatz (
1.15
) for the metric, along with the following ansatz for the scalar field
\phi
:

\phi(t,x)\approx A(x)\log t+B(x).

(1.22)

In order for (
1.15
) and (
1.22
) to solve the Einstein-scalar field equations asymptotically, the Kasner relations (
1.16
) are modified to

\sum_{I=1}^{3}p_{I}(x)=1,\qquad\sum_{I=1}^{3}p_{I}(x)^{2}+2A(x)^{2}=1.

(1.23)

The key observation made in
[
8
]
is that the modified Kasner relations (
1.23
) allow for exponents with
0<p_{1}(x)<p_{2}(x)<p_{3}(x)
. In such regimes,
1+p_{1}-p_{2}-p_{3}=2p_{1}
is now positive, and the previously problematic term (
1.20
) can be treated as an error.

It can also be shown that the energy-momentum tensor terms
T_{i}^{\phantom{i}j}
and
T_{0}^{\phantom{0}0}
appearing on the right hand side of (
1.18
) combine to produce an error which can similarly be treated as negligible. The conclusion is that (
1.15
) and (
1.22
) produce self-consistent asymptotics near a
t=0
singularity, so long as the exponents lie in the so-called
subcritical regime
where
\min p_{I}(x)>0
. In the context of Theorem
I
and (
1.13
), this corresponds to
\inf\Psi^{2}(u,v)>1
.

As remarked earlier, within spherical symmetry, the subcriticality assumption is not necessary with or without a scalar field. In order to observe such instabilities, and the necessity of studying such subcritical regimes, we add another matter source, namely an electromagnetic field.

1.5
The role of electromagnetism

Recall once again that an electromagnetic field is described by a
1
-form
A_{\mu}
and its corresponding Maxwell field
F=dA
. Being a
2
-form, the Maxwell field
F_{\mu\nu}
can be considered as an anisotropic matter source – unlike the scalar field
\phi
– and could potentially mimic the effects of gravitational perturbations outside of symmetry. So one could ask: how exactly does this arise in our setting?

In the context of the BKL ansatz (
1.15
), electromagnetic matter was studied by Belinski and Khalatnikov
[
9
]
(see also Chapter 4 of
[
7
]
), where alongside the ansatz
(
1.15
) for the metric, the authors suggest the following asymptotics for the electromagnetic field:

E^{I}(x,t)\approx\frac{1}{t}\Phi_{E}^{I}(x),\qquad B^{I}(x,t)\approx\frac{1}{t}\Phi_{B}^{I}(x).

(1.24)

Note that here we define

E_{I}(x,t)\coloneqq F\left({\partial_{t}},e_{I}\right)
and
B_{I}(x,t)\coloneqq*F\left(\partial_{t},e_{I}\right)

to be the usual
1+3
electric-magnetic decomposition of
F_{\mu\nu}
, evaluated with respect to the spatial frame
\{e_{I}\}
.

However, even (
1.24
) turns out to be inconsistent outside the subcritical regime
\min\{p_{I}(x)\}>0
. Assuming
p_{1}(x)<p_{2}(x)<p_{3}(x)
, the
e_{1}(x)
projections of Maxwell’s equations (
1.8
) in the BKL ansatz yield:

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(tE^{1}(x))\approx-(\lambda^{1}_{23})^{2}t^{2p_{1}-1}\cdot tB^{1}(x),\quad\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(tB^{1}(x))\approx+(\lambda^{1}_{23})^{2}t^{2p_{1}-1}\cdot tE^{1}(x).

(1.25)

Hence if
p_{1}<0
the right-hand side would appear to be non-integrable.

Nonetheless, the system (
1.25
) does suggest that the expression

\omega^{2}(t,x)\coloneqq t^{2}[E^{1}(x)^{2}+B^{1}(x)^{2}]

(1.26)

is (approximately) constant in time, and, abusing notation slightly, we set
\omega^{2}(x)=\omega^{2}(t,x)
in the following discussion.

As well as
{}^{(3)}\mathrm{Ric}[{}^{(3)}h]
, we must now also estimate the matter term on the right hand side of (
1.18
). Writing
T_{\mu\nu}^{EM}
in terms of
E^{i}
and
B^{i}
, one finds the following expression:

-2T_{ij}+{}^{(3)}h_{ij}(T_{0}^{\phantom{0}0}+T_{k}^{\phantom{k}k})=2(E_{i}E_{j}+B_{i}B_{j})-{}^{(3)}h_{ij}(E_{k}E^{k}+B_{k}B^{k}),

which in light of (
1.26
) – the contributions of
E_{I}
and
B_{I}
for
I=2,3
are part of the error – yields

\left|t^{-p_{I}-p_{J}+2}\cdot[-2T_{IJ}+{}^{(3)}h_{IJ}(T_{0}^{\phantom{0}0}+T_{k}^{\phantom{k}k})]\right|=\omega^{2}\cdot t^{2p_{1}}\delta_{IJ}+O(t^{\epsilon}).

(1.27)

The term (
1.27
) with
\omega\neq 0
sources a similar instability to that of (
1.20
) with
\lambda^{1}_{23}\neq 0
. Perhaps more remarkably, the analysis of
[
9
]
suggests that as long as either
\omega
or
\lambda^{1}_{23}
fails to vanish, we obtain a Kasner bounce with the transition map (
1.21
). This concludes our brief explanation of why the electromagnetic field acts as a proxy for spatial curvature in the BKL setting.

To see how these ideas are manifested in the spherically symmetric setting, we note that in a regular double null gauge
(u,v)
, one of the components of Einstein’s equation (see already (
2.12
)) is

\partial_{u}(-r\partial_{v}r)\approx-\frac{Q^{2}\Omega^{2}}{4r^{2}},

(1.28)

at least in the vicinity of
\{r=0\}
, where
Q
is a constant quantifying the strength of the Maxwell field.

From (
I
) of Theorem
I
, we expect that
-r\partial_{u}r
and
-r\partial_{v}r
are positive and bounded away from
0
. Further, recalling (
1.11
), we integrate (
1.28
) towards
r=0
and change variables, finding

\displaystyle-r\partial_{v}r

\displaystyle\approx\text{ data }-\int\frac{Q^{2}\Omega^{2}}{4r^{2}}\,du,

\displaystyle\approx\text{ data }-\int Q^{2}\cdot C(u)\cdot r^{\Psi^{2}-3}\,du

\displaystyle\approx\text{ data }-\int\frac{Q^{2}\cdot C(u)}{-r\partial_{u}r}\cdot r^{\Psi^{2}-2}\,dr.

Now, the expression preceding
r^{\Psi^{2}-2}
in the integrand is bounded and tends to a positive constant as
r\to 0
, so it suffices to consider the integrability of
r^{\Psi^{2}-2}
. The key observation is that while
r^{\Psi^{2}-2}
is indeed integrable near
r=0
for
\Psi^{2}>1
, this fails to be true when instead
\Psi^{2}\leq 1
. Furthermore, this computation would suggest that in the latter case,
-r\partial_{v}r
would eventually become positive, which contradicts the fact that we lie in a trapped region!

Therefore, when
Q\neq 0
, spacelike singularity formation is only expected when the quantity
\Psi
obeys
\Psi^{2}>1
. By (
1.13
), this indeed corresponds to the subcritical regime of Kasner exponents as expected. In the present article, we impose
\Psi^{2}>1
whenever
Q\neq 0
via the additional assumption (
SKE
).

1.6
Ideas of the proof

We now briefly describe several of the key steps in the proof of Theorem
I
. For simplicity, we focus on a single point
p\in\mathcal{S}
and its causal past
J^{-}(p)
. The essential claims will be that for any
(u,v)\in J^{-}(p)
, there exists some
\alpha>0
such that:

(I)

There exist positive constants
C_{u},C_{v}>0
such that in
J^{-}(p)
, one has

-r\partial_{u}r=C_{u}+O(r^{\alpha}),\qquad-r\partial_{v}r=C_{v}+O(r^{\alpha}).

(II)

There exist constants
\Psi_{\infty},\Xi_{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}
such that in
J^{-}(p)
, one has

r^{2}\partial_{u}\phi=C_{u}\cdot\Psi_{\infty}+O(r^{\alpha}),\qquad r^{2}\partial_{v}\phi=C_{v}\cdot\Psi_{\infty}+O(r^{\alpha}),

\phi=\Psi_{\infty}\log r^{-1}+\Xi_{\infty}+O(r^{\alpha}).

(III)

There exists some
C_{\Omega}>0
such that in
J^{-}(p)
, one has

\Omega^{2}=C_{\Omega}\cdot r^{\Psi_{\infty}^{2}-1}+O(r^{\Psi_{\infty}^{2}-1+\alpha}).

In the full proof, each of these must be shown simultaneously, but for the purpose of illustrating the ideas we simply sketch a proof of each of (
I
)–(
III
) assuming the others.

We start with (
III
), which is proven in Section
6.1
, particularly Proposition
6.1
. The idea is to rewrite the Raychaudhuri equation (see already (
2.9
)) as:

\partial_{v}\log\left(\frac{\Omega^{2}}{-\partial_{v}r}\right)=\frac{\partial_{v}r}{r}\cdot\left(\frac{r^{2}\partial_{v}\phi}{-r\partial_{v}r}\right)^{2}.

Assuming (
I
) and (
II
), the rightmost expression can be written as
\Psi_{\infty}^{2}+O(r^{\alpha})
. Therefore, subtracting
\Psi_{\infty}^{2}\cdot\partial_{v}(\log r)=\Psi_{\infty}^{2}\cdot\frac{\partial_{v}r}{r}
from both sides, one finds

\partial_{v}\log\left(\frac{\Omega^{2}}{-\partial_{v}r}\cdot r^{-\Psi_{\infty}^{2}}\right)=\partial_{v}r\cdot O(r^{\alpha-1}).

The right hand side is now integrable towards
r=0
, hence
\frac{\Omega^{2}}{-\partial_{v}r}\cdot r^{-\Psi_{\infty}^{2}}
tends to some positive constant as
r\to 0
. Combining again with (
I
), and performing similar integrations in the
u
-direction, we indeed find (
III
).

Next, we move onto (
I
), see already (
6.14
) in Section
6.2
(note though that in the course of our actual proof we largely circumvent the need for (
I
) by using instead the gauge-invariant derivatives (
2.18
)). For this, we use the following wave-like evolution equation for
r^{2}(u,v)
:

\partial_{u}(-r\partial_{v}r)=\partial_{v}(-r\partial_{u}r)=\frac{\Omega^{2}}{4}\left(1-\frac{Q^{2}}{r^{2}}\right).

But by (
III
), we may bound
\Omega^{2}
by a power of
r
, giving

\partial_{u}(-r\partial_{v}r)=\begin{cases}O(r^{\Psi_{\infty}^{2}-1})&\text{ if }Q\equiv 0,\\
O(r^{\Psi_{\infty}^{2}-3})&\text{ if }Q\not\equiv 0.\end{cases}

To integrate this, we change variables from
u
to
r
. This is where we use (
QTS
), which tells us that
du=O(r)dr
. Furthermore, when
Q\neq 0
, we require
\Psi_{\infty}^{2}>1
in order for the integral of
O(r^{\Psi^{2}-3})
in
u
not to diverge as
r\to 0
– justifying why we need the assumption (
SKE
). If either
Q\equiv 0
, or
Q\neq 0
but (
QTS
) and (
SKE
) hold, then we get (
I
) as claimed.

We remark here that the
O(r^{\alpha})
error term in (
I
) is not optimal, and that the true first-order correction will be
O(r^{\gamma})
with
\gamma
depending on
\Psi_{\infty}^{2}
. However, for our goal of deriving the leading order asymptotics, our
O(r^{\alpha})
errors are sufficient.

Finally, we derive (
II
), giving asymptotics for the main dynamical quantity
\phi(u,v)
. This is the main objective of Section
5
, particularly Proposition
5.4
. There, we use the wave equation in the following form:

\partial_{u}(r^{2}\partial_{v}\phi)=-r\partial_{v}r\cdot\partial_{u}\phi+r\partial_{u}r\cdot\partial_{v}\phi=\tilde{X}\phi,

where
\tilde{X}
is defined as
\tilde{X}=-r\partial_{v}r\partial_{u}+r\partial_{u}r\partial_{v}
.
The procedure will be to first estimate
|\tilde{X}\phi|
and then integrate this equation towards
r=0
.

The fact
\tilde{X}\phi
appears is crucial. If the vector field
2
2

2

The vector field
\tilde{X}

\tilde{X}

The reason for this is that since

Indeed, we shall obtain

This concludes our sketch of the proof of Theorem

(Disappearance of singularity.)

(Gauge degeneration.)

\adjustlimits{\liminf}_{r_{*}\to 0}{\sup}_{r(p)\leq r_{*}}\hat{\Psi}^{2}(p)\geq 1.

We next use the EMSFSS system to determine null transport equations for the weighted quantities

(4.7)

\partial_{v}(r\underline{L}\phi)=\frac{\partial_{v}\phi}{r}-\frac{\Omega^{2}}{-4r\partial_{u}r}\left(1-\frac{Q^{2}}{r^{2}}\right)\cdot r\underline{L}\phi.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
(u,v)\in\mathcal{D}
for either (A) the ESFSS system with general initial data, or (B) the EMSFSS system supplemented with (
QTS
) and(
SKE
), one has

|r^{2}L\phi(u,v)|\leq D,\qquad|r^{2}\underline{L}\phi(u,v)|\leq D.

(4.9)

Proof.

Define the function
A(r)
to be

A(\tilde{r})=\sup_{\tilde{r}\leq\hat{r}\leq r_{0}}\max\,\left\{\max_{(u,v)\in\Sigma_{\hat{r}}}|rL\phi(u,v)|,\max_{(u,v)\in\Sigma_{\hat{r}}}|r\underline{L}\phi(u,v)|\,\right\}.

The objective is to show that
A(r)\leq D/r
for some constant
D
depending only on data,
R_{0}
and
\alpha
.

We integrate (
4.7
) in the interval
[u_{0},u]
; one finds after a change of variables
\tilde{u}\mapsto r(\tilde{u},v)
that

\displaystyle rL\phi(u,v)

\displaystyle=\int_{u_{0}}^{u}\left[\frac{\partial_{u}\phi}{r}(\tilde{u},v)-\frac{\Omega^{2}}{-4r\partial_{v}r}\left(1-\frac{Q^{2}}{r^{2}}\right)\cdot rL\phi(\tilde{u},v)\right]\,d\tilde{u}+rL\phi(u_{0},v),

\displaystyle=\int_{r(u_{0},v)}^{r(u,v)}\left[-\frac{1}{r}\cdot r\underline{L}\phi-\frac{\Omega^{2}}{-4r\partial_{u}r\partial_{v}r}\left(1-\frac{Q^{2}}{r^{2}}\right)\cdot rL\phi\right]\,dr(\tilde{u},v)+rL\phi(u_{0},v).

One deduces from this the integral inequality

|rL\phi(u,v)|\leq\int^{r_{0}}_{r(u,v)}\left[\frac{1}{\hat{r}}+\sup_{(\tilde{u},\tilde{v})\in\Sigma_{\hat{r}}}\frac{\Omega^{2}}{-4r\partial_{u}r\partial_{v}r}\cdot\left|\,1-\frac{Q^{2}}{r^{2}}\,\right|(\tilde{u},\tilde{v})\right]\cdot A(\hat{r})\,d\hat{r}+\sup_{\tilde{v}\in[v_{0},v_{1}]}|rL\phi(u_{0},\tilde{v})|.

A similar integral inequality can be found for
|r\underline{L}\phi(u,v)|
. Taking the maximum over
(u,v)\in\Sigma_{\tilde{r}}
, we find

A(\tilde{r})\leq\int_{\tilde{r}}^{r_{0}}\left[\frac{1}{\hat{r}}+\sup_{(\tilde{u},\tilde{v})\in\Sigma_{\hat{r}}}\frac{\Omega^{2}}{-4r\partial_{u}r\partial_{v}r}\cdot\left|\,1-\frac{Q^{2}}{r^{2}}\,\right|(\tilde{u},\tilde{v})\right]\cdot A(\hat{r})\,d\hat{r}+\sup_{C_{0}\cup\underline{C}_{0}}\max\{|rL\phi|,|r\underline{L}\phi|\}.

(4.10)

The last term is controlled by data, and we now apply Grönwall’s inequality. The
1/\hat{r}
appearing in this inequality is responsible for the
1/\tilde{r}
growth of
A(\tilde{r})
, while the term involving
\Omega^{2}
will be controlled by Lemma
4.1
:

\frac{\Omega^{2}}{-4r\partial_{u}r\partial_{v}r}\cdot\left|\,1-\frac{Q^{2}}{r^{2}}\,\right|\lesssim\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }Q=0,\\
r^{\alpha-1}&\text{ if }Q\neq 0\text{ but (\ref{eq:cond_r}) holds}.\end{cases}

(4.11)

Since
\alpha>0
, this expression is integrable in
r
as
r\to 0
, and there exists some
\tilde{D}
, such that

\int^{r_{0}}_{\tilde{r}}\left[\frac{1}{\hat{r}}+\sup_{(\tilde{u},\tilde{v})\in\Sigma_{\hat{r}}}\frac{\Omega^{2}}{-4r\partial_{u}r\partial_{v}r}\cdot\left|\,1-\frac{Q^{2}}{r^{2}}\,\right|(\tilde{u},\tilde{v})\right]\,d\hat{r}\leq\log\left(\frac{r_{0}}{\tilde{r}}\right)+\tilde{D}.

(4.12)

Hence applying Grönwall to (
4.10
) and using this estimate (
4.12
), we find that

A(\tilde{r})\leq e^{\tilde{D}}\cdot\sup_{C_{0}\cup\underline{C}_{0}}\max\{|rL\phi|,|r\underline{L}\phi|\}\cdot\frac{r_{0}}{\tilde{r}}.

(4.13)

Multiplying both sides by
\tilde{r}
, we have a uniform bound on
|r^{2}L\phi(u,v)|
and
|r^{2}\underline{L}\phi(u,v)|
as required.
∎

Remark
.

) in the

4.3
Upper bound estimates for general inhomogeneous wave equations

We prove a result, analogous to Proposition
4.2
, producing sharp upper bounds for scalar quantities
\psi
satisfying a (possibly inhomogeneous) wave equation in the region
\mathcal{D}
. This will be useful when commuting the wave equation with various vector fields, as will be necessary in Section
4.4
and Section
5
.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\psi
be a function on
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
obeying the following inhomogeneous wave equation:

\partial_{u}\partial_{v}\psi=-\frac{\partial_{u}r\cdot\partial_{v}\psi}{r}-\frac{\partial_{v}r\cdot\partial_{u}\psi}{r}+F.

(4.14)

Here
F
is a function of
(u,v)\in\mathcal{D}
, such that for some
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
and
F_{0}>0
fixed,
F
satisfies:

\left|\frac{F(u,v)}{\partial_{u}r\,\partial_{v}r}\right|\leq F_{0}\,r^{\gamma}(u,v).

(4.15)

Then there exists a constant
C
, depending only upon the initial data for
(\Omega^{2},r,\phi,Q)
at
C_{0}\cup\underline{C}_{0}
, the exponent
\gamma
, and the quantities
R_{0}
and
\alpha
in (
QTS
) and (
SKE
) when
Q\neq 0
, such that we have the following estimates for the first derivatives
L\psi
and
\underline{L}\psi
:

|r^{2}L\psi(u,v)|+|r^{2}\underline{L}\psi(u,v)|\leq C\sup_{C_{0}\cup\underline{C}_{0}}\max\{|rL\psi|,|r\underline{L}\psi|\}+{CF_{0}}\cdot\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }\gamma>-2,\\
\log\left(\frac{r_{0}}{r(u,v)}\right)&\text{ if }\gamma=-2,\\
r(u,v)^{\gamma+2}&\text{ if }\gamma<-2.\end{cases}

(4.16)

Furthermore, the quantity
\psi
itself obeys the following estimate:

|\psi(u,v)|\leq|\psi(u_{0},v_{0})|+C\sup_{C_{0}\cup\underline{C}_{0}}\max\{|rL\psi|,|r\underline{L}\psi|\}\log\left(\frac{r_{0}}{r(u,v)}\right)+{CF_{0}}\cdot\begin{cases}\log\left(\frac{r_{0}}{r(u,v)}\right)&\text{ if }\gamma>-2,\\
\log^{2}\left(\frac{r_{0}}{r(u,v)}\right)&\text{ if }\gamma=-2,\\
r(u,v)^{\gamma+2}&\text{ if }\gamma<-2.\end{cases}

(4.17)

Proof.

The proof follows that of Proposition
4.2
. We first rewrite (
4.14
) as propagation equations for the quantities
rL\psi
and
r\underline{L}\psi
. One finds

\partial_{u}(rL\psi)=+\frac{\partial_{u}\psi}{r}-\frac{\Omega^{2}}{-4\partial_{v}r}\left(1-\frac{Q^{2}}{r^{2}}\right)\cdot rL\psi+\frac{F}{-\partial_{v}r},

(4.18)

\partial_{v}(r\underline{L}\psi)=+\frac{\partial_{v}\psi}{r}-\frac{\Omega^{2}}{-4\partial_{u}r}\left(1-\frac{Q^{2}}{r^{2}}\right)\cdot r\underline{L}\psi+\frac{F}{-\partial_{u}r}.

(4.19)

One now integrates (
4.18
) and (
4.19
) from
C_{0}
and
\underline{C}_{0}
respectively. Integrating (
4.18
) and changing variables to
r(\tilde{u},v)
as before yields:

rL\psi(u,v)=\int_{r(u_{0},v)}^{r(u,v)}\left[-\frac{1}{r}\cdot r\underline{L}\psi-\frac{\Omega^{2}}{-4r\partial_{u}r\partial_{v}r}\left(1-\frac{Q^{2}}{r^{2}}\right)\cdot rL\psi\right]\,dr(\tilde{u},v)\\
+rL\psi(u_{0},v)+\int_{r(u_{0},v)}^{r(u,v)}\frac{F}{-\partial_{u}r\partial_{v}r}\,dr(\tilde{u},v).

(4.20)

We wish to find an integral inequality similar to (
4.10
). To simplify notation, we define

\beta(\tilde{r})=\frac{1}{\tilde{r}}+\sup_{(\tilde{u},\tilde{v})\in\Sigma_{\tilde{r}}}\frac{\Omega^{2}}{-4r\partial_{u}r\partial_{v}r}\cdot\left|1-\frac{Q^{2}}{r^{2}}\right|(\tilde{u},\tilde{v}).

(4.21)

Due to (
4.11
), there exist constants
D_{0}<D_{1}
depending only on the data
(r,\Omega^{2},\phi,Q)
at
C_{0}\cup\underline{C}_{0}
as before, such that we have the following bounds:

\frac{D_{0}}{\tilde{r}}\leq\beta(r)\leq\frac{D_{1}}{\tilde{r}},

(4.22)

\frac{D_{0}r_{0}}{\tilde{r}}\leq\gamma(\tilde{r})\coloneqq\exp(\int^{r_0}_{\tilde{r}} \beta(\hat{r}) \, d \hat{r})\leq\frac{D_{1}r_{0}}{\tilde{r}}.

(4.23)

We also use (
4.15
) to bound the rightmost term appearing in (
4.20
). To avoid having to distinguish cases further based on whether
\gamma\geq-1
, we write

\int_{r(u_{0},v)}^{r(u,v)}\frac{F}{-\partial_{u}r\partial_{v}r}\,dr(\tilde{u},v)\leq CF_{0}\,r(u,v)^{\min\{\gamma+1,-1/2\}}.

(4.24)

We can now write down the inequality analogous to (
4.10
) in Proposition
4.2
. Define

A_{\psi}(\tilde{r})=\sup_{\tilde{r}\leq\hat{r}\leq r_{0}}\max\,\left\{\max_{(u,v)\in\Sigma_{\hat{r}}}|rL\psi(u,v)|,\max_{(u,v)\in\Sigma_{\hat{r}}}|r\underline{L}\psi(u,v)|\,\right\}.

Then using (
4.20
) and the corresponding equation for
r\underline{L}\psi
, as well as (
4.24
), one finds

A_{\psi}(\tilde{r})\leq\int^{r_{0}}_{\tilde{r}}\beta(\hat{r})A_{\psi}(\hat{r})\,d\hat{r}+P_{0}+CF_{0}\,\tilde{r}^{\min\{\gamma+1,-1/2\}}.

(4.25)

Here
P_{0}=\sup_{C_{0}\cup\underline{C}_{0}}\max\{|rL\psi|,|r\underline{L}\psi|\}
is the term arising from the initial data for
\psi
.

Since the usual integral Grönwall inequality applied to (
4.25
) would produce a weaker estimate than the required (
4.16
), we instead use (
4.25
) to produce a differential inequality for the quantity

B_{\psi}(\tilde{r})=\int^{r_{0}}_{\tilde{r}}\beta(\hat{r})A_{\psi}(\hat{r})\,d\hat{r}.

From (
4.25
), we find the following inequality for
B_{\psi}(\tilde{r})
:

\frac{d}{d\tilde{r}}B_{\psi}(\tilde{r})=-\beta(\tilde{r})A_{\psi}(\tilde{r})\geq-\beta(\tilde{r})B_{\psi}(\tilde{r})-P_{0}\,{{\beta(\tilde{r})}}-CF_{0}\,\beta(\tilde{r})\,\tilde{r}^{\min\{\gamma+1,-1/2\}}.

Defining
\gamma(\tilde{r})
as in (
4.23
), one thus finds

\frac{d}{d\tilde{r}}(\gamma^{-1}(\tilde{r})B_{\psi}(\tilde{r}))\geq-P_{0}\gamma^{-1}(\tilde{r}){{\beta(\tilde{r})}}-CF_{0}\gamma^{-1}(\tilde{r})\beta(\tilde{r})\,\tilde{r}^{\min\{\gamma+1,-1/2\}}.

(4.26)

Next, we integrate (
4.26
) backwards in
\tilde{r}
, and use that, from (
4.22
) and (
4.23
), we have
\gamma^{-1}(\tilde{r})\sim\tilde{r}
and
\beta(\tilde{r})\sim\tilde{r}^{-1}
, to get

\tilde{r}B_{\psi}(\tilde{r})\leq C\left(P_{0}r_{0}+\int^{r_{0}}_{r}F_{0}\tilde{r}^{\min\{\gamma+1,-1/2\}}\,d\tilde{r}\right).

Thus, distinguishing cases based on the integrability of
r^{\min\{\gamma+1,-1/2\}}
, we find that

\tilde{r}B_{\psi}(\tilde{r})\leq CP_{0}{{r_{0}}}+{CF_{0}}\cdot\begin{cases}{{r_{0}^{\min\{\gamma+2,1/2\}}}}&\text{ if }\gamma>-2,\\
\log\left(\frac{r_{0}}{\tilde{r}}\right)&\text{ if }\gamma=-2,\\
\tilde{r}^{\gamma+2}&\text{ if }\gamma<-2.\end{cases}

(4.27)

Inserting (
4.27
) into (
4.25
), it is evident that the
\tilde{r}B_{\psi}(\tilde{r})
in the left hand side of (
4.27
) can be replaced by
\tilde{r}A_{\psi}(\tilde{r})
, at the cost of increasing the constant
C
. Upon also absorbing any powers of
r_{0}
into this constant
C
, this estimate is exactly the desired (
4.16
).

To find the final estimate (
4.17
) for
\psi
itself, note that the vector fields
rL
and
r\underline{L}
are morally
\frac{d}{dr}
-derivatives, taken in null directions. To make this concrete, given
G(r)
any real function of
r
and
\zeta:\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
a
C^{1}
function satisfying
|rL\zeta(u,v)|,|r\underline{L}\zeta(u,v)|\leq G(r(u,v))
, then

\displaystyle|\zeta(u,v)|

\displaystyle\leq|\zeta(u_{0},v_{0})|+|\zeta(u_{0},v)-\zeta(u_{0},v_{0})|+|\zeta(u,v)-\zeta(u_{0},v)|,

\displaystyle\leq|\zeta(u_{0},v_{0})|+\int^{v}_{v_{0}}|\partial_{v}\zeta(u_{0},\tilde{v})|\,d\tilde{v}+\int^{u}_{u_{0}}|\partial_{u}\zeta(\tilde{u},v)|\,d\tilde{u},

\displaystyle=|\zeta(u_{0},v_{0})|+\int^{r(u_{0},v)}_{r_{0}}|rL\zeta(u_{0},\tilde{v})|\,dr(u_{0},\tilde{v})+\int^{r(u,v)}_{r(u_{0},v)}|r\underline{L}\zeta(\tilde{u},v)|\,dr(\tilde{u},v),

\displaystyle\leq|\zeta(u_{0},v_{0})|+\int^{r_{0}}_{r(u,v)}G(\tilde{r})\,d\tilde{r}.

(4.28)

Using (
4.16
) and applying (
4.28
) with
\zeta=\psi
, we deduce (
4.17
) as claimed.
∎

4.4
Higher order estimates

As in Section 7 of
[
4
]
, it will be important in later sections to control higher derivatives of the quantities
(r,\Omega^{2},\phi)
. We start with the geometric quantities
r
and
\Omega^{2}
:

Proposition 4.4
.

We have the following estimates for derivatives of
\Omega^{2}(u,v)
:

(A)

For the ESFSS system, there exists a constant
D
such that

|\partial_{u}\Omega^{2}|\leq\frac{D}{r^{3}(u,v)},\qquad|\partial_{v}\Omega^{2}|\leq\frac{D}{r^{3}(u,v)}.

(4.29)

(B)

For the EMSFSS system supplemented with
QTS
) and (
SKE
), we have instead that:

|\partial_{u}\Omega^{2}|\leq\frac{D}{r^{4-\alpha}(u,v)},\qquad|\partial_{v}\Omega^{2}|\leq\frac{D}{r^{4-\alpha}(u,v)}.

(4.30)

Moving onto estimates for derivatives of
r^{2}(u,v)
, we have

(A)

For the ESFSS system, with
D
as above, we have

|\partial_{u}(-r\partial_{v}r)|=|\partial_{v}(-r\partial_{u}r)|\leq\frac{D}{r(u,v)},

(4.31)

|\partial_{u}(-r\partial_{u}r)|\leq\frac{D}{r(u,v)},\qquad|\partial_{v}(-r\partial_{v}r)|\leq\frac{D}{r(u,v)}.

(4.32)

(B)

For the EMSFSS system, with
D
as above, we have

|\partial_{u}(-r\partial_{v}r)|=|\partial_{v}(-r\partial_{u}r)|\leq\frac{D}{r^{2-\alpha}(u,v)},

(4.33)

|\partial_{u}(-r\partial_{u}r)|\leq\frac{D}{r^{2-\alpha}(u,v)},\qquad|\partial_{v}(-r\partial_{v}r)|\leq\frac{D}{r^{2-\alpha}(u,v)}.

(4.34)

(C)

In both cases, one also has

|\partial_{u}\partial_{u}r|\leq\frac{D}{r^{3}(u,v)},\qquad|\partial_{u}\partial_{v}r|\leq\frac{D}{r^{3}(u,v)},\qquad|\partial_{v}\partial_{v}r|\leq\frac{D}{r^{3}(u,v)}.

(4.35)

Remark
.

Regarding (
4.29
)–(
4.35
), due to (
2.19

\partial_{u},\partial_{v}

Proof.

Consider the evolution equation (
2.11

4.1

(4.36)

\displaystyle|\partial_{v}\log\Omega^{2}|(u,v)

\displaystyle\lesssim|\partial_{v}\log\Omega^{2}|(u_{0},v)+\int_{u_{0}}^{u}\frac{1}{r^{4}}(\tilde{u},v)\,d\tilde{u},

\displaystyle\lesssim|\partial_{v}\log\Omega^{2}|(u_{0},v)+\int_{r(u,v)}^{r(u_{0},v)}\frac{1}{r^{3}}(\tilde{u},v)\,dr(\tilde{u},v)\lesssim\frac{1}{r^{2}(u,v)},

noting that the first term on the right hand side arises from data and can be ignored.

Writing
|\partial_{v}\Omega^{2}|=\Omega^{2}\cdot|\partial_{v}\log\Omega^{2}|
and using Lemma
4.1
to bound
\Omega^{2}
, one deduces the
\partial_{v}\Omega^{2}
estimates in (
4.29
) and (
4.30
). The
\partial_{u}\Omega^{2}
estimates follow analogously.

We turn to the second derivative estimates for
r^{2}(u,v)
. (
4.31
) and (
4.33
) follow easily from the evolution equations (

) and (
2.13
), where Lemma
4.1
is used to bound the
\Omega^{2}
arising in these equations. On the other hand, to deal with
\partial_{u}(-r\partial_{u}r)
for instance, we differentiate (

) in
u
to find:

\partial_{v}\partial_{u}(-r\partial_{u}r)=\frac{\partial_{u}\Omega^{2}}{4}\left(1-\frac{Q^{2}}{r^{2}}\right)+\frac{\Omega^{2}}{4}\frac{Q^{2}\partial_{u}r}{r^{3}}.

(4.37)

Using (
4.29
) or (
4.30
) alongside Lemma
4.1
, one therefore finds:

|\partial_{v}\partial_{u}(-r\partial_{u}r)|\leq\begin{cases}D\,r(u,v)^{-3}&\text{ for ESFSS},\\
D\,r(u,v)^{-4+\alpha}&\text{ for EMSFSS, given (\ref{eq:cond_r}), (\ref{eq:cond_phi}).}\end{cases}

(4.38)

Integrating (
4.38
) and changing the integration variable from
v
to
r
in a familiar fashion, one gets

|\partial_{u}(-r\partial_{u}r)|\leq\begin{cases}D\,r(u,v)^{-1}&\text{ for ESFSS},\\
D\,r(u,v)^{-2+\alpha}&\text{ for EMSFSS, given (\ref{eq:cond_r}), (\ref{eq:cond_phi}).}\end{cases}

(4.39)

An analogous estimate holds for
\partial_{v}(-r\partial_{v}r)

4.32
) and (
4.34
). The final estimate (
4.35
) is straightforward from combining all previous estimates, e.g.

|\partial_{u}\partial_{u}r|=\frac{1}{r}\left|(\partial_{u}r)^{2}+\partial_{u}(-r\partial_{u}r)\right|\lesssim\frac{1}{r^{3}}.

This completes the proof of Proposition
4.4
.
∎

We now use Propositions
4.4
and
4.3
to derive estimates for second order derivatives of
\phi(u,v)
.

Proposition 4.5
.

For either the ESFSS system or EMSFSS supplemented with (
QTS
), (
SKE
), there exists a constant
D
such that the second order derivatives of
\phi
satisfy

|\partial_{u}\partial_{u}\phi|\leq\frac{D}{r^{4}(u,v)},\qquad|\partial_{u}\partial_{v}\phi|\leq\frac{D}{r^{4}(u,v)},\qquad|\partial_{v}\partial_{v}\phi|\leq\frac{D}{r^{4}(u,v)}.

(4.40)

As in Proposition
4.4
, we may find a similar estimate to (
4.35
) with
\partial_{u},\partial_{v}
replaced by
\underline{L},L
respectively.

Proof.

Commute the wave equation (
2.14
) for
\phi
with the coordinate derivative
\partial_{u}
. One finds:

\partial_{u}\partial_{v}(\partial_{u}\phi)=-\frac{\partial_{u}r\cdot\partial_{v}(\partial_{u}\phi)}{r}-\frac{\partial_{v}r\cdot\partial_{u}(\partial_{u}\phi)}{r}+F_{u}.

(4.41)

Here the inhomogeneity
F
is given by:

F_{u}=\left(\frac{(\partial_{u}r)^{2}}{r^{2}}-\frac{\partial_{u}\partial_{u}r}{r}\right)\partial_{v}\phi+\left(\frac{\partial_{u}r\partial_{v}r}{r^{2}}-\frac{\partial_{u}\partial_{v}r}{r}\right)\partial_{u}\phi.

(4.42)

Using Proposition
4.4
, particularly (
4.35
), and Proposition
4.2
, one estimates

\left|\frac{F_{u}}{\partial_{u}r\partial_{v}r}\right|\lesssim\frac{1}{r^{4}}.

In light of (
4.41
), one now applies Proposition
4.3
with
\psi=\partial_{u}\phi
and
\gamma=-4
to find that

|r^{2}L\partial_{u}\phi|+|r^{2}\underline{L}\partial_{u}\phi|\lesssim\frac{1}{r^{2}}.

Since
L
and
\underline{L}
are uniformly equivalent to
\partial_{v}
and
\partial_{u}
respectively, one therefore deduces the
\partial_{u}\partial_{u}\phi
and
\partial_{u}\partial_{v}\phi
estimate in (

). The
\partial_{v}\partial_{v}\phi
estimate follows by commuting with
\partial_{v}
instead of
\partial_{u}
.
∎

5
Precise asymptotics for the scalar field

The aim of the present section will be to prove the parts of Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
concerning the scalar field
\phi
. The key step will be to show that the weighted derivatives
r^{2}L\phi
,

can be extended in a

As discussed in Section
1.6
, the key ingredient will be the following: treating instead
r^{2}\partial_{v}\phi=-r\partial_{v}r\cdot r^{2}L\phi
in this preliminary discussion, one has from (
2.15
) the equation

\partial_{u}(r^{2}\partial_{v}\phi)=-r\partial_{v}r\cdot\partial_{u}\phi+r\partial_{u}r\cdot\partial_{v}\phi.

The results of Section
4
, particularly Proposition
4.2
, imply that the right-hand side is
O(r^{-2})
, which is borderline non-integrable in the variable
u
as
r\to 0
.

Thankfully, the operator
\tilde{X}=-r\partial_{v}r\cdot\partial_{v}+r\partial_{u}r\cdot\partial_{u}
turns out to be a better derivative than the standard coordinate derivatives
\partial_{u}
,
\partial_{v}
. Since
\tilde{X}r=0
, the vector field
\tilde{X}
is adapted to the constant-
r
spacelike hypersurfaces
\Sigma_{\tilde{r}}
, see Figure
10
. Hence one expects that such a “spatial” derivative behaves better.

Indeed, we shall see from Proposition
5.3
that
\tilde{X}\phi
is integrable up to the
r=0
singularity, allowing us to extend
r^{2}\partial_{u}\phi
and
r^{2}\partial_{v}\phi
to the singularity in a continuous manner. As in Section
4
, the argument is streamlined upon replacing
\partial_{u}
and
\partial_{v}
by their gauge-independent counterparts
\underline{L}
and
L
. In particular, instead of treating
\tilde{X}
as above we will study the spatial vector field
X=L-\underline{L}
.

5.1
The vector field
X=L-{\underline{L}}

Define the vector field
X
by

X=L-\underline{L}=\frac{1}{-r\partial_{v}r}\partial_{v}-\frac{1}{-r\partial_{u}r}\partial_{u}.

(5.1)

From (
5.1
), it immediately follows that
Xr=0
, hence
X
is tangent to the constant-
r
hypersurfaces
\Sigma_{\tilde{r}}
. In particular,
X
is spacelike, and we can further compute

g(X,X)=-2g(L,\underline{L})=\frac{\Omega^{2}}{r^{2}\partial_{u}r\,\partial_{v}r}>0.

(5.2)

We wish to show that
X\phi
obeys better estimates than those of
L\phi
,
\underline{L}\phi
found in Proposition
4.2
. For this purpose, we commute the wave equation (
2.14
) with
X
, and apply Proposition
4.3
. We therefore first produce some preliminary estimates regarding the various commutator terms that arise.

Lemma 5.1
.

For
X
as defined in (
5.1
), decompose the commutator vector field
[\partial_{u},X]
with respect to the coordinate basis
\partial_{u},\partial_{v}
in the usual way:

[\partial_{u},X]=[\partial_{u},X]^{u}\partial_{u}+[\partial_{u},X]^{v}\partial_{v},

and similarly for
[\partial_{v},X]
and
[L,X]=-[L,\underline{L}]=[\underline{L},X]
. Then there exists a constant
D>0
, depending on the data at
C_{0}\cup\underline{C}_{0}
, such that

|[\partial_{v},X]^{u}|+|[\partial_{u},X]^{v}|+|[\partial_{v},X]^{u}|+|[\partial_{v},X]^{v}|+|[L,X]^{u}|+|[L,X]^{v}|\\
\leq\begin{cases}D\,r(u,v)^{-1}&\text{ for ESFSS},\\
D\,r(u,v)^{-2+\alpha}&\text{ for EMSFSS, given (\ref{eq:cond_r}), (\ref{eq:cond_phi}).}\end{cases}

(5.3)

Alternatively, one may decompose the commutator vector fields with respect to the basis
L,\underline{L}
:

[\partial_{u},X]=[\partial_{u},X]^{\underline{L}}\underline{L}+[\partial_{v},X]^{L}L,

and similarly for
[\partial_{v},X]
and
[L,X]
. Then (
5.3
) holds with the
u
,
v
indices replaced by
\underline{L},L
indices.

Proof.

This will follow from the higher derivative estimates of Section
4.4
. Using (
5.1
), we have

\displaystyle[\partial_{u},X]

\displaystyle=\partial_{u}\left(\frac{1}{-r\partial_{v}r}\right)\partial_{v}-\partial_{u}\left(\frac{1}{-r\partial_{u}r}\right)\partial_{u},

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial_{u}(-r\partial_{v}r)}{(-r\partial_{v}r)^{2}}\partial_{v}+\frac{\partial_{u}(-r\partial_{u}r)}{(-r\partial_{u}r)^{2}}\partial_{u}.

Hence we may read off
[\partial_{u},X]^{v}
and
[\partial_{v},X]^{u}
, and bound these using Proposition
4.4
and the lower bounds for
-r\partial_{u}r
and
-r\partial_{v}r
. The commutators
[\partial_{v},X]
and
[L,X]
are dealt with similarly.

For the final statement of the lemma, note that changing basis from
\partial_{u}
,
\partial_{v}
to
\underline{L}
,
L
simply involves multiplying and dividing by
-r\partial_{u}r
and
-r\partial_{v}r
. Since these are upper and lower bounded (see Section
2.4
and (
QTS
)), the estimate (
5.3
) still holds when computing components of the various commutator vector fields with respect to the
L
,
\underline{L}
basis.
∎

We also need to consider commutation with the second order wave operator
\partial_{u}\partial_{v}
:

Lemma 5.2
.

We write the second order operator
[\partial_{u}\partial_{v},X]
in the form

[\partial_{u}\partial_{v},X]=\mathcal{X}_{uu}\partial_{u}\partial_{u}+\mathcal{X}_{uv}\partial_{u}\partial_{v}+\mathcal{X}_{vv}\partial_{v}\partial_{v}+\mathcal{X}_{u}\partial_{u}+\mathcal{X}_{v}\partial_{v},

(5.4)

where
\mathcal{X}_{uu},\mathcal{X}_{uv},\mathcal{X}_{vv},\mathcal{X}_{u},\mathcal{X}_{v}
are each scalar functions of
(u,v)\in\mathcal{D}
. Then we have the following bounds:

|\mathcal{X}_{uu}|+|\mathcal{X}_{uv}|+|\mathcal{X}_{vv}|\leq\begin{cases}D\,r(u,v)^{-1}&\text{ for ESFSS},\\
D\,r(u,v)^{-2+\alpha}&\text{ for EMSFSS, given (\ref{eq:cond_r}), (\ref{eq:cond_phi}),}\end{cases}

(5.5)

|\mathcal{X}_{u}|+|\mathcal{X}_{v}|\leq\begin{cases}D\,r(u,v)^{-3}&\text{ for ESFSS},\\
D\,r(u,v)^{-4+\alpha}&\text{ for EMSFSS, given (\ref{eq:cond_r}), (\ref{eq:cond_phi}).}\end{cases}

(5.6)

Proof.

Once again, we may write explicit formulae for each of
\mathcal{X}_{uu},\mathcal{X}_{uv},\mathcal{X}_{vv},\mathcal{X}_{u},\mathcal{X}_{v}
, then appeal to (
4.4
). Using
[\partial_{u}\partial_{v},X]=\partial_{u}\circ[\partial_{v},X]+[\partial_{u},X]\circ\partial_{v}
, we find that:

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{uu}=[\partial_{v},X]^{u}=\partial_{v}\left(\frac{-1}{-r\partial_{u}r}\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{uv}=[\partial_{v},X]^{v}+[\partial_{u},X]^{u}=\partial_{v}\left(\frac{1}{-r\partial_{v}r}\right)+\partial_{u}\left(\frac{-1}{-r\partial_{u}r}\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{vv}=[\partial_{u},X]^{v}=\partial_{u}\left(\frac{1}{-r\partial_{v}r}\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{u}=\partial_{u}([\partial_{v},X]^{u})=\partial_{u}\partial_{v}\left(\frac{-1}{-r\partial_{u}r}\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{v}=\partial_{u}([\partial_{v},X]^{v})=\partial_{u}\partial_{v}\left(\frac{1}{-r\partial_{v}r}\right).

In particular, (
5.5
) follows immediately from (
5.3
), while for (
5.6
), we first use the equations (
2.12
) and (
2.13
). For instance, we rewrite
\mathcal{X}_{u}
using (
2.13
) as

\mathcal{X}_{u}=\partial_{u}\left(\frac{1}{(-r\partial_{u}r)^{2}}\cdot\frac{\Omega^{2}}{4}\left(1-\frac{Q^{2}}{r^{2}}\right)\right).

The
\partial_{u}
derivative could hit any of
(-r\partial_{u}r)^{-1}
,
\Omega^{2}
or
r^{-1}
. However, we see from Lemma
4.1
and Proposition
4.4
that in any of these cases, the result remains bounded by the right hand side of (
5.6
). The estimate for
\mathcal{X}_{v}
is analogous, concluding the proof of Lemma
5.2
.
∎

5.2
Estimates on
X\phi

In this section we prove Proposition
5.3
, producing upper bounds on
X\phi
and its derivatives. One achieves this by commuting (
2.14
) with the vector field
X
, then appealing to Proposition
4.3
. For convenience, we record (
2.14
) again here:

\partial_{u}\partial_{v}\phi=-\frac{\partial_{u}r\cdot\partial_{v}\phi}{r}-\frac{\partial_{v}r\cdot\partial_{u}\phi}{r}.

(5.7)

Proposition 5.3
.

For the ESFSS system or the EMSFSS system supplemented with the additional assumptions (
QTS
), (
SKE
), we have the following upper bounds for
X\phi
and its weighted derivatives:

|X\phi(u,v)|+|r^{2}LX\phi(u,v)|+|r^{2}\underline{L}X\phi(u,v)|\leq\begin{cases}D\,r^{-1}(u,v)&\text{ for ESFSS},\\
D\,r^{-2+\alpha}(u,v)&\text{ for EMSFSS, given (\ref{eq:cond_r}), (\ref{eq:cond_phi})}.\end{cases}

(5.8)

Proof.

In light of Proposition
4.3
, we seek an equation of the form (
4.14
), with
\psi=X\phi
. We apply
X
to equation (
5.7
); since
Xr=0
we find

\partial_{u}\partial_{v}X\phi=-\frac{\partial_{u}r\cdot\partial_{v}X\phi}{r}-\frac{\partial_{v}r\cdot\partial_{u}X\phi}{r}+F_{X},

(5.9)

where the inhomogeneous term
F_{X}
is given exactly by

F_{X}=[\partial_{u}\partial_{v},X]\phi+\frac{\partial_{u}r}{r}\cdot[\partial_{v},X]\phi+\frac{\partial_{v}r}{r}\cdot[\partial_{u},X]\phi+\frac{[\partial_{u},X]r}{r}\cdot\partial_{v}\phi+\frac{[\partial_{v},X]r}{r}\cdot\partial_{u}\phi.

(5.10)

It simply remains to estimate this inhomogeneous term
F_{X}
using the results of Section
5.1
.
We introduce some schematic notation; we write
\mathcal{X}_{**}
to represent some element of
\{[\partial_{u},X]^{u},[\partial_{u},X]^{v},[\partial_{v},X]^{u},[\partial_{v},X]^{v}\}
or any linear combination thereof. Note that
\mathcal{X}_{uu},\mathcal{X}_{uv},\mathcal{X}_{vv}
are included in
\mathcal{X}_{**}
, see the proof of Lemma
5.2
. Likewise, denote
\mathcal{X}_{*}
to represent one of
\mathcal{X}_{u}
or
\mathcal{X}_{v}
as in Lemma
5.2
. Finally, denote
\partial_{*}
to be one of the two null coordinate derivatives
\partial_{u},\partial_{v}
.

Using this notation and Lemma
5.2
,
F_{X}
can be written in the schematic form:

F_{X}=\mathcal{X}_{*}\cdot\partial_{*}\phi+\mathcal{X}_{**}\cdot\partial_{*}\partial_{*}\phi+\mathcal{X}_{**}\cdot\frac{\partial_{*}r}{r}\cdot\partial_{*}\phi.

(5.11)

Now, from Lemmas
5.1
and
5.2
, we may deduce that

|r^{2}\mathcal{X}_{*}|+|\mathcal{X}_{**}|\lesssim\begin{cases}r^{-1}(u,v)&\text{ for ESFSS},\\
r^{-2+\alpha}(u,v)&\text{ for EMSFSS, given (\ref{eq:cond_r}), (\ref{eq:cond_phi})}.\end{cases}

We also need to control
\partial_{*}r
,
\partial_{*}\phi
and
\partial_{*}\partial_{*}\phi
. The upper bounds for
-r\partial_{*}r
give that
-\partial_{*}r\lesssim r^{-1}
, while derivatives of
\phi
are controlled using Proposition
4.2
for
\partial_{*}\phi
and Proposition
4.5
for
\partial_{*}\partial_{*}\phi
. These yield

|\partial_{*}\phi|\lesssim r^{-2},\qquad|\partial_{*}\partial_{*}\phi|\lesssim r^{-4}.

Applying all of these bounds to (
5.11
), we find that

|F_{X}|\lesssim\begin{cases}r^{-5}(u,v)&\text{ for ESFSS},\\
r^{-6+\alpha}(u,v)&\text{ for EMSFSS, given (\ref{eq:cond_r}), (\ref{eq:cond_phi})}.\end{cases}

Using again lower bounds for
-r\partial_{*}r
, we therefore have that

\left|\frac{F_{X}}{\partial_{u}r\,\partial_{v}r}\right|\leq F_{X,0}\cdot r^{\gamma}(u,v).

(5.12)

Here
F_{X,0}
is some constant depending only on data, while
\gamma=-3
and
\gamma=-4+\alpha
in the context of the ESFSS and the EMSFSS system respectively. We are now in a position to apply Proposition
4.3
to (
5.9
).

Since we have
\gamma<-2
in both cases, the final term on the right hand sides of (
4.16
) and (
4.17
) arising from the inhomogeneity dominate any terms arising from the data for
\psi=X\phi
at
C_{0}\cup\underline{C}_{0}
. Therefore the estimates (
4.16
) and (
4.17
) applied to
\psi=X\phi
yield (
5.8
), as required.
∎

5.3
Asymptotics for the scalar field at the singularity

Armed with Proposition
5.3
, we now prove the asymptotics for
\phi
given in Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
, including the appropriate Hölder continuity for the quantities
\Psi
and
\Xi
.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
(r,\Omega^{2},\phi,Q)
be a strongly singular solution to the EMSFSS system with an
r=0
spacelike singularity as in Section
2.4
, where we assume (
QTS
), (
SKE
) to hold if
Q\neq 0
. Define the functions
\Psi:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\Xi:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
to be:

\displaystyle\Psi(u,v)\coloneqq r^{2}\cdot\frac{(L+\underline{L})\phi(u,v)}{2},

(5.13)

\displaystyle\Xi(u,v)\coloneqq\phi(u,v)-\Psi(u,v)\cdot\log\left(\frac{r_{0}}{r(u,v)}\right).

(5.14)

Then
\Psi
is
C^{0,\beta}
in
\mathcal{D}
, with Hölder exponent
\beta=1/2
for the ESFSS system and
\beta=\alpha/2
for the EMSFSS system with (
QTS
), (
SKE
).
\Xi
, on the other hand, is
C^{0,\beta,\log}
(see (
3.2
) for a definition of log-Hölder continuity).
In fact,
\|\Psi\|_{C^{0,\beta}}
and
\|\Xi\|_{C^{0,\beta,\log}}
are bounded by a constant
D
depending on the data:

\|\Psi\|_{C^{0,\beta}}+\|\Xi\|_{C^{0,\beta,\log}}\leq D.

(5.15)

Remark
.

To explain the definitions (
5.13
)–(
5.14
), recall from Theorem
3.1
(
II
) and Theorem
3.2
(
II
) that we wish to write
\phi=\Psi\cdot\log( \frac{r_0}{r} )+\Xi
. This immediately justifies (
5.14
), while to justify (
5.13
), we recall that
L
and
\underline{L}
are, in some sense, derivatives of the form
-r^{-1}\frac{d}{dr}
adapted to null hypersurfaces. As a result,
r^{2}\cdot\frac{L+\underline{L}}{2}
can be viewed as a
-r\frac{d}{dr}
derivative, and thus identifies the coefficient
\Psi
in front of
\log(\frac{r_0}{r})
.

Proof.

We study the derivatives of the quantities
\Psi
and
\Xi
. Using the wave equation (
5.7
), we produce some null transport equations for
r^{2}L\phi
and
r^{2}\underline{L}\phi
. Using (
2.15
) as well as (
2.12
), one finds

\underline{L}(r^{2}L\phi)=-X\phi+\frac{\Omega^{2}}{4r^{2}\partial_{u}r\partial_{v}r}\left(\frac{Q^{2}}{r^{2}}-1\right)\cdot r^{2}L\phi.

(5.16)

One finds an analogous equation for
r^{2}\underline{L}\phi
:

L(r^{2}\underline{L}\phi)=X\phi+\frac{\Omega^{2}}{4r^{2}\partial_{u}r\partial_{v}r}\left(\frac{Q^{2}}{r^{2}}-1\right)\cdot r^{2}\underline{L}\phi.

(5.17)

As
\Psi
contains both
L
and
\underline{L}
derivatives, it is useful to also include the following null transport equations for
r^{2}X\phi
. Using that
Lr^{2}=\underline{L}r^{2}=-2
, we have:

\displaystyle\underline{L}(r^{2}X\phi)=r^{2}\underline{L}X\phi-2X\phi,

(5.18)

\displaystyle L(r^{2}X\phi)=r^{2}LX\phi-2X\phi.

(5.19)

By Proposition
5.3
and Lemma
4.1
, all the terms on the right hand sides of (
5.16
), (
5.17
), (
5.18
), (
5.19
) are
O(r^{-1})
when
Q=0
and
O(r^{-2+\alpha})
when
Q\neq 0
. This implies that these equations can be integrated up to
r=0
. These equations, along with the definition (
5.13
) for
\Psi
, yield

\displaystyle\underline{L}\Psi=-\frac{1}{2}r^{2}\underline{L}X\phi+\frac{\Omega^{2}}{4r^{2}\partial_{u}r\,\partial_{v}r}\left(\frac{Q^{2}}{r^{2}}-1\right)\cdot r^{2}\underline{L}\phi.

(5.20)

\displaystyle L\Psi=\frac{1}{2}r^{2}LX\phi+\frac{\Omega^{2}}{4r^{2}\partial_{u}r\,\partial_{v}r}\left(\frac{Q^{2}}{r^{2}}-1\right)\cdot r^{2}L\phi.

(5.21)

Applying Proposition
5.3
, there exists a constant
D
, depending on the data,
R_{0}
and
\alpha
, such that

|\underline{L}\Psi|+|L\Psi|\leq D\,r(u,v)^{-2+2\beta}.

(5.22)

Here
\beta
is as defined in the statement of Proposition
5.4
. We now use this to prove the desired
C^{0,\beta}
regularity: we first control the Hölder seminorm in the
u
-direction:

\displaystyle|\Psi(\tilde{u}_{1},v)-\Psi(\tilde{u}_{0},v)|

\displaystyle=\left|\int^{\tilde{u}_{1}}_{\tilde{u}_{0}}\partial_{u}\Psi(\tilde{u},v)\,d\tilde{u}\right|,

\displaystyle\leq\int^{\tilde{u}_{1}}_{\tilde{u}_{0}}|r\partial_{u}r\cdot\underline{L}\Psi(\tilde{u},v)|\,d\tilde{u},

\displaystyle\leq D\int^{r(\tilde{u}_{0},v)}_{r(\tilde{u}_{1},v)}\tilde{r}^{-1+2\beta}\,d\tilde{r}=\frac{D}{2\beta}\cdot(r(\tilde{u}_{0},v)^{2\beta}-r(\tilde{u}_{1},v)^{2\beta}).

We next use the inequality
x^{\beta}-y^{\beta}\leq(x-y)^{\beta}
for
x>y>0
to get

|\Psi(\tilde{u}_{1},v)-\Psi(\tilde{u}_{0},v)|\leq\frac{D}{2\beta}\cdot|r^{2}(\tilde{u}_{0},v)-r^{2}(\tilde{u}_{1},v)|^{\beta}.

(5.23)

Since the Hölder seminorm is defined with respect to
u
and not
r
, we need to again use the upper bounded for
-r\partial_{u}r
; we have
|r^{2}(\tilde{u}_{0},v)-r^{2}(\tilde{u}_{1},v)|\leq|\tilde{u}_{1}-\tilde{u}_{0}|\cdot\sup_{\tilde{u}_{0}\leq\tilde{u}\leq\tilde{u}_{1}}(-2r\partial_{u}r(\tilde{u},v))
. Since
-r\partial_{u}r\leq R_{1}
is uniformly bounded by Lemma
2.1
, we deduce from (
5.23
) that

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}[\tilde{u}_{0},\tilde{u}_{1}]\times\{v\}\\
\subset\mathcal{D}\end{subarray}}\frac{|\Psi(\tilde{u}_{1},v)-\Psi(\tilde{u}_{0},v)|}{|\tilde{u}_{1}-\tilde{u}_{0}|^{\beta}}\leq D.

(5.24)

The
\beta
-Hölder seminorm in the
v
-direction is similar, while the boundedness of
|\Psi|
follows immediately from the seminorm bounds and the fact that
\mathcal{D}
is a bounded domain. This completes the estimates for
\Psi
.

We now move onto the quantity
\Xi
defined in (
5.14
), describing the
O(1)
term in the asymptotic expansion of
\phi
. From (
5.14
), (
5.13
), and
L\log r=\underline{L}\log r=-\frac{1}{r^{2}}
, one finds

\displaystyle\underline{L}\Xi=\underline{L}\phi-\frac{\Psi}{r^{2}}-\underline{L}\Psi\cdot\log\left(\frac{r_{0}}{r}\right)=-\frac{1}{2}X\phi-\underline{L}\Psi\cdot\log\left(\frac{r_{0}}{r}\right),

(5.25)

\displaystyle L\Xi=L\phi-\frac{\Psi}{r^{2}}-L\Psi\cdot\log\left(\frac{r_{0}}{r}\right)=+\frac{1}{2}X\phi-L\Psi\cdot\log\left(\frac{r_{0}}{r}\right).

(5.26)

From Proposition
5.3
that
|X\phi|\lesssim r^{-2+2\beta}
, while the terms involving
L\Psi
and
\underline{L}\Psi
on the right hand side are bounded using (
5.22
). Unfortunately, the presence of the
\log(r_0 / r)
term on the right hand side produces some loss on the exponent, and we therefore only have

|L\Xi|+|\underline{L}\Xi|\leq\tilde{D}\,r^{-2+2\beta}\log r^{-1}.

(5.27)

Given (
5.27
), the remainder of the proof follows the same strategy as for
\Psi
, with appropriate modifications due to the
\log r^{-1}
term. This concludes the proof of the proposition.
∎

Remark
.

The important steps of this proof are (
5.22
) and (
5.27
). Once these estimates are found, there are other ways to characterise the regularity of
\Psi
and
\Xi
. For instance, one can instead find uniform Sobolev regularity on null hypersurfaces of the form:

\Psi\in L^{\infty}_{u}W^{1,p_{\beta}^{-}}_{v}\cap L^{\infty}_{v}W^{1,p_{\beta}^{-}}_{u},\quad\Xi\in L^{\infty}_{u}W^{1,p_{\beta}^{-}}_{v}\cap L^{\infty}_{v}W^{1,p_{\beta}^{-}}_{u},\quad p_{\beta}=\frac{1}{1-\beta}.

Proofs of the main Theorems

In this section, we conclude the proofs of Theorems
3.1
and

, as well as the proof of Corollary

.

6.1
Asymptotics for
\Omega^{2}

We firstly provide the precise asymptotics for the null lapse
\Omega^{2}(u,v)
, and related geometric quantities.

Proposition 6.1
.

Consider a strongly singular solution to the EMSFSS system as in Section
2.4
. Recalling the function
\Psi:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
from (
5.13
) in Proposition
5.4
, there exists
D>0
depending on data,
R_{0}
and
\alpha
, such that

\left|\log\left(\Omega^{2}\cdot\left(\frac{r_{0}}{r}\right)^{-\Psi^{2}+1}\right)\right|\leq D.

(6.1)

In fact we find the following precise asymptotics for the gauge-invariant quantity
g(\nabla r,\nabla r)=4\Omega^{-2}\partial_{u}r\,\partial_{v}r
. There exists a positive function
\mathfrak{M}:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
,
C^{0,\beta,\log}
up to the
\{r=0\}
boundary, with

4\Omega^{-2}\partial_{u}r\,\partial_{v}r\eqqcolon\mathfrak{M}\cdot\left(\frac{r}{r_{0}}\right)^{-(\Psi^{2}+1)},

(6.2)

Here the Hölder exponent
\beta
is as in Proposition
5.4
, and one finds the following bounds on
\mathfrak{M}
:

\|\log\mathfrak{M}\|_{C^{0,\beta,\log}}+\|\mathfrak{M}\|_{C^{0,\beta,\log}}\leq D.

(6.3)

Proof.

We use the Raychaudhuri equation (
2.8
), which we record again for convenience:

\partial_{u}(-\Omega^{-2}\partial_{u}r)=\Omega^{-2}r|\partial_{u}\phi|^{2},

We rewrite this equation as a null transport equation for the following logarithmic quantity involving
\Omega^{2}
, where we introduce a harmless factor of
4
in order to be consistent with the left hand side of (
6.2
):

\partial_{u}\log\left(\frac{\Omega^{2}}{-4\partial_{u}r}\right)=\frac{r}{\partial_{u}r}\cdot|\partial_{u}\phi|^{2}.

Converting to the gauge-invariant derivative
\underline{L}
, one finds:

\underline{L}\log\left(\frac{\Omega^{2}}{-4\partial_{u}r}\right)=-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\cdot|r^{2}\underline{L}\phi|^{2}.

(6.4)

Of course, there is also the analogous equation:

L\log\left(\frac{\Omega^{2}}{-4\partial_{v}r}\right)=-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\cdot|r^{2}L\phi|^{2}.

(6.5)

By Proposition
4.2
, the right hand sides of (
6.4
) and (
6.5
) are
O(r^{-2})
, and thus borderline non-integrable as
r\to 0
. We solve this issue by subtracting from (

) and (

) the leading order term, which is exactly
\Psi^{2}\cdot r^{-2}
. Since
L\log r=\underline{L}\log r=-\frac{1}{r^{2}}
, we therefore find:

\underline{L}\log\left(\frac{\Omega^{2}}{-4\partial_{u}r}\right)-\underline{L}\left(\Psi^{2}\log\left(\frac{r}{r_{0}}\right)\right)=-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\cdot(|r^{2}\underline{L}\phi|^{2}-\Psi^{2})-2\Psi(\underline{L}\Psi)\log\left(\frac{r}{r_{0}}\right)

Taking the difference of two squares on the right hand side, and combining the terms on the left, we get:

\underline{L}\log\left(\frac{\Omega^{2}}{-4\partial_{u}r}\cdot\left(\frac{r}{r_{0}}\right)^{-\Psi^{2}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}(X\phi)(r^{2}\underline{L}\phi+\Psi)-2\Psi(\underline{L}\Psi)\log\left(\frac{r}{r_{0}}\right).

(6.6)

We now follow a similar method to the proof of Proposition
5.4
. By (
5.8
) and (
5.22
), the right hand side of (
6.6
) is bounded by
r^{-2+2\beta}\log r^{-1}
:

\left|\underline{L}\log\left(\frac{\Omega^{2}}{-4\partial_{u}r}\cdot\left(\frac{r}{r_{0}}\right)^{-\Psi^{2}}\right)\right|\leq D\cdot r^{-2+2\beta}\log\left(\frac{r_{0}}{r}\right),

(6.7)

Hence integrating in the
\underline{L}
direction, where as usual this involves multiplying by
r
then integrating in
r
, we observe that the right-hand side is integrable towards
r=0
, and

\left|\log\left(\frac{\Omega^{2}}{-4\partial_{u}r}\cdot\left(\frac{r}{r_{0}}\right)^{-\Psi^{2}}\right)\right|\lesssim 1.

We finally exponentiate this expression, and use that
-r\partial_{u}r\lesssim 1
to get the sharp upper bound (
6.1
) for
\Omega^{2}
as a polynomial power of
r
.

To find the sharp asymptotics for
\mathfrak{M}
as defined in (
6.2
), the additional ingredient is to use (
2.12
) to get:

\underline{L}\log( - r \partial_v r )=\frac{\Omega^{2}}{4\partial_{u}r\partial_{v}r}\cdot\left(1-\frac{Q^{2}}{r^{2}}\right).

(6.8)

Combining (
6.8
) with (
6.6
), we therefore find that

\underline{L}\log\mathfrak{M}=\frac{\Omega^{2}}{4\partial_{u}r\partial_{v}r}\cdot\left(1-\frac{Q^{2}}{r^{2}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}(X\phi)(r^{2}\underline{L}\phi+\Psi)+2\Psi(\underline{L}\Psi)\log\left(\frac{r}{r_{0}}\right).

(6.9)

Similarly, in the
v
-direction we have

L\log\mathfrak{M}=\frac{\Omega^{2}}{4\partial_{u}r\partial_{v}r}\cdot\left(1-\frac{Q^{2}}{r^{2}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}(X\phi)(r^{2}L\phi+\Psi)+2\Psi(L\Psi)\log\left(\frac{r}{r_{0}}\right).

(6.10)

Now we may proceed in the same manner we used to derive the Hölder continuity for
\Psi
and
\Xi
in Proposition
5.4
. Using Proposition
5.3
, Proposition
5.4
, and Lemma
4.1
, one has

|L\log\mathfrak{M}|+|\underline{L}\log\mathfrak{M}|\leq D\cdot r^{-2+2\beta}\log\left(\frac{r_{0}}{r}\right).

(6.11)

The remainder of the proof then follows exactly as in the proof of Proposition
5.4
.∎

Remark

To estimate the Kretschmann scalar, we list all the non-vanishing components of the Riemann tensor, whose components are evaluated with respect to the following null frame:

\mbox{Riem}(V_{1},V_{2},V_{3},V_{4})=\mbox{Riem}(V_{3},V_{4},V_{1},V_{2})=-\mbox{Riem}(V_{2},V_{1},V_{3},V_{4})

The most famous “cosmological” solutions to Einstein’s equation (

Our final explicit example of a spherically symmetric spacetime possessing a spacelike singularity are the scale-invariant solutions

), (

) possessing a conformal Killing vector field

, known as the

In a suitable double-null gauge, where
\mathbf{S}
can be written as
\mathbf{S}=u\frac{\partial}{\partial u}+v\frac{\partial}{\partial v}

\Omega^{2}\equiv 1

g=-dudv+r^{2}(u,v)d\sigma_{\mathbb{S}^{2}},

where the area-radius

These solutions feature a loss of regularity across the ingoing null hypersurface

will have a jump discontinuity at

\partial_{v}\phi
merely of bounded variation
[
