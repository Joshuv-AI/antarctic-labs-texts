A roadmap to cosmological parameter analysis with third-order shear statistics II: Analytic covariance estimate The modelling code is available at https://github.com/sheydenreich/threepoint/releases .
Laila Linke, Sven Heydenreich, Pierre A. Burger, Peter Schneider

Abstract
Context. Third-order weak lensing statistics are a promising tool for cosmological analyses since they extract cosmological information in the non-Gaussianity of the cosmic large-scale structure. However, such analyses require precise and accurate models for the covariance of the statistics. Aims. In this second paper of a series on third-order weak lensing statistics, we derive and validate an analytic model for the covariance of the third-order aperture statistics ⟨ M ap 3 ⟩ \expectationvalue{\Map^3} . Methods. We derive the covariance model from a real-space estimator for ⟨ M ap 3 ⟩ \expectationvalue{\Map^3} , including the Gaussian and non-Gaussian parts. We validate the model by comparing it to estimates from simulated Gaussian random fields (GRFs) and two sets of N N -body simulations. Finally, we perform mock cosmological analyses with the model covariance and the simulation estimate to compare the resulting parameter constraints. Results. We find good agreement between the analytic model and the simulations, both for the GRFs and the N N -body simulations. The figure of merit in the S 8 S_{8} - Ω m \Omega_{\mathrm{m}} plane from our covariance model is within 3% of the one obtained from the simulated covariances. We also show that our model, which is based on an estimator using convergence maps, can be used to obtain upper and lower bounds for the covariance of an estimator based on three-point shear correlation functions. This second estimator is required to measure ⟨ M ap 3 ⟩ \expectationvalue{\Map^3} in realistic survey data. In our derivation, we find that the covariance of ⟨ M ap 3 ⟩ \expectationvalue{\Map^3} cannot be obtained from the bispectrum covariance and that it includes several ‘finite-field terms’ that do not scale with the inverse survey area. Conclusions. Our covariance model is sufficiently accurate for analysing stage III surveys. Covariances for statistics in Fourier space cannot always be straightforwardly converted into covariance for real-space statistics.

Introduction

Using weak gravitational lensing of the cosmic large-scale structure (LSS) has become a precise and accurate method to infer cosmological parameters (
Heymans et al. 2021
,
Hikage et al. 2019
,
Abbott et al. 2022
). Weak-lensing analyses have mainly concentrated on second-order statistics, such as shear two-point correlation functions or galaxy-galaxy-lensing. However, since the cosmic LSS is non-Gaussian, second-order statistics do not contain its total information content. To access the additional information, higher-order statistics (HOS) are needed. These HOS depend on cosmological parameters differently than second-order statistics, so they can be used to break degeneracies (
Takada & Jain 2004
,
Kilbinger & Schneider 2005
,
Kayo et al. 2013
) or constrain nuisance parameters such as galaxy bias or intrinsic alignment (
Pyne & Joachimi 2021
,
Troxel & Ishak 2012
).

Many HOS have been suggested to complement second-order analyses, for example, peak statistics (
Kacprzak et al. 2016
,
Zürcher et al. 2021

Martinet et al. 2021
), density split statistics (
Friedrich et al. 2018
,
Gruen et al. 2018
,
Burger et al. 2022
), or persistent homology
(
Heydenreich et al. 2021
;
Heydenreich et al. 2022a
)
. Most of these statistics, though, cannot be modelled from analytical theories and instead require time-consuming realistic
N
-body simulations. Additionally, for many HOS, the measurement requires converting weak lensing shear estimates to mass convergence maps. This conversion becomes complicated in the presence of masks and for finite survey areas, which can lead to biased estimators
(
Seitz & Schneider 1996
)
.

In this series of papers, we are considering the third-order aperture mass
\expectationvalue{\Map^3}
, which is not affected by these problems. It is linearly related to the third-order shear correlation functions
\Gamma_{i}
(
Jarvis et al. 2004
,
Schneider et al. 2005
), which can be directly measured in shear catalogues and converted into
\expectationvalue{\Map^3}
.
This was demonstrated recently by
Secco et al. 2022
, who measured
\expectationvalue{\Map^3}
with high significance from the Dark Energy Survey Year 3 shear catalogues. Moreover, in contrast to many other HOS,
\expectationvalue{\Map^3}
and
\Gamma_{i}
can be modelled analytically without directly relying on simulations.

There are several advantages to using
\expectationvalue{\Map^3}
instead of
\Gamma_{i}
, among them that
\expectationvalue{\Map^3}
compress the information in the
\Gamma_{i}
from a data vector with thousands of entries to one containing only tens of entries in a non-tomographic setup, and that the
\expectationvalue{\Map^3}
have a clear separation into
E
- and
B
-modes, allowing for the detection of untreated systematic effects. Our goal is to lay the groundwork for a cosmological analysis with
\expectationvalue{\Map^3}
. Such an analysis requires two ingredients: a model of
\expectationvalue{\Map^3}
and an estimate of its covariance matrix. The first ingredient is the subject of
Heydenreich et al. 2022b
, where we present and test an analytic model of
\expectationvalue{\Map^3}
based on the
BiHalofit
bispectrum model
(
Takahashi et al. 2020
)
. Here, we are concerned with the second ingredient, which is the covariance.

The covariance of the estimator of a statistic can be obtained in three ways. The first possibility is applying the estimator to a large set of (quasi-)independent cosmological
N
-body simulations and using the sample covariance of the estimates. However, an unbiased and precise estimate requires many more realisations than entries in the data vector. For example, for a tomographic analysis of
\expectationvalue{\Map^3}
with four redshift bins and four different scale radii, the data vector contains 400 entries, so hundreds of simulations are needed for a simulated covariance estimate. Such an estimate would be very time-consuming, as both the creation of this many independent simulations and the measurement of
\expectationvalue{\Map^3}
in them is computationally demanding. The second possibility is to estimate the covariance directly from the data using jackknife resampling or bootstrap methods. For this, the survey area is divided into smaller patches, the statistics are estimated separately on each of these patches, and the sample covariance of these individual estimates is taken as the covariance estimate. However, this method has two main disadvantages. First, information on correlations larger than the patch size is lost. Since the number of patches must be at least as large as the length of the data vector, the required number of patches becomes large and their size small. Second, the method implicitly assumes that the individual patches are independent of each other. This assumption is not true, in particular, if small, neighbouring patches are considered. This causes biases in the covariance estimate. Third, triplets covering two or three patches need to be neglected for the estimation of the data vector. Otherwise, the covariance from jackknife or bootstrap resampling would overestimate the true covariance of the data.

Instead, cosmic shear covariances can also be calculated analytically for a given estimator. This was done, for example, for two-point statistics
(
Joachimi et al. 2008
)
or the matter bispectrum
(
Joachimi et al. 2009
)
. While the derivation of an expression for a covariance can be tedious, once it is done, evaluating them for the characteristics of a specific survey is usually accurate and quick, at least compared to running hundreds of cosmological simulations. It is also easier to infer the dependence of the covariance on survey area and geometry from an analytic expression. Consequently, we follow this approach and derive an analytical expression for the covariance of
\expectationvalue{\Map^3}
. We test our result by comparing it with the covariance measured in simulated mock data, both simple Gaussian shear fields and full cosmological simulations, and investigate the influence of different terms in the covariance by performing mock cosmological analyses.

In our derivation, we make five crucial findings. First, the covariance consists of six terms, two of which belong to the Gaussian part and four to the non-Gaussian part. These terms all depend on the survey area and window function (Sect.
3.1
and
3.2
).
Second, the individual covariance terms can be estimated from correlation functions of the aperture mass map. These correlation functions need to be known only on scales inside the survey area (Sect.
3.3
). Third, under the ‘large-field approximation’, which assumes a broad survey window function, two covariance terms vanish, while the others scale inversely with the survey area. The vanishing terms decrease faster than
1/A
with survey area
A
. We refer to them as finite-field terms. One of these terms is already present for Gaussian fields (Sect.
3.4
). Fourth, the covariance of
\expectationvalue{\Map^3}
cannot be obtained from the bispectrum covariance unless the large-field approximation is valid. Using a linear transformation of the covariance of a Fourier space quantity to obtain the covariance for a real-space observable has been done for second-order statistics
(
Joachimi et al. 2021
;
Friedrich et al. 2021
)
. However, for
\expectationvalue{\Map^3}
this approach already fails for Gaussian fields if the large-field approximation is not used (Sect.
4
). Finally, our covariance model, based on an estimator using convergence maps, can be used to obtain upper and lower limits on the covariance for an estimator based on third-order shear correlation functions (Sect.
5.3.2
).

This paper is structured as follows. In Sect.
2
we give a short overview of third-order shear statistics, and we introduce our notation, the third-order aperture mass
\expectationvalue{\Map^3}
, and its estimator. We derive the covariance of this estimator in Sect.
3
. In Sect.
4
we show that an alternative derivation of the
\expectationvalue{\Map^3}
covariance from the bispectrum covariance is not correct. We validate the model by comparing its predictions to simulated data in Sect.
5
. Finally, in Sect.
6
, we perform mock cosmological analyses to compare the results from the validation data to our model and investigate the impact of the individual covariance terms. We conclude with a summary and discussion in Sect.
7
. Throughout this paper, we assume a spatially flat universe. Our covariance modelling code is publically available

Theoretical background

In this section, we briefly review some fundamental weak lensing quantities relevant to third-order shear statistics and the covariance calculation in the remainder of this work. We refer to
Bartelmann & Schneider 2001
;
Hoekstra & Jain 2008
or
Bartelmann 2010
for in-depth reviews on weak lensing.

One of the fundamental quantities in weak gravitational lensing is the convergence
\kappa(\bm{\vartheta})
, which is the normalised surface mass density at angular position
\bm{\vartheta}
. In a flat universe, it is related to the density contrast
\delta(\chi\bm{\vartheta},\chi)
at angular position
\bm{\vartheta}
and comoving distance
\chi

\kappa(\bm{\vartheta})=\frac{3H_{0}^{2}\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}}{2c^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{\chi}\;q(\chi)\,\frac{\delta(\chi\bm{\vartheta},\chi)}{a(\chi)}\;,\textrm{where }q(\chi)=\int_{\chi}^{\infty}\differential{\chi'}\,p(\chi^{\prime})\,\frac{\chi^{\prime}-\chi}{\chi^{\prime}}\;,

(1)

with the Hubble constant
H_{0}
, the matter density parameter
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
, the speed-of-light
c
, the cosmic scale factor
a(\chi)
at
\chi
, normalised to unity today, and
the probability distribution
p(\chi)\,\differential{\chi}
of source galaxies in comoving distance.

We treat

\mathcal{P}_{n}

\expectationvalue{\tilde{\kappa}(\ellvec_1)\dots\tilde{\kappa}(\ellvec_n)}_{\mathrm{c}}=\mathcal{P}_{n}(\bm{\ell}_{1},\dots,\bm{\ell}_{n})\,(2\pi)^{2}\,\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{\ell}_{1}+\dots+\bm{\ell}_{n})\;,

(2)

\displaystyle P(\ell)=P_{2}(\bm{\ell},-\bm{\ell})\,,\quad B(\bm{\ell}_{1},\bm{\ell}_{2})=\mathcal{P}_{n}(\bm{\ell}_{1},\bm{\ell}_{2},-\bm{\ell}_{1}-\bm{\ell}_{2})\,,\quad T(\bm{\ell}_{1},\bm{\ell}_{2},\bm{\ell}_{3})=\mathcal{P}_{n}(\bm{\ell}_{1},\bm{\ell}_{2},\bm{\ell}_{3},-\bm{\ell}_{1}-\bm{\ell}_{2}-\bm{\ell}_{3})\,,

(3)

\displaystyle P_{6}(\bm{\ell}_{1},\bm{\ell}_{2},\bm{\ell}_{3},\bm{\ell}_{4},\bm{\ell}_{5})=\mathcal{P}_{n}(\bm{\ell}_{1},\bm{\ell}_{2},\bm{\ell}_{3},\bm{\ell}_{4},\bm{\ell}_{5},-\bm{\ell}_{1}-\bm{\ell}_{2}-\bm{\ell}_{3}-\bm{\ell}_{4}-\bm{\ell}_{5})\;.

(4)

The convergence cannot be directly observed, but it is related to the weak lensing shear
\gamma
, which describes the change in the observed ellipticity of source galaxies due to the lensing effect. For third-order shear statistics, we are interested in the three-point correlation function of the shear, whose so-called natural components
\Gamma_{i}
were derived by
Schneider & Lombardi 2003

10^{3}
) and are complex to model because they relate to the matter bispectrum via multi-dimensional integrals involving oscillating Bessel functions. As shown in
15

\expectationvalue{\Map^3}

Aperture statistics are moments of the aperture mass
M_{\mathrm{ap}}
, a smoothed convergence map, which depends on its position
\mathbf{\alpha}
and a characteristic smoothing scale

M_{\mathrm{ap}}(\mathbf{\alpha},\theta)=\int\differential[2]{\vartheta}\;U_{\theta}(|\mathbf{\alpha}-\bm{\vartheta}|)\,\kappa(\bm{\vartheta})\;,\textrm{ with }U_{\theta}(\vartheta)=\frac{1}{\theta^{2}}\,u\left(\frac{\vartheta}{\theta}\right)\;,

(6)

where

\theta

\int\differential{\vartheta}\vartheta\,U_{\theta}(\vartheta)=0\;,

the aperture mass can be estimated from the tangential shear

(

M_{\mathrm{ap}}(\mathbf{\alpha},\theta)=\int\differential[2]{\vartheta}\;Q_{\theta}(|\mathbf{\alpha}-\bm{\vartheta}|)\,\gamma_{\mathrm{t}}(\mathbf{\alpha};\bm{\vartheta})\;,\quad\textrm{with}\quad Q_{\theta}(\vartheta)=\frac{2}{\vartheta^{2}}\,\int_{0}^{\vartheta}\differential{\vartheta'}\;\vartheta^{\prime}U_{\theta}(\vartheta^{\prime})-U_{\theta}(\vartheta)\;.

(7)

Here, we are interested in the covariance of the third-order aperture statistics

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A^{2}}\int\differential[2]{\alpha_1}\int\differential[2]{\alpha_2}W_{A}(\mathbf{\alpha}_{1})\,W_{A}(\mathbf{\alpha}_{2})\,\Bigg[\prod_{i=1}^{3}\int\differential[2]{\vartheta_i}U_{\theta_{i}}(|\mathbf{\alpha}_{1}-\bm{\vartheta}_{i}|)\Bigg]\,\Bigg[\prod_{j=4}^{6}\int\differential[2]{\vartheta_j}U_{\theta_{j}}(|\mathbf{\alpha}_{2}-\bm{\vartheta}_{j}|)\Bigg]

\displaystyle\quad\times\Big[\left(\expectationvalue{\kappa_1\,\kappa_2}_{\mathrm{c}}\,\expectationvalue{\kappa_3\,\kappa_4}_{\mathrm{c}}\,\expectationvalue{\kappa_5\,\kappa_6}_{\mathrm{c}}+14\,\mathrm{Perm.}\right)+\left(\expectationvalue{\kappa_1\,\kappa_2\,\kappa_3}_{\mathrm{c}}\,\expectationvalue{\kappa_4\,\kappa_5\,\kappa_6}_{\mathrm{c}}+9\,\mathrm{Perm.}\right)

(16)

\displaystyle\qquad+\left(\expectationvalue{\kappa_1\,\kappa_2}_{\mathrm{c}}\,\expectationvalue{\kappa_3\,\kappa_4\,\kappa_5\,\kappa_6}_{\mathrm{c}}+14\,\mathrm{Perm.}\right)+\expectationvalue{\kappa_1\,\kappa_2\,\kappa_3\,\kappa_4\,\kappa_5\,\kappa_6}_{\mathrm{c}}\Big]

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{\MapMapMapEst\, \MapMapMapEst}_{\textrm{G}}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})+\expectationvalue{\MapMapMapEst\, \MapMapMapEst}_{BB}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})+\expectationvalue{\MapMapMapEst\, \MapMapMapEst}_{PT}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})

\displaystyle\quad+\expectationvalue{\MapMapMapEst\, \MapMapMapEst}_{P_{6}}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})\;.

The first term
\expectationvalue{\MapMapMapEst\, \MapMapMapEst}_{\textrm{G}}
is the Gaussian part of the covariance – the only part that is non-zero for Gaussian random fields. It depends only on the two-point correlation of
\kappa
, or, equivalently, the matter power spectrum
P(\ell)
. The other terms comprise the non-Gaussian part of the covariance. They depend on higher-order polyspectra, namely the bi-, tri- and the pentaspectrum.

3.1
Gaussian part

We first concentrate on the Gaussian part
\expectationvalue{\MapMapMapEst\MapMapMapEst}_{\mathrm{G}}
, given by

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\MapMapMapEst\MapMapMapEst}_{\mathrm{G}}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A^{2}}\int\differential[2]{\alpha_1}\int\differential[2]{\alpha_2}W_{A}(\mathbf{\alpha}_{1})\,W_{A}(\mathbf{\alpha}_{2})\,\Bigg[\prod_{i=1}^{3}\int\differential[2]{\vartheta_i}U_{\theta_{i}}(|\mathbf{\alpha}_{1}-\bm{\vartheta}_{i}|)\Bigg]\,\Bigg[\prod_{j=4}^{6}\int\differential[2]{\vartheta_j}U_{\theta_{j}}(|\mathbf{\alpha}_{2}-\bm{\vartheta}_{j}|)\Bigg]

\displaystyle\quad\times\left[\expectationvalue{\kappa_1\,\kappa_2}_{\mathrm{c}}\,\expectationvalue{\kappa_3\,\kappa_4}_{\mathrm{c}}\,\expectationvalue{\kappa_5\,\kappa_6}_{\mathrm{c}}+14\,\mathrm{Perm.}\right]\;.

(17)

We split the permutations in Eq. (
3.1
) into two groups, as

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\MapMapMapEst\MapMapMapEst}_{\mathrm{G}}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A^{2}}\int\differential[2]{\alpha_1}\int\differential[2]{\alpha_2}W_{A}(\mathbf{\alpha}_{1})\,W_{A}(\mathbf{\alpha}_{2})\,\Bigg[\prod_{i=1}^{3}\int\differential[2]{\vartheta_i}U_{\theta_{i}}(|\mathbf{\alpha}_{1}-\bm{\vartheta}_{i}|)\Bigg]\,\Bigg[\prod_{j=4}^{6}\int\differential[2]{\vartheta_j}U_{\theta_{j}}(|\mathbf{\alpha}_{2}-\bm{\vartheta}_{j}|)\Bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left[\expectationvalue{\kappa_1\,\kappa_4}_{\mathrm{c}}\,\expectationvalue{\kappa_3\,\kappa_5}_{\mathrm{c}}\,\expectationvalue{\kappa_2\,\kappa_6}_{\mathrm{c}}+5\,\textrm{Perm.}\right]

(18)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{A^{2}}\int\differential[2]{\alpha_1}\int\differential[2]{\alpha_2}W_{A}(\mathbf{\alpha}_{1})\,W_{A}(\mathbf{\alpha}_{2})\,\Bigg[\prod_{i=1}^{3}\int\differential[2]{\vartheta_i}U_{\theta_{i}}(|\mathbf{\alpha}_{1}-\bm{\vartheta}_{i}|)\Bigg]\,\Bigg[\prod_{j=4}^{6}\int\differential[2]{\vartheta_j}U_{\theta_{j}}(|\mathbf{\alpha}_{2}-\bm{\vartheta}_{j}|)\Bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left[\expectationvalue{\kappa_1\,\kappa_2}_{\mathrm{c}}\,\expectationvalue{\kappa_3\,\kappa_4}_{\mathrm{c}}\,\expectationvalue{\kappa_5\,\kappa_6}_{\mathrm{c}}+8\,\textrm{Perm.}\right]

\displaystyle=T_{PPP,1}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})+T_{PPP,2}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})\;.

The first group consists of six terms, in which for all three
\expectationvalue{\kappa_i\,\kappa_j}_{\mathrm{c}}
, the first index
i\in\{1,2,3\}
, and the second index
j\in\{4,5,6\}
. The second group consists of the nine other permutations. To evaluate
T_{PPP,1}
and
T_{PPP,2}
, we use Eq. (
2
) for
n=2
and

\int\differential[2]{\vartheta}\,U_{\theta}(|\mathbf{\alpha}-\bm{\vartheta}|)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{\ell}\cdot\bm{\vartheta}}=\tilde{u}(\ell\,\theta)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{\ell}\cdot\mathbf{\alpha}}\;,

(19)

and find

\displaystyle T_{PPP,1}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A^{2}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{3}\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell_i}}{(2\pi)^{2}}P(\ell_{i})\right]\,\left[\tilde{u}(\ell_{1}\,\theta_{1})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{2}\,\theta_{2})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{3}\,\theta_{3})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{1}\,\theta_{4})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{2}\,\theta_{5})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{3}\,\theta_{6})+\textrm{5 Perm.}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\times\int\differential[2]{\alpha_1}\int\differential[2]{\alpha_2}W_{A}(\mathbf{\alpha}_{1})\,W_{A}(\mathbf{\alpha}_{2})\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(\mathbf{\alpha}_{1}-\mathbf{\alpha}_{2})\,(\bm{\ell}_{1}+\bm{\ell}_{2}+\bm{\ell}_{3})}

(20)

\displaystyle=\left[\prod_{i=1}^{3}\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell_i}}{(2\pi)^{2}}P(\ell_{i})\right]\,G_{A}(\bm{\ell}_{1}+\bm{\ell}_{2}+\bm{\ell}_{3})\,\left[\tilde{u}(\ell_{1}\,\theta_{1})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{2}\,\theta_{2})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{3}\,\theta_{3})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{1}\,\theta_{4})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{2}\,\theta_{5})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{3}\,\theta_{6})\,+\textrm{5 Perm.}\right]\;,

and

\displaystyle T_{PPP,2}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A^{2}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{3}\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell_i}}{(2\pi)^{2}}P(\ell_{i})\right]\,\left[\tilde{u}(\ell_{1}\,\theta_{1})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{1}\,\theta_{2})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{2}\,\theta_{3})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{2}\,\theta_{4})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{3}\,\theta_{5})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{3}\,\theta_{6})+\textrm{8 Perm.}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\times\int\differential[2]{\alpha_1}\int\differential[2]{\alpha_2}W_{A}(\mathbf{\alpha}_{1})\,W_{A}(\mathbf{\alpha}_{2})\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(\mathbf{\alpha}_{1}-\mathbf{\alpha}_{2})\cdot\bm{\ell}_{2}}

(21)

\displaystyle=\left[\prod_{i=1}^{3}\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell_i}}{(2\pi)^{2}}P(\ell_{i})\right]\,G_{A}(\bm{\ell}_{2})\,\left[\tilde{u}(\ell_{1}\,\theta_{1})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{1}\,\theta_{2})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{2}\,\theta_{3})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{2}\,\theta_{4})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{3}\,\theta_{5})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{3}\,\theta_{6})+\textrm{8 Perm.}\right]\;,

where the geometry factor
G_{A}(\bm{\ell})
, defined by

G_{A}(\bm{\ell})=\frac{1}{A^{2}}\int\differential[2]{\alpha_1}\int\differential[2]{\alpha_2}\;W_{A}(\mathbf{\alpha}_{1})\,W_{A}(\mathbf{\alpha}_{2})\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(\mathbf{\alpha}_{1}-\mathbf{\alpha}_{2})\cdot\bm{\ell}}\;,

(22)

contains the full dependence of the covariance on the survey area and geometry. For a square survey with side length
\vartheta_{\mathrm{max}}=\sqrt{A}
, and
\bm{\ell}=(\ell_{x},\ell_{y})
the factor is

G_{A,\mathrm{square}}(\bm{\ell})=\frac{4\sin^{2}(\ell_{x}\,\vartheta_{\mathrm{max}}/2)}{\ell_{x}^{2}\,\vartheta_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}}\,\frac{4\sin^{2}(\ell_{y}\,\vartheta_{\mathrm{max}}/2)}{\ell_{y}^{2}\,\vartheta_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}}\;.

(23)

The geometry factor
G_{A}
is related to a function
E_{A}(\mathbf{\eta})
, which, for a point
\mathbf{\alpha}
within
A
, gives the probability for a second point at
\mathbf{\alpha}+\mathbf{\eta}
to lie within
A
as well. It is given by

E_{A}(\mathbf{\eta})=\frac{1}{A}\int\differential[2]{\alpha}\,W_{A}(\mathbf{\alpha})\,W_{A}(\mathbf{\alpha}+\mathbf{\eta})\;,\quad\textrm{so that}\quad G_{A}(\bm{\ell})=\frac{1}{A}\int_{A}\differential[2]{\eta}\,E_{A}(\mathbf{\eta})\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\mathbf{\eta}\cdot\bm{\ell}}\;.

(24)

For a square area,
E_{A}(\mathbf{\eta})
was determined by
Heydenreich et al. 2020
. It is unity for vanishing separation
|\mathbf{\eta}|=0
and smoothly declines to zero for
\mathbf{\eta}
outside the square.

3.2
Non-Gaussian part

We now derive the non-Gaussian part of the covariance. First, we calculate the covariance part
\expectationvalue{\MapMapMapEst\MapMapMapEst}_{BB}
, which depends on third-order correlations of
\kappa
. We divide this term into two parts, as

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\MapMapMapEst\MapMapMapEst}_{BB}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A^{2}}\int\differential[2]{\alpha_1}\int\differential[2]{\alpha_2}\Bigg[\prod_{i=1}^{3}\int\differential[2]{\vartheta_i}U_{\theta_{i}}(|\mathbf{\alpha}_{1}-\bm{\vartheta}_{i}|)\Bigg]\,\left[\prod_{j=4}^{6}\int\differential[2]{\vartheta_j}U_{\theta_{j}}(|\mathbf{\alpha}_{2}-\bm{\vartheta}_{j}|)\right]

\displaystyle\quad\times W_{A}(\mathbf{\alpha}_{1})\,W_{A}(\mathbf{\alpha}_{2})\,\left\{\expectationvalue{\kappa_1\,\kappa_2\,\kappa_3}_{\mathrm{c}}\,\expectationvalue{\kappa_4\,\kappa_5\,\kappa_6}_{\mathrm{c}}+\left[\expectationvalue{\kappa_4\,\kappa_2\,\kappa_3}_{\mathrm{c}}\,\expectationvalue{\kappa_1\,\kappa_5\,\kappa_6}_{\mathrm{c}}+8\textrm{ Perm. }\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{\MapMapMapEst(\theta_1, \theta_2, \theta_3)}\expectationvalue{\MapMapMapEst(\theta_4, \theta_5, \theta_6)}+T_{BB}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})\;.

(25)

The first term cancels with the second term in the definition of the covariance of the estimator in Eq. (
14
).
The term
T_{BB}
is

\displaystyle T_{BB}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A^{2}}\int\differential[2]{\alpha_1}\int\differential[2]{\alpha_2}\Bigg[\prod_{i=1}^{3}\int\differential[2]{\vartheta_i}U_{\theta_{i}}(|\mathbf{\alpha}_{1}-\bm{\vartheta}_{i}|)\Bigg]\,\left[\prod_{j=4}^{6}\int\differential[2]{\vartheta_j}U_{\theta_{j}}(|\mathbf{\alpha}_{2}-\bm{\vartheta}_{j}|)\right]\,W_{A}(\mathbf{\alpha}_{1})\,W_{A}(\mathbf{\alpha}_{2})\,

(26)

\displaystyle\quad\times\left[\expectationvalue{\kappa_4\,\kappa_2\,\kappa_3}_{\mathrm{c}}\,\expectationvalue{\kappa_1\,\kappa_5\,\kappa_6}_{\mathrm{c}}+\textrm{8 Perm.}\right]\;.

Using Eq. (
2
) for
n=3
to introduce the bispectrum and the definition of
G_{A}
in Eq. (
22
) leads to

\displaystyle T_{BB}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell_1}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell_2}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell_4}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell_5}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\;B(\bm{\ell}_{1},\bm{\ell}_{2})\,B(\bm{\ell}_{4},\bm{\ell}_{5})\,G_{A}(\bm{\ell}_{1}-\bm{\ell}_{4})

(27)

\displaystyle\quad\times\left[\tilde{u}(\ell_{4}\,\theta_{1})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{2}\,\theta_{2})\,\tilde{u}(|\bm{\ell}_{1}+\bm{\ell}_{2}|\,\theta_{3})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{1}\,\theta_{4})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{5}\,\theta_{5})\,\tilde{u}(|\bm{\ell}_{4}+\bm{\ell}_{5}|\,\theta_{6})+\textrm{8 Perm.}\right]\;.

Second, we calculate the covariance part
\expectationvalue{\MapMapMapEst\MapMapMapEst}_{PT}
. We divide this term into two parts as

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\MapMapMapEst\MapMapMapEst}_{PT}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A^{2}}\int\differential[2]{\alpha_1}\int\differential[2]{\alpha_2}\Bigg[\prod_{i=1}^{3}\int\differential[2]{\vartheta_i}U_{\theta_{i}}(|\mathbf{\alpha}_{1}-\bm{\vartheta}_{i}|)\Bigg]\,\left[\prod_{j=4}^{6}\int\differential[2]{\vartheta_j}U_{\theta_{j}}(|\mathbf{\alpha}_{2}-\bm{\vartheta}_{j}|)\right]

\displaystyle\quad\times W_{A}(\mathbf{\alpha}_{1})\,W_{A}(\mathbf{\alpha}_{2})\,\Big[\expectationvalue{\kappa_1\,\kappa_4}_{\mathrm{c}}\,\expectationvalue{\kappa_2\,\kappa_3\,\kappa_5\,\kappa_6}_{\mathrm{c}}+8\textrm{ Perm. }+\expectationvalue{\kappa_1\,\kappa_2}_{\mathrm{c}}\,\expectationvalue{\kappa_3\,\kappa_4\,\kappa_5\,\kappa_6}_{\mathrm{c}}+5\textrm{ Perm. }\Big]

\displaystyle=T_{PT,1}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})+T_{PT,2}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})\;.

(28)

Here, the first permutations contain all terms where the two-point correlation
\expectationvalue{\kappa_i\,\kappa_j}
has
i\in\{1,2,3\}
and
j\in\{4,5,6\}
. The second permutations contain all other terms. We introduce the power- and trispectrum with Eq. (
2
), and find

\displaystyle T_{PT,1}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell_1}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell_2}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell_3}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell_4}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\;P(\ell_{1})\,T(\bm{\ell}_{2},\bm{\ell}_{3},\bm{\ell}_{4})\,G_{A}(\bm{\ell}_{1}+\bm{\ell}_{2}+\bm{\ell}_{3})

(29)

\displaystyle\quad\times\left[\tilde{u}(\ell_{1}\,\theta_{1})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{2}\,\theta_{2})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{3}\,\theta_{3})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{1}\,\theta_{4})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{4}\,\theta_{5})\,\tilde{u}(|\bm{\ell}_{2}+\bm{\ell}_{3}+\bm{\ell}_{4}|\,\theta_{6})+\textrm{8 Perm.}\right]\;,

and

\displaystyle T_{PT,2}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell_1}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell_2}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell_3}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell_4}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\;P(\ell_{1})\,T(\bm{\ell}_{2},\bm{\ell}_{3},\bm{\ell}_{4})\,G_{A}(\bm{\ell}_{2})

(30)

\displaystyle\quad\times\left[\tilde{u}(\ell_{1}\,\theta_{1})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{1}\,\theta_{2})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{2}\,\theta_{3})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{3}\,\theta_{4})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{4}\,\theta_{5})\,\tilde{u}(|\bm{\ell}_{2}+\bm{\ell}_{3}+\bm{\ell}_{4}|\,\theta_{6})+\textrm{5 Perm.}\right]\;.

Finally, we consider
\expectationvalue{\MapMapMapEst\MapMapMapEst}_{P_{6}}
, which is

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\MapMapMapEst\MapMapMapEst}_{P_{6}}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A^{2}}\int\differential[2]{\alpha_1}\int\differential[2]{\alpha_2}W_{A}(\mathbf{\alpha}_{1})\,W_{A}(\mathbf{\alpha}_{2})

\displaystyle\quad\times\Bigg[\prod_{i=1}^{3}\int\differential[2]{\vartheta_i}U_{\theta_{i}}(|\mathbf{\alpha}_{1}-\bm{\vartheta}_{i}|)\Bigg]\,\Bigg[\prod_{j=4}^{6}\int\differential[2]{\vartheta_j}U_{\theta_{j}}(|\mathbf{\alpha}_{2}-\bm{\vartheta}_{j}|)\Bigg]\,\expectationvalue{\kappa_1\,\kappa_2\,\kappa_3\,\kappa_4\,\kappa_5\,\kappa_6}_{\mathrm{c}}

\displaystyle=T_{P_{6}}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})\;.

(31)

The term
T_{P_{6}}
is, with Eq. (
2
) for
n=6
,

\displaystyle T_{P_{6}}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell_1}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell_2}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell_3}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell_4}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell_5}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\;P_{6}(\bm{\ell}_{1},\bm{\ell}_{2},\bm{\ell}_{3},\bm{\ell}_{4},\bm{\ell}_{5})\,G_{A}(\bm{\ell}_{1}+\bm{\ell}_{2}+\bm{\ell}_{3})

(32)

\displaystyle\quad\times\tilde{u}(\ell_{1}\,\theta_{1})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{2}\,\theta_{2})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{3}\,\theta_{3})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{4}\,\theta_{4})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{5}\,\theta_{5})\,\tilde{u}(|\bm{\ell}_{1}+\bm{\ell}_{2}+\bm{\ell}_{3}+\bm{\ell}_{4}+\bm{\ell}_{5}|\,\theta_{6})\;.

3.3
Connection of covariance terms to aperture mass correlation functions

We now show that the individual covariance terms are related to correlation functions of the aperture mass. While this might appear like a meaningless algebraic exercise, it has an important corollary: Since the correlation functions can be measured, we can estimate all individual terms of the covariance in the validation data. Thereby, the analytic expressions can be validated.

We define the
n
-th order aperture mass correlation functions
M_{\mathrm{ap}}^{n,m}
, as

M_{\mathrm{ap}}^{n,m}(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{m};\theta_{m+1},\dots,\theta_{n};\mathbf{\eta})=\expectationvalue{\Map(\varthetavec, \theta_1)\,\dots\,\Map(\varthetavec, \theta_m)\, \Map(\varthetavec+\etavec, \theta_{m+1}) \Map(\varthetavec+\etavec, \theta_n)}\;.

(33)

This function is symmetric in its first
m
aperture radii and in its second
n-m
aperture radii. The correlation functions can be expressed in terms of the convergence as

M_{\mathrm{ap}}^{n,m}(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{m};\theta_{m+1},\dots,\theta_{n};\mathbf{\eta})=\Bigg[\prod_{i=1}^{m}\int\differential[2]{\vartheta_i}\,U_{\theta_{i}}(\vartheta_{i})\Bigg]\,\Bigg[\prod_{j=m+1}^{n}\int\differential[2]{\vartheta_j}\,U_{\theta_{j}}(|\bm{\vartheta}_{j}+\mathbf{\eta}|)\Bigg]\expectationvalue{\kappa_1\dots\kappa_n}\;,

(34)

where
\kappa_{i}=\kappa(\bm{\vartheta}_{i})
and the average over the convergence contains both connected and unconnected terms.
Using
n=2
, Eq. (
3.1
) and the definition of
E(\mathbf{\eta})
from Eq. (
24
), the first Gaussian covariance term can be written as

\displaystyle T_{PPP,1}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A^{2}}\int\differential[2]{\alpha_1}\int\differential[2]{\alpha_2}\;W_{A}(\mathbf{\alpha}_{1})\,W_{A}(\mathbf{\alpha}_{2})\,M_{\mathrm{ap}}^{2,1}(\theta_{1};\theta_{4};|\mathbf{\alpha}_{1}-\mathbf{\alpha}_{2}|)\,M_{\mathrm{ap}}^{2,1}(\theta_{2};\theta_{5};|\mathbf{\alpha}_{1}-\mathbf{\alpha}_{2}|)

(35)

\displaystyle\quad\times M_{\mathrm{ap}}^{2,1}(\theta_{3};\theta_{6};|\mathbf{\alpha}_{1}-\mathbf{\alpha}_{2}|)\textrm{+ 5 Perm.}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A}\int_{A}\differential[2]{\eta}\;E_{A}(\mathbf{\eta})\,M_{\mathrm{ap}}^{2,1}(\theta_{1};\theta_{4};\mathbf{\eta})\,M_{\mathrm{ap}}^{2,1}(\theta_{2};\theta_{5};\mathbf{\eta})\,M_{\mathrm{ap}}^{2,1}(\theta_{3};\theta_{6};\mathbf{\eta})\textrm{+ 5 Perm.}

Similarly,
T_{PPP,2}
can be written as

\displaystyle T_{PPP,2}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})=M_{\mathrm{ap}}^{2}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\,M_{\mathrm{ap}}^{2}(\theta_{5},\theta_{6})\,\frac{1}{A}\int_{A}\differential[2]{\eta}\,E_{A}(\mathbf{\eta})\,M_{\mathrm{ap}}^{2,1}(\theta_{3};\theta_{4};\mathbf{\eta})+\textrm{ 8 Perm.}\;,

(36)

where
M_{\mathrm{ap}}^{2}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=M_{\mathrm{ap}}^{2,0}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\eta=0)
is the second-order aperture statistic.

The term
T_{BB}
becomes

T_{BB}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})=\frac{1}{A}\int_{A}\differential[2]{\eta}\;E_{A}(\mathbf{\eta})\,M_{\mathrm{ap}}^{3,2}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2};\theta_{4};\mathbf{\eta})\,M_{\mathrm{ap}}^{3,2}(\theta_{5},\theta_{6};\theta_{3};\mathbf{\eta})+\textrm{ 8 Perm.}

(37)

We relate
T_{PT,1}
and
T_{PT,2}
to the correlation functions
M_{\mathrm{ap}}^{4,3}
and
M_{\mathrm{ap}}^{4,2}
. We stress that these correlation functions depend on both connected and unconnected correlations of the convergence field.
Using Eq. (
3.2
), we find

T_{PT,1}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})=\frac{1}{A}\int_{A}\differential[2]{\eta}\;E_{A}(\mathbf{\eta})\,M_{\mathrm{ap}}^{2,1}(\theta_{1};\theta_{4};\mathbf{\eta})\,M_{\mathrm{ap}}^{4,2}(\theta_{2},\theta_{3};\theta_{5},\theta_{6};\mathbf{\eta})+\textrm{8 Perm.}-\big[3\,T_{PPP,1}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})+T_{PPP,2}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})\big]\;,

(38)

and

T_{PT,2}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})=M_{\mathrm{ap}}^{2}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\,\frac{1}{A}\int_{A}\differential[2]{\eta}\;E_{A}(\mathbf{\eta})\,M_{\mathrm{ap}}^{4,3}(\theta_{4},\theta_{5},\theta_{6};\theta_{3};\mathbf{\eta})+\textrm{5 Perm.}-\big[2\,T_{PPP,2}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})\big]\;,

(39)

where the square brackets denote the terms caused by unconnected terms in
M_{\mathrm{ap}}^{4,3}
and
M_{\mathrm{ap}}^{4,2}
.
The term
T_{P_{6}}
cannot be obtained from the aperture mass correlation functions independently of the other covariance terms. However, using Eq. (
3
), the full covariance
C_{\hat{M}_{\mathrm{ap}}^{3}}
is

C_{\hat{M}_{\mathrm{ap}}^{3}}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})=\frac{1}{A}\int_{A}\differential[2]{\eta}\;E_{A}(\mathbf{\eta})\,M_{\mathrm{ap}}^{6,3}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3};\theta_{4},\theta_{5},\theta_{6};\mathbf{\eta})-\hat{M}_{\mathrm{ap}}^{3}(\Theta_{1})\hat{M}_{\mathrm{ap}}^{3}(\Theta_{2})\;,

(40)

so
T_{P_{6}}
can be estimated with

T_{P_{6}}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})=C_{\hat{M}_{\mathrm{ap}}^{3}}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})-T_{PPP,1}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})-T_{PPP,2}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})-T_{BB}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})-T_{PT,1}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})-T_{PT,2}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})\;.

(41)

The expressions of
C_{\hat{M}_{\mathrm{ap}}^{3}}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})
in terms of the
M_{\mathrm{ap}}^{n,m}
also hold if shape noise is present. Measuring the
M_{\mathrm{ap}}^{n,m}
from a convergence field with a noise component
N
is equivalent to replacing the
\kappa
in Eq. (
34
) by
K_{N}=\kappa+N
. Then, as shown in Appendix
B
, Eq. (
40
) leads to

\expectationvalue{\MapMapMapEst\, \MapMapMapEst}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})=\frac{1}{A}\int_{A}\differential[2]{\eta}\;E_{A}(\mathbf{\eta})\,\Bigg[\prod_{i=1}^{3}\int\differential[2]{\vartheta_i}\,U_{\theta_{i}}(\vartheta_{i})\Bigg]\,\Bigg[\prod_{j=4}^{6}\int\differential[2]{\vartheta_j}\,U_{\theta_{j}}(|\bm{\vartheta}_{j}+\mathbf{\eta}|)\Bigg]\expectationvalue{K_N(\varthetavec_1)\dots K_N(\varthetavec_6)}

(42)

which is equivalent to
\expectationvalue{\MapMapMapEst\, \MapMapMapEst}
with shapenoise, as given by Eq. (
B
).

3.4
Large-field approximation

In the previous sections, we have derived six components for the covariance, which might appear analogous to each other. However, we show here that the terms
T_{PPP,2}
and
T_{PT,2}
show significantly different behaviour with survey size than the other terms. For this, we consider the case of a large survey area, for which the window function
W_{A}
can be approximated as one everywhere. In this case, the geometry factor
G_{A}
becomes

G_{A}(\bm{\ell})\rightarrow\frac{(2\pi)^{2}}{A}\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{\ell})\;.

(43)

In this approximation, the terms
T_{PPP,1},T_{BB},T_{PT,1}
and
T_{P_{6}}
become

\displaystyle T_{PPP,1}^{\infty}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A}\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell_1}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell_2}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\;P(\ell_{1})\,P(\ell_{2})\,P(|\bm{\ell}_{1}+\bm{\ell}_{2}|)\,

(44)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left[\tilde{u}(\ell_{1}\,\theta_{1})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{2}\,\theta_{2})\,\tilde{u}(|\bm{\ell}_{1}+\bm{\ell}_{2}|\,\theta_{3})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{1}\,\theta_{4})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{2}\,\theta_{5})\,\tilde{u}(|\bm{\ell}_{1}+\bm{\ell}_{2}|\,\theta_{6})+\textrm{5 Perm.}\right]\;,

\displaystyle T_{P_{6}}^{\infty}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})

and

\displaystyle T_{PPP,2}^{\infty}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A}\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell_1}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell_3}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\;P(\ell_{1})\,P(0)\,P(\ell_{3})\,\left[\tilde{u}(\ell_{1}\,\theta_{1})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{1}\,\theta_{2})\,\tilde{u}(0)\,\tilde{u}(0)\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{3}\,\theta_{5})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{3}\,\theta_{6})+\textrm{8 Perm.}\right]=0\;,

(48)

\displaystyle T_{PT,2}^{\infty}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A}\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell_1}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell_2}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell_3}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\;P(\ell_{1})\,T(0,\bm{\ell}_{2},\bm{\ell}_{3})\,\big[\tilde{u}(\ell_{1}\,\theta_{1})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{1}\,\theta_{2})\,

(49)

\displaystyle\quad\times\tilde{u}(0)\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{2}\,\theta_{4})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{3}\,\theta_{5})\,\tilde{u}(|\bm{\ell}_{2}+\bm{\ell}_{3}|\,\theta_{6})+\textrm{5 Perm.}\big]=0\;.

\tilde{u}(0)=0
for a compensated filter. The same can be observed when writing
T_{PPP,2}
in terms of the aperture mass correlation function (Eqs.
36

, so for a square survey with sidelength
\vartheta_{\mathrm{max}}

\displaystyle T_{PPP,2}^{\infty}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})=M_{\mathrm{ap}}^{2}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\,M_{\mathrm{ap}}^{2}(\theta_{5},\theta_{6})\,\frac{1}{A}\int_{A}\differential[2]{\eta}M_{\mathrm{ap}}^{2,1}(\theta_{3};\theta_{4};\mathbf{\eta})+\textrm{ 8 Perm.}\;,

Since

\mathbf{\eta}
vanishes as

and

declines to zero faster than

. The same argument can be made for

, which depends on the integral of the correlation function

As shown in Eq. (
23
), the geometry factor
G_{A}
is typically a strongly oscillating function. Consequently, integrals over this function are difficult to evaluate numerically. Therefore, in the following, we use the approximations
T_{PPP,1}^{\infty}
,
T_{BB}^{\infty}
and
T_{PT,1}^{\infty}
unless otherwise noted. However, as we will show in Sect.
6

instead of

C
,

can be approximated as

(51)

with
T_{P_{6},\mathrm{2h}}
given by Eq. (

is similar to the finite-field terms

and

since it does not scale inversely with survey area and vanishes under the large-field approximation.

The approximations
T_{PPP,1}^{\infty}
,
T_{BB}^{\infty}
, and
T_{PT,1}^{\infty}
also neglect part of the covariance. However, since these terms are sub-dominant for the total covariance, as we will show in Sect.

4
Attempt at alternative derivation of Gaussian
\hat{M}_{\mathrm{ap}}^{3}
covariance from bispectrum covariance

An alternative approach to finding an analytic covariance estimate of a real-space observable is via the covariance of the corresponding Fourier space quantity. As the
\expectationvalue{\Map^3}
are related to the bispectrum
B
via Eq. (
9
), it might appear natural to derive the covariance of
\expectationvalue{\Map^3}
from the covariance of
B
. However, this is impossible for finite survey areas without using the large-field approximation, even for Gaussian fields. To see this, we rewrite Eq. (
9
) as

\expectationvalue{\Map^3}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3})=(2\pi)^{2}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{3}\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell_i}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{i}\,\theta_{i})\right]\,\mathcal{P}_{3}(\bm{\ell}_{1},\bm{\ell}_{2},\bm{\ell}_{3})\,\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{\ell}_{1}+\bm{\ell}_{2}+\bm{\ell}_{3})\;,

(52)

where we remind that
\mathcal{P}_{3}(\bm{\ell}_{1},\bm{\ell}_{2},\bm{\ell}_{3})=B(\bm{\ell}_{1},\bm{\ell}_{2})
. One could then assume that the covariance
C_{\expectationvalue{\Map^3}}
were given by

C_{\hat{M}_{\mathrm{ap}}^{3}}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})=(2\pi)^{4}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{6}\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell_i}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{i}\,\theta_{i})\right]\,C_{B}(\bm{\ell}_{1},\bm{\ell}_{2},\bm{\ell}_{3},\bm{\ell}_{4},\bm{\ell}_{5},\bm{\ell}_{6})\,\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{\ell}_{1}+\bm{\ell}_{2}+\bm{\ell}_{3})\,\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{\ell}_{4}+\bm{\ell}_{5}+\bm{\ell}_{6})\;,

(53)

where
C_{B}
is the covariance of the bispectrum. Then, it should be possible to infer
C_{B}
from
C_{\hat{M}_{\mathrm{ap}}^{3}}
. However, the Gaussian part of
C_{\hat{M}_{\mathrm{ap}}^{3}}
is

\displaystyle T_{PPP,1}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})+T_{PPP,2}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{6}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{6}\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell_i}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{i}\,\theta_{i})\right]\,P(\ell_{1})\,P(\ell_{2})\,P(\ell_{3})

(54)

\displaystyle\quad\times\Big\{[G_{A}(\bm{\ell}_{1}+\bm{\ell}_{2}+\bm{\ell}_{3})\,\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{\ell}_{1}+\bm{\ell}_{4})\,\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{\ell}_{2}+\bm{\ell}_{5})\,\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{\ell}_{3}+\bm{\ell}_{6})+\textrm{5 Perm.}]

\displaystyle\qquad+[G_{A}(\bm{\ell}_{2})\,\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{\ell}_{1}+\bm{\ell}_{2})\,\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{\ell}_{3}+\bm{\ell}_{4})\,\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{\ell}_{5}+\bm{\ell}_{6})+\textrm{8 Perm.}]\Big\}\;.

For Eq. (
53
) to hold true, we need the expression in braces to equal
f(\bm{\ell}_{1},\bm{\ell}_{2},\bm{\ell}_{3},\bm{\ell}_{4},\bm{\ell}_{5},\bm{\ell}_{6})\,\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{\ell}_{1}+\bm{\ell}_{2}+\bm{\ell}_{3})\,\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{\ell}_{4}+\bm{\ell}_{5}+\bm{\ell}_{6})
for some function

. However, this is impossible unless
G_{A}
is proportional to a Dirac function. Therefore, Eq. (
53
) cannot hold, and
C_{\expectationvalue{\Map^3}}
and
C_{B}
are not related by simple integration.

Under the large-field approximation, though, Eq. (
53
) can be used. We show this in Appendix
D
, where we start from the Gaussian bispectrum covariance from
Joachimi et al. 2009
to recover
T_{PPP,1}^{\infty}
.

7
Discussion

In this work, we derived an analytic model for the covariance of the third-order aperture statistics
\expectationvalue{\Map^3}
. We make our modelling code publically available
3
3

3

https://github.com/sheydenreich/threepoint/releases
.

We performed three tests for this analytic model. First, we compared the Gaussian part of the covariance to simulated GRFs. We found that all elements of the modelled covariance agree with the GRFs within 30%, with most elements agreeing better than 10% with the simulated data. Second, we compared the full model to two independent
N
-body simulations, the SLICS and T17 simulations. These comparisons showed that the model agrees within 1–2 times the statistical uncertainty on the simulations with the simulated covariance estimates. Third, we compared the expected constraints from a KiDS-1000-like survey for the cosmological parameters
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
and
S_{8}
using the covariance from the T17 simulations and the analytic expression. The constraints were remarkably similar, with only a 3% deviation in the FoM on
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
-
S_{8}
. These three tests confirmed that our covariance model is sufficiently accurate for analysing a stage III survey.

Additionally, we found that all terms of the
\hat{M}_{\mathrm{ap}}^{3}
covariance can be estimated by measuring aperture mass correlation functions. Using this approach, we compared the individual terms of the model to measurements in the SLICS and confirmed that the model agrees with the simulations.

In the derivation of the covariance model, we found terms that decrease faster than the inverse survey area and vanish entirely under the large-field approximation, which assumes an infinitely broad survey window function. These finite-field terms show a complex dependence on the survey geometry, while the other terms mainly scale with the inverse survey area. The finite field term
T_{PPP,2}
is already present in the Gaussian case. Comparing the model to GRFs showed that it is the dominating contribution for non-diagonal elements, and neglecting it leads to a severe bias.
However, the effect of the finite-field term is small for the realistic covariances, including non-Gaussianity, as they are dominated by the pentaspectrum term
T_{P_{6}}
. Ignoring the finite-field terms leads to an increment of only 12% in the FoM on
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
-
S_{8}
.

We showed that it is incorrect to derive the covariance for the real-space statistic
\hat{M}_{\mathrm{ap}}^{3}
from a bispectrum covariance. Using an integral over a bispectrum covariance to model the covariance of
\hat{M}_{\mathrm{ap}}^{3}
is only possible under the large-field approximation. However, in this case, one automatically neglects the finite-field terms.

We note that our covariance model assumes the flat-sky approximation. Nevertheless, the favourable comparison to the T17 simulation indicates that this assumption is valid at the relatively small scales considered here. This is not a surprising finding since we consider
\expectationvalue{\Map^3}
at small, sub-degree scales. At these small scales, the flat-sky approximation introduces no significant bias. Our model should therefore be applicable to actual observations.

Our covariance model is based on an estimator for
\expectationvalue{\Map^3}
that is easily applicable to simulations but should not be applied to realistic survey data, which include masks, because it requires a continuous convergence (or shear) map. Instead, one must use an estimator based on the third-order shear correlation functions. However, we have shown that our model can quantify the covariance of this estimator. The model covariance for the entire survey area gives a lower bound to the expected covariance, while the model covariance for a smaller effective area after boundary removal gives an upper bound. For a square survey with an area of
1000\,\mathrm{deg}^{2}
and a boundary cut-off of
4\times$$
, the upper bound is around
15\%
above the lower bound, which gives relatively tight constraints on the true covariance. For even larger stage IV surveys, the bound will be tighter, as the boundary is a smaller fraction of the overall area.

In conclusion, we have presented and validated an analytic model for the covariance of third-order aperture statistics. Together with the analytical model for
\expectationvalue{\Map^3}
presented in
15
, our covariance model paves the way for the cosmological analysis of third-order shear statistics of stage III and stage IV weak lensing surveys.

We have deliberately avoided the term super-sample covariance (SSC) to describe parts of
C_{\hat{M}_{\mathrm{ap}}^{3}}
. This term, originally devised for parts of the covariance of the power spectrum
(
Takada & Hu 2013
)
, is used to describe covariance parts depending on
\ell
-modes larger than a given survey area. The term
T_{P_{6},\mathrm{2h}}
corresponds to what is commonly called SSC for the bispectrum
(
Chan et al. 2018
;
Pyne & Joachimi 2021
)
. However, as we will show in a forthcoming paper, the SSC can be defined as the difference between the exact form of a covariance and its large-field approximation. By this definition, the finite-field terms
T_{PPP,2}
and
T_{PT,2}
are also part of the SSC for
\hat{M}_{\mathrm{ap}}^{3}
.

Appendix B
Impact of shape noise on aperture mass covariance

In this section, we show that the impact of shape noise on
C_{\hat{M}_{\mathrm{ap}}^{3}}
can be estimated by replacing the power spectrum
P(\ell)
in the expressions derived in Sect.
3
by
P(\ell)+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}/2n
, where
\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}
is the two-component intrinsic ellipticity dispersion and
n
is the galaxy number density. For this, we consider the smoothed convergence
K
, which is

K(\bm{\vartheta})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{N}F_{\sigma}(|\bm{\vartheta}-\bm{\vartheta}_{i}|)\,\kappa(\bm{\vartheta}_{i})\;,

(61)

where
F_{\sigma}
is a smoothing kernel of width
\sigma
, the sum runs over the
N
galaxies and
\bm{\vartheta}_{i}
is the position of the
i
th galaxy. We assume that the galaxies are distributed uniformly within the survey area
A^{\prime}
. The kernel
F_{\sigma}
needs to be normalised, such that
\int_{A^{\prime}}\differential[2]{\vartheta}F_{\sigma}(\bm{\vartheta})=1
. As noted in
van Waerbeke 2000
, the smoothed convergence field
K_{\mathrm{N}}
including shape noise can be written as

K_{\mathrm{N}}(\bm{\vartheta})=K(\bm{\vartheta})+\mathcal{N}(\bm{\vartheta})\quad\textrm{with}\quad\mathcal{N}(\bm{\vartheta})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\tilde{F}_{\sigma}(\bm{\ell})\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{\ell}\cdot(\bm{\vartheta}-\bm{\vartheta}_{i})}\left[\cos{2\phi_\ell}\,\epsilon_{1}^{\mathrm{s}}(\bm{\vartheta}_{i})+\sin{2\phi_\ell}\,\epsilon_{2}^{\mathrm{s}}(\bm{\vartheta}_{i})\right]\;,

(62)

where
\mathcal{N}
is the noise contribution,
\phi_{\ell}
is the polar angle of
\bm{\ell}
and
\epsilon_{1/2}^{\mathrm{s}}(\bm{\vartheta}_{i})
are the components of the intrinsic ellipticity
\epsilon^{\mathrm{s}}
of the galaxy at
\bm{\vartheta}_{i}
. In general,
K_{\mathrm{N}}
has an imaginary component. However, aperture statistics, the quantity we are ultimately interested in, do not depend on the imaginary parts, which is why we concentrate on the real part here.

We now introduce a filter function
U^{\prime}_{\theta}
, defined such that

\int\differential[2]{\vartheta}U^{\prime}_{\theta}(|\mathbf{\alpha}-\bm{\vartheta}|)\,K(\bm{\vartheta})=\int\differential[2]{\vartheta}U_{\theta}(|\mathbf{\alpha}-\bm{\vartheta}|)\,\kappa(\bm{\vartheta})\;.

(63)

This indicates that the aperture filter
U
is a convolution of
U^{\prime}
and the smoothing kernel
F_{\sigma}
. Therefore, we can write Eq. (
3
) including shape noise with
K_{\mathrm{N}}
as

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\MapMapMapEst\, \MapMapMapEst}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A^{2}}\int\differential[2]{\alpha_1}\int\differential[2]{\alpha_2}W_{A}(\mathbf{\alpha}_{1})\,W_{A}(\mathbf{\alpha}_{2})\,\Bigg[\prod_{i=1}^{3}\int\differential[2]{\vartheta_i}U^{\prime}_{\theta_{i}}(|\mathbf{\alpha}_{1}-\bm{\vartheta}_{i}|)\Bigg]\,\Bigg[\prod_{j=4}^{6}\int\differential[2]{\vartheta_j}U^{\prime}_{\theta_{j}}(|\mathbf{\alpha}_{2}-\bm{\vartheta}_{j}|)\Bigg]

\displaystyle\quad\times\,\expectationvalue{K_\mathrm{N}(\varthetavec_1)\,K_\mathrm{N}(\varthetavec_2)\,K_\mathrm{N}(\varthetavec_3)\,K_\mathrm{N}(\varthetavec_4)\,K_\mathrm{N}(\varthetavec_5)\,K_\mathrm{N}(\varthetavec_6)}

(64)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A^{2}}\int\differential[2]{\alpha_1}\int\differential[2]{\alpha_2}W_{A}(\mathbf{\alpha}_{1})\,W_{A}(\mathbf{\alpha}_{2})\,\Bigg[\prod_{i=1}^{3}\int\differential[2]{\vartheta_i}U^{\prime}_{\theta_{i}}(|\mathbf{\alpha}_{1}-\bm{\vartheta}_{i}|)\Bigg]\,\Bigg[\prod_{j=4}^{6}\int\differential[2]{\vartheta_j}U^{\prime}_{\theta_{j}}(|\mathbf{\alpha}_{2}-\bm{\vartheta}_{j}|)\Bigg]

\displaystyle\quad\times\,\Big\{\expectationvalue{K(\varthetavec_1)\,K(\varthetavec_2)\,K(\varthetavec_3)\,K(\varthetavec_4)\,K(\varthetavec_5)\,K(\varthetavec_6)}

\displaystyle\qquad+\left[\expectationvalue{\mathcal{N}(\varthetavec_1)\,\mathcal{N}(\varthetavec_2)}\expectationvalue{K(\varthetavec_3)\,K(\varthetavec_4)\,K(\varthetavec_5)\,K(\varthetavec_6)}+\textrm{14 perm.}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\left[\expectationvalue{\mathcal{N}(\varthetavec_1)\,\mathcal{N}(\varthetavec_2)\,\mathcal{N}(\varthetavec_3)\,\mathcal{N}(\varthetavec_4)}\,\expectationvalue{K(\varthetavec_5)\,K(\varthetavec_6)}+\textrm{14 perm.}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\expectationvalue{\mathcal{N}(\varthetavec_1)\,\mathcal{N}(\varthetavec_2)\,\mathcal{N}(\varthetavec_3)\,\mathcal{N}(\varthetavec_4)\,\mathcal{N}(\varthetavec_5)\,\mathcal{N}(\varthetavec_6)}\Big\}\;,

where we used that all odd moments of the noise vanish and that
\mathcal{N}
and
K
are uncorrelated.

The expectation values are taken by averaging over all galaxy positions inside
A^{\prime}
and taking the ensemble average. For the second-order moment of
\mathcal{N}
,
van Waerbeke 2000
showed that this leads to

\expectationvalue{\mathcal{N}(\varthetavec)\,\mathcal{N}(\varthetavec')}=\frac{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}}{2n}\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\bm{\ell}\cdot(\bm{\vartheta}-\bm{\vartheta}^{\prime})}\,|\tilde{F}_{\sigma}(\bm{\ell})|^{2}\;.

(65)

For
K
we find

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{K(\thetavec)\,K(\thetavec')}

\displaystyle=\left[\prod_{g=1}^{N}\frac{1}{A^{\prime}}\int\differential[2]{\vartheta_g}\right]\sum_{i=0}^{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N}F_{\sigma}(|\mathbf{\theta}-\bm{\vartheta}_{i}|)\,F_{\sigma}(|\mathbf{\theta}^{\prime}-\bm{\vartheta}_{j}|)\,\expectationvalue{\kappa_i\, \kappa_j}

(66)

\displaystyle=\frac{N(N-1)}{n^{2}\,A^{\prime 2}}\int\differential[2]{\vartheta_1}\,\int\differential[2]{\vartheta_2}F_{\sigma}(|\mathbf{\theta}-\bm{\vartheta}_{1}|)\,F_{\sigma}(|\mathbf{\theta}^{\prime}-\bm{\vartheta}_{2}|)\expectationvalue{\kappa_1\, \kappa_2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{N}{n^{2}\,A^{\prime}}\int\differential[2]{\vartheta}\,F_{\sigma}(|\mathbf{\theta}-\bm{\vartheta}|)\,F_{\sigma}(|\mathbf{\theta}^{\prime}-\bm{\vartheta}|)\expectationvalue{\kappa^2(\varthetavec)}

\displaystyle\simeq\int\differential[2]{\vartheta_1}\,\int\differential[2]{\vartheta_2}F_{\sigma}(|\mathbf{\theta}-\bm{\vartheta}_{1}|)\,F_{\sigma}(|\mathbf{\theta}^{\prime}-\bm{\vartheta}_{2}|)\expectationvalue{\kappa_1\, \kappa_2}+\frac{\expectationvalue{\kappa^2}}{n}\,\int\differential[2]{\vartheta}\,F_{\sigma}(|\mathbf{\theta}-\bm{\vartheta}|)\,F_{\sigma}(|\mathbf{\theta}^{\prime}-\bm{\vartheta}|)\;,

where we assumed that
N\gg 1
and used that
\expectationvalue{\kappa^2(\varthetavec)}
is the
\kappa

\bm{\vartheta}
. The second summand describes the noise contribution due to the finite number of galaxies
(
Schneider et al. 1998
)
. However, since
\expectationvalue{\kappa^2}
is much smaller than
\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}
for realistic shape noise, this term is small compared to
\expectationvalue{\mathcal{N}(\varthetavec)\,\mathcal{N}(\varthetavec')}
and we neglect it in the following. With the same approximation,

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{K(\thetavec_1)\,K(\thetavec_2)\, \,K(\thetavec_3)\, \,K(\thetavec_4)}

\displaystyle\simeq\left[\prod_{i=1}^{4}\int\differential[2]{\vartheta_i}\,F_{\sigma}(|\mathbf{\theta}_{i}-\bm{\vartheta}_{i}|)\,\right]\expectationvalue{\kappa_1\, \kappa_2\, \kappa_3\, \kappa_4}

(67)

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{K(\thetavec_1)\,K(\thetavec_2)\, \,K(\thetavec_3)\, \,K(\thetavec_4)\,K(\thetavec_5)\,K(\thetavec_6)}

\displaystyle\simeq\int\left[\prod_{i=1}^{6}\int\differential[2]{\vartheta_i}\,F_{\sigma}(|\mathbf{\theta}_{i}-\bm{\vartheta}_{i}|)\,\right]\,\expectationvalue{\kappa_1\, \kappa_2\, \kappa_3\, \kappa_4\,\kappa_5\, \kappa_6}\;.

(68)

We now assume that the width
\sigma
of the smoothing kernel is much smaller than the aperture radii
\theta
. In that case, we can approximate the
F_{\sigma}
with Dirac functions, so

U_{\theta}(\vartheta)=\int\differential[2]{\vartheta'}U^{\prime}_{\theta}(|\bm{\vartheta}-\bm{\vartheta}^{\prime}|)\,F_{\sigma}(\bm{\vartheta}^{\prime})=\int\differential[2]{\vartheta'}U^{\prime}_{\theta}(|\bm{\vartheta}-\bm{\vartheta}^{\prime}|)\,\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{\vartheta}^{\prime})=U^{\prime}_{\theta}(\vartheta)\;,

(69)

and

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\MapMapMapEst\, \MapMapMapEst}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A^{2}}\int\differential[2]{\alpha_1}\int\differential[2]{\alpha_2}W_{A}(\mathbf{\alpha}_{1})\,W_{A}(\mathbf{\alpha}_{2})\,\Bigg[\prod_{i=1}^{3}\int\differential[2]{\vartheta_i}U_{\theta_{i}}(|\mathbf{\alpha}_{1}-\bm{\vartheta}_{i}|)\Bigg]\,\Bigg[\prod_{j=4}^{6}\int\differential[2]{\vartheta_j}U_{\theta_{j}}(|\mathbf{\alpha}_{2}-\bm{\vartheta}_{j}|)\Bigg]

\displaystyle\quad\times\,\Bigg\{\expectationvalue{\kappa_1\,\kappa_2\,\kappa_3\,\kappa_4\,\kappa_5\,\kappa_6}+\left[\frac{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}}{2n}\,\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{\vartheta}_{1}-\bm{\vartheta}_{2})\,\expectationvalue{\kappa_3\, \kappa_4\,\kappa_5\, \kappa_6}+\textrm{14 perm.}\right]

(70)

\displaystyle\qquad+\left[\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}}{2n}\right)^{2}\,\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{\vartheta}_{1}-\bm{\vartheta}_{2})\,\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{\vartheta}_{3}-\bm{\vartheta}_{4})\,\expectationvalue{\kappa_5\,\kappa_6}+\textrm{44 perm.}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\left[\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}}{2n}\right)^{3}\,\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{\vartheta}_{1}-\bm{\vartheta}_{2})\,\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{\vartheta}_{3}-\bm{\vartheta}_{4})\,\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{\vartheta}_{5}-\bm{\vartheta}_{6})+\textrm{14 perm.}\right]\Bigg\}\;,

By decomposing the six- and four-point function into its connected components and considering all permutations, one can show that this is equal to the right-hand side of Eq. (
3
) after replacing
\expectationvalue{\kappa_i\, \kappa_j}
by
\expectationvalue{\kappa_i\, \kappa_j}+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}\,\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{\vartheta}_{i}-\bm{\vartheta}_{j})/2n
. This implies a power spectrum
P^{\prime}
given by

\displaystyle P^{\prime}(\ell)\,(2\pi)^{2}\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{\ell}+\bm{\ell}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int\differential[2]{\vartheta_1}\,\int\differential[2]{\vartheta_2}\left[\expectationvalue{\kappa_1\, \kappa_2}+\frac{\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}}{2n}\,\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{\vartheta}_{1}-\bm{\vartheta}_{2})\right]\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\bm{\ell}\cdot\bm{\vartheta}_{1}+\bm{\ell}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{\vartheta}_{2})}

(71)

\displaystyle=\left[P(\ell)+\frac{\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}}{2n}\right]\,(2\pi)^{2}\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{\ell}+\bm{\ell}^{\prime})\;,

where

is the power spectrum without shape noise. Consequently, replacing
P(\ell)
by
P(\ell)+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}/2n
in the covariance expressions from Sect.
3
gives the correct covariance for
\expectationvalue{\Map^3}
in the presence of shape noise.

We here derive an approximation for

(73)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{6}^{\mathrm{(3d)}}(\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2},\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{k}_{1}-\mathbf{k}_{2},\mathbf{k}_{3},\mathbf{k}_{4},-\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{k}_{3}-\mathbf{k}_{4};\chi)

Appendix D
Derivation of Gaussian
\expectationvalue{\Map^3}

In this appendix, we try to use the Gaussian bispectrum covariance derived by
Joachimi et al. 2009
to obtain
C_{\expectationvalue{\Map^3}}
. We will show that this approach only recovers the large-field approximation
T_{PPP,1}^{\infty}
. In this appendix (and only here), we parameterise the bispectrum by the lengths of three
\bm{\ell}
, namely using

\expectationvalue{\hat{\kappa}(\ellvec_1)\,\hat{\kappa}(\ellvec_2)\,\hat{\kappa}(\ellvec_3)}=(2\pi)^{2}\,B(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3})\,\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{\ell}_{1}+\bm{\ell}_{2}+\bm{\ell}_{3})\;.

(79)

The bispectrum covariance has been derived for Gaussian fields by
Joachimi et al. 2009
. It is

\displaystyle\mathrm{C}_{B}(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3};\ell_{4},\ell_{5},\ell_{6})

\displaystyle=\frac{(2\pi)^{3}\Lambda^{-1}(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3})}{A\,\,A_{R}(\ell_{1})\,A_{R}(\ell_{2})\,A_{R}(\ell_{3})}\,\left[\delta_{1,4}\,\delta_{2,5}\,\delta_{3,6}+\text{5 permutations}\right]\,{P}(\ell_{1})\,{P}(\ell_{2})\,{P}(\ell_{3})\;,

(80)

where
A_{R}(\ell)
is the size of the bin of
\bm{\ell}
, defined as
A_{R}(\ell)=2\pi\,\ell\,\Delta\ell
, the
\delta_{i,j}
denote Kronecker-deltas
and
\Lambda
is defined by

\displaystyle\left[\prod_{i=1}^{3}\,\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell_i}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\right]\,\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{\ell}_{1}+\bm{\ell}_{2}+\bm{\ell}_{3})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\differential{\ell_1}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\differential{\ell_2}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\differential{\ell_3}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\;\ell_{1}\,\ell_{2}\,\ell_{3}\,2\pi\,\Lambda(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3})\;.

(81)

We note that Eq. (
80
) was derived under the assumption that the Fourier transform
\tilde{\kappa}(\bm{\ell})
of the convergence field is known. However, this assumption cannot be fulfilled if
\tilde{\kappa}
is derived from the
\kappa
on only a finite survey area, as the Fourier transform formally requires
\kappa
on all of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Nevertheless, we try to derive the covariance of
\hat{M}_{\mathrm{ap}}^{3}
from
C_{B}
. Using Eq. (
9

81

\expectationvalue{\Map^3}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3})=\left[\prod_{i=1}^{3}\,\int{\frac{\differential{\ell_i}}{2\pi}}\,\ell_{i}\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{i}\,\theta_{i})\right]\,B(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3})\,\Lambda(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3})\;.

(82)

We go from the continuous integration to a discrete sum in the
\ell_{i}
, so

\expectationvalue{\Map^3}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\sum_{ijk}\Delta\ell_{i}\,\Delta\ell_{j}\,\Delta\ell_{k}\,\ell_{i}\,\ell_{j}\,\ell_{k}\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{i}\,\theta_{1})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{j}\,\theta_{2})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{k}\,\theta_{3})\,B(\ell_{i},\ell_{j},\ell_{k})\,(2\pi)^{3}\Lambda(\ell_{i},\ell_{j},\ell_{k})\;.

(83)

Here, the
\Delta\ell_{i}
are the bin sizes along the
\ell_{i}
.
Using Eq. (
83
), we derive
C_{\hat{M}_{\mathrm{ap}}^{3}}
from
C_{B}
with

\displaystyle C_{\hat{M}_{\mathrm{ap}}^{3}}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{6}}\sum_{ijk}\sum_{lmn}\Delta\ell_{i}\,\Delta\ell_{j}\,\Delta\ell_{k}\,\Delta\ell_{l}\,\Delta\ell_{m}\,\Delta\ell_{n}\,\ell_{i}\,\ell_{j}\,\ell_{k}\,\ell_{l}\,\ell_{m}\,\ell_{n}\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{i}\,\theta_{1})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{j}\,\theta_{2})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{k}\,\theta_{3})

(84)

\displaystyle\quad\times\tilde{u}(\ell_{l}\,\theta_{4})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{m}\,\theta_{5})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{n}\,\theta_{6})\,C_{B}(\ell_{i},\ell_{j},\ell_{k};\ell_{l},\ell_{m},\ell_{n})\,\Lambda(\ell_{i},\ell_{j},\ell_{k})\,\Lambda(\ell_{l},\ell_{m},\ell_{n})\;.

So the Gaussian covariance of the aperture statistics is

\displaystyle C_{\hat{M}_{\mathrm{ap}}^{3}}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{6}}\sum_{ijk}\sum_{lmn}\Delta\ell_{i}\,\Delta\ell_{j}\,\Delta\ell_{k}\,\Delta\ell_{l}\,\Delta\ell_{m}\,\Delta\ell_{n}\,\ell_{i}\,\ell_{j}\,\ell_{k}\,\ell_{l}\,\ell_{m}\,\ell_{n}\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{i}\,\theta_{1})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{j}\,\theta_{2})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{k}\,\theta_{3})

(85)

\displaystyle\quad\times\tilde{u}(\ell_{l}\,\theta_{4})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{m}\,\theta_{5})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{n}\,\theta_{6})\,\frac{(2\pi)^{3}\Lambda^{-1}(\ell_{i},\ell_{j},\ell_{k})}{A\,\,\Delta\ell_{i}\,\Delta\ell_{j}\,\Delta\ell_{k}\,\ell_{i}\,\ell_{j}\,\ell_{k}}\,\left[\delta_{i,l}\,\delta_{j,m}\,\delta_{k,n}+\text{5 Perm.}\right]\,

\displaystyle\quad\times P(\ell_{i})\,P(\ell_{j})\,P(\ell_{k})\,\Lambda(\ell_{i},\ell_{j},\ell_{k})\,\Lambda(\ell_{l},\ell_{m},\ell_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}\,A\,}\,\sum_{ijk}\sum_{lmn}\Delta\ell_{l}\,\Delta\ell_{m}\,\Delta\ell_{n}\,\ell_{l}\,\ell_{m}\,\ell_{n}\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{i}\,\theta_{1})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{j}\,\theta_{2})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{k}\,\theta_{3})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{l}\,\theta_{4})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{m}\,\theta_{5})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{n}\,\theta_{6})

\displaystyle\quad\times\left[\delta_{i,l}\,\delta_{j,m}\,\delta_{k,n}+\text{5 Perm.}\right]\,P(\ell_{i})\,P(\ell_{j})\,P(\ell_{k})\,\Lambda(\ell_{l},\ell_{m},\ell_{n})\;.

We evaluate the sums over the
l
,
m
, and
n
using the Kronecker-Deltas, so

\displaystyle C_{\hat{M}_{\mathrm{ap}}^{3}}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}\,A\,}\,\sum_{ijk}\Delta\ell_{i}\,\Delta\ell_{j}\,\Delta\ell_{k}\,\ell_{i}\,\ell_{j}\,\ell_{k}\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{i}\,\theta_{1})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{j}\,\theta_{2})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{k}\,\theta_{3})

(86)

\displaystyle\quad\times\left[\tilde{u}(\ell_{l}\,\theta_{4})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{m}\,\theta_{5})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{n}\,\theta_{6})+\text{5 Perm.}\right]\,P(\ell_{i})\,P(\ell_{j})\,P(\ell_{k})\,\Lambda(\ell_{i},\ell_{j},\ell_{k})\;.

Finally, we go from the discrete sum back to continuous integrals (assuming
\Delta\ell_{i}\rightarrow 0
), so

\displaystyle C_{\hat{M}_{\mathrm{ap}}^{3}}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{3}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\differential{\ell_i}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\ell_{i}\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{i}\,\theta_{i})\right]\,P(\ell_{1})\,P(\ell_{2})\,P(\ell_{3})\,(2\pi)^{3}\,\Lambda(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3})\,\left[\tilde{u}(\ell_{1}\,\theta_{4})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{2}\,\theta_{5})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{3}\,\theta_{6})+\text{5 Perm.}\right]\,\;.

(87)

This can also be written as

\displaystyle C_{\hat{M}_{\mathrm{ap}}^{3}}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{3}\,\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell_i}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{i}\,\theta_{i})\right]\,P(\ell_{1})\,P(\ell_{2})\,P(\ell_{3})\,(2\pi)^{2}\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{\ell}_{1}+\bm{\ell}_{2}+\bm{\ell}_{3})\,\left[\tilde{u}(\ell_{1}\,\theta_{4})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{2}\,\theta_{5})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{3}\,\theta_{6})+\text{5 Perm.}\right]\,

(88)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A}\,\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell_1}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\frac{\differential[2]{\ell_2}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\tilde{u}(\ell_{1}\,\theta_{1})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{2}\,\theta_{2})\,\tilde{u}(|\bm{\ell}_{1}+\bm{\ell}_{2}|\,\theta_{3})\,P(\ell_{1})\,P(\ell_{2})\,P(|\bm{\ell}_{1}+\bm{\ell}_{2}|)

\displaystyle\quad\times\left[\tilde{u}(\ell_{1}\,\theta_{4})\,\tilde{u}(\ell_{2}\,\theta_{5})\,\tilde{u}(|\bm{\ell}_{1}+\bm{\ell}_{2}|\,\theta_{6})+\text{5 Perm.}\right]\,\;.

By comparing Eq. (
88
) with Eq. (
44
), we see that this expression corresponds to
T_{PPP,1}^{\infty}
, which is the Gaussian covariance of
\hat{M}_{\mathrm{ap}}^{3}
in the limiting case of large survey areas
A
. The term
T_{PPP,2}
is not recovered from the bispectrum covariance. This is a direct consequence of the assumption that
\tilde{\kappa}(\bm{\ell})
can be reconstructed from the
\kappa
on the finite survey window
A
in the derivation of Eq. (
80
). This assumption is equivalent to assuming a window function
W_{A}
, which is one on the whole
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, leading to the large-field approximation for
G_{A}
in Eq. (
43
). This approximation directly reduces
T_{PPP,1}
to
T_{PPP,1}^{\infty}
and
T_{PPP,2}
to zero.
