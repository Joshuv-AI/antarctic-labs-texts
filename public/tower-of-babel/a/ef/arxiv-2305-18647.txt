An Alternate Proof of Near-Optimal Light Spanners


Abstract
In 2016, a breakthrough result of Chechik and Wulff-Nilsen [SODA ’16] established that every n n -node graph G G has a ( 1 + ε ) ​ ( 2 ​ k − 1 ) (1+\varepsilon)(2k-1) -spanner of lightness O ε ​ ( n 1 / k ) O_{\varepsilon}(n^{1/k}) , and recent followup work by Le and Solomon [STOC ’23] generalized the proof strategy and improved the dependence on ε \varepsilon . We give a new proof of this result, with the improved ε \varepsilon -dependence. Our proof is a direct analysis of the often-studied greedy spanner, and can be viewed as an extension of the folklore Moore bounds used to analyze spanner sparsity.

Introduction

We study

Definition 1.1

(Spanners

Given a graph

\text{dist}_{H}(u,v)\leq t\cdot\text{dist}_{G}(u,v)

The usual goal is to design a spanner with a favorable tradeoff between its stretch

There are two different ways that spanner size is commonly measured.
One is by the sparsity (number of edges) of the spanner.
The stretch/sparsity tradeoff has long been understood, thanks to the following classic theorem by Althöfer et al.
[

Here and throughout the paper, all graphs are undirected and may have arbitrary positive edge weights.

The other popular way to measure spanner size is by the

In general, the total edge weight required for a

So in order to prove stretch/weight tradeoffs, we typically normalize the spanner weight by the weight of a minimum spanning tree (MST) of the original graph.
This size measure is called

Definition 1.3

(Spanner Lightness)

The lightness of a subgraph

\ell(H\mid G):=\frac{w(H)}{w(\texttt{MST}(G))}

where

is a minimum spanning tree of

One part of this counting argument is the following

Lemma 2.2

(Unweighted Dispersion Lemma)
.

Suppose for contradiction that

This cycle must have

The dispersion lemma implies an upper bound of

The other part of the argument is a

Only the last “full” counting lemma in the following sequence is used, but the proof strategy is to bootstrap it by starting with weaker versions.

Lemma 2.4

If

Since

Since

Thus, any subpath of

Lemma 2.6

(Unweighted Medium Counting Lemma)

k

Proof 2.7

Repeat the following process until no longer possible: find an edge-simple

By the weak counting lemma, we may repeat this process for at least

(Unweighted Full Counting Lemma)
.

Let

If

Let

Let

be the number of edge-simple

On one hand, for any edge-simple

On the other hand, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[p^{\prime}]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}[|E(H^{\prime})|-n]

\displaystyle=|E(H)|\cdot\frac{2}{d}-n

\displaystyle=2n-n

Combining these inequalities, we get

Meanwhile, the dispersion lemma implies that

Comparing these estimates, we get

Rearranging terms in this inequality, we get

Lightness Reductions from Prior Work

3.1
The Weighted Girth Framework

In our previous proof of the Moore bounds, the first step is to observe that the output spanner of the greedy algorithm has high girth.
For lightness, Elkin, Neiman, and Solomon
[
15
]
formalized the analogous method, which we will use in this paper.

Definition 3.1

(Normalized Weight and Weighted Girth
[
15
]
)
.

For a cycle
C
in
G
, we define its normalized weight to be

w^{*}(C):=\frac{w(C)}{\max_{e\in C}w(e)}.

The weighted girth of
G
is the minimum value of
w^{*}(C)
over all cycles
C
in
G
.

Lemma 3.2

(
[
15
]
)
.

The greedy algorithm with parameter
t
returns a graph
H
with weighted girth
>t+1
.

Proof 3.3
.

Let
C
be a cycle in
G
of normalized weight
w^{*}(C)\leq t+1
.
It suffices to argue that not all edges of
C
will be added to
H
.
Let
(u,v)
be the last edge in
C
considered by the greedy algorithm, and suppose that all previous edges in
C
were added to
H
.
Then there is a
u\leadsto v
path in
H
, through the other edges in
C
, of total weight

\displaystyle w(C)-w(u,v)\leq(t+1)\cdot w(u,v)-w(u,v)=t\cdot w(u,v).

This inequality means that the greedy algorithm will reject the edge
(u,v)
, rather than adding it to
H
, and so the cycle
C
does not survive in
H
.

Also note that the greedy algorithm essentially contains a run of Kruskal’s algorithm within it
[
14
]
, and so the output spanner
H
contains an
MST
of
G
.
This means that instead of bounding
\ell(H\mid G)
, we may equivalently bound
\ell(H)
over all graphs
H
of weighted girth
>(1+\varepsilon)2k
.

3.2
Reduction to Unit-Weight Spanning Cycles

It will be convenient in the main proof to reduce to the setting where the spanner
H
has a very particular structure for its
MST

Definition 3.4

(Unit-Weight Spanning Cycles)
.

We say that a cycle
\mathcal{C}
in
H
is a unit-weight spanning cycle if
\mathcal{C}
is Hamiltonian (it contains each node exactly once), all edges in
\mathcal{C}
have weight
1
, and all edges in
E(H)
have weight
\geq 1
.

So, for example, any tree created by deleting any one edge from a unit-weight spanning cycle
\mathcal{C}
is an
MST
.
We will reduce to the case where
H
has a unit-weight spanning cycle; the parts of this reduction all appear implicitly or explicitly in prior work
[
13
,
22
,
15
]
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
H
be a non-forest
n
-node graph with weighted girth
>t
and lightness
\ell
.
Then there exists a graph
H^{\prime}
with
O(n)
nodes, weighted girth
>t

\Omega(\ell)
, and a unit weight spanning cycle.

Proof 3.6
.

We split the reduction into two steps: first we modify to a unit-weight

, and then we change the

into a spanning cycle.

Reduction to Unit-Weight
MST
.

•

Rescale the edge weights of

so that the average edge weight in
\texttt{MST}(H)
is
1

Rescaling affects neither the weighted girth nor the lightness of
H

•

For all edges

of weight

, add new nodes to subdivide
e
into a path of
\lceil w(e)\rceil

Each new edge is assigned weight
w(e)/\lceil w(e)\rceil
.
Notice that:

–

The total weight in
\texttt{MST}(H)
is

after the previous rescaling step, and therefore we add at most
n-1

in this step.

–

Additionally, the weighted girth of

does not change as we subdivide edges.
This holds since for each cycle

is unchanged, and also

must have a heaviest edge that is not in
\texttt{MST}(H)
; since this heaviest edge is not subdivided, the normalized weight

remains the same.

•

Finally, for all edges
e\in E(H)
of weight
<1
, increase

to
1
(this step is applied to both
MST

Notice that:

–

By the previous step every edge in
\texttt{MST}(H)
has weight in the range
[1/2,1]
, and so this step increases

by at most a factor of
2
, and

–

The weighted girth of

is nondecreasing in this step.
This is because for each cycle

is increasing, and moreover: (1) if

has an edge of weight
\geq 1
then its heaviest edge weight does not change, and (2) if all edges in
C

\leq 1
, then after this step all edges have weight

, so its normalized weight is
w^{*}(C)=|C|>t
.

Reduction to Spanning Cycle.

Next, we reduce to the setting where
H
has a spanning cycle
C
.

One of the advantages of reducing to the case where

Lemma 3.7

Let

>t

.
Then all edges in

have weight

Proof 3.8
.

Consider an edge
(u,v)
, and consider the cycle formed by
(u,v)

The normalized weight of this cycle is at most

\frac{w(u,v)+n/2}{w(u,v)}=1+\frac{n}{2w(u,v)}.

By the weighted girth of

, this quantity must be

.
Rearranging, we get

w(u,v)<\frac{n}{2(t-1)}
.

Bucket-Safe Paths and Full Proof Intuition

Our full proof will save a factor of

We will consider two extreme opposing cases for the structure of

In the following discussion we define the

This leads to our second piece of intuition on how to gain a
