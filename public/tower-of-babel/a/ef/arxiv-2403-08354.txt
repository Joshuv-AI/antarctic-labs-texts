Centrality of star and monotone factorisations
Jesse Campion Loth, Amarpreet Rattan

Abstract
. A factorisation problem in the symmetric group is central if any two permutations in the same conjugacy class have the same number of factorisations. We give the first fully combinatorial proof of the centrality of transitive star factorisations that is valid in all genera, which answers a natural question of Goulden and Jackson from 2009. Our proof is through a bijection to a class of monotone double Hurwitz factorisations. These latter factorisations are also not obviously central, so we also give a combinatorial proof of their centrality. As a corollary we obtain new formulae for some monotone double Hurwitz numbers, and a new relation between Hurwitz and monotone Hurwitz numbers. We also generalise a theorem of Goulden and Jackson from 2009 that states that the transitive power of Jucys-Murphy elements are central. Our theorem states that the transitive image of any symmetric function evaluated at Jucys-Murphy elements is central, which gives a transitive version of Jucys’ original result from 1974.

Monotone factorisations

Our focus is on three well-known factorisation problems in

The number of transpositions is

H2’.

The transpositions satisfy H2.

We let

1.2.
Star factorisation and the transitivity operator

We begin by recalling
the notion of a subset of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
acting transitively. For a subset
\{\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{t}\}\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{n}
,
let
\langle\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{t}\rangle
be the subgroup of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}

generated by the subset.
Let
\mathrm{Orb}(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{t})
be the set partition of
[n]
describing
the orbits of the natural action on
[n]
by
\langle\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{t}\rangle
. That is, the elements
i,j\in[n]

are in the same part of
\mathrm{Orb}(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{t})
if and only if there
exists some
\sigma\in\langle\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{t}\rangle
with

\sigma(i)=j
. We say
\{\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{t}\}

acts
transitively on
[n]
if
\mathrm{Orb}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{t})=\{[n]\}
, the set partition of
[n]
with a single part.

For the third factorisation problem, let
\omega\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. A
genus

g
transitive star factorisation
of
\omega
is a tuple of
transpositions
(a_{1}\,n)(a_{2}\,n)\cdots(a_{m}\,n)=\omega

that satisfies the following two conditions.

S1.

The number of transpositions is
m=n+c(\omega)-2+2g
.

S2.

The tuple of transpositions acts transitively on
[n]
.

The terminology “star” comes from the shape of the graph whose edges are given
by the allowable set of transpositions; each transposition must contain the
symbol
n
.
Because all factors contain the symbol
n
, condition S2 is equivalent to requiring
the tuple in a star factorisation to contain, for each
1\leq i\leq n-1
, the transposition

(i\,n)
at least once.
3
3

3

While this simpler condition for transitivity
is more convenient for star factorisations, we presented S2 with the more
general transitivity condition for historical context. Transitivity
conditions are often present in factorisation problems in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
; see Goulden and
Jackson
[
GJ16
]
.
Let
A_{g}(\omega)
be the set of such factorisations
and
a_{g}(\omega)
be its cardinality.
Without the transitivity constraint S2, the number of genus
g
star
factorisations of
\omega
is evidently
[\omega]J_{n}^{m}
.
To account for transitivity, we introduce the
transitivity operator
,
denoted by
T_{n}
. It is defined on products of permutations by

T_{n}(\tau_{1}\tau_{2}\dots\tau_{t})=\begin{cases}\tau_{1}\tau_{2}\dots\tau_{t},&\text{if }\{\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{t}\}\text{ acts transitively on }[n],\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The operator
T_{n}
is analogously defined on all of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}^{t}
, and we extend
T_{n}
linearly to apply to any member of
\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{S}_{n}^{t}
. Thus
the operator
T_{n}
is by definition linear on
\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{S}_{n}^{t}
, but it is obviously not an algebra
homomorphism.

The connection between transitive star factorisations and the transitivity operator is
simple; for
m=n+c(\omega)-2+2g
, we have

(3)

a_{g}(\omega)=[\omega]T_{n}(J_{n}^{m}).

The operator
T_{n}
was introduced by Goulden and Jackson
[
GJ09
]
, who
referred to

as a
transitive power of
the Jucys-Murphy element
J_{n}
. It is easy to verify that
J_{n}^{m}

is not central in general. From (
3
), the function
a_{g}(\cdot)
is central
(
i.e.

a_{g}(\omega)=a_{g}(\gamma)
whenever
\omega
and
\gamma
are conjugate)
if and only if
T_{n}(J_{n}^{m})
is central in the group algebra. We have the following theorem, also found in

[
GJ09
]
.

Theorem 3
.

For any nonnegative integers
t
and
n
, the transitive power

T_{n}(J_{n}^{t})
is central;
i.e.

T_{n}(J_{n}^{t})\in Z(\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{S}_{n})
. Equivalently, for any nonnegative

, the function
a_{g}(\cdot)

is central.

One can easily observe that
T_{n}(J_{n}^{t})=T_{n}(p_{t}(\Upxi_{n}))
, but clearly

J_{n}^{t}\neq p_{t}(\Upxi_{n})
, as seen in the following example where

:

\displaystyle J_{4}^{4}

\displaystyle=15e+8\left((2\,3\,4)+(2\,4\,3)+(1\,2\,4)+(1\,3\,4)+(1\,4\,2)+(1\,4\,3)\right)

\displaystyle\phantom{+++}+3\left((1\,2\,3)+(1\,3\,2)\right)+4\left((1\,2)(3\,4)+(1\,4)(2\,3)\right)\notin Z(\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{S}_{n}),

\displaystyle p_{4}(\Upxi_{4})

\displaystyle=22K_{1111}+8K_{31}+4K_{22},

\displaystyle T_{4}(J_{4}^{4})

\displaystyle=T_{4}(p_{4}(\Upxi_{4}))=3K_{31}+4K_{22}.

In the above equations, partitions are presented as concatenations of integers for easier readability.
That

is central for any

to be the number of genus

More concretely, letting

Since transitivity is essential to centrality for star

We note that if
\omega
and
\gamma
are conjugate and have no fixed points,

[\omega]T_{n}(J_{n}^{\ell})=[\omega]J_{n}^{\ell}=[\omega]p_{\ell}(\Upxi_{n})=[\gamma]p_{\ell}(\Upxi_{n})=[\gamma]J_{n}^{\ell}=[\gamma]T_{n}(J_{n}^{\ell}).

Thus the main content of Theorem

Their results generalise findings of Irving and the second author

1.3.
Main results

We now give the main results of this paper, which tie together the objects
introduced above. Our first result connects monotone double Hurwitz numbers to those
enumerating star factorisations.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\omega\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. Then for any nonnegative integer
g
, we have

a_{g}(\omega)=md_{g}(\omega).

In Section
2
we give a simple recursive proof of this result.
This proof will be independent of the combinatorial constructions found in
subsequent sections.

It was explained Sections
1.1
and
1.2
that
a_{g}(\cdot)
,
m_{g}(\cdot)
and

md_{g}(\cdot)
are known to be central from past results. Our next results
are that these can all be shown
combinatorially
, and our arguments apply
to all
g\geq 0
, thus resolving the problem of finding a combinatorial proof
of the centrality of star factorisations posed by Goulden and Jackson. Our
resolution to this problem takes the following path. Let
\omega
and
\gamma

be conjugate permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and
g
a nonnegative integer. First, we give
bijective proofs of the centrality
m_{g}(\cdot)
and
md_{g}(\cdot)

in Section
3
. In particular, in Corollary
10
,
we give a bijection
\Theta:MD_{g}(\omega)\rightarrow MD_{g}(\gamma)
. Next, in Section
4
, we give
a bijection
\Gamma:A_{g}(\omega)\rightarrow MD_{g}(\omega)
proving

4
.
Finally, composing the bijections

(5)

A_{g}(\omega)\overset{\Gamma}{\longrightarrow}MD_{g}(\omega)\overset{\Theta}{\longrightarrow}MD_{g}(\gamma)\overset{\Gamma^{-1}}{\longrightarrow}A_{g}(\gamma)

gives a combinatorial proof of the centrality of star
factorisations.

It follows from Theorems
2
and
3
that

T_{n}(J_{n}^{t})
is expressible as a symmetric function of Jucys-Murphy elements.
The following corollary, which immediately follows from Theorem
4

and (

), gives such an expression.

Corollary 5
.

For any

and

, we have

T_{n}(J_{n}^{n-1+k})=T_{n}(p_{n-1+k}(\Upxi_{n}))=J_{2}\cdots J_{n}\cdot h_{k}(\Upxi_{n})=e_{n-1}(\Upxi_{n})\cdot h_{k}(\Upxi_{n}).

Our final result, which is proved in Section
5
, is along the lines of Corollary
5
,
and gives a result analogous to the first part of Theorem
2

for transitive images. In particular, it also gives another indirect proof of the centrality of star factorisations.

Theorem 6
.

For any symmetric function
f

T_{n}(f(\Upxi_{n}))\in Z(\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{S}_{n})

1.4.
Hurwitz moves

We introduce a fundamental tool in our analysis below:
Hurwitz
moves
. We give the necessary details here, but the reader is referred to

[
AR14
]
for a different application. For permutations
\gamma

and
\omega
, we set
\gamma^{\omega}:=\omega\gamma\omega^{-1}
. A Hurwitz move takes as input a
factorisation and a pair
\tau\sigma
of adjacent transpositions,
and it outputs a factorisation of the same length with only the factors
\tau

and
\sigma
changed. There are two
kinds of Hurwitz moves.

•

The
rightward Hurwitz move
(RHM) takes
\tau\sigma
to

\sigma^{\tau}\tau
.

•

The
leftward Hurwitz move
(LHM) takes
\tau\sigma
to
\sigma\tau^{\sigma}
.

In the output of an RHM we interpret
\sigma^{\tau}\tau
as the
product of the two transpositions
\sigma^{\tau}
(interpreted as one
transposition) and
\tau
; we do likewise for an LHM. Thus the number of
factors in a factorisation after applying a Hurwitz move is indeed preserved.
Observe that the two moves are inverses of each other. Next, if
\tau
and

\sigma
commute (
i.e.
they have disjoint support) than each Hurwitz move
reduces to simply swapping
\tau
and
\sigma
in the factorisation. Finally,
both Hurwitz moves preserve the product of the moved factors, so both moves
preserve the product of the factorisation.

2.
A first proof of Theorem
4

In this section we give a proof of Theorem
4
by showing
both sides of the equation satisfy the same recurrence. In this proof we allow

[

]

\omega

. To that end, we

and

\gamma\vdash k
, we let

be the

\mathcal{K}^{(i)}_{\gamma}

Now let

\sigma

\sigma

\sigma\tau

i
and

are in the same cycle (
\tau

join
for

\sigma
); otherwise, the symbols

and
j
are in different cycles of

\sigma\tau
(

is a
cut
for
\sigma
).

Case 1:
\tau_{m}=(j\,n)
for some

. Then
\sigma\tau_{1}\tau_{2}\dots\tau_{m-1}=\omega(j\,n)
is a monotone Hurwitz factorisation of
\beta:=\omega(j\,n)
. We then have two cases based on the positions of
j
and
n
in the cycles of
\omega
.

Case 1a:
j
and
n
are in the same cycle of
\omega
. Then
(j\,n)
is a
cut for
\omega
, and
j
and
n
are in separate cycles
\beta
. Then
for some

the symbol
n
is in a new cycle of length
i-t
and
j
is in a new
cycle of length
t
in
\beta
, so
\beta\in\mathcal{K}^{(i-t)}_{\alpha\cup t}
. Here

is obtained as the smallest natural number such that
\omega^{t}(j)=n
. A quick computation gives that
\sigma\tau_{1}\dots\tau_{m-1}
is a

genus factorisation of
\beta
. Since there are
i-1

same cycle as

in
\omega
, we obtain each possible value of

in this way. The contribution from this case is therefore
\sum_{t=1}^{i-1}md_{g-1}(i-t,\alpha\cup t)
.

Case 1b:
j
and
n
are in different cycles of
\omega
. Then
(j\,n)
is a
join for
\omega
, and two cycles of
\omega
are merged into a new cycle
containing

in
\beta
. This means that if

is in a cycle of length

\alpha_{t}
, then
\beta\in\mathcal{K}^{(\alpha_{t}+i)}_{\alpha\setminus\alpha_{t}}
and

\sigma\tau_{1}\dots\tau_{m-1}

\beta
in

\alpha_{t}

\alpha_{t}
. The

\sum_{t=1}^{\ell(\alpha)}\alpha_{t}md_{g}(i+\alpha_{t},\alpha\setminus\alpha_{t})
.

Case 2:
\tau_{m}=(j\,k)
with
j<k<n
. Since the factorisation
\sigma\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{m}=\omega
is monotone, no transposition contains
the symbol
n
. It follows that
\sigma^{-1}(n)=\omega^{-1}(n)
. Set

(7)

\sigma^{\prime}\tau_{1}\tau_{2}\dots\tau_{m}=\beta,

. Note that

can view the factorisation (

\mathfrak{S}_{n-1}
. The construction in (

Clearly

\alpha\cup i-1
. By the centrality of
monotone factorisations, the set

is counted by

md_{g}(\alpha\cup i-1)=md_{g}(i-1,\alpha)
.

Putting these cases together gives the following recurrence, which is the same as for transitive star factorisations:

md_{g}(i,\alpha)=md_{g}(i-1,\alpha)+\sum_{t=1}^{\ell(\alpha)}\alpha_{t}md_{g}(i+\alpha_{t},\alpha\setminus\alpha_{t})+\sum_{t=1}^{i-1}md_{g-1}(i-t,\alpha\cup t).

The initial conditions are also the same for the two problems:
a_{0}(1,id)=md_{0}(1,id)=1
. This proves the equality.

3.
A combinatorial proof of centrality of monotone factorisations

In this section we give combinatorial proofs of the centrality of both types of monotone
Hurwitz factorisations, and we begin with
M_{g}(\omega)
. Let
\prec
be the ordering on
[n]
given by
i_{1}\prec i_{2}\prec\cdots\prec i_{n}
and let
\omega\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. A
monotone factorisation of
\omega

relative to
\prec
is a factorisation where condition H2 is replaced by the
condition that each transposition satisfies
a_{i}\prec b_{i}
and
b_{1}\preceq b_{2}\preceq\cdots\preceq b_{n}
. Let
M^{\prec}_{g}(\omega)

be the set of such factorisations and
m^{\prec}_{g}(\omega)
its
cardinality. Thus for the natural order
<
, we have

M_{g}(\omega)=M^{<}_{g}(\omega)
by definition.

Now let
\prec_{j}
be the ordering obtained from
\prec
, but
i_{j}
and
i_{j+1}

are swapped. We have the following theorem, whose proof is fundamental to this
manuscript. The promised bijective proofs of the centrality of monotone Hurwitz
and double Hurwitz numbers are given in Corollaries
9
and

10
.

Theorem 7
.

Let
\omega\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and
g
a nonnegative integer. Then for any
integer
1\leq j\leq n-1
, there exists a bijection
\Lambda_{j}:M^{\prec}_{g}(\omega)\rightarrow M^{\prec_{j}}_{g}(\omega)
.

Proof.

Throughout, when a transposition
(a\,b)
is written, it is assumed
that
a
is less than
b
in the order being considered. The ordering
at the beginning of the proof will be
\prec
, and this will remain so until
noted.
We define the function
\Lambda_{j}
in two stages. Let
f\in M^{\prec}_{g}(\omega)
.

Stage 1:
The factorisation
f
has a maximal contiguous
substring of transpositions of the form

(8)

\cdots\underbrace{(\cdot\,i_{j})\cdots(\cdot\,i_{j})}_{\text{string }1}\underbrace{(\cdot\,i_{j+1})\cdots(\cdot\,i_{j+1})}_{\text{string }2}\cdots.

String
1
contains all transpositions of the form
(a\,i_{j})
where
a\prec i_{j}
and
string
2
contains all transpositions of the form
(a\,i_{j+1})
, where

a\prec i_{j+1}
.
The transpositions to the left of string 1 contain only symbols less than

i_{j}
under
\prec
, while all the transpositions to the right of string
2

have the form
(a\,b)
where
i_{j+1}\prec b
. The function
\Lambda_{j}
will
produce a factorisation of
\omega
, and it will leave all transpositions
outside of the strings 1 and 2 fixed, so
f
and
\Lambda_{j}(f)
will differ on
only the substring (
8
). Note that
f
may contain some
transpositions of the form
(i_{j}\,i_{j+1})
, and these will only be in string

2
. If either string 1 or string 2 is empty, we skip straight to Stage 2, so we
assume otherwise in what remains of Stage 1.

Beginning with the rightmost transposition in string
1
,
the function
\Lambda_{j}
will apply Hurwitz moves to adjacent
transpositions of the form
(a\,i_{j})
and
(b\,i_{j+1})
according to
the following cases: 1)
a\neq b
and
b\neq i_{j}
; 2)
a=b
; and 3)
b=i_{j}
. Note that
a\neq i_{j+1}
because
a\prec i_{j}\prec i_{j+1}
.

Case 1:

a\neq b
and
b\neq i_{j}
. In this case the two
transpositions have disjoint support and commute, so we may apply either a LHM or RHM. The output of the move is the same in either case, and it is
(b\,i_{j+1})(a\,i_{j})
.

Case 2:

a=b
. In this case we apply a RHM, and the output of
the move is
(i_{j}\,i_{j+1})(a\,i_{j})
.

Case 3:

b=i_{j}
. In this case we also apply a RHM, and the output of the move is

(a\,i_{j+1})(a\,i_{j})
.

In all Cases 1-3, the two transpositions created by the operations are
each, individually, consistent with the order
\prec
. For example, if Case 1 is
applied, both transpositions
(b\,i_{j+1})
and
(a\,i_{j})
are consistent with

\prec
,
i.e.

b\prec i_{j+1}
and
a\prec i_{j}
. This observation includes the transposition
(i_{j}\,i_{j+1})
produced
in Case 2.

After an application of one of the moves above, in all cases the transposition
on the right is
(a\,i_{j})
,
while the transposition to the left contains
i_{j+1}
. We continue to move
the transposition
(a\,i_{j})
to the right in this manner
until there are no transpositions of the form
(b\,i_{j+1})
to the
right of it. We apply this process again for the second
rightmost transposition in string
1
in the original factorisation
f
, and
then repeat. In particular, a transposition of the form
(i_{j}\,i_{j+1})
is never moved to the right.

This ends Stage 1. Notice that all the transpositions originally in string
1
are
preserved, in order, and to the right of all transpositions originally in string
2
of
f
,
all of which may have changed, but all have the property that they contain
i_{j+1}
.
As Hurwitz moves preserve the product of the factorisation, the resulting object is
a factorisation of
\omega
. Thus, at this point, we have a factorisation of

\omega
of the form

(9)

\cdots\underbrace{(\cdot\,i_{j+1})\cdots(\cdot\,i_{j+1})}_{\text{string }1^{\prime}}\underbrace{(\cdot\,i_{j})\cdots(\cdot\,i_{j})}_{\text{string }2^{\prime}}\cdots,

where string
2^{\prime}
is precisely string
1
. This new factorisation may not, however, lie in

M^{\prec_{j}}_{g}(\omega)
because of the presence of transpositions of
the form
(i_{j}\,i_{j+1})
in string
1^{\prime}
(in
\prec_{j}
we have
i_{j+1}\prec_{j}i_{j}
). These transpositions may have been in the original factorisation
f
or
created in Case 2. Transpositions of the form
(i_{j}\,i_{j+1})
are only in
string
1^{\prime}
. We deal with these transpositions now, but we first note that we can recover the original
factorisation by starting with the leftmost factor in string
2^{\prime}
of
(
9
) and reversing the process in Stage 1 by applying LHMs.

Stage 2:
Let the position of the rightmost transposition of the form
(i_{j}\,i_{j+1})
in
string
1^{\prime}
be
r
. To its right is a substring of transpositions in string
1^{\prime}
consisting of transpositions of the form
(a\,i_{j+1})
where
a\neq i_{j}
; let the transpositions in this
string be
(a_{k}\,i_{j+1})
for
1\leq k\leq t
(note that
t=0
is possible
in which case the substring is empty). That is,
the factorisation at the end of Stage 1 has the form

(10)

\cdots\underbrace{(\cdot\,i_{j+1})\cdots(i_{j}\,i_{j+1})\cdots\mathop{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 3.0pt\cr\tiny\downbracefill\crcr\kern 3.0pt\cr$\hfil\displaystyle{(i_{j}\,i_{j+1})}\hfil$\crcr}}}\limits^{\mathclap{r^{\text{th}}\text{ transposition}}}(a_{1}\,i_{j+1})\cdots(a_{t}\,i_{j+1})}_{\text{string }1^{\prime}}\underbrace{(\cdot\,i_{j})\cdots(\cdot\,i_{j})}_{\text{string }2^{\prime}}\cdots.

Move the transposition
(i_{j}\,i_{j+1})
in position
r
to after the
transposition
(a_{t}\,i_{j+1})
, and for each transposition
(a_{k}\,i_{j+1})
,
replace it with
(a_{k}\,i_{j})
. The output of applying this
procedure to (
10
) is

(11)

\cdots(\cdot\,i_{j+1})\cdots(i_{j}\,i_{j+1})\cdots{\mathop{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 3.0pt\cr\tiny\downbracefill\crcr\kern 3.0pt\cr$\hfil\displaystyle{(\phantom{i_{j}\,i_{j+1}})}\hfil$\crcr}}}\limits^{\mathclap{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{2}{r^{\scriptscriptstyle\upth}
{\scriptstyle\text{ transposition}}}{\text{moved}}}}}(a_{1}\,i_{j})\cdots(a_{t}\,i_{j})\mathop{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 3.0pt\cr\tiny\downbracefill\crcr\kern 3.0pt\cr$\hfil\displaystyle{(i_{j+1}\,i_{j})}\hfil$\crcr}}}\limits^{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{2}{{\scriptstyle\text{to}}}{\text{here}}}\underbrace{(\cdot\,i_{j})\cdots(\cdot\,i_{j})}_{\text{string 2'}}\cdots.

We write the new transposition as
(i_{j+1}\,i_{j})
to indicate that it is now
consistent with the order
\prec_{j}
.
We leave it to the reader to check that this move preserves the product of the
factorisation (moving the transposition as stated can be seen as a sequence of RHMs).
Note that if the moved transposition
(i_{j+1}\,i_{j})
is the only such
transposition, then the transpositions
(a_{1}\,i_{j})\cdots(a_{t}\,i_{j})
are the
only transpositions to the left of the newly positioned
(i_{j+1}\,i_{j})
. Thus reversing this procedure is possible.

We repeat this process until all transpositions in string
1^{\prime}
of the form
(i_{j}\,i_{j+1})
have been moved to the right of all transpositions of the form

(a\,i_{j+1})
in string 2’, where
a
is less than
i_{j+1}
in both
\prec

and
\prec_{j}
.
This ends Stage 2 of the process and completes the description of
\Lambda_{j}
.

Stage 2 is reversible because of the following reasoning. We find the leftmost
amongst all the transpositions of the form
(i_{j+1}\,i_{j})
in

\prec_{j}
. Some factors, though potentially none, to its left are of the form

(a\,i_{j})
, where
a\prec i_{j}
(thus also
a\prec i_{j+1}
). We take all these transpositions and reverse the procedure (looking
at (
11
) as a guide). We then continue with the new leftmost factor

(i_{j+1}\,i_{j})
and repeat.

The final output at the end of Stage 2 is clearly a factorisation in
M^{\prec_{j}}_{g}(\omega)
.
The brief arguments above explain how Stage 1 and Stage 2 are both reversible.
It follows that
\Lambda_{j}
is invertible, so
\Lambda_{j}
is bijective.
∎

We state two important corollaries of Theorem
7

Corollary 8
.

Let
\prec
be an ordering on
[n]
.
Then for any nonnegative integer
g
,
there is a bijection
\Lambda^{\prec}:M^{\prec}_{g}(\omega)\rightarrow M_{g}(\omega)
.

Proof.

It is well-known that any permutation can be written as a product of
simple reflections (transpositions of the form
(j\,j+1)
). Fix some such
product for the permutation defined by

\prec
. Then, for each transposition
(j\,j+1)
in the product,
determine the bijection
\Lambda_{j}
and compose them all in order. This is a bijection
from
M^{\prec}_{g}(\omega)
to
M_{g}(\omega)
.
∎

We now give a bijection showing the centrality of the
monotone Hurwitz numbers.

Corollary 9
.

Let
\lambda\vdash n
and suppose that
\omega,\gamma\in C_{\lambda}
.
Then for any nonnegative integer
g
, there exists a bijection
\Delta:M_{g}(\omega)\rightarrow M_{g}(\gamma)
.

Proof.

Since
\omega
and
\gamma
are conjugate, there is a
\delta\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
such that
\omega=\delta^{-1}\gamma\delta
. Let
\prec
be the ordering

\delta^{-1}(1)\prec\delta^{-1}(2)\prec\cdots\prec\delta^{-1}(n)
. Then the
function
\Phi
that takes an
f\in M_{g}(\omega)
to the
factorisation
f^{\prime}
, where
f^{\prime}
is obtained from
f
by replacing every
transposition
\tau
by the transposition
\tau^{\delta}
, is a bijection from
M_{g}(\omega)
to

M^{\prec}_{g}(\gamma)
. However, from Corollary
8
,
there is a bijection
\Lambda^{\prec}:M^{\prec}_{g}(\gamma)\rightarrow M_{g}(\gamma)
. Thus the composition
\Delta:=\Lambda^{\prec}\circ\Phi
is the
requisite bijection.
∎

Above we proved Corollary
9
(centrality of monotone Hurwitz
numbers) from Corollary
8
,
but it is easy to show that Corollary
9
also implies Corollary

8
. Thus the condition in Corollary
8
is
equivalent to the centrality of monotone Hurwitz numbers.

We now give a combinatorial proof of the centrality of monotone double Hurwitz numbers.

Corollary 10
.

Let
\lambda\vdash n
and suppose
\omega,\gamma\in C_{\lambda}
.
Then for any nonnegative integer
g
, there exists a bijection
\Theta:MD_{g}(\omega)\rightarrow MD_{g}(\gamma)
.

Proof.

Since
\omega
and
\gamma
are conjugate, there exists a permutation

\delta
such that
\gamma=\delta\omega\delta^{-1}=\omega^{\delta}
. Let
\prec
be the ordering given by
\delta^{-1}(1)\prec\cdots\prec\delta^{-1}(n)
. We describe the
requisite function
\Theta:MD_{g}(\omega)\rightarrow MD_{g}(\gamma)
as
follows. Let
f=\sigma\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{m}\in MD_{g}(\omega)
be a
factorisation of
\omega
. It
follow that
\sigma^{\delta}\tau_{1}^{\delta}\cdots\tau_{m}^{\delta}=\gamma
, but
the transpositions
\tau_{1}^{\delta},\ldots,\tau_{m}^{\delta}
do not necessarily satisfy the
monotone criteria. Set
\beta:=(\sigma^{\delta})^{-1}\gamma
, so

\tau_{1}^{\delta}\cdots\tau_{m}^{\delta}=\beta
. Then, as in the proof of
Corollary
9
, we see that
\tau_{1}^{\delta},\ldots,\tau_{m}^{\delta}
is a monotone factorisation of
\beta
relative to
\prec
.
The genus
g^{\prime}
of this factorisation is given by
m=n-c(\beta)+2g^{\prime}
, so
\tau_{1}^{\delta},\ldots,\tau_{m}^{\delta}\in M^{\prec}_{g^{\prime}}(\beta)
. Now we apply

\Lambda^{\prec}
from Corollary
8
to
\tau_{1}^{\delta},\ldots,\tau_{m}^{\delta}
to obtain a
factorisation
\tau_{1}^{\prime}\cdots\tau_{m}^{\prime}\in M_{g^{\prime}}(\beta)
.
Finally, set
\Theta(f)=\sigma^{\delta}\tau_{1}^{\prime}\cdots\tau_{m}^{\prime}
. It is
clear that
\Theta
is well-defined. We leave it to the reader to verify it

∎

We note that applying the Hurwitz moves in Cases 1-3 preserves transitivity of
factorisations. This means that the same proof as above shows that

transitive
monotone factorisations are also central. That is,

T_{n}(h_{m}(\Upxi_{n}))
lies in the centre of the symmetric group algebra

Z(\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{S}_{n})
. In Section

we shall see that this centrality still holds if we replace

with any symmetric function.

4.
A bijection between star factorisations and monotone double Hurwitz
factorisations

We now give a bijection that proves Theorem
4
.

Theorem 11
.

Let
\omega\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. Then for any nonnegative integer

g
, there exists a bijection
\Gamma:A_{g}(\omega)\rightarrow MD_{g}(\omega)
.

Proof.

We first describe the function
\Gamma
. Let
f\in A_{g}(\omega)
be given by

f=(a_{1}\,n)(a_{2}\,n)\cdots(a_{n-1+k}\,n)=\omega,

where

k=c(\omega)-1+2g
. Underline the first appearance, when reading from left to
right, of each transposition
(i\,n)
for
i<n
in this factorisation. Each
of these transpositions must appear at least once as the factorisation is
transitive. This gives

(12)

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle=\underline{(i_{1}\,n)}(b_{1}^{2}\,n)\cdots(b_{j_{2}}^{2}\,n)\underline{(i_{2}\,n)}(b_{1}^{3}\,n)\cdots(b_{j_{3}}^{3}\,n)\underline{(i_{3}\,n)}\cdots\underline{(i_{n-1}\,n)}

\displaystyle\cdot(b_{1}^{n}\,n)\cdots(b_{j_{n}}^{n}\,n)=\omega,

which defines the ordering on
[n]
given by

(13)

i_{1}\prec i_{2}\prec\cdots\prec i_{n-1}\prec i_{n}=n.

We explain the notation and properties of
f
in (
12
).

•

There are
j_{p}
transpositions between the underlined
(i_{p-1}\,n)

and underlined
(i_{p}\,n)
, where
j_{p}\geq 0
. These transpositions are
(b_{1}^{p}\,n)(b_{2}^{p}\,n)\dots(b_{j_{p}}^{p}\,n)
.

•

There are
j_{n}
transpositions after
\underline{(i_{n-1}\,n)}
,
and there are no transpositions before
\underline{(i_{1}\,n)}
, so
j_{1}=0
.

•

The underlined
(i_{p}\,n)
occurs in position
\sum_{i=1}^{p}(j_{i}+1)
.

•

For each
p=1,2,\dots,n-1
, there are no factors before

\underline{(i_{p}\,n)}
containing
i_{p}
. Whence, for each
1\leq t\leq p
and
k=1,2,\dots,j_{t}
, we have
b_{k}^{t}\prec i_{p}
.

We now shuffle the underlined transpositions in the factorisation using LHM as
follows. Beginning with
p=2
, we apply LHM to the pair
\tau\underline{(i_{p}\,n)}
,
to obtain
\underline{(i_{p}\,n)}\tau^{(i_{p}\,n)}
. Repeatedly apply LHM to

\underline{(i_{p}\,n)}
and the transposition to its left until the transposition to the left of
\underline{(i_{p}\,n)}
is

\underline{(i_{p-1}\,n)}
. Repeat this for
p=3,\ldots,n
. Once
completed, the first

factors in the product will
be
\underline{(i_{1}\,n)}\dots\underline{(i_{n-1}\,n)}
. Also, since
Hurwitz moves preserve the product of a factorisation, the product of the
factorisation remains
\omega
.

More precisely, when we apply the LHM to
(b_{k}^{p}\,n)\underline{(i_{p}\,n)}
, we obtain

\underline{(i_{p}\,n)}(b_{k}^{p}\,n)^{(i_{p}\,n)}=\underline{(i_{p}\,n)}(b_{k}^{p}\,i_{p})
. As noted earlier,
we have
b_{k}^{p}\prec i_{p}
, so all transpositions are written consistently with

\prec
. Any Hurwitz move involving
(b_{k}^{p}\,i_{p})
at a
later step of this process will be with some
\underline{(i_{p^{\prime}}\,n)}
for
p^{\prime}>p
. Since
b_{k}^{p}\prec i_{p}\prec i_{p^{\prime}}
, the transpositions
(b_{k}^{p}\,i_{p})

and
\underline{(i_{p^{\prime}}\,n)}
commute, so both will be unchanged by the
Hurwitz move. This means that the transposition
(b_{k}^{p}\,i_{p})
appears in the factorisation outputted at the end of the process.

Therefore, this process gives the factorisation

(14)

\underline{(i_{1}\,n)}\dots\underline{(i_{n-1}\,n)}(b_{1}^{2}\,i_{2})\dots(b_{j_{2}}^{2}\,i_{2})\dots(b_{1}^{n}\,n)\dots(b_{j_{n}}^{n}\,n)=\omega

There are
k
transpositions in (

), and all are, crucially, in monotone order
relative to
\prec
.
Observe that
(i_{1}\,n)\dots(i_{n-1}\,n)=(i_{1}\,i_{2}\,\cdots\,i_{n-1}\,n)
and

, so (

) becomes

(15)

\underline{(i_{1}\,i_{2}\,\cdots\,i_{n-1}\,i_{n})}(b_{1}^{2}\,i_{2})\dots(b_{j_{2}}^{2}\,i_{2})\dots(b_{1}^{n}\,i_{n})\dots(b_{j_{n}}^{n}\,i_{n})=\omega.

From (
15
) the process is reversible. That is, given
a full cycle
\sigma=(i_{1}\,\cdots\,i_{n})
and a factorisation
\sigma\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{k}
of
\omega

, we can obviously reconstruct the factorisation in
(

). Then to each underlined
(i_{j}\,n)
, in the

(i_{j}\,n)
and the transposition
immediately to its right until
(i_{j}\,n)
lies to the left of a
transposition of the form
(a\,n)
. This produces a star factorisation.

Call the portion of (

) that is not underlined (the sequence of
transpositions)

and the portion that is underlined (the cycle)

.
Note that product of the transpositions of

equals
\gamma:=\sigma^{-1}\omega
. While the number of

), the genus of

) factorisation of

satisfies

. Thus

, with the ordering

defined by the cycle

,

i.e.

i_{1}\prec i_{2}\prec\cdots\prec i_{n}=n
.
The remainder of the proof uses the bijection
\Lambda^{\prec}
in

\Lambda^{\prec}
we obtain a factorisation

. Replacing

in (

) with

produces a factorisation in

. This last step is reversible. That
is, given a full cycle

between

5.
The transitivity operator on symmetric polynomials of Jucys-Murphy
elements

We now give a proof of Theorem
6
.

Proof of Theorem
6
.

We fix a positive integer
n
throughout and use the abbreviation
T:=T_{n}
. We show that the result is true for the elementary symmetric functions

e_{\lambda}
, where
\lambda
is some partition (
\lambda
does not need to
be a partition of
n
). Since these symmetric functions form a linear basis for
\Lambda
, this proves the theorem. Note that it is not
enough to show the property for
e_{k}
(
i.e.
the one part elementary
symmetric functions) since
T(e_{k})T(e_{j})\neq T(e_{k}e_{j})
. First, recall that by Theorem
1
each permutation
\omega
with
n-k
cycles appears exactly once in
e_{k}(\Upxi_{n})
. We start by giving a more explicit expression for this term.

Let
(a_{1}\,a_{2}\,\cdots\,a_{k})
be a
k
-cycle in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, where
a_{1}
is the smallest symbol. We can write

(a_{1}\,a_{2}\,\cdots\,a_{k})=(a_{1}\,a_{2})(a_{1}\,a_{3})\dots(a_{1}\,a_{k}).

Notice that each transposition is written with the larger symbol on the right,
as
a_{1}<a_{i}
for
i=2,\dots,k
. Now for any
\omega\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
we can
factor each cycle individually as above, then string together each of these tuples of
transpositions to obtain a factorisation
\omega=\tau_{1}\tau_{2}\dots\tau_{n-c(\omega)}
. Since the cycles are disjoint, the sets of transpositions factoring a cycle share no symbols with the transpositions factoring the other cycles. Therefore this set of transpositions satisfies
\mathrm{Orb}(\omega)=\mathrm{Orb}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\dots\tau_{n-c(\omega)})
, and no two
transpositions have the same larger symbol.

Now use Hurwitz moves on adjacent pairs of transpositions in the factorisation, preserving the larger symbols,
so that these larger symbols are increasing. To see why this is possible,
any two adjacent transpositions have the form
(a\,b)(c\,d)
, where
b\neq d
. We may assume
b>d
. If the transpositions have no symbol in common, either Hurwitz
move will work. Otherwise either
a=c
or
a=d
. If
a=c
, then an LHM
suffices. We leave the remaining case to the reader.

This gives a term
\tau^{\prime}_{1}\tau^{\prime}_{2}\dots\tau^{\prime}_{n-c(\omega)}=\omega
appearing in
e_{n-c(\omega)}(\Upxi_{n})
, but since each permutation appears exactly once, this must the unique term equalling
\omega
. Note that since each Hurwitz move preserves the
orbits of the transpositions, this term satisfies
\mathrm{Orb}(\omega)=\mathrm{Orb}(\tau^{\prime}_{1},\tau^{\prime}_{2},\dots\tau^{\prime}_{n-c(\omega)})
.

Now let
m
be a positive integer and
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{\ell})\vdash m
. Extending
Theorem
1
to
\lambda
gives

(16)

e_{\lambda}(\Upxi_{n})=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\omega\\
c(\omega)=n-\lambda_{i}\end{subarray}}\omega.

Therefore applying the transitivity operator to (
16
) gives

\displaystyle T(e_{\lambda}(\Upxi_{n}))=\sum_{(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{\ell})}\omega_{1}\dots\omega_{\ell},

where the sum is over all
(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{\ell})
with

c(\omega_{i})=n-\lambda_{i}
for each
i
, and where
\mathrm{Orb}(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{\ell})=\{[n]\}
.

Now take any
\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. In order to complete the proof we must show that

(17)

\tau T(e_{\lambda}(\Upxi_{n}))\tau^{-1}=T(e_{\lambda}(\Upxi_{n})).

However, for any
\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\dots\omega_{\ell}
, we have

\tau\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\dots\omega_{\ell}\tau^{-1}=\tau\omega_{1}\tau^{-1}\tau\omega_{2}\tau^{-1}\dots\tau\omega_{\ell}\tau^{-1}.

Each
\tau\omega_{i}\tau^{-1}
satisfies
c(\tau\omega_{i}\tau^{-1})=c(\omega_{i})=n-\lambda_{i}
. Also, since conjugating by
\tau
just
permutes the symbols in each factor, the set
\{\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\dots,\omega_{\ell}\}

acts transitively on
[n]
if and only if the set
\{\tau\omega_{1}\tau^{-1},\tau\omega_{2}\tau^{-1},\dots,\tau\omega_{\ell}\tau^{-1}\}
acts transitively on

[n]
. Thus each term on the left-hand side of (
17
)
corresponds to a term on the right-hand side, completing the proof.
∎

3
gives an expression for

, and

Question 13
.

Are there any other symmetric functions
f
for which
T_{n}(f(\Upxi_{n}))
has
a nice expression in terms of other symmetric functions evaluated at the
Jucys-Murphy elements?

Goulden and Jackson
[
GJ09
]
give formulae calculating the numbers

a_{g}(\lambda)
in terms of other factorisations in the symmetric group, so
Theorem
4
likewise gives formulae for some
monotone double Hurwitz numbers. To make these formulae align with the notation of
previous papers, we let
\overrightarrow{H}^{g}(\lambda,(n))=md_{g}(\lambda)
be
the number of genus
g
monotone double Hurwitz numbers of type
\lambda
. Then
simplifying the formula from
[
GJ09
, Theorem 1.1]
gives

(18)

\displaystyle\overrightarrow{H}^{g}_{(n),(n)}=S(n+2g,n-1)/\binom{n}{2},

where
S(\cdot,\cdot)
is the Stirling number of the second kind. That is,

S(n+2g,n-1)
is the number of ways to partition a set of
n+2g
objects into

n-1
nonempty parts. Also, we have

(19)

\displaystyle\overrightarrow{H}^{g}_{1^{n},(n)}=\mathrm{Cat}_{n-1}T(n-1+g,n-1),

where
\mathrm{Cat}_{n-1}
is the
(n-1)^{th}
Catalan number, and
T(\cdot,\cdot)
is the central factorial number: see Exercise 5.8 in
[
Sta99
]
.
That is,
T(n-1+g,n-1)
is the number of ways to partition
\{1,2,\dots,n-1+g,1^{\prime},2^{\prime},\dots,(n-1+g)^{\prime}\}
into
n-1
nonempty parts so that for each part
P
, if
i
is the least integer so that
i
or
i^{\prime}
is in
P
, then both
i
and
i^{\prime}
are in
P
. This formula is equivalent to the recurrence

(20)

\displaystyle n\overrightarrow{H}^{g}_{1^{n},(n)}=n(n-1)^{2}\overrightarrow{H}^{g-1}_{1^{n},(n)}+2(2n-3)\overrightarrow{H}^{g}_{1^{n-1},(n-1)}.

This recurrence is reminiscent of the Harer-Zagier recurrence for one face maps,
and suggests a simple combinatorial interpretation of this type of monotone
double Hurwitz number may exist. Finally, Goulden and Jackson
[
GJ09
]
give a
simple relation between the number of transitive star factorisations and a class
of double Hurwitz numbers, and ask for a combinatorial explanation. In light of
our main bijection, this gives a relation between certain classes of double Hurwitz
numbers and monotone double Hurwitz numbers. More precisely, let
H^{g}_{(n),\beta}
be the number
of tuples
\sigma\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{m}
satisfying, for a fixed
\omega\in C_{\beta}
, conditions H0 and H1, but not necessarily
condition H2 (so the factorisation is not necessarily monotone). Unsurprisingly
these numbers are called
double Hurwitz numbers
.
Then for a positive integer
n
and
\alpha\vdash n
with
\omega\in C_{\alpha}
, we have

(21)

\displaystyle H^{g}_{(2n-1),\alpha\cup 1^{n-1}}

\displaystyle=n!(2n-1)^{n+\ell(\alpha)+2g-3}a_{g}(\omega)

\displaystyle=n!(2n-1)^{n+\ell(\alpha)+2g-3}\overrightarrow{H}^{g}_{(n),\alpha}

The first equality is due to Goulden and Jackson
[
GJ09
, Corollary 1.4]
,
while the second follows from Theorem
4
.
In fact, Goulden and Jackson suggest proving the
first equation of (
21
) combinatorially to prove the centrality
of star factorisations. We do not use their suggested method to prove
centrality, so finding a
combinatorial proof for the first equation in (
21
) remains
open.
Intriguingly, the last equality connects double Hurwitz numbers with monotone
double Hurwitz numbers.
