ON THE ABELIANIZATION OF CERTAIN GROUPS OF FORMAL POWER SERIES


Abstract
We compute the abelianization of the Jennings group 𝒥 k ​ ( ℤ ) \mathcal{J}_{k}(\mathbb{Z}) of powers series with constant coefficient 0 0 , linear coefficient equal to 1 1 and vanishing coefficients in orders greater or equal than 2 2 and less than k k , where k ⩾ 2 k\geqslant 2 . This is accomplished by directly dealing with the equivalence classes in the corresponding abelianizations, in contrast with the work of I. K. Babenko and S. A. Bogatyĭ [ BB08 ] , who give an explicit abelianization morphism for the case k = 2 k=2 .

1
Introduction

Let
R
be a commutative ring. We define the Jennings group over
R
to be

\mathcal{J}(R):=\left\{f\in R[[x]]\penalty\ \bigg|\penalty\ f=x+\sum_{i\geqslant 2}\alpha_{i}x^{i}\right\}.

In this generality, this group was first considered by S.A. Jennings (hence the name) in
[
Jen54
]
.

For each
k\geqslant 2
, we consider the subgroup

\mathcal{J}_{k}(R):=\left\{f\in\mathcal{J}(R)\penalty\ \bigg|\penalty\ f=x+\sum_{i\geqslant k}\alpha_{i}x^{i}\right\}.

The family of these subgroups will be the central object in this article, and we will get more acquainted with them later.

The group
\mathcal{J}(R)
has a natural topological group structure. This can be seen by identifying
R[[x]]
with
R^{\mathbb{N}}
as sets, and thus endowing it with the product topology (if
R
is not explicitly said to be a topological ring, it can be assumed to be one with the discrete topology). Since the (composition) operation in
\mathcal{J}(R)
can be encoded by sums and products of coefficients, this makes it a continuous operation in the subspace topology.

As the naming convention would suggest, we are not the first studying such groups. Even though the study of groups of formal power series dates back to the works of S. Bochner and W.T. Martin
[
BM48
]
, and M. Gôto
[
Got50
]
, it is in his work
[
Jen54
]
that S.A. Jennings proves that
\mathcal{J}(R)
is a topological group under composition, but not necessarily with the same topology that we defined. In his work, Jennings established many elementary results, one of which will be useful for us:

Proposition 1.0.1
.

One has
[\mathcal{J}_{k}(R),\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)]\subseteq\mathcal{J}_{2k}(R)
for all
k\geq 2
.

It is important to note that, in this context, the symbol
[\mathcal{J}_{k}(R),\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)]
refers to the closure of the subgroup generated by the commutators of
\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)
, as opposed to simply such subgroup. Even though we stated that the topology studied by Jennings is not necessarily the same topology we will study in this article, this propositions still holds for our cases.

We will focus on the case of
R=\mathbb{Z}
. This presents some difficulties, in general by the fact that
\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{Z})
is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}
, which is not locally compact and restricts us from using results derived from the existence of a Haar measure, and in particular by the fact that
\mathbb{Z}
has noninvertable elements.

In
[
BB08
]
, I. K. Babenko and S. A. Bogatyĭ, among many other results, prove that the abelianization of
\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{Z})
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\bigoplus(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{2}
. This was done by proving that the function

\displaystyle\varphi:\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}

\displaystyle x+\sum_{i\geqslant 2}\alpha_{i}x^{i}

\displaystyle\mapsto\left(\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}-\alpha_{2}^{\penalty\ 2},\alpha_{4}+\alpha_{5}+\frac{\alpha_{3}(\alpha_{3}+1)}{2}\pmod{2},\alpha_{7}+\alpha_{5}\alpha_{3}+\alpha_{5}\pmod{2}\right)

is a surjective morphism whose kernel is
[\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{Z}),\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{Z})]
. In contrast with this result, we will compute the abelianization of the groups
\mathcal{J}_{k}(\mathbb{Z})
for
k\geqslant 2
without an explicit abelianization morphism. This will be done by directly computing the equivalence classes in
H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(\mathbb{Z}))
. To make this possible, we will prove that for all
k\geqslant 2
, there exists
c_{k}>k
such that
\mathcal{J}_{c_{k}}(\mathbb{Z})\subset[\mathcal{J}_{k}(\mathbb{Z}),\mathcal{J}_{k}(\mathbb{Z})]
. This will give us a simple method to verify some of the properties of the equivalence classes of
H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(\mathbb{Z}))
.

Below we state our main result:

Theorem 1.0.2
.

Let
k\geqslant 2
.

i.

If
k\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, then

H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(\mathbb{Z}))\cong\mathbb{Z}^{k}\bigoplus(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{\frac{k+1}{2}}.

ii.

If
k\equiv 2\pmod{4}
, then

H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(\mathbb{Z}))\cong\mathbb{Z}^{k}\bigoplus(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{\frac{k+2}{2}}.

iii.

If
k\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, then

H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(\mathbb{Z}))\cong\mathbb{Z}^{k}\bigoplus\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}\bigoplus(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{\frac{k-3}{2}}.

iv.

If
k\equiv 0\pmod{4}
, then

H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(\mathbb{Z}))\cong\mathbb{Z}^{k}\bigoplus\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}\bigoplus(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{\frac{k-2}{2}}.

As we mentioned before, this theorem does not give explicit abelianization morphisms. We hope to detect such morphisms in a future work.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Jennings Groups

Before getting into the new results, we will familiarize ourselves with the Jennings groups. We will try to maintain the highest possible generality through this article. With this goal in mind, a torsion-free domain will be any integral domain
R
such that the unique ring morphism
\mathbb{Z}\to R
is injective. For this section, the ring
R
will be a unitary commutative ring, unless specified otherwise.

In general, finding the coefficients of
f\circ g
, for any
f,g\in\mathcal{J}(R)
, is not an easy task. Thankfully for us, given some conditions, we can give a formula for the first two non-linear coefficients of both
f\circ g
and, in some cases, for
[f,g]
.

Proposition 2.1.1
.

Let
f=x+\alpha_{m}x^{m}+\alpha_{m+s}x^{m+s}+\cdots
and

g=x+\beta_{n}x^{n}+\beta_{n+r}x^{n+r}+\cdots
be elements of
\mathcal{J}(R)
. Let
t=\min\{s,r,n-1\}
and

\gamma_{1}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}(m+s)\alpha_{m+s}\beta_{n}&\text{if }t=s\\
0&\text{if }t\neq s\end{array}\right.,\penalty\ \gamma_{2}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}m\alpha_{m}\beta_{n+r}&\text{if }t=r\\
0&\text{if }t\neq r\end{array}\right.,\penalty\ \gamma_{3}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\binom{m}{2}\alpha_{m}\beta_{n}^{\penalty\ 2}&\text{if }t=n-1\\
0&\text{if }t\neq n-1\end{array}\right..

Then:

i.

There exists
E_{f,g}\in x^{m+n+t}R[[x]]
such that

f\circ g=f+g-x+m\alpha_{m}\beta_{n}x^{m+n-1}+(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{3})x^{m+n+t-1}+E_{f,g}.

ii.

If
m>n
and
r>s>n
, then

[f,g]=x+(m-n)\alpha_{m}\beta_{n}x^{m+n-1}+C(m,n)\alpha_{m}\beta_{n}^{2}x^{m+2(n-1)}+\cdots

where
C(m,n)=\binom{m}{2}-(m-n)(m+n-1)
.

Proof.

i.

See Proposition 2.3 of
[
BB08
]
. The proof therein is done for integer coefficients, but it easily generalizes to
R
.

ii.

Using the formula in
a)
we have

\displaystyle f\circ g-g\circ f

\displaystyle=(m-n)\alpha_{m}\beta_{n}x^{m+n-1}+\binom{m}{2}\alpha_{m}\beta_{n}^{\penalty\ 2}x^{m+2(n-1)}+\cdots

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow[f,g]-x

\displaystyle=(f\circ g-g\circ f)\circ(g\circ f)^{-1}

\displaystyle=(m-n)\alpha_{m}\beta_{n}x^{m+n-1}+C\alpha_{m}\beta_{n}^{\penalty\ 2}x^{m+2(n-1)}+\cdots

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow[f,g]

\displaystyle=x+(m-n)\alpha_{m}\beta_{n}x^{m+n-1}+C\alpha_{m}\beta_{n}^{\penalty\ 2}x^{m+2(n-1)}+\cdots.

It follows from the previous equation that

\displaystyle[f,g]\circ(g\circ f)

\displaystyle=g\circ f+(m-n)\alpha_{m}\beta_{n}(g\circ f)^{m+n-1}+C\alpha_{m}\beta_{n}^{\penalty\ 2}(g\circ f)+\cdots

\displaystyle=g+f-x+n\beta_{n}\alpha_{m}x^{m+n-1}+(m-n)\alpha_{m}\beta_{n}(x+\beta_{n}x^{n}+\cdots)^{m+n-1}+C\alpha_{m}\beta_{n}^{\penalty\ 2}(x+\cdots)+\cdots

\displaystyle=g+f-x+((n+(m-n))\alpha_{m}\beta_{n})x^{m+n-1}+((m+n-1)(m-n)+C)\alpha_{m}\beta_{n}^{\penalty\ 2}x^{m+2(n-1)}+\cdots.

Since
[f,g]\circ g\circ f=f\circ g
, we can identify coefficients using the formula in
a)
. From this, we get the following:

\binom{m}{2}=C+(m-n)(m+n-1).

Thus
C
depends only on
m
and
n
, and equals
\binom{m}{2}-(m-n)(m+n-1)
.

∎

In some cases, we will simplify the formula for
f\circ g
by writing

f\circ g=f+g-x+E,

with
E\in x^{m+n-1}R[[x]]
.

It is worth mentioning that the
l^{th}
coefficient of
E_{f,g}
is a multiple variable polynomial evaluated in
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i=m}^{l-1}\cup\{\beta_{i}\}_{i=n}^{l-1}
.

Another concept that will help us in this article is the following:

Definition 2.1.2
.

Let
f=x+\sum_{i\geqslant 2}\alpha_{i}x^{i}\in\mathcal{J}(R)\setminus\{x\}
. We denote the level of
f
as

lvl(f):=\min\{i\geqslant 2|\alpha_{i}\neq 0\}.

For the special case of the identity element, we define
lvl(x)=\infty
.

Said plainly, the level of an element of
\mathcal{J}(R)
is the minimal power of
x
that is larger than
1
and whose coefficient is not
0
.

Thanks to Proposition
2.1.1
, we deduce the following:

Corollary 2.1.3
.

Let
f
and
g
be elements in
\mathcal{J}(R)
. Then:

i.

lvl(f\circ g)\geqslant\min\{lvl(f),lvl(g)\}
.

ii.

lvl(f^{-1})=lvl(f)
, and there exists
E_{f}\in x^{2lvl(f)-1}R[[x]]
such that
f^{-1}=2x-f+R
.

iii.

If
R
is a torsion-free domain, then
lvl(f^{l})=lvl(f)
. Furthermore, for all
l\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
there exists
E_{l}\in x^{2lvl(f)-1}R[[x]]
such that
f^{l}=lf-(l-1)x+R_{l}
.

iv.

lvl([f,g])\geqslant lvl(f)+lvl(g)-1
.

This way, we can rewrite

\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)=\{f\in\mathcal{J}(R)|lvl(f)\geqslant k\}.

In particular
\mathcal{J}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})=\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{Z})
. We now take time to address the fact that, in the literature, these subgroups are usually indexed by
k-1
. We are breaking with this convention because it results in a more natural way of working with the subgroups and their elements, and some constants will become easier to manage. The maths underlying this work is nonetheless invariant, so for anyone who wishes to maintain convention, it suffices to translate where it should correspond.

With this notation, it is easy to prove the following:

Proposition 2.1.4
.

Given
k\geqslant 2
and another integer
l
, one has
l\geqslant k
if and only if
\mathcal{J}_{l}(R)\trianglelefteq\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)
.

For
k\geqslant 2
and
l\geqslant k
, we will write
\mathcal{J}_{k}^{l}(R)
for
\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)/\mathcal{J}_{l}(R)
.

Studying
\mathcal{J}(R)
as a topological group can be cumbersome, in particular for the cases where
R
is not compact. Luckily for us, the subgroups
\mathcal{J}_{k}(\mathbb{Z})
will let us disregard this aspect.

Intuition would suggest that
\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)
is related to

\langle x+\alpha x^{n}|\alpha\in R,n\geqslant k\rangle.

The following lemma confirms some of this suspicion.

Lemma 2.1.5
.

For all
k\geqslant 2
, the group
\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)
is contained in

\overline{\langle x+\alpha x^{n}|\alpha\in R,n\geqslant k\rangle}.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)
. By definition, this means that

f=x+\sum_{i\geqslant k}\alpha_{i}x^{i}.

Define the sequence
\{g_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}}
as

g_{i}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}x+\alpha_{k}x^{k}&\text{for }i\leqslant k\\
g_{i-1}\circ(x+(\alpha_{i}-\beta_{i,i-1})x^{i})&\text{for }i\geqslant k+1\end{array}\right.,

where
\beta_{l,n}
is the
l
th coefficient of
g_{n}
. We will prove that, for any neighborhood
V
of
f
, there exists
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
such that

g_{n}\in V\cap\langle x+\alpha x^{n}|\alpha\in R,n\geqslant k\rangle.

Let
V
be a neighborhood of
f
. Since
R[[x]]
is homeomorphic to
R^{\mathbb{N}}
with the product topology, there exists a finite subset
I
of
\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant k}
and a family
\{U_{i}\}_{I}
of open subsets of
R
such that
\alpha_{i}\in U_{i}
for all
i\in I
, and

\gamma_{i}\in U_{i},\forall i\in I\Rightarrow x+\sum_{i\geqslant k}\gamma_{i}x^{i}\in V.

Let
n=\max I
. Note that
\beta_{i,n}=\alpha_{i}
for all
i\in\{k,\dots,n\}
. This implies that
\beta_{i,n}\in U_{i}
for all
i\in I
. Thus

g_{n}\in V\cap\langle x+\alpha x^{n}|\alpha\in R,n\geqslant k\rangle.

We conclude that
V\cap\langle x+\alpha x^{n}|\alpha\in R,n\geqslant k\rangle\neq\emptyset
for any neighborhood
V
of
f
. This is equivalent to
f\in\overline{\langle x+\alpha x^{n}|\alpha\in R,n\geqslant k\rangle}
. We conclude that
\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)\subseteq\overline{\langle x+\alpha x^{n}|\alpha\in R,n\geqslant k\rangle}
.
∎

Furthermore, if we assume
R
to be Hausdorff, then the equality holds, giving a set of generators for these subgroups. This will let us to check many properties.

Proposition 2.1.6
.

If the topology of
R
is
\mathcal{T}_{1}
, then
\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)=\overline{\langle x+\alpha x^{n}|\alpha\in R,n\geqslant k\rangle}
.

Proof.

By the latter lemma, it suffices to prove that
\overline{\langle x+\alpha x^{n}|\alpha\in R,n\geqslant k\rangle}\subseteq\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)
.

Nota that, under the identification of
R[[x]]
and
R^{\mathbb{N}}
as a topological spaces, we have that
\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)
corresponds to

\pi_{1}^{-1}(0)\bigcap\pi_{2}^{-1}(1)\bigcap\left(\bigcap_{i=2}^{k-1}\pi_{i}^{-1}(0)\right),

where
\pi_{i}
is the projection in the
i
-th coordinate of
R^{\mathbb{N}}
. Since
R
is
\mathcal{T}_{1}
, the set
\pi_{i}(\alpha)
is closed for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and all
\alpha\in R
. Thus the latter set is closed. This implies that
\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)
is closed.

The definition of
\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)
implies that
x+\alpha x^{n}\in\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)
for all
\alpha\in R
and for all
n\geqslant k
. Thus

\langle x+\alpha x^{n}|\alpha\in R,n\geqslant k\rangle\subseteq\mathcal{J}_{k}(R).

Since
\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)
is closed, this implies that

\overline{\langle x+\alpha x^{n}|\alpha\in R,n\geqslant k\rangle}\subseteq\mathcal{J}_{k}(R).

Thus concluding the proof
∎

In order to use this proposition, we will assume that
R
is Hausdorff from here on out.

We can characterize the equivalence classes in the quotients via the following proposition.

Proposition 2.1.7
.

Let
k
and
l
be positive integers such that
l>k\geqslant 2
, and let
p:\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)\to\mathcal{J}_{k}^{l}(R)
be the canonical projection. Let
f,g\in\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)
, with
f=x+\sum_{i\geqslant k}\alpha_{i}x^{i}
and
g=x+\sum_{i\geqslant k}\beta_{i}x^{i}
. Then the following are equivalent:

i.

p(f)=p(g)
.

ii.

\alpha_{i}=\beta_{i}
for all
i\in\{k,\dots,l-1\}
.

iii.

f+x^{l}R[[x]]=g+x^{l}R[[x]]
as elements of
R[[x]]/x^{l}R[[x]]
.

Proof.

It is clear that
b)\Leftrightarrow c)
.

We will prove that
a)\Rightarrow c)
. If
p(f)=p(g)
then, by definition,
there exists
h\in\mathcal{J}_{l}(R)
such that
f=g\circ h
. By Proposition
2.1.1
, this means that there exists
E\in x^{k+l-1}
such that

f=g+h-x+E.

Thus

f-g=h-x+E\in x^{l}R[[x]].

This implies that
c)
holds.

Now, we will prove that
b)\Rightarrow a)
. If
b)
holds, then
f-g=E\in x^{l}R[[x]]
. We see that

g\circ f^{-1}=(f-E)(f^{-1})=f\circ f^{-1}-E(f^{-1})=x+(-E)(f^{-1})\in\mathcal{J}_{l}(R).

This implies that
a)
holds.
∎

In virtue of the latter proposition, we will write the class of
f=x+\sum_{i\geqslant k}\alpha_{i}x^{i}
in
\mathcal{J}_{k}^{l}(R)
as
f+O(x^{l})
or
x+\sum_{i=k}^{k-1}\alpha_{i}x^{i}+O(x^{l})
. Furthermore, we can compute the class of
f\circ g
with the operations of
R[[x]]/x^{l}R[[x]]
. This allows giving a set of generators for
\mathcal{J}_{k}^{l}(R)
, as stated below.

Corollary 2.1.8
.

For every
l\geq k
one has

\mathcal{J}_{k}^{l}(R)=\langle x+\alpha x^{n}+O(x^{l})|\alpha\in R,k\leqslant n\leqslant l-1\rangle.

Proof.

Given
\tilde{f}\in\mathcal{J}_{k}^{l}(R)
, let
f\in\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)
be such that
f+O(x^{l})=\tilde{f}
. Take
\{g_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}}
as in the proof of Lemma
2.1.5
. Note that
g_{l-1}
has the same coefficients as
f
up to the
(l-1)^{th}
power of
x
. By the latter proposition, this implies that

\tilde{f}=f+O(x^{l})=g_{l-1}+O(x^{l})\in\langle x+\alpha x^{n}+O(x^{l})|\alpha\in R,k\leqslant n\leqslant l-1\rangle.

Since
\tilde{f}
is arbitrary, we conclude that
\mathcal{J}_{k}^{l}(R)\subseteq\langle x+\alpha x^{n}|\alpha\in R,n\geqslant k\rangle
.
∎

We now have a better understanding of the groups
\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)
, which, if a bit rudimentary, will let us move forward in their study.

2.2
A useful lemma

In this section we will present a result that will be central in this article.

This is inspired by the work of I. K. Babenko and S. A. Bogatyĭ
[
BB08
]
and proceeds as follows: given
k\geqslant 2
, we will find
c\geqslant k
such that

\mathcal{J}_{c}(R)\subset[\mathcal{J}_{k}(R),\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)]
.

Recall that
\mathcal{J}_{c}(R)=\overline{\langle x+\alpha x^{l}|\alpha\in R,l\geqslant c\rangle}
. Thus, our goal translates into finding

c>k
such that
x+\alpha x^{l}\in[\mathcal{J}_{k}(R),\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)]
for all
\alpha\in R
, and all
l\geqslant c
. Verifying this condition directly is not easy, but we will slightly modify it to an easier-to-check yet equivalent condition.

Proposition 2.2.1
.

Let
k\geqslant 2
. If
c\geqslant k
, then
x+\alpha x^{l}\in[\mathcal{J}_{k}(R),\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)]
for all
\alpha\in R
and all
l\geqslant c
if and only if for all
\alpha\in R
and all
l\geqslant c
, there exists an element
x+\alpha x^{l}+\cdots
in
[\mathcal{J}_{k}(R),\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)]
.

The proof of the latter proposition is analogous to the proof of Lemma
2.1.5
.

Remember that, if
f=x+\alpha_{m}x^{m}+\alpha_{m+s}x^{m+s}+\cdots
and
g=x+\beta_{n}x^{n}+\beta_{n+r}x^{n+r}+\cdots
are elements of
\mathcal{J}(R)
such that
m>n
and
r>s\geqslant n
, then

[f,g]=x+(m-n)\alpha_{m}\beta_{n}x^{m+n-1}+C(m,n)\alpha_{m}\beta_{n}^{2}x^{m+2(n-1)}+\cdots.

The following is a consequence of this:

Proposition 2.2.2
.

Let
k\geqslant 2
.

i.

For all
\alpha\in R
and
l\geqslant k
, there exists an element
x+\alpha x^{2l}+\cdots
in
[\mathcal{J}_{k}(R),\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)]
.

ii.

Let
l\geqslant k
and
\alpha\in R
. Let
n\in\mathbb{Z}
be such that
n\equiv 1\pmod{2}
. If there is an element
x+n\alpha x^{2l+1}+\cdots
in
[\mathcal{J}_{k}(R),\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)]
,
then there is an element
x+\alpha x^{2l+1}+\cdots
in
[\mathcal{J}_{k}(R),\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)]
.

iii.

If
R
is a domain such that
2\in R^{*}
, then for all
\alpha\in R
and
l\geqslant k
, there exists an element
x+\alpha x^{2l+1}+\cdots
in
[\mathcal{J}_{k}(R),\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)]
.

Proof.

i.

Let
l\geqslant k
and
\alpha\in R
. It suffices to note that

[x+\alpha x^{l+1},x+x^{l}]=x+\alpha x^{2l}+C(l+1,l)\alpha x^{3l-1}+\cdots

is such an element.

ii.

By hypothesis, we know that there exists

m\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
n=1+2m
. Furthermore

[x+\alpha x^{l+2},x+x^{l}]=x+2\alpha x^{2l+1}+C(l+2,l)\alpha x^{3l}+\cdots.

Thus, we have that

(x+n\alpha x^{2l+1}+\cdots)\circ[x+\alpha x^{l+2},x+x^{l}]^{-m}=x+\alpha x^{2l+1}+\cdots.

Since
[\mathcal{J}_{k}(R),\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)]
is a group, this implies that
x+\alpha x^{2l+1}+\cdots
is contained in it.

iii.

Let
l\geqslant k
and
\alpha\in R
. It suffices to note that

[x+2^{-1}x^{l+2},x+\alpha x^{l}]=x+\alpha x^{2l+1}+C(l+2,l)2^{-1}\alpha^{2}x^{3l}+\cdots

is such an element.

∎

Remark 2.2.3
.

For the case of a domain
R
such that
2\in R^{*}
, this proves that

\mathcal{J}_{2k}(R)\subset[\mathcal{J}_{k}(R),\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)].

We will return to this result later.

For the case of a torsion-free domain
R
, we note that the elements of the form
[x+\alpha x^{l+1},x+x^{l}]
and
[x+\alpha x^{l+2},x+x^{l}]
will play an important role, since they will be the main tool for us to compute classes in
H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(R))
.

Given
k\geqslant 2
, the latter proposition reduces our problem to determine for which
l\geqslant k
and
\alpha\in R

there exists a product of commutators equal to some
x+n\alpha x^{2l+1}+\cdots
with
n\equiv 1\pmod{2}
.
The rest of this section is dedicated to the manufacture of such elements via the following proposition:

Proposition 2.2.4
.

Let
R
be torsion-free domain. Given
k\geqslant 2

and

m-2>n

Then, for all

Proof.

Let

[x+\alpha x^{\frac{m+n-1}{2}+1},x+x^{\frac{m+n-1}{2}}]^{-(m-n)}=x+(-(m-n))\alpha x^{m+n-1}+E,

On the other hand, if

with

[x+\alpha x^{m},x+x^{n}]\circ[x+\alpha x^{\frac{m+n-2}{2}+2},x+x^{\frac{m+n-2}{2}}]^{-\frac{m-n}{2}}=x+C(m,n)\alpha x^{m+2(n-1)}+\cdots.

Let
\ell(m,n):=m+2(n-1)

\ell(m,n)\equiv m\pmod{2}

Given an odd integer

and
\alpha\in R

Proposition 2.2.5
.

Let
m
and
n
be positive integers such that
m>n
. Then
C(m,n)\equiv 1\pmod{2}
if and only if
m\equiv 2\pmod{4}
or
m\equiv 3\pmod{4}
.

Proof.

Recall that

C(m,n)=\binom{m}{2}+(m-n)(m+n-1).

By noting that
(m-n)(m+n-1)=m^{2}-m-n^{2}+n
, it is clear that
C(m,n)\equiv\binom{m}{2}\pmod{2}
.

It can be easily seen that
\binom{m}{2}\equiv 1\pmod{2}
if and only if
m\equiv 2\pmod{4}
or
m\equiv 3\pmod{4}
. Thus,
C(m,n)\equiv 1\pmod{2}
if and only if
m\equiv 2\pmod{4}
or
m\equiv 3\pmod{4}
.
∎

Thanks to the latter proposition, given an odd integer

Definition 2.2.6
.

We say a pair

Given

Remark 2.2.8
.

Let

, with

Thus, given

and an odd integer

-useful pair
(m,n)
such that

and

Proposition 2.2.9

Let

s\in\mathbb{Z}

It is clear that

and that
(n+8,n)

\ell(n+6,n)=n+6+2(n-1)=3n+3+1=3(n+1)+1=l.

It is clear that

It is clear that

∎

The latter lemma has two main consequences, the first one of which is:

Remark 2.2.12
.

Let
k\geqslant 2
. Let
R
be a torsion-free domain and
\pi:\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)\to H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(R))
the natural projection. The following diagram commutes:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(R))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{J}_{k}^{c_{k}}(R)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tilde{\pi}}

Said informally: the classes in

can be computed using the classes of
\mathcal{J}_{k}^{c_{k}}(R)
. This is very useful, since
\mathcal{J}_{k}^{c_{k}}(R)

The second consequence is more important. For a torsion-free domain

\mathcal{J}_{c_{k}}(R)=[\mathcal{J}_{k}(R),\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)]

\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)
is in
[\mathcal{J}_{k}(R),\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)]
or not. With the latter remark, we have a good tool to make some headway into the question of when two elements are in the same class in

3
Main results

As we discussed in the introduction, our main objective is to prove the following theorem:

Theorem
1.0.2

Let
k\geqslant 2
.

i.

If
k\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, then

H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(\mathbb{Z}))\cong\mathbb{Z}^{k}\bigoplus(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{\frac{k+1}{2}}.

ii.

If
k\equiv 2\pmod{4}
, then

H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(\mathbb{Z}))\cong\mathbb{Z}^{k}\bigoplus(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{\frac{k+2}{2}}.

iii.

If
k\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, then

H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(\mathbb{Z}))\cong\mathbb{Z}^{k}\bigoplus\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}\bigoplus(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{\frac{k-3}{2}}.

iv.

If
k\equiv 0\pmod{4}
, then

H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(\mathbb{Z}))\cong\mathbb{Z}^{k}\bigoplus\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}\bigoplus(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{\frac{k-2}{2}}.

As in the previous section, we will give the results in the greatest possible generality we were able to prove. At some point we will have to restrict ourselves to
\mathbb{Z}
in order to do computations.

For the rest of the section, we will fix
k\geqslant 2
and denote by
\pi:\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)\to H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(R))
the natural projection. We will prove the main theorem via a series of intermediate results.

3.1
Identifying the torsion of
H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(R))

As the statement of the main theorem would suggest,
determining the torsion of
H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(\mathbb{Z}))
is the part that will give us more problems. For this reason, we will dedicate this subsection to giving a more amenable perspective on the torsion of
H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(R))
, for the cases where either
R
is a domain such that
2\in R^{*}
or
R
is a torsion-free domain. We will denote the torsion of
H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(R))
by
T(H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)))
.

The first result in this direction is one from the original paper
[
Jen54
]
, in which, among many other results, S. A. Jennings proved the following.

Proposition
1.0.1
For all
k\geqslant 2
, one has
[\mathcal{J}_{k}(R),\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)]\subset\mathcal{J}_{2k}(R).

This actually yields an answer for the case of
R
being a domain such that
2\in R^{*}
.

Corollary 3.1.1
.

If
R
is a domain such that
2\in R^{*}
, then
[\mathcal{J}_{k}(R),\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)]=\mathcal{J}_{2k}(R).

Proof.

This follows from Proposition
1.0.1
and Remark
2.2.3

∎

For our other case of interest, we will have to do a bit more work. We will prove the following proposition:

Proposition 3.1.2
.

If
R
is a torsion-free domain, then
T(H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)))=\pi(\mathcal{J}_{2k}(R))
.

In order to prove this, we will prove that
T(H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)))
is contained in
\pi(\mathcal{J}_{2k}(R))
and vice-versa. We will assume that
R
is a torsion-free domain for the rest of the section.

Proposition 3.1.3
.

If
R
is a torsion-free domain, then
T(H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)))\subseteq\pi(\mathcal{J}_{2k}(R))
.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{f}\in T(H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)))
. Since
\pi
is surjective, there exists
f\in\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)
such that
\pi(f)=\tilde{f}
. By definition, there exists
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
such that
\tilde{f}^{n}=0
. Since

[\mathcal{J}_{k}(R),\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)]=ker(\pi),

this is equivalent to
f^{n}\in[\mathcal{J}_{k}(R),\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)]
. By Proposition
1.0.1
, this implies that
lvl(f^{n})\geqslant 2k
. By Corollary
2.1.3
, we have that
lvl(f)=lvl(f^{n})
. Thus
lvl(f)\geqslant 2k
. This implies that
f\in\mathcal{J}_{2k}(R)
. Therefore
\pi(f)\in\pi(\mathcal{J}_{2k}(R))
. We conclude that
\pi(\mathcal{J}_{2k}(R))\supseteq T(H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)))
.
∎

Proving the inverse containment will require more work. We will prove that
\pi(\mathcal{J}_{2k}(R))
is generated by elements of finite order.

Remark 3.1.4
.

By Remark
2.2.12
, we have that

\pi(\mathcal{J}_{2k}(R))=\tilde{\pi}(\mathcal{J}_{2k}^{c_{k}}(R)).

Since

\mathcal{J}_{k}^{l}(R)=\langle x+\alpha x^{n}+O(x^{c_{k}})|\alpha\in R,k\leqslant n\leqslant l-1\rangle,

the latter implies that

\tilde{\pi}(\mathcal{J}_{2k}^{c_{k}}(R))=\langle\tilde{\pi}(x+\alpha x^{n}+O(x^{c_{k}}))|\alpha\in R,2k\leqslant n\leqslant c_{k}-1\rangle.

Thus,

\pi(\mathcal{J}_{2k}(R))=\langle\pi(x+\alpha x^{n})|\alpha\in R,2k\leqslant n\leqslant c_{k}-1\rangle.

Since
H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(R))
is abelian, it will suffice to prove that
|\pi(x+\alpha x^{n})|<\infty
for all
\alpha\in R
and for all
n\geqslant k
. For this purpose we will prove the following lemma:

Lemma 3.1.5
.

If
l\geqslant 2k+1
, then
\pi(x+\alpha x^{l})^{2}=\pi(x+2\alpha x^{l})
, for all
\alpha\in R
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha\in R
. We know that

(x+\alpha x^{l})^{\penalty\ 2}=x+2\alpha x^{l}+E,

with
E\in x^{2l-1}R[[x]]
. Note that

2l-1\geqslant 2(2k+1)-1=4k+1.

If
k\geqslant 4
, then
4k+1>3k+4\geqslant c_{k}
. On the other hand, if
k\in\{2,3\}
, then it is clear that
4k+1>c_{k}
. Thus

(x+\alpha x^{l}+O(x^{c_{k}}))^{2}=x+2\alpha x^{l}+O(x^{c_{k}}).

This implies that

\tilde{\pi}(x+\alpha x^{l}+O(x^{c_{k}}))^{2}=\tilde{\pi}(x+2\alpha x^{l}+O(x^{c_{k}})).

By Remark
2.2.12
, this is equivalent to
\pi(x+\alpha x^{l})^{2}=\pi(x+2\alpha x^{l})
.
∎

Proposition 3.1.6
.

If
l\geqslant k+2
, then
\pi(x+\alpha x^{2l})=0
and
|\pi(x+\alpha x^{2l+1})|\leqslant 2
, for all
\alpha\in R
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha\in R
. Recall that, for all
l\geqslant k
,

[x+\alpha x^{l+1},x+x^{l}]=x+\alpha x^{2l}+C(l+1,l)\alpha x^{3l-1}+\cdots.

Thus
(x+\alpha x^{2l})\circ[x+\alpha x^{l+1},x+x^{l}]^{-1}\in\mathcal{J}_{c_{k}}(\mathbb{Z})
if and only if
3l-1\geqslant c_{k}
. Note that
l\geqslant k+2
implies that
3l-1\geqslant 3k+5
. Since
3k+5>c_{k}
, this implies that
\pi(f_{2l})=0
.

On the other hand, for all
l\geqslant k
,

(x+2\alpha x^{2l+1})\circ[x+\alpha x^{l+2},x+x^{l}]^{-1}\in\mathcal{J}_{3l}(\mathbb{Z}).

Note that
l\geqslant k+2
implies that
3l>c_{k}
. Thus,
(x+2\alpha x^{2l+1})\circ[x+\alpha x^{l+2},x+x^{l}]^{-1}\in\mathcal{J}_{c_{k}}(\mathbb{Z})
. Therefore,

\pi(x+\alpha x^{2l+1})^{2}=\pi(x+2\alpha x^{l})=0.

We conclude that
|\pi(x+\alpha x^{2l+1})|\leqslant 2
.
∎

This determines the order of most generators and reduces their number. It also suggests some of the structure of
\pi(\mathcal{J}_{2k}(R))
.

At this point, we can state the following:

Proposition 3.1.7
.

The group
\pi(\mathcal{J}_{2k}(R))
is generated by

\{\pi(x+\alpha x^{2i+1})|k\leqslant i\leqslant\frac{c_{k}}{2}-1,\alpha\in R\}
.

Proof.

By the latter proposition, we know that
\pi(\mathcal{J}_{2k}(R))
is generated by

\{\pi(x+\alpha x^{2k})|\alpha\in R\}\cup\{\pi(x+\alpha x^{2k+2})|\alpha\in R\}\cup\{\pi(x+\alpha x^{2i+1})|k\leqslant i\leqslant\tfrac{c_{k}}{2}-1,\alpha\in R\}.

We will prove that
\pi(x+\alpha_{0}x^{2k})
and
\pi(x+\alpha_{0}x^{2k+2})
are contained in
\langle\{\pi(x+\alpha x^{2i+1})|k\leqslant i\leqslant\frac{c_{k}}{2}-1,\alpha\in R\}\rangle
for all
\alpha_{0}\in R
.

Let
\alpha_{0}\in R
. Consider

[x+\alpha_{0}x^{k+1},x+x^{k}]=x+\alpha_{0}x^{2k}+C(k+1,k)\alpha_{0}x^{3k-1}+\cdots.

Note that
(x+\alpha_{0}x^{2k})\circ[x+\alpha_{0}x^{k+1},x+x^{k}]^{-1}\in\mathcal{J}_{3k-1}(R)
. This implies that

\pi(x+\alpha_{0}x^{2k})\in\langle\{\pi(x+\alpha x^{i})|3k-1\leqslant i\leqslant c_{k}-1,\alpha\in R\}\rangle.

Since
k\geqslant 2
, we have that
3k-1\geqslant 2k+1
. Thus,

\pi(x+\alpha_{0}x^{2k})\in\langle\{\pi(x+\alpha x^{i})|2k+1\leqslant i\leqslant c_{k}-1,\alpha\in R\}\rangle.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\langle\{\pi(x+\alpha x^{2k})|\alpha\in R\}\cup\{\pi(x+\alpha x^{2k+2})|\alpha\in R\}\cup\{\pi(x+\alpha x^{2i+1})|k\leqslant i\leqslant\tfrac{c_{k}}{2}-1,\alpha\in R\}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\{\pi(x+\alpha x^{2k+2})|\alpha\in R\}\cup\{\pi(x+\alpha x^{2i+1})|k\leqslant i\leqslant\tfrac{c_{k}}{2}-1,\alpha\in R\}\rangle.

Analogously, note that

[x+\alpha_{0}x^{k+2},x+x^{k+1}]=x+\alpha_{0}x^{2k+2}+C(k+2,k+1)\alpha_{0}x^{3k+2}+\cdots

and that
k\geqslant 2
implies
3k+2>2k+3
. Having this, it suffices to apply the same argument as in the latter case.
∎

In order to finish this proof, we have to show that

\pi(x+\alpha x^{2k+1})
and
\pi(x+\alpha x^{2k+3})
have finite order for all
\alpha\in R
. We will distinguish
between the different cases given by the class of
k
modulo
4
. This will be done this way, since it will be used in later sections.

Proposition 3.1.8
.

If
k\not\equiv 0\pmod{4}
, then
|\pi(x+\alpha x^{2k+3})|\leqslant 2
for all
\alpha\in R
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha\in R
. Note that

[x+\alpha x^{k+3},x+x^{k+1}]=x+2\alpha x^{2k+3}+C(k+3,k+1)\alpha x^{3k+3}+\cdots.

This implies that

(x+2\alpha x^{2k+3})\circ[x+\alpha x^{k+3},x+x^{k+1}]^{-1}\in\mathcal{J}_{3k+3}(R).

Since
k\not\equiv 0\pmod{4}
, we know that
3k+3\geqslant c_{k}
. This implies that
\mathcal{J}_{3k+3}(R)\subseteq\mathcal{J}_{c_{k}}(R)
. Therefore,

(x+2x^{2k+3})\circ[x+\alpha x^{k+3},x+x^{k+1}]^{-1}\in\mathcal{J}_{c_{k}}(R).

We conclude that
\pi(x+\alpha x^{2k+3})^{2}=\pi(x+2\alpha x^{2k+3})=0
, thus
|\pi(x+\alpha x^{2k+3})|\leqslant 2
.
∎

Proposition 3.1.9
.

If
k\equiv 0\pmod{4}
, then
|\pi(x+\alpha x^{2k+3})|
divides
4
for all
\alpha\in R
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha_{0}\in R
. Note that

[x+\alpha_{0}x^{k+3},x+x^{k+1}]=x+2\alpha_{0}x^{2k+3}+C(k+3,k+1)\alpha_{0}x^{3k+3}+\cdots.

This implies that

(x+2\alpha_{0}x^{2k+3})\circ[x+\alpha_{0}x^{k+3},x+x^{k+1}]^{-1}\in\mathcal{J}_{3k+3}(R).

Since
k\equiv 0\pmod{4}
, we know that
\pi(\mathcal{J}_{3k+3}(R))=\langle\{\pi(x+\alpha x^{3k+3})|\alpha\in R\}\rangle
. Thus,

\pi(x+\alpha_{0}x^{2k+3})^{2}=\pi(x+2\alpha_{0}x^{2k+3})\in\langle\{\pi(x+\alpha x^{3k+3})|\alpha\in R\}\rangle.

This implies that there exist
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset R
and
\{\epsilon_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{Z}
such that

\pi(x+\alpha_{0}x^{2k+3})^{2}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\pi(x+\alpha_{i}x^{3k+3})^{\epsilon_{i}}.

Since
3k+3\geqslant 2k+4
, we have that

\pi(x+\alpha_{0}x^{2k+3})^{4}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\pi(x+\alpha_{i}x^{3k+3})^{2\epsilon_{i}}=0.

We conclude that
|\pi(x+\alpha_{0}x^{2k+3})|
divides
4
.
∎

Proposition 3.1.10
.

If
k\not\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, then
|\pi(x+\alpha x^{2k+1})|
divides
4
for all
\alpha\in R
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha_{0}\in R
. Note that

[x+\alpha_{0}x^{k+2},x+x^{k}]=x+2\alpha_{0}x^{2k+1}+C(k+2,k)\alpha_{0}x^{3k}+\cdots.

We have that

(x+2\alpha_{0}x^{2k+1})\circ[x+\alpha_{0}x^{k+2},x+x^{k}]^{-1}\in\mathcal{J}_{3k}(R).

This implies that

\pi(x+\alpha_{0}x^{2k+1})^{2}=\pi(x+2\alpha_{0}x^{2k+1})\in\langle\{\pi(x+\alpha x^{i})|3k\leqslant i\leqslant c_{k}-1,\alpha\in R\}\rangle.

This means that for all
i\in\{3k,\dots,c_{k}-1\}
, there exists
\{\alpha_{i,j}\}_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\subset R
and
\{\epsilon_{i,j}\}_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\subset\mathbb{Z}
such that

\pi(x+\alpha_{0}x^{2k+1})^{2}=\prod_{i=3k}^{c_{k}-1}\prod_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\pi(x+\alpha_{i,j}x^{i})^{\epsilon_{i,j}}.

We distinguish between several cases. If
k\equiv 0\pmod{4}
or
k\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, then
k\geqslant 4
. This implies that
3k\geqslant 2k+4
. Since
H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(\mathbb{Z}))
is abelian, we have that

\pi(x+\alpha_{0}x^{2k+1})^{4}=\left(\prod_{i=3k}^{c_{k}-1}\prod_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\pi(x+\alpha_{i,j}x^{i})^{\epsilon_{i,j}}\right)^{2}=\prod_{i=3k}^{c_{k}-1}\prod_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\pi(x+\alpha_{i,j}x^{i})^{2\epsilon_{i,j}}=0.

For this case, we conclude that
|\pi(x+\alpha_{0}x^{2k+1})|
divides
4
.

If
k\equiv 2\pmod{4}
, then
3k\equiv 0\pmod{2}
. This implies that

\langle\{\pi(x+\alpha x^{i})|3k\leqslant i\leqslant c_{k}-1,\alpha\in R\}\rangle=\langle\{\pi(x+\alpha x^{i})|3k+1\leqslant i\leqslant c_{k}-1,\alpha\in R\}\rangle.

Since
k\geqslant 2
implies that
3k+1\geqslant 2k+3
, we can use an argument analogous to that of the previous case to conclude that
|\pi(x+\alpha_{0}x^{2k+1})|
divides
4
.
∎

Proposition 3.1.11
.

If
k\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, then
|\pi(x+\alpha x^{2k+1})|\leqslant 2
for all
\alpha\in R
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha\in R
. The hypothesis implies that
c_{k}=3k+1
and
C(k+2,k)\equiv 0\pmod{2}
. Note that

[x+\alpha x^{k+2},x+x^{k}]=x+2\alpha x^{2k+1}+C(k+2,k)\alpha x^{3k}+\cdots,

and

[x+\alpha x^{\frac{3k-1}{2}+2},x+x^{\frac{3k-1}{2}}]=x+2\alpha x^{3k}+E,

with
E\in x^{3\frac{3k-1}{2}}R[[x]]
. Consider

g=[x+\alpha x^{k+2},x+x^{k}]^{-1}\circ[x+\alpha x^{\frac{3k-1}{2}+2},x+x^{\frac{3k-1}{2}}]^{\frac{C(k+2,k)}{2}+1}=x-2\alpha x^{2k+1}+2\alpha x^{3k}+\cdots.

It is clear that
g\in[\mathcal{J}_{k}(R),\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)]
.

On the other hand, note that

(x+\alpha x^{2k+1})^{2}\circ g=x+2\alpha x^{3k}+\cdots.

This implies that
\pi(x+\alpha x^{2k+1})^{2}=\pi(x+\alpha x^{3k})^{2}
.

Since
k\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, we have that
k\geqslant 3
, thus
3k\geqslant 2k+3
. This implies that
\pi(x+\alpha x^{3k})^{2}=0
. Therefore
\pi(x+\alpha x^{2k+1})^{2}=0
. We conclude that
|\pi(x+\alpha x^{2k+1})|\leqslant 2
.
∎

We can finally prove the following:

Proposition 3.1.12
.

If
R
is a torsion-free domain, then
\pi(\mathcal{J}_{2k}(R))\subseteq T(H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)))
.

Proof.

Note that
\pi(\mathcal{J}_{2k}(R))
is generated by elements of finite order. Since
H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(R))
is abelian, this implies that every element of
\pi(\mathcal{J}_{2k}(R))
has finite order. Thus
\pi(\mathcal{J}_{2k}(R))\subseteq T(H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)))
.
∎

With this we conclude the proof of Proposition
3.1.2
.

3.2
Approximation of the torsion of
H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(R))

Throughout this section we will assume that
R
is a torsion-free domain. Given the results of the previous section, we will make no distinction between
T(H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)))
and
\pi(\mathcal{J}_{2k}(R))
.

We know that
\pi(\mathcal{J}_{2k}(R))
is an abelian group, and we have a set of generators for it. We would like to find relations between these generators. For this purpose, we will reduce
\mathcal{J}_{k}^{c_{k}}(R)
in order to give the structure of a finitely generated
R
-module to one of its subgroups, and show that, for some rings, the group
\pi(\mathcal{J}_{2k}(R))
is a quotient of this
R
-module.

We have previously made allusion to the fact that, in order to compute the class of an element
x+\sum_{i=2k}^{c_{k}-1}\alpha_{i}x^{i}+O(x^{c_{k}})
in
H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(R))
, we can disregard the coefficient
\alpha_{2i}
and reduce modulo
2
the coefficient
\alpha_{2i+1}
, for all
i\geqslant k
. We will formalize this notion. In order to do this, we define the following number:

d_{k}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}2k+1&\text{if }k\equiv 3\pmod{4}\\
2k+2&\text{if }k\equiv 2\pmod{4}\text{ or }k\equiv 1\pmod{4}\\
2k+4&\text{if }k\equiv 0\pmod{4}\end{array}\right.

Note that if
k\not\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, then
d_{k}=c_{k}-k
. Define the following set:

H_{k}:=\left\{x+\sum_{i=d_{k}}^{c_{k}-1}\alpha_{i}x^{i}+O(x^{c_{k}})\in\mathcal{J}_{k}^{c_{k}}(R)\bigg|\alpha_{i}\in 2R,\forall i\in(2\mathbb{Z}+1)\cap\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant d_{k}}\right\}.

We will prove that this is a normal subgroup of
\mathcal{J}_{k}^{c_{k}}(R)
and
\pi
factors through the corresponding quotient.

Proposition 3.2.1
.

The set
H_{k}
is a subgroup of
\mathcal{J}_{k}^{c_{k}}(R)
.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{f},\tilde{g}\in H_{k}
. By definition, we know that

\tilde{f}=x+\sum_{i=d_{k}}^{c_{k}-1}\alpha_{i}x^{i}+O(x^{c_{k}})

and

\tilde{g}=x+\sum_{i=d_{k}}^{c_{k}-1}\beta_{i}x^{i}+O(x^{c_{k}}),

with
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}\in 2R
for all odd
i\geqslant d_{k}
. Let
f
and
g
be elements of
\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)
such that
f+O(x^{c_{k}})=\tilde{f}
and
g+O(x^{c_{k}})=\tilde{g}
. Note that

f\circ g=f+g+E,

with
E\in x^{2d_{k}-1}R[[x]]
. Since
d_{k}\geqslant 2k+1
, we have that

2d_{k}-1\geqslant 2(2k+1)-1=4k+1>c_{k}.

Thus

\tilde{f}\circ\tilde{g}=x+\sum_{i=2k}^{c_{k}-1}(\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i})x^{i}+O(x^{c_{k}}).

Since
2R
is an ideal of
R
, this implies that
\tilde{f}\circ\tilde{g}\in H_{k}
. We conclude that
H_{k}H_{k}\subset H_{k}
.

On the other hand, let
\tilde{f}\in H_{k}
. Let
f\in\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)
be such that
f+O(x^{c_{k}})=\tilde{f}
, with
f=x+\sum_{i\geqslant d_{k}}\alpha_{i}x^{i}
. It is clear that
f^{-1}+O(x^{c_{k}})=\tilde{f}^{-1}
. We know that
lvl(f^{-1})=lvl(f)\geqslant d_{k}
. Thus
f^{-1}=x+\sum_{i\geqslant d_{k}}\beta_{i}x^{i}
. By the previous computation, we have that

x+O(x^{c_{k}})=\tilde{f}\circ\tilde{f}^{-1}=x+\sum_{i=2k}^{c_{k}-1}(\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i})x^{i}+O(x^{c_{k}}).

Thus
\beta_{i}=-\alpha_{i}
for all
i\in\{d_{k},\dots,c_{k}-1\}
. Since
\alpha_{i}\equiv-\alpha_{i}\pmod{2}
for all
i\geqslant d_{k}
, the latter equation implies that
\beta_{i}\in 2R
for every odd integer
i
in
\{d_{k},\dots,c_{k}-1\}
. The fact that
\tilde{f}\in H_{k}
follows. We conclude that
(H_{k})^{-1}\in H_{k}
.

Finally, since
0\in 2R
, we have that
x+O(x^{c_{k}})\in H_{k}
.
∎

Proposition 3.2.2
.

The subgroup
H_{k}
is normal in
\mathcal{J}_{k}^{c_{k}}(R)
.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{f}\in\mathcal{J}_{k}^{c_{k}}(R)
and
\tilde{g}\in H_{k}
. Let
f
and
g
be elements in
\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)
such that
f+O(x^{c_{k}})=\tilde{f}
and
g+O(x^{c_{k}})=\tilde{g}
. Note that

f\circ g\circ f^{-1}=[f,g]\circ g.

By definition, we have that
lvl(g)\geqslant d_{k}
. Furthermore, we know that

lvl([f,g])\geqslant lvl(f)+lvl(g)-1.

On the one hand, if
lvl(f)\geqslant k+1
or
lvl(g)\geqslant d_{k}+1
, then

lvl([f,g])\geqslant k+d_{k}=c_{k}.

Thus
[\tilde{f},\tilde{g}]=x+O(x^{c_{k}})
. This implies that
\tilde{f}\circ\tilde{g}\circ\tilde{f}^{-1}=\tilde{g}\in H_{k}
.

On the other hand, if
k\not\equiv 1\pmod{4},lvl(f)=k
and
lvl(g)=d_{k}
, then
[\tilde{f},\tilde{g}]=x+(k-d_{k})x^{c_{k}-1}+O(x^{c_{k}})
. This implies that

\tilde{f}\circ\tilde{g}\circ\tilde{f}^{-1}=(x+(k-d_{k})x^{c_{k}-1}+O(x^{c_{k}}))\circ g=g-(d_{k}-k)x^{c_{k}-1}+O(x^{c_{k}}).

Note that
d_{k}-k=c_{k}-2k
. Since
c_{k}\equiv 0\pmod{2}
, we have that
d_{k}-k\equiv 0\pmod{2}
. This implies that
\tilde{f}\circ\tilde{g}\circ\tilde{f}^{-1}\in H_{k}
.

Finally, if
k\equiv 1\pmod{4},lvl(f)=k
and
lvl(g)=d_{k}
, then we can apply the same argument as in the first case.

We conclude that
H_{k}
is normal.
∎

Define
\Gamma_{k}:=\mathcal{J}_{k}^{c_{k}}(R)/H_{k}
.

Proposition 3.2.3
.

The subgroup
H_{k}
is contained
ker(\tilde{\pi})
.

Proof.

Let

E:=\{x+\alpha x^{i}+O(x^{c_{k}})|\alpha\in R,d_{k}\leqslant i\leqslant c_{k}-2,\text{ and $i$ is even}\},

and

O:=\{(x+\alpha x^{i}+O(x^{c_{k}}))^{2}|\alpha\in R,d_{k}\leqslant i\leqslant c_{k}-1,\text{ and $i$ is odd}\}.

Note that

H_{k}=\langle E\cup O\rangle.

We have that

\displaystyle E=\{x+\alpha x^{i}+O(x^{c_{k}})|\alpha\in R,d_{k}\leqslant i\leqslant 2k+2,\text{ and $i$ is even}\}\cup\{x+\alpha x^{2i}+O(x^{c_{k}})|\alpha\in R,k+2\leqslant i\leqslant\tfrac{c_{k}}{2}-1\},

and

\displaystyle O

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(x+\alpha x^{i}+O(x^{c_{k}}))^{2}|\alpha\in R,d_{k}\leqslant i\leqslant 2k+3,\text{ and $i$ is odd}\}\cup\{(x+\alpha x^{2i+1}+O(x^{c_{k}}))^{2}|\alpha\in R,k+2\leqslant i\leqslant\tfrac{c_{k}}{2}-1\}.

Since
\tilde{\pi}\circ p=\pi
, we have that
\pi(x+\alpha x^{i})=\tilde{\pi}(x+\alpha x^{i}+O(x^{c_{k}}))
for all
\alpha\in R
and for all
i\geqslant k
. By Proposition
3.1.6
, this implies that

\{x+\alpha x^{2i}+O(x^{c_{k}})|\alpha\in R,k+2\leqslant i\leqslant\tfrac{c_{k}}{2}-1\}\subset ker(\tilde{\pi}).

and

\{(x+\alpha x^{2i+1}+O(x^{c_{k}}))^{2}|\alpha\in R,k+2\leqslant i\leqslant\tfrac{c_{k}}{2}-1\}\subset ker(\tilde{\pi}).

Thus, it suffices to prove that
\tilde{\pi}(x+\alpha x^{i}+O(x^{c_{k}}))=0
for all
\alpha\in R
and for every even integer
i
in
\{d_{k},\dots,2k+2\}
,
and that
\tilde{\pi}(x+\alpha x^{i}+O(x^{c_{k}}))^{2}=0
for all
\alpha\in R

and for every odd integer
i
in
\{d_{k},\dots,2k+3\}
. For this purpose we will take cases depending on the class modulo
4
of
k
.

i.

If
k\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, then
d_{k}=2k+1
. We know that

\tilde{\pi}(x+\alpha x^{2k+1}+O(x^{c_{k}}))^{2}=0=\tilde{\pi}(x+\alpha x^{2k+3}+O(x^{c_{k}}))^{2},

for all
\alpha\in R
. Thus, it will suffice to prove that
\tilde{\pi}(x+\alpha x^{2k+2})=0
for all
\alpha\in R
.

Let
\alpha\in R
. We have that

[x+\alpha x^{k+2},x+x^{k+1}]=x+\alpha x^{2k+2}+C(k+2,k+1)\alpha x^{3k+3}+\cdots.

Note that
(x+\alpha x^{2k+2})\circ[x+\alpha x^{k+2},x+x^{k+1}]^{-1}\in\mathcal{J}_{3k+3}(R)\subset\mathcal{J}_{c_{k}}(R)
. This implies that

\tilde{\pi}(x+\alpha x^{2k+2}+O(x^{c_{k}}))=\pi(x+\alpha x^{2k+2})=0.

ii.

If
k\equiv 2\pmod{4}
or
k\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, then
d_{k}=2k+2
. We know that
\tilde{\pi}(x+\alpha x^{2k+3}+O(x^{c_{k}}))^{2}=0
for all
\alpha\in R
. The proof that
\tilde{\pi}(x+\alpha x^{2k+2})=0
for all
\alpha\in R
is analogous to the previous case.

iii.

If
k\equiv 0\pmod{4}
, then
d_{k}=2k+4
and we are already done.

Since
E\cup O\subset ker(\tilde{\pi})
, we conclude that
H_{k}\subset ker(\tilde{\pi})
.

∎

We can now apply the factor theorem to obtain a unique morphism
\rho:\Gamma_{k}\to H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(R))
such that the following diagram commutes:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{J}_{k}^{c_{k}}(R)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(R))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Gamma_{k}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tilde{\pi}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho}

Moreover, we can compose this diagram with the diagram from Remark
2.2.12
to obtain the following commutative diagram:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(R))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Gamma_{k}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p^{\prime}\circ p}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho}

From any of the previous diagrams, it is clear that
\pi(\mathcal{J}_{2k}(R))=¸\rho\circ p^{\prime}(\mathcal{J}_{2k}^{c_{k}}(R))
. We endow
p^{\prime}(\mathcal{J}_{2k}^{c_{k}}(R))
with the structure of an
R
-module.

Proposition 3.2.4
.

Let
f
and
g
be elements in
\Gamma_{k}
, with
f=p^{\prime}(x+\sum_{i=2k}^{c_{k}-1}\alpha_{i}x^{i}+O(x^{c_{k}}))
and
g=p^{\prime}(x+\sum_{i=2k}^{c_{k}-1}\beta_{i}x^{i}+O(x^{c_{k}}))
. Then

f\circ g=p^{\prime}\left(x+\sum_{i=2k}^{c_{k}-1}(\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i})x^{i}+O(x^{c_{k}}))\right).

Proof.

From Proposition
2.1.1
we have that

\displaystyle f\circ g=

\displaystyle p^{\prime}\left(x+\sum_{i=2k}^{c_{k}-1}\alpha_{i}x^{i}+O(x^{c_{k}})\right)\circ p^{\prime}\left(x+\sum_{i=2k}^{c_{k}-1}\beta_{i}x^{i}+O(x^{c_{k}})\right)

\displaystyle p^{\prime}\left(x+\sum_{i=2k}^{c_{k}-1}(\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i})x^{i}+2k\alpha_{2k}\beta_{2k}x^{4k-1}+O(x^{c_{k}})\right).

∎

From the previous proposition, it is clear that

\displaystyle R\times p^{\prime}(\mathcal{J}_{2k}^{c_{k}}(R))

\displaystyle\to p^{\prime}(\mathcal{J}_{2k}^{c_{k}}(R))

\displaystyle\left(r,p^{\prime}\left(x+\sum_{i=2k}^{c_{k}-1}\alpha_{i}x^{i}+O(x^{c_{k}})\right)\right)

-module on

. In order to translate this

-module structure onto

, we need to prove that
[\Gamma_{k},\Gamma_{k}]
is an
R
-submodule of
p^{\prime}(\mathcal{J}_{2k}^{c_{k}}(R))
. This is not necessarily the case for any ring, but it will hold for the case of

. If such a condition is met, we can use the following proposition:

Proposition 3.2.5
.

Let
R
be a torsion-free PID. If
[\Gamma_{k},\Gamma_{k}]
is an
R
-submodule of

where
n_{k}\leqslant\frac{c_{k}-2k}{2}
and
p_{i}|4
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n_{k}\}

Proof.

By hypothesis, we have that

Since

T(H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)))\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n_{k}}R/(p_{i}),

∎

3.4
The free submodule of

In order to complete our computation of

Let
R
be a domain. We know that
[\mathcal{J}_{k}(R),\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)]\subset\mathcal{J}_{2k}(R)
. By the factor theorem, this implies that the following diagram commutes:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{J}_{k}^{2k}(R)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(R))}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi}

Since
p
is surjective, the morphism
\varphi
is surjective. From the diagram we can deduce that
ker(\varphi)=\pi(\mathcal{J}_{2k}(R))
. From here, we have two ways forward.

Proposition 3.4.1
.

If
R
is a domain such that
2\in R^{*}
, then

H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(R))\cong\mathcal{J}_{k}^{2k}(R).

Proof.

By corollary
3.1.1
, we have that
[\mathcal{J}_{k}(R),\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)]=\mathcal{J}_{2k}(R)
. This implies that
\pi(\mathcal{J}_{2k}(R))=\{0\}
. We conclude that
\varphi
is an isomorphism.
∎

Proposition 3.4.2
.

If
R
is a torsion-free domain, then

0\to T(H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(R)))\to H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(R))\overset{\varphi}{\to}\mathcal{J}_{k}^{2k}(R)\to 0

is a group extension.

For our case of interest, it suffices to note that

\{\pi(x+x^{i})|k\leqslant i\leqslant 2k-1\}\cup\{\pi(x+x^{2i+1})|k\leqslant i\leqslant\frac{c_{k}}{2}-1\}

is a finite set of generators for

. This implies that
H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(\mathbb{Z}))
is a finitely generated abelian group, which in turn implies that

H_{1}(\mathcal{J}_{k}(\mathbb{Z}))\cong\mathcal{J}_{k}^{2k}(\mathbb{Z})\bigoplus\pi(\mathcal{J}_{k}(\mathbb{Z})).

For this reason, we study the structure of
\mathcal{J}_{k}^{2k}(R)
.

Proposition 3.4.3
.

The group
\mathcal{J}_{k}^{2k}(R)
is isomorphic to a subgroup of
R^{k}
.

Proof.

Define

\displaystyle\psi:\mathcal{J}_{k}^{2k}(R)

\displaystyle\to R^{k}

\displaystyle x+\sum_{i=k}^{2k-1}\alpha_{i}x^{i}+O(x^{2k})

\displaystyle\mapsto(\alpha_{i})_{i=k}^{2k-2}\oplus(k\alpha_{k}^{\penalty\ 2}-2\alpha_{2k-1}).

In order to prove that this is a group morphism, we will describe the product in
\mathcal{J}_{k}^{2k}(R)
. Let
f
and
g
be elements in
\mathcal{J}_{k}^{2k}(R)
, with
f=x+\sum_{i=k}^{2k-1}\alpha_{i}x^{i}+O(x^{2k-1})
and
g=x+\sum_{i=k}^{2k-1}\beta_{i}x^{i}+O(x^{2k})
. By Proposition
2.1.1
we have that

f\circ g=x+\sum_{i=k}^{2k-2}(\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i})x^{i}+(\alpha_{2k-1}+\beta_{2k-1}+k\alpha_{k}\beta_{k})x^{2k-1}+O(x^{2k}).

Since all but the last coordinate of

is a projection, it suffices to note that

k(\alpha_{k}+\beta_{k})^{2}-2(\alpha_{2k-1}+\beta_{2k-1}+k\alpha_{k}\beta_{k})=k\alpha_{k}^{\penalty\ 2}-2\alpha_{2k-1}+k\beta_{k}^{\penalty\ 2}-2\beta_{2k-1}.

We conclude that

is a group morphism.

In order to prove that
\psi
is injective, note that
\psi(x+\sum_{i=k}^{2k-1}\alpha_{i}x^{i})=0
if and only if
2\alpha_{2k-1}=k\alpha_{k}^{\penalty\ 2}
and
\alpha_{i}=0
for all

\alpha_{k}=0
, thus
\alpha_{2k-1}=0

\mathcal{J}_{k}^{2k}(R)\cong Im(\psi)<R^{n}

∎

We take this paragraph to note that the last coordinate in the function

We conclude with the following corollary:

Corollary 3.4.4

The isomorphism

Proof.

We will prove that

∎

Appendix: The product on a quotient

Let
R
be a torsion-free domain. In this section we will give a description for the product in
\mathcal{J}_{k}^{c_{k}}(R)
, for
k\geqslant 5
. Later, we give a description for the product, inverse and commutator in
\Gamma_{k}
. These descriptions can be found at the end
of this section.

Let
f
and
g
be elements in
\mathcal{J}_{k}^{c_{k}}(R)
, with
f=x+\sum_{i=k}^{c_{k}-1}\alpha_{i}x^{i}+O(x^{c_{k}})
and
g=x+\sum_{i=k}^{c_{k}-1}\beta_{i}x^{i}+O(x^{c_{k}})
. We know that
f\circ g=x+\sum_{i=k}^{c_{k}-1}\beta_{i}x^{i}+\sum_{i=k}^{c_{k}-1}\alpha_{i}(x+\sum_{j=k}^{c_{k}-1}\beta_{j}x^{j})^{i}+O(x^{c_{k}})
. We would like to write the coefficients of
f\circ g
in function of the coefficients of
f
and
g
. For this purpose, we will make use of the multinomial theorem. In order to state this theorem comfortably, we introduce some definitions.

Definition 4.0.1
.

Let
v\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0})^{m}
and
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
. We write
v_{i}
for the
i^{th}
coordinate of
v
.

Definition 4.0.2
.

For
v\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0})^{m}
we define
|v|:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}
.

Definition 4.0.3
.

Given
v\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0})^{m}
such that
|v|=n
, we define the number

\binom{n}{v}:=\frac{n!}{\prod_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}!}.

Theorem 4.0.4

(Multinomial Theorem.)
.

For a finite collection
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
in
R
one has that

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\right)^{n}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0})^{m}\\
|v|=n\end{subarray}}\binom{n}{v}\prod_{i=1}^{m}(\alpha_{i})^{v_{i}}.

In order to fit this theorem to our case we will introduce some notation. Note that an element of
\mathcal{J}_{k}^{c_{k}}(R)
has
c_{k}-k+1
terms. We define
m_{k}:=c_{k}-k+1
. We fix
w^{k}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0})^{m_{k}}
as the tuple of coordinates
(w^{k})_{1}=1
and
(w^{k})_{i}=k+(i-2)
for
i\in\{2,\dots,m_{k}\}
. For
v
and
w
in
(\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0})^{m}
, we define
v\cdot w:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}w_{i}
. Since any element of
\mathcal{J}_{k}^{c_{k}}(R)
is in particular an element of
R[[x]]/x^{c_{k}}R[[x]]
, any term of
f\circ g
that is divisible by
x^{c}_{k}
vanishes. Thus, we define

S_{n,k}:=\{v\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0})^{m_{k}}|v\cdot w^{k}<c_{k}\text{ and }|v|=n\}.

We can write
f\circ g
as follows:

Corollary 4.0.5
.

Let
f
and
g
be elements in
\mathcal{J}_{k}^{c_{k}}(R)
, with
f=x+\sum_{i=k}^{c_{k}-1}\alpha_{i}x^{i}+O(x^{c_{k}})
and
g=x+\sum_{i=k}^{c_{k}-1}\beta_{i}x^{i}+O(x^{c_{k}})
. Then

f\circ g=x+\sum_{i=k}^{c_{k}-1}\beta_{i}x^{i}+\sum_{l=k}^{c_{k}-1}\alpha_{l}\sum_{v\in S_{l,k}}\binom{l}{v}\left(\prod_{i=k}^{c_{k}-1}(\beta_{i})^{v_{i-k+2}}\right)x^{v\cdot w^{k}}+O(x^{c_{k}}).

Proof.

Note that

f\circ g=x+\sum_{i=k}^{c_{k}-1}\beta_{i}x^{i}+\sum_{i=k}^{c_{k}-1}\alpha_{i}\left(x+\sum_{j=k}^{c_{k}-1}\beta_{j}x^{j}\right)^{i}+O(x^{c_{k}}).

Let

. By the multinomial theorem we have that

\displaystyle\left(x+\sum_{j=k}^{c_{k}-1}\beta_{j}x^{j}\right)^{l}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in(\mathbb{Z}_{0\geqslant})^{m_{k}}\\
|v|=l\end{subarray}}\binom{l}{v}x^{v_{1}}\prod_{j=k}^{c_{k}-1}(\beta_{j}x^{j})^{v_{j-k+2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in(\mathbb{Z}_{0\geqslant})^{m_{k}}\\
|v|=l\end{subarray}}\binom{l}{v}\prod_{j=k}^{c_{k}-1}(\beta_{j})^{v_{j-k+2}}x^{v_{1}}\prod_{i=2}^{m_{k}}(x^{k+(i-2)})^{v_{i}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in(\mathbb{Z}_{0\geqslant})^{m_{k}}\\
|v|=l\end{subarray}}\binom{l}{v}\prod_{j=k}^{c_{k}-1}(\beta_{j})^{v_{j-k+2}}\prod_{i=1}^{m_{k}}x^{(w^{k})_{i}v_{i}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in(\mathbb{Z}_{0\geqslant})^{m_{k}}\\
|v|=l\end{subarray}}\binom{l}{v}\prod_{j=k}^{c_{k}-1}(\beta_{j})^{v_{j-k+2}}x^{w^{k}\cdot v}

If
v\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0})^{m_{k}}
is such that
|v|=l
and
v\cdot w^{k}\geqslant c_{k}
, then

\binom{l}{v}\prod_{j=k}^{c_{k}-1}(\beta_{j})^{v_{j-k+2}}x^{w^{k}\cdot v}\equiv 0\pmod{x^{c_{k}}}.

On the other hand, if
v\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0})^{m_{k}}

∎

Lemma 4.0.6
.

Let

and

\displaystyle v\cdot w^{k}

\displaystyle\geqslant v_{i_{1}}(k+(i_{1}-2))+v_{i_{2}}(k+(i_{2}-2))+v_{i_{3}}(k+(i_{3}-2))

\displaystyle\geqslant v_{i_{1}}k+v_{i_{2}}(k+1)+v_{i_{3}}(k+2)

∎

We will restrict ourselves to the case of
k\geqslant 5
, since this will allow us to describe
S_{l,k}
in a general fashion. For the three cases not included, it suffices to compute the sets
S_{l,k}
by hand.

Lemma 4.0.7
.

Let
k
and
l
be integers such that
l\geqslant k\geqslant 5
. Given
v\in S_{l,k}
, we have the following possibilities:

i.

If
\{i\in\{2,\dots,m_{k}\}|v_{i}\neq 0\}=\{i_{1},i_{2}\}
, then
v_{i_{1}}=1=v_{i_{2}}
.

ii.

If
\{i\in\{2,\dots,m_{k}\}|v_{i}\neq 0\}=\{i\}
, then
v_{i}\leqslant 2
.

Proof.

i.

Without loss of generality, assume that
i_{1}<i_{2}
. Since
2\leqslant i_{1}
, we have that
k+(i_{1}-2)\geqslant k
and
k+(i_{2}-2)\geqslant k+1
. Assume that
v_{i_{1}}\geqslant 2
. This implies that

\displaystyle v\cdot w^{k}

\displaystyle=v_{1}+v_{i_{1}}(k+(i_{1}-2))+v_{i_{2}}(k+(i_{2}-2))

\displaystyle=l-v_{i_{1}}-v_{i_{2}}+v_{i_{1}}(k+(i_{1}-2))+2(k+(i_{2}-2))

\displaystyle=l+v_{i_{1}}(k+(i_{1}-2)-1)+v_{i_{2}}(k+(i_{2}-2)-1)

\displaystyle\geqslant k+2(k-1)+k

\displaystyle=4k-2

\displaystyle\geqslant c_{k}.

This is a contradiction, thus
v_{i_{1}}=1
. This implies that
v_{1}=l-1-v_{i_{2}}
. If we assume that
v_{i_{2}}\geqslant 2
, then

\displaystyle v\cdot w^{k}

\displaystyle=v_{1}+v_{i_{1}}(k+(i_{1}-2))+v_{i_{2}}(k+(i_{2}-2))

\displaystyle=l-1-v_{i_{2}}+k+(i_{1}-2)+v_{i_{2}}(k+(i_{2}-2))

\displaystyle=l-1+k+(i_{1}-2)+v_{i_{2}}(k+(i_{2}-2)-1)

\displaystyle\geqslant k-1+k+2k

\displaystyle=4k-1

\displaystyle\geqslant c_{k}.

This is a contradiction. We conclude that
v_{i_{1}}=1=v_{i_{2}}
.

ii.

Assume that
v_{i}\geqslant 4
. This implies that

v\cdot w^{k}\geqslant 4k\geqslant c_{k}.

This is a contradiction. We conclude that
v_{i}\leqslant 3
.

Assume that
v_{i}=3
. Note that
|v|=l
implies that
v_{1}=l-3
. We have that

\displaystyle v\cdot w^{k}

\displaystyle=v_{1}+v_{i}(k+(i-2))

\displaystyle=l-3+3(k+(i-2))

\displaystyle\geqslant k-3+3k

\displaystyle=4k-3

\displaystyle\geqslant c_{k}.

This is a contradiction. We conclude that
v_{i}\leqslant 2
.

∎

Remark 4.0.8
.

Let
l\geqslant k
. If
v\in S_{l,k}
, then
v_{1}\neq 0
.

Definition 4.0.9
.

Let
l\geqslant k
. Let
v\in S_{l,k}
. We say that
v
is of:

i.

Type A if it has two nonzero coordinates and
v_{1}=l-1
.

ii.

Type B if it has two nonzero coordinates and
v_{1}=l-2
.

iii.

Type C if it has three nonzero coordinates, in which case
v_{1}=l-2
.

This lead us us to describe
S_{l,k}
as the union of
\{(l,0,\dots,0)\}
and the vectors of types A, B and C contained in it. With this, we can classify the monomials of
f\circ g
.

Lemma 4.0.10
.

Let
k\geqslant 5
. Let
f
and
g
be elements in
\mathcal{J}_{k}^{c_{k}}(R)
, with
f=x+\sum_{i=k}^{c_{k}-1}\alpha_{i}x^{i}+O(x^{c_{k}})
and
g=x+\sum_{i=k}^{c_{k}-1}\beta_{i}x^{i}+O(x^{c_{k}})
. Let
l\geqslant k
and
v\in S_{l,k}
. We have that:

i.

If
v
is of type A, with
i\geqslant k
such that
v_{i-k+2}=1
, then

\alpha_{l}\binom{l}{v}\left(\prod_{e=k}^{c_{k}-1}(\beta_{e})^{v_{e-k+2}}\right)x^{v\cdot w^{k}}=l\alpha_{l}\beta_{i}x^{l-1+i},

and
v\cdot w^{k}\geqslant 2k-1
.

ii.

If
v
is of type B, with
i\geqslant k
such that
v_{i-k+2}=2
, then

\alpha_{l}\binom{l}{v}\left(\prod_{e=k}^{c_{k}-1}(\beta_{e})^{v_{e-k+2}}\right)x^{v\cdot w^{k}}=\binom{l}{2}\alpha_{l}\beta_{i}^{\penalty\ 2}x^{l-2+2i},

and
v\cdot w^{k}\geqslant 3k-2
.

iii.

If
v
is of type C, with
j>i\geqslant k
such that
v_{i-k+2}=1=v_{j-k+2}
, then

\alpha_{l}\binom{l}{v}\left(\prod_{e=k}^{c_{k}-1}(\beta_{e})^{v_{e-k+2}}\right)x^{v\cdot w^{k}}=l(l-1)\alpha_{l}\beta_{i}\beta_{j}x^{l-2+i+j},

and
v\cdot w^{k}\geqslant 3k-1
.

iv.

If
v=(l,0,\dots,0)
, then

\alpha_{l}\binom{l}{v}\left(\prod_{e=k}^{c_{k}-1}(\beta_{e})^{v_{e-k+2}}\right)x^{v\cdot w^{k}}=\alpha_{l}x^{l}.

Proof.

i.

Note that
v=(l-1,0\dots,0,1,0\dots,0)
. This implies that

\binom{l}{v}=\frac{l!}{(l-1)!0!\cdots 1!\cdots 0!}=l

and

\prod_{e=k}^{c_{k}-1}(\beta_{e})^{v_{e-k+2}}=\beta_{i}.

Furthermore, we have that

v\cdot w^{k}=l-1+i\geqslant k-1+i\geqslant k-1+k=2k-1.

ii.

Note that
v=(l-2,0\dots,0,2,0\dots,0)
. This implies that

\binom{l}{v}=\frac{l!}{(l-2)!0!\cdots 2!\cdots 0!}=\binom{l}{2}

and

\prod_{e=k}^{c_{k}-1}(\beta_{e})^{v_{e-k+2}}=\beta_{i}^{\penalty\ 2}.

Furthermore, we have that

v\cdot w^{k}=l-2+2i\geqslant k-2+2i\geqslant k-2+2k=3k-2.

iii.

Note that
v=(l-2,0\dots,0,1,0\dots,0,1,0,\dots,0)
. This implies that

\binom{l}{v}=\frac{l!}{(l-2)!0!\cdots 1!\cdots 0!1!\dots 0!}=l(l-1)

and

\prod_{e=k}^{c_{k}-1}(\beta_{e})^{v_{e-k+2}}=\beta_{i}\beta_{j}.

Furthermore, since
j>i\geqslant k
, we have that
j\geqslant k+1
. This implies that

v\cdot w^{k}=l-2+i+j\geqslant k-2+i+j\geqslant k-2+k+k+1=3k-1.

iv.

We have that

\binom{l}{v}=\frac{l!}{l!0!\cdots 0!\cdots 0!}=1

and

\prod_{e=k}^{c_{k}-1}(\beta_{e})^{v_{e+k-2}}=1.

Furthermore, we have that
v\cdot w^{k}=l
. This proves the equality.

∎

From here, it suffices to order the monomials of
f\circ g
and factor the common powers of
x
in order to prove the following.

Theorem 4.0.11
.

Let
k\geqslant 5
. Let
f
and
g
be elements in
\mathcal{J}_{k}^{c_{k}}(R)
, with
f=x+\sum_{i=k}^{c_{k}-1}\alpha_{i}x^{i}+O(x^{c_{k}})
and
g=x+\sum_{i=k}^{c_{k}-1}\beta_{i}x^{i}+O(x^{c_{k}})
. Let
\gamma_{l}
be the
l
th coefficient of
f\circ g
.

i.

If
k\leqslant l\leqslant 2k-2
, then
\gamma_{l}=\alpha_{l}+\beta_{l}
.

ii.

If
2k-1\leqslant l\leqslant 3k-3
, then
\gamma_{l}=\alpha_{l}+\beta_{l}+\sum_{i=k}^{l-k+1}i\alpha_{i}\beta_{l-i+1}
.

iii.

\gamma_{3k-2}=\alpha_{3k-2}+\beta_{3k-2}+\sum_{i=k}^{2k-1}i\alpha_{i}\beta_{3k-1-i}
+
\binom{k}{2}\alpha_{k}\beta_{k}^{\penalty\ 2}
.

iv.

\gamma_{3k-1}=\alpha_{3k-1}+\beta_{3k-1}+\sum_{i=k}^{2k}i\alpha_{i}\beta_{3k-i}
+
\binom{k+1}{2}\alpha_{k+1}\beta_{k}^{\penalty\ 2}+k(k-1)\alpha_{k}\beta_{k}\beta_{k+1}
.

v.

\gamma_{3k}=\alpha_{3k}+\beta_{3k}+\sum_{i=k}^{2k+1}i\alpha_{i}\beta_{3k+1-i}+\binom{k}{2}\alpha_{k}\beta_{k+1}^{\penalty\ 2}+\binom{k+2}{2}\alpha_{k+2}\beta_{k}^{\penalty\ 2}+k(k-1)\alpha_{k}\beta_{k}\beta_{k+2}+(k+1)k\alpha_{k+1}\beta_{k}\beta_{k+1}.

vi.

If
k\equiv 0\pmod{2}
, then

\gamma_{3k+1}=\alpha_{3k+1}+\beta_{3k+1}+\sum_{i=k}^{2k+2}i\alpha_{i}\beta_{3k+2-i}+\binom{k+1}{2}\alpha_{k+1}(\beta_{k+1})^{2}+\binom{k+3}{2}\alpha_{k+3}\beta_{k}^{\penalty\ 2}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\leqslant l,i,j<c_{k}\\
i<j\\
l-2+i+j=3k+1\end{subarray}}l(l-1)\alpha_{l}\beta_{i}\beta_{j}.

vii.

If
k\equiv 0\pmod{4}
, then

\displaystyle\gamma_{3k+2}=

\displaystyle\alpha_{3k+2}+\beta_{3k+2}+\sum_{i=k}^{2k+3}i\alpha_{i}\beta_{3k+3-i}+\binom{k}{2}\alpha_{k}(\beta_{k+2})^{2}+\binom{k+2}{2}\alpha_{k+2}(\beta_{k+1})^{2}+\binom{k+4}{2}\alpha_{k+4}\beta_{k}^{\penalty\ 2}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\leqslant l,i,j<c_{k}\\
i<j\\
l-2+i+j=3k+2\end{subarray}}l(l-1)\alpha_{l}\beta_{i}\beta_{j}.

and

\displaystyle\gamma_{3k+3}=

\displaystyle\alpha_{3k+3}+\beta_{3k+3}+\sum_{i=k}^{2k+4}i\alpha_{i}\beta_{3k+4-i}+\binom{k+1}{2}\alpha_{k+1}(\beta_{k+2})^{2}+\binom{k+3}{2}\alpha_{k+3}(\beta_{k+1})^{2}+\binom{k+5}{2}\alpha_{k+5}\beta_{k}^{\penalty\ 2}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\leqslant l,i,j<c_{k}\\
i<j\\
l-2+i+j=3k+3\end{subarray}}l(l-1)\alpha_{l}\beta_{i}\beta_{j}.

Corollary 4.0.12
.

Let
k\geqslant 5
Let
f
and
g
be elements in
\Gamma_{k}
, with
f=p^{\prime}(x+\sum_{i=k}^{c_{k}-1}\alpha_{i}x^{i}+O(x^{c_{k}}))
and
g=p^{\prime}(x+\sum_{i=k}^{c_{k}-1}\beta_{i}x^{i}+O(x^{c_{k}}))
. Let
\gamma_{l}
be the
l
th coefficient of
f\circ g
.

i.

If
k\leqslant l\leqslant 2k-2
, then
\gamma_{l}=\alpha_{l}+\beta_{l}
.

ii.

If
2k-1\leqslant l\leqslant d_{k}-1
, then
\gamma_{l}=\alpha_{l}+\beta_{l}+\sum_{i=k}^{l-k+1}i\alpha_{i}\beta_{l-i+1}
.

iii.

If
d_{k}\leqslant l\leqslant 3k-3
, then

\gamma_{l}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\alpha_{l}+\beta_{l}+\sum_{i=k}^{l-k+1}i\alpha_{i}\beta_{l-i+1}\pmod{2}&,\text{ if }l\equiv 1\pmod{2}\\
0&,\text{ if }l\equiv 0\pmod{2}\end{array}\right.

.

iv.

If
k\equiv 0\pmod{2}
, then
\gamma_{3k-2}=0
. If
k\equiv 1\pmod{2}
, then

\gamma_{3k-2}\equiv\alpha_{3k-2}+\beta_{3k-2}+\sum_{i=k}^{2k-1}i\alpha_{i}\beta_{3k-1-i}+\binom{k}{2}\alpha_{k}\beta_{k}^{\penalty\ 2}\pmod{2}.

v.

If
k\equiv 0\pmod{2}
, then

\gamma_{3k-1}\equiv\alpha_{3k-1}+\beta_{3k-1}+\sum_{i=k}^{2k}i\alpha_{i}\beta_{3k-i}+\binom{k+1}{2}\alpha_{k+1}\beta_{k}^{\penalty\ 2}\pmod{2}.

If
k\equiv 1\pmod{2}
, then
\gamma_{3k-1}=0
.

vi.

If
k\equiv 0\pmod{2}
, then
\gamma_{3k}=0
. If
k\equiv 1\pmod{2}
, then

\gamma_{3k}\equiv\alpha_{3k}+\beta_{3k}+\sum_{i=k}^{2k+1}i\alpha_{i}\beta_{3k+1-i}+\binom{k}{2}\alpha_{k}(\beta_{k+1})^{2}+\binom{k+2}{2}\alpha_{k+2}\beta_{k}^{\penalty\ 2}\pmod{2}.

vii.

If
k\equiv 0\pmod{2}
, then

\gamma_{3k+1}\equiv\alpha_{3k+1}+\beta_{3k+1}+\sum_{i=k}^{2k+2}i\alpha_{i}\beta_{3k+2-i}+\binom{k+1}{2}\alpha_{k+1}(\beta_{k+1})^{2}+\binom{k+3}{2}\alpha_{k+3}\beta_{k}^{\penalty\ 2}\pmod{2}.

viii.

If
k\equiv 0\pmod{4}
, then
\gamma_{3k+2}=0
, and

\gamma_{3k+3}\equiv\alpha_{3k+3}+\beta_{3k+3}+\sum_{i=k}^{2k+4}i\alpha_{i}\beta_{3k+4-i}+\alpha_{k+3}(\beta_{k+1})^{2}\pmod{2}.

\Gamma_{k}

Corollary 4.0.13
.

Let
k\geqslant 5
. Let
f\in\Gamma_{k}
, with
f=p^{\prime}(x+\sum_{i=k}^{c_{k}-1}\alpha_{i}x^{i}+O(x^{c_{k}}))
. Let
\beta_{l}
be the
l
th coefficient of
f^{-1}
.

i.

If
k\leqslant l\leqslant 2k-2
, then
\beta_{l}=-\alpha_{l}
.

ii.

If
2k-1\leqslant l\leqslant d_{k}-1
, then
\beta_{l}=-\alpha_{l}+\sum_{i=k}^{l-k+1}i\alpha_{i}\alpha_{l-i+1}
.

iii.

If
d_{k}-1\leqslant l\leqslant 3k-3
, then

\beta_{l}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}-\alpha_{l}+\sum_{i=k}^{l-k+1}i\alpha_{i}\alpha_{l-i+1}\pmod{2}&,\text{ if }l\equiv 1\pmod{2}\\
0&,\text{ if }l\equiv 0\pmod{2}\end{array}\right..

iv.

If
k\equiv 0\pmod{2}
, then
\beta_{3k-2}=0
. If
k\equiv 1\pmod{2}
, then

\beta_{3k-2}\equiv\alpha_{3k-2}+\sum_{i=k}^{2k-1}i\alpha_{i}\alpha_{3k-1-i}+\left(\binom{k}{2}+1\right)\alpha_{k}^{\penalty\ 3}\pmod{2}.

v.

If
k\equiv 0\pmod{2}
, then

\beta_{3k-1}\equiv\alpha_{3k-1}+\sum_{i=k}^{2k}i\alpha_{i}\alpha_{3k-i}+\binom{k+1}{2}\alpha_{k+1}\alpha_{k}^{\penalty\ 2}\pmod{2}.

If
k\equiv 1\pmod{2}
, then
\beta_{3k-1}=0
.

vi.

If
k\equiv 0\pmod{2}
, then
\beta_{3k}=0
. If
k\equiv 1\pmod{2}
, then

\beta_{3k}\equiv\alpha_{3k}+\sum_{i=k}^{2k+1}i\alpha_{i}\alpha_{3k+1-i}+\binom{k}{2}\alpha_{k}(\alpha_{k+1})^{2}+\left(\binom{k+2}{2}+1\right)\alpha_{k+2}\alpha_{k}^{\penalty\ 2}\pmod{2}.

vii.

If
k\equiv 0\pmod{2}
, then

\beta_{3k+1}\equiv\alpha_{3k+1}+\sum_{i=k}^{2k+2}i\alpha_{i}\alpha_{3k+2-i}+\left(\binom{k+1}{2}+1\right)(\alpha_{k+1})^{3}+\binom{k+3}{2}\alpha_{k+3}\alpha_{k}^{\penalty\ 2}\pmod{2}.

viii.

If
k\equiv 0\pmod{4}
, then
\beta_{3k+2}=0
, and

\beta_{3k+3}\equiv\alpha_{3k+3}+\sum_{i=k}^{2k+4}i\alpha_{i}\alpha_{3k+4-i}\pmod{2}.

Corollary 4.0.14
.

Let
k\geqslant 5
. Let
f
and
g
be elements in
\Gamma_{k}
, with
f=p^{\prime}(x+\sum_{i=k}^{c_{k}-1}\alpha_{i}x^{i}+O(x^{c_{k}}))
and
g=p^{\prime}(x+\sum_{i=k}^{c_{k}-1}\beta_{i}x^{i}+O(x^{c_{k}}))

\delta_{l}
be the
l
th coefficient of
[f,g]
.

If
k\leqslant l\leqslant 2k-1

\delta_{l}=0
.

ii.

If
2k\leqslant l\leqslant d_{k}-1

iii.

k\equiv 0\pmod{2}

\delta_{3k-2}=0

k\equiv 0\pmod{2}

\delta_{3k-1}\equiv\binom{k+1}{2}(\alpha_{k+1}\beta_{k}^{\penalty\ 2}+\beta_{k+1}\alpha_{k}^{\penalty\ 2})\pmod{2}.

\delta_{3k}=0

If
