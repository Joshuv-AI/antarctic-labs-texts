A Distribution Free Truncated Kernel Ridge Regression Estimator and Related Spectral Analyses
Asma Ben Saber

Abstract




a
University of Carthage,
Department of Mathematics, Faculty of Sciences of Bizerte, Tunisia.

Abstract
— It is well known that kernel ridge regression (KRR) is a popular approach for nonparametric regression based estimator. Nonetheless, in the presence of a large data set with size
n\gg 1,
the KRR based estimator has the drawback to require an intensive computational load. Recently, some scalable KRR approaches have been proposed with the aims to reduce the computational complexity of the KRR, while maintaining its superb convergence rate. In this work, we study a new scalable KRR based
approach for nonparametric regression. The proposed truncated kernel ridge regression (TKRR) approach is simple. It is based on substituting the full
n\times n
random kernel or Gram matrix
B_{n},
associated with a Mercer’s kernel
\mathbb{K},
by its main
n\times N
sub-matrix
A_{N},
where usually
N\ll n.
Unlike some of the existing scalable KRR estimators, the TKRR does not require a pre-processing step, such as an SVD or a sketching by a certain random matrix. Also, we show that the TKRR works with
d-
dimensional random sampling data following an unknown probability law. To do so, we give a spectral analysis for the compact kernel integral operator, associated with a probability measure, different from its usual probability measure. This decay estimate is then extended to the decay of the tail of the trace of the associated random Gram matrix. We expect that this spectral analysis is also useful for the study of others machine learning subjects such as the kernels based unsupervised domain learning problems. A special interest is devoted to develop rules for the optimal choices of the involved truncation order
N
and the value for regularization parameter
\lambda>0.
The proposed rules are based on the behavior and the decay rate of the spectrum of the positive integral operator, associated with the kernel
\mathbb{K}.
These optimal values of the parameters ensure that in terms of the empirical risk error, the TKRR and the full KRR estimators have the same optimal convergence rate. Finally, we provide the reader with some numerical simulations that illustrate the performance of our proposed TKRR estimator.

Keywords:
Nonparametric regression, scaled KRR, spectral analysis, random Gram matrix, reproducing kernel Hilbert space, eigenvalues.

1
Introduction

For an integer
d\geq 1,
let
\boldsymbol{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a compact set. Then, we consider the usual
d-
dimensional non-parametric regression problem given by

Y_{i}=f^{*}(X_{i})+\varepsilon_{i},\quad 1\leq i\leq n.

(1)

Here, the
\boldsymbol{Y}=[Y_{i}]^{T}_{1\leq i\leq n}
is the vector of real valued random responses,

f^{*}:\boldsymbol{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the unknown
d-
variate regression function to be estimated. The
X_{i}\in\boldsymbol{X}
are the
\ n
covariates. The
\varepsilon_{i}
are
n
i.i.d. centered random variables with variance
\mathbb{E}\big[\varepsilon_{i}^{2}\big]=\sigma^{2}.

Also, we assume that the
\varepsilon_{i}
are independent from the
X_{i}.
In the literature, there is a variety of schemes providing different estimators for the regression function
f^{*}.
The Kernel Ridge Regression (KRR) is among the popular schemes for providing fairly good estimate of
f^{*}
under the hypothesis that this latter is well approximated by its projection over the associated reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS).

Recently, there is a growing interest in developing scalable methods for KRR algorithm with the main objective: A drastic reduction of the computational complexity time of the KRR while maintaining the high convergence rate of this latter.
Among the existing scalable KRR estimators, we briefly describe the following four different improved KRR based estimator. The first estimator is given by
[
2
]
. It is briefly described as follows.
The covariates
{x_{i}}
are assumed to be deterministic and the regression function
f^{*}
belongs to the RKHS
\mathcal{H},
associated with a positive semi-definite kernel
\mathbb{K}(\cdot,\cdot),
defined on
\boldsymbol{X}\times\boldsymbol{X}.
Then, instead of using the full KRR estimator which involves the inversion of the regularized
n\times n
Kernel Gram matrix, the author
has proposed an approximation scheme where the Gram matrix is replaced by its best rank
r-
approximation, for some integer
1\leq r\leq n.
This is obtained by performing an SVD to the main Gram matrix and then considering only the main significant
r
eigenvalues in the diagonal matrix and setting to zero the remaining
n-r
eigenvalues. The second scalable KRR estimator is the Sketching based estimator given in
[
25
]
, see also,
[
3
]
. More precisely,
given
n
samples and a positive integer
m<n,
the idea of the sketching is to replace the full kernel matrix by
m-
dimensional randomized sketches of this latter. The projection dimension
m
and the sketch matrix are chosen in such a way to maintain the optimal convergence rate of the KRR estimator. In particular, the sketch dimension
m
has to be proportional (modulo logarithmic factors) to the statistical dimension of the kernel or Gram matrix
{\bf K.}
Recall that for a given regularization parameter
\lambda>0,
the most
adopted definition of the statistical dimension of a kernel matrix
{\bf K}
is given by

d_{\bf K}(\lambda)={\bf Tr}\big(({\bf K}+\lambda I_{n})^{-1}{\bf K}\big),

(2)

where
{\bf Tr}(A)
denotes the trace of a matrix
A.

The third scalable KRR estimator is given in
[
20
]
and it is known as FALKON. This algorithm is based on the use of
random projections in order to reduce the computational complexity and the memory requirements of the conventional KRR. More precisely, given samples
\{x_{i},y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, then only a subset
\{\tilde{x}_{1}\cdots\tilde{x}_{M}\}\subseteq\{x_{1}\cdots x_{n}\}
with
M\ll n
training points sampled uniformly are used to construct the estimator

\widehat{f}_{\lambda,M}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\tilde{\alpha}_{i}\mathbb{K}(x_{i},\tilde{x}_{i}).

Typically,
M=O(\sqrt{n})
suffices for the FALKON algorithm to achieve optimal statistical accuracy of the full
KRR
. The fourth but the pioneer of the scalable KRR is the Random Fourier Features (RFF), due to
[
17
]
. The RFF requires that the positive-definite kernel
\mathbb{K}(\cdot,\cdot)
is a convolution or stationary kernel of Bochner’s type. Thanks to the structure of such a kernel, this latter is efficiently approximated by its Monte-carlo approximation that involves
s
Fourier features,
where
s\ll n.
Consequently, the large size kernel matrix
K
is substituted with an
s\times s
positive semi-definite matrix involving
s
Fourier features.
Recently, an extension of the RFF
to more general kernels has been provided in
[
28
]
. A more detailed description of the RFF approach will be given in the next section.

We should mention that our proposed TKRR and the RFF have some common points. Nonetheless, the TKRR does not require a convolution type structure of the positive-definite kernel. It is based on a substitution of the
n\times n
random kernel matrix

{\displaystyle B_{n}=\left[\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{K}(X_{i},X_{j})\right]_{1\leq i,j\leq n}}
by its
n\times N
main submatrix
{\displaystyle A_{N}=\left[\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{K}(X_{i},X_{j})\right]_{\underset{1\leq j\leq N}{1\leq i\leq n}}},
where
N\ll n.
Moreover, the
n
covariates
X_{i}
are i.i.d. and drawn according to an unknown probability law
\rho(\cdot).
We assume that the measure
d\rho
is absolutely continuous with respect the the usual probability measure
dP,
associated with a Mercer’s kernel
\mathbb{K}(\cdot,\cdot).
More precisely, our TKRR is given as follows,

\widehat{f}_{N,\lambda}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\widehat{\omega}_{j}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{K}(X_{j},x),\qquad\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}=\big[\widehat{\omega}_{j}\big]^{T}_{1\leq j\leq N}=\big(A_{N}^{*}A_{N}+\lambda I_{N}\big)^{-1}A_{N}^{*}\boldsymbol{Y}.

(3)

In particular, we show that the choice of the optimal value of the truncation order is based on the behavior of the spectrum of the Hilbert-Schmidt integral operator
T_{\mathbb{K}},
defined on
L^{2}(\boldsymbol{X},dP).
The decay rate of the spectrum of
T_{\mathbb{K}},
as well as the rank from which this decay holds true are crucial for the choice of optimal truncation order
N.

We assume that for the probability measure
dP
on
\boldsymbol{X},
the spectral properties of the
operator
T_{\mathbb{K}}

are known. The first main result of this work is the proof that
under some assumptions on the unknown sampling measure
d\rho(\cdot),
the eigenvalues of full random kernel matrix, as well as the sequence of singular values of the truncated
n\times N
Gram matrix have
similar decay rate as the eigenvalues of the operator
T_{\mathbb{K}},
when defined on
L^{2}(\boldsymbol{X},dP).
The second main result of this work is an empirical
L^{2}-
risk error of our TKRR estimator for solving nonparametric (NP) regression problems. In particular, we show that the proposed TKRR estimator has three main advantages: Its empirical
L^{2}-
risk error is similar to the
L^{2}-
risk of the full KRR algorithm, it is much faster than the original KRR based algorithm and it is adapted for a whole class of unknown sampling probability laws. This makes our proposed distribution free truncated KRR estimator well adapted for analysis of driven data.

This work is organized as follows. In section 2, we give some mathematical preliminaries on the classical KRR estimator as well as on the Random Fourier Features scalable KRR estimator. Then, we describe our proposed TKRR estimator. In section 3, we prove a fairly useful bound for the eigenvalues of a compact kernel integral operator, associated with an unknown probability measure, in terms of the eigenvalues of this operator when associated with its usual probability measure. Then, we use this result to estimate
the decay rate of the eigenvalues of a random kernel matrix, associated with a sampling set drawn from an unknown probability density. In section 4, we give the empirical risk error of our TKRR estimator. Then, by using the spectral analysis of section 3, we give the convergence rate of the TKRR estimator under the assumptions that the spectrum of the associated kernel integral operator has an exponential or polynomial decay rates. In section 5, we give some spectral properties of the Sinc-kernel and Gaussian kernel integral operators. Then, we provide the reader with some numerical simulations that illustrate the theoretical properties and the performance of our proposed TKRR estimator.

2
Preliminaries on RKHS and related KRR based estimators

In this paragraph, we first give some preliminaries on the conventional KRR estimator. Then, we describe the scalable Random Fourier Features estimator (RFF). The proposed TKRR shares some ideas behind the RFF. Nonetheless, these two scalable KRR estimators have a fundamental difference. The RFF is based on a Monte-Carlo approximation of a convolution kernel of Bochner’s type, while the TKRR is based on a truncation of a fairly general kernel random matrix. Finally, we describe our proposed TKRR estimator.

The KRR estimator for problem (
1
) is briefly described as follows. We first recall that a real-valued kernel
\mathbb{K}(\cdot,\cdot)
defined on
\boldsymbol{X}\times\boldsymbol{X}
is said to be a Mercer’s kernel if it is continuous and positive semi-definite. For simplicity, we may assume in the sequel that
\mathbb{K}(\cdot,\cdot)
is positive-definite. Moreover, let
d\rho
be a probability measure with support
\boldsymbol{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
(\varphi_{n})_{n\geq 1},(\lambda_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be the orthonormal eigenfunctions and the corresponding eigenvalues of the associated Hilbert-Schmidt operator
T_{\mathbb{K}}.
That is

T_{\mathbb{K}}\varphi_{n}(x)=\int_{\boldsymbol{X}}\mathbb{K}(x,y)\varphi_{n}(y)\,d\rho(y)=\lambda_{n}\,\varphi_{n}(x),\quad x\in\boldsymbol{X}.

Then, by Mercer’s Theorem, we have:
\mathbb{K}(x,y)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}\,\varphi_{n}(x)\varphi_{n}(y)
for
x,y\in\boldsymbol{X}
.
This sum converges uniformly over the compact set
\boldsymbol{X}\times\boldsymbol{X}.
Moreover, the associated RKHS
\mathcal{H}
is given by:

\mathcal{H}=\left\{f\in L^{2}(\boldsymbol{X},d\rho),\,\,f=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}(f)\varphi_{n},\,\,\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{|a_{n}(f)|^{2}}{\lambda_{n}}<+\infty\right\}.

(4)

For a given regularization parameter
\lambda>0,
the KRR algorithm consists in finding a solution
f_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{H}
of the minimization problem

\widehat{f}_{K}=\arg\min_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(f(X_{i})-Y_{i})\Big)^{2}+\lambda\|f\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}\right\}.

(5)

It is well known, see for example
[
22
]
, that a solution of (
5
) is given by the estimator

\widehat{f}_{K}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{c}_{i}\mathbb{K}(X_{i},x),

(6)

where the expansion coefficients vector
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}=(\widehat{c}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
is a solution of the system

\left[\Big[\mathbb{K}(x_{i},x_{j})\Big]_{1\leq i,j\leq n}+n\lambda I_{n}\right]\widehat{\mathbf{C}}=G_{\lambda}\,\widehat{\mathbf{C}}=\mathbf{Y},\quad\mathbf{Y}=(Y_{i})^{T}_{1\leq i\leq n}.

(7)

Here,
I_{n}
is the
n\times n
identity matrix and
\frac{1}{n}[\mathbb{K}(x_{i},x_{j})]_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
is the random Gram matrix associated with the kernel
\mathbb{K}(\cdot,\cdot).
This result is a consequence of the famous representer theorem. This theorem is particularly useful in the sense that even if the RKHS associated with the minimization problem (
5
) is of infinite dimension, the solution (
6
) always lies in a finite dimensional space.
Also, under the condition that the regularized random Gram matrix
G_{\lambda}
given by (
7
) is invertible, the expansion coefficients vector
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}
is given by

\widehat{\mathbf{C}}=G_{\lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{Y}=\left[\Big[\mathbb{K}(x_{i},x_{j})\Big]_{1\leq i,j\leq n}+n\lambda I_{n}\right]^{-1}\mathbf{Y},\quad\mathbf{Y}=(Y_{i})^{T}_{1\leq i\leq n}.

(8)

As we have already mentioned, the previous conventional KRR estimator has advantage of having a fast convergence rate. Nonetheless, it has the main drawback to require an intensive computational load or a high time complexity. To overcome this drawback, some scalable KRR estimators have been recently proposed in the literature. The pioneer of these KRR approaches is the Random Fourier Features (RFF) estimator, proposed in
[
17
]
. This latter uses the convolution and positive definite kernels of
Bochner’s type. That is those kernels of the form
{\displaystyle\mathbb{K}(x,y)=\kappa(x-y),\,x,y\in\boldsymbol{X},}
where

\kappa(x-y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-2i\pi\omega^{T}\cdot(x-y)}\,dP(\omega)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-2i\pi\omega^{T}\cdot x}e^{2i\pi\omega^{T}\cdot y}\,dP(\omega).

Here,
dP(\omega)
is a probability measure. Typically, the kernel is real valued. Consequently, we may only consider the real part of this kernel. The idea behind the RFF is to replace the previous kernel by its following Monte-Carlo approximation

\widetilde{\mathbb{K}}(x,y)=\frac{1}{s}\sum_{j=1}^{s}Z^{*}_{\omega_{j}}(x)Z_{\omega_{j}}(y)=Z_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{*}(x)Z_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(y),\quad Z_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}}\Big(\cos(2\pi\omega_{1}^{T}\cdot x),\ldots,\cos(2\pi\omega_{s}^{T}\cdot x)\Big)^{T}.

Here, the
\omega_{i}
are i.i.d. and drawn according to
P(\cdot)
and
s
is the number of Fourier features, with the usual assumption that
s\ll n.
The
n\times n
kernel matrix
\bf K
and its Monte-Carlo kernel matrix approximation
\widetilde{\bf K}
are given by

{\bf K}=\Big[\kappa(X_{i}-X_{j})\Big]_{1\leq i,j\leq n},\quad\widetilde{\bf K}=\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{Z}^{*},\quad\boldsymbol{Z}=\Big[Z_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(X_{1}),\ldots,Z_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(X_{n})\Big]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times s}.

(9)

Note that since
{\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(\widetilde{\bf K}\big)={\bf K},}
then
\widetilde{\bf K}
is an unbiased approximation of the kernel matrix
{\bf K}.
The RFF estimator is given by

\widetilde{f}_{s,\lambda}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{s}\widetilde{\alpha}_{j}Z_{\omega_{j}}(x)=Z^{T}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\alpha}},\qquad\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\alpha}}=\big(\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}\boldsymbol{Z}+s\lambda I_{s}\big)^{-1}\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}\boldsymbol{Y}.

(10)

That is
\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
is the solution of the minimization problem

\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\alpha}}=\argmin_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{s}}\frac{1}{n}\big\|\boldsymbol{Y}-\boldsymbol{Z}\alpha\big\|^{2}_{\ell_{2,n}}+s\lambda\big\|\alpha\big\|^{2}_{\ell_{2,n}}.

(11)

Here,
\|\cdot\|_{\ell_{2,n}}
is the usual Euclidean norm of
\mathbb{R}^{n}.

For more details, see for example
[
4
]
. Recently, in
[
28
]
, an extension of the RFF has been provided and studied. It extends the RFF approach to those kernels of the form

\mathbb{K}(x,y)=\int_{\mathcal{V}}z(\omega,x)z(\omega,y)\,dP(\omega),

(12)

where
z:{\mathcal{V}}\times\boldsymbol{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a continuous and bounded function.

Next, we describe how to derive our proposed TKRR estimator.
For two positive integers
N\leq n
, we let
S_{X}=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}
be a random sampling set following a probability law with a pdf
P(\cdot),
supported on
\boldsymbol{X}.
Let
\mathbb{K}(\cdot,\cdot)
be a continuous and positive-definite Mercer’s kernel, so that the integral operator
T_{\mathbb{K}}
defined on
L^{2}(\boldsymbol{X},dP)
by

T_{\mathbb{K}}(f)(x)=\int_{\boldsymbol{X}}\mathbb{K}(x,y)f(y)dP(y)

(13)

is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator with a countable set of positive eigenvalues, arranged in the decreasing order

\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}\geq\cdots\geq 0.

The eigenvalues
\lambda_{n}
are associated to an orthonormal set of eigenfunctions
\{\varphi_{n},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\}.
Moreover, under the hypothesis that the integral operator
T_{\mathbb{K}}
is one to one, the
\varphi_{n}
constitute an orthonormal basis of the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\boldsymbol{X},dP).

Recall that the Mercer’s kernel has the spectral decomposition

\mathbb{K}(x,y)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{m}\varphi_{m}(x)\varphi_{m}(y),\qquad\forall\,x,y\in\boldsymbol{X}.

(14)

In the sequel, we adopt the Notations:

\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=(\varepsilon_{1},\cdots,\varepsilon_{n}),\quad A_{N}^{*}A_{N}=K_{N}^{2},\quad\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{n}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{2},\quad\boldsymbol{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(15)

For
\boldsymbol{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

K_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times N},\,K_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
two positive definite matrices and
\lambda>0,

we aim to find

\underset{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}{\argmin}\|K_{1}\boldsymbol{\omega}-\boldsymbol{Y}\|_{n}^{2}+\lambda\omega^{T}\cdot K_{2}\omega=\underset{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}{\argmin}J_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{\omega})

(16)

Using the linearity of
K_{1}
and the bi-linearity of
<\cdot,\cdot>,
the usual inner product of
\mathbb{R}^{n},
together with the fact that
K_{2}^{*}=K_{2},
on gets

\displaystyle\nabla J_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\cdot\boldsymbol{h}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{n}<K_{1}\boldsymbol{\omega}-\boldsymbol{Y},K_{1}\boldsymbol{h}>+2\lambda<K_{2}\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{h}>=\frac{2}{n}<K_{1}^{*}K_{1}\boldsymbol{\omega}-K_{1}^{*}\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{h}>+2\lambda<K_{2}\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{h}>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2<\frac{1}{n}K_{1}^{*}K_{1}\boldsymbol{\omega}-\frac{1}{n}K_{1}^{*}\boldsymbol{Y}+\lambda K_{2}\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{h}>

So that
{\displaystyle\nabla J_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{\omega})=0}
whenever
{\displaystyle\Big(\frac{1}{n}K_{1}^{*}K_{1}+\lambda K_{2}\Big)\boldsymbol{\omega}=\frac{1}{n}K_{1}^{*}\boldsymbol{Y}.}
That is

\boldsymbol{\omega}=\Big(K_{1}^{*}K_{1}+n\lambda K_{2}\Big)^{-1}K_{1}^{*}\boldsymbol{Y}.

(17)

The TKRR estimator is described as follows. For a given compact set
\boldsymbol{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d},
a positive definite Mercer’s kernel
\mathbb{K}
defined on
\boldsymbol{X}\times\boldsymbol{X}
and a set of
n
i.i.d. sampling points following an unknown probability density supported on
\boldsymbol{X},
we consider an integer
1\leq N\leq n
and the rectangular random Gram matrix

K_{1}=A_{N}=\left[\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{K}(X_{i},X_{j})\right]_{1\leq i\leq n,1\leq j\leq N}

(18)

Note that
K_{1}^{*}K_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is positive semi-definite and we can control the eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}\big(K_{1}^{*}K_{1}\big)
of
K_{1}^{*}K_{1}
. Our TKRR estimator is denoted by
\widehat{f}_{N,\lambda}
and it is given by the solution of the minimization problem (
16
) with the previous Gram matrix
K_{1}=A_{N}
and the matrix
{\displaystyle K_{2}=\frac{1}{n}I_{N}.}
Hence, by using
(
17
) with a convenient value for the regularization parameter
\lambda>0,
the estimator
\widehat{f}_{N,\lambda}
is given by the following explicit formula

\widehat{f}_{N,\lambda}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\widehat{\omega}_{j}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{K}(X_{j},x),\qquad\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}=\big(A_{N}^{*}A_{N}+\lambda I_{N}\big)^{-1}A_{N}^{*}\boldsymbol{Y},\quad\boldsymbol{Y}=\Big[Y_{i}\Big]^{T}_{1\leq i\leq n}.

(19)

3
Spectrum decay of distributions free Gram matrices

In this paragraph, we first investigate the important problem of the decay rate estimate of a random Gram matrix associated with a fairly general and unknown sampling probability law. Then, we extend this decay rate to the singular values of a rectangular random Gram matrix. This last decay rate estimate will be used in the empirical risk error of our truncated KRR. More importantly, the issue of decay rate of the eigenvalues of Gram matrices is particularly useful in many kernels based algorithms for machine learning and data science applications. This is the case for example for kernels based unsupervised domain learning problems, where usually the source probability measure is different from the target probability measure. This latter is used to estimate the expected risk of the prediction of the output
y\in\bf Y\subset\mathbb{R}
from the input
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.
For more details on this issue, the reader is referred to
[
14
]
and the references therein.

Typically, when a KRR algorithm is used for solving a learning problem, the associated Gram matrix is generally constructed from a set of
d-
dimensional sampling random vectors following an unknown probability measure
d\rho
which is generally different from the usual probability measure
dP,
associated with the corresponding kenel integral operator. For example, in the one dimensional case, for a positive parameter
c>0,
the Sinc-kernel
{\displaystyle\mathbb{K}_{c}(x,y)=\frac{\sin(c(x-y))}{\pi(x-y)}},
defined on
I=[-1,1]^{2}
is associated to the uniform measure over
I,
given by
{\displaystyle dP(x)=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}_{[-1,1]}(x)\,dx}.
Also, for two positive real numbers
\xi,c>0,
the Gaussian kernel
{\displaystyle\mathbb{K}_{G}(x,y)=e^{-\xi|x-y|^{2}}}
is associated to its usual Gaussian measure
{\displaystyle dP_{c}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2c}}e^{-2cx^{2}}}
. For more details on the spectra and the super-exponential decay rate of the eigenvalues of these two last kernels operators, the reader is referred to
[
8
]
and
[
18
]
, respectively.

It is well known that a Gram matrix has similar spectrum properties as the associated integral operator, see for example
[
21
,
8
]
. Consequently, we are first interested in comparing the spectra of two self-adjoint Hilbert-Schmidt operators,
T_{{\mathbb{K}}}
and
T_{\mathbb{K},\rho},
associated with the same continuous Mercer’s kernel
\mathbb{K}(\cdot,\cdot)
but acting on different Hilbert spaces
L^{2}(\boldsymbol{X},dP)
and
L^{2}(\boldsymbol{X},d\rho).

Here,
dP
is the original considered probability measure and
d\rho
is an unknown probability measure supported on the compact set
\boldsymbol{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}.
Moreover, we assume the
following two hypotheses:

\bf H_{0}:
The operator
T_{\mathbb{K}}:L^{2}(\boldsymbol{X},dP)\rightarrow L^{2}(\boldsymbol{X},dP)
is one-to-one.

\bf H_{1}:

d\rho
is absolutely continuous with respect to
dP
and

{\displaystyle\frac{d\rho}{dP}}
is bounded on
\boldsymbol{X}.

We let

{\displaystyle\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}\geq\cdots\geq 0\mbox{\ and \ }\lambda_{1,\rho}\geq\lambda_{2,\rho}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n,\rho}\geq\cdots\geq 0}

denote the eigenvalues of
T_{\mathbb{K}}
and
T_{{\mathbb{K}},\rho},
arranged in the decreasing order. Also, we let
\big\{\varphi_{n},\,n\in\mathbb{N}\big\}
denote the set of the eigenfunctions of
T_{\mathbb{K}}.
Note that by hypothesis
\bf H_{0}
and the assumption that the kernel
\mathbb{K}(\cdot,\cdot)
is a continuous Mercer’s kernel, the
\varphi_{n}
form an orthonormal basis of the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\boldsymbol{X},dP).
In practice, regression estimators have to deal with
d-
dimensional data samples. These samples are usually assumed to be i.i.d. and following an unknown probability law with unknown pdf
\rho(\cdot).
Given the previous continuous Mercer’s kernel
\mathbb{K}(\cdot,\cdot),
with associated self-adjoint Hilbert operator
T_{{\mathbb{K}}}
, having known spectral properties, we consider the following general case of
n\times n
random Gram matrix

B_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\Big[\mathbb{K}(X_{i},X_{j})\Big]_{1\leq i,j\leq n},

(20)

where the
X_{i}
are i.i.d. random samples drawn according to
\rho(\cdot).
The following theorem provides us with interesting and useful upper bounds for the unknown eigenvalues
\lambda_{k,\rho}
as well as the expected value for the tail of the trace of the random matrix
B_{n}.

Theorem 1
.

Under the previous notations and hypotheses
\bf H_{0}
and
\bf H_{1},
we have

\lambda_{m,\rho}\leq\sum_{j\geq m}\lambda_{j}\|\varphi_{j}\|^{2}_{\rho},\quad\forall\,m\geq 1.

(21)

Moreover, for any integer
1\leq k\leq n,
we have

\mathcal{T}_{k}(B_{n})=\mathbb{E}_{Z}\Big[\sum_{j\geq k}\lambda_{j}(B_{n})\Big]\leq\sum_{j\geq k}\lambda_{j,\rho}\lesssim\lambda_{k}\|\varphi_{k}\|^{2}_{\rho},\quad\mbox{ whenever }\sum_{i\geq k}\frac{(i-k+1)\lambda_{i}\|\varphi_{i}\|^{2}_{\rho}}{\lambda_{k}\|\varphi_{k}\|^{2}_{\rho}}<+\infty.

(22)

Here,
{\displaystyle\|\varphi_{j}\|^{2}_{\rho}=\int_{\boldsymbol{X}}|\varphi^{2}_{j}(y)|\,d\rho(y).}

Proof:
We first note that hypothesis
\bf H_{1}
and the well known and powerful Radon-Nikodym theorem give us the existence of the measure derivative
{\displaystyle\frac{d\rho}{dP}.}
Moreover, for any function
f\in L^{1}(\boldsymbol{X},d\rho),
we have the change of variable-type formula,

\|f\|_{\rho}=\int_{\boldsymbol{X}}|f(y)|d\rho(y)=\int_{\boldsymbol{X}}|f(y)|\frac{d\rho(y)}{dP(y)}\,dP(y)=\int_{\boldsymbol{X}}|f(y)|\frac{\rho(y)}{P(y)}\,dP(y).

In particular, since the Mercer’s kernel
\mathbb{K}(\cdot,\cdot)
is continuous, then the eigenfunctions
\varphi_{m}
of
T_{\mathbb{K}}
are continuous over the compact set
\boldsymbol{X}.
Consequently, they are bounded and belong to
L^{2}(\boldsymbol{X},dP)\cap L^{2}(\boldsymbol{X},d\rho).
That is the
\|\varphi_{m}\|_{\rho}
are well defined for any integer
m\geq 1.
Also, it is easy to see that the function

{\displaystyle\psi_{k}:x\rightarrow\varphi_{k}(x)\sqrt{\frac{P(x)}{\rho(x)}}}
belongs to
L^{2}(\boldsymbol{X},d\rho).

Next, for an integer
m\geq 1,
consider the two
m-
dimensional subspaces of
L^{2}(\boldsymbol{X},d\rho),
given by

S_{m}=\mbox{Span}\Big\{\varphi_{k}(x)\sqrt{\frac{P(x)}{\rho(x)}};\,1\leq k\leq m\Big\},\quad V_{m}=\mbox{Span}\Big\{\varphi_{k}(x);\,1\leq k\leq m\Big\}.

The linear independence of the
\varphi_{k}
in
L^{2}(\boldsymbol{X},d\rho)
is a consequence of their orthogonality in
L^{2}(\boldsymbol{X},dP)
and the condition
\bf H_{1}.
More precisely, if
{\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}\varphi_{k}(x)=0,}
then
{\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}{a_{k}}\varphi_{k}(x)\frac{P(x)}{\rho(x)}=0.}
Consequently for any
1\leq j\leq m,
we have
{\displaystyle a_{j}=<\sum_{k=1}^{m}{a_{k}}\varphi_{k}(x)\frac{P(x)}{\rho(x)},\varphi_{j}>_{\rho}=0.}
That is the
\varphi_{j}
are also linearly independent in
L^{2}(\boldsymbol{X},d\rho).

Next, we show that
S_{m}^{\perp}
and
V_{m}^{\perp},
the orthogonal of
S_{m}
and
V_{m}
in
L^{2}(\boldsymbol{X},d\rho)
are given by

S_{m}^{\perp}=\mbox{Span}\Big\{\varphi_{k}(x)\sqrt{\frac{P(x)}{\rho(x)}};\,k\geq m+1\Big\},\quad V_{m}^{\perp}=\mbox{Span}\Big\{\varphi_{k}(x)\frac{P(x)}{\rho(x)};\,k\geq m+1\Big\}.

It is clear that from the change of variable-type formula, we have

\int_{\boldsymbol{X}}\varphi_{j}(x)\varphi_{k}(x)\frac{P(x)}{\rho(x)}d\rho(x)=\int_{\boldsymbol{X}}\varphi_{j}(x)\varphi_{k}(x)dP(x)=0,\quad\forall j\leq m,\,k\geq m+1.

That is any element from
S_{m}^{\perp}
and
V_{m}^{\perp}
is orthogonal to every element of
S_{m}
and
V_{m},
respectively. Next, assume that there exists
\psi\in L^{2}(\boldsymbol{X},d\rho)
which is orthogonal to
S_{m},
then we check that
\psi\in S_{m}^{\perp}.
Since the function
\psi(\cdot)\sqrt{\frac{\rho(\cdot)}{P(\cdot)}}
belongs to
L^{2}(\boldsymbol{X},dP),
then by writing for
1\leq k\leq m,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\boldsymbol{X}}\varphi_{k}(x)\sqrt{\frac{P(x)}{\rho(x)}}\psi(x)\,\rho(x)dx=\int_{\boldsymbol{X}}\varphi_{k}(x)\psi(x)\sqrt{\frac{\rho(x)}{P(x)}}\,P(x)dx,

one concludes that
\psi(\cdot)\sqrt{\frac{\rho(\cdot)}{P(\cdot)}}

belongs to the orthogonal subspace
E_{m}^{\perp}=\mbox{Span}\Big\{\varphi_{k}(x),\,1\leq k\leq m\Big\}^{\perp}.
Moreover, since
\{\varphi_{j},\,j\geq 1\}
is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\boldsymbol{X},dP),
then
E_{m}^{\perp}=\mbox{Span}\{\varphi_{k}(x),\,k\geq m+1\Big\}.
That is
\psi(\cdot)\in S_{m}^{\perp}.
In a similar manner, we check that if
\psi\in L^{2}(\boldsymbol{X},d\rho)
is orthogonal to
V_{m},
then
\psi\in\mbox{Span}\Big\{\varphi_{k}(x)\frac{P(x)}{\rho(x)};\,\,k\geq m+1\Big\}.
This is a simple consequence of the fact that

0=\int_{\boldsymbol{X}}\varphi_{j}(x)\psi(x)d\rho(x)=\int_{\boldsymbol{X}}\varphi_{j}(x)\sqrt{\frac{P(x)}{\rho(x)}}\psi(x)\sqrt{\frac{\rho(x)}{P(x)}}d\rho(x),\quad 1\leq j\leq m

(23)

and the second statement of hypothesis
\bf H_{1}
that implies that the function
{\displaystyle\psi(\cdot)\sqrt{\frac{\rho(\cdot)}{P(\cdot)}}\in L^{2}(\boldsymbol{X},d\rho).}

Consequently, from (
23
), one concludes that

\psi(\cdot)\sqrt{\frac{\rho(\cdot)}{P(\cdot)}}\in S_{n}^{\perp}
which implies that
\psi(\cdot)\in V_{m}^{\perp}.

Next, we use the Min-Max characterization of positive eigenvalues that are arranged in decreasing order of self-adjoint compact operator
T
acting on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}.
More precisely,

\lambda_{m}=\min_{U_{m-1}}\max_{f\in U_{m-1}^{\perp},\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq 1}<Tf,f>_{\mathcal{H}}.

Here, the
U_{m}
run over the
m-
dimensional subspaces of

\mathcal{H}
and
<\cdot,\cdot>_{\mathcal{H}}
is the inner product of
\mathcal{H}.
In particular, for the special case
\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(\boldsymbol{X},d\rho)
and
U_{m-1}=V_{m-1},
one gets

\lambda_{m,\rho}\leq\max_{f\in V_{m-1}^{\perp},\|f\|_{\rho}\leq 1}<T_{\mathbb{K},\rho}f,f>_{\rho}.

Since
f\in V_{m-1}^{\perp},
then

{\displaystyle f(x)=\sum_{k\geq m}\alpha_{k}\varphi_{k}(x)\frac{P(x)}{\rho(x)}.}
Hence, by using the spectral decomposition of the Mercer’s kernel
\mathbb{K}(\cdot,\cdot),
together with the orthonormality of the
\varphi_{k}
in
L^{2}(\boldsymbol{X},dP),
one gets for
f\in V_{m-1}^{\perp},

\displaystyle<T_{\mathbb{K},\rho}f,f>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\boldsymbol{X}}\left(\int_{\boldsymbol{X}}\sum_{n\geq 1}\lambda_{n}\varphi_{n}(x)\varphi_{n}(y)\cdot\sum_{k\geq m}\alpha_{k}\varphi_{k}(y)\frac{P(y)}{\rho(y)}d\rho(y)\right)\sum_{j\geq m}\alpha_{j}\varphi_{j}(x)\frac{P(x)}{\rho(x)}d\rho(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\boldsymbol{X}}\left(\sum_{n\geq 1,k\geq m}\varphi_{n}(x)\int_{\boldsymbol{X}}\lambda_{n}\cdot\alpha_{k}\,\varphi_{n}(y)\varphi_{k}(y)dP(y)\right)\sum_{j\geq m}\alpha_{j}\varphi_{j}(x)\frac{P(x)}{\rho(x)}d\rho(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\boldsymbol{X}}\sum_{k\geq m}\lambda_{k}\cdot\alpha_{k}\,\varphi_{k}(x)\sum_{j\geq m}\alpha_{j}\varphi_{j}(x)dP(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq m}\lambda_{k}\cdot\alpha_{k}^{2}.

On the other hand, from the previous expansion of
f
and Cauchy-Schwartz inequality,

|\alpha_{k}|^{2}=|<f,\varphi_{k}>_{\rho}|^{2}\leq\|f\|^{2}_{\rho}\,\|\varphi_{k}\|^{2}_{\rho}.

Consequently,

\max_{f\in V_{m-1}^{\perp},\|f\|_{\rho}\leq 1}<T_{\mathbb{K},\rho}f,f>_{\rho}\leq\sum_{k\geq n}\lambda_{k}\cdot\|\varphi_{k}\|^{2}_{\rho}.

This concludes the proof of (
21
). To prove (
22
), the first inequality can be found in
[
21
]
,
see also
[
8
]
. To prove the second inequality,
we use (
21
) and get

\mathbb{E}_{Z}\Big[\sum_{j\geq k}\lambda_{j}(B_{n})\Big]\leq\sum_{j\geq k}\sum_{i\geq j}\lambda_{i}\|\varphi_{i}\|^{2}_{\rho}\leq\sum_{i\geq k}(i-k+1)\lambda_{i}\|\varphi_{i}\|^{2}_{\rho}\lesssim\lambda_{k}\|\varphi_{k}\|^{2}_{\rho}.

To check the previous third inequality, it suffices to write

\sum_{i\geq k}(i-k+1)\lambda_{i}\|\varphi_{i}\|^{2}_{\rho}=\lambda_{k}\|\varphi_{k}\|^{2}_{\rho}\cdot\sum_{i\geq k}\frac{(i-k+1)\lambda_{i}\|\varphi_{i}\|^{2}_{\rho}}{\lambda_{k}\|\varphi_{k}\|^{2}_{\rho}}\lesssim\lambda_{k}\|\varphi_{k}\|^{2}_{\rho},

whenever
{\displaystyle\sum_{i\geq k}\frac{(i-k+1)\lambda_{i}\|\varphi_{i}\|^{2}_{\rho}}{\lambda_{k}\|\varphi_{k}\|^{2}_{\rho}}<+\infty.}
In a similar manner, one gets the previous second inequality for the upper bound of
\mathbb{E}_{Z}\Big[\sum_{j\geq k}\lambda_{j}(B_{n})\Big].

∎

We should mention that many examples of Mercer’s kernels used by KRR algorithms have sets of eigenvalues
\lambda_{m}
that decay exponentially to zero. That is
\lambda_{m}\lesssim e^{-bm},
for some
b>0.
The magnitudes
\|\varphi_{m}\|_{\infty}
of their associated set of eigenfunctions
\varphi_{m}
are bounded by a quantity of
O(m^{a/2}),
for some
a\geq 0.
For the unknown probability measure
d\rho
which we assume to be absolutely continuous with respect to the original measure
dP,
and according to (
21
), we have
for any
m\geq 0,

\lambda_{m+1,\rho}\lesssim\sum_{k\geq m+1}k^{a}e^{-bk}\lesssim\int_{m}^{\infty}t^{a}e^{-bt}\,dx=\frac{1}{b^{a+1}}\int_{bm}^{\infty}u^{a}e^{-u}\,du=\frac{1}{b^{a+1}}\Gamma(a+1,bm).

(24)

Here,
\Gamma(a+1,bm)
is the upper incomplete Gamma function. It is well known that for
x>a-1,
we have
{\displaystyle\Gamma(a,x)\leq\frac{x^{a}e^{-x}}{x+1-a}.}
Consequently, the eigenvalues of such an integral operator with a given Mercer’s kernel and an eventually unknwon probability measure
d\rho,
have the following upper bounds,

\lambda_{m+1,\rho}\lesssim\frac{(bm)^{a+1}e^{-bm}}{bm-a}\lesssim m^{a}e^{-bm}.

Moreover, in this case for a Gram matrix
B_{n},
given by (
20
) and according to (
22
), the expected value of its trace tail is bounded by

\mathbb{E}_{Z}\Big[\sum_{j\geq m}\lambda_{j}(B_{n})\Big]\lesssim m^{a}e^{-bm}.

(25)

Next, for a positive integer
N\leq m,
we extend the decay rate estimate (
22
) for the eigenvalues of the Gram matrix
B_{n}
to the case of the singular values of
A_{N},
the principal rectangular
m\times N
sub-matrix of
B_{n}.
That is

A_{N}=\frac{1}{n}\Big[\mathbb{K}(X_{i},X_{j})\Big]_{1\leq i\leq n,\,1\leq j\leq N}.

(26)

We recall that the
X_{i}
are i.i.d. random samples following an unknown probability law with pdf
\rho(\cdot).
For this purpose, we use the following interlacing inequalities for the singular values of sub-matrices, given in
[
23
]
.

Interlacing inequalities of singular values

[
23
]
:
Let
B
be an
m\times n
matrix with singular values

\sigma_{1}(B)\geq\sigma_{2}(B)\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{\min(m,n)}(B).

Let
D
be a
p\times q
submatrix of
B,
with singular values

\mu_{1}(D)\geq\mu_{2}(D)\geq\cdots\geq\mu_{\min(p,q)}(D).

Then, we have

\sigma_{i}(B)\geq\mu_{i}(D)\geq\sigma_{i+(m-p)+(n-q)}(B),\qquad\forall\,1\leq i\leq\min(p+q-m,p+q-n).

(27)

By using the first inequality of (
27
) and the inequalities (
22
) of Theorem 2, one gets the following Corollary.

Corollary 1
.

Let
B_{n}
and
A_{N}
be the Gram matrix and its principal sub-matrix, given by (
20
) and (
26
), respectively. Under the hypotheses of Theorem 2 and the previous notation, if
{\displaystyle\kappa_{1}=\sup_{x\in\boldsymbol{X}}\mathbb{K}(x,x),}
then for any
1\leq m\leq N,
we have

\mathcal{T}_{M}=\mathbb{E}_{Z}\Big[\sum_{j\geq m}\mu_{j}(A_{N})\Big]\lesssim\lambda_{m}\|\varphi_{m}\|^{2}_{\rho}

(28)

and

\mathbb{E}_{Z}\Big[\sum_{j\geq m}\mu_{j}^{2}(A_{N})\Big]\lesssim\frac{\kappa_{1}}{m}\lambda_{m}\|\varphi_{m}\|^{2}_{\rho}.

(29)

Proof:
By using the interlacing property (
27
) and since the Gram matrix
B_{n}
is positive semi-definite, that is
\sigma_{i}(B_{n})=\lambda_{i}(B_{n})
, then we have

\mu_{i}(A_{N})\leq\lambda_{i}(B_{n}),\qquad\forall\,1\leq i\leq N.

(30)

By using the previous inequality and (
22
), one gets

\mathbb{E}_{Z}\Big[\sum_{j\geq m}\lambda_{j}(B_{n})\Big]\leq\sum_{j\geq m}\lambda_{j,\rho}\lesssim\lambda_{m}\|\varphi_{m}\|^{2}_{\rho}.

To check (
29
), we note that
since
{\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(B_{n})\geq\lambda_{2}(B_{n})\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}(B_{n})\geq 0}
and since

{\bf Tr}(B_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}(B_{n})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{K}(Z_{i},Z_{i})\leq\kappa_{1},

then
m\cdot\lambda_{m}(B_{n})\leq\kappa_{1}.
Hence, by using (
30
), one gets
{\displaystyle\mu_{m}(A_{N})\leq\frac{\kappa_{1}}{m}}
. Consequently, one gets

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Z}\Big[\sum_{j\geq m}\mu_{j}^{2}(A_{N})\Big]\leq\mathbb{E}_{Z}\Big[\lambda_{m}(B_{n})\sum_{j\geq m}\mu_{j}(A_{N})\Big]\leq\frac{\kappa_{1}}{m}\mathbb{E}_{Z}\Big[\sum_{j\geq m}\lambda_{j}(B_{n})\Big]\lesssim\frac{\kappa_{1}}{m}\lambda_{m}\|\varphi_{m}\|^{2}_{\rho}.

4
Empirical risk error analysis and convergence rate of the TKRR estimator

In this paragraph, we study the empirical risk error of our TKRR estimator
\widehat{f}_{N,\lambda},
given by (
19
)
and under the usual assumption that
the true regression function
f^{*}
belongs to
\mathcal{H},
the RKHS associated with the positive definite Mercer’s kernel
\mathbb{K}(\cdot,\cdot).
Recall that this empirical risk error is given by

\mathcal{R}(\widehat{f}_{N,\lambda})=\mathbb{E}\Big(\big\|\big(\widehat{f}_{N,\lambda}(X_{i})-f^{*}(X_{i})\big)_{i}\big\|_{n}^{2}\Big)=\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\widehat{f}_{N,\lambda}(X_{i})-f^{*}(X_{i})\big)^{2}\Big).

(31)

Here,
\mathbb{E}=\mathbb{E}_{X\times\varepsilon}=\mathbb{E}_{X}\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon},
the product of the expectations with respect to the independent random variables
X_{i}
and
\varepsilon_{i},
respectively. Recall that the given i.i.d. random sampling points
X_{i}
are drawn according to
\rho(\cdot).
We consider the following finite dimensional subspace of the RKHS
\mathcal{H},
given by

\mathcal{H}_{N}=\mbox{Span}\Big\{\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{K}(X_{j},\cdot),\,1\leq j\leq N\Big\},\qquad\mathcal{F}_{N}=\mbox{Span}\Big\{\varphi_{j}(\cdot),\,1\leq j\leq N\Big\}.

(32)

We assume that
\mathcal{H}
is a subspace of
L^{2}(\boldsymbol{X},dP),

and the true regression function
f^{*}\in\mathcal{H}.
That is

{\displaystyle f^{*}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j}(f^{*})\varphi_{j},\,\,a_{j}(f^{*})=<f^{*},\varphi_{j}>_{\mathcal{H}}.}
Also, we consider the following orthogonal projection of
f^{*}
over the subspaces
\mathcal{H}_{N}
and
\mathcal{F}_{N},
given by

\widetilde{\pi}_{N}f^{*}(\cdot)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\widetilde{\omega}_{j}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{K}(X_{j},\cdot),\qquad\pi_{N}f^{*}(\cdot)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}(f^{*})\varphi_{j}(\cdot).

(33)

Recall that
\widetilde{\pi}_{N}f^{*}
and
\pi_{N}f^{*}
are the best approximations of
f^{*}
in the
L^{2}(dP)-
norm, by elements from
\mathcal{H}_{N}
and
\mathcal{F}_{N},
respectively.
In the sequel, we use the following two technical hypothesis:

{\bf H_{2}:}
The random projection matrix
{\displaystyle\Big[\varphi_{j}(X_{i})\Big]_{1\leq i\leq n,1\leq j\leq N}}
has full rank.

Next, we consider the truncated projection kernel,

\mathbb{K}_{N}(x,y)=\sum_{m=1}^{N}\lambda_{m}\varphi_{m}(x)\varphi_{m}(y),\quad x,y\in\boldsymbol{X}.

(34)

It is easy to see that under condition
\bf H_{2},
the projection
\pi_{N}f^{*}
has the following second equivalent expansion form

\pi_{N}f^{*}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\omega^{\prime}_{j}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{K}_{N}(X_{j},x),\quad x\in\boldsymbol{X}.

(35)

Finally, to study the empirical risk error of our TKRR estimator, we also need the following hypothesis on the coefficients of the projections
\widetilde{\pi}_{N}f^{*}
and
\pi_{N}f^{*},
given by (
33
) and
(
35
):

{\bf H_{3}:}

{\displaystyle\max\big(\big\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\big\|_{n}^{2},\big\|{\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime}}\big\|_{n}^{2}\big)\leq 1,\quad}
where
{\displaystyle\quad\big\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\big\|_{n}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\widetilde{\omega}_{j}^{2},\quad\big\|{\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime}}\big\|_{n}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\omega^{\prime}}_{j}^{2}.}

Note that the previous hypothesis can be relaxed by substituting the upper bound
1
by any constant
C>0.

The following technical lemma will be needed in the proof of our TKRR empirical risk error. It shows that
\widetilde{\pi}_{N}f^{*}
is well approximated by
\pi_{N}f^{*},
whenever
f^{*}\in\mathcal{H}.

Lemma 1
.

Under the previous notation and hypotheses
{\bf H_{1}},\,{\bf H_{2}}
and
{\bf H_{3}},
let
f\in\mathcal{H},
then we have

\mathbb{E}\Big(\big\|\big((\pi_{N}f-\widetilde{\pi}_{N}f)(X_{i})\big)_{i}\big\|^{2}_{n}\Big)\lesssim\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k\geq N+1}\lambda_{k}\|\varphi_{k}\|^{2}_{\infty}+\lambda_{N+1}\|f\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}.

(36)

Proof:
It is well known that if
f\in\mathcal{H},
then we have

\displaystyle\|f-\pi_{N}f\|^{2}_{P}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq N+1}|a_{k}(f)|^{2}=\sum_{k\geq N+1}\lambda_{k}\frac{|a_{k}(f)|^{2}}{\lambda_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lambda_{N+1}\sum_{k\geq N+1}\frac{|a_{k}(f)|^{2}}{\lambda_{k}}\leq\lambda_{N+1}\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}.

Here,
{\displaystyle a_{k}(f)=<f,\varphi_{k}>_{P},}
where
<\cdot,\cdot>_{P}
is the usual inner product of
L^{2}(\boldsymbol{X},dP).
Also, by using
\bf H_{1},
we have for any
g\in\mathcal{H},

\|g\|^{2}_{\rho}=\int_{\boldsymbol{X}}|g(x)|^{2}\,d\rho(x)\lesssim\int_{\boldsymbol{X}}|g(x)|^{2}\,dP(x)=\|g\|^{2}_{L^{2}(dP)}.

(37)

On the other hand, for
1\leq i\leq n,
we have

\mathbb{E}\Big(\big((\pi_{N}f-\widetilde{\pi}_{N}f)(X_{i})\big)^{2}\Big)=\int_{\boldsymbol{X}}|\widetilde{\pi}_{N}(f)(x)-\pi_{N}f(x)|^{2}\,d\rho(x)\lesssim\|\widetilde{\pi}_{N}(f)-\pi_{N}(f)\|_{L^{2}(dP)}^{2}.

Moreover, since
\widetilde{\pi}_{N}
is a projection operator, then
we have

\big\|\widetilde{\pi}_{N}\big(f-\pi_{N}f\big)\big\|_{L^{2}(dP)}\leq\|f-\pi_{N}f\|_{L^{2}(dP)}\leq\sqrt{\lambda_{N+1}}\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}.

(38)

Also, since
\widetilde{\pi}_{N}(\pi_{N}f)
is the best approximation of
\pi_{N}f
by elements from the subspace
\mathcal{H}_{N}
and since
the non truncated kernel expansion

{\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\omega^{\prime}_{j}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{K}(X_{j},x)\in\mathcal{H}_{N},}
then we have

\displaystyle\big\|\widetilde{\pi}_{N}(\pi_{N}f)-\pi_{N}f\big\|_{L^{2}(dP)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\Big\|\sum_{j=1}^{N}\omega^{\prime}_{j}\sum_{k\geq N+1}\lambda_{k}\varphi_{k}(X_{j})\varphi_{k}(\cdot)\Big\|_{L^{2}(dP)}^{2}\leq\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k\geq N+1}\lambda_{k}\|\varphi_{k}\|^{2}_{\infty}\Big)\|\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime}\|_{n}^{2},

Finally, since

\|\widetilde{\pi}_{N}f-\pi_{N}f\|_{L^{2}(dP)}\leq\|\widetilde{\pi}_{N}(f-\pi_{N}f)\|_{L^{2}(dP)}+\|\widetilde{\pi}_{N}(\pi_{N}f)-\pi_{N}f\|_{L^{2}(dP)},

then, by using the previous inequalities, one gets the desired result
(
36
).

The following theorem provides us with an upper bound for the empirical risk error of our TKRR estimator.

Theorem 2
.

Let
n,N>0
be two positive integers and
\lambda>0.
Under the previous notation, and hypotheses
{\bf H_{1}},\,{\bf H_{2}}
and
{\bf H_{3}},
we have for
f^{*}\in\mathcal{H},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big(\big\|\big(\widehat{f}_{N,\lambda}-f^{*}\big)(X_{i})\big\|_{n}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{2}+2\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{X}\Big[\Big(\frac{\mu_{j}^{2}}{\mu_{j}^{2}+\lambda}\Big)^{2}\Big]

(39)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\,C_{1}\,\Big(\lambda_{N+1}\|f^{*}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k\geq N+1}\lambda_{k}\|\varphi_{k}\|^{2}_{\infty}\Big).

Here,
C_{1}>0
is a uniform positive constant.

Proof :
Let
\widetilde{\pi}_{N}
and
\pi_{N}
be the projection operators defined by (
33
), then we have

f^{*}(X_{j})=\widetilde{\pi}_{N}f^{*}(X_{j})+\Big(f^{*}(X_{j})-\pi_{N}f^{*}(X_{j})\Big)+\Big(\pi_{N}f^{*}(X_{j})-\widetilde{\pi}_{N}f^{*}(X_{j})\Big),\quad 1\leq j\leq n.

(40)

Hence, from (
19
), we have

\Big[\widehat{f}_{N,\lambda}(X_{i})\Big]^{T}_{1\leq i\leq n}=A_{N}\big(A_{N}^{*}A_{N}+\lambda I_{N}\big)^{-1}A_{N}^{*}\Big[f^{*}(X_{j})+\varepsilon_{j}\Big]_{1\leq j\leq n}^{T}.

(41)

By combining the previous two identities, one gets with
{\displaystyle F_{N}=\big(\widetilde{\pi}_{N}f^{*}(X_{i})\big)_{1\leq i\leq n},}

\displaystyle\Big[\widehat{f}_{N,\lambda}(X_{i})\Big]^{T}_{1\leq i\leq n}=A_{N}\big(A_{N}^{*}A_{N}+\lambda I_{N}\big)^{-1}A_{N}^{*}\Big[\big(\widetilde{\pi}_{N}f^{*}(X_{j})+\varepsilon_{j})+

(42)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\big(f^{*}(X_{j})-\pi_{N}f^{*}(X_{j})\big)+\big(\pi_{N}f^{*}(X_{j})-\widetilde{\pi}_{N}f^{*}(X_{j})\big)\Big]_{1\leq j\leq n}^{T}.

Let
\|A\|_{2}
denote the
2-
norm of a matrix
A.
By using (
40
) and (
42
), together with the fact that
\big\|A_{N}\big(A_{N}^{*}A_{N}+\lambda I_{N}\big)^{-1}A_{N}^{*}\big\|_{2}\leq 1,
one gets

\displaystyle\big\|\big(\widehat{f}_{N,\lambda}(X_{i})-f^{*}(X_{i})\big)_{i}\big\|_{n}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\big\|A_{N}(K_{N}^{2}+\lambda I_{N})^{-1}A_{N}^{*}(F_{N}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})^{T}-F_{N}^{T}\|_{n}

(43)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle 2\Big(\big\|\big(f^{*}(X_{i})-\pi_{N}f(X_{i})\big)_{i}\big\|_{n}+\big\|\big(\pi_{N}f^{*}(X_{i})-\widetilde{\pi}_{N}f(X_{i})\big)_{i}\big\|_{n}\Big).

Since
f^{*}\in\mathcal{H},
then as in the proof of lemma 1, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\big\|\big(f^{*}(X_{i})-\pi_{N}f(X_{i})\big)_{i}\big\|^{2}_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\boldsymbol{X}}|f^{*}(x)-\pi_{N}f^{*}(x)|^{2}d\rho(x)

(44)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\int_{\boldsymbol{X}}|f^{*}(x)-\pi_{N}f^{*}(x)|^{2}dP(x)=\|f^{*}-\pi_{N}f^{*}\|_{L^{2}(dP)}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\lambda_{N+1}\|f^{*}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}.

Moreover, from Lemma 1, we have

\mathbb{E}\Big(\big\|\big((\pi_{N}f^{*}-\widetilde{\pi}_{N}f^{*})(X_{i})\big)_{i}\big\|^{2}_{n}\Big)\lesssim\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k\geq N+1}\lambda_{k}\|\varphi_{k}\|^{2}_{\infty}+\lambda_{N+1}\|f^{*}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}.

(45)

Next, we estimate the following main quantity in the empirical risk error, given by

R_{n,N}=\mathbb{E}\left(\big\|\big(A_{N}\big(A_{N}^{*}A_{N}+\lambda I_{N}\big)^{-1}A_{N}^{*}\big[\big(\widetilde{\pi}_{N}f^{*}(X_{j})+\varepsilon_{j}\big)_{j}\big]-\widetilde{\pi}_{N}f^{*}(X_{i})\big)_{i}\big\|^{2}_{n}\right).

By using some techniques similar to those used in the proof of the main theorem of
[
2
]
, but in the present random sampling setting, one has the following singular values decomposition of
A_{N}=U\Sigma_{n,N}V^{*}
where
U,V
are orthogonal matrices and
\Sigma_{n,N}
is an
n\times N
rectangular diagonal matrix. Consequently, we have

K_{N}^{2}=A_{N}^{*}A_{N}=\big(U\Sigma_{n,N}V^{*}\big)^{*}\big(U\Sigma_{n,N}V^{*}\big)=V\Sigma_{n,N}^{*}\Sigma_{n,N}V^{*}.

Let
D_{N}=\Sigma_{n,N}^{*}\Sigma_{n,N}.
Then,

(K_{N}^{2}+\lambda I_{N})^{-1}=\big(V(D_{N}+\lambda I_{N})\big)V^{*})^{-1}=V(D_{N}+\lambda I_{N})^{-1}V^{*}

and

\displaystyle A_{N}(K_{N}^{2}+\lambda I_{N})^{-1}A_{N}^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle U\Sigma_{n,N}V^{*}V(D_{N}+\lambda I_{N})^{-1}V^{*}V\Sigma_{n,N}^{*}U^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle U\Sigma_{n,N}(D_{N}+\lambda I_{N})^{-1}\Sigma_{n,N}^{*}U^{*}.

So that

\displaystyle\big\|A_{N}(K_{N}^{2}+\lambda I_{N})^{-1}A_{N}^{*}(F_{N}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})^{T}-F_{N}^{T}\|_{n}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|U\Sigma_{n,N}(D_{N}+\lambda I_{N})^{-1}\Sigma_{n,N}^{*}U^{*}F_{N}^{T}-F_{N}^{T}\|_{n}

(46)

\displaystyle+\|U\Sigma_{n,N}(D_{N}+\lambda I_{N})^{-1}\Sigma_{n,N}^{*}U^{*}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{T}\|_{n}.

Next let
\|\cdot\|_{\ell_{2},n}
and
\|\cdot\|_{2}
denote the usual
2-
norm of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a rectangular matrix, respectively. In particular, for an
n\times N
matrix
A
,
\|A\|_{2}=\sigma_{1}(A),
the largest singular value of
A.
Also, since

{\displaystyle\widetilde{\pi}_{N}f^{*}(X_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\widetilde{\omega}_{j}\,\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{K}(X_{i},X_{j}),\qquad 1\leq i\leq n,}

then we have

F_{N}^{T}=\Big(\widetilde{\pi}_{N}f^{*}(X_{i})\Big)^{T}_{1\leq i\leq n}=A_{N}\,\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}=U\Sigma_{n,N}V^{*}\,\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\qquad\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}=(\widetilde{\omega}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\omega}_{N})^{T}.

Moreover, since the
2-
norm is invariant under multiplication
by an orthogonal matrix, one gets

\displaystyle\big\|U\Sigma_{n,N}(D_{N}+\lambda I_{N})^{-1}\Sigma_{n,N}^{*}U^{*}F_{N}^{T}-F_{N}^{T}\big\|_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\big\|U\big(\Sigma_{n,N}(D_{N}+\lambda I_{N})^{-1}\Sigma_{n,N}^{*}-I_{n}\big)\Sigma_{n,N}V^{*}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\big\|_{\ell_{2},n}^{2}

(47)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\big\|\big(\Sigma_{n,N}(D_{N}+\lambda I_{N})^{-1}\Sigma_{n,N}^{*}-I_{n}\big)\Sigma_{n,N}V^{*}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\big\|_{\ell_{2},n}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\big\|\big(\Sigma_{n,N}(D_{N}+\lambda I_{N})^{-1}\Sigma_{n,N}^{*}-I_{n}\big)\Sigma_{n,N}\big\|_{2}^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\|V^{*}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\|_{\ell_{2},n}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\big\|\big(\Sigma_{n,N}(D_{N}+\lambda I_{N})^{-1}\Sigma_{n,N}^{*}-I_{n}\big)\Sigma_{n,N}\big\|_{2}^{2}\cdot\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\|_{n}^{2}.

Let
{\displaystyle\phi_{n}^{\lambda}=\Sigma_{n,N}(D_{N}+\lambda I_{N})^{-1}\Sigma_{n,N}^{*}-I_{n},}

then we have

\big\|\big(\Sigma_{n,N}(D_{N}+\lambda I_{N})^{-1}\Sigma_{n,N}^{*}-I_{n}\big)\Sigma_{n,N}\big\|_{2}^{2}=\big|\sigma_{1}\big(\phi_{n}^{\lambda}\Sigma_{n,N}\big)\big|^{2}.

(48)

On the other hand, we have
{\displaystyle\Sigma_{n,N}=\big[s_{i,j}\big]_{\underset{1\leq j\leq N}{1\leq i\leq n}},\quad s_{i,j}=\mu_{j}\delta_{i,j},}
where the
\mu_{j}=\sqrt{\lambda_{j}(A_{N}^{*}A_{N})}
are the singular values of
A_{N}.
Consequently, we have

\phi_{n,N}^{\lambda}\Sigma_{n,N}=\big[\gamma_{i,j}\big]_{\underset{1\leq j\leq N}{1\leq i\leq n}},\quad\gamma_{i,j}=\Big(\frac{{\mu}_{j}^{2}}{{\mu}_{j}^{2}+\lambda}-1\Big)\mu_{j}\delta_{i,j}=\frac{-\lambda\mu_{j}}{{\mu}_{j}^{2}+\lambda}\delta_{i,j}.

Hence,

{\displaystyle|\sigma_{1}\big(\phi_{n}^{\lambda}\Sigma_{n,N}\big)|^{2}=\underset{1\leq j\leq N}{\max}\big(\frac{\lambda}{{\mu}_{j}^{2}+\lambda}\big)^{2}\mu_{j}^{2}.}

Since
{\displaystyle\underset{x\geq 0}{\sup}\frac{\lambda x}{x^{2}+\lambda}=\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{2},}
then
{\displaystyle\underset{1\leq j\leq N}{\max}\Big(\frac{\lambda}{{\mu}_{j}^{2}+\lambda}\Big)^{2}\mu_{j}^{2}=\frac{\lambda}{4}.}
That is

\big|\sigma_{1}\big(\phi_{n}^{\lambda}\Sigma_{n,N}\big)\big|^{2}\leq\frac{\lambda}{4}.

(49)

Hence, by using (
47
)–(
49
), together with hypothesis

{\bf H_{3}},
one gets

\mathbb{E}\left(\big\|U\Sigma_{n,N}(D_{N}+\lambda I_{N})^{-1}\Sigma_{n,N}^{*}U^{*}F_{N}^{T}-F_{N}^{T}\big\|_{n}^{2}\right)\leq\frac{\lambda}{4}.

(50)

Next, to bound the expectation of the second term of the right hand-side
of (
46
), we first note that if
B_{n}=\big[b_{ij}\big]_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
is an
n\times n
matrix with real coefficients, then its Frobenius norm
\|B_{n}\|_{F}
is given by

\|B_{n}\|^{2}_{F}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(b_{ij})^{2}=\mbox{{\bf Tr}}(B_{n}^{*}B_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}^{2}(B_{n}),

where, the
\sigma_{i}(B_{n})
are the singular values of
B_{n}.
Moreover, the Frobenius norm is invariant under left or right multiplication by orthogonal matrices. We consider the special choice of

B_{n}=U\Sigma_{n,N}(D_{N}+\lambda I_{N})^{-1}\Sigma_{n,N}^{*}U^{*}.

(51)

In this case,
B_{n}
has at most rank
N
and

\|B_{n}\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}b^{2}_{ij}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Big(\frac{\mu_{j}^{2}}{\mu_{j}^{2}+\lambda}\Big)^{2}.

Also, by using the fact that the
\varepsilon_{i}
are i.i.d. and independent from the
X_{i}
with

\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon_{i})=0
and
\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon^{2}_{i})=\sigma^{2},

one can easily check that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}_{X\times\varepsilon}\left(\big\|B_{n}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{T}\big\|^{2}_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}_{X}\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{ij}\varepsilon_{j}\right)^{2}

(52)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}b^{2}_{ij}\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon_{j}^{2})\right]=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Big(\frac{\mu_{j}^{2}}{\mu_{j}^{2}+\lambda}\Big)^{2}\right].

By squaring both sides of (
43
) and using the inequality
{(a+b)^{2}\leq 2(a^{2}+b^{2})},
for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
and then taking the expectations, one gets

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\big\|\big(\widetilde{f}_{N,\lambda}(X_{i})-f^{*}(X_{i})\big)_{i}\big\|^{2}_{n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\,\mathbb{E}\left(\big\|A_{N}(K_{N}^{2}+\lambda I_{N})^{-1}A_{N}^{*}F_{N}^{T}-F_{N}^{T}\|_{n}^{2}\right)

(53)

\displaystyle+2\,\mathbb{E}\left(\big\|A_{N}(K_{N}^{2}+\lambda I_{N})^{-1}A_{N}^{*}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{T}\|_{n}^{2}\right)+4\,\mathbb{E}\left(\big\|\big(f^{*}(X_{i})-\pi_{N}f^{*}(X_{i})\big)_{i}\big\|^{2}_{n}\right)

\displaystyle+4\,\mathbb{E}\left(\big\|\big(\pi_{N}f^{*}(X_{i})-\widetilde{\pi}_{N}f^{*}(X_{i})\big)_{i}\big\|^{2}_{n}\right).

Finally, by combining the previous inequality with (
44
), (
45
), (
46
), (
50
) and (
52
), one gets the desired result (
39
).

Remark 1
.

Although, the risk error (
39
) of our TKRR estimator has some similarities with the risk error of the RFF estimator, given for example in
[
4
]
, there is a fundamental difference between these two risks and their proofs. More precisely, the risk error (
39
) involves the eigenvalues of the integral operator
T_{\mathbb{K}},
as well as a weighted tail trace of this latter. Moreover, it works for a fairly general random kernel matrix with unknown random sampling set. The empirical risk error of the RFF estimator involves the eigenvalues of the kernel matrix
{\bf K}
. A similar remark is also valid for the empirical risk error of the spectrally truncated KRR estimator developed by
[
2
]
.

Remark 2
.

The condition
f^{*}\in\mathcal{H}
can be relaxed. We may assume that the RKHS
\mathcal{H}
is a closed subspace of
L^{2}(\boldsymbol{X},dP)
and that the regression function
f^{*}
belongs to another subspace
\mathcal{F}
of
L^{2}(\boldsymbol{X},dP).
Let
\pi_{\mathcal{H}}
be the orthogonal projection operator over
\mathcal{H}
and let
g^{*}=\pi_{\mathcal{H}}f^{*}\in\mathcal{H}.
Then by writing
f^{*}=g^{*}+(f^{*}-g^{*})
and using the same proof of the previous theorem, one gets
the following empirical risk error for
f^{*}\in\mathcal{F},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big(\big\|\big(\widehat{f}_{N,\lambda}-f^{*}\big)(X_{i})\big\|_{n}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{2}+2\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{X}\Big[\Big(\frac{\mu_{j}^{2}}{\mu_{j}^{2}+\lambda}\Big)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle C_{1}\,\Big(\lambda_{N+1}\|\pi_{H}f^{*}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k\geq N+1}\lambda_{k}\|\varphi_{k}\|^{2}_{\infty}+\|f^{*}-\pi_{\mathcal{H}}f^{*}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(dP)}\Big).

The previous general setting empirical risk error depends on the extra projection error
\|f^{*}-\pi_{\mathcal{H}}f^{*}\|_{L^{2}(dP)},
that has to be estimated.

Next, we use the risk error (
39
) to study the two main issues related to our proposed TKRR: The choice of the convenient value of the regularization parameter
\lambda,
and the convergence rate of our TKRR estimator.
Note that from (
39
), the previous two issues are essentially based on the behavior of the sequence of the eigenvalues

(\lambda_{k})_{k\geq 1}.
In particular, the decay rate of the

\lambda_{k}
and the smallest rank
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}

(depending only on the kernel
\mathbb{K}
), from which this decay rate holds true, play an important role in deriving the optimal convergence rate of our TKRR estimator. For this purpose, we consider the two frequently encountered types of the spectra:
The spectra with a polynomial decay rate and those with an exponential decay rate. More precisely, we assume that for some real
a\geq 0,
the eigenfunctions
\varphi_{k}
of the integral operator
T_{\mathbb{K}}
satisfy the following uniform bound

\|\varphi_{k}\|_{\infty}\lesssim k^{a/2},\qquad k\geq 1.

(54)

Note that for some
positive real numbers
s>a\geq 0
and
b>0,
the exponential and polynomial decay rates of the eigenvalues of
T_{\mathbb{K}},
are given by

\lambda_{k}\lesssim e^{-bk},\quad\forall\,k\geq N_{b}

(55)

and

\lambda_{k}\lesssim k^{-2s},\quad\forall\,k\geq N_{s},

(56)

respectively. Here,
N_{b}
and
N_{s}
are the ranks from which these decay rates hold true. In particular, since in (
39
), we have
{\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\Big(\frac{\mu_{j}^{2}}{\mu_{j}^{2}+\lambda}\Big)^{2}\right]\leq\sigma^{2}\frac{N}{n},}
then by using (
54
) and (
55
) for the exponential decay rate case, the inequality
(
39
) is rewritten as follows

\mathbb{E}\Big(\big\|\big(\widehat{f}_{N,\lambda}-f^{*}\big)(X_{i})\big\|_{n}^{2}\Big)\leq\frac{\lambda}{2}+2\sigma^{2}\frac{N}{n}+C_{2}\,N^{\eta}\lambda_{N},\quad\eta=\max(0,a-1).

(57)

Consequently, the optimal values of the parameters
\lambda,N
in terms of the dataset size
n,
are given by

\lambda=O\Big(\sigma^{2}\frac{N}{n}\Big)=O\Big(N^{\eta}e^{-bN}\Big),\quad N\geq N_{b}.

Straightforward computations show that this is the case when

N=\max\Big(N_{b},\frac{1}{b}\log\left(\frac{n}{\sigma^{2}}\right)\Big),\quad\lambda=O\left(\sigma^{2}\min\Big(\frac{N_{b}}{n},\frac{1}{b\,n}\log\big(\frac{n}{\sigma^{2}}\big)\Big)\right).

(58)

In a similar manner, when the spectrum of
T_{\mathbb{K}}
has a polynomial decay rate and by using (
56
), the optimal values of the parameters
N,\lambda
are given by

N=\max\left(N_{s},\Big(\frac{n}{\sigma^{2}}\Big)^{1/(2s+\gamma)}\Big)\right),\quad\lambda=O\left(\min\Big(\frac{N_{s}}{n},\Big(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\Big)^{1-1/(2s+\gamma)}\right),\quad\gamma=\min(1,2-a).

(59)

We have just proved the following proposition that provides us with the optimal convergence rate of our TKRR estimator when
f^{*}\in\mathcal{H}
and in the presence of an exponential or a polynomial decay rate of the eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}.

Proposition 1
.

Under the hypotheses of Theorem 2, the optimal convergence rates of the TKRR estimator
\widehat{f}_{N,\lambda}
under the assumption
(
55
) is given by

\mathbb{E}\Big(\big\|\big(\widehat{f}_{N,\lambda}-f^{*}\big)(X_{i})\big\|_{n}^{2}\Big)=O\left(\sigma^{2}\max\Big(\frac{N_{b}}{n},\frac{1}{b\,n}\log\big(\frac{n}{\sigma^{2}}\big)\Big)\right),\quad N\geq N_{b}.

(60)

Moreover, under the assumption (
56
), we have

\mathbb{E}\Big(\big\|\big(\widehat{f}_{N,\lambda}-f^{*}\big)(X_{i})\big\|_{n}^{2}\Big)=O\left(\max\Big(\sigma^{2}\frac{N_{s}}{n},\Big(\frac{n}{\sigma^{2}}\Big)^{-\frac{(2s+\gamma-1)}{(2s+\gamma)}}\Big)\right),\quad\gamma=\min(1,2-a),\quad N\geq N_{s}.

(61)

Remark 3
.

By using the concept of the uniform degrees of freedom at level
\epsilon>0
of a positive and compact integral operator
T_{\mathbb{K}},
one may generalize and improve the result (
60
) of the previous proposition. Then, this uniform degrees of freedom is denoted by
d_{\infty}(T_{\mathbb{K}},\epsilon)
and defined by

d_{\infty}(T_{\mathbb{K}},\epsilon)=\min\{k\in\mathbb{N};\,\lambda_{k}\leq\epsilon\}=N_{T_{\mathbb{K}}}(\epsilon)=\#\{\lambda_{j};\,\lambda_{j}>\epsilon\}+1.

(62)

In this case, a refined truncation order
N,
appearing in the right hand-side of (
57
) is simply given by

N=N(\epsilon_{n})=d_{\infty}(T_{\mathbb{K}},\epsilon_{n})\quad\mbox{with}\quad\epsilon_{n}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\big(d_{\infty}(T_{\mathbb{K}},\epsilon_{n})\big)^{1-\eta},\quad\eta=\max(0,a-1).

(63)

Besides its good convergence properties, given by the previous proposition, our TKRR estimator has the desirable property of requiring a low computational load, compared with the full KRR estimator.

Remark 4
.

From (
18
) and (
19
), the TKRR estimator has time complexity of
O(nN^{2}),
whereas the full KRR estimator requires time complexity of
O(n^{3}).
Since, in general
N\ll n,
then the TKRR is much faster than the classical KRR. On the other hand, our TKRR has the same time complexity as the spectrally truncated KRR, given in
[
2
]
. Nonetheless our TKRR does not require any SVD decomposition and handles random sampling dataset drawn from a fairly general unknown multivariate probability law. Moreover, the proposed TKRR is competitive with the combined sketching-KRR estimator, given in
[
25
]
. This last estimator requires a time complexity of
O(n^{2}N^{2})
for dense sketches and an
O(n^{2}\log(N)),
for Fourier and Hadamard sketches.

An other important feature of our TKRR is its optimal convergence rate that coincides with the optimal convergence rate of the full KRR estimator. More precisely, the following proposition shows that the full KRR estimator has the same convergence rates (
60
) and (
61
). Note that the full KRR estimator
\widehat{f}_{n}^{\lambda}
is given by (
6
)–(
8
).

Proposition 2
.

Under the hypotheses of Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 and the assumptions (
55
) and (
56
), the optimal convergence rate of the full KRR estimator
\widehat{f}_{n}^{\lambda}
coincide with the convergence rates (
60
) and (
61
), respectively.

Proof:
To derive the empirical risk error of the full KRR estimator (
6
)–(
8
), one might use a simple adaptation of the proof of Theorem 2. More precisely, instead of considering the pseudo-inverse of the regularized truncated matrix
A_{N},
given by (
42
), it suffices to consider the inverse of the regularized full Gram matrix

{\displaystyle A_{n}+\lambda I_{n}},
given by (
7
). Also, in this case and thanks to the representer theorem and the hypothesis that
f^{*}\in\mathcal{H}
and as it is done in
[
2
]
, we may assume that
f^{*}(X_{i})=\widetilde{\pi}_{n}f^{*}(X_{i})
for
1\leq i\leq n.

In this case, the empirical risk error of
\widehat{f}_{n}^{\lambda}
is given by

\mathbb{E}\Big(\big\|\big(\widehat{f}_{n}^{\lambda}-f^{*}\big)(X_{i})\big\|_{n}^{2}\Big)\leq\frac{\lambda}{2}+2\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{X}\Big[\Big(\frac{\lambda_{j}(A_{n})}{\lambda_{j}(A_{n})+\lambda}\Big)^{2}\Big]

(64)

Since
{\displaystyle 0<\frac{\lambda_{j}(A_{n})}{\lambda_{j}(A_{n})+\lambda}<1,}
then for any integer
1\leq N<n,
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big(\big\|\big(\widehat{f}_{n}^{\lambda}-f^{*}\big)(X_{i})\big\|_{n}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{2}+2\sigma^{2}\frac{N}{n}+2\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\sum_{j=N+1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{X}\Big(\frac{\lambda_{j}(A_{n})}{\lambda_{j}(A_{n})+\lambda}\Big)

(65)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{2}+2\sigma^{2}\frac{N}{n}+2\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n\,\lambda}\sum_{j=N+1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{X}\big(\lambda_{j}(A_{n})\big).

Under the hypothesis that the sequence of the eigenvalues of the integral operator

has an exponential decay, given by (

), one gets for

\mathbb{E}\Big(\big\|\big(\widehat{f}_{n}^{\lambda}-f^{*}\big)(X_{i})\big\|_{n}^{2}\Big)\lesssim\frac{\lambda}{2}+2\sigma^{2}\frac{N}{n}+2\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n\,\lambda}\lambda_{N+1}\|\varphi_{N+1}\|^{2}_{\infty}.

(66)

Consequently, the optimal values of
\lambda
and

satisfy the estimates

\lambda=O\Big(\sigma^{2}\frac{N}{n}\Big)=O\Big(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}N^{\eta}e^{-bN}\Big),\quad\eta=\max(0,a-1),\quad N\geq N_{b}.

(67)

By combining (

In a similar manner, one can easily check that this is also the case under the hypothesis (

5
Examples and numerical simulations

In this section, we first illustrate the results of section 3 and section 4. We consider two examples of the Sinc and Gaussian kernels. These Mercer’s kernels are frequently used in the framework of KRR based NP regression estimators. Then, we give numerical simulations that illustrate the theoretical properties of our proposed TKRR estimator.

5.1
The Sinc and Gaussian kernels examples

5.1.1
Sinc kernel case

It is well known that there is a rich literature concerning the spectral analysis of the Sinc kernel operator, as well as the various
mathematical statistics and signal processing applications related to this kernel, see for example
[
11
]
. This kernel and its associated integral operator are briefly described as follows. Consider a positive real number
c>0
called bandwidth, then the uni-dimensional Sinc-kernel is defined by

\mathbb{K}_{c}(x,y)=\frac{\sin(c(x-y))}{\pi(x-y)},\quad x,y\in I=[-1,1].

(68)

Since
\mathbb{K}_{c}(x,y)=\kappa(x-y),
where
\kappa(\cdot)
is the Fourier transform of the uniform probability measure over
I=[-1,1],
then by Bochner’s theorem,
\mathbb{K}_{c}(\cdot,\cdot)
is a positive definite kernel. It is well known that the associated RKHS is given by the space of bandlimited functions with bandwidth
c.
That is the subspace of functions from
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with Fourier transforms supported in
[-c,c].
The usual probability measure associated with the Sinc-kernel is given by the uniform measure
{\displaystyle d\mu(x)=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}_{[-1,1]}(x)dx.}

We recall that if
\mathcal{Q}_{c}
is the Hilbert-Schmidt operator defined on
L^{2}(I,d\mu)
with kernel
\mathbb{K}_{c}(x,y)
, then we have

\mathcal{Q}_{c}(\varphi_{m,c})(x)=\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\sin(c(x-y))}{\pi(x-y)}\varphi_{m,c}(y)dy=\lambda_{m}(c)\varphi_{m,c}(x),\quad x\in[-1,1].

Here,
\varphi_{m,c},\,\lambda_{m}(c)
is the
m-
th eigenfunction and the associated eigenvalue of
\mathcal{Q}_{c}.
The
\varphi_{m,c}
are known as the prolate spheroidal wave functions (PSWFs). The rich properties of the PSWFs and the behavior of their eigenvalues have been extensively studied in the literature since the pioneer work in the subject starting from the 1960’s by D. Slepian and his co-authors H. Landau and H. Pollak, see
[
11
]
for more details. Since these early works on the PSWFs, it is known that the sequence of the
\lambda_{m}(c)
has an asymptotic super-exponential decay rate. Recently, it has been shown in
[
7
]
, that the optimal asymptotic super-exponential decay rate
of the
\lambda_{m}(c)
is given by

\lambda_{m}(c)\lesssim\exp\Big(-2m\log\Big(\frac{4m}{ec}\Big)\Big),\quad m\gg 1.

Also, in
[
9
]
, the following non-asymptotic decay rate of the
\lambda_{m}(c)
has been given,

\lambda_{m}(c)\leq\exp\Big(-(2m+1)\log\Big(\frac{2(m+1)}{ec}\Big)\Big),\quad m\geq\frac{ec}{2}=N_{c}.

(69)

Consequently, for
d=1
and for the Sinc kernel case, one can take
b=2
and
{\displaystyle N_{b}=\frac{ec}{2}}
in the empirical risk error (
60
) of Proposition 1. That is

\mathbb{E}\Big(\big\|\big(\widehat{f}_{N,\lambda}-f^{*}\big)(X_{i})\big\|_{n}^{2}\Big)=O\left(\sigma^{2}\max\Big(\frac{ec}{2n},\frac{1}{2\,n}\log\big(\frac{n}{\sigma^{2}}\big)\Big)\right),\quad N\geq\frac{ec}{2}.

(70)

Note that for large values of the bandwidth
c,
the decay rank
N_{c}=\frac{ec}{2}
is pessimistic. In fact, it is known from the work of H. Landau, that the decay region of the
\lambda_{n}(c)
starts at
{\displaystyle N_{1,c}=\frac{2c}{\pi}+O\big(\log(c)\big).}
More precisely, it has been shown that for the Sinc kernel, the uniform degrees of freedom at level
\epsilon>0
is given by

d_{\infty}(T_{\mathbb{K}_{c}},\epsilon)=\frac{2c}{\pi}+\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\log\Big(\frac{1-\epsilon}{\epsilon}\Big)\log\Big(\frac{2c}{\pi}\Big)+o\big(\log c\big),\quad c\gg 1.

Also, it has been shown in
[
9
]
that

\sup_{x\in[-1,1]}|\varphi_{m,c}(x)|^{2}\lesssim\big(m(m+1)+c^{2}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\lesssim m,\quad\forall\;m\geq\Big[\frac{2c}{\pi}\Big].

(71)

That is by using (
63
) for the Sinc kernel with
a=1
and the previous uniform degrees of freedom, a refined truncation order is given by

N=N(\epsilon_{n})=\left\lceil\frac{2c}{\pi}+\Big(\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\log\big(\frac{1}{\epsilon_{n}}\big)+1\Big)\log\Big(\frac{2c}{\pi}\Big)\right\rceil,

(72)

where
\epsilon_{n}
is a solution of the equation

\epsilon_{n}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\left(\frac{2c}{\pi}+\Big(\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\log\Big(\frac{1}{\epsilon_{n}}\Big)+1\Big)\log\Big(\frac{2c}{\pi}\Big)\right).

(73)

Here,
\lceil x\rceil
denotes the smallest integer greater or equal to
x.
Finally, for the dimension
d\geq 2,
one may use the tensor product Sinc kernel

\mathbb{K}_{c}^{d}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{K}_{c}(x_{i}-y_{i}),\quad\boldsymbol{x}=\big(x_{i}\big)_{i=1}^{d},\quad\boldsymbol{y}=\big(y_{i}\big)_{i=1}^{d}\in I^{d}.

The
d-
variate eigenfunctions associated to
\mathbb{K}_{c}^{d}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
and their corresponding eigenvalues are given by the tensor products of the
\varphi_{k,c}(x_{i})
and their corresponding eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}(c).
This allows us to derive a decay rate estimate for the eigenvalues, as well as a uniform degrees of freedom associated with a
d-
dimensional tensor product of Sinc kernels. We leave the details for the readers.

5.1.2
The Gaussian kernel case

As for the sinc kernel, we first recall the uni-dimensional Gaussian kernel
{\displaystyle\mathbb{K}_{G}(x,y),\,x,y\in\mathbb{R}}
and some of its associated spectral properties. Then, we show how to extend these
properties to the
d-
dimensional case. It is well known that the Gaussian kernel is one of the most used kernels in mathematical statistics and machine learning applications. This kernel is described as follows. Consider two positive real numbers
\xi,c>0,
then
\mathbb{K}_{G}
is the positive definite convolution kernel with associated usual probability measure
dP_{c}(x),
given by

\mathbb{K}_{G}(x,y)=e^{-\xi(x-y)^{2}},\qquad dP_{c}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2c}}e^{-2cx^{2}}\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R}.

(74)

From
[
29
]
, the eigenvalues of the integral operator
T_{\mathbb{K}_{G}}
are given by

\lambda_{k}=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\xi+c+\sqrt{c^{2}+2c\xi}}}\left(\frac{\xi}{c+\xi+\sqrt{c^{2}+2c\xi}}\right)^{k-1},\quad k\geq 1.

(75)

The associated eigenfunctions
\phi_{k},\,k\geq 1
are the well known dilated Hermite functions, given by

\varphi_{k}(x,\gamma)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\gamma}}\phi_{k}(\sqrt{2\gamma}x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\gamma}}\alpha_{k-1}H_{k-1}\big(\sqrt{2\gamma}x\big)e^{-\gamma x^{2}},\quad\gamma=\sqrt{c^{2}+2c\xi},\quad k\geq 1.

(76)

Here,
{\displaystyle\alpha_{k}=\frac{2^{-k/2}}{\pi^{1/4}\sqrt{k!}}}
and
{\displaystyle H_{k}(x)=(-1)^{k}e^{x^{2}}\frac{d^{k}}{dx^{k}}\Big(e^{-x^{2}}\Big),\,k\geq 0}
are the Hermite polynomials.
Moreover, it is well known that the normalized Hermite functions
\phi_{k}
satisfy the following uniform bound,

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|\phi_{k}(x)|\leq 1,\quad k\geq 1.

(77)

Hence, by using (
75
)–(
77
), as well as the empirical risk error (
57
) with
\eta=0,
one can get the optimal values of the truncation order
N
and the regularization parameter
\lambda>0,
in terms of the parameters
\xi,\,c>0
and according to the rule (
58
) with
{\displaystyle b=\frac{c+\xi+\sqrt{c^{2}+2c\xi}}{\xi}.}

Finally, for the general
d-
dimensional case, the Gaussian kernel and its associated probability measure are given by

\mathbb{K}_{G}^{d}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=e^{-\xi\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|^{2}_{2}},\quad dP_{c}(\boldsymbol{x})=\left(\frac{\pi}{2c}\right)^{d/2}e^{-2c\|\boldsymbol{x}^{2}\|_{2}^{2}},\quad\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

The associated
d-
variates eigenfunctions and their corresponding eigenvalues are given by the tensor products of the uni-variate

\varphi_{k}(x_{i},\gamma)
and their corresponding
\lambda_{k}.
