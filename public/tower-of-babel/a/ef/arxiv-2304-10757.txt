Scattering Dynamics and Boundary States of a Non-Hermitian Dirac Equation
Yun Yong Terh, Rimi Banerjee, Haoran Xue, Y. D. Chong

Abstract
We study a non-Hermitian variant of the (2+1)-dimensional Dirac wave equation that hosts a real energy spectrum with pairwise-orthogonal eigenstates. In the spatially uniform case, the Hamiltonian’s non-Hermitian symmetries allow its eigenstates to be mapped to a pair of Hermitian Dirac subsystems. When a wave is transmitted across an interface between two spatially uniform domains with different parameters, an anomalous form of Klein tunneling occurs, whereby reflection is suppressed while the transmittance is substantially higher or lower than unity. The interface can even function as a simultaneous laser and coherent perfect absorber. Remarkably, the violation of flux conservation occurs entirely at the interface, with no wave amplification or damping taking place in the bulk. Moreover, at energies within the Dirac mass gaps, the interface can support exponentially localized boundary states with real energies. These features of the continuum model can be reproduced in non-Hermitian lattice models.



I
Introduction

The Dirac equation, formulated by Dirac in 1929 to describe relativistic electrons and positrons
Dirac 1928
, has long fascinated physicists with its rich implications, from the determination of the electron’s magnetic moment to the phenomenon of Klein tunneling
Klein 1929
. In recent years, the Dirac equation has also taken on a key role in condensed matter and other non-relativistic systems: 2D and 3D Dirac equations are used to describe electronic states in materials such as graphene
Novoselov et al. 2005
;
Zhang et al. 2005
;
Castro Neto et al. 2009
;
Young et al. 2012
, and bandstructures near a topological phase transition typically simplify to a Dirac equation or one of its relatives
Haldane 1988
;
Hasan and Kane 2010
. Moreover, by exploiting the mathematical similarity of single-particle Schrödinger equations and classical wave equations, the Dirac equation can be realized in a variety of classical wave metamaterials
Ozawa et al. 2019
;
Ni et al. 2022
, such as photonic and acoustic crystals
Sepkhanov et al. 2007
;
Zhang and Liu 2008a
;
Ochiai and Onoda 2009
;
Bittner et al. 2010
;
Huang et al. 2011
;
Zhong and Zhang 2011
;
Torrent et al. 2013
;
Lu et al. 2014
, resonator arrays
Yang et al. 2017
, and waveguide arrays
Tran et al. 2014
;
Koke et al. 2020
;
Leykam et al. 2016
.

The study of classical wave metamaterials has also motivated interest in non-Hermitian wave equations, due to the ease with which gain and/or loss can be controlled in such systems. The first non-Hermitian Hamiltonians to be thus studied were those obeying parity/time-reversal (PT) symmetry, a non-Hermitian symmetry originally conceived by Bender and Boettcher for a hypothetical generalization of quantum mechanics
Bender and Boettcher 1998
;
Bender et al. 1999
. PT symmetric Hamiltonians have now been realized in a wide range of experimental platforms, such as optical waveguide arrays
El-Ganainy et al. 2007
;
Rüter et al. 2010
;
El-Ganainy et al. 2018
. Their two most remarkable features—the ability to exhibit real energy spectra despite being non-Hermitian, and the spontaneous breaking of PT symmetry whereby pairs of real energies merge and turn complex—have been found to produce many interesting applications, such as unidirectional invisibility
Lin et al. 2011
and laser mode stabilization
Zhao et al. 2018
. More recently, there has also been progress on other aspects of non-Hermitian physics, including the topology of non-Hermitian bandstructures
Rudner and Levitov 2009
;
Liang and Huang 2013
;
Shen et al. 2018
;
Bergholtz et al. 2021
, which is associated with phenomena such as non-Hermitian topological modes
Liang and Chong 2013
;
Lee 2016
;
Leykam et al. 2017
and the non-Hermitian skin effect
Hatano and Nelson 1996
;
Martinez Alvarez et al. 2018
;
Borgnia et al. 2020
;
Okuma et al. 2020
.

Recently, Xue
et al.
proposed a class of non-Hermitian Hamiltonians whose bandstructures host non-Hermitian Dirac cones
Xue et al. 2020
. These Hamiltonians obey a set of non-Hermitian symmetries related to but distinct from PT symmetry, leading to the existence of pairs of real energy eigenvalues with eigenvectors that are mutually orthogonal, according to the usual inner product employed for Hermitian systems (by contrast, the eigenvectors of PT symmetric Hamiltonians need not be orthogonal in this sense). When two such eigenvalues become degenerate, they form a diabolic point (e.g., a Dirac point), unlike the exceptional point degeneracies more commonly found in non-Hermitian systems
El-Ganainy et al. 2018
;
Bergholtz et al. 2021
. The non-Hermitian Dirac points were shown to have properties matching Dirac points in Hermitian bandstructures, such as the ability to undergo band inversions giving rise to topological boundary states
Xue et al. 2020
. However, these features were explored only in the context of discrete (tight-binding) models. The nature of the non-Hermitian Dirac equation, which ought to govern the non-Hermitian Dirac-like bands in the continuum limit, was not discussed in detail.

In this paper, we develop the non-Hermitian Dirac equation (NHDE) that governs the non-Hermitian Dirac cones described in Ref.
Xue et al. 2020
, and study its physical implications. The NHDE takes the form of a 4-component Dirac-like equation in (2+1)D, with a non-Hermitian mass operator. By a variation of Dirac’s argument
Dirac 1928
, we show that it supports relativistic (hyperbolic) dispersion relations. The NHDE can be transformed into a pair of 2-component Hermitian Dirac subsystems, whose Dirac masses correspond to the eigenvalues of the non-Hermitian mass operator. Notably, the mass gaps depend on a non-Hermitian parameter corresponding to on-site gain and loss, so it is possible for band inversions to occur via gain/loss tuning
Takata and Notomi 2018
;
Kawabata et al. 2018
;
Luo and Zhang 2019
;
Wu et al. 2020
;
Xue et al. 2020
.

Next, we study the nature of wave propagation based on the NHDE. For a plane wave incident on an interface separating two uniform semi-infinite domains with unequal scalar potential and/or certain choices of Hamiltonian parameters, we show that the NHDE can exhibit exactly the same reflection and transmission characteristics as the Hermitian Dirac equation, including Klein tunneling (i.e., 100% transmission when the incident energy is far detuned from the mass gap
Klein 1929
).

For other parameter choices, flux conservation is violated, resulting in an anomalous form of Klein tunneling whereby reflection is suppressed but the transmitted flux is larger or smaller than the incident flux. Remarkably, the violation of flux conservation appears to originate at the domain wall itself, since wave amplification or damping does not occur in the bulk domains, which host real-energy plane wave solutions. At certain energies, the violation of flux conservation can become so extreme that the domain wall acts as a simultaneous laser and coherent perfect absorber
Chong et al. 2010
;
Baranov et al. 2017
;
Longhi 2010a
;
Chong et al. 2011
, spontaneously emitting flux with no input while also perfectly absorbing a specific configuration of input waves. On the other hand, at energies lying in the mass gaps, the domain wall can host chiral boundary states that behave much like the topological boundary states of the Hermitian Dirac equation. These boundary states have real energies, even if the domain wall violates flux conservation at energies outside the mass gaps.

II
Non-Hermitian 2D Dirac Equation

Consider the following time-dependent Dirac-like equation, defined in 2+1 dimensions:

i\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}=\Big(\mathcal{M}-i\alpha_{1}\partial_{1}-i\alpha_{2}\partial_{2}\Big)\psi(\mathbf{r},t).

(1)

Here,
\mathbf{r}=(x,y)
denotes 2D spatial coordinates,
t
is the time coordinate,
\partial_{i}=\partial/\partial x_{i}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
, and
\{\mathcal{M},\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\}
are matrices to be specified. If we choose
\mathcal{M}=m\sigma_{3}
and
\alpha_{i}=\sigma_{i}
, where
\{\sigma_{i}\}
are the Pauli matrices, Eq. (
1
) reduces to the standard (2+1)D Dirac equation, with
\psi
a two-component wavefunction,
m
representing the Dirac mass, and the speed of light and
\hbar
both set to unity. We are interested in a
non-Hermitian
Dirac equation obtained with an alternative choice of
\mathcal{M}
and
\alpha_{1,2}
. We shall see that the non-Hermitian model can exhibit numerous features normally associated with the Hermitian Dirac equation
Xue et al. 2020
.

Before proceeding, we define

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\mu}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\sigma_{\mu}\\
\sigma_{\mu}&0\end{bmatrix}=\tau_{1}\sigma_{\mu},

(2)

where
\mu\in\{0,1,2,3\}
, and
\tau_{\nu}\sigma_{\mu}
denotes the tensor product between Pauli matrices
\tau_{\nu}
and
\sigma_{\mu}
. In Ref.
Xue et al. 2020
, it was shown that if a Hamiltonian
H
obeys the symmetries

\displaystyle\Sigma_{0}H\Sigma_{0}

\displaystyle=H^{\dagger},

(3)

\displaystyle\{H,\Sigma_{3}\Sigma_{1}T\}

\displaystyle=0,

(4)

where
T
is the complex conjugation operator, then there are two consequences. First, the eigenvalues are either real (symmetry-unbroken) or appear in complex conjugate pairs (symmetry-unbroken). Second, if
|\psi\rangle
is an eigenstate with eigenvalue
E
, then
|\psi^{\prime}\rangle=\Sigma_{1}\Sigma_{3}T|\psi\rangle
is an orthogonal eigenstate with energy
-E^{*}
(where
T
is the complex conjugation operator and the orthogonality relation
\langle\psi^{\prime}|\psi\rangle=0
uses the standard Hermitian inner product). Thus, the non-Hermitian
H
retains a remnant of the two key properties of Hermitian operators, eigenvalue reality and eigenstate orthogonality. By contrast, PT symmetric non-Hermitian Hamiltonians can exhibit real eigenvalues, but generally have non-orthogonal eigenstates
Bender and Boettcher 1998
;
Bender et al. 1999
;
Bender 2007
.

For brevity, we will refer to Hamiltonians obeying Eqs. (
3
)–(
4
) as “semi-Hermitian”. When semi-Hermicity is unbroken, the eigenvalues form two pairs,
\{\pm E_{1}\}
and
\{\pm E_{2}\}
. Within a pair (but not between pairs), the corresponding eigenstates are orthogonal.

Returning to the 2D Dirac-type equation (
1
), let
\mathcal{M}
be the
4\times 4
matrix

\mathcal{M}=\begin{bmatrix}W&V\\
V&W^{\dagger}\end{bmatrix},

(5)

where

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}m+i\gamma&b\\
b^{*}&-m+i\gamma\end{bmatrix},

(6)

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=V^{\dagger}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda&c\\
c^{*}&-\lambda\end{bmatrix},

(7)

for
b,c\in\mathbb{C}
and
m,\gamma,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. Then
\mathcal{M}
obeys Eq. (
3
)–(
4
) and is semi-Hermitian. We will see later how
\mathcal{M}
could be physically realized. By analogy with the Hermitian Dirac equation, we seek
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
such that

\displaystyle\{\alpha_{i},\alpha_{j}\}

\displaystyle=2\delta_{ij},\quad i,j\in\{1,2\},

(8)

\displaystyle\{\mathcal{M},\alpha_{i}\}

\displaystyle=0.

(9)

We do not assume
\mathcal{M}^{2}
to be proportional to the identity, in a departure from the formulation of the Hermitian Dirac equation. If we take the representation
\alpha_{i}=\Sigma_{i}
, which satisfies Eq. (
8
), then Eq. (
9
) implies
b=c=0
, and
\mathcal{M}
has the form

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{M}&=m\sigma_{3}+i\gamma\tau_{3}+\lambda\tau_{1}\sigma_{3}.\end{aligned}

(10)

We thus arrive at the non-Hermitian Dirac equation (NHDE)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} i\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}&=\Big(\mathcal{M}-i\Sigma_{1}\partial_{1}-i\Sigma_{2}\partial_{2}\Big)\psi(\mathbf{r},t)\\
&=\tau_{1}\left(-i\sum_{j=1}^{2}\sigma_{j}\partial_{j}+\lambda\sigma_{3}\right)+m\sigma_{3}+i\gamma\tau_{3}.\end{aligned}

(11)

Thus, the model can also be interpreted as a pair of Dirac “valleys” that interact via both a Hermitian coupling,
m\sigma_{3}
, and a non-Hermitian coupling,
i\gamma\tau_{3}
.

To derive the dispersion relation, recall that in the standard Dirac equation, one takes the square of the Hamiltonian and uses the anticommutivity of the Dirac matrices
Dirac 1928
. A variant of this procedure can be applied to the non-Hermitian equation (
11
). Let
|\psi\rangle
be an eigenstate of energy
E
and momentum
\mathbf{k}
. Squaring the Hamiltonian and using Eqs. (
8
)—(
9
), we obtain

\Big(\mathcal{M}^{2}+|\mathbf{k}|^{2}\Big)\ket{\psi}=E^{2}\ket{\psi},

(12)

where
\mathcal{M}^{2}\neq I
. This implies that
\ket{\psi}
is also an eigenvector of
\mathcal{M}^{2}
. If the semi-Hermiticity of
\mathcal{M}
is unbroken, it has real eigenvalues
\pm\mu_{n}
, where
n=1,2
; then
\mathcal{M}^{2}
has doubly degenerate eigenvalues
\mu_{n}^{2}
, and

\displaystyle E=\pm\sqrt{\mu_{n}^{2}+|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}.

(13)

Therefore, for unbroken semi-Hermiticity, the dispersion relation consists of a pair of relativistic hyperbolas, as shown in Fig.
1
(a).

Note that even if
\ket{\psi}
is an eigenvector of
\mathcal{M}^{2}
, it need not be an eigenvector of
\mathcal{M}
; in general,
\ket{\psi}
could be a superposition of two eigenvectors of
\mathcal{M}
whose eigenvalues have the same square. To find these, take the ansatz
|\varphi\rangle|\!\updownarrow\rangle
, where
\sigma_{3}|\!\updownarrow\rangle=\pm|\!\updownarrow\rangle
. Applying Eq. (
10
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\,|\varphi^{n}_{\pm}\rangle\,|\!\updownarrow\rangle

\displaystyle=\pm\mu_{n}|\varphi^{n}_{\pm}\rangle\,|\!\updownarrow\rangle,

(14)

\displaystyle H_{\pm}|\varphi_{\pm}^{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=\pm\mu_{n}|\varphi_{\pm}^{n}\rangle,

(15)

\displaystyle H_{\pm}

\displaystyle=\pm\left(\lambda\tau_{1}+m\pm i\gamma\tau_{3}\right),

(16)

where
H_{\pm}
is a
2\times 2
matrix, and
n
indexes its eigenvectors. These sub-Hamiltonians obey the PT symmetry
Bender and Boettcher 1998
;
Bender et al. 1999
;
Bender 2007

[H_{\pm},\tau_{1}T]=0,

(17)

and are related by

H_{\pm}=-\tau_{1}H_{\mp}\tau_{1}.

(18)

Let us take

\displaystyle\ket{\varphi^n_+}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
e^{i\phi_{n}}\end{bmatrix},\quad|\varphi_{\pm}^{n}\rangle=\tau_{1}|\varphi_{\mp}^{n}\rangle,

(19)

for some
\phi_{n}
. Using Eqs. (
15
)–(
16
), we find that

\mu_{n}=m+i\gamma+\lambda e^{i\phi_{n}}.

(20)

The real and imaginary parts of this equation are

\displaystyle\cos\phi_{n}

\displaystyle=(\mu_{n}-m)/\lambda,

(21)

\displaystyle\sin\phi_{n}

\displaystyle=-\gamma/\lambda.

(22)

Given
m
and
\gamma
, Eqs. (
21
)–(
22
) have two sets of solutions,
\{\phi_{1},\mu_{1}\}
and
\{\phi_{2},\mu_{2}\}
. Fig.
1
(b) illustrates the geometric relationship between these quantities.
The above derivation also clarifies how the regime of unbroken semi-Hermiticity, introduced in Ref.
Xue et al. 2020
, corresponds to the PT-unbroken phase for an underlying pair of
2\times 2
sub-Hamiltonians (
16
).

Now, for fixed
n\in\{1,2\}
, consider

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_n}=c^{n}_{+}\ket{\varphi_+^n}\ket{\uparrow}+c^{n}_{-}\ket{\varphi_-^n}\ket{\downarrow}.

(23)

Plugging this into the NHDE, and using the above properties of
\ket{\varphi_\pm^n}\ket{\updownarrow}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Big(k_{1}\sigma_{1}+k_{2}\sigma_{2}+\mu_{n}\sigma_{3}\Big)\begin{bmatrix}c_{+}^{n}\\
c_{-}^{n}\end{bmatrix}=E\begin{bmatrix}c_{+}^{n}\\
c_{-}^{n}\end{bmatrix},

(24)

which is the Hermitian 2D Dirac equation with mass
\mu_{n}
.

There is one Hermitian 2D Dirac equation (
24
) for each
n\in\{1,2\}
, where
n
enumerates the solutions to the underlying
2\times 2
eigenproblem (
15
). The relationship between the two
2\times 2
sub-Hamiltonians, Eq. (
18
), plays a role similar to particle-hole symmetry. In the Hermitian limit
\gamma=0
, this becomes a standard particle-hole symmetry, and the Dirac masses are
\mu_{1,2}=m\pm\lambda
.

We can use the signs of the Dirac masses to derive a phase diagram for the non-Hermitian Dirac Hamiltonian. This is shown in Fig.
1
(c), plotted against
m
and
\gamma
. If semi-Hermicity is unbroken, there are three distinct phases: inside the circle
\sqrt{m^{2}+\gamma^{2}}=|\lambda|
, the Dirac masses have opposite signs, and outside it they are both either positive or negative. Along the phase boundary, at least one of the masses vanishes. This occurs when, in Fig.
1
(b), the dashed blue circle, centered at
m+i\gamma
and of radius
\lambda
, crosses the origin.

Notably, we can drive the system across the phase boundary by varying the non-Hermitian parameter
\gamma
, while keeping
m
and
\lambda
unchanged. Gain/loss-induced topological phase transitions have also recently been observed in other models

;
Kawabata et al. 2018
;
Luo and Zhang 2019
;
Wu et al. 2020
;
Xue et al. 2020
. These band inversions are associated with boundary states, as discussed in Sec.
V
.

III
Non-Hermitian Lattice Model

In this section, we present an exemplary tight-binding lattice model that gives rise to the NHDE, Eq. (
11
), in its long-wavelength limit. Such lattices can be realized physically using metamaterials, such as photonic or acoustic structures
Ozawa et al. 2019
;
Ota et al. 2020
;
Parto et al. 2021
;
Price et al. 2022
;
Ma et al. 2019
;
Xue et al. 2022
. Previously, in Ref.
Xue et al. 2020
, different tight-binding models satisfying the semi-Hermitian symmetries (
3
)–(
4
) have been derived, and it was shown that they can serve as non-Hermitian analogues for Chern insulators, Weyl semimetals, etc. However, the relationship to the Dirac Hamiltonian was not studied in detail.

Consider the 2D square lattice shown in Fig.
1
(d). Each unit cell contains 4 sites with complex on-site potentials. All inter-site couplings are Hermitian; some, drawn as solid lines and dashes, are reciprocal (i.e., the forward and backward hopping amplitudes are the same), while others are nonreciprocal (i.e., the reverse hopping amplitudes, opposite to the arrows, are complex conjugates of the forward amplitudes). Such nonreciprocal hoppings are implementable in metamaterials, e.g., by using coupling resonators with appropriate phase shifts
Hafezi et al. 2011
;
Hafezi et al. 2013
.

Let
\psi_{n}
denote the set of four amplitudes on unit cell
n
. The time-dependent Schrödinger equation is

\displaystyle i\frac{\partial\psi_{n}}{\partial t}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\psi_{n}+\sum_{i=1,2}\left(\mathcal{J}_{i}\,\psi_{n-r_{i}}+\mathcal{J}_{i}^{\dagger}\,\psi_{n+r_{i}}\right),

(25)

where
\mathcal{H}_{1}
is the Hamiltonian for a single unit cell, and
n\pm r_{i}
denotes the unit cell displaced by one period forward/backward in the
i

\ell
. The
\mathcal{J}_{i}
matrices are

\mathcal{J}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0&0\\
0&0&i\kappa&0\\
0&0&0&0\\
i\kappa&0&0&0\end{bmatrix},\;\;\mathcal{J}_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0&\kappa\\
0&0&0&0\\
0&\kappa&0&0\\
0&0&0&0\end{bmatrix},

(26)

where
\kappa
is the hopping parameter defined in Fig.
1
(d). In the long-wavelength (continuum) limit,

\psi_{n\pm r_{i}}=\psi_{n}\pm\ell\big(\partial_{i}\psi\big)_{n}+\frac{\ell^{2}}{2}\left(\partial^{2}_{i}\psi\right)_{n}+\cdots

(27)

Thus, the time-dependent Schrödinger equation becomes

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} i\frac{\partial\psi_{n}}{\partial t}&=\left[\mathcal{H}_{1}+\sum_{i=1,2}(\mathcal{J}_{i}+\mathcal{J}_{i}^{\dagger})\right]\psi_{n}\\
&+\sum_{i=1,2}(\mathcal{J}_{i}^{\dagger}-\mathcal{J}_{i})\;\ell\;\big(\partial_{i}\psi\big)_{n}+\mathcal{O}(\ell^{2}),\end{aligned}

(28)

where
\mathcal{H}_{1}
is the Hamiltonian for one unit cell,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}m+i\gamma&0&\lambda&-(1-i)\kappa\\
0&-m+i\gamma&-(1+i)\kappa&-\lambda\\
\lambda&-(1-i)\kappa&m-i\gamma&0\\
-(1+i)\kappa&-\lambda&0&-m-i\gamma\end{bmatrix}.

From Eq. (
26

\mathcal{J}_{i}^{\dagger}-\mathcal{J}_{i}=-i\kappa\Sigma_{i}
. Omitting terms of
\mathcal{O}(\ell^{2})
and higher, we arrive at a wave equation
i\partial\psi/\partial t=\mathbf{H}\psi(\mathbf{x},t)
. The effective Hamiltonian is

\mathbf{H}=\mathcal{M}-i\kappa\ell\left(\Sigma_{1}\,\partial_{1}+\Sigma_{2}\,\partial_{2}\right),\\

(29)

where
\mathcal{M}
has precisely the form given in Eq. (
10
). After taking the normalization
\kappa\ell=1
, this is our desired non-Hermitian 2D Dirac equation.

IV
Scattering from a domain wall

We now ask how the NHDE is affected by spatial non-uniformity. As discussed in Sec.
II
, the spatially uniform model with unbroken semi-Hermiticity behaves as a pair of Hermitian Dirac subsystems [see Eq. (
19
)]. If we now let the parameters vary in space, the Hermitian-like behavior should be expected to persist if the variations keep
\gamma/\lambda
constant; on the other hand, if
\gamma/\lambda
is not constant, non-Hermitian effects should become important. We will focus on two important cases of spatial non-uniformity. This section will cover reflection and transmission between two spatially uniform domains; the case of boundary states localized at domain walls is studied in Sec.
V

We are particularly interested in the phenomenon of Klein tunneling

\Phi_{0}

E

Klein 1929
, Klein tunneling has also been observed with Dirac Hamiltonians arising in 2D materials
Katsnelson et al. 2006
;
Beenakker 2008
;
Stander et al. 2009
;
Young and Kim 2009
and photonic lattices
Bahat-Treidel et al. 2010
;
Dreisow et al. 2012
;
Jiang et al. 2020
;
Ozawa et al. 2017
;
Nguyen-The and Tran 2020
.

V
Boundary states

The Hermitian Dirac equation supports boundary solutions that are exponentially localized to domain walls. These play a particularly important role in the theory of band topology
Hasan and Kane 2010
;
Cayssol and Fuchs 2021
. For example, the Su-Schrieffer-Heeger (SSH) lattice model reduces to a 1D Dirac Hamiltonian in the limit of long wavelengths and a small band gap. The SSH model is known to host zero-energy boundary states tied to a topological invariant, the Zak phase
Su et al. 1979
;
Zak 1989
, and in the continuum limit these states reduce to the Jackiw-Rebbi zero mode for a 1D Dirac equation with a mass inversion
Jackiw and Rebbi 1976
. In a similar fashion, the 2D Haldane model can be reduced to a pair of 2D Dirac Hamiltonians, and its topological phase transitions can be understood in terms of the sign changes of the two Dirac masses
Haldane 1988
.

In this section, we show that the 2D NHDE can host boundary states with real energies, not only when
\gamma/\lambda
is constant (i.e., there is a consistent decomposition into Hermitian Dirac subsystems), but also when it is not. The latter case is notable because, in Sec.
IV
, we saw that such a domain wall generally violates flux conservation.

As with Sec.
IV
, we assume a setup with a domain wall at
x_{1}=0
, with the parameters in the left (
L
) and (
R
) domains given by
m_{L/R}
,
\gamma_{L/R}
, etc. Both domains have
\Phi=0
and unbroken semi-Hermiticity. We look for boundary states with real
E
lying in the mass gaps, so that the wavefunction decays exponentially away from the domain wall in both directions. As described in Appendix
A
, the scattering matrix framework used for studying reflection and transmission in Sec.
IV
can be adapted for finding boundary states. When
E
lies in the mass gaps, we can pick an appropriate mode-sorting convention so that boundary states correspond to poles of the scattering matrix
S
.

Fig.
5
(a) shows the dispersion relation for the boundary states at a domain wall. The colors represent the heat map of
I_{\mathrm{max}}
, defined as before as the maximum eigenvalue of
S^{\dagger}S
, plotted against
E
(taken to be real) and
q
(the conserved momentum along
x_{2}
, parallel to the domain wall). The domains have Dirac masses
\mu_{1L}=1.57
,
\mu_{2L}=-0.17
,
\mu_{1R}=1.32
, and
\mu_{2R}=0.28
. We see that there is a branch of real-energy boundary states (
I_{\mathrm{max}}\rightarrow\infty
) within the Dirac mass gap, with a chiral dispersion relation. In this plot,
\gamma_{L}/\lambda_{L}=\gamma_{R}/\lambda_{R}
, so the boundary states can be associated with a band inversion in one of the Dirac subsystems,
\mu_{2L}<0
and
\mu_{2R}>0

24

\mu_{2R}>\mu_{2L}

In Fig.
5

\gamma_{L}/\lambda_{L}\neq\gamma_{R}/\lambda_{R}
so the two domains do not have the same decomposition into Hermitian Dirac subsystems. Nonetheless, we still find a family of boundary states with real energies and chiral dispersion. The intensity profile for the

Note that the real-energy boundary states in Fig.

\gamma

;
Kawabata et al. 2018

Luo and Zhang 2019

\mathrm{Im}(E)\neq 0

\mathrm{Im}(E)=0
can be achieved in a non-Hermitian system whose bulk states all have real energy. This is the case even if the domain wall violates flux conservation for reflection/transmission outside the mass gap (as we saw in Sec.
IV

VI
Conclusion

We have presented a (2+1)-dimensional non-Hermitian Dirac equation (NHDE) that exhibits numerous similarities to the Hermitian Dirac equation, but also important differences. The model relies on the non-Hermitian symmetries introduced in Ref.
Xue et al. 2020
, referred to in this paper as “semi-Hermiticity”, which allow a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian to have real spectra with pairwise-orthogonal eigenstates. In the context of the NHDE, under translationally invariant conditions, we used semi-Hermiticity to derive an explicit relationship between the plane wave eigenstates of the NHDE and a pair of Hermitian Dirac equations.

We then considered two uniform domains with different scalar potentials and/or NHDE parameters, with each having unbroken semi-Hermiticity so that their bulk solutions are like Hermitian Dirac modes. Remarkably, depending on the parameter choices, reflection and transmission at the interface can be identical to the Hermitian case (incuding Klein tunneling
Klein 1929
), or significantly different due to the breakdown of flux conservation. In the latter case, the flux nonconservation occurs at the domain wall, not in the bulk, and can be strong enough for the domain wall to act as a laser and coherent perfect absorber
Chong et al. 2010
;
Baranov et al. 2017
;
Longhi 2010a
;
Chong et al. 2011
. These domain walls also host exponentially localized boundary states with real energies.

In future work, it would be interesting to experimentally realize some of the above phenomena, such as the domain wall laser/absorber, using appropriately-designed classical wave metamaterials. It would also be worthwhile to investigate other phenomena associated with the Hermitian Dirac equation, such as zitterbewegung
Zhang and Liu 2008b
;
Longhi 2010b
;
Dreisow et al. 2010
and Dirac solitons
Fushchych and Zhdanov 2006
;
Poddubny and Smirnova 2018
, in the context of the NHDE. In particular, whether relativistic symmetries can play any meaningful role in this non-Hermitian system is presently unclear.

Take the NHDE (
11
), with all model parameters constant in space and time. As shown in Sec.

, when semi-Hermiticity is unbroken, there can be plane wave solutions
\psi(\mathbf{r},t)=u\exp[i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}-Et)]

\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

where

\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{M}+\Sigma_{1}\,k_{1}+\Sigma_{2}\,k_{2}\right)u=Eu.

(38)

The probability flux
\mathbf{J}=(J_{1},J_{2})

\displaystyle\psi^{\dagger}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\psi^{\dagger}}{\partial t}\psi

\displaystyle=-i\psi^{\dagger}\left(\mathcal{M}-\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}\right)\psi-\partial_{1}\left(\psi^{\dagger}\Sigma_{1}\psi\right)-\partial_{2}\left(\psi^{\dagger}\Sigma_{2}\psi\right).

Comparing this to

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\psi^{\dagger}\psi\right)=[\text{gain/loss term}]-\nabla\cdot J

yields the result

\displaystyle J_{j}=u^{\dagger}\Sigma_{j}u.

(39)

This derivation is unaltered if we introduce a uniform scalar potential
\Phi

x_{1}=0
, separating two spatially uniform domains indexed by

(i.e.,

) and

(i.e.,
x_{1}>0
). The domains may have different

, each domain has four real eigenvalues
\{\pm\mu_{1d},\pm\mu_{2d}\}
—i.e.,

lies outside the Dirac mass gaps. Then each domain hosts plane waves with eigenvectors

\displaystyle\{u_{nd}^{s}\},\;\;\mathrm{where}\;s=\pm,\;\;n\in\{1,2\},\;\;d\in\{L/R\}.

(40)

Each
u_{nd}^{s}
is associated with a wave-vector
(\pm k_{nd},q)
, with the sign of the first component chosen so that the direction of probability flux is
s
. We use Eq. (
39
) to impose the normalization
|J_{1}|=1
. The eigenvectors satisfy Eq. (
38

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{2}u_{nd}^{s}

\displaystyle=\mu_{n}u_{nd}^{s},

(41)

\displaystyle E^{2}

\displaystyle=\mu_{n}^{2}+k_{n,d}^{2}+q^{2}.

(42)

In each domain

\displaystyle\psi(x_{1})=\sum_{sn}a^{s}_{nd}u_{nd}^{s}e^{\pm ik_{nd}x_{1}}.

Continuity of the wavefunction at
x_{1}=0
then implies

\begin{bmatrix}u_{1L}^{+}&u_{2L}^{+}&-u_{1R}^{-}&-u_{2R}^{-}\end{bmatrix}_{j}\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{in}}\\
=\begin{bmatrix}-u_{1L}^{-}&-u_{2L}^{-}&u_{1R}^{+}&u_{1R}^{+}\end{bmatrix}_{j}\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{out}},

(43)

for each eigenvector component
j=1,\dots,4
, where

\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{in}}=\begin{bmatrix}a^{+}_{1L}\\
a^{+}_{2L}\\
a^{-}_{1R}\\
a^{-}_{2R}\end{bmatrix},\;\;\;\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{out}}=\begin{bmatrix}a^{-}_{1L}\\
a^{-}_{2L}\\
a^{+}_{1R}\\
a^{+}_{2R}\end{bmatrix}.

(44)

We use this define the scattering matrix
S
:

S(E,q)\;\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{in}}=\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{out}}.

(45)
