Abstract
For the numerical solution of nonsmooth problems, sometimes it is not necessary that an exact subgradient/generalized Jacobian is at our disposal, but it suffices that a semismooth derivative, i.e., a mapping satisfying a certain semismoothness property, is available. In this paper we consider not only semismooth derivatives of single-valued mappings, but also its interplay with the semismoothness ∗ property for multifunctions. In particular, we are interested in semismooth derivatives of solution maps to parametric semismooth ∗ inclusions. Our results are expressed in terms of suitable generalized derivatives of the set-valued part, i.e., by limiting coderivatives or by SC (subspace containing) derivatives. Further we show that semismooth derivatives coincide a.e. with generalized Jacobians and state some consequences concerning strict proto-differentiability for semismooth ∗ multifunctions.



Key words.
Nonsmooth problems, semismoothness, solution map, generalized derivative

1
Introduction

As stated in numerous works in the literature, semismoothness plays an important role in solving various nonsmooth problems. Let us list three typical problem classes of this sort.

1.

The first one provides the main motivation for this paper and concerns optimization problems
of the form

\displaystyle\min\theta(x)\quad\mbox{ subject to }\quad x\in U_{\rm ad},

(1.1)

where
\theta:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a locally Lipschitz function and
U_{\rm ad}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a closed set. For the numerical solution of these problems, the use of
bundle methods
is a popular approach. There exists a bunch of bundle methods in the literature, cf.
[
23
,
19
,
37
]
, and all these methods have in common that they require an

oracle
, which returns for arbitrary
x\in U_{\rm ad}
the function value
\theta(x)
and one subgradient
g(x)\in\partial^{c}\theta(x)
,

(1.2)

where
\partial^{c}\theta
denotes the Clarke subdifferential. Then, under the additional assumption that

\theta
is weakly semismooth (see Remark
3.10
below),

it can be shown that the bundle method either stops after finitely many steps at a C-stationary point
\bar{x}
or, if the produced sequence
x_{k}
remains bounded, one of its accumulation points is C-stationary.

From a limited number of bundle methods, applicable to the numerical solution of (
1.1
) (e.g.
[
23
,
19
,
37
]
), we consider in this paper the Bundle-Trust region algorithm (BT algorithm) from Schramm and Zowe
[
37
]
, described also in
[
41
]
.
It seems that Dempe and Bard
[
8
]
were the first who observed that the BT algorithm also works, when the oracle delivers only a so-called
pseudogradient
. Pseudogradients were introduced by Norkin
[
29
,
30
]
and have the remarkable feature that they fulfill a certain semismoothness property. Although this property does not necessarily imply that
\theta
is weakly semismooth, one can show that a similar convergence result holds as above: If the BT-algorithm produces a bounded sequence then at least one accumulation point is
pseudo-stationary
, i.e.,
0
is a pseudogradient of
\theta
at this point. By checking the convergence proof for other bundle methods
[
19
,
23
]
one can show
that a similar assertion holds: If the oracle delivers only a pseudogradient, one can still prove that the method works and returns pseudo-stationary points.

2.

Semismoothness also plays an important role in another field of numerical analysis, namely the solution of nonsmooth equations

G(x)=0,

where
G:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a locally Lipschitz mapping. Whereas in the original semismooth Newton method, introduced by Kummer
[
22
]
and Qi and Sun
[
35
]
, matrices from the Clarke generalized Jacobian were used, it was soon observed that the Clarke generalized Jacobian can be replaced by another mapping which fulfills a certain semismoothness property, closely related to the one imposed on pseudogradients. In what follows the elements of this mapping, both in the scalar- and vector-valued case, will be termed
semismooth derivatives
. This is particularly important for carrying over the semismooth Newton method to the infinite-dimensional setting, where no generalized Jacobian exists. We refer the reader to
[
39
]
for the semismooth Newton method in the infinite-dimensional setting. Note that, whereas the Clarke calculus often yields only inclusions, the calculus of semismooth derivatives yields again a semismooth derivative. Though the semismoothness property, imposed on the semismooth derivatives in the scalar- and vector-valued case, is essentially the same, there is a big difference: Whereas for the semismooth Newton method the semismoothness needs to hold only at the solution, when working with pseudogradients in the framework of a bundle method it needs to hold at each
x\in U_{ad}
.

3.

Finally, for the numerical solution of inclusions

0\in F(x),

the so-called semismooth
∗
Newton method was introduced in
[
11
]
, where
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a set-valued mapping. Here, the semismooth
∗
property was defined in terms of the
limiting (Mordukhovich) coderivative
and was later simplified for the important subclass of SCD (subspace containing derivatives) mappings by using the notion of SC (subspace containing) derivatives in
[
12
]
. The SC derivative can be considered as a generalization of the B-Jacobian (Bouligand differential) of single-valued mappings to multifunctions and acts as a kind of skeleton for the limiting coderivative. The semismooth
∗
Newton method has been already successfully applied to several difficult problems, see, e.g.,
[
14
,
15
,
9
,
10
]
.

We observe that semismoothness plays an important role in solving different types of nonsmooth problems: optimization problems, equations and inclusions. The respective notions of semismoothness are slightly different for the different problem types and it is not clear how they can combined for the numerical solution of problems assembled by different nonsmooth and set-valued objects. As an example consider an optimization problem of the form

\displaystyle\min\,

\displaystyle\varphi(x,y)

subject to

\displaystyle 0\in F(x,y),

(1.3)

\displaystyle x\in U_{\rm ad},

where
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
is a locally Lipschitz function,
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a set-valued mapping and
U_{\rm ad}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a closed set. Problems of such type arise, e.g., in bilevel programming and mathematical programs with equilibrium constraints (MPECs).

One possibility for tackling (
1.3
) is the so-called
implicit programming approach
(ImP), cf.
[
24
]
, where one typically requires that for every
x
the inclusion
0\in F(x,\cdot)
has a unique solution denoted by
\sigma(x)
. Hence we can eliminate
y
from the model above and arrive at the (reduced) problem

\displaystyle\min\theta(x):=\varphi(x,\sigma(x))\quad\mbox{ subject to }x\in U_{\rm ad}.

(1.4)

Under the additional assumption that
\sigma
is locally Lipschitz, the objective
\theta
is locally Lipschitz as well and we can apply a bundle method for its numerical solution, provided a suitable oracle is available.
In most cases, however, one cannot guarantee that at all points a Clarke subgradient of
\theta
can be computed. Nevertheless, if the original data
\varphi
and
F
are semismooth and semismooth
∗
, respectively, then it is possible to construct an oracle returning an element of a semismooth derivative of
\theta
. This oracle will then ensure the convergence of the used bundle method to a stationary point.

In this paper we will provide the theoretical base for this approach. In particular, we will conduct a deep analysis of various available notions of the semismoothness property and find out some important mutual relationships. We will show that a single-valued mapping possesses some semismooth derivative on an open set
\Omega
if and only if it is G-semismooth (semismooth in the sense of Gowda
[
16
]
) there. Further, this mapping is almost everywhere strictly differentiable and the semismooth derivative coincides with the generalized Jacobian almost everywhere in
\Omega
. Moreover, its generalized Jacobian is contained in another semismooth derivative obtained from the original one by performing some closure operation and taking the convex hull of the values.
For set-valued mappings we introduce a generalization of the semismooth
∗
property (denoted
\Gamma
-ss
∗
property) and the so-called SCD-ss
∗
property. These new notions enable us, among other things, to analyze the semismoothness of solution maps to inclusions appearing in (
1.3
) by examination of the underlying mapping
F
. Note that, up to now, only results on the semismoothness property of single-valued solution mappings given implicitly by single-valued equations are available, cf.
[
38
,
31
,
16
,
25
,
21
]
.

As already observed by Chen, Sun and Zhang in the very recent paper
[
5
]
, there are some relationships between G-semismoothness and the semismooth
∗
property. We extend these results by showing that a graphically Lipschitzian mapping (i.e., a multifunction whose graph coincides with the graph of a Lipschitz single-valued mapping after a change of coordinates) is SCD-ss
∗
if and only if the transformed mapping is G-semismooth. As a byproduct we obtain that an SCD-ss
∗
mapping is almost everywhere strictly proto-differentiable and the SC derivative is almost everywhere a singleton showing the easiness of its computation there.

Finally, on the basis of the above theoretical results, we will derive for problem
(
1.3
) two implementable algorithmic schemes, enabling us the computation of a semismooth derivative of
\theta
given by (
1.4
) via either the limiting coderivative or the SC derivatives of
F
. The respective oracles will then, in cooperation with a suitable bundle method, generate stationary points even in some situations outside the classical area of applicability of ImP. This is illustrated by an academic example. In a subsequent paper we intend to work out this approach to several instances of (
1.3
) having a practical relevance and to demonstrate its efficiency by numerical experiments.

Let us mention that for single-valued mappings there is a close relation between the semismoothness property and conservative derivatives, the latter being explored in a recent paper by Bolte and Pauwels
[
2
]
. In fact, it was shown in
[
7
]
that both notions are equivalent for semialgebraic maps. As semismooth derivatives also obey a chain rule like conservative Jacobians, the considerations about automatic differentiation from
[
2
]
carry over to our setting.
Recently, a conservative Jacobian for solution maps given implicitly by equations was established in
[
3
]
. In
[
4
]
, a conservative Jacobian for the solution map of a parametric monotone inclusion was obtained by rewriting the inclusion as an equivalent fixed-point equation for the forward-backward map by means of the resolvent. Though the resolvents are explicitly known for a lot of monotone mappings, it is not clear how a conservative Jacobian of the resolvent can be computed in the general case. In fact, the resolvent itself may be considered as the solution map of a shifted inclusion. On the contrary, our approach is based on the differentiation of the underlying set-valued mapping by SC derivatives.

The paper is organized in the following way. Section 2 is divided into several subsections with the first two containing the basic notions of variational analysis and generalized differentiation. In Subsection
2.3
we describe a general principle, analogous to the construction of Clarke generalized Jacobian, how to generate from an arbitrary multifunction another multifunction having some favorable properties. In the next subsection we recall the basic properties of SCD mappings. In Subsection
2.5
we recall the semismooth
∗
property of set-valued mappings and its extension to SCD mappings.

The main new results are presented in Sections 3–5. Section 3 is devoted to the semismoothness property of single-valued mappings with respect to certain multifunctions. This concept is commonly used in semismooth Newton methods for solving nonsmooth equations, in particular in infinite dimensions.
However, in contrast to these applications, we do not consider the case that semismoothness only holds at the solution, but on the whole domain.
There is a close relation with the seminal work of Norkin
[
29
,
30
]
on generalized differentiation of real-valued functions. In Section 4 we present important relationships between the G-semismooth and the SCD-ss
∗
property for graphically Lipschitzian multifunctions. Section 5 concerns the semismooth
∗
property of solution maps to inclusions. We establish the semismooth
∗
property of general solution maps and the semismooth property for continuous localizations and selections, respectively. Finally it will be shown that the analysis considerably simplifies for SCD mappings.

2
Background from variational analysis and preliminary results

2.1
Nonsmooth analysis

Given a parameterized family
C_{t}
of subsets of a metric space, where
t
also belongs to a metric space, we define the upper (outer) and lower(inner) limits in the sense of Painlevé–Kuratowski as

\displaystyle\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{t\to\bar{t}}C_{t}:=\{x\,\big|\,\liminf_{t\to\bar{t}}\,{\rm dist}(x,C_{t})=0\},\ \liminf_{t\to\bar{t}}C_{t}:=\{x\,\big|\,\limsup_{t\to\bar{t}}\,{\rm dist}(x,C_{t})=0\}.

Consider a mapping
F:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an open set. In the sequel we denote by
{\cal O}_{F}
the set of points where
F
is (Fréchet) differentiable and we denote the Jacobian by
\nabla F(x)
,
x\in{\cal O}_{F}
. We use this notation also for scalar-valued functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, i.e.,
\nabla f(x)
is an
1\times n
matrix, i.e., a row vector.

The
B-Jacobian (B-differential)
is defined as

\overline{\nabla}F(\bar{x})=\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{x{\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{{\cal O}_{F}}}\bar{x}}\{\nabla F(x)\},\ \bar{x}\in\Omega.

Then the Clarke generalized Jacobian is given by
\mathrm{conv}\,\overline{\nabla}F(\bar{x})
.

If
F
is locally Lipschitz, by Rademacher’s Theorem the set
\Omega\setminus{\cal O}_{F}
has Lebesgue measure
0
.
Since the Jacobians
\nabla F(x)
,
x\in{\cal O}_{F}
, are also locally bounded
by the Lipschitz constant, we obtain
\emptyset\not=\overline{\nabla}F(x)\subset\mathrm{conv}\,\overline{\nabla}F(x)
,
x\in\Omega
.
Further, we say that
F
is
semismooth
at
\bar{x}\in\Omega
if
F
is directionally differentiable at
\bar{x}
and

\lim_{{\textstyle{x\mathop{\rightarrow}\limits^{\Omega}\bar{x}\atop x\not=\bar{x}}}}\frac{\sup\{\|F(x)-F(\bar{x})-A(x-\bar{x})\|\,\big|\,A\in\mathrm{conv}\,\overline{\nabla}F(x)\}}{\|x-\bar{x}\|}=0.

Note that this definition of semismoothness is different but equivalent to the original ones by Mifflin
[
26
]
(for the scalar case) and by Qi and Sun
[
35
]
. If the requirement of directional differentiability is dropped we arrive at the semismooth property as defined by Gowda
[
16
]
, which is also called
G-semismoothness
in the literature.

We say that
F
is
strictly differentiable
at
\bar{x}\in\Omega
if
F
is Lipschitz near
\bar{x}
and there is an
m\times n
matrix
A
such that

\lim_{{\textstyle{x\to\bar{x}\atop t\downarrow 0}}}\frac{F(x+tu)-F(x)}{t}=Au,\ u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

For a locally Lipschitz function
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
open, we can define the
Clarke subdifferential
as

\partial^{c}f(x):=\mathrm{conv}\,\overline{\nabla}f(x)^{T}:=\{g^{T}\,\big|\,g\in\mathrm{conv}\,\overline{\nabla}f(x)\},\ x\in\Omega.

The elements of
\partial^{c}f(x)
are called
subgradients
and are column vectors.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open and let
F:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be Lipschitz near
\bar{x}\in\Omega
. Then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

F
is strictly differentiable at
\bar{x}
.

(ii)

\overline{\nabla}F(\bar{x})
is a singleton.

(iii)

\lim_{x{\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{{\cal O}_{F}}}\bar{x}}\nabla F(x)
exists.

Proof.

ad (i)
\Rightarrow
(iii): If
F
is strictly differentiable at
\bar{x}
then
\nabla F
is continuous at
\bar{x}
relative to
{\cal O}_{F}
, cf.
[
36
, Exercise 9.64]
. Thus
\lim_{x{\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{{\cal O}_{F}}}\bar{x}}\nabla F(x)
exists (and equals to
\nabla F(\bar{x})
).

ad (iii)
\Rightarrow
(ii): This is evident from the definitions.

ad (ii)
\Rightarrow
(i): Let
\overline{\nabla}F(\bar{x})=\{A\}
. For every
i=1,\ldots,m
, we conclude from
[
6
, Theorem 2.6.6]
that the
i
-th component of
F
,
i=1,\ldots m
, satisfies
\bar{\nabla}F_{i}(\bar{x})=\{A_{i}\}
, where
A_{i}
denotes the
i
-th row of
A
. Thus
\partial^{c}F_{i}(\bar{x})
is a singleton and therefore
F_{i}
is strictly differentiable at
\bar{x}
by
[
6
, Proposition 2.2.4]
. Since every component
F_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots m
, is strictly differentiable at
\bar{x}
, so is
F
as well.

2.2
Variational geometry and differentiation of multifunctions

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
and
\bar{x}\in\Omega
.

(i)

The tangent (contingent, Bouligand) cone
T_{\Omega}(\bar{x})
, the paratingent cone
T^{P}_{\Omega}(\bar{x})
and the regular (Clarke) tangent cone
\widehat{T}_{\Omega}(\bar{x})
to
\Omega
at
\bar{x}
are given by

T_{\Omega}(\bar{x}):=\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}\limits_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{\Omega-\bar{x}}{t},\quad T^{P}_{\Omega}(\bar{x}):=\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}\limits_{{\textstyle{x\mathop{\rightarrow}\limits^{\Omega}\bar{x}\atop t\downarrow 0}}}\frac{\Omega-x}{t},\quad\widehat{T}_{\Omega}(\bar{x}):=\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm inf}}_{{\textstyle{x\mathop{\rightarrow}\limits^{\Omega}\bar{x}\atop t\downarrow 0}}}\frac{\Omega-x}{t}.

(ii)

The set
\widehat{N}_{\Omega}(\bar{x}):=(T_{\Omega}(\bar{x}))^{\circ}
,
i.e. the (negative) polar cone to the tangent cone
T_{\Omega}(\bar{x})
, is the regular (Fréchet) normal cone to
\Omega
at
\bar{x}
, and

N_{\Omega}(\bar{x}):=\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}\limits_{x\mathop{\rightarrow}\limits^{\Omega}\bar{x}}\widehat{N}_{\Omega}(x)

is the limiting (Mordukhovich) normal cone to
\Omega
at
\bar{x}
.

If
\Omega
is convex, then
\widehat{N}_{\Omega}(\bar{x})=N_{\Omega}(\bar{x})
amounts to the classical normal cone in the sense of convex analysis and we will write
N_{\Omega}(\bar{x})
.

The above listed cones enable us to describe the local behavior of set-valued maps via various
generalized derivatives.

Definition 2.3
.

Consider a multifunction
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
and let
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
.

(i)

The multifunction
DF(\bar{x},\bar{y}):\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
given by
\mathrm{gph}\,DF(\bar{x},\bar{y})=T_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is called the graphical derivative of
F
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
.

(ii)

The multifunction
D_{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y}):\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
given by
\mathrm{gph}\,D_{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})=T^{P}_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is called the strict (paratingent) derivative of

at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
.

(iii)

The multifunction
\widehat{D}^{\ast}F(\bar{x},\bar{y}):\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined by

\mathrm{gph}\,\widehat{D}^{\ast}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})=\{(y^{*},x^{*})\,\big|\,(x^{*},-y^{*})\in\widehat{N}_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\}

is called the regular (Fréchet) coderivative of
F
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
.

(iv)

The multifunction
D^{\ast}F(\bar{x},\bar{y}):\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
, defined by

\mathrm{gph}\,D^{\ast}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})=\{(y^{*},x^{*})\,\big|\,(x^{*},-y^{*})\in N_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\}

is called the limiting (Mordukhovich) coderivative of
F
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
.

We call
F

single-valued
, if for every
x\in\mathrm{dom}\,F
the set

is a singleton. In this case, we can omit the second argument and write
DF(x)
,
\widehat{D}^{*}F(x),\ldots
instead of
DF(x,F(x))
,
\widehat{D}^{*}F(x,F(x)),\ldots
.

If a mapping
F:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
open, is differentiable at
\bar{x}\in\Omega
then

DF(\bar{x})(u)=\nabla F(\bar{x})u,

If

, then also

D_{*}F(\bar{x})(u)=\nabla F(\bar{x})u,\ u\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad D^{*}F(\bar{x})(v^{*})=\nabla F(\bar{x})^{T}v^{*},\ v^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

(2.5)

holds, cf.
[
36
]
. A generalization of strict differentiability to general set-valued mappings is provided by the notion of strict proto-differentiability.
According to

Lemma 2.4
.

If
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}

\displaystyle=T_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})=\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{(x,y){\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{gph}\,F}}(\bar{x},\bar{y})}T_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(x,y)=\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm inf}}_{(x,y){\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{gph}\,F}}(\bar{x},\bar{y})}T_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\widehat{T}_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})=\mathrm{gph}\,D_{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y}).

Proof.

By taking into account

) that

\displaystyle\mathrm{gph}\,DF

\displaystyle(\bar{x},\bar{y})=T_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\subset\mathrm{gph}\,D_{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})=\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{{(x,y){\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{gph}\,F}}(\bar{x},\bar{y})},\ {t\downarrow 0}}\frac{\mathrm{gph}\,F-(x,y)}{t}

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm inf}}_{{(x,y){\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{gph}\,F}}(\bar{x},\bar{y})},\ {t\downarrow 0}}\frac{\mathrm{gph}\,F-(x,y)}{t}=\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm inf}}_{(x,y){\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{gph}\,F}}(\bar{x},\bar{y})}T_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(x,y)=\widehat{T}_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\subset T_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y}).

Hence, in order to prove (
2.7
) there remains to show the equality

A:=\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{(x,y){\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{gph}\,F}}(\bar{x},\bar{y})}T_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(x,y)=\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{(x,y){\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{gph}\,F}}(\bar{x},\bar{y}),\ t\downarrow 0}\big(\mathrm{gph}\,F-(x,y)\big)/t=:B.

Clearly, by the inclusions above we have
B=\mathrm{gph}\,D_{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})=T_{\mathrm{gph}}\,F(\bar{x},\bar{y})\subset A
.
By
[
18
, Proposition 2.1(b)]
the reverse inclusion
A\subset B
always holds and the equality
A=B
is established.

Finally, in order to show that
T_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is a subspace, observe first that
T_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})=\mathrm{gph}\,D_{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})=\widehat{T}_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is a convex cone. By
[
18
, Proposition 2.1(a)]
, the strict derivative also satisfies
\mathrm{gph}\,D_{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})=-\mathrm{gph}\,D_{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and thus
T_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is a subspace.
∎

2.3
Closure of mappings

It is well-known that the Clarke subdifferential
\partial^{c}\theta
of a Lipschitz continuous function
\theta:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
open, is upper semicontinuous (usc) and has nonempty, convex and compact values
\partial^{c}\theta(x)
for every
x\in\Omega
.
We now discuss a possibility of how to extend an arbitrary locally bounded mapping to a mapping having these properties.

Given a mapping
\Psi:X\rightrightarrows Y
between two metric spaces
X
and
Y
, we denote by
\mathrm{cl}\,\Psi
the mapping whose graph equals to the closure of
\mathrm{gph}\,\Psi
, i.e.,

\mathrm{gph}\,(\mathrm{cl}\,\Psi)=\mathrm{cl}\,(\mathrm{gph}\,\Psi)=\{(x,y)\,\big|\,(x,y)=\lim_{k\to\infty}(x_{k},y_{k})\mbox{ for some sequence }(x_{k},y_{k})\in\mathrm{gph}\,\Psi\}.

Obviously,
\mathrm{cl}\,(\mathrm{cl}\,\Psi)=\mathrm{cl}\,\Psi
and for every
x\in\mathrm{dom}\,(\mathrm{cl}\,\Psi)
we have that
(\mathrm{cl}\,\Psi)(x)
is closed. Further,
\mathrm{dom}\,(\mathrm{cl}\,\Psi)\subset\mathrm{cl}\,(\mathrm{dom}\,\Psi)
and equality holds, e.g., when the metric space
Y
is compact.

In most cases we consider set-valued mappings between Euclidean spaces.
The mapping
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
is called
locally bounded

at
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
if there is a neighborhood
U
of
\bar{x}
and a nonnegative real
M
such that

\|y\|\leq M\ \forall y\in\Psi(x)\ \forall x\in U.

(2.8)

We denote by
\mathrm{bnd}\,\Psi(\bar{x})
the infimum of all nonnegative reals
M
such that (
2.8
) holds for some neighborhood
U
with the convention
\mathrm{bnd}\,\Psi(\bar{x}):=\infty
if
\Psi
is not locally bounded at
\bar{x}
.
We claim that the function
\mathrm{bnd}\,\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
is usc, i.e.,
\limsup_{x\to\bar{x}}\mathrm{bnd}\,\Psi(x)\leq\mathrm{bnd}\,\Psi(\bar{x})
,
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Indeed, fix any
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. If
\mathrm{bnd}\,\Psi(\bar{x})=\infty
there is nothing to prove. If
\mathrm{bnd}\,\Psi(\bar{x})<\infty
for every
\epsilon>0
we can find an open neighborhood
U
of
\bar{x}
such that (
2.8
) holds with
M=\mathrm{bnd}\,\Psi(\bar{x})+\epsilon
. Then it immediately follows that
\mathrm{bnd}\,\Psi(x)\leq\mathrm{bnd}\,\Psi(\bar{x})+\epsilon
,
x\in U
, verifying upper semicontinuity of
\mathrm{bnd}\,\Psi
at
\bar{x}
because
\epsilon>0
can be taken arbitrarily small.

Further, we denote by
{\rm cocl}\,\Psi
the mapping defined by

({\rm cocl}\,\Psi)(x):=\mathrm{conv}\,\big((\mathrm{cl}\,\Psi)(x)\big),\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

It is easy to see that
\mathrm{dom}\,({\rm cocl}\,\Psi)=\mathrm{dom}\,(\mathrm{cl}\,\Psi)
. Moreover, if
\Psi
is locally bounded at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
then
\mathrm{cl}\,\Psi
and
{\rm cocl}\,\Psi
are also locally bounded at
x
and
({\rm cocl}\,\Psi)(x)
is a convex and compact set. Further, both
\mathrm{cl}\,\Psi
and
{\rm cocl}\,\Psi
are usc at
x
.

2.4
SCD mappings

In this subsection we collect only some basic facts from the theory of SCD-mappings which are needed in this paper. We refer the interested reader to
[
12
,
13
]
for more details and more far-reaching properties.

Let us denote by
{\cal Z}_{n,m}
the metric space of all
n
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
equipped with the metric

d_{{\cal Z}_{n,m}}(L_{1},L_{2}):=\|P_{1}-P_{2}\|,

where
P_{i}
is the symmetric
(n+m)\times(n+m)
matrix representing the orthogonal projection onto
L_{i},i=1,2
. By
[
13
, Lemma 3.1]
,
{\cal Z}_{n,m}
is (sequentially) compact.

For notational reasons, we usually identify
\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
with
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
, i.e., for a subspace
L\in{\cal Z}_{n,m}
we denote its elements by
(u,v)
instead of
\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\end{pmatrix}
. Analogously, given two matrices
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
so that
L={\rm rge\;}\begin{pmatrix}A\\
B\end{pmatrix}
, we write
L={\rm rge\;}(A,B)
.

With each
L\in{\cal Z}_{n,m}
one can associate its
adjoint
subspace
L^{*}
defined by

L^{*}:=\{(-v^{*},u^{*})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\,\big|\,(u^{*},v^{*})\in L^{\perp}\}.

Since
\dim L^{\perp}=m
, it follows that
L^{*}\in{\cal Z}_{m,n}
(i.e., its dimension is
m
).
Further we have
(L^{*})^{*}=L
and
d_{{\cal Z}_{m,n}}(L_{1}^{*},L_{2}^{*})=d_{{\cal Z}_{n,m}}(L_{1},L_{2})
for all subspaces
L_{1},L_{2}\in{\cal Z}_{n,m}
and thus the mapping
L\to L^{*}
is an isometry between
{\cal Z}_{n,m}
and
{\cal Z}_{m,n}
, cf.
[
13
]
.
In what follows, the symbol
L^{*}
signifies both the adjoint subspace to some
L\in{\cal Z}_{n,m}
as well as an arbitrary subspace from
{\cal Z}_{m,n}
. This double role, however, cannot lead to a confusion.

Consider now a mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Definition 2.5
.

We say that
F
is graphically smooth of dimension
n
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
if
T_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in{\cal Z}_{n,m}
. By
\mathcal{O}_{F}

we denote the subset of
\mathrm{gph}\,F
, where
F
is graphically smooth of dimension
n
.

Clearly, for
(x,y)\in\mathcal{O}_{F}
and
L=T_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(x,y)=\mathrm{gph}\,DF(x,y)
it holds that

L^{\perp}=\hat{N}_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(x,y)
and
L^{*}=\mathrm{gph}\,\widehat{D}^{*}F(x,y)
.

Next we introduce the two derivative-like mappings

{\mathcal{S}}F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows{\cal Z}_{n,m}
and
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows{\cal Z}_{m,n}
defined by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}F(x,y):

\displaystyle=\{L\in{\cal Z}_{n,m}\,\big|\,\exists(x_{k},y_{k}){\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{{\cal O}_{F}}}(x,y)\mbox{ such that }\lim_{k\to\infty}d_{{\cal Z}_{n,m}}\big(L,T_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(x_{k},y_{k})\big)=0\},

and

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}^{*}}F(x,y):

\displaystyle=\{L^{*}\,\big|\,L\in{\mathcal{S}}F(x,y)\}.

Both
{\mathcal{S}}F
and
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F
are in fact generalized derivatives of
F
whose elements, by virtue of the above definitions, are subspaces. Further we have the relation

\displaystyle L^{*}\subset\mathrm{gph}\,D^{*}F(x,y)\ \forall L^{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F(x,y),

cf.
[
13
, Eq 15]
.

In what follows
{\mathcal{S}}F
will be called
primal SC (subspace containing) derivative
or
SC limiting graphical derivative
and
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F
will be termed

adjoint SC derivative (SC limiting coderivative)
. Let us mention that in
[
13
]
the SC adjoint derivative
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F
was defined in a different but equivalent manner.

Note that
{\mathcal{S}}F
coincides with the mapping
\mathrm{cl}\,\widehat{\mathcal{S}}F
, where

\widehat{\mathcal{S}}F(x,y):=T_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(x,y),\ (x,y)\in\mathrm{dom}\,\widehat{\mathcal{S}}F:={\cal O}_{F}.

In particular,
\mathrm{gph}\,{\mathcal{S}}F
is closed in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times{\cal Z}_{n,m}
and, due to the isometry
L\leftrightarrow L^{*}
,
\mathrm{gph}\,{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F
is closed in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times{\cal Z}_{m,n}
as well.

On the basis of these SC derivatives we may now introduce the following notion playing a crucial role in the sequel.

Definition 2.6
.

A mapping

F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
is said to have the SCD property at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
, provided
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})\neq\emptyset
.
F
is termed an SCD mapping if it has the SCD property at all points of
\mathrm{gph}\,F
.

By virtue of the definition, the SCD property at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is obviously equivalent with the condition
{\mathcal{S}}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})\not=\emptyset
. Since we consider convergence in the compact metric space
{\cal Z}_{n,m}
we readily obtain that
F
has the SCD property at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
if and only if
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{cl}\,{\cal O}_{F}
.

For SCD mappings, the adjoint SC derivative
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F
forms some kind of skeleton of the limiting coderivative
D^{*}F
. It contains the information which is important in applications, in particular for numerical computations, but is much easier to compute than the limiting coderivative. On the other hand, the limiting coderivative is a more general object important for arbitrary set-valued mappings.

The derivatives
{\mathcal{S}}F
and
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F
can be considered as a generalization of the B-Jacobian to multifunctions. If
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
open, is continuous, then for every
x\in\Omega
there holds

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}f(x):={\mathcal{S}}\big(x,f(x)\big)\supseteq\{{\rm rge\;}(I,A)\,\big|\,A\in\overline{\nabla}f(x)\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}^{*}f(x):={\mathcal{S}}^{*}\big(x,f(x)\big)\supseteq\{{\rm rge\;}(I,A^{T})\,\big|\,A\in\overline{\nabla}f(x)\}.

If
f
is Lipschitz near
x
, these inclusions hold with equality and
f
has the SCD property at all points
x^{\prime}
sufficiently close to
x
, cf.
[
13
, Lemma 3.5]
.

We refer to
[
12
,
13
]
for several calculus rules for SCD mappings.
An important class of SCD mappings is constituted by graphically Lipschitzian mappings:

Definition 2.7

(cf.
[
36
, Definition 9.66]
)
.

A mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
is graphically Lipschitzian of dimension
d
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
if there is an open neighborhood
W
of
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and a one-to-one mapping
\Phi
from
W
onto an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
with
\Phi
and
\Phi^{-1}
continuously differentiable, such that
\Phi(\mathrm{gph}\,F\cap W)
is the graph of a Lipschitz continuous mapping
f:U\to\mathbb{R}^{n+m-d}
, where
U
is an open set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Lemma 2.8

(cf.
[
13
, Corollary 4.3]
)
.

Assume that
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
is graphically Lipschitzian of dimension
n
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
. Then
F
has
the SCD property at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
. Moreover, if
\Phi
and
f
are as in Definition
2.7
, then

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}F(\bar{x},\bar{y}

\displaystyle)=\{\nabla\Phi(\bar{x},\bar{y})^{-1}{\rm rge\;}(I,A)\,\big|\,A\in\overline{\nabla}f(\bar{u})\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})

\displaystyle=\{S_{nm}\nabla\Phi(\bar{x},\bar{y})^{T}S^{T}_{nm}{\rm rge\;}(I,A^{T})\,\big|\,A\in\overline{\nabla}f(\bar{u})\}\mbox{ with }S_{nm}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&-I_{m}\\
I_{n}&0\end{pmatrix},

where
\big(\bar{u},f(\bar{u})\big)=\Phi(\bar{x},\bar{y})
.

Prominent examples for graphically Lipschitzian mappings are the subdifferential mapping
\partial f
for prox-regular and subdifferentially continuous functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(-\infty,\infty]
, cf.
[
36
, Proposition 13.46]
or, more generally, locally maximally hypomonotone mappings, cf.
[
12
]
.

2.5
On semismooth
∗
mappings

The following definition goes back to the recent paper
[
11
]
.

Definition 2.9
.

A set-valued mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
is called semismooth
∗
at a point
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
, if for every
\epsilon>0
there is some
\delta>0
such that

\displaystyle|\langle x^{*},x-\bar{x}\rangle-\langle y^{*},y-\bar{y}\rangle|

(2.9)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\epsilon\|(x,y)-(\bar{x},\bar{y})\|\|(y^{*},x^{*})\|\ \forall(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F\cap{\cal B}_{\delta}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\ \forall(y^{*},x^{*})\in\mathrm{gph}\,D^{*}F(x,y).

Note that the semismooth
∗
property was defined in
[
11
]
in a different but equivalent way. The class of semismooth
∗
mappings is rather broad. For instance, every mapping whose graph is either the union of finitely many convex sets or a closed subanalytic set, is semismooth
∗
at every point of its graph
[
12
, Proposition 2.10]
.

A locally Lipschitz mapping
F:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
open, is semismooth
∗
at
\bar{x}
if and only if
F
is G-semismooth at
\bar{x}

[
11
, Proposition 3.7]
. However, there are also single-valued mappings which are semismooth
∗
but neither locally Lipschitz and nor G-semismooth, e.g., the function
x\mapsto\sqrt{|x|}
at
0
.

Sometimes it is useful to replace
\mathrm{gph}\,D^{*}F(x,y)
in the relation (
2.9
) by another set.

Definition 2.10
.

Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\Gamma:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
be mappings and let
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
. We say that
F
is semismooth
∗
with respect to
\Gamma
(or shortly
\Gamma
-ss
∗
) at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
, if the following three conditions are fulfilled:

1.

\Gamma
is cone-valued,

2.

there is some neighborhood
U
of
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
such that
\mathrm{gph}\,F\cap U\subset\mathrm{dom}\,\Gamma
,

3.

for every
\epsilon>0
there is some
\delta>0
such that

\displaystyle|\langle x^{*},x-\bar{x}\rangle-\langle y^{*},y-\bar{y}\rangle|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\epsilon\|(x,y)-(\bar{x},\bar{y})\|\|(y^{*},x^{*})\|\ \forall(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F\cap{\cal B}_{\delta}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\ \forall(y^{*},x^{*})\in\Gamma(x,y).

We say that
F
is
\Gamma
-ss
∗
around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
if
F
is
\Gamma
-ss
∗
for all
(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
sufficiently close to
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
. Finally,
F
is called
\Gamma
-ss
∗
if
F
is
\Gamma
-ss
∗
at every
(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
.

Note that the semismooth
∗
property introduced in Definition
2.9
amounts to the semismooth
∗
property of
F
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
with respect to
\Gamma(x,y)=\mathrm{gph}\,D^{*}F(x,y)
.

The notion of
\Gamma
-semismoothness
∗
has two main applications. Firstly, the calculus rules for coderivatives often yield only inclusions of the form
D^{*}F(x,y)(y^{*})\subset\Sigma(x,y)(y^{*})
with some closed-graph mapping
\Sigma
.
However, as in our motivating application, sometimes it is not necessary to compute the exact limiting coderivative but elements from
\Sigma
related to the semismooth
∗
property. Thus, if we can show that
F
is
\Gamma
-ss
∗
with respect to the mapping
\Gamma(x,y)=\mathrm{gph}\,\Sigma(x,y)
then we can also work with
\Sigma
instead of
D^{*}F
.

Secondly, the
\Gamma
-ss
∗
property will be useful in connection with the theory of SCD mappings.

Definition 2.11
.

We say that the mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
is SCD-ss
∗
at (around)
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
if it is semismooth
∗
at (around)
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
with respect to the mapping

\bigcup{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
given by

(\bigcup{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F)(x,y):=\bigcup_{L^{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F(x,y)}L^{*}.

The mapping
F
is called SCD-ss
∗
if it is SCD-ss
∗
at every point of its graph.

It follows from the definition of the
\Gamma
-ss
∗
property that
F
has the SCD property around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
whenever
F
is SCD-ss
∗
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
.

The SCD-ss
∗
property has also a useful primal interpretation:

Proposition 2.12

(cf.
[
9
, Proposition 2.12]
)
.

Assume that
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
has the SCD property around the pair
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
. Then the following two statements are equivalent:

1.

F
is SCD-ss
∗
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
.

2.

\limsup\limits_{(x,y){\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{gph}\,F}}(\bar{x},\bar{y})}\ \sup\limits_{L\in{\mathcal{S}}F(x,y)}{{\rm dist}((x-\bar{x},y-\bar{y}),L)}/{\|(x-\bar{x},y-\bar{y})\|}=0.

3
On
\Psi
-semismooth single-valued mappings

The following definition is closely related to the definition of semismooth operators in Banach spaces, cf.
[
39
, Definition 3.1]
.

Definition 3.1
.

Consider the mapping
F:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is open, and the multifunction
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
.

1.

We say that
F
is semismooth with respect to
\Psi
(shortly,
\Psi
-ss) at
\bar{x}\in\Omega
if
\mathrm{dom}\,\Psi
is a neighborhood of
\bar{x}
,
\Psi
is locally bounded at
\bar{x}
and

\lim_{{\textstyle{x\mathop{\rightarrow}\limits^{\Omega}\bar{x}\atop x\not=\bar{x}}}}\frac{\sup\{\|F(x)-F(\bar{x})-A(x-\bar{x})\|\,\big|\,A\in\Psi(x)\}}{\|x-\bar{x}\|}=0.

(3.10)

2.

We say that
F
is
\Psi
-ss on a subset
U\subset\Omega
if
F
is
\Psi
-ss at every
x\in U
. The mapping
\Psi
is called a semismooth derivative of
F
on
U
.

By this definition, a locally Lipschitz mapping
F
is
\mathrm{conv}\,\overline{\nabla}F
-ss if and only if it is G-semismooth.

Our definition of semismoothness differs from Ulbrich’s one
[
39
, Definition 3.1]
insofar as we do not suppose local continuity of
F
but require local boundedness of
\Psi
.
If
F
is
\Psi
-ss at
\bar{x}
then
\Psi
is also called a
Newton map
for
F
at
\bar{x}
in
[
20
]
. If
\Psi
is single-valued then it is also called a Newton derivative (or slanting function) for
F
at
\bar{x}
in the literature. We dispense with using this terminology because they are normally used at single points, namely solutions of the nonsmooth equation
F(x)=0
. However, for our purposes it is essential that the properties hold not only at one single point
\bar{x}
but also on a neighborhood of
\bar{x}
. For instance, for solving (
1.1
) with the BT-algorithm, we will show below that any semismooth derivative of
\theta
on an open superset of
U_{\rm ad}
is a feasible choice for the oracle.

In the literature one can find also several papers about the
\Psi
-ss property under some additional assumptions on
\Psi
: For instance, if
F
is
\Psi
-ss at
\bar{x}
,
\Psi(x)
is compact for all
x
close to
\bar{x}
and
\Psi
is usc at
\bar{x}
, then
F
is called
C-differentiable
at
\bar{x}
and
\Psi
is called a
C-differential operator
of
F

[
34
]
.

Though we do not explicitly require (Lipschitz) continuity of
F
in Definition
3.1
, it is implied by (
3.10
) together with the boundedness of
\Psi
. It is easy to see that a mapping
F:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
open, which is
\Psi
-ss at
\bar{x}\in\Omega
, is calm at
\bar{x}
and
\mathrm{clm}\,F(\bar{x})\leq\mathrm{bnd}\,\Psi(\bar{x})
. Further, as we will show below in Theorem
3.8
, if
F
is
\Psi
-ss on
\Omega
, then it is also locally Lipschitz there. The main reason for omitting the requirement of Lipschitz continuity in Definition
3.1
is that we want to deal also with mappings which are implicitly defined by solution mappings of generalized equations, where verifying Lipschitz continuity with conventional tools like limiting coderivatives might be a difficult issue.

There is a big difference between pointbased semismoothnes and semismoothness on a neighborhood. For instance, given any mapping
F:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
open, and
\bar{x}\in\Omega
, the mapping
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
given by
\mathrm{dom}\,\Psi=\Omega
and

\Psi(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{(F(x)-F(\bar{x}))(x-\bar{x})^{T}}{\|x-\bar{x}\|^{2}}&\mbox{if $x\in\Omega\setminus\{\bar{x}\}$,}\\
0&\mbox{if $x=\bar{x}$}\end{cases}

obviously satisfies (
3.10
). If
F
is calm at
\bar{x}
then
\Psi
is also locally bounded at
\bar{x}
and therefore
F
is
\Psi
-ss at
\bar{x}
. However,
F
will not be
\Psi
-ss on any neighborhood of
\bar{x}
, except for very special cases. As an example consider
F:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,
F(x):=x^{2}
and
\bar{x}:=0
. The formula above gives
\Psi(x)=x
and

\lim_{{\textstyle{x\to x^{\prime}\atop x\not=x^{\prime}}}}\frac{\|F(x)-F(x^{\prime})-\Psi(x)(x-x)\|}{\|x-x^{\prime}\|}=\lim_{{\textstyle{x\to x^{\prime}\atop x\not=x^{\prime}}}}\frac{|x^{2}-x^{\prime 2}-x(x-x^{\prime})|}{|x-x^{\prime}|}=|x^{\prime}|\not=0

for every
x^{\prime}\not=\bar{x}
.

Moreover, in the pointbased setting we may modify a semismooth derivative
\Psi
by adding matrices of order
{\cal O}(\|F(x)-F(\bar{x})\|)

to
\Psi(x)
without violating (
3.10
). This issue is sometimes exploited in semismooth Newton methods for finding zeros of
F
by neglecting terms of order
{\cal O}(\|F(x)\|)
, see, e.g.
[
32
]
. However, with such a modification the semismoothness property on a neighborhood will no longer hold.

If a mapping
F:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
open, is
\Psi
-ss at
\bar{x}\in\Omega
, it follows immediately from the definition that each component
F_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,m
, is semismooth at
\bar{x}
with respect to

\Psi_{i}(x):=\{e_{i}^{T}A\,\big|\,A\in\Psi(x)\},

(3.11)

where
e_{i}
denotes the
i
-th unit vector. Conversely. if each component
F_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,m
, is semismooth at
\bar{x}\in\Omega
with respect to some mapping
\Psi_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{1\times n}
, then
F
is semismooth at
\bar{x}
with respect to

\Psi(x):=\{A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\,\big|\,e_{i}^{T}A\in\Psi_{i}(x)\}.

(3.12)

This construction resembles the one of Qi’s C-subdifferential, see, e.g.
[
39
, Proposition 2.1]
.

Clearly, continuously differentiable mappings
F
are
\nabla F
-ss.

It is well-known that Newton maps satisfy a chain rule, see, e.g.,
[
20
, Theorem 6.14]
. For the sake of completeness we state the following result, where in contrast to
[
20
]
we do not assume Lipschitz continuity of the involved mappings
F_{1},F_{2}
:

Lemma 3.2
.

Consider the mappings
F_{1}:U\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
F_{2}:V\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, where
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
are open sets with
F_{1}(U)\subset V
. Assume that
F_{1}
is semismooth at
\bar{x}\in U
with respect to the mapping
\Psi_{1}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
and that
F_{2}
is semismooth at
F_{1}(\bar{x})
with respect to the mapping
\Psi_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
. Then the composite mapping
F:U\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
given by
F(x):=F_{2}(F_{1}(x))
is
\Psi
-ss at
\bar{x}
with
\Psi
given by

\Psi(x):=\{BA\,\big|\,A\in\Psi_{1}(x),\ B\in\Psi_{2}(F_{1}(x))\}.

Proof.

By calmness of
F_{1}
at
\bar{x}
and calmness of
F_{2}
at
F_{1}(\bar{x})
we can find neighborhoods
\tilde{U}\subset U
of
\bar{x}
and
\tilde{V}\subset V
of
F_{1}(\bar{x})
together with two positive reals
\kappa_{1},\ \kappa_{2}
such that
\|F_{1}(x)-F_{1}(\bar{x})\|\leq\kappa_{1}\|x-\bar{x}\|

\forall x\in\tilde{U}
,
\|F_{2}(y)-F_{2}(F_{1}(\bar{x}))\|\leq\kappa_{2}\|y-F_{1}(\bar{x})\|

\forall y\in\tilde{V}
and
F_{1}(\tilde{U})\subset\tilde{V}
. By possibly shrinking
\tilde{U}
,
\tilde{V}
and enlarging
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}
we may also assume that
\mathrm{bnd}\,\Psi_{1}(x)\leq\kappa_{1}

\forall x\in\tilde{U}
and
\mathrm{bnd}\,\Psi_{2}(y)\leq\kappa_{2}

\forall y\in\tilde{V}
. Next, for arbitrary
\epsilon>0
we choose a neighborhood
V_{\epsilon}\subset\tilde{V}
of
F_{1}(\bar{x})
such that for each
y\in V_{\epsilon}
and each
B\in\Psi_{2}(y)
one has
\|F_{2}(y)-F_{2}(F_{1}(\bar{x}))-B(y-F_{1}(\bar{x}))\|\leq\epsilon\|y-F_{1}(\bar{x})\|
. Finally we choose a neighborhood
U_{\epsilon}\subset\tilde{U}
such that
F_{1}(U_{\epsilon})\subset V_{\epsilon}
and for every
x\in U_{\epsilon}
and every
A\in\Psi_{1}(x)
one has
\|F_{1}(x)-F_{1}(\bar{x})-A(x-\bar{x})\|\leq\epsilon\|x-\bar{x}\|
. Thus, for every
x\in U_{\epsilon}
, every
A\in\Psi_{1}(x)
and every
B\in\Psi_{2}(F_{1}(x))
we obtain

\displaystyle\|F(x)-F(\bar{x})-BA(x-\bar{x})\|

\displaystyle\leq\|F_{2}(F_{1}(x))-F_{2}(F_{1}(\bar{x}))-B(F_{1}(x)-F_{1}(\bar{x}))\|+\|B\big(F_{1}(x)-F_{1}(\bar{x})-A(x-\bar{x})\big)\|

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon\|F_{1}(x)-F_{1}(\bar{x})\|+\kappa_{2}\epsilon\|x-\bar{x}\|\leq\epsilon(\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2})\|x-\bar{x}\|.

In order to show the local boundedness of
\Psi
at
\bar{x}
just note that for every
x\in\tilde{U}
and every
BA\in\Psi(x)
we have
\|BA\|\leq\|B\|\|A\|\leq\kappa_{2}\kappa_{1}
. This proves that
F
is
\Psi
-ss at
\bar{x}
.
∎

Using similar arguments one can prove the following lemma.

Lemma 3.3
.

Consider an open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, two mappings
F_{i}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
and two mappings
\Psi_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
i=1,2
. If
F_{i}
is
\Psi_{i}
-ss,
i=1,2
, at
\bar{x}\in\Omega
then
F_{1}+F_{2}
is
(\Psi_{1}+\Psi_{2})
-ss at
\bar{x}
.

The next auxiliary statement is important in the application we have in mind.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
F:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
open, be continuous and assume that
F
is semismooth at
\bar{x}\in\Omega
with respect to
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
. Then
F
is also
\mathrm{cl}\,\Psi
-ss and
{\rm cocl}\,\Psi
-ss at
\bar{x}
.

Proof.

Let
\epsilon>0
be arbitrarily fixed. By definition we can find some
\delta>0
such that there holds

\|F(x)-F(\bar{x})-A(x-\bar{x})\|\leq\epsilon\|x-\bar{x}\|\ \forall x\in{\cal B}_{\delta}(\bar{x})\ \forall A\in\Psi(x).

For every
x\in{\cal B}_{\delta/2}(\bar{x})\setminus\{\bar{x}\}
and
A\in(\mathrm{cl}\,\Psi)(x)
we can find some sequence
(x_{k},A_{k}){\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{gph}\,\Psi}}(x,A)
. Since
x_{k}\in{\cal B}_{\delta}(\bar{x})
for all
k
sufficiently large, we can conclude

\frac{\|F(x)-F(\bar{x})-A(x-\bar{x})\|}{\|x-\bar{x}\|}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\|F(x_{k})-F(\bar{x})-A_{k}(x_{k}-\bar{x})\|}{\|x_{k}-\bar{x}\|}<\epsilon,

verifying that
F
is
\mathrm{cl}\,\Psi
-ss at
\bar{x}
. The
{\rm cocl}\,\Psi
-ss property of
F
at
\bar{x}
follows now easily from the fact that any matrix
A\in({\rm cocl}\,\Psi)(x)
can be written as convex combination
A=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}A_{i}
with
A_{i}\in(\mathrm{cl}\,\Psi)(x)
and
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}=1
,
\lambda_{i}\geq 0
,
i=1,\ldots,N
.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

As a consequence of this lemma we obtain that a continuous mapping
F:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
open, which is
\Psi
-ss at
\bar{x}\in\Omega
, is also C-differentiable at
\bar{x}
with C-differential operator
\mathrm{cl}\,\Psi
or
{\rm cocl}\,\Psi
. Further, for any sequence
x_{k}\mathop{\rightarrow}\limits^{\Omega}\bar{x}
we can select a bounded sequence
A_{k}\in\Psi(x_{k})
and, by passing to a subsequence
x_{k_{j}}
such that
A_{k_{j}}
converges to some
A\in(\mathrm{cl}\,\Psi)(\bar{x})
, we obtain that

F(x_{k_{j}})-F(\bar{x})-A(x_{k_{j}}-\bar{x})=o(\|x_{k_{j}}-\bar{x}\|),

i.e.,
F
is H-differentiable at
\bar{x}
with H-differential
(\mathrm{cl}\,\Psi)(\bar{x})
in the sense of
[
17
]
.

Consider now the following definition.

Definition 3.6

(cf.
[
29
,
30
]
)
.

A function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is called generalized differentiable if there exists an usc mapping
G:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
with nonempty, convex and compact values
G(x)
such that
for every
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
there holds

\sup_{g\in G(x)}|f(x)-f(\bar{x})-\langle g,x-\bar{x}\rangle|=o(\|x-\bar{x}\|).

The elements of
G(x)
are called pseudogradients.

By Lemma
3.4
, a
\Psi
-ss function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is also generalized differentiable with

G(x)=\{g\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,\big|\,g^{T}\in({\rm cocl}\,\Psi)(x)\}.

Note that pseudogradients are column vectors whereas the elements of
\Psi
are
1\times n
matrices, i.e., row vectors.

Generalized differentiable functions have a lot of interesting properties, cf.
[
29
,
30
,
27
]
, which can be carried over to
\Psi
-semismooth functions
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
open. For the sake of completeness we state some of these properties in the following proposition.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open and let
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be semismooth on
\Omega
with respect to the mapping
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{1\times n}
. Then the following statements hold true.

(i)

f
is locally Lipschitz on
\Omega
and for every
x\in\Omega
there holds
\partial^{c}f(x)^{T}\subset({\rm cocl}\,\Psi)(x)
.

(ii)

Let
\Omega_{\Psi}
denote the set of all
x\in\Omega
such that
({\rm cocl}\,\Psi)(x)
is a singleton. Then
\Omega\setminus\Omega_{\Psi}
has Lebesgue measure zero. Moreover, the function
f
is strictly differentiable at every
x\in\Omega_{\Psi}
with
\{\nabla f(x)\}=({\rm cocl}\,\Psi)(x)=\Psi(x)
.

Proof.

ad (i): The assertions have been shown in
[
27
, Theorems 1.1, 1.10]
for the special case
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{n}
and it is an easy task to carry over the proofs to general open sets
\Omega
.

ad (ii): In order to show that the set
\Omega\setminus\Omega_{\Psi}
is negligible, one can take over the proof of
[
27
, Theorem 1.12]
to show that for every compact cube
K(x,\delta):=\{x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,\big|\,\max_{i}|x^{\prime}_{i}-x_{i}|\leq\delta\}\subset\Omega
the set
K(x,\delta)\setminus\Omega_{\Psi}
is negligible. Consider the countable collection
K(x_{1},\delta_{1}),K(x_{2},\delta_{2}),\ldots
of cubes contained in
\Omega
with rational
x_{i}
and rational
\delta_{i}
. This collection covers
\Omega
and therefore the set
\Omega\setminus\Omega_{\Psi}
is negligible as the countable union of the negligible sets
K(x_{i},\delta_{i})\setminus\Omega_{\Psi}
.

For every
x\in\Omega_{\Psi}
we conclude from (i) that
\partial^{c}f(x)=({\rm cocl}\,\Psi)(x)=\Psi(x)
is a singleton and thus
f
is strictly differentiable at
x
with
\partial^{c}f(x)=\{\nabla f(x)\}
by
[
6
, Proposition 2.2.4]
.
∎

We are now in the position to state the main result of this section.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open and assume that
F:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is semismooth on
\Omega
with respect to a mapping
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
. Then the following statements hold:

(i)

F
is locally Lipschitz on
\Omega
.

(ii)

For every
x\in\Omega
there holds
\emptyset\not=\mathrm{conv}\,\overline{\nabla}F(x)\subset({\rm cocl}\,\Psi)(x)
.

(iii)

Let
\Omega_{\Psi}
denote the set of all
x\in\Omega
such that
({\rm cocl}\,\Psi)(x)
is a singleton. Then
\Omega\setminus\Omega_{\Psi}
has Lebesgue measure zero. Moreover, the mapping
F
is strictly differentiable at every
x\in\Omega_{\Psi}
with
\mathrm{conv}\,\overline{\nabla}F(x)=\{\nabla F(x)\}=({\rm cocl}\,\Psi)(x)=\Psi(x)
.

Proof.

ad (i): Each component
F_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,m
, is semismooth on
\Omega
with respect to
\Psi_{i}
given by (
3.11
) and therefore locally Lipschitz on
\Omega
by Proposition
3.7
. Hence,
F
is locally Lipschitz on
\Omega
as well.

ad (ii): Let
\Pi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
denote the mapping which assigns to each
x
the set of all
m\times n
matrices whose
i
-th row belongs to
({\rm cocl}\,\Psi_{i})(x)
. Clearly,

({\rm cocl}\,\Psi)(x)\subset\Pi(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Next let
\tilde{\Omega}:=\bigcap_{i=i}^{m}\Omega_{\Psi_{i}}
. For every
x\in\tilde{\Omega}
we obtain from Proposition
3.7
(ii) that for every
i=1,\ldots,m
the component function
F_{i}
is strictly differentiable at
x
and
({\rm cocl}\,\Psi_{i})(x)=\{\nabla F_{i}(x)\}
is a singleton. Hence,
F
is also strictly differentiable at
x
and
({\rm cocl}\,\Psi)(x)=\Pi(x)=\{\nabla F(x)\}
. Since the set
\Omega\setminus\tilde{\Omega}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}(\Omega\setminus\Omega_{\Psi_{i}})
is negligible, the set
\tilde{\Omega}
differs from the set
{\cal O}_{F}
, the set of all points where
F
is differentiable, by a negligible set. By defining the mapping
\nabla_{s}F(x):=\nabla F(x)
for
x\in\mathrm{dom}\,\nabla_{s}F:=\tilde{\Omega}

and taking into account
\mathrm{conv}\,\overline{\nabla}F={\rm cocl}\,\nabla F
, it follows from
[
40
, Theorem 4]
that
\mathrm{conv}\,\overline{\nabla}F={\rm cocl}\,\nabla_{s}F
. Since
\nabla_{s}F(x)\subset({\rm cocl}\,\Psi)(x)

\forall x\in\Omega
, we obtain
\mathrm{conv}\,\overline{\nabla}F\subset\big({\rm cocl}\,({\rm cocl}\,\Psi)\big)(x)=({\rm cocl}\,\Psi)(x)

\forall x\in\Omega
.

ad (iii): Since
\tilde{\Omega}\subset\Omega_{\Psi}\subset\Omega
and the set
\Omega\setminus\tilde{\Omega}
is negligible, so must be the set
\Omega\setminus\Omega_{\Psi}
as well. For every
x\in\Omega_{\Psi}
we deduce from (ii) that
\mathrm{conv}\,\overline{\nabla}F(x)
is also a singleton and therefore
F
is strictly differentiable at
x
by Lemma
2.1
.
∎

Corollary 3.9
.

Let
F:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a mapping, where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is open. Then the following two statements are equivalent and entail that

is strictly differentiable on
\Omega
up to a set of Lebesgue measure zero.

1.

F
is semismooth on
\Omega
with respect to some mapping
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
.

2.

is locally Lipschitz and
\mathrm{conv}\,\overline{\nabla}F
-ss on

, i.e., G-semismooth.

Let us return to problem (
1.1
) under the additional assumption that
U_{\rm ad}
is a convex polyhedron. The latter condition enables us to solve (

) numerically via the BT-algorithm which admits only

given by affine linear inequalities and equalities.

Assume that
\theta
is locally Lipschitz and suppose that at each iteration of the BT-algorithm instead of the oracle (
1.2
) one uses another

oracle
, which returns for arbitrary

the function value
\theta(x)
and one element

with

,

is fulfilled, which ensures that a certain line-search procedure in the BT-algorithm is finite. Then, as already observed by

4
On strict proto-differentiability and the SCD-ss
∗
property of graphically Lipschitzian mappings

At first we state the relationship between strict proto-differentiability of a mapping and the property that its SC derivative is a singleton.

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume that the mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
has the SCD property at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
. If
F
is strictly proto-differentiable at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and
\mathrm{gph}\,F
is locally closed at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
then
{\mathcal{S}}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is a singleton.
If, in addition,
F
is graphically Lipschitzian of dimension
n
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
then the reverse implication holds true as well.

Proof.

Assume that
F
is strictly proto-differentiable, then we know by Lemma
2.4
that
\bar{L}:=T_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is a subspace. Further, from (
2.7
) we conclude that

\bar{L}\supset\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{(x,y){\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{{\cal O}_{F}}}(\bar{x},\bar{y})}T_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(x,y)\supset\bigcup_{L\in{\mathcal{S}}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})}L\not=\emptyset.

Hence,
\bar{n}:=\dim\bar{L}\geq n
and if
\bar{n}=n
then
{\mathcal{S}}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})=\{\bar{L}\}
follows. Now assume that
\bar{n}>n
and choose an orthogonal basis
w_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,\bar{n}
, of
\bar{L}
. Since
\bar{z}:=(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in(\mathrm{cl}\,{\cal O}_{F})\setminus{\cal O}_{F}
, there must be a sequence
z_{k}:=(x_{k},y_{k}){\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{{\cal O}_{F}}}(\bar{x},\bar{y})
satisfying
z_{k}\not=\bar{z}

\forall k
. Taking into account (
2.7
), for every
k
sufficiently large and every
i=1,\ldots,\bar{n}
, we can find elements
w_{i}^{k}\in L_{k}:=T_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(z_{k})
with
\|w_{i}^{k}-w_{i}\|<1/\sqrt{\bar{n}}
. We claim now that for those
k
the vectors
w_{i}^{k}
,
i=1,\ldots,\bar{n}
, are linearly independent. Assume on the contrary, that for some large
k
these vectors are linearly dependent and we can find reals
\lambda_{i}^{k}
,
i=1,\ldots,\bar{n}
, with

\sum_{i=1}^{\bar{n}}\lambda_{i}^{k}w_{i}^{k}=0
and
\sum_{i=1}^{\bar{n}}(\lambda_{i}^{k})^{2}=1.

Then by orthogonality of
w_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,\bar{n}
, together with the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we obtain that

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\|\sum_{i=1}^{\bar{n}}\lambda_{i}^{k}w_{i}\|\leq\|\sum_{i=1}^{\bar{n}}\lambda_{i}^{k}w_{i}^{k}\|+\|\sum_{i=1}^{\bar{n}}\lambda_{i}^{k}(w_{i}-w_{i}^{k})\|

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{\bar{n}}(\lambda_{i}^{k})^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{\bar{n}}\|w_{i}-w_{i}^{k}\|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}<1\cdot(\sum_{i=1}^{\bar{n}}\frac{1}{\bar{n}})^{1/2}=1,

a contradiction. Thus
w_{i}^{k}
,
i=1,\ldots,\bar{n}
, are linearly independent and
\dim L_{k}\geq\bar{n}
follows. However, by definition of the set
{\cal O}_{F}
we have
\dim L_{k}=n
, a contradiction. Hence
\bar{n}=n
and
{\mathcal{S}}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})=\{\bar{L}\}
holds true.

To show the second assertion, assume that

is graphically Lipschitzian of dimension
n
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and let

,
\Phi
,
U
and
f
be as in Definition

. Note that for all sequences

(x_{k},y_{k}){\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{gph}\,F\cap W}}(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and

such that

\lim_{k\to\infty}{((\hat{x}_{k},\hat{y}_{k})-(x_{k},y_{k}))}/{t_{k}}=:(\xi,\eta)

exists, we have that
\Phi(x_{k},y_{k})=:(u_{k},f(u_{k})){\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{gph}\,f}}(\bar{u},f(\bar{u}))=\Phi(\bar{x},\bar{y})

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{(\hat{u}_{k},f(\hat{u}_{k}))-(u_{k},f(u_{k}))}{t_{k}}

Proposition 4.2
.

Assume that the mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
is graphically Lipschitzian of dimension
n
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
and let
\Phi
and
f
be as in Definition
2.7
. Then
F
is SCD-ss
∗
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
if and only if
f
is
\overline{\nabla}f
-semismooth at
\bar{u}
, where
(\bar{u},f(\bar{u}))=\Phi(\bar{x},\bar{y})
.

Proof.

Consider
z:=(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F\cap W
and the corresponding pair
(u,f(u))=\Phi(z)
, where
W
is as in Definition
2.7
. Let
L\in{\mathcal{S}}F(z)
and
A\in\overline{\nabla}f(u)
such that
L=\nabla\Phi(z)^{-1}({\rm rge\;}(I,A))
according to Lemma
2.8
. Then, setting
\bar{z}:=(\bar{x},\bar{y})
, we obtain that

\displaystyle{\rm dist}(z-\bar{z},L)

\displaystyle=\inf_{p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|\nabla\Phi(z)^{-1}\big(\nabla\Phi(z)(z-\bar{z})-(p,Ap)\big)\|

\displaystyle=\inf_{p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|\nabla\Phi(z)^{-1}\big(\Phi(z)-\Phi(\bar{z})+o(\|z-\bar{z}\|)-(p,Ap)\big)\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla\Phi(z)^{-1}\|\big(\inf_{p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|(u-\bar{u}-p,f(u)-f(\bar{u})-Ap)\|+o(\|z-\bar{z}\|)\big)

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla\Phi(z)^{-1}\|\big(\|f(u)-f(\bar{u})-A(u-\bar{u})\|+o(\|z-\bar{z}\|)\big).

On the other hand there holds

\displaystyle\inf_{p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|(u-\bar{u}-p,f(u)-f(\bar{u})-Ap)\|^{2}=\inf_{q\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|q\|^{2}+\|f(u)-f(\bar{u})-A(u-\bar{u})-Aq\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\|w\|^{2}}{\|A\|^{2}}+\|f(u)-f(\bar{u})-A(u-\bar{u})-w\|^{2}=\frac{1}{1+\|A\|^{2}}\|f(u)-f(\bar{u})-A(u-\bar{u})\|^{2},

resulting in

\displaystyle{\rm dist}(z-\bar{z},L)

\displaystyle=\inf_{p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|\nabla\Phi(z)^{-1}\big(\Phi(z)-\Phi(\bar{z})+o(\|z-\bar{z}\|)-(p,Ap)\big)\|

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|\nabla\Phi(z)^{-1}(u-\bar{u}-p,f(u)-f(\bar{u})-Ap)\|-\|\nabla\Phi(z)^{-1}\|o(\|z-\bar{z}\|)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\|\nabla\Phi(z)\|}\inf_{p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|(u-\bar{u}-p,f(u)-f(\bar{u})-Ap)\|-\|\nabla\Phi(z)^{-1}\|o(\|z-\bar{z}\|)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\|\nabla\Phi(z)\|\sqrt{1+\|A\|^{2}}}\|f(u)-f(\bar{u})-A(u-\bar{u})\|-\|\nabla\Phi(z)^{-1}\|o(\|z-\bar{z}\|).

Since
\|A\|
is bounded by the Lipschitz constant
\kappa
of
f
and

\|u-\bar{u}\|\begin{cases}\geq\frac{\|(u-\bar{u},f(u)-f(\bar{u}))\|}{\sqrt{1+\kappa^{2}}}\geq\frac{\|z-\bar{z}\|}{\|\nabla\Phi(z)^{-1}\|\sqrt{1+\kappa^{2}}}+o(\|z-\bar{z}\|)&,\\
\leq\|(u-\bar{u},f(u)-f(\bar{u}))\|\leq\|\nabla\Phi(z)\|\|z-\bar{z}\|+o(\|z-\bar{z}\|),\end{cases}

we conclude from these inequalities that

\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{z{\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{gph}\,F}}\bar{z}}\sup_{L\in{\mathcal{S}}F(z)}\frac{{\rm dist}(z-\bar{z},L)}{\|z-\bar{z}\|}=0\ \Leftrightarrow\ \limsup_{u\to\bar{u}}\sup_{A\in\overline{\nabla}f(u)}\frac{\|f(u)-f(\bar{u})-A(u-\bar{u})\|}{\|u-\bar{u}\|}=0

and the assertion follows from Proposition
2.12
and the definition of
\overline{\nabla}f
-semismoothness.
∎

\Theta:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{1\times n}

\Theta(x):=\{g_{x}^{T}+{x^{*}}^{T}\,\big|\,x^{*}\in\Sigma(x,\sigma(x))(g_{y}),(g_{x}^{T},g_{y}^{T})\in\Phi(x,\sigma(x))\},\ x\in\mathrm{dom}\,\Theta:=\Omega.

This corollary enables us to apply the ImP approach to the numerical solution of a class of problems (
1.3
) with nonsmooth objectives
\varphi
via a suitable bundle method. Apart from the case when
\sigma
is a single-valued and Lipschitz localization of
S
for all
x\in\Omega
, however, the construction of
\Psi
according to Remark
5.4
is rather complicated. We will now show that the theory considerably simplifies in the case of SCD mappings.

For an SCD mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the subspaces
L^{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F(x,y,z)
belong to
{\cal Z}_{m,n+m}
and therefore
L^{*}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m+n+m}
. By identifying
\mathbb{R}^{m+n+m}
with
\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we denote the elements of
L^{*}
by
(z^{*},x^{*},y^{*})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
We start our analysis with the following auxiliary result:

Proposition 5.6
.

For every subspace
L^{*}\in{\cal Z}_{m,{n+m}}
satisfying the implication

(z^{*},x^{*},0)\in L^{*}\Rightarrow z^{*}=0,\ x^{*}=0

(5.20)

there are unique matrices
Z_{L^{*}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
and
X_{L^{*}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
such that

L^{*}={\rm rge\;}(Z_{L^{*}},X_{L^{*}},I)=\{(Z_{L^{*}}p,X_{L^{*}}p,p)\,\big|\,p\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\}.

(5.21)

In addition, there holds

\kappa(L^{*}):=\big\|\begin{pmatrix}Z_{L}^{*}\\
X_{L^{*}}\end{pmatrix}\big\|=\sup\{\|(z^{*},x^{*})\|\,\big|\,(z^{*},x^{*},y^{*})\in L^{*},\|y^{*}\|\leq 1\}.

Proof.

Consider an
(m+n+m)\times m
matrix
B
whose columns form a basis for the
m
-dimensional subspace
L^{*}\subset R^{m+n+m}
. We can partition
B
into the matrices
B_{z^{*}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
,
B_{x^{*}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
and
B_{y^{*}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
so that

B=\begin{pmatrix}B_{z^{*}}\\
B_{x^{*}}\\
B_{y^{*}}\end{pmatrix}.

We claim that condition (
5.20
) ensures the nonsingularity of the matrix
B_{y^{*}}
. Assume on the contrary that there is some
0\not=p\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
satisfying
B_{y^{*}}p=0
. Then we have

Bp=(B_{z^{*}}p,B_{x^{*}}p,0)\in L^{*}
and consequently
B_{z^{*}}p=0
,
B_{x^{*}}p=0
due to (
5.20
). Thus
Bp=0
, which is not possible since the columns of
B
form a basis for
L^{*}
and are therefore linearly independent. Hence the matrix
B_{y^{*}}
is nonsingular and the columns of the matrix
BB_{y^{*}}^{-1}
form another basis for
L^{*}
. Representation (
5.21
) now follows with

Z_{L^{*}}=B_{z^{*}}B_{y^{*}}^{-1},\ X_{L^{*}}=B_{x^{*}}B_{y^{*}}^{-1}

and it is easy to see that it is unique. Finally,

\sup\{\|(z^{*},x^{*})\|\,\big|\,(z^{*},x^{*},y^{*})\in L^{*},\|y^{*}\|\leq 1\}=\sup\{\|(Z_{L^{*}}y^{*},X_{L^{*}}y^{*})\|\,\big|\,\|y^{*}\|\leq 1\}=\big\|\begin{pmatrix}Z_{L}^{*}\\
X_{L^{*}}\end{pmatrix}\big\|

and we are done.
∎

Let us denote by
{\cal Z}_{m,n+m}^{\rm reg}
the collection of all subspaces
L^{*}\in{\cal Z}_{m,n+m}
satisfying (
5.20
).
In the following statements we collect some useful relations concerning the subspaces
L^{*}
from
{\cal Z}_{m,n+m}^{\mathrm{reg}\,}
and the moduli
\kappa(L^{*})
and
{\rm scd\,reg\,}F(x,y,z)
(introduced below).

Lemma 5.7
.

Assume that
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
has the SCD property at
(x,y,z)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
. Then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

(z^{*},x^{*},0)\in(\bigcup{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F)(x,y,z)\Rightarrow z^{*}=0,\ x^{*}=0.

(ii)

{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F(x,y,z)\subset{\cal Z}_{m,n+m}^{\rm reg}
.

(iii)

{\rm scd\,reg\,}F(x,y,z):=\sup\{\|(z^{*},x^{*})\|\,\big|\,(z^{*},x^{*},y^{*})\in(\bigcup{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F)(x,y,z),\|y^{*}\|\leq 1\}<\infty
.

In this case one also has

{\rm scd\,reg\,}F(x,y,z):=\sup\{\kappa(L^{*})\,\big|\,L^{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F(x,y,z)\}.

(5.22)

Proof.

The implication (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii) follows immediately from Proposition
5.6
. We prove the implication (ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii). Assuming that (iii) does not hold there are sequences
(z_{k}^{*},x_{k}^{*},y_{k}^{*})\in L_{k}^{*}\subset(\bigcup{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F)(x,y,z)
with
\|y_{k}^{*}\|\leq 1
for all
k
and
\|(z_{k}^{*},x_{k}^{*})\|\to\infty
as
k\to\infty
. Since
L^{*}_{k}
is a subspace, we have
(\tilde{z}^{*}_{k},\tilde{x}^{*}_{k},\tilde{y}^{*}_{k}):=(z_{k}^{*},x_{k}^{*},y_{k}^{*})/\|(z_{k}^{*},x_{k}^{*},y_{k}^{*})\|\in L_{k}^{*}
for all
k
. Taking into account that the metric space
{\cal Z}_{m,n+m}
is compact, by possibly passing to a subsequence we can assume that
L_{k}^{*}
converges to some
L^{*}\in{\cal Z}_{m,n+m}
and
(\tilde{z}^{*}_{k},\tilde{x}^{*}_{k},\tilde{y}^{*}_{k})
converges to some
(\tilde{z}^{*},\tilde{x}^{*},\tilde{y}^{*})\in L^{*}
with
\|(\tilde{z}^{*},\tilde{x}^{*},\tilde{y}^{*})\|=1
. Since
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F(x,y,z)
is closed in
{\cal Z}_{m,n+m}
, there holds
L^{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F(x,y,z)
and because of
\tilde{y}_{k}^{*}/\|(z_{k}^{*},x_{k}^{*},y_{k}^{*})\|\to 0
we have
\tilde{y}^{*}=0
. Hence
(\tilde{z}^{*},\tilde{x}^{*},0)\in L^{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F(x,y,z)
and
\|(\tilde{z}^{*},\tilde{x}^{*})\|=1
showing
L^{*}\not\in{\cal Z}_{m,n+m}^{\rm reg}
. This proves the implication (ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii). The implication (iii)
\Rightarrow
(i) holds trivially. Finally, formula (
5.22
) is a consequence of the definitions of
{\rm scd\,reg}F
,
(\bigcup{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F)
and
\kappa(L^{*})
.
∎

By following the proof of
[
12
, Proposition 4.8]
one can easily show the following result.

Proposition 5.8
.

Assume that
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
has the SCD property around some point
(x,y,z)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
. Then

\limsup_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime}){\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{gph}\,F}}(x,y,z)}{\rm scd\,reg\,}F(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\leq{\rm scd\,reg\,}F(x,y,z).

The next statement pertains to the
\Psi
-ss property of a single-valued continuous selection of (
5.14
) in the SCD case.

Theorem 5.9
.

Consider a mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
and a point
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
with
0\in F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
. Assume that
F
has the SCD property around
(\bar{x},\bar{y},0)
and
{\rm scd\,reg\,}F(\bar{x},\bar{y},0)<\infty
. Further suppose that there exists a neighborhood
\Omega
of
\bar{x}
and a continuous function
\sigma:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
\sigma(\bar{x})=\bar{y}
and
0\in F(x,\sigma(x))
,
x\in\Omega
. Then there exists an open neighborhood
U\subset\Omega
of
\bar{x}
such that for every
x\in U
there holds
{\rm scd\,reg\,}F(x,\sigma(x),0)<\infty
and
\sigma
is semismooth at
x
with respect to the mapping
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
given by

\Psi(x):=\{-X_{L^{*}}^{T}\,\big|\,L^{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F(x,\sigma(x),0)\},\ x\in\mathrm{dom}\,\Psi:=U,

provided
F
is SCD semismooth
∗
at
(x,\sigma(x),0)
.

Proof.

Due to the definition of the SCD property around a point and Proposition
5.8
we can find an open neighborhood
U^{\prime}\times V^{\prime}\times W^{\prime}
of
(\bar{x},\bar{y},0)
such that for every
(x,y,z)\in U^{\prime}\times V^{\prime}\times W^{\prime}
the mapping
F
has the SCD property around
(x,y,z)
and
{\rm scd\,reg\,}F(x,y,z)<\infty
. Next consider the open neighborhood
U^{\prime\prime}:=\{x\in\Omega\cap U^{\prime}\,\big|\,\sigma(x)\in V^{\prime}\}
and choose an open neighborhood
U
of
\bar{x}
satisfying
\mathrm{cl}\,U\subset U^{\prime\prime}
. This choice of
U
ensures that
{\rm scd\,reg\,}F(x,\sigma(x),0)<\infty
,
x\in U
. We now show that
\Psi
is locally bounded at
x\in U
. Indeed, we have
\gamma:={\rm scd\,reg\,}F(x,\sigma(x),0)<\infty
and, due to the continuity of
\sigma
and Proposition
5.8
, we can find a neighborhood
\tilde{U}\subset\Omega
of
x
satisfying
{\rm scd\,reg\,}F(x^{\prime},\sigma(x^{\prime}),0)<\gamma+1

\forall x^{\prime}\in\tilde{U}
. Hence,
\|X^{*}\|<\gamma+1
for every
X^{*}\in\Psi(x^{\prime})
and our claim is established. In order to show the last assertion, consider
x\in U
such that
F
is SCD semismooth
∗
at
(x,\sigma(x),0)
, set
\gamma:={\rm scd\,reg\,}F(x,\sigma(x),0)
and choose
\tilde{\delta}>0
such that
{\rm scd\,reg\,}F(x^{\prime},\sigma(x^{\prime}),0)<\gamma+1

\forall x^{\prime}\in{\cal B}_{\tilde{\delta}}(x)
. By definition of the SCD semismooth
∗
property we can find for every
\epsilon>0
some
\delta\in(0,\tilde{\delta})
such that for every
x^{\prime}\in{\cal B}_{\delta}(x)
, every subspace
L^{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F(x^{\prime},\sigma(x^{\prime}),0)
and every
p\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
we have

\displaystyle|\langle Z_{L^{*}}p,0-0\rangle-\langle X_{L^{*}}p,x^{\prime}-x\rangle

\displaystyle-\langle p,\sigma(x^{\prime})-\sigma(x)\rangle|=|\langle p,X_{L^{*}}^{T}(x^{\prime}-x)+\sigma(x^{\prime})-\sigma(x)\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon\|(x^{\prime}-x,\sigma(x^{\prime})-\sigma(x),0-0)\|\|(Z_{L^{*}}p,X_{L^{*}}p,p)\|

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon(\|x^{\prime}-x\|+\|\sigma(x^{\prime})-\sigma(x)\|)\sqrt{1+\kappa(L^{*})^{2}}\|p\|

and
\kappa(L^{*})\leq\gamma+1
. This shows that

\|X_{L^{*}}^{T}(x^{\prime}-x)+\sigma(x^{\prime})-\sigma(x)\|\leq\epsilon(\|x^{\prime}-x\|+\|\sigma(x^{\prime})-\sigma(x)\|)\gamma^{\prime}

with
\gamma^{\prime}=\sqrt{1+(1+\gamma)^{2}}
. Thus
(1-\epsilon\gamma^{\prime})\|\sigma(x^{\prime})-\sigma(x)\|\leq\|x^{\prime}-x\|(\|X_{L^{*}}^{T}\|+\epsilon\gamma^{\prime})

implying
\|\sigma(x^{\prime})-\sigma(x)\|\leq 2\|x^{\prime}-x\|((\gamma+1)+1/2)

whenever
\epsilon\in(0,1/(2\gamma^{\prime}))
. Hence

\|X_{L^{*}}^{T}(x^{\prime}-x)+\sigma(x^{\prime})-\sigma(x)\|\leq\epsilon\|x^{\prime}-x\|\gamma^{\prime}(2\gamma+4),

verifying the
\Psi
-ss property of
\sigma
at
x
. This completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 5.10
.

Consider a mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
and a continuous mapping
\sigma:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
open, satisfying
0\in F(x,\sigma(x))
,
x\in\Omega
. Further assume that for every
x\in\Omega
the mapping
F
is SCD semismooth
∗
at
(x,\sigma(x),0)
and
\mathrm{scd\,reg}\,F(x,\sigma(x),0)<\infty
. Then
\sigma
is semismooth on
\Omega
with respect to the mapping
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
given by

\Psi(x):=\{-X_{L^{*}}^{T}\,\big|\,L^{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F(x,\sigma(x),0)\},\ x\in\mathrm{dom}\,\Psi:=\Omega.

In particular, for every function
\varphi:D\to\mathbb{R}
defined on an open set
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
containing
\mathrm{gph}\,\sigma
, which is semismooth on
\mathrm{gph}\,\sigma
with respect to some mapping
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{1\times(n+m)}
, there holds that the function
\theta:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
,
\theta(x):=\varphi(x,\sigma(x))
is semismooth on
\Omega
with respect to the mapping
\Theta:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{1\times n}
given by

\Theta(x):=\{g_{x}^{T}-g_{y}^{T}X_{L^{*}}^{T}\,\big|\,(g_{x}^{T},g_{y}^{T})\in\Phi(x,\sigma(x)),L^{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F(x,\sigma(x),0)\},\ x\in\mathrm{dom}\,\Theta:=\Omega.

Note that both in Corollary
5.5
and Corollary
5.10
the
\Psi
-semismoothness of
\sigma
on
\Omega
implies that
\sigma
is locally Lipschitz and almost everywhere differentiable on
\Omega
.
However, in Corollary
5.5
we require that
\sigma
is some continuous localization of the solution map
S
, whereas in Corollary
5.10
the mapping
\sigma
only needs to be any continuous
selection
of
S
. In case of a single-valued mapping
F
, a similar result has already been stated in
[
21
]
. However, since most implicit function theorems (see, e.g.,
[
16
,
25
,
31
]
) guarantee existence of a
locally unique implicit function, it is clear that the assumptions of such theorems cannot
be satisfied in situations where
\sigma
is not locally unique.

To illustrate Corollary
5.10
consider the following academic example.

Example 5.11
.

Consider the functions
f_{1},f_{2}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and the mapping
F:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}
given by

f_{1}(x,y):=\frac{1}{2}y^{2}-xy,\ f_{2}:=-\frac{1}{2}y^{2},\ F(x,y):=\partial^{c}_{y}f(x,y)\ \mbox{with}\ f(x,y):=\max\{f_{1}(x,y),f_{2}(x,y)\}.

(5.23)

Note that the respective solution mapping
S(x)=\{y\,\big|\,0\in F(x,y)\}
amounts to the C-stationary-point mapping of the nonsmooth program
\min_{y}f(x,y)
parameterized by
x
.
For every
x\in\mathbb{R}
we have

f(x,y)=\begin{cases}f_{2}(x,y)&\mbox{for $y\in[x^{-},x^{+}]$},\\
f_{1}(x,y)&\mbox{for $y\not\in(x^{-},x^{+})$,}\end{cases}

where
x^{-}:=\min\{x,0\}
and
x^{+}:=\max\{x,0\}
. Thus, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{gph}\,\partial^{c}_{y}f(x,\cdot)

\displaystyle=\{(y,y-x)\,\big|\,y\not\in[x^{-},x^{+}]\}\cup\{(y,-y)\,\big|\,y\in(x^{-},x^{+})\}

\displaystyle\qquad\cup(\{x^{-}\}\times[x^{-}-x,-x^{-}])\cup(\{x^{+}\}\times(-x^{+},x^{+}-x))

and we see that
S(x)=\{x,0\}
does not possess a single-valued localization around
(0,0)
. Let us consider the continuous selection
\sigma(x)=\{x\}=\mathop{\rm arg\,min}_{y}f(x,y)
. We compute now the SC derivative
{\mathcal{S}}F(x,x,0)
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
. By the above representation of
\mathrm{gph}\,\partial^{c}_{y}f(x,\cdot)
it follows that
{\cal O}_{F}
is the union of the four sets

\displaystyle O_{1}:=\{(x,y,y-x)\,\big|\,x\in\mathbb{R},y\not\in[x^{-},x^{+}]\},\ O_{2}:=\{(x,y,-y)\,\big|\,x\not=0,y\in(x^{-},x^{+})\}

\displaystyle O_{3}:=\{(x,x,z^{*})\,\big|\,x\not=0,z^{*}\in(x^{-}-x,x^{+}-x)\},\ O_{4}:=\{(x,0,z^{*})\,\big|\,x\not=0,z^{*}\in(-x^{+},-x^{-})\}.

Moreover, defining the four subspaces

\displaystyle L_{1}:=\{(u,v,v-u)\,\big|\,(u,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\},\ L_{2}:=\{(u,v,-v)\,\big|\,(u,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\},

\displaystyle L_{3}:=\{(u,u,v)\,\big|\,(u,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\},\ L_{4}:=\{(u,0,v)\,\big|\,(u,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\},

we have that
T_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(x,y,z^{*})=L_{k}
for all
(x,y,z^{*})\in O_{k}
and
k=1,\ldots,4
. Hence,
{\mathcal{S}}F(x,\sigma(x),0)=\{L_{1},L_{3}\}
for
x\not=0
and
{\mathcal{S}}F(0,0,0)=\{L_{1},L_{2},L_{3},L_{4}\}
.
All the adjoint subspaces

L_{1}^{*}={\rm rge\;}(1,-1,1),\ L_{2}^{*}={\rm rge\;}(-1,0,1),\ L_{3}^{*}={\rm rge\;}(0,-1,1),\ L_{4}^{*}={\rm rge\;}(0,0,1)

belong to
{\cal Z}_{1,2}^{\rm reg}
and thus we can conclude from Corollary
5.10
that
\sigma
is semismooth with respect to

\Psi(x):=\begin{cases}\{1\}&\mbox{if $x\not=0$,}\\
\{1,0\}&\mbox{if $x=0$.}\end{cases}

(5.24)

Note that in accordance with Theorem
3.8
(iii) the mapping
\Psi
is almost everywhere single-valued, although
{\mathcal{S}}F(x,\sigma(x),0)
does not have this property for any
x
.

These results enable us to apply the considered ImP approach to the following nonsmooth bilevel program

\min_{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}}\varphi(x,y):=2\big|y-|x_{1}|\big|-|x_{2}|+\frac{1}{2}x_{1}^{2}\quad\mbox{subject to}\quad y\in\mathop{\rm arg\,min}f(\eta(x),\cdot),

given by
\eta(x)=|x_{1}|-|x_{2}|
. The solution of this bilevel program is
\bar{x}=(0,0)
,
\bar{y}=0
and we solved it by applying the BT algorithm to the (reduced)

\min\theta(x):=\varphi(x,\sigma(\eta(x))),

where
\sigma(\xi):=\mathop{\rm arg\,min}_{y}f(\xi,y)
is evaluated by solving the nonlinear program

\min_{z,y}z\quad\mbox{subject to}\quad z\geq f_{1}(\xi,y),\ z\geq f_{2}(\xi,y)

|\cdot|
is G-semismooth, i.e.,

t\mapsto{\rm sgn}(t):=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if $t\geq 0$,}\\

0&\mbox{if $\xi=0$}\end{cases}

of

(x_{1},x_{2})\mapsto\big(2{\rm sgn}(u(x))(\tilde{\Psi}(\eta(x))-1){\rm sgn}(x_{1})+x_{1},-2{\rm sgn}(u(x))\tilde{\Psi}(\eta(x)){\rm sgn}(x_{2})-{\rm sgn}(x_{2})\big),

where

.
We did not make any attempt to compute exact Clarke subgradients of

6
Conclusion

The paper contains a deep analysis of some generalizations of the classical semismoothness property. Namely, it deals with the so-called
\Psi
-semismoothness and new notions of
\Gamma
-semismoothness
∗
and SCD-semismoothness
∗
. These notions are closely related to each other and have a number of useful properties concerning, in particular, some fundamental numerical approaches to nonsmooth problems. For instance, whereas the standard rules of generalized differential calculus attain typically the form of inclusions, the calculus of semismooth derivatives yields again a semismooth derivative. This is especially helpful in connection with a family of bundle methods in nonsmooth optimization.

A special attention has been paid to the semismoothness of localizations and selections of solution maps to parameter-dependent inclusions and their possible compositions with real-valued objectives. The presented analysis of these mappings paves the way to an efficient extension of the ImP approach to a broader class of problems with equilibrium constraints.
