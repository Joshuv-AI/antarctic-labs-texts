ON THE STABILITY OF GENERALIZED HYPERBOLIC OPERATORS


Abstract
. Our main goal is to prove that every invertible generalized hyperbolic operator on a Banach space has a stability property, known as time-dependent stability, which was introduced by J. M. Franks (Invent. Math. 24 (1974), 163–172) and is stronger than structural stability.

1.
Introduction

Structural stability is one of the most important concepts in the modern theory of dynamical systems and differential equations.
It was originally introduced by A. Andronov and L. Pontrjagin
[
1
]
for a certain class of differentiable flows on the plane.
Later, variations of the original concept were introduced and investigated in different contexts.
In the area of differentiable dynamics, a diffeomorphism
f:M\to M
on a compact manifold is said to be
structurally stable

if every diffeomorphism
g:M\to M
that is sufficiently close to
f
in the
C^{1}
-topology is
topologically conjugate
to
f
, that is,
there exists a homeomorphism
h:M\to M
such that
g=h\circ f\circ h^{-1}
.
The importance of this concept lies in the fact that models of physical systems are only approximations of the true systems
and it is important to know whether the qualitative behaviors of the approximation and the true dynamical system are the same.
We refer the reader to
[
15
,
22
,
23
]
for nice expositions about structural stability.

J. Franks
[
10
]
observed that, in most physical situations, it seems likely that the true dynamical system is not really autonomous
but is instead to a certain extent time dependent.
So, he proposed the following concept of stability:
A diffeomorphism
f:M\to M
on a compact manifold is said to be
time-dependent stable
if there is a neighborhood
\mathcal{N}
of
f

in the
C^{1}
-topology such that
g_{1}\circ\cdots\circ g_{p}
is topologically conjugate to
f^{p}
whenever
g_{1},\ldots,g_{p}\in\mathcal{N}

(an important point here is that
\mathcal{N}
is independent of
p
).
It is clear that this concept is stronger than structural stability.

In the present note we are interested in the dynamics of invertible operators on Banach spaces.
Our main motivation comes from the following classical result.

Hartman’s theorem.

Every invertible hyperbolic operator on a Banach space is structurally stable.

The name “Hartman’s theorem” for the above result was coined by C. Pugh
[
21
]
.
Actually, P. Hartman
[
14
]
established it for operators on finite-dimensional euclidean spaces.
The extension to arbitrary Banach spaces was independently obtained by J. Palis
[
20
]
and C. Pugh
[
21
]

(both were motivated by an argument due to J. Moser
[
19
]
).
This theorem is the main tool for proving the celebrated
Grobman-Hartman theorem

on the local linearization of diffeomorphisms around hyperbolic fixed points in Banach spaces
[
20
,
21
]
.
We refer the reader to
[
13
,
15
,
18
,
23
]
for more details on this important

linearization theorem
and its applications.

A basic question is whether or not the converse of
Hartman’s theorem
is always true.
It was soon realized that the answer is positive in the finite-dimensional setting (see J. Robbin
[
22
]
),
but the full question remained open for more than 50 years.
This question was finally answered in the negative in the recent paper
[
6
]
of the author with A. Messaoudi,
where the first examples of structurally stable operators that are not hyperbolic were obtained.
A little later, the same authors proved that every invertible generalized hyperbolic operator on a Banach space is structurally stable

[
5
, Theorem 1]
, which implied a
generalized Grobman-Hartman theorem

[
5
, Theorem 3]
.
Moreover, in the recent papers
[
3
,
16
]
, it was independently shown that
every invertible generalized hyperbolic operator on a Banach space is also
topologically stable
,
a well-known concept of stability introduced by P. Walters
[
24
]
.

In short, the significant developments in the field of linear dynamics in recent decades have motivated some authors
to revisit stability theory in this context
[
2
,
3
,
5
,
6
,
16
,
17
]
.
Several relevant results have already been obtained, and we believe that this branch of linear dynamics will continue
to undergo significant development for many years to come.
The present note contains another contribution to this line of investigation.
We address the following basic question related to
Hartman’s theorem
:

Is every invertible hyperbolic operator on a Banach space time-dependent stable?

Our main goal is to give a positive answer to this question. Actually, we will establish the following more general result.

Theorem 1
.

Every invertible generalized hyperbolic operator on a Banach space is time-dependent stable.

In fact, this theorem will be derived from an even more general result (Theorem
4
).

The note is organized as follows.
In Section
2
we present all the necessary preliminaries for the present work.
In Section
3
we prove a refinement of Theorem
1
, namely Theorem
4
,
in which we also analyze the problem of uniqueness of the homeomorphism that establishes the conjugation
and the possibility of choosing this homeomorphism in such a way that we have continuous dependence with respect to the perturbation.
Some additional remarks are also presented at the end of the section.

2.
Preliminaries

Throughout
\mathbb{K}
denotes either the field
\mathbb{R}
of real numbers or the field
\mathbb{C}
of complex numbers,

\mathbb{N}
denotes the set of all positive integers and
\mathbb{N}_{0}\!:=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.

All Banach spaces are assumed to be over
\mathbb{K}
, unless otherwise specified.

Given a continuous linear map
T
from a Banach space
X
into a Banach space
Y
,
recall that the
norm
of
T
is the non-negative real number given by

\|T\|=\sup_{\|x\|\leq 1}\|T(x)\|.

This notion will play an important role in the next section.
If
F:X\to Y
is any map, we define

\|F\|_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in X}\|F(x)\|\ \ \text{ and }\ \ \operatorname{Lip}(F)=\sup_{x\neq x^{\prime}}\frac{\|F(x)-F(x^{\prime})\|}{\|x-x^{\prime}\|}\,\cdot

We say that
F
is a
bounded map
if
\|F\|_{\infty}<\infty
and it is a
Lipschitz map
if
\operatorname{Lip}(F)<\infty
.

A continuous linear map from a Banach space
X
into itself will be called simply an
operator
on
X
.
We denote by
I_{X}
the
identity operator
on
X
.
In this note we are interested in the dynamics of
invertible operators
.

Recall that an invertible operator
T
on a Banach space
X
is said to be
hyperbolic
if its spectrum

\sigma(T)
does not intersect the unit circle in the complex plane.
It is well known that
T
is hyperbolic if and only if there are an equivalent norm
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\cdot\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}
on
X
and a direct sum decomposition

X=X_{s}\oplus X_{u},\ T=T_{s}\oplus T_{u},

called the
hyperbolic splitting
of
T
, such that
X_{s}
and
X_{u}
are closed
T
-invariant subspaces of
X

(the
stable
and the
unstable subspaces
for
T
),

T_{s}=T|_{X_{s}}
is a proper contraction (i.e.,
\|T_{s}\|<1
) and
T_{u}=T|_{X_{u}}
is a proper dilation (i.e.,
\|T_{u}^{-1}\|<1
),
where the norms
\|T_{s}\|
and
\|T_{u}^{-1}\|
are calculated with respect to the new norm
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\cdot\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}
on
X
.

Recall that an invertible operator
T
on a Banach space
X
is said to be
generalized hyperbolic

if there is a direct sum decomposition

X=M\oplus N,

where
M
and
N
are closed subspaces of
X
with the following properties:

(GH1)

T(M)\subset M
and
\sigma(T|_{M})\subset\mathbb{D}
,

(GH2)

T^{-1}(N)\subset N
and
\sigma(T^{-1}|_{N})\subset\mathbb{D}
,

where
\mathbb{D}
denotes the open unit disc in the complex plane.
This class of operators appeared in the statement of
[
4
, Theorem A]
, which was the major tool to prove
the existence of operators that have the shadowing property but are not hyperbolic
[
4
, Theorem B]
.
However, the terminology “generalized hyperbolic” was introduced in
[
8
]
,
where additional dynamical properties of these operators were investigated
(see also
[
3
,
5
,
7
]
).

In the definition of the concept of structural stability, it is usual to consider perturbations that are small in the sense of the
C^{1}
-topology.
However, in certain situations, some authors allow more general perturbations, namely:
Lipschitz perturbations with small sup norm and small Lipschitz constant (see
[
21
]
, for instance).
As in
[
5
,
6
]
, we adopt the following definition of structural stability in the setting of linear dynamics.

Definition 2
.

An invertible operator
T
on a Banach space
X
is said to be
structurally stable
if there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

T+L\text{ is topologically conjugate to }T

whenever
L:X\to X
is a Lipschitz map with
\|L\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon
and
\operatorname{Lip}(L)<\varepsilon
.

Therefore, it is natural to adopt the following definition of time-dependent stability in the present context.

Definition 3
.

An invertible operator
T
on a Banach space
X
is said to be
time-dependent stable
if there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

(T+L_{1})\circ\cdots\circ(T+L_{p})\text{ is topologically conjugate to }T^{p}

whenever
p\in\mathbb{N}
and
L_{1},\ldots,L_{p}
are Lipschitz maps from
X
into itself with

\|L_{j}\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon
and
\operatorname{Lip}(L_{j})<\varepsilon
for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
.

3.
Main result

Let us now state and prove our main result, which clearly implies Theorem
1
.

Theorem 4
.

For any invertible generalized hyperbolic operator
T
on any Banach space
X
, the following properties hold:

(A)

There exists
\varepsilon>0
such that for any
p\in\mathbb{N}
and any bounded Lipschitz maps
L_{j}:X\to X
,
j=1,\ldots,p
, with

\operatorname{Lip}(L_{j})<\varepsilon\ \text{ for all }j\in\{1,\ldots,p\},

there exists a homeomorphism
h:X\to X
satisfying

(T+L_{1})\circ\cdots\circ(T+L_{p})=h\circ T^{p}\circ h^{-1}\ \text{ and }\ \ \|h-I_{X}\|_{\infty}<\infty.

(B)

Given
\delta>0
, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that for any
p\in\mathbb{N}
and any Lipschitz maps
L_{j}:X\to X
,

j=1,\ldots,p
, with

\|L_{j}\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon\ \text{ and }\ \operatorname{Lip}(L_{j})<\varepsilon\ \text{ for all }j\in\{1,\ldots,p\},

there exists a homeomorphism
h:X\to X
satisfying

(T+L_{1})\circ\cdots\circ(T+L_{p})=h\circ T^{p}\circ h^{-1}\ \text{ and }\ \ \|h-I_{X}\|_{\infty}<\delta.

Moreover, the homeomorphism
h
given in (A) (or (B)) is unique if and only if the operator
T
is hyperbolic.
Nevertheless, it is always possible to choose such a homeomorphism
h_{\mathcal{L}}
for each
p
-tuple
\mathcal{L}=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{p})
of maps
as in (A) (resp. (B)) in such a way that the correspondence
\mathcal{L}\to h_{\mathcal{L}}
is uniformly continuous in the following sense:
given
\gamma>0
, there exists
\eta>0
such that

\|h_{\mathcal{L}}-h_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}\|_{\infty}<\gamma

whenever
\mathcal{L}=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{p})
and
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}=(L^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,L^{\prime}_{p})
are
p
-tuples of maps as in (A) (resp. (B)) with

\|L_{j}-L^{\prime}_{j}\|_{\infty}<\eta\ \text{ for all }j\in\{1,\ldots,p\}.

Note that the assumption that the
L_{j}
’s are small in the sup norm is not necessary to guarantee that

(T+L_{1})\circ\cdots\circ(T+L_{p})
is topologically conjugate to
T^{p}
,
the important assumption is that the
L_{j}
’s are bounded maps with small Lipschitz constants.
However, by adding the hypothesis that the
L_{j}
’s are small in the sup norm,
we can ensure that the homeomorphism that establishes the conjugation can be chosen to be uniformly close to the identity map.

In the proof below we combine ideas from
[
5
]
and
[
10
]
with some new ideas.

Proof.

Fix an invertible generalized hyperbolic operator
T
on a Banach space
X
. Let

(1)

X=M\oplus N

be a direct sum decomposition given by the definition of generalized hyperbolicity and consider the closed subspace of
X
given by

Y=M+T^{-1}(N).

By the spectral radius formula, there exist
a\geq 1
and
t\in(0,1)
such that

(2)

\|T^{n}(y)\|\leq a\,t^{n}\|y\|\ \text{ and }\ \|T^{-n}(z)\|\leq a\,t^{n}\|z\|\ \text{ whenever }n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},y\in M,z\in N.

Let
P_{M}:X\to M
and
P_{N}:X\to N
denote the canonical projections associated to the direct sum decomposition (
1
) and define

(3)

b=\max\{\|P_{M}\|,\|P_{N}\|\}.

Choose
\delta\in(0,1)
. We shall prove that the number

\varepsilon=\min\Big\{\frac{1-t}{a\,b\,(1+t)},\frac{1}{\|T^{-1}\|}\Big\}\,\delta

satisfies properties (A) and (B). Indeed, fix
p\in\mathbb{N}
and a
p
-tuple
\mathcal{L}=(L_{0},\ldots,L_{p-1})
of bounded Lipschitz maps

L_{j}:X\to X
such that

\operatorname{Lip}(L_{j})<\varepsilon\ \text{ for all }j\in\{0,\ldots,p-1\}.

Let
S_{j}=T+L_{j}
for each
j
. Since
\operatorname{Lip}(L_{j})<\|T^{-1}\|^{-1}
, we have that
S_{j}
is a homeomorphism.
We shall prove that
S_{p-1}\circ\cdots\circ S_{0}
is topologically conjugate to
T^{p}
.
For this purpose, let
\mathbb{Z}_{p}=\{0,\ldots,p-1\}
endowed with the operation
+_{p}
of addition mod
p
and with the trivial metric
d_{0}
.
Consider the product
\widetilde{X}=X\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}
endowed with the metric

d((x,j),(x^{\prime},j^{\prime}))=\max\{\|x-x^{\prime}\|,d_{0}(j,j^{\prime})\}.

For
(x,j),(x^{\prime},j)\in\widetilde{X}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{K}
, we define

(x,j)\pm(x^{\prime},j)=(x\pm x^{\prime},j),\ \ \ \lambda(x,j)=(\lambda x,j)\ \ \text{ and }\ \ |(x,j)|=\|x\|.

Note that the above operations of “addition” and “subtraction” on
\widetilde{X}
are defined only for pairs of points with the same second coordinate.
Moreover, we define

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(x_{k},j)=\Big(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}x_{k}\,,j\Big),

provided
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}x_{k}
is a convergent series in
X
. We will need the function spaces defined below:

•

\mathcal{F}
is the set of all continuous bounded maps
F:\widetilde{X}\to\widetilde{X}
such that

F(X\times\{j\})\subset Y\times\{j\}\ \text{ for all }j\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}.

•

\mathcal{G}
is the set of all continuous bounded maps
F:\widetilde{X}\to\widetilde{X}
such that

F(X\times\{j\})\subset X\times\{j+_{p}1\}\ \text{ for all }j\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}.

We recall that
F:\widetilde{X}\to\widetilde{X}
is a
bounded map
if its range
F(\widetilde{X})
is a bounded set in
\widetilde{X}
,
i.e., a set with finite diameter.
Although
\widetilde{X}
is not a vector space and
|\cdot|
is not a norm, both
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
are Banach spaces
when endowed with the algebraic operations defined pointwise

(F_{1}+F_{2})(x,j)=F_{1}(x,j)+F_{2}(x,j)\ \ \text{ and }\ \ (\lambda F)(x,j)=\lambda F(x,j),

and the supremum norm

|F|_{\infty}=\sup_{(x,j)\in\widetilde{X}}|F(x,j)|.

Note that the zero vector of
\mathcal{F}
(resp.
\mathcal{G}
) is the map given by

O_{\mathcal{F}}(x,j)=(0,j)\ \ \ \ (\text{resp.}\ O_{\mathcal{G}}(x,j)=(0,j+_{p}1)).

Let
Q_{M}:\widetilde{X}\to M\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}
and
Q_{N}:\widetilde{X}\to N\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}
be given by

Q_{M}(x,j)=(P_{M}(x),j)\ \ \text{ and }\ \ Q_{N}(x,j)=(P_{N}(x),j).

Consider the homeomorphisms
\widetilde{T}:\widetilde{X}\to\widetilde{X}
and
S:\widetilde{X}\to\widetilde{X}
defined by

\widetilde{T}(x,j)=(T(x),j+_{p}1)\ \ \text{ and }\ \ S(x,j)=(S_{j}(x),j+_{p}1).

Given a homeomorphism
R:\widetilde{X}\to\widetilde{X}
with
R(X\times\{j\})=X\times\{j+_{p}1\}
for all
j
,
we consider the map

(4)

\Psi:F\in\mathcal{F}\mapsto F\circ R-\widetilde{T}\circ F\in\mathcal{G}.

It is easy to check that
\Psi
is linear and continuous.

We claim that
\Psi
is injective. Indeed, let
F\in\mathcal{F}
with
\Psi(F)=O_{\mathcal{G}}
. We have to show that
F=O_{\mathcal{F}}
.
The equality
\Psi(F)=O_{\mathcal{G}}
gives

(5)

F\circ R^{n}=\widetilde{T}^{n}\circ F\ \ \text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

Hence, for every
(x,j)\in\widetilde{X}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

(6)

F(x,j)=\widetilde{T}^{n}(F(R^{-n}(x,j)))=\alpha_{n}(x,j)+\beta_{n}(x,j),

where

\alpha_{n}(x,j)=\widetilde{T}^{n}(Q_{M}(F(R^{-n}(x,j))))\ \text{ and }\ \beta_{n}(x,j)=\widetilde{T}^{n}(Q_{N}(F(R^{-n}(x,j)))).

It is clear that, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

(7)

\alpha_{n}(x,j)\in M\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}\ \ \text{ for all }(x,j)\in\widetilde{X}.

Let us show that, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

(8)

\beta_{n}(x,j)\in T^{-1}(N)\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}\ \ \text{ for all }(x,j)\in\widetilde{X}.

Since
F(R^{-1}(x,j))\in Y\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, we must have
Q_{N}(F(R^{-1}(x,j)))\in T^{-1}(N)\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, and so

\beta_{1}(x,j)=\widetilde{T}(Q_{N}(F(R^{-1}(x,j))))\in N\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}.

Hence, since
\alpha_{1}(x,j)\in M\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}
and
\alpha_{1}(x,j)+\beta_{1}(x,j)=F(x,j)\in Y\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}
,
we see that (
8
) holds for
n=1
. Assume that (
8
) holds for a certain
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then,

\beta_{n+1}(x,j)=\widetilde{T}(\beta_{n}(R^{-1}(x,j)))\in N\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}.

As before, we conclude that (
8
) holds with
n+1
in the place of
n
. By induction, (
8
) holds for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
By (
6
), (
7
) and (
8
),

Q_{M}(F(x,j))=\alpha_{n}(x,j)=\widetilde{T}^{n}(Q_{M}(F(R^{-n}(x,j))))\ \ \text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

Thus, by (
2
) and (
3
),

|Q_{M}(F(x,j))|\leq a\,b\,t^{n}|F|_{\infty}\ \ \text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N},

implying that

(9)

Q_{M}(F(x,j))=(0,j)\ \ \text{ for all }(x,j)\in\widetilde{X}.

Now, by (
5
), for every
(x,j)\in\widetilde{X}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

(10)

F(x,j)=\widetilde{T}^{-n}(F(R^{n}(x,j)))=\varphi_{n}(x,j)+\psi_{n}(x,j),

where

\varphi_{n}(x,j)=\widetilde{T}^{-n}(Q_{M}(F(R^{n}(x,j))))\ \text{ and }\ \psi_{n}(x,j)=\widetilde{T}^{-n}(Q_{N}(F(R^{n}(x,j)))).

It is clear that, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

(11)

\psi_{n}(x,j)\in T^{-1}(N)\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}\ \ \text{ for all }(x,j)\in\widetilde{X}.

Let us show that, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

(12)

\varphi_{n}(x,j)\in M\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}\ \ \text{ for all }(x,j)\in\widetilde{X}.

Since

\displaystyle\widetilde{T}(Q_{N}(\varphi_{1}(x,j)))

\displaystyle=\widetilde{T}(\varphi_{1}(x,j))-\widetilde{T}(Q_{M}(\varphi_{1}(x,j)))

\displaystyle=Q_{M}(F(R(x,j)))-\widetilde{T}(Q_{M}(\varphi_{1}(x,j)))\in M\times\mathbb{Z}_{p},

we have that

(13)

Q_{N}(\varphi_{1}(x,j))\in T^{-1}(M)\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}.

By (
10
) with
n=1
, we can write

F(x,j)=Q_{M}(\varphi_{1}(x,j))+(Q_{N}(\varphi_{1}(x,j))+\psi_{1}(x,j)).

Since
Q_{M}(\varphi_{1}(x,j))\in M\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}
and
Q_{N}(\varphi_{1}(x,j))+\psi_{1}(x,j)\in N\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}
,
the fact that
F(x,j)\in Y\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}
implies that
Q_{N}(\varphi_{1}(x,j))+\psi_{1}(x,j)\in T^{-1}(N)\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.
Hence, by (
11
),

(14)

Q_{N}(\varphi_{1}(x,j))\in T^{-1}(N)\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}.

By (
13
) and (
14
),
Q_{N}(\varphi_{1}(x,j))\in\{0\}\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, which gives (
12
) for
n=1
.
Assume that (

) holds for a certain
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then,

\displaystyle\widetilde{T}(Q_{N}(\varphi_{n+1}(x,j)))

\displaystyle=\widetilde{T}(\varphi_{n+1}(x,j))-\widetilde{T}(Q_{M}(\varphi_{n+1}(x,j)))

\displaystyle=\varphi_{n}(R(x,j))-\widetilde{T}(Q_{M}(\varphi_{n+1}(x,j)))\in M\times\mathbb{Z}_{p},

and so

Q_{N}(\varphi_{n+1}(x,j))\in T^{-1}(M)\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}.

By arguing as above, we obtain
Q_{N}(\varphi_{n+1}(x,j))\in\{0\}\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, and so (
12
) holds with
n+1
in the place of
n
.
By induction, (
12
) holds for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
By (
10
), (

) and (

),

Q_{N}(F(x,j))=\psi_{n}(x,j)=\widetilde{T}^{-n}(Q_{N}(F(R^{n}(x,j))))\ \ \text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

Therefore, by (

) and (

|Q_{N}(F(x,j))|\leq a\,b\,t^{n}|F|_{\infty}\ \ \text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N},

which implies that

(15)

Q_{N}(F(x,j))=(0,j)\ \ \text{ for all }(x,j)\in\widetilde{X}.

By (
9
) and (
15
), we conclude that
F=O_{\mathcal{F}}
, as desired.

Let us now prove that
\Psi
is surjective (hence bijective) and its inverse is given by

(16)

\Psi^{-1}(G)(x,j)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\widetilde{T}^{k}(Q_{M}(G(R^{-k-1}(x,j))))-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\widetilde{T}^{-k}(Q_{N}(G(R^{k-1}(x,j)))).

For this purpose, take
G\in\mathcal{G}
. Note that the partial sums of both series in (
16
) are well defined.
Moreover, by (
2
) and (
3
