Cores of Games via Total Dual Integrality, with Applications to Perfect Graphs and Polymatroids
Vijay V. Vazirani

Abstract
LP-duality theory has played a central role in the study of cores of games, right from the early days of this notion to the present time. The classic paper of Shapley and Shubik [ SS71 ] introduced the “right” way of exploiting the power of this theory, namely picking problems whose LP-relaxations support polyhedra having integral vertices. So far, the latter fact was established by showing that the constraint matrix of the underlying linear system is totally unimodular . We attempt to take this methodology to its logical next step – using total dual integrality – thereby addressing new classes of games which have their origins in two major theories within combinatorial optimization, namely perfect graphs and polymatroids. In the former, we address the stable set and clique games and in the latter, we address the matroid independent set game. For each of these games, we prove that the set of core imputations is precisely the set of optimal solutions to the dual LPs. Another novelty is the way the worth of the game is allocated among sub-coalitions. Previous works follow the bottom-up process of allocating to individual agents; the allocation to a sub-coalition is simply the sum of the allocations to its agents. The natural process for our games is top-down . The optimal dual allocates to “objects” in the grand coalition; a sub-coalition inherits the allocation of each object with which it has non-empty intersection.

Introduction

The
core
is a quintessential solution concept in cooperative game theory. It captures all possible ways of distributing the total worth of a game among individual agents and sub-coalitions in such a way that the grand coalition remains intact, i.e., a sub-coalition will not be able to generate more profits by itself and therefore has no incentive to secede from the grand coalition.

LP-duality theory has played a central role in the study of the core, right from its early days all the way to the present time. The early works of Bondareva
[
Bon63
]
and Shapley
[
Sha65
]
gave a necessary and sufficient condition for non-emptiness of the core of a game, namely that it is “balanced”. The proof of this fact is based on LP-duality theory and uses the Farkas Lemma. Although this is an insightful fact, the LP employed for proving it involves exponentially many variables, one for each subset of players. Consequently, to the best of our knowledge, it did not lead to an efficient way of computing core imputations for any natural game.

The classic paper of Shapley and Shubik
[
SS71
]
introduced the ‘‘right’’ way of exploiting the power of LP-duality theory for characterizing core imputations. They did this in the context of the assignment game

In general, an optimal solution to such an LP will be fractional, i.e., it will pick agents fractionally. Clearly, that is not very meaningful. In retrospect,
a key ingenuity of Shapley and Shubik

the polytope defined by its constraints has integral vertices

Related Works

An imputation in the core has to ensure that
each
of the exponentially many sub-coalitions is “happy” — clearly, that is a lot of constraints. As a result, the core is non-empty only for a handful of games, some of which are mentioned in the Introduction. A different kind of game, in which preferences are ordinal, is based on the stable matching problem defined by Gale and Shapley
[
GS62
]

]

gave linear programming formulations for stable matchings; the vertices of their underlying polytopes are integral and are stable matchings. More recently, Kiraly and Pap
[
KP08
]
showed that the linear system of Rothblum is in fact TDI.

To deal with games having an empty core, e.g., the general graph matching game, the following two notions have been given in the past. The first is that of

]

[

]

A more well known notion is that of

For an imputation

In 1998,
[

]
stated the problem of computing the nucleolus of the matching game in polynomial time. For the unit weight case, this was done by Kern and Paulusma
[
KP03
]
, and for arbitrary weights by Konemann et al.
[
KPT20
]
. However, their algorithm makes extensive use of the ellipsoid algorithm and is therefore neither efficient nor does it give deep insights into the underlying combinatorial structure. They leave the open problem of finding a combinatorial polynomial time algorithm. We note that the difference

appearing in the least core and nucleolus has not been upper-bounded for any standard family of games, including the general graph matching game.

A different notion was recently proposed in
[

]
, namely

2/3

b
-matching games in general graphs by Xiaoet al.
[
XLF21
]
.

Konemann et al.
[

]
showed that computing the nucleolus of the constrained bipartite

-matching game, in which an edge can be matched at most once, is NP-hard even for the case

Deng and Papadimitriou
[

define several computational questions regarding the core, e.g., testing non-emptyness, membership etc. and study them for a specific game on an undirected graph with positive and negative weight edges. Granot and Huberman
[

4.2
Characterizing the Core

An
imputation
describes how the worth of the game leads to allocation of “profits” to sub-coalitions. In the assignment game, an optimal dual distributes the worth of the game among the agents, i.e., the vertices of the graph. The allocation to a sub-coalition is simply the sum of the allocations to the agents in it.

In our setting, an optimal dual distributes the worth of the game among the cliques of
G
. However, dividing the worth
y_{Q}
, given to clique
Q
, among the agents in the clique in not very meaningful. Instead, for a clique
Q
, we will view
the worth
y_{Q}
as residing in every sub-clique of
Q
. However, this is done not by simply duplicating the worth onto each sub-clique, an operation that is clearly not legitimate. It is done in a more interesting manner, so as to define allocations to sub-coalitions in a consistent manner.

Before providing further details, let us establish terminology that is more appropriate for the setting at hand. We will think of
y_{Q}
as the “satisfaction” of clique
Q
. Consider a sub-coalition
T\subseteq V
such that
Q\cap T\neq\emptyset
, and let
Q^{\prime}=Q\cap T
. As stated above, the satisfaction of
y_{Q}
resides on every sub-clique of
Q
, including
Q^{\prime}
. The crucial point is that
the latter viewpoint will manifest itself only when we “think” of the sub-coalition
T
. Moreover,
Q
will allocate only to its largest sub-clique in
T
. The details given below will clarify these ideas.

Let
y:(\mbox{cliques in}\ G)\rightarrow\boldsymbol{\mathit{R}}_{+}
be a function assigning satisfaction to the cliques of
G
. Define

\mbox{\rm satisfaction}(G)=\sum_{\mbox{clique $Q$ in}\ G}{y_{Q}}.

Let
T\subseteq V
and let
G^{\prime}
be the subgraph of
G
induced on
T
. Each clique
Q
in
G
will allocate satisfaction to at most one clique in
G^{\prime}
as follows. If
Q\cap T=\emptyset
, then
Q
will make no allocation to any clique in
G^{\prime}
. Otherwise, it will allocate
y_{Q}
to
Q^{\prime}=Q\cap T
. In general, there may be several cliques in
G
which may allocate to
Q^{\prime}
. Hence the total allocation to
Q^{\prime}
is defined to be

z_{Q^{\prime}}=\sum_{Q\ {\mbox{in}\ G}:Q\cap T=Q^{\prime}}{y_{Q}},

where
z:(\mbox{cliques in}\ G^{\prime})\rightarrow\boldsymbol{\mathit{R}}_{+}
is the function which assigns satisfaction to the cliques of
G^{\prime}
.

Finally, define

\mbox{\rm satisfaction}(G^{\prime})=\sum_{\mbox{clique $Q^{\prime}$ in}\ G^{\prime}}{z_{Q^{\prime}}}.

Definition 11
.

We will say that imputation
y
is in the core of the stable set game if

1.

\mbox{\rm worth}(G)=\mbox{\rm satisfaction}(G)

2.

\forall G^{\prime}\subseteq G,\ \mbox{\rm worth}(G^{\prime})\leq\mbox{\rm satisfaction}(G^{\prime})

Remark 2
.

The contrast between the way allocations are made to sub-coalitions in the assignment game and in our game can be summarized as follows: Whereas the former follows a bottom-up process, we follow a top-down process. To the best of our knowledge, all previous works characterizing the cores of games follow a bottom-up process, similar to the assignment game.

Theorem 3
.

An imputation
y
is in the core of the stable set game over a perfect graph if and only if it is an optimal solution to the dual, LP(
6
).

Proof.

(
\Leftarrow
) First assume that
y
is an optimal solution to the dual, LP(
6
). By the LP-duality theorem and the TDI of its linear system,
\mbox{\rm worth}(G)=\mbox{\rm satisfaction}(G)
.

Let
T\subseteq V
and let
G^{\prime}
be the graph induced on
T
. By the allocation procedure described above, for
v\in T
, each clique
Q
in
G
containing
v
will contribute
y_{Q}
to its largest sub-clique
Q^{\prime}
in
G^{\prime}
, which will contain
v
. Therefore,

\forall v\in T:\ \sum_{Q^{\prime}\ni v}{z_{Q^{\prime}}}=\sum_{Q\ni v}{y_{Q}}\geq w_{v}.

Therefore
z
is feasible for the dual LP (
6
), restricted to
G^{\prime}
. Hence by the weak duality theorem, we get that the objective value function of the primal LP restricted to
G^{\prime}
is bounded by
\mbox{\rm satisfaction}(G^{\prime})
. Finally, since this restricted system is also TDI, we get
\mbox{\rm worth}(G^{\prime})\leq\mbox{\rm satisfaction}(G^{\prime})
.

(
\Rightarrow
) Next, assume that imputation
y
is in the core of the stable set game over a perfect graph. By definition,
\mbox{\rm worth}(G)=\mbox{\rm satisfaction}(G)=\sum_{\mbox{clique $Q$ in}\ G}{y_{Q}}
.

Let
v\in V
and let
G^{\prime}
be
G
restricted to
v
. By the definition of core and the way
y
yields
z
,

w_{v}=\mbox{\rm worth}(G^{\prime})\leq\mbox{\rm satisfaction}(G^{\prime})=\sum_{Q^{\prime}\ni v}{z_{Q^{\prime}}}=\sum_{Q\ni v}{y_{Q}}.

Therefore,
y
satisfies the constraint for each
v\in V
in the dual, LP(
6
). Hence
y
is dual feasible. This together with the previous statement gives that
y
is an optimal solution to the dual, LP(
6
).
∎

Corollary 1
.

The core of the stable set game over a perfect graph is non-empty.

The Matroid Independent Set Game, Given via a Rank Oracle

In Section
5.1
, we present the necessary background information. Once again, we will not credit individual papers for these (mostly well-known) facts; instead we refer the reader to Chapter 10 in
[
GLS88
]
.

5.1
Definitions and Preliminaries

Let
\mathcal{M}=(U,\mathcal{I})
be a matroid, where
U
is the ground set with
n
elements and
\mathcal{I}
is its set of independent sets. Let
w:U\rightarrow\boldsymbol{\mathit{R}}_{+}
be a function that assigns weights to the elements. Assume further that we are given a
rank oracle

r
: for any set
S\subseteq U
,
r(S)
is the rank of
S
, i.e., the size of a maximum independent set in
S
. The rank function of a matroid is
submodular
, i.e.,

r(S)+r(T)\geq r(S\cap T)+r(S\cup T).

It is well known that a maximum weight independent set can be found via the following greedy algorithm. Sort the elements of
U
by decreasing weight, say
e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}
. For
1\leq i\leq n
do: Pick
e_{i}
if
r(e_{1},\ldots e_{i})>r(e_{1},\ldots,e_{i-1})
.

Definition 12
.

Given matroid
\mathcal{M}=(U,\mathcal{I})
and weight function
w
on its elements, the worth of the
matroid independent set game
is defined to be the weight of a maximum weight independent set in
\mathcal{M}
and is denoted by
\mbox{\rm worth}(\mathcal{M})
.

We wish to characterize the core imputations of the matroid independent set game. LP (
7
) is the LP-relaxation of the maximum weight independent set problem; here
x_{e}
is the extent to which element
e
is picked.

\displaystyle\underset{\displaystyle}{\mathrm{max}}\quad\sum_{e\in U}{w_{e}x_{e}}\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle

(7)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\phantom{}}{\mathrm{max}}$}][c]{\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\mathrm{max}$}][l]{\mathrm{\kern 1.00006pts.t.}}}\quad

\displaystyle x(S)

\displaystyle\leq r(S)\quad

\displaystyle\forall S\subseteq U,

\displaystyle x_{e}

\displaystyle\geq 0

\displaystyle\forall e\in U

The linear system of LP (
7
) is TDI and therefore its polytope has integral vertices, i.e., they are independent sets of the matroid. This polytope is called a
polymatroid
. Taking
u_{i}
and
v_{j}
to be the dual variables for the first and second constraints of (
7
), we obtain the dual LP:

\displaystyle\underset{\displaystyle}{\mathrm{min}}\quad\sum_{S\subseteq U}{r(S)\cdot y_{S}}\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle

(8)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\phantom{}}{\mathrm{min}}$}][c]{\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\mathrm{min}$}][l]{\mathrm{\kern 1.00006pts.t.}}}\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{S\ni e}{y_{S}}

\displaystyle\geq w_{e}\quad

\displaystyle\forall e\in U,

\displaystyle y_{S}

\displaystyle\geq 0

Given an oracle for the rank function

5.2
Characterizing the Core

The process of allocating the worth of the game to sub-coalitions is similar to that followed in the stable set game over perfect graphs, i.e., it is top-down, as described in Remark
2
. An optimal dual distributes the worth of the game among subsets
S\subseteq U
. Once again, dividing the worth
y_{S}
, given to set
S
, among the agents in the set in not very meaningful. Instead, again we will view the worth
y_{S}
as residing in every subset of
S
, and this viewpoint manifests itself only when we “think” of the sub-coalition
T
. As before, we will think of
y_{S}
as the “satisfaction” of set
S
.

Remark 3
.

An added source of confusion in this setting is that the worth of the game is distributed among subsets of
U
, and sub-coalitions are also subsets of
U
. We will try to mitigate this confusion by naming the former
S,S^{\prime}
etc. and the latter as
T,T^{\prime}
etc.

Let
y:2^{U}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{\mathit{R}}_{+}
be a function assigning satisfaction to the subsets of
U
. Define

\mbox{\rm satisfaction}(U)=\sum_{S\subseteq U}{y_{S}}.

Consider sub-coalition
T\subseteq U
. Each subset
S
of
U
will allocate satisfaction to at most one subset of
T
as follows. If
S\cap T=\emptyset
, then
S
will make no allocation to any subset of
T
. Otherwise, it will allocate
y_{S}
to the set
S^{\prime}=S\cap T
, i.e., the largest subset of
S
in sub-coalition
T
. Once again, there may be several subsets of
U
which may allocate to
S^{\prime}
. The total allocation to
S^{\prime}
is defined to be

z_{S^{\prime}}=\sum_{S\subseteq U:S\cap T=S^{\prime}}{y_{S}},

where
z:2^{T}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{\mathit{R}}_{+}
is the function which assigns satisfaction to subsets of
T
.

Finally, define

\mbox{\rm satisfaction}(T)=\sum_{S^{\prime}\subseteq T}{z_{S^{\prime}}}.

Definition 13
.

We will say that imputation
y
is in the core of the matroid independent set game if

1.

\mbox{\rm worth}(U)=\mbox{\rm satisfaction}(U)

2.

\forall T\subseteq U,\ \mbox{\rm worth}(T)\leq\mbox{\rm satisfaction}(T)

Theorem 4
.

An imputation
y
is in the core of the matroid independent set game if and only if it is an optimal solution to the dual, LP(
8
).

Proof.

(
\Leftarrow
) First assume that
y
is an optimal solution to the dual, LP(
8
). By the LP-duality theorem and the TDI of this linear system,
\mbox{\rm worth}(U)=\mbox{\rm satisfaction}(U)
.

Consider sub-coalition
T\subseteq U
. By the allocation procedure described above,

\forall e\in T:\ \sum_{S^{\prime}\subseteq T:\ S^{\prime}\ni e}{z_{S^{\prime}}}=\sum_{S\subseteq U:\ S\ni e}{y_{S}}\geq w_{e}.

Therefore
z
is feasible for the dual LP (
8
), restricted to
T
. Therefore, by the weak duality theorem, we get that the objective value function of the primal LP restricted to
T
is bounded by
\mbox{\rm satisfaction}(T)
. Finally, since this restricted system is also TDI, we get
\mbox{\rm worth}(T)\leq\mbox{\rm satisfaction}(T)
.

(
\Rightarrow
) Next, assume that imputation
y
is in the core of the stable set game over a perfect graph. By definition,
\mbox{\rm worth}(U)=\mbox{\rm satisfaction}(U)=\sum_{S\subseteq U}{y_{S}}
.

Let
e\in U
and let
T^{\prime}
be the sub-coalition
\{e\}
. By the definition of core and the way
y
yields

,

w_{e}=\mbox{\rm worth}(T^{\prime})\leq\mbox{\rm satisfaction}(T^{\prime})=\sum_{S^{\prime}\subseteq T^{\prime}:\ S^{\prime}\ni e}{z_{S^{\prime}}}=\sum_{S\subseteq U:\ S\ni e}{y_{S}}.

Therefore,
y
satisfies the constraint for each
e\in U
in the dual LP (
8
). Hence
y
is dual feasible. This together with the previous statement gives that
y
is an optimal solution to the dual LP (
8
).
∎

Corollary 2
.

The core of the matroid independent set game is non-empty.
