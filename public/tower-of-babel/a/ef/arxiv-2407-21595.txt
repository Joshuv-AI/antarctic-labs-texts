Precomputing approach for a two-scale phase transition model
Michael Eden, Tom Freudenberg, Adrian Muntean

Abstract
In this study, we employ analytical and numerical techniques to examine a phase transition model with moving boundaries. The model displays two relevant spatial scales pointing out to a macroscopic phase and a microscopic phase, interacting on disjoint inclusions. The shrinkage or the growth of the inclusions is governed by a modified Gibbs-Thomson law depending on the macroscopic temperature, but without accessing curvature information. We use the Hanzawa transformation to transform the problem onto a fixed reference domain. Then a fixed-point argument is employed to demonstrate the well-posedness of the system for a finite time interval. Due to the model’s nonlinearities and the macroscopic parameters, which are given by differential equations that depend on the size of the inclusions, the problem is computationally expensive to solve numerically. We introduce a precomputing approach that solves multiple cell problems in an offline phase and uses an interpolation scheme afterward to determine the needed parameters. Additionally, we propose a semi-implicit time-stepping method to resolve the nonlinearity of the problem. We investigate the errors of both the precomputing and time-stepping procedures and verify the theoretical results via numerical simulations.



Keywords: Phase transitions, two-scale model, moving boundary problem, numerical analysis, simulation

Introduction

Phase transition models describe the heat dynamics in a system where phase transitions can occur, such as water-ice or austenite-pearlite microstructures in steel.
One of the main difficulties is the need to account for the changing geometries inherent to the transition process.
In some cases, there is an additional scale separation: the phase transition processes might happen at the microscale while our interest might be in the effective macroscopic behaviors.
Examples include microstructural changes in steel
[
10
,
45
]
, liquid–solid phase transitions in metals
[
8
,
26
]
, or thawing processes in permafrost
[
38
]
. In these cases, the focus is not on the geometric changes at the microscale but on their impact on macroscopic properties.
However, via homogenization techniques (e.g., two-scale convergence or asymptotic expansion) it is easy to see that the microstructural changes can not be neglected: In the resulting models, the phase transitions are still present on the microscale and directly influence the effective material parameters, see, e.g.,
[
11
,
8
,
46
]
.
Such two-scale models (sometimes also called micro-macro model or distributed microstructures model) are computationally extremely costly, mainly due to the following two factors:

(a)

Strong non-linear coupling between the scales:
The macroscopic temperature field is driving the microstructural changes and the microstructural changes are in turn non-linearly influencing the macroscopic material parameters.

(b)

Family of moving boundary problems:
Every macroscopic point is associated with corresponding moving boundary problems at the microscale both via the interaction with the microscopic temperature but also via the geometric changes influencing the heat conductivity via the associated cell problems.

Since one of the main goals of homogenization is the derivation of simpler models that are computationally less heavy, these issues might at first glance call into question the usefulness of such limit models.
There is, however, one important advantage these two-scale models have, namely the explicit separation of the scales.
This can be exploited in many different ways: via iteration procedures
[
20
]
, parallel computing
[
3
]
, machine learning
[
21
]
, and/or interpolation techniques
[
34
]
.

In this work, we propose, analyze, and implement a precomputing strategy in which the calculations of the macroscopic coefficients are pushed into an
offline phase
.
Instead of having to calculate the effective conductivity via geometry-dependent cell problems at every macroscopic point and at each time step, we precompute the conductivity instead for a range of potential parameter values and interpolate in between.
Please note, that this precomputing is
perfectly parallizable
.
We show that this interpolation is stable and that the introduced error in the two-scale system can be controlled via the step size in the parameter space and the order of the interpolation.
This idea is not completely new and similar approaches have been outlined before, see
Section

1.2
for a short literature overview.
The non-linearities in the model are resolved with a semi-implicit time-stepping method which we show to converge linearly.
We confirm these convergence results via simulations and the corresponding FEniCS code is made freely available
[
16
]
.

1.2
Discussion on related literature

An early work on two-scale models in phase transitions is due to A. Visintin
[
46
]
, where the transition between the two scales is achieved via convolution with a Gaussian kernel but without explicitly looking at evolving microstructures.
More closely related to our work, in
[
8
,
7
]
, two-scale models for dendritic growth with prescribed geometry changes are formally derived via asymptotic expansion.
In
[
10
,
9
]
two-phase models for phase transitions are derived, albeit with a priori known geometry evolution.
Very recently, in
[
11
]
, an effective one-phase model for phase transitions was rigorously derived via two-scale convergence.

Very similar models and challenges can be found in the context of reactive transport or coagulation/fragmentation dynamics in evolving porous media which has garnered quite a bit of interest in recent years.
We point to the upscaling works in
[
4
,
18
,
19
,
33
,
40
,
49
]
, which are concerned with the derivation of two-scale models in that context, and note that the resulting models are structurally quite similar to the phase transition problem considered in this work.
Naturally, there are quite a few works looking more closely at the resulting two-scale models: In
[
32
]
, the well-posedness for such a two-scale model was established for a simplified setup where the geometry changes do not impact the diffusivity except via the porosity.
In
[
22
]
, strong local-in-time solutions for a two-scale model, even allowing for nonuniform geometry changes, are established via maximal parabolic regularity and a level set approach.

Such models often come with huge computational challenges due to their nonlinear nature and complex coupling.
Our main approach of dealing with this is a precomputing strategy in which the calculations of the effective macroscopic coefficients are pushed into an offline phase; introducing an interpolation error but significantly speeding up the calculations.
Similar ideas are often explored in the context of shape or topology optimization, see e.g.,
[
30
,
29
,
47
]
and references therein.
In
[
34
]
, the convergence of a precomputing scheme for a two-scale dispersion model for porous media is shown, however, there are no geometry changes present.

Naturally, there are other approaches to tackle the computational complexity of two-scale models:
Instead of solving the entire coupled system in a classical FE approach, an alternative idea is to explicitly decouple the macro and micro temperatures by solving between them iteratively.
This is closely related to the FE
2
approach
[
15
]
and the Heterogeneous Multiscale Method
[
6
]
.
This approach has two main advantages: the calculations on the microscale can be run in parallel and an adaptive interpolation strategy can be used to only solve the microscale problems for a small number of macroscopic points.
However, for each time step, there may be a need for several iteration steps, which again can be costly, especially when the coupling is complex.
In addition, the iteration process introduces a new error.
The approach has been studied and investigated for many different two-scale problems, see
[
3
,
13
,
17
,
31
,
35
]
.
As a direct extension of the iterative scheme, an adaptive clustering approach has been developed in
[
41
]
.
Here, micro problems with comparable macroscopic data are paired together and solved via one representative system.
This allows for a speed-up and even enables fast simulations in higher-dimensional setups.
Of course, this approach produces further error sources, for which, to our knowledge, no estimates are available.
But the numerical results in
[
41
]
show good approximation accuracy.

Another approach is the hierarchical FEM as they are used for multiscale problems in
[
25
,
36
]
.
Here, special FE spaces are constructed to reduce the DOFs of those functions which depend on multiple scales.
This method is comparable to a wavelet approach and allows for analytical error estimates, but is more complicated to set up than the previous methods.
Finally, we want to mention a model reduction approach which is often encountered in mechanics, see
[
23
]
and the references therein.
A model reduction for microscopic crystal growth was considered in
[
42
]
.
This approach can lead to significant speed-ups, but requires, especially for time-dependent problems, a large amount of data to obtain reasonable models.

(Weak solution in fixed coordinates)

(ii)

(iii)

The temperatures

(2.4)

for all

In

Lemma 1
.

(i)

There are
0<c<C
independent of

and

(ii)

Let

,

,

Then,

\|F_{1}-F_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(Y)^{d\times d}}+\|F_{1}^{-1}-F_{2}^{-1}\|_{L^{\infty}(Y)^{d\times d}}+\|J_{1}-J_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(Y)}\leq C|h_{1}-h_{2}|

where
C>0
independent of

.

Proof.

(i)
. These bounds exist as
|F_{h}(y)|,|F_{h}^{-1}(y)|\leq 2
uniformly and independently of
h\in[-a^{*},a^{*}]
.

(ii)
. Outside the tubular neighborhood
U_{\Gamma}
, the transformation is trivial, i.e.,
F_{i}=\operatorname{Id}
and

, so we only have to look at

.
We immediately get

|F_{1}(y)-F_{2}(y)|=|\left(h_{1}-h_{2}\right)D_{y}\psi(y)|\leq C|h_{1}-h_{2}|.

We already know that the
F_{i}
are invertible with

F_{i}^{-1}(y)=(\operatorname{Id}+h_{i}D_{y}\psi(y))^{-1}

As a consequence,

Finally, for the determinant a second order Taylor approximation yields

Analysis for prescribed height function

We take a look at the weak form for the heat system, i.e.,

Lemma 4

(Existence for prescribed height functions)
.

Let

and

There exists a unique weak solution
(\Theta,\vartheta)\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
with

\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}(C(h)\Theta)\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\int_{Y_{0}}\partial_{t}(c(h)\vartheta)\phi\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}K(h)\nabla\Theta\cdot\nabla\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\int_{Y_{0}}\kappa(h)\nabla_{y}\vartheta\cdot\nabla_{y}\phi\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x\\

.

, which is independent of

\|\Theta\|_{L^{\infty}((0,t)\times\Omega)}+\|\vartheta\|_{L^{\infty}((0,t)\times\Omega\times Y_{0})}\leq C\left(1+M^{2}\sqrt{t}\right)

For any such height function, all coefficients are well-defined and essentially bounded.

As a consequence, this is a linear implicit evolution equation in the form of

Regarding the estimates: First of all, the advective velocity

.

for

+C\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\int_{Y_{0}}|\partial_{t}h||\vartheta||\vartheta^{k}|+|\vartheta||v(h)||\nabla_{y}\vartheta^{k}|+|f(h)||\vartheta^{k}|\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{t}h||\Theta||\Theta^{k}|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq 2M\left(\|\Theta^{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+k^{2}|A_{\Theta}(t,k)|\right),

\displaystyle\int_{Y_{0}}|\partial_{t}h||\vartheta||\vartheta^{k}|\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq 2M\left(\|\vartheta^{k}\|_{L^{2}(Y_{0})}^{2}+k^{2}|A_{\vartheta}(t,x,k)|\right).

The latent heat term, we estimate via (with
k\geq\max_{h\in[-a^{*},a^{*}]}|L(h)|
)

\int_{\Omega}|L(h)\partial_{t}h||\Theta^{k}|\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{M}{2}\left(\|\Theta^{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+k^{2}|A_{\Theta}(t,k)|\right).

Looking at the convection term (
v(h)\leq CM
):

\int_{Y_{0}}|\vartheta||v(h)||\nabla_{y}\vartheta^{k}|\,\mathrm{d}y\leq\delta\|\nabla_{y}\vartheta^{k}\|_{L^{2}(Y_{0})}^{2}+\frac{M^{2}}{4\delta}\left(\|\vartheta^{k}\|_{L^{2}(Y_{0})}^{2}+k^{2}|A_{\vartheta}(\tau,x,k)|\right).

for any
\delta>0
.
We set

k^{*}=\max\left\{\|\Theta_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)},\|\theta_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega\times Y_{0})},\|F\|_{L^{\infty}((0,T_{M})\times\Omega)},\|f\|_{L^{\infty}((0,T_{M})\times\Omega\times Y_{0})},\max_{h\in[-a,a]}|L(h)|\right\}

and conclude, for all
k\geq k^{*}
and assuming
M\geq 1
,

\|\Theta^{k}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\vartheta^{k}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega\times Y_{0})}+\|\nabla\Theta^{k}\|^{2}_{L^{2}((0,t)\times\Omega)}+\|\nabla_{y}\vartheta^{k}\|^{2}_{L^{2}((0,t)\times\Omega\times Y_{0})}\\
\leq CM^{2}\Bigg(\|\theta^{k}\|_{L^{2}((0,t)\times\Omega)}^{2}+\|\vartheta^{k}\|_{L^{2}((0,t)\times\Omega\times Y_{0})}^{2}+k^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left(|A_{\theta}(\tau,k)|+\int_{\Omega}|A_{\vartheta}(\tau,x,k)|\,\mathrm{d}x\right)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\Bigg).

Thus, Gronwall’s inequality allows us to conclude for
0<t<T_{M}

\|\Theta^{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,t;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}+\|\vartheta^{k}\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(0,t;L^{2}(\Omega\times Y_{0}))}+\|\nabla\Theta^{k}\|_{L^{2}(0,t\times\Omega)}^{2}+\|\nabla_{y}\vartheta^{k}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,t\times\Omega\times Y_{0})}\\
\leq C\exp(tM^{2})M^{2}k^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left(|A_{\theta}(\tau,k)|+\int_{\Omega}|A_{\vartheta}(\tau,x,k)|\,\mathrm{d}x\right)\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

where the constant
C>0
is still independent of
k
,
h
, and
M
.
From here, we apply
[
28
, Chapter 2, §6, Theorem 6.1, Remark 6.2]
to conclude the existence of
k_{M}
, which is independent of
h
but does depend on
M
, such that
|A_{\theta}(\tau,k_{M})|,|A_{\vartheta}(\tau,x,k_{M})|=0
almost everywhere.
More precisely

\|\Theta\|_{L^{\infty}((0,t)\times\Omega)}+\|\vartheta\|_{L^{\infty}((0,t)\times\Omega)}\leq 2k^{*}\left(1+C\exp(\nicefrac{{5}}{{4}}M^{2}t)M^{2}\sqrt{t}\right)\quad(t\in(0,T_{M}))

Moreover,
tM^{2}\leq a^{*}
for
0\leq t\leq\sqrt{T_{M}}
.
Over this time interval, we have

\|\Theta\|_{L^{\infty}((0,t)\times\Omega)}+\|\vartheta\|_{L^{\infty}((0,t)\times\Omega)}\leq 2k^{*}\left(1+CM^{2}\sqrt{t}\right)\quad(t\in(0,\sqrt{T_{M}}).

This implies uniformity in
M
of the
L^{\infty}
estimates for

.
∎

Fixed-point argument and long time behavior

We first note that

4
Numerical Analysis

From a numerical perspective, the present model is quite challenging and computationally expensive to solve due to the different scales, changing domains, and non-linear coupling.
In this section, we introduce and investigate a precomputing strategy together with a semi-implicit time-stepping scheme to resolve these problems.

First note, that independent of the underlying numerical algorithm, the effective conductivity
K
has to be recomputed multiple times throughout the simulation process: after each discrete time step and at every macroscopic node since
K
depends on the local value of the height function
h
.
This is quite expensive since differential equations (cell problems) have to be solved to compute
K
.
Seeing that in the underlying setup, the general cell topology does not change for different heights and only depends on
h
, it would be advantageous if one could precompute the conductivity
K
for a discrete number of height values in an offline phase and utilize these values in the simulation by interpolation.
We start by analyzing this idea and the influence of the solution in more detail.

4.1
Precomputing strategy

Given
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we evenly split the interval
[-a^{*},a^{*}]
into
N
sub-intervals
[h_{i},h_{i+1}]
with
h_{i}=-a^{*}+2a^{*}\tfrac{i}{N}
, for
i=0,\dots,N
.
We then compute for each
h_{i}
the corresponding conductivity
K_{i}=K(h_{i})
given by
Eq.

2.2b
.
We also set
\Delta h=\nicefrac{{1}}{{N}}
in the following.
7
7

7

Depending on the expected behavior of
K(h)
with respect to
h
, it might be advantageous to utilize a nonuniform grid (see e.g.,
[
34
]
).
In our case, this is unnecessary as the general behavior is quite smooth and uniform (for comparison, see
Fig.

3
).

For any
h\in[-a^{*},a^{*}]
the precomputed conductivity
K_{\text{pre}}
is then defined via an interpolation scheme. This raises the question of what interpolation scheme is suitable given the properties of
K
.
In line with the Lipschitz continuity of
K
(
Lemma

3
), a piecewise linear interpolation is a natural candidate, e.g.,

K_{\text{lin}}(h)=\left(1-\frac{h-h_{i}}{\Delta h}\right)K_{i}+\frac{h-h_{i}}{\Delta h}K_{i+1},\quad\text{if }h\in[h_{i},h_{i+1}),

(4.1)

This Lipschitz continuity immediately guarantees at least a first-order interpolation error:

|K(h)-K_{\text{lin}}(h)|\leq C\frac{\Delta h}{2}.

We need higher regularity results for the effect conductivity to ensure higher order errors, which we investigate in the following lemma.

Lemma 7
.

For the effective conductivity it holds
K\in C^{1,1}([-a^{*},a^{*}],\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
.

Proof.

Recall, that the effective conductivity is given by

\displaystyle K_{ij}(h)=\int_{Y\setminus Y(h)}\mathcal{K}(\nabla\xi_{h,j}+e_{j})\cdot e_{i}\,\mathrm{d}y=\int_{Y\setminus Y_{0}}\mathcal{K}(F_{h}^{-1}\nabla(S_{h}^{-1}\xi_{h,j})+e_{j})\cdot e_{i}J_{h}\,\mathrm{d}y.

Therefore, by the product rule, we obtain the following expression assuming that all parts are well-defined:

\begin{split}\partial_{h}K_{ij}(h)&=\int_{Y\setminus Y_{0}}K(\partial_{h}F_{h}^{-1}\nabla(S_{h}^{-1}\xi_{h,j}))\cdot e_{i}J_{h}\,\mathrm{d}y+\int_{Y\setminus Y_{0}}K(F_{h}^{-1}\partial_{h}\nabla(S_{h}^{-1}\xi_{h,j}))\cdot e_{i}J_{h}\,\mathrm{d}y\\
&\hskip 50.0pt+\int_{Y\setminus Y_{0}}K(F_{h}^{-1}\nabla(S_{h}^{-1}\xi_{h,j})+e_{j})\cdot e_{i}\partial_{h}J_{h}\,\mathrm{d}y.\end{split}

(4.2)

Now, we investigate if all the derivatives on the right-hand side are well-defined and Lipschitz with respect to
h
.
For the Matrix
F_{h}^{-1}=(\operatorname{Id}+hD_{y}\psi)^{-1}
, it holds
\partial_{h}F_{h}^{-1}=-F_{h}^{-1}D_{y}\psi F_{h}^{-1}
.
Therefore,
\partial_{h}F_{h}^{-1}
is well-defined and also Lipschitz in
h
.
For the determinate
J_{h}=\det(\operatorname{Id}+hD_{y}\psi)
, it holds

\displaystyle\partial_{h}J_{h}

\displaystyle=\partial_{h}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{d}}\text{sgn}(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{d}(\delta_{i,\sigma(i)}+hD_{y}\psi_{i,\sigma(i)})=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{d}}\text{sgn}(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{d}p_{k,i,\sigma(i)},

where
S_{d}
denotes the set of all permutations in dimension
d
,
\text{sgn}(\sigma)=\pm 1
depending on if the permutation is even or odd, and

p_{k,i,\sigma(i)}=\begin{cases}(\delta_{i,\sigma(i)}+hD_{y}\psi_{i,\sigma(i)}),&\text{ if }i\neq k,\\
D_{y}\psi_{i,\sigma(i)},&\text{ if }i=k.\end{cases}

This is a sum of multiple determinants (each time removing the identity and
h
from a different column of the matrix
\operatorname{Id}+hD_{y}\psi
), hence
\partial_{h}J_{h}
is also well-defined and by the same arguments as in
Lemma

2
also Lipschitz with respect to
h
.

Next, we investigate the derivative of the cell solution
S_{h}^{-1}\xi_{h,j}
with respect to
h
. To this end, we define the operator
E_{j}:[-a^{*},a^{*}]\times V\to V^{*}
, where

V\coloneqq\left\{v\in H^{1}_{\#}(Y\setminus Y_{0}):\int_{Y\setminus Y_{0}}v\,\mathrm{d}y=0\right\},

and the operator is given by

E_{j}(h,v)=\int_{Y\setminus Y_{0}}J_{h}F^{-1}_{h}\mathcal{K}F^{-T}_{h}\nabla_{y}v\cdot\nabla_{y}\ast\,\mathrm{d}y-\int_{\Gamma_{0}}J_{h}e_{j}\cdot n\ast\,\mathrm{d}y.

Here
\ast
denotes a placeholder, for the argument of the functional.
Note, that the cell solutions
S_{h}^{-1}\xi_{h,j}
are implicitly defined by
E_{j}(h,S_{h}^{-1}\xi_{h,j})=0
.
We want to utilize the implicit function theorem
[
24
, Theorem 1.41]
to show the existence of the Fréchet-derivative of the cell solution and find a characterization of it.
Given that
E_{j}
is linear in
v\in V
and
F_{h},J_{h}
are differentiable in
h\in[-a^{*},a^{*}]
, it is straightforward to check that
E_{j}
is Fréchet-differentiable. The derivative
\partial_{u}E_{j}(h,S_{h}^{-1}\xi_{h,j})
is a bounded linear operator from
V\to V^{*}
and defined by

\partial_{u}E_{j}(h,S_{h}^{-1}\xi_{h,j})[v]=\int_{Y\setminus Y_{0}}J_{h}F^{-1}_{h}\mathcal{K}F^{-T}_{h}\nabla_{y}v\cdot\nabla_{y}\ast\,\mathrm{d}y.

For
v=0\in V
it holds
\|\partial_{u}E_{j}(h,S_{h}^{-1}\xi_{h,j})[v]\|_{V^{*}}=0
and for
v\neq 0

\displaystyle\|\partial_{u}E_{j}(h,S_{h}^{-1}\xi_{h,j})[v]\|_{V^{*}}=\hskip-3.0pt\sup_{\varphi\in V,\varphi\neq 0}\hskip-3.0pt\frac{|\partial_{u}E_{j}(h,S_{h}^{-1}\xi_{h,j})[v](\varphi)|}{\|\varphi\|_{H^{1}(Y\setminus Y_{0})}}\geq\frac{|\partial_{u}E_{j}(h,S_{h}^{-1}\xi_{h,j})[v](v)|}{\|v\|_{H^{1}(Y\setminus Y_{0})}}\geq C\|v\|_{H^{1}(Y\setminus Y_{0})},

where in the last inequality we applied assumption (A1),
Lemma

1
(i) and the Poincaré inequality. Therefore,
\|\partial_{u}E_{j}(h,S_{h}^{-1}\xi_{h,j})[v]\|_{V^{*}}\geq C\|v\|_{H^{1}(Y\setminus Y_{0})}
for all
v\in V
. With
[
44
, Theorem 6.7.1]
we can conclude that
\partial_{u}E_{j}(h,S_{h}^{-1}\xi_{h,j})
is injective and the image is closed, hence the inverse exists. Additionally, the inverse is also bounded. By the implicit function theorem, we obtain that the solution operator
h\mapsto S_{h}^{-1}\xi_{h,j}
is well-defined and Fréchet-differentiable, where the derivative is given by

\displaystyle\partial_{u}E_{j}(h,S_{h}^{-1}\xi_{h,j})[\partial_{h}(S_{h}^{-1}\xi_{h,j})]

\displaystyle=-\partial_{h}E_{j}(h,S_{h}^{-1}\xi_{h,j})

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\int_{Y\setminus Y_{0}}J_{h}F^{-1}_{h}\mathcal{K}F^{-T}_{h}\nabla_{y}(\partial_{h}(S_{h}^{-1}\xi_{h,j}))\cdot\nabla_{y}\ast\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=-\int_{Y\setminus Y_{0}}\partial_{h}\left(J_{h}F^{-1}_{h}\mathcal{K}F^{-T}_{h}\right)\nabla_{y}(S_{h}^{-1}\xi_{h,j})\cdot\nabla_{y}\ast\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle+\int_{\Gamma_{0}}\partial_{h}J_{h}e_{j}\cdot n\ast\,\mathrm{d}y,

understood as an element in
V^{*}
. With the same arguments as for the estimate in
Eq.

3.3
one can show that
\partial_{h}(S_{h}^{-1}\xi_{h,j})
is Lipschitz in
h
as well.
Finally, we conclude that
\partial_{h}K_{ij}
is well-defined and Lipschitz in
h
since all parts of the right-hand side of
Eq.

4.2
are bounded and Lipschitz.
∎

The above regularity ensures a quadratic error order if we utilize linear interpolation:

|K(h)-K_{\text{lin}}(h)|\leq C(\Delta h)^{2}\quad\text{for all }h\in[-a^{*},a^{*}].

Remark 3
.

Since
F_{h}^{-1}
and
J_{h}
are smooth regarding
h
, the implicit function theorem can also be used to show the existence of higher derivatives of the solution operator. Therefore, higher-order interpolation schemes can be applied as well. But we have to note, that the Lipschitz constant grows exponentially with
\nicefrac{{1}}{{a^{*}}}
. E.g., for the first derivative we see in the above proof, that
\|\partial_{h}F_{h}^{-1}\|\leq C\|D_{y}\psi\|\leq\nicefrac{{5C}}{{a^{*}}}
, which leads to

|\partial_{h}K(h+\Delta h)-\partial_{h}K(h)|\leq C\left(1+\frac{1}{(a^{*})^{2}}\right)\Delta h.

This means that high-order interpolations may need a small step size
\Delta h
to ensure the desired convergence order.

Remark 4
.

By assumption (A1) we have that the underlying conduction matrix
\mathcal{K}
is not dependent on the space variable. For some applications this may not hold and
\mathcal{K}
may be a function of
y
. In this case the results of
Section

3
are still valid under the condition that
\mathcal{K}
is Lipschitz in regard to
y
. The property
K\in C^{1,1}([-a^{*},a^{*}],\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
requires the additional assumption that
\mathcal{K}\in C^{1,1}(Y\setminus Y_{0})
.

In the following,
K_{\text{pre}}
stands for the precomputed conductivity, while
\mathcal{E}_{\text{pre}}:=\sup_{|h|\leq a^{*}}|K(h)-K_{\text{pre}}(h)|
denotes the corresponding interpolation error.
In the case that
K_{\text{pre}}=K_{\text{lin}}
, or a higher order interpolation,
K_{\text{pre}}
is a Lipschitz continuous function in
h
and the results of
Section

3
can be transferred to ensure the existence of solutions for the precomputed system.
The important question is how the interpolation error propagates over time, this is analyzed in the following theorem.

Theorem 2

(Error propagation of precomputation)
.

Let
(h,\Theta,\vartheta)
denote the solution with the exact conductivity and
(h_{\text{pre}},\Theta_{\text{pre}},\vartheta_{\text{pre}})
the solution with the precomputed values
K_{\text{pre}}
. Set
\bar{T}^{*}=\min(T^{*},T_{\text{pre}}^{*})
such that both solutions are defined on
(0,\bar{T}^{*})
. Assume that

\partial_{t}\Theta_{\text{pre}},\nabla\Theta_{\text{pre}}\in L^{\infty}((0,\bar{T}^{*})\times\Omega)
,

\partial_{t}\vartheta_{\text{pre}},\nabla_{y}\vartheta_{\text{pre}}\in L^{\infty}((0,\bar{T}^{*})\times\Omega\times Y_{0})
. Then, there exists a
C>0
(depending on
\bar{T}^{*}
) such that

\|\bar{\Theta}\|_{L^{\infty}((0,\bar{T}^{*});L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\bar{\vartheta}\|_{L^{\infty}((0,\bar{T}^{*});L^{2}(\Omega\times Y_{0}))}+\|\nabla\bar{\Theta}\|_{L^{2}((0,\bar{T}^{*})\times\Omega)}+\|\nabla\bar{\vartheta}\|_{L^{2}((0,\bar{T}^{*})\times\Omega\times Y_{0})}\leq C\mathcal{E}_{\text{pre}},

where
(\bar{h},\bar{\Theta},\bar{\vartheta})\coloneqq(h-h_{\text{pre}},\Theta-\Theta_{\text{pre}},\vartheta-\vartheta_{\text{pre}})_{|(0,\bar{T}^{*})}
denotes the difference of both solutions.

Proof.

The proof follows the same idea as the uniqueness proof in
Lemma

5
. Here, we only have to consider the macroscopic conductivity term in more detail, all other terms can be estimated as previously shown. For the integral
I_{3}
in the proof of
Lemma

5
, we have

\displaystyle I_{3}=\int_{\Omega}K(h)\nabla\bar{\Theta}\cdot\nabla\bar{\Theta}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\left[K(h)-K_{\text{pre}}(h_{\text{pre}})\right]\nabla\Theta_{\text{pre}}\nabla\bar{\Theta}\,\mathrm{d}x.

The integral on the right can be further estimated by

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}

\displaystyle|\left[K(h)-K_{\text{pre}}(h_{\text{pre}})\right]\nabla\Theta_{\text{pre}}\cdot\nabla\bar{\Theta}|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}|(K(h)-K(h_{\text{pre}}))\nabla\Theta_{\text{pre}}\cdot\nabla\bar{\Theta}|\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}|(K(h_{\text{pre}})-K_{\text{pre}}(h_{\text{pre}}))\nabla\Theta_{\text{pre}}\cdot\nabla\bar{\Theta}|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C\|\nabla\Theta_{\text{pre}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|\nabla\bar{\Theta}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left(\|\bar{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\mathcal{E}_{\text{pre}}\right).

Now plugging this estimate into the proof steps of
Lemma

5
, we obtain, for any time interval
(0,t)\subset(0,\bar{T}^{*})
, the following estimate

\|\bar{\Theta}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\bar{\vartheta}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega\times Y_{0})}^{2}+\|\nabla\bar{\Theta}\|^{2}_{L^{2}((0,t)\times\Omega)}+\|\nabla_{y}\bar{\vartheta}\|^{2}_{L^{2}((0,t)\times\Omega\times Y_{0})}\\
\leq C\mathcal{E}_{\text{pre}}^{2}+C(1+t^{2})\left(\|\bar{\Theta}\|_{L^{2}((0,t)\times\Omega)}^{2}+\|\bar{\vartheta}\|_{L^{2}((0,t)\times\Omega\times Y_{0})}^{2}\right).

Applying the Grönwall inequality leads to the desired result.
∎

4.2
Resolving the nonlinearity.

Even with the precomputing strategy at hand, we are still facing a nonlinear two-scale problem. We propose a semi-implicit time-stepping method to resolve the nonlinearity, which is often applied to nonlinear equations in numerical simulations. To this end, given a time step
\Delta t
we define the discrete time point
t_{i}=i\Delta t
. Then we are looking at each
i>0
for the time-discrete solution
(\Theta_{i},\vartheta_{i})\in\mathrm{X}
of

\begin{split}&\int_{\Omega}C(h_{i})\frac{\Theta_{i}-\Theta_{i-1}}{\Delta t}\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\int_{Y_{0}}c(h_{i})\frac{\vartheta_{i}-\vartheta_{i-1}}{\Delta t}\phi\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}K_{\text{pre}}(h_{i})\nabla\left(\frac{\Theta_{i}+\Theta_{i-1}}{2}\right)\cdot\nabla\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&+\int_{\Omega}\int_{Y_{0}}\kappa(h_{i})\nabla_{y}\frac{\vartheta_{i}+\vartheta_{i-1}}{2}\cdot\nabla_{y}\phi\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x+\hskip-2.0pt\int_{\Omega}\int_{Y_{0}}\vartheta_{i}v(h_{i})\cdot\nabla_{y}\phi\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x=\hskip-2.0pt\int_{\Omega}\left(F_{i}-L(h_{i})\frac{h_{i}-h_{i-1}}{\Delta t}\right)\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&+\int_{\Omega}\int_{Y_{0}}f(h_{i})\phi\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}\partial_{h}C(h_{i})\frac{h_{i}-h_{i-1}}{\Delta t}\Theta_{i-1}\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}\partial_{h}c(h_{i})\frac{h_{i}-h_{i-1}}{\Delta t}\vartheta_{i-1}\phi\,\mathrm{d}x\end{split}

(4.3)

for all
(\Phi,\phi)\in\mathrm{X}
where

\mathrm{X}\coloneqq\left\{(\Phi,\phi)\in H^{1}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega,H^{1}(Y_{0})):\Phi(x)=\phi(x,y)\text{ a.e. on }\Omega\times\Gamma_{0}\right\}.

To find
(\Theta_{i},\vartheta_{i})
a differential equation has to be solved at each time step. But we now linearize the problem by using an explicit scheme to compute the height function
h
, to be precise

h_{i}=h_{i-1}+\Delta t(\Theta_{i-1}-\Theta^{ref})\quad\text{ for }i>0\quad\text{and}\quad h_{0}=0.

This also leads to the simplification of
\nicefrac{{(h_{i}-h_{i-1})}}{{\Delta t}}=\Theta_{i-1}-\Theta^{ref}
in the above scheme. In (
4.3
) we used the product rule to split the time derivative into the derivative of
\Theta
and of
C
, respectively for
\vartheta
and
c
. This simplifies the proof for the time stepping error. Note, that also the more natural discretization

\partial_{t}(C(h)\Theta)\approx\frac{C(h_{i})\Theta_{i}-C(h_{i-1})\Theta_{i-1}}{\Delta t}

could be used and only deviates from the above scheme by a term of order
\Delta t
. For the gradients, we utilize an average between the current and previous time step since this fits more naturally in the following proof idea. Note, that this scheme requires
\nabla\Theta_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
\nabla_{y}\vartheta_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega\times Y_{0})
. But again, just
\nabla\Theta_{i}
, and
\nabla_{y}\vartheta_{i}
, could be used.

If
|h_{i}|\leq a^{*}
, the solution of the system (
4.3
) exists and is unique since it is a linear time-discretized parabolic two-scale equation. Given that
\Theta_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
we can always carry out one time step if
\Delta t
is small enough. To show that the discrete solution exists on a larger time interval
(0,T^{*}_{\text{dis}})
, we need to show that the discrete solution
\Theta_{i}
can be bounded in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
independent of the height function. For this, we can proceed as in
Lemma

4
, e.g., we would need to carry out the following steps:

1.

Choose a
M\geq 1
and a
N\in\mathbb{N}
, then define the time horizon
T^{*}_{\text{dis}}=\nicefrac{{a^{*}}}{{M^{4}}}
and step
\Delta t=\nicefrac{{T^{*}_{\text{dis}}}}{{N}}
.

2.

Start with a fixed height function
\{h_{i}\}_{i=0}^{N}
defined by

h_{i}=h_{i-1}+\Delta tb_{i},\quad h_{0}=0\quad\text{ and }\quad\|b_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq M.

3.

Derive a
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
bound for the solution of
Eq.

4.3
for the prescribed height function
\{h_{i}\}_{i=0}^{N}
independent of
M
. This follows with similar computations as in
Lemma

4
and by testing with
\Phi=\Theta_{i}+\Theta_{i-1},\phi=\vartheta_{i}+\vartheta_{i-1}
and summing over all time steps. As the proof is similar to the previous one, it will not be presented in detail here.

4.

Choose the
M
corresponding to the solution bound and obtain that the discrete method is reasonable and the solution exists on a nonempty interval
(0,T^{*}_{\text{dis}})
.

Note, that the above procedure also ensures that for a smaller time step
\Delta t
the discrete solution exists, at least, on the same time interval. The error we introduce through this scheme is analyzed in the next lemma.

Lemma 8

(Error of time stepping scheme)
.

Let
(\hat{h},\hat{\Theta},\hat{\vartheta})
denote the solution that is continuous in time, exists on the time interval
(0,T^{*})
and uses the precomputed values
K_{\text{lin}}
. Denote with
\{h_{i},\Theta_{i},\vartheta_{i}\}_{i=0}^{N}
the time discrete solution of
Eq.

4.3
that exists on
[0,T^{*}_{\text{dis}}]
, with
K_{\text{pre}}=K_{\text{lin}}
. Let the assumptions

•

\hat{\Theta}\in W^{2,\infty}((0,T^{*});W^{1,\infty}(\Omega))
,

•

F\in C(\overline{S\times\Omega})
,

•

\Delta t\leq\nicefrac{{C(a^{*})}}{{8K(a^{*})}}
,

•

\hat{\vartheta}\in W^{2,\infty}((0,T^{*});W^{1,\infty}(\Omega\times Y_{0}))
,

•

f\in C(\overline{S\times\Omega};C^{0,1}(\overline{Y_{0}}))
,

be fulfilled. Let
N_{t}\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
\Delta tN_{t}<\min{(T^{*},T^{*}_{\text{dis}})}
and
\Delta t(1+N_{t})\geq\min{(T^{*},T^{*}_{\text{dis}})}
. Then, there exists a
C>0
(depending on
(\hat{\Theta},\hat{\vartheta})
) such that

\sup_{i=1,\dots,N_{t}}\|\hat{\Theta}(t_{i})-\Theta_{i}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\|\hat{\vartheta}(t_{i})-\vartheta_{i}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\|\hat{h}(t_{i})-h_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C(\Delta t+\Delta h).

Proof.

We follow the proof ideas of
[
14
, Section 3 and 4]
where numerical approaches for similar nonlinear problems are analyzed. The main difference in our case is, that we consider a two-scale system, the nonlinearity is coupled to the height function
h
, a less regular conductivity, and a different right-hand side because of
C(h)
. But the general idea from
[
14
]
can be transferred to our case. We denote by
\bar{\cdot}_{i}
the difference between the continuous and discrete solution at time point
t_{i}
, e.g.,
\bar{\Theta}_{i}=\hat{\Theta}(t_{i})-\Theta_{i}
. We start by investigating the difference between the height functions of both solutions. By definition of both functions, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\bar{h}_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\|\hat{h}(t_{i})-h_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\|\hat{h}(t_{i-1})-h_{i-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\|\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}(\hat{\Theta}(s)-\Theta^{ref})\,\mathrm{d}s-\Delta t(\Theta_{i-1}-\Theta^{ref})\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\|\hat{h}(t_{i-1})-h_{i-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\Delta t\|\bar{\Theta}_{i-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\|\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\int_{t_{i-1}}^{s}\partial_{t}\hat{\Theta}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\,\mathrm{d}s\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\|\hat{h}(t_{i-1})-h_{i-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\Delta t\|\bar{\Theta}_{i-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C(\Delta t)^{2}

in the estimates we used the fundamental theorem of calculus and that
\partial_{t}\hat{\Theta}
is bounded. Iteratively applying the above argument leads to

\|\bar{h}_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\sum_{n=1}^{i-1}\left[\Delta t\|\bar{\Theta}_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C(\Delta t)^{2}\right].

(4.4)

Next, we derive the estimates for the temperature. To this end, we only show the error estimate for the macroscopic function
\Theta
. The reason for this is, that the estimates for
\vartheta
follow with the same steps since the microscopic equation has a similar structure. This allows us to reduce the length of the proof considerably. Therefore, let the continuous solution
\hat{\Theta}
fulfill

\int_{\Omega}C(\hat{h})\partial_{t}\hat{\Theta}\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\partial_{h}C(\hat{h})\partial_{t}\hat{h}\hat{\Theta}\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}K_{\text{lin}}(\hat{h})\nabla\hat{\Theta}\cdot\nabla\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}L(\hat{h})\partial_{t}\hat{h}\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x

(4.5)

and the time-discrete solution

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}C(h_{i})\frac{\Theta_{i}-\Theta_{i-1}}{\Delta t}&\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\partial_{h}C(h_{i})(\Theta_{i-1}-\Theta^{ref})\Theta_{i-1}\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&+\int_{\Omega}K_{\text{lin}}(h_{i})\nabla\left(\frac{\Theta_{i}+\Theta_{i-1}}{2}\right)\cdot\nabla\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}L(h_{i})(\Theta_{i-1}-\Theta^{ref})\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

(4.6)

To derive an error estimate at
t_{i}
, we consider (
4.5
) localized at
t_{i}
, subtract the
Eq.

4.5
and (
4.6
) from each other and test with
\Phi=\bar{\Theta}_{i}-\bar{\Theta}_{i-1}
. We obtain four terms which we estimate individually. Let us introduce the discrete time derivative
d_{t}
which is defined by
d_{t}\Theta_{i}=\nicefrac{{\Theta_{i}-\Theta_{i-1}}}{{\Delta t}}
. Additionally, we abuse the notation and write
d_{t}\hat{\Theta}_{i}=\nicefrac{{\hat{\Theta}(t_{i})-\hat{\Theta}(t_{i-1})}}{{\Delta t}}
. We start with the time derivative term, which yields

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}

\displaystyle C(h_{i})d_{t}\Theta_{i}\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}C(\hat{h})\partial_{t}\hat{\Theta}(t_{i})\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x=\underbrace{\int_{\Omega}C(h_{i})d_{t}\Theta_{i}\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}C(h_{i})d_{t}\hat{\Theta}_{i}\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x}_{=I_{1}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\int_{\Omega}C(h_{i})d_{t}\hat{\Theta}_{i}\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}C(\hat{h}(t_{i}))d_{t}\hat{\Theta}_{i}\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x}_{=I_{2}}+\underbrace{\int_{\Omega}C(\hat{h}(t_{i}))d_{t}\hat{\Theta}_{i}\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}C(\hat{h}(t_{i}))\partial_{t}\hat{\Theta}(t_{i})\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x}_{=I_{3}}.

For terms on the right-hand side, it holds

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\Delta t\int_{\Omega}C(h_{i})(d_{t}\bar{\Theta}_{i})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\geq\Delta tC(a^{*})\|d_{t}\bar{\Theta}_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},

\displaystyle|I_{2}|

\displaystyle\leq C\|\bar{h}_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\bar{\Theta}_{i}-\bar{\Theta}_{i-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

\displaystyle|I_{3}|

\displaystyle\leq C\Delta t\|\partial_{tt}\hat{\Theta}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\|_{L^{\infty}((0,T^{*})\times\Omega)}\|\bar{\Theta}_{i}-\bar{\Theta}_{i-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C(\Delta t)^{3}+\varepsilon\Delta t\|d_{t}\bar{\Theta}_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Here, we used for
I_{2}
and
I_{3}
that
C
is Lipschitz, the Hölder inequality, and the Young inequality with a small
\varepsilon>0
.
I_{2}
can be estimated further by utilizing
Eq.

4.4
,

\displaystyle|I_{2}|

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{n=1}^{i-1}\left[\Delta t\|\bar{\Theta}_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C(\Delta t)^{2}\right]\|\bar{\Theta}_{i}-\bar{\Theta}_{i-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=C\sum_{n=1}^{i-1}\left[\Delta t\|\bar{\Theta}_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\Delta t\|d_{t}\bar{\Theta}_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C(\Delta t)^{2}\Delta t\|d_{t}\bar{\Theta}_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{n=1}^{i-1}\left[(\Delta t)^{2}\|\bar{\Theta}_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\varepsilon(\Delta t)^{2}\|d_{t}\bar{\Theta}_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+C(\Delta t)^{4}+\varepsilon(\Delta t)^{2}\|d_{t}\bar{\Theta}_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C(\Delta t)^{3}+C\varepsilon\Delta t\|d_{t}\bar{\Theta}_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+C\Delta t\sum_{n=1}^{i-1}\Delta t\|\bar{\Theta}_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Next, the term with
\partial_{h}C
, where we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\partial_{h}C(h_{i})

\displaystyle(\Theta_{i-1}-\Theta^{ref})\Theta_{i-1}\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}\partial_{h}C(\hat{h}(t_{i}))(\hat{\Theta}(t_{i})-\Theta^{ref})\hat{\Theta}(t_{i})\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\int_{\Omega}\partial_{h}C(h_{i})(\Theta_{i-1}-\Theta^{ref})\Theta_{i-1}\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}\partial_{h}C(h_{i})(\Theta_{i-1}-\Theta^{ref})\hat{\Theta}(t_{i-1})\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x}_{=I_{4}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\int_{\Omega}\partial_{h}C(h_{i})(\Theta_{i-1}-\Theta^{ref})\hat{\Theta}(t_{i-1})\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}\partial_{h}C(h_{i})(\hat{\Theta}(t_{i-1})-\Theta^{ref})\hat{\Theta}(t_{i-1})\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x}_{=I_{5}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\int_{\Omega}\partial_{h}C(h_{i})(\hat{\Theta}(t_{i-1})-\Theta^{ref})\hat{\Theta}(t_{i-1})\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}\partial_{h}C(\hat{h}(t_{i}))(\hat{\Theta}(t_{i-1})-\Theta^{ref})\hat{\Theta}(t_{i-1})\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x}_{=I_{6}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\int_{\Omega}\partial_{h}C(\hat{h}(t_{i}))(\hat{\Theta}(t_{i-1})-\Theta^{ref})\hat{\Theta}(t_{i-1})\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}\partial_{h}C(\hat{h}(t_{i}))(\hat{\Theta}(t_{i})-\Theta^{ref})\hat{\Theta}(t_{i})\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x}_{=I_{7}}

Using that
\partial_{h}C
is well-defined and bounded (since
\partial_{h}J_{h}
is well-defined and bounded; see the proof of
Lemma

7
) and that the solutions are bounded, we can obtain with similar arguments as before

\displaystyle|I_{4}|+|I_{5}|

\displaystyle\leq C\|\bar{\Theta}_{i-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\bar{\Theta}_{i}-\bar{\Theta}_{i-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\Delta t\|\bar{\Theta}_{i-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\varepsilon\Delta t\|d_{t}\bar{\Theta}_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},

\displaystyle|I_{6}|

\displaystyle\leq C\|\bar{h}_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\bar{\Theta}_{i}-\bar{\Theta}_{i-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

\displaystyle|I_{7}|

\displaystyle\leq C\Delta t\|\partial_{t}\hat{\Theta}\|_{L^{\infty}((0,T^{*})\times\Omega)}\|\bar{\Theta}_{i}-\bar{\Theta}_{i-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C(\Delta t)^{3}+\varepsilon\Delta t\|d_{t}\bar{\Theta}_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Since the term containing
L
is comparable to the previous ones, we first handle it and afterward estimate the gradients. By introducing the mixed terms, we have the following equality

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}L(h_{i})(\Theta_{i-1}-\Theta^{ref})\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}L(\hat{h}(t_{i}))(\hat{\Theta}(t_{i})-\Theta^{ref})\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\int_{\Omega}L(h_{i})(\Theta_{i-1}-\Theta^{ref})\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}L(h_{i})(\hat{\Theta}(t_{i-1})-\Theta^{ref})\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x}_{=I_{8}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\int_{\Omega}L(h_{i})(\hat{\Theta}(t_{i-1})-\Theta^{ref})\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}L(h_{i})(\hat{\Theta}(t_{i})-\Theta^{ref})\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x}_{=I_{9}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\int_{\Omega}L(h_{i})(\hat{\Theta}(t_{i})-\Theta^{ref})\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}L(\hat{h}(t_{i}))(\hat{\Theta}(t_{i})-\Theta^{ref})\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x}_{=I_{10}}

All of
I_{8},I_{9}
and
I_{10}
are of the same structure as before and we can find the following estimates

\displaystyle|I_{8}|

\displaystyle\leq C\Delta t\|\bar{\Theta}_{i-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\varepsilon\Delta t\|d_{t}\bar{\Theta}_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},

\displaystyle|I_{9}|

\displaystyle\leq C(\Delta t)^{3}+\varepsilon\Delta t\|d_{t}\bar{\Theta}_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},

\displaystyle|I_{10}|

\displaystyle\leq C\|\bar{h}_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\bar{\Theta}_{i}-\bar{\Theta}_{i-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Let us next consider the gradient terms, here the estimates are more involved but follow the general idea outlined in
[
14
]
. Introducing mixed terms into the difference first leads to

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}

\displaystyle K_{\text{lin}}(h_{i})\nabla\left(\frac{\Theta_{i}+\Theta_{i-1}}{2}\right)\cdot\nabla\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}K_{\text{lin}}(\hat{h}(t_{i}))\nabla\hat{\Theta}(t_{i})\cdot\nabla\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\int_{\Omega}K_{\text{lin}}(h_{i})\nabla\left(\frac{\Theta_{i}+\Theta_{i-1}}{2}\right)\cdot\nabla\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}K_{\text{lin}}(h_{i})\nabla\left(\frac{\hat{\Theta}(t_{i})+\hat{\Theta}(t_{i-1})}{2}\right)\cdot\nabla\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x}_{=I_{11,i}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\int_{\Omega}K_{\text{lin}}(h_{i})\nabla\left(\frac{\hat{\Theta}(t_{i})+\hat{\Theta}(t_{i-1})}{2}\right)\cdot\nabla\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}K_{\text{lin}}(\hat{h}(t_{i}))\nabla\left(\frac{\hat{\Theta}(t_{i})+\hat{\Theta}(t_{i-1})}{2}\right)\cdot\nabla\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x}_{=I_{12,i}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\int_{\Omega}K_{\text{lin}}(\hat{h}(t_{i}))\nabla\left(\frac{\hat{\Theta}(t_{i})+\hat{\Theta}(t_{i-1})}{2}\right)\cdot\nabla\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}K_{\text{lin}}(\hat{h}(t_{i}))\nabla\hat{\Theta}(t_{i})\cdot\nabla\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x}_{=I_{13,i}}.

Starting with
I_{11,i}
, where we obtain

\displaystyle 2I_{11,i}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}K_{\text{lin}}(h_{i})\nabla(\bar{\Theta}_{i}+\bar{\Theta}_{i-1})\cdot\nabla(\bar{\Theta}_{i}-\bar{\Theta}_{i-1})\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}K_{\text{lin}}(h_{i})(\nabla\bar{\Theta}_{i}\cdot\nabla\bar{\Theta}_{i}-\nabla\bar{\Theta}_{i-1}\cdot\nabla\bar{\Theta}_{i-1})\,\mathrm{d}x.

To further estimate the error, we now sum over
m=1,\dots,i
, and using summation by parts we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{i}\int_{\Omega}K_{\text{lin}}(h_{i})(\nabla\bar{\Theta}_{i}\cdot\nabla\bar{\Theta}_{i}-\nabla\bar{\Theta}_{i-1}\cdot\nabla\bar{\Theta}_{i-1})\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{i-1}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\bar{\Theta}_{m}\cdot\nabla\bar{\Theta}_{m}(K_{\text{lin}}(h_{m})-K_{\text{lin}}(h_{m+1}))\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 50.0pt+\int_{\Omega}\nabla\bar{\Theta}_{i}\cdot\nabla\bar{\Theta}_{i}K_{\text{lin}}(h_{i})\,\mathrm{d}x.

The sum on the right can be further estimated by

\left|\sum_{m=1}^{i-1}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\bar{\Theta}_{m}\cdot\nabla\bar{\Theta}_{m}(K_{\text{lin}}(h_{m})-K_{\text{lin}}(h_{m+1}))\,\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq C\sum_{m=1}^{i-1}\Delta t\|\nabla\bar{\Theta}_{m}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},

where we used that
K_{\text{lin}}
is Lipschitz. For
I_{12}
we start with a similar strategy,

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{i}I_{12,i}=\underbrace{\int_{\Omega}(K_{\text{lin}}(h_{i})-K_{\text{lin}}(\hat{h}(t_{i})))\nabla\bar{\Theta}_{i}\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{\hat{\Theta}(t_{i})+\hat{\Theta}(t_{i-1})}{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x}_{=I_{12,a}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\sum_{m=1}^{i-1}\int_{\Omega}(K_{\text{lin}}(h_{m})-K_{\text{lin}}(\hat{h}(t_{m})))\nabla\bar{\Theta}_{m}\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{\hat{\Theta}(t_{m})+\hat{\Theta}(t_{m-1})}{2}-\frac{\hat{\Theta}(t_{m+1})+\hat{\Theta}(t_{m})}{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x}_{=I_{12,b}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\sum_{m=1}^{i-1}\int_{\Omega}\left[K_{\text{lin}}(h_{m})-K_{\text{lin}}(\hat{h}(t_{m}))-K_{\text{lin}}(h_{m+1})+K_{\text{lin}}(\hat{h}(t_{m+1}))\right]\nabla\bar{\Theta}_{m}\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{\hat{\Theta}(t_{m})+\hat{\Theta}(t_{m-1})}{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x}_{=I_{12,c}}.

The first term
I_{12,a}
can be estimated with the same ideas as before, leading to

|I_{12,a}|\leq\varepsilon\|\nabla\bar{\Theta}_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C(\Delta t)^{2}+C\sum_{n=1}^{i-1}\Delta t\|\bar{\Theta}_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

For
I_{12,b}
we need
Eq.

4.4
and that the time derivative of
\nabla\hat{\Theta}
exists, to get the estimate

\displaystyle|I_{12,b}|

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{m=1}^{i-1}\Delta t\|\nabla\bar{\Theta}_{m}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left[\Delta t+\sum_{n=1}^{m-1}\Delta t\|\bar{\Theta}_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right]

\displaystyle=C\sum_{m=1}^{i-1}\Delta t\|\nabla\bar{\Theta}_{m}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left[\Delta t+(i-1-m)\Delta t\|\bar{\Theta}_{m}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C(\Delta t)^{2}+C\sum_{m=1}^{i-1}\Delta t\|\bar{\Theta}_{m}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}.

To bound
I_{12,c}
we use that
K_{\text{lin}}\in H^{1}((-a^{*},a^{*}))
and since the exact
K
is in
C^{1,1}
we also have
|\partial_{h}K_{\text{lin}}(h_{1})-\partial_{h}K_{\text{lin}}(h_{2})|\leq C|h_{1}-h_{2}|+\Delta h
, for a.a.
h_{1},h_{2}\in(-a^{*},a^{*})
. This yields

\displaystyle|I_{12,c}|

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{m=1}^{i-1}\Delta t\int_{\Omega}\|\nabla\bar{\Theta}_{m}\|\int_{0}^{1}\Bigl|\partial_{h}K(h_{m}+\tau(h_{m+1}-h_{m}))

\displaystyle\hskip 230.0pt-\partial_{h}K(\hat{h}(t_{m})+\tau(\hat{h}(t_{m+1})-\hat{h}(t_{m})))\Bigr|\,\mathrm{d}\tau\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{m=1}^{i-1}\Delta t\int_{\Omega}\|\nabla\bar{\Theta}_{m}\|\int_{0}^{1}\left|\Delta h+h(t_{m})-h_{m}+\tau\left[h_{m+1}-h_{m}-\hat{h}(t_{m+1})+\hat{h}(t_{m})\right]\right|\,\mathrm{d}\tau\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{m=1}^{i-1}\Delta t\|\nabla\bar{\Theta}_{m}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left[\Delta h+\Delta t+\sum_{n=1}^{m-1}\Delta t\|\bar{\Theta}_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right].

Next, we apply the Young-inequality and can proceed in the same way as for the estimate of
I_{12,a}
. Lastly,
I_{13}
can again be estimated by summation, leading to

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{m=1}^{i}I_{13,i}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\left|K_{\text{lin}}(h(t_{i}))\nabla\left(\frac{\hat{\Theta}(t_{i-1})-\hat{\Theta}(t_{i})}{2}\right)\nabla\bar{\Theta}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}x\right|

\displaystyle+\sum_{m=1}^{i-1}\left|\int_{\Omega}(K_{\text{lin}}(h(t_{m}))-K_{\text{lin}}(h(t_{m+1})))\nabla\bar{\Theta}_{m}\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{\hat{\Theta}(t_{m-1})-\hat{\Theta}(t_{m})}{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\right|

\displaystyle+\sum_{m=1}^{i-1}\left|\int_{\Omega}K_{\text{lin}}(h(t_{m+1}))\nabla\bar{\Theta}_{m}\cdot\left(\frac{\hat{\Theta}(t_{m-1})-\hat{\Theta}(t_{m})}{2}-\frac{\hat{\Theta}(t_{m})-\hat{\Theta}(t_{m+1})}{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\Delta t\|\nabla\bar{\Theta}_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\sum_{m=1}^{i-1}C\|\nabla\bar{\Theta}_{m}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}(\Delta t)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C(\Delta t)^{2}+\varepsilon\|\nabla\bar{\Theta}_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\sum_{m=1}^{i-1}\Delta t\|\nabla\bar{\Theta}_{m}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

In the first inequality above the Lipschitz continuity of
K_{\text{lin}}
, the Hölder inequality, and the time-regularity of
\nabla\hat{\Theta}
were utilized. In the second inequality, we used Young again.

We can now combine all of the above estimates. To this end, we also have to sum
I_{1},\dots I_{10}
over
1,\dots,i
to combine them with the gradient estimates, to be precise we would first sum and then carry out the above computations inside the sum. This leads to the estimate

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{i}

\displaystyle\Delta tC(a^{*})\|d_{t}\bar{\Theta}_{m}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+K(a^{*})\|\nabla\bar{\Theta}_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C(\Delta t)^{2}+C\varepsilon\|\nabla\bar{\Theta}_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\varepsilon\sum_{m=1}^{i}\Delta tC\|d_{t}\bar{\Theta}_{m}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+C\sum_{m=1}^{i-1}\Delta t\left[\|\bar{\Theta}_{m}\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\|\bar{\Theta}_{m}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla\bar{\Theta}_{m}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right]+C\sum_{m=1}^{i}\Delta t\sum_{n=1}^{m-1}\Delta t\|\bar{\Theta}_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C(\Delta h)^{2}.

(4.7)

We are missing the
L^{2}
-norm of
\bar{\Theta}_{i}
on the left for the final estimate. Therefore, we use the same computation as
[
14
, Eq. (3.19)]
to obtain

\begin{split}K(a^{*})\left(\|\bar{\Theta}_{i}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\bar{\Theta}_{i-1}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)&\leq 2K(a^{*})\Delta t\int_{\Omega}d_{t}\bar{\Theta}_{i}\bar{\Theta}_{i-1}\,\mathrm{d}x+K(a^{*})(\Delta t)^{2}\|d_{t}\bar{\Theta}_{i}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
&\leq\frac{C(a^{*})\Delta t}{4}\|d_{t}\bar{\Theta}_{i}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C\Delta t\|\bar{\Theta}_{i-1}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.\end{split}

(4.8)

For the last inequality, we used the assumption
\Delta t\leq\nicefrac{{C(a^{*})}}{{8K(a^{*})}}
. Next, we sum
Eq.

4.8
from
1,\dots,i
and add the estimate to
4.7
. Then the sum over
\|d_{t}\bar{\Theta}\|
which originates when summing (
4.8
) can be collected into the already present term in (
4.7
).
Additionally, the
\varepsilon
on the right side originates from the Young inequality and can be chosen such, that all terms connected to
\varepsilon
can be incorporated into the left-hand side. All other sums on the right can be further bounded by the
H^{1}
-norm, which yields

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{i}\Delta tC\|d_{t}\bar{\Theta}_{m}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C\|\bar{\Theta}_{i}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C(\Delta t)^{2}+C(\Delta h)^{2}+C\sum_{m=1}^{i-1}\Delta t\|\bar{\Theta}_{m}\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}.

Finally, applying the discrete Grönwall lemma gives the desired result. As mentioned at the start the estimates for
\bar{\vartheta}
follow analogously. Plugging the estimate for
\bar{\Theta}
back into
Eq.

4.4
, we find the estimate for
\bar{h}
.

Remark 5
.

The convergence of the time-stepping scheme requires that the precomputing also gets refined with smaller time steps. This condition appears because the linear interpolation has a piecewise constant derivative. This is not necessary for a smoother interpolation, for example, quadratic splines.

4.3
Further error sources

In addition to the discretization in time, we also have to discretize the function space.
To this end, we consider a finite element (FE) approach.
Define the triangulation
\mathcal{T}_{H_{M},\Omega}
of
\Omega
and
\mathcal{T}_{H_{m},Y_{0}}
of
Y_{0}
, where
H_{M}
and
H_{m}
denote the largest edge length of the simplices in
\mathcal{T}_{H_{M},\Omega}
and
\mathcal{T}_{H_{m},Y_{0}}
respectively.
Denote with
\mathbb{P}_{1}
the space of linear polynomials. On the macro scale, we define the finite element space
\mathrm{X}_{H_{M},\Omega}\subset H^{1}(\Omega)
by

\mathrm{X}_{H_{M},\Omega}=\left\{\Phi\in C(\overline{\Omega}):\Phi_{|T}\in\mathbb{P}_{1}(T)\text{ for all }T\in\mathcal{T}_{H_{M},\Omega}\right\}.

Similarly, on the microscale the space
\mathrm{X}_{H_{m},Y_{0}}\subset H^{1}(Y_{0})
is given by

\mathrm{X}_{H_{m},Y_{0}}=\left\{\phi\in C(\overline{Y_{0}}):\phi_{|T}\in\mathbb{P}_{1}(T)\text{ for all }T\in\mathcal{T}_{H_{m},Y_{0}}\right\}.

Let
N_{M}
denote the degrees of freedom (DOFs) on the macroscale and
N_{m}
DOFs on the microscale. Define the basis functions
\{\Phi_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N_{M}}
such that
\text{span}(\Phi_{n})=\mathrm{X}_{H_{M},\Omega}
and equally the micro basis functions
\{\phi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N_{m}}
. The time discretized finite element approximation of the solutions
(\Theta,\vartheta,h)
are then given by (
H=(H_{M},H_{m})
)

\displaystyle\Theta_{i,H}=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{M}}\alpha_{i,n}\Phi_{n}(x),\quad\vartheta_{i,H}=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{M}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{m}}\beta_{i,n,k}\Phi_{n}(x)\phi_{k}(y)\quad\text{ and }\quad h_{i,H}=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{M}}h_{i,n}\Phi_{n}(x),

with the additional condition
\Theta_{i,H}=\vartheta_{i,H\,|\Gamma_{0}}
for all
i=1,\dots,N
. For the coefficients it holds,
\alpha_{i,n},\beta_{i,n,k},h_{i,n}\in\mathbb{R}
.

The above FE approach introduces another error in the numerical approximation. The error estimate from
Lemma

8
can also be extended to include the projection error in regards to the FE space. This was carried out for a comparable problem in
[
14
, Section 3]
and could be transferred to our setup under regularity assumptions of the solutions. Here, we do not carry out the complete proof, instead, we demonstrate the error in the following section through numerical simulations.

Note that the above FE approach is a natural candidate for discretizing the problem, but can become quite expensive due to the high dimensionality of
\vartheta
, especially in three dimensions. We still use the above method since we only want to demonstrate the previous analytical results on a two-dimensional setup. Parallelization is used to speed up the calculations; see
Section

5
for details. However, in other applications, this procedure may not be feasible. In
Section

1.2
we gave an overview of possible extensions and different approaches. Note, that the presented precomputing method and theory could also be combined with the alternatives to reduce the computational effort.

Next to the discretization of
(\Theta,\vartheta)
we also need to solve the cell problems
Eq.

2.2e
with a FE approach. To this end introduce the mesh size
H_{\text{cell}}
of the mesh for
Y\setminus Y(h)
. Let
K_{H}
denote the effective conductivity computed with the FE solution. Under the assumption that we use piecewise linear FEs for solving the cell problems and the cell solutions are sufficiently smooth, we can obtain the error estimate

|K(h)-K_{H}(h)|\leq CH_{\text{cell}},

for a
C<\infty
independent of
H_{\text{cell}}
.

Simulations

In this last section, we want to investigate and back up the previous analysis results with numerical simulations. To this end, we apply the time-discretized, finite element approach with precomputation as explained in
Section

4
. Before we study the convergence behavior, we introduce the general problem setup and explain some implementation details.
We use the FEM library FEniCS
[
1
]
and implement a custom parallelization for the micro problems. Our code is freely available online
[
16
]
.

As mentioned in the previous section, we want to apply an approach where the time step for
\Theta
and
\vartheta
is carried out simultaneously. Therefore, at time step
i
, we have to solve the linear system

\begin{pmatrix}A&C_{1}&C_{2}&\dots&C_{N_{M}}\\
D_{1}&B_{1}&0&\dots&0\\
D_{2}&0&\ddots&&\vdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\ddots&0\\
D_{N_{M}}&0&\dots&0&B_{N_{M}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{i}\\
\beta_{i,1}\\
\vdots\\
\vdots\\
\beta_{i,N_{M}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}F_{i}\\
f_{i,1}\\
\vdots\\
\vdots\\
f_{i,N_{M}}\end{pmatrix}.

Here, we do not want to go too deep into the implementation details of each matrix block, for this, we point to the available source code. Instead, we only explain the role of each part:

•

The matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{M}\times N_{M}}
encodes the macroscopic bilinear form.

•

The matrices
B_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{m}\times N_{m}}
represent the microscopic bilinear form, for
k=1,\dots N_{M}
.

•

C_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{M}\times N_{m}}
describes the heat flow across the micro boundary
\Gamma_{0}
, and can be created by computing
\kappa(h_{i})\nabla\vartheta_{i}\cdot n
over
\Gamma_{0}
.

•

D_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{m}\times N_{m}}
enforces the coupling that
\Theta=\vartheta
on
\Gamma_{0}
. Therefore this matrix only contains 0 and 1 on the corresponding entries. In particular, the row structure of all
D_{k}
is the same, only one column contains ones and for each
k
this column is shifted to a different position. In the corresponding rows of matrix
B_{k}
only a
-1
is present and on the right-hand side a 0.

•

The right-hand side
F_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{M}}
and
f_{i,k}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{m}}
include the heat sources and information from the previous time step.

The role of
C_{k}
and
D_{k}
is the coupling between macro and micro scale. Note that there is no direct coupling between the different cells and the block containing the
B_{k}
matrices is diagonal. Additionally, all matrices are sparse.
The matrices
A,B_{k},C_{k}
need to be recomputed in each time step, since they depend on the current height
h_{i}
. This is easily parallelizable once
h_{i}
is known.

Finally, we want to briefly mention the computation time. On our setup, an AMD EPYC 7282 16-core CPU, it takes about 10 minutes to solve a cell problem for
H_{\text{cell}}=5\mathrm{e}{-4}
, including the mesh generation. Since the precomputation is easily parallelizable, it took about 5 hours to compute the full dataset of 320 cell problems, as we were able to spread the work over several CPUs. Then, solving the entire problem in parallel on 8 cores, using the discretization mentioned above, takes about 13 seconds. If we had not used the precomputation scheme, the cell problems would need to be solved at each macro DOF and possibly at each time step, this would result in computation times of several days or weeks. This is also the reason why we do not use such a solution as a reference for the convergence study.

6
Concluding remarks

We have suggested, analyzed, and implemented a precomputing strategy for non-linearly coupled two-scale systems with evolving microstructures.
Utilizing the Hanzawa transformation, the existence of local-in-time, weak solutions was established.
Under more restrictive conditions on the data, it is expected that the solutions are unique and strong (see also
[
5
,
22
]
).
We showed that the additional interpolation error introduced via the precomputing can be controlled by the step size and the interpolation order given that the underlying relationship in the material parameters is smooth.
We also introduced a semi-implicit time-stepping method to resolve the nonlinearities inherent to the moving microsctructures and showed linear convergence.
We note that it is possible to improve convergence by using, e.g., a Crank-Nicolson-Galerkin
approximation (
[
14
]
).
The simulation results presented in
Section

5
confirm the analytical convergence.
The implementation was done in FEniCS and our code is freely available online
[
16
]
.

While the model in question in this paper is concerned with phase transitions on the microscale, we want to point out that the same kind of model with very similar analytical and numerical challenges appears in many different applications: precipitation in porous media, swelling of biological tissues, and degradation of concrete to name just a few.
The main advantage of our approach is the fact that a significant part of the computational effort is pushed into an offline phase in a way that is additionally
perfectly parallelizable
.
There are of course potential drawbacks as well: When the underlying functions are discontinuous or extremely volatile, the interpolation error might be difficult to control.
Moreover, in our case we only have a one-dimensional bounded parameter space
h\in[-a^{*},a^{*}]
, which is relatively easy to exhaust.
In cases where shape changes are possible (e.g.,
[
22
,
30
]
) the parameter space becomes much larger.
