Introduction

The problem of determining whether a random graph contains a Hamilton cycle, i.e. a cycle passing through every vertex exactly once, dates back to the inception of the study of random graphs. In 1960, Erdős and Rényi asked whether their eponymous random graph
G_{n,p}
, obtained by including any edge independently with probability
p
, contains a Hamilton path
[
8
]
. The problem was settled by Komlós and Szemerédi
[
15
]
, who showed that

\lim{\mbox{Pr}\left\{G_{n,p}\text{ is Hamiltonian}\right\}}=\lim{\mbox{Pr}\left\{\delta(G_{n,p})\geq 2\right\}},

where
\delta
denotes the minimum degree of a graph. This was strengthened to a hitting time result, independently by Bollobás
[
5
]
and by Ajtai, Komlós and Szemerédi
[
1
]
, stated as follows. Suppose
G_{n,m}
is an increasing sequence of random graphs, where
G_{n,m}
is obtained by adding a uniformly chosen edge to
G_{n,m-1}
. Let
\tau_{2}
be the smallest
m
for which
\delta(G_{n,m})\geq 2
. Then with high probability,
G_{n,\tau_{2}}
is Hamiltonian. For integers
k\geq 1
, let
\mathcal{A}_{k}
denote the graph property of containing
\left\lfloor k/2\right\rfloor
Hamilton cycle, as well as a matching of size
\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor
when
k
is odd. Bollobás and Frieze
[
6
]
strengthened the hitting time result above to showing that
G_{n,\tau_{k}}\in\mathcal{A}_{k}
with high probability. For a more thorough history, see Frieze’s recent survey on Hamilton cycles
[
10
]
.

In recent years, some attention has been turned to random subgraphs of a host graph
\Gamma_{n}
. The random subgraph
\Gamma_{n,p}
is obtained by including any edge of
\Gamma_{n}
independently with probability
p
, and a the random graph process
\Gamma_{n,m}
on
\Gamma_{n}
is obtained by ordering the edges of
\Gamma_{n}
uniformly at random. An early example is the random bipartite graph
G_{n,n,p}
, obtained by letting
\Gamma_{n}=K_{n,n}
. Frieze
[
9
]
determined the threshold in this case. Frieze and Krivelevich
[
13
]
showed that if
\Gamma_{n}
is in a certain class of pseudorandom graphs (specified below) then
\Gamma_{n,\tau_{2}}\in\mathcal{A}_{2}
whp. The author
[
14
]
showed that the same holds when
\delta(\Gamma_{n})\geq(1/2+\varepsilon)n
for some constant
\varepsilon>0
(and that it need not hold when
\delta(\Gamma_{n})=n/2
). Alon and Krivelevich
[
3
]
showed that
\Gamma_{n,\tau_{2k}}\in\mathcal{A}_{2k}
whp for any
k=O(1)
in three dense classes of host graphs, which include some pseudorandom graphs and graphs with
\delta(\Gamma_{n})\geq(1/2+\varepsilon)n
.

Both
[
13
]
and
[
3
]
consider pseudorandom graphs known as
(n,d,\mu)
-graphs. A graph
\Gamma
is an
(n,d,\mu)
-graph if it has
n
vertices, every vertex has degree
d
, and the second largest eigenvalue of its adjacency matrix is at most
\mu
in absolute value. We strengthen both results in the following special case of our main theorem. Let
\tau_{\mathcal{A}_{k}}
be the smallest
m
for which
\Gamma_{n,m}\in\mathcal{A}_{k}
.

Theorem

(Theorem
1.2
, pseudorandom graph case)
.

Let
k=O(1)
. Suppose
\Gamma_{n}
is an
(n,d,\mu)
-graph with
\mu\leq d(d/n)^{\alpha}
for some constant
\alpha>0
. Then the random graph process on
\Gamma_{n}
has
\tau_{\mathcal{A}_{k}}=\tau_{k}
with high probability.

In
[
13
]
it was required that
\mu=o(d^{5/2}/(n\ln n)^{3/2})
, which only holds if
d\gg n^{3/4}(\ln n)^{3}
, while
[
3
]
asked that
\mu=O(d^{2}/n)
and
d=\Omega(n(\ln\ln n)/\ln n)
. This result strengthens both, with an implicit degree bound of
d=n^{\Omega(1)}
owing to the fact that
\mu=\Omega(d^{1/2})
(see e.g.
[
18
]
).

Our full result concerns a more general inhomogeneous random graph. Suppose
P
is a symmetric
n\times n
matrix with entries
P(u,v)\in[0,1]
. We then define a random graph
G_{n,P}
by independently including each edge
\{u,v\}
with probability
P(u,v)
. If
R
is a symmetric
n\times n
matrix with
R(u,v)\geq 0
for all
uv
, we also define a random graph process
G_{n,R}(t)
as follows. Each pair
\{u,v\}
is independently assigned a random value
E(u,v)
, exponentially distributed with rate
R(u,v)
, taken to equal
\infty
if
R(u,v)=0
. We let

G_{n,R}(t)=([n],\{uv:E(u,v)\leq t\}).

Note that
G_{n,R}(t)
equals
G_{n,P}
in distribution when
P(u,v)=1-e^{-R(u,v)t}
. Note that this framework generalizes
\Gamma_{n,p}
and
\Gamma_{n,m}
(now in continuous time), re-obtained by letting
R
be the adjacency matrix of
\Gamma_{n}
. Anastos, Frieze and Gao
[
4
]
considered Hamiltonicity in the stochastic block model, which is
G_{n,P}
with
P
in a specific class of block matrices.

For vertex sets
A,B
we let
R(A,B)=\sum_{u\in A,v\in B}R(u,v)
. Let
d_{R}(u)=R(u,V)
and
d_{R}(A)=R(A,V)
. A key tool in our proof is the random walk induced by
R
, which jumps from
u
to
v
with probability
M(u,v)=R(u,v)/d_{R}(u)
. The transition matrix
M
has real eigenvalues
1=\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{n}\geq-1
(see e.g.
[
16
]
), and we let
\lambda(R)=\max\{|\lambda_{2}|,|\lambda_{n}|\}
. Let
\sigma(u)=d_{R}(u)/d_{R}(V)
be the stationary distribution of the random walk, and define
\|M\|=\max_{u,v}M(u,v)
.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\mathrm{RM}
be the set of rate matrices
R
with transition matrix
M
such that there exist constants
\alpha\in[0,1/2)
,
\gamma>0
,
b>0
such that
\lambda(R)=o(1)
and

\lambda(R)\leq(n\|M\|)^{-\alpha-\gamma},

(1.1)

and
R
satisfies the following:

(a)

there exists a
d=d(n)
such that
d_{R}(u)\geq d
for all
u
, and
d_{R}(V)\leq bdn
.

(b)

for any
A\subseteq V
,

\sigma(A)=\frac{d_{R}(A)}{d_{R}(V)}\leq b\left({\frac{|A|}{n}}\right)^{1-2\alpha},

(1.2)

(c)

\|R\|\leq dn^{-\gamma}
.

We state our main result.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
k=O(1)
. If
R\in\mathrm{RM}
, then whp
G_{n,R}(t)
satisfies

\tau_{k}=\tau_{\mathcal{A}_{k}}.

For any symmetric non-negative matrix
R
on
V
, let

\gamma_{k}(R)=\sum_{u\in V}d_{R}(u)^{k-1}e^{-d_{R}(u)}.

Let
\mathrm{RM}(1)
be the set of
R\in\mathrm{RM}
with
\gamma_{1}(R)=1
. Note that for any
x>0
, the graphs
G_{n,R}(\tau_{k})
and
G_{n,xR}(\tau_{k})
are equal in distribution, so it is enough to prove Theorem
1.2
for
R\in\mathrm{RM}(1)
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
k=O(1)
. Suppose
P\in\mathrm{RM}
has
\gamma_{k}(P)\to\gamma_{k}\in[0,\infty]
. Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mbox{Pr}\left\{G_{n,P}\in\mathcal{A}_{k}\right\}}=e^{-\gamma_{k}}.

Note that if
P
has constant row sums
d_{P}(u)=d=\ln n+(k-1)\ln\ln n+c_{n,k}
, then
\gamma_{k}=\exp\{-\lim_{n}c_{n,k}\}
.

2
Proof outline

Let us first discuss the overarching proof idea. Traditionally, many proofs of Hamiltonicity in random graphs rely on finding so-called
booster edges
for a graph
G
, which are edge
e\notin G
such that
G\cup\{e\}
is closer to being Hamiltonian, typically meaning it contains a longer path than
G
does. If random edges are added to
G
, one argues that some booster is likely to be added.

Montgomery
[
17
]
more generally defined boosters as sets
T
of edges whose addition gets
G
closer to Hamiltonicity, and used sets
|T|\leq 2
. A similar idea was used earlier in
[
11
]
. Booster pairs were also used by Alon and Krivelevich in their recent paper
[
3
]
. In this paper we move to general boosters, i.e. edge sets
T
of any (constant) size whose addition improves
G
. These are found using random alternating walks.

2.1
Random subgraphs

For proving Hamiltonicity, the most important property of
G_{n,R}(\tau_{k})
is expansion (see Lemma
2.1
below for the definition). A major drawback of
G_{n,R}(\tau_{k})
for our purposes is its high average degree, with most vertices having degree
\Omega(\ln n)
. We therefore define a random subgraph
H\subseteq G_{n,R}(\tau_{k})
, which retains expansion and connectivity with high probability while containing only
O(n)
edges. We do not show that
H
itself is Hamiltonian, but the graph will be important to our proof.

Recall the construction of
G_{n,R}(t)
, in which each edge
\{u,v\}
is included at a random time
E(u,v)
. Let
D\geq k
be a constant integer and let

T_{D}(u)=\inf\{t>0:d_{t}(u)\geq D\}

be the random time at which
u
attains degree
D
. Define
H(t)\subseteq G_{n,R}(t)
by including any edge
\{u,v\}
with
E(u,v)\leq t
and
E(u,v)\leq\max\{T_{D}(u),T_{D}(v)\}
. Let
H=H(\tau_{k})
. In other words, an edge is included in
H
if it is among the first
D
edges attached to one of its endpoints in
G_{n,R}(\tau_{k})
.

For a graph
G=(V,E)
and
A\subseteq V
we let
N(A)=\{u\notin A:\{u,v\}\in E,\text{ some $v$}\}
and
\widehat{N}(A)=A\cup N(A)

8
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose
R\in\mathrm{RM}
, and let
k=O(1)
. For a large enough constant
D
, the following holds.

(i)

(Light tail) Let
\theta
tend to infinity with
n
and let
S_{\theta}
be the set of
u
with
d_{H}(u)\geq\theta
or
d_{R}(u)\geq\theta d
. Then with high probability,
|\widehat{N}_{H}(S_{\theta})|=o(n)
.

(ii)

(Expansion) There exists a constant
\beta=\beta(R,k)>0
such that with high probability, every vertex set
A
with
|A|<\beta n
satisfies
|N_{H}(A)|\geq k|A|
.

Let
\mathrm{SE}_{k}(R)
(“sparse expanders”) be the set of graphs satisfying (i) – (ii). Note that if
G\in\mathrm{SE}_{k}
and
\Delta(F)\leq\ell<k
then
G\setminus F\in\mathrm{SE}_{k-\ell}
and
G\cup F\in\mathrm{SE}_{k}
. Note also that
\mathrm{SE}_{k}\subseteq\mathrm{SE}_{\ell}
.

For
t>0
let

\mathbb{S}(t)=\{u:T_{D}(u)>t\}=\{u:d_{t}(u)<D\},

and
\mathbb{L}(t)=V\setminus\mathbb{S}(t)
. For

define a random graph

and

. Then
G_{n,R}(t_{1})\subseteq G_{n,R}^{*}(t_{0},t_{1})\subseteq G_{n,R}(\tau_{k})
whenever

, and

for any

. Suppose

and define

For

Suppose

Proof.

∎

We state some preliminary, for the most part standard, results, and leave the proofs for Section

3.1
Degrees

Recall that
d_{R}(u)=\sum_{v}R(u,v)
. We will often assume that
\gamma_{1}(R)=1
, and note that this implies that
d=\Theta(\ln n)
where
d=\min d_{R}(u)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
R\in\mathrm{RM}(1)
.

(i)

For any integer
D\geq 1
,
u\in V
and
S\subseteq V
with
|V\setminus S|=O(1)
, and
t=\Omega(1)
,

{\mbox{Pr}\left\{e_{t}(u,S)<D\right\}}=\exp\{-td_{R}(u)+O(\ln d_{R}(u))\}.

(ii)

Let
\mathbb{S}(t)
denote the set of vertices in
G_{n,R}(t)
with degree less than
D
. If
t=\Omega(1)
then
|\mathbb{S}(t)|=o(n)
whp.

Recall the definition
\gamma_{k}(P)=\sum_{u}e^{-d_{P}(u)}d_{P}(u)^{k-1}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
k\geq 1
. Suppose
P\in\mathrm{RM}
has
\gamma_{k}(P)\to\gamma_{k}\in[0,\infty]
. Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mbox{Pr}\left\{\delta(G_{n,P})\geq k\right\}}=e^{-\gamma_{k}}.

If
R\in\mathrm{RM}(1)
and

p[\mathcal{Q}(x)]\leq\Delta^{r-1}
, so

\displaystyle p[\mathcal{M}(S)]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x\notin S}p(x)p[\mathcal{Q}(x)\setminus\mathcal{Q}(S)]

(5.4)

\displaystyle\leq p(U)\Delta^{r-1}+p(X\setminus S)\frac{1}{3}\frac{p[\mathcal{T}]}{p(X)}\leq p(U)\Delta^{r-1}+\frac{1}{3}p[\mathcal{T}].

(5.5)

Combining (
5.3
) and (
5.5
) gives
p(U)\geq p[\mathcal{T}]/3\Delta^{r-1}
.
∎

Consider sampling
S\subseteq X_{p}
by the following procedure.

1.

Initially let
S_{0}=\emptyset
and
Z_{0}=\emptyset
. Set
i=1
.

2.

Let
x_{i}\notin Z_{i-1}
be an
S_{i-1}
-useful element. Let
Z_{i}=Z_{i-1}\cup\{x_{i}\}
, and with probability
p(x_{i})
let
S_{i}=S_{i-1}\cup\{x_{i}\}
, otherwise let
S_{i}=S_{i-1}
.

3.

If
p[\mathcal{Q}(S_{i})]\geq p[\mathcal{T}]/3\Delta
, declare
success
and end the procedure. If
p(Z_{i})\geq p[\mathcal{T}]/3\Delta^{r-1}
, declare
failure
and end the procedure. Otherwise, increase
i
by 1 and go to step 2.

By Claim
5.1
, Step 2 can be carried out as long as neither
success
nor
failure
has been declared, since then
p(U\setminus Z_{i-1})>0
where
U
is the set of
S_{i-1}
-useful elements.

Since each
x_{i}
is
S_{i-1}
-useful at time of sampling, we have

p[\mathcal{Q}(S_{\ell})]\geq\frac{p[\mathcal{T}]}{3p(X)}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\xi_{i},

where the
\xi_{i}
are independent indicator random variables. Letting
\xi(\ell)=\sum_{i\leq\ell}\xi_{i}
, we have
\mathbb{E}\left[\xi(\ell)\right]=p(Z_{\ell})
. If
failure
is declared, there exists some
\ell
for which
p(Z_{\ell})\geq p[\mathcal{T}]/3\Delta^{r-1}
while
\xi(\ell)<p(X)/\Delta=o(p(Z_{\ell}))
. By the Chernoff bound (
3.2
) we have

\displaystyle{\mbox{Pr}\left\{\xi(\ell)<\frac{p(X)}{\Delta}\text{ and }p(Z_{\ell})\geq\frac{p[\mathcal{T}]}{3\Delta^{r-1}}\right\}}\leq\exp\left\{-\frac{p[\mathcal{T}]}{24\Delta^{r-1}}\right\}.

(5.6)

Since
p[\mathcal{T}_{1}]\geq p[\mathcal{Q}(S_{\ell})]
, the lemma follows.
∎

We can now prove Lemma
5.1
.

Proof of Lemma
5.1
.

If
r=1
then
\mathcal{T}
is a collection of elements of
X
, and

{\mbox{Pr}\left\{\mathcal{T}\cap X_{p}=\emptyset\right\}}=\prod_{x\in\mathcal{T}}(1-p(x))\leq e^{-p[\mathcal{T}]}.

(5.7)

Suppose
r>1
. Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{r}
be independent random subsets of
X
, each sampling any
x\in X
with probability
p^{\prime}(x)=p(x)/r
. Then any
x
is independently in
X_{1}\cup\dots\cup X_{r}
with probability
1-(1-p(x)/r)^{r}\leq p(x)
.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{0}=\mathcal{T}
, and for
0<i<r
let
\mathcal{T}_{i}
be the random set of
(x_{i+1},\dots,x_{r})
such that
(x_{1},\dots,x_{i},x_{i+1},\dots,x_{r})\in\mathcal{T}
for some
x_{j}\in X_{j}
,

.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{i}
denote the event that
p^{\prime}[\mathcal{T}_{i}]\geq p^{\prime}[\mathcal{T}]/(3\Delta)^{i}
. Lemma
5.2
shows that for

,

\displaystyle{\mbox{Pr}\left\{\overline{\mathcal{E}_{i}}\mid\mathcal{E}_{i-1}\right\}}\leq\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{3^{i}\Delta^{r-1}}p^{\prime}[\mathcal{T}]\right\}.

(5.8)

We then have

{\mbox{Pr}\left\{\overline{\mathcal{E}_{r-1}}\right\}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}{\mbox{Pr}\left\{\overline{\mathcal{E}_{i}}\mid\mathcal{E}_{i-1}\right\}}\leq(r-1)\exp\left\{-\left({\frac{1}{3\Delta}}\right)^{r-1}p[\mathcal{T}]\right\}.

Finally, note that
\mathcal{T}_{r-1}
is a set of elements in
X
. Repeating the argument behind (
5.7
) gives

{\mbox{Pr}\left\{T\nsubseteq X_{p},\text{ all $T\in\mathcal{T}$}\mid\mathcal{E}_{r-1}\right\}}\leq\exp\left\{-\left({\frac{1}{3\Delta}}\right)^{r-1}p^{\prime}[\mathcal{T}]\right\}.

With

, this finishes the proof.
∎

Finishing the high-level proof

We can now prove Lemma
2.3
. Suppose
R\in\mathrm{RM}
and
G\in\mathrm{SE}_{i}
with

for some

. Note that

since
\gamma_{1}(R)=1
. Let
\theta
tend to infinity arbitrarily slowly, and let

be the set of edges incident to

. Propositions

and
4.9
show, for

Suppose

is an edge set with

, and that

satisfies

for all

. We then have

\displaystyle{\mbox{Pr}\left\{s_{i}(G^{p})=s_{i}(G)\right\}}={\mbox{Pr}\left\{T\nsubseteq X_{p},\text{ all $T\in\mathcal{T}_{r}$}\right\}}.

(6.1)

Let

7
Proofs for Section
4.1
: alternating walks mix

Suppose
G
is a graph and
R
a rate matrix on
V
, with associated transition matrix
M
. In Section
4.1
we defined the simple, lazy
(R,G)
-alternating random walk, which is a special case of the following definition. Recall that
\widehat{N}(A)=A\cup N(A)
.

Definition 7.1
.

Given a graph
G
, a random walk
\pi
on
V
is a random
(R,G)
-alternating walk if the following hold for all
j\geq 0
:

\displaystyle\pi(w_{2j+1}\mid w_{0},\dots,w_{2j})

\displaystyle=M(w_{2j},w_{2j+1}),

(7.1)

\displaystyle\pi(w_{2j+2}\mid w_{0},\dots,w_{2j+1})

\displaystyle=0,\quad w_{2j+2}\notin\widehat{N}_{G}(w_{2j+1}).

(7.2)

In short, a random
(R,G)
-alternating walk alternates between memoryless transitions weighted by
M
, and (lazy) steps restricted to the edges of
G
. We use
\pi_{j}
to denote the measure induced by the
j
–th vertex
w_{j}
, and note that the initial distribution
\pi_{0}
may be any distribution on
V
.

Before going into mixing of the random
(R,G)
-alternating walk, we restate and prove Lemma
4.3
. Define
S_{\theta}
as the set of vertices
u
with
d_{G}(u)\geq\theta
or
d_{R}(u)\geq\theta d
, and say that a walk avoids
S_{\theta}
if it contains no vertex of
S_{\theta}
.

Lemma 7.2
.

Suppose
R\in\mathrm{RM}
and that
G
is light-tailed, and let
j=O(1)
. Let
\mathcal{C}_{j}^{\theta}
be the set of
G
-alternating walks of length
j
which avoid
S_{\theta}
and either (a) repeat some vertex, or (b) are not strictly
G
-alternating. If
\theta^{j+1}\|M\|=o(1)
then
R_{\mathrm{alt}}[\mathcal{C}_{j}^{\theta}]=o(nd^{\left\lceil j/2\right\rceil})
.

Proof.

If a
G
-alternating walk
W=(w_{0},\dots,w_{j})
repeats a vertex or has
\{w_{2i},w_{2i+1}\}\in G
for some
i
, there must exist some
i
such that
w_{2i+1}\in\widehat{N}_{G}(w_{0},\dots,w_{2i})
. If
d_{G}(w_{i})<\theta
for all
i
, then

M(w_{2i},\widehat{N}_{G}(w_{0},\dots,w_{2i}))\leq\|M\|(2i+1)\theta,

so
\pi(\mathcal{C}_{j}^{\theta})=O(\theta\|M\|)
for any random
(R,G)
-alternating walk
\pi
. Note that for any walk
W=(w_{0},\dots,w_{j})
which avoids
S_{\theta}
,

R_{\mathrm{alt}}[W]\leq\prod_{i=0}^{\left\lceil j/2\right\rceil-1}\frac{\theta d}{d_{R}(w_{2i})}R(w_{2i},w_{2i+1})\prod_{i=0}^{\left\lfloor j/2\right\rfloor-1}\frac{\theta}{d_{G}(w_{2i-1})+1}\\
\leq\theta^{j}d^{\left\lceil j/2\right\rceil}\times n\pi_{G}(W),

(7.3)

where
\pi_{G}
is the simple, lazy
(R,G)
-alternating walk initiated at a vertex chosen uniformly at random. Since
\theta^{j+1}\|M\|=o(1)
, it follows that
R_{\mathrm{alt}}[\mathcal{C}_{j}^{\theta}]=o(nd^{\left\lceil j/2\right\rceil})
.

∎

7.1
Mixing for the random alternating walk

The
R
-steps of the
(R,G)
-alternating walk gets
\pi_{j}
closer to stationarity by Lemma
3.8
, while the
G
-steps may pull it back. The following lemma bounds the harm done.

Lemma 7.3
.

Suppose
R\in\mathrm{RM}
with
\lambda(R)=\lambda
and stationary distribution
\sigma
. Suppose
\pi
is a random
(R,G)
-alternating walk for some graph
G
with maximum degree
\theta-1
and
d_{G}(u)=0
whenever
d_{R}(u)\geq\theta d
. Then for
i\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mu_{\sigma}(\pi_{2i+1})

\displaystyle\leq\lambda\mu_{\sigma}(\pi_{2i}),

(7.4)

\displaystyle\mu_{\sigma}(\pi_{2i+2})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\theta^{4}\left(\mu_{\sigma}(\pi_{2i+1})^{2}+1\right).

(7.5)

In particular, if
\lambda\theta^{2}=o(1)
then for all
i\geq 0
,

\mu_{\sigma}(\pi_{2i+1})^{2}\leq\lambda^{2i}\theta^{4i}\mu_{\sigma}(\pi_{1})^{2}+O(\lambda^{2}\theta^{4}).

(7.6)

Proof.

Lemma
3.8
immediately gives (
7.4
), and we prove (
7.5
). Note that for any
i\geq 1
and
u\in V
,

\displaystyle\pi_{2i}(u)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in\widehat{N}(u)}\pi_{2i-1}(v)\pi(w_{2i}=u\mid w_{2i-1}=v)\leq\theta\max_{v\in\widehat{N}(u)}\pi_{2i-1}(v).

(7.7)

Suppose
v\in\widehat{N}(u)
. Then
u=v
or
d_{R}(u)\leq\theta d
, since
d_{G}(u)=0
whenever
d_{R}(u)\geq\theta d
. Since
d_{R}(v)\geq d
, in either case we conclude that
\sigma(u)/\sigma(v)=d_{R}(u)/d_{R}(v)\leq\theta
, and

\displaystyle\mu_{\sigma}(\pi_{2i})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v}\frac{\pi_{2i}(v)^{2}}{\sigma(v)}\leq\sum_{v}\frac{\theta^{2}}{\sigma(v)}\max_{u\in\widehat{N}(v)}\pi_{2i-1}(u)^{2}.

(7.8)

Any vertex
u
is counted at most
\theta
times in this sum, so

\displaystyle\mu_{\sigma}(\pi_{2i})^{2}\leq\theta^{3}\sum_{u}\frac{\pi_{2i-1}(u)^{2}}{\sigma(u)}\max_{v\in\widehat{N}(u)}\frac{\sigma(u)}{\sigma(v)}\leq\theta^{4}\left(\mu_{\sigma}(\pi_{2i-1})^{2}+1\right).

(7.9)

This shows (
7.5
). Repeatedly applying (
7.4
) and (
7.5
) with
\lambda\theta^{2}=o(1)
gives (
7.6
).
∎

Recall that for a family
\mathcal{W}
of walks and a vertex
v
,
\mathcal{W}^{\to v}
is the set of walks in
\mathcal{W}
ending at
v
. Say that a walk
W=(w_{0},\dots,w_{j})
is
non-lazy
if
w_{i}\neq w_{i+1}
for all
0\leq i<j
.

For any random
(R,G)
-alternating walk
\pi
we define a variant
\pi^{\theta}
by the following holding for any
W=(w_{0},\dots,w_{2j-1})
: if
w_{2j-1}\in S_{\theta}
then
w_{2j}=w_{2j-1}
, and if
w_{2j-1}\notin S_{\theta}
then

\pi^{\theta}(w_{2j}\mid W)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&w_{2j}\in S_{\theta},\\
\pi(w_{2j}\mid W)+\sum_{v\in S_{\theta}}\pi(v\mid W),&w_{2j}=w_{2j-1},\\
\pi(w_{2j}\mid W),&w_{2j}\notin\{w_{2j-1}\}\cup S_{\theta}.\end{array}\right.

(7.10)

Then
\pi^{\theta}
is a random
(R,G^{\theta})
-alternating walk, where
G^{\theta}\subseteq G
is obtained from
G
by removing any edge incident to
S_{\theta}
. The walk
\pi^{\theta}
is designed to satisfy the conditions of Lemma
7.3
as well as satisfying
\pi^{\theta}(\mathcal{W})=\pi(\mathcal{W})
for any family
\mathcal{W}
of walks which either (a) are non-lazy and avoid
S_{\theta}
, or (b) have
w_{i}\notin\widehat{N}(S_{\theta})
for odd
i
.

Lemma 7.4
.

Suppose
R\in\mathrm{RM}
with
\lambda(R)=\lambda
, suppose
F
is a graph with maximum degree
\Delta(F)\leq 2
, and suppose
\pi
is a random
(R,F)
-alternating walk. Suppose
\theta\leq\lambda^{-1/4}
tends to infinity with
n
, and let
j\geq 2-2\frac{\ln(n\|M\|)}{\ln\lambda}
be an integer. Let
c>0
be constant. Suppose
\mathcal{W}
is a set of non-lazy
S_{\theta}(R,F)
-avoiding walks of length
2j
, such that
\pi(\mathcal{W})\geq c
. Then there exists a constant
\rho>0
such that there exists a set
|U_{2j}|\geq\rho n
, such that any
u\in U_{2j}
has
\pi(\mathcal{W}^{\to u})\geq\rho/n
.

Proof.

Let
S_{\theta}=S_{\theta}(R,F)
and consider the walk
\pi^{\theta}
defined above. Since
\theta\leq\lambda^{-1/4}
and
\lambda=o(1)
, Lemma
7.3
implies that

\mu_{\sigma}(\pi_{2j-1}^{\theta})^{2}\leq\lambda^{j-1}\mu_{\sigma}(\pi_{1}^{\theta})^{2}+O(\lambda).

(7.11)

We have
\pi_{1}^{\theta}(u)=\sum_{v}\pi_{0}^{\theta}(v)M(v,u)\leq\|M\|
for all
u
, and since
\sigma(u)\geq 1/bn
for all
u
,

\mu_{\sigma}(\pi_{1})^{2}\leq\sum_{u}\frac{\pi_{1}(u)^{2}}{\sigma(u)}\leq bn^{2}\|M\|^{2}.

So for
j\geq 2-2\ln(n\|M\|)/\ln(\lambda)
, (
7.11
) becomes
\mu_{\sigma}(\pi_{2j-1}^{\theta})^{2}=O(\lambda)
. Define
T
as the set of vertices
u
with
|\pi_{2j-1}^{\theta}(u)-\sigma(u)|<\lambda^{1/3}\sigma(u)
. Then by definition of
\mu_{\sigma}
,

\displaystyle O(\lambda)=\mu_{\sigma}(\pi_{2j-1}^{\theta})^{2}\geq\sum_{v\notin T}\left(\frac{\pi_{2j-1}^{\theta}(v)}{\sigma(v)}-1\right)^{2}\sigma(v)\geq\lambda^{2/3}\sigma(\overline{T}).

(7.12)

Then
\sigma(T)=1-O(\lambda^{1/3})
. By definition of
T
,

\displaystyle\pi_{2j-1}^{\theta}(T)

\displaystyle\geq(1-\lambda^{1/3})\sigma(T)=1-O(\lambda^{1/3}),

(7.13)

and for any vertex set
A
,

\pi_{2j-1}^{\theta}(A)\leq(1+\lambda^{1/3})\sigma(A\cap T)+\pi_{2j-1}^{\theta}(\overline{T})\leq\sigma(A)+O(\lambda^{1/3}).

(7.14)

Let
\mathcal{W}_{2j-1}\subseteq\mathcal{W}
be the set of walks obtained by removing the final vertex from walks in
\mathcal{W}
. Note that
c\leq\pi(\mathcal{W}_{2j-1})=\pi^{\theta}(\mathcal{W}_{2j-1})
. For any
v\in V
let
\mathcal{W}_{2j-1}^{\to v}
be the set of walks in
\mathcal{W}_{2j-1}
which end at
v
, and let

be the set of

. Then

c\leq\pi^{\theta}(\mathcal{W}_{2j-1})=\sum_{v}\pi^{\theta}(\mathcal{W}_{2j-1}^{\to v})\\
\leq\frac{c}{2n}|\overline{U_{2j-1}}|+\pi_{2j-1}^{\theta}(U_{2j-1})\leq\frac{c}{2}+\sigma(U_{2j-1})+O(\lambda^{1/3}).

(7.15)

Since
\lambda=o(1)
, we conclude that
\sigma(U_{2j-1})\geq c/3
. By Lemma
3.3
we then have
|U_{2j-1}|\geq c^{\prime}n
for some constant

. Let

U_{2j}=\left\{v:\pi^{\theta}(\mathcal{W}^{\to v})\geq\frac{1}{3}\frac{c}{2n}\right\}.

Since
\Delta_{F}\leq 2
, every

has at least one neighbour in

, counting self-loops, and

. Since

∎

7.2
Hitting times for sets

For a random walk
(w_{0},w_{1},\dots)
and
S\subseteq V
, recall that
\tau(S)
is the smallest
i
for which
w_{i}\in S
.

Lemma 7.5
.

Suppose
R\in\mathrm{RM}
and that
G
is light-tailed. Suppose
\pi
is a random
(R,G)
-alternating walk with
\pi_{0}(u)=1/|A|
for
u\in A
, where
A\subseteq V
has size
|A|=\Omega(n)
.

(i)

If
j\geq 0
is constant and
|S|=o(n)
, then

\pi(\tau(S)\leq 2j)=o(1).

(ii)

If
|S|=\Omega(n)
then

\pi(\tau(S)\leq 1)=\Omega(1).

Proof.

We prove (i). Let
\theta=o(n/|S|)
tend to infinity with
n
. We may assume that
S_{\theta}\subseteq S
, since replacing
S
by
S\cup S_{\theta}
only increases the probability in question. Note that since
G
is light-tailed,

|\widehat{N}(S)|\leq|\widehat{N}(S\setminus S_{\theta})|+|\widehat{N}(S_{\theta})|\leq\theta|S|+o(n)=o(n).

Note that either
\tau(S)=0
or
\tau(S)\geq\tau_{\mathrm{odd}}(\widehat{N}(S))
. We then have

\pi(\tau(S)\leq 2j)\leq\pi_{0}(S)+\pi(\tau_{\mathrm{odd}}(\widehat{N}(S))<2j\mid\overline{S}).

(7.16)

The first term equals
|S|/|A|=o(1)
.

Let
\pi^{\theta}
be the modification of
\pi
defined in (
7.10
). Note that since
S_{\theta}\subseteq S
, any walk
W\in\{\tau_{\mathrm{odd}}(\widehat{N}(S))<2j\}
has
\pi^{\theta}(W)=\pi(W)
. Since
\sigma(u)\geq 1/bn
for all
u
for some constant
b\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\mu_{\sigma}(\pi_{0}^{\theta})^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{u\in Z}\frac{(1/|Z|)^{2}}{\sigma(u)}\right)-1\leq\frac{bn}{|Z|}-1=O(1).

(7.17)

By Lemma
7.3
and since
\theta\leq\lambda^{-1/4}
, for any odd

we have
\mu_{\sigma}(\pi_{i}^{\theta})^{2}\leq\mu_{\sigma}(\pi_{1}^{\theta})^{2}+O(\lambda)=O(\lambda)
.
As in (
7.14
), we have
\pi_{i}^{\theta}(\widehat{N}(S))\leq\sigma(\widehat{N}(S))+o(1)
. Then

\pi(\tau(S)\leq 2j)\leq o(1)+\pi^{\theta}(\tau_{\mathrm{odd}}(\widehat{N}(S))<2j)\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i<2j\\
i\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}\pi_{i}^{\theta}(\widehat{N}(S))=o(1).

Part (ii) follows from
\mu_{\sigma}(\pi_{1})^{2}=O(\lambda)
. Applying (
7.14
) to the complement of

, we have

\pi(\tau(S)\leq 1)\geq\pi_{1}(S)\geq\sigma(S)-o(1).

∎

Recall that

Lemma 8.1
.

\theta

\sigma(u)M(u,v)=\frac{d_{R}(u)}{d_{R}(V)}\frac{R(u,v)}{d_{R}(u)}=\frac{d_{R}(v)}{d_{R}(V)}\frac{R(v,u)}{d_{R}(v)}=\sigma(v)M(v,u).

For vectors
f,g:V\to\mathbb{R}
we define an inner product

\langle f,g\rangle_{\sigma}=\sum_{v}f(v)g(v)\sigma(v),

(9.15)

and the associated norm
\|f\|_{\sigma}=\langle f,f\rangle_{\sigma}^{1/2}
. Then for probability measures
\pi
,

\left\|\frac{\pi(\cdot)}{\sigma(\cdot)}-\mathbf{1}\right\|_{\sigma}=\sqrt{\left(\sum_{v}\frac{\pi(v)^{2}}{\sigma(v)}\right)-1}=\mu_{\sigma}(\pi),

(9.16)

where
\mathbf{1}=(1,1,\dots,1)
.

Let
1=\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{n}\geq-1
be the eigenvalues of
M
, with a corresponding eigenbasis
\mathbf{1}=f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
, orthonormal with respect to the
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\sigma}
inner product. Then (see e.g.
[
16
]
)

\displaystyle\frac{\pi M(v)}{\sigma(v)}-1

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=2}^{n}\sum_{u}\pi(u)f_{j}(u)f_{j}(v)\lambda_{j}

(9.17)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=2}^{n}\lambda_{j}f_{j}(v)\sum_{u}\left[\left(\frac{\pi(u)}{\sigma(u)}-1\right)f_{j}(u)\sigma(u)+f_{j}(u)\sigma(u)\right]

(9.18)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=2}^{n}\lambda_{j}f_{j}(v)\left[\left\langle\frac{\pi}{\sigma}-\mathbf{1},f_{j}\right\rangle_{\sigma}+\langle f_{j},\mathbf{1}\rangle_{\sigma}\right].

(9.19)

For any
j>1
, orthonormality implies
\langle f_{j},\mathbf{1}\rangle_{\sigma}=0
. Let
F(j)=\langle\pi/\sigma-\mathbf{1},f_{j}\rangle_{\sigma}
, and note that
\mu_{\sigma}(\pi)^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}F(j)^{2}
. Then

\displaystyle\mu_{\sigma}(\pi M)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{v}\sigma(v)\left(\sum_{j=2}^{n}\lambda_{j}f_{j}(v)F(j)\right)^{2}

(9.20)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\geq 2}\lambda_{j}^{2}F(j)^{2}\|f_{j}\|_{\sigma}^{2}+2\sum_{k>j\geq 2}\lambda_{j}\lambda_{k}F(j)F(k)\langle f_{j},f_{k}\rangle_{\sigma}

(9.21)

Since the
f_{j}
are orthonormal in the
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\sigma}
inner product, we are left with

\displaystyle\mu_{\sigma}(\pi M)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\geq 2}\lambda_{j}^{2}F(j)^{2}\leq\lambda^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}F(j)^{2}=\lambda^{2}\mu_{\sigma}(\pi)^{2}.

(9.22)

∎

9.4
The expander mixing lemma

Proof of Lemma
3.4
.

For
A\subseteq V
let
\mathbf{1}_{A}:V\to\{0,1\}
be the indicator for
A
. Let
\pi(u)=\mathbf{1}_{A}/|A|
. One easily checks that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|A|}M(A,B)-\sigma(B)=\left\langle\frac{\pi M(\cdot)}{\sigma(\cdot)}-\mathbf{1}_{V},\mathbf{1}_{B}\right\rangle_{\sigma}.

(9.23)

Cauchy-Schwarz’ inequality and Lemma
3.8
then give

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{|A|}M(A,B)-\sigma(B)\right|^{2}\leq\left\|\frac{\pi M}{\sigma}-\mathbf{1}_{V}\right\|_{\sigma}^{2}\|\mathbf{1}_{B}\|_{\sigma}^{2}\leq\lambda\mu_{\sigma}(\pi)^{2}\sigma(B).

(9.24)

Since
\mu_{\sigma}(\pi)^{2}\leq\sum_{u\in A}\pi(u)^{2}/\sigma(u)\leq bn/|A|
, (
3.1
) follows.

To see how (i) follows, note that
R(u,v)\geq dM(u,v)
for all
u,v
. Lemma
3.3
gives
\sigma(B)=\Omega(1)
whenever
|B|=\Omega(n)
. Since
\lambda(R)=o(1)
, for
|A|,|B|=\Omega(n)
we then have

R(A,B)\geq dM(A,B)\geq d(|A|\sigma(B)-O(\lambda\sqrt{n|A|\sigma(B)})=\Omega(dn).

For (ii), let
c>0
be some constant to be chosen, and for each
|A|\leq n/2
define
A^{\prime}
as the set of
u\in A
with
M(u,A)\geq(|A|/n)^{c}
. If
|A|<(n^{c}\|M\|)^{-1/(1-c)}
then any
u\in A
has

M(u,A)\leq\|M\||A|<\left({\frac{|A|}{n}}\right)^{c},

so
A^{\prime}=\emptyset
. Suppose
(n^{c}\|M\|)^{-1/(1-c)}\leq|A|\leq n/2
. Then (
3.1
) and the power law condition (
1.2
) give, for some constant
0\leq\alpha<1/2
,

\left({\frac{|A|}{n}}\right)^{c}\leq\frac{1}{|A^{\prime}|}M(A^{\prime},A)\leq\sigma(A)+\lambda\sqrt{\frac{bn\sigma(A)}{|A^{\prime}|}}\\
\leq\left[\left({\frac{|A|}{n}}\right)^{1-2\alpha-2c}+\lambda\sqrt{\frac{b|A|}{|A^{\prime}|}}\left({\frac{n}{|A|}}\right)^{\alpha+2c}\right]\left({\frac{|A|}{n}}\right)^{2c}.

(9.25)

For
2c<1-2\alpha
, the first term in square brackets is at most 1. Since
\lambda\leq(n\|M\|)^{-\alpha-\gamma}
for some constant
\gamma>0
, we have for
|A|\geq(n^{c}\|M\|)^{-1/(1-c)}
that

\displaystyle\left({\frac{n}{|A|}}\right)^{\alpha+2c}

\displaystyle\leq\left({\frac{n}{n^{-\frac{c}{1-c}}\|M\|^{-\frac{1}{1-c}}}}\right)^{\alpha+2c}=(n\|M\|)^{\frac{\alpha+2c}{1-c}}.

(9.26)

We have
n\|M\|\geq 1
since
M
is a transition matrix. Since
\lambda=o(1)
and
\lambda\leq(n\|M\|)^{-\alpha-\gamma}
for some constant
\gamma>0
, we conclude that for
c>0
small enough,
\lambda(n/|A|)^{\alpha+2c}=o(1)
. From (
9.25
) we then have, for
|A|\leq n/2
,

\displaystyle 1<2^{c}\leq\left({\frac{n}{|A|}}\right)^{c}\leq 1+o\left(\sqrt{\frac{b|A|}{|A^{\prime}|}}\right).

(9.27)

We conclude that
|A^{\prime}|=o(|A|)
.
∎
