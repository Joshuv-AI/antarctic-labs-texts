Communication Complexity of Graph Isomorphism, Coloring, and Distance Games
Pierre Botteron ID, Moritz Weber

Abstract
In quantum information, nonlocal games are particularly useful for differentiating classical, quantum, and non-signalling correlations. An example of differentiation is given by the principle of no-collapse of communication complexity, which is often interpreted as necessary for a feasible physical theory. It is satisfied by quantum correlations but violated by some non-signalling ones. In this work, we investigate this principle in the context of three nonlocal games related to graph theory, starting from the well-known graph isomorphism and graph coloring games, and introducing a new game, the vertex distance game, with a parameter D ∈ ℕ D\in\mathbb{N} , that generalizes the former two to some extent. For these three games, we prove that perfect non-signalling strategies collapse communication complexity under favorable conditions. We also define a refinement of fractional isomorphism of graphs, namely D D -fractional isomorphisms, and we show that this characterizes perfect non-signalling strategies for the vertex distance game. Surprisingly, we observe that non-signalling strategies provide a finer distinction for the new game compared to classical and quantum strategies since the parameter D D is visible only in the non-signalling setting.



Introduction

Motivation

The non-signalling theory (
\mathcal{N\!S}
) is a generalization of the quantum theory (
\mathcal{Q}
), which allows any correlations satisfying the no-faster-than-light communication principle.
Although many physical experiments proved the relevance of the quantum theory
[
21
,
1
,
26
]
, the non-signalling theory might be too general and include unrealistic states.

This is where the principle of communication complexity (CC) comes into play: a collapse of CC is strongly believed to be unachievable in nature
[
17
,
9
,
11
,
4
]
, so one may view this no-collapse principle as an axiom for a feasible physical theory.
In contrast with the quantum theory which does satisfy the no-collapse principle, some of the correlations predicted by the non-signalling theory violate it and thus seem unlikely to exist in nature. In this sense, showing that non-signalling correlations collapse CC contributes to showing that the quantum theory is already “the best theory” to some extent, see also the discussion in the
Conclusion
.

For most of the non-signalling correlations, we do not know whether they collapse CC, the question is open.
In this manuscript, we present new ways to determine collapsing non-signalling correlations, with techniques based on nonlocal games and graphs.

Preliminary Definitions

Our results connect three notions that we recall below: nonlocal games, nonlocal boxes and communication complexity.

Nonlocal Games.

A two-player nonlocal game is a cooperative game played by two characters, commonly named Alice and Bob, who agree on a common strategy
\mathcal{S}
beforehand, but who are space-like separated during the game, meaning that communication is forbidden. Each of the players is provided with a “question”
h_{A}
(resp.
h_{B}
) by a referee, and the players prepare their “answer”
g_{A}
(resp.
g_{B}
) based on the chosen strategy
\mathcal{S}
, possibly using “shared resources” (like shared randomness or a pair of entangled particles).
Finally, the referee verifies whether the players won the game: he computes a deterministic Boolean function depending on the questions and answers, called the “rule” of the game.
The goal of Alice and Bob is to maximize the winning probability. If they win almost surely,
i.e.
with probability
1
, then we say that their strategy
\mathcal{S}
is a
perfect strategy
.

Example
.

The best-known example of a nonlocal game is the
\mathtt{CHSH}
game, named after Clauser, Horne, Shimony, and Holt
[
15
]
. In this game, the questions
h_{A},h_{B}
and the answers

are classical bits in
\{0,1\}
, and Alice and Bob win if and only if
g_{A}\oplus g_{B}=h_{A}\cdot h_{B}
, where “
\oplus
” is the sum modulo
2
and “
\cdot
” is the product.
We will comment on the perfect strategies for this game below.

Communication Complexity.

Communication complexity
(CC) is a notion that was introduced by Yao in
[
47
]

and later reviewed in
[
28
,
41
]
.
It quantifies the difficulty of performing a distributed computation.
Say we have access to two distant computers and we want to compute the value of a Boolean function
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\times\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}
evaluated at some
(X,Y)
, where the
X\in\{0,1\}^{n}
is given to the first computer and
Y\in\{0,1\}^{m}
to the other, with the constraint of minimizing the cost of communication bits between the computers.
The CC of the function
f
is then defined as the minimal number of bits that the computers need to communicate so that the first computer is able to compute the value
f(X,Y)\in\{0,1\}
.
For instance, when
n=m=2
,
X=(x_{1},x_{2})
,
Y=(y_{1},y_{2})
, the CC of
f_{1}:=x_{1}\cdot(y_{1}\oplus y_{2})
equals
1
, using the communication bit
y_{1}\oplus y_{2}
, which can be computed by the second computer and then communicated to the first computer as it has access to
Y
. However, it is possible to show that the CC of
f_{2}:=(x_{1}\cdot y_{1})\oplus(x_{2}\cdot y_{2})
equals
2
, using communication bits
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
, which then means that
f_{2}
is more complex than
f_{1}
in the sense of CC.
Yao also introduced in
[
47
]
a probabilistic version of CC, in which the computers can access shared randomness, and where the condition is relaxed: there exists
p>1/2
such that for all
X,Y
the first computer has to output the correct value of
f(X,Y)
with probability at least
p
.
This notion is linked with nonlocal boxes: if a nonlocal box
\mathtt{P}
is used in the protocol to compute the value
f(X,Y)
and if the probabilistic CC of
any
function
f
is at most
1
independently of its entry size and with fixed
p
, then we say that the box
\mathtt{P}

collapses
communication complexity.
In this definition, an arbitrary number of copies of the box
\mathtt{P}
can be used in the protocol.
Such a collapse is strongly believed to be unachievable in nature since it would imply the absurdity that a single bit of communication is sufficient to distantly estimate any value of any Boolean function
f

[
17
,
9
,
11
,
4
]
.

Example
.

The
\mathtt{PR}
box is known to collapse CC
[
17
]
, so this correlation is physically unfeasible according to the principle of communication complexity.
More generally, we know that some noisy versions of the
\mathtt{PR}
box also collapse CC for different types of noise
[

,

,
10
,
6
,

,

]
.
On the other hand, we know that quantum correlations do
not

[

]
, and neither does a slightly wider set named “almost quantum correlations”
[
35
]
.
To this day, the question is still open whether the remaining non-signalling boxes are collapsing, meaning that there is still a gap to be filled.
We refer to
[

for a figure that summarizes the contributions.

1.1
Definition of the Game

The graph isomorphism game was introduced by Atserias, Mančinska, Roberson, Šámal, Severini, and Varvitsiotis in
[
3
]
.
This is a nonlocal game based on two graphs, namely
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
, in which the players Alice and Bob try to pretend to a referee that the two graphs are isomorphic in the classical sense. Recall that
\mathcal{G}
is said
isomorphic
to
\mathcal{H}
, denoted
\mathcal{G}\cong\mathcal{H}
, if there exists a bijection
\varphi
from the vertex set
V(\mathcal{G})
to the vertex set
V(\mathcal{H})
such that adjacency is preserved in both ways:

\forall g,g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G},\quad\quad g\sim g^{\prime}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\varphi(g)\sim\varphi(g^{\prime})\,,

(1)

where the symbol “
\sim
” denotes the adjacency relation.

The game consists in the following. The referee provides the players Alice and Bob with respective questions
x_{A},x_{B}\in V=V(\mathcal{G})\cup V(\mathcal{H})
, where
V(\mathcal{G})
and
V(\mathcal{H})
are assumed to be disjoint. In return, Alice and Bob use a predetermined strategy
\mathcal{S}
in order to produce some vertices
y_{A},y_{B}\in V
, and they send
y_{A},y_{B}
to the referee, who finally verifies if the players won the game.
The first condition they need to satisfy is that
x_{A}
and
y_{A}
have to be in different vertex sets, and similarly for
x_{B}
and
y_{B}
, meaning that

x_{A}\in V(\mathcal{G})\Leftrightarrow y_{A}\in V(\mathcal{H})\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad x_{B}\in V(\mathcal{G})\Leftrightarrow y_{B}\in V(\mathcal{H})\,,

(2)

otherwise they lose the game. Now, assuming that this condition holds, only one vertex among
x_{A}
and
y_{A}
is in
V(\mathcal{G})
, let us call it
g_{A}\in V(\mathcal{G})
, and the other
h_{A}\in V(\mathcal{H})
, and similarly for
g_{B}\in V(\mathcal{G})
and
h_{B}\in V(\mathcal{H})
.
The second condition for Alice and Bob to win the game is that
g_{A}
has the same relation to
g_{B}
as
h_{A}
has to
h_{B}
, that is the three following equivalences are satisfied:

g_{A}=g_{B}\Leftrightarrow h_{A}=h_{B}\,,\quad\quad g_{A}\sim g_{B}\Leftrightarrow h_{A}\sim h_{B}\,,\quad\quad g_{A}\not\simeq g_{B}\Leftrightarrow h_{A}\not\simeq h_{B}\,,

(3)

where the symbol “
\not\simeq
’’ means neither equal nor adjacent.

1.2
Key Ideas

In this subsection, we work on a simple case to present the key ideas that will be generalized in the theorem of
Subsection
1.4
​​

. The assumption here is that
\mathcal{H}
admits exactly two connected components that are both complete.

Definition 1.1
.

We denote by
\mathcal{C}_{n}
the
cycle
of size
n\geqslant 1
,
i.e.
the finite undirected graph with vertices
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
and edges
v_{i}\sim v_{i+1}
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n-1
and
v_{n}\sim v_{1}
.
We denote by
\mathcal{K}_{n}
the
complete graph
of size
n\geqslant 1
,
i.e.
the finite undirected graph with vertices
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
and edges
v_{i}\sim v_{j}
for any
i\neq j
.
We also define the
path graph
, denoted
\mathcal{L}_{n}
, as the cycle
\mathcal{C}_{n}
from which we remove one edge.
The distance
d
between two vertices
v_{1},v_{2}
in a graph
\mathcal{G}
is defined as the smallest number of edges of a path connecting
v_{1}
to
v_{2}
over all possible paths. By convention, it is taken to be
\infty
when there is no path connecting the vertices. Here, we call
diameter
of a graph
\mathcal{G}
, denoted
\diam(\mathcal{G})
, the largest distance between two connected vertices
g_{1},g_{2}
of
\mathcal{G}
(in this definition, the diameter of a finite graph is finite, even if it is not connected).

Theorem 1.2

(Collapse of CC)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
be two graphs such that

\diam(\mathcal{G})\geqslant 2
, and that
\mathcal{H}
admits exactly two connected components
\mathcal{H}_{0}
and
\mathcal{H}_{1}
, which are both complete. Then any perfect strategy
\mathcal{S}
for the graph isomorphism game
\mathcal{G}\cong_{ns}\mathcal{H}
collapses communication complexity.

Proof.

The proof simply consists in simulating the
\mathtt{PR}
box from the strategy
\mathcal{S}
, in the sense that from inputs
x,y\in\{0,1\}
, we want to produce outputs
a,b\in\{0,1\}
with the same behavior as the
\mathtt{PR}
would do,
i.e.
to obtain
a\oplus b=xy
in a non-signalling way. It is enough to simulate
\mathtt{PR}
since this nonlocal box is known to collapse communication complexity
[
17
]
.
As the diameter is
\diam(\mathcal{G})\geqslant 2
, the graph
\mathcal{G}
admits the path graph with three vertices
\mathcal{G}_{0}=\mathcal{L}_{3}
as a subgraph.
We will use the protocol described in
Figure
5
​​

.
In this protocol, bits
x
and
y
are given to Alice and Bob respectively. They apply the respective processes
A_{1}
and
B_{1}
as described in item (b) and obtain vertices
g_{A}
and
g_{B}
in
V(\mathcal{G}_{0})
. They use these two vertices in the graph isomorphism game of
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H})
and receive outputs
h_{A},h_{B}\in V(\mathcal{H})
. Finally, they process these vertices with
A_{2}
and
B_{2}
as described in item (c) to obtain their output
a,b\in\{0,1\}
.

​

Theorem
1.2

.
In item (a), we simulate a
\mathtt{PR}
box from a perfect
\mathcal{N\!S}
-strategy for the graph isomorphism game, called “Isom. Game” in the figure, together with the local processes
A_{1},A_{2},B_{1},B_{2}
that are described in items (b) and (c).
In item (b), the graph
\mathcal{G}_{0}
is a subgraph of
\mathcal{G}
, in which Alice and Bob choose some input vertices
g_{A}
and
g_{B}
.
In item (c), the graphs
\mathcal{H}_{1}

\mathcal{H}_{2}
are the two connected components of
\mathcal{H}

h_{A}
and

Let us prove that this protocol indeed simulates the
\mathtt{PR}
box.
On the one hand, if
xy=1
, then
x=1=y
, which gives
g_{A}\not\simeq g_{B}
and therefore
h_{A}\not\simeq h_{B}
. It yields that the vertices
h_{A}
and
h_{B}
are in different components
\mathcal{H}_{i}
and
\mathcal{H}_{i\oplus 1}

On the other hand, if

or

or

. It follows that the vertices
h_{A}
and
h_{B}
are both in the same component
\mathcal{H}_{i}
, and
a\oplus b=i\oplus i=0=xy
as expected as well. In addition, note that this protocol does
not
signal between Alice and Bob. Hence the
\mathtt{PR}
is perfectly simulated, and there is a collapse of communication complexity.
∎

1.3
Symmetric Strategies

We define a new type of perfect strategy for the graph isomorphism game, that we call
symmetric strategies
, for which we show a collapse of communication complexity in the next subsection.

Definition 1.5
.

An
\mathcal{N\!S}
-strategy
\mathcal{S}
for the graph isomorphism game
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H})
is said
symmetric
from
\mathcal{G}
to the components of
\mathcal{H}
if it is perfect (
i.e.
the winning probability is
1
) and if there exist a disjoint decomposition
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\sqcup\mathcal{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
and some constants
\eta,\nu_{g_{A},g_{B}}\in[0,1]
such that:

\forall g_{A},g_{B}\in\mathcal{G},\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathcal{S}}\big(h_{A}\in\mathcal{H}_{1},\,h_{B}\in\mathcal{H}_{2}\,\big|\,g_{A},\,g_{B}\big)\,=\,{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathcal{S}}\big(h_{A}\in\mathcal{H}_{2},\,h_{B}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}\,\big|\,g_{A},\,g_{B}\big)=:\nu_{g_{A},g_{B}}\\
{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathcal{S}}\big(h_{A}\in\mathcal{H}_{1},\,h_{B}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}\,\big|\,g_{A},\,g_{B}\big)\,=\,\eta-\nu_{g_{A},g_{B}}\,.\end{array}\right.

Examples of graphs admitting symmetric strategies can be found in
Example
1.8
​​

.
For the sake of the next proposition, we recall that two finite undirected graphs
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
are
\mathcal{N\!S}
-isomorphic if and only if they are fractionally isomorphic
[
3
]
, if and only if they admit a common equitable partition
[
40
]
, whose definition is recalled below.

Common Equitable Partition
[
40
]
.

Given a graph
\mathcal{G}
,
define a partition
\mathscr{C}=\{C_{1},\dots,C_{k}\}
of its vertices, that is subsets
C_{i}\subseteq V(\mathcal{G})
such that every vertex
g
of
\mathcal{G}
belongs to exactly one set
C_{i_{g}}
, which may be viewed as assigning a (unique) color
C_{i_{g}}
to each vertex.
We say that this partition is
equitable
if, for all
i,j\in[k]
, any vertex of color
C_{i}
admits precisely a fixed number, denoted
c_{ij}
, of neighbors colored with
C_{j}
.
Note that
c_{ij}
and
c_{ji}
may differ, but the equality
c_{ij}|C_{i}|=c_{ji}|C_{j}|
always holds (see
Lemma
3.10
​​

for a proof of a generalized result).
A trivial example of an equitable partition is the minimal partition
\mathscr{C}=\{\{g\}\,:\,g\in V(\mathcal{G})\}
, where to each vertex a different color is assigned and where the matrix
[c_{ij}]_{i,j}
is the adjacency matrix.
Another example is the maximal partition
\mathscr{C}=\{V(\mathcal{G})\}
, which is equitable if and only if the graph
\mathcal{G}
is regular of degree
c_{11}
.
Now, we say that two graphs
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
admit a
common equitable partition
if they admit equitable partitions of same length
\mathscr{C}=\{C_{1},\dots,C_{k}\}
and
\mathscr{D}=\{D_{1},\dots,D_{k}\}
such that the cells have same size
|C_{i}|=|D_{i}|=:n_{i}
and the partition parameters coincide
c_{ij}=d_{ij}
for all
i,j\in[k]
.
When such partitions exist, we may concisely describe them in terms of the parameters
\big(k,(n_{i}),(c_{ij})\big)
and, when the context is clear, we may consider
\mathscr{C}
as an equitable partition of both
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
.
As mentioned above, we will use the fact that
\mathcal{G}\cong_{ns}\mathcal{H}
if and only if the graphs admit a common equitable partition
[
40
]
. For instance the graphs
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{C}_{6}
and
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{C}_{3}\sqcup\mathcal{C}_{3}
are both
2
-regular, so they admit admit a common equitable partition
\big(k=1,(n_{1}=6),(c_{11}=2)\big)
, which is why they are
\mathcal{N\!S}
-isomorphic.

Proposition 1.6

(Existence of Symmetric Strategies)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}\cong_{ns}\mathcal{H}
such that
\mathcal{H}
is not connected:
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\sqcup\mathcal{H}_{2}
. Denote the partitions
\mathscr{C}=\{C_{1},\dots,C_{k}\}
and
\mathscr{D}=\{D_{1},\dots,D_{k}\}
forming a common equitable partition for
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
, and assume that the proportion of vertices of
\mathcal{H}_{1}
assigned to
D_{i}
is independent of
i
:

\forall i,j\in[k],\quad\quad\frac{|D_{i}\cap\mathcal{H}_{1}|}{|D_{i}|}\,=\,\frac{|D_{j}\cap\mathcal{H}_{1}|}{|D_{j}|}\,.

(H)

Then the isomorphism game of
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H})
admits a symmetric strategy.

Proof.

Let
\big(k,\,[n_{1},\dots,n_{k}],\,[c_{ij}]_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant k}\big)
be the parameters of a common equitable partition for
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
, and consider
\overline{c_{ij}}:=n_{j}-c_{ij}-\delta_{ij}
the number of non-neighbours a vertex of
C_{i}
has in
C_{j}
for all
i,j
, where
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker delta function.
We define the following strategy
\mathcal{S}
as in
[
3
, Lemma 4.4]
for which the authors prove it is perfect for the isomorphism game of
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H})
:

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}(h_{A},h_{B}\,|\,g_{A},g_{B})\,:=\,\left\{\begin{array}[]{c l}1/n_{i}&\text{if $g_{A}=g_{B}$ and $h_{A}=h_{B}$ and $(\star)$},\\
1/n_{i}c_{ij}&\text{if $g_{A}\sim g_{B}$ and $h_{A}\sim h_{B}$ and $(\star)$},\\
1/n_{i}\overline{c_{ij}}&\text{if $g_{A}\not\simeq g_{B}$ and $h_{A}\not\simeq h_{B}$ and $(\star)$},\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{array}\right.

(4)

where
(\star)
denotes the condition “
g_{A}\in C_{i},\,g_{B}\in C_{j},\,h_{A}\in D_{i},\,h_{B}\in D_{j}
”.
Note that
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}
is well defined because in each case the division by zero is prevented using the condition of occurrence. Let us show that
\mathcal{S}
is symmetric in two steps.

\bullet
First, we compute the constants
\nu_{g_{A},g_{B}}:={\mathbb{P}}_{\mathcal{S}}\big(h_{A}\in\mathcal{H}_{1},\,h_{B}\in\mathcal{H}_{2}\,\big|\,g_{A},\,g_{B}\big)
. Notice that
\nu_{g_{A},g_{B}}=0
when
g_{A}=g_{B}
or
g_{A}\sim g_{B}

by disconnectedness of
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
. Now if
g_{A}\not\simeq g_{B}
for some
g_{A}\in C_{i}
and
g_{B}\in C_{j}
, then:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\big(h_{A}\in\mathcal{H}_{1},h_{B}\in\mathcal{H}_{2}\,\big|\,g_{A}\not\simeq g_{B}\big)\hskip 5.69046pt

\displaystyle=\hskip-5.69046pt\sum_{(h_{A},h_{B})\in\mathcal{H}_{1}\times\mathcal{H}_{2}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}(h_{A},h_{B}\,|\,g_{A}\not\simeq g_{B})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tiny\begin{array}[]{c}(h_{A},h_{B})\in\mathcal{H}_{1}\times\mathcal{H}_{2}\\
\text{s.t. }h_{A}\in D_{i},h_{B}\in D_{j}\end{array}}\frac{1}{n_{i}\,\overline{c_{ij}}}

\displaystyle=\quad\frac{|D_{i}\cap\mathcal{H}_{1}|\times|D_{j}\cap\mathcal{H}_{2}|}{n_{i}\,\overline{c_{ij}}}\,.

But as
|D_{i}|=|D_{i}\cap\mathcal{H}_{1}|+|D_{i}\cap\mathcal{H}_{2}|
for all
i
, we see that the assumption
(H)
is equivalent to saying
|D_{i}\cap\mathcal{H}_{1}|\times|D_{j}\cap\mathcal{H}_{2}|=|D_{i}\cap\mathcal{H}_{2}|\times|D_{j}\cap\mathcal{H}_{1}|
.
Therefore, the above quantity also equals
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\big(h_{A}\in\mathcal{H}_{2},h_{B}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}\,\big|\,g_{A},g_{B})
, and we obtain:

\quad\quad\nu_{g_{A},g_{B}}\,=\,\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\displaystyle{|D_{i}\cap\mathcal{H}_{1}|\times|D_{j}\cap\mathcal{H}_{2}|}/{n_{i}\,\overline{c_{ij}}}&\text{if $g_{A}\not\simeq g_{B}$ and $g_{A}\in C_{i}$ and $g_{B}\in C_{j}$},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

\bullet
Second, we compute
\eta:={\mathbb{P}}_{\mathcal{S}}\big(h_{A}\in\mathcal{H}_{1},\,h_{B}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}\,\big|\,g_{A},\,g_{B}\big)+\nu_{g_{A},g_{B}}
, which should be independent of
g_{A},g_{B}
. Let
g_{A}\in C_{i}
and
g_{B}\in C_{j}
. We split the study into three cases.

(i)
If
g_{A}=g_{B}
, then:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\big((h_{A},h_{B})\in{\mathcal{H}_{1}}^{2}\,\big|\,g_{A}=g_{B}\big)\hskip 5.69046pt

\displaystyle=\hskip-5.69046pt\sum_{(h_{A},h_{B})\in{\mathcal{H}_{1}}^{2}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}(h_{A},h_{B}\,|\,g_{A}=g_{B})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tiny\begin{array}[]{c}h\in\mathcal{H}_{1}\\
\text{s.t. }h\in D_{i}\end{array}}\underbrace{\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}(h,h\,|\,g_{B}=g_{B})}_{=1/n_{i}}

\displaystyle=\quad\frac{|D_{i}\cap\mathcal{H}_{1}|}{n_{i}}\quad=\quad\eta-\nu_{g_{A},g_{B}}\,,

where we fixed
\eta:=\frac{|D_{i}\cap\mathcal{H}_{1}|}{n_{i}}
, and where we have
n_{i}\neq 0
because
g_{A}\in C_{i}
.
Let us verify that this
\eta
is appropriate in the other cases as well.

(ii)
If
g_{A}\sim g_{B}
, then:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\big((h_{A},h_{B})\in{\mathcal{H}_{1}}^{2}\,\big|\,g_{A}\sim g_{B}\big)\hskip 5.69046pt

\displaystyle=\hskip-5.69046pt\sum_{(h_{A},h_{B})\in{\mathcal{H}_{1}}^{2}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}(h_{A},h_{B}\,|\,g_{A}\sim g_{B})

\displaystyle=\hskip-8.5359pt\sum_{\tiny\begin{array}[]{c}(h_{A},h_{B})\in{\mathcal{H}_{1}}^{2}\\
\text{s.t. }h_{A}\in D_{i},\\
h_{B}\in D_{j}\cap N(h_{A})\end{array}}\underbrace{\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}(h_{A},h_{B}\,|\,g_{A}\sim g_{B})}_{=1/n_{i}c_{ij}}\,,

where
N(h_{A})
is the set of adjacent vertices to
h_{A}
and where
c_{ij}\neq 0
because
g_{A}\sim g_{B}
, so:

\displaystyle= \hskip-2.84544pt\sum_{h_{A}\in D_{i}\cap\mathcal{H}_{1}}\frac{\big|D_{j}\cap N(h_{A})\cap\mathcal{H}_{1}\big|}{n_{i}\,c_{ij}}\,.

But by disconnectedness of
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
we have
|D_{j}\cap N(h_{A})\cap\mathcal{H}_{1}|=|D_{j}\cap N(h_{A})|

, which is equal to

by definition. Hence, it simplifies with the coefficient in the denominator, so we obtain:

\displaystyle=\hskip-2.84544pt\sum_{h_{A}\in D_{i}\cap\mathcal{H}_{1}}\frac{1}{n_{i}}\quad=\quad\frac{|D_{i}\cap\mathcal{H}_{1}|}{n_{i}}\ \quad=\quad\eta-\nu_{g_{A},g_{B}}\,.

(iii)
If
g_{A}\not\simeq g_{B}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\big((h_{A},h_{B})\in{\mathcal{H}_{1}}^{2}\,\big|\,g_{A}\not\simeq g_{B}\big)\hskip 5.69046pt

\displaystyle=\sum_{(h_{A},h_{B})\in{\mathcal{H}_{1}}^{2}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}(h_{A},h_{B}\,|\,g_{A}\not\simeq g_{B})

\displaystyle=\hskip-17.07182pt\sum_{\tiny\begin{array}[]{c}(h_{A},h_{B})\in{\mathcal{H}_{1}}^{2}\\
\text{s.t. }h_{A}\in D_{i},\\
h_{B}\in D_{j}\backslash(N(h_{A})\cup\{h_{A}\})\end{array}}\hskip-14.22636pt\underbrace{\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}(h_{A},h_{B}\,|\,g_{A}\not\simeq g_{B})}_{=1/n_{i}\overline{c_{ij}}}\,,

where
\overline{c_{ij}}\neq 0
because
g_{A}\not\simeq g_{B}
, so:

\displaystyle=\sum_{h_{A}\in D_{i}\cap\mathcal{H}_{1}}\frac{\big|D_{j}\cap\mathcal{H}_{1}\backslash(N(h_{A})\cup\{h_{A}\})\big|}{n_{i}\,\overline{c_{ij}}}\,.

But by definition
n_{j}:=|D_{j}|=|D_{j}\cap\mathcal{H}_{2}|+|D_{j}\cap\mathcal{H}_{1}\backslash(N(h_{A})\cup\{h_{A}\})|+|D_{j}\cap\mathcal{H}_{1}\cap(N(h_{A})\cup\{h_{A}\})|
, where the last term is
c_{ij}+\delta_{ij}
. After reordering the terms, it yields that the second term is
|D_{j}\cap\mathcal{H}_{1}\backslash(N(h_{A})\cup\{h_{A}\})|=\overline{c_{ij}}-|D_{j}\cap\mathcal{H}_{2}|
, therefore:

\displaystyle=\sum_{h_{A}\in D_{i}\cap\mathcal{H}_{1}}\frac{\overline{c_{ij}}-|D_{j}\cap\mathcal{H}_{2}|}{n_{i}\,\overline{c_{ij}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{|D_{i}\cap\mathcal{H}_{1}|}{n_{i}}-\frac{|D_{i}\cap\mathcal{H}_{1}|\times|D_{j}\cap\mathcal{H}_{2}|}{n_{i}\,\overline{c_{ij}}}

\displaystyle=\eta-\nu_{g_{A},g_{B}}\,.

Hence, the coefficient
\eta
is the same in the three cases and independent of
g_{A},g_{B}
, so we proved that
\mathcal{S}
is indeed symmetric. ∎

1.5
All Perfect Non-Signalling Strategies Collapse CC

In
Theorem
1.9
​​

above, the statement was that
some
perfect strategies collapse communication complexity. Now, if we add regularity and transitivity conditions on
\mathcal{H}
, we obtain that
every
perfect strategy collapses communication complexity. First, we recall the definition of the automorphism group of a graph.

1.5.1
Automorphism Group

The
automorphism group
of
\mathcal{H}
, denoted
\Aut(\mathcal{H})
, is the set of all bijective maps
\varphi:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
that preserve the adjacency relation, meaning that
h_{1}\sim h_{2}
if and only if
\varphi(h_{1})\sim\varphi(h_{2})
. As a consequence, any automorphism
\varphi\in\Aut(\mathcal{H})
also preserves the relation “
\not\simeq
”, and therefore a graph
\mathcal{H}
and its complement
\mathcal{H}^{c}
have the same automorphism group:
\Aut(\mathcal{H})=\Aut(\mathcal{H}^{c})
.
Moreover, the composition of two automorphisms is again an automorphism, and
\varphi^{-1}\in\Aut(\mathcal{H})
, which endows the set
\Aut(\mathcal{H})
with a group structure.
For instance, the automorphism group of the complete graph
\mathcal{K}_{N}
is the symmetric group
\Aut(\mathcal{K}_{N})=\mathfrak{S}_{N}
of order
N!
, and the one of the cycle
\mathcal{C}_{N}
is the dihedral group
\Aut(\mathcal{C}_{N})=D_{N}
of order
2N
.
We refer to
[
25
]
for more details on automorphism groups.

1.5.2
Graph Transitivity

The graph
\mathcal{H}
is said to be
vertex-transitive
if for all vertices
h,h^{\prime}\in V(\mathcal{H})
, there exists a graph automorphism
\varphi\in\Aut(\mathcal{H})
such that
\varphi(h)=h^{\prime}
.
Similarly, we say that
\mathcal{H}
is
edge-transitive
if for all edges
h_{1}\sim h_{2},\,h_{1}^{\prime}\sim h_{2}^{\prime}\in E(\mathcal{H})
, there exists a graph automorphism
\varphi\in\Aut(\mathcal{H})
such that
\varphi(h_{1})=h_{1}^{\prime}
and
\varphi(h_{2})=h_{2}^{\prime}
.
We refer to
[
25
]
for more details on graph transitivity, and to
[
24
,
44
]
for related notions.
In the definition below, we strengthen the vertex- and edge- transitivity of a graph
\mathcal{H}
and its complement
\mathcal{H}^{c}
in what we call the
strong transitivity
:

Definition 1.12

(Strongly Transitive)
.

We say that a graph
\mathcal{H}
is
strongly transitive
if there exists a subset of the automorphism group
S\subseteq\Aut(\mathcal{H})
such that the three following conditions hold:

(i)

There is a constant
d_{1}\geqslant 1
such that for all vertices
h,h^{\prime}\in V(\mathcal{H})
, there exist exactly
d_{1}
automorphisms
\varphi\in S
such that
\varphi(h)=h^{\prime}
.

(ii)

There is a constant
d_{2}\geqslant 1
such that for all edges
h_{1}\sim h_{2},\,h_{1}^{\prime}\sim h_{2}^{\prime}\in E(\mathcal{H})
, there exist exactly
d_{2}
automorphisms
\varphi\in S
such that
\varphi(h_{1})=h_{1}^{\prime}
and
\varphi(h_{2})=h_{2}^{\prime}
.

(iii)

There is a constant
d_{3}\geqslant 1
such that for all edges in the complement graph
h_{1}\sim h_{2},\,h_{1}^{\prime}\sim h_{2}^{\prime}\in E(\mathcal{H}^{c})
, there exist exactly
d_{3}
automorphisms
\varphi\in S
such that
\varphi(h_{1})=h_{1}^{\prime}
and
\varphi(h_{2})=h_{2}^{\prime}
.

Notice that strong transitivity implies vertex-transitivity and edge-transitivity of both
\mathcal{H}
and its complement
\mathcal{H}^{c}
, since it is possible to pick one among the respective
d_{1}
,
d_{2}
,
d_{3}
automorphisms
\varphi\in S
satisfying the wanted condition for each vertices and edges.
Note that if
\mathcal{H}
is strongly transitive, then its complement
\mathcal{H}^{c}
is also strongly transitive.
Note also that
S
cannot be the empty set
\emptyset
because of condition
(i)
, unless the graph
\mathcal{H}
is itself empty.

Let us prove the following characterization of strong transitivity, and then provide some examples of strongly transitive graphs. First recall that a graph
\mathcal{H}
is called
distance-transitive
if for any
d\in\mathbb{N}
and any two pairs
(h_{1},h_{2})
and
(h_{1}^{\prime},h_{2}^{\prime})
of vertices
h_{1},h_{2},h_{1}^{\prime},h_{2}^{\prime}\in V(\mathcal{H})
with distance
d(h_{1},h_{2})=d(h_{1}^{\prime},h_{2}^{\prime})=d
, there is an automorphism
\varphi
of
\mathcal{H}
such that
\varphi(h_{1})=h_{1}^{\prime}
and
\varphi(h_{2})=h_{2}^{\prime}
.

Lemma 1.13

(Characterization of Strong Transitivity)
.

A graph
\mathcal{H}
is strongly transitive if and only if it is distance-transitive and its diameter satisfies
\diam(\mathcal{H})\leqslant 2
. Moreover, we may always choose
S=\Aut(\mathcal{H})
in
Definition
1.12
​​

.

Proof.

Assume that
\mathcal{H}
is strongly transitive in the sense of
Definition
1.12
​​

.
First, we prove distance-transitivity for three instances of
d\in\mathbb{N}
:
vertex-transitivity (
d=0
) is a particular case of item
(i)
of the definition;
edge-transitivity (
d=1
) is a particular case of item
(ii)
;
and in any other case (
d\geqslant 2
), vertices at distance
d
in
\mathcal{H}
are adjacent in the complement graph
\mathcal{H}^{c}
, so the existence of automorphism
\varphi
follows from item
(iii)
; hence the distance-transitivity.
We then prove that all vertices in
\mathcal{H}
have distance at most
2
. Assume by contradiction that there are two vertices
h_{1},h_{2}\in V(\mathcal{H})
with
d(h_{1},h_{2})>2
. Hence, there is a path from
h_{1}
to
h_{2}
of length greater than two, which needs to pass through a vertex
h_{3}\in V(\mathcal{H})
with
d(h_{1},h_{3})=2
. Now, as
h_{1}\sim h_{2}
and
h_{1}\sim h_{3}
are edges of the complement graph
\mathcal{H}^{c}
, item
(iii)
of
Definition
1.12
tells us that we can find an automorphism
\varphi\in S
with
\varphi(h_{1})=h_{1}
and
\varphi(h_{2})=h_{3}
, and as automorphisms preserve distances, we have
d(h_{1},h_{2})=d(\varphi(h_{1}),\varphi(h_{2}))=d(h_{1},h_{3})
. But this contradicts
d(h_{1},h_{2})>d(h_{1},h_{3})
, so we have
\diam(\mathcal{H})\leqslant 2
as claimed.

Conversely, assume that
\mathcal{H}
is distance-transitive with
\diam(\mathcal{H})\leqslant 2
, and choose
S=\Aut(\mathcal{H})
. We prove the three items of
Definition
1.12
​​

in the canonical order. Given vertices
h,h^{\prime}\in V(\mathcal{H})
denote by
a_{h,h^{\prime}}
the number of automorphisms
\varphi\in\Aut(\mathcal{H})
with
\varphi(h)=h^{\prime}
. It is nonzero since
\mathcal{H}
is distance-transitive so in particular vertex-transitive. Now, given two further vertices
k,k^{\prime}\in V(\mathcal{H})
, there are automorphisms
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}\in\Aut(\mathcal{H})
with
\varphi_{1}(h)=k
and
\varphi_{2}(h^{\prime})=k^{\prime}
. For any automorphism
\psi
with
\psi(k)=k^{\prime}
, the map
\varphi_{2}^{-1}\circ\psi\circ\varphi_{1}
yields an automorphism mapping
h
to
h^{\prime}
. This shows
a_{k,k^{\prime}}\leqslant a_{h,h^{\prime}}
. Now, by the symmetry of the argument, this is actually equality, and we may set
d_{1}:=a_{h,h^{\prime}}\geqslant 1
, thus proving item
(i)
.
Then, for item
(ii)
, the proof is very similar, using the edge-transitivity of
\mathcal{H}
.
Finally, for item
(iii)
, note that edges in the complement graph
\mathcal{H}^{c}
correspond to pairs of vertices in
\mathcal{H}
at distance
2
, so once again distance-transitivity allows us to conclude with the same argument as for item
(i)
.
∎

Example 1.14
.

From this characterization, we deduce that the following graphs are examples of strongly transitive graphs, among others:
the complete graphs
\mathcal{K}_{N}
and their complement
\mathcal{K}_{N}^{c}
for any
N\geqslant 0
(note that the empty graph
\mathcal{K}_{N}^{c}
has diameter
0
with our convention),
the path graphs
\mathcal{L}_{N}
for
N\leqslant 3
,
the cycle graphs
\mathcal{C}_{N}
for
N\leqslant 5
,
the complete bipartite graph
\mathcal{K}_{3,3}
,
and the famous Petersen graph.
Moreover, several finite groups may be written as the automorphism group of a distance-transitive graph with diameter
2
, see details in
[
25
]
.

We prove that when
\mathcal{H}
is strongly transitive, there is a strong connection between cardinalities, which will be useful in the proof of the collapse of CC in
Theorem
1.16
​​

.

Lemma 1.15
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a strongly transitive graph different from the complete graph
\mathcal{K}_{N}
and its complement
\mathcal{K}_{N}^{c}
, together with its associated subset
S\subseteq\Aut(\mathcal{H})
and parameters
(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3})
. Then the size of the set
S
is necessarily

|S|\,=\,d_{1}\,|V(\mathcal{H})|\,=\,2\,d_{2}\,|E(\mathcal{H})|\,=\,2\,d_{3}\,|E(\mathcal{H}^{c})|\,.

Proof.

We prove the first equality by showing the two inequalities. Let us index
h_{1},\dots,h_{n}
the vertices of
\mathcal{H}
, where
n=|V(\mathcal{H})|
.
On the one hand, using condition
(i)
, we know that there are exactly
d_{1}
automorphisms
\varphi\in S
sending
h_{1}
to itself, and again
d_{1}
other automorphisms sending
h_{1}
to
h_{2}
, so on and so forth until
h_{n}
. Note also that a given automorphism
\varphi\in\Aut(\mathcal{H})
cannot send
h_{1}
to two different vertices. It yields that the set
S
contains at least
d_{1}n
elements.
On the other hand, each element
\varphi
of
S
necessarily sends
h_{1}
to a vertex
h_{i}
of
\mathcal{H}
, so
S
contains at most
d_{1}n
elements, which gives the first equality.
For the other two equalities, proceed similarly using conditions
(ii)
and
(iii)
, where the factor “
2
” comes from the fact that graphs are undirected, so the two relations
h_{1}\sim h_{2}
and
h_{2}\sim h_{1}
are counted as only one edge.
Note also that the condition
\mathcal{H}\neq\mathcal{K}_{N}
and
\mathcal{K}\neq\mathcal{K}_{N}^{c}
prevents the sets
E(\mathcal{H})
and
E(\mathcal{H}^{c})
to be empty.
Hence the wanted chain of equalities.
∎

1.5.3
Collapse of Communication Complexity

In the theorem below, we combine this notion of strong transitivity with
d
-regularity of
\mathcal{H}
to obtain a collapse of CC. The key idea will be to compute an expectation
\mathbb{E}
over
\varphi
uniformly sampled in a subset of the automorphism group
S\subseteq\Aut(\mathcal{H})
and to use the strong transitivity to obtain a symmetric strategy that collapses CC. Recall that
\mathcal{H}
is said to be
d
-regular if every vertex is connected to exactly
d
other vertices.

Theorem 1.16

(All Perfect Strategies Collapse CC)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
be two graphs such that the conditions of
Theorem
1.9
​​

hold. Assume moreover that
\mathcal{H}
is strongly transitive and
d
-regular, and that the players share randomness. Then
every
perfect non-signalling strategy for the isomorphism game associated with
\mathcal{G}\cong_{ns}\mathcal{H}
collapses communication complexity.

Proof.

Denote
\mathtt{P}
an arbitrary perfect strategy for
\mathcal{G}\cong_{ns}\mathcal{H}
, and denote
S\subseteq\Aut(\mathcal{H})
and
(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3})
as given in the definition of strong transitivity.
We will post-process
\mathtt{P}
in order to generate a symmetric strategy, which will allow us to generate a collapsing nonlocal box.
The two players Alice and Bob can use their shared randomness to pick uniformly at random an automorphism
\varphi\in S
that is known by the two of them.
They apply the following protocol: once they receive the outputs
h_{A},h_{B}
from
\mathtt{P}
, they compute the images
h_{A}^{\prime}=\varphi(h_{A})
and
h_{B}^{\prime}=\varphi(h_{B})
and they call
\tilde{\mathtt{P}}(h_{A}^{\prime},h_{B}^{\prime}\,|\,g_{A},g_{B})
the new strategy, in other words this is the following expectation:

\tilde{\mathtt{P}}(h_{A}^{\prime},h_{B}^{\prime}\,|\,g_{A},g_{B})\,=\,\underset{\varphi\in S}{\scalebox{1.4}{$\mathbb{E}$}}\,\mathtt{P}\big(\varphi^{-1}(h_{A}^{\prime}),\varphi^{-1}(h_{B}^{\prime})\,\big|\,g_{A},g_{B}\big)\,.

Observe that
\tilde{\mathtt{P}}
is non-signalling by the composition of a non-signalling strategy with a non-signalling post-process.
Let us compute the expression of
\tilde{\mathtt{P}}
.
First, if
g_{A}=g_{B}
, then:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathtt{P}}(h_{A}^{\prime},h_{B}^{\prime}\,|\,g_{A},g_{A})

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{1}{d_{1}\,|V(\mathcal{H})|}\sum_{\varphi\in S}\,\mathtt{P}\big(\varphi^{-1}(h_{A}^{\prime}),\varphi^{-1}(h_{B}^{\prime})\,\big|\,g_{A},g_{A}\big)

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{1}{d_{1}\,|V(\mathcal{H})|}\sum_{\varphi\in S}\,\mathtt{P}\big(\varphi^{-1}(h_{A}^{\prime}),\varphi^{-1}(h_{A}^{\prime})\,\big|\,g_{A},g_{A}\big)\,\delta_{\varphi^{-1}(h_{A}^{\prime})=\varphi^{-1}(h_{B}^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{1}{d_{1}\,|V(\mathcal{H})|}\,\,d_{1}\underbrace{\sum_{h\in V(\mathcal{H})}\,\mathtt{P}\big(h,h\,\big|\,g_{A},g_{A}\big)}_{=1}\,\delta_{h_{A}^{\prime}=h_{B}^{\prime}}\,=\,\frac{\delta_{h_{A}^{\prime}=h_{B}^{\prime}}}{|V(\mathcal{H})|}\,,

where the first equality follows from the definition of
\tilde{\mathtt{P}}
combined with

1.15
​​

, the second one from the rules of the isomorphism game, and the third one from condition
(i)
in the definition of strong transitivity of

; moreover, the Kronecker delta condition is changed using the bijectivity property of an automorphism
\varphi

Second, if

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathtt{P}}(h_{A}^{\prime},h_{B}^{\prime}\,|\,g_{A},g_{B})

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{1}{2\,d_{2}\,|E(\mathcal{H})|}\sum_{\varphi\in S}\,\mathtt{P}\big(\varphi^{-1}(h_{A}^{\prime}),\varphi^{-1}(h_{B}^{\prime})\,\big|\,g_{A},g_{B}\big)

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{1}{2\,d_{2}\,|E(\mathcal{H})|}\,\,d_{2}\!\underbrace{\sum_{h_{1}\sim h_{2}\in V(\mathcal{H})}\,\mathtt{P}\big(h_{1},h_{2}\,\big|\,g_{A},g_{B}\big)}_{=1}\,\delta_{h_{A}^{\prime}\sim h_{B}^{\prime}}\,=\,\frac{\delta_{h_{A}^{\prime}\sim h_{B}^{\prime}}}{2\,|E(\mathcal{H})|}\,.

\mathcal{H}

\tilde{\mathtt{P}}(h_{A}^{\prime},h_{B}^{\prime}\,|\,g_{A},g_{B})\,=\,\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1/|V(\mathcal{H})|&\text{if $g_{A}=g_{B}$ and $h_{A}^{\prime}=h_{B}^{\prime}$}\,,\\
1/2|E(\mathcal{H})|&\text{if $g_{A}\sim g_{B}$ and $h_{A}^{\prime}\sim h_{B}^{\prime}$}\,,\\

2
Graph Coloring Game

2.1
Definition of the Graph Homomorphism and the Graph Coloring Games

Here, we introduce two non-local games, namely the
graph homomorphism game
and the
graph coloring game
, the latter being a valuable particular case of the former.

Graph Homomorphism Game
[
32
]
.

Similarly to the isomorphism game, in this game associated with some graphs
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H})
, the players try to convince a referee that they know a homomorphism
\varphi:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{H}
. Recall that a map
\varphi:V(\mathcal{G})\to V(\mathcal{H})
is a graph homomorphism if it preserves the adjacency, meaning that an edge
g\sim g^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{G}
is sent to an edge
\varphi(g)\sim\varphi(g^{\prime})
in
\mathcal{H}
. Note that a graph isomorphism additionally requires that
\varphi
is bijective and that the converse also holds, as stated in equation (
1
).
Note also that the composition of two graph homomorphisms is again a graph homomorphism itself – this defines a category.
The nonlocal game associated with this notion is naturally called
(graph) homomorphism game
. This game consists in giving respective vertices
g_{A},g_{B}\in V(\mathcal{G})
to Alice and Bob, they answer some vertices
h_{A},h_{B}\in V(\mathcal{H})
, and the referee declares that they win the game if and only if they satisfy the following conditions:

g_{A}=g_{B}\Longrightarrow h_{A}=h_{B}\,,\quad\quad\quad g_{A}\sim g_{B}\Longrightarrow h_{A}\sim h_{B}\,.

As opposed to the isomorphism game, in this game it may happen that
g_{A}\not\sim g_{B}
but still
h_{A}=h_{B}
or
h_{A}\sim h_{B}
.
We denote
\mathcal{G}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
if Alice and Bob can perfectly win the game using classical resources, which is equivalent to saying that there exists a graph homomorphism from
\mathcal{G}
to
\mathcal{H}
.
We similarly denote
\mathcal{G}\rightarrow_{q}\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{G}\rightarrow_{ns}\mathcal{H}
when the game can be perfectly won using quantum and non-signalling resources respectively.

Graph Coloring Game
[
13
]
.

Interestingly, when
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{K}_{N}
is complete, the homomorphism game corresponds to proving to a referee that the graph
\mathcal{G}
is
N
-colorable, so this particular case is called the
(graph) coloring game
. Indeed, we say that a graph
\mathcal{G}
is
N
-colorable if, from a set of
N
different colors, we can assign one color to each vertex of
\mathcal{G}
so that no adjacent vertex shares the same color. This is the exact same constraint for a graph homomorphism
\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{K}_{N}
, since two adjacent vertices in
\mathcal{G}
are sent to adjacent vertices in
\mathcal{K}_{N}
,
i.e.
to two different “colors”.
The complete graph
\mathcal{K}_{M}
is
N
-colorable if and only if
M\leqslant N
, but interestingly when we consider the coloring game with non-signalling strategies, Alice and Bob are able to pretend to the referee that they know an
N
-coloring for
\mathcal{K}_{M}
:

Example 2.1
.

We have
\mathcal{K}_{M}\to_{ns}\mathcal{K}_{N}
for any
M,N\geqslant 2
, that is
\mathcal{K}_{M}
admits a perfect non-signalling strategy for the
N
-coloring game:

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\big(h_{A},\,h_{B}\,\big|\,g_{A},\,g_{B}\big)\,=\,\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}1/N&\text{if $h_{A}=h_{B}$ and $g_{A}=g_{B}$,}\\
1/N(N-1)&\text{if $h_{A}\sim h_{B}$ and $g_{A}\sim g_{B}$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\\
\end{array}\right.

This strategy
\mathcal{S}
indeed satisfies the rules of the homomorphism game by construction. Moreover, it is a well-defined probability distribution: it is non-negative by construction; and for all fixed
g_{A},g_{B}\in V(\mathcal{K}_{M})
, it sums to
1
over
h_{A},h_{B}\in V(\mathcal{K}_{N})
because (i) for
g_{A}=g_{B}
, see that
\mathcal{K}_{N}
admits exactly
N
vertices, (ii) for
g_{A}\sim g_{B}
, there are exactly
N^{2}-N=N(N-1)
pairs
(h_{A},h_{B})\in V(\mathcal{K}_{N})^{2}
such that
h_{A}\neq h_{B}
, and (iii) the case
g_{A}\not\simeq g_{B}
never happens in
\mathcal{K}_{M}
. Finally, we prove that this strategy is indeed non-signalling because Alice’s marginal is independent of Bob’s input
g_{B}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{h_{A}}\,\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}(h_{A},\,h_{B}\,|\,g_{A},\,g_{B})

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{1}{N}\sum_{h_{A}}\delta_{h_{A}=h_{B}}\delta_{g_{A}=g_{B}}+\frac{1}{N(N-1)}\sum_{h_{A}}\delta_{h_{A}\sim h_{B}}\delta_{g_{A}\sim g_{B}}

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{1}{N}\,\delta_{g_{A}=g_{B}}+\frac{N-1}{N(N-1)}\,\delta_{g_{A}\sim g_{B}}\,=\,\frac{1}{N}\,,

where the last line holds because
\mathcal{K}_{N}
is complete so exactly one of the Kronecker deltas is
1
and the other is zero,
and similarly for Bob’s marginal.

3
Vertex Distance Game [new]

In this section, we introduce and study a generalization of the graph isomorphism game (
Section
1
​​

). We name it the
vertex distance game
.

3.1
Definition of the Game

We introduce a new nonlocal game that we call
vertex distance game
with parameter
D\in\mathbb{N}
, or simply
D
-distance game
. Given two graphs
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
with disjoint vertex sets, two question vertices are chosen by a referee
x_{A},x_{B}\in V=V(\mathcal{G})\cup V(\mathcal{H})
and distributed to space-like separated players Alice and Bob who are not allowed to communicate.
See footnote
2
at page
2
to understand why we choose the inputs
x_{A},x_{B}
in
V
and not simply in
V(\mathcal{G})
.
In order to win the game, Alice and Bob try to output vertices
y_{A},y_{B}\in V
satisfying two conditions. The first one is the same first rule as (
2
) for the graph isomorphism game:

x_{A}\in V(\mathcal{G})\Leftrightarrow y_{A}\in V(\mathcal{H})\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad x_{B}\in V(\mathcal{G})\Leftrightarrow y_{B}\in V(\mathcal{H})\,.

(6)

Assuming this relation holds, we relabel the vertices into
g_{A},g_{B},h_{A},h_{B}
as in the isomorphism game: only one vertex among
x_{A}
and
y_{A}
is in
V(\mathcal{G})
, let us call it
g_{A}\in V(\mathcal{G})
, and the other
h_{A}\in V(\mathcal{H})
, and similarly for
g_{B}\in V(\mathcal{G})
and
h_{B}\in V(\mathcal{H})
.
Then, the second condition is that distances are preserved until
D
:

d(h_{A},h_{B})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}d(g_{A},g_{B})&\text{if $d(g_{A},g_{B})\leqslant D$}\,,\\
>D&\text{otherwise}\,.\end{array}\right.

(7)

We write
\mathcal{G}\!\cong^{D}\!\!\mathcal{H}
, and we say that the graphs
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
are
D
-isomorphic, if there exists a perfect classical strategy for the
D
-distance game, and similarly
\cong^{D}_{q}
and
\cong_{ns}^{D}
with perfect quantum and non-signalling strategies.
These notations will make sense with regard to
Example
3.1
​​

because this game generalizes the isomorphism game.
Note that:

\dots\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathcal{G}\cong^{D=2}_{s}\mathcal{H}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathcal{G}\cong^{D=1}_{s}\mathcal{H}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathcal{G}\cong^{D=0}_{s}\mathcal{H}\,,

for any strategy type
s
such as classical, quantum, non-signalling, or any other type.
Note that we do not need to assume that
|V(\mathcal{G})|=|V(\mathcal{H})|
since it is a consequence of the setting, see equation (
8
) below.
Three cases are noticeable:

Example 3.1
.

1.

The case
D=0
corresponds to the Graph Bisynchronous Game
[
37
]
, where we require that same vertices
g_{A}=g_{B}
are mapped to same vertices
h_{A}=h_{B}
, and that different vertices are mapped to different vertices.
In particular, if we consider the graphs
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{K}_{M}
and
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{K}_{N}
, the case
D=0
exactly corresponds to the
N
-coloring game of
\mathcal{K}_{M}
.

2.

The case
D=1
corresponds precisely to the Graph Isomorphism Game introduced in
Section
1
​​

, where the relations
\{=,\sim,\not\simeq\}
are preserved. Hence the vertex distance game is a generalization of the graph isomorphism game.

3.

The case
D=\diam(\mathcal{H})
corresponds to:

d(h_{A},h_{B})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}d(g_{A},g_{B})&\text{if $d(g_{A},g_{B})\leqslant\diam(\mathcal{H})$,}\\
\infty&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Note that it requires that
\mathcal{H}
admits at least two connected components if the diameter of
\mathcal{G}
is larger than the one of
\mathcal{H}
.
We will particularly be interested in this third case in what follows.

Remark 3.2
.

There is another way to define the vertex distance game.
From a given graph
\mathcal{G}
, construct
\mathcal{G}_{t}
the graph with the same vertex set as
\mathcal{G}
,
by putting an edge between two vertices in
\mathcal{G}_{t}
if the distance in
\mathcal{G}
is exactly
t
.
Then, observe that winning the
D
-distance game is equivalent to winning the graph isomorphism games of
(\mathcal{G}_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t})
for all
t\leqslant D
.

We will need the following lemma that generalizes
[
3
, Lemma 4.1]
:

Lemma 3.3
.

If
\mathtt{P}\in\mathcal{N\!S}
is a perfect non-signalling strategy for the
D
-distance game of
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H})
, then for all
g\in V(\mathcal{G})
and
h\in V(\mathcal{H})
:

(i)

\sum_{h\in V(\mathcal{H})}\mathtt{P}(h,h\,|\,g,g)=1=\sum_{g\in V(\mathcal{G})}\mathtt{P}(h,h\,|\,g,g)
,

(ii)

\mathtt{P}(h,h\,|\,g,g)=\mathtt{P}(g,h\,|\,h,g)=\mathtt{P}(h,g\,|\,g,h)=\mathtt{P}(g,g\,|\,h,h)
.

Proof.

The first equality of item
(i)
follows from the first condition of the game in equation (
6
), which states that both outputs have to be in
V(\mathcal{H})
if both inputs are in
V(\mathcal{G})
, combined with the condition in equation (
7
) with distance
0
stating that equality is preserved. The second equality is similarly shown.
As for item
(ii)
, if we denote
V=V(\mathcal{G})\cup V(\mathcal{H})
, the winning conditions of the game together with the non-signalling condition give rise to:

\mathtt{P}(h,h\,|\,g,g)\,=\,\sum_{y\in V}\mathtt{P}(y,h\,|\,g,g)\,=\,\sum_{y\in V}\mathtt{P}(y,h\,|\,h,g)\,=\,\mathtt{P}(g,h\,|\,h,g)\,.

We obtain the other equalities with a similar method.
∎

In particular, by combining items
(i)
and
(ii)
, we obtain that the vertex sets have the same cardinality:

\displaystyle\begin{split}|V(\mathcal{G})|\,=\,\sum_{g\in V(\mathcal{G})}1&\,=\,\sum_{g\in V(\mathcal{G})}\sum_{h\in V(\mathcal{H})}\mathtt{P}(h,h\,|\,g,g)\\
&\,=\,\sum_{h\in V(\mathcal{H})}\sum_{g\in V(\mathcal{G})}\mathtt{P}(g,g\,|\,h,h)\,=\,\sum_{h\in V(\mathcal{H})}1\,=\,|V(\mathcal{H})|\,,\end{split}

(8)

which was not obvious at first glance.

3.2
Characterization of Perfect Classical and Quantum Strategies

Surprisingly, as shown in the following proposition, the case
D=1
may be extended to any
D\geqslant 1
when strategies are deterministic, classical, or quantum. On the contrary, we will see in
Subsection
3.4
​​

that it is not the case for non-signalling strategies.

Proposition 3.4

(Characterization)
.

For any
D\geqslant 1
, perfect deterministic/classical/quantum strategies coincide for the graph isomorphism game and the
D
-distance game:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\cong\mathcal{H}\quad\quad

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad\quad\forall D\geqslant 1,\quad\mathcal{G}\cong^{D}\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad\quad\exists D\geqslant 1,\quad\mathcal{G}\cong^{D}\mathcal{H}\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\cong_{q}\mathcal{H}\quad\quad

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad\quad\forall D\geqslant 1,\quad\mathcal{G}\cong^{D}_{q}\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad\quad\exists D\geqslant 1,\quad\mathcal{G}\cong^{D}_{q}\mathcal{H}\,.

Proof.

Let
D\geqslant 1
.
On the one hand, as the isomorphism game consists in preserving distances
\{0,1,>\!1\}
, we see that any perfect strategy for the
D
-distance game is also perfect for the isomorphism game, whether it is deterministic, classical, quantum or non-signalling.
On the other hand:

(a)

Perfect deterministic strategies for the isomorphism game preserve the distances. Indeed, if
D^{\prime}
is the distance separating some vertices
g_{A}
and
g_{B}
in
\mathcal{G}
, then there is a path
(g_{0},g_{1},\dots,g_{D^{\prime}})
in
\mathcal{G}
, where
g_{0}=g_{A}
,
g_{d}=g_{B}
and
g_{i}\sim g_{i+1}
for all
i
. It follows that:

\displaystyle d(g_{A},g_{B})

\displaystyle\,=\,d(g_{0},g_{1})+\dots+d(g_{D-1},g_{D^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\,=\,d(h_{0},h_{1})+\dots+d(h_{{D^{\prime}}-1},h_{D^{\prime}})\,\geqslant\,d(h_{0},h_{D^{\prime}})\,,

where
h_{i}
is the image of
g_{i}
under the bijection
\varphi:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{H}
induced by the deterministic strategy, and where the last inequality holds by the triangular inequality. We note that
h_{0}
,
h_{D}
are the respective images of
g_{A}
,
g_{B}
.
Assume by contradiction that the last inequality is strict. Then there is a path in
\mathcal{H}
connecting
h_{0}
to
h_{D^{\prime}}
of length
D^{\prime\prime}<{D^{\prime}}
. Applying
\varphi^{-1}
, we obtain a path in
\mathcal{G}
connecting
g_{A}
to
g_{B}
of length
D^{\prime\prime}<{D^{\prime}}
, which contradicts the fact that
{D^{\prime}}
is the distance between
g_{A}
and
g_{B}
. Thus
d(g_{A},g_{B})=d(h_{0},h_{D^{\prime}})
and we have the desired result.

(b)

A classical strategy is a convex combination of deterministic strategies:

\mathtt{P}(h_{A},h_{B}\,|\,g_{A},g_{B})\,=\,\sum_{i}p_{i}\,\mathtt{P}^{\text{det}}_{i}(h_{A},h_{B}\,|\,g_{A},g_{B})\,,

with
\sum_{i}p_{i}=1
and the sum index is finite. It means that we apply the strategy
\mathtt{P}^{\text{det}}_{i}
with probability
p_{i}
. If
\mathtt{P}
is a perfect classical strategy, then it wins with probability one, and therefore each
\mathtt{P}^{\text{det}}_{i}
also has to be perfect. So by item
(a)
, each
\mathtt{P}^{\text{det}}_{i}
preserves the distance, thus
\mathtt{P}
also preserves the distance.

(c)

Let
\mathtt{P}
be a perfect quantum strategy for the isomorphism game. This proof is an easy adaptation of
[
43
, Theorem 1.1]
.
We want to show that distance is preserved,
i.e.
that the event “
d(h_{A},h_{B})\neq d(g_{A},g_{B})
” has zero probability.
Using
[
3
, Theorem 5.14]
, we know that there exists a
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
with a tracial state
\tau
and projections
E_{gh}\in\mathcal{A}
for
g\in V(\mathcal{G})
and
h\in V(\mathcal{H})
such that
u
is a quantum permutation matrix and:

u_{g_{A}h_{A}}u_{g_{B}h_{B}}\,=\,0\quad\quad\text{if \quad$\rel(g_{A},g_{B})\neq\rel(h_{A},h_{B})$}\,,

(9)

where the function
\rel(g_{A},g_{B})
takes value
0
if
g_{A}=g_{B}
, value
1
if
g_{A}\sim g_{B}
, and value
2
if
g_{A}\not\simeq g_{B}
.
Using these notations, the correlation
\mathtt{P}
is then of the form

\mathtt{P}(h_{A},h_{B}\,|\,g_{A},g_{B})=\tau(u_{g_{A}h_{A}}u_{g_{B}h_{B}})\,.

(10)

Now, as the isomorphism game is equivalent to the
1
-distance game, we already know that distances
0
and
1
are preserved. It remains to show the results for distances
\geqslant 2
.
Consider vertices
g_{A},g_{B},h_{A},h_{B}
such that
2\leqslant d(g_{A},g_{B})=t<d(h_{A},h_{B})
. We will show that then
\mathtt{P}(h_{A},h_{B}\,|\,g_{A},g_{B})=0
.
By definition, there exist a path in
\mathcal{G}
with adjacent vertices
g_{1},\dots,g_{t}\in V(\mathcal{G})
going from
g_{1}=g_{A}
to
g_{t}=g_{B}
,
but no such path exists in
\mathcal{H}
from
h_{A}
to
h_{B}
. So for all
h_{1},\dots,h_{t}\in V(\mathcal{H})
such that
h_{1}=h_{A}
and
h_{t}=h_{B}
, we infer there exists at least one index
s\in\{1,\dots,t-1\}
such that
h_{s}\not\sim h_{s+1}
but
g_{s}\sim g_{s+1}
.
By equation (
9
), we deduce that
u_{g_{s}h_{s}}u_{g_{s+1}h_{s+1}}=0
, and therefore:

\displaystyle u_{g_{A}h_{A}}u_{g_{B}h_{B}}

\displaystyle\,=\,u_{g_{A}h_{A}}\cdot\mathbf{I}\cdot...\cdot\mathbf{I}\cdot u_{g_{B}h_{B}}

\displaystyle\,=\,\sum_{h_{2},...,h_{t-1}\in V(\mathcal{H})}u_{g_{A}h_{A}}u_{g_{2}h_{2}}\dots u_{g_{t-1}h_{t-1}}u_{g_{B}h_{B}}\,=\,0\,.

Thus using equation (
10
), we obtain that the event “
d(h_{A},h_{B})\neq d(g_{A},g_{B})
” has probability probability zero, and similarly for the event with the opposite inequality. ∎

A direct consequence of
Proposition
3.4
​​

and the results
[
3
,
30
,
14
,
31
,
33
]
displayed in

​​

is the following lists of characterizations:

[

]
,

[

]
.
Along this subsection, we relax the definitions of fractional isomorphism and of common equitable partition with a parameter

, and the combination of all lemmata leads to the following theorem:

Theorem 3.7

(Characterization of Perfect
\mathcal{N\!S}
-Strategies)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}

be an integer. The following are equivalent:

,

(ii)

,

(iii)

.

Remark 3.8

(Characterization for the Game with Inputs in
\mathcal{G}
)
.

If we consider the similar – yet different –
D

x_{A},x_{B}
are always in

instead of
V
, then a similar proof shows the following statement.
Let
\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}
two graphs with the same number of vertices, and let
D\geqslant 0
be an integer. Then, the following are equivalent:

(i’)

This version of the game admits a perfect strategy
\mathtt{P}\in\mathcal{N\!S}
such that the flipped correlation
\mathtt{P}^{\prime}(g_{A},g_{B}\,|\,h_{A},h_{B}):=\mathtt{P}(h_{A},h_{B}\,|\,g_{A},g_{B})
is also in
\mathcal{N\!S}

.

(ii)

There exists a

.

3.3.1
Generalized Fractional Isomorphism

Let
D\geqslant 0
. Two graphs
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
are said to be
D
-fractionally isomorphic
, denoted
\mathcal{G}\cong_{frac}^{D}\mathcal{H}
, if there exists a bistochastic matrix
u\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
(that is a matrix whose entries are non-negative and whose rows and columns sum to one) such that for all distances
t\leqslant D
we have:

\forall\,g\in V(\mathcal{G}),\,\,\forall\,h\in V(\mathcal{H}),\quad\quad\sum_{h^{\prime}\in C(h,t)}u_{gh^{\prime}}\,=\,\sum_{g^{\prime}\in C(g,t)}u_{g^{\prime}h}\,,

(11)

where
C(g,t)
is the circle of radius
t
in
\mathcal{G}
centered at
g
,
i.e.
the set of neighbors of
g
in
\mathcal{G}
at distance exactly
t
.
Note that in the case
D=1
, we retrieve the usual notion of fractional isomorphism, because the condition in the equation amounts to
\sum_{h^{\prime}\sim h}u_{gh^{\prime}}\,=\,\sum_{g^{\prime}\sim g}u_{g^{\prime}h}
, which is equivalent to saying that the adjacency matrices satisfy
u\,A_{\mathcal{G}}=A_{\mathcal{H}}\,u
. We can rephrase equation (
11
) in terms of a generalization of the adjacency matrix. We call the matrix
A_{\mathcal{G}}^{(D)}
the
D
-adjacency matrix
of a graph
\mathcal{G}
, whose coefficients
a_{ij}
are
1
if the distance between
g_{i}
and
g_{j}
satisfies
d(g_{i},g_{j})=D
, and
0
otherwise. We have the equivalence:

\text{Equation\penalty\ \eqref{eq:definition-of-D-fractionally-isomorphism}}\quad\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\quad u\,A_{\mathcal{G}}^{(t)}=A_{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}\,u\,.

(12)

Note that the
D
-adjacency matrix may be expressed in terms of the (usual) powers
A_{\mathcal{G}}^{t}
of the adjacency matrix. The coefficients of the latter matrix may be interpreted as taking value
1
if and only if there exists a path of length
t
in
\mathcal{G}
joining the corresponding vertices. We see that a coefficient is
1
in the
D
-adjacency matrix
A_{\mathcal{G}}^{(D)}
if and only if there exists a path of length
D
in
\mathcal{G}
joining the corresponding vertices, but no path of length
t<D
.
In other words
A_{\mathcal{G}}^{(t)}
is the adjacency matrix of the graph
\mathcal{G}_{t}
as described in
Remark
3.2
​​

.
We have the following relation:

A_{\mathcal{G}}^{(D)}\,=\,\Big(A_{\mathcal{G}}^{D}\div A_{\mathcal{G}}^{D}\Big)\circledast\Big(\mathbf{1}-A_{\mathcal{G}}^{D-1}\div A_{\mathcal{G}}^{D-1}\Big)\circledast...\circledast\Big(\mathbf{1}-A_{\mathcal{G}}^{0}\div A_{\mathcal{G}}^{0}\Big)\,,

where
\div
and
\circledast
are the element-wise division and multiplication of matrices (
a.k.a.
the Hadamard division and product, or Schur product), and where
\mathbf{1}
is the matrix with all entries
1
of the same size as
A_{\mathcal{G}}
.
Observe that
A_{\mathcal{G}}^{(0)}=\mathbf{I}
the identity matrix, and
A_{\mathcal{G}}^{(1)}=A_{\mathcal{G}}
the adjacency matrix, and
A_{\mathcal{G}}^{(D)}=\mathbf{0}
the zero matrix for all
D>\diam(\mathcal{G})
because the graph
\mathcal{G}
admits no vertices with such a distance
D
.
Note that the
D
-adjacency matrix may be equivalently recursively defined:

A_{\mathcal{G}}^{(D)}\,=\,\Big(A_{\mathcal{G}}^{D}\div A_{\mathcal{G}}^{D}\Big)\circledast\left(\mathbf{1}-\sum_{t=0}^{D-1}A_{\mathcal{G}}^{(t)}\right)\,,

because two vertices of
\mathcal{G}
have distance
D
if and only if there is a path of length
D
joining them and they do not have distance
t\leqslant D-1
.
Note that we will provide in
Subsection
3.4
an example of sequence of graphs
(\mathcal{G}_{D},\mathcal{H}_{D})
such that
\mathcal{G}_{D}\cong_{frac}^{D}\mathcal{H}_{D}
but
\mathcal{G}_{D}\not\cong_{frac}^{(D+1)}\mathcal{H}_{D}
.
Here is a sufficient condition in order to have a
D
-fractional isomorphism:

Lemma 3.9
.

If
\mathcal{G}\cong_{ns}^{D}\mathcal{H}
for some integer
D\geqslant 0
,
then
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
are
D
-fractionally isomorphic:

\mathcal{G}\cong_{ns}^{D}\mathcal{H}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathcal{G}\cong_{frac}^{D}\mathcal{H}\,.

Proof.

This proof generalizes
[
3
, Lemma 4.2]
.
We want to construct a bistochastic matrix
u
such that equation (
11
) holds for all
t\leqslant D
. We will index the elements of the matrix
u
by the vertices of
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
, for instance
u_{gh}
.
As the strategy
\mathtt{P}
is a valid probability distribution, we can define
u_{gh}=\mathtt{P}(h,h\,|\,g,g)\geqslant 0
, and using
Lemma
3.3
​​

(i)
, we have that rows and columns sum to one,
\sum_{h}u_{gh}=1
and
\sum_{g}u_{gh}=1
, so the matrix
u
is bistochastic.
Let us verify the equality of equation (
11
) for an arbitrary integer
t\leqslant D
and vertices
g\in V(\mathcal{G})
and
h\in V(\mathcal{H})
. We have:

\displaystyle\sum_{h^{\prime}\in C(h,t)}u_{gh^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\,=\,\sum_{h^{\prime}\in C(h,t)}\mathtt{P}(h^{\prime},h^{\prime}\,|\,g,g)\,=\,\sum_{h^{\prime}\in C(h,t)}\sum_{g^{\prime}\in V(\mathcal{G})}\mathtt{P}(h^{\prime},h^{\prime}\,|\,g,g^{\prime})\,,

which holds because
\mathtt{P}
is perfect so it satisfies the rule that the outputs need to be equal if and only if the inputs
g
and
g^{\prime}
are equal.
Now, using the non-signalling condition of
\mathtt{P}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N\!S}
on Bob’s marginal, we obtain:

\displaystyle\,=\,\sum_{h^{\prime}\in C(h,t)}\sum_{g^{\prime}\in V(\mathcal{G})}\mathtt{P}(h^{\prime},h\,|\,g,g^{\prime})\,=\,\sum_{h^{\prime}\in C(h,t)}\sum_{g^{\prime}\in C(g,t)}\mathtt{P}(h^{\prime},h\,|\,g,g^{\prime})\,,

where the last equality holds because
\mathtt{P}
is perfect so it satisfies the rule that the the distance
t
is the same for the outputs
h^{\prime},h
and the inputs
g,g^{\prime}
. Then, we swap the two sums and we use that
\mathtt{P}\in\mathcal{N\!S}
and similar arguments as before to derive what follows:

\displaystyle\,=\,\sum_{g^{\prime}\in C(g,t)}\sum_{h^{\prime}\in C(h,t)}\mathtt{P}(h^{\prime},h\,|\,g,g^{\prime})\,=\,\sum_{g^{\prime}\in C(g,t)}\sum_{h^{\prime}\in V(\mathcal{H})}\mathtt{P}(h^{\prime},h\,|\,g,g^{\prime})

\displaystyle\,=\,\sum_{g^{\prime}\in C(g,t)}\sum_{h^{\prime}\in V(\mathcal{H})}\mathtt{P}(h^{\prime},h\,|\,g^{\prime},g^{\prime})\,=\,\sum_{g^{\prime}\in C(g,t)}\mathtt{P}(h,h\,|\,g^{\prime},g^{\prime})\,=\,\sum_{g^{\prime}\in C(g,t)}u_{g^{\prime}h}\,.

Hence, equation (
11
) holds, and the graphs
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
are
D
-fractionally isomorphic.
∎

For
Theorem
3.7
​​

, we also generalize the notion of common equitable partition. Recall that the usual notion of common equitable partition was defined on page
1.3
.

3.3.2
Generalized Common Equitable Partition

Let
D\geqslant 0
.
We say that two graphs
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
admit a
D
-common equitable partition
if they admit respective partitions
\mathscr{C}=(C_{1},\dots,C_{k})
and
\mathscr{D}=(D_{1},\dots,D_{\ell})
with the following common parameters:

\displaystyle k=\ell\,,

\displaystyle\forall i\in\{1,\dots,k\}\quad

\displaystyle|C_{i}|=|D_{i}|=:n_{i}\,,

\displaystyle\forall t\leqslant D,\forall i,j\in\{1,\dots,k\},\forall g\in C_{i},\forall h\in D_{i},\quad

\displaystyle|C_{j}\cap C(g,t)|=|D_{j}\cap C(h,t)|=:c_{ij}^{(t)}\,.

Note that the case
D=1
corresponds exactly to the usual notion of common equitable partition.
Note that
c_{ij}^{(0)}=\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker delta, and that
c_{ij}^{(t)}=0
when
t>\min\{\diam(\mathcal{G}),\diam(\mathcal{H})\}
.
We do not necessarily have
c_{ij}^{(t)}=c_{ji}^{(t)}
, but we always have the following relation:

Lemma 3.10
.

If the graph
\mathcal{G}
admits a
D
-equitable partition with parameters as above,
then:

n_{i}\,c_{ij}^{(t)}=n_{j}\,c_{ji}^{(t)}\,.

Proof.

This proof is a generalization of
[
40
, Section 2.1]
.
Up to reordering the rows and columns of the
t
-adjacency matrix
A_{\mathcal{G}}^{(t)}
, this matrix can be decomposed in blocks
A_{ij}^{(t)}
of size
n_{i}\times n_{j}
such that the rows sum to
c_{ij}^{(t)}
.
By symmetry of the
t
-adjacency matrix, the blocks satisfy
A_{ij}^{(t)\top}=A_{ji}^{(t)}
, so the columns of
A_{ij}^{(t)}
sum to
c_{ji}^{(t)}
.
Now, as the sum of all the elements of
A_{ij}^{(t)}
equals both the sum of its rows and the sum of its columns, we obtain

n_{i}\,c_{ij}^{(t)}=n_{j}\,c_{ji}^{(t)}
,
hence the wanted result.
∎

We prove that
D
-fractional isomorphic is a sufficient condition for the graphs to admit a
D
-common equitable partition:

Lemma 3.11
.

If
\mathcal{G}\cong_{frac}^{D}\mathcal{H}
for some integer
D\geqslant 0
,
then there exists a
D
-common equitable partition of
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H})
.

Proof.

This proof generalizes
[
40
, Theorem 2.2]
.
We use an equivalent characterization of
\cong_{frac}^{D}
as the one given in equation (
12
),
i.e.
there exists a bistochastic matrix
u
such that for all
t\leqslant D
:

A_{\mathcal{G}}^{(t)}\,u=u\,A_{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}\,.

(13)

From this matrix
u
, we define a partition
\mathscr{C}
on
V(\mathcal{G})
based on the following equivalence relation:

g\leftrightarrow g^{\prime}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\begin{matrix}\text{there exists a ``link'' from $g$ to $g^{\prime}$, {i.e.}\penalty\ $\exists n,g_{1},...,g_{n},h_{1},...,h_{n}$ such that:}\\
\text{$g_{1}=g$, \quad\quad$g_{n}=g^{\prime}$,\quad\quad$u_{g_{1}h_{1}}\cdot u_{g_{2}h_{1}}\cdot u_{g_{2}h_{2}}\cdot...\cdot u_{g_{n}h_{n}}>0$\,,}\end{matrix}

and we similarly define a partiton
\mathscr{D}
on
V(\mathcal{H})
.
By construction, up to reordering the rows and columns of
u
, these partitions
\mathscr{C}
and
\mathscr{D}
are in correspondence with a block decomposition
u=U_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus U_{k}
, where each
U_{i}
is an indecomposable
n_{i}\times m_{i}
bistochastic matrix for some
n_{i},m_{i}
. In particular, we have that both partitions
\mathscr{C}
and
\mathscr{D}
have
k
cells and that each cell
C_{i}
and
D_{i}
has respective size
n_{i}
and
m_{i}
.
Using the fact that
u
is bistochastic, we have:

m_{i}\,=\,\sum_{h\in D_{i}}1\,=\,\sum_{h\in D_{i}}\sum_{g\in C_{i}}u_{gh}\,=\,\sum_{g\in C_{i}}\sum_{h\in D_{i}}u_{gh}\,=\,\sum_{g\in C_{i}}1\,=\,n_{i}\,,

hence
|C_{i}|=|D_{i}|=n_{i}
as wanted. It remains to prove that
\mathscr{C}
and
\mathscr{D}
admit common parameters
c_{ij}^{(t)}
. Let
t\leqslant D
and denote
A^{(t)}:=A_{\mathcal{G}}^{(t)}
and
B:=A_{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}
the
t
-adjacency matrices of the graphs
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
. Write
A^{(t)}
with blocks
A_{ij}^{(t)}
of size
n_{i}\times n_{j}
induced by the partition
\mathscr{C}
, and similarly for
B^{(t)}
with blocks
B_{ij}^{(t)}
of the same size. From equation (
13
), we deduce the following relations:

\forall i,j\in[k],\quad\quad A_{ij}^{(t)}\,U_{j}\,=\,U_{i}\,B_{ij}^{(t)}\,.

(14)

As well, after swapping
i
and
j
, we have
A_{ji}^{(t)}\,U_{i}=U_{j}\,B_{ji}^{(t)}
, and taking the transpose we obtain:

\forall i,j\in[k],\quad\quad U_{i}^{\top}\,A_{ij}^{(t)}\,=\,B_{ij}^{(t)}\,U_{j}^{\top}\,.

(15)

Let
v_{ij}^{(t)}:=A_{ij}^{(t)}\,\mathbf{1}
be the vector such that each coordinate corresponds to a
g\in C_{i}
and represents the number of
g^{\prime}\in C_{j}
at distance exactly
t
of
g
, where
\mathbf{1}
denotes the vector of appropriate size with all entries
1
. In order to have a “
D
-equitable” partition, we want that all the coordinates of the vector
v_{ij}^{(t)}
have the same value
c_{ij}^{(t)}\in\mathbb{R}
,
i.e.
that
v_{ij}^{(t)}=c_{ij}^{(t)}\,\mathbf{1}
.
Similarly, we define the vector
w_{ij}^{(t)}:=B_{ij}^{(t)}\,\mathbf{1}
, and in order to have a
D
-“common” equitable partition, we want to prove that:

v_{ij}^{(t)}=w_{ij}^{(t)}=c_{ij}^{(t)}\,\mathbf{1}\,.

(16)

Using the fact that
U_{j}
is bistochastic and then equation (
14
), we have:

v_{ij}^{(t)}\,:=\,A_{ij}^{(t)}\,\mathbf{1}\,=\,A_{ij}^{(t)}\,U_{j}\,\mathbf{1}\,=\,U_{i}\,B_{ij}^{(t)}\,\mathbf{1}\,=\,U_{i}\,w_{ij}^{(t)}\,,

and similarly, using the fact that
U_{j}^{\top}
is bistochastic and then equation (
15
), we get:

Lastly, we prove that the generalized notion of common equitable partition is sufficient in order to have a perfect non-signalling strategy for the

Lemma 3.12
.

Let

If
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H})
admits a
D
-common equitable partition,
then
\mathcal{G}\cong_{ns}^{D}\mathcal{H}

Proof.

This proof generalizes
[
3
, Lemma 4.4]

Denote
\big(k,[n_{1},\dots,n_{k}],[c_{ij}^{(t)}]\big)
, for
t\in\{0,\dots,D\}
, the parameters of the given
D
-common equitable partition of
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H})
, and consider
\overline{c_{ij}}:=n_{j}-\sum_{t=0}^{D}c_{ij}^{(t)}
the number of elements in
C_{j}
that are at distance
>D
of a fixed element in
C_{i}
.
We consider the following correlation:

\mathtt{P}(h_{A},h_{B}\,|\,g_{A},g_{B})\,=\,\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}1/n_{i}c_{ij}^{(t)}&\text{if $d(h_{A},h_{B})=t=d(g_{A},g_{B})$ and $t\leqslant D$ and $(\star)$\,,}\\
1/n_{i}\overline{c_{ij}}&\text{if $d(h_{A},h_{B})>D$ and $d(g_{A},g_{B})>D$ and $(\star)$\,,}\\
0&\text{otherwise\,,}\end{array}\right.

where the condition

Moreover, define:

\mathtt{P}(h,h^{\prime}\,|\,g,g^{\prime})=\mathtt{P}(g,h^{\prime}\,|\,h,g^{\prime})=\mathtt{P}(h,g^{\prime}\,|\,g,h^{\prime})=\mathtt{P}(g,g^{\prime}\,|\,h,h^{\prime})\,,

for all
g,g^{\prime}\in V(\mathcal{G})
and all

, and set
\mathtt{P}=0
in all the cases not yet accounted for.
By construction, this is a perfect strategy for the
D
-distance game because the probability of losing is zero, so it remains to show that
\mathtt{P}\in\mathcal{N\!S}
.
First of all, the non-negativity condition is satisfied by the construction of
\mathtt{P}
. Let
V=V(\mathcal{G})\cup V(\mathcal{H})
.
Let us check the marginal condition in the case where both inputs
x_{A},x_{B}
are in

: fix

and

and

On the one hand, if

\sum_{y\in V}\mathtt{P}(h_{A},y\,|\,g_{A},g_{B})\,=\,\sum_{h_{B}\in D_{j}\cap C(h_{A},t)}\frac{1}{n_{i}\,c_{ij}^{(t)}}\,=\,\frac{|D_{j}\cap C(h_{A},t)|}{n_{i}\,c_{ij}^{(t)}}\,=\,\frac{1}{n_{i}}\,,

because
c_{ij}^{(t)}=|D_{j}\cap C(h_{A},t)|
.
On the other hand, if

\sum_{y\in V}\mathtt{P}(h_{A},y\,|\,g_{A},g_{B})\,=\,\sum_{h_{B}\in D_{j}\cap B(h_{A},D)^{c}}\frac{1}{n_{i}\,\overline{c_{ij}}}\,=\,\frac{|D_{j}\cap B(h_{A},D)^{c}|}{n_{i}\,\overline{c_{ij}}}\,=\,\frac{1}{n_{i}}\,,

because
\overline{c_{ij}}=|D_{j}\cap B(h_{A},D)^{c}|
,
where
B(h_{A},D)^{c}
is the complement of the ball centered at
h_{A}
of radius
D
,
i.e.
the element of
V(\mathcal{H})
that are at distance
>D
of
h_{A}
. In both equations, the result does not depend on
g_{B}
, hence Alice’s marginal
\mathtt{P}(h_{A}\,|\,g_{A})
is well-defined. Similarly, Bob’s marginal
\mathtt{P}(h_{B}\,|\,g_{B})=1/n_{j}
is also well-defined using the relation

from

3.10
​​

.
A similar proof works in all the other choices of

, and

, and

are “common” for
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
, and we have
\mathtt{P}(g_{A}\,|\,h_{A})=1/n_{i}
and
\mathtt{P}(g_{B}\,|\,h_{B})=1/n_{j}
.
Finally, for any

and

, the normalization condition is verified by summing the marginals:

\sum_{y_{A},y_{B}\in V}\mathtt{P}(y_{A},y_{B}\,|\,x_{A},x_{B})\,=\,\sum_{y_{A}\in D_{i}}\mathtt{P}(y_{A}\,|\,x_{A})\,=\,\sum_{y_{A}\in D_{i}}\frac{1}{n_{i}}\,=\,\frac{|D_{i}|}{n_{i}}\,=\,1\,,

and similarly in the case

.
We therefore obtain the wanted result.

-distance game is perfect for the isomorphism game. In the theorem, we gave sufficient conditions on graphs so that all perfect strategies for the isomorphism game collapse CC. Hence, with the same conditions, we have that the result also holds for the
D
-distance game for any
D\geqslant 1
. Recall that the proof of this theorem was based on tools from graph automorphism theory and graph transitivity notions.

Theorem 3.27

(Collapse of CC)
.

Let
D\geqslant 1
.
Let
\mathcal{G}\cong_{ns}^{D}\mathcal{H}
such that
2\leqslant\diam(\mathcal{G})

and such that
\mathcal{H}
is not connected:
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\sqcup\mathcal{H}_{2}
, where each of
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
may possibly be decomposed in several connected components.
Let
\mathscr{C}=\{C_{1},\dots,C_{k}\}
and
\mathscr{D}=\{D_{1},\dots,D_{k}\}
form a
1
-common equitable partition for
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
such that condition
(H′)
holds.
Assume moreover that
\mathcal{H}
is strongly transitive and
d
-regular, and that the players share randomness.
Then
every
perfect non-signalling strategy for the
D
-distance game of
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H})
collapses communication complexity. ∎

Conclusion

Our investigations contribute to the line of research that “the quantum world is the best possible one”: indeed, we just proved for many instances that, going beyond the quantum case, some non-signalling correlations collapse CC – and are hence physically unfeasible according to the present intuition of nature
[
17
,
9
,
11
,
4
]
.
The importance of our results is emphasized by considering the many known impossibility results
[
4
,
34
,
45
]
.
However, notice that there is still a gap to be filled in between: there remain some non-signalling correlations for which we need to know if they are feasible according to the principle of CC.
See a recent overview of what remains to be shown in
[
8
, Figure 2]
.

In addition to the study of CC, our work also adds value to the study of non-signalling correlations themselves – not only for promoting the quantum theory, but also as a field of research as such. Indeed, we remark that although there is no difference between our new vertex
D
-distance game and the graph isomorphism game in terms of perfect classical or quantum strategies, the two games do not admit the same perfect non-signalling strategies. More precisely, we proved that it is even possible to build a sequence of graphs
(\mathcal{G}_{D},\mathcal{H}_{D})
that are
D
-isomorphic but not
(D+1)
-isomorphic in the sense of the vertex distance game. Therefore, non-signalling strategies yield a finer distinction of this non-local game than quantum and classical strategies.
