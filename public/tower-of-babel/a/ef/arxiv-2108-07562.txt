Class of meromorphic functions partially shared values with their differences or shifts
Molla Basir Ahamed

Abstract
. Two meromorphic functions f f and g g are said to share a value s ∈ ℂ ∪ { ∞ } s\in\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\} C ​ M CM ( I ​ M ) (IM) provided that f ⁡ ( z ) − s f(z)-s and g ⁡ ( z ) − s g(z)-s have the same set of zeros counting multiplicities (ignoring multiplicities). We say that a meromorphic function f f share s ∈ ℂ s\in\mathbb{C} partially C ​ M CM with a meromorphic function g g if E ⁡ ( s , f ) ⊆ E ⁡ ( s , g ) E(s,f)\subseteq E(s,g) . It is easy to see that the condition “partially shared values C ​ M CM ” is more general than the condition “shared value C ​ M CM ”. With the idea of partially shared values, in this paper, we prove some uniqueness results between non-constant meromorphic functions and their shifts or generalized differences. We exhibit some examples to show that the result of Charak et al. [ 9 ] is not true for k = 2 k=2 or k = 3 k=3 . We find some gaps in proof of the result of Lin et al. [ 25 ] , and we not only correct them but also generalize their result in a more convenient way. A number of examples have been exhibited to validate certain claim of the main results of this paper and also to show that some of the conditions are sharp. In the end, we have posed some open questions for further investigation of the main result of the paper.

3.
Key lemmas

In this section, we present some necessary lemmas which will play key role to prove the main results. For a non-zero complex number
c
and for integers
n\geqslant 1
, we define the higher order difference operators
\Delta_{c}^{n}f:=\Delta_{c}^{n-1}(\Delta_{c}f)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

[
35
]

Let
c\in\mathbb{C}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
f
be a meromorphic function
of finite order. Then any small periodic function
a\equiv a(z)\in\mathcal{S}(f)

\displaystyle m\left(r,\frac{\Delta_{c}^{n}f}{f(z)-a(z)}\right)=S(r,f),

where the exponential set associated with
S(r,f)
is of at most finite logarithmic
measure.

Lemma 3.2
.

[
30
,
32
]

If
\mathcal{R}(f)
is rational in
f
and has small meromorphic
coefficients, then

\displaystyle T(r,\mathcal{R}(f))=\deg_{f}(\mathcal{R})T(r,f)+S(r,f).

Lemma 3.3
.

[
36
]

Suppose that
h
is a non-constant entire function such that
f(z)=e^{h(z)}
, then
\rho_{2}(f)=\rho(h)
.

In
[
14
,
18
]
, the first difference analogue
of the lemma on the logarithmic derivative was proved and for the hyper-order
\rho_{2}(f)<1
, the following is the extension, see
[
20
]
.

Lemma 3.4
.

[
20
]

Let
f
be a non-constant finite order meromorphic function and
c\in\mathbb{C}
. If
c
is of finite order, then

\displaystyle m\left(r,\frac{f(z+c)}{f(z)}\right)=O\left(\frac{\log r}{r}T(r,f)\right)

for all
r
outside of a set
E
with zero logarithmic density. If the hyper
order
\rho_{2}(f)<1
, then for each
\epsilon>0
, we have

\displaystyle m\left(r,\frac{f(z+c)}{f(z)}\right)=0\left(\frac{T(r,f)}{r^{1-\rho_{2}-\epsilon}}\right)

for all
r
outside of a set of finite logarithmic measure.

Lemma 3.5
.

[
34
]

Let
f
be a non-constant meromorphic function,
s_{j}\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}(f)
,
j=1,2,...,q,\;

(q\geqslant 3)
. Then for any positive real number
\epsilon
, we have

\displaystyle(q-2-\epsilon)T(r,f)\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{q}\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{f-s_{j}}\right),\;r\not\in E,

where
E\subset[0,\infty)
and satisfies
\displaystyle\int_{E}d\log\log r<\infty
.

We now prove the following lemma, a similar proof of this lemma can also be found in
[
2
]
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
f
be a non-constant meromorphic function such that

\displaystyle\overline{E}(s_{j},f)\subseteq\overline{E}(s_{j},c_{1}f(z+c)+c_{0}f(z)),\;\;j=1,2,

where
s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathcal{S}(f)
,
c,\;c_{0},\;c_{1}(\neq 0)\in\mathbb{C^{*}}
, then
f

is not a rational.

Proof.

We wish to prove this lemma by the method of contradiction. Let
f
be a rational function. Then
f(z)={P(z)}/{Q(z)}
where
P
and
Q
are two polynomials relatively prime to each other and
P(z)Q(z)\not\equiv 0
. Hence

(3.1)

\displaystyle E(0,P)\cap E(0,Q)=\phi

It is easy to see that

\displaystyle c_{1}f(z+c)+c_{0}f(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{1}\frac{P(z+c)}{Q(z+c)}+c_{0}\frac{P(z)}{Q(z)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{c_{1}P(z+c)Q(z)+c_{0}P(z)Q(z+c)}{Q(z+c)Q(z)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{P_{1}(z)}{Q_{1}(z)},\text{(say)}

where
P_{1}
and
Q_{1}
are two relatively prime polynomials and
P_{1}(z)Q_{1}(z)\not\equiv 0
.

Since
\overline{E}(s_{1},f)\subseteq\overline{E}(s_{1},c_{1}f(z+c)+c_{0}f(z))
and
f
is a rational function, there must exists a polynomial
h(z)
such that

\displaystyle c_{1}f(z+c)+c_{0}f(z)-s_{1}=(f-s_{1})h(z),

which can be re-written as

(3.2)

\displaystyle\frac{c_{1}P(z+c)Q(z)+c_{0}P(z)Q(z+c)}{Q(z+c)Q(z)}-s_{1}\equiv\left(\frac{P(z)}{Q(z)}-s_{1}\right)h(z).

We now discuss the following cases:

Case 1.
Let
P(z)
is non-constant.

Then by the

Fundamental Theorem of Algebra
, there exists
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
such
that
P(z_{0})=0
. Then it follows from (
3.2
) that

(3.3)

\displaystyle c_{1}\frac{P(z_{0}+c)}{Q(z_{0}+c)}\equiv(1-h(z_{0}))s_{1}^{0},

where
s_{1}^{0}=s_{1}(z_{0})
.

Subcase 1.1.
Let
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
be such that
s_{1}(z_{0})=0
.

Then from (
3.3
), it is easy to see that
P(z_{0}+c)=0
. Then we can deduce
from (
3.1
) that
P(z_{0}+mc)=0
for all positive integer
m
. However,
this is impossible, and hence we conclude that the polynomial
P(z)
is a
non-zero constant.

Subcase 1.2.
Let
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
be such that
s_{1}(z_{0})\neq 0
.

Then from (
3.3
), we obtain that

\displaystyle P(z_{0}+c)\equiv\frac{s_{1}^{0}}{c_{1}}(1-h(z_{0}))Q(z_{0}+c).

Next proceeding
exactly same way as done in above, we obtain

(3.4)

\displaystyle P(z_{0}+mc)\equiv\frac{s_{1}^{0}}{c_{1}}(1-h(z_{0}))Q(z_{0}+mc).

In view of (
3.3
)
and (
3.4
), a simple computation shows that

\displaystyle\frac{P(z_{0}+c)}{Q(z_{0}+c)}=\frac{P(z_{0}+mc)}{Q(z_{0}+mc)}\;\;\text{for all
positive integers $m$,}

which contradicts the fact that
E(0,P)\cap E(0,Q)=\phi
.

Therefore, we see that
f(z)
takes the form
f(z)={\eta}/{Q(z)}
, where
P(z)=\eta=\text{constant}\;(\neq 0).

Case 2.
Let
Q(z)
be non-zero constant.

Now

(3.5)

\displaystyle c_{1}f(z+c)+c_{0}f(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{c_{1}\eta\;Q(z)+c_{0}\eta\;Q(z+c)}{Q(z+c)Q(z)}.

Since
E(s_{2},f)=E(s_{2},c_{1}f(z+c)+c_{0}f(z))
then
there exists a polynomial
h_{1}(z)
such that
c_{1}f(z+c)+c_{0}f(z)-s_{2}=(f-s_{2})h_{1}(z),
which can be written as

(3.6)

\displaystyle c_{1}\;Q(z)+c_{0}Q(z+c)\equiv\frac{\eta-s_{2}Q(z)}{d}h_{1}(z)Q(z+c).

Since
Q(z)
and hence
Q(z+c)
be non-constant polynomials, hence by the
Fundamental Theorem of Algebra
, there exist
z_{0}
and
z_{1}
such that
Q(z_{0})=0=Q(z_{1}+c)
.

Subcase 2.1.
When
Q(z_{0})=0
, then from (
3.6
), we
see that
h_{1}(z_{0})=-{c_{0}}/{\eta}
, which is absurd.

Subcase 2.2.
When
Q(z_{1}+c)=0
, then from (
3.6
),
we get
Q(z_{1})=0
, which is not possible.

Thus we conclude that
Q(z)
is a non-zero constant, say
\eta_{2}
. Thus we have

f(z)={\eta}/{\eta_{2}}
, a constant, which is a contradiction. This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.7
.

[
20
]

Let
T:[0,+\infty]\rightarrow[0,+\infty]
be a non-decreasing continuous
function, and let
s\in(0,+\infty)
. If the hyper-order of
T
is strictly
less than one, i.e.,

\displaystyle\limsup_{r\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{\log^{+}\log^{+}T(r)}{\log r}=\rho_{2}<1,

then

\displaystyle T(r+s)=T(r)+o\left(\frac{T(r)}{r^{1-\rho_{2}-\epsilon}}\right),

where
\epsilon>0
and
r\rightarrow\infty
, outside of a set of finite logarithmic
measure.
