Comparing the cost of violating causal assumptions in Bell experiments: locality, free choice and arrow-of-time
Pawel Blasiak 1,2 and Christoph Gallus 3

Abstract
The causal modelling of Bell experiments relies on three fundamental assumptions: locality, freedom of choice, and arrow-of-time. It turns out that nature violates Bell inequalities, which entails the failure of at least one of those assumptions. Since rejecting any of them – even partially – proves to be enough to explain the observed correlations, it is natural to ask about the cost in each case. This paper follows up on the results in PNAS 118 e2020569118 (2021), showing the equivalence between the locality and free choice assumptions, adding to the picture retro-causal models explaining the observed correlations. Here, we consider more challenging causal scenarios which allow only single-arrow type violations of a given assumption. The figure of merit chosen for the comparison of the causal cost is defined as the minimal frequency of violation of the respective assumption required for a simulation of the observed experimental statistics.

In this paper, we take an impartial position with a view to analysing the consequences of a violation of each of the three causal assumptions. Within the causal Bayesian model framework, we first discuss a baseline Bell scenario which obeys all three assumptions. It allows us to see a violation of a given assumption as a departure from the baseline case by adding certain arrows. Clearly, the reasoning can be reversed, i.e., admitting the presence of a given arrow in a causal account of the observed statistics indicates a violation of the corresponding assumption. 2
Causal modelling of Bell experiment

Let us consider the usual Bell-type scenario with two parties, called Alice and Bob, performing experiments on two separated systems
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. We will assume that the
measurement settings
are labelled by
x\in\mathcal{X}
for Alice and
y\in\mathcal{Y}
for Bob, and their
measurement outcomes
are labelled respectively
a\in\mathcal{A}
and
b\in\mathcal{B}
.
For the sake of generality, we do not restrict the cardinality of the sets
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{B}
,
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
(just assume that they are finite to avoid technical issues). A Bell experiment consists of a series of trials in which Alice and Bob each choose a setting and make a measurement registering the outcomes. Then, after many repetitions, they compare their results, arriving at statistics given by the joint distribution
P_{abxy}
. It is conventionally split into the conditional probability
P_{ab|xy}
of Alice and Bob observing outcomes
a
and
b
given the choice of settings
x
and
y
, and the distribution of settings
P_{xy}
chosen in the experiment. This follows the idea of understanding a measurement as probing an existing system by questions
x
,
y
, which are chosen by the experimenters Alice and Bob (or some devices on their behalf), to which the system responds with the respective answers
a
and
b
. In the following, we will call conditional probabilities
P_{ab|xy}
the
behaviour
, while
\big\{P_{ab|xy}\,,P_{xy}\big\}
will be called the
(experimental) statistics
.
Note that without assuming anything about the causal structure underlying the experiment, any experimental statistics is admissible as long it is well-normalised, i.e. we have
\sum_{a,b}\,P_{ab|xy}\,=\,1
for each
(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, and
\sum_{x,y}\,P_{xy}\,=\,1
.

Quantum theory gives a method for calculating the behaviour
P_{ab|xy}
based on the formalism of Hilbert spaces. Strikingly, it raises a challenge to our most cherished intuitions/assumptions about the workings of the world. The first assumption concerns
locality
. A Bell experiment can be arranged in a way that Alice and Bob are space-like separated, which suggests the lack of any influence between Alice’s and Bob’s sides. The second assumption relates to
arrow-of-time
. It stipulates that the causes must precede the effects, and hence nothing can affect the past, rendering
retrocausal
explanations implausible. The third assumption is about
freedom of choice
(or
measurement independence
). It amounts to the belief that experimental settings can be chosen independently of anything related to the investigated systems, and this extends to the choices made by random generators (on Alice’s and Bob’s behalf). Those three assumptions underly the famous Bell inequalities, whose violation by quantum mechanical correlations (observed in numerous experiments
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
]
) indicates the necessity of revising some fundamental concepts in our approach to modelling physical reality.

In the following, we take the causal point of view to modelling the Bell experiment. We will use causal DAGs (
directed acyclic graphs
) to encode the various assumptions as well as their violations (in terms of additional arrows) to discuss possible ways of explaining the observed statistics. The tool of choice in this analysis is the framework of Bayesian causal models
[

[

4
Causal fraction as the cost of violation of a given assumption

Suppose that the observed experimental statistics obeys
Assumption
1
(for all distributions of settings
P_{xy}
), which means that it can be explained by causal models
(
NL
)
,
(
R
)
or
(

)

Perhaps it is enough for the extra arrow to be effective only occasionally to explain the observations. In other words, in the repeated experiment, one may refrain from using it on some fraction of trials and still be able to account for the observed statistics. So to say, the modeller can perform better by being frugal with extra resources/arrows and still achieve the goal of simulating the experiment.

This idea can be formalised by decomposing the observed experimental statistics
\big\{P_{ab|xy}\,,P_{xy}\big\}

\displaystyle(1-q)\,P^{\,*}_{ab|xy}\,+\,q\,P^{\,\#}_{ab|xy}\,,

NF
)
. Most importantly, this allows for a fair comparison of the causal assumptions in the Bell scenario based on the measure built on the very same notion of the fractional measure.

It may be a surprise that despite conceptual differences (and interpretational implications) between those assumptions, there is no difference in their fractional causal cost. This is shown by the following general structural result about the weight of causal explanations in the Bell scenario.

Theorem 1
.

For any behaviour
P_{ab|xy}
satisfying
Assumption
1
(for all distributions
P_{xy}
), the fractional causal measures in Eq. (
12
) defined for non-local, retrocausal and non-free explanations are all the
same
, i.e. we have

\displaystyle\mu^{\scriptscriptstyle NL}\ =\ \mu^{\scriptscriptstyle R}\ =\ \mu^{\scriptscriptstyle NF}\,,

(13)

where the labels NL, R and NF stand respectively for the non-local arrow
x\rightarrow b
in
(NL)
, retrocausal arrow
x\rightarrow\lambda
in
(R)
, and non-free arrow
\lambda\rightarrow x
in
(NF)
.

Proof.

For the sake of generality, let the label # be a placeholder for whichever case
(
NL
)
,
(
R
)
or
(
NF
)
. Let the behaviour
P_{ab|xy}
be fixed and choose some distribution of settings
P_{xy}
which admit decomposition in Eqs. (
10
)-(
11
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\big\{P^{\,*}_{ab|xy}\,,P^{\,*}_{xy}\big\}\quad\text{is a baseline statistics {\color[rgb]{0,0,1}($*$)}, and}

(14)

\displaystyle\big\{P^{\,\#}_{ab|xy}\,,P^{\,\#}_{xy}\big\}\quad\text{is a statistics compatible with \#\,.}

(15)

Note that the latter statistics being compatible with # has to satisfy
Assumption
1
, which entails by
Lemma
1
that it is also compatible with any other
\#^{\,\prime}
. Thus we can also write

\displaystyle P_{ab|xy}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-q)\,P^{\,*}_{ab|xy}\,+\,q\,P^{\,\#^{\,\prime}}_{ab|xy}\,,

(16)

\displaystyle P_{xy}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-q)\,P^{\,*}_{xy}\,+\,q\,P^{\,\#^{\,\prime}}_{xy}\,,

(17)

where
\big\{P^{\,\#^{\,\prime}}_{ab|xy}\,,P^{\,\#^{\,\prime}}_{xy}\big\}
is compatible with
\#^{\,\prime}
. This establishes for the statistics
\big\{P_{ab|xy}\,,P_{xy}\big\}
a one-to-one correspondence between the decompositions in Eqs. (
10
)-(
11
) and Eqs. (
16
)-(
17
), each pertaining to the respective case
\#
and
\#^{\,\prime}
. Since the weight
q
is the same, we infer that

\displaystyle\min_{decomp.\atop(\ref{decomp-1})\text{-}(\ref{decomp-2})}\ q

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{decomp.\atop(\ref{decomp-1'})\text{-}(\ref{decomp-2'})}\ q\,.

(18)

Since it holds for every
P_{xy}
, we conclude that
\mu^{\scriptscriptstyle\#}=\mu^{\scriptscriptstyle\#^{\,\prime}}
. This ends the proof.
∎

5
Discussion

The research in the paper concerns the analysis of assumptions in Bell’s theorem. The adopted framework is based on the causal Bayesian models by Pearl and others
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
, which subsumes Bell’s original ideas
[
1
,
17
]
. It has the advantage of articulating the underlying assumptions and their possible violations in causal language with a simple graphical representation. Our main focus in the paper is the question of whether the formalism in itself can adjudicate between different causal mechanisms/arrows leading to the violation of the respective assumptions, without bias regarding their philosophical/interpretative consequences. We show that the cost of relaxing either assumption —
locality, arrow-of-time, or free choice
— is always the
same
, see
Theorem
1
. This result is formally established via the so-called
causal fraction measure
, which computes the least frequency of violation of a given assumption required to simulate the observed behaviour. In each case, the measure is defined in a similar manner in order to provide a unified basis for a fair comparison. This completes the results in
[
37
]
with the discussion of retrocausal models.

In this work we insist on a single-arrow type of violation of a given assumption, see
(
NL
)
,
(
R
)
or
(
NF
)
. That is, we consider the most modest scenarios that are enough to explain the observed statistics. We are guided here by the paradigm in graphical causal models, saying that the fewer arrows, the less expressive the power of the model (or, in other words, if a given causal graph is able to explain the statistics, then a model with more arrows will be able to explain it too). Therefore, the fewer arrows (or simpler DAG), the better, as far as causal explanations go. From this point of view, adding only a single extra arrow to the baseline scenario
(
{*}
)
makes explaining the desired statistics more challenging and interesting at the same time. This is what we were able to achieve in this paper (cf.
Lemma
1
).

The results presented here are derived under
Assumption
1
which is the consequence of single-arrow type violations in
(
NL
)
,
(
R
)
or
(
NF
)
. Thus, we admit a broader range of experimental statistics with one-sided signalling only. Let us remark that even in the case of non-signalling correlations in both directions (like in quantum mechanics) this may add interpretational flexibility. This might be useful, for example, when the experimental design needs the asymmetric causal structure explaining the results (e.g., due to the specific temporal arrangement of events).

Note that we take a viewpoint where causal concepts are treated as fundamental in physical theories. Furthermore, we adopt the approach to causality as laid out by Pearl and others
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
. Of course, one may dismiss the importance of causality as such, seeing it as an illusory or emergent phenomenon
[
28
]
. Another approach is considering the possibility of a different causal framework on the fundamental level. See
[
29
,
30
,
31
,
32
,
33
,
34
]
for a few research directions along those lines. See also a discussion of the relationship between context independence and freedom of choice in
[
40
,
32
,
41
]
.

We remark that the models constructed in this work are necessarily fine-tuned (cf.
Lemma
1
). It is in line with the result in
[
42
]
, where it was shown that the violation of Bell inequalities together with non-signalling conditions entails fine-tuning. Furthermore, we are not concerned with the overhead costs of simulation, i.e. the cardinality of the hidden variables is unrestricted. Although this is a standard assumption in the causal inference framework, it does not have to be the case
[
43
]
.

Finally, we note that the causal fraction has been considered in the literature to measure non-locality in the Bell scenario and explicitly calculated in a few cases
[
44
,
45
,
46
,
47
,
48
,
49
,
50
]
. See
[
37
]
for a compilation of those results. As for the arrow-of-time and free choice assumption, all quantitative measures designed in the literature are based on completely different notions. See
[
51
]
for retrocausality and
[
52
,
53
,
54
,
55
,
56
]
for violation of free choice. Therefore, comparing those measures does not serve the purpose of adjudicating on the cost of violation of the respective assumptions in the Bell experiment. The present paper gives an impartial treatment of all three causal assumptions underlying Bell’s theorem based on the unifying concept of causal fractions.

Appendix: Proof of
Lemma
1

From the
Assumption
1
we have that

\displaystyle P_{a|x}\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle(\ref{assump})}}{{=}}\ P_{a|xy}\ =\ \sum_{b}\,P_{ab|xy}

(19)

is well-defined given the behaviour
P_{ab|xy}
. Furthermore, the distribution of settings factorises

\displaystyle P_{xy}\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle(\ref{assump})}}{{=}}\ P_{x}\,P_{y}\,,

(20)

where
P_{x}={\sum}_{y}\,P_{xy}
and
P_{y}={\sum}_{x}\,P_{xy}
are marginals of
P_{xy}
.

It will be useful to observe that the distribution
P_{a|x}
in Eq. (
19
) can be decomposed as a convex combination of deterministic strategies
\delta_{a=f(x)}
. That is, we have

\displaystyle P_{a|x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{f\in\mathfrak{F}}\,\delta_{a=f(x)}\,P_{f}\,,

(21)

where
\mathfrak{F}:=\big\{\,f:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}\,\big\}\equiv\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{X}}
is the set of functions from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathcal{A}
, and
P_{f}
is a well-normalized distribution
{\sum}_{f}\,P_{f}=1
.

For the sake of simplicity, let us assume that all probabilities are non-zero, so as to comfortably use Bayes’ rule without bothering with divisions by zero (those cases can be treated separately in a straightforward manner).

For the proof, in each particular causal model
(
NL
)
,
(
R
)
and
(
NF
)
, we will need to explicitly define the hidden variable space
\Lambda
, give the corresponding set of parameters
\widetilde{P}
, and show that it reproduces the desired experimental statistics
\big\{P_{ab|xy}\,,P_{xy}\big\}
via the respective Eqs. (
3
)-(
4
), Eqs. (
5
)-(
6
) and Eqs. (
7
)-(
8
).

Proof of case
(NL)
.

Let the hidden variable space be as follows

\displaystyle\Lambda\ :=\ \mathfrak{F}\,,\quad\text{i.e. we have}\quad\lambda\ \equiv\ f\,,

(22)

and define the parameters to be

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{a|x\lambda}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\delta_{a=f(x)}\,,

(23)

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{b|xy\lambda}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{P_{f(x)b|xy}}{P_{f(x)|x}}\,,

(24)

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle{P}_{f}\,,

(25)

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{x}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle P_{x}\,,

(26)

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{y}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle P_{y}\,.

(27)

Then we calculate

\displaystyle{\sum}_{\lambda}\,\widetilde{P}_{a|x\lambda}\,\widetilde{P}_{b|xy\lambda}\,\widetilde{P}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\sum}_{f}\ \delta_{a=f(x)}\ \frac{P_{f(x)b|xy}}{P_{f(x)|x}}\ P_{f}

(28)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\sum}_{f}\ \delta_{a=f(x)}\ \frac{P_{ab|xy}}{P_{a|x}}\ \ P_{f}\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle(\ref{Pa|x-det})}}{{=}}\ \ \frac{P_{ab|xy}}{P_{a|x}}\ P_{a|x}\ \ =\ \ P_{ab|xy}\,,

(29)

which proves Eq. (
3
), and

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{x}\,\widetilde{P}_{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P_{x}\,P_{y}\ \ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle(\ref{Pxy})}}{{=}}\ \ P_{xy}\,,

(30)

which proves Eq. (
4
).
∎

Proof of case
(R)
.

Here, we take the hidden variable space in the form

\displaystyle\Lambda\ :=\ \mathfrak{F}\times\mathcal{X}\,,\quad\text{i.e. we have}\quad\lambda\ \equiv\ (f,\tilde{x})\,,

(31)

and define the parameters in the following way

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{a|x\lambda}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\delta_{a=f(x)}\,,

(32)

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{b|y\lambda}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{P_{f(\tilde{x})b|\tilde{x}y}}{P_{f(\tilde{x})|\tilde{x}}}\,,

(33)

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{\lambda|x}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle{P}_{f}\ \delta_{x=\tilde{x}}\,,

(34)

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{x}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle P_{x}\,,

(35)

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{y}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle P_{y}\,.

(36)

This gives

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda}\,\widetilde{P}_{a|x\lambda}\,\widetilde{P}_{b|y\lambda}\,\widetilde{P}_{\lambda|x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{f,\tilde{x}}\ \delta_{a=f(x)}\ \frac{P_{f(\tilde{x})b|\tilde{x}y}}{P_{f(\tilde{x})|\tilde{x}}}\ P_{f}\ \delta_{x=\tilde{x}}

(37)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{f}\ \delta_{a=f(x)}\ \frac{P_{f({x})b|{x}y}}{P_{f({x})|{x}}}\ P_{f}

(38)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{f}\ \delta_{a=f(x)}\ \frac{P_{ab|{x}y}}{P_{a|{x}}}\ P_{f}\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle(\ref{Pa|x-det})}}{{=}}\ \ \frac{P_{ab|xy}}{P_{a|x}}\ P_{a|x}\ \ =\ \ P_{ab|xy}\,,

(39)

which proves Eq. (
5
), and

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{x}\,\widetilde{P}_{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P_{x}\,P_{y}\ \ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle(\ref{Pxy})}}{{=}}\ \ P_{xy}\,,

(40)

which proves Eq. (
6
).
∎

Proof of case
(NF)
.

In this case, let the hidden variable space be as follows

\displaystyle\Lambda\ :=\ \mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{X}\,,\quad\text{i.e. we have}\quad\lambda\ \equiv\ (\tilde{a},\tilde{x})\,,

(41)

and put the parameters in the following form

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{a|x\lambda}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\delta_{a=\tilde{a}}\,,

(42)

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{b|y\lambda}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{P_{\tilde{a}b|\tilde{x}y}}{P_{\tilde{a}|\tilde{x}}}\,,

(43)

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle{P}_{\tilde{a}\tilde{x}}\,,

(44)

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{x|\lambda}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\delta_{x=\tilde{x}}\,,

(45)

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{y}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle P_{y}\,.

(46)

Note that the distribution
P_{ax}
obtains either via
P_{ax}\ =\ P_{a|x}\,P_{x}
or
P_{ax}\ =\ {\sum}_{b,y}\,P_{ab|xy}\,P_{xy}
.

From those definitions by the Bayes’ rule, it follows that

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{\lambda|x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\widetilde{P}_{x|\lambda}\ \widetilde{P}_{\lambda}}{\widetilde{P}_{x}}\ \ =\ \ \frac{\delta_{x=\tilde{x}}\ P_{\tilde{a}\tilde{x}}}{P_{x}}\ \ =\ \ \frac{\delta_{x=\tilde{x}}\ P_{\tilde{a}\tilde{x}}}{P_{\tilde{x}}}\ \ =\ \ \delta_{x=\tilde{x}}\ P_{\tilde{a}|\tilde{x}}\,,

(47)

where in the denominator we have used the fact that

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{x}\ =\ {\sum}_{\lambda}\,\widetilde{P}_{x|\lambda}\,\widetilde{P}_{\lambda}\ =\ \sum_{\tilde{a},\tilde{x}}\,\delta_{x=\tilde{x}}\,P_{\tilde{a}\tilde{x}}\ =\ \sum_{\tilde{a}}\,P_{\tilde{a}x}\ =\ P_{x}\,.

(48)

Then we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda}\,\widetilde{P}_{a|x\lambda}\,\widetilde{P}_{b|y\lambda}\,\widetilde{P}_{\lambda|x}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle(\ref{Plambda|x})}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\sum_{\tilde{a},\tilde{x}}\ \delta_{a=\tilde{a}}\ \frac{P_{\tilde{a}b|\tilde{x}y}}{P_{\tilde{a}|\tilde{x}}}\ \delta_{x=\tilde{x}}\ P_{\tilde{a}|\tilde{x}}\ \ =\ \ P_{ab|xy}\,,

(49)

which proves Eq. (
7
), and

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda}\,\widetilde{P}_{x|\lambda}\,\widetilde{P}_{\lambda}\,\widetilde{P}_{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\tilde{a},\tilde{x}}\,P_{\tilde{a}\tilde{x}}\,\delta_{x=\tilde{x}}\,{P}_{y}\ \ =\ \ \sum_{\tilde{a}}\,P_{\tilde{a}{x}}\,{P}_{y}\ \ =\ \ {P}_{x}\,{P}_{y}\ \ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle(\ref{Pxy})}}{{=}}\ \ P_{xy}\,,

(50)

which proves Eq. (
8
).
∎
