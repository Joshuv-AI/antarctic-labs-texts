Kernel interpolation generalizes poorly
Yicheng Li, Haobo Zhang, Qian Lin

Abstract
One of the most interesting problems in the recent renaissance of the studies in kernel regression might be whether kernel interpolation can generalize well, since it may help us understand the ‘benign overfitting phenomenon’ reported in the literature on deep networks. In this paper, under mild conditions, we show that for any ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 , the generalization error of kernel interpolation is lower bounded by Ω ⁡ ( n − ε ) \Omega(n^{-\varepsilon}) . In other words, the kernel interpolation generalizes poorly for a large class of kernels. As a direct corollary, we can show that overfitted wide neural networks defined on the sphere generalize poorly.

1
Introduction

The ‘benign overfitting phenomenon’ that over-parametrized neural networks can interpolate noisy data but also generalize well is widely observed in the literature on neural networks
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
.
This observation challenges the traditional ‘bias-variance trade-off’ doctrine in statistical learning theory
[
4
]
where models interpolating noisy data are supposed to generalize poorly.
Since
Jacot et al. 2018
illustrated that when the width of neural network tends to infinity, the dynamics of the gradient flow when training the neural network can be approximated by the gradient flow of a kernel regression with respect to the time-invariant neural tangent kernel, we have experienced a renaissance of studies of kernel regression.
For example, since a number of works

[
6
,
7
]

have shown that a wide neural network can overfit any data, whether neural kernel interpolation can generalize might be one of the most important questions towards a theoretical explanation of the ‘benign overfitting phenomenon’.

Suppose that we have observed the given samples
(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{n},y_{n})
and perform regression on
\mathcal{H}
,
a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) associated with some kernel
k
.
Traditional results

[
8
,
9
]

in regularized kernel regression, namely
kernel ridge regression
, showed that

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{y_{i}-f(x_{i})\}^{2}+\lambda\norm{f}_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\right],

(1)

where
\norm{\cdot}_{\mathcal{H}}
is the corresponding norm, is minimax optimal if
\lambda
is chosen properly according to the sample size;
subsequent works

[
10
,
11
]

have also extended the optimality results to more general settings.
In the limiting case where
\lambda\to 0
, we obtain the kernel minimum-norm interpolation
[
12
, Section 12.5]

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{\mathrm{inter}}=\hat{f}_{0}=\argmin_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\norm{f}_{\mathcal{H}}\quad\mbox{subject to}\quad f(x_{i})=y_{i}\quad(i=1,\dots,n).

(2)

However, there has been little discussion about the generalization performance of
\hat{f}_{\mathrm{inter}}
.

Recently, many papers have considered the generalization ability of kernel regression in the high dimensional regime
where the dimension
d
of the inputs grows with the sample size
n
.
In this regime, the so-called ‘benign overfitting phenomenon’ has been reported and generalization error bounds are provided
under certain additional assumptions.

Liang and Rakhlin 2020
proved that kernel interpolation can generalize when
n\asymp d
and the data have a low-dimensional structure;

Liang et al. 2020
showed similar results when
n=d^{l}
and
l>1
is not an integer.
Considering a square-integrable regression function,

Ghorbani et al. 2020
and its follow-up work
Mei and Montanari 2022

showed that kernel methods can fit at most a degree-
l
polynomial if
n=d^{l}
for
l>1
;

Ghosh et al. 2021
proved similar results for the gradient flow with early-stopping.

On the other hand, under the traditional fixed dimension setting,
the inconsistency of interpolation has been widely observed and believed
[
18
]
.

Recently, several works claimed the inconsistency of kernel interpolation.

Rakhlin and Zhai 2018
showed that the kernel interpolation
\hat{f}_{\mathrm{inter}}
with Laplace kernel is not consistent in odd dimensions;

Buchholz 2022
then extended the result to kernels associated with the Sobolev space
H^{s}
for
d/2<s<3d/4

and all (fixed) dimensions;
assuming that data are located in uniformly spaced grids,

Beaglehole et al. 2022
established inconsistency results for a class of shift-invariant periodic kernels with mild spectral assumptions.
Since the neural tangent kernels are not included in all the aforementioned situations,
it is unclear if these works can help us understand the ‘benign overfitting phenomenon’ in neural network.

In this paper, we show that under mild assumptions,
the kernel interpolation
\hat{f}_{\mathrm{inter}}
generalizes poorly in fixed dimensions.
To be precise, we prove that
for any
\varepsilon>0
, the generalization error

\displaystyle\norm{\hat{f}_{\mathrm{inter}} - f^*}^{2}_{L^{2}}=\Omega(n^{-\varepsilon})

holds with high probability, where
f^{*}
is the underlying regression function.
In subsection
2.2
, we further illustrate that our class of kernels not only include the aforementioned kernels but also include the neural tangent kernels.
In particular, we rigorously show that overfitted wide neural networks on spheres generalize poorly.

Throughout this paper, we use
L^{p}(\mathcal{X},\differential\mu)
to represent the Lebesgue
L^{p}
spaces,
where the corresponding norm is denoted by
\norm{\cdot}_{L^{p}}
.
For a function
f
on
\mathcal{X}
, we also define the sup-norm
\norm{f}_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\absolutevalue{f(x)}
,
which should be distinguished from the
L^{\infty}
-norm.
We use the asymptotic notations
O(\cdot)
,
o(\cdot)
, and
\Omega(\cdot)
.
We also denote
a_{n}\asymp b_{n}
iff
a_{n}=O(b_{n})
and
a_{n}=\Omega(b_{n})
.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Reproducing kernel Hilbert space

Let a compact set
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the input space.
We fix a continuous positive definite kernel
k
over
\mathcal{X}

and denote by
\mathcal{H}
the separable reproducing kernel Hilbert space associated with
k
.
Let
\rho
be a probability measure on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}
and
\mu
be the marginal probability measure of
\rho
on
\mathcal{X}
.
We denote by
L^{2}=L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\differential\mu)
the Lebesgue space for short.
Then, it is known
[
9
,
22
]
that
the natural embedding
S_{\mu}:\mathcal{H}\to L^{2}
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator with the Hilbert-Schmidt
norm
\norm{S_\mu}_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}\leq\kappa^{2}=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}k(x,x)
.
Moreover, denoting by
S_{\mu}^{*}:L^{2}\to\mathcal{H}
the adjoint operator of
S_{\mu}
,
the operator
T=S_{\mu}S_{\mu}^{*}:L^{2}\to L^{2}

is an integral operator given by

\displaystyle(Tf)(x)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}k(x,y)f(y)\differential\mu(y).

(3)

Then, it is well known
[
8
,
22
]
that
T
is self-adjoint, positive and trace-class (thus compact)
with trace norm
\norm{T}_{1}\leq\kappa^{2}
.
Thus, the spectral theorem of compact self-adjoint operators and Mercer’s theorem

[
22
]

yield that

\displaystyle T=\sum_{i\in N}\lambda_{i}\left\langle{\cdot,e_{i}}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}e_{i},\quad\quad k(x,y)=\sum_{i\in N}\lambda_{i}e_{i}(x)e_{i}(y),

(4)

where
N\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is an index set,

\left\{\lambda_{i}\right\}_{i\in N}
is the set of positive eigenvalues of
T
in descending order,

e_{i}
is the corresponding eigenfunction which is chosen to be continuous, and the convergence of the kernel expansion is absolute and uniform.
In addition,
\left\{e_{i}\right\}_{i\in N}
forms an orthonormal basis
of
\overline{\ran S_{\mu}}\subseteq L^{2}

and
\left\{\lambda_{i}^{1/2}e_{i}\right\}_{i\in N}
forms an orthonormal basis of

\overline{\ran S_{\mu}^{*}}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
.

The eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
characterize the span of the reproducing kernel Hilbert space and the interplay between
\mathcal{H}
and
\mu
.
We will assume
N=\mathbb{N}
and impose the following polynomial eigenvalue decay condition:

Assumption 1

(Eigenvalue decay)
.

There are some
\beta>1
and constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that

\displaystyle c_{1}i^{-\beta}\leq\lambda_{i}\leq c_{2}i^{-\beta}\quad(i=1,2,\dots),

(5)

where
\lambda_{i}
is the eigenvalue of
T
defined in (
4
).

This assumption is quite standard in the literature

[
8
,
10
,
23
,
11
,
24
]
.
We also remark that the upper bound in (
5
) enables us to provide upper bounds for the convergence rate,
while the lower bound is essential for the minimax lower bound
[
8
]
and also for our lower bound.
This assumption is satisfied by commonly considered kernels including kernels associated with Sobolev RKHS (see Example
2.1
)
and neural tangent kernels on spheres
[
25
]
.

2.2
The embedding index
\alpha_{0}
of an RKHS

The decomposition (
4
) also allows us to introduce the following interpolation spaces

[
22
,
11
,
24
]
.
For
s\geq 0
, we define the fractional power
T^{s}:L^{2}\to L^{2}

by

\displaystyle T^{s}(f)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in N}\lambda_{i}^{s}\left\langle{f,e_{i}}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}e_{i}

(6)

and define the interpolation space
[\mathcal{H}]^{s}
by

\displaystyle[\mathcal{H}]^{s}=\ran T^{s/2}=\left\{\sum_{i\in N}a_{i}\lambda_{i}^{s/2}e_{i}~\Big|~\sum_{i\in N}a_{i}^{2}<\infty\right\}\subseteq L^{2},

(7)

which is equipped with the norm

\displaystyle\norm{\sum_{i \in N} a_i \lambda_i^{s/2} e_i}_{[\mathcal{H}]^{s}}=\left(\sum_{i\in N}a_{i}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

(8)

One may easily verify that
[\mathcal{H}]^{s}
is also a separable Hilbert space
and
\left\{\lambda_{i}^{s/2}e_{i}\right\}_{i\in N}
forms an orthonormal basis of it.
It is clear that we have
[\mathcal{H}]^{0}=\overline{\ran S_{\mu}}\subseteq L^{2}
and

[\mathcal{H}]^{1}=\overline{\ran S_{\mu}^{*}}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
.
There are also compact inclusions
[\mathcal{H}]^{s_{1}}\hookrightarrow[\mathcal{H}]^{s_{2}}
for
s_{1}>s_{2}\geq 0
.

Furthermore, we say
\mathcal{H}
has an embedding property of order
\alpha\in(0,1]

if
[\mathcal{H}]^{\alpha}
can be continuously embedded into
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X},\differential\mu)
, that is,
the operator norm

\norm{[\mathcal{H}]^\alpha\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X},\mu)}=M_{\alpha}<\infty.

(9)

Fischer and Steinwart 2020
shows that

\displaystyle\norm{[\mathcal{H}]^\alpha\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X},\mu)}=\norm{k^\alpha_{\mu}}_{L^{\infty}},

(10)

where
\norm{k^\alpha_{\mu}}_{L^{\infty}}
is the
L^{\infty}
-norm of the
\alpha
-power of
k
given by

\displaystyle\norm{k^\alpha_{\mu}}_{L^{\infty}}^{2}=\operatorname*{ess~sup}_{x\in\mathcal{X},~\mu}\sum_{i\in N}\lambda_{i}^{\alpha}e_{i}(x)^{2}.

(11)

Since we have assumed that
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}k(x,x)\leq\kappa^{2}
, we know that (
9
) holds for
\alpha=1
, i.e., any
\mathcal{H}
associated with a bounded kernel function has the embedding property of order
1
.
By the inclusion relation of interpolation spaces, it is clear that if
\mathcal{H}
has the embedding property of order
\alpha
, then it has the embedding
properties of order
\alpha^{\prime}
for any
\alpha^{\prime}\geq\alpha
. Thus, we may introduce the following definition

Definition 2.1
.

The embedding index
\alpha_{0}
of an RKHS
\mathcal{H}
is defined by

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}=\inf\left\{\alpha:\norm{[\mathcal{H}]^\alpha\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X},\mu)}<\infty\right\}.

(12)

It is shown in
Steinwart et al. 2009
and
Fischer and Steinwart 2020
that

\alpha_{0}\geq\beta
and the equality holds if the eigenfunctions are uniformly bounded ( i.e.

\sup_{i\in N}\norm{e_i}_{L^{\infty}}<\infty
).
Throughout this paper, we make the following assumption.

Assumption 2

(Embedding index)
.

The embedding index
\alpha_{0}=1/\beta
,
where
\beta
is the eigenvalue decay in (
5
).

Remark 2.2
.

Many RKHSs satisfy the embedding index condition. We list several examples below:

Example 2.1

(Sobolev spaces)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded domain with smooth boundary
and suppose
\mu
is induced by the Lebesgue measure.
The Sobolev space
H^{s}(\mathcal{X})
(for a definition, see e.g.,
Adams and Fournier 2003
) is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space if
s>d/2
.
Moreover, it can be shown
[
11
]
that

H^{s}(\mathcal{X})
satisfies Assumption
1
with
\beta=2s/d
,
and the interpolation space
[H^{s}(\mathcal{X})]^{\alpha}\cong H^{\alpha s}(\mathcal{X})
.
By the Sobolev embedding theorem
[
26
]
,
if
\alpha s>d/2
, i.e.,
\alpha>d/(2s)=\beta^{-1}
,
we have

\displaystyle H^{s\alpha}\hookrightarrow C^{0,\theta}(\mathcal{X})\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X},\differential\mu),

(13)

where
C^{0,\theta}(\mathcal{X})
is the Hölder space and
0<\theta<2\alpha s/d
.
It shows that Assumption
2
is satisfied.

Example 2.2

(Translation invariant periodic kernel)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}=[-\pi,\pi)^{d}
and
\mu
be the uniform distribution on
\mathcal{X}
.
Suppose that the kernel satisfies
k(x,y)=f(x-y\bmod[-\pi,\pi)^{d})
, where we denote
a\bmod[-\pi,\pi)=\left\{(a+\pi)\bmod 2\pi\right\}-\pi\in[-\pi,\pi)
and
x-y\bmod[-\pi,\pi)^{d}
is understood to be element-wise.
Then, it is shown in
Beaglehole et al. 2022
that
the Fourier basis
\phi_{m}(x)=e^{i\left\langle{m,x}\right\rangle},~m\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
are eigenfunctions of
T
.
Since
\{\phi_{m}\}
are uniformly bounded, Assumption
2
is satisfied.

Example 2.3

(Dot-product kernel on spheres)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the unit sphere
and assume that
\mu
is the uniform distribution on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
Let
k
be a dot-product kernel, that is,
k(x,y)=f(\left\langle{x,y}\right\rangle)
for some function
f
.
Then, it is well known
[
27
]
that
k
can be decomposed using the spherical harmonics
Y_{n,l}
by

\displaystyle k(x,y)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mu_{n}\sum_{l=1}^{a_{n}}Y_{n,l}(x)Y_{n,l}(y),

(14)

where
a_{n}
is the dimension of order-
n
homogeneous harmonic polynomials and
\mu_{n}
is an eigenvalue of
T

with multiplicity
a_{n}
.
Assuming the polynomial eigenvalue decay
\mu_{n}\asymp n^{-(d-1)\beta}
,
it is shown in
Zhang et al. 2023
that the embedding index
\alpha_{0}=1/\beta

and thus Assumption
2
holds.

2.3
Kernel ridge regression

Suppose that we have observed
n
i.i.d. samples
(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{n},y_{n})
from
\rho
.
We denote by
X=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
the sample inputs and

Y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}
.
Thanks to the representer theorem (see e.g.,
Andreas Christmann 2008
), we could solve the optimization problem (
1
) explicitly:

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{K}(x,X)\left\{\mathbb{K}(X,X)+n\lambda\right\}^{-1}Y=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{K}(x,X)(K+\lambda)^{-1}Y

(15)

where
\mathbb{K}(x,X)=\left(k(x,x_{1}),\dots,k(x,x_{n})\right)
,
\mathbb{K}(X,X)=\big(k(x_{i},x_{j})\big)_{n\times n}

and
K=\mathbb{K}(X,X)/n
is the normalized kernel matrix.
Similarly, we also have an explicit formula for the kernel interpolation
[
12
, Section 12.5]
:

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{\mathrm{inter}}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{K}(x,X)\mathbb{K}(X,X)^{-1}Y=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{K}(x,X)K^{-1}Y.

(16)

Here we notice that (
16
) is well-defined since we have assumed that
k
is positive definite and thus
K
is invertible.
For conditions and examples of positive definite kernels, we refer to e.g.,
Wendland 2004
.

In the last two decades, the theories of integral operators and empirical processes are utilized to understand the generalization ability of
\hat{f}_{\lambda}(x)
from various aspects (e.g.,
Caponnetto and De Vito 2007
,
Fischer and Steinwart 2020
,
Li et al. 2023
).
One of the essential steps in these works is an integral operator interpretation of the formula (
15
).
More precisely, they introduced the sampling operator
K_{x}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{H}
given by
K_{x}y=yk(x,\cdot)
, their adjoint operator

K_{x}^{*}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
given by
K_{x}^{*}f=f(x)

and the sample covariance operator
T_{X}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
given by

\displaystyle T_{X}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{x_{i}}K_{x_{i}}^{*}.

(17)

With these operators, there is an alternative expression of
\hat{f}_{\lambda}(x)
:

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{\lambda}=(T_{X}+\lambda)^{-1}g_{Z},

(18)

where
g_{Z}=n^{-1}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{x_{i}}y_{i})\in\mathcal{H}
.

3
Theoretical Results

3.1
Main theorem

To state the main theorem, we need two more mild conditions on the kernel
k
and the noise
y-f_{\rho}^{*}(x)
.

Assumption 3
.

The kernel
k
is Hölder-continuous,
that is, there exists some
s\in(0,1]
and
L>0
such that

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{k(x_1,x_2) - k(y_1,y_2)}\leq L\norm{(x_1,x_2) - (y_1,y_2)}_{\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}}^{s},\quad\forall x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathcal{X}.

(19)

Assumption 4

(Noise)
.

The conditional variance of the noise satisfies that

\displaystyle E_{(x,y)\sim\rho}\left[\left\{y-f^{*}_{\rho}(x)\right\}^{2}\mid x\right]\geq\sigma^{2}>0,\quad\mu\text{-a.e. }x\in\mathcal{X}.

(20)

Assumption
3
requires that
k
is Hölder continuous and Assumption
4
simply requires that the noise is non-vanishing.
It is clear that both these assumptions are quite mild.
For example, Assumption
3
holds for the Laplacian kernels, RBF kernels, neural tangent kernels, etc.;
Assumption
4
holds for the usual regression models
y=f^{*}(x)+\epsilon
where
\epsilon
is an independent non-zero noise.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose that the Assumptions
1
,
2
,
3
and
4
hold.
There is an absolute constant
c>0
such that
for any
\varepsilon>0
and any
\delta\in(0,1)
,
when
n
is sufficiently large (depending on
\varepsilon
and
\delta
), one has that

\displaystyle E\left(\norm{\hat{f}_{\mathrm{inter}} - f^*_\rho}_{L^{2}}^{2}\mid X\right)\geq c\sigma^{2}n^{-\varepsilon}

holds with probability at least
1-\delta
with respect to the random samples.

Remark 3.2
.

In Theorem
3.1
, since the sufficiently large
n
depends on
\varepsilon
and
\delta
,
the statement can not imply the inconsistency of kernel interpolation.
Nevertheless, this theorem actually suggests that for a fairly large class of positive definite kernels, one can not expect that the kernel interpolation can generalize well.

3.2
Discussion

The ‘benign overfitting phenomenon’ might be one of the most puzzling observations reported in the literature on neural networks.
Since
Jacot et al. 2018
introduced the neural tangent kernel, it has become a common strategy to study the wide neural network through analyzing the kernel regression. Thus, the ‘benign overfitting phenomenon’ raised a natural question that
whether the kernel interpolations, especially the neural tangent kernel interpolation, can generalize well?

There are few works
[
19
,
20
,
21
]
showed that the kernel interpolation generalized poorly in various settings.
For example,
Rakhlin and Zhai 2018
showed that when the dimension
d
is a fixed odd number,
the Laplace kernel interpolation generalizes poorly for random observation with Rademacher noise;

Buchholz 2022
considered the regression with respect to the kernel associated with the Sobolev space
H^{s}
and showed that the corresponding kernel interpolation generalizes poorly if
d/2<s<3d/4
;
Beaglehole et al. 2022
further showed that the kernel interpolation with respect to the periodical translation invariant kernel generalizes poorly for the grid data.
However, to the best of our knowledge, the poor generalization ability of the NTK interpolation has been only obtained for one dimensional grid data in
Lai et al. 2023
.

Theorem
3.1
states that for any kernel
k
satisfying that the embedding index
\alpha_{0}
of the RKHS associated to
k

equals to
1/\beta
, the inverse of the eigen-decay rate, the corresponding kernel interpolation generalized poorly.
Note that the listed examples in Section
2.2
clearly contains all the kernels appeared in
Buchholz 2022
,
Beaglehole et al. 2022
,
Rakhlin and Zhai 2018
. Thus, it is clear that our result is more general.
Moreover, our result also applies to the neural tangent kernel (NTK) on spheres
[
25
,
31
]
,
which is not covered in either of the previous works.
Consequently, we could assert that the neural tangent kernel interpolation and the overfitted wide neural network generalized poorly,
a statement contradicts to the widely observed ‘benign overfitting phenomenon’.
Thus, our result suggests that we may need to explain the ‘benign overfitting phenomenon’ from other perspective.

3.3
Proof sketch

It is clear that the generalization error of kernel ridge regression (including the interpolation case of
\lambda=0
) is lower bounded by
the variance term
V(\lambda)
, i.e.,

\displaystyle E\left(\norm{\hat{f}_{\lambda} - f^*_\rho}^{2}_{L^{2}}\mid X\right)\geq V(\lambda)=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n^{2}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{K}(x,X)(K+\lambda)^{-2}\mathbb{K}(X,x)\differential\mu(x).

Let us rewrite
V(\lambda)
in the following operator form:

\displaystyle V(\lambda)=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\norm{(T_{X}+\lambda)^{-1}k(x,\cdot)}_{L^{2},n}^{2}\differential\mu(x),

(21)

where
\norm{f}_{L^{2},n}^{2}=n^{-1}\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i})^{2}\}
.
We then invoke an important observation appeared in
Li et al. 2023
which claims that
for
\lambda=\Omega(n^{-1/2})
,

\displaystyle V(\lambda)\approx\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\norm{(T+\lambda)^{-1}k(x,\cdot)}_{L^{2},n}^{2}\differential\mu(x)\approx\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\norm{(T+\lambda)^{-1}k(x,\cdot)}_{L^{2}}^{2}\differential\mu(x)\asymp\frac{\sigma^{2}\lambda^{-1/\beta}}{n}.

(22)

However, the requirement that
\lambda=\Omega(n^{-1/2})
is far from enough to show the nearly constant lower bound.
As one of our major technical contributions in this paper,
we sharpen the estimation by the embedding index assumption and prove the approximation
(
22
) actually holds for
\lambda\asymp n^{-\beta+\epsilon}
for any
\epsilon>0
.
Combining with the elementary observation
V(0)\geq V(\lambda)
, we get the desired claim.
We refer to the supplementary material for a complete proof.

4
Application to neural networks

Suppose that we have observed
n
i.i.d. samples
(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{n},y_{n})
from
\rho
.
For simplicity, we further assume that the marginal distribution
\mu
of
\rho
is the uniform distribution on the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
We use a two-layer neural network of width
m
to perform the regression on
(x_{i},y_{i})
’s.
More precisely,
we consider the following two-layer neural network:

\displaystyle f(x;\theta)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{m}}\sum_{r=1}^{m}a_{r}\sigma\left(w_{r}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}x\right),

(23)

where
\sigma(x)=\max(x,0)
is the ReLU activation and
\theta=(w_{1},\dots,w_{m},a_{1},\dots,a_{m})
are the parameters.
With
\theta
randomly initialized as
\theta(0)
, we consider the training process given by the gradient flow

\dot{\theta}(t)=-\partial L/\partial\theta
,
where the loss function is

\displaystyle L(\theta)=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{y_{i}-f(x_{i};\theta)\right\}^{2}.

(24)

Then, when the network is over-parametrized, namely
m
is large, the theory of lazy training (see e.g.,
Lai et al. 2023
,
Lee et al. 2019
) shows that the trained network

\hat{f}^{\mathrm{NN}}_{t}(x)=f(x;\theta(t))
can be approximated by a kernel gradient method with respect the following neural tangent kernel

A.1
More preliminaries

In the following, we will denote
h_{x}=k(x,\cdot)
for
x\in\mathcal{X}
.
For a function

, we use
f[X]=(f(x_{1}),\dots,f(x_{n}))^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}
.
To simplify the notation, we will use

to represent constants, which may change in the context;
we will also abbreviate

\displaystyle T_{\lambda}=T+\lambda,\quad T_{X\lambda}=T_{X}+\lambda.

Following
Li et al. 2023
, we consider the sample subspace

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{n}=\spn\left\{k(x_{1},\cdot),\dots,k(x_{n},\cdot)\right\}\subset\mathcal{H}.

(27)

Then, it is easy to verify that
\ran T_{X}=\mathcal{H}_{n}
and
K
is the representation matrix of
T_{X}

under the natural basis
\left\{k(x_{1},\cdot),\dots,k(x_{n},\cdot)\right\}
.
Consequently, for any continuous function
\varphi
we have

\displaystyle\varphi(T_{X})\mathbb{K}(X,\cdot)=\varphi(K)\mathbb{K}(X,\cdot).

(28)

where the left-hand side is understood by applying the operator elementwise.
Since from the property of reproducing kernel Hilbert space we have
\left\langle{h_{x},f}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=f(x)
for
f\in\mathcal{H}
,
taking inner product elementwise between (
28
) and
f
, we obtain

\displaystyle(\varphi(T_{X})f)[X]=\varphi(K)f[X].

(29)

Moreover, for
f,g\in\mathcal{H}
, we define empirical semi-inner product

\displaystyle\left\langle{f,g}\right\rangle_{L^{2},n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i})g(x_{i})=\frac{1}{n}f[X]^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}g[X],

(30)

and denote by
\norm{\cdot}_{L^{2},n}^{2}
the corresponding empirical semi-norm.
Then,
Li et al. 2023
established the following simple but important connection.

Proposition A.1
.

For
f,g\in\mathcal{H}
, we have

\left\langle{f,g}\right\rangle_{L^{2},n}=\left\langle{T_{X}f,g}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\left\langle{T_{X}^{1/2}f,T_{X}^{1/2}g}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

(31)

Proof.

Notice that
T_{X}f=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i})h_{x_{i}}
, and thus

\displaystyle\left\langle{T_{X}f,g}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i})\left\langle{h_{x_{i}},g}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i})g(x_{i})=\left\langle{f,g}\right\rangle_{L^{2},n}.

The second inequality comes from the definition of
T_{X}^{1/2}
.
∎

A.2
The variance term

The first step of the proof is to lower bound the generalization error by
the variance term
V(\lambda)
, whose explicit expression can be derived.
This step is elementary and quite standard in the literature, so we omit the proof here.

Proposition A.2
.

Under Assumption
4
, for any
\lambda\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle E\left(\norm{\hat{f}_{\lambda} - f^*_\rho}^{2}_{L^{2}}\mid X\right)\geq V(\lambda)=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n^{2}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{K}(x,X)(K+\lambda)^{-2}\mathbb{K}(X,x)\differential\mu(x).

(32)

Proof.

Let us denote
K_{\lambda}=K+\lambda
.
Recalling (
15
), we have

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{\lambda}-f^{*}_{\rho}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{K}(x,X)K_{\lambda}^{-1}Y

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{K}(x,X)K_{\lambda}^{-1}\left(f^{*}_{\rho}[X]+\bm{\epsilon}\right)-f^{*}_{\rho}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{K}(x,X)K_{\lambda}^{-1}\bm{\epsilon}+\mathbb{K}(x,X)K_{\lambda}^{-1}f^{*}_{\rho}[X]-f^{*}_{\rho}(x),

where
\bm{\epsilon}
is the column vector of the noises
\epsilon_{i}=y_{i}-f^{*}_{\rho}(x_{i})
.
Since
\epsilon_{i}|X
are independent with mean zero and variance
\sigma^{2}_{x_{i}}\geq\sigma^{2}
,
simple calculation shows

\displaystyle E\left(\norm{\hat{f}_{\lambda} - f^*_\rho}^{2}_{L^{2}}\mid X\right)

\displaystyle=E\left[\int_{\mathcal{X}}\left\{\hat{f}_{\lambda}(x)-f^{*}_{\rho}(x)\right\}^{2}\differential\mu(x)\mid X\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}E\left[\left\{\hat{f}_{\lambda}(x)-f^{*}_{\rho}(x)\right\}^{2}\mid X\right]\differential\mu(x)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathrm{var}\left\{\hat{f}_{\lambda}(x)\mid X\right\}\differential\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{K}(x,X)K_{\lambda}^{-1}E\left(\bm{\epsilon}\bm{\epsilon}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\mid X\right)K_{\lambda}^{-1}\mathbb{K}(X,x)\differential\mu(x)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n^{2}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{K}(x,X)K_{\lambda}^{-2}\mathbb{K}(X,x)\differential\mu(x).

∎

The simple but critical observation based on the matrix form in (
32

\displaystyle(K+\lambda_{1})^{-2}\succeq(K+\lambda_{2})^{-2}\mbox{\quad if\quad}\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2},

where
\succeq
represents the partial order of positive semi-definite matrices,
and thus we have the following proposition
[
24
]
.

Proposition A.3
.

For
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq 0
, we have
V(\lambda_{1})\leq V(\lambda_{2})
.
Particularly, for any
\lambda\geq 0

\displaystyle V(0)\geq V(\lambda).

This proposition allows us to consider the variance term of slightly bigger
\lambda

where concentration results can be established.
However, it is still difficult to directly analyze the matrix form.
The key is to rewrite the matrix form into the operator form using empirical semi-norm introduced in

Li et al. 2023
.

Lemma A.4
.

The variance term in
(
32
)
satisfies

\displaystyle V(\lambda)=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\norm{(T_{X}+\lambda)^{-1}k(x,\cdot)}_{L^{2},n}^{2}\differential\mu(x)

(33)

Proof.

By definition it is obvious that
h_{x}[X]=\mathbb{K}(X,x)

From (

\displaystyle\left((T_{X}+\lambda)^{-1}h_{x}\right)[X]=(K+\lambda)^{-1}h_{x}[X]=(K+\lambda)^{-1}\mathbb{K}(X,x),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{K}(x,X)(K+\lambda)^{-2}\mathbb{K}(X,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\norm{\{ (T_X+\lambda)^{-1}h_x\}[X]}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\norm{(T_X+\lambda)^{-1}h_x}_{L^{2},n}^{2}.

from the definition (

∎

The operator form (

The main difference is that we allow

\displaystyle\norm{(T_X+\lambda)^{-1}h_x}_{L^{2},n}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle A}}{{\approx}}\norm{(T+\lambda)^{-1}h_x}_{L^{2},n}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle B}}{{\approx}}\norm{(T+\lambda)^{-1}h_x}_{L^{2}}^{2}.

holds with probability at least
1-\delta

Then, we have

\displaystyle V(\lambda)=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\norm{T_{X\lambda}^{-1} h_x}_{L^{2},n}^{2}\differential\mu(x)\geq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2n}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\norm{T_\lambda^{-1} h_x}_{L^{2}}^{2}\differential\mu(x)-o(\frac{\lambda^{-1/\beta}}{n}).

For the integral, applying Mercer’s theorem,
we get

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}

\displaystyle\norm{T_\lambda^{-1} h_x}_{L^{2}}^{2}\differential\mu(x)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda+\lambda_{i}}\right)^{2}e_{i}(x)^{2}\differential\mu(x)

(46)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda+\lambda_{i}}\right)^{2}=\mathcal{N}_{2}(\lambda)\geq c\lambda^{-1/\beta},

where the last inequality comes from Proposition
B.2
.
Therefore, we conclude that

\displaystyle V(\lambda)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{c\sigma^{2}}{2n}\lambda^{-1/\beta}-o(\frac{\lambda^{-1/\beta}}{n})\geq c_{1}\frac{\lambda^{-1/\beta}}{n}.

Now we prove (
45
).
Let us choose
\alpha>\alpha_{0}=1/\beta
sufficiently close but fixed.
The requirement of
\alpha
will be seen in the following proof.
Then, Lemma
A.7
and Lemma
A.9
yield that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\norm{T_{\lambda}^{-1} h_x}_{L^{2}}^{2}-R\leq\norm{T_{\lambda}^{-1} h_x}_{L^{2},n}^{2}\leq\frac{3}{2}\norm{T_{\lambda}^{-1} h_x}_{L^{2}}^{2}+R,

(47)

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{\norm{T_X^{1/2}T_{X\lambda}^{-1} h_x}_{\caH} - \norm{T_X^{1/2}T_{\lambda}^{-1} h_x}_{\caH}}\leq R^{1/2},\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{X},

(48)

with probability at least
1-\delta
for large
n
, where

\displaystyle R=CM_{\alpha}^{4}\frac{\lambda^{-2\alpha}}{n}\ln\frac{n}{\delta}

for some constant

not depending on
n,\delta,p,\alpha
.
Consequently, the

-norm bound in (

)
and (
47
) yield

\displaystyle\norm{T_X^{1/2}T_{\lambda}^{-1} h_x}_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\norm{T_{\lambda}^{-1} h_x}_{L^{2},n}\leq C\norm{T_{\lambda}^{-1} h_x}_{L^{2}}+R^{1/2}\leq C\lambda^{-\alpha/2}+R^{1/2}

which, together with (
48
), implies

\displaystyle\norm{T_X^{1/2}T_{X\lambda}^{-1} h_x}_{\mathcal{H}}+\norm{T_X^{1/2}T_{\lambda}^{-1} h_x}_{\mathcal{H}}\leq 2\norm{T_X^{1/2}T_{\lambda}^{-1} h_x}_{\mathcal{H}}+R^{1/2}\leq C(\lambda^{-\alpha/2}+R^{1/2})

Then, we obtain the approximation of the squared norm

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{\norm{T_X^{1/2}T_{X\lambda}^{-1} h_x}_{\caH}^2 - \norm{T_X^{1/2}T_{\lambda}^{-1} h_x}_{\caH}^2}\leq CR^{1/2}(\lambda^{-\alpha/2}+R^{1/2}).

(49)

Combining (
49
) and (
47
), we get

\displaystyle\norm{T_{X\lambda}^{-1} h_x}_{L^{2},n}^{2}

\displaystyle=\norm{T_X^{1/2}T_{X\lambda}^{-1} h_x}_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\norm{T_X^{1/2}T_{\lambda}^{-1} h_x}_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}-CR^{1/2}(\lambda^{-\alpha/2}+R^{1/2})

\displaystyle=\norm{T_{\lambda}^{-1} h_x}_{L^{2},n}^{2}-CR^{1/2}(\lambda^{-\alpha/2}+R^{1/2})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\norm{T_{\lambda}^{-1} h_x}_{L^{2}}^{2}-R-CR^{1/2}(\lambda^{-\alpha/2}+R^{1/2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\norm{T_{\lambda}^{-1} h_x}_{L^{2}}^{2}-CR^{1/2}(R^{1/2}+\lambda^{-\alpha/2}).

(50)

Finally, we show that the error terms in (

) are infinitesimal with respect to the main term
\lambda^{-1/\beta}
.
We recall that
\lambda=\Omega(n^{-\beta+p})
and
\alpha_{0}=1/\beta
, so

\displaystyle R^{1/2}=CM_{\alpha}^{2}\left(\frac{\lambda^{-\alpha}}{n}\right)^{1/2}\lambda^{-\alpha/2}\left(\ln\frac{n}{\delta}\right)^{1/2}\leq CM_{\alpha}^{2}\lambda^{-\alpha/2}

if
1/\beta<\alpha<1/(\beta-p)
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\lambda^{1/\beta}R^{1/2}(R^{1/2}+\lambda^{-\alpha/2})

\displaystyle\leq C\lambda^{1/\beta}R^{1/2}\cdot M_{\alpha}^{2}\lambda^{-\alpha/2}

\displaystyle\leq CM_{\alpha}^{4}n^{-1/2}\lambda^{1/\beta-3\alpha/2}\left(\ln\frac{n}{\delta}\right)^{1/2},

and thus
R^{1/2}(R^{1/2}+\lambda^{-\alpha/2})=o(\lambda^{-1/\beta})
if
we further set
\beta^{-1}<\alpha<\beta^{-1}\cdot(3\beta-2p)/(3\beta-3p)
.
∎

The following concentration inequality is introduced in

Lemma B.3
.

Let
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}
be

(52)

[

Proposition B.4
.

Let

Lemma B.5
.

We will prove by Lemma
