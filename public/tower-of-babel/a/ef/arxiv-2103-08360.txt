Quantum marginals, faces, and coatoms
Stephan Weis 1,∗, João Gouveia 2

Abstract
. The set of quantum marginals is a central object in quantum statistics. The faces of this convex set play a decisive role regarding the information projection to a hierarchical model, and regarding state tomography from marginals. However, the faces of this convex set are widely unexplored. Here, we provide an experimental method to explore the maximal faces, the socalled coatoms, in the lattice of exposed faces of the set of marginals. The method proceeds in three steps: a) sampling extreme points from the dual spectrahedron, b) guessing the exact algebraic form of these extreme points, and c) verifying the algebraic result. The third step employs ground projectors of local Hamiltonians. Using this method, we found a two-parameter family of ground projectors of rank five, which support a family of maximal faces of the set of two-body marginals of three qubits (the rank is six for three bits). In classical information theory, we show that a probability distribution factors with respect to an interaction pattern only if it is supported by the ground projector of a frustration-free Hamiltonian. We discuss nonexposed points.



1.
Introduction

Quantum marginals have been studied in quantum chemistry since the 1960’s, see
for example
[
12
,
49
]
. The marginals are an
economic representation of a density matrix regarding local interaction patterns.
As an example, the
k
-body marginals capture all information of a quantum state
that can be observed through a
k
-local Hamiltonian, an observable that ignores
interactions which cannot be described by subsystems of
k
units.

The set of marginals is fundamental to hierarchical models in statistics
and to the information projection onto such models.
In classical information theory
[
19
]
, these models represent
patterns of many-body interaction
[
5
, Section 2.9]
and the
information projection encodes the maximum-likelihood estimate

[
14
]
. The information projection can also be used to
define a complexity measure for a many-body system in terms of the entropy
distance from a hierarchical model
[
5
, Section 6.1]
. To
define the information projection with probability one, one needs to know
the face of the set of marginals onto which a distributions of nonmaximal
support projects
[
14
,
21
,
39
]
.

Faces of marginals are even more important in quantum than in classical
information theory.
The information projection of a probability distribution of nonmaximal
support can be approximated continuously, but a similar approximation fails
in quantum mechanics due to the discontinuity of the maximum-entropy inference
map
[
43
]
. Hence, the quantum mechanical analogue

[
30
,
50
,
44
,
49
]

of the aforementioned measure of complexity can be computed only for
commutative subalgebras, for example, stabilizer states
[
50
]
.
In any case, finding the maximum-entropy state, and hence the information
projection, is computationally hard
[
15
]
.

Prior studies of faces of marginal have appeared in quantum chemistry

[
16
,
34
]
and quantum state tomography

[
11
,
12
,
13
,
22
,
25
,
49
]
. Still, to the best of our
knowledge, no faces of the set of two-body marginals of three qubits
are known besides the extreme points. The extreme points are characterized by the
fact that each pure state is uniquely determined among all states by its two-body
marginals
[
25
]
, except for the pure states of the form

\alpha\ket{000}+\beta\ket{111}
, the so-called GHZ states. Actually, each of the
uniquely determined pure states is the unique ground state of a two-local
Hamiltonian
[
13
]
. The latter implies that the set of marginals
has no nonexposed points as we will see in Section
4.6
. In
contrast, using tools from dissipative quantum control theory, recent studies
show that the set of two-body marginals of six qubits has nonexposed points

[
22
]
.

We contribute to faces of marginals by describing an experimental
method. The method allows us to explore the lattice of exposed faces through the
coatoms (maximal elements). The basic idea is that the coatoms are in a one-to-one
correspondence with the extreme points of the dual spectrahedron. We sample from
these extreme points by solving random semidefinite programs numerically. Upon
guessing the exact algebraic form of an extreme point, or of a family of extreme
points, linear algebra allows us to test whether a candidate is indeed an extreme
point, see Remark
3.7
. This test employs the isomorphism from the
lattice of exposed faces of the set of marginals to the lattice of ground projectors
of local Hamiltonians
[
41
,
46
]
. Thereby, the coatoms of the
lattice of exposed faces are in one-to-one correspondence with the coatoms of
the lattice of ground projectors. All other exposed faces are intersections of
coatoms.

Our experimental method shows that there are coatoms of rank five in the lattice of
ground projectors of two-local three-qubit Hamiltonians
(Section
4.5
). This breaks the pattern of
diagonal matrices representing classical information theory
[
19
]
,
where the rank is six. Geometrically, we owe the coatoms of rank five to the
fact that a spectrahedron of noncommutative matrices typically looks like an
inflated polyhedron that has high-rank extreme points on its curved boundary.
An instructive example is the spectrahedron

\left\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\mid\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&x&y\\
x&1&z\\
y&z&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\succeq 0\right\},

which contains the one-skeleton of a tetrahedron and which is bounded by the
Cayley cubic, see Figure
1
. The four vertices of the underlying
tetrahedron correspond to rank-
1
matrices, while the rest of the yellow surface
corresponds to rank-
2
matrices. The relative interiors of the six edges of the
tetrahedron constitute the set of boundary points that are not extreme.

We also present results in classical statistics. We describe the faces of the set
of two-body marginals of three bits in terms of edges of a graph
(Section
4.4
), and we analyze the boundaries of hierarchical models.
A probability distribution in such a model is equivalently characterized through a
factorization property and defined as the exponential of a local Hamiltonian. A
well-known example is the Hammersley-Clifford theorem (Gibbs-Markov equivalence)
that characterizes the factorization with respect to undirected graphs

[
24
]
. The problem of extending the equivalence from maximal to
nonmaximal support has found a general answer
[
17
]
. In
Section
4.2
, we add to this topic by proving that a probability
distribution factors only if its support set is the ground projector of a
frustration-free Hamiltonian.

Returning to quantum mechanics, one reason why the convex geometry of marginals
is a challenging problem might be the computational complexity of two related
problems: the local Hamiltonian problem of estimating
the ground state energy of a local Hamiltonian and the marginal problem
of deciding whether a collection of states is the collection of marginals of a
global state. The local Hamiltonian problem
[
23
]
and the quantum
marginal problem
[
26
]
are QMA-complete, which means they cannot be solved
efficiently on a quantum computer. The marginal problem can be solved by a
hierarchy of semidefinite programs
[
48
]
if the global state is
assumed to be pure. The quantum marginal problem with non-overlapping subsystems
is trivial but is QMA-complete for indistinguishable particles, fermions or bosons

[
27
,
40
]
, where a spectral polytope describes its solution

[
3
,
35
,
36
,
28
]
.

2

3
explains the
experimental method in the general setting of the joint numerical range

[

]
. Section
4
addresses quantum

The set of two-body marginals of three qubits still offers research challenges.
It is an open problem to describe the set of
(AB,BC)
-marginals of a three-qubit
system

, see
[
47
]
and
[

. The
analysis of the lattice of faces may be simplified by replacing complex with

[

,
29
,

]
.

2.
Matrix
\ast
-Algebras

This section introduces the algebras we employ throughout the paper.

2.1.
Lattices, State Spaces, Projectors

A
lattice

[
8
]
is a
partially ordered set in which any pair of elements has an infimum and a supremum.
A lattice is
complete
if every subset has an infimum and a supremum. Let

\mathcal{L}
be a lattice with least element
0
and greatest element
1
. An

atom
of
\mathcal{L}
is a minimal element of
\mathcal{L}\setminus\{0\}
. A
coatom
of

\mathcal{L}
is a maximal element of
\mathcal{L}\setminus\{1\}
. The lattice
\mathcal{L}
is

atomistic
if each of its element is the supremum of a set
of atoms (such a lattice is called atomic in
[
8
]
). The lattice

\mathcal{L}
is
coatomistic
if each of its element is the infimum of a set of coatoms.

Let
M_{d}
denote the
\ast
-algebra of complex
d\times d
matrices, and
I_{d}
the

d\times d
identity matrix. We write the matrix product of
A,B\in M_{d}
in the
form
A.B
to distinguish it from the tensor product
AB=A\otimes B
in
Section
4.1
. We will work with a
\ast
-algebra
\mathcal{A}\subseteq M_{d}

over the reals as this give us the possibility to decrease the dimension (see
Section
2.4
). This also includes the
\ast
-algebras over the
complex field. The
Hilbert-Schmidt inner product
on
\mathcal{A}
is
defined by
\langle A,B\rangle=\Tr(A^{\ast}\!.\,B)
for all
A,B\in\mathcal{A}
. The real vector space
of hermitian matrices

\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{A})=\{A\in\mathcal{A}:A^{\ast}=A\}

is a Euclidean space with the restricted Hilbert-Schmidt inner product. The
set
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{A})
is partially ordered by the
Loewner order

A\preceq B
,
or equivalently
B\succeq A
, which is valid if
B-A
is positive semidefinite for
all
A,B\in\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{A})
. We denote the set of positive semidefinite matrices by

\mathcal{A}^{+}=\{A\in\mathcal{A}:A\succeq 0\}.

The
state space

[
2
]
of the algebra
\mathcal{A}
is the set

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})=\{\rho\in\mathcal{A}^{+}:\Tr(\rho)=1\}.

The set
\mathcal{A}^{+}
is a closed, convex cone and
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
is a compact, convex set.
The elements of
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
are called
density matrices
or
quantum states

[
7
]
. The extreme points of
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
are called the

pure states
of
\mathcal{A}
. Endowed with the restricted Loewner order, the set of

projectors
in
\mathcal{A}
,

\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})=\{P\in\mathcal{A}:P=P^{\ast}=P^{2}\},

is a complete lattice
[
2
]
.

Rank-one projectors are important, as they are the atoms of
\mathcal{P}(M_{d})
. Every
rank-one projector
P\in\mathcal{P}(M_{d})
is a pure state and we write it as

P=\ket{\psi}\!\!\bra{\psi}
in Dirac’s notation, where
\ket{\psi}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
is
any unit vector in the image of
P
. If every atom of
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})
has rank one, then
the converse holds: Every pure state of
\mathcal{A}
is a rank-one projector.

From now on, we assume the
\ast
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
contains the
d\times d
identity
matrix
I_{d}
. In contrast, the multiplicative identity of the
\ast
-algebra

P.\mathcal{A}.P=\{P.A.P:A\in\mathcal{A}\}

is
P
for all projectors
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})
. The assumption of
I_{d}\in\mathcal{A}
guarantees
that every eigenvalue of a matrix
A\in\mathcal{A}
is a spectral value of
A
in the
algebra
\mathcal{A}
, which is important in our definition of a ground projector in
Section
3.3
.

2.2.
Diagonal Matrices

Given a finite set
X
, the space
\mathbb{C}^{X}
of functions
X\to\mathbb{C}
is a
\ast
-algebra.
Let
\delta_{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{X}
be defined by
\delta_{x}(y)=0
if
x\neq y
and
\delta_{x}(y)=1

if
x=y
, for all
x,y\in X
. The
support
of a function
f\in\mathbb{C}^{X}
is the set
of points
\{x\in X\mid f(x)\neq 0\}
. We identify the set of functions
C(d)\to\mathbb{C}

on the
configuration space

C(d)=\{0,\ldots,d-1\}
with the set of
d\times d

diagonal matrices, in such a way that
f\in\mathbb{C}^{C(d)}
corresponds to the diagonal
matrix
\,\diag(f(0),f(1),\dots,f(d-1))\,
. In the notation
Section
2.1
, the space
\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{C}^{C(d)})
of hermitian
matrices is the set of real functions
C(d)\to\mathbb{R}
, the state space
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{C}^{C(d)})

is the simplex of probability distributions on
C(d)
, and the set
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{C}^{C(d)})

of projectors is the set of
\{0,1\}
-valued functions on
C(d)
. There is a lattice
isomorphism

(1)

\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{C}^{C(d)})\to 2^{C(d)},\quad P\mapsto\supp(P),

from the set of projectors to the power set of the configuration space
C(d)
, which
maps the rank-one projector
\delta_{x}
to
x
for all
x\in C(d)
.

2.3.
The Qubit-Algebra

The
qubit
is the information unit of quantum theory. The algebra
associated with the qubit is the complex
\ast
-algebra of
2\times 2
matrices

M_{2}
, spanned by the identity matrix

I=I_{2}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)

and the
Pauli matrices

X=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right),\qquad Y=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-\mathrm{i}\\
\mathrm{i}&0\end{smallmatrix}\right),\qquad Z=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{smallmatrix}\right).

These matrices also span the real space
\mathcal{H}(M_{2})
of hermitian matrices.
Any traceless hermitian matrix can be written in the form

\hat{n}\!\cdot\!\vec{\sigma}=n_{x}X+n_{y}Y+n_{z}Z

where
\hat{n}=(n_{x},n_{y},n_{z})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the
Bloch vector
and

\vec{\sigma}=(X,Y,Z)
the
Pauli vector
. The matrix

\hat{n}\!\cdot\!\vec{\sigma}
has the eigenvalues
\pm|\hat{n}|
and the
spectral decomposition

\hat{n}\!\cdot\!\vec{\sigma}=|\hat{n}|(I+\tfrac{\hat{n}}{|\hat{n}|}\!\cdot\!\vec{\sigma})/2-|\hat{n}|(I-\tfrac{\hat{n}}{|\hat{n}|}\!\cdot\!\vec{\sigma})/2,\qquad\hat{n}\neq 0.

The state space of
M_{2}
is the
Bloch ball

\mathcal{D}(M_{2})=\left\{(I+\hat{n}\!\cdot\!\vec{\sigma})/2:\hat{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{3},|\hat{n}|\leq 1\right\}.

The set of pure states is the
Bloch sphere

\{(I+\hat{n}\!\cdot\!\vec{\sigma})/2:\hat{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{3},|\hat{n}|=1\}
.

2.4.
The Disk-Algebra

The real
\ast
-algebra
M_{2}(\mathbb{R})=\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{I,X,Z,\mathrm{i}Y\}
is interesting
as it is noncommutative and has a smaller dimension than
M_{2}
. The
space of hermitian matrices is

\mathcal{H}(M_{2}(\mathbb{R}))=\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{I,X,Z\}.

The state space is the disk

\mathcal{D}(M_{2}(\mathbb{R}))=\{\rho\in\mathcal{D}(M_{2}):\langle\rho,Y\rangle=0\}
, a cross section of the
Bloch ball.

2.5.
The Bit-Algebra

The information unit of digital computers is the
bit
, which has the
configuration space
C(2)=\{0,1\}
. Thinking of the elements of
\mathbb{C}^{C(2)}
as

2
-by-
2
diagonal matrices as in Section
2.2
, we
write
\mathbb{C}^{C(2)}
as the span of the identity matrix
I
and the Pauli matrix

Z
introduced in Section
2.3
. As per the lattice isomorphism
(
1
), the rank-one projectors

\tfrac{1}{2}\big(I+(-1)^{x}Z\big),\qquad x=0,1,

of
\mathbb{C}^{C(2)}
are in a one-to-one correspondence with the configurations

0
and
1
.

3.1.
Exposed Faces and Normal Cones

Let
(\mathbb{E},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
be a Euclidean space and
C\subseteq\mathbb{E}
a convex
subset. An
exposed face
of
C
is a subset of
C
, which is either empty or
equal to the set of points at which a linear function attains its minimum on

C
. We denote the set of exposed faces of
C
by
\mathcal{F}(C)
. If
C
is compact then
the minimum
\mu_{C,u}=\min_{x\in C}\langle x,u\rangle
exists for all
u\in\mathbb{E}
and we define
the map

F_{C}:\mathbb{E}\to\mathcal{F}(C),\qquad u\mapsto\{x\in C:\langle x,u\rangle=\mu_{C,u}\}.

We call
F_{C}(u)
the exposed face of
C

exposed
by the vector
u
. A point

x\in C
is an
exposed point
if
\{x\}
is an exposed face. Partially ordered
by inclusion, the set
\mathcal{F}(C)
is a complete lattice, and the infimum is the
intersection.

The
normal cone
to
C
at a point
x\in C
is the closed convex cone

N_{C}(x)=\{u\in\mathbb{E}\mid\langle y-x,u\rangle\geq 0\;\forall y\in C\}.

The
normal cone
to
C
at a nonempty convex subset
G\subseteq C
is
defined as the intersection
N_{C}(G)=\cap_{x\in G}N_{C}(x)
. We put

N_{C}(\emptyset)=\mathbb{E}
. Partially ordered by inclusion, the set
\mathcal{N}(C)
of normal
cones to
C
is a complete lattice, and the infimum is the intersection.

In a slight abuse of the symbol
N_{C}
, we define the map

(2)

N_{C}:\mathcal{F}(C)\to\mathcal{N}(C),\qquad F\mapsto N_{C}(F).

If
C
is not a singleton, then this map is an antitone lattice isomorphism. The
statements of this section are proved in
[
42
]
.

3.2.
Convex Duality

Let
(\mathbb{E},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
be a Euclidean space and denote the orthogonal
projection onto a subspace
U\subseteq\mathbb{E}
by
\pi_{U}:\mathbb{E}\to\mathbb{E}
. The

dual convex cone
to a subset
C\subseteq\mathbb{E}
is the closed convex cone

C^{\vee}=\{u\in\mathbb{E}\mid\langle u,x\rangle\geq 0\;\forall x\in C\}.

If
C
is a closed convex cone, then
C=(C^{\vee})^{\vee}
holds. If
C=C^{\vee}
,
then
C
is called a
self-dual convex cone
. The
dual convex set
to
any subset
C\subseteq\mathbb{E}
is

C^{\circ}=\{u\in\mathbb{E}\mid 1+\langle u,x\rangle\geq 0\;\forall x\in C\}.

The set
C^{\circ}
is a closed convex set containing the origin. If
C

is a compact, convex set containing the origin as an interior point, then the dual
convex set
C^{\circ}
is compact and contains the origin as an interior point, too

[
33
,
37
]
.

Section
3.6
uses the following one-to-one correspondence between
normal cones of
C
and exposed faces of
C^{\circ}
. If
C
is a compact, convex set
containing the origin as an interior point and if
\dim(\mathbb{E})\geq 1
, then the map

(3)

\chi_{C}:\mathcal{N}(C)\to\mathcal{F}(C^{\circ}),\qquad N\mapsto\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}N\cap\partial C^{\circ}&\text{if $N\neq\mathbb{E}$},\\
C^{\circ}&\text{if $N=\mathbb{E}$},\end{array}\right.

is an isotone lattice isomorphism, where
\partial C^{\circ}
is the boundary of

C^{\circ}
. The composition of the maps (
2
) and
(
3
) is the antitone lattice isomorphism
\mathcal{F}(C)\to\mathcal{F}(C^{\circ})

that maps an exposed face to its
conjugate face

[
37
]
. The
inverse isomorphism to (
3
) is

(4)

\chi_{C}^{-1}:\mathcal{F}(C^{\circ})\to\mathcal{N}(C),\qquad F\mapsto\pos(F),

where
\pos(F)=\{\lambda x:\lambda\geq 0,x\in F\}
is the positive hull of
any nonempty exposed face
F
of
C^{\circ}
, and
\pos(\emptyset)=\{0\}
, see for
example
[
42
, Section 8]
. Lemma 7.2 of
[
42
]
shows that the
cone
\chi_{C}^{-1}(F)
is the normal cone to
C
at the exposed face
F_{C}(u)
of

C
which is exposed by any nonzero vector
u
in the relative interior of

\chi_{C}^{-1}(F)
, for all exposed faces
F\neq C^{\circ}
of
C^{\circ}
.

The following construction is fundamental in Section
3.6
.
Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{E}
be a closed convex cone with interior point
\epsilon\neq 0
. Then

B_{C,\epsilon}=\{u\in C^{\vee}:\langle u,\varepsilon\rangle=1\}

is a compact convex set, which is a
base
of
C^{\vee}
. Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{E}
be a
linear subspace incident with
\epsilon
, let

V_{U,\epsilon}=\{u\in U:\langle u,\varepsilon\rangle=0\}

be the orthogonal complement to
\epsilon
in
U
, and

S_{C,U,\epsilon}=\{x\in V_{U,\epsilon}:\varepsilon+x\in C\}

an affine section of the cone
C
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{E}
be a closed convex cone and let
\epsilon\neq 0
be an interior
point of
C
. Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{E}
be a linear subspace incident with
\epsilon
. Then

S_{C,U,\epsilon}
is the dual convex set to
\pi_{V_{U,\epsilon}}(B_{C,\epsilon})

with respect to the Euclidean space
V_{U,\epsilon}
.

Proof.

Let
x\in V_{U,\epsilon}
and let
B=B_{C,\epsilon}
. Then

\displaystyle x\in S_{C,U,\epsilon}

\displaystyle\iff\varepsilon+x\in C\iff\forall u\in C^{\vee}:\langle u,\varepsilon+x\rangle\geq 0

\displaystyle\iff\forall u\in B:\langle u,\varepsilon+x\rangle\geq 0\iff\forall u\in B:1+\langle u,x\rangle\geq 0

\displaystyle\iff\forall u\in\pi_{V_{U,\epsilon}}(B):1+\langle u,x\rangle\geq 0.

This proves the claim.

3.3.
Geometry of the State Space

The exposed faces and the normal cones of the state space
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
are
represented in terms of projectors.

Let
P_{0}:\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{A})\to\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})
denote the map from the set of hermitian matrices to the
set of projectors, where
P_{0}(A)
is the spectral projector of
A
corresponding to the
smallest eigenvalue of
A
. We call
P_{0}(A)
the
ground projector
of
A
by its
name in physics if
A
represents an energy observable.

The exposed face of the state space
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
exposed by
A\in\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{A})
is

F_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})}(A)=\mathcal{D}(P_{0}(A).\mathcal{A}.P_{0}(A)).

Note that

F_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})}(A)=\{\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})\mid S(\rho)\preceq P_{0}(A)\},

where
S(\rho)
is the
support projector
of
\rho
, the sum of the spectral
projectors corresponding to the nonzero eigenvalues. Moreover, the map

(5)

\phi_{\mathcal{A}}:\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})\to\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})),\qquad P\mapsto\mathcal{D}(P.\mathcal{A}.P)

is an isotone lattice isomorphism from the lattice of projectors
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})
to the
lattice of exposed faces of
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
, see for example
[
2
]
or

[
41
, Section 2.3]
. As

(6)

F_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})}(A)=\phi_{\mathcal{A}}\circ P_{0}(A),\qquad A\in\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{A}),

the map
F_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})}:\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{A})\to\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}))
factors through
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})
.

The concatenation of the maps (
5
) and (
2

Remark 3.7

(Experimental Search for Coatoms)
.

The coatoms of the lattice
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{W})
of exposed faces of the joint numerical
range
\mathcal{W}=\pi_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}))
are amenable to a numerical exploration, supported
by convex geometry and linear algebra.

The map from the exposed faces of
\mathcal{W}
to their conjugate faces defines an
antitone isomorphism
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{W})\to\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{U}))
to the lattice of exposed faces of
the spectrahedron
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{U})
. This map induces a one-to-one correspondence between
the coatoms of
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{W})
and the atoms of
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{U}))
, which are the extreme
points of
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{U})
. Numerically, one can draw linear functionals from the dual
space
\mathcal{V}^{\ast}
of
\mathcal{V}
at random, and minimize them on
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{U})
. The minimum
of a generic linear functional is attained at a single extreme point of
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{U})
.
This means that this random search will allow us to sample extreme points, or at
least numerical approximations of such. The minimization can be done efficiently,
using semidefinite programming
[
6
]
.

To illustrate some subtleties of the underlying process let us revisit the Cayley
cubic example of Figure
1
. In that case there are two types of
extreme points, the four rank one vertices and the surface of rank two extreme
points. While there are only four rank one extreme points, their normal cones have
a high volume, hence it is quite likely that while searching in a random direction
we end up sampling those points. In Figure
3
one can see the
possible search directions in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, color coded by which type of extreme
point they lead to. The directions that lead to rank one matrices form four equal
spherical caps pairwise tangent. One can easily calculate that a random search
would therefore lead to a rank one matrix around
84.5\%
of the times, and a rank
two matrix otherwise. The exceptional directions that would lead to linear forms
that are minimized in higher dimensional faces are the six tangency points of the
caps.

This gives us some hint of possible issues if one wants to find representatives for
all classes of extreme points. In high dimensions, if the union of the normal cones
of the extreme points in some class is of very low volume, it might be hard to
sample by a uniformly generated random search direction. This problem should not be
as acute in moderate dimensions, and does not stop us from attempting to find new
interesting classes of extreme points.

We construct a basis for each summand in the direct sum (

Let
\mathcal{B}_{i}

\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{A}_{i})
,

of

\left\{B_{1}B_{2}\ldots B_{N}\mid\mbox{$B_{i}\in\mathcal{B}_{i}$ and $B_{i}=I_{d_{i}}$ if and only if $i\in\bar{\nu}$

\nu
-factor

\,\prod_{i\in\nu}(\dim\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{A}_{i})-1)

As per
\,\mar_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{a})}=\mar_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{a})}\circ\pi_{\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{a})}

\,\mar_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{a})}\,

between the joint numerical range and the set of marginals. The dimension of the set

In the sequel, we focus mainly on three-body systems, where

A special class of local Hamiltonians appears in information theory and statistical

]

\textstyle\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathrm{ff}}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{a})=\left\{\bigwedge_{\nu\in\mathfrak{g}}P_{\nu}I_{\bar{\nu}}:P_{\nu}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A}_{\nu}),\nu\in\mathfrak{g}\right\}.

lies in

\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathrm{ff}}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{a})

is a complete

[

, Section I.4]
. Furthermore, the lattice

is

(21)

\textstyle\bigcup_{\nu\in\mathfrak{g}}\left\{P_{\nu}I_{\bar{\nu}}:\mbox{$P_{\nu}$ is a coatom of $\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A}_{\nu})$}\right\}

if
\mathfrak{g}
is a generating class.

4.2.
Probability Distributions that Factor

A probability distribution factors if and only if it satisfies a set of
polynomial equations and if its support set satisfies a certain condition

[
17
]
. Here we show that the latter condition means that
the support set is the ground projector of a frustration-free Hamiltonian.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{i}=\mathbb{C}^{C(d_{i})}
be the
\ast
-algebra of complex functions on the
configuration space
C(d_{i})=\{0,1,\dots,d-1\}
,
i\in\Omega
, introduced in
Section
2.2
. The algebra
\mathcal{A}_{\nu}
of the subsystem

\nu\subseteq\Omega
is the set
\mathcal{A}_{\nu}=\mathbb{C}^{C_{\nu}}
of complex functions on the
configuration space

C_{\nu}=\bigtimes_{i\in\nu}C_{i}.

If
x=(x_{i})_{i\in\Omega}
is an element of
C_{\Omega}
and
\nu\subseteq\Omega
,
then
x_{\nu}
denotes the truncation of
x
to
\nu
, that is to say,

x_{\nu}=((x_{\nu})_{i})_{i\in\nu}
is the element of
C_{\nu}
which satisfies

(x_{\nu})_{i}=x_{i}
for all
i\in\nu
. Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a family of subsets of

\Omega
and let

C_{\mathfrak{g}}=\{(\nu,y)\mid y\in C_{\nu},\nu\in\mathfrak{g}\}

denote the disjoint union of the configuration spaces
C_{\nu}
,
\nu\in\mathfrak{g}
.
The matrix
M=(m_{(\nu,y),x})
of the map (
16
) has the
coefficients

(22)

\displaystyle m_{(\nu,y),x}

\displaystyle=\mar_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{a})}(\delta_{x})(\nu,y)

\displaystyle=\Tr_{\bar{\nu}}(\delta_{x})(y)=\delta_{x_{\nu}}(y),\qquad(\nu,y)\in C_{\mathfrak{g}},x\in C_{\Omega},

with respect to the bases
(\delta_{x})_{x\in C_{\Omega}}
of
\mathbb{R}^{C_{\Omega}}
and

(\delta_{(\nu,y)})_{(\nu,y)\in C_{\mathfrak{g}}}
of
\bigtimes_{\nu\in\mathfrak{g}}\mathbb{R}^{C_{\nu}}
.
The set of marginals is the convex hull of the columns of the matrix
M
, which
is called the marginal polytope
[
39
]
.

By definition, a probability distribution
P\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})

factors
with
respect to
\mathfrak{g}
if there exist a function
\psi_{\nu}:C_{\nu}\to\mathbb{R}
for each
\nu\in\mathfrak{g}

such that

\textstyle P(x)=\prod_{\nu\in\mathfrak{g}}\psi_{\nu}(x_{\nu}),\qquad x\in C_{\Omega}.

It is well known
[
17
]
that a probability distribution

P\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})
factors with respect to
\mathfrak{g}
if and only if
P=P_{\theta}

for some
\theta\in[-\infty,\infty)^{C_{\mathfrak{g}}}
, where

(23)

\textstyle P_{\theta}(x)=\frac{1}{Z(\theta)}e^{\langle\theta,T(x)\rangle},\qquad x\in C_{\Omega}.

Here,
T(x)=(m_{(\nu,y),x})_{(\nu,y)\in C_{\mathfrak{g}}}
is the column with index

x\in C_{\Omega}
of the matrix
M
defined above in Equation (
22
). The
bracket
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{C_{\mathfrak{g}}}
restricted to
nonnegative values in the second argument and extended to minus infinity in the
first, by defining
(-\infty)\cdot 0=0
and
(-\infty)\cdot t=-\infty
for all

t>0
. The number
Z(\theta)
is a normalization constant. As
e^{-\infty}=0
,
the Equation (
23
) defines a probability distribution
if and only if
\langle\theta,T(x)\rangle>-\infty
holds for at least one
x\in C_{\Omega}
.
Parametric models of the form (
23
) are called

hierarchical models
in the literature
[
24
,
5
]
,
they are special cases of
exponential families
or
log-linear models

[
17
]
.

Probability distributions that factor have been characterized in terms of support sets
and commutative algebra. A subset
F\subseteq C_{\Omega}
is
M
-feasible
if

\textstyle\supp T(x)\not\subseteq\bigcup_{y\in F}\supp T(y),\qquad\text{for all $x\in C_{\Omega}\setminus F$.}

The
nonnegative toric variety

X_{M}
is the set of all vectors

\xi=(\xi_{x})_{x\in C_{\Omega}}
in
[0,\infty)^{C_{\Omega}}
which satisfy

\textstyle\prod_{x\in C_{\Omega}}\xi_{x}^{u_{x}}=\prod_{x\in C_{\Omega}}\xi_{x}^{v_{x}}

whenever
u=(u_{x})_{x\in C_{\Omega}},v=(v_{x})_{x\in C_{\Omega}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{C_{\Omega}}
are
vectors of nonnegative integers such that
u-v
is in the kernel of
M
.

Theorem 4.1

(Geiger et al.
[
17
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{i}=\mathbb{C}^{C(d_{i})}
for all
i\in\Omega
and let
P\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})
be
a probability distribution. Then
P
factors with respect to
\mathfrak{g}
if and only
if the support of
P
is
M
-feasible and
P
lies in the nonnegative toric
variety
X_{M}
.

We describe the support condition of Theorem
4.1
in terms of ground
projectors. We use the map (
1
) to identify projectors in
\mathcal{A}_{\nu}

and subsets of
C_{\nu}
,
\nu\subseteq\Omega
. The complementary projector
to
P\subseteq C_{\nu}
is
P^{\prime}=C_{\nu}\setminus P=I_{\nu}-P
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{i}=\mathbb{C}^{C(d_{i})}
for all
i\in\Omega
and let
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})
be a
projector. The following assertions are equivalent.

(1)

P
is
M
-feasible,

(2)

P=\bigcap_{x\in P^{\prime}}\bigcap_{\nu\in\mathfrak{g}_{x}}\{x_{\nu}\}^{\prime}I_{\bar{\nu}}
,
where
\mathfrak{g}_{x}=\{\nu\in\mathfrak{g}\mid\text{$x_{\nu}\neq y_{\nu}$ for all $y\in P$}\}
,

(3)

there exists a frustration-free
\mathfrak{g}
-local Hamiltonian

A\in\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{a})
such that
P=P_{0}(A)
is the ground projector of
A
.

Proof.

Let
P
be
M
-feasible. Then for all
x\in P^{\prime}
there exists
\nu\in\mathfrak{g}

such that
x_{\nu}\neq y_{\nu}
holds for all
y\in P
. This shows that the set

\mathfrak{g}_{x}
above is nonempty for all
x\in P^{\prime}
. Without any assumptions
on the projector
P
, the inclusion “
\subseteq
” of the assertion 2) holds.
Since each of the sets
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
,
x\in P^{\prime}
, is nonempty, the right-hand side of
the equation 2) cannot contain any points of
P^{\prime}
. This proves
1)
\Rightarrow
2). The implication 2)
\Rightarrow
3) was discussed in
Equation (
20
).

Let
P=\bigcap_{\nu\in\mathfrak{g}}P_{\nu}I_{\bar{\nu}}
, where

P_{\nu}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A}_{\nu})
for all
\nu\in\mathfrak{g}
, and let
x\in P^{\prime}
. Then there exists

\nu\in\mathfrak{g}
such that
x\not\in P_{\nu}I_{\bar{\nu}}
, that is to say,

x_{\nu}\not\in P_{\nu}
. Since
y_{\nu}\in P_{\nu}
holds for all
y\in P
, this proves
that
P
is
M
-feasible, hence 3)
\Rightarrow
1).
∎

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{i}=\mathbb{C}^{C(d_{i})}
,
i\in\Omega
, and let
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})
be a nonzero
projector. Then
P
is the ground projector of a frustration-free
\mathfrak{g}
-local
Hamiltonian if and only if there are functions
A_{\nu}\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{\nu}}
,
\nu\in\mathfrak{g}
,
such that
P=\prod_{\nu\in\mathfrak{g}}A_{\nu}I_{\bar{\nu}}
. If this is the case, then there
are projectors
P_{\nu}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A}_{\nu})
,
\nu\in\mathfrak{g}
, such that

P=\prod_{\nu\in\mathfrak{g}}P_{\nu}I_{\bar{\nu}}
.

Proof.

If the projector
P
factors, then the uniform probability distribution
\frac{P}{|P|}

on the set
P
factors. Theorem
4.1
then shows that
P
is
M
-feasible
and Lemma
4.2
concludes that
P
is the ground projector of a
frustration-free Hamiltonian. Conversely, if
P
is the ground projector of
a frustration-free Hamiltonian, then Equation (
20
) shows

P=\bigcap_{\nu\in\mathfrak{g}}P_{\nu}I_{\bar{\nu}}
, where
P_{\nu}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A}_{\nu})
,

\nu\in\mathfrak{g}
. This proves the claim, as

\bigcap_{\nu\in\mathfrak{g}}P_{\nu}I_{\bar{\nu}}=\prod_{\nu\in\mathfrak{g}}P_{\nu}I_{\bar{\nu}}
.
∎

4.3.
A First Glimpse at Three Qubits

We consider a system of
N\in\mathbb{N}
qubits. The algebras

\textstyle\mathfrak{a}_{N,\mathrm{qu}}=(\underbrace{M_{2},M_{2},\ldots,M_{2}}_{\text{$N$ copies}})

of the units are all equal to the algebra
M_{2}
of a single qubit
(Section
2.3
). The algebra

\textstyle\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}=M_{2}^{\otimes N}=M_{2^{N}}

of the full system is the
N
-fold tensor product of
M_{2}
.
The space of hermitian matrices
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})
has the orthogonal basis

\textstyle\{A_{1}A_{2}\ldots A_{N}:A_{i}\in\{I,X,Y,Z\},i\in\Omega=\{1,2,\ldots,N\}\}.

We begin with an observation regarding the space
\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{c}_{3},\mathfrak{a}_{3,\mathrm{qu}})
of two-local
three-qubit Hamiltonians.

Lemma 4.4
.

The space
\,\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{c}_{3},\mathfrak{a}_{3,\mathrm{qu}})\,
contains no matrix of rank one. In other words,
the lattice of ground projectors
\mathcal{P}_{0}(\mathfrak{c}_{3},\mathfrak{a}_{3,\mathrm{qu}})
contains no projector
of rank seven.

Proof.

Let
P=\ket{\psi}\!\!\bra{\psi}
be the projector onto the line spanned by a
unit vector
\psi\in(\mathbb{C}^{C(2)})^{\otimes 3}
. It is known
[
1
]
that,
up to a local unitary transformation, there are

\kappa_{0},\kappa_{1},\ldots,\kappa_{4}\geq 0
and
\theta\in[0,\pi)
such that

\psi=\kappa_{0}e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\ket{000}+\kappa_{1}\ket{001}+\kappa_{2}\ket{010}+\kappa_{3}\ket{100}+\kappa_{4}\ket{111}.

Let us assume that
P
lies in
\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{c}_{3},\mathfrak{a}_{3,\mathrm{qu}})
. Then the inner products of

P
with all three-factor interactions vanish. In particular

0=\langle P,ZZZ\rangle=\kappa_{0}^{2}-\kappa_{1}^{2}-\cdots-\kappa_{4}^{2},

which is only possible if
\kappa_{0}=1/\sqrt{2}
, as
\psi
is a unit vector.
Hence,

\displaystyle\langle P,ZZX\rangle

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}\,\kappa_{1}\cos(\theta),

\displaystyle\langle P,ZXZ\rangle

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}\,\kappa_{2}\cos(\theta),

\displaystyle\langle P,XZZ\rangle

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}\,\kappa_{3}\cos(\theta),

\displaystyle\langle P,XXX\rangle

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}\,\kappa_{4}\cos(\theta),

which shows
\kappa_{1}=\kappa_{2}=\kappa_{3}=\kappa_{4}=0
if
\theta\neq\frac{\pi}{2}

modulo
\pi
. Also,

\displaystyle\langle P,ZZY\rangle

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{2}\,\kappa_{1}\sin(\theta),

\displaystyle\langle P,ZYZ\rangle

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{2}\,\kappa_{2}\sin(\theta),

\displaystyle\langle P,YZZ\rangle

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{2}\,\kappa_{3}\sin(\theta),

\displaystyle\langle P,YYY\rangle

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}\,\kappa_{4}\sin(\theta),

shows
\kappa_{1}=\kappa_{2}=\kappa_{3}=\kappa_{4}=0
if
\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}
modulo
\pi
.
In any case,

1/2=\kappa_{0}^{2}+\kappa_{1}^{2}+\kappa_{2}^{2}+\kappa_{3}^{2}+\kappa_{4}^{2}=\braket{\psi|\psi}=1

is a contradiction.
∎

4.4.
All About Three Bits

We consider a system of
N\in\mathbb{N}
bits, which is a special case of the
setup discussed in Section
4.2
. The configuration space of
a bit is
C(2)=\{0,1\}
. The algebras

\mathfrak{a}_{N,\mathrm{cl}}=(\underbrace{\mathbb{C}^{C(2)},\mathbb{C}^{C(2)},\ldots,\mathbb{C}^{C(2)}}_{\text{$N$ copies}})

of the units are all equal to the algebra
\mathbb{C}^{C(2)}
of
2
-by-

matrices, associated with a single bit (Section

\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}=(\mathbb{C}^{C(2)})^{\otimes N}=\mathbb{C}^{C_{\Omega}}

N

\mathbb{C}^{C(2)}
, which is the
set of complex functions on
C_{\Omega}
. The set

is an orthogonal basis of the space of hermitian matrices

\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})=\mathbb{R}^{C_{\Omega}}
. We identify two representations of rank-one
projectors in
\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}
, using the isomorphism of Equation (

(24)

x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{N}=\tfrac{1}{2^{N}}\big(I+(-1)^{x_{1}}Z\big)\big(I+(-1)^{x_{2}}Z\big)\ldots\big(I+(-1)^{x_{N}}Z\big),

for all
N
-digit binary numbers

. On the left-hand
side of Equation (

space
C_{\Omega}
, and on the right-hand side there is a diagonal

matrix. The number
x
marks the position of the diagonal entry

of this matrix,
which has all other entries equal to
0
. The position increases from

at the top left to

at the bottom right of the diagonal.

We focus on
N=3
where
\Omega=\{1,2,3\}
. The coatoms of the lattice of ground
projectors of the space
\,\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{c}_{3},\mathfrak{a}_{3,\mathrm{cl}})\,
of two-local three-bit Hamiltonians are
in a one-to-one correspondence with the edges of the graph
K_{4,4}
.
We simplify the proof
[
46
]
of this statement in Lemma
4.5

below. We also describe the ground projectors of frustration-free Hamiltonians, in

4.7
, and of Hamiltonians interacting along a path without a

4.8
.

Note that the space
\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{c}_{3},\mathfrak{a}_{3,\mathrm{cl}})
is the orthogonal complement to
f=ZZZ
in

\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})
. We have

f(x_{1}x_{2}x_{3})=(-1)^{x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}},\qquad x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\in C(2),

We identify the vertex set of the complete bi-partite graph
K_{4,4}
with

C_{\Omega}
, the bi-partition being defined by the two fibers of
f
. In other
words,
\{x,y\}\subseteq C_{\Omega}
is an edge of
K_{4,4}
if and only if the
digit sums of
x
and
y
differ modulo two.

The projectors in
\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}
are in a one-to-one correspondence with the subsets
of
C_{\Omega}
. The complementary projector to
P\subseteq C_{\Omega}
is

P^{\prime}=C_{\Omega}\setminus P=III-P
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
P\subseteq C(2)^{\times 3}
. The projector
P
is a coatom of

\mathcal{P}_{0}(\mathfrak{c}_{3},\mathfrak{a}_{3,\mathrm{cl}})
if and only if
P^{\prime}
is an edge of the graph
K_{4,4}
.
The projector
P
lies in
\mathcal{P}_{0}(\mathfrak{c}_{3},\mathfrak{a}_{3,\mathrm{cl}})
if and only if

is a union
of edges of
K_{4,4}
(possibly empty).

Proof.

We abbreviate
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{c}_{3},\mathfrak{a}_{3,\mathrm{cl}})
and
\mathcal{P}_{0}=\mathcal{P}_{0}(\mathfrak{c}_{3},\mathfrak{a}_{3,\mathrm{cl}})
. The lattice
\mathcal{P}_{0}

has no elements of rank seven by Lemma
4.4
.

If

has rank at most five, then

is not a coatom. Indeed, as

is

\mathcal{U}

.
Hence, there are mutually distinct points

\mathcal{H}(P^{\prime}\!.\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}.P^{\prime})\cap\mathcal{U}
has dimension
at least two, as it contains the linearly independent rank-two projectors
\{x,y\}

and
\{x,z\}
. According to Equation (
15
), this proves that

is not a coatom of
\mathcal{P}_{0}
.

The preceding part of the proof shows that a projector

is a coatom of
\mathcal{P}_{0}
if and only if
P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}
and
|P|=6
. Let
P^{\prime}=\{x,y\}
with

and consider the cone
\mathcal{K}(P)=P^{\prime}\!.\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}^{+}.P^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{U}
defined in

\mathcal{K}(P)
is the ray spanned by

\{x,y\}
. If

then
\mathcal{K}(P)=\{0\}

4.6.
Tomography and Nonexposed Faces

Karuvade et al.
[
22
]
recently discovered a six-qubit state
that is uniquely determined by its two-body marginals, but which is not the
unique ground state of any two-local Hamiltonian. We discuss the convex geometric
consequences of this result. Related observations have been made before

[
13
,
34
]
.

A basic problem of quantum state tomography is to find conditions under which a
state can be recovered from certain data, for example from its image under the
projection
\pi_{\mathcal{U}}
onto a space of hermitian matrices
\,\mathcal{U}
. We say a state

\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
is
uniquely determined by
\pi_{\mathcal{U}}
if
\rho=\sigma

whenever
\pi_{\mathcal{U}}(\rho)=\pi_{\mathcal{U}}(\sigma)
for all states
\sigma\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
.
We say a subset of
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
is
uniquely determined by
\pi_{\mathcal{U}}
if all
its elements are.

Another problem of tomography is concerned with ground states. A state

\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
is a
ground state
of a hermitian matrix
A\in\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{A})

if
\rho
is supported by the ground projector
P_{0}(A)
, that is to say, if

\rho
lies in
\phi_{\mathcal{A}}(P_{0}(A))
, as defined in Equation (
5
).
A state
\rho
is the
unique ground state
of
A
if we have

\{\rho\}=\phi_{\mathcal{A}}(P_{0}(A))
. In this case
\rho=P_{0}(A)/\Tr(P_{0}(A))
holds.

The above notions of tomography have counterparts in terms of faces of the joint
numerical range
\pi_{\mathcal{U}}(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}))
. A
face
of a convex set
C
in a
Euclidean space is a convex subset
F
of
C
such that whenever

(1-\lambda)x+\lambda y
lies in
F
for some
\lambda\in(0,1)
and
x,y\in C
,
then
x
and
y
are also in
F
. It is well known that every exposed face of

C
is a face of
C
. A face that is not an exposed face is called a

nonexposed
face. If
x\in C
and
\{x\}
is a face or nonexposed face,
then
x
is called an
extreme point
or
nonexposed point
, respectively.

A subset
F
of
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
is
lift-invariant
under
\pi_{\mathcal{U}}
if

F=\pi_{\mathcal{U}}|_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})}^{-1}(\pi_{\mathcal{U}}(F))
. Note that every subset of
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})

which is uniquely determined by
\pi_{\mathcal{U}}
is lift-invariant under
\pi_{\mathcal{U}}
.

Lemma 4.9
.

A subset

is the preimage

holds for some

.

Proof.

The first statement is proved in Prop. 5.7 in

]
for faces

This and the fact that preimages of faces are faces prove the statement for
general subsets
F
. The second statement follows from
Equation (

).
∎

It was an open problem
[
12
,
13
]
whether the set of
quantum marginals can have nonexposed faces. Here, we discuss prior work

[
22
,
13
]
regarding two-body marginals of
N
qubits.
Using the notation of the Sections
4.1
and
4.3
,
we employ the algebra
\mathcal{A}_{i}=M_{2}
for each unit
i\in\Omega=\{1,2,\dots,N\}
. We
denote the linear map (
16
), which assigns marginals, by

\textstyle\mar_{N}=\mar_{({N\choose 2},\mathfrak{a}_{N,\mathrm{qu}})}

and the space of two-local
N
-qubit Hamiltonians (
17
) by

\textstyle\mathcal{U}_{N}=\mathcal{U}({N\choose 2},\mathfrak{a}_{N,\mathrm{qu}}).

Equation (
19
) shows that the map
\,\mar_{N}
factors through

\,\mathcal{U}_{N}
and that

(25)

\pi_{\mathcal{U}_{N}}(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mar_{N}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mar_{N}(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}))

is a bijection from the joint numerical range onto the set of two-body
marginals of
N
qubits.
The set
\,\mar_{6}(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}))
has a nonexposed point:

Remark 4.11

(Six Qubits)
.

Using dissipative quantum control theory,
Karuvade et al.
[
22
, Section IV.B]
discovered a pure six-qubit
state
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})
that is uniquely determined by its two-body
marginals, but which is not the unique ground state of any matrix in
\mathcal{U}_{6}
,
the space of two-local six-qubit Hamiltonians. Equation (
25
) shows
that
\rho
is uniquely determined by the projection
\pi_{\mathcal{U}_{6}}
. Hence the point

\pi_{\mathcal{U}_{6}}(\rho)
is a nonexposed point of the joint numerical range

\pi_{\mathcal{U}_{6}}(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}))
by Corollary
4.10
, and the point

\,\mar_{6}(\rho)
is a nonexposed point of the set
\,\mar_{6}(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}))

of two-body marginals of six qubits, again by Equation (
25
).

Lemma 4.12

(Three Qubits)
.

The set
\,\mar_{3}(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}))
of two-body marginals of three qubits has no
nonexposed points.

Proof.

Let
x
be an extreme point of the convex set
\,\mar_{3}(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}))
. Then

x=\mar_{3}(\rho)
is the image of an extreme point (pure state)
\rho
of the state
space
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
. First, let
\rho
be of the GHZ type

[
7
]

\ket{\mathrm{GHZ}}=\alpha\ket{000}+\beta\ket{111}

for some
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
satisfying
|\alpha|^{2}+|\beta|^{2}=1
. That is to say,

\rho=U_{1}U_{2}U_{3}.\ket{\mathrm{GHZ}}\!\!\bra{\mathrm{GHZ}}.U_{1}^{\ast}U_{2}^{\ast}U_{3}^{\ast}

for some unitaries
U_{1},U_{2},U_{3}\in U(2)
. Applying a unitary similarity, we can
take
U_{i}=I
,
i=1,2,3
, without loss of generality. Then the two-body marginals
are
\mar_{3}(\rho)=y(|\beta|^{2})
, where

y(\lambda)=(\sigma,\sigma,\sigma)\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma=(1-\lambda)\ket{00}\!\!\bra{00}+\lambda\ket{11}\!\!\bra{11},\qquad 0\leq\lambda\leq 1.

Since the segment
\{y(\lambda)\mid 0\leq\lambda\leq 1\}
lies in

\mar_{3}(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}))
and since the point
x=y(|\beta|^{2})
is an extreme
point, we have
\sigma=\ket{ii}\!\!\bra{ii}
and
\rho=\ket{iii}\!\!\bra{iii}

either for
i=0
or
i=1
. This shows that
\rho
is the unique ground state of
the two-local Hamiltonian

(I_{\{1,2\}}-\sigma)I_{\{3\}}+I_{\{1\}}(I_{\{2,3\}}-\sigma).

Second, if the pure state
\rho
is not of the GHZ type, then
\rho
is the
unique ground state of a two-local Hamiltonian, too

[
13
, Section V.A]
.

In both cases, the state
\rho
is the unique ground state of a two-local
Hamiltonian. Equation (
9
) then shows that
\pi_{\mathcal{U}_{3}}(\rho)
is
an exposed point of the joint numerical range
\pi_{\mathcal{U}_{3}}(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}))
.
Equation (
25
) proves that

\,\mar_{3}(\rho)
is an exposed point of the set of marginals

\,\mar_{3}(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}))
.
∎
