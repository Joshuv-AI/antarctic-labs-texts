Thermal equilibrium in Gaussian dynamical semigroups
Fabricio Toscano, Fernando Nicacio

Abstract
We characterize all Gaussian dynamical semigroups in continuous variables quantum systems of n n -bosonic modes which have a thermal Gibbs state as a stationary solution. This is performed through an explicit relation between the diffusion and dissipation matrices, which characterize the semigroup dynamics, and the covariance matrix of the thermal equilibrium state. We also show that Alicki’s quantum detailed-balance condition, based on a Gelfand-Naimark-Segal inner product, allows the determination of the temperature dependence of the diffusion and dissipation matrices and the identification of different Gaussian dynamical semigroups which share the same thermal equilibrium state.



I
Introduction

In modern Quantum Information Theory for continuous variable systems,
i.e
,
systems described by
n
-bosonic modes,
Gaussian channels are the standard models in most of the quantum communication
protocols
Holevo et al. 1999
;
Holevo and Werner 2001
;
Holevo 2002
;
Cerf et al. 2007
;
Caruso et al. 2008
;
Holevo 2019
.
These channels are defined as those bosonic channels which transform Gaussian states
into Gaussian states
Weedbrook et al. 2012
;
Adesso et al. 2014
.
Further, these states have an exceptional role in quantum communication as,
for example, they are optimal for the transmission of classical information
through Gaussian bosonic quantum channels with additive capacity
Wolf et al. 2006
.

The most general form of a one-parameter Gaussian channel of
n
-bosonic modes is
a Gaussian Dynamical Semigroup (GDS
)
Heinosaari et al. 2010
;
Toscano et al. 2021
, constituting thus
the tool to describe the dynamics of all memoryless continuous-in-time
Gaussian quantum channels.
This is the reason why they are widely used to describe noisy quantum channels in
continuous variable systems
Heinosaari et al. 2010
;
Giovannetti et al. 2010
;
De Palma et al. 2016
.
Further,
GDS
s are able to describe all processes which can
formally be written as decomposition and production of noninteracting particles (or quasi-particles)
which can be treated at least approximately as bosons
Alicki and Lendi 2007
.
In this context,
GDS
s are known as quasi-free completely-positive semigroups
Vanheuverzwijn 1978
;
Vanheuverzwijn 1979
;
Demoen et al. 1979
, which happens,
for example, in damped collective modes in deep inelastic collisions
Sǎndulescu and Scutaru 1987
.

The dynamics of any Quantum Dynamical Semigroup (QDS
),
not necessarily Gaussian,
also known as Quantum Markov Semigroups
Carlen and Maas 2017
,
is described by a master equation in the Lindblad form
Lindblad 1976
;
Gorini et al. 1976
.
In an analogous way, a
GDS
verifies a Lindblad master equation
where the unitary part of the evolution is set by a quadratic Hamiltonian
which describes the
n
-bosonic modes.
By a quadratic Hamiltonian, we mean one composed by products of any two canonical conjugate operators, positions and momenta,
or the set of self-adjoint operators that constitutes a representation of the Heisenberg canonical commutation relations
of the particular bosonic system considered
Tarasov 2008
.
Meanwhile, the non-unitary part is given by Lindblad operators corresponding to complex linear functions of positions and momenta,
which simplifies the non-unitary dynamics to be described only in terms of two real
2n\times 2n
matrices,
the diffusion and dissipation matrices
Nicacio et al. 2016
;
Toscano et al. 2021
.

Alternatively, the Weyl-Wigner representation
Ozorio de Almeida 1998
for the master equation of a
GDS
s
can be employed and corresponds to a linear Fokker-Planck equation for the evolution of the Wigner function
of the evolved states
Nicacio et al. 2010
;
Nicacio et al. 2016
;
Toscano et al. 2021
.
This is the quantum counterpart of the classical channels corresponding to Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes,
thus also known as Bose-Ornstein–Uhlenbeck semigroups
Carlen and Maas 2017
,
whose evolved probability distributions satisfy exactly the same Fokker-Planck equation as the
GDS
s.

Notably when the
GDS
dynamics has a steady state,
this will be a dynamically invariant Gaussian state which attracts, over long times,
the evolution of any initial condition
Frigerio 1977
;
Frigerio 1978
;
Carmichael 1999
.
Thus, for a given quadratic Hamiltonian,
the characterization of stationary situations in
GDS
s corresponds
to find all the diffusion and dissipation matrices that allow a stationary state
Toscano et al. 2021
.
Of particular importance are the stationary states which also corresponds to
thermal equilibrium states, characterized by a Gibbs state of a quadratic Hamiltonian.
This is the main subject of our study.

Thermal equilibrium in
QDS
s in finite-dimensional quantum systems has its own well-established theory
based on the so called Quantum Detailed Balance Condition (QDBC
),
as first stated by Alicki
Alicki 1976
;
Alicki and Lendi 2007
, see also
Carlen and Maas 2017
).
Of note are the results in
Carlen and Maas 2017
where the authors prove, using the
QDBC
,
that the evolution governed by a
QDS
is a gradient flow, in a particular Riemannian metric on the set of states,
for the relative entropy of a state with respect to a Gibbs state.
On the other hand, the study of thermal equilibrium in
GDS
s is scarce,
which is particularly true for the multimode scenario;
a prominent exception is the extension of the results for
QDS
s
in finite-dimensional quantum systems to the case of a one mode
GDS
performed in
Carlen and Maas 2017
.

Here we fill this gap and give a complete characterization of all
n
-mode
GDS
s with a thermal equilibrium state.
To this aim, we employ the Fokker-Planck equation for the evolution of the Wigner function
and show that the thermal probability current is always null in every phase space point.
This enable us to conclude that
GDS
s with thermal equilibrium are characterized by a set of three commuting matrices.
Two of the them are the Hamiltonian matrices associated to the covariance matrix of the thermal state and
the diffusion matrix of the
GDS
. The third one is the skew-Hamiltonian matrix associated to
the dissipation matrix of the
GDS
.
By another side,
this condition neglects that different
GDS
s, characterized by the diffusion and dissipation matrices,
may share the same thermal equilibrium state,
as a consequence of the fact that the relation among that matrices does not set their temperature dependence.
To circumvent this, we show that these characterizations
are possible by extending Alicki’s
QDBC
to bosonic-mode systems.

The extended
QDBC
leads to a master equation for a
GDS
s
with the form of a Quantum Master Optical Equation (QOME
)
Gardiner and Zoller 2000

and the temperature dependence of the diffusion and dissipation matrices is established
for this type of
GDS
s.
From this temperature characterization, we establish that all
GDS
that leads to thermal equilibrium
satisfy a
QDBC
if we allow an arbitrary temperature dependence for coupling constants
between the system and the environment.
Finally, we discriminate the Hamiltonians corresponding to the unitary part of the dynamics of a
GDS

which allow the occurrence of the thermalization. Although,
we show that the thermalization process itself is not affected by these Hamiltonians.

II.2
Wigner Function and Fokker-Planck Equation

In continuous variable systems, the following sets

IV
GDSs satisfying a detailed balance condition

The notion of detailed balance is the principle governing the way thermal equilibrium is attained
by classical Markov processes
Risken 1996

It has several different quantum versions (see
Carlen and Maas 2017
and references therein)
and in the context of
QDS
for finite-dimensional systems,
the one due to Alicki
Alicki 1976
stands out because it allows the extension
of time-reversal invariance
of classical equilibrium to the quantum realm
Carlen and Maas 2017

Inspired by the classical case in Markov processes,
where the time-reversal invariance of transition probabilities
is related to a particular definition of an inner product,
Alicki’s definition for quantum detailed balance is based on the

\hat{\bar{\rho}}
-Gelfand-Naimark-Segal (
\hat{\bar{\rho}}
-GNS
)
inner product in finite dimension Hilbert spaces:

\langle{\hat{A},\hat{B}}\rangle_{\text{GNS}}=\Tr(\hat{\bar{\rho}}\, \hat A^\dagger\hat B),

(69)

where
\hat{\bar{\rho}}
is a positive operator
8
8

8

The operator
\hat{\bar{\rho}}
in Alicki’s
work
Alicki 1976
is a full rank density operator in finite dimensional systems.
However, it is more convenient to extend
the definition of a
\hat{\bar{\rho}}
-
GNS
inner product for unnormalized density operators
\hat{\bar{\rho}}

and, in particular, to unnormalized Gibbs states like

\hat{\bar{\sigma}}^{\mathrm{th}}
in (
41
), see
Carlen and Maas 2017
.
,

\hat{\bar{\rho}}=\hat{\bar{\rho}}^{\dagger}>0

the operators
\hat{A}
and
\hat{B}
belong to a finite-dimensional C
∗
-algebra.
Thus, Alicki’s

with respect to the
\hat{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm th}
-
GNS
inner product,
where
\hat{\bar{\rho}}^{\rm th}=e^{-\beta\hat{H}}
is an unnormalized Gibbs state with Hamiltonian
\hat{H}

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\Lambda[\hat A],\hat B}_{\text{GNS}}=\expectationvalue{\hat A,\Lambda[\hat B]}_{\text{GNS}},

and
\hat{B}

∗
-algebra.

The extension of Alicki’s approach for continuous variable systems relies on a

-inner-product is well defined.

VII
The role of
\hat{H}_{\rm eff}
in thermalization

So far, we completely answer the question of which kind of environments,
characterized by the diffusion and dissipation matrices
\mathbb{D}
and
\mathbb{C}
,
allows the existence of a given thermal equilibrium state in a
GDS
.
This state is given by a Gaussian Gibbs state,

\hat{\sigma}^{\mathrm{th}}=e^{-\beta\hat{H}}/{\cal Z}^{\mathrm{th}}
,
corresponding to a quadratic Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
of positive-elliptic type, which
is completely characterized by its covariance matrix
\mathbb{V}^{\mathrm{th}}
such that
\mathsf{J}\mathbb{V}^{\mathrm{th}}

belongs to the commuting set
\{\mathsf{J}\mathbb{V}^{\mathrm{th}},\mathsf{J}\mathbb{D},\mathsf{J}\mathbb{C}\}
.

However, it is interesting to analyze this result from the perspective of a fixed
GDS

that governs the system to thermal equilibrium.
In this case, the commuting matrices
\mathsf{J}\mathbb{D}
and
\mathsf{J}\mathbb{C}
determine
\mathsf{J}\mathbb{B}

through the relation (
62
) and (
47
).
Then, it is clear that the effective action of the environment, through the non-unitary part of the evolution in (
9
),
is to confine the system in phase space (with coordinates
\mathbf{x}
)
around the the equilibrium point
\expectationvalue{\hbx}^{\mathrm{th}}=0
.
The energetic balance between the environment and the system is described by the quantum positive-elliptic Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
,
which is dynamically stable (with spectrum necessarily discrete with bounded eigenstates).
If we admit that every
GDS
that leads to thermal equilibrium satisfies a
QDBC
, the form of the master equation
in (
92
) together with (
93
) and (
94
), helps to understand in detail the
energetic balance of the thermal equilibrium process. Indeed, this process is determined by the action of the eigenoperators
\hat{X}_{j}^{\pm}
of
\hat{H}
,
which promote transitions between the energy levels of this Hamiltonian as a consequence of the interaction with the environment.
This contrasts with the usual derivation
Gardiner and Zoller 2000

of the
QOME
in (
88
),
where
\{\hat{X}_{j}^{\pm}\}
is considered as a basis of eigenoperators of
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{OME}}
,
which expands the algebra of operators acting on the system
Gardiner and Zoller 2000
.

The induced environmental confinement process of the system in thermal equilibrium
is not influenced by the unitary dynamics generated by
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}
,
since the commuting set of matrices
\{\mathsf{J}\mathbb{V}^{\mathrm{th}},\mathsf{J}\mathbb{D},\mathsf{J}\mathbb{C}\}
,
which ultimately determines the thermal equilibrium through (
62
),
do not depend on the Hessian matrix
\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
of
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}
.
However, there are some of these Hamiltonians that allows the process of thermalization to occur,
these are determined by the condition
[\hat{H},\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}]=0
.
Using Eq.(
43
) and the results in Appendix
H
,
all these Hamiltonians are written as

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{1}{2}\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{\intercal}\mathbb{B}^{\prime}\hat{\mathbf{x}}=\hat{U}_{{\mathsf{S}}^{\mathrm{th}}}^{\dagger}\hat{H}^{\prime}_{{\rm ho}}\hat{U}_{({\mathsf{S}}^{\mathrm{th}})},

(108)

where we employed the same reasoning performed in Eq.(
96
)
with
\mathbb{B}^{\prime}=({\mathsf{S}}^{\mathrm{th}})^{\intercal}(\mathbb{x}\oplus\mathbb{x}){\mathsf{S}}^{\mathrm{th}}
.
The Hamiltonian
\hat{H}^{\prime}_{{\rm ho}}
is the one of a multimode harmonic oscillator,
i.e.,

\hat{H}^{\prime}_{{\rm ho}}:=\frac{1}{2}\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{\intercal}(\mathbb{x}\oplus\mathbb{x})\hat{\mathbf{x}}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda_{j}}{2}(\hat{p}_{j}^{2}+\hat{q}_{j}^{2})

with
\mathbb{x}=\text{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
and
\lambda_{j}\in\mathds{R}
,
j=1,...,n
.

Likewise
\hat{H}
, the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}
in (
108
) is also elliptic, or dynamically stable.
However, contrary to the multimode harmonic oscillator
\hat{H}_{{\rm ho}}
in the expression (
96
),
the arbitrary frequencies
\lambda_{j}
of
\hat{H}^{\prime}_{{\rm ho}}
can be positive, negative,
or even null.
Consequently,
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}
can be the null Hamiltonian and the system still thermalizes
to the same state
\hat{\sigma}^{\mathrm{th}}
.
Note, however, that taking a null Hamiltonian is quite different of considering the interaction representation
of the
QOME
in (
88
), where the density operator turns to

\hat{\rho}_{t}^{I}=e^{\frac{\imath}{\hbar}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{OME}}t}\hat{\rho}_{t}e^{-\frac{\imath}{\hbar}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{OME}}t}
, since
the frequencies
\tilde{\omega}_{k}
still correspond to
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{OME}}
through the Planck factor
\bar{n}_{k}
.

It is worth to note that the operators
\hat{X}^{\pm}_{k}
are eigenoperators of
\hat{H}

when the master equation (
92
) of a
GDS
thermalizes, see Eq. (
93
),
but they are also eigenoperators of
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}
,
i.e.,
[\hat{H},\hat{X}_{j}^{\pm}]=\pm\hbar\lambda_{j}\hat{X}_{j}^{\pm}
with
j=1,\ldots,n
.
Nevertheless, the frequencies
\omega_{k}
in the master equation (
92
) are associated with
\hat{H}
.
Thus, the energetic balance of the thermalization process is associated
with transitions in the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
associated with the confinement of the system.

VIII
Conclusions

When a
GDS
has a thermal equilibrium state
\hat{\sigma}^{\mathrm{th}}
,
every initial condition of the evolution ends up on this state.
Without lost of generality, we can choose
\expectationvalue{\hbx}^{\mathrm{th}}=0
,
so the thermal state
\hat{\sigma}^{\mathrm{th}}
must have the form in (
41
) with

\hat{H}
in (
42
), and the Hamiltonian of the unitary part of the evolution for the GDS given by

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}
, of the form in (
44
) such that
\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}
or
[\hat{H},\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}]=0
.
Another prerequisite for
\hat{\sigma}^{\mathrm{th}}
came from the normalization of a density operator:
in order to have unity trace, the Hessian matrix
\mathbb{B}
of
\hat{H}
must be positive definite
Nicacio 2021b
.
This means that
\hat{H}
is elliptic,
i.e.,
its classical counterpart in (
54
) generates a Hamiltonian flow
given by the symplectic matrix
\tilde{\mathsf{S}}_{t}
in (
55
) around the elliptic fixed point
\mathbf{x}=0
.
Therefore,
\hat{H}
is dynamically stable having a discrete energy spectrum corresponding to bound states.

The non-unitary part of the evolution of a
GDS
is determined by the diffusion and dissipation matrices,

\mathbb{D}
and
\mathbb{C}
, respectively.
These are the real and imaginary parts, respectively, of the decoherence matrix
\mathbb{\Gamma}
, see Eq.(
11
).
In (
62
), we show the relation between the covariance matrix
\mathbb{V}^{\mathrm{th}}
of
\hat{\sigma}^{\mathrm{th}}

and the matrices
\mathbb{D}
and
\mathbb{C}
for every
GDS
with a thermal equilibrium state,
which has appeared before in
Toscano et al. 2021
in a narrower context.
Further, we show that
\mathbb{V}^{\mathrm{th}}
is completely determined by the symplectic matrix
{\mathsf{S}}^{\mathrm{th}}
in (
49
),
which diagonalizes symplectically the Hessian matrix
\mathbb{B}
as shown in (
51
),
and by the symplectic spectrum of
\mathbb{B}
, which contains the eigenfrequencies
\omega_{j}>0

of the system Hamiltonian.

However, Eq.(
62
) does not establish neither the temperature dependence of
\mathbb{D}
and
\mathbb{C}
, nor
characterize all the different GDSs that have the same thermal equilibrium state
\hat{\sigma}^{\mathrm{th}}
.
This deficiency can be fixed extending Alicki’s
QDBC
(developed for systems described by discrete Hilbert spaces)
to
n
-mode bosonic systems.
We have shown how to implement this extension and the result is that
all GDSs verifying a
QDBC
have a thermal equilibrium state whose dynamics is
characterized by symplectically invariant diffusion and dissipation matrices,
see Eq.(
72
).
This condition allows the characterization of the Lindblad operators of a

GDS
satifying a
QDBC
in Theorem
5
.

The corresponding master equation
has the structure of a Quantum Optical Master Equation (QOME),
see Eq. (
92
) together with Eqs. (
93
) and (
94
).
As a consequence, the matrices
\mathbb{D}
and
\mathbb{C}
have the specific structure in (
101
),
which sets explicitly their dependence on temperature.
These expressions also show that different GDSs with the same thermal equilibrium state
differ from each other only on the value of the coupling constants,
namely
\bar{\gamma}_{j}
in (
94
),
because
{\mathsf{S}}^{\mathrm{th}}
is determined by the symplectic diagonalization of
\mathbb{B}
,
i.e.,
the Hessian matrix of
\hat{H}
.
Usually the coupling constants
\bar{\gamma}_{j}
do not depend on temperature, however we show that
if we allow these to have an arbitrary temperature dependence,
then it is possible to say that every
GDS
with thermal equilibrium satisfies a
QDBC
.

We also have shown that it is possible to define a pure diffusive regime
for any
GDS
satisfying the
QDBC
,
where the
GDS
lost its stationary solution.
This is a specific example of the necessity to balance diffusion and dissipation to achieve a
stationary solution in any quantum dynamical semigroup
Toscano et al. 2021
.

Finally, we also show that the contribution of
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}
to the dynamics of a
GDS

has no influence in the thermalization process. However, by determining all
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}
such that

[\hat{H},\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}]=0
, we have determined all Hamiltonians that allows thermalization.
These Hamiltonians are also dynamically stable as
\hat{H}
,
however with arbitrary eigenenergy frequencies.
Therefore, the energetic balance of the thermalization process,
determined by
\hat{H}
, is completely independent of
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}
.
This marks a fundamental difference between the
QOME
for a
GDS
satisfying
a
QDBC
and the usual
QOME

Gardiner and Zoller 2000
.

Appendix A
Proof of Lemma
1

Let us prove first that
\mathbb{D}>0
under the hypothesis of the Lemma.
Since
\mathbb{C}
is antisymmetric (
\mathbb{C}=-\mathbb{C}^{\intercal}
) and invertible,
the spectral theorem
Horn and Johnson 1990
guarantees that there is an unitary diagonalizing matrix
\mathbb{U}
, such that

\mathbb{U}\mathbb{C}\mathbb{U}^{\dagger}=(-\imath\mathbb{c})\oplus(\imath\mathbb{c})=\mathbb{C}^{\prime}
,
where
\mathbb{c}=\mbox{diag}(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})
,
with
c_{i}>0
, for
i=1,\ldots,n
.
Thus, using (
13
) we have
\mathbb{U}\hbar\mathbb{\Gamma}\mathbb{U}^{\dagger}=\mathbb{D}^{\prime}+\mathbb{C}^{\prime}\geq 0
, where
\mathbb{D}^{\prime}=\mathbb{U}\mathbb{D}\mathbb{U}^{\dagger}
and

\mathbb{C}^{\prime}=(\hbar\mathbb{c})\oplus(-\hbar\mathbb{c})
.
Now consider the matrix
\mathbb{\Gamma}^{\intercal}
and apply the same unitary transformation,
reaching
\mathbb{U}\mathbb{\Gamma}^{\intercal}\mathbb{U}^{\dagger}=\mathbb{D}^{\prime}-\mathbb{C}^{\prime}\geq 0
,
which is positive-semidefinite, since
\mathbb{\Gamma}^{\intercal}
is Hermitian and has the same
eigenvalues of
\mathbb{\Gamma}
.
For a complex
2n
-dimensional vector
\bf z
,
the above matrix inequalities are equivalent to

{\bf z}^{\dagger}\mathbb{D}^{\prime}{\bf z}\pm{\bf z}^{\dagger}\mathbb{C}^{\prime}{\bf z}\geq 0
,
for all vectors
{\bf z}
, which implies
{\bf z}^{\dagger}\mathbb{D}^{\prime}{\bf z}>0
, thus
\mathbb{D}>0
.

To prove that
\mathbb{\Gamma}>0
, it is enough to use that
{\bf z}^{\dagger}\mathbb{C}{\bf z}=0
for all vectors
\bf z
,
since
\mathbb{C}
is antisymmetric.
Consequently,
\hbar{\bf z}^{\dagger}\mathbb{\Gamma}{\bf z}={\bf z}^{\dagger}\mathbb{D}{\bf z}
,
since
\mathbb{D}>0
under the hypothesis of the in Sec.
VIII
, thus
\mathbb{\Gamma}>0
.
This finishes the proof.

Observing the particular case where
\mathbb{C}=0
, one has
\mathbb{\Gamma}>0
iff
\mathbb{D}>0
,
which shows that the converse of the Lemma is not true.

{P}_{n{{}_{j}}\pm 1}=\langle\phi_{{\bf n}_{j}^{\pm}}|\hat{\sigma}^{\mathrm{th}}|\phi_{{\bf n}_{j}^{\pm}}\rangle
. As can be directly checked, the solution is

{P}_{n{{}_{j}}}=\frac{\hbar\bar{\gamma}_{j}}{|s_{j}|^{2}}e^{-\hbar\beta\omega_{j}n_{j}}.

(129)

The constant
\bar{\gamma}_{j}
is included in order to
{\hbar\bar{\gamma}_{j}}/{|s_{j}|^{2}}
be dimensionless.
Comparing Eqs. (
129
) and (
128
) we arrive to (
94
).

Appendix H
The algebra of commuting elliptic Hamiltonian matrices

In this appendix we will prove the following theorem:

Theorem 6

Consider a positive definite symmetric matrix
\mathbb{B}
and a symmetric matrix
\mathbb{B}^{\prime}

such that
[\mathsf{J}\mathbb{B},\mathsf{J}\mathbb{B}^{\prime}]=0
, then

\mathsf{J}\mathbb{B}^{\prime}=(\mathbb{Q}\mathsf{S})^{-1}\,\imath\mathbb{x}\oplus(-\imath\mathbb{x})\,(\mathbb{Q}\mathsf{S}),

(130)

where
\mathbb{Q}
is defined in (
26
),
\mathbb{x}:={\rm{diag}}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
with

\lambda_{i}\in\mathds{R}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
,
and
\mathsf{S}
is a symplectic matrix that diagonalizes symplectically
\mathbb{B}
, i.e.,

\mathsf{S}^{-\intercal}\mathbb{B}\mathsf{S}=\mathbb{w}\oplus\mathbb{w},\,\,\,\mathbb{w}={\rm{diag}}(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n})

(131)

for
\omega_{i}>0
and
i=1,\ldots,n
.
Further, Eq.(
130
) is equivalent to a “symplectic diagonalization”
of
\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
:

\mathsf{S}^{-\intercal}\mathbb{B}^{\prime}\mathsf{S}^{-1}=\mathbb{x}\oplus\mathbb{x}.

(132)

Let
\tilde{\mathsf{S}}
be a symplectic matrix such that
\tilde{\mathsf{S}}^{-\intercal}\mathbb{B}\tilde{\mathsf{S}}=\mathbb{w}\oplus\mathbb{w}
,
then

\displaystyle[\mathsf{J}\mathbb{B},\mathsf{J}\mathbb{B}^{\prime}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[\mathsf{J}{\tilde{\mathsf{S}}}^{\intercal}(\mathbb{w}\oplus\mathbb{w})\tilde{\mathsf{S}},\mathsf{J}\mathbb{B}^{\prime}]

(133)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathsf{J}\tilde{\mathsf{S}}^{\intercal}\,[\mathsf{J}(\mathbb{w}\oplus\mathbb{w}),{\tilde{\mathsf{S}}}^{-\intercal}\mathbb{B}^{\prime}{\tilde{\mathsf{S}}}^{-1}]\,{\tilde{\mathsf{S}}},

where we used the symplectic condition

{\tilde{\mathsf{S}}}\mathsf{J}=\mathsf{J}{\tilde{\mathsf{S}}}^{-\intercal}
and

\mathsf{J}(\mathbb{w}\oplus\mathbb{w})=(\mathbb{w}\oplus\mathbb{w})\mathsf{J}
.
Writing

\tilde{\mathbb{B}}:={\tilde{\mathsf{S}}}^{-\intercal}\mathbb{B}^{\prime}{\tilde{\mathsf{S}}}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\mathbb{x}}&\tilde{\mathbb{y}}\\
\tilde{\mathbb{y}}^{\intercal}&\tilde{\mathbb{z}}\\
\end{pmatrix},

where
\tilde{\mathbb{x}},\tilde{\mathbb{y}}
and
\tilde{\mathbb{z}}
are
n\times n

real matrices such that
\tilde{\mathbb{x}}=\tilde{\mathbb{x}}^{\intercal}
and
\mathbb{z}=\mathbb{z}^{\intercal}
,
and using the commutation relation
[\mathsf{J}\mathbb{B},\mathsf{J}\mathbb{B}^{\prime}]=0
in (
133
) one attains

\tilde{\mathbb{y}}^{\top}=-\mathbb{w}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbb{y}}\mathbb{w}=-\mathbb{w}\tilde{\mathbb{y}}\mathbb{w}^{-1}\,\,\,{\text{and}}\,\,\,\tilde{\mathbb{z}}=\mathbb{w}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbb{x}}\mathbb{w}=\mathbb{w}\tilde{\mathbb{x}}\mathbb{w}^{-1};

consequently,
[\tilde{\mathbb{y}},\mathbb{w}]=[\tilde{\mathbb{x}},\mathbb{w}]=0
,
thus
\tilde{\mathbb{x}}=\tilde{\mathbb{z}}
and

\tilde{\mathbb{y}}
is skew-symmetric
\tilde{\mathbb{y}}^{\intercal}=-\tilde{\mathbb{y}}
.
All of these relations enable us to write

\tilde{\mathbb{B}}:={\tilde{\mathsf{S}}}^{-\intercal}\mathbb{B}^{\prime}{\tilde{\mathsf{S}}}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\mathbb{x}}&\tilde{\mathbb{y}}\\
-\tilde{\mathbb{y}}&\tilde{\mathbb{x}}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(134)

Multiplying by
\mathsf{J}
from left, considering the symplecticity of
\tilde{\mathsf{S}}
, and using
\mathbb{Q}
,
last equation is equivalently rewritten as

(\mathbb{Q}\tilde{\mathsf{S}})\,\mathsf{J}\mathbb{B}^{\prime}\,(\mathbb{Q}\tilde{\mathsf{S}})^{-1}=\mathbb{Q}\mathsf{J}\tilde{\mathbb{B}}\mathbb{Q}^{\dagger}=(\imath\tilde{\mathbb{x}}-\tilde{\mathbb{y}})\oplus(-\imath\tilde{\mathbb{x}}-\tilde{\mathbb{y}}).

(135)

The above particular block structure is a consequence of
the degenerated structure of the diagonal matrix
\mathbb{w}\oplus\mathbb{w}
,
where each diagonal element is at least doubly-degenerated.

The two blocks in the matrix of rightmost equality in (
135
) are skew-Hermitian
and moreover they are complex conjugate of each other.
Recalling that a skew-Hermitian matrix has pure imaginary (possibly null)
eigenvalues and is unitarily diagonalizable
Horn and Johnson 2013
,
then there exists an unitary matrix
\mathbb{u}
such that

\mathbb{u}(\imath\tilde{\mathbb{x}}-\tilde{\mathbb{y}})\mathbb{u}^{\dagger}=\imath\mathbb{x}
,
where
\mathbb{x}=\text{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})

is the diagonal matrix containing the eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}\in\mathds{R}

of the matrix
\imath\tilde{\mathbb{x}}-\tilde{\mathbb{y}}
.

Defining
\mathsf{S}=\mathsf{R}\,\tilde{\mathsf{S}}
with

\mathsf{R}=\mathbb{Q}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{u}&0\\
0&\mathbb{u}^{*}\\
\end{pmatrix}\mathbb{Q}^{\dagger}\,\,\in{\rm Sp}(2n,\mathds{R})\;\cap\;{\rm O}(2n)

(136)

a real symplectic and orthogonal matrix, from (
135
) we can write

\mathbb{Q}\mathsf{S}\;\mathsf{J}\mathbb{B}^{\prime}\;(\mathbb{Q}\mathsf{S})^{-1}=\,\imath\mathbb{x}\oplus(-\imath\mathbb{x}),

(137)

which is Eq.(
130
). Since
\mathsf{S}
is symplectic and using that
\mathbb{Q}^{\dagger}\,(\imath\mathbb{x}\oplus(-\imath\mathbb{x}))\mathbb{Q}=\mathsf{J}(\mathbb{x}\oplus\mathbb{x})
, we immediately recover Eq.(
132
) from (
137
).

However it remains to prove that
\mathsf{S}
satisfies (
131
).
If the symplectic spectrum in
\mathbb{w}
is non-degenerate,
conditions
[\tilde{\mathbb{y}},\mathbb{w}]=[\tilde{\mathbb{x}},\mathbb{w}]=0
,
\tilde{\mathbb{x}}^{\intercal}=\tilde{\mathbb{x}}
, and
\tilde{\mathbb{y}}^{\intercal}=-\tilde{\mathbb{y}}

imply
\tilde{\mathbb{x}}=\mathbb{x}=\text{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
and
\mathbb{y}=0
, so one can choose
\mathbb{u}=\mathbb{1}

such that
\mathsf{R}=\mathbb{1}
and
\mathsf{S}=\tilde{\mathsf{S}}
;
consequently
\mathsf{S}
satisfies (
131
), as claimed.
When the symplectic spectrum in
\mathbb{w}
is degenerate,

\tilde{\mathbb{x}}
is diagonal outside the degenerate subspaces of
\mathbb{w}
, while

\mathbb{y}
is null outside the same subspaces. Therefore,
the unitary matrix
\mathbb{u}
have to diagonalize
\imath\tilde{\mathbb{x}}-\tilde{\mathbb{y}}
only inside the degenerate subspaces of
\mathbb{w}
.
This is possible choosing
\mathbb{u}
block diagonal such
\mathbb{u}\imath\tilde{\mathbb{x}}-\tilde{\mathbb{y}}\mathbb{u}^{\dagger}=\imath\mathbb{x}
. In this case
we also have that
\mathsf{R}^{\intercal}\mathbb{w}\oplus\mathbb{w}\,\mathsf{R}=\mathbb{w}\oplus\mathbb{w}
, and
therefore
\mathsf{S}=\mathsf{R}\,\tilde{\mathsf{S}}
also diagonalize symplectically
\mathbb{B}
,

i.e.,

\mathbb{B}=\mathsf{S}^{\intercal}\mathbb{w}\oplus\mathbb{w}\mathsf{S}=\tilde{\mathsf{S}}^{\intercal}\mathsf{R}^{\intercal}\mathbb{w}\oplus\mathbb{w}\,\mathsf{R}\tilde{\mathsf{S}}=\tilde{\mathsf{S}}^{\intercal}\mathbb{w}\oplus\mathbb{w}\,\tilde{\mathsf{S}},

(138)

which shows that
\mathsf{S}
satisfies (
131
) for the degenerate case, with this we finish the proof of the theorem.
