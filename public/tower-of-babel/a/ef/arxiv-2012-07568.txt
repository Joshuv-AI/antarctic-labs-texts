Introduction

In modern times polynomial factorization has become a fundamental part of computer algebra systems. The problem of factoring a polynomial into lower degree polynomials has a long and renowned history. D. Knuth
[
62
]
traces back to Isaac Newton who first attempted to factor a polynomial of arbitrary degree. In
Arithmetica Universalis
(1707), Newton gave a method to find linear and quadratic factors of a polynomial with integer coefficients. Since then several mathematicians attempted to find irreducibility criteria for different kinds of polynomials and many other tried to find significant properties of their factors when reducible. For example, knowing one of its factors is a cyclotomic polynomial facilitates us to reduce the computational time while checking the reducibility of a family of polynomials. Before defining the cyclotomic polynomial, recall that the reciprocal of a polynomial
f(x)=a_{n}x^{n}+\cdots+a_{1}x+a_{0}
of degree
n
is defined as
\tilde{f}(x)=x^{n}f\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)=a_{0}x^{n}+a_{1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{n}.
A polynomial
f(x)
is said to be a
reciprocal polynomial
if
f(x)=\pm\tilde{f}(x)
, otherwise it is called
non-reciprocal.
It follows from the definition that if
f(x)
is a reciprocal polynomial,
f(0)\neq 0
, then
f(x)
is reducible if and only if
\tilde{f}(x)
is reducible. If
\alpha
is a nonzero root of a reciprocal polynomial
f(x)
, then
\frac{1}{\alpha}
is also a root of
f(x)
. One of the widely studied reciprocal families is the family of cyclotomic polynomials. The
n^{\text{th}}
cyclotomic polynomial is defined as

\Phi_{n}(x)=\underset{(j,n)=1}{\prod\limits_{1\leq j\leq n}}\left(x-e^{\frac{2\pi ij}{n}}\right),i^{2}=-1.

The irreducibility problem of binomials
ax^{n}+b,a,b\in{\mathbb{Q}}
can be converted to the irreducibility of binomials of the form
x^{n}-a,a\in{\mathbb{Q}}
. It has been resolved completely. The criterion is now known as Capelli’s theorem due to Capelli
[
22
]
.

Theorem 1

(
[
22
]
,
[
91
]
,
[
115
]
,
[
63
]
)
.

Let
n\geq 2,a\in{\mathbb{Q}}
. The polynomial
x^{n}-a
is reducible over
{\mathbb{Q}}
if and only if either
a=b^{t}
for some
t|n,t>1
or
4|n,a=-4b^{4}
for some

.

Naturally, the question arises about the irreducibility criterion for trinomials
f(x)=ax^{n}+bx^{m}+c,
where
a\in{\mathbb{N}},

b,c\in{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus\{\,0\,\}
and
(a,b,c)=1
. Starting from Eisenstein, many irreducibility criteria appeared for different types of trinomials. Yet the question is not fully solved. There is no single criterion which fits to every trinomial. Although several results appeared over time, to the best of our knowledge there is no survey article on this particular topic. Therefore, in this article, we tried to collect an exhaustive list of the irreducibility criteria for trinomials available in the literature.

We compiled the existing irreducibility criteria of trinomials in section
2

3

Due to the diversity and intricacies of the theory applied in the polynomial factorization, we will concentrate on irreducibility criteria over rational numbers only. Unless specified, throughout the article, we will assume that all the polynomials have content
1
. By this assumption, the irreducibility over
{\mathbb{Q}}
and irreducibility over
{\mathbb{Z}}
become the same. Further, by

2
Reducibility of trinomials

This section is divided into various subsections depending upon the irreducibility criteria, which apply to a particular type of trinomials. We will first discuss those families, whose reducibility nature has been resolved completely. Later we proceed to compile the results providing a partial answer to the question of the irreducibility of trinomials.

2.1
Reducibility of
\boldsymbol{x^{n}+b{\epsilon}_{1}x^{m}+{\epsilon}_{2}}

Let
n,m,b\in{\mathbb{N}}
and let
f(x)=x^{n}+b{\epsilon}_{1}x^{m}+{\epsilon}_{2}
be a trinomial of degree
n,{\epsilon}_{1},{\epsilon}_{2}\in\{\,-1,+1\,\}
. The polynomial
f(x)
is reducible if and only if
{\epsilon}_{2}\tilde{f}(x)=x^{n}+b{\epsilon}_{1}{\epsilon}_{2}x^{n-m}+{\epsilon}_{2}
is reducible. Thus, it is sufficient to check the reducibility of
x^{n}+b{\epsilon}_{1}x^{m}+{\epsilon}_{2}
for
1\leq m\leq\frac{n}{2}
.

2.1.1
Reducibility of
\boldsymbol{x^{n}+b{\epsilon}_{1}x+{\epsilon}_{2}}

Perron
[
87
]
is the first mathematician who gave an irreducibility criterion of a polynomial depending upon the magnitude of the coefficients. He proved that,

Perron’s criterion
[
87
]
:
Let
f(x)=x^{n}+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{1}x+a_{0}
be a monic polynomial with integer coefficients and
a_{0}\neq 0
. If

|a_{n-1}|>1+|a_{n-2}|+\cdots+|a_{1}|+|a_{0}|,

then
f(x)
is irreducible over
{\mathbb{Z}}
.

By using Perron’s criterion, the polynomial
{\epsilon}_{2}\tilde{f}(x)=x^{n}+b{\epsilon}_{1}{\epsilon}_{2}x^{n-1}+{\epsilon}_{2}
, and hence
f(x)=x^{n}+b{\epsilon}_{1}x+{\epsilon}_{2}
is irreducible for every
b\geq 3
. If
b
is either
1
or
2
, then there are polynomials which are reducible. For example,

\displaystyle x^{3}-2x+1=(x-1)(x^{2}+x-1);

\displaystyle x^{8}+{\epsilon}x+1=(x^{2}+{\epsilon}x+1)(x^{6}-{\epsilon}x^{5}+{\epsilon}x^{3}-x^{2}+1).

Selmer
[
104
]
came across the question of reducibility of trinomials of the form
x^{n}\pm x\pm 1
while studying continued fractions. With a change of sign in
x
, it is sufficient to consider
x^{n}+x+1
and
x^{n}-x-1
only. For example, if
n
is odd and
f(x)=x^{n}-x+1
, then
-f(-x)=x^{n}-x-1.
Hence, if
n
is odd, then
f(x)
is reducible if and only if
-f(-x)
is reducible and so on.

If
\alpha_{i}
is a root of
f(x)=x^{n}+{\epsilon}(x+1)
, then Selmer considered the following sum

S_{f}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\left(\alpha_{i}-\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}}\right).

Since
S_{f}
is a symmetric function of roots of
f
,
S_{f}\in{\mathbb{Q}},
and
S_{f}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
for
f(x)=x^{n}+{\epsilon}(x+1)
. As any factor of
f(x)
over
{\mathbb{Z}}
has a constant term
\pm 1,
reducibility of
f(x)
over
{\mathbb{Z}}
yields an integer partition of
S_{f}
. Using this fact, Selmer proved that

Theorem 2

(Selmer
[
104
]
)
.

The polynomial
x^{n}+{\epsilon}(x+1)
is irreducible except for
{\epsilon}=1
and
n\equiv 2\pmod{3}
. If
{\epsilon}=1
and
n\equiv 2\pmod{3}
, then
x^{n}+x+1=\Phi_{3}(x)g(x)
, where
g(x)
is either identically
1
or an irreducible polynomial.

Selmer attempted to answer the reducibility problem of trinomials
x^{n}+{\epsilon}_{1}x^{m}+{\epsilon}_{2}
for arbitrary values of
m
. By considering the sum

S_{f}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}(\alpha_{i}^{m}-\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}^{m}}),

where
\alpha_{i}
is a root of
x^{n}+{\epsilon}_{1}x^{m}+{\epsilon}_{2}
, he gave an upper bound for the number of irreducible factors of it.

Theorem 3

(Selmer
[
104
]
)
.

Let

and
f(x)=x^{n}+{\epsilon}_{1}x^{m}+{\epsilon}_{2}
be a polynomial of degree

. Then all the roots of
f(x)
which lie on the unit circle are roots of unity and
\Phi_{3}(x^{d})
divides
f(x)
only when
n_{1}+m_{1}\equiv 0\pmod{3}.
Apart from cyclotomic factors,
f(x)
has at most
m
irreducible factors, each is of degree greater than
5
for

.

If
b=2
, then a partial answer can be drawn from the work of Schinzel
[
93
]
on the trinomial

. However for complete answer one can refer either the works of Harrington

(see Theorem

(see Theorem

below). The trinomial

factors as

for some irreducible polynomial

and

. If
n=2,
then
x^{2}+2{\epsilon}_{1}x+{\epsilon}_{2}
is either irreducible or has a cyclotomic factor. Combiningly we have

Theorem 4
.

Let
b\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
f(x)=x^{n}+b{\epsilon}_{1}x+{\epsilon}_{2}
be a trinomial of degree

. Then the followings are true.

(a)

If

is irreducible.

(b)

If

is reducible if and only if it factor as

(A)

\boldsymbol{b\leq 2}

Let

One can get an upper bound on the number of irreducible factors of

considered Newton’s formula for power sum of roots and proved a necessary and sufficient condition for the reducibility of

Theorem 5

(Tverberg
[

]
)
.

Suppose

is a polynomial of degree

. Then

is irreducible if and only if
f(x)
has no root on the unit circle. If
f(x)
has a root on the unit circle, then it is the roots of unity and the other factor of

is irreducible.

In the same year, Ljunggren
[
72

developed an ingenious tool to study the behavior of the factors of a polynomial

having a small Euclidean norm. If
f(x)=a_{n}x^{n}+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{1}x+a_{0}
, then the

of

is defined as

\|f\|_{2}=\left(a_{n}^{2}+a_{n-1}^{2}+\cdots+a_{1}^{2}+a_{0}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Suppose
f(x)=f_{1}(x)f_{2}(x)
is a non-trivial factorization of
f(x)
. Ljunggren considered the polynomials
g(x)=\tilde{f}_{1}(x)f_{2}(x)
and
\tilde{g}(x)
so that
g(x)\tilde{g}(x)=f(x)\tilde{f}(x)
. If the value of
\|f\|_{2}^{2}
is small, then one can effectively compute the nature of the factors of
f
. It is interesting to note that
\|f\|^{2}_{2}
is the coefficient of
x^{n}
in
f(x)\tilde{f}(x)
.

Ljunggren applied this method to study trinomials and quadrinomials of the form
x^{n}+{\epsilon}_{1}x^{m}+{\epsilon}_{2}
and
x^{n}+{\epsilon}_{1}x^{m}+{\epsilon}_{2}x^{r}+{\epsilon}_{3}
, respectively. For several other applications of this method, readers can refer
[

]
,
[

]
,
[

]
,
[

]
,
[

]
,
[

. In the case of trinomials, Ljunggren proved that

Theorem 6

(Ljunggren
[
72
]
)
.

If
n=n_{1}d,m=m_{1}d,(n_{1},m_{1})=1,n\geq 2m,
then the polynomial

f(x)=x^{n}+{\epsilon}_{1}x^{m}+{\epsilon}_{2},

is irreducible, apart from the following three cases, where
n_{1}+m_{1}\equiv 0\pmod{3}
:

n_{1},m_{1}
both odd,
{\epsilon}_{1}=1
;
n_{1}
even,
{\epsilon}_{2}=1
;
m_{1}
even,
{\epsilon}_{1}={\epsilon}_{2}
.

In these three cases,

is the product of the polynomial

and a second irreducible polynomial.

However, the above factor is not correct in all cases. For example, by Theorem
6

has factor
x^{4}+x^{2}+1
but they are divisible by
x^{4}-x^{2}+1
. More generally, if

is even,
d_{1}\equiv 5\pmod{6}
,

and

is odd, then the polynomials
x^{dd_{1}}+x^{d}-1,x^{dd_{2}}-x^{2d}-1
and
x^{2^{d_{3}}d}-x^{d}+1
are divisible by
x^{2d}+x^{d}+1
by Theorem

. But they are divisible by
x^{2d}-x^{d}+1
. One can check that Ljunggren’s result is true only when
{\epsilon}_{1}={\epsilon}_{2}=1
. We corrected these errors in
[

and proved that

Theorem 7

(Koley & Reddy
[

Let

and

where

If
{\epsilon}_{1}+{\epsilon}_{2}\neq 2

is reducible, then
q>b

{\epsilon}_{1}=-{\epsilon}_{2}=1,a=p

(b)

{\epsilon}_{1}=-1,a{\epsilon}_{2}<p{\epsilon}_{2}

In each of these cases, the cyclotomic part of

f_{c}(x)=\Phi_{6}(x^{(n,m)})

[

studied the polynomials of the form

(Schinzel

Let

(x^{(n,m)}-1)g(x)

\displaystyle x^{7k}-2x^{2k}+1=(x^{k}-1)(x^{3k}+x^{2k}+1)(x^{3k}+x^{k}+1);

\displaystyle x^{7k}-2x^{5k}+1=(x^{k}-1)(x^{3k}+x^{2k}+1)(x^{3k}-x^{k}-1),

for every

.

If

Let

\displaystyle x^{13}-3x^{4}+1=(x^{3}-x^{2}+1)(x^{10}+x^{9}+x^{8}-x^{6}-2x^{5}-2x^{4}-x^{3}+x^{2}+1);

Suppose

(Nagell

Location of zeros of trinomials

The location of zeros of a polynomial sometimes helps us in determining the irreducibility of the polynomial. For example, if a monic polynomial
f(x)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x],f(0)\neq 0
has exactly one root in

f(x)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x],f(0)\neq 0

k

|z|<1

f(x)
can have at most
k
irreducible factors. In this section, we will discuss about the location of zeros of trinomials. Unless specified otherwise, throughout the section. it is assumed that all the coefficients are non-zero real numbers. Kennedy
[
61
]
specified the region in which all the roots of the trinomial

Theorem 36

(Kennedy
[

]
)

If

\alpha\leq|z|\leq\beta

However, finding a positive real root of

(Kennedy
[

]

respectively, then the roots of
x^{n}+bx^{m}+c
lie in the annulus
\alpha<|z|<\beta
.

4
Discriminant of trinomials

Suppose
f(x)=a_{n}x^{n}+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{1}x+a_{0}\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]
is a polynomial of degree
n
. Let
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
be the roots of
f(x)
. The
discriminant
of
f(x)
is defined as

D_{f}=a_{n}^{2n-2}\prod\limits_{i<j}(\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j})^{2}=(-1)^{\binom{n}{2}}a_{n}^{2n-2}\prod\limits_{i\neq j}(\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j}).

The quantity
(-1)^{\binom{n}{2}}a_{n}D_{f}
is called the
resultant
of
f(x)
. More precisely, the resultant of
f(x)
, sometimes denoted by
\operatorname{Res}(f,f^{\prime})
, is defined as

\operatorname{Res}(f,f^{\prime})=(-1)^{\binom{n}{2}}a_{n}^{2n-1}\prod\limits_{i<j}(\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j})^{2}=a_{n}^{2n-1}\prod\limits_{i\neq j}(\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j}),

where
f^{\prime}(x)=na_{n}x^{n-1}+(n-1)a_{n-1}x^{n-2}+\cdots+a_{1}
is the formal derivative of
f(x)
.

From the definition, it follows that
f(x)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]
has multiple roots if and only if
D_{f}=0
. If
f(x)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]
has multiple roots, then
f(x)
is said to be a
non-separable
polynomial over
{\mathbb{Q}}
. Otherwise, it is said to be a
separable
polynomial over
{\mathbb{Q}}
.

There is another concept, called the discriminant of an algebraic number field. Suppose
{\mathbb{K}}
is an algebraic number field and
{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{K}}}
is the ring of integers of
{\mathbb{K}}
. Let
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n}
be an integral basis of
{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{K}}}
and let
\{\,\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n}\,\}
be the set of embeddings of
{\mathbb{K}}
into
{\mathbb{C}}
. Then the
discriminant
of
{\mathbb{K}}
, denoted by
\Delta_{{\mathbb{K}}}
, is defined as

\Delta_{{\mathbb{K}}}=\det(B)^{2},\,\,\qquad\mbox{ where $B=(\sigma_{i}(b_{j}))_{i,j}$.}

Example
.

Let
d
be a square-free integer and let
{\mathbb{K}}={\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{d})
be a quadratic field. Then the discriminant of
{\mathbb{K}}
is (see
[
74
]
for details)

\Delta_{{\mathbb{K}}}=\begin{cases}d,&\mbox{ if $d\equiv 1\pmod{4};$}\\
4d,&\mbox{ if $d\equiv 2,3\pmod{4}.$}\end{cases}

The discriminant of a polynomial and discriminant of an algebraic number field are closely related. Let
f(x)
be a monic irreducible polynomial with a root
\alpha
and
{\mathbb{K}}={\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)
. Then the discriminant of
f(x)
is

D_{f}=i(\alpha)^{2}\Delta_{{\mathbb{K}}},\,\,\qquad\mbox{ where $i(\alpha)=\#[{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{K}}}:{\mathbb{Z}}[\alpha]].$}

If
D_{f}
is square-free, then
i(\alpha)^{2}=1
would imply that
{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{K}}}={\mathbb{Z}}[\alpha],
and hence
{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{K}}}
has a basis in powers of
\alpha
. In this case,
{\mathbb{K}}
is said to be a
monogenic field
. There are several advantages of knowing a field to be monogenic. For example, let
{\mathbb{K}}
be a monogenic field. Then the Galois closure
{\mathbb{L}}
of
{\mathbb{K}}
has Galois group
S_{n}
and
{\mathbb{L}}
is everywhere unramified over its subfield
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{D_{f}})
. Therefore, the knowledge of the square-freeness of the discriminant of a polynomial is useful. However, it is very difficult to provide a formula for the discriminant of a polynomial with more than three non-zero terms. To the best of our knowledge,
only quadrinomials for which the discriminant is known as of today are of the form
x^{n}+t(x^{2}+bx+c)
(see
[
