Chapter 14
Hypersurface singularities

The group of diffeomorphisms
Diff(M)
acts on the space of smooth functions
C^{\infty}(M)
on a manifold
M
and
the partition of the function space into orbits is of considerable interest.
If we fix a point
p\in M
, we can look at the local version of this problem: we consider
germs of functions at
p
, and look at the action of the sub-group of diffeomorphisms that
fix
p
. If we introduce coordinates
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}

for
M
at
p
, we are dealing with germs of functions
f(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
and the group
of coordinate transformations preserving the origin. Here we will study the related actions
on formal and convergent power series. Our presentation will be rather brief, as this material
is covered at many places in much more detail. The main purpose is to illustrate the fact that
the formal arguments can be lifted directly to the level of Kolmogorov spaces to give a
corresponding result for convergent series.

14.1.
Formal finite determinacy

We fix a field
K
of characteristic
0
(most often
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
) and consider the
K
-algebra

R=K[[x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}]]

of formal power series. It is a local ring with maximal ideal

{\mathcal{M}}:=\{f\in R:f(0)=0\}

consisting of series without a constant term.
The powers of the maximal ideal form a separated filtration on
R
i.e.:

\displaystyle R\supset{\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle\supset{\mathcal{M}}^{2}\supset\ldots\supset{\mathcal{M}}^{k}\supset\cdots

\displaystyle\bigcap_{k}{\mathcal{M}}^{k}

\displaystyle=(0).

The algebra
R
is naturally equipped with the
{\mathcal{M}}
-adic topology
for which the
{\mathcal{M}}^{k}
form a basis of
0
-neighbourhoods. This means that two series are close to each other if their
coefficients are equal up to some high order. The algebra
R
is
complete
with respect to this topology.

Any automorphism
\varphi\in Aut(R)
preserves the filtration and is determined by the
n

power series

y_{i}:=\varphi(x_{i})\in{\mathcal{M}},\;\;\;i=1,2,\ldots,n

as for any power series
f\in R
one must have

\varphi(f)=f(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{n}).

In this sense
Aut(R)
is the same thing as the group of formal change of variables: it acts on
R
via coordinate transformations and the map

x_{i}\mapsto y_{i}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})

is a formal change of variables. This is a very peculiar feature of algebra automorphisms: if

x_{i}
is mapped to
y_{i}
then
x_{i}^{2}
is mapped to
y_{i}^{2}
and so on. Therefore the knowledge of
the map on the coordinates, that is on a finite dimensional
K
-vector space, is sufficient to reconstruct the map on the whole algebra.

More generally, an
n
-tuple
(y_{1}(x),y_{2}(x),\ldots,y_{n}(x))

of power series from
{\mathcal{M}}
defines a endomorphism

R\longrightarrow R,\;\;x_{i}\mapsto y_{i}(x)

of the ring
R
. The
formal inverse function theorem
states that it is an automorphism if and only if the jacobian determinant is invertible:

\det\left(\frac{\partial y_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\right)\not\in{\mathcal{M}}.

Definition 14.1
.

Two series
f,g\in R
are called right equivalent , if they belong to the same orbit of the action of
Aut(R)
on
R
:

\varphi(f)=g\ \textup{ or equivalently:}\;\;\;f(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{n})=g(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})

Let us look first at power series in one variable, i.e.
R=K[[x]]
.
A non-zero series
f\in R
of order
k
can be written as
written as

f(x)=\alpha x^{k}+\sum_{n\geq k+1}a_{n}x^{n}=\alpha x^{k}(1+b_{1}x+b_{2}x^{2}+\ldots),

where
\alpha\neq 0
.
The series

y(x)=x(1+b_{1}x+b_{2}x^{2}+\ldots)^{1/k}=x+\frac{b_{1}}{k}x^{2}+\ldots

defines a formal change of variables such that

f(y)=y^{k}.

Therefore the associated automorphism defined by

\varphi(x):=x(1+b_{1}x+b_{2}x^{2}+\ldots)^{1/k}

maps
f
to
\alpha x^{k}:

\varphi(f)=\alpha x^{k}

If the field
K
contains a
k
-th root of
\alpha
, a further coordinate
change
x\mapsto\alpha^{1/k}x
shows that
f
is right equivalent to the
pure monomial
x^{k}
. This describes the right equivalence classes in the
one-variable case:

Proposition 14.2
.

Let
K
be algebraically closed, and let
R=K[[x]]
be the
formal power series ring. Then
R
is the countable union of the orbits of the
monomials
x^{k},\ k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\{0\}

under the action of
Aut(R)
.

The module of derivations
\Theta_{R/K}=Der_{K}(R)
of the ring
R
can be identified with

formal vector fields

v=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}(x)\partial_{i},\ a_{i}\in R,\;\;\;\partial_{i}:=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}},

and is filtered by powers of
{\mathcal{M}}
in a similar way. If we denote by
{\rm Der\,}_{K}(R)^{(k)}
the space of derivations with coefficients in
{\mathcal{M}}^{k}
, we have a filtration

Der_{K}(R)\supset Der_{K}(R)^{(1)}\supset Der_{K}(R)^{(2)}\supset\ldots\supset Der_{K}(R)^{(k)}\supset\ldots

Clearly, if
f\in{\mathcal{M}}^{k}
and
v\in{\rm Der\,}(R)^{(l)}
then
v(f)\in{\mathcal{M}}^{k+l-1}
.
We can try to define the exponential of
v\in Der_{K}(R)
using the
exponential series:

\exp(v)(f)=f+v(f)+\frac{1}{2!}v(v(f))+\ldots

A vector field
v\in Der_{K}(R)^{(2)}
maps
{\mathcal{M}}^{k}
to
{\mathcal{M}}^{k+1}
and as
a result, the above series converges in the
{\mathcal{M}}
-adic topology of
R
, that
is, order by order. So we obtain a well-defined exponential mapping

\exp:{\rm Der\,}_{K}(R)^{(2)}\longrightarrow Aut(R)

in the most naïve way, just using the power series definition of the exponential.
Clearly, the automorphism
\exp(v)
thus defined has the property that

exp(v)(f)-f\in{\mathcal{M}}^{2}

We say that it is
tangent to the identity
meaning that it is the identity modulo
{\mathcal{M}}^{2}
.

More generally, we can define the sub-group

Aut_{K}(R)^{(k)}:=\{\phi\in Aut_{K}(R)\;\;|\;\;\phi(f)-f\in{\mathcal{M}}^{k}\}

of automorphisms tangent to order
(k-1)
to the identity, and
clearly
\exp
maps
{\rm Der\,}_{K}(R)^{(k)}
to
Aut_{K}(R)^{(k)}
.

Definition 14.3
.

We define the tangent space
Tf
at
f
as the image of the map

{\rm Der\,}(R)\longrightarrow R,\ v\mapsto v(f).

The above map is
R
-linear and therefore
Tf
is an
R
-module, commonly called the
Jacobian ideal

J_{f}
, that is,
the ideal generated by the partial derivatives of
f
:

Tf=J_{f}:=(\partial_{1}f,\partial_{2}f,\ldots,\partial_{n}f)\subset R

In this setting, the normal space

Nf:=R/J_{f}.

is often called the
Milnor-algebra

of
f
. Its dimension is called the
Milnor number
and is denoted by
\mu(f)
:

\mu(f):=\dim Nf.

For instance, if
f=x^{k+1}
, then the Jacobian ideal is
{\mathcal{M}}^{k}
and the Milnor algebra is generated by the classes of
1,x,\dots,x^{k-1}
,
\mu(f)=k
.

The fact that the tangent space
Tf
has the structure of an
R
-module is a special feature of singularity theory.
The reason is that there are no differential relations involved in the action of the automorphism group. This fundamental property explains the success of commutative algebra to study group actions in this context. This is already reflected in the following simple lemma.

Lemma 14.4
.

If
\mu:=\mu(f)<+\infty
, then

{\mathcal{M}}^{\mu}\subset Tf.

Proof.

The ideal
{\mathcal{M}}^{\mu}
is generated as
R
-module by the monomials
x^{I}
with
|I|=\mu
. We can write
x^{I}
as a product of monomials of degree one:

x^{I}=x_{i_{1}}\dots x_{i_{\mu}}.

As
Nf
is of dimension
\mu
, the classes of any
\mu
monomials together with the class of
1
are linearly dependent. In particular there exists a relation of the form

\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}x_{i_{1}}+\alpha_{2}x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}+\dots+\alpha_{\mu}x_{i_{1}}\dots x_{i_{\mu}}=0\in Nf

If
\alpha_{k}
ist the first non-zero coefficient in this relation, we can write this
relation in the form

x_{i_{1}}\dots x_{i_{k}}(\alpha_{k}+g)\in J_{f},\;\;\;g\in{\mathcal{M}}.

As in the local ring
R
any element which is not in the maximal ideal is invertible, this shows that
x_{i_{1}}\dots x_{i_{k}}\in J_{f}
and consequently
x^{I}\in J_{f}
. This proves our assertion.
∎

We immediately conclude:

Corollary 14.5
.

The image of the map
{\rm Der\,}_{K}(R)^{(2)}\longrightarrow R,v\mapsto v(f)
contains

{\mathcal{M}}^{\mu+2}
.

We know that in the one variable case any formal power series can be reduced to the first non-vanishing term by an automorphism. For the case of more variables, this statement can be generalised as follows:

Proposition 14.6
.

If
\mu(f)<\infty
, then for any element of
g\in{\mathcal{M}}^{\mu+2}
there exists an automorphism
\varphi\in{\rm Aut\,}_{K}(R)
such that

\varphi(f+g)=f.

Proof.

Use induction on the order of
g
. Assume that there exists an automorphism

\varphi_{k}
such that:

\varphi_{k}(f+g)=f+g_{k},g_{k}\in{\mathcal{M}}^{\mu+2+k}

If follows from corollary
14.5
that there exists a derivation
v_{k+1}\in{\rm Der\,}(R)^{(2)}
such that

v_{k+1}(f)=g_{k}.

As
v_{k+1}\in{\rm Der\,}_{K}(R)^{(2)}
and
g_{k}\in{\mathcal{M}}^{\mu+2+k}
we have

\exp(v_{k+1})(g_{k})-g_{k}\in{\mathcal{M}}^{\mu+2+k+1},

so we get

e^{-v_{k+1}}(f+g_{k})=f\ {\rm mod\,}\ {\mathcal{M}}^{\mu+2+k+1}=:f+g_{k+1}

If we set
\varphi_{k+1}=e^{-v_{k+1}}\varphi_{k}
we then have

\varphi_{k+1}(f+g)=e^{-v_{k+1}}(f+g_{k})=f+g_{k+1}

So we can repeat the procedure and in this way we obtain a sequence of
derivations
v_{k}
and the automorphism
\varphi
given by

\varphi=\lim_{k\longrightarrow+\infty}e^{-v_{k}}\cdots e^{-v_{0}}

maps
f+g
to
f
.
∎

This is the classical
finite determinacy theorem
for series with finite Milnor number: terms of degree
\geq\mu+2
can be omitted, so any series with
\mu<\infty
is right equivalent to a polynomial; for
\mu=1
we deduce the formal Morse lemma.

Note that for a general
v\in Der_{K}(R)
the exponential series does not define an automorphism
of
R
. Only if
v\in Der_{K}(R)^{(2)}
the series converges in the
{\mathcal{M}}
-adic topology to an automorphism tangent to the identity. This is the origin of the exponent
\mu+2
in the statement of the finite determinacy theorem.

However, if
v\in Der_{K}(R)^{(1)}
, then sometimes one can make sense of the exponential series
as a well-defined as an automorphism of
R
. This is the case if
K=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
for which the exponential
of a linear map is always well-defined. We record the following variant of the above finite determinacy theorem:

Proposition 14.7
.

(
K=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
.) If
\mu(f)<\infty
, then there is a neighbourhood

U
of the origin in
{\mathcal{M}}^{\mu+1}
such that for any
g\in U
, there exists an automorphism
\varphi\in{\rm Aut\,}_{K}(R)
with

\varphi(f+g)=f.

For instance, consider the case
n=1,f=x^{2}
then the neighbourhood may be defined by

U=\left\{\sum_{i\geq 2}a_{i}x^{i}:|a_{2}|<1\right\}.

The condition defining
U
ensures that
f+g
has a non-zero quadratic part.

14.2.
Analytic finite determinacy

Now let us look at the ring of convergent power series

R:=\mathbb{C}\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}.

This is also a local ring filtered by the powers of its maximal ideal

{\mathcal{M}}:=\{f\in R:f(0)=0\}.

and, as before, any automorphism
\varphi\in Aut(R)
is given by an
n
-tuple of convergent power series

y_{i}:=\varphi(x_{i})\in{\mathcal{M}},\;\;\;i=1,2,\ldots,n.

The module of derivations
\Theta_{R}=Der(R)
is the module of analytic vector fields

v=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}(x)\partial_{i},\ a_{i}\in R,\;\;\;\partial_{i}:=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}},

and we define the (analytic) normal space
Nf
to be the cokernel of the map

Der(R)\longrightarrow R,\;\;\;v\mapsto v(f).

So again

Nf=R/J_{f},

but now
J_{f}
is the ideal generated by the partial derivatives in the ring
R=\mathbb{C}\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}\}
of
convergent power series. The (analytic) Milnor number is defined as before

\mu(f):=\dim Nf.

An identical proof shows that if
\mu(f)<\infty
, then
{\mathcal{M}}^{\mu}\subset J_{f}
and therefore that
the image of the map

Der(R)^{(2)}\longrightarrow R,v\mapsto v(f)

contains
{\mathcal{M}}^{\mu+2}
.

Proposition 14.8
.

If
\mu(f)<\infty
, then for any element of
g\in{\mathcal{M}}^{\mu+2}
there exists an automorphism
\varphi\in{\rm Aut\,}(R)
such that

\varphi(f+g)=f.

Proof.

The series
f
defines a holomorphic function on some neighborhood
V
of the origin,
f\in{\mathcal{O}}(V)
. On
V
we consider the sheaf
\Theta={\rm Der\,}({\mathcal{O}})
of holomorphic vector fields and the map of sheaves

\rho:\Theta\longrightarrow{\mathcal{O}},\;v\mapsto v(f)

Cartan’s Theorem
\alpha
(see Appendix
A.5
) implies that

i)

there exists a fundamental system of polycylinders
\Delta=(\Delta_{\rho})
for which
\Theta^{c}(\Delta)
,
{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(\Delta)
are Kolmogorov spaces.

ii)

the induced Kolmogorov space morphism
\rho^{c}
admits a local inverse
B
over its image.

The Kolmogorov version of the map

\rho^{c}:(\Theta^{c})^{(2)}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(\Delta),\;v\mapsto v(f)

has an image containing

M={\mathcal{O}}^{c}(\Delta)^{(\mu+1)}=\left({\mathcal{M}}^{\mu+1}\right)^{c}(\Delta).

The preimage
of
M
is a Kolmogorov subspace
\mathfrak{g}
.

To apply the general normal form theorem in its homogeneous form (Theorem 13.3), we must check three conditions:

1)

u(f)\in M
for any
u\in\mathfrak{g}
(true by definition of
\mathfrak{g}
).

2)

e^{u}(f+M)\subset(f+M)
holds because
u^{k}(f)\subset M
for
k>0
.

3)

the map
\rho
admits a local right quasi-inverse.

All three condition are satisfied. The theorem implies the existence of a neighbourhood of the origin

U={\mathcal{X}}(r)\cap{\mathcal{X}}(R,k,l,\lambda)

such that for
g\in U
, there exists a sequence of derivations
(v_{1},\dots,v_{n},\dots)
such that
the composed automorphisms

e^{v_{n}}\cdot e^{v_{n-1}}\cdots e^{v_{0}}

converge to a partial morphism
\varphi
which maps
f+g
to the restriction of
f
to a smaller neighbourhood.

If we now pass to the direct limit the partial morphism defines a ring automorphism sending
f+g
to
f
and the direct limit of
U
contains
{\mathcal{M}}^{k+l+1}
. As we know from the formal case that for any
N\geq\mu+2
, the orbit
contains
f+g
an element in
f+{\mathcal{M}}^{N}
this concludes the proof.
∎

In the one dimensional Morse example, we saw that the bound
(k+l+1)
given by the case of convergent power series is the optimal bound
\mu+2
.

Note that, except from this, the proof is almost identical to the proof in the formal case. Indeed, our basic strategy is to
lift all constructions from the formal level to the level of Kolmogorov spaces to obtain simple and transparent
proofs in the convergent case. As we shall now see, the versal deformation theorem provides another example which is from an abstract viewpoint identical to that of finite determinacy.

Nota also that the theorem holds more generally for any subspace
M
which satisfies the condition 1), 2) and
3).

14.3.
Formal versal deformations

The concept of versal deformation

The problem of versal deformations is a variant with parameters of the previous one.
To explain this, let us start with an example. Consider the following two deformations of
the function
f(x)=x^{3}

F=x^{3}+\lambda_{1}x+\lambda_{2}

and

G=x^{3}+3\alpha^{2}x^{2}+\alpha.

The second one can be obtained from the first in the following way. As

x^{3}+3\alpha^{2}x^{2}=(x+\alpha^{2})^{3}-3\alpha^{4}(x+\alpha^{2})+(\alpha+2\alpha^{6})

we have

G(x,\alpha)=F(x+\alpha^{2},-3\alpha^{4},\alpha+2\alpha^{6}).

So, by a substitution of the parameters and a (parameter dependent) coordinate transformation, the second family may be
obtained from the first. We say that
G
can be

from

. In this example, we get the following
picture:

The dotted curve

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}

\displaystyle=-3\alpha^{4}

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}

\displaystyle=\alpha+2\alpha^{6}

Let us put this relationship in a more algebraic form. We start with our ring of formal power series

and a ring of parameters

. An element

Versality and group actions

The inducing map on the parameter space needs not to be an automorphism
In the previous example, we had

\alpha\mapsto(-3\alpha^{4},\alpha+\alpha^{6})

So even the number of variables is not the same! Therefore versality cannot be formulated directly in terms of group actions. Nevertheless one can use a trick due to Martinet. Although we shall not apply it elsewhere, it is an important feature that it can be used in other problems involving normal forms. This one of the interesting aspects of having a formal theory: every particular trick can potentially have a wide range of applications.

So let

Let us go back to our previous example. In this case

The automorphism

As

which as expected gives back the inducing map

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}

\displaystyle=-3\alpha^{4}

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}

\displaystyle=\alpha+2\alpha^{6}

Some basic local algebra

We will need some basic facts from local algebra and start by recalling the
following version of

Proposition 14.9
.

Consider a finitely generated module
M

m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{r}\in M

\bar{m}_{1},\bar{m}_{2},\ldots,\bar{m}_{n}

Proof.

The implication
\implies

M/{\mathcal{M}}M=\{0\}

If this were not the case, replace

M^{\prime}=M/N

\dim N/{\mathcal{M}}N=\dim M/{\mathcal{M}}M.

So we now assume that

M/{\mathcal{M}}M=\{0\}\implies M=\{0\}

Consider any set of generators

As

Without loss of generality, we may assume that

(1)

S=K[[\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}]]
is a subring of
R=K[[x_{1},\dots,x_{n},\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}]]
.

(2)

R=K[[x,\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}]]
that is
n=1
.

(1) Write
T:=K[[x,\lambda]]
, the homomorphism
\varphi:S\longrightarrow R

factors as

S\hookrightarrow T\twoheadrightarrow R

where the first map is the inclusion and the second is

x_{i}\mapsto x_{i},\;\;\;\lambda_{i}\mapsto\varphi(\lambda_{i}).

As any set of generators for
M
as
R
-module are also generators for
M
as
T
-module, this proves the assertion.

(2) We use a descending induction. Assume the theorem is proved only for
n=1
. Define

S_{i}=K[[x_{1},\dots,x_{i},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{m}]].

Applying successively the result we get that
M
is a finitely generated
S_{i}
-module for
i=n-1
,
i=n-2,\dots,i=0
.

Remark that unlike the case of the Nakayama lemma we cannot assume that
M=\{0\}
because by taking the quotient of
M
with an
S
-module we usually loose the
R
-module structure.

Let
m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{r}
be generators of
M
as
R
-module.

Lemma 14.12
.

There exists
N\geq 0
such that

x^{N}m_{i}\equiv 0\ {\rm mod\,}{\mathcal{M}}_{S}

for any
i\in\{1,\dots,r\}
.

Proof.

Put
d=\dim M/{\mathcal{M}}_{S}M
, the classes

m_{i},xm_{i},\dots,x^{d}m_{i}

are not linearly independent, that is:

\sum_{i\geq k_{i}}\alpha_{i}x^{i}m_{i}\equiv 0\ {\rm mod\,}{\mathcal{M}}_{S},

where
\alpha_{k_{i}}\neq 0
is the first non-vanishing coefficient.
So we have

x^{k_{i}}u_{i}(x)m_{i}\equiv 0\ {\rm mod\,}{\mathcal{M}}_{.}

where
u_{i}=\sum_{i\geq k_{i}}\alpha_{i}x^{i-k_{i}}
is a unit. Therefore

x^{k_{i}}m_{i}\equiv 0\ {\rm mod\,}{\mathcal{M}}_{S}

Defining
N=\max(k_{i})
, we get that:

x^{N}m_{i}\equiv 0\ {\rm mod\,}{\mathcal{M}}_{S}.

This proves the lemma.
∎

Writing out the relation of the lemma in matrix terms, we find a matrix
A
with entries
a_{ij}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{S}
such that

x^{N}m=Am

So the element
g(x,y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{n}):=\det(x^{N}I-A)\in R
has the property that:

g\cdot m_{i}=0,\ g(x,0)=x^{N}

for all
i=1,2,\ldots,r
that is:

g\cdot M=0.

The formal case of the Weierstraß preparation theorem
can be used to write

g=u\cdot h,\;\;\;h=x^{N}+a_{1}x^{N-1}+\dots+a_{N}

where
a_{i}\in S,a_{i}(0)=0
and
u
is a unit in
R
. As
u
is a unit, we also have

h\cdot M=0

Thus
M
can be considered as a module over the factor ring
R/(h)
. But
R/(h)
is a free
S
-module with basis
1,x,x^{2},\ldots,x^{N-1}
, and therefore the elements

x^{i}m_{j},\;\;\;i=0,1,\ldots,N-1,\;j=1,2,\ldots,r

form a set of generators of
M
as an
S
-module. This proves the theorem.
∎

The formal versal deformation theorem

Theorem 14.13
.

A deformation
F\in R[[\lambda]]
of
f\in R
is versal if and only if
the classes of the
\partial_{\lambda_{i}}F(-,\lambda=0)
’s generate the
K
-vector space
Tf=R/J_{f}
.

Example 14.14
.

The deformation

F(x,\lambda)=x^{3}+\lambda_{1}x+\lambda_{2}

is a versal deformation of
x^{3}
.
Indeed:

\partial_{\lambda_{1}}F(-,\lambda=0)=x,\ \partial_{\lambda_{2}}F(-,\lambda=0)=1,\ J_{f}={\mathcal{M}}^{2},\ K[[x]]/J_{f}=K\bar{1}\oplus K\bar{x}.

Proof.

We use Martinet’s trick and show that the
Thom-Sebastiani sum
of both deformations

(F\oplus G)(x,\alpha,\lambda)=F(x,\lambda)+G(x,\alpha)-f(x)

is isomorphic to
F
.

We use the notations

\displaystyle{\rm Der\,}_{\alpha,\lambda}K[[x,\alpha,\lambda]]:=

\displaystyle{\rm Der\,}_{K[[\alpha,\lambda]]}K[[x,\alpha,\lambda]],

\displaystyle{\rm Der\,}_{\alpha}K[[\alpha,\lambda]]:=

\displaystyle{\rm Der\,}_{K[[\alpha]]}K[[\alpha,\lambda]]

and so on. So for example, an element of
{\rm Der\,}_{\alpha,\lambda}K[[x,\alpha,\lambda]]
are formal vector of the form

v=\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}},\;\;\;A_{i}\in K[[x,\alpha,\lambda]].

We assert that the map

\displaystyle\rho:

\displaystyle{\rm Der\,}_{\alpha,\lambda}K[[z,\lambda,\alpha]]\oplus{\rm Der\,}_{\alpha}K[[\lambda,\alpha]]\longrightarrow K[[z,\lambda,\alpha]]

\displaystyle v\mapsto v(F\oplus G)

is surjective.
To show it, consider the
K[[x,\alpha,\lambda]]
module
M:=Coker(\rho)
and consider it as
K[[\alpha,\lambda]]
-module.
Clearly, by putting
\alpha=0
, we find
M^{\prime}:=M/(\alpha)M=Coker(\rho^{\prime})
, where

\rho^{\prime}:{\rm Der\,}_{\lambda}K[[z,\lambda]]\oplus{\rm Der\,}_{K}K[[\lambda]]\longrightarrow K[[z,\lambda]],v\mapsto v(F)

is obtained from
\rho
by setting
\alpha=0
.
If we now also divide out the
\lambda
, we find that
M/(\alpha,\lambda)=M^{\prime}/(\lambda)M^{\prime}

can be identified with
Tf/(\partial_{\lambda_{i}}F_{|\lambda=0})
. But assumption, this is zero.
If follows from Theorem
14.10
that
M
is finitely generated as
K[[\alpha,\lambda]]
-module
and hence is zero by Nakayama’s lemma
14.9
.

Let us denote by
{\mathcal{M}}_{\alpha}\subset K[[z,\lambda,\alpha]]
the module of series vanishing at
\alpha=0
and filter
the ring
K[[z,\lambda,\alpha]]
by powers of
{\mathcal{M}}_{\alpha}
. Assuming the existence of an automorphism
\varphi
over the base of the deformation such that:

\varphi(F+G)=F\ {\rm mod\,}K[[z,\lambda,\alpha]]^{(k)}=F+R\ {\rm mod\,}{\mathcal{M}}_{\alpha}^{k+1}

We choose a derivation
v\in({\rm Der\,}_{\alpha,\lambda}K[[z,\lambda,\alpha]]\oplus{\rm Der\,}_{\alpha}K[[\lambda,\alpha]])^{(2)}
such that

\rho(v)=R.

The automorphism
\varphi^{\prime}=e^{-v}\varphi
gives

\varphi^{\prime}(F+G)=F\ {\rm mod\,}\ {\mathcal{M}}_{\alpha}^{k+1}

In this way, we construct order by order an automorphism which maps

to

. This concludes the proof
of the theorem.
∎

14.4.
Analytic versal deformations

We would like to prove the existence of a versal deformation for convergent power series. So we consider the ring

\mathbb{C}\{z,\lambda\}:=\mathbb{C}\{z_{1},\dots,z_{n},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{k}\}

considered as algebra over

\mathbb{C}\{\lambda\}:=\mathbb{C}\{\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k}\}.

The ring
\mathbb{C}\{z,\lambda\}
is filtrated by powers of the maximal ideal
{\mathcal{M}}_{l}
of the ring
\mathbb{C}\{\lambda\}

consising of the functions vanishing at
\lambda=0
.

Theorem 14.15
.

A deformation
F\in\mathbb{C}\{z,\lambda\}
of
f\in\mathbb{C}\{z\}
is versal if
and only if the classes of the
\partial_{\lambda_{i}}F(\lambda=0,-)
’s generate the vector space
Tf
.

Proof.

We proceed like in the formal case. Given a deformation
G
of
f
over
\mathbb{C}\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{l}\}
, we construct the Thom-Sebastiani sum as before
F\oplus G
.

The generalised preparation theorem (Theorem
14.10
) also holds for convergent power series,
with a near identical proof where one replaces the formal Weierstraß division theorem by its analytic
version (see Appendix). Therefore like in the formal case, the cokernel of the map

\displaystyle\rho:

\displaystyle{\rm Der\,}_{\alpha,\lambda}\mathbb{C}\{z,\lambda,\alpha\}\oplus{\rm Der\,}_{\alpha}\mathbb{C}\{\lambda,\alpha\}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}\{z,\lambda,\alpha\}

\displaystyle v\mapsto v(F\oplus G)

reduces to zero.

We lift the discussion at the level of Kolmogorov spaces. We choose polycylinders
\Delta=(\Delta_{s})
adapted to the
map
\rho
. Denote by
{\mathcal{M}}_{S}
(resp.
\Theta
), the sheaf of holomorphic function (resp. derivations) which vanish at
\alpha=0,\lambda=0
.

Like for finite determinacy, we apply the normal form theorem in its homogeneous form with

E=\left({\mathcal{O}}^{c}(\Delta)\right),\ M={\mathcal{M}}_{S}^{c}(\Delta),\ \mathfrak{g}=\Theta^{c}(\Delta),\ a=F.

To apply the general normal form theorem in its homogeneous form (Theorem8.3), we must check three conditions:

1)

u(f)\in M
for any
u\in\mathfrak{g}
(true by definition of
\mathfrak{g}
).

2)

e^{u}(f+M)\subset(f+M)
holds trivially.

3)

the map
\rho
admits a singular local right inverse over
M
(true because of Cartan’s theorem
\alpha
).

The theorem implies the existence of a neighbourhood of the origin
U
such that for
G\in U
, there exists a sequence of derivations
(v_{1},\dots,v_{n},\dots)
such that
the composed automorphisms

e^{v_{n}}\cdot e^{v_{n-1}}\cdots e^{v_{0}}

converge to a partial morphism
\varphi
which maps
F\oplus G
to the restriction of
F
to a smaller neighbourhood.

If we now pass to the direct limit the partial morphism defines a ring automorphism sending any deformation of
F
to
F
and the direct limit of
U
contains
{\mathcal{M}}_{S}^{k}
for some
k
.

We know from the formal case that
F\oplus G
contains an element in
F+{\mathcal{M}}_{S}^{k}
. In this way, we get that
F\oplus G
lies in the orbit of
F
. This proves the versal deformation theorem.
∎

Chapter 15
Normal forms of vector fields

The group
Diff(M)
of diffeomorphisms of a manifold
M
acts naturally on the
spaces of tensor fields on
M
. Apart from the action on the space of functions

C^{\infty}(M)
that we discussed in the previous section, the action on the space
\Theta(M)

of
C^{\infty}
vector fields is of particular interest. As a vector field
can be seen as an infinitesimal diffeomorphism, the infinite dimensional Lie-algebra of vector fields can be
seen as the tangent space to
Diff(M)
at the identity, and the action of
Diff(M)
on
\Theta(M)

is an infinite dimensional version of the adjoint representation of a Lie group.
If we pick a point
p\in M
, one can look at action of the diffeomeorphisms fixing
p
on the
germs of vector fields at
p
. Introducing coordinates
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
we may represent a vector field as

v=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}},

and where the coeffients
a_{i}=a_{i}(x)
are smooth functions of the coordinates.
In this chapter we will study the cases where the coefficients
a_{i}
are formal of convergent power series.
Contrary to the case of hypersurface singularities, in the case of vector fields non-trivial convergence
issues play a role and provide a first non-trivial application of the normal form theorem.

15.1.
Vector fields

The diffeomorphism group
Diff(M)
of a manifold
M
acts on itself by conjugation:

(\varphi,\psi)\mapsto\varphi\circ\psi\circ\varphi^{-1}.

In this formula
\varphi
is viewed as a change of variables and

\psi:M\longrightarrow M

as a map that we can possibly iterate to define a discrete dynamical system on
M
.

Due to the fact that diffeomorphisms are read from right to left and automorphisms from left to right, if we regard diffeomorphisms as automorphisms of
C^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R})
, then the action is the other
way

(\varphi,\psi)\mapsto\varphi^{-1}\psi\varphi.

So we have two languages, geometric and algebraic, for the same object.

Integrating a vector field
v
at time
t
yields a diffeomorphism
\varphi_{t}
. A change of variables transforms

\varphi_{t}
and also the vector field
v
into a vector field
\varphi\cdot v
.
This is the adjoint representation of the diffeomorphism group
G=Diff(M)
into the Lie algebra of vector fields
\mathfrak{g}=\Theta(M)
.

In local coordinates
x=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
, the vector field takes the form

v=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}.

If we transform
v
to a different coordinate system
y=(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{n})
,
one has

\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}=\sum_{j}\frac{\partial y_{j}}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{j}},

and so one has

v=\sum_{i,j}a_{i}(x)\frac{\partial y_{j}}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{j}}

This formula describes the
effect of a diffeomeorphism

\varphi:(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\mapsto(y_{1}(x),\ldots,y_{n}(x))

on the vector field
v
, in which case we would write

\varphi\cdot v=\sum_{i,j}a_{i}(x)\frac{\partial y_{j}}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{j}}.

If the vector field
v
is now viewed as a derivation of the ring
R=C^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R})

then the action of an automorphism
\varphi:R\longrightarrow R
on
v
is by conjugation. This is the adjoint representation
of the group
G=Aut(R)
on the module of derivations. Therefore these two procedures produce the same result:

\varphi\cdot v=\varphi^{-1}v\varphi.

Concretly this means that computing into two different ways leads to the same result. Let us consider a simple example of a vector field defined on the one-dimensional line
described with a coordinate
x
:

v=a(x)\frac{\partial}{\partial x}.

Now consider the local diffeomorphism

\varphi:x\mapsto 2x+x^{2}=:y

with inverse

\varphi^{-1}:y\mapsto-1+\sqrt{1+y}=\frac{1}{2}y-\frac{1}{8}y^{2}+\ldots

We have

\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}=2+2x=2\sqrt{1+y},

so the vector field
v
is transformed into

\varphi\cdot v=2\sqrt{1+y}\ a(-1+\sqrt{1+y})\partial_{y}.

If we now localise at the origin, so that now
v
is a derivation of the local
ring of
C^{\infty}
-germs:

R\longrightarrow R,\ f(x)\mapsto a(x)f^{\prime}(x)

and
\varphi
is the automorphism

\varphi:R\longrightarrow R,\ x\mapsto 2x+x^{2}

and

\varphi^{-1}:R\longrightarrow R,\ x\mapsto-1+\sqrt{1+x}

Composing with
\varphi
, we get that:

\displaystyle\varphi^{-1}a(x)\partial_{x}\varphi(x)

\displaystyle=\varphi^{-1}\left(a(x)\partial_{x}(2x+x^{2})\right)

\displaystyle=\varphi^{-1}(a(x))\varphi^{-1}(2+2x)

\displaystyle=\varphi^{-1}(a(x))(2+2\varphi^{-1}(x))

\displaystyle=a(-1+\sqrt{1+x})2\sqrt{1+x}.

which is of course the same result if we replace the letter
x
by the letter
y
. We see in this simple example that, contrary to the case of singularity theory, the action is quite involved already in the one dimensional case.

15.2.
Adjoint orbits

Having clarified the relation between change of variables in vector fields
and adjoint action on derivations, we may now consider the classical theory
of formal normal forms for vector fields. This theory goes back to the work
of Poincaré and thus predates the simpler normal form theory for hypersurface
singularities.

So let
K
be a field of characteristic zero and consider the ring
R:=K[[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]]
of formal power series in
n

variables. A formal vector fields is an expression of the form:

\sum_{i\geq 0}a_{i}(x)\partial_{i},\ \partial_{i}:=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}

with
a_{i}\in R
. These are the derivations of the ring
R
. We investigate the orbit of a derivation under the automorphism group of
R
or which is the same the action of changing variables in a vector field. If we regard a vector field
v
as derivation of
R
, the action of an automorphism

\varphi\in{\rm Aut\,}(R)
is given by:

w=\varphi v\varphi^{-1}.

Now if we take a vector field
u\in{\rm Der\,}(R)^{(2)}
, it exponentiates and gives rise to an automorphism
e^{u}
. The action of such an automorphism
is given by

e^{u}(f)=f+u(f)+\frac{1}{2!}u\circ u(f)+\cdots

It acts on a derivation
w
as:

e^{v}we^{-v}=w+[v,w]+\dots

where the dots stand for terms at least quadratic in
v
. So we recover the fact that the infinitesimal is given by the Lie bracket: if

v=\sum_{i}a_{i}\partial_{i},\;\;w=\sum_{i}b_{i}\partial i,

then

[v,w]=\sum_{i}c_{i}\partial_{i},

where

c_{i}=\sum_{j}a_{j}\partial_{j}b_{i}-b_{j}\partial_{j}a_{i}.

Like for functions, our aim is to study orbits, called
adjoint orbits
, under this action. It can be considered as infinite dimensional version of the example studied in section 5.2. Let us start the elementary case of the rectification of
a non-singular vector field

v\in Der(R)^{(0)}\setminus Der(R)^{(1)}.

Proposition 15.1
.

Assume that
v=\sum_{i\geq 0}a_{i}(x)\partial_{i}
is such that one of the
a_{i}
’s does not vanish at
0
, then the derivation
v
lies in the adjoint orbit of
\partial_{1}
.

Proof.

The automorphism group acts transitively on linear derivations thus we may assume that:

v=\partial_{1}\ {\rm mod\,}{\rm Der\,}(R)^{(1)}.

Let us show by induction on
k
that the orbit of
v
contains an element of the form

v^{\prime}=\partial_{1}\ {\rm mod\,}{\rm Der\,}(R)^{(k)}.

Write

v^{\prime}=\partial_{1}+w,\ w\in{\rm Der\,}(R)^{(k+1)}

If
\xi=\sum\xi_{i}\partial_{i}
is a vector field, then

[\xi,\partial_{1}]=-\sum_{i}\partial_{1}\xi_{i}\partial_{i}

So we can always find
\xi\in{\rm Der\,}(R)^{(k+2)}
such that

[\xi,\partial_{1}]+w=0\ {\rm mod\,}\ {\rm Der\,}(R)^{(k+2)}.

We have

e^{\xi}v^{\prime}e^{-\xi}=\partial_{1}\ {\rm mod\,}{\rm Der\,}(R)^{(k+2)}.

This proves the proposition.
∎

15.3.
Formal Poincaré-Dulac theorem

Now we turn to the case of a linear vector field

v=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}x_{i}\partial_{i}.

and study its perturbations

v+w,w\in Der(R)^{(2)}

obtained by adding higer order terms.
The infinitesimal action is given by a commutator and an explicit computation shows that:

\displaystyle[x^{I}\partial_{i},v]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}[x^{I}\partial_{i},x_{j}\partial_{j}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\lambda_{i}x^{I}\partial_{i}x_{j})\partial_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}x_{j}[x^{I}\partial_{i},\partial_{j}]

\displaystyle=\lambda_{i}x^{I}\partial_{i}-\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}I_{j}\right)x^{I}\partial_{i}=(\lambda_{i}-(\lambda,I))x^{I}\partial_{i}

where
(-,-)
denotes the Euclidean scalar product. We have proved the

Proposition 15.2
.

Let
v\in{\rm Der\,}(R)
be a derivation of the form

v=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}x_{i}\partial_{i}.

The linear map

{\rm Der\,}(R)\longrightarrow{\rm Der\,}(R),\xi\mapsto[\xi,v]

is diagonal in the basis
x^{I}\partial_{i}
with eigenvalues
(\lambda_{i}-(\lambda,I))
.

The situation is therefore similar to that encountered when we discussed normal forms of Hamiltonian functions.
Remark that we always have

[x_{i}\partial_{i},v]=0

but the these linear vector fields
x_{i}\partial_{i}
exponentiate to scalings of
the coordinates.

We call
v

non-resonant
if the commutant
reduces to the linear span of the
x_{i}\partial x_{i},i=1,2.\ldots,n
:

[\xi,v]=0\iff\xi\in\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{C}x_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}.

Otherwise,
v

is called
resonant
, and a monomial
x^{I}\partial_{i}
commuting with
v
a
resonant monomial
.
According to the previous computation:

x^{I}\partial_{i}\text{ resonant}\iff(\lambda_{i}-(\lambda,I))=0.

For instance, in the two variable case
x_{1}\partial_{x}+\alpha x_{2}\partial_{2}
is resonant precisely when
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}
.

It is natural to study the transformations under the sub-group of automorphisms tangent to the identity:

Aut^{(2)}(R):=\{\varphi\in{\rm Aut\,}(R)\;|\;\varphi=Id\ {\rm mod\,}\ {\mathcal{M}}^{2}\}.

Theorem 15.3
.

Let
v\in{\rm Der\,}(R)
be a derivation of the form

v=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}x_{i}\partial_{i}.

The commutant of
v
:

F:=\{w\in{\rm Der\,}(R)^{(2)}:[v,w]=0\}.

is a transversal to the adjoint action of
Aut^{(2)}(R)
on
v+{\rm Der\,}(R)^{(2)}
.

In other words, for any
w\in{\rm Der\,}(R)^{(2)}
,
there exists an automorphism
\varphi\in Aut^{(2)}(R)

such that

\varphi(v+w)\varphi^{-1}=v+r,\ r\in{\rm Der\,}(R)^{(2)},

and
[r,v]=0
.

Example 15.4
.

Take for
K
any field containing
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})
(for instance
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
) and consider the vector field

v=\sqrt{2}x\partial_{x}+y\partial_{y}.

According to our previous computation

\displaystyle[x^{i}y^{j}\partial_{x},v]

\displaystyle=(\sqrt{2}-\sqrt{2}i-j)x^{i}y^{j}\partial_{x}

\displaystyle[x^{i}y^{j}\partial_{y},v]

\displaystyle=(1-\sqrt{2}i-j)x^{i}y^{j}\partial_{y}

As
1
and
\sqrt{2}
are
\mathbb{Q}
-independent, the vector field is non resonant and the commutator reduces to the two dimensional vector space

K(x\partial_{x})\oplus K(y\partial_{y})\subset{\rm Der\,}(R).

The Poincaré-Dulac theorem states that any formal vector field of the form

v+w
with
w\in{\rm Der\,}(R)^{(2)}
belongs to the adjoint orbit of
v
.

Example 15.5
.

Consider the vector field

v=2x\partial_{x}+y\partial_{y}.

According to our previous computation we have:

\displaystyle[x^{i}y^{j}\partial_{x},v]

\displaystyle=(2-2i-j)x^{i}y^{j}\partial_{x},

\displaystyle[x^{i}y^{j}\partial_{y},v]

\displaystyle=(1-2i-j)x^{i}y^{j}\partial_{y}.

The vector field is now resonant since:

[y^{2}\partial_{x},v]=0.

The commutator of
v
is a three dimensional vector space:

\{w\in{\rm Der\,}(R):[v,w]=0\}=K\,x\partial_{x}\oplus K\,y\partial_{y}\oplus K\,y^{2}\partial_{x}.

Among these vectors, only
y^{2}\partial_{x}
lies in
{\rm Der\,}(R)^{(2)}
. The Poincaré-Dulac theorem states that any formal vector field of the form

v+w
with
w\in{\rm Der\,}(R)^{(2)}
belongs to the adjoint orbit of

(2x+\alpha y^{2})\partial_{x}+y\partial_{y}

for some
\alpha\in K
. So we get a polynomial normal form depending on one parameter.

Example 15.6
.

The situation is radically different for the vector field:

v=x\partial_{x}-y\partial_{y}.

According to our previous computation

\displaystyle[x^{i}y^{j}\partial_{x},v]

\displaystyle=(1-i+j)x^{i}y^{j}\partial_{x},

\displaystyle[x^{i}y^{j}\partial_{y},v]

\displaystyle=(-1-i+j)x^{i}y^{j}\partial_{y}.

the commutator is now generated by monomials of the form

x^{j+1}y^{j}\partial_{x},\ x^{i}y^{i+1}\partial_{y}.

This means that our normal form is not a polynomial but a formal power series of the form

v+\sum_{i\geq 1}\alpha_{i}x^{i+1}y^{i}\partial_{x}+\sum_{i\geq 1}\beta_{i}x^{i}y^{i+1}\partial_{y}.

We now prove the theorem.

Proof.

We use induction on the order. Assume that we have some derivation

v+r_{k}+w^{\prime},w^{\prime}\in{\rm Der\,}(R)^{(k+1)},\ k\geq 1,

with
[r_{k},v]=0
in the orbit of
v+w
. There exists
\xi_{k}\in{\rm Der\,}(R)^{(k+1)}
,
s_{k}\in F
such that

[\xi_{k},v]+w^{\prime}=s_{k}\ {\rm mod\,}{\rm Der\,}(R)^{(k+2)}.

We have

e^{-\xi_{k}}ve^{\xi_{k}}=v+r_{k}+s_{k}\ {\rm mod\,}{\rm Der\,}(R)^{(k+2)}

and
r_{k+1}=r_{k}+s_{k}\in F
.
As we are only using exponentials of elements from
Der(R)^{(2)}
, the
resulting automorphism acts trivially on
{\mathcal{M}}/{\mathcal{M}}^{2}
.
∎

A vector field of the form

v+r,\ [r,v]=0,r\in{\rm Der\,}(R)^{(2)}

is said to be in
Poincaré-Dulac normal form
. There is an unique element of this form in the orbit under
Aut^{(2)}(R)
.
This is again due to the fact that for a diagonal operator the kernel is transversal to the image.

It appears that there are two essentially distinct cases to consider.

Poincaré domain
:
0
lies
not
in the convex hull of the
\lambda_{i}
’s
In this case, the commutant is a finite dimensional vector space.

Siegel domain:

0
lies in the convex hull of the
\lambda_{i}
.

In this case the commutant is either infinite dimensional or trivial.

For instance, going back to the previous example the vector
(2,1)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
belong to the Poincaré domain while
(1,-1)
lies in the Siegel domain.

15.4.
The Poincaré-Siegel theorem

We now lift the discussion at the level of Kolmogorov space so now
K=\mathbb{C}
and
R
is the ring
\mathbb{C}\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
of
convergent power series. As usual we consider the family
D=(D_{s})
of polydiscs centred at the origin of radius
s
.
The normal form theorem (Theorem 13.3) implies the following
finite determinacy theorem for vector fields

Theorem 15.7
.

Consider a vector field
v\in E:={\rm Der\,}({\mathcal{O}}^{c}(D))

and assume that for
k>1
the map

\rho:{\rm Der\,}(E)^{(2)}\longrightarrow{\rm Der\,}(E)^{(k)},\ w\mapsto[v,w]

admits a local right-inverse.
Then there is a defining set
A
such that:

\forall w\in{\rm Der\,}(E)^{(k)},\ \exists\varphi\in Aut_{A}(E),\ \varphi\cdot(v+w)=\iota_{E}v.

So the problem reduces to knowing if our formal inverses constructed in the formal case
are local or not. There are two classical examples one due to Poincaré and the other one due to Siegel.

Definition 15.8
.

We say that a vector
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
lies in the Poincaré domain if
0
is not contained in the convex hull of its components.

The following observation is due to Poincaré:

Proposition 15.9
.

If
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
belongs to the Poincaré domain then for any
i=1,\dots,n
the set

\{\left|(\lambda,I)-\lambda_{i}\right|:I\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\}\setminus\{0\}

is bounded from below and the set

\{I\in\mathbb{N}^{n}:(\lambda,I)-\lambda_{i}=0\}

is finite.

Proof.

The condition of being in the Poincaré is equivalent to saying that we can rotate the eigenvalues in such way that their real
part becomes strictly positive.

We perform such a rotation denote by
m
the minimum of these real value and by
M
its maximum.
We have

Re\left((\lambda,I)-\lambda_{i}\right)\geq|I|m-M,

with
|I|=i_{1}+i_{2}+\dots+i_{n}
.
Therefore if the left hand side vanishes then

|I|\leq\frac{M}{m}.

Thus there are only a finite number of indices
I
for which this hold.

The semi-group
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
generated by the real parts of the
\lambda_{i}
’s is discrete and therefore the distance from a point
x\notin\Gamma
:

d(x,\Gamma)=\inf\{|x_{g}|:g\neq x,g\in\Gamma\}

is strictly positive. This concludes the proof
∎

Corollary 15.10
.

Consider a linear vector field

v=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}x\partial_{x_{i}}.

If the vector
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
belongs to the Poincaré domain then the map

{\rm Der\,}(R)^{(2)}\longrightarrow{\rm Der\,}(R)^{(2)},\ w\mapsto[v,w]

admits a local right-inverse.

Proof.

Put
R^{h}={\mathcal{O}}^{h}(D)
, the map

j:{\rm Der\,}^{2}(R^{h})\longrightarrow{\rm Der\,}^{2}(R^{h}),\ x^{I}\partial_{i}\mapsto\frac{x^{I}}{\lambda_{i}-(\lambda,I)}\partial_{i}

is local right inverse. Indeed

|j(\sum_{I\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}a_{I}x^{I}\partial_{i})|_{s}\,\leq\,|\sum_{I\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\frac{a_{I}}{\lambda_{i}-(\lambda,I)}x^{I}\partial_{i}|_{s}=\sum_{I\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\frac{|a_{I}|^{2}}{|\lambda_{i}-(\lambda,I)|^{2}}|x^{I}|_{s}.

The denominators
|\lambda_{i}-(\lambda,I)|
are bounded from below by a constant thus the map
j
is
0
-local in the
{\mathcal{O}}^{h}
-norm.
As the sheaves
{\mathcal{O}}^{h}
and
{\mathcal{O}}^{c}
are local equivalent, this proves that
j
is also
{\mathcal{O}}^{c}
-local.
∎

In this way, we proved the theorem of Poincaré:

Theorem 15.11
.

Consider a non-resonant linear vector field

v=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}x\partial_{x_{i}}.

If the vector
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
belongs to the Poincaré domain, then for any
w\in{\rm Der\,}(\mathbb{C}\{x\})^{(2)}
, there exists
\varphi\in Aut(\mathbb{C}\{x\})
with
\ \varphi(v+w)\varphi^{-1}=v.

Definition 15.12
.

We say that a vector
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
satisfies the Siegel arithmetic condition,
if there exists constants
(C,k)
such that

|\lambda_{i}-(\lambda,I)|\geq\frac{C}{|I|^{k}}

for any
I\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
and any
i=1,\dots,n
.

The condition is of course similar to the Kolmogorov arithmetic condition and we already observed that it implies locality (Proposition 10.4). Thus we get with the same ease
Siegel’s theorem:

Theorem 15.13
.

Consider a non-resonant linear vector field

v=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}x\partial_{x_{i}}

and assume that the vector
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{n})
satisfies the Siegel arithmetic condition. Then for any
w\in{\rm Der\,}(\mathbb{C}\{x\})^{(2)}
,
there exists
\varphi\in Aut(\mathbb{C}\{x\})
with
\ \varphi(v+w)\varphi^{-1}=v.

Thus both in the Poincaré and in the Siegel cases, the same abstract theorem applies.

Example 15.14
.

Consider the vector field

v=x\partial_{x}-\sqrt{2}y\partial_{y}.

The vector
\lambda=(1,-\sqrt{2})
does not belong to the Poincaré domain. However it satisfies the Siegel arithmetic condition because
\sqrt{2}
is algebraic (Liouville approximation theorem). Consequently for any
w\in{\rm Der\,}(R)^{(2)}
there exists an automorphism
\varphi
of
R
such that

\varphi(x\partial_{x}-\sqrt{2}y\partial_{y}+w)\varphi^{-1}=x\partial_{x}-\sqrt{2}y\partial_{y}.

Chapter 16
Invariant varieties in Hamiltonian systems

In this chapter we return to our original problem of stability of
quasi-periodic movements and give a complete proof of Kolmogorov’s theorem.
In fact, it is a direct application the theory we have developed so far,
in particular of the normal form theorem in Kolmogorov spaces. As all
arguments are of a general nature, we prove a generalised Kolmogorov
theorem with the same ease.

16.1.
The Hamiltonian derivation

Let us consider a Hamiltonian function of the form

For

of

over an arbitrary field

and so the space

consists
of series with

.
We showed that this fact implies that at the formal level that any deformation of

In chapter I, 2.9, we also saw that this simple state of affairs does not hold at the analytic level and that the corresponding operator

is of great complexity. Although in the non-resonant case the kernel is
still

However if we look at the operator along the torus defined by

As we want to identify an appropriate transversal for the group action near

,
let us see what happens with

Adding an element from the ideal

Adding only terms from

This means that vector fields associated to the Hamiltonians

16.3.
The singular torus theorem

Instead of considering deformation with respect to a parameter

The Hamiltonian

\sum_{i=1}^{n}(p_{i}^{2}+\omega_{i}q_{i}^{2})

describing the system of
n
-uncoupled harmonic oscillators, with frequencies
\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\ldots,\omega_{n}
.
The
n
quantities
p_{i}^{2}+q_{i}^{2}
Poisson commute and their common zero-set defines the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
.

However, in the complex domain it has a more interesting geometry.
A complex symplectomorphism transforms the above Hamiltonian, up to a factor, into the form

H=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}p_{i}q_{i}

The elements
p_{i}q_{i}
for
i=1,2,\ldots,n
Poisson commute and define a Lagrangian variety that
consists of
2^{n}
linear subspaces of dimension
n
: for a subset
S\subset\{1,2,\ldots,n\}

we put
p_{i}=0
for
i\in S
and
q_{j}=0
for
j\not\in S
.

We now look what happens if we add a perturbation to the Hamiltonian. Will this Lagrangian variety
persist?

To formulate the question more precisely, let us consider the Poisson algebra

A:=\mathbb{C}\{q,p\}:=\mathbb{C}\{q_{1},\dots,q_{n},p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\}

of convergent power series, with maximal ideal
{\mathcal{M}}
, endowed with the standard Poisson structure.
Furthermore, we consider the pair
(H,I)
in
A
where

H=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}p_{i}q_{i}

and

I:=(p_{1}q_{1},p_{2}q_{2},\ldots,p_{n}q_{n})\subset A

is the involutive ideal generated by the Poisson-commuting generators
p_{i}q_{i}
.

Theorem 16.5
.

Assume that
\omega=(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n})
satisfies Kolmogorov’s Diophantine condition,
then for any
R\in{\mathcal{M}}^{3}
there exists a symplectic automorphism
\varphi\in{\rm Aut\,}(\mathbb{C}\{q,p\})

such that

\varphi(H+R)=H\ {\rm mod\,}\ I^{2}

In particular,
H+R
admits an invariant ideal isomorphic to
I
.

Proof.

Consider the open set

U\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0},

with fibre

\displaystyle U_{s}

\displaystyle=\{(q,p)\in\mathbb{C}^{2n}:|q_{i}|<s,\ |p_{i}|<s\}

and consider the Kolmogorov-Poisson algebra
E=A={\mathcal{O}}^{c}(U)
.
Define

M=E^{(3)}=\left({\mathcal{M}}^{3}\right)^{c}(U),\ I^{c}=(p_{1}q_{1},\dots,p_{n}q_{n}).

A straighforward variant of Proposition
16.2
shows that the maps

(I^{2})^{c}\oplus\mathbb{C}\oplus{{\mathcal{P}}}oiss(A)^{(3)}\longrightarrow H+M,(\alpha,\beta,v)\mapsto v(f)+\alpha+\beta

admit uniformly bounded local inverse
j_{f}
,
F\in H+(I^{2})^{c}
. Thus the theorem is again a direct consequence of the general normal form theorem 13.5.

∎

Appendix A
Holomorphic functions of several variables

The theory of analytic spaces and coherent sheaves arose in a long process of merging
function theory with local algebra that can be traced back at least to the lectures of
Weierstrass. The many results obtained by various authors ( Behnke, Stein, Oka,…)
were developed to formal perfection by Cartan and Serre in the early fifties of
the last century. There are several excellent text books covering these topics in much detail.
We mention the classical books by Grauert and Remmert and the more recent three volume series by Gunning.
Only the simplest results from this theory are used at several places of the book and we outline here some salient points.

A.1.
Coherent sheaves

The theory of sheaves (of abelian groups) is obtained by abstracting from
the example of sheaves of functions and can be developed in great generality in the
context of arbitrary topological spaces. One can define homomorphisms of sheaves
making it into a category, and given a homomorphism of sheaves, one can define the
kernel, image and cokernel in the category of of sheaves. In this way we obtain for
any topological space an abelian category
Sh(X)
of sheaves (of abelian groups) on
X
.
It is fundamental fact that a sequence of sheaves

0\longrightarrow{\mathcal{F}}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{G}}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{H}}\longrightarrow 0

on a topological space
X
is exact, if and only if for any
x\in X
the corresponding
sequences on the level of stalks

0\longrightarrow{\mathcal{F}}_{x}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{G}}_{x}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{H}}_{x}\longrightarrow 0

are exact sequences of abelian groups.

An analytic space
X
comes with a sheaf of holomorphic functions
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
on it,
called the
structure sheaf
.
The prototype of such analytic spaces are
\mathbb{C}^{n}
with its sheaf
{\mathcal{O}}
described above.
An open subset
U\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
becomes an analytic space on its own by providing it
with the restriction
{\mathcal{O}}_{U}
of the sheaf
{\mathcal{O}}
to
U
as its structure sheaf.
If
f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{r}
are functions holomorphic on
U\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
, then their common
zero set

X:=\{x\in U\;\;|\;\;f_{i}(x)=0,i=1,2,\ldots,r\}\subset U

is naturally an analytic space if we define its structure sheaf to be the quotient of
{\mathcal{O}}_{U}

by the ideal sheaf
{\mathcal{I}}=(f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{r})\subset{\mathcal{O}}_{U}
generated by the
f_{i}
:

{\mathcal{O}}_{X}={\mathcal{O}}/{\mathcal{I}}.

A general analytic space is locally isomorphic to an example of this kind.

From now on we will only consider sheaves of
{\mathcal{O}}
-modules. A
free sheaf

(of rank
p
) on
X
is a direct sum of
p
copies of the structure sheaf:

{\mathcal{O}}^{p}_{X}:=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p}{\mathcal{O}}_{X}.

Its sections over an open set are just
p
-tuples of holomorphic function on that open set.

A sheaf
{\mathcal{F}}
on
X
is called
locally free
(of rank
p
) if each point
x\in X
has
a neighbourhood
U
such that
{\mathcal{F}}_{U}
is isomorphic to
{\mathcal{O}}_{U}^{p}
.
A sheaf
{\mathcal{F}}
on
X
is called
locally finitely generated
if for any
x\in X

there exists an open neighbourhood
U
of
x
and a surjection
\alpha

{\mathcal{O}}_{U}^{p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\mathcal{F}}_{U}\longrightarrow 0

The kernel sheaf of such a homomorphism need not be finitely generated, but if it
is for
any
such surjection, the sheaf
{\mathcal{F}}
is called
coherent
.

It is a non-trivial theorem that
{\mathcal{O}}
itself is coherent ( Oka’s theorem),
but once this is known one may say that a sheaf
{\mathcal{F}}
on
X
is coherent precisely
if each point has a neighbourhood
U
such that
{\mathcal{F}}_{U}
is isomorphic to the
cokernel

of a map of free sheaves, giving rise to an exact sequence of the form

{\mathcal{O}}_{U}^{p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle A}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\mathcal{O}}_{U}^{q}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{F}}_{U}\longrightarrow 0,

usually called a
presentation

of the sheaf
{\mathcal{F}}
. Here the homomorphism

A\in Hom({\mathcal{O}}_{U}^{p},{\mathcal{O}}_{U}^{q})

can be seen as a matrix with entries from
{\mathcal{O}}(U)
. So any coherent sheaf
may by given by such a matrix, but of course, very different matrices may
give rise to the same sheaf
{\mathcal{F}}
.
If
f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{r}
are functions homolorphic on
U
, then we obtain
a map

{\mathcal{O}}_{U}^{r}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(f_{1},f_{2},\ldots f_{r})}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\mathcal{O}}_{U}

Its image is the ideal sheaf
{\mathcal{I}}\subset{\mathcal{O}}_{U}
and the cokernel is just

{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
, where
X
is the common zero set of the
f_{i}
in
U
. It is
a non-trivial theorem of Cartan that
{\mathcal{I}}
is a coherent sheaf.

Given a homomorphism between coherent sheaves, kernel, image and cokernel
are again coherent, and we obtain an abelian category
Coh(U)
of coherent
sheaves on
U
.

A.2.
Cartan’s theorem A and B

Cartan proved that any closed polycylinder
K\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}

has a neighbourhood
U
containing
K
, such that for any coherent sheaf
the following holds:

Theorem A:
The restriction map that associates to a section its germ
at
a\in U

{\mathcal{F}}(U)\longrightarrow{\mathcal{F}}_{a}

is surjective. In other words,
{\mathcal{F}}
is globally generated.

Theorem B:
The higher cohomology groups of
{\mathcal{F}}
vanish:

H^{p}(U,{\mathcal{F}})=0,\;p\geq 1\;.

These cohomology groups
H^{p}(-)
can be defined in terms of Cech-complexes.
One may say that ’all’ information about the sheaf
{\mathcal{F}}
on
U
is contained
in its space of global sections.

An analytic space
X
like
U
, for which Theorem A and B hold for all
coherent sheaves, is called a
Stein space
and
this property can be characterised function-theoretically.

A.3.
Weierstraß polynomials

We denote the ring of convergent power series by

R={\mathcal{O}}_{0}=\mathbb{C}\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}\}.

An element
f\in R
has a power series expansion

f=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}a_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}

that converges and thus represents a holomorphic function on an open neighbourhood
U
of
0
. By collecting terms of equal total degree we can write

f=f_{0}+f_{1}+f_{2}+\ldots

there
f_{k}
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
k
in the coordinates

x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
. If the constant term
f(0)=f_{0}
is non-zero,
f
is non-zero
in a neighbourhood of
0
and
f
is a unit in the ring
R
. Otherwise, if
f
is a non-zero
element of
R
, the set

V(f):=\{x\in U\;\;|f(x)=0\}

defines a hypersurface which contains the origin.

If
x\in U
is a further point, then a point
\lambda x
on the line
\ell
connecting
0
and
x
lies on
V(f)

precisely when

0=f(\lambda x)=f_{1}(\lambda x)+f_{2}(\lambda x)+\ldots=f_{0}+\lambda f_{1}(x)+\lambda^{2}f_{2}(x)+\ldots

In particular
f_{k}(x)=0
for all
k
’s.
The variety of points defined by the vanishing of the polynomials

0=f_{1}(x)=f_{2}(x)=f_{3}(x)=\ldots

is conical and the corresponding set in projective space of directions
at
0
will be called the
variety
B
of bad directions
of
f
:

B:=\{P\in\mathbb{P}^{n-1}\;\;|\;\;f_{k}(P)=0,k=1,2,3,\ldots\}.

Clearly, if
f
is a non-zero element of
R
, then
B
is a proper
subset of
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
which consists of the lines through the origin contained in
V(f)
.
In this case, we have a Zariski dense open complementary set

\mathbb{P}^{n-1}\setminus B
of good directions of
f
that we simply call

f
-directions.

The smallest number
d
for which

f_{1}(x)=f_{2}(x)=\ldots,f_{d-1}(x)=0,\;\;f_{d}(x)\neq 0

is called the
multiplicity
of the series
f
.

Assume that
\ell
be an
f_{d}
-direction. By a linear change of coordinates,
we may make
\ell
into the
x_{n}
-axis. We write

x=(x^{\prime},y),\;\;x^{\prime}:=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n-1}),\;\;y:=x_{n}

and say that
f
is
y
-general of order
d
. Such an
f
has a representation of the form

f=uy^{d}+a_{1}y^{d-1}+a_{2}y^{d-2}+\ldots+a_{n-1}y+a_{n}

where
u\in R
is a unit, and
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}\in R^{\prime},\;\;a_{i}(0)=0
and
where we have put

R^{\prime}:=\mathbb{C}\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n-1}\}.

By a
Weierstraß polynomial
of degree
d
we mean a monic polynomial of degree
d
in
y
with coefficients
from
R^{\prime}
:

g=y^{d}+a_{1}y^{d-1}+\ldots+a_{d},\;\;\;a_{i}\in R^{\prime},\;\;a_{i}(0)=0.

A.4.
The Weierstraß division theorem

Theorem A.1
.

(Weierstraß division theorem)
If
g
is a Weierstraß-polynomial of degree
d
, then for any

f\in R
there exist unique
q\in R
and
r\in R^{\prime}[y]
of degree
<d
in
y

such that

f=qg+r

Example A.2
.

Consider the polynomial

g(x,y)=y^{2}-x.

Then any holomorphic function
f(x,y)
can be written in the form:

f=(y^{2}-x)g(x,y)+a(x)y+b(x).

Thus the theorem implies that
R/(y^{2}-x)
is a free
\mathbb{C}\{x\}
-module generated by the classes of
1
and
y
.

A proof was given in II.9. section 7. We used an integral
representation, which has the advantage that one obtains estimates that
show that the division and remainder operators are
(d,0)
-local over
appropriate neighbourhoods.

A.5.
The Weierstraß preparation theorem

To do a division by an
y
-general series rather than a Weierstrass polynomial,
one can use the Weierstrass preparation theorem:

Theorem A.3
.

(Weierstraß preparation theorem)
If
f\in R
is
y
-general of order
d
, there exists a unique Weierstraß
polynomial
g
of degree
d
and a unit
u\in R
such that

f=ug

Proof.

Without loss of generality we may suppose that

f(0,y)=y^{d}+o(y^{d}).

We choose an neighbourhood
\Delta\times D
adapted to
f
.
Fix
x\in\mathbb{C}^{n-1}
and denote by
\gamma_{x}
the oriented circle
\{x\}\times\partial D
and let

y_{1}(x),\dots,y_{d}(x)

be the solutions of
f(x,y)=0
. Using the residue theorem, we get

I_{k}(x):=\int_{\gamma_{x}}\frac{\xi^{k}\partial_{y}f(x,\xi)}{f(x,\xi)}d\xi=p_{k}(y_{1}(x),\dots,y_{d}(x)),

where
p_{k}
denotes the
k
-power sums of the variables:

p_{k}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{d}):=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}^{d}.

By the formulas going back to Newton, the elementary symmetric functions

\sigma_{k}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{d}):=\sum_{i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{k}}\alpha_{i_{1}}\alpha_{i_{2}}\dots\alpha_{i_{k}}

can be expressed universally in terms of the
p_{k}
:

\sigma_{1}=p_{1},\;\;\sigma_{2}=\frac{1}{2}(p_{1}^{2}-p_{2}),\ldots,\sigma_{k}=N_{k}(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k}),\ldots,

where the
N_{k}
are polynomials. We put

J_{k}(x):=N_{k}(I_{1}(x),I_{2}(x),\ldots,I_{k}(x)),\;\;\;k=1,2,\ldots,d.

The roots of the Weierstraß polynomial

g(x,y)=y^{d}-J_{1}(x)y^{d-1}+J_{2}(x)y^{d-2}+\dots+(-1)^{d}J_{d}(x)

are now the zeros of
f(x,-)
and so the function
f/g
extends holomorphically.
As its value at the origin is
1
, it is a unit
u
in
R
. Hence
f=ug
.
∎

A.6.
Cartan’s theorem
\alpha

We now formulate an important application of the division theorem to the
case of matrices.
If a vector
v=(v_{i})\in R^{q}
lies in the image of a matrix map

R^{p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle A}}{{\longrightarrow}}R^{q},

we can write

v_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}A_{ij}u_{j}

Now in general there are many choices for
u
and we want to make sure that one
can make a choice where
u
becomes small when
v
becomes small. This can be
achieved by constructing a linear map

R^{q}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle B}}{{\longrightarrow}}R^{p},

with explicit estimates on its coefficients, such that
B
is a
right
inverse over the image of
A
:

ABA=A.

The map
P:=AB
then satisfies
P^{2}=ABAB=AB=P
, hence is a projector
on the sub-space
Im(A)
:
Im(P)=Im(A)\subset R^{q}
.
As the sequence of
R
-modules

0\longrightarrow Im(A)\longrightarrow R^{q}\longrightarrow Coker(A)\longrightarrow 0

usually does not split as
R
-modules, such a map
B
can not be expected to
be
R
-linear; the entries of the matrix
B
will not be holomorphic functions,
but rather will contain division and remainder operators, but the resulting
singular behavour of
B
will be under control by the Weierstraß theorem
with estimate II.9.11.

Definition A.4
.

Let
A\in Mat(p\times q,R)
be a matrix of germs of holomorphic functions
and let
U
be an open subset on which the entries belong to

.
We say a fundamental system of neighbourhoods consisting of polydiscs

(\Delta_{\rho})\subset U

A
, there exists a linear map

B=(B_{ij}):R^{q}\longrightarrow R^{p}

with

ABA=A,

such that the entries
\|B_{ij}\|_{\rho}
are bounded by
C/\|\rho\|^{k}
for
some integer

and constant
C
, which depends only on
A
.

Theorem A.5

(Cartan’s Theorem
\alpha
)
.

Let

a matrix with entries from

. Then there
exists a Zariski dense open subset

\rho_{2}&\leq&s_{2}(\rho_{1})\\
\rho_{3}&\leq&s_{3}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})\\

We follow the arguments given by Cartan. The proof runs

We assume the truth of the theorem for

The theorem holds for the pair
(n,1)
.

ii)

If the theorem holds for

(n,q)

\displaystyle\tilde{A}\tilde{B}\tilde{A}=\tilde{A}.

In terms of these data, we define a map
B:R^{q}\longrightarrow R^{p}
by setting

B:=J\tilde{B}\lambda(Id-Ab\rho)+b\rho.

Claim:

ABA=A
.

Proof:
Let
v\in R^{p}
. One has

a(v-bav)=av-abav=av-av=0,

so we can write

v-bav=J(k)
, for some
k\in R^{N}
. Note that

(Id-Ab\rho)Av=A(Id-b\rho A)v=A(v-bav)=AJ(k)=j\tilde{A}(k).

So we find

\begin{array}[]{ccl}ABAv&=&AJ\tilde{B}\lambda(Id-Ab\rho)Av+Ab\rho Av\\
&=&j\tilde{A}\tilde{B}\lambda j\tilde{A}(k)+Abav\\
&=&j\tilde{A}\tilde{B}\tilde{A}(k)+Abav\\
&=&j\tilde{A}(k)+Abav\\
&=&AJ(k)+Abav\\
&=&A(v-bav)+Abav\\
&=&Av\\
\end{array}

where we only used the above identities between maps. This completes the
proof.

By induction, we have polydiscs adapted to
a
and
\tilde{A}
and corresponding estimates

\|b\|\leq\frac{C}{\|\rho\|^{k}},\;\;\;\|\tilde{B}_{ij}\|\leq\frac{\tilde{C}}{\|{\rho}\|^{k}}

for
\rho_{i}\leq s_{i}^{\prime}
,
\rho_{i}\leq s^{\prime\prime}_{i}
respectively and corresponding

are then
adapted to
A
for

∎

A.7.
Polycylinders adapted to a sheaf

If
U\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is an open set with compact closure
K=\overline{U}

and
A\in Hom({\mathcal{O}}^{p}(K),{\mathcal{O}}^{q}(K))
a matrix with entries holomorphic on
a neighbourhood of
K
then
A
defines a presentation of a coherent
sheaf
{\mathcal{F}}
on
U
:

{\mathcal{O}}^{p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle A}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\mathcal{O}}^{q}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{F}}\longrightarrow 0.

If we now assume that
U
is a Stein space, then there is an exact
sequence of sections over
U
:

{\mathcal{O}}^{p}(U)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle A}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\mathcal{O}}^{q}(U)\longrightarrow{\mathcal{F}}(U)\longrightarrow 0.

Furthermore, we obtain a well-defined map of Banach spaces:

{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(U)^{p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle A}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(U)^{q}.

We define
{\mathcal{F}}^{c}(U)
to be the cokernel of this map. The resulting
topology obtained on
{\mathcal{F}}^{c}(U)
is
independent
of the chosen
presentation.

Theorem A.7

(Theorem of the closed image)
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
be Stein, and let

0\longrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}(U)^{p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle A}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\mathcal{O}}(U)^{q}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{F}}(U)\longrightarrow 0

be a presentation of a coherent sheaf. Denote by
\Delta\subset U
an

A
-adapted polydisc centered at
a\in U
. Then the image of the map

{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(\Delta)^{p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle A}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(\Delta)^{q}

is closed and provides the cokernel
{\mathcal{F}}^{c}(\Delta)
with the structure of a
Banach space.

Proof.

(Cartan)
An element
g\in{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(U)^{q}
in the closure of the image of the map

A:{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(U)^{p}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(U)^{q}

can be represented as limit of a sequence of elements
g_{k}\in{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(U)^{q}

in the image of
A
with

|g_{k+1}-g_{k}|_{\Delta}\leq\frac{1}{2^{k}}

Using
A.5
we obtain
f_{k}\in{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(U)^{p}
with

g_{k+1}-g_{k}=Af_{k}

and

|f_{k}|\leq\frac{C}{2^{k}}

Consequently, the series
\sum_{k}f_{k}
converges to an element
f
with

Af=g
. So the image of
A
is closed.
∎

In particular, one can find a fundamental system of neighbourhoods of the
origin consisting of polydiscs adapted to a coherent sheaf.

A.8.
The Douady-Pourcin theorem

For more precise statements one needs to define certain analytic subsets naturally associated to
{\mathcal{F}}
, and their position
with respect to the natural stratification of the boundary
\partial\Delta
of the polydisc.
Recall that the
depth
of finitely generated module
F
over a local ring
R
is the length of a maximal
F
-regular sequence.
For
R=\mathbb{C}\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}\}
a module has depth
n
if and only if

F
free. One defines for
p=0,1,2,\ldots,n
the sets

S_{p}({\mathcal{F}}):=\{a\in U\;|\;\textup{depth}_{a}({\mathcal{F}})\leq p\}.

These are closed analytic subsets
and let
\Delta^{(p)}
the set of points of
\Delta
where exactly
p
of its coordinates of the point that lie on the boundary of the corresponding factor.
So
\Delta^{(0)}
is the interior of
\Delta
and
\Delta^{(n)}
is what we
called earlier the edge
e(\Delta)
.

Then one has the following beautiful theorem:

Theorem A.8
.

(Pourcin)
The interior of
\Delta
is
{\mathcal{F}}
-open if and only if for all
p=0,1,2,\ldots,n

S_{p}({\mathcal{F}})\cap\Delta^{(p+1)}=\emptyset.

So, for
\Delta
to be privileged, the locus where the depth of
{\mathcal{F}}
is
<n
has to be disjoint from the edge of
\Delta
. The proof of the theorem is
discussed in the notes below.
