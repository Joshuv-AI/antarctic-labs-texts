Abstract




Recursions and ODEs for the correlators in integrable systems and random matrices.

Abstract

An integrable system is often formulated as a flat connection, satisfying a Lax equation. It is given in terms of compatible systems having a common solution called the “wave function”
\Psi
living in a Lie group
G
, which satisfies some differential equations with rational coefficients.
From this wave function, it is usual to define a sequence of “correlators”
W_{n}
, that play an important role in many applications in mathematical physics.
Here, we show how to systematically obtain ordinary differential equations (ODE) and recursion relations with polynomial coefficients for the correlators.

An application is random matrix theory, where the wave functions are the expectation value of the characteristic polynomial, they form a family of orthogonal polynomials, and are known to satisfy an integrable system. The correlators are then the correlation functions of resolvents or of eigenvalue densities. We give the ODE and recursion on the matrix size that they satisfy. In addition, we discuss generic Fuchsian systems, namely, Schlesinger systems.

Introduction

Given a flat section we can describe an integrable system by a Lax equation or equivalently by compatible systems whose common solution is called the “wave function”
\Psi
living in a Lie group
G
, this solution satisfies some differential equations with rational coefficients.
Using the wave function we can define a sequence of “correlators”
(W_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, that play an important role in many applications in mathematical physics. These correlators were first introduced in the contexte of random matrix theory
[
BE09
]
, then used in
[
BDY16a
,
BDY16
]
for integrable systems, KDV, KP and intersection numbers. Let’s consider a flat section of a reductive Lie group
G
over
\mathbb{C}P^{1}
, it is known that the flat section satisfies an equation of the form
\nabla\Psi=0
, taking a faithful representation of the Lie group we can write it as ODE for the wave function, we say in this case that the system is integrable. Furthermore, given the relation between Lie group flat section and adjoint flat section one can derive a Lax equation for this system, then we can define the correlators in their most general form, this allows us to present a general framework of deriving ODEs and recursion relations for the defined correlation functions.

The paper is organized as follows:

Section 2
is devoted to present the ingredients we need for our approach, namely, the flat sections of principal bundle and adjoint bundle, we present the relation between these two flat sections, we give a general definition for the correlators.

In
Section 3
we present our approach of deriving ODEs for the defined correlators, we start by the correlator
W_{1}
then we push our method to higher correlators, in addition to the ODEs, we get that the coefficients are polynomial of all the arguments.

Then in
Section 4
we consider a family of systems indexed by a second argument

In
Section 5

Section 6.1
is devoted to applying our formalism to one-matrix model, we start by recalling basic definitions of random matrix theory, we define a matrix wave function via the normalized and dual wave function, we then make the relation between correlators and cumulants of the one-matrix model, from which we derive the ODEs and recursion for these cumulants using the approach developped in the previous sections.

We recall two-matrix model basic definitions
Section 6.2
and emphasize its relation with our formalism, we construct linear ODE and recursions for the cumulants of two-matrix model, it is related to the general definition of correlators by a well chosen adjoint flat section.

In

Preliminaries

In short, we consider an invertible matrix
\Psi(x)
that satisfies a linear differential equation with rational coefficients

\frac{d}{dx}\Psi(x)=\mathcal{D}(x)\ \Psi(x)\qquad\mathcal{D}(x)\in M_{r}({\mathbb{C}}(x)),

where
M_{r}({\mathbb{C}}(x))
denotes the set of
r\times r
matrices with entries rational functions with complex coefficients.
Invertibility of
\Psi(x)
suggests that it belongs to a representation of the Lie group
GL(r,{\mathbb{C}})
for each
x\in{\mathbb{C}}
. Therefore, it is locally a holomorphic section of a bundle over
{\mathbb{C}}P^{1}={\mathbb{C}}\cup\{\infty\}
, whose fiber is the Lie group
G=GL(r,{\mathbb{C}})
.
\nabla=d-\mathcal{D}(x)dx
is a rational connexion on this bundle and
\Psi(x)
is a flat section.

2.2
Adjoint Flat sections

The connection
\nabla
acts in the adjoint bundle by the commutator, we define
\nabla_{\text{adj}}=d-[\mathcal{D}dx,.]
.
A flat section

for each

\nabla_{\text{adj}}M=0,

(2-12)

in other words

\frac{d}{dx}M(x)=[\mathcal{D}(x),M(x)].

(2-13)

Lemma 2.1

Let
\Psi(x)
be a flat section of the group bundle. Every adjoint flat section
M(x)
must be of the form:

M(x)=\Psi(x)E\Psi(x)^{-1}=\operatorname{Ad}_{\Psi(x)}E,

(2-14)

where
E\in{\mathfrak{g}}
is constant
dE/dx=0
.

proof:
This is a classical result.
Let us redo it here, in a matrix representation:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}M(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\left(\Psi(x)E\Psi(x)^{-1}\right)

(2-15)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\Psi(x)\ \ E\Psi(x)^{-1}+\Psi(x)\ \ E\frac{d}{dx}\Psi(x)^{-1}+\Psi(x)\frac{dE}{dx}\Psi(x)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\Psi(x)\ \ E\Psi(x)^{-1}-\Psi(x)\ \ E\Psi(x)^{-1}\frac{d}{dx}\Psi(x)\ \Psi(x)^{-1}+\Psi(x)\frac{dE}{dx}\Psi(x)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\frac{d}{dx}\Psi(x)\Psi(x)^{-1},\Psi(x)\ \ E\Psi(x)^{-1}\right]+\Psi(x)\frac{dE}{dx}\Psi(x)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\mathcal{D}(x),\ M(x)\right]+\Psi(x)\frac{dE}{dx}\Psi(x)^{-1}.

It satisfies
dM/dx=[\mathcal{D},M]
if and only if
dE/dx=0
.

\square

Definition 2.2
(Correlators)

Let
\Psi(x)
be a once for all chosen flat section of the group bundle.
Let
M(x.E)=\Psi(x)E\Psi(x)^{-1}
be a flat section of the adjoint bundle, parametrized by

.
We define:

W_{1}(x.E)={\rm Tr}\,\mathcal{D}(x)M(x.E),

(2-18)

W_{2}(x_{1}.E_{1},x_{2}.E_{2})=\frac{1}{(x_{1}-x_{2})^{2}}{\rm Tr}\,M(x_{1}.E_{1})M(x_{2}.E_{2}),

(2-19)

and for

W_{n}(x_{1}.E_{1},\dots,x_{n}.E_{n})=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}^{1-\text{cycle}}}(-1)^{\sigma}\frac{{\rm Tr}\,\prod_{i=1}^{n}M(x_{\sigma^{i}(1)}.E_{\sigma^{i}(1)})}{\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x_{\sigma(i)}-x_{i})},

(2-20)

where
\mathfrak{S}_{n}^{1-\text{cycle}}
is the subset of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
of permutations having just 1-cycle.

Example 2.4
(Correlator

\displaystyle=

3.1
ODE for
W_{1}

Let us warm up with
W_{1}(X)
with
X=x.E
. Let
′

We have

\displaystyle W^{\prime}_{1}(X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle Tr}\,\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(x)M(X)+{\rm Tr}\,\mathcal{D}(x)M^{\prime}(X)

(3-1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle Tr}\,\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(x)M(X)+{\rm Tr}\,\mathcal{D}(x)[\mathcal{D}(x),M(X)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle Tr}\,\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(x)M(X)+{\rm Tr}\,[\mathcal{D}(x),\mathcal{D}(x)]M(X)\qquad

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle Tr}\,\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(x)M(X),

where the previous to last equality follows due to cyclicity of the trace.

Then

\displaystyle W^{\prime\prime}_{1}(X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle Tr}\,\mathcal{D}^{\prime\prime}(x)M(X)+{\rm Tr}\,\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(x)M^{\prime}(X)

(3-2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle Tr}\,\mathcal{D}^{\prime\prime}(x)M(X)+{\rm Tr}\,\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(x)[\mathcal{D}(x),M(X)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle Tr}\,\mathcal{D}^{\prime\prime}(x)M(X)+{\rm Tr}\,[\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(x),\mathcal{D}(x)]M(X)\qquad

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle Tr}\,\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime\prime}(x)+[\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(x),\mathcal{D}(x)]\right)M(X).

We can repeat this and get

\frac{d^{k}}{dx^{k}}W_{1}(X)={\rm Tr}\,\mathcal{D}_{k}(x)M(X),

(3-3)

with the matrix
\mathcal{D}_{k}(x)
computed as follows

\mathcal{D}_{0}=\mathcal{D},\quad\mathcal{D}_{1}=\mathcal{D}^{\prime},\quad\mathcal{D}_{2}=\mathcal{D}^{\prime\prime}+[\mathcal{D}^{\prime},\mathcal{D}],\quad\mathcal{D}_{3}=\mathcal{D}^{\prime\prime\prime}+2[\mathcal{D}^{\prime\prime},\mathcal{D}]+[[\mathcal{D}^{\prime},\mathcal{D}],\mathcal{D}]

(3-4)

and recursively

\mathcal{D}_{k+1}=\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{k}+[\mathcal{D}_{k},\mathcal{D}].

(3-5)

Now notice that each
\mathcal{D}_{k}(x)
is a rational function of
x
valued in the Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}
, that is a finite dimensional vector space of dimension
\dim{\mathfrak{g}}
.
Therefore, at most
\dim{\mathfrak{g}}
of them can be linearly independent over the field
{\mathbb{C}}(x)

which implies that there must exist some rational coefficients
\alpha_{k}(x)
, such that

\sum_{k=0}^{\dim{\mathfrak{g}}}\alpha_{k}(x)\mathcal{D}_{k}(x)=0.

(3-6)

And upon multiplying by the common denominator, we can assume that
\alpha_{k}(x)\in{\mathbb{C}}[x]
are polynomial.
Therefore,

Theorem 3.1

W_{1}(x)
satisfies a linear differential equation of order
\dim{\mathfrak{g}}
, with polynomial coefficients
\alpha_{k}(x)

\sum_{k=0}^{\dim{\mathfrak{g}}}\alpha_{k}(x)\frac{d^{k}}{dx^{k}}W_{1}(x.E)=0.

(3-7)

The coefficients

are determined by

\sum_{k=0}^{\dim{\mathfrak{g}}}\alpha_{k}(x)\mathcal{D}_{k}(x)=0\qquad\text{where}\quad\mathcal{D}_{0}=\mathcal{D},\quad\mathcal{D}_{k+1}=\mathcal{D}_{k}^{\prime}+[\mathcal{D}_{k},\mathcal{D}].

(3-8)

Notice that the matrices

are independent of
E

\alpha_{k}(x)

This implies that for any choice of

Therefore,

Theorem 3.2

The space of solutions of

\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\dim{\mathfrak{g}}}\alpha_{k}(x)\frac{d^{k}}{dx^{k}}\right)w(x)=0

(3-9)

is the vector space of dimension
\dim{\mathfrak{g}}
generated by

(3-10)

We begin by writing

\displaystyle+\sum_{l}\left(Q_{k;i^{\prime},j^{\prime},l,j}(x_{1},x_{2})\mathcal{D}(x_{1})_{l,i}-Q_{k;i^{\prime},j^{\prime},i,l}(x_{1},x_{2})\mathcal{D}(x_{1})_{j,l}\right).

Now consider a compatible

Proposition 5.1
(Compatibility)

The ODE and recursion are compatible:

\mathcal{D}^{(N+1)}(x)\mathcal{R}^{(N)}(x)-\mathcal{R}^{(N)}(x)\mathcal{D}^{(N)}(x)=\frac{d}{dx}\mathcal{R}^{(N)}(x).

(5-3)

We write it as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{(N+1)}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{(N)}(x)\mathcal{D}^{(N)}(x)\mathcal{R}^{(N)}(x)^{-1}+\frac{d}{dx}\mathcal{R}^{(N)}(x)\ \mathcal{R}^{(N)}(x)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{(N)}(x)\ \mathcal{D}^{(N,1)}(x)\ \mathcal{R}^{(N)}(x)^{-1},

\mathcal{D}^{(N,1)}(x)=\mathcal{D}^{(N)}(x)+\mathcal{R}^{(N)}(x)^{-1}\frac{d}{dx}\mathcal{R}^{(N)}(x).

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle Tr}\,\mathcal{R}^{(N)}(x)^{-1}\mathcal{D}^{(N+1)}(x)\mathcal{R}^{(N)}(x)M^{(N)}(x.E)

E_{1},\dots,E_{n}
:

\sum_{k=0}^{(\dim{\mathfrak{g}})^{n}}\tilde{\alpha}_{k,n}^{(N)}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})W_{n}^{(N+k)}(x_{1}.E_{1},\dots,x_{n}.E_{n})=0,

(5-14)

where
\tilde{\alpha}_{k,n}^{(N)}
are determined by:

\displaystyle\text{Initial term}:

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}^{(N)}_{0,n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=Q_{0,n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}).

(5-15)

\displaystyle\text{Recursion}:

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}^{(N)}_{k+1,n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=

\displaystyle\left(\left(\operatorname{Ad}_{\mathcal{R}^{(N+k)}(x_{1})}\otimes\dots\otimes\operatorname{Ad}_{\mathcal{R}^{(N+k)}(x_{n})}\right)\tilde{Q}^{(N)}_{k,n}\right)(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}).

\displaystyle\text{Linear relation}:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{(\dim{\mathfrak{g}})^{n}}\tilde{\alpha}_{k,n}^{(N)}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\tilde{Q}^{(N)}_{k,n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=0.

Notice that the initial term
\tilde{Q}^{(N)}_{0,n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=Q_{0,n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
is actually independent of
N
.

proof:
By definition
W_{n}^{(N+k)}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\tilde{Q}_{k,n}^{(N)}(M(x_{1}.E_{1}),\dots,M(x_{n}.E_{n}))
. Therefore, proving the recursion and linear relation will conclude the proof.

The tensor
Q_{0,n}
is given by the application:

\displaystyle Q_{0,n}\colon{\mathfrak{g}}^{\otimes n}

\displaystyle\to{\mathbb{C}}

(5-16)

\displaystyle(M_{1},...,M_{n})

\displaystyle\mapsto\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}^{1-\text{cycle}}}(-1)^{\sigma}\frac{{\rm Tr}\,\prod_{i=1}^{n}M_{\sigma^{i}(1)}}{\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x_{\sigma(i)}-x_{i})}

(5-17)

Due to
Definition 2.2
and
Proposition 5.2
we get,

\displaystyle W_{n}^{(N+1)}\

\displaystyle=Q_{0,n}(M^{(N+1)}(X_{1}),\cdots,M^{(N+1)}(X_{n}))

(5-18)

\displaystyle=Q_{0,n}(\operatorname{Ad}_{\mathcal{R}^{(N)}(x_{1})}M^{(N)}(X_{1}),\cdots,\operatorname{Ad}_{\mathcal{R}^{(N)}(x_{n})}M^{(N)}(X_{n}))

(5-19)

\displaystyle\eqqcolon\left(\left(\operatorname{Ad}_{\mathcal{R}^{(N)}(x_{1})}\otimes\dots\otimes\operatorname{Ad}_{\mathcal{R}^{(N)}(x_{n})}\right)Q_{0,n}\right)(M^{(N)}(X_{1}),\cdots,M^{(N)}(X_{n})).

(5-20)

By induction, we conclude that,

\tilde{Q}^{(N)}_{k+1,n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\left(\left(\operatorname{Ad}_{\mathcal{R}^{(N+k)}(x_{1})}\otimes\dots\otimes\operatorname{Ad}_{\mathcal{R}^{(N+k)}(x_{n})}\right)\tilde{Q}^{(N)}_{k,n}\right)(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}).

(5-21)

Finally, since
Q^{(N)}_{k,n}\in({\mathfrak{g}}^{*})^{\otimes n}
at most
(\dim{\mathfrak{g}})^{n}
of them can be linearly independent. Therefore, there exist coefficients
\tilde{\alpha}_{k,n}^{(N)}\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
such that

\sum_{k=0}^{(\dim{\mathfrak{g}})^{n}}\tilde{\alpha}_{k,n}^{(N)}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\tilde{Q}^{(N)}_{k,n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=0.

(5-22)

\square

Remark, that in the case of
{\mathfrak{g}}={\mathfrak{g}}l(r,{\mathbb{C}})
the presented proof for the recursion could be formulated using index notation,
eq. 3-45
reads

Q_{0,n}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}^{1-cycle}}(-1)^{\sigma}\sum_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{n}}\frac{e^{1}_{l_{1}l_{\sigma(1)}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e^{n}_{l_{n}l_{\sigma(n)}}}{\prod_{i}(x_{\sigma(i)}-x_{i})}.

(5-23)

Moreover, with the use of
Definition 2.2
and
Proposition 5.2

\displaystyle W_{n}^{(N+1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}^{1-\text{cycle}}}(-1)^{\sigma}\frac{{\rm Tr}\,\prod_{i=1}^{n}M^{(N+1)}(x_{\sigma^{i}(1)}.E_{\sigma^{i}(1)})}{\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x_{\sigma(i)}-x_{i})}

(5-24)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}^{1-cycle}}(-1)^{\sigma}\frac{{\rm Tr}\,\left(\prod_{i}\mathcal{R}^{(N)}(x_{\sigma^{i}(1)})M^{(N)}(x_{\sigma^{i}(1)})\mathcal{R}^{(N)}(x_{\sigma^{i}(1)})^{-1}\right)}{\prod_{i}(x_{\sigma(i)}-x_{i})}

\displaystyle=\left(Q_{0,n}\rvert_{e^{i}_{l_{i},l_{\sigma(i)}}\to\operatorname{Ad}_{\mathcal{R}^{(N)}\left(x_{i}\right)}e^{i}_{l_{i},l_{\sigma(i)}}}\right)(M^{(N)}(x_{1}),\ldots,M^{(N)}(x_{N}))

which leads to

\tilde{Q}^{(N)}_{1,n}=\left(\left(\operatorname{Ad}_{\mathcal{R}^{(N)}(x_{1})}\otimes\dots\otimes\operatorname{Ad}_{\mathcal{R}^{(N)}(x_{n})}\right)Q_{0,n}\right)(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}).

(5-25)

Finally,
\tilde{Q}^{(N)}_{k,n}
is obtained by induction.

6
Applications

6.1
1-Matrix model

Expectation values of spectral densities of random matrices, typically fit in the framwork developed here.
So let us see how it applies to random matrices to get differential equations and recursions.

6.1.1
Introduction

Usually, random matrix theory is defined with a probability law on a set of Hermitian (resp. Normal) matrices with a measure continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure.

However, since most of the time the random variables of interest concern the eigenvalues, we may also directly define random matrix theory as its marginal law of eigenvalues.

The eigenvalues induced measure (possibly complex measure) takes the following form for the diagonal matrix
\Lambda
of eigenvalues:

d\mu(\Lambda)=\frac{1}{N!Z}\ \Delta(\Lambda)^{2}e^{-{\rm Tr}\,V(\Lambda)}d\Lambda,\quad\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{N})\in\gamma^{N},

(6-1)

where the function
V
is called the potential,
d\Lambda
is the product measure
d\Lambda=d\lambda_{1}\dots d\lambda_{N}
on
\gamma^{N}
,
and the Vandermonde
\Delta(\Lambda)
is defined as

\Delta(\Lambda)=\prod_{i<j}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}),

(6-2)

and the partition function
Z
is

Z=\frac{1}{N!}\int_{\gamma^{N}}\ \Delta(\Lambda)^{2}e^{-{\rm Tr}\,V(\Lambda)}d\lambda_{1}\dots d\lambda_{N}.

(6-3)

The integration domain
\gamma
is the space to which the
\lambda_{i}
s belong.
It needs not be the real axis, it may be any space on which this measure can be defined, it could be for example a Polish subspace of
{\mathbb{R}}
, but it can also be a Jordan arc in the complex plane, as long as
Z
is an absolutely convergent integral.

Example 6.1
(Gaussian Unitary Ensemble (GUE))

The Gaussian Unitary Ensemble is the case
\gamma={\mathbb{R}}
and
V(x)=\frac{1}{2}x^{2}
. It is, thus, a probability law on
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
:

d\mu(\Lambda)=\frac{1}{Z^{(N)}}\ \Delta(\Lambda)^{2}\ e^{-\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}\,\Lambda^{2}}d\Lambda,

(6-4)

Z^{(N)}
is known to be proportional to the Barnes function

Z^{(N)}=(2\pi)^{\frac{N}{2}}\ \prod_{k=0}^{N-1}k!.

(6-5)

6.1.2
Potentials and Jordan arcs

It is customary to consider potentials to be polynomials or rational, and to choose
\gamma
a Jordan arc such that the measure vanishes at the boundaries of
\gamma
(the boundaries may be at
\infty
).

Polynomial case
:

Let
V(x)\in{\mathbb{C}}[x]
be a polynomial with
\deg V\geq 1
called the potential.
Notice that
e^{-V(x)}
can vanish only at
\infty
.

An integral

\int_{\gamma}f(x)e^{-V(x)}dx,

(6-6)

where
f(x)\in{\mathbb{C}}[x]
is invariant under homotopic deformations of the smooth Jordan arc
\gamma
, and it can be absolutely convergent only if
\gamma
goes to
\infty
in directions in which
\Re V(x)\to+\infty
.

There are exactly
\deg V
angular sectors around
\infty
in which
\Re V(x)\to+\infty
, and thus there are
\deg V-1=\deg V^{\prime}
independent possible Jordan arcs (up to addition by concatenation), that go from an allowed sector to another one.
We shall say that the homology space of “admissible” paths is of dimension

\dim(\text{Homology space of admissible paths})=\deg V^{\prime}.

(6-7)

If
V(x)
is an even degree real polynomial whose leading coefficient is
>0
, then in that case,
\gamma={\mathbb{R}}
is an admissible path.

For example if
V(x)=\frac{1}{2}x^{2}
, then
\deg V^{\prime}=1
and thus
\gamma={\mathbb{R}}
is the only (up to homotopy and multiplication by a non-zero scalar) admissible path.

Otherwise admissible paths are often complex paths.

Rational case
:

We repeat the same considerations if
V^{\prime}(x)\in{\mathbb{C}}(x)
is a rational function. This means that
V(x)
may have logarithmic singularities.
e^{-V}
may vanish only at the poles of
V^{\prime}
.

An integral

\int_{\gamma}f(x)e^{-V(x)}dx,

(6-8)

where
f(x)\in{\mathbb{C}}[x]
is invariant under homotopic deformations of the smooth Jordan arc
\gamma
, and it can be convergent only if
\gamma
approaches singularities of
V
in directions in which
\Re V(x)\to+\infty
.

It is explained in
[
BE06a
]
how to construct the homology space of all admissible paths, and its dimension is found to be

\dim=\deg V^{\prime}=\text{sum of degrees of all poles, including the pole at }\infty.

(6-9)

6.1.3
Definitions

Wave functions and orthogonal polynomials
:

Definition 6.1
(Wave functions)

Let

p^{(N)}(x)=\int_{\gamma^{N}}d\mu(\Lambda)\ \det(x-\Lambda).

(6-10)

It is a polynomial of
x
of degree
N
(it is the expectation value of the characteristic polynomial).

Proposition 6.1
(Orthogonal polynomials)

The polynomials
p^{(N)}
form a family of orthogonal polynomials for the measure
e^{-V(x)}dx
on
\gamma
:

\int_{\gamma}dx\ p^{(N)}(x)\ p^{(N^{\prime})}(x)e^{-V(x)}=h^{(N)}\ \delta_{N,N^{\prime}}.

(6-11)

proof:
It is a well known result
[
Hei78
,
Sze39
,
Meh04
]

\square

Definition 6.2
(Normalized wave functions)

We thus define

\psi^{(N)}(x)=\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}V(x)}}{\sqrt{h^{(N)}}}\ p^{(N)}(x),\quad h^{(N)}=\int_{\gamma}dx\ p^{(N)}(x)p^{(N)}(x)e^{-V(x)},\quad\gamma_{N}=\sqrt{\frac{h^{(N)}}{h^{(N-1)}}}.

(6-12)

They form an orthonormal
L_{2}(\gamma)
family:

\int_{\gamma}dx\ \psi^{(N)}(x)\psi^{(N^{\prime})}(x)=\delta_{N,N^{\prime}}.

(6-13)

Definition 6.3
(Dual wave functions)

We also define

\phi^{(N)}(x)=\frac{e^{\frac{1}{2}V(x)}}{\sqrt{h^{(N)}}}\int_{\gamma}dx^{\prime}e^{-V(x^{\prime})}\frac{p^{(N)}(x^{\prime})}{x-x^{\prime}}.

(6-14)

Definition 6.4
(Matrix of wave functions)

The matrix wave function is defined as:

\Psi^{(N)}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\psi^{(N-1)}(x)&\phi^{(N-1)}(x)\cr\psi^{(N)}(x)&\phi^{(N)}(x)\cr\end{pmatrix}.

(6-15)

Example 6.2
(Gaussian (GUE))

For the Hermite polynomial,
p^{(N)}(x)=H_{N}(x)
, we have:

\int_{\mathbb{R}}H_{N}(x)H_{M}(x)e^{-\frac{1}{2}x^{2}}dx=\sqrt{2\pi}N!\ \delta_{N,M},\quad h^{(N)}=\sqrt{2\pi}N!,\quad\gamma^{(N)}=\sqrt{N}.

(6-16)

6.1.4
Resolvents

Definition 6.5
(Resolvents)

Let

W_{1}^{(N)}(x)=\int_{\gamma^{N}}d\mu(\Lambda)\ {\rm Tr}\,\frac{1}{x-\Lambda},

(6-17)

and in general

\hat{W}_{n}^{(N)}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\frac{\delta_{n,2}}{(x_{1}-x_{2})^{2}}+\int_{\gamma^{N}}\ d\mu(\Lambda)\prod_{i=1}^{n}{\rm Tr}\,\frac{1}{x_{i}-\Lambda}.

(6-18)

Let
W_{n}
be their cumulants, defined by inverting the formula:

\hat{W}_{n}^{(N)}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\sum_{\mu\vdash\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell(\mu)}W^{(N)}_{|\mu_{i}|}(\mu_{i}),

(6-19)

i.e. the
\hat{W}_{n}^{(N)}
are the sums over all partitions of the
n
variables, of products of cumulants of each part.

The system of equations (
6-19
) that define the cumulants is a triangular system easy to invert, it gives for example
W_{1}^{(N)}=\hat{W}_{1}^{(N)}
, and the
n=2
cumulant is the covariance of resolvents:

W_{2}^{(N)}(x_{1},x_{2})=\hat{W}_{2}^{(N)}(x_{1},x_{2})-W_{1}^{(N)}(x_{1})W_{1}^{(N)}(x_{2}).

(6-20)

Similarly

\displaystyle W_{3}^{(N)}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{W}_{3}^{(N)}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})-W_{1}^{(N)}(x_{1})\hat{W}_{2}^{(N)}(x_{2},x_{3})

(6-21)

\displaystyle-W_{1}^{(N)}(x_{2})\hat{W}_{2}^{(N)}(x_{1},x_{3})-W_{1}^{(N)}(x_{3})\hat{W}_{2}^{(N)}(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle+2W_{1}^{(N)}(x_{1})W_{1}^{(N)}(x_{2})W_{1}^{(N)}(x_{3}).

6.1.5
Spectral densities and correlations

Definition 6.6
(Spectral density and correlations)

Let
x\in\gamma
, define the “expected spectral density”

\rho_{1}^{(N)}(x)=\int_{\gamma^{N}}d\mu(\Lambda)\ \sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta(x-\lambda_{i}).

(6-22)

In other words:

\frac{1}{N}\rho_{1}^{(N)}(x)
is the probability that
x
is an eigenvalue of
\Lambda
.

and in general for
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\gamma^{n}
, define

\hat{\rho}_{n}^{(N)}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\int_{\gamma^{N}}\ d\mu(\Lambda)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta(x_{i}-\lambda_{j})\right).

(6-23)

Let
\rho_{n}
be their cumulants, defined by the formula:

\hat{\rho}_{n}^{(N)}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\sum_{\mu\vdash\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell(\mu)}\rho^{(N)}_{|\mu_{i}|}(\mu_{i}),

(6-24)

i.e. the
\hat{\rho}_{n}^{(N)}
are the sums over all partitions of the
n
variables of products of cumulants of each part.

In other words:

\frac{1}{N^{n}}\hat{\rho}_{n}^{(N)}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
is the expectation that
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
are simultaneously eigenvalues, it is interpreted as the correlation function of
n
eigenvalues.

The cumulants
\frac{1}{N^{n}}{\rho}_{n}^{(N)}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
are called the connected correlation function of
n
eigenvalues, they generalize the covariance for
n\geq 3
.

Resolvents and spectral correlations are closely related.

•

Resolvents are “Stieltjes transforms” of spectral correlations:

W_{1}^{(N)}(x)=\int_{x^{\prime}\in\gamma}\frac{1}{x-x^{\prime}}\ \rho_{1}^{(N)}(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime},

(6-25)

W_{n}^{(N)}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\int_{\gamma^{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{x_{i}-x^{\prime}_{i}}\ \rho_{n}^{(N)}(x^{\prime}_{1},\dots,x^{\prime}_{n})dx^{\prime}_{1}\dots dx^{\prime}_{n}.

(6-26)

•

Spectral correlations are discontinuities of resolvents.
Notice that the resolvents are ill-defined whenever some
x_{i}
’s belong to the Jordan arc
\gamma
.
In fact they have a discontinuity across
\gamma
, and this discontinuity is proportional to the spectral density:

\rho_{1}^{(N)}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\left(W^{(N)}_{1}(x_{\text{left}})-W^{(N)}_{1}(x_{\text{right}})\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\operatorname{Disc}W^{(N)}_{1}(x),

(6-27)

\rho_{n}^{(N)}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\frac{1}{(2\pi i)^{n}}\operatorname{Disc}_{x_{1}}\dots\operatorname{Disc}_{x_{n}}W^{(N)}_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}).

(6-28)

Corollary 6.1
(Same linear ODE and recursions)

As a consequence of
eq. 6-28
, if
W_{n}
satisfies a linear relation (ODE, PDE or recursion) with rational coefficients (in the variables
x,x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
), then the spectral correlation
\rho_{n}
satisfy the same linear relation.

6.1.6
Determinantal formulae

The following relation is well known
[
BE09
]
,
[
EKR18
, p. 162-163]

Proposition 6.2
(Determinantal formulae)

Let

E=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\cr 0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad M^{(N)}(x)=\Psi^{(N)}(x)E\Psi^{(N)}(x)^{-1}.

(6-29)

We have

W_{1}^{(N)}(x)=\frac{V^{\prime}(x)}{2}+{\rm Tr}\,\Psi^{(N)^{\prime}}(x)E\Psi^{(N)}(x)^{-1},

(6-30)

W_{2}^{(N)}(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{1}{(x_{1}-x_{2})^{2}}{\rm Tr}\,M^{(N)}(x_{1})M^{(N)}(x_{2})-\frac{1}{(x_{1}-x_{2})^{2}},

(6-31)

and for
n\geq 3
we have

W_{n}^{(N)}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=W_{n}(x_{1}.E,\dots,x_{n}.E),

(6-32)

where the right hand side is
eq. 2-20
of

.

6.1.7
Recursion and ODE for the wave functions

3-terms recursion relations
:

The following is well known
[
Sze39
,
Meh04
]

Proposition 6.3
(Jacobi 3-term recursion relation)

The orthogonal polynomials satisfy a 3 terms recursion relation

xp^{(N)}(x)=p^{(N+1)}(x)+S_{N}p^{(N)}(x)+\frac{h^{(N)}}{h^{(N-1)}}p^{(N-1)}(x),

(6-33)

which gives

x\psi^{(N)}(x)=\gamma^{(N+1)}\psi^{(N+1)}(x)+S_{N}\psi^{(N)}(x)+\gamma^{(N)}\psi^{(N-1)}(x).

(6-34)

We write it:

x\psi^{(N)}(x)=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}}Q_{N,m}\psi^{(m)}(x),

(6-35)

where
Q
is an infinite matrix, with only 3 bands:

Q_{n,m}=S_{n}\delta_{n,m}+\gamma^{(n)}\delta_{n,m+1}+\gamma^{(m)}\delta_{n+1,m}.

(6-36)

It is symmetric,
Q_{n,m}=Q_{m,n}
i.e.

Q=Q^{T}.

(6-37)

In particular,
S_{N}=Q_{N,N}
.
It implies a recursion for the matrix
\Psi
:

\Psi^{(N+1)}(x)=\mathcal{R}^{(N)}(x)\Psi^{(N)}(x),

(6-38)

where

\mathcal{R}^{(N)}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\cr\frac{-\gamma^{(N)}}{\gamma^{(N+1)}}&\frac{x-S_{N}}{\gamma^{(N+1)}}\end{pmatrix}

(6-39)

is called the shift operator, or sometimes the “ladder” operator.

\mathcal{R}^{(N)}(x)
is a matrix polynomial of
x
of degree 1.
Its inverse is also polynomial of degree 1:

\mathcal{R}^{(N)}(x)^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{x-S_{N}}{\gamma^{(N)}}&-\frac{\gamma^{(N+1)}}{\gamma^{(N)}}\cr 1&0\end{pmatrix}.

(6-40)

Example 6.3
(GUE)

Hermite polynomials satisfy

p^{(N+1)}(x)=xp^{(N)}(x)-Np^{(N-1)}(x)

(6-41)

\implies\quad\gamma^{(N+1)}\psi^{(N+1)}(x)=x\psi^{(N)}(x)-\gamma^{(N)}\psi^{(N-1)}(x).

(6-42)

Definition 6.7
(Iterated shift operator)

Let

\mathcal{R}^{(N,k)}(x):=\mathcal{R}^{(N+k-1)}(x)\mathcal{R}^{(N+k-2)}(x)\cdots\mathcal{R}^{(N)}(x),

(6-43)

which is a matrix, polynomial of
x
of degree at most
k
.

It is such that

\Psi^{(N+k)}(x)=\mathcal{R}^{(N,k)}(x)\Psi^{(N)}(x).

(6-44)

ODE for the wave function
:

Proposition 6.4
(ODE for
\Psi
)

If
V^{\prime}(x)\in{\mathbb{C}}(x)
is a rational function, then the orthogonal polynomials satisfy an ODE with rational coefficients, which in matrix form can be written

\frac{d}{dx}\Psi^{(N)}(x)=\mathcal{D}^{(N)}(x)\Psi^{(N)}(x),

(6-45)

where

\mathcal{D}^{(N)}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{-V^{\prime}(x)}{2}&0\cr 0&\frac{V^{\prime}(x)}{2}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}w_{N-1,N-1}(x)&w_{N-1,N}(x)\cr w_{N,N-1}(x)&w_{N,N}(x)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&\gamma^{(N)}\cr-\gamma^{(N)}&0\end{pmatrix},

(6-46)

and

\displaystyle w_{N,N^{\prime}}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{h^{(N)}h^{(N^{\prime})}}}\int_{\gamma}e^{-V(x^{\prime})}dx^{\prime}\ p^{(N)}(x^{\prime})\frac{V^{\prime}(x)-V^{\prime}(x^{\prime})}{x-x^{\prime}}p^{(N^{\prime})}(x^{\prime})

(6-47)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{V^{\prime}(x)-V^{\prime}(Q)}{x-Q}\right)_{N,N^{\prime}},

with the matrix
Q
defined in
eq. 6-36
.

We have

{\rm Tr}\,\mathcal{D}^{(N)}(x)=0.

(6-48)

proof:
See
[
BE06a
]
,
[
EKR18
, p. 155]
.
\square

Example 6.4
(GUE)

Hermite polynomials satisfy

\frac{d}{dx}p^{(N)}(x)=Np^{(N-1)}(x)=xp^{(N)}(x)-p^{(N+1)}(x),\quad\frac{d}{dx}p^{(N+1)}(x)=(N+1)p^{(N)}(x).

(6-49)

Therefore

\frac{d}{dx}\begin{pmatrix}p^{(N-1)}(x)\cr p^{(N)}(x)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}x&-1\cr N&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}p^{(N-1)}(x)\cr p^{(N)}(x)\end{pmatrix},

(6-50)

which implies

\frac{d}{dx}\Psi^{(N)}(x)=\mathcal{D}^{(N)}(x)\Psi^{(N)}(x),\quad\mathcal{D}^{(N)}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{2}x&\gamma^{(N)}\cr-\gamma^{(N)}&\frac{1}{2}x\end{pmatrix}.

(6-51)

Notice that

(6-52)

\det\left(y-\mathcal{D}^{(N)}(x)\right)=y^{2}-\frac{1}{4}x^{2}+(\gamma^{(N)})^{2}=y^{2}-\frac{1}{4}x^{2}+N.

(6-53)

The eigenvalues of

.

(6-54)

with
\mathcal{R}^{(N)}=\mathcal{R}^{(N)}(0)
. And
(\mathcal{R}^{(N)^{\prime}})^{2}=0
nilpotent, where

(6-55)

proof:

Both properties follow from direct computation.

\square

\mathcal{R}^{(N)}(x_{2})\mathcal{R}^{(N)}(x_{1})^{-1}=\mathcal{R}^{(N)}(\text{Id}+(x_{2}-x_{1})\mathcal{R}^{(N)^{\prime}})(\mathcal{R}^{(N)})^{-1}.

(6-56)

M^{(N+1)}(x)=\mathcal{R}^{(N)}(x)M^{(N)}(x)\mathcal{R}^{(N)}(x)^{-1},\quad\frac{d}{dx}M^{(N)}(x)=[\mathcal{D}^{(N)}(x),M^{(N)}(x)].

(6-57)

proof:

W_{1}^{(N+k)}(x)={\rm Tr}\,\mathcal{D}^{(N)}_{k}(x)M^{(N)}(x)+\frac{V^{\prime}(x)}{2},

(6-59)

Theorem 6.1
(Recursion)

At most 3 of the matrices
\mathcal{D}^{(N)}_{0}(x),\dots,\mathcal{D}^{(N)}_{3}(x)
can be linearly independent (use

), therefore there exist some coefficients
C^{(N,k)}(x)

x
, such that

\sum_{k=0}^{3}C^{(N,k)}(x)\mathcal{D}^{(N)}_{k}(x)=-2x.

(6-60)

In analogy with
Theorem 3.1
, this implies

\sum_{k=0}^{3}C^{(N,k)}(x)W_{1}^{(N+k)}(x)=-2x,

(6-61)

i.e.
W_{1}^{(N)}(x)
satisfies a linear recursion relation of order 3, with coefficients polynomials of
x
.

Example 6.5
(Recursion of
W_{1}^{(N)}
in GUE)

In the case of GUE
W_{1}^{(N)}
satisfies
eq. 6-61
with

\displaystyle C^{(N,0)}=-N-1\,,\quad C^{(N,1)}=x^{2}-N-3\,,\quad C^{(N,2)}=-x^{2}+N\,,\quad C^{(N,3)}=N+2\,.

(6-62)

This gives

(N+2)W_{1}^{(N+3)}(x)-(x^{2}-N)W_{1}^{(N+2)}(x)+(x^{2}-N-3)W_{1}^{(N+1)}(x)-(N+1)W_{1}^{(N)}(x)=-2x.

(6-63)

Theorem 6.2
(ODE)

Let

{\hat{\mathcal{D}}}^{(N,0)}(x)=\mathcal{D}^{(N)}(x),\quad{\hat{\mathcal{D}}}^{(N,k+1)}(x)=\frac{d}{dx}{\hat{\mathcal{D}}}^{(N,k)}(x)+[{\hat{\mathcal{D}}}^{(N,k)}(x),\mathcal{D}^{(N)}(x)].

(6-64)

At most 3 of the matrices
{\hat{\mathcal{D}}}^{(N,0)}(x),\dots,{\hat{\mathcal{D}}}^{(N,3)}(x)
can be linearly independent, therefore there exist some coefficients
{\hat{C}}^{(N,k)}(x)
, polynomials of
x
, such that

\sum_{k=0}^{3}{\hat{C}}^{(N,k)}(x){\hat{\mathcal{D}}}^{(N,k)}(x)=2N.

(6-65)

This implies

\sum_{k=0}^{3}{\hat{C}}^{(N,k)}(x)\frac{d^{k}}{dx^{k}}\ W_{1}^{(N)}(x)=2N,

(6-66)

W_{1}^{(N)}
satisfies an ODE of order 3 with polynomial coefficients.

Example 6.6
(GUE)

W_{1}^{(N)}(x)
satisfies an ODE of order 3:

\left(x+(4N-x^{2})\frac{d}{dx}+\frac{d^{3}}{dx^{3}}\right)W_{1}^{(N)}(x)=2N.

(6-67)

Example 6.7
(Consequence for moments of GUE)

Let the moments of traces of powers:

t_{k}^{(N)}=\int_{\gamma^{N}}d\mu(\Lambda){\rm Tr}\,\Lambda^{k}\ \ =\int_{\gamma}x^{k}\rho_{1}^{(N)}(x)dx.

(6-68)

We have
t_{0}^{(N)}=N
, and it is clear that for the Gaussian measure, which is symmetric under
\Lambda\to-\Lambda
, all the odd moments vanish
t^{(N)}_{2k+1}=0
.
The resolvent can be written

W^{(N)}_{1}(x)=\sum_{k\geq 0}t^{(N)}_{k}x^{-k-1}.

(6-69)

The ODE (
6-67
) implies a recursion relation for the even moments
t_{2k}
.

t^{(N)}_{2k}=2N\frac{2k-1}{k+1}t^{(N)}_{2k-2}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{(2k-3)(2k-2)(2k-1)}{k+1}t^{(N)}_{2k-4}\,,\quad k\geq 2,

(6-70)

with initial terms
t^{(N)}_{0}=N,t^{(N)}_{2}=N^{2}
.

The recursion (
6-63
) implies:

t^{(N+2)}_{k+2}-t^{(N+1)}_{k+2}=(N+2)t^{(N+3)}_{k}+Nt^{(N+2)}_{k}-(N+3)t^{(N+1)}_{k}-(N+1)t^{(N)}_{k}.

(6-71)

Recursion and ODE for
W_{2}^{(N)}
:

We have

\displaystyle W_{2}^{(N+k)}(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(x_{1}-x_{2})^{2}}{\rm Tr}\,

\displaystyle M^{(N)}(x_{1})\mathcal{R}^{(N,k)}(x_{1})^{-1}\mathcal{R}^{(N,k)}(x_{2})

(6-72)

\displaystyle M^{(N)}(x_{2})\mathcal{R}^{(N,k)}(x_{2})^{-1}\mathcal{R}^{(N,k)}(x_{1}).

At most 4 of the
2\times 2
matrices
\mathcal{R}^{(N,k)}(x_{1})^{-1}\mathcal{R}^{(N,k)}(x_{2})M^{(N)}(x_{2})\mathcal{R}^{(N,k)}(x_{2})^{-1}\mathcal{R}^{(N,k)}(x_{1})
for
k=0,1,2,3,4
can be linearly independent. By
Definition 6.7

\mathcal{R}^{(N,k)}(x)
and its inverse are polynomial in
x
. Therefore,

Proposition 6.7

There exist some coefficients
C_{2}^{(N,k)}(x_{1};x_{2})
, polynomials of
x_{1}
, such that

\sum_{k=0}^{4}\alpha_{2}^{(N,k)}(x_{1},x_{2})\mathcal{R}^{(N,k)}(x_{1})^{-1}\mathcal{R}^{(N,k)}(x_{2})M^{(N)}(x_{2})\mathcal{R}^{(N,k)}(x_{2})^{-1}\mathcal{R}^{(N,k)}(x_{1})=0,

(6-73)

i.e.
W_{2}^{(N)}(x_{1},x_{2})
satisfies a linear recursion relation of order 3, with coefficients polynomials of
x_{1}
(possibly transcendental of
x_{2}
).

Moreover, we get

Proposition 6.8

There exist some coefficients
\alpha_{2}^{(N,k)}(x_{1};x_{2})\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},x_{2}]
, such that

\sum_{k=0}^{4}\alpha_{2}^{(N,k)}(x_{1},x_{2})(W_{2}^{(N+k)}(x_{1},x_{2})-\frac{1}{(x_{1}-x_{2})^{2}})=0.

(6-74)

proof:
We apply
Theorem 5.2
for
{\mathfrak{g}}=\mathfrak{gl}(2,\mathbb{C})
.

\square

Recursion and ODE for
W_{n}^{(N)},n\geq 3
:

We have

W_{n}^{(N+k)}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}^{1-cycle}}\hskip-1.0pt(-1)^{\sigma}\frac{{\rm Tr}\,\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{R}^{(N,k)}(x_{\sigma^{i}(1)})M^{(N)}(x_{\sigma^{i}(1)})\mathcal{R}^{(N,k)}(x_{\sigma^{i}(1)})^{-1}}{\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x_{\sigma(i)}-x_{i})}.

(6-75)

We can write it:

W_{n}^{(N)}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})={\rm Tr}\,M^{(N)}(x_{1})\ H_{n}^{(N)}(x_{1};x_{2},\dots,x_{n}),

(6-76)

where
H_{n}^{(N)}(x_{1};x_{2},\dots,x_{n})
is a
2\times 2
matrix, rational function of
x_{1}
.

Theorem 6.3

At most
4
of the
2\times 2
matrices
\mathcal{R}^{(N,k)}(x_{1})^{-1}H_{n}^{(N+k)}(x_{1};x_{2},\dots,x_{n})\mathcal{R}^{(N,k)}(x_{1})
for
k=0,1,2,\dots,4
can be linearly independent, therefore there exist some coefficients
C_{n}^{(N,k)}(x_{1};x_{2},\dots,x_{n})
, polynomials of
x_{1}
, such that

\sum_{k=0}^{4}C_{n}^{(N,k)}(x_{1};x_{2},\dots,x_{n})W_{n}^{(N+k)}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})=0.

(6-77)

Following
Theorem 5.2
, we can also write:

W_{n}^{(N+k)}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\mathcal{H}^{(N+k)}_{n}(M^{(N)}(x_{1}),M^{(N)}(x_{2}),\dots,M^{(N)}(x_{n})),

(6-78)

where
\mathcal{H}^{(N)}_{n}(x_{1};x_{2},\dots,x_{n})
is a linear form on
\mathfrak{gl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})^{\otimes n}
, it belongs to the dual
{\mathfrak{gl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})^{\otimes n}}^{*}
, which has dimension
4^{n}
, therefore at most
4^{n}
of
\mathcal{H}^{(N+k)}_{n}(x_{1};x_{2},\dots,x_{n})
for

can be independent.

This implies

Theorem 6.4

There exist some coefficients
\tilde{C}_{n}^{(N,k)}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})\in{\mathbb{C}}[x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}]
, such that

\sum_{k=0}^{4^{n}}\tilde{C}_{n}^{(N,k)}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})W_{n}^{(N+k)}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})=0.

(6-79)

Similarly, we get an ODE for

as shown in
Section 3.5
. We begin with

\frac{d^{k}}{dx^{k}_{1}}W_{n}^{(N)}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})={\rm Tr}\,M^{(N)}(x_{1})\ H_{k,n}^{(N)}(x_{1};x_{2},\dots,x_{n}),

(6-80)

where
H_{k,n}^{(N)}(x_{1};x_{2},\dots,x_{n})
is computed recursively

\displaystyle H_{0,n}^{(N)}(x_{1};x_{2},\dots,x_{n})

\displaystyle=H_{n}^{(N)}(x_{1};x_{2},\dots,x_{n})

(6-81)

\displaystyle H_{k+1,n}^{(N)}(x_{1};x_{2},\dots,x_{n})

\displaystyle=[H_{k,n}^{(N)}(x_{1};x_{2},\dots,x_{n}),\mathcal{D}^{(N)}(x_{1})]

\displaystyle+\frac{d}{dx_{1}}H_{k,n}^{(N)}(x_{1};x_{2},\dots,x_{n}).

Hence,

Theorem 6.5

There exist some coefficients
{\hat{C}}^{(N,k)}_{n}(x_{1};x_{2},\dots,x_{n})
, polynomials of
x_{1}
, such that

\sum_{k=0}^{4}{\hat{C}}^{(N,k)}_{n}(x_{1};x_{2},\dots,x_{n})\frac{d^{k}}{dx_{1}^{k}}W_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=0.

(6-82)

Theorem 6.6

There exist some coefficients
{\tilde{C}}^{(N,k)}_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in{\mathbb{C}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
, such that

\sum_{k=0}^{4^{n}}{\tilde{C}}^{(N,k)}_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\frac{d^{k}}{dx_{1}^{k}}W_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=0.

(6-83)

6.2
2-Matrix model

All the steps mentioned so far can be applied to the 2-matrix model.
The only difference with the 1-matrix model is that the Lie algebra is not
\mathfrak{s}l(2)
but
\mathfrak{s}l(\tilde{d})
for some
\tilde{d}\geq 2
, i.e. matrices can have higher dimensions than
2
.

The link between the 2-matrix model and the formalism needed here, i.e. the wave functions
\Psi(x)
, the connection
\nabla=d-\mathcal{D}(x)dx
, the adjoint sections
M(x.E)
and the determinantal correlators
W_{n}(x_{1}.E_{1},\dots,x_{n}.E_{n})
can all be found in the literature
[
BE06a
,
BE06
,
EKR18
,
Meh04
]
, let us recall them in short.

The following defines what is called the “2-matrix model” measure, actually its marginal measure for eigenvalues.

Definition 6.8
(Matrix-eigenvalues measure)

Let two potentials
V
and
\tilde{V}
be such that
V^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{C}}(x)
, and
\tilde{V}^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{C}}(x)
.
Let
\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\Lambda_{1},\dots,\Lambda_{N})
and
\tilde{\Lambda}=\operatorname{diag}(\tilde{\Lambda}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\Lambda}_{N})
be two
N\times N
diagonal matrices. We define

Z^{(N)}=\int_{\gamma^{N}\times\tilde{\gamma}^{N}}d\Lambda d\tilde{\Lambda}\ \Delta(\Lambda)\Delta(\tilde{\Lambda})\ \det\left(e^{\Lambda_{i}\tilde{\Lambda}_{j}}\right)e^{-{\rm Tr}\,\left(V(\Lambda)+\tilde{V}(\tilde{\Lambda})\right)},

(6-84)

and we define the marginal measure for
\Lambda\in\gamma^{N}
:

d\mu^{(N)}\Lambda=\frac{1}{Z^{(N)}}d\Lambda\int_{\tilde{\gamma}^{N}}d\tilde{\Lambda}\ \Delta(\Lambda)\Delta(\tilde{\Lambda})\ \det\left(e^{\Lambda_{i}\tilde{\Lambda}_{j}}\right)e^{-{\rm Tr}\,\left(V(\Lambda)+\tilde{V}(\tilde{\Lambda})\right)},

(6-85)

and the marginal measure for
\tilde{\Lambda}\in\tilde{\gamma}^{N}
:

d\tilde{\mu}^{(N)}\tilde{\Lambda}=\frac{1}{Z^{(N)}}d\tilde{\Lambda}\int_{\gamma^{N}}d\Lambda\ \Delta(\Lambda)\Delta(\tilde{\Lambda})\ \det\left(e^{\Lambda_{i}\tilde{\Lambda}_{j}}\right)e^{-{\rm Tr}\,\left(V(\Lambda)+\tilde{V}(\tilde{\Lambda})\right)}.

(6-86)

We denote

d=1+\deg V^{\prime},\quad\tilde{d}=1+\deg\tilde{V}^{\prime},

(6-87)

where
\deg V^{\prime}
is the sum of degrees of all poles, including the pole at
\infty
.
(if
V
, resp.
\tilde{V}
is a polynomial, then
d=\deg V
, resp.
\tilde{d}=\deg\tilde{V}
).

Definition 6.9
(Wave functions)

Let

p^{(N)}(x):=\int_{\gamma^{N}}d\mu^{(N)}\Lambda\ \ \det(x-\Lambda),\quad q^{(N)}(y):=\int_{\tilde{\gamma}^{N}}d\tilde{\mu}^{(N)}\tilde{\Lambda}\ \ \det(y-\tilde{\Lambda}).

(6-88)

Let

h^{(N)}:=\int_{\gamma\times\tilde{\gamma}}dxdy\,p^{(N)}(x)q^{(N)}(y)e^{-(V(x)+\tilde{V}(y)-xy)},\quad\gamma^{(N)}=\sqrt{h^{(N)}/h^{(N-1)}}.

(6-89)

It is well known
[
Meh04
,
BE06
]
that
p^{(N)},q^{(N)}
form a biorthogonal family:

\int_{\gamma\times\tilde{\gamma}}dxdy\,p^{(N)}(x)q^{(\tilde{N})}(y)\ e^{-(V(x)+\tilde{V}(y)-xy)}=h^{(N)}\delta_{N,\tilde{N}}.

(6-90)

We define:

\psi^{(N)}(x):=\frac{e^{-V(x)}}{\sqrt{h^{(N)}}}\ p^{(N)}(x)\quad,\quad\tilde{\psi}^{(N)}(y):=\frac{e^{-\tilde{V}(y)}}{\sqrt{h^{(N)}}}\ q^{(N)}(y),

(6-91)

and we define the vector of dimension
\tilde{d}
:

\vec{\psi}^{(N)}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{j}^{(N-\tilde{d}+1)}(x)\cr\vdots\cr\psi_{j}^{(N)}(x)\end{pmatrix}.

(6-92)

Definition 6.10
(Dual wave functions)

We define the Hilbert transform

\phi^{(N)}(x):=\frac{e^{V(x)}}{\sqrt{h^{(N)}}}\int_{\gamma\times\tilde{\gamma}}dx^{\prime}dy^{\prime}\,\frac{1}{x-x^{\prime}}q^{(N)}(y^{\prime})\ e^{-(V(x^{\prime})+\tilde{V}(y^{\prime})-x^{\prime}y^{\prime})},

(6-93)

and the vector

\vec{\phi}^{(N)}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\phi^{(N-1)}(x)\cr\phi^{(N)}(x)\cr\vdots\cr\phi^{(N+\tilde{d}-2)}(x)\end{pmatrix}.

(6-94)

We also define the Fourier transforms,

\phi^{(N,i)}(x):=\int_{\tilde{\gamma}_{i}}dy^{\prime}\phi^{(N)}(y^{\prime})\ e^{x^{\prime}y^{\prime}},

(6-95)

where
\tilde{\gamma}_{i},\ i=1,\dots,\tilde{d}-1
form a basis of homology paths on which the integral can be defined.

We define the
\tilde{d}\times\tilde{d}
matrix by its matrix elements
i,j=1,\dots,\tilde{d}
:

(\Phi^{(N)}(x))_{j,i}=\phi^{(N-\tilde{d}+i,j-1)}(x),

(6-96)

where we defined
\phi^{(N,0)}(x)=\phi^{(N)}(x)
.

6.2.1
Recursion and ODE for wave functions

Proposition 6.9
(Recursion relation)

The wave function satisfies a recursion relation of order
\tilde{d}
, written:

x\psi^{(N)}(x)=\gamma^{(N+1)}\psi^{(N+1)}(x)+\sum_{k=0}^{\tilde{d}-1}Q_{N,N-k}\psi^{(N-k)}(x),

(6-97)

or written in matrix form

\vec{\psi}^{(N+1)}(x)=\mathcal{R}^{(N)}(x)\vec{\psi}^{(N)}(x),\quad\mathcal{R}^{(N)}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&&\cr 0&&\ddots&\cr 0&&&1\cr\frac{-Q_{N,N-\tilde{d}+1}}{\gamma^{(N+1)}}&\dots&\frac{-Q_{N,N-1}}{\gamma^{(N+1)}}&\frac{x-Q_{N,N}}{\gamma^{(N+1)}}\cr\end{pmatrix}.

(6-98)

\mathcal{R}^{(N)}(x)
is a matrix of size
\tilde{d}\times\tilde{d}
, polynomial of
x
of degree 1.
Its inverse is also polynomial of degree 1.

Similarly for the dual wave functions we have, for
N\geq 1

x\phi^{(N)}(x)=\gamma^{(N)}\phi^{(N-1)}(x)+\sum_{k=0}^{\tilde{d}-1}Q_{N+k,N}\phi^{(N+k)}(x),

(6-99)

which can be written

\vec{\phi}^{(N+1)}(x)={\tilde{\mathcal{R}}}^{(N)}(x)\vec{\phi}^{(N)}(x),\quad{\tilde{\mathcal{R}}}^{(N)}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{x-Q_{N,N}}{\gamma^{(N)}}&\dots&\frac{-Q_{N+\tilde{d}-2,N}}{\gamma^{(N)}}&\frac{-Q_{N+\tilde{d}-1,N}}{\gamma^{(N)}}\cr 1&&&0\cr&\ddots&&\vdots\cr&&1&0\cr\end{pmatrix}^{-1}.

(6-100)

proof:

See
[
BE06a
, p. 6]
.

\square

Definition 6.11
(Iterated shift operator)

Let

\mathcal{R}^{(N,k)}(x):=\mathcal{R}^{(N+k-1)}(x)\mathcal{R}^{(N+k-2)}(x)\dots\mathcal{R}^{(N)}(x),

(6-101)

which is a matrix, polynomial of
x
of degree at most
k
.
It is such that

\vec{\psi}^{(N+k)}(x)=\mathcal{R}^{(N,k)}(x)\vec{\psi}^{(N)}(x).

(6-102)

Similarly

{\tilde{\mathcal{R}}}^{(N,k)}(x):={\tilde{\mathcal{R}}}^{(N+k-1)}(x){\tilde{\mathcal{R}}}^{(N+k-2)}(x)\cdots{\tilde{\mathcal{R}}}^{(N)}(x),

(6-103)

which is a matrix, polynomial of
x
of degree at most
k
.
It is such that

\vec{\phi}^{(N+k)}(x)={\tilde{\mathcal{R}}}^{(N,k)}(x)\vec{\phi}^{(N)}(x).

(6-104)

The wave functions satisfy a differential system of order
\tilde{d}
(see
[
BE06a
]
):

Proposition 6.10
(ODE)

We have:

\frac{d}{dx}\vec{\psi}^{(N)}(x)=\mathcal{D}^{(N)}(x)\vec{\psi}^{(N)}(x),

(6-105)

where
\mathcal{D}^{(N)}(x)
is a matrix of size
\tilde{d}\times\tilde{d}
, polynomial of
x
of degree less or equal to degree of
V^{\prime}
.
The matrix
\mathcal{D}^{(N)}(x)
is given explicitely in
[
BE06a
,
BE06
]
.

Similarly, there is a
\tilde{d}\times\tilde{d}
, matrix
{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}^{(N)}(x)
polynomial of
x
of degree less or equal to degree of
V^{\prime}
such that

\frac{d}{dx}\vec{\phi}^{(N)}(x)={\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}^{(N)}(x)\vec{\phi}^{(N)}(x).

(6-106)

Definition 6.14

Definition 6.15
