Impossibility of Depth Reduction in Explainable Clustering
Chengyuan Deng, Surya Teja Gavva, Karthik C. S, Parth Patel, Adarsh Srinivasan

Abstract
Over the last few years Explainable Clustering has gathered a lot of attention. Dasgupta et al. [ICML’20] initiated the study of explainable k k -means and k k -median clustering problems where the explanation is captured by a threshold decision tree which partitions the space at each node using axis parallel hyperplanes. Recently, Laber et al. [Pattern Recognition’23] made a case to consider the depth of the decision tree as an additional complexity measure of interest. In this work, we prove that even when the input points are in the Euclidean plane, then any depth reduction in the explanation incurs unbounded loss in the k k -means and k k -median cost. Formally, we show that there exists a data set X ⊆ ℝ 2 X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2} , for which there is a decision tree of depth k − 1 k-1 whose k k -means/ k k -median cost matches the optimal clustering cost of X X , but every decision tree of depth less than k − 1 k-1 has unbounded cost w.r.t. the optimal cost of clustering. We extend our results to the k k -center objective as well, albeit with weaker guarantees.

Introduction

Clustering is a fundamental task that needs to be carried out for numerous applications in the areas of data analysis and unsupervised learning. The input to clustering in these applications is typically
a set of points in Euclidean space and the goal is to find a solution that minimizes a specified clustering objective. Arguably, the three most popular clustering objectives are
k
-means,
k
-median, and
k
-center, and there is a long line
of theoretical research on designing algorithms for optimizing these objectives
[
Vaz01
]
.

Explainable AI has gained significant popularity in recent years
[
Mol20
,
MSK+19
]
as it provides reliable and helpful insights about the model and its predictions, and more importantly allows human users to comprehend and trust the results and output created by machine learning algorithms. Dasgupta et al.
[
DFMR20
]
introduced a notion of
Explainable Clustering
for the
k
-means and
k
-median objectives, which produces a threshold decision tree with
k
leaves as the clustering result. That is, all cuts are axis-parallel at some thresholds (e.g.
x_{r}=\theta
, where
r
is a certain coordinate and
\theta
is a threshold value). The performance of explainable clustering is then evaluated by the
price of explainability
, defined to be the ratio of the clustering objective cost given by the decision tree to the optimal cost of the clustering. The initial attempt by
[
DFMR20
]
gave an upper bound of
O(k^{2})
(resp.
O(k)
) and a lower bound of
\Omega(k)

(resp.
\Omega(\log k)
) on the price of explainability of
k
-means clustering (resp.
k
-median clustering).

Laber and Murtinho
[
LM21
]
expanded the study of explainable clustering to the
k
-center objective, giving an upper bound of
O(\sqrt{d}k^{1-1/d})
and a lower bound of
\Omega\left(\sqrt{d}\cdot\frac{k\sqrt{\ln\ln k}}{\ln^{1.5}k}\right)
on the price of explainability for
k
-center clustering.
Over the last two years, a sequence of works has almost closed the above gap on price of explainability for all three clustering objectives
[
GJPS21
,
MS21
,
EMN22
,
CH22
,
MS23
,
GPSY23
]
. Additionally, other aspects and variants of explainable clustering have been explored in recent literature, some examples being, bicriteria approximability of the price of explainability
[
FMR20
,
MS22
]
, computational complexity of finding good explanations
[
Lab22
]
, providing explanations via oblique decision trees
[
GCP22
]
, constructing a polyhedron around each cluster as an explanation
[
LG22
]
, removing outliers from clusters
for better explainability
[
BFG+22
]
, and so on.

Piltaver et al.
[
PLGMI16
]
studied how tree structure parameters (such as the number of leaves, branching factor, tree depth) affect tree interpretability, and the authors concluded that based on empirical data (from a survey with 98 questions answered by 69 respondents),
that the most important parameter is question depth (i.e., the length of the path that must be traversed from the root to a leaf to answer a question in a classification tree).
Building on this study, Laber, Murtinho, and Oliveira
[
LMO23
]
argued that in practice, an explanation via decision trees is useful only if the decision tree is of low depth (a.k.a. shallow decision trees).
Indeed, intuitively explaining leaves that are
far from the root involve many tests, which makes it harder to
grasp the model’s logic. In other words, short explanations are easy to understand and thus trust!

The authors of
[
LMO23
]
proposed an empirically-effective algorithm that leverages the price of the tree depth in the objective to return a desired cut. However, there has been no theoretical study in this direction. We thus raise the following question:

Can we always obtain short explanations without compromising too much on the classification quality?

In order to answer the above question, we first introduce a notion called
price of depth reduction
. Given an input point-set
X
, the
price of depth reduction
to
h
, denoted
\mathsf{D}^{\downarrow}(X,h)
, is the ratio of the clustering objective cost of the clustering given by the best decision tree of depth
h
to the cost of the clustering given by the optimal decision tree.
Note that the depth of any decision tree with
k
leaves is at least
\log_{2}k
and at most
k-1
. Therefore, we always have
\mathsf{D}^{\downarrow}(X,k-1)=1
.

One may wonder, if every point-set can be explained by decision trees of low depth (say
O(\log k)
) while only paying a small price of depth reduction. Our first result is an answer in the negative; we show that in high dimensions, there exists a point-set which admits a decision tree of depth
k-1
whose
k
-means/
k
-median/
k
-center cost is equal to the cost of an optimal cluster, but the cost of every decision tree of depth
k-2
for the point-set arbitrarily high relative to the optimum. Our result indicates that for certain datasets, it is impossible to reduce the decision tree depth regardless of the price of depth reduction that one is willing to pay. This also demystifies why we do not have an algorithm for depth reduction in explainable clustering with theoretically provable worst-case guarantees.

Theorem 1.1

(Impossibility of Shallow Explanations in High Dimensions; Informal version of
Theorem

3.1
)
.

The following holds for
k
-means,
k
-median, and
k

For every

, there is a point-set

is unbounded. Moreover, the price of explainability of

is 1.

The above unbounded inapproximability result stands in stark contrast to clustering literature (for

-means,
k
-median, and
k
-center) for which we have constant factor polynomial time approximation algorithms
[
Gon85
,
HS86
,
ANSW20
,
CEMN22
]
. One may attribute the above bad dataset to the
Curse of Dimensionality

[
Köp00
,
VF05
,
Tay19
]
.

That said, while we know high dimensional clustering is hard to approximate for the classical clustering problems
[
FG88
,
CK19
,
CKL21
,
CKL22
]
, the
k
-means and
k
-median objectives inapproximability is alleviated in fixed dimensions as they admit a PTAS
[

,
FRS16
,
KR07
,
Coh18
,
CFS18
]
. Even in the explainable clustering literature, we have algorithms which have lower price of explainability when the dataset is in fixed dimenesions
[

,

]
.
However, our next result is that the impossibility of shallow explanations continues to hold for

-means and

Theorem 1.2

(Impossibility of Shallow Explanations for

-median and

)
.

The following holds for
k
-means and
k
-median clustering objectives.

, there is a point-set

, such that

is 1.

We remark that while the construction of the dataset in
Theorem

is quite simple, the dataset construction in the above theorem is quite involved and very subtle. It is inspired from what can be termed as “blocking examples”, that are seen in discrete geometry (for example, in the context of the conjectured extremal examples for the Pach-Tardos conjecture on axis parallel rectangles
[

]
).

Our results for
k
-center in the plane are not as strong as the ones above for
k
-means and

-median. Nevertheless we are able to prove a lower bound of 2 on the price of depth reduction in the plane for

-center.

(Lower Bound on Price of Depth Reduction for

)
.

For every

and every

, there is a point-set

, such that

Preliminaries

Throughout the paper, we say a point set
X
has dimension
d
with size
n
, and denote by
\mathsf{dist}(x,y)
the Euclidean distance between points
x
and
y
in the point set.
Given two sets of points
X
and
C
in Euclidean space, we define
the
k
-means cost of
X
for
C

to be
\textnormal{{cost}}_{2}(C):=\underset{x\in X}{\sum}\left(\underset{c\in C}{\min}\ \left(\mathsf{dist}(x,c)\right)^{2}\right)
, the
k
-median cost to be
\textnormal{{cost}}_{1}(C):=\underset{x\in X}{\sum}\left(\underset{c\in C}{\min}\ \mathsf{dist}(x,c)\right)
, and the
k
-center cost to be
\textnormal{{cost}}_{\infty}(C):=\underset{x\in X}{\max}\left(\underset{c\in C}{\min}\ \mathsf{dist}(x,c)\right)
.
Given a set of points
X
, the
k
-means/
k
-median/
k
-center
objective is the minimum over all point-sets
C
of cardinality
k
of the
k
-means/
k
-median/
k
-center
cost of
X
for
C
. Given a cluster
Y\subseteq X
, the contribution of
Y
to the

k
-means/
k
-median cost of
X
is simply the
1
-means/
1
-median cost of
Y
.

Dasgupta et al.
[
DFMR20
]
introduced explainable clustering through decision trees. In a threshold decision tree
\mathsf{T}
of depth
h_{\mathsf{T}}
and
k
leaves, every non-leaf node is labelled by the hyperplane
x_{r}=\theta
for some
r\in[d]
and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
. A point
x
is then classified by the node to the left if it’s
r^{\text{th}}
coordinate is less than
\theta

and to the right otherwise.

They also introduced the notion of
price of explainability
, which is formally defined in
Definition

1
. Furthermore, we define
price of depth reduction
in
Definition

2
to evaluate the cost of obtaining shallow explanations.

Definition 1

(Price of explainability)
.

Let
\Lambda(k)
be the optimal cost of
k
-means/
k
-median/
k
-center objective for a point-set
X
, and let
\tilde{\Lambda}(k)
be the optimal cost for the same clustering objective but provided through explanations from a decision tree, the price of explainability is defined to be
\nicefrac{{\widetilde{\Lambda}(k)}}{{\Lambda(k)}}
.

Definition 2

(Price of depth reduction)
.

Let
\widetilde{\Lambda}(k)
be the optimal cost of
k
-means/
k
-medinas/
k
-center objective for a point-set
X
provided through explanations from a decision tree (of arbitrary depth), and let
\widehat{\Lambda}(k)
be the optimal cost for the same clustering objective but provided through explanations from a decision tree of depth
h
, then the price of depth reduction is defined to be
\mathsf{D}^{\downarrow}(X,h):=\nicefrac{{\widehat{\Lambda}(k)}}{{\widetilde{\Lambda}(k)}}
.

3
Impossibility of Depth Reduction in High Dimension

In this section, we give a simple construction of a dataset in high dimensions which has unbounded price of depth reduction for all three clustering objectives of interest. Formally, our results are stated in
Theorem

3.1
.

Theorem 3.1
.

For every
k\geq 3,d\geq 2
, such that
d\geq\nicefrac{{k}}{{2}}
, and every polynomial function
p:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
, there exists a point-set
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
(which can be represented in
|X|^{O(1)}
bits) such that the following properties hold for
k
-means/
k
-median/
k
-center objectives:

Correctness:

There exists a decision tree of depth
k-1
such that the price of explainability is 1.

Price of Depth Reduction:

For every
h<k-1
, every decision tree of depth
h
yields a clustering whose cost is at least
p(|X|)
times the optimum. In other words,
\mathsf{D}^{\downarrow}(X,k-2)\geq p(|X|)
.

We emphasize that
\mathsf{D}^{\downarrow}(X,k-2)
in the above theorem is lower bounded by some polynomial in the size of
X
and thus is larger than every function of
k
.

3.1
Point-set Construction

We show the proof of
Theorem

3.1
by explicitly constructing the desired point-set
X
. The construction is simple and applies to all three objectives. The formal description of the point-set is shown below. Let
n
and
N
be natural numbers.

High-Dimensional Point-set
X(n,N,d)

Let
\varepsilon=1/N
. For each coordinate
i,j\in[d]
, and each
a,b\in\{1,-1\}
,
X
contains
n
copies of the point
a\cdot\vec{e}_{i}+\varepsilon\cdot b\cdot\vec{e}_{j}
, where
\vec{e}_{i}
is the
i
-th standard basis unit vector.

One may observe that the point-set is well-structured with
k
balls of radius
\varepsilon
in
d
-dimension (named
\varepsilon
-balls from now on), each
\varepsilon
-ball contains
\nicefrac{{n}}{{k}}
points evenly distributed on the sphere. Therefore the set of centers
C:=\{a\cdot\vec{e}_{i}:i\in[d],a\in\{1,-1\}\}
gives optimal cost for all three objectives, provided that
\varepsilon
is not too big (
\varepsilon<\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
). The optimal costs for the
k
-means,
k
-median,
k
-center objectives are
2nkd\cdot\varepsilon^{2}
,
2kd\cdot\varepsilon
, and
\varepsilon
respectively.

Note that
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and each of the points in
X
can be stored using
2\log(N)
bits. Hence,
X
can be stored using
O(|X|\log N)
bits of storage, where
|X|
is the number of points in the set
X
. It transpires later that
N=1/\varepsilon=O(p(|X|))
, therefore
X
requires at most
O(|X|^{O(1)})
bits.

3.2
Proof of
Theorem

3.1

We consider the case that
k
is even. We then set
d=k/2
and show that for an appropriate choice of
N
and
d
, the set of centers
C:=\{a\cdot\vec{e}_{i}:i\in[d],a\in\{1,-1\}\}
gives optimal cost for all
k
-means,
k
-median and
k
-center objective functions, up to any polynomial approximation factor in the size of above point set point set. If
k
is odd, we just use a slightly different point set, where we set
d={\left\lceil{k/2}\right\rceil}
, and for
i={\left\lceil{d/2}\right\rceil}
, we only include
n
copies of the point
\vec{e}_{i}+\varepsilon\cdot b\cdot\vec{e}_{j}
for each
j\in[d]
and
b\in\{0,1\}
and not
-\vec{e}_{i}+\varepsilon\cdot b\cdot\vec{e}_{j}
. The proofs of all three objectives proceed in a similar fashion.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
d=k/2
. We can chose
N
such that the optimal clustering for
X(n,N,d)
w.r.t.
k
-means/
k
-median/
k
-center objective is given by the set of clusters
C:=\{C_{i,a}:0\leq i\leq d,a\in\{1,-1\}\}
, where
C_{i,a}:=\{a\cdot\vec{e}_{i}+\varepsilon\cdot b\cdot\vec{e}_{j}:1\leq j\leq n,b\in\{1,-1\}\}
. Furthermore, for any polynomial function
p:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
, any other choice of explainable clustering
\tilde{C}\neq C
has
k
-means/
k
-median/
k
-center cost at least
p(|X|)
times the optimum.

Proof.

We first calculate the costs of for three objectives with
C
as a clustering assignment. Note that the cluster center for the cluster
C_{i,a}
is
\mu_{i,a}=a\cdot\vec{e}_{i}
. Hence, the
k
-means objective has total cost of
2nkd\cdot\varepsilon^{2}=\varepsilon|X|
, the
k
-median objective has
2nkd\cdot\varepsilon=\varepsilon|X|
. For the
k
-center objective, recall that
\textnormal{{cost}}_{\infty}(C):=\underset{x\in X}{\max}\left(\underset{i\in[d],a\in\{1,-1\}\}}{\min}\ \mathsf{dist}(x,\mu_{i,a})\right)
, and every point has distance of
\varepsilon
to the center, implying that the optimal cost is
\varepsilon
. Now consider any alternative clustering, there must exist two points located at different
\varepsilon
-balls being assigned to the same cluster. We show that only the cost contributed by this particular pair will introduce a significant loss.

Denote an
\varepsilon
-ball as
B_{i}^{a}\subseteq X
for every
i\in[d]
and
a\in\{-1,1\}
, which is a set of
\{a\cdot\vec{e}_{i}+\varepsilon\cdot b\cdot\vec{e}_{j}:\forall j\in[d],\forall b\in\{-1,1\}\}
. Suppose for another clustering assignment
\tilde{C}
, we have point
p\in B_{i}^{a}
and
q\in B_{j}^{b}
, it follows that
\mathsf{dist}(p,q)\geq\sqrt{2}/2-\varepsilon
. For
k
-means objective, it certainly holds that
\textnormal{{cost}}(\widetilde{C})\geq\mathsf{dist}^{2}(p,q)
(Similarly,
\textnormal{{cost}}(\widetilde{C})\geq\mathsf{dist}(p,q)
for
k
-median and
\textnormal{{cost}}(\widetilde{C})\geq\mathsf{dist}(p,q)/2
for
k
-center). Now, we show that there exists
\varepsilon
such that
\textnormal{{cost}}(\tilde{C})\geq p(|X|)\cdot\textnormal{{cost}}(C)
for all three objectives as follows.

•

k
-means:
\textnormal{{cost}}(\tilde{C})\geq\frac{(\sqrt{2}/2-\varepsilon)^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}|X|}\cdot\textnormal{{cost}}(C)
, where
\textnormal{{cost}}(C)=\varepsilon^{2}|X|
. Hence, we can set
\varepsilon
to be any value in
(0,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2|X|\cdot p(|X|)-2}})
.

•

k
-median:
\textnormal{{cost}}(\tilde{C})\geq\frac{\sqrt{2}/2-\varepsilon}{\varepsilon|X|}\cdot\textnormal{{cost}}(C)
, where
\textnormal{{cost}}(C)=\varepsilon|X|
. Hence, we can set
\varepsilon
to be any value in
(0,\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2|X|\cdot p(|X|)+2})
.

•

k
-center:
\textnormal{{cost}}(\tilde{C})\geq\frac{\sqrt{2}/2-\varepsilon}{2\varepsilon}\cdot\textnormal{{cost}}(C)
, where
\textnormal{{cost}}(C)=\varepsilon
. Hence, we can set
\varepsilon
to be any value in
(0,\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4p(|X|)+2})
.

The above results already imply that
C
is the optimal clustering.∎

Next, we explicitly construct the decision tree of depth
k-1
that outputs the optimal clustering, thus concluding the correctness of
Theorem

3.1
.

Lemma 3.2
.

There exists a decision tree of depth
k-1
which outputs the optimal clustering for the point-set
X
w.r.t.
k
-means/
k
-median/
k
-center objective.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
k
is even. The decision tree makes the following
k-1
-length sequence of hyperplanes, where
x_{r}=\theta
indicates the hyperplane cut is on the
r
-th coordinate and at value
\theta
.

For
1\leq i\leq k-1
, we describe the
i
-th level of the decision tree (the root is at level
1
). Each level has one hyperplane cut, when
i
is odd, the cut threshold is
x_{i}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
. Any point with the
i
-th coordinate larger than this is classified into the cluster with center
e_{i}
(this is a leaf), and if the
i
-th coordinate of a point is smaller than this threshold, it moves to the next level of the decision tree. If
i
is even, the node at that level considers the threshold at
x_{i}=-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
. Any point with the
i
-th coordinate smaller than this is classified into the cluster with center
e_{i}
, and if the
i
-th coordinate of a point is larger than this threshold, it moves to the next level of the decision tree (if
i=k-1
, this is the final level of the decision tree and it is just classified to the cluster with center
-e_{k}
). Every node in this decision tree has two children, one of which is a leaf except the last level. Note that the leaf represents a subspace that contains an
\varepsilon
-ball as constructed, thus the decision tree induces the optimal clustering for all objectives.
∎

Now, to prove the price of depth reduction in
Theorem

3.1
, it only remains to show that any decision tree of depth less than
k-1
yields a clustering
\widetilde{C}\neq C
, as claimed by
Lemma

3.3
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Every decision tree of depth less than
k-1
outputs a clustering which misclassifies (w.r.t. the clustering
C
in Lemma
3.1
) at least one point.

Proof.

We say that a node
v
in the decision tree is
trivial
for the point set
X
, if the node has a child, which contains exactly the same points as
v
. In other words, a trivial node does not partition any points in the remaining point-set into a cluster. We can assume that the minimum depth decision tree does not contain any trivial nodes because we can contract that node without changing the final classification of
X
, and the depth can only decrease as a result of this contraction. Hence, without loss of generality, we can assume that every node in the tree is non-trivial.

Consider any decision tree
\mathcal{T}
of depth at most
k-2
with
k
leaves that induces an explainable clustering for
X
. There must exist at least two nodes in
\mathcal{T}
with two leaf children, by the pigeon-hole principle. We argue that such decision tree inevitably misclassifies at least one point. For any cluster
C_{i,a}
with a hyperplane cut
x_{i}=\theta
, if the threshold value
\theta>1+\varepsilon
or
\theta<-1-\varepsilon
, then there exists a trivial node in
\mathcal{T}
. If
-\varepsilon\leq\theta\leq\varepsilon
, then at least one point is misclassified since it cuts through other
\varepsilon
-balls on the orthogonal coordinate. Therefore to produce the optimal clustering, every threshold value of hyperplane cuts satisfies
\theta\in(-1+\varepsilon,-\varepsilon)\cup(\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon)
. Notice that only one cluster can be separated from the remaining point-set by cuts of this type, with the only exception on the last cut. Therefore every node has one leaf child and the last node in the decision tree has two leaf children. If there are two such nodes, some hyperplane cut must have threshold in
(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
, leading to misclassified points. A demonstration of this argument with the point-set
X
in two dimensions is shown in
Figure

1
.
∎

Figure 1:
An illustration of the high-dimensional point-set
X
in 2D with axis-parallel cuts. On the left, (a) shows that with threshold
\theta\in(-1+\varepsilon,-\varepsilon)\cup(\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon)
, the optimal clustering of the four
\varepsilon
-balls can be induced by a 3-depth decision tree. On the right, (b) shows that any shallower decision tree has a cut with threshold
-\varepsilon\leq\theta\leq\varepsilon
, thus inevitably output a clustering with at least one misclassified point.

We have shown in
Lemma

3.1
that any alternative clustering
\tilde{C}
that misclassifies one point must have
\textnormal{{cost}}(\tilde{C})\geq p(|X|)\cdot\textnormal{{cost}}(C)
, therefore completing the proof.

4
Impossibility of Depth Reduction in the Plane for
k
-means and
k
-median

In this section, we prove a strong lower bound on the price of depth reduction for
k
-means and
k
-median in two dimensions. As in
Theorem

3.1
, we construct a point-set
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
, for which the optimal clustering can be explained by a decision tree of depth
k-1
whose price of explanation is 1. However, we show that, the low-dimensionality does not help reduce the price of depth reduction. Formally, we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

For every
k\geq 3
, and every polynomial function
p:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
, there exists a point-set
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
(which can be represented in
|X|^{O(1)}
bits) such that the following properties hold for
k
-means/
k
-median objectives:

Correctness:

There exists a decision tree of depth
k-1
such that the price of explainability is 1.

Price of depth reduction:

For every
h<k-1
, every decision tree of depth
h
yields a clustering whose cost is at least
p(|X|)
times the optimum. In other words,
\mathsf{D}^{\downarrow}(X,k-2)\geq p(|X|)
.

The proofs of the above theorem for the
k
-means and
k
-median objectives are very similar. Briefly, the underlying idea is to construct a point-set whose clusters are positioned such that the axis-parallel (either
x
or
y
-axis) interval between any two clusters except one pair are blocked by a new cluster. Since the partition of clusters must be parallel to either
x
or
y
-axis, we have that any cut line going through that interval will separate the points in the block cluster, and assign at least one point incorrectly. Therefore, to produce a perfect clustering, the decision tree must follow an ‘onion-peeling’ approach, that is to output one cluster at each partition of the remaining space, leading to a decision tree of depth
k-1
. To get a lower bound on the price of depth reduction, it is imperative that the clusters are geometrically unbalanced, and we choose the distances between the clusters and the number of points in each cluster carefully.

6
Discussion and Open Problems

In this paper, we introduced the notion of price of depth reduction, and showed that even in the plane, there is a dataset for which it is unbounded for the
k
-means and
k
-median objectives. However, one may wonder if our lower bound construction is too pathological. To address this concern, we now informally argue that our impossibility result gives a characterization of every dataset w.r.t. its optimal decision tree for explainable clustering.

We (informally) claim that our paper gives a complete characterization of the structure of datasets w.r.t. their decision tree depth. Call a rooted decision tree “totally skewed” if it’s depth is 1 less than the number of leaves in the tree. We note that in every decision tree, we can identify non-leaf nodes, say

v_{1},\ldots,v_{t}
(for the smallest value of
t
), with the following two guarantees: (i) Let
L_{i}

be the subset of leaves contained in the subtree rooted at
v_{i}
. Then the

L_{i}
s form partition of the set of leaves (i.e., point-set) (ii) Each subtree rooted at

v_{i}
is either a complete binary tree or a totally skewed tree. This is a simple graph-theoretic claim and even admits an algorithmic proof.

We also note that if the optimal explanation for a dataset is a totally skewed tree then the dataset is ”isomorphic” to our seemingly pathological dataset given in
Section

4
. To see this, consider all the cuts except the first cut. If the last (closest to the root) cluster didn’t intersect the second cut, we can use all the cuts except the first and add an additional cut somewhere else in the subtree to separate the last cluster, thus getting a smaller depth tree. So, the second cut intersects the last cluster. This means that the last cluster has to block the gap between some previous clusters. We can repeat this argument to show that the clusters are added in layers blocking the space between previous clusters.

Putting these two observations together, if we only have access to the optimal explanation of a dataset, then we apply the decomposition mentioned above to obtain the subtrees, and depending on whether the subtree is complete or totally skewed, we can infer the structure of the clusters in space (using our construction in
Section

4
) without looking at the dataset.
In other words, given the optimal decision tree of the

k
-clustering of a dataset, by using the above partitioning we can identify local “pockets” where the tree is complete or totally skewed. From this we can visualize the pockets as either having very good hyperplane separators or being isomorphic to the construction in
Section

4
. Another inference one can draw is that the pocket of clusters corresponding to the totally skewed subtree is highly unbalanced (more about this later). So it is entirely possible that for any dataset if it admits roughly balanced clusters then it can be approximated by low depth decision tree, and we leave this as a direction to explore for future research.

Additionally, we believe that the algorithm given in
[
EMN22
]
yields a low depth decision tree (with close to the optimal clustering cost), if the dataset is in
fixed dimensions
and admits a low depth decision tree (with optimal clustering cost). In fact, if we look at our construction in
Section

4
and make the clusters balanced (while preserving the distances), then it is easy to note that the cost of the clustering is dominated by the “outermost” clusters. Thus, as long as the explanations preserves the outermost clusters, the rest of the dataset can be partitioned in any way desired (to lower the depth), and the increase in the clustering cost would be negligible.

However, for high dimensions (such as

d=\Omega(k)
), our construction from
Section

3
is balanced, and in fact quite natural. To see this, assume a generative model coming from a mixture of

k
Gaussians, where we first draw
k

independent random points from a Gaussian distribution and then generate the entire dataset by using these
k

points as the means of the
k

many Gaussian distributions. With high probability these
k

points (centers) will be nearly orthogonal, and therefore the dataset will be morally like our construction in
Section

3
, and thus admit no low depth explanation. Of course, one needs to be careful in the analysis as the claim relies on the radius of each cluster (variance of the Gaussian) and how ”rotated” the
k

centers are from the standard basis. We leave this analysis and computation for future research.

Finally, we dedicate the rest of the section to highlight a couple of open problems.

Open Problem 1.

Our dataset construction for the price of depth reduction for the
k
-center objective in the plane, only gave a lower bound of (slightly less than) two. We believe that it might not be possible to improve our lower bound any further and leave it as an important open problem to find an algorithm (if it exists) that provides a shallow decision tree explanation for all datasets in the plane and whose price of depth reduction is upper bounded by 2 (or at least by some constant independent of
k
).

Open Problem 2.

We think that an intriguing direction for future research is to explore bicriteria approximation of price of depth reduction.
Elaborating, we wonder if it is possible to obtain a tradeoff between the number of leaves (clusters) in the decision tree and the price of depth reduction. This suggested research direction is a natural extension of
[
MS22
]
, where the authors showed a near optimal tradeoff between the number of leaves in the decision tree and the price of explanation.
