A Radó theorem for complex spaces
Viorel Vîjîitu

Abstract




Abstract
. We generalize Radó’s extension theorem
from the complex plane to reduced complex spaces.

2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
: 32D15, 32D20, 32C15

Key words
: Radó’s theorem, complex space,
c-holomorphic function

1
Introduction

A theorem due to Radó asserts that
a continuous complex valued function
on an open subset of the complex plane
is holomorphic
provided that it is holomorphic off its zero set.

Essentially this theorem was proved in
[
10
]
.
Since then many other proofs have been proposed,
e.g.

[
2
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
6
]
, and
[
7
]
.
The articles
[
1
]
,
[
11
]
and
[
14
]

give some generalizations.

Radó’s statement remains true for complex manifolds
(or, more generally, for normal complex spaces)
as well as in the complex plane.

In this short note we investigate a natural
extension of Radó’s theorem when the ambient space
has (non normal) singularities.

Complex spaces, unless explicitely stated,
are assumed to be reduced and countable at infinity.
Let
{\hbox{\BBb{N}}}=\{1,2,\ldots\}
.

Here we state our main results.

Proposition 1

There is an irreducible Stein curve

X
and a continuous function
f:X\longrightarrow{\hbox{\BBb{C}}}

that is holomorphic off its zero set but no
power
f^{n}
,
n\in{\hbox{\BBb{N}}}
, is globally holomorphic.

Theorem 1

Let
X
be a complex space
and
\Omega\subset X
be a relatively compact open set.
Then, there is
n_{\Omega}\in{\hbox{\BBb{N}}}
such that,
for every continuous function
f:X\longrightarrow{\hbox{\BBb{C}}}

that is holomorphic off its zero set,
for every integer
n\geq n_{\Omega}
, the power

f^{n}
is holomorphic on
\Omega
.

Recall the following definition. Let
X
be a complex space.
A function
f:X\longrightarrow{\hbox{\BBb{C}}}
is
c
-
holomorphic

if it is continuous and the restriction of
f
to the subset

{\rm Reg}(X)
of manifold points of
X

is holomorphic
[
15
]
.

2
Proof of Proposition 1

The singularities that we implant at the points

2,3,\ldots
, of
ℂ
are generalized cusp singularities.

Let
p
and
q
be integers
\geq 2
that are coprime.
Consider the cusp like irreducible and locally irreducible complex curve

\Gamma=\{(z_{1},z_{2})\in{\hbox{\BBb{C}}}^{2}\;:\,z_{1}^{p}=z_{2}^{q}\}\subset{\hbox{\BBb{C}}}^{2}.

Its normalization is
ℂ
and

\pi:{\hbox{\BBb{C}}}\longrightarrow\Gamma
,
t\mapsto(t^{q},t^{p})
,
is the normalization map. Note that
\pi
is a
homeomorphism.

A continuous function

h:\Gamma\longrightarrow{\hbox{\BBb{C}}}
that is holomorphic off its zero set,
but fails to be globally holomorphic
is produced as follows.

Select natural numbers
m
and
n

with
mq-np=1
, and define

h:\Gamma\longrightarrow{\hbox{\BBb{C}}}
by setting
for
(z_{1},z_{2})\in\Gamma
,

h(z_{1},z_{2}):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{z_{1}^{m}}/{z_{2}^{n}}&\mbox{ if }z_{2}\neq 0,\\
0&\mbox{ if }z_{2}=0.\\
\end{array}\right.

It is easily seen that
h
is continuous (as
\pi
is a homeomorphism, the continuity of
h
follows from that
of
h\circ\pi
, that equals
{\rm id}
of
ℂ
),
h
is holomorphic off its zero set
(incidentally, here, this is the set of smooth points of

\Gamma
), and
h
is not holomorphic about
(0,0)
(use a Taylor series expansion
about
(0,0)\in{\hbox{\BBb{C}}}^{2}
of a presumably holomorphic extension).

Furthermore,
h^{k}
is globally holomorphic provided that

k\geq(p-1)(q-1)
. (Because every integer

\geq(p-1)(q-1)
can be written
in the form
\alpha p+\beta q
with

\alpha,\beta\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
,
and since
h^{p}
and
h^{q}
are holomorphic being the restrictions of
z_{2}
and
z_{1}
to
\Gamma
respectively.)

Also,
z_{1}^{a}z_{2}^{b}h
is holomorphic on
\Gamma
provided that

q\lfloor(m+a)/p\rfloor+b\geq n,

where
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
is the floor function.

It is perhaps interesting to note that
the stalk of germs of weakly holomorphic
functions
\widetilde{\cal O}_{0}
at
0

is generated as an
{\cal O}_{0}
-module by the
germs at
0
of
1,h,\ldots,h^{r}
, where

r=-1+\min\{p,q\}
..

Now, for each integer
k\geq 2
, let

\Gamma_{k}:=\{(z_{1},z_{2})\in{\hbox{\BBb{C}}}^{2}\;;\,z_{1}^{k}=z_{2}^{k+1}\}
. As previously noted,

\Gamma_{k}
is an irreducible curve
whose normalization map is
\pi_{k}:{\hbox{\BBb{C}}}\longrightarrow\Gamma_{k},\,t\mapsto(t^{k+1},t^{k})
, and the function

h_{k}:\Gamma_{k}\longrightarrow{\hbox{\BBb{C}}}
defined for
(z_{1},z_{2})\in\Gamma_{k}
by

h(z_{1},z_{2}):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{z_{1}}/{z_{2}}&\mbox{ if }z_{2}\neq 0,\\
0&\mbox{ if }z_{2}=0.\\
\end{array}\right.

has the following properties:

{\bf a}_{k})

The function
h_{k}
is weakly holomorphic.

{\bf b}_{k})

The power
h_{k}^{k-1}
is not holomorphic.

{\bf c}_{k})

The function
z_{1}^{k-1}h_{k}
is holomorphic
because it is the restriction of
z_{2}^{k}
to
\Gamma_{k}
.

Here, with these examples of singularities at hand,
we change the standard complex structure of

ℂ
at the discrete analytic set

\{2,3,\ldots\}
by complex surgery in order to obtain an irreducible Stein complex curve
X
and a discrete subset

\Lambda=\{x_{k}:k=2,3,\ldots\}
such that,
at the level of germs

(X,x_{k})
is biholomorphic to
(\Gamma_{k},0)
.

The surgery, that we recall
for the commodity of the reader because in some
monographs like
[
8
]

the subsequent condition
(\star)
is missing, goes as follows,

Let
Y
and
U^{\prime}
be complex spaces together with
analytic subsets
A
and
A^{\prime}
of
Y
and
U^{\prime}
respectively,
such that there is an open neighborhood
U
of
A
in
Y
and

\varphi:U\setminus A\longrightarrow U^{\prime}\setminus A^{\prime}
that is biholomophic.

Then define

X:=(Y\setminus A)\sqcup_{\varphi}U^{\prime}:=(Y\setminus A)\sqcup U^{\prime}/_{\sim}

by means of the equivalence relation

U\setminus A\ni y\sim\varphi(y)\in U^{\prime}\setminus A^{\prime}
.

Then there exists exactly one complex structure
on
X
such that
U^{\prime}
and

Y\setminus A
can be viewed as open subsets of
X

in a canonical way provided that the
following condition is satisfied:

(\star)

For every
y\in\partial U
and
a^{\prime}\in A^{\prime}
there are open neighborhoods

D
of
y
in
Y
,
D\cap A=\emptyset
, and

B
of
a^{\prime}
in
U^{\prime}
such that

\varphi(D\cap U)\cap B\subseteq A^{\prime}.

Thus
X
is formed from
Y
by ”replacing“
A
with
A^{\prime}
.

In practice, the condition
(\star)
is fulfilled if
\varphi^{-1}:U^{\prime}\setminus A^{\prime}\longrightarrow U\setminus A

extends to a continuous function
\psi:U^{\prime}\longrightarrow U
such that
\psi(A^{\prime})=A
. In this case, if
D
and

V
are disjoint open neighborhoods of
\partial U
and
A
in
Y
respectively, then

B=A^{\prime}\cup\varphi(V\setminus A)
is open in
U^{\prime}
because it equals
\psi^{-1}(V)
and
(\star)
follows immediately.
(This process is employed, for instance,
in the construction of the blow-up of a point in a complex manifold!)

Coming back to our setting, consider
Y={\hbox{\BBb{C}}}
,
A=\{2,3,\ldots\}
and for each
k=2,3,\ldots
,
let
\Delta(k,1/3)
be the disk in
ℂ
centered at
k
of radius
1/3
that is mapped holomorphically
onto an open neighborhood
U_{k}
of
(0,0)\in\Gamma_{k}
through the holomorphic map
t\mapsto\pi_{k}(z-k)
.
Applying surgery, we get an irreducible Stein curve
X
and the discrete subset
\Lambda
with the aforementioned
properties.

It remains to produce the function
f
as stated. For this
we let
{\cal I}\subset{\cal O}_{X}
be the
coherent ideal sheaf with support
\Lambda

and such that
{\cal I}_{x_{k}}=\mathfrak{m}^{k-1}_{x_{k}}

for
k=2,3\ldots
, where
\mathfrak{m}_{x_{k}}

is the maximal ideal of the analytic algebra of the stalk of
{\cal O}_{X}
at
x_{k}
.

From the exact sequence

0\longrightarrow{\cal I}\longrightarrow\widetilde{\cal O}\longrightarrow\widetilde{\cal O}/{{\cal I}}\longrightarrow 0,

where
\widetilde{\cal O}
stands for the sheaf of germs of weakly holomorphic functions
in
X
,
we obtain a weakly holomorphic function
f
on
X

such that, for each
k=2,3,\ldots
,
at germs level
f
equals
f_{k}
(mod
{\cal I}_{x_{k}}
).

By properties
{\bf a}_{k})
,
{\bf b}_{k})
and

{\bf a}_{k})
from above, it follows that
no
n\in{\hbox{\BBb{N}}}
exists for which
f^{n}
becomes
holomorphic on
X
. (For instance, if
f=f_{k}+g_{k}^{k-1}
,
for certain
g_{k}\in\mathfrak{m}_{x_{k}}
, then

f^{k-1}
is not holomorphic about
x_{k}
.)

3
Proof of Theorem 1

Before starting the proof, we collect a few definitions and
facts.

Let
(A,a)
be a germ of a local analytic subset in
{\hbox{\BBb{C}}}^{n}
.
The third Whitney cone
{\rm C}_{a}(A)

consists of all vectors
{\bf v}\in{\hbox{\BBb{C}}}^{n}
such that there
is a sequence
(a_{j})_{j}
of points in
A
converging to
a
and a sequence
(n_{j})_{j}
of
ℕ
such that

n_{j}(a_{j}-a)\mapsto{\bf v}\;\mbox{ as }\>j\mapsto\infty.

If
A
is pure
k
-dimensional, then
{\rm C}_{a}(A)
is also a pure
k
-dimensional
analytic set. This cone
{\rm C}_{a}(A)
lies in the tangent space
T_{a}(A)
to
A
at
a

(
T_{a}(A)
is the sixth Withney cone; see
[
4
]

for more details).

Assume that the analytic germ
(A,0)\subset{\hbox{\BBb{C}}}^{k}_{z}\times{\hbox{\BBb{C}}}_{w}^{m-k}
,
with
x=(z,w)
, is pure
k
-dimensional such that the projection

\pi(z,w)=z
is proper on it, and
\pi^{-1}(0)\cap{\rm C}_{0}(A)=\{0\}
.
Then
\pi
is a branched covering on
A
with covering number
d:=\mbox{deg}_{0}A

and critical set
\Sigma
. Now, whenever
x\not\in\Sigma
, we may define, for a non-constant
{\rm c}
-holomorphic
germ
f:(A,0)\longrightarrow({\hbox{\BBb{C}}},0)
, the polynomial

\omega(x,t)=\prod_{\pi(x^{\prime})=\pi(x)}(t-f(x^{\prime})).

Since
f
is holomorphic on the regular part
\mbox{Reg}(A)
of
A
and
f
is continuous on
A
,
we obtain a distinguished Weierstrass polynomial of degree
d
with holomorphic coefficients,

W(z,t)=t^{d}+a_{1}(z)t^{d-1}+\cdots+a_{d}(z)
, such that
W(x,f(x))=0
for
x\in A
.

Note that
W
does not depend on
w
. Besides, if
W(t,z)=0
, then

|t|={\rm O}(\|z\|^{1/d})\mbox{ as }(z,t)\rightarrow 0

meaning that there are positive constants
M
and
\epsilon
such that, if
W(z,t)=0
and

\max\{|t|,\|z\|\}<\epsilon
, then
|t|\leq M\|z\|^{1/d}
.

Therefore, if
f:(A,0)\longrightarrow({\hbox{\BBb{C}}},0)
is a non-constant c-holomorphic germ on a pure
k
-dimensional
analytic germ at
0\in{\hbox{\BBb{C}}}^{m}
, then

|f(\zeta)|=O(\|\zeta\|^{1/d})\mbox{ as }\zeta\rightarrow 0,

where
d
equals the degree of the covering of the projection
\pi
satisfying
\pi^{-1}(0)\cap{\rm C}_{0}(A)=\{0\}
.

In other words, if
f:(A,a)\longrightarrow({\hbox{\BBb{C}}},0)
is a non-constant c-holomorphic germ on a pure dimensional analytic germ
A
at
a
, we may define the
order of flatness

of
f
at
a
to be

\mbox{\rm ord}_{a}f=\max\{\alpha>0\,:\,|f(x)|={\rm O}(\|x-a\|^{\alpha})\mbox{ as }x\rightarrow a\}.

It follows from the above discussion that
\mbox{\rm ord}_{a}f\geq 1/d
.

Following Spallek
[
13
]
, a germ function
f:(A,a)\longrightarrow({\hbox{\BBb{C}}},0)

is said to be
{\rm O}^{N}
-
approximable at

a
if there exists a polynomial
P(z,\bar{z})
of degree at most
N-1

in the variables
z_{i}-a_{i},\overline{z_{i}-a_{i}}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, such that

|f(z)-P(z,\bar{z})|={\rm O}(\|z-a\|^{N})\mbox{ as }z\rightarrow a.

Here we quote from Siu
[
12
]
the following
result that improves onto Spallek’s similar one
(
loc. cit.
).

Proposition 2

For every compact set
K
of a complex space
X
there exists a positive integer

N

(
depending on
K
)
such that, if
f
is a

c
-holomorphic function germ
at
x\in K
and the real part
\Re f
of
f
is
{\rm O}^{N}
-approximable in some neighborhood of
x
, then
f
is
a holomorphic germ at
x
.

The above discussion concludes readily the proof of Theorem 1.

4
A final remark

Below we answer a question raised by Th. Peternell at the XXIV Conference on
Complex Analysis and Geometry, held in Levico-Terme, June 10–14, 2019. He asked whether or not a similar statement
like Theorem 1 does hold for non reduced complex spaces.

More specifically, let
(X,{\cal O}_{X})
be a not necessarily reduced complex space
and

f:X\longrightarrow{\hbox{\BBb{C}}}
be continuous such that, if
A
denotes the zero set of
f
, then
X\setminus A
is dense in
X
and
there is a section
\sigma\in\Gamma(X\setminus A,{\cal O}_{X})
whose reduction

{\rm Red}(\sigma)
equals
f|_{X\setminus A}
.

Is it true that, for every relatively compact open subset

D
of
X
, there is a positive integer
n
such that
\sigma^{n}
extends to a section
in
\Gamma(D,{\cal O}_{X})
?

We show that the answer is ”No“.

Recall that, if
R
is a commutative ring with unit
and
M
is an
R
-module, we can endow
the direct sum
R\oplus M
with a ring structure with the obvious addition, and multiplication
defined by

(r,m)\cdot(r^{\prime},m^{\prime})=(rr^{\prime},rm^{\prime}+r^{\prime}m).

This is the Nagata ring structure from algebra
[
9
]
.

Now, if
(X,{\cal O}_{X})
is a complex space, and
\cal F
a coherent
{\cal O}_{X}
-module,
then
{\cal H}:={\cal O}_{X}\oplus{\cal F}

becomes a coherent sheaf of analytic algebras and

(X,{\cal H})
a complex space (
[
5
]
, Satz 2.3).

The example is as follows. Let
{}_{n}{\cal O}
denotes the structural sheaf of
{\hbox{\BBb{C}}}^{n}
. The above discussion
produces a complex space
({\hbox{\BBb{C}}},{\cal H})
such that

{\cal H}={}_{1}{\cal O}\oplus{}_{1}{\cal O}
,
that can be written in a suggestive way

{\cal H}={}_{1}{\cal O}+\epsilon\cdot{}_{1}{\cal O}
,
where
\epsilon
is a symbol with
\epsilon^{2}=0
.
As a matter of fact, if we consider
{\hbox{\BBb{C}}}^{2}
with complex
coordinates
(z,w)
and the
coherent ideal
{\cal I}
generated by
w^{2}
, then
{\cal H}

is the analytic restriction of the quotient

{}_{2}{\cal O}/{{\cal I}}
to
ℂ
.

The reduction of
({\hbox{\BBb{C}}},{\cal H})
is
({\hbox{\BBb{C}}},{}_{1}{\cal O})
.
A holomorphic section of
{\cal H}
over an open set

U\subset{\hbox{\BBb{C}}}
consists of couple of ordinary
holomorphic functions on
U
.

Now take
f
the identity function
{\rm id}
on
ℂ
, and
the holomorphic section

\sigma\in\Gamma({\hbox{\BBb{C}}}^{\star},{\cal H})

given by
\sigma={\rm id}+\epsilon g
,
where
g
is holomorphic on
{\hbox{\BBb{C}}}^{\star}

having a singularity at
0
, for
instance
g(z)=1/z
.

Obviously, the reduction of
\sigma
is
the restriction of
{\rm id}

on
{\hbox{\BBb{C}}}^{\star}
, and
no power
\sigma^{k}
of
\sigma

extends across
0
to a section in

\Gamma({\hbox{\BBb{C}}},{\cal H})
, since
\sigma^{k}={\rm id}+\epsilon kg
and

g
does not extend holomorphically across
0\in{\hbox{\BBb{C}}}
.
