Chasing Convex Bodies and Functions with Black-Box Advice
Nicolas Christianson, Tinashe Handina, Adam Wierman

Abstract
We consider the problem of convex function chasing with black-box advice, where an online decision-maker aims to minimize the total cost of making and switching between decisions in a normed vector space, aided by black-box advice such as the decisions of a machine-learned algorithm. The decision-maker seeks cost comparable to the advice when it performs well, known as consistency , while also ensuring worst-case robustness even when the advice is adversarial. We first consider the common paradigm of algorithms that switch between the decisions of the advice and a competitive algorithm, showing that no algorithm in this class can improve upon 3-consistency while staying robust. We then propose two novel algorithms that bypass this limitation by exploiting the problem’s convexity. The first, Interp , achieves ( 2 + ϵ ) (\sqrt{2}+\epsilon) -consistency and 𝒪 ⁡ ( C ϵ 2 ) {\mathcal{O}}(\frac{C}{\epsilon^{2}}) -robustness for any ϵ > 0 \epsilon>0 , where C C is the competitive ratio of an algorithm for convex function chasing or a subclass thereof. The second, BdInterp , achieves ( 1 + ϵ ) (1+\epsilon) -consistency and 𝒪 ⁡ ( C ​ D ϵ ) {\mathcal{O}}(\frac{CD}{\epsilon}) -robustness when the problem has bounded diameter D D . Further, we show that BdInterp achieves near-optimal consistency-robustness trade-off for the special case where cost functions are α \alpha -polyhedral.

1
Introduction

We study the problem of convex function chasing (
{\mathsf{CFC}}
), in which a player chooses decisions
{\vec{x}}_{t}
online from a normed vector space
{\mathcal{X}}=(X,\|\cdot\|)
in order to minimize the total cost
\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}({\vec{x}}_{t})+\|{\vec{x}}_{t}-{\vec{x}}_{t-1}\|
, where each
f_{t}
is a convex “hitting” cost function that is revealed prior to the player’s selection of
{\vec{x}}_{t}
, and the term
\|{\vec{x}}_{t}-{\vec{x}}_{t-1}\|
penalizes changing decisions between rounds. A number of subclasses of
{\mathsf{CFC}}
have been discussed in the literature, characterized by various restrictions on the class of cost functions
f_{t}
. Of particular note is the special case of convex body chasing (
{\mathsf{CBC}}
), in which each cost function
f_{t}
is the
\{0,\infty\}
indicator of a convex set
K_{t}
, so that each decision
{\vec{x}}_{t}
must reside strictly within
K_{t}
. Algorithms for
{\mathsf{CFC}}
and its special cases are judged on the basis of their competitive ratio, i.e., the worst-case ratio in cost between the algorithm and the hindsight optimal sequence of decisions (Definition
1
).

Convex body chasing and function chasing were introduced by
Friedman and Linial 1993
as continuous versions of several fundamental problems in online algorithms, including Metrical Task Systems (
Borodin et al. 1992
) and the
k
-server problem (
Koutsoupias and Papadimitriou 1995
).
{\mathsf{CFC}}
has also been studied recently as the problem of “smoothed online convex optimization” (
{\mathsf{SOCO}}
), introduced by
Lin et al. 2012
. The basic premise of
{\mathsf{CFC}}
/
{\mathsf{SOCO}}
, of choosing decisions online to optimize per-round costs with minimal movement between decisions, has seen wide application in a number of domains, including data center load-balancing (
Lin et al. 2012
) and right-sizing (
Lin et al. 2013
;
Albers and Quedenfeld 2018
), electric vehicle charging (
Kim and Giannakis 2014
), and control (
Goel and Wierman 2019
;
Li et al. 2021
).

In high-dimensional settings, the performance of algorithms for
{\mathsf{CBC}}
and
{\mathsf{CFC}}
can be arbitrarily poor:
Friedman and Linial 1993
showed a
\sqrt{d}
lower bound on the competitive ratio of any algorithm for
{\mathsf{CBC}}
(and thus
{\mathsf{CFC}}
) in
d
-dimensional Euclidean space, which
Bubeck et al. 2019
extended to an
\Omega(\max\{\sqrt{d},d^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\})
lower bound in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with the
\ell^{p}
norm. Prospects are poor even for subclasses of
{\mathsf{CFC}}
with additional restrictions on the functions
f_{t}
. For instance,
{\mathsf{CFC}}
with
\alpha
-polyhedral
cost functions, i.e. where each
f_{t}
has a unique minimizer away from which it grows with slope at least
\alpha>0
, has been studied widely in the
{\mathsf{SOCO}}
literature. State-of-the-art algorithms in this setting achieve competitive ratio
{\mathcal{O}}(\alpha^{-1})
, which grows arbitrarily large in the
\alpha\to 0
limit (
Chen et al. 2018
;
Zhang et al. 2021
).

The modern tools of machine learning wield great promise for improving upon these pessimistic performance guarantees. That is, for practical applications, there is often large amounts of data recorded from past problem instances, enabling the training of machine learning models that can outperform traditional, conservative online algorithms. However, these machine-learned algorithms are “black boxes,” in the sense that they lack rigorous, worst-case performance guarantees. Such black-box algorithms might
typically
outperform robust online algorithms, but their lack of uncertainty quantification can lead to arbitrarily poor performance in the worse case, if they are deployed on held-out problem instances or under distribution shift.

Thus, a natural question arises:
is it possible to develop algorithms that achieve both the worst-case guarantees of traditional online algorithms for
{\mathsf{CFC}}
and the average-case performance of machine-learned algorithms or other sources of black-box “advice”?

These desiderata are naturally encoded in the notions of
robustness
and
consistency
introduced by
Lykouris and Vassilvtiskii 2018
in the context of competitive caching. In this framework, a
consistent
algorithm is one with a competitive ratio with respect to the black-box advice, implying that when the advice is accurate, the algorithm will perform well; on the other hand, a
robust
algorithm is one that has a finite competitive ratio, regardless of advice performance. Our goal is to develop algorithms with tunable robustness and consistency guarantees, so that a decision-maker can decide in advance the trade-off they wish to make between exploiting good advice performance and ensuring worst-case robustness in the case that advice performs poorly.

1.1
Contributions

We answer the question above by proposing novel algorithms with tunable robustness and consistency bounds for
{\mathsf{CFC}}
and any subclass thereof. In particular, we reduce the problem of designing robust and consistent algorithms for
{\mathsf{CFC}}
to the design of
bicompetitive meta-algorithms
(Definitions
4
,
5
), which are unified “recipes” for combining black-box advice with a robust algorithm in a manner that guarantees a competitive ratio with respect to both ingredients. These “recipes” are very general – they can be used to combine advice with
any
algorithm for any subclass of
{\mathsf{CFC}}
to obtain a customized robustness and consistency guarantee for that subclass without explicit knowledge of the algorithm or advice design.

More specifically, our contributions are twofold. We first consider the class of “switching” algorithms, which switch between the decisions of the advice and a robust algorithm. This class of algorithms has received considerable attention in the literature on robustness and consistency, and in particular, all prior algorithms for
{\mathsf{CFC}}
with black-box advice in dimension greater than one have been switching algorithms. We prove a fundamental limit on the robustness and consistency of any switching algorithm for
{\mathsf{CFC}}
, showing that
no
switching algorithm for
{\mathsf{CFC}}
can improve on
3
-consistency while obtaining finite robustness (Theorem
8
). We give a switching meta-algorithm
Switch
(Appendix
B.1
, Algorithm
4
) achieving this fundamental limit, obtaining
(3+{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon))
-consistency and
{\mathcal{O}}(\frac{C}{\epsilon^{2}})
-robustness for any
\epsilon>0
, where
C
is the competitive ratio of any algorithm for
{\mathsf{CFC}}
or a subclass thereof. We further show that the fundamental limit on switching algorithms can be broken in the special case of nested
{\mathsf{CBC}}
, in which successive bodies are nested. In this setting, we provide an algorithm
NestedSwitch
(Algorithm
1
) achieving
(1+\epsilon)
-consistency along with
{\mathcal{O}}(\frac{d}{\epsilon})
-robustness for nested
{\mathsf{CBC}}
in
d
dimensions (Proposition
9
).

Second
, galvanized by the limitations of switching algorithms, we develop algorithms exploiting the convexity of the
{\mathsf{CFC}}
problem to obtain improved robustness and consistency bounds. We propose a meta-algorithm
Interp
(Algorithm
2
) that, given a
C
-competitive algorithm for a subclass of
{\mathsf{CFC}}
, achieves
(\mu({\mathcal{X}})+\epsilon)
-consistency and
{\mathcal{O}}(\frac{C}{\epsilon^{2}})
-robustness for any desired
\epsilon>0
, where
\mu({\mathcal{X}})
is a geometric constant depending on the structure of the normed space
{\mathcal{X}}
that is
\sqrt{2}
in any Hilbert space and is strictly less than
3
in any
\ell^{p}
space,
p\in(1,\infty)
(Theorem
11
). Moreover, under the additional assumption that the advice and the
C
-competitive algorithm are never farther apart than some distance
D
, we give a meta-algorithm
BdInterp
(Algorithm
3
) that achieves
(1+\epsilon)
-consistency and
{\mathcal{O}}(\frac{CD}{\epsilon})
-robustness (Theorem
12
). In particular,
BdInterp
gives nearly-optimal consistency and robustness for the problem of
{\mathsf{CFC}}
with
\alpha
-polyhedral cost functions when
D=O(1)
in
\alpha
.

A key feature of our results is their generality: our main results on bicompetitive meta-algorithms (Proposition
7
, Theorems
11
,
12
) hold in vector spaces with any norm and arbitrary, even infinite, dimension. This enables application to problems where the decisions
{\vec{x}}_{t}
are infinite-dimensional objects such as probability measures, which could arise in settings such as iterated games. Moreover, these meta-algorithms enable the design of customized robust and consistent algorithms for
any
subclass of
{\mathsf{CFC}}
, since they are agnostic to the specific algorithms used. We illustrate this by giving specific robustness and consistency results for the cases of
{\mathsf{CFC}}
,
{\mathsf{CBC}}
, and
{\mathsf{CFC}}
restricted to
\alpha
-polyhedral hitting cost functions; we give further examples in Appendix
F
.

1.2
Related work

Our work contributes to the literatures on
{\mathsf{CBC}}
,
{\mathsf{CFC}}
, and
{\mathsf{SOCO}}
as well as the emerging literature on online algorithms with black-box advice. We discuss each in turn below.

1.2.1
{\mathsf{CBC}}
,
{\mathsf{CFC}}
, and
{\mathsf{SOCO}}

The problems of convex body chasing and function chasing were introduced by
Friedman and Linial 1993
, who gave a competitive algorithm for
{\mathsf{CBC}}
in 2-dimensional Euclidean space. The problem in general dimension
d
has been largely settled in the last few years. In the setting where subsequent bodies are nested,
Argue et al. 2019
gave an
{\mathcal{O}}(d\log d)
-competitive algorithm in any norm, and
Bubeck et al. 2019
later gave an
{\mathcal{O}}(\min\{d,\sqrt{d\log T}\})
-competitive algorithm in the Euclidean setting that uses the geometric Steiner point of the convex bodies. Later,
Argue et al. 2021
and
Sellke 2020
concurrently obtained
{\mathcal{O}}(d)
-competitive algorithms for general
{\mathsf{CFC}}
. The latter work builds upon the methods of
Bubeck et al. 2019
, developing a “functional” Steiner point algorithm that is
d
-competitive for
{\mathsf{CBC}}
and
(d+1)
-competitive for
{\mathsf{CFC}}
in any normed space, matching the lower bound of
d
in the
\ell^{\infty}
norm setting.

Several special cases of
{\mathsf{CFC}}
/
{\mathsf{SOCO}}
with restrictions on hitting cost structure have been studied in the literature to the end of obtaining “dimension-free” competitive ratios for these subclasses.
Chen et al. 2018
obtained the first such bound for the subclass of
{\mathsf{CFC}}
where hitting cost functions
f_{t}
are
\alpha
-polyhedral, which we call
{\mathsf{\alpha CFC}}
. The authors propose an algorithm, “Online Balanced Descent” (OBD), which achieves a competitive ratio
{\mathcal{O}}(\frac{1}{\alpha})
. This upper bound has been successively refined, with the most recent entry a simple greedy algorithm from
Zhang et al. 2021
that achieves competitive ratio
\max\{1,\frac{2}{\alpha}\}
in any normed vector space of arbitrary (even infinite) dimension by moving to the minimizer of each hitting cost function. The
{\mathcal{O}}(\frac{1}{\alpha})
upper bound has been broken by
Lin 2022
in the finite-dimensional Euclidean setting with an
{\mathcal{O}}(\frac{1}{\alpha^{1/2}})
-competitive algorithm, Greedy OBD, that is optimal within the class of memoryless, rotation- and scale-invariant algorithms. Another subclass of
{\mathsf{CFC}}
that has received attention is that with
(\kappa,\gamma)
-well-centered hitting cost functions, which generalize well-conditioned functions.
Argue et al. 2020
propose an algorithm achieving an
{\mathcal{O}}(2^{\gamma/2}\kappa)
competitive ratio for this subclass, and an improved algorithm achieving competitive ratio
{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{\kappa})
for the particular class of
\kappa

\Omega(\kappa^{1/3})

1.2.2
Online Algorithms with Black-Box Advice

The idea of using machine-learned, black-box advice to improve online algorithms was first proposed by
Mahdian et al. 2012
to design algorithms for online ad allocation, load balancing, and facility location. Formal notions of
robustness
and
consistency
were later coined by
Lykouris and Vassilvtiskii 2018
in the context of designing learning-augmented algorithms for caching. The last few years have seen a surge in the application of the robustness and consistency paradigm in designing online algorithms augmented with black-box advice for a multitude of problems, for example ski rental and non-clairvoyant scheduling (
Purohit et al. 2018
;
Wei and Zhang 2020
), energy generation scheduling (
Lee et al. 2021
), bidding and bin-packing (
Angelopoulos et al. 2020
), and Q-learning (
Golowich and Moitra 2021
).

Closest to our work are the recent papers of
Antoniadis et al. 2020
and
Rutten et al. 2022
. The former considers the problem of designing algorithms for
metrical task systems
(MTS) with black-box advice. MTS can be thought of as (non-convex) function chasing on general metric spaces, and hence their results also give robustness/consistency guarantees for
{\mathsf{CFC}}
. They apply two classical results on combining
k
-server algorithms (
Fiat et al. 1994
;
Baeza-Yates et al. 1993
) and on combining MTS algorithms via
k
-experts algorithms (
Blum and Burch 1997
;
Freund and Schapire 1997
) to devise, in our parlance, bicompetitive meta-algorithms for MTS. In particular, their first algorithm switches between the advice and a
C
-competitive algorithm for MTS, and achieves
9
-consistency and
9C
-robustness. They also propose a randomized switching algorithm that, under the assumption that the metric space has bounded diameter
D
, obtains cost bounded in expectation by
\min\{(1+\epsilon){\mathrm{C}}_{{\textsc{Adv}}}+{\mathcal{O}}(\frac{D}{\epsilon}),(1+\epsilon)C\cdot{\mathrm{C}}_{\textsc{Opt}}+{\mathcal{O}}(\frac{D}{\epsilon})\}
, where
{\mathrm{C}}_{\textsc{Adv}}
is the cost of the advice and
{\mathrm{C}}_{\textsc{Opt}}
is the optimal cost. However, the large
{\mathcal{O}}(\frac{D}{\epsilon})
additive factors in their result preclude
(1+\epsilon)
-consistency, since when
{\mathrm{C}}_{\textsc{Adv}}={\mathcal{O}}(1)
, the consistency bound will be
1+\epsilon+\Omega(\frac{D}{\epsilon})
. This is to be expected, since as we show in Section
3
, no deterministic switching algorithm can improve on 3-consistency while having finite robustness. Moreover, their results due not allow tuning robustness and consistency, i.e., neither algorithm allows trading-off robustness in order to obtain consistency arbitrarily close to 1.

On the other hand,
Rutten et al. 2022
considers the problem of
{\mathsf{CFC}}
with
\alpha
-polyhedral hitting costs (
{\mathsf{\alpha CFC}}
), but with the convexity assumption dropped from the hitting costs
f_{t}
. They obtain a
(1+\epsilon)
-consistent,
2^{\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}(\frac{1}{\alpha\epsilon})}
-robust algorithm in this setting, together with a lower bound showing that this exponential trade-off between robustness and consistency is necessary due to their non-convex setting. Their algorithm is a switching algorithm and crucially depends on the
\alpha
-polyhedral structure of the hitting cost functions, and hence cannot be extended to general
{\mathsf{CFC}}
. The authors also propose an algorithm for
{\mathsf{CFC}}
in the 1-dimensional case (where
X={\mathbb{R}}
) that achieves
(1+\epsilon)
-consistency and
{\mathcal{O}}(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}})
robustness, and they prove a lower bound of
(1+\epsilon)
-consistency and
{\mathcal{O}}(\frac{1}{\epsilon})
robustness on
any
algorithm for
{\mathsf{CFC}}
with black-box advice. They leave open the broader problem of developing robust and consistent algorithms for
{\mathsf{CFC}}
and its many subclasses in the higher-dimensional setting.

\|\cdot\|
-ball of radius
r\geq 0

{\vec{x}}
, and
\Pi_{K}{\vec{x}}

2
Preliminaries

We consider the general problem of
convex function chasing
(
{\mathsf{CFC}}
) on a real normed vector space
{\mathcal{X}}=(X,\|\cdot\|)
. In particular, we make no assumption on either the dimension of
{\mathcal{X}}
or on the choice of norm
\|\cdot\|
. In
{\mathsf{CFC}}
, a decision-maker begins at some initial point
{\vec{x}}_{0}\in X
, and at each time
t\in{\mathbb{N}}
is handed a convex function
f_{t}:X\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
and must choose some
{\vec{x}}_{t}\in X
, paying both the
hitting cost

f_{t}({\vec{x}}_{t})
, as well as the
movement
or
switching cost

\|{\vec{x}}_{t}-{\vec{x}}_{t-1}\|
induced by the norm. Crucially,
{\vec{x}}_{t}
is chosen prior to the revelation of any future cost functions
f_{k}
,
k>t
, i.e., decisions are made
online
. The game ends at some time
T\in{\mathbb{N}}
, which is unknown to the decision-maker in advance. We refer to a tuple
({\vec{x}}_{0},f_{1},\ldots,f_{T})
as an
instance
of the
{\mathsf{CFC}}
problem. The total cost incurred by the decision-maker on a problem instance is
\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}({\vec{x}}_{t})+\|{\vec{x}}_{t}-{\vec{x}}_{t-1}\|
.

Informally, an
online algorithm
for
{\mathsf{CFC}}
is an algorithm that, on a given instance of
{\mathsf{CFC}}
, produces decisions online. We denote by
{\textsc{Alg}}_{t}
the
t
th
decision made by an online algorithm
Alg
; by convention,
{\textsc{Alg}}_{0}\coloneqq{\vec{x}}_{0}
, the starting point of the instance. Then the cost
{\mathrm{C}}_{\textsc{Alg}}
incurred by
Alg
on an instance is

{\mathrm{C}}_{\textsc{Alg}}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}({\textsc{Alg}}_{t})+\|{\textsc{Alg}}_{t}-{\textsc{Alg}}_{t-1}\|.

We also introduce the partial cost notation
{\mathrm{C}}_{\textsc{Alg}}(t,t^{\prime})=\sum_{i=t}^{t^{\prime}}f_{i}({\textsc{Alg}}_{i})+\|{\textsc{Alg}}_{i}-{\textsc{Alg}}_{i-1}\|
, defined for
1\leq t\leq t^{\prime}\leq T
. We refer to the set of all online algorithms for
{\mathsf{CFC}}
as
{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathsf{CFC}}
.

We typically compare online algorithms for
{\mathsf{CFC}}
against
Opt
, the offline optimal algorithm that chooses the hindsight optimal sequence of decisions for any problem instance. Its cost is the optimal value of the following convex program:

{\mathrm{C}}_{\textsc{Opt}}={\mathrm{C}}_{\textsc{Opt}}({\vec{x}}_{0},f_{1},\ldots,f_{T})\coloneqq\min_{{\vec{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\vec{x}}_{T}\in X}\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}({\vec{x}}_{t})+\|{\vec{x}}_{t}-{\vec{x}}_{t-1}\|

and its decisions are determined by the optimal solution. To evaluate the performance of an online algorithm for
{\mathsf{CFC}}
, we consider the
competitive ratio
, which measures the worst case ratio in costs between an algorithm and
Opt
. In the following, we define both the conventional competitive ratio as well as a generalization that allows for comparing against arbitrary benchmark algorithms.

Definition 1
.

Let
\textsc{Alg}^{(1)}
be an online algorithm for
{\mathsf{CFC}}
, and let
\textsc{Alg}^{(2)}
be another (not necessarily online) algorithm for
{\mathsf{CFC}}
.
1
1

1

Like
Opt
, the decision of
\textsc{Alg}^{(2)}
at some time
t
is allowed to depend on problem instance data revealed after time
t
.

\textsc{Alg}^{(1)}
is defined to be
C
-competitive with respect to

\textsc{Alg}^{(2)}
if, regardless of problem instance,

{\mathrm{C}}_{\textsc{Alg}}^{(1)}\leq C\cdot{\mathrm{C}}_{\textsc{Alg}}^{(2)}.

In particular, if
\textsc{Alg}^{(2)}={\textsc{Opt}}
, we simply say that
\textsc{Alg}^{(1)}
is
C
-competitive
, or has
competitive ratio
C
.

2.1
Subclasses of Convex Function Chasing

{\mathsf{CFC}}
is a broad set of problems and many subclasses have received attention in the literature. We consider several subclasses of the general
{\mathsf{CFC}}
problem in this work, distinguished by different assumptions on the hitting cost functions. In this section, we briefly define the subclasses of
{\mathsf{CFC}}
which we refer to in our later results in the main text. We give more detailed definitions of these and several other subclasses of
{\mathsf{CFC}}
in Appendix
A.1
.

2.1.1
Convex Body Chasing

In the problem of convex body chasing (
{\mathsf{CBC}}
), the decision-maker must choose each decision
{\vec{x}}_{t}
from a convex body
K_{t}\subseteq X
that is revealed online. This can be seen as a special case of
{\mathsf{CFC}}
where
f_{t}
is
0
on
K_{t}
and
\infty
elsewhere; see Appendix
A.1.1
for more details on this equivalence. A notable special case of
{\mathsf{CBC}}
is the problem of
nested
convex body chasing (
{\mathsf{NCBC}}
), in which subsequent bodies are nested, i.e.,
K_{t}\supseteq K_{t+1}
for each
t
. We define
{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathsf{CBC}}
as the set of all online algorithms for
{\mathsf{CBC}}
that are
feasible
, i.e., that produce decisions within the convex body
K_{t}
at each time. We define
{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathsf{NCBC}}
similarly as the set of
feasible
online algorithms for
{\mathsf{NCBC}}
.

2.1.2
\alpha
-Polyhedral Convex Function Chasing

Several subclasses of
{\mathsf{CFC}}
have been studied in the literature with hitting cost functions
f_{t}
restricted so as to enable dimension-free competitive ratios. One of the most well-studied such subclasses is the problem of
\alpha
-polyhedral convex function chasing (
{\mathsf{\alpha CFC}}
), e.g.
Chen et al. 2018
;
Zhang et al. 2021
, in which each hitting cost function
f_{t}
is restricted to be globally
\alpha
-polyhedral, meaning intuitively that it has a unique minimizer, away from which it grows with slope at least
\alpha>0
.

Definition 2
.

Let
(X,\|\cdot\|)
be a normed vector space, and let
\alpha>0
. A function
f:X\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is
globally
\alpha
-polyhedral
if it has unique minimizer
{\vec{x}}^{*}\in X
, and in addition,

f({\vec{x}})\geq f({\vec{x}}^{*})+\alpha\|{\vec{x}}-{\vec{x}}^{*}\|\qquad\text{for all ${\vec{x}}\in X$}.

2.2
Using Black-Box Advice: Robustness, Consistency, and Bicompetitive Analysis

In this work, we seek algorithms for
{\mathsf{CFC}}
and its subclasses that can exploit the good performance of a black-box advice algorithm, such as a reinforcement learning model, while maintaining rigorous worst-case performance guarantees. More specifically, we strive for algorithms that can obtain cost not much worse than optimal when the black-box advice is perfect, yet which have uniformly bounded competitive ratio when the advice is arbitrarily bad or even adversarial. This dual objective is naturally formulated in terms of
robustness
and
consistency
, which were introduced by
Lykouris and Vassilvtiskii 2018
and are defined as follows.

Definition 3
.

Let
Alg
be an online algorithm for
{\mathsf{CFC}}
, and let
Adv
be a black-box advice algorithm.
Alg
is said to be
c
-consistent
if it is
c
-competitive with respect to
Adv
. On the other hand,
Alg
is defined to be
r
-robust
if it is
r
-competitive, independent of the performance of
Adv
.

Our precise goal is to design algorithms achieving
(1+\epsilon)
-consistency and
R(\epsilon)
-robustness for
{\mathsf{CFC}}
and its subclasses, where
\epsilon>0
is a hyperparameter chosen by the decision-maker that encodes confidence in the advice. The dependence of the robustness
R(\epsilon)
on
\epsilon
anticipates a trade-off between exploiting advice and worst-case robustness. We ideally seek algorithms with robustness
R(\epsilon)
as small as possible, so that the trade-off between consistency and robustness is tight.

Our methodology for designing robust and consistent algorithms is very general, in the sense that we do not restrict to any special cases of
{\mathsf{CFC}}
and do not consider in our analysis the explicit behavior of the advice or of any specific algorithm for
{\mathsf{CFC}}
. This is in contrast to the work of
Rutten et al. 2022
, whose main robustness and consistency guarantees depend crucially upon the
\alpha
-polyhedral setting. Rather, we approach the task of designing robust and consistent algorithms via a more general problem of designing
bicompetitive

meta-algorithms
for
{\mathsf{CFC}}
, which, informally, are “recipes” for combining two
{\mathsf{CFC}}
algorithms to produce a single algorithm with competitive guarantees with respect to both input algorithms. More formally, we give the following definitions.

Definition 4
.

An online algorithm
Alg
for
{\mathsf{CFC}}
is
(c,r)
-bicompetitive
with respect to a pair of algorithms
(\textsc{Alg}^{(1)},\textsc{Alg}^{(2)})
if
Alg
is simultaneously
c
-competitive with respect to
\textsc{Alg}^{(1)}
and
r
-competitive with respect to
\textsc{Alg}^{(2)}
. Equivalently, the cost of
Alg
can be bounded as

{\mathrm{C}}_{\textsc{Alg}}\leq\min\left\{c\cdot{\mathrm{C}}_{\textsc{Alg}^{(1)}},r\cdot{\mathrm{C}}_{\textsc{Alg}^{(2)}}\right\}.

Definition 5
.

A
meta-algorithm

Meta
for
{\mathsf{CFC}}
is a mapping
{\textsc{Meta}}:{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathsf{CFC}}\times{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathsf{CFC}}\to{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathsf{CFC}}
. That is,
Meta
takes as input two online algorithms for
{\mathsf{CFC}}
and returns a single online algorithm for the problem.
Meta
is said to be
(c,r)
-bicompetitive
if its output is always
(c,r)
-bicompetitive with respect to its inputs.

It follows immediately from the previous two definitions that if
Meta
is
(c,r)
-bicompetitive,
Adv
is the advice, and
Rob
is a
b
-competitive algorithm for (a subclass of)
{\mathsf{CFC}}
, then
{\textsc{Meta}}({\textsc{Adv}},{\textsc{Rob}})
is
c
-consistent and
rb
-robust. We discuss this observation in more detail in Appendix
A.2
. Thus bicompetitive meta-algorithms give a general approach for designing robust and consistent algorithms for
{\mathsf{CFC}}
and its subclasses.

The idea of approaching robust and consistent algorithm design via the design of bicompetitive meta-algorithms has been considered to some extent in the literature on other online problems, e.g. in the work of
Antoniadis et al. 2020
on combining algorithms for MTS. To our knowledge, however, our specific terminology has not seen wide use in the literature.

3
Warmup: Switching Algorithms and Their Fundamental Limits

A natural first approach for designing bicompetitive meta-algorithms for
{\mathsf{CFC}}
is to consider the class of switching algorithms, whose decisions switch between two other algorithms:

Definition 6
.

A meta-algorithm
Meta
is a

{{\textsc{Meta}}_{t}(\textsc{Alg}^{(1)},\textsc{Alg}^{(2)})}

Switching algorithms have garnered significant attention in the literature on robustness and consistency in recent years, e.g.,
Antoniadis et al. 2020
;
Lee et al. 2021
;
Angelopoulos 2021
;
Rutten et al. 2022
. In particular, the only robust and consistent algorithms for
{\mathsf{CFC}}
or subclasses thereof in general dimension are the switching algorithms of
Antoniadis et al. 2020
for MTS and
Rutten et al. 2022
for
{\mathsf{\alpha CFC}}
. In the following proposition, we refine these prior results, showing the existence of a switching meta-algorithm for general
{\mathsf{CFC}}
with tunable bicompetitive bound.

Proposition 7

Suppose
{\textsc{Adv}},{\textsc{Rob}}

{\mathsf{CFC}}

\left(3+{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon),5+{\mathcal{O}}(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}})\right)

Beyond Switching Algorithms: Exploiting Convexity to Break 3-Consistency

In this section, we present our main results: two novel meta-algorithms that transcend the limitations of switching algorithms by exploiting the convexity of
{\mathsf{CFC}}
. The key insight that enables this improved performance is that
hedging
between
Adv
and
Rob
, i.e., choosing a decision that is a convex combination of the two, allows for more nuanced algorithm behavior than switching permits.

4.1
Interp

(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon,{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-2}))

We preface our first algorithm with some definitions from the geometry of real normed vector spaces
{\mathcal{X}}=(X,\|\cdot\|)
which we employ in the algorithm’s statement and performance bound. We present abridged introductions of these notions here, giving more detail in Appendix

We begin by introducing the
rectangular constant

\mu({\mathcal{X}})
of a normed vector space
{\mathcal{X}}
, which is bounded between
\sqrt{2}
and
3

\mu({\mathcal{X}})=\sqrt{2}
when

is Hilbert and

Definition 10

(
Rieffel 2006

On a normed vector space

is the metric projection onto the closed ball of radius

\rho({\vec{x}};r)=\begin{cases}{\vec{x}}&\text{if $\|{\vec{x}}\|\leq r$}\\
r\frac{{\vec{x}}}{\|{\vec{x}}\|}&\text{if $\|{\vec{x}}\|>r$.}\end{cases}

On a fixed normed space

(

(Appendix

22

4.2

-Consistency in Bounded Instances with

In the preceding section, we proved that in the Hilbert space setting,
Interp
(Algorithm
2
) obtains consistency
(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon)
while remaining competitive with respect to
Rob
. While this is a significant improvement on the limit of 3-consistency faced by switching algorithms, the question remains: can we devise an algorithm that achieves
(1+\epsilon)
-consistency and
R(\epsilon)<\infty

Rob
for
any

\epsilon>0
, in
any
normed vector space? In this section, we provide a simple sufficient condition under which this is possible: if there exists some constant

for which
\|{\textsc{Adv}}_{t}-{\textsc{Rob}}_{t}\|\leq D

, then there is a meta-algorithm that is
(1+\epsilon,{\mathcal{O}}(\frac{D}{\epsilon}))
-bicompetitive with respect to
({\textsc{Adv}},{\textsc{Rob}})
. We call this condition
D
-boundedness
of
Adv
and
Rob
; it arises naturally in a number of settings, for example in any
{\mathsf{CBC}}
instance in which the diameter of each body

is bounded by
D
.

We present the algorithm achieving this bicompetitive bound,

(line

by taking a greedy step toward

from

(line

5
Conclusion

In this work, we examine the question of integrating black-box advice into algorithms for convex function chasing using the notions of robustness and consistency from the literature on online algorithms with machine-learned advice. We first propose an algorithm that switches between the decisions of an arbitrary
C
-competitive algorithm
Rob
and the advice, showing that it obtains
(3+{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon))
-consistency and finite robustness for any
\epsilon>0
. We moreover show that this is optimal, in the sense that
no
switching algorithm can improve upon 3-consistency while maintaining finite robustness. We then move beyond switching algorithms, and propose two algorithms,
Interp
and
BdInterp
, which obtain improved robustness and consistency guarantees by exploiting the convexity inherent in the
{\mathsf{CFC}}
problem. In particular,
Interp
obtains
(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon)
-consistency and
{\mathcal{O}}(\frac{C}{\epsilon^{2}})
-robustness, and under the additional assumption of
D
-boundedness,
BdInterp
can obtain
(1+\epsilon)
-consistency and
{\mathcal{O}}(\frac{CD}{\epsilon})
-robustness. We show that
BdInterp
is nearly optimal for the problem of
{\mathsf{CFC}}
with
\alpha
-polyhedral hitting cost functions
f_{t}
, so long as
D=O(1)
in
\alpha
.

Several interesting questions remain open for future work: in particular, (a) the question of whether
(1+\epsilon)
-consistency and finite robustness can be obtained for general
{\mathsf{CFC}}
without the
D
-boundedness assumption, and (b) the question of tight lower bounds on robustness and consistency for
{\mathsf{CFC}}
and its many subclasses. Specifically, for the case of
{\mathsf{CFC}}
in general, we pose the question of whether
(1+\epsilon)
-consistency is possible together with
{\mathcal{O}}(\frac{d}{\epsilon})
-robustness, or even whether the dependence on
\epsilon
and
d
can be further improved in the robustness bound.

A.1.2

A.1.3
\alpha
-polyhedral convex function chasing

A class of functions that has been studied extensively in the literature on online optimization with switching costs (
{\mathsf{SOCO}}
) is the class of globally
\alpha
-polyhedral functions, e.g.,

Definition 14
.

A.1.4

-well-centered convex function chasing

Another class of functions that has received attention in the design of algorithms for subclasses of

A.2
Bicompetitive meta-algorithms give robust and consistent algorithms

In this section, we briefly justify the claim that if

{\mathsf{CFC}}

-robustness for the subclass.

B.4
Proof of Proposition
9

Let us first recall Theorem 2.1 of
Bubeck et al. 2019
, which characterizes the cost incurred by moving to the Steiner point of each nested body.

Theorem 16

(
(
Bubeck et al. 2019
, Theorem 2.1)
)
.

Let
{\vec{x}}_{0}=\vec{0}
and
K_{1}\subseteq B(\vec{0},r)
for some
r>0
. Then following the Steiner point of each nested body
K_{t}
incurs total movement cost no more than
rd
.

We now prove Proposition
9
.
For clarity, we abbreviate
NestedSwitch
in this proof as
NS
.

If
NS
only ever follows
Adv
, then
\epsilon\cdot{\mathrm{C}}_{{\textsc{Adv}}}<r(d+2)
and
{\mathrm{C}}_{{\textsc{NS}}}={\mathrm{C}}_{{\textsc{Adv}}}
, so
{\mathrm{C}}_{\textsc{NS}}\leq\frac{r(d+2)}{\epsilon}
. Thus
NS
is 1-competitive with respect to
Adv
and
\frac{r(d+2)}{\epsilon}
-robust, since
{\mathrm{C}}_{\textsc{Opt}}\geq 1
.

On the other hand, if
NS
only ever follows
Rob
, then
{\mathrm{C}}_{\textsc{NS}}={\mathrm{C}}_{\textsc{Rob}}
and
\epsilon\cdot{\mathrm{C}}_{\textsc{Adv}}\geq r(d+2)
. Since
Rob
just follows the Steiner point of each nested body, we have
{\mathrm{C}}_{\textsc{Rob}}\leq r+rd
, where the
rd
comes from Theorem
16
and the extra factor of
r
arises from the triangle inequality applied to the
t=1
movement:

\|{\vec{s}}_{1}-{\vec{x}}_{0}\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\|{\vec{s}}_{1}-{\vec{y}}\|_{\ell^{2}}+\|{\vec{y}}-{\vec{x}}_{0}\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\|{\vec{s}}_{1}-{\vec{y}}\|_{\ell^{2}}+r.

Thus
{\mathrm{C}}_{\textsc{NS}}={\mathrm{C}}_{\textsc{Rob}}\leq r(d+1)\leq(1+\epsilon){\mathrm{C}}_{\textsc{Adv}}
, and the desired robustness also holds.

Finally, suppose
NS
switches to
Rob
at time
t\in[T]
; i.e.,
{\textsc{NS}}_{1}={\textsc{Adv}}_{1},\ldots,{\textsc{NS}}_{t-1}={\textsc{Adv}}_{t-1},{\textsc{NS}}_{t}={\textsc{Rob}}_{t},\ldots,{\textsc{NS}}_{T}={\textsc{Rob}}_{T}
. We know that

{\mathrm{C}}_{\textsc{NS}}(1,t-1)={\mathrm{C}}_{{\textsc{Adv}}}(1,t-1)<\frac{r(d+2)}{\epsilon}

and since
K_{t}\subseteq B({\vec{y}},r)
,

{\mathrm{C}}_{\textsc{NS}}(t,t)=\|{\textsc{Rob}}_{t}-{\textsc{Adv}}_{t-1}\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq 2r

and finally

{\mathrm{C}}_{\textsc{NS}}(t+1,T)={\mathrm{C}}_{{\textsc{Rob}}}(t+1,T)\leq rd.

Thus in sum,

{\mathrm{C}}_{\textsc{NS}}\leq\frac{r(d+2)}{\epsilon}+rd+2r=\left(1+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)r(d+2).

This gives both the robustness and consistency bounds, since
\epsilon\cdot{\mathrm{C}}_{\textsc{Adv}}\geq r(d+2)
and
{\mathrm{C}}_{\textsc{Opt}}\geq 1
.
\jmlrQED

Appendix C
Background from the geometry of normed vector spaces

In this appendix, we introduce some notions and results from the literature on the geometry of normed vector spaces, expanding on the brief definitions of the rectangular constant and the radial retraction given in the main text in Section
4.1
. In the following definitions and results,
{\mathcal{X}}=(X,\|\cdot\|)
is an arbitrary real normed vector space.

We begin by defining Birkhoff-James orthogonality, which generalizes the usual Hilbert space orthogonality.

Definition 17

(
(
Birkhoff 1935
, p.169)
;
(
James 1947
, p.265)
)
.

{\vec{x}}\in X
is
Birkhoff-James orthogonal
to
{\vec{y}}\in X
, denoted
{\vec{x}}\perp{\vec{y}}
, if
\|{\vec{x}}\|\leq\|{\vec{x}}+\lambda{\vec{y}}\|
for all
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

Note that, unlike orthogonality in Hilbert spaces, Birkhoff-James orthogonality is not generally symmetric. However, it is homogeneous.

Lemma 18

(
(
James 1947
, p.265)
;
(
Joly 1969
, Remark 1)
)
.

If
{\vec{x}}\perp{\vec{y}}
, then
a{\vec{x}}\perp b{\vec{y}}
for all
a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

Using Birkhoff-James orthogonality, we can formally define define the first constant we introduced in Section
4.1
: the rectangular constant. It is motivated by the following observation: in a finite-dimensional inner product space, orthogonality of
{\vec{x}}
and
{\vec{y}}
implies that
\frac{\|{\vec{x}}\|+\|{\vec{y}}\|}{\|{\vec{x}}+{\vec{y}}\|}\leq\sqrt{2}
. In an arbitrary normed vector space, the upper bound
\sqrt{2}
is replaced with the
rectangular constant
, defined as follows using Birkhoff-James orthogonality.

Definition 19

(
(
Desbiens 1990
, Definition 2)
; original from
(
Joly 1969
, Definition 2)
)
.

The
rectangular constant

\mu({\mathcal{X}})
of a real normed vector space
{\mathcal{X}}
is defined as

\mu({\mathcal{X}})=\sup_{{\vec{x}}\perp{\vec{y}}}\frac{\|{\vec{x}}\|+\|{\vec{y}}\|}{\|{\vec{x}}+{\vec{y}}\|}.

It is known that
\sqrt{2}\leq\mu({\mathcal{X}})\leq 3

(
Joly 1969
, Section II)
, and these bounds are tight:
\mu({\mathcal{X}})=\sqrt{2}
for any Hilbert space
(
Joly 1969
, Example 1; Section III)
, and
\mu({\mathcal{X}})=3
for “nonuniformly nonsquare” spaces such as
\ell^{1}
and
\ell^{\infty}
(
Baronti et al. 2021
). Moreover,
\mu(\ell^{p})<3
for all
p\in(1,\infty)
. In fact, tighter bounds are known for the
\ell^{p}
spaces: we review these in the following theorem.

Theorem 20

(
(
Baronti et al. 2021
, Theorems 5.2, 5.4, 5.5)
)
.

For
1<p\leq 2
,

\mu(\ell^{p})\leq\min\left\{\left(1+\left(2^{1/(p-1)}-1\right)^{p-1}\right)^{1/p},\sqrt{\frac{p}{p-1}}\right\}.

For
p\geq 2
,

\mu(\ell^{p})\leq\left(1+\left(2^{p-1}-1\right)^{1/(p-1)}\right)^{(p-1)/p}.

Together, these constitute an upper bound on
\mu(\ell^{p})
that attains a (tight) minimum of
\sqrt{2}
at
p=2
, and that continuously increases toward 3 as
p\to\infty
and
p\to 1
.

We now reiterate the definition of the radial retraction and its Lipschitz constant given in Section
4.1
.

Definition 21

(
Rieffel 2006
)
.

On a normed vector space
{\mathcal{X}}=(X,\|\cdot\|)
, the radial retraction
\rho(\cdot\,;r):X\to B(\mathbf{0},r)
is the metric projection onto the closed ball of radius
r\geq 0
:

\rho({\vec{x}};r)=\begin{cases}{\vec{x}}&\text{if $\|{\vec{x}}\|\leq r$}\\
r\frac{{\vec{x}}}{\|{\vec{x}}\|}&\text{if $\|{\vec{x}}\|>r$.}\end{cases}

We define
k({\mathcal{X}})
to be the Lipschitz constant of
\rho(\cdot\,;1)
, i.e., the smallest real number satisfying

\|\rho({\vec{x}};1)-\rho({\vec{y}};1)\|\leq k({\mathcal{X}})\|{\vec{x}}-{\vec{y}}\|

for all
{\vec{x}},{\vec{y}}\in X
.

It holds that
k({\mathcal{X}})
is bounded between 1 and 2 in any normed vector space
{\mathcal{X}}
(
Thele 1974
). Moreover,
k({\mathcal{X}})
is identically the Lipschitz constant of
\rho(\cdot\,;r)
for any
r>0
(
Rieffel 2006
). To see that this is the case, observe that
\rho({\vec{x}};r)=r\cdot\rho(\frac{{\vec{x}}}{r};1)
; it then follows that

\|\rho({\vec{x}};r)-\rho({\vec{y}};r)\|=r\left\|\rho\left(\frac{{\vec{x}}}{r};1\right)-\rho\left(\frac{{\vec{y}}}{r};1\right)\right\|\leq k({\mathcal{X}})\|{\vec{x}}-{\vec{y}}\|.

Thus
k({\mathcal{X}})
is an upper bound on the Lipschitz constant of
\rho({\vec{x}};r)
for general
r>0
. Similar reasoning shows that
\rho({\vec{x}};r)
can have no smaller Lipschitz constant than
k({\mathcal{X}})
; so
k({\mathcal{X}})
is the Lipschitz constant for all
\rho({\vec{x}};r)
,
r>0
.

We conclude this section with a result relating
k({\mathcal{X}})
with
\mu({\mathcal{X}})
.

Proposition 22
.

On a real normed vector space
{\mathcal{X}}=(X,\|\cdot\|)
, it holds that
k({\mathcal{X}})\leq\mu({\mathcal{X}})
.

Proof.

(
Thele 1974
, Theorem 1)
characterizes
k({\mathcal{X}})
as follows:

k({\mathcal{X}})=\sup_{{\vec{x}}\perp{\vec{y}},{\vec{y}}\neq\vec{0},\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}}\frac{\|{\vec{y}}\|}{\|{\vec{y}}-\lambda{\vec{x}}\|}.

(13)

Since, by Lemma
18
, Birkhoff-James orthogonality is homogeneous, it is straightforward to see that (
13
) can be equivalently expressed as

k({\mathcal{X}})=\sup_{{\vec{x}}\perp{\vec{y}},{\vec{y}}\neq\vec{0}}\frac{\|{\vec{y}}\|}{\|{\vec{x}}+{\vec{y}}\|}.

Then it is clear that

k({\mathcal{X}})=\sup_{{\vec{x}}\perp{\vec{y}},{\vec{y}}\neq\vec{0}}\frac{\|{\vec{y}}\|}{\|{\vec{x}}+{\vec{y}}\|}\leq\sup_{{\vec{x}}\perp{\vec{y}}}\frac{\|{\vec{x}}\|+\|{\vec{y}}\|}{\|{\vec{x}}+{\vec{y}}\|}=\mu({\mathcal{X}}).

∎

D.1
Geometric lemmas

Before presenting the analysis of Algorithm

11

The first lemma characterizes (a modified form of) the radial retraction as a metric projection onto the boundary of a closed ball.

Lemma 23
.

Let
(X,\|\cdot\|)
be a normed vector space, and consider arbitrary
r\geq 0
,
{\vec{x}}\in X
, and
{\vec{y}}\in X\setminus\{{\vec{x}}\}
. Define
\hat{{\vec{y}}}={\vec{x}}+r\frac{{\vec{y}}-{\vec{x}}}{\|{\vec{y}}-{\vec{x}}\|}
. Then
\|{\vec{y}}-\hat{{\vec{y}}}\|\leq\|{\vec{y}}-{\vec{w}}\|
for all
{\vec{w}}\in\partial B({\vec{x}},r)
.

Proof.

It suffices to consider the case when
{\vec{x}}=\mathbf{0}
. If
r=0
,
\partial B(\vec{0},r)=\{\vec{0}\}
, so the result is clear. Otherwise, fix arbitrary
{\vec{w}}\in\partial B(\mathbf{0},r)
and observe

\displaystyle\|{\vec{y}}-{\vec{w}}\|

\displaystyle\geq|\|{\vec{y}}\|-\|{\vec{w}}\||

by the triangle inequality

\displaystyle=|\|{\vec{y}}\|-r|

\displaystyle=\left|\|{\vec{y}}\|-\|\hat{{\vec{y}}}\|\right|

\displaystyle=\|{\vec{y}}-\hat{{\vec{y}}}\|

where the last step follows from collinearity of
{\vec{y}},\hat{{\vec{y}}}
, and
\mathbf{0}
.
∎

The second lemma generalizes the following geometric fact in the Euclidean plane to an arbitrary normed vector spaces: given a triangle
{\vec{a}}{\vec{b}}{\vec{c}}
in
({\mathbb{R}}^{2},\|\cdot\|_{\ell^{2}})
, and points
{\vec{x}}\in[{\vec{a}},{\vec{b}}],{\vec{y}}\in[{\vec{a}},{\vec{c}}]
with
\|{\vec{x}}-{\vec{b}}\|_{\ell^{2}}=\|{\vec{y}}-{\vec{c}}\|_{\ell^{2}}
, it holds that
\|{\vec{x}}-{\vec{y}}\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\|{\vec{b}}-{\vec{c}}\|_{\ell^{2}}
. In the general setting, this becomes a statement about the distance between the radial retractions of a single point onto two balls of the same radius with different centers.

Lemma 24
.

Let
(X,\|\cdot\|)
be a normed vector space, and fix arbitrary
{\vec{a}},{\vec{b}},{\vec{c}}\in X
and
r\geq 0
. Define
{\vec{x}}={\vec{b}}+\rho({\vec{a}}-{\vec{b}};r)
and
{\vec{y}}={\vec{c}}+\rho({\vec{a}}-{\vec{c}};r)
. Then
\|{\vec{x}}-{\vec{y}}\|\leq\|{\vec{b}}-{\vec{c}}\|
.

Proof.

We may assume without loss of generality that
\|{\vec{a}}-{\vec{b}}\|\geq\|{\vec{a}}-{\vec{c}}\|
. If
{\vec{b}}={\vec{c}}
,
\|{\vec{x}}-{\vec{y}}\|=0=\|{\vec{b}}-{\vec{c}}\|
. Thus we restrict to the case where
{\vec{b}}\neq{\vec{c}}
and distinguish cases based on the value of
r
. We may further restrict to the case where
\|{\vec{a}}-{\vec{c}}\|>0
, as the case
{\vec{a}}={\vec{c}}
is trivial.

If
r=0
, then
{\vec{x}}={\vec{b}}
and
{\vec{y}}={\vec{c}}
. Thus
\|{\vec{x}}-{\vec{y}}\|=\|{\vec{b}}-{\vec{c}}\|
. On the other hand, if
r\geq\|{\vec{a}}-{\vec{b}}\|
, then
r\geq\|{\vec{a}}-{\vec{c}}\|
as well, so
{\vec{x}}={\vec{y}}={\vec{a}}
, and certainly
\|{\vec{x}}-{\vec{y}}\|=0\leq\|{\vec{b}}-{\vec{c}}\|
.

Next, suppose
\|{\vec{a}}-{\vec{c}}\|\leq r<\|{\vec{a}}-{\vec{b}}\|
. Then
{\vec{y}}={\vec{a}}
, so
\|{\vec{x}}-{\vec{y}}\|=\|{\vec{x}}-{\vec{a}}\|
. Moreover,
\|{\vec{x}}-{\vec{b}}\|=r\geq\|{\vec{a}}-{\vec{c}}\|
. Then by the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\|{\vec{b}}-{\vec{c}}\|

\displaystyle\geq|\|{\vec{a}}-{\vec{b}}\|-\|{\vec{a}}-{\vec{c}}\||

\displaystyle=|\|{\vec{a}}-{\vec{x}}\|+\|{\vec{x}}-{\vec{b}}\|-\|{\vec{a}}-{\vec{c}}\||

\displaystyle\geq\|{\vec{a}}-{\vec{x}}\|

\displaystyle=\|{\vec{x}}-{\vec{y}}\|.

Finally, suppose
0<r<\|{\vec{a}}-{\vec{c}}\|
, and define
\lambda=1-\frac{r}{\|{\vec{a}}-{\vec{c}}\|}
. Since
{\vec{y}}+{\vec{b}}-{\vec{c}}={\vec{b}}+r\frac{{\vec{a}}-{\vec{c}}}{\|{\vec{a}}-{\vec{c}}\|}
, we know that
{\vec{y}}+{\vec{b}}-{\vec{c}}\in\partial B({\vec{b}},r)
. Observe moreover that

{\vec{z}}\coloneqq{\vec{y}}+\lambda({\vec{b}}-{\vec{c}})={\vec{b}}+r\frac{{\vec{a}}-{\vec{b}}}{\|{\vec{a}}-{\vec{c}}\|}\in[{\vec{a}},{\vec{b}}]

and
{\vec{z}}\neq{\vec{b}}
by assumption that
r>0
. Thus
\frac{{\vec{z}}-{\vec{b}}}{\|{\vec{z}}-{\vec{b}}\|}=\frac{{\vec{a}}-{\vec{b}}}{\|{\vec{a}}-{\vec{b}}\|}
, so
{\vec{x}}={\vec{b}}+r\frac{{\vec{z}}-{\vec{b}}}{\|{\vec{z}}-{\vec{b}}\|}
. Thus:

\displaystyle\|{\vec{b}}-{\vec{c}}\|

\displaystyle=\|({\vec{y}}+{\vec{b}}-{\vec{c}})-{\vec{y}}\|

\displaystyle=\|({\vec{y}}+{\vec{b}}-{\vec{c}})-{\vec{z}}\|+\|{\vec{z}}-{\vec{y}}\|

by collinearity of
{\vec{y}},{\vec{z}},{\vec{y}}+{\vec{b}}-{\vec{c}}

\displaystyle\geq\|{\vec{x}}-{\vec{z}}\|+\|{\vec{z}}-{\vec{y}}\|

applying Lemma
23

(14)

\displaystyle\geq\|{\vec{x}}-{\vec{y}}\|

by triangle inequality.

where, in (
14
),
{\vec{x}},{\vec{y}},\hat{{\vec{y}}},{\vec{w}},
and
r
in Lemma
23
are instantiated during its invocation with this proof’s
{\vec{b}},{\vec{z}},{\vec{x}},({\vec{y}}+{\vec{b}}-{\vec{c}}),
and
r
, respectively.
∎

The next geometric lemma provides a bound on the total distance traveled first between two points on a sphere, and then from the second point to a scaled version thereof, in terms of the rectangular constant and the distance between the initial and final points.

Lemma 25
.

Let
(X,\|\cdot\|)
be a normed vector space, and let
t>1
,
r>0
, and
{\vec{x}},{\vec{y}}\in\partial B(\mathbf{0},r)
. Then

\|{\vec{y}}-{\vec{x}}\|+(t-1)\|{\vec{y}}\|\leq\mu({\mathcal{X}})\|t{\vec{y}}-{\vec{x}}\|.

Proof.

By a corollary of the Hahn-Banach theorem
(
Bollobás 1999
, Chapter 3, Corollary 7)
, there exists a
support functional

f\in X^{*}
at
{\vec{y}}
, i.e., some bounded linear functional
f:X\to{\mathbb{R}}
, with
\|f\|_{X^{*}}=1
,
f({\vec{y}})=\|{\vec{y}}\|=r
, and the property that the hyperplane
H(r)\coloneqq\{{\vec{z}}\in X:f({\vec{z}})=r\}
contains no points in
\text{int}(B(\mathbf{0},r))
. Note that we can equivalently write
H(r)
in affine subspace form
H(r)={\vec{y}}+\ker(f)=\{{\vec{z}}\in X:{\vec{z}}={\vec{y}}+{\vec{h}},{\vec{h}}\in\ker(f)\}
, by linearity of
f
. The fact that
H(r)
contains no points in the interior of
B(\mathbf{0},r)
means that
{\vec{y}}\perp{\vec{h}}
for all
{\vec{h}}\in\ker(f)
.

Define
s=f({\vec{x}})
, and note that since
\|{\vec{x}}\|=r
and
\|f\|_{X^{*}}=1
, we must have
s\leq r
. Then define
{\vec{z}}=\frac{s}{r}{\vec{y}}
, and observe
H(s)={\vec{x}}+\ker(f)={\vec{z}}+\ker(f)
. Thus
{\vec{x}}={\vec{z}}+{\vec{h}}
for some specific
{\vec{h}}\in\ker(f)
. By homogeneity of Birkhoff-James orthogonality (Lemma
18
), it follows that
(t-\frac{s}{r}){\vec{y}}\perp-{\vec{h}}
. As such,

\displaystyle\|{\vec{y}}-{\vec{x}}\|+(t-1)\|{\vec{y}}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|{\vec{y}}-{\vec{z}}\|+\|{\vec{x}}-{\vec{z}}\|+(t-1)\|{\vec{y}}\|

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{s}{r}\right)\|{\vec{y}}\|+\|{\vec{h}}\|+(t-1)\|{\vec{y}}\|

\displaystyle=\|-{\vec{h}}\|+\left(t-\frac{s}{r}\right)\|{\vec{y}}\|

\displaystyle\leq\mu({\mathcal{X}})\left\|-{\vec{h}}+\left(t-\frac{s}{r}\right){\vec{y}}\right\|

\displaystyle=\mu({\mathcal{X}})\|t{\vec{y}}-{\vec{x}}\|.

∎

Finally, we present a lemma building upon Lemma
25
that will be indispensable for the consistency analysis of Algorithm
2
.

Lemma 26
.

Let
(X,\|\cdot\|)
be a normed vector space, and fix arbitrary
r\geq 0
,
{\vec{w}},{\vec{y}}\in X
, and
{\vec{x}}\in X\setminus\mathrm{int}(B({\vec{w}},r))
. Define
\hat{{\vec{x}}}={\vec{w}}+\rho({\vec{x}}-{\vec{w}};r)
and
\hat{{\vec{y}}}={\vec{w}}+\rho({\vec{y}}-{\vec{w}};r)
. Then

\|\hat{{\vec{y}}}-\hat{{\vec{x}}}\|+\|{\vec{y}}-\hat{{\vec{y}}}\|\leq\mu({\mathcal{X}})\|{\vec{y}}-{\vec{x}}\|+\|{\vec{x}}-\hat{{\vec{x}}}\|.

Proof.

If
r=0
, then
B({\vec{w}},r)=\{{\vec{w}}\}
, so
\hat{{\vec{x}}}=\hat{{\vec{y}}}={\vec{w}}
, and the result follows from the triangle inequality, as
\mu({\mathcal{X}})\geq\sqrt{2}
. Thus we restrict to the case that
r>0
.

It suffices to consider the case where
{\vec{w}}=\mathbf{0}
. Then
\hat{{\vec{x}}}=\rho({\vec{x}};r)
and
\hat{{\vec{y}}}=\rho({\vec{y}};r)
. We distinguish two cases.

First, suppose
{\vec{y}}\in B(\mathbf{0},r)
. Then
\hat{{\vec{y}}}={\vec{y}}
, and by the triangle inequality,

\|\hat{{\vec{y}}}-\hat{{\vec{x}}}\|+\|{\vec{y}}-\hat{{\vec{y}}}\|=\|{\vec{y}}-\hat{{\vec{x}}}\|\leq\|{\vec{y}}-{\vec{x}}\|+\|{\vec{x}}-\hat{{\vec{x}}}\|\leq\mu({\mathcal{X}})\|{\vec{y}}-{\vec{x}}\|+\|{\vec{x}}-\hat{{\vec{x}}}\|.

Second, suppose
{\vec{y}}\in X\setminus B(\mathbf{0},r)
. Then
\hat{{\vec{y}}}=r\frac{{\vec{y}}}{\|{\vec{y}}\|}
. We distinguish two further subcases.

1.

Suppose that
\|{\vec{y}}-\hat{{\vec{y}}}\|\leq\|{\vec{x}}-\hat{{\vec{x}}}\|
. Then

\displaystyle\|\hat{{\vec{y}}}-\hat{{\vec{x}}}\|+\|{\vec{y}}-\hat{{\vec{y}}}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\hat{{\vec{y}}}-\hat{{\vec{x}}}\|+\|{\vec{x}}-\hat{{\vec{x}}}\|

\displaystyle\leq k({\mathcal{X}})\|{\vec{y}}-{\vec{x}}\|+\|{\vec{x}}-\hat{{\vec{x}}}\|

\displaystyle\leq\mu({\mathcal{X}})\|{\vec{y}}-{\vec{x}}\|+\|{\vec{x}}-\hat{{\vec{x}}}\|

by Proposition
22
.

2.

On the other hand, suppose that
\|{\vec{y}}-\hat{{\vec{y}}}\|>\|{\vec{x}}-\hat{{\vec{x}}}\|
, or equivalently,
\|{\vec{y}}\|\geq\|{\vec{x}}\|
, and define
{\vec{z}}=\rho({\vec{y}};\|{\vec{x}}\|)=\|{\vec{x}}\|\frac{{\vec{y}}}{\|{\vec{y}}\|}
. By collinearity,
\|{\vec{z}}-\hat{{\vec{y}}}\|=\|{\vec{x}}\|-r=\|{\vec{x}}-\hat{{\vec{x}}}\|
. Furthermore, we have that

\|\hat{{\vec{y}}}-\hat{{\vec{x}}}\|\leq\frac{\|{\vec{x}}\|}{r}\|\hat{{\vec{y}}}-\hat{{\vec{x}}}\|=\|{\vec{z}}-{\vec{x}}\|.

It then follows that

\displaystyle\|\hat{{\vec{y}}}-\hat{{\vec{x}}}\|+\|{\vec{y}}-\hat{{\vec{y}}}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|{\vec{z}}-{\vec{x}}\|+\|{\vec{y}}-{\vec{z}}\|+\|{\vec{z}}-\hat{{\vec{y}}}\|

\displaystyle=\|{\vec{z}}-{\vec{x}}\|+\left(\frac{\|{\vec{y}}\|}{\|{\vec{x}}\|}-1\right)\|{\vec{z}}\|+\|{\vec{x}}-\hat{{\vec{x}}}\|

\displaystyle\leq\mu({\mathcal{X}})\|{\vec{y}}-{\vec{x}}\|+\|{\vec{x}}-\hat{{\vec{x}}}\|

where the final inequality follows from Lemma
25
, where
{\vec{x}},{\vec{z}}\in\partial B(\vec{0},\|{\vec{x}}\|)
are (respectively) the points
{\vec{x}},{\vec{y}}
in that lemma’s statement.

∎

D.2
Proof of bicompetitive bound

We prove the bicompetitive bound of Theorem
11
in two parts: we will first show the competitive ratio with respect to
Adv
, and will follow with the competitive ratio with respect to
Rob
. Both results proceed via potential function arguments: the first uses the potential function
\|\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t}-{\vec{x}}_{t}\|
, and the second uses the potential function
c\|{\vec{x}}_{t}-{\vec{s}}_{t}\|
(with
c
to be defined later on). The robustness and consistency claim then follows immediately from the bicompetitive bound and the observation in Appendix
A.2
.

Proof of competitiveness with respect to
Adv
.
We define “phases” of the algorithm as follows: if
{\vec{x}}_{t}
is determined by line
2
of the algorithm, then the advice is in the “
Adv
” phase. Otherwise, if
{\vec{x}}_{t}
is determined by line
2
, then the advice is in the “
Rob
” phase. We refer to the time indices in which the algorithm is in the “
Rob
” phase as
R_{1},\ldots,R_{k}\in[T]
(where
k\leq T
, and
R_{1}<\cdots<R_{k}
are in increasing order). If the algorithm is never in the “
Rob
” phase, then
{\vec{x}}_{t}=\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t}

\forall t\in[T]
, and thus
Interp
is 1-competitive with respect to
Adv
. Thus we restrict to the case that there is at least one time index in which the algorithm is in the “
Rob
” phase. By design, for each

,
{\mathrm{C}}_{{\textsc{Rob}}}(1,R_{j})\leq\delta\cdot{\mathrm{C}}_{{\textsc{Adv}}}(1,R_{j})
.

Now we break into two cases depending on the phase. First, suppose that
Interp
is in the “
Adv
” phase. This means that
{\vec{x}}_{t}=\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t}
. Then

\displaystyle f_{t}({\vec{x}}_{t})+\|{\vec{x}}_{t}-{\vec{x}}_{t-1}\|+\|\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t}-{\vec{x}}_{t}\|

\displaystyle=f_{t}(\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t})+\|\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t}-{\vec{x}}_{t-1}\|

\displaystyle\leq f_{t}(\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t})+\|\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t}-\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t-1}\|+\|\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t-1}-{\vec{x}}_{t-1}\|

(15)

follows immediately from the triangle inequality.

Second, consider the case that the algorithm is in the “
Rob
” phase. This means that
{\vec{x}}_{t}
is determined by line
2
of the algorithm; and there exists some
\lambda\in[0,1]
for which
{{\vec{x}}_{t}=\lambda{\vec{s}}_{t}+(1-\lambda)\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t}}
. In this case, observe

\displaystyle f_{t}({\vec{x}}_{t})+\|{\vec{x}}_{t}-{\vec{x}}_{t-1}\|+\|\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t}-{\vec{x}}_{t}\|

\displaystyle\leq\quad

\displaystyle\lambda f_{t}({\vec{s}}_{t})+(1-\lambda)f_{t}(\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t})+2\|{\vec{x}}_{t}-{\vec{z}}_{t}\|+2\|{\vec{z}}_{t}-{\vec{y}}_{t}\|+\|{\vec{y}}_{t}-{\vec{x}}_{t-1}\|+\|\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t}-{\vec{y}}_{t}\|

\displaystyle\leq\quad

\displaystyle 2\cdot{\mathrm{C}}_{{\textsc{Rob}}}(t,t)+2\gamma\cdot{\mathrm{C}}_{{\textsc{Adv}}}(t,t)+f_{t}(\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t})+\|{\vec{y}}_{t}-{\vec{x}}_{t-1}\|+\|\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t}-{\vec{y}}_{t}\|

where (

) follows from convexity of
f_{t}
and the triangle inequality, and (
17
) follows from bounding
\|{\vec{x}}_{t}-{\vec{z}}_{t}\|
via Corollary

and
\|{\vec{z}}_{t}-{\vec{y}}_{t}\|
via line (

) of the algorithm. Invoking Corollary

\displaystyle f_{t}({\vec{x}}_{t})+\|{\vec{x}}_{t}-{\vec{x}}_{t-1}\|+\|\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t}-{\vec{x}}_{t}\|

\displaystyle\leq\quad

\displaystyle 2\cdot{\mathrm{C}}_{{\textsc{Rob}}}(t,t)+2\gamma\cdot{\mathrm{C}}_{{\textsc{Adv}}}(t,t)+f_{t}(\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t})+\mu({\mathcal{X}})\|\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t}-\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t-1}\|+\|\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t-1}-{\vec{x}}_{t-1}\|

\displaystyle\leq\quad

\displaystyle 2\cdot{\mathrm{C}}_{{\textsc{Rob}}}(t,t)+(\mu({\mathcal{X}})+2\gamma){\mathrm{C}}_{{\textsc{Adv}}}(t,t)+\|\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t-1}-{\vec{x}}_{t-1}\|

(18)

Summing (
15
) and (

) over time and noting that the left-hand side
\|\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t}-{\vec{x}}_{t}\|
and right-hand side
\|\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t-1}-{\vec{x}}_{t-1}\|

\displaystyle{\mathrm{C}}_{{\textsc{Interp}}}(1,T)

\displaystyle\leq\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}({\vec{x}}_{t})+\|{\vec{x}}_{t}-{\vec{x}}_{t-1}\|+\|\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{T}-{\vec{x}}_{T}\|

\displaystyle\leq\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{t\in\{R_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k}}2\cdot{\mathrm{C}}_{{\textsc{Rob}}}(t,t)+(\mu({\mathcal{X}})+2\gamma){\mathrm{C}}_{{\textsc{Adv}}}(t,t)+\sum_{t\in[T]\setminus\{R_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k}}{\mathrm{C}}_{{\textsc{Adv}}}(t,t)

\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot{\mathrm{C}}_{{\textsc{Rob}}}(1,R_{k})+(\mu({\mathcal{X}})+2\gamma){\mathrm{C}}_{{\textsc{Adv}}}(1,T)

\displaystyle\leq 2\delta\cdot{\mathrm{C}}_{{\textsc{Adv}}}(1,R_{k})+(\mu({\mathcal{X}})+2\gamma){\mathrm{C}}_{{\textsc{Adv}}}(1,T)

\displaystyle\leq(\mu({\mathcal{X}})+\epsilon){\mathrm{C}}_{{\textsc{Adv}}}(1,T)

where the second to last inequality follows from the assumption that the algorithm is in the “

Proof of Theorem

with respect to

Proof of competitiveness with respect to

.
We define “phases” of the algorithm as follows: if

is determined by line
3
of the algorithm, then the advice is in the “

” phase. Otherwise, if
{\vec{x}}_{t}
is determined by line
3

{\vec{x}}_{t}={\textsc{Adv}}_{t}

\forall t\in[T]
, and thus

is 1-competitive with respect to
Adv
. Thus we restrict to the case that there is at least one time index in which the algorithm is in the “
Rob
” phase. By design, for each
j\in[k]

{\mathrm{C}}_{{\textsc{Rob}}}(1,R_{j})\leq\delta\cdot{\mathrm{C}}_{{\textsc{Adv}}}(1,R_{j})

Now we break into two cases depending on the phase. First, suppose the
BdInterp
is in the “
Adv
” phase. This means that
{\vec{x}}_{t}=\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t}
. Then

\displaystyle f_{t}({\vec{x}}_{t})+\|{\vec{x}}_{t}-{\vec{x}}_{t-1}\|+\|\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t}-{\vec{x}}_{t}\|

\displaystyle=f_{t}(\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t})+\|\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t}-{\vec{x}}_{t-1}\|

\displaystyle\leq f_{t}(\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t})+\|\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t}-\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t-1}\|+\|\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t-1}-{\vec{x}}_{t-1}\|

(27)

follows immediately from the triangle inequality.

Second, consider the case that the algorithm is in the “

{\vec{x}}_{t}
is determined by line
3
of the algorithm; and there exists some
\lambda\in[0,1]

{\vec{x}}_{t}=\lambda{\vec{s}}_{t}+(1-\lambda)\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t}

\displaystyle f_{t}({\vec{x}}_{t})+\|{\vec{x}}_{t}-{\vec{x}}_{t-1}\|+\|\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t}-{\vec{x}}_{t}\|

\displaystyle\leq\quad

\displaystyle\lambda f_{t}({\vec{s}}_{t})+(1-\lambda)f_{t}(\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t})+2\|{\vec{x}}_{t}-{\vec{y}}_{t}\|+\|{\vec{y}}_{t}-{\vec{x}}_{t-1}\|+\|\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t}-{\vec{y}}_{t}\|

\displaystyle f_{t}({\vec{s}}_{t})+f_{t}(\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t})+2\gamma\cdot{\mathrm{C}}_{{\textsc{Adv}}}(t,t)+\|{\vec{y}}_{t}-{\vec{x}}_{t-1}\|+\|\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t}-{\vec{y}}_{t}\|

where (

f_{t}
and the triangle inequality, (
29

3

\displaystyle\|\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t}-{\vec{y}}_{t}\|

\displaystyle=(1-\nu)\|\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t}-{\vec{s}}_{t}\|

where the final equality follows by definition of
\nu

\displaystyle f_{t}({\vec{x}}_{t})+\|{\vec{x}}_{t}-{\vec{x}}_{t-1}\|+\|\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t}-{\vec{x}}_{t}\|

\displaystyle\leq\quad

\displaystyle 2\cdot{\mathrm{C}}_{{\textsc{Rob}}}(t,t)+(1+2\gamma){\mathrm{C}}_{{\textsc{Adv}}}(t,t)+\|\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{t-1}-{\vec{x}}_{t-1}\|

27

\displaystyle\leq\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}({\vec{x}}_{t})+\|{\vec{x}}_{t}-{\vec{x}}_{t-1}\|+\|\tilde{{\vec{x}}}_{T}-{\vec{x}}_{T}\|

\displaystyle\leq\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{t\in\{R_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k}}2\cdot{\mathrm{C}}_{{\textsc{Rob}}}(t,t)+(1+2\gamma){\mathrm{C}}_{{\textsc{Adv}}}(t,t)+\sum_{t\in[T]\setminus\{R_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k}}{\mathrm{C}}_{{\textsc{Adv}}}(t,t)

\displaystyle\leq(1+\epsilon){\mathrm{C}}_{{\textsc{Adv}}}(1,T)

where the second to last inequality follows from the assumption that the algorithm is in the “
