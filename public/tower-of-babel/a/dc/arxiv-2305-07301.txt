Aspects of the commuting graph Note: To the memory of Richard Parker
V. Arvind, Peter J. Cameron, Xuanlong Ma, Natalia V. Maslova

Abstract
The commuting graph of a group G G is the simple graph whose vertices are the elements of G G , two distinct vertices joined if they commute. Our purpose in this paper is twofold. (a) We discuss the computational problem of deciding whether a given graph is the commuting graph of a finite group; we give a quasipolynomial algorithm, and a polynomial algorithm for the case when the group is an extraspecial p p -group for p p an odd prime. (b) We give new results on the question of whether the commuting graph of a given group is a cograph or a chordal graph, two classes of graphs defined by forbidden subgraphs. The problems are not unrelated, since there are a number of cases where hard computational problems on graphs are easier when restricted to special classes of graphs; we conjecture that the recognition problem is polynomial for cographs and chordal graphs.

Let

only finitely many simple groups with even order such that they have a prescribed centralizer. In fact, in the seminal paper
[

]
, they
considered the induced subgraph of the commuting graph on the set of all non-trivial elements of the group.
Since then, the commuting graphs have received

have been well studied in the literature.
For example, Segev
et al.

[

,
67
,
68

established some longstanding conjectures on division algebras by
using some combinatorial parameters of commuting graphs.
Britnell and Gill
[

classified all finite quasi-simple groups whose commuting graph is perfect, where the vertex set of the commuting graph is the set consisting of all non-central elements.
Giudici and Parker
[

showed that

[

Morgan and Parker
[

proved that, for a finite group

.
Recently, Lewis
et al.

[
9

extended some results by Morgan and Parker
[
60

about commuting graphs. Moreover, some further applications of commuting graphs
in group theory can be found in
[

The first part of this paper is devoted to the following algorithmic
question.

Question 1

\Gamma

We give a quasi-polynomial algorithm in general, and a polynomial-time

A number of important graph classes, including perfect graphs,
cographs, chordal graphs, split graphs, and threshold graphs, can be

[
56
]

[
21

For which finite groups is the commuting graph a perfect graph, or a

Britnell and Gill
[

studied the finite groups whose commuting graph is perfect. The second and third authors

completely classified the finite groups whose commuting graph is a split graph or a threshold graph.

In Part II of the paper (Sections

11
,

12

These issues are relevant to the recognition question
(Question
1
). There are extra hypotheses which could make
the problem of recognition of commuting graphs easier and yield faster
algorithms. One is to restrict the groups to a known class, such as
nilpotent groups. Another is to restrict the graphs to a class in
which computation is easier. For example, the result of the second and
third authors
[
53
]
describes precisely the split or threshold
graphs which are commuting graphs; these are the complete graphs,
together with the graphs consisting of an
n
-vertex complete graph
(where
n
is odd), with
n
pendant edges added at one of its
vertices. Clearly these are easily recognised. We have not pursued
this issue further.

We note that some works, especially on connectedness and diameter,
define the commuting graph to have vertex set
G\setminus Z(G)
(that
is, omitting dominating vertices). This has little effect on our
results. For the recognition problem, if we are given the commuting
graph on
G\setminus Z(G)
, then the commuting graph on
G
is found
by adding
|Z(G)|
dominating vertices to the graph, and this number
is a divisor of
|G\setminus Z(G)|
so there are not many
possibilities. In the second part of the paper too, as mentioned
before Proposition
10.2
, we can define the commuting graph
without including the centre
Z(G)
and the results essentially remain
the same.

We conclude the introduction with a suggestion for further research,
which would link the two parts of the paper together more closely.

Part I
Recognising the commuting graph

3
The Recognition Problem

The main focus of this part is the
recognition problem
of
commuting graphs. It is basically an algorithmic question. Given an
undirected graph
X=(V,E)
as input, the aim is to design an algorithm
that efficiently checks if there is a group
G
with
|V|
elements
such that the input graph
X
is isomorphic to
\Gamma(G)
, and if so
to efficiently determine such an isomorphism.

We summarize our results.

(a)

We obtain a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm for the case
of extraspecial
p
-groups for odd primes
p
. Extraspecial

p
-groups are a special case of
p
-groups of nilpotence class
2
.
A formal definition is in Section
7
.

(b)

For the general case, we obtain a quasipolynomial-time
algorithm. The algorithm is essentially based on a simple

2^{O(\log^{3}n)}
time algorithm that enumerates the multiplication
tables of all distinct groups of order
n
(where by distinct we
mean mutually non-isomorphic groups of order
n
). This enumeration
algorithm combined with Babai’s quasipolynomial time algorithm for
graph isomorphism
[
4
]
yields a simple
2^{O(\log^{3}n)}
time
algorithm to check if a given
n
-vertex graph
X
is the commuting
graph of a group of order
n
: for each
n
-element group
G
that
is output by the enumeration algorithm we check if
\Gamma(G)
is
isomorphic to
X
by using Babai’s isomorphism test
[
4
]
.

(c)

A natural question in connection with our algorithm for recognizing
the commuting graphs of extraspecial groups is whether groups with the
same commuting graphs are isoclinic. This holds for extraspecial
groups which is exploited by the algorithm, and it is natural to
conjecture that this property holds for all groups of nilpotence class
2 (of which extraspecial groups are a subclass). We present
counter-examples for class-3 nilpotent groups and conjecture that the
property holds for class-2 nilpotent groups.

(d)

Additionally, we also show an efficient reduction of the
commuting graph recognition problem to the recognition problem of
commuting graphs of indecomposable groups. We also note recognizing
the commuting graph of generalized dihedral groups along with an extension
to Frobenius groups.

that shows the following: every

-vertex graph

with at least two vertices of degree

commuting graph of a semigroup. It is easy to see that their
construction actually gives a polynomial-time algorithm for finding a
semigroup with
n
elements and a bijection from it to the vertex set
of
X
such that the edges of
X
realize the commuting relation of
the semigroup. It is a nearly complete answer to the question in the
semigroups setting.

Solomon and Woldar
[
71
]
have shown that the commuting graph

\Gamma(G)
of a finite simple group
G
uniquely determines
the group. More precisely, they have shown that for any finite group

H
and any finite simple group

, their commuting graphs

\Gamma(H)
and
\Gamma(G)
are isomorphic if and only if the groups

G
and

are isomorphic. An interesting question is whether or not
the proof of this result
[
71
]
yields a polynomial-time
algorithm for checking if a given graph

is the commuting graph of
a simple group.

4
Basic properties

We being with some preliminary observations that are well-known in the
literature (see, e.g., the survey
[
21
]
).

Let
G
be a finite group. What are the cliques of
\Gamma(G)
? If a
vertex subset
S
induces a clique in
\Gamma(G)
then
S
is a
commuting subset of elements of
G
. Conversely, every commuting
subset of elements of
G
forms a clique in
\Gamma(G)
. Which cliques
of
\Gamma(G)
correspond to subgroups of
G
? Although we cannot
directly infer the group multiplication from
\Gamma(G)
, we can
observe the following.

Lemma 4.1

Let
G
be a finite group and
\Gamma(G)
its commuting graph. A
vertex subset
S
is a
maximal
clique of
\Gamma(G)
if and only if
S

is a maximal abelian subgroup of
G
.

Proof 3

Suppose
H
is a maximal abelian subgroup of
G
. Clearly,
H
is a
clique in
X
. If
x\notin H
is adjacent to all of
H
then
x

commutes with all of
H
implying that
\langle H\cup\{x\}\rangle
is an
abelian subgroup of
G
strictly larger than
H
. Hence the clique
induced by
H
is maximal.

Conversely, suppose
S\subset G
is a maximal clique in
\Gamma(G)
.
Then any two elements
x,y\in S
commute with each other. Hence, the
subgroup
H=\langle S\rangle
generated by
S
is an abelian subgroup of

G
which means that the vertex subset
H
is a clique in

\Gamma(G)
containing
S
. As
S
is maximal, by assumption, it
follows that
S=H
which means
S
is a maximal abelian subgroup. ∎

4.1
The vertex degrees of
\Gamma(G)
and conjugacy classes of
G

Let
X=(G,E)
be the commuting graph of a finite group
G
. Let
x^{G}

denote the
conjugacy class
of
x\in G
:

x^{G}=\{g^{-1}xg\mid g\in G\},

which is the orbit of
x
under the conjugation action of
G
on
itself. Let
\deg(v)
denote the degree of a node
v
in the graph

X
.
The orbit-stabilizer lemma
[
19
]
directly implies the following.

Proposition 4.2

For each
x\in G
its centralizer
C_{G}(x)
is the closed neighborhood

\bar{N}(x)
of
x
in
\Gamma(G)
, and

|C_{G}(x)|=1+\deg(x)={\frac{|G|}{|x^{G}|}},\textrm{ for all }x\in G.

Let
n=|G|
, let
m
denote the number of edges in the commuting graph
\Gamma(G)
,
and let
k
denote the number of conjugacy classes. As
\sum_{x\in G}\deg(x)=2m
, we have:

2m+n=\sum_{x\in G}{\frac{|G|}{|x^{G}|}}=|G|\cdot\sum_{x\in G}{\frac{1}{|x^{G}|}}=n\cdot k.

(6)

Thus the number of conjugacy classes of
G
is
k=(2m+n)/n
, which, by
the above equation, can be inferred from the commuting graph. Thus,
for example, the only regular commuting graphs are the complete
graphs, which are the commuting graphs of abelian groups.

Let
f(n)
denote the minimum number of conjugacy classes in a group
of order
n
. The function
f(n)
is quite well
studied. Landau
[
52
]
showed that
f(n)\to\infty
as

n\to\infty
. The first lower bound was obtained by
Brauer
[
11
]
and Erdős and Turán
[
34
]
, who showed
that
f(n)\geq\log\log n
(logarithms to base
2
). This was improved
to
\epsilon\log n/(\log\log n)^{8}
by Pyber
[
63
]
. The exponent

8
in the previous expression was reduced to
7
by Keller

[
49
]
, and then to
3+\epsilon
by Baumeister,
Maróti and Tong-Viet
[
8
]
, for any
\epsilon>0
. It is
conjectured that a bound of the form
f(n)\geq C\log n
holds for some
constant
C
.

This is relevant to the recognition problem because of the following
observation, which follows immediately from equation (
6
).

Proposition 4.3

An
n
-vertex graph
X
with fewer than
\frac{n(f(n)-1)}{2}
edges
cannot be the commuting graph of a group, where
f(n)
is the
minimum number of conjugacy classes that a group of order
n
can have.

At the other extreme, we can rule out very dense incomplete graphs by
the
5/8
-theorem
[
43
]
for finite groups. More precisely, for
any finite non-abelian group
G
with
n
elements:

|C|=|\{(x,y)\mid x,y\in G\textrm{ and }xy=yx\}|\leq\frac{5}{8}\cdot n^{2},

where
C\subset G\times G
denotes the set of ordered pairs
(x,y)
of
commuting elements. Then, in the commuting graph
\Gamma(G)=(G,E)

the number of edges

|E|=\frac{|C|-n}{2}\leq\frac{5}{16}\cdot n^{2}-\frac{n}{2}\leq\frac{5}{8}\cdot{n\choose 2}.

Putting it together, we have:

Proposition 4.4

An incomplete graph
X=(V,E)
cannot be the commuting graph of a group
if
|E|>\frac{5}{8}\cdot{|V|\choose 2}
.

4.2
The commuting graph and maximal abelian subgroups

For a finite group
G
, let
\mathcal{M}\subseteq 2^{G}
denote the set of all

maximal abelian subgroups
of
G
. Associated with
G
is the
natural
hypergraph

(G,\mathcal{M})
, where the hyperedges are precisely
the maximal abelian subgroups of
G
.

Proposition 4.5

The commuting graph of a finite group determines the hypergraph of
its maximal abelian subgroups, and, conversely the maximal abelian
subgroups hypergraph of the group determines its commuting graph.

Proof 4

Let
G
be a finite group. The maximal abelian subgroups are the maximal
cliques in
\Gamma(G)
; and the edges of
\Gamma(G)
are the
2
-element
subsets of maximal abelian subgroups.∎

Remark 1

Since the commuting graph
\Gamma(G)
of a finite simple group
G

uniquely determines the group
[
71
]
, by Proposition
4.5

it follows that the set of maximal abelian groups of a finite simple
group
G
uniquely determines
G
.

For a finite group
G
, the number of maximal abelian subgroups is
bounded by
{|G|}\choose{\log|G|}
because every subgroup of
G
has
a generating set of size bounded by
\log|G|
.

This simple bound is tight apart from a constant in the exponent. To
see this, consider the extraspecial group
G
of order
p^{2n+1}
and
exponent
p
, where
p
is an odd prime. The centre has order
p
, and

G/Z(G)
is isomorphic to a
2n
-dimensional vector space
V
over the
field
F
of
p
elements, with the bilinear form from
V\times V
to

F
corresponding to the commutation map from
G/Z(G)\times G/Z(G)
to

Z(G)
. Maximal abelian subgroups contain the centre, and correspond
to maximal totally isotropic subspaces of
V
. It is known that the
number of such subspaces is
\prod_{i=1}^{n}(p^{i}+1)
(see

[
75
]
). As

\prod_{i=1}^{n}(p^{i}+1)>p^{n(n+1)/2}>p^{n(2n+1)/4}=|G|^{n/4},

it follows that the number of maximal abelian subgroups of the
extraspecial
p
-group

is
|G|^{\Omega(\log|G|)}
for any odd
prime
p
.

5
Commuting graphs of product groups

A natural approach to the problem of recognizing commuting graphs is
to examine the connection between product decompositions of the
commuting graph
\Gamma(G)
and the direct product decomposition of
the group
G
.

Let
G
and
H
be finite groups. Consider the commuting graph

\Gamma(G\times H)
of their direct product. We recall the
definition of strong products of graphs.

Definition 1

[
44
, Chapter 4, Page 35]

Let
X=(V,E)
and
X^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
be simple undirected graphs. Their

strong product
, denoted
X\boxtimes X^{\prime}
, is a simple undirected
graph with the cartesian product
V\times V^{\prime}
as its vertex set and
edges defined as follows: distinct pairs
(u,u^{\prime})
and
(v,v^{\prime})
are
adjacent if and only if one of the following holds:

(a)

u=v
and
(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in E^{\prime}
,

(b)

u^{\prime}=v^{\prime}
and
(u,v)\in E
,

(c)

(u,v)\in E
and
(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in E^{\prime}
.

The following proposition is immediate from the definition.

Proposition 5.1

For finite groups
G
and
H

\Gamma(G\times H)=\Gamma(G)\boxtimes\Gamma(H).

The strong product is both
commutative
and
associative

[
44
, Chapter 4]
, in the sense that
X\boxtimes Y\cong Y\boxtimes X
and
(X\boxtimes Y)\boxtimes Z\cong X\boxtimes(Y\boxtimes Z)
.

By a
non-trivial graph
we mean a graph with more than one
vertex. Given a graph
X
, a
factorization

X\cong Y\boxtimes Z
is
non-trivial
if both
Y
and
Z
are non-trivial. A graph

X
is said to be a
prime graph
if it does not have a
non-trivial factorization. (We warn the reader that this term is also used
for graphs defined by arithmetic parameters of a finite group; see for
example
[
55
]
.)

It turns out that any connected graph
X
has a unique factorization
(up to isomorphism) into a strong product of prime graphs
[
44
, Theorem
7.14, Pg 85]
. Moreover, this prime factorization can be
computed in polynomial time
[
35
]
. As a consequence, we
obtain the following polynomial-time reduction.

Recall the a group
G
is said to be
indecomposable
if it
cannot be expressed as the direct product of two non-trivial groups.

Theorem 5.2

The problem of recognizing the commuting graphs of groups is
polynomial-time reducible to the problem of recognizing the
commuting graphs of indecomposable groups.

Proof 5

Suppose we have an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
for recognizing the
commuting graphs of indecomposable groups. Using
\mathcal{A}
as
subroutine, we present a polynomial-time algorithm for recognizing
the commuting graphs of all finite groups.

Let
X=(V,E)
be an undirected graph on
n
vertices which is a
purported commuting graph.

First, using the polynomial-time algorithm of Feigenbaum and
Schäffer
[
35
]
we can factorize
X
as

X=X_{1}\boxtimes X_{2}\boxtimes\cdots\boxtimes X_{k},

where each
X_{i}
is a prime graph on at least two vertices. It
follows that
k\leq\log n
. Now, for each subset
S\subseteq[k]

of the prime graphs we combine them by taking the strong
direct product to define the graph

X_{S}=\boxtimes_{i\in S}X_{i}.

Notice that any order in which the strong product of these graphs

X_{i}
is computed yields the same graph, up to isomorphism.

Thus, in time which is polynomial in
n
, we have computed graphs
X_{S}
for
each subset
S
of
[k]
. Now, we invoke the subroutine

\mathcal{A}
that checks if
X_{S}
is the commuting graph of an
indecomposable group
G_{S}
, and if so, finds a labeling of the
vertices of
X_{S}
with elements of
G_{S}
consistent with the
commuting relations.

We can now check if the input graph
X
is the commuting graph of a
group with a straightforward dynamic programming strategy based on
the following claim which directly follows from
Proposition
5.1
.

Claim 5.3

For any two disjoint subsets
S,S^{\prime}
of
[k]
such that
X_{S}
and

X_{S^{\prime}}
are the commuting graphs of groups
G_{S}
and
G_{S^{\prime}}
the
graph
X_{S}\boxtimes X_{S^{\prime}}
is the commuting graph of the direct
product group
G_{S}\times G_{S^{\prime}}
.

Now, the algorithm works in stages, computing subsets
S
of
[k]

along with the graph
X_{S}
and group
G_{S}
such that

X_{S}=\Gamma(G_{S})
.

(a)

for
stages
0

to

k

do

(b)

Stage
0
we have subsets
S
such that
G_{S}
is an
indecomposable group. We mark all such subsets
S
as true. We mark
the remaining subsets as false.

(c)

Stage
i+1
For each pair of disjoint subsets
S
and

S^{\prime}
marked true in Stages
1,2,\ldots,i
, such that
S\cup S^{\prime}
is
marked false, we mark
S\cup S^{\prime}
true and compute
X_{S\cup S^{\prime}}=X_{S}\boxtimes X_{S^{\prime}}
and
G_{S\cup S^{\prime}}=G_{S}\times G_{S^{\prime}}
.

(d)

end-for

(e)

If
[k]
is marked true then the input
X
is the commuting
graph of the group
G_{[k]}
computed above.

The above description checks if
X
is the commuting graph of a group
with at most
2^{k}\leq n
calls to the subroutine
\mathcal{A}
and the
running time of the remaining computation is clearly polynomially
bounded in
n
.∎

From Theorem
5.2
we can easily deduce from the
Solomon–Woldar theorem
[
71
]
that direct products of simple
groups too have uniquely determined commuting graphs.

Corollary 5.4

If
G
is a direct product of simple groups, then
G
is uniquely
determined by its commuting graph
\Gamma(G)
.

We do not know if the commuting graph of an indecomposable group
G

is necessarily a prime graph. However, if
G
is simple then it turns
out, as we show below, that
\Gamma(G)
is a prime graph.

Lemma 5.5

The commuting graph of a finite simple group is a prime graph under the
strong product.

Proof 6

We use the fact that, if
G
is a non-abelian simple group and

g\in G\setminus\{1\}
, then there exists
h\in G
such that

\langle g,h\rangle=G

[
42
]
. Now if
\langle g,h\rangle=G
, then

(a)

g
and
h
are nonadjacent in the commuting graph (since
G

is non-abelian);

(b)

g
and
h
have no non-identity common neighbour in the commuting
graph (since a common neighbour would belong to
Z(G)
, but
Z(G)=\{1\}
.

Now suppose for a contradiction that
\Gamma(G)
is the strong product
of two non-trivial graphs with vertex sets
A
and
B
(i.e., with

|A|>1
and
|B|>1
). Then we can identify
G
, as the vertex set of

\Gamma(G)
, with the Cartesian product
A\times B
. Suppose that

(a,b)
is the identity of
G
. Then
(a,b)
is joined to all other
vertices in the commuting graph.

It follows that
a
is joined to every other vertex in
A
, and
b
to
every other vertex in
B
; therefore, for all
x\in A\setminus\{a\}
,

y\in B\setminus\{b\}
, the three vertices
(a,y)
,
(x,b)
and

(x,y)
are adjacent to each other in the commuting graph
\Gamma(G)
.

Choose
y\in B\setminus\{b\}
, and suppose that

\langle(a,y),(u,v)\rangle=G
. Now

(a)

if
u=a
then
(a,y)
and
(a,v)
are both joined to
(x,b)

for any
x\in A\setminus\{a\}
;

(b)

if
v=b
then
(a,y)
and
(u,b)
are joined;

(c)

if neither of the above, then
(a,y)
and
(u,v)
are both
joined to
(u,b)
.

Each case is contradictory; so our assumption that
\Gamma(G)
is the
strong product of two non-trivial graphs is false, and the theorem is
proved.∎

Remark 2

The proof of Lemma
5.5
only requires that

Z(G)=1
and that any non-identity element is contained in a

2
-element generating set. These assumptions are valid in any
almost simple group
G
with simple normal subgroup
S
such that

G/S
is cyclic
[
17
]
.

As a consequence of Lemma
5.5
, we obtain the
following variant of Theorem
5.2
.

Theorem 5.6

The problem of recognizing the commuting graph of a direct product
of simple groups is polynomial-time reducible to the problem of
recognizing the commuting graph of a simple group.

The algorithm for the reduction in the above theorem is much simpler
and more efficient than in Theorem
5.2
. For we only need to
consider the prime factors
X_{i}
, in the factorization

X=\boxtimes_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}
, and not all the strong products
X_{S}
for
arbitrary subsets
S\subseteq[k]
.

6
Commuting graphs of semidirect products

In this section we explore whether we can recognize the commuting
graphs of semidirect products
G=H\rtimes K
if
H
and
K
are both
from group classes whose commuting graphs are easily recognizable.

For example, consider the commuting graph of the dihedral group

D_{2n}
. Let
D_{2n}=\langle a,b\rangle
where
a^{n}=b^{2}=1
and

bab=a^{-1}
. For
n
odd,
1
is the only dominating vertex, there is
an
n
-clique corresponding to
\langle a\rangle
and the
ba^{i}
correspond
to pendant vertices. Thus, the commuting graph of
D_{2n}
is easily
recognizable. For
n
even, it is a bit different with
b^{n/2}

being the other dominating vertex.

More generally, consider the generalized dihedral group
\langle A,t\rangle

defined earlier, where
A
is abelian of order
2n
,
n
odd. Its
commuting graph is isomorphic to that of the dihedral group
D_{2n}

of the same order. Hence, as observed above, we can recognize that a
graph is the commuting graph of a generalized dihedral group, but
cannot decide which one.

Seeking a generalization of this example we consider Frobenius groups.

6.1
Commuting graphs of Frobenius groups

In this subsection we demonstrate that we can recognise from its
commuting graph that a group
G
is a Frobenius group. These groups
were defined earlier. Let
G
be a Frobenius group with kernel
N

and complement
H
.

Thompson
[
76
]
proved that the Frobenius kernel is
nilpotent, and Zassenhaus
[
80
]
worked out the detailed
structure of a Frobenius complement. Everything we need about
Frobenius groups is explained in standard group theory texts

[
61
,
45
,
69
]
.

In the commuting graph of a group
G
, the identity is a dominating
vertex (that is, joined to all others); indeed, any vertex in the centre
is dominating, so if
Z(G)
is non-trivial then the commuting graph is

2
-connected.

Lemma 6.1

(a)

A Frobenius kernel has non-trivial centre.

(b)

A Frobenius complement has non-trivial centre.

Proof 7

(a) By Thompson’s theorem
[
76
, Theorem 1]
, a Frobenius
kernel is nilpotent, and nilpotent groups have a non-trivial centre.

(b) Let
H
be a Frobenius complement.
By
[
45
, Theorem 6.3, Corollary 6.10]
,
if
H
has even order, then it has a unique element of order
2
, which
lies in the centre; while, if
H
has odd order, then it has a unique
subgroup of order
p
for every
p
dividing
|H|
. In the latter case,
choose
p
to be the smallest prime divisor of
H
, and
P=\langle a\rangle
the
unique subgroup of order
p
. Then
P
is a normal subgroup of
H
. For
any
h\in H
, we have
a^{h}=a^{m}
for some
m
with
1\leq m\leq p-1
. If

m>1
, let
q
be a prime divisor of
m
; then some power of
h
has order

q
, a contradiction. So
a^{h}=a
for all
h\in H
, whence
a\in Z(H)
.∎

Theorem 6.2

Let
G
be a group of order
nk
, where
n>1
,
k>1
, and
n
and
k
are
relatively prime. Then
G
is a Frobenius group with Frobenius
complement of order
k
if and only if its commuting graph

\Gamma(G)
satisfies the following conditions:

(a)

there is a unique dominating vertex
1
in
\Gamma(G)
,
where
1
corresponds to the identity element of
G
.

(b)

\Gamma(G)\setminus\{1\}
has a component of size
n-1
and

n
components of size
k-1
, and each component has a dominating
vertex.

Proof 8

Suppose that
G
is a Frobenius group with kernel
N
of order
n
and
complement
H
of order
k
. The identity is a dominating vertex. Also
non-identity vertices in the kernel do not commute with non-identity
vertices in a complement, and non-identity vertices in different
complements do not commute with each other. Thus the components of

\Gamma(G)\setminus\{1\}
are as stated, and Lemma
6.1

shows that they have dominating vertices.

Conversely, suppose that the commuting graph
\Gamma(G)
has
properties (a) and (b). If
C
is a component with a dominating vertex

c
, then the centralizer
C_{G}(c)
is equal to
C\cup\{1\}
, which is
thus a subgroup of
G
. Let
N
be the subgroup containing the
component of size
n-1
, and let
H_{1},\ldots,H_{n}
be the subgroups
containing the other components. Note that
|N|=n
and
|H_{i}|=k,1\leq i\leq n
.

Since
\Gamma(G)
is invariant under automorphisms of
G
, we have an
action of
G
on the set
\Omega
of components of

\Gamma(G)\setminus\{1\}
of size
k-1
by conjugation. We show that
this action satisfies the conditions for a Frobenius group given
above.

Choose a prime
p
dividing
k
. Since
n
and
k
are relatively
prime,
p
does not divide
n
. So the subgroup
N
cannot contain a
Sylow
p
-subgroup; but each of
H_{1},\ldots,H_{n}
contains such a
subgroup. By the conjugacy part of Sylow’s theorem,
G
acts
transitively on
\Omega
.

For
1\leq i\leq n
, each element of
H_{i}\setminus\{1\}
acting by
conjugation, fixes itself and so fixes the component induced by

H_{i}\setminus\{1\}
. That is, a non-identity element of

G\setminus N
, acting by conjugation, fixes itself, and so fixes the
component containing it. We must show that it fixes no other
component. To that end, we count the fixed points of elements of
G

acting on
\Omega
. The identity fixes
n
; each non-identity element
of
N
fixes some non-negative number; and each of the remaining
elements
G\setminus N
fix a positive number of elements. So the sum
of the fixed point numbers is at least
n+n(k-1)=nk
. But, by the
Orbit-counting Lemma, this sum is equal to
|G|=nk
, since
G
is
transitive. Consequently, non-identity elements of
N
do not fix any
point of
\Omega
, and the remaining non-identity elements fix exactly
one point each. So
G
is a Frobenius group.∎

Suppose
X
is a graph given as input such that
X=\Gamma(G)
for some
group
G
. Then Theorem
6.2
gives us a polynomial-time test
for checking if the group
G
is a Frobenius group.

Remark 3

Above we assumed that our graph is a commuting graph. However, it is easy
to construct a graph satisfying the conditions of Theorem
6.2

which is not a commuting graph. Can we find necessary and sufficient
conditions for
\Gamma
to be the commuting graph of a Frobenius group?

7
Recognizing commuting graphs of extraspecial groups

In this section we design a polynomial-time algorithm that takes a
simple undirected graph

, with

Remark 4

We will describe an elementary self-contained polynomial-time
algorithm for recognising commuting graphs of extraspecial
groups. We have an alternative algorithm, based on the
Buekenhout-Shult theorem
[
16
]
classifying certain point-line
geometries, that exploits the finite geometry structure of the
quotient group

. However, we present the former in the
interest of keeping the proof entirely self-contained in the
context of this paper.

Lemma 7.1

Given as input the multiplication table of an extraspecial group
G

of order
p^{2r+1}
, in time polynomial in

we can
construct its associated symplectic form
\langle,\rangle
, as an explicit
bilinear map from
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2r}\times\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2r}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}
.

Proof 9

Let
n=p^{2r+1}
. First, we compute the centre
Z=\langle z\rangle
of
G

from the multiplication table. In order to compute
G/Z
we can find
its multiplication table from the given multiplication table of
G
.
We can find a decomposition of
G/Z
as the direct product of
2r

cyclic groups of order
p
by finding
independent generators

for
G/Z
using a simple algorithm. Suppose

g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{j}\in G/Z
are independent (i.e. the identity can
be expressed as a product of their powers only if the exponents are
all zero). If

then the subgroup

H_{j}=\langle g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{j}\rangle
which is of size

can be
listed in time polynomial in

. We can pick
g_{j+1}
as any
element in

and continue the process until we pick

2r
such independent generators which will generate
G/Z
. That
gives us an efficient identification of
G/Z
with
V(2r,p)
. For

xZ,yZ\in G/Z
we will have corresponding vectors

. If the commutator
[x,y]=z^{i_{x,y}}
then we
can define the symplectic form as
\langle v_{x},v_{y}\rangle=i_{x,y}
, where

i_{x,y}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
. This completes the proof. ∎

of the symplectic space

.

In particular, let

and

denote the

of the extraspecial groups

,

Lemma 7.2

(a)

The commuting graphs
\Gamma(G_{1})
and
\Gamma(G_{2})

are isomorphic.

(b)

The associated symplectic graphs

and

are isomorphic.

Proof 10

Recall, as defined at the beginning of this section, that both

G_{1}=\langle x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{r},y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{r},z\rangle
and

G_{2}=\langle x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{r},y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{r},z\rangle
. The commuting
relations between the generators are identical. The only difference

G_{2}
each

is of order

, and in

they are
all order

.

Because of the commutation relation

in both groups,
where

is the centre, each element in both groups
can be expressed as products of the form

where

for all

. Suppose now we have two
elements
x=\prod_{i=1}^{r}x_{i}^{e_{i}}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{r}y_{i}^{f_{i}}\cdot z^{c}
and
y=\prod_{i=1}^{r}x_{i}^{e^{\prime}_{i}}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{r}y_{i}^{f^{\prime}_{i}}\cdot z^{c^{\prime}}
. Then, using the commutation
relation
[x_{i},y_{i}]=z
, which holds in both
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, we can
check that
x
and

commute if and only if

\sum_{i=1}^{r}e_{i}f^{\prime}_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}f_{i}e^{\prime}_{i}(\Mod p)
. It follows that

(x,y)
is an edge in
\Gamma(G_{1})
if and only if it is an edge in

\Gamma(G_{2})
. Hence the commuting graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are isomorphic.

Let
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
denote the symplectic spaces corresponding to
the groups
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, respectively. For
G\in\{G_{1},G_{2}\}
, as
already observed

is isomorphic to
V(2r,p)
(as an additive
group). Let
x_{i}Z\mapsto\bar{x}_{i}
and
y_{i}Z\mapsto\bar{y}_{i}
,

1\leq i\leq r
, under this isomorphism, for
\bar{x}_{i},\bar{y}_{i}\in V(2r,p),1\leq i\leq r
. Then,
xZ
for

x=\prod_{i=1}^{r}x_{i}^{e_{i}}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{r}y_{i}^{f_{i}}\cdot z^{c}

maps to
\bar{x}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}(e_{i}\bar{x}_{i}+f_{i}\bar{y}_{i})
. Likewise,

y=\prod_{i=1}^{r}x_{i}^{e^{\prime}_{i}}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{r}y_{i}^{f^{\prime}_{i}}\cdot z^{c^{\prime}}

maps to

\bar{y}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}(e^{\prime}_{i}\bar{x}_{i}+f^{\prime}_{i}\bar{y}_{i})
. Therefore, we can
evaluate
\langle\bar{x},\bar{y}\rangle
using the fact that

\langle\bar{x}_{i},\bar{x_{j}}\rangle=0
,
\langle\bar{y}_{i},\bar{y_{j}}\rangle=0
, for
all

and for all

we have
\langle\bar{x}_{i},\bar{y}_{i}\rangle=1

and
\langle\bar{y}_{i},\bar{x_{i}}\rangle=-1
.

\langle\bar{x},\bar{y}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}(e_{i}f^{\prime}_{i}-e^{\prime}_{i}f_{i})

It follows that the vertices
\bar{x}
and
\bar{y}
are adjacent in
the symplectic graph

if and only if they are adjacent in

. ∎

Thus, the equivalence classes defined by the closed-twins equivalence

Claim 7.5

For any two order

subgroups

and

.

7.1

We now show that the symplectic graph of a non-degenerate symplectic

on a vertex set

of

be a graph with

be recursive, with correctness proof based on an inductive

(b)

(c)

as any

and

from the set

\{\alpha\bar{u}\mid\alpha\in\mathbb{F}^{*}_{p}\}
of
p-1
vectors, for some
non-zero vector
\bar{u}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Span}}(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{y}_{1})
.

Proof of Claim. It is clear from the definition of
V_{12}
that the
vertices in it must be labeled by nonzero vectors in

\mathop{\mathrm{Span}}(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{y}_{1})
. The proof follows easily by observing
that a non-zero vector
\alpha\bar{x}_{1}+\beta\bar{y}_{1}
is
orthogonal only to itself and its scalar multiples and to no other
vectors in
\mathop{\mathrm{Span}}(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{y}_{1})
.

By the above claim, we can consistently label each vertex
u\in V_{12}
by a vector
\bar{u}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Span}}(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{y}_{1})
assuming

X
is a symplectic graph.

Claim 7.10

If
X=(V,E)
is the symplectic graph of the symplectic space

V(2r,p)
and
V^{\prime}
corresponds to the symplectic subspace

V(2r-2,p)
spanned by
\{\bar{x}_{i},\bar{y}_{i}\mid 2\leq i\leq r\}

then for any vertex
u\in V_{12}
the vertex subset

V[u]=\{v\in V\setminus V^{\prime}\mid(v,u)\in E\}

has to be labeled by the vectors

\{\alpha\bar{u}+\bar{v}\mid\alpha\neq 0,\bar{v}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Span}}(\cup_{i=2}^{r}\{\bar{x}_{i},\bar{y}_{i}\})\}

of
V(2r,p)
.

Proof of Claim. Suppose
v\in V\setminus V^{\prime}
is labeled by the vector

\bar{a}+\bar{b}
, where
\bar{a}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Span}}(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{y}_{1})
and

\bar{b}\in V(2r-2,p)
. As
v\in V[u]
we have

\langle\bar{a}+\bar{b},\bar{u}\rangle=0.

As
\langle\bar{b},\bar{u}\rangle=0
it implies that

\langle\bar{a},\bar{u}\rangle=0
which means
\bar{a}=\alpha\bar{u}
for some non-zero scalar
\alpha
. Conversely, any
vertex labeled by
\alpha\bar{u}+\bar{b}
for
\bar{b}\in V(2r-2,p)
has to lie in
V[u]
if the labeling is consistent with
the symplectic graph
X
.

It follows that
|V[u]|=(p-1)\cdot p^{2r-2}
. Furthermore, if vertices

u
and
v
are in the same
(p-1)
-clique in the graph
X_{12}
then

V[u]=V[v]
. As

(p+1)(p-1)\cdot p^{2r-2}=p^{2r}-p^{2r-2}=|V\setminus V^{\prime}|
, it follows
that the different
V[u]
are all pairwise disjoint and form a
partition of
V\setminus V^{\prime}
. Consequently, we can identify the
p+1

many equisized vertex subsets
V[u]
which form a partition of

V\setminus V^{\prime}
.

Labelling vertices in
V[u]

By Claim
7.10
, the vertices of
V[u]
have to be labeled by
vectors from
\{\alpha\bar{u}+\bar{v}\mid v\in V^{\prime},\alpha\neq 0\}
. We
will examine the adjacencies in the subgraph induced by
V[u]
and
also the adjacencies across the different
V[u]
and
V[u^{\prime}]
to
determine a consistent labeling. Let
X[u]
denote the subgraph of
X

induced by
V[u]
.

For distinct vertices
v_{1},v_{2}\in V^{\prime}
notice that

\langle\bar{v}_{1},\bar{v}_{2}\rangle=\langle\alpha\bar{u}+\bar{v}_{1},\beta\bar{u}+\bar{v}_{2}\rangle.

(7)

Thus, in any consistent labeling of
V[u]
suppose

S_{u,v_{i}}\subset V[u]
are the
p-1
vertices labeled by

\{\alpha\bar{u}+\bar{v}_{i}\mid\alpha\neq 0\}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
. Then
Equation
7
implies that in the subgraph
X[u]
the
bipartite graph induced by the subsets
S_{u,v_{1}}
and
S_{u,v_{2}}

is either complete or empty.

Furthermore,
\langle\alpha\bar{u}+\bar{v},\beta\bar{u}+\bar{v}\rangle=0

for all non-zero
\alpha,\beta
. Hence each
S_{u,v},v\in V^{\prime}
induces
a
(p-1)
-clique.

How do we identify each
S_{u,v}
? Suppose
v_{1},v_{2}\in V^{\prime}
are
distinct vertices. In
V^{\prime}
(which is already labeled by
V(2r-2,p)
)
we can find a vertex
v_{3}
such that
\langle v_{1},v_{3}\rangle\neq 0
and

\langle v_{2},v_{3}\rangle=0
. Hence each
S_{u,v}
can be identified because

(a)

All
p-1
vertices in
S_{u,v}
are adjacent to each other. They
all have identical neighborhoods in
X[u]
by the above argument.

(b)

Every vertex in
V[u]\setminus S_{u,v}
has a different
neighborhood in
X[u]
than the vertices in
S_{u,v}
.

Putting it together, in summary, we have shown the following.

Claim 7.11

In polynomial time we can uniquely identify all the subsets

S_{u,v},v\in V^{\prime}
, each of size
p-1
, forming a partition of

V[u]
, for each
u\in V_{12}
.

Thus, we have identified the subsets
S_{u,v}\subset V[u]
for
v\in V^{\prime}
. We now need to label the elements of
S_{u,v}
by vectors

\{\alpha\bar{u}+\bar{v}\mid\alpha\in\mathbb{F}^{*}_{p}\}
.

Claim 7.12

Assuming
X
is a symplectic graph, for each
v\in V^{\prime}
and
u\in V_{12}
, we can label the
p-1
vertices in
S_{u,v}
by the
p-1

vectors
\alpha\bar{u}+\bar{v},\alpha\in\mathbb{F}^{*}_{p}
arbitrarily to get a
labeling for
V\setminus V^{\prime}
that is consistent with the labeling of

V^{\prime}
.

Proof of Claim. We first observe that adjacencies within
V[u]
and
between
V[u]
and
V^{\prime}
are consistent with such a labeling by the
arguments preceding the claim.

We now examine the adjacencies across two disjoint
V[u]
and

V[u^{\prime}]
. Consider
S_{u,v}\subset V[u]
and
S_{u^{\prime},v^{\prime}}\subset V[u^{\prime}]
. There is an edge between these subsets if and only if for
some non-zero scalars
\alpha
and
\beta
we have

\langle\alpha\bar{u}+\bar{v},\beta\bar{u^{\prime}}+\bar{v^{\prime}}\rangle=0
. Now,

\langle\bar{u},\bar{u^{\prime}}\rangle\neq 0
. Hence we must have

\alpha\beta=\frac{-\langle\bar{v},\bar{v^{\prime}}\rangle}{\langle\bar{u},\bar{u^{\prime}}\rangle}.

Since
\alpha
and
\beta
are non-zero scalars, it follows that

\langle\bar{v},\bar{v^{\prime}}\rangle\neq 0
. It follows that
v
and
v^{\prime}
being
non-adjacent in the graph induced by
V^{\prime}
is a necessary condition
for
S_{v}
and
S_{v^{\prime}}
to have any edges between them. Furthermore,
the above equation implies that for the vertex, say
v_{\alpha}\in S_{u,v}
labeled
\alpha\bar{u}+\bar{v}
there is a unique
\beta\neq 0
such that
v_{\alpha}
is adjacent to the vertex, say
v^{\prime}_{\beta}
in

S_{u^{\prime},v^{\prime}}
labeled
\beta\bar{u^{\prime}}+\bar{v^{\prime}}
. Hence, either there
are no edges between
S_{v}
and
S_{v^{\prime}}
(if
v
and
v^{\prime}
are
adjacent) or there is a perfect matching between
S_{v}
and
S_{v^{\prime}}
(
if
v
and
v^{\prime}
are non-adjacent).

Since these are all the possible edges in
V\setminus V^{\prime}
and it is
clearly consistent with any labeling of
S_{u,v}
by the
p-1

vectors
\alpha\bar{u}+\bar{v},\alpha\neq 0
for all
u\in V_{12}

and
v\in V^{\prime}
, this completes the proof of the claim.

Putting it together, clearly if
X
is the symplectic graph of some
non-degenerate symplectic form on
V(2r,p)
the above algorithm
verifies that by constructing a symplectic form consistent with the
symplectic graph. The algorithm is described as a recursive procedure
below. Essentially, given a graph
X=(V,E)
with
|V|=p^{2r}
vertices
as input it will try to compute a labeling of
V
by the
p^{2r}

vectors in

V(2r,p)=\{\sum_{i=1}^{r}(\alpha_{i}\bar{x}_{i}+\beta_{i}\bar{y}_{i})\mid\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}\}
, such that the edges of
X
are consistent
with some symplectic form on
V(2r,p)
. If
X
is a symplectic graph
it will succeed. Otherwise, the procedure will fail and detect that

X
is not symplectic.

Procedure Label
(X,2r,p)

The procedure takes as input a simple graph
X=(V,E)
with

p^{2r}
vertices for an odd prime
p
and computes a
labeling consistent with some symplectic form on
V(2r,p)
if

X
is a symplectic graph. Otherwise, it returns “fail”.

(a)

Identify the unique dominant vertex
v_{0}\in V
and label it by

0
(the zero vector).

(b)

Pick any pair of nonadjacent vertices
x_{1},y_{1}\in V\setminus\{v_{0}\}
and label them by the basis vectors
\bar{x}_{1}
and

\bar{y}_{1}
respectively.

(c)

Let
V^{\prime}
be the set of all vertices adjacent to both
x_{1}
and

y_{1}
. Check
|V^{\prime}|=p^{2r-2}
. Let
X^{\prime}
be the subgraph induced by

V^{\prime}
. Recursively call Label
(X^{\prime},2r-2,p)
and obtain a labeling of

V^{\prime}
by vectors in
V(2r-2,p)
defined by the basis

\bar{x}_{i},\bar{y}_{i},2\leq i\leq p
and consistent with a symplectic
form on
V(2r-2,p)
(Claim
7.7
).

(d)

Determine the
p^{2}-1
vertices
V_{12}
(Claim
7.8
)
consisting of vertices in
V\setminus V^{\prime}
that are adjacent to all
vertices in
V^{\prime}
. Determine the partition of
V_{12}
into
p+1

many
(p-1)
-cliques in the induced subgraph
G_{12}
(Claim

7.9
).

(e)

From the adjacencies between
V\setminus V^{\prime}
and
V^{\prime}
determine
the partition of
V\setminus V^{\prime}
into
p+1
subsets
V[u]

(Claim
7.10
).

(f)

From the subgraphs
G[u]
induced by the respective
V[u]
,
determine the partition of
V[u]
into
p-1
-subsets
S_{u,v},v\in V^{\prime}
(Claim
7.11
) .

(g)

Label the vertices in
S_{u,v},v\in V^{\prime}
and
u\in V_{12}
by
the vectors
\alpha\bar{u}+\bar{v},\alpha\neq 0

(Claim
7.12
).

(h)

If any step above does not satisfy the stated property then
output
fail
. Else output
pass
along with the complete
labeling of
V
by elements of
V(2r,p)
as the witness that
X
is
a symplectic graph.

Running time analysis
Let
T(n)
denote the
running time for constructing a symplectic form
\langle,\rangle
for

V(2r,p)
consistent with
X
as the symplectic graph, where

n=p^{2r}
is the number of vertices in
X
. The inductive
construction and the rest of the computation implies the recurrence

T(n)=O(|V|^{2})+T(n-1)
, which gives an overall cubic bound

T(n)=O(|V|^{3})=O(p^{6r+3})
on the running time.

Putting it together, we have a polynomial-time algorithm that checks
if
X
is the symplectic graph for the symplectic space by finding
out a labeling of vertices by the vectors along with a consistent
symplectic form. ∎

In summary, we have the following.

Theorem 7.13

Given a graph
X=(V,E)
with
p^{2r+1}
vertices we can determine in
polynomial time if it is the commuting graph of an extraspecial
group of order
p^{2r+1}
and, if so, label vertices by unique group
elements satisfying the commuting relation.

Proof 13

By Lemma
7.3
we can obtain the graph

X_{s}=(V_{s},E_{s})
from
X
in polynomial time. By
Theorem
7.6
we can check if
X_{s}
is the
symplectic graph of a symplectic form
\langle,\rangle
on
V(2n,p)

and also find the symplectic form.

From the proof of Lemma
7.3
, we have the
following observations.

(a)

The vertex of degree
|V_{s}|-1
in
X_{s}
is the
0
element of

V(2n,p)
, and corresponds to
Z
in
G
.

(b)

For each vertex
x\in V_{s}
there is a closed-twins equivalence
class of size
p-1
containing
x
. There is a corresponding subset
of vertices
H_{x}\setminus Z
in
X
of size
p^{2}-p
, where
H_{x}
, in
turn, corresponds to a subgroup of
G
of order
p^{2}
that contains

Z
. We have a labeling of the vertex
x
by a linear combination

\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\alpha_{i}\bar{x}_{i}+\beta_{i}\bar{y}_{i})
, and the remaining
p-2

vertices in the equivalence class are labeled by non-zero scalar
multiples of this linear combination.

Now, using the description of the extraspecial group
G_{1}
we can
label the vertices of the clique
H_{x}\setminus Z
with the group
elements

z^{j}\cdot\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}.y_{i}^{\beta_{i}}\right)^{k},0\leq j\leq p-1,1\leq k\leq p-1.

This will give us the labeling of
X
with the elements of the
extraspecial group
G_{1}
of order

, consistent with the
commuting graph
X
.

Observe that if

is not a symplectic graph then Procedure

7.6
will detect that and
return

. This completes the proof. ∎

8
A quasipolynomial time algorithm in the general case

Assuming CFSG (the classification of finite simple groups

[
41
]
), in this section we present a
2^{O(\log^{3}n)}
time
algorithm for checking if a given
n
-vertex graph is the commuting
graph of some
n
-element group and, if so, labeling the vertices of
the graph by the group elements consistent with all the commuting
pairs. For CFSG in this section we will refer to the (concrete)
descriptions of the finite simple groups in Wilson’s text
[
79
]
.

(a)

The main idea is the notion of
description

\mathop{\mathrm{desc}}(G)
for
any finite group
G
. It is essentially an encoding of the group
G

as a
binary string
of length at most
c\cdot\log^{3}|G|
, for
some fixed constant
c>0
, from which
G
can be recovered
efficiently. More precisely, we will describe an algorithm

\mathcal{D}
that takes
\mathop{\mathrm{desc}}(G)
as input and computes the
multiplication table of
G
as output in time polynomial in
|G|
.

(b)

This algorithm
\mathcal{D}
can be used to generate the
multiplication tables of all groups
G
of order
n
as follows. We
enumerate all possible binary strings
w
of length at most

c\cdot\log^{3}n
. For each
w
we run the algorithm
\mathcal{D}

on it. If
w=\mathop{\mathrm{desc}}(G)
for some
G
then
\mathcal{D}
will output
its multiplication table. We compute
\Gamma(G)
from
G
and check
if
X
is isomorphic to
\Gamma(G)
in time
2^{O(\log^{3}n)}
using
Babai’s graph isomorphism algorithm
[
4
]
.

Thus, the main task of this section is to define
\mathop{\mathrm{desc}}(G)
and
describe the algorithm
\mathcal{D}
. We will first define
\mathop{\mathrm{desc}}(G)

for finite simple groups, along with how the algorithm
\mathcal{D}

will compute the multiplication table for
G
given
\mathop{\mathrm{desc}}(G)
as
input. We will then extend the notion of
\mathop{\mathrm{desc}}(G)
to all groups by
using a composition series for
G
combined with
\mathop{\mathrm{desc}}(N)
for each
composition factor
N
of
G
.

Remark 5

We emphasize here that our notion of “short” description is
entirely different from the well-studied notion of
generator-relator presentations of groups (see e.g.
[
5
]
). Indeed,
\mathop{\mathrm{desc}}(G)
and its construction will be
significantly simpler. It suffices for the purpose of recovering the
multiplication table of the group
G
in time polynomial in
|G|
.

8.1
Short descriptions for finite simple groups assuming CFSG

We first briefly summarize the classification theorem of finite simple
groups (CFSG)
[
41
]
.

(a)

Cyclic groups of prime order.

(b)

Alternating groups
A_{n},n\geq 5
.

(c)

Finite simple groups of Lie type.

(d)

Sporadic finite simple groups.

As there are only finite many sporadic finite simple groups, for the
algorithm
\mathcal{D}
they can be trivially dealt with. The cyclic
groups of prime order are also easy to deal with for the algorithm

\mathcal{D}
as there is a unique such subgroup of order
p
for each
prime
p
.

Lemma 8.1

If
G
is cyclic of prime order
p
then
\mathop{\mathrm{desc}}(G)
can be defined
as the binary encoding
\mathop{\mathrm{bin}}(p)
of
p
which is of length
\log p

bits. We can include
1
as the generator with the additive group

\mathbb{Z}_{p}
as the group it encodes.

Proof 14

The algorithm
\mathcal{D}
will take the binary encoding
\mathop{\mathrm{bin}}(p)

of
p
, verify that
p
is prime using the polynomial-time primality
test, and then write out the group table for the additive group of
integers modulo
p
. ∎

For several of the remaining cases, the following simple lemma gives a
framework for defining
\mathop{\mathrm{desc}}(G)
and computing
G
’s multiplication
table from it. We use the well-known fact that any finite simple group
can be generated with two generators (see
[
57
]
).

Lemma 8.2

Let
H
be a finite group and
N\lhd K\leq H
be subgroups such that

(a)

\mathop{\mathrm{desc}}(H)
is of length
m
.

(b)

The multiplication table for
H
can be computed from

\mathop{\mathrm{desc}}(H)
in time polynomial in
|H|
.

(c)

We can test membership of elements in
N
in time polynomial
in
|H|
.

Suppose
G=K/N
is simple. Then
\mathop{\mathrm{desc}}(G)
has size

O(m+\log|H|)
and in time polynomial in
|H|
we can compute the
multiplication table for
G
.

Proof 15

As
G=K/N
is simple it can be generated by two elements

Ng_{1},Ng_{2}\in K/N
where
g_{1},g_{2}\in K
. Let
\mathop{\mathrm{desc}}(G)
be the
indices of these two elements in
H
along with
\mathop{\mathrm{desc}}(H)
. By
indices here we mean the positions of these elements in the
multiplication table for
H
. Clearly,
\mathop{\mathrm{desc}}(G)
is of size
O(\log|H|)
. The claimed algorithm
\mathcal{D}
will first use
\mathop{\mathrm{desc}}(H)

to compute the multiplication table for
H
. It can enumerate
H

and use the indices to identify
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
. Using the
multiplication table for
H
it can generate the subgroup

K=\langle g_{1},g_{2}\rangle
, listing down all its elements in time
polynomial in
|H|
. Next, the algorithm can identify the normal
subgroup
N
(as given in the hypothesis) in time polynomial in

|H|
and obtain the multiplication table for
G=K/N
.∎

We will apply the above lemma to the remaining cases of finite simple
groups.

Lemma 8.3

If
G=A_{n}
for some
n\geq 5
then we can define
\mathop{\mathrm{desc}}(G)

of size
O(\log|G|)
.

Proof 16

We can define
\mathop{\mathrm{desc}}(A_{n})
as the encoding of two generators of
A_{n}
,
by giving the index of the two generating permutations, say
g_{1},g_{2}

in
S_{n}
in the natural order of enumerating
S_{n}
. Hence,
\mathop{\mathrm{desc}}(G)

will be of size
2\log n!=O(\log|A_{n}|)
. We can now apply
Lemma
8.2
with
H=S_{n}
,
K=A_{n}
and
N=\{1\}
to complete
the proof.∎

We now consider the finite simple groups of Lie type. It is convenient
to deal with them in two categories: classical and exceptional. We
will follow the (concrete) constructions of these groups as
explained in Wilson’s text
[
79
]
.

8.1.1
Classical Lie type

Lemma 8.4

For each finite simple group
G
that is of classical Lie type, there
is a description
\mathop{\mathrm{desc}}(G)
of
O(\log|G|)
size such that the
multiplication table for
G
can be computed from
\mathop{\mathrm{desc}}(G)

in time polynomial in
|G|
.

Proof 17

These finite simple groups are categorized below, following Wilson’s
text
[
79
, Chapter 3]
.

(a)

\mathop{\mathrm{PSL}}\nolimits_{n}(q)
for either
n>2
or
q>3
. These are the projective
special linear group arising from the matrix group
\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits_{n}(q)
(the
finite group of
n\times n
invertible matrices over the finite
field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
).

(b)

\mathop{\mathrm{PSp}}\nolimits_{2m}(q)
which is the projective symplectic group,
obtained as quotient of the symplectic group
\mathop{\mathrm{Sp}}\nolimits_{2m}(q)
, and
where
\mathop{\mathrm{Sp}}\nolimits_{2m}(q)
itself is a subgroup of
\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits_{2m}(q)
.

(c)

\mathop{\mathrm{PSU}}\nolimits_{n}(q)
which is the projective unitary group, obtained as
a quotient of the special unitary group
\mathop{\mathrm{SU}}\nolimits_{n}(q)
. The general
unitary group
\mathop{\mathrm{GU}}\nolimits_{n}(q)
itself is a subgroup of the matrix group

\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits_{n}(q^{2})
.

(d)

Finally, the orthogonal groups which are also obtained
essentially as quotients of matrix groups, but there are different
subcases depending on both
n
and
q
.

Consider
\mathop{\mathrm{PSL}}\nolimits_{n}(q)
. The special linear group

\mathop{\mathrm{SL}}\nolimits_{n}(q)=\{A\in\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits_{n}(q)\mid\det(A)=1\}
is a subgroup of
\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits_{n}(q)

of index
q-1
.
\mathop{\mathrm{PSL}}\nolimits_{n}(q)
is obtained as the quotient
\mathop{\mathrm{SL}}\nolimits_{n}(q)/Z

where

consists of all “scalar matrices”
\lambda I_{n}
of
determinant
1
. Thus,
|\mathop{\mathrm{PSL}}\nolimits_{n}(q)|\geq|\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits_{n}(q)|/q^{2}
. We can apply
Lemma
8.2
, letting
H=\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits_{n}(q)
,
K=\mathop{\mathrm{SL}}\nolimits_{n}(q)
and
N=Z
in it.
Notice that
\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits_{n}(q)
has a description of size

O(\log n+\log q)
. Given
n
and
q
we need to enumerate

\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits_{n}(q)
in time polynomial in
|\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits_{n}(q)|
. The algorithm will
enumerate all
n\times n
matrices
M
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, one by one, run
a polynomial time algorithm for checking if
M
is invertible (say,
using Gaussian elimination with
O(n^{3})
many field operations) and
if so include it in
\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits_{n}(q)
. Since a large fraction of matrices
are invertible (for
n
more than some constant), this procedure
will run in time polynomial in
|GL_{n}(q)|
. Thus, by
Lemma
8.2
we can obtain the desired description for

\mathop{\mathrm{PSL}}\nolimits_{n}(q)
.

Next, consider
\mathop{\mathrm{PSp}}\nolimits_{2m}(q)
. Recall that the symplectic group

\mathop{\mathrm{Sp}}\nolimits_{2m}(q)
is the subgroup of
\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits_{2m}(q)
consisting of all

Definition 4
(Erdős-Rényi cube generating sequences)

Let

(abbreviated as c.g.s.) for

Thus, given the cube generating sequence
(g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{k})
, each
element of

can be defined by the bit vector

(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2},\ldots,\epsilon_{k})

vector need not be unique for a group element.

Theorem 8.7

[

9
Isoclinism of groups and commuting graphs

Motivated by the polynomial-time algorithm for recognizing commuting
graphs of extraspecial groups, in this section we briefly discuss
isoclinism of groups and its connected to commuting graphs.

The
lexicographic product
of graphs
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
is the
graph on the vertex set
V(\Gamma_{1})\times V(\Gamma_{2})
, in which
(v_{1},w_{1})

and
(v_{2},w_{2})
are joined if and only if either
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
are joined in

\Gamma_{1}
, or
v_{1}=v_{2}
and
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
are joined in
\Gamma_{2}
. If

\Gamma_{2}
is a complete graph, then the lexicographic product is obtained
from
\Gamma_{1}
by blowing up each vertex to a complete graph on

|V\Gamma_{2}|
vertices.

Commutation in a group
G
is a map from
G\times G
to the derived group
G^{\prime}
;
multiplying the arguments by elements of
Z(G)
does not change the function
value, so commutation induces a map from
G/Z(G)\times G/Z(G)
to
G^{\prime}
.
We say that groups
G
and
H
are
isoclinic
if there are isomorphisms

\theta:G/Z(G)\to H/Z(H)
and
\phi:G^{\prime}\to H^{\prime}
which intertwine the commutation
map; that is, for all
g_{1},g_{2}\in G
,

[\theta(g_{1}Z(G)),\theta(g_{2}Z(G))]=\phi([g_{1}Z(G),g_{2}Z(G)]).

Now the commuting graph of a group
G
is the lexicographic product of a
graph on
G/Z(G)
with
g_{1}Z(G)
and
g_{2}Z(G)
joined if
[g_{1},g_{2}]=1

(this graph can also be described as the induced subgraph of the commuting
graph of
G
on a transversal for
Z(G)
in
G
)
with a complete graph on
Z(G)
; the graph on
G/Z(G)
is determined by the
isoclinism type. Hence, as observed by Pezzott and Nakaoka
[
62
]
, we have:

Theorem 9.1

Isoclinic groups of the same order have isomorphic commuting graphs.

Remark 8

It follows from Lemma
7.2
that the extraspecial groups

G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, defined in Section
7
, are
isoclinic. Indeed, the recognition algorithm of
Section
7
implicitly uses this property.

Is there a converse to Theorem
9.1
? That is, if two
groups have isomorphic commuting graphs, must they be isoclinic? This
holds for various classes of groups, such as abelian groups,
non-abelian simple groups, and extraspecial groups (as we have seen).

However, it is not true in general; we recycle an example taken from

[
23
]
to show this. Let
G
be the group of order
64
which is

SmallGroup(64,182)
in the SmallGroups library in
GAP

[
37
]
. A
Schur cover
of
G
is a group
H
of maximal
order subject to having a subgroup
Z\leq H^{\prime}\cap Z(H)
such that

H/Z\cong G
. The Schur multiplier of
G
has order
2
, so any Schur
cover of
G
has order
128
. Moreover, the Bogomolov multiplier (see

[
23
]
for context and other references) of
G
is equal to the
Schur multiplier, which implies that any Schur cover
H
is

commutation-preserving
: that is, two elements
a,b\in H

commute if and only if their projections
Za,Zb\in G
commute. This
implies that the commuting graph of a Schur cover is obtained from the
commuting graph of
G
by replacing each vertex with a clique of size

2
, with all edges between cliques corresponding to adjacent
vertices. This procedure also describes the commuting graph of

G\times C_{2}
. On the other hand, it is easy to verify computationally
that the derived groups of
H
and
G\times C_{2}
are not isomorphic,
so these groups cannot be isoclinic.

We note that the group
G
has nilpotency class
3
, as do all of its
Schur covers (these are
SmallGroup(128,
i
)
for
i\in\{789,790,791,815,816,817\}
in the
GAP
library). So the
following question is still open:

Conjecture

Is it true that a nilpotent group of class
2
is
determined up to isoclinism by its commuting graph?

This is interesting because, as observed, extraspecial groups are
nilpotent of class two
2
and are determined up to isoclinism by
their commuting graphs.

Preliminaries

A number of important graph classes can be defined either structurally or in terms of forbidden induced subgraphs. Here are structural descriptions.

(a)

\Gamma
is called a
cograph
if it can be built from
1
-vertex graphs
by the operations of disjoint union and complementation.

(b)

\Gamma
is called a
chordal graph
provided that
\Gamma
contains no induced cycles of length greater than
3
; in other words,
any cycle of length at least
4
contains a chord.

The characterization by forbidden subgraphs is a composite of results from

[
36
,
27
,
72
,
26
]
.

Proposition 10.1

(a)

A graph is a cograph if and only if it forbids
P_{4}
.

(b)

A graph is chordal if and only if it forbids
\{C_{n}:n\geq 4\}
.

Since a cycle of length
5
or more contains a
4
-vertex path, we have the following observation.

Observation 3

If a graph is
\{P_{4},C_{4}\}
-free, then it is chordal.

We conclude this section with a general remark.
In the commuting graph
\Gamma(G)
of a group
G
, elements of the centre

Z(G)
are dominating vertices; this is the reason why, to investigate questions
about connectedness, they are excluded. However, in our case,
it doesn’t matter:

Proposition 10.2

(a)

The class of groups whose commuting graph is
\mathcal{F}
-free is
subgroup-closed.

(b)

If no graph in
\mathcal{F}
has a dominating vertex, then the class of
groups with
\mathcal{F}
-free commuting graph is independent of whether
vertices in
Z(G)
are included or excluded.

Proof 23

(a) This is immediate from the fact that, if
H
is a subgroup of
G
, then

\Gamma(H)
is an induced subgraph of
\Gamma(G)
. (We remark that the same
result holds for various other graphs such as the power graph, enhanced power
graph, nilpotency and solvability graphs. But it does not hold for yet others
such as the generating graph or the deep commuting graph.)

(b) If
X
is an induced subgraph of
\Gamma(G)
, then vertices of

X\cap Z(G)
would be dominating vertices in
X
. ∎

In the cases we are interested in (chordal graphs and
cographs), the excluded subgraphs are paths and cycles with more than three
vertices. None of these have dominating vertices, so we can take the
vertex set of the commuting graph to be all of
G
, but to decide if it has
any of these properties we can delete vertices in
Z(G)
.

Also to prove main results of this paper, we need the following
assertion which generalizes a result obtained by
Wielandt
[
78
]
in 1939.

Proposition 10.3

Let
G
be a finite group. Assume that one of the following statements holds:

(a)

Z(G)=1
;

(b)

G=G^{\prime}
.

Then
C_{\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}(G)}(\mathop{\mathrm{Inn}}(G))=1
.

Proof 24

Let
\phi
be an automorphism of
G
which commutes with each inner
automorphism of
G
. Then for each
g,h\in G
, we
have

\phi(h)^{g}=\phi(h^{g})=\phi(h)^{\phi(g)},

i. e.
[g,\phi]=g^{-1}\phi(g)\in Z(G)
and
\phi(g)=g\cdot z_{g}

for some
z_{g}\in Z(G)
(depending of
g
).

If
Z(G)=1
, then
z_{g}=1
for each
g
, therefore
\phi
is trivial and
C_{\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}(G)}(\mathop{\mathrm{Inn}}(G))=1
, as required.

Now assume that
G
is perfect, i.e.
G^{\prime}=G
. Note that
\phi([x,y])=[\phi(x),\phi(y)]=[x,y]
for each
x,y\in G
. Since
G
is generated by commutators, we have
z_{g}=1
for each
g
. Thus, again
\phi
is trivial and so,
C_{\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}(G)}(\mathop{\mathrm{Inn}}(G))=1
. ∎

Structure of groups whose commuting graphs are cographs or chordal graphs

In this section, we will investigate the group-theoretical structure
of a group whose commuting graph is a cograph or a chordal graph. In
particular, in some sense, the class of such groups generalizes the
class of finite solvable groups.

First, we investigate special cases of groups like direct and central
products and Frobenius groups whose commuting graph is a cograph or a
chordal graph, and then prove the general structure theorem.

11.1
Direct and central products

Observation 4

Let

be an abelian group. Then
\Gamma(A\times G)
is a cograph
(
resp. a chordal graph
)
if and only if
\Gamma(G)
is a cograph
(

)

Lemma 11.1

Let

and

be non-abelian groups.
Then
\Gamma(H\times K)
has an induced subgraph isomorphic to

and an induced subgraph isomorphic to

Proof 25

Now let

and

be non-commuting elements of

, and

and

(a,e_{K})\sim(e_{H},u)\sim(b,e_{K})\sim(e_{H},v)\sim(a,e_{K})

is an induced cycle of length

By Lemma

and Observation

, the following result holds.

Proposition 11.2

Let

and

be groups. Then

(a)

and

-free;

(b)

and

is abelian and the other is

-free for all

.

Recall that a finite nilpotent group is the direct product of its Sylow subgroups. Thus, Proposition

implies the following result.

Corollary 11.3

Let

be a non-abelian nilpotent group. Then
\Gamma(G)
is a

(
resp. a chordal graph
)
if and only if there exists exactly one non-abelian Sylow subgroup in

whose commuting graph is

-free
(

-free for all

)
, while the other Sylow subgroups are abelian.

We saw in the first part the definition of the strong product of graphs and
the fact that the commuting graph of

is

(Proposition

).

We can extend this to central products as follows. Let

be the class of all finite nilpotent groups of nilpotency class at most

Lemma 11.4

Let

and

be two groups not in the class

Then

Proof 26

By assumption, we can choose

such that the commutator

is not in the centre of

Then

(a,u)\sim(a,e_{K})\sim(e_{H},v)\sim(b,v)

11.3
A general structure theorem

The main result of this section is the following theorem.

Theorem 11.7

Let
G
be a finite group such that
\Gamma(G)
is a cograph
(
resp. a chordal graph
)
, and let
F(G)
be the Fitting subgroup of
G
.

(a)

One of the following statements holds:

(i)

C_{G}(F(G))=Z(F(G))
;

(ii)

F(G)
is of nilpotent class at most
2
and
G/F(G)
is almost simple;
in particular,
G
has a unique non-abelian composition factor.

(b)

If
\mathop{\mathrm{Soc}}(G)
is non-abelian, then
G
has a normal subgroup
N=A\times S
, where
A
is abelian,
S
is simple and
\Gamma(S)
is a cograph
(
resp. a chordal graph
)
, such that
G/N
is isomorphic to a subgroup of
\mathop{\mathrm{Out}}(S)
and, therefore,
G/N
is solvable.

Proof 28

Let
F^{*}(G)
be the generalized Fitting subgroup of
G
.
Let
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
be distinct elements from
\mathop{\mathrm{Comp}}(G)
. By
[
3
, 31.5]
,
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
commute, therefore
\langle H_{1},H_{2}\rangle=H_{1}\circ H_{2}
is a subgroup of
G
. By Lemma
11.4
,
\Gamma(G)
is neither a cograph nor a chordal graph, a contradiction to Proposition
10.2
. Thus,
|\mathop{\mathrm{Comp}}(G)|\leq 1
.

If
|\mathop{\mathrm{Comp}}(G)|=0
, then
F^{*}(G)=F(G)
, and because we have
C_{G}(F^{*}(G))\leq F^{*}(G)
by
[
3
, 31.13]
, statement (a)(i) holds.

Assume that
|\mathop{\mathrm{Comp}}(G)|=1
. Then
G
has a quasisimple characteristic subgroup
N=E(G)
. Consider
C=C_{G}(N)
, which is a normal subgroup of
G
. Let
H=\langle N,C\rangle=N\circ C
. By Proposition
10.2
and Lemma

,
C
is nilpotent of nilpotency class at most
2
, therefore
C\leq F(G)
.
On the other hand,
F(G)\leq C
by (

). Thus,
C=F(G)
and, therefore
G/F(G)
is a subgroup of
\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}(N)
which has a simple normal subgroup
H/C\cong N/Z(N)
.

Now we show that
\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}(N)
is an almost simple group and
\mathop{\mathrm{Soc}}(\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}(N))\cong N/Z(N)
. Indeed,
N/Z(N)\cong\mathop{\mathrm{Inn}}(N)
is a normal subgroup of
\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}(N)
and
N/Z(N)
is simple. By Proposition
10.3
, we have

\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}(N)\cong\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}(N)/C_{\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}(N)}(\mathop{\mathrm{Inn}}(N))

is a subgroup of
\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}(\mathop{\mathrm{Inn}}(N))\cong\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}(N/Z(N))
. So, we have that
\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}(N)
is a subgroup of an almost simple subgroup
\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}(N/Z(N))
which contains a normal subgroup
\mathop{\mathrm{Inn}}(N)\cong N/Z(N)
. Thus,
\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}(N)
is an almost simple group with socle
N/Z(N)
, as required.

So,
G/F(G)
is isomorphic to a subgroup of
\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}(N)
which is almost simple with socle isomorphic to
\mathop{\mathrm{Inn}}(N)
and contains a subgroup
H/F(G)\cong\mathop{\mathrm{Inn}}(N)
, therefore
G/F(G)
is almost simple. Thus, statement (a)(ii) holds.

Now we prove statement (b). Assume that
L=\mathop{\mathrm{Soc}}(G)
is non-abelian. Then
L=A\times S
, where
A
is abelian and
S
is the direct product of non-abelian simple groups, moreover, both

and

are normal subgroups of
G
. By Propositions

and

,

is simple and
\Gamma(S)
is a cograph
(
resp. a chordal graph
)
. If

, then

and

since

is simple.
Thus,

is a normal subgroup of

. By Proposition
10.2
,
\Gamma(N)
is also a cograph (resp. a chordal graph). Using Proposition
11.2
, we conclude that

is abelian.
Moreover,
G/C
is isomorphic to a subgroup of
\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}(S)
which is almost simple, therefore
G/N
is isomorphic to a subgroup of
\mathop{\mathrm{Out}}(S)
. Thus, by the Schreier Conjecture (Theorem

),

is solvable, and therefore statement (b) holds. ∎

The following problem is of interest.

Problem 1

Classify finite simple groups whose commuting graphs are chordal.

12
Simple groups whose commuting graph is a cograph or a chordal graph

In this section, we investigate when the commuting graph of a simple sporadic or alternating group is a cograph or a chordal graph, and then obtain a complete classification of finite simple groups whose commuting graph is a cograph.

12.1
CA-groups and AC-groups

A group
G
is a
CA-group
if the centralizer of every non-identity
element of
G
is abelian.
Another name is
CT-groups
, since they are the groups for which
the commutation relation on non-identity elements is transitive. (If

x\sim y\sim z
in the CA-group
G
, then
x,z\in C_{G}(y)
, so
x
and

z
commute. The converse is similar.)

If
G
is a CA-group, then the induced subgraph
\Gamma(G)
on non-identity
elements is a disjoint union of complete graphs. As a result, we see:

Proposition 12.1

Let
G
be a CA-group. Then
\Gamma(G)
is a cograph, chordal, and perfect.

CA-groups played an important historical role in developing techniques for
attacking problems on finite groups; they are now well-understood.
From results of Weisner
[
77
]
, Brauer, Suzuki and Wall

[
13
]
and Suzuki
[
73
]
, we obtain:

Proposition 12.2

A finite CA-group
G
satisfies one of the following:

(a)

G
is abelian;

(b)

G
is a Frobenius group with abelian Frobenius kernel and cyclic
Frobenius complement; or

(c)

G\cong\mathop{\mathrm{PSL}}\nolimits(2,q)
for
q
a power of
2
.

More generally,
G
is said to be an
AC-group
if
C_{G}(x)
is abelian
for every element
x\in G\setminus Z(G)
. This is a weaker condition, though
clearly a group with trivial centre is an AC-group if and only if it is a
CA-group.

AC-groups investigated by Schmidt
[
65
]
and Rocke
[
64
]
. (Schmidt
investigated groups in which two maximal centralizers intersect just in

Z(G)
; it is not hard to see that, for finite groups, this is equivalent to
the AC property.) Abdollahi
et al.

[
2
]
investigated the
connection of this property with the
non-commuting graph
, the
complement of the commuting graph. The classification of the AC-groups is not
as complete as that of CA-groups, but the cited authors have proved a
number of results about these groups.

From our point of view, AC-groups are as good as CA-groups:

Proposition 12.3

Let
G
be an AC-group. Then
\Gamma(G)
is a cograph and a chordal graph.

Proof 29

By Proposition
10.2
, it suffices to consider the induced subgraph on
the set
G\setminus Z(G)
; and this graph is a disjoint union of complete
graphs. ∎

Here is a simple general result which we will use.

Proposition 12.4

Let
G
be a finite group with the property that every non-identity element
of
G/Z(G)
has cyclic centralizer. Then
G
is an AC-group.

Proof 30

Take
a\in G\setminus Z(G)
. Then
Z(G)\leq C_{G}(a)
, and
C_{G}(a)/Z(G)\leq C_{G}(aZ(G))
(in

G/Z(G)
); so
C_{G}(a)/Z(G)
is cyclic, whence
C_{G}(a)
is abelian. ∎

12.2
Generalized dihedral groups and generalized quaternion groups

In this subsection we show that these groups are AC-groups, so that their
commuting graphs are cographs as well as chordal graphs.

Proposition 12.5

Let

be the generalized dihedral group as presented in

is an AC-group. Hence

Proof 31

The centre of

is the subgroup

consisting of elements of orders

or
2

is a clique, and the elements outside

fall into cliques of the form
\langle t,T\rangle\setminus T
for

; there are no edges between these cliques. So the induced subgraph is a disjoint union of complete graphs. Now Proposition
10.2
gives the
result.∎

Proposition 12.6

Let

be the generalized quaternion group as presented in
Equation (
5

is an AC-group. Hence
\Gamma(Q_{4m})
is a
cograph as well as a chordal graph.

Proof 32

The proof is similar to the preceding theorem. We find that, with

,
we have

(Observation
2
); for

12.5
Simple groups of Lie type whose commuting graph is a cograph

Now we turn to simple groups of Lie type. In this analysis, we only consider
cographs, not chordal graphs.

Proposition 12.11

Let
G=\mathop{\mathrm{PSL}}\nolimits(2,q)
, with
q\geq 4
. Then
\Gamma(G)
is a cograph if and only if
either
q
is a power of
2
or
q=5
.

Note that
\mathop{\mathrm{PSL}}\nolimits(2,5)\cong\mathop{\mathrm{PSL}}\nolimits(2,4)
.

Proof 37

The subgroup structure of
\mathop{\mathrm{PSL}}\nolimits(2,q)
was determined by Dickson
[
31
]
,
and can be found, for example, in
[
14
]
.

Suppose first that
q\geq 4
is a power of
2
.
Then every non-trivial element of
\mathop{\mathrm{PSL}}\nolimits(2,q)
has order
2
or a prime divisor of
q-1
and
q+1
. Also,
the centralizers of involutions of
\mathop{\mathrm{PSL}}\nolimits(2,q)
are elementary abelian groups with order
q
, and the centralizers of other non-trivial elements of
\mathop{\mathrm{PSL}}\nolimits(2,q)
are cyclic of order
q-1
or
q+1
. It follows that in this case
\mathop{\mathrm{PSL}}\nolimits(2,q)
is a CA-group, and so by Proposition
12.2
,
\Gamma(\mathop{\mathrm{PSL}}\nolimits(2,q))
is a cograph.

Suppose next that
q
is odd at least
5
. Let
\epsilon\in\{+1,-1\}
be chosen so that
q+\epsilon 1
is divisible by
4
.
Note that
\mathrm{PSL}(2,q)
contains a Klein group such that
the centralizers of its involutions are Dihedral groups
D_{2(q+\epsilon 1)}
which contain disjoint cyclic groups of order
(q+\epsilon)/2
.
If
q>5
, then
(q+\varepsilon)/2>2
. So two involutions together with generators of cyclic groups of order
(q+\epsilon)/2
in their centralizers induce a path of length
4
. (The list of subgroups of
G
shows that these generators do not commute.) So
\Gamma(\mathop{\mathrm{PSL}}\nolimits(2,q))
is not a cograph. ∎

Remark 12

It is well-known that
\mathop{\mathrm{PSL}}\nolimits(2,2)\cong S_{3}
and
\mathop{\mathrm{PSL}}\nolimits(2,3)\cong A_{4}
, therefore their commuting graphs are cographs by Propositions
12.7
and
12.9
, respectively.

Proposition 12.12

Let
G
be either
\mathop{\mathrm{PSL}}\nolimits(3,q)
or a group of Lie type of BN-rank at least
3
.
Then
\Gamma(G)
is not a cograph.

Proof 38

We begin by showing that, if
G=\mathop{\mathrm{SL}}\nolimits(3,q)
, then
\Gamma(G)
is not a cograph.

Suppose first that
q\notin\{2,4\}
. Choose a non-zero element
a\in\mathop{\mathrm{GF}}\nolimits(q)

such that
a^{3}\neq 1
, and let
b=a^{-2}
, so that
b\neq a
.

Consider the four elements
g_{1},\ldots,g_{4}
given by

\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&1&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{array}\right),\,\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}a&0&0\\
0&a&0\\
0&0&b\end{array}\right),\,\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}b&0&0\\
0&a&0\\
0&0&a\end{array}\right),\,\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&0&0\\
0&1&1\\
0&0&1\end{array}\right).

It is clear that all of them belong to
G
, and that

g_{1}\sim g_{2}\sim g_{3}\sim g_{4}
in
\Gamma(G)
. A short calculation gives the
commutators of the other three pairs; none of them is trivial. So we have
an induced path in
\Gamma(G)
.

But the argument gives more. In fact none of the commutators is a scalar
matrix, so if
Z
is any subgroup of the centre of
G
(the group of scalar
matrices), then we also have an induced path in
\Gamma(G/Z)
, so that

\Gamma(G/Z)
is not a cograph. In particular,
\Gamma(\mathop{\mathrm{PSL}}\nolimits(3,q))
is not a
cograph.

Now we can cover the remaining two cases. Since
\mathop{\mathrm{PSL}}\nolimits(3,2)\cong\mathop{\mathrm{PSL}}\nolimits(2,7)
,
the commuting graph of
\mathop{\mathrm{PSL}}\nolimits(3,2)
is not a cograph. Also,
\mathop{\mathrm{PSL}}\nolimits(3,2)
is a
subgroup of both
\mathop{\mathrm{SL}}\nolimits(3,4)
and
\mathop{\mathrm{PSL}}\nolimits(3,4)
, so their commuting graphs are not
cographs either.

For the remainder of the proof, we use the Levi decomposition of parabolic
subgroups of groups with BN-pairs (see
[
25
, Section 8.5]
).
The Borel subgroup
H
of such a group is generated by the root subgroups
corresponding to the positive roots with respect to a fundamental system

\Pi
of roots. Parabolic subgroups
P_{\Phi}
are those which contain
H
; they
are generated by
H
together with the root subgroups corresponding to the
negatives of a subset
\Phi
of the negatives of the fundamental group.
According to the Levi decomposition,
P_{\Phi}
is the semidirect product
of its unipotent radical by the
Levi factor

L_{\Phi}
, a group with
BN-pair corresponding to the roots
\Phi
.
In the covering group,
L_{\Phi}
may have a centre, which may or may not be
killed when we factor out the centre of
G
. (For example,
\mathop{\mathrm{SL}}\nolimits(n,q)
has a
parabolic subgroup corresponding to the first two roots, generated by all
upper unitriangular matrices together with the lower unitriangular matrices
with non-zero off-diagonal elements in positions
1,2,3
only. These generate

\mathop{\mathrm{SL}}\nolimits(3,q)
; its image in
\mathop{\mathrm{PSL}}\nolimits(n,q)
is either
\mathop{\mathrm{SL}}\nolimits(3,q)
or
\mathop{\mathrm{PSL}}\nolimits(3,q)

depending on the congruence of
n
and
q
mod
3
.)

Now the Coxeter–Dynkin diagram of any simple group
G
of Lie type with
BN-pair of rank at least
3
contains a single edge, corresponding to a root
system of type
A_{2}
; so
G
has a parabolic
subgroup whose Levi factor is a central quotient of
\mathop{\mathrm{SL}}\nolimits(3,q)
, as in the
example above. From Proposition
10.2
,
we conclude that
\Gamma(G)
is not a cograph. ∎
