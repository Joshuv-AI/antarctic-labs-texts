What’s Decidable about (Atomic) Polymorphism? (Extended Version)
Paolo Pistone, Luca Tranchini

Abstract
Due to the undecidability of most type-related properties of System F like type inhabitation or type checking, restricted polymorphic systems have been widely investigated (the most well-known being ML-polymorphism). In this paper we investigate System Fat, or atomic System F, a very weak predicative fragment of System F whose typable terms coincide with the simply typable ones. We show that the type-checking problem for Fat is decidable and we propose an algorithm which sheds some new light on the source of undecidability in full System F. Moreover, we investigate free theorems and contextual equivalence in this fragment, and we show that the latter, unlike in the simply typed lambda-calculus, is undecidable.

Introduction

Polymorphism has been a central topic in programming language theory since the late sixties.
Today, most general purpose programming languages employ some kind of polymorphism.
At the same time, under the Curry-Howard correspondence, quantification over types corresponds to quantification over propositions, that is, to second-order logic. In particular,
System F, the
archetypical
type system for polymorphism, can be seen as a proof-system for (the
\Rightarrow,\forall
-fragment of) second-order intuitionistic logic.

In spite of the numerous applications of polymorphism, practically all interesting type-related properties of (Curry-style) System F (e.g. type checking, type inhabitation, etc.) are undecidable, making this language impractical for any reasonable implementation.
This is one of the reasons why a wide literature has investigated more manageable subsystems of System F.
Notably,
\mathrm{ML}
-
polymorphism

[
41
,
42
,
40
]
has found much success due to its decidable type-checking.

Another direction of research was that of investigating
predicative
subsystems of System F
[
32
,
33
,
34
,
6
]
. In particular, the so-called
finitely stratified polymorphism

[
33
]
yields a stratification of System F through a sequence of predicative systems
(\mathrm{F}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of growing expressive power (notably,
F_{0}
is the simply typed
\lambda
-calculus
{\mathrm{ST\lambda C}}
, and

\mathrm{ML}
-polymorphism coincides with the rank-1 part of
{\mathrm{F}_{1}}
).
Yet, in spite of such limitations, type checking becomes undecidable already at level 1 of this hierarchy
[
18
]
.

Could one tell exactly at which point, in the range from the simply typed
\lambda
-calculus and
\mathrm{ML}
to full System F, the type-related properties of polymorphism become undecidable?

Atomic Polymorphism

In more recent times Ferreira et al. have undertaken the investigation of what can be seen as the least expressive predicative fragment of F, System
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
, or
atomic
System F
[

In this way

Actually,

{\mathrm{ST\lambda C}}

In spite of its very limited expressive power, Ferreira et al. have shown that, thanks to polymorphism,

{\mathrm{ST\lambda C}}

{\mathrm{F_{at}}}

However, while propositional logic is decidable,

[

) can be extended to show that the

{\mathrm{ST\lambda C}}

{\mathrm{F_{at}}}

Plan of the paper

After recalling the syntax of F and its fragment in Curry-style and Church-style, we address the properties (TI), (TC), (T) and (CE).

Type Inhabitation

In Section
3
we shortly discuss the undecidability of (TI), by showing how the argument in
[
56
]
for System F applies to
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
too. This argument yields an encoding inside
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
of an undecidable fragment of
first-order
intuitionistic logic. We also observe that
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
is actually equivalent to a first-order system, namely to the
\Rightarrow,\forall
-fragment
\mathrm{1Mon}^{\Rightarrow,\forall}
of first-order
monadic
intuitionistic logic in a language with a unique monadic predicate.
To our knowledge, the undecidability of
\mathrm{1Mon}^{\Rightarrow,\forall}
has not been previously observed (although some slightly more expressive fragments - e.g. including a primitive disjunction
[
19
]
or finitely many monadic predicates
[
53
]
- have been proven undecidable).

Type-Checking and Typability

In Section
4
we consider the type-checking problem.
The undecidability of (TC) for System F was established by Wells in
[
63
]
, and was later extended to all predicative systems
\mathrm{F}_{n}
, for
n>0

[
18
]
. In all these cases this result was obtained by reducing an undecidable variant of
second-order unification
(SOU) to the type-checking problem. On the other hand, the decidability of (TC) for
\mathrm{ML}
(and
\mathrm{F}_{0}={\mathrm{ST\lambda C}}
) is based on the famous Hindley-Milner algorithm
[
40
]
, which reduces this problem to
first-order unification
(FOU), which is decidable.

The fundamental source of undecidability of SOU is
the presence of
cyclic
dependences between second order variables, expressed in the simplest case by equations of the form
X(t)=f(v_{1},\dots,v_{k-1},X(u),v_{k+1},\dots,v_{n})
. In fact,
acyclic
SOU is decidable
[
36
]
.
When type-checking polymorphic programs, such cyclic dependencies are generated by
self-applications
, i.e. terms of the form
\lambda\vec{x}.xt_{1}\dots t_{k-1}xt_{k+1}\dots t_{n}
. In fact, in this case the type
\forall X.A
assigned to the variable
x
must satisfy a cyclic equation of the form

A[X\mapsto C_{1}]=B_{1}\Rightarrow\dots\Rightarrow B_{k-1}\Rightarrow A[X\mapsto C_{2}]\Rightarrow B_{k+1}\Rightarrow\dots\Rightarrow B_{n}

(where
C_{1},C_{2}
are suitable type instantiations of
X
).
By constrast, no term containing a self-application can be typed in
{\mathrm{ST\lambda C}}
, since cyclic equations cannot be solved by FOU.

Since the terms typable in
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
can also be typed in
{\mathrm{ST\lambda C}}
, it follows that
self-applications cannot be typed in
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
either. Using this observation,
we describe a type-checking algorithm for
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
which works in two phases: first, it checks (using FOU) the presence of cyclic dependencies, and returns
\mathtt{failure}
if it detects one; then, if phase 1 succeeds, it applies (a suitable variant of) acyclic SOU to decide type-checking.
From the decidability of (TC), we deduce the decidability of (T) by a standard argument (see
[
4
]
).

Contextual Equivalence

Studying the typable terms of
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
might not seem very interesting from a computational viewpoint,
as these terms are already typable in
{\mathrm{ST\lambda C}}
.
However, due to the presence of some form of polymorphism, investigating programs in
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
can be interesting for
equational
reasoning, as we investigate in Sections
5
and
6
. In standard type systems, beyond the standard notions of program equivalence arising from the operational semantics (i.e.
\beta\eta
-equivalence), there may exist several other congruences arising from either denotational models or from some notion of
contextual equivalence
.
In
{\mathrm{ST\lambda C}}
, it is well-known that
\simeq_{\beta\eta}
coincides with the congruence induced by any infinite extensional model
[
57
]
, as well as with several notions of contextual equivalence (see
[
5
]
,
[
7
]
).
In polymorphic type systems the picture is rather different, since
\beta\eta
-equivalence is usually weaker than the congruences arising from extensional models (see
[
3
,
23
]
), and also weaker than standard notions of contextual equivalence.
Moreover, while
\beta\eta
-equivalence is decidable, contextual equivalence is undecidable.
Since in many practical situations (see
[
61
,
1
]
) it is more convenient to reason up to notions of equivalence stronger than
\beta\eta
-equivalence,
several techniques to compute (approximations of) contextual equivalence have been investigated, e.g.
free theorems

[
62
]
,
parametricity

[
52
]
,
dinaturality

[
3
]
.

Our investigation of contextual equivalence starts in Section
5
with an exploration of equational reasoning in
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
using free theorems.
We show that the predicative encodings of sum and product types of Ferreira et al. produce products and coproducts in
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
in the categorical sense, provided terms are considered up to (CE) (a fact which is known to hold in F for the usual, impredicative, encodings
[
23
,
60
]
).
We then investigate (CE) for typable numerical functions. Using the fact that the primitive recursive functions are
uniquely
defined in System F up to (CE),
we show that (CE) for the representable numerical functions is decidable in
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
, and undecidable in
\mathrm{ML}
.
Such results rely on the observation that (CE) becomes undecidable as soon as some super-polynomial function (like bounded multiplication) becomes representable.
As a consequence, (CE) is undecidable in all fragments
F_{n}
, for
n>0
, of the finitely stratified hierarchy as well.

Finally, in Section
6

we establish that (CE) is undecidable also in
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
, by showing that the type inhabitation problem for a suitable extension of
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
can be reduced to it.
This result, together with the previous ones, shows that there is no hope to get a decidable contextual equivalence for polymorphic programs through a predicative restriction, and one has rather to look for other kinds of restrictions (see for instance
[
49
]
).

System F is impredicative:
any
type can figure as a witness. In particular, one can construct “circular” instantiations, in which a term of type
\forall X.A
is instantiated with the same type as witness.
A
predicative
fragment of System F is one in which witnesses are restricted in such a way to avoid such circular instantiations.

We will focus on three predicative fragments of System F, both in Church- and Curry-style.
The first is System
{\mathrm{F}_{1}}
, which is the fragment of F in which witnesses are
quantifier-free
.
The second is System
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
, which is the fragment of F in which witnesses are
atomic
, that is, type variables.
The third is system
\mathrm{ML}

[
41
,
40
]
, which essentially coincides with the
rank 1
fragment of
{\mathrm{F}_{1}}
.
For any type
A
, the rank
\mathsf{r}(A)
is the maximum number of nesting between
\Rightarrow
and
\forall
, and is defined inductively by
\mathsf{r}(X)=0
,
\mathsf{r}(A\Rightarrow B)=\max\{\mathsf{r}(A)+1,\mathsf{r}(B)\}
and
\mathsf{r}(\forall X_{1}\dots X_{n}.A)=\mathsf{r}(A)+1
(where
n>0
and
A
does not start with a quantifier).
To define
\mathrm{ML}
(since type-checking is decidable in
\mathrm{ML}
, we limit ourselves to Curry-style) one first has to enrich the set of
\lambda
-terms with the
\mathsf{let}
-constructor, and add a rule

\Gamma,x\mapsto A\vdash t:B

\Gamma\vdash u:A

\mathrm{let}

\Gamma\vdash\mathsf{let}\ x\ \mathsf{be}\ u\ \mathsf{in}\ t:B

\mathrm{ML}
is the fragment of the resulting system in which typing rules only contain judgements
\Gamma\vdash t:A
, where
\mathsf{r}(A)\leq 1
and for all
B\in\mathrm{Im}(\Gamma)
,
\mathsf{r}(B)\leq 1
.

Observe that in
{\mathrm{F}_{1}}
one can encode

, so that the rule above becomes derivable. This is not possible in

Impredicative and Predicative Encodings

It is well-known that sum and product types can be encoded inside System F by

\displaystyle A\widetilde{+}B

\displaystyle=\forall X.(A\Rightarrow X)\Rightarrow(B\Rightarrow X)\Rightarrow X

\displaystyle A\widetilde{\times}B

\displaystyle=\forall X.(A\Rightarrow B\Rightarrow X)\Rightarrow X

where the type variable
X
is fresh. The encoding of term constructors
\iota_{i}(\cdot)

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle

and term destructors
\mathrm{Case}_{C}(\cdot,x^{A}.\cdot,x^{B}.\cdot)
and
\pi_{i}(\cdot)

\displaystyle\iota_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=\Lambda X.\lambda f^{A\Rightarrow X}.\lambda g^{B\Rightarrow X}.ft

\displaystyle\qquad\mathrm{Case}_{C}(t,x^{A}.u,x^{B}.v)

\displaystyle=tC(\lambda x^{A}.u)(\lambda x^{B}.v)

\displaystyle\iota_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=\Lambda X.\lambda f^{A\Rightarrow X}.\lambda g^{B\Rightarrow X}.gt

\displaystyle\qquad\pi_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=tA\lambda x^{A}.\lambda y^{B}.x

\displaystyle\langle t,u\rangle

\displaystyle=\Lambda X.\lambda f^{A\Rightarrow B\Rightarrow X}.ftu

\displaystyle\qquad\pi_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=tB\lambda x^{A}.\lambda y^{B}.y

At the level of provability, the encoding is
faithful
: a type is inhabited in the extension of System F with sum and product types iff the encoded type is inhabited in System F. Moreover, the encoding of
\widetilde{+}
satisfies the

:
A\widetilde{+}B

A

At the level of conversions, the encoding translates
\beta

-reduction in F. Instead, the

\eta

The encoding of sum and product types is impredicative: the encoding of term destructors requires witnesses of arbitrary complexity. Notably, given a term

Type Inhabitation

In this section we discuss type inhabitation in the systems
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
and
{\mathrm{F}_{1}}
. We briefly recall the undecidability argument for (TI) in System F from
[
56
]
, and observe that this applies to
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
(a more detailed reconstruction can be found in
[
51
]
).

The argument in
[
56
]
(which was later simplified in
[
8
]
) is based on an embedding inside F of an undecidable fragment of first-order logic.
We recall the argument in a few more details, so that it will be clear that the same argument shows the undecidability of type inhabitation in both
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
and
{\mathrm{F}_{1}}
.

We consider the
\Rightarrow,\forall
-fragment of

intuitionistic first-order logic
\mathsf{DyIL_{\Rightarrow,\forall^{1}}}
, that is, in a language with no function symbol and a finite number of at most binary relation symbols. We consider sequents of the form
\Gamma\vdash\bot
where
\Gamma
consists of three type of assumptions:

i.

atomic formulas different from
\bot

ii.

closed formulas of the form
\forall\vec{\alpha}.(\varphi_{1}\Rightarrow\dots\Rightarrow\varphi_{n}\Rightarrow\psi)
, where
\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{n},\psi
are atomic formulas and each variable in
\psi
occurs in some the
\varphi_{i}
;

iii.

closed formulas of the form
\forall\alpha(\forall\beta(p(\alpha,\beta)\Rightarrow\bot)\Rightarrow\bot)
.

We fix a finite number of distinguished type variables:

•

for each relation symbol
p

p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}
;

•

five more variables
\spadesuit,\bullet,\circ_{1},\circ_{2},\star
.

We let, for any type
A

A^{\bullet}:=A\Rightarrow\bullet
, and we define, for all types
A,B

\displaystyle p_{AB}

\displaystyle=(A^{\bullet}\Rightarrow p_{1})\Rightarrow(B^{\bullet}\Rightarrow p_{2})\Rightarrow p_{3}

\displaystyle p(A,B)

\displaystyle=p_{AB}\Rightarrow\star

For any type
A

\mathcal{U}(A)
be the set of all types
(A^{\bullet}\Rightarrow p_{i})\Rightarrow\circ_{1},A^{\bullet}\Rightarrow\circ_{2}
, where

. Given a finite list of types

\mathcal{U}(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})\Rightarrow B

C_{1},\dots,C_{k}
are the types in

\bigcup_{i}\mathcal{U}(A_{i})
.

Each formula
\varphi

\mathsf{DyIL_{\Rightarrow,\forall^{1}}}
is translated into a type

as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbin{\overline{p(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{j})}}

\displaystyle=p(X_{i},X_{j})\qquad\mathbin{\overline{\bot}}=\spadesuit

\displaystyle\mathbin{\overline{\varphi\Rightarrow\psi}}

\displaystyle=\mathbin{\overline{\varphi}}\Rightarrow\mathbin{\overline{\psi}}

\displaystyle\mathbin{\overline{\forall\alpha_{i}.\varphi}}

\displaystyle=\forall\vec{X}_{i}.(\mathcal{U}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\Rightarrow\mathbin{\overline{\varphi}})

One can easily check the following by induction:

Proposition 3.1
.

If
\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{n}\vdash\varphi

\mathsf{DyIL_{\Rightarrow,\forall^{1}}}
and
\alpha_{i_{1}},\dots,\alpha_{i_{k}}

\mathrm{FV}(\varphi)
but not in
\mathrm{FV}(\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{n})

x_{1}\mapsto\mathbin{\overline{\varphi}}_{1},\dots,x_{n}\mapsto\mathbin{\overline{\varphi}}_{n},\vec{y}\mapsto\mathcal{U}(X_{i_{1}},\dots,X_{i_{k}})\vdash t:\mathbin{\overline{\varphi}}
holds in
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
for some term

The less trivial part is the following:

Theorem 3.2

(
[
56
]
, Theorem 11.6.14)
.

If
\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{n}
satisfy

iii

Since

{\mathrm{F_{at}}}

{\mathrm{F}_{1}}

Remark 3.1
.

Although
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
and

are both undecidable, they are not equivalent at the level of provability. For instance, the type

(\forall X.X\Rightarrow Y)\Rightarrow(X\Rightarrow X)\Rightarrow Y

(by the term

Remark 3.2
.

The undecidability of the atomic fragment of (full) second-order intuitionistic logic has been known since (at least)
[

. However, from this one cannot deduce the undecidability of

, due to the fact that disjunction is not faithfully definable in

(see also
[
47
]
).

Remark 3.3
.

It is not difficult to see that System

is equivalent to a first-order system, namely to the

18

4
Typability and Type-checking

In usual implementations of polymorphic type systems the Church-style type discipline is generally considered inconvenient, due to the heavy amount of type annotations. Instead, Curry-style languages, for which a compiler can (either completely or partially) reconstruct type annotations, are generally preferred (two standard examples are the languages
\mathsf{ML}
and
\mathsf{Haskell}
).
This is the reason why type-checking algorithms for polymorphic type systems in Curry-style (or in some variants of Curry-style with
partial
type annotations
[
45
]
) have been extensively investigated
[
24
,
26
,
63
,
18
]
.

However, while
\mathrm{ML}
admits a decidable type checking in Curry-style (a main reason for its success), type checking has been shown to be undecidable
for System F and most of its variants (including the predicative system
{\mathrm{F}_{1}}

[
18
]
), making the Curry-style version of such systems impractical for implementation.

For the simply typed
\lambda
-calculus (and crucially also for
\mathrm{ML}
), the type-checking problem can be reduced to
first-order unification
(FOU), that is, to the problem of unifying first-order terms (in a language with a unique binary function symbol corresponding to
\Rightarrow
). Typically, an application
tu:b
will produce a first-order equation of the form

a_{t}=a_{u}\Rightarrow b
, where
a_{t},a_{u}
are variables indicating the type of
t
and the type of
u
, respectively.
As FOU is decidable, this suffices to show that type-checking is decidable in this case.

In the case of polymorphism FOU is not sufficient to solve type-checking. In fact, already in
{\mathrm{F}_{1}}
one can type terms, like e.g.
\lambda x.xx
, which contain
self-applications
. Using FOU, this term yields the unsolvable equation

a_{x}=a_{x}\Rightarrow b
, showing that
\lambda x.xx
is not typable in either
{\mathrm{ST\lambda C}}
or
\mathrm{ML}
. To type-check System F programs one can replace FOU with either
semi-unification

[
24
,
26
]
or
second order unification
(SOU)
[
45
,
18
]
.
Here we focus on the latter: in SOU one tries to unify equations involving terms constructed from first-order variables
a,b,c,\dots
as well as second order variables
\mathsf{F},\mathsf{G},\dots
. For instance, the term
\lambda x.xx
above yields the equations

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}a

\displaystyle=(\mathsf{F}b)\Rightarrow\mathsf{G}

(1)

where
\forall X.A=\forall X.\mathsf{F}X
indicates the type of
x
, and the variables
a,b

encode the possible witnesses which permit to type
xx
(in Church-style one could indicate this with

\lambda x^{\forall X.\mathsf{F}X}.((xa)^{\mathsf{F}a=\mathsf{F}b\Rightarrow\mathsf{G}}(xb)^{\mathsf{F}b})^{\mathsf{G}}
). For instance, a (non-unique) solution to this problem is given by

\mathsf{F}\mapsto\lambda x.x
,
\mathsf{G}\mapsto Z
,
a\mapsto Y\Rightarrow Z
,
b\mapsto Y
.

Unfortunately, SOU is undecidable
[
22
]
. Moreover, one can encode restricted (but still undecidable) variants of SOU in the type checking problem for
{\mathrm{F}_{1}}

[
18
]
, showing that (TC) is undecidable for
{\mathrm{F}_{1}}
.
A fundamental ingredient of these undecidability arguments is the appeal to
variable cycles
(see the discussion in
[
36
]
) like the one in Eq. (
1
), that is, to unification problems from which one can deduce an equation of the form
\mathsf{F}a_{1}\dots a_{n}=u[\mathsf{F}]
equating a second-order variable
\mathsf{F}
with some term containing
\mathsf{F}
itself.

Conversely,
acyclic
SOU, that is, the problem of unifying SOU problems containing no variable cycles, is decidable
[
36
]
. These observations can be used to show that type-checking is actually decidable in
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
.
In fact, a fundamental property of
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
(and a reason for its very limited expressive power) is that any term typable in
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
is already typable in the simply-typed
\lambda
-calculus. Indeed, the following is easily checked by induction:

Lemma 4.1
.

If
\Gamma\vdash t:A
is derivable in the Curry-style
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
, then
|\Gamma|\vdash t:|A|
is derivable in the simply typed
\lambda
-calculus, where
|A|
is defined by

|X|=o
,
|A\Rightarrow B|=|A|\Rightarrow|B|
,
|\forall X.A|=|A|
, and
|\Gamma|(x)=|\Gamma(x)|
.

An immediate consequence of Lemma
4.1
is that one cannot type
\lambda x.xx
in
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
and, more generally, that any
\lambda
-term that would give rise to a variable cycle cannot be typed in
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
.
Observe that the converse does not hold: from the fact that
|\Gamma|\vdash t:|A|
holds, one cannot deduce
\Gamma\vdash t:A
(take for instance
t=x
,
\Gamma(x)=X
and
A=\forall X.X
).

However, these observations suggest that type checking for
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
can be decided by reasoning in two phases:
to check if
\Gamma\vdash t:A
is derivable in
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
, first check if
|\Gamma|\vdash t:|A|
is derivable in
{\mathrm{ST\lambda C}}
using FOU;
if this first step fails, then the original problem must fail; if the first step succeeds, then the original type-checking problem for
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
yields an instance of (a suitable variant of) acyclic SOU, which must be decidable. By reasoning in this way, one can thus establish:

Theorem 4.2
.

(TC) for Curry-style
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
is decidable.

In App.
A
we describe in detail the decision algorithm for
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
, which is based on a variant of second-order unification, that we call
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
-unification
.
The fundamental idea is to consider SOU problems in a language with first-order
sequence variables

a,b,\dots
and two kinds of second-order variables:
projection variables

\alpha,\beta,\dots
and
type schemes

\mathsf{F},\mathsf{G},\dots
. The intuition is that a term of the form
\alpha a_{1}\dots a_{n}
describes a (skolemized) witness; since the witnesses in
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
are type variables, solving for
\alpha
means associating it with either a constant function or a projection. Instead, a term of the form
\mathsf{F}\mathfrak{a}_{1}\dots\mathfrak{a}_{n}
stands for the application of suitable witnesses
\mathfrak{a}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{a}_{n}
to some type
\mathsf{F}
, hence solving for
\mathsf{F}
means associating it with some function
\lambda X_{1}\dots X_{n}.A(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
, where
A(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
is some type expression parametric on the type variable
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
.
Hence, for example, checking if
\Gamma\vdash xy:\forall Z.Z
holds in
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
, where
\Gamma(x)=\forall X.X\Rightarrow X
and
\Gamma(y)=\forall Y.Y
, yields the equations

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}X

\displaystyle=X\Rightarrow X

\displaystyle\qquad\mathsf{G}Y

\displaystyle=Y

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}(\alpha Z)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{G}(\beta Z)\Rightarrow\mathsf{H}Z

\displaystyle\qquad\mathsf{H}Z

\displaystyle=Z

which admit the solution
\mathsf{F}\mapsto\lambda X.X\Rightarrow X
,
\mathsf{G},\mathsf{H}\mapsto\lambda X.X
and
\alpha,\beta\mapsto\lambda X.X
.
Instead, checking if
\Gamma\vdash xy:\forall Z:Z
, where now
\Gamma(x)=\forall X.X\Rightarrow X
and
\Gamma(y)=Y
, yields the equations

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}X

\displaystyle=X\Rightarrow X

\displaystyle\qquad\mathsf{G}

\displaystyle=Y

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}(\alpha Z)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{G}\Rightarrow\mathsf{H}Z

\displaystyle\qquad\mathsf{H}Z

\displaystyle=Z

which have no solution (since one can deduce
Z=\mathsf{H}Z=Y
), showing that (TC) fails in this case (although
|\Gamma|\vdash xy:|\forall Z.Z|
holds in the simply typed
\lambda
-calculus).

From the decidability of (TC) one can deduce the decidability of (T) by a standard argument: we can reduce (T) to (TC) by showing that a type
A
such that
\Gamma\vdash{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t}:A
holds exists iff
\Gamma\vdash(\lambda xy.y){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t}:\forall X.X\Rightarrow X
holds.
In fact, if
\Gamma\vdash{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t}:A
holds in
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
, then from
\Gamma\vdash\lambda xy.y:A\Rightarrow\forall X.(X\Rightarrow X)
we deduce

\Gamma\vdash(\lambda xy.y){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t}:\forall X.X\Rightarrow X
. Conversely, from
\Gamma\vdash(\lambda xy.y){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t}:\forall X.X\Rightarrow X
, we deduce that there exists a type
A
such that

\Gamma\vdash\lambda xy.y:A\Rightarrow(X\Rightarrow X)
and

\Gamma\vdash{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t}:A
holds.

Corollary 4.1
.

(T) for Curry-style
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
is decidable.

5
Equational Reasoning in System
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}

As a consequence of Lemma
4.1
from the previous section, all terms which are typable in Curry-style
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
are simply typable. In other words,
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
can be seen as a
type refinement
system for
{\mathrm{ST\lambda C}}
, in the sense of
[
39
]
.
In particular, as we show below, the numerical functions which can be typed in
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
are precisely the simply typable ones (i.e. the so-called
extended polynomials

[
54
,
16
]
).

For this reason, investigating the typable terms of
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
might seem not very interesting from a computational viewpoint.
However, in this section we show that studying such terms can be interesting for equational reasoning. In fact, similarly to System F, standard notions of contextual equivalence are stronger than
\beta\eta
-equivalence, and one can exploit well-known techniques like
free theorems

[
62
]
, to compute equivalences of
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
-typable terms (which do not hold when viewing these terms as typed in
{\mathrm{ST\lambda C}}
).

We first recall two standard notions of contextual equivalence:

Notation 1
.

We let
\mathsf{Bool}=\forall X.X\Rightarrow X\Rightarrow X
and
\mathsf{Nat}=\forall X.(X\Rightarrow X)\Rightarrow(X\Rightarrow X)
. We let
\mathbf{t}=\lambda xy.x
and
\mathbf{f}=\lambda xy.y
indicate the two normal forms of type
\mathsf{Bool}
, and
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we let
\mathbf{n}=\lambda fx.(f)^{n}x
indicate the
n
-th Church numeral.

Definition 5.1

(contextual equivalence)
.

Let
F^{*}\in\{{\mathrm{F_{at}}},\mathrm{ML},{\mathrm{F}_{1}},\mathrm{F}\}
.
For all closed terms
t,u
of type
A
in
F^{*}
, we let

•

t\simeq_{\mathsf{Bool}}^{F^{*}}u:A
iff for any context
\mathtt{C}[\ ]:A\vdash\mathsf{Bool}
in
F^{*}
,
\mathtt{C}[t]\simeq_{\beta\eta}\mathtt{C}[u]
;

•

t\simeq_{\mathsf{Nat}}^{F*}u:A
iff for any context
\mathtt{C}[\ ]:A\vdash\mathsf{Nat}
in
F^{*}
,
\mathtt{C}[t]\simeq_{\beta\eta}\mathtt{C}[u]
.

It is easily seen that
\simeq_{\mathsf{Bool}}^{F^{*}}
and
\simeq_{\mathsf{Nat}}^{F^{*}}
are congruences of the terms of
F^{*}
. Moreover, in System F these two congruences coincide, due to the fact that the identity relation
\mathtt{id}:\mathsf{Nat}\Rightarrow\mathsf{Nat}\Rightarrow\mathsf{Bool}
is typable.
Since this function is also typable in
\mathrm{ML}
, the same holds for
\mathrm{ML}
and
{\mathrm{F}_{1}}
.
On the other hand, since the identity relation is
not
simply typable, we can deduce (see Lemma
5.4
below) that it is not typable in
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
. For this reason the congruences
\simeq_{\mathsf{Bool}}^{{\mathrm{F_{at}}}}
and
\simeq_{\mathsf{Nat}}^{{\mathrm{F_{at}}}}
must be treated separately. In what follows we will mostly focus on the latter, since the former identifies distinct normal forms of type
\mathsf{Nat}
, which is not convenient for obvious computational reasons.

Remark 5.1
.

The typability of the identity relation
\mathtt{id}
implies that any extensional model must be
infinite
, since for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the interpretations of
\mathbf{n}
and
\mathbf{n+1}
cannot coincide.
Instead, it is not difficult to construct an extensional model of
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
in which any type is interpreted by a
finite
set (to give an idea, let
\mathcal{C}_{k}
be a collection of sets of cardinality bounded by a fixed
k\in\mathbb{N}
; one can let then
\llbracket X\rrbracket\in\mathcal{C}_{k}
,
A\Rightarrow B=\llbracket B\rrbracket^{\llbracket A\rrbracket}
and

\llbracket\forall X.A\rrbracket=\prod_{S\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}\llbracket A\rrbracket[X\mapsto S]
).

The so-called free theorems are a class of syntactic equations for typable terms which can be justified by relying on either relational parametricity
[
52
]
or dinaturality
[
3
]
. We let
t\approx u:A
indicate that
t,u
have type
A
in System F, and that the equivalence
t\simeq u
can be deduced using
\beta
-,
\eta
-rules, standard congruence rules (i.e. reflexivity, symmetry, transitivity and context closure), as well as instances of free theorems for System F.

Free theorems can be used to deduce contextual equivalence of
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
-terms, thanks to the following:

Lemma 5.1

(free theorems in
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
)
.

Let
t,u
be terms of type
A
in
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
. If
t\approx u:A
, where
t,u
are seen as terms of System F, then

t\simeq_{\mathsf{Nat}}^{{\mathrm{F_{at}}}}u:A
.

Proof.

From
t\approx u:A
it follows
t\simeq_{\mathsf{Nat}}^{\mathrm{F}}u:A
, since
\simeq_{\mathsf{Nat}}^{\mathrm{F}}
is the coarsest congruence not equating normal forms of type
\mathsf{Nat}
. From
t\simeq_{\mathsf{Nat}}^{\mathrm{F}}u:A
we deduce

t\simeq_{\mathsf{Nat}}^{{\mathrm{F_{at}}}}u:A
, since any context in
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
is a context in F.
∎

We discuss below two applications of free theorems to study (CE) in
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
.

Categorical Products and Coproducts

As mentioned in Section
2
, the usual encoding of products and coproducts in System F preserves
\beta
-equivalence but not
\eta
-equivalence.
For this reason, the encodings of
\times
and
+
do not form categorical products and coproducts in System F up to
\beta\eta
-equivalence (more precisely, in the syntactic category in which objects are the types of System F and arrows are the typable terms up to
\simeq_{\beta\eta}
).
Instead, it is well-known
[
50
,
23
,
60
]
that
\eta
-equivalence of
\times
and
+
is preserved in System F up to free theorems: hence
\times
and
+
do form categorical products and coproducts in System F up to
\simeq_{\mathsf{Nat}}^{\mathrm{F}}
(more precisely, in the syntactic category whose arrows are the typable terms up to
\simeq_{\mathsf{Nat}}^{\mathrm{F}}
).

In a similar way, the predicative encodings of
\times
and
+
in
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
, although preserving some restricted case of
\eta
-equivalence, still do not form categorical products and coproducts in
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
up to
\simeq_{\beta\eta}
. We will show that they similarly do form categorical products and coproducts in
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
up to
\simeq_{\mathsf{Nat}}^{{\mathrm{F_{at}}}}
, as a consequence of the application of free theorems.

For simplicity, we here only consider the case of
+
. However, our argument scales straightforwardly to the encoding of all finite polynomial types, i.e. of all types of the form
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\prod_{j=1}^{k_{i}}A_{ij}
(see the pre-print
[
47
]
for a more detailed discussion).

The fundamental step is showing that the impredicative and predicative encodings are equivalent up to free theorems:

Lemma 5.2
.

For all types
A,B,C
and terms
x\mapsto A\vdash u:C
and
x\mapsto B\vdash v:C
, the equivalence

\mathsf{IO}^{+}_{C}[y](\lambda x.u)(\lambda x.v)\approx\mathrm{Case}_{C}(y,x.u,x.v):C
holds in System
\mathrm{F}
.

Proof.

The free theorem associated with the type
A\widetilde{+}B
is the schematic equation

\mathrm{Case}_{E}(t_{1},x.\mathtt{C}[t_{2}],x.\mathtt{C}[t_{3}])\approx\mathtt{C}\Big[\mathrm{Case}_{D}(t_{1},x.t_{2},x.t_{3})\Big]

(2)

where
t_{1}:A\widetilde{+}B
,
t_{2}[x:A]:D
,
t_{2}[x:B]:D
and
\mathtt{C}[\ ]:D\vdash E
. In fact, this equation is an instance of the
dinaturality
condition for the type
A\widetilde{+}B
(see
[
50
,
23
,
49
]
).

We argue by induction on
C
:

•

if
C=Y
, then
\mathsf{IO}^{+}_{C}[y](\lambda x.u)(\lambda x.v)=yY(\lambda x.u)(\lambda x.v)=\mathrm{Case}_{C}(y,x.u,x.v)
;

•

if
C=C_{1}\Rightarrow C_{2}
, then

\displaystyle\mathsf{IO}^{+}_{C}[y](\lambda x.u)(\lambda x.v)

\displaystyle=\Big(\lambda fgz.\mathsf{IO}^{+}_{C_{2}}[y](\lambda x.fxz)(\lambda x.gxz)\Big)(\lambda x.u)(\lambda x.v)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{[I.H.]}}}{{\approx}}\Big(\lambda fgz.\mathrm{Case}_{C_{2}}(y,x.fxz,x.gxz)\Big)(\lambda x.u)(\lambda x.v)

\displaystyle\simeq_{\beta}\lambda z.\mathrm{Case}_{C_{2}}(y,x.uz,x.vz)

\displaystyle\approx\lambda z.\Big(\mathrm{Case}_{C}(y,x.u,x.v)\Big)z

\displaystyle\simeq_{\eta}\mathrm{Case}_{C}(y,x.u,x.v)

where in the penultimate step we applied Eq. (
2
) with the context
\mathtt{C}[\ ]=[\ ]z:C\vdash C_{2}
.

•

if
C=\forall Z.C^{\prime}
, then

\displaystyle\mathsf{IO}^{+}_{C}[y](\lambda x.u)(\lambda x.v)

\displaystyle=\Big(\lambda fg.\Lambda Z.\mathsf{IO}^{+}_{C^{\prime}}[y](\lambda x.fxZ)(\lambda x.gxZ)\Big)(\lambda x.u)(\lambda x.v)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{[I.H.]}}}{{\approx}}\Big(\lambda fg.\Lambda Z.\mathrm{Case}_{C^{\prime}}(y,x.fxZ,x.gxZ)\Big)(\lambda x.u)(\lambda x.v)

\displaystyle\simeq_{\beta}\Lambda Z.\mathrm{Case}_{C^{\prime}}(y,x.uZ,x.vZ)

\displaystyle\approx\Lambda Z.\Big(\mathrm{Case}_{C}(y,x.u,x.v)\Big)Z

\displaystyle\simeq_{\eta}\mathrm{Case}_{C}(y,x.u,x.v)

where in the penultimate step we applied Eq. (
2
) with the context
\mathtt{C}[\ ]=[\ ]Z:C\vdash C^{\prime}
.

∎

Proposition 5.3
.

A\widetilde{+}B
is a categorical coproduct in
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
up to
\simeq_{\mathsf{Nat}}^{{\mathrm{F_{at}}}}
.

Proof.

It suffices to check that the
\eta
-rule of the coproduct
(see
[
29
]
) holds in
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
. By translating this rule in F one obtains the equation

y\approx\mathrm{Case}_{A\widetilde{+}B}(y,x.\iota_{1}(x),x.\iota_{2}(x)):A\widetilde{+}B

which holds in F up to free theorems (see
[
50
,
23
,
60
]
).
Using Lemma
5.2
we thus deduce that
y\approx\mathsf{IO}^{+}_{A\widetilde{+}B}[y](\lambda x.\iota_{1}(x))(\lambda x.\iota_{2}(x)):A\widetilde{+}B
holds in F, and by Lemma
5.1
we deduce

y\simeq_{\mathsf{Nat}}^{{\mathrm{F_{at}}}}\mathsf{IO}^{+}_{A\widetilde{+}B}[y](\lambda x.\iota_{1}(x))(\lambda x.\iota_{2}(x)):A\widetilde{+}B
.
∎

Numerical Functions

We now consider the representable numerical functions, that is, the typable terms of type

\mathsf{Nat}\Rightarrow\dots\Rightarrow\mathsf{Nat}\Rightarrow\mathsf{Nat}
. In this case we can strengthen Lemma
4.1
:

Lemma 5.4
.

For any
\beta
-normal
\lambda
-term
t
,

\vdash t:\mathsf{Nat}\Rightarrow\dots\Rightarrow\mathsf{Nat}\Rightarrow\mathsf{Nat}
holds in Curry-style
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
iff

\vdash t:|\mathsf{Nat}|\Rightarrow\dots\Rightarrow|\mathsf{Nat}|\Rightarrow|\mathsf{Nat}|
holds in
{\mathrm{ST\lambda C}}
.

Proof.

One direction follows from Lemma
4.1
. For the converse one, let
t
(which we can suppose w.l.o.g. to be of the form
\lambda x_{1}\dots x_{k}.u
) be such that
\vdash t:|\mathsf{Nat}|\Rightarrow\dots\Rightarrow|\mathsf{Nat}|\Rightarrow|\mathsf{Nat}|
. By letting
\mathsf{Nat}[X]=(X\Rightarrow X)\Rightarrow(X\Rightarrow X)
we deduce that

\{x_{i}\mapsto\mathsf{Nat}[X]\}\vdash u:\mathsf{Nat}[X]
holds in
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
, and thus that

\{x_{i}\mapsto\mathsf{Nat}\}\vdash u:\mathsf{Nat}[X]
holds too, from which we conclude

\vdash u:\mathsf{Nat}\Rightarrow\dots\Rightarrow\mathsf{Nat}\Rightarrow\mathsf{Nat}
.
∎

A consequence of Lemma
5.4
is that the representable numerical functions in
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
are precisely the extended polynomials, i.e. the smallest class of functions arising from projections, constant functions, addition, multiplication and the
\mathtt{iszero}
function.
Instead, it is well-known that the predecessor function (which is not an extended polynomial) is
typable in
\mathrm{ML}

[
17
]
and, more generally, the representable functions of
\mathrm{ML}

are included in the class
\mathcal{E}_{3}
of the Grzegorczyk hierarchy
[
33
]
.

Still, in both
{\mathrm{ST\lambda C}}
and
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
the same extended polynomial can be represented by different normal forms. For instance the two normal forms
\lambda xyfz.x(yf)z
and
\lambda xyfz.y(xf)z
(encoding the algorithms
n,m\mapsto\underbrace{m+\dots+m}_{n\text{ times}}
and
n,m\mapsto\underbrace{n+\dots+n}_{m\text{ times}}
) both represent the multiplication function.

In System F, one can show that all primitive recursive functions are
uniquely defined
up to free theorems, that is, that for any two terms
t,u
representing the same primitive recursive function, one can prove
t\approx u
(see
[
48
]
, Section 7.5). Using Lemma
5.1
we deduce then:

Lemma 5.5
.

For all
t,u:\mathsf{Nat}\Rightarrow\dots\Rightarrow\mathsf{Nat}\Rightarrow\mathsf{Nat}
in
\mathrm{F}^{*}\in\{{\mathrm{F_{at}}},\mathrm{ML},{\mathrm{F}_{1}}\}
, if for all
p_{1},\dots,p_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
,

t\mathbf{p}_{1}\dots\mathbf{p}_{k}\simeq_{\beta\eta}u\mathbf{p}_{1}\dots\mathbf{p}_{k}:\mathsf{Nat}
, then
t\simeq_{\mathsf{Nat}}^{\mathrm{F}^{*}}u
.

The problem
\mathrm{Eq}_{\mathcal{C}}
of deciding
f=g
, where
f,g
belong to some subclass
\mathcal{C}
of the primitive recursive functions, is well-investigated. In particular, it is known that:

•

if
\mathcal{C}
is the class of extended polynomials, then
\mathrm{Eq}_{\mathcal{C}}
is decidable
[
38
]
;

•

if
\mathcal{C}
contains projections, constants, +,
\times
and bounded multiplication, then
\mathrm{Eq}_{\mathcal{C}}
is undecidable
[
31
]
.

From these facts, using Lemma
5.5
, we deduce then:

Proposition 5.6
.

i.

The problem of deciding
\simeq_{\mathsf{Nat}}^{{\mathrm{F_{at}}}}
over numerical functions in
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
is decidable.

ii.

The problem of deciding
\simeq_{\mathsf{Nat}}^{\mathrm{F}^{*}}
over numerical functions in
\mathrm{F}^{*}\in\{\mathrm{ML},{\mathrm{F}_{1}}\}
is undecidable.

Proof.

i. is immediate from Lemma
5.4
and Lemma
5.5
. To prove ii. it suffices to show that the representable functions in
\mathrm{ML}
are closed under bounded multiplication. We show this fact in detail in App.
B
.
∎

An immediate corollary is that (CE) is undecidable in both
\mathrm{ML}
and
{\mathrm{F}_{1}}
.

6
Contextual Equivalence is Undecidable

In this section we show that the congruences

To do this, we will reduce the type inhabitation problem for a suitable extension of
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
to contextual equivalence.
We discuss in some detail the undecidability argument for

Let
{\mathrm{F_{at}^{\clubsuit}}}

{\mathrm{F_{at}}}

{\widetilde{\top}}:\forall X.X\Rightarrow X

\beta

{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
iff

is inhabited in
{\mathrm{F_{at}^{\clubsuit}}}
.

Let us fix a type

of
{\mathrm{F_{at}^{\clubsuit}}}
,

.

For all

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathtt{K}}[\ ]:(A^{*}\widetilde{+}\widetilde{\top})\Rightarrow\mathsf{Bool}\vdash^{x\mapsto Z,x^{\prime}\mapsto Z}Z

-normal term context. Then either
A
is inhabited in
{\mathrm{F_{at}^{\clubsuit}}}
or
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathtt{K}}[\ ]\in\mathbb{G}_{1}

Proof.

We will prove the following claim: either there exists contexts
\Gamma,\Theta,\Delta,\Sigma
, where

\displaystyle\Gamma

\displaystyle=\{x_{1}\mapsto Z_{1},x^{\prime}_{1}\mapsto Z_{1},\dots,x_{p}\mapsto Z_{p},x^{\prime}_{p}\mapsto Z_{p}\}

\displaystyle\Theta

\displaystyle=\{w_{1}\mapsto W_{1},\dots,w_{q}\mapsto W_{q}\}

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle=\{y_{1}\mapsto A^{*}\Rightarrow Y_{1},\dots,y_{r}\mapsto A^{*}\Rightarrow Y_{r}\}

\displaystyle\Sigma

\displaystyle=\{z_{1}\mapsto\widetilde{\top}\Rightarrow Y_{1},\dots,z_{r}\mapsto\widetilde{\top}\Rightarrow Y_{r}\}

for some

and variables

pairwise distinct and disjoint from

,
and a context
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathtt{H}}[\ ]:(A^{*}\widetilde{+}\widetilde{\top})\Rightarrow\mathsf{Bool}\vdash^{\Gamma,\Theta,\Delta,\Sigma}A^{*}

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathtt{K}}[\ ]\in\mathbb{G}_{1}

If the main claim is true we can deduce the statement of the lemma as follows: suppose
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathtt{K}}[\ ]\notin\mathbb{G}_{1}

by

1.

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathtt{K}}[\ ]\in\mathbb{G}_{1}
;

2.

for all
\Gamma,\Theta,\Delta,\Sigma
as above, if
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathtt{K}}[\ ]:(A^{*}\widetilde{+}\widetilde{\top})\Rightarrow\mathsf{Bool}\vdash^{\Gamma,\Theta,\Delta,\Sigma}Y_{i}

3.

for all
\Gamma,\Theta,\Delta,\Sigma
as above, if

is an elimination context, then
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathtt{K}}[\ ]\in\mathbb{G}_{3}
;

4.

for all
\Gamma,\Theta,\Delta,\Sigma
as above, if
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathtt{K}}[\ ]:(A^{*}\widetilde{+}\widetilde{\top})\Rightarrow\mathsf{Bool}\vdash^{\Gamma,\Theta,\Delta,\Sigma}W_{i}

.

.

The congruences
\simeq_{\mathsf{Bool}}^{{\mathrm{F_{at}}}}
and

Conclusion

Related works

The literature on ML-polymorphism, both at theoretical and applicative level, is vast. Several extensions of ML to account for first-class polymorphism while retaining a decidable type-checking have been investigated, mostly following two directions: first, that of considering type systems with explicit type annotations (as the system
\mathsf{PolyML}

[
20
]
); second, that of encoding first-class polymorphism in a ML-style system by means of
coercions
(as in System
\mathrm{Fc}

[
59
]
or in in
\mathrm{ML}^{\mathrm{F}}

[
30
]
).
In the last case, coherently with our discussion on FOU and SOU, the price to pay to remain decidable is that self-applications of
\lambda
-abstracted variables must come with explicit type annotations.
This approach is currently followed in the design of the Haskell compiler, which supports first-class polymorphism.

Predicative restrictions of System F and their expressive power have been also largely investigated
[
32
,
33
,
6
]
. For example, the numerical functions representable in Leivant’s finitely stratified polymorphism are precisely those at the 3rd level of Grzegorczyk’s hierarchy
[
33
]
, and transfinitely stratified systems have been shown to represent all primitive recursive functions
[
6
]
. In
[
34
]
a system with expressive power comparable to System
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
is shown to characterize the polytime functions.

Research by Ferreira and her collaborators on System
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
has mostly focused on predicative translations of intuitionistic logic and their reduction properties
[
12
,
11
,
10
]
. As mentioned before, these translations rely on the observation that for certain types the unrestricted
\forall
E-rule is admissible in
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
. The characterization of the class of types satisfying this property is an open problem (a partial characterization is described in
[
46
]
).

Another way to obtain interesting subsystems of System F is by restricting the class of types which can be universally quantified (instead of the admissible witnesses). For instance, the system in
[
2
]
forbids quantifier nestings, while the system in
[
35
]
only allows quantification
\forall X.A
when
X
occurs at depth at most 2 in
A
(i.e. when
X
occurs at most twice to the left of an implication). Interestingly, both systems have the expressive power of Gödel’s System T (which is not a first-order system).

Another kind of restrictions on the shape of types have been investigated by the authors in
[
49
,
48
]
, motivated by ideas from the categorical semantics of polymorphism
[
3
]
. The two resulting fragments
\mathrm{F}^{\kappa\leq 0},\mathrm{F}^{\kappa\leq 1}
are equivalent, respectively, to the simply typed
\lambda
-calculus with finite sums and products, and to its extension with least and greatest fixpoints (in particular, (CE) is decidable in
\mathrm{F}^{\kappa\leq 0}
).

Finally, polymorphism in
linear
type systems has been investigated too. Interestingly, (TI)
[
28
,
27
]
and (CE)
[
43
]
remain undecidable even in this case.

Future work

The main interest we found in investigating
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
was to shed some new light on the source of undecidability of type-related properties for full System F. Yet, one might well ask whether the decidability of type-checking makes
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
a reasonable candidate for implementations.
Admittedly, our decision algorithm, which was only oriented to prove decidability, is not very practical: checking failure is
\mathsf{coNP}
with respect to the number of type symbols. Yet, it does not seems unlikely that more optimized algorithms can be developed.

By the way, given that the terms typable in
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
are simply typable, would an implementation of atomic polymorphism be interesting at all?
In contrast with
\mathrm{ML}
, type-checking atomically polymorphic programs is decidable at any rank.
One could thus investigate extensions of
\mathrm{ML}
with first class atomic polymorphism (realistically, in presence of other type constructors like e.g. some restricted version of dependent types, see
[
64
]
).

A more interesting direction, suggested by our decision algorithm, would be to investigate systems with full, impredicative, polymorphism, but obeying some condition ensuring
acyclicity
, so that TC (based on SOU) remains decidable. One would thus retain some advantages of first-class polymorphism (e.g. the modularity/genericity of programs) while admitting self-applications only in “
\mathrm{ML}
-style” (or with explicit type annotations, as in
\mathrm{ML}^{\mathrm{F}}

[
30
]
).
For instance, a way to ensure acycliclity might be to require that a polymorphic
\lambda
-abstracted variable be used in an
affine
way, i.e. at most once.

Appendix B
Bounded Multiplication in
\mathrm{ML}

We show that the numerical functions representable in
\mathrm{ML}
are closed under bounded multiplication: if the function
g:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
is representable, then also the function
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
defined by
f(n)=\prod_{k\leq n}g(k)
is representable.

The first step is to define an encoding of pairs inside
{\mathrm{ST\lambda C}}
. Let
A,B
be two simple types, and let
\langle A,B\rangle:=(A\Rightarrow B\Rightarrow o)\Rightarrow o
. We let
\langle t,u\rangle
be the term
\lambda f.ftu
. We define the projections

\mathsf{P}_{1}^{A}:\langle A,B\rangle\Rightarrow A
and
\mathsf{P}_{2}^{B}:\langle A,B\rangle\Rightarrow B
by essentially following the predicative encoding of products in
{\mathrm{F_{at}}}
. We first define a term
\mathsf{T}^{C}:\langle A,B\rangle\Rightarrow(A\Rightarrow B\Rightarrow C)\Rightarrow C
by induction on a simple type
C
as follows:

•

if
C=o
, then
\mathsf{T}^{C}=\lambda gh.gh
;

•

if
C=C_{1}\Rightarrow C_{2}
, then
\mathsf{T}^{A}=\lambda ghz.\mathsf{T}^{A_{2}}g(\lambda ab.habz)
.

We finally let
\mathsf{P}_{1}^{A}:=\mathsf{T}^{A}\lambda xy.x
and
\mathsf{P}_{2}^{B}=\mathsf{T}^{B}\lambda xy.y
.
This definition can be straightforwardly extended to
n
-ary products
\langle A_{1},\dots,A_{n}\rangle
, with projection functions
\mathsf{P}_{i,n}^{A_{i}}:\langle A_{1},\dots,A_{n}\rangle\Rightarrow A_{i}
