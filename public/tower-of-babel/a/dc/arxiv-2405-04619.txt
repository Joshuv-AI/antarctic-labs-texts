Non-anomalous non-invertible symmetries in 1+1D from gapped boundaries of SymTFTs
Pavel Putrov, and Rajath Radhakrishnan

Abstract
We study the anomalies of non-invertible symmetries in 1+1D QFTs using gapped boundaries of its SymTFT. We establish the explicit relation between Lagrangian algebras which determine gapped boundaries of the SymTFT, and algebras which determine non-anomalous/gaugeable topological line operators in the 1+1D QFT. If the Lagrangian algebras in the SymTFT are known, this provides a method to compute algebras in all fusion categories that share the same SymTFT. We find necessary conditions that a line operator in the SymTFT must satisfy for the corresponding line operator in the 1+1D QFT to be non-anomalous. We use this constraint to show that a non-invertible symmetry admits a 1+1D trivially gapped phase if and only if the SymTFT admits a magnetic Lagrangian algebra. We define a process of transporting non-anomalous line operators between fusion categories which share the same SymTFT and apply this method to the three Haagerup fusion categories.

6.3

Consider two fusion categories
\mathcal{C}

\mathcal{D}

\mathcal{D}

\mathcal{C}

\mathcal{C}
could be a fusion category without multiplicity in its fusion coefficients while
\mathcal{D}
is not multiplicity-free.

Let
[B_{1}],...[B_{n}]
be the
n

\mathcal{D}
for some integer
n

of the SymTFT

\mathcal{D}

\mathcal{C}

\mathcal{C}

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we studied the non-anomalous line operators in a 1+1D QFT with fusion category symmetry
\mathcal{C}
using gapped boundaries of the SymTFT
\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{C})
. Physically equivalent gaugings in 1+1D correspond to the same gapped boundary of the SymTFT. Our analysis determines the explicit map between them.

•

Given a Lagrangian algebra object
L
in
\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{C})
the corresponding physically equivalent gaugings in
\mathcal{C}
can be found from
F(L)
, where
F
is the bulk-to-boundary map. (Theorem
4.1
).

•

In fact,
F
maps the algebra structure of
L
to the line operator
F(L)
through equation (
32
).

•

The explicit examples considered in this paper show that in some cases Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
can be used to determine all non-anomalous line operators in
\mathcal{C}
even without the knowledge of the algebra structure on the Lagrangian algebras in
\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{C})
.

•

Conversely, knowledge of non-anomalous line operators in
\mathcal{C}
puts strong constraints on which line operators in the bulk SymTFT can form a Lagrangian algebra. We used this to find a necessary condition for a line operator
A\in\mathcal{C}
to be non-anomalous. This was then used to prove that
\mathcal{C}
-symmetric trivially gapped phases exist if and only if the bulk SymTFT admits a magnetic Lagrangian algebra.

•

We discussed the notion of transporting non-anomalous line operators between fusion categories which share the same SymTFT. This method can be used to determine non-anomalous line operators in a fusion category from those in a “simpler” fusion category. This provides an alternative to using NIM-reps to determine algebra objects in a fusion category
\mathcal{C}
.

The following are some interesting future directions:

•

It will be interesting tp generalize the analysis in this paper to SymTFTs for fermionic symmetries
Wen:2024udn
.

•

Generalization to higher dimensions. It will be interesting to apply the ideas presented here to the setting in
Putrov:2023jqi
;
Antinucci:2023ezl
;
Cordova:2023bja
;
Argurio:2024oym
.

•

General relation between anomalies and gapped boundaries for SymTFTs of non-finite and/or continuous symmetries
kong2017boundary
;
Putrov:2022pua
;
Brennan:2024fgj
;
Antinucci:2024zjp
;
Apruzzi:2024htg
;
Bonetti:2024cjk
.

•

For an invertible non-anomalous symmetry
G
, the discrete torsion takes values in
H^{2}(G,U(1))
. It would be interesting to explore the structure of generalized discrete torsions for a fixed non-anomalous line operator using gapped boundaries of the SymTFT.

•

In
Cordova:2024vsq
, the authors show that massive 1+1D QFTs with spontaneously broken non-invertible symmetries often have particle degeneracies. A crucial part of finding the degeneracies involves determining the kernel and cokernel of certain maps relating different 1D gapped boundaries of the QFT. It will be interesting to use the map from gapped interfaces between 1+1D gapped boundaries of
\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{C})
and 1D gapped boundaries of 1+1D QFTs/TQFTs studied in this work to give a bulk perspective on the results in

\sum_{\operatorname{\text{m}}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}}A_{\operatorname{\text{m}}}=4\mathbf{1}+4\nu+3\eta+2\mu~.

(361)

For the module category
\mathcal{M}_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle A_{\operatorname{\text{m}}_{1}}=\mathbf{1}+\mu+\nu,

(362)

\displaystyle A_{\operatorname{\text{m}}_{2}}=\mathbf{1}+2\nu+\eta,

(363)

\displaystyle A_{\operatorname{\text{m}}_{3}}=\mathbf{1}+\mu+\nu,

(364)

\displaystyle A_{\operatorname{\text{m}}_{4}}=\mathbf{1}+\mu+\nu~.

(365)

Therefore, we get

\sum_{\operatorname{\text{m}}\in\mathcal{M}_{2}}A_{\operatorname{\text{m}}}=4\mathbf{1}+5\nu+\eta+3\mu~.

(366)

Finally, for the module category
\mathcal{M}_{3}
, we have

\displaystyle A_{\operatorname{\text{m}}_{1}}=\mathbf{1}+\eta,

(367)

\displaystyle A_{\operatorname{\text{m}}_{2}}=\mathbf{1}+\eta,

(368)

\displaystyle A_{\operatorname{\text{m}}_{3}}=\mathbf{1}+\mu+3\nu+2\eta,

(369)

\displaystyle A_{\operatorname{\text{m}}_{4}}=\mathbf{1}+\eta~.

(370)

Therefore, we get

\sum_{\operatorname{\text{m}}\in\mathcal{M}_{3}}A_{\operatorname{\text{m}}}=4\mathbf{1}+3\nu+5\eta+\mu~.

(371)

E.2
Module categories over
\mathscr{H}_{2}

Consider the Haagerup fusion category
\mathscr{H}_{2}
with simple line operators

\mathbf{1},\alpha,\alpha^{2},\rho,\alpha\rho,\alpha^{2}\rho~.

(372)

There are three module categories
\mathcal{M}_{1}
,
\mathcal{M}_{2}
and
\mathcal{M}_{3}
over
\mathscr{H}_{2}
given by the category of modules over the algebras
\mathbf{1}
,
\mathbf{1}+\rho
and
\mathbf{1}+\alpha+\alpha^{2}
, respectively .

The module category
\mathcal{M}_{1}
contains 4 objects, say
\operatorname{\text{m}}_{1},\dots,\operatorname{\text{m}}_{4}
. We have

\displaystyle A_{\operatorname{\text{m}}_{1}}=\mathbf{1}+\rho,

(373)

\displaystyle A_{\operatorname{\text{m}}_{2}}=\mathbf{1}+\alpha\rho,

(374)

\displaystyle A_{\operatorname{\text{m}}_{3}}=\mathbf{1}+\alpha^{2}\rho,

(375)

\displaystyle A_{\operatorname{\text{m}}_{4}}=\mathbf{1}+\alpha+\alpha^{2}+2H~.

(376)

Therefore, we get

\sum_{\operatorname{\text{m}}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}}A_{\operatorname{\text{m}}}=4\mathbf{1}+\alpha+\alpha^{2}+3H~.

(377)

The module category
\mathcal{M}_{2}
is the regular module with
6
objects corresponding to the simple objects in
\mathscr{H}_{2}
.
Using the graphs in
(
grossman2012quantum
, Corollary 3.16)
(see also
(
Huang:2021ytb
, Section 7)
), we can read off the following

\displaystyle A_{1}=A_{\alpha}=A_{\alpha^{2}}=\mathbf{1}~,

(378)

\displaystyle A_{\rho}=\mathbf{1}+H,

(379)

\displaystyle A_{\alpha\rho}=\mathbf{1}+H,

(380)

\displaystyle A_{\alpha^{2}\rho}=\mathbf{1}+H~.

(381)

where
H=\rho+\alpha\rho+\alpha^{2}\rho
. Therefore, we get

\sum_{\operatorname{\text{m}}\in\mathcal{M}_{2}}A_{\operatorname{\text{m}}}=6\mathbf{1}+3H~.

(382)

Finally, the module category
\mathcal{M}_{3}
contains two simple objects, say
\operatorname{\text{m}}_{1}
and
\operatorname{\text{m}}_{2}
.
We have

\displaystyle A_{\operatorname{\text{m}}_{1}}=\mathbf{1}+\alpha+\alpha^{2},

(383)

\displaystyle A_{\operatorname{\text{m}}_{2}}=\mathbf{1}+\alpha+\alpha^{2}+3H~.

(384)

Therefore, we get

\sum_{\operatorname{\text{m}}\in\mathcal{M}_{3}}A_{\operatorname{\text{m}}}=2\mathbf{1}+2\alpha+2\alpha^{2}+3H~.

(385)

E.3
Module categories over
\mathscr{H}_{3}

There are again three module categories
\mathcal{M}_{1},\mathcal{M}_{2}
and
\mathcal{M}_{3}
over
\mathscr{H}_{3}
. The Morita equivalent classes of algebras corresponding to these module categories can be read off from the graphs in
(
grossman2012quantum
, Theorem 3.25)
.

\mathcal{M}_{1}
is a module category with four objects say
\operatorname{\text{m}}_{1},\dots,\operatorname{\text{m}}_{4}
. We have

\displaystyle A_{\operatorname{\text{m}}_{1}}=\mathbf{1}+\alpha\rho+\alpha^{2}\rho,

(386)

\displaystyle A_{\operatorname{\text{m}}_{2}}=\mathbf{1}+\alpha+\alpha^{2}+H,

(387)

\displaystyle A_{\operatorname{\text{m}}_{3}}=\mathbf{1}+\rho+\alpha^{2}\rho,

(388)

\displaystyle A_{\operatorname{\text{m}}_{4}}=\mathbf{1}+\rho+\alpha\rho~.

(389)

Therefore, we get

\sum_{\operatorname{\text{m}}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}}A_{\operatorname{\text{m}}}=4\mathbf{1}+\alpha+\alpha^{2}+3H~.

(390)

Note that even though this sum is the same as (
377
), the individual algebras
A_{\operatorname{\text{m}}_{i}}
that constitute this sum is different in
\mathscr{H}_{2}
and
\mathscr{H}_{3}
.

The structure of the module category
\mathcal{M}_{2}
is the same as in the module category
\mathcal{M}_{3}
in
\mathscr{H}_{2}
. Therefore, we get

\sum_{\operatorname{\text{m}}\in\mathcal{M}_{2}}A_{\operatorname{\text{m}}}=2\mathbf{1}+2\alpha+2\alpha^{2}+3H~.

(391)

Finally,
\mathcal{M}_{3}
is the regular module and the Morita equivalent algebras are the same as in the module category
\mathcal{M}_{2}
in
\mathcal{H}_{2}
. We get

\sum_{\operatorname{\text{m}}\in\mathcal{M}_{3}}A_{\operatorname{\text{m}}}=6\mathbf{1}+3H~.

(392)
