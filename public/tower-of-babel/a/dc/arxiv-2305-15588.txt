A support theorem for exponential metrics of log-correlated Gaussian fields in arbitrary dimension
Andres A. Contreras Hip and Ewain Gwynne University of Chicago

Abstract
Let h h be a log-correlated Gaussian field on ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} , let γ ∈ ( 0 , 2 ​ d ) , \gamma\in(0,\sqrt{2d}), let μ h \mu_{h} be the γ \gamma -Gaussian multiplicative chaos measure, and let D h D_{h} be an exponential metric associated with h h satisfying certain natural axioms. In the special case when d = 2 d=2 , this corresponds to the Liouville quantum gravity (LQG) measure and metric. We show that the closed support of the law of ( D h , μ h ) (D_{h},\mu_{h}) includes all length metrics and probability measures on ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} . That is, if 𝔡 \mathfrak{d} is any length metric on ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} and 𝔪 \mathfrak{m} is any probability measure on ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} , then with positive probability ( D h , μ h ) (D_{h},\mu_{h}) is close to ( 𝔡 , 𝔪 ) (\mathfrak{d},\mathfrak{m}) with respect to the uniform distance and the Prokhorov distance. Key ingredients include a scaling limit theorem for a first passage percolation type model associated with h h , a special version of the white noise decomposition of h h in arbitrary dimension, and an approximation property by conformally flat Riemannian metrics in the uniform sense. Our results provide a robust tool to show that the LQG measure and metric, and its higher dimensional analogs, satisfy certain properties with positive probability.

Introduction

Liouville quantum gravity (LQG) is a family of random metric measure spaces parametrized by Riemann surfaces, depending on a parameter
\gamma\in(0,2)
, which describe the scaling limits of random planar maps.
LQG was first introduced (non-rigourously) by Polyakov in
[
Pol81
]
as a class of canonical models of random surfaces.
One can define LQG surfaces with the topology of any desired orientable surface
[
DMS21
,
DKRV16
,
DRV16
,
Rem18
,
GRV19
]
. However, in this paper we will only consider LQG surfaces with the topology of the whole plane.

The main results of this paper describe the closed support of the law of the LQG metric measure space. More precisely, we show that if
\mathfrak{d}
is any length metric on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\mathfrak{m}
is any probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, then with positive probability
(D_{h},\mu_{h})
is close to
(\mathfrak{d},\mathfrak{m})
with respect to the uniform distance and the Prokhorov distance (Theorems
1.4
and Corollary
1.6
).
Our results provide a convenient “black box” for whenever one needs to show that certain events for LQG hold with positive probability.

Several recent works (see, e.g.,
[
DGZ23
,
DHKS23
,
DHKS21
,
Cer22
]
) have studied analogs of LQG in dimension
d\geq 3
. The results of this paper also extend to this higher-dimensional setting, conditional on some properties of the higher-dimensional analog of the LQG metric whose proofs have not yet been written down. In this setting, our results raise important questions about what it means for a random Riemannian metric to be “conformally flat”; see Section
1.3
for more details.

Roughly speaking, the aforementioned works on higher-dimensional analogs of LQG consider a conformally flat Riemannian metric tensor on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 3
, of the form

e^{\gamma h}(dx_{1}^{2}+\dots+dx_{d}^{2}),

(1.1)

where
\gamma\in(0,\sqrt{2d})
and
h
is a log-correlated Gaussian field on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, or some variant thereof (the works
[
DHKS21
,
Cer22
]
also consider similar Riemannian metric tensors on
d
-manifolds for even values of
d
).

As in the two-dimensional case, the Riemannian metric tensor (
1.1
) does not make literal sense since
h
is a generalized function and is not pointwise defined. However, one can construct the volume form associated with (
1.1
) as a random measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
via the general theory of Gaussian multiplicative chaos
[
Kah85
,
RV14
,
Ber17
,
BP
]
.

The recent paper
[
DGZ23
]
proves the tightness of a natural approximation scheme for the Riemannian distance function associated with (
1.1
). This paper also shows that every subsequential limit is a metric on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
which induces the Euclidean topology. It is expected, but not yet proven, that the subsequential limit is unique and is characterized by a similar list of axioms as in the two-dimensional case
[
GM21b
]
; see Definition
1.3
. Our results for
d\geq 3
are proven conditional on these axioms.

1.1
Measure and metric definitions

We will now discuss the definitions of the measure and metric associated with the log-correlated Gaussian field
h.

Definition 1.1
.

The
log-correlated Gaussian field
is the centered Gaussian random generalized function
h^{0}
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
up to additive constants, with covariance structure

\operatorname{Cov}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h^{0}(z),h^{0}(w)}}\right)=G(z,w):=\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{|z-w|}}}\right).

(1.2)

That is, if
f
is a smooth compactly supported test function on
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
with
\int_{\partial B_{1}(0)}fd\sigma=0,
then
\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}f(z)h^{0}(z)\,dz
is a centered Gaussian random variable with variance
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(z)f(w)\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{|z-w|}}}\right)\,dz\,dw
.
We define the field
h
to be the field
h^{0}
normalized so that is unit sphere average is zero, i.e.,

h=h^{0}-\int_{\partial B_{1}(0)}h^{0}(z)d\sigma(z).

In dimension
d=2,
the field
h
is the whole-plane Gaussian free field (appropriately normalized) and has the explicit covariance structure given by

\operatorname{Cov}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h(z),h(w)}}\right):=\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\max\{|z|,1\}\max\{|w|,1\}}{|z-w|}}}\right).

(1.3)

For an exposition of the Gaussian free field in
d=2
, see, e.g.,
[
WP20
,
BP
,
She07
]
. For an exposition of log-correlated Gaussian fields in general dimension, see, e.g.,
[
LSSW16
,
DRSV17
]
.

Formally, for
\gamma\in(0,\sqrt{2d})
, the
\gamma
-exponential geometry
associated with
h
is described by the random Riemannian metric tensor

e^{\gamma h}\,(dx_{1}^{2}+\cdots+dx_{d}^{2}),

(1.4)

where
dx_{1}^{2}+\cdots+dx_{d}^{2}
denotes the Euclidean metric tensor on
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
. This metric tensor does not make literal sense since
h
is not a random function but instead it is a random generalized function, and hence
e^{\gamma h}
is not well defined. However, at least in dimension
2,
we can still define a random metric and measure associated with (
1.4
). To do so, we first approximate
\tilde{h}
with well chosen mollifications, then pass to the limit.

One possible construction in dimension
d=2
is as follows. Let
p_{t}
denote the heat kernel in dimension
2,

p_{t}(z):=\frac{1}{2\pi t}e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}}{2t}}.

(1.5)

Define the mollified log-correlated Gaussian field

h_{\varepsilon}(z):=p_{\varepsilon^{2}/2}\ast h(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h(w)p_{t}(z-w)\,dw,

where the integral is interpreted in the distributional sense.
We can then define the
\gamma
-LQG measure
as the a.s. weak limit

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{\gamma^{2}/2}e^{\gamma h_{\varepsilon}(z)}.

(1.6)

This construction is a special case of the theory of
Gaussian multiplicative chaos

[
Kah85
,
RV14
,
Ber17
,
BP
]
. The measure
\mu_{h}
is locally finite, non-atomic, assigns positive mass to every open set, and is mutually singular with respect to Lebesgue measure. This construction is also possible in higher dimensions. We also have analogous properties for the Gaussian multiplicative chaos measure
\mu_{h}:

1.

Almost surely,
\mu_{h}
is a non-atomic Radon measure.

2.

If
U\subset{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
is any deterministic open set, then
\mu_{h}(U)
is given by a measurable function of
h|_{U}.

3.

Almost surely, we have that for any continuous function
f:{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\to\mathbb{R},

e^{\gamma f}\cdot\mu_{h}=\mu_{h+f}.

4.

Almost surely, we have the following statement. Suppose that
\phi
is a composition of dilations, translations, and rotations. Then

\mu_{h\circ\phi+Q\log|\phi^{\prime}|}(A)=\mu_{h}(\phi(A))

for any measurable
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}.

The
\gamma
-LQG metric is defined similarly.
Let
d_{\gamma}>2
be the fractal dimension of
\gamma
-LQG.
Prior to the construction of the LQG metric, this number was shown to arise in various approximations of the LQG metric in
[
DZZ19
,
DG18
]
.
After the metric was constructed, it was shown that
d_{\gamma}
is its Hausdorff dimension
[
GP19
, Corollary 1.7]
.
We note that
d_{\gamma}
is not known explicitly except that
d_{\sqrt{8/3}}=4
.
Let

\xi:=\frac{\gamma}{d_{\gamma}}.

(1.7)

Similarly to (
1.6
), we define the approximating metrics

D_{h}^{\varepsilon}(z,w):=\inf_{P:z\to w}\int_{0}^{1}e^{\xi h_{\varepsilon}(P(t))}|P^{\prime}(t)|\,dt

(1.8)

where the infimum is over all piecewise continuously differentiable paths from
z
to
w
. It was shown in the series of papers
[
DDDF20
,
GM20b
,
DFG+20
,
GM20a
,
GM21b
]
that there are normalizing constants
\{\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
and a random metric
D_{h}
on
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
such that

D_{h}(z,w)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}D_{h}^{\varepsilon}(z,w)

(1.9)

in probability with respect to the topology of uniform convergence on compact subsets of
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\times{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
. The metric
D_{h}
is defined to be the
\gamma
-LQG metric
. The metric
D_{h}
induces the same topology on
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
as the Euclidean metric, but has very different geometric properties. See
[
DDG21
]
for a survey of known results about the LQG metric.

In higher dimensions, the metric measure space
D_{h}
associated with the log-correlated Gaussian field
h
can be defined, but is not yet defined uniquely
[
DGZ23
]
. However, it is conjectured that such a metric can be defined uniquely, and that it satisfies the following “strong” axioms. First, we define length metrics.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
(X,\mathfrak{d})
be a metric space.
For a path
P:[a,b]\to X
, we define the
\mathfrak{d}
-length
of
P
by

\ell_{\mathfrak{d}}(P):=\sup_{T}\sum_{i=1}^{\#T}\mathfrak{d}(P(t_{i}),P(t_{i-1}))

where the supremum is over all partitions
T:a=t_{0}<\dots<t_{\#T}=b
of
[a,b]
.
We say that
\mathfrak{d}
is a
length metric
if for each
x,y\in X
, the distance
\mathfrak{d}(x,y)
is equal to the infimum of the
\mathfrak{d}
-lengths of the
\mathfrak{d}
-continuous paths from
x
to
y
.

Now we will define the Weyl scaling as follows: for any continuous
f:{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\to\mathbb{R}
and any length metric
\mathfrak{d}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
inducing the Euclidean topology, we let

(e^{f}\cdot\mathfrak{d})(x,y)=\inf_{P}\int_{0}^{\ell(P)}e^{f(P(t))}dt

(1.10)

where
\ell(P)
is the
\mathfrak{d}
-length of
P
and the inf is over all paths from
x
to
y
parametrized by
\mathfrak{d}
-length. For any open set
U\subseteq{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
we also define the internal metric
\mathfrak{d}(\cdot,\cdot;U)
by

\mathfrak{d}(x,y;U)=\inf_{P\subseteq U}\ell_{\mathfrak{d}}(P),

where the infimum is taken over paths
P:[0,1]\to U
such that
P(0)=x
and
P(1)=y.

Then we will assume the metric
D_{h}
satisfies the following axioms.

Definition 1.3
.

We say a map from random generalized random functions
h
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to metric measure spaces
(\mathbb{R}^{d},D_{h},\mu_{h})
satisfies the strong LQG axioms if there exist constants
\xi,\gamma
such that the following hold:

1.

Almost surely,
D_{h}
is a length metric.

2.

Let
U
be a deterministic open set. Then the internal metric
D_{h}(\cdot,\cdot;U)
is a measurable function of
h|_{U}.

3.

Weyl scaling: Recall the definition of Weyl scaling (
1.10
).
Then, a.s., for every continuous function
f
, we have
e^{\xi f}\cdot D_{h}=D_{h+f}
.

4.

Coordinate change: let
\lambda>0
and
z_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}.
Then almost surely,

D_{h}(\lambda x+z_{0},\lambda y+z_{0})=D_{h(\lambda\cdot+z_{0})+Q\log\lambda}(x,y),\quad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

where
Q:=\frac{\gamma}{2}+\frac{d}{\gamma}.

Note that the above axioms are satisfied by the LQG metric in dimension
d=2
(see
[
GM21b
]
and
[
DFG+20
]
). It is believed that the above axioms also hold for any subsequential limit obtain in
[
DGZ23
]
for
d\geq 3,
but no proof of this has been written yet.

We will also assume that

\frac{\gamma}{\xi}>d

(1.11)

as in the two dimensional case. We expect this to hold, since one expects
\frac{\gamma}{\xi}=\dim(\mathbb{R}^{d};D_{h})
and
\dim(\mathbb{R}^{d};D_{h})>d
since
D_{h}
’s geometry should be strictly “rougher” than Euclidean. We expect this can be proven rigorously with comparison arguments, but we do not do this here, and is the subject of forthcoming work of the first author and Z. Zhuang.

We will additionally assume that

\mathbb{E}(\mathrm{diam}_{D_{h}}([0,1]^{d})^{\bar{p}})<\infty

(1.12)

for some
\bar{p}>d.
This also holds for the LQG metric in dimension
2
(see
[
DFG+20
, Proposition 3.9]
). The axioms above are all used throughout the paper, while the bounds (
1.11
) and (
1.12
) are technical assumption, only used in Subsection
3.1
and Subsection
3.2
, respectively.

1.2
Main results

Recall that our goal is to determine the closed support of the law of
({\mathbb{R}^{d}},D_{h},\mu_{h})
with respect to some reasonable topology on the space of metric measure spaces, assuming
h
is as in Definition
1.1
.

The LQG metric (and any higher dimensional analogue) is a length metric essentially by construction. The class of length metrics is preserved under various forms of convergence, e.g., uniform convergence
[
BBI01
, Exercise 2.4.19]
and Gromov-Hausdorff convergence
[
BBI01
, Theorem 7.5.1]
. Hence, the LQG metric cannot approximate a metric which is not a length metric. Our first main result implies that this is essentially the only constraint.

We say that
(X,\mathfrak{d})
is
boundedly compact
is each closed bounded subset of
X
is
\mathfrak{d}
-compact.
1
1

1

The reason for imposing the bounded compactness assumption is to make it so that all of the
\mathfrak{d}
-geodesics going between points in
[-R,R]^{d}
are contained in some (
\mathfrak{d}
-dependent) compact set. We expect that Theorem
1.4
is true without the boundedly compact assumption, and that this can be obtained by approximating the restriction to
[-R,R]^{d}
of a length metric which is not boundedly compact by the corresponding restrictions of boundedly compact length metrics. However, for the sake of brevity, we do not carry this out here.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\mathfrak{d}
be a boundedly compact length metric on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
which induces the Euclidean topology and let
\mathfrak{m}
be a locally finite Borel measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Suppose that there exists a map
h\mapsto(\mathbb{R}^{d},D_{h},\mu_{h})
as in Definition
1.3
with
\xi<1,
and suppose
h
is a log-correlated Gaussian field. Let
D_{h},\mu_{h}
be as Definition
1.3
. Then
D_{h}
and
\mu_{h}
approximate the metric
\mathfrak{d}
and the measure
\mathfrak{m}
respectively with positive probability. More precisely, for any
\varepsilon,R>0,
we have with positive probability that

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|D_{h}(x,y)-\mathfrak{d}(x,y)}}\right|<\varepsilon

for all
x,y\in[-R,R]^{d}
and at the same time for any Borel set
A\subseteq[-R,R]^{d}
we have

\mathfrak{m}(A)\leq\mu_{h}(A_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon

and

\mu_{h}(A)\leq\mathfrak{m}(A_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon

where
A_{\varepsilon}:=\{z\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}:\inf_{u\in A}|z-u|<\varepsilon\}.

Perhaps surprisingly, we do not need to assume any relationship between
\mathfrak{d}
and
\mathfrak{m}
in Theorem
1.4
.
Even though
D_{h}
and
\mu_{h}
are closely related to each other (see, e.g.,
[
GS22
]
), there is a positive chance for them to behave quite differently.
A much weaker result in this direction in the case of
d=2
appears as
[
BG22
, Proposition 11.9]
.

In dimension two, Theorem
1.4
can be viewed as a general-purpose theorem for making the LQG measure and metric do things with positive probability.
In this sense, these results are an LQG analog of “support theorems” for various stochastic processes, which characterize the elements of the state space that they can approximate with positive probability. Examples of such support theorems include the classical fact that
d
-dimensional Brownian motion can be made to approximate any continuous path in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
; and analogous statements for Schramm-Loewner evolution (see, e.g.,
[
MW17
, Section 2]
).

We expect that Theorem
1.4
and the some of the techniques used in its proof will be useful in future works concerning LQG and its higher dimensional analogs. Indeed, when studying LQG, one often needs to show that
D_{h}
and/or
\mu_{h}
has some prescribed behavior with positive probability. Prior to this work, this was typically done via ad hoc methods based on the fact that adding a smooth bump function to
h
changes its law in an absolutely continuous way. See
[
DDG21
, Section 4.1]
for an explanation of this technique. Arguments of this type can sometimes be quite complicated, see, e.g.,
[
BRG22
, Sections 11.2 and 11.3]
or
[
GM21a
, Section 5]
. In future work, such arguments could be replaced by applications of Theorem
1.4
.

In the course of proving Theorem
1.4
in dimension
d\geq 3
, we also establish several independently interesting facts about log-correlated Gaussian fields which we expect to be useful elsewhere (see e.g. Lemmas
2.4
,
2.5
, and
2.6
).

1.3
Interpretation of results in dimension
d\geq 3

In dimension
d\geq 3
, Theorem
1.4
is of interest for the same reasons as in the two-dimensional case, as discussed just above. It also raises interesting questions about the interpretation of the random Riemannian metric tensor (
1.1
), which we will discuss in this section.

A Riemannian metric tensor
g
on a domain
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
conformally flat
if there is a continuous function
f:U\to\mathbb{R}
such that
g=e^{f}(dx_{1}^{2}+\dots+dx_{d}^{2})
, where
dx_{1}^{2}+\dots+dx_{d}^{2}
is the Euclidean Riemmanian metric tensor. More generally, a
d
-dimensional Riemannian manifold is
locally conformally flat
if it admits an atlas where in each chart, the Riemannian metric is conformally flat.
It is a classical theorem that every two dimensional Riemannian manifold is locally conformally flat, i.e., it can locally be expressed in isothermal coordinates. However, this is not true in dimension
d\geq 3
. Rather, a
d
-dimensional Riemannian manifold is locally conformally flat if and only if its Cotton tensor (for
d=3
) or its Weyl tensor (for
d\geq 4
) is identically zero.

Theorem
1.4
implies that the random metric and measure associated with (
1.1
) can approximate
any
length metric and locally finite Borel measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with positive probability, even a metric and measure associated with a Riemannian metric tensor which is not conformally flat. This leads one to wonder whether the random Riemannian metric tensor associated with (
1.1
) should be thought of as a random conformally flat metric on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, or as a general random Riemannian metric on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This is closely related to some fundamental questions about analogs of LQG in higher dimensions, e.g., the following.

•

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
M_{n}
be a uniform sample from the set of triangulations of the
d
-sphere (i.e., simplicial complexes homeomorphic to
\mathbb{S}^{d}
) with
n

d
-simplices. For
d=2
, it is known that
M_{n}
(equipped with its graph distances, appropriately rescaled) converges in the Gromov-Hausdorff sense to
\sqrt{8/3}
-LQG
[
Le 13
,
Mie13
,
MS20b
,
MS21a
]
(see also
[
HS23
]
for a stronger topology of convergence). For
d\geq 3
, does
M_{n}
converge in the scaling limit (e.g., with respect to the Gromov-Hausdorff distance) to a
d
-manifold, equipped with a random Riemannian distance function of the form (
1.1
)? Or is it the case that to get convergence to a random Riemannian distance function of the form (
1.1
), one needs to instead take a uniform sample from a restricted set of triangulations of
\mathbb{S}^{d}
which are required to satisfy some discrete analog of conformal flatness? See
[
DGZ23
,
BG24
]
for related discussions.

•

Consider the following random Riemannian metric tensor on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}e^{\beta h_{i,j}}dx_{i}dx_{j}

(1.13)

where the
\beta>0
is a parameter and
h_{i,j}
s are log-correlated Gaussian fields on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(coupled together in some manner).
Can one make rigorous sense of the distance function associated with (
1.13
)? If so, then this distance function superficially seems to be supported on a more general class of metrics than (
1.1
) since (
1.13
) is not required to be conformally flat. However, our results show that any length metric on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be approximated with positive probability by the Riemannian distance function associated with (
1.13
) (conditional on the conjecture that this Riemannian distance function satisfies the strong axioms). So, in what sense does (
1.13
) actually give a more general Riemannian metric than (
1.1
)? Could it be that the limit of a natural approximation scheme for the Riemannian distance function associated with (
1.13
) is actually just the Riemannian distance function associated with (
1.1
), where
h
is some function of
\{h_{i,j}:i,j=1,\dots,d\}
and
\gamma
is some function of
\beta
and
d
?

We do not know the answers to the above questions, even conjecturally, but they seem to be worth pursuing further.

Applications of Theorem
1.4
in the two-dimensional case

1.4.1
Application to the LQG sphere

As a consequence of Theorem
1.4
, one can obtain a similar result concerning a special LQG surface called the
\gamma
-LQG sphere, which is represented by a certain special variant of the GFF.
There are a number of equivalent ways of defining the
\gamma
-LQG sphere. The first definitions appeared in
[
DMS21
,
DKRV16
]
and were proven to be equivalent in
[
AHS17
]
. The definition we give here is
[
AHS17
, Definition 2.2]
with
k=3
and
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=\alpha_{3}=\gamma
.

Definition 1.5
.

Let
\gamma\in(0,2)
and let

Q:=\frac{2}{\gamma}+\frac{\gamma}{2}.

(1.14)

The
Liouville field
is the random generalized function

h=h^{\mathbb{C}}+\gamma G(0,\cdot)+\gamma G(1,\cdot)-(2Q-\gamma)\log|\cdot|_{+}

(1.15)

where
h^{\mathbb{C}}
is the whole-plane GFF,
G(\cdot,\cdot)
is its covariance kernel as in (
1.2
), and
\log|\cdot|_{+}:=\log(\max\{|z|,1\}).
The
(triply marked, unit area)
\gamma
-LQG sphere
is the LQG surface parametrized by
\mathbb{C}
represented by the field
h
, where the law of
\mathbb{P}_{h}
of
h
is equal to the law
\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{h}}
of

\tilde{h}:=h-\frac{1}{\gamma}\log\mu_{h}(\mathbb{C})

(1.16)

weighted by a
\gamma
-dependent constant times
[\mu_{h}(\mathbb{C})]^{4/\gamma^{2}-2}
, i.e.,
\frac{d\mathbb{P}_{h}}{d\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{h}}}=C(\gamma)\times[\mu_{h}(\mathbb{C})]^{4/\gamma^{2}-2}
.

We note that if
h
is as in Definition
1.5
, then
\mu_{h}(\mathbb{C})=1
(this is because of the subtraction of
\frac{1}{\gamma}\log\mu_{h}(\mathbb{C})
in (
1.16
)).
Furthermore, one can check that the LQG metric associated with the LQG sphere extends continuously to the one-point compactification
\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
, so it can be viewed as a metric on the sphere (not just on
\mathbb{C}
).
One reason why the
\gamma
-LQG sphere is special is that it is the LQG surface which arises as the scaling limit of random planar maps with the sphere topology. See e.g. subsubsection
1.4.2
for more details.

We also have the following result for LQG in dimension 2.

Corollary 1.6
.

\mathfrak{d}
be a length metric on the sphere

and let

denote the metric and measure associated with the

, it holds with positive probability that

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|D_{\tilde{h}}(x,y)-\mathfrak{d}(x,y)}}\right|\leq\varepsilon,\quad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{S}^{2}

(1.17)

and at the same time for any Borel set

(1.18)

and

(1.20)

To obtain Corollary
1.6
, one can write the quantum sphere field
{\tilde{h}}
in terms of the Gaussian free field,

\tilde{h}:=\bar{h}-\frac{1}{\gamma}\log\mu_{\bar{h}}(\mathbb{C}),

\bar{h}=h+\gamma G(0,\cdot)+\gamma G(1,\cdot)-(2Q-\gamma)\log|\cdot|_{+}.

lets us “fix” the total measure

it suffices to consider

Due to the convergence of uniform random planar maps toward

To explain this, let

Let

\mathfrak{m}_{1},\mathfrak{m}_{2}
are Borel measures on a metric space

The Prokhorov distance is given by

d_{\mathrm{P}}(\mathfrak{m}_{1},\mathfrak{m}_{2}):=\inf\{\varepsilon>0:\mathfrak{m}_{1}(A)\leq\mathfrak{m}_{2}(A_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon,\;\mathfrak{m}_{2}(A)\leq\mathfrak{m}_{1}(A_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon\mbox{ for all closed }A\},

where

A_{\varepsilon}:=\{x\in X:\inf_{y\in A}\mathfrak{d}(x,y)<\varepsilon\}.

Also, recalling that the Hausdorff distance between pairs of sets
A,B
in a metric space
(X,\mathfrak{d})
is defined by

d_{H}(A,B):=\inf\{r>0:A\subseteq\cup_{x\in B}B_{\mathfrak{d}}(x),\;B\subseteq\cup_{x\in A}B_{\mathfrak{d}}(x)\}.

We define the Gromov-Hausdorff distance between two metric spaces
(X_{1},\mathfrak{d}_{1})
and
(X_{2},\mathfrak{d}_{2})
as the infimum over all

such that there exists a third metric space
(Z,\mathfrak{d})
and subspaces

that are isometric to

and

respectively, and additionally
d_{H}(X_{1}^{\prime},X_{2}^{\prime})<r.
Finally, the Gromov-Hausdorff-Prokhorov distance between two metric measure spaces
(X_{1},\mathfrak{d}_{1},\mu_{1})
and
(X_{2},\mathfrak{d}_{2},\mu_{2})
is defined as

d_{GHP}((X_{1},\mathfrak{d}_{1},\mu_{1}),(X_{2},\mathfrak{d}_{2},\mu_{2}))=\inf_{(Z,\mathfrak{d}),\iota_{1},\iota_{2}}d_{H}(X_{1},X_{2})+d_{P}(\iota_{1}^{*}\mu_{1},\iota_{2}^{*}\mu_{2}),

where the infimum is taken over isometric embeddings
\iota_{1}:X_{1}\to Z
and
\iota_{2}:X_{2}\to Z.

Outline

In Section
3
we prove the following variant of Theorem
1.4
:

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
\varepsilon>0,
and let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth compactly suported function. Then with positive probability we have that

\sup_{x,y\in[-R,R]^{d}}|D_{h}(x,y)-e^{f}\cdot d_{0}(x,y)|\leq\varepsilon

and at the same time,

\mu_{h}([-R,R]^{d})\leq\varepsilon.

To prove this, we use an analogue of the white noise decomposition for the Gaussian free field to decompose
h
as
h=h_{0,\beta}+h_{\beta,\infty}
, where
\beta>0
is small,
h_{0,\beta}
is a random generalized function, and
h_{\beta,\infty}
is an independent random smooth function (see (
2.13
) for a precise statement). The generalized function
h_{0,\beta}
has very weak long-range correlations and its law is invariant under rotations and translations of
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
. The function
h_{\beta,\infty}
does not enjoy these properties, but it is smooth. Once we do this, we discretize
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and use a first passage percolation argument as in
[
Boi90
]
followed by scaling arguments for LQG to show that
\beta^{-(\xi Q-1)}D_{h_{0,\beta}}
is approximately Euclidean and simultaneously
\beta^{-(\gamma Q-d)}\mu_{h_{0,\beta}}
is approximately the Lebesgue measure with high probability when
\beta
is small (Propositions
3.2
and
3.3
). After this, in Lemma
3.4
one controls the smooth part corresponding to
h_{\beta,\infty}
by forcing it to be close to any given function with positive probability. Forcing
h_{\beta,\infty}
to be approximately
\frac{\xi Q-1}{\xi}\log\beta
and using Weyl scaling, one obtains that with positive probability, that
D_{h}
approximates any conformally flat metric, while
\mu_{h}([-R,R]^{d})
is bounded by a multiple of
\theta=O(\beta^{\frac{\gamma}{\xi}-d}).

In Section
4
we show that the measure can be modified in such a way that we do not change the metric very much. In Proposition
4.3
we add bump functions to
h
to show that we can make
\mu_{h}
approximate an arbitrary finite sum of point masses. Then in Proposition
4.4
we show that the metric is not greatly modified, by showing that geodesic can freely go around the added bumps without adding much length. The bump functions we add will be large on the union of a large collection of small squares, and very negative on a small neighborhood of each of these squares. See Section
4.1
for a precise definition. This construction is similar to Section 11.2 in
[
BG22
]
. Together with the results in Section
3

1.6

2.1

Let
W
be a space-time white noise on

. That is,

is the Gaussian random generalized function such that for each

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x,t)W(dx,dt)
is centered Gaussian with variance
\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(x,t)^{2}\,dx\,dt
. For

, we define the kernel
p_{t}(x)
to be the kernel associated with

that is

is formally the solution to

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{matrix}\partial_{t}p_{t}+(-\Delta)^{\frac{d}{2}}p_{t}=0,\\
p_{0}=\delta_{0}.\end{matrix}}}\right.

(2.2)

Rigorously,
p_{t}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the unique kernel such that the (unique) solution to

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{matrix}\partial_{t}u+(-\Delta)^{\frac{d}{2}}u=0\\
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x)\end{matrix}}}\right.

is given by

u(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p_{t}(x-y)u_{0}(y)dy.

To see that this kernel is well defined, one can take the Fourier transform on both sides of the equation for

to obtain

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{matrix}\partial_{t}\hat{p}_{t}+(2\pi|\xi|^{2})^{\frac{d}{2}}\hat{p}_{t}=0,\\
\hat{p}_{0}(\xi)=1,\end{matrix}}}\right.

which suggests we define

p_{t}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}e^{-(2\pi|\xi|^{2})^{\frac{d}{2}}t}.

Note that by scaling, we have the identity

p_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{t}p_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{x}{t^{\frac{1}{d}}}}}\right)

(2.3)

For
0\leq\alpha\leq\beta<\infty
, we also define

h_{\alpha,\beta}(y):=\int_{\alpha^{2}}^{\beta^{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}p_{t/2}(x-y,t)W(dx,dt).

(2.4)

We will need the following basic property about the kernel

Lemma 2.1
.

Let

be defined as in (

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p_{t}(z_{1}-x)p_{s}(x-z_{2})dx=p_{t+s}(z_{1}-z_{2}).

Proof.

By translation invariance, it suffices to show

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p_{t}(x)p_{s}(x-z)dx=p_{s+t}(z).

For this, suppose

is the solution to

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{matrix}\partial_{t}u+(-\Delta)^{\frac{d}{2}}u=0,\\
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x)\end{matrix}}}\right.

where
u_{0}
is any smooth compactly supported initial condition. Let

v(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p_{t}(z)p_{s}(x-y-z)dz}}\right)u_{0}(y)dy.

Then

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v(t,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p_{t}(z)p_{s}(x-y-z)u_{0}(y)dydz}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\partial_{t}(p_{t}(z))p_{s}(x-y-z)u_{0}(y)dydz

\partial_{t}v(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\partial_{t}(p_{t}(z))p_{s}(x-y-z)u_{0}(y)dydz.

(2.5)

On the other hand,

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{\frac{d}{2}}v(t,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(-\Delta_{x})^{\frac{d}{2}}p_{t}(x-y-z)(p_{s}(z))u_{0}(y)dydz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((-\Delta_{x})^{\frac{d}{2}}p_{t}}}\right)(x-y-z)p_{s}(z)u_{0}(y)dydz.

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\partial_{t}+(-\Delta)^{\frac{d}{2}}}}\right)v=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\partial_{t}+(-\Delta)^{\frac{d}{2}}}}\right)p_{t}(z)p_{s}(x-y-z)u_{0}(y)dydz=0.

Finally, note that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}v(t,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p_{t}(z)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p_{s}(x-y-z)u_{0}(y)dy}}\right)dz

2.2
Cameron-Martin property for log-correlated Gaussian fields

We will need the following property, analogous to the Cameron-Martin property for the GFF.

Lemma 2.6
.

Suppose that
f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a smooth compactly supported function. Then the law of
h+f
is absolutely continuous with respect to the law of
h
up to additive constant.

Proof.

By Proposition 2.3 in
[
JSW18
]
we have the Karhunen–Loéve expansion of
h
(up to additive constant)

h=\sum_{n\geq 0}A_{n}\sqrt{\lambda_{n}}\varphi_{n}(x)

where
\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is the set of eigenvalues of the Hilbert-Schmidt operator associated with the kernel
p_{t},

\{\varphi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors in
L^{2},
and
\{A_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
is an i.i.d. set of standard Gaussian random variables. Since
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),
there exist coefficients
\{c_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
such that

f=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}c_{n}\sqrt{\lambda_{n}}\varphi_{n}.

Indeed, since
f\in H_{0}^{\frac{d}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),
we have

f=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}d_{n}\varphi_{n}

for some coefficients
d_{n},
and so

\infty>\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\Delta^{\frac{d}{4}}f(z)|^{2}dz=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(z)\Delta^{\frac{d}{2}}f(z)dz\geq\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}|d_{n}|\frac{|d_{n}|}{\lambda_{n}}

and so
c_{n}=\frac{d_{n}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{n}}}\in\ell^{2}.

Then

h+f=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}(A_{n}+c_{n})\varphi_{n}.

Therefore
h+f
is absolutely continuous with respect to
h,
with Radon-Nikodym derivative given by

\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}e^{-\frac{c_{n}^{2}}{2}}e^{c_{n}x}=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}c_{n}^{2}+\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}c_{n}A_{n}}

which converges, since
c_{n}\in\ell^{2}.
This completes the proof.
∎

Now using Proposition

we obtain that

\mathrm{Diam}_{D_{h_{0,\beta}+f}}([-R,R]^{d})\leq\beta^{\xi Q-1}\nu 2\sqrt{2}+\varepsilon\beta^{\xi Q-1}.

Plugging this into (
3.6
) we obtain that if
\varepsilon
is small enough, for all
x,y\in[-R,R]^{d}
we have

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|D_{h}(x,y)-D_{h_{0,\beta}+f}(x,y)}}\right|\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e^{2\xi\varepsilon}-1}}\right)\beta^{\xi Q-1}4\nu.

Combining this with (
3.5
) and using the triangle inequality, we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|D_{h}(x,y)-\nu\beta^{\xi Q-1}e^{\xi f}\cdot d_{0}(x,y)}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|D_{h}(x,y)-D_{h_{0,\beta}+f}(x,y)}}\right|+|D_{h_{0,\beta}+f}(x,y)-\nu\beta^{\xi Q-1}e^{\xi f}\cdot d_{0}(x,y)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\beta^{\xi Q-1}\varepsilon+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e^{2\xi\varepsilon}-1}}\right)\beta^{\xi Q-1}4\nu.

Therefore for small enough
\beta>0
and shrinking
\varepsilon
if necessary, we obtain our claim (
3.4
).

Again recall we are assuming the conclusions of Propositions
3.2
,
3.3
and
3.4
hold simultaneously. Using the white noise decomposition
h=h_{p,\beta}+h_{\beta,\infty}-(h_{0,\beta})_{1}(0),
we see that by Proposition
3.4
and (
3.3
) we have

\lVert h-h_{0,\beta}-f\rVert_{\infty}\leq 2\varepsilon.

Hence

\mu_{h}([-R,R]^{d})\leq e^{\gamma\lVert f\rVert_{\infty}+2\gamma\varepsilon}\mu_{h_{0,\beta}}([-R,R]^{d})\leq C_{0}e^{\gamma\lVert f\rVert_{\infty}+2\gamma\varepsilon}\beta^{\gamma Q-2-c}.

(3.7)

This implies that if the conclusions of Propositions
3.2
,
3.3
, and
3.4
hold, then we have that for any
x,y\in[-R,R]^{d},

|D_{h}(x,y)-\nu\beta^{\xi Q-1}e^{\xi f}\cdot d_{0}(x,y)|\leq\beta^{\xi Q-1}\varepsilon,

and

\mu_{h}([-R,R]^{d})\leq C_{0}e^{\gamma\lVert f\rVert_{\infty}+2\gamma\varepsilon}\beta^{\gamma Q-2-c}.

Now replacing
f
by
f+(\frac{1}{\xi}-Q)\log(\beta)-\frac{\log\nu}{\xi},
we obtain

\sup_{x,y\in[-R,R]^{d}}|D_{h}(x,y)-e^{\xi f}\cdot d_{0}(x,y)|\leq\varepsilon

and for any Borel set
A,

\mu_{h}(A)\leq\theta\int_{A_{\varepsilon}}f(z)dz+\varepsilon

where

\theta=\alpha\nu^{-\frac{\gamma}{\xi}}\beta^{\frac{\gamma}{\xi}-d}.

Note that by (
1.11
),
\theta\to 0
as
\beta\to 0,
and so this completes the proof of Theorem
1.8
.
∎

3.2
First passage percolation argument

From now on, let
\mbox{Diam}_{h}
denote the diameter with respect to the metric
D_{h}
and define
\mbox{Diam}_{h_{0,\beta}}
analogously.
For the proof of this Proposition
3.2
, we first treat the case where
y
is fixed. We will look at the asymptotic behaviour of
D_{h_{0,1}}(0,x)
in a single direction first, and then prove convergence independent of the direction. To this end, we will need the following lemma.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
c>0
be a sufficiently small fixed constant. Then we have that almost surely,

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in[-2^{n},2^{n}]^{d}\\
|x-y|\leq 1\end{subarray}}D_{h_{0,1}}(x,y)\leq 2^{(1-c)n}

for all but finitely many integers
n.

Proof.

We recall that
e(p):=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{Diam}_{D_{h_{0,1}}}([0,1]^{d})}}\right)^{p}}}\right)<\infty
for some
p>d
by (
1.12
). By Markov’s inequality,

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{Diam}_{D_{h_{0,1}}}([0,1]^{d})>2^{(1-c)n}}}\right)\leq\frac{C}{2^{np(1-c)}}.

for some absolute constant
C
only depending on
p.
Now note that if
\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in[-2^{n},2^{n}]^{d}\\
|x-y|\leq 1\end{subarray}}D_{h_{0,1}}(x,y)>2^{(1-c)n+d},
then there must exist
x_{0},y_{0}
such that
|x-y|\leq 1,
but
D_{h_{0,1}}(x,y)>2^{(1-c)n+2}.
This implies that there must exist a cube
[k_{1},k_{1}+1]\times[k_{2},k_{2}+1]\cdot\times[k_{d},k_{d}+1]
with
-2^{n}\leq k_{1},k_{2},\ldots,k_{d}\leq 2^{n}-1
such that

\mathrm{Diam}_{D_{h_{0,1}}}([k_{1},k_{1}+1]\times[k_{2},k_{2}+1]\cdot\times[k_{d},k_{d}+1])>2^{(1-c)n}.

Since there are
2^{dn}
possibilities for the pair
(k_{1},k_{2}),
we obtain

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in[-2^{n},2^{n}]^{d}\\
|x-y|\leq 1\end{subarray}}{D_{h_{0,1}}}(x,y)>2^{(1-c)n}}}\right)\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2^{n}}}\right)^{d-p(1-c)}.

Choosing
p
to be close enough to
2
such that
d-p(1-c)<0,
we can apply Borel-Cantelli to obtain that almost surely, for all but finitely many
n
we have

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in[-2^{n},2^{n}]^{d}\\
|x-y|\leq 1\end{subarray}}D_{h_{0,1}}(x,y)\leq 2^{(1-c)n}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
x\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\setminus\{0\}
be fixed. There is a deterministic constant
\nu>0
, not depending on
x
, such that a.s.

\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{D_{h_{0,1}}(0,\lambda x)}{\lambda|x|}-\nu}}\right|=0.

Proof.

Recall that
h_{0,1}
is stationary and ergodic by Lemma
2.2
and therefore so is
D_{h_{0,1}}(0,x),
which together with subadditivity (which is the triangle inequality for
D_{h_{0,1}}
) and Kingman’s ergodic theorem (see
[
Kin68
]
) shows that there exists a deterministic function
\nu:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{D_{h_{0,1}}(0,nx)}{\lambda|x|}-\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{x}{|x|}}}\right)}}\right|=0.

Combining this with Lemma
3.8
we see that

\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{D_{h_{0,1}}(0,\lambda x)}{\lambda|x|}-\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{x}{|x|}}}\right)}}\right|=0,

where here
\lambda
is a real number. Now all that remains is to see why

is in fact constant. Simply note that the law of
D_{h_{0,1}}(0,x)
is rotationally invariant with respect to

by Lemma
2.2
and hence

is independent of the direction.
∎

Lemma 3.10
.

Almost surely,
\limsup_{|x|\to\infty,x\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{D_{h_{0,1}}(0,x)}{|x|}-\nu}}\right|=0.

For the proof of this lemma, we follow the argument used in the main theorem in
[
Boi90

. The argument is almost exactly the same, but we reproduce it here for the reader’s convenience.
First we will outline the proof. The first crucial element is the maximal lemma, Lemma
3.11
below, which gives an upper tail bound for the random variable
\sup_{|x|>1}D_{h_{0,1}}(0,x)/|x|
. The next step is Lemma
3.12
, which tells us that given any direction
\theta
there is a

in that direction at a far enough distance from the origin such that

for a large constant
\lambda>0
which does not depend on

Now if the lemma is not true, then we must have a sequence

such that

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{D_{h_{0,1}}(0,x_{i})}{|x_{i}|}-\nu}}\right|>\bar{\varepsilon},\quad\forall i\in\mathbb{N}.

By extracting a subsequence, we can assume that
\frac{x_{i}}{|x_{i}|}\to y.
We can then compare the distances

and

where

with

is an appropriate direction,

and

Combining everything we can conclude that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{D_{h_{0,1}}(0,x_{i})}{|x_{i}|}-\nu}}\right|<\bar{\varepsilon},
yielding a contradiction.

First we claim that that the probability of

Lemma 3.11

For some fixed constant

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}},|x|>1}\frac{D_{h_{0,1}}(0,x)}{|x|}>\lambda}}\right\}\leq\frac{K_{1}}{\lambda^{d}}

Let

be the set of closed squares of side length

For each pair of squares
S,S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}

For each

S_{x}\in\mathcal{S}

where the infimum runs over all paths

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}},|x|>1}\frac{D_{h_{0,1}}(0,x)}{|x|}>\lambda}}\right\}\leq\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}},|x|>1}\frac{L(0,x)}{|x|}>\lambda}}\right\}.
