Contributions.

In this paper, we propose efficient quantum simulation algorithms in first quantization for multi-particle real-space Schrödinger equations.

Our primary consideration is to control the error in a discrete approximation of the continuum solution of a Schrödinger equation. We perform spatial discretization using the Fourier spectral method. Since the error of this method decreases exponentially with the number of basis functions
[
17
]
, it provides a high-precision approximation, as characterized in
Lemma
1
.
The Fourier spectral method yields a discretized Hamiltonian of the form
H=A+B
, where
A
is the (truncated) kinetic term and
B
is the (discretized) potential term.
We explore three different techniques to simulate this discretized Hamiltonian.

First, we develop and analyze a
k
th-order product formula method
for simulating the Hamiltonian
H=A+B
. This method uses the fact that the evolution operators
e^{-iAt}
and
e^{-iBt}
can be efficiently implemented, since the potential operator
B
is diagonal in the computational basis, while the quantum Fourier transform diagonalizes the kinetic operator
A
. Such an approach was considered in early work of Wiesner
[
66
]
and Zalka
[
67
]
, although they did not rigorously bound the complexity. The
k
th-order product formula method uses
O(5^{2k}(\|H\|T)^{1+1/2k}/\epsilon^{1/2k})
exponentials to simulate
H
for time
T
with error at most
\epsilon

[
12
]
. Furthermore, its empirical performance can be better in practice than other Hamiltonian simulation methods for modestly sized classically hard instances of particular models such as spin systems
[
25
]
. Combining the error analysis of the Fourier spectral method and the
k
th-order product formula method, we obtain the following result.

2
Simulating Schrödinger equations in real space

2.1
Fourier spectral method

In this section, we develop an approach to simulating the Schrödinger equation in real space that combines the Fourier spectral method with Hamiltonian simulation. The Fourier spectral method (also known as the Fourier pseudospectral method) provides a global approximation to the exact solution of a partial differential equation with periodic boundary conditions. This approch can be contrasted with local approximations—such as the finite difference method—that approximate the solution on a set of grid points. In general, the Fourier spectral method approximates the solution by a linear combination of Fourier basis functions with undetermined time-dependent coefficients. By interpolating the partial differential equations at uniformly spaced nodes, we obtain a system of ordinary differential equations that can be solved numerically
[
17
,
56
]
. Applying this approach to the Schrödinger equation, we obtain a discretized Hamiltonian system that can be handled by standard Hamiltonian simulation algorithms.

At first glance, the Fourier spectral method looks similar to plane-wave methods widely used in first-quantized quantum simulations. Although these two approaches both employ Fourier basis functions, their primary difference is that they approximate the infinite-dimensional functional space in different finite-dimensional subspaces, and in particular, result in different discretized Hamiltonian systems. To illustrate this difference, let
\Phi(x,t)
and
\widetilde{\Phi}(x,t)
denote the exact and approximated solutions, respectively, of a one-dimensional Schrödinger equation, and let

\displaystyle R_{n}(x,t)=\Big[i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}-f(x)\Big]\widetilde{\Phi}(x,t)

(9)

denote the
residue
, where
n+1
is the number of the basis functions. The residue quantifies the extent to which the approximated solution fails to satisfy the Schrödinger equation (
1
). In general,
R_{n}
cannot be zero as a function of
t
unless the exact solution
\Phi
is a finite combination of basis functions. Instead, we seek a reasonable choice of
\widetilde{\Phi}
such that the projection of
R_{n}
onto some finite-dimensional subspace vanishes. As we describe below in more detail, the Fourier spectral approach requires the residue to vanish at the set of interpolation nodes, while Galerkin plane wave methods instead guarantee that its integrations with Fourier test functions are zero.

In the Galerkin approach
[
18
,
61
]
, the residue is orthogonal to a subspace of
n+1
chosen test functions, denoted
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=0}^{n}
. In other words, we require that

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{j}|R_{n}\rangle=\langle\phi_{j}|H|\widetilde{\Phi}\rangle=0,\qquad j\in[{n+1}]_{0}=\{0,1,\ldots,n\},

(10)

where angle brackets denote the inner product over the spatial domain, and
H
is the Galerkin discretized Hamiltonian.
For the Schrödinger equation (
1
), we have
H=T+V
where the matrix elements of the discretized kinetic and potential terms are given by
[
58
]

\displaystyle T_{pq}

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}r\,\phi^{\ast}_{p}(x)\biggl(-\frac{\nabla^{2}}{2}\biggr)\phi_{q}(x),

(11)

\displaystyle V_{pq}

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}r\,\phi^{\ast}_{p}(x)f(x)\phi_{q}(x).

(12)

(See Appendix B of
[
58
]
for Galerkin representations of molecular Hamiltonians.)

Equation (
10
) is a system of
n+1
ordinary differential equations with time-dependent coefficients. For first-quantized plane-wave methods, the basis functions used in constructing
\widetilde{\Phi}
as well as the test functions
\{\phi_{j}\}
are all chosen as Fourier basis functions. While this discretization is commonly used in first-quantized quantum simulations
[
62
,
6
,
5
,
58
]
, previous studies neglect the discretization error. If the Schrödinger operator includes an unbounded potential or is highly oscillatory, such a Galerkin representation may not provide a reasonable approximation.

On the other hand, in the spectral approach
[
17
,
56
]
, we choose the test functions
\{\phi_{j}\}
in (
10
) to be delta functions on the uniform interpolation nodes
\{\chi_{j}\}_{j=0}^{n}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\langle\delta_{j}|R_{n}\rangle=R_{n}(\chi_{j},t)=0,\qquad k\in[{n+1}]_{0},

(13)

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\Big[i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}-f(\mathbf{x})\Big]\widetilde{\Phi}(\chi_{j},t)=0,\qquad k\in[{n+1}]_{0}.

(14)

This choice again defines a system of
n+1
ordinary differential equations with time-dependent coefficients. The spectral approach can provide a straightforward approximation of real-space quantum dynamics by determining the number of interpolation points
n+1
explicitly as a function of the allowed discretization error, the particle number, and the norms of high-order derivatives of the wave function. In contrast, previous simulations based on the Galerkin approach
[
62
,
6
,
5
,
58
]
did not explicitly take the real-space discretization error into account, and instead merely determined the complexity in terms of the number of basis functions used.

We now introduce our Fourier spectral approach for time-dependent Schrödinger equations of the general form

\displaystyle i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Phi(\mathbf{x},t)=\Big[-\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}+f(\mathbf{x},t)\Big]\Phi(\mathbf{x},t)

(15)

where
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
represents the position of the quantum particle,
t\in\mathbb{R}
represents time,
\nabla=\bigl(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\bigr)\big|_{i=1}^{d}
is the gradient, and
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is the potential function. This generalizes (
1
) to the case of time-dependent potentials. We assume access to the potential through a unitary oracle
U_{f}
such that for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, and
z\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle U_{f}|\mathbf{x}\rangle|t\rangle|z\rangle=|\mathbf{x}\rangle|t\rangle|f(\mathbf{x})+z\rangle.

(16)

For concreteness, we consider
\mathbf{x}\in\Omega:=[0,1]^{d}
and assume periodic boundary conditions for
\Phi(\mathbf{x},0)
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{(p)}}{\partial x_{j}^{(p)}}\Phi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{j-1},0,x_{j+1},\ldots,x_{d},0)=\frac{\partial^{(p)}}{\partial x_{j}^{(p)}}\Phi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{j-1},1,x_{j+1},\ldots,x_{d},0)

(17)

holds for all
p\in\mathbb{N},\penalty\ j\in[d]
, where
\frac{\partial^{(p)}}{\partial x_{j}^{(p)}}
is the
p
th-order partial derivative with respect to the
j
th coordinate of
\mathbf{x}=[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]^{T}
.

We first apply the Fourier spectral method for the spatial discretization. We approximate the solution
\Phi(\mathbf{x},t)
by a Fourier series of the form

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Phi}(\mathbf{x},t)=\sum_{\|\mathbf{k}\|_{\infty}\leq n}c_{\mathbf{k}}(t)\phi_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x})

(18)

for some even number
n\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\mathbf{k}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{d})
with
k_{j}\in[{n+1}]_{0}
,
c_{\mathbf{k}}(t)\in\mathbb{C}
, and

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x})=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\phi_{k_{j}}(x_{j})

(19)

with

\displaystyle\phi_{k}(x):=e^{2\pi i(k-n/2)x}

(20)

for
k\in[{n+1}]_{0}
and
x\in[0,1]
.

Plugging (
18
) into (
15
), we obtain an approximated PDE system

\displaystyle i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\widetilde{\Phi}(\mathbf{x},t)=\Big[-\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}+f(\mathbf{x},t)\Big]\widetilde{\Phi}(\mathbf{x},t)

(21)

with the initial condition

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Phi}(\mathbf{x},0)=\Phi(\mathbf{x},0).

(22)

In terms of the basis functions, this gives

\displaystyle i\sum_{\|\mathbf{k}\|_{\infty}\leq n}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}{t}}c_{\mathbf{k}}(t)\phi_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\|\mathbf{k}\|_{\infty}\leq n}c_{\mathbf{k}}(t)\Big[-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\|\mathbf{r}\|_{\infty}\leq n}[{\mathbf{L}}_{n,d}]_{\mathbf{kr}}\phi_{\mathbf{r}}(\mathbf{x})+f(\mathbf{x},t)\phi_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x})\Big]

(23)

where
{\mathbf{L}}_{n,d}
is the multi-dimensional Laplacian matrix

\displaystyle{\mathbf{L}}_{n,d}:=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d}D^{2}_{n}=D^{2}_{n}\otimes I^{\otimes d-1}+I\otimes D^{2}_{n}\otimes I^{\otimes d-2}+\cdots+I^{\otimes d-1}\otimes D^{2}_{n}

(24)

and
D_{n}
is a differential matrix for the Fourier basis functions (
20
), the
(n+1)
-dimensional diagonal matrix with entries

\displaystyle[D_{n}]_{kk}=2\pi i(k-n/2)

(25)

for
k\in[{n+1}]_{0}
.

To produce a system of ordinary differential equations, we introduce the
uniform interpolation nodes

\{\mathbf{\chi}_{\mathbf{l}}=(\chi_{l_{1}},\ldots,\chi_{l_{d}})\}_{\|\mathbf{l}\|_{\infty}\leq n}
with
l_{j}\in[{n+1}]_{0}
, where

\displaystyle\chi_{l_{j}}=\frac{l_{j}}{n+1}.

(26)

Considering (
23
) at the uniform interpolation nodes (
26
), we obtain an
(n+1)^{d}
-dimensional approximated ODE system

\displaystyle i\sum_{\|\mathbf{k}\|_{\infty}\leq n}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}{t}}c_{\mathbf{k}}(t)\phi_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{\chi}_{\mathbf{l}})|l_{1}\rangle\ldots|l_{d}\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{\|\mathbf{k}\|_{\infty}\leq n}c_{\mathbf{k}}(t)\Big[-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\|\mathbf{r}\|_{\infty}\leq n}[{\mathbf{L}}_{n}]_{\mathbf{kr}}\phi_{\mathbf{r}}(\mathbf{\chi}_{\mathbf{l}})+f(\mathbf{\chi}_{\mathbf{l}},t)\phi_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{\chi}_{\mathbf{l}})\Big]|l_{1}\rangle\ldots|l_{d}\rangle,

(27)

where
l_{j}\in[{n+1}]_{0},\penalty\ j\in[d]
.

The well-known
quantum Fourier transform
(QFT) maps the
(n+1)
-dimensional quantum state
v=(v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
to the state
\hat{v}=(\hat{v}_{0},\hat{v}_{1},\ldots,\hat{v}_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
with

\hat{v}_{l}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\exp\Bigl(\frac{2\pi ikl}{n+1}\Bigr)v_{k},\quad l\in[{n+1}]_{0}.

(28)

In other words, the QFT is the unitary transform

F_{n}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}}\sum_{k,l=0}^{n}\exp\Bigl(\frac{2\pi ikl}{n+1}\Bigr)|l\rangle\langle k|.

(29)

The closely related
quantum shifted Fourier transform
(QSFT) maps the
(n+1)
-dimensional quantum state
v\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
to the state
\hat{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
with

\hat{v}_{l}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\exp\Bigl(\frac{2\pi i(k-n/2)l}{n+1}\Bigr)v_{k},\quad l\in[{n+1}]_{0}.

(30)

In other words, the QSFT is the unitary transform

\displaystyle F^{s}_{n}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}}\sum_{k,l=0}^{n}\exp\Bigl(\frac{2\pi i(k-n/2)l}{n+1}\Bigr)|l\rangle\langle k|.

(31)

Notice that the QSFT can be written as the product

F^{s}_{n}=S_{n}F_{n},

(32)

of the QFT defined above and the diagonal matrix

S_{n}=\sum_{l=0}^{n}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\pi inl}{n+1}\Bigr)|l\rangle\langle l|.

(33)

The QSFT can be performed with gate complexity
O(\log n\log\log n)

[
24
, Lemma 5]
. Using (
18
) in the one-dimensional case with
v_{k}=c_{k}(t)
, the QSFT maps the state
v
to
\hat{v}=F^{s}_{n}v
satisfying

\hat{v}_{l}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}(t)\phi_{k}(\chi_{l})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}}\widetilde{\Phi}(\chi_{l},t),\quad l\in[{n+1}]_{0}.

(34)

In other words, the QSFT maps the coefficient vector
v=\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}(t)|k\rangle
to approximate interpolated solution
\hat{v}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}}\sum_{l=0}^{n}\widetilde{\Phi}(\chi_{l},t)|l\rangle
.
We use the QSFT (instead of the ordinary QFT) to align with the phase convention specified in (
18
).

We also define the multi-dimensional QSFT as

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}^{s}_{n,d}:=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{d}F^{s}_{n}.

(35)

Letting

\displaystyle c(t):=\sum_{\|\mathbf{k}\|_{\infty}\leq n}c_{\mathbf{k}}(t)|k_{1}\rangle\ldots|k_{d}\rangle,\qquad|c(t)\rangle:=\frac{c(t)}{\|c(t)\|},

(36)

and

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{n,d}(t):=\sum_{\|\mathbf{l}\|_{\infty}\leq n}f(\mathbf{\chi}_{\mathbf{l}},t)|l_{1}\rangle\ldots|l_{d}\rangle\langle l_{1}|\ldots\langle l_{d}|,

(37)

the ODE system (
2.1
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle i\mathbf{F}^{s}_{n,d}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}{t}}|c(t)\rangle=\mathbf{F}^{s}_{n,d}{\mathbf{L}}_{n,d}|c(t)\rangle+\mathbf{V}_{n,d}(t)\mathbf{F}^{s}_{n,d}|c(t)\rangle.

(38)

Equivalently,

\displaystyle i\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}{t}}|c(t)\rangle={\mathbf{H}}_{n,d}(t)|c(t)\rangle=[\mathbf{L}_{n,d}+(\mathbf{F}^{s}_{n,d})^{-1}\mathbf{V}_{n,d}(t)\mathbf{F}^{s}_{n,d}]|c(t)\rangle

(39)

which is a Hamiltonian system in the momentum space,
with the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle{\mathbf{H}}_{n,d}(t):=\mathbf{L}_{n,d}+(\mathbf{F}^{s}_{n,d})^{-1}\mathbf{V}_{n,d}(t)\mathbf{F}^{s}_{n,d}.

(40)

Alternatively, (
39
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle i\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}{t}}[\mathbf{F}^{s}_{n,d}|c(t)\rangle]=[\mathbf{F}^{s}_{n,d}{\mathbf{L}}_{n,d}(\mathbf{F}^{s}_{n,d})^{-1}+\mathbf{V}_{n,d}(t)][\mathbf{F}^{s}_{n,d}|c(t)\rangle].

(41)

Using (
18
), we
write

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Phi}(t)=\sum_{\|\mathbf{l}\|_{\infty}\leq n}\widetilde{\Phi}(\mathbf{\chi}_{\mathbf{l}},t)|l_{1}\rangle\ldots|l_{d}\rangle=\sum_{\|\mathbf{l}\|_{\infty}\leq n}c_{\mathbf{k}}(t)\phi_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{\chi}_{\mathbf{l}})|l_{1}\rangle\ldots|l_{d}\rangle,\qquad|\widetilde{\Phi}(t)\rangle:=\frac{\widetilde{\Phi}(t)}{\|\widetilde{\Phi}(t)\|},

(42)

such that

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Phi}(t)=\mathbf{F}^{s}_{n,d}c(t),\qquad|\widetilde{\Phi}(t)\rangle=\mathbf{F}^{s}_{n,d}|c(t)\rangle

(43)

provide approximations of the exact solution and its
\ell_{2}
normalized state

\displaystyle\Phi(t)=\sum_{\mathbf{l}}\Phi(\mathbf{\chi}_{\mathbf{l}},t)|l_{1}\rangle\ldots|l_{d}\rangle,\qquad|\Phi(t)\rangle:=\frac{\Phi(t)}{\|\Phi(t)\|},

(44)

respectively.
Thus we see that Eq. (
39
) is a Hamiltonian system in position space

\displaystyle i\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}{t}}|\widetilde{\Phi}(t)\rangle=\mathbf{\widetilde{H}}_{n,d}(t)|\widetilde{\Phi}(t)\rangle=[\mathbf{F}^{s}_{n,d}\mathbf{L}_{n,d}(\mathbf{F}^{s}_{n,d})^{-1}+\mathbf{V}_{n,d}(t)]|\widetilde{\Phi}(t)\rangle,

(45)

with the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle{\mathbf{\widetilde{H}}}_{n,d}(t)

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{F}^{s}_{n,d}{\mathbf{L}}_{n,d}(\mathbf{F}^{s}_{n,d})^{-1}+\mathbf{V}_{n,d}(t)

(46)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{F}^{s}_{n,d}{\mathbf{H}}_{n,d}(t)(\mathbf{F}^{s}_{n,d})^{-1}.

(47)

Furthermore, we assume the
\ell_{2}
norm of the exact
(n+1)^{d}
-dimensional initial condition
\Phi(0)
satisfies

\displaystyle\|\Phi(0)\|^{2}=\sum_{\|\mathbf{l}\|_{\infty}\leq n}|\Phi(\mathbf{\chi}_{\mathbf{l}},0)|^{2}=(n+1)^{d}.

(48)

This is a discrete analog of the condition
\int_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega}|\Phi(\mathbf{x},0)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}=1
on the
(n+1)^{d}
uniform interpolation nodes
\{\mathbf{\chi}_{\mathbf{l}}\}
. In more detail, consider the trapezoidal rule for numerical integration
[
36
]
. On each
d
-dimensional grid cell, we replace the integration of
\Phi(\mathbf{x},0)
by the average value of
2d
nearby interpolation points
\Phi(\mathbf{\chi}_{\mathbf{l}},0)
times the volume
\frac{1}{(n+1)^{d}}
of the
d
-dimensional grid cell. In this setting,
\|\Phi(0)\|^{2}/(n+1)^{d}
approximates
\int_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega}|\Phi(\mathbf{x},0)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}
. For convenience, we normalize the state according to (
48
).
5
5

5

Given an arbitrary initial condition
\Phi(\mathbf{x},0)
and its corresponding discretized state
\Phi(0)
, we can rescale the initial condition as
\Phi(\mathbf{x},0)\to\frac{(n+1)^{d/2}}{c}\Phi(\mathbf{x},0)
and
\Phi(0)\to\frac{(n+1)^{d/2}}{c}\Phi(0)
where

, such that the rescaled state satisfies
\|\Phi(0)\|=(n+1)^{d}
.

Because the Schrödinger equation is unitary, this ensures that

\displaystyle\|\Phi(t)\|^{2}=\sum_{\|\mathbf{l}\|_{\infty}\leq n}|\Phi(\mathbf{\chi}_{\mathbf{l}},t)|^{2}=(n+1)^{d}

(49)

for all

2.2
Truncation number of the Fourier spectral method

The overall simulation error includes two contributions: the error introduced by discretizing the problem with the Fourier spectral method and the error introduced by the Hamiltonian simulation algorithm. To ensure overall error at most
\epsilon
, we choose the parameters of the Fourier spectral method to upper bound the spatial discretization error between the exact and approximated normalized states (
|\Phi(t)\rangle
and
|\widetilde{\Phi}(t)\rangle
, respectively) by
\epsilon/2
, and choose the parameters of the Hamiltonian simulation algorithm to also upper bound its error by
\epsilon/2
. The latter calculation uses standard analysis to bound the error accumulated over the course of the simulation. In the following, we analyze the error of the Fourier spectral method.

Spectral methods typically exhibit exponential convergence
if the solution is smooth
[
17
]
. In particular, we establish exponential convergence for approximating
\Phi(\mathbf{x},t)
by
\widetilde{\Phi}(\mathbf{x},t)
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\Phi(\mathbf{x},t)
and
\widetilde{\Phi}(\mathbf{x},t)

denote the exact and approximated solutions of (
1
) by the Fourier spectral method, respectively, where
\Phi(\mathbf{x},t)
is analytic in
t
and
\mathbf{x}
. Then for any even integer
n\geq 6
, the error from the Fourier spectral method satisfies

\displaystyle\max_{\mathbf{x},t}|\Phi(\mathbf{x},t)-\widetilde{\Phi}(\mathbf{x},t)|\leq\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{\max_{t}\|\Phi^{(n/2)}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{1}}}{(n/2)^{n/2}}.

(50)

Lemma
1
gives an estimate of the maximal error of approximating
\Phi(\mathbf{x},t)
by
\widetilde{\Phi}(\mathbf{x},t)
in space and time. We prove
Lemma
1
in
Appendix
B
.

Using this error estimate, we can determine a sufficient truncation number
n
that ensures the approximated solution
\widetilde{\Phi}(\mathbf{x},t)
is within the allowed error tolerance. For simplicity, we denote

\displaystyle g^{\prime}:=\max_{t}\|\Phi^{(n/2)}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{1}}.

(51)

The parameter
g^{\prime}
describes the higher-order regularity of the wave function
\Phi(\mathbf{x},t)
. Usually, when
\Phi(\mathbf{x},t)
is not strongly localized, it is common to assume
g^{\prime}
is bounded from above
[
42
,
38
]
. In fact, Bourgain
[
16
]
shows that the derivatives of the wave function
\Phi(\mathbf{x},t)
are bounded when the potential function
f(\mathbf{x},t)
is sufficiently smooth. However, to our best knowledge, the exact scaling of
g^{\prime}
in terms of
d
and
n
remains unknown. Therefore, in the present analysis, we parametrize the overall complexity by
g^{\prime}
.

Using (
50
), for any
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{\chi}_{\mathbf{l}}
defined in (
26
), we have

\displaystyle\Bigl|\Phi(\mathbf{\chi}_{\mathbf{l}},t)-\widetilde{\Phi}(\mathbf{\chi}_{\mathbf{l}},t)\Bigr|\leq\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{g^{\prime}}{(n/2)^{n/2}}.

(52)

Recall that the sets of all entries of
\Phi(t)
and
\widetilde{\Phi}(t)
as presented in (
44
) and (
42
) are
\{\Phi(\mathbf{\chi}_{\mathbf{l}},t)\}
and
\{\widetilde{\Phi}(\mathbf{\chi}_{\mathbf{l}},t)\}
for all
\mathbf{\chi}_{\mathbf{l}}
, respectively. Each entry of
\Phi(t)-\widetilde{\Phi}(t)
is bounded by the right-hand side of (
52
), giving

\displaystyle\Bigl\|\Phi(t)-\widetilde{\Phi}(t)\Bigr\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{g^{\prime}}{(n/2)^{n/2}}

(53)

for any
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. With respect to the
\ell_{2}
norm, using
\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{(n+1)^{d}}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\infty}
for the
(n+1)^{d}
-dimensional vector
\mathbf{v}=\Phi(t)-\widetilde{\Phi}(t)
, we have

\displaystyle\Bigl\|\Phi(t)-\widetilde{\Phi}(t)\Bigr\|\leq\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{g^{\prime}}{(n/2)^{n/2}}(n+1)^{d/2}

(54)

for any
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

This bound implies that the error of the normalized states
|\Phi(t)\rangle
and
|\widetilde{\Phi}(t)\rangle
satisfies

\displaystyle\Bigl\||\Phi(t)\rangle-|\widetilde{\Phi}(t)\rangle\Bigr\|\leq\frac{\bigl\|\Phi(t)-\widetilde{\Phi}(t)\bigr\|}{\min\{\|\Phi(t)\|,\|\widetilde{\Phi}(t)\|\}}\leq\frac{\delta}{\|\Phi(t)\|-\delta},

(55)

where
\delta:=\max_{t}\bigl\|\Phi(t)-\widetilde{\Phi}(t)\bigr\|
. Recall that
\|\Phi(t)\|=(n+1)^{d/2}
by (
49
). To satisfy the inequality

\displaystyle\frac{\delta}{\|\Phi(t)\|-\delta}=\frac{\delta}{(n+1)^{d/2}-\delta}\leq\epsilon/2\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\delta\leq\frac{\epsilon/2}{1+\epsilon/2}(n+1)^{d/2},

(56)

based on (
54
), we choose
n
so that

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{g^{\prime}}{(n/2)^{n/2}}(n+1)^{d/2}\leq\frac{\epsilon/2}{1+\epsilon/2}(n+1)^{d/2},

(57)

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle(n/2)^{n/2}\geq\frac{4g^{\prime}(1+\epsilon/2)}{\pi\epsilon}.

(58)

Since
\epsilon/2\leq 1
, and noticing the condition
n\geq 6
in
Lemma
1
, it suffices to select

\displaystyle n=\max\biggl\{2\Bigl\lceil\frac{\log(\omega)}{\log(\log(\omega))}\Bigr\rceil,6\biggr\},

(59)

where

\displaystyle\omega=\frac{4g^{\prime}}{\pi\epsilon}.

(60)

2.3
Hamiltonian simulation with product formulas

Early work of Wiesner
[
66
]
and Zalka
[
67
]
used the so-called split-operator method to simulate real-space quantum dynamics on quantum computers. This method uses the truncated Fourier series to discretize the Schrödinger equation in space and construct a discrete Hamiltonian system, with the Hamiltonian as a sum of the potential and kinetic operators. The diagonal potential operator is encoded in the position space, and the kinetic operator is diagonalized by quantum Fourier transform in the momentum space. The kinetic and potential operators are propagated independently, and these time evolutions are combined using product formulas.
Subsequently, Kassal et al.
[
41
]
applied this method to chemical dynamics. However, these previous works do not provide rigorous error analysis.
In particular, they all replace the continuous kinetic operator by the discretized one without accounting for the discretization error, as discussed in
[
42
, Theorem 4]
.

Having derived the Fourier spectral method with concrete real-space error analysis to obtain the discrete Hamiltonian system (
45
), we now describe the simulation using product formulas. Given a Hamiltonian
\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B}
, the standard
2k
th-order Suzuki product formula
[
60
]
is defined recursively as

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}^{T}_{2}(t)

\displaystyle:=e^{-i\frac{t}{2}\mathbf{A}}\cdot e^{-it\mathbf{B}}\cdot e^{-i\frac{t}{2}\mathbf{A}},

(61)

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}^{T}_{2k}(t)

\displaystyle:=\mathscr{S}_{2k-2}(u_{k}t)^{2}\mathscr{S}_{2k-2}((1-4u_{k})t)\mathscr{S}_{2k-2}(u_{k}t)^{2}

(62)

where
u_{k}:=1/(1-4^{1/(2k-1)})
.
In our problem, the Hamiltonian
{\mathbf{\widetilde{H}}}_{n,d}(t)
in (
46
) is the sum of

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{F}^{s}_{n,d}{\mathbf{L}}_{n,d}(\mathbf{F}^{s}_{n,d})^{-1},

(63)

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{V}_{n,d}(t).

(64)

Instead of directly simulating

in the momentum space. In other words,

\displaystyle i\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}{t}}|\widetilde{\Phi}(t)\rangle=\mathbf{F}^{s}_{n,d}\mathbf{L}_{n,d}(\mathbf{F}^{s}_{n,d})^{-1}|\widetilde{\Phi}(t)\rangle\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad i\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}{t}}|c(t)\rangle=\mathbf{L}_{n,d}|c(t)\rangle,

(65)

by (

Therefore, the split-operator method with the

th-order Suzuki product formula for simulating (

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}^{B}_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{F}^{s}_{n,d})^{-1}e^{-i\frac{t}{2}\mathbf{V}_{n,d}}\cdot\mathbf{F}^{s}_{n,d}e^{-it\mathbf{L}_{n,d}}\cdot(\mathbf{F}^{s}_{n,d})^{-1}e^{-i\frac{t}{2}\mathbf{V}_{n,d}},

(66)

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}^{B}_{2k}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{S}_{2k-2}(u_{k}t)^{2}\mathscr{S}_{2k-2}((1-4u_{k})t)\mathscr{S}_{2k-2}(u_{k}t)^{2}.

(67)

2.5
Interacting multi-particle systems

Now we consider simulating a multi-particle Schrödinger equation

\displaystyle i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Phi(\mathbf{x},t)=\Big[-\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}+f(\mathbf{x},t)\Big]\Phi(\mathbf{x},t)

(80)

with a fixed number of particles
\eta
in
d
dimensions, interacting through a potential function
f(x)
.
Here
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{\eta d}
represents the positions of the particles, where entries
x_{(j-1)d+1},\ldots,x_{jd}
indicate the position of particle
j\in[\eta]
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
represents time,
\nabla=\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\right)\Big|_{i=1}^{\eta d}
, and
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{\eta d}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is the potential function of the Schrödinger equation.
As above, we consider the case where
f
is independent of time in this section.
Also, we assume for simplicity that all particles have the same mass; this is easily generalized to the case of particles with different masses, as discussed in
Section
4.1
.

To make simulation tractable, we consider an
\eta d
-dimensional hypercubic domain
\Omega=[0,1]^{\eta d}
and assume the wave function can be treated as periodic on this domain
[
42
,
47
,
58
,
8
]
. The periodic boundary condition is natural for crystalline solids. As for a non-periodic system subject to a long-range potential such as the Coulomb potential, we can embed the system into a sufficiently large periodic box
\Omega
such that the particles remain far from the boundary. Then, we can implement quantum simulations within the periodic box
\Omega
because the tail of the wave function outside of
\Omega
is negligible. In this case, one can imagine the full space
\mathbb{R}^{\eta d}
is covered by repeated copies of the potential function restricted to
\Omega
, but the periodic images of the potential outside of the box
\Omega
do not significantly interact with the wave functions supported on
\Omega

[
47
,
58
,
8
]
. In practice, it is not necessary to simulate the periodic images of the potential function outside of
\Omega
.

We also want the potential function
f(\mathbf{x})
to be bounded, i.e.,
\|f(\mathbf{x})\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\|f\|_{\max}
. However, typical potentials arising in physics include singularities, such as the divergence of the Coulomb potential for two particles at the same location. We can handle this by modifying the potential in a way that does not significantly affect the solution at relevant length scales. For example, a
d
-dimensional generalization of the Coulomb potential can be modified as
[
42
]

\displaystyle f_{\text{Coulomb}}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq\eta}\frac{q_{i}q_{j}}{\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{d}\bigl(x_{(i-1)d+k}-x_{(j-1)d+k}\bigr)^{2}+\Delta^{2}}},

(81)

where
q_{i}
is the charge of the
i
th particle and
\Delta>0
serves to keep the potential bounded
[
42
]
. Letting
q:=\max_{i}|q_{i}|
, we have

\displaystyle\|f(\mathbf{x})\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\frac{\eta(\eta-1)q^{2}}{2\Delta}=\|f\|_{\max}.

(82)

The parameter
\Delta
captures how closely the modified Coulomb potential (
81
) approximates the unbounded potential. To accurately reproduce the behavior of the unbounded Coulomb potential, we would like to simulate the model for small
\Delta>0
, and we expect the complexity of the simulation to grow with
1/\Delta
as a consequence of the upper bound (
82
). In practice, the modified Coulomb potential (
81
) should give a good approximation of the original Coulomb potential provided particles remain separated by distances large compared with
\Delta
.

As considered in
[
13
,
42
,
46
]
, we analyze the gate complexity of implementing the sparse Hamiltonian oracle, where we count a query to the modified Coulomb potential in the implementation as one gate.

Theorem
4
and

directly imply quantum algorithms for simulating (
80
) using product formulas and the truncated Taylor series method, respectively.

3
Simulating time-dependent Schrödinger equations

So far, we have focused on quantum algorithms for simulating systems with time-independent potentials. However, we saw in
Section
2.1
that the Fourier spectral method can be readily applied to time-dependent Schrödinger equations, retaining exponential convergence. Thus the quantum simulation problem for time-dependent potentials effectively reduces to a time-dependent Hamiltonian simulation problem with a discretized Hamiltonian (
46
) of the form
H(t)=A+B(t)
. In this section, we apply known methods for simulating time-dependent Hamiltonians
[
46
,
14
]
to give concrete bounds on the complexity of simulating time-dependent Schrödinger equations in real space.

3.1
Review of time-dependent Hamiltonian simulation methods

We take a detour from the real-space simulation problem to motivate the two main techniques used in this section: Hamiltonian simulation in the interaction picture
[
46
]
and the rescaled Dyson-series algorithm
[
14
, Section 4]
.

Suppose we want to simulate a time-dependent Hamiltonian of the form
H(t)=A+B(t)
. The quantum state evolves as

\displaystyle\ket{\psi(t)}=\mathcal{T}\left[e^{-i\int^{t}_{0}H(s)\mathrm{d}s}\right]\ket{\psi(0)},

(85)

where
\mathcal{T}
is the time-ordering operator, so that
\mathcal{T}\left[e^{-i\int^{t}_{0}H(s)\mathrm{d}s}\right]=\lim_{r\to\infty}\prod^{r}_{j=1}e^{-iH(jt/r)t/r}
.

Define the interaction-picture Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{I}(t):=e^{iAt}B(t)e^{-iAt}

(86)

and
\ket{\psi_I(t)}:=e^{iAt}\ket{\psi(t)}
for all
t
. Moving to the interaction picture may be advantageous since
\|H_{I}(t)\|=\|B(t)\|\leq\|H(t)\|
. One can easily check that

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\ket{\psi_I(t)}=H_{I}(t)\ket{\psi_I(t)}.

(87)

In
[
46
]
, the time-dependent Hamiltonian simulation problem (
87
) is addressed using the Dyson-series technique, giving query and gate complexity that scales with the
\max
-norm of the interaction Hamiltonian
H_{I}(t)

[
46
, Section 5]
. This can be improved to scale with the
L^{1}
-norm of
H_{I}(t)
using the rescaled Dyson-series algorithm
[
14
, Section 4]
.

For a time-dependent Hamiltonian
H(t)
, we define the rescaled Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}(\varsigma):=\frac{H(g^{-1}(\varsigma))}{\|H(g^{-1}(\varsigma))\|_{\max}},

(88)

where

\displaystyle g(t):=\int^{t}_{0}\|H(s)\|_{\max}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}s

(89)

is the
L^{1}
-norm of
\|H(t)\|_{\max}
. Further, we define

\displaystyle\|H\|_{\max,1}:=\int_{0}^{T}\|H(t)\|_{\max}\mathrm{d}t=g(T),

(90)

where
T
denotes the total simulation time. A key observation is that the rescaling of the Hamiltonian does not affect the target state:

\displaystyle\ket{\psi(t)}=\mathcal{T}\left[e^{-i\int^{t}_{0}H(s)\,\mathrm{d}s}\right]\ket{\psi(0)}=\mathcal{T}\left[e^{-i\int^{g(t)}_{0}\widetilde{H}(\varsigma)\,\mathrm{d}\varsigma}\right]\ket{\psi(0)}.

(91)

Given this rescaling procedure, if we have

1.

an algorithm that simulates the rescaled Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}(\varsigma)
for
0\leq\varsigma\leq T
with
L^{\infty}
-norm cost, i.e., with complexity
O(T\|\tilde{H}(\varsigma)\|_{\max,\infty})
where

\displaystyle\|\tilde{H}(\varsigma)\|_{\max,\infty}=\sup_{\varsigma\in[0,T]}\|\tilde{H}(\varsigma)\|_{\max},

(92)

and

2.

the ability to compute
g^{-1}(\varsigma)
for any
\varsigma\in[0,T]
and the max-norm
\|H(s)\|_{\max}
for any
s\in[0,T]
(so that we have access to the rescaled Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}(\varsigma)
for any
\varsigma
),

then we are able to simulate the original Hamiltonian
H(T)
for
0\leq t\leq T
with
L^{1}
-norm cost, i.e., with complexity
O(\int^{T}_{0}\|H(t)\|_{\max}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}t)
. To see this, note that
\|\tilde{H}(\varsigma)\|_{\max}=1
for all
\varsigma\in[0,g(T)]
. Therefore, if we apply the simulation algorithm with
L^{\infty}
-norm cost to
\tilde{H}(\varsigma)
for
0\leq\varsigma\leq g(T)
, the cost is bounded by

g(T)
, which is the the
L^{1}
-norm of
\|H(s)\|_{\max}
:

\displaystyle g(T)\|\tilde{H}(\varsigma)\|_{\max,\infty}=g(T),

(93)

which is the
L^{1}
norm.

Now, we apply the above rescaling procedure to the interaction-picture Hamiltonian
H_{I}(t)
(
86
). The rescaled interaction-picture Hamiltonian
\widetilde{H}_{I}(\varsigma)
satisfies

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}_{I}(\varsigma)=e^{iAg^{-1}(\varsigma)}\widetilde{B}(\varsigma)e^{-iAg^{-1}(\varsigma)},

(94)

where

\displaystyle\widetilde{B}(\varsigma):=B(g^{-1}(\varsigma))/\|B(g^{-1}(\varsigma))\|_{\max}

(95)

stands for the rescaled operator for
B
.

Lemma 4
.

The max-norm of the rescaled interaction-picture Hamiltonian
\widetilde{H}_{I}(\varsigma)
as defined in (
94
) is bounded by
1
for any
0\leq\varsigma\leq g(t)
:

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{H}_{I}(\varsigma)\|_{\max}\leq 1.

(96)

Proof.

First, note that the max-norm of any matrix is upper bounded by its
\ell_{2}
-norm, we have

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{H}_{I}(\varsigma)\|_{\max}\leq\|\widetilde{H}_{I}(\varsigma)\|\leq\|e^{iAg^{-1}(\varsigma)}\|\|\widetilde{B}(\varsigma)\|\|e^{-iAg^{-1}(\varsigma)}\|\leq\|\widetilde{B}(\varsigma)\|.

(97)

The last two steps hold because the matrix
\ell_{2}
-norm is sub-multiplicative:
\|AB\|\leq\|A\|\|B\|
, while the unitary operators
e^{iAg^{-1}(\varsigma)}
and
e^{-iAg^{-1}(\varsigma)}
have unit
\ell_{2}
-norm. Since the rescaled operator
\widetilde{B}(\varsigma)
is diagonal, its max-norm and
\ell_{2}
-norm are equally
1
. This proves our claim that
\|\widetilde{H}_{I}(\varsigma)\|_{\max}\leq 1
.
∎

Similarly, if we have an
L^{\infty}
-norm algorithm (e.g., the standard Dyson-series simulation) that simulates the rescaled interaction-picture Hamiltonian
\widetilde{H}_{I}(\varsigma)
for
0\leq\varsigma\leq g(t)
as well as the access to rescaled Hamiltonian
\widetilde{H}_{I}(\varsigma)
, we can simulate the original Hamiltonian
H(s)
with the total cost
O(g(t))=O(\int^{t}_{0}\|B(s)\|_{\max}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}s)
. Note that although the Hamiltonian is a sum of two operators,
H(t)=A+B(t)
, the simulation cost only depends on the
L^{1}
-norm of
\|B(s)\|_{\max}
and not
A
. This is the advantage of using the interaction picture simulation method.

3.2
Block-encoding of the rescaled interaction-picture Hamiltonian

The input model of the truncated Dyson series method is a unitary oracle for a so-called block-encoding (defined in
Appendix

). In the present context, the values of the potential

can be produced by an evaluation oracle (

Efficient simulations of

.

Interaction picture simulation is adventageous when it is easy to implement

, even for large

. When simulating the time-dependent Schrödinger equation (

\displaystyle A=\mathbf{F}^{s}_{n,d}{\mathbf{L}}_{n,d}(\mathbf{F}^{s}_{n,d})^{-1},

(98)

where
{\mathbf{L}}_{n,d}
is an explicit diagonal matrix and
\mathbf{F}^{s}_{n,d}
is the multi-dimensional quantum shifted Fourier transform (QSFT). The transformation
\mathbf{F}^{s}_{n,d}
and its inverse can be performed with gate complexity
{O}(d\log n\log\log n)

[

e^{iAt}
can be simulated as
\mathbf{F}^{s}_{n,d}e^{i{\mathbf{L}}_{n,d}t}(\mathbf{F}^{s}_{n,d})^{-1}
. By standard techniques (see for example Rule 1.6 in
[
22
, Section 1.2]
),

can be simulated with gate complexity
\tilde{O}(d\log n)

Lemma 5

Let
B\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{w}\times 2^{w}}
be a diagonal matrix. Suppose we have access to the evaluation oracle
U_{f}
that returns the values of diagonal entries of

in binary as

\displaystyle U_{f}\ket{j}\ket{t}\ket{0^{z}}=\ket{j}\ket{t}\ket{f_{j}(t)}=\ket{j}\ket{t}\ket{B_{jj}(t)},\quad\forall j\in[2^{w}]-1,

(99)

where
B_{jj}=f_{j}(t)
is a
z
-bit binary description of the
j
th diagonal entry of
B(t)
. Additionally, suppose we have the following two oracles
6
6

6

This is a standard assumption; see for example
[
14
, Section 4.2]
.
that implement the inverse change-of-variable and compute the max-norm:

\displaystyle O_{\inv}\ket{\varsigma,z}

\displaystyle=\ket{\varsigma,z \oplus g^{-1}(\varsigma)},

(100)

\displaystyle O_{\nrm}\ket{\tau,z}

\displaystyle=\ket{\tau, z\oplus\|B(\tau)\|_{\max}}.

(101)

Then we can implement the following evaluation oracle
U_{\widetilde{B}}

We now discuss the cost of simulating an
\eta

. However, more advanced numerical techniques for
\eta

[

and the fast multipole method
[

can evaluate the
\eta

is a constant.

In particular, for
d=3
the Barnes-Hut algorithm
[

]
proceeds as follows. We divide the unit cube into cubic cells in an octree structure of height
h
until each cell contains at most one particle. We then compute the pairwise Coulomb interactions between particles in nearby cells. Finally, we approximate the remaining Coulomb interactions between particles in distant cells by treating nearby particles as a single large particle located at their geometric center. The cost of this approach is as follows.

Lemma 9

(
[
54
]
)
.

For a system of
\eta
particles in a three-dimensional unit cube,
the Barnes-Hut algorithm
[

]
of height

[

as a subroutine to estimate the Coulomb interaction between well-separated clusters of particles, the reulting multipole-based Barnes-Hut algorithm approximates the total Coulomb potential to accuracy
\epsilon
in time
O(\eta h\log^{2}(1/\epsilon))
.

In particular, the error introduced by the fast multipole subroutine is bounded as follows.

Lemma 10

(
[
33
]
)
.

Suppose that
k
particles of charge
q_{j}
for

are located within a sphere centered at

. Then after a reusable preprocessing step with time complexity

, for any point

at a distance

from the origin, the

\phi(P)
to accuracy
\frac{Q}{r-r_{s}}\big(\frac{r_{s}}{r}\big)^{p+1}
in time

, where
Q:=\sum_{j=1}^{k}|q_{j}|
.

This method can be generalized to approximate the
d
-dimensional modified Coulomb potential defined in
Lemma
5
. We analyze the complexity of this method in a random-access memory (RAM) model, which allows for fast retrieval of the information stored in the tree data structure. In particular, to use this algorithm on a quantum computer, we work in the quantum RAM (QRAM) model, in which we can perform a memory access gate

\displaystyle\ket{j}\ket{y}\ket{x_1,\ldots,x_m}\mapsto\ket{j}\ket{y \oplus x_j}\ket{x_1,\ldots,x_m}

(115)

at unit cost. Note that implementing this operation with elementary two-qubit gates requires
\Omega(m)

[

qubits.
We leave it as an open question whether similar performance can be achieved without QRAM.

Lemma 11
.

Under the setting of
Theorem
8
, the modified Coulomb potential evaluation oracle
U_{f}
defined in
Lemma
5
can be approximately implemented
using
O(\eta(4d)^{d/2}\log^{3}(d/\Delta))
one- and two-qubit gates and
O(\eta(4d)^{d/2}\log(1/\Delta))
QRAM operations.
The error of this evaluation is of the same order as the error due to the difference between the modified Coulomb potential and the actual Coulomb potential.

Proof.

We implement
U_{f}
using a straightforward generalization of the multipole-based Barnes-Hut algorithm to the
d
-dimensional case. Specifically, we divide the simulation region into hypercubic cells in a tree structure, so that the out-degree of each node is
2^{d}
. The subdivision stops when each cell contains at most one particle or its length is at most
\delta:=\Delta^{2}/(D_{\max}\sqrt{d})
, where
D_{\max}
is the maximum possible distance of two particles in the simulation region (we call such a cell a
leaf cell
). Since our simulation region is a
d
-dimensional unit hypercube, we have
D_{\max}\leq\sqrt{d}
.

If multiple particles are in the same leaf cell, we move them to the center of the cell (a distance of at most
\delta\sqrt{d}
) and calculate their Coulomb interactions. We now discuss the relative error caused by this movement. For two particles with charges
q_{1},q_{2}
and distance
D
, the error of this step is at most

\displaystyle\frac{q_{1}q_{2}}{\sqrt{D^{2}+\Delta^{2}}}-\frac{q_{1}q_{2}}{\sqrt{(D+\delta\sqrt{d})^{2}+\Delta^{2}}}\leq\frac{q_{1}q_{2}}{\sqrt{D^{2}+\Delta^{2}}}-\frac{q_{1}q_{2}}{\sqrt{D^{2}+\Delta^{2}+2D\delta\sqrt{d}}}.

(116)

On the other hand, the error between the actual Coulomb potential and the modified Coulomb potential defined in (
81
) is

\displaystyle\frac{q_{1}q_{2}}{D}-\frac{q_{1}q_{2}}{\sqrt{D^{2}+\Delta^{2}}}.

(117)

Since our simulation region is a unit hypercube, we have
D\leq\sqrt{d}
, so the error introduced by the truncation is at most

\displaystyle\frac{q_{1}q_{2}}{\sqrt{D^{2}+\Delta^{2}}}-\frac{q_{1}q_{2}}{\sqrt{D^{2}+3\Delta^{2}}},

(118)

which is of the same order as the error between the modified Coulomb potential and the actual Coulomb potential.

During the process of subdividing the simulation region, we construct and store the corresponding tree structure in the following way. For each node in the tree, we perform the preprocessing step of
Lemma
9
(generalized to
d
dimensions), store the geometric center of all the electrons in the corresponding cell, and maintain
2^{d}
pointers storing the memory locations of its children (for use with subsequent QRAM operations). Since the height of the tree is at most
\log(1/\Delta)
, the tree structure can be constructed with gate complexity
O(2^{d}\eta\log(1/\Delta))
. We assume that our QRAM operates on the entire memory space in which the tree data structure is computed, so we do not require separate QRAM writing operations during the construction of the tree.

As in
Lemma
9
, we can use this tree structure to approximate the Coulomb potential on any particle
P
to any desired accuracy
\epsilon
in a recursive way. We maintain a set of nodes
\mathcal{S}
during the recursion, where all the cells corresponding to the nodes in
\mathcal{S}
have the same size. Initially, we set
\mathcal{S}
to contain only the root cell. At each recursive step, we create a set
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
to store the children of all the nodes in
\mathcal{S}
and set
\mathcal{S}
to
\varnothing
. Then we enumerate through all the nodes in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
. Let
l
denote the length of the cell currently being processed and
D
the distance from the geometric center of the cell to
P
. If we reach a leaf cell, we include the Coulomb interaction between the cluster of particles in this cell and
P
by computing exact pairwise interactions. If we reach a cell with
l/D<\frac{1}{2\sqrt{d}}
, we include the Coulomb interaction between the cluster of particles in this cell and
P
by using the
p
th-order fast multipole approximation. Since in the latter case all the particles of this cell are within a hyperball with radius
l\sqrt{d}/2
, the error of the
p
th-order fast multipole method is at most

\displaystyle\frac{Q}{D-l\sqrt{d}/2}\Big(\frac{l\sqrt{d}}{2D}\Big)^{p+1},

(119)

where
Q
denotes the absolute sum of all the charges in this cell.
Choosing
p=\log(d/\Delta^2)=O(\log(d/\Delta))
, the cumulative error of the fast-multipole approximations is
O(Q\Delta^{2}/Dd)
. This is of the same order as the error of the modified Coulomb potential, which is at least of order

\displaystyle\Omega\Big(\frac{Q}{D}-\frac{Q}{\sqrt{D^{2}+\Delta^{2}}}\Big)=\Omega\Big(\frac{Q\Delta^{2}}{Dd}\Big)

(120)

(since
D\leq\sqrt{d}
for a
d
-dimensional hypercubic simulation region).
If we did not reach a leaf cell and
l/D\geq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{d}}
, we add the corresponding node into the set
\mathcal{S}
. The recursive process stops when both
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
are empty.

Observe that in any recursive step, the set
\mathcal{S}
essentially stores all the cells of some specific size
l
that are either not leaf cells or that have distances
D
to
P
that are not far enough (in particular, those with
D\leq\frac{1}{2l\sqrt{d}}
). Hence, during the recursive process, the size of
\mathcal{S}
can be upper bounded by
O(d^{d/2})
, whereas the size of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
can be upper bounded by
O((4d)^{d/2})
. Thus, there are in total
O((4d)^{d/2})
lookup operations to the tree structure, or equivalently,
O((4d)^{d/2})
queries to the QRAM.

Moreover, since the height of the tree is at most
O(\log(1/\Delta))
, the number of recursive steps is at most
O(\log(1/\Delta))
. Thus the gate complexity for evaluating the Coulomb potential for one particle is

\displaystyle O((4d)^{d/2}p^{2}\log(1/\Delta))=O((4d)^{d/2}\log^{3}(d/\Delta)).

(121)

Therefore, the overall complexity is
O(\eta(4d)^{d/2}\log^{3}(d/\Delta))
, and the overall number of QRAM queries is
O(\eta(4d)^{d/2}\log(1/\Delta))
, as claimed.
∎

4
Applications

In this section, we study the applications of quantum simulation in real space. Our targets are several computational problems of fundamental importance in quantum chemistry, solid-state physics, and optimization.

4.1
Quantum chemistry

One of the most well-studied applications of quantum simulation is the electronic structure problem in quantum chemistry, which aims to determine ground states or low-lying excited states of the electronic Hamiltonian of molecules
[
10
,
20
]
. To prepare eigenstates, quantum simulation can either be used as a subroutine in quantum phase estimation, or directly used in an adiabatic state preparation procedure. Thus our improved simulations could potentially be used to give faster algorithms for these eigenstate determination problems.

However, we focus here on quantum simulation of chemical dynamics, which go beyond static properties to consider dynamical effects in chemical recation processes. To describe chemical dynamics, we start from the exact molecular Schrödinger equation

\displaystyle i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Phi(\mathbf{x},t)=\hat{H}_{m}\Phi(\mathbf{x},t)=\Big[\hat{T}_{n}+\hat{H}_{e}\Big]\Phi(\mathbf{x},t),

(124)

where the molecular Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{m}=\hat{T}_{n}+\hat{H}_{e}
is a sum of the nuclear kinetic energy

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{n}=-\sum_{A=1}^{\eta_{n}}\frac{1}{2M_{A}}\nabla^{2}_{A}

(125)

and the electronic Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{e}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\eta_{e}}\nabla^{2}_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{1}{|\hat{r}_{i}-\hat{r}_{j}|}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,A}\frac{Z_{A}}{|\hat{r}_{i}-\hat{R}_{A}|}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{A\neq B}\frac{Z_{A}Z_{B}}{|\hat{R}_{A}-\hat{R}_{B}|},

(126)

where
\hat{r}_{i}
and
\hat{R}_{A}
are the positions of the
i
th electron and the
A
th nucleus, and
M
and
Z
are the mass and the atomic number of the nucleus, respectively. This problem can be simplified using the well-known Born-Oppenheimer approximation. In this approximation, the nuclear kinetic energy
\hat{T}_{n}
is neglected, the positions of the nuclei
\hat{R}
are fixed, and the time-independent electronic Schrödinger equation

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{e}\chi(\hat{r},\hat{R})=E_{e}(\hat{R})\chi(\hat{r},\hat{R})

(127)

is solved to obtain the electronic wave function
\chi(\hat{r},\hat{R})
and the electronic energy eigenvalue
E_{e}(\hat{R})
. Varying
\hat{R}
and repeatedly solving (
127
), one can obtain the potential energy surface
E_{e}(\hat{R})
as a function of the nuclear positions. Then, the time-dependent Schrödinger equation of the nuclei dynamics

\displaystyle i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Psi(\hat{R},t)=\Big[\hat{T}_{n}+E_{e}(\hat{R})\Big]\Psi(\hat{R},t).

(128)

can be solved separately.
Since the nuclei move much more slowly than the electrons, this approach often provides a good approximation and leads to a more practical method.

In classical computational chemistry, the Born-Oppenheimer approximation is often used to simplify calculations for chemical reactions, because the overall cost of calculating
E_{e}(\hat{R})
from (
127
) with varying
\hat{R}
and then calculating
\Psi(\hat{R},t)
from (
128
) is less than the cost of simulating the full dynamics (
124
). Such an approximation can be trusted if the potential energy surfaces of the electronic states are well separated. However, Kassal et al.
[
41
]
pointed out that simulating the full dynamics on a quantum computer will not only yield more accurate results, but can also be faster than the Born-Oppenheimer approximation. In particular, Fig. 3 of
[
41
]
shows that the computational resources for fitting a potential energy surface
E_{e}(\hat{R})
from interpolation increases exponentially with the atomic number
Z_{A}
, while the cost of simulating the full dynamics only increases polynomially. For chemical reactions with more than 4 atoms, it is more efficient for a quantum computer to evolve all the nuclei and electrons than to use the Born-Oppenheimer approximation
[
41
]
.

As an application of multi-particle Schrödinger equation, we apply
Theorem
7
on the full dynamics (
124
). Similar to (

), generalizations of electron-electron, electron-nucleus, and nucleus-nucleus Coulomb potentials are modified as

\displaystyle f_{\text{e-e}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{A\neq B}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{3}\bigl(x_{3(i-1)+k}-x_{3(j-1)+k}\bigr)^{2}+\Delta^{2}}},

(129)

\displaystyle f_{\text{n-e}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,A}\frac{Z}{\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{3}\bigl(x_{3(i-1)+k}-x_{3(A-1)+k}\bigr)^{2}+\Delta^{2}}},

(130)

\displaystyle f_{\text{n-n}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{A\neq B}\frac{Z^{2}}{\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{3}\bigl(x_{3(A-1)+k}-x_{3(B-1)+k}\bigr)^{2}+\Delta^{2}}}.

4.2
Uniform electron gas

The uniform electron gas, also known as jellium, is a simple theoretical model of delocalized electrons in a metal. It considers a large system of interacting electrons with a homogeneous jelly-like continuum of positive background charge, so that the entire system is charge-neutral
[

Despite its simplicity, the jellium model can be classically hard to simulate in some regimes, and it is widely used as a benchmark problem for new classical quantum simulation methods

For concreteness, we consider

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\sum^{\eta}_{i=1}\frac{\hat{p}^{2}_{i}}{2m}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{e^{2}}{|\hat{r}_{i}-\hat{r}_{j}|}+\hat{H}_{e-b}+\hat{H}_{b-b},

(135)

\displaystyle=-e^{2}\eta\sum^{\eta}_{i=1}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{|\hat{r}_{i}-R|}\mathrm{d}R,

(136)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{b-b}

\displaystyle=e^{2}\eta^{2}\int_{\Omega^{2}}\frac{1}{|R-R^{\prime}|}\mathrm{d}R\mathrm{d}R^{\prime}.

(137)

The background-background interaction operator

is constant. Because of the homogeneity of jellium, the electron-background operator

as constant operators merely add a global phase to the wave function in the quantum evolution.

Similarly to Eq. (

\displaystyle f_{e-e}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{e^{2}}{\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{3}\bigl(x_{3(i-1)+k}-x_{3(j-1)+k}\bigr)^{2}+\Delta^{2}}}.

5
Conclusions

In this paper, we conducted a systematic study of quantum algorithms for simulating real-space dynamics.
We also gave applications to several computational problems in quantum chemistry, solid-state physics, and optimization.

Our work also leaves several other natural open questions for future investigation:

•

Can we achieve better bounds using additional assumptions about the potential? For instance, in Hamiltonian simulation, faster quantum algorithms can be achieved using commutator bounds for various classes of Hamiltonians
[
26
]
and Lieb-Robinson bounds for geometrically local Hamiltonians
[
34
]
. It is natural to investigate whether such ideas can be incorporated into simulation of real-space dynamics.

•

Can we prove lower bounds on the number of queries to the quantum evaluation oracle? There is an
\Omega(T)
quantum lower bound for simulating finite-dimensional systems known as the “no-fast-forwarding theorem”
[
12
]
, and we believe that the same bound holds for real-space simulation. Can we prove lower bounds in terms of other parameters such as the spatial dimension and the simulation error?

•

Since the Fourier spectral method is widely used to solve PDEs, it might be possible to generalize our results to other kinds of PDEs with various boundary conditions. Due to the diverse conditions and properties related to the existence, uniqueness, and well-posedness of the solutions, it may be difficult to merge solvers of different types of PDEs into a unified algorithmic framework. Future work might begin by focusing on PDEs with a similar structure to the Schrödinger equation.

•

One approach to simulating quantum field theories is to relate them to multi-particle Schrödinger equations
[
67
]
. Related ideas have been discussed in the literature
[
40
,
52
]
, but to the best of our knowledge, a systematic and rigorous treatment of the simulation cost is lacking.

•

The recent paper
[
57
]
proved that the probability density function
\psi_{s}
of a continuous
version of the stochastic gradient descent algorithm satisfies the Fokker–Planck–Smoluchowski equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi_{s}}{\partial t}=\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}\psi_{s}+\Big(\frac{\nabla^{2}f}{2}-\frac{\|\nabla f\|_{2}^{2}}{2}\Big)\psi_{s},

(159)

which is similar to the Schrödinger equation except for the absence of the imaginary unit
i
. It might be worth exploring whether the dynamics of stochastic gradient descent could be accelerated using real-space quantum simulation.

•

We generalize the Barnes-Hut algorithm to construct a quantum evaluation oracle for Coulomb interactions with
O(\eta(4d)^{d/2}\log^{3}(d/\Delta))
gates and
O(\eta(4d)^{d/2}\log(1/\Delta))
additional QRAM operations, achieving a quadratic gate complexity advantage in
\eta
compared to the straightforward method of evaluating all
\eta^{2}
pairwise interactions (for constant dimension
d
). Can similar simulation performance can be achieved without QRAM?

Appendix A
Notation

Throughout the paper,
\mathbb{N}
denotes the set of all positive integers, and
\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. We also let
[n]:=\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
[{n+1}]_{0}:=\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
. We introduce a variety of norms that are used in our analysis.

Definition 1

(Vector norms)
.

For a vector
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},...,v_{d})^{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
, and
1\leq p\leq\infty
, the vector
l_{p}
norm of
\mathbf{v}
is

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{v}\|_{p}:=\begin{cases}\left(\sum^{d}_{j=1}|v_{j}|^{p}\right)^{1/p}&1\leq p<\infty\\
\max_{j=1,2,...,d}|v_{j}|&p=\infty.\end{cases}

(160)

Definition 2

(Matrix norms)
.

For a matrix
A\in M^{d\times d}(\mathbb{C})
, the Schatten
p
-norm of
A
is

\displaystyle\|A\|_{p}:=\begin{cases}\operatorname{Tr}\left(\sqrt{A^{\dagger}A}\right)&p=1\\
\sqrt{\operatorname{Tr}(A^{\dagger}A)}&p=2\\
\max_{\|\ket{\psi}\|_{2}=1}\|A\ket{\psi}\|_{2}&p=\infty.\end{cases}

(161)

The Schatten
\infty
-norm is also referred to as the spectral norm, as it equals the largest singular value of
A
. In this paper, we usually let
\|A\|
denote the spectral norm of
A
when the context is unambiguous.

Definition 3

(Function norms)
.

If
f\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{C}
is a continuous function defined on a set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the
L^{p}
-norm of the function
f
is

\displaystyle\|f\|_{p}:=\begin{cases}\left(\int_{\Omega}|f(x)|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/p}&1\leq p<\infty\\
\sup_{x\in\Omega}|f(x)|&p=\infty.\end{cases}

(162)

We combine these notations to define norms for vector-valued (or operator-valued) functions. If
\mathbf{v}\colon[0,t]\to\mathbb{C}^{d}
is a continuous vector-valued function, where the
j
th coordinate at time
s
is
v_{j}(s)
, then the notation
\|\mathbf{v}\|_{p,q}
indicates that we take the
l_{p}
norm
\|\mathbf{v}(s)\|_{p}
for every
s\in[0,t]
and compute the
L^{q}
-norm of the resulting scalar function. For instance,

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{v}\|_{1,\infty}:=\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\sum^{d}_{j=1}|v_{j}(s)|.

(163)

Definition 4
.

The largest matrix element of
A
in absolute value is denoted

\displaystyle\|A\|_{\max}:=\max_{j,k}|A_{j,k}|.

(164)

Note that
\|A\|_{\max}
is a vector norm of
A
but does not satisfy the sub-multiplicative property of a matrix norm.

Lemma 14

(
[
23
, Lemma 1]
)
.

For any Hermitian matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{N}\times\mathbb{C}^{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\|A\|_{\max}\leq\|A\|\leq N\|A\|_{\max}.

(165)

Finally, we introduce the notion of block encoding and some related concepts.

Definition 5

(Block encoding)
.

Suppose that
A
is an
s
-qubit operator,
\alpha,\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, and

. Then we say the
(s+a)
-qubit unitary

-block-encoding of

\displaystyle\|A-\alpha\left(\bra{0}^{\otimes a}\otimes\mathbbmss{1}_{s})U(\ket{0}^{\otimes a}\otimes\mathbbmss{1}_{s})\right)\|\leq\epsilon.

(166)

\displaystyle O_{c}\colon\ket{\ell}\ket{j}\to\ket{c_{\ell j}}\ket{j}\qquad\forall\ell\in[s_{c}],\,j\in[2^{\omega}]-1,

Appendix B
Exponential convergence of the Fourier spectral method

Here we establish a concrete error estimate for the Fourier spectral method.

Lemma 16

(
[
17
, Theorem 20]
)
.

Let
f(x)
have the exact, infinite trigonometric polynomial representation

\displaystyle f(x)=\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}\cos(kx)+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\beta_{k}\sin(kx).

(173)

Let
S_{n+1}(x)
denote the trigonometric polynomial that interpolates to a function
f(x)
on (
26
),

\displaystyle S_{n+1}(x)=\frac{a_{0}}{2}+\sum_{k=0}^{n/2}a_{k}\cos(kx)+\sum_{k=0}^{n/2}b_{k}\sin(kx).

(174)

Then the error from the Fourier spectral method satisfies

\displaystyle|f(x)-S_{n+1}(x)|\leq 2\sum_{k=n/2+1}^{\infty}(|\alpha_{k}|+|\beta_{k}|).

(175)

Lemma
16
tells us that the error from the Fourier spectral method is bounded by an infinite sum of the Fourier coefficients of
f(x)
. It is well-known that the Fourier coefficients decay rapidly in terms of
n
, which is formally established as follows.

Lemma 17
.

Let
f(x)
have the exact, infinite Fourier series representation

\displaystyle f(x)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{f}(k)e^{ikx}.

(176)

Assume
f(x)\in\mathbb{C}^{p}
. Then the Fourier coefficient
\hat{f}(k)
satisfies

\displaystyle|\hat{f}(k)|\leq\frac{\|f^{(p)}\|_{L^{1}}}{k^{p}}.

(177)

Proof.

The Fourier coefficient
\hat{f}(k)
on
[-\pi,\pi]
is

\displaystyle\hat{f}(k)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x)e^{-ikx}\mathrm{d}{x}.

(178)

Using
f(x)\in\mathbb{C}^{p}
and
f^{(k)}(-\pi)=f^{(k)}(\pi)
for arbitrary
k\in\mathbb{N}
, integrating by parts
p
times, we obtain

\displaystyle\hat{f}(k)=\frac{(-i)^{k}}{2\pi k^{p}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f^{(p)}(x)e^{-ikx}\mathrm{d}{x}.

(179)

Thus, the magnitude of the Fourier coefficient has the gbound

\displaystyle|\hat{f}(k)|\leq\frac{1}{2\pi k^{p}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|f^{(p)}(x)|\mathrm{d}{x}=\frac{\|f^{(p)}\|_{L^{1}}}{2\pi k^{p}}

(180)

as claimed.
∎

Note that the coefficients of the trigonometric polynomial and Fourier series of
f(x)
are related as

\displaystyle\hat{f}(k)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}(\alpha_{k}+i\beta_{k}),\qquad&k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+};\\
\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{0},\qquad&k=0;\\
\frac{1}{2}(\alpha_{k}-i\beta_{k}),\qquad&k\in\mathbb{Z}^{-}.\end{cases}

(181)

Combining
Lemma
16
with
Lemma
17
gives an error bound for the Fourier spectral method.

Lemma 18
.

Under the same assumptions as
Lemma
16
, the error from the Fourier spectral method satisfies

\displaystyle|f(x)-S_{n+1}(x)|\leq\frac{2\|f^{(p)}\|_{L^{1}}}{\pi(p-1)(n/2+1)^{p-1}}.

(182)

Proof.

Using (
175
) and (
181
), we upper bound the error by

\displaystyle|f(x)-S_{n+1}(x)|\leq 2\sum_{k=n/2+1}^{\infty}(|\alpha_{k}|+|\beta_{k}|)\leq 4\sum_{k=n/2+1}^{\infty}|\hat{f}(k)|.

(183)

Plugging in (
177
) gives

\displaystyle\sum_{k=n/2+1}^{\infty}|\hat{f}(k)|\leq\sum_{k=n/2+1}^{\infty}\frac{\|f^{(p)}\|_{L^{1}}}{2\pi k^{p}}.

(184)

Such an infinite sum can be bounded by an infinite integral, using

\displaystyle\sum_{k=n/2+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^{p}}\leq\sum_{k=n/2+1}^{\infty}\int_{k-1}^{k}\frac{1}{y^{p}}\mathrm{d}{y}=\int_{n/2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{y^{p}}\mathrm{d}{y}=\frac{1}{(p-1)(n/2+1)^{p-1}}.

(185)

Therefore

\displaystyle|f(x)-S_{n+1}(x)|\leq\frac{2\|f^{(p)}\|_{L^{1}}}{\pi(p-1)(n/2+1)^{p-1}}

(186)

as claimed.
∎

Since we are concerned with analytic
f(x)
, it suffices to choose
p=n/2
(for
n\geq 6
) to obtain the following result.

Lemma 19
.

If
f(x)
is analytic, according to
Lemma
16
, the error from the Fourier spectral method satisfies

\displaystyle|f(x)-S_{n+1}(x)|\leq\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{\|f^{(n/2)}\|_{L^{1}}}{(n/2)^{n/2}}

(187)

for any even integer
n\geq 6
.

This result implies an exponential convergence rate in terms of
n
.
