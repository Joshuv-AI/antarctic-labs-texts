Counterfactual Sensitivity in Equilibrium Models
Bas Sanders, Harvard University

Abstract
Counterfactuals in equilibrium models are functions of the current state of the world, the exogenous change variables and the model parameters. Current practice treats the current state of the world, the observed data, as perfectly measured, but there is good reason to believe that they are measured with error. The main aim of this paper is to provide tools for quantifying uncertainty about counterfactuals, when the current state of the world is measured with error. I propose two methods, a Bayesian approach and an adversarial approach. Both methods are practical and theoretically justified. I apply the two methods to the application in Adao, Costinot, and Donaldson 2017 and find non-trivial uncertainty about counterfactuals.



1
Introduction

After economists have introduced an equilibrium model, they are interested
in answering various counterfactual questions. For example, what is
the effect on welfare when the trade costs between a set of countries
decreases because of an infrastructure investment? How will the expenditure
shares change if some countries obtain a novel technology which makes
them more productive at producing certain goods? These counterfactual
questions generally have the same structure: what will happen to some
function of the endogenous variables when the exogenous variables
change in some specific way?

In this paper, I introduce a general notational framework that allows
us to study counterfactual analyses in equilibrium models. I discuss
three important assumptions, of which the most crucial is that the
model exactly matches the data. These assumptions are general and
for example are satisfied by two common classes of models, invertible
models and exact hat algebra models. Under these assumptions, we can
write the counterfactual change variable of interest as a function
of the observed data and change variables that describe the counterfactual
question we are interested in. This insight allows us to think about
the question of how measurement error in the data feeds forward to
the counterfactual change variable of interest.

To answer this question, I provide two methods to use the introduced
measurement error to do uncertainty quantification for the counterfactual
change variable of interest. The first method takes a Bayesian approach,
and it requires a model for the measurement error and a prior on the
true data. Using these, we can obtain a posterior distribution on
the counterfactual change variable of interest and hence can compute
Bayesian credible sets. The second method considers the problem where
an adversary tries to change the conclusions by the largest amount
by adding perturbations to the data, subject to some constraints on
the magnitude of those perturbations. This avoids the distributional
assumption of the Bayesian approach by focusing on worst-case performance.
For now, the adversarial approach relies on a first order Taylor approximation.
Note that these uncertainty quantification methods can also be interpreted
as sensitivity analysis methods, by systematically varying the measurement
error variance or the bounds on the adversarial perturbations.

I apply these methods for uncertainty quantification to my running
example, which is the Armington model
(
Armington 1969
)

and to the results in
Adao, Costinot, and Donaldson 2017
. For both applications
I model mismeasurement in trade flows. For the Armington model, I
find substantial heterogeneity in sensitivity when varying the counterfactual
question, the baseline values of the data, the model parameters and
the degree of measurement error. Even though I can explain part of
this heterogeneity using economic intuition, this finding illustrates
that the sensitivity measure can vary in rich ways which need not
be obvious ex ante. This motivates case-by-case uncertainty quantification.

For the counterfactual analysis in
Adao, Costinot, and Donaldson 2017
,
which considers China’s welfare effects from joining the WTO, I estimate
the measurement error variances of log trade flows using the mirror
trade dataset of
Linsi, Burgoon, and Mügge 2023
. From this dataset I obtain
a panel with two noisy measures per period for each bilateral trade
flow, one from the importer and one from the exporter. This allows
me to estimate the measurement error variance-covariance matrix. Using
the calibrated measurement error model I replicate the main figure
which plots the change in China’s welfare from 1996 to 2011. I find
wide confidence regions, with the upper bound being often around twice
as large as the estimate.

I mainly contribute to the small literature on improving counterfactual
calculations in quantitative spatial economics and international trade.
The main paper in this literature is
Dingel and Tintelnot 2020
, which
studies exact hat algebra in granular settings. There, the authors
consider models which in addition to assuming that the data are perfectly
explainable, also assume there is a continuum of agents. Then, in
the case where there are only a handful of observations, individual
idiosyncrasies do not wash out and are incorporated the model, which
causes overfitting and bad performance out-of-sample. I focus on the
complementary question of uncertainty quantification, which arises
even in non-granular settings. Another important paper in the literature
of understanding counterfactual analysis in equilibrium models is

Adao, Costinot, and Donaldson 2017
. In that paper, the authors provide
a nonparametric generalization of exact hat algebra as initially introduced
by
Dekle, Eaton, and Kortum 2008
. I extend this insight even further by
using a general notational framework to provide more general sufficient
conditions for when we can apply exact hat algebra.

I make a novel connection between the econometrics literature of measurement
error modeling and the literatures on quantitative spatial economics
and international trade. The papers
Goes 2023
and

Linsi, Burgoon, and Mügge 2023
argue that there is measurement error in
trade data, and
Aruoba et al. 2016
estimates measurement
error in GDP using two different measures, which is similar to how
I estimate the measurement error variance of log trade flows. However,
these papers do not discuss how to incorporate measurement error into
counterfactual analyses for equilibrium models, for which I propose
two novel approaches.

The Bayesian approach is under-explored in the literature on international
trade and quantitative spatial economics, with the exception of
Ranjan and Tobias 2007
.
There, the authors use a Bayesian hierarchical model to estimate trade
flows. In work in progress, I will use a similar procedure to replace
the flat prior that I currently use for the Bayesian approach. The
adversarial approach has not yet been considered in the context of
equilibrium models. In the machine learning literature,
Balda, Behboodi, and Mathar 2019

considers the first-order adversarial approach for a general regression
problem. In the current version of this paper I am also relying on
local linearity, but in work in progress I will discuss relaxing this
assumption.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. The next section introduces
the setting I consider and discusses the underlying assumptions that
are made to do counterfactual analyses in equilibrium models. Section

3
explains which sources of uncertainty
are relevant for equilibrium models and outlines how I add mismeasurement.
Section
4
introduces my two methods for
uncertainty quantification and discusses heterogeneity in sensitivity.
Section
5
applies my methods to the application
in
Adao, Costinot, and Donaldson 2017
and discusses how to use mirror
trade flows to estimate the measurement error variance-covariance
matrix. Section
6
concludes.

2
Unrestricted Equilibrium Models

This section provides a general notational framework for equilibrium
models. I introduce the assumptions we need to do counterfactual analyses
and discuss two common classes of equilibrium models.

2.1
Notation and Assumptions

Consider an equilibrium, denoted by
\left(X_{O},X_{U},N_{O},N_{U}\right)
.
Here,
X_{O}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{X,O}}
are observed exogenous variables,

X_{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{X,U}}
are unobserved exogenous variables,

N_{O}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{N,O}}
are observed endogenous variables,
and
N_{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{N,U}}
are unobserved endogenous variables.
These equilibrium variables are unrestricted in the sense that they
are non-stochastic, i.e. not assumed to be drawn from an underlying
distribution. They are dependent, and connected to each other through
the equilibrium conditions. These are given by

f\left(X_{O},X_{U},N_{O},N_{U};\theta\right)=\boldsymbol{0},

(1)

for some function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d_{X,O}+d_{X,U}+d_{N,O}+d_{N,U}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{N,O}+d_{N,U}}

and some parameter vector
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}}
. We
need the following two assumptions on the function
f\left(\cdot;\theta\right)
:

Assumption 1

(Uniqueness)
.

For any
\left(X_{O},X_{U}\right)
, there
exists a (possibly up to a multiplicative constant) unique
\left(N_{O},N_{U}\right)

such that (
1
) holds exactly.

Assumption 2

(Model exactly matches the data)
.

For any
\left(X_{O},N_{O}\right)
, there exists
some
\left(X_{U},N_{U}\right)
such that (
1
) holds
exactly.

Assumption
1
states that the equilibrium conditions
yield a, potentially implicit, unique solution for the endogenous
variables
\left(N_{O},N_{U}\right)
as a function of the exogenous
variables
\left(X_{O},X_{U}\right)
. Note that this is a theoretical
relationship, and in practice we cannot actually solve these equilibrium
conditions for the endogenous variables if some exogenous variables
are unobserved. This assumption will not hold if there are multiple
equilibria, which I rule out in this paper. Assumption
2

states that the model exactly matches the data, in that the unobserved
variables
X_{U}
and
N_{U}
take on values such that the equilibrium
conditions (
1
) hold exactly. Note that this assumption
does not require uniqueness, and it will often be the case that we
cannot separately identify the elements of the unobserved variables

X_{U}
and
N_{U}
.

Starting from these equilibrium conditions, researchers are interested
in how the endogenous variables
\left(N_{O},N_{U}\right)
adjust
if we change the exogenous variables from their baseline values
\left(X_{O},X_{U}\right)

to some counterfactual values
\left(X_{O}^{{}^{\prime}},X_{U}^{{}^{\prime}}\right)
.
By assumption
1
, we know that for each
\left(X_{O}^{{}^{\prime}},X_{U}^{{}^{\prime}}\right)
,
there exists a unique
\left(N_{O}^{{}^{\prime}},N_{U}^{{}^{\prime}}\right)
, so that
the counterfactual equilibrium is denoted by
\left(X_{O}^{{}^{\prime}},X_{U}^{{}^{\prime}},N_{O}^{{}^{\prime}},N_{U}^{{}^{\prime}}\right)
.
In practice, however, we may not observe, or be to able to solve for,
the levels of all baseline variables. For when this is the case, researchers
might still be interested in the question of what will happen to the
endogenous variables when they change the exogenous variables proportionally.
To answer these kinds of proportional counterfactual questions, we
do not need knowledge of the levels of all baseline variables. For
this scenario, it will be convenient to also define the equilibrium
of “change variables”
\left(\hat{X}_{O},\hat{X}_{U},\hat{N}_{O},\hat{N}_{U}\right)
,
where
\hat{X}_{O,j}=\frac{X_{O,j}^{{}^{\prime}}}{X_{O,j}}
,
\hat{X}_{U,j}=\frac{X_{U,j}^{{}^{\prime}}}{X_{U,j}}
,

\hat{N}_{O,j}=\frac{N_{O,j}^{{}^{\prime}}}{N_{O,j}}
and
\hat{N}_{U,j}=\frac{N_{U,j}^{{}^{\prime}}}{N_{U,j}}
.

Our goal is then to solve for the endogenous change variables
\hat{N}_{O}

and
\hat{N}_{U}
as functions of the observed data, which are
X_{O}

and
N_{O}
, and the counterfactual questions we are interested in,
described by the exogenous change variables
\hat{X}_{O}
and
\hat{X}_{U}
.
We hence aim to find a new system of equations

g\left(X_{O},N_{O},\hat{X}_{O},\hat{X}_{U},\hat{N}_{O},\hat{N}_{U};\theta\right)=\boldsymbol{0},

(2)

for some function
g:\mathbb{R}^{2d_{X,O}+2d_{N,O}+d_{X,U}+d_{N,U}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{N,O}+d_{N,U}}
.
This leads to the third assumption we will need:

Assumption 3
.

For any equilibrium conditions such that assumptions

1
and
2
hold, there exists
a system of equations (
2
) that for any
\left(X_{O},N_{O},\hat{X}_{O},\hat{X}_{U}\right)

allows us to find a (possibly up to a multiplicative constant) unique
solution
\left(\hat{N}_{O},\hat{N}_{U}\right)
.

Assumption
3
states that to uniquely solve for
the endogenous change variables, we only need knowledge of the data
and the counterfactual questions of interest.

Finally, denote a scalar counterfactual change variable in which the
researchers are ultimately interested in by
\hat{k}
. This counterfactual
change variable of interest could for example be a change in welfare
or simply one of the endogenous change variables. It will generally
depend on the observed variables and the change variables:

\hat{k}=k_{\mathrm{full}}\left(X_{O},N_{O},\hat{X}_{O},\hat{X}_{U},\hat{N}_{O},\hat{N}_{U};\theta\right).

for some function
k_{\mathrm{full}}:\mathbb{R}^{2d_{X,O}+2d_{N,O}+d_{X,U}+d_{N_{U}}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
However, under assumption
3
we can uniquely solve
(
2
) for
\left(\hat{N}_{O},\hat{N}_{U}\right)

once we know
\left(X_{O},N_{O},\hat{X}_{O},\hat{X}_{U}\right)
.
This leads to the following proposition:

Proposition 1
.

Under assumptions
1
,

2
and
3
, we can write the counterfactual
change variable of interest
\hat{k}
as a function of the observed
data
\left(X_{O},N_{O}\right)
and the exogenous change variables

\left(\hat{X}_{O},\hat{X}_{U}\right)
:

\displaystyle\hat{k}

\displaystyle=k\left(X_{O},N_{O},\hat{X}_{O},\hat{X}_{U};\theta\right),

(3)

for some function
k:\mathbb{R}^{2d_{X,O}+d_{N,O}+d_{X,U}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.

2.2
Two Common Classes of Models

I briefly discuss two common classes of models from the literature
on equilibrium models: invertible models and exact hat algebra models.

2.2.1
Invertible Models

There is a subset of these equilibrium models in which there is a
one-to-one mapping between the exogenous variables and the endogenous
variables. For those models, we are able to invert the model and identify
all the exogenous variables from only knowing the observed endogenous
variables. Such models are called invertible in the quantitative spatial
literature
(
Redding and Rossi-Hansberg 2017
)
. In particular, the unobserved
exogenous variables
X_{U}
can be recovered from the data
X_{O}

and
N_{O}
, and there cannot be unobserved endogenous variables,
so
N_{U}=\left\{\right\}
. One could argue that
X_{U}
also
should be an empty set because we can always recover it, but here
I will consider the unobserved exogenous variables to be variables
that are not directly observed in the data. In these models, we can
exactly track the mechanism through which counterfactual exogenous
variables map to the levels of counterfactual endogenous variables.
The procedure will be to first solve for the unobserved exogenous
variable
X_{U}
from the data
X_{O}
and
N_{O}
using assumption

2
, and then solve for the endogenous variables
N_{O}^{{}^{\prime}}

from the counterfactual exogenous variables,
X_{U}^{{}^{\prime}}
and
X_{O}^{{}^{\prime}}

using assumption
1
.

2.2.2
Exact Hat Algebra Models

Another important subset contains exact hat algebra models. For these
models, at least one of
X_{U}
and
N_{U}
is non-empty and cannot
be recovered from the observed variables
X_{O}
and
N_{O}
. We
therefore can never identify the counterfactual levels and hence need
to shift our focus from levels to changes. To satisfy assumption
3

for these models, we need that the endogenous change variables must
not depend on the levels of the unobserved variables. Loosely, in
the literature, this is often achieved by having homogeneity or a
specific ratio structure in the equilibrium conditions.

2.3
Running Example

The simplest workhorse model in trade is the Armington model
(
Armington 1969
)
,
as for example outlined in
Costinot and Rodríguez-Clare 2014
. There are countries
indexed by
i,j=1,...,n
, each endowed with
Q_{i}
units of a distinct
good (
Q_{i}
can also be interpreted as the productivity of country

i
for making its good). Preferences are CES, so utility, which
equals real consumption, for country
j
is given by

C_{j}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{ij}^{\frac{1-\sigma}{\sigma}}C_{ij}^{\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}}\right)^{\frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1}},\qquad j=1,...,n.

Here,
\psi_{ij}
is an exogenous preference shifter,
C_{ij}
is
the demand for good
i
in country
j
and
\sigma>1
is the elasticity
of substitution between goods from different countries. Trade between
countries is subject to iceberg trade costs, which means that in order
to sell one unit of a good in country
j
, country
i
must ship

\tau_{ij}\geq 1
units, with
\tau_{ii}=1
. It follows that the
price of the good from country
i
in country
j
is
P_{ij}=Y_{i}\tau_{ij}/Q_{i}
,
where
Y_{i}
is the total income in country
i
.

The relevant exogenous variables are then endowments
\left\{Q_{i}\right\}
,
trade costs
\left\{\tau_{ij}\right\}
and demand shifters
\left\{\psi_{ij}\right\}
.
The endogenous variables are income levels
\left\{Y_{i}\right\}

and expenditure shares
\left\{\lambda_{ij}\right\}
. Hence, using
the general notation of this paper, we have

\displaystyle X_{O}

\displaystyle=\left\{\right\}

\displaystyle X_{U}

\displaystyle=\left(\left\{Q_{i}\right\},\left\{\tau_{ij}\right\},\left\{\psi_{ij}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle N_{O}

\displaystyle=\left(\left\{Y_{i}\right\},\left\{\lambda_{ij}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle N_{U}

\displaystyle=\left\{\right\}.

The only parameter of the model is the international elasticity of
substitution
\sigma
. Defining
\varepsilon=\sigma-1
, what is
called the trade elasticity in
Costinot and Rodríguez-Clare 2014
, it can be
shown that the equilibrium conditions are:

\displaystyle Y_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\lambda_{ij}Y_{j},\qquad i=1,...,n,

(4)

\displaystyle\lambda_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\psi_{ij}\tau_{ij}Y_{i}\right)^{-\varepsilon}Q_{i}^{\varepsilon}}{\sum_{k}\left(\psi_{kj}\tau_{kj}Y_{k}\right)^{-\varepsilon}Q_{k}^{\varepsilon}},\qquad i,j=1,...,n.

(5)

We can plug the second set of equations into the first set of equations
and solve for income levels
\left\{Y_{i}\right\}
as functions
of the exogenous variables. By Walras’ Law, income levels are only
pinned down up to a multiplicative constant. Having solved for
\left\{Y_{i}\right\}
,
we can then also solve for
\left\{\lambda_{ij}\right\}
. Note
that this version of the Armington model is an exact hat algebra model
because we cannot identify the baseline levels of the exogenous variables.

Now, say we are interested in the counterfactual where we change the
trade costs
\left\{\tau_{ij}\right\}
to
\left\{\tau_{ij}^{{}^{\prime}}\right\}
,
so assume that endowments
\left\{Q_{i}\right\}
and demand shifters

\left\{\psi_{ij}\right\}
stay constant. In appendix
A.1

it is shown that we can find:

\displaystyle\hat{Y}_{i}Y_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\hat{\lambda}_{ij}\lambda_{ij}\hat{Y}_{j}Y_{j},\qquad i=1,...,n,

(6)

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\hat{\tau}_{ij}\hat{Y}_{i}\right)^{-\varepsilon}}{\sum_{k}\left(\hat{\tau}_{kj}\hat{Y}_{k}\right)^{-\varepsilon}\lambda_{kj}},\qquad i,j=1,...,n.

So we now have obtained a system of equations in
\left\{\hat{Y}_{i}\right\}

and
\left\{\hat{\lambda}_{ij}\right\}
, where the solution only
depends on the parameters of the model, the assumed counterfactual
changes and the observed baseline variables. By again plugging in
the second set of equations into the first one, we can then solve
for the changes in income levels
\left\{\hat{Y}_{i}\right\}
which
again, by Walras’ Law, are only pinned down up to a multiplicative
constant, and subsequently solve for
\left\{\hat{\lambda}_{ij}\right\}
.
For completeness, in this case
f\left(\cdot;\theta\right)
and
g\left(\cdot;\theta\right)

are

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}Y_{1}-\sum_{j}\lambda_{1j}Y_{j}\\
\vdots\\
Y_{n}-\sum_{j}\lambda_{nj}Y_{j}\\
\lambda_{11}-\frac{\left(\psi_{11}\tau_{11}Y_{1}\right)^{-\varepsilon}Q_{1}^{\varepsilon}}{\sum_{k}\left(\psi_{k1}\tau_{k1}Y_{k}\right)^{-\varepsilon}Q_{k}^{\varepsilon}}\\
\lambda_{12}-\frac{\left(\psi_{12}\tau_{12}Y_{1}\right)^{-\varepsilon}Q_{1}^{\varepsilon}}{\sum_{k}\left(\psi_{k2}\tau_{k2}Y_{k}\right)^{-\varepsilon}Q_{k}^{\varepsilon}}\\
\vdots\\
\lambda_{nn}-\frac{\left(\psi_{nn}\tau_{nn}Y_{n}\right)^{-\varepsilon}Q_{n}^{\varepsilon}}{\sum_{k}\left(\psi_{kn}\tau_{kn}Y_{k}\right)^{-\varepsilon}Q_{k}^{\varepsilon}}\end{array}\right)\;\mathrm{and}\;\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\hat{Y}_{1}Y_{1}-\sum_{j}\hat{\lambda}_{1j}\lambda_{1j}\hat{Y}_{j}Y_{j}\\
\vdots\\
\hat{Y}_{n}Y_{n}-\sum_{j}\hat{\lambda}_{nj}\lambda_{nj}\hat{Y}_{j}Y_{j}\\
\hat{\lambda}_{11}-\frac{\left(\hat{\tau}_{11}\hat{Y}_{1}\right)^{-\varepsilon}}{\sum_{k}\left(\hat{\tau}_{k1}\hat{Y}_{k}\right)^{-\varepsilon}\lambda_{k1}}\\
\hat{\lambda}_{12}-\frac{\left(\hat{\tau}_{12}\hat{Y}_{1}\right)^{-\varepsilon}}{\sum_{k}\left(\hat{\tau}_{k2}\hat{Y}_{k}\right)^{-\varepsilon}\lambda_{k2}}\\
\vdots\\
\hat{\lambda}_{nn}-\frac{\left(\hat{\tau}_{nn}\hat{Y}_{n}\right)^{-\varepsilon}}{\sum_{k}\left(\hat{\tau}_{kn}\hat{Y}_{k}\right)^{-\varepsilon}\lambda_{kn}}\end{array}\right),

respectively. The relevant counterfactual change variables of interest
are the changes in the endogenous variables or the changes in welfare,
which in this case correspond to the changes in real consumption
\left\{\hat{C}_{i}\right\}
.
In
Costinot and Rodríguez-Clare 2014
, it is shown that these changes in welfare
are

\hat{C}_{i}=\hat{\lambda}_{ii}^{-1/\varepsilon},\qquad i=1,...,n.

3
Uncertainty and Sensitivity

Having introduced a general notational framework, we can now examine
the various types of uncertainty we need to consider. In particular,
I will focus on mismeasurement and parameter estimation error.

3.1
Sources of Uncertainty

Currently, equilibrium models imply perfect confidence in the counterfactual
conclusions. From proposition
1
we know that
the counterfactual change variable of interest can be written as a
function of the data and the exogenous change variables:
\hat{k}=k\left(X_{O},N_{O},\hat{X}_{O},\hat{X}_{U};\theta\right)
.
From this insight, we learn that there are various reasons to distrust
these counterfactual conclusions. The first reason is model misspecification,
which considers concerns about the mapping
k\left(\cdot;\theta\right)
,
which might not be the true mapping if the model is misspecified.
The second reason is the validity of the counterfactual question of
interest, which is described by the exogenous change variables
\hat{X}_{O}

and
\hat{X}_{U}
. When we are interested in the effects of a specific
economic policy, it is not always clear how to formulate this using
the exogenous change variables of the model. The third reason is uncertainty
about the current state of the world, which are the data
X_{O}

and
N_{O}
. These economic variables can come from various data
sources, and are often measured with error. The fourth reason is estimation
error in the structural parameter
\theta
. In this paper, I will
only consider measurement error in the observed data and parameter
estimation error.

3.2
Adding Mismeasurement to the Model

Ortiz-Ospina and Beltekian 2018
and
Goes 2023
highlight
that there are large discrepancies between and within various data
sources from trade and international economics. Hence, instead of
the true
X_{O}
and
N_{O}
, I assume that we observe some noisy
versions
\tilde{X}_{O}
and
\tilde{N}_{O}
, respectively, where

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\tilde{X}_{O}\\
\tilde{N}_{O}\end{array}\right)=m\left(X_{O},N_{O},\xi\right),

(7)

for some measurement error variable
\xi
and some measurement error
function
m\left(\cdot\right)
. In this paper, I will assume that

m\left(\cdot\right)
is known and that we can estimate the distribution
of
\xi
.

3.3
Parameter Estimation

The function that maps the data and exogenous change variables to
the counterfactual change variable of interest also depends on a structural
parameter
\theta
. Often this structural parameter is taken from
the literature and is assumed to have no uncertainty around it. However,
when this
\theta
is estimated using the data, denoted by
\hat{\theta}\left(X_{O},N_{O}\right)
,
we have to incorporate the resulting estimation error into our uncertainty
quantification. Note that even if we would perfectly observe
\left(X_{O},N_{O}\right)
,
we would still have estimation error equal to
\hat{\theta}\left(X_{O},N_{O}\right)-\theta.

By not considering this, our uncertainty quantification will yield
confidence regions that are too small. In work in progress, I work
out how to combine uncertainty due to estimation error with uncertainty
due to measurement error.

3.4
Running Example (Continued)

For the Armington model, I will consider measurement error in bilateral
trade flows
F_{ij}
. This is plausible because in
Linsi, Burgoon, and Mügge 2023

it is shown that there are so-called mirror discrepancies in bilateral
trade flows between almost all countries. This means that, for instance,
while the value that Germany reports it imported from France and the
value that France reports it exported to Germany should be the same,
in practice they are often different. Trade flows are relevant for
the Armington model because they are the underlying data that determine
the expenditure shares through
\lambda_{ij}=\frac{F_{ij}}{\sum_{\ell}F_{\ell j}}.

Because trade flows are non-negative, I assume measurement error to
be log-normal:

\log\tilde{F}_{ij}=\log F_{ij}+\xi_{ij},\quad\xi_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\varsigma_{ij}^{2}\right),

and I constrain the covariances of measurement errors to be zero.
In section
5
, I will calibrate this measurement
error model using mirror trade data. I do not consider measurement
error in own-country trade flows.

One should be careful when taking this model to the data. Looking
at the equilibrium condition for income of country
i
in
6
,
we observe that this equation only depends on endogenous observed
variables. With real data, this equation will very rarely hold exactly.
This could be due to measurement error or to model misspecification,
which are hard to disentangle. This issue is resolved by introducing
a supplementary slack variable

\rho_{i}=\sum_{j}\lambda_{ij}Y_{j}-Y_{i},\qquad i=1,...,n,

which is the difference between gross expenditure and gross production
in country
i
and is assumed to be constant as we perform our counterfactual
analyses.

Remark 1
.

In appendix
A.2
, I consider an
exactly identified version of the two-country Armington model where
trade costs and exogenous preference shifters are assumed to be observed.
In that case, we can exactly track how measurement errors in various
observed variables affect the levels of the counterfactual unobserved
variables, and how these errors ultimately propagate to the levels
of the counterfactual endogenous variables.

4
Two Methods for Uncertainty Quantification

After introducing mismeasurement to equilibrium models, I now provide
a framework for how to think about mapping the introduced uncertainty
in the data to uncertainty about the counterfactual change variable
of interest
\hat{k}
. I consider two approaches, which rely on different
assumptions and information from the researcher. The first approach
requires both a model for the measurement error and a prior on the
true data, whereas the second approach only requires some bounds on
how far the data could be off. I apply these methods to the Armington
model and find substantial heterogeneity in sensitivity.

4.1
Bayesian Uncertainty Quantification

Taking a Bayesian approach, we can regard the observed noisy data

\tilde{X}_{O}
and
\tilde{N}_{O}
in (
7
) as noisy
signals of the true data
X_{O}
and
N_{O}
. Hence, after having
specified a prior on the true data and a model for the measurement
error, we can use Bayes’ rule to obtain the posterior
\left(X_{O},N_{O}\right)|\left(\tilde{X}_{O},\tilde{N}_{O}\right)

using conjugate distributions or MCMC. In this version of the paper
I will only consider flat priors, which is unrealistic in many economic
settings. In work in progress I will discuss non-flat priors that
are relevant for the settings considered.

Towards uncertainty quantification, we can draw from the posterior
of the true data given the noisy data and for each of these draws
calculate the counterfactual of interest
\hat{k}
using equation
(
3
), keeping the exogenous change
variables
\hat{X}_{O}
and
\hat{X}_{U}
constant throughout this
exercise. We hence obtain many draws from the posterior of
\hat{k}
,
which allows us to obtain a Bayesian credible set for the counterfactual
change variable of interest. The Bayesian approach to uncertainty
quantification is theoretically simple, but may be computationally
expensive when choosing a non-standard prior. Furthermore, it requires
a model for measurement error and a prior on
\left(X_{O},N_{O}\right)
.

4.1.1
Frequentist Interpretation: Discussion on Delta Method Approach

If one opts to use a flat prior on the data, this allows for simple
computation of the posterior because it will be proportional to the
likelihood. For example, if one assumes the measurement errors are
(log-)normal, the posterior on the true data will also be (log-)normal:

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}X_{O}\\
N_{O}\end{array}\right)|\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\tilde{X}_{O}\\
\tilde{N}_{O}\end{array}\right)\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\tilde{X}_{O}\\
\tilde{N}_{O}\end{array}\right),\Sigma\right).

In this case, if the relationship between the counterfactual change
variable of interest
\hat{k}
and the data
\left(X_{O},N_{O}\right)

is approximately (log-)linear, we can actually also use a first-order
approximation, which will be equivalent to applying the delta method.
Then, the jacobian
\frac{\partial k}{\partial\left(X_{O},N_{O}\right)}

is constant and the delta method and the Bayesian approach agree:

\tilde{k}-\hat{k}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{\partial k}{\partial\left(X_{O},N_{O}\right)}\Sigma\frac{\partial k}{\partial\left(X_{O},N_{O}\right)}^{{}^{\prime}}\right),

for
\tilde{k}
and
\hat{k}
the noisy and the true counterfactual
change variable of interest, respectively. Therefore, under the strong
assumptions of (log-)linearity and (log-)normality, both approaches
have a frequentist interpretation. Consequently, if we are willing
to assume (log-)normal measurement error, we can gauge the degree
of non-linearity by comparing the Bayesian credible sets and first-order
confidence bands. If there is a substantial gap between them, we can
conclude that the (log-)linear approximation is not working well for
that specific combination of counterfactual question, baseline values
of the data and degree of measurement error.

From this discussion, one might argue that the Bayesian approach is
always preferred, because it is robust against non-linearity, and
can be extended to allow for non-normality of the measurement error.
There are however two advantages of the first-order approach that
are worth noting. The first is that it is easy to compute, as calculating
the jacobian
\frac{\partial k}{\partial\left(X_{O},N_{O}\right)}

just amounts to taking a single large numerical derivative. The second
is that, under (log-)normality of the measurement error and approximate
linearity, all uncertainty around
\hat{k}
is summarized by a single
value, namely the standard error
\sqrt{\frac{\partial k}{\partial\left(X_{O},N_{O}\right)}\Sigma\frac{\partial k}{\partial\left(X_{O},N_{O}\right)}^{{}^{\prime}}}
,
which is easier to report than a full posterior distribution. That
being said, due to non-linearities in equilibrium models, I recommend
to use the Bayesian approach rather than the first-order approach
when it is computationally feasible to compute Bayesian credible sets.

4.2
Adversarial Uncertainty Quantification

The Bayesian approach requires a prior on the data and a distributional
assumption on the measurement errors. A contrasting perspective is
taken by what I will call the adversarial approach to uncertainty
quantification. This approach borrows insights from the literature
on “adversarial perturbations” in machine learning, where very
small changes to images can lead neural networks to misclassify them
with high probability
(
Moosavi-Dezfooli et al. 2017
;
Balda, Behboodi, and Mathar 2019
)
.

I transfer these ideas to the setting in this paper and define the
data vector
D_{O}=\left(X_{O},N_{O}\right)^{{}^{\prime}}
and the perturbed
counterfactual function

\hat{k}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}=k_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\left(\cdot\right)=k\left(D_{O}+\boldsymbol{\eta},\hat{X}_{O},\hat{X}_{U},\hat{N}_{O},\hat{N}_{U}\right),

with
\boldsymbol{\eta}
some perturbation vector. The relevant problems
to solve then are:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\min}

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\eta}:\left|\boldsymbol{\eta}_{j}\right|\leq\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{j}\forall j}\hat{k}_{\boldsymbol{0}}-\hat{k}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\max}

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\eta}:\left|\boldsymbol{\eta}_{j}\right|\leq\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{j}\forall j}\hat{k}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}-\hat{k}_{\boldsymbol{0}}.

So for some constraints on the perturbation
\boldsymbol{\eta}
,
we are trying to find the largest deviations we can cause. If
D_{O}

is low-dimensional, we can brute-force solve these two problems. However,
as the dimension of
D_{O}
increases, this becomes increasingly
hard.

If we assume that
k_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\left(\cdot\right)
is approximately
linear in
D_{O}
on
\left[D_{O}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon},D_{O}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\right]
,
then it follows that

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{\min}

\displaystyle=-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}*\mathrm{sign}\boldsymbol{J}_{k}\left(D_{O}\right)^{{}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{\max}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\epsilon}*\mathrm{sign}\boldsymbol{J}_{k}\left(D_{O}\right)^{{}^{\prime}},

where
*
denotes element-wise multiplication, the function
\mathrm{sign}\left(\cdot\right)

returns the signs of each element of a vector, and
\boldsymbol{J}_{k}\left(D_{O}\right)

denotes the jacobian of
k\left(\cdot\right)
at
D_{O}
.

This first-order adversarial approach is crude, and suffers from the
same issues as the approach that uses the delta method for uncertainty
quantification. However, note that it still provides a useful lower
bound. Furthermore, we can get a sense of how close it is to the global
bound by either comparing the Bayesian and the first-order approach,
or by comparing the predicted values
\hat{k}\pm\boldsymbol{J}_{k}\left(D_{O}\right)\left\{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}*\mathrm{sign}\boldsymbol{J}_{k}\left(D_{O}\right)^{{}^{\prime}}\right\}

and the actual values
\hat{k}_{\pm\boldsymbol{\epsilon}*\mathrm{sign}\boldsymbol{J}_{k}\left(D_{O}\right)^{{}^{\prime}}}

at the first order bounds.

A relevant question for the adversarial approach to uncertainty quantification
is how to choose
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
. For this I will use the

10\%
confidence interval of the calibrated measurement error. So
if for example we consider normal measurement error with mean
0

and variance
\varsigma^{2}
, the
10\%
confidence interval will
be
\left[-0.13\varsigma,0.13\varsigma\right]
. Researchers could
also be agnostic about how large of a confidence interval for the
measurement error should be used to choose
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
.
They could then plot the resulting first-order lower bounds against
the sizes of these confidence intervals and draw a vertical line where
they think a valid choice is. I will illustrate such a sensitivity
analysis plot in section
5
.

4.3
Running Example (Continued)

Using the Armington model, I consider a simple two-country economy
with fundamentals

Q=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}3\\
1\end{array}\right)\quad\tau=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&2\\
2&1\end{array}\right)\quad\psi=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&1.5\\
0.5&1\end{array}\right)\quad\varepsilon=5.

So country 1 is three times as productive as country 2 and the bilateral
trade cost is twice as high as the own-country trade cost. Country
1 likes its own good better than country 2’s good, whereas country
2 likes country 1’s good better than its own good. The trade elasticity

\varepsilon
is assumed to by
5
, which is a standard choice in
the literature. Using these fundamentals I can compute the endogenous
variables:

Y=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1.64\\
1\end{array}\right)\quad\lambda=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0.95&0.08\\
0.05&0.92\end{array}\right).

Note that country 2’s income is normalized to one. I then act like
I only observe these endogenous variables and the model parameter,
and consider the counterfactual question

\hat{\tau}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&1.5\\
1.5&1\end{array}\right).

In words, this means “What happens to welfare, expenditure shares
and income if the bilateral trade costs between the two countries
increase by
50\%
?”.

4.3.1
Varying the Degree of Mismeasurement

For mismeasurement, I consider homoskedastic measurement error in
bilateral trade flows with variance
\varsigma^{2}
, zero covariances
and no measurement error in the own-country trade flows, so that
\varsigma_{12}^{2}=\varsigma_{21}^{2}=\varsigma^{2}
.
For the adversarial approach, I will use
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}=\left(0.13\varsigma,0.13\varsigma\right)^{{}^{\prime}}
,
as this corresponds to the
10\%
confidence interval of the measurement
error.

Then, in figure
1
, I vary the measurement error
variance over the range
\left[0,0.05\right]
and plot the resulting
bounds for the changes in income, the changes in expenditure shares
and the changes in welfare. For this simple two-country Armington
model, we can brute-force solve the global adversarial problems. The
resulting bounds are small compared to the Bayesian and first-order
approaches. From comparing the Bayesian and first-order approaches,
we find that the linear approximation performs well for the changes
in own-country expenditure shares and for changes in welfare, but
poorly for the changes in income and for the change in cross-country
trade shares. Interestingly, both the adversarial and the Bayesian
approaches seem to converge for some counterfactual change variables
of interest; at some point increasing the measurement error variance
will not result in larger confidence regions anymore.

Heterogeneous Counterfactual Sensitivity

To show that there is heterogeneity in sensitivity, I again consider

\hat{\tau}_{12}=\hat{\tau}_{21}
,
the model parameter
\varepsilon
, the underlying baseline value
of the data through
Q_{1}
, and the measurement error variance
\varsigma^{2}
.
The baseline values for
\hat{\tau}_{12}=\hat{\tau}_{21}
,

and

will be as above, and the baseline value for

will be

. To make the plots clearer, I now plot the relative

,
and for the change in country 1’s cross-country expenditure share,

\hat{\lambda}_{21}
. The resulting plot can be found in figure

,
in which in the first row I plot the relative lengths of the confidence

, and in the second row the relative

\hat{\lambda}_{12}
. In the

over the range

, in the second column I vary

over the range

,

It is striking that for the change in cross-country expenditure share

expenditure share, the linear approximation performs better and the

the non-linear behavior when the trade elasticity varies. This non-linearity
is probably due to the fact that the trade elasticity influences the

for the exactly
identified version of the Armington model.

Figure
2
demonstrates substantial heterogeneity
in sensitivity when varying the counterfactual question, the model
parameter and the data. To show that this heterogeneity in sensitivity
is not arbitrary, I provide some economic intuition for the Armington
model that explains part of the observed heterogeneity. Leveraging
the CES setup of the Armington model, we know that a transformation
of a country’s expenditure is proportional to its price index:

\left(\sum_{\ell}F_{\ell j}\right)^{-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\propto P_{j}=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\psi_{ij}^{-\varepsilon}P_{ij}^{-\varepsilon}\right)^{-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}},\qquad j=1,...,n.

The price index, and hence a country’s expenditure, turns out to be
a powerful predictor of how sensitive a country’s change in welfare
is to perturbations. To demonstrate this, I consider the Armington
model with
n
countries and draw many different economies from the
set of economies

\displaystyle\left\{\varepsilon\in\left[4,6\right],\;\left\{A_{i}\right\}\in\left[1,2\right]^{n},\;\tau_{ii}=1\>\forall i,\;\left\{\tau_{ij}\right\}\in\left[1,2\right]^{n\left(n-1\right)},\right.

\displaystyle\left.\quad\hat{\tau}_{ii}=1\>\forall i,\;\left\{\hat{\tau}_{ij}\right\}\in\left[\frac{1}{\underset{i,j}{\max}\>\tau_{ij}},2\right]^{n\left(n-1\right)},\;\psi_{ii}=1\>\forall i,\;\left\{\psi_{ij}\right\}\in\left[0.5,1.5\right]^{n\left(n-1\right)}\right\}.

The lower bound on each
\hat{\tau}_{ij}
ensures that it does not
become cheaper to trade with any other country than with your own
country. Then, for every country
j
in each of these drawn economies,
I calculate
\left(\sum_{\ell}F_{\ell j}\right)^{-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}

and the relative length of the Bayesian confidence region when adding
measurement errors to bilateral trade flows with variance
\varsigma^{2}=0.01

and zero covariances. Figure
3
shows a scatterplot
of the relative lengths against the measure of the price index for

n=8
. The resulting correlation coefficient is around
0.7
, and
it is robust against varying
n
. This is evidence for the fact that
as the price index of a country increases, its sensitivity to shocks
in the data also increases.

5
Application: Adao, Costinot and Donaldson
(2017)

In this section, I apply the methods from the paper to do uncertainty
quantification for the application in
Adao, Costinot, and Donaldson 2017
.
I calibrate a measurement error model using mirror trade data and
find non-trivial uncertainty about counterfactuals.

5.1
Model Setup

In the empirical application of
Adao, Costinot, and Donaldson 2017
, the
authors investigate the effects of China joining the WTO, the so-called
China shock. The relevant system of equations, the function
g\left(\cdot;\theta\right)

in (
2
), is

\displaystyle\left\{\hat{\lambda}_{ij,t}\lambda_{ij,t}\right\}

\displaystyle\in\chi_{j}\left(\left\{\hat{P}_{ij,t}\right\},\hat{Y}_{j,t}Y_{j,t}\right),\qquad i,j=1,...,n,\quad t=1,...,T,

\displaystyle\hat{Y}_{j,t}Y_{j,t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\hat{\lambda}_{ij,t}\lambda_{ij,t}\left(\hat{Y}_{j,t}Y_{j,t}+\rho_{j,t}\right),\qquad j=1,...,n,\quad t=1,...,T,

\displaystyle\hat{\omega}_{ij,t}

\displaystyle=\hat{Y}_{j,t}\hat{\tau}_{ij,t},\qquad i,j=1,...,n,\quad t=1,...,T.

Here,
\lambda_{ij,t}
denotes the expenditure share from country

i
in country
j
in period
t
,
\chi_{j}\left(\cdot\right)

denotes the factor demand system of country
j
,
\omega_{ij}^{n}

is the effective price for goods from country
i
in country j in
period
t
,
Y_{i,t}
denotes the total payment of country
i

in period
t
,
\rho_{i,t}
is the difference between aggregated
gross expenditure and gross production in country
i
in period
t
,
and
\tau_{ij,t}
denotes the trade cost between country
i
and

j
in period
t
. The authors are interested in the change in China’s
welfare resulting from counterfactual changes in trade costs,
\hat{\tau}_{ij,t}
,
such that Chinese trade costs are brought back to their 1995 levels:

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}_{ij,t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau_{ij,95}}{\tau_{ij,t}},\quad\mathrm{if}\>i\>\mathrm{or}\>j\>\mathrm{is}\>\mathrm{China,}

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}_{ij,t}

\displaystyle=1,\quad\mathrm{otherwise.}

So the counterfactual question of interest is what the change in China’s
welfare is due to joining the WTO. Here, welfare is defined as the
percentage change in income that the representative agent in China
would be indifferent about accepting instead of the counterfactual
change in trade costs from
\left\{\tau_{ji,t}\right\}
to
\left\{\tau_{ji,t}^{{}^{\prime}}\right\}
.

5.2
Uncertainty Quantification

One might argue that the counterfactual question of interest also
suffers from measurement error here, because it depends on the estimated
ratios of trade costs. However, I will only focus on measurement error
in the data. We observe a panel of trade flows, and similarly as for
the Armington model in section
3.4
, I conjecture
that instead of the true trade trade flows, we observe some noisy
estimate
\left\{\tilde{F}_{ij,t}\right\}
, which I model using
log-normally distributed measurement error with a variance-covariance
matrix of bilateral log trade flows that is constant over time. This
results in the following measurement error model, where for any two
trade flows:

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\log\tilde{F}_{ij,t}\\
\log\tilde{F}_{k\ell,t}\end{array}\right)\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\log F_{ij,t}\\
\log F_{k\ell,t}\end{array}\right),\Sigma_{ij,k\ell}\right).

We are then interested in estimating
\Sigma_{ij,k\ell}
. Note that
in general we do not restrict the various bilateral trade flows to
be uncorrelated within a time period so
\Sigma_{ij,k\ell}
does
not have to be diagonal. For each trade flow in each period, we have
two observations. The two observations come from the mirror trade
dataset from
Linsi, Burgoon, and Mügge 2023
. The first one is reported
by the exporter of the trade flow and the second on is reported by
the importer of the trade flow. We hence have

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\log\tilde{F^{1}}_{ij,t}\\
\log\tilde{F^{1}}_{k\ell,t}\end{array}\right),\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\log\tilde{F^{2}}_{ij,t}\\
\log\tilde{F^{2}}_{k\ell,t}\end{array}\right)\overset{\mathrm{ind.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}\left(\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\log F_{ij,t}\\
\log F_{k\ell,t}\end{array}\right),\Sigma_{ij,k\ell}\right),

and we can take the difference to find:

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\log\tilde{F^{1}}_{ij,t}\\
\log\tilde{F^{1}}_{k\ell,t}\end{array}\right)-\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\log\tilde{F^{2}}_{ij,t}\\
\log\tilde{F^{2}}_{k\ell,t}\end{array}\right)\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,2\Sigma_{ij,k\ell}\right).

It follows that an estimator of
\Sigma_{ij,k\ell}
is given by

\hat{\Sigma}_{ij,k\ell}=\frac{1}{2T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\log\tilde{F^{1}}_{ij,t}\\
\log\tilde{F^{1}}_{k\ell,t}\end{array}\right)-\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\log\tilde{F^{2}}_{ij,t}\\
\log\tilde{F^{2}}_{k\ell,t}\end{array}\right)\right\}\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\log\tilde{F^{1}}_{ij,t}\\
\log\tilde{F^{1}}_{k\ell,t}\end{array}\right)-\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\log\tilde{F^{2}}_{ij,t}\\
\log\tilde{F^{2}}_{k\ell,t}\end{array}\right)\right\}^{{}^{\prime}}.

For now, assume that the covariances of bilateral trade flows are
zero, so we have:

\Sigma_{ij,k\ell}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\varsigma_{ij}^{2}&0\\
0&\varsigma_{k\ell}^{2}\end{array}\right)\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\hat{\varsigma}_{ij}^{2}=\frac{1}{2T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\log\tilde{F^{1}}_{ij,t}-\log\tilde{F^{2}}_{ij,t}\right)^{2}.

In work in progress, I relax this assumption and consider the full
covariance structure.

So using the mirror trade data, we can obtain initial estimates for
the measurement error variances,
\left\{\hat{\varsigma}_{ij}^{2}\right\}
.
However, these estimates do not incorporate any specific country information.
I will leverage country information by considering two simple fixed
effect models. The first one assumes that a country contributes in
a similar way to the variance if it is an importer or an exporter:

\hat{\varsigma}_{ij}^{2}=e^{\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{j}+u_{ij}},

(8)

for
\alpha_{i}
and
\alpha_{j}
country fixed effects and for

u_{ij}
an error term. One might argue that the contributions of
importer and exporter to the measurement error variance are not equal.
Therefore I also fit a second fixed effect model:

\hat{\varsigma}_{ij}^{2}=e^{\alpha_{i,\mathrm{exp}}+\alpha_{j,\mathrm{imp}}+v_{ij}},

(9)

for
\alpha_{i,\mathrm{exp}}
and
\alpha_{j,\mathrm{imp}}
country-exporter
and country-importer fixed effects, respectively, and
v_{ij}
an
error term. Then, rather than using
\hat{\varsigma}_{ij}^{2}
I
will use the fitted value
\tilde{\varsigma}_{ij}^{2}=e^{\hat{\alpha}_{i}+\hat{\alpha}_{j}}

or
\check{\varsigma}_{ij}^{2}=e^{\hat{\alpha}_{i,\mathrm{exp}}+\hat{\alpha}_{j,\mathrm{imp}}}
,
which will increase the precision by aggregating information over
trade flows.

Having introduced the approach to estimating the measurement error
variance, I now turn to uncertainty quantification. For the specific
calibration, I consider three approaches. For the first approach I
use a uniform measurement error variance for all trade flows. This
measurement error variance is the average of
\left\{\tilde{\varsigma}_{ij}^{2}\right\}

obtained from model (
8
), but only considering
the countries France, Germany, Great Britain, Japan and the United
States, which are all generally considered to be reliable reporters
of trade data. This average can hence be interpreted as a lower bound
on the measurement error variance, and it equals
\varsigma_{LB}^{2}=0.0064

(the average using
\left\{\check{\varsigma}_{ij}^{2}\right\}

is comparable at
0.0081
). The second approach allows for heteroskedasticity
among countries, where I fit the model (
8
) for
all countries that are considered in
Adao, Costinot, and Donaldson 2017
.
For the third approach, I fit the model (
9
)
and allow for heteroskedasticity among countries and whether a country
is an importer or an exporter. In appendix
A.3

I discuss the details of this calibration exercise and report the
estimated fixed effect estimates for both heteroskedastic models.

For each of these three approaches I then reproduce the main figure
of
Adao, Costinot, and Donaldson 2017
in figures
4
,
5

and
6
. These figures show estimates for China’s
change in welfare for each year in the period 1996-2011, and include
confidence regions based on Bayesian, first-order and adversarial
uncertainty quantification. For the Bayesian approach I currently
consider a flat prior on the true trade flows. In work in progress
I consider a non-flat prior. Note that there is a very significant
difference between the confidence regions for the homoskedastic lower
bound approach and the heteroskedastic approaches, with the confidence
regions for the heteroskedastic approaches being comparable and remarkably
large. For the version with a uniform measurement error variance calibrated
using only reliable countries, the Bayesian and first-order approaches
seem to agree, which is evidence that the linear approximation works
well here. The confidence region seem to be around plus and minus
15%, which is still quite consequential. Furthermore, the fact that
the Bayesian and first-order approach are similar also increases the
reliability of the adversarial approach, which is currently based
on a first-order approximation.

For the adversarial approach I rely on the first-order approach, and
again use the
10\%
confidence interval of the measurement error
distribution to choose
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
. This now amounts
to choosing
\epsilon_{ij}=0.13\varsigma_{ij}
for all
i,j
, where

\epsilon_{ij}
is the adversarial bound corresponding to the log
trade flow between country
i
and
j
. In figure
7

I also depict the sensitivity analysis plot for the confidence intervals
of the measurement error for choosing
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
, for
the changes in welfare in 2007 and 2011. I drew a vertical line for
my choice of
10\%
. Interestingly, the lower bounds for different
years seems to converge, while the upper bounds diverge.

Note that I only investigate uncertainty around the trade flows. I
do not consider uncertainty in the model parameter, measurement error
in other variables, or uncertainty about the counterfactual question.
One could make the case for each of these sources of uncertainty to
be non-zero and influential, and including any of them would widen
the confidence regions. Furthermore, these bounds also do not include
estimation error, as discussed in section
3.3
.
Also note that these plots only consider the regular CES approach,
whereas in
Adao, Costinot, and Donaldson 2017
, the authors also consider
a mixed CES approach. In work in progress I consider both approaches
and discuss the impact of using either method on uncertainty quantification.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, I introduced notation and a framework to think about
possible sources of uncertainty when doing counterfactual analyses
in equilibrium models. I focus on uncertainty about the current state
of the world and take a measurement error approach. I provide two
ways to do uncertainty quantification, a Bayesian approach and an
adversarial approach. Using the Armington model as an illustration,
I find substantial sensitivity heterogeneity, which motivates case-by-case
uncertainty quantification. I apply these methods to
Adao, Costinot, and Donaldson 2017

using a measurement error model that I calibrate using mirror trade
data, and find large confidence regions.

In work in progress, I am working on the following extensions of the
current paper. Firstly, I discuss how to combine measurement error
and estimation error. Secondly, I augment the measurement error model
by incorporating richer covariance structure. Thirdly, for the Bayesian
approach I exchange the flat prior for a more realistic prior. Fourthly,
I discuss relaxing the linearity assumption for adversarial uncertainty
quantification. Lastly, I extend my section on applications by also
considering the non-CES approach for Adao et al (2017), and possibly
adding another application that uses sector-level trade flows, which
are often noisier than country-level trade flows.
