Modeling Path Importance for Effective Alzheimer’s Disease Drug Repurposing
Shunian Xiang 1∗, Patrick J. Lawrence 1∗, Bo Peng 2, ChienWei Chiang 1, PhD, Dokyoon Kim 3, PhD, Li Shen 3, PhD, and Xia Ning 1,2,4†, PhD

Abstract
Recently, drug repurposing has emerged as an effective and resource-efficient paradigm for 𝙰𝙳 \mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits drug discovery. Among various methods for drug repurposing, network-based methods have shown promising results as they are capable of leveraging complex networks that integrate multiple interaction types, such as protein-protein interactions, to more effectively identify candidate drugs. However, existing approaches typically assume paths of the same length in the network have equal importance in identifying the therapeutic effect of drugs. Other domains have found that same length paths do not necessarily have the same importance. Thus, relying on this assumption may be deleterious to drug repurposing attempts. In this work, we propose 𝙼𝙿𝙸 \mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits (Modeling Path Importance), a novel network-based method for 𝙰𝙳 \mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits drug repurposing. 𝙼𝙿𝙸 \mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits is unique in that it prioritizes important paths via learned node embeddings, which can effectively capture a network’s rich structural information. Thus, leveraging learned embeddings allows 𝙼𝙿𝙸 \mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits to effectively differentiate the importance among paths. We evaluate 𝙼𝙿𝙸 \mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits against a commonly used baseline method that identifies anti- 𝙰𝙳 \mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits drug candidates primarily based on the shortest paths between drugs and 𝙰𝙳 \mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits in the network. We observe that among the top-50 ranked drugs, 𝙼𝙿𝙸 \mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits prioritizes 20.0% more drugs with anti- 𝙰𝙳 \mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits evidence compared to the baseline. Finally, Cox proportional-hazard models produced from insurance claims data aid us in identifying the use of etodolac, nicotine, and 𝙱𝙱𝙱 \mathop{\mathtt{BBB}}\limits -crossing 𝙰𝙲𝙴 ​ - ​ 𝙸𝙽𝙷𝚜 \mathop{\mathtt{ACE}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits as having a reduced risk of 𝙰𝙳 \mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits , suggesting such drugs may be viable candidates for repurposing and should be explored further in future studies.

1
Introduction

Alzheimer’s Disease, denoted
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
, is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder that accounts for 60%-70% of dementia cases
and affects more than 50 million people worldwide today
[
1
,
2
]
.
Given the large number of affected individuals and
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
’s life-threatening nature
[
3
]
,
extensive resources have been dedicated to developing
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
-modifying drugs.
Since 2003, inefficacy or toxicity has accounted for a 95+% failure rate
among candidates evaluated for
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
treatment
[
4
,
5
]
.
Furthermore, none of the current US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
drugs are curative;
they only slow disease progression.
Because of the immense resources required to conduct clinical trials
[
6
]
,
the numerous failed clinical trials have necessitated the development of a more
resource-efficient method for
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
drug discovery.
In the last decade, the identification of new therapeutic indications for existing FDA-approved drugs, referred to as drug repurposing
[
7
]
,
has emerged as an effective and resource-efficient paradigm for drug discovery
[
8
]
.
This is an attractive option as the toxicity, pharmacokinetics, and pharmacodynamics of FDA-approved drugs have already been thoroughly investigated by previous clinical trials
[
7
,
9
]
.

Recently, the curation of comprehensive drug databases has enabled the development of computational methods for
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
drug repurposing
[
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
.
Among all the methods,
network-based methods have shown promising results and emerged as a popular approach
[
13
,
15
,
16
]
.
Network-based methods utilize comprehensive protein-protein, drug-target, and
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
-protein interactions to effectively reveal potential therapeutic effects of drugs on
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
.
Though promising, existing methods
[
13
]
measure the therapeutic effects of drugs on
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
primarily using
count and length of the paths connecting drug nodes and the
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
node in the network.
Paths of the same length are considered equivalently effective at identifying the therapeutic effect of drugs by these methods.
However, in other domains, paths of the same length have been shown to exhibit substantially different levels of importance
[
17
,
18
]
.
As such, assuming equal length paths have equal importance could be detrimental to effective drug repurposing for
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
.

In this work, we propose a novel method to conduct drug repurposing for
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
,
\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
(Modeling Path Importance), to address this limitation.
Similar to existing methods
[
13
,
14
]
,
\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
leverages the interactions between drugs and
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
via proteins
as indications of the potential therapeutic effects of drugs on
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
.
Based on the interactions,
\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
introduces a scoring function to score and rank drugs for their anti-
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
effectiveness.

\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
is unique in that it learns node embeddings
[
19
]

and prioritizes important paths via these learned embeddings.
Recent work
[
20
]
has shown that the learned node embeddings can effectively capture the rich structure information within a network.
Thus, scoring paths using node embeddings allows
\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
to utilize the network structure information to better
prioritize paths for effective
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
drug repurposing.
Specifically, in this study,
\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
leverages
\mathop{\mathtt{DeepWalk}}\limits

[
21
]
,
a widely used network learning approach, to generate node embeddings.
Edges are scored using a normalized dot product between the learned node embeddings;
paths and drugs are scored by multiplying individual edge scores.
Note that because
\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
serves as a general framework, other network learning approaches,
such as
\mathop{\mathtt{Node2Vec}}\limits

[
20
]
and graph neural networks
[
22
]
,
could also be easily incorporated to generate node embeddings.

In this study, we construct a network to conduct drug repurposing for
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
by combining protein-protein interactions (PPIs), drug-target interactions (DTIs),
and
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
-protein interactions (APIs) from multiple data sources.
To investigate the effectiveness of
\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
,
we compare
\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
against a commonly utilized
network-based drug repurposing method for
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits

[
13
,
23
]
,
denoted as
\mathop{\mathtt{BSL}}\limits
, using our network.
Our experimental results demonstrate that among the top-50 ranked drugs,

\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
prioritizes 20% more drugs with anti-
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
evidence compared to
\mathop{\mathtt{BSL}}\limits
.
We examine published literature and analyze insurance claims meta data
to evaluate the evidence of anti-
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
activity among
\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
’s top prioritized candidates.
The results of our evaluation find consensus between published experimental results and our own analysis for a few drug candidates.
Notably, angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors (
\mathop{\mathtt{ACE}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
) represent a class of drugs
that should be further explored for their anti-AD properties.
Moreover, other drugs, such as nicotine, that enhance the brains response to acetylcholine
and reduce cholinergic atrophy should be examined as well.
Conversely, we find that, relative to other evaluated drugs,
long-term use of trihexyphenidyl increases the risk of
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
.
This was corroborated by previously published
in vivo
experiments
[
24
]
.
Finally, we find etodolac to confer the lowest risk of developing
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
among all cyclooxygenase inhibitors (
\mathop{\mathtt{COX}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
) in our network.
Altogether, these findings suggest that
\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
may be a viable option with respect to
identifying repurposing candidates to treat
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
.

Materials and Methods

2.1
Network construction

PPIs, DTIs and APIs have shown utility for
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
drug repurposing
[
13
]
.
As such, we construct our network using these interactions.
Below, we describe our process for compiling
the PPIs, DTIs and APIs used to construct our network from public data sources.
In total, our network has 327,924, 2,854, and 230 edges corresponding to PPIs, DTIs, and APIs.
These edges connect one
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
node, 18,527 protein nodes, and 386 drug nodes.

2.1.1
Protein-protein interactions (PPIs)

Following Chen
et al.

[
13
]
, we include a comprehensive list of human PPIs consisting of 327,924 interactions.
This list aggregates a total of 21 bioinformatics and systems biology databases with combinations of five types of experimental evidence.
We refer the audience of interest to Chen
et al.

[
13
]
for a detailed description of the databases.

2.1.2
Drug-target interactions (DTIs)

We assemble drug-target interactions and
bioactivity data from 4 commonly used databases (each downloaded in November 2022):
the ChEMBL database
[
25
]
(v31),
the binding database
[
26
]
,
the therapeutic target database
[
27
]
,
and the IUPHAR/BPS guide to pharmacology database
[
28
]
.
We retain the drug-target interactions that satisfy
the following inclusion criteria:
1) binding affinities, including

\text{K}_{i}
,
\text{K}_{d}
,
\text{IC}_{50}
, or
\text{EC}_{50}
, must be less than or equal to 10
\mu
M;
2) protein targets and their respective proteins must have a unique UniProt
[
29
]
accession number;
3) protein targets must be marked as reviewed in the UniProt database;
4) protein targets must be present in
homo sapiens
.

Additionally, we retain drugs for which we have sufficient sample size
to conduct quantitative analysis using MarketScan
[
30
]
insurance claims meta data
(see Section
2.4
).
Specifically, included drugs have at least 100 patients with their
first dose at least 2 years prior to an
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
diagnosis (dx).
Additionally, these drugs must have at least 15 patients who eventually received an
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
dx.
Applying these filters yielded
2,854 edges connecting 386 FDA-approved drugs to 548 protein targets.

-protein interactions (APIs)

-like amyloid or tau pathology.
We also include 93 unique

\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits

,
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
,
36
,

]
.
We further incorporate a set of 118
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
-associated proteins introduced in at least 2 out of the 6 following databases (each was downloaded in November 2022):

[
38
]
,

[

,

,

(v7.0),

2.2
Modeling path importance for
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
drug repurposing

In this work,
we denote the constructed network as
G
.
Each node in
G
is denoted as
v_{i}
.
Specifically, drug nodes, protein nodes and the
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
node are

v^{d}_{i}
,
v^{g}_{i}
, and
v^{a}_{i}

, respectively.
Note that the index,
i
, does not apply to the
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
node as there are not multiple in our network.
Each edge that connects node
v_{i}
to node
v_{j}

is denoted as
e_{ij}
.
Each path is denoted as
p_{m}
, and the set of edges involved in a path is denoted
\mathbb{E}_{p_{m}}
.
Below, we denote matrices, scalars and row vectors using uppercase,
lowercase, and bold lowercase letters, respectively.

In
\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
, we leveraged
\mathop{\mathtt{DeepWalk}}\limits

[
21
]
,
a widely used node embedding approach,
to learn embeddings for each node in
G
.
First, for each node
v_{i}
in the network,
we conduct 256 random walks originating from this node,
and terminating once the path length reaches 128.

\mathop{\mathtt{DeepWalk}}\limits
is then trained by sliding a window of length 10 over the generated paths.
Nodes within the same window are forced to have similar embeddings following the objective function defined in the original paper
[
21
]
.
Node embeddings for
\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
are produced such that they have 128 dimensions.

After generating node embeddings, we score edge,
e_{ij}
,
using a normalized dot product of the embedding of

v_{i}^{x}
(
x
=
d
,
g
or
a
) and
v_{j}^{y}
(
y
=
d
,
g
or
a
) as follows:

w_{ij}=\frac{\exp(\mathbf{v}_{i}^{x}{\mathbf{v}_{j}^{y}}^{\top})}{\sum_{k\in\mathbb{V}}\exp(\mathbf{v}_{i}^{x}{\mathbf{v}_{k}^{y}}^{\top})},

(1)

where
w_{ij}
is the score of the edge
e_{ij}
;

\mathbf{v}_{i}^{x}
and
\mathbf{v}_{j}^{y}

is the learned embedding of node
v_{i}^{x}
and
v_{j}^{y}
, respectively;

\exp(\cdot)
is the exponential function;
and
\mathbb{V}
is the set of all the nodes in the network.
Note that, in Equation
1
, only one of
v_{i}^{x}
and
v_{j}^{y}
could be the
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
node.
These edge scores are calculated with node embeddings which implicitly capture the rich structural information within the network.
Thus, compared to existing methods,
\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
can better leverage a network’s structural information for
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
drug repurposing.
We calculate the score for each path by multiplying the scores of its individual edges as follows:

s_{p_{m}}=\prod_{e_{ij}\in\mathbb{E}_{p_{m}}}w_{ij},

(2)

where
s_{p_{m}}
is the score of the path
p_{m}
;
and
\mathbb{E}_{p}
is the set of all the edges in the path
p
.
The score for each drug (i.e.,
s_{v_{i}^{d}}
) is then defined as
the summation of the scores from all 3-hop or shorter paths
that originate from the
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
node and terminate at the drug node.

2.3
Baseline method

To evaluate the performance of
\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
,
we compare
\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
against a network-based method recently developed by Cheng
et al.

[
13
]
,
denoted as
\mathop{\mathtt{BSL}}\limits
.

\mathop{\mathtt{BSL}}\limits
scores drugs based on the shortest distance between the drug targets and the
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
-associated proteins (Section
2.1.3
) in the network.
Specifically, we denote
\mathbb{T}(i)
as the set of protein targets associated with a given drug
v_{i}^{d}
, and denote
\mathbb{P}
as the set of
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
-associated proteins.
The proximity between these two sets is calculated as the average shortest distance between elements
in
\mathbb{T}(i)
and
\mathbb{P}
as follows:

r(\mathbb{T}(i),\mathbb{P})=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{T}(i)|+|\mathbb{P}|}\left(\sum_{v_{j}\in\mathbb{T}(i)}\min_{v_{k}\in\mathbb{P}}d(v_{j},v_{k})+\sum_{v_{k}\in\mathbb{P}}\min_{v_{j}\in\mathbb{T}(i)}d(v_{k},v_{j})\right),

(3)

where
r(\mathbb{T}(i),\mathbb{P})
is the proximity between these two sets;

|\mathbb{T}(i)|
and
|\mathbb{P}|
is
the size of
\mathbb{T}(i)
and
\mathbb{P}
, respectively;
and
\min_{v_{k}\in\mathbb{P}}d(v_{j},v_{k})
is the shortest distance between
v_{j}
and any elements in
\mathbb{P}
.
Subsequently, we conduct a permutation test to assess the statistical significance of the calculated proximity.
The resulting z-score from this test is used as the score of drug
v_{i}

[
13
]
.
In
\mathop{\mathtt{BSL}}\limits
, a lower drug score implies a higher potential for effective
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
treatment.

2.4
Validation using MarketScan database

We use MarketScan medicare supplemental database from 2012–2021
to evaluate drug impact on
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
onset via Cox proportional-hazard models
[
30
]
.
The MarketScan database includes data for over 8 million unique individuals
and is comprised of demographic information, administrative information, diagnoses, procedures, and pharmacy records.
International Classification of Disease (ICD)-9/ICD-10 codes denote diagnoses
and National Drug Codes (NDCs) record pharmacy claims.
We use the ICD-9/ICD-10 codes listed in Supplementary Table S4
♠
to define
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
and comorbidities,
which are included as covariates in Cox proportional-hazard models.
We conduct our analysis over 1,632,218 unique individuals who were at least 65 years by 2022
and possessed a minimum of five years insurance enrollment prior of first
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
diagnosis.
Drugs from our constructed network are mapped to NDC codes
by partial matching of generic names from MartketScan redbook.
We only include patients who took or started taking a drug at least two years prior to
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
diagnosis
to mitigate the possibility that patients starting a drug already had
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
given that
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
is difficult to diagnosis.

In this study,
we curate a network consisting PPIs, DTIs and AGIs and
propose a novel network-based method,
\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
, for
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits

We propose
\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits

1) proteins that associated with
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits

the comprehensive human PPI network;
2) the drug target(s) for a disease may also be targeted for other diseases (e.g.,
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits

3) if a drug node is linked to the
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits

\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits

\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits

\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits

1) integrate
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits

to generate a comprehensive network (Figure

2) employ
\mathop{\mathtt{DeepWalk}}\limits
to learn node embeddings which capture the structural information within the network (Figure
1
b), and
3) score edges, paths and drugs based on the learned embeddings to leverage the structural information for better
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
drug repurposing (Figure
1
c).
Then we identify plausible treatment candidates
from the top-ranked drugs using a literature search of the published evidence.
We collected 327,924 PPIs from 21 bioinformatics and systems biology databases (Section

).
We also collected 2,854 DTIs
from 4 commonly used databases (Section
2.1.2
),
and 230 comprehensive APIs from multiple resources (Section
2.1.3
).
By aggregating all the interactions,
we construct a drug-protein-
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
network comprised of
386 drug nodes, 18,527 protein nodes, 1
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
node, and 331,008 edges.
More details about the network construction are available in Section
2
.

\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
is the first method which effectively repurposes drug candidates for
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
treatment
by prioritizing paths between drug nodes and the
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
node using learned node embeddings.

drug galantamine, while only 4 drugs from
\mathop{\mathtt{BSL}}\limits
are evidential.
As presented in Section
2
, compared to
\mathop{\mathtt{BSL}}\limits
,
\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
learns node embeddings to capture the rich structural information within the network, and leverage the structural information to better identify anti-
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
drugs.
The superior performance of
\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
over
\mathop{\mathtt{BSL}}\limits
demonstrates the effectiveness of leveraging the network structural
information to conduct repurposing to identify candidates for
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
treatment.
We also notice that both
\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{BSL}}\limits
prioritize 17 drugs in concordance within their top-50 drug lists.
Among the 17 drugs, 5 drugs demonstrate anti-
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
evidence:
donepezil is an FDA-approved anti-
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
drug;
nicotine and rasagiline have clinical anti-
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
evidence;
and fluvoxamine and fluoxetine have
in vivo
anti-
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
evidence.
The drugs nicotine, rasagiline, fluvoxamine, and fluoxetine could be promising repurposing candidates. We leave the investigation of these drugs to future research.

3.3
Identifying repurposing candidates with anti-
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
activity

In order to identify plausible candidates for repurposing,
we produce Cox proportional-hazard models (see Section
2.4
)
to ascertain whether there is consensus between the MarketScan insurance data
and the
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
-related evidence we found for top ranked candidates prioritized by
\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
.
Specifically, we use hazard ratios (
\mathop{\mathtt{HR}}\limits
) to identify whether any evidential drug elicited reduced the risk of
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
diagnosis among patients who took the drug compared against those that did not.
We present each drug’s
\mathop{\mathtt{HR}}\limits
with their significance levels in Supplementary Table S5b;
the
\mathop{\mathtt{HR}}\limits
for each drug’s covariates (sex, age, and additional common comorbidities)
are reported in Supplementary Table S5c-t.
A
\mathop{\mathtt{HR}}\limits
below 1 indicates that a drug has a protective effect,
while a
\mathop{\mathtt{HR}}\limits
above 1 indicates that a drug has a damaging effect.
Figure
3(a)
plots Kaplan-Meier (
\mathop{\mathtt{KM}}\limits
) survival curves.
These plots depict a patient’s likelihood of being diagnosed with
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
following long-term use of
either an individual prescribed drug or a drug with a given mechanism of action (MOA).
For MOAs, we group highly-prioritized drugs with
published evidence of anti-
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
activity (see Table
1
).
Bupropion (
\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{HR}}\limits$}=1.04
; non-significant) was included as a negative control
as clinical trials found the drug had no significant effect on cognition in AD patients
[
56
]
.
Trihexyphenydil (
\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{HR}}\limits$}=1.71;\ \alpha<0.001
) was included as a positive control for damaging effects
due to the evidence documented in Supplementary Table S1.
The
\mathop{\mathtt{COX}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
group includes the following drugs:
piroxicam, meloxicam, etodolac, and flurbiprofen.
The
\mathop{\mathtt{ACE}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
group includes the following drugs:
fosinopril, trandolapril, and lisinopril.
Note that we only include blood brain barrier (
\mathop{\mathtt{BBB}}\limits
) crossing
\mathop{\mathtt{ACE}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
in this group
as non-
\mathop{\mathtt{BBB}}\limits
-crossing
\mathop{\mathtt{ACE}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
have exhibited very limited effects on
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits

[
57
]
.
We also include time-to-event analysis for 4 of
\mathop{\mathtt{BSL}}\limits
’s top prioritized drugs (See Supplementary Figure S1).
Unlike
\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
, we observe only one of
\mathop{\mathtt{BSL}}\limits
’s drugs (sulindac) with reduced time-to-event compared to bupropion;
however, this difference is not significant.

3.4
Analyzing the MOAs of
\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
’s top-50 drugs

To identify groups of drugs whose anti-
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
properties should be further examined and explored,
we examine the top-50 drugs prioritized by
\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
for any common MOAs.
We find that
\mathop{\mathtt{COX}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{ACE}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
are the most common MOAs prioritized by
\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
.
Both
\mathop{\mathtt{COX}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{ACE}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
have published evidence of anti-
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
activity.
That said, experimental results suggest that long-term administration of
\mathop{\mathtt{COX}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
may only have protective properties, reducing the risk of
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
onset
[
46
]
.
Moreover, meloxicam (
\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{HR}}\limits$}=0.86;\ \alpha<0.05
), has even shown therapeutic potential, reversing
cognitive decline via inhibition of neuronal apoptosis
[
49
,
48
]
.
However, in Figure
3(a)
,
we observe that
\mathop{\mathtt{COX}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
as a class do not yield reduced risk of
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
compared to the negative control.
That said, we find etodolac significantly reduces
the risk of
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
(
\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{HR}}\limits$}=0.78;\ \alpha<0.001
) compared to other
\mathop{\mathtt{COX}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
,
including flurbiprofen (
\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{HR}}\limits$}=0.95
; non-significant) (Figure
3(b)
).
This suggests that only certain
\mathop{\mathtt{COX}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
, such as etodolac, may elicit protective effects against
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
onset.
Importantly, this may be a result of differences in target as etodolac targets
COX2
,
while flurbiprofen targets
COX1
.
On the other hand,
\mathop{\mathtt{ACE}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
were found to also protect against
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
onset in Figure
3(a)
.
Specifically, we evaluate only
\mathop{\mathtt{ACE}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
that cross the blood brain barrier (
\mathop{\mathtt{BBB}}\limits
) as previous insurance claims metadata analyses have indicated those that do not cross the
\mathop{\mathtt{BBB}}\limits
have no effect on
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits

[
57
]
.
To see if any of the
\mathop{\mathtt{BBB}}\limits
crossing
\mathop{\mathtt{ACE}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
have a greater protective effect that others,
we produce a
\mathop{\mathtt{KM}}\limits
plot for fosinopril, lisinopril, and trandolapril (Figure
3(c)
).
Unlike for
\mathop{\mathtt{COX}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
,
\mathop{\mathtt{ACE}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
do not elicit any significant by-drug difference in
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
onset
as illustrated in Figure
3(c)
.
While
\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
prioritized four
\mathop{\mathtt{BBB}}\limits
crossing (
\mathop{\mathtt{BBBx}}\limits
) and
four non-
\mathop{\mathtt{BBBx}}\limits

\mathop{\mathtt{ACE}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
in the top-50,
the
\mathop{\mathtt{BBBx}}\limits

\mathop{\mathtt{ACE}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
had a lower average rank compared to the non-
\mathop{\mathtt{BBBx}}\limits

\mathop{\mathtt{ACE}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
(15 and 19, respectively).

Another important distinction between
\mathop{\mathtt{COX}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{ACE}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
is that
\mathop{\mathtt{ACE}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
have been shown to have some ameliorative potential;
whereas,
\mathop{\mathtt{COX}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
have only shown protective effects.
In fact, fosninpril and lisinopril (ranked
2^{nd}
and
24^{th}
by
\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
, respectively) was found to reduce cognitive decline in animal models of
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits

[
44
,
58
]
.
In Figure
3(a)
, we find that
\mathop{\mathtt{BBBx}}\limits

\mathop{\mathtt{ACE}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
consistently exhibit decreased risk of
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
relative to our negative control drug, bupropion.
Additionally, there does not appear to be a significant difference between any of the
\mathop{\mathtt{BBBx}}\limits

\mathop{\mathtt{ACE}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
with respect to their protection against
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
, indicating that they are possibly all viable candidates for repurposing.
This is in agreement with other published evidence that has identified
\mathop{\mathtt{BBBx}}\limits

\mathop{\mathtt{ACE}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
as having protective effect on
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
development.
Interestingly,
\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
prioritized 133.6% more
\mathop{\mathtt{COX}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
and 700.9% more
\mathop{\mathtt{ACE}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
than
\mathop{\mathtt{BSL}}\limits
in the top-50 from all such drugs in our network.

\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
’s ability to prioritize more drugs from MOAs with known anti-AD activity
suggests that it may be a more viable option when identifying candidates for drug repurposing.

\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
also highly prioritizes drugs that increase the brain’s response to acetylcholine, either by reducing its degradation (acetylcholinesterase inhibitors,
\mathop{\mathtt{AChE}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
) or by stimulating its receptors (acetylcholine receptor agonists,
\mathop{\mathtt{AChR}\texttt{-}\mathtt{Ags}}\limits
).
This is important as acetylcholine’s (
\mathop{\mathtt{ACh}}\limits
) synaptic bioavailability is an important contributor to
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
progression.
That is, there is evidence that cholenergic atrophy and
\mathop{\mathtt{ACh}}\limits
deficiency is linked with cognitive decline in
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
patients
[
59
]
.
Moveover, many of the current FDA-approved drugs indicated to slow
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
progression target this mechanism of disease progression via
\mathop{\mathtt{AChE}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
(e.g., donepezil, rivastigmine, and galantamine).

\mathop{\mathtt{AChR}\texttt{-}\mathtt{Ags}}\limits
, also enhances
\mathop{\mathtt{ACh}}\limits
signaling.
Such drugs, such as nicotine,
accomplish this by increasing the response of
\mathop{\mathtt{ACh}}\limits
receptors located on the post-synaptic neuron.
Interestingly, nicotine, was found to significantly improve cognition
in patients with mild cognitive impairment, which is a precursor to
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits

[
45
]
.
We also find long-term nicotine use to have a protective effect (
\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{HR}}\limits$}=0.532;\ \alpha<0.001
),
with respect to
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
onset.
In Figure
3(a)
, we observe similar risk of developing
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
to
\mathop{\mathtt{ACE}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
after six to seven years.
Conversely, we find evidence that long-term use of trihexyphenidyl,
which reduces the activity of
\mathop{\mathtt{ACh}}\limits
receptors,
is associated with
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
-like neurodegeneration in rats
[
24
]
.
This is corroborated by Figure
3(a)
,
where we observe the highest risk of
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
elicited by trihexyphenidyl.
More than eight years on trihexyphenidyl was associated
with a substantial increase in the risk of
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
relative to the other drugs evaluated in Figure
3(a)
.
These findings confirm that
\mathop{\mathtt{ACh}}\limits
signaling is closely linked with
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
progression.
As such, exploring other drugs and drug classes which either
increase
\mathop{\mathtt{ACh}}\limits
synaptic bioavailability or enhance neuronal response to
\mathop{\mathtt{ACh}}\limits
should be further examined for anti-
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
activity.

4
Discussion

In this work, we propose a novel network-based,
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
-specific drug repurposing approach called
\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
.

\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
improves upon prior network-based methods by leveraging node embeddings learned via
\mathop{\mathtt{DeepWalk}}\limits
to prioritize
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
-associated paths.
Moreover, the use of learned embeddings allows
\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
to more effectively capture a network’s rich topology
than previous approaches, such as
\mathop{\mathtt{BSL}}\limits
.
In a direct comparison, we find that 20% more of
\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
’s highly prioritized drug candidates (top-50)
have published anti-AD evidence compared to
\mathop{\mathtt{BSL}}\limits
’s highly prioritized drug candidates.
In addition to evidence in literature,
we leverage insurance claims data to produce Cox proportional-hazard models.
Among all the drugs we evaluate, these models identified
\mathop{\mathtt{BBBx}}\limits

\mathop{\mathtt{ACE}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
as having the lowest risk of
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
.
Similarly, etodolac was found to have the lowest risk of
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
among the four
\mathop{\mathtt{COX}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
we evaluated (Figure
3(b)
),
indicating that this drug in particular may have protective effect despite the class as a whole
not exhibiting a significantly reduced risk of
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
compared to our negative control (Figure
3(a)
).
Additionally,
\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
highly prioritizes drugs that target the cholinergic system.
Each of the approved
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
drugs in our dataset that are also
\mathop{\mathtt{AChE}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
are prioritized in the top-50 by
\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
.

\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
also highly prioritizes nicotine, an
\mathop{\mathtt{AChR}\texttt{-}\mathtt{Ags}}\limits
.
This prioritization is supported by both literature and our Cox models,
which suggest nicotine is associated with reduced risk of
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
.
Altogether, the results presented in this work highlight
etodolac, nicotine, and
\mathop{\mathtt{ACE}\texttt{-}\mathtt{INHs}}\limits
as viable candidates for repurposing to treat
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
and, as such, deserve further examination in future studies.

Despite its promising results,
\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
exhibits a few limitations.
The PPI network we construct is a simplification of molecular pathways.
Like many other network-based approaches,

\mathop{\mathtt{MPI}}\limits
does not consider loops nor the directionality of PPI
as these can be difficult for models to learn.
In our context, this means that highly ranked candidates are only likely
to be in close proximity to
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
-related genes.
To improve drug prioritization, models must be capable of identifying drugs
that are both upstream of and in close proximity to these
\mathop{\mathtt{AD}}\limits
-related genes.
In future studies, we will leverage directed interactions either by hard coding them or learning them.
One way directionality might be learned is through the use of multi-omics data.
Examining how changes to genomic and epigenomic profiles affect gene expression
could facilitate learning where genes are in pathways.
Furthermore, by leveraging multi-omics data, we may be able to provide more personalized drug recommendations.
