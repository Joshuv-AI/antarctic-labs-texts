Well-posedness and qualitative properties of quasilinear degenerate evolution systems
K. Mitra, S. Sonner

Abstract
We analyze nonlinear degenerate coupled PDE-PDE and PDE-ODE systems that arise, for example, in the modelling of biofilm growth. One of the equations, describing the evolution of a biomass density, exhibits degenerate and singular diffusion. The other equations are either of advection-reaction-diffusion type or ordinary differential equations. Under very general assumptions the existence of weak solutions is proven by considering regularized systems, deriving uniform bounds and using fixed point arguments. Assuming additional structural assumptions we also prove the uniqueness of solutions. Global-in-time well-posedness is established for Dirichlet and mixed boundary conditions, whereas, only local well-posedness can be shown for homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions. Using a suitable barrier function and comparison theorems we formulate sufficient conditions for finite-time blow-up or uniform boundedness of solutions. Finally, we show that solutions of the degenerate parabolic equation inherit additional global spatial regularity if the diffusion coefficient has a power-law growth.

Introduction

This paper investigates the well-posedness and qualitative properties of weak solutions of a wide class of quasilinear parabolic systems where one of the equations shows degenerate and singular diffusion.
We also consider couplings of such degenerate parabolic equations with ordinary differential equations (ODEs).
The motivation for our work is models describing the growth of spatially heterogeneous biofilms in dependence of growth limiting substrates. The models are either formulated as systems of partial differential equations (PDEs) or as coupled PDE-ODE systems, e.g. see
[
7
,
6
]
.
Their characteristic and challenging features are the degenerate and singular diffusion effects in the equation for the biomass density and the nonlinear coupling of this equation to additional ODEs and/or PDEs for the substrates.

We aim to develop a unifying solution theory for a large class of systems with degenerate diffusion that is motivated by models for biofilm growth, but the analysis is not limited to these applications. In fact, we expect that such models can also be used, e.g. to describe cancer cell invasion or the spread of wildfires.
In our paper we extend previous well-posedness results in the following directions:

(a)
Well-posedness results for PDE-PDE systems:
our results extend the theory developed for systems with one substrate in
[
15
]
to systems with an arbitrary number of substrates
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Moreover, the existence of weak solutions is proven for a broad class of diffusion coefficients
D_{0}
and

, reaction terms
f_{j}
, and allows for flow-fields
\bm{v}_{j}
which has not been considered in earlier works.

(b)
Well-posedness of PDE–ODE systems (

The well-posedness of PDE-ODE systems of the form (
1.1
) with a degenerate and/or singular diffusion coefficient
D_{0}
has been an open problem. The theory we develop applies to the cellulolytic biofilm model
[

and implies its local well-posedness.

(c)
Mixed as well as homogeneous Neumann conditions for

Global well-posedness is shown for mixed Dirichlet-Neumann boundary conditions and a local well-posedness result is established assuming homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions for
M
.
Moreover, apart from well-posedness results we also analyze qualitative properties such as boundedness or blow-up of solutions.

(d)

We further show that under certain porous medium type growth conditions on

close to zero, the biomass concentration

inherits some global spatial regularity.

The outline of our paper is as follows:
In Section

we introduce notation, state our assumptions on the data and introduce the concept of weak solutions. In Section

we prove global well-posedness for systems with Dirichlet or mixed Dirichlet-Neumann boundary conditions for

In

Problem formulation

(2.8)

(P2)

Remark 2.2

(Generalizations of the assumptions

(P4)

(Assumptions

Lemma 3.1

(Existence for a regularized problem)
.

–

Then there exists a unique

(3.4)

Furthermore, if

\displaystyle\|\nabla\Phi_{\varepsilon}(M_{s,\varepsilon}(t))\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{|\partial_{t}\Phi(M_{s,\varepsilon})|^{2}}{\Phi^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(M_{s,\varepsilon})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\nabla\Phi_{\varepsilon}(M_{0})\|^{2}+\|\Phi^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(M_{s,\varepsilon})\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\left[1+\int_{0}^{T}\|\vec{s}\|^{2}\right].

(3.5)

The proof of

Lemma 3.2

(Boundedness for the regularized problem)
.

In addition to the hypothesis of Lemma
3.1
we assume that
(P4enumi)
holds.
Let
M_{s,\varepsilon}\in{\bf{\cal X}}+h^{e}_{0}
denote the solution in
Lemma

3.1
. Moreover, let
\hat{M}\in C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{+})
denote the solution of the integral equation

\hat{M}(t)=\overline{M}+\int_{0}^{t}f_{\max}(\hat{M}(\varrho))\,\mathrm{d}\varrho,\qquad t\in[0,T].

(a)

Then,
0\leq M_{s,\varepsilon}(t)\leq\hat{M}(t)
a.e. in
\Omega
for all
t\in[0,T]
.

(b)

If, in addition,
\Gamma_{1}
has non-zero measure, then a constant
\delta\in(0,1)
exists such that

0\leq M_{s,\varepsilon}(t)\leq 1-\delta\ \text{ a.e. in }\Omega\text{ for all }t\in[0,T].

Observe that the above lemma implies that

M_{s,\varepsilon}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega))

and the family
M_{s,\varepsilon}
is uniformly bounded with respect to
\varepsilon>0
in
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega))
.

Proof.

The existence of
\hat{M}
follows from the Picard-Lindelöf Theorem since
f_{\max}\in\mathrm{Lip}({\mathbb{R}})
. Moreover, it satisfies
\partial_{t}\hat{M}=f_{\max}(\hat{M})
and therefore,
\hat{M}\geq\overline{M}
since

was assumed to be non-negative in
(P4enumi)
.

(Step 1)
\mathbf{M_{s,\varepsilon}\geq 0}
:
Inserting the test function
\varphi=[M_{s,\varepsilon}]_{-}
in (
3.3
) implies that

\int_{0}^{T}\left[\partial_{t}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\|[M_{s,\varepsilon}]_{-}\|^{2}\right)+\varepsilon\|\nabla[M_{s,\varepsilon}]_{-}\|^{2}\right]\overset{\ref{prop:fg1}}{\leq}C_{L}\int_{0}^{T}\|[M_{s,\varepsilon}]_{-}\|^{2}.

Since
[M_{s,\varepsilon}(0)]_{-}=[M_{0}]_{-}=0
, we have
\|[M_{s,\varepsilon}]_{-}(T)\|=0
using Gronwall’s Lemma (
2.5a
).

(Step 2)
\mathbf{M_{s,\varepsilon}\leq\hat{M}}
:
Inserting the test function
\varphi=[M_{s,\varepsilon}-\hat{M}]_{+}\in L^{2}(0,T;{\cal H}^{1})
in (
3.3
) we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\langle[M_{s,\varepsilon}-\hat{M}]_{+},\partial_{t}M_{s,\varepsilon}\rangle=\int_{0}^{T}\langle[M_{s,\varepsilon}-\hat{M}]_{+},\partial_{t}[M_{s,\varepsilon}-\hat{M}]\rangle+\int_{0}^{T}\langle[M_{s,\varepsilon}-\hat{M}]_{+},\partial_{t}\hat{M}\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\,=\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{t}\left(\frac{1}{2}\|[M_{s,\varepsilon}-\hat{M}]_{+}\|^{2}\right)+\int_{0}^{T}(\partial_{t}\hat{M},[M_{s,\varepsilon}-\hat{M}]_{+})

\displaystyle\qquad\overset{\ref{prop:IC}}{=}\frac{1}{2}\|[M_{s,\varepsilon}-\hat{M}]_{+}(T)\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}(\partial_{t}\hat{M},[M_{s,\varepsilon}-\hat{M}]_{+}),

(3.6a)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}(\nabla\Phi_{\varepsilon}(M_{s,\varepsilon}),\nabla[M_{s,\varepsilon}-\hat{M}]_{+})=\int_{0}^{T}({\Phi_{\varepsilon}}^{\prime}(M_{s,\varepsilon})\nabla M_{s,\varepsilon},\nabla[M_{s,\varepsilon}-\hat{M}]_{+})\geq 0,

(3.6b)

and for the reaction term we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}(f_{0}(M_{s,\varepsilon},\vec{s}),[M_{s,\varepsilon}-\hat{M}]_{+})=\int_{0}^{T}(f_{0}(M_{s,\varepsilon},\vec{s})-f_{0}(\hat{M},\vec{s})+f_{0}(\hat{M},\vec{s}),[M_{s,\varepsilon}-\hat{M}]_{+})

\displaystyle\overset{\ref{prop:fg1},\ref{prop:fg2}}{\leq}C_{L}\int_{0}^{T}\|[M_{s,\varepsilon}-\hat{M}]_{+}\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}f_{\max}(\hat{M})[M_{s,\varepsilon}-\hat{M}]_{+}.

(3.6c)

Combining the estimates in (
3.6
) it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|[M_{s,\varepsilon}-\hat{M}]_{+}(T)\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}(\partial_{t}\hat{M}-f_{\max}(\hat{M}),[M_{s,\varepsilon}-\hat{M}]_{+})\leq C_{L}\int_{0}^{T}\|[M_{s,\varepsilon}-\hat{M}]_{+}\|^{2}.

(3.7)

The second term is zero by the definition of
\hat{M}
. Hence, using Gronwall’s Lemma (
2.5a
) we have the result.

(Step 3)
\mathbf{M_{s,\varepsilon}\leq 1-\delta}
:
This is a generalization of Proposition 6 in
[
9
]
to the case of mixed or homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions, see also the proof of Theorem 2.7 in
[
15
]
.
For
f_{\max}(\cdot)
introduced in
(P4enumi)
, let
\hat{u}\in{\cal H}^{1}
solve the elliptic problem

\displaystyle(\nabla\hat{u},\nabla\varphi)=(\hat{C},\varphi)\quad\text{for all}\ \varphi\in{\cal H}^{1},\quad\text{where }\hat{C}:=\max_{0\leq t\leq T}f_{\max}(\hat{M}(t)).

(3.8)

The existence of a unique weak solution
\hat{u}
directly follows from the Lax-Milgram Lemma. If

(one space-dimension), then we immediately have
\hat{u}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
from Morrey’s inequality
[
11
, Chapter 5]
. Hence, let
d\geq 2
. Set
q=2d/(d-2)
for
d>2
, and
q>2
for

. Then for
m\geq 0
, inserting the test function
\varphi=[\hat{u}-m]_{+}\in{\cal H}^{1}
and denoting

we have the estimates,

\displaystyle\|\nabla[\hat{u}-m]_{+}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\hat{C}\,\|[\hat{u}-m]_{+}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)},

\displaystyle\|[\hat{u}-m]_{+}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq|A(m)|^{1-\frac{1}{q}}\|[\hat{u}-m]_{+}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}\leq C^{\prime}|A(m)|^{1-\frac{1}{q}}\|\nabla[\hat{u}-m]_{+}\|,

where the last inequality follows from the Sobolev inequality
[
11

. Hence, we have
\|[\hat{u}-m]_{+}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\leq{C^{\prime}}^{2}\hat{C}|A(m)|^{\gamma}
, where
\gamma=2-\frac{2}{q}>1
.
Thus, following the steps of
[
13

we conclude that
\hat{u}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
.
Hence, using the comparison principle
[
21
]
, one has
\Phi_{\varepsilon}(M_{s,\varepsilon}(t))\leq\hat{u}+\bar{M}<\infty
a.e. in
\Omega
for all
\varepsilon>0
and

, which concludes the proof.

Proof.

In this proof, we will first assume that

3.2
and the assumption

, imply the existence of

\displaystyle M_{s,\varepsilon}\in[0,1-\bar{\delta}],\ \ \text{and consequently, }\ \ \phi_{\varepsilon}:=\Phi_{\varepsilon}(M_{s,\varepsilon})\in[0,\Phi(1-\bar{\delta})]\ \text{ a.e. in }Q,

(Step 1) Convergence of
\mathbf{M_{s,\varepsilon}}
and
\mathbf{\phi_{\varepsilon}}
, assuming (
3.10

Taking (
3.11

(3.12b)

Setting

\displaystyle M_{s}:=\Phi^{-1}(\phi)\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega))

(3.13)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}M_{s,\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup\partial_{t}M_{s}\text{ weakly in }L^{2}(0,T;{\cal H}^{-1}(\Omega)),

To see this, we consider a convex strictly increasing function

Using (

\displaystyle\eta\left(\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}|M_{s,\varepsilon}-M_{s}|\right)\overset{\eqref{eq:ConvexFuncIneq1}}{\leq}\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}\eta(|M_{s,\varepsilon}-M_{s}|)\overset{\eqref{eq:ConvexFuncIneq2}}{\leq}\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}|\eta(M_{s,\varepsilon})-\eta(M_{s})|

\displaystyle\|\Phi(M_{s,\varepsilon})-\Phi(M_{s})\|_{L^{1}(Q)}\leq\|\Phi(M_{s,\varepsilon})-\phi_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{1}(Q)}+\|\phi_{\varepsilon}-\Phi(M_{s})\|_{L^{1}(Q)}

\displaystyle\|\Phi(M_{s,\varepsilon})-\Phi_{\varepsilon}(M_{s,\varepsilon})\|_{L^{1}(Q)}+\|\phi_{\varepsilon}-\phi\|_{L^{1}(Q)}\overset{\eqref{eq:propPhieps},\eqref{eq:convergencePhiL2}}{\longrightarrow}0,\quad\text{ for }\varepsilon\searrow 0.

\displaystyle|\Phi(M_{s,\varepsilon})-\Phi_{\varepsilon}(M_{s,\varepsilon})|\overset{\eqref{eq:PhiepsDef},\eqref{eq:MPhiLess1}}{=}\left|\int_{0}^{M_{s,\varepsilon}}(D(\varrho)-\max\{\varepsilon,D(\varrho)\})\,\mathrm{d}\varrho\right|\leq C\varepsilon.

\displaystyle|(M_{s,\varepsilon}(t)-M_{s}(t),\varphi)|

\displaystyle=|(M_{s,\varepsilon}(t)-M_{s}(t),\varphi-\tilde{\varphi})+(M_{s,\varepsilon}(t)-M_{s}(t),\tilde{\varphi})|

\displaystyle\|M_{s}(t+\tau)-M_{s}(t)\|^{2}=(M_{s}(t+\tau)-M_{s,\varepsilon}(t+\tau),M_{s}(t+\tau)-M_{s}(t))

\displaystyle\quad+(M_{s,\varepsilon}(t+\tau)-M_{s,\varepsilon}(t),M_{s}(t+\tau)-M_{s}(t))+(M_{s,\varepsilon}(t)-M_{s}(t),M_{s}(t+\tau)-M_{s}(t)).

(

3.4
A fixed point argument for proving
Theorem

3.1

In this section, we use Schauder’s fixed point theorem to prove the existence of solutions for general diffusion coefficients
D_{j}
satisfying
(P2)
. Since the case of ODE-PDE couplings, where
\nu_{j}=0
, is already covered by the previous section, here we assume that

\nu_{j}>0
. Similarly as in the previous section, we define the map
\mathfrak{B}:(L^{2}(Q))^{k}\to(L^{2}(Q))^{k}
such that for all
j\in\{1,\dots,k\}
,
\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s})_{j}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))\cap H^{1}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))
, and for all
\zeta_{j}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}[\langle\zeta_{j},\partial_{t}\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s})_{j}\rangle_{H^{1}_{0},H^{-1}}+\nu_{j}(D_{j}(M_{s},\vec{s})\nabla\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s})_{j}+\bm{v}_{j}\,\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s})_{j},\nabla\zeta_{j})]=\int_{0}^{T}(f_{j}(M_{s},\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s})),\zeta_{j}),

(3.25a)

\displaystyle\text{with }\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s})_{j}(0)=S_{0,j},\text{ and }\mathfrak{B}(s)_{j}=h_{j}\text{ on $\partial\Omega$ in the trace sense}.

(3.25b)

Lemma 3.5

(Schauder criteria for
\mathfrak{B}
)
.

Let
\nu_{j}>0
for all
j\in\{1,\dots,k\}
. Then under the assumptions of
Lemma

3.3
, the operator
\mathfrak{B}:(L^{2}(Q))^{k}\to(L^{2}(Q))^{k}
introduced in (
3.25
) is well-defined, continuous, compact, and
\|\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s})\|_{\bf{\cal Z}}
is bounded for all
\vec{s}\in(L^{2}(Q))^{k}
.

Proof.

(Step 1): Well-posedness, boundedness and compactness.
Recalling
Remark

3.1
, we have existence and boundedness of weak solutions
M_{s}\in{\bf{\cal W}}
for any
\vec{s}\in(L^{2}(Q))^{k}
.
We observe that
D_{j}
satisfies the ellipticity condition by
(P2)
,
\bm{v}_{j}\in(L^{\infty}(\Omega))^{d}
,
f_{j}(\cdot,s)
is Lipschitz continuous, and for
h^{e}_{j}
in
(P6)
, we have

|f_{j}(M_{s},h^{e}_{j})|\overset{\ref{prop:fg1}}{\leq}C(1+|h^{e}_{j}|+|M_{s}|)\in L^{2}(\Omega)\text{ for a constant }C>0.

Consequently,
\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s})_{j}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))\cap H^{1}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))
is well-defined.
This is also evident from a Schaefer’s fixed point argument
[
11
, Chapter 9]
using the a-priori estimate obtained by inserting
\zeta_{j}=\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s})_{j}-h^{e}_{j}
in (
3.25
). For the first term this yields,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\langle\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s})_{j}-h^{e}_{j},\partial_{t}\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s})_{j}\rangle_{H^{1}_{0},H^{-1}}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\|\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s}(T))_{j}-h^{e}_{j}\|^{2}-\|S_{0,j}-h^{e}_{j}\|^{2}\right],

and for the diffusion and convection terms we obtain,

\displaystyle\nu_{j}\int_{0}^{T}(D_{j}(M_{s},\vec{s})\nabla\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s})_{j},\nabla(\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s})_{j}-h^{e}_{j}))

\displaystyle\qquad\quad=\frac{\nu_{j}}{2}\int_{Q}D_{j}(M_{s},\vec{s})\left[|\nabla\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s})_{j}|^{2}-|\nabla h^{e}_{j}|^{2}+|\nabla(\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s})_{j}-h^{e}_{j})|^{2}\right],

\displaystyle\left|\nu_{j}\int_{0}^{T}(\bm{v}_{j}\,\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s})_{j},\nabla(\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s})_{j}-h^{e}_{j}))\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\overset{\eqref{Eq:YoungsIneq}}{\leq}\tfrac{\nu_{j}\|\bm{v}_{j}\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}}{2D_{\min}}\int_{0}^{T}\|\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s})_{j}\|^{2}+\tfrac{\nu_{j}D_{\min}}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\|\nabla(\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s})_{j}-h^{e}_{j})\|^{2}.

Finally, we can estimate the reaction term by

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}(f_{j}(M_{s},\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s})),\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s})_{j}-h^{e}_{j})=\int_{0}^{T}(f_{j}(M_{s},\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s}))-f_{j}(M_{s},\vec{h}^{e})+f_{j}(M_{s},\vec{h}^{e}),\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s})_{j}-h^{e}_{j})

\displaystyle\qquad\overset{\eqref{Eq:YoungsIneq},\ref{prop:fg1}}{\leq}C\sum_{i=1}^{k}\int_{0}^{T}\|\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s})_{i}-h^{e}_{i}\|^{2}_{(L^{2}(\Omega))^{k}}+\int_{0}^{T}\|f_{j}(M_{s},\vec{h}^{e})\|^{2}.

Combining the above estimates, using Young’s inequality and summing from
j=1
to
k
, one has

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left[\|\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s}(T))_{j}-h^{e}_{j}\|^{2}+\nu_{j}D_{\min}\int_{0}^{T}\|\nabla\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s})_{j}\|^{2}\right]\lesssim 1+\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\|\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s})_{j}-h^{e}_{j}\|^{2}.

Thus, using Gronwall’s Lemma (
2.5a
), we conclude that
\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s})
is uniformly bounded in
L^{2}(0,T;(H^{1}(\Omega))^{k})
and
L^{\infty}(0,T;(L^{2}(\Omega))^{k})
. Moreover, from (
3.25
), we obtain

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s})_{j}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|\zeta_{j}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))}=1\end{subarray}}\int_{0}^{T}\left[\nu_{j}(D_{j}(M_{s},\vec{s})\nabla\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s})_{j}+\bm{v}_{j}\,\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s})_{j},\nabla\zeta_{j})-(f_{j}(M_{s},\mathfrak{B}\vec{s}),\zeta_{j})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\nu_{j}(D_{\max}\|\nabla\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s})_{j}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}+\|\bm{v}_{j}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s})_{j}\|_{L^{2}(Q)})+C_{\Omega}\,\|f_{j}(M_{s},\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s}))\|_{L^{2}(Q)}.

Hence
\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s})_{j}-h^{e}_{j}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
and
\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s})_{j}\in H^{1}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))
is bounded uniformly with respect to
\vec{s}\in(L^{2}(Q))^{k}
. Due to the compact embedding
L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))\cap H^{1}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))\hookrightarrow\hookrightarrow L^{2}(Q)
(see
[
23
]
), the mapping
\mathfrak{B}
is also compact. Moreover, due to the continuous embedding
L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))\cap H^{1}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))\subset C([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
,
\|\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s})\|_{\bf{\cal Z}}
is bounded uniformly.

(Step 2): Continuity.
Let the sequence
\{\vec{s}^{i}\}_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converge to
\vec{s}^{\,\star}
in
(L^{2}(Q))^{k}
. Let
M_{s}^{i}
and
M^{\star}_{s}
denote the solutions of (
3.9
) for
\vec{s}=\vec{s}^{i}
and
\vec{s}=\vec{s}^{\,\star}
respectively. Then, by using the
L^{1}
-contraction result (
3.23
) we have that

\|(M_{s}^{i}-M_{s}^{\star})(t)\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\to 0
for all
t\in(0,T]
. Since,
M_{s}^{i}
are bounded in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
(
Lemma

3.3
), one further has that

\displaystyle\|\vec{s}^{i}-\vec{s}^{\,\star}\|_{(L^{2}(Q))^{k}}+\|M_{s}^{i}-M_{s}^{\star}\|_{C([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))}\rightarrow 0\ \text{ as }i\rightarrow\infty.

(3.26)

Observe that, since
\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s}^{\,\star})_{j}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
, for any given
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
s_{j}^{\varepsilon,\star}\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
such that

\displaystyle\|\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s}^{\,\star})_{j}-s_{j}^{\varepsilon,\star}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\leq\varepsilon/4D_{\max}.

(3.27)

We consider the difference of
\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s}^{i})_{j}
and
\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s}^{\,\star})_{j}
by subtracting two versions of (
3.25
).
First, we split up the diffusion term,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}(D_{j}(M_{s}^{i},\vec{s}^{i})\nabla\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s}^{i})_{j}-D_{j}(M_{s}^{\star},\vec{s}^{\,\star})\nabla\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s}^{\,\star})_{j},\nabla\zeta_{j})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}(D_{j}(M_{s}^{i},\vec{s}^{i})\nabla(\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s}^{i})_{j}-\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s}^{\,\star})_{j})+(D_{j}(M_{s}^{i},\vec{s}^{i})-D_{j}(M_{s}^{\star},\vec{s}^{\,\star}))\nabla\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s}^{\,\star})_{j},\nabla\zeta_{j}),

(3.28a)

and use Hölder’s inequality to estimate the second term as follows

\displaystyle\|(D_{j}(M_{s}^{i},\vec{s}^{i})-D_{j}(M_{s}^{\star},\vec{s}^{\,\star}))\nabla\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s}^{\,\star})_{j}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq\|(D_{j}(M_{s}^{i},\vec{s}^{i})-D_{j}(M_{s}^{\star},\vec{s}^{\,\star}))\nabla s^{\varepsilon,\star}_{j}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}+\|(D_{j}(M_{s}^{i},\vec{s}^{i})-D_{j}(M_{s}^{\star},\vec{s}^{\,\star}))\nabla(\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s}^{\,\star})_{j}-s^{\varepsilon,\star}_{j})\|_{L^{2}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq\|s^{\varepsilon,\star}_{j}\|_{C^{1}(Q)}\|D_{j}(M_{s}^{i},\vec{s}^{i})-D_{j}(M_{s}^{\star},\vec{s}^{\,\star})\|_{L^{2}(Q)}+2D_{\max}\|\nabla(\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s}^{\,\star})_{j}-s^{\varepsilon,\star}_{j}))\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\overset{\eqref{eq:MSto0inL2},\eqref{eq:Sstar_eps_def}}{\leq}\varepsilon,

(3.28b)

for all

, where

is large enough. Here we used the Lipschitz continuity of

, see
(P2)
.
Furthermore (

) implies for

, where

is large enough, that

\displaystyle\|f_{j}(M_{s}^{i},\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s}^{i}))-f_{j}(M_{s}^{\star},\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s}^{\,\star}))\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\overset{\ref{prop:fg1}}{\lesssim}\varepsilon+\|\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s}^{i})-\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s}^{\,\star})\|_{(L^{2}(\Omega))^{k}}.

(3.28c)

Hence, defining
\eth s_{j}^{i}:=\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s}^{i})_{j}-\mathfrak{B}(\vec{s}^{\,\star})_{j}

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{T}[\langle\zeta_{j},\partial_{t}\,\eth s^{i}_{j}\rangle_{H^{1}_{0},H^{-1}}+\nu_{j}(D_{j}(M_{s}^{i},\vec{s}^{i})\nabla\eth s^{i}_{j}+\bm{v}_{j}\,\eth s^{i}_{j},\nabla\zeta_{j})]\right|

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:DTermsplit}}{\lesssim}\int_{0}^{T}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{k}\|\eth s^{i}_{j}\|\|\zeta_{j}\|+\varepsilon\|\zeta_{j}\|+\varepsilon\|\nabla\zeta_{j}\|\right]

\displaystyle\ \,\lesssim\ \int_{0}^{T}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{k}\|\eth s^{i}_{j}\|\|\zeta_{j}\|+\varepsilon+\varepsilon\left(\|\zeta_{j}\|^{2}+\|\nabla\zeta_{j}\|^{2}\right)\right].

and sum up the resulting estimates from

to

. Then for

)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left[\|\eth s^{i}_{j}\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\|\nabla\eth s^{i}_{j}\|^{2}\right]\lesssim\varepsilon.

for all

.
This shows that the operator

is continuous, thus, concluding the proof.

The following lemma implies the well-posedness of the time–discrete formulation.

Lemma 4.1

(Well-posedness a semilinear elliptic problem)
.

For a given
F\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, there exists a unique solution

of the elliptic problem

\displaystyle(\beta_{\varepsilon}(w),\zeta)+(\nabla w,\nabla\zeta)=(F,\zeta)\qquad\text{for all }\zeta\in{\cal H}^{1}.

(4.4)

Proof.

The proof is based on monotonicity arguments. Let the operator
\mathfrak{F}:{\cal H}^{1}\to{\cal H}^{-1}
be defined by the inner product

\displaystyle\langle\zeta,\mathfrak{F}(w)\rangle:=(\beta_{\varepsilon}(w),\zeta)+(\nabla w,\nabla\zeta).

(4.5)

Then

is strongly monotone since

\langle w-v,\mathfrak{F}(w)-\mathfrak{F}(v)\rangle\geq\varepsilon\|w-v\|^{2}+\|\nabla(w-v)\|^{2}\geq\varepsilon\|w-v\|_{{\cal H}^{1}}^{2}.

Furthermore,
\mathfrak{F}
is Lipschitz continuous since, using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,
we obtain

\|\mathfrak{F}(w)-\mathfrak{F}(v)\|_{{\cal H}^{-1}}\leq\sup_{\zeta\in{\cal H}^{1}}\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{-1}\|w-v\|\|\zeta\|+\|\nabla(w-v)\|\|\nabla\zeta\|}{\|\zeta\|_{{\cal H}^{1}}}\right)\leq\varepsilon^{-1}\|w-v\|_{{\cal H}^{1}}.

Hence, invoking the nonlinear Lax-Milgram Lemma
[
26

completes the proof.
∎

Observe that the operator
\widetilde{\mathfrak{F}}:{\cal H}^{1}\to{\cal H}^{-1}
defined by the inner product

\displaystyle\langle\zeta,\widetilde{\mathfrak{F}}(w)\rangle:=(\beta_{\varepsilon}(w)-\tau\,f_{0}(\beta_{\varepsilon}(w),\vec{s}(t_{n})),\zeta)+(\nabla w,\nabla\zeta).

(4.6)

is strictly monotone with respect to

\int_{0}^{T}[\langle\zeta,\partial_{t}\beta_{\varepsilon}(u)\rangle+(\nabla u,\nabla\zeta)]=\int_{0}^{T}(f_{0}(\beta_{\varepsilon}(u),\vec{s}),\zeta).

Defining
M_{s,\varepsilon}=\beta_{\varepsilon}(u)
we obtain the desired solution. We conclude that

M_{s,\varepsilon}\in{\bf{\cal X}}\hookrightarrow C([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
, see
[
11
, Section 5.9]
for the continuous embedding result.

(Step 2) A-priori bounds:
The a-priori estimate (
3.4
) follows by inserting
\varphi=M_{s,\varepsilon}
and
\varphi=\Phi_{\varepsilon}(M_{s,\varepsilon})
in (
3.3
) and proceeding similar to the steps of the time-discrete case in
Lemma

4.3
.
Lemma

4.4
shows that if in addition
M_{0}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
then
\partial_{t}M_{s,\varepsilon}\in L^{2}(Q)
which implies by the definition of weak derivatives that

\Delta\Phi_{\varepsilon}(M_{s,\varepsilon})=(\partial_{t}M_{s,\varepsilon}-f_{0})\in L^{2}(Q).

Multiplying the above equation with
\partial_{t}\Phi_{\varepsilon}(M_{s,\varepsilon})=\partial_{t}u\in L^{2}(Q)
, integrating in
Q
, and using integration by parts we conclude that

\int_{Q}\partial_{t}u\,\Delta u=-\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{t}(\tfrac{1}{2}\|\nabla u\|^{2})=\tfrac{1}{2}\|\nabla\Phi_{\varepsilon}(M_{0})\|^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\|\nabla u(T)\|^{2},

which proves (
3.1
). The detailed steps mimic its discrete counterpart in
Lemma

4.4
.

4.2
Finite time blow-up

The model (
1.1
) breaks down when
M
reaches
1
. Henceforth, we will refer to this as
blow-up
. Unlike in the case of Dirichlet or mixed boundary conditions, this situation cannot in general be excluded for homogeneous Neumann conditions. Whether a solution will blow-up in finite time or not depends on the initial values
M_{0},\,\vec{S}_{0}
.
One can construct cases when the solution will definitely blow-up in finite time. We give a simple example below.

Example 4.1

(Constant initial states)
.

Let us focus on the cellulolytic biofilm model with a single substrate
[
6
]
, i.e., we look at the system

\displaystyle\partial_{t}M

\displaystyle=\Delta\Phi(M)+f_{0}(M,S_{1})\text{ in }Q,

\displaystyle M(0)

\displaystyle=M_{0}\text{ in }\Omega,

\displaystyle[\nabla M\cdot\bm{\hat{n}}]|_{\partial\Omega}

\displaystyle=0,

(4.19a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}S_{1}

\displaystyle=f_{1}(M,S_{1})\text{ in }Q,

\displaystyle S_{1}(0)

\displaystyle=S_{0,1}\text{ in }\Omega.

(4.19b)

Moreover, the reaction terms are given by a non-dimensionalized version of (
1.2
),

\displaystyle f_{0}(m,s)=\left(\frac{s}{1+s}-\lambda\right)m,\quad f_{1}(m,s)=-\frac{s\,m}{1+s}.

(4.20)

For the initial and boundary values we assume that

\displaystyle M_{0}\equiv\bar{M}\in(0,1),\quad S_{0,1}\equiv\bar{S},

(4.21)

where
\bar{M},\,\bar{S}>0
are given constants.
Then it is clear that the solution
(M,S_{1})
of (
1.1
) remains constant in space for a given time. Hence, the system evolves according to the system of ODEs

\displaystyle\partial_{t}M=\frac{MS_{1}}{1+S_{1}}-\lambda\,M,\quad\partial_{t}S_{1}=-\frac{MS_{1}}{1+S_{1}}\ \text{ for }t>0\ \text{ with }(M(0),S_{1}(0))=(\bar{M},\bar{S}).

(4.22)

Clearly, for
\bar{M}
close to 1,
\bar{S}
large, and
\lambda
small, the biomass density
M
reaches 1 in finite time.

It is possible to generalize
Lemma

3.2
to provide a necessary condition for blow-up in finite time, or a sufficient condition for
M
to stay bounded away from
1
. This is stated in the following proposition for the single substrate (
k=1
) case.

Proposition 4.1

(Upper and lower bounds of
(M,S_{1})
)
.

Let
(P1)
–
(P6)
and
(P4enumi)
be satisfied,
k=1
(single substrate) and
\Gamma_{1}=\emptyset
(homogeneous Neumann condition) in (
1.1
). Recall
(P5)
, and let
f_{0}(m,s)
be increasing with respect to
s\geq 0
for fixed
m
, and let
f_{1}(m,s)
be decreasing with respect to
m\geq 0
for fixed
s
.
Let
(\check{M},\check{S},\hat{M},\hat{S})\in C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{+})^{4}
be the solution of the ODE system

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\check{M}=f_{0}(\check{M},\check{S}),\qquad\partial_{t}\check{S}=f_{1}(\hat{M},\check{S}),\\
\partial_{t}\hat{M}=f_{0}(\hat{M},\hat{S}),\qquad\partial_{t}\hat{S}=f_{1}(\check{M},\hat{S}),\end{cases}

(4.23)

with
(\check{M},\check{S},\hat{M},\hat{S})=(\underline{M},\underline{S},\overline{M},\overline{S})
at
t=0
. Further, assume that if
\nu_{1}>0
, then for
h_{1}
defined in
(P6)
,
\check{S}(t)\leq h_{1}\leq\hat{S}(t)
a.e. in
\partial\Omega
for all
t\in[0,T]
.
Let
(M,S)
be the weak solution of (
1.1
) in the sense of
Equation

2.9
. Then for all
t\in[0,T]
,

\displaystyle\check{M}(t)\leq M(t)\leq\hat{M}(t)\ \text{ and }\ \check{S}(t)\leq S_{1}(t)\leq\hat{S}(t)\ \text{ a.e. in }\Omega.

(4.24)

Remark 4.2

(Assumptions in
Proposition

4.1
)
.

Observe that the assumptions of
Proposition

4.1
are satisfied by the reaction terms in (
1.2
) and (
4.20
) which were considered, e.g., in
[
7
,
6
]
. Moreover, the assumption
h_{1}\in[\check{S}(t),\hat{S}(t)]
a.e. in
\partial\Omega
is a consistency condition that can be omitted in the case of immobile substrates (
\nu_{1}=0
) which occurs in the models for cellulolytic biofilms
[
6
]
, or when homogeneous Neumann conditions are assumed for
S
.

Proof.

The proof generalizes the arguments in
Lemma

3.2
and follows the proof of Proposition 1 of
[
18
]
. The existence and uniqueness of the solution
(\check{M},\check{S},\hat{M},\hat{S})
is evident from the Picard-Lindelöf Theorem. Moreover,
f_{0}(m,s)
is increasing with
s
,
f_{1}(m,s)
is decreasing with
m
, along with
\hat{M}(0)=\bar{M}\geq\underline{M}=\check{M}(0)
and
\hat{S}(0)=\bar{S}\geq\underline{S}=\check{S}(0)
, together imply for all
t>0
,

\displaystyle\hat{M}(t)\geq\check{M}(t),\quad\text{ and }\quad\hat{S}(t)\geq\check{S}(t).

(4.25)

This follows by writing from (
4.23
),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}[\check{M}(t)-\hat{M}(t)]_{+}^{2}=\int_{0}^{t}[\check{M}-\hat{M}]_{+}(f_{0}(\check{M},\check{S})-f_{0}(\hat{M},\hat{S})),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}[\check{S}(t)-\hat{S}(t)]_{+}^{2}=\int_{0}^{t}[\check{S}-\hat{S}]_{+}(f_{1}(\hat{M},\check{S})-f_{1}(\check{M},\hat{S})),

for
t>0
. Then, following the manipulations in
Lemma

3.2
(also repeated below), Gronwall’s Lemma yields
[\check{M}(t)-\hat{M}(t)]_{+}=[\check{S}(t)-\hat{S}(t)]_{+}=0
. We omit the detailed proof for brevity.

Insert the test functions
\varphi=[M-\hat{M}]_{+}
and
\zeta_{1}=[S_{1}-\hat{S}]_{+}
in (
2.9
). Observe that,
\zeta_{1}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
is a valid test function for
\nu_{1}>0
since
S_{1}-\hat{S}=h_{1}-\hat{S}\leq 0
on
\partial\Omega
. Then following the manipulations in
Lemma

3.2
, one obtains from the first equation that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{t}\left(\frac{1}{2}\|[M-\hat{M}]_{+}\|^{2}\right)\leq\int_{0}^{T}(f_{0}(M,S_{1})-f_{0}(\hat{M},\hat{S}),[M-\hat{M}]_{+})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}(f_{0}(M,S_{1})-f_{0}(\hat{M},S_{1}),[M-\hat{M}]_{+})+\int_{0}^{T}(f_{0}(\hat{M},S_{1})-f_{0}(\hat{M},\hat{S}),[M-\hat{M}]_{+})

\displaystyle\overset{\ref{prop:fl}}{\leq}C_{L}\int_{0}^{T}\|[M-\hat{M}]_{+}\|^{2}+C_{L}\int_{0}^{T}([S_{1}-\hat{S}]_{+},[M-\hat{M}]_{+})

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{T}[\|[M-\hat{M}]_{+}\|^{2}+\|[S_{1}-\hat{S}]_{+}\|^{2}].

(4.26a)

Here, we used that
f_{0}(\hat{M},\cdot)
is increasing to conclude that

(f_{0}(\hat{M},S_{1})-f_{0}(\hat{M},\hat{S}))[M-\hat{M}]_{+}\leq C_{L}[S_{1}-\hat{S}]_{+}[M-\hat{M}]_{+}.

Similarly, from the second equation, noting that
f_{1}(\cdot,s)
is decreasing for a given
s
, one obtains

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{t}\left(\frac{1}{2}\|[S_{1}-\hat{S}]_{+}\|^{2}\right)\leq\int_{0}^{T}(f_{1}(M,S_{1})-f_{1}(\check{M},\hat{S}),[S_{1}-\hat{S}]_{+})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}(f_{1}(M,S_{1})-f_{1}(\check{M},S_{1}),[S_{1}-\hat{S}]_{+})+\int_{0}^{T}(f_{1}(\check{M},S_{1})-f_{1}(\check{M},\hat{S}),[S_{1}-\hat{S}]_{+})

\displaystyle\leq C_{L}\int_{0}^{T}([M-\check{M}]_{-},[S_{1}-\hat{S}]_{+})+C_{L}\int_{0}^{T}\|[S_{1}-\hat{S}]_{+}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{T}[\|[M-\check{M}]_{-}\|^{2}+\|[S_{1}-\hat{S}]_{+}\|^{2}].

(4.26b)

Finally, inserting the test functions
\varphi=[M-\check{M}]_{-}
and
\zeta_{1}=[S_{1}-\check{S}]_{-}
in (
2.9
) we get analogous estimates to (
4.26
). Adding these inequalities and using Gronwall’s Lemma completes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.3

(Guaranteed finite time blow-up/ boundedness)
.

If the solution
\check{M}
in
Proposition

4.1
reaches
1
in finite time, then it implies that the solution of the original system
(M,S_{1})
blows up in finite time. On the other hand, if the solution
\hat{M}
remains bounded by a constant strictly less than
1
, then
M
does not blow up and hence, the solution
(M,S_{1})
is global-in-time. The bounds are sharp if
|\bar{M}-\underline{M}|
and
|\bar{S}-\underline{S}|
are small.

5
Spatial regularity of the biomass density

In this section, we analyze the spatial regularity of solutions of the degenerate diffusion equation (
1.1a
), i.e., we focus on the scalar equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}M=\nabla\cdot[D(M)\nabla M]+f(M,\cdot)\qquad\text{in }Q,

(5.1)

where
D:[0,1)\to[0,\infty)
and
f:[0,\infty)\times Q\to{\mathbb{R}}
.
The regularity results we derive apply to a broad class of degenerate diffusion problems, see
Remark

5.1
, including the biofilm growth models
[
7
,
6
]
.

It is well known that the degeneracy of the diffusion coefficient
D(0)=0
causes a finite speed of propagation and sharp fronts at the interface between the regions
\{M>0\}
and
\{M=0\}
corresponding to steep gradients of
M
. Despite this fact, the solution
M
is locally Hölder continuous. This was shown for porous medium type equations in
[
5
]
and for equations with degenerate and singular diffusion in
[
14
]
. The global space-time regularity of solutions of the porous medium equation in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
has also been studied extensively using optimal regularity theory, see
[
12
]
and the references therein.
Assuming homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions, in this section we show that
M
can further inherit global spatial regularity in the more general case (
5.1
), i.e.
M\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{r}(\Omega))
, where
r=1
for
a<2
, and
r<1
otherwise. This fact is not only mathematically intriguing but has important consequences in designing numerical tools and test functions for such problems. We now specify the assumptions on the functions
D
and
f
.

Assumption 5.1

(Assumptions on
D
and
f
)
.

The diffusion coefficient satisfies
(P1)
. In addition, there exists
a\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
and a constant
C>0
such that

D(m)\geq Cm^{a}

for all
m\in[0,1)
. The function
f:[0,\infty)\times Q\to{\mathbb{R}}
is Lipschitz continuous with respect to the first variable, and there exists a non-negative function
f_{\max}\in\mathrm{Lip}({\mathbb{R}})
such that
f(\cdot,(\bm{x},t))\leq f_{\max}(\cdot)
. Moreover, we assume that
f(0,(\bm{x},t))\geq 0
for all
(\bm{x},t)\in Q
.

Theorem 5.1

(Global spatial regularity of
M
)
.

Let
\Gamma_{1}=\emptyset
(homogeneous Neumann condition). Let
M\in{\bf{\cal W}}
with
\Phi(M)=\int_{0}^{M}D\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
, and
M(0)=M_{0}
(see
(P5)
) be the weak solution of (
5.1
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\langle\varphi,\partial_{t}M\rangle+\int_{0}^{T}(\nabla\Phi(M),\nabla\varphi)=\int_{0}^{T}(f(M,\cdot),\varphi),

(5.2)

for all
\varphi\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
. Then under the Assumption
5.1
,
M\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{r}(\Omega))
for

(a)

r=1
if either
a<2
or
\underline{M}=\mathrm{ess}\inf\{M_{0}\}>0
.

(b)

all
r<2/a
, if
a\geq 2
and
\underline{M}=0
.

Remark 5.1

(Generality of Theorem
5.1
)
.

Theorem
5.1
applies to the solution
M
of the coupled system (
1.1
) under the conditions
(P1)
–
(P6)
and
(P4enumi)
. Since the spatial irregularity of
M
stems from the degeneracy at
M=0
, our regularity results also cover diffusion coefficients
D
that are degenerate but non-singular, for instance, porous medium type equations. In this case, the additional assumption that
f
is bounded by
f_{\max}
can be omitted as solutions are not required to take values in
[0,1)
.

Remark 5.2

(Assumptions on the boundary conditions in Theorem
5.1
)
.

To simplify notations
Theorem

5.1
is stated for homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions. However, the result remains valid for Dirichlet or mixed boundary conditions provided that
\Phi(M)=\Phi(h^{e}_{0})
at
\Gamma_{1}
and the functions
\Psi_{\varepsilon}(h^{e}_{0})\in H^{1}(\Omega)
are uniformly bounded with respect to
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, where
\Psi_{\varepsilon}
is introduced in (
5.3
) and
h^{e}_{0}
in
(P6)
.

The rest of this section is dedicated to the proof of
Theorem

5.1
. The main idea behind the proof is to use a test function
\varphi
of the form
M^{-\alpha}
(
\alpha>0
) in (
5.2
). However,
\varphi
might not be a valid test function due to
M
not being sufficiently regular, and
M^{-\alpha}
having a singularity at
0
. To resolve this, we will construct a modified function that is admissible.

5.1
Some auxiliary functions

As in the proof of
Theorem

5.1
we consider the regularized problem introduced in
Lemma

3.1
. The function
\Phi_{\varepsilon}
is taken as in (
3.2
). For a given constant
\alpha>0
, we further introduce the
C^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
function

\displaystyle\Psi_{\varepsilon}(m):=\int_{1}^{m}\frac{\mathrm{d}\varrho}{\min\{\max\{\varepsilon,\varrho^{\alpha}\},1\}}.

(5.3)

Note that

\displaystyle\Psi^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}\geq 0\quad\text{ and }\quad\Psi_{\varepsilon}(m)<0\quad\text{ for }m<1.

(5.4)

Lemma 5.1

(Growth of
\Psi_{\varepsilon}
)
.

For a given
\alpha>0
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, let
\Psi_{\varepsilon}
be defined as in (
5.3
). Then, the following estimate holds,

\displaystyle|m\Psi_{\varepsilon}(m)|\lesssim 1+\int_{1}^{m}\Psi_{\varepsilon}\qquad\text{ for all }m\geq 0.

(5.5)

Proof.

Case 1 (
1<m
):
For
m>1
,
\Psi_{\varepsilon}(m)=m-1
, and the inequality can be verified directly.

Case 2 (
\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\leq m\leq 1
):
If
\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\leq m\leq 1
and
\alpha\not=1
, we have

\displaystyle|m\,\Psi_{\varepsilon}(m)|\leq\left|m\int^{m}_{1}\frac{1}{\varrho^{\alpha}}\right|d\rho|=\left|m\left(\frac{m^{1-\alpha}-1}{1-\alpha}\right)\right|\lesssim|m^{2-\alpha}-m|.

(5.6a)

Observe that, if
\alpha\leq 2
then the right hand side is bounded since
m\leq 1,
and (
5.5
) definitely holds. The case when
\alpha=1
can also be handled rather easily since it yields
\Psi_{\varepsilon}(m)=\log(m)
. The interesting case is when
\alpha>2
. Then, we can estimate the right-hand side of (
5.5
) as follows,

\displaystyle 1+\int_{1}^{m}\Psi_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=1+\int_{1}^{m}\int_{1}^{s}\frac{1}{\varrho^{\alpha}}d\rho ds=1+\int_{1}^{m}\frac{s^{1-\alpha}-1}{\alpha-1}\,\mathrm{d}s\gtrsim\int_{1}^{m}s^{1-\alpha}\gtrsim m^{2-\alpha}-1.

(5.6b)

Combining (
5.6
) and noting that
m<1
, we have (
5.5
) for this case.

Case 3 (
0\leq m<\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}
):

We only focus on
\alpha>2
since the case
\alpha\leq 2
can be shown exactly as in Case 2. We observe that

\displaystyle|m\,\Psi_{\varepsilon}(m)|=

\displaystyle\left|m\Psi_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{\alpha}})+m\smallint^{m}_{\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\tfrac{\mathrm{d}\varrho}{\max(\varepsilon,\varrho^{\alpha})}\right|=\left|m\Psi_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{\alpha}})+m\smallint^{m}_{\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|m\Psi_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{\alpha}})|+\frac{m(\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}-m)}{\varepsilon}\leq|\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\Psi_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{\alpha}})|+\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{\alpha}-1}(\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}-m).

(5.7a)

From Case 2 we conclude that
|\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\Psi_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{\alpha}})|\lesssim 1+\int^{\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}_{1}\Psi_{\varepsilon}
, and we obtain

\displaystyle 1+\int_{1}^{m}\Psi_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=1+\int_{1}^{\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\Psi_{\varepsilon}+\int^{m}_{\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\int_{1}\tfrac{\mathrm{d}\varrho}{\max(\varepsilon,\varrho^{\alpha})}=1+\int_{1}^{\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\Psi_{\varepsilon}+\int^{\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}_{m}\int^{1}\tfrac{\mathrm{d}\varrho}{\max(\varepsilon,\varrho^{\alpha})}

\displaystyle\geq 1+\int_{1}^{\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\Psi_{\varepsilon}+\int_{m}^{\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\int_{\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}^{1}\tfrac{\mathrm{d}\varrho}{\max(\varepsilon,\varrho^{\alpha})}\gtrsim|\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\Psi_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{\alpha}})|+(\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}-m)\int_{\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}^{1}\tfrac{\mathrm{d}\varrho}{\varrho^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\gtrsim|\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\Psi_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{\alpha}})|+\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{\alpha}-1}(\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}-m)-(\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}-m).

(5.7b)

Hence, combining again (
5.7
) we have (
5.5
).

∎

5.2
Boundedness of
M
in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{r}(\Omega))

To prove
Theorem

5.1
we first show the following lemma.

Lemma 5.2

(An estimate for the regularized solutions)
.

Let Assumption
5.1
hold and
\Gamma_{1}=\emptyset
. For
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
\alpha>0
, let
\Phi_{\varepsilon}
and
\Psi_{\varepsilon}
be defined by (
3.2
) and (
5.3
) respectively. Let
M_{\varepsilon}\in{\bf{\cal X}}
satisfy
M_{\varepsilon}(0)=M_{0}
and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\langle\varphi,\partial_{t}M_{\varepsilon}\rangle+\int_{0}^{T}(\nabla\Phi_{\varepsilon}(M_{\varepsilon}),\nabla\varphi)=\int_{0}^{T}(f(M_{\varepsilon},\cdot),\varphi),

for all
\varphi\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\min\{M_{\varepsilon}^{a-\alpha},1\}|\nabla M_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\lesssim 1+\int_{\Omega}\int_{1}^{M_{0}}\Psi_{\varepsilon}.

(5.8)

Proof.

First, we show that the following estimate holds,

\displaystyle\Phi^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(m)\,\Psi^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(m)\gtrsim\min\{1,m^{a-\alpha}\}\qquad\text{ for all }m\geq 0.

(5.9)

We distinguish several cases.

Case 1:
{\Phi_{\varepsilon}}^{\prime}(m)=D(m)
.

This implies that
\varepsilon\leq{\Phi_{\varepsilon}}^{\prime}(m)=D(m)\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
and hence,
m<1
.
If
{\Psi_{\varepsilon}}^{\prime}(m)=m^{-\alpha}
, then the result follows from Assumption
5.1
.
If
{\Psi_{\varepsilon}}^{\prime}(m)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
, then
\Phi^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(m)\,\Psi^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(m)=D(m)/\varepsilon>1
.
Finally, if
{\Psi_{\varepsilon}}^{\prime}(m)=1
, then
m\geq 1
which is excluded.

Case 2:
{\Phi_{\varepsilon}}^{\prime}(m)=\varepsilon
.
Consequently,
m<1
. The definition of
\Phi_{\varepsilon}
in (
3.2
) implies that
\varepsilon\geq D(m)\gtrsim m^{a}
, where the last inequality holds by Assumption
5.1
. Hence, for
\Psi^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon^{-1}
the product
\Phi^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}\,\Psi^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}=1
and for
\Psi^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}=m^{-\alpha}
we have
\Phi^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}\,\Psi^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}\gtrsim m^{a-\alpha}
.

Case 3:
{\Phi_{\varepsilon}}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
.
This case follows similarly.

Inserting the test function
\varphi=\Psi_{\varepsilon}(M_{\varepsilon})
in (
3.3
), the first term becomes

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\langle\partial_{t}M_{\varepsilon},\Psi_{\varepsilon}(M_{\varepsilon})\rangle=\int_{\Omega}\int_{1}^{M_{\varepsilon}(T)}\Psi_{\varepsilon}-\int_{\Omega}\int_{1}^{M_{0}}\Psi_{\varepsilon}.

(5.10a)

The second term of (
3.3
) gives

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}(\nabla\Phi_{\varepsilon}(M_{\varepsilon}),\nabla\Psi_{\varepsilon}(M_{\varepsilon}))

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Phi^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(M_{\varepsilon})\,\Psi^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(M_{\varepsilon})|\nabla M_{\varepsilon}|^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:PhiPsi}}{\gtrsim}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\min\{M_{\varepsilon}^{a-\alpha},1\}|\nabla M_{\varepsilon}|^{2}.

(5.10b)

Finally, the third term of (
3.3
) yields using
f(0,\cdot)\geq 0
and (
5.4
) that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}(f(M_{\varepsilon},\cdot),\Psi_{\varepsilon}(M_{\varepsilon}))=\int_{0}^{T}(f(M_{\varepsilon},\cdot)-f(0,\cdot),\Psi_{\varepsilon}(M_{\varepsilon}))+\int_{0}^{T}(f(0,\cdot),\Psi_{\varepsilon}(M_{\varepsilon}))

\displaystyle\quad\overset{\ref{prop:fl}}{\leq}C_{L}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|M_{\varepsilon}\Psi_{\varepsilon}(M_{\varepsilon})|\overset{f(0,\cdot)\geq 0}{+}\int_{0}^{T}(f(0,\cdot),[\Psi_{\varepsilon}(M_{\varepsilon})]_{+})

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|M_{\varepsilon}\Psi_{\varepsilon}(M_{\varepsilon})|\overset{\eqref{eq:PsiIneq}}{\lesssim}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}[1+\int_{1}^{M_{\varepsilon}}\Psi_{\varepsilon}].

(5.10c)

In the above, noting that
\Psi_{\varepsilon}(m)>0
only when
m>1
, we estimated
f(0,\cdot)[\Psi_{\varepsilon}(M_{\varepsilon})]_{+}\leq f_{\max}(0)|M_{\varepsilon}\Psi_{\varepsilon}(M_{\varepsilon})|
. Combining the inequalities (
5.10
) we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\int_{1}^{M_{\varepsilon}(T)}\Psi_{\varepsilon}+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\min\{M_{\varepsilon}^{a-\alpha},1\}|\nabla M_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\lesssim 1+\int_{\Omega}\int_{1}^{M_{0}}\Psi_{\varepsilon}+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\int_{1}^{M_{\varepsilon}}\Psi_{\varepsilon}.

(5.11)

Using Gronwall’s Lemma (
2.5a
) the estimate (
5.8
) follows.
∎

To conclude the proof of
Theorem

5.1
from (
5.8
), we need the following lemma.
For its proof we refer to Lemma 1.3 and Lemma B.1 of
[
24
]
.

Lemma 5.3

(Property of
H^{r}(\Omega)
)
.

If
u^{\gamma}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
for some
\gamma>1
then
u\in H^{r}(\Omega)
for all
r\in(0,\gamma^{-1}]
.

Proof of
Theorem

5.1
.

Case 1 (
\underline{M}=\mathrm{ess}\inf\{M_{0}\}>0
):
In this case, taking
\varepsilon_{1}^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}<\underline{M}
we conclude by (
5.3
)
that

\int_{\Omega}\int_{1}^{M_{0}}\Psi_{\varepsilon}\quad\text{ is uniformly bounded for all }\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{1}.

Hence, taking
\alpha=a
in Lemma
5.2
provides a uniform bound on
\int_{0}^{T}\|\nabla M_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}
. Moreover,
\|M_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega))}
is bounded by
Lemma

3.2
. Hence,
M_{\varepsilon}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
. Passing to the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, the convergence of
M_{\varepsilon}
to a unique
M\in{\bf{\cal W}}
follows from
Lemma

3.3

(see (
3.12
)). Consequently, the uniform bound implies that
M\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
.

Case 2 (
a<2
):
In this case, put
\alpha=a
. Then, passing the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
on the right hand side of (
5.8
) one has

\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\int_{\Omega}\int_{1}^{M_{0}}\Psi_{\varepsilon}\lesssim\int_{\Omega}\int_{M_{0}}^{1}(1-\varrho^{1-a})\,\mathrm{d}\varrho\lesssim 1.

Here we used that
M^{2-a}_{0}\leq 1
a.e. in
\Omega
by assumption
(P5)
. Hence, we again obtain a uniform bound on
\int_{0}^{T}\|\nabla M_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}
and consequently,
M\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
.

Case 3 (
a\geq 2
):
We set
\alpha=2-\delta
for sufficiently small
\delta>0
. Then the previous case and (
5.8
) gives that
(M_{\varepsilon})^{1+\frac{a-\alpha}{2}}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
and it is uniformly bounded. From
Lemma

5.3
it follows that
M_{\varepsilon}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{r}(\Omega))
for

r\leq\frac{2}{2+a-\alpha}=\frac{2}{a+\delta}\qquad\text{and sufficiently small }\delta>0.

This concludes the proof.
∎
