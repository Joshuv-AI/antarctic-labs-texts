Abstract
. In this paper, we ask: for which ( g , n ) (g,n) is the rational Chow or cohomology ring of ℳ ¯ g , n \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n} generated by tautological classes? This question has been fully answered in genus 0 0 by Keel (the Chow and cohomology rings are tautological for all n n [ 35 ] ) and genus 1 1 by Belorousski (the rings are tautological if and only if n ≤ 10 n\leq 10 [ 5 ] ). For g ≥ 2 g\geq 2 , work of van Zelm [ 54 ] shows the Chow and cohomology rings are not tautological once 2 ​ g + n ≥ 24 2g+n\geq 24 , leaving finitely many open cases. Here, we prove that the Chow and cohomology rings of ℳ ¯ g , n \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n} are isomorphic and generated by tautological classes for g = 2 g=2 and n ≤ 9 n\leq 9 and for 3 ≤ g ≤ 7 3\leq g\leq 7 and 2 ​ g + n ≤ 14 2g+n\leq 14 . For such ( g , n ) (g,n) , this implies that the tautological ring is Gorenstein and ℳ ¯ g , n \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n} has polynomial point count.

1.3.
Overview of the new techniques

We prove Theorem
1.4
with a novel method, which allows us to take advantage of the flexibility of excision in Chow, yet still obtain results in cohomology at the end. The key idea is prove our spaces have the
Chow–Künneth generation Property (CKgP)
, which is a way of making up for the lack of a Künneth formula in Chow. We say
X
has the CKgP if for all
Y
, there is a surjection

A^{*}(X)\otimes A^{*}(Y)\rightarrow A^{*}(X\times Y).

Below are some important observations about the CKgP, which pave the way for our approach. Each italicized statement is not difficult to establish (and some have already appeared in the literature
[
3
,
52
]
), but they combine into a very powerful tool when applied to moduli spaces of curves:

(1)

The CKgP plays well with stratifications, products, and finite group quotients.
Thus, using the inductive nature of its boundary, we can reduce showing
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
has the CKgP and
A^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})=R^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})
to showing the open moduli spaces
\mathcal{M}_{g^{\prime},n^{\prime}}
have the CKgP and
A^{*}(\mathcal{M}_{g^{\prime},n^{\prime}})=R^{*}(\mathcal{M}_{g^{\prime},n^{\prime}})
for
g^{\prime}\leq g
and
2g^{\prime}+n^{\prime}\leq 2g+n
(as explained in Section
4
).

(2)

If
X\rightarrow Y
is proper and surjective, and
X
has the CKgP, then
Y
has the CKgP
. Thus, to attack each piece
\mathcal{M}_{g^{\prime},n^{\prime}}
, we can pass from studying its gonality strata to studying Hurwitz spaces of covers with marked points (see Sections
8
–
10
).

(3)

If
U\subset X
is open and
X
has the CKgP, then
U
has the CKgP
. Often, we can construct a moduli space of marked curves (or curves with a map to
\mathbb{P}^{1}
or
\mathbb{P}^{2}
) as a quotient of affine space minus some discriminant locus. In contrast with having polynomial point count or knowing generators in cohomology, we may throw out
arbitrary
closed subsets and still preserve CKgP and generators in Chow.

(4)

If
X
is smooth and proper and has the CKgP, then the cycle class map is an isomorphism.
In other words — no matter what techniques from Chow we have used in (
2
) and (
3
) — so long as we put our pieces back together into a smooth, proper space, we now also have access to cohomology.

Part (1) above naturally leads us prove results for the collection of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
that satisfy
2g+n\leq a
for some
a
(a triangular region bounded by the axes and a line of slope
-2
).
From this perspective, one should hope to prove

A^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})=R^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})
for all
2g+n\leq 12
(the largest such triangular region that does not contain
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{1,11}
).
Theorem
1.4
confirms this hope, but also provides some answers to Questions
1.1
and
1.2

above
this region. Obtaining filled circles with
2g+n>12
, such as
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{7}
, requires some more delicate, ad hoc arguments, and we view the results in this region as more surprising.

1.4.
The Gorenstein property

Having determined many cases where tautological classes generate the Chow ring, a natural next question is what are the relations among them?
Pixton conjectured a set of relations among the generators of the tautological ring, which were later proven to hold by Pandharipande–Pixton–Zvonkine
[
42
]
(in cohomology) and Janda
[
33
]
(in the Chow ring). Pixton’s relations restrict to the Faber–Zagier relations on
\mathcal{M}_{g}
, and one can also restrict Pixton’s relations on
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
to
\mathcal{M}^{\ct}_{g,n}
or
\mathcal{M}^{\rt}_{g,n}

[
49
]
. We will let
R^{*}_{P}(\mathcal{M})
denote the ring generated by tautological classes modulo Pixton’s relations, where
\mathcal{M}
is any of the above moduli spaces of curves. Pixton has conjectured that
R_{P}^{*}(\mathcal{M})=R^{*}(\mathcal{M})
.

It is known that
R^{i}(\mathcal{M})=0
for
i>d

[
39
,
22
]
and
R^{d}(\mathcal{M})=\mathbb{Q}

[
39
,
28
,
31
,
22
]
where

d:=\begin{cases}g-2+n-\delta_{0,g}&\text{if $\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{\rt}$}\\
2g-3+n&\text{if $\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{\ct}$}\\
3g-3+n&\text{if $\mathcal{M}=\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}$.}\end{cases}

One of the prominent questions asked about the tautological ring is if
R^{*}(\mathcal{M})
is Gorenstein with socle in codimension
d
, meaning there is a perfect pairing

R^{i}(\mathcal{M})\times R^{d-i}(\mathcal{M})\to R^{d}(\mathcal{M})\cong\mathbb{Q}.

The Gorenstein property is known to hold for
R^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})=R^{*}_{P}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})
corresponding to
black filled circles on the left of Figure
1
, as well as for
R^{*}(\mathcal{M}_{g})=R^{*}_{P}(\mathcal{M}_{g})
for
g\leq 23
by Faber. In these cases, it was computationally proven that
R^{*}_{P}(\mathcal{M})
is Gorenstein using the Sage package admcycles
[
17
]
; this in turn implies
R^{*}(\mathcal{M})=R^{*}_{P}(\mathcal{M})
, since the perfect pairing prohibits any further relations. Although it was previously speculated that
R^{*}(\mathcal{M}_{g})
may be Gorenstein
[
27
]
, it is known that
R_{P}^{*}(\mathcal{M}_{g})
is not Gorenstein for
g\geq 24
.

Furthermore, the Gorenstein property was shown to fail by Petersen and Tommasi for
R^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{2,n})
and
RH^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{2,n})
with
n\geq 20
, as well as

R^{*}(\mathcal{M}_{2,n}^{\ct})
with
n\geq 8

[
47
,
46
]
.
Pixton has also computationally shown
R_{P}^{*}(\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{\rt})
is not Gorenstein in some other cases
[
49
, Appendix A]
. However, a case of Gorenstein failure has yet to be proven for
R^{*}(\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{\rt})
(which includes the case of
R^{*}(\mathcal{M}_{g})
). See
[
41
]
for further discussion.

Using Poincaré duality, the
(g,n)
in the top row of Theorem
1.4
provide many new cases where
the Gorenstein property does hold in Chow and cohomology.

Corollary 1.8
.

Suppose
(g,n)
satisfies
g=2
and
n\leq 9
or
g\geq 3
and
2g+n\leq 14
. Then
A^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})=R^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})=H^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})=RH^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})
is Gorenstein.

In these cases, the ring is (in theory) completely determined:
a class is zero if and only if it pairs to zero with all generators in complementary dimension. The pairing on generators of

is can be effectively computed (as explained in

Corollary

also provides a large region

We therefore propose the cases in Theorem

1.6.
Notations and conventions.

All schemes are taken over a fixed algebraically closed field of characteristic
0
or greater than

2.
Preliminaries on the tautological ring

2.1.
The stable graph stratification

The moduli space
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
of stable curves of genus
g
with
n
marked points is stratified by topological type. Associated to each topological type is a stable graph
\Gamma
. To a stable graph
\Gamma
, one associates the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma}
consisting of points in
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
with dual graph
\Gamma
. The disjoint union

\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}=\coprod\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma}

is called the
stable graph stratification
.
We will also consider the space

\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\Gamma}=\prod_{v}\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g(v),n(v)},

where
v
is a vertex of the stable graph
\Gamma
. There is an associated morphism

(2.1)

\xi_{\Gamma}:\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\Gamma}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}

whose image is the closure of
\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma}
. The spaces
\xi_{\Gamma}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\Gamma})
are in fact the union of
\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma^{\prime}}
for graphs
\Gamma^{\prime}

that can be taken to
\Gamma
via a sequence of edge contractions.
A union of strata
\bigcup_{\Gamma\in S}\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma}
is open if and only if the set
S
is closed under contractions. (Contracting as far as possible, one gets to the graph with a single vertex of genus
g
, which is
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}
.)

2.2.
Tautological rings

Roughly speaking, the tautological ring
R^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})\subset A^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})
is the
\mathbb{Q}
-subalgebra of the Chow ring generated by the “naturally occurring” cycles on
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
.
There are several natural morphisms between the moduli spaces of stable curves with marked points
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
as
g
and
n
vary. First, there are the forgetful morphisms

\pi_{j}:\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n-1}

obtained by forgetting the
j^{\mathrm{th}}
marking. In addition, there are the gluing morphisms

\xi_{\Gamma}:\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\Gamma}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}

as in (
2.1
) for stable graphs
\Gamma
.

Definition 2.1
.

(1)

The system of tautological rings
\{R^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})\}
is the set of smallest
\mathbb{Q}
-subalgebras of the Chow rings
A^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})
containing the unit elements
[\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}]
and that is closed under the pushforwards by the forgetful morphisms
\pi_{j}
and the gluing morphisms
\xi_{\Gamma}
for each stable graph
\Gamma
.

(2)

Let
U
be an open substack of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
. The tautological ring
R^{*}(U)
is defined to be the image of
R^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})
under the natural restriction map
A^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})\rightarrow A^{*}(U)
.

(3)

The tautological ring in cohomology
RH^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})
is defined as the image of
R^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})
under the cycle class map

A^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})\rightarrow H^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n},\mathbb{Q}).

The tautological rings are also closed under pullbacks along the forgetful and gluing maps (see
[
21
, Section 0.3]
).

It is also useful to have a more explicit description of the tautological ring for a given pair
(g,n)
. Let
f:\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{g,n}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
be the universal curve, which comes equipped with
n
sections
\sigma_{i}:\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{g,n}
, corresponding to the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
marked point. We define the
\psi
classes as

\psi_{i}=\sigma_{i}^{*}c_{1}(\omega_{f}).

Under the identification of
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{g,n}
with
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+1}
, the universal curve map
f:\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{g,n}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
is identified with the map forgetting the last marked point. Following
[
1
]
, we set

\kappa_{j}=f_{*}(\psi_{n+1}^{j+1}).

The
\lambda
classes are the Chern classes of the Hodge bundle

(2.2)

\lambda_{i}:=c_{i}(f_{*}\omega_{f}).

They are expressed in terms of the
\kappa
classes with rational coefficients using Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch.

Let
\alpha
be a polynomial in
\psi
and
\kappa
classes on
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\Gamma}
. The pushforward of
\alpha
under
\xi_{\Gamma}
is called a decorated stratum class on
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
. The decorated stratum classes provide generators for
R^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})
as a
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space
[
30
, Proposition 11]
.

3.
The Chow–Künneth generation property

Because of the nature of the stable graph stratification, we will want to compute generators for Chow rings of spaces
A^{*}(\prod_{i}\mathcal{M}_{g_{i},n_{i}})
. But unlike for cohomology, there is no Künneth formula in general for Chow rings, so it is not obvious how the exterior product maps

\bigotimes_{i}A_{*}(\mathcal{M}_{g_{i},n_{i}})\rightarrow A_{*}(\prod_{i}\mathcal{M}_{g_{i},n_{i}})

behave.
In this section, we study when the exterior product map

A_{*}(Y)\otimes A_{*}(X)\rightarrow A_{*}(Y\times X)

is surjective.
All of the stacks we need to consider later in the paper are finite type over the base field and admit a stratification by global quotient stacks. So throughout this section, we assume that all stacks are of this form. All Deligne–Mumford stacks admit a stratification by global quotient stacks (see
[
36
, Proposition 4.5.5]
).

The following definition is taken from
[
3
]
.

Definition 3.1

(Definition 2.5 of
[
3
]
)
.

We say
Y
has the
Chow–Künneth generation Property
(CKgP, for short) if for all algebraic stacks
X
(of finite type and admitting a stratification by global quotient stacks), the exterior product map

A_{*}(Y)\otimes A_{*}(X)\to A_{*}(Y\times X)

is surjective.

3.1.
Basic lemmas

We collect below several basic lemmas about the CKgP.

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose that
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
have the CKgP. Then
Y_{1}\times Y_{2}
has the CKgP. More generally, if
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}
have CKgP then
Y_{1}\times\cdots\times Y_{n}
has CKgP.

Proof.

The first claim is
[
3
, Lemma 2.8]
, and the second claim follows by induction on
n
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose
Y
has the CKgP and
U\subset Y
is open. Then
U
has the CKgP.

Proof.

For any
X
, we have a commuting square
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{*}(Y)\otimes A_{*}(X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{*}(U)\otimes A_{*}(X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{*}(Y\times X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{*}(U\times X).}

The composition

A_{*}(Y)\otimes A_{*}(X)\rightarrow A_{*}(Y\times X)\rightarrow A_{*}(U\times X)

is surjective by the fact that
Y
has the CKgP and excision. It follows that the right vertical arrow
is also surjective.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose
Y
admits a finite stratification
Y=\coprod_{S\in\mathcal{S}}S
such that each
S\in\mathcal{S}
has the CKgP. Then
Y
has the CKgP.

Proof.

We induct on the size of the stratification,
\#\mathcal{S}
. The case
\#\mathcal{S}=1
is clear. Let
T
be a minimal element of
\mathcal{S}
, which exists because
\mathcal{S}
is finite. Set
U=Y\smallsetminus T
. Let
X
be any finite type stack. The following commutative diagram consists of excision sequences, with vertical maps given by the exterior product.
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{*}(T)\otimes A_{*}(X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{*}(Y)\otimes A_{*}(X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{*}(U)\otimes A_{*}(X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{*}(T\times X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{*}(Y\times X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{*}(U\times X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}

By induction the right vertical map is surjective (using the stratification
\mathcal{S}-T
of
U
). The left vertical map is surjective because
T
has the CKgP. A diagram chase shows that the middle map is surjective as well, so
Y
has the CKgP.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\pi:V\rightarrow Y
be an affine bundle. Then
V
has the CKgP if and only if
Y
does.

Proof.

For any algebraic stack
X
, the map
V\times X\rightarrow Y\times X
is an affine bundle. We have the following commutative diagram:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{*}(V)\otimes A_{*}(X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{*}(V\times X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{*}(Y)\otimes A_{*}(X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{*}(Y\times X)}

The vertical maps are isomorphisms induced by pullback. Therefore, one horizontal map is surjective if and only if the other is.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\pi:\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow Y
be a gerbe banded by a finite group. Then
Y
has the CKgP if and only if
\mathcal{Y}
does.

Proof.

The proof is the same as Lemma
3.5
, using that pullback induces an isomorphism on Chow rings for gerbes banded by finite groups.
∎

Lemma 3.7
.

Suppose that
Y
has the CKgP and that
G\rightarrow Y
is a Grassmann bundle over
Y
. Then
G
has the CKgP.

Proof.

The Chow ring of a Grassmann bundle is generated over the Chow ring of the base by the Chern classes
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}
of the tautological subbundle. Consider the diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A^{*}(Y)[c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}]\otimes A^{*}(X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A^{*}(Y\times X)[c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A^{*}(G)\otimes A^{*}(X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A^{*}(G\times X)}

The top horizontal arrow is surjective because
Y
has the CKgP. The right vertical arrow is surjective because
G\times X\to Y\times X
is a Grassmann bundle whose tautological subbundle is the pullback of the tautological subbundle on
G
. It follows that the bottom horizontal arrow is also surjective.
∎

Lemma 3.8
.

The following stacks have the CKgP:

(1)

The classifying stack
\BGL_{n}
,

(2)

The classifying stack
\BSL_{n}
,

(3)

The classifying stack
\BPGL_{n}
.

Proof.

(1) Take
V_{k}
be the representation of
\GL_{n}
given by matrices of size
n\times k
. Let
U_{k}\subset V_{k}
be the open subset of full rank matrices. The complement of
U_{k}
has codimension
k-n+1
. Then

[U_{k}/\GL_{n}]\times X\cong G(n,k)\times X.

Taking
k\to\infty
and noting that
G(n,k)
has the CKgP by Lemma
3.7
, we see that
\BGL_{n}
has the CKgP.

(2) The case of
\BSL_{n}
is similar, except that the quotient
[U_{k}/\SL_{n}]
is isomorphic to the complement of the zero section of the line bundle
\det\mathcal{S}\rightarrow G(n,k)
, where
\mathcal{S}
is the tautological subbundle. By Lemmas
3.3
and
3.5
, we see
[U_{k}/\SL_{n}]
has CKgP too, and taking
k\to\infty
, we see that
\BSL_{n}
has CKgP.

(3) The map
\BSL_{n}\rightarrow\BPGL_{n}
is a gerbe banded by
\mu_{n}
. Thus,
\BPGL_{n}
has the CKgP by (2) and Lemma
3.6
.
∎

Lemma 3.9
.

Suppose
Y
is a stack that admits a coarse moduli space
\pi:Y\rightarrow M
. Then
Y
has the CKgP if and only if
M
does.

Proof.

Let
X
be a stack that admits a stratification by global quotient stacks. By noetherian induction, we may reduce to the case that
X
is a global quotient stack. By
[
36
, Proposition 4.5.6]
, for any integer
N
, there exists a vector bundle
E\to X
and a representable open substack
U\subset E
whose complement has codimension at least
N
. Formation of coarse moduli spaces commutes with flat base change, so
M\times U
is a coarse moduli space for
Y\times U
. By
[
55
, Proposition 6.1]
, there is an isomorphism
A^{*}(M\times U)\cong A^{*}(Y\times U)
. Then,
for
*<N
, we have

A^{*}(X\times Y)\cong A^{*}(E\times Y)\cong A^{*}(U\times Y)\cong A^{*}(U\times M)\cong A^{*}(E\times M)\cong A^{*}(X\times M).

Taking
N
to be arbitrarily large, we have an isomorphism
A^{*}(X\times Y)\cong A^{*}(X\times M)
.

Now we have the following commutative diagram, where both horizontal maps are isomorphisms.
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{*}(Y)\otimes A_{*}(X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{*}(M)\otimes A_{*}(X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{*}(Y\times X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{*}(M\times X).}

If one of the vertical maps is surjective, then so is the other one.
∎

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
f:Y\rightarrow X
be a surjective proper morphism that is representable by Deligne–Mumford stacks. (For example, any quotient by a finite group
Y\to Y/G
.) If
Y
has the CKgP, then
X
has the CKgP.

Proof.

This is a special case of
[
3
, Lemma 2.9]
.
∎

3.2.
The cycle class map

One important consequence of the CKgP is that, if the space is smooth and proper, the cycle class map is an isomorphism.
The proof of the following lemma was inspired by the argument in
[
34
, Theorem 3.6]
, which involves constructing an algebraic decomposition of the diagonal to prove surjectivity of the cycle class map.

Lemma 3.11
.

Suppose
X
is a smooth, proper Deligne–Mumford stack of dimension
d
.
If
X
has the CKgP, then the cycle class map
\mathrm{cl}:A^{*}(X)\to H^{*}(X,\mathbb{Q})
is an isomorphism.

Proof.

Let
p_{1}:X\times X\to X
and
p_{2}:X\times X\to X
be the projection maps onto the first and second factors.
Because
X
has the CKgP, the map
\bigoplus_{i=0}^{d}A^{i}(X)\otimes A^{d-i}(X)\to A^{d}(X\times X)
is surjective. In particular, the class of the diagonal
[\Delta]\in A^{d}(X\times X)
has the form

(3.1)

[\Delta]=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\sum_{j=0}^{a_{i}}n_{i,j}\cdot(p_{1}^{*}y_{i,j})\cdot(p_{2}^{*}z_{i,j})\in A^{d}(X\times X).

where
y_{i,j}\in A^{i}(X)
and
z_{i,j}\in A^{d-i}(X)
. Applying the cycle class map gives a decomposition of the diagonal in cohomology:

[\Delta]=\sum_{i=0}^{d}n_{i,j}\cdot p_{1}^{*}\mathrm{cl}(y_{i,j})\cdot p_{2}^{*}\mathrm{cl}(z_{i,j})\in H^{2d}(X\times X).

We use this to show that all cohomology is algebraic. Given
\alpha\in H^{n}(X)
, we have

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=p_{2*}(p_{1}^{*}\alpha\cdot[\Delta])

\displaystyle=p_{2*}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{d}\sum_{j=0}^{a_{i}}p_{1}^{*}\alpha\cdot p_{1}^{*}\mathrm{cl}(y_{i,j})\cdot p_{2}^{*}\mathrm{cl}(z_{i,j})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\sum_{j=0}^{a_{i}}p_{2*}p_{1}^{*}(\alpha\cdot\mathrm{cl}(y_{i,j}))\cdot\mathrm{cl}(z_{i,j}).

For dimension reasons,
\alpha\cdot\cl(y_{i,j})=0
whenever
n+2i>2d
.
On the other hand,

p_{2*}p_{1}^{*}(\alpha\cdot\mathrm{cl}(y_{i}))
has codimension
n+2i-2d
, so it vanishes if
n+2i<2d
. It follows that the only non-zero terms in the sum above are those with
n+2i=2d
. In this case,
\alpha\cdot\mathrm{cl}(y_{i,j})\in H^{2d}(X)=\mathbb{Q}
is top degree, so we get

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{a_{i}}\deg(\alpha\cdot\mathrm{cl}(y_{i,j}))\cdot\mathrm{cl}(z_{i,j}),

showing
\alpha
is in the image of
\mathrm{cl}
.

For injectivity, let
\beta\in A^{i}(X)
be in the kernel of
\mathrm{cl}
. Then for any class
\gamma\in A^{d-i}(X)
, we have

0=\mathrm{cl}(\beta)\cup\mathrm{cl}(\gamma)=\mathrm{cl}(\beta\cdot\gamma)=\deg(\beta\cdot\gamma).

Applying the diagonal correspondence in Chow (
3.1
), and using the push–pull formula as in the proof of surjectivity, we then have

\beta=\sum_{j=0}^{a_{i}}\deg(\beta\cdot y_{i,j})\cdot z_{i,j}=0.\qed

.

As an immediate consequence of Lemma
3.11
, we see that the CKgP allows us to convert results in Chow to results in cohomology.

Lemma 3.14
.

Suppose
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
has the CKgP and
A^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})=R^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})
. Then we also have
H^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})=RH^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})
.

3.2.1.
Tautological cohomology and polynomial point counts

By the Grothendieck–Lefschetz trace formula, the cohomology of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
is intimately related to its point counts over finite fields. Given a Deligne–Mumford stack
X
that is smooth and proper over
\mathbb{Z}
, we say
X
has
polynomial point count
if
\#X(\mathbb{F}_{q})=P(q)
for some polynomial
P
with integer coefficients. Here, the count of points is weighted by size of the automorphism group of each point:

\#X(k)=\sum_{\xi\in X(k)}\frac{1}{|\mathrm{Aut}(\xi)|}.

If
X
has polynomial point count, then van den Bogaart–Edixhoven
[
53
]
show that the polynomial
P
determines the cohomology of
X
.
Conversely, information about the cohomology of
X

can determine information about its point counts.
The following fact was explained to us by Sam Payne.

Proposition 3.15
.

If
H^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})=RH^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})
, then
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
has polynomial point count.

Proof.

This result relies crucially on the fact that
X:=\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
is smooth and proper over
\mathbb{Z}
.
The
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-points of
X
are the fixed points of the action of Frobenius on
X_{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
.
Using the Grothendieck–Lefschetz trace formula
[
4
]
,
X
has polynomial point count if Frobenius acts by powers of
q
on

H^{*}_{\text{\'{e}t}}(X_{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}},\mathbb{Q}_{\ell})
for
\ell
a prime not equal to
p
.
Frobenius always acts by
q^{k}
on the fundamental class of an algebraic subvariety of codimension
k
defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
Thus, it suffices to show that

H^{*}_{\text{\'{e}t}}(X_{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}},\mathbb{Q}_{\ell})
is generated by algebraic classes defined over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.

The tautological ring is generated by algebraic classes defined over
\mathbb{Z}
. Therefore, there is a surjection
R^{*}(X_{\mathbb{Z}})\to RH^{*}(X_{\mathbb{C}})
. Assuming
H^{*}(X_{\mathbb{C}})=RH^{*}(X_{\mathbb{C}})
, we obtain the diagram below where the arrow from the left to the far upper right is surjective.

(3.2)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A^{*}(X_{\mathbb{C}})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{*}(X_{\mathbb{C}})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces R^{*}(X_{\mathbb{Z}})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A^{*}(X_{\mathbb{F}_{p}})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{*}_{\text{\'{e}t}}(X_{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}},\mathbb{Q}_{\ell})}

Because
X
is smooth over
\mathbb{Z}
, we have (see
[
53
, Proposition 3.1]
)

H^{*}_{\text{\'{e}t}}(X_{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}},\mathbb{Q}_{\ell})\cong H^{*}_{\text{\'{e}t}}(X_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}},\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}).

Meanwhile, choosing an isomorphism
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}\cong\mathbb{C}
, the comparison theorem gives an isomorphism

H^{*}_{\text{\'{e}t}}(X_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}},\mathbb{Q}_{\ell})\cong H^{*}(X_{\mathbb{C}},\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}).

Thus, upon tensoring the vector spaces in (
3.2
) up to
\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}
, there is an isomorphism between the two vector spaces in the rightmost column.
This implies that the image of
R^{*}(X_{\mathbb{Z}})\to H^{*}_{\text{\'{e}t}}(X_{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}},\mathbb{Q}_{\ell})
generates
H^{*}_{\text{\'{e}t}}(X_{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}},\mathbb{Q}_{\ell})
over
\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}
.
Hence, the image of
A^{*}(X_{\mathbb{F}_{p}})\to H^{*}_{\text{\'{e}t}}(X_{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}},\mathbb{Q}_{\ell})
generates

H^{*}_{\text{\'{e}t}}(X_{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}},\mathbb{Q}_{\ell})
over
\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}
. Frobenius acts on such elements by powers of
q
, so we obtain a polynomial point count.
∎

By Proposition
3.15
,
Corollary
1.5

Lemma 7.1

To access pointed curves of genus

, so this only excludes characteristic

In
[

, Faber–Pandharipande study pushforwards to

of cycles from
Hurwitz spaces of covers with specified ramification behavior. This gives rise to many examples of effective, geometrically defined cycles on
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
that are tautological.

8.1.

Fix a genus
g
and a degree

. Let

\mu^{1},\dots,\mu^{m}

be a collection of partitions of

satisfying

(8.1)

2g-2+2k=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(k-\ell(\mu^{i}))

where
\ell
is the length of the partition.
Faber–Pandharipande study the space

, which parametrizes degree

parametrizes simply branched covers together with a marking of all of the points in the ramified fibers.

The admissible covers compactification of this space, which they call
\overline{H}_{g}(\mu^{1},\ldots,\mu^{m})

\rho:\overline{H}_{g}(\mu^{1},\ldots,\mu^{m})\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,\sum\ell(\mu^{i})}
by taking the stabilization of the marked source curve. Faber–Pandharipande prove that the pushforward of the fundamental class of
\overline{H}_{g}(\mu^{1},\ldots,\mu^{m})
is tautological.
We might then consider the image

of this cycle in
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
, after forgetting some subset of the marked points. When the composition

\overline{H}_{g}(\mu^{1},\ldots,\mu^{m})\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,\sum\ell(\mu^{i})}\to\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}

is generically finite onto

, it follows that

. Restricting to the locus of smooth curves we find:

Lemma 8.1
.

If the map
H_{g}(\mu^{1},\ldots,\mu^{m})\to\mathcal{M}_{g,\sum\ell(\mu^{i})}\to\mathcal{M}_{g,n}
has generically finite fibers, then
the closure of the image of
H_{g}(\mu^{1},\ldots,\mu^{m})
in
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}
has tautological fundamental class.

Definition 8.2
.

When
H_{g}(\mu^{1},\ldots,\mu^{m})\to\mathcal{M}_{g,\sum\ell(\mu^{i})}\to\mathcal{M}_{g,n}
has generically finite fibers, we will call the closure of the image a
Faber–Pandharipande Hurwitz cycle
, or
FPH cycle
, for short. Lemma
8.1
says FPH cycles are tautological.

Remark 8.3
.

Although Faber–Pandharipande state their results over
\mathbb{C}
,
the main tool they use is equivariant localization for torus actions, which holds over arbitrary algebraically closed fields (see
[
20
]
).
Their results thus remain valid over any algebraically closed field of characteristic
>k
(taking the characteristic
>k
is needed to ensure that there will be no wild ramification so the Riemann–Hurwitz formula (
8.1
) continues to hold).

9.2.

The basic idea is that, using structure theorems for degree

Proof.

The evaluation map factors as

\displaystyle H^{0}(\mathbb{P}E^{\vee},L)

\displaystyle\rightarrow H^{0}(C,L|_{C})\rightarrow H^{0}(\Gamma,L|_{\Gamma}).

The first map is surjective because
H^{1}(\mathbb{P}E^{\vee},\mathcal{O})=0

In the construction of

Hence,

The map

By Serre duality,

∎

Now, define
U\subset(\mathbb{P}\mathcal{E}^{\vee})^{n}
be the locus over which the evaluation map (
9.2
) is surjective. We know that
U
is open, but a priori it could be empty, and in fact
U
will be empty when
n
is too large.
However, when
n\leq g+7
,
Lemma
9.1
shows that the image of
\mathcal{H}_{3,g,n}
inside
(\mathbb{P}\mathcal{E}^{\vee})^{n}
is contained in
U
.
Hence, we find that the inclusion
\mathcal{H}_{3,g,n}\subset\mathcal{Y}
factors through
\mathcal{H}_{3,g,n}\subset\mathcal{Y}|_{U}
.
Moreover, by definition of
\mathcal{Y}
and
U
, we have that
\mathcal{Y}|_{U}
is the kernel of the restriction of (
9.2
) to
U
.
In particular,
\mathcal{Y}|_{U}
is a vector bundle over
U
. This implies the following.

Lemma 9.2
.

\mathcal{H}_{3,g,n}
has the CKgP for
n\leq g+7
.

Proof.

We have maps

\mathcal{H}_{3,g,n}\subset\mathcal{Y}|_{U}\to U\subset(\mathbb{P}\mathcal{E}^{\vee})^{n}\to\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{B}^{\prime}

where each
\subset
is an open inclusion and each arrow is a vector bundle or product of projective bundles. Using Lemmas
3.3
,
3.5
and
3.7
, it thus suffices to show that
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
has the CKgP. But
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
is a quotient of an open subset of affine space by
\GL_{g+2}\times\GL_{g+4}\times\BSL_{2}
(see
[
13
, Proposition 4.2]
). Equivalently
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
is an open inside a vector bundle over
\BGL_{g+2}\times\BGL_{g+4}\times\BSL_{2}
. Now we are done because
\BGL_{d}
and
\BSL_{2}
have the CKgP (Lemma
3.8
).
∎

9.3.
Generators for the Chow ring

Our construction of
\mathcal{H}_{3,g,n}
gives rise to generators for its Chow ring.

Lemma 9.3
.

For
n\leq g+7
,
there is a surjection
A^{*}((\mathbb{P}\mathcal{E}^{\vee})^{n})\to A^{*}(\mathcal{H}_{3,g,n})
.

Proof.

By excision, we have a series of surjections

A^{*}((\mathbb{P}\mathcal{E}^{\vee})^{n})\rightarrow A^{*}(U)\cong A^{*}(\mathcal{Y}|_{U})\rightarrow A^{*}(\mathcal{H}_{3,g,n}).

The middle map is an isomorphism because
\mathcal{Y}|_{U}
is a vector bundle over
U
.
∎

Let
z_{i}:=\eta_{i}^{*}\gamma^{*}c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{P}}(1))
and
\zeta_{i}:=\eta_{i}^{*}c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}\mathcal{E}^{\vee}}(1))
.
The classes

z_{1},\ldots,z_{n},\zeta_{1},\ldots,z_{n}
generate
A^{*}((\mathbb{P}\mathcal{E}^{\vee})^{n})
as an
algebra
over
A^{*}(\mathcal{B}^{\prime})
.
The projective bundle theorem gives us relations

(9.3)

\zeta_{i}^{2}+c_{1}(\mathcal{E}^{\vee})\zeta_{i}+c_{2}(\mathcal{E}^{\vee})=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad z_{i}^{2}+c_{2}(\pi_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{P}}(1))=0.

Taking into account these relations, we see
A^{*}((\mathbb{P}\mathcal{E}^{\vee})^{n})
is generated
as a
module
over
A^{*}(\mathcal{B}^{\prime})
by monomials of the form

(9.4)

z_{1}^{a_{1}}z_{2}^{a_{2}}\cdots z_{n}^{a_{n}}\zeta_{1}^{b_{1}}\zeta_{2}^{b_{2}}\cdots\zeta_{n}^{b_{n}}\qquad a_{i},b_{i}\leq 1.

Combining this with Lemma
9.3
, we see that the classes in (
9.4
) generate
A^{*}(\mathcal{H}_{3,g,n})
as a module over the image of
A^{*}(\mathcal{B}^{\prime})\to A^{*}(\mathcal{H}_{3,g,n})
.

Meanwhile,
the map
\mathcal{H}_{3,g,n}\to\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
factors through
\mathcal{H}_{3,g,n}\to\mathcal{H}_{3,g}\to\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
. We proved in
[
11
, Theorem 1.1(1)]
that

is surjective and that for

, the image is generated by the pullback of
\kappa_{1}
along
\mathcal{H}_{3,g}\to\mathcal{M}_{g}
.
Thus, the image of
A^{*}(\mathcal{B}^{\prime})\to A^{*}(\mathcal{H}_{3,g,n})
is just the subring generated by
\widetilde{\kappa}_{1}
, defined as the pullback of
\kappa_{1}
along
\mathcal{H}_{3,g,n}\to\mathcal{M}_{g,n}\to\mathcal{M}_{g}
. (Note that the tautological rings of
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}
are closed under pullbacks, so
\widetilde{\kappa}_{1}
on
\mathcal{H}_{3,g,n}
is the pullback of a tautological class on
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}
.)
Hence, we obtain:

Lemma 9.4
.

Suppose
g\geq 4
and
n\leq g+7
.
The classes in (
9.4
) generate

A^{*}(\mathcal{H}_{3,g,n})
as a module over the subring generated by
\widetilde{\kappa}_{1}
.

Our task now is to relate
z_{i}
and
\zeta_{i}
to some more geometrically defined classes.

Lemma 9.5
.

We have
\psi_{i}=\zeta_{i}-2z_{i}
.

Proof.

It suffices to treat the one pointed case.
Because
\mathcal{H}_{3,g,1}
is the universal curve
f:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{H}_{3,g}
, we see that
\psi=c_{1}(\omega_{f})
. The result now follows from the first equation of
[
13
, Example 3.12]
.
∎

Using Lemmas
9.3
and
9.5
together with the relations in (
9.3
), we obtain:

Lemma 9.6
.

Suppose
g\geq 4
and
n\leq g+7
. As a module over the subring generated by
\widetilde{\kappa}_{1},\psi_{1},\ldots\psi_{n}
, we have
A^{*}(\mathcal{H}_{3,g,n})
is generated by the monomials
\zeta_{1}^{b_{1}}\zeta_{2}^{b_{2}}\cdots\zeta_{n}^{b_{n}}
with
b_{i}\leq 1
.

Thus, our goal is to show that these monomials
\zeta_{1}^{b_{1}}\zeta_{2}^{b_{2}}\cdots\zeta_{n}^{b_{n}}
push forward to tautological classes on
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}
. To do so, we use some geometrically defined cycles that represent these monomials.
Recall the divisors

R_{i}=\{(C,p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}):p_{i}\text{ is a ramification point of $C\to\mathbb{P}^{1}$}\}

introduced in Section
8
.
The first step is to identify the fundamental class of

in terms of our generators.

Lemma 9.7
.

We have
[R_{i}]=\zeta_{i}
.

Proof.

The map
\eta_{i}:\mathcal{H}_{3,g,n}\to\mathbb{P}\mathcal{E}^{\vee}
is the composition of the

\sigma_{i}:\mathcal{H}_{3,g,n}\to\mathcal{C}
with the universal embedding

. By construction of the embedding,
\iota^{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}\mathcal{E}^{\vee}}(1)\cong\omega_{\alpha}
, where
\alpha:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{P}
is the universal degree

map
[

, Theorem 2.1]
.
Thus,
\zeta_{i}=\eta_{i}^{*}c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}\mathcal{E}^{\vee}}(1))=\sigma_{i}^{*}c_{1}(\omega_{\alpha})
, so the result follows from (

).
∎

Let
\beta_{n}:\mathcal{H}_{3,g,n}\to\mathcal{M}_{g,n}\smallsetminus\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{2}
be the forgetful map. We now show that our module generators for

over

push forward to tautological classes.

Lemma 9.8
.

Let
i_{1},\ldots,i_{j}
be a subset of distinct indices in
1,\ldots,n
. Then
\beta_{n*}(\zeta_{i_{1}}\cdots\zeta_{i_{j}})
is tautological in
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}\smallsetminus\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{2}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
9.7
, we have

\zeta_{i_{1}}\cdots\zeta_{i_{j}}=[R_{i_{1}}]\cdots[R_{i_{j}}]
, so the result follows from Proposition
8.4
.
∎

We now conclude that the trigonal locus has our desired properties, when the number of marked points is sufficiently small.

Lemma 9.9
.

Suppose
g\geq 4
and
n\leq g+7
. Then
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{3}
has the CKgP and all classes supported on
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{3}
are tautological.

Proof.

By Theorem
6.1
, we know that when
n\leq 2g+6
all classes supported on
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{2}
are tautological and
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{2}
has the CKgP, so it remains to show the same for
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{3}\smallsetminus\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{2}
.
The map
\mathcal{H}_{3,g,n}\to\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{3}\smallsetminus\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{2}
is proper and surjective.
Combining Lemma
9.2
and
3.10
, we see that
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{3}\smallsetminus\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{2}
has the CKgP. Applying Lemma
3.4
, the union
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{3}=(\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{3}\smallsetminus\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{2})\cup\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{2}
has the CKgP.

Meanwhile, the map
\mathcal{H}_{3,g,n}\to\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{3}\smallsetminus\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{2}

induces a surjection on Chow groups with rational coefficients. In particular, every class supported on
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{3}\smallsetminus\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{2}\subset\mathcal{M}_{g,n}\smallsetminus\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{2}
lies in the image of
(\beta_{n})_{*}:A^{*}(\mathcal{H}_{3,g,n})\to A^{*}(\mathcal{M}_{g,n}\smallsetminus\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{2})
. Therefore, it suffices to show that the image of
(\beta_{n})_{*}
is tautological.
By Lemma
9.6
and the push-pull formula, we are reduced to showing that
(\beta_{n})_{*}(\zeta_{i_{1}}\cdots\zeta_{i_{j}})
is tautological for any subset of distinct indices
i_{1},\ldots,i_{j}
. This is proved in Lemma
9.8
.
∎

Remark 9.10
.

One can adapt the arguments of this section to work in the case
g=3
.
We would then have two separate proofs showing that classes supported on

are tautological, but they seem to work for different values of

using plane curves, gives

, and the second, Lemma

using the Hurwitz space, gives

on a non-hyperelliptic curve

comes from projecting from a point

in the canonical embedding. Therefore, the Hurwitz space
\mathcal{H}_{3,3}
really corresponds to
\mathcal{M}^{3}_{3,1}\smallsetminus\mathcal{M}^{2}_{3,1}
, which explains why the plane model appears to give one more marked point than the Hurwitz space model.

In genus
4
, we have
\mathcal{M}_{4,n}=\mathcal{M}_{4,n}^{3}
, so Lemma
9.9
gives the following.

Lemma 9.11
.

If
n\leq 11
, then
\mathcal{M}_{4,n}
has the CKgP and
A^{*}=R^{*}
.

10.
Tetragonal curves

In this section, we will attempt to show that classes supported on
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{4}
are tautological. We do not succeed to show this in full for arbitrary
g
. In fact, it is not true in general that all classes supported on
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{4}
are tautological
[
54
]
. But the argument will suffice for the low genus cases treated this paper.

10.1.
Strategy

The strategy is quite similar to the trigonal case, but with a few extra complications. We first define a subring of
A^{*}(\mathcal{H}_{4,g,n})
that deserves to be called the tautological ring. By relating the generators to geometrically defined classes we show that these tautological classes on
\mathcal{H}_{4,g,n}
push forward to tautological classes on
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}
.
Although
A^{*}(\mathcal{H}_{4,g,n})
is not generated by tautological classes in general,
we find a large open substack
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{4,g,n}\subset\mathcal{H}_{4,g,n}
such that
A^{*}(\mathcal{H}_{4,g,n}^{\prime})
is generated by tautological classes. The next task is to understand the complement of
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{4,g,n}\subset\mathcal{H}_{4,g,n}
. In general, there may be
non-tautological
classes coming from this complement. In particular, the bielliptic locus in genus
12
lies in the complement. For the genera of interest in this paper, however, we will be able to give a complete description of the complement and show that any Chow classes coming from it are indeed tautological. For example, in genus
6
, the complement consists of pointed hyperelliptic, trigonal, and plane quintic curves, but we know from previous sections that any classes supported on these loci are tautological.

\displaystyle\in S^{1}\cdot\zeta_{i}^{2}+S^{2}\cdot\zeta_{i}+S^{3}.

Now,
suppose we are given a monomial of the form in (
10.5
). Using Lemma
10.2
(1), we can replace each
z_{i}
with
\frac{1}{2}(\zeta_{i}-\psi_{i})
. This will give us a sum of terms of the form
S^{*}\cdot\zeta_{1}^{b_{1}^{\prime}}\cdots\zeta_{n}^{b_{n}^{\prime}}
where
b_{i}^{\prime}\leq b_{i}+a_{i}\leq 3
. We can then use (
10.7
) to eliminate any terms with a
\zeta_{i}^{3}
and obtain a sum of terms of the form
S^{*}\cdot\zeta_{1}^{b_{1}^{\prime\prime}}\cdots\zeta_{n}^{b_{n}^{\prime\prime}}
where
b_{i}^{\prime\prime}\leq 2
. Finally, we can use Lemma
10.2
(2) to eliminate each
\zeta_{i}^{2}

that appears, thus leaving a sum of terms of the form
S^{*}\cdot\zeta_{1}^{b_{1}^{\prime\prime\prime}}\cdots\zeta_{n}^{b_{n}^{\prime\prime\prime}}
where
b_{i}^{\prime\prime\prime}\leq 1
.
∎

We can now prove our desired result, which roughly says tautological classes on
\mathcal{H}_{4,g,n}
push forward to tautological classes on
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}
.

Lemma 10.5
.

Let

\beta_{n}^{\prime}:\mathcal{H}_{4,g,n}\smallsetminus\beta_{n}^{-1}(\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{3})\to\mathcal{M}_{g,n}\smallsetminus\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{3}
be as in (
8.2
). Suppose that
x\in A^{*}(\mathcal{H}_{4,g,n}\smallsetminus\beta_{n}^{-1}(\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{3}))
lies in the image of

A^{*}(\mathbb{P}\mathcal{E}^{\vee})^{n})\to A^{*}(\mathcal{H}_{4,g,n})\to A^{*}(\mathcal{H}_{4,g,n}\smallsetminus\beta_{n}^{-1}(\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{3})).

Then
\beta_{n*}^{\prime}x
is tautological on
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}\smallsetminus\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{3}
.

Proof.

Using the push-pull formula, it suffices to show that each of the generators in (
10.4
) pushes forward to a tautological class on
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}\smallsetminus\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{3}
. This is Proposition
8.4
.
∎

Our next task is to find a large open substack
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{4,g,n}\subset\mathcal{H}_{4,g,n}
whose Chow ring is generated by tautological classes.

10.3.
Construction of a certain substack

The tetragonal curves live inside
\mathbb{P}\mathcal{E}^{\vee}
as the vanishing loci of sections of the rank
2
bundle
\mathcal{W}:=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}\mathcal{E}^{\vee}}(2)\otimes\gamma^{*}\mathcal{F}^{\vee}
.
Set
\mathcal{U}=\gamma_{*}(\mathcal{W})=\Sym^{2}\mathcal{E}\otimes\mathcal{F}^{\vee}
. Let
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{B}
be the complement of the support of
R^{1}\pi_{*}\mathcal{U}
. By cohomology and base change,
\mathcal{X}:=\pi_{*}\mathcal{U}|_{\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}
is a vector bundle over
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
whose fibers correspond to equations of tetragonal curves. Define
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{4,g}\subset\mathcal{X}
to be the open substack representing smooth tetragonal curves.

We construct the moduli space
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{4,g,n}
similarly. Throughout this section, we shall work over
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
, so when we write
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathbb{P}\mathcal{E}^{\vee}
, we mean
\mathcal{P}|_{\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}
and
\mathbb{P}\mathcal{E}^{\vee}|_{\pi^{-1}\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}
. Let
\eta_{i}:(\mathbb{P}\mathcal{E}^{\vee})^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}\mathcal{E}^{\vee}
be the projection to the
i^{\text{th}}
factor, so we have a diagram:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\epsilon^{*}\mathcal{X}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\gamma^{*}\pi^{*}\mathcal{X}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{X}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\mathbb{P}\mathcal{E}^{\vee})^{n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{P}\mathcal{E}^{\vee}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{P}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{B}^{\prime}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\eta_{i}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\epsilon}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\gamma}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}

We have evaluation maps
in a rank
2
bundle

(10.8)

\gamma^{*}\pi^{*}\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{W}

on
\mathbb{P}\mathcal{E}^{\vee}
.
Pulling back to
(\mathbb{P}\mathcal{E}^{\vee})^{n}
, and taking a sum over the factors, we obtain an evaluation map on
(\mathbb{P}\mathcal{E}^{\vee})^{n}

(10.9)

\epsilon^{*}\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}^{*}\mathcal{W}.

We define
\mathcal{Y}\subset\epsilon^{*}\mathcal{X}
to be the preimage of the zero section under (
10.9
). The stack
\mathcal{Y}
parametrizes tuples
(E,F,C,p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
such that
C\subset\mathbb{P}E^{\vee}
is the vanishing locus of a section of
F^{\vee}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}E^{\vee}}(2)
which contains
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}
. There is an open inclusion
\mathcal{H}_{4,g,n}\subset\mathcal{Y}
corresponding to the open conditions that
C
is smooth and
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}
are distinct.

We want to know when (
10.9
) is surjective. By cohomology and base change, we reduce to the case of a single curve
C
, which is a complete intersection on
\mathbb{P}E^{\vee}
in class
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}E^{\vee}}(2)\otimes\gamma^{*}F^{\vee}
.

Lemma 10.6
.

Let
E=\mathcal{O}(e_{1})\oplus\mathcal{O}(e_{2})\oplus\mathcal{O}(e_{3})
and
F=\mathcal{O}(f_{1})\oplus\mathcal{O}(f_{2})
with
f_{1}\leq f_{2}
be vector bundles of degree
g+3
on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
.
Let
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{P}E^{\vee}
be a collection of
n\leq 4f_{1}-2g+1
distinct points. Suppose that there exists a smooth, irreducible curve
C
that is the zero locus of a section of
W:=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}E^{\vee}}(2)\otimes\gamma^{*}F^{\vee}
such that
\Gamma\subset C
. Then the evaluation map

H^{0}(\mathbb{P}E^{\vee},W)\rightarrow H^{0}(\Gamma,W|_{\Gamma})

is surjective.

Proof.

We factor the evaluation map through

H^{0}(\mathbb{P}E^{\vee},W)\rightarrow H^{0}(C,W|_{C})\rightarrow H^{0}(\Gamma,W|_{\Gamma}).

The first map is surjective because
H^{1}(\mathbb{P}E^{\vee},\mathcal{O})=0
.
By
[
15
, Theorem 2.1(2)]
or
[
11
, Example 3.12]
,
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}E^{\vee}}(1)
restricts to
\omega_{C}\otimes\gamma^{*}\omega_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}^{\vee}
.
Consider the exact sequence on
C

0\rightarrow W|_{C}(-\Gamma)\rightarrow W\rightarrow W|_{\Gamma}\rightarrow 0.

The map
H^{0}(C,W)\rightarrow H^{0}(\Gamma,W|_{\Gamma})
is surjective if
H^{1}(C,W|_{C}(-\Gamma))=0
. By Serre duality,

H^{1}(C,W|_{C}(-\Gamma))=H^{0}(C,\omega_{C}\otimes W|_{C}^{\vee}(\Gamma))^{\vee}.

For
i=1,2
, set
W_{f_{i}}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}E^{\vee}}(2)\otimes\gamma^{*}\mathcal{O}(f_{i})
, so that
W=W_{f_{1}}\oplus W_{f_{2}}
.
We have

\deg W_{f_{i}}|_{C}=\deg(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}E^{\vee}}(2)|_{C}\otimes\gamma^{*}\mathcal{O}(-f_{i})|_{C})=2(2g-2+4\cdot 2)-4f_{i}=4g+12-4f_{i}.

Then

H^{0}(C,\omega_{C}\otimes W|_{C}^{\vee}(\Gamma))=H^{0}(C,\omega_{C}\otimes W_{f_{1}}|_{C}^{\vee}(\Gamma))\oplus H^{0}(C,\omega_{C}\otimes W_{f_{2}}|_{C}^{\vee}(\Gamma)).

Thus, the required vanishing will occur if

0>\deg\omega_{C}\otimes W_{f_{i}}|_{C}^{\vee}(\Gamma)=2g-2-(4g+12-4f_{i})+n=4f_{i}+n-2g-14,

equivalently if,
n\leq 2g+13-4f_{i}
.
Since
f_{1}+f_{2}=g+3
and
f_{1}\leq f_{2}
, we can rewrite this as

n\leq 2g+13-4(g+3-f_{1})=4f_{1}-2g+1
. ∎

Remark 10.7
.

In the degree
3
case, our curve was defined by the vanishing of a section of a line bundle. In degree
4
, we have the zero locus of a section of a rank
2
vector bundle
W=F^{\vee}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}E^{\vee}}(2)
, and the splitting type of the rank
2
bundle
F
enters into the calculation of how many points we can mark and still know we are imposing independent conditions.
Observe also that
f_{1}\leq\frac{g+3}{2}
, so the number of marked points we can hope to get with this technique is bounded (independent of
g
) via
n\leq 4f_{1}-2g+1\leq 7
.

It turns out that the locus of covers for which
f_{1}
is small often corresponds curves with special geometry properties (see Lemmas
10.9
and
10.10
below). One might hope to access these special curves by other means and then focus on their complement, the locus of covers with
f_{1}\geq f
for some
f
. We therefore make the following definition.

Definition 10.8
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{4,g,n}^{f}\subset\mathcal{H}_{4,g,n}^{\prime}
be the union of Casnati–Ekedahl strata where
f_{1}\geq f
. Equivalently,
\mathcal{H}_{4,g,n}^{f}=\mathcal{H}_{4,g,n}^{\prime}\smallsetminus\Supp R^{1}\pi_{*}\mathcal{F}(f-1)
.

Recall that we write
\beta:\mathcal{H}_{4,g,n}\to\mathcal{M}_{g,n}
for the map to the moduli space of curves.

Lemma 10.9
.

We have
\mathcal{H}_{4,5,n}^{4}=\mathcal{H}_{4,5,n}\smallsetminus\beta^{-1}(\mathcal{M}_{5,n}^{3})
.

Proof.

By
[
12
, Section 4.3]
, the complement of
\mathcal{H}_{4,5}^{\prime}
is
\beta^{-1}(\mathcal{M}_{5}^{2})
, which is also the locus where
f_{1}=2
. Meanwhile, if
f_{1}=3
, then we claim
C
is trigonal. First, note that
\Psi_{2}
in
[
12
, Section 4.3]
is actually empty because
f_{2}>2e_{2}
, which forces such curves to be singular by
[
12
, Equation 4.7]
. Therefore, if
F
has
splitting type
(3,5)
then
E
has splitting type
(2,3,3)
. In this case,
the image of
\mathbb{P}E^{\vee}
in
\mathbb{P}^{4}
is a cone over
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
and
C
passes through the cone point because
p_{11}=0
and
\deg q_{11}=1
. Projecting from the cone point sends
C
to a curve of bidegree
(4,3)
on
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
, so we see that
C
is trigonal.
∎

There is also a nice geometric explanation of the locus where

is small in genus

. Let

be the preimage of the locus

Lemma 10.10
.

We have
\mathcal{H}_{4,6,n}^{4}=\mathcal{H}_{4,6,n}\smallsetminus\beta^{-1}(\mathcal{M}_{6,n}^{3}\cup PQ_{n})
.

Proof.

The allowed pairs of splitting types were determined in
[
12
, Section 4.3]
.
The claim follows immediately from Lemma 4.5 and Remark 4.7 of
[

]
.
∎

Thus, for our purposes in genus

and

at least, it will be enough to know information about

for

.
To gain a better understanding of

, define

to be the locus over which the evaluation map (

) is surjective. We know that

is open, but a priori it could be empty, and in fact

will be empty when

is too large.
However, when

,

shows that the image of

inside

Hence, we find that the inclusion

.

.

.

If

\mathcal{H}_{4,g,n}^{f}

Proof.

Considering (

10.4.
Generators for the Chow ring

Just like in the degree
3
case, our demonstration that
\mathcal{H}_{4,g,n}^{f}
has the CKgP also gives rise to natural generators for its Chow ring. In particular, they are all tautological in the sense discussed in Section
10.2

Lemma 10.12
.

For
n\leq 4f-2g+1
, the map
A^{*}((\mathbb{P}\mathcal{E}^{\vee})^{n})\to A^{*}(\mathcal{H}^{f}_{4,g,n})
is surjective.

Proof.

By excision, we have a series of surjections

A^{*}((\mathbb{P}\mathcal{E}^{\vee})^{n}\rightarrow A^{*}(U)\cong A^{*}(\mathcal{Y}|_{U})\rightarrow A^{*}(\mathcal{H}^{f}_{4,g,n}).

The middle map is an isomorphism because
\mathcal{Y}|_{U}
is a vector bundle over
U
.
∎

10.5.
Conclusion when
g=5

In genus
5
, we
use push forward along the proper map

\beta^{\prime}_{n}:\mathcal{H}_{4,5,n}^{4}=\mathcal{H}_{4,5,n}\smallsetminus\beta^{-1}(\mathcal{M}_{5,n}^{3})\to\mathcal{M}_{5,n}\smallsetminus\mathcal{M}_{5,n}^{3}.

When
f=4
, we have

.

Lemma 10.13
.

If
n\leq 7
, then
\mathcal{M}_{5,n}
has the CKgP and

.

Proof.

We already know from Lemma

, that

has the CKgP and that all classes supported on it are tautological. It thus remains to show that
\mathcal{M}_{5,n}\smallsetminus\mathcal{M}_{5,n}^{3}
has the CKgP and

.

We have a proper surjective map

.
We also know that
\beta_{n*}^{\prime}
induces a surjection on Chow groups, so it suffices to show that the image of
\beta_{n*}^{\prime}
is tautological.
The result now follows from Lemmas
10.12
and
10.5
.
∎
