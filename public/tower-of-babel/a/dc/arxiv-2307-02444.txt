Abstract
. Let k k be a field and let 𝒞 \mathcal{C} be a small category. A k k -linear representation of 𝒞 \mathcal{C} , or a k ​ 𝒞 k\mathcal{C} -module, is a functor from 𝒞 \mathcal{C} to the category of finite dimensional vector spaces over k k . Unsurprisingly, it turns out that when the category 𝒞 \mathcal{C} is more general than a linear order, then its representation type is generally infinite and in most cases wild. Hence the task of understanding such representations in terms of their indecomposable factors becomes difficult at best, and impossible in general. This paper offers a new set of ideas designed to enable studying modules locally. Specifically, inspired by work in discrete calculus on graphs, we set the foundations for a calculus type analysis of k ​ 𝒞 k\mathcal{C} -modules, under some restrictions on the category 𝒞 \mathcal{C} . As a starting point, for a k ​ 𝒞 k\mathcal{C} -module M M we define its gradient gradient ∇ [ M ] \nabla[M] as a virtual module in the appropriate Grothendieck group. Pushing the analogy with ordinary differential calculus and discrete calculus on graphs, we define left and right divergence via the appropriate left and right Kan extensions and two bilinear pairings on modules and study their properties, specifically with respect to adjointness relations between the gradient and the left and right divergence. The left and right divergence are shown to be rather easily computable in favourable cases. Having set the scene, we concentrate specifically on the case where the category 𝒞 \mathcal{C} is a finite poset. Our main result is a necessary and sufficient condition for the gradient of a module M M to vanish under certain hypotheses on the poset. We next investigate implications for two modules whose gradients are equal. Finally we consider the resulting left and right Laplacians, namely the compositions of the divergence with the gradient, and study an example of the relationship between the vanishing of the Laplacians and the gradient.



Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category and let
\mathcal{A}
be an abelian category. The category
\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{C}}
of functors
M\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{A}
and natural transformations between them is again an abelian category. When
\mathcal{A}
is the category
{\mathrm{Vect}_{k}}
of finite dimensional vector spaces over a field
k
, objects of
\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{C}}
are frequently referred to as
k
-linear representations of
\mathcal{C}
. When
\mathcal{C}
has finitely many objects the representations can be identified as
k\mathcal{C}
-modules
, i.e. modules over the category algebra. In this article we shall be interested in the category
k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
of such modules. A special case of interest, motivated in part by its prevalence in topological data analysis, is where
\mathcal{C}
is a finite category, i.e. all its morphisms form a finite set, or even more specifically, a finite poset.

Drozd trichotomy theorem
[
5
]
states that if
\mathcal{C}
is a finite category generated by an acyclic quiver and
k
is an algebraically closed field, then there are exactly three possibilities: either
k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
contains finitely many isomorphism types of indecomposable modules (finite representation type), or it contains an infinite family of indecomposable modules that can be parametrised by an
n
-parameter family (tame representation type), or there is an
n
-parameter family of indecomposable modules for arbitrarily large
n
in
k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
(wild representation type). Arbitrary finite posets are typically of infinite (tame or wild) representation type, which makes classification by decomposition into indecomposable summands difficult in the tame case and impossible in the wild case.

In this article we introduce a new approach to the study of
k\mathcal{C}
-modules. Instead of attempting to understand modules globally as a sum of indecomposable modules, or by taking projective resolutions, we propose a
calculus of
k\mathcal{C}
-modules
. That is, a very general methodology that enables one to extract local information about any given module. The analogy with ordinary calculus is clear: in exploring properties of a nice real valued function of several variables one typically employs standard differential techniques, which allow studying the function locally. The notions of gradient, divergence and Laplacian come to mind in this context. Inspired by these ideas, there is a discrete version of multivariable calculus for weighted directed graphs
[
11
]
. Our treatment of
k\mathcal{C}
-module theory puts the ideas of discrete calculus on graphs into a categorical framework. In particular, under certain restrictions, we shall define the notion of a gradient for isomorphism classes of
k\mathcal{C}
-modules, as well as concepts corresponding to the divergence and the Laplacian. This paper aims to establish the basic concepts and study some of their basic properties.

This paper is motivated in part by the ever growing interest within the applied algebraic topology community in
generalised persistence modules
, which can be thought of as
k\mathcal{C}
-modules where
\mathcal{C}
is typically a poset (possibly with nontrivial topology on its object set). Applications of the formalism presented here will be considered in subsequent work.

1.
Statement of Results

To state our main results some preparation is required. We provide a brief description here, and more details in Section
2
.
Calculus on weighted directed graphs
is a discrete calculus for functions whose domain is the vertex set of a finite graph with weighted directed edges
[
11
]
. In this context the gradient is an operation which takes functions on the vertex set of a graph to functions on its edge set. We shall work in general with
quivers
, also known as
directed multidigraphs
.
These are graphs with a vertex set
V
and a set valued function
E
on
V\times V
, which indicates directed edges between ordered pairs of vertices. We will assume throughout that quivers are
loop free
, namely that for each
v\in V
, the set
E(v,v)
is empty.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
be a quiver. If
f
is a function on
V
with values in some additive group
A
, then the graph theoretic gradient is defined to be a function from
E
to
A
, which takes an edge to the difference between the value at its target vertex and the value at its source vertex
[
11
]
. We wish to categorify this idea, and so we work with categories that are generated, in the appropriate sense, by a quiver. To proceed, we need two extra ingredients.

First, we want the gradient of a
k\mathcal{C}
-module to be an object of the same type, namely a
k\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
-module, for some category
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
related to
\mathcal{C}
. To achieve this we associate with a quiver
\mathcal{G}
another quiver
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}
, whose vertices are the edges of
\mathcal{G}
. Given a quiver
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
, its
line digraph
is a digraph
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}=(\widehat{V},\widehat{E})
, with
\widehat{V}=E
, and such that edges in
\widehat{E}
are compositions of two consecutive directed edges in
\mathcal{G}
. We will show that
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}
is always a digraph (rather than a general quiver, namely that each pair of vertices in
\widehat{V}
are connected by at most a single edge. See Section
2.2
). Thus the graph theoretic gradient can be thought of as an operator that takes functions on the vertices of
\mathcal{G}
to functions on the vertices of
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}
.

The second ingredient we need is a structure that will allow us to take the difference of modules. This motivates the use of the Grothendieck ring of isomorphism classes of modules, where the sum and product operations are given by direct sum and tensor product (over the field
k
), respectively.

If
\mathcal{G}
is a generating quiver for a small category
\mathcal{C}
, namely the category
\mathcal{C}
is a quotient category of the path category
P(\mathcal{G})
(See Section
2
for details), then our gradient ought to be an operation that takes a module
M\in k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
to a difference of two modules in
k\widehat{\mathcal{C}}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
, where
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
is a category that is obtained from
\mathcal{C}
using the line digraph
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}
associated to
\mathcal{G}
, and is referred to as a
line category associated to
\mathcal{C}
.
The category
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
comes with two obvious functors
\phi,\beta\colon\widehat{\mathcal{C}}\to\mathcal{C}
that associate with an object of
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
its target and source object in
\mathcal{C}
, respectively.
We define the gradient as the difference
\nabla\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\phi^{*}-\beta^{*}
of two natural homomorphisms
\phi^{*},\beta^{*}\colon\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{C})\to\EuR{Gr}(k\widehat{\mathcal{C}})
between the Grothendieck rings, (the
front
and
back
morphisms) that captures the variation of a module along each
indecomposable
morphism in
\mathcal{C}
. The indecomposable morphisms in
\mathcal{C}
can be thought of as discrete analogs of infinitesimally small moves in a metric space. We show in Example
3.6
that our gradient, interpreted appropriately, is a generalisation of the graph theoretic gradient.

Having defined a gradient for
k\mathcal{C}
-modules we embark on studying its behaviour. The basic properties one expects from a gradient follow rather easily from the definition. In particular we show that
\nabla
is a well defined group homomorphism, that its kernel contains all
locally constant
modules (i.e., functors that take each morphism in
\mathcal{C}
to an isomorphism of vector spaces), and that it satisfies a form of a Leibniz rule. We also show that
\nabla
is natural with respect to restrictions (Proposition
3.7
).

Having prepared the general background, we restrict attention to the main object of study - modules over posets. Finite posets are an important family of small categories that is of general interest, as well as being central to generalised persistence module theory. While the results stated below do not always require this restriction, some of the problems that arise in the general case are avoidable when one deals with modules over posets. The more general case will be studied in future works. Thus throughout the introduction and from Section
5
and on, we restrict attention to the case where the small category under consideration is a finite poset. One immediate advantage is that a finite poset
\mathcal{P}
is generated by a canonical digraph that is its
Hasse diagram
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
- the digraph consisting of all indecomposable morphisms in
\mathcal{P}
.

By analogy to ordinary calculus, an obvious question is whether a vanishing gradient of a module
M
implies that it is locally constant. The answer turns out to be quite subtle. A
directed tree
is a quiver
\mathcal{T}
with the property that between any two distinct vertices
a
and
b
in
\mathcal{T}
there is at most one directed path. We say that a poset
\mathcal{P}
is
line connected
if the line digraph of its Hasse diagram is connected. By convention when we refer to a relation
x\leq y
in a poset
\mathcal{P}
as indecomposable, we exclude the possibility
x=y
. The following theorem is our first main result.

Theorem A

(Thm.
5.3
)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a finite poset, and let
\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
be a line connected subgraph that is a tree. Let
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
denote the sub-poset generated by
\mathcal{T}
. Let
M\in k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
be a module, and let
M_{\mathcal{T}}
denote the restriction of
M
to
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}}
. Assume that
\nabla[M_{\mathcal{T}}]=0
in
\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}})
. Then the following statements hold.

(1)

For any objects
u,v\in\mathrm{Obj}(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}})
, there is an isomorphism

\alpha_{u,v}\colon M(u)\to M(v),

such that
\alpha_{u,u}=1_{M(u)}
and
\alpha_{v,w}\alpha_{u,v}=\alpha_{u,w}
.

(2)

For every pair of indecomposable relations
u\leq w
and
s\leq t
in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}}
,

\alpha_{w,t}\circ M(u\leq w)=M(s\leq t)\circ\alpha_{u,s}.

(3)

M_{\mathcal{T}}
is locally constant if and only if
M(u<v)
is an isomorphism for some non-identity indecomposable relation
u<v
in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}}
.

Furthermore, if
M\in k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
is a module such that for any indecomposable relation
u\leq v
in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}}
there exists an isomorphism
\alpha_{u,v}\colon M(u)\to M(v)
that satisfy Conditions (
1
) and (
2
), then
\nabla[M_{\mathcal{T}}]=0
.

The restriction to posets generated by line connected trees is essential. If
\mathcal{Q}
is a poset such that
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Q}}
is not a tree, it is rather easy to find examples of modules in
k\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
that are not the gradient of any
k\mathcal{Q}
-module. Thus one may think of such modules, in some sense, as
not integrable
. In particular, in this situation it is possible to construct
k\mathcal{Q}
-modules with a vanishing gradient that do not satisfy Conditions (
1
) and (
2
) of Theorem
A
. This stands in sharp contrast to ordinary calculus, where any differentiable function whose gradient vanishes on a domain is constant in the interior of that domain, and any real valued continuous function on a reasonable domain is integrable there.

On the other hand, if
\mathcal{P}
is a general finite poset, and
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
is a sub-poset whose Hasse diagram is a line connected tree, then Theorem
A
gives information for any module
M\in k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
on its restriction to
\mathcal{Q}
. This fits well with the idea of a local approach to studying modules. Notice that if
\mathcal{P}
is line connected, then one can show that
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
contains a line-connected maximal tree
\mathcal{T}
. In that case statements (
1
) – (
3
) of Theorem
A
apply to all objects of
\mathcal{P}
.

We next examine the implication for a pair of
k\mathcal{P}
-modules of having isomorphic gradients. A typical element in the Grothendieck ring
\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{P})
, which is ordinarily referred to as a
virtual module
, is a difference of two equivalence classes of genuine modules. Hence understanding modules of equal gradient is equivalent to gaining information about the kernel of the gradient homomorphism. As one can expect, in view of Theorem
A
, there are virtual modules of a vanishing gradient which are not locally constant (See Example
5.8
). This in particular implies that the gradient is not a complete invariant, and so it makes sense to compare it to another incomplete invariant - the rank invariant.

For
M\in k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
, define the
rank invariant

\operatorname{rk}\nolimits(M)\colon\operatorname{Mor}(\mathcal{P})\to\mathbb{N}
to be the function that takes a relation
x\leq y
to the rank of the homomorphism
M(x\leq y)
. The rank invariant clearly extends by additivity to a group homomorphism
\operatorname{rk}\nolimits\colon\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{P})\to\mathbb{Z}
, since it depends only on the isomorphism type of a module. Notice also that our definition of the rank invariant includes the so called
Hilbert function
, which appears as the rank of
M
applied to identity morphisms. It is well known that the rank invariant is a complete invariant for modules over
(\mathbb{R},\leq)
or any other linear order, but not for more general posets
[
3
]
. Our next theorem relates the gradient to the rank invariant.

Theorem B

(Thm.
5.11
)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a finite poset. Let
[X]=[M]-[N]\in\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{P})
be any element.

(1)

Assume that
\nabla[X]=0
. Then
\operatorname{rk}\nolimits[X](u_{0}<v_{0})=\operatorname{rk}\nolimits[X](u_{1}<v_{1})
for any pair of comparable objects
(u_{0},u_{1})<(v_{0},v_{1})
in
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
.

Assume in addition that
\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
is a line connected tree, and let
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
be the sub-poset generated by
\mathcal{T}
and let
[X_{\mathcal{T}}]
denote the restriction of
[X]
to
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}}
. If
[X]
has a vanishing gradient on
\mathcal{T}
, then

(2)

\operatorname{rk}\nolimits[X_{\mathcal{T}}]
is constant on all identity morphisms in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}}
, and

(3)

\operatorname{rk}\nolimits[X_{\mathcal{T}}]
is constant on all non-identity indecomposable relations in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}}
, namely for any pair
u_{0}<u_{1}
, and
v_{0}<v_{1}
of such relations, one has
\operatorname{rk}\nolimits[X](u_{0}<u_{1})=\operatorname{rk}\nolimits[X](v_{0}<v_{1})
.

Example
5.12
shows that two modules may have equal gradients, but different rank invariants. On the other hand, Example
5.17
gives infinitely many non-isomorphic modules with equal rank invariants and different gradients. Thus the gradient and the rank invariant are complementary in the data they detect.

In discrete calculus for finite weighted digraphs one considers real valued functions on vertices or edges as elements in finite dimensional real vector spaces, and as such one has the ordinary inner product defined on the spaces of vertex and edge functions,
\langle-,-\rangle_{V}
and
\langle-,-\rangle_{E}
respectively. This pairing allows one to define divergence and Laplacian for digraphs
[
11
]
. The adjoint operator
\nabla^{*}
with respect to the inner product, also referred to as the
divergence
, is then defined by the requirement that the relation

\langle\nabla^{*}(f),g\rangle_{V}=\langle f,\nabla(g)\rangle_{E}

holds.

In our context there is no obvious analog of an inner product on a real vector space, and hence defining an operator analogous to divergence is not straightforward. We propose two (related) substitutes. For modules
M,N\in k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
define two pairings

\langle[M],[N]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\dim_{k}(\mathrm{Hom}_{k\mathcal{P}}(M,N)),\quad\text{and}\quad\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([M],[N])\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\chi(\mathrm{Ext}^{*}_{k\mathcal{P}}(M,N)),

which we refer to as the
Hom pairing
and the
Euler pairing
, respectively. Extend the definition to pairings on
\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{P})
by additivity. Similarly define the corresponding pairings on
\EuR{Gr}(k\widehat{\mathcal{P}})
. Since
\mathcal{P}
is assumed to be a finite poset and we assume modules in
k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
to be finitely generated,
\mathrm{Ext}^{i}_{k\mathcal{P}}(M,N)
is a finite dimensional vector space for all
i
and it vanishes for
i
sufficiently large (See Lemma
4.3
). Hence the Euler pairing is well defined. Going back to the motivating example of graph theory we show in Example
4.6
that, with a suitable interpretation of functions on graphs as modules in an appropriate category, the two pairings coincide with each other, and are directly analogous to the ordinary inner product. In general, of course, neither pairing is symmetric.

We next offer our analog of the divergence. Considering the Hom pairing as an analog of an inner product, the left and right Kan extensions offer themselves as a natural way of constructing a left adjoint
\nabla^{*}
and a right adjoint
\nabla_{*}
of the gradient. Thus for
N\in k\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
, we define
left divergence
\nabla^{*}
and right divergence
\nabla_{*}
by

\nabla^{*}[N]\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}[L_{\phi}(N)]-[L_{\beta}(N)],\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla_{*}[N]=[R_{\phi}(N)]-[R_{\beta}(N)],

where
L_{\phi}(N)
and
L_{\beta}(N)
are the left Kan extensions, and
R_{\phi}(N)
and
R_{\beta}(N)
are the right Kan extensions, in both cases along
\phi
and
\beta
respectively.
Example
3.9
demonstrates that with the right categorical setup for ordinary weighted digraphs, the left and right divergence operators coincide with each other and amount to the ordinary definition of the graph theoretic divergence. With this setting the following relations

\langle\nabla[X],[Y]\rangle_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}=\langle[X],\nabla_{*}[Y]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}\quad\text{and}\quad\langle\nabla^{*}[X],[Y]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}=\langle[X],\nabla[Y]\rangle_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}

are straightforward, and can in fact be used as a definition of the left and right divergence.

While the Euler pairing offers some better properties, the adjointness relations above do not hold for it in general. We shall elaborate on this point in Section
4
.

Computing the left and right divergence could be quite involved for a general poset. However, here too we recall our local approach and consider the case where the Hasse diagram for the poset in question is a tree. In that case the next theorem offers a rather easy calculation.

Theorem C

(Thm.
7.9
)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a finite poset whose Hasse diagram is a tree, and let
N\in k\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
. For any object
y\in\mathcal{P}
let
\mathcal{N}_{y}
denote the neighbourhood of
y
in
\mathcal{P}
, and let
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{y}
be the associated line poset. Then the following statements hold.

(1)

L_{\phi}(N)(y)\cong\bigoplus_{(u,y)\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}N(u,y),\quad{and}\quad L_{\beta}(N)(y)\cong\mathop{\textup{colim}}\limits_{\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{y}}N|_{\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{y}}
.

(2)

R_{\phi}(N)(y)\cong\mathop{\textup{lim}}\limits_{\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{y}}N|_{\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{y}},\quad\text{and}\quad R_{\beta}(N)(y)\cong\bigoplus_{(y,v)\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}N(y,v)
.

If the Hasse diagram of
\mathcal{P}
is a tree, thus in particular an acyclic quiver, then
\mathrm{Ext}^{i}_{k\mathcal{P}}(M,N)
vanishes for
i>1

[
4
, Theorem 2.3.2]
, and the Euler pairing can be computed as an ordinary inner product of dimension vectors
[
4
, Proposition 2.5.2]
(See Lemma
4.5
). For more general posets Proposition
6.3
uses projective (or injective) resolutions of a module and standard homological algebra to give an easy relation between the two types of pairings.

Composing the gradient with the left and the right divergence, we obtain
left and right Laplacians
\Delta^{0}
and
\Delta_{0}
, respectively. Borrowing terminology from analysis, we define
left and right harmonic modules
, namely such that their corresponding Laplacians vanish. By analogy to graph theory, where the left and right Laplacians coincide, one may wonder whether left and/or right harmonic modules also have a vanishing gradient. The answer to this question turns out to be much more subtle, but in the case where the poset in question is linear this turns out to be the case.

Proposition D

(Prop.
7.12
)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be the poset with objects
0\leq i\leq n
with the usual order relation. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

\nabla[M]=0
.

(2)

\Delta^{0}[M]=0
.

(3)

\Delta_{0}[M]=0
.

Laplacians, higher dimensional generalisation and a possible setup for Hodge theory for
k\mathcal{C}
-modules will be studied in future work.

The paper is organised as follows. Section
2
contains all the technical background material we use throughout the paper. In Section
3
we prepare the general setup for the construction of the gradient and the divergence in the context of module categories. In Section
4
we study some properties of the Hom pairing and the Euler pairing in the context of modules over category algebras. Section
5
is dedicated to the study of the gradient of virtual modules over finite posets and the proof of Theorems
A
and
B
. In Section
6
we specialise the Hom and Euler pairings for modules over posets. We compute the pairings in some interesting cases and study the relationships between them. Section
7
is dedicated to the (left and right) divergence, some adjointness relations with the gradient and the proof of Theorem
C
. We then define the corresponding Laplacians, and prove Proposition
D
.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we record the definitions, notation and all preliminary material that will be used throughout the paper.

2.1.
k\mathcal{C}
-Modules

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category and let
\mathcal{A}
be an abelian category. Then the functor category
\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{C}}
whose objects are functors from
\mathcal{C}
to
\mathcal{A}
and whose morphisms are natural transformations is also an abelian category. If
\Phi\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
is a functor then pre-composition with
\Phi
induces a functor

\Phi^{*}\colon\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{D}}\to\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{C}}.

This functor, which is sometimes referred to as the
restriction along
\Phi
, will be used in our definition of the gradient in Section
3
(Definition
3.5
). If
\mathcal{A}
is complete and cocomplete, then the restriction
\Phi^{*}
generally has left and right adjoints given by the left and right Kan extensions, respectively (Section
2.4
). These will be used in Section
3
to define a left and a right divergence (Definition
3.8
). A good reference for the general theory of functor categories is
[
8
]
.

Fix a field
k
. Unless otherwise specified we will not assume that
k
is algebraically closed. Let
{\mathrm{Vect}_{k}}
be the category of
finite dimensional
vector spaces over
k
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category. A
k\mathcal{C}
-module
is a functor
M\colon\mathcal{C}\to{\mathrm{Vect}_{k}}
. Let
k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
denote the category of
k\mathcal{C}
-modules and natural transformations between them.

When the category
\mathcal{C}
has finitely many objects the category
k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
has an equivalent description, which is useful for some purposes.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
k
be a field and let
\mathcal{C}
have finitely many objects. The
category algebra

k\mathcal{C}
is the unital algebra generated as a
k
-vector space by all morphisms
x\to y
in
\mathcal{C}
(including identities). Two morphisms multiply by composition, and non-composable morphisms multiply to 0
[
16
]
. The unit is the
k
-algebra map
\eta\colon k\to k\mathcal{C}
that sends
1\in k
to the element
\mathbf{1}\in k\mathcal{C}
, that is the sum over all objects in
\mathcal{C}
of the identity morphisms
1_{x}\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}x\to x
.

Clearly, finiteness is required in this definition only for the unit element to make sense. The following theorem due to Mitchell allows one to alternate between functor categories and categories of modules in the usual sense, when the category in question is finite.

Theorem 2.3

(
[
13
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a category with finitely many objects and let
k
be a field. The category of modules over the category algebra
k\mathcal{C}
and
k\mathcal{C}
-linear homomorphisms is equivalent to the category
k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
of functors
M\colon\mathcal{C}\to{\mathrm{Vect}_{k}}
and natural transformations between them.

The equivalence in Mitchell’s theorem is given as follows. If
M\colon\mathcal{C}\to{\mathrm{Vect}_{k}}
is a functor, let

\mathbf{M}\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\bigoplus_{x\in\mathrm{Obj}(\mathcal{C})}M(x).

The (left) action of
k\mathcal{C}
on
\mathbf{M}
is determined by
the way in which each morphism
\varphi\colon y\to z
in
\mathcal{C}
acts on a typical element of
\mathbf{M}
. Firstly, for any
m\in M(y)
we define

\varphi\cdot m=M(\varphi)(m)\text{ if }\varphi\in\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(y,z)\text{ for some }z\in\mathcal{C}.

Otherwise we define
\varphi\cdot m=0
. Then extending this operation linearly to any
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbf{M}
and
f\in k\mathcal{C}
gives the desired
k\mathcal{C}
-module structure.

Conversely, if
\mathbf{N}
is a left module over the category algebra
k\mathcal{C}
, let
N\colon\mathcal{C}\to{\mathrm{Vect}_{k}}
be the functor that takes an object
x\in\mathcal{C}
to
N(x)\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}1_{x}\cdot\mathbf{N}
and a morphism
\varphi\colon y\to z
to the homomorphism
N(\varphi)
that maps
1_{y}\cdot\mathbf{a}\in 1_{y}\cdot\mathbf{N}
to
\varphi\cdot\mathbf{a}\in 1_{z}\cdot\mathbf{N}
.

The following general terminology is rather standard and will become useful in our analysis.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category. We say that a module
M\in k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
is

•

locally constant
if for every morphism
\varphi
in
\mathcal{C}
the induced homomorphism
M(\varphi)
is an isomorphism,

•

virtually trivial
if for every non-identity morphism
\varphi
in
\mathcal{C}
the induced homomorphism
M(\varphi)
is trivial.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category and let
M,N\in k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
be virtually trivial modules. Let
\mathrm{Obj}_{\mathcal{C}}
denote the discrete category (only identity morphisms) with objects set
\mathrm{Obj}(\mathcal{C})
, and let
j_{\mathcal{C}}\colon\mathrm{Obj}_{\mathcal{C}}\to\mathcal{C}
denote the inclusion. Then there is a natural isomorphism of groups

J\colon\mathrm{Hom}_{k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}}(M,N)\to\mathrm{Hom}_{k\mathrm{Obj}_{\mathcal{C}}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}}(j_{\mathcal{C}}^{*}(M),j_{\mathcal{C}}^{*}(N)),

where on both sides
\mathrm{Hom}
denotes the group of natural transformations between the respective modules, and
j_{\mathcal{C}}^{*}
denotes pre-composition with
j_{\mathcal{C}}
.

Proof.

Since
M
and
N
are virtually trivial, a natural transformation from
M
to
N
amounts exactly to a homomorphism of vector spaces
M(x)\to N(x)
for each
x\in\mathrm{Obj}(\mathcal{C})
. Thus
J
is an isomorphism of sets. Since the functor categories are abelian and
j^{*}_{\mathcal{C}}
is additive, the lemma follows.
∎

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category and let
M,N\in k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
be virtually trivial modules. Then
M\cong N
if and only if
M(x)\cong N(x)
for each object
x\in\mathrm{Obj}(\mathcal{C})
.

2.2.
Quivers and their line digraphs

A central idea in our work is to define a gradient on
k\mathcal{C}
-modules that yields a virtual
k\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
-module (See Section
2.3
), where
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
is a certain category associated to
\mathcal{C}
and a certain generating digraph. This is where the classical graph theoretic construction of the line digraph becomes useful. We start by recalling some basic concepts.

Definition 2.7
.

A
quiver
is a pair
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
, where
V
is a vertex set,
and
E
is a set valued function on
V\times V
, that associates with each pair of vertices
(u,v)\in V\times V
a (possibly empty) finite set
E(u,v)
.
The union of all values of
E
is referred to as the
edge set
of
\mathcal{G}
. For each pair
(u,v)\in V\times V
, the set
E(u,v)
is understood graphically as the set of directed edges from
u
to
v
.
A quiver comes equipped with natural
front
and
back
functions
\phi,\beta\colon E\to V
, defined on
e\in E(u,v)
by
\phi(e)=v
and
\beta(e)=u
.

A
directed graph
, or a
digraph
,
\mathcal{G}
is a a quiver where the values of
E
are either empty or a singleton.

Definition
2.7
allows reciprocal connections between any pair of vertices in a quiver, but in a digraph at most one edge is allowed in a given direction from one vertex to another. When
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
is a digraph, we will consider its edge set simply as a subset
E\subseteq V\times V
and write
e=(u,v)
to denote that
e
is an edge from
u
to
v
. (Clearly this only makes sense for digraphs, and not for general quivers).
A quiver is said to be
acyclic
if there is no directed path that starts and ends at the same vertex (in particular, no reciprocal connections are allowed). While acyclicity is not assumed throughout, in the context of this work we do assume that all quivers are
loop-free
, namely that the restriction of
E
to the diagonal in
V\times V
obtains empty values.

Definition 2.8
.

Given a quiver
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
, the associated
line digraph

\widehat{\mathcal{G}}=(\widehat{V},\widehat{E})
is the digraph with vertices
\widehat{V}=E
. If
e\in E(u,v)
and
e^{\prime}\in E(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})
, then
(e,e^{\prime})
is a directed edge
e\to e^{\prime}
in
\widehat{E}
whenever
v=u^{\prime}
. If
\mathcal{G}
is a digraph, then the directed edges in
\widehat{E}
are denoted by ordered triples
(u,v,z)
, where
(u,v)
and
(v,z)
are edges in
E
.

Notice also that the terminology
line digraph
is justified because
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}
is always a digraph (rather than a general quiver)
. Indeed, let
e\in E(u,v)
and
e^{\prime}\in E(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})
be vertices in
\widehat{V}
. Then there is at most one edge in
\widehat{E}
from
e
to
e^{\prime}
, which exists if and only if
v=u^{\prime}
. Thus
\widehat{G}
has no multiple edges in the same direction between the same pair of vertices. For instance, if
\mathcal{G}
has three vertices
u,v,w
with
n
directed edges from
u
to
v
and
k
directed edges from
v
to
w
, then
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}
is an
n
to
k
directed bipartite graph.

The line digraph construction is clearly functorial with respect to inclusions. Namely, if
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
is an inclusion of quivers, then
\widehat{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}\subseteq\widehat{\mathcal{G}}
.

A quiver
\mathcal{G}
is said to be
connected
if, ignoring edge direction and collapsing multiple and bidirectional edges between the same pair of vertices into a single undirected edge, one obtains a connected graph. The line digraph associated to a quiver
\mathcal{G}
is not generally connected. But if
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}
is a connected line digraph of some quiver
\mathcal{G}
, then
\mathcal{G}
is connected. The following concept of connectivity is useful for our purposes.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a quiver with a line digraph
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}
. A sub-quiver
\mathcal{G}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
is said to be a
line component of
\mathcal{G}
if there exists a connected component
\widehat{\mathcal{G}_{0}}\subseteq\widehat{\mathcal{G}}
such that
\widehat{\mathcal{G}_{0}}
is the line digraph associated to
\mathcal{G}_{0}
. A quiver
\mathcal{G}
is said to be
line connected
if
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}
is connected, or equivalently if the only line component of
\mathcal{G}
is
\mathcal{G}
itself.

2.3.
The Grothendieck ring

As is the case for any category of modules, many questions about
k\mathcal{C}
-modules can be reduced to questions about isomorphism classes. In this context the concept of the Grothendieck ring is very useful.

Let
k
be a field, let
A
be a unital
k
-algebra and let
A\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
denote the category of finitely generated left
A
-modules. Then
A\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
is a monoid with operation given by direct sum, and thus the set of isomorphism classes of
A
-modules forms a commutative monoid where

(1)

[M]+[N]\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}[M\oplus N].

The Grothendieck group
\EuR{Gr}(A)
of finitely generated
A
-modules is the group completion of this monoid. Namely, it is the quotient of the free abelian group generated by isomorphism classes of
A
-modules, subject to the relation (
1
). Thus any element
[X]\in\EuR{Gr}(A)
can be written uniquely as a difference
[X]=[M]-[N]
, where
M,N\in A\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
. In particular, an equation of the form
[M]-[N]=[U]-[V]
in
\EuR{Gr}(A)
where
M,N,U,V\in A\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
should be interpreted as
M\oplus V\cong N\oplus U
. We refer to elements of
\EuR{Gr}(A)
as
virtual modules
.

Tensor product over
k
, with the (left) diagonal action of
A
on the factors, turns
\EuR{Gr}(A)
into a ring, known as the Grothendieck ring, where
[M]\cdot[N]=[M\otimes N]
. This ring does not in general have a unit element. If
f^{*}\colon B\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}\to A\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
is a functor that preserves tensor products, then
f^{*}
induces a ring homomorphism

f^{*}\colon\EuR{Gr}(B)\to\EuR{Gr}(A).

An important example of this construction is given by the Grothendieck ring
\EuR{Gr}(k)
of finite dimensional vector spaces over
k
, which is
isomorphic to the ring of integers
\mathbb{Z}
. A much more interesting family of examples that is a subject of extensive study arises in group representation theory. If
G
is a group and
kG
its group algebra, then
\EuR{Gr}(kG)
is the Grothendieck ring of all virtual linear representations of the group
G
over
k
.

Definition 2.10
.

Let
A
be a unital
k
-algebra. Define the
reduced Grothendieck ring

\EuR{Gr}_{e}(A)
to be the quotient of the Grothendieck ring
\EuR{Gr}(A)
, by an additional family of relations:

[M]=[M^{\prime}]+[M^{\prime\prime}]

if there is a short exact sequence of
A
-modules
0\to M^{\prime}\to M\to M^{\prime\prime}\to 0
.

The reduced Grothendieck ring
\EuR{Gr}_{e}(A)
inherits a ring structure from that of
\EuR{Gr}(A)
, since the tensor product over the ground field
k
is an exact functor, and the obvious projection
\EuR{Gr}(A)\to\EuR{Gr}_{e}(A)
is a ring homomorphism. However,
\EuR{Gr}_{e}(A)
is a much less interesting object than
\EuR{Gr}(A)
, since the class of any module is equal to the sum of all simple modules in a composition series. Thus
\EuR{Gr}_{e}(A)
is isomorphic to the free commutative algebra generated by the simple
A
-modules.

Definition 2.11
.

For a small category
\mathcal{C}
with object set
V
, define
\mathbf{dim}_{k}\colon\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{C})\to\mathbb{Z}^{V}
by

\mathbf{dim}_{k}[M](v)\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\dim_{k}(M(v)),

for any
M\in k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
and for
[X]=[M]-[N]
with
M,N\in k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
, let

\mathbf{dim}_{k}[X]\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\mathbf{dim}_{k}[M]-\mathbf{dim}_{k}[N].

For
[X]\in\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{C})
we refer to
\mathbf{dim}_{k}[X]
as the
dimension vector of
[X]
.

Since the addition operation in
\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{C})
is induced by direct sum, the function
\mathbf{dim}_{k}
is clearly a group homomorphism, whose kernel is the subgroup of all virtual modules
[X]=[M]-[N]
such that
\dim_{k}M(v)=\dim_{k}N(v)
for every
v\in V
. Clearly for
M\in k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
,
\mathbf{dim}_{k}[M]=0
if and only if
M=0
.

Definition 2.12
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category. An element
[X]\in\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{C})
is said to be
locally constant
, if it can be represented as a difference of the isomorphism classes of two locally constant modules, and similarly for
virtually trivial
(Definition
2.4
).

Remark 2.13
.

Recall that if
\mathcal{C}
is a category with a finite set of objects and
k
is a field, then the functor category
k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
is equivalent to the category of modules over the category algebra
k\mathcal{C}

[
16
]
. If
F\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
is a functor, then one obtains a homomorphism
kF\colon k\mathcal{C}\to k\mathcal{D}
, which is a ring homomorphism if and only if
F
is injective on object sets
[
16
, Proposition 2.2.3]
, and otherwise is only a homomorphism of vector spaces. However,
F^{*}
does preserve tensor products over
k
, and hence induces a ring homomorphism
F^{*}\colon\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{D})\to\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{C})
.

2.4.
Functor categories and Kan extensions

Kan extensions will be used to define the divergence operators in Section
3
, and we recall here the basic notions. Let
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D}
be small categories, and let
\mathcal{A}
be a category that is bicomplete (i.e., all small limits and colimits exist in
\mathcal{A}
). Let
\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{C}}
and
\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{D}}
denote the categories of functors from
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
to
\mathcal{A}
, respectively, with morphisms given by natural transformations. If
F\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
is a functor, then the restriction
F^{*}\colon\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{D}}\to\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{C}}
admits left and right adjoints, given by the left and right Kan extensions
[
12
, X.3, Corollary 2]
,

L_{F},R_{F}\colon\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{C}}\to\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{D}}.

For the sake of completeness, we briefly describe these constructions. The reader is referred to
[
12
, Chapter X]
for details. For any object
d\in\mathcal{D}
, let
F\downarrow d
denote the
overcategory of
d
with respect to
F
, whose objects are pairs
(c,\varphi)
, where
c
is an object in
\mathcal{C}
and
\varphi\colon F(c)\to d
is a morphism in
\mathcal{D}
. A morphism
(c,\varphi)\to(c^{\prime},\varphi^{\prime})
in
F\downarrow d
is a morphism
\beta\colon c\to c^{\prime}
, such that
\varphi^{\prime}\circ F(\beta)=\varphi
. There is a forgetful functor
\#\colon F\downarrow d\to\mathcal{C}
,
sending an object
(c,\varphi)
to
c
. By analogy one defines
d\downarrow F
, the
undercategory of
d
with respect to
F
, and similarly a forgetful functor to
\mathcal{C}
. For
M\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{C}}
, left and right Kan extensions of
M
along
F
are defined by

L_{F}(M)(d)\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\mathop{\textup{colim}}\limits_{F\downarrow d}M_{\#},\quad\text{and}\quad R_{F}(M)(d)\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\mathop{\textup{lim}}\limits_{d\downarrow F}M_{\#},

where in both cases
M_{\#}
denotes the composition of
M
with the respective forgetful functor. Thus if
N\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{C}}
and
M\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{D}}
, we have the adjointness relations:

\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{D}}}(L_{F}(N),M)\cong\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{C}}}(N,F^{*}(M)),\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{C}}}(F^{*}(M),N)\cong\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{D}}}(M,R_{F}(N)).

The following lemma is standard homological algebra. A proof is included for the convenience of the reader.

Lemma 2.14
.

Let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
be small abelian categories and assume that
\mathcal{A}
has enough projectives. Let
F\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{B}
be a functor with a right adjoint
G\colon\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{A}
. Then the following statements are equivalent.

(1)

F
sends projective objects in
\mathcal{A}
to projective objects in
\mathcal{B}
.

(2)

G
is an exact functor.

Dually, if
\mathcal{B}
has enough injectives, then the following statements are equivalent.

(3)

G
sends injective objects in
\mathcal{B}
to injective objects in
\mathcal{A}
.

(4)

F
is an exact functor.

Proof.

We prove the equivalence of (
1
) and (
2
). The equivalence of (
3
) and (
4
) follows by analogy. Let
P\in\mathcal{A}
be a projective object. Then
F(P)\in\mathcal{B}
is projective if and only if the functor
\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{B}}(F(P),-)\cong\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{A}}(P,G(-))
is exact. Thus, if
G
is exact then
F(P)
is projective. This shows that (
2
) implies (
1
).

Conversely, since
\mathcal{A}
is assumed to have enough projectives, every object
X\in\mathcal{A}
admits an epimorphism
P\to X
, where
P
is projective in
\mathcal{A}
. Let

0\to B\xrightarrow{\alpha}C\xrightarrow{\beta}D\to 0

be an exact sequence in
\mathcal{B}
. Since
G
is a right adjoint, it is left exact, so it suffices to show that

G(C)\xrightarrow{G(\beta)}G(D)\to 0

is exact. Let
\varphi\colon P\to G(D)
be an epimorphism in
\mathcal{A}
, where
P
is projective. By (
1
),
F
sends projective objects to projective objects. Thus

\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{B}}(F(P),C)\xrightarrow{\beta_{*}}\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{B}}(F(P),D)\to 0

is exact, and by adjointness

\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{A}}(P,G(C))\xrightarrow{G(\beta)_{*}}\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{A}}(P,G(D))\to 0

is exact. Hence there exists
\psi\in\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{A}}(P,G(C))
such that
G(\beta)_{*}(\psi)=G(\beta)\circ\psi=\varphi
. Since
\varphi
is an epimorphism, so is
G(\beta)
, as claimed.
∎

As a corollary we have the following lemma.

Lemma 2.15
.

Let
\gamma\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
be a functor between small categories. Let
\mathcal{A}
be a bicomplete abelian category, and let
\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{C}}
and
\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{D}}
denote the respective functor categories. Let
\gamma^{*}\colon\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{D}}\to\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{C}}
denote the restriction, and let
L_{\gamma}
and
R_{\gamma}
denote its left and right Kan extensions. Then for any
M\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{C}}
and
N\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{D}}
,

\mathrm{Ext}^{*}_{\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{D}}}(L_{\gamma}(M),N)\cong\mathrm{Ext}^{*}_{\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{C}}}(M,\gamma^{*}N)

if
\mathcal{A}
has enough projectives and either of the following two equivalent conditions holds.

(1)

\gamma^{*}
sends injective modules to injective modules.

(2)

L_{\gamma}
is an exact functor.

Similarly, if
\mathcal{A}
has enough injectives then

\mathrm{Ext}^{*}_{\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{D}}}(N,R_{\gamma}(M))\cong\mathrm{Ext}^{*}_{\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{C}}}(\gamma^{*}N,M)

if either of the following equivalent conditions holds.

(3)

\gamma^{*}
sends projective modules to projective modules.

(4)

R_{\gamma}
is an exact functor.

Proof.

We prove the first statement. The second follows by analogy. Let
N\to I^{*}
be an injective resolution of
N
in
\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{D}}
. Since
\gamma^{*}
is exact and sends injective modules to injective modules,
\gamma^{*}N\to\gamma^{*}(I^{*})
is an injective resolution of
\gamma^{*}N
in
\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{C}}
. Then

\mathrm{Ext}^{*}_{\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{C}}}(M,\gamma^{*}N)=H^{*}(\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{C}}}(M,\gamma^{*}(I^{*}))\cong H^{*}(\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{D}}}(L_{\gamma}(M),I^{*}))=\mathrm{Ext}_{\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{D}}}^{*}(L_{\gamma}(M),N),

as claimed. The equivalence of Conditions (
1
) and (
2
) follows from Lemma
2.14
.
∎

3.
Categorifying the discrete gradient and divergence

In this section we present a categorical analog of the discrete gradient and divergence on digraphs. With the appropriate setup, the standard notions of these concepts then occur as special cases. In sections
5
and
7
we will specialise these constructions to the case where the category
\mathcal{C}
is a finite poset. This case is particularly well behaved and is strongly related to the notion of generalised persistence modules.

We start by recalling the graph-theoretic definitions of gradient and divergence. Let
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
be a digraph. Let
A
be an abelian group, and let
f\colon V\to A
and
F\colon E\to A
be arbitrary functions. Then one defines the gradient
\nabla(f)\colon E\to A
and the divergence
\nabla^{*}(F)\colon V\to A
by

(2)

\nabla(f)(v\to w)\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}f(w)-f(v),\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla^{*}(F)(v)=\sum_{u\to v}F(u\to v)-\sum_{v\to w}F(v\to w).

If
V
and
E
are finite and
A=\mathbb{R}
, then the gradient and divergence are related to each other as adjoint operators by means of the standard inner product. The reader is referred to
[
11
]
for a comprehensive discussion of the gradient and the divergence on digraphs.

3.1.
Categories generated by a quiver

Let
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
be a quiver. The path category associated to
\mathcal{G}
is the category
P(\mathcal{G})
with
V
as an object set and whose morphisms are all directed compositions of the directed edges in
E
. Thus
P(\mathcal{G})
is the category that is freely generated by
\mathcal{G}
. In particular, if
\mathcal{G}
is acyclic, then the only endomorphisms in
P(\mathcal{G})
are identities. If, in addition
\mathcal{G}
is finite, then
P(\mathcal{G})
is also finite, but if
\mathcal{G}
contains cycles then
P(\mathcal{G})
is infinite since each morphism corresponding to a cycle can be iterated arbitrarily many times. In this article we will almost always work with acyclic quivers.

Any small category
\mathcal{C}
(with discrete topology on object and morphism sets) is a quotient category of the path category of a quiver whose vertex set is in
1-1
correspondence with the object set of
\mathcal{C}
, and whose directed edges form a subset of its morphisms, where the equivalence relations are given by the commutativity relations in
\mathcal{C}
(See
[
2
, Section 5]
for more detail). A canonical example of this type is given by considering the quiver of all morphisms in a category
\mathcal{C}
. Then
\mathcal{C}
is reconstructed from that quiver by declaring two paths equivalent if the corresponding morphisms in
\mathcal{C}
coincide. Clearly, restricting to digraphs rather than general quivers puts some restrictions on the type of categories that appear as quotient categories of the corresponding path categories.

If
\mathcal{G}
is an acyclic quiver, then in its path category
P(\mathcal{G})
there are no non-identity endomorphisms, and hence the same holds for any quotient category of
P(\mathcal{G})
. For instance, a poset
\mathcal{P}
can be thought of as a quotient of the path category of its Hasse diagram
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
(the digraph of all objects in
\mathcal{P}
and all indecomposable morphisms between pairs of them) by the relations imposed by requiring that between any two objects
u
and
v
there is exactly one morphism if there is a directed path in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
from
u
to
v
. But categories generated by acyclic quivers are much more general than posets.

Definition 3.1
.

If
\mathcal{C}
is a category obtained as a quotient category of the path category
P(\mathcal{G})
of some quiver
\mathcal{G}
, we say that
\mathcal{G}
is a
generating quiver
for
\mathcal{C}
, or that
\mathcal{C}
is
generated
by
\mathcal{G}
.

Notice that the choice of a generating quiver for a small category
\mathcal{C}
is generally not unique. Also, if
\mathcal{G}
is acyclic then every category it generates will also be cycle-free. Hence we say that a category
\mathcal{C}
is acyclic if it is generated by an acyclic quiver.

Next we associate with any small acyclic category
\mathcal{C}
and a generating acyclic quiver
\mathcal{G}
another small category
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
that is generated by the associated line digraph
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}
. The category
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
will depend on the choice of the generating quiver, but for any such choice one has two functors
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}\to\mathcal{C}
that will allow us to define a gradient and two divergence operators.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category generated by an acyclic quiver
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
, and let
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}=(\widehat{V},\widehat{E})
denote its associated line digraph. Let
P(\mathcal{G})
and
P(\widehat{\mathcal{G}})
denote the associated path categories. Define a category
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
with the same object set as
P(\widehat{\mathcal{G}})
. Let
e\in E(u,t)
and
e^{\prime}\in E(s,v)
be objects in
P(\widehat{\mathcal{G}})
.
Assume that

\displaystyle\alpha\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}

\displaystyle\{e\to e_{1}\to e_{2}\to\cdots\to e_{m-1}\to e^{\prime}\},\quad\text{and}\quad

\displaystyle\gamma\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}

\displaystyle\{e\to f_{1}\to f_{2}\to\cdots\to f_{k-1}\to e^{\prime}\}

are morphisms in the path category
P(\widehat{\mathcal{G}})
, where
e_{i}\in E(a_{i},a_{i+1}),\;\;1\leq i\leq m-1
and
f_{j}\in E(b_{j},b_{j+1}),\;\;1\leq j\leq k-1
,
a_{1}=b_{1}=t
, and
a_{m}=b_{k}=s
. For the pair of objects
(u,t)
and
(s,v)
, define an equivalence relation on the morphism set
P(\widehat{\mathcal{G}})((u,t),(s,v))
, to be the transitive closure of the relation
\alpha\sim\gamma
if the compositions

t=a_{1}\to\cdots\to a_{m}=s,\quad\text{and}\quad t=b_{1}\to\cdots\to b_{k}=s

coincide in
\mathcal{C}
. Define
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}((u,t),(s,v))
to be the set of equivalence classes of this relation
.

The assignment
\mathcal{C}\mapsto\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
does not define an endofunctor on the category of small categories, since it depends on the choice of a generating digraph
\mathcal{G}
for
\mathcal{C}
(although if one takes the generating quiver as part of the data, it is possible to define the construction functorially). We refer to
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
as the
line category associated to
\mathcal{C}
with respect to the generating quiver
\mathcal{G}
.

3.2.
The categorical gradient

We are now ready to define a categorical version of the gradient. We start by defining a pair of graph maps
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}\to\mathcal{G}
, which in turn induce functors
\phi,\beta\colon\widehat{\mathcal{C}}\to\mathcal{C}
, where
\mathcal{C}
and its associated line category are generated by
\mathcal{G}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}
respectively.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a quiver, and let
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}
be its associated line digraph. Let
\phi,\beta\colon\widehat{\mathcal{G}}\to\mathcal{G}
denote the
front
and
back
graph morphisms, defined by
\phi(e)=\tau(e)
and
\beta(e)=\sigma(e)
, where
\tau
and
\sigma
are the target and source functions, respectively. For an edge
(e,e^{\prime})\in\widehat{\mathcal{G}}
, define
\phi(e,e^{\prime})=e^{\prime}
and
\beta(e,e^{\prime})=e
.

The maps
\phi
and
\beta
induce functors

\phi,\beta\colon P(\widehat{\mathcal{G}})\to P(\mathcal{G}).

The following lemma shows that the front and back functors induce the corresponding functors on the line category.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category generated by a quiver
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
.
Let
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
denote the line category associated to
\mathcal{C}
with respect to
\mathcal{G}
. Then the front and back functors
\phi
and
\beta
on path categories induce functors

\phi,\beta\colon\widehat{\mathcal{C}}\to\mathcal{C}.

Proof.

The category
\mathcal{C}
is a quotient category of the path category
P(\mathcal{G})
by the relations among morphisms in
\mathcal{C}
. Thus it suffices to show that the square of functors

\textstyle{P(\widehat{\mathcal{G}})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\psi}
\scriptstyle{\widehat{\pi}}
\textstyle{P(\mathcal{G})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\pi}
\textstyle{\widehat{\mathcal{C}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\bar{\psi}}
\textstyle{\mathcal{C}}

where
\psi
is either
\phi
or
\beta
, commutes. We prove the statement for
\phi
. The proof for
\beta
is essentially the same.
The objects in
P(\widehat{\mathcal{G}})
are the edges
e\in E(u,v)
in
\mathcal{G}
, and
\phi(e)=v
. Define
\bar{\phi}(e)=v
. The square commutes on objects by definition.
Let
e\in E(u,t)
and
e^{\prime}\in E(s,v)
be objects in
P(\widehat{\mathcal{G}})
, and let

\displaystyle\alpha\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}

\displaystyle\{e\to e_{1}\to e_{2}\to\cdots\to e_{m-1}\to e^{\prime}\},\quad\text{and}\quad

\displaystyle\gamma\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}

\displaystyle\{e\to f_{1}\to f_{2}\to\cdots\to f_{k-1}\to e^{\prime}\}

be morphisms in
P(\widehat{\mathcal{G}})
, as in Definition

, such that
\widehat{\pi}(\alpha)=\widehat{\pi}(\gamma)
. Then, by definition, the compositions

t=a_{1}\to\cdots\to a_{m}=s,\quad\text{and}\quad t=b_{1}\to\cdots\to b_{k}=s

coincide in
\mathcal{C}
. But these are exactly the projections
\pi(\phi(\alpha))
and
\pi(\phi(\gamma))
in

\mathcal{C}
. This shows that
\bar{\phi}
is well defined and that the square commutes on morphisms, and hence the proof is complete.
∎

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a category, and let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category, with a generating quiver
\mathcal{G}
and an associated line digraph
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}
. Let
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
be the associated line category with respect to
\mathcal{G}
. The functors
\phi,\beta\colon\widehat{\mathcal{C}}\to\mathcal{C}
induce
restriction functors

\phi^{*},\beta^{*}\colon\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{C}}\to\mathcal{S}^{\widehat{\mathcal{C}}},

where
\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{C}}
and
\mathcal{S}^{\widehat{\mathcal{C}}}
are the functor categories from
\mathcal{C}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
to
\mathcal{S}
, respectively. We are now ready to define the gradient.

Definition 3.5
.

Fix a small category
\mathcal{C}
and an associated line category
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
with respect to some generating quiver
\mathcal{G}
. Let
\mathcal{S}
be an additive category. Let
\EuR{Gr}(\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{C}})
denote the Grothendieck group of isomorphism classes of functors
\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{S}
, with the operation
[F]+[G]=[F\sqcup G]
, where
(F\sqcup G)(c)=F(c)\sqcup G(c)
, and where
\sqcup
denotes the coproduct in
\mathcal{S}
.
Define the
gradient

\nabla\colon\EuR{Gr}(\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{C}})\to\EuR{Gr}(\mathcal{S}^{\widehat{\mathcal{C}}})

by

\nabla[F]=[\phi^{*}(F)]-[\beta^{*}(F)]

for each functor
F\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{C}}
. Extend
\nabla
by additivity to the whole Grothendieck group.

Next we show that the standard definition of a gradient for a digraph with vertex and edge weight functions taking values in the group of integers is a particular case of our setup.

Example 3.6
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
be a quiver with line digraph
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}
. Then
\phi
and
\beta
induce functors

\phi^{*},\beta^{*}\colon\mathcal{S}^{P(\mathcal{G})}\to\mathcal{S}^{P(\widehat{\mathcal{G}})}.

Consider
V
and
E
as discrete categories, and let
V\to P(\mathcal{G})
and
E\to P(\widehat{\mathcal{G}})
be the obvious inclusions. Restriction of
\phi^{*}
and
\beta^{*}
gives functors

\phi^{*},\beta^{*}\colon\mathcal{S}^{V}\to\mathcal{S}^{E}.

Now, assume that
\mathcal{S}={\mathrm{Vect}_{k}}
, the category of vector spaces over a field
k
, with the coproduct
\sqcup
given by the direct sum. Thus
\EuR{Gr}(\mathcal{S}^{V})=\EuR{Gr}(kV)
and
\EuR{Gr}(\mathcal{S}^{E})=\EuR{Gr}(kE)
are the groups that consist of functions on
V
or
E
, respectively, which take a vertex or an edge to a finite dimensional
k
-vector space.
Since vector spaces are determined up to isomorphism by their dimension, these Grothendieck groups are easy to compute:

\EuR{Gr}(kV)=\mathbb{Z}^{V}\cong\bigoplus_{v\in V}\mathbb{Z},\quad\text{and}\quad\EuR{Gr}(kE)=\mathbb{Z}^{E}\cong\bigoplus_{e\in E}\mathbb{Z}.

Thus the gradient is given by

\nabla[f](u,v)=\dim_{k}(f(v))-\dim_{k}(f(u)),

where
[f]\in\EuR{Gr}(kV)
.

Let
\varphi\colon V\to\mathbb{Z}
be an arbitrary function that takes non-negative values. Considering
V
again as a discrete category, let
f\colon V\to{\mathrm{Vect}_{k}}
be the functor defined by
f(v)=k^{\varphi(v)}
. Then

\nabla[f](u,v)=\nabla(\varphi)(u,v),

where the left hand side is the gradient of the element
[f]\in\EuR{Gr}(\mathcal{S}^{V})
, and the right hand side is the graph theoretic definition for the gradient of the function
\varphi
, as in (
2
).

We next study some basic properties of the gradient.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category generated by a quiver
\mathcal{G}
, and let
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
denote the associated line category. Then the gradient
\nabla=\nabla_{\mathcal{C}}\colon\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{C})\to\EuR{Gr}(k\widehat{\mathcal{C}})
satisfies the following properties:

(1)

\nabla
is a well defined group homomorphism.

(2)

If
[X]\in\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{C})
is locally constant then
\nabla[X]=0
.

(3)

\nabla
satisfies a Leibniz type rule, i.e. for all
[X],[Y]\in\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{C})
, the identity

\nabla([X]\cdot[Y])=\nabla[X]\cdot\phi^{*}[Y]+\beta^{*}[X]\cdot\nabla[Y]

holds in
\EuR{Gr}(k\widehat{\mathcal{C}})
.

Furthermore,
\nabla
is natural with respect to restrictions, namely, if
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
is a subcategory that is generated by a sub-quiver
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
, and
\iota\colon\mathcal{D}\xrightarrow{\subseteq}\mathcal{C}
is the inclusion, then
\iota^{*}\circ\nabla_{\mathcal{C}}=\nabla_{\mathcal{D}}\circ\iota^{*}
.

Proof.

Since
\phi^{*},\beta^{*}\colon\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{C})\to\EuR{Gr}(k\widehat{\mathcal{C}})
are ring homomorphisms (see Remark
2.13
), they are in particular homomorphisms of abelian groups, and hence so is their difference. This proves Part
1
.

Let
M\in k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
be locally constant. Define a natural isomorphism
\mu\colon\beta^{*}M\to\phi^{*}M
, by sending an object
e
in
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
to the morphism

\mu_{e}\colon\beta^{*}M(e)=M(\beta(e))\xrightarrow{M(e)}M(\phi(e))=\phi^{*}M(e).

Naturality is clear. It follows that
\nabla[M]=0
. By definition, an element of
[X]\in\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{C})
is locally constant if it is represented as a difference of locally constant modules. By additivity
\nabla
vanishes on any locally constant virtual module in
\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{C})
. This proves Part
2
.

Finally, since
\phi^{*}
and
\beta^{*}
are ring homomorphisms, one has for

M,N\in k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
,

\displaystyle\nabla[M]\cdot\phi^{*}[N]+\beta^{*}[M]\cdot\nabla[N]

\displaystyle=(\phi^{*}[M]-\beta^{*}[M])\cdot\phi^{*}[N]+\beta^{*}[M]\cdot(\phi^{*}[N]-\beta^{*}[N])

\displaystyle=\phi^{*}[M]\cdot\phi^{*}[N]-\beta^{*}[M]\cdot\beta^{*}[N]

\displaystyle=\nabla([M]\cdot[N])

as claimed in Part
3
. For virtual modules
[X]=[M]-[N]
, extend the statement by linearity of
\phi^{*}
,
\beta^{*}
and
\nabla
.

The last statement follows from commutativity of the square

\textstyle{k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\iota^{*}}
\scriptstyle{\alpha^{*}}
\textstyle{k\mathcal{D}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\alpha^{*}}
\textstyle{k\widehat{\mathcal{C}}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\iota^{*}}
\textstyle{k\widehat{\mathcal{D}}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}}

where
\alpha
is either
\phi
or
\beta
.
∎

3.3.
The categorical divergence

Next we define the divergence operators in our context. For this we require that the category
\mathcal{S}
is additive and bicomplete, namely that all small limits and colimits exist in
\mathcal{S}
. In that case the functors
\phi^{*}
and
\beta^{*}
have left and right adjoints given by the left and right Kan extensions. Notice that because of pre-additivity of
\mathcal{S}
finite products and coproducts coincide in
\mathcal{S}
.

Definition 3.8
.

Fix a small category
\mathcal{C}
and an associated line category
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
with respect to some generating quiver
\mathcal{G}
. Let
\mathcal{S}
be a small bicomplete additive category.
Define the
left divergence
and the
right divergence
(with respect to
\mathcal{G}
)

\nabla^{*},\nabla_{*}\colon\EuR{Gr}(\mathcal{S}^{\widehat{\mathcal{C}}})\to\EuR{Gr}(\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{C}})

by

\nabla^{*}[T]=[L_{\phi}(T)]-[L_{\beta}(T)],\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla_{*}[T]=[R_{\phi}(T)]-[R_{\beta}(T)]

for each functor
T\colon\widehat{\mathcal{C}}\to\mathcal{S}
. Extend by additivity to the whole Grothendieck group.

Since limits commute with limits and colimits with colimits, and since finite products and coproducts coincide in an additive category, both operators are well defined group homomorphisms.

Divergence in calculus on graphs is defined by the requirement that it must be adjoint to the gradient with respect to the inner product. Since in our context there is no direct analog to an inner product (but see Section
4
), a justification of the definition is required. We proceed by showing how the standard divergence operator for digraphs, as in (
2
) with vertex and edge weight functions taking values in the integers, is a particular example of the general setup of Definition
3.8
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
be a quiver with line digraph
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}
. Let
\mathcal{S}
be a category satisfying the requirement of Definition
3.8
.
Consider
V
and
E
as discrete subcategories of
P(\mathcal{G})
and
P(\widehat{\mathcal{G}})
respectively, as in Example
3.6
.
Restriction of
\phi^{*}
and
\beta^{*}
gives functors

\phi^{*},\beta^{*}\colon\mathcal{S}^{V}\to\mathcal{S}^{E}.

Since
V
and
E
are discrete, we have for
f\in\mathcal{S}^{E}

L_{\phi}(f)(v)\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\mathop{\textup{colim}}\limits_{\phi\downarrow v}f=\coprod_{(u,v)\in E}f(u,v),\quad\text{and}\quad L_{\beta}(f)(v)\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\mathop{\textup{colim}}\limits_{\beta\downarrow v}f=\coprod_{(v,w)\in E}f(v,w).

Similarly

R_{\phi}(f)(v)\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\mathop{\textup{lim}}\limits_{v\downarrow\phi}f=\prod_{(u,v)\in E}f(u,v),\quad\text{and}\quad R_{\beta}(f)(v)\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\mathop{\textup{lim}}\limits_{v\downarrow\beta}f=\prod_{(v,w)\in E}f(v,w).

Example 3.9
.

Restrict attention to the case where
\mathcal{S}={\mathrm{Vect}_{k}}
and the quiver
\mathcal{G}
is finite. Then, finite products (cartesian products) and finite coproducts (direct sums) are isomorphic. Hence, the right and left Kan extensions coincide.
As in Example
3.6
, we have

\EuR{Gr}(kV)=\mathbb{Z}^{V}\cong\bigoplus_{v\in V}\mathbb{Z},\quad\text{and}\quad\EuR{Gr}(kE)=\mathbb{Z}^{E}\cong\bigoplus_{e\in E}\mathbb{Z}.

Hence

\nabla^{*}[f](v)=\nabla_{*}[f](v)=\sum_{(u,v)\in E}\dim_{k}f(u,v)-\sum_{(v,w)\in E}\dim_{k}f(v,w)

for
f\colon E\to{\mathrm{Vect}_{k}}
.

Let
\gamma\colon E\to\mathbb{Z}
be an arbitrary function that takes non-negative values. Considering
E
as a discrete category again, let
g\colon E\to{\mathrm{Vect}_{k}}
denote the functor defined by
g(e)=k^{\gamma(e)}
. Then

\nabla^{*}[g]=\nabla_{*}[g]=\nabla^{*}(\gamma)

where the left and centre in the equation are the left and right divergence of
[g]
in
\EuR{Gr}(kV)
, and the right hand side is the graph theoretic divergence of
\gamma
, as in (
2
).

Notice that restricting to finite quivers is essential because the category of finite dimensional vector spaces is only finitely bicomplete.

3.4.
A special case - poset categories

A family of examples that are particularly relevant to this article arises from considering posets and their associated Hasse diagrams.

Any poset
\mathcal{P}
is uniquely determined by its associated
Hasse diagram

\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
, which is the transitive reduction of the acyclic digraph underlying the poset
\mathcal{P}
. Namely the digraph
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
has as vertices the objects of
\mathcal{P}
, and directed edges are the non-identity indecomposable relations in
\mathcal{P}
, namely those relations
u<v
in
\mathcal{P}
such that there is no intermediate relation
u<y<v
. The corresponding edge in the digraph
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
is then denoted by
(u,v)
. By taking the transitive closure of
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
one recovers
\mathcal{P}
.

Example 3.10
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a poset, let
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
be its Hasse diagram and let
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{P}}
be the line digraph associated to
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
. Then
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{P}}
is the Hasse diagram of the line category
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
, as in Definition
3.2
. We claim that
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
is again a poset.
To see this, note that a Hasse diagram is, by definition, an acyclic and transitively reduced digraph. Thus it suffices to show that if
\mathcal{H}=(V,E)
is a transitively reduced acyclic digraph, then
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}
is also acyclic and transitively reduced.

It is immediate from the definition that the existence of a cycle in
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}
implies that
\mathcal{H}
itself contains a cycle. Let
(u,v,w)
be an edge in
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}
, and assume that it can be decomposed as
(u,v,z)\cdot(v,z,w)
(See Definition
2.8
for the notation). Then
\mathcal{H}
contains the composable sequence

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces u}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces v}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces z}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces w}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(u,v)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(v,z)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(z,w)}

Hence either
(u,v)
is not composable with
(z,w)
or
v=z
. The first option contradicts the assumption that
(u,v,w)
is an edge in
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}
and the second option contradicts the assumption that
(v,z,w)
is an edge in
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}
, or alternatively acyclicity of
\mathcal{H}
due to the self-loop
(v,v)
. Thus
(u,v,w)
is indecomposable, and so
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}
is transitively reduced.

If
\mathcal{P}
is a poset and
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
is the associated line poset, then the objects of
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
are the edges
(u,v)
in
\mathcal{H}_{P}
and will be denoted by the same notation. A relation
(u,v)\leq(z,w)
holds in
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
if there is a sequence of directed edges, for
n\geq 2
,

(u,v)=(a_{0},a_{1})\xrightarrow{(a_{0},a_{1},a_{2})}(a_{1},a_{2})\xrightarrow{(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})}(a_{2},a_{3})\to\cdots\xrightarrow{(a_{n-2},a_{n-1},a_{n})}(a_{n-1},a_{n})=(z,w),

where
(a_{i-1},a_{i},a_{i+1})
corresponds to the sequence
a_{i-1}\to a_{i}\to a_{i+1}
of consecutive edges in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
.

4.
Bilinear pairings on
\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{C})

In this section we define two bilinear forms on
\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{C})
, where
\mathcal{C}
is a finite acyclic category (i.e., a category generated by a finite acyclic quiver).

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a finite acyclic quiver that generates a category
\mathcal{C}
. Define the
Hom pairing

\langle-,-\rangle_{\mathcal{C}}\colon\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{C})\times\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{C})\to\mathbb{Z}

by

\langle[M],[N]\rangle_{\mathcal{C}}\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\dim_{k}\left(\mathrm{Hom}_{k\mathcal{C}}(M,N)\right),

where
M
and
N
represent their isomorphism classes in
\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{C})
. Extend the definition to the full Grothendieck group by additivity.

Notice that acyclicity and finiteness of
\mathcal{G}
guarantees that the category
\mathcal{C}
is finite, and so the Hom objects are guaranteed in turn to be finite dimensional. The Hom pairing is clearly bilinear and can be defined by analogy also on
\EuR{Gr}(k\widehat{\mathcal{C}})
. Since the left and right Kan extensions are left and right adjoints to the restrictions
\phi^{*}
and
\beta^{*}
we have

(3)

\langle\nabla^{*}[X],[Y]\rangle_{\mathcal{C}}=\langle[X],\nabla[Y]\rangle_{\widehat{\mathcal{C}}},\quad\text{and}\quad\langle\nabla[X],[Y]\rangle_{\widehat{\mathcal{C}}}=\langle[X],\nabla_{*}[Y]\rangle_{\mathcal{C}}

for
[X]\in\EuR{Gr}(k{\widehat{\mathcal{C}}})
and
[Y]\in\EuR{Gr}(k{\mathcal{C}})
. Notice that the Hom pairing is not generally symmetric; however, see Example
4.6
below.

Next we define another useful pairing on
\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{C})
.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a finite acyclic quiver that generates a category
\mathcal{C}
. Define the
Euler pairing

\chi_{\mathcal{C}}\colon\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{C})\times\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{C})\to\mathbb{Z}

by

\chi_{\mathcal{C}}([M],[N])\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\chi(\mathrm{Ext}_{k\mathcal{C}}^{*}(M,N))=\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{n}\dim_{k}(\mathrm{Ext}^{n}_{k\mathcal{C}}(M,N)),

where
M
and
N
represent their isomorphism classes in
\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{C})
, and extend to the full Grothendieck group by additivity.

To show that this is well defined, we must argue that the
\mathrm{Ext}^{n}
groups vanish for
n
sufficiently large. This is implied by Lemma
4.3
below (see also
[
16
]
, Theorem 4.2.4).

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a finite acyclic quiver that generates a category
\mathcal{C}
. Then the category algebra
k\mathcal{C}
has finite projective dimension.

Proof.

Since we assume that
\mathcal{G}
is finite and acyclic, composition length in
\mathcal{C}
is bounded. For each object
x\in\mathcal{C}
, let
F_{x}\in k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
denote the projective module defined by

F_{x}=k\mathcal{C}(x,-).

Let
M
be a finitely generated
k\mathcal{C}
-module. For each object
x\in\mathcal{C}
, choose a basis
\{v^{x}_{1},\ldots,v^{x}_{n_{x}}\}
for
M(x)
. For
1\leq i\leq n_{x}
let

q^{x}_{i}\colon F_{x}\to M

denote the natural transformation determined by taking
1_{x}\in\mathcal{C}(x,x)
, to
v^{x}_{i}
. Thus one obtains a surjection of
k\mathcal{C}
-modules

q_{M}\colon\bigoplus_{x\in\mathcal{C}}F_{x}^{d_{x}}\to M,

where
d_{x}=\dim_{k}(M(x))
and
F_{x}^{d_{x}}=\bigoplus_{d_{x}}F_{x}
.
Let
M_{1}
denote the
\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits(q_{M})
. If
x
is a minimal object in the sense that
\mathcal{C}(-,x)=\emptyset
, then
M_{1}(x)=0
. Thus
M_{1}
vanishes on all minimal objects in
\mathcal{C}
. Notice that if
d_{x}=0
, then no copy of
F_{x}
is included in the cover. Next, construct a projective cover of
M_{1}
in a similar fashion, avoiding all minimal objects in
\mathcal{C}
, and all those of graph distance
1
from a minimal object, for which
M_{1}
vanishes. Let
M_{2}
be the kernel of this cover. Then
M_{2}
vanishes on all minimal objects and all objects of graph distance
1
from a minimal object. By induction on the length of the maximal chains in
\mathcal{C}
, we obtain a finite projective resolution for
M
of length bounded above by the length of a maximal chain in
\mathcal{C}
.
∎

The pairing
\chi_{\mathcal{C}}
is clearly bilinear, but in general it is neither symmetric, nor is it true that

(4)

\chi_{\mathcal{C}}(\nabla^{*}[X],[Y])=\chi_{\widehat{\mathcal{C}}}([X],\nabla[Y]),\quad\text{and}\quad\chi_{\mathcal{C}}([X],\nabla_{*}[Y])=\chi_{\widehat{\mathcal{C}}}(\nabla[X],[Y]).

This is the case because in general
L_{\beta}
and
R_{\phi}
are not exact functors (
L_{\phi}
and
R_{\beta}
are exact if
\mathcal{C}
is generated by a rooted tree, but not in general otherwise)
.

A special case occurs when the category
\mathcal{C}
is generated by a finite acyclic quiver, due to the fact that path algebras over acyclic quivers are hereditary (namely submodules of projective modules are projective)
[
4
, Theorem 2.3.2]
.
Hence for any
M,N\in k\mathcal{C}
, one has
\mathrm{Ext}_{k\mathcal{C}}^{i}(M,N)=0
for
i>1
. Thus in this case

\chi_{\mathcal{C}}([M],[N])=\dim_{k}\mathrm{Hom}_{k\mathcal{C}}(M,N)-\dim_{k}\mathrm{Ext}^{1}_{k\mathcal{C}}(M,N),

and the Euler pairing is rather easy to compute, as it depends only on the dimension of point modules
. To make this statement precise, we need the following definition.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}=(V,E)
be a finite quiver. Let
f,g\in\mathbb{Z}^{V}
be integer valued functions on
V
. Define a function
\chi_{\mathcal{Q}}\colon\mathbb{Z}^{V}\times\mathbb{Z}^{V}\to\mathbb{Z}

by

\chi_{\mathcal{Q}}(f,g)\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\sum_{x\in V}f(x)g(x)-\sum_{e\in E}f(\beta(e))g(\phi(e)),

where
\beta
and
\phi
are the source and target functions on
E
.
The function
\chi_{\mathcal{Q}}
is called the
Euler form for
\mathcal{Q}
.

Lemma 4.5

(
[
4
, Proposition 2.5.2]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a category generated by a finite acyclic quiver
\mathcal{Q}
. Then for any
M,N\in k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}

\chi_{\mathcal{C}}([M],[N])=\chi_{\mathcal{Q}}(\mathbf{dim}_{k}[M],\mathbf{dim}_{k}[N]).

Notice that if the quiver
\mathcal{Q}
is a finite tree, then the category it generates is in fact a poset. Also, the lemma shows that symmetry almost always fails, even under rather favourable circumstances, because the second term in the definition of the Euler form is not symmetric.

Once again, it makes sense to compare these constructions to the corresponding concept in discrete calculus on graphs. As before we consider the vertices
V
and the edges
E
of a quiver
\mathcal{G}
as discrete categories. The next example shows that in this case Hom pairing and the Euler pairing coincide and in the appropriate setting they are equal to the inner product pairing.

Example 4.6
.

As in Example
3.9
the sets
V
and
E
are considered as discrete categories. Hence
kV
and
kE
are simply the
k
-vector spaces generated by
V
and
E
, respectively, with trivial products, and modules over them are again just an assignment of a finite dimensional vector space to each object.

\displaystyle\chi_{V}([f],[g])=\langle[f],[g]\rangle_{V}=\dim_{k}(\mathrm{Hom}_{kV}(f,g))=

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V}\dim_{k}(f(v))\dim_{k}(g(v)),\quad\text{and}\quad

\displaystyle\chi_{E}([h],[s])=\langle[h],[s]\rangle_{E}=\dim_{k}(\mathrm{Hom}_{kE}(h,s))=

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in E}\dim_{k}(h(e))\dim_{k}(s(e))

Thus, if we let
\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime\prime}\colon V\to\mathbb{Z}
and
\delta^{\prime},\delta^{\prime\prime}\colon E\to\mathbb{Z}
be arbitrary functions with nonnegative values, and we define
g^{\prime},g^{\prime\prime}\colon V\to{\mathrm{Vect}_{k}}
and
h^{\prime},h^{\prime\prime}\colon E\to{\mathrm{Vect}_{k}}
to be the functors defined by
g^{\prime}(v)=k^{\gamma^{\prime}(v)}
and
g^{\prime\prime}(v)=k^{\gamma^{\prime\prime}(v)}
, and similarly define
h^{\prime}h^{\prime\prime}
using
\delta^{\prime},\delta^{\prime\prime}
, then

\displaystyle\chi_{V}([g^{\prime}],[g^{\prime\prime}])

\displaystyle=\langle[f],[g]\rangle_{V}=(\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime\prime}),\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\chi_{E}([h^{\prime}],[h^{\prime\prime}])

\displaystyle=\langle[h],[s]\rangle_{E}=(\delta^{\prime},\delta^{\prime\prime}),

where the right hand side denotes the ordinary inner product with the corresponding functions considered as vectors.
Notice that the
pairings in this case are commutative
. Also, by Example
3.9
the divergence operators
\nabla^{*}
and
\nabla_{*}
coincide in this case. In particular, the adjointness relations (

Having established the general property of the gradient that it is vanishing on locally constant modules, the next obvious question is the converse, namely what can be said about a module with a vanishing gradient. To answer this question we need a preparatory lemma.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a line connected finite poset. Then its Hasse diagram
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
has a line connected maximal tree.

Proof.

The Hasse diagram
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
is a connected digraph (a line connected digraph is in particular connected), and by definition it is acyclic and transitively reduced. Let
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{P}}
denote its line digraph, as usual. If
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
is not a tree, then there are two objects
x<y\in\mathcal{P}
and at least two distinct paths in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
from
x
to
y
. We proceed by showing that one can disconnect one of the paths from
x
to
y
in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
with the resulting digraph remaining line connected. The claim then follows by induction on the number of multiple paths between pairs of points in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
.

Let
x<y\in\mathcal{P}
be two distinct vertices, and assume that there are more than one directed path from
x
to
y
. Denote the collection of all directed paths from
x
to
y
by
l_{1},\ldots,l_{n}
, for
n>1
. We may assume that in all
l_{j}
, except possibly one of them (since double edges are not allowed in a poset), there is a vertex
x<a_{i}<y
, such that either
x<a_{i}
or
a_{i}<y
is indecomposable in
\mathcal{P}
. We may assume without loss of generality that the
l_{j}
have no common vertices except
x
and
y
.

We consider four possibilities.

(1)
x
is not minimal and
y
is maximal:
Then there exists some relation
x_{0}<x
in
\mathcal{P}
, and removing a single edge
x<a_{j}
from all
l_{j}
except one of them, disconnects all the
l_{j}
except the one that was not modified. Since this leaves exactly one path from
x
to
y
, the resulting digraph is still line connected.

(2)
y
is not maximal and
x
is minimal:
Then there exists a relation
y<y_{0}
in
\mathcal{P}
, and removing a single edge
a_{j}<y
from each
l_{j}
except one of them, disconnects all the
l_{j}
except the one that was not modified. Again the resulting digraph is line connected.

(3)
x
is not minimal and
y
is not maximal:
Then remove a single indecomposable edge from each
l_{j}
except one of them, as in (1) and (2). In that case as well the resulting digraph is line connected.

(4)
x
is minimal and
y
is maximal:

In that case the associated line digraph splits into
n
connected components, each containing the line digraph of
l_{j}
for a unique
1\leq j\leq n
. This contradicts the assumption that
\mathcal{P}
is line connected.

The proof is now complete by inductively performing this procedure for any set of multiple paths between pairs of vertices in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
.
∎

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}

\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}}\subseteq\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}

\nabla[M_{\mathcal{T}}]=0

\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}})

(1)

For any pair of objects

, there is an isomorphism
\alpha_{u,v}\colon M(u)\to M(v)
, such that
\alpha_{u,u}=1_{M(u)}

.

\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}}

\alpha_{w,t}\circ M(u\leq w)=M(s\leq t)\circ\alpha_{u,s}.

u\leq v

\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}}

\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}}

\alpha_{u,v}

Let
u_{0},u_{0}^{\prime}

\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}}

\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}}
is line connected by construction, it is in particular connected. Hence there are minimal objects
u_{0}=v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{k}=u_{0}^{\prime}
, such that for each
1\leq i\leq k-1

a_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}_{\geq v_{i}}}\cap\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}_{\geq v_{i+1}}}

\alpha_{a_{i},v_{i+1}}\circ\alpha_{v_{i},a_{i}}=\alpha_{v_{i+1},a_{i}}^{-1}\circ\alpha_{v_{i},a_{i}}\colon M(v_{i})\to M(v_{i+1}).

\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}}

\mathcal{T}
, compositions of isomorphisms of the form
\alpha_{x,y}
, where
(x,y)

\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{T}}
, and the inverses of such isomorphisms define
\alpha_{u,v}
for any pair of objects

. Since
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}}

1
).

(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})

\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{T}}

The next obvious question is what can be said about a virtual module

with a vanishing gradient on a tree, where

Example 5.8

Let
\mathcal{P}
be any finite poset. Consider the constant module

that assigns

Corollary 5.14
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a finite line connected poset, let
\mathcal{T}
be a line connected maximal tree for
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
, and let
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
be the sub-poset generated by

. Let

be an element of vanishing gradient on

, with

. Then there is an integer

, such that
[K_{M}]=[K_{N}]+[\underline{D}]
and
[C_{M}]=[C_{N}]+[\underline{D}]
in

, where

denotes the virtually trivial module that associates

with every object of
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{T}}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.13
,
K_{M}
and
K_{N}
, as well as
C_{M}
and
C_{N}
are virtually trivial, and hence their isomorphism type is determined by their values on objects by Corollary

. Thus, it suffices to prove that the appropriate ranks coincide on all objects of
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{T}
.

By Theorem
5.11
,
\operatorname{rk}\nolimits[X]=\operatorname{rk}\nolimits[M]-\operatorname{rk}\nolimits[N]
is constant on all identity morphisms and all indecomposable morphisms

in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}}
. Thus we may write
\dim_{k}M(x)-\dim_{k}N(x)=K
for all
x\in\mathcal{P}
, and
\operatorname{rk}\nolimits[M](x<y)-\operatorname{rk}\nolimits[N](x<y)=T
for all indecomposable relations
x<y
in
\mathcal{T}
, where
K
and
T
are some fixed non-negative integers. Set
D=K-T
. Thus for an object
(x,y)\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{T}}
,

\displaystyle\dim_{k}K_{M}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{rk}\nolimits[M](x\leq x)-\operatorname{rk}\nolimits[M](x<y)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{rk}\nolimits[N](x\leq x)-\operatorname{rk}\nolimits[N](x<y)+K-T

\displaystyle=\dim_{k}K_{N}(x,y)+D.

Since both
K_{M}
and
K_{N}
are virtually trivial, it follows that
[K_{M}]=[K_{N}]+[\underline{D}]
.
This proves the statement for
K_{M}
and
K_{N}
.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\dim_{k}C_{M}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{rk}\nolimits[M](y\leq y)-\operatorname{rk}\nolimits[M](x<y)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{rk}\nolimits[N](y\leq y)-\operatorname{rk}\nolimits[N](x<y)+K-T

\displaystyle=\dim_{k}C_{N}(x,y)+D.

By the same argument as above
[C_{M}]=[C_{N}]+[\underline{D}]
, as claimed.

Recall that for any ring
A
one has a ring homomorphism

\Upsilon_{A}\colon\EuR{Gr}(A)\to\EuR{Gr}_{e}(A),

where
\EuR{Gr}_{e}(A)
is the quotient of
\EuR{Gr}(A)
by the relation
[K]-[M]+[C]
, if
M
is an extension of
C
by
K
(See Definition
2.10
). Since
\phi^{*}
and
\beta^{*}
are exact functors, they induce group homomorphisms

\phi_{e}^{*},\beta_{e}^{*}\colon\EuR{Gr}_{e}(k\mathcal{P})\to\EuR{Gr}_{e}(k\widehat{\mathcal{P}}),

such that
\Upsilon_{k\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}\circ\phi^{*}=\phi_{e}^{*}\circ\Upsilon_{k\mathcal{P}}
, and similarly for
\beta^{*}
.
Thus, one may define

\nabla_{e}\colon\EuR{Gr}_{e}(k\mathcal{P})\to\EuR{Gr}_{e}(k\widehat{\mathcal{P}})

by
\nabla_{e}\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\phi^{*}_{e}-\beta^{*}_{e}
. The following proposition shows that a significant amount of information about modules is lost upon passing to the reduced Grothendieck groups.

Corollary 5.15
.

For any
M\in k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
, one has

\nabla_{e}[M]=[C_{M}]-[K_{M}]

in
\EuR{Gr}_{e}(k\widehat{\mathcal{P}})
.

Proof.

For each
M\in k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
let
I_{M}\in k\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
denote the image functor, i.e.
I_{M}(u,v)\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\operatorname{Im}(M(u<v))
. Then we obtain two short exact sequences in
k\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}

0\to K_{M}\to\beta^{*}M\to I_{M}\to 0\quad\text{and}\quad 0\to I_{M}\to\phi^{*}M\to C_{M}\to 0.

It follows that in
\EuR{Gr}_{e}(k\widehat{\mathcal{P}})
,

[\beta_{e}^{*}M]=[K_{M}]+[I_{M}]\quad\text{and}\quad[\phi_{e}^{*}M]=[I_{M}]+[C_{M}],

so
\nabla_{e}[M]\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}[\phi_{e}^{*}M]-[\beta_{e}^{*}M]=[C_{M}]-[K_{M}]
.
∎

Remark 5.16
.

The functor
K_{M}\in k\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
evaluated at an object
(x,y)
returns the subspace of elements that are “present” at
x
, but do not “survive” to
y
. Thus for a fixed
x\in\mathcal{P}
, the intersection
\bigcap_{(x,y)\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}K_{M}(x,y)
is the subspace of
M(x)
of all elements that “die” at
x
.

Similarly, the functor
C_{M}\in k\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
, evaluated at an object
(x,y)
returns the quotient of
M(y)
by the image of
M(x<y)
. For a fixed object
y
, consider the system of all homomorphisms
M(y)\to C_{M}(x,y)
for all
(x,y)\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
. The coequaliser of this system can be thought of as the space representing all elements in
M(y)
that are “born” at
y
. The coequaliser is easily seen to be the quotient of
M(y)
by the image of the composite

\bigoplus_{(x,y)}M(x)\xrightarrow{\oplus M(x<y)}\bigoplus_{(x,y)}M(y)\xrightarrow{\sum}M(y),

where
\Sigma
is the map given by summing coordinates.

Thus if
\mathcal{P}
is line connected and
M,N\in k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
are modules with equal gradients, then for any line connected maximal tree
\mathcal{T}
, spaces of “births” and “deaths” of
M
and
N
restricted to
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}}
object-wise coincide.

a_{2,2}
in terms of the other variables in two ways, and by comparing them we obtain the relation

wxtu=zyvs

that must be satisfied for
\Theta_{0}
to be well defined. Since this relation is not satisfied in general, this shows that there exist modules
M,N\in k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
with the same rank invariant, such that
\nabla[M]\neq\nabla[N]
(and hence also
[M]\neq[N]
).

6.
The Hom pairing and the Euler pairing

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a finite poset. The Hom pairing of Definition
4.1
and the Euler pairing of Definition
4.2
in the case of
k\mathcal{P}
-modules take the form

\langle[M],[N]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\dim_{k}(\mathrm{Hom}_{k\mathcal{P}}(M,N))\quad\text{and}\quad\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([M],[N])\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\chi(\mathrm{Ext}^{*}_{k\mathcal{P}}(M,N)),

with
M,N\in k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
. Since
\mathrm{Hom}
and
\mathrm{Ext}
commute with finite direct sums, the pairings can be extended to arbitrary elements in the respective Grothendieck groups.
Notice that
\mathrm{Ext}_{k\mathcal{P}}^{i}(M,N)=0
for all
i>0
if
M
is projective or if
N
is injective. In either case the two pairings coincide.

For a finite poset
\mathcal{P}
, and an object
v\in\mathcal{P}
, consider the modules
F_{v},G_{v},S_{v}\in k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
, defined on objects by

•

F_{v}(u)\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}k\operatorname{Mor}_{\mathcal{P}}(v,u)=\begin{cases}k&v\leq u\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

•

G_{v}(u)\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}k\operatorname{Mor}_{\mathcal{P}}(u,v)=\begin{cases}k&u\leq v\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

•

S_{v}(u)\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\begin{cases}k&u=v\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

If
v\leq u\leq u^{\prime}
, then
F_{v}(u)\to F_{v}(u^{\prime})
is the identity, and otherwise
F_{v}(u\leq u^{\prime})
is the zero homomorphism. Similarly, if
u\leq u^{\prime}\leq v
then
G_{v}(u)\to G_{v}(u^{\prime})
is the identity and otherwise it is
0
. For
S_{v}
all non-identity morphisms are
0
. By
[
4
, Proposition 2.2.3]
the modules
F_{v}
are precisely the indecomposable projective modules and the modules
G_{v}
are the indecomposable injective modules in
k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
. By
[
15
, Corollary 4.2]
the modules
S_{v}
are the simple modules in
k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
. Notice that
F_{v}
is locally constant on
\mathcal{P}_{\geq v}
, while
G_{v}
is locally constant on
\mathcal{P}_{\leq v}
. Also, any virtually trivial module
M
is a direct sum of simple modules,

M=\bigoplus_{v\in\mathcal{P}\atop M(v)\neq 0}\dim_{k}M(v)\cdot S_{v}.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a finite poset and let
F_{v}
and
G_{v}
be the indecomposable projective and the indecomposable injective modules determined by
v
. Let
[X]=[M]-[N]
, where
M,N\in k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
. Then,

(1)

\langle[F_{v}],[X]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}=\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([F_{v}],[X])=\dim_{k}M(v)-\dim_{k}N(v)
.
More generally, if
Q\in k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
is any finitely generated projective module, then

\langle[Q],[X]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}=\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([Q],[X])=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{P}}\epsilon_{v}(\dim_{k}M(v)-\dim_{k}N(v)),

where
Q\cong\bigoplus_{v\in\mathcal{P}}\epsilon_{v}F_{v}
, with
\epsilon_{v}\in\mathbb{N}
for each
v\in\mathcal{P}
.

(2)

\langle[X],[G_{v}]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}=\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([X],[G_{v}])=\dim_{k}M(v)-\dim_{k}N(v)
.
More generally, if
I\in k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
is any finitely generated injective module, then

\langle[X],[I]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}=\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([X],[I])=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{P}}\epsilon_{v}(\dim_{k}M(v)-\dim_{k}N(v)),

where
I\cong\bigoplus_{v\in\mathcal{P}}\epsilon_{v}G_{v}
, with
\epsilon_{v}\in\mathbb{N}
for each
v\in\mathcal{P}
.

Proof.

By projectivity of
F_{v}
,

\mathrm{Ext}^{i}_{k\mathcal{P}}(F_{v},M)=\mathrm{Ext}^{i}_{k\mathcal{P}}(F_{v},N)=0
for
i>0
, so

\displaystyle\langle[F_{v}],[X]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}=\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([F_{v}],[X])

\displaystyle\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\chi(\mathrm{Ext}^{*}_{k\mathcal{P}}(F_{v},M))-\chi(\mathrm{Ext}^{*}_{k\mathcal{P}}(F_{v},N))

\displaystyle=\dim_{k}\mathrm{Hom}_{k\mathcal{P}}(F_{v},M)-\dim_{k}\mathrm{Hom}_{k\mathcal{P}}(F_{v},N)

\displaystyle=\dim_{k}M(v)-\dim_{k}N(v).

If
Q\in k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
is any finitely generated projective module, then
Q\cong\bigoplus_{v\in\mathcal{P}}\epsilon_{v}F_{v}
, where
\epsilon_{v}\in\mathbb{N}
for each
v\in\mathcal{P}
. Thus

\displaystyle\langle[Q],[X]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}

\displaystyle=\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([Q],[X])

\displaystyle=\dim_{k}\mathrm{Hom}_{k\mathcal{P}}(Q,M)-\dim_{k}\mathrm{Hom}_{k\mathcal{P}}(Q,N)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{P}}\epsilon_{v}(\dim_{k}M(v)-\dim_{k}N(v)),

as claimed in (
1
).

Similarly,
\mathrm{Ext}^{i}_{k\mathcal{P}}(M,G_{v})=\mathrm{Ext}^{i}_{k\mathcal{P}}(N,G_{v})=0
for any
i>0
. Thus
\mathrm{Hom}_{k\mathcal{P}}(M,G_{v})\cong M(v)^{*}
, and
\mathrm{Hom}_{k\mathcal{P}}(N,G_{v})\cong N(v)^{*}
, so

\langle[X],[G_{v}]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}=\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([X],[G_{v}])=\dim_{k}M(v)-\dim_{k}N(v).

The second claim of (
2
) follows similarly.
∎

Corollary 6.2
.

Let
0\to M_{r}\to M_{r-1}\to\cdots\to M_{0}\to 0
be an exact sequence in
k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
. Let
P
be a finitely generated projective
k\mathcal{P}
-module and let
I
be a finitely generated injective
k\mathcal{P}
-module. Then

\sum_{i=0}^{r}(-1)^{i}\langle[P],[M_{i}]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}=\sum_{i=0}^{r}(-1)^{i}\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([P],[M_{i}])=0,\quad\text{and}\quad

\sum_{i=0}^{r}(-1)^{i}\langle[M_{i}],[I]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}=\sum_{i=0}^{r}(-1)^{i}\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([M_{i}],[I])=0.

Furthermore, let

0\to P_{n}\to\cdots\to P_{0}\to M\to 0,\quad\text{and}\quad 0\to M\to I_{0}\cdots\to I_{n}\to 0

be a projective resolution and an injective resolution of
M
in
k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
.
Then

\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([M],[M])=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{i+j}\langle[P_{i}],[P_{j}]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{i+j}\langle[I_{r}],[I_{s}]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}.

Proof.

By Lemma
6.1
, for each
0\leq i\leq r
, one has
\langle[P],[M_{i}]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}=\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([P],[M_{i}])
, and
\langle[M_{i}],[I]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}=\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([M_{i}],[I])
. Hence the sums in the first statement of the corollary are equal. Since
P
is projective the functor
\mathrm{Hom}_{k\mathcal{P}}(P,-)
is exact, and so applying it to the given exact sequence preserves exactness. Therefore the first alternating sum vanishes, and by equality so does the second. Similarly
\mathrm{Hom}_{k\mathcal{P}}(-,I)
is exact, since
I
is injective, and the second alternative sum vanishes by the same argument.

For the second statement, consider the given projective resolution on
M
. Applying the functor
\mathrm{Hom}_{k\mathcal{P}}(-,M)
we obtain the sequence

0\to\mathrm{Hom}_{k\mathcal{P}}(P_{0},M)\to\mathrm{Hom}_{k\mathcal{P}}(P_{1},M)\to\cdots\to\mathrm{Hom}_{k\mathcal{P}}(P_{n},M)\to 0

whose cohomology is
\mathrm{Ext}^{*}_{k\mathcal{P}}(M,M)
. Hence

\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([M],[M])=\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{i}\dim_{k}\mathrm{Hom}_{k\mathcal{P}}(P_{i},M)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{i}\langle[P_{i}],[M]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}.

By the first statement, for each
0\leq i\leq n
,

\langle[P_{i}],[M]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{j}\langle[P_{i}],[P_{j}]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}.

The second statement for the projective resolution follows. The corresponding equality of the injective resolution follows similarly.
∎

A main result of this section is the following proposition, which establishes an easy relation between the Euler pairing and the Hom pairing and allows explicit computation of the Euler pairing.

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a finite poset, and let
M,N\in k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
. Let
\langle-,-\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}
and
\chi_{\mathcal{P}}(-,-)
be the Hom pairing and the Euler pairing respectively. Let

0\to P_{n}\to\cdots\to P_{0}\to M\to 0,\quad\text{and}\quad 0\to N\to I_{0}\to\cdots I_{n}\to 0

be a projective resolution for
M
and an injective resolution for
N
.
Then

(10)

\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([M],[N])=\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{i}\langle[P_{i}],[N]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{i}\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([P_{i}],[N]),

and

(11)

\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([M],[N])=\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{j}\langle[M],[I_{j}]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{j}\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([M],[I_{j}]).

Furthermore, write
P_{i}\cong\bigoplus\epsilon^{i}_{v}F_{v}
and
I_{j}=\bigoplus\delta_{u}^{j}G_{u}
, with
\epsilon^{i}_{v},\delta^{j}_{u}\in\mathbb{N}
and
v,u\in\mathcal{P}
. Then

(12)

\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([M],[N])=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{i}\epsilon_{v}^{i}\dim_{k}N(v)=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{j}\delta_{u}^{j}\dim_{k}M(u).

Proof.

Apply
\mathrm{Hom}_{k\mathcal{P}}(-,N)
to the given projective resolution of
M
. The
i
-th term of the resulting cochain complex is
\mathrm{Hom}_{k\mathcal{P}}(P_{i},N)
and its
i
-th cohomology is
\mathrm{Ext}_{k\mathcal{P}}^{i}(M,N)
. Hence,

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([M],[N])

\displaystyle\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{i}\dim_{k}\mathrm{Ext}_{k\mathcal{P}}(P_{i},N)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{i}\dim_{k}\mathrm{Hom}_{k\mathcal{P}}(P_{i},N)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{i}\langle[P_{i}],[N]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{i}\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([P_{i}],[N]),

where the second equality follows by standard homological algebra,
the third equality follows by definition of the
\mathrm{Hom}
pairing, and the last equality follows from Lemma
6.1
(
1
).

Equation (
11
) follows by analogy and Lemma
6.1
(
2
).

By (
10
) and Lemma
6.1
(
1
),

\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([M],[N])=\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{i}\langle[P_{i}],[N]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{i}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{P}}\epsilon_{v}^{i}\dim_{k}N(v).

The first equality in Equation (
12
) follows by rearranging the summands. The second equality follows by analogy, using an injective resolution for
N
and Lemma
6.1
(
2
).
∎

A nice interpretation of the Euler pairing occurs for the constant module on
\mathcal{P}
with value
k
.

Example 6.4
.

Let
\underline{k}
denote the constant
k\mathcal{P}
-module with value
k
at each object and the identity map for each morphism. Then for each
M\in k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
,

\mathrm{Ext}^{*}_{k\mathcal{P}}(\underline{k},M)=H^{*}(\mathcal{P},M).

Thus
\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([\underline{k}],[M])=\chi(H^{*}(\mathcal{P},M))
. In particular
\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([\underline{k}],[\underline{k}])=\chi(|\mathcal{P}|)
, where
|\mathcal{P}|
denotes the nerve of
\mathcal{P}
.

Combining Lemma
6.1
and Example
6.4
, one observes that if
\mathcal{P}
has an initial object
\emptyset
, then
\underline{k}=F_{\emptyset}
is projective. In that case the positive degree cohomology of any
k\mathcal{P}
-module vanishes, and

\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([\underline{k}],[M])=\dim_{k}H^{0}(\mathcal{P},M)=\dim_{k}M(\emptyset).

Next we consider pairing with the simple modules
S_{v}
. We start with the Hom pairing.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a poset and let
v\in\mathcal{P}
be an object. Then for any module
N\in k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
,

\mathrm{Hom}_{k\mathcal{P}}(S_{v},N)\cong\bigcap_{v<w}\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits(N(v<w))\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits(N|_{\mathcal{P}_{>v}})

and

\mathrm{Hom}_{k\mathcal{P}}(N,S_{v})\cong N(v)\left/\operatorname{Im}\left(\bigoplus_{u<v}N(u)\to N(v)\right)\right.\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\mathrm{CoKer}(N|_{\mathcal{P}_{<v}})^{*}.

Proof.

Fix an object
v
and let
w>v
be another object in
\mathcal{P}
. Let
\phi\colon S_{v}\to N
be a morphism in
k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
. Then

0=\phi_{w}\circ S_{v}(v<w)=N(v<w)\circ\phi_{v}.

Hence
\operatorname{Im}(\phi_{v})\subseteq\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits(N(v<w))
. Since this holds for every
w>v
, it follows that

\operatorname{Im}(\phi_{v})\subseteq\bigcap_{v<w}\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits(N(v<w)),

and since
\phi
is completely determined by
\phi_{v}
, we have

\mathrm{Hom}_{k\mathcal{P}}(S_{v},N)\cong\mathrm{Hom}_{k}(k,\bigcap_{v<w}\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits(N(v<w)))\cong\bigcap_{v<w}\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits(N(v<w)).

This proves the first statement.

Let
\psi\colon N\to S_{v}
be a morphism in
k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
. Then
\psi
is determined by
\psi_{v}
, and for each
u<v
in
\mathcal{P}
one has
\psi_{v}\circ N(u<v)=S_{v}(u<v)\circ\psi_{u}=0
. Hence

\psi_{v}(\operatorname{Im}(N(u<v)\colon N(u)\to N(v)))=0.

Since this holds for each
u<v
, the homomorphism
\psi_{v}
vanishes on
\operatorname{Im}\left(\bigoplus_{u<v}N(u)\to N(v)\right)
and hence it factors through

N(v)/\operatorname{Im}\left(\bigoplus_{u<v}N(u)\to N(v)\right)\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\mathrm{CoKer}(N|_{\mathcal{P}_{<v}}).

Thus one may identify
\mathrm{Hom}_{k\mathcal{P}}(N,S_{v})
with the dual space of
\mathrm{CoKer}(N|_{\mathcal{P}_{<v}})
, which is the claim of the second statement.
∎

Next we consider the Euler pairing with simple modules.

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a finite poset and let
v\in\mathcal{P}
be an object, and let
S_{v}\in k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
be the simple module determined by
v
. Let
\pi_{v}\colon F_{v}\to S_{v}
and
\iota_{v}\colon S_{v}\to G_{v}
denote the obvious projection and injection, respectively. Let
K_{v}=\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits(\pi_{v})
, and let
C_{v}=\operatorname{coKer}\nolimits(\iota_{v})
. Then

\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([S_{v}],[M])=\dim_{k}M(v)-\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([K_{v}],[M]),

and

\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([M],[S_{v}])=\dim_{k}M(v)-\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([M],[C_{v}]).

In particular, if the Hasse diagram of the sub-poset
\mathcal{P}_{\geq v}
is a tree, then

\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([S_{v}],[M])=\dim_{k}M(v)-\sum_{v<u}\dim_{k}M(u),

and if the Hasse diagram for the sub-poset
\mathcal{P}_{\leq v}
is a tree, then

\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([M],[S_{v}])=\dim_{k}M(v)-\sum_{u<v}\dim_{k}M(u),

where in both cases the sum runs only over the indecomposable relations of the type specified.

Proof.

The module
K_{v}
is the submodule of
F_{v}
, which coincides with it on each object, except
v
on which it vanishes. Thus we have a short exact sequence in
k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}

0\to K_{v}\to F_{v}\to S_{v}\to 0

that gives rise to a long exact sequence of
\mathrm{Ext}
groups, upon applying
\mathrm{Hom}_{k\mathcal{P}}(-,M)
. By examining that long exact sequence, applying additivity of the Euler characteristic and using Lemma
6.1
(
1
) we conclude that

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([S_{v}],[M])

\displaystyle\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\chi(\mathrm{Ext}^{*}_{k\mathcal{P}}(S_{v},M))

\displaystyle=\chi(\mathrm{Ext}^{*}_{k\mathcal{P}}(F_{v},M))-\chi(\mathrm{Ext}^{*}_{k\mathcal{P}}(K_{v},M))

\displaystyle=\dim_{k}M(v)-\chi(\mathrm{Ext}^{*}_{k\mathcal{P}}(K_{v},M))

\displaystyle=\dim_{k}M(v)-\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([K_{v}],[M]).

Similarly, using the exact sequence
0\to S_{v}\to G_{v}\to C_{v}\to 0
and Lemma
6.1
(
2
), we have

\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([M],[S_{v}])=\dim_{k}M(v)-\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([M],[C_{v}]).

In cases where the Hasse diagram of
\mathcal{P}_{\geq v}
is a tree, the module
K_{v}
can be easily seen to be a projective module

K_{v}=\bigoplus_{v<u}F_{u},

where the sum runs over all indecomposable relations
v<u
in
\mathcal{P}
.
Therefore, in that case by Lemma
6.1
(
1
),

\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([S_{v}],[M])=\dim_{k}M(v)-\sum_{v<u}\dim_{k}M(u).

Similarly, if the Hasse diagram for
\mathcal{P}_{\leq v}
is a tree, then
C_{v}
is a sum of indecomposable injective modules, indexed by the indecomposable relations
u<v
. The corresponding statement follows by Lemma
6.1
(
2
).
∎

We end this section with a discussion of some general properties of the Hom and Euler pairings. Notice first that the Hom pairing is non-degenerate on classes of genuine modules, because for any
M\in k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
,

\langle[M],[M]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}=\dim_{k}\mathrm{Hom}_{k\mathcal{P}}(M,M)\geq 1,

since any multiple of the identity natural transformation by a field element is nontrivial. However, it is easy to find examples of nonzero virtual modules whose Hom pairing is zero, as we next observe.

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a finite poset, and let
u,v\in\mathcal{P}
be any two objects. Then

(1)

\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([F_{v}],[F_{u}])=\langle[F_{v}],[F_{u}]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}=\begin{cases}1&v\geq u\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(2)

\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([G_{v}],[G_{u}])=\langle[G_{v}],[G_{u}]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}=\begin{cases}1&v\leq u\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Proof.

Both statements are particular cases of Lemma
6.1
. We prove the first statement. The second follows by analogy. One has

\langle[F_{v}],[F_{u}]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}=\dim_{k}F_{u}(v)=\begin{cases}1&v\geq u\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

as claimed. ∎

The following immediate corollary of Lemma
6.7
shows that both the Hom pairing and the Euler pairing are degenerate on virtual modules.

Corollary 6.8
.

Let
u,v\in\mathcal{P}
be two non-comparable objects. Let
[X]=[F_{v}]-[F_{u}]
and
[Y]=[G_{v}]-[G_{u}]
. Then

\langle[X],[X]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}=\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([X],[X])=\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([Y],[Y])=\langle[Y],[Y]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}=0.

Similarly, it is easy to observe that
\langle[S_{v}],[S_{u}]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}=1
if and only if
u=v
and is
0
otherwise. This is not the case for the Euler pairing. In that case, assuming
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
is a tree, we have by Lemma
6.6
,

\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([S_{v}],[S_{u}])=\dim_{k}S_{u}(v)-\sum_{v<w}\dim_{k}S_{u}(w)=\begin{cases}\phantom{-}1&v=u\\
-1&v<u\;\text{indecomposable}\\
\phantom{-}0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Finally, we consider the Euler pairing
\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([M],[M])
for modules
M\in k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
. Assume that the Hasse diagram
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
of the poset
\mathcal{P}
is a tree, and thus in particular an acyclic quiver. In that case, by Lemma
4.5
for any
M,N\in k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}

\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([M],[N])=\chi_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}}(\mathbf{dim}_{k}[M],\mathbf{dim}_{k}[N]),

where
\mathbf{dim}_{k}\colon\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{P})\to\mathbb{Z}^{V}
is the dimension vector homomorphism (Definition
2.11
), and
\chi_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}}
is the Euler form for the digraph
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
(Definition
4.4
). Then the Euler pairing
\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([M],[M])
coincides with the Tits form
T_{\mathcal{P}}\colon\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{P})\to\mathbb{Z}
,
[
4
, Definition 4.1.2]
. By
[
14
, Theorem 8.6]
the Tits form is positive definite if and only if
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
is of Dynkin type ADE, and positive semi-definite if and only if
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
is of extended type ADE. This corresponds to
\mathcal{P}
being of finite and tame representation type, respectively. In general however,
T_{\mathcal{P}}
, and hence the Euler pairing, is indefinite.

7.
Divergence, adjointness and the Laplacian

In this section we define and study the basic properties of the divergence and the Laplacian for modules over finite posets.
Recall Definition
3.8

Definition 7.1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a poset. The
left and right divergence
are defined, respectively, to be the homomorphisms

\nabla^{*},\nabla_{*}\colon\EuR{Gr}(k\widehat{\mathcal{P}})\to\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{P})

given by
\nabla^{*}[N]\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}[L_{\phi}(N)]-[L_{\beta}(N)]
, and
\nabla_{*}[N]\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}[R_{\phi}(N)]-[R_{\beta}(N)]
.

For
[X]\in\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{P})
and
[Y]\in\EuR{Gr}(k\widehat{\mathcal{P}})
, we have the adjointness relations with respect to the Hom pairing:

\langle\nabla^{*}[Y],[X]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}=\langle[Y],\nabla[X]\rangle_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}\quad\text{and}\quad\langle[X],\nabla_{*}[Y]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}=\langle\nabla[X],[Y]\rangle_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}

The following corollary is an immediate consequence of Proposition
6.3
, which is as close as we can get to adjointness relations with respect to the Euler Pairing.

Corollary 7.2
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a finite poset such that
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
is a tree. Let
M\in k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
be a module, and let

0\to Q\to P\to M\to 0,\quad\text{and}\quad 0\to M\to I\to J\to 0

be a projective resolution and an injective resolution for
M
in
k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
. Then for any
N\in k\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
,

\chi_{\mathcal{P}}(\nabla^{*}[N],[M])=\langle\nabla^{*}[N],[I]-[J]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}=\langle[N],\nabla([I]-[J])\rangle_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}

and

\chi_{\mathcal{P}}([M],\nabla_{*}[N])=\langle[P]-[Q],\nabla_{*}[N]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}=\langle\nabla([P]-[Q]),[N]\rangle_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}.

The following example motivates referring to
\nabla^{*}
and
\nabla_{*}
as divergence.

Example 7.3
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be the poset whose Hasse diagram is shown on the left below, with its line digraph on the right.

\textstyle{3}
\textstyle{4}
\textstyle{03}
\textstyle{04}
\textstyle{0\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{1\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{2\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{10\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{20\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

We compute the left and right divergence of a module
M\in k\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
at the object
0
.

Starting with left divergence, notice that
\phi\downarrow 0
is the discrete category with objects
10
and
20
, while
\beta\downarrow 0
is isomorphic to
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
. Hence
L_{\phi}M(0)\cong M(10)\oplus M(20)
, and

L_{\beta}M(0)\cong\mathop{\textup{colim}}\limits_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}M.

The computation of this colimit is elementary and can be shown to be isomorphic to the cokernel of the map

(13)

M(10)\oplus M(20)\to M(03)\oplus M(04)

that takes an element
(x,z)
to
(M_{103}(x)+M_{203}(z),-M_{104}(x)-M_{204}(z))
,
where
M_{xyz}
stands for
M(xy<yz)
for short.

The computation of right divergence is similarly basic. Here we have
0\downarrow\phi
isomorphic to
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
and
0\downarrow\beta
the discrete category on objects
03
and
04
. Hence
R_{\beta}M(0)\cong M(03)\oplus M(04)
, and

R_{\phi}M(0)\cong\mathop{\textup{lim}}\limits_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}M.

The computation of the limit is again elementary, and can be shown to be the kernel of the same map as in (
13
). Thus we have an exact sequence

0\to R_{\phi}M(0)\to M(10)\oplus M(20)\to M(03)\oplus M(04)\to L_{\beta}M(0)\to 0.

Consider the values of these functors in terms of “flow” relative to the object
0
. Thus one may think of
L_{\phi}M(0)\cong M(10)\oplus M(20)
as the in-flow at
0
, and of
L_{\beta}M(0)
as the quotient of the out-flow at
0
, where the “effect” (image) of the in-flow has been divided out, or in other words, the net out-flow at
0
. Similarly,
R_{\beta}M(0)\cong M(03)\oplus M(04)
can be thought of as the out-flow at
0
, while
R_{\phi}M(0)
is the flow that is “wasted” (vanishes) on the way to
0
.

Thus
\mathbf{dim}_{k}\nabla^{*}[M](0)<0
indicates that the net out-flow at
0
is larger than the in-flow, or that passing through
0
“amplifies” the flow. Similar interpretations can be given for
\mathbf{dim}_{k}\nabla^{*}[M](0)>0
and in the corresponding situations for
\nabla_{*}[M](0)
.

A nice case of Lemma
6.1
occurs when pairing a projective module with the gradient of a module.

Example 7.4
.

Let
(u,v)\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
be an object. Then

\langle F_{(u,v)},\nabla[M]\rangle_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}=\langle F_{(u,v)},\phi^{*}[M]\rangle_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}-\langle F_{(u,v)},\beta^{*}[M]\rangle_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}=\dim_{k}M(v)-\dim_{k}M(u).

Recall the modules
K_{M},C_{M}\in k\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
defined for any
M\in k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
as the kernel and cokernel of the natural transformation
\eta\colon\beta^{*}\to\phi^{*}
(See Lemma
5.13
).

Proposition 7.5
.

Let
M\in k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
and let
N\in k\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
. Then

\chi_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}(\nabla[M],[N])=\chi_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}([C_{M}],[N])-\chi_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}([K_{M}],[N]),

and

\chi_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}([N],\nabla[M])=\chi_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}([N],[C_{M}])-\chi_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}([N],[K_{M}]).

Proof.

The modules
K_{M}
and
C_{M}
are defined as the kernel and cokernel of the natural transformation
\eta\colon\beta^{*}\to\phi^{*}
that takes an object
(x,y)\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
to the morphism

\beta^{*}M(x,y)=M(x)\xrightarrow{M(x<y)}M(y)=\phi^{*}M(x,y).

Thus, one has an exact sequence of
k\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
-modules

0\to K_{M}\to\beta^{*}M\xrightarrow{\eta_{M}}\phi^{*}M\to C_{M}\to 0,

which can be split into two short exact sequences in
k\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}

(14)

0\to K_{M}\to\beta^{*}M\to I_{M}\to 0\quad\text{and}\quad 0\to I_{M}\to\phi^{*}M\to C_{M}\to 0,

where
I_{M}
is the image functor. By applying
\mathrm{Hom}_{k\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}(-,N)
, these give long exact
\mathrm{Ext}
sequences and it follows by additivity of the Euler characteristic, that

\chi(\mathrm{Ext}_{k\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}^{*}(\beta^{*}M,N))=\chi(\mathrm{Ext}_{k\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}^{*}(K_{M},N))+\chi(\mathrm{Ext}_{k\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}^{*}(I_{M},N))

and that

\chi(\mathrm{Ext}_{k\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}^{*}(\phi^{*}M,N))=\chi(\mathrm{Ext}_{k\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}^{*}(C_{M},N))+\chi(\mathrm{Ext}_{k\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}^{*}(I_{M},N)).

Thus

\displaystyle\chi_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}(\nabla[M],[N])

\displaystyle=\chi_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}([\phi^{*}M],[N])-\chi_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}([\beta^{*}M],[N])

\displaystyle=\chi_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}([C_{M}],[N])-\chi_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}([K_{M}],[N]).

The second statement follows similarly, by applying
\mathrm{Hom}_{k\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}(N,-)
to the short exact sequences (
14
).
∎

Corollary 7.6
.

Let
M\in k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
and let
N\in k\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
. Then

\displaystyle\chi_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}(\nabla[M],[N])=\sum_{(u,v)\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\atop C_{M}(u,v)\neq 0}\dim_{k}C_{M}(u,v)\cdot\chi_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}([S_{u,v}],[N])

\displaystyle-\sum_{(u,v)\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\atop K_{M}(u,v)\neq 0}\dim_{k}K_{M}(u,v)\cdot\chi_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}([S_{u,v}],[N]),

and

\displaystyle\chi_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}([N],\nabla[M])=\sum_{(u,v)\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\atop C_{M}(u,v)\neq 0}\dim_{k}C_{M}(u,v)\cdot\chi_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}([N],[S_{u,v}])

\displaystyle-\sum_{(u,v)\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\atop K_{M}(u,v)\neq 0}\dim_{k}K_{M}(u,v)\cdot\chi_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}([N],[S_{u,v}]).

Proof.

By Lemma
5.13
both
K_{M}
and
C_{M}
are virtually trivial. Hence they are isomorphic to a direct sum of simple modules. Thus

[K_{M}]=\sum_{(u,v)\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\atop K_{M}(u,v)\neq 0}\dim_{k}K_{M}(u,v)\cdot[S_{u,v}],\quad\text{and}\quad C_{M}=\sum_{(u,v)\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\atop C_{M}(u,v)\neq 0}\dim_{k}C_{M}(u,v)\cdot[S_{u,v}].

The claim follows from bilinearity of
\chi_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}
.
∎

Notice that by Lemma
6.6
,
\chi_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}(\nabla[M],[N])
is computable in the case where the Hasse diagram for
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\geq(u,v)}
is a tree for each
(u,v)\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
, for which
C_{M}(u,v)
and
K_{M}(u,v)
are nonzero. A similar comment applies to computability of
\chi_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}([N],\nabla[M])
. In particular if
\mathcal{P}
is a poset such that
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
is a tree, then so is
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{P}}
.

Lemma 7.7
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a finite poset such that
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
is a tree. Then so is
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{P}}
.

Proof.

If
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{P}}
is not a tree, then there is a pair of objects
(x,y),(s,t)\in\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{P}}
with at least two directed paths from
(x,y)
to
(s,t)
in
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{P}}
. But this implies that there are at least two paths from
y
to
s
in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
. Hence
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
is not a tree, contradicting our assumption.
∎

A consequence of Lemma
7.7
is that if
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
is a tree then the Euler pairing of
\nabla[M]
for any
M\in k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
with any
N\in k\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
can be computed explicitly. Next we examine an implication of vanishing left divergence in the case where
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
is a
rooted tree
, i.e. where
\mathcal{P}
has a unique minimal object.

Proposition 7.8
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a finite poset such that its Hasse diagram
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
is a rooted tree. Let
[X]=[U]-[V]\in\EuR{Gr}(k\widehat{\mathcal{P}})
be any element, with
U,V\in k\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
, such that
\nabla^{*}[X]=0
. Then
\dim_{k}U(x,y)=\dim_{k}V(x,y)
for all
(x,y)\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
.

Proof.

Let
x_{0}
denote the unique minimal object in
\mathcal{P}
. For any
z\in\mathcal{P}
there is a unique path from
x_{0}
to
z
, and since
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
is a tree, it is the longest path in
\mathcal{P}
that ends in
z
. Furthermore, the assumption that
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
is a rooted tree implies that the in-degree at each vertex in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
except
x_{0}
is exactly
1
. Fix an object
(x,y)\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
, and let
n=d(x_{0},y)
, where
d(-,-)
denotes the directed path distance function on
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
. Let
G_{(x,y)}
be the indecomposable injective
k\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
-module determined by the object
(x,y)
(see proof of Lemma
). We shall define a module
M\in k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
such that
\nabla[M]=[G_{(x,y)}]
. Having done this, it follows that

\displaystyle\dim_{k}U(x,y)=

\displaystyle\langle[U],[G_{(x,y)}]\rangle_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}=\langle[U],\nabla[M]\rangle_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}=

\displaystyle\langle\nabla^{*}[U],[M]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}=\langle\nabla^{*}[V],[M]\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}=

\displaystyle\langle[V],\nabla[M]\rangle_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}=\langle[V],[G_{(x,y)}]\rangle_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}=\dim_{k}V(x,y),

where the first and last equalities follow from injectivity of
G_{(x,y)}
in
k\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
and Lemma
6.1
. The third and sixth equalities follow by adjointness.

It remains to define the module
M
and show that it has the required property. The definition will be carried out in three steps. In the first step we define
M
on the down-set
\mathcal{P}_{\leq y}
. In the second step we define it on each branch emanating from an object
z\in\mathcal{P}_{\leq y}
. Finally in the third step we show that
\nabla[M]=[G_{(x,y)}]
.

Step 1.
Since
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
is a rooted tree,
\mathcal{P}_{\leq y}
is the unique path from
x_{0}
to
y
. Set
M(y)=k^{n}
, and for any
v\in\mathcal{P}_{\leq y}
with
d(v,y)=r
in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
, let
M(v)=k^{n-r}
. This is well defined since
\mathcal{P}_{\leq y}
is a linear order. If
u<v
is an indecomposable relation in
\mathcal{P}_{\leq y}
, then
d(u,y)=d(v,y)+1
. Define
M(u<v)
to be the inclusion

M(u<v)\colon M(u)=k^{n-r-1}\to k^{n-r}=M(v)

into the last
n-r-1
coordinates. In matrix notation, with respect the the standard basis, one may write
M(u<v)
as the
(n-r)\times(n-r-1)
matrix

A_{u<v}=\left(\begin{matrix}0\cdots 0\\
\hline\cr I_{n-r-1}\end{matrix}\right)

Step 2.
Let
x_{0}\neq z\in\mathcal{P}_{\leq y}
be any object, and let
\mathcal{P}_{z}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{\geq z}
denote the sub-poset generated by all objects in
\mathcal{P}_{\geq z}
except those which form the branch emanating from
z
that contains
y
, if
z\neq y
, and if
z=y
set
\mathcal{P}_{y}=\mathcal{P}_{\geq y}
. Then the Hasse diagram of
\mathcal{P}_{z}
is a rooted tree with root
z
that intersects with
\mathcal{P}_{\leq y}
only on the vertex
z
. For each object
w\in\mathcal{P}_{z}
, define
M(w)=M(z)
.
Let
t<z
be the (unique) in-neighbour of
z
in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
, and let
d(z,y)=r-1
. For any indecomposable relation
u<v
in
\mathcal{P}_{z}
, define

M(u<v)\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}M(t<z)\oplus 0.

Again, in matrix notation
M(u<v)
in this case is represented by the
(n-r)\times(n-r)
matrix

B_{u<v}=\left(\begin{tabular}[]{c|c}$A_{t<z}$&$0$\\
\end{tabular}\right)=\left(\begin{tabular}[]{c|c}$0\cdots 0$&$0$\\
\hline\cr$I_{n-r-1}$&$0$\end{tabular}\right)

If
z=x_{0}
, then
M(x_{0})=0
, by definition, and for any object
w\in\mathcal{P}_{x_{0}}
define
M(w)=0
.

Step 3.
We are now ready to analyse
\beta^{*}M
and
\phi^{*}M
. By definition we have

\beta^{*}M(u,v)=M(u)=\begin{cases}k^{n-r}&d(u,y)=r\\
M(z)&u\notin\mathcal{P}_{\leq y},\ z\in\mathcal{P}_{\leq y},\ d(z,u)\text{ minimal}\end{cases}

Similarly,

\phi^{*}M(u,v)=M(v)=\begin{cases}k^{n-r}&d(v,y)=r\\
M(z)&v\notin\mathcal{P}_{\leq y},\ z\in\mathcal{P}_{\leq y},\ d(z,u)\text{ minimal}\end{cases}

Let
(u,v)\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\leq(x,y)}
. Then, either
(u,v)=(x,y)
or
v\leq x
. In the first case
\beta^{*}M(x,y)=M(x)=k^{n-1}
, while
\phi^{*}M(x,y)=M(y)=k^{n}
. In the second case,
\beta^{*}M(u,v)=M(u)=k^{n-d(u,y)}
, while
\phi^{*}M(u,v)=M(v)=k^{n-d(v,y)}=k^{n-d(u,y)+1}
. Thus by definition, for any
(u,v)\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\leq(x,y)}
one has
\beta^{*}M(u,v)\oplus G_{(x,y)}\cong\phi^{*}M(u,v)
.
Let
(u,v)<(v,w)
be an indecomposable morphism in
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\leq(x,y)}
, and let
d(u,y)=r+1
for some
r\geq 1
. Then

\beta^{*}M((u,v)<(v,w))=M(u<v)

is represented by the
(n-r)\times(n-r-1)
matrix

\left(\begin{matrix}0\cdots 0\\
\hline\cr I_{n-r-1}\end{matrix}\right)
, and so

(\beta^{*}M\oplus G_{(x,y)})((u,v)<(v,w))=M(u<v)\oplus 1_{r}

is represented by the
(n-r+1)\times(n-r)
matrix

\left(\begin{tabular}[]{c|c}$0\cdots 0$&$0$\\
\hline\cr$I_{n-r-1}$&$0$\\
\hline\cr$0\cdots 0$&1\end{tabular}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}0\cdots 0\\
\hline\cr I_{n-r}\end{matrix}\right),

which also represents
\phi^{*}M((u,v)<(v,w))=M(v<w)
.
Thus we see that

(\beta^{*}M\oplus G_{(x,y)})|_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\leq(x,y)}}\cong\phi^{*}M|_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\leq(x,y)}}

as modules.

Let
x_{0}\neq z\in\mathcal{P}_{\leq y}
, let
t<z
be the unique in-neighbour of
z
, and let
d(t,y)=r+1
, where
r\geq 0
. Let
\mathcal{P}_{z}^{+}\subset\mathcal{P}
denote the sub-poset generated by
\mathcal{P}_{z}
and
t
. Thus, the Hasse diagram
\mathcal{H}^{+}_{z}
of
\mathcal{P}^{+}_{z}
consists of the edge
t<z
union with the Hasse diagram
\mathcal{H}_{z}
of
\mathcal{P}_{z}
. Hence
\mathcal{H}_{z}^{+}
is a rooted tree with root
t
.

By definition
M|_{\mathcal{P}_{z}^{+}}
obtains the value
M(t)
on
t
and for each
t\neq w\in\mathcal{P}_{z}^{+}
it obtains the value
M(z)\cong M(t)\oplus k
. Furthermore, the homomorphism
M(t<z)
is represented by the
(n-r)\times(n-r-1)
matrix

A_{t<z}=\left(\begin{matrix}0\cdots 0\\
\hline\cr I_{n-r-1}\end{matrix}\right)
,
and for any other indecomposable relation
u<v
in
\mathcal{P}_{z}\subset\mathcal{P}_{z}^{+}
the representing matrix for
M(u<v)
is the
(n-r)\times(n-r)
matrix

B_{u<v}=\left(\begin{tabular}[]{c|c}$0\cdots 0$&$0$\\
\hline\cr$I_{n-r-1}$&$0$\end{tabular}\right)
.

The restriction of
G_{(x,y)}
to
\widehat{\mathcal{P}_{z}^{+}}
obtains the value
k
on the vertex
(t,z)
, since
(t,z)\leq(x,y)
, and
0
on all other vertices. The restriction of
\beta^{*}M
to
\widehat{\mathcal{P}_{z}^{+}}
take the value
M(t)
on
(t,z)
and
M(z)
everywhere else, while the restriction of
\phi^{*}M
to
\widehat{\mathcal{P}_{z}^{+}}
obtains the value
M(z)\cong M(t)\oplus k
on all objects there. Hence

(\beta^{*}M\oplus G_{(x,y)})|_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}_{z}^{+}}}\cong\phi^{*}M|_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}_{z}^{+}}}

on objects. Next, examine these modules on morphisms. Let
u
be an out-neighbour of
z
, such that
u\nleq y
. Then

(\beta^{*}M\oplus G_{(x,y)})(t,z)=M(t)\oplus k,\quad(\beta^{*}M\oplus G_{(x,y)})(z,u)=M(z)\oplus 0,

and
(\beta^{*}M\oplus G_{(x,y)})((t,z)<(z,u))
is represented by the matrix
\left(\begin{tabular}[]{c|c}$A_{t<z}$&$0$\\
\end{tabular}\right)=B_{z<u}
, which by definition also represents the homomorphism
\phi^{*}M((t,z)<(z,u))
.
On any other indecomposable relation in
\widehat{\mathcal{P}_{z}^{+}}
the modules
(\beta^{*}M\oplus G_{(x,y)})
and
\phi^{*}M
also coincide. Hence

(\beta^{*}M\oplus G_{(x,y)})|_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}_{z}^{+}}}\cong\phi^{*}M|_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}_{z}^{+}}}

as modules.

Next notice that, for any
x_{0}\neq z\in\mathcal{P}_{\leq y}
, the sub-posets
\widehat{\mathcal{P}_{z}^{+}}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\leq(x,y)}
have only the object
(t,z)
in common and no morphisms, and on that object
\beta^{*}M\oplus G_{(x,y)}
and
\phi^{*}M
take the same value. Hence, the restrictions of
\beta^{*}M\oplus G_{(x,y)}
and
\phi^{*}M
to the connected component that contains
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\leq(x,y)}
are isomorphic.

If
(u,v)\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
is an object such that
u\nleq y
and the nearest object to
u
in
\mathcal{P}_{\leq y}
is
x_{0}
, then in
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
this object is on a connected component that is disjoint from the component of
(x,y)
. By definition
M(u)=M(v)=0
, and so
\beta^{*}M\oplus G_{(x,y)}
and
\phi^{*}(M)
coincide on
(u,v)
.

This shows that
\beta^{*}M(u,v)\oplus G_{(x,y)}\cong\phi^{*}M(u,v)
on the full line poset
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
. Thus, in
\EuR{Gr}(k\widehat{\mathcal{P}})
,

\nabla[M]\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}[\phi^{*}M]-[\beta^{*}M]=[G_{(x,y)}].

This completes the proof.
∎

An analogous statement can be made for elements in
\EuR{Gr}(k\widehat{\mathcal{P}})
of vanishing right divergence. In that case one needs to consider posets that have a unique maximal object and projective modules
F_{(x,y)}
rather than injective. We leave the details for the reader.

Notice also that in the appropriate sense Proposition
7.8
says that the module
M
constructed in its proof is the “integral” of the injective module
G_{(x,y)}
. It is also easy to see that this integral is defined up to a constant summand. Further discussion of integration will appear in future studies.

We also point out that the assumption that
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
is a rooted tree can be relaxed in many cases. The only place where this assumption is used is in the definition of
M
outside
\mathcal{P}_{\leq y}
, which requires that any object there has a well defined distance from
\mathcal{P}_{\leq y}
.

Next we prove Theorem
C
, which gives a formula for the computation of left and right divergence on
k\mathcal{P}
-modules, under the assumption that the Hasse diagram
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
is a tree. For any
y\in\mathcal{P}
let
\mathcal{N}_{y}
denote the
neighbourhood of
y
in
\mathcal{P}
, i.e. the subgraph of
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
generated by
y
and all its in- and out-neighbours.

Theorem 7.9
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a finite poset whose Hasse diagram is a tree, and let
N\in k\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
. For any object
y\in\mathcal{P}
let
\mathcal{N}_{y}
denote the neighbourhood of
y
in
\mathcal{P}
, and let
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{y}
be the associated line poset. Then the following statements hold.

(1)

The left divergence
\nabla^{*}[N]=[L_{\phi}(N)]-[L_{\beta}(N)]
, where the left Kan extensions are given by

L_{\phi}(N)(y)\cong\bigoplus_{(u,y)\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}N(u,y),\quad{and}\quad L_{\beta}(N)(y)\cong\mathop{\textup{colim}}\limits_{\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{y}}N|_{\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{y}}.

(2)

The right divergence
\nabla_{*}[N]=[R_{\phi}(N)]-[R_{\beta}(N)]
, where the right Kan extensions are given by

R_{\phi}(N)(y)\cong\mathop{\textup{lim}}\limits_{\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{y}}N|_{\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{y}},\quad\text{and}\quad R_{\beta}(N)(y)\cong\bigoplus_{(y,v)\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}N(y,v).

The proof is carried out in four steps. In Step 1 we analyse the categories
\phi\downarrow y
and
y\downarrow\beta
. In Step 2 we study the categories
\beta\downarrow y
and
y\downarrow\phi
. Finally, in Steps 3 and 4, we prove Statements (
1
) and (

Step 1.

Objects of the form
((u,y),y=y)
are maximal in
\phi\downarrow y
. Furthermore, if
(u,y)
and
(u^{\prime},y)
are two distinct objects in
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
, then

(\phi\downarrow y)_{\leq((u,y),y=y)}\cap(\phi\downarrow y)_{\leq((u^{\prime},y),y=y)}=\emptyset,

since otherwise there is some object

(a,b)\leq(u,y)
and
(a,b)\leq(u^{\prime},y)
. Thus there are two distinct paths

and

is a tree.

Similarly, objects of the form

and
y\to v^{\prime}\to a
, contradicting the assumption that

Step 2.

Objects of the form
((y,v),y=y)

\beta\downarrow y

(y,v)\leq(a,b)

.

Step 3.

By the first part of Step 1, the overcategory
\phi\downarrow y

, and in each such subcategory the object

L_{\phi}(N)(y)\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\mathop{\textup{colim}}\limits_{\phi\downarrow y}N_{\#}\cong\bigoplus_{(u,y)\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}N(u,y),

where

denotes the restriction of
N
to

. This proves the first statement in Part (
1
).

Similarly, by the second part of Step 1, the undercategory

With gradient and divergence operators in place, we can now define the corresponding Laplacians.

Definition 7.10
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a finite poset. Define the
left and right Laplacians

\Delta^{0}
and
\Delta_{0}
respectively in
\mathrm{End}(\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{P}))
, to be the group endomorphisms

\Delta^{0}\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\nabla^{*}\circ\nabla\quad\text{and}\quad\Delta_{0}\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\nabla_{*}\circ\nabla.

A virtual module
[X]\in\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{P})
is said to be
left harmonic
if
\Delta^{0}[X]=0
and
right harmonic
if
\Delta_{0}[X]=0
. A virtual module is said to be
harmonic
if it is both left and right harmonic.

Corollary 7.11
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a finite poset, whose Hasse diagram is a rooted tree, and let
[X]=[M]-[N]\in\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{P})
with
M,N\in k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
be a left harmonic virtual module. Then, for each object
(u,v)\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
,

\dim_{k}M(v)-\dim_{k}M(u)=\dim_{k}N(v)-\dim_{k}N(u).

In particular, if
N=0
, then for each
x,y\in\mathcal{P}
,
\dim_{k}M(x)=\dim_{k}M(y)
.

Proof.

Write

\nabla[X]=[\phi^{*}M\oplus\beta^{*}N]-[\phi^{*}N\oplus\beta^{*}M].

Then
0=\Delta^{0}[X]=\nabla^{*}(\nabla[X])
and Proposition
7.8
applies. Thus, for each
(u,v)\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
,

\displaystyle\dim_{k}M(v)+\dim_{k}N(u)=

\displaystyle\dim_{k}(\phi^{*}M(u,v)\oplus\beta^{*}N(u,v))=

\displaystyle\dim_{k}(\phi^{*}N(u,v)\oplus\beta^{*}M(u,v))=

\displaystyle\dim_{k}N(v)+\dim_{k}M(u).

The first claim follows.

Since
\mathcal{P}
is generated by a rooted tree, it is in particular connected. The second claim follows from the first by connectivity of
\mathcal{P}
and induction on the length of paths in
\mathcal{P}
.
∎

Recall that our gradient is a categorified version of the discrete gradient on digraphs
[
11
]
, and similarly, the left and right divergence operators are a categorification of the discrete divergence (See Examples
3.6
and
3.9
). In graph theory one can define for a digraph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
a cochain complex
C_{*}(\mathcal{G})
, such that
C_{0}(\mathcal{G})
and
C_{1}(\mathcal{G})
are the vector spaces of functions from
V
and
E
to the ground field
k
, respectively. With this setup one shows that the kernel of the Laplacian and that of the gradient coincide
[
11
, Section 4.3]
.
It makes sense to ask whether the same holds in our context. For modules over a linear poset the answer turns out to be positive, as claimed in Proposition
D
, which we prove next.

Proposition 7.12
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be the poset with objects
0\leq i\leq n
with the usual order relation. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

\nabla[M]=0
.

(2)

\Delta^{0}[M]=0
.

(3)

\Delta_{0}[M]=0
.

Proof.

By definition (
1
) implies (
2
) and (
3
). It remains to prove the other implications.

Consider the line poset
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
,

(0,1)<(1,2)<\cdots<(n-1,n).

Let
M\in k\mathcal{P}
be any module. Let
\alpha_{i}
denote the transformation
M(i<i+1)
for
0\leq i\leq n-1
.
The modules
\beta^{*}M
and
\phi^{*}M
take the form

M(0)\xrightarrow{\alpha_{0}}M(1)\xrightarrow{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\xrightarrow{\alpha_{n-2}}M(n-1),\quad\text{and}\quad M(1)\xrightarrow{\alpha_{1}}M(2)\xrightarrow{\alpha_{2}}\cdots\xrightarrow{\alpha_{n-1}}M(n),

respectively.

We turn to the computation of left and right Kan Extensions for
\phi
and
\beta
. Since
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
is again a linear poset, over- and under-categories of
\phi,\beta\colon\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\to\mathcal{P}
are also linear. Thus for a module
N\in k\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
, the left Kan extension at an object
x
is determined by the value of
N
on the maximal object in
\alpha\downarrow x
, where
\alpha=\phi
or
\beta
. Similarly, the right Kan extension at
x
is determined by the value of
N
on the minimal object in
x\downarrow\alpha
. The following table gives the values of the corresponding functor on each of the objects.

\begin{array}[]{| l | c | c | c | c |}\hline\cr x&L_{\phi}(N)(x)&L_{\beta}(N)(x)&R_{\phi}(N)(x)&R_{\beta}(N)(x)\\
\hline\cr 0&0&N(0,1)&N(0,1)&N(0,1)\\
\hline\cr 0<k<n&N(k-1,k)&N(k,k+1)&N(k-1,k)&N(k,k+1)\\
\hline\cr n&N(n-1,n)&N(n-1,n)&N(n-1,n)&0\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

Take
N=\phi^{*}M
and
\beta^{*}M
. We have:

\begin{array}[]{| l | c | c | c | c |}\hline\cr x&L_{\phi}(\phi^{*}M)(x)&L_{\beta}(\phi^{*}M)(x)&L_{\phi}(\beta^{*}M)(x)&L_{\beta}(\beta^{*}M)(x)\\
\hline\cr 0&0&M(1)&0&M(0)\\
\hline\cr 0<k<n&M(k)&M(k+1)&M(k-1)&M(k)\\
\hline\cr n&M(n)&M(n)&M(n-1)&M(n-1)\\
\hline\cr\hline\cr x&R_{\phi}(\phi^{*}M)(x)&R_{\beta}(\phi^{*}M)(x)&R_{\phi}(\beta^{*}M)(x)&R_{\beta}(\beta^{*}M)(x)\\
\hline\cr 0&M(1)&M(1)&M(0)&M(0)\\
\hline\cr 0<k<n&M(k)&M(k+1)&M(k-1)&M(k)\\
\hline\cr n&M(n)&0&M(n-1)&0\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

We prove that (
2
) implies (
1
). The proof that (
3
) implies (
1
) is similar and is left to the reader.

The
k\mathcal{P}
-module
L_{\phi}(\phi^{*}M)\oplus L_{\beta}(\beta^{*}M)
has the form:

0\oplus M(0)\xrightarrow{0\oplus\alpha_{0}}M(1)\oplus M(1)\xrightarrow{}\cdots\xrightarrow{}M(k)\oplus M(k)\xrightarrow{}\cdots\xrightarrow{\alpha_{n-1}\oplus I}M(n)\oplus M(n-1),

where
I
is the identity matrix on
M(n-1)
. The morphisms that are not marked in the sequence take the form

\alpha_{k}\oplus\alpha_{k}\colon M(k)\oplus M(k)\to M(k+1)\oplus M(k+1),

for
0<k<n-1
.
Similarly, the module
L_{\phi}(\beta^{*}M)\oplus L_{\beta}(\phi^{*}M)
has the form:

0\oplus M(1)\xrightarrow{0\oplus\alpha_{1}}M(0)\oplus M(2)\xrightarrow{}\cdots M(k-1)\oplus M(k+1)\xrightarrow{}\cdots\xrightarrow{\alpha_{n-2}\oplus I}M(n-1)\oplus M(n),

where
I
is the identity on
M(n)
. The unmarked morphisms in the sequence take the form

\alpha_{k-1}\oplus\alpha_{k+1}\colon M(k-1)\oplus M(k+1)\to M(k)\oplus M(k+2),

for
0<k<n-1
.

Assume that
L_{\phi}(\phi^{*}M)\oplus L_{\beta}(\beta^{*}M)\cong L_{\phi}(\beta^{*}M)\oplus L_{\beta}(\phi^{*}M)
. It follows at once that
M(0)\cong M(1)
and by induction on
k
,
M(k-1)\cong M(k)
for all
0\leq k\leq n
. Similarly
\operatorname{rk}\nolimits(\alpha_{0})=\operatorname{rk}\nolimits(\alpha_{1})
, and by induction
\operatorname{rk}\nolimits(\alpha_{k-1})=\operatorname{rk}\nolimits(\alpha_{k})
for all
k
.

Next, notice that

\operatorname{rk}\nolimits((\alpha_{1}\oplus\alpha_{1})\circ(0\oplus\alpha_{0}))=\operatorname{rk}\nolimits(\alpha_{1}\alpha_{0})=\operatorname{rk}\nolimits(\alpha_{2}\alpha_{1})=\operatorname{rk}\nolimits((\alpha_{0}\oplus\alpha_{2})\circ(0\oplus\alpha_{1}))

\operatorname{rk}\nolimits(\alpha_{k}\alpha_{k-1}\cdots\alpha_{0})=\operatorname{rk}\nolimits(\alpha_{k+1}\alpha_{k}\cdots\alpha_{1})
, for all
k<n-1
. In the same fashion,

\displaystyle\operatorname{rk}\nolimits(\alpha_{n-1}\alpha_{n-2})+\operatorname{rk}\nolimits(\alpha_{n-2})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{rk}\nolimits((\alpha_{n-1}\oplus I_{M(n-1)})\circ(\alpha_{n-2}\oplus\alpha_{n-2}))

\displaystyle=\operatorname{rk}\nolimits((\alpha_{n-2}\oplus I_{M(n)})\circ(\alpha_{n-3}\oplus\alpha_{n-1}))

\displaystyle=\operatorname{rk}\nolimits(\alpha_{n-2}\alpha_{n-3})+\operatorname{rk}\nolimits(\alpha_{n-1}),

\operatorname{rk}\nolimits(\alpha_{n-2}\alpha_{n-3})=\operatorname{rk}\nolimits(\alpha_{n-1}\alpha_{n-2})
. More generally we have
\operatorname{rk}\nolimits(\alpha_{n-2}\cdots\alpha_{k})=\operatorname{rk}\nolimits(\alpha_{n-1}\cdots\alpha_{k+1})

\displaystyle\operatorname{rk}\nolimits((\alpha_{m}\oplus\alpha_{m})\circ\cdots\circ(\alpha_{k}\oplus\alpha_{k}))

\displaystyle=\operatorname{rk}\nolimits(\alpha_{m-1}\cdots\alpha_{k-1})+\operatorname{rk}\nolimits(\alpha_{m+1}\cdots\alpha_{k+1})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{rk}\nolimits((\alpha_{m-1}\oplus\alpha_{m+1})\circ\cdots\circ(\alpha_{k-1}\oplus\alpha_{k+1})).

For

this reads:
2\operatorname{rk}\nolimits(\alpha_{m}\cdots\alpha_{1})=\operatorname{rk}\nolimits(\alpha_{m-1}\cdots\alpha_{0})+\operatorname{rk}\nolimits(\alpha_{m+1}\cdots\alpha_{2})
and this implies that
\operatorname{rk}\nolimits(\alpha_{m}\cdots\alpha_{1})=\operatorname{rk}\nolimits(\alpha_{m+1}\cdots\alpha_{2})
. Assume by induction that we have shown
\operatorname{rk}\nolimits(\alpha_{m-1}\cdots\alpha_{k-2})=\operatorname{rk}\nolimits(\alpha_{m}\cdots\alpha_{k-1})
. It then follows that
\operatorname{rk}\nolimits(\alpha_{m}\cdots\alpha_{k-1})=\operatorname{rk}\nolimits(\alpha_{m+1}\cdots\alpha_{k})
. This completes the proof that
\beta^{*}M
and
\phi^{*}M
have the same rank invariant, and by completeness of the rank invariant for linear posets they are isomorphic. Thus
\nabla[M]=0
and so (
2
) implies (
1
).
∎

8.
Examples

In this final section we demonstrate some nice properties of the gradient by means of two examples that highlight the advantages that it offers, particularly in potential applications. As already pointed out in the introduction, almost all quivers (including almost all finite posets) are of infinite or wild representation type and so classifying their indecomposable representations is hard or, in the wild case, impossible. Hence understanding modules globally by means of their indecomposable summands is not feasible.

Our calculus methods lend themselves naturally to
local
investigation of modules, and hence are not tied to the representation type of posets. We consider two examples. The first is a family of posets that is generally of wild representation type, but where the associated line poset has finite representation type, and hence the gradient of any module over these posets can be well understood. The second example involves a module over a
10\times 10
grid poset (wild representation type again), where the gradient easily gives partial information about the module.

8.1.
Commutative Ladders

An in-depth investigation of modules over so called
commutative ladders
appeared in
[
6
]
. While classification of indecomposable modules over those ladder posets may be hard or even impossible, the gradient is much easier to understand.

Denote a left-to-right arrow by
F
and a right-to-left arrow by
B
. Following terminology from quiver representations, we call the finite poset with
n
objects obtained schematically as a juxtaposition of arrows of type
F
or
B
in any order as an
\mathbb{A}_{n}
poset
.
By Gabriel’s theorem
[
7
]
,
[
4
, Theorem 4.2.4]
such posets are of finite representation type, i.e. they admit finitely many isomorphism classes of indecomposable representations.
Any
\mathbb{A}_{n}
poset is uniquely characterised by a sequence
X_{1}X_{2}\cdots X_{n-1}
, where each
X_{i}
is either
F
or
B
. We refer to the characterising sequence as the
type
of the poset. A
commutative ladder of length
n
is a poset that can be written schematically as two
\mathbb{A}_{n}
posets
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
of the same type with arrows from each object of
L_{1}
to the corresponding object in
L_{2}
. By
[
6
, Theorem 3, 4]
general commutative ladders of any type with length
at most
4
are of finite representation type, whereas they are of infinite representation type if their length is
\geq 5
.

Consider two specific types of commutative ladders. The first is of any length and is made of a related pair of alternating sequences of type either
FBFB\cdots
or
BFBF\cdots

(either may end with
B
or
F
)
. We refer to a poset of this form and length
n
as a
zig-zag ladder of length
n
. The second is of even length
2n
and is made of a related pair of sequences of the form
FFBBFF\cdots
or
BBFFBB\cdots

(either may end with
BB
or
FF
)
. This type will be referred to as a
double zig-zag ladder of length
2n
. As pointed out any commutative ladder poset of length at least
5
is of infinite representation type.

Lemma 8.1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a commutative ladder poset. Then

(1)

if
\mathcal{P}
is a zig-zag ladder of any length, then
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
is a disjoint union of
\mathbb{A}_{n}
posets with
n\leq 2
, and

(2)

if
\mathcal{P}
is a double zig-zag ladder of any length, then
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
is an
\mathbb{A}_{n}
poset.

Proof.

Corollary 8.2
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a zig-zag or double zig-zag ladder of any length
n
. Then for any
M\in k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
the gradient
\nabla[M]
is equal to a difference of two finite sums of isomorphism classes of
k\mathbb{A}_{n}
-modules.

There are obviously more types of commutative ladders, and more general posets, whose gradients can be decomposed as a finite combination of isomorphism classes of modules of finite representation type. For instance a
ladder poset whose horizontal parts are juxtapositions of sequences of type
BBF
also has a line poset that is a disjoint union of
\mathbb{A}_{n}
posets. Similarly certain trees have line posets of type
\mathbb{A}_{n}
or disjoint union of them, see Figure
2
.
The classification of posets whose associated line posets are of finite representation type
is an interesting question for which we do not know the answer
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{m,n}

\{0<1<\dots<m\}
and
\{0<1<\dots<n\}
, which can be depicted as a commutative grid on two coordinates:

Modules
M\in k\mathcal{A}_{m,n}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
are referred to as
grid modules
. We denote by
k\mathcal{A}^{e,\ast}_{m,n}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
the subcategory of
k\mathcal{A}_{m,n}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
consisting of modules such that all the homomorphisms induced by
M
on horizontal morphisms in
\mathcal{A}_{m,n}
are surjective. The representation theory of
k\mathcal{A}^{e,\ast}_{m,n}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
was studied in
[
1
]
, based on earlier works by
[
9
]
and
[
10
]
. By
[
1
, Corollary 1.6]
there are finitely many isomorphism types of indecomposable
k\mathcal{A}^{e,\ast}_{m,n}
-modules exactly when
n\leq 2
, or
m=1
, or when
(m,n)\in\{(2,3),(2,4),(2,5),(3,3),(4,3)\}
. The representation type, under the same restriction, is tame exactly when
(m,n)\in\{(2,6),(3,4),(5,3)\}
, and is wild in all other cases.

Following a procedure similar as outlined in
[
1
, Section 1]
we created random examples of grid modules
M\in k\mathcal{A}^{e,\ast}_{10,10}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
.
For a specific example of such a module
M
, Figure
3
shows the dimension vector
\mathbf{dim}_{k}[M]
(left) and the (virtual) dimension vector
\mathbf{dim}_{k}\nabla[M]
of its gradient.

While analysing the global (virtual) module structure of
\nabla[M]
would be as hard as that of the original module, the dimension vectors alone are very easy to compute (regardless of the size of the grid) and give some easily accessible information. For instance, consider the
horizontal objects

(i,i+1,j)
and
vertical objects

(i,j,j+1)
in
\widehat{A}_{10,10}
corresponding to the morphisms in
\mathcal{A}_{10,10}
from
(i,j)
to
(i+1,j)
and
(i,j+1)
, respectively. For a given module
M\in k\mathcal{A}^{e,\ast}_{10,10}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
it follows easily from the definition

\mathbf{dim}_{k}\nabla[M](i,j,j+1)=\dim_{k}M(i,j+1)-\dim_{k}M(i,j)
, and
\mathbf{dim}_{k}\nabla[M](i,i+1,j)=\dim_{k}M(i+1,j)-\dim_{k}M(i,j)
.

Now consider two adjacent horizontal objects
(i,i+1,j)
and
(i,i+1,j+1)
, and assume that
\mathbf{dim}_{k}\nabla[M](i,i+1,j)=\mathbf{dim}_{k}\nabla[M](i,i+1,j+1)=0
. Then
\dim_{k}M(i,j)=\dim_{k}M(i+1,j)
, and
\dim_{k}M(i,j+1)=\dim_{k}M(i+1,j+1)
, and since by hypothesis
M(k,k+1,r)
is an epimorphism for all
k
and
r
, it follows that
M(i,i+1,j)
and similarly
M(i,i+1,j+1)
are isomorphisms. Thus it also follows that

M(i+1,j,j+1)=M(i,i+1,j+1)\circ M(i,j,j+1)\circ M(i,i+1,j)^{-1}.
