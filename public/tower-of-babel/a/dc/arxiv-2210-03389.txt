Abstract
Physical models with uncertain inputs are commonly represented as parametric partial differential equations (PDEs). That is, PDEs with inputs that are expressed as functions of parameters with an associated probability distribution. Developing efficient and accurate solution strategies that account for errors on the space, time and parameter domains simultaneously is highly challenging. Indeed, it is well known that standard polynomial-based approximations on the parameter domain can incur errors that grow in time. In this work, we focus on advection–diffusion problems with parameter-dependent wind fields. A novel adaptive solution strategy is proposed that allows users to combine stochastic collocation on the parameter domain with off-the-shelf adaptive timestepping algorithms with local error control. This is a non-intrusive strategy that builds a polynomial-based surrogate that is adapted sequentially in time. The algorithm is driven by a so-called hierarchical estimator for the parametric error and balances this against an estimate for the global timestepping error which is derived from a scaling argument.

Introduction

Parametric partial differential equations (PDEs) are commonly used to model physical problems with uncertain inputs that are represented as random variables. Briefly, if the uncertain inputs can be expressed as functions of a finite set of independent random variables
\xi_{i}:\Omega\to\Gamma_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}
,
i=1,\ldots,d
on a probability space
(\Omega,{\cal F},\mathbb{P})
, then with the change of variables
y_{i}=\xi_{i}(\omega)
,
i=1,\ldots d
, we may recast the inputs and PDE solution as functions of the parameter vector
\mathbf{y}=[y_{1},\ldots,y_{d}]^{\top}\in\Gamma
where
\Gamma:=\Gamma_{1}\times\cdots\times\Gamma_{d}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the parameter domain. Denoting the Borel
\sigma
-algebra by
\mathcal{B}(\Gamma)
and the joint probability density by
\rho(\mathbf{y})=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\rho_{i}(y_{i})
, where
\rho_{i}(y_{i})
is the probability density of
\xi_{i}
we can formulate the problem and compute statistical quantities of interest with respect to the equivalent probability space
(\Gamma,\mathcal{B}(\Gamma),\rho(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y})
.

In this work, we are interested in the efficient and accurate numerical solution of parametric time-dependent advection-diffusion problems of the form

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}u(\mathbf{x},t,\mathbf{y})-\epsilon\Delta u(\mathbf{x},t,\mathbf{y})+\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\cdot\nabla u(\mathbf{x},t,\mathbf{y})=0,

(1)

where the
d
input parameters
y_{i}
are images of random variables that encode our uncertainty about the spatially-varying advection field or ‘wind’
\mathbf{w}
. Such time-dependent parametric PDEs are more challenging to solve than their steady-state counterparts. Different choices of
\mathbf{y}
yield different space-time solutions that may be pushed further away from each other in time. This makes
u(\mathbf{x},t,\mathbf{y})
harder to approximate as a function of
\mathbf{y}
as time
t\to\infty
. This is not a new observation, for example it is discussed in
Wan2006
, where the authors investigate a transport problem with uncertain velocity.

Numerical solution strategies for parametric PDEs can be categorised as either non-intrusive methods, which involve the solution of decoupled deterministic problems for particular choices of
\mathbf{y}
or intrusive methods, based on Galerkin projection, which require the solution of a single large coupled problem. Among these, polynomial-based approximation schemes such as stochastic collocation
Xiu2005
;
Nobile2008a
;
Babuska2007
and generalised polynomial chaos (gPC) approximation
Xiu2002
;
Babuska2004
that lead to surrogates that can be cheaply evaluated for any
\mathbf{y}\in\Gamma
are useful for tasks like uncertainty quantification, optimisation and design. Non-intrusive schemes that allow the reuse of existing space-time solvers are especially appealing for practitioners.

Both intrusive and non-intrusive solution strategies have been extensively studied for parametric elliptic PDEs and state-of-the-art adaptive solvers exist for such problems.
Anisotropic
Nobile2008b
and adaptive
Nobile2016
collocation schemes have been proposed for problems with a moderately large number of parameters.
Adaptive variants of sparse grid collocation typically use a strategy based on Gerstner and Griebel’s quadrature algorithm
Gerstner2003
, creating an operator that is a sum of appropriately chosen hierarchical surplus operators.
More recently, residual based a posteriori error estimation strategies have been developed for stochastic collocation approximations of solutions to parametric elliptic PDEs
Guignard2018
and the convergence of adaptive algorithms driven by such estimators has been analysed
Eigel2022
;
Feischl2021
.
Less work has been done for time-dependent parametric PDEs.
However, a residual-based error estimator was used to drive an adaptive stochastic collocation algorithm for the heat equation in
Nobile2021
and time-adapted bases were used in the context of dynamical low rank approximation for parametric parabolic problems in
Kazashi2021
.
For gPC approximations, the use of polynomial bases that are adapted in time is discussed in
Gerritsma2010
.

We will use sparse grid stochastic collocation
Smolyak1963
;
Barthelmann2000
;
Novak1999
for the parametric approximation of (
1
).
This is appealing due to its non-intrusive nature and the fact that it leads to a polynomial surrogate.
The main challenge we address is how to combine such a method with an adaptive timestepping scheme so that the distinct contributions to the total error remain balanced.
For this, an efficient a posteriori error estimator is required.
The strategy we propose is different to the one proposed in
Nobile2021
.
Our estimator is not residual-based.
Moreover, we use adaptive timestepping methods in a black-box fashion and adapt the polynomial approximation
sequentially
in time.

In
Section

2
we introduce the parametric problem of interest in more detail and describe the numerical solution approach, combining sparse grid stochastic collocation on
\Gamma
and an adaptive timestepping algorithm with local error control.
In
Section

3
, we consider a related ODE test problem and motivate the need to adapt the parametric approximation in time.
An a posteriori error estimator combining a Gerstner and Griebel type strategy on the parameter domain and a global timestepping error estimation strategy is defined in
Section

4
.
A novel adaptive algorithm that is driven by the error estimator is described in
Section

5
and numerical results are presented in
Section

6
and
Section

7
.

Problem Statement and Approximation Scheme

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
(
n=2,3
) be an open polygonal spatial domain with Lipschitz boundary
\partial D
and let
\mathbf{y}=[y_{1},\ldots,y_{d}]^{\top}
be a vector of
d
parameters taking values in a parameter domain
\Gamma=\Gamma_{1}\times\cdots\times\Gamma_{d}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with associated joint probability density
\rho(\mathbf{y})=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\rho_{i}(y_{i})
.
We consider an advection-diffusion problem with a fixed diffusion coefficient
\epsilon>0
and a parameter-dependent, spatially divergence-free advection field
\mathbf{w}:D\times\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
That is, we seek a solution
u:D\times[0,T]\times\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial t}u(\mathbf{x},t,\mathbf{y})&-\epsilon\Delta u(\mathbf{x},t,\mathbf{y})+\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\cdot\nabla u(\mathbf{x},t,\mathbf{y})=0,\end{aligned}&(\mathbf{x},t)\in D\times(0,T],\\
u(\mathbf{x},0,\mathbf{y})=u_{0}(\mathbf{x}),&\mathbf{x}\in D,\end{cases}

(2)

\rho
-almost surely on
\Gamma
subject to the Dirichlet boundary condition

u(\mathbf{x},t,\mathbf{y})=u_{\partial D}(\mathbf{x},t),\quad(\mathbf{x},t,\mathbf{y})\in\partial D\times[0,T]\times\Gamma.

(3)

For the weak formulation, we define
H^{1}_{\partial D}(D):=\{v\in H^{1}(D)\text{ s.t. }\trace v=u_{\partial D}\}
and seek
u(\cdot,t,\mathbf{y})\in H^{1}_{\partial D}(D)
with
\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(\cdot,t,\mathbf{y})\in H^{-1}(D)
that solves

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \int_{D}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}u(&\mathbf{x},t,\mathbf{y})v(\mathbf{x})+\epsilon\nabla u(\mathbf{x},t,\mathbf{y}){\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\cdot}\nabla v(\mathbf{x})\\
&+\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\cdot\nabla u(\mathbf{x},t,\mathbf{y})v(\mathbf{x})\textrm{d}\mathbf{x}=0,\end{aligned}&t\in(0,T],\\
\\
\int_{D}u(\mathbf{x},0,\mathbf{y})v(\mathbf{x})\textrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\int_{D}u_{0}(\mathbf{x})v(\mathbf{x})\textrm{d}\mathbf{x},\end{cases}

(4)

\rho
-almost surely on
\Gamma
for all test functions
v\in H^{1}_{0}(D):=\{v\in H^{1}(D)\text{ s.t. }\trace v=0\}
.
Conditions for existence and uniqueness of a solution for a fixed
\mathbf{y}
are outlined in Evans
(
Evans2010
, Section 7.1.2)
.
In particular, we need to assume that
\epsilon>0
is finite and
\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=[w^{1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),w^{2}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})]^{\top}
where
w^{1},w^{2}\in L^{\infty}(D)
,
\rho
-almost surely on
\Gamma
.
In our numerical experiments we will choose wind fields of the form

\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):=\mathbf{w}_{0}(\mathbf{x})+\sum_{i=1}^{d}y_{i}\mathbf{w}_{i}(\mathbf{x})

(5)

where
\{\mathbf{w}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{d}
are spatial fields with
w^{1}_{i},w^{2}_{i}\in L^{\infty}(D)
for each
i=0,...,d
and the parameters
y_{i}\in[-1,1]
(i.e., the associated random variables are bounded).

For the spatial discretisation, we apply finite element approximation. We do not specify a particular method here but assume that the chosen approximation is globally continuous and defined piecewise with respect to a spatial mesh of non-overlapping triangular (tetrahedral if
n=3
) or rectangular (hexahedral) elements with characteristic edge length
h
. The associated nodes are partitioned into two sets
\mathcal{N}^{h}=\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{{m_{D}}}\cup\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i={m_{D}}+1}^{{m_{D}}+{m_{\partial}}}
corresponding to interior and boundary nodes, respectively, as are the associated sets of nodal basis functions
\{\phi^{h}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{{m_{D}}}\cup\{\phi^{h}_{i}\}_{i={m_{D}}+1}^{{m_{D}}+{m_{\partial}}}
. For each
(t,\mathbf{y})\in[0,T]\times\Gamma
we seek an approximation of the form

u^{h}(\mathbf{x},t,\mathbf{y})=\sum_{i=1}^{{m_{D}}}u^{h}_{i}(t,\mathbf{y})\phi^{h}_{i}(\mathbf{x})+\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{{m_{\partial}}}u_{\partial D}(\mathbf{x}_{{m_{D}}+i},t)\phi^{h}_{{m_{D}}+i}(\mathbf{x})}_{=:u_{\partial D}^{h}(\mathbf{x},t)}

(6)

where the coefficients associated with the boundary nodes are fixed. Substituting this into (
4
) and using the test space
S^{h}_{0}=\{\phi^{h}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{{m_{D}}}\subset H^{1}_{0}(D)
yields a parametric system of ODEs for the vector of coefficients
\mathbf{u}^{h}(t,\mathbf{y}):=[u^{h}_{1}(t,\mathbf{y}),...,u^{h}_{{m_{D}}}(t,\mathbf{y})]^{\top}
. To formulate this system, we define a mass matrix
Q^{h}
, a diffusion matrix
D^{h}
and a parameter-dependent advection matrix
A^{h}(\mathbf{y})
associated with the interior nodes by

\displaystyle\left[Q^{h}\right]_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\int_{D}\phi^{h}_{i}(\mathbf{x})\phi^{h}_{j}(\mathbf{x})\textrm{d}\mathbf{x},

(7)

\displaystyle\left[D^{h}\right]_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\int_{D}\nabla\phi^{h}_{i}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\nabla\phi^{h}_{j}(\mathbf{x})\textrm{d}\mathbf{x},

\displaystyle\left[A^{h}(\mathbf{y})\right]_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\int_{D}\left(\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\cdot\nabla\phi^{h}_{j}(\mathbf{x})\right){\phi^{h}_{i}(\mathbf{x})}\textrm{d}\mathbf{x}

for
i,j=1,...,{m_{D}}
. We also define analogous matrices
Q^{h}_{\partial D},D^{h}_{\partial D}
and
A^{h}_{\partial D}(\mathbf{y})
that account for boundary interactions, for
i=1,...,{m_{D}}
and
j=1,...,{m_{\partial}}
, as follows

\displaystyle\left[Q^{h}_{\partial D}\right]_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\int_{D}\phi^{h}_{i}(\mathbf{x})\phi^{h}_{{m_{D}}+j}(\mathbf{x})\textrm{d}\mathbf{x},

(8)

\displaystyle\left[D^{h}_{\partial D}\right]_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\int_{D}\nabla\phi^{h}_{i}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\nabla\phi^{h}_{{m_{D}}+j}(\mathbf{x})\textrm{d}\mathbf{x},

\displaystyle\left[A^{h}_{\partial D}(\mathbf{y})\right]_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\int_{D}\left(\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\cdot\nabla\phi^{h}_{{m_{D}}+j}(\mathbf{x})\right){\phi^{h}_{i}(\mathbf{x})}\textrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

The coefficient vector
\mathbf{u}^{h}:[0,T]\times\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}^{{m_{D}}}
then satisfies the ODE system

\begin{cases}Q^{h}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathbf{u}^{h}(t,\mathbf{y})+\epsilon D^{h}\mathbf{u}^{h}(t,\mathbf{y})+A^{h}(\mathbf{y})\mathbf{u}^{h}(t,\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{f}^{h}(t,\mathbf{y}),&t\in(0,T],\\
\mathbf{u}^{h}(0,\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{u}^{h}_{0}=[u_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{1}),...,u_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{{m_{D}}})]^{\top},&\end{cases}

(9)

\rho
-almost surely on
\Gamma
subject to the forcing term

\mathbf{f}^{h}(t,\mathbf{y}):=-Q^{h}_{\partial D}\frac{\textrm{d}\mathbf{u}^{h}_{\partial D}(t)}{\textrm{d}t}-\left(\epsilon D^{h}_{\partial D}+A^{h}_{\partial D}(\mathbf{y})\right)\mathbf{u}^{h}_{\partial D}(t),

(10)

where
\mathbf{u}^{h}_{\partial D}(t):=[u_{\partial D}(\mathbf{x}_{{m_{D}}+1},t),...,u_{\partial D}(\mathbf{x}_{{m_{D}}+{m_{\partial}}},t)]^{\top}
. After solving this system, the spatially-discrete approximation
u^{h}:D\times[0,T]\times\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}
can be reconstructed as

\displaystyle u^{h}(\mathbf{x},t,\mathbf{y}):=\sum_{i=1}^{{m_{D}}}\left[\mathbf{u}^{h}(t,\mathbf{y})\right]_{i}\phi^{h}_{i}(\mathbf{x})+u_{\partial D}^{h}(\mathbf{x},t).

(11)

Note that in general,
u_{\partial D}^{h}\neq u_{\partial D}
unless the boundary condition is a piecewise polynomial of an appropriate degree. However, we will ignore this error here. Our focus is on approximating the solution of the ODE system (
9
), or equivalently the function
u^{h}
in (
11
), that arises after applying the spatial discretisation.

For the parametric approximation, we will construct a global polynomial approximation on
\Gamma
that interpolates
u^{h}
at a set of collocation points. Specifically, we use the sparse grid construction proposed by Smolyak
Smolyak1963
and developed by Barthelmann, Novak and Ritter
Barthelmann2000
. The reader is referred to the extensive literature (e.g., see
Novak1999
;
Gerstner1998
;
Gerstner2003
) for more details. Sparse grid stochastic collocation has been used extensively for solving parametric elliptic and parabolic PDEs
Babuska2007
;
Nobile2008a
;
Xiu2005
. Here, we give only a brief outline to set up notation.

First, we require a set of one-dimensional interpolation operators. Let
\{{Z}^{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of sets of
n_{\alpha}
distinct collocation points with
{Z}^{\alpha}=\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{\alpha}}\subset\Gamma_{1}=[-1,1]
. For each ‘level’ number
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
and for continuous functions
f:\Gamma_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
, we define an interpolation operator
\mathrm{I}^{\alpha}:C^{0}(\Gamma_{1})\to\mathcal{P}^{n_{\alpha}-1}(\Gamma_{1})
in the usual way by

\mathrm{I}^{\alpha}[f](y):=\sum_{{z}\in{Z}^{\alpha}}f(z)L_{z}^{\alpha}(y),

(12)

where
L_{z}^{\alpha}\in\mathcal{P}^{n_{\alpha}-1}(\Gamma_{1})
is the Lagrange polynomial of degree
n_{\alpha}-1
that takes the value one at
y=z
and zero at all the other points in
{Z}^{\alpha}
. If we set
\Delta^{1}:=\mathrm{I}^{1}
and define the
detail operator

\Delta^{\alpha}:C^{0}(\Gamma_{1})\to\mathcal{P}^{n_{\alpha}-1}(\Gamma_{1})
for
\alpha>1
as

\Delta^{\alpha}:=\mathrm{I}^{\alpha}-\mathrm{I}^{\alpha-1}

(13)

then clearly
\mathrm{I}^{\alpha}=\sum_{{i}=1}^{\alpha}\Delta^{{i}}
.

Next, switching to
d
dimensions, for any multi-index
\underline{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
we can define the so-called
hierarchical surplus
(or multi-dimensional detail operator)

\Delta^{\underline{\alpha}}:=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{d}\Delta^{\alpha_{i}}.

(14)

Given an
admissible
multi-index set
{I}\subset\mathbb{N}^{d}
, that is, a set
{I}
such that for every
\underline{\alpha}\in{I}
,

\underline{\alpha}-\underline{e}_{j}\in{I}\text{ for }j=1,...,d\text{ where }\alpha_{j}>1

(15)

and
\underline{e}_{j}:=[0,...,\underbrace{1}_{j\text{-th}},...,0]^{\top},
we define the sparse grid operator
\mathrm{I}^{{I}}
as follows

\mathrm{I}^{{I}}[f](\mathbf{y}):=\sum_{\underline{\alpha}\in{I}}\Delta^{\underline{\alpha}}[f](\mathbf{y}).

(16)

This is an interpolant if the sets of one-dimensional points are nested, i.e.,
{Z}^{1}\subset{Z}^{2}\subset{Z}^{3}...

(
Barthelmann2000
, Proposition 6)
. In that case, we can rewrite (
16
) as

\mathrm{I}^{{I}}[f](\mathbf{y}):=\sum_{{\mathbf{z}}\in{Z}^{{I}}}f({\mathbf{z}})L_{{\mathbf{z}}}^{{I}}(\mathbf{y})

(17)

for appropriate interpolation polynomials
L_{{\mathbf{z}}}^{{I}}
and interpolation points
{Z}^{{I}}\subset\Gamma
. We will use Clenshaw–Curtis points.

Definition 1
(Clenshaw–Curtis points
Clenshaw1960
)

On the interval
\Gamma_{1}=[-1,1]
the set of
n
Clenshaw–Curtis (CC) points is defined by

z_{i}=-\cos\left(\pi\frac{i-1}{n-1}\right),\qquad i=1,...,n,

(18)

for
n>1
and we set
z_{1}=0
for
n=1
.

To construct nested sets of
n_{\alpha}
CC points we set
n_{1}=1
and choose

n_{\alpha}=2^{\alpha-1}+1\text{ for }\alpha>1.

(19)

Remark 1
(Use of sparse grids)

The motivation for using sparse grids is to reduce the growth in computational cost as the number
d
of parameters increases. For tensor product grids the number of points required to achieve a fixed total degree of polynomial exactness grows exponentially. The analogous asymptotic result for sparse grids shows an optimal polynomial growth
(
Barthelmann2000
, Remark 5)
.

Applying the sparse grid interpolation operator to
u^{h}
in (
11
) gives

u^{h,{I}}(\mathbf{x},t,\mathbf{y}):=\mathrm{I}^{{I}}[u^{h}(\mathbf{x},t,\mathbf{y})].

(20)

Re-writing (
20
) as a linear combination of Lagrange polynomials as in (
17
) reveals that we need to solve the ODE system (
9
) for each of the chosen collocation points
{\mathbf{z}}\in{Z}^{{I}}
. The solution to each of these deterministic ODE systems needs to be further approximated using an appropriate timestepping algorithm. If an
adaptive
scheme is used, then for each
{\mathbf{z}}\in{Z}^{{I}}
the timestepping method will select a (potentially different) set of timesteps
\{\Delta t_{k}\}_{k=0}^{n-1}
and generate a sequence of corresponding approximations

\mathbf{u}^{h,\delta}_{k}({\mathbf{z}})\approx\mathbf{u}^{h}(t_{k};{\mathbf{z}})

(21)

to the ODE solution for times
\{t_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}=\{\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\Delta t_{i}\}_{k=1}^{n}
where
t_{n}\geq T
. We will consider stable, convergent, implicit methods with local error control. Local error control means that we select the timestep
\Delta t_{k}
so that the local error
\mathcal{E}^{\text{local}}_{k}({\mathbf{z}})
satisfies

\mathcal{E}^{\text{local}}_{k}({\mathbf{z}}):=\|\mathbf{u}^{h}_{local}(t_{k}+\Delta t_{k},{\mathbf{z}})-\mathbf{u}^{h,\delta}_{k+1}({\mathbf{z}})\|_{Q^{h}}\approx\delta,

(22)

where
\delta
is a user-chosen tolerance,
\mathbf{u}^{h}_{local}(t,{\mathbf{z}})
is the ODE solution on
[t_{k},t_{k+1}]
with initial data
\mathbf{u}^{h}_{local}(t_{k},{\mathbf{z}}):=\mathbf{u}^{h,\delta}_{k}({\mathbf{z}})
and
\|\cdot\|_{Q^{h}}
is the discrete analogue of
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}(D)}.

After applying a timestepping method for each collocation point
{\mathbf{z}}\in{Z}^{{I}}
, a space-time approximation
{u}^{h,\delta}(\mathbf{x},t;{\mathbf{z}})
can be defined in a similar way to (
11
) by first constructing continuous-in-time coefficients from the approximations
\mathbf{u}^{h,\delta}_{k}({\mathbf{z}})
using linear interpolation on each subinterval
[t_{k},t_{k+1})
as follows

\mathbf{u}^{h,\delta}(t;{\mathbf{z}}):=\mathbf{u}^{h,\delta}_{k}({\mathbf{z}})+\frac{t-t_{k}}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}\left(\mathbf{u}^{h,\delta}_{k+1}({\mathbf{z}})-\mathbf{u}^{h,\delta}_{k}({\mathbf{z}})\right).

(23)

The final approximation to the solution of the parametric PDE (
2
) is then

u^{h,{I},\delta}(\mathbf{x},t,\mathbf{y}):=\sum_{{\mathbf{z}}\in{Z}^{{I}}}u^{h,\delta}(\mathbf{x},t;{\mathbf{z}})L_{{\mathbf{z}}}^{{I}}(\mathbf{y}).

(24)

The simple example in
Section

3
motivates the need to adapt the multi-index set
{Z}^{{I}}
in time as well as use adaptive timestepping.

Motivating Example

Applying the Fourier transform in space to the advection-diffusion problem on
D=\mathbb{R}
gives a system of ODEs with complex coefficients that depend on the wavenumber. We consider a single ODE of this form with a parameter-dependent coefficient
\alpha(y)
where
y
is the image of a uniform random variable on
\Gamma=[-1,1]
with probability density
\rho(y)=\frac{1}{2}
. That is, we wish to approximate
u:[0,T]\times\Gamma\to\mathbb{C}
satisfying

\begin{cases}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}u(t,y)=\alpha(y)u(t,y)&t\in(0,T]\\

\end{cases}

(25)

\rho
-almost surely on
\Gamma
where
\alpha:\Gamma\to\mathbb{C}
is defined by

\alpha(y)=(-\epsilon+\mathrm{i}y).

(26)

Here,
\Re[\alpha(y)]=-\epsilon
is associated with diffusion and damps the solution in time and

represents an uncertain advective term. The exact solution to (
25
) is

u(t,y)=u_{0}\exp(-\epsilon t)\exp(\mathrm{i}yt)

(27)

with mean

\mathbb{E}\left[{u(t,y)}\right]=u_{0}\exp(-\epsilon t)\sinc(t)

(28)

and standard deviation

\mathrm{stddev}\left[{u(t,y)}\right]=u_{0}\exp(-\epsilon t)\sqrt{1-\sinc(t)^{2}}.

These functions are plotted in

3.1
Interpolation

Since we have a one-dimensional parameter domain, we define the sequence of multi-index sets
{I}_{k}:=\{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}\text{ s.t. }\alpha\leq 1+k\}
for
k=1,2,\ldots
. We construct interpolants using nested sets of CC collocation points with the doubling relation (
19
) so that for a fixed
k
the resulting polynomial is of degree
2^{k}
. The exact solution is available at each collocation point so timestepping is not necessary and here we simply denote approximations by
u^{{I}_{k}}:=\mathrm{I}^{{I}_{k}}[u]
, for
k=1,2,\ldots.
Errors in the approximation of the mean and the standard deviation are plotted in
Figs.

2(a)
and
2(b)
for
k=1,2,3,4
. These plots illustrate two important points. Firstly, for a
fixed
approximation space (fixed
k
) the error grows with time (at least initially). Secondly, if we want to suppress the error we must grow the multi-index set
{I}_{k}
in time, increasing the degree of the polynomial approximation. In
Fig.

3
the approximation error
\|u(t,y)-u^{{I}_{k}}(t,y)\|_{L^{2}_{\rho}(\Gamma)}
is estimated using the
n_{11}
-point CC quadrature rule. Again the error is seen to grow in time for a fixed polynomial approximation space and can be suppressed by increasing the degree of the polynomial approximation. It is clear that adapting the set

in time would be advantageous.

4
Error Estimation

Error estimation will be performed in the norm
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}_{\rho}(\Gamma;L^{2}(D))}
where

\displaystyle\|u(\mathbf{x},t,\mathbf{y})\|_{L^{2}_{\rho}(\Gamma;L^{2}(D))}:=\left(\int_{\Gamma}\int_{D}\left(u(\mathbf{x},t,\mathbf{y})\right)^{2}\textrm{d}\mathbf{x}~\rho(\mathbf{y})\textrm{d}\mathbf{y}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(31)

The
L^{2}(D)
norm in space is chosen as it is the most natural choice in timestepping algorithms for local error control. The
L^{2}_{\rho}(\Gamma)
norm is chosen as we consider problems with uncertain inputs and solutions that have finite variance.

To simplify the analysis the
spatial
contribution to the error is not estimated — we are interested in approximating the solution to the parametric ODE system (
9
). The focus is on estimating and balancing the interpolation and timestepping errors so we consider the splitting of the error

\displaystyle\|u^{h}-u^{h,\delta,{I}}\|_{L^{2}_{\rho}(\Gamma;L^{2}(D))}

\displaystyle\leq{\|u^{h}-u^{h,{I}}\|_{L^{2}_{\rho}(\Gamma;L^{2}(D))}}+{\|u^{h,{I}}-u^{h,{I},\delta}\|_{L^{2}_{\rho}(\Gamma;L^{2}(D))}}

(32)

\displaystyle=:{\mathcal{E}^{{I}}(t)}+\mathcal{E}^{\delta}(t).

The interpolation error
\mathcal{E}^{{I}}(t)
is estimated using a hierarchical approach by comparing the approximation
u^{h,{I}}
to an
enhanced approximation
and appealing to a saturation assumption. This approach follows the adaptive algorithm first proposed by Gerstner and Griebel
Gerstner2003
.
To define an enhanced approximation, we first define two additional multi-index sets.
The
margin

{M}_{{I}}
of
{I}
is defined as the set of all neighbouring multi-indices,

{M}_{{I}}:=\bigcup_{\underline{\alpha}\in{I}}\{\underline{\alpha}+\underline{e}_{j}\text{ for }j\in 1,...,d\}.

(33)

The
reduced margin

{R}_{{I}}
is defined as the set of multi-indices in
{M}_{{I}}
that can be individually added to
{I}
whilst maintaining the admissibility property. That is, the set

{R}_{{I}}:=\{\underline{\alpha}\in{M}_{{I}}\text{ s.t. }{I}\cup\underline{\alpha}\text{ is admissible}\}.

(34)

The enhanced multi-index set is defined as
{{I}}^{*}:={I}\cup{R}_{{I}}
and is used to construct the enhanced approximation
u^{h,{{I}}^{*}}
.
The error estimation strategy we adopt is underpinned by the following assumption.

Assumption 1
(Saturation Assumption)

Let
{{I}}^{*}:={I}\cup{R}_{{I}}
be the enhanced multi-index set. There exists a saturation constant
\gamma(t)\in[0,1)
for each
t\in[0,T]
such that

\displaystyle\|u^{h}({\cdot},t,{\cdot})-

\displaystyle u^{h,{{I}}^{*}}({\cdot},t,{\cdot})\|_{L^{2}_{\rho}(\Gamma;L^{2}(D))}

(35)

\displaystyle\leq\gamma(t)\|u^{h}({\cdot},t,{\cdot})-u^{{h,{I}}}({\cdot},t,{\cdot})\|_{L^{2}_{\rho}(\Gamma;L^{2}(D))}.

A saturation assumption for the interpolation error estimation is a standard ingredient in a posteriori error analysis of elliptic PDEs with random data, see e.g.
(
BSX22
, Section 4)
. The assumption can be justified by considering the analyticity of the solution with respect to
{\bf y}
, see e.g.
Feischl2021
.

Corollary 1
(Interpolation Error Bound)

If
1
holds, then the following upper bound holds for the interpolation error,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{{I}}(t)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1-\gamma(t)}\|u^{h,{{I}}^{*}}(\cdot,t,\cdot)-u^{h,{{I}}}(\cdot,t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}_{\rho}(\Gamma;L^{2}(D))}.

(36)

Proof

The result follows by writing

\displaystyle\|u^{h}(\cdot,t,\cdot)-u^{h,{{I}}}(\cdot,t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}_{\rho}(\Gamma;L^{2}(D))}=

(37)

\displaystyle\|u^{h}(\cdot,t,\cdot)-u^{h,{{I}}^{*}}(\cdot,t,\cdot)+

\displaystyle u^{h,{{I}}^{*}}(\cdot,t,\cdot)-u^{h,{{I}}}(\cdot,t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}_{\rho}(\Gamma;L^{2}(D))}

and applying the triangle inequality and
1
.

The upper bound in (
36
) is not computable — the saturation constant is unknown and the approximations
u^{h,{I}}(\mathbf{x},t,\mathbf{y})
and
u^{h,{{I}}^{*}}(\mathbf{x},t,\mathbf{y})
are only known
after
further approximation with timestepping. Hence, we consider the further splitting

\displaystyle\|u^{h,{{I}}^{*}}(\cdot

\displaystyle,t,\cdot)-u^{h,{I}}(\cdot,t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}_{\rho}(\Gamma;L^{2}(D))}

(38)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\underbrace{\|u^{h,{{I}}^{*},\delta}(\cdot,t,\cdot)-u^{h,{I},\delta}(\cdot,t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}_{\rho}(\Gamma;L^{2}(D))}}_{\pi_{{I}}(t)}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\|u^{h,{{I}}^{*}}(\cdot,t,\cdot)-u^{h,{{I}}^{*},\delta}(\cdot,t,\cdot)-u^{h,{I}}(\cdot,t,\cdot)+u^{h,{I},\delta}(\cdot,t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}_{\rho}(\Gamma;L^{2}(D))}}_{correction}

where
\pi_{{I}}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
is computable but there is now an additional correction term to estimate. We will call
\pi_{{I}}{(t)}
the interpolation error estimate.

Remark 2
(Interpolation error indicators)

We can bound the interpolation error estimate
\pi_{{I}}
as

\pi_{{I}}(t)\leq\sum_{\underline{\alpha}\in{R}_{{I}}}\pi_{{I},\underline{\alpha}}(t)

(39)

where

\displaystyle\pi_{{I},\underline{\alpha}}(t):=\bigg\|\sum_{{z}\in{Z}^{{{I}\cup\underline{\alpha}}}}u^{h,\delta}(\mathbf{x},t;{\mathbf{z}})L_{{\mathbf{z}}}^{{I}\cup\underline{\alpha}}(\mathbf{y})-\sum_{{z}\in{Z}^{{{I}}}}u^{h,\delta}(\mathbf{x},t;{\mathbf{z}})L_{{\mathbf{z}}}^{{I}}(\mathbf{y})\bigg\|_{L^{2}_{\rho}(\Gamma;L^{2}(D))}.

(40)

These indicators will guide the adaptive algorithm introduced in
Section

5
.

Now consider
u^{h,{{I}}},u^{h,{I},\delta},u^{h,{{I}}^{*}}
and
u^{h,{{I}}^{*},\delta}
in the correction term in (
38
). Rewriting these terms as linear combinations of Lagrange polynomials as in (
17
) gives

\displaystyle\big(u^{h,{{I}}^{*}}(\mathbf{x},t,\mathbf{y})-u^{h,{{I}}^{*},\delta}(\mathbf{x},t,\mathbf{y})\big)-\big(u^{h,{I}}(\mathbf{x},t,\mathbf{y})-u^{h,{I},\delta}(\mathbf{x},t,\mathbf{y})\big)

(41)

\displaystyle=\sum_{{z}\in{Z}^{{{I}}^{*}}\setminus{Z}^{{{I}}}}e^{h,\delta}(\mathbf{x},t;{\mathbf{z}})L_{{\mathbf{z}}}^{{{I}}^{*}}(\mathbf{y})+\sum_{z\in{Z}^{{{I}}}}e^{h,\delta}(\mathbf{x},t;{\mathbf{z}})\left(L_{{\mathbf{z}}}^{{{{I}}^{*}}}(\mathbf{y})-L_{{\mathbf{z}}}^{{{I}}}(\mathbf{y})\right),

where we have now introduced the
global timestepping error

e^{h,\delta}(\mathbf{x},t;{\mathbf{z}}):=u^{h}(\mathbf{x},t;{\mathbf{z}})-u^{h,\delta}(\mathbf{x},t;{\mathbf{z}})

(42)

for each collocation point
{\mathbf{z}}
at all times
t\in[0,T]
. Note that this error represents both timestepping error and an interpolation error due to the extension of the approximation from discrete times

to
[0,t_{n}]
in (
23
). Let
\mathcal{E}^{\text{ge}}(t;{\mathbf{z}}):=\|e^{h,\delta}(\mathbf{x},t;{\mathbf{z}})\|_{L^{2}(D)}
.
An upper bound for the correction term is then given by

\displaystyle\|u^{h,{{I}}^{*}}(\mathbf{x},t,\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle-u^{h,{{I}}^{*},\delta}(\mathbf{x},t,\mathbf{y})+u^{h,{I}}(\mathbf{x},t,\mathbf{y})-u^{h,{I},\delta}(\mathbf{x},t,\mathbf{y})\|_{L^{2}_{\rho}(\Gamma;L^{2}(D))}

(43)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{{z}\in{Z}^{{{I}}^{*}}\setminus{Z}^{{{I}}}}\mathcal{E}^{\text{ge}}(t;{\mathbf{z}})\|L_{{\mathbf{z}}}^{{{I}}^{*}}(\mathbf{y})\|_{L^{2}_{\rho}(\Gamma)}

\displaystyle+\sum_{{z}\in{Z}^{{{I}}}}\mathcal{E}^{\text{ge}}(t;{\mathbf{z}})\|L_{{\mathbf{z}}}^{{{I}}^{*}}(\mathbf{y})-L_{{\mathbf{z}}}^{{{I}}}(\mathbf{y})\|_{L^{2}_{\rho}(\Gamma)}

\displaystyle=:E^{{I},\delta}(t).

A similar approach can be used to bound the timestepping error in (
32
) as follows

\mathcal{E}^{\delta}(t)\leq E^{\delta}(t):=\sum_{{z}\in{Z}^{{{I}}}}\mathcal{E}^{\text{ge}}(t;{\mathbf{z}})\|L_{{\mathbf{z}}}^{{{I}}}(\mathbf{y})\|_{L^{2}_{\rho}(\Gamma)}.

(44)

For each
{\mathbf{z}}\in{Z}^{{{I}}^{*}}
, let
\pi_{ge}(t;{\mathbf{z}}):[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
be a global timestepping error estimate,

\pi_{\text{ge}}(t;{\mathbf{z}})\approx\mathcal{E}^{\text{ge}}(t;{\mathbf{z}}).

(45)

Then, using the set of error estimates
\{\pi_{\text{ge}}(t;{\mathbf{z}})\}_{{\mathbf{z}}\in{Z}^{{{I}}^{*}}}
the correction bound (

) can be estimated by

\displaystyle\pi_{{I},\delta}(t):=\sum_{{z}\in{Z}^{{{I}}^{*}}\setminus{Z}^{{{I}}}}

\displaystyle\pi_{ge}(t;{\mathbf{z}})\|L_{{\mathbf{z}}}^{{{I}}^{*}}(\mathbf{y})\|_{L^{2}_{\rho}(\Gamma)}

(46)

\displaystyle+\sum_{{z}\in{Z}^{{{I}}}}\pi_{ge}(t;{\mathbf{z}})\|L_{{\mathbf{z}}}^{{{I}}^{*}}(\mathbf{y})-L_{{\mathbf{z}}}^{{{I}}}(\mathbf{y})\|_{L^{2}_{\rho}(\Gamma)}

and the timestepping bound (

\pi_{\delta}(t):=\sum_{{z}\in{Z}^{{{I}}}}\pi_{\text{ge}}(t;{\mathbf{z}})\|L_{{\mathbf{z}}}^{{{I}}}(\mathbf{y})\|_{L^{2}_{\rho}(\Gamma)}.

(47)

Returning to the full bound (

\|u^{h}-u^{h,{I},\delta}\|_{L^{2}_{\rho}(\Gamma;L^{2}(D))}\leq\frac{1}{1-\gamma(t)}\left(\pi_{{I}}(t)+E^{{I},\delta}(t)\right)+E^{\delta}(t).

(48)

Dropping the unknown

and replacing

and

with the computable estimates

and

, we arrive at the final error estimator

{\pi(t):=\pi_{{I}}(t)+\pi_{{I},\delta}(t)+\pi_{\delta}(t),\quad\quad\quad t\in[0,T].}

(49)

4.1
Global Timestepping Error Estimation

The error estimators
\pi_{{I},\delta}
and
\pi_{\delta}
defined in (
46
) and (
47
) require a global timestepping error estimate for each collocation point
{\mathbf{z}}\in{Z}^{{{I}}^{*}}
. Relating the global error incurred by timestepping algorithms to the local error is a challenging problem with an array of literature on the subject
Shampine1976b
;
Skeel1986
;
Cao2004
.
A simple approach is to compare the approximation computed with local error tolerance
\delta
to a reference approximation computed with a smaller local error tolerance
{\delta}^{*}\ll\delta
and estimate the global error as follows
Skeel1986

\pi_{ge}(t;{\mathbf{z}}):=\|u^{h,{\delta}^{*}}(\mathbf{x},t;{\mathbf{z}})-u^{h,{\delta}}(\mathbf{x},t;{\mathbf{z}})\|_{L^{2}(D)}.

(50)

This is an expensive method — the enhanced approximation is even more expensive to compute than the original one.
The cost can be reduced by using a scaling argument which we describe next.

We assume that we are using a stable, convergent, order
p\geq 1
time integrator with local error control and that the ODE system (
9
) rewritten in the form

\frac{\textrm{d}\mathbf{u}^{h}}{\textrm{d}t}(t;{\mathbf{z}})=\mathbf{g}\left(t,\mathbf{u}^{h}(t;{\mathbf{z}})\right)

(51)

satisfies the Lipschitz condition where the solution is
p+1
times continuously differentiable.
The global error is then
\mathcal{E}^{\text{ge}}(t;{\mathbf{z}})=\mathcal{O}\left({\Delta t^{p}}\right)

(
Butcher2008
, Definition 2.3)
. Adaptive timestepping methods generally select timesteps based on the relationship between the local truncation error and timestep. For an order
p
method the local truncation error is
\mathcal{O}\left({\Delta t^{p+1}}\right)
and for a fixed local error tolerance
\delta
the timesteps are chosen so that
\Delta t\propto\delta^{\frac{1}{p+1}}

Shampine2005
;
Gresho2008
. Hence,

\mathcal{E}^{\text{ge}}(t;{\mathbf{z}})=\mathcal{O}\left({\delta^{\frac{p}{p+1}}}\right).

(52)

Using (
52
), for two local error tolerances
\delta
and
\delta_{0}\gg\delta
we may assume the crude relationship

\frac{\mathcal{E}^{\text{ge},\delta}(t;{\mathbf{z}})}{\mathcal{E}^{\text{ge},\delta_{0}}(t;{\mathbf{z}})}\approx\left(\frac{\delta}{\delta_{0}}\right)^{\frac{p}{p+1}},

(53)

where the superscript denotes the global error corresponding to the local error tolerance
\delta
, and similarly for
\delta_{0}
. Therefore,

{\mathcal{E}^{\text{ge},\delta}(t;{\mathbf{z}})}\approx\left(\frac{\delta}{\delta_{0}}\right)^{\frac{p}{p+1}}\mathcal{E}^{\text{ge},\delta_{0}}(t;{\mathbf{z}}).

(54)

Using a low fidelity approximation
u^{h,\delta_{0}}
with tolerance
\delta_{0}\gg\delta
and comparing to the computed approximation
u^{h,\delta}
means a global error estimate for the tolerance
\delta_{0}
can be formed as

\pi_{ge,\delta_{0}}(t,{\mathbf{z}}):=\|u^{h,{\delta}}(\mathbf{x},t;{\mathbf{z}})-u^{h,{\delta_{0}}}(\mathbf{x},t;{\mathbf{z}})\|_{L^{2}(D)}\approx\mathcal{E}^{\text{ge},\delta_{0}}(t,{\mathbf{z}}).

(55)

Combining this with (
54
) yields a cheap to compute global error estimate for approximations computed with the local error tolerance
\delta
. That is,

\displaystyle\pi_{\text{ge}}(t;{\mathbf{z}}):=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\delta}{\delta_{0}}\right)^{\frac{p}{p+1}}\pi_{\text{ge},\delta_{0}}(t,{\mathbf{z}})

(56)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\delta}{\delta_{0}}\right)^{\frac{p}{p+1}}\|u^{h,{\delta}}(\mathbf{x},t;{\mathbf{z}})-u^{h,{\delta_{0}}}(\mathbf{x},t;{\mathbf{z}})\|_{L^{2}(D)}.

We can then use this to compute our error estimates
\pi_{{I},\delta}(t)
and
\pi_{\delta}(t)
in (
46
) and (
47
).

Remark 3
(Choice of timestepping method)

In our numerical experiments we will use the
stabtr
implementation of TR-AB2 that is included in the
Matlab
software package
IFISS 3.6

Elman2007
. This is an order
p=2
method. Many alternative algorithms with local error control exist, including the built-in
Matlab
integrators described in
Shampine1997
. Ideally, we would use a time integration method that includes global error estimation but these are rarely implemented and used in practice. An example of such a method is
GERK

Shampine1976
but this is both an explicit method and more expensive than the TR-AB2 integrator.

5
Adaptive Strategy

To efficiently construct an approximation to the solution of the semi-discrete parametric advection–diffusion problem (
9
), we will
adapt
the multi-index set
{I}
in time whilst allowing the timestepping algorithm to adaptively choose timesteps for the deterministic systems associated with individual collocation points. The construction (
24
) only requires a small modification to allow this.
The approximation is now constructed with a time-dependent multi-index set

{I}:[0,T]\to\{\text{admissible multi-index sets in }d\text{ parameters}\}

(57)

with the adaptive approximation defined pointwise in time as

u^{\text{adapt}}(\mathbf{x},t,\mathbf{y}):=\sum_{{{\mathbf{z}}}\in{Z}^{{{I}(t)}}}u^{h,\delta}(\mathbf{x},t;{\mathbf{z}})L_{{\mathbf{z}}}^{{I}(t)}(\mathbf{y}).

(58)

To construct such an approximation we use a solve–estimate–mark–refine type loop in the spirit of Gerstner and Griebel to select an appropriate multi-index set
Gerstner2003
.
At carefully selected times, we estimate the interpolation error by
\pi_{{I}}(t)
as defined in (
38
), noting that the multi-index set
{I}
can now change through time. If the estimated interpolation error is greater than a chosen tolerance
\mathrm{tol}
at time
t
, we mark a set of multi-indices
\mathcal{J}\subset{R}_{{I}(t)}
based on the error indicators
\{\pi_{{I},\underline{\alpha}}(t)\}_{\underline{\alpha}\in{R}_{{I}(t)}}
defined in (
40
). The approximation (
58
) is then refined by adding the marked multi-indices to the construction of the approximation.

The time dependence in the problem provides an additional challenge as this adaptive strategy must be embedded
inside a time propagation loop. The approximation at each collocation point is constructed independently with different discrete timesteps. To
compute error estimates, the set of approximations must be synchronised at a common set of
synchronisation times
.
Such a set of synchronization times could be specified a priori, but would be better generated dynamically.
This can be done by starting at time
t=0
with an initial
synchronization timestep
\tau:=\tau_{0}
.
The approximation (
58
) and the error estimators
\pi_{{I}}
,

\pi_{{I},\delta}
and
\pi_{\delta}
defined in
(
38
), (
46
) and (
47
) would then be
computed at the new time
t+\tau
.
At this new time we must decide if the multi-index set needs to be refined by comparing
\pi_{{I}}(t+\tau)
to a chosen tolerance
\mathrm{tol}(t+\tau)
.
Given that the goal is to balance the error contributions then a
sensible strategy is to link the interpolation error tolerance
to the correction error estimated by (
46
). Specifically, we define

\mathrm{tol}(t):=c_{\mathrm{tol}}\cdot\pi_{{I},\delta}(t),

(59)

with a
safety factor

c_{\mathrm{tol}}>1
.
If the computed interpolation error estimate
\pi_{{I}}(t+\tau)

is less than
\mathrm{tol}(t+\tau)
then the synchronisation step would be
accepted
.
In this case, the time is reset
t\leftarrow t+\tau
and
the synchronization timestep is increased
\tau\leftarrow c_{+}\tau
,
where
c_{+}\geq 1
is a user-defined input.
Otherwise, the step is
rejected
.
In this case the interpolant would be refined so that
{I}\leftarrow{I}\cup\mathcal{J}

and the synchronization timestep is decreased
\tau\leftarrow c_{-}\tau

The attraction of this construction is that it generates an approximation in which the

In our experiments, time integration over each interval

\delta

The TR–AB2 algorithm outputs a sequence of spatial approximations and the corresponding

On each interval, for each collocation point, the adaptive timestepping will be initialised with the corresponding solution approximation at the previous two time integrator steps. This is necessary as AB2 is not self-starting.

)

Remark 4
(Shrinking the multi-index set)

The diffusive term will smooth the solution through time as illustrated in the motivational example in
Section

3
.
This suggests that it may be desirable to simplify the parametric approximation by shrinking the multi-index set.
This could be achieved by computing the error indicators (
40
) for each
\underline{\alpha}\in{I}{}
and defining a pruning algorithm to remove the smallest contributions.
Care would be required to ensure the resulting multi-index set is still admissible.
In practice, the computational savings may not be significant.
At later times when the diffusive smoothing is the dominant effect, the timesteps will have already grown large as seen in
Fig.

5(b)
.
Advancing the approximation in time, even with a large number of collocation points, is then cheap to compute.

6.1
Exploiting stability for computational savings

We now discuss how the smoothing in time that is inherent in the solution of parabolic PDE problems can be exploited to give computational savings.
By way of motivation, consider two ODE systems of the form (
9
) for a particular parameter
{\mathbf{z}}\in\Gamma
with initial data
\mathbf{u}^{h}(t_{k},{\mathbf{z}})
and
\mathbf{v}^{h}(t_{k},{\mathbf{z}})
respectively at a time
t_{k}\in[0,T]
.
The respective solutions
\mathbf{u}^{h}(t,{\mathbf{z}})
and
\mathbf{v}^{h}(t,{\mathbf{z}})
for
t\in[t_{k},T]
can be represented as matrix exponentials and the difference between the two solutions
\mathbf{r}^{h}(t,{\mathbf{z}})=\mathbf{u}^{h}(t,{\mathbf{z}})-\mathbf{v}^{h}(t,{\mathbf{z}})
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}^{h}(t,{\mathbf{z}})=

\displaystyle\exp\left(-(Q^{h})^{-1}(\epsilon D^{h}+A^{h}({\mathbf{z}}))(t-t_{k})\right)\mathbf{r}^{h}(t_{k},{\mathbf{z}}).

(67)

If
\mathbf{r}^{h}(t_{k},{\mathbf{z}})
represents the error at time
t_{k}
, then the behaviour of the norm of this error is controlled by the real components of the eigenvalues of the matrix
(Q^{h})^{-1}\left(\epsilon D^{h}+A^{h}({\mathbf{z}})\right)
.
The diffusion matrix
D^{h}
is symmetric and the advection matrix
A^{h}({\mathbf{z}})
is skew-symmetric for all
{\mathbf{z}}\in\Gamma
hence the range of the real components of the eigenvalues depends only on the diffusion matrix
Loghin2006
.
The diffusion matrix
D^{h}
is positive-definite hence the eigenvalues are real and positive.
This means that, for some constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
and
t\geq t_{k}

\|\mathbf{r}^{h}(t,{\mathbf{z}})\|_{L^{2}(D)}\leq C_{1}\exp(-C_{2}\epsilon(t-t_{k})).

(68)

We can interpret (
68
) as stating that an error at time
t_{k}
will (eventually) decay to zero through time.
Note that there is no parameter dependence in the diffusion matrix, so the bound is valid for all
{\mathbf{z}}\in\Gamma
.

When solving the ODE system (
9
) using adaptive timestepping, the accumulation of local error through time is related to the decay (
68
).
The local error made at each step will eventually decay through time and this is why local error control can give good global error control.
The bound (
68
) is independent of
{\mathbf{z}}
which suggests that the accumulation of local error into global error may be similar for all collocation points.
This is in fact the case, as demonstrated in
Fig.

8
.
This insight suggests that it should be possible to use a single global timestepping error estimate
\pi_{\text{ge}}(t)\approx\mathcal{E}^{h,\delta}(t;{\mathbf{z}})
for all collocation points
{\mathbf{z}}\in{Z}^{\text{adapt}}
as a mechanism for reducing the overall computational cost.
We could choose a fixed
{{\mathbf{z}}}^{*}\in\Gamma
, assume
\mathcal{E}^{h,\delta}(t;{{\mathbf{z}}}^{*})\approx\mathcal{E}^{h,\delta}(t;{\mathbf{z}})
for all
{\mathbf{z}}\in{Z}^{{{I}}^{*}}
and then let
\pi_{\text{ge}}(t):=\pi_{\text{ge}}(t;{{\mathbf{z}}}^{*})
.
Since the parametrisation of the wind in (
64

\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}\right]=\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{0})

in this case a sensible choice would be

Specifically,
\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\nabla\times\psi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

\mathcal{C}:L^{2}(D)\to L^{2}(D)
associated with a covariance function
C:D\times D\to\mathbb{R}

\left(\mathcal{C}\psi_{i}\right)(\mathbf{x}):=\int_{D}C(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\psi_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\textrm{d}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\lambda_{i}\psi_{i}(\mathbf{x}).

(72)

\mathbf{w}_{0}(\mathbf{x})=\nabla\times\psi_{0}(\mathbf{x})

62

C(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}):=\left(\prod_{i,j=1}^{2}(1-x_{i,j}^{2})\right)\sigma_{0}^{2}\exp\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{x}_{1}-\mathbf{x}_{2}\|_{2}^{2}}{L^{2}}\right)

(73)

. This is a modified version of the so-called Gaussian covariance function. The first term ensures that the stream function has zero variance on the boundary and therefore
\psi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=0

. The ensures that the wind field is always parallel to the boundary of

Conclusions

We introduced an adaptive nonintrusive stochastic collocation approximation scheme for a
time-dependent, parametric PDE and developed a corresponding error estimation strategy.
The algorithm is used to monitor errors relating to the timestepping and construct an adaptive approximation
in which the interpolation error is controlled to a related tolerance. The efficiency of the error estimation strategy is demonstrated numerically, highlighting the necessity of adaptive stochastic collocation approximation. The adaptive approximation is able to control the error by refining the polynomial approximation space through time and sequentially constructing a tailored approximation capturing the evolving uncertainty in the solution.

The results presented demonstrate the control the algorithm inputs give over constructing an approximation.
It is clear that tighter error control can be achieved by reducing the timestepping local error tolerance, but global error control in the timestepping would be required if we want rigorous control of the overall approximation error.
Stability of the underlying PDE plays an important role, firstly by ensuring local error control in the timestepping results in a good approximation and secondly by allowing the computational cost of constructing an approximation to be reduced through interpolation.
The results can be shown to extend to higher dimensional parameter spaces.
