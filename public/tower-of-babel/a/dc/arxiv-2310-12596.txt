The moduli space of flat maximal space-like embeddings in pseudo-hyperbolic space
Nicholas Rungi, Andrea Tamburelli

Abstract
. We study the moduli space of flat maximal space-like embeddings in ℍ 2 , 2 \mathbb{H}^{2,2} from various aspects. We first describe the associated Codazzi tensors to the embedding in the general setting, and then, we introduce a family of pseudo-Kähler metrics on the moduli space. We show the existence of two Hamiltonian actions with associated moment maps and use them to find a geometric global Darboux frame for any symplectic form in the above family.



1.
Introduction

This paper is the latest in a series that studies the pseudo-Riemannian and symplectic geometry of representations of surface groups into Lie groups of rank two (
[
MST21
,
RT21
,
RT23
]
). By work of Goldman (
[
Gol84
]
), character varieties of semi-simple Lie groups have a natural symplectic form. On the other hand, Labourie (
[
Lab17
]
) and Alessandrini-Collier (
[
AC19
]
) showed that in rank 2 many of their connected components enjoy a mapping class group invariant complex structure. The relation between the symplectic and complex geometry of character varieties appears, however, to be mysterious. The current paper has the purpose of shedding some light on the topic by introducing a pseudo-Kähler structure on the space of faithful representations of
\pi_{1}(T^{2})
into
\mathrm{SO}_{0}(2,3)
, up to conjugations.

First, given that
\mathrm{SO}_{0}(2,3)
can be identified with the group of orientation-preserving isometries of
\mathbb{H}^{2,2}
, we build on the recent theory of maximal space-like surfaces developed in
[
LT23
,
LTW23
,
Nie22
,
TW20
]
to identify the space of faithful representations of
\pi_{1}(T^{2})
into
\mathrm{SO}_{0}(2,3)
with the space
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{F}(T^{2})
of equivariant flat maximal embeddings of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
into
\mathbb{H}^{2,2}
. Using the embedding data of such maximal surfaces, our first result gives a parameterization of
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{F}(T^{2})
that is reminiscent of the analog parameterization of equivariant space-like maximal surfaces in
\mathbb{H}^{2,2}
in higher genus (
[
CTT19
]
):

Theorem A

(Theorem
2.4
)
.

The space
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{F}(T^{2})
of equivariant flat maximal embeddings
\mathbb{R}^{2}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{H}^{2,2}
is parameterized by the complement of the zero section of the bundle
\mathcal{Q}^{4}(T^{2})
of holomorphic quartic differentials over the Teichmüller space of the torus.

Next, we introduce new tensors related to maximal space-like embeddings into
\mathbb{H}^{2,2}
, which did not appear in the aforementioned papers, and use them to define a pseudo-Kähler structure on
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{F}(T^{2})
:

Theorem B

(Theorem
3.15
)
.

Let
f:[0,+\infty)\to(-\infty,0]
be a smooth function such that:

(i)

f^{\prime}(t)<0,\ \forall t\geq 0

(ii)

f(0)=0
.

Then the space
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{F}(T^{2})
admits a
\mathrm{MCG}(T^{2})
-invariant pseudo-Kähler structure
(\mathbf{g}_{f},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{f},\mathbf{I})
such that the complex structure
\mathbf{I}
comes from the identification of
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{F}(T^{2})
with the complement of the zero section in
\mathcal{Q}^{4}\big(T^{2}\big)
.

One of the main feature of this family of pseudo-Riemannian metrics, beside their compatibility with the natural complex structure on
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{F}(T^{2})
, is the fact that, once the function
f
is chosen as in Theorem
B
, they can be explicitly written in terms of a complex structure
J
and the real part of a holomorphic quartic differential on the torus, thus potentially allowing further investigations on the geometric properties of these metrics. As an example, we study the behaviour of two natural actions on
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{F}(T^{2})
with respect to these pseudo-Kähler structures. Indeed, identifying
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{F}(T^{2})
with the complement of the zero section in
\mathcal{Q}^{4}\big(T^{2}\big)
, we see that
S^{1}
acts on
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{F}(T^{2})
by rotations along the fibers and
\mathbb{P}\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
acts on a pair
(J,q)\in\mathcal{Q}^{4}\big(T^{2}\big)
by pull-back. It turns out these actions are geometric and can be used to better understand the symplectic forms
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{f}
:

Theorem C
.

The
S^{1}
- and
\mathbb{P}\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
-action on
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{F}(T^{2})
are Hamiltonian with respect to
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{f}
.
Moreover, their Hamiltonians can be chosen as action variables in a global Darboux frame for
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{f}
.

1.1.
Outline of the paper

The paper is structured as follows: in Section
2.1
we briefly introduce space-like maximal surfaces in
\mathbb{H}^{2,2}
and describe their Gauss-Codazzi-Ricci equations. In Section
2.2
we introduce new tensors that are closely related to holomorphic quartic differentials, and in Section
2.3
we analyze the equivariant flat case, showing that the associated moduli space can be parameterized as an open subset in the total space of a holomorphic bundle over the Teichmüller space of the torus. After introducing the proper tensors to be considered for the zero-curvature case and after studying their properties (Section
3.2
), the main result of the paper, namely, the existence of the pseudo-Kähler metric, is contained in Section
3.3
, the proof of which is also continued in Section
3.4
. Finally, other results regarding the properties of the pseudo-Kähler metric are obtained: the existence of an isometric and Hamiltonian circular action (Section
4.1
), the existence of an
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
Hamiltonian action (Section
4.2
), and the existence of a geometric global Darboux frame for the symplectic form (Section
4.3
).

2.
Maximal surfaces in
\mathbb{H}^{2,2}

In this section we recall some basic facts about maximal surfaces in the pseudo-hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{2,2}
and their geometric invariants. Most of the material covered here can be found in
[
LT23
]
and
[
Nie22
]
.

2.1.
Background

Let
\eta
be a quadratic form in
\mathbb{R}^{5}
of signature
(2,3)
. The pseudo-hyperbolic space is

\mathbb{H}^{2,2}=\mathbb{P}(\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{5}\ |\ \eta(x)<0\})\ .

The restriction of
\eta
to the tangent space
T_{x}\mathbb{H}^{2,2}
at each
x\in\mathbb{H}^{2,2}
induces a pseudo-Riemannian metric
g
of signature
(2,2)
and of constant sectional curvature
-1
. Its topological boundary

\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{2,2}=\mathbb{P}(\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{5}\ |\ \eta(x)=0\})

is the boundary at infinity for the pseudo-Riemannian metric
g
and it is endowed with a conformally flat Lorentzian structure.

Definition 2.1
.

An embedded surface
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{H}^{2,2}
is space-like if the restriction
g_{T}
of
g
to
T\Sigma
is positive definite. We say that
g_{T}
is the induced metric on
\Sigma
and the surface is called
complete
if its induced metric is complete.

If
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{H}^{2,2}
is a space-like surface, then its normal bundle
N\Sigma:=(T\Sigma)^{\perp_{g}}
inside
T\mathbb{H}^{2,2}
is endowed with a negative definite Riemannian metric
g_{N}
. Using the splitting
T\mathbb{H}^{2,2}=T\Sigma\perp_{g}N\Sigma
, the Levi-Civita connection on
\mathbb{H}^{2,2}
decomposes as

\nabla=\begin{pmatrix}\nabla^{T}&B\\
I\!\!I&\nabla^{N}\end{pmatrix}\ ,

where
\nabla^{T}
is the Levi-Civita connection of the induced metric on
\Sigma
,
I\!\!I\in\Omega^{1}(\Sigma,\Hom(T\Sigma,N\Sigma)
is the
second fundamental form
,
B\in\Omega^{1}(\Sigma,\Hom(N\Sigma,T\Sigma))
is the
shape operator
, and
\nabla^{N}
is compatible with the metric
g_{N}
. Since
\nabla
is torsion-free, the second fundamental form is actually symmetric, in the sense that

I\!\!I(X,Y)=I\!\!I(Y,X)\ \ \ \ \forall X,Y\in\Gamma(T\Sigma).

Moreover, the second fundamental form and the shape operator are related by

g_{N}(I\!\!I(X,Y),\xi)=-g_{T}(Y,B(X,\xi))

for all
X,Y\in\Gamma(T\Sigma)
and normal vector fields
\xi
.

Definition 2.2
.

A space-like surface
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{H}^{2,2}
is maximal if
\tr_{g_{T}}(I\!\!I)=0
.

It follows easily from the definition that a surface
\Sigma
is maximal if and only if in a
g_{T}
-orthonormal frame
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
of
T\Sigma
, we have

I\!\!I(e_{1},e_{1})=-I\!\!I(e_{2},e_{2})\ .

The induced metric, the second fundamental form and the metric on the normal bundle of a maximal surface satisfy some fundamental equations:

(1)

the Gauss equation

K_{g_{T}}=-1+\frac{1}{2}\|I\!\!I\|^{2}\ ,

where
K_{g_{T}}
denotes the Gaussian curvature of the induced metric
g_{T}
on
\Sigma
and the norm of the second fundamental form is defined by the formula

\|I\!\!I\|^{2}=-g_{N}(I\!\!I(e_{1},e_{1}),I\!\!I(e_{1},e_{1}))-g_{N}(I\!\!I(e_{2},e_{1}),I\!\!I(e_{2},e_{1}))

for a
g_{T}
-orthonormal frame
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
of
T\Sigma
;

(2)

the Ricci equation

R^{N}=\omega\otimes\varphi\ ,

where
R^{N}
denoted the curvature tensor of
g_{N}
,
\omega
is the area form on
\Sigma
for its induced metric, and
\varphi
is the unique endomorphism of
N\Sigma
such that

g_{T}(B(X,\xi),B(Y,\eta))-g_{T}(B(Y,\xi),B(X,\eta))=\omega(X,Y)g_{N}(\varphi(\xi),\eta)

for all
X,Y\in\Gamma(T\Sigma)
and
\xi,\eta\in\Gamma(N\Sigma)
.

(3)

the Codazzi equation

d^{D}I\!\!I=0

where
D
is the connection induced on
\Hom(T\Sigma,N\Sigma)
by
\nabla
and
\nabla^{N}
and the operator
d^{D}
is defined by

(d^{D}\theta)(X)Y:=D_{X}(\theta(Y))-D_{Y}(\theta(X))-\theta([X,Y])

for
X,Y\in\Gamma(T\Sigma)
.

As a consequence of the Codazzi equation, it is possible to introduce some holomorphic data on a maximal surface
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{H}^{2,2}
. Let
J
be the complex structure on
\Sigma
compatible with its induced metric and denote by
T^{\mathbb{C}}\Sigma
and
N^{\mathbb{C}}\Sigma
the complexifications of the tangent and normal bundle of
\Sigma
with respect to this complex structure. Let
K_{\Sigma}
be the holomorphic cotangent bundle of
\Sigma
. By
[
LT23
, Lemma 5.6]
, there exists a holomorphic section
\sigma
of
K^{2}_{\Sigma}\otimes N^{\mathbb{C}}\Sigma
such that
I\!\!I=\mathcal{R}e(\sigma)
. Moreover, the Gauss equation can be rewritten in terms of
\sigma
as

K_{g_{T}}=-1+\|\sigma\|^{2}_{h}\ ,

where
h
is the Hermitian extension of
g_{T}^{2}\otimes(-g_{N})
on
K^{2}_{\Sigma}\otimes N^{\mathbb{C}}\Sigma
.

The main holomorphic object that we are going to use in the sequel is the holomorphic quartic differential
q
on
\Sigma
defined out of
\sigma
by the expression

q:=-\frac{1}{4}g_{N}(\sigma,\sigma)\ .

2.2.
Other tensors

In this section we define other tensors related to maximal surfaces in
\mathbb{H}^{2,2}
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
S
be a smooth oriented surface endowed with a Riemannian metric
h
and let
J
be the (almost) complex structure defined by the conformal class of
h
. Suppose that a
(0,4)
tensor
T
and a
(1,3)
tensor
U
are related by
U=h^{-1}T
, then

(1)

T
is totally symmetric if and only if
U(X,Y)=U(Y,X)
is an
h
-symmetric endomorphism such that
U(X,Y)Z=U(Z,X)Y=U(Y,Z)X
for any
X,Y,Z\in\Gamma(TS)
;

(2)

T
is the real part of a complex quartic differential
q
on
(S,J)
if and only if the endomorphism
U(X,Y)
is trace-less and
U(JX,Y)=U(X,JY)=U(X,Y)J
for any
X,Y\in\Gamma(TS)
;

(3)

q
is holomorphic if and only if
\mathrm{d}^{\nabla}T=0
, where
\nabla
is the Levi-Civita connection of
h
. Moreover, the quartic differential can be written as

q=T(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)-iT(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot,J\cdot)\ .

Proof.

By definition, the tensor
U
is characterized by the property that

T(X,Y,Z,W)=h(U(X,Y)Z,W)\ \ \ \ \forall\ X,Y,Z,W\in\Gamma(TS)\ .

(1)

Let us prove that
U(X,Y)
is an
h
-symmetric endomorphism:

(
T
is totally symmetric)

\displaystyle h(U(X,Y)Z,W)

\displaystyle=T(X,Y,Z,W)

\displaystyle=T(X,Y,W,Z)

\displaystyle=h(U(X,Y)W,Z)\ .

The proofs of the other properties are similar and left to the reader.

(2)

Define
q=T(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)-iT(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot,J\cdot)
: it is a complex quartic differential with real part
T
if and only if
q(J\cdot,\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)=q(\cdot,J\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)=q(\cdot,\cdot,J\cdot,\cdot)=q(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot,J\cdot)=iq(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot,\cdot).
Since the last equality clearly holds, it is sufficient to prove that
T(J\cdot,\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)=T(\cdot,J\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)=T(\cdot,\cdot,J\cdot,\cdot)=T(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot,J\cdot)
. In terms of the tensor
U
, these conditions are equivalent to
U(JX,Y)=U(X,JY)=U(X,Y)J=-JU(X,Y)
for all
X,Y\in\Gamma(TS)
. Notice that the last equality says that the
h
-symmetric endomorphism
U(X,Y)
anti-commutes with
J
and this can happen if and only if
U(X,Y)
is trace-less.

(3)

We do this computation in a local holomorphic normal coordinate
z=x+iy
on
S
, which exists because the metric
h
is trivially Kähler. By item
(ii)
, the quartic differential
q
can be written locally as
q=fdz^{4}
for some smooth function
f
. We need to prove that
f
is holomorphic if and only if the tensor
T
is Codazzi. Now, using the symmetries of the tensor
T
proved in part
(i)
and
(ii)
, the Codazzi equation
0=(d^{\nabla}T)(\partial_{x},\partial_{y},\cdot,\cdot,\cdot):=(\nabla_{\partial_{x}}T)(\partial_{y},\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)-(\nabla_{\partial_{y}}T)(\partial_{x},\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
reduces to

\begin{cases}\partial_{x}(T(\partial_{y},\partial_{x},\partial_{x},\partial_{x})=\partial_{y}(T(\partial_{x},\partial_{x},\partial_{x},\partial_{x})\\
\partial_{x}(T(\partial_{x},\partial_{x},\partial_{x},\partial_{x})=-\partial_{y}(T(\partial_{y},\partial_{x},\partial_{x},\partial_{x})\end{cases}\ .

Since
f=q(\partial_{z},\partial_{z},\partial_{z},\partial_{z})=T(\partial_{x},\partial_{x},\partial_{x},\partial_{x})-iT(\partial_{x},\partial_{x},\partial_{x},\partial_{y}))
, we see that the above equations are exactly the Cauchy-Riemann equations for
f
, hence
q
is holomorphic.

∎

As a consequence, if
\Sigma
is a maximal surface in
\mathbb{H}^{2,2}
with associated quartic differential
q
, the real part
T
of
q
is a
(0,4)
-tensor satisfying the properties of Theorem
2.3
. It follows from the definitions that
T
can be expressed in terms of the second fundamental form as

T=g_{N}(I\!\!I(\cdot,\cdot),I\!\!I(\cdot,\cdot))-g_{N}(I\!\!I(\cdot,J\cdot),I\!\!I(\cdot,J\cdot))\ .

2.3.
The case of the torus

In this paper, we are interested in complete maximal embeddings
\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}^{2,2}
that are equivariant with respect to a faithful representation
\rho:\pi_{1}(T^{2})\rightarrow\mathrm{SO}_{0}(2,3)
. In this setting the tensors defined in Section
2.1
and
2.2
simplify considerably. First, by
[
LT23
, Corollary 5.7]
a complete maximal surface in
\mathbb{H}^{2,2}
is negatively curved, so an application of Gauss-Bonnet implies that it must be flat. Moreover, the quartic differential
q
is constant and non-zero because, otherwise, since
q=-\frac{1}{4}g_{N}(\sigma,\sigma)
, the holomorphic section
\sigma
and hence
I\!\!I=\mathcal{R}e(\sigma)
would vanish identically, implying that the maximal surface would be totally geodesic, but in
\mathbb{H}^{2,2}
totally geodesic space-like surfaces are isometric to
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. Complete flat maximal surfaces in
\mathbb{H}^{2,2}
are well-known: they are all obtained as orbits of a point under the action of a Cartan subgroup of
\mathrm{SO}_{0}(2,3)
and are known in the literature as Barbot surfaces (
[
LT23
]
) or horospherical surfaces (
[
BS10
,
Tam19
,
TW20
]
). In particular, their geometry is completely determined by a conformal structure and a non-zero holomorphic quartic differential:

Theorem 2.4
.

The space of complete equivariant flat maximal surfaces
(\Sigma,\rho)
up to the action of
\mathrm{SO}_{0}(2,3)
, is parameterized by the bundle of non-zero holomorphic quartic differentials over the Teichmüller space of the torus
\mathcal{T}(T^{2})
.

Proof.

Let
\rho:\pi_{1}(T^{2})\rightarrow\mathrm{SO}_{0}(2,3)
be a faithful representation and let
\Sigma
be a
\rho
-equivariant maximal surface in
\mathbb{H}^{2,2}
. Since all complete flat maximal surfaces in
\mathbb{H}^{2,2}
are equivalent under the action of
\mathrm{SO}_{0}(2,3)
(
[
LT23
, Proposition 2.27]
), up to conjugation, we can assume that
\Sigma
is the standard Barbot surface
\Sigma_{0}
. An explicit parameterization of
\Sigma_{0}\subset\mathbb{H}^{2,2}
is given by

f_{0}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{2}\sinh(2y),\sqrt{2}\sinh(2x),\cosh(2x)+\cosh(2y),\cosh(2x)-\cosh(2y),0\right)\ .

Note that
\Sigma_{0}
intersects the boundary at infinity of
\mathbb{H}^{2,2}
in four points
[0,\pm\sqrt{2},1,1,0]
and
[\pm\sqrt{2},0,1,-1,0]
connected by light-like segments.
Since
\Sigma_{0}
endowed with the complex structure compatible with its induced metric is biholomorphic to
\mathbb{C}
, the quotient
\Sigma_{0}/\rho(\pi_{1}(T^{2}))
defines a point
J\in\mathcal{T}(T^{2})
. Let
q
be the holomorphic quartic differential on the torus induced by
\Sigma_{0}
. The map that associates to
(\Sigma,\rho)
the pair
(J,q)
constructed in this way is a bijection. Indeed, it is easy to check that the parameterization
f_{0}
is conformal and the associated quartic differential is
q=dz^{4}
, for
z=x+iy
, thus all pairs
(J,q)
can be obtained by pre-composing
f_{0}
with an element of
\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R})
. On the other hand, let
(\Sigma_{1},\rho_{1})
and
(\Sigma_{2},\rho_{2})
be two complete equivariant flat maximal surfaces with the same data
(J,q)
. After renormalizing the surfaces by post-composing with a global isometry of
\mathbb{H}^{2,2}
, we can assume that they coincide with the standard Barbot surface
\Sigma_{0}
. Let
f_{i}:\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\Sigma_{0}
be conformal
\rho_{i}
-equivariant parameterizations of
\Sigma_{0}
. Then
\phi=f_{1}^{-1}\circ f_{2}
is a biholomorphism of
\mathbb{C}
such that
\phi^{*}q=q
. The only possibilities are
\phi(z)=\zeta_{4}z
for some forth root of unity
\zeta_{4}
. However, these coordinate changes have the effect of cyclically moving the four points at infinity of
\Sigma_{0}
, which can also realized by post-composing with an isometry
A\in\mathrm{SO}_{0}(2,3)
preserving the Barbot surface. Therefore, we can conclude that
f_{1}=A\circ f_{2}
and
\rho_{1}
is conjugate to
\rho_{2}
.
∎

Remark 2.5
.

The parameterization
f_{0}
given in the previous theorem is actually contained in the pseudo-hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{2,1}
, which coincides with the
3
-dimensional Anti-de Sitter space. We thus find the same surface studied by Bonsante-Seppi (
[
BS20
]
) in the case of equivariant flat immersions in
\mathbb{H}^{2,1}
.

3.
Geometry of the moduli space

In this section we focus on the case
\Sigma=T^{2}
, hence we are going to consider tensors associated with flat maximal space-like embeddings. For this reason, the complex structure and the second fundamental form induced on the surface, as well as the metric on the normal bundle, will no longer depend on the point but will be constant tensors defined on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. A similar approach was first used in
[
MST21
, §3.2,§3.3]
, and then in
[
RT21
, §3.1]
.

3.1.
Linear almost-complex structures

Let
\rho_{0}:=\mathrm{d}x_{0}\wedge\mathrm{d}y_{0}
be the standard area form on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and let us introduce

\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2}):=\{J\in\End(\mathbb{R}^{2})\ |\ J^{2}=-\mathds{1},\ \rho_{0}(v,Jv)>0\ \text{for some}\ v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}\}

to be the space of
\rho_{0}
-compatible linear-complex structures on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. It is a real two dimensional manifold and for any
J\in\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, the pairing
g_{J}(\cdot,\cdot):=\rho_{0}(\cdot,J\cdot)
gives a well-defined scalar product on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, with respect to which
J
is an orthogonal endomorphism. By differentiating the identity
J^{2}=-\mathds{1}
, it follows that

T_{J}\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})=\{\dot{J}\in\End(\mathbb{R}^{2})\ |\ J\dot{J}+\dot{J}J=0\}\ .

Equivalently, the space
T_{J}\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
can be identified with the trace-less and
g_{J}
-symmetric endomorphisms of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. It carries a natural (almost) complex structure given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}:T_{J}

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to T_{J}\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

\displaystyle\dot{J}\mapsto-J\dot{J}\ .

There is a natural scalar product defined on the tangent space

\langle\dot{J},\dot{J}^{\prime}\rangle_{J}:=\frac{1}{2}\tr(\dot J\dot J')

for each
\dot{J},\dot{J}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and it is easy to check that
\mathcal{I}
preserves
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{J}
. It is also possible to define a symplectic form on
T_{J}\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
by

\Omega_{J}(\dot{J},\dot{J}^{\prime})=-\langle\dot{J},J\dot{J}^{\prime}\rangle_{J}\ .

The group
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
acts by conjugation on
\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
: for any
A\in\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
one defines
A\cdot J:=AJA^{-1}
. The same formula can be used to define an action on
T_{J}\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

Lemma 3.1
.

The triple
\big(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle,\mathcal{I},\Omega\big)
defines a
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
-invariant Kähler metric on
\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

Lemma 3.2

(
[
Tra18
, Lemma 4.3.2]
)
.

Let
\mathbb{H}^{2}
be the hyperbolic plane with complex coordinate
z=x+iy
and with Kähler structure

g_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}=\frac{\mathrm{d}x^{2}+\mathrm{d}y^{2}}{y^{2}},\qquad\qquad\omega_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}=-\frac{\mathrm{d}x\wedge\mathrm{d}y}{y^{2}}\ .

Then, there exists a unique
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
-invariant Kähler isometry
j:\mathbb{H}^{2}\to\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
such that
j(i)=J_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
. It is given by the formula

(3.1)

j(x+iy):=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{x}{y}&-\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{y}\\
\frac{1}{y}&-\frac{x}{y}\end{pmatrix}\ .

After defining Teichmüller space of the torus
\mathcal{T}(T^{2})
as the set of isotopy classes of unit area flat metrics on
T^{2}
, then it is easy to see that there is a
\mathrm{MCG}(T^{2})\cong\SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
-equivariant isomorphism between
\mathcal{T}(T^{2})
and
\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. In particular, the Kähler metric
\big(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle,\mathcal{I},\Omega\big)

3.2.
A vector bundle over Teichmüller space

The next step is to define a vector bundle over
\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, using the pairwise description
(J,U)
where
U
is the tensor defined in Section

, so that the fiber over a point in Teichmüller space
\mathcal{T}(T^{2})
can be identified with the space of holomorphic quartic differentials on
(T^{2},J)
. In this regard, let us consider the following set

(3.2)

\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2}):=\{(J,T)\in\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\times S_{4}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\ |\ T(J\cdot,J\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)=-T\}

where

is the space of totally-symmetric
(0,4)

4
which fibre over
\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Moreover, for any
(J,T)\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
we can define the associated
(1,3)
tensor
U:=g_{J}^{-1}T
, namely
U

(3.3)

g_{J}\big(U(X,Y)Z,W\big)=T(X,Y,Z,W),\ \text{for any}\ X,Y,Z,W\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\ .

Since

is totally-symmetric, we have that
U(X,Y)Z=U(Z,X)Y=U(Y,Z)X
for any
X,Y,Z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Hence, we can consider the element

as a bi-linear symmetric form in the first two entries so that for any vectors
X,Y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
which is symmetric with respect to
g_{J}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

For any point
(J,T)
in
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
the following properties hold:

(i)

T(J\cdot,J\cdot,J\cdot,J\cdot)=T
;

(ii)

T(J\cdot,\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)=T(\cdot,J\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)=T(\cdot,\cdot,J\cdot,\cdot)=T(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot,J\cdot);

(iii)

U(JX,Y)Z=U(X,JY)Z=U(X,Y)JZ,\ \text{for any}\ X,Y,Z\in\mathbb{R}^{2};

(iv)

JU(X,Y)Z=-U(X,Y)JZ,\ \text{for any}\ X,Y,Z\in\mathbb{R}^{2};

(v)

The endomorphism part of

is trace-less.

Proof.

Throughout the proof we will denote by
X,Y,Z
and
W
arbitrary vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

(i)
The first property can be easily deduced:

(
T\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
)

\displaystyle T(JX,JY,JZ,JW)

\displaystyle=-T(J^{2}X,J^{2}Y,JZ,JW)

(
T

is totally-symmetric
)

\displaystyle=-T(JZ,JW,X,Y)

(
T\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
)

\displaystyle=T(J^{2}Z,J^{2},X,Y)

(
T

is totally-symmetric

\displaystyle=T(X,Y,Z,W)\ .

(ii)
The second claim follows from (

) and the following computation:

T(JX,Y,Z,W)=T(J^{2}X,JY,JZ,JW)=T(X,JY,J^{2}Z,J^{2}W)=T(X,JY,Z,W)\ ,

and the same applies to the other entries.

(iii)
The third one can be deduced from the definition of
U
and an application of
(ii)
. In fact,

\displaystyle g_{J}\big(U(JX,Y)Z,W\big)

\displaystyle=T(JX,Y,Z,W)

\displaystyle=T(X,JY,Z,W)

\displaystyle=g_{J}\big(U(X,JY)Z,W\big)

\displaystyle=T(X,Y,JZ,W)

\displaystyle=g_{J}\big(U(X,Y)JZ,W\big)\ .

(iv)
The fourth property is a consequence of the relation
g_{J}(J\cdot,J\cdot)=g_{J}
, as shown by the calculations performed below

\displaystyle g_{J}\big(JU(X,Y)Z,W\big)

\displaystyle=g_{J}\big(J^{2}U(X,Y)Z,JW\big)

\displaystyle=-T(X,Y,Z,JW)

(Property
(ii)
)

\displaystyle=-T(X,Y,JZ,W)

\displaystyle=-g_{J}\big(U(X,Y)JZ,W\big)\ .

(v)
Since the endomorphism
U(X,Y)
anti-commute with

it is both
g_{J}
-symmetric and trace-less.
∎

Proposition 3.5
.

There is an isomorphism as smooth vector bundles between

\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

Proof.

Recall that

-symmetric and trace-less, thus by Theorem

\displaystyle\Phi:\

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\longrightarrow\mathcal{Q}^{4}\big(T^{2}\big)

∎

In the following we will go on to explore further properties of the

Thus, we can write

Lemma 3.6
.

For any
(J,U)\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and using the notations above, we get
JU_{2}=U_{1}

Proof.

Let us write the matrices

c_{1}&d_{1}\end{pmatrix},\quad U_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{2}&b_{2}\\
c_{2}&d_{2}\end{pmatrix},\quad JU_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}-c_{2}&-d_{2}\\
a_{2}&b_{2}\end{pmatrix}\ .

We prove that the four entries of

are the same as those of
JU_{2}
. Details will be given for only one case, but the other three follow easily. In fact,

\displaystyle a_{1}=g_{J}\big(U_{1}\cdot e_{1},e_{1}\big)=g_{J}\big(U(e_{1},e_{1})e_{1},e_{1}\big)=T(e_{1},e_{1},e_{1},e_{1})

\displaystyle-c_{2}

∎

Let
(J,U)
and

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\Big(\tr(U_1U_1')\tr(g_J^{-1}\sigma_1g_J^{-1}\sigma_1)+\tr(U_2U'_2)\tr(g_J^{-1}\sigma_2g_J^{-1}\sigma_2)\Big)

In particular, the norm of a tensor

||U||_{J}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\tr((U_1)^2)+\tr((U_2)^2)\Big)=\tr((U_1)^2)\ ,

since

Lemma 3.7

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle A\cdot U^{\prime}=AU_{1}^{\prime}A^{-1}\otimes(A^{-1})^{*}\sigma_{1}+AU_{2}^{\prime}A^{-1}\otimes(A^{-1})^{*}\sigma_{2}\ .

Lemma 3.8

For any

3.3.
The pseudo-Kähler metric

In this section we first describe tangent vectors to the vector bundle
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and then we explain how to adapt the scalar product we defined in (
3.5
) on such elements, which will still be invariant for the
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
-action. Finally, we define a pseudo-Riemannian metric and a complex structure on
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
that will give rise to an
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
-invariant pseudo-Kähler metric on the subspace
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{F}(T^{2})
we are interested in.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
(J,U)\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and let
\dot{U}:=g_{J}^{-1}\dot{T}
be the unique
(1,3)
-tensor such that
g_{J}\big(\dot{U}(X,Y)Z,W\big)=\dot{T}(X,Y,Z,W)
for all
X,Y,Z,W\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

(\dot{J},\dot{U})\in T_{J}\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\times\Big(S_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\otimes\End(\mathbb{R}^{2})\Big)

belongs to
T_{(J,U)}\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
if and only if the following two conditions are satisfied:

\bullet

\dot{U}_{0}=\dot{\widetilde{U}}_{0}+C(J,U,\dot{J})
, where
\dot{U}_{0}
is the endomorphism full trace-less part of
\dot{U}
, while the tensor
\dot{\widetilde{U}}_{0}
is the trace-less part of
\dot{U}
which is symmetric and
g_{J}
-traceless in the bi-linear form part and

in a local basis, with
E=U_{1}J\dot{J}\mathrm{diag}(-1,1)
;

\bullet

\tr(\dot U(X,Y))=-\tr(J\dot JU(X,Y))
for any
X,Y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Proof.

The proof of the first requirement will become clear when, in Section
3.4
, we write the tangent vectors
(\dot{J},\dot{U})
in coordinates. Regarding the second one, notice that

\dot{g}_{J}=\rho(\cdot,\dot{J}\cdot)=-\rho(\cdot,J^{2}\dot{J}\cdot)=-g_{J}(\cdot,J\dot{J}\cdot)\ .

Moreover, for any
X,Y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
the endomorphism
U(X,Y)
is trace-less, hence

0=\delta\tr(U(X,Y))=\tr(\dot U(X,Y))-\tr(g^{-1}_J\dot{g_J}g^{-1}_JT)\ .

Using the formula for the variation of the metric we obtain
g_{J}^{-1}\dot{g_{J}}=-J\dot{J}
and so we conclude that

\tr(\dot U(X,Y))=-\tr(J\dot JU(X,Y))\ .

∎

Remark 3.10

For each
(J,U)\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

\delta U=\delta(g_{J}^{-1}T)=-g^{-1}_{J}\dot{g_{J}}g^{-1}_{J}T+g_{J}^{-1}\dot{T}=J\dot{J}U+\dot{U}\ .

\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

\delta U
. In addition, the vector
\dot{U}

Recall there is an

Let

g_{J}

From the formula of

Proposition 3.12
.

The endomorphism
\mathbf{I}

\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

Proof.

We need to initially prove that
\mathbf{I}^{2}=-\mathrm{Id}

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}^{2}(\dot{J},\dot{U})

\displaystyle=(J^{2}\dot{J},-(-\dot{U}J-U\dot{J})J-U(-J\dot{J}))

(

)

\displaystyle=(-\dot{J},-\dot{U}+U\dot{J}J+UJ\dot{J})

\displaystyle=(-\dot{J},-\dot{U})\ .

Since the almost-complex structure
\mathbf{I}

\dot{J}
as that of Lemma
3.1

-i

\mathcal{Q}^{4}\big(T^{2}\big)

-i\dot{q}

q
is a quartic

\mathcal{R}e(-iq)=\imaginary(q)=-T(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot,J\cdot)=-T(\cdot,\cdot,J\cdot,\cdot)\ .

Then, its variation satisfies

\displaystyle\widetilde{T}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot,\cdot):=\mathcal{R}e(-i\dot{q})=-\dot{T}(\cdot,\cdot,J\cdot,\cdot)-T(\cdot,\cdot,\dot{J}\cdot,\cdot)\ .

Let us denote with
\widetilde{U}

(1,3)

\widetilde{U}=g_{J}^{-1}\widetilde{T}

\displaystyle g_{J}\big(\widetilde{U}(X,Y)Z,W\big)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{T}(X,Y,Z,W)

\displaystyle=-\dot{T}(X,Y,JZ,W)-T(X,Y,\dot{J}Z,W)

\displaystyle=-g_{J}\big(\dot{U}(X,Y)JZ+U(X,Y)\dot{J}Z,W\big),\quad\text{for any}\ X,Y,Z,W\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\ .

In other words
\widetilde{U}=-\dot{U}J-U\dot{J}
, and we obtain the claim.
∎

Lemma 3.13
.

For any
(\dot{J},\dot{U})\in T_{(J,U)}\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, we have the following decomposition

(3.11)

\dot{U}J+U\dot{J}=\dot{U}_{0}J+\dot{U}_{\text{tr}}(\cdot,J\cdot)\ ,

where
\dot{U}_{0}J
and
\dot{U}_{\text{tr}}(\cdot,J\cdot)
represents the endomorphism full trace-less part and trace part, respectively.

Proof.

First recall that by Proposition
3.9
we can write
\dot{U}=\dot{U}_{0}+\dot{U}_{\text{tr}}
, hence

\dot{U}J+U\dot{J}=\dot{U}_{0}J+\dot{U}_{\text{tr}}J+U\dot{J}\ .

We obtain the claim if we show that
\dot{U}_{\text{tr}}J+U\dot{J}=\dot{U}_{\text{tr}}(\cdot,J\cdot)
. Let
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
be a
g_{J}
-orthonormal basis and let
\{e_{1}^{*},e_{2}^{*}\}
the dual one. Let
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
be the basis in (
3.4
), then

\dot{U}_{\text{tr}}J=(\dot{U}_{1})_{\text{tr}}J\otimes\sigma_{1}+(\dot{U}_{2})_{\text{tr}}J\otimes\sigma_{2},\quad(\dot{U}_{i})_{\text{tr}}=\frac{1}{2}\tr(\dot U_i)\mathds{1}\ \text{for}\ i=1,2\ .

Moreover, using Lemma
3.3
, we get

\displaystyle U\dot{J}

\displaystyle=U_{1}\dot{J}\otimes\sigma_{1}+U_{2}\dot{J}\otimes\sigma_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\Big(\tr(U_1\dot J)\mathds{1}-\tr(JU_1\dot J)J\Big)\otimes\sigma_{1}+\Big(\tr(U_2\dot J)\mathds{1}-\tr(JU_2\dot J)J\Big)\otimes\sigma_{2}\bigg)\ .

Next,
JU_{2}=U_{1}
implies that
\tr(U_1\dot J)=\tr(JU_2\dot J)=\tr(\dot U_2)
(see Proposition
3.9
) and
\tr(U_2\dot J)=-\tr(JU_1\dot J)=-\tr(\dot U_1)
. Summing up the two terms we are interested in, we can conclude that

\dot{U}_{\text{tr}}J+U\dot{J}=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\tr(\dot U_2)\mathds{1}\otimes\sigma_{1}-\tr(\dot U_1)\mathds{1}\otimes\sigma_{2}\bigg)\ ,

and it is not difficult to see that the last term is equal to
\dot{U}_{\text{tr}}(\cdot,J\cdot)
.
∎

Proposition 3.14
.

The symmetric bi-linear form
\mathbf{g}_{f}
is compatible with the complex structure
\mathbf{I}

Proof.

It boils down in proving that for each
(J,U)\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
the following holds:

(\mathbf{g}_{f})_{(J,U)}\big(\mathbf{I}(\dot{J},\dot{U}),\mathbf{I}(\dot{J}^{\prime},\dot{U}^{\prime})\big)=(\mathbf{g}_{f})_{(J,U)}\big((\dot{J},\dot{U}),(\dot{J}^{\prime},\dot{U}^{\prime})\big)\ .

By definition of the complex structure we obtain

\displaystyle(\mathbf{g}_{f})_{(J,U)}\big(\mathbf{I}(\dot{J},\dot{U}),\mathbf{I}(\dot{J}^{\prime},\dot{U}^{\prime})\big)

\displaystyle=(1-f)\langle J\dot{J},J\dot{J}^{\prime}\rangle+\frac{f^{\prime}}{2}\langle(\dot{U}J+U\dot{J})_{0},(\dot{U}^{\prime}J+U\dot{J}^{\prime})_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ -\frac{f^{\prime}}{2}\langle(\dot{U}J+U\dot{J})_{\text{tr}},(\dot{U}^{\prime}J+U\dot{J}^{\prime})_{\text{tr}}\rangle\ ,

where the functions
f
and
f^{\prime}
are evaluated at
\frac{\|U\|^{2}}{2}
, hence they are not changed after applying the complex structure. The first term can be simplified using the relation
J\dot{J}=-\dot{J}J
, indeed

\langle J\dot{J},J\dot{J}^{\prime}\rangle=\tr(J\dot J J\dot J')=-\tr(J^2\dot J\dot J')=\langle\dot{J},\dot{J}^{\prime}\rangle\ .

Regarding the second term, we use the decomposition (
3.11
), so that

\displaystyle\langle(\dot{U}J+U\dot{J})_{0},(\dot{U}^{\prime}J+U\dot{J}^{\prime})_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\dot{U}_{0}J,\dot{U}_{0}^{\prime}J\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\tr((\dot U_1)_0J(\dot U_1')_0J)+\tr((\dot U_2)_0J(\dot U_2')_0J)\bigg)+\tr(EJE'J)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\tr((\dot U_1)_0(\dot U_1')_0)+\tr((\dot U_2)_0(\dot U_2')_0)\bigg)+\tr(EJE'J)\ ,

where we used that the endomorphism part of
(\dot{U}_{i})_{0}
and
(\dot{U}_{i}^{\prime})_{0}
is
g_{J}
-symmetric and trace-less. Moreover, since
E=U_{1}J\dot{J}\mathrm{diag}(-1,1)
and
E^{\prime}=U_{1}J\dot{J}^{\prime}\mathrm{diag}(-1,1)
we conclude that
\tr(EJE'J)=\tr(EE')
which is exactly what we aimed for. Finally, for the last term, we use once again (
3.11
) and the expression in a
g_{J}
-orthonormal basis

\dot{U}_{\text{tr}}(\cdot,J\cdot)=(\dot{U}_{2})_{\text{tr}}\otimes(e_{2}^{*}\otimes e_{2}^{*}-e_{1}\otimes e_{1}^{*})+(\dot{U}_{1})_{\text{tr}}\otimes(e_{1}^{*}\otimes e_{2}^{*}+e_{2}^{*}\otimes e_{1}^{*})\ .

In fact,

\displaystyle\langle(\dot{U}J+U\dot{J})_{\text{tr}},(\dot{U}^{\prime}J+U\dot{J}^{\prime})_{\text{tr}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\dot{U}_{\text{tr}}(\cdot,J\cdot),\dot{U}^{\prime}_{\text{tr}}(\cdot,J\cdot)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\tr((\dot U_2)_{\text{tr}}(\dot U_2')_{\text{tr}})+\tr((\dot U_1)_{\text{tr}}(\dot U_1')_{\text{tr}})\bigg)

\displaystyle=\langle\dot{U}_{\text{tr}},\dot{U}_{\text{tr}}^{\prime}\rangle\ .

∎

Proposition 3.18
.

For any

\mathbf{g}_{f}

\boldsymbol{\omega}_{f}
is closed on
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}\cong\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

Proof.

The tensor
\mathbf{g}_{f}
can be written as:

(\mathbf{g}_{f})_{(z,w)}=\begin{pmatrix}\Theta&\Xi\\
\Gamma&\Delta\end{pmatrix}

where
\Theta,\Xi,\Gamma,\Delta
are

\Theta=\frac{1}{y^{2}}\big(1-f+4y^{4}(u^{2}+v^{2})f^{\prime}\big)\mathds{1}_{2\times 2},\qquad\Delta=y^{4}f^{\prime}\mathds{1}_{2\times 2}\ .

\Theta
and

\det\Big((\g_f)_{(z,w)}\Big)=y^{4}(f^{\prime})^{2}(1-f)^{2}\ .

The right hand side of the last equation is always non-zero thanks to the property of the function

(\mathbf{g}_{f})_{(z,w)}
is non-degenerate at each point
(z,w)\in\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}
.

Coefficient

Definition 4.1

be the map
\psi_{g}(p):=g\cdot p
, then we say the group
G
acts by symplectomorphisms on
(M,\omega)
if
\psi_{g}^{*}\omega=\omega
for all
g\in G
.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
G
be a Lie group, with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, acting on a symplectic manifold
(M,\omega)
by symplectomorphisms. We say the action is
Hamiltonian
if there exists a smooth function
\mu:M\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}
satisfying the following properties:

(i)

The function
\mu
is equivariant with respect to the
G
-action on
M
and the co-adjoint action on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, namely

(4.1)

\mu_{g\cdot p}=\Ad^{*}(g)(\mu_{p}):=\mu_{p}\circ\Ad(g^{-1})\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}\ .

(ii)

Given
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
, let
X_{\xi}
be the vector field on
M
generating the action of the
1
-parameter subgroup generated by
\xi
, i.e.
X_{\xi}=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\text{exp}(t\xi)\cdot p|_{t=0}
. Then, for every
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
we have

(4.2)

\mathrm{d}\mu^{\xi}=\iota_{X_{\xi}}\omega=\omega(X_{\xi},\cdot)

where
\mu^{\xi}:M\to\mathbb{R}
is the function
\mu^{\xi}(p):=\mu_{p}(\xi)
.

A map
\mu
satisfying the two properties above is called a
moment map
for the Hamiltonian action.

4.1.
The circle action

The space of holomorphic quartic differentials on
T^{2}
has a natural circle action given by

\displaystyle S^{1}\times\mathcal{Q}^{4}(T^{2})

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}^{4}(T^{2})

\displaystyle(\theta,J,q)

\displaystyle\mapsto(J,e^{-i\theta}q)\ .

By taking the real part of
q
, we have an induced circle action on
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

\displaystyle\Psi_{\theta}:\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

\displaystyle(J,U)

\displaystyle\mapsto(J,\cos(\theta)U-\sin(\theta)UJ)\ .

Theorem 4.3
.

The function

H(J,U)=\frac{1}{2}f\left(\frac{\|U\|^{2}}{2}\right)\

is a Hamiltonian for the circle action on
(\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),\boldsymbol{\omega}_{f})
. Moreover,
\Phi_{\theta}^{*}\mathbf{g}_{f}=\mathbf{g}_{f}
.

Proof.

The infinitesimal generator of the circle action is

X_{(J,U)}=\frac{d}{d\theta}_{\big|_{\theta=0}}\Psi_{\theta}(J,U)=(0,-UJ)\ .

Now, at a point
(J,U)\in\mathcal{MF}(T^{2})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{X}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{f}((\dot{J},\dot{U}))

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{f}((\dot{J},\dot{U}),(0,UJ))=\mathbf{g}_{f}((\dot{J},\dot{U}),\mathbf{I}(0,UJ))

\displaystyle=\mathbf{g}_{f}((\dot{J},\dot{U}),(0,-UJ^{2}))=\mathbf{g}_{f}((\dot{J},\dot{U}),(0,U))

\displaystyle=\frac{f^{\prime}}{2}\langle\dot{U}_{0},U\rangle

where the function
f^{\prime}
is evaluated at
\|U\|^{2}/2
. It remains to check that this last expression coincides with the differential of
H
. To this aim, we first compute the derivative of the norm of
U
in the direction of
(\dot{J},\dot{U})
:

\displaystyle(\|U\|_{J}^{2})^{\prime}

\displaystyle=2\langle U,U^{\prime}\rangle

(
\langle U,\dot{U}_{tr}\rangle=0
)

\displaystyle=2\langle U,\dot{U}\rangle+2\langle U,J\dot{J}U\rangle

\displaystyle=2\langle U,\dot{U}_{0}\rangle+2\langle U,J\dot{J}U\rangle

\displaystyle=2\langle U,\dot{U}_{0}\rangle

because

2\langle U,J\dot{J}U\rangle=\tr(U_{1}J\dot{J}U_{1})+\tr(U_{2}J\dot{J}U_{2})

and both traces vanish by Lemma
3.3
. As a consequence,

\mathrm{d}H_{(J,U)}(\dot{J},\dot{U})=\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime}\left(\frac{\|U\|^{2}}{2}\right)\cdot\frac{1}{2}(\|U\|_{J}^{2})^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime}\left(\frac{\|U\|^{2}}{2}\right)\langle U,\dot{U}_{0}\rangle

so the function
H
is an Hamiltonian for the circle action.

Let us now prove that
\Psi_{\theta}
preserves the metric. First we note that the differential of
\Psi_{\theta}
at a point
(J,U)\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\Psi_{\theta}(\dot{J},\dot{U})

\displaystyle=(\dot{J},\cos(\theta)\dot{U}-\sin(\theta)(\dot{U}J+U\dot{J}))

\displaystyle=(\dot{J},\cos(\theta)\dot{U}+\sin(\theta)\mathbf{I}(\dot{U}))

and then we compute

\displaystyle(\Psi_{\theta}^{*})\mathbf{g}_{f}((\dot{J},\dot{U}),

\displaystyle(\dot{J}^{\prime},\dot{U}^{\prime}))=\mathbf{g}_{f}(\mathrm{d}\Psi_{\theta}(\dot{J},\dot{U}),\mathrm{d}\Psi_{\theta}(\dot{J}^{\prime},\dot{U}^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\mathbf{g}_{f}((\dot{J},\cos(\theta)\dot{U}+\sin(\theta)\mathbf{I}(\dot{U})),(\dot{J}^{\prime},\cos(\theta)\dot{U}^{\prime}+\sin(\theta)\mathbf{I}(\dot{U}^{\prime})))

\displaystyle=(1-f)\langle\dot{J},\dot{J}^{\prime}\rangle+\frac{f^{\prime}}{2}\Bigg[\cos^{2}(\theta)\bigg(\langle\dot{U}_{0},\dot{U}_{0}^{\prime}\rangle-\langle\dot{U}_{tr},\dot{U}_{tr}^{\prime}\rangle\bigg)

\displaystyle\ \ +\sin^{2}(\theta)\bigg(\langle\mathbf{I}(\dot{U})_{0},\mathbf{I}(\dot{U}^{\prime})_{0}\rangle-\langle\mathbf{I}(\dot{U})_{tr},\mathbf{I}(\dot{U}^{\prime})_{tr}\rangle\bigg)

\displaystyle\ \ +\cos(\theta)\sin(\theta)\bigg(\langle\mathbf{I}(\dot{U})_{0},\dot{U}_{0}^{\prime}\rangle+\langle\dot{U}_{0},\mathbf{I}(\dot{U}^{\prime})_{0}\rangle-\langle\mathbf{I}(\dot{U})_{tr},\dot{U}_{tr}^{\prime}\rangle-\langle\dot{U}_{tr},\mathbf{I}(\dot{U}^{\prime})_{tr}\rangle\bigg)\Bigg]\ .

We already observed that
\mathbf{I}(\dot{U})_{0}=-\dot{U}_{0}J
and
\langle\dot{U}_{0}J,\dot{U}_{0}^{\prime}J\rangle=\langle\dot{U}_{0},\dot{U}_{0}^{\prime}\rangle
. Similarly,
\mathbf{I}(\dot{U})_{tr}=-\dot{U}_{tr}(\cdot,J\cdot)
and
\langle\dot{U}_{tr}(\cdot,J\cdot),\dot{U}_{tr}^{\prime}(\cdot,J\cdot)\rangle=\langle\dot{U}_{tr},\dot{U}_{tr}^{\prime}\rangle
. Therefore, in order to complete the proof, it is sufficient to check that the term multiplied by
\cos(\theta)\sin(\theta)
vanishes. This follows from a direct computation similar to Proposition
3.14
.
∎

4.2.
The
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
-action

In Section
3.2
, we introduced an
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
-action on
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
(see expression
3.6
). We now prove that it is Hamiltonian for the symplectic forms
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{f}
.

Proposition 4.4
.

The
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
-action on
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is Hamiltonian with moment map

\mu_{X}(J,U)=(1-f)\tr(JX)

where
f
is evaluated at
\|U\|^{2}/2
and
X\in\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

We start by computing the infinitesimal generator of this action for a given
X\in\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})

V_{X}(J,U)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(e^{tX}Je^{-tX},(e^{-tX})^{*}T)|_{t=0}

The first component is clearly equal to
XJ-JX=[X,J]
. For the second component, define
P_{t}:=e^{tX}
. We need to compute

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}T((P_{t})^{-1}\cdot,(P_{t})^{-1}\cdot,

\displaystyle(P_{t})^{-1}\cdot,(P_{t})^{-1}\cdot)|_{t=0}=

\displaystyle-T(X\cdot,\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)-T(\cdot,X\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)-T(\cdot,\cdot,X\cdot,\cdot)-T(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot,X\cdot)\ .

If
\widetilde{T}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
is defined as the right hand side term of the equation above, then the associated
(3,1)
-tensor
\widetilde{U}=g_{J}^{-1}\widetilde{T}
satisfies

\displaystyle g_{J}(\widetilde{U}(Y,Z)W,R)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{T}(Y,Z,W,R)

\displaystyle=-T(XY,Z,W,R)-T(Y,XZ,W,R)-T(Y,Z,XW,R)-T(Y,Z,W,XR)

\displaystyle=-g_{J}(U(XY,Z)W,R)-g_{J}(U(Y,XZ)W,R)-g_{J}(U(Y,Z)XW,R)+

\displaystyle\ \ \ -g_{J}(U(Y,Z)W,XR)

\displaystyle=-g_{J}(U(XY,Z)W+U(Y,XZ)W+U(Y,Z)XW+X^{*}U(Y,Z)W,R)

for all
Y,Z,W,R\in\Gamma(T\mathbb{R}^{2})
, where
X^{*}
denotes the adjoint of
X
with respect to
g_{J}
. Hence, we have

\widetilde{U}(\cdot,\cdot)=-U(X\cdot,\cdot)-U(\cdot,X\cdot)-UX-X^{*}U\ .

By using the decomposition
X=X^{s}+X^{a}
in its symmetric and skew-symmetric part, we can write the second component of
V_{X}(J,U)
as:

(4.3)

\underbrace{-U(X^{a}\cdot,\cdot)-U(X^{s}\cdot,\cdot)-U(\cdot,X^{a}\cdot)-U(\cdot,X^{s}\cdot)+[X^{a},U]}_{\text{trace-less part}}-\underbrace{(UX^{s}+X^{s}U)}_{\text{trace part}}\ .

We now need to check that the function
\mu_{X}
in the statement satisfies the properties of a moment map, as in Definition
4.2
. The equivariance (Property (i)) is a direct consequence of Lemma
3.6
: if
A\in\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
and
X\in\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
, then

\displaystyle\mu_{A\cdot(J,U)}(X)

\displaystyle=\bigg(1-f\bigg(\frac{1}{2}||A\cdot U||_{A\cdot J}^{2}\bigg)\bigg)\tr(AJA^{-1}X)

\displaystyle=\bigg(1-f\bigg(\frac{1}{2}||U||^{2}_{J}\bigg)\bigg)\tr(JA^{-1}XA)

\displaystyle=\mu_{(J,U)}\circ\Ad(A^{-1})(X)

\displaystyle=\Ad^{*}(A)(\mu_{(J,U)})(X)\ .

Let us now move to Property (ii). Let
\mu^{X}:\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}
be the map

\mu^{X}(J,U)=\Big(1-f\bigg(\frac{\|U\|^{2}_{J}}{2}\bigg)\Big)\tr(JX)\ ,

then

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{(J,U)}\mu^{X}(\dot{J},\dot{U})

\displaystyle=-\frac{f^{\prime}}{2}\bigg(||U||^{2}_{J}\bigg)^{\prime}\tr(JX)+(1-f)\tr(\dot JX)

\displaystyle=-f^{\prime}\langle U,\dot{U}_{0}\rangle\tr(JX)+(1-f)\tr(\dot JX)\ .

Now let
V_{X}
be the infinitesimal generator of the action, then

(4.4)

\displaystyle\iota_{V_{X}}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{f}(\dot{J},\dot{U})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{g}_{f}(V_{X}(J,U),\mathbf{I}(\dot{J},\dot{U}))

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{f-1}{2}\tr([X,J]J\dot J)}_{(i)}+\frac{f^{\prime}}{2}\bigg[\underbrace{\langle[X^{a},U],-\dot{U}_{0}J\rangle}_{(ii)}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\langle-U(X^{a}\cdot,\cdot)-U(\cdot,X^{a}\cdot),-\dot{U}_{0}J\rangle}_{(iii)}+

\displaystyle-\langle U(X^{s}\cdot,\cdot)-U(\cdot,X^{s}\cdot),-\dot{U}_{0}J\rangle-\langle-(UX^{s}+X^{s}U),-\dot{U}_{tr}(\cdot,J\cdot)\rangle\bigg]

where we used the decomposition in (
4.3
). The term
(i)
is

\displaystyle\frac{1-f}{2}\tr(\dot JX+JXJ\dot J)=(1-f)\tr(\dot J X)

by trace symmetry and
\dot{J}J+J\dot{J}=0
. Using the fact that
X^{a}=-\frac{1}{2}\tr(JX)J
and the properties of the tensor
U
in Lemma
3.4
, the sum of terms
(ii)
and
(iii)
can be further simplified into

\displaystyle(ii)+(iii)

\displaystyle=-\frac{f^{\prime}}{4}\tr(JX)\bigg[2\langle UJ,\dot{U}_{0}J\rangle+2\langle UJ,\dot{U}_{0}J\rangle\bigg]

\displaystyle=-f^{\prime}\tr(JX)\langle U,\dot{U}_{0}\rangle\ .

It only remains to show that

(4.5)

\langle U(X^{s}\cdot,\cdot)+U(\cdot,X^{s}\cdot),\dot{U}_{0}J\rangle=\langle UX^{s}+X^{s}U,\dot{U}_{tr}(\cdot,\cdot)\rangle

To do so, we will use a basis of
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
, namely we can write
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})=\Span_{\mathbb{R}}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3}\}
where

\xi_{1}=J_{0},\qquad\xi_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad\xi_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\ .

The only symmetric matrices of this basis are
\xi_{2}
and
\xi_{3}
, hence it is sufficient to prove equation (
4.5
) when
X^{s}=\xi_{2}
and
X^{s}=\xi_{3}
, since all the elements are linear in
X\in\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
. In both cases we use the description in coordinates
z=x+iy
and
w=u+iv
for points in
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and their variations, introduced in Section
3.4
. In particular we can do the computation in
(z,w)=(i,w)
by
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
-invariance. We denote by
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
a
g_{J}
-orthonormal basis and by
\{e_{1}^{*},e_{2}^{*}\}
its dual. Moreover, in order to simplify the notation, we put
e_{ij}^{*}:=e_{i}^{*}\otimes e_{j}^{*}
.

•

Consider
X^{s}=\xi_{2}
. In this case
X^{s}e_{1}=e_{1}
and
X^{s}e_{2}=-e_{2}
, hence

\displaystyle U(X^{s}\cdot,\cdot)

\displaystyle=(e_{11}^{*}+e_{22}^{*})U_{1}+(e_{12}^{*}-e_{21}^{*})U_{2}

\displaystyle U(\cdot,X^{s}\cdot)

\displaystyle=(e_{11}^{*}+e_{22}^{*})U_{1}+(-e_{12}^{*}+e_{21}^{*})U_{2}\ .

Therefore,

\displaystyle\langle U(X^{s}\cdot,\cdot)+U(\cdot,X^{s}\cdot),\dot{U}_{0}J\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bigg[\tr((g_{J}^{-1}e_{11}^{*})^{2})\tr(U_{1}(\dot{U}_{1})_{0}J)+

\displaystyle\ \ \ +\tr((g_{J}^{-1}e_{22}^{*})^{2})\tr(U_{1}(2EJ-(\dot{U}_{1})_{0}J))\bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bigg[\tr(U_{1}(\dot{U}_{1})_{0}J)+2\tr(U_{1}EJ)-\tr(U_{1}(\dot{U}_{1})_{0}J)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\tr(U_{1}EJ)=2|w|^{2}\dot{x}\ .

On the other hand,

\displaystyle UX^{s}+X^{s}U

\displaystyle=(e_{11}^{*}-e_{22}^{*})(U_{1}X^{s}+X^{s}U_{1})+(e_{12}^{*}+e_{21}^{*})(U_{2}X^{s}+X^{s}U_{2})

\displaystyle\dot{U}_{tr}(\cdot,J\cdot)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(e_{11}^{*}-e_{22}^{*})\tr(\dot{U}_{2})\mathds{1}-\frac{1}{2}(e_{12}^{*}+e_{21}^{*})\tr(\dot{U}_{1})\mathds{1}\ ,

thus

\displaystyle\langle UX^{s}+X^{s}U,\dot{U}_{tr}(\cdot,J\cdot)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\bigg[\frac{1}{2}\tr(\dot{U}_{2})\tr((g_{J}^{-1}(e_{11}^{*}-e_{22}^{*}))^{2})\tr(U_{1}X^{s}+X^{s}U_{1})+

\displaystyle\ \ \ -\frac{1}{2}\tr(\dot{U}_{1})\tr((g_{J}^{-1}(e_{11}^{*}+e_{22}^{*}))^{2})\tr(U_{2}X^{s}+X^{s}U_{2})\bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}[\tr(\dot{U}_{2})\tr(U_{1}X^{s})-\tr(\dot{U}_{1})\tr(U_{2}X^{s})]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}[4(u\dot{x}-v\dot{y})u+2(u\dot{y}+v\dot{x})v]=2|w|^{2}\dot{x}\ .

•

If
X_{s}=\xi_{3}
, we have instead
X_{s}e_{1}=e_{2}
and
X_{s}e_{2}=e_{1}
, hence

\displaystyle U(X^{s}\cdot,\cdot)

\displaystyle=(e_{11}^{*}+e_{22}^{*})U_{2}+(-e_{12}^{*}+e_{21}^{*})U_{1}

\displaystyle U(\cdot,X^{s}\cdot)

\displaystyle=(e_{11}^{*}+e_{22}^{*})U_{2}+(e_{12}^{*}-e_{21}^{*})U_{1}\ .

Therefore,

\displaystyle\langle U(X^{s}\cdot,\cdot)+U(\cdot,X^{s}\cdot),\dot{U}_{0}J\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bigg[\tr(U_{2}(\dot{U}_{1})_{0}J)+2\tr(U_{2}EJ)-\tr(U_{2}(\dot{U}_{1})_{0}J)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\tr(U_{2}EJ)=-2|w|^{2}\dot{y}\ .

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\langle UX^{s}+X^{s}U,\dot{U}_{tr}(\cdot,J\cdot)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\bigg[\frac{1}{2}\tr(\dot{U}_{2})\tr((g_{J}^{-1}(e_{11}^{*}-e_{22}^{*}))^{2})\tr(U_{1}X^{s}+X^{s}U_{1})+

\displaystyle\ \ \ -\frac{1}{2}\tr(\dot{U}_{1})\tr((g_{J}^{-1}(e_{11}^{*}+e_{22}^{*}))^{2})\tr(U_{2}X^{s}+X^{s}U_{2})\bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}[\tr(\dot{U}_{2})\tr(U_{1}X^{s})-\tr(\dot{U}_{1})\tr(U_{2}X^{s})]

\displaystyle=2v(u\dot{x}-v\dot{y})-2u(u\dot{y}+v\dot{x})=-2|w|^{2}\dot{y}\ .

Thus, equation (
4.5
) is proved and the theorem as well.
∎

4.3.
Global action-angle variables

Let
(M,\omega)
be a symplectic manifold and
f\in C^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R})
, then the
Hamiltonian vector field

\mathbb{X}_{f}\in\Gamma(TM)
associated with
f
is defined by the following property

(4.6)

\omega(\mathbb{X}_{f},Y)=\mathrm{d}f(Y),\quad\forall Y\in\Gamma(TM)\ .

If
(M,\omega)
is an Hamiltonian system, namely it is a symplectic manifold endowed with a smooth function
H
, then
f\in C^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R})
is called an integral of motion if

\omega(\mathbb{X}_{f},\mathbb{X}_{H})=0\ .

An Hamiltonian system
(M,\omega,H)
is called
completely integrable
if there exist
n
integral of motions
f_{1}=H,\dots,f_{n}
such that

(i)
The differentials
(\mathrm{d}f_{1})_{p},\dots,(\mathrm{d}f_{n})_{p}
are linearly independent for each
p\in M
.

(ii)
They are pairwise in involution, i.e.
\omega(\mathbb{X}_{f_{i}},\mathbb{X}_{f_{j}})=0
for each
i,j=1,\dots,n
.

To each completely integrable Hamiltonian system, special coordinates
\{\theta_{1},\psi_{1},\dots,\theta_{n},\psi_{n}\}
on
M
can be found as a consequence of the Arnlod-Liouville Theorem (
[
Arn13
]
). They form a global Darboux frame for the symplectic form

\omega=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{d}\theta_{i}\wedge\mathrm{d}\psi_{i}\ ,

but in general, the action variables
\{\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{n}\}
are not the given integral of motions. In the following we will show first that our space
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{F}(T^{2})
can be endowed with the above structure and later that, two of the four coordinates given by the Arnlod-Liouville Theorem are actually the integral of motions, and thus have geometric significance.

In the following, we will use the existence of the Hamiltonian actions of
S^{1}
and
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
on
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{F}(T^{2})
(see Section
4.1
and Section
4.2
).

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
(M,\omega)
be a symplectic manifold endowed with a Hamiltonian
G
-action and moment map
\mu_{G}:M\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}
. If
H\leq G
is any closed subgroup, then the restricted
H
-action is Hamiltonian with moment map
\mu_{H}:M\to\mathfrak{h}^{*}
given by
\mu_{H}:=|_{\mathfrak{h}}\circ\mu_{G}
, where
|_{\mathfrak{h}}:\mathfrak{g}^{*}\to\mathfrak{h}^{*}
is the map which associates to each functional on
\mathfrak{g}
its restriction on
\mathfrak{h}
.

Notice that inside
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
there is the subgroup of diagonal matrices with determinant equal to one, namely

(4.7)

\bigg\{\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&0\\
0&\frac{1}{\lambda}\end{pmatrix}\ \bigg|\ \lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{*}\bigg\}<\SL(2,\mathbb{R})\ .

In particular, such a subgroup can be identified with a copy of
\mathbb{R}^{*}
which still acts in a Hamiltonian fashion, by the previous lemma, on the space
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{F}(T^{2})
. Moreover, from Theorem
2.4
we know that
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{F}(T^{2})
can be identified with
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}^{*}
. Let
z=x+iy
be a complex coordinate on
\mathbb{H}^{2}
and
w=u+iv
on
\mathbb{C}
, then the Hamiltonian function of the circular action is given by
H_{1}(z,w)=\frac{1}{2}f\big(y^{4}|w|^{2}\big)
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\mathbb{R}^{*}
be a copy of the subgroup of diagonal matrices in
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
and consider its restricted Hamiltonian action on
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{F}(T^{2})
, then the Hamiltonian function is given by

H_{2}(z,w)=2\frac{x}{y}\big(1-f(y^{4}|w|^{2})\big)\ .

Proof.

The Lie algebra of
\mathbb{R}^{*}
can be identified with

\mathfrak{h}:=\bigg\{\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&0\\
0&-\alpha\end{pmatrix}\ \bigg|\ \alpha\in\mathbb{R}\bigg\}\ .

If
\mu
denotes the moment map for the
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
-action of Proposition
4.4
, then the associated moment map for the restricted
\mathbb{R}^{*}
-action
\mu_{\mathfrak{h}}
is

\mu^{X}_{\mathfrak{h}}(z,w)=\big(1-f(y^{4}|w|^{2})\big)\tr(j(z)X),

where
X\in\mathfrak{h}
. Let
\xi:=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}\in\mathfrak{h}
, then the Hamiltonian function
H_{2}:\mathcal{M}\mathcal{F}(T^{2})\to\mathbb{R}
is
H_{2}(z,w):=\mu^{\xi}_{\mathfrak{h}}(z,w)
, given that
\mathrm{d}\mu^{\xi}_{\mathfrak{h}}=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{f}(V_{\xi},\cdot)
, where

V_{\xi}=2\bigg(x\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+y\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\bigg)-4\bigg(u\frac{\partial}{\partial u}+v\frac{\partial}{\partial v}\bigg)

is the infinitesimal generator of the action. Finally, since

\tr(j(z)\xi)=\tr\bigg(\begin{pmatrix}\frac{x}{y} & -\frac{x^2+y^2}{y} \\ \frac 1{y} & -\frac x{y}
\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1
\end{pmatrix}\bigg)=2\frac{x}{y}

we get
\displaystyle H_{2}(z,w)=2\frac{x}{y}\big(1-f(y^{4}|w|^{2})\big)
.
∎

Proposition 4.7
.

The Hamiltonian system
(\mathcal{M}\mathcal{F}(T^{2}),\boldsymbol{\omega}_{f},H_{1})
is completely integrable. The integral of motions are given by

H_{1}(z,w)=\frac{1}{2}f\big(y^{4}|w|^{2}\big),\quad H_{2}(z,w)=2\frac{x}{y}\big(1-f(y^{4}|w|^{2})\big)\ .

Proof.

Let
\mathbb{X}_{H_{1}},\mathbb{X}_{H_{2}}
be the Hamiltonian vector fields associated with
H_{1},H_{2}
. An explicit expression is given by

\mathbb{X}_{H_{1}}=u\frac{\partial}{\partial v}-v\frac{\partial}{\partial u},\quad\mathbb{X}_{H_{2}}=2\bigg(x\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+y\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\bigg)-4\bigg(u\frac{\partial}{\partial u}+v\frac{\partial}{\partial v}\bigg)\ .

It is clear that they are point-wise linearly independent on
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{F}(T^{2})
, hence to end the proof we only need to show that they are involutive. The symplectic form is

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{f})_{(z,w)}=

\displaystyle\bigg(-1+f-4f^{\prime}y^{4}(u^{2}+v^{2})\bigg)\frac{\mathrm{d}x\wedge\mathrm{d}y}{y^{2}}-f^{\prime}y^{4}\mathrm{d}u\wedge\mathrm{d}v

The map
\pi:=(H_{1},H_{2}):\mathcal{M}\mathcal{F}(T^{2})\to B
is a Lagrangian fibration over the open subset
B:=\pi\big(\mathcal{M}\mathcal{F}(T^{2})\big)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, which is actually contained in the set of regular values of
\pi
. In particular, by using the explicit description of the Hamiltonian functions, it is not difficult to see that
B
is diffeomorphic to
\{(u_{1},u_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\ |\ u_{1}<0\}
, hence it is contractible. The fibre over a point
b\in B

\pi^{-1}(b)=\big\{(z,w)\in\mathcal{M}\mathcal{F}(T^{2})\ |\ \frac{1}{2}f\big(y^{4}|w|^{2}\big)=b_{1},\ 2\frac{x}{y}\big(1-f(y^{4}|w|^{2})\big)=b_{2}\big\}\ ,

which is a copy of
\mathbb{R}\times S^{1}
. Let us denote with
\{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\}
the angle coordinates given by the Arnold-Liouville theorem applied to
\big(\mathcal{M}\mathcal{F}(T^{2}),\boldsymbol{\omega}_{f},H_{1},H_{2}\big)
, then we can prove the following

Theorem 4.8
.

The coordinates
\{\theta_{1},H_{1},\theta_{2},H_{2}\}
are a global Darboux frame for
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{f}
, or in other words

\boldsymbol{\omega}_{f}=\mathrm{d}\theta_{1}\wedge\mathrm{d}H_{1}+\mathrm{d}\theta_{2}\wedge\mathrm{d}H_{2}\ .

Proof.

\pi^{-1}(b)
over a point

diffeomorphic to

. Moreover, the Hamiltonian vector fields
