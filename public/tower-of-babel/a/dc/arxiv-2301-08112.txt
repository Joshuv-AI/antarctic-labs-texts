The Existence and Dimension of the Attractor for a 3D Flow of a Non-Newtonian Fluid subject to Dynamic Boundary Conditions Thanks: The authors were supported by the project No. 20-11027X financed by GAČR.
Dalibor Pražák, Buddhika Priyasad

Abstract
We consider non-Newtonian incompressible 3D fluid of Ladyzhenskaya type, in the setting of the dynamic boundary condition. Assuming sufficient growth rate of the stress tensor with respect to the velocity gradient, we establish explicit dimension estimate of the global attractor in terms of the physical parameters of the problem.



1
Introduction.

The existence of global attractor, its finite-dimensionality, and
possibly even the construction of a finite-dimensional exponential
attractor belong to prototypical results of the dynamical
theory of nonlinear evolutionary PDEs.
These goals are often attained, as long as the system
is well-posed and dissipative. The literature being too extensive
to quote, let us
mention the basic monographs

[
7
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
22
]
,
[
23
]
. On the other hand, an explicit dimension estimate of the
attractor is a different matter, requiring additional tools from
functional analysis, and considerably more demanding in view of the
regularity of the underlying solution semigroup.

Focusing to the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations as a model
problem, one can say that in 2D, the problem of the attractor dimension
is rather well understood. Reasonable upper estimates are available for
various domains, even unbounded ones, and the results are known to be sharp
for the torus
, see
recent paper
[
12
]
and the references therein.
For the 3D case, weak solutions
exist
globally, but the uniqueness remains a
famous open problem even for the torus. One can still define (sort of) an attractor,
but nothing can be said about its dimension. Consequently, various regularizations of
the problem, more or less well-motivated physically, have been proposed, for
which these problems were then successfully addressed, cf. for
example
[
11
]
for the so-called Euler-Bardina
regularization.

In the present paper, we consider one such classical modification,
going back to Ladyzhenskaya
[
14
]
, where additional gradient integrability
is induced by a non-linear modification of the viscous stress tensor via the

r
-Laplacian type term
\displaystyle\left\lvert\boldsymbol{Du}\right\rvert^{r-2}\boldsymbol{Du}
. Thus, one the one hand, the
problem becomes well-posed
in 3D
for values only slightly above the
NSE-critical value
r=2
. On the other hand, such
a
highest order
nonlinearity brings additional complications to the analysis, as in
particular higher regularity of weak solutions is difficult to obtain in
dimensions other than two. Note that this so-called Ladyzhenskaya model
is well-motivated physically
[
17
]
.

The problem of the attractor dimension, and more generally, the
structural complexity of the dynamics, is presumable highly sensitive to
the adopted boundary condition. Motivated by this, we further generalize
our setting to allow for a non-linear evolution on
\partial\Omega
,
which is driven by the normal stress force of the fluid, exerted across
the boundary. Our result is new in particular by providing an explicit
(asymptotic) dimension estimate for 3D fully non-linear problem, while remaining
in the setting of weak solutions only.

Let us finally mention some related publications and results concerning
our model, i.e. the Ladyzhenskaya
r
-fluid. For basic existence and
uniqueness theory of weak solutions under dynamic boundary conditions,
see recent paper
[
1
]
, cf. also
[
18
]
.
Existence of finite-dimensional exponential attractors was recently
established in a rather general setting, but without explicit dimension estimates

[
21
]
. Concerning the Dirichlet boundary conditions,
explicit dimension estimates in 3D setting were previously
obtained in
[
4
]
, to which the current paper is
a direct generalization. Improved dimension estimates, based
on the volume contraction method, were also obtained
in the 2D setting by
[
13
]
, and for suitably
regularized problem again in 3D setting
[
19
]
.

2
Formulation of the Problem and the Main Result.

We consider generalized Navier-Stokes equations with dynamic
boundary condition on a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3},\ \Omega\in{\mathcal{C}}^{0,1}
and bounded time interval
(0,T)
. We
denote space-time domain by
Q:=(0,T)\times\Omega
, and by

\Gamma:=(0,T)\times\partial\Omega
the space-time boundary.
We further denote unknown velocity by
v:Q\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}

and unknown pressure of the fluid by
\pi:Q\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
The quantity
\boldsymbol{S}
is called the extra stress tensor and
here it is assumed to be a function of the symmetric velocity
gradient
2\boldsymbol{Dv}=\nabla\boldsymbol{v}+(\nabla\boldsymbol{v})^{T}
.
The external body force
\boldsymbol{f}:Q\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
is
independent of time.

An essential feature of our model is that we incorporate
the so-called dynamic boundary condition, so that the
tangential velocity component is subject to a certain
non-linear response
\boldsymbol{s}=\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{v})
on
\Gamma
.
Our system thus reads

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}-\text{div}\ \boldsymbol{S}+\text{div}\ (\boldsymbol{v}\otimes\boldsymbol{v})+\nabla\pi

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{f}

\displaystyle\text{ in }Q,

(2.1a)

\displaystyle\text{div}\ \boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{ in }Q,

(2.1b)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{ on }\Gamma,

(2.1c)

\displaystyle-(\boldsymbol{Sn})_{\tau}

\displaystyle=\alpha\boldsymbol{s}+\beta\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle\text{ on }\Gamma,

(2.1d)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}(0)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{v}_{0}

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega\cup\partial\Omega.

(2.1e)

Concerning the constitutive functions
\boldsymbol{S}=\boldsymbol{S}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v})

and
\boldsymbol{s}=\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{v})
,
we
assume polynomial growth in terms of certain
r
and
q\geq 2
. More precisely:

for all
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{D}_{1},\boldsymbol{D}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{sym}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{S}(\boldsymbol{0})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0},

(2.2)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\boldsymbol{S}(\boldsymbol{D}_{1})-\boldsymbol{S}(\boldsymbol{D}_{2})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}\left(\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}\left(\left\lvert\boldsymbol{D}_{1}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\boldsymbol{D}_{2}\right\rvert\right)^{r-2}\right)\left\lvert\boldsymbol{D}_{1}-\boldsymbol{D}_{2}\right\rvert,

\displaystyle\left(\boldsymbol{S}(\boldsymbol{D}_{1})-\boldsymbol{S}(\boldsymbol{D}_{2})\right):\left(\boldsymbol{D}_{1}-\boldsymbol{D}_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq c_{2}\left(\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}\left(\left\lvert\boldsymbol{D}_{1}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\boldsymbol{D}_{2}\right\rvert\right)^{r-2}\right)\left\lvert\boldsymbol{D}_{1}-\boldsymbol{D}_{2}\right\rvert^{2}.

Furthermore, it is assumed that
\boldsymbol{S}
has a potential,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{S}(\boldsymbol{D})

\displaystyle=\partial_{\boldsymbol{D}}\Phi\left(\left\lvert\boldsymbol{D}\right\rvert^{2}\right),

(2.3)

\displaystyle c_{3}\left(\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}\left\lvert\boldsymbol{D}\right\rvert^{r-2}\right)\left\lvert\boldsymbol{D}\right\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\Phi\left(\boldsymbol{D}\right)\leq c_{4}\left(\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}\left\lvert\boldsymbol{D}\right\rvert^{r-2}\right)\left\lvert\boldsymbol{D}\right\rvert^{2}.

Typical example is the so-called Ladyzhenskaya fluid

\boldsymbol{S}(\boldsymbol{D})=\nu_{1}\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}+\nu_{2}\left\lvert\boldsymbol{Dv}\right\rvert^{r-2}\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}

(2.4)

Regarding the boundary nonlinearity
\boldsymbol{s}
, we require that for
all
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}
,
\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{0})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0},

(2.5)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{v}_{1})-\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq c_{5}\left\lvert\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\right\rvert,

\displaystyle\left(\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{v}_{1})-\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\right)\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq c_{6}\left\lvert\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\right\rvert^{2},

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{v}}

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\displaystyle\geq c_{7}\left(\left\lvert\boldsymbol{s}\right\rvert^{\bar{q}}+\left\lvert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rvert^{q}\right),\text{ where }{}^{1}\!/_{q}+{}^{1}\!/_{\bar{q}}=1.}

Here, we also impose the existence of a potential, i.e.

\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{v})=\partial_{\boldsymbol{v}}\mathcal{S}(\boldsymbol{v})

(2.6)

Without loss of generality, let
\mathcal{S}(\boldsymbol{0})=\boldsymbol{0}
.
It is obvious that
\mathcal{S}
obeys upper and lower

q
-growth bounds
, in view of (
2.5
).

Our main result, stated somewhat informally, reads as follows.

Main Theorem.

Let
r>12/5
and
\boldsymbol{f}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. Then
the system (
2.1a
–
2.1e
) has a global attractor in
L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
. Moreover, its dimension can be explicitly estimated in terms of the data.

See Theorem
4.1
below for a precise statement and proof. We note that the solutions are not uniquely determined by initial conditions in
L^{2}
only. Yet they immediately become more regular (and hence unique), as follows from Theorems
3.2
and
3.3
. This issue of initial nonuniqueness is easily avoided in our setting of short trajectories.

As a by-product of the time regularity, we obtain that the attractor is bounded
in
W^{1,r}
, and the solutions on attractor are
1/2
-Hölder continuous with
values in
L^{2}
. One can expect that additional, i.e. spatial regularity is
also available, so that the solutions would be in fact strong. We leave this
problem to the forthcoming paper.

3
Well-posedness and Additional Time Regularity.

We carry out our analysis with dynamical boundary condition which includes the time derivative of the velocity
\boldsymbol{v}
of the fluid weighted by the parameter
\beta
. This set up demands a specific type function spaces. First we introduce such function spaces and later we define the Gelfand triplet. We essentially follow the functional set up used in
[
1
, Section 3]
.

For
\Omega
a Lipschitz domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e.,
\Omega\in{\mathcal{C}}^{0,1},\ \beta\geq 0
and
r\in(0,\infty)
, we define
{\mathcal{V}}\subset{\mathcal{C}}^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})\times{\mathcal{C}}^{0,1}(\partial\Omega)
as

{\mathcal{V}}:=\left\{\left(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{g}\right)\in{\mathcal{C}}^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})\times{\mathcal{C}}^{0,1}(\partial\Omega):\text{div}\ \boldsymbol{v}=0\text{ in }\Omega,\ \boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0,\text{ and }\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{g}\text{ on }\partial\Omega\right\}

With the help of
{\mathcal{V}}
, we define

\displaystyle V_{r}

\displaystyle:=\overline{{\mathcal{V}}}^{\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{V_{r}}},\ \text{where}\ \left\lVert(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{g})\right\rVert_{V_{r}}:=\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{W^{1,r}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{g}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)},

(3.1)

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle:=\overline{{\mathcal{V}}}^{\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{H}},\ \text{where}\ \left\lVert(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{g})\right\rVert^{2}_{H}:=\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\beta\left\lVert\boldsymbol{g}\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}

(3.2)

Note that
H
is a Hilbert space with respect to the above norm.
We also remark that if
(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{g})\in V_{r}
, then necessarily

\boldsymbol{g}=tr\ \boldsymbol{v}
. With some abuse of notation,
V_{r}
can thus
be identified with its first component
v
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\in H
,
\boldsymbol{f}\in L^{r^{\prime}}(0,T;V^{\prime}_{r})
,

be given,
and let

.
Then there exists at least one weak solution
\boldsymbol{v}
to (
2.1
),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H)\cap L^{r}(0,T;V_{r}),

(3.3)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle\in L^{r^{\prime}}(0,T;V^{\prime}_{r}).

The solution satisfies energy equality, and the initial
condition
\boldsymbol{v}(0)=\boldsymbol{v}_{0}
holds for the representative

\boldsymbol{v}\in C([0,T];H)
.

Proof.

We only sketch the proof, referring to
[
1
]
for details.
Take the scalar product of (
2.1a
) with an arbitrary

\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in V_{r}
, integrate the result over
\Omega
, and use integration by parts to obtain

\int_{\Omega}[\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}+(\boldsymbol{S}-\boldsymbol{v}\otimes\boldsymbol{v}):\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi}-\pi\text{div}\boldsymbol{\varphi}]\ dx+\int_{\partial\Omega}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi}\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{v}\otimes\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{S}\right]\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}\ dS=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}\ dx

(3.4)

By utilizing the symmetry of
\boldsymbol{S}
, (
2.1c
), (
2.1d
), and the properties of
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
(
\text{div}\boldsymbol{\varphi}=0
in
\Omega

\boldsymbol{\varphi}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0

\partial\Omega

\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}\ dx+\beta\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}\ dS+\int_{\Omega}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{S}(\boldsymbol{Dv})}-\boldsymbol{v}\otimes\boldsymbol{v}\right]:\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi}\ dx+\alpha\int_{\partial\Omega}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{s}}(\boldsymbol{v})\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}\ dS\\
=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}\ dx

(3.5)

Formally, we set
\boldsymbol{\varphi}:=\boldsymbol{v}
in (
3.5
), and use

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{v}\otimes\boldsymbol{v}):\nabla\boldsymbol{v}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\partial_{i}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}dx=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\partial_{i}\left\lvert\boldsymbol{v_{j}}\right\rvert^{2}dx,

(3.6)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(-\int_{\Omega}\text{div}\boldsymbol{v}\left\lvert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rvert^{2}dx+\int_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\left\lvert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rvert^{2}dS\right)=0,

(3.7)

where we have used (

\frac{1}{2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{d}{dt}}\left(\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rvert^{2}\ dx+\beta\int_{\partial\Omega}\left\lvert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rvert^{2}\ dS\right)+\int_{\Omega}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{S}(\boldsymbol{Dv}):\boldsymbol{Dv}}\ dx+\alpha\int_{\partial\Omega}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{s}}(\boldsymbol{v})\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\ dS={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\langle\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle_{V_{r}^{\prime},V_{r}}}.

For the right hand side of (

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle_{V_{r}^{\prime},V_{r}}\leq\left\lVert\boldsymbol{f}\right\rVert_{V_{r}^{\prime}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{V_{r}}\leq c_{1}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{f}\right\rVert_{V_{r}^{\prime}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{W^{1,r}}

\displaystyle\leq c_{2}(\varepsilon)\left\lVert\boldsymbol{f}\right\rVert^{r^{\prime}}_{V_{r}^{\prime}}+\frac{\varepsilon}{c_{3}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert^{r}_{W^{1,r}},\ (\varepsilon>0)

Then by
(

) and

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert^{2}_{H}+c_{5}\left[\nu_{1}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{Dv}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}+\nu_{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{Dv}\right\rVert^{r}_{r}\right]{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}+c_{4}\alpha\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{L^{q}(\Gamma)}^{q}}\leq c_{2}(\varepsilon)\left\lVert\boldsymbol{f}\right\rVert^{r^{\prime}}_{V_{r}^{\prime}}+\varepsilon\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert^{r}_{H}.

(3.9)

We combine compactness and monotonicity arguments to obtain the
existence of a solution as a limit of a suitable approximate
problem, e.g. the Galerkin scheme. Remark that

is the
critical value which ensures that the convective term belongs
to the proper dual space. Hence in particular, any weak

]
or
[

]
.
∎

Weak solutions are non-unique in general, unless additional
regularity is assumed. In particular, analogously to

[
4

, one proves:

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}
be weak solutions with
\boldsymbol{u}(0)=\boldsymbol{v}(0)
, and furthermore, let
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}\in L^{\frac{2r}{2r-3}}(0,T;V_{r})

Proof.

Test the equation for
\boldsymbol{w}:=\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}
by
\boldsymbol{w}
.
Using the identity

\int_{\Omega}\left(\boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}\otimes\boldsymbol{v}\right):\nabla\boldsymbol{w}=\int_{\Omega}\left(\boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}\otimes\boldsymbol{v}\right):\nabla\boldsymbol{w}=\int_{\Omega}\left(\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{w}

(
in view of

\displaystyle\text{div}\left(\boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{u}\right)=\left(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}\right)\boldsymbol{u}+\left(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla\right)\boldsymbol{u}
) as well as (
2.2

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}+\beta\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\right)+c_{2}\int_{\Omega}I^{2}(\boldsymbol{Du},\boldsymbol{Dv})\ dx+\alpha\int_{\partial\Omega}\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{s}}(\boldsymbol{u})-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{s}}(\boldsymbol{v})\right)\boldsymbol{w}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}dS}\leq\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\boldsymbol{w}\right\rvert^{2}\left\lvert\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\right\rvert{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}dx},

where

By monotonicity we have

\alpha\int_{\partial\Omega}\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{s}}(\boldsymbol{u})-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{s}}(\boldsymbol{v})\right)\boldsymbol{w}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}dS}\geq 0.

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}+\beta\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\right)+c_{2}\int_{\Omega}I^{2}(\boldsymbol{Du},\boldsymbol{Dv})\ dx\leq\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\boldsymbol{w}\right\rvert^{2}\left\lvert\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\right\rvert{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}dx}

(3.13)

By Korn inequality (Lemma

in the Appendix), we have

\int_{\Omega}I^{2}(\boldsymbol{Du},\boldsymbol{Dv})\ dx\geq\nu_{1}\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\boldsymbol{Dw}\right\rvert^{2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}dx}+\nu_{2}\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\boldsymbol{Dw}\right\rvert^{r}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}dx}\geq c\nu_{1}\left(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}\right\rVert^{2}_{W^{1,2}(\Omega)}-\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\right).

(3.14)

We further estimate, using (
B.2
), cf.
the Appendix,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\boldsymbol{w}\right\rvert^{2}\left\lvert\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\right\rvert{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}dx}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{r}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}\right\rVert^{2}_{\frac{2r}{r-1}}\leq c_{3}\left\lVert\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{r}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}\right\rVert^{\frac{2r-3}{r}}_{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}\right\rVert^{\frac{3}{r}}_{W^{1,2}(\Omega)},

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c_{2}}{4}\nu_{1}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}\right\rVert^{2}_{W^{1,2}(\Omega)}+c_{4}\nu_{1}^{-\frac{3}{2r-3}}\left\lVert\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert^{\frac{3}{2r-3}}_{r}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Then with (
3.14
) we obtain,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}\right\rVert^{2}_{H}+c_{5}\nu_{1}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}\right\rVert^{2}_{W^{1,2}(\Omega)}+c_{5}\int_{\Omega}

\displaystyle I^{2}(\boldsymbol{Du},\boldsymbol{Dv})\ dx

\displaystyle\leq c_{4}\nu_{1}^{-\frac{3}{2r-3}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert^{\frac{3}{2r-3}}_{W^{1,r}(\Omega)}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+c_{6}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)},

\displaystyle\leq c_{4}\nu_{1}^{-\frac{3}{2r-3}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert^{\frac{3}{2r-3}}_{W^{1,r}(\Omega)}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+c_{6}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)},

\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}(t)\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\leq K\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}(s)\right\rVert^{2}_{2},\quad 0\leq s\leq t\leq T.

Now by (
2.6

\int_{\partial\Omega}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{s}}(\boldsymbol{v})\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}\ dS=\frac{d}{dt}\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}(\boldsymbol{v})\ dS\right).

Then we obtain the following inequality,

\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert^{2}_{H}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\Phi(\boldsymbol{Dv})\ dx}+\frac{d}{dt}\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}(\boldsymbol{v})\ dS\right)\leq c_{8}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert^{2}_{\frac{2r}{r-2}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert^{2}_{W^{1,r}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{f}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

(3.18)

This can be more compactly written as

\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert^{2}_{H}+\frac{d}{dt}U\\
\leq c_{8}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert^{2}_{\frac{2r}{r-2}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert^{2}_{W^{1,r}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{f}\right\rVert_{2}^{2},

(3.19)

where

\text{and hence}\ U\sim 1+\nu_{1}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{Dv}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\nu_{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{Dv}\right\rVert_{r}^{r}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}(\boldsymbol{v})\right\rVert_{L^{1}(\partial\Omega)},

(3.20)

by (

) and Korn’s inequality (
B.2
). Now we distinguish two cases:

(i)

Then we estimate the first term on the right hand side of (

\displaystyle\leq\nu_{2}^{-\frac{10}{5r-6}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert^{\frac{2(5r-12)}{5r-6}}_{2}\left[\nu_{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert^{r}_{W^{1,r}(\Omega)}\right]^{\frac{10}{5r-6}},

(ii)

\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{\frac{2r}{r-2}}\lesssim\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{2}^{\frac{r-3}{r}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{6}^{\frac{3}{r}}.

\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{\frac{2r}{r-2}}\lesssim\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{2}^{\frac{r-2}{r}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{W^{1,3}(\Omega)}^{\frac{2}{r}}\leq c_{11}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{2}^{\frac{r-2}{r}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{W^{1,r}(\Omega)}^{\frac{2}{r}}.

4
Dimension of the Attractor

We follow the general scheme of method of trajectories presented in

16
]
. The main
modification
here is that we explicitly keep track of all a priori estimates.

Lemma 4.1
.

There exists an absorbing, positively invariant set
\hat{{\mathcal{B}}}\subset H
such that

B_{0}:=\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\hat{{\mathcal{B}}}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{H}\leq c_{1}\min\left\{\kappa_{1}^{-1}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{f}\right\rVert_{2},\left[\kappa_{2}^{-1}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{f}\right\rVert_{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{s-1}}\right\},

(4.1)

where
s=\min\{r,q\}
, and
\kappa=\min\{\nu_{2},\alpha\}
.

Proof.

As in Theorem
3.1
, we obtain

\frac{d}{dt}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert^{2}_{H}+c_{2}\left[\nu_{1}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{Dv}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}+\nu_{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{Dv}\right\rVert^{r}_{r}+\alpha\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert^{q}_{L^{q}(\Gamma)}\right]\leq c_{3}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{f}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{H}.

(4.2)

Then by dropping the term
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{Dv}\right\rVert^{r}_{r}
, we compute by Korn’s inequality in Lemma
B.2
,

\frac{d}{dt}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert^{2}_{H}+c_{2}\kappa_{1}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert^{2}_{H}\leq c_{3}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{f}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{H},\ \text{where }\kappa_{1}=\min\{\nu_{1},\alpha\}

(4.3)

Thus
\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert^{2}_{H}\leq-\gamma\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert^{2}_{H}
if
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{H}>c_{4}\kappa_{1}^{-1}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{f}\right\rVert_{2}
for some
\gamma>0
. Now we drop the term
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{Dv}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}
and obtain,

\frac{d}{dt}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert^{2}_{H}+c_{2}\left[\nu_{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{Dv}\right\rVert^{r}_{r}+\alpha\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert^{q}_{L^{q}(\Gamma)}\right]\leq c_{3}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{f}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{H}.

(4.4)

Then we use the following estimate for
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{H}\geq 1
,

\nu_{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{Dv}\right\rVert^{r}_{r}+\alpha\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert^{q}_{L^{q}(\Gamma)}\geq c_{5}\kappa\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert^{s}_{H},\text{ where }s=\min\{r,q\},\text{ and }\kappa_{2}=\min\{\nu_{2},\alpha\}.

Thus we obtain
\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert^{2}_{H}\leq-\gamma\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{H}^{2}
if
\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{H}>c_{6}\left[\kappa^{-1}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{f}\right\rVert_{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{s-1}}
for some
\gamma>0
. Hence the conclusion follows.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

There exists an absorbing, positively invariant
{\mathcal{B}}\subset\hat{{\mathcal{B}}}
such that
{\mathcal{B}}
is closed in
H
, and

B_{r}:=\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\hat{{\mathcal{B}}}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{W^{1,r}}\leq\begin{cases}c_{12}B^{\frac{5(5r-6)}{2(5r-11)}}_{0},\ r\in(12/5,3],\\

(4.5)

Proof.

Set

{\mathcal{B}}:=\left\{\boldsymbol{v}(2T);\ \boldsymbol{v}\text{ is a weak solution on }[0,2T],\text{and }\boldsymbol{v}(0)\in\hat{{\mathcal{B}}}\right\},

and we take
T=B_{0}
. Recalling (
4.3
) and taking
U=\nu_{1}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{Dv}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}+\nu_{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{Dv}\right\rVert^{r}_{r}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}T}}U(t)\ dt

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}T}}\left[\left\lVert\boldsymbol{f}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{H}+\frac{d}{dt}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert^{2}_{H}\right]\ dt,

\displaystyle\lesssim B_{0}^{2}+TB_{0}\leq 2c_{1}B_{0}^{2}.

(4.6)

By the mean value theorem of integrals, we obtain for
\tau\in(0,T)
such that

U(\tau)\leq c_{2}B_{0}.

(4.7)

Assume
r\leq 3
. Integrating (
3.24
) over
(\tau,2T)
yields

\displaystyle U^{\mu}(2T)

\displaystyle\leq c_{3}\nu_{2}^{-\frac{10}{5r-6}}B_{0}^{\frac{2(5r-12)}{5r-6}}\int_{\tau}^{2T}U(t)\ dt+\int_{\tau}^{2T}\left[\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert^{4}_{2}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{f}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]\ dt+U^{\mu}(\tau),

\displaystyle\leq c_{4}\nu_{2}^{-\frac{10}{5r-6}}B_{0}^{\frac{4(5r-9)}{5r-6}}+c_{5}B_{0}^{\mu}+c_{6}B_{0}^{5}+c_{7}B_{0}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{f}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

4.1
Attractors and Method of Trajectories.

Observe that by Theorems
3.2
,
3.3
,
the solution operator
S(t):\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\to\boldsymbol{v}(t)
is
well-defined for
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\in{\mathcal{B}}
. It follows that

{\mathcal{A}}=\omega({\mathcal{B}})=\bigcap_{\tau\geq 0}\bigcup_{t\geq\tau}\overline{S(t){\mathcal{B}}}^{H}

(4.8)

is the so-called
global attractor
. Our ultimate goal is
to estimate its fractal dimension, defined as

d^{H}_{f}({\mathcal{A}})=\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\frac{\ln N_{H}({\mathcal{A}},\varepsilon)}{-\ln\varepsilon}

(4.9)

where
N_{H}({\mathcal{A}},\varepsilon)
is the smallest number of

\varepsilon
-balls in the space
H
that cover
{\mathcal{A}}
. We employ the
method of trajectories. Since the argument is very similar to

[
4
]
, we only briefly sketch the main points.
We refer to
[
16
]
for a more
detailed description of the method; see also the introduction
for other related references.

Let
\ell>0
be fixed; the exact value will specified in (
4.12
) below.
The space of trajectories is defined as

\mathcal{B}_{\ell}=\left\{\chi\in H_{\ell};\textrm{ $\chi$ is a weak solution on $[0,\ell]$, $\chi(0)\in{\mathcal{B}}$}\right\}\,,

(4.10)

with the underlying metric of
H_{\ell}=L^{2}(0,\ell;H)
. Note
however that any trajectory
\chi
has additional regularity, cf. Theorem
3.1
. In particular, we always work with the
representative
\chi\in C([0,\ell];H)
, so that the value

\chi(t)
is well-defined for any
t\in[0,\ell]
.
The operators
{\mathcal{L}}:\mathcal{B}_{\ell}\to\mathcal{B}_{\ell}
,
b:{\mathcal{B}}\to\mathcal{B}_{\ell}
and

e:\mathcal{B}_{\ell}\to{\mathcal{B}}
are defined via the conditions

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}(\chi)

\displaystyle=\psi\iff\chi(\ell)=\psi(0)\,,

\displaystyle e(\chi)

\displaystyle=\chi(\ell)\,,

\displaystyle b(\boldsymbol{v}_{0})

\displaystyle=\chi\iff\chi(0)=\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\,.

Observe that
S(\ell)=e\circ b
and
b\circ e={\mathcal{L}}
, hence
{\mathcal{L}}
is an equivalent (discrete) description
of the dynamics of
S(t)
on
\mathcal{B}_{\ell}=b({\mathcal{B}})
. In particular,
one has
\mathcal{A}_{\ell}=b({\mathcal{A}})
,
{\mathcal{A}}=e(\mathcal{A}_{\ell})
, where
\mathcal{A}_{\ell}
is
the global attractor for the dynamical system

({\mathcal{L}}^{n},\mathcal{B}_{\ell})
.

In view of the Lipschitz continuity of operators
e
,
b

(see for example
[
16
, Lemma 2.1]
,
[
16
, Lemma 1.2]
)

d_{f}^{H}({\mathcal{A}})=d_{f}^{L^{2}(0,\ell;H)}(\mathcal{A}_{\ell})

(4.11)

Thus, it suffices to estimate the last quantity. This will be
done using the so-called smoothing property, see
[
16
, Lemma
1.3]
; see also
[
4
, Theorem 4.1]
.
It remains to explicitly estimate the appropriate Lipschitz
constants, which is done in the following lemma. Finally, the
asymptotics of covering numbers is investigated in the
Appendix.

Lemma 4.3
.

Set

\ell:=\left[\nu_{1}^{-\frac{3}{2r-3}}B^{\frac{2r}{2r-3}}_{r}+1\right]^{-1}.

(4.12)

Then for all
\chi,\psi\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\ell}

\displaystyle\left\lVert{\mathcal{L}}\chi-{\mathcal{L}}\psi\right\rVert_{L^{2}(0,\ell;V_{2})}\leq L_{1}\left\lVert\chi-\psi\right\rVert_{H_{\ell}},

(4.13)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\partial_{t}{\mathcal{L}}\chi-\partial_{t}{\mathcal{L}}\psi\right\rVert_{L^{2}(0,\ell;V^{\prime}_{r})}\leq L_{2}\left\lVert\chi-\psi\right\rVert_{H_{\ell}},

(4.14)

where

\displaystyle L_{1}

\displaystyle=c_{1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nu_{1}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\ell^{-\frac{1}{2}},

(4.15)

\displaystyle L_{2}

\displaystyle=U+W{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}+Q},

(4.16)

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle=c_{2}\nu_{1}L_{1}(1+M_{r}),

(4.17)

\displaystyle M_{r}

\displaystyle=\nu_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\nu_{2}^{\frac{1}{2}}B^{\frac{r-2}{2}}_{r},

(4.18)

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}c_{4}B_{0}^{\frac{5r-12}{5r-6}}B_{r}^{\frac{6}{5r-6}},\ r\in(12/5,3],\\
c_{4}B_{0}^{\frac{r-2}{r}}B_{r}^{\frac{2}{r}},\ r>3.\end{cases}

(4.19)

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}
be two weak solutions on
[0,2\ell]
such that
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}|_{[0,\ell]}=\chi,\boldsymbol{u}|_{[0,\ell]}=\psi
, and set
\boldsymbol{w}:=\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}
. In view of (
4.12

3.15

\frac{d}{dt}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}\right\rVert^{2}_{H}+c_{5}\nu_{1}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}\right\rVert^{2}_{W^{1,2}(\Omega)}+c_{5}\int_{\Omega}I^{2}(\boldsymbol{Du},\boldsymbol{Dv})\ dx\leq c_{7}\left[\nu_{1}^{-\frac{3}{2r-3}}B^{\frac{2r}{2r-3}}_{r}+1\right]\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}\right\rVert_{H}^{2}.

We replace norm of the second term of the left hand side with the equivalent norm
\left\lVert\ \right\rVert_{V_{2}}
.This yields,

\frac{d}{dt}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}\right\rVert^{2}_{H}+c_{8}\nu_{1}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}\right\rVert^{2}_{V_{2}}+c_{8}\int_{\Omega}I^{2}(\boldsymbol{Du},\boldsymbol{Dv})\ dx\leq c_{7}\left[\nu_{1}^{-\frac{3}{2r-3}}B^{\frac{2r}{2r-3}}_{r}+1\right]\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}\right\rVert_{H}^{2}.

Then by (
4.12
) we obtain,

\frac{d}{dt}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}\right\rVert^{2}_{H}+c_{8}\nu_{1}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}\right\rVert^{2}_{V_{2}}+c_{8}\int_{\Omega}I^{2}(\boldsymbol{Du},\boldsymbol{Dv})\ dx\leq c_{7}\ell^{-1}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}\right\rVert_{H}^{2}.

(4.20)

Neglecting the positive terms of the
left hand side
, we obtain from

Lemma

\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}(t)\right\rVert^{2}_{H}\leq c_{9}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}(s)\right\rVert^{2}_{H},\quad 0<s<t<2\ell,

(4.21)

where
c_{9}=\text{exp}\left((t-s)\ell^{-1}\right)\leq\text{exp}(2c_{7})
. In other words, the smallness of
\ell
eliminates the (exponential) dependence of the Lipschitz constant of
S(t)
on the viscosities.

Integrating (
4.20
) over
(s,2\ell)
, where
s\in(0,\ell)
is fixed, one further derives

c_{8}\nu_{1}\int_{s}^{2\ell}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(t)}\right\rVert^{2}_{V_{2}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}dt}+c_{8}\int_{s}^{2\ell}\int_{\Omega}I^{2}(\boldsymbol{Du},\boldsymbol{Dv})\ dx{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}dt}\leq\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}(s)\right\rVert_{H}^{2}+c_{7}\ell^{-1}\int_{s}^{2\ell}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t})\right\rVert_{H}^{2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}dt}.

By (
4.21
), we obtain
\displaystyle\int_{s}^{2\ell}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}(t)\right\rVert^{2}_{H}\ dt\leq 2c_{9}\ell\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}(s)\right\rVert_{H}^{2}
. By substituting this back in the above inequality, we obtain,

c_{8}\nu_{1}\int_{s}^{2\ell}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(t)}\right\rVert^{2}_{V_{2}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}dt}+c_{8}\int_{s}^{2\ell}\int_{\Omega}I^{2}(\boldsymbol{Du},\boldsymbol{Dv})\ dx{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}dt}\leq c_{10}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}(s)\right\rVert_{H}^{2}.

Integrating over

yields,

\ell\nu_{1}\int_{s}^{2\ell}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(t)}\right\rVert^{2}_{V_{2}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}dt}+\ell\int_{s}^{2\ell}\int_{\Omega}I^{2}(\boldsymbol{Du},\boldsymbol{Dv})\ dx{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}dt}\leq c_{11}\int_{0}^{\ell}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}(s)\right\rVert_{H}^{2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}ds}.

This proves (

). We also note here that

\left[\int_{s}^{2\ell}\int_{\Omega}I^{2}(\boldsymbol{Du},\boldsymbol{Dv})\ dx{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}dt}\right]^{{}^{1}\!/_{2}}\leq c_{12}\nu_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}L_{1}\left[\int_{0}^{\ell}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}(s)\right\rVert_{H}^{2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}ds}\right]^{{}^{1}\!/_{2}}.

(4.22)

To prove (
4.14
), (
2.1
) is used to get

Lemma B.4
.
