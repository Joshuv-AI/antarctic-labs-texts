Fibrations of algebras
Danel Ahman, Greta Coraglia, Davide Castelnovo, Fosco Loregian, Nelson Martins-Ferreira, Ülo Reimaa

Abstract
. We study fibrations arising from indexed categories of the following form: fix two categories 𝒜 , 𝒳 \mathcal{A},\mathcal{X} and a functor F : 𝒜 × 𝒳 ⟶ 𝒳 F:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{X}\longrightarrow\mathcal{X} , so that to each F A = F ⁡ ( A , − ) F_{A}=F(A,-) one can associate a category of algebras 𝐀𝐥𝐠 𝒳 ​ ( F A ) \mathbf{Alg}_{\mathcal{X}}(F_{A}) (or an Eilenberg–Moore, or a Kleisli category if each F A F_{A} is a monad). We call the functor ∫ 𝒜 𝐀𝐥𝐠 → 𝒜 \int^{\mathcal{A}}\mathbf{Alg}\to\mathcal{A} , whose typical fibre over A A is the category 𝐀𝐥𝐠 𝒳 ​ ( F A ) \mathbf{Alg}_{\mathcal{X}}(F_{A}) , the fibration of algebras obtained from F F . Examples of such constructions arise in disparate areas of mathematics, and are unified by the intuition that ∫ 𝒜 𝐀𝐥𝐠 \int^{\mathcal{A}}\mathbf{Alg} is a form of semidirect product of the category 𝒜 \mathcal{A} , acting on 𝒳 \mathcal{X} , via the ‘representation’ given by the functor F : 𝒜 × 𝒳 ⟶ 𝒳 F:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{X}\longrightarrow\mathcal{X} . After presenting a range of examples and motivating said intuition, the present work focuses on comparing a generic fibration with a fibration of algebras: we prove that if 𝒜 \mathcal{A} has an initial object, under very mild assumptions on a fibration p : ℰ ⟶ 𝒜 p:\mathcal{E}\longrightarrow\mathcal{A} , we can define a canonical action of 𝒜 \mathcal{A} letting it act on the fibre ℰ ∅ \mathcal{E}_{\varnothing} over the initial object. This result bears some resemblance to the well-known fact that the fundamental group π 1 ​ ( B ) \pi_{1}(B) of a base space acts naturally on the fibers F b = p − 1 ​ b F_{b}=p^{-1}b of a fibration p : E → B p:E\to B .

2.2.
A whirlwind tour of examples of fibrations of algebras

The scope of this section is to collect examples of diverse origin.

Among the simplest instances of parametrized monads and comonads there are the ones arising from a Cartesian or coCartesian category of parameters acting on itself by co/product. This quite specific setting is already enough to provide interesting examples. Subsequently we show how more general monoidal categories offer equally compelling examples, and we start outlining the universal property enjoyed by the construction in
Example 2.28
.

More examples will follow when we will have established additional bits of the theory (e.g. how colimits are computed in total categories of the form
\mathcal{A}\ltimes\mathcal{X}
), see
subsection 4.3
, and the 2-dimensional universal property thereof,
Theorem 3.3
.

3.
A characterization, and on semidirect products

In this section we focus on better understanding inherent features of the fibration of algebras construction: first we provide a new characterization by means of a certain Eilenberg–Moore object (cf.

3.1.

4.3.
Colimits in fibrations of algebras

In Proposition
4.1
we obtained that limits in
\mathcal{A}\ltimes\mathcal{X}
are created by a monadic functor
\mathcal{A}\ltimes\mathcal{X}\longrightarrow\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{X}
. Colimits in categories of algebras, on the other hand, are usually way more difficult to compute as, even when they exist, they tend to be complicated objects – this is the case, for example, for initial endofunctor algebras.

The present subsection deeper investigates the two following connected problems:

•

how to induce/compute colimits in the total categories of the Eilenberg–Moore or endofunctor algebra fibrations?

•

how to ensure that reindexings (which preserve all limits, given the fact that these are created in the base) have left adjoints?

A partial answer was already given in
subsection 4.2
, but we find that the second problem can be addressed by looking at the ‘classifying’ fibration of algebras and then argue that some relevant property is preserved under pullback. In fact, we leverage on the following result.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
p:\mathcal{E}\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{A}
be a fibration such that each reindexing
u^{\ast}:\mathcal{E}_{A^{\prime}}\longrightarrow\mathcal{E}_{A}
preserves (co)limits; let
F:\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\longrightarrow\mathcal{A}
be a functor and
q
the fibration obtained pulling back
p
along
F
. Then, each reindexing of
q
also preserves (co)limits.

Therefore all fibrations obtained by pulling back the classifying fibration of algebras share its properties in terms of limit-preserving reindexings.

Let us start on our pursuit with the case of monads.
Based on
[
Bor94
, 4.3.6]
and on the fact that
\mathcal{A}\ltimes\mathcal{X}
is monadic over
\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{X}
, one can prove very swiftly the following.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
T:\mathcal{A}\longrightarrow\mathsf{Mnd}(\mathcal{X})
be a parametrized monad such that
T
preserves filtered colimits in each variable separately; if
\mathcal{X}
is cocomplete, then so is
\mathcal{A}\ltimes\mathcal{X}
.

Remark 4.7
.

In order to proceed with the discussion, let’s make blanket assumptions the ones in

One preliminary remark is the following well-known observation by Linton

5.
Comparing fibrations against fibrations of algebras

Our main goal in this section is to show how to build a parametrized monad out of a suitably nice fibration
p:\mathcal{E}\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{B}
, and explore some consequences of this result: we can then attempt to ‘measure’ how far the original fibration was from being a fibration of algebras of its corresponding parametrized monad.

In doing so, we prove that the construction that associates a parametrized monad to
p
is ‘free’, in the sense of being a left adjoint to a suitable functor in the opposite direction, building the fibration of Eilenberg–Moore algebras of a parametrized monad. This sets up an adjunction

(5.1)

\textstyle{\mathsf{pFib}(\mathcal{A})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\perp}
\textstyle{\mathsf{ParMnd}_{\emptyset}(\mathcal{A})}

between a category of fibrations (that we call
pruned
in
Definition 5.8
) and a category of monads (that we also call
pruned
in
Definition 5.9
), and a fibration
p
arises as a fibration of Eilenberg–Moore algebras if and only if the unit of this adjunction is an isomorphism at
p
.

Clearly then, fibrations of algebras arise as fixpoints of this construction, hence every fibration of Eilenberg–Moore algebras will be an example of pruned fibration, under mild assumptions on its category of parameters and of carriers.

The first observation that we need to carry on our analysis is that an Eilenberg–Moore fibration has full and faithful left and right adjoints under very mild conditions. The easiest way to see this is using the following standard fact about fibrations.

Lemma 5.1
.

A fibration
p:\mathcal{E}\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{A}
has

(1)

a full and faithful left adjoint
p_{L}
precisely when each of the fibres
\mathcal{E}_{A}
contains an initial object
\emptyset_{A}
and

(2)

a full and faithful right adjoint
p_{R}
precisely when each of the fibres
\mathcal{E}_{A}
contains a terminal object
\ast_{A}
and the reindexings
\mathcal{E}_{A}\to\mathcal{E}_{A^{\prime}}
preserve terminal objects.

Remark 5.2
.

There is a concise way to rephrase the above observation in terms of a property of
p
as an object of the 2-category of fibrations over
\mathcal{A}
: the Cartesian functor
p:p\longrightarrow\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{A}}
has a fibred left (resp., right) adjoint if and only if
p
, regarded as an object of
\mathsf{Fib}/\mathcal{A}
admits an initial (resp., terminal) object, which boils down exactly to an initial (resp., terminal) object in each fiber, preserved by reindexings. Cf.
[
Jac98
, 1.8.8]
.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
T:\mathcal{A}\longrightarrow\mathsf{Mnd}(\mathcal{X})
be a parametrized monad. If
\mathcal{X}
has an initial and a terminal object, then each monad
T_{A}
will have an initial algebra
\emptyset^{T}_{A}
and a terminal algebra
\ast^{T}_{A}
which allow us to define the full and faithful functors

(5.2)

p_{L}:\mathcal{A}\longrightarrow\mathcal{A}\ltimes\mathcal{X}\,,\;\;A\mapsto\emptyset^{T}_{A}\quad\text{ and }\quad p_{R}:\mathcal{A}\longrightarrow\mathcal{A}\ltimes\mathcal{X}\,,\;\;A\mapsto\ast^{T}_{A}\,,

that are respectively the left adjoint and right adjoint of the functor

p:\mathcal{A}\ltimes\mathcal{X}\longrightarrow\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

The initial and terminal algebra of
T_{A}
will respectively be given by the free
T_{A}
-algebra on the initial object of
\mathcal{X}
and the unique algebra on the terminal object of
\mathcal{X}
. As the reindexing of a fibration of algebras does not change the carrier, the terminal algebras will be preserved.
∎

We mostly make use of the left adjoint, but existence of the right adjoint is an important property which can help identity fibrations of algebras in nature.

Remark 5.4
.

The fibration of algebras of a parametrized endofunctor
F:\mathcal{A}\longrightarrow[\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}]

will also have a right adjoint if
\mathcal{X}
has a terminal object, and the algebra structure will still be created by the forgetful functor, but since the existence of initial algebras of endofunctors is a much more complicated question than for monads, then the existence of the left adjoint is very much dependent on the particular parametrized endofunctor.

Since we are dealing with fibrations, there is a well-behaved sense in which we can take ‘kernels’.

Construction 5.5
.

If the codomain of a fibration
p:\mathcal{E}\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{A}
has an initial object
\emptyset

\mathcal{E}_{\emptyset}
over
\emptyset

of

along with its inclusion

of the fibration

.

One of the reasons for taking the initial fibre over other fibres is the following.

Proposition 5.6
.

For any fibration
p:\mathcal{E}\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{A}
where
\mathcal{A}
contains an initial object
\emptyset

The right adjoint

acts by mapping an object

, into an object

in the initial fibre by reindexing

∎

We are now ready to express how we can turn fibrations into adjunctions, sliced over

5.1.
Examples of the fibration to parametrized monad construction

Instances of our

Example 5.16

(Artin glueing)
.

Given a finite limit preserving functor

between elementary toposes, the

[
Wra74
]
of
\mathcal{E},\mathcal{E}^{\prime}

is defined as the comma category
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}/F
. This arises as the total category of the coEilenberg–Moore opfibration of the parametrized comonad
\mathcal{E}\to\mathsf{coMnd}(\mathcal{E}^{\prime})
,
E\mapsto FE\times\,\rule{5.0pt}{0.4pt}\,
.

In
[
FMS23
]
, the authors consider ‘adjoint split extensions of toposes’, which, they show, always arise from the Artin glueing construction. An adjoint split extension of toposes is a diagram of toposes and functors, where one of the functors can be shown to be an opfibration. An application of the dual of
Theorem 5.13
to that opfibration yields precisely a parametrized comonad of the form
E\mapsto FE\times\,\rule{5.0pt}{0.4pt}\,
, and the unit
\eta_{p}
will be an equivalence.

This is an instance of a setting where the category

of coalgebras truly plays the role of a semidirect product for a class of categories.

Another class of applications of

can be found where for a fibration

need not be an equivalence, but one is particularly interested in those parameters for which

As an instance of this, we have the following example from categorical algebra. An account on this which is close to ours can be found in

[

]

Example 5.17

(The fibration of points)
.

The
