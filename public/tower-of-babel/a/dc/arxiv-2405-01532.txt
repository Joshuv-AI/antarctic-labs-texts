Robustness of Fixed Points of Quantum Channels and Application to Approximate Quantum Markov Chains
Robert Salzmann, Bjarne Bergh, Nilanjana Datta

Abstract
Given a quantum channel and a state which satisfy a fixed point equation approximately (say, up to an error ε \varepsilon ), can one find a new channel and a state, which are respectively close to the original ones, such that they satisfy an exact fixed point equation? It is interesting to ask this question for different choices of constraints on the structures of the original channel and state, and requiring that these are also satisfied by the new channel and state. We affirmatively answer the above question, under fairly general assumptions on these structures, through a compactness argument. Additionally, for channels and states satisfying certain specific structures, we find explicit upper bounds on the distances between the pairs of channels (and states) in question. When these distances decay quickly (in a particular, desirable manner) as ε → 0 \varepsilon\to 0 , we say that the original approximate fixed point equation is rapidly fixable . We establish rapid fixability, not only for general quantum channels, but also when the original and new channels are both required to be unitary, mixed unitary or unital. In contrast, for the case of bipartite quantum systems with channels acting trivially on one subsystem, we prove that approximate fixed point equations are not rapidly fixable. In this case, the distance to the closest channel (and state) which satisfy an exact fixed point equation can depend on the dimension of the quantum system in an undesirable way. We apply our results on approximate fixed point equations to the question of robustness of quantum Markov chains (QMC) and establish the following: For any tripartite quantum state, there exists a dimension-dependent upper bound on its distance to the set of QMCs, which decays to zero as the conditional mutual information of the state vanishes.

2.2
Fixed points of channels

In this section we review some basic facts about fixed points of quantum channels. In fact, the following section applies to all positive and trace preserving maps
\mathcal{N}:\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
without requiring complete positivity. For a more complete overview including also several facts which exclusively hold for quantum channels, consider

Wat18

The space of fixed points of
\mathcal{N}
is denoted by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{N})\coloneqq\left\{x\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})\Big|\,\mathcal{N}(x)=x\right\}=\ker\left({\rm{id}}-\mathcal{N}\right).

Furthermore, we can define the
Cesàro mean
of
\mathcal{N}
by

\displaystyle A_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathcal{N}^{k}.

(2.9)

If the limit
P\coloneqq\lim_{n\to\infty}A_{n}
exists,
P
given by the projection onto the fixed point space
{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{N})
and for
\mathcal{N}\in{\rm{CPTP}}(\mathcal{H})
we also have
P\in{\rm{CPTP}}(\mathcal{H}).

First, note that on a finite dimensional Hilbert space, the fixed point space of a positive and trace preserving map
\mathcal{N}
is non-trivial. This is a direct consequence of the fact that
\mathcal{N}
maps the convex and compact set of states on
\mathcal{H}
onto itself and therefore there exists, by Brouwer’s fixed point theorem, a fixed point state

\displaystyle\rho\in\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{H})\cap{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{N}).

(2.10)

Again, in finite dimensions, the limit of the Cesàro mean
A_{n}
as

always exists by a standard compactness argument.

For infinite dimensional separable Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}
these arguments based on compactness break down. In fact, one can consider as an example the shift channel
\mathcal{N}_{\text{shift}}
which, for
\left(|i\rangle\right)_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
being an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}
, is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\text{shift}}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\langle i|x|i\rangle|i+1\rangle\!\langle i+1|

for all
x\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H}).
Assuming
\mathcal{N}_{\text{shift}}(x)=x
immediately gives
\langle i|x|j\rangle=0
for all
i\neq j
and furthermore
\langle i|x|i\rangle=\langle i+1|x|i+1\rangle
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}.
This already shows that
x=c\mathbbm{1}
for some
c\in\mathbb{C}
and as
x
is supposed to be a trace class operator, we have
c=0.
Therefore,
\mathcal{N}_{\text{shift}}
cannot have any non-trivial fixed points, i.e.

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{N}_{\text{shift}})=\{0\}.

However, we still have that

is in the spectrum of
\mathcal{N}_{\text{shift}}
which follows as the pure state
|1\rangle\!\langle 1|
does not lie in the image of
{\rm{id}}-\mathcal{N}_{\text{shift}}.

Another useful fact for
\mathcal{N}:\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
being a positive and trace preserving is that for any fixed point

6

6

\displaystyle[\Re(A)]_{+},[\Re(A)]_{-},[\Im(A)]_{+},[\Im(A)]_{-}\in{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{N}).

(2.11)

Lastly, for any non-zero and positive semi-definite operator

3
Fixability of approximate fixed point equations

As explained in the Introduction, in this paper we adress the following question:

Given a quantum channel and a quantum state which almost satisfy a fixed point equation, can we find a new channel and a new state, which are respectively close to the original ones, such that they satisfy an exact fixed point equation?

This question can be asked under many interesting constraints in which the original channel and state are assumed to have certain structures which the new channel and state are supposed to satisfy as well.

In precise mathematical terms we consider, on some separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, subsets of states
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{H})
and subsets of channels
\mathcal{C}\subseteq{\rm{CPTP}}(\mathcal{H})
which correspond to certain structures of interest. For example
\mathcal{S}
could be the set of pure states, general mixed states, or, on a bipartite Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{AB}=\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B},
the set of entangled states. Similarly,
\mathcal{C}
could for example be the set of general quantum channels, classical channels, unitary channels, unital channels or, on a bipartite Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}\equiv\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
, local channels of the form
\mathcal{N}_{AB}\equiv{\rm{id}}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{N}_{B}.

For error parameter
\varepsilon\geq 0,

\rho\in\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{N}\in\mathcal{C}

we say
\rho
is an
approximate fixed point
of
\mathcal{N}
if

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\rho)\approx^{\varepsilon}\rho.

(3.1)

For a given pair
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C})
we are hence concerned with whether approximate fixed point equations like (
3.1
) can be fixed in the sense of the following definition:

Definition 3.1
(Fixability of approximate fixed points)

We say approximate fixed point equations
8
8

8

In the following we often interchangeably refer to an approximate fixed point being fixable meaning fixability of the corresponding approximate fixed point equation.

\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{H})
and
\mathcal{C}\subseteq{\rm{CPTP}}(\mathcal{H}),
with
\mathcal{H}
being some separable Hilbert space, if the following holds true: There exist approximation functions
f,\,g:\mathbbm{R}_{+}\to\mathbbm{R}_{+}
such that we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}f(\varepsilon)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}g(\varepsilon)=0

(3.2)

and for all
\rho\in\mathcal{S},\,\mathcal{N}\in\mathcal{C}
and
\varepsilon\geq 0
such that
\mathcal{N}(\rho)\approx^{\varepsilon}\rho

\displaystyle\sigma\in\mathcal{S}\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad\mathcal{M}\in\mathcal{C},\quad

(3.3)

which are close to original ones, i.e.

\displaystyle\sigma\approx^{f(\varepsilon)}\rho\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad\mathcal{M}\approx^{g(\varepsilon)}\mathcal{N},

(3.4)

and which are a fixed point pair, i.e. they satisfy the fixed point equation

9

9

Putting this differently, approximate fixed point equations are fixable for the pair

if and only if we have for the worst case error
\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\rho,\mathcal{N})\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{C}\\
\mathcal{N}(\rho)\approx^{\varepsilon}\rho\end{subarray}}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\sigma,\mathcal{M})\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{C}\\
\mathcal{M}(\sigma)=\sigma\end{subarray}}\max\{\|\rho-\sigma\|_{1},\|\mathcal{N}-\mathcal{M}\|_{\diamond}\}\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\to 0]{}0.

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\sigma)=\sigma.

It is important to note that the functional form of

and

(and hence also on the underlying Hilbert space

\mathcal{N}\in\mathcal{C}
and

Note also that for finite dimensional Hilbert spaces with dimension

To illustrate Definition

we first note that for general sets
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{C},

\mathcal{S}=\{\rho\}

\mathcal{C}=\{\mathcal{N}\}
such that
\mathcal{N}(\rho)\neq\rho.
In that case approximate fixed point equations are not fixable, simply as there exists no pair

satisfying an exact fixed point equation. More interesting counterexamples occur when one or both of the sets are non-closed. For example, take as Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{C}^{2}
, and then the convex but open set
\mathcal{S}=\{(1-\varepsilon)|0\rangle\!\langle 0|+\varepsilon|1\rangle\!\langle 1|\ \big|\ 0<\varepsilon<1\}
and furthermore
\mathcal{C}=\{\mathrm{Tr}(\,\cdot\,)|0\rangle\!\langle 0|,\mathrm{Tr}(\,\cdot\,)\mathbbm{1}/2\}.
First of all, as
\sigma=\mathbbm{1}/2\in\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{M}=\mathrm{Tr}(\,\cdot\,)\mathbbm{1}/2\in\mathcal{C},

However, for every

As

the approximate fixed point equation (

\mathcal{S}
and

Building both examples above on top of each other gives an example of an approximate fixed point equation which can only be fixed by both varying state and channel. More, precisely, we take as Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{2}=\mathbb{C}^{5}

and channel

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{N}_{1,\varepsilon}\oplus\mathcal{N}_{2}

This pair satisfies the approximate fixed point equation
\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}(\rho_{\varepsilon})\approx^{\varepsilon}\rho_{\varepsilon}
but, by the above arguments, cannot be fixed by soley changing either state or channel.

12

12

Here, for a more detailed argument, denote by

and
\pi_{2}
the orthogonal projections on
\mathcal{H}
onto the subspaces
\mathcal{H}_{1}

\mathcal{H}_{2}

Lastly, we want to point out that the difficulty of the problem outlined above lies in the fact that we demand certain structures (meaning membership of the sets
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{C}

are a state and a channel respectively. To illustrate this, let
V
be some

\varepsilon\geq 0,

with
\|v\|=1
and
A:V\to V
some linear map such that
\|Av-v\|\leq\varepsilon.
We would like to fix this approximate fixed point equation by changing
A
to
B
while only demanding that
B
must be linear as well. For that consider the following construction: Let
\left(v_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{d}
be a basis of
V
with

We can now define
B
simply on each basis vector and then extend linearly. Taking hence

\displaystyle Bv_{1}=v\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad Bv_{i}=Av_{i}\quad\text{for all $i=2,\cdots,d$}

and then extending linearly, gives a well-defined linear map
B:V\to V
satisfying the fixed point equation
Bv=v
and furthermore

\displaystyle\left\|B-A\right\|

\displaystyle=\max_{\|w\|=1}\left\|(B-A)w\right\|=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|w\|=1\end{subarray}}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{d}\omega_{i}(B-A)v_{i}\right\|

\displaystyle=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|w\|=1\end{subarray}}|\omega_{1}|\left\|v-Av\right\|\leq c_{d}\,\varepsilon.

(3.12)

Here,
\left(\omega_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{d}\subseteq\mathbb{C}
denote the coefficients of the vector

in the given basis and

is some constant whose precise form may depend on the dimension

the norm on

and the choice of basis

For example, if the norm

is induced by a scalar product on

we can pick the basis to be orthonormal with respect to this scalar product and

follows. For general norms the existence of such finite

follows by equivalence of all norms in finite dimensions. This shows that fixing approximate fixed point equations becomes a lot easier when only demanding to preserve linearity of the map when changing

to
B

where also positivity of the map was supposed to be preserved, it suffices to only change the map

to

but leave the the vector

4
Fixability for closed sets

In this section we first state Proposition
4.1
which provides for the finite dimensional case that approximate fixed point equations are always fixable in the sense of Definition
3.1
as long as the sets
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{H})
and
\mathcal{C}\subseteq{\rm{CPTP}}(\mathcal{H})
are closed and contain a fixed point pair. The proof relies on a compactness argument and hence we merely prove existence of approximation functions
f
and

satisfying the conditions in Defintion

but do not provide any specific form they need to satisfy.

We then review some of the theory of quantum Markov chains and their approximate analogs. We see that they can be defined by a certain (approximate) fixed point equation which they need to satisfy. Applying Proposition
4.1
to fix this particular approximate fixed point equation enables us to solve an open problem in the robustness theory of quantum Markov chains. In particular, we show that for every approximate Markov chain there is an exact quantum Markov chain close to it (Proposition
4.2

f(d_{ABC},\varepsilon)
depending necessarily on the intrinsic dimension of the problem captured in
d_{ABC}
and the error parameter
\varepsilon\geq 0
of the approximate fixed point equation and which decays as
\varepsilon\to 0.

Proposition 4.1
(Fixability for closed sets
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{C}
)

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a finite dimensional Hilbert space. Further, let
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{H})
and
\mathcal{C}\subseteq{\rm{CPTP}}(\mathcal{H})
be closed and such that there exist
\sigma_{\star}\in\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\star}\in\mathcal{C}
satisfying the fixed point equation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\star}(\sigma_{\star})=\sigma_{\star}.

(4.1)

Then, there exist functions

such that

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}f(\varepsilon)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}g(\varepsilon)=0

(4.2)

and the following holds: For all

,
\mathcal{N}\in\mathcal{C}
and
\varepsilon\geq 0
with

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\rho)\approx^{\varepsilon}\rho,

(4.3)

there exist

and
\mathcal{M}\in\mathcal{C}
with

(4.5)

holds. In other words, all approximate fixed point equations are fixable for the pair

in the sense of Definition

.

Note that the approximation functions

and

We defer the proof of Proposition

4.1
Application to the robustness of quantum Markov chains

We review some basic definitions and well-known facts regarding quantum Markov chains and their approximate analogs (see
[
Sut18
]
for a nice overview of all the mentioned results): We consider tripartite Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{ABC}=\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{C}
with finite dimensions
d_{A}\coloneqq\dim(\mathcal{H}_{A})
,
d_{B}\coloneqq\dim(\mathcal{H}_{B})=d_{B}
and
d_{C}\coloneqq\dim(\mathcal{H}_{C})
. For a state
\rho_{ABC}
on
\mathcal{H}_{ABC}
we can define the
conditional mutual information

\displaystyle I(A:C|B)_{\rho}\coloneqq S(\rho_{AB})+S(\rho_{BC})-S(\rho_{ABC})-S(\rho_{B})

(4.6)

where we denoted the
von Neumann entropy
of a state
\sigma
by
S(\sigma)\coloneqq-\mathrm{Tr}(\sigma\log(\sigma))
and denoted the reduced states of
\rho_{ABC}
by
\rho_{AB}\coloneqq\mathrm{Tr}_{C}(\rho_{ABC})
and analogously for the other combinations. Note that the conditional mutual information is always non-negative,
I(A:C|B)_{\rho}\geq 0,
which is due to the strong subadditivity of the von Neumann entropy
[
LR73
]
.

A state
\rho_{ABC}
is a
(short) quantum Markov chain (QMC)
if it satisfies strong subadditivity
with equality, i.e.

\displaystyle I(A:C|B)_{\rho}=0.

(4.7)

This entropic characterisation is equivalent to the existence of a
recovery channel

[
Pet03
]
, i.e. a completely positive and trace preserving linear map
\mathcal{R}_{B\to BC}:{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H}_{B})\to{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H}_{BC})
such that

\displaystyle({\rm{id}}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{R}_{B\to BC})(\rho_{AB})=\rho_{ABC}.

(4.8)

Intuitively this means that system
C
can be recovered exclusively by focusing on system
B
while ignoring
A.
Note that a quantum Markov chain is hence characterised by satisfying a certain fixed point equation

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\rho_{ABC})=\rho_{ABC}

(4.9)

for
\mathcal{N}
being a channel of the specific form
\mathcal{N}=({\rm{id}}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{R}_{B\to BC})\circ\mathrm{Tr}_{C}.
In
[
HJPW04
]

: There exists an orthogonal sum decomposition

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{B}=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{H}_{b^{L}_{j}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{b^{L}_{j}}

with
n\in[d_{B}]
and Hilbert spaces
(\mathcal{H}_{b^{L}_{j}})_{j=1}^{n}
and
(\mathcal{H}_{b^{R}_{j}})_{j=1}^{n}
and such that

\displaystyle\rho_{ABC}=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{n}q_{j}\,\rho_{Ab^{L}_{j}}\otimes\rho_{b^{R}_{j}C}

(4.10)

for some probability distribution
\left(q_{j}\right)_{j=1}^{n}
and states
\rho_{Ab^{L}_{j}}
on
\mathcal{H}_{Ab^{L}_{j}}=\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{b_{j}^{L}}
and

on

for all

An essential component in the proof of

[

which implies a comparable orthogonal sum structure for one channel and multiple states, which are all fixed points of the channel. On the other hand, it is also easy to see that (

It is important to note that the above is a sensible quantum generalisation of (classical) Markov chains: A probability distribution

More precisely, we can define the stochastic matrix

Equation (

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

Define the function

\displaystyle F:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle\to\mathbbm{R}_{+}

\displaystyle(\rho,\mathcal{N})

\displaystyle\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathcal{N}(\rho)-\rho\right\|_{1}.

Firstly, note that
F
is continuous. Moreover, the preimage
F^{-1}(\{0\})
is exactly the set of pairs of states and channels in
\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{C}
which satisfy an exact fixed point equation and is non-empty by assumption.

Now, since the set
\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{C}
is closed and hence compact, we see in the following

\displaystyle\forall\delta>0\,\exists\varepsilon>0\,\text{ s.t}\ \forall(\rho,\mathcal{N})\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{C}\text{ with}\ F(\rho,\mathcal{N})\leq\varepsilon\ \text{ we have }{\rm{dist}}((\rho,\mathcal{N}),F^{-1}(\{0\}))\leq\delta,

(4.28)

where

{\rm{dist}}((\rho,\mathcal{N}),F^{-1}(\{0\}))=\min_{(\sigma,\mathcal{M})\in F^{-1}(\{0\})}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left\|\rho-\sigma\right\|_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathcal{N}-\mathcal{M}\right\|_{\diamond}\right).

To prove that (
4.28
) holds, we assume the opposite to arrive at a contradiction: In that case there exists
\delta>0
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
(\rho_{n},\mathcal{N}_{n})\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{C}
with
F(\rho_{n},\mathcal{N}_{n})\leq\frac{1}{n}
and
{\rm{dist}}((\rho,\mathcal{N}),F^{-1}(\{0\}))>\delta.
But, by compactness of
\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{C},
the sequence
\left((\rho_{n},\mathcal{N}_{n})\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is, up to taking a subsequence, convergent. This already gives a contradiction as by continuity of
F
the limit of named subsequence must lie in
F^{-1}(\{0\})
.

Define now for
\varepsilon\geq 0

\displaystyle\delta_{\star}(\varepsilon)=\sup_{(\rho,\mathcal{N})\in F^{-1}([0,\varepsilon])}{\rm{dist}}((\rho,\mathcal{N}),F^{-1}(\{0\})).

Since
\emptyset\neq F^{-1}(\{0\})\subseteq F^{-1}([0,\varepsilon]),
we have
\delta_{\star}(\varepsilon)\geq 0.

Furthermore, we can show that

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\delta_{\star}(\varepsilon)=0.

(4.29)

To see this, let
\delta^{\prime}>0
and use that by (
4.28
) there exists an
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
such that for all
(\rho,\mathcal{N})\in F^{-1}([0,\varepsilon^{\prime}])

\displaystyle\leq\delta\quad\text{ for all $i\in I_{l}$ and}

\displaystyle|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|

\displaystyle>\delta\quad\text{ for all $i\in I_{l},j\in I_{l^{\prime}}$ with $l\neq l^{\prime}.$}

(6.8)

We then define for each cluster the corresponding spectral projection

\displaystyle E_{l}\coloneqq\sum_{i\in I_{l}}\pi_{i}=\sum_{i=k_{l-1}+1}^{k_{l}}\pi_{i}

(6.9)

and denote the average spectral point by

\displaystyle\mu_{l}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\mathrm{Tr}(E_{l})}\sum_{i\in I_{l}}\lambda_{i}\mathrm{Tr}(\pi_{i})

(6.10)

With that we can define a new quantum state

\displaystyle\sigma\coloneqq\sum_{l=1}^{n}\mu_{l}E_{l}.

(6.11)

Note that
\sigma
indeed defines a quantum state as positive semi-definiteness is immediate from the definition and normalisation follows by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(\sigma)=\sum_{l=1}^{m}\mu_{l}\mathrm{Tr}(E_{l})=\sum_{l=1}^{m}\sum_{i\in I_{l}}\lambda_{i}\mathrm{Tr}(\pi_{i})=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho)=1.

The following lemma shows that for
\delta
small,
\sigma
is a good approximation of
\rho.

Lemma 6.2

Let
\rho
be a state on
d
-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and
\delta\geq 0.
Denote by
\sigma
the state defined through the spectral clustering for
\rho
and
\delta
as outlined above, i.e. (
6.11
).
Then we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|\rho-\sigma\right\|_{1}\leq\frac{d^{2}\delta}{2}.

(6.12)

Proof.
Note that by construction of the clustering (
6.8
), the averages
\mu_{l}
satisfy for all
l\in[n]
and
i\in I_{l}

\displaystyle|\lambda_{i}-\mu_{l}|\leq\sum_{j\in I_{l}}\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\pi_{j})}{\mathrm{Tr}(E_{l})}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|\leq\sum_{j\in I_{l}}\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\pi_{j})}{\mathrm{Tr}(E_{l})}|I_{l}|\delta=|I_{l}|\delta.

By that we see

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|\rho-\sigma\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\sum_{l=1}^{m}\sum_{i\in I_{l}}(\lambda_{i}-\mu_{l})\pi_{i}\right\|_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{m}\sum_{i\in I_{l}}\mathrm{Tr}(\pi_{i})|\lambda_{i}-\mu_{l}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{m}\sum_{i\in I_{l}}\mathrm{Tr}(\pi_{i})|I_{l}|\leq\frac{d^{2}\delta}{2},

where we have used that
\sum_{l=1}^{n}\sum_{i\in I_{l}}\mathrm{Tr}(\pi_{i})=d.

6.2
Rotation lemmas

In this section we state and prove the rotation lemmas mentioned above, which are a main ingredient in the proofs of Theorems
6.10
,
6.11
,
6.13
and
6.17
. All of the rotation lemmas are concerned with providing an unitary close to the identity which rotates nearby subspaces onto each other. To be more precise we find unitaries
U\approx\mathbbm{1}
which in the case of

1.

\left(|v_{i}\rangle\right)_{i=1}^{n}
and
\left(|w_{i}\rangle\right)_{i=1}^{n}
being orthonormal systems with
|v_{i}\rangle\approx|w_{i}\rangle
satisfy
U|v_{i}\rangle=|w_{i}\rangle
(Lemma
6.5
),

2.

\left(|\psi_{i}\rangle\right)_{i=1}^{n}
being an orthonormal system and
F
being an orthogonal projection with
F|\psi_{i}\rangle\approx|\psi_{i}\rangle
satisfy
U|\psi_{i}\rangle\in\operatorname{ran}(F)
(Lemma
6.6
),

3.

\left(E_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{n}
and
\left(F_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{n}
being orthogonal projections with
E_{i}\approx F_{i}
satisfy
UE_{i}U^{*}=F_{i}
(Lemma
6.7
).

We first state and prove Lemmas
6.3
and
6.4
, which are at the core of the proofs of the results mentioned above and then continue to show the rotation lemmas.

Lemma 6.3

Let
A
be a linear operator and
E
be a projection on some finite dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
satisfying

\displaystyle\|A-E\|<1.

(6.13)

Then
\dim(\operatorname{ran}(A))\geq\dim(\operatorname{ran}(E)).

In particular, this gives by symmetry for
E
and
F
being projections, satisfying

\displaystyle\|E-F\|<1,

(6.14)

that
\dim(\operatorname{ran}(E))=\dim(\operatorname{ran}(F)).

Proof.
We write
d_{E}=\dim(\operatorname{ran}(E)).

Assume for contradiction
\dim(\text{ran}(E))>\dim(\text{ran}(A))
and take
\left(\varphi_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{d_{E}}
to be a basis of
\text{ran}(E)
. As
\left(A\varphi_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{d_{E}}
cannot be linearly independent, there exists
(\lambda_{i})_{i=1}^{d_{E}}\neq 0\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{E}}
such that for
\varphi=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{E}}\lambda_{i}\varphi_{i}\neq 0\in\mathcal{H}
we have
A\phi=0
.
But this gives

\displaystyle\|E-A\|\geq\frac{\|(E-A)\varphi\|}{\|\varphi\|}=\frac{\|E\varphi\|}{\|\varphi\|}=\frac{\|\varphi\|}{\|\varphi\|}=1

contradicting the assumption of the lemma and therefore giving
\dim(\text{ran}(E))\leq\dim(\text{ran}(A)).

Lemma 6.4

Let
E
and
F
be orthogonal projections on some finite dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
satisfying

\displaystyle\|E-F\|\leq\varepsilon

for some
0\leq\varepsilon<1
. Then there exists an unitary
U
with
\|U-\mathbbm{1}\|\leq 2\varepsilon
and

\displaystyle UEU^{*}=F.

(6.15)

Proof.
By assumption and Lemma
6.3
we have
\dim(\operatorname{ran}(E))=\dim(\operatorname{ran}(F))
and we write in the following
d_{E}=\dim(\operatorname{ran}(E)).

Define now

\displaystyle A\coloneqq FE+(\mathbbm{1}-F)(\mathbbm{1}-E)

and note that

\displaystyle\|A-\mathbbm{1}\|=\|(F-E)E-(F-E)(\mathbbm{1}-E)\|=\|F-E\|\leq\varepsilon,

(6.16)

where for the second equality we have used that
\mathbbm{1}-2E
is unitary.
By singular value decomposition (SVD) we can write

\displaystyle A=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{E}}a_{i}|v_{i}\rangle\!\langle w_{i}|+\sum_{i=d_{E}+1}^{d}a_{i}|v_{i}\rangle\!\langle w_{i}|

(6.17)

with
a_{i}\geq 0
being the singular values of
A
and
\left(|v_{i}\rangle\right)_{i=1}^{d_{E}}
and
\left(|w_{i}\rangle\right)_{i=1}^{d_{E}}
being orthonormal bases of
\text{ran}(F)
and
\text{ran}(E)
respectively and
\left(|v_{i}\rangle\right)_{i=d_{E}+1}^{d}
and
\left(|w_{i}\rangle\right)_{i=d_{E}+1}^{d}
being orthonormal bases of
\text{ran}(\mathbbm{1}-F)
and
\text{ran}(\mathbbm{1}-E)
respectively. That the SVD of
A
has this form follows by block diagonality of
A
or equivalently by considering SVDs of
FE
and
(\mathbbm{1}-F)(\mathbbm{1}-E)
individually leading to the first and second summand in (
6.17
).

Denoting the smallest singular value of
A
by
a_{i^{\prime}}
we find

\displaystyle\varepsilon\geq\|A-\mathbbm{1}\|

\displaystyle\geq\|a_{i^{\prime}}|v_{i^{\prime}}\rangle-|w_{i^{\prime}}\rangle\|=\sqrt{a^{2}_{i^{\prime}}+1-2a_{i^{\prime}}\Re\langle v_{i^{\prime}},w_{i^{\prime}}\rangle}\geq\sqrt{a^{2}_{i^{\prime}}+1-2a_{i^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=1-a_{i^{\prime}}=\max_{i=1,\dots,d}|1-a_{i}|,

where we have used in the second line that
a_{i}\leq 1
for all
i=1,\dots,d
which holds since
A^{*}A=EFE+(\mathbbm{1}-E)(\mathbbm{1}-F)(\mathbbm{1}-E)\leq\mathbbm{1}.

Define now the unitary

\displaystyle U=\sum_{i=1}^{d}|v_{i}\rangle\!\langle w_{i}|.

By the above we have

\displaystyle\|U-\mathbbm{1}\|\leq\|U-A\|+\|A-\mathbbm{1}\|=\max_{i=1,\dots,d}|a_{i}-1|+\|A-\mathbbm{1}\|\leq 2\varepsilon.

(6.18)

Moreover, by construction we have
UEU^{*}=F
.

We now state and prove the the rotations lemmas mentioned in points
1
-
3
above.

Lemma 6.5

Let
\left(|v_{i}\rangle\right)_{i=1}^{n}
and
\left(|w_{i}\rangle\right)_{i=1}^{n}
be orthonormal systems on some finite dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
such that

\displaystyle\||v_{i}\rangle-|w_{i}\rangle\|\leq\varepsilon

for some
\varepsilon\geq 0
. Then there exists an unitary
U
such that
\|U-\mathbbm{1}\|\leq 5\sqrt{n}\,\varepsilon
and

\displaystyle U|v_{i}\rangle=|w_{i}\rangle

(6.19)

for all
i\in[n].

Proof.
Define the orthogonal projections
E=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|v_{i}\rangle\!\langle v_{i}|
and
F=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|w_{i}\rangle\!\langle w_{i}|

and note that they satisfy

\displaystyle\left\|E-F\right\|\leq\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(|v_{i}\rangle-|w_{i}\rangle\right)\!\langle v_{i}|\right\|+\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(|v_{i}\rangle-|w_{i}\rangle\right)\!\langle w_{i}|\right\|\leq 2\varepsilon\sqrt{n},

(6.20)

where we used the triangle inequality, the
*
-invariance of the operator norm and for the last inequality

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(|v_{i}\rangle-|w_{i}\rangle\right)\!\langle v_{i}|\right\|\leq\sup_{\|\psi\|=1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\||v_{i}\rangle-|w_{i}\rangle\right\||\langle v_{i},\psi\rangle|\leq\varepsilon\sqrt{n}\sup_{\|\psi\|=1}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\langle v_{i},\psi\rangle|^{2}}\leq\varepsilon\sqrt{n},

which follows by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and similarly for the second term in (
6.20
).
Hence, by Lemma
6.4
there exists an unitary
U_{1}
such that
\|U_{1}-\mathbbm{1}\|\leq 4\varepsilon\sqrt{n}
and

\displaystyle U_{1}EU_{1}^{*}=F.

Furthermore, we define
the partial isometry

\displaystyle U_{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|w_{i}\rangle\!\langle v_{i}|

with support
\operatorname{ran}(E)
and image
\operatorname{ran}(F).
Clearly
U_{2}|v_{i}\rangle=|w_{i}\rangle
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\|U_{2}-E\|

\displaystyle=\sup_{\|\psi\|=1}\|(U_{2}-E)|\psi\rangle\|\leq\sup_{\|\psi\|=1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\||w_{i}\rangle-|v_{i}\rangle\right\||\langle v_{i},\psi\rangle|\leq\varepsilon\sqrt{n}.

We can now define

\displaystyle U=U_{2}\oplus U_{1}(\mathbbm{1}-E),

which is unitary since
U_{1}(\mathbbm{1}-E)
is a partial isometry with support
\operatorname{ran}(\mathbbm{1}-E)
and image
\operatorname{ran}(\mathbbm{1}-F).

Furthermore, it satisfies
U|v_{i}\rangle=|w_{i}\rangle
and

\displaystyle\left\|U-\mathbbm{1}\right|\leq\left\|U_{2}-E\right\|+\left\|U_{1}-\mathbbm{1}\right\|\leq 5\varepsilon\sqrt{n}.

Lemma 6.6

Let
\left(|\psi_{i}\rangle\right)_{i=1}^{n}
be an orthonormal system and
F
be some orthogonal projection on some finite dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
satisfying

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sum_{l=1}^{n}\left\|(\mathbbm{1}-F)|\psi_{i}\rangle\right\|^{2}}\leq\varepsilon.

(6.21)

for some
0\leq\varepsilon<1
and all
i\in[n].
Then there exists an unitary
U
such that
\|U-\mathbbm{1}\|\leq 2\,\varepsilon
and

\displaystyle FU|\psi_{i}\rangle=U|\psi_{i}\rangle

(6.22)

for all
i\in[n].

In particular if

\displaystyle F|\psi_{i}\rangle\approx^{\varepsilon}|\psi_{i}\rangle

(6.23)

for some
0\leq\varepsilon<1/\sqrt{n},
then there exists an unitary
U
such that
\|U-\mathbbm{1}\|\leq 2\sqrt{n}\,\varepsilon
satisfying (
6.22
).

Proof.
We define the orthogonal projection
E=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\psi_{i}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{i}|
. Note that we have by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and assumption (
6.21
)

\displaystyle\|E-FE\|

\displaystyle=\|(\mathbbm{1}-F)E\|\leq\sup_{\|\psi\|=1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|(\mathbbm{1}-F)|\psi_{i}\rangle\|\,|\langle\psi_{i},\psi\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\sup_{\|\psi\|=1}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\langle\psi_{i},\psi\rangle|^{2}}\leq\varepsilon<1.

Using now Lemma
6.3
we see
\dim(\operatorname{ran}(FE))\geq\dim(E)
and since the opposite inequality holds trivially we have
\dim(\operatorname{ran}(FE))=\dim(E).

Let now
F^{\prime}
be the orthogonal projection onto
\text{ran}(FE)
and note that it satisfies
F^{\prime}=F^{\prime}F
and hence
F^{\prime}E=F^{\prime}FE=FE
. Since, as established
\dim(\operatorname{ran}(F^{\prime}))=\dim(\operatorname{ran}(FE))=\dim(\operatorname{ran}(E))
,
F^{\prime}
and
E
are unitarily equivalent and hence also

\displaystyle\|F^{\prime}(\mathbbm{1}-E)\|=\|(\mathbbm{1}-E)F^{\prime}\|=\|(\mathbbm{1}-F^{\prime})E\|=\|(\mathbbm{1}-F)E\|\leq\varepsilon.

Therefore, using orthogonality, we have

\displaystyle\|E-F^{\prime}\|=\|(\mathbbm{1}-F^{\prime})E+F^{\prime}(E-\mathbbm{1})\|=\max\Big\{\|F^{\prime}(\mathbbm{1}-E)\|,\|(\mathbbm{1}-F^{\prime})E\|\Big\}\leq\varepsilon.

Hence, using now Lemma
6.4
again, there exists an unitary
U
satisfying
\|U-\mathbbm{1}\|\leq 2\varepsilon
and
UEU^{*}=F^{\prime}
, which gives

\displaystyle FU|\psi_{i}\rangle=FUE|\psi_{i}\rangle=FF^{\prime}U|\psi_{i}\rangle=F^{\prime}U|\psi_{i}\rangle=UE|\psi_{i}\rangle=U|\psi_{i}\rangle.

For the statement under the assumption (
6.23
), we simply use that in this case

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sum_{l=1}^{n}\left\|(\mathbbm{1}-F)|\psi_{i}\rangle\right\|^{2}}\leq\sqrt{\sum_{l=1}^{n}\varepsilon^{2}}=\sqrt{n}\varepsilon<1

and apply the first part of the lemma.

Lemma 6.7

Let
\left(E_{l}\right)_{l=1}^{n}
and
\left(F_{l}\right)_{l=1}^{n}
be families of orthogonal projections on some
d
-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with
n\in[d]
satisfying
\sum_{l=1}^{n}E_{l}\leq\mathbbm{1}
,
\sum_{l=1}^{n}F_{l}\leq\mathbbm{1}
and

\displaystyle\|E_{l}-F_{l}\|\leq\varepsilon

for some
0\leq\varepsilon<1
and all
l\in[n]
. Then there exists an unitary
U
with
\|U-\mathbbm{1}\|\leq 6\varepsilon\sqrt{n}.
and

\displaystyle UE_{l}U^{*}=F_{l}

(6.24)

for all
l\in[n].

Proof.
Using for each
l\in[n]
the Lemma
6.4
individually, we find unitaries
U_{l}
with
\|U_{l}-\mathbbm{1}\|\leq 2\varepsilon
and

\displaystyle U_{l}E_{l}U_{l}^{*}=F_{l}.

We can now define the partial isometries
V_{l}=U_{l}E_{l}
with support
\operatorname{ran}(E_{l})
and image
\operatorname{ran}(F_{l})
, which by the above satisfy
\|V_{l}-E_{l}\|\leq 2\varepsilon.
We denote
E=\sum_{l=1}^{n}E_{l}
and
F=\sum_{l=1}^{n}F_{l}
and define

\displaystyle V=\bigoplus_{l=1}^{n}U_{l},

which by construction is also a partial isometry with support
\operatorname{ran}(E)
and image
\operatorname{ran}(F)
. Furthermore, note that we have

\displaystyle\|V-E\|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\|\psi\|=1}\sum_{l=1}^{n}\left\|(U_{l}-E_{l})\psi\right\|\leq 2\varepsilon\sup_{\|\psi\|=1}\sum_{l=1}^{n}\left\|E_{l}\psi\right\|

\displaystyle\leq 2\varepsilon\sqrt{n}\sup_{\|\psi\|=1}\sqrt{\sum_{l=1}^{n}\left\|E_{l}\psi\right\|^{2}}\leq 2\varepsilon\sqrt{n},

where for the second line we used the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and then Pythagorean identity. This gives

\displaystyle\left\|(\mathbbm{1}-F)-(\mathbbm{1}-E)\right\|=\left\|F-E\right\|=\left\|VEV^{*}-E\right\|\leq 2\|V-E\|\leq 4\varepsilon\sqrt{n}.

Hence, applying Lemma
6.4
again for
\mathbbm{1}-E
and
\mathbbm{1}-F,
we find another unitary
U_{\perp}
which satisfies

\displaystyle\|U_{\perp}-\mathbbm{1}\|\leq 4\varepsilon\sqrt{n}

and
U_{\perp}(\mathbbm{1}-E)U_{\perp}^{*}=\mathbbm{1}-F.
Analogously to the above we define the partial isometry
V_{\perp}=U_{\perp}(\mathbbm{1}-E)
, which has support
\operatorname{ran}(\mathbbm{1}-E)
and image
\operatorname{ran}(\mathbbm{1}-F)
, and then finally the unitary

\displaystyle U=V\oplus V_{\perp}.

Note that by construction,
U
satisfies
UE_{l}U^{*}=F_{l}
for all
l\in[n]
and furthermore

\displaystyle\left\|U-\mathbbm{1}\right\|\leq\left\|V-E\right\|+\|(U_{\perp}-\mathbbm{1})(\mathbbm{1}-E)\|\leq 6\varepsilon\sqrt{n}.

Another useful observation which is used in the proofs of the results below is that two channels with Stinespring isometries being close to each other in operator norm, are close to each other in diamond norm. This fact is stated and proved in the following lemma for later convenience.

Lemma 6.8

Let
V:\mathcal{H}_{A}\to\mathcal{H}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E}
and
W:\mathcal{H}_{A}\to\mathcal{H}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E}
be isometries on Hilbert spaces satisfying

\displaystyle\|V-W\|\leq\varepsilon

(6.25)

for
\varepsilon\geq 0
. Then, defining the quantum channels
\mathcal{N}=\mathrm{Tr}_{E}(V(\,\cdot\,)V^{*})
and
\mathcal{M}=\mathrm{Tr}_{E}(W(\,\cdot\,)W^{*})
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathcal{N}-\mathcal{M}\right\|_{\diamond}\leq\varepsilon.

(6.26)

Proof.
Using the definition of the diamond norm, monotonicity of the trace norm under under the partial trace and Hölder’s inequality we find

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{N}-\mathcal{M}\right\|_{\diamond}

\displaystyle=\sup_{d\in\mathbb{N},x\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d})\,\|x\|_{1}=1}\left\|\mathrm{Tr}_{E}(VxV^{*})-\mathrm{Tr}_{E}(WxW^{*})\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{d\in\mathbb{N},x\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d})\,\|x\|_{1}=1}\left\|VxV^{*}-WxW^{*}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{d\in\mathbb{N},x\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d})\,\|x\|_{1}=1}\left\|(V-W)xV^{*}\right\|_{1}+\sup_{d\in\mathbb{N},x\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d})\,\|x\|_{1}=1}\left\|Wx(V^{*}-W^{*})\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|V-W\|\leq 2\varepsilon,

where we dropped the identities
{\rm{id}}_{d}
for notational simplicity.

6.3
Generalised depolarising trick

In this section we establish the
generalised depolarising trick
(Lemma
6.9
below), which is a useful tool for fixing approximate fixed point equations in the sense of Definitions
3.1
and
3.2
and is employed in the proof of Theorem
6.13
.

We consider two states
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
which satisfy the operator inequality

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}\leq(1+p)\sigma

(6.27)

for some
p\in[0,1].
The main observation is then that we can find a generalised depolarising channel of the form

\displaystyle\Phi=(1-p){\rm{id}}+p\mathrm{Tr}(\,\cdot\,)\,\omega

(6.28)

for some state
\omega,
which ‘pulls back’
\sigma^{\prime}
onto
\sigma,
i.e.
\Phi(\sigma^{\prime})=\sigma.
Furthermore, by the explicit form of (
6.28
) it is immediate to see that
\Phi\approx^{p}{\rm{id}}.
In the context of an approximate fixed point equation
\mathcal{N}(\sigma)\approx\sigma
we can take
\sigma^{\prime}=\mathcal{N}(\sigma).
Hence, if (
6.27
) is satisfied we can define
\mathcal{M}=\Phi\circ\mathcal{N}
which has
\sigma
as an exact fixed point and is close to
\mathcal{N},
i.e.
\mathcal{M}\approx^{p}\mathcal{N}.
For an illustration of the generalised depolarising trick consider Figure
1
.

(a)

Example 1

(b)

Example 2

Figure 1
:
Consider the Bloch sphere representation of a qubit, i.e. the isomorphism between real vectors with Euclidean norm less than or equal to 1 and states on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
given by
r\mapsto\frac{1}{2}(\mathbbm{1}+r_{x}\sigma_{x}+r_{y}\sigma_{y}+r_{z}\sigma_{z}).
In the figures only the slice of the Bloch sphere with
r_{y}=0
is pictured. Two examples of states
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
are illustrated, together with a third state
\omega
lying on the crossing point of the continuation of the line from
\sigma^{\prime}
to
\sigma
and the boundary of the sphere. Note, that
\omega
is given by
(\sigma-(1-p_{*})\sigma^{\prime})/p_{*}
with
p_{*}\in[0,1]
being the smallest number such that
\sigma-(1-p)\sigma^{\prime}\geq 0.
As
\sigma=(1-p_{*})\sigma^{\prime}+p_{*}\omega_{p_{*}},
the value
p_{*}
can pictorially be identified as the ratio between the distances of
\sigma^{\prime}
and
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
and
\omega.
The idea of the generalised deopolarising trick is to define the channel
\Phi=(1-p_{*}){\rm{id}}+p_{*}\mathrm{Tr}(\,\cdot\,)\omega
which satisfies
\Phi\approx^{p_{*}}{\rm{id}}
and ’pulls’
\sigma^{\prime}
onto
\sigma,
i.e.
\Phi(\sigma^{\prime})=\sigma.
Figure
1(a)
presents an example where
p_{*}
is rather small and Figure
1(b)
an example where
p_{*}
is rather big and hence the whole Bloch sphere must be significantly deformed by
\Phi
in this case. Note, as shown in the proof of Lemma
6.9
, we have
p_{*}\leq p
if
\sigma^{\prime}\leq(1+p)\sigma.

We now state and prove the generalised depolarising trick in the following lemma. In fact, we not just only prove it for single states
\sigma^{\prime}
and
\sigma
but for families, as this the context in which it is employed in the proof of Theorem
6.13
below.

Lemma 6.9
(Generalised depolarising trick)

Let
\left(\sigma_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{n}
and
\left(\sigma^{\prime}_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{n}
be families of states on a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
satisfying

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}_{i}\leq(1+p)\sigma_{i}

(6.29)

for some
p\in[0,1]
and all
i\in[n].
Furthermore, assume that
\sigma^{\prime}_{i}
and
\sigma^{\prime}_{j}
have orthogonal support for
i\neq j.
Then there exists a channel

\displaystyle\Phi\approx^{p}{\rm{id}}

(6.30)

such that

\displaystyle\Phi(\sigma^{\prime}_{i})=\sigma_{i},

(6.31)

for all
i\in[n].

Proof.
Without loss of generality assume
p>0
as otherwise
\sigma^{\prime}_{i}=\sigma_{i}
holds true immediately. Note that by assumption
\sigma_{i}-(1-p)\sigma^{\prime}_{i}\geq 0
, which follows trivially for
p=1
and otherwise by

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}-(1-p)\sigma_{i}^{\prime}=(1-p)\left(\frac{1}{1-p}\sigma_{i}-\sigma_{i}^{\prime}\right)\geq(1-p)\Big((1+p)\sigma_{i}-\sigma_{i}^{\prime}\Big)\geq 0.

Hence, we can define the normalised state

\omega_{i}\coloneqq\frac{\sigma_{i}-(1-p)\sigma_{i}^{\prime}}{p}.

Furthermore, since
\sigma^{\prime}_{i}
and
\sigma^{\prime}_{j}
have orthogonal support for
i\neq j
, there exists a family of orthogonal projections
\left(\pi_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{n}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{i}=\mathbbm{1}
and

\displaystyle\pi_{i}\sigma_{j}^{\prime}\pi_{i}=\delta_{ij}\sigma^{\prime}_{j},

with
\delta_{ij}
denoting the Kronecker delta. With that we can now define
the quantum channel

\displaystyle\Phi(\,\cdot\,)\coloneqq(1-p){\rm{id}}+p\sum_{i=1}^{n}\,\mathrm{Tr}(\pi_{i}(\,\cdot\,)\pi_{i})\,\omega_{i}.

Note, by the triangle inequality and the fact that every quantum channel has diamond norm equal to 1, we see

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|\Phi-{\rm{id}}\right\|_{\diamond}\leq\frac{p}{2}\left(\left\|{\rm{id}}\right\|_{\diamond}+\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{Tr}(\pi_{i}(\,\cdot\,)\pi_{i})\,\omega_{i}\right\|_{\diamond}\right)=p.

Moreover, by definition we have for all

\displaystyle\Phi(\sigma_{i}^{\prime})=(1-p)\sigma_{i}^{\prime}+p\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\pi_{j}\sigma^{\prime}_{i}\pi_{j}\right)\omega_{j}=(1-p)\sigma_{i}^{\prime}+\sigma_{i}-(1-p)\sigma_{i}^{\prime}=\sigma_{i},

which finishes the proof.

Proof of Theorem

.

We employ the spectral clustering technique outlined in Section

\delta\geq 0

We use the fact that the Hilbert-Schmidt norm is dominated by the trace-norm and the dual representation of the Hilbert-Schmidt norm to see for every

\displaystyle 2\varepsilon

\displaystyle\geq\left\|\rho-\sum_{i=1}^{d^{4}}p_{i}U_{i}\rho U_{i}^{*}\right\|_{1}\geq\left\|\rho-\sum_{i=1}^{d^{4}}p_{i}U_{i}\rho U_{i}^{*}\right\|_{2}=\sup_{\|\omega\|_{2}=1}\left|\mathrm{Tr}\left(\omega^{*}\left(\rho-\sum_{i=1}^{d^{4}}U_{i}\rho U_{i}^{*}\right)\right)\right|

From that we get

\displaystyle\left\|\rho-U_{j}\rho U_{j}^{*}\right\|^{2}_{2}=2\left(\|\rho\|^{2}_{2}-\mathrm{Tr}\left(\rho U_{j}\rho U_{j}^{*}\right)\right)\leq\frac{4\varepsilon\|\rho\|_{2}}{p_{i}}\leq\frac{4\varepsilon}{p_{i}}.

For (

Remark 6.16

Lemmas

from a few. By that, having only knowledge of the channel’s output on some input states, we might be able to determine the whole channel fully or at least partially. Let us illustrate this by considering the example of an unital channel
\mathcal{N},
which we know to have an additional exact fixed point state
\rho.
Assume furthermore that the state
\rho
has simple spectrum and hence can be written in spectral decomposition

\displaystyle\rho=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}|\psi_{i}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{i}|

and an orthonormal basis

From Lemma

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(|\psi_{i}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{i}|)=|\psi_{i}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{i}|

\displaystyle V|\psi_{i}\rangle=|\psi_{i}\rangle|\phi_{i}\rangle

on the environment Hilbert space

Therefore, for any linear operator

which can be written in matrix components as

we see that the channel’s output is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(A)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}a_{i,j}\langle\phi_{j},\phi_{i}\rangle\,|\psi_{i}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{j}|.

Therefore, only from the two fixed points

uniquely up to knowledge of the complex numbers

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}\approx^{f(d,\varepsilon)}\rho_{i}\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad\mathcal{M}\approx^{g(d,\varepsilon)}\mathcal{N},

(7.3)

and which satisfy the fixed point equations

(7.4)

Importantly, here we demand that the new channel

satisfies an exact fixed point equation for all states

at the same time.

Furthermore, analogously to Definition

we say

approximate fixed point equations are

if the functions

and

in the above satisfy

\displaystyle f(d,\varepsilon)=c\,d^{b}\varepsilon^{a}\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad g(d,\varepsilon)=c^{\prime}d^{b^{\prime}}\varepsilon^{a^{\prime}},

(7.5)

for some
a,a^{\prime}>0
(and typically

) and

and

Theorem

below shows (via a concrete counterexample) that already in the classical case, i.e. for
\mathcal{S}={\rm{Prob}}({\cal X})
and
\mathcal{C}={\rm{Stoch}}({\cal X})
, rapid fixability of two approximate fixed points at once is in general impossible.
Here, we find for every dimension
d
, two probability vectors and a stochastic matrix which satisfy approximate fixed point equations with error parameter vanishing exponentially as
d\to\infty
, i.e.
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{2^{d}}.
However, for any two new probability vectors and a stochastic matrix, which fix named approximate fixed point equations at once, at least one needs to be at a constant distance from the corresponding original one.

Theorem 7.1
(No rapid fixing of 2 approximate fixed points in the classical case)

For every
d\in\mathbb{N}
there exist probability vectors
\vec{P}_{1}
and
\vec{P}_{2}
and a stochastic matrix
T
on a classical alphabet
{\cal X}
with
|{\cal X}|=d

such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|\vec{P}_{1}-\vec{P}_{2}\right\|_{1}=1

(7.6)

and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|T\vec{P}_{1}-\vec{P}_{1}\right\|_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2^{d}},\quad\quad\frac{1}{2}\left\|T\vec{P}_{2}-\vec{P}_{2}\right\|_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2^{d}},

(7.7)

and which satisfy the following: For any pair of probability vectors
\vec{Q}_{1}
and
\vec{Q}_{2}
and any stochastic matrix
S
such that

\displaystyle S\vec{Q}_{1}=\vec{Q}_{1}\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad S\vec{Q}_{2}=\vec{Q}_{2},

(7.8)

we either have

\displaystyle\|S-T\|\geq\frac{1}{4}\quad\text{or}\quad\frac{1}{2}\left\|\vec{Q}_{1}-\vec{P}_{1}\right\|_{1}\geq\frac{1}{2}\quad\text{or}\quad\frac{1}{2}\left\|\vec{Q}_{2}-\vec{P}_{2}\right\|_{1}\geq\frac{1}{2}.

(7.9)

This shows that two approximate fixed points are, in general, not rapidly fixable at once for the pair
\mathcal{S}={\rm{Prob}}(\mathcal{{\cal X}})
and
\mathcal{\mathcal{C}}={\rm{Stoch}}(\mathcal{{\cal X}})
for classical alphabets of size
|{\cal X}|=d.

We can embed this counterexample into the quantum setting using the natural constructions (
2.6
)
and (
2.7
). Hence, we find for every dimension

, two states and a channel which satisfy approximate fixed point equations with error parameter vanishing exponentially in the dimension. Importantly, in the quantum setting we have a bigger set of possible states and channels to fix named approximate fixed point equation. Therefore, the impossibility statement of Theorem

does not immediately also give the corresponding impossibility statement in the quantum case. However, using a slight adjustment of the classical argument we are still able to show that for any new two states and a channel, which fix named approximate fixed point equations at once, at least one needs to be at a distance of order
\mathcal{O}(1/d)
from the corresponding original one. Since, as mentioned, we have for the error parameter

Theorem 7.2
(No rapid fixing of 2 approximate fixed points in quantum case)

For every
d\in\mathbb{N}
there exist states
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
and a channel
\mathcal{N}
on a
d

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\right\|_{1}=1

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathcal{N}(\rho_{1})-\rho_{1}\right\|_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2^{d}},\quad\quad\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathcal{N}(\rho_{2})-\rho_{2}\right\|_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2^{d}},

and which satisfy the following: For all states
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{M}-\mathcal{N}\|_{\diamond}\geq\frac{1}{16d}\quad\text{or}\quad\frac{1}{2}\left\|\sigma_{1}-\rho_{1}\right\|_{1}\geq\frac{1}{2}\quad\text{or}\quad\frac{1}{2}\left\|\sigma_{2}-\rho_{2}\right\|_{1}\geq\frac{1}{2}.

\mathcal{S}=\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{H})

\mathcal{C}={\rm{CPTP}}(\mathcal{H}).

Therefore, by (

Hence, by assumption (

2.2
) we also have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left((\mathbbm{1}-\pi_{1})\mathcal{N}_{T}(\pi_{1})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\left((\mathbbm{1}-\pi_{1})\left(\mathcal{N}_{T}(\pi_{1})-\mathcal{M}(\pi_{1})\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\pi_{1})}{2}\left\|\mathcal{N}_{T}-\mathcal{M}\right\|_{\diamond}\leq\frac{1}{16}

(7.32)

and similarly

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left((\mathbbm{1}-\pi_{2})\mathcal{N}_{T}(\pi_{2})\right)\leq\frac{1}{16}.

(7.33)

Now note that this gives that either
\langle d|\pi_{1}|d\rangle<\frac{1}{2}
or
\langle d|\pi_{2}|d\rangle<\frac{1}{2}
as otherwise by definition of the stochastic matrix
T
in (
7.21
)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left((\mathbbm{1}-\pi_{1})\mathcal{N}_{T}(\pi_{1})\right)\geq\mathrm{Tr}\left(\pi_{2}\mathcal{N}_{T}(\pi_{1})\right)\geq T_{dd}\langle d|\pi_{2}|d\rangle\langle d|\pi_{1}|d\rangle\geq\frac{1}{4}.

Without loss of generality we hence assume

\displaystyle\langle d|\pi_{1}|d\rangle<1/2

(7.34)

This gives that there exists
i_{*}\in[d-1]
such that

\displaystyle\langle i_{*}|\pi_{1}|i_{*}\rangle\geq\frac{1}{2}

(7.35)

as otherwise
\langle j|(\mathbbm{1}-\pi_{1})|j\rangle>\frac{1}{2}
for all
j\in[d]
and hence

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left((\mathbbm{1}-\pi_{1})\mathcal{N}_{T}(\pi_{1})\right)\geq\sum_{j=1}^{d}T_{jj}\langle j|(\mathbbm{1}-\pi_{1})|j\rangle\langle j|\pi_{1}|j\rangle>\frac{1}{8}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\langle j|\pi_{1}|j\rangle=\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\pi_{1})}{8},

which is a contradiction to (
7.32
) since
\pi_{1}\neq 0
. Note that since
\langle i_{*}|\pi_{1}|i_{*}\rangle\geq 1/2
also
\langle i_{*}+1|\pi_{1}|i_{*}+1\rangle\geq 1/2
as otherwise we have by definition of the stochastic matrix
T
in (
7.21
)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left((\mathbbm{1}-\pi_{1})\mathcal{N}_{T}(\pi_{1})\right)\geq T_{i_{*}+1\,i_{*}}\langle i_{*}+1|(\mathbbm{1}-\pi_{1})|i_{*}+1\rangle\langle i_{*}|\pi_{1}|i_{*}\rangle>\frac{1}{16},

which is a contradiction to (
7.32
). However, by iterating this argument we see
\langle j|\pi_{1}|j\rangle\geq 1/2
for all
j\in\{i_{*},\cdots,d\},
which is a contradiction to (
7.34
). To conclude, we have shown that
\mathcal{M}
cannot have two orthogonal invariant subspaces, and therefore
\dim({\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{M}))=1
, as this would otherwise contradict assumption (
7.32
).

Proof of Theorem
7.2
.

The proof of Theorem
7.2
follows from exactly the same lines as the one of Theorem
7.1
, with the usage of Lemma
7.6
instead of Lemma
7.5
. We still give it here explicitly for completeness.

We use the natural embedding (
2.6
) and pick for every dimension
d\in\mathbb{N}
the states
\rho_{1}\coloneqq\rho_{\vec{P}_{1}}
and
\rho_{2}\coloneqq\rho_{\vec{P}_{2}}
with probability vectors
\vec{P}_{1}
and
\vec{P}_{2}
defined in (
7.19
) and (
7.20
).
We again have by orthogonality

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\right\|_{1}=1.

(7.36)

Furthermore, using the embedding (
2.7
), we pick as channel
\mathcal{N}\coloneqq\mathcal{N}_{T}
with
T
being the stochastic matrix defined in (
7.21
) and see from (
7.22
) and (
7.23
) that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathcal{N}(\rho_{1})-\rho_{1}\right\|_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\left\|T\vec{P}_{1}-\vec{P}_{1}\right\|_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2^{d}},\quad\quad\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathcal{N}(\rho_{2})-\rho_{2}\right\|_{1}=0.

Hence,
\rho_{1}
,
\rho_{2}
and
\mathcal{N}
satisfy the assumptions of the theorem. To prove the final statement of the theorem, i.e. (
7.13
), assume that
\mathcal{M}
is a channel satisfying
\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{M}-\mathcal{N}\|\leq\frac{1}{16d}
. Then by Lemma
7.6
, we see that if
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
are states and fixed points of
\mathcal{M},
we necessarily have
\sigma\coloneqq\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}.
Therefore, using (
7.36
) we have

\displaystyle 1=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\right\|_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\|\rho_{1}-\sigma\right\|_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|\sigma-\rho_{2}\right\|_{1}

and hence either

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|\rho_{1}-\sigma\right\|_{1}\geq\frac{1}{2}\quad\text{or}\quad\frac{1}{2}\left\|\rho_{2}-\sigma\right\|_{1}\geq\frac{1}{2}.

8
Summary and open problems

We have defined the notion of fixability and rapid fixability of approximate fixed point equations of states and channels, which are, respectively, elements of the fixed sets
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{H})
and
\mathcal{C}\subseteq{\rm{CPTP}}(\mathcal{H})
(Definition
3.1
and
3.2
). For finite dimensional Hilbert spaces and under the assumption that
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{C}
are closed and contain a fixed point pair, we have shown that approximate fixed point equations are always fixable in the sense of Definition
3.1
(Proposition
4.1
). We have applied this result to show that for any approximate quantum Markov chain
\rho_{ABC}
there exists an exact quantum Markov chain close to
\rho_{ABC}.
Here, the provided upper bound on the distance between
\rho_{ABC}
and
\sigma_{ABC}
necessarily depends on the dimensions of the systems and decays for vanishing conditional mutual information
I(A:C|B)_{\rho}
(Proposition
4.2
).

Furthermore, we have established rapid fixability of approximate fixed point equations for the choices

1.

\mathcal{S}=\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{H})
and
\mathcal{C}={\rm{CPTP}}(\mathcal{H})
(Theorem
5.1
),

2.

\mathcal{S}={\rm{Prob}}({\cal X})
and
\mathcal{C}={\rm{Stoch}}({\cal X})
(Theorem
5.2
),

3.

\mathcal{S}=\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{H})
and
\mathcal{C}
being the set of unitary channels (Theorem
6.10
),

4.

\mathcal{S}=\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{H})
and
\mathcal{C}
being the set of mixed-unitary channels (Theorem
6.11
),

5.

\mathcal{S}=\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{H})
and
\mathcal{C}
being the set of unital channels (Theorem
6.13
),

6.

\mathcal{S}
being the set of pure states on a bipartite Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{AB}=\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
and
\mathcal{C}={\rm{id}}_{A}\otimes{\rm{CPTP}}(\mathcal{H}_{B})
(Theorem
6.17
).

Lastly, in Corollary
7.3
we have shown that approximate fixed point equations are not rapidly fixable for

1.

\mathcal{S}=\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{H}_{AB})
and
\mathcal{C}={\rm{id}}_{A}\otimes{\rm{CPTP}}(\mathcal{H}_{B}),

2.

\mathcal{S}={\rm{Prob}}({\cal X}\times\mathcal{Y})
and
\mathcal{C}={\rm{id}}_{X}\otimes{\rm{Stoch}}(\mathcal{Y}).

For the last result, we generalised the notion of rapid fixability to the situation of multiple approximate fixed point states of one specific channel. In this context, we show that these multiple approximate fixed point equations are not rapidly fixable at once, by providing an explicit counterexample (Theorems
7.1
and Theorem
7.2
).

In the following we list some interesting open problems which can be subject to future research:

It would be interesting to obtain optimal bounds on the approximation functions
f
and
g
introduced in Definitions
3.1
and
3.2
for different choices of
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{C}.
Here, to establish optimality one needs to prove the corresponding lower bounds on the possible approximation functions. For that, we have already seen in Remark
5.4
that at least the scaling of
\max\{f,g\}
for the cases
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C})=(\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{H}),{\rm{CPTP}}(\mathcal{H}))
and
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C})=({\rm{Prob}}({\cal X}),{\rm{Stoch}}({\cal X}))
given in Theorems
5.1
and
5.2
is optimal. However, providing optimal bounds on both
f
and
g
individually, including the correct constants is still open.
Furthermore, the constructions carried out for the above mentioned results on rapid fixability for all other choices of
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{C}
led to bounds on the approximation functions which explicitly depend on the dimension of the underlying Hilbert space. It would be of great interest to determine when this dimension dependence is necessary.

Probably the most significant open problem, which lies outside the immediate scope of fixability of approximate fixed point equations, is to establish an improved version of Proposition
4.2
, with an explicit control on the robustness of quantum Markov chains. As outlined at the end of Section
4
(in particular around (
4.23
)) it would be highly desirable to prove a bound for tripartite states
\rho_{ABC}
of the form

\displaystyle\min_{\sigma_{ABC}\ \text{QMC}}\,\frac{1}{2}\left\|\rho_{ABC}-\sigma_{ABC}\right\|_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{\leq}}c\,d^{\,b_{A}\,}_{A}d^{\,b_{B}\,}_{B}d^{\,b_{C}}_{C}\,I(A:C|B)^{a}_{\rho},

(8.1)

with constants
a>0
and
b_{A},b_{B},b_{C},c\geq 0
independent of the dimensions
d_{A},d_{B},d_{C}
and
\rho_{ABC}.
