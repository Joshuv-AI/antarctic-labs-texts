Matsuo algebras in characteristic 2
Tom De Medts, Mathias Stout

Abstract
. We extend the theory of Matsuo algebras, which are certain non-associative algebras related to 3-transposition groups, to characteristic 2. Instead of idempotent elements associated to points in the corresponding Fischer space, our algebras are now generated by nilpotent elements associated to lines. For many 3-transposition groups, this still gives rise to a ℤ / 2 ​ ℤ \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} -graded fusion law, and we provide a complete classification of when this occurs. In one particular small case, arising from the 3-transposition group Sym ⁡ ( 4 ) \Sym(4) , the fusion law is even stronger, and the resulting Miyamoto group is an algebraic group 𝔾 a 2 ⋊ 𝔾 m \mathbb{G}_{a}^{2}\rtimes\mathbb{G}_{m} .

Introduction

Axial algebras
have been introduced by Jon Hall, Felix Rehren and Sergey Shpectorov in
[
HRS15
]
. They form a class of non-associative algebras generated by
idempotents
, such that each idempotent gives rise to a decomposition of the algebra respecting a certain
fusion law
.

One of the early examples that has been studied in more detail, are the so-called
Matsuo algebras
, related to
3
-transposition groups. More precisely, if
(G,D)
is a
3
-transposition group (where
G
is the group and
D
is the distinguished generating set of involutions), then for each commutative field
k
with
\Char(k)\neq 2
and each
\eta\in k\setminus\{0,1\}
, there is a
k
-algebra
A

spanned
by idempotents corresponding to the elements of
D
, with a very precise multiplication table (see
Definition

3.1
below). Each of these generating idempotents
e
then gives rise to a decomposition of
A
into eigenspaces for the left multiplication by
e
, and the multiplication of these eigenspaces satisfies the
Jordan fusion law
, making it very similar in structure as the Peirce decomposition for Jordan algebras.
(We refer to
[
DMR17
]
for a deeper connection between Matsuo algebras and Jordan algebras.)

Extending the theory to characteristic
2
is not obvious. First, the decomposition into eigenspaces for an idempotent can never have a
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-graded fusion law, as we observe in
Proposition

4.1
below. Therefore, it seems more natural to use a spanning set of
nilpotent
elements rather than idempotents. (In this case, the role of the parameter
\eta
becomes irrelevant.) Even so, just using nilpotents associated to the elements of
D
gives rise to highly uninteresting decompositions, because the multiplication by such a nilpotent turns out to be a nilpotent operator (and hence has
0
as its only eigenvalue).

We solve this by using nilpotent elements associated to
lines
in the Fischer space associated to the
3
-transposition group, or put differently, to triples of involutions contained in a common
\Sym(3)
-subgroup of
G
. Somewhat surprisingly, this does indeed give rise to interesting decompositions in many cases.

Perhaps the most interesting case is the smallest non-trivial case arising from the
3
-transposition group
\Sym(4)
with as generating set
D
the set of all
6
transpositions. In this case, we have only
4
lines, but the decompositions satisfy a fusion law stronger than a
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-grading and allows for the action of a multiplicative torus
\mathbb{G}_{m}
on each decomposition. Together, these four tori generate a group of the form
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{2}\rtimes\mathbb{G}_{m}
; see
Theorem

6.11
. We also compute the full automorphism group of this algebra in
Proposition

6.14
.

For Fischer spaces of
symplectic type
, we get (ordinary)
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-graded decompositions, as we show in
Theorem

5.24
.
If the Fischer space is not of symplectic type, then additional problems arise. In the smallest case, which is the affine plane of order
3
, associated to the
3
-transposition group
3^{2}:2
, we have to pass to the
generalized eigenspaces
(rather than the eigenspaces), which does not fit with the original definition of axial algebras from
[
HRS15
]
but still complies with the more general framework of
decomposition algebras
developed in
[
DMPSVC20
]
. In this specific case, the resulting decomposition algebra is again
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-graded; see
Theorem

5.17
.

However, as soon as the Fischer space is not of symplectic type and has rank
\geq 4
, then things break down and the resulting nilpotent Matsuo algebra is no longer
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-graded, as we show in
Theorem

5.27
below.

When we had almost finished writing this paper, we became aware of the surprising fact that in
[
CHPS12
]
, the authors study exactly the same algebras as we do, but only over the field
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. They had different goals than we do, so our results are almost completely disjoint from each other.

Definition 2.3
.

Let

The most interesting examples of decomposition algebras arise from fusion laws with a

Definition 2.5
.

(1)

Let

Then the map

.
The identity element of

.

(2)

Let
(X,*)

A

.

(3)

Let
(X,*)
be a fusion law and let

be a group fusion law.
A

.
In this paper, the grading group

The Jordan fusion law from

If the fusion law is graded by a non-trivial group, then we can construct an interesting subgroup of the automorphism group

.

Definition 2.6
.

(1)

Let

3.
Matsuo algebras

One of the first classes of examples of axial decomposition algebras with a Jordan fusion law that has been systematically studied, is the class of
Matsuo algebras
arising from
3
-transposition groups.
We recall their definition.

Definition 3.1
.

(1)

A
3-transposition group
is a pair
(G,D)
where
G
is a group and
D\subset G
is a conjugacy class of involutions in
G
such that
G=\langle D\rangle
and such that for all
d,e\in D
, the order
o(de)
of the element

is at most
3
.

(2)

Assume that
2
is invertible in
R
, and let
\eta\in R
be arbitrary. Let
A
be a free
R
-module with basis
D
.
We make
A
into an
R
-algebra by declaring

d\cdot e:=\begin{cases}d&\text{if }d=e,\\
0&\text{if }o(de)=2,\\
\tfrac{\eta}{2}(d+e-f)&\text{if }o(de)=3,\text{ where $f=ded=ede$}.\end{cases}

This algebra will be denoted by
M_{R}(G,D,\eta)
and will be called the
Matsuo algebra
(over
R
) of the
3
-transposition group
(G,D)
with parameter
\eta
.

The 3-transposition groups have a geometric counterpart known as
Fischer spaces
.

Definition 3.2
.

(1)

A

\mathcal{G}
is a pair

\mathcal{P}
is a set of

and

such that any

Two distinct points

are called

if they are contained in a (necessarily unique) common line, and we denote this by

.

(2)

A partial linear space is called a

if every line has exactly
3

In this case, for any two collinear points

, the unique third point on the line through

and

will be denoted by
x\wedge y
.

(3)

A non-empty subset

\mathcal{P}
is called a

if it is closed under the operation
\wedge
, and if

\mathcal{P}

containing

(4)

Assume now that
(G,D)

Such a partial triple system is called a

Remark 3.3

Sometimes, the defining set

In particular, a Fischer space of rank

It is often useful to go directly from the Fischer space to the Matsuo algebra.

Definition 3.8
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L})
be a Fischer space. Then we define the Matsuo algebra
M_{R}(\mathcal{G},\eta)
as the
R
-algebra with underlying free
R
-module with basis
\mathcal{P}
, with multiplication given by

x\cdot y:=\begin{cases}x&\text{if }x=y,\\

\tfrac{\eta}{2}(x+y-x\wedge y)&\text{if }x\sim y.\end{cases}

Matsuo algebras are examples of axial decomposition algebras, with all elements of the basis
\mathcal{P}
as axes.
More precisely, for each

, there is a corresponding decomposition of

into three parts, with parameters

,

\ad_{x}
.

4.
Axial algebras in characteristic 2

In the original definition of axial algebras as in
[
HRS15
]
, the axes were always assumed to be
idempotent
elements, and this is also how the Matsuo algebras had originally been studied.
In terms of the defining parameters in
Eq.

2.4
, this corresponds to the assumption that
\lambda_{e}=1
.
Often, however, this is an unnecessary and sometimes unwanted restriction; in fact, if
2=0
in
R
, this essentially excludes interesting decomposition algebras with a
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-grading, as the following observation shows.
Recall that all algebras are assumed to be commutative.

Proposition 4.1
.

Assume that
2=0
in
R
and let
A
be an axial decomposition algebra over
R
with respect to a
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-graded fusion law
(X,*)
such that all axes are idempotents.
Then all axes are contained in the
zero-core
, i.e., the intersection of the
0
-graded part of all of the decompositions.

In particular, the algebra
A
is not generated by its axes unless all its
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-decompositions are trivial.

Proof.

Let
a,b\in A
be two axes, and decompose
b
with respect to the
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-decomposition corresponding to
a
, as
b=b_{0}+b_{1}
, with
b_{0}\in A_{0}^{a}
and
b_{1}\in A_{1}^{a}
.
Then, since
2=0
, we have

b=b^{2}=(b_{0}+b_{1})^{2}=b_{0}^{2}+b_{1}^{2}\in A_{0}^{a}

because both
A_{0}^{a}\cdot A_{0}^{a}\subseteq A_{0}^{a}
and
A_{1}^{a}\cdot A_{1}^{a}\subseteq A_{0}^{a}
.
∎

Because of
Proposition

4.1
, we will, instead, assume that our axes are
nilpotent
elements. We point out that this also makes sense for the deeper reason that the origin of the theory of axial algebras (and Matsuo algebras in particular) is found in the theory of VOAs, where an important role is played by elements
e
for which
e^{2}=2e
(rather than
e^{2}=e
). This seems like an innocent rescaling but further illustrates that over arbitrary rings, the assumption
e^{2}=2e
is more natural than
e^{2}=e
and naturally leads to nilpotent elements in the case that
2=0
.

5.
Nilpotent Matsuo algebras

Assume, from now on, that
2=0
in
R
.
In view of the previous section, the following definition is natural.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L})
be a Fischer space. Assume that
2=0
in
R
and let
\eta\in R
with
\eta\neq 0
.
Then we define the
nilpotent Matsuo algebra

M_{R}(\mathcal{G},\eta)
as the
R
-algebra with underlying free
R
-module with basis
\mathcal{P}
, with multiplication given by

x\cdot y:=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }x=y\text{ or }x\not\sim y,\\
\eta(x+y+x\wedge y)&\text{if }x\sim y.\end{cases}

Remark 5.2
.

(1)

For each line
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
, the product of two distinct points on
\ell
is independent of the choice of these points; it is always equal to
\eta
times the sum of these three points.

(2)

The role of the parameter
\eta
is negligible. In fact, if
R
is a field, then we can simply replace each point
x
by
\eta^{-1}x
and get the same multiplication on
A
with
\eta
replaced by
1
.

From now on, we keep the assumptions that
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L})
is a Fischer space, that
2=0
in
R
and that
A=M_{R}(\mathcal{G},1)
is the corresponding nilpotent Matsuo algebra (so
\eta=1
).

A natural attempt now is to construct decompositions corresponding to the elements of the basis
\mathcal{P}
. The algebra
A
no longer decomposes into eigenspaces for
\ad_{x}
, but even passing to generalized eigenspaces does not work, because of the following observation.

Proposition 5.3
.

For each
x\in\mathcal{P}
, the operator
\ad_{x}
is nilpotent. More precisely, we have
\ad_{x}^{2}=0
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
x\cdot xy=0
for each
y\in\mathcal{P}
.
If
x=y
or
x\not\sim y
, this is obvious, so assume that
x\sim y
.
Then
x\cdot xy=x(x+y+x\wedge y)=xy+x(x\wedge y)
.
Since
xy=x(x\wedge y)
by
Remark

5.2
Item

1
, the result follows.
∎

However, not only the points
x\in\mathcal{P}
are nilpotent, but in fact, every single element in
A
has square
0
. It turns out that the
line nilpotents
are a better candidate to obtain decompositions of the algebra
A
.

Definition 5.4
.

For each line
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
, we let
s_{\ell}\in A
be the sum of the three points on
\ell
, and we call this a

of
A
.
In fact, we will simply write

for

; this will not cause confusion.

We first collect some easy computations that we will use repeatedly.
Recall the two examples from
3.5
.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
and
x\in\mathcal{P}
.

(1)

If
x\in\ell
or if

is not collinear to any point of

, then
x\ell=0
in
A
.

(2)

If the subspace spanned by

and

; in this case,
x\ell=m+n

. We also have
x\ell=\ell+\ell^{\prime}

\ell^{\prime}
is the unique line in this subspace different from

and not containing

.

(3)

If the subspace spanned by

and
\ell
is an affine plane, then there is a unique line

in this subspace parallel to
\ell
and not containing

; in this case,
x\ell=x+\ell+m
in

.

Proof.

This is straightforward.
∎

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
\ell,m\in\mathcal{L}
.

(1)

If
\ell=m
, then
\ell m=0
in

.

(2)

If
\ell\cap m=\{x\}
and they span a complete quadrilateral, then
\ell m=\ell+m
in

.

(3)

If
\ell\cap m=\{x\}
and they span an affine plane, then
\ell m=a+b+c+d

, where

are the four points in this subspace not on

.

(4)

If
\ell\cap m=\emptyset

\ell m=s

, where

Proof.

This is straightforward.

Nilpotent Matsuo algebras are always somewhat degenerate, as they contain annihilating elements.

Definition 5.7
.

An element
a\in A
is
annihilating
if
ab=0
for all
b\in A
. We write

\Ann(A):=\{a\in A\mid a\text{ is annihilating}\}.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
s
be the sum of all points in
\mathcal{P}
.
Then
\Ann(A)=\langle s\rangle
.

Proof.

To show that
s\in\Ann(A)
, it suffices to show that
sx=0
for all
x\in\mathcal{P}
. Let
\mathcal{L}_{x}
be the set of lines containing

5.4.

Finally, we show that when the Fischer space

is not of symplectic type, then the corresponding nilpotent Matsuo algebra has a decomposition that is no longer

-graded.
It suffices to go through the list of possible Fischer spaces of rank

It will turn out that in each case, the relation

We first recall the classification of Fischer spaces of rank

Let

Remark 5.28

Interestingly, it can happen that for

*

0

\{0\}

\{1\}

1

\{1\}

Notice that the map from

In particular, the algebras of Theorem

Observe that the

Definition 6.1
.

Let
(A,\mathcal{I},\Omega)
be a
\mathbb{Z}
-decomposition algebra.
For

and

\tau_{i,\lambda}(a)=\lambda^{k}a\quad\text{for all }a\in A^{i}_{k}.

Remark 6.2

If

and
\ell
is a line of
\mathcal{G}
such that
A
decomposes into eigenspaces
A=A_{0}^{\ell}\oplus A_{1}^{\ell}

\tau_{\ell,\lambda}=\mathrm{id}+(1+\lambda)\ad_{\ell}.

Let
(A,\mathcal{I},\Omega)
be a
\mathbb{Z}
-decomposition algebra. For each fixed

T_{i}(R):=\langle\tau_{i,\lambda}\mid\lambda\in R^{\times}\rangle\cong R^{\times}

of automorphisms, so it makes sense to investigate the corresponding Miyamoto groups as

, rather than as abstract ones (at least when

is finite-dimensional over a field).
Following
[

]
, we will view algebraic groups as functors from the category of commutative

-algebras to the category of groups.

Definition 6.3
.

Fix a base field

and let
(A,\mathcal{I},\Omega)
be a
\mathbb{Z}
-decomposition algebra, with

of

as the algebraic group over

\Miy(A,\mathcal{I},\Omega):=\langle T_{i}\mid i\in\mathcal{I}\rangle\leq\Aut(A).

Remark 6.4
.

Recall that, by definition, this is the smallest

subgroup of
\Aut(A)
containing these tori

. In particular, it will
not
be true in general that the group of
R
-points
\Miy(A,\mathcal{I},\Omega)(R)
is generated by the groups

for all
k
-algebras

. We will see this explicitly in

From now on, let

be the

.

(1)

Lemma 6.9
.

Consider the algebraic group
G=\mathbb{G}_{a}^{2}\rtimes\mathbb{G}_{m}
, where the (left) action of
\mathbb{G}_{m}
on
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{2}
is given by multiplication.
Then the set of matrices

\{S_{\alpha,\beta,\lambda}\mid\alpha,\beta\in R,\lambda\in R^{\times}\}

forms a group under matrix multiplication, isomorphic to

.

Proof.

It is straightforward to check that

S_{\alpha,\beta,\lambda}\cdot S_{\gamma,\delta,\mu}=S_{\alpha+\lambda\gamma,\,\beta+\lambda\delta,\,\lambda\mu}

for all
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\in R
and
\lambda,\mu\in R^{\times}
. In particular,

\displaystyle\{S_{\alpha,\beta,1}\mid\alpha,\beta\in R\}\cong R^{2},

\displaystyle\{S_{0,0,\lambda}\mid\lambda\in R^{\times}\}\cong R^{\times},

and the conjugation action of the second subgroup on the first is given by

S_{0,0,\lambda}\cdot S_{\alpha,\beta,1}\cdot S_{0,0,\lambda}^{-1}=S_{\lambda\alpha,\lambda\beta,1}.\qed

This follows from

Theorem 6.11
.

We have
\Miy(A,\mathcal{I},\Omega)\cong\Miy(A^{\prime},\mathcal{I},\Omega)\cong\mathbb{G}_{a}^{2}\rtimes\mathbb{G}_{m}
, where the action of
\mathbb{G}_{m}
on
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{2}
is given by multiplication.

Proof.

Let
G=\mathbb{G}_{a}^{2}\rtimes\mathbb{G}_{m}
. For each
k
-algebra
R
, let

H(R)=\{S_{\alpha,\beta,\lambda}\mid\alpha,\beta\in R,\lambda\in R^{\times}\}\cong G(R)

as in
Lemma

6.9
.
By
Lemma

6.10
, we already have
\Miy(A,\mathcal{I},\Omega)\leq H
, and to show equality, it only remains to show that the algebraic group
T
generated by the four tori

\displaystyle T_{1}(R)

\displaystyle:=\langle S_{0,\,0,\,\lambda}\mid\lambda\in R^{\times}\rangle,

\displaystyle T_{2}(R)

\displaystyle:=\langle S_{1+\lambda,\,0,\,\lambda}\mid\lambda\in R^{\times}\rangle,

\displaystyle T_{3}(R)

\displaystyle:=\langle S_{0,\,1+\lambda,\,\lambda}\mid\lambda\in R^{\times}\rangle,

\displaystyle T_{4}(R)

\displaystyle:=\langle S_{1+\lambda,\,1+\lambda,\,\lambda}\mid\lambda\in R^{\times}\rangle,

coincides with the whole group
H
.
Observe that
S_{\alpha,\beta,\lambda}\cdot S_{0,0,\lambda^{-1}}=S_{\alpha,\beta,1}
for all
\alpha,\beta\in R,\lambda\in R^{\times}
, so we already have
S_{1+\lambda,0,1}\in T(R)
and
S_{0,1+\lambda,1}\in T(R)
for all
\lambda\in R^{\times}
.
For

, the separable closure of

, so together with the

for all

, we get the whole group
\{S_{\alpha,\beta,\lambda}\mid\alpha,\beta\in k^{s},\lambda\in(k^{s})^{\times}\}=H(k^{s})
, so
T(k^{s})=H(k^{s})
.
Since
T\leq H
and
H\cong G
is smooth, this implies that
T=H
as algebraic groups.

It remains to show that
\Miy(A,\mathcal{I},\Omega)\cong\Miy(A^{\prime},\mathcal{I},\Omega)
. Notice that each Miyamoto map of
(A,\mathcal{I},\Omega)
fixes
\Ann(A)
elementwise. Since
A^{\prime}=A/\Ann(A)
, we get an epimorphism

\rho\colon\Miy(A,\mathcal{I},\Omega)\twoheadrightarrow\Miy(A^{\prime},\mathcal{I},\Omega).

With respect to the basis
\mathcal{B}
for
A
as in
Eq.

6.6
and the corresponding basis

(6.12)

\mathcal{B}^{\prime}=(a,\ b,\ \ell,\ \ell x,\ \ell y)

for
A^{\prime}
, this map is simply given by canceling the last row and the last column of the matrix.
Since all matrices
S_{\alpha,\beta,\lambda}
have the same last row and last column, this implies that
\rho
is injective, and hence an isomorphism.
∎

Remark 6.13
.

As indicated in
Remark

6.4
, it is not true that
\langle T_{i}(R)\rangle_{i=1,2,3,4}\cong(\mathbb{G}_{a}^{2}\rtimes\mathbb{G}_{m})(R)
for all
k
-algebras
R
.
For instance, take
R=k[x]
, the algebra of polynomials over
k
. Then
T_{i}(R)=R^{\times}=k^{\times}
, so that the group generated by the
T_{i}(R)
is
k^{2}\rtimes k^{\times}\not\cong R^{2}\rtimes R^{\times}
.

To finish this section, we point out that the full automorphism groups
\Aut(A)
and
\Aut(A^{\prime})
are substantially larger than the Miyamoto groups.

Proposition 6.14
.

We have isomorphisms as algebraic groups

(1)

\Aut(A^{\prime})\cong\mathbb{G}_{a}^{2}\rtimes\GL_{2}
and

(2)

\Aut(A)\cong\mathbb{G}_{a}^{2}\rtimes(\Aut(A^{\prime})\times\mathbb{G}_{m})
, where
\GL_{2}\leq\Aut(A^{\prime})
acts “quadratically” and
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{2}\leq\Aut(A^{\prime})
acts trivially on the first copy of
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{2}
, see below.

Proof.

We will continue to use the bases
\mathcal{B}
for
A
and
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
for
A^{\prime}
given in (
6.6
) and (
6.12
).
It will be convenient to use the multiplication table from page
6
.

(1)

Let
\theta\in\Aut(A^{\prime})
be arbitrary.
By
Remark

6.7
, its matrix representation with respect to
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
must be of the form

\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{1}&\beta_{1}&0&0&0\\
\alpha_{2}&\beta_{2}&0&0&0\\
\alpha_{3}&\beta_{3}&\gamma_{3}&0&0\\
\alpha_{4}&\beta_{4}&\gamma_{4}&\delta_{4}&\eta_{4}\\
\alpha_{5}&\beta_{5}&\gamma_{5}&\delta_{5}&\eta_{5}\end{pmatrix}.

Since this matrix is invertible, so is its submatrix
\begin{pmatrix}\delta_{4}&\eta_{4}\\
\delta_{5}&\eta_{5}\end{pmatrix}
.
The equality
\theta(\ell)\cdot\theta(\ell x)=\theta(\ell x)\neq 0
then implies
\gamma_{3}=1
.
From
\theta(a)\theta(\ell x)=0
and
\theta(a)\theta(\ell y)=\theta(\ell x)
, we get

\begin{pmatrix}\delta_{4}&\delta_{5}\\
\eta_{4}&\eta_{5}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{3}\\
\alpha_{1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\delta_{4}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\begin{pmatrix}\delta_{4}&\delta_{5}\\
\eta_{4}&\eta_{5}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{2}\\
\alpha_{3}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\delta_{5}\end{pmatrix}.

In particular,
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}
are uniquely determined by
\delta_{4},\delta_{5},\eta_{4},\eta_{5}
,
and a similar argument holds for
\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3}
.
Next, using
\theta(a)\theta(\ell)=0
, we get

\alpha_{4}=\alpha_{1}\gamma_{5}+\alpha_{3}\gamma_{4}\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha_{5}=\alpha_{2}\gamma_{4}+\alpha_{3}\gamma_{5},

so
\alpha_{4}
and
\alpha_{5}
are uniquely determined by the
\gamma
’s,
\delta
’s and
\epsilon
’s. The same holds for
\beta_{4}
and
\beta_{5}
, so we conclude that the restriction morphism

\rho\colon\Aut(A^{\prime})\to\Aut(\langle\ell,\ell x,\ell y\rangle)\leq\GL_{3}

is injective.
Moreover, by checking all remaining relations
\theta(e_{1})\cdot\theta(e_{2})=\theta(e_{1}\cdot e_{2})
, for
e_{1},e_{2}\in\{a,b,\ell,\ell x,\ell y\}
, we see that
\im(\rho)\leq\GL_{3}
consists of
all
invertible matrices of the form

\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
\gamma_{4}&\delta_{4}&\eta_{4}\\
\gamma_{5}&\delta_{5}&\eta_{5}\end{pmatrix}.

It is now straightforward to check that
\im(\rho)\cong\mathbb{G}_{a}^{2}\rtimes\GL_{2}
.

(2)

Notice from the multiplication table that
A_{1}=\langle a,b,\ell,\ell x,\ell y\rangle
is a subalgebra of
A
, isomorphic to the quotient
A^{\prime}=A/\langle s\rangle
. Moreover, each automorphism of
A_{1}
extends to a unique automorphism of
A
fixing
s
, so
\Aut(A^{\prime})
is isomorphic to a subgroup of
\Aut(A)
.
Keeping in mind that the subalgebra
\langle\ell,\ell x,\ell y\rangle
is stabilized by each automorphism, it follows that an arbitrary
\theta\in\Aut(A)
has matrix form

\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc|c}&&&&&0\\
&&&&&0\\
&&\Theta&&&0\\
&&&&&0\\
&&&&&0\\
\hline\cr\kappa&\lambda&0&0&0&\nu\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc|c}\alpha_{1}&\beta_{1}&0&0&0&0\\
\alpha_{2}&\beta_{2}&0&0&0&0\\
\alpha_{3}&\beta_{3}&1&0&0&0\\
\alpha_{4}&\beta_{4}&\gamma_{4}&\delta_{4}&\eta_{4}&0\\
\alpha_{5}&\beta_{5}&\gamma_{5}&\delta_{5}&\eta_{5}&0\\
\hline\cr\kappa&\lambda&0&0&0&\nu\end{array}\right)

for some
\Theta\in\Aut(A_{1})
and some
\kappa,\lambda\in R
and
\nu\in R^{\times}
.
Hence
\Aut(A)\cong\mathbb{G}_{a}^{2}\rtimes(\Aut(A_{1})\times\mathbb{G}_{m})
, where the action can be determined by multiplying two such matrices.
Clearly, the
\mathbb{G}_{m}
acts on
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{2}
by multiplication.
To determine the action of
\Aut(A_{1})
on
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{2}
, we see that the action is given by right multiplication with the upper left
(2\times 2)
-block

\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{1}&\beta_{1}\\
\alpha_{2}&\beta_{2}\end{pmatrix}
.
Using the equations from part (i), we see that this block can be rewritten as

\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{1}&\beta_{1}\\
\alpha_{2}&\beta_{2}\end{pmatrix}=r\begin{pmatrix}\delta_{4}^{2}&\eta_{4}^{2}\\
\delta_{5}^{2}&\eta_{5}^{2}\end{pmatrix},

where
r
is precisely the inverse of the determinant of the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}\delta_{4}&\eta_{4}\\
\delta_{5}&\eta_{5}\end{pmatrix}
,
so that this block

\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{1}&\beta_{1}\\
\alpha_{2}&\beta_{2}\end{pmatrix}

has determinant
1
.
In other words, the action of
\GL_{2}
on
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{2}
is given by first applying the group homomorphism

\GL_{2}\to\SL_{2}\colon M=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\gamma&\delta\end{pmatrix}\mapsto(\det M)^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha^{2}&\beta^{2}\\
\gamma^{2}&\delta^{2}\end{pmatrix}

and then applying right multiplication on
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{2}
.
∎

Remark 6.15
.

It is natural to ask whether we can use the
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-gradings of the other examples to produce interesting Miyamoto groups. The usual definition of the Miyamoto maps as in
Definition

2.6
seems less useful (for instance, they are trivial if the base ring
R
has no nilpotent elements). A possible solution is to consider the automorphism group
\Aut(A)
as an algebraic group in characteristic
2
and define Miyamoto maps as elements of a copy of the algebraic group
\mu_{2}
, which is not smooth in characteristic
2
. The groups generated by these Miyamoto maps is then an algebraic subgroup of
\Aut(A)
.
We have tried to perform this computation for the symplectic Fischer space associated to
\Sym(5)
, but we have not been able to find a meaningful description of the resulting Miyamoto group.
