QCD Thermodynamics and Neutral Pion in a Uniform Magnetic Field: Finite Volume Effects
Prabal Adhikari, Brian C. Tiburzi

Abstract
We address finite volume effects of lattice QCD calculations in background magnetic fields. Using chiral perturbation theory at next-to-leading order, volume effects are calculated for thermodynamic quantities: the chiral condensate, pressure anisotropy, and magnetization. The neutral pion effective action in a finite volume is additionally derived. For these charge neutral observables, volume and source averaging are shown to capitalize on magnetic periodicity, which is the remnant translational invariance of the finite-volume theory. For a fixed magnetic field strength, certain volume and source averaged quantities are independent of the size of the lattice transverse to the magnetic field. Despite this simplifying feature, finite volume corrections to the magnetic field dependence of the chiral condensate and neutral pion magnetic polarizability can be non-negligible. The pressure anisotropy at fixed magnetic flux, moreover, appears acutely sensitive to the lattice volume.

In this work,
we study finite volume effects on lattice QCD observables in a homogeneous, background magnetic field.
The Landau levels of charged particles in magnetic fields exhibit infinite degeneracy due to translational invariance.
When the volume is finite,
however,
only a remnant of the continuous translational invariance remains.
We explore the consequences of this magnetic periodicity on QCD observables using chiral perturbation theory,
in which the dominant corrections arise from finite volume modifications to charged pion loops.
Even for charge neutral quantities,
magnetic periodicity produces coordinate dependence,

e.g.

the chiral condensate varies in the plane transverse to the magnetic field,
and the neutral pion experiences a transverse coordinate-dependent potential.
Volume averaging
(for bulk thermodynamic quantities)
and source averaging
(for the neutral pion two-point function)
are shown to remove certain transverse area effects at a fixed value of the magnetic field.
Despite this simplification,
finite volume effects on the magnetic field dependence of observables can be non-negligible.
We find this to be the case of the chiral condensate and neutral pion magnetic polarizability.
In the case of the pressure anisotropy at fixed magnetic flux,
moreover,
we find a substantial finite volume effect for the lowest flux quanta,
even at

m_{\pi}L=4

II
Finite-Volume Green’s Function in a Magnetic Field

The finite-volume Green’s function of the charged pion in a magnetic field is central to the chiral perturbation theory calculations in subsequent sections.
In this section,
the magnetic periodic boundary conditions required of the Green’s function are first reviewed.
Our treatment follows the quantum mechanical finite-volume problem detailed in
Ref.
Al-Hashimi and Wiese 2009
.
The charged pion propagator is then explicitly constructed using the method of magnetic periodic
images
Tiburzi 2014
.
The coincident propagator (namely that from a point back to itself)
is shown to depend on coordinates transverse to the magnetic field,
but maintains the remnant translational invariance of the finite-volume theory.

II.1
Boundary Conditions

We work in Euclidean spacetime characterized by the finite-size parameters

1

The Green’s function for a charged particle depends on the gauge chosen for

Observables computed in this work,

are all charge neutral.
Thus,

For example,
the one-loop corrections with tadpole topology involve the coincident charged pion propagator.
Such one-loop corrections are necessarily gauge invariant;
but,

Wilson lines that wrap the compact dimensions are also gauge invariant quantities.

On a torus,

{\mathcal{A}}_{\mu}(x)

is periodic up to a gauge transformation

{\mathcal{A}}_{\mu}(x+L_{2}\hat{x}_{2})={\mathcal{A}}_{\mu}(x)+\partial_{\mu}{\Lambda}_{2}(x),

(2)

where

{\Lambda}_{2}(x)=-BL_{2}x_{1}
.
The constant gauge potential

B_{\mu}

can only be gauged away in infinite volume.
Note that we have set

B_{0}=B_{3}=0

for simplicity.

For a complex scalar field

\varphi

of charge

Q
(which we identify with the charged pion starting in Sec.
II.2
),
we write

{\mathcal{D}}_{\mu}\varphi=\left(\partial_{\mu}+iQ{\mathcal{A}}_{\mu}\right)\varphi
.
This derivative will be a gauge covariant derivative provided the field transforms as

\varphi\to e^{-iQ{\Lambda}}\varphi

under the gauge transformation

{\mathcal{A}}_{\mu}\to{\mathcal{A}}_{\mu}+\partial_{\mu}{\Lambda}
.
A periodic

\varphi

field coupled to

{\mathcal{A}}_{\mu}

includes extra effects due to the finite volume.
These are best removed from

{\mathcal{A}}_{\mu}

at the cost of modifying the boundary conditions on the scalar field.
The new field

\phi(x)\equiv e^{-iQB_{\mu}x_{\mu}}\varphi(x)

removes

B_{\mu}

from the action,
but leads to twisted boundary conditions
of the form

\phi(x+L_{\mu}\hat{x}_{\mu})=e^{-iQ\theta_{\mu}}\phi(x)
,
where Einstein’s summation convention is suspended in writing this and any subsequent boundary conditions.
Addressing the effect of non-periodicity of the gauge potential,
moreover,
requires a gauge transformation at the boundary;
after which,
there is an additional factor

\phi(x+L_{\mu}\hat{x}_{\mu})=e^{-iQ\theta_{\mu}}e^{-iQ{\Lambda}_{\mu}(x)}\phi(x),

(3)

leading to magnetic periodic boundary conditions with a twist.
It is best to write the phase acquired by wrapping the torus as a Wilson line

W_{2}(x_{1})=e^{-iQ\theta_{2}}e^{-iQ{\Lambda}_{2}(x)}=e^{-iQ(\theta_{2}-x_{1}BL_{2})}.

(4)

For convenience,
we define another Wilson line

W_{1}(x_{2})=e^{-iQ(\theta_{1}+x_{2}BL_{1})},

(5)

for later use.

Consistency of the boundary conditions requires quantization of the magnetic field
’t Hooft 1979
.
On the one hand,
we can write

\displaystyle\phi(x+L_{1}\hat{x}_{1}+L_{2}\hat{x}_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-iQ\theta_{1}}\phi(x+L_{2}\hat{x}_{2})

(6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-iQ\theta_{1}}W_{2}(x_{1})\phi(x),

while on the other,
we see

\displaystyle\phi(x+L_{1}\hat{x}_{1}+L_{2}\hat{x}_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W_{2}(x_{1}+L_{1})\phi(x+L_{1}\hat{x}_{1})

(7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-iQ\theta_{1}}W_{2}(x_{1}+L_{1})\phi(x).

These are equivalent boundary conditions provided

e^{-iQL_{1}L_{2}B}=1\quad\longrightarrow\quad QB=\frac{2\pi N_{\Phi}}{L_{1}L_{2}}.

(8)

The magnetic field strength is thus determined in terms of the integer flux quantum

N_{\Phi}
.
The flux quantum determines the size of the remnant translational invariance group,
which is

\mathbb{Z}_{N_{\Phi}}

Al-Hashimi and Wiese 2009
.
For

n\in\mathbb{Z}_{N_{\Phi}}
,
we see this invariance manifested in the translational properties of the Wilson lines

\displaystyle W_{1}\left(x_{2}+\tfrac{n}{N_{\Phi}}L_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W_{1}(x_{2}),

\displaystyle W_{2}\left(x_{1}+\tfrac{n}{N_{\Phi}}L_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W_{2}(x_{1}).

(9)

This translational invariance is
in addition to periodicity,
under which we have

W_{1}(x_{2}+nL_{2})=W_{1}(x_{2})

and

W_{2}(x_{1}+nL_{1})=W_{2}(x_{1})
,
for all

n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

II.2
Charged Pion Propagator

Identifying the complex scalar field with the charged pion

\phi(x)=\pi^{+}(x)
,
the propagator for the charged scalar takes the form

\displaystyle G_{+}(x^{\prime},x)\equiv\big\langle\pi^{+}(x^{\prime})\pi^{-}(x)\,\big\rangle,

(10)

where the angled brackets denote vacuum expectation values.
Adopting a quantum mechanical notation
Schwinger 1951
,
we have

\displaystyle G_{+}(x^{\prime},x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle x^{\prime}|\frac{1}{-D_{\mu}D_{\mu}+m^{2}}|x\rangle,

(11)

where the covariant derivative

D_{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}-iQA_{\mu}

acts on the negatively charged pion,
and

m

is the tree-level pion mass.
As such,

\left(-D_{\mu}D_{\mu}+m^{2}\right)G_{+}(x^{\prime},x)=\prod_{\mu=0}^{3}\delta_{L_{\mu}}(x^{\prime}_{\mu}-x_{\mu}),

x_{\mu}\in[\,0,L_{\mu})

{\mu}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\,e^{-sm^{2}}

(13)

\perp

\parallel

The latter directions give rise to a contribution to the proper-time integral having the form

\displaystyle G_{\parallel}(x^{\prime}_{\parallel},x_{\parallel}|s)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\langle x^{\prime}_{\parallel}|\,e^{\partial_{\parallel}^{2}s}\,|x_{\parallel}\rangle=\frac{1}{4\pi s}\sum_{\nu_{\parallel}}e^{-\frac{\Delta x_{\parallel}^{2}}{4s}},

(14)

\mu

The sum over

-directions,

\displaystyle G_{\perp}(x^{\prime}_{\perp}+L_{2}\hat{x}_{2},x_{\perp}|s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W_{2}(x^{\prime}_{1})G_{\perp}(x^{\prime}_{\perp},x_{\perp}|s),

\displaystyle G_{\perp}(x^{\prime}_{\perp},x_{\perp}+L_{1}\hat{x}_{1}|s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{+iQ\theta_{1}}G_{\perp}(x^{\prime}_{\perp},x_{\perp}|s),

\displaystyle G_{\perp}(x^{\prime}_{\perp},x_{\perp}+L_{2}\hat{x}_{2}|s)

II.3
Coincident Propagator

In the calculations that follow
(the chiral condensate, free energy, and derivative-free tadpole contributions to the neutral pion effective action),
the charged pion propagator from a point back to itself is required.
Due to the remnant translational invariance,
such coincident contributions are not coordinate independent.
Instead,
they depend on the transverse coordinates,
but maintain

\mathbb{Z}_{N_{\Phi}}

translational invariance.

The charged pion propagator from a point

x_{\mu}

back to itself is straightforward to evaluate
using Eq. (
13
).
Not surprisingly,
it can be expressed in terms of a sum over images

\displaystyle G_{+}(x,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{{\nu}_{\mu}}f({\nu}_{\perp},x_{\perp})\,g_{+}({\nu}_{\mu}),

(21)

where the coordinate dependence appears in the phase function

\displaystyle f({\nu}_{\perp},x_{\perp})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{N_{\Phi}{\nu}_{1}{\nu}_{2}}\left[W_{1}^{*}(x_{2})\right]^{{\nu}_{1}}\left[W_{2}^{*}(x_{1})\right]^{{\nu}_{2}}.\,\,\,

(22)

Notice that each transverse image appearing in the sum is accompanied by the corresponding Wilson lines through the function

f(\nu_{\perp},x_{\perp})
,
and maintains

\mathbb{Z}_{N_{\Phi}}

translational invariance via Eq. (
9
).
Each image,
moreover,
has a sign determined by

(-1)^{N_{\Phi}{\nu}_{1}{\nu}_{2}}
,
which can be attributed to a finite volume Aharonov-Bohm effect
Tiburzi 2014
.
The remaining image dependence appears under the coordinate-independent, proper-time integral

\displaystyle g_{+}({\nu}_{\mu}){=}\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\frac{e^{-sm^{2}}}{(4\pi s)^{2}}\frac{QBs}{\sinh QBs}e^{-\frac{({\nu}_{\parallel}L_{\parallel})^{2}}{4s}-\frac{QB({\nu}_{\perp}L_{\perp})^{2}}{4\tanh QBs}}.

(23)

The proper-time integral for the term with

{\nu}_{\mu}=(0,0,0,0)\equiv 0_{\mu}

is divergent in the ultraviolet

s\ll 1
,
however,
this is the only term that survives the infinite-volume limit.
The ultraviolet behavior of the infinite-volume integrand,
moreover,
is independent of the magnetic field.
To regulate the divergence,
we introduce a proper-time cutoff

s_{0}\ll 1

into the offending term

g_{+}(0_{\mu})\equiv\int_{s_{0}}^{\infty}ds\,\frac{e^{-sm^{2}}}{(4\pi s)^{2}}\frac{QBs}{\sinh QBs}.

(24)

Accordingly,
the one-loop calculations can be renormalized by a magnetic field independent, infinite-volume subtraction of the form

g_{+}(0_{\mu})\to g^{\infty}_{+}=\lim_{s_{0}\to 0}\left[g_{+}(0_{\mu})-\int_{s_{0}}^{\infty}ds\,\frac{e^{-sm^{2}}}{(4\pi s)^{2}}\right],

(25)

where the subtracted term arises from renormalizing the magnetic field independent parameters of the infinite-volume theory.
3
3

3

It is instructive to write out the term introduced for the subtraction.
Removing the multiplicative factor of

(4{\pi})^{-2}
,
we have

\int_{s_{0}}^{\infty}ds\,\frac{e^{-sm^{2}}}{s^{2}}=\frac{1}{s_{0}}+m^{2}\left[\log(s_{0}\,m^{2})+{\gamma}_{E}-1\right]+{\mathcal{O}}(s_{0}).

The divergence is absorbed by a

-\frac{1}{s_{0}}

counterterm that is pion mass independent.
The
s_{0}
-dependence of the chiral logarithm is compensated by the contribution from a low-energy constant.
Schematically,
we write such a contribution to the above as

m^{2}\,\ell(s_{0})
.
The low-energy constant then satisfies the renormalization group equation

s_{0}\frac{d}{ds_{0}}\ell(s_{0})=-1

to keep the net result

s_{0}
-independent.
As our concern is with the magnetic field and finite volume dependence of observables,
we renormalize the chiral corrections into the parameters, so that they take their physical values
in the combined zero-field and infinite-volume limit.

The behavior with respect to the magnetic field and finite volume is hence ultraviolet finite.

The function

g^{\infty}_{+}

describes the magnetic field dependence of the coincident propagator in the infinite-volume limit.
The proper-time cutoff can be removed

s_{0}\to 0
,
leading to

g^{\infty}_{+}=\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\,\frac{e^{-sm^{2}}}{(4\pi s)^{2}}\left[\frac{QBs}{\sinh QBs}{-}\,1\right]\equiv\frac{QB}{(4{\pi})^{2}}\,{\mathcal{I}}\left(\frac{m^{2}}{QB}\right).

(26)

The required Laplace transform

{\mathcal{I}}({\alpha})

is defined to be dimensionless.
It is related to the Hurwitz zeta-function,
and can be evaluated in terms of the digamma function
Cohen et al. 2007

{\mathcal{I}}({\alpha})={\alpha}(1-\log\tfrac{{\alpha}}{2})+2\log\Gamma(1+{\alpha})-\log 2{\pi}.

(27)

With the infinite-volume limit removed,
the coincident propagator in
Eq. (
21
)
simply becomes

\displaystyle{\overline{G}}_{+}(x,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{{\nu}_{\mu}\neq\,0_{\mu}}f({\nu}_{\perp},x_{\perp})\,g_{+}({\nu}_{\mu}),

(28)

and includes effects of finite volume,
both with and without the magnetic field.
In what follows,
the sum over images is useful for analytic manipulations,
however,
a more economical expression is needed for numerical evaluations.
For this purpose,
the image sums can be expressed in terms of
Jacobi elliptic-theta functions,
resulting in the formula

\displaystyle{\overline{G}}_{+}(x,x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\,\frac{e^{-sm^{2}}}{(4{\pi}s)^{2}}\frac{QBs}{\sinh QBs}\Big[\Theta(x_{\perp}|s){-}\,1\Big].

(29)

The expression for

\Theta(x_{\perp}|s)

is somewhat lengthy and
appears in
App.
A
.
In infinite volume,
we have the limit

\Theta(x_{\perp}|s)\to 1
;
and,
this finite-volume effect appropriately vanishes.

One-loop corrections in chiral perturbation theory also arise from neutral pion contributions.
While these contributions do not produce magnetic field dependence at the order we work,
they lead to volume dependence,
which we discuss for completeness.
The neutral pion propagator is simply the charged pion propagator evaluated at

Q=0
.
Using the expression from above,
we have the coincident, neutral pion propagator

G_{0}(0,0)\equiv G_{0}(x,x)=\sum_{{\nu}_{\mu}}g_{0}({\nu}_{\mu}),

(30)

where the contribution from a periodic image

{\nu}_{\mu}

is

\displaystyle g_{0}({\nu}_{\mu})=\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\,\frac{e^{-sm^{2}}}{(4\pi s)^{2}}\,e^{-\frac{({\nu}_{\mu}L_{\mu})^{2}}{4s}}.

(31)

This result is translationally invariant,
for which we employ the notation

G_{0}(0,0)
.

Using the infinite-volume renormalization scheme,
one has

{\overline{g}}_{0}(0_{\mu})=0

and the sum over all

{\nu}_{\mu}

is replaced by a sum over

{\nu}_{\mu}\neq 0_{\mu}
,
for which the infinite-volume limit accordingly vanishes.
The renormalized coincident propagator

{\overline{G}}_{0}(0,0)

can be economically written in terms of Jacobi elliptic-theta functions

\displaystyle{\overline{G}}_{0}(0,0)=\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\,\frac{e^{-sm^{2}}}{(4\pi s)^{2}}\Big[\Theta_{0}(s)-1\Big],

(32)

where

\Theta_{0}(s)

is defined in
Eq. (
103
).
The difference of

{\overline{G}}_{0}(0,0)

compared to

G_{0}(0,0)

amounts to the subtraction of unity in the proper-time integral.
This subtraction arises due to the absence of

{\nu}_{\mu}=0_{\mu}

in the sum,
and ensures that the proper-time integral converges in the ultraviolet.
In infinite spacetime volume,

\Theta_{0}(s)=1

and

{\overline{G}}_{0}(0,0)

appropriately vanishes.

III
Finite-Volume Thermodynamics in a Magnetic Field

Having spelled out the charged pion propagator in a magnetic field,
finite-volume thermodynamic quantities can be computed using chiral perturbation theory.
Specifically,
we address the chiral condensate, free energy, magnetization and pressure anisotropy.
Although we can readily evaluate their temperature dependence for temperatures that are small compared to the chiral symmetry breaking scale,
we show results at zero temperature (
{\beta}=\infty

III.1
Chiral Condensate

Due to the remnant translational invariance in a magnetic field,
the chiral condensate in finite volume is a local quantity,
which we denote by

\langle\,{\overline{\psi}}(x)\psi(x)\,\rangle
.
It can be obtained from the partition function

Z

by functional differentiation with respect to a local scalar source

S(x)
,
4
4

4

We use an isoscalar source, because there is no isospin breaking in the chiral condensate to the order we are working.

namely

\displaystyle\langle\,{\overline{\psi}}(x)\psi(x)\,\rangle=-\frac{\delta\log Z}{\delta S(x)}.

(33)

When the source is replaced by a uniform value,
such as the quark mass

s(x)\to m_{q}
,
partial differentiation produces the volume averaged condensate

\langle\,{\overline{\psi}}\psi\,\rangle=-\frac{1}{{\beta}V}\frac{\partial\log Z}{\partial m_{q}},

(34)

which can also be obtained by directly computing the spacetime average of the local condensate

\langle\,{\overline{\psi}}\psi\,\rangle=\frac{1}{{\beta}V}\int d^{4}x\,\langle\,{\overline{\psi}}(x)\psi(x)\,\rangle.

(35)

In scenarios with translational invariance,
the local and average condensates are equal.
In a magnetic field at finite volume,
however,
they are no longer equal as

\langle\,{\overline{\psi}}(x)\psi(x)\,\rangle

depends on

x_{\perp}

due to the remnant

\mathbb{Z}_{N_{\Phi}}

translational invariance.

In chiral perturbation theory for two flavors
Gasser and Leutwyler 1984
,
the leading-order value and one-loop correction to the chiral condensate arise from terms in the Euclidean action density

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S\,\lambda\left[1-\frac{1}{2F^{2}}({\pi}^{0})^{2}-\frac{1}{F^{2}}\pi^{+}\pi^{-}\right],

In the action density,

is the chiral-limit value of the pion decay constant,

Taking the action density with

Using the action density,

The result has the schematic form

Denoting

III.2
Free Energy

The free energy density

{\mathcal{F}}

is related to the thermodynamic partition function through the relation

{\mathcal{F}}=-\frac{1}{{\beta}V}\log Z.

(48)

In chiral perturbation theory,
the free energy density of pions can be obtained from
Eq. (
34
)
through the relation

\frac{\partial{\mathcal{F}}}{\partial m^{2}}=F^{2}\frac{\langle\,{\overline{\psi}}\psi\,\rangle}{\,\,\langle\,{\overline{\psi}}\psi\,\rangle_{0}},

(49)

where

m^{2}

is the tree-level pion mass,
and the Gell-Mann–Oakes–Renner relation has been applied.
In what follows,
we focus on the magnetic free energy density,
which we define as

{\mathcal{F}}_{B}={\mathcal{F}}-{\mathcal{F}}\,\big|_{B=0}-\tfrac{1}{2}B^{2}.

(50)

The subtraction of

\frac{1}{2}B^{2}

removes the pure gauge contribution from the free energy density,
so that

{\mathcal{F}}_{B}

is exclusively the matter contribution.

Integrating the regularized expression for the charged pion contribution to the condensate

\langle\,{\overline{\psi}}\psi\,\rangle

leads to the free energy density

{\mathcal{F}}_{B}

up to an integration constant.
The result,
however,
requires renormalization.
As in Sec.
II.3
,
only the

{\nu}_{\mu}=0_{\mu}

term is divergent,
and this term is the infinite-volume limit.
Hence,
we make the further separation

{\mathcal{F}}_{B}={\mathcal{F}}_{B}^{\infty}+{\mathcal{F}}^{\text{FV}}_{B},

(51)

in order to isolate the divergent term

{\mathcal{F}}_{B}^{\infty}

in the sum over images.
From
Eq. (
49
),
it has the form

{\mathcal{F}}_{B}^{\infty}=-\int dm^{2}\,\left(g_{+}(0_{\mu})-g_{+}(0_{\mu})\Big|_{B=0}\right)+c_{B}(s_{0}),

(52)

where

g_{+}(0_{\mu})

appears in
Eq. (
24
),
and

c_{B}(s_{0})

is independent of

m^{2}
.
Even after subtraction of the

B=0

limit,
the

{\mathcal{O}}(B^{2})

term in

{\mathcal{F}}_{B}^{\infty}

logarithmically diverges
as

s_{0}\to 0
.
In this case,
one requires a magnetic field-dependent counterterm from the chiral Lagrangian

c_{B}\propto B^{2}
,
which,
due to its independence from pion fields, is called a high-energy constant in the language of
Gasser and Leutwyler 1984
.
The renormalization condition on the

{\mathcal{O}}(B^{2})

term of the free energy density

{\mathcal{F}}

is chosen to preserve the classical value

\frac{1}{2}B^{2}
,
which ensures that the magnetic field strength is not renormalized from matter fields
Schwinger 1951
.
After renormalization,
the infinite volume contribution to the magnetic free energy density is thus

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}_{B}^{\infty}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{ds}{(4\pi)^{2}s^{3}}\,e^{-sm_{\pi}^{2}}\left[\frac{QBs}{\sinh QBs}-1+\frac{(QBs)^{2}}{6}\right].

The finite volume contribution,
by contrast,
requires no renormalization,
and is given by

\displaystyle\noindent{\mathcal{F}}^{\text{FV}}_{B}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{ds}{(4\pi)^{2}s^{3}}\,e^{-sm_{\pi}^{2}}\left[\frac{QBs}{\sinh QBs}-1\right]

(54)

\displaystyle\phantom{spaces}\times\left[\vartheta_{3}\big(0,e^{-\frac{L_{3}^{2}}{4s}}\big)-1\right].

In Eqs. (
III.2
) and (
54
),
we have replaced the tree-level pion mass

m

with the physical pion mass

m_{\pi}
,
because the difference is higher order.
Notice that the finite volume effect involves only images in the longitudinal direction.
This is the

A_{\perp}\to\infty

limit with

QB

fixed that we encounter above for the chiral condensate.
The free energy is a spacetime average,
rather than a local distribution.

III.3
Magnetic Pressure Anisotropy and Magnetization

The addition of a magnetic field breaks the isotropy of space,
which can manifest itself in thermodynamic quantities.
Allowing for such anisotropy,
the matter contribution to the magnetic pressure in the

i^{\rm th}

direction can be defined as
Ferrer et al. 2010

\displaystyle p_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{L_{i}}{V}\left(\frac{\partial F_{B}}{\partial L_{i}}\right)_{L_{j},B},

(55)

where

j{\neq}i

and

F_{B}

is the free energy,
namely

F_{B}=V{\mathcal{F}}_{B}
.
In this definition,
the magnetic field is held fixed.
Due to the field quantization condition on a torus,
however,
lattice practitioners cannot generally vary the size of a transverse direction while keeping the magnetic field fixed.
In lattice QCD calculations,
a more accessible definition of the magnetic pressure is
Bali et al. 2013

\displaystyle\widetilde{p}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{L_{i}}{V}\left(\frac{\partial F_{B}}{\partial L_{i}}\right)_{L_{j},N_{\Phi}},

(56)

In the longitudinal direction,
these two definitions of pressure are identical

\widetilde{p}_{3}=p_{3}=-{\mathcal{F}}_{B}-L_{3}\frac{\partial{\mathcal{F}}_{B}}{\partial L_{3}}.

(57)

Due to magnetic flux quantization,
however,
the magnetic pressure in the transverse directions differs between the two definitions

\widetilde{p}_{\perp}-p_{\perp}=B\left(\frac{\partial{\mathcal{F}}_{B}}{\partial B}\right)_{V}\equiv-B\mathcal{M}_{r},

(58)

where we have identified the renormalized magnetization

{\mathcal{M}}_{r}
,
given by

(59)

The renormalized magnetization is a measure of the magnetic response of the
QCD vacuum
Kabat et al. 2002
;
Cohen and Werbos 2009
.
Note that by subtracting the gauge contribution to the free energy density

{\mathcal{F}}_{B}

in Eq. (
50

To access the renormalized magnetization in lattice QCD calculations,

(60)

Using
Eqs. (
56
)
and

.
The latter owes to Eq. (

i.e.
,

Carrying out integration by parts symmetrically thus enables the replacement

\displaystyle\overleftarrow{\partial_{\mu}}V_{1}(y)\overrightarrow{\partial_{\mu}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\overleftarrow{\partial_{\mu}}V_{1}(y)\overleftarrow{\partial_{\mu}}+\overrightarrow{\partial_{\mu}}V_{1}(y)\overrightarrow{\partial_{\mu}}\right).

(69)

With this replacement made,
the coordinate-space operator

\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}}(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\overleftarrow{\partial_{\mu}}\overleftarrow{\partial_{\mu}}V_{1}(y)+V_{1}(y)\overrightarrow{\partial_{\mu}}\overrightarrow{\partial_{\mu}}\right)-V_{2}(y)

(70)

\displaystyle-\overleftarrow{\partial_{\mu}}[\partial_{\mu}V_{1}(y)]-[\partial_{\mu}V_{1}(y)]\overrightarrow{\partial_{\mu}}.

The first two terms above will permit simplification in

The last two terms above simplify when one of them is integrated by parts,

The net result of these manipulations is the simplified form

\displaystyle\left(-\overleftarrow{\partial_{\mu}}\overleftarrow{\partial_{\mu}}+m^{2}\right)\tfrac{1}{2}V_{1}(y)

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{2}V_{1}(y)\left(-\overrightarrow{\partial_{\mu}}\overrightarrow{\partial_{\mu}}+m^{2}\right)-V(y),

where

\displaystyle V(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m^{2}V_{1}(y)+V_{2}(y)-\partial^{2}_{\mu}V_{1}(y).

(72)

The coordinate-space operator

in coordinate space,

|y\rangle{\mathcal{O}}(y)\langle y|={\mathcal{O}}|y\rangle\langle y|

Including the perturbative correction in Eq. (

\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}_{0}^{-1}

\displaystyle(1-\tfrac{1}{2}V_{1})G_{0}^{-1}(1-\tfrac{1}{2}V_{1})+V+\cdots.

IV.1.2
Renormalization

The expressions written above have an ultraviolet divergence in

V

that is regulated by a proper-time cutoff

s_{0}\ll 1
.
As in Sec.
II.3
,
the divergence is independent of both the magnetic field and the volume,
and is canceled by inclusion of the appropriate counterterms from the chiral Lagrangian.
A further effect of such counterterms is a shift of the mass-squared appearing in

G_{0}^{-1}
.
After the divergence is canceled,
one can renormalize the tree-level mass

m

to the physical pion mass

m_{\pi}
,

i.e.
the mass in the zero-field and infinite-volume limit.
There is no remaining

s_{0}

dependence in this renormalization scheme.

To focus specifically on finite volume effects,
we arrange the terms of the effective action by adding and subtracting the infinite-volume limit in nonzero magnetic fields.
To this end,
the renormalized neutral pion effective action is written as

(78)

The neutral pion energy

\displaystyle E^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m_{\pi}^{2}\left[1+\frac{QB}{(4{\pi}F_{\pi})^{2}}\,{\mathcal{I}}\left(\frac{m_{\pi}^{2}}{QB}\right)\right],

(79)

where the magnetic field dependence arises through the function

{\mathcal{I}}({\alpha})

given in Eq. (
27

This infinite-volume result was obtained using chiral perturbation theory in
Ref.
Tiburzi 2008
.

The remaining contribution to the effective action appearing in
Eq. (
78
)
is the effective potential

{\mathcal{V}}={\mathcal{V}}(y_{\perp})
.
This is defined to be solely a finite volume effect,
9
9

9

As such,
the volume effect includes that in a vanishing magnetic field.
Setting

we obtain

Taking the magnetic field to vanish,
we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathcal{V}}(y_{\perp})=\frac{m_{\pi}^{2}}{F_{\pi}^{2}}\left[{\overline{G}}_{+}(y,y)-\frac{1}{2}{\overline{G}}_{0}(0,0)\right]-\frac{\partial^{2}_{\mu}G_{+}(y,y)}{F_{\pi}^{2}}.

V
Summary of Key Results

Chiral perturbation theory gives a model-independent description of low-energy QCD.
We utilize this effective theory to determine finite volume effects on QCD observables in a uniform magnetic field.
Our attention is restricted to charge neutral observables at next-to-leading order in the chiral expansion,
including:
the chiral condensate, magnetization, pressure anisotropy, and neutral pion effective action.
These observables receive loop corrections from charged pions,
which are subject to magnetic periodic boundary conditions.
Due to magnetic periodicity,
the finite-volume theory retains a remnant of the continuous translational invariance of the Landau level problem in infinite volume.
This remnant translational invariance has a desirable feature for charged pion loop contributions:
spacetime averaging produces the infinite transverse-area results at a given magnetic field,
namely

A_{\perp}\to\infty

with

QB

held fixed.
Finite volume effects of suitably averaged quantities thus depend on the transverse size only through the magnetic field quantization
condition,
not through transverse images.
Such averaging,
moreover,
is essentially a standard part of nearly all lattice QCD calculations of these observables.

A summary of key results is as follows.

(i)

Finite volume corrections to the chiral condensate in a magnetic field are computed in Sec.
III.1
.
The chiral condensate in finite volume is spatially varying due to the remnant translational invariance,
as shown in
Fig.
1
.
The spatially averaged condensate,
however,
receives only image corrections from the longitudinal direction,
as given by
Eq. (
45
).
The finite volume effect on the magnetic field dependence of the condensate is shown in
Fig.
2
.
For the smallest magnetic flux quanta,
the effect can be

\gtrsim 10\%
,
even for

m_{\pi}L=4
.
The finite volume serves to further catalyze chiral symmetry breaking in a magnetic field.

(ii)

We compute the magnetic pressure anisotropy using chiral perturbation theory in Sec.
III.3
.
The pressure anisotropy at fixed flux can be used on the lattice to determine the magnetization of the QCD vacuum
Bali et al. 2013
.
In finite volume,
the matter contribution to the pressure anisotropy depends on the magnetization,
but there is an additional term that is purely a finite volume artifact,
see Eq. (
61
).
The finite volume effect on the pressure anisotropy is shown in
Fig.
3
.
The additional term leads to the dominant finite volume effect,
which is quite substantial.
The finite volume produces a larger pressure anisotropy,
consequently a larger apparent magnetization (in magnitude).

(iii)

The neutral pion in a magnetic field is taken up in Sec.
IV
.
Calculation of the neutral pion effective action in finite volume is efficaciously performed using coordinate-space methods
pioneered by Schwinger.
The end result
Eq. (
78
)
features a coordinate-dependent potential for the neutral pion that complicates the behavior of its correlation function
Eq. (
84
).
Even after projecting the sink onto vanishing momentum,
the two-point function generally depends on the location of the source.
Fortunately,
this finite volume effect is estimated to be quite small,
as shown in
Fig.
4
.

(iv)

While the coordinate-dependent effective potential of the neutral pion leads to momentum non-conservation between the source and sink,
source averaging is carried out in practice to mitigate gauge noise.
The effect of source averaging is to approximately project the correlation function to zero momentum at the source,
thus restoring translational invariance.
With momentum conserved,
the two-point function takes a simple form
Eq. (
91
),
from which the finite volume correction to the neutral pion energy can be identified.
One application of this result is the determination of the finite volume effect
on the magnetic polarizability of the neutral pion,
which is shown in
Fig.
5

and points to a

10\%

effect for

m_{\pi}L=4
.

Finally, although our investigation concerns the finite-volume effects in lattice QCD calculations,
our results hint at potentially important finite-size effects due to field inhomogeneities.
It would be interesting to adapt the techniques presented here to the study of
magnetic fields of finite spatial extent, which could be relevant for heavy ion collisions.

(101)

\displaystyle\phantom{S}\times\vartheta_{3}\big(\tfrac{Q\theta_{2}}{2}-\tfrac{{\pi}N_{\Phi}x_{1}}{L_{1}},e^{-\frac{QBL_{2}^{2}}{4\tanh QBs}}\big)

\displaystyle+\,\vartheta_{2}\big(Q\theta_{1}+\tfrac{2{\pi}N_{\Phi}x_{2}}{L_{2}},e^{-\frac{QBL_{1}^{2}}{\tanh QBs}}\big)

\displaystyle\phantom{S}\times\vartheta_{4}\big(\tfrac{Q\theta_{2}}{2}-\tfrac{{\pi}N_{\Phi}x_{1}}{L_{1}},e^{-\frac{QBL_{2}^{2}}{4\tanh QBs}}\big),\quad

after separating the even and odd images in the
\hat{x}_{1}
-direction.
Identical results are obtained from an analogous expression that separates the even and odd images in the

\hat{x}_{2}
-direction.
This is due to the readily proven identity

\displaystyle\vartheta_{3}(2z_{1},q_{1}^{4})\,\vartheta_{3}(z_{2},q_{2})+\vartheta_{2}(2z_{1},q^{4}_{1})\,\vartheta_{4}(z_{2},q_{2})=

\displaystyle\vartheta_{3}(2z_{2},q_{2}^{4})\,\vartheta_{3}(z_{1},q_{1})+\vartheta_{2}(2z_{2},q^{4}_{2})\,\vartheta_{4}(z_{1},q_{1}).

(102)

Results for the neutral pion propagator
can be obtained from those of the charged pion by evaluation at

Q=0
.
The zero-charge limit produces the coincident finite-volume propagator of the neutral pion

{\overline{G}}_{0}(0,0)

in
Eq. (
32
).
In that case,
the image sums are contained in the function

\Theta_{0}(s)
,
which is defined as

\Theta_{0}(s)=\prod_{\mu=0}^{3}\vartheta_{3}\Big(0,e^{-\frac{L_{\mu}^{2}}{4s}}\Big).

(103)

At zero temperature,
one has

\beta=\infty

and consequently

\vartheta_{3}(0,0)=1
.
The product over all

{\mu}

is then reduced to that over the three spatial directions,
and reflects that only

{\nu}_{0}=0

contributions remain.
This feature is shared by the charged pion propagator,
due to the temperature dependence of
Eq. (
99
).

Appendix B
Covariant Derivatives of the Coincident Propagator

In computing the effective action of the neutral pion in
Sec.
IV.1
,
an additional one-loop diagram element is required beyond the coincident propagators given in
Sec.
II.3
.
The new element is the charged pion tadpole diagram with two covariant derivatives of the pion propagator,
specifically of the form

D^{2}G_{+}(x,x)\equiv\lim_{x^{\prime}\to x}\big\langle\,D^{\prime}_{\mu}\pi^{+}(x^{\prime})D_{\mu}\pi^{-}(x)\,\big\rangle.

(104)

Point splitting has been introduced to regulate an ultraviolet divergence in a way consistent with dimensional regularization.
As this divergence is independent of both the magnetic field and the volume,
it would nevertheless be removed in the zero-field, infinite-volume renormalization scheme that we employ.

While the covariant derivatives of the charged pion propagator are straightforward to evaluate,
a more circuitous route to compute
Eq. (
104
)
proves beneficial.
One notes that

[D^{\prime}_{\mu},D_{\mu}]=0

for each

{\mu}
,
thus allowing us to arrive at the identity

D^{\prime}_{\mu}D_{\mu}=-\tfrac{1}{2}\left(D^{\prime}_{\mu}D^{\prime}_{\mu}+D_{\mu}D_{\mu}\right)+\tfrac{1}{2}(D^{\prime}_{\mu}+D_{\mu})^{2}.

(105)

Making use of this identity and the Green’s function relation given in
Eq. (
12
),
we find

D^{2}G_{+}(x,x)=-m^{2}G_{+}(x,x)+\tfrac{1}{2}\partial^{2}_{\mu}G_{+}(x,x).

(106)

To obtain this relation,
note that point splitting requires one to evaluate at

x^{\prime}\neq x

before taking the spacetime points to be coincident

x^{\prime}\to x
.
The relation in Eq. (
106
) agrees with that obtained by direct covariant differentiation,
however,
a proper-time integration by parts is needed to uncover the form given above.
It is also possible to arrive at the relation by considering how the diagram element enters as a perturbative
correction to the neutral pion Green’s function Eq. (
66
).
In that case,
one can perform integration by parts over the intermediate spacetime coordinate.
Such integration by parts is efficacious for other contributions to the effective action,
as discussed in Sec.
IV.1
.

Using
Eq. (
106
),
the new one-loop diagram element can be expressed in terms of the coincident charged pion propagator
Eq. (
21
)
and derivatives thereof.
The latter contribution is given by

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{\mu}G_{+}(x,x)=-\sum_{{\nu}_{\mu}}(QB{\nu}_{\perp}L_{\perp})^{2}f({\nu}_{\perp},x_{\perp})\,g_{+}({\nu}_{\mu}),\,\,\,

(107)

and is written in terms of the functions

f({\nu}_{\perp},x_{\perp})

and

g_{+}({\nu}_{\mu})

that appear in Eqs. (
22
) and (
23
),
respectively.
Alternatively,
this contribution can be expressed in terms of a proper-time integral

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{\mu}G_{+}(x,x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\,\frac{e^{-sm_{\pi}^{2}}}{(4{\pi}s)^{2}}\frac{QBs}{\sinh QBs}\partial^{2}_{\mu}\Theta(x_{\perp},s),\,\,\,

(108)

where

\Theta(x_{\perp},s)

is given in Eq. (
98
).
It is straightforward to differentiate this function,
with the result producing dependence on various

\vartheta^{\prime\prime}_{j}(z,q)
,
where primes denote differentiation with respect to the first argument of
the elliptic-theta functions.
The expression for

\partial^{2}_{\mu}\Theta(x_{\perp},s)

is quite lengthy,
and is omitted for brevity.

Appendix C
Two-Point Function Modification

After projection onto zero spatial momentum at the sink,
the neutral pion two-point function in
Eq. (
81
)
can be cast in a form very similar to Eq. (
84
)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}_{0}(T,x_{\perp})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-ET}}{2E}\Big[1+{\mathcal{R}}(T,x_{\perp},\theta_{\perp})\Big],

(109)

where,
after integration over the intermediate time,
the modification function

{\mathcal{R}}(T,x_{\perp},\theta_{\perp})

is given by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}(T,x_{\perp},\theta_{\perp})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{p}_{\perp}}\int_{0}^{L_{\perp}}d^{2}y_{\perp}\,e^{i\vec{p}_{\perp}\cdot(\vec{y}-\vec{x})_{\perp}}\,{\mathcal{V}}(y_{\perp})

(110)

\displaystyle\phantom{space}\times\frac{\frac{E}{\,E_{\vec{p}_{\perp}}}e^{(E-E_{\vec{p}_{\perp}})T}{-}1}{\vec{p}\,{}_{\perp}^{2}},

with

E_{\vec{p}}=\sqrt{E^{2}+\vec{p}\,{}^{2}}
.
Explicit coordinate dependence of the effective potential

{\mathcal{V}}(y_{\perp})

leads to
non-conservation of momentum between the source and sink.
Thus,
there are contributions from momentum modes with

\vec{p}_{\perp}\neq 0
.
This applies to the charged pion contribution to the effective potential.
By contrast,
the neutral pion contribution
maintains momentum conservation,
and can readily be included in the finite volume effect on the energy

{\Delta}E

in
Eq. (
84
).

Writing the charged pion contribution to the effective potential
Eq. (
80
)
in terms of its image contributions,
the transverse position integral appearing in

{\mathcal{R}}(T,x_{\perp},\theta_{\perp})

can be performed,
because it is simply a Fourier transform

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{L_{\perp}}d^{2}y_{\perp}\,e^{i\vec{p}_{\perp}\cdot\vec{y}_{\perp}}f({\nu}_{\perp},y_{\perp})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{n_{1},N_{\Phi}{\nu}_{2}}\,\delta_{n_{2},-N_{\Phi}{\nu}_{1}}

(111)

\displaystyle\times\,A_{\perp}\,f({\nu}_{\perp},0),

where the phase function

f({\nu}_{\perp},x_{\perp})

is given in Eq. (
22
).
The transverse momentum sums are now trivial to perform,
leading to the charged pion contribution

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}(T,x_{\perp},\theta_{\perp})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{{\nu}}-\vec{0}}\tfrac{\,m_{\pi}^{2}+(QB{\nu}_{\perp}L_{\perp})^{2}}{F_{\pi}^{2}}f({\nu}_{\perp},0)\,g_{+}(\vec{{\nu}})

(112)

\displaystyle\times\,e^{-i\vec{p}_{\nu}\cdot\vec{x}_{\perp}}\,\frac{\frac{E}{E_{\vec{p}_{\nu}}}e^{(E-E_{\vec{p}_{\nu}})T}{-}1}{\vec{p}\,{}_{\nu}^{2}},

where

g_{+}({\nu}_{\mu})

is given in Eq. (
23
),
and the image-dependent momentum modes are specified by

\vec{p}_{\nu}=2\pi N_{\Phi}\left(\frac{{\nu}_{2}}{L_{1}},-\frac{{\nu}_{1}}{L_{2}}\right)
.

The charged pion contribution to the modification function can be additively decomposed into two terms

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}(T,x_{\perp},\theta_{\perp})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{\parallel}(T)+{\mathcal{R}}_{\perp}(T,x_{\perp},\theta_{\perp}),

(113)

where

{\mathcal{R}}_{\perp}(T,x_{\perp},\theta_{\perp})

is the contribution from all images with

{\nu}_{\perp}\neq 0
,
while

{\mathcal{R}}_{\parallel}(T)

is the contribution from all images with

{\nu}_{\perp}=0
.
The former contribution does not exhibit any simplifications.
It is given by a formula almost identical to
Eq. (
112
),
but with

\sum_{\vec{{\nu}}-\vec{0}}

replaced by

\sum_{\vec{{\nu}},{\nu}_{\perp}\neq 0}
,
and is the

{\mathcal{R}}_{\perp}(T,x_{\perp},\theta_{\perp})

function appearing in
Eq. (
84
).
For the twist angle and source location averaging discussed in
Sec.
IV.2
,
it is crucial to note that the dependence on twist angles enters via the phase function

f({\nu}_{\perp},0)=(-1)^{N_{\Phi}{\nu}_{1}{\nu}_{2}}\,e^{iQ\theta_{\perp}\cdot{\nu}_{\perp}}
,
whereas the transverse location of the source appears exclusively in the Fourier phase

e^{-i\vec{p}_{\nu}\cdot\vec{x}_{\perp}}
.
Both averages thus project onto the sector with

{\nu}_{\perp}=0
;
consequently,
these averages of

{\mathcal{R}}_{\perp}(T,x_{\perp},\theta_{\perp})

are zero.

By contrast,
the charged pion contribution to

{\mathcal{R}}_{\parallel}(T)

has

\vec{p}_{\nu}=0
,
for which momentum is conserved between the source and sink.
In taking the limit of vanishing momentum,
we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{\parallel}(T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left(T+\frac{1}{E}\right)\frac{m_{\pi}}{2F_{\pi}^{2}}\sum_{{\nu}_{3}\neq 0}g_{+}({\nu}_{3}),

(114)

where we have approximated a multiplicative factor of

E

by

m_{\pi}
,
because the difference is

{\mathcal{O}}(F_{\pi}^{-4})
.
The form of

{\mathcal{R}}_{\parallel}(T)

is exactly what one expects for a small correction

{\Delta}E

to the energy

E
.
In terms of a two-point function with energy

E+{\Delta}E
,
we have

\frac{e^{-(E+{\Delta}E)T}}{2(E+{\Delta}E)}=\frac{e^{-ET}}{2E}\left[1-\left(T+\frac{1}{E}\right){\Delta}E\right]+\cdots.

(115)

Provided

{\Delta}E\,T\ll 1

and

{\Delta}E/E\ll 1
,
one can treat

{\Delta}E

as a perturbative correction to the energy obtained from the time dependence of a two-point function with a simple exponential falloff.
Hence,
we identify

{\Delta}E

of Eq. (
84
)
with

\displaystyle{\Delta}E=\frac{m_{\pi}}{2F_{\pi}^{2}}\left[\sum_{{\nu}_{3}\neq 0}g_{+}({\nu}_{3})-\frac{1}{2}\,{\overline{G}}_{0}(0,0)\right],

(116)

which additionally includes the neutral pion contribution.
This finite volume effect
is expressed as a proper-time integral in
Eq. (
92
).
