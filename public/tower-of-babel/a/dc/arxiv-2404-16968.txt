Detecting fast vanishing loops in complex-analytic germs (and detecting germs that are inner metrically conical) Thanks: We were supported by the Israel Science Foundation, grants No. 1910/18 and 1405/22
Dmitry Kerner and Rodrigo Mendes

Abstract
. Let X X be a reduced complex-analytic germ of pure dimension n ≥ 2 , n\geq 2, with arbitrary singularities (not necessarily normal or complete intersection). Various homology cycles on L ​ i ​ n ​ k ϵ ​ [ X ] Link_{\epsilon}[X] vanish at different speeds when ϵ → 0 . \epsilon\to 0. We give a condition ensuring fast vanishing loops on X . X. The condition is in terms of the discriminant and the covering data for “convenient” coverings X → ( ℂ n , o ) X\to(\mathbb{C}^{n},o) . No resolution of singularities is involved. For surface germs ( n = 2 n=2 ) this condition becomes necessary and sufficient. A corollary for surface germs that are strictly complete intersections detects fast loops via singularities of the projectivized tangent cone of X . X. Fast loops are the simplest obstructions for X X to be inner metrically conical. Hence we get simple necessary conditions to the IMC property. For normal surface germs these conditions are also sufficient. We give numerous classes of non-IMC germs and IMC germs.

1.
Introduction

1.1.

Take a complex-analytic germ
X\subset(\mathbb{C}^{N},o).

The first step in its visualization is Milnor’s Conic structure theorem:

X
is homeomorphic to the (standard) cone over the link,

Cone[Link[X]]\subset(\mathbb{C}^{N},o).

In “most cases” this (non-embedded) homeomorphism cannot be chosen differentiable in any sense.

In some cases this topological equivalence can be strengthened to (non-embedded) bi-Lipschitz equivalence, as follows.
The (standard) metric on
(\mathbb{C}^{N},o)
induces the inner metrics on
X
and
Cone[Link[X]],
by the length of the shortest path between two points.
The germ
X
is called inner-metrically-conical (IMC) if the homeomorphism
X\xrightarrow[\,\smash{\raisebox{3.46594pt}{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}\,]{}{}Cone[Link[X]]
can be chosen bi-Lipschitz with respect to these inner metrics.
The metrically conical structure is the simplest possible metric structure. E.g. if
X
metrically conical, then it is bi-Lipschitz equivalent to its triangulation.

Any complex analytic curve germ is IMC. In higher dimensions the situation is more complicated.
Initially many complex-analytic surface germs were thought to be IMC. The first
weighted-homogeneous non-IMC surface germs were found in
[
Birbrair-Fernandes.08
]
.
If
X
is a weighted-homogeneous IMC-germ of quotient type,
{\footnotesize\left.\raisebox{1.37775pt}{$\mathbb{C}^{2}$}\!/\!\raisebox{-1.37775pt}{$\mu$}\right.},
then its two lowest weights are necessarily equal,
[
B.F.N.08
]
.
The converse statement was verified in
[
B.F.N.09
]
for Brieskorn-Pham singularities in
(\mathbb{C}^{3},o)
.
Finally,
[
B.N.P.14
]
established the thick-thin decomposition for normal surface germs.
In particular, this gave an algorithm to verify the (non)IMC-property via the resolution graph of the singularity.
See also
[
B.F.G.17
]
.
Using this
[
Okuma.17
]
proved: a Brieskorn-Pham surface germ that is an isolated complete intersection is IMC iff its two lowest weights are equal.
In higher dimensions, among the
A_{k}
-types (with equation
\sum^{n}_{i=1}x^{2}_{i}+x^{k+1}_{n+1}=0
), the only IMC-case is
A_{1},

[
B.F.N.G.O’S.09
]
.

In all these cases the first reason be non-IMC is the existence of so-called “fast loops”, and their generalizations, “choking horns”,
[
B.F.G.17
]
These are topologically non-trivial loops on
Link[X]
that vanish faster than linearly, as one approaches the base point
o\in X.

Fast loops (and fast cycles in higher dimensions) are important far beyond the IMC-context.
They carry essential information on the (Lipschitz part of) local geometry and topology of
X.

E.g., each fast loop lies inside a thin zone, a rigid sector of the germ, where the metric is essentially distorted (as compared to the metric on a cone).
To detect/forbid these fast loops is in general a complicated task.
The only well-studied case is the normal surface singularities,
[
B.N.P.14
]
, with the fast-loops criteria via the resolution graph of
X
.

In
[
Kerner-Mendes.23
]
we gave sufficient criteria for fast cycles on complete intersection germs of any (co)dimension that are perturbations of weighted-homogeneous germs. In “most cases” if the
n
lowest weights do not all coincide, then
X
has a fast cycle.

All these results suggested: IMC-germs are very rare, to the extent that, e.g. complex-analytic IMC’s in
(\mathbb{C}^{3},o)
could be possibly classified, up to the ambient topological equivalence.

Now we give simple necessary/sufficient conditions for reduced germs to posses fast loops. These germs are of any dimension and codimension, not necessarily normal or complete intersection, possibly with non-isolated singularities.

•

For surface germs the conditions are in terms of the discriminant of the covering. They are necessary and sufficient.

For ​normal ​surface ​germs ​this ​allows ​simple ​verification ​of ​the ​IMC ​property.
​In ​particular ​one gets: while IMC’s are rare,
their classification is in some sense impossible. (Even in the case
X\subset(\mathbb{C}^{3},o)
.)

•

The higher dimensional case admits (in many cases) a reduction to the surface case. Hence an easy way to detect a fast cycle. (And thus an obstruction to the IMC-property.)

Some of our results (for normal surface germs) could be possibly obtained by the methods of
[
B.N.P.14
]
. But our proofs do not use resolution of singularities and are independent of those of
[
B.N.P.14
]
.

A remark: our results allow to detect the homotopy types of fast loops, and their vanishing speeds. This will be done in the subsequent work.

1.2.
The fast cycles and (non)IMC-criteria

1.2.1.

Let
X
be a reduced, complex-analytic surface germ, of multiplicity
p,
not necessarily normal, possibly with non-isolated singularity. Present it as a covering space,
\pi:X\ \lower 0.86108pt\hbox{$\mathrel{\mathop{\to}\limits^{p:1}}$}\ (\mathbb{C}^{2},o).
(This covering is assumed to be “convenient”.)
This map is ramified over the discriminant,
\Delta\subset(\mathbb{C}^{2},o).

We detect fast loops by the explicit numerical condition.

Theorem
3.2
.

X
has no fast loops iff the inequality
p>r_{k}-1+\sum q_{i}\cdot mult(\Delta_{k,i})
holds for each non-smooth tangential component of
\Delta.

The ingredients
\{r_{k}\},

\{q_{i}\},

\{mult(\Delta_{k,i})\}
form the ramification data of the covering
X\ \lower 0.86108pt\hbox{$\mathrel{\mathop{\to}\limits^{p:1}}$}\ (\mathbb{C}^{2},o),
and are defined in §
3.1
. In many cases this data is easily computable from the equations of
X.
Thus the theorem is handy in applications.
In fact, as the proof shows,

r_{k}+\sum q_{i}\cdot mult(\Delta_{k,i})-p
is the lower bound on the number of fast loops “hanging over
\Delta_{k}
”.

Corollary
3.3
.

A normal surface germ is IMC iff

p>r_{k}-1+\sum q_{i}\cdot mult(\Delta_{k,i})
for each non-smooth tangential component of
\Delta.

The absence of fast loops, i.e. this numerical inequality, imposes strong restrictions on the projectivized tangent cone.
Let
X\subset(\mathbb{C}^{2+c},o)
be an isolated strictly complete intersection surface germ.
Its projectivized tangent cone,
\mathbb{P}T_{X}\subset\mathbb{P}^{1+c},
is a curve (singular, possibly non-reduced).
Take its degree,
deg[\mathbb{P}T_{X}],
and the degree of is reduced locus,
deg[\mathbb{P}T^{red}_{X}].

Suppose the germs
(\mathbb{P}T_{X},pt_{i})
are reduced and singular for some points
\{pt_{i}\}.
Take their Milnor numbers,

\mu_{i}:=\mu(\mathbb{P}T_{X},pt_{i}).

Theorem
3.10
.

1. Suppose these points
\{pt_{i}\}
lie in one hyperplane,
\mathbb{P}^{c}\subset\mathbb{P}^{1+c},
that is “non-tangent” to
\mathbb{P}T_{X}.

If
\sum\mu_{i}\geq 1+deg[\mathbb{P}T_{X}]-deg[\mathbb{P}T^{red}_{X}],
then
X
has a fast loop.

2. In particular, if
\mathbb{P}T_{X}
is reduced, and
X
is IMC, then
\mathbb{P}T_{X}
must be smooth. (Hence
X
is an ordinary multiple point.) Additionally, when
\mathbb{P}T_{X}=\mathbb{P}T_{X}^{red}
any reduced singular point of
\mathbb{P}T_{X}\cap\mathbb{P}^{c}
produces a fast loop.

This is a very simple (and useful) IMC-obstruction. For example:

•

The only IMC’s among the super-isolated surface germs are the ordinary multiple points.
(Example
3.11
)

•

The only IMC’s among the surface singularities in
(\mathbb{C}^{3},o)
of right modality
\leq 2
are:
A_{1},D_{4},X_{9},P_{8}.

(Corollary
3.13
)

1.2.2.

In higher-dimensional case our results are weaker, giving just a sufficient condition for fast loops. Take a convenient covering
\pi:X\to(\mathbb{C}^{n},o),
with the discriminant
\Delta\subset(\mathbb{C}^{n},o),
and restrict it to a smooth surface germ,
\pi_{\mathcal{S}}:X\cap\pi^{-1}\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{S}\cong(\mathbb{C}^{2},o).
Accordingly one gets the discriminantal curve
\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}:=\Delta\cap\mathcal{S},
and the ramification data,

r_{k},

\{q_{i}\},

\{mult(\Delta_{k,i})\},
as above. Suppose
\mathcal{S}
is “weakly non-special”, i.e. the same numerical data is obtained for any other surface that is
tangent
to
\mathcal{S}.

Proposition
3.14
.

If
p\leq r_{k}-1+\sum q_{i}\cdot mult(\Delta_{k,i})
for some non-smooth tangential component of
\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}
then
X
has a fast loop.

This gives a quick check of fast loops/non-IMC property in higher dimensions. E.g. (corollary
3.16
): if
\mathbb{P}T_{\Delta}
has a point of high multiplicity, then
X
has a fast loop.

The contents/structure of the paper

​§2​

​§2.2​

recalls the tangent cone,

​§2.4​

​§2.5​

​§2.6​

The covering

detects fast loops on

The intersection

1.5.
Notations and conventions

i.

Through the paper by germs we mean their small representatives.

Everywhere in the paper
X\subset(\mathbb{C}^{N},o)
is a reduced pure-dimensional complex-analytic germ, of dimension
n\geq 2,
and multiplicity
p,
possibly with non-isolated singularity.

All the other germs are subanalytic and closed.

ii.

All our arcs are (real) subanalytic. Usually we take their length-parametrization,
\gamma(t)=(s_{1}\cdot t+o(t),\dots,s_{N}\cdot t+o(t)),
with
(s_{1},\dots,s_{N})\neq(0,\dots,0).

By a foliation we always mean a singular foliation of the germ
X

by real arcs, all passing through the origin.

iii.

The (inner) tangency order of two arcs
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\subset X
is
tord_{X}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}):=ord_{t}d_{X}(\gamma_{1}(t),\gamma_{2}(t)).

Here
d_{X}:X\times X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a subanalytic distance equivalent to the distance given by the infimum of lengths of paths connecting points of
X

[
Kurdyka-Orro.97
]
. Note that
tord_{X}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
can be non-integer.

If
X=(\mathbb{R}^{N},o)
then
d_{X}=\|\dots\|
(the usual Euclidean distance) and we write just
tord(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}).

By a LNE germ
X
means a representative where the inner distance
d_{X}
and the outer distance
\|\dots\|
on
X
are equivalent.

The tangency order of an arc and a subgerm,
\gamma,Y\!\subset\!X,
is
tord_{X}(\gamma,Y\!)\!:=\!\sup\limits_{\!\gamma_{Y}\subset Y}tord_{X}(\gamma,\gamma_{Y})\!\leq\!\infty.

The “order of difference” of
two subgerms,
Y_{1},Y_{2}\!\subset\!X,
is

(1)

dif\!f.ord_{X}(Y_{1},Y_{2}):=\inf\limits_{\gamma_{1}\subset Y_{1}}tord_{X}(\gamma_{1},Y_{2})=\inf\limits_{\gamma_{2}\subset Y_{2}}tord_{X}(\gamma_{2},Y_{1})\leq\infty.

iv.

Take an
\mathbb{R}
-analytic germ
Z\subset(\mathbb{R}^{N},o).
The tangent cone
T_{Z}
can be defined set-theoretically, e.g. as the Whitney cone
C_{3}
,

[
Whitney
, Chapter 7]
,
[
Chirka
, Chapter 2]
. The definition of
T_{Z}
is the same for subanalytic germs.
The tangent cone is subanalytic as well, with
dim_{\mathbb{R}}T_{Z}\leq dim_{\mathbb{R}}Z.
In particular,
if
dim_{\mathbb{R}}T_{Z}=1,
then
T_{Z}
is a finite union of lines and half-lines.

v.

Let
Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a sub-analytic set.
Every homology class in
H_{*}(Y,\mathbb{Z})
admits a subanalytic representative. Indeed, the singular homology is isomorphic to the simplicial homology.
And sub-analytic manifolds admit subanalytic triangulations,
[
Coste
, §4.3]
.

vi.

The singular locus of a complete intersection
X=V(f_{1},\dots,f_{c})\subset(\mathbb{C}^{n+c},o)
is defined (inside
X
) by the ideal of maximal minors
I_{c}[f^{\prime}_{1},\dots,f^{\prime}_{c}].
Here
\{f^{\prime}_{j}\}
are the column gradients.

2.
Preparations

2.1.
The (projectivized) tangent cone for complete intersections

2.1.1.
Hypersurfaces

Let
X\!\!=\!V(f)\!\subset\!(\mathbb{C}^{N},o),
then
T_{X}\!=\!V(l.t.(f))\!\subset\!\mathbb{C}^{N},
the lowest-order terms of
f.

Observe that the polynomial
l.t.(f)
can be not square-free, in which case
T_{X}
is non-reduced.

The projectivized tangent cone is
\mathbb{P}T_{X}=V(l.t.(f))\subset\mathbb{P}^{N-1}.
This is a projective hypersurface, possibly reducible, non-reduced.
Its (total) degree equals the multiplicity of
X,
i.e. the Taylor order
ord(f).

2.1.2.
Complete intersections

Let
X=V({\underline{f}})\subset(\mathbb{C}^{n+c},o),
where
{\underline{f}}:=f_{1},\dots,f_{c}\in\mathbb{C}\{{\underline{x}}\}
is a regular sequence.
Then
T_{X}=V(l.t.({\underline{f}})),
defined by the lowest order terms of all the elements of the ideal
({\underline{f}}).

In particular,
l.t.({\underline{f}})\supseteq(l.t.(f_{1}),\dots,l.t.(f_{c})).
This inclusion can be proper.

Example 2.1
.

For
f_{1}(x,y)\!=\!x^{2}y\!+\!y^{4},

f_{2}(x,y)\!=\!xy^{2}\!+\!x^{4},
one has
l.t.({\underline{f}})\!=\!(x^{2}y,xy^{2},x^{5}\!-\!y^{5})\!\supsetneq\!(l.t.(f_{1}),l.t.(f_{2})).

Therefore
T_{X}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n+c}
is not necessarily a complete intersection.
The germ
X
is called a
strictly complete intersection
if both
X
and
T_{X}
are complete intersections,
[
Bennett.77
]
.
In this case:

•

There exists a choice of defining equations,
X=V(f_{1},\dots,f_{c})
with
T_{X}=V(l.t.(f_{1}),\dots,l.t.(f_{c}))
and

deg(\mathbb{P}T_{X})=multX=\prod ord(f_{i}).

•

The projectivization
\mathbb{P}T_{X}\subset\mathbb{P}^{n+c-1}
is a complete intersection (possibly reducible, non-reduced).

•

The strict transform under blowup,
\widetilde{X}\subset Bl_{o}(\mathbb{C}^{n+c},o),
is a locally complete intersection at all its points.

2.2.
The convenient covering and its critical locus

Let
X
be as in §
1.5
.i.
Take the coordinate plane
\mathbb{C}^{n}=V(x_{n+1},\dots,x_{N})\subset\mathbb{C}^{N}
and the projection
\mathbb{C}^{N}\ni\ x\to(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
We call the restriction of this projection to the germ,
X\ \lower 0.86108pt\hbox{$\mathrel{\mathop{\to}\limits^{\pi}}$}\ (\mathbb{C}^{n},o)
,
convenient
if:

i.

\pi
is a (finite) ramified covering;

ii.

The kernel of the projection at
o
is non-tangent to
X,
i.e.

T_{X}\cap V(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=o\in\mathbb{C}^{N}.

These assumptions hold for generic projections (observe that the condition ii. is enough to get convenience). A projection can be quite non-generic, and yet convenient. E.g. the covering
(\mathbb{C}^{3},o)\supset V(z^{p}-f(x,y))\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},o)
is totally ramified over the

curve germ
V(f)\subset(\mathbb{C}^{2},o).
This covering is convenient iff
ord\ f\geq p.
The critical locus of
\pi
consists of those points where
\pi
is locally not an isomorphism ​onto ​its ​image.
​Thus ​
Crit(\pi)\!\subset\!X
​is ​an ​analytic ​subgerm, ​and

dim_{\mathbb{C}}Crit(\pi)\!\leq\!(n-1).
​(By Sard’s theorem.)

\begin{matrix}(\mathbb{C}^{N},o)\supset X\supset Crit(\pi)\\
\quad\quad\quad\searrow\quad\downarrow\pi\quad\quad\downarrow\pi_{|}\\
\quad\quad\quad\quad(\mathbb{C}^{n},o)\supset\Delta\quad\end{matrix}

​We remark: ​
Crit(\pi)\!\supseteq\!Sing(X).

The discriminant of
\pi
is the image of the critical locus,
\Delta:=\pi(Crit(\pi))
. As
\pi
is a finite
morphism,
\Delta\subset(\mathbb{C}^{n},o)
is an analytic subgerm,
dim_{\mathbb{C}}\Delta\leq n-1.
(By Grauert’s direct image theorem.)
The inequality can occur here. E.g. let
X
be the union of two planes (of dimension
n
) intersecting at one point.
Then
\Delta
is just one point,
o\in\mathbb{C}^{n}.

When
X
is a complete intersection, these set-theoretic definitions are replaced by more precise algebraic ones,
§
2.4
, and
\Delta
is well-behaved, e.g.
dim_{\mathbb{C}}\Delta=n-1.

Recall the basic properties of convenient projections.

•

A small deformation of the projection
\pi
preserves the convenience of the covering.

•

If
\pi
is convenient, then the restriction
\pi_{Y}=\pi|_{\pi^{-1}Y}
is a convenient covering for any smooth subvariety
Y\subset(\mathbb{C}^{n},o)
, assuming
Y\not\subseteq\Delta
and
\pi^{-1}Y
is reduced.

•

A small deformation of the base point does not preserve the convenience. E.g., let
o\neq x_{o}\in Crit(\pi)
be a smooth point of
X.

Then the projection
(X,x_{o})\to(\mathbb{C}^{n},\pi(x_{o}))
is non-convenient.

and

are finite morphisms with smooth images.

Thus to verify the flatness it is enough to verify: the degree of fibres is constant.
For each point

the fibre
{\tilde{\pi}}^{-1}(\tilde{x})
is of degree

(=the degree of the covering
\pi
).
For each point

(2)

Here (after a

The equalities

\blacksquare

The scheme structure on the critical locus and the discriminant

Let

.i. Let

Lemma 2.3
.

If the germ

​
1.​

\Delta\subset(\mathbb{C}^{n},o)

​

the column.

​
3.​

The diagram of §

•

\mathcal{O}_{Crit(\phi^{*}\pi)}=\mathcal{O}_{Crit(\pi)}\otimes_{(\mathbb{C}^{n},o)}\mathcal{O}_{(\mathbb{C}^{m},o)}

via

Proof.
First we define
Crit(\pi)\subset X
and
\Delta\subset(\mathbb{C}^{n},o)
as analytic subgerms. Then we deduce the statements.

a.

Recall the standard definitions
[
Looijenga
]
,
[
A.G.L.V.II
]
. When

is smooth, the critical locus of the projection

is the degeneracy locus of the induced morphism

In the general case the critical locus is defined as the degeneracy locus of the
n
-form

.
For the defining equations,

Thus the critical locus is the set of points of

where the form

Writing this in coordinates, the critical locus is defined (inside

We get the analytic subgerm

b.

We claim: the subgerm

det[\dots]
is regular in

(c+1)
equations. But

Altogether, the sequence

.

Equation (
3
) gives then Part 2 of the statement.

The discriminant is the image of

As

.

2.4.1.
The discriminant for hypersurface germs

Take a hypersurface germ
X=V(f)\subset(\mathbb{C}^{n+1},o)
of multiplicity
p
. Suppose the projection
X\to(\mathbb{C}^{n}_{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}},o)
is convenient.
Thus
T_{X}\cap V(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=(o),

therefore
f
contains the monomial
x^{p}_{n+1}.
By Weierstraß preparation theorem one can present:

(6)

f(x)=u(x)\big(x^{p}_{n+1}+x^{p-1}_{n+1}\cdot a_{p-1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})+\cdots+a_{0}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\big).

Here
u
is invertible and
a_{0},\dots,a_{p-1}
are analytic power series, with
ord(a_{j})\geq p-j
.
Thus we omit
u.

This hypersurface is the pullback of the universal hypersurface,
V(z^{p}+z^{p-1}a_{p-1}+\cdots+a_{0})\subset\mathbb{C}^{1+p}_{z,a_{p-1},\dots,a_{0}},

under the map
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\to(a_{p-1}(x),\dots,a_{0}(x)).

Therefore (by part 3 of lemma
2.3
) the discriminant of the projection,
\Delta\subset\mathbb{C}^{n},
is obtained as the pullback of the classical discriminant
\Delta_{class}
of the polynomial

z^{p}+z^{p-1}a_{p-1}+\cdots+a_{0}
. Here
\Delta_{class}\in\mathbb{Z}[\{a_{\bullet}\}]
is a (complicated) polynomial in the coefficients
\{a_{\bullet}\}
. We use the following properties
[
G.K.Z.
]
:

•

Assign the weights to the coefficients,
w(a_{j})=p-j
, then the polynomial
\Delta_{class}
is weighted-homogeneous, of total weight
p(p-1)
.

•

\Delta_{class}(\{a_{\bullet}\})=(\frac{a_{0}}{p-1})^{p-1}-(\frac{a_{1}}{p})^{p}+g(\{a_{\bullet}\})
,
where
g(\{a_{\bullet}\})\in(a_{0},a_{1})\cdot(a_{2}\dots a_{p-1})
.

•

For
p\geq 3
the hypersurface

is singular in codimension 1.

2.5.
Preimage of a thin set is thin (lifting tangent arcs to tangent arcs)

The convenient covering
(\mathbb{C}^{N},o)\supset X\ \lower 0.86108pt\hbox{$\mathrel{\mathop{\to}\limits^{\pi}}$}\ (\mathbb{C}^{n},o)
is a Lipschitz map. But
\pi
is far from being locally bi-Lipschitz.
In particular,
\pi
can increase the tangency order of arcs.
Yet, a weaker property holds.

Recall, a subanalytic germ
Y
is called
thin
if
dim_{\mathbb{R}}Y>dimT_{Y}
, see §
2.1
.
The following statement is well known.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\pi:X\to(\mathbb{C}^{n},o)
be a convenient covering and

Y\subseteq X
a subanalytic sub-germ with connected link.

​
1.​

The ​tangent ​cones ​satisfy:
dim_{\mathbb{R}}T_{Y}\!=\!dim_{\mathbb{R}}T_{\pi(Y)}.

In particular,
Y
is thin iff
(\pi(Y),o)
is thin.

​
2.​

Suppose
T_{(\pi(Y),o)}
is a (real) half-line.

​
a.​

Then the tangent cone
T_{Y}\subset\mathbb{C}^{N}
is a half-line,
i.e. any two arcs on
Y
are
outer
-tangent.

​
b.​

Moreover, any two arcs on
Y
are
inner
-tangent, i.e.

tord_{Y}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})>1.

Remark 2.7
.

Suppose the arcs
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\subset Y\subset(\mathbb{R}^{N},o)
are real-analytic, i.e. their length-parametrization is by power series with only integer exponents.
Then their outer tangency order (in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
) is an integer.
One might hope that the inner tangency order will be an integer and Part 2.b will give a stronger bound:
tord_{Y}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\geq 2.

This does not hold. Take a real-analytic germ
Y\subset(\mathbb{R}^{3},o)
that is not LNE, and such that
T_{Y}
is a half-line.
Suppose the (real-analytic) arcs
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\subset Y
realize the non-LNE assumption, i.e.
tord_{Y}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})<tord_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})=2.

Then
1\leq tord_{Y}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})<2.

An explicit example is the set
Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
defined by the equation
(x^{2}+y^{2})^{2}=z^{3}(x^{2}-y^{2})+\epsilon^{2}z^{7},
with
0<\epsilon\ll 1.

(It is instructive to draw its section by
z=1
.) The tangent cone is the
\hat{z}
-axis. The two arcs of the intersection
Y\cap\{x=0,z\geq 0\}
are real-analytic. Their parametrization is
(0,\frac{\pm 2\epsilon z^{2}}{\sqrt{1+\sqrt{1+z}}},z).

Their outer contact is 2. But their inner contact is
\frac{3}{2}.

Corollary 2.8
.

Suppose two arcs
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\subset(\mathbb{C}^{n},o)
are tangent. For any preimage
\gamma^{X}_{1}\subset X\subset(\mathbb{C}^{N},o)
there exists a preimage
\gamma^{X}_{2}\subset X
satisfying

tord_{X}(\gamma^{X}_{1},\gamma^{X}_{2})>1.

Proof.
As the arcs are tangent, we can assume the parameterizations
\gamma_{i}(t)=(t,{\underline{h}}_{i}(t)),
where all the entries of
{\underline{h}}_{i}(t)
are
o(t).

Build the Hölder triangle on these arcs,

(7)

H\ddot{o}ld[\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}]:=\cup_{s\in[0,1]}\big[s\gamma_{1}+(1-s)\gamma_{2}\big]:=\cup_{s\in[0,1]}\big[t,s\cdot{\underline{h}}_{1}(t)+(1-s)\cdot{\underline{h}}_{2}(t)\big]\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}.

By its construction
H\ddot{o}ld[\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}]\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a subanalytic subset (with connected link) whose tangent cone is the (real) half-line.
For a fixed lifting
\gamma^{X}_{1}\subset X
we lift the curve
Link[H\ddot{o}ld[\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}]]
(homeomorphically) to
a curve
Link^{X}[H\ddot{o}ld[\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}]]\subset X
that starts at
Link[\gamma^{X}_{1}].

This defines the lifting
H\ddot{o}ld^{X}[\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}]\subset X.

Now apply lemma
2.6
, with
Y=H\ddot{o}ld^{X}[\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}]
.

\blacksquare

Remark 2.9
.

This corollary fails if any of its assumptions are omitted.

i.

(Non-convenient covering) Take the covering
\mathbb{C}^{3}_{xyz}\supset X:=V(z^{p}-x)\to\mathbb{C}^{2}_{xy}
and the parameterized arcs

\gamma_{1}(t)=(0,t),

\gamma_{2}(t)=(t^{l},t)
for
1<l\leq p.
Then the only liftings are
\gamma^{X}_{1}(t)=(0,t,0)
and

\gamma^{X}_{2}(t)=(t^{l},t,\omega\cdot t^{\frac{l}{p}}),
where
\omega^{p}=1.
And
1=tord_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}(\gamma^{X}_{1},\gamma^{X}_{2})

Thus

1=tord_{X}(\gamma^{X}_{1},\gamma^{X}_{2})<tord_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})=l.

ii.

(Non-finite map) Let
\mathbb{C}^{3}_{xyz}\supset V(zy)\to\mathbb{C}^{2}_{xy}.
Take
\gamma_{1}(t)=(t,o)
and
\gamma_{2}(t)=(t,t^{2}),
thus
tord_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})=2.

For the lifting
\gamma^{X}_{1}(t)=(t,0,t)
there does not exist
\gamma^{X}_{2}
satisfying:
tord_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}(\gamma^{X}_{1},\gamma^{X}_{2})>1.

iii.

The conclusion cannot be strengthened to
tord_{X}(\gamma^{X}_{1},\gamma^{X}_{2})=tord_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
. For example, let
f(x,y,z)=z^{2}+x(x^{p}+y^{p})
,

.
The projection
\mathbb{C}^{3}\supset V(f)\to\mathbb{C}^{2}_{xy}\supset\Delta
is ramified over

.
Take two arcs,
\gamma_{1}=(0,t)\subset\Delta
and

.
Then

Therefore
tord_{X}(\gamma^{X}_{1},\gamma^{X}_{2})\leq\frac{q+p}{2}<q
.

2.6.
Fast cycles

A fast cycle is (roughly speaking) a thin germ that does not admit a hornic deformation-retraction onto a non-thin germ.

Definition 2.10
.

Fix a (subanalytic) subgerm
Y\subset X.

​
1.​

A subanalytic neighborhood
Y\!\subseteq\!\mathcal{U}(Y)\!\subseteq\!X
is
hornic
if

dif\!f.ord_{X}(Y,\mathcal{U}(Y))\!>\!1,
see (
1
).

​
2.​

Y
is called
linkwise retractible
to a subgerm
Y^{\prime}\subset Y
if there exists a deformation-retraction
Y\rightsquigarrow Y^{\prime}
(inside
X
) that retracts the links, i.e.
Link_{t}[Y]\rightsquigarrow Link_{t}[Y^{\prime}]
for all
0<t\ll 1.

​
3.​

A thin subgerm
Y\subset X
is called a
fast cycle
if
Y
does not admit
a hornic neighborhood,
Y\subseteq\mathcal{U}(Y)\subset X,
that linkwise retracts to a subgerm
Y^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{U}(Y)
with
dim_{\mathbb{R}}(Y^{\prime})=dim_{\mathbb{R}}(T_{Y^{\prime}})\leq dim_{\mathbb{R}}T_{Y}.

​
4.​

Let
Y\subset X
be a fast cycle. Suppose
Y
admits a hornic linkwise-retraction to
Y^{\prime}\subset X,
with
dim_{\mathbb{R}}Y^{\prime}=1+l,
and this
l
is the minimal possible.
Then
Y
is called
a fast cycle of dimension
l
.

A fast cycle whose link is
S^{1}
is called
a fast loop
.

​
5.​

The exponent of a fast cycle is
dif\!f.ord_{X}(Y,T_{Y}),
see ​(
1
).

A remark: these properties depend on the (subanalytic) inner-Lipschitz type of
X
only, not on a particular embedding
X\hookrightarrow(\mathbb{R}^{N},o).

.

i.

is hornic, then

In particular, if

then

is a thin set.

A simple fast loop is the horn

with

considered inside

Note that

is contractible inside

but not hornically.

A simple fast cycle with

iii.

.

.

Example 2.13

Proof.
The locus

consists of 2 lines and a parabola. At each point of

3.
Detecting fast loops via the discriminant of covering

3.1.
The covering data

Take a reduced complex-analytic germ
X\!\subset\!(\mathbb{C}^{n+c}\!,\!o),
possibly with

non-isolated singularity, not necessarily a complete intersection.
Take a convenient covering, of degree
p.
Take the discriminant
\Delta
with its reduced structure.
Below

and we assume:
\Delta\subset(\mathbb{C}^{2},o)
is a curve germ, see §
2.2
.

\begin{matrix}(\mathbb{C}^{n+c},o)\supset X\supset Crit(\pi)\\
\quad\quad\searrow\quad\swarrow\pi\quad\swarrow\pi_{|}\quad\\
\quad\quad(\mathbb{C}^{n},o)\supset\Delta\ \ \underbrace{=\cup\Delta_{k}}_{for\ n=2}\end{matrix}

•

Take the tangential decomposition

Here

is one (possibly multiple) complex line, and

for

.
(But each

for some

•

For each

\Delta_{k}=\cup\Delta_{k,i}.
They are all tangent,
their common tangent (complex) line is

For each
\Delta_{k,i}
let

be the total ramification index of the covering
\pi:X\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},o)

over
\Delta_{k,i}.
Namely,

for

For generic projections, and

But we often take convenient non-generic projections.

•

Blowup the origin, take the strict transforms and exceptional divisors, see the diagram.

Note that the curve germs
\{{\tilde{\Delta}}_{j}\}
are now disjoint.

Fix some

its tangent cone

is just one line. Thus the intersection
{\tilde{\Delta}}_{k}\cap\mathbb{P}^{1}

The fibre
{\tilde{\pi}}^{-1}(o_{k})\subset{\tilde{X}}
is a finite set of points, of cardinality

\begin{matrix}\hskip 17.07182pt\mathbb{P}^{1+c}\subset Bl_{o}(\mathbb{C}^{2+k},o)\supset{\tilde{X}}\supset{\tilde{\pi}}^{-1}(o_{k})\\
\hskip 85.35826pt\rotatebox{20.0}{$\longleftarrow$}{\tilde{\pi}}\hskip 34.14322pt\searrow\\
\quad\quad\mathbb{P}^{1}\subset Bl_{o}(\mathbb{C}^{2},o)\supset{\tilde{\Delta}}=\coprod{\tilde{\Delta}}_{j}\ni o_{k}\end{matrix}

Some of them can be not on a critical locus.

•

Accordingly

is a (reduced) multi-germ. The number of its connected components is just
\sharp{\tilde{\pi}}^{-1}(o_{k}).

Each connected component of

can be further reducible (even if

is irreducible), see Example

.
Denote the total number of the connected components of the multi-germ

Thus

In Step 2 of the proof of theorem

also as the number of conected components of

where

is a small section transversal to some

Example 3.1
.

i.

If

has an isolated singularity, then

is smooth. Then

coincides with the total number of

ii.

Cases with

occur often, even if

is irreducible, with an isolated singularity. As the simplest case consider the surface germ
X=V(z^{2}-f(x,y))\subset(\mathbb{C}^{3},o).
Suppose

and moreover

contains the monomial

Blowup the origin, the space

Take the chart with

, its coordinates are

(Thus

)
The strict transform

is then defined by
\sigma^{2}_{z}+x^{2d-2}(1+g(x,\sigma_{y}))

This surface germ is reducible, hence

3.2.
Fast loop criterion for surface germs

(Keeping the notations of §
3.1
.)

Theorem 3.2
.

The germ
X
has no fast loops iff one the following holds:

•

either
\Delta
is one point or a curve germ that is an ordinary multiple point (i.e. all its branches are smooth and pairwise non-tangent);

•

or
\Delta
is a curve germ and
each non-smooth tangential component
\Delta_{k}\subset\Delta
(with
mult(\Delta_{k})\geq 2
and the data
\Delta_{k}=\cup_{i}\Delta_{k,i}
,
q_{i}
,
r_{k}
, as above) satisfies:

p>r_{k}-1+\sum_{i}q_{i}\cdot mult(\Delta_{k,i}).

Proof.
We realize
Link[X]
as
X\cap V(|x_{1}|^{2}+|x_{2}|^{2}-\epsilon),\ 0<\epsilon\ll 1
, by the convenience of the covering.

A fast loop (if any) must lie in a hornic neighborhood of the critical locus,
Crit(\pi)\subset X.

Therefore in the case “
\Delta
is one point” (and hence
Crit(\pi)
is a finite set) there can be no fast loops.

Below we assume that the critical locus is a curve germ, and hence also
\Delta.

Then the image of a fast loop must lie in a hornic neighborhood
\mathcal{U}(\Delta_{k})
for some tangential component
\Delta_{k}\subset\Delta.
Moreover, this image cannot lie in
\mathcal{U}(\Delta_{k})
for a smooth component
\Delta_{k}.
Indeed, if
mult(\Delta_{k})=1,
then each component of
X
lying over
\mathcal{U}(\Delta_{k})
is
linkwise-contractible, see definition
2.10
.
Therefore below we consider only tangential components with
ord\ \Delta_{k}\geq 2.

•

In Step 1 we show:
Link[X]
contains a non-contractible loop “over
\mathcal{U}(\Delta_{k})
” iff
X
has a fast loop in a hornic neighborhood of
\pi^{-1}\Delta_{k}.

Here the direction
\Lleftarrow
is immediate.

•

In Step 2 we translate the condition “
Link[X]
contains a non-contractible loop over
\mathcal{U}(\Delta_{k})
”
into “
p\leq r_{k}-1+\sum_{i}q_{i}\cdot mult(\Delta_{k,i})
”.

Step 1.

Take the
\hat{x}_{1}
-axis as the tangent of
\Delta_{k}\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}_{x_{1}x_{2}}.

We work hornically near
\Delta_{k}
.
For each
0<t_{o}\ll 1
take
the section
V(x_{1}-t_{o})\cap\Delta_{k}
. These are several points, their number is
mult(\Delta_{k})
.

As the parameter
t\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
goes to zero these points draw the subanalytic arcs,
\{\gamma_{i}\}_{i}\subset\Delta_{k}\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
.
Each of these arcs has the length-parametrization
(t,g_{i}(t))
,
here
g_{i}(t)=o(t)
because
T_{\Delta_{k}}=Span_{\mathbb{C}}(\hat{x}_{1})
. In particular, these arcs
\{\gamma_{i}\}
are all tangent in
(\mathbb{C}^{2},o).

For each pair of these arcs we take the Hölder triangle, as in (
7
),

(8)

H\ddot{o}ld[\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j}]:=\cup_{s\in[0,1]}\big[s\cdot\gamma_{i}+(1-s)\cdot\gamma_{j}\big]:=\cup_{s\in[0,1]}\big[t,s\cdot g_{i}(t)+(1-s)\cdot g_{j}(t)\big]\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}.

This defines the tangent foliation on each
H\ddot{o}ld[\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j}].

Indeed, any two arcs
\gamma_{s},\gamma_{s^{\prime}}\subset H\ddot{o}ld[\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j}]
intersect at
o
only and are tangent.
(Because
g_{i}(t)=g_{j}(t)
occurs when either
i=j
or
t=0
.)

The union
\mathbb{C}^{2}\supset H\ddot{o}ld:=\cup_{i\neq j}H\ddot{o}ld[\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j}]

is the “Hölder polyhedron”. This is a subanalytic set and its tangent cone is the real line
Span_{\mathbb{R}}(\hat{x}_{1}).

Its link is connected and can be realized as
H\ddot{o}ld\cap V(x_{1}-t_{o}).

The lifted Hölder polyhedron is the full pre-image:

(9)

H\ddot{o}ld^{X}:=\pi^{-1}H\ddot{o}ld=\cup_{i\neq j}\pi^{-1}(H\ddot{o}ld[\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j}])\subset X.

Puncturing at
o
we get several connected components,
H\ddot{o}ld^{X}\setminus o=\coprod_{l}(H\ddot{o}ld^{X}_{l}\setminus o).

Each
H\ddot{o}ld^{X}_{l}
is a (real) subanalytic surface, foliated by the preimages of the foliations on
H\ddot{o}ld[\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j}]
for all
i,j.

And
Link[H\ddot{o}ld^{X}_{l}]
is connected.
Lemma
2.6
gives: the tangent cone of each
H\ddot{o}ld^{X}_{l}
is a real line, and on each
H\ddot{o}ld^{X}_{l}
the arcs of our foliation are inner-tangent.

Inside the complex line
V(x_{1}-t_{o})\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}

take an open ball
Ball
satisfying:

(10)

\Delta_{k}\cap V(x_{1}-t_{o})\subset Ball\quad\text{ and }\quad(\Delta\setminus\Delta_{k})\cap Ball=\varnothing.

Such a ball exists for
0<t_{o}\ll 1
as every branch of
\Delta\setminus\Delta_{k}
is non-tangent to
\Delta_{k}.

Suppose the full preimage
\pi^{-1}Ball\subset X\cap V(x_{1}-t_{o})
contains a (compact, connected) non-contractible loop
Z
.
By deforming/shrinking
Z\subset\pi^{-1}Ball
we can assume
\pi(Z)\subset H\ddot{o}ld\cap Ball.

Indeed, fix a simply-connected path
\eta\subset H\ddot{o}ld\cap Ball
that visits all the points of
\Delta_{k}\cap V(x_{1}-t_{o}).

The retraction
Ball\rightsquigarrow\eta
lifts to
\pi^{-1}Ball\rightsquigarrow\pi^{-1}\eta.

Therefore
Z
lies inside
H\ddot{o}ld^{X}\cap V(x_{1}-t_{o}).
Then it lies inside a connected component,

Z\subset H\ddot{o}ld^{X}_{l}\cap V(x_{1}-t_{o})
for some
l.
Finally, one can assume:
Z
is subanalytic, §
1.5
.v.

Using the (inner-tangent) foliation on that component
H\ddot{o}ld^{X}_{l}
we get the subanalytic surface germ

(11)

\mathbb{R}_{>0}Z:=\cup_{z\in Z}\gamma_{z}\subset H\ddot{o}ld^{X}_{l}\subset X.

Its tangent cone is a real half-line, it lies inside the tangent cone of
H\ddot{o}ld^{X}_{l}.

Thus
\mathbb{R}_{>0}Z
is a thin germ.
By our construction the germ
\mathbb{R}_{>0}Z
is not linkwise-retractible inside
\pi^{-1}Cone[Ball],
see §
2.6
. Hence it is not linkwise retractible inside any hornic neighborhood of
T_{\mathbb{R}_{>0}Z}

Therefore
\mathbb{R}_{>0}Z
is the promised fast loop.

Step 2.

It remains to check whether a non-contractible (connected, compact) loop
Z\subset\pi^{-1}Ball
exists. Namely (in terms of the first homology), whether
h^{1}(\pi^{-1}Ball)>0.

Therefore it remains to show the equivalence:

(12)

h^{1}(\pi^{-1}Ball)>0\quad\quad\quad\text{ if and only if }\quad\quad\quad p\leq r_{k}-1+\sum_{i}q_{i}\cdot mult(\Delta_{k,i}).

Thus we compute the Euler characteristic
\chi(\pi^{-1}Ball)
and the number of connected components

h^{0}(\pi^{-1}Ball).

•

The map
X\supset\pi^{-1}Ball\ \lower 0.86108pt\hbox{$\mathrel{\mathop{\to}\limits^{p:1}}$}\ Ball\subset V(x_{1}-t_{o})\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
is a covering of complex analytic curves.
If
X
has a non-isolated singularity then the complex-analytic curve
\pi^{-1}(Ball)
can be singular.
The covering is ramified over
the discriminantal points
\Delta_{k}\cap V(x_{1}-t_{o})
.
For the branch decomposition
\Delta_{k}=\cup\Delta_{k,i}
we get
\sharp|\Delta_{k,i}\cap V(x_{1}-t_{o})|=mult(\Delta_{k,i})
, and these points are of ramification index
q_{i}
.
Riemann-Hurwitz formula applied to this covering gives:

(13)

\chi(\pi^{-1}Ball)=p\cdot\chi(Ball)-\sum q_{j}=p-\sum q_{i}\cdot mult(\Delta_{k,i}).

•

We compute the number of connected components:
h^{0}(\pi^{-1}Ball)\!=\!r_{k}.
Blowup the

origin to get the diagram, as in §
3.1
. Take the intersection point

o_{k}\!:={\tilde{\Delta}}\!\cap\!\mathbb{P}^{1}\!\cap\!\widetilde{Cone[Ball]}\!=\!{\tilde{\Delta}}_{k}\!\cap\!\mathbb{P}^{1}.

Take the pre-images
{\tilde{\pi}}^{-1}(o_{k})\subset\mathbb{P}T_{X}\subset{\tilde{X}}.

\begin{matrix}\coprod{\tilde{\Delta}}_{k}\subset Bl(\mathbb{C}^{2},o)&\ \lower 0.86108pt\hbox{$\mathrel{\mathop{\leftarrow}\limits^{{\tilde{\pi}}}}$}\ {\tilde{X}}\subset&Bl_{o}(\mathbb{C}^{N},o)\\
\downarrow\hskip 36.98866pt\downarrow\sigma&\downarrow&\downarrow\\
\cup\Delta_{k}\subset(\mathbb{C}^{2},o)&\ \lower 0.86108pt\hbox{$\mathrel{\mathop{\leftarrow}\limits^{\pi}}$}\ X\subset&(\mathbb{C}^{N},o)\end{matrix}

We get the multi-germ
({\tilde{X}},{\tilde{\pi}}^{-1}(o_{k})).

By definition,
r_{k}
is the number of connected components of

({\tilde{X}},{\tilde{\pi}}^{-1}(o_{k}))\setminus\mathbb{P}T_{X}=:\coprod{\tilde{Y}}_{l}.
Here each
{\tilde{Y}}_{l}
is a connected (locally-closed) complex-analytic surface.

Starting from
Ball\subset V(x_{1}-t_{o})\subset\mathbb{C}^{2},
we get
(\sigma\circ{\tilde{\pi}})^{-1}Ball\subset\widetilde{V(x_{1}-t_{o})}\cap{\tilde{X}}.

As we work with the multi-germ
({\tilde{X}},{\tilde{\pi}}^{-1}(o_{k}))
we can assume:

(\sigma\circ{\tilde{\pi}})[\widetilde{V(x_{1}-t_{o})}\cap(\coprod{\tilde{Y}}_{l})]\subset Ball.

Therefore we can ignore all the components of
\Delta\setminus\Delta_{k},
i.e. we can assume
\Delta=\Delta_{k}.

It is enough to prove:
{\tilde{Y}}_{l}\cap((\sigma\circ{\tilde{\pi}})^{-1}Ball)
is connected for each
l.

This will imply: the number of connected components of
\coprod{\tilde{Y}}_{l}\cap((\sigma\circ{\tilde{\pi}})^{-1}Ball)
coincides with the number of connected components of
\coprod{\tilde{Y}}_{l}.
And therefore:

(14)

h^{0}(\pi^{-1}Ball)=h^{0}((\sigma\circ{\tilde{\pi}})^{-1}Ball)=h^{0}((\coprod{\tilde{Y}}_{l})\cap((\sigma\circ{\tilde{\pi}})^{-1}Ball))=h^{0}(\coprod{\tilde{Y}}_{l})=r_{k}.

Fix two points,
\tilde{p}_{1},\tilde{p}_{2}\in{\tilde{Y}}_{l}\cap(\sigma\circ{\tilde{\pi}})^{-1}Ball.
We can assume (w.l.o.g.)
\tilde{p}_{1},\tilde{p}_{2}\not\in Crit({\tilde{\pi}}).

Take a path
[\tilde{p}_{1}\ \lower 0.86108pt\hbox{$\mathrel{\mathop{\rightsquigarrow}\limits^{\tilde{\eta}}}$}\ \tilde{p}_{2}]\subset{\tilde{Y}}_{l}.
We want to deformation-retract
\tilde{\eta}
into a path inside

{\tilde{Y}}_{l}\cap(\sigma\circ{\tilde{\pi}})^{-1}Ball.
Take the image
\eta:=(\sigma\circ{\tilde{\pi}})(\tilde{\eta})\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}\setminus\{o\}.
It connects the points

\sigma({\tilde{\pi}}(\tilde{p}_{1})),\sigma({\tilde{\pi}}(\tilde{p}_{2}))\in Ball.

i.

Suppose
\tilde{\eta}\cap Crit({\tilde{\pi}})=\varnothing.
Then we can assume
\eta\cap\Delta=\varnothing,
i.e.
\eta\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}\setminus\Delta.
Then
\eta
can be deformation-retracted (inside
\mathbb{C}^{2}\setminus\Delta
, and preserving the end-points) to a path inside

This deformation lifts uniquely to the deformation of
\tilde{\eta}
inside
{\tilde{X}}\setminus Crit({\tilde{\pi}})
, while preserving the points
\tilde{p}_{1},\tilde{p}_{2}.
The deformed path,

[\tilde{p}_{1}\ \lower 0.86108pt\hbox{$\mathrel{\mathop{\rightsquigarrow}\limits^{\tilde{\eta}_{1}}}$}\ \tilde{p}_{2}]
lies fully inside
{\tilde{Y}}_{l}\cap(\sigma\circ{\tilde{\pi}})^{-1}Ball.

ii.

The path
\tilde{\eta}
can happen to intersect the critical locus

Moreover,
\tilde{\eta}
can be non-deformable off

E.g. let

be connected but reducible, with
\tilde{p}_{1},\tilde{p}_{2}
lying in distinct components. Then
\tilde{\eta}
inevitably intersects the locus

We claim:
\tilde{\eta}
can be deformed (inside
{\tilde{Y}}_{l}
) to a path
[\tilde{p}_{1}\ \lower 0.86108pt\hbox{$\mathrel{\mathop{\rightsquigarrow}\limits^{\tilde{\eta}_{1}}}$}\ \tilde{p}_{2}]

Indeed, we can assume (w.l.o.g) that

is a finite number of points. Therefore the set

is finite. Over each such intersection point we can slightly deform
\tilde{\eta}
to get: the intersection

is transverse.

Now we slide-move all the points of
\eta\cap\Delta
(along
\Delta
) into

This deformation lifts uniquely to a deformation of
\tilde{\eta}.
(Because
\Delta\setminus o
is a smooth curve and
\eta\pitchfork\Delta.
)

Finally, having reached the path with

one applies part i. to all the connected components of
\tilde{\eta}_{1}\setminus(\tilde{\eta}_{1}\cap Crit({\tilde{\pi}})).

We have proved:
h^{1}(\pi^{-1}Ball)=r_{k}-p+\sum q_{i}\cdot mult(\Delta_{k,i}).

This gives equation (

)
Hence the statement.

\blacksquare

3.2.3.
An example: germs of type
{V(z^{p}+za_{1}(x,y)+a_{0}(x,y))\!\subset\!(\mathbb{C}^{3},o),\ p\!\geq\!3}

Here
ord(a_{o})\!\geq\!p,

ord(a_{1})\!\geq\!p-1.

By §
2.4
we get a simple expression for the discriminant,

\Delta=(\frac{a_{0}(x,y)}{p-1})^{p-1}-(\frac{a_{1}(x,y)}{p})^{p}\in\mathbb{C}\{x,y\}.
This can be not square-free.
Denote its reduced version by
\Delta_{red}.
Take the lowest order terms,
l.t.(\Delta),
resp.
l.t.(\Delta_{red}).
These homogeneous polynomials define the tangent-cone
of
V(\Delta),
resp. of
V(\Delta_{red}).
Blowup the origin,
\mathbb{P}^{2}\subset Bl(\mathbb{C}^{3},o)\to(\mathbb{C}^{3},o),
and take the strict transform
{\tilde{X}}\to X.

Corollary 3.7
.

​
1.​

Suppose
a_{1}=0.
Then
X
has no fast loops iff
V(a_{o})\subset(\mathbb{C}^{2},o)
is set-theoretically an ordinary multiple point.

​
2.​

Suppose
a_{1}\neq 0.
Factorize the lowest order terms,
l.t.(\Delta)=\prod l_{k}(x,y)^{m_{k}},
resp.
l.t.(\Delta_{red})=\prod l_{k}(x,y)^{m_{k,red}}.

Then
X
has no fast loops iff for each
k
with
m_{k,red}\geq 2
the following conditions hold:

i.

m_{k}\leq p.

ii.

The total number of irreducible components of the analytic germ
{\tilde{X}}
at all the points of

{\tilde{X}}\cap\mathbb{P}^{2}\cap\widetilde{V(l_{k})}
is at most
p-m_{k}.

A remark: in most cases already the condition 2.i. is violated, i.e. one has
m_{k}>p.

Proof.
In both cases we should verify the bound
p>r_{k}-1+\sum_{i}q_{i}\cdot mult(\Delta_{k,i})
of theorem
3.2
.

​
1.​

If
a_{1}=0
then the covering
X\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},o)
is totally ramified over the curve
V(a_{o}(x,y))\subset(\mathbb{C}^{2},o).

The total ramification index is then
q_{i}=p-1,
and
r_{k}\leq p.

Then the bound holds iff
mult(\Delta_{k})=1
for all
k.

​
2.​

In this case
m_{k,red}=mult(\Delta_{k})
and

\sum_{i}q_{i}\cdot mult(\Delta_{k,i})=m_{k}.
Thus the necessary and sufficient condition to be IMC, i.e.
p>r_{k}-1+m_{k},
means exactly the conditions i. and ii.

\blacksquare

Already in this (very particular) case we get a vast amount of IMC’s.

Example 3.8
.

Let
X=V(z^{p}+za_{1}(x,y)+a_{0}(x,y))\subset(\mathbb{C}^{3},o),
with

p\geq 3,
and an isolated singularity. Suppose the power series
a_{0}(x,y),a_{1}(x,y)
are co-prime.

•

Suppose
\frac{ord(a_{1}(x,y))}{p-1}>\frac{ord(a_{0}(x,y))}{p}.
Then
l.t.(\Delta)=l.t.(a_{0}(x,y)^{p-1}).
Thus

X
is IMC iff the (set-theoretic) curve germ
V(a_{0}(x,y))\subset(\mathbb{C}^{2},o)
is an ordinary multiple point.

•

Suppose
\frac{ord(a_{1}(x,y))}{p-1}<\frac{ord(a_{0}(x,y))}{p}.
Then
l.t.(\Delta)=l.t.(a_{1}(x,y)^{p}).

Thus
X
is IMC iff the (set-theoretic) curve germ
V(a_{1}(x,y))\subset(\mathbb{C}^{2},o)
is an ordinary multiple point.

3.3.
Fast loops imposed by singular points of the tangent cone

Suppose a surface germ
X\subset(\mathbb{C}^{2+c},o)

is a strictly complete intersection with
isolated
singularity, §
2.1.2
.
The projectivized tangent cone is then a (globally) complete intersection curve,
\mathbb{P}T_{X}\subset\mathbb{P}^{1+c}\subset Bl_{o}(\mathbb{C}^{2+c},o).

It can be reducible, non-reduced, i.e. can have multiple components.
Occasionally we take the underlying set-germ (with reduced structure),
\mathbb{P}T^{red}_{X}.

One has the total degree,
deg[\mathbb{P}T_{X}]=p,
and the set-theoretic degree,

deg[\mathbb{P}T^{red}_{X}]\leq p.

Call a point
pt\in\mathbb{P}T_{X}

reduced
if the curve germ
(\mathbb{P}T_{X},pt)
is reduced.
Thus
(\mathbb{P}T_{X},pt)
is an ICIS, and its Milnor number,
\mu(\mathbb{P}T_{X},pt),
is well defined.

Call a hyperplane
\mathbb{P}^{c}\subset\mathbb{P}^{1+c}\subset Bl_{o}(\mathbb{C}^{2+c},o)

non-tangent
to the curve
\mathbb{P}T_{X}
if
\mathbb{P}^{c}
is transverse to the tangent cone of the germ
(\mathbb{P}T_{X},pt)
at each intersection point
pt\in\mathbb{P}^{c}\cap\mathbb{P}T_{X}.

Example 3.9
.

i.

The generic hyperplane
\mathbb{P}^{c}\subset\mathbb{P}^{1+c}
is non-tangent. It intersects
\mathbb{P}T^{red}_{X}
transversally and only at smooth points of
\mathbb{P}T^{red}_{X}.

ii.

Suppose the germ
(\mathbb{P}T_{X},pt)
is reduced and singular.
Among all the hyperplanes through
pt,
take the generic one. It is non-tangent to
\mathbb{P}T_{X}.

iii.

More generally, take some reduced singular points,
pt_{1},\dots,pt_{j}\in Sing(\mathbb{P}T_{X}),
with
j\leq c.
The linear system of hyperplanes through these points has dimension
\geq c+1-j.

And the generic member of this family is non-tangent to
\mathbb{P}T_{X}
at the points
pt_{1},\dots,pt_{j},
unless these points are in degenerate position with respect to the curve
\mathbb{P}T_{X}.

Theorem 3.10
.

​
1.​

If
X
is IMC and the curve
\mathbb{P}T_{X}
is reduced then
\mathbb{P}T_{X}
is smooth. (I.e.
T_{X}
has an isolated singularity, i.e.
X
is a “generalized ordinary multiple point”.)

​
2.​

Suppose a hyperplane
\mathbb{P}^{c}\subset\mathbb{P}^{1+c}
is non-tangent to
\mathbb{P}T_{X},
and contains reduced singular points
\{pt_{i}\}
of the curve
\mathbb{P}T_{X}.

If their Milnor numbers satisfy
\sum\mu(\mathbb{P}T_{X},pt_{i})\geq 1+deg[\mathbb{P}T_{X}]-deg[\mathbb{P}T^{red}_{X}],
then
X
has a fast loop.

In particular, if
\mathbb{P}T_{X}=\mathbb{P}T_{X}^{red}
, any (reduced) singular point of
\mathbb{P}T_{X}\cap\mathbb{P}^{c}
produces a fast loop.

The converse to part 1 is Example 3.8 in
[
Kerner-Mendes.23
]
.

Proof.
Part 1 follows straight from Part 2. Part 2 is proved in steps.

•

Take the (convenient) projection
\mathbb{C}^{2+c}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
associated to
\mathbb{P}^{c}.
Then
\mathbb{P}^{c}
is sent to a point
o_{k}\in\mathbb{P}^{1}\subset Bl_{o}(\mathbb{C}^{2},o).

Take the corresponding tangential component of the discriminant,
\Delta_{k}\subseteq\Delta.

To use theorem
3.2

we verify the condition
p\leq r_{k}-1+\sum q_{i}\cdot mult(\Delta_{k,i}).

First we bound
r_{k}\geq\sharp{\tilde{\pi}}^{-1}(o_{k}).

•

We express
\sum q_{i}\cdot mult(\Delta_{k,i})
as the multiplicity of the polar curve, and use the formula of §
2.4.2
.

Step 1.

The projection
(\mathbb{C}^{2+c},o)\ \lower 0.86108pt\hbox{$\mathrel{\mathop{\to}\limits^{\pi}}$}\ (\mathbb{C}^{2}_{x_{1}x_{2}},o)
lifts to

Bl_{o}(\mathbb{C}^{2+c},o)\supset\mathbb{P}^{1+c}\setminus\widetilde{V(x_{1},x_{2})}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}\subset Bl_{o}(\mathbb{C}^{2},o).
By a preliminary
GL(\mathbb{C}^{2+c})
transformation one can assume:

(15)

\mathbb{P}^{c}=\widetilde{V(x_{1})}\cap\mathbb{P}^{1+c},\quad\quad\widetilde{V(x_{1},x_{2})}\cap\mathbb{P}T_{X}=\varnothing,

\hskip 56.9055pt\text{the intersection }\widetilde{V(a_{1}x_{1}+a_{2}x_{2})}\cap\mathbb{P}T_{X}\text{ is finite for all}[a_{1}:a_{2}]\in\mathbb{P}^{1}.

Indeed, once the first condition is satisfied, one uses the remaining freedom
x_{i}\to x_{i}+l_{i}(x_{1}\dots x_{2+c})
for
i=2,\dots,2+c,
and generic linear forms
\{l_{i}\}.

Thus
\pi
sends
\mathbb{P}^{c}\setminus\widetilde{V(x_{1},x_{2})}
to the point
o_{k}:=[0:1]\in\mathbb{P}^{1}.
We get the diagram:

(16)

\begin{matrix}&&(\mathbb{C}^{2+c},o)&\leftarrow&Bl_{o}(\mathbb{C}^{2+c},o)&\supset&\mathbb{P}^{1+c}\\
&&\cup&&\cup&&\cup\\
Crit(\pi)&\subset&X&\leftarrow&{\tilde{X}}&\supset&\mathbb{P}T_{X}&\supset&{\tilde{\pi}}^{-1}(o_{k})&\supseteq\{pt_{i}\}\\
\downarrow&&\pi\downarrow&&{\tilde{\pi}}\downarrow&&\downarrow{\tilde{\pi}}_{|}&&\downarrow\\
\Delta&\subset&(\mathbb{C}^{2},o)&\leftarrow&Bl_{o}(\mathbb{C}^{2},o)&\supset&\mathbb{P}^{1}&\ni&o_{k}&\end{matrix}

Here are the needed properties of this diagram.

i.

The projection
\pi
is convenient, as
T_{X}\cap V(x_{1},x_{2})=(o),
as
\widetilde{V(x_{1},x_{2})}\cap\mathbb{P}T_{X}=\varnothing.

ii.

The projection
{\tilde{\pi}}
is finite, as
\pi
is convenient. (By part 1 of Lemma
2.2
.)

iii.

The curve

\mathbb{P}T_{X}\subset\mathbb{P}^{1+c}
is a complete intersection of the (total) degree
p.

Therefore,
{\tilde{\pi}}_{|}
is a (flat, ramified)
p:1
covering. (Part 2 of Lemma
2.2
.)

iv.

The fibre
{\tilde{\pi}}^{-1}(o_{k})\subset\mathbb{P}T_{X}
contains the reduced singular points
\{pt_{i}\}.

Its cardinality is bounded,
\sharp{\tilde{\pi}}^{-1}(o_{k})\leq p-\sum_{\tilde{o}_{j}\in{\tilde{\pi}}^{-1}(o_{k})}(mult(\mathbb{P}T_{X},\tilde{o}_{j})-1).

As the hyperplane
\mathbb{P}^{c}
is non-tangent to
\mathbb{P}T_{X},
this bound is an equality.

Altogether, we get:

(17)

r_{k}\geq\sharp{\tilde{\pi}}^{-1}(o_{k})=p-\sum_{\tilde{o}_{j}\in{\tilde{\pi}}^{-1}(o_{k})}(mult(\mathbb{P}T_{X},\tilde{o}_{j})-1)=

\hskip 199.16928pt=p-\sum(mult(\mathbb{P}T_{X},pt_{i})-1)-deg[\mathbb{P}T_{X}]+deg[\mathbb{P}T^{red}_{X}].

v.

The projection
Crit(\pi)\to\Delta
lifts to the (finite) projection

Crit({\tilde{\pi}})\to{\tilde{\Delta}}\cup\mathbb{P}^{1}.
Here
{\tilde{X}}
is locally-complete intersection, thus
Crit({\tilde{\pi}})
as well (part 1 of Lemma
2.3
).

We claim:
Crit({\tilde{\pi}})\!=\!\widetilde{Crit(\pi)}\!\cup\!Non.Red(\mathbb{P}T_{X}),
the non-reduced components of
\mathbb{P}T_{X}.

Indeed,
{\tilde{\pi}}
is not a local isomorphism at any point of
Non.Red(\mathbb{P}T_{X}).
(Because the local ramification index of
{\tilde{\pi}}
at each point of
Non.Red(\mathbb{P}T_{X})
is the multiplicity of
Non.Red(\mathbb{P}T_{X}),
hence
\geq 2.
)
Vice versa,
Crit({\tilde{\pi}})
is a locally complete intersection curve,
thus
\overline{Crit({\tilde{\pi}})\setminus Non.Red(\mathbb{P}T_{X})}
is of pure dimension 1.
Hence, besides
\widetilde{Crit(\pi)},
it can consist only of
some irreducible components of
\mathbb{P}T_{X}.

But the restriction of
{\tilde{\pi}}
to each irreducible, reduced component of
\mathbb{P}T_{X}
is a local isomorphism (over
\mathbb{P}^{1}
) except at a finite number of points.

Step 2.

Take the image
{\tilde{\Delta}}_{k}:={\tilde{\pi}}(Crit({\tilde{\pi}}),\pi^{-1}(o_{k}))\subset(Bl_{o}(\mathbb{C}^{2},o),o_{k}).
This is the strict transform of
\Delta_{k}\subset(\mathbb{C}^{2},o),

a tangential component of
\Delta.

Take the coordinate change
\Phi:x\to x+h(x),
where the terms
h(x)=(h_{1}(x),\dots,h_{2+c}(x))
of order two and generic.
This lifts to an automorphism
\tilde{\Phi}\circlearrowright Bl_{o}(\mathbb{C}^{2+c},o),
with
\tilde{\Phi}|_{\mathbb{P}^{1+c}}=Id.
(Because
\Phi^{\prime}|_{o}=Id.
)
Therefore
\tilde{\Phi}
preserves
o_{k}
, and all the properties of Step 1 are preserved. And applying
\Phi
the covering

X\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},o)
becomes generic in the following sense:
q_{i}=1
for all
i.

Therefore
\sum_{i}q_{i}\cdot mult(\Delta_{k,i})=mult(\Delta_{k},o),

and we compute this multiplicity.

Pass from
(\mathbb{C}^{2},o)
to
Bl_{o}(\mathbb{C}^{2},o)
by

mult(\Delta_{k})=\langle{\tilde{\Delta}}_{k},\mathbb{P}^{1}\rangle.
By our choice of coordinates,
o_{k}=[0:1]\in\mathbb{P}^{1},
i.e. the tangent line of
\Delta_{k}
is
Span(\hat{x}_{2}).

Thus at any point of
{\tilde{\pi}}^{-1}(o_{k})
the exceptional divisor is defined locally by
x_{2}=0.

Then
\langle{\tilde{\Delta}}_{k},\mathbb{P}^{1}\rangle=\langle{\tilde{\Delta}}_{k},\widetilde{V(x_{2}-t_{o})}\rangle,
as
{\tilde{\Delta}}_{k}\subset Bl_{o}(\mathbb{C}^{2},o)
is a hypersurface.
Take the multi-germ
Crit_{k}({\tilde{\pi}}):=(Crit({\tilde{\pi}}),{\tilde{\pi}}^{-1}(o_{k})).

Then

(18)

mult(\Delta_{k})=\langle Crit_{k}({\tilde{\pi}}),\widetilde{V(x_{2}-t_{o})}\rangle\geq\langle\overline{Crit_{k}({\tilde{\pi}})\setminus\mathbb{P}T_{X}},\mathbb{P}^{1+c}\rangle\geq\sum\langle(Crit({\tilde{\pi}}),pt_{i}),\mathbb{P}^{1+c}\rangle.

Both inequalities hold here as
Crit({\tilde{\pi}})
(with its scheme structure) is a locally complete intersection (lemma
2.3
).

At the chosen reduced singular points,
pt_{i}\in\mathbb{P}T_{X}\cap\mathbb{P}^{c},

one has:

\langle Crit({\tilde{\pi}}),\mathbb{P}^{1+c}\rangle_{pt_{i}}=\mu_{i}+mult(\mathbb{P}T_{X},pt_{i})-1.
(Apply §
2.4.2
to the reduced curve germ with
(\mathbb{P}T_{X},pt).
)

Altogether we have:
mult(\Delta_{k})\!\geq\!\sum(\mu_{i}+mult(\mathbb{P}T_{X},pt_{i})-1).
In particular
mult(\Delta_{k})\!\geq\!2.

Combine this with (
17
) to get:

r_{k}+mult(\Delta_{k})-p\geq\sum\mu_{i}-deg[\mathbb{P}T_{X}]+deg[\mathbb{P}T^{red}_{X}].

Together with the assumption
\sum\mu_{i}\geq 1+deg[\mathbb{P}T_{X}]-deg[\mathbb{P}T^{red}_{X}]
we get:

r_{k}+\sum q_{i}\cdot mult(\Delta_{k,i})\geq p+1.
Hence the statement.

\blacksquare

.

For any super-isolated surface singularity in

X

]

.

.

3.4.
Detecting fast loops in higher dimensions,
n\geq 3

Let
X\subset(\mathbb{C}^{N},o)
be as in §
1.5
.i, with an arbitrary singularity.
Take a convenient covering,
(\mathbb{C}^{N},o)\supset X\to(\mathbb{C}^{n},o)\supset\Delta.

We assume
dim_{\mathbb{C}}\Delta=n-1,
e.g. this holds if
X
is a complete intersection.

Fix a smooth surface germ
\mathcal{S}\subset(\mathbb{C}^{n},o)
, and
(set-theoretically) restrict the covering,
X\cap\pi^{-1}\mathcal{S}\ \lower 0.86108pt\hbox{$\mathrel{\mathop{\to}\limits^{\pi|_{\mathcal{S}}}}$}\ \mathcal{S}\subset(\mathbb{C}^{n},o).

Then
X\cap\pi^{-1}\mathcal{S}
is a (pure dimensional, possibly reducible) surface, and the projection
\pi|_{\mathcal{S}}
is still a convenient covering (see §
2.2
), of degree
p
.
Now
\pi|_{\mathcal{S}}
is ramified over
the curve germ
\Delta|_{\mathcal{S}}:=\Delta\cap\mathcal{S}.

As in §
3.1
we take a tangential component,
\Delta|_{\mathcal{S}}\rightsquigarrow\Delta_{k}=\cup(\Delta_{k,i},o),
and the corresponding covering data
r_{k},

\{q_{i}\},

\{mult(\Delta_{k,i})\}.

Observe that the embedded topological type of the planar curve germ
\Delta|_{\mathcal{S}}\subset\mathcal{S}\cong(\mathbb{C}^{2},o)
can vary when the section
\mathcal{S}\subset(\mathbb{C}^{n},o)
varies.

Proposition 3.14
.

Suppose the section
\Delta|_{\mathcal{S}}
has a tangential component
\Delta_{k}
with

mult(\Delta_{k})\geq 2.
Suppose any deformation
\mathcal{S}(t)
of the surface
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}(o)
that preserves the tangent plane

T_{\mathcal{S}}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
results in an equisingular family of the planar curve germs,
\Delta_{k}(t)\subset\mathcal{S}(t).

If
p\leq r_{k}-1+\sum_{i}q_{i}\cdot mult(\Delta_{k,i})
is satisfied for
\mathcal{S}(0),
then
X
has a fast loop.

Here
t\in\mathbb{R}^{1}
in the family
\mathcal{S}(t),
i.e.
t
is not necessarily small.

Proof.
Restrict the covering as in the assumption,
X\supset X_{\mathcal{S}}\to\mathcal{S}\supset\Delta|_{\mathcal{S}}.

This is still a convenient covering.
As in the proof of theorem
3.2
we get a fast loop
\mathbb{R}_{>0}Z\subset X_{\mathcal{S}}.

We show that it is a fast loop also inside
X.

Any deformation of
\mathcal{S}
that preserves the tangent plane
T_{\mathcal{S}}
induces an equisingular family
\Delta_{k}(t).

Thus it induces a family of fast loops,
\mathbb{R}_{>0}Z(t)\subset X_{\mathcal{S}(t)},
with no hornic link-wise retraction, §
2.6
. Thus
\mathbb{R}_{>0}Z
could be possibly link-wise retractible inside
X
only in a family with varying tangent planes. But such a retraction is non-hornic.
Therefore
\mathbb{R}_{>0}Z
is a fast loop also inside
X
, by §
2.6
.

\blacksquare

Remark 3.15
.

The assumption of equisingularity of
\Delta_{k}(t)\cap\mathcal{S}(t)

is important.
Without it one could start, e.g. from a homogeneous
\Delta\subset(\mathbb{C}^{n},o)
, and take an intersection with a tangent smooth surface, to get a singular branch of high multiplicity. This would ensure a germ that is a fast cycle inside
X\cap\pi^{-1}\mathcal{S},
but admits a hornic linkwise retraction inside
X.

As a corollary we get: points of high multiplicity of
\mathbb{P}T_{\Delta}\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}\subset Bl_{o}(\mathbb{C}^{n},o)
ensure fast loops in
X.

Corollary 3.16
.

Let
X\subset(\mathbb{C}^{n+c},o)
be a complete intersection of dimension
n\geq 3
and multiplicity
p.
Take a convenient covering
X\to(\mathbb{C}^{n},o)\supset\Delta
and suppose the total ramification index over each point of
\Delta
is at least
q,
see §
3.1
.
Suppose a point
o_{k}\in\mathbb{P}T_{\Delta}
satisfies:

mult(\mathbb{P}T_{\Delta},o_{k})\geq\frac{p}{q}
and
mult(\mathbb{P}T_{\Delta},o_{k})>mult({\tilde{\Delta}},o_{k}).

Then
X
has a fast loop.

Proof.
To apply proposition
3.14
we blowup
Bl_{o}(\mathbb{C}^{n},o)\ \lower 0.86108pt\hbox{$\mathrel{\mathop{\to}\limits^{\sigma}}$}\ (\mathbb{C}^{n},o)
and
fix a smooth (
\mathbb{C}
-analytic) surface germ
\mathcal{S}\subset(\mathbb{C}^{n},o)
satisfying:

o_{k}\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}\cap\mathbb{P}^{n-1}\subset Bl_{o}(\mathbb{C}^{n},o)\quad\text{but
$\mathcal{S}$ is generic otherwise.}

Then
\mathcal{S}\cap\Delta
is a reduced plane curve germ. One of its tangential component is
\Delta_{k}:=\sigma(\tilde{\mathcal{S}}\cap{\tilde{\Delta}},o_{k}).

We bound its multiplicity,
mult(\Delta_{k})=\langle{\tilde{\Delta}}_{k},\mathbb{P}^{n-1}\rangle=mult(\mathbb{P}T_{\Delta},o_{k})\geq\frac{p}{q}.
Therefore we have the assumption of
proposition
3.14
:

(19)

p=\frac{p}{q}\cdot q\leq(r_{k}-1)+q\cdot mult(\Delta_{k})\leq(r_{k}-1)+\sum_{i}q_{i}\cdot mult(\Delta_{k,i}).

It remains to verify: any deformation
\mathcal{S}(t)
that preserves the tangent plane,
T_{\mathcal{S}(t)}=T_{\mathcal{S}(0)},
induces
an equisingular family of planar curve germs,
\Delta_{k}(t)\subset\mathcal{S}(t).
This is done by explicit computation.
Denote
mult(\Delta)=d
and
mult(\mathbb{P}T_{\Delta},o_{k})=m.
Choose the coordinates in
(\mathbb{C}^{n},o)
such that
o_{k}=[0:\dots:0:1]\in\mathbb{P}T_{\Delta},
but generically otherwise. Then the defining equation of
\Delta
belongs to the ideal

(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1})^{m}\cdot(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})^{d-m}+(x_{n})^{d+1}\subset\mathbb{C}\{x\}.
Moreover, by the genericity of the coordinate choice, the defining equation of
\Delta
contains the monomials

x^{d}_{1},\dots,x^{d}_{n-1},

x^{m}_{1}x^{d-m}_{n},\dots,x^{m}_{n-1}x^{d-m}_{n},

x^{d+m-1}_{n}.
(The last monomial is ensured by the condition
mult(\mathbb{P}T_{\Delta},o_{k})>mult({\tilde{\Delta}},o_{k}).
) After a
GL(n-1)
-transformation on the coordinates
x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1},
the surface
\mathcal{S}
is defined by
the ideal
\langle x_{2}-a_{2}(x_{1},x_{n}),\dots,x_{n-1}-a_{n-1}(x_{1},x_{n})\rangle,
with
a_{i}\in(x_{1})+(x_{n})^{2}.

Thus the curve germ
\Delta\cap\mathcal{S}
is defined by :
x^{d+m-k}_{n}+x^{m}_{1}x^{d-m}_{n}+x^{d}_{1}+(h.o.t.),
for some
m>k>0,
and with some coefficients.
Any deformation
\mathcal{S}(t),
i.e. a deformation of
a_{2}(x_{1},x_{n}),\dots,a_{n-1}(x_{1},x_{n})
by terms in
(x_{1},x_{n})^{2},
results in monomials strictly above the Newton diagram. Hence it gives an equisingular family
\Delta_{k}(t).

Thus both assumptions of proposition
3.14
are satisfied.

\blacksquare

Example 3.17
.

Consider hypersurface germ
X=V(x^{p}_{n+1}-f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}))\subset(\mathbb{C}^{n+1},o).
Suppose
ord(f)\geq p\geq 2
and
f
is square-free.
Then the covering
X\to(\mathbb{C}^{n}_{x_{1}\dots x_{n}},o)
is convenient, and its discriminant,
\Delta=V(f)\subset(\mathbb{C}^{n},o),
is reduced. The total ramification index over each point of
\Delta
equals
(p-1).
Therefore the ratio in corollary

3.16
is
\frac{p}{q}=\frac{p}{p-1}\leq 2.
Suppose
\Delta
is not an ordinary multiple point, i.e.

\mathbb{P}T_{\Delta}
has a singular point, say
o_{k}\in\mathbb{P}^{n-1}.
If
mult(\mathbb{P}T_{\Delta},o_{k})>mult({\tilde{\Delta}},o_{k}),
then
X
has a fast loop.

As a particular case, let
X=V(x^{p}_{n+1}-\sum^{n}_{i=1}x^{d_{i}}_{i})\subset(\mathbb{C}^{n+1},o),
with
d_{1}\geq d_{2}\geq\cdots\geq d_{n}\geq p\geq 2.

The discriminant is
\Delta=V(\sum^{n}_{i=1}x^{d_{i}}_{i})\subset(\mathbb{C}^{n},o).

Suppose
d_{1}>d_{n},
then
\mathbb{P}T_{\Delta}
has multiplicity
d_{n}
at the point
o_{k}:=[1:0:\cdots:0]\in\mathbb{P}^{n-1}\subset Bl_{o}(\mathbb{C}^{n},o).

Define the local coordinates at this point via
x_{1}=\tilde{x}_{1},x_{2}=\tilde{x}_{2}\cdot\tilde{x}_{1},
…,
x_{n}=\tilde{x}_{n}\cdot\tilde{x}_{1}.

Take the strict transform
{\tilde{\Delta}}=V(\tilde{x}_{1}^{d_{1}-d_{n}}+\cdots+\tilde{x}^{d_{n}}_{n})\subset Bl_{o}(\mathbb{C}^{n},o).

Then
mult(\mathbb{P}T_{\Delta},o_{k})>mult({\tilde{\Delta}},o_{k})
iff
d_{1}<2d_{n}.
Altogether, if
d_{n}<d_{1}<2d_{n}
then
X
has a fast loop.
In fact, a sufficient condition for a fast loop is just
d_{n}<d_{1},
see
[
Kerner-Mendes.23
]
.
