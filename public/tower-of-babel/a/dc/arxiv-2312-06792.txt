Abstract
. A reflection mapping is a singular holomorphic mapping obtained by restricting the quotient mapping of a complex reflection group. We study the analytic structure of double point spaces of reflection mappings. In the case where the image is a hypersurface, we obtain explicit equations for the double point space and for the image as well. In the case of surfaces in ℂ 3 \displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{3} , this gives a very efficient method to compute the Milnor number and delta invariant of the double point curve.

1.
Introduction

In the theory of singular mappings, there are few known examples which are degenerate and also have desirable properties. The problem, rather than an actual lack of mappings of this kind, seems to be the difficulty of the calculations.
Reflection mappings were introduced in the prequel of this work
[
17
]
, as a means to produce degenerate mappings which are easy to understand. This stablished the existence of the examples we were looking for in certain dimensions. In this paper we go one step further and show how to compute the double point spaces—and, in the hypersurface case, the image as well— for reflection mappings. In short, a reflection mapping is a holomorphic singular mapping
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{C}^{p}
obtained by restricting the quotient map of a reflection group
\displaystyle W
to a submanifold
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
of the vector space
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}
where the group acts (see Section
2
for details). The idea is that the group action, together with the way
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
sits in
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}
, must encode the geometry of the mapping.

The reflection mapping class contains the first families of quasi-homogeneous finitely determined map-germs
\displaystyle(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{p},0)
having unbounded multiplicity, for arbitrary
\displaystyle n
and
\displaystyle p=2n-1
or
\displaystyle p=2n

[
17
, Theorems 9.5 and 9.6]
(see Example
2.5
for low dimensional examples). These mappings where later shown by Ruas and Silva to be counterexamples to a long standing conjecture of Ruas
[
21
]
. Brasselet, Thuy and Ruas used them to show the density of the finitely determined mappings among certain spaces of quasi-homogeneous mappings
[
2
]
. Silva noticed that these reflection mappings show that the topological type of a generic transverse slice, in the case of
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
-finite quasihomogeneous mappings, is not determined by their weights and degrees
[
22
]
. Rodrigues Hernandes and Ruas have shown that every finitely determined monomial mapping
\displaystyle(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{p},0)
, with
\displaystyle p\geq 2n
, is a reflection mapping
[
19
]
. In contrast, for
\displaystyle p<2n-1
, the only finitely determined reflection mappings are folding maps
[
17
, Theorem 8.5]
. Reflection mappings were also shown to satisfy an extended version of Lê’s conjecture, a rather misterious problem relating injectivity and corank
[
17
, Proposition 4.3]
.

Apart from the theory of singular mappings, reflection mappings have proven to be relevant in differential geometry. The original idea is due to Bruce and Wilkinson
[
3
,
4
]
, who noticed that the singularities produced by folding a surface with respect to a plane (in our terminology these are
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}/2
-reflection mappings) reveal interesting extrinsic information of the surface. This idea has since been extended to the other spaces
[
1
,
7
,
15
]
as well as to different reflection groups
[
18
]
. The reflection mappings used by Bruce and Wilkinson are known as folding mappings and have been widely studied (apart from the previous references, see
[
6
]
). They belong to a subclass called reflected graphs
[
17
, Definition 13]
, which we describe in Example
2.4
. This class includes other previously studied classes of mappings, such as double folds
[
8
,
16
]
and
\displaystyle k
-folding mappings
[
18
]
. Double folds provided the first examples of finitely determined quasi-homogeneous corank two map-germs. Inspecting the classification of all simple map germs
\displaystyle(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{3},0)

[
12
]
reveals that they are either folding mappings or
\displaystyle 3
-folding mappings.

Now we can discuss the content of the present work. Singular mappings are understood via the study of their multiple point spaces, the most fundamental of which are the double point spaces. Moreover, given the coordinate functions of a map-germ
\displaystyle(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{n+1},0)
, one would like to know the equation of its image. As it turns out, both the image and the double points spaces become degenerate very fast as the complexity of the coordinate functions increases. We show how to study these objects for reflection mappings, in a much simpler way than in the case of arbitrary singular mappings. In the hypersurface case, the image of reflection mappings is described as well.

For reflection mappings, there are three decompositions of double point spaces indexed by the reflection group, namely

K_{2}(f)=\bigcup_{\sigma\in W\setminus\{1\}}K_{2}^{\sigma}(f),\qquad D^{2}(f)=\bigcup_{\sigma\in W\setminus\{1\}}D_{2}^{\sigma}(f),\qquad D(f)=\bigcup_{\sigma\in W\setminus\{1\}}D_{\sigma}(f).

These decompositions appeared in
[
17
, Sections 6 and 7]
already, but just at the set theoretical level. In contrast, here the analytic structure of the branches
\displaystyle K_{2}^{\sigma}(f)
,
\displaystyle D_{2}^{\sigma}(f)
and
\displaystyle D_{\sigma}(f)
is described explicitly. These analytic structures are a fundamental part of the theory of singular mappings, which requires
\displaystyle K_{2}(f)
,
\displaystyle D^{2}(f)
and
\displaystyle D(f)
to be complex spaces, sometimes with a non-reduced structure indicating higher degeneracy.

For arbitrary mappings
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
with
\displaystyle\operatorname{dim}\mathcal{Y}=n
, the double point space
\displaystyle D(f)\subset\mathcal{Y}
and the image
\displaystyle\operatorname{Im}f\subset\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
are hypersurfaces (or more precisely, complex spaces defined locally by principal ideals, as they are not always reduced), but computing their equations is usually very hard. One of the main achievements of this work are the explicit formulas

\operatorname{Im}f=V\Big(\prod_{\sigma\in W}(\sigma L)\circ s\Big),\qquad D(f)=V\Big(\prod_{\sigma\in W\setminus\{1\}}\lambda_{\sigma}\Big)

for reflection mappings. As a bonus, the formula for
\displaystyle D(f)
gives a much faster way to compute the Milnor number and delta invariant of
\displaystyle D(f)
for germs of reflection mappings
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{3}
.

2.
Notation and preliminaries

In this section we summarize the prerequisites and set the notation for reflection groups and reflection mappings. For a more detailed account and proper citations, we refer to
[
17
]
.
To avoid repetition, we fix the meaning of the symbols and notations summarized in this section, which will not be reintroduced.

Reflection groups and the orbit mapping

Throughout the text,
\displaystyle W
stands for a (complex) reflection group acting on a
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}
-vector space
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}
. We write
\displaystyle p=\operatorname{dim}\mathcal{V}
.

The orbit of a subset
\displaystyle S\subseteq\mathcal{V}
(or better, the union of the orbits of the points in
\displaystyle S
) is denoted by
\displaystyle WS\subseteq\mathcal{V}
.
We adopt the convention that the action of
\displaystyle\sigma\in W
on a function
\displaystyle H
in
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{V}}
is

\displaystyle(\sigma H)(u)=H(\sigma^{-1}u).

Hence, a zeroset
\displaystyle S=V(J)
is transformed by
\displaystyle\sigma
into the set
\displaystyle\sigma S=\{\sigma u\mid u\in S\}=V(\sigma J)
.
The
stabilizer
of
\displaystyle S
is

W^{S}=\{\sigma\in W\mid\sigma S=S\}.

and the
pointwise stabilizer
of
\displaystyle S
is

W_{S}=\{\sigma\in W\mid\sigma u=u,\text{ for all }u\in S\}.

\displaystyle W^{S}
is the maximal subgroup of
\displaystyle W
acting on
\displaystyle S
. The pointwise stabilizer
\displaystyle W_{S}
is a normal subgroup of
\displaystyle W^{S}
and the quotient
\displaystyle W^{S}/W_{S}
acts faithfully on
\displaystyle S
.

The union of the reflecting hyperplanes of all reflections in
\displaystyle W
is called the
hyperplane arrangement
and is written as

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}\subset\mathcal{V}
.
The reflecting hyperplanes induce a partition of
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}
into subsets, called facets, consisting of those points contained in exactly the same hyperplanes. The set of facets is denoted by
\displaystyle\mathscr{C}
and called the
complex
of
\displaystyle W
.
Clearly, facets
\displaystyle C\in\mathscr{C}
are open subsets of linear subspaces, hence have the same tangent everywhere, which may be identified with the closure
\displaystyle\overline{C}
. Similarly, we often write
\displaystyle C^{\bot}
instead of
\displaystyle T_{y}C^{\bot}
, for any
\displaystyle y\in C
.

The celebrated Shephard-Todd-Chevalley Theorem characterizes reflection groups as the only subgroups of the group of unitary transformations of
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}
for which the quotient mapping
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}/W
can be realized as a polynomial mapping

\omega\colon\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{C}^{p}

whose coordinate functions
\displaystyle\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{p}
are homogeneous polynomials (they are a set of generators of the ring of
\displaystyle W
-invariant polynomials). More geometrically, the map
\displaystyle\omega
identifies an orbit to a point, that is, for any
\displaystyle u\in\mathcal{V}
, we have that
\displaystyle\omega^{-1}(\omega(u))=Wu
.

Reflection groups act faithfully, hence
\displaystyle\omega
is generically
\displaystyle|W|
-to-one, and it is well known that the ramification locus is precisely the hyperplane arrangement. More precissely, given a point
\displaystyle u\in\mathcal{V}
, contained in a facet
\displaystyle C\in\mathscr{C}
, we have that
\displaystyle\ker\operatorname{d}\!\omega_{u}=C^{\bot}
.

By the universal property of quotient mappings, the mapping
\displaystyle\omega
is well defined up to
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}
-equivalence (that is, up to changes of coordinates in the target). As it will become clear, replacing
\displaystyle\omega
by an
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}
-equivalent map does not change the
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
-class of the associated reflection mappings. Since we study reflection mappings up to
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
-equivalence, we may pretend
\displaystyle\omega
to be unique, and abusively call it
the orbit map
of
\displaystyle W
.

Example 2.1

(Products of cyclic groups)
.

The product
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}/{d_{1}}\times\dots\times\mathbb{Z}/{d_{p}}
is a reflection group acting on
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{p}
by

(a_{1},\dots,a_{p})\cdot(u_{1},\dots,u_{p})=(\xi_{1}^{a_{1}}u_{1},\dots,\xi_{p}^{a_{p}}u_{p})\text{, with }\xi_{j}=e^{\frac{2\pi i}{d_{j}}}.

An element
\displaystyle(a_{1},\dots,a_{p})
is a reflection if and only if exactly one
\displaystyle a_{i}
is nonzero. The orbit map is defined by
\displaystyle\omega(u_{1},\dots,u_{p})=(u_{1}^{d_{1}},\dots,u_{p}^{d_{p}}).

Example 2.2

(Dihedral groups,
\displaystyle D_{2n}
)
.

These are the groups of symmetries of regular polygon of
\displaystyle n
sides. We will use the group
\displaystyle D_{8}
, consisting of the identity, four reflections

\sigma_{1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&-1\end{array}\right),\quad\sigma_{2}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right),\quad\sigma_{3}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-1&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right),\quad\sigma_{4}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-1\\
-1&0\end{array}\right)

and three rotations

\rho_{1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-1\\
1&0\end{array}\right),\quad\rho_{2}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-1&0\\
0&-1\end{array}\right),\quad\rho_{3}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
-1&0\end{array}\right).

The orbit map of
\displaystyle D_{8}
is the mapping
\displaystyle\omega\colon\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
, given by

(u,v)\mapsto(u^{2}+v^{2},u^{2}v^{2}).

Example 2.3

(The group
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{4}
of symmetries of the tetrahedron)
.

Consider a regular tetrahedron centered at the origin of
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{3}
, for example the one with vertices

V_{1}=\left(1,0,\frac{-1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\quad V_{2}=\left(-1,0,\frac{-1}{\sqrt{2}}\right),\quad V_{3}=\left(0,1,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right),\quad V_{4}=\left(0,-1,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right).

The group of unitary automorphisms of the tetrahedron is a reflection group, isomorphic as an abstract group to the permutation group
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{4}
of its four vertices. This group is generated by the permutations
\displaystyle(i\;i+1)
, which in matrix form are

(1\;2)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}-1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{array}\right),\;(2\;3)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&-1&-\sqrt{2}\\
-1&1&-\sqrt{2}\\
-\sqrt{2}&-\sqrt{2}&0\end{array}\right),\;(3\;4)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&0&0\\
0&-1&0\\
0&0&1\end{array}\right).

The group
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{4}
contains
\displaystyle 6
reflections, which are precisely the permutations
\displaystyle(i\;j)
. The orbit map is

\omega(u,v,w)=\big(u^{2}+v^{2}+w^{2},(u+v)(u-v)w,(2u^{2}-w^{2})(2v^{2}-w^{2})\big).

Reflection mappings

Take an embedding
\displaystyle h\colon\mathcal{X}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{V}
of an
\displaystyle n
-dimensional complex manifold
\displaystyle\mathcal{X}
into
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}
. The image of
\displaystyle h
is an
\displaystyle n
-dimensional complex submanifold

\mathcal{Y}=h(\mathcal{X})\subseteq\mathcal{V}.

A
reflection mapping
is a map obtained as the composition of the orbit map
\displaystyle\omega
and the embedding
\displaystyle h
, that is,

f=\omega\circ h\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{C}^{p}.

It is often convenient to replace
\displaystyle h
by the inclusion
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{V}
, obtaining a reflection mapping which, abusively, is also denoted

f=\omega|_{\mathcal{Y}}\colon\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{C}^{p}.

The choice between these two equivalent settings will be clear from the context.
To finish fixing our notation, locally at any point,
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
is defined in
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}
by a collection of regular equations, which we write as

L=(L_{1},\dots,L_{p-n})=0.

(technically, it would be better to express our results in terms of the ideal sheaf
\displaystyle I(\mathcal{Y})
of holomorphic functions vanishing on
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
. For the sake of clarity, we have chosen to ignore this issue, which can be fixed by a standard glueing process).

Example 2.4

(Reflected graphs)
.

Take a reflection group
\displaystyle W
acting on
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}
, and a mapping
\displaystyle H\colon\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{C}^{p}
. We may regard
\displaystyle W
as a reflection group acting on
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}\times\mathbb{C}^{p}
, by extending the action trivially on
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{p}
, and take
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\times\mathbb{C}^{p}
to be the graph of
\displaystyle H
(or equivalently, take the graph embedding
\displaystyle h\colon\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}\times\mathbb{C}^{p}
) . The resulting reflection mapping
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{p}
is called a
reflected graph
and has the form

x\mapsto\big(\omega(x),H(x)\big).

A typical and much studied example of reflected graphs are folding maps

(x,y)\mapsto(x,y^{2},H(x,y)),\quad x\in\mathbb{C}^{n-1},y\in\mathbb{C}.

Observe that, after a target change of coordinates,
\displaystyle H(x,y)
can be taken to be of the form
\displaystyle yP(x,y^{2})
. Similarly, double folds are
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}/2\times\mathbb{Z}/2
-reflected graphs of the form
\displaystyle(x,y)\mapsto(x^{2},y^{2},xP_{1}(x^{2},y^{2})+yP_{2}(x^{2},y^{2})+xyP_{3}(x^{2},y^{2}))
and
\displaystyle k
-folding mappings are
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}/k
-reflected graphs
\displaystyle(x,y)\mapsto(x,y^{k},H(x,y))
(See the Introduction for references for folding maps, double folds and
\displaystyle k
-folding mappings).

An interesting example is the
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}/k\times\mathbb{Z}/k
-reflection graph
\displaystyle(x,y)\mapsto(x^{k},y^{k},xy)
, defined by the graph of the function
\displaystyle H(x,y)=xy
. It parametrizes the
\displaystyle A_{k-1}
-singularity
\displaystyle\{Z^{k}=XY\}
, but it does so in a generically
\displaystyle k
-to-one way, as explained in Example
2.9
(observe that
\displaystyle A_{k-1}
being normal prevents it from being parametrized in a generically one-to-one way).

Also, taking the group
\displaystyle D_{8}
(Example
2.2
) and the functions
\displaystyle H_{1}(x,y)=x+2y
and
\displaystyle H_{2}(x,y)=x^{2}+xy-y^{2}+x^{3}+x^{2}y-2xy^{2}-y^{3}
, we obtain the reflection graphs

f^{D_{8}}_{1}\colon(x,y)\mapsto(x^{2}+y^{2},x^{2}y^{2},x+2y),

f^{D_{8}}_{2}\colon(x,y)\mapsto(x^{2}+y^{2},x^{2}y^{2},2x^{2}+3xy-y^{2}+2x^{3}+8x^{2}y-2xy^{2}-2y^{3}).

The last two examples are depicted in Figure

.

Example 2.5

(Reflection mappings with unbounded multiplicity)
.

The map-germs

f^{(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3})}\colon(x,y)\mapsto(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}},(x+y)^{d_{3}}),

f^{(d_{1},\dots,d_{5})}\colon(x,y,z)\mapsto(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}},z^{d_{3}},(x+y+z)^{d_{4}},(x-y+2z)^{d_{5}}),

with
\displaystyle d_{i}
pairwise coprime positive integers, belong to a family of map-germs
\displaystyle(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{2n-1},0)
, introduced in
[
17
, Theorems 9.5 and 9.6]
), which, to this day, are the only known family of
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
-finite map-germs whose coordinate functions have unbounded order. These are the germs studied by Brasselet, Ruas, Silva and Thuy mentioned in the Introduction.

Unfoldings and
\displaystyle W
-unfoldings

In the theory of singularities of mappings, the notion of deformation of a space-germ is replaced by that of unfolding of a map-germ. An
unfolding
of a map-germ
\displaystyle f\colon(\mathcal{Y},S)\to(\mathcal{Z},z)
is a map-germ

F\colon(\mathcal{Y},S)\times(\mathbb{C}^{r},0)\to(\mathcal{Z},z)\times(\mathbb{C}^{r},0)

of the form
\displaystyle F(x,t)=(f_{t}(x),t)
, satisfying
\displaystyle f_{0}=f
. Fixed a small representative of
\displaystyle F
and a value
\displaystyle\epsilon
of the parameter
\displaystyle t
, the map
\displaystyle f_{\epsilon}
is called a
perturbation
of
\displaystyle f
.

A reflection mapping may be perturbed into a non reflection mapping, for example, by perturbing
\displaystyle\omega
in a way unrelated to the action of the group
\displaystyle W
. At the same time, perturbing
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
while keeping
\displaystyle\omega
intact gives a family of reflection mappings containing the original reflection mapping defined by
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
. If we want to study unfoldings of reflection mappings without leaving the reflection mapping seting, these are the deformations we want to consider. In the following lines we formalize this construction.

Let
\displaystyle f\colon\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{C}^{p}
be a reflection mapping, and consider a complex submanifold
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\times\Delta
, such that
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}\to\Delta
defines a trivial deformation of
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
over an open subset
\displaystyle\Delta\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{r}
containing the origin. We may extend the action of
\displaystyle W
to
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}\times\mathbb{C}^{r}
, trivially on the
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{r}
coordinates, so that the corresponding orbit mapping is
\displaystyle\widetilde{\omega}=\omega\times\operatorname{id}_{\mathbb{C}^{r}}
.

Definition 2.6
.

In the above setting, the reflection mapping

F=\widetilde{\omega}_{|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}}}\colon\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}\to\mathbb{C}^{p}\times\Delta

is called a
\displaystyle W
-unfolding
of
\displaystyle f
. If
\displaystyle f\colon(\mathcal{Y},S)\to(\mathcal{Z},z)
is a germ of reflection mapping, a
\displaystyle W
-unfolding of
\displaystyle f
is the germ at
\displaystyle S\times\{0\}
of a
\displaystyle W
-unfolding of a representative.

Observe that the triviality condition on the deformation
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}\to\mathbb{C}^{r}
ensures that
\displaystyle(\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}},S\times\{0\})
and
\displaystyle(\mathcal{Y},S)\times(\Delta,0)
are isomorphic. In particular, every
\displaystyle W
-unfolding of a germ
\displaystyle f
is, up to
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
-equivalence, an unfolding of
\displaystyle f
.

Example 2.7

(A family of tetrahedral reflection mappings)
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{4}
be the group of symmetries of a tetrahedron, as in Example
2.3
. Consider
the family of reflection mappings

f_{t}^{\mathfrak{S}_{4}}=\omega|_{\mathcal{Y}_{t}}\colon\mathcal{Y}_{t}\to\mathbb{C}^{3}\quad\text{with}\quad\mathcal{Y}_{t}=\{u=t(2v+w)\}.

Equivalently, we may parametrize
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{t}
by
\displaystyle(x,y)\mapsto(t(2x+y),x,y)
and think of
\displaystyle f_{t}^{\mathfrak{S}_{4}}
as mappings
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{3}
of the form

\big(x,y\big)\mapsto\Big(t^{2}(2x+y)^{2}+x^{2}+y^{2},\big(t^{2}(2x+y)^{2}-x^{2}\big)y,\big(2t^{2}(2x+y)^{2}-y^{2}\big)\big(2x^{2}-y^{2}\big)\Big)

As it turns out,
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{0}=\{u=0\}=\operatorname{Fix}(1\;2)
is one of the reflecting hyperplanes of
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{4}
. Since
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{4}
acts transitively on its reflecting hyperplanes, we have that
\displaystyle W\mathcal{Y}_{0}=\mathscr{A}
, thus

\operatorname{Im}f_{0}^{\mathfrak{S}_{4}}=\omega(\mathcal{Y}_{0})=\omega(\mathscr{A}),

which means that the image of
\displaystyle f_{0}^{\mathfrak{S}_{4}}
is precisely the discriminant of the orbit map
\displaystyle\omega
.

The degree of a reflection mapping

Before turning our attention to the image and double points space, we show how the degree of a reflection map is encoded by the stabilizers of
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
in a very simple way. This is essential to our study of the image in the hypersurface case, but it applies to all dimensions.

In this work, when we talk about degree of a mapping
\displaystyle f\colon\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{Z}
, we mean the number of preimages of a generic point in
\displaystyle f(\mathcal{Y})
. For this to make sense, there must be an open dense subset of
\displaystyle f(\mathcal{Y})
on which this number of preimages is contant. This happens if
\displaystyle f(\mathcal{Y})
is irreducible and
\displaystyle f\colon\mathcal{Y}\to f(\mathcal{Y})
is proper. The degree for finite map-germs is defined by taking a proper representative. For mappings where
\displaystyle f(\mathcal{Y})
fails to be irreducible, a different degree is associated to
\displaystyle f
on each irreducible component of
\displaystyle f(\mathcal{Y})
.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
\displaystyle f
be a reflection mapping such that
\displaystyle f\colon\mathcal{Y}\to f(\mathcal{Y})
is proper and
\displaystyle f(\mathcal{Y})
is irreducible. The degree of
\displaystyle f
is
\displaystyle|W^{\mathcal{Y}}:W_{\mathcal{Y}}|
.

Proof.

The degree of
\displaystyle f
is the number of preimages of a generic point in
\displaystyle\omega(\mathcal{Y})
, that is, the number of points in
\displaystyle Wu\cap\mathcal{Y}
, for
\displaystyle u
in a certain open dense subset
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
. Observe that, given
\displaystyle\sigma\in W
and
\displaystyle u\in\mathcal{Y}
, the condition
\displaystyle\sigma u\in\mathcal{Y}
may hold even if
\displaystyle\sigma
does not fix the set
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
, that is, even if
\displaystyle\sigma\notin\mathcal{W}^{\mathcal{Y}}
. But, by definition of
\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{\mathcal{Y}}
, this must happen only on a proper closed subset of
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
. Hence, there is an open dense subset
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
such that the orbit of
\displaystyle u\in\mathcal{U}_{2}
by the action of
\displaystyle W^{\mathcal{Y}}/W_{\mathcal{Y}}
is
\displaystyle Wu\cap\mathcal{Y}
. Since
\displaystyle W^{\mathcal{Y}}/W_{\mathcal{Y}}
acts faithfully on
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
, there is an open dense subset
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{3}
, such that the orbit of a point
\displaystyle u\in\mathcal{U}_{3}
consists of

Corollary 2.10
.

Let
\displaystyle f
be a reflection mapping such that
\displaystyle f\colon\mathcal{Y}\to f(\mathcal{Y})
is proper. Then,
\displaystyle f
is generically one-to-one if and only if, for all
\displaystyle\sigma\in W\setminus\{1\}

\displaystyle\operatorname{dim}((\mathcal{Y}\cap\sigma\mathcal{Y})\setminus\operatorname{Fix}\sigma)\leq n-1

Proof.

Lemma 2.11

(Generically One-To-One Unfolding)
.

Any multi-germ of reflection mapping admits a one-parameter
\displaystyle W
-unfolding, given by
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\times\mathbb{C}
, such that
\displaystyle\operatorname{dim}(\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}\cap\sigma\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}})<\operatorname{dim}\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}
, for all
\displaystyle\sigma\in W\setminus\{1\}
.
In particular, this unfolding is generically one-to-one.

Fitting ideals

It is well known that the image of a finite holomorphic mapping is an analytic set (indeed it is enough for the mapping to be proper). However, given an unfolding
\displaystyle F=(f_{t},t)
, the ideal of the image of
\displaystyle f_{0}
need not be the same as the result of computing the ideal of the image of
\displaystyle F
and replacing
\displaystyle t=0
. This is a problem for the study of singular mappings, where deformations are regarded as an essential part of the theory. Luckily enough, there is a solution consisting on declaring the image of a finite mapping
\displaystyle f\colon\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{Z}
to be, rather than just a set, a complex space

\operatorname{Im}f=V(\mathcal{F}_{0}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{Y}})),

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{0}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{Y}})
stands for the
\displaystyle 0
th Fitting ideal sheaf of the pushforward module. This sometimes gives
\displaystyle\operatorname{Im}f
a non-reduced analytic structure, but this is the price we pay in order for the analytic structure to behave well under deformations. For map-germs, one uses the
\displaystyle 0
th Fitting ideal, written
\displaystyle F_{0}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{Y}})
. For information about Fitting ideals, we refer to
[
11
]
. We only include here the results we need.

Proposition 2.12
.

If
\displaystyle F=(f_{t},t)\colon\mathcal{Y}\times\Delta\to\mathcal{Z}\times\Delta
is a finite mapping and
\displaystyle\epsilon\in\Delta
then
\displaystyle V(\mathcal{F}_{0}(f_{\epsilon*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{Y}}))=V(\mathcal{F}_{0}(F_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{Y}}))\cap\{t=\epsilon\}
.

Proposition 2.13
.

Let
\displaystyle f\colon X\to\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
be a finite mapping defined on a reduced
\displaystyle n
-dimensional Cohen-Macaulay space, and assume the irreducible decomposition of
\displaystyle X
to be
\displaystyle X_{1},\dots,X_{m}
. Let
\displaystyle g_{i}
be a generator of the ideal of
\displaystyle f(X_{i})
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
, and let
\displaystyle d_{i}
be the degree of
\displaystyle f
restricted to
\displaystyle X_{i}
. Then
\displaystyle F_{0}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X})
is a principal ideal, generated by

g=\prod_{i=1}^{m}g_{i}^{d_{i}}.

The first result follows from
[
11
, Lemma 1.2]
, the second is
[
11
, Proposition 3.2]
. The next one is a slight modification, tailored to our needs:

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
\displaystyle f\colon X\to\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
be a finite map-germ defined on an
\displaystyle n
-dimensional Cohen-Macaulay space. Let
\displaystyle X_{1},\dots,X_{r}
be
\displaystyle n
-dimensional Cohen-Macaulay complex spaces, forming a set theoretical decomposition
\displaystyle X=\cup_{i=1}^{r}X_{i}
,
where
\displaystyle X_{i}
and
\displaystyle X_{j}
have no common components if
\displaystyle i\neq j
.
Assume each
\displaystyle X_{i}
to be isomorphic to
\displaystyle X
on an open dense subset of
\displaystyle X_{i}
. Then,

F_{0}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X})=\prod_{i=1}^{r}F_{0}((f|_{X_{i}})_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X_{i}}).

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle\tilde{X}
be the disjoint union of the spaces
\displaystyle X_{i}
and take the obvious mapping
\displaystyle\tilde{f}\colon\tilde{X}\to\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
. Since being Cohen-Macaulay is a local property satisfied by each of the
\displaystyle X_{i}
, the space
\displaystyle\tilde{X}
is Cohen-Macaulay. Since

\tilde{f}_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\tilde{X}}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r}(f|_{X_{i}})_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X_{i}},

it follows from the construction of Fitting ideals that
\displaystyle F_{0}(\tilde{f}_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\tilde{X}})=\Pi_{i=1}^{r}F_{0}((f|_{X_{i}})_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X_{i}})
. Since
\displaystyle f
and
\displaystyle\tilde{f}
are the same map on an open dense subset of their targets, it follows that
the stalks of
\displaystyle F_{0}(\tilde{f}_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\tilde{X}})
and
\displaystyle F_{0}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X})
are the same on that open dense subset. But
\displaystyle F_{0}(\tilde{f}_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\tilde{X}})
and
\displaystyle F_{0}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X})
are principal ideals, hence they must agree.∎

3.
The image of a reflection mapping
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n+1}

In this section we show explicit formulas for the image of a reflection map
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
. For any finite map-germ
\displaystyle f\colon(\mathcal{Y}^{n},0)\to(\mathcal{Z}^{n+1},0)
between complex manifolds, the ideal
\displaystyle F_{0}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{Y}})
is principal (this holds, more generally, whenever
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
is an
\displaystyle n
-dimensional Cohen Macaulay space, and it follows from
[
11
, Section 2.2]
). Hence, letting
\displaystyle g
be a generator of
\displaystyle F_{0}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{Y}})
, the image of
\displaystyle f
is

\operatorname{Im}f=V(g).

Putting together Proposition
2.13
and Corollary
2.10
, one obtains what follows:

Proposition 3.1
.

For any reflection mapping
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
, the space
\displaystyle\operatorname{Im}f
is reduced if and only if
\displaystyle f
is generically one-to-one, if and only if
\displaystyle\operatorname{dim}((\mathcal{Y}\cap\sigma\mathcal{Y})\setminus\operatorname{Fix}\sigma)\leq n-1
, for all
\displaystyle\sigma\in W\setminus\{1\}
, .

Theorem 3.2
.

For any reflection mapping
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}^{n}\to\mathcal{\mathbb{C}}^{n+1}
,
\displaystyle\operatorname{Im}f
is the zero locus of

g=\prod_{\sigma\in W}(\sigma L)\circ s,

where
\displaystyle s\colon\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\to\mathcal{V}
is a section of
\displaystyle\omega
. Equivalently,
\displaystyle g(X)=\Pi_{u\in\omega^{-1}(X)}L(u)^{|W^{u}|}
.

Proof.

It is immediate that
\displaystyle g
vanishes precisely at
\displaystyle\operatorname{Im}f
, hence we must check that
\displaystyle g
is holomorphic and that the analytic structure of
\displaystyle V(g)
is that of
\displaystyle V(F_{0}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{Y}}))
. This may be verified locally in the target and, for simplicity, we will do it just at the origin.

Before comparing both structures, we show that
\displaystyle g
is holomorphic and study the space
\displaystyle V(g)
. The ring homomorphism
\displaystyle\omega^{*}\colon\mathcal{O}_{n+1}\to\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{V},0}
may be identified with the inclusion
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{V},0}^{W}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{V},0}
, where
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{V},0}^{W}
stands for the subring of
\displaystyle W
-invariant germs. This identifies the ideal
\displaystyle\omega_{*}^{-1}(\langle\Pi_{\sigma\in W}\sigma L)\rangle
in
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{n+1}
with
\displaystyle\langle\Pi_{\sigma\in W}\sigma L\rangle\cap\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{V},0}^{W}
. From the fact that
\displaystyle\langle\Pi_{\sigma\in W}\sigma L\rangle
is a principal ideal generated by a
\displaystyle W
-invariant function, it follows that this ideal is precisely the ideal generated by
\displaystyle\Pi_{\sigma\in W}\sigma L
in
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{V},0}^{W}
. In particular,
\displaystyle\omega_{*}^{-1}(\Pi_{\sigma\in W}\sigma L)
is a principal ideal generated by a holomorphic function
\displaystyle\tilde{g}
, such that
\displaystyle\Pi_{\sigma\in W}\sigma L=\tilde{g}\circ\omega
. Since
\displaystyle\omega
is surjective, the function
\displaystyle\tilde{g}
is uniquely determined, not only among the holomorphic functions, but among all functions
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\to\mathbb{C}
. Since the function
\displaystyle g(X)=\Pi_{u\in\omega^{-1}(X)}L(u)^{|W^{u}|}

satisfies the desired equality, we conclude
\displaystyle g=\tilde{g}
and, in particular,
\displaystyle g
is holomorphic. Moreover, we have identified the coordinate ring of
\displaystyle V(g)
with the quotient of
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{V}}^{W}
by
\displaystyle\langle\Pi_{\sigma\in W}\sigma L\rangle\cap\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{V},0}^{W}
. Note that this ring is a subring of
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{V}}/\langle\Pi_{\sigma\in W}\sigma L\rangle
.

Now we show
\displaystyle V(g)\cong V(F_{0}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{Y}}))
. Obviously, the explicit formula given for
\displaystyle g
behaves well under
\displaystyle W
-unfoldings in the same way
\displaystyle F_{0}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{Y}})
does for any unfolding (see Proposition
2.12
). Consequently, it suffices to show the isomorphism for an unfolding of
\displaystyle f
. Hence, in view of the Generically One-to-one Unfolding Lemma
2.11
, we may assume
\displaystyle f
to satisfy
\displaystyle\operatorname{dim}(\mathcal{Y}\cap\sigma\mathcal{Y})\leq n-1
. Since
\displaystyle V(g)
and
\displaystyle V(F_{0}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{Y}}))
are the same at the set theoretical level, it suffices for both spaces to be reduced for them to be equal. The space
\displaystyle V(F_{0}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{Y}}))
is reduced by Proposition
3.1
. At the same time the condition
\displaystyle\operatorname{dim}(\mathcal{Y}\cap\sigma\mathcal{Y})\leq n-1
is equivalent to the condition that
\displaystyle V(\Pi_{\sigma\in W}\sigma L)
is reduced, which forces
\displaystyle V(g)
to be reduced because, as we mentioned before, the stalk of
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{V(g)}
is a subrings of stalks of
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{V}}/\langle\Pi_{\sigma\in W}\sigma L\rangle
.
∎

Computing the image via elimination of variables

Computing explicit expressions of sections of orbit mappings can be hard and, even if we manage to find them, the formula
\displaystyle\Pi_{\sigma\in W}\,\sigma L\circ s
becomes too tedious to compute by hand when we take bigger reflection groups. If we try to implement the expression in some computer software, we face the fact that the coordinate functions of sections are non-polynomial, which is a problem when using commutative algebra software such as
Singular
. Here we introduce two alternative ways of computing the ideal
\displaystyle F_{0}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{Y}})
defining the image, without resorting to sections of
\displaystyle\omega
. As a trade-off, this methods use elimination of variables, which is less explicit, but it is a task computers are happy to do, or at least to try to.

In the case at hand, elimination of variables means what follows: Consider the ring
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}[u,X]
of polynomials on the variables
\displaystyle u_{1},\dots,u_{p}
and
\displaystyle X_{1},\dots X_{p}
(the same works for, say, the ring of germs of holomorphic functions at the origin). Contained in
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}[u,X]
, there is the ideal

J=\langle\prod_{\sigma\in W}\sigma L(u)\rangle+\langle X_{1}-\omega_{1}(u),\dots,X_{p}-\omega_{p}(u)\rangle,

and the subring
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}[X]
of polynomials in
\displaystyle X_{1},\dots X_{p}
. Then,
\displaystyle J\cap\mathbb{C}[X]
is an ideal in
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}[X]
, said to be obtained by
eliminating
the
\displaystyle u
variables in
\displaystyle J
. Alternatively, consider the ring homomorphism

\omega^{*}\colon\mathbb{C}[X]\to\mathbb{C}[u],

given by
\displaystyle H\mapsto H\circ\omega
. Then, the elimination can be expressed as

J\cap\mathbb{C}[X]=(\omega^{*})^{-1}\langle\prod_{\sigma\in W}\sigma L(u)\rangle.

Proposition 3.7
.

For any reflection mapping
\displaystyle f\colon\mathcal{Y}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
,
\displaystyle\operatorname{Im}f
is the zero locus of

F_{0}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{Y}})=(\omega^{*})^{-1}\langle\prod_{\sigma\in W}\sigma L\rangle=\left((\omega^{*})^{-1}\langle L\rangle\right)^{|W^{\mathcal{Y}}:W_{\mathcal{Y}}|}.

Proof.

The first equality was shown in the proof of Theorem
3.2
. For the second equality, let

K=\langle L(u)\rangle+\langle X_{1}-\omega_{1}(u),\dots,X_{p}-\omega_{p}(u)\rangle,

Since
\displaystyle V(K)\cong V(L)=\mathcal{Y}
, it follows that
\displaystyle K
is radical, hence
\displaystyle K\cap\mathbb{C}[X]
is radical. This means that
\displaystyle K\cap\mathbb{C}[X]
is the (radical) ideal of the image of
\displaystyle f
. At the same time, we know by Proposition
2.8
that the degree of
\displaystyle f
is
\displaystyle|W^{\mathcal{Y}}:W_{\mathcal{Y}}|
. Putting both things together, the equality
\displaystyle F_{0}(f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{Y}})=(K\cap\mathbb{C}[X])^{|W^{\mathcal{Y}}:W_{\mathcal{Y}}|}
is a direct application of Proposition
2.13
.
∎

In practice, our experience shows that computing

\displaystyle(\omega^{*})^{-1}\langle L\rangle

is a

4.
Decomposition of the double point spaces
\displaystyle K_{2}(f),D^{2}(f)
and
\displaystyle D(f)
.

Given a germ of singular mapping, studying only the equations of the image somehow means forgetting that these are parametrizable singularities, that is, that these singularities are obtained by glueing one disk by means of a holomorphic mapping (or several disks, in the multi-germ case). Instead, it usual to study singular mappings by means of their multiple-point spaces, which are spaces designed to encode how points in the source glue together to form the image of the map.

There are double point and triple point spaces, as well as higher multiplicity ones, but here we restrict ourselves to double points. Even then, there are several different double point spaces one can look at. Our original interest is to prove an explicit formula for the double point space
\displaystyle D(f)\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
in the case where
\displaystyle f
is a reflection mapping given by a hypersurface
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
(see Theorem
5.2
. The definitions of
\displaystyle D(f)
and other double point spaces are given later on this section). However, as the logic structure of this and the following section reveal, this is best done by studying first the double point space
\displaystyle D^{2}(f)\subseteq\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}
. This in turn requires looking at a more abstract double point space
\displaystyle K^{2}(f)
, which enjoys a key functorial property (Proposition
4.2
). The results before Section
5
do not require
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
to be a hypersurface and are given for all codimensions.

Decomposition of the double point space
\displaystyle K_{2}(f)

Here we study Kleiman’s double points for reflection mappings. We skip many details, for which we refer to
[
13
,
20
]
.
For this description of the double point space
\displaystyle K^{2}(f)
for mappings (not necessarily reflection mappings) let
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
be an
\displaystyle n

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
along the diagonal as

B_{2}(\mathcal{Y})=\operatorname{Bl}_{\Delta\mathcal{Y}}(\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y})=\{(u,u^{\prime},v)\in\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\times\mathbb{P}^{n-1}\mid v\wedge(u^{\prime}-u)=0\},

where
\displaystyle v\wedge(u^{\prime}-u)=0

\displaystyle 2\times 2
minors of the matrix

\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}u^{\prime}_{1}-u_{1}&\dots&u^{\prime}_{n}-u^{\prime}_{n}\\
v_{1}&\dots&v_{n}\end{array}\right).

We write the exceptional divisor as
\displaystyle E=\Delta\mathcal{Y}\times\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
.

Now let
\displaystyle f\colon\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{Z}
be a holomorphic mapping between manifolds (not necessarily a reflection mapping) of dimensions
\displaystyle n
and
\displaystyle p
, both admiting a global coordinate system. Think of
\displaystyle u^{\prime}-u

\displaystyle f(u^{\prime})-f(u)

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}}

\displaystyle n
and
\displaystyle p

\displaystyle p\times n
matrix
\displaystyle\alpha_{f}
, with entries in
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}}
, such that

f(u^{\prime})-f(u)=\alpha_{f}\cdot(u^{\prime}-u)

Then, Kleiman’s double point space is

K_{2}(f)=\{(u,u^{\prime},v)\in B_{2}(\mathcal{Y})\mid\alpha_{f}(u,u^{\prime})\cdot v=0\}.

The proof that
\displaystyle K_{2}(f)
does not depend on
\displaystyle\alpha
is in
[
10
, Proposition 3.1]
. Away from the exceptional divisor,
\displaystyle K_{2}(f)
is just the fibered product

K_{2}(f)\setminus E\cong(\mathcal{Y}\times_{\mathcal{Z}}\mathcal{Y})\setminus\Delta\mathcal{Y},

via the blowup map.
On the exceptional divisor,
\displaystyle K_{2}(f)
keeps track of the kernel of the differential of
\displaystyle f
, as follows:

K_{2}(f)\cap E=\{(u,u,v)\in B_{2}(\mathcal{Y})\mid v\in\ker\operatorname{d}\!f_{u}\}.

Proposition 4.1
.

[
13

The dimension of
\displaystyle K_{2}(f)
is at least
\displaystyle 2n-p
. If
\displaystyle K_{2}(f)
has dimension
\displaystyle 2n-p

The double point spaces
\displaystyle K_{2}
enjoy the following key functorial property, very much related to the construction of reflection mappings. First, any embedding of complex manifolds,
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{V}
, induces an embedding
\displaystyle B^{2}(\mathcal{Y})\hookrightarrow B^{2}(\mathcal{V})
.

Proposition 4.2
.

[
13
, Theorem 2.13]

Given a mapping
\displaystyle F\colon\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{Z}
between complex manifolds, the double point space of the restriction
\displaystyle f=F_{|_{\mathcal{Y}}}\colon\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{Z}
is

K_{2}(f)=K_{2}(F)\cap B_{2}(\mathcal{Y}).

In particular, the double points of reflection mappings are slices of the double points of orbit mappings. Conveniently enough, we understand
\displaystyle K_{2}(\omega)
quite well. Fixed
\displaystyle\sigma\in W\setminus\{\operatorname{id}\}
, let
\displaystyle r=\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{Fix}\sigma

\operatorname{Fix}\sigma=V(\ell_{\sigma})\qquad\text{and}\qquad(\operatorname{Fix}\sigma)^{\bot}=V(\ell_{\sigma}^{\bot}).

The following description of

Proposition 4.3

The double point space
\displaystyle K_{2}(\omega)

\displaystyle p

where

In view of Proposition

Definition 4.4

D(f)=V(\mathcal{F}_{0}(\pi_{*}\mathcal{O}_{D^{2}(f)})).

As a set, we have that

D(f)=\{u\in\mathcal{Y}\mid|f^{-1}(f(u))|>1\}\cup\{u\in\mathcal{Y}\mid\operatorname{rk}(\operatorname{d}\!f_{u})<\operatorname{dim}\mathcal{Y}\}.

Now come back to the reflection mapping setting. Since we have the set theoretical decomposition
\displaystyle D^{2}(f)=\cup_{\sigma}D_{2}^{\sigma}(f)

\displaystyle D(f)=\pi(D^{2}(f))
must be the union of the sets
\displaystyle\pi(D_{2}^{\sigma}(f))

Definition 4.13
.

For any
\displaystyle\sigma\in W\setminus\{1\}
, we define
\displaystyle D_{\sigma}(f)=V(\mathcal{F}_{0}(\pi_{*}\mathcal{O}_{D_{2}^{\sigma}(f)}))
.

We obtain the set theoretical decomposition

D(f)=\bigcup_{\sigma\in W\setminus\{1\}}D_{\sigma}(f).

Since the equations
\displaystyle u^{\prime}=\sigma u
are among the equations defining
\displaystyle D_{2}^{\sigma}(f)

\displaystyle\pi(D_{2}^{\sigma}(f))
and
\displaystyle D_{2}^{\sigma}(f)
isomorphic. Hence the spaces

\displaystyle r\geq n

If
\displaystyle r<n

D_{\sigma}(f)=\mathcal{Y}\cap\mathcal{\sigma}^{-1}\mathcal{Y}.

As the spaces
\displaystyle D_{2}^{\sigma}(f)
and
\displaystyle D_{\sigma}(f)
are isomorphic, Proposition

can be recast as a result about
\displaystyle D_{\sigma}(f)

Proposition 4.14
.

For every
\displaystyle\sigma\in W\setminus\{1\}

\displaystyle D_{\sigma}(f)
satisfies the following properties:

(1)

The dimension of
\displaystyle D_{\sigma}(f)
is at least
\displaystyle 2n-p
. If
\displaystyle\operatorname{dim}D_{\sigma}(f)=2n-p

\displaystyle D_{\sigma}(f)
is Cohen-Macaulay. If furthermore
\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{Fix}\sigma<n

(2)

As complex spaces,
\displaystyle D_{\sigma}(f)\setminus\operatorname{Fix}\sigma=(\mathcal{Y}\cap\sigma^{-1}\mathcal{Y})\setminus\operatorname{Fix}\sigma

(3)

Let

\displaystyle D(f)
and
\displaystyle D_{\sigma}(f)

(4)

\displaystyle D_{\sigma}(f)

\displaystyle D_{\sigma^{-1}}(f)
via
\displaystyle\sigma

If there is no risk of confusion, it is common to write

A formula for

in the hypersurface case

Let

\displaystyle L

Before giving an explicit formula for the double point space

Lemma 5.1

(Unfolding With Good Double Points)
.

Every multi-germ of reflection mapping
\displaystyle f\colon(\mathcal{Y}^{n},Wy)\to\mathcal{\mathbb{C}}^{n+1}

admits a
\displaystyle W
-unfolding
\displaystyle F
, such that, for all
\displaystyle\sigma,\tau\in W\setminus\{1\}

Theorem 5.2
.

As a complex space, the double point space
\displaystyle D(f)
is the zero locus of

\lambda=\prod_{\sigma\in W\setminus\{1\}}\lambda_{\sigma}.

Proof.

The expression
\displaystyle\Pi_{\sigma\in W\setminus\{1\}}\lambda_{\sigma}
behaves well under
\displaystyle W

\displaystyle D(f)

The data of the

Appendix A
Proofs of the unfolding lemmata
2.11
and
5.1

In this section we prove the Generically One-To-One Unfolding Lemma
2.11
and the Unfolding With Good Double Points Lemma
5.1
, which are key in the proofs of the explicit equations of the image and double point spaces of reflection mappings in the hypersurface case. The statements of these lemmata are quite intuitive and, if there is anything surprising about them, it is that we could not prove them more easily. One has to keep in mind however that
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
is any smooth analytic of codimension one germ, with no hypothesis on how bad its relation to
\displaystyle W
is, and most usual transversality arguments do not apply in this setting.

For the most part, it is enough to apply an origin preserving rigid motion to
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
in order to attain the conditions that we desire. However, certain elements of the group may impose problems that cannot be fixed in this way. We start by formalizing who the problematic elements are.

Lemma A.1
.

Given a linear endomorphism
\displaystyle\sigma\colon\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}
, the following statements are equivalent:

(1)

\displaystyle\sigma
preserves all
\displaystyle n
-dimensional vector subspaces of
\displaystyle V
, for certain
\displaystyle 1<n<\operatorname{dim}\mathcal{V}
.

(2)

\displaystyle\sigma
preserves all vector subspaces of
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}
.

(3)

The matrix representation of
\displaystyle\sigma
in some (or any) basis of
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}
is

\sigma=\begin{bmatrix}\xi&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&\xi\end{bmatrix},

for some
\displaystyle\xi\in\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

That the first statement implies the second follows easily after observing that, fixed
\displaystyle 1\leq n<\operatorname{dim}\mathcal{V}
, any vector subspace can be expressed by means of intersection and sums of
\displaystyle n
-dimensional subspaces. The remaining implications are immediate.
∎

Definition A.2
.

Any linear endomorphism
\displaystyle\sigma\colon\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}
satisfying the conditions above is called a
complex homothety
(centered at the origin). Given a subspace
\displaystyle K\leq\mathcal{V}
, we say that
\displaystyle\sigma

acts as a complex homothety on
\displaystyle K
if
\displaystyle\sigma(K)=K
and
\displaystyle\sigma_{|_{K}}\colon K\to K
is a complex homothety.

We will use the following basic result, whose proof is ommited.

.

Let
\displaystyle\sigma\colon\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}

Proof of the Generically One-To-One Unfolding Lemma
2.11

Let
\displaystyle f
be a multi-germ of reflection mapping, given by a germ
\displaystyle(\mathcal{Y},Wu)
. Take the decomposition into mono-germs

(\mathcal{Y},Wu)=(\mathcal{Y}_{1},u^{1})\sqcup\dots\sqcup(\mathcal{Y}_{\ell},u^{\ell}),

We want to find a representative
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{Y}_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}
having a trivial deformation

\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}=\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{\ell}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\times\Delta,

such that
\displaystyle\operatorname{dim}(\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}\cap\sigma\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}})<\operatorname{dim}\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}
, for all
\displaystyle\sigma\in W\setminus\{1\}
. Observe that, if the representatives
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{i}
are chosen adequately, the desired dimension drop is equivalent to the condition that, for all
\displaystyle i,j
and all
\displaystyle t\in\Delta\setminus\{0\}
, we have that

\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{i,t}\neq\sigma\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{j,t}.

To justify the existence of
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}
, we consider the consider the cases
\displaystyle u=0
and
\displaystyle u\neq 0
separately.

In the case of
\displaystyle u\neq 0
, we have in our advantage the fact that no complex homothety fixes any of the points in
\displaystyle Wu
. In particular, if
\displaystyle\sigma\in W
is a complex homothety and the representatives
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{i}
are chosen small enough, then
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{i}\cap\sigma\mathcal{Y}_{i}=\emptyset
. Then, any small enough deformation satisfies
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{i,t}\cap\sigma\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{i,t}=\emptyset
, for all
\displaystyle i\leq\ell
.

By Lemma
A.3
and the Curve Selection Lemma, there exists a curve

\gamma\colon\Delta\to\operatorname{Grass}(n,\mathcal{V})\times\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ell}}{{\dots}}\times\operatorname{Grass}(n,\mathcal{V}),

such that, identifying
\displaystyle T_{u_{i}}\mathcal{V}\cong\mathcal{V}
as usual, we have that
\displaystyle\gamma(0)=(T_{u_{1}}\mathcal{Y},\dots,T_{u_{\ell}}\mathcal{Y})
and

\gamma(t)\in\bigcap_{\sigma\in W\setminus\{1\}}\mathscr{H}_{\sigma,\mathcal{V},\ell,n}

for all
\displaystyle t\in\Delta\setminus\{0\}
. Letting
\displaystyle H_{i,t}=\gamma_{i}(t)
, there exists an analytic family of linear automorfisms
\displaystyle\alpha_{i,t}\colon\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}
, such that
\displaystyle\alpha_{i,t}(T_{u^{i}}\mathcal{Y})=H_{i,t}
. Finally, the germs of biholomorphism

\varphi_{i,t}\colon(\mathcal{V},u^{i})\to(\mathcal{V},u^{i}),

given by
\displaystyle u\mapsto u+\alpha_{i,t}(u-u^{i})
, define deformations
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{i,t}=\varphi_{i,t}(\mathcal{Y}_{i})
of
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{i}
, such that
\displaystyle u^{i}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{i,t}
, for all
\displaystyle t\in\Delta
, and
\displaystyle T_{u^{i}}\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{i,t}=H_{i,t}
. We must check that, given
\displaystyle\sigma\in W\setminus\{1\}
and assuming that either
\displaystyle i\neq j
or
\displaystyle\sigma
is not an homothety, the deformations
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{i}
satisfy
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{i,t}\neq\sigma\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{j,t},
for all
\displaystyle t\in\Delta\setminus\{0\}
. This is trivial if
\displaystyle\sigma u^{j}\neq u^{i}
and, otherwise, it follows from the fact that
\displaystyle\gamma(t)\in\mathscr{H}_{\sigma,\mathcal{V},\ell,n}
, because then

T_{u^{i}}\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{i}=H_{i,t}\neq\sigma H_{j,t}=\sigma T_{u^{j}}\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{j,t}=T_{\sigma u^{j}}\sigma\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{j,t}=T_{u^{i}}\sigma\tilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{j,t}.

Now consider the case where
\displaystyle u=0
. Here there is the advantage that the orbit of
\displaystyle u
is just
\displaystyle\{0\}
. By the same reasoning as in the case of
\displaystyle u\neq 0
, there exists a family of linear automorphisms
\displaystyle\varphi_{t}\colon\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}
, such that,
\displaystyle\varphi_{0}(\mathcal{Y})=\mathcal{Y}
and, for all
\displaystyle t\neq 0
and every
\displaystyle\sigma\in W
which is not a complex homothety,

T_{0}(\varphi_{t}(\mathcal{Y}))\neq T_{0}(\varphi_{t}(\sigma\mathcal{Y})).

Now let
\displaystyle\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{r}
be the homotheties in
\displaystyle W\setminus\{1\}
, such that
\displaystyle\sigma_{i}\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{Y}
(we do not need to care about complex homotheties
\displaystyle\sigma
with
\displaystyle\sigma\mathcal{Y}\neq\mathcal{Y}
, since they will satisfy
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{t}\neq\sigma\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{t}
, for any small enough deformation of
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
). We may assume
\displaystyle\operatorname{dim}\mathcal{Y}>0
, as our problem is trivial otherwise. Then,
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
cannot be contained in any
\displaystyle\operatorname{Fix}\sigma_{i}
, because a complex homothety which is not the identity fixes the origin only. Consequently, we may find a point
\displaystyle u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}
, such that
\displaystyle u^{\prime}\neq\sigma_{i}(u^{\prime}),

for
\displaystyle i=1,\dots,r
. Since
\displaystyle\sigma_{i}(u^{\prime})\neq 0
, we may take a polynomial function
\displaystyle p
on
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}
, such that

\displaystyle p(u^{\prime})=0
,
\displaystyle p(\sigma u^{\prime})\neq 0
, and
\displaystyle p
vanishes at the origin with order of vanishing at least two. Now take the equations
\displaystyle L=(L_{1},\dots,L_{p-n})=0
of
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
and define

\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{t}=\varphi_{t}\big(V(L_{1}+t\,p,L_{2},\dots,L_{p-n})\big).

For
\displaystyle t\neq 0
, the fact that
\displaystyle p
vanishes at
\displaystyle 0
with order at least two implies that
\displaystyle 0\in\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{t}
and that, for every
\displaystyle\sigma\in W
which is not a complex homothety,

T_{0}\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{t}=T_{0}(\varphi_{t}(\mathcal{Y}))\neq T_{0}(\varphi_{t}(\sigma\mathcal{Y}))=T_{0}(\sigma\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{t}),

which clearly implies
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{t}\neq\sigma\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{t}
, as desired.

Finally, for
\displaystyle t\neq 0
and any of the complex homotheties
\displaystyle\sigma_{i}\in W\setminus\{\operatorname{id}\}
above, it suffices to show that
\displaystyle\varphi_{t}(u^{\prime})\in\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{t}\setminus\sigma\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{t}
. On one hand, by construction, the conditions
\displaystyle\varphi_{t}(u^{\prime})\in\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{t}
and
\displaystyle u^{\prime}\in V(L_{1}+t\,p,L_{2},\dots,L_{p-n})
are equivalent, and the second holds because
\displaystyle u^{\prime}
is in
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
and
\displaystyle p
vanishes at
\displaystyle u^{\prime}
. On the other hand,
\displaystyle\varphi_{t}(u^{\prime})\in\sigma\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{t}
is equivalent to
\displaystyle u^{\prime}\in\varphi_{t}^{-1}\sigma\varphi_{t}\big(V(L_{1}+t\,p,L_{2},\dots,L_{p-n})\big)
.
Now observe that complex homotheties commute with linear transformations, and thus
\displaystyle\varphi_{t}^{-1}\sigma\varphi=\sigma
. Since
\displaystyle\sigma_{i}u^{\prime}\in\sigma_{i}\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{Y}
, we conclude
\displaystyle L(\sigma_{i}u^{\prime})=0
, and since
\displaystyle p(\sigma_{i}u^{\prime})\neq 0
, it follows that
\displaystyle\varphi_{t}(u^{\prime})\notin\sigma\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{t}
, as desired.

Proof of the Unfolding With Good Double Points Lemma
5.1

Recall that our goal is to show that any multi-germ of reflection mapping admits a
\displaystyle W
-unfolding
\displaystyle F
, such that, for all
\displaystyle\sigma,\tau\in W\setminus\{1\}

\operatorname{dim}(D_{\sigma}(F)\cap\operatorname{Fix}\tau)<\operatorname{dim}D_{\sigma}(F).

Lemma A.4
.

Any germ of codimension one submanifold
\displaystyle(\mathcal{Y},Wy)\subseteq\mathcal{V}
admits a
\displaystyle W
-unfolding, given by
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\times\Delta
, such that, for every facet
\displaystyle C\in\mathscr{C}
and for all
\displaystyle t\in\Delta\setminus\{0\}
, the intersection
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{t}\cap C
is smooth of dimension
\displaystyle\operatorname{dim}C-1
, and such that
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}
is transverse to the reflecting hyperplanes in
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}\times\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

Taking the equation
\displaystyle L
of
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
and letting
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}=\{L(u)=t\}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\times\mathbb{C}
, the projection
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}\cap C\to\overline{\mathbb{C}}
on the
\displaystyle t
parameter is the Milnor fibration of
\displaystyle V(L)
inside
\displaystyle\overline{C}
. This implies the claim that
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{t}\cap C
is smooth of dimension
\displaystyle\operatorname{dim}C-1
. The transversality of
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}
to the reflecting hyperplanes, which have the form
\displaystyle H\times\mathbb{C}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\times\mathbb{C}
, is obvious from its equation.
∎

Since a
\displaystyle W
-unfolding of a
\displaystyle W
-unfolding of
\displaystyle f
is still a
\displaystyle W
-unfolding of
\displaystyle f
and we do not care about the number of parameters needed for the
\displaystyle W
-unfolding in Lemma
5.1
, the original
\displaystyle f
may be replaced by the
\displaystyle W
-unfolding given by the Generically One-To-One Unfolding Lemma. This may in turn be replaced by one satisfying the conditions of Lemma
A.4
as well. In other words, in order to prove Lemma
5.1
, we may assume our original multi-gem
\displaystyle f\colon(\mathcal{Y}^{n},Wy)\to\mathcal{\mathbb{C}}^{n+1}
to satisfy the following conditions:

(1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
intersects transversely all reflecting hyperplanes.

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
intersects properly all facets of
\displaystyle\mathscr{C}
of dimension
\displaystyle n-1
.

(3)

\displaystyle\operatorname{dim}(\mathcal{Y}\cap\sigma\mathcal{Y})=n-1
, for all
\displaystyle\sigma\in W\setminus\{1\}
.

Now observe that, to show the existence of an unfolding satisfying
\displaystyle\operatorname{dim}(D_{\sigma}(F)\cap\operatorname{Fix}\tau)<\operatorname{dim}D_{\sigma}(F)
, we do not need to deal with all pairs
\displaystyle\sigma
and
\displaystyle\tau
at once. We may fix
\displaystyle\sigma
and
\displaystyle\tau
, find an a good unfolding for them and move on to the next pair of elements. We may also assume
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
to have at most two branches, one containing some point
\displaystyle u
and, when
\displaystyle\sigma u\neq u
, another one containing
\displaystyle\sigma u
. If more branches were present, we would solve the problem taking consecutive unfoldings, one pair of branches at a time. Additionally, condition (
2
) allows us to assume
\displaystyle\tau
to be a reflection, since otherwise the dimension of
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}\cap\operatorname{Fix}\tau
is already smaller than that of
\displaystyle D_{\sigma}(f)
. By condition (
1
), the intersection
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}\cap\operatorname{Fix}\tau
is smooth of dimension
\displaystyle n-1
. We are now reduced to showing the following result:

Lemma A.5
.

Let
\displaystyle\sigma\in W\setminus\{\operatorname{id}\}
and let
\displaystyle\tau
be a reflection in
\displaystyle W
. Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
be a germ of
\displaystyle n
-dimensional complex manifold satisfying the conditions (
1
), (
2
) and (
3
) above, and of one of the following forms:

•

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
is a bigerm
\displaystyle(\mathcal{Y}_{1},u)\sqcup(\mathcal{Y}_{2},\sigma u)
with
\displaystyle\sigma u\neq u
.

•

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
is a monogerm
\displaystyle(\mathcal{Y},u)
with
\displaystyle u\in\operatorname{Fix}\sigma
.

Then, there exists a deformation of
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
whose fibers, for
\displaystyle t\neq 0
, satisfy
\displaystyle\operatorname{dim}(D_{\sigma}(f_{t})\cap\operatorname{Fix}\tau)<n-1
.

Proof.

In the bigerm case, we may proceed as in the proof of the Generically One-To-One Unfolding Lemma
2.11
, but restricting everything to
\displaystyle\operatorname{Fix}\tau
. To be precise, we start by observing that, by condition (
1
) and the fact that
\displaystyle W
acts on the set of hyperplanes, the sets
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{1}\cap\operatorname{Fix}\tau
and
\displaystyle\sigma^{-1}\mathcal{Y}_{2}\cap\operatorname{Fix}\tau
are complex manifolds of dimension
\displaystyle n-1
. Now using Lemma
A.3
with
\displaystyle K=\operatorname{Fix}\tau
and the Curve Selection Lemma, we produce a deformation of
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
whose fibers
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{1,t}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{2,t}
contain
\displaystyle u
and
\displaystyle\sigma^{-1}u
, respectively, and satisfy

T_{u}(\mathcal{Y}_{1,t}\cap\operatorname{Fix}\tau)\neq T_{\sigma^{-1}u}(\sigma^{-1}\mathcal{Y}_{2,t}\cap\operatorname{Fix}\tau).

This implies
\displaystyle\operatorname{dim}(\mathcal{Y}_{1,t}\cap\sigma^{-1}\mathcal{Y}_{2,t}\cap\operatorname{Fix}\tau)<n-1
, and the claim follows from the inclusion
\displaystyle D_{\sigma}(f_{t})\subseteq\mathcal{Y}_{1,t}\cap\mathcal{\sigma}^{-1}\mathcal{Y}_{2,t}
.

Now we deal with the monogerm case. Let
\displaystyle Z
be the union of all
\displaystyle(n-1)
-dimensional components of
\displaystyle D_{\sigma}(f)\cap\operatorname{Fix}\tau
. Transversality forces
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}\cap\operatorname{Fix}\tau
to be a complex manifold of dimension
\displaystyle n-1
. Since the irreducible components of
\displaystyle Z
have dimension
\displaystyle n-1
and
\displaystyle Z\subseteq\mathcal{\operatorname{Fix}}\tau
, we conclude that
\displaystyle Z
is either empty or equal to
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}\cap\operatorname{Fix}\tau
. We may assume
\displaystyle Z
to be non-empty, because otherwise a trivial deformation
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{t}=\mathcal{Y}
already satisfies our claim. We distinguish two cases:

If
\displaystyle Z\not\subseteq\operatorname{Fix}\sigma
, from the inclusion
\displaystyle Z\subseteq D_{\sigma}(f)\cap\operatorname{Fix}\tau\subseteq\mathcal{Y}\cap\sigma^{-1}\mathcal{Y}\cap\operatorname{Fix}\tau
we obtain

\mathcal{Y}\cap\sigma^{-1}\mathcal{Y}\cap\operatorname{Fix}\tau\not\subseteq\operatorname{Fix}\sigma.

This means that, for any representative of
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
(also denoted by
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
), there exists
\displaystyle u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}\cap\operatorname{Fix}\tau
, with
\displaystyle\sigma u^{\prime}\neq u^{\prime}
, such that
\displaystyle\sigma u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}
. Now take an equation
\displaystyle L=0
for
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
, and a polynomial
\displaystyle p
, such that
\displaystyle p(u^{\prime})=0
and
\displaystyle p(\sigma u^{\prime})\neq 0
, and define

\mathcal{Y}_{t}=V(L+tp).

For all
\displaystyle t\neq 0
, just as in the
proof of the generically one-to-one unfolding Lemma
2.11
, the fiber
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{t}
satisfies
\displaystyle u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}_{t}\cap\operatorname{Fix}\tau
and
\displaystyle u^{\prime}\notin\sigma^{-1}\mathcal{Y}_{t}
, in particular,
\displaystyle u^{\prime}\notin\sigma^{-1}\mathcal{Y}_{t}\cap\operatorname{Fix}\tau
. This implies
\displaystyle\operatorname{dim}(\mathcal{Y}_{t}\cap\sigma^{-1}\mathcal{Y}_{t}\cap\operatorname{Fix}\tau)<n-1
, hence
\displaystyle\operatorname{dim}(D_{\sigma}(f_{t})\cap\operatorname{Fix}\tau)<n-1
, as desired.

Finally, consider the case where
\displaystyle\emptyset\neq Z\subseteq\operatorname{Fix}\sigma
. This hypothesis requires
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}\cap\operatorname{Fix}\sigma\cap\operatorname{Fix}\tau
to have dimension
\displaystyle n-1
. Since
\displaystyle\operatorname{Fix}\sigma\cap\operatorname{Fix}\tau
is the closure of a facet of the complex of
\displaystyle W
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
intersects properly the facets of dimension
\displaystyle n-1
, it follows that
\displaystyle\operatorname{Fix}\sigma\cap\operatorname{Fix}\tau
must be a reflecting hyperplane. Then,
\displaystyle\sigma
and
\displaystyle\tau
are reflections with respect to this hyperplane and, since
\displaystyle D_{\sigma}(f)\cap\operatorname{Fix}\tau
depends only on
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y},\sigma
and
\displaystyle\tau
, we may assume the reflection group to be
\displaystyle W=\mathbb{Z}/d
.

Then, the hypothesis
\displaystyle Z\neq\emptyset
implies
\displaystyle D_{\sigma}(f)\neq\emptyset
, which forces the germ
\displaystyle f
to be non-immersive. Since
\displaystyle\ker\operatorname{d}\!\omega_{u}=\operatorname{Fix}\sigma^{\bot}
, this implies that
\displaystyle T_{y}\mathcal{Y}
contains
\displaystyle\operatorname{Fix}\sigma^{\bot}
. In particular, we may choose coordinates on
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
, so that the expression of
\displaystyle f
is

f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}^{d},H(x)),

for some analytic function
\displaystyle H
of the form

H(x)=H_{0}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1},x_{n}^{d})+x_{n}H_{1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1},x_{n}^{d})+\dots+x_{n}^{d-1}H_{d}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1},x_{n}^{d}),

with
\displaystyle H_{0}(0)=0
. Since
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
is the graph
\displaystyle H
, it is isomorphic to its projection on
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\{0\}\cong\mathbb{C}^{n}
and, under this isomorphism,
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}\cap\operatorname{Fix}\tau
becomes the subset
\displaystyle\{x_{n}=0\}
and
\displaystyle D_{\sigma}(f)
becomes the zero locus of

\lambda_{\sigma}=(1-\xi^{\sigma})H_{1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1},x_{n}^{d})+\dots+(1-\xi^{(d-1)\sigma})x_{n}^{d-2}H_{d}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1},x_{n}^{d}).

Clearly, we may perturb
\displaystyle H
in a way that the perturbed function
\displaystyle\lambda_{\sigma,t}
becomes not divisible by
\displaystyle x_{n}
for
\displaystyle t\neq 0
, which forces
\displaystyle\operatorname{dim}(D_{\sigma}(f_{t})\cap\operatorname{Fix}\tau)=n-2<n-1
, as desired.
∎
