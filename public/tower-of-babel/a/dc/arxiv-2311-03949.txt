Quantum signal processing over SU(N)
Lorenzo Laneve

Abstract
Quantum signal processing (QSP) and the quantum singular value transformation (QSVT) are pivotal tools for simplifying the development of quantum algorithms. These techniques leverage polynomial transformations on the eigenvalues or singular values of block-encoded matrices, achieved with the use of just one control qubit. In contexts where the control qubit is used to extract information about the eigenvalues of singular values, the amount of extractable information is limited to one bit per protocol. In this work, we extend the original QSP ansatz by introducing multiple control qubits. We show that, much like in the single-qubit case, nearly any vector of polynomials can be implemented with a multi-qubit QSP ansatz, and the gate complexity scales polynomially with the dimension of such states. Moreover, assuming that powers of the matrix to transform are easily implementable – as in Shor’s algorithm – we can achieve polynomial transformations with degrees that scale exponentially with the number of control qubits. This work aims to provide a partial characterization of the polynomials that can be implemented using this approach, with phase estimation schemes and discrete logarithm serving as illustrative examples.



Quantum algorithmic design is more an art than a science. To this day, quantum procedures across the literature that can provably provide complexity advantage over classical models of computation usually involve the alternations of pairs of reflections (resembling Grover’s search
Grover 1996
;
Grover 1997
) or the quantum Fourier transform to efficiently expose some cyclic structure (thus generalizing Shor’s algorithm
Shor 1997
). Just like in classical computation, the development of a methodology (intended as a toolbox of generally-applicable techniques) is crucial to understand how and to what extent we can achieve speed-ups in new settings. Notable examples of such techniques, which sometimes also helped to develop further intuition about already-known algorithms, include quantum walks
Kempe 2003
;
Szegedy 2004
;
Ambainis 2004
;
Magniez et al. 2012
;
Laneve et al. 2023
, span programs
Reichardt and Spalek 2012
;
Hoyer et al. 2007
;
Cornelissen et al. 2020
, learning graphs
Belovs 2014
, quantum divide and conquer
Childs et al. 2022
, and quantum signal processing
Low et al. 2016
;
Low and Chuang 2017
;
Gilyén et al. 2019
.

In particular, quantum signal processing (QSP) is a construction that arose from the context of composite pulses in nuclear magnetic resonance
Vandersypen and Chuang 2005
. In this setting, a parameter
z
characterizing some quantum process is embedded in a single-qubit operator and, by intertwining this operator with a sequence of gates (independent of
z
), we can induce a polynomial transformation on
z
, thus transforming also the structure of the original underlying process.
For example, if one takes
z
to be the eigenvalues of a unitary matrix
U
representing some algorithm, QSP allows to carry out a uniform polynomial transformation
P
on all the eigenvalues of
U
, i.e.,

\displaystyle|0\rangle|\psi\rangle\mapsto|0\rangle P(U)|\psi\rangle+|1\rangle Q(U)|\psi\rangle

(1)

where
Q(U)
must be chosen in such a way that
|P|^{2}+|Q|^{2}\equiv 1
, in order for the state to be normalized (post-selecting on
|0\rangle
will then enact
P(U)
). The degree of

is equal to the number of steps (and calls to
U
) needed for its implementation, thus marking a clear connection with the required complexity.
With some more effort, one can also use QSP to carry out a transformation on the singular values of a matrix that is
block-encoded
in a unitary, e.g., a non-necessarily-square matrix in the top-left corner of a unitary (the so-called
quantum singular value transformation

Gilyén et al. 2019
;
Tang and Tian 2023
).

Quantum eigenvalue transformation initially turned out to be a useful tool for Hamiltonian simulation
Low and Chuang 2019
;
Low and Chuang 2017
, where optimal algorithms were found with simple and intuitive ideas (later also improved in
Martyn et al. 2023
). However, the true potential of quantum signal processing was discovered with the advent of the quantum singular value transformation
Gilyén et al. 2019
(hereinafter, QSVT), where other known algorithms were found to be rewritten in terms of simple applications of the main theorem, such as fixed-point amplitude amplification
Brassard et al. 2002
;
Yan et al. 2022
, linear systems solving
Harrow et al. 2009
and quantum-state preparation
McArdle et al. 2022
;
Laneve 2023
. Other applications include a QSVT-based version of phase estimation and factoring
Martyn et al. 2021
. These results show how the QSP framework gives life to a
grand unification
of many known quantum algorithms. The powerfulness of this framework is also studied in
Rossi et al. 2023
, where a function-first theory for devising quantum algorithms is proposed on top of the QSP/QSVT framework.

Equation
1
can nonetheless be seen from a different perspective: instead of ‘hoping’ that the control qubit collapses to
|0\rangle
so that
|\psi\rangle
will undergo a desired (non-unitary) transformation, we could design

Martyn et al. 2021
, when information about the phase is extracted one bit at a time). With this approach in mind, the single control qubit constitutes a limitation (of one bit) on the information we can extract from the eigenvalues or singular values, forcing us to compose multiple protocols (even in non-trivial ways) to extract more information, which in turn sacrifices the simplicity of the analysis and the algorithm. Intuitively, if we allow
b>1
control qubits we should design not two polynomials, but up to
2^{b}
, and we can use the idea of QSP to extract more than one bit of information at once. In this work we show that, much like in the single-qubit case, nearly any vector of
2^{b}
polynomials can be implemented with a multi-qubit QSP ansatz, extending previous work
Haah 2019
;
Dong et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2023
;
Motlagh and Wiebe 2023
for
b>2
control qubits. We also show that the signal processing operators can be implemented efficiently, provided that their dimension is polynomial in the input size (i.e., the number of control qubits is logarithmic in the input size). As an example, we present phase location, a problem similar to phase estimation where the eigenphase has to be localized in one of
s
arcs of the complex circle given on input. With a fairly simple analysis, we devise a procedure that returns the correct interval with high probability, which in uneven instances turns out to be more efficient than using phase estimation with sufficient accuracy.

Moreover, in situations where the unitary
U
to transform is such that the powers
\{U^{k}\}_{k}
are efficiently implementable (more than naively repeating
U

k
times), one can use such powers to achieve polynomials of
U
with higher degree in fewer steps. Although this cannot be always the case (a general scheme for efficiently implementing powers of a unitary is excluded by the so-called
no-fast forwarding theorem

), there exist unitaries that satisfy this condition.

As an example, we show how (a reinterpretation of) Shor’s algorithm for the discrete logarithm
de Wolf 2019
emerges by itself from a special case of multi-qubit quantum signal processing, adding more algorithms under the QSP framework. However, we do not need this assumption for these results to be useful: we also derive the original phase estimation algorithm, as well as a more robust variant involving a gaussian window.

We start in Section
I

II
Quantum signal processing over multiple qubits

We now consider the case where the control qubits are
b>1
. Consider the following signal operator

\displaystyle\tilde{w}=\operatorname{diag}(1,\ldots,1,z,\ldots,z)=\operatorname{diag}(1,z)\otimes\mathbbm{1}.

(2)

This operator only requires a single controlled version of the unitary
U
whose transformation we want to carry out. Our first result shows that Theorem
1
has a natural extension to multiple qubits.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\{P_{x}\}_{0\leq x<2^{b}}\in\mathbb{C}[z]
. There exist
A_{0},\ldots,A_{n}\in SU(2^{b})
such that:

\displaystyle A_{n}\tilde{w}A_{n-1}\tilde{w}\cdots\tilde{w}A_{0}|0\rangle=\sum_{x}P_{x}(z)|x\rangle

if and only if:

(i)

\deg P_{x}\leq n
for every
0\leq x<2^{b}
;

(ii)

\sum_{x}|P_{x}(z)|^{2}=1
for every
z\in U(1)
.

Theorem
3
tells that, just like in the single-qubit case, any normalized vector of
2^{b}
polynomials can be implemented with
n
steps, where
n
is the maximum degree of any of the polynomials. For convenience, we introduce some definitions that will be useful throughout the work: a
polynomial state

|\gamma(z)\rangle=\sum_{k}|\gamma_{k}\rangle z^{k}
is a parameterized vector that is a valid quantum state for every choice of
z\in U(1)
, i.e.,
\langle\gamma(z)|\gamma(z)\rangle\equiv 1
. The degree of
|\gamma\rangle
is the highest
k
such that
|\gamma_{k}\rangle\neq 0
. Thus, Theorem
3
tells us that any polynomial state of degree
n
(of any dimension) can be achieved with a
n
-length single-qubit QSP ansatz. Curiously enough, by looking at the proof one can see that the result does not depend on how many 1’s and
z
’s are present in the diagonal of Equation
2
(as soon as we have least one of each). This suggests that the non-uniqueness of the
A_{k}
’s implementing a given polynomial state is even more severe than in the single-qubit case.

An important question arises about the efficient implementation of the signal processing operators
A_{k}
which, for
b\gg 1
, is not even guaranteed to exist. For any constant
b
(i.e., not depending on our input),
A_{k}
has an efficient implementation by the Solovay-Kitaev theorem
Nielsen and Chuang 2010
;
Childs
. When
d=2^{b}
is polynomial in the input size, then it is possible to implement any unitary matrix (this involved a non-trivial construction, see Appendix
A
), thus concluding that QSP over polynomial dimension is implementable in polynomial time.

We state here some corollaries and additional results that will be useful in practice: if we consider the Laurent signal operator
\tilde{v}=\operatorname{diag}(z^{-1},z)\otimes\mathbbm{1}
, we obtain a version of multi-qubit QSP in the Laurent picture.

Corollary 4
.

Let
\{P_{x}\}_{0\leq x<2^{b}}\in\mathbb{C}[z,z^{-1}]
. There exist
A_{0},\ldots,A_{n}\in SU(2^{b})
such that:

\displaystyle A_{n}\tilde{v}A_{n-1}\tilde{v}\cdots\tilde{v}A_{0}|0\rangle=\sum_{x}P_{x}(z)|x\rangle

if and only if:

(i)

\deg P_{x}\leq n
for every
0\leq x<2^{b}
;

(ii)

\sum_{x}|P_{x}(z)|^{2}=1
for every
z\in U(1)
;

(iii)

All
P_{x}
have parity
n\bmod 2
.

This corollary is proven exactly like Corollary
2
. Moreover, it is important to remark that, as in the single-qubit case, if we are allowed to use both
\tilde{w},\tilde{w}^{\dagger}
, we can remove condition (iii) by simply doubling the number of steps. The second result we extend from the single-qubit case involves completion of polynomial states.

Theorem 5
.

Let
P_{x}(z)
be
2^{b}-1
Laurent polynomials of degree at most
n
such that
\sum_{x=0}^{2^{b}-2}|P_{x}|^{2}\leq 1
on the unit circle. Then there exists a polynomial
P_{2^{b}}(z)
such that
\sum_{x=0}^{2^{b}-1}|P_{x}(z)|^{2}=1
. The degree of this polynomial will be at most
n
.

Proof.

The polynomial
R(z)=1-\sum_{x=0}^{2^{b}-2}|P_{x}(z)|^{2}
is non-negative on the unit circle by assumption. Therefore, by the Fejér-Riesz theorem
Geronimo and Woerdeman 2004
;
Hussen and Zeyani 2021
, there exists an analytic polynomial

such that:

\displaystyle|w(z)|^{2}=R(z)

Since
R(z)
is a Laurent polynomial of degree at most
2n
, then the
w(z)
will have degree at most
2n
, and
P_{2^{b}-1}(z)=z^{-n}w(z)
will be a Laurent polynomial of degree at most
n
satisfying the same condition.
∎

III
Exponential steps

We now consider a different signal operator on
b
qubits:

\displaystyle\tilde{w}_{b}=\operatorname{diag}(1,z^{2^{b-1}})\otimes\cdots\otimes\operatorname{diag}(1,z^{2})\otimes\operatorname{diag}(1,z)=\operatorname{diag}(1,z,z^{2},\ldots,z^{2^{b}-1})

i.e., a diagonal containing the first
2^{b}
powers of the eigenvalue
z
. By the first decomposition it is clear that we can implement
\tilde{w}_{b}
with controlled powers of
U^{2^{k}}
. This is particularly useful when
U^{2^{k}}
is efficiently implementable (more than merely repeating calls to
U
), as the efficient implementations of high powers are a shortcut to high-degree polynomials (one call to
U^{k}
already gives a
k
-degree polynomial). It is important to remark that this assumption surely does not hold for every unitary
U
: the no fast-forwarding theorem
Atia and Aharonov 2017
excludes such a general implementation scheme. There are, however, particular unitaries exploiting some algebraic structure, for example:

•

Let
f(x):\mathbb{Z}_{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{M}
be an efficiently computable function, and let
U
be its phase kickback oracle, i.e.,

\displaystyle U|x\rangle=e^{2\pi if(x)/M}|x\rangle

This unitary is efficiently implementable with two copies of the circuit for
f
, and a quantum Fourier transform over
\mathbb{Z}_{M}
. An implementation of
U^{2^{k}}
consists in replacing the circuit for
f(x)
with a circuit for
2^{k}\cdot f(x)
(i.e., shift the bits of the output by
k
).

•

Let
G
be a (non-necessarily abelian) group, whose operation
(x,y)\mapsto x\cdot y
is efficiently computable. The unitary
U:|x\rangle\mapsto|a\cdot x\rangle
is efficiently computable (here,
a\in G
and
|x\rangle
is intended to be some unique encoding of
x\in G
). Moreover, the unitary
U^{2^{k}}
realizes the transformation

\displaystyle U^{2^{k}}|x\rangle=|a^{2^{k}}\cdot x\rangle

which is also efficiently computable, by simply replacing
a
with
a^{2^{k}}
(encoded classically) in the circuit for
U
.

The second example, in particular, is the main property for the efficiency of algorithms solving the Hidden Subgroup Problem
Childs
;
de Wolf 2019
.

Unfortunately, characterizing implementable polynomial states with this ansatz is arguably hard, since it is a special case of (a multi-qubit extension of) multivariable quantum signal processing (M-QSP)
Rossi and Chuang 2022
. If the degree of a polynomial state does not exceed its dimension, we can nonetheless give a full characterization:

Theorem 7
.

Let
|\gamma(z)\rangle=\sum_{k}|\gamma_{k}\rangle z^{k}
be a polynomial state of degree
\leq 2^{b}-1
. There exist
A_{0},A_{1}\in SU(2^{b})
such that

\displaystyle A_{1}\tilde{w}_{b}A_{0}|0\rangle=|\gamma(z)\rangle

if and only if
\{|\gamma_{k}\rangle\}_{k}
are pairwise orthogonal.

The proof of this claim can be found in Appendix
C
, and it shows that
A_{1}
is a unitary containing the normalized
|\gamma_{k}\rangle
as columns, while
A_{0}
prepares the state

\displaystyle A_{0}|0\rangle=\sum_{k}\sqrt{\langle\gamma_{k}|\gamma_{k}\rangle}|k\rangle.

In other words, given an analytic form of
|\gamma(z)\rangle
, Theorem
7
yields an analytic form for
A_{0},A_{1}
. Notice the difference with general-length QSP, where the signal processing operators must be estimated numerically. The availability of a simple analytic form allows us to express elements of
SU(2^{b})
and, in many cases, a simple circuit is available to implement such transformations. We conclude by stressing that, although the existence of these ‘fast-forwardable’ unitaries is an important motivation for this work, algorithm design can benefit from Theorem
7
even when it is necessary to call
U
for
2^{b}
times, as we shall see in the next section.

III.1
Original phase estimation

Phase estimation is an important problem in quantum computation: given a unitary
U
(along with its powers
U^{2^{k}}
) as a black-box, and an eigenstate
|\psi\rangle
such that
U|\psi\rangle=e^{2\pi i\varphi}|\psi\rangle
for
\varphi\in[0,1)
, it provides a
b
-bit approximation of
2^{b}\varphi
with high probability. While algorithms for phase estimation leveraging QSVT are already known
Martyn et al. 2021
(they essentially decompose the quantum Fourier transform into a binary search), here we want to show how the original phase estimation circuit comes out by itself with a simple argument involving Theorem
7
.

Assuming that
2^{b}\varphi\in\mathbb{N}
for simplicity (a more robust algorithm is shown in the next section), in order to devise an algorithm for phase estimation using multiple-qubit QSP, we need to design a family of polynomials
\{P_{x}(z)\}_{x}
such that:

\displaystyle P_{x}(e^{2\pi i\varphi})=\delta_{x,2^{b}\varphi}=\frac{1}{2^{b}}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{b}-1}\omega_{2^{b}}^{k(2^{b}\varphi-x)}

which, by replacing
z=e^{2\pi i\varphi}
becomes:

\displaystyle P_{x}(z)=\frac{1}{2^{b}}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{b}-1}\omega_{2^{b}}^{-xk}z^{k}

where
\delta_{\cdot,\cdot}
is the Kronecker delta, and we simply used the expansion using the roots of unity
\omega_{2^{b}}^{y}
. By grouping the
2^{b}
polynomials into the polynomial state
|\gamma(z)\rangle
, the coefficient vectors are

\displaystyle|\gamma_{k}\rangle=\frac{1}{2^{b}}\sum_{y=0}^{2^{b}-1}\omega_{2^{b}}^{-xk}|x\rangle

As
\langle\gamma_{k}|\gamma_{\ell}\rangle=0
for
k\neq\ell
, following the proof of Theorem
7
we find
A_{1},A_{0}
, where
A_{1}
turns out to be the inverse quantum Fourier transform and
A_{0}
is any unitary mapping
|0\rangle
to the
b
-fold Hadamard state
|+\rangle^{\otimes b}
. This shows that phase estimation in its original form
(
Nielsen and Chuang 2010
, p. 221)
can be expressed in terms of quantum signal processing, where the presence of the quantum Fourier transform is a consequence (and not a cause) of the expansion of the Kronecker delta.

III.3
Discrete logarithm

In the discrete logarithm problem, we are given a cyclic group

\displaystyle U|a\rangle

\displaystyle=|g\cdot a\rangle

\displaystyle V|a\rangle

\displaystyle=|r\cdot a\rangle=U^{\ell}|a\rangle

where
|a\rangle

b

U^{2^{k}},V^{2^{k}}

G

\displaystyle P_{x,y}(\omega_{N}^{s})

IV
Remarks on the general case

We give some final remarks on the general case
n,b>1
with exponential steps, to set up a path for future works. The definitions here are also relevant for the proofs of the results, which are summarized in Figure

and formally shown in Appendix

.

Definition 10
.

For a
m
-dimensional polynomial state
|\gamma(z)\rangle=\sum_{k}|\gamma_{k}\rangle z^{k}
, we define the square coefficient matrix
\Gamma_{j}
, whose columns are the
m
coefficients vectors
|\gamma_{j}\rangle,|\gamma_{j+1}\rangle,\ldots,|\gamma_{j+m-1}\rangle
.

Notice that if we apply a unitary
A
(independent of
z
) to the polynomial state
|\gamma(z)\rangle
, by linearity we are simultaneously transforming all the coefficient vectors
|\gamma_{k}\rangle\mapsto A|\gamma_{k}\rangle
, and thus also
\Gamma_{j}\mapsto A\Gamma_{j}
. The crucial step in proving feasibility of construction for a given polynomial state
|\gamma(z)\rangle
is to look for a so-called
reduction
.

Definition 11
.

A
m
-dimensional polynomial state
|\gamma^{\prime}(z)\rangle
is said to be a
\tilde{w}
-reduction of the state
|\gamma(z)\rangle
if there exists
A\in SU(m)
such that

\displaystyle|\gamma^{\prime}(z)\rangle=(A\tilde{w})^{\dagger}|\gamma(z)\rangle.

This condition can be rewritten as
|\gamma\rangle=A\tilde{w}|\gamma^{\prime}\rangle
, i.e.,
|\gamma\rangle
can be constructed from
|\gamma^{\prime}\rangle
with an additional QSP step. Clearly we obtain a much different definition if we replace the single-step operator
\tilde{w}
with the one with exponential steps
\tilde{w}_{b}
defined in Section
III
.

In order to decompose a given
|\gamma\rangle
into a QSP protocol, we want to look for reductions of lower degree. Each reduction will lower the degree until a zero-degree polynomial (i.e. a quantum state) is reached. Thus
A_{0}
will produce this state, and the rest of the protocol is simply given by the reduction steps in reverse order. A first question is: when does a polynomial state of degree
n
admit a reduction of lower degree? For this we have a definite and clear answer

Lemma 12
.

The polynomial state
|\gamma(z)\rangle
of degree
n
has a reduction

\displaystyle|\gamma^{\prime}(z)\rangle=(A\tilde{w}_{b})^{\dagger}|\gamma(z)\rangle

of degree
\leq n-(2^{b}-1)
for some
A\in SU(2^{b})
if and only if
\Gamma_{0}^{\dagger}\Gamma_{n-(2^{b}-1)}
is lower triangular.

Notice that from this lemma, we immediately deduce Theorem
7
, since
\Gamma_{n-(2^{b}-1)}=\Gamma_{0}
, and
\Gamma_{0}^{\dagger}\Gamma_{0}
being lower triangular means that the columns
\{|\gamma_{k}\rangle\}
are pairwise orthogonal. A stronger result is possible if we are only looking for a reduction of one degree.

Lemma 13

(Linear reduction)
.

Any polynomial state
|\gamma(z)\rangle
of degree
n
admits a reduction of degree
\leq n-1
.

This result yields Theorems

and
3

n

7
is limited compared to Theorem

is because a polynomial state whose degree was exponentially reduced does not necessarily admit another exponential reduction (i.e., the condition of Lemma

V
Discussion

In this work we considered variants and extensions of quantum signal processing involving multiple control qubits. The first claim shows that the result available in the single-qubit setting (stating that every vector of polynomials can be implemented) carries over to any dimension. As an example we showed how a problem called phase location can be solved by devising an algorithm in a convenient way, by using fewer gates than merely using phase estimation with sufficient accuracy. We then constructed a different signal operator
\tilde{w}_{b}=\operatorname{diag}(1,z,z^{2},\ldots,z^{2^{b}-1})
and characterized the polynomials obtainable with a single step of the new ansatz. This allowed us to (re-)derive phase estimation algorithms, with uniform and gaussian window states. In cases when the unitary
U
to transform is easy to fast-forward (i.e., the cost of
U^{k}
scales logarithmically with
k
), this allows a one-length QSP protocol to reach exponentially high degrees. A crucial example is Shor’s algorithm and the class of HSP algorithms
Childs
, whose special case for the discrete logarithm is explicitly shown. The algorithms are thus developed with a function-first approach, i.e., starting from the family of polynomials that we want to achieve (in the spirit of
Rossi et al. 2023
): as a consequence, the quantum Fourier transform in the examples of Section
III
emerged from the expansion of the Kronecker delta (and not the other way around).

The hardness of characterizing the arising polynomials for this ansatz is also due to the tight relation with multivariable quantum signal processing (M-QSP)
Rossi and Chuang 2022
: indeed both ideas have a common extension (in the Laurent picture) involving multiple qubits and a signal operator embedding multiple variables such as

\displaystyle\tilde{w}(a,b)=\begin{bmatrix}a^{-1}&0\\
0&a\end{bmatrix}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}b^{-1}&0\\
0&b\end{bmatrix}

while in
Rossi and Chuang 2022
one forces all the control qubits (but one) to be classical, here we are giving up the simplicity of the single control qubit, but we return to the univariate case by making the restriction
a=z,b=z^{2}
. It is a possible future work to see if one can exploit the result on triangular matrices in Appendix
B
to characterize implementable multivariable polynomials.

It is also important to remark that, while the QSVT
Gilyén et al. 2019
is usually referred to as the ‘multiple-qubit extension of quantum signal processing’, it is fundamentally different from our construction: the focus of QSVT is usually the transformation of the block-encoded operator, and the control qubit is mainly used as a success flag indicating that the correct block is picked out (usually one is only interested in one of them, and the complementing polynomial is derived using the Fejér-Riesz theorem
Hussen and Zeyani 2021
).

Perhaps a multiple-qubit quantum singular value transformation is possible (where singular values of a block-encoded matrix are transformed simultaneously by the
2^{b}
polynomials in each subspace), but it requires a definition of multi-qubit QSP in the Chebyshev picture, which does not seem to be unique.

We conclude by highlighting a limitation of multi-qubit QSP: while
SU(2)
operations are easy to implement, implementing general
SU(2^{b})
elements is not always possible efficiently when
b
is super-logarithmic in the input. We nonetheless show that such protocol is efficient on polynomial dimensions, which is of theoretical interest. On the other hand, when using an ansatz with exponential steps, Theorem
7
allows to retrieve an analytical expression for the two signal processing operators, which in some cases allows to understand them enough to find efficient circuits to implement them, even on exponential dimension.
It is an open question, however, to characterize sub-algebras of the
b
-qubit algebra like in
Chao et al. 2020
, containing only efficiently computable operators even for polynomial
b
.

Appendix A
Approximating unitary transformations of polynomial size

In order for multi-qubit QSP to be practically useful, one needs a systematic way to implement the signal processing operators
A_{k}
. Not surprisingly, approximating a unitary transformation from
SU(d)
is much more difficult when
d>2
. The Solovay-Kitaev theorem
Nielsen and Chuang 2010
can certainly be useful when
d=\mathcal{O}(1)
, but when the number of qubits involved is polynomial in the input size, this problem is more general than quantum state preparation, which requires a number of steps polynomial in
d
(i.e., exponential in the number of qubits)
Sanders et al. 2019
;
Bausch 2022
;
Laneve 2023
. When the dimension
d
itself is polynomial in the input, however, we can use QSVT to implement any unitary
U
efficiently.

Theorem 14
.

Let
U
be a
d\times d
unitary matrix stored in a classical computer. The transformation
U
can be implemented up to error
\epsilon
in
2
-norm by a quantum circuit using
\mathcal{O}(d^{3}\log(1/\epsilon))

gates.

Proof.

We first classically compute
H
with
\lVert H\rVert\leq 1
such that
U=e^{iH}
. We implement a block encoding of
H
using encoding of sparse matrices
(
Gilyén et al. 2019
, Lemma 48)
, so that implementing a block encoding of
H
takes
\mathcal{O}(d^{3})
steps (this includes a round of amplitude amplification). We then implement
U
using a fully-coherent algorithm for Hamiltonian simulation with
t=1

Martyn et al. 2023
.
∎

We remark that the unitaries implemented with these methods are not exact, as we introduce an
\epsilon
error. Nonetheless, each implementation only introduces an additive factor to the overall error in the QSP protocol, so by replacing
\epsilon\leftarrow\epsilon/(n+1)
in the implementation of each signal processing operator, we will be able to construct the given polynomial state up to
\epsilon
error.

Appendix B
Triangular matrices

It is known that, if two matrices
A,B
are both upper (lower) triangular, then also
AB
is upper (lower) triangular. Here we are interested in the converse: given that
AB
is upper (lower) triangular, can we conclude that
A
and
B
are upper (lower) triangular? The answer is yes, up to a common unitary transformation.

Lemma 15
.

Let
A,B
be two
N\times N
matrices. Then the product
A^{\dagger}B
is lower triangular if and only if there exists a unitary
Q
such that
Q^{\dagger}A
is upper triangular and
Q^{\dagger}B
is lower triangular.

It is already known that for any square matrix
A
there always exists a unitary
Q
such that
Q^{\dagger}A
is upper triangular (the so-called
QR
-decomposition
Trefethen and Bau 2022
), and a similar decomposition for lower triangular matrices. Lemma
15
gives a necessary and sufficient condition for two matrices having a common triangularizing matrix, which is similar in spirit to the fact that two matrices share an eigenbasis if and only if they commute.

Proof.

We first prove the easy direction: if such
Q
exists then

\displaystyle A^{\dagger}B=A^{\dagger}QQ^{\dagger}B=(Q^{\dagger}A)^{\dagger}Q^{\dagger}B

which is a product of two lower triangular matrices.

For the converse, let
|a_{k}\rangle,|b_{k}\rangle
be the columns of
A,B
, respectively. We proceed by induction on
N
: the claim is trivial for
N=1
, as any scalar is trivially both upper and lower triangular. For
N=2
,
A^{\dagger}B
being lower triangular means that
\langle a_{0}|b_{1}\rangle=0
, and choosing
|u_{0}\rangle\propto|a_{0}\rangle,|u_{1}\rangle\propto|b_{1}\rangle
as the columns of
Q
will obtain the desired triangularization (possibly completing the basis if zero vectors arise). For
N>2
, we fix the first and the last column of
Q
, as in the case
N=2
:

\displaystyle|u_{0}\rangle\propto|a_{0}\rangle,\ \ |u_{N-1}\rangle\propto|b_{N-1}\rangle.

Notice that these two vectors are orthogonal, since
A^{\dagger}B
is lower triangular. Moreover,

\displaystyle\langle N-1|Q^{\dagger}A|j\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle u_{N-1}|a_{j}\rangle\propto\langle b_{N-1}|a_{j}\rangle=0

for
j<N-1

\displaystyle\langle 0|Q^{\dagger}B|j\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle u_{0}|b_{j}\rangle\propto\langle a_{0}|b_{j}\rangle=0

for
j>0

\displaystyle\langle i|Q^{\dagger}A|0\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle u_{i}|a_{0}\rangle\propto\langle u_{i}|u_{0}\rangle=0

for
i>0

\displaystyle\langle i|Q^{\dagger}B|N-1\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle u_{i}|b_{N-1}\rangle\propto\langle u_{i}|u_{N-1}\rangle=0

for
i<N-1

The first two statements also follow from the fact that
A^{\dagger}B
is lower triangular, except in two limit cases: if only
|a_{0}\rangle=0
, we will take
|u_{0}\rangle
to be an arbitrary vector orthogonal to
|b_{j}\rangle
for every
j>0
in order to enforce the second condition (and analogously if only
|b_{N-1}\rangle=0
). If
|a_{0}\rangle=|b_{N-1}\rangle=0
, we can take
|u_{0}\rangle
as above, while we choose
|u_{N-1}\rangle
to be an arbitrary vector orthogonal to both
\operatorname{span}\{|a_{j}\rangle:j<N-1\}
and
|u_{0}\rangle
(this is possible because
|a_{0}\rangle=0
, so
\operatorname{span}\{|a_{j}\rangle:j<N-1\}
has dimension at most
N-2
). The other two conditions hold as long as we will choose the other columns of
Q
to be orthogonal to
|u_{0}\rangle,|u_{N-1}\rangle
. By only choosing these two columns of
Q
, the four conditions above fix the ‘frame’ of the two matrices, i.e.,

\displaystyle Q^{\dagger}A=\begin{bmatrix}*&\cdots&*&*\\
0&&&*\\
\vdots&&&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&*\end{bmatrix},\ \ \ Q^{\dagger}B=\begin{bmatrix}*&0&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&&&\vdots\\
*&&&0\\
*&*&\cdots&*\end{bmatrix}.

The inner blocks of the two matrices are of size
N-2\times N-2
. We complete the unitary
Q
with arbitrary columns and we call the inner blocks in this representation
A^{\prime},B^{\prime}
. Now notice that, for
0<i<j<N-1
:

\displaystyle 0=\langle i|A^{\dagger}B|j\rangle=\langle i|A^{\dagger}QQ^{\dagger}B|j\rangle=\begin{bmatrix}*\\
A^{\prime}|i-1\rangle\\
0\end{bmatrix}^{\dagger}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
B^{\prime}|j-1\rangle\\
*\end{bmatrix}=\langle i-1|A^{\prime{\dagger}}B^{\prime}|j-1\rangle

implying that
A^{\prime{\dagger}}B^{\prime}
is lower triangular and, by induction, there exists a
N-2\times N-2
unitary matrix
Q^{\prime}
that triangularizes
A^{\prime},B^{\prime}
as desired. The claim then holds since the unitary

\displaystyle Q\begin{bmatrix}1&&\\
&Q^{\prime}&\\
&&1\end{bmatrix}

triangularizes
A,B
, as desired.

∎

Lemma
13

(Linear reduction)
.

Any polynomial state
|\gamma(z)\rangle
of degree
n
admits a reduction of degree
\leq n-1
.

Proof.

Consider
|\gamma(z)\rangle
as a polynomial of degree
n+2^{b}-2
(whose last
2^{b}-2
coefficients are zero).
Its coefficient matrix
\Gamma_{n-1}
has
|\gamma_{n-1}\rangle,|\gamma_{n}\rangle
as the first two columns and zeroes everywhere else. By the definition of polynomial state

\displaystyle\langle\gamma|\gamma\rangle=\sum_{k,\ell}\langle\gamma_{\ell}|\gamma_{k}\rangle z^{k-\ell}=1.

Since this holds as a polynomial equation, every coefficient (except the zero-th one) has to be zero, in particular the
n
-th coefficient which gives
\langle\gamma_{0}|\gamma_{n}\rangle=0
. Therefore the product
\Gamma_{0}^{\dagger}\Gamma_{n+2^{b}-2}
satisfies:

\displaystyle\langle 0|\Gamma_{0}^{\dagger}\Gamma_{n-1}|1\rangle=\langle\gamma_{0}|\gamma_{n}\rangle=0

along with the fact that all the other columns are zero (because they are zero in
\Gamma_{n-1}
), we conclude that
\Gamma_{0}^{\dagger}\Gamma_{n-1}
is lower triangular. By Lemma
12
, this implies the existence of a unitary
A
such that the reduction
\tilde{w}^{\dagger}_{2}A^{\dagger}|\gamma\rangle
has degree
\leq n+(2^{b}-2)-(2^{b}-1)=n-1
.
∎

1
for single-qubit quantum signal processing
Martyn et al. 2021
;
Haah 2019
;
Motlagh and Wiebe 2023

Theorem
3
.

Let
\{P_{x}\}_{0\leq x<2^{b}}\in\mathbb{C}[z]
. There exist
A_{0},\ldots,A_{n}\in SU(2^{b})
such that:

\displaystyle A_{n}\tilde{w}A_{n-1}\tilde{w}\cdots\tilde{w}A_{0}|0\rangle=\sum_{x}P_{x}(z)|x\rangle

if and only if:

(i)

\deg P_{x}\leq n
for every
0\leq x<2^{b}
;

(ii)

\sum_{x}|P_{x}(z)|^{2}=1
for every
z\in U(1)
.

Proof.

For
n=0
, the all the polynomials are constant and
|\gamma\rangle
is a quantum state which can be implemented with
A_{0}|0\rangle
for a suitable choice of
A_{0}
(also the sequence
\mathbbm{1},\ldots,\mathbbm{1},A_{0}
implements the same polynomial state, for any
n
).
For
n>0
, by Theorem
13
there is a reduction
(A_{n}\tilde{w})^{\dagger}|\gamma\rangle
of degree
n-1
for some
A_{n}\in SU(2^{b})
. This reduction can be implemented with a
n-1
protocol
A_{0},\ldots,A_{n-1}
, by induction. Hence, the sequence
A_{0},\ldots,A_{n}
implements
|\gamma\rangle
.
∎

While we proved that any polynomial state of degree
n
can be implemented within
n
steps, we can leverage the presence of higher powers of
z
to reduce the degree by more than one during a step of the protocol, provided that some additional orthogonality conditions are given. The extremal case is when all the coefficient vectors are orthogonal.

Theorem
7
.

Let
|\gamma(z)\rangle
be a
2^{b}
-dimensional polynomial state. Then there exist
A_{0},A_{1}\in SU(2^{b})
such that

\displaystyle A_{1}\tilde{w}_{b}A_{0}|0\rangle=|\gamma(z)\rangle

if and only if

(i)

P_{x}(z):=\langle x|\gamma(z)\rangle
has degree
\leq 2^{b}-1
for every
0\leq x<2^{b}
;

(ii)

\sum_{x}|P_{x}(z)|^{2}=1
for every
z\in U(1)
;

(iii)

\Gamma_{0}^{\dagger}\Gamma_{0}
is diagonal, i.e., the coefficient vectors
\{|\gamma_{k}\rangle\}_{k}
are pairwise orthogonal.

Proof.

If a polynomial state
|\gamma\rangle
has degree
\leq 2^{b}-1
, then it can be reduced to a quantum state in a single step if and only if
\Gamma_{0}^{\dagger}\Gamma_{2^{b}-1}
is lower triangular, by Lemma
12
. Since
\Gamma_{0}^{\dagger}\Gamma_{2^{b}-1}=\Gamma_{0}^{\dagger}\Gamma_{0}
, this matrix is also Hermitian and thus being lower triangular is equivalent to being diagonal. Conversely,
|\gamma^{\prime}\rangle=\tilde{w}_{b}A_{0}|0\rangle
has the
k
-th coefficient vector
|\gamma^{\prime}_{k}\rangle\propto|k\rangle
by construction, thus the coefficient vectors are pairwise orthogonal, and
A_{1}
preserves this fact.
∎

From the proof one can see that the elements of the diagonal
\Gamma_{0}^{\dagger}\Gamma_{0}
are precisely the amplitudes squared of
A_{0}|0\rangle
(they sum up to
1
because of condition (ii)). In the meanwhile, the final
|\gamma_{k}\rangle
’s form an orthogonal basis which, if normalized, give the columns of
A_{1}
.
