Divergence-free cut finite element methods for Stokes flow Thanks: This research was supported by the Swedish Research Council Grant No. 2018-05262 and the Wallenberg Academy Fellowship KAW 2019.0190.
Thomas Frachon, Erik Nilsson *, Sara Zahedi

Abstract
We develop two unfitted finite element methods for the Stokes equations based on 𝐇 div \mathbf{H}^{\dive} -conforming finite elements. Both cut finite element methods exhibit optimal convergence order for the velocity, pointwise divergence-free velocity fields, and well-posed linear systems, independently of the position of the boundary relative to the computational mesh. The first method is a cut finite element discretization of the Stokes equations based on the Brezzi-Douglas-Marini (BDM) elements and involves interior penalty terms to enforce tangential continuity of the velocity at interior edges in the mesh. The second method is a cut finite element discretization of a three-field formulation of the Stokes problem involving the vorticity, velocity, and pressure and uses the Raviart-Thomas (RT) space for the velocity. We present mixed ghost penalty stabilization terms for both methods so that the resulting discrete problems are stable and the divergence-free property of the 𝐇 div \mathbf{H}^{\dive} -conforming elements is preserved also on unfitted meshes. In both methods boundary conditions are imposed weakly. We show that imposing Dirichlet boundary conditions weakly introduces additional challenges; 1) The divergence-free property of the RT and the BDM finite elements may be lost depending on how the normal component of the velocity field at the boundary is imposed. 2) Pressure robustness is affected by how well the boundary condition is satisfied and may not hold even if the incompressibility condition holds pointwise. We study two approaches of weakly imposing the normal component of the velocity at the boundary; we either use a penalty parameter and Nitsche’s method or a Lagrange multiplier method. We show that appropriate conditions on the velocity space has to be imposed when Nitsche’s method or penalty is used. Pressure robustness can hold with both approaches by reducing the error at the boundary but the price we pay is seen in the condition numbers of the resulting linear systems, independent of if the mesh is fitted or unfitted to the boundary.

Introduction

Computational methods for simulations of incompressible fluids that fail to produce pointwise divergence-free velocity approximations may result in numerical solutions with large errors and instabilities
VolkerDivConstr2017
. In this paper our aim is to present unfitted finite element discretizations of the Stokes equations that produce pointwise divergence-free velocity approximations, independently of how the boundary is positioned relative to the computational mesh.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open bounded domain with a piecewise smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
, occupied by an incompressible fluid. Denote by
\boldsymbol{n}
the normal vector to
\partial\Omega
. Let
\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)
be the space of vector-valued functions with each component in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Given a source
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)
, boundary data
\boldsymbol{g}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)
such that
\int_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0
, and fluid viscosity
\mu>0
, we seek fluid velocity
\boldsymbol{u}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
and pressure
p:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying the Stokes equations:

\displaystyle-\dive(\mu\nabla\boldsymbol{u}-p\boldsymbol{I})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{f}

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega,

(1.1)

\displaystyle\hfill\dive\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega,

(1.2)

\displaystyle\hfill\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{g}

\displaystyle\text{ on }\partial\Omega.

(1.3)

Unfitted discretizations can be an alternative to standard fitted finite element discretizations when the domain
\Omega
is complicated to mesh. In applications where the boundary
\partial\Omega
or interfaces (internal boundaries) are evolving discretizations that can avoid remeshing processes are of great interest, see e.g.
FrZa19
;
FraZah23
for unfitted discretizations of two-phase flow problems.

For the Stokes equations Cut Finite Element Methods (CutFEM) with optimal rates of convergence of the approximate velocity and pressure have been developed, see e.g.
BecBurHan09
;
HaLaZa14
;
KirGroReu16
for the Stokes interface problem and see e.g.
BurHan14
;
MaLaLoRo14
for the Stokes fictitious domain problem. A popular strategy for ensuring that the discretization is robust and the discrete problem is well-posed independent of the position of the boundary or the interface relative to the computational mesh, is to add ghost penalty terms
Bu10
in the weak form, see for example the works mentioned above. The ghost penalty terms also ensure that the condition number of the resulting matrix from the cut finite element discretization scales with mesh size as for the fitted finite element discretization. An alternative to adding stabilization terms in the weak formulation is to agglomerate elements (merge elements). In that case one needs to have methods that can deal with the general shape that agglomerated elements can have, see e.g.
BurDelErn20
.

Existing cut finite element discretizations for the Stokes problem are mostly based on popular inf-sup stable element pairs such as the Taylor-Hood elements see
KirGroReu16
, mini-elements
BurHan14
, or the P1 iso P2-P1 elements
HaLaZa14
and do not result in pointwise divergence-free approximations. Recently, unfitted discretizations for the Stokes problem based on finite element spaces that result in pointwise divergence-free velocity approximations and are conforming in
\mathbf{H}^{1}
have been developed and analyzed in
LiuNeiOls23
;
BurHanLarst23
. In
LiuNeiOls23
a cut finite element discretization based on the Scott-Vogelius pair is proposed. However, the approximate velocity is pointwise divergence-free only outside a band around the unfitted boundary. In
BurHanLarst23
unfitted discretizations are developed using a low order divergence-free element. The proposed discretizations in
BurHanLarst23
do inherit the pointwise divergence-free property of the underlying element but fail to be pressure robust. It is unclear if the condition numbers for the linear systems associated to the unfitted discretizations in
BurHanLarst23
are controlled and scale with mesh size as for the fitted method.

The construction of efficient divergence-free mixed finite element discretizations of the Stokes problem is not trivial, especially not on general triangulations and in three space dimensions. Several strategies exists, with their pros and cons, see e.g.
VolkerDivConstr2017
and references therein. The Scott-Vogelius finite element pair which was used in
LiuNeiOls23
, where continuous piecewise polynomials of degree
k
are used for approximating the velocity and discontinuous piecewise polynomials of degree
(k-1)
are used for the pressure, has the advantage that on special triangulations the element pair is inf-sup stable in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
k\geq d
. On unfitted meshes these triangulations are not complicated to contruct and thus this element pair can provide a simple and good base for an inf-sup stable and divergence-free CutFEM for the Stokes problem. Our experience is that using the stabilization terms we propose in
FraHaNilZa22
instead of the ones in
LiuNeiOls23
it is possible to modify the discretization in
LiuNeiOls23
to retain optimal divergence-free velocity fields in the entire computational domain using the Scott-Vogelius pair. However, to obtain optimal accuracy a high order representation of the boundary and high order integration on cut elements are required. In this work we choose to study in more detail two first and second order accurate discretizations of the Stokes problem and focus on studying how to guarantee unfitted discretizations to inherit the divergence-free property of the underlying element and being pressure robust. In this paper, we work in two space dimensions, i.e.,
d=2
. However, we believe our main findings and conclusions are valid also in three space dimensions and are of interest also when other divergence-free elements are used.

A way to bypass the challenge of constructing conforming, inf-sup stable, and divergence-free elements is to develop discretizations based on
\mathbf{H}^{\dive}
-conforming elements. The Brezzi-Douglas-Marini (BDM) space and Raviart-Thomas (RT) space are two
\mathbf{H}^{\dive}
-conforming finite element spaces
BrFo12
. Together with their appropriate pressure spaces they form inf-sup stable pairs for the Stokes equations. However, these spaces are non-conforming with respect to
\mathbf{H}^{1}
. One approach of handling the lack of smoothness of these spaces has been to modify the weak formulation using interior penalty terms as in discontinuous Galerkin methods to enforce tangential continuity across interior edges of the mesh
WangHdiv2007
;
CocKanSch07
. We combine this strategy (presented for fitted meshes) with ghost penalty stabilization terms we developed in
FraHaNilZa22
and present a cut finite element discretization for the Stokes problem based on the BDM space for the velocity with the following properties:

1.

Optimal rates of convergence of the approximate velocity
and pressure;

2.

Well-posed linear systems where the condition number of the system matrix scales as for the fitted finite element discretization;

3.

Pointwise divergence-free velocity approximations;

We also propose an unfitted discretization based on the vorticity-velocity-pressure formulation of the Stokes equations. For some boundary conditions this formulation is beneficial
Dub02
;
Hanot2021
since discretizations that fall into an appropriate de Rham complex have been developed for fitted meshes
Hanot2021
using the Raviart-Thomas (RT) space for the velocity. We develop a cut finite element discretization for this formulation based on the Raviart-Thomas space for the velocity field and propose new ghost penalty stabilization terms. We study two strategies for imposing Dirichlet boundary conditions weakly. The tangential component of the velocity field is imposed as a natural boundary condition but the normal component,
\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}
, is weakly imposed either via a penalty parameter or via a Lagrange multiplier variable.

Our challenge has been to develop unfitted discretizations that have all the three properties above. We have chosen to utilize
\mathbf{H}^{\dive}
-conforming elements in order to be able to also obtain high order approximations for the Stokes equations. In order to guarantee pointwise divergence-free approximations we show that it is necessary to use proper stabilization terms and to guarantee that the approximate velocity
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
satisfies
\int_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0
.

The third property above usually implies pressure robustness for standard mixed finite element methods
VolkerDivConstr2017
. Our numerical results indicate that this is only the case when the normal component
\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}
at the boundary is prescribed strongly. Thus, when the boundary condition is imposed weakly pressure robustness can be lost due to the error at the boundary.
We do not analyze the proposed methods in this paper but we provide a careful numerical study that illustrates challenges for unfitted discretizations. In particular, we discuss errors due to weak imposition of essential boundary conditions and study how to reduce these errors.

The outline of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
we introduce the computational mesh and the finite element spaces which our discretizations will be built upon. In Section
3
we present a discretization for the standard velocity-pressure formulation and in Section
4
we present two dicretizations of the vorticity-velocity-pressure formulation of the Stokes problem. In Section
5
we show that the proposed discretizations results in velocity approximations that are pointwise divergence-free. We discuss the influence on the divergence-free property of the approximate velocity when Dirichlet boundary conditions are enforced weakly in Section
5.1
. Efficient implementations of the stabilization terms are discussed in Section
6
. We present results from numerical experiments in Section
7
and finally, in Section
8
we summarize our findings.

3
A non-conforming mixed method - Method NC

Functions in
\mathbf{V}_{k,h}
are tangentially discontinuous vector-valued functions, meaning that for
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{V}_{k,h}
, the tangential component
\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{t}
is a discontinuous function across interior edges in the mesh. The space
\mathbf{V}_{k,h}
lacks the smoothness to be conforming with respect to
\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{\mathcal{T}_{h}})
, thus
\mathbf{V}_{k,h}\not\subset\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{\mathcal{T}_{h}})
. One approach of handling the lack of smoothness is to modify the weak formulation using interior penalty terms to enforce tangential continuity across all interior edges of the mesh, see
WangHdiv2007
;
WangRobust2009
. Following this approach we present a cut finite element discretization of the Stokes problem (
1.1
)-(
1.3
). Find
(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h})\in\mathbf{V}_{k,h}\times Q_{\hat{k},h}^{0}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})-\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},p_{h})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h}),\

\displaystyle\text{ for all }\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{V}_{k,h}^{0},

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{0}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},q_{h})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\text{ for all }q_{h}\in Q_{\hat{k},h}.

(3.2)

Here

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}):=a(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})+s_{a}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}),

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},q_{h}):=b(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},q_{h})+s_{b}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},q_{h}),

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{0}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},q_{h}):=b_{0}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},q_{h})+s_{b}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},q_{h}),

(3.5)

\displaystyle a(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}(\mu\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{h})_{T\cap\Omega}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{T\in\mathcal{G}_{h}}\left(-(\mu\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})_{T\cap\partial\Omega}+(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\mu\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n})_{T\cap\partial\Omega}+(\lambda_{\boldsymbol{u}}h^{-1}\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})_{T\cap\partial\Omega}\right)+t(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}),

(3.6)

\displaystyle t(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{E}_{h,\Omega}}\left(-(\{\mu\nabla(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{t})\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\},\llbracket\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{t}\rrbracket)_{F}+(\llbracket\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{t}\rrbracket,\{\mu\nabla(\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{t})\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\})_{F}+(\lambda_{t}h^{-1}\llbracket\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{t}\rrbracket,\llbracket\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{t}\rrbracket)_{F}\right),

(3.7)

\displaystyle b_{0}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},p_{h})

\displaystyle:=(\dive\boldsymbol{v}_{h},p_{h})_{\Omega},

(3.8)

\displaystyle b(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},p_{h})

\displaystyle:=b_{0}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},p_{h})-\sum_{T\in\mathcal{G}_{h}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n},p_{h})_{T\cap\partial\Omega},

(3.9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h})

\displaystyle:=(\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})_{\Omega}+\sum_{T\in\mathcal{G}_{h}}\left((\lambda_{\boldsymbol{u}}h^{-1}\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})_{T\cap\partial\Omega}+(\boldsymbol{g},\mu\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n})_{T\cap\partial\Omega}\right),

(3.10)

where
\lambda_{t}
and
\lambda_{\boldsymbol{u}}
are penalty parameters and chosen to be positive constants and we use the mixed ghost penalty stabilization terms developed in
FraHaNilZa22

\displaystyle s_{a}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}\sum_{j=0}^{k+1}\tau_{a}h^{2j+\gamma_{a}}(\llbracket D^{j}_{\boldsymbol{n}_{F}}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\rrbracket,\llbracket D^{j}_{\boldsymbol{n}_{F}}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rrbracket)_{F},

(3.11)

\displaystyle s_{b}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},q_{h})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}\sum_{j=0}^{\hat{k}}\tau_{b}h^{2j+1}(\llbracket D^{j}(\dive\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\rrbracket,\llbracket D^{j}q_{h}\rrbracket)_{F}.

(3.12)

Note that
\llbracket D^{j}q_{h}\rrbracket
denotes the jump in the generalised derivative of order
j
across the edge
F
. Similarly,
\llbracket D^{j}_{\boldsymbol{n}_{F}}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rrbracket
denotes the jump in the normal component of the generalised derivative, with
\llbracket D^{0}_{\boldsymbol{n}_{F}}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rrbracket=\llbracket\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rrbracket
. The stabilization parameters
\tau_{a}>0,\tau_{b}>0,
and we choose
\gamma_{a}=-1
for this method.

To derive this method we multiplied the Stokes problem with test functions
(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},q_{h})\in\mathbf{V}_{k,h}^{0}\times Q_{\hat{k},h}
(we clarify why we choose these test spaces and not for example
\mathbf{V}_{k,h}\times Q_{\hat{k},h}^{0}
in Section
5.1
), applied integration by parts elementwise, and added terms in the weak form to enforce tangential continuity and the Dirichlet boundary condition weakly following Nitsche’s method. We have chosen to make the bilinear form
a(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})
anti-symmetric. The advantage of the non-symmetric version of the interior penalty method and Nitsche’s method is that the choice of the penalty parameter is less sensitive than in the symmetric version, see e.g.
FreSte95
;
BoiBur16
. Important to note is that the presented method is non-symmetric due to the difference between
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{B}_{0}
. We use
\mathcal{B}_{0}
since we do not want to perturb the divergence condition, see Theorem
1
. Enforcing the boundary conditions with a Lagrange multiplier (instead of Nitsche’s method), as in
BurHanLarst23
, would lead to a symmetric method. See also Method C2 in Section
4
.

Since the proposed scheme is based on the boundary-fitted scheme of
WangHdiv2007
;
WangRobust2009
, the convergence order of the pressure variable is expected to be optimal with respect to the larger pair
\mathbf{BDM}_{k+1}\times Q_{k}
but not with respect to the pair
\mathbf{RT}_{k}\times Q_{k}
. For the Raviart-Thomas space the method is expected to achieve a convergence in the pressure of order
\hat{k}
. See the a priori analysis in
WangHdiv2007
;
VolkerDivConstr2017
for details.

4
A conforming mixed vorticity method - Method C

We start by rewriting the velocity-pressure formulation (
1.1
)-(
1.3
) of the Stokes equations by introducing the vorticity. We then present two conforming numerical discretizations of the vorticity-velocity-pressure formulation of the Stokes equations. In the first discretization a penalty parameter is utilized to enforce the boundary condition and we refer to this method as Method C1 while in the second discretization, Method C2 a Lagrange multiplier is used.

We introduce a new variable, the vorticity

\omega:=\mu\crl\boldsymbol{u}.

(4.1)

We make use of the incompressibility
\dive\boldsymbol{u}=0
to write
-\dive(\mu\nabla\boldsymbol{u})=\crl(\mu\crl\boldsymbol{u})-\nabla(\mu\dive\boldsymbol{u})=\crl\omega.
The Stokes problem (
1.1
)-(
1.3
) becomes
Dub02
;
Hanot2021

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \mu^{-1}\omega-\crl\boldsymbol{u}&=0,&&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
\crl\omega+\nabla p&=\boldsymbol{f},&&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
\dive\boldsymbol{u}&=0,&&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
\boldsymbol{u}&=\boldsymbol{g},&&\text{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

(4.2)

Note that the following exact sequence holds

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\hookrightarrow H^{1}(\Omega)\overset{\crl}{\to}\mathbf{H}^{\dive}(\Omega)\overset{\dive}{\to}L^{2}(\Omega)\to 0

(4.3)

That the sequence above is exact means that the kernel of a given operator in the sequence above is the image of the preceding operator in the same sequence. In particular, the divergence of the curl of a scalar field in two space dimensions is
0
by virtue of the commutativity of
x-
and
y
-derivatives.

Multiplying the second equation of (
4.2
) with a test function
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{H}^{\dive}(\Omega)
and integrating by parts we get

\int_{\Omega}\crl\omega\cdot\boldsymbol{v}+\int_{\partial\Omega}p\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}-\int_{\Omega}p\dive\boldsymbol{v}=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}.

(4.4)

Multiplying the first equation of (
4.2
) with
\phi\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega):=H^{1}(\Omega)
, and integrating by parts we have

\int_{\Omega}\mu^{-1}\omega\phi-\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\crl\phi=\int_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{t}\phi.

(4.5)

Since the term
-(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{t},\phi)_{\partial\Omega}
appears naturally in the integration by parts, we handle the boundary condition (
1.3
) such that the tangential component
\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{t}
is treated as a natural boundary condition. The normal component
\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}
can be enforced strongly or weakly. We define the space
\mathbf{H}^{\dive}_{\boldsymbol{g}}(\Omega)
as the space of vector fields in
\mathbf{H}^{\dive}(\Omega)
(see (
2.7
)) that satisfy (in the distributional sense) the boundary condition
\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}
on
\partial\Omega
.
The weak formulation of (
4.2
) when
\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}
is imposed strongly, i.e. in the space, reads as follows. Find
(\omega,\boldsymbol{u},p)\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega)\times\mathbf{H}^{\dive}_{\boldsymbol{g}}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)
such that

\displaystyle(\mu^{-1}\omega,\phi)_{\Omega}-(\boldsymbol{u},\crl\phi)_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{t},\phi)_{\partial\Omega},\

\displaystyle\forall\phi\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega),

(4.6)

\displaystyle(\crl\omega,\boldsymbol{v})_{\Omega}+(p,\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{n})_{\partial\Omega}-(p,\dive\boldsymbol{v})_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v})_{\Omega},\

\displaystyle\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{H}^{\dive}_{0}(\Omega),

(4.7)

\displaystyle(\dive\boldsymbol{u},q)

\displaystyle=0,\

\displaystyle\forall q\in L^{2}(\Omega).

(4.8)

In the next section we develop two unfitted finite element schemes where
\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}
is imposed weakly, either via a penalty parameter or via a Lagrange multiplier.

4.1
The finite element method

We choose conforming finite element subspaces
W_{k+1,h}\subset\mathcal{H}(\Omega),\mathbf{V}_{k,h}\subset\mathbf{H}^{\dive}(\Omega_{\mathcal{T}_{h}})
with
\mathbf{V}_{k}=\mathbf{RT}_{k}
, and
Q_{k,h}\subset L^{2}(\Omega_{\mathcal{T}_{h}})
. These are the piecewise continuous polynomial functions of degree less than or equal to
k+1
, Raviart-Thomas elements, and piecewise discontinuous polynomial functions of degree less than or equal to
k
.

Here we present two discretizations. The first, which we refer to as Method C1, uses penalty and the second, which we refer to as Method C2, uses a Lagrange multiplier in order to enforce Dirichlet boundary conditions on unfitted boundaries. See Section
7.4
for details on the discretization with penalty (Method C1) in 3D.
Method C2 requires an extra variable which needs to be stabilized, but results in a symmetric formulation and in our numerical experiments the method performed better for Dirichlet boundary conditions.

4.1.1
Method C1: enforcing the essential boundary condition with penalty

Find
(\omega_{h},\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h})\in W_{k+1,h}\times\mathbf{V}_{k,h}\times Q_{k,h}^{0}
such that

\displaystyle m(\omega_{h},\phi_{h})-c(\phi_{h},\boldsymbol{u}_{h})

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{t},\phi_{h})_{\partial\Omega},\

\displaystyle\forall\phi_{h}\in W_{k+1,h},

(4.9)

\displaystyle\check{a}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})+c(\omega_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})-\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},p_{h})

\displaystyle\forall\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{V}_{k,h}^{0},

(4.10)

\displaystyle\forall q_{h}\in Q_{k,h}.

(4.11)

and

(4.13)

\displaystyle:=(\crl\phi_{h},\boldsymbol{u}_{h})_{\Omega}+s_{c}(\phi_{h},\boldsymbol{u}_{h}),

(4.14)

\displaystyle s_{c}(\phi_{h},\boldsymbol{u}_{h})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\tau_{c}h^{2j+1}(\llbracket D^{j}_{\boldsymbol{n}_{F}}(\crl\phi_{h})\rrbracket,\llbracket D^{j}_{\boldsymbol{n}_{F}}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\rrbracket)_{F}.

(4.15)

5
The divergence-free property

We now show that all the cut finite element discretizations presented in Section
3
-
4
produce pointwise divergence-free approximations of solenoidal velocity fields, as fitted standard FEM does with the considered element pairs. We follow the proof in
FraHaNilZa22
. Define the standard ghost penalty term for the pressure:

\displaystyle s_{p}(p_{h},q_{h})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}\sum_{j=0}^{\hat{k}}\tau_{b}h^{2j+1}(\llbracket D^{j}p_{h}\rrbracket,\llbracket D^{j}q_{h}\rrbracket)_{F}.

(5.1)

Theorem 1
(The divergence-free property)

Assume
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\in\mathbf{V}_{k,h}
satisfies
\mathcal{B}_{0}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},q_{h})=0
, for all
q_{h}\in Q_{\hat{k},h}
then
\dive\boldsymbol{u}_{h}=0
.

Proof

We have that
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\in\mathbf{V}_{k,h}
satisfies

0=\mathcal{B}_{0}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},q_{h})=\int_{\Omega}\dive\boldsymbol{u}_{h}q_{h}+\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}\sum_{j=0}^{\hat{k}}\tau_{b}h^{2j+1}(\llbracket D^{j}(\dive\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\rrbracket,\llbracket D^{j}q_{h}\rrbracket)_{F},\quad\forall q_{h}\in Q_{\hat{k},h}.

We may choose
q_{h}=\dive\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
since
\dive\mathbf{V}_{k,h}\subset Q_{\hat{k},h}
. Then we have

0=\|\dive\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{\Omega}^{2}+s_{p}(\dive\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\dive\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\gtrsim\|\dive\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{\Omega_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}}^{2}\geq 0,

(5.2)

where we used that for any
q_{h}\in Q_{\hat{k},h}
,
\|q_{h}\|_{\Omega}^{2}+s_{p}(q_{h},q_{h})\gtrsim\|q_{h}\|^{2}_{\Omega_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}}
. See Lemma 3.8 in
HaLaZa14
or Lemma 5.1 in
MaLaLoRo14
. We can thus conclude that
\dive\boldsymbol{u}_{h}=0
in
\Omega_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}
.

We see from this theorem that it is important to not disturb the condition
\mathcal{B}_{0}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},q_{h})=0
. Next we show that prescribing the mean in the pressure test space will perturb the incompressibility condition when we impose boundary conditions by Nitsche’s method or penalty, and motivate our choice of using the spaces
(\mathbf{V}_{k,h}^{0},Q_{\hat{k},h})
as test spaces.

5.1
Justification and implementation of
\mathbf{V}_{k,h}^{0}

We know that the pressure is only determined up to a constant when Dirichlet boundary conditions are imposed. Determining this unknown constant is usually handled by modifying the space
Q_{\hat{k},h}
by setting the mean value:

Q_{\hat{k},h}^{0}=\left\{q_{h}\in\bigoplus_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}Q_{\hat{k}}(T):\ \int_{\Omega}q_{h}=0\right\}.

(5.3)

The space (
5.3
) is difficult to work with numerically (see
(
BofBreFor13
, Section 5.2.5)
), so equivalently one adds a Lagrange multiplier
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\alpha\int_{\Omega}q_{h}
is added to the weak formulation.

The resulting discrete linear system for Method NC would be: Find solutions
(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h},\alpha)\in\mathbf{V}_{k,h}\times Q_{\hat{k},h}\times\mathbb{R}
to

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})-\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},p_{h})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h}),\

\displaystyle\text{ for all }\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{V}_{k,h},

(5.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{0}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},q_{h})+(\alpha,q_{h})_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\text{ for all }q_{h}\in Q_{\hat{k},h},

(5.5)

\displaystyle(\sigma,p_{h})_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\sigma\in\mathbb{R}.

(5.6)

From (
5.5
) we have for
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
q_{h}=1
that

\displaystyle 0=\int_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}+\int_{\Omega}\alpha

\displaystyle\implies|\Omega|\alpha=-\int_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n},

(5.7)

where we have used integration by parts. Imposing the boundary condition
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|_{\partial\Omega}=\boldsymbol{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}
strongly,
with
\int_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0
as a compatibility criterion on the datum
\boldsymbol{g}
, implies
\alpha=0
for a well-posed problem. By choosing
\chi_{h}=1
in (
4.21
) we see that when
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|_{\partial\Omega}=\boldsymbol{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}
is imposed weakly via a Lagrange multiplier method the condition

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=\int_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0

(5.8)

is satisfied. However, prescribing the normal component
\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}
weakly via Nitsche’s method or penalty yields
\int_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\approx\int_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}
. It is easy to see that the approximate solution
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
will then satisfy

\dive\boldsymbol{u}_{h}=-\alpha.

(5.9)

In the numerical experiments we see that
\alpha
is typically not
0
in this case and the divergence of the approximate velocity is incorrect by exactly the value of
\alpha
, see Section
7.1.2
. Thus, to remedy this issue we introduced the space
\mathbf{V}_{k,h}^{0}
.

We avoid the Lagrange multiplier in (
5.5
) by pairing
\int_{\Omega}p_{h}=0
with a linear equation on the DOFs of the test space
\mathbf{V}_{k,h}
; namely
\int_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0
. This condition is enforced by a Lagrange multiplier in the momentum equation instead. In this way we avoid perturbing the incompressibility condition. We have already written our methods with this in mind by using
\mathbf{V}_{k,h}^{0}
as test space, see equation (

)-(

) for Method NC. We note that since the incompressibility condition holds pointwise, i.e.,
\dive\boldsymbol{u}_{h}=0
, we have
\int_{\Omega}\dive\boldsymbol{u}_{h}=\int_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0
. Hence
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\in\mathbf{V}_{k,h}^{0}
.

6
Efficient stabilization

To obtain stability and control of the condition number of the resulting linear systems independently of how the boundary
\partial\Omega
is positioned relative to the background mesh we have utilized stabilization terms. To obtain less fill in and to have a method that is less sensitive to the stabilization constants one can add these ghost penalty terms only where they are necessary. This can be done by partitioning the unfitted mesh into macro-elements that have a large intersection with
\Omega
and apply stabilization only at interior edges of these macro-elements and never on the interface between two macro-elements, see
LaZa21
. For higher order elements than linear it is easier to implement the version of the ghost penalty terms proposed in
Prauss
and hence avoid jump terms of derivatives. In our implementation of the proposed methods we have used these two modifications of the stabilization terms.

6.1
Macroelement stabilization

To partition the mesh into macroelements that have a large intersection with
\Omega
each element in the active mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}
is first classified either as having a large intersection with the domain
\Omega
or a small intersection. We say that an element
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
has a large
\Omega
-intersection if

\delta\leq\frac{|T\cap\Omega|}{|T|},

(6.1)

where
0<\delta\leq 1
is a constant which is chosen independent of the element and the mesh parameter. We collect all elements with a large intersection in

\mathcal{T}_{H}=\left\{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}:|T\cap\Omega|\geq\delta|T|\right\}.

(6.2)

Using this classification we create a macroelement partition
\mathcal{M}_{h}
of
\Omega_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}
:

•

To each
T_{L}\in\mathcal{T}_{H}
we associate a macroelement mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}(T_{L})
containing
T_{L}
and possibly adjacent elements that are in
\mathcal{T}_{h}\setminus\mathcal{T}_{H}
, i.e., elements classified as having a small intersection with
\Omega
and are connected to
T_{L}
via a bounded number of internal edges.

•

Each element
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
belongs to precisely one macroelement mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}(T_{L})
.

•

Each macroelement
M\in\mathcal{M}_{h}
is the union of elements in
\mathcal{T}_{h}(T_{L})
, i.e.,

M=\bigcup_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}(T_{L})}T.

(6.3)

We denote by
\mathcal{F}_{h}(M)
the set consisting of interior edges of
M\in\mathcal{M}_{h}
. Note that
\mathcal{F}_{h}(M)
is empty when
T_{L}
is the only element in
\mathcal{T}_{h}(T_{L})
.
See Figure
6.1
for an illustration of a macroelement partitioning. Edges in
\mathcal{F}_{h}(M)
are illustrated by dotted lines and black thick lines illustrate the boundary of macroelements consisting of more than one element. We follow Algorithm 1 in
LaZa21
when we construct the macroelement partition in our implementation.

:

macroelements consisting of more than one triangle are colored orange, separated by thick edges. Given a macroelement

, edges

are illustrated by dotted lines.

6.2
Macro patch stabilization for high order elements

For high order elements the framework suggested in
Prauss
for applying stabilization can be beneficial since it avoids the evaluation of kth-order derivatives of the basis functions but yields the same control as the standard ghost penalty terms including derivatives up to order
k
. We therefore apply our proposed mixed stabilization terms into that framework. For each face
F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}(M)
we denote the patch consisting of the two elements sharing face F by
\mathcal{P}_{F}=T\cup T^{\prime}
.
The stabilization terms used in our implementation read:

\displaystyle s_{a}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})

\displaystyle=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}_{h}}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}(M)}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\tau_{a}h^{1-\gamma_{a}}([D^{j}_{\boldsymbol{n}_{F}}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}],[D^{j}_{\boldsymbol{n}_{F}}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}])_{\mathcal{P}_{F}},

(6.4)

\displaystyle s_{b}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},q_{h})

\displaystyle=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}_{h}}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}(M)}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\tau_{b}([D^{j}(\dive\boldsymbol{u}_{h})],[D^{j}q_{h}])_{\mathcal{P}_{F}},

(6.5)

\displaystyle s_{c}(\tau_{h},\boldsymbol{u}_{h})

\displaystyle=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}_{h}}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}(M)}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\tau_{c}([D^{j}_{\boldsymbol{n}_{F}}(\crl\tau_{h})],[D^{j}_{\boldsymbol{n}_{F}}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}])_{\mathcal{P}_{F}},

(6.6)

\displaystyle s_{m}(\omega_{h},\tau_{h})

\displaystyle=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}_{h}}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}(M)}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\tau_{m}([D^{j}_{\boldsymbol{n}_{F}}\omega_{h}],[D^{j}_{\boldsymbol{n}_{F}}\phi_{h}])_{\mathcal{P}_{F}},

(6.7)

\displaystyle s_{p}(p_{h},q_{h})

\displaystyle=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}_{h}}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}(M)}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\tau_{b}([D^{j}p_{h}],[D^{j}q_{h}])_{\mathcal{P}_{F}}.

(6.8)

Here,

[\boldsymbol{u}_{h}]=\boldsymbol{u}_{T}^{e}(\mathbf{x})-\boldsymbol{u}_{T^{\prime}}^{e}(\mathbf{x}),\ \text{for $\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{P}_{F}$},

(6.9)

where
\boldsymbol{u}_{T}
is the restriction of
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
to element
T
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{T}^{e}
is the canonical extension of the polynomial
\boldsymbol{u}_{T}
so that it can be evaluated for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{P}_{F}
.

7.2
Example 2 - Coriolis force, pressure robustness

We consider an example from
VolkerDivConstr2017
, where for
\boldsymbol{u}=(u_{1},u_{2},0)
the momentum balance is modified with a Coriolis force
\boldsymbol{c}\times\boldsymbol{u}
,
so that we consider the problem

\displaystyle-\dive(\mu\nabla\boldsymbol{u}-p\mathbf{I})+\boldsymbol{c}\times\boldsymbol{u}=-\dive(\mu\nabla\boldsymbol{u}-p\mathbf{I})+2\Lambda\begin{bmatrix}-u_{2}\\
u_{1}\end{bmatrix}=0,\quad\Lambda>0.

(7.2)

In the weak formulations of Method NC and Method C, the term
(2\Lambda\begin{bmatrix}-u_{2}\\
u_{1}\end{bmatrix},\boldsymbol{v})_{\Omega}
is added in (
3.1
) and (
4.10
).

For standard boundary-fitted FEM, pressure robustness in a discretization of Stokes and incompressible Navier-Stokes is the following property (which we only describe very briefly, see
VolkerDivConstr2017
for details): A modification of the momentum balance with a gradient term should only impact the pressure solution, i.e.

\displaystyle-\dive(\mu\nabla\boldsymbol{u}-p\mathbf{I})+\nabla\psi\implies(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h})\mapsto(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h}+\psi).

(7.3)

In the present example, since
\crl(\boldsymbol{c}\times\boldsymbol{u})=0
, there is a potential function
\psi
such that
\nabla\psi=\boldsymbol{c}\times\boldsymbol{u}
, and therefore the term
\boldsymbol{c}\times\boldsymbol{u}
should not impact the velocity in a pressure robust scheme.
A necessary condition for (
7.3
) to hold is that
\dive\mathbf{V}_{k,h}\subset Q_{\hat{k},h},
and a question is whether or not this also holds in the unfitted setting.

8
Conclusion

We developed and studied several cut finite element discretizations for the Stokes equations that result in divergence-free velocity approximations. We presented an unfitted discretization, Method NC, based on the non-conforming method of
WangHdiv2007
where essential boundary conditions where enforced using Nitsche’s method, see Section
3
. We also presented two unfitted discretizations for the vorticity-velocity-pressure formulation of the Stokes problem, that we refer to as Method C1 and Method C2, see Section
4
. We obtained best results with Method C2 where
\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}
was imposed using a Lagrange multiplier method compared to Method C1 where a penalty parameter was used. We obtained optimal convergence order both for the velocity and the pressure, with pointwise divergence-free velocity approximations for Method NC with
\mathbf{BDM}
-elements and for Method C2 with
\mathbf{RT}
-elements. The condition number of the resulting linear systems scaled with respect to mesh size as for standard finite element methods. In our implementation, the assembly and solve of the linear systems associated to Method NC is slower than Method C. Method C has an extra variable for the vorticity (and yet another for Method C2), but Method NC has interior edge terms. With that being said, the assembly and solve speed of course depends on the implementation and factors such as CPU, GPU, type of solver, and so on.
x
To guarantee divergence-free velocity approximations two conditions where of importance 1) we used ghost penalty stabilization terms that do not perturb the divergence condition; 2) The condition
\int_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=\int_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0
has to be satisfied.
Our numerical experiments illustrated that a scheme which produces pointwise divergence-free velocity approximations is pressure robust in case
\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}
is imposed strongly, but not necessarily otherwise. Thus, the weak imposition of Dirichlet boundary conditions creates a challenge in the development of a pressure robust unfitted discretization. We found that when Nitsche’s method is used the penalty parameter may have to be chosen very large in order to obtain pressure robustness. In the Lagrange multiplier method the stabilization term is of importance for stability, but perturbs the boundary condition. However, by choosing a larger space for the Lagrange multiplier; we choose
\tilde{k}=k+1
, improved the approximation at the boundary. We also showed that scaling the terms involving the Lagrange multipliers improved the errors from the boundary, though at the price of a larger condition number.
