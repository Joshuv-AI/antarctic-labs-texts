Measurements of Phase Dynamics in Planar Josephson Junctions and SQUIDs
D. Z. Haxell, E. Cheah, F. Krizek, R. Schott, M. F. Ritter, M. Hinderling, W. Belzig, C. Bruder, W. Wegscheider, H. Riel, F. Nichele

Abstract
We experimentally investigate the stochastic phase dynamics of planar Josephson junctions (JJs) and superconducting quantum interference devices (SQUIDs) defined in epitaxial InAs/Al heterostructures, and characterized by a large ratio of Josephson energy to charging energy. We observe a crossover from a regime of macroscopic quantum tunneling to one of phase diffusion as a function of temperature, where the transition temperature T ∗ T^{*} is gate-tunable. The switching probability distributions are shown to be consistent with a small shunt capacitance and moderate damping, resulting in a switching current which is a small fraction of the critical current. Phase locking between two JJs leads to a difference in switching current between that of a JJ measured in isolation and that of the same JJ measured in an asymmetric SQUID loop. In the case of the loop, T ∗ T^{*} is also tuned by a magnetic flux.

Here we investigate the stochastic phase dynamics of hybrid JJs defined in an InAs/Al planar heterostructure
Shabani et al. 2016
. We show that macroscopic quantum tunneling (MQT) and phase diffusion (PD) are the most relevant phase escape regimes. The low-temperature mean switching current
I_{\mathrm{M}}
is a small fraction of the critical current
I_{\mathrm{C}}
, although the Josephson energy
E_{\mathrm{J}}
is significantly larger than the charging energy
E_{\mathrm{C}}

I_{\mathrm{C}}

The dominant phase-escape mechanism is further tuned via temperature, gate voltages and fluxes threading the SQUID. Contrary to conventional metallic JJs, no indication of thermal phase activation is observed. Characteristic experimental features are reproduced with a Monte Carlo simulation of the phase dynamics. Our results indicate that phase dynamics significantly affect the switching current of hybrid devices, and guide towards the realization of novel quantum architectures.

In conclusion, the switching current of planar JJs with highly transmissive ABSs is strongly affected by phase dynamics, even for
E_{\mathrm{J}}/E_{\mathrm{C}}\gg 1
. As a result of moderate dissipation (
Q_{0}<10
) and large plasma frequency,

can differ largely from
I_{\mathrm{C}}
, depending on the details of the JJs and of their electrostatic environment. Phase dynamics can be modified by embeddeding JJs in asymmetric SQUID geometries, resulting in significant changes of
I_{\mathrm{M}}

This intricate physics is relevant for realizing gate-tunable quantum devices and investigating topological phenomena, where hybrid JJs with phase control are widespread.

less reliable; hence we do not present SPDs at temperatures

I.3
Phase Escape in Single JJs

Phase dynamics in Josephson junctions are often described using the analogy of a massive phase particle in a tilted washboard potential, where the particle mass corresponds to the JJ capacitance and its damping to the inverse of its resistance. The zero-resistance state corresponds to the particle being trapped in an energy minimum while the resistive state corresponds to the particle moving along the potential. A transition to the resistive state generally takes place, via quantum or thermal fluctuations, at switching currents
I_{\mathrm{SW}}
lower than the JJ critical current
I_{\mathrm{C}}
. To measure this transition, we apply a sawtooth signal from a waveform generator through a bias resistor to rapidly ramp
I_{\mathrm{DC}}
, and detect a switch to the resistive state by measuring the point at which the voltage across the JJ increases above a threshold. The value of
I_{\mathrm{DC}}
at which this switch occurs is recorded as the switching current,
I_{\mathrm{SW}}
. We measure
I_{\mathrm{SW}}
more than 10,000 times and collect the results in a switching probability distribution (SPD). The SPDs are recorded as a function of temperature, from
20~\mathrm{mK}
to
1~\mathrm{K}
.

T=20~\mathrm{mK}
,
500~\mathrm{mK}
and
800~\mathrm{mK}

\Gamma_{\mathrm{I}}
, MQT escape rate
\Gamma_{\mathrm{Q}}
, retrapping rate

are compared across the bias range

. The green dot indicates

, at which point escape events are measurable. (d) SPDs of JJ1: Experimental data (points) compared with Monte Carlo simulation (lines) for temperatures

. The temperature is indicated by the color, as in the Main Text. (e) Escape rates corresponding to the SPDs in (d).

The SPDs are dependent on the rate at which the DC current is increased,
\nu
. To compare with theoretical expressions for phase dynamic mechanisms, we must convert to a measurement-independent quantity: the escape rate,
\Gamma
. This is done via KFD transform using the following equation
Bezryadin 2012
:

\Gamma(I_{\mathrm{SW}})=\mathrm{SPD}(I_{\mathrm{SW}})\nu\left[1-\int_{0}^{I_{\mathrm{SW}}}\mathrm{SPD}(I)dI\right]^{-1}

(S.1)

where
\nu
is the ramp rate of the DC current, which was
\nu=240~\mathrm{\mu As^{-1}}
for all measurements.

In the following, we will consider equations derived for JJs with a sinusoidal current-phase relation. While our JJs are known to contain highly-transmissive modes, leading to deviations from this sinusoidal behavior, we use the existing theory in the absence of an alternative model and expect only small numerical deviation from the results presented.

The rate of escape of a Josephson junction to the resistive state under the action of a DC bias follows the general dependence
Massarotti et al. 2012
:

\Gamma(I_{\mathrm{SW}},T)=\Omega(I_{\mathrm{SW}},T)e^{-\Delta U(I_{\mathrm{SW}},T)/k_{B}T},

(S.2)

where
\Omega
is the attempt frequency and
\Delta U
is the potential barrier height. Under a reduced DC bias of
\gamma=I_{\mathrm{DC}}/I_{\mathrm{C}}
, the approximate barrier height is
\Delta U=2E_{\mathrm{J}}\left(\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}-\gamma\cos^{-1}\gamma\right)
, with a Josephson energy of
E_{\mathrm{J}}=\hbar I_{\mathrm{C}}/2e
.

A transition to the resistive state can be promoted, for currents lower than
I_{\mathrm{C}}
, by macroscopic quantum tunnelling (MQT) or thermal activation (TA) across the barrier
\Delta U
. In the case of MQT, equation
S.1
is adjusted to the analytical formula for escape
Massarotti et al. 2012

\Gamma_{\mathrm{Q}}=\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{P}}}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{864\pi\Delta U}{\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{P}}}}\mathrm{exp}\left(-7.2\left(1+\frac{0.87}{Q}\right)\frac{\Delta U}{\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{P}}}\right),

(S.3)

where
\omega_{\mathrm{P}}=\omega_{P0}\left(1-\gamma^{2}\right)^{1/4}
is the plasma frequency at
\gamma
and
Q
is the quality factor of the junction. The junction capacitance
C
enters in the bare plasma frequency,
\omega_{P0}=\sqrt{2eI_{\mathrm{C}}/\hbar C}
.

When dissipation is large it is possible for the junction to transition from the resistive to the superconducting state, referred to as retrapping. In this regime, many escape events are required to turn the junction resistive. This is referred to as phase diffusion (PD). For
Q\gg 1
, we can use an analytical formula for this retrapping rate
Massarotti et al. 2012
:

\Gamma_{\mathrm{R}}=\frac{I_{\mathrm{SW}}-I_{\mathrm{R}}}{I_{\mathrm{C}}}\omega_{P0}\sqrt{\frac{E_{\mathrm{J}}}{2\pi k_{B}T}}\mathrm{exp}\left(-\left(\frac{I_{\mathrm{SW}}-I_{\mathrm{R}}}{I_{\mathrm{C}}}\right)^{2}\frac{E_{\mathrm{J}}Q^{2}}{2k_{B}T}\right)

(S.4)

where
I_{\mathrm{R}}
is the retrapping current. We measure intermediately damped junctions with
Q\gtrsim 1
, so this relation can only be considered as an approximation.

The interplay between
\Gamma_{\mathrm{Q}}
and
\Gamma_{\mathrm{R}}
determines the phase escape regime:
\Gamma_{\mathrm{Q}}\gg\Gamma_{\mathrm{R}}
in the MQT regime, whereas the reverse is true for phase diffusion. The rates depend on the junction properties: the critical current
I_{\mathrm{C}}
, the capacitance
C
and the quality factor, which has the zero-temperature value
Q_{0}
. We show in Figs.
S.2
(a-c) the relevant rates in the system for the fit parameters for JJ1:
I_{\mathrm{C}}=3~\mathrm{\mu A}
,
C=1~\mathrm{fF}
and
Q_{0}=7
, as discussed in more detail later. Each panel corresponds to a different temperature:
T=20~\mathrm{mK}
,
T=500~\mathrm{mK}
and
T=800~\mathrm{mK}
, respectively. These highlight the change in the dominant regime from MQT to PD on increasing
T
.

The ramp rate
\Gamma_{\mathrm{I}}(I)=\nu/I
defines the lowest frequencies at which an escape event can be measured, for a given bias current
I
. The point at which this intersects the escape rate gives the lowest bias current
I_{\mathrm{IE}}
at which an escape event is measurable. Since quantum tunneling is the dominant escape mechanism, we define
I_{\mathrm{IE}}
as the current at which
\Gamma_{\mathrm{I}}=\Gamma_{\mathrm{Q}}
, marked in Figs.
S.2
(a-c) with the green dot. The retrapping rate
\Gamma_{\mathrm{R}}
is large for low bias currents but quickly decreases with an increase in
I
. Retrapping is significant when
\Gamma_{\mathrm{R}}
reaches a similar magnitude as
\Gamma_{\mathrm{Q}}
. Escape by thermal activation is given by
\Gamma_{\mathrm{T}}
, and is much smaller than
\Gamma_{\mathrm{Q}}
for all temperatures.

At the lowest temperature, escape by quantum tunneling dominates. This is clear since
\Gamma_{\mathrm{Q}}\gg\Gamma_{\mathrm{R}}
for
I>I_{\mathrm{IE}}
. No retrapping of the phase occurs: a single escape event is sufficient to transition to the resistive state. At
T=500~\mathrm{mK}
,
\Gamma_{\mathrm{Q}}\lesssim\Gamma_{\mathrm{R}}
close to
I_{\mathrm{IE}}
. For these low bias currents, the retrapping probability is high so the probability of escape in the junction is reduced relative to quantum tunneling alone. However,
\Gamma_{\mathrm{Q}}\gg\Gamma_{\mathrm{R}}
at larger bias so escape occurs unhindered by phase diffusion. As the temperature increases, the effect of retrapping becomes more significant. At the high temperature of
800~\mathrm{mK}
, phase diffusion is dominant since
\Gamma_{\mathrm{Q}}\ll\Gamma_{\mathrm{R}}
across the range of escape currents.

The transition temperature
T^{*}
between MQT and PD regimes is experimentally defined as the inflection point of the standard deviation as a function of temperature. This is the point at which the retrapping rate
\Gamma_{\mathrm{R}}
becomes dominant above MQT escape
\Gamma_{\mathrm{Q}}
. We can specify this further as the temperature at which the escape, retrapping and ramp rates are equal,
T^{*}_{\mathrm{IER}}
. This is similar to Ref.
Fenton and Warburton 2008
. The trend of
I_{\mathrm{M}}
with temperature, for the single junctions and the SQUID, changes at
T^{*}
(see Figs. 2(e) and 3(c) in the Main Text). This suggests that our definition of
T^{*}
is appropriate as a measure of the transition between MQT and PD regimes.

The rates in Figs.
S.2
(a-c) are used to model the junction behavior in a Monte Carlo simulation, as in Ref.
Fenton and Warburton 2008
. As the DC bias current is increased, the simulated junction stochastically switches between the superconducting (0) and resistive (1) states according to the relative escape and retrapping rates. The junction is said to be resistive when the state, averaged over a window of current, exceeds 0.1. This process is performed 20,000 times and the generated
I_{\mathrm{SW}}
are combined into a switching probability distribution (SPD).

The simulation uses the critical current
I_{\mathrm{C}}
, capacitance
C
and quality factor
Q_{0}
as input parameters for the rates
\Gamma_{\mathrm{Q}}
and
\Gamma_{\mathrm{R}}
. We fit by hand the SPD at low temperature, obtaining
I_{\mathrm{C}}=3~\mathrm{\mu A}
,
C=1~\mathrm{fF}
and
Q_{0}=7
. We use the Bardeen formula for the temperature dependence of
I_{\mathrm{C}}(T)=I_{\mathrm{C}}(1-T^{2}/T_{C}^{2})^{3/2}
, with
T_{C}=1.18~\mathrm{K}
from experimental results. Since
Q\propto I_{\mathrm{C}}^{1/2}
, we use
Q(T)=Q_{0}(1-T^{2}/T_{C}^{2})^{3/4}
. We use the low-temperature fit result and the assumed temperature dependence to simulate the full dataset. Figures
S.2
(d) and (e) show the SPDs and escape rates for JJ1, respectively. The experimental data (circles) is fitted well by the simulated curve (lines). Deviation at high temperatures between the fit and the data is explained by the simple model used for the temperature dependence. Despite this, we capture the characteristic trend in the data.

We now consider the impact of temperature on the switching current in the MQT regime. In Fig.
S.3
we calculate the SPD as a function of temperature using the same fit parameters as Fig.
S.2
, but considering only MQT escape without the presence of PD (
\Gamma_{\mathrm{R}}=0
). This is instructive to see the impact of the temperature dependence of
I_{\mathrm{C}}
and
Q
on SPDs at low

. In particular, we see that the mean switching current

decreases with increasing temperature [Fig.

(a)]. This is due to the low

of Al, which causes a change in

even at the lowest temperature of our experiment. This is evident in Fig.

(b) (dashed line). The experimental data for
I_{\mathrm{M,1}}
(squares) aligns with the simulated result for MQT in the absence of PD (solid line) up to
T\sim T^{*}
. This is indicative of the dominance of MQT over PD up to
T\sim T^{*}
in the experimental data. In Fig.
S.3
(c) we show the experimental standard deviation
\sigma_{\mathrm{1}}
(squares) and the modelled standard deviation in the case of pure MQT (line). The slight temperature dependence of the fit result is due to the temperature dependence of
Q
, again a consequence of the low

in Al.

In general, the quality factor of the junction is described by
Q=RC\omega_{P}
. For
I_{\mathrm{C}}=3~\mathrm{\mu A}
and
C=1~\mathrm{fF}
we get a resistance of

, much larger than the normal state resistance at low frequency of
R_{N,1}=150~\mathrm{\Omega}

Q\gg 1

. For JJ1,
I_{\mathrm{R}}=600~\mathrm{nA}
giving

, close to the fit value.

Escape by quantum tunneling is dominant up to the critical temperature. By comparing Eqs.
S.2
and
S.3
, we obtain an effective temperature of quantum tunneling escape,

k_{B}T_{Q}=\frac{\hbar\omega_{P}}{7.2(1+0.87/Q)}.

(S.5)

At low temperature,

for JJ1. This exemplifies the large scale of quantum fluctuations relative to thermal excitations. The temperature at which
T_{Q}<T
for the parameters of JJ1 is approximately
1~\mathrm{K}
: thermal activation is not significant up to the critical temperature.

I.4
Estimation of the Geometrical Capacitance

The capacitance
C=1~\mathrm{fF}
obtained in the Monte Carlo simulation is consistent with the geometrical capacitance of JJ1, calculated as the coplanar capacitance between epitaxial Al electrodes and given by the formula
Paul
:

C=\frac{\epsilon_{0}\epsilon_{r}W}{\pi}\ln\left[-2\left(\frac{\beta+1}{\beta-1}\right)\right],

(S.6)

where
\beta=\sqrt[4]{1-\frac{L^{2}}{(L+2L_{S})^{2}}}
. We use the dielectric constant
\epsilon_{r}=12.3
of InAs at high frequency. The geometrical parameters of the junction are: lateral extent of JJ electrodes,
W=5~\mathrm{\mu m}
; separation of JJ electrodes,
L=40~\mathrm{nm}
; and length of superconducting leads,
L_{S}=250~\mathrm{nm}
. This gives
C=1.4~\mathrm{fF}
. The calculation does not consider the effect of the top gate electrode, which is grounded via a low-impedance terminal. It therefore acts as a screening layer for the electric field between the two junction electrodes. This reduces the capacitance, up to a factor of 2 for complete screening of half of the field between the Al electrodes. The presence of JJ2 in the SQUID loop contributes an additional shunt capacitance of
C_{2}=0.2~\mathrm{fF}
, for junction parameters
W_{2}=1.6~\mathrm{\mu m}
,
L_{2}=100~\mathrm{nm}
and
L_{S,2}=250~\mathrm{nm}
. Screening of the field by the electrostatic gates may reduce this by up to a factor of 2. The contribution of JJ2 to the capacitance of JJ1 is therefore small, and does not modify the conclusions of the Main Text.

The large leads of the device might contribute as well to the shunt capacitance. Their geometrical capacitance, calculated with Eq.
S.6
, is about
3~\mathrm{fF}
. This value is likely decreased by the presence of the large gate electrodes between them. This analysis does not consider shunt capacitances present in the bonding pads and in the wiring of the dilution refrigerator. The extent to which shunt capacitances contributes to the plasma frequency is an open question, investigated in Refs.
Devoret et al.
;
Kautz and Martinis
;
Gloos and Anders
. The interpretation in the Main Text of a planar Josephson junction with a small capacitance
C
is supported by the result of an analytical fit to Eq.
S.3
, a Monte Carlo simulation of the temperature dependence, a consideration of the geometrical capacitance of the junction and a comparison to values previously reported in literature
Lee et al. 2011
;
Mayer et al. 2020
.

The regime of the SQUID is dominated by JJ1, the large
I_{\mathrm{C}}
component, so we can change the SQUID behavior by varying
V_{\mathrm{G1}}
. For
V_{\mathrm{G1}}=-300~\mathrm{mV}
, the SQUID undergoes direct transitions between MQT and PD depending on
B_{\perp}
[Fig. 4(b) in the Main Text]. The full dataset is shown in Fig.
S.8
for
20~\mathrm{mK}
to
900~\mathrm{mK}
: the mean switching current
I_{\mathrm{M,S}}
in (a) and the standard deviation in (b).

The oscillation amplitude
\Delta I_{\mathrm{M,S}}/2
is shown in Fig.
S.8
(c). The enhancement in switching current at low temperatures is again observed, where quantum tunneling is dominant. We also observe the characteristic kink in
\Delta I_{\mathrm{M,S}}/2
, in this case at
T\approx 0.52~\mathrm{K}
concomitant with the lower transition temperature to the phase diffusive regime.

At
T=20~\mathrm{mK}
, we observe a large variation in the standard deviation
\sigma_{\mathrm{S}}
depending on the field
B_{\perp}
. At the maximum of
I_{\mathrm{M,S}}
(diamond),
\sigma_{\mathrm{S}}
is large at low temperature. This is consistent with quantum tunneling as the dominant mechanism of phase escape. Instead,
\sigma_{\mathrm{S}}=20~\mathrm{nA}
at the minimum (triangle), indicating that phase diffusive effects are strong. The traces in
\sigma_{\mathrm{S}}
at these field values are shown in Fig.
S.8
(d) by their respective markers. The large difference in
\sigma_{\mathrm{S}}
is evident at low temperatures, as indicated by the blue shading, where the external magnetic field determines the extent of phase diffusion in the SQUID. On increasing
T
towards the transition temperature, the difference in
\sigma_{\mathrm{S}}
reduces until the SQUID is fully phase-diffusive at all values of
B_{\perp}
.

Figures

(b). At the minima, the SQUID is fully phase diffusive with

in Fig.

(d). We note that at some values of

in the low-temperature curve the switching current went above the signal amplitude, artificially truncating the SPD and rendering its standard deviation unphysical: these points have been removed from the trace.
