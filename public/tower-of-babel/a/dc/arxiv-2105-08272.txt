Two-species competition model with chemotaxis: well-posedness, stability and dynamics
Guanlin Li, Yao Yao

Abstract
. We study a system of PDEs modeling the population dynamics of two competitive species whose spatial movements are governed by both diffusion and mutually repulsive chemotaxis effects. We prove that solutions to this system are globally well-posed, without any smallness assumptions on the chemotactic coefficients. Moreover, in the weak competition regime, we prove that neither species can be driven to extinction as the time goes to infinity, regardless of how strong the chemotaxis coefficients are. Finally, long-time behaviors of the system are studied both analytically in the weakly nonlinear regime, and numerically in the fully nonlinear regime.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

In this paper, we consider two biological species that compete for resources, with their spatial movements governed by diffusion and
mutually repulsive
chemotaxis effects.
Let us denote by
u(x,t)
and
v(x,t)
the population densities of the two species, and let the spatial domain be a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with smooth boundary, where
n\geq 1
.
The time evolution of
(u(x,t),v(x,t))
is governed by the following three effects: competition, diffusion, and chemotactic movements. Before stating the full system of equations, we will discuss each of the three effects and briefly review the relevant literature.

(1)
Competition between the two species
. The competition effect will be modeled by a Lotka–Volterra-type system
[
39
,
58
]
for competing species. If
u
and
v
are spatially homogeneous (thus they are only functions of
t
), after a normalization by suitable multiplicative constants, their time evolution is described by the following system of ODEs

\begin{cases}\dot{u}(t)=b_{1}u(1-u-a_{1}v),\\
\dot{v}(t)=b_{2}v(1-v-a_{2}u),\end{cases}

(1.1)

where
a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2}>0
. It is well-known (see
[
35
, Section 12]
for example) that solutions to (
1.1
) exhibit different types of asymptotic behavior depending on the coefficients
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
. Namely, in the
weak competition
regime
a_{1},a_{2}\in(0,1)
, there is a unique positive steady state

(\overline{u},\overline{v})=\left(\frac{1-a_{1}}{1-a_{1}a_{2}},\frac{1-a_{2}}{1-a_{1}a_{2}}\right),

(1.2)

and it is the global attractor of all solutions with positive initial data. This indicates that the two species co-exist in the long run. In the
strong competition
regime
a_{1}>1
,
a_{2}>1
, (
1.2
) is still the unique positive steady state, however it is unstable, and for a generic positive initial data, the solution either converges to
(0,1)
or
(1,0)
as
t\to\infty
, depending on the initial data. Finally, if
0<a_{1}<1<a_{2}
, there is no positive steady state, and all positive solutions converge to
(1,0)
. Likewise, if
0<a_{2}<1<a_{1}
all positive solutions converge to
(0,1)
.

(2)
Spatial diffusion effects
. If the species have random spatial movements, this effect is typically modeled by diffusion terms
d_{1}\Delta u
and
d_{2}\Delta v
in the evolution for
u
and
v
, where
d_{1},d_{2}>0
. When both diffusion and competition effects are taken into account, the time evolution of
(u(x,t),v(x,t))
satisfies the following system of reaction-diffusion equations:

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u=d_{1}\Delta u+b_{1}u(1-u-a_{1}v)&\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\partial_{t}v=d_{2}\Delta v+b_{2}v(1-v-a_{2}u)&\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}=\frac{\partial v}{\partial\nu}=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\end{cases}

(1.3)

where
\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}
is the outward normal derivative at
\partial\Omega
, and the Neumann boundary condition indicates that both densities have zero flux at the boundary.
This system has been extensively studied by both mathematicians and ecologists. In the weak competition regime, it is known that the spatially homogeneous positive steady state (
1.2
) is the global attractor for any positive initial population
[
15
,
19
]
, and there are no non-constant steady states. In the strong competition regime things are more complicated: in addition to the (unstable) steady state (
1.2
) and the stable constant steady states
(0,1)
and
(1,0)
, the system can have some stable spatially inhomogeneous steady states for some non-convex
\Omega
(e.g. dumbbell shaped) when the diffusion coefficients
d_{1},d_{2}
are not too large
[
43
,
44
]
. See
[
14
,
18
,
24
,
27
]
for results on co-existence steady states of (
1.3
) with Dirichlet boundary conditions. When
\Omega=\mathbb{R}
is the whole real line, traveling wave solutions to (
1.3
) have been well-studied
[
26
,
33
,
48
,
52
,
55
]
. For further results on this system and related models (e.g. non-homogeneous environment, cross diffusion), see
[
6
,
7
,
11
,
12
,
20
,
40
,
41
,
45
,
53
]
and the references therein.

(3)
Chemotactic movements.
Besides the above two effects, the two species may also experience chemotaxis effects in response to certain chemicals. For a single species, the most classical model for chemotaxis is the Keller-Segel equation
[
34
]
. It describes the collective motion of cells (slime mold or certain bacteria such as E. coli) with density
\rho(x,t)
that are attracted by a chemical substance with density
c(x,t)
. The chemical attractant is emitted by the cells themselves, and it also diffuses in space and decays in time. This leads to the following system of parabolic equations

\begin{cases}\rho_{t}=\Delta\rho-\chi\nabla\cdot(\rho\nabla c)&\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T)\\
\varepsilon c_{t}=\Delta c-c+\rho&\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T)\\
\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial\nu}=\frac{\partial c}{\partial\nu}=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T).\end{cases}

(1.4)

Since the chemical attractant reaches its equilibrium in a much faster timescale, it is common to assume that
c
is quasi-stationary by taking the
\varepsilon\to 0
limit, so that the second equation of (
1.4
) is replaced by the elliptic equation

c-\Delta c=\rho,

(1.5)

and (
1.4
) becomes a parabolic-elliptic system. Since the primary interest of this work is on two-species chemotaxis models, we will not discuss the vast literature on the Keller-Segel equation. We refer the readers to the surveys
[
2
,
28
,
29
,
30
]
and the references therein. Below we will state the two-species system with all three effects, and introduce some relevant literature on similar models.

1.2.
Two-species system with competition, diffusion, and chemotaxis

In addition to the diffusion and competition effects in (
1.3
), we further assume that
u
and
v
each emits some chemical substance with their concentrations denoted by
c[u]
and
c[v]
respectively, and each species exhibits chemotactic movements driven by the chemical emitted by the other species. The new system with chemotaxis reads

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u=d_{1}\Delta u+\chi_{1}\nabla\cdot(u\nabla c[v])+b_{1}u(1-u-a_{1}v)&\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\partial_{t}v=d_{2}\Delta v+\chi_{2}\nabla\cdot(v\nabla c[u])+b_{2}v(1-v-a_{2}u)&\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}=\frac{\partial v}{\partial\nu}=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\end{cases}

(1.6)

where the sign and magnitude of the chemotactic coefficients
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}
indicate the type (attractive or repulsive) and strength of the chemotactic movements.
For a population density
\rho
(here
\rho
is either
u
or
v
), following (
1.5
) (which comes from the
\varepsilon\to 0
limit of the second equation in (
1.4
)), we assume that the chemical concentration
c[\rho]
solves the elliptic equation

\begin{cases}c-\Delta c=\rho&\text{ in }\Omega\\
\frac{\partial c}{\partial\nu}=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

(1.7)

Note that in the system (
1.6
), the drift velocity for
u
and
v
are given by
-\chi_{1}\nabla c[v]
and
-\chi_{2}\nabla c[u]
respectively.
Since
u
and
v
are competing species, throughout this paper we assume that
\chi_{1}>0
and
\chi_{2}>0
, indicating that both species tend to move away from the chemical emitted by the other species (i.e.
u
and
v
are
mutually repulsive
). The goal of this paper is to investigate the influence of the mutually repulsive chemotactic effect on the solutions, in particular, whether it affects the global well-posedness and long time behaviors of solutions.

In the past two decades, two-species chemotaxis models have attracted much interest. Many previous works deal with diffusion and chemotaxis effects only (i.e. the competition terms are absent), where the mass of each species is conserved. In this case, the system often has an associated monotone energy functional (as in the Keller–Segel equation), and many analysis results are based on this variational structure. When multiple species are mutually attracted to each other by reacting to different chemicals,
[
64
]
studied global existence of solutions and equilibria using the energy functional. When both species are attracted by the same chemical substance produced by themselves,
[
25
]
identified some conditions that lead to global existence and finite time blow-up, and later a sharp condition in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
was established in
[
13
]
, and blow-up examples for
n>2
were constructed in
[
3
]
. For a general multi-species chemotaxis model with attraction and repulsion between the species, the steady states of the system and their stability were studied by
[
31
]
. For the system (
1.6
)–(
1.7
) without the competition terms, various criteria for global well-posedness and blow-up were derived in
[
56
]
. When the two-species system has nonlocal interaction, well-posedness of solutions via gradient flow theory and properties of steady states are studied by
[
22
,
23
,
42
]
. See
[
5
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
21
,
22
]
for studies on two-species systems with nonlocal interaction and cross-diffusion.

In the presence of all the three effects (diffusion, chemotaxis, and competition), note that the system does not have any variational structure. When there is only one chemical
c
(where
c
is generated/consumed by both
u
and
v
in the sense that
\varepsilon c_{t}=\Delta c-c\pm(u+v)
), the parabolic-elliptic system (with
\varepsilon=0
) was studied by
[
47
,
54
,
57
]
, and the corresponding parabolic-parabolic systems (with
\varepsilon>0
) were studied by
[
1
,
4
,
37
,
46
,
60
,
62
,
63
]
. In these works, conditions that lead to global well-posedness were established, and parameter regimes for which the constant steady state (
1.2
) is asymptotically stable were also identified.

More recently, the system (
1.6
)–(
1.7
) with two chemicals
c[u]
and
c[v]
has been studied in the following works.
[
65
]
considered the mutually attractive case (i.e.
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}<0
), and showed that when
-\chi_{1},-\chi_{2}
are below certain thresholds, the system is globally well-posed and converges towards the steady state (
1.2
).
[
17
]
showed that when
|\chi_{1}|,|\chi_{2}|
are below certain thresholds, (
1.6
)–(
1.7
) has a global solution, and the solution converges towards (
1.2
) in the weak competition regime, and converges towards
(1,0)
if
0<a_{1}<1<a_{2}
.
[
59
]
studied the mutually attractive case, and showed that solutions converge towards constant steady states when
b_{1},b_{2}
are sufficiently large.
[
61
]
studied the system when
c[u]
and
c[v]
depends on
u,v
in a nonlinear way. Let us also point out that for the corresponding parabolic-parabolic system, conditions that leads to global well-posedness and finite-time blow-up were established in
[
38
,
49
,
51
]
.

1.3.
Our results.

Our goal is to study the well-posedness, stability of steady state, and the dynamics of the system (
1.6
)–(
1.7
) in the
mutually repulsive
case
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}>0
. We aim to explore the following questions:

(a)

For any
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}>0
, is the solution
(u,v)
to (
1.6
)–(
1.7
) always globally well-posed in time?

(b)

When
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}>0
and the system is in the weak competition regime
a_{1},a_{2}\in(0,1)
, is it possible that one species becomes extinct as
t\to\infty
?

(c)

What is the long-time behavior of (
1.6
)–(
1.7
)?

Our first result is as follows, which gives a positive answer to question (a). It shows that in the mutually repulsive case
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}>0
, regardless of how large
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}
are, the system (
1.6
)–(
1.7
) is globally well-posed in all dimensions
n\geq 1
, and any solution is globally bounded in time. Recall that global well-posedness has been previously established by
[
17
,
65
]
under some smallness assumptions of
|\chi_{1}|
and
|\chi_{2}|
. Also, for any
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}>0
, global well-posedness for the system (
1.6
)–(
1.7
) without the competition terms was obtained in
[
56
]
in dimensions
n\leq 3
.

Theorem 1.1
.

For
n\geq 1
, let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded domain with smooth boundary, and assume
\chi_{1},\chi_{2},a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2}>0
. For any non-negative initial data
u_{0},v_{0}\in C(\overline{\Omega})
, the unique classical solution
(u,v)
to (
1.6
)–(
1.7
) exists globally in time, where
u,v\in C^{2,1}(\Omega\times(0,\infty))\cap C(\overline{\Omega}\times[0,\infty))
, and
u,v
are uniformly bounded above for all times.

In addition, for any
t_{0}>0
, the equation has the instant regularization effect, in the sense that

\sup_{t>t_{0}}\left(\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|v(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}}\right)\leq g(t_{0}),

(1.8)

where
g(t_{0})
only depends on
t_{0},\Omega,n
and the coefficients of (
1.6
), and in particular does not depend on the initial data
u_{0},v_{0}
.

The proof of Thorem
1.1
is given in Section
2.1
. The proof is based on an energy estimate, where the key observation is that the mutually repulsive nature of the two species allows us to obtain a priori
L^{p}
bounds on
u,v
that are uniformly bounded in time for any
1\leq p<\infty
.

Next we move on to question (b) that concerns the
weak competition regime

a_{1},a_{2}\in(0,1)
. In this regime, for small
|\chi_{1}|,|\chi_{2}|
,
[
17
]
showed that the constant steady state (
1.2
) is the global attractor for the system (
1.6
)–(
1.7
). Clearly we do not expect this property to hold for large
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}
: a simple linear stability criterion in Section
3.1
shows that (
1.2
) is linearly unstable for large
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}
. Indeed, numerics in Section
4
suggest that solutions can form many patterns for large
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}
, including some steady states where one species has a much smaller mass than the other (see Figure
3
(b)). It is then a natural question whether one of the species could be driven to extinction as
t\to\infty
for large
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}
.
Our second result shows that this can never happen in the weak competition regime for any
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}>0
, giving a negative answer to question (b).

Theorem 1.2
.

For
n\geq 1
, let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded domain with smooth boundary, and assume
\chi_{1},\chi_{2},a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2}>0
. Assume that
a_{1},a_{2}
are in the weak competition regime, that is,
a_{1},a_{2}\in(0,1)
. For any initial data
u_{0}(x),v_{0}(x)\in C(\overline{\Omega})
that are not identically zero, the solution
(u,v)
to (
1.6
)–(
1.7
) satisfies

\|u(t)\|_{L^{1}}\geq\delta\min\{\|u(1)\|_{L^{1}},1\}\text{ and }\|v(t)\|_{1}\geq\delta\min\{\|v(1)\|_{L^{1}},1\}\quad\text{ for all }t\geq 1,

(1.9)

where
\delta>0
only depends on
\Omega,n
and the coefficients of (
1.6
), and in particular is independent of
u_{0},v_{0}
.

Note that (
1.9
) gives a lower bound of the mass of
u(\cdot,t),v(\cdot,t)
for all
t\geq 1
(where
0<\delta\ll 1
is a constant that is independent of the initial data), which implies that neither species can be driven to extinction. We point out that it is necessary to let the right hand side of (
1.9
) dependent on the mass of
u(\cdot,1),v(\cdot,1)
(rather than
u_{0}
,
v_{0}
), since an initial data
u_{0}
with mass 1 but very spiky profile (close to a delta function) can have its mass drop to an arbitrarily small number after a very short time, due to the
-b_{1}u^{2}
reaction term in (
1.6
).

Our next results concern the question (c). We first consider the case when
\chi_{1}=\chi_{2}
(call it
\chi
), and obtain a linear stability criteria for the constant steady state (
1.2
) in terms of
\chi
in Section
3.1
. Namely, there is a constant
\chi^{*}
, such that (
1.2
) is linearly stable when
0<\chi<\chi^{*}
, and linearly unstable when
\chi>\chi^{*}
. In the
weakly nonlinear regime
, i.e. when
\chi=\chi^{*}+\varepsilon
with
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
, we use a standard perturbative expansion approach to derive the amplitude equation for our system in Section
3.2
, and compared the analytical solution to the numerical solution in Section
3.3
.

Finally, in Section
4
we turn to the fully nonlinear regime with large
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}
, and perform extensive numerical simulations that suggest a variety of long-time behaviors in one dimension. Some behaviors are commonly observed in other two-species population dynamics models: for example, when
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}
are large, small perturbations to the (linearly unstable) constant steady state (
1.2
) leads to periodic patterns (see Figure
2
and
3
). Also, if
\Omega=\mathbb{R}
is the whole real line, initially segregated initial data lead to traveling wave solutions with one species invading the other (see Figure
4
and
5
), similar to those found in
[
48
]
for the system (
1.6
) in the absence of chemotaxis. But the chemotaxis effect also brings us some unexpected patterns: when
u_{0},v_{0}
are both compactly supported, for large
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}
, we noticed that the two densities form clusters and propagates outwards in turn (see Figure
6
). In Section
4.4
, we also show two examples of 2D numerical simulations in the weakly nonlinear regime and fully nonlinear regime respectively.

1.4.
Organization of the paper

In Section
2
, we prove Theorem
1.1
that gives the global well-posedness result for any
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}>0
and any dimension
n\geq 1
, as well as Theorem

, which shows neither species could extinct in the weak competition regime. Section

2.
Global well-posedness and quantitative estimates

2.1.
Global well-posedness of solutions

In this subsection we aim to prove Theorem
1.1
, which gives the global well-posedness for the system (
1.6
)–(
1.7
) in any dimension
n\geq 1
when the two species are mutually repulsive (that is,
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}>0
).
Given a pair of non-negative initial condition
u_{0},v_{0}\in C(\overline{\Omega})
with
u_{0},v_{0}\not\equiv 0
, local well-posedness of (
1.6
)–(
1.7
) follows from an almost identical argument as
[
54
, Lemma 2.1]
, which we do not repeat here. Namely, there exists a maximal time
T_{\max}>0
and a unique pair of classical solution
(u,v)\in C(\overline{\Omega}\times(0,T_{\max}))\cap C^{2,1}(\Omega\times(0,T_{\max}))
. Furthermore,
u,v
are both positive during their existence, and the following blow-up criterion holds: if
T_{\max}<\infty
, then
\lim\sup_{t\nearrow T_{\max}}(\|u(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|v(t)\|_{L^{\infty}})=\infty
.

Before the proof, we collect some results regarding the solution
c
to (
1.7
).

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
f\in L^{p}(\Omega)
for some
1\leq p<\infty
, and let
c[f]
be the solution to (
1.7
). Then

c[f]\in W^{2,p}(\Omega)
with

\|c[f]\|_{W^{2,p}(\Omega)}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{p}},

(2.1)

where
C
only depends on
n,p
and
\Omega
. In addition, if
f\geq 0
, then

\|c[f]\|_{L^{1}}=\|f\|_{L^{1}},

(2.2)

and

0\leq c[f]\leq\sup_{\Omega}f

(2.3)

if
f
is also bounded above.

Proof.

(
2.1
) follows from the standard elliptic regularity estimate (e.g.
[
36
, pp. 194]
). If
f\geq 0
, then (
2.3
) follows from the maximum principle. Integrating
c-\Delta c=f
in
\Omega
and using the Neumann boundary condition and the facts that
f,c[f]\geq 0
directly yields (
2.2
).
∎

Now we are ready to prove Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Due to the blow-up criterion that we discussed at the beginning of this section, in order to show that the system (
1.6
)–(
1.7
) is globally well-posed, it suffices to show that both densities has a uniform-in-time upper bound. The proof is split into the following 3 steps.

Step 1. Estimates on the mass.
We start with a simple observation that the masses of
u
and
v
are uniformly bounded in time during the existence of a solution. To see this, let
M_{u}(t):=\int_{\Omega}u(x,t)dx
be the mass of
u
at time
t
, and note that
M_{u}(t)=\|u(t)\|_{L^{1}}
since
u\geq 0
for all times. Taking its time derivative and apply the divergence theorem, we have

\frac{d}{dt}M_{u}(t)=\int_{\Omega}b_{1}u(1-u-a_{1}v)dt\leq b_{1}M_{u}(t)-b_{1}\int_{\Omega}u(x,t)^{2}dt\leq b_{1}M_{u}\left(1-\frac{M_{u}}{|\Omega|}\right),

(2.4)

implying that

M_{u}(t)\leq\max\{M_{u}(0),|\Omega|\}\quad\text{ for all }t\geq 0,

and a similar estimate can be derived for
M_{v}(t):=\int_{\Omega}v(x,t)dx
. Summing up the estimates, the total mass
M(t):=\int_{\Omega}u(x,t)+v(x,t)dx
satisfies

M(t)\leq\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}}+\|v_{0}\|_{L^{1}}+2|\Omega|,

(2.5)

which is uniformly bounded in time.

Next we will show that for any
t_{0}>0
,
\sup_{t>t_{0}}M(t)
is independent of the initial data. Note that (
2.4
) gives
M_{u}^{\prime}(t)\leq-\frac{b_{1}}{2|\Omega|}M_{u}(t)^{2}
whenever
M_{u}(t)>2|\Omega|
, and solving this differential inequality gives the following upper bound of
M_{u}(t)
that is independent of the initial data (however it becomes unbounded as
t\to 0^{+}
):

M_{u}(t)\leq\max\left\{\frac{2|\Omega|}{b_{1}}t^{-1},2|\Omega|\right\}.

(2.6)

A similar estimate can be derived for
M_{v}
, with
b_{1}
replaced by
b_{2}
. Summing them up yields

M(t)\leq 2|\Omega|((b_{1}^{-1}+b_{2}^{-1})t^{-1}+2).

Thus for any
t_{0}>0
, we have

\sup_{t>t_{0}}M(t)\leq 2|\Omega|((b_{1}^{-1}+b_{2}^{-1})t_{0}^{-1}+2)=:g_{M}(t_{0}),

(2.7)

where
g_{M}(t_{0})
only depends on
t_{0},\Omega,b_{1},b_{2}
, and in particular does not depend on the initial data
u_{0},v_{0}
. (However note that
g_{M}(t_{0})\to\infty
as
t_{0}\to 0^{+}
.)

Step 2.
L^{p}
estimates.
For any
1<p<\infty
, let

F_{p}(t):=\int_{\Omega}u(x,t)^{p}+v(x,t)^{p}\,dx.

In this step, we will show that
F_{p}(t)
is uniformly bounded in time for any
1<p<\infty
, where the bound depends on
u_{0},v_{0},p,\Omega
and the coefficients of (
1.6
). In addition, for any
t_{0}>0
, we will also show
\sup_{t>t_{0}}F_{p}(t)\leq g_{p}(t_{0})
for some
g_{p}(t_{0})
independent of
u_{0},v_{0}
.

Taking the time derivative of
\int_{\Omega}u^{p}dx
and applying the divergence theorem, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}u^{p}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}pu^{p-1}\big(d_{1}\Delta u+\chi_{1}\nabla\cdot(u\nabla c[v])+b_{1}u(1-u-a_{1}v)\big)dx

\displaystyle=-\frac{4(p-1)d_{1}}{p}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla u^{\frac{p}{2}}\right|^{2}dx+(p-1)\chi_{1}\int_{\Omega}u^{p}\Delta c[v]dx+pb_{1}\int_{\Omega}u^{p}(1-u-a_{1}v)dx

\displaystyle\leq(p-1)\chi_{1}\int_{\Omega}u^{p}c[v]dx+pb_{1}\int_{\Omega}u^{p}dx-pb_{1}\int_{\Omega}u^{p+1}dx

\displaystyle=:I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3},

(2.8)

where in the inequality we used
u,v\geq 0
as well as
\Delta c[v]=c[v]-v\leq c[v]
.

Among the three terms on the right hand side of (
2.8
),
I_{3}
is always negative, thus it suffices to control
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
. Next we aim to control
I_{1}
using
\int u^{p+1}dx
,
\int v^{p+1}dx
and
\|v\|_{L^{1}}
. Applying Hölder’s inequality and Young’s inequality, we have

I_{1}\leq(p-1)\chi_{1}\left(\int_{\Omega}u^{p+1}dx\right)^{\frac{p}{p+1}}\|c[v]\|_{L^{p+1}}\leq\frac{pb_{1}}{4}\int_{\Omega}u^{p+1}dx+C\|c[v]\|_{L^{p+1}}^{p+1},

where
C
only depends on
p
and the coefficients of (
1.6
). (In the following, the value of
C
may vary from line to line.) Using the Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequality, we control
\|c[v]\|_{L^{p+1}}
as

\|c[v]\|_{L^{p+1}}\leq C\|c[v]\|_{W^{2,{p+1}}}^{\theta}\|c[v]\|_{L^{1}}^{1-\theta}\leq C\|v\|_{L^{p+1}}^{\theta}\|v\|_{L^{1}}^{1-\theta},

where
\theta=\frac{np}{np+2(p+1)}<1
, and the last inequality follows from (
2.1
) of Lemma
2.1
. Plugging this into the estimate of
I_{1}
gives

I_{1}\leq\frac{pb_{1}}{4}\int_{\Omega}u^{p+1}dx+C\|v\|_{L^{p+1}}^{(p+1)\theta}\|v\|_{L^{1}}^{(p+1)(1-\theta)}\leq\frac{pb_{1}}{4}\int_{\Omega}u^{p+1}dx+\frac{pb_{2}}{4}\int_{\Omega}v^{p+1}dx+C\|v\|_{L^{1}}^{p+1},

(2.9)

where the last inequality follows from Young’s inequality. Applying this estimate to (
2.8
), we have

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}u^{p}dx\leq pb_{1}\int_{\Omega}u^{p}dx-\frac{3pb_{1}}{4}\int_{\Omega}u^{p+1}dx+\frac{pb_{2}}{4}\int_{\Omega}v^{p+1}dx+CM(t)^{p+1}.

(2.10)

Using an identical argument as above, the evolution of
\int_{\Omega}v^{p}dx
satisfies a similar differential inequality as (
2.10
), except that
u
and
v
become interchanged, and so do
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
. Adding it with (
2.10
) gives

\frac{d}{dt}F_{p}(t)\leq p(b_{1}+b_{2})F_{p}(t)-\frac{pb_{1}}{2}\int_{\Omega}u^{p+1}dx-\frac{pb_{2}}{2}\int_{\Omega}v^{p+1}dx+CM(t)^{p+1}.

Note that Hölder’s inequality gives

\int_{\Omega}u^{p}dx\leq\left(\int_{\Omega}u^{p+1}dx\right)^{\frac{p}{p+1}}|\Omega|^{\frac{1}{p+1}},

and applying it to above yields

\frac{d}{dt}F_{p}(t)\leq-cF_{p}(t)^{\frac{p+1}{p}}+CF_{p}(t)+CM(t)^{p+1},

(2.11)

where
c
and
C
are positive constants only depending on
p,\Omega
and the coefficients of (
1.6
). Since
M(t)
is uniformly bounded in time by (
2.5
), we have that
F_{p}(t)
is also uniformly bounded in time, where the bounds depends on
u_{0},v_{0},p,\Omega
and the coefficients of (
1.6
).

In addition, recall that for any
t_{0}>0
, (
2.7
) gives that
\sup_{t>t_{0}}M(t)\leq g_{M}(t_{0})
which does not depend on
u_{0},v_{0}
. Thus for all
t>t_{0}
,
F_{p}
satisfies the differential inequality (
2.11
) with all
M
replaced by
g_{M}(t_{0})
, which implies that
F_{p}^{\prime}\leq-cF_{p}^{\frac{p+1}{p}}
if
F_{p}\gg\max\{g_{M}(t_{0})^{p},1\}
. Solving this differential inequality, we have
\sup_{t>t_{0}}F(t)\leq g_{p}(t_{0})
for some decreasing function
g_{p}(t_{0})
with
\lim_{t_{0}\to 0^{+}}g_{p}(t_{0})=\infty
, where
g_{p}
depends on
p,t_{0},\Omega
and the coefficients of (
1.6
), but does not depend on the initial data.

Step 3.
L^{\infty}
estimates.
Once we obtain the uniform-in-time
L^{p}
estimates for all
p\in(1,\infty)
, the
L^{\infty}
estimates follows from a standard comparison principle argument. Using that
\Delta c[v]=c[v]-v
and
u,v\geq 0
, the equation for
u
satisfies the following (where we treat
v
and
c[v]
as a priori given functions):

\begin{split}\partial_{t}u&=d_{1}\Delta u+\chi_{1}\nabla u\cdot\nabla c[v]+\chi_{1}u(c[v]-v)+b_{1}u(1-u-a_{1}v)\\
&\leq d_{1}\Delta u+\chi_{1}\nabla u\cdot\nabla c[v]+u(\chi_{1}\|c[v]\|_{L^{\infty}}+b_{1})-b_{1}u^{2}.\end{split}

To control
\|c[v]\|_{L^{\infty}}
, note that for any
p\in(n,\infty)
(e.g. we can fix
p=2n
), the Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequality yields

\|c[v]\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\|c[v]\|_{W^{1,p}}^{\theta}\|c[v]\|_{L^{1}}^{1-\theta}\leq C\|v\|_{L^{p}}^{\theta}\|v\|_{L^{1}}^{1-\theta}

(2.12)

for
\theta=\frac{np}{np-n+p}
, where the second inequality follows from Lemma
2.1
. As a result, the uniform-in-time bound on
\|v\|_{L^{1}}
and
\|v\|_{L^{p}}
in Step 1 and 2 yields that
\|c[v(t)]\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C
for all
t\geq 0
, where
C
depends on
u_{0},v_{0},n,\Omega
and the coefficients of (
1.6
).

For such
C
, let
w
be the constant-in-space solution to the ODE

\partial_{t}w=(\chi_{1}C+b_{1})w-b_{1}w^{2}

(2.13)

with initial data
w=\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}
. Such
w
is a supersolution to the PDE for
u
, thus comparison principle yields that
u(\cdot,t)\leq w(t)
for all time during the existence of
u
. Since
w(t)\leq\max\{w(0),\frac{\chi_{1}C+b_{1}}{b_{1}}\}
for all time,
\|u(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}
also has the same upper bound. An identical argument can be done for
v
, thus the solution
(u,v)
exists globally in time.

Finally, for any
t_{0}>0
, recall that in step 1 and 2 we have that
\sup_{t>t_{0}}M(t)\leq g_{M}(t_{0})
and
\sup_{t>t_{0}}F_{p}(t)\leq g_{p}(t_{0})
, where
g_{M}
and
g_{p}
are independent of the initial data. Applying these bounds to (
2.12
) yields that
\sup_{t>t_{0}}\|c[v]\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C(t_{0})
for some
C(t_{0})
independent of the initial data. Applying this bound to (
2.13
), we have that for any
t_{0}>0
,
\sup_{t>t_{0}}\|u(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}
has an upper bound that is independent of
u_{0},v_{0}
(although the bound goes to infinity as
t_{0}\to 0^{+}
), and the same can be done for
v
. This finishes the proof of (
1.8
).
∎

Remark 2.2
.

Once we obtain (
1.8
), we can apply regularity theory for parabolic equations to upgrade it to the following: for any
t_{0}>0
, there exists some
\alpha\in(0,1)
, such that

\|u\|_{C^{\alpha,\frac{\alpha}{2}}(\overline{\Omega}\times[t_{0},\infty))}+\|v\|_{C^{\alpha,\frac{\alpha}{2}}(\overline{\Omega}\times[t_{0},\infty))}\leq h(t_{0}),

(2.14)

where
h(t_{0})
only depends on
t_{0},\Omega,n
and the coefficients of (
1.6
). To see this, we rewrite the
u
equation following the notation of
[
50
]
(where we treat
v
and
c[v]
as a priori given functions):

\partial_{t}u-\nabla\cdot a(u,\nabla u)=b(u),

where

a(u,\nabla u)=d_{1}\nabla u+\chi_{1}u\nabla c[v]\quad\text{ and }b(u)=b_{1}u(1-u-a_{1}v).

Recall that (
1.8
) gives
v\in L^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega}\times[t_{0},\infty))\leq g(t_{0})
(where
g(t_{0})
is independent of the initial data), and combining this with Lemma
2.1
we also have
\nabla c[v]\in L^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega}\times[t_{0},\infty))
. We can then apply
[
50
, Theorem 1.3]
to conclude that
\|u\|_{C^{\alpha,\frac{\alpha}{2}}(\overline{\Omega}\times[t_{0},\infty))}\leq h(t_{0})
(where
h(t_{0})
is independent of the initial data), the the same can be done for the
v
equation.

2.2.
No extinction for weak competition

In this subsection we aim to prove Theorem
1.2
, which shows that for two mutually repulsive species, if the coefficients
a_{1},a_{2}
are in the weak competition regime, i.e.
a_{1},a_{2}\in(0,1)
, neither of the species would become extinct as
t\to\infty
regardless of how large
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}
is.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

We start with showing a useful fact that will be used often in this proof: for all
t\geq 1
,
\|u(t)\|_{L^{1}}
being small implies that
\|u(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}
is small; and the same can be said for
v(t)
. Namely, there exist some
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
C
that are independent of the initial data, such that

\|u(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\|u(t)\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+n}}\text{ and }\|v(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\|v(t)\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+n}}\quad\text{ for all }t\geq 1.

(2.15)

To see this, recall that (
2.14
) yields

\sup_{t\geq 1}\|u(t)\|_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}+\|v(t)\|_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C_{1},

(2.16)

where
C_{1}
and
\alpha
are independent of the initial data. If for some
t\geq 1
the maximum of
u(\cdot,t)
is achieved at
x_{0}
, we have
u(x,t)\geq\frac{\|u(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}}{2}
for all
|x-x_{0}|\leq(\frac{\|u(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}}{2C_{1}})^{1/\alpha}
, thus

\|u(t)\|_{L^{1}}\geq c(\alpha,n,C_{1})\|u(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}^{1+\frac{n}{\alpha}}.

A similar argument can be done for
v
, which finishes the proof of (
2.15
).

In the rest of the proof we aim to prove the inequality for
u
in (
1.9
), and a parallel argument yields the inequality for
v
. For a sufficiently small
0<\delta\ll 1
to be fixed later, let
t_{1}>1
be the first time such that

M_{u}(t_{1})=\delta\min\{M_{u}(1),1\}.

(Recall that
M_{u}(t):=\int_{\Omega}u(x,t)dx=\|u(t)\|_{L^{1}}.
) By definition of
t_{1}
, we have
M_{u}(t)>\delta\min\{M_{u}(1),1\}
for
t\in(1,t_{1})
, which implies that

\frac{d}{dt}M_{u}(t_{1})\leq 0.

(2.17)

Our goal is to show that if
0<\delta\ll 1
is sufficiently small, we must have

1-u(\cdot,t_{1})-a_{1}v(\cdot,t_{1})>0\quad\text{ in }\Omega.

(2.18)

Once this is shown, it immediately implies that

\frac{d}{dt}M_{u}(t_{1})=b_{1}\int_{\Omega}u(1-u-a_{1}v)dx>0,
contradicting (
2.17
).

Using that
a_{1}\in(0,1)
, throughout the rest of the proof we define

\gamma_{1}:=\frac{1-a_{1}}{4}>0,\quad\gamma_{2}:=\frac{1+a_{1}}{2a_{1}}>1,

and note that they satisfy
1-\gamma_{1}-a_{1}\gamma_{2}=\frac{1-a_{1}}{4}>0
. To show (
2.18
), it suffices to show that
\|u(t_{1})\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\gamma_{1}
and
\|v(t_{1})\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\gamma_{2}
for
\delta\ll 1
. The
u
estimate immediately follows from (
2.15
): since
\|u(t_{1})\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\|u(t_{1})\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+n}}\leq C\delta^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+n}}
, one can simply choose
\delta\ll 1
such that
\|u(t_{1})\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\gamma_{1}
.

The rest of the proof is dedicated to showing that

\|v(t_{1})\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\gamma_{2}\quad\text{ if }\delta\ll 1.

(2.19)

This estimate is more involved, since
v
is coupled to
u
in a more delicate way. Note that (
2.16
) already gives that
\|v(t_{1})\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C_{1}
, so it suffices to focus on the case
C_{1}>\gamma_{2}
.

Heuristically speaking, the idea to prove (
2.19
) is that if
M_{u}(t_{1})\leq\delta\ll 1
, we will show that
u
must have stayed small for a long time interval
[t_{1}-T,t_{1}]
before time
t_{1}
. During this interval,
v
almost does not feel the presence of
u
. Note that if
u\equiv 0
in the
v
equation in (
1.6
),
v
would decay exponentially to 1. Thus if
u\ll 1
in a long time interval
[t_{1}-T,t_{1}]
, we can still show (using comparison principle) that
v
drops below
\gamma_{2}>1
at time
t_{1}
.
The proof is divided into the following steps:

Step 1. Smallness of
M_{u}(t)
before time
t_{1}
.
In this step, we will show that if
\delta\ll 1
,
M_{u}(t)
must have stayed small for a long time interval before time
t_{1}
.
To see this, note that for all
t>1
,
M_{u}(t)
can at most decay exponentially:

\frac{d}{dt}M_{u}(t)=b_{1}\int_{\Omega}u\underbrace{(1-u-a_{1}v)}_{\geq-2C_{1}}dx\geq-2C_{1}b_{1}M_{u}(t)\quad\text{ for all $t\geq 1$,}

(2.20)

where we used that
a_{1}<1
and
u(\cdot,t),v(\cdot,t)<C_{1}
for all
t\geq 1
. This differential inequality yields that

M_{u}(t)\leq e^{2C_{1}b_{1}(t_{1}-t)}M_{u}(t_{1})\quad\text{ for all }1\leq t\leq t_{1}.

(2.21)

As a result, for some fixed
\tilde{\delta}\in(0,1)
and
T>0
(they only depend on
\Omega,n
and the coefficients of (
1.6
), and will be fixed momentarily in Step 2 and 4), if
\delta
is sufficiently small such that
e^{2C_{1}b_{1}T}\delta\leq\tilde{\delta}
, then (
2.21
) implies that for all
\max\{1,t_{1}-T\}\leq t\leq t_{1}
,

\begin{split}M_{u}(t)&\leq e^{2C_{1}b_{1}T}M_{u}(t_{1})=e^{2C_{1}b_{1}T}\delta\min\{M_{u}(1),1\}\\
&\leq\tilde{\delta}\min\{M_{u}(1),1\}.\end{split}

(2.22)

Note that in this case we automatically have
t_{1}-T>1
: if not, we would have
M_{u}(1)\leq\tilde{\delta}M_{u}(1)
, contradicting that
\tilde{\delta}\in(0,1)
. Thus (
2.22
) actually holds for
t\in[t_{1}-T,1]
.

Step 2. Choice of
\tilde{\delta}
.
In this step, we fix
\tilde{\delta}\in(0,1)
such that (
2.22
) implies

\|c[u(t)]\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\frac{b_{2}(\gamma_{2}-1)}{2\chi_{2}}\quad\text{ for }t\in[t_{1}-T,t_{1}].

(2.23)

To control
\|c[u(t)]\|_{L^{\infty}}
, recall that
for any
p\in(n,\infty)
(e.g. fix
p=2n
), the Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequality and Lemma
2.1
yield

\|c[u]\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\|c[u]\|_{W^{1,p}}^{\theta}\|c[u]\|_{L^{1}}^{1-\theta}\leq C\|u\|_{L^{p}}^{\theta}\|u\|_{L^{1}}^{1-\theta}\leq C\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\theta(1-\frac{1}{p})}\|u\|_{L^{1}}^{1-\theta(1-\frac{1}{p})}

(2.24)

for
\theta=\frac{np}{np-n+p}
, where the second inequality follows from Lemma
2.1
and the third inequality follows from Hölder’s inequality.
Combining this with (
2.15
) gives that

\|c[u(t)]\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\|u(t)\|_{L^{1}}^{\beta}\quad\text{ for all }t\geq 1

(2.25)

for some
\beta>0
. Thus if
\tilde{\delta}
is sufficiently small, we obtain (
2.23
) by combining (
2.22
) and (
2.25
).

Step 3. Controlling
v
by comparison principle
. During the time interval
[t_{1}-T,t_{1}]
, using that
\Delta c[u]=c[u]-u
and
u,v\geq 0
, the equation for
v
satisfies

\begin{split}\partial_{t}v&=d_{2}\Delta v+\chi_{2}\nabla v\cdot\nabla c[u]+\chi_{2}v(c[u]-u)+b_{2}v(1-v-a_{2}u)\\
&\leq d_{2}\Delta v+\chi_{2}\nabla v\cdot\nabla c[u]+b_{2}v\left(1+\frac{\chi_{2}}{b_{2}}\|c[u]\|_{L^{\infty}}-v\right).\end{split}

Combining this with (
2.23
), we have

\partial_{t}v\leq d_{2}\Delta v+\chi_{2}\nabla v\cdot\nabla c[u]+b_{2}v\left(\frac{1+\gamma_{2}}{2}-v\right)\quad\text{ for all }t\in[t_{1}-T,t_{1}],

(2.26)

and since
t_{1}-T>1
, we have
\|v(t_{1}-T)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C_{1}
due to (
2.16
).

To control
v
from above, we will compare it with
w
, which solves the ODE

w^{\prime}=b_{2}w\left(\frac{1+\gamma_{2}}{2}-w\right)

(2.27)

with initial data
w(t_{1}-T)=C_{1}
. Comparing it with (
2.26
), we know that
w
is a supersolution to the PDE for
v
, thus comparison principle yields that

v(\cdot,t)\leq w(t)\quad\text{ for all }t\in[t_{1}-T,t_{1}].

(2.28)

Step 4. Choice of
T
.
In this step, we choose
T
such that the solution
w
to (
2.13
) with initial data
w(t_{1}-T)=C_{1}
satisfies
w(t_{1})\leq\gamma_{2}
. Recall that in the discussion after (
2.19
), it suffices to focus on the case
C_{1}>\gamma_{2}
, and note that it implies
C_{1}>\frac{1+\gamma_{2}}{2}
since
\gamma_{2}>1
. In this case
w(t)
is monotone decreasing in time. In addition, for any
t>t_{1}-T
such that
w(t)>\gamma_{2}
, (
2.27
) implies that
w^{\prime}(t)\leq b_{2}\gamma_{2}\frac{1-\gamma_{2}}{2}<0
. As a result, we can set
T:=\frac{2(C_{1}-\gamma_{2})}{b_{2}\gamma_{2}(1-\gamma_{2})}
. Such
T
guarantees that
\omega(t_{1})\leq\gamma_{2}
: if not, we would have
\omega(t)>\gamma_{2}
in
[t_{1}-T,t_{1}]
, thus
\omega(t_{1})\leq C_{1}+Tb_{2}\gamma_{2}\frac{1-\gamma_{2}}{2}=\gamma_{2}
, a contradiction.

Finally, combining such choice of
T
with step 3, (
2.28
) implies that

v(\cdot,t_{1})\leq w(t_{1})\leq\gamma_{2},

finishing the proof of (
2.19
).
∎

3.
Stability and dynamics in the weak competition regime

In this section, we will derive the criteria under which the constant steady state (
1.2
) is linearly stable, and obtain the approximate dynamics of the system when (
1.2
) is “slightly linearly unstable”. Since the system (
1.6
)–(
1.7
) has many parameters, to make the analysis easier, let us consider the system where the coefficients for
u
and
v
are symmetric about each other. Let us set
d_{1}=d_{2}=1
,
b_{1}=b_{2}=1
,
a_{1}=a_{2}=a
, and
\chi_{1}=\chi_{2}=\chi>0
. That is, (
1.6
) now becomes

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u=\Delta u+\chi\nabla\cdot(u\nabla c[v])+u(1-u-av)&\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\partial_{t}v=\Delta v+\chi\nabla\cdot(v\nabla c[u])+v(1-v-au)&\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}=\frac{\partial v}{\partial\nu}=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\end{cases}

(3.1)

where
c[u]
and
c[v]
again solve the elliptic equation (
1.7
). Throughout this section, we assume
a\in(0,1)
is a fixed parameter in the weakly competition regime.

3.1.
Linear stability analysis for a general domain

For the system (
3.1
)–(
1.7
), one can easily check that (
1.2
) is still the unique positive steady state, and since we have set that
a_{1}=a_{2}=a
, it can be written as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\begin{pmatrix}\overline{u}\\
\overline{v}\end{pmatrix}\ =\ \frac{1}{a+1}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
1\end{pmatrix},\end{split}

(3.2)

whose corresponding chemical concentrations are
c[\overline{u}]=c[\overline{v}]=\frac{1}{a+1}
. For a fixed
a\in(0,1)
, in this section we will perform the standard linear stability analysis to determine the linear stability criteria of (
3.1
) in terms of
\chi
.

Since
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a bounded domain with smooth boundary, it is well known that the Neumann eigenvalue problem

\begin{cases}\Delta\varphi+\lambda\varphi=0&\text{ in }\Omega\\
\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial\nu}=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega\end{cases}

(3.3)

has an infinite sequence of eigenfunctions
\{\varphi_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
with corresponding eigenvalues
0=\lambda_{0}<\lambda_{1}\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{k}\leq\dots
, where
\{\varphi_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
form an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\Omega)
, and
\varphi_{0}=|\Omega|^{-1/2}
. In particular, in the special case
n=1
and
\Omega=[0,L]
, we have

\varphi_{k}(x)=\sqrt{2}|\Omega|^{-1/2}\cos(w_{k}x)~~\text{ and }~~\lambda_{k}=w_{k}^{2}\quad\text{ for }k\in\mathbb{N}^{+},

(3.4)

where
w_{k}:=\frac{k\pi}{L}
.

For a solution
(u,v)
to (
3.1
)–(
1.7
), we can use the above Neumann eigenfunction expansion to write it as

u(x,t)=\overline{u}+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}(t)\varphi_{k}(x),\quad v(x,t)=\overline{v}+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k}(t)\varphi_{k}(x).

When
(u,v)
has a small perturbation to the constant steady state (
3.2
) (i.e. all
|a_{k}|,|b_{k}|\ll 1
for
k\in N
),
substituting
u(x,t)
and
v(x,t)
into (
3.1
)–(
1.7
), for each mode
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
a_{k}
and
b_{k}
satisfy the ODE

\frac{d}{dt}\begin{pmatrix}a_{k}(t)\\
b_{k}(t)\end{pmatrix}=\mathcal{J}_{k}\begin{pmatrix}a_{k}(t)\\
b_{k}(t)\end{pmatrix}+O(\|u-\overline{u}\|^{2}+\|v-\overline{v}\|^{2}),

where
\mathcal{J}_{k}
is a
2\times 2
matrix given by

\mathcal{J}_{k}:=\begin{pmatrix}-\lambda_{k}-\overline{u}&-\chi\overline{u}\frac{\lambda_{k}}{1+\lambda_{k}}-a\overline{u}\\
-\chi\overline{v}\frac{\lambda_{k}}{1+\lambda_{k}}-a\overline{v}&-\lambda_{k}-\overline{v}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{ for }k\in\mathbb{N}.

(3.5)

The linear stability of the constant steady state
(\overline{u},\overline{v})
is determined by the eigenvalues of the matrices
\mathcal{J}_{k}
: namely,
(\overline{u},\overline{v})
is linearly stable if and only if all eigenvalues of
\mathcal{J}_{k}
have negative real parts for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Due to our assumptions
\chi>0,a>0
and the fact that
\overline{u}=\overline{v}>0
, each
\mathcal{J}_{k}
is of the form
\begin{pmatrix}c_{k}&d_{k}\\
d_{k}&c_{k}\end{pmatrix}
with
c_{k}<0
and
d_{k}<0
, thus its eigenvalues are
c_{k}\pm d_{k}
. Clearly we have
c_{k}+d_{k}<0
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. For
c_{k}-d_{k}
, we have

c_{k}-d_{k}=\frac{\chi\lambda_{k}}{(a+1)(1+\lambda_{k})}-\lambda_{k}+\frac{a-1}{a+1}\quad\text{ for all }k\in\mathbb{N},

thus
c_{0}-d_{0}<0
(since
\lambda_{0}=0
), and for
k\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
we have
c_{k}-d_{k}<0
if and only if
0<\chi<\chi_{k}^{*}
, where

\chi_{k}^{*}:=\frac{1+\lambda_{k}}{\lambda_{k}}(\lambda_{k}(a+1)+1-a)=2+\frac{1-a}{\lambda_{k}}+\lambda_{k}(1+a)\quad\text{ for }k\in\mathbb{N}^{+}.

(3.6)

Note that
\chi_{k}^{*}>0
for each
k\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, and
\chi_{k}^{*}\to\infty
as
k\to\infty
(since
\lim_{k\to\infty}\lambda_{k}=\infty
).
This leads to the following criteria for the linear stability of constant steady state:

Proposition 3.1
.

For the system (
3.1
)–(
1.7
) with
\chi>0
,
0<a<1
, the positive constant solution (
3.2
) is linearly stable if
0<\chi<\chi^{*}
, where

\chi^{*}:=\inf_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{+}}\chi_{k}^{*},
with
\chi_{k}^{*}
given by (
3.6
).

3.2.
Weakly nonlinear analysis for a 1D interval

In this subsection, we focus on the case
n=1
and
\Omega=[0,L]
, and consider the regime
\chi>\chi^{*}
, where the positive constant steady state (
3.2
) is linearly unstable. The goal is to perform weakly nonlinear analysis in the regime
0<\chi-\chi^{*}\ll 1
, and derive the amplitude equations for system (
3.1
)–(
1.7
) using a standard perturbation expansion approach, following
[
16
, Section 6]
.

Let us point out that when
\Omega=[0,L]
, (
3.4
) gives that
\lambda_{k}=w_{k}^{2}=(\frac{k\pi}{L})^{2}
, thus

\chi_{k}^{*}=2+\frac{1-a}{w_{k}^{2}}+(1+a)w_{k}^{2}=2+\frac{(1-a)L^{2}}{k^{2}\pi^{2}}+(1+a)\frac{k^{2}\pi^{2}}{L^{2}}

is a convex function in
k
(for
k\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
). As a result, in the definition
\chi^{*}:=\inf_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{+}}\chi_{k}^{*}
, the infimum is achieved at either one or two modes.

In the rest of this section, without loss of generality we assume that the infimum is achieved at only one mode (denote it by
k^{*}\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
), which is the case for almost every
L>0
. In this case, we have
\chi^{*}=\chi_{k^{*}}^{*}
, and
\chi^{*}<\chi_{k}^{*}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}^{+}\setminus\{k^{*}\}
. In particular, when
\chi=\chi^{*}
, the matrices
\mathcal{J}_{k}
defined in (
3.5
) (call them
\mathcal{J}_{k}^{*}
) are invertible for any
k=\mathbb{N}\setminus\{k^{*}\}
and have two negative eigenvalues, and is singular only at
k=k^{*}
with
(1,-1)^{T}
in its kernel.

In the weakly nonlinear regime
\chi=\chi^{*}+\varepsilon
with
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
, we have
\chi>\chi_{k^{*}}^{*}
, and
\chi<\chi_{k}^{*}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}^{+}\setminus\{k^{*}\}
. Therefore, among all matrices
\mathcal{J}_{k}
defined in (
3.5
), only
\mathcal{J}_{k^{*}}
has a positive eigenvalue, given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sigma(k^{*})=\varepsilon\frac{\overline{u}w_{k^{*}}^{2}}{1+w_{k^{*}}^{2}},\end{split}

(3.7)

and its corresponding eigenvalue is
(1,-1)^{T}
. It means for a small perturbation near the constant steady state (
3.2
), only the mode
\cos(w_{k^{*}}x)(1,-1)^{T}
is growing exponentially with growth rate
\sigma(k^{*})
that is of order
\varepsilon
, and all the other modes are decaying.
Our goal is to derive the
amplitude equation
that gives the dynamics for this growing mode.

Since this mode has a tiny growth rate of order
\varepsilon
, we are motivated to define a slow time variable
\tau:=\varepsilon t
that describes the dynamics of the system when
\chi=\chi^{*}+\varepsilon
is slightly above the stability threshold. Under the slow time variable, the dynamics in (
3.1
) can be written as

\begin{cases}\varepsilon\partial_{\tau}u=\partial_{xx}u+(\chi^{*}+\varepsilon)\,\partial_{x}(u\,\partial_{x}c[v])+u(1-u-av)&\text{ in }[0,L]\times(0,\infty),\\
\varepsilon\partial_{\tau}v=\partial_{xx}v+(\chi^{*}+\varepsilon)\,\partial_{x}(v\,\partial_{x}c[u])+v(1-v-au)&\text{ in }[0,L]\times(0,\infty),\\
\partial_{x}u=\partial_{x}v=0&\text{ on }\{0,L\}\times(0,\infty).\end{cases}

(3.8)

Next we will express
u
and
v
as the following expansions in terms of the small parameter
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\begin{pmatrix}u(x,\tau)\\
v(x,\tau)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\overline{u}\\
\overline{v}\end{pmatrix}+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{\frac{j}{2}}\begin{pmatrix}u^{(j)}(x,\tau)\\
v^{(j)}(x,\tau)\end{pmatrix},\end{split}

(3.9)

where we chose the expansion to be powers of
\varepsilon^{1/2}
, since we expect a pitchfork bifurcation. To see this, using the symmetry between the
u,v
equation and the fact that the only unstable mode for
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
is of the form
\cos(w_{k^{*}}x)(1,-1)^{T}
, we expect that for small
\varepsilon
there are two steady states approximately of the form
(\overline{u}+f,\overline{v}-f)
and
(\overline{u}-f,\overline{v}+f)
for some small
f
, suggesting a pitchfork bifurcation.

Again, the Neumann boundary condition in (
3.1
) allows us to expand each
(u^{(j)}(x,T),v^{(j)}(x,T))^{T}
in cosine series:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\begin{pmatrix}u^{(j)}(\tau,x)\\
v^{(j)}(\tau,x)\end{pmatrix}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\cos(w_{k}x)\begin{pmatrix}a_{k}^{(j)}(\tau)\\
b_{k}^{(j)}(\tau)\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{ for }j\in\mathbb{N}^{+}.\end{split}

(3.10)

Once
(u,v)
is written in the expansion (
3.9
) in terms of
\varepsilon
, their chemicals
(c[u],c[v])^{T}
can also be written in an expansion in terms of
\varepsilon
as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\begin{pmatrix}c[u(\tau)]\\
c[v(\tau)]\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\overline{u}\\
\overline{v}\end{pmatrix}+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{\frac{j}{2}}\begin{pmatrix}c[u^{(j)}]\\
c[v^{(j)}]\end{pmatrix},\end{split}

(3.11)

where for each
j
, using (
1.7
) and (
3.10
), the term
(c[u^{(j)}],c[v^{(j)}])^{T}
is given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\begin{pmatrix}c[u^{(j)}(\tau)]\\
c[v^{(j)}(\tau)]\end{pmatrix}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{1+w_{k}^{2}}\cos(w_{k}x)\begin{pmatrix}a_{k}^{(j)}(\tau)\\
b_{k}^{(j)}(\tau)\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{ for }j\in\mathbb{N}^{+}.\end{split}

(3.12)

Next we will plug the expansions (
3.9
) and (
3.11
) into (
3.8
), and collect terms of order
\varepsilon^{\frac{j}{2}}
for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
respectively. Note that there are no terms of order one in (
3.8
), and the lowest order terms are of order
\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

\bullet

Order
\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}
terms:

Collecting all terms of order
\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{xx}u^{(1)}+\chi^{*}\overline{u}\,\partial_{xx}c[v^{(1)}]-\overline{u}(u^{(1)}+av^{(1)})\\
\partial_{xx}v^{(1)}+\chi^{*}\overline{v}\,\partial_{xx}c[u^{(1)}]-\overline{v}(v^{(1)}+au^{(1)})\end{pmatrix}.\end{split}

(3.13)

Plugging (
3.10
) and (
3.12
) with
j=1
into (
3.13
), the equation becomes

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\cos(w_{k}x)\mathcal{J}_{k}^{*}\begin{pmatrix}a_{k}^{(1)}(\tau)\\
b_{k}^{(1)}(\tau)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\end{pmatrix},

(3.14)

where
\mathcal{J}_{k}^{*}
is the matrix
\mathcal{J}_{k}
in (
3.5
) with
\chi
replaced by
\chi^{*}
. As we discussed in the beginning of this subsection,
\mathcal{J}_{k}^{*}
is invertible for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
except at
k=k^{*}
, where
\mathcal{J}_{k^{*}}^{*}
has an eigenvalue 0 with corresponding eigenvector
(1,-1)^{T}
. Combining this fact with the orthogonality of
\{\cos(w_{k}x)\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, we know that
(a_{k}^{(1)}(\tau),b_{k}^{(1)}(\tau))^{T}\equiv 0
for all
k\neq k^{*}
, and for
k=k^{*}
we have
(a_{k^{*}}^{(1)}(\tau),b_{k^{*}}^{(1)}(\tau))^{T}
is a multiple of
(1,-1)^{T}
for each
\tau
. Thus
(u^{(1)},v^{(1)})^{T}
must take the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}\begin{pmatrix}u^{(1)}\\
v^{(1)}\end{pmatrix}=P(\tau)\cos(w_{k^{*}}x)\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-1\end{pmatrix}\end{split}

(3.15)

where the amplitude
P(\tau)
can be any function of
\tau
. (Note that this implies that
c[u^{(1)}]=\frac{P(\tau)}{1+w_{k^{*}}^{2}}\cos(w_{k^{*}}x)
, with
c[v^{(1)}]
being its negative.) As we explained above, our goal is to find the amplitude equation, which is the differential equation that
P(\tau)
satisfies. In order to do this, we need to go to higher order terms.

\bullet

Order
\varepsilon
terms:

The equations of order
\varepsilon
satisfy the following, and note that the right hand side contain quadratic products of the order
\varepsilon^{1/2}
terms
u^{(1)},v^{(1)}
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{xx}u^{(2)}+\chi^{*}\overline{u}\partial_{xx}c[v^{(2)}]-\overline{u}(u^{(2)}+av^{(2)})\\
\partial_{xx}v^{(2)}+\chi^{*}\overline{v}\partial_{xx}c[u^{(2)}]-\overline{v}(v^{(2)}+au^{(2)})\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\chi^{*}\partial_{x}(u^{(1)}\partial_{x}c[v^{(1)}])-u^{(1)}(u^{(1)}+av^{(1)})\\
\chi^{*}\partial_{x}(v^{(1)}\partial_{x}c[u^{(1)}])-v^{(1)}(v^{(1)}+au^{(1)})\end{pmatrix}.\end{split}

(3.16)

As before, we plug (
3.10
) and (
3.12
) (with
j=1,2
) into (
3.16
). Note that the first term on the right hand side is identical as the right hand side of (
3.13
), except that all
1
’s in the superscript become
2
’s. Thus it is equal to the left hand side of (
3.14
), except that
a_{k}^{(1)}
and
b_{k}^{(1)}
are replaced by
a_{k}^{(2)}
and
b_{k}^{(2)}
. To deal with the second term on the right hand side of (
3.16
), we first plug in the expressions of
u^{(1)}
and
v^{(1)}
from (
3.15
), then use the trigonometric identities
\cos(w_{k^{*}}x)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\cos(2w_{k^{*}}x)
and
\sin(w_{k^{*}}x)\cos(w_{k^{*}}x)=\frac{1}{2}\sin(2w_{k^{*}}x)
to write the quadratic products into a linear combination of trigonometric functions. After an elementary computation (which is omitted here), (
3.16
) becomes

\displaystyle\begin{split}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\end{pmatrix}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\cos(w_{k}x)\mathcal{J}_{k}^{*}\begin{pmatrix}a_{k}^{(2)}(\tau)\\
b_{k}^{(2)}(\tau)\end{pmatrix}+P(\tau)^{2}\left(\frac{a-1}{2}+c_{1}\cos(2w_{k^{*}}x)\right)\begin{pmatrix}1\\
1\end{pmatrix},\end{split}

(3.17)

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}c_{1}=\chi^{*}\frac{w_{k^{*}}^{2}}{1+w_{k^{*}}^{2}}+\frac{a-1}{2}=\frac{1-a}{2}+(1+a)w_{k^{*}}^{2}>0,\end{split}

(3.18)

and the second identity follows from (
3.6
), which gives
\chi^{*}\frac{\lambda_{k^{*}}}{1+\lambda_{k^{*}}}=\lambda_{k^{*}}(a+1)-(a-1)
(recall that
\lambda_{k^{*}}=w_{k^{*}}^{2}
).

Again using orthogonality of
\{\cos(w_{k}x)\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
and the fact that
\mathcal{J}_{k}^{*}
is invertible for
k\neq k^{*}
, we know that the coefficients
(a_{k}^{(2)}(\tau),b_{k}^{(2)}(\tau))\equiv 0
for all
k
except three modes:
k=k^{*}
,
k=0
, and
k=2k^{*}
. For the
k=k^{*}
term, we have
(a_{k^{*}}^{(2)}(\tau),b_{k^{*}}^{(2)}(\tau))^{T}=Q(\tau)(1,-1)^{T}
for some function
Q(\tau)
, where we do not have any more information about
Q(\tau)
. For the
k=0
and
k=2k^{*}
terms, note that (
3.17
) leads to the two equations

\mathcal{J}_{0}^{*}\begin{pmatrix}a_{0}^{(2)}(\tau)\\
b_{0}^{(2)}(\tau)\end{pmatrix}=-\frac{a-1}{2}P(\tau)^{2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
1\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathcal{J}_{2k^{*}}^{*}\begin{pmatrix}a_{2k^{*}}^{(2)}(\tau)\\
b_{2k^{*}}^{(2)}(\tau)\end{pmatrix}=-c_{1}P(\tau)^{2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

Solving the two linear systems of equations using the definitions of
\mathcal{J}_{k}^{*}
in (
3.5
) with
\chi=\chi^{*}
(note that the matrices are easy to invert since they are of the form
\begin{pmatrix}c&d\\
d&c\end{pmatrix}
), we obtain that the order
\varepsilon
terms are of the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}\begin{pmatrix}u^{(2)}\\
v^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}=P(\tau)^{2}\left(c_{2}\cos(2w_{k^{*}}x)+\frac{a-1}{2}\right)\begin{pmatrix}1\\
1\end{pmatrix}+Q(\tau)\cos(w_{k^{*}}x)\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-1\end{pmatrix}\end{split}

(3.19)

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}c_{2}=\frac{c_{1}}{\chi^{*}\frac{\overline{u}w_{2k^{*}}^{2}}{1+w_{2k^{*}}^{2}}+w_{2k^{*}}^{2}+1}>0.\end{split}

(3.20)

\bullet

Order
\varepsilon^{\frac{3}{2}}
terms:

Next we collect terms in higher order
\varepsilon^{\frac{3}{2}}
. As we will see, at this order
\frac{d}{d\tau}P(\tau)
finally appears, which allows us to close the equation for
P(\tau)
. Similar to the computation of the order
\varepsilon
terms (
3.16
), for order
\varepsilon^{\frac{3}{2}}
terms we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{\tau}u^{(1)}\\
\partial_{\tau}v^{(1)}\end{pmatrix}=&\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{xx}u^{(3)}+\chi^{*}\overline{u}\partial_{xx}c[v^{(3)}]-\overline{u}(u^{(3)}+av^{(3)})\\
\partial_{xx}v^{(3)}+\chi^{*}\overline{v}\partial_{xx}c[u^{(3)}]-\overline{v}(v^{(3)}+au^{(3)})\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\chi^{*}\partial_{x}(u^{(1)}\partial_{x}c[v^{(2)}])-u^{(1)}(u^{(2)}+av^{(2)})\\
\chi^{*}\partial_{x}(v^{(1)}\partial_{x}c[u^{(2)}])-v^{(1)}(v^{(2)}+au^{(2)})\end{pmatrix}\\
&+\begin{pmatrix}\chi^{*}\partial_{x}(u^{(2)}\partial_{x}c[v^{(1)}])-u^{(2)}(u^{(1)}+av^{(1)})\\
\chi^{*}\partial_{x}(v^{(2)}\partial_{x}c[u^{(1)}])-v^{(2)}(v^{(1)}+au^{(1)})\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\bar{u}\,\partial_{xx}c[v^{(1)}]\\
\bar{v}\,\partial_{xx}c[u^{(1)}]\end{pmatrix},\end{split}

(3.21)

where the last term is contributed by the
\varepsilon
term in
\chi=\chi^{*}+\varepsilon
.

Next we plug the expressions for the lower order terms (
3.15
) and (
3.19
) into above, and again we use the trigonometric identities to rewrite all quadratic products into linear terms. This computation is elementary but somewhat tedious, and we omit it here. Once all terms in (
3.21
) are written into a linear combination of cosine series, let us only collect the
\cos(w_{k^{*}}x)
terms (which is allowed due to the orthogonality of the cosine series), resulting in the equation

\displaystyle\begin{split}P^{\prime}(\tau)\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-1\end{pmatrix}=\mathcal{J}_{k^{*}}^{*}\begin{pmatrix}a_{k^{*}}^{(3)}(\tau)\\
b_{k^{*}}^{(3)}(\tau)\end{pmatrix}+\lambda_{1}P(\tau)^{3}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-1\end{pmatrix}+\lambda_{2}P(\tau)\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-1\end{pmatrix},\end{split}

(3.22)

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lambda_{1}:=\chi^{*}\left(\frac{(a-1-c_{2})w_{k^{*}}^{2}}{2(1+w_{k^{*}}^{2})}-\frac{c_{2}w_{k^{*}}w_{2k^{*}}}{2(1+w_{2k^{*}}^{2})}\right)-(c_{2}+a-1);\quad\lambda_{2}:=\frac{\overline{u}w_{k^{*}}^{2}}{1+w_{k^{*}}^{2}}>0.\end{split}

(3.23)

Multiplying
(1,-1)
on the left to both sides of (
3.22
) (and recall that
(1,-1)
is in the left kernel of
\mathcal{J}_{k^{*}}^{*}
, we arrive at a scalar differential equation for
P(\tau)
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{d}{d\tau}P(\tau)=\lambda_{1}P(\tau)^{3}+\lambda_{2}P(\tau),\end{split}

(3.24)

with
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
are given by (
3.23
).
In order to recover the evolution of the amplitude in the regular time scale
t
, we define
A(t)=\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}P(t)
, which satisfies the amplitude equation

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}A(t)=\varepsilon\lambda_{2}A(t)+\lambda_{1}A(t)^{3}.\end{split}

(3.25)

As expected, this differential equation undergoes a pitchfork bifurcation at
\varepsilon=0
, where the type of bifurcation (supercritical v.s. subcritical) is determined by the sign of
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
. Note that
\lambda_{2}
is always positive, whereas the sign of
\lambda_{1}
depends on
a
and
L
in a rather implicit manner. Using the formula of
\lambda_{1}
in (
3.23
), we have numerically checked that
\lambda_{1}<0
for all
a\in[0,1]
and
L\in[0,4]
, indicating that (
3.25
) undergoes a supercritical bifurcation in
\varepsilon
for these parameters.

Finally, to obtain the dynamical solution
(u(x,t),v(x,t))
for
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
, plugging (
3.15
) into (
3.9
) (and using the fact that
A(t)=\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}P(t)
), the dynamical solution near the constant steady state can be approximated as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\begin{pmatrix}u(x,t)\\
v(x,t)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\overline{u}\\
\overline{v}\end{pmatrix}+A(t)\cos(w_{k^{*}}x)\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-1\end{pmatrix}+O(\varepsilon),\end{split}

(3.26)

where
A(t)
solves (
3.25
).

For
\varepsilon>0
, when
\lambda_{1}<0,\lambda_{2}>0
, one can easily check that (
3.25
) has three steady states: an unstable steady state zero, and two other stable steady states
\pm\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon\lambda_{2}}{-\lambda_{1}}}
. Thus for
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
, in addition to the unstable constant steady state
(\overline{u},\overline{v})
in (
3.2
), we also expect the system (
3.1
)–(
1.7
) to have two stable almost-constant steady states of the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}\begin{pmatrix}u_{s}\\
v_{s}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\overline{u}\\
\overline{v}\end{pmatrix}\pm\varepsilon^{1/2}\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{2}}{-\lambda_{1}}}\cos(w_{k^{*}}x)\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-1\end{pmatrix}+O(\varepsilon).\end{split}

(3.27)

Appendix A
Numerical scheme

In this section we describe the numerical scheme we use for the numerical simulations in Section
3.3
and Section
4
.
We will only describe the numerical scheme in two dimensions, since the numerical scheme in one dimension is similar and easier. Our numerical method is a semi-implicit finite volume scheme. The system (
3.1
)–(
1.7
) is solved on the square domain
\Omega=[0,L]^{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
and we uniformly divide it into
N^{2}
cells
\{\mathcal{C}_{j,k}\}
using a Cartesian mesh, where

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{C}_{j,k}:=\big[x_{j-\frac{1}{2}},x_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\big]\times\big[y_{k-\frac{1}{2}},y_{k+\frac{1}{2}}\big]\quad\text{ for }j,k=1,2,...,N.\end{split}

(A.1)

Each cell has size
|\mathcal{C}_{j,k}|=\Delta x\Delta y
and the cell
\mathcal{C}_{j,k}
is centered at
(x_{j},y_{k})
, where
\Delta x=x_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-x_{j-\frac{1}{2}}
and
\Delta y=y_{k+\frac{1}{2}}-y_{k-\frac{1}{2}}
for all
j,k=1,2,...N
respectively. We also discretize the time interval
[0,T]
as
t^{n}=n\Delta t
, where
n=0,1,...,\frac{T}{\Delta t}
.

The computed quantities are the cell averages. For a density
\rho(x,y,t)
that is either
u
or
v
, let us denote by
\overline{\rho}_{j,k}(t)
the spatial average of
\rho(t)
over the cell
\mathcal{C}_{j,k}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\overline{\rho}_{j,k}(t)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}_{j,k}|}\iint_{\mathcal{C}_{j,k}}\rho(x,y,t)\ dxdy.\end{split}

(A.2)

Note that the chemotaxis term gives the
\rho
equation a flux
\eta=\chi\rho\nabla c
, where
c
is
c[v]
if
\rho=u
, and
c=c[u]
if
\rho=v
. Integrating (
3.1
) over a spatial-temporal cell
\mathcal{C}_{j,k}\times[t^{n},t^{n+1}]
, we arrive at the fully discrete, fully implicit scheme

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\overline{\rho}_{j,k}^{n+1}-\overline{\rho}_{j,k}^{n}}{\Delta t}&=-\left(\frac{\eta_{j+\frac{1}{2},k}^{(x)}-\eta_{j-\frac{1}{2},k}^{(x)}}{\Delta x}+\frac{\eta_{j,k+\frac{1}{2}}^{(y)}-\eta_{j,k-\frac{1}{2}}^{(y)}}{\Delta y}\right)\\
&+\left(\frac{\overline{\rho}_{j-1,k}^{n+1}-2\overline{\rho}_{j,k}^{n+1}+\overline{\rho}_{j+1,k}^{n+1}}{\Delta x^{2}}+\frac{\overline{\rho}_{j,k-1}^{n+1}-2\overline{\rho}_{j,k}^{n+1}+\overline{\rho}_{j,k+1}^{n+1}}{\Delta y^{2}}\right)+f^{n+1}_{j,k}.\end{split}

(A.3)

Here
\eta_{j\pm\frac{1}{2},k}^{(x)}
and
\eta_{j,k\pm\frac{1}{2}}^{(y)}
are the numerical fluxes on cell boundaries, and
f^{n+1}_{j,k}
are the nonlinear terms due to competition. The numerical fluxes on cell boundaries are computed using the Lax-Friedrichs numerical flux: for example, the numerical flux
\eta_{j+\frac{1}{2},k}^{(x)}
is estimated as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\eta_{j+\frac{1}{2},k}^{(x)}(\overline{\rho}^{n+1}_{j,k},\overline{\rho}^{n+1}_{j+1,k})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\phi(\overline{\rho}^{n+1}_{j,k})+\phi(\overline{\rho}^{n+1}_{j+1,k})\right)-\frac{\Delta x}{2\Delta t}(\overline{\rho}^{n+1}_{j+1,k}-\overline{\rho}^{n+1}_{j,k})\end{split}

(A.4)

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}\phi(\overline{\rho}^{n+1}_{j,k})=\chi\ \overline{\rho}^{n+1}_{j,k}\left(\frac{\overline{c}_{j+1,k}^{n}-\overline{c}_{j-1,k}^{n}}{2\Delta x}\right).\end{split}

(A.5)

The average chemical density
\{\overline{c}_{j,k}^{n}\}
on the cells are recovered from either
\{\overline{u}_{j,k}^{n}\}
or
\{\overline{v}_{j,k}^{n}\}
by solving the equation (
1.7
) with a standard central difference scheme, which is implemented with an efficient iterative method.

Since the terms
f^{n+1}_{j,k}
in (
A.3
) is a nonlinear function of
\overline{u}^{n+1}_{j,k}
and
\overline{v}^{n+1}_{j,k}
, to avoid solving a nonlinear system of equations at each iteration, we linearize it as

\displaystyle\begin{split}f_{j,k}^{n+1}\approx f(\overline{u}_{j,k}^{n},\overline{v}_{j,k}^{n})+\frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\Big|_{(\overline{u}_{j,k}^{n},\overline{v}_{j,k}^{n})}(\overline{u}^{n+1}_{j,k}-\overline{u}^{n}_{j,k})+\frac{\partial f}{\partial v}\Big|_{(\overline{u}_{j,k}^{n},\overline{v}_{j,k}^{n})}(\overline{v}^{n+1}_{j,k}-\overline{v}^{n}_{j,k})\end{split}

(A.6)

to lower the computation costs, and note that the linearization does not affect the first order scheme accuracy.

From our discussion above, for
j,k=1,\dots,N
and
\rho=u,v
, (
A.3
) becomes a system of linear equations with the unknowns being
\overline{u}_{j,k}^{n+1}
and
\overline{v}_{j,k}^{n+1}
. To reduce the computational cost, in practice we implement an incomplete alternating directional implicit (ADI) method, which we describe below. To shorten the notations, let us introduce the following standard spatial difference operators

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{D}_{x}(\overline{\rho}_{j,k}):=\frac{\eta_{j+\frac{1}{2},k}^{(x)}-\eta_{j-\frac{1}{2},k}^{(x)}}{\Delta x}\quad;\quad\mathcal{D}_{xx}(\overline{\rho}_{j,k}):=\ \frac{\overline{\rho}_{j-1,k}-2\overline{\rho}_{j,k}+\overline{\rho}_{j+1,k}}{\Delta x^{2}}.\end{split}

(A.7)

We define
\mathcal{D}_{y}(.)
and
\mathcal{D}_{yy}(.)
in the same way. Thus by moving all unknowns of (
A.3
) (at time step
n+1
) to the left-hand side, we can rearrange the terms so that can be suppressed to a compact form in terms of operators we defined above,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\overline{\rho}^{n+1}-\left((\mathcal{D}_{xx}-\mathcal{D}_{x})+(\mathcal{D}_{yy}-\mathcal{D}_{y})+\frac{d}{d\rho}f(\overline{\rho}^{n})\right)\overline{\rho}^{n+1}\Delta t=\overline{\rho}^{n}+f(\overline{\rho}^{n})\Delta t-\frac{d}{d\rho}f(\overline{\rho}^{n})\overline{\rho}^{n}\Delta t,\end{split}

(A.8)

where the right hand side only contains terms that are known (at the time step
n
).

Let us split the operators into different directions, denoted by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{A}^{(x)}:=\mathcal{D}_{xx}-\mathcal{D}_{x},\ \mathcal{A}^{(y)}:=\mathcal{D}_{yy}-\mathcal{D}_{y},\ \mathcal{A}^{{}^{\prime}}:=\frac{d}{d\rho}f(\overline{\rho}^{n}).\end{split}

(A.9)

Let us perform the imperfect factorization of of (
A.8
):

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left(\mathcal{I}-(\mathcal{A}^{(x)}+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{A}^{{}^{\prime}})\Delta t\right)\left(\mathcal{I}-(\mathcal{A}^{(y)}+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{A}^{{}^{\prime}})\Delta t\right)\overline{\rho}^{n+1}+O(\Delta t^{2})=\text{RHS of \eqref{Eq: compact scheme}},\end{split}

(A.10)

where
\mathcal{I}
is the identity operator. Since the numerical scheme (
A.3
) is a first order scheme, we do not lose any accuracy from the factorization procedure. Omitting the
O(\Delta t^{2})
terms in (
A.10
), we can then write numerical integration process from
t^{n}
to
t^{n+1}
in two steps,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left(\mathcal{I}-(\mathcal{A}^{(x)}+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{A}^{{}^{\prime}})\Delta t\right)\rho^{*}&=\text{RHS of \eqref{Eq: compact scheme}}\\
\left(\mathcal{I}-(\mathcal{A}^{(y)}+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{A}^{{}^{\prime}})\Delta t\right)\overline{\rho}^{n+1}&=\rho^{*}\end{split}

(A.11)

where
\rho^{*}
is an intermediate solution. Finally, we use the Thomas algorithm (tridiagonal matrix algorithm) to solve the linear systems in (
A.11
).
