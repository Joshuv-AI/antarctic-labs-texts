Ehrhart Bounds for Panhandle and Paving Matroids Through Enumeration of Chain Forests
Danai Deligeorgaki, Daniel McGinnis, Andrés R. Vindas-Meléndez

Abstract
. Panhandle matroids are a specific family of lattice-path matroids corresponding to panhandle-shaped Ferrers diagrams. Their matroid polytopes are the subpolytopes carved from a hypersimplex to form matroid polytopes of paving matroids. It has been an active area of research to determine which families of matroid polytopes are Ehrhart positive. We prove Ehrhart positivity for panhandle matroid polytopes, thus confirming a conjecture of Hanely, Martin, McGinnis, Miyata, Nasr, Vindas-Meléndez, and Yin (2023). Another standing conjecture posed by Ferroni (2022) asserts that the coefficients of the Ehrhart polynomial of a connected matroid are bounded above by those of the corresponding uniform matroid. We prove Ferroni’s conjecture for paving matroids – a class conjectured to asymptotically contain all matroids. These results follow from purely enumerative statements, the main one being conjectured by Hanely et. al concerning the enumeration of a certain class of ordered chain forests. Thus, the content and proofs in this paper reflect an enumerative combinatorics perspective, driven by considerations in Ehrhart theory.



1.
Introduction

Two elemental objects in combinatorics and discrete geometry, namely matroids and polytopes, have garnered the attention of mathematicians for years.
Matroid (base) polytopes form a bridge between these two objects.
In this paper we resolve conjectures on the Ehrhart theory (the theory of integer points in polytopes) of two types of matroid polytopes: those arising from
panhandle
and
paving matroids
.

A
matroid

M
on a finite ground set
E
can be defined by its basis system
\mathcal{B}
, a nonempty collection of subsets of
E
, all of the same size, satisfying a certain
exchange condition
.
Given a matroid
M
with ground set
[n]=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
and rank
r
, the
matroid (base) polytope

\mathcal{P}_{M}
is the convex hull in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of the indicator vectors of its bases.
Matroid polytopes provide geometric insight into the structure of matroids.
A panhandle matroid, denoted
\Pan_{r,s,n}
, is a lattice-path matroid corresponding to panhandle-shaped Ferrers diagrams. It is described by the basis system:

\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}_{r,s,n}=\left\{B\in\binom{[n]}{r}:\ |B\cap[s]|\geq r-1\right\}.

See
[
HMM+23
]
for more information on panhandle matroids, although it is not needed to understand the main result of this paper and its proof.
A matroid of rank
r
is called
paving
if every
circuit
has cardinality greater than or equal to
r
, and
sparse paving
if it and its dual are both paving.
Paving matroids are noteworthy as it is conjectured and widely believed that asymptotically all matroids are paving, or even sparse paving (see
[
MNWW11
]
,
[
PvdP15
]
,
[
Oxl11
, Chapter 15.5]
).
For a more in depth background on matroids, we recommend
[
Oxl11
]
and
[
Wel76
]
.

The
Ehrhart function
of a polytope
P
(i.e., the convex hull of finitely many points)
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, is the lattice-point enumerator
\ehr_{P}(t):=|tP\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|
, where
tP=\{t\mathbf{x}:\ \mathbf{x}\in P\}
for
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
Ehrhart proved that when
P
is a lattice polytope (i.e., its vertices have integer coordinates), the Ehrhart function is a polynomial in
t
of degree equal to the dimension of
P
, commonly known as the
Ehrhart polynomial

[
Ehr62
]
.
The leading coefficient of the Ehrhart polynomial of a lattice polytope
P
is equal to its relative volume, the second-leading coefficient is equal to half the relative surface area, and the constant coefficient is 1.
The other coefficients of
\ehr_{P}(t)
are more mystifying, and in general can be negative (see
[
Liu19
, Section 4]
for several examples of Ehrhart polynomials with negative coefficients).
Ehrhart polynomials are an important invariant of lattice polytopes, as they encode much of a polytope’s geometry, arithmetic, and combinatorics.
As a whole, Ehrhart theory has developed into a key topic, playing a role in and benefiting from other areas of mathematics including, but not limited to, number theory
[
Bec99
,
BV08
,
MM15
]
, commutative algebra
[
CLS11
,
Ful93
]
, algebraic geometry
[
Mor93
,
Pom93
]
, enumerative combinatorics
[
BR15
,
BS18
]
, and integer programming
[
Bar06
,
Bru23
]
.

In
[
DLHK09
]
, De Loera, Haws, and Köppe conjectured that the Ehrhart polynomial of every matroid polytope has nonnegative coefficients (i.e., they are
Ehrhart positive
).
Their conjecture was disproved by Ferroni
[
Fer22a
]
, who constructed an infinite family of matroids whose matroid polytopes are not Ehrhart positive.
Determining families of matroid polytopes that do exhibit Ehrhart positivity is itself both a challenging and interesting problem, as evidenced by the extensive literature (see, for example
[
HMM+23
,
FM22
,
Fer21
,
Fer22b
,
FJS22
,
JR22
,
KMSRA18
]
).
The techniques used to show Ehrhart positivity for different polytopes vary widely.
In this paper, we rely solely on combinatorial techniques.

Volume formulas for general matroid polytopes were given by Ardila, Benedetti, and Doker
[
ABD10
]
and Ashraf
[
Ash20
]
, but their Ehrhart polynomials are not as well understood.
One example of a family of matroid polytopes for which their Ehrhart polynomial is determined is
minimal matroids
(i.e., matroids with the least number of bases for their rank and ground set size among all such connected matroids).
In
[
Fer22b
]
, Ferroni calculated the Ehrhart polynomial of minimal matroid polytopes and proved that they are Ehrhart positive.
Subsequently, in
[
Fer22a
]
, Ferroni used the fact that the matroid polytopes of a sparse paving matroid could be obtained from a hypersimplex by slicing with (geometric) hyperplanes.
Each piece sliced off in this way is itself a matroid polytope of the minimal matroid, which resulted in an explicit formula for the Ehrhart polynomial of a sparse paving matroid polytope, for which Ferroni was able to show is not always Ehrhart positive.

In
[
HMM+23
]
, the authors extended Ferroni’s methods from sparse paving to paving matroids and showed that the matroid polytope of any paving matroid can be obtained from slicing pieces off from the hypersimplex using hyperplanes.
The pieces sliced off are matroid polytopes of panhandle matroids.
With combinatorial techniques, they calculated the Ehrhart function of a panhandle matroid polytope and provided several equivalent formulas for it.
One such formula is the following:

Theorem 1.1

(Corollary 5.4 in
[
HMM+23
]
)
.

Let
1\leq r\leq s<n
.
The Ehrhart polynomial of
\mathcal{P}_{\Pan_{r,s,n}}
can be written as

\ehr_{\Pan_{r,s,n}}(t)=\frac{n-s}{(n-1)!}\binom{t+n-s}{n-s}\varphi_{r,s,n}(t)

where

(1)

\varphi_{r,s,n}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{s-r}(-1)^{i}\binom{s}{i}\sum_{\ell=0}^{s-1}(n-2-\ell)!\ell!\binom{s-1-\ell-i+t(s-r-i+1)}{s-1-\ell}\binom{s-1-i+t(s-r-i)}{\ell}.

Though the authors presented the Ehrhart polynomial of a panhandle matroid
1
1

1

For simplicity, we write “the Ehrhart polynomial of the matroid
M
” (denoted,
\ehr_{M}(t)
), to mean the “Ehrhart polynomial of the matroid polytope of

”.
, its Ehrhart positivity is not immediately evident.
Observe that, in the formula for
\ehr_{\Pan_{r,s,n}}(t)
given in Theorem
1.1
,
since
\binom{t+n-s}{n-s}
is a polynomial in

with positive coefficients, in order to prove the Ehrhart positivity of panhandle matroids, it suffices to show that the polynomial
\varphi_{r,s,n}(t)
has positive coefficients.
In
[
HMM+23
]
, the authors conjectured that panhandle matroids are Ehrhart positive
[
HMM+23
, Conjecture 6.1]
, and they showed that the positivity of
\varphi_{r,s,n}(t)
follows from the purely combinatorial statement below. We defer the relevant definitions to Section
2
, and we include the statement here to emphasize that the contents of this paper are mostly of enumerative combinatorics. A proof of Conjecture
1.2
is our first main result.

Conjecture 1.2

(Conjecture 6.9 in
[
HMM+23
]
)
.

Let
1\leq m\leq k\leq s
,
q\geq 0
, and
0\leq\ell\leq s-1
be integers. The number of naturally ordered chain forests
\mathcal{F}
of
[s]
with
k
blocks and weight
q
, such that
\gamma(\mathcal{F},\ell)=m
, is given by

(2)

|\CF(q,s,k,\ell,m)|=\sum_{i=0}^{q}(-1)^{i}\binom{s}{i}\Pi^{s-\ell-m}_{-i+1,s-1-\ell-i}\Pi^{\ell-(k-m)}_{s-\ell-i,s-1-i}\binom{k-1+q-i}{k-1}.

The left-hand side is the number of combinatorial objects called
ordered chain forests
, which are special ordered sets partitions with several additional properties, and the
\Pi
terms on the right-hand side are sums of products of integers that can be thought of as elementary symmetric functions evaluated at integers. As mentioned earlier, the problem of proving Ehrhart positivity for panhandle matroids was reduced to Conjecture
1.2
in
[
HMM+23
]
. Thus, we obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 1.3
.

Given positive integers
r\leq s\leq n-1
, the panhandle matroid
\Pan_{r,s,n}
is Ehrhart positive.

In fact, we prove a stronger bound, namely that the Ehrhart polynomial of
\Pan_{r,s,n}
is bounded below (coefficient-wise) by the Ehrhart polynomial of a product between a simplex and a hypersimplex (Remark
5.7
).

This work is in part motivated by the fact that panhandle matroids are special instances of other well-studied families of matroids, including Schubert matroids, lattice-path matroids, and positroids.
Panhandle matroid polytopes are also instances of the more general family of (weighted) multi-hypersimplices, a family of alcoved polytopes considered in
[
LP05
]
.
In fact, they are polypositroids
[
LP20
]
, a subclass of alcoved polytopes that are conjectured to be Ehrhart positive
[
FJS22
, Conjecture 6.3]
.
Additionally, panhandle matroids are
notched rectangle matroids
, defined in
[
FL23
]
(also known as
cuspidal matroids
in
[
FS22
]
), which are conjectured to be Ehrhart positive in both works. Hence, our results, namely Theorem
1.3
, supports these conjectures. Furthermore,
uniform matroids
are special instances of panhandle matroids as
\Pan_{r,n-1,n}=U_{r,n}
is the uniform matroid with rank
r
and ground set
[n]
. On the other hand, we have
\Pan_{r,r,n}
is the minimal matroid
T_{r,n}
. Therefore, Theorem
1.3
generalizes and interpolates between the corresponding Ehrhart positivity results of Ferroni showing that both
T_{r,n}
and
U_{r,n}
are Ehrhart positive
[
Fer21
,
Fer22b
]
.

We then continue to discuss a related problem to Conjecture
1.2
.
Ferroni posed in
[
Fer22b
]
a conjecture asserting that for a connected matroid
M
with rank
r
and
n
elements, the Ehrhart polynomial of
\mathcal{P}_{M}
is coefficient-wise bounded below and above by the Ehrhart polynomial of the corresponding minimal matroid and the uniform matroid
U_{r,n}
, respectively.
In general, the lower bound is not true as Ferroni himself proved that there are matroids which fail to be Ehrhart positive
[
Fer22a
]
while the Ehrhart polynomials of minimal matroids have positive coefficients
[
Fer22b
]
.
The second main result of this paper is to confirm Ferroni’s Ehrhart-coefficient upper-bound conjecture, for the class of paving matroids.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
M
be a paving matroid of rank
r
with ground set
[n]
. Then

\ehr_{M}(t)\preceq\ehr_{U_{r,n}}(t).

The proof of this theorem relies on a general formula for the Ehrhart polynomial of a paving matroid that was first presented in
[
HMM+23
]
.
Again, a proof of Theorem
1.4
can be reduced to a combinatorial result akin to Conjecture
1.2
, which we resolve using similar methods.
We further show that the relaxation of an Ehrhart-positive matroid is also Ehrhart positive (see Section
5
for relevant definitions and results).

The techniques in this paper lay in the realm of enumerative combinatorics, and the proofs stand without particular knowledge of matroids, matroid polytopes, or Ehrhart theory. These topics are simply the sole motivation of this paper. Therefore, we omit detailed background information on these topics and refer to
[
HMM+23
]
for the precise definition of panhandle matroids and an overview of their Ehrhart theory.

The paper is structured as follows:

•

In Section
2
, we define
ordered chain forests
, which are combinatorial objects that emerge from specific set partitions. We also define auxiliary combinatorial objects which are not directly present in the statement of Conjecture
1.2
but are required to support our proof of Conjecture
1.2
.

•

Section
3
is concerned with understanding each term within the summation appearing in Conjecture
1.2
.
We do so by constructing a bijection between objects defined in Section
2
.

•

Section
4
presents the proof of Conjecture
1.2
, implying that panhandle matroids are Ehrhart positive, via a sign-reversing involution type argument.

•

In Section
5
, we use the tools developed in previous sections to confirm Ferroni’s Ehrhart-coefficient upper-bound conjecture in the case of paving matroids by proving another enumerative combinatorial statement akin to Conjecture
1.2
. We further show that Ehrhart-positivity is preserved through stressed-hyperplane relaxations.

2.
Toward Ehrhart positivity: Combinatorial objects

We recall that in Section 6 of
[

Definition 2.1
.

An
ordered chain forest
of
[s]
is an ordered partition of
[s]
into a set of blocks, where each block is itself internally ordered.

We write an ordered chain forest
\mathcal{F}
as a sequence of blocks
\mathcal{F}=\mathsf{B}_{1}\cdots\mathsf{B}_{k}
.
Example
2.2
illustrates some of these definitions.

(1)

The first and last element of a block is called its
leader
and
trailer
, respectively.

(2)

An ordered chain forest is said to be

if the blocks are ordered increasingly according to their leaders.

(3)

The
weight of a block

\mathsf{B}
, denoted
\wt(\mathsf{B})
, in an ordered chain forest is the number of elements of the block that are less than its leader.
If an element is less than the leader of its block, we say this element
contributes weight
to the block.

(4)

The

is the sum of the weights of its blocks.

Example 2.2
.

Consider the ordered chain forests
\mathcal{F}_{1}=[1,3][4,2][5]
and
\mathcal{F}_{2}=[4,2][1,3][5]
.
Observe that
\mathcal{F}_{1}
is naturally ordered, while
\mathcal{F}_{2}
is not, and they both are distinct from one another because the ordering of their blocks differs.
Additionally,
[3,1][4,2][5]
is distinct from
[1,3][4,2][5]
as well since the ordering of the elements within each block matters.
Now, consider the ordered chain forest
\mathcal{F}=[1,3][5,2,4][7,6][8].

The weights of each block are 0, 2, 1, and 0, respectively.
The total weight of
\mathcal{F}
is 3.

Given an ordered chain forest
\mathcal{F}=\mathsf{B}_{1}\cdots\mathsf{B}_{k}
of
[s]

0\leq l\leq s-1
, we define

\gamma(\mathcal{F},\ell):=\left|\left\{j\in[k]\mid\sum_{i=1}^{j}|\mathsf{B}_{i}|>\ell\right\}\right|.

In other words, if we make note of the sequence of numbers obtained by writing the elements as they appear in
\mathcal{F}
, then
\gamma(\mathcal{F},\ell)
is the number of trailers that appear after the
\ell^{th}
position.
For example, if
\mathcal{F}=[1,3][4,2][5]
, then the corresponding sequence of numbers is
1\ 3\ 4\ 2\ 5
and the trailers are 3, 2, and 5.
Here, we say 1 is in the first position, 3 is in the second position, 4 is in the third position, and so on. Thus, we have that

\gamma(\mathcal{F},0)=3,\,\gamma(\mathcal{F},1)=3,\,\gamma(\mathcal{F},2)=2,\,\gamma(\mathcal{F},3)=2,\,\gamma(\mathcal{F},4)=1.

3.

Recall that the blocks of a valued

-distinguished ordered chain forest

3.1.
An algorithm defined on valued
A
-distinguished ordered chain forests.

Let

-distinguished ordered chain forest of

Algorithm 1

(Processing valued ordered chain forests)
.

\mathtt{Input}
: valued ordered chain forest

,

, naturally-ordered list

\mathtt{Output}

(1)

Find the leftmost block

Lemma 3.4

Let

Proof.

We proceed by induction.
Let

denote the valued ordered chain forest and

By definition, the statement of the lemma is true for

Assume that the statement is true for
\mathsf{B}_{1,i}^{\beta_{1,i}}\cdots{\mathsf{B}_{k,i}^{\beta_{k,i}}}

Let

be the smallest index such that
\mathsf{B}_{j,i}

\mathsf{B}_{j,i}

Let

Lemma 3.5

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
(\mathcal{F},v,A)\in DCF(q,s)

Lemma 3.7
.

Let

(3)

Take the largest processed element

(4)

Combining everything discussed, we have that

Finally, it is clear that

The blocks

(2)

The

(3)

\mathcal{F}

We have that

At each iterative step in Algorithm

4.

This section contains a proof of Conjecture
1.2
(also printed below). We note that the validity of Conjecture

was shown in

]
to imply the Ehrhart positivity of panhandle matroids (Theorem

The proof of Conjecture

has an inclusion-exclusion flavor, utilizing the combinatorial interpretation discussed in Proposition

. In particular, Proposition

provides an interpretation for each of the summands in Conjecture

The proof is presented in detail below.

Conjecture

Let
1\leq m\leq k\leq s

of

Let
S

(\mathcal{F}^{\prime},v^{\prime},\varnothing)=\phi((\mathcal{F},v,\varnothing))

\phi(DCF(q,s,k,\ell,m))

Since

Now, we define a bijection

Let
(\mathcal{F},v,A)=(\mathsf{B}_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\cdots\mathsf{B}_{r}^{\beta_{r}}\mathsf{C}_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}\cdots\mathsf{C}_{p}^{\gamma_{p}},A)

(1)

\phi((\mathcal{F},v,A))
, or
j\geq 1
and the leader of
\mathsf{C}_{1}
is greater than the leader of
\mathsf{B}_{r}^{\prime}
, then we define

f((\mathcal{F},v,A))=\phi^{-1}((\mathsf{B}_{1}^{\prime\beta_{1}^{\prime}}\cdots\mathsf{B}_{r}^{\prime\beta_{r}^{\prime}}\mathsf{C}_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}\cdots\mathsf{C}_{p}^{\gamma_{p}},A\setminus\mathsf{C}_{1})),

where by a slight abuse of notation we associate
\mathsf{C}_{1}
with the set of its elements;

(2)

Otherwise, if
j\geq 1
and the leader of
\mathsf{C}_{1}
is less than the leader of
\mathsf{B}_{r}^{\prime}
(or
A=\varnothing
), then we define

f((\mathcal{F},v,A))=\phi^{-1}((\mathsf{B}_{1}^{\prime\beta_{1}^{\prime}}\cdots\mathsf{B}_{r}^{\prime\beta_{r}^{\prime}}\mathsf{C}_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}\cdots\mathsf{C}_{p}^{\gamma_{p}},A\cup\mathsf{B}_{r}^{\prime})).

Again, we are associating
\mathsf{B}_{r}^{\prime}
with the set of its elements.

In the first case,
f((\mathcal{F},v,A))
has 1 less block in its distinguished part, and in the second case,
f((\mathcal{F},v,A))
has 1 more block in its distinguished part.
Hence,
f((\mathcal{F},v,A))\in\mathscr{P}
in both cases.
Additionally, it follows from Proposition
3.10
that
(\mathsf{B}_{1}^{\prime\beta_{1}^{\prime}}\cdots\mathsf{B}_{r}^{\prime\beta_{r}^{\prime}}\mathsf{C}_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}\cdots\mathsf{C}_{p}^{\gamma_{p}},A\setminus\mathsf{C}_{1})
in case (1) and
(\mathsf{B}_{1}^{\prime\beta_{1}^{\prime}}\cdots\mathsf{B}_{r}^{\prime\beta_{r}^{\prime}}\mathsf{C}_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}\cdots\mathsf{C}_{p}^{\gamma_{p}},A\cup\mathsf{B}_{r}^{\prime})
in case (2) are elements of
\phi(DCF(q,s,k,\ell,m))
, i.e.,
f
is well-defined.

We now show that the map
f
is injective. First note that if
f((\mathcal{F},v,A))=f((\mathcal{G},w,E))
and the distinguished parts of
(\mathcal{F},v,A)
and
(\mathcal{G},w,E)
have the same number of blocks, then the same case i.e., (1) or (2) in the definition of
f
applies to both and it follows immediately from the fact that
\phi
is a bijection that
(\mathcal{F},v,A)=(\mathcal{G},w,E)
.
Therefore, we can assume without loss of generality that the distinguished part of
(\mathcal{F},v,A)
has
p
blocks and the distinguished part of
(\mathcal{G},w,E)
has
p-2
blocks and that case (1) in the definition of
f
applies to
(\mathcal{F},v,A)
and case (2) applies to
(\mathcal{G},w,E)
.
Then, writing

\phi((\mathcal{F},v,A))=(\mathsf{B}_{1}^{\prime\beta_{1}^{\prime}}\cdots\mathsf{B}_{r}^{\prime\beta_{r}^{\prime}}\mathsf{C}_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}\cdots\mathsf{C}_{p}^{\gamma_{p}},A),

where the blocks
\mathsf{C}_{i}
are the blocks containing elements of
A
, we have that

f((\mathcal{F},v,A))=\phi^{-1}((\mathsf{B}_{1}^{\prime\beta_{1}^{\prime}}\cdots\mathsf{B}_{r}^{\prime\beta_{r}^{\prime}}\mathsf{C}_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}\cdots\mathsf{C}_{p}^{\gamma_{p}},A\setminus\mathsf{C}_{1})).

Hence,

\phi(f((\mathcal{F},v,A)))=(\mathsf{B}_{1}^{\prime\beta_{1}^{\prime}}\cdots\mathsf{B}_{r}^{\prime\beta_{r}^{\prime}}\mathsf{C}_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}\cdots\mathsf{C}_{p}^{\gamma_{p}},A\setminus\mathsf{C}_{1}),

and since
\phi(f((\mathcal{G},w,E)))=\phi(f((\mathcal{F},v,A)))
we must have that

\phi((\mathcal{G},w,E))=(\mathsf{B}_{1}^{\prime\beta_{1}^{\prime}}\cdots\mathsf{B}_{r}^{\prime\beta_{r}^{\prime}}\mathsf{C}_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}\cdots\mathsf{C}_{p}^{\gamma_{p}},A\setminus(\mathsf{C}_{1}\cup\mathsf{C}_{2})).

By item (4) of Proposition
3.10
applied to
\phi((\mathcal{F},v,A))
, for
0\leq j\leq r
, we have that

\sum_{i=1}^{j-r}\wt(\mathsf{B}_{i}^{\prime})+\sum_{i=j-r+1}^{r}(|\mathsf{B}_{i}^{\prime}|+\beta_{i}^{\prime})+|A|+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\gamma_{i}=q,

or equivalently,

\sum_{i=1}^{j-r}\wt(\mathsf{B}_{i}^{\prime})+\sum_{i=j-r+1}^{r}(|\mathsf{B}_{i}^{\prime}|+\beta_{i}^{\prime})+(|\mathsf{C}_{1}|+\gamma_{1})+(|\mathsf{C}_{2}|+\gamma_{2})+|A\setminus(\mathsf{C}_{1}\cup\mathsf{C}_{2})|+\sum_{i=3}^{p}\gamma_{i}=q.

Thus, by items (4) and (2) of Proposition
3.10
applied to
\phi((\mathcal{G},w,E))
, we have that there is some
j^{\prime}\geq 2
such that the last
j^{\prime}
blocks among
\mathsf{B}_{1}^{\prime\beta_{1}^{\prime}},\dots,\mathsf{B}_{r}^{\prime\beta_{r}^{\prime}},\mathsf{C}_{1}^{\gamma_{1}},\mathsf{C}_{2}^{\gamma_{2}}
of
\phi((\mathcal{G},w,E))
are ordered increasingly according to their leaders.
In particular, the leader of
\mathsf{C}_{1}
is less than the leader of
\mathsf{C}_{2}
.
However, the fact that these are the first two blocks in the distinguished part of
\phi((\mathcal{F},v,A))
implies that the leader of
\mathsf{C}_{1}
is greater than the leader of
\mathsf{C}_{2}
.
This is a contradiction and completes the proof of the injectivity of
f
.

Although it is not hard to show directly that
f
is a surjection, we present a different argument to complete the proof.
Let us fix suitable
s,k,l
and allow
m
to vary between
1
and
k
.
For
1\leq m\leq k
, the fact that
f
is an injection implies that, for some
p_{m}\geq 0
,

\sum_{i=0}^{q}(-1)^{i}\binom{s}{i}\Pi^{s-\ell-m}_{-i+1,s-\ell-1-i}\Pi^{\ell-(k-m)}_{s-\ell-i,s-1-i}\binom{k-1+q-i}{k-1}=|\CF(q,s,k,\ell,m)|+p_{m}.

Specifically,
p_{m}=|\mathscr{P}|-|f(\mathscr{N})|
.
Hence, if we prove that
p_{m}=0
, then
f(\mathscr{N})=\mathscr{P}
and it follows that
f
is a bijection.

Summing over
m
, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{k}\sum_{i=0}^{q}(-1)^{i}\binom{s}{i}\Pi^{s-\ell-m}_{-i+1,s-\ell-1-i}\Pi^{\ell-(k-m)}_{s-\ell-i,s-1-i}\binom{k-1+q-i}{k-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{q}(-1)^{i}\binom{s}{i}\sum_{m=0}^{k}\left(\Pi^{s-\ell-m}_{-i+1,s-\ell-1-i}\Pi^{\ell-(k-m)}_{s-\ell-i,s-1-i}\right)\binom{k-1+q-i}{k-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{q}(-1)^{i}\binom{s}{i}\Pi^{s-k}_{-i+1,s-1-i}\binom{k-1+q-i}{k-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{k}\left(|\CF(q,s,k,\ell,m)|+p_{m}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\CF(q,s,k)|+\sum_{m=0}^{k}p_{m}.

However, we know from Equation (
3
) that

|\CF(q,s,k)|=\sum_{i=0}^{q}(-1)^{i}\binom{s}{i}\Pi^{s-k}_{-i+1,s-1-i}\binom{k-1+q-i}{k-1},

which implies that
\sum_{m=0}^{k}p_{m}=0
, proving that
f
is indeed a bijection.
Therefore, the result follows.
∎

This completes the proof of Conjecture
1.2
, and with it the proof of Theorem
1.3
, namely, Ehrhart positivity of panhandle matroids.

5.
Ehrhart-coefficient upper-bound conjecture for paving matroids

Recall that
U_{r,n}
denotes the uniform matroid with rank
r
and
n
elements, matroid polytope
\mathcal{P}_{U_{r,n}}
is precisely the hypersimplex
\Delta_{r,n}
.
It is known that the Ehrhart polynomial of
\mathcal{P}_{U_{r,n}}
has positive coefficients
[
Fer21
]
.
The uniform matroid
U_{r,n}
is the unique matroid with rank
r
and
n
elements and
\binom{n}{r}
bases.
On the other hand, by a result of Dinolt
[
Din71
]
and Murty
[
Mur71
]
, there is a unique connected matroid, which we have referred to as the minimal matroid and denoted
T_{r,n}
, with rank
r
and
n
elements that has the minimum possible number of bases.

In what follows, for two polynomials
p(t)
and
q(t)
of degree
n
,
p(t)\preceq q(t)
means that the
d^{{th}}
-coefficient of
p(t)
is bounded above by the
d^{{th}}
-coefficient of
q(t)
, for every
0\leq d\leq n
.
In
[
Fer22b
]
, Ferroni conjectured the following.

Conjecture 5.1

(Conjecture 1.5 in
[
Fer22b
]
)
.

For a connected matroid
M
with rank
r
and
n
elements,

\ehr_{T_{r,n}}(t)\preceq\ehr_{M}(t)\preceq\ehr_{U_{r,n}}.

It is known that the lower-bound portion of the conjecture
\ehr_{T_{r,n}}(t)\preceq\ehr_{M}(t)
is not true in general as Ferroni himself proved that there are matroids whose Ehrhart polynomials have negative coefficients
[
Fer22a
]
, while the Ehrhart polynomials of minimal matroids have positive coefficients
[
Fer22b
]
.
However, at the time of writing, there are no counterexamples to the upper-bound portion of the conjecture,
\ehr_{M}(t)\preceq\ehr_{U_{r,n}}
. In this section, building on the work in
[
HMM+23
]
, we show that the upper-bound portion of Conjecture
5.1
holds when
M
is a paving matroid.

Theorem
1.4
.

Let
M
be a paving matroid of rank
r
with ground set
[n]
. Then

\ehr_{M}(t)\preceq\ehr_{U_{r,n}}(t).

In order to communicate a result refining Theorem
1.4
and explain how it follows from the contents of this section, we will need the following definitions and known results.

Definition 5.3
.

For a matroid
M
with basis system
\mathcal{B}
, let
\Rel_{S}(\mathcal{B}):=\mathcal{B}\cup\binom{S}{r}
, for a set
S\subseteq E
containing no basis.
We say that
S

can be relaxed
if
\mathcal{B}\cup\binom{S}{r}
is a matroid basis system.
In this case, the resulting matroid is called the
relaxation of
M
at
S
, denoted by
\Rel_{S}(M)
.

A hyperplane
H
of a matroid
M
is a
stressed hyperplane
if every subset of
H
of size
r
is a circuit.

It turns out that stressed hyperplanes are connected to paving matroids.

Proposition 5.4
.

[
FNV22
, Proposition 3.16]

A matroid is paving if and only if all its hyperplanes are stressed.

Proposition 5.5
.

[
FNV22
, Theorem 1.2]

If
H
is a stressed hyperplane of
M
, then
H
can be relaxed.

When
S
is a circuit-hyperplane, the relaxation of a matroid
M
at
S
coincides with the common notion of a relaxation as in
[
Oxl11
, Section 1.5]
, and in
[
HMM+23
, Definition 3.4]
that, when
S
is a stressed hyperplane, the stressed-hyperplane relaxation is recovered.
In this section, the sets relaxed are always stressed hyperplanes, though in principle it is possible to relax other sets in matroids too.
In
[
HMM+23
]
, a formula for the Ehrhart polynomial of a relaxation of
M
was determined in terms of
\ehr_{M}(t)
, which is presented in the following theorem.

Theorem 5.6

(Theorem 5.9 in
[
HMM+23
]
)
.

Let
M
be a rank
r
matroid with ground set
[n]
and let
H
be a stressed hyperplane such that
|H|=s\geq r
. Then

\displaystyle\ehr_{\Rel_{H}(M)}(t)=\ehr_{M}(t)+\frac{n-s}{(n-1)!}\binom{t-1+n-s}{n-s}\psi_{r,s,n}(t)

where

\psi_{r,s,n}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{s-r}(-1)^{i}\binom{s}{i}\sum_{\ell=0}^{s-1}(n-2-\ell)!\ell!\binom{s-2-\ell-i+t(s-r-i+1)}{s-1-\ell}\binom{s-1-i+t(s-r-i)}{\ell}.

For our purposes, the significance of Theorem
5.6
is that it implies that if the polynomial

\binom{t-1+n-s}{n-s}\psi_{r,s,n}(t)

has nonnegative coefficients for all
r\leq s\leq n-1
, then

\ehr_{M}(t)\preceq\ehr_{\Rel_{H}(M)}(t).

Since
\binom{t-1+n-s}{n-s}
is a polynomial with nonnegative coefficients, it suffices to show that
\psi_{r,s,n}(t)
has nonnegative coefficients.

Remark 5.7
.

We have that

\frac{n-s}{(n-1)!}\binom{t-1+n-s}{n-s}\psi_{r,s,n}(t)=\ehr_{\Pan_{r,s,n}}(t)-\ehr_{U_{r-1,s}\oplus U_{1,n-s}}(t)

(see the proof of Theorem 5.8 in
[
HMM+23
]
for instance). Therefore, our proof of non-negativity of
\psi_{r,s,n}(t)
, together with the factorization

\ehr_{U_{r-1,s}\oplus U_{1,n-s}}(t)=\ehr_{\Delta_{r-1,s}\times\Delta_{1,n-s}}(t)

imply that

\ehr_{\Delta_{r-1,s}\times\Delta_{1,n-s}}(t)\preceq\ehr_{\Pan_{r,s,n}}(t).

In other words, the Ehrhart polynomial of the pandhandle matroid is bounded below (coefficient-wise) by the Ehrhart polynomial of a product of a hypersimplex with a simplex. This may be interesting in view of Ferroni’s conjecture (see also Theorem
1.4
) asserting that

\ehr_{\Pan_{r,s,n}}(t)\preceq\ehr_{\Delta_{r,n}}(t).

It is also a strengthening of Theorem
1.3
, since
\ehr_{\Delta_{r-1,s}\times\Delta_{1,n-s}}(t)=\ehr_{\Delta_{r-1,s}}(t)\ehr_{\Delta_{1,n-s}}(t)
and
hypersimplices are Ehrhart positive
[
Fer21
]
.
Even though Theorem
5.12
alone implies the non-negativity of Ehrhart polynomials of panhandle matroids, we believe Conjecture
1.2
is interesting in its own right from a purely enumerative perspective, since it enumerates a more natural set of objects. Therefore, we provide full details for the proof of Conjecture
1.2
and then discuss how the arguments can be adapted to prove the claim in Theorem
5.12
.

Combining the previous result and the following lemma about relaxations of paving matroids, which follows from Propositions
5.4
and
5.5
, we can deduce that Ferroni’s upper-bound conjecture follows for the class of paving matroids from showing the nonnegativity of
\psi_{r,s,n}(t)
.

Lemma 5.8

(Corollary 3.17 in
[
FNV22
]
)
.

Let
M
be a paving matroid with rank
r
on the ground set
[n]
.
The uniform matroid
U_{r,n}
is obtained after relaxing each (stressed) hyperplane of
M
.

In
[
HMM+23
]
, it was shown that
\psi_{r,s,n}(t)
has nonnegative coefficients if the expression given by

(7)

\sum_{i=0}^{q}(-1)^{i}\binom{s}{i}\Pi^{s-\ell-m}_{-i,s-\ell-2-i}\Pi^{\ell-(k-m)}_{s-\ell-i,s-1-i}\binom{k-1+q-i}{k-1}

is nonnegative for all
k\geq 1
,
1\leq m\leq k
,
q\geq 0
and
0\leq\ell\leq s-1
.
Thus, we have the following.

Observation 5.9
.

If for all integers
k\geq 1
,
1\leq m\leq k
,
q\geq 0
and
0\leq\ell\leq s-1
, the expression

\sum_{i=0}^{q}(-1)^{i}\binom{s}{i}\Pi^{s-\ell-m}_{-i,s-\ell-2-i}\Pi^{\ell-(k-m)}_{s-\ell-i,s-1-i}\binom{k-1+q-i}{k-1}

is nonnegative, then for any paving matroid
M
with rank
r
on ground set
[n]
, we have that

\ehr_{M}(t)\preceq\ehr_{U_{r,n}}(t).

In what follows, we state and prove Theorem
5.12
, affirming that expression (
7
) is nonnegative.
Specifically, we give a combinatorial interpretation for this expression similar to that given in Conjecture
1.2
.
Considering that the above expression is so similar to the expression in Conjecture
1.2
, many of the arguments are analogous.

Another consequence of Theorem
5.12
is the following, which confirms Conjecture 6.2 in
[
HMM+23
]
.

Corollary 5.10

(Conjecture 6.2 in
[
HMM+23
]
)
.

Let
M
be a matroid with rank
r
and let
H
be a stressed hyperplane with
|H|\geq r
.
If
M
is Ehrhart positive, then so is
\Rel_{H}(M)
.

Proof.

Theorem
5.12
and Observation
5.9
imply that
\psi_{r,s,n}(t)
has positive coefficients for all
r\leq s\leq n-1
.
It then follows by Theorem
5.6
that
\Rel_{H}(M)(t)
has positive coefficients.
∎

In the following definition, we introduce the combinatorial objects that expression (
7
) enumerates.
These objects may seem unnatural compared to the naturally ordered chain forests from Conjecture
1.2
, however, these are precisely the objects that are relevant for the statement of Theorem
1.4
.
We note that, unlike Conjecture
1.2
, the authors of
[
HMM+23
]
did not conjecture a combinatorial interpretation for expression (
7
).
Rather, we are able to deduce Theorem
1.4
by applying the reasoning from the proof of Conjecture
1.2
to expression (
7
).

Definition 5.11
.

Let
\CF^{1}(q,s,k,\ell,m)
be the number of ordered chain forests
\mathcal{F}
of
[s]
with
k
blocks, and
\gamma(\mathcal{F},\ell)=m\geq 2
with the following properties.
Writing
\mathcal{F}=\mathsf{B}_{1}\cdots\mathsf{B}_{k-1}\mathsf{C}_{1}
, we have:

(1)

1\in\mathsf{C}_{1}
and 1 is the leader of
\mathsf{C}_{1}
,

(2)

\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\wt(\mathsf{B}_{i})+|\mathsf{C}_{1}|=q
,

(3)

The blocks
\mathsf{B}_{1},\dots,\mathsf{B}_{k-1}
are naturally ordered,

(4)

The leader of the
(k-m+2)^{th}
block is in the
(\ell+2)^{th}
position.

We note that the reason we require
m\geq 2
is due to the fact that if the leader of a block is in the
(\ell+2)^{th}
position, then some block ends at the
(\ell+1)^{th}
position.
Hence, there are at least 2 blocks that end after the
\ell^{th}
position.

We prove the following theorem, which shows that the expression in question enumerates the objects in Definition
5.11
, thus proving Ferroni’s Ehrhart-coefficient upper-bound conjecture for paving matroids.

Theorem 5.12
.

Let
1\leq m\leq k\leq s
,
q\geq 0
, and
0\leq\ell\leq s-1
be integers. Then

|\CF^{1}(q+1,s+1,k+1,\ell,m+1)|=\sum_{i=0}^{q}(-1)^{i}\binom{s}{i}\Pi^{s-\ell-m}_{-i,s-\ell-2-i}\Pi^{\ell-(k-m)}_{s-\ell-i,s-1-i}\binom{k-1+q-i}{k-1}.

We now outline a proof of Theorem
5.12
.
The proofs of the statements in this section are analogous to those in Sections
2
and
3
.
Therefore, we frequently omit the details and reference analogous results in previous sections.

Definition 5.13
.

Let
1\leq m\leq k\leq s
,
q\geq 0
, and
0\leq\ell\leq s-1
be integers.
We define
DCF^{1}(q,s,k,\ell,m)
to be the set of valued
A
-distinguished ordered chain forests
(\mathcal{F},v,A)\in DCF(q,s,k,\ell,m)
that have the following additional properties:

(1)

1\in A
,

(2)

The leader of the
(k-m+2)^{th}
block is in the
(\ell+2)^{th}
position,

(3)

For the block
\mathsf{B}
containing
1
,
v(\mathsf{B})=0
.

The following is analogous to Proposition
2.12
.

Proposition 5.14
.

Let
1\leq m\leq k\leq s
,
q\geq 0
, and
0\leq\ell\leq s-1
be integers.
Let
T^{1}_{i}
be the set of
i
-element subsets
A
of
[s+1]
such that
1\in A
, and let
Z=DCF^{1}(q+1,s+1,k+1,\ell,m+1)
.
Then

\displaystyle(-1)^{i}\binom{s}{i}\Pi^{s-\ell-m}_{-i,s-\ell-2-i}\Pi^{\ell-(k-m)}_{s-\ell-i,s-1-i}\binom{k-1+q-i}{k-1}=\sum_{A\in T^{1}_{i+1}}\sum_{(\mathcal{F},v,A)\in Z}(-1)^{|B_{A}(\mathcal{F})|-1}.

Proof.

The proof is analogous to the proof of Proposition
2.12
.
The quantity
\binom{s}{i}
is the number of ways to choose an
i+1
-element subset
A
of
[s+1]
that contains 1.
The quantity
\Pi^{s-\ell-m}_{-i,s-\ell-2-i}\Pi^{\ell-(k-m)}_{s-\ell-i,s-1-i}
is the sum of all products of the form
i_{1}\cdots i_{s-\ell-m}\cdot j_{1}\cdots j_{\ell-(k-m)}
where

-i\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{s-\ell-m}\leq s-\ell-2-i

and

s-\ell-i\leq j_{1}<\cdots<j_{\ell-(k-m)}\leq s-1-i.

In particular, among the numbers between
-i
and
s-1-i
(inclusive) there are precisely
k
numbers that do not get selected to be in the product.
As in the proof of Proposition
2.12
, these
k
numbers correspond to starting a block at a specific position.
Since, in addition, the first position is always the beginning of some block, we have
k+1
blocks in total.
Additionally,
s-\ell-1-i
never appears in such a product, and since this is the
(\ell+1)^{th}
largest element among
-i,\dots,s-1-i
, this means we will always start a block at the
(\ell+2)^{th}
position (again following the proof of Proposition
2.12
).
Moreover, since there are
k-m
numbers greater than
s-\ell-1-i
that do not appear in the product, it follows that there are
k-m+1
blocks (including the block starting at the first position) that come before the block starting at the
(\ell+2)^{th}
position.
Hence, the block starting at the
(\ell+2)^{th}
position is the
(k-m+2)^{th}
block.

The sign of such a product is given by
(-1)^{i+1-B_{A}}
where
B_{A}
is the number of blocks that are filled with elements of
A
.
Together with the
(-1)^{i}
term, we have that the product
(-1)^{i}i_{1}\cdots i_{s-\ell-m}\cdot j_{1}\cdots j_{\ell-(k-m)}
comes with the sign
(-1)^{|B_{A}|-1}
.

Finally, the quantity
\binom{k-1+q-i}{k-1}
is the number of ways to assign nonnegative values to the blocks that do not contain 1.
Thus, by a similar reasoning as in the proof of Proposition
2.12
, we obtain the result.
∎

The following statements are very similar to Propositions
3.9
and
3.10
, and we omit the proofs.

Proposition 5.15
.

The map
\phi:DCF^{1}(q,s,k,\ell,m)\longrightarrow\phi(DCF^{1}(q,s,k,\ell,m))
is a bijection.

Proposition 5.16
.

An element
(\mathcal{F},v,A)
is contained in
\phi(DCF^{1}(q,s,k,\ell,m)
if and only if
(\mathcal{F},v)=\mathsf{B}_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\cdots\mathsf{B}_{r}^{\beta_{r}}\mathsf{C}_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}\cdots\text{{$\mathsf{C}_{p-1}^{\gamma_{p-1}}$}}\mathsf{C}_{p}^{0}
has the following properties:
For some integer
0\leq j\leq r
,

(1)

The blocks
\mathsf{C}_{1},\dots,\mathsf{C}_{p}
consist only of elements in
A
, and the remaining blocks do not contain elements in
A
.
The blocks
\mathsf{C}_{1},\dots,\mathsf{C}_{p}
