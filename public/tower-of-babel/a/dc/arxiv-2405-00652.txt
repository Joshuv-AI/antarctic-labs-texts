Domain-wall circulation gating and pinning via compositional engineering of cylindrical nanowires
Laura Álvaro-Gómez, Claudia Fernández-González, Sandra Ruiz-Gómez, Nicolas Mille, Jean Christophe Toussaint, Rachid Belkhou, Michael Foerster, Eva Pereiro, Lucía Aballe, Núria Bagués, Christophe Thirion, Daria Gusakova, Aurelien Masseboeuf, Olivier Fruchart, Lucas Pérez

Abstract
Spintronic devices based on cylindrical magnetic nanowires have been proposed as a route toward three‑dimensional information storage and processing by harnessing the controlled motion of domain walls (DWs). In this geometry, the Bloch-point wall (BPW) is the characteristic DW type. It features a curling of magnetization around a Bloch Point singularity, a topology that has been predicted to enable propagation at high speed without undergoing a Walker breakdown. We demonstrate that compositional modulations of larger magnetization, which host spontaneous curling states while the rest of the wire remains axial, fulfil a dual role: they act as deterministic pinning sites and as active gates for BPW circulation. Combining magnetic transmission X‑ray microscopy and micromagnetic simulations, we reveal that DW–modulation interactions are the result of a superposition of long‑range magnetostatic repulsion and short‑range, circulation‑dependent exchange contributions: parallel circulation produces an attractive well, while opposite circulation generates a repulsive barrier. This results in DW pinning at or near a compositional modulation. A DW’s internal circulation can be inverted upon traversing a modulation, enabling on‑demand switching of its circulation state. This dual mechanism demonstrates that both DW position and circulation can be tuned in a controlled manner, with potential implications for multistate spintronic devices.

Here we experimentally demonstrate that engineered compositional modulations — nanoscale Fe-rich zones (Fe

II
Experimental evidence

II.1
Domain wall pinning

In this section, we report on the interaction between domain walls (DWs) and periodic Fe-rich compositional modulations embedded in Permalloy nanowires, resulting in pinning effects. DWs were previously nucleated either by applying an external magnetic field of approximately
1\text{\,}\mathrm{T}
perpendicular to the wire axis or by injecting a

1\text{\times}{10}^{12}\text{\,}\mathrm{A}\text{${}\cdot{}$}{\mathrm{m}}^{-2}

The combination of high spatial resolution and direct probing of magnetization provides unambiguous information about the DW type and inner structure.

II.2
Domain wall circulation gate

In the previous section we showed domain walls either residing at a modulation or remaining nearby, depending on whether their circulation matches or opposes that of the modulation. This raises the question of how a DW behaves when it is forced to propagate through a modulation, especially when its circulation is initially opposite to that of the defect.

To address this intrinsic experimental limitation in the particular case of domain wall transformation, we have performed a theoretical topological analysis at the end of the next section.

III
Discussion: micromagnetic simulations

The experiments reported in the previous section demonstrate that the interaction of domain walls with chemical modulations affects both their position along the nanowire and the sign of their circulation. To clarify the mechanisms underlying these observations, we now turn to micromagnetic simulations. In particular, we evaluate the mutual interaction energy of a Bloch-point domain wall with a chemical modulation as a function of wire diameter, modulation length, and relative circulation. For this purpose, we simulate the motion of a DW under an applied magnetic field, gradually increasing the field strength in plateaus of
2\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{s}
duration and rising magnitude.

III.1
Energy landscape of a DW at a modulation

Given the one-dimensional character of cylindrical nanowires, we reduce the domain wall energy landscape to a 1D profile, as in the classical Becker-Kondorski framework
[
33
]
. In micromagnetic simulations, one common method to evaluate the energy landscape of a domain wall is to let it drift slowly in the absence of external forces, under high damping conditions. This ensures that the system remains in a quasi-static regime throughout the process. Such an approach was successfully applied to diameter-modulated nanowires in previous work
[
45
]
. However, spontaneous drift may be insufficient to map energy landscapes with non-monotonic profiles, especially for repulsive parts. In such cases, we apply an axial magnetic field to push the DW in a controlled manner while maintaining its internal structure nearly unchanged. In practice, to maintain quasi-static conditions and minimize dynamic distortions, the damping constant is set to
\alpha=1
.

At a long distance from the modulation, typically
250\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}
or more, both circulation configurations yield overlapping energy profiles, revealing a repulsive interaction. Indeed, at long range, the interaction is dominated by magnetostatics and is insensitive to the relative circulation between the BPW and the modulation. This is consistent with the known fact that circulations of curling structures do not interact with one another via dipolar interactions
[
57
]
, as the magnetic potential does not depend on the sign of circulation. The repulsion results from the interaction of the positively-charged head-to-head BPW with the dipolar field of the modulation, whose positively-charged interface is the one closest to the wall
[
3
]
. A similar repulsion would occur for a tail-to-tail BPW encountering a negatively-charged modulation interface.

At shorter separations, however, the interaction depends on the relative circulations of wall and modulation: it becomes increasingly repulsive for opposite circulations, while it becomes attractive for identical circulations. This results from the direct exchange interaction between the two magnetization textures: for matching circulations, the longitudinal magnetization gradients decrease, minimizing exchange energy and producing a local potential well (blue curve). Conversely, opposite circulations increase longitudinal magnetization gradients, raising exchange energy and resulting in an energy barrier (black curve). This behavior is consistent with previous reports on the interaction of curling end structures in short nanotubes
[
57
]
, and matches the experimental observations presented earlier, where DWs with the same circulation as the modulation were found at the center of the defect [Fig.
1
(c)], while those with opposite circulation stopped before crossing it [Fig.
1
(f,g)].

III.2.2
The case of opposite circulations – Modulations as a polarizing gate

In sharp contrast to the parallel-circulation scenario, the internal energy landscape of the antiparallel configuration
displays a discontinuity and a pronounced barrier, as shown in Figure
5
c. Simulations were carried out for modulation lengths of
60\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}
and
100\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}
, with the field amplitude increased stepwise in
2\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{s}
plateaus (red symbols).

In this case, the DW cannot propagate smoothly through the modulation. Instead, it undergoes a sharp jump in internal energy. It requires substantially higher magnetic fields to overcome the barrier: approximately
11\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{T}
for the shorter modulation and up to
11.5\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{T}
for the longer one.

Micromagnetic snapshots in Figure
5

5
c). At point (b), the associated Bloch point vanishes, resulting in a transient TVW configuration (black symbols). At point (c), a Bloch point reappears to the right of the modulation, where the BPW and the modulation now share the same configuration, confirming that the wall has reversed its circulation. The resulting BPW with negative circulation (green symbols in Figure
5
c) subsequently depins and drifts into the potential well defined by parallel circulations.

These results demonstrate that transmission across an antiparallel modulation does not proceed by simple drift but instead involves a topological transformation sequence (BPW
\rightarrow
TVW

This provides new opportunities for deterministic control of domain wall topology. One may wonder why it is the chemical modulation, a micromagnetic object only
20\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}

This robustly stabilizes the circulation within the modulation, closing the magnetic flux and minimizing magnetostatic energy, so that the BPW adapts to the modulation rather than the opposite.

To assess the viability of this gating mechanism against ambient fluctuations, we examine the effective energy barriers (
{\Delta E}\sim{10}^{-18}

. Providing that, in the memory industry, a ratio of 40 to 60 is considered sufficient to prevent switching for several years, the Néel–Arrhenius analysis indicates that the system is highly resilient against thermally activated switching. Furthermore, the experimental operational window (approx. 10–30 mT) ensures that typical environmental field noise (
\ll 1
mT) cannot trigger unintended transformations, confirming the structural robustness of the proposed topological gate.

IV
Conclusion

In this work we investigated the interaction between Bloch-point domain walls and longitudinal chemical modulations in cylindrical magnetic nanowires, both characterized by a circulation of magnetization around the wire axis. Using a combination of magnetic imaging and micromagnetic simulations, we showed that the interaction depends critically on their relative position and circulation sense, giving rise to a non-monotonic energy landscape. At long range, beyond the domain-wall width, the interaction is purely magnetostatic—always repulsive and independent of circulation. At shorter distances, however, exchange dominates: identical circulations produce an attractive potential well that pins the DW at the modulation, while opposite circulations generate strong repulsion.

When driven across a modulation by an external longitudinal field, the DW response depends on circulation matching. For identical circulations the wall depins smoothly, whereas for opposite circulations and sufficiently narrow modulations its circulation reverses. This reversal involves the annihilation and subsequent renucleation of the Bloch point, analogous to transformations previously reported in homogeneous nanowires. Overall, our results highlight the key role of relative circulation in the interaction of 3D magnetization textures, and establish high-magnetization chemical modulations as effective circulation gates for Bloch-point domain walls.

Finally, this study focuses solely on the fundamental physics of isolated nanostructures to uncover the intrinsic mechanisms of domain wall pinning without interwire interactions. While template-assisted electrodeposition enables low-cost synthesis of cylindrical nanowires with tailored chemical modulations, full device-level integration remains a major technological hurdle. Developing scalable integration protocols and demonstrating multi-bit functionality are beyond the scope of this study, aimed to establish domain wall polarity gating as a foundational building block for future three-dimensional spintronic architectures.

II
Micromagnetic simulation: scaling study

To assess the robustness of the circulation reversal of a Bloch-point wall (BPW) upon propagation through a chemical modulation of antiparallel circulation, we performed a systematic scaling study using
MuMax3
, considering nanowire diameters ranging from 80 nm to 150 nm and modulation lengths of 50 nm and 100 nm. The initial configuration consists of a BPW with a circulation antiparallel to that of the chemical modulation. An axial magnetic field is then applied in a stepwise manner using 2 ns-long field plateaus of increasing magnitude. In all simulated cases, the same sequence of events is observed: the domain wall approaches the modulation while the internal energy increases due to the repulsive interaction associated with the antiparallel circulation. The system eventually reaches a maximum energy configuration (the effective energy barrier), after which the BPW undergoes a circulation reversal and subsequently depins from the modulation once a critical field threshold is reached. These results demonstrate that, while the quantitative thresholds scale with the specific geometry, the qualitative behavior — namely circulation-selective pinning and deterministic switching — remains fully robust across the entire explored parameter range.
