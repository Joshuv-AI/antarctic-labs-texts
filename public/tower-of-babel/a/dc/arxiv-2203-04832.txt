On proving consistency of equational theories in Bounded Arithmetic
Arnold Beckmann and Yoriyuki Yamagata

Abstract
. We consider pure equational theories that allow substitution but disallow induction, which we denote as 𝐏𝐄𝐓𝐒 \mathbf{PETS} , based on recursive definition of their function symbols. We show that the Bounded Arithmetic theory S 2 1 \mathrm{S}^{1}_{2} proves the consistency of 𝐏𝐄𝐓𝐒 \mathbf{PETS} . Our approach employs models for 𝐏𝐄𝐓𝐒 \mathbf{PETS} based on approximate values resembling notions from domain theory in Bounded Arithmetic, which may be of independent interest.

1.
Introduction

The question whether the hierarchy of Bounded Arithmetic theories
is strict or not, is an important open problem
due to its connections to corresponding questions
about levels of the Polynomial Time Hierarchy
[
3
]
.
One obvious route to address this problem is to make use of
Gödel’s 2nd Incompleteness Theorem,
using statements expressing consistency for Bounded Arithmetic theories.
Early lines of research aimed to restrict the formulation
of consistency suitably to achieve this aim
[
3
,
8
]
.

One particular programme is to consider consistency statements
based on equational theories.
Buss and Ignjatović
[
2
]
have shown
that the consistency of an induction free version
of Cook’s equational theory PV
[
5
]
is not provable in
\mathrm{S}^{1}_{2}
,
where
\mathrm{S}^{1}_{2}
is the Bounded Arithmetic theory related to polynomial time reasoning.
Their version of induction free PV is formulated in a system that allows,
in addition to equations, also inequalities between terms, and Boolean formulas.
Furthermore, a number of properties have been stated as axioms.

On the other hand, in a pure equational setting, where lines in derivations
are equations between terms, axioms are restricted
to recursive definition of function symbols, and induction is not allowed,
consistency becomes provable in Bounded Arithmetic:
The first author has shown in
[
1
]

that the consistency of pure equational theories,
in which substitution is not allowed,
is provable in
\mathrm{S}^{1}_{2}
– in particular this result applies to
Cook’s PV
[
5
]
without substitution and induction.
The second author of this paper has improved on this result
in
[
9
]
by showing that the consistency of PV
without induction but with substitution is provable in
\mathrm{S}^{2}_{2}
,
the second level of the hierarchy of Bounded Arithmetic theories.

In this paper, we extend both our previous results
[
1
,
9
]
.
With
\mathbf{PETS}(\mathrm{Ax})
we denote the pure equational theory
with substitution but without induction,
based on some nice set of axioms
\mathrm{Ax}
–
Cook’s original PV
[
5
]
without induction but with substitution is one example of such a theory.
The main aim of this paper is to show that the consistency of
\mathbf{PETS}(\mathrm{Ax})

is provable in
\mathrm{S}^{1}_{2}
, thus improving on both
[
1
,
9
]
.
To this end we employ a novel method of defining models in Bounded Arithmetic based
on approximate values, which may be of independent interest.
Our approach resembles elements from domain theory, however we leave a full
treatment of domain theory in Bounded Arithmetic to future research.

In the next section, we briefly introduce Bounded Arithmetic
and fix some notions used throughout the paper.
In Section
3
we define pure equational theories
\mathbf{PETS}(\mathrm{Ax})
,
which will be more general than PV without induction
in that arbitrary recursive functions may be considered.
This is followed in Section
4
by an introduction
of approximate values and semantics based on approximation,
leading to feasible evaluations of terms based on such approximation semantics.
Section
5
then defines models for equational theories
based on approximation semantics, including a suitably restricted version
which can be expressed as a bounded formula and used in induction arguments
inside Bounded Arithmetic.
A key notion will be a way of updating such models with further information
about approximate values of functions, in a way that preserves the notion
of being a model, provably in
\mathrm{S}^{1}_{2}
.
In Section
6
we prove our first main theorem
showing a form of soundness for
\mathbf{PETS}(\mathrm{Ax})
based on
approximation semantics in
\mathrm{S}^{2}_{2}
– an immediate consequence will be
that
\mathrm{S}^{2}_{2}
proves the consistency of
\mathbf{PETS}(\mathrm{Ax})
.
The final sections improve on this approach to obtain proofs in
\mathrm{S}^{1}_{2}
:
In Section
7
we introduce instructions
that allow to encode sequences of terms and operations on them,
which are extracted from derivations in
\mathbf{PETS}(\mathrm{Ax})
.
In this way we obtain an explicit way of describing model constructions
related to
\mathbf{PETS}(\mathrm{Ax})
derivations, which allows us to reduce
the bounded quantifier complexity of induction assertion in the proof
of our second main theorem in Section
8

to show an improved soundness property for
\mathbf{PETS}(\mathrm{Ax})
provable in
\mathrm{S}^{1}_{2}
.

2.
Bounded Arithmetic

2.1.
Language of Bounded Arithmetic

We give a brief introduction to Bounded Arithmetic to support the
developments in this paper.
For more in depth discussions and results we refer the interested
reader to the literature
[
3
,
7
]
.
Theories of Bounded Arithmetic are first order theories of
arithmetic similar to Peano Arithmetic, their domain of discourse are
the non-negative integers.
For the purpose of this paper we can assume that the language of
Bounded Arithmetic
contains a symbol for each polynomial time computable function,
including

0
,
1
,
+
,
\cdot
,
|.|
,
\mathbin{\#}
,
representing zero, one, addition, multiplication,
the binary length function
|x|
that computes the number of bits in a
binary representation of
x
and can be defined by

|x|=\lceil\log_{2}(x+1)\rceil
,
and smash
\mathbin{\#}
computing
x\mathbin{\#}y=2^{|x|\cdot|y|}
.

2.2.
Theories of Bounded Arithmetic

Theories of Bounded Arithmetic contain suitable defining axioms
for its function symbols.
The main differentiator are various forms of induction for
various classes of bounded formulas, which we will define next.

Bounded quantifiers
are defined as follows:

\displaystyle(\forall x\leq t)A

\displaystyle\quad\text{abbreviates}\quad(\forall x)(x\leq t\rightarrow A)

\displaystyle(\exists x\leq t)A

\displaystyle\quad\text{abbreviates}\quad(\exists x)(x\leq t\wedge A)

If the bounding term
t
of a bounded quantifier is of the form

|t^{\prime}|
, then the quantifier is called
sharply bounded
.

Classes of bounded formulas
\Sigma^{\mathrm{b}}_{i}
and
\Pi^{\mathrm{b}}_{i}
are defined in
[
3
]

by essentially counting alternations between existential and universal bounded quantifiers.
Predicates defined by
\Sigma^{\mathrm{b}}_{i}
and
\Pi^{\mathrm{b}}_{i}
formulas define
computational problems in corresponding classes of the Polynomial
Time Hierarchy of decision problems
\Sigma^{\mathrm{p}}_{i}
and
\Pi^{\mathrm{p}}_{i}
, respectively.
For example, those defined by
\Sigma^{\mathrm{b}}_{1}
correspond exactly to NP.

In particular, the
\Sigma^{\mathrm{b}}_{i}
and
\Pi^{\mathrm{b}}_{i}
classes include the following formulas:

•

\Sigma^{\mathrm{b}}_{0}=\Pi^{\mathrm{b}}_{0}
is the class of formulas build from atomic formulas and
closed under Boolean connectives and sharply bounded quantification.

•

\Sigma^{\mathrm{b}}_{i+1}
includes all formulas of the form

(\exists x\leq t)A
with
A\in\Pi^{\mathrm{b}}_{i}
.

•

\Pi^{\mathrm{b}}_{i+1}
includes all formulas of the form

(\forall x\leq t)A
with
A\in\Sigma^{\mathrm{b}}_{i}
.

The theories
\mathrm{S}^{i}_{2}
,
i\geq 0
, of Bounded Arithmetic have been defined
in
[
3
]
,
establishing a close relationship between fragments of Bounded Arithmetic
and levels of the Polynomial Time Hierarchy of functions.
More precisely, the
\Sigma^{\mathrm{b}}_{i+1}
-definable functions of

\mathrm{S}^{i+1}_{2}
, that is the functions whose graph can be described by
a
\Sigma^{\mathrm{b}}_{i+1}
formula, and whose totality is provable in

\mathrm{S}^{i+1}_{2}
, form exactly the
i{+}1
-st level of
the Polynomial Time Hierarchy of functions,

\mathrm{FP}^{\Sigma^{\mathrm{p}}_{i}}
.

Instead of defining the theory
\mathrm{S}^{i}_{2}
, we state some characteristic properties about induction provable in them.
Let
\Sigma^{\mathrm{b}}_{i}\mathrm{-IND}
be the schema of induction for
\Sigma^{\mathrm{b}}_{i}
-properties,
consisting of formulas of the form

A(0)\wedge(\forall x)(A(x)\rightarrow A(x+1))\rightarrow(\forall x)A(x)

for
A\in\Sigma^{\mathrm{b}}_{i}
.
The schema of
logarithmic induction

\Sigma^{\mathrm{b}}_{i}\mathrm{-LIND}
is then obtained by restricting the conclusion of induction to logarithmic values, that is

A(0)\wedge(\forall x)(A(x)\rightarrow A(x+1))\rightarrow(\forall x)A(|x|)

for
A\in\Sigma^{\mathrm{b}}_{i}
.
We have the following:

Theorem 1

(
[
3
]
)
.

The instances of
\Sigma^{\mathrm{b}}_{i}\mathrm{-LIND}
and
\Pi^{\mathrm{b}}_{i}\mathrm{-LIND}
are provable in
\mathrm{S}^{i}_{2}
.

We already mentioned the intricate relationship between Bounded Arithmetic theories and
the Polynomial Time Hierarchy in terms of definable functions.
Furthermore, it is know that a collapse of the hierarchy of Bounded Arithmetic theories is equivalent to a collapse of the Polynomial Time Hierarchy, provable in Bounded Arithmetic
[
6
,
4
,
10
]
;
With
\mathrm{T}^{i}_{2}
denoting the theory
\Sigma^{\mathrm{b}}_{i}\mathrm{-IND}
we have that

\mathrm{T}^{i}_{2}=\mathrm{S}^{i+1}_{2}
is equivalent to
\Sigma^{\mathrm{p}}_{i+1}\subseteq\Pi^{\mathrm{p}}_{i+1}/\mathit{poly}
provable in
\mathrm{T}^{i}_{2}
.

The Bounded Arithmetic theory
\mathrm{S}^{1}_{2}
is able to formalize meta-mathematics and essential constructions to prove Gödel’s Incompleteness Theorems
[
3
]
.
A basis for such formalization is a feasible encoding of sequences of numbers.
For this paper we assume that a suitable encoding of sequences and operations on them can be formalized in
\mathrm{S}^{1}_{2}
as done in
[
3
]
.
We assume the following notation:

•

With
\langle{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}}\rangle
we denote the encoding of sequence

x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
.
We will use
\sigma
,
\tau
etc to range over sequence encodings.

•

With ‘
\mathbin{\,{:}{:}\,}
’ we denote concatenation of sequences:

\langle{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}}\rangle\mathbin{\,{:}{:}\,}\langle{x_{k+1},\dots,x_{k+\ell}}\rangle\quad=\quad\langle{x_{1},\dots,x_{k},x_{k+1},\dots,x_{k+\ell}}\rangle

•

With ‘
\mathbin{\,{:}\,}
’ we denote the function that adds an element to the left or
right of a sequence:

\displaystyle x\mathbin{\,{:}\,}\sigma

\displaystyle\quad=\quad\langle{x}\rangle\mathbin{\,{:}{:}\,}\sigma

\displaystyle\sigma\mathbin{\,{:}\,}x

\displaystyle\quad=\quad\sigma\mathbin{\,{:}{:}\,}\langle{x}\rangle

The predicate ‘being a sequence encoding’, as well as the operations ‘
\mathbin{\,{:}\,}
’ and ‘
\mathbin{\,{:}{:}\,}
’,
can be defined in
\mathrm{S}^{1}_{2}
with their usual properties proven.

In the following we will concentrate on bounding the number of symbols in transformations and constructions.
For an object
o
we will define its length
\lh(o)
to be the number of symbols occurring in
o
.
As all our constructions will happen in the context of a given derivation
\mathcal{D}
, we obtain that
the size of the Gödelization of object
o
can then be bounded by
\lh(o)
times the size of the Gödelization of
\mathcal{D}
.

Furthermore, the constructions for defining
o
in the context of
\mathcal{D}
will always be explicit and simple enough to be formalizable in
\mathrm{S}^{1}_{2}
, similar to constructions in
[
3
]
dealing with meta-mathematical notions.
In those cases where more involved induction is needed (as in Theorems
40
and
51
) these will be analyzed carefully.

3.
Equational Theories

3.1.
Domain of discourse

The intended domain of discourse
\mathbb{B}
will be binary strings representing numbers.

\mathbb{B}
can be defined inductively as follows:

v::=\epsilon\mid v0\mid v1

We will also use terms denoting binary strings, which are formed from
constant
\epsilon
using unary function symbols
\suc_{0}
and
\suc_{1}

to add a single digit to the right of a string.
We also use
t0
to denote
\suc_{0}(t)
, and
t1
for
\suc_{1}(t)

for terms
t
.

Remark
.

Our results are not restricted to binary strings, but can be applied to general free algebras as domains of discourse as done in
[
1
]
.
However, for sake of simplicity we will only consider binary strings in this paper.

Terms

We will consider axioms consisting of equations that satisfy

Let

\displaystyle=t

\displaystyle f(\epsilon,\overline{{x}})

\displaystyle=t_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle f(x0,\overline{{x}})

\displaystyle=t_{0}

\displaystyle f(x1,\overline{{x}})

\displaystyle=t_{1}.

.

The definition implies that for any

Remark
.

Consider a term

A

Definition 10

(Pure Equational Theories)

The

Proposition 11

For

Definition 12

(Formal Consistency)

With

4.
Approximation Semantics

Infeasible values, although finite, can be considered as infinite bit
strings within theories of feasibility like Bounded Arithmetic.
This is relevant when evaluating functions formally in Bounded
Arithmetic.
Thus we will make use of notions from domain theory to define a method
for evaluating terms occurring in equational proofs.

4.1.
Approximate values

The notion of approximate values
v
is defined
in
[
9
]
,
which adds ‘unknown value’ of a term
[
1
]
, denoted with
‘
*
’, to bit-strings.

Definition 13

(Approximate values)
.

The set
\mathbb{D}
of
approximate values
is defined
inductively as follows:

v::=\epsilon\mid v0\mid v1\mid*

The
gauge

\G(v)
of

is defined recursively:

\displaystyle\G(\epsilon)=\G(*)=1

\displaystyle\G(v0)=\G(v1)=\G(v)+1

For a tuple
\overline{{w}}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})\in\mathbb{D}^{n}

is given as

\G(\overline{{w}})=\max\{\G(w_{1}),\ldots,\G(w_{n})\}

and its

as
\E(\overline{{w}})=n
.

4.2.
Approximation relation

A relation
\unlhd
has been defined
in
[
1
]
.
Here we will consider the converse
\sqsubseteq

Definition 14

(Approximation relation)
.

The

\sqsubseteq
is a binary relation
over
\mathbb{D}
, defined inductively as follows:

•

*\sqsubseteq v
for any
v\in\mathbb{D}
.

•

\epsilon\sqsubseteq\epsilon
.

•

if
v_{1}\sqsubseteq v_{2}
, then

v_{1}0\sqsubseteq v_{2}0
and
v_{1}1\sqsubseteq v_{2}1
.

We pronounce ‘

approximates

We extend
\sqsubseteq

(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\sqsubseteq(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})

v_{i}\sqsubseteq w_{i}
for each

.

Proposition 15
.

\sqsubseteq
is a partial order on
\mathbb{D}^{n}
, that is, it is reflexive,
anti-symmetric and transitive.
∎

Definition 16

(Compatible)
.

u,v\in\mathbb{D}

or
v\sqsubseteq u
.

(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})
and
(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})
in

compatible if each
u_{i}
and

are.
We denote
\overline{{u}}
and

\overline{{u}}\mathbin{\triangle}\overline{{v}}

The following lemma has already been proven in
[
1
]
:

Lemma 17
.

If
\overline{{u}},\overline{{v}},\overline{{w}}\in\mathbb{D}^{n}
and
\overline{{u}},\overline{{v}}\sqsubseteq\overline{{w}}
,
then
\overline{{u}}\mathbin{\triangle}\overline{{v}}
.
∎

Lemma 18
.

Any finite subset
S
of
\mathbb{D}
of pairwise compatible elements
has a maximal element w.r.t.
\sqsubseteq
which we denote with

\maxapprx(S)
,
where
\maxapprx(\emptyset)=*
.
∎

Definition 19

(Generator)
.

A
generator
for
\mathbb{D}^{n}\to\mathbb{D}
is a mapping
\overline{{u}}\mapsto v

with
\overline{{u}}\in\mathbb{D}^{n}
and

.

Definition 20

(Consistent set)
.

A
consistent set

\tilde{{f}}
of
\mathbb{D}^{n}\to\mathbb{D}
is a
finite set of generators satisfying the following condition:

if
\overline{{x}}\mapsto y,\overline{{u}}\mapsto v\in\tilde{{f}}
and
\overline{{x}}\mathbin{\triangle}\overline{{u}}
,
then
y\mathbin{\triangle}v
.

Definition 21

(Finitely generated maps)
.

A consistent set
\tilde{{f}}
defines a mapping, which we call a
finitely generated map
or just
map
, via

\tilde{{f}}(\overline{{x}})=\maxapprx\{v\mid\exists\overline{{w}},\overline{{w}}\sqsubseteq\overline{{x}}\text{ and }\overline{{w}}\mapsto v\in\tilde{{f}}\}.

We sometimes write
\tilde{{f}}[\overline{{x}}]
to denote the set

\{v\mid\exists\overline{{w}},\overline{{w}}\sqsubseteq\overline{{x}}\text{ and }\overline{{w}}\mapsto v\in\tilde{{f}}\}

so that
\tilde{{f}}(\overline{{x}})=\maxapprx\tilde{{f}}[\overline{{x}}]
.

To see that maps are well-defined, consider two generators

\overline{{w}}\mapsto v
and
\overline{{w}}^{\prime}\mapsto v^{\prime}
in
\tilde{{f}}

with
\overline{{w}},\overline{{w}}^{\prime}\sqsubseteq\overline{{x}}
.
With Lemma
17
we obtain
\overline{{w}}\mathbin{\triangle}\overline{{w}}^{\prime}
.
Hence
v\mathbin{\triangle}v^{\prime}
as
\tilde{{f}}
is consistent.
Thus, using Lemma
18
, the set

\tilde{{f}}[\overline{{x}}]=\{v\mid\exists\overline{{w}},\overline{{w}}\sqsubseteq\overline{{x}}\text{ and }\overline{{w}}\mapsto v\in\tilde{{f}}\}

has a maximal element w.r.t.
\sqsubseteq
.

Lemma 22

(Expansion property of maps)
.

Let
\tilde{{f}}_{1}
and
\tilde{{f}}_{2}
be consistent sets of

\mathbb{D}^{n}\to\mathbb{D}
.
If
\tilde{{f}}_{1}\subseteq\tilde{{f}}_{2}
, then

\tilde{{f}}_{1}(\overline{{v}})\sqsubseteq\tilde{{f}}_{2}(\overline{{v}})

for
\overline{{v}}\in\mathbb{D}^{n}
.

Proof.

From
\tilde{{f}}_{1}\subseteq\tilde{{f}}_{2}
we immediately obtain

\tilde{{f}}_{1}[\overline{{v}}]\subseteq\tilde{{f}}_{2}[\overline{{v}}]
.
Hence the assertion follows.
∎

For
\overline{{x}}\sqsubseteq\overline{{y}}
we have
\tilde{{f}}[\overline{{x}}]\subseteq\tilde{{f}}[\overline{{y}}]
, thus we
obtain that finitely generated maps are monotone.

Lemma 23

(Monotonicity of finitely generated maps)
.

Finitely generated maps are monotone w.r.t.
\sqsubseteq
.
∎

Remark
.

There are monotone maps which cannot be represented by finite consistent sets.
For example, the identity function from
\mathbb{D}
to
\mathbb{D}
is monotone but
cannot be represented by a finite consistent set.

Definition 24

(Measures for consistent sets)
.

We define two measures for consistent sets
\tilde{{f}}
:

•

The gauge
\G(\tilde{{f}})
is given as

\max\{\G(\overline{{v}}),\G(w)\mid\overline{{v}}\mapsto w\in\tilde{{f}}\}\kern 5.0pt.

•

The extent
\E(\tilde{{f}})
is given as

\max\{\E(\overline{{v}})\mid\overline{{v}}\mapsto w\in\tilde{{f}}\}\kern 5.0pt.

Remark
.

Using the above measures, the length of
\tilde{{f}}
,
\lh(\tilde{{f}})
, in some natural serialization of
\tilde{{f}}
, can be bounded by

\lh(\tilde{{f}})=\Oh(\#\tilde{{f}}\cdot\E(\tilde{{{f}}})\cdot\G(\tilde{{{f}}}))
.

Definition 25

(Frame)
.

A
frame

F
is a partial, finite mapping of
function symbols
f\in\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{B}

to consistent sets.
We extend
F
to all
f\in\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{B}

by setting
F(f)=\bot
for
f\notin\mathcal{B}\cup\dom(F)
,
where
\bot
denotes the empty set
\emptyset
.

The set of frames is partially ordered by

F_{1}\sqsubseteq F_{2}\iff\forall f,F_{1}(f)\subseteq F_{2}(f)\kern 5.0pt.

A frame
F
defines an
evaluation

F(f)(v)
for

f\in\mathcal{F}
and
\overline{{v}}\in\mathbb{D}^{\ar(f)}
as follows:

•

If
f\in\mathcal{B}
, let
F(f)(\overline{{v}})=f(\overline{{v}})

•

If
f\notin\mathcal{B}
and
F(f)=\tilde{{f}}
, let

F(f)(\overline{{v}})=\tilde{{f}}(\overline{{v}})
.

Observe that for
f\notin\dom(F)\cup\mathcal{B}
,
we have
F(f)=\bot
,
hence
F(f)(\overline{{v}})=\bot(\overline{{v}})=*
.

Definition 26

(Measures for frames)
.

We use the following measures for frames:

•

The width of
F
is given by

\mathbf{w}(F)=\max\{\#F(f)\mid f\in\dom(F)\}
.

•

The gauge of
F
is given by

\G(F)=\max\{\G(f)\mid f\in\dom(F)\}
.

•

The extent of
F
is given by

\E(F)=\max\{\E(f)\mid f\in\dom(F)\}
.

Remark
.

Using the above measures, the length of
F
,
\lh(F)
, in some natural serialization of
F
, can be bounded by

\lh(F)=\Oh(\#\dom(F)\cdot\mathbf{w}(F)\cdot\E(F)\cdot\G(F))
.

Definition 27

(Assignments)
.

An
assigment

\rho
is a partial, finite mapping from
variables
\mathcal{X}
to approximations
\mathbb{D}
.
We extend assignments outside their domain, by setting

\rho(x)=*
for
x\notin\dom(\rho)
.

We extend the approximation order
\sqsubseteq
to assignments
pointwise:

\text{Let}\quad\rho_{1}\sqsubseteq\rho_{2}\quad\text{iff}\quad\forall x,\rho_{1}(x)\sqsubseteq\rho_{2}(x)\kern 5.0pt.

With
\rho[x\mapsto v]
we denote the assignment that behaves like

\rho
but maps variable
x
to approximation
v
:

\rho[x\mapsto v](y)\quad=\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{l@{\ \colon\ }l}v&\text{if }y=x\\
\rho(y)&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

We apply assignments to generalized variables in the natural way,
e.g.,
\rho(\suc_{i}(x))=\suc_{i}(\rho(x))
.

Definition 28

(Measures for assignments)
.

We use the following measures for assignments:

•

The width of
\rho
is given by

\mathbf{w}(\rho)=\#\dom(\rho)
.

•

The gauge of
\rho
is given by

\G(\rho)=\max\{\G(v)\mid v\in\rng(\rho)\}
.

Remark
.

Using the above measures, the length of
\rho
,
\lh(\rho)
, in some natural serialization of
\rho
, can be bounded by
\lh(\rho)\ =\ \Oh(\mathbf{w}(\rho)\cdot\G(\rho))
.

Definition 29

(Evaluation)
.

Let
\rho
be an assignment,
F
a frame, and
t
a term.
The
evaluation
\llbracket{t}\rrbracket_{F,\rho}
of
t
under
F,\rho

is defined recursively as follows:

\displaystyle\llbracket{x}\rrbracket_{F,\rho}

\displaystyle\ =\ \rho(x)\quad\text{for a variable }x\in\mathcal{X};

\displaystyle\llbracket{f(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})}\rrbracket_{F,\rho}

\displaystyle\ =\ F(f)(\llbracket{t_{1}}\rrbracket_{F,\rho},\dots,\llbracket{t_{n}}\rrbracket_{F,\rho})\kern 5.0pt.

We have the following immediate properties of evaluations.

Lemma 30
.

(1)

\llbracket{t}\rrbracket_{F,\rho}\in\mathbb{D}

(2)

\llbracket{t}\rrbracket_{F,\rho}
is monotone in
F
and
\rho
w.r.t.
\sqsubseteq
.

Proof.

(1) follows immediately from the definition.

We prove (2) by induction on
t
, showing that
for
F\sqsubseteq F^{\prime}
and
\rho\sqsubseteq\rho^{\prime}
,

\llbracket{t}\rrbracket_{F,\rho}\sqsubseteq\llbracket{t}\rrbracket_{F^{\prime},\rho^{\prime}}\kern 5.0pt.

If
t\equiv x
, the assertion holds because

\rho(x)\sqsubseteq\rho^{\prime}(x)
.
If
t\equiv f(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})
, we compute

\llbracket{f(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})}\rrbracket_{F,\rho}\ =\ F(f)(\llbracket{t_{1}}\rrbracket_{F,\rho},\dots,\llbracket{t_{n}}\rrbracket_{F,\rho})\\
\ \sqsubseteq\ F^{\prime}(f)(\llbracket{t_{1}}\rrbracket_{F,\rho},\dots,\llbracket{t_{n}}\rrbracket_{F,\rho})\\
\ \sqsubseteq\ F^{\prime}(f)(\llbracket{t_{1}}\rrbracket_{F^{\prime},\rho^{\prime}},\dots,\llbracket{t_{n}}\rrbracket_{F^{\prime},\rho^{\prime}})\ =\ \llbracket{t}\rrbracket_{F^{\prime},\rho^{\prime}}\kern 5.0pt,

where the first approximation uses the expansion property of maps,
Lemma
22
, and the second the
induction hypothesis and monotonicity of maps,
Lemma
23
.
∎

Lemma 31
.

Let
\rho
be an assignment,
F
a frame, and
t
a term.
Then

\G(\llbracket{t}\rrbracket_{F,\rho})\ \leq\ \max(\G(\rho),\G(F))+\lh(t)\kern 5.0pt.

Proof.

By induction on
t
.
If
t
is a variable
x
, we have

\G(\llbracket{t}\rrbracket_{F,\rho})\ =\ \G(\rho(x))\ \leq\ \G(\rho)\kern 5.0pt.

If
t
is
\epsilon
we compute

\G(\llbracket{t}\rrbracket_{F,\rho})=1=\lh(t)
.
For
t
of the form
\suc_{i}(t_{1})
we have

\G(\llbracket{t}\rrbracket_{F,\rho})\ =\ \G(\suc_{i}(\llbracket{t_{1}}\rrbracket_{F,\rho}))\ =\ \G(\llbracket{t_{1}}\rrbracket_{F,\rho})+1\\
\leq\ \max(\G(\rho),\G(F))+\lh(t_{1})+1\ =\ \max(\G(\rho),\G(F))+\lh(t)\kern 5.0pt.

Finally, assume
t
is of the form
f(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
with

f\notin\mathcal{B}
.
Then we have
\G(\llbracket{t}\rrbracket_{F,\rho})\leq\G(F)
.
∎

Lemma 32

(Substitution Lemma)
.

\llbracket{t(u)}\rrbracket_{F,\rho}=\llbracket{t(x)}\rrbracket_{F,\rho[x\mapsto\llbracket{u}\rrbracket_{F,\rho}]}

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
t
.
∎

5.
Frame Models

In this section we develop frames into models for equational theories based on nice axioms.

Definition 33

(Model)
.

A frame
F
is a
model of
\mathrm{Ax}
iff
for any
t=u
in
\mathrm{Ax}
and any assignment
\rho
,

\llbracket{t}\rrbracket_{F,\rho}\sqsubseteq\llbracket{u}\rrbracket_{F,\rho}
.

Remark
.

In general, the notion of being a model cannot be expressed as a bounded formula, thus will
usually be in
\Pi_{1}
, but not in
\Pi^{\mathrm{b}}_{1}
.

We restrict the notion of being a model to obtain a bounded property.
Let
\kappa
be a positive integer, which is intended to bound the gauge
of approximations occurring in frames and assignments
that need to be considered in the definition of models.
Furthermore, we restrict the definition to axioms to those occurring in a
particular derivation
\mathcal{D}
.

For the remainder of this section,
we assume that
\kappa
and
\mathcal{D}
are fixed.

With
\var(\mathcal{D})
we denote the variables occurring in
\mathcal{D}
.

Definition 34

(
\kappa
-Model)
.

A frame
F
is a
\kappa
-model of
\mathcal{D}
iff

\G(F)\leq\kappa
, and
for any
t=u
in
\mathrm{Ax}
occurring in
\mathcal{D}
and any assignment

\rho
with
\dom(\rho)\subseteq\var(\mathcal{D})
and
\G(\rho)\leq\kappa
,
we have
\llbracket{t}\rrbracket_{F,\rho}\sqsubseteq\llbracket{u}\rrbracket_{F,\rho}
.

Remark
.

The notion of
F
being a
\kappa
-model of
\mathcal{D}
can be written as a
\Pi^{\mathrm{b}}_{1}
formula.

Lemma 35
.

The empty frame is a
\kappa
-model of
\mathcal{D}
.

Proof.

Let
F
be the empty frame.
Consider
t=u
in
\mathrm{Ax}
, and assignment
\rho
.
Then
t
is of the form
f(\overline{{t}})
for some
f
in
\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{B}
.
We have
F(f)=\bot
, hence

\llbracket{f(\overline{{t}})}\rrbracket_{F,\rho}=*\sqsubseteq\llbracket{u}\rrbracket_{F,\rho}
.
∎

We will now define the notion of

that can be used to expand models based on axioms occurring in
\mathcal{D}
.

Definition 36

(Updates)
.

Let
F
be a
\kappa
-model of
\mathcal{D}
.
An

,
\kappa

\mathcal{D}

is any

and generator
\overline{{v}}\mapsto w
,

\G(\overline{{v}},w)\leq\kappa

there exists
t=u
in
\mathrm{Ax}
occurring in
\mathcal{D}

and an assignment
\rho

satisfying that

•

t
is of the form
f(\overline{{t}})
,

•

v_{i}=\rho(t_{i})
for

,

•

and
w=\llbracket{u}\rrbracket_{F,\rho}
.

With
F\;*\;f\colon\overline{{v}}\mapsto w
we denote the frame
F^{\prime}
given by

\displaystyle F^{\prime}(g)

\displaystyle=F(g)\quad\text{if }g\neq f

\displaystyle F^{\prime}(f)

\displaystyle=F(f)\cup\{\overline{{v}}\mapsto w\}

The gauge of

Remark

The length of update

\lh(f\colon\overline{{v}}\mapsto w)\ =\ \Oh(\E(f\colon\overline{{v}}\mapsto w)\cdot\G(f\colon\overline{{v}}\mapsto w))\kern 5.0pt.

Remark
.

The arguments

to

is nice.
Thus

is well-defined.
Furthermore, in each term of

Hence, an update uniquely determines an axiom in

and substitution

Remark

For

We are now in the position to prove a form of soundness of pure equational reasoning in

Lemma 50

Let

\rho

are

By induction on
\mathcal{D}
.
We only consider the case for

, the case of

The only rule which changes

\mathcal{D}

t=u

t[s/x]=u[s/x]

to some
