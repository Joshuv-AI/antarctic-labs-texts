Abstract
. We focus on the second part of Hilbert’s 16th problem and provide an upper bound on the number of limit cycles that a polynomial, differential, planar system may have, depending exclusively on the degree n n of the system. Such a bound turns out to be a polynomial of degree 4 4 in n n . More specifically, if H ⁡ ( n ) H(n) indicates the maximum number of limit cycles among planar, differential, polynomial systems of degree n n , then H ⁡ ( n ) ≤ 5 2 ​ n 4 − 23 2 ​ n 3 + 43 2 ​ n 2 − 37 2 ​ n + 7 ​ if n is even, and \displaystyle H(n)\leq\dfrac{5}{2}n^{4}-\dfrac{23}{2}n^{3}+\dfrac{43}{2}n^{2}-\dfrac{37}{2}n+7\,\,\,\,\mbox{if $n$ is even, and} H ⁡ ( n ) ≤ 5 2 ​ n 4 − 23 2 ​ n 3 + 41 2 ​ n 2 − 33 2 ​ n + 6 ​ if n is odd . \displaystyle H(n)\leq\dfrac{5}{2}n^{4}-\dfrac{23}{2}n^{3}+\dfrac{41}{2}n^{2}-\dfrac{33}{2}n+6\,\,\,\,\mbox{if $n$ is odd}. For quadratic systems, we find H ⁡ ( 2 ) = 4 H(2)=4 . Our proof is entirely variational and utilizes in a fundamental way tools and facts from global analysis to the point that no particular expertise in dynamical systems is necessary or required.

1.
Introduction and main result

This paper deals with planar differential systems of the form

(1.1)

x^{\prime}(t)=P(x(t),y(t)),\quad y^{\prime}(t)=Q(x(t),y(t)).

As soon as the two functions

P(x,y),Q(x,y):\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}

are smooth, there is a unique smooth integral curve of the system passing through every point in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. This is a standard result that is studied in every first course in ODEs. Equilibria, i.e. points
(x_{0},y_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
where both
P
and
Q
simultaneously vanish, play a central role in the overall dynamics of such a system. Periodic solutions too deserve a special place concerning the global dynamics of the system.

Definition 1.1
.

Every periodic solution of (
1.1

We restrict attention in this paper to the case of planar, polynomial, differential systems for which the maximum of the degrees of

Our aim is the complete proof of the following central theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Consider the polynomial differential system (
1.1
) of degree
n>1
. Assume that:

(1)

P
and
Q
have no common non-trivial factor.

(2)

All of the connected components of the algebraic curve

P_{x}+Q_{y}=0,

are homeomorphic to a straight line or to an oval (i.e. such
curve has no singular points), and that there are

M
of such components.

(3)

The polynomial system

(1.2)

\begin{array}[]{r}P(P_{xx}+Q_{yx})+Q(P_{xy}+Q_{yy})=0,\\
P_{x}+Q_{y}=0,\end{array}

only has
N

(i.e.
N

(P,Q)
with the curve

P_{x}+Q_{y}=0
).

Then an upper bound for the number
H(n)
of limit cycles that such a
differential system (
1.1
) may have is

(1.3)

H(n)\leq 1+(n-1)^{2}(M+N).

Though this theorem can be used to find upper bounds for the number of limit cycles of special families of differential systems, or even for individual ones,
upper bound (
1.3
) can serve, together with the classic results of Bezout and Harnack to relate parameters

and
N

, to establish an upper bound for the number of limit cycles of such regular, planar, polynomial, differential system of degree
n
only in terms of
n
.
On the other hand, the important hypotheses assumed in this statement are generic, in the sense that if they do not hold for a particular planar polynomial system, a small perturbation of
P
and
Q
, without changing their degree, permits to have them. A standard genericity argument then may be utilized to extend bound (
1.3
) in Theorem
1.1
to arbitrary systems of degree

[

Theorem 1.2
.

An upper bound for the maximum number

\displaystyle H(n)\leq\dfrac{5}{2}n^{4}-\dfrac{23}{2}n^{3}+\dfrac{43}{2}n^{2}-\dfrac{37}{2}n+7\,\,\,\,\mbox{if $n$ is even, and}

\displaystyle H(n)\leq\dfrac{5}{2}n^{4}-\dfrac{23}{2}n^{3}+\dfrac{41}{2}n^{2}-\dfrac{33}{2}n+6\,\,\,\,\mbox{if $n$ is odd}.

The number

1.1.
Main corollaries of central theorem

It is a well-established fact that in counting limit cycles for planar, differential systems, the two components
P
and
Q
can be assumed not to have non-trivial common factors.
It is also well-known that, under small perturbations in the coefficients
of
P
and
Q
, the components of the algebraic curve
\operatorname{Div}=0
become
homeomorphic either to a straight line or to an oval, and that the
number of contact points, understood as solutions of system (
1.2
), of the vector field
(P,Q)
with the curve

\operatorname{Div}=0
, is finite, and they all are simple. We refer to this case as the generic situation.
Given that the bound for the generic case is uniform on the degree
n
of the system, showing that such an upper bound for the
number of limit cycles of a polynomial differential system of degree

n
extends to a non–generic vector field of the same degree
n
is not a big deal. Such perturbation argument will be described in the final section. As indicated, it enables to extend bounds on generic differential systems of a certain degree to general systems of the same degree. Because of this remark, we will always take for granted those generic assumptions, knowing that they extend without trouble to the general situation.

Our principal, fundamental concern is to prove Theorem
1.1
. If one assumes that it has been shown, then
Theorem
1.2
in the generic case is easy to prove.
This amounts to calculating the value of the two parameters
M
and
N
in this result in terms of the degree
n
of the system.

To do so, we recall two
classical theorems:

Theorem 1.3

(Bezout Theorem,
[
19
]
)
.

Let
R(x,y)
and
S(x,y)
be two polynomials with coefficients in

\mathbb{R}
. If both polynomials do not share a non-trivial common factor,
then the algebraic system of equations

R(x,y)=S(x,y)=0

has at most degree
(R)
degree
(S)
solutions.

Theorem 1.4

(Harnack Theorem,
[
21
]
)
.

The maximum number of connected components of an algebraic curve of
degree
k
is

(a)

1+(k-1)(k-2)/2
if
k
is even,

(b)

(k-1)(k-2)/2
if
k
is odd.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
based on
Theorem
1.1
.

We need to find an upper bound for the
number
N
of the solutions that system (
1.2
) may have when

P
and
Q
are polynomials of at most degree
n
. By Bezout’s
theorem we have that

N\leq 2(n-1)^{2},

because in the generic case we
discard the possibility that the two equations of system
(
1.2
) may have a non–trivial common factor. Note that the degree of the first equation in system (
1.2
) is
2n-2
while that of the second one is
n-1
.

By Theorem
1.4
the number
M
of components of
\operatorname{Div}=0

satisfies

M\leq\frac{1}{2}(n-2)(n-3)+1,

if
n
is even, and

M\leq\frac{1}{2}(n-2)(n-3),

if
n
is odd. Note that
k=n-1
.

The final number in the statement of the theorem is then a direct
consequence of Theorem
1.1
, i.e.

\begin{array}[]{rl}1+(n-1)^{2}(N+M)\leq&1+(n-1)^{2}\left(\dfrac{1}{2}(n-2)(n-3)+1+2(n-1)^{2}\right)\\
=&\dfrac{5}{2}n^{4}-\dfrac{23}{2}n^{3}+\dfrac{43}{2}n^{2}-\dfrac{37}{2}n+7,\end{array}

if
n
is even, while

\begin{array}[]{rl}1+(n-1)^{2}(N+M)\leq&1+(n-1)^{2}\left(\dfrac{1}{2}(n-2)(n-3)+2(n-1)^{2}\right)\\
=&\dfrac{5}{2}n^{4}-\dfrac{23}{2}n^{3}+\dfrac{41}{2}n^{2}-\dfrac{33}{2}n+6,\end{array}

if
n
is odd. This yields the numbers in the statement of Theorem

1.2
in the generic case.

Corollary 1.5
.

If the divergence of a quadratic polynomial differential system
(
1.1
) is constant or zero, then it has no limit cycles.
Otherwise if the straight line
\operatorname{Div}=0
of a quadratic polynomial
differential system (
1.1
) has:

(a)

either one or two contact points, then it cannot have more than
4

limit cycles.

(b)

no contact points, then it has no limit cycles.

At any rate,
H(2)=4
.

Proof.

The set
\operatorname{Div}=0
for a quadratic polynomial differential system is
either empty, a straight line, or the whole plane. If it is empty,
i.e. if
\operatorname{Div}
is a non-zero constant, then the system has no limit
cycles by Bendixon criterium (see for instance Theorem 7.10 of

[
14
]
). If it is the whole plane, the system is Hamiltonian and
so it has no limit cycles. We assume that
\operatorname{Div}=0
is a straight line.
So using (
1.3
), we have

n=2,\quad M=1,\quad N\in\{0,1,2\}.

If
N=2
, contact points are simple, and then

1+(n-1)^{2}(M+N)=1+1+2=4.

If
N=1
, the unique contact point has multiplicity two, and the upper bound is the same.
If
N=0
, then there are no contact points and the limit cycles
cannot intersect the straight line
\operatorname{Div}=0
. Again by Bendixon
criterium, the system has no limit cycles.

Since quadratic systems with four limit cycles are known (
[

]
,
[

]
), we conclude that indeed
H(2)=4

We consider the Liénard polynomial differential systems

where

Proof.

It is well known that a system (
1.4

\operatorname{Div}=f^{\prime}(x)=0

corresponds to
the critical values of the polynomial

is a vertical straight line in the

(
1.2

(y-f(x))f^{\prime\prime}(x)=0,\qquad f^{\prime}(x)=0,

for differential system (

f^{\prime\prime}(x)=f^{\prime}(x)=0

has a solution

1.2.
Driving idea for the proof of Theorem
1.1

Our guiding principle for the proof of Theorem
1.1
, expressed in a single sentence, is:

Limit cycles of the differential system (
1.1
) can be interpreted as zeros of the following functional associated with it in a natural way

(1.5)

E_{0}(x,y)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{2}(P(x,y)y^{\prime}-Q(x,y)x^{\prime})^{2}\,dt.

Use then Morse’s inequalities to count them in terms of its critical paths.

Even if the following parallelism may be blamed as too naive, it may help some readers stay oriented about where we are heading. It is exactly a similar situation in dimension one.
If we are interested in knowing how many real roots a single equation

f(x)=0

may posses, one possibility is to look for global minimizers of the function

g(x)=(1/2)f(x)^{2}.

It is obvious that roots of the first correspond exactly to absolute minimizers of the second, given that
g\geq 0
always. Critical points for
g
are roots of

f(x)f^{\prime}(x)=0;

and those not corresponding to absolute minimizers

f(x)\neq 0,

are zeros of
f^{\prime}(x)
. Morse’s inequalities in this simple context reduce just to classical Rolle’s theorem that yields the upper bound

\#(\hbox{roots of $f$})\leq 1+\#(\hbox{roots of $f^{\prime}$}\setminus\hbox{roots of $f$}).

Going back to our functional (
1.5
), note how every periodic solution of (
1.1
), suitably reparametrized in [0, 1] for normalization, is a zero of
E_{0}
in (
1.5
).

Our contribution consists in an attempt to rigorously show that the simple idea just expressed can be carried out to its fulfillment for the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Our work is organized in four main overall stages.

(1)

Describe and prepare the analytical setting around functional (
1.5
) for the legitimate use of Morse’s inequalities that permit to estimate absolute minimizers of a Morse functional in terms of the number of its critical points other than those absolute minimizers. This requires to work with a suitable perturbation
E_{\epsilon}
of
E_{0}
in addition to determining the functional analytical scenario. As a consequence of Morse’s inequalities, we will be able to bound the number of connected components of sub-level sets
\{E_{\epsilon}\leq a\}
by the number of its critical points with critical value for
E_{0}
uniformly away from zero (with respect to
\epsilon
).

(2)

Identify suitable classes of paths where limit cycles of the original polynomial differential system (
1.1
) can be identified in a one-to-one manner with some of the connected components of sub-level sets
\{E_{\epsilon}\leq a\}
.

(3)

Examine carefully the form of the critical point equation for the relevant functional
E_{\epsilon}
that comes from
E_{0}
by perturbation.

(4)

Count the number of critical paths of
E_{\epsilon}
not associated with absolute minimizers of
E_{0}
, and check that an upper estimate, independent of
\epsilon
, on those is possible. This step is clearly divided in two main parts.

(a)

Determine the possible asymptotic distinct behaviors of such branches of critical paths as
\epsilon\searrow 0
.

(b)

Derive an upper bound on the number of those possible branches converging to each such possible limit behavior.

This is a general, global description of our strategy. It may be important to have it in mind as we proceed to a much more detailed explanation. Since we will be moving at several levels of different generality, we will systematically used text within boxes, like the following one, to constantly establishing the parallelism with the application to Hilbert’s 16th problem:

Hilbert’s 16th problem

In this way, readers will have a constant reference to the application of more abstract or more general principles to our main particular problem.

Our hope is to be able to apply the above simple principle and the corresponding program to our situation. The full path is, however, anything but simple or straightforward.

Strategy and organization

We will be moving at three levels.

(1)

Some of our ideas can be examined in an abstract setting without paying attention to the particular form of spaces or functionals.

(2)

Some others require to restrict attention to the special nature of spaces and functionals, and yet results enjoy a certain degree of generality.

(3)

Still others refer specifically to Hilbert’s problem.

We will therefore be moving from the general to the specific.

2.1.
The abstract setting

Suppose we are working in a given Hilbert space
\mathbb{L}
.
There is a certain important property
(P)
that some elements of
\mathbb{L}
enjoy, and we would like to test our ability to count them. Let
\mathbb{P}\subset\mathbb{L}
be the subset of those elements
\mathbf{x}
of
\mathbb{L}
enjoying property
(P)
.

Hilbert’s 16th problem

\mathbb{L}
is the class of parameterizations of closed (periodic) plane paths or curves with certain differentiability and integrability properties. The subset
\mathbb{P}
would stand for those parameterizations of limit cycles of our differential system (
1.1
).

Problem 2.1
.

Find an upper bound for the cardinality of
\mathbb{P}
in terms of some of its defining features.

Since it does not look feasible to deal directly with
\mathbb{P}
in a quantitative way, we realize that there is a natural functional

E:\mathbb{L}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}

with the remarkable property

(2.1)

\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{P}\Longrightarrow E(\mathbf{x})=0.

Hence, we would like to explore the possibility of solving Problem
2.1
by counting how many zeroes
E
may have in
\mathbb{L}
.

Hilbert’s 16th problem

Functional
E
is given in (
1.5
), namely

E(\mathbf{u})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}(P(x(t),y(t))y^{\prime}(t)-Q(x(t),y(t))x^{\prime}(t))^{2}\,dt,\quad\mathbf{u}=(x,y).

It is elementary to realize that
E(\mathbf{u})=0
for any parametrization
\mathbf{u}
of a limit cycle of differential system (
1.1
) in the interval
[0,1]
.

There is a standard way to deal with and count absolute minimizers of smooth, regular functionals. It is like a big, global Rolle’s theorem in infinite dimension as already indicated earlier.

Theorem 2.2

(Morse inequalities)
.

Let
E:\mathbb{L}\to\mathbb{R}

be a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-functional defined over a Hilbert space
\mathbb{L}
, which is bounded from below, coercive, enjoying the Palais-Smale property, and having a finite number of critical points, all of which are non-degenerate and of a finite index. Put
M_{k}
for the (finite) number of critical points of
E
, for each fixed index

k
. Then

\displaystyle M_{0}\geq 1,\quad M_{1}-M_{0}\geq-1,\quad M_{2}-M_{1}+M_{0}\geq 1,\quad\dots,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}M_{k}=1.

There is a number of fundamental concepts in this statement that need to be examined before its conclusion is utilized. To avoid breaking the thread of our discussion at this stage, let us ignore them for the time being and see how one could use this important result.

We are most interested in counting a selected set of the zeroes of a non-negative functional

E:\mathbb{L}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}

that is assumed to comply with all of the requirements for Theorem
2.2
to be applied. In particular, zeroes of
E
must be isolated.
Those zeroes are part of the class of absolute minimizers of
E
, which in turn is a subclass of the full set of local minimizers. The number of local minimizers is precisely
M_{0}
. Therefore, from (
2.2
), we find

M_{abs}+(M_{0}-M_{abs})=M_{0}=1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{k+1}M_{k}\leq 1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}M_{k},

if
M_{abs}\leq M_{0}
is the number of absolute minimizers. Finally

M_{abs}\leq 1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}M_{k}+(M_{0}-M_{abs}).

The sum on the right-hand side is the number of all critical points of
E

\mathbf{u}(0)=\mathbf{u}(1),\quad\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(0)=\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(1).

The specific path

in
\mathbb{H}

Hilbert’s 16th problem

We will be working with the explicit perturbation

E_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{u})=E_{0}(\mathbf{u})+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\int_{0}^{1}[|\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime}(t)-\mathbf{v}^{\prime\prime}_{0}(t)|^{2}+|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)-\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{0}(t)|^{2}+|\mathbf{u}(t)-\mathbf{v}_{0}(t)|^{2}]\,dt.

In addition to this passage to second-order problems, there are three main issues worth realizing.

(1)

The choice for
\mathbb{O}_{d}
. Recall that

\mathbb{O}_{d}

Hilbert’s 16th problem

Since our main concern is focused on limit cycles of planar, polynomial vector fields, we will consider the set

(2)

A main point in this part of our development is to understand how to set up and manipulate the equation for critical paths of integral functionals as the ones written above. It is something well-known that we are talking about systems of ODEs of order four.

(3)

The fact that the perturbed functionals

2.6.
The concrete setting, and the full program

We have already introduced the final ingredient to be specified in order to have a full description of the situation.
The basic functional

It is pretty clear that if

For the sake of readers not familiar with these facts, we include the following statement which have been mentioned earlier.

Proposition 2.7
.

Paths in

are absolutely continuous. Paths in

\mathcal{C}^{1}
with a derivative

Proof.

Note that

for

is absolutely continuous. The same inequality replacing

and

by
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime}
, respectively, when
\mathbf{u}\in H^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})
, shows the second part.
∎

At this stage, we are ready to specify the program we would like to cover to its fulfillment for the proof of our central result Theorem

.

(1)

Show how to select auxiliary path

(a)

Argue that

(b)

For

Hilbert’s 16th problem

As indicated earlier in several places, our specific family of functionals is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon}{2}\int_{0}^{1}[|\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime}(t)-\mathbf{v}^{\prime\prime}_{0}(t)|^{2}+|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)-\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{0}(t)|^{2}+|\mathbf{u}(t)-\mathbf{v}_{0}(t)|^{2}]\,dt

Note that singularly-perturbed problems for critical points, and not just for minimizers, are not usually treated. 3.
Abstract results

We gather in this section those results that can be shown in an abstract setting without specifying the nature of spaces or functionals.
The fundamental result that is driving us in this section is Theorem
2.4
which we restate below.

There is even a more general statement than Theorem
2.4
that incorporates some fundamental topological invariants like the Euler characteristic, the Betti numbers, etc, and that can be stated in the context of infinite-dimensional manifolds (
[
9
]
,
[
39
]
-
[
41
]
). Since all that we need for our goal in this article is inequality (
3.1
) in Theorem
3.1
below, we will restrict attention to the situation in the statement, and forget about those more general cases.
Our plan is then to define and discuss the concepts involved in Definition
2.1
, which are
mentioned in this statement of Theorem
3.1
in preparation to prove those conditions for a perturbation
E_{\epsilon}
of a given initial functional
E_{0}
.
Our discussion is intended right to the point with a minimal number of elements for a full and rigorous proof. Recall (Definition
2.1
) that a functional

E:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}
defined in a Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}

is called a Morse functional if:

•

it is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-, non-negative, coercive;

•

it enjoys the Palais-Smale property;

•

it has a finite number of critical points over each sub-level set

\{E\leq c\}
for each non-critical value
c
, all of which are
non-degenerate and with a finite index.

3.1.
Morse inequalities

Morse inequalities in the full space
\mathbb{H}
(Theorem
2.2
) is such a classical result that we will take it for granted without proof.
In an infinite-dimensional setting, it can be found in several places, for instance,
Corollary (6.5.10) of
[
4
]
or Theorem 4.3 of Chapter 1 in

[
9
]
. As a matter of fact, our above version of Morse inequalities restricted to an invariant set
\mathbb{I}
is not easy to find as such. Corollary (6.5.11) of
[
4
]
is a particular version of it when
\mathbb{I}
is an invariant ball in
\mathbb{H}
. The proof of this case in that reference
[
4
]
, however, makes it very clear that the result is valid not just for a ball, but for a general invariant subset
\mathbb{I}
which is topologically equivalent to a ball.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
E:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}
be a Morse functional (according to Definition
2.1
) over a Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
. Let
\mathbb{I}
be open, topologically equivalent to a ball,
E
-invariant, and put
M_{k}(\mathbb{I})
for the (finite) number of critical points of
E
in
\mathbb{I}
, for each fixed index

k
. Then

\displaystyle M_{0}(\mathbb{I})\geq 1,\quad M_{1}(\mathbb{I})-M_{0}(\mathbb{I})\geq-1,\quad M_{2}(\mathbb{I})-M_{1}(\mathbb{I})+M_{0}(\mathbb{I})\geq 1,\quad\dots,

(3.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}M_{k}(\mathbb{I})=1.

Proof.

If a set
\mathbb{I}
is invariant for a Morse functional
E
, every concept or fact that depends exclusively on the flow of
-E
can be restricted to
\mathbb{I}
without change.
On the other hand, since
\mathbb{I}
is topologically equivalent to a ball, all of its homology groups coincide with those of the full space
\mathbb{H}
. Since Morse inequalities depend on those topological invariants, they remain valid when restricted to such invariant sets.
∎

When a certain base functional
E_{0}
does not comply with the hypotheses of Theorem
3.1
, there is a general, abstract procedure that permits to perturb it appropriately in such a way that the resulting perturbation
E_{\epsilon}
turns out to be a Morse functional, i.e. all of above requirements in Definition
2.1
and Theorem
3.1
hold for
E_{\epsilon}
. In addition, we would have to be convinced that the topology of sub-level sets
\{E_{0}\leq a\}
for
a
near the absolute minimum of
E_{0}
is not changed when we replace
E_{0}
by
E_{\epsilon}
.

3.2.
Main concepts

The statement of Theorem
3.1
based on Definition
2.1
, involves
the notion of Morse index for a non-degenerate critical point of a
smooth functional defined on a Hilbert space. Unless otherwise explicitly stated, we will take, throughout this section,
E:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}
to be a functional defined in an abstract Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
with elements
\mathbf{u}
.

Definition 3.1
.

(1)

E
is coercive if

E(\mathbf{u})\to\infty\hbox{ as }\|\mathbf{u}\|\to\infty.

(2)

E
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
if for every
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{H}
there is a linear functional

E^{\prime}(\mathbf{u}):\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}

such that

\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{v}\|}\|E(\mathbf{v})-\langle E^{\prime}(\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{v}\rangle-E(\mathbf{u})\|\to 0\hbox{ as }\|\mathbf{v}\|\to 0.

The Riesz representation theorem implies that
E^{\prime}(\mathbf{u})\in\mathbb{H}
.

(3)

An element
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{H}
is a critical point of a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-functional
E
if

E^{\prime}(\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{H}.

A real number

c\in\mathbb{R}
is a critical value of
E
if there is a critical point

\mathbf{u}
,
E^{\prime}(\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{0}
, such that
c=E(\mathbf{u})
.

(4)

E
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
if it is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
, and for each
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{H}
, there is a bilinear, symmetric map

E^{\prime\prime}(\mathbf{u}):\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}

such that

\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{v}\|^{2}}\|E(\mathbf{u})-E^{\prime\prime}(\mathbf{u})(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v})-\langle E^{\prime}(\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{v}\rangle-E(\mathbf{u})\|\to 0\hbox{ as }\|\mathbf{v}\|\to 0.

Again by the Riesz representation theorem, one can interpret the Hessian
E^{\prime\prime}(\mathbf{u})
as a linear map

E^{\prime\prime}(\mathbf{u}):\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H},\quad\langle E^{\prime\prime}(\mathbf{u})\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\rangle=E^{\prime\prime}(\mathbf{u})(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}).

Suppose

E:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}

is a non-negative, coercive,

\mathcal{C}^{2}
-functional defined over a Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
. Let
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{H}

be a critical point of
E
, i.e.
E^{\prime}(\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{0}
.

Definition 3.2
.

A critical point
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{H}
of
E
is called non-degenerate is the self-adjoint operator

E^{\prime\prime}(\mathbf{u}):\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}

is invertible. Otherwise,
\mathbf{u}
is said to be degenerate. When the number of
negative eigenvalues of
E^{\prime\prime}(\mathbf{u})
is finite, such number is called
the
(
Morse
)
index of
\mathbf{u}
.

A main, indispensable condition for Morse theory
to hold is the
Palais-Smale condition
. It enables the passage of typical arguments in finite dimension to an infinite-dimensional setting.
For a
general, smooth
\mathcal{C}^{1}
- functional

E:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R},

this important
compactness property reads:

If for a sequence
\{\mathbf{u}_{j}\}
we have that
E(\mathbf{u}_{j})\leq K
for all

j
and a fixed positive constant
K
, and
E^{\prime}(\mathbf{u}_{j})\to\mathbf{0}
as

j\to\infty
, then a certain subsequence of
\{\mathbf{u}_{j}\}
converges
(strongly) in
\mathbb{H}
.

If
E
is coercive, we can replace the boundedness of
E
along the
sequence
\{\mathbf{u}_{j}\}
by the uniform boundedness of
\{\mathbf{u}_{j}\}
in

\mathbb{H}
.

Though we have already talked about invariant sets of functionals in Theorem
3.1
, for the sake of completeness we include here a more formal, precise definition.
Suppose
E:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-functional, and consider the steepest-descent flow
\mathbf{X}
of
-E
, i.e.

\mathbf{X}^{\prime}(t,\mathbf{u})=-E^{\prime}(\mathbf{X}(t,\mathbf{u}))\hbox{ for }t>0,\quad\mathbf{X}(0,\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{u}.

The regularity assumed on
E
implies that the flow is defined for every positive
t
. For fixed positive
t
, the mapping

\mathbf{X}(t,\cdot):\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}

can be easily shown to be
\mathcal{C}^{1}
. This is standard.

Definition 3.3
.

A connected open subset
\mathbb{S}\subset\mathbb{H}
is said to be
E
-invariant, for a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-functional
E
, if its boundary
\partial\mathbb{S}
does not contain a critical point of
E
, and

\mathbf{X}(t,\mathbb{S})\subset\mathbb{S}

for all positive
t
. The intersection of
E
-invariant sets is also
E
-invariant. When one deals with one functional, we will simply talk about invariant sets.

Note that connected components of sublevel sets

\{E\leq c\}=\{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{H}:E(\mathbf{u})\leq c\}

for a non-critical value
c
are invariant. If they are intersected with an additional invariant set, they remain invariant.

3.3.
Perturbation

Suppose we have two nested Hilbert spaces
\mathbb{H}\subset\mathbb{L}
with associated norms (coming from their respective inner products)
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{H}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{L}}
, respectively. The norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{H}}
is strictly finer (larger) than the restriction of
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{L}}
to
\mathbb{H}
.
We take as a fact that bounded sequences in
\mathbb{H}
are relatively compact in
\mathbb{L}
. The norm
\|\cdot\|
always means
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{H}}
.

Let
E_{0}:\mathbb{L}\to\mathbb{R}
be a certain non-negative,
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-functional. It turns out that
E_{0}
is far from being a Morse functional so that Theorem
3.1
cannot be applied to it. Our intention is to perturb it through an additional term of the form

\tilde{E}_{\epsilon}:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R},\quad\tilde{E}_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{u})=E_{0}(\mathbf{u})+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}

and show that a choice
\mathbf{v}_{0}\in\mathbb{H}
is possible in such a way that the modified functional

E_{\epsilon}:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R},\quad E_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{u})=E_{0}(\mathbf{u})+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}_{0}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}

is a Morse functional for every positive
\epsilon
. As a matter of fact, we have plenty of freedom to choose
\mathbf{v}_{0}
. The success of this process requires the base functional
E_{0}
to comply with the two compactness properties that follow:

(1)

E^{\prime}_{0}:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
(regarded as defined in
\mathbb{H}
) is a (non-linear) compact operator:

\mathbf{u}_{j}\rightharpoonup\mathbf{u}\hbox{ implies }E^{\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{j})\to E^{\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u}).

The sign
\rightharpoonup
indicates weak convergence in
\mathbb{H}
, i.e.

\langle\mathbf{u}_{j},\mathbf{v}\rangle\to\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle

for every fixed
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{H}
.

(2)

For each
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{H}
, the self-adjoint, linear operator

E^{\prime\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u}):\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}

is compact.

More specifically, we want to prove the two following results. Our starting point is
the non-negative,
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-functional

E_{0}:\mathbb{H}\subset\mathbb{L}\to\mathbb{R}

where the injection
\mathbb{H}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{L}
is compact.

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose
E^{\prime}_{0}:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
is a compact operator, and let
\mathbb{V}\subset\mathbb{H}
be an open subset. There is
\mathbf{v}_{0}\in\mathbb{V}

such that the perturbed functional

(3.2)

E_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{u})=E_{0}(\mathbf{u})+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}_{0}\|^{2}

is non-negative, coercive,
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-, complies with the Palais-Smale
condition, and has a finite number (possibly depending on
\epsilon
and

\alpha
) of non-degenerate critical points in every finite sub-level
set of the form
\{E_{\epsilon}<\alpha\}
for arbitrary non-critical value
\alpha
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Assume, in addition to the main assumption in Lemma
3.2
, that for each
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{H}
, the linear, self-adjoint operator

E^{\prime\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u}):\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}

is compact. Let
E_{\epsilon}
be the perturbation in that lemma. Then for each positive
\epsilon
, every critical point of
E_{\epsilon}
is non-degenerate and has a finite Morse index.

We can sum up these two fundamental facts into a single important, general statement.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\mathbb{H}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{L}
be two nested Hilbert spaces with compact injection.
Let

E_{0}:\mathbb{H}\subset\mathbb{L}\to\mathbb{R}

be a non-negative,
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-functional such that the derivative operators

E^{\prime}_{0}:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H},\quad E^{\prime\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u}):\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}

are compact, the second one
for every fixed
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{H}
. Then for every open subset
\mathbb{V}\subset\mathbb{H}
, there is

\mathbf{v}_{0}\in\mathbb{V}

such that the perturbed functional

(3.3)

E_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{u})=E_{0}(\mathbf{u})+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}_{0}\|^{2}

is a Morse functional.

For proving this main result, we need some preliminary abstract definitions
and facts, which we state next for the sake of completeness, most of which can be found in the book of Berger

[
4
]
, among others.

3.4.
More concepts

Suppose that

E:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}

is a smooth
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-functional defined in a
Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
. We shall use the following concepts in addition to the ones already introduced earlier.

(i)

An element
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{H}
is called a
regular
point for a non-linear

\mathcal{C}^{1}
-operator

\mathbb{F}:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}

if the linear operator

\mathbb{F}^{\prime}(\mathbf{u}):\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}

is surjective. Otherwise,
\mathbf{u}
is called a
singular
point for
\mathbb{F}
. When
\mathbb{F}
is the derivative of a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-functional
E:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}
, then a critical point
\mathbf{u}
for
E
is degenerate (respectively, non-degenerate) if it is singular (respectively, regular) for
\mathbb{F}=E^{\prime}
. Note that in this case
\mathbb{F}^{\prime}=E^{\prime\prime}
is a self-adjoint operator, and so it is surjective if and only if it is bijective, see Section 2.7 in
[
7
]
, for instance. The image
\mathbb{F}(\mathbf{u})
of a singular point
\mathbf{u}
is called a singular value of
\mathbb{F}
.

(ii)

A mapping

\mathbb{F}:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}

is a
non-linear Fredholm operator
if its Fréchet derivative

\mathbb{F}^{\prime}(\mathbf{u}):\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}

is a linear Fredholm map for each
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{H}
. The
index
of
\mathbb{F}
is defined to be the difference of the dimensions of the kernel and the cokernel of
\mathbb{F}^{\prime}(\mathbf{u})
. This index is independent of
\mathbf{u}
.

(iii)

The functional
E
is a
Fredholm functional
if

E^{\prime}:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}

is a Fredholm mapping, i.e. if

E^{\prime\prime}(\mathbf{u}):\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}

is a linear Fredholm map for each
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{H}
.

We state several interesting facts (page 100 in
[
4
]
).

Proposition 3.5
.

The following statements hold.

a)

Any diffeomorphism between Banach spaces is a Fredholm map of index zero.

b)

If
\mathbb{F}
is a Fredholm map, and
\mathbb{G}
is a compact operator, then the sum
\mathbb{F}+\mathbb{G}
is also Fredholm with the same index as
\mathbb{F}
.

We recall two additional classic results. The first one is the
Inverse Function Theorem (page 113,
[
4
]
) for Banach spaces.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-mapping defined in a neighborhood of some
point
\mathbf{u}
of a Banach space
\mathbb{X}
, with range in a Banach
space
\mathbb{Y}
. If
\mathbb{F}^{\prime}(\mathbf{u})
is a linear homeomorphism of

\mathbb{X}
onto
\mathbb{Y}
, then
\mathbb{F}
is a local homeomorphism of a
neighborhood
\mathbf{U}(\mathbf{u})
of
\mathbf{u}
to a neighborhood
of
\mathbb{F}(\mathbf{u})
.

The second one is a version of Sard’s theorem for
infinite-dimensional spaces (page 125 of
[
4
]
).

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a
\mathcal{C}^{q}
-Fredholm mapping of a separable Banach space

\mathbb{X}
into a separable Banach space
\mathbb{Y}
. If
q>\max(\hbox{index
}\mathbb{F},0)
, the set of singular values of
\mathbb{F}
are no-where dense (its
closure has empty interior) in
\mathbb{Y}
.

The proof of Theorem
3.4
, through Lemmas
3.2
and
3.3
, will make use of Proposition

3.5
, and Theorems
3.6
and
3.7
, in addition to standard properties of compact operators.

3.5.
Main proofs

We are now in a position to prove Lemma
3.2
. We begin by checking that the perturbation
E_{\epsilon}
, for fixed
\epsilon
, in
(
3.3
) complies with the Palais-Smale property, regardless of how the vector
\mathbf{v}_{0}
is selected : if
\{\mathbf{u}_{j}\}
is a sequence in
\mathbb{H}
such that

E_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{j})\hbox{ is bounded},\quad E^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{j})\to\mathbf{0},

then some subsequence of
\{\mathbf{u}_{j}\}
converges in
\mathbb{H}
.
Note that, since
E_{\epsilon}
is coercive in
\mathbb{H}
, we can replace the boundedness of
E_{\epsilon}
along the
sequence
\{\mathbf{u}_{j}\}
by the uniform boundedness of
\{\mathbf{u}_{j}\}
in

\mathbb{H}
.

Note first that each
E_{\epsilon}
is coercive for fixed
\epsilon
.
On the other hand,

(3.4)

E^{\prime}_{\epsilon}=E_{0}^{\prime}+\epsilon\mathbf{1}-\epsilon\mathbf{v}_{0},

where
\mathbf{1}:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
is the identity operator.

Suppose
\{\mathbf{u}_{j}\}
is uniformly bounded. Since
E^{\prime}_{0}
is assumed to be compact, there is a subsequence
\mathbf{u}_{j}
(not relabelled)
such that

E^{\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{j})\to\overline{\mathbf{u}},\quad\overline{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathbb{H}.

To check the Palais-Smale conditions, if
E^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{j})\to\mathbf{0}
, due to
(
3.4
),
we have

\varepsilon\mathbf{u}_{j}=E^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{j})-E_{0}^{\prime}(\mathbf{u}_{j})+\epsilon\mathbf{v}_{0}\to-\overline{\mathbf{u}}+\epsilon\mathbf{v}_{0}\hbox{ as }j\to\infty.

Hence
\{\mathbf{u}_{j}\}
converges strongly in
\mathbb{H}
. This is exactly the
required property for each
E_{\epsilon}
.

Consider now the functional

\tilde{E}_{\epsilon}:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R},\quad\tilde{E}_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{u})=E_{0}(\mathbf{u})+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\|\mathbf{u}\|^{2}.

Its derivative

\mathbb{F}_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{u})\equiv\tilde{E}^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{u})=\epsilon\mathbf{u}+E^{\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u})

is the sum of a diffeomorphism,
\epsilon\mathbf{1}
, and a compact operator,
E^{\prime}_{0}
. By
Proposition
3.5
, this derivative is a Fredholm operator of
index zero. By Theorem
3.7
, the set of critical values of the
derivative
\mathbb{F}_{\epsilon}
, that is

\mathbb{C}_{\epsilon}\equiv\{\mathbb{F}_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{u})\in\mathbb{H}:\mathbb{F}^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{0}\},

is no-where dense. The union

\mathbb{C}\equiv\cup_{\epsilon>0}\mathbb{C}_{\epsilon}

is thus a meager or first-category subset. Notice here that we can, without loss of generality, restrict attention to some appropriate sequence of values for
\epsilon
and work henceforth with such a sequence. For the sake of simplicity, we will keep using
\epsilon
without indicating explicitly
\{\epsilon_{j}\}
, and hope that this will not create confusion. After all, our main perturbation argument is valid if applied to some sequence of functionals
\{E_{\epsilon}\}
for some sequence of values for
\epsilon
converging to zero.

Since
\mathbb{H}
is a complete metric space, the classical Baire category theorem implies that
\mathbb{C}
has empty interior, and consequently, we can choose an element

\mathbf{v}_{0}\in\mathbb{V}\setminus\mathbb{C},

with the properties claimed in the statement of
the theorem, so that every solution
\mathbf{u}
of the family of equations

\mathbb{F}_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{u})+\mathbf{v}_{0}=\mathbf{0}

cannot be a singular point for none of the
\mathbb{F}^{\prime}_{\epsilon}
s, i.e.

\mathbb{F}^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{u})=\tilde{E}^{\prime\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u})

is bijective, and so
\mathbf{u}
is non-degenerate. This
argument implies indeed that the critical points of
E_{\epsilon}
are
non-degenerate, once
\mathbf{v}_{0}
has been chosen in this way and has
been added to
\tilde{E}_{\epsilon}
, because

E^{\prime\prime}_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{u})=\tilde{E}^{\prime\prime}_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{u}).

The Inverse Function Theorem
3.6
implies directly that non-degenerate
critical points of a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-functional
E_{\epsilon}
are isolated.

Finally, we argue why the number of critical points in sets of the form
\{E_{\epsilon}\leq\alpha\}
is finite.
Indeed, if we
let
\alpha
be a positive real number and assume that there is
an infinite number
\{\mathbf{u}_{j}\}
of critical points with

E_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{j})\leq\alpha,\quad E^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{j})=\mathbf{0},

the Palais-Smale condition for
E_{\epsilon}
would ensure the existence of a suitable
subsequence converging to some
\overline{\mathbf{u}}
which would be a
critical, non-isolated point. This is a contradiction with the
previous statement about the fact that the critical points are isolated, and
so the number of such critical points has to be finite. This completes
the proof of Lemma
3.2

The proof of Lemma
3.3
relies on the standard fact that eigenvalues of a linear,
self-adjoint, compact operator in a Banach space, like
E^{\prime\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u})
,
always has a sequence of (real) eigenvalues converging to zero (see,
for instance, Chapter 6 of
[
7
]
). By differentiating in
(
3.4
),

E^{\prime\prime}_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{u})=E^{\prime\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u})+\epsilon\mathbf{1},

and hence eigenvalues of
E^{\prime\prime}_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{u})
are eigenvalues of

E^{\prime\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u})
plus
\epsilon
. The conclusion is that there cannot be an infinite number of
negative eigenvalues.

Theorem
3.4
is then proved.

3.6.
Perturbation and critical points

Let
E_{0}:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-, convex functional, bounded from below
E_{0}\geq M
for some
M\in\mathbb{R}
, and let
E:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-, strictly convex functional. In most situations of interest, one would take

E(\mathbf{u})=\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{u}\|^{2}\hbox{ or }E(\mathbf{u})=\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}_{0}\|^{2}

for a fixed vector
\mathbf{u}_{0}\in\mathbb{H}
. We will consider the perturbed functional

E_{\epsilon}:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R},\quad E_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{u})=E_{0}(\mathbf{u})+\epsilon E(\mathbf{u}),

which turns out to be strictly convex for every positive
\epsilon
. As such it admits a unique minimizer
\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon}
which is determined as the unique solution of the critical point equation

E^{\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon})+\epsilon E^{\prime}(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon})=\mathbf{0}.

This is standard.

Proposition 3.8
.

In the situation just described,
E^{\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon})\to\mathbf{0}
in
\mathbb{H}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{H}
be an arbitrary vector. Because
\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon}
is a minimizer for
E_{\epsilon}
, we have that

E_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon})\leq E_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon})=E_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon})+\epsilon E(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon})\leq E_{0}(\mathbf{u})+\epsilon E(\mathbf{u}).

If we take limits in
\epsilon
, we conclude that

\liminf_{\epsilon\to 0}E_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon})\leq\limsup_{\epsilon\to 0}E_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon})\leq E_{0}(\mathbf{u}),

and the arbitrariness of
\mathbf{u}
leads to

\inf_{\mathbb{H}}E_{0}\leq\liminf_{\epsilon\to 0}E_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon})\leq\limsup_{\epsilon\to 0}E_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon})\leq\inf_{\mathbb{H}}E_{0}.

Hence
\{\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon}\}
is minimizing for
E_{0}
, and because
E_{o}
is bounded from below,
E^{\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon})\to\mathbf{0}
(even if
\|\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon}\|\to\infty
).
∎

The convexity of the base functional
E_{0}
looks unavoidable in this proof to ensure the uniqueness of minimizers
\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon}
, yet a stronger result is possible with no reference to convexity. Recall that statements concerning
\epsilon\to 0
exactly means that it holds along some sequence of values of
\epsilon
. This suffices for our purposes, as has been indicated earlier in this section.

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
E_{0}:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-functional, and let
\{\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon}\}
be a branch of critical points for the family of functionals

E_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{u})=E_{0}(\mathbf{u})+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}_{0}\|^{2}

for a fixed element
\mathbf{v}_{0}\in\mathbb{H}
, i.e.

(3.5)

E_{0}^{\prime}(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon})+\epsilon(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon}-\mathbf{v}_{0})=\mathbf{0}

for every positive
\epsilon
sufficiently small. Then:

(1)

E^{\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon})\to\mathbf{0}
in
\mathbb{H}
as
\epsilon\to 0
; and

(2)

there is no such branch with

(3.6)

E_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon})\to 0,\quad E_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon})>a,

for a fixed value
a>0
.

Proof.

The first observation is that the branch
\{\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon}\}
is differentiable with respect to
\epsilon
as a consequence of the differentiability of
E_{0}
and the smooth dependence of solutions of critical equations with respect to parameters. Let
\delta>0
be fixed. Then

E_{\delta}(\mathbf{u}_{\delta})-\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}E_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon})=\int_{0}^{\delta}\frac{d}{d\epsilon}[E_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon})]\,d\epsilon.

The differentiation (with respect to
\epsilon
) under the integral sign together with (
3.5
) leads immediately to

E_{\delta}(\mathbf{u}_{\delta})-\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}E_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon})=\int_{0}^{\delta}\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon}-\mathbf{v}_{0}\|^{2}\,d\epsilon.

We will express this identity in the form

(3.7)

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\left[E_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon})+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\|\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon}-\mathbf{v}_{0}\|^{2}\right]+\int_{0}^{\delta}\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon}-\mathbf{v}_{0}\|^{2}\,d\epsilon=E_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{\delta})+\frac{\delta}{2}\|\mathbf{u}_{\delta}-\mathbf{v}_{0}\|^{2}.

From this basic equality we will conclude the two claimed facts.

Since all terms involved in (
3.7
) are non-negative, we see that

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\epsilon}{2}\|\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon}-\mathbf{v}_{0}\|^{2}\leq E_{\delta}(\mathbf{u}_{\delta}).

On the other hand, (
3.5
) implies that both vectors
E^{\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon})
and
\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon}-\mathbf{v}_{0}
are co-linear and, in addition,

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}-\frac{1}{2}E^{\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon})\cdot(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon}-\mathbf{v}_{0})=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\epsilon}{2}\|\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon}-\mathbf{v}_{0}\|^{2}\leq E_{\delta}(\mathbf{u}_{\delta}).

Since the upper bound on the right-hand side is independent of
\epsilon
, if
\|\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon}-\mathbf{v}_{0}\|
converges to infinity, then
E^{\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon})
must converge necessarily to zero. If
\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon}-\mathbf{v}_{0}
is uniformly bounded, then (
3.5
) clearly implies that
E^{\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon})
converges to zero as well. At any rate,
E^{\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon})\to\mathbf{0}
in
\mathbb{H}
.

Concerning our second point, suppose, seeking a contradiction, that we could find a branch of critical paths
\{\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon}\}
, complying with (
3.5
), for which (
3.6
) holds for some fixed
a>0
. Then

0<a\leq\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}E_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon})=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\left[E_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon})+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\|\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon}-\mathbf{v}_{0}\|^{2}\right]=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\epsilon}{2}\|\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon}-\mathbf{v}_{0}\|^{2},

and (
3.7
) leads to

a+\int_{0}^{\delta}\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon}-\mathbf{v}_{0}\|^{2}\,d\epsilon\leq E_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{\delta})+\frac{\delta}{2}\|\mathbf{u}_{\delta}-\mathbf{v}_{0}\|^{2}.

If
\|\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon}-\mathbf{v}_{0}\|^{2}
tends to infinity (as
\epsilon\to 0
), then for some selected sequence of values for
\delta
tending to zero, we should have

\frac{\delta}{2}\|\mathbf{u}_{\delta}-\mathbf{v}_{0}\|^{2}\leq\int_{0}^{\delta}\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon}-\mathbf{v}_{0}\|^{2}\,d\epsilon.

Taking this fact into the previous inequality, we realize that

a+\frac{\delta}{2}\|\mathbf{u}_{\delta}-\mathbf{v}_{0}\|^{2}\leq E_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{\delta})+\frac{\delta}{2}\|\mathbf{u}_{\delta}-\mathbf{v}_{0}\|^{2},

and
a\leq E_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{\delta})
for some sequence of values
\delta
tending to zero. This is a contradiction with (
3.6
). If, on the other hand,
\|\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon}-\mathbf{v}_{0}\|^{2}
is uniformly bounded, then as before

0<a\leq\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}E_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon})=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}E_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon})=0,

which is again a contradiction.
∎

4.
General results

We start specifying the nature of some ingredients of spaces and functionals for Hilbert’s 16th problem. As already indicated in a previous section, our basic Hilbert spaces are

\mathbb{L}=H^{1}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})

of continuous, periodic paths,
\mathbf{u}(0)=\mathbf{u}(1)
, with a weak first derivative
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)
which is square-integrable in the unit interval

\int_{0}^{1}|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)|^{2}\,dt<\infty;

and

\mathbb{H}=H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})

of
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-periodic paths with a weak second derivative
\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime}(t)
which is square-integrable

\int_{0}^{1}|\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime}(t)|^{2}\,dt<\infty,\quad\mathbf{u}(0)=\mathbf{u}(1),\quad\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(0)=\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(1).

There are three main points that require our attention:

(1)

Isolate an appropriate subset
\mathbb{O}
of
H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})
in which the image set of paths in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
are essentially identified with the paths themselves. Basically, we would like to avoid the possibility that such image sets are run more than once either counter- or clockwise.

(2)

Describe the differential systems that critical paths of local, integral functionals of first- and second-order ought to verify.

(3)

Establish the connection between critical paths of integral functionals under fixed end-point conditions and periodic conditions.

4.1.
Some analytical preliminaries

We briefly state here some basic notions about spaces of functions with weak derivatives having suitable integrability properties, as well as recalling again concepts like the coercivity of a functional. It may be convenient to do so for some interested readers not familiar with these concepts.
We refer to
[
7
]
for a main, accesible source in this regard, and much more related information.

The underlying natural Hilbert space for
E_{\epsilon}
is

\displaystyle H^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})=\left\{(x,y):[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}:\right.

\displaystyle\left.\int_{0}^{1}[x^{2}+y^{2}+(x^{\prime})^{2}+(y^{\prime})^{2}+(x^{\prime\prime})^{2}+(y^{\prime\prime})^{2}]\,dt<\infty\right\}.

This is nothing but the classical Sobolev space
of paths with square-integrable weak derivatives up to order two. The
inner product in this space is

\langle(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})\rangle=\int_{0}^{1}(x_{1}x_{2}+y_{1}y_{2}+x^{\prime}_{1}x^{\prime}_{2}+y^{\prime}_{1}y^{\prime}_{2}+x^{\prime\prime}_{1}x^{\prime\prime}_{2}+y^{\prime\prime}_{1}y^{\prime\prime}_{2})\,dt,

and the associated norm

\|(x,y)\|^{2}=\int_{0}^{1}[x^{2}+y^{2}+(x^{\prime})^{2}+(y^{\prime})^{2}+(x^{\prime\prime})^{2}+(y^{\prime\prime})^{2}]\,dt.

Norms and inner products occurring henceforth are meant to be these.
Paths in
H^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})
have continuous first derivatives. Since parameterizations of limit cycles as integral curves of the corresponding polynomial differential system, suitably normalized to the unit interval, are
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
, they belong to this space.

We recall that coercivity for a general functional
E
defined in a Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
means that

E(\mathbf{u})\to+\infty\quad\hbox{ as }\quad\|\mathbf{u}\|\to\infty\hbox{ with }\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{H}.

If a functional
E_{0}
defined in
H^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})
is non-negative, the perturbation

E_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{u})=E_{0}(\mathbf{u})+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}_{0}\|^{2}

automatically becomes coercive for every positive
\epsilon
, and every fixed element
\mathbf{v}_{0}
.

Hilbert’s 16th problem

To summarize in a compact form, and anticipate our analytical framework, we will concentrate on the family of functionals

E_{\epsilon}:H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}^{+}

where

\displaystyle H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})=\{\mathbf{u}\in H^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2}):\mathbf{u}(0)=\mathbf{u}(1),\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(0)=\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(1)\},

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle=\int_{0}^{1}(\mathbf{u}(t)\cdot\mathbf{v}(t)+\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)\cdot\mathbf{v}^{\prime}(t)+\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime}(t)\cdot\mathbf{v}^{\prime\prime}(t))\,dt,

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{u}\|^{2}=\|\mathbf{u}\|^{2}_{H^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})}=\int_{0}^{1}\big(|\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime}(t)|^{2}+|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)|^{2}+|\mathbf{u}(t)|^{2}\big)\,dt,

\displaystyle E_{0}(\mathbf{u})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}(\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{u}^{\prime})^{2}\,dt,

\displaystyle E_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{u})=E_{0}(\mathbf{u})+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\|\mathbf{u}\|^{2}-\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}_{0}\rangle+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\|\mathbf{v}_{0}\|^{2}=E_{0}(\mathbf{u})+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}_{0}\|^{2},

and

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{u}=(x,y),\quad\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{u})=(P(x,y),Q(x,y)),\\
\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})=(-Q(\mathbf{u}),P(\mathbf{u})).\end{array}

Once again, a fundamental fact for us to bear in mind is that paths in
H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})
are
\mathcal{C}^{1}
, and convergence in
H^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})
implies uniform convergence of first derivatives (
[
7
]
). The following is a more precise statement of Proposition
2.7
, that we will use for future reference.

Proposition 4.1
.

Paths in
H^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})
are
\mathcal{C}^{1}
.
If
\mathbf{u}_{j}\to\mathbf{u}
in
H^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})
, then

\mathbf{u}_{j}\to\mathbf{u},\quad\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{j}\to\mathbf{u}^{\prime}

uniformly in
[0,1]
.

4.2.
Ambient set for our analysis

One fundamental ingredient of our analysis focuses on isolating a suitable increasing family of subsets

\mathbb{O}_{d}\subset H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2}),\quad d\in\mathbb{N},

where images of paths cannot admit different reparameterizations covering such images more than once either counter- or clockwise.
To make this idea rigorous, we will rely on important concepts and results in
[
51
]
. Though some of those are rather classical, for the sake of readers we state them here with some care.

Definition 4.1
.

A planar, parametrized regular closed curve is a continuously differentiable mapping

\mathbf{u}(t):[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}

such that

\mathbf{u}(0)=\mathbf{u}(1),\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(0)=\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(1),\quad|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)|>0\hbox{ for all }t\in[0,1].

Note how every parametrized regular curve belongs to
H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})
according to Proposition
4.1
.

For such a regular curve
\mathbf{u}
, we can consider the normalized tangent vector

\mathbf{n}(t)=\frac{1}{|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)|}\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t):[0,1]\to\mathbb{S}.

Definition 4.2
.

(1)

The winding number of a regular curve
\mathbf{u}
is the total number of full turns, taking into account its sense, of the normalized tangent vector
\mathbf{n}
in the unit circle
\mathbb{S}
as
t
runs in the unit interval.

(2)

The absolute winding number is the number of full turns, regardless of whether they are clock- or counterclockwise, that
\mathbf{n}
runs around
\mathbb{S}
as
t
runs through
[0,1]
.

In this way, a regular curve
\mathbf{u}
could have winding number
+1
, and yet its absolute winding number could be larger or even much larger. The point is that the tangent vector
\mathbf{n}(t)
turns fully around as many times clockwise as counter-clockwise plus one.

The main result from
[
51
]
shows that two regular curves can be continuously deformed into each other if and only if they share the winding number.

Definition 4.3
.

We put
\mathbb{O}_{d}
for the subset of
H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})
of regular curves with a nowhere-null tangent vector, winding number
+1
, and absolute winding number not greater than
d
.

Proposition 4.2
.

\mathbb{O}_{d}
is an open subset of
H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

Proof.

Note that convergence in
H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})
implies uniform convergence of tangent vectors (Proposition
4.1
). This is standard.
∎

The following fact may help in better understanding the absolute winding number of paths.

Proposition 4.3
.

Suppose
\mathbf{u}_{j}\in\partial\mathbb{O}_{d_{j}}
with
d_{j}\to\infty
as
j\to\infty
, and that

\mathbf{u}_{j}+\delta\mathbf{U}_{j}\in\bigcup_{r>d_{j}}\mathbb{O}_{r}

for all
\delta>0
and
j
large. Then
\{\mathbf{U}_{j}\}
cannot converge strongly in
H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

Proof.

Our main hypothesis means that
\mathbf{U}_{j}
:

(1)

is capable of pushing
\mathbf{u}_{j}
to run beyond full rounds in smaller and smaller subintervals of
[0,1]
(because
d_{j}\to\infty
); and

(2)

\mathbf{U}_{j}
must take on larger and larger values because the multiple
\delta
can be arbitrarily small.

The combined effect implies that
\{\mathbf{U}_{j}\}
should develop concentration effects, and hence it cannot converge strongly in
H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
∎

4.3.
Critical paths of functionals

The object of this subsection is to derive and study the differential equations
which must satisfy critical closed paths of local, integral functionals (like
E_{\epsilon}
) in

H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})
. The proof uses standard ideas in the Calculus of Variations,
but we include them because their understanding is crucial for our counting procedure.
We first discuss first-order problems as a preliminary step to gain some familiarity with the underlying techniques, and then focus on second-order problems.

4.3.1.
First-order problems

Let

F(t,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{z}):[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}

be a
(\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
-) function
with respect to
(t,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{z})
, with partial derivatives

F_{\mathbf{u}}
,
F_{\mathbf{z}}
. The associated functional is

E(\mathbf{u})=\int_{0}^{1}F(t,\mathbf{u}(t),\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t))\,dt.

It is important for us to understand the role played by end-point conditions when looking for critical paths of
E
. Specifically, we will proceed in three successive steps:

(1)

the most general and standard situation is to look for critical paths under fixed end-point conditions, so that feasible paths
\mathbf{u}(t)
are such that

\mathbf{u}(t)\in H^{1}_{\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{u}_{1}}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})=\{\mathbf{v}\in H^{1}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2}):\mathbf{v}(0)=\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{v}(1)=\mathbf{u}_{1}\},

for
\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{u}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
arbitrary but fixed vectors;

(2)

in the second step we just take
\mathbf{u}_{0}=\mathbf{u}_{1}
, a fixed vector, and admissible paths are

\mathbf{u}(t)\in H^{1}_{\mathbf{u}_{0}}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})=\{\mathbf{v}\in H^{1}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2}):\mathbf{v}(0)=\mathbf{v}(1)=\mathbf{u}_{0}\};

(3)

in the final step we demand
\mathbf{u}(0)=\mathbf{u}(1)
but this vector is free, and feasible paths are

\mathbf{u}(t)\in H^{1}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2}).

Assume that

belongs to
L^{1}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{2})
for every feasible
\mathbf{v}
, according to the situation considered.

Theorem 4.4
.

Suppose that the functional

E(\mathbf{u})=\int_{0}^{1}F(t,\mathbf{u}(t),\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t))\,dt

admits a critical closed path
\mathbf{u}(t):[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
either in:

(1)

H^{1}_{\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{u}_{1}}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})
; or

(2)

H^{1}_{\mathbf{u}_{0}}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})
; or

(3)

H^{1}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})

(0,1)
,

(4.1)

$t$ in }(0,1),

(1)

\mathbf{u}(0)=\mathbf{u}_{0}

\mathbf{u}(1)=\mathbf{u}_{1}

(2)

\mathbf{u}(0)=\mathbf{u}(1)=\mathbf{u}_{0}

(4.2)

Proof.

\mathbf{u}

(4.3)

We consider the special subspace
\mathbb{U}
of variations
\mathbf{U}
defined

where

is the orthogonal complement, in

\{1\}
. Since these

a continuous, bounded function by hypothesis. For

)

(4.4)

\int_{0}^{1}\left[-\Psi(t)\cdot\mathbf{U}^{\prime}(t)+F_{\mathbf{z}}(t,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{U}^{\prime}(t)\right]\,dt=0,

for test fields

\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{U}

Due to the arbitrariness of
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{U}
, according to (

This covers end-point conditions for the two first situations.

For the case of periodic end-point conditions, we take the information in (
4.6
) back to (
4.3
) for a general

not necessarily belonging to

∎

The following simple example is enlightening. Let

\epsilon>0,\quad\mathbf{v}(t):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{2},\quad P(\mathbf{u}):\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R},

be a positive number; a smooth, one-periodic path; and a polynomial in two variables, respectively. We would like to apply the previous main result to the integrand

F(t,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{z})=\frac{\epsilon}{2}|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{v}(t)|^{2}+P(\mathbf{u}).

The corresponding functional is

E(\mathbf{u})=\int_{0}^{1}\left[\frac{\epsilon}{2}|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)-\mathbf{v}(t)|^{2}+P(\mathbf{u}(t))\right]\,dt.

In particular, we would like to clearly see the interplay between the two kinds of boundary conditions

\mathbf{u}(0)=\mathbf{u}(1)=\mathbf{p}

for fixed vector
\mathbf{p}
, and

\mathbf{u}(0)-\mathbf{u}(1)=\mathbf{0}.

Note that the differential system in the open interval
(0,1)
is exactly the same in both cases, according to Theorem

. The crucial difference lies on the end-point condition: in the first case, end-point conditions are imposed directly on feasible paths; in the periodic situation, critical paths must be
\mathcal{C}^{1}
in the full interval
[0,1]
. Notice that this is so because

F_{\mathbf{z}}=\epsilon(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{v}(t))

and
\mathbf{v}
is assumed to be smooth so that
\mathbf{v}(0)=\mathbf{v}(1)
.

4.3.2.
Second-order problems

The treatment of second-order problems is formally the same. We first concentrate on periodic boundary conditions, and then focus on end-point conditions afterwards, as understanding the interplay between both for second-order problems is relevant for us.

Let

F(t,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{z},\mathbf{Z}):[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}

be a
(\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
-) function
with respect to
(t,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{z},\mathbf{Z})
, with partial derivatives

F_{\mathbf{u}}
,
F_{\mathbf{z}}
,
F_{\mathbf{Z}}
. Assume that

F_{\mathbf{u}}(t,\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}^{\prime},\mathbf{v}^{\prime\prime})

and

F_{\mathbf{z}}(t,\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}^{\prime},\mathbf{v}^{\prime\prime})-\int_{0}^{t}F_{\mathbf{u}}(t,\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}^{\prime},\mathbf{v}^{\prime\prime})\,ds

belong to
L^{1}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{2})
for every feasible

\mathbf{v}\in H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2}).

Theorem 4.5
.

Suppose that the functional

E(\mathbf{u})=\int_{0}^{1}F(t,\mathbf{u}(t),\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t),\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime}(t))\,dt

admits a critical closed path

\mathbf{u}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\hbox{ in }H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2}).

Then the function

(4.9)

\frac{d}{dt}F_{\mathbf{Z}}(t,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}^{\prime},\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime})-F_{\mathbf{z}}(t,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}^{\prime},\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime})

is absolutely continuous in
[0,1]
, and

(4.10)

\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{d}{dt}F_{\mathbf{Z}}(t,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}^{\prime},\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime})-F_{\mathbf{z}}(t,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}^{\prime},\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime})\right)+F_{\mathbf{u}}(t,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}^{\prime},\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime})=\mathbf{0}\,\,\,\hbox{for a.e.
$t$ in }(0,1).

Moreover

(4.11)

[F_{\mathbf{Z}}(t,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}^{\prime},\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime})]_{t=0}=\mathbf{0}.

Brackets in (
4.11
) indicate the jump of the field inside at
the time indicated (difference between
t=1
, and
t=0
), that is

\displaystyle[F_{\mathbf{Z}}(t,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}^{\prime},\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime})]_{t=0}=

\displaystyle\left.F_{\mathbf{Z}}(t,\mathbf{u}(t),\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t),\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime}(t))\right|_{t=1^{-}}

\displaystyle-\left.F_{\mathbf{Z}}(t,\mathbf{u}(t),\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t),\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime}(t))\right|_{t=0^{+}}.

Notice that the integrability demanded on those combinations of
partial derivatives of
F
in the statement of Theorem

4.5
is equivalent to having

F_{\mathbf{u}}(t,\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}^{\prime},\mathbf{v}^{\prime\prime})\hbox{ and }F_{\mathbf{z}}(t,\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}^{\prime},\mathbf{v}^{\prime\prime})

integrable for every feasible

\mathbf{v}\in H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2}).

We have however decided to keep the
statement as it is for that is exactly the form in which those
combinations of partial derivatives will occur in the proof. Our proof mimics that of Theorem
4.4
.

Proof of Theorem
4.5
.

Take

\mathbf{U}\in H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2}).

If
\mathbf{u}

E
,

\left.\frac{d}{d\delta}E(\mathbf{u}+\delta\mathbf{U})\right|_{\delta=0}=0,

\left.\frac{d}{d\delta}\right|_{\delta=0}\int_{0}^{1}F(t,\mathbf{u}(t)+\delta\mathbf{U}(t),\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)+\delta\mathbf{U}^{\prime}(t),\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime}(t)+\delta\mathbf{U}^{\prime\prime}(t))\,dt=0.

This derivative has the form

(4.12)

\int_{0}^{1}\left[F_{\mathbf{u}}(t,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}^{\prime},\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime})\cdot\mathbf{U}(t)+F_{\mathbf{z}}(t,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}^{\prime},\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime})\cdot\mathbf{U}^{\prime}(t)+F_{\mathbf{Z}}(t,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}^{\prime},\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime})\cdot\mathbf{U}^{\prime\prime}(t)\right]\,dt=0.

We consider the special subspace
\mathbb{U}
of variations
\mathbf{U}
defined
by

where
\{1,t\}^{\perp}
is the orthogonal complement, in

, of the subspace generated by

\mathbb{U}=\{\mathbf{U}\in H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2}):\mathbf{U}(0)=\mathbf{U}(1)=\mathbf{U}^{\prime}(0)=\mathbf{U}^{\prime}(1)=\mathbf{0}\}.

We also set

\displaystyle\Psi(t)=\int_{0}^{t}F_{\mathbf{u}}(s,\mathbf{u}(s),\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(s),\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime}(s))\,ds,

two continuous, bounded functions by hypothesis. For

, an

)

(0,1)

and even further

not necessarily belonging to

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left[\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{d}{dt}F_{\mathbf{Z}}(t,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}^{\prime},\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime})-F_{\mathbf{z}}(t,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}^{\prime},\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime})\right)+F_{\mathbf{u}}(t,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}^{\prime},\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime})\right]\cdot\mathbf{U}(t)\,dt

last condition implies that

is absolutely continuous in the interval

, including the

∎

4.4.
End-point conditions

Second-order variational problems, like the one considered in Theorem
4.5
, are typically studied under fixed, end-point conditions at end-points
\{0,1\}
up to one order less than the highest order explicitly participating in the functional.
For second-order problems, end-point conditions would involve the four values

\mathbf{u}(0),\mathbf{u}(1),\quad\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(0),\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(1).

Our periodicity conditions would demand

\mathbf{u}(0)=\mathbf{u}(1),\quad\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(0)=\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(1),

but these two common values are unknown. There might be several vectors
\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{z}
for which critical paths for the same functional

E(\mathbf{u})=\int_{0}^{1}F(t,\mathbf{u}(t),\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t),\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime}(t))\,dt

for
\mathbf{u}\in H^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})
under fixed end-point conditions

(4.19)

\mathbf{u}(0)=\mathbf{u}(1)=\mathbf{y},\quad\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(0)=\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(1)=\mathbf{z},

would also be critical paths for our system in Theorem
4.5
without imposing such end-point conditions but just periodicity. Those distinguished values for
\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{z}
would be such that condition (
4.11
) turns out to be correct.

Hilbert’s 16th problem

We will be very much interested in being capable of counting how many such pairs
(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})
there might be for our particular second-order perturbation
E_{\epsilon}
of the basic, first-order functional
E_{0}
, as given in (
2.16
). Parallel versions of Theorem
4.5
taking into account specific end-point conditions will help us in counting branches of critical paths for our perturbed functional
E_{\epsilon}
. See below.

More explicitly, the following versions of Theorem
4.5
take into account our discussion above concerning the role played by end-point conditions. To this end, we introduce the notation

\displaystyle H^{2}_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})=\{\mathbf{v}\in H^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2}):\mathbf{v}(0)=\mathbf{v}(1)=\mathbf{y},\mathbf{v}^{\prime}(0)=\mathbf{v}^{\prime}(1)=\mathbf{z}\},

\displaystyle H^{2}_{\mathbf{y},}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})=\{\mathbf{v}\in H^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2}):\mathbf{v}(0)=\mathbf{v}(1)=\mathbf{y},\mathbf{v}^{\prime}(0)=\mathbf{v}^{\prime}(1)\},

\displaystyle H^{2}_{,\mathbf{z}}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})=\{\mathbf{v}\in H^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2}):\mathbf{v}(0)=\mathbf{v}(1),\mathbf{v}^{\prime}(0)=\mathbf{v}^{\prime}(1)=\mathbf{z}\},

for fixed vectors
\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Note that

(4.20)

H^{2}_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})=H^{2}_{\mathbf{y},}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap H^{2}_{,\mathbf{z}}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2}),

and that this subspace can also be represented as

(4.21)

H^{2}_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})

if the subspace

H^{2}_{\mathbf{y}_{0},\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{0},\mathbf{z}_{1}}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})

is given through

\{\mathbf{v}\in H^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2}):\mathbf{v}(0)=\mathbf{y}_{0},\mathbf{v}(1)=\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{v}^{\prime}(0)=\mathbf{z}_{0},\mathbf{v}^{\prime}(1)=\mathbf{z}_{1}\}.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let integrand
F
and corresponding functional
E
be as in
Theorem
4.5
. Let

4.6.

What is important for us, as stressed in the preceding sections, is the fundamental distinction between an initial-value and an end-point-value problem in terms of uniqueness of solutions: under very reasonable regularity hypotheses for the differential problem, the solution of an initial-value problem is unique; however, this may not be so for an end-point-value version of it. Let

\displaystyle\int_{J}\rho_{j}(t)\left[\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial r^{n}}P(u(t,r_{j}))-\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial r^{n}}P(u(t,r_{\infty}))\right]\,dt+

\displaystyle\int_{J}\rho_{j}(t)\left[\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial r^{n}}P(u(t,r_{\infty}))-\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial r^{n}}P(u(0,r_{\infty}))\right]\,dt.

Take into account the smoothness of

to use the Lebesgue dominated convergence principle for the first integral.
This is standard.

Hilbert’s 16th problem

The particular form of the composition

The same proof es valid for the family of functions

Proposition 4.16
.

Assume that the function

is smooth, and

We can even let the function

be the result of an asymptotic process depending on several variables
(t,\mathbf{r})

provided we are allowed to modulate the mollifier

with respect to

Proposition 4.17
.

Let

be a sequence of smooth functions of several variables

(t,\mathbf{r})\in J\times I\subset\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(1)

For every subsequence

sufficiently advanced,

\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{J_{k(j)}}\rho_{k(j)}(t)u_{j}(t,\mathbf{r})\,dt=\lim_{j\to\infty}u_{j}(0,\mathbf{r})

for all

in a compact set of

(2)

For every subsequence

sufficiently advanced,

\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{0}^{2\alpha_{k(j)}}\rho_{k(j)}(t)u_{j}(t,\mathbf{r})\,dt=\lim_{j\to\infty}u_{j}(\alpha_{k(j)},\mathbf{r})

for all

Proof.

The proof is straightforward after the ideas utilized in the proof of Proposition

For

5.1.1.

5.1.2.

Let

Given any

Note that

Let

The first assertion is clear because winding and absolute winding numbers are not related to parameter dependence, but only on taken-on values of the normalized tangent vectors.
For the second part, note that because
\Lambda
is a convex set, it is topologically equivalent to a ball.
\Lambda_{\mathbf{u}}
, being the image under a continuous mapping of

We next treat the family of perturbed functionals

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
\mathbb{C}_{d}
be a component of
\{E_{0}=0\}
in

, topologically equivalent to a ball, according to Lemma

\mathbb{C}_{a,\epsilon,d}

Proof.

Because

is smooth and topologically equivalent to a ball, a smooth one-to-one map

can be found so that

is smooth and convex. The composition functional

, being smooth, non-negative, and with zero set

.
∎

5.2.1.
The first derivative

For our functional

\langle E^{\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{U}\rangle=\left.\frac{d}{d\tau}\right|_{\tau=0}E_{0}(\mathbf{u}+\tau\mathbf{U}).

Since

E_{0}(\mathbf{u}+\tau\mathbf{U})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}[\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u}+\tau\mathbf{U})\cdot(\mathbf{u}^{\prime}+\tau\mathbf{U}^{\prime})]^{2}\,dt,

(5.6)

\langle E^{\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{U}\rangle=\int_{0}^{1}(\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{u}^{\prime})[(D\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})\mathbf{U})\cdot\mathbf{u}^{\prime}+\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{U}^{\prime}]\,dt.

We are, therefore, seeking an element

\mathbf{v}(=E^{\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u}))\in H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})

such that

\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{U}=\langle E^{\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{U}\rangle,

that is to say

(5.7)

\int_{0}^{1}(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{U}+\mathbf{v}^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{U}^{\prime}+\mathbf{v}^{\prime\prime}\cdot\mathbf{U}^{\prime\prime})\,dt=\int_{0}^{1}(\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{u}^{\prime})[(D\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})\mathbf{U})\cdot\mathbf{u}^{\prime}+\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{U}^{\prime}]\,dt

for every

\mathbf{U}\in H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2}).

There is a unique such
\mathbf{v}
, which
turns out to be the minimizer (with respect to

\mathbf{U}\in H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2}))

of the augmented functional

(5.8)

\int_{0}^{1}[\frac{1}{2}|\mathbf{U}^{\prime\prime}|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}|\mathbf{U}^{\prime}|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}|\mathbf{U}|^{2}-(\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{u}^{\prime})[(D\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})\mathbf{U})\cdot\mathbf{u}^{\prime}+\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{U}^{\prime}]]\,dt.

The existence of a unique minimizer for this problem, which is
quadratic, is a direct consequence of the classical Lax-Milgram Theorem (see
Corollary 5.8 of
[
7
]
for instance).

Therefore the equation for

\mathbf{v}=E^{\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u})\in H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})

will be the
associated Euler-Lagrange system for the functional (
5.8
) as
it is given by this last theorem

(5.9)

[\mathbf{v}^{\prime\prime}]^{\prime\prime}-[\mathbf{v}^{\prime}+(\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{u}^{\prime})\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})]^{\prime}+\mathbf{v}+(\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{u}^{\prime})\mathbf{u}^{\prime T}D\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{0}\hbox{ in }(0,1).

Its weak formulation is exactly (
5.7
).

5.2.2.
The second derivative

If, in general, we have a certain smooth,
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-functional

E:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}
over a Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
with derivative

E^{\prime}:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
, there are various ways to deal with the second
derivative, but probably the best suited for our purposes is to
consider the derivative

\langle E^{\prime\prime}(\mathbf{u}),(\mathbf{U},\overline{\mathbf{U}})\rangle=\left.\frac{d}{d\delta}\right|_{\delta=0}\langle E^{\prime}(\mathbf{u}+\delta\mathbf{U}),\overline{\mathbf{U}}\rangle,

where both vector fields
\mathbf{U}
and
\overline{\mathbf{U}}
belong to
\mathbb{H}
. In
our situation, and in view of (
5.6
), we have

\begin{array}[]{rl}\langle E^{\prime\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u}),(\mathbf{U},\overline{\mathbf{U}})\rangle=&\left.\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\delta}\right|_{\delta=0}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}(\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u}+\delta\mathbf{U})\cdot(\mathbf{u}^{\prime}+\delta\mathbf{U}^{\prime}))\times\\
&\qquad\qquad[\nabla\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u}+\delta\mathbf{U})\overline{\mathbf{U}}\cdot(\mathbf{u}^{\prime}+\delta\mathbf{U}^{\prime})+\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u}+\delta\mathbf{U})\cdot\overline{\mathbf{U}}^{\prime}]\,dt\\
=&\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}(\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{u}^{\prime})[\nabla^{2}\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u}):(\mathbf{U},\overline{\mathbf{U}},\mathbf{u}^{\prime})+\nabla\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u}):(\overline{\mathbf{U}},\mathbf{U}^{\prime})\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad+\nabla\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u}):(\mathbf{U},\overline{\mathbf{U}}^{\prime})]\,dt\\
&+\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}[\nabla\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})\mathbf{U}\cdot\mathbf{u}^{\prime}+\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{U}^{\prime}]\times\\
&\qquad\qquad[\nabla\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})\overline{\mathbf{U}}\cdot\mathbf{u}^{\prime}+\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})\cdot\overline{\mathbf{U}}^{\prime}]\,dt\end{array}

Through this long formula, we can understand, for such a
\mathbf{u}
given
and fixed, the linear operator

E^{\prime\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u}):H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})\to H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2}).

Let

\mathbf{U}\in H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})

be given. The image

\mathbf{V}=E^{\prime\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u})\mathbf{U}\in H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})

of
\mathbf{U}
under the linear map
E^{\prime\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u})
is determined through the identity

(5.10)

\langle E^{\prime\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u}),(\mathbf{U},\overline{\mathbf{U}})\rangle=\langle E^{\prime\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u})\mathbf{U},\overline{\mathbf{U}}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{V},\overline{\mathbf{U}}\rangle

for all

\overline{\mathbf{U}}\in H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2}).

The element
\mathbf{V}
defined through
(
5.10
) is the solution of a standard quadratic variational
problem for which the weak form of its optimality condition is
precisely (
5.10
).

5.3.
Compactness

The calculations in the preceding subsection enable us to prove the following facts.

5.3.1.
Compactness of
E^{\prime}_{0}

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
\{\mathbf{u}_{j}\}
be a uniformly bounded sequence in
H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})
, and

\{\mathbf{v}_{j}\}
the sequence of derivatives

\mathbf{v}_{j}=E^{\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{j})\in H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})

which are solutions of
(
5.9
) for
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}_{j}
. Then
\{\mathbf{v}_{j}\}
is relatively
compact in
H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

Proof.

For the sake of notation, set

\mathbf{G}_{j}\equiv(\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u}_{j})\cdot\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{j})\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u}_{j}),\quad\mathbf{H}_{j}\equiv(\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u}_{j})\cdot\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{j})\mathbf{u}^{\prime T}_{j}D\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u}_{j}).

If
\{\mathbf{u}_{j}\}
is uniformly bounded in
H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})
, we know that a
certain subsequence (not relabelled) of
\{\mathbf{u}_{j}\}
converges weakly
to some
\mathbf{u}
in
H^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Set

\mathbf{G}\equiv(\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{u}^{\prime})\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u}),\quad\mathbf{H}\equiv(\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{u}^{\prime})\mathbf{u}^{\prime T}D\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u}).

If we put

\mathbf{v}_{j}=E^{\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{j}),\quad\mathbf{v}=E^{\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u}),

then
(
5.7
) implies

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}[\mathbf{v}^{\prime\prime}_{j}\cdot\mathbf{U}^{\prime\prime}+(\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{j}+\mathbf{G}_{j})\cdot\mathbf{U}^{\prime}+(\mathbf{v}_{j}+\mathbf{H}_{j})\cdot\mathbf{U}]\,dt=0,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}[\mathbf{v}^{\prime\prime}\cdot\mathbf{U}^{\prime\prime}+(\mathbf{v}^{\prime}+\mathbf{G})\cdot\mathbf{U}^{\prime}+(\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{H})\cdot\mathbf{U}]\,dt=0,

for every

\mathbf{U}\in H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2}).

By substracting one from the other

\int_{0}^{1}[(\mathbf{v}^{\prime\prime}_{j}-\mathbf{v}^{\prime\prime})\cdot\mathbf{V}^{\prime\prime}+(\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{j}-\mathbf{v}^{\prime}+\mathbf{G}_{j}-\mathbf{G})\cdot\mathbf{V}^{\prime}+(\mathbf{v}_{j}-\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{H}_{j}-\mathbf{H})\cdot\mathbf{V}]\,dt=0

for every

\mathbf{U}\in H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2}).

We can take
\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{v}_{j}-\mathbf{v}
to find that

\int_{0}^{1}[|\mathbf{v}^{\prime\prime}_{j}-\mathbf{v}^{\prime\prime}|^{2}+(\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{j}-\mathbf{v}^{\prime}+\mathbf{G}_{j}-\mathbf{G})\cdot(\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{j}-\mathbf{v}^{\prime})+(\mathbf{v}_{j}-\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{H}_{j}-\mathbf{H})\cdot(\mathbf{v}_{j}-\mathbf{v})]\,dt=0.

This equality can be reorganized as

\|\mathbf{v}_{j}-\mathbf{v}\|^{2}_{H^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})}=-\int_{0}^{1}[(\mathbf{G}_{j}-\mathbf{G})\cdot(\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{j}-\mathbf{v}^{\prime})+(\mathbf{H}_{j}-\mathbf{H})\cdot(\mathbf{v}_{j}-\mathbf{v})]\,dt,

Hence, by the standard Hölder inequality for integrals, we can also
have

(5.11)

\|\mathbf{v}_{j}-\mathbf{v}\|_{H^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq\|\mathbf{G}_{j}-\mathbf{G}\|_{L^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})}+\|\mathbf{H}_{j}-\mathbf{H}\|_{L^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})}.

Since the weak convergence of
\mathbf{u}_{j}
to
\mathbf{u}
in
H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})
implies
weak convergence up to second derivatives, by the classical
Rellich–Kondrachov Theorem, which implies that the injection
W^{2,p}\subset W^{1,p}
is always compact (see Theorem 9.16 in

[
7
]
for the case with first derivatives
W^{1,p}\subset L^{p}
), we conclude the convergences

\mathbf{G}_{j}\to\mathbf{G},\quad\mathbf{H}_{j}\to\mathbf{H}

strongly in

L^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})
. The proof is then a direct consequence of
(
5.11
).
∎

As a direct consequence of Lemma
5.6
, we have:

Corollary 5.7
.

The map

E^{\prime}_{0}:H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})\to H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})

is compact.

This compactness property is the only reason why the functional
E_{0}

has to be perturbed by a norm involving up to second derivatives. If
we had just perturbed
E_{0}
up to first derivatives, we would not
have the strong convergence of the vector fields
\mathbf{G}_{j}
and
\mathbf{H}_{j}

to
\mathbf{G}
and
\mathbf{H}
, respectively, in the proof of Lemma

5.6
.

5.3.2.
Compactness of
E^{\prime\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u})

Another main necessary ingredient is the
compactness of the linear operator

E^{\prime\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u}):H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})\to H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})

for each fixed
\mathbf{u}
.

The form (
5.10
) is especially suited to show
the compactness we are after. Set

\begin{array}[]{rl}\overline{\mathbf{F}}=&(\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{u}^{\prime})\nabla^{2}\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u}):\mathbf{u}^{\prime}+\nabla\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\otimes\nabla\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})\mathbf{u}^{\prime},\\
\overline{\mathbf{G}}=&\nabla\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})+\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})\otimes\nabla\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})\mathbf{u}^{\prime},\\
\overline{\mathbf{H}}=&\nabla\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})+\nabla\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\otimes\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u}),\\
\overline{\mathbf{J}}=&\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})\otimes\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u}).\end{array}

This choice is dictated so that

\langle E^{\prime\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u}),(\mathbf{U},\overline{\mathbf{U}})\rangle=\int_{0}^{1}[\overline{\mathbf{F}}(\mathbf{U},\overline{\mathbf{U}})+\overline{\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{U}^{\prime},\overline{\mathbf{U}})+\overline{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{U},\overline{\mathbf{U}}^{\prime})+\overline{\mathbf{J}}(\mathbf{U}^{\prime},\overline{\mathbf{U}}^{\prime})]\,dt.

Exactly as in Lemma
5.6
, one can show the following.

Lemma 5.8
.

For fixed, given

\mathbf{u}\in H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2}),

the operator

\mathbf{U}\mapsto\mathbf{V}=E^{\prime\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u})\mathbf{U}

is self-adjoint and compact.

Proof.

Assume
\{\mathbf{U}_{j}\}
is bounded in
H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2})
. In particular, and for
reasons already pointed out earlier,
\mathbf{U}^{\prime}_{j}\to\mathbf{U}^{\prime}
uniformly for
some

\mathbf{U}\in H^{2}_{O}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2}).

Let
\mathbf{V}_{j}
and
\mathbf{V}
determined through
(
5.10
), respectively. Then

\|\mathbf{V}_{j}-\mathbf{V}\|^{2}=\langle E^{\prime\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u})(\mathbf{U}_{j}-\mathbf{U}),\mathbf{V}_{j}-\mathbf{V}\rangle,

and

\|\mathbf{V}_{j}-\mathbf{V}\|\leq\|E^{\prime\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u})(\mathbf{U}_{j}-\mathbf{U})\|.

The key point is to realize, in the formulas above, that in

E^{\prime\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{u})(\mathbf{U}_{j}-\mathbf{U})
only up to first derivatives of the
differences
\mathbf{U}_{j}-\mathbf{U}
occur, and these converge strongly to zero.
Hence

\|\mathbf{V}_{j}-\mathbf{V}\|\to 0.

∎

can be chosen analytic as well. This is no restriction, as we have remarked in various occasions that there is plenty of choice to select
\mathbf{v}_{0}
from: it can be chosen analytic, and uniformly bounded.

5.6.
Asymptotic behavior

For the sake of transparency, and to facilitate a few interesting
computations, we recast system (
5.17
) or (
5.19
) in its two components

\begin{array}[]{r}(ZQ)^{\prime}+Z(-Q_{x}x^{\prime}+P_{x}y^{\prime})=-\epsilon\alpha_{1},\\
(ZP)^{\prime}+Z(Q_{y}x^{\prime}-P_{y}y^{\prime})=\epsilon\alpha_{2}.\end{array}

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}=(P,Q),\quad\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon}=(x,y),\quad\mathbf{v}_{0}=(X,Y)

\displaystyle Z\equiv\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon})\cdot\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{\epsilon}=P(x,y)y^{\prime}-Q(x,y)x^{\prime},

\displaystyle W\equiv\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon})\cdot\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{\epsilon}=P(x,y)x^{\prime}+Q(x,y)y^{\prime},

\displaystyle\operatorname{Div}\equiv P_{x}+Q_{y},\quad\alpha_{1}=\overline{x}^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}-\overline{x}^{\prime\prime}+\overline{x},\quad\alpha_{2}=\overline{y}^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}-\overline{y}^{\prime\prime}+\overline{y},

with

\overline{x}=x+X,\quad\overline{y}=y+Y.

Note that
Z^{2}/2
is
precisely the integrand for
E_{0}
, and recall that all close paths
involved are
1
-periodic,
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
and belong to
\mathbb{O}
, so
that we can freely differentiate in
t
as many times as needed. In
particular, the two equations of the system of critical closed paths
become

(5.20)

Z^{\prime}Q+Z\operatorname{Div}y^{\prime}=-\epsilon\alpha_{1},\quad Z^{\prime}P+Z\operatorname{Div}x^{\prime}=\epsilon\alpha_{2}.

We manipulate the two equations in (
5.20
) in two ways:

(1)

multiply the first equation by
Q
, the second by
P
, and add
up the results to find

(5.21)

Z^{\prime}(P^{2}+Q^{2})=-\epsilon(\alpha_{1}Q-\alpha_{2}P)-ZW\operatorname{Div};

(2)

then, multiply the first by
P
, the second by
Q
, and subtract
the results to have

Z^{2}\operatorname{Div}=-\epsilon(\alpha_{1}P+\alpha_{2}Q).

We remind readers that we are
searching for periodic solutions of this system for which
E_{\epsilon}
is away from
zero. Indeed, recall that in seeking an upper bound for the right-hand side in (
5.13
),
a
is small, but kept fixed, when computing the inner limit

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}M_{cri,\epsilon,a}.

The second part of Theorem
3.9
ensures that there cannot be branches of critical paths
\{\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon}\}
with
E_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon})\to 0
.
We will therefore discard from our consideration those
critical paths
\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon}
for which
E_{0}
is arbitrarily small. In particular, we do
not need to consider asymptotic behaviors reducing to a point, and
so, bearing in mind that equilibria of our polynomial, differential
system are isolated and they could only be associated with critical
closed paths of the kind we are not interested in (those with small value for
E_{0}
), we can further multiply
(
5.21
) by
Z
and divide by
P^{2}+Q^{2}
, to have, taking into
account the other equation,

(Z^{2})^{\prime}=2\epsilon(\alpha_{1}x^{\prime}+\alpha_{2}y^{\prime}).

Hence, system (
5.17
) can be written in the simplified,
equivalent form

(Z^{2})^{\prime}=2\epsilon(\alpha_{1}x^{\prime}+\alpha_{2}y^{\prime}),\quad Z^{2}\operatorname{Div}=-\epsilon(\alpha_{1}P+\alpha_{2}Q).

To avoid confusion, we will rather write

(5.22)

(Z_{\epsilon}^{2})^{\prime}=2\epsilon(\alpha_{1}x^{\prime}_{\epsilon}+\alpha_{2}y^{\prime}_{\epsilon}),\quad Z_{\epsilon}^{2}\operatorname{Div}_{\epsilon}=-\epsilon(\alpha_{1}P_{\epsilon}+\alpha_{2}Q_{\epsilon}).

to stress the dependence on
\epsilon
of all quantities. Recall that

Z_{\epsilon}=\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon})\cdot\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{\epsilon},\quad\operatorname{Div}_{\epsilon}=\operatorname{div}\mathbf{F}(x_{\epsilon},y_{\epsilon}),\quad\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon}=(x_{\epsilon},y_{\epsilon}).

The first point in Theorem
3.9
informs us that in fact, along branches of critical paths
(x_{\epsilon},y_{\epsilon})
, we can neglect terms multiplied by
\epsilon
, and
have that

(Z_{\epsilon}^{2})^{\prime}\to 0,\quad Z_{\epsilon}^{2}\operatorname{Div}_{\epsilon}\to 0

in
L^{2}(0,1)
. We record this fact in a formal statement.

Lemma 5.14
.

Let
(x_{\epsilon},y_{\epsilon})
be a branch of solutions of (
5.22
).
For a suitable subsequence (not relabeled),

(Z_{\epsilon}^{2})^{\prime}\to 0,\quad Z_{\epsilon}^{2}\operatorname{Div}_{\epsilon}\to 0,

in
L^{2}(0,1)
and pointwise for a.e.
t\in[0,1]
.

Note how the
L^{2}
-convergence claimed in this statement forbids that
Z_{\epsilon}^{2}
could converge to a non-constant function.
We are therefore entitled to understand all possible asymptotic behaviors of critical closed paths

(x_{\epsilon},y_{\epsilon})\in\mathbb{O}

through an analysis of the limit system

(5.23)

(Z^{2})^{\prime}=0,\quad Z^{2}\operatorname{Div}=0,

setting
\epsilon=0
in (
5.22
).
The first equation in (
5.23
) implies that
Z^{2}=k^{2}
(note that here is crucial the
L^{2}
-convergence in Lemma
5.14
),
but since we are only interested in the asymptotic behavior for
critical closed paths whose value for
E_{0}
stays away from zero, we
discard the case
k=0
. In this situation, the second equation in
(
5.23
), implies
\operatorname{Div}=0
. We would like to understand
the nature of solutions of the limit system

(5.24)

Z^{2}=k^{2}>0,\quad\operatorname{Div}=0.

We write this system in the form, differentiating the second
equation,

(5.25)

\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{u}^{\prime}=\pm k\neq 0,\quad\nabla\operatorname{Div}(\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{u}^{\prime}=0.

This is an implicit, first-order system that becomes singular when
the determinant

\nabla\operatorname{Div}(\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{u})

of the matrix of the system

\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})\\
\nabla\operatorname{Div}(\mathbf{u})\end{pmatrix}

vanishes. These singular points are precisely the
contact points of our differential system over the curve
\operatorname{Div}=0
. The
fact that

\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{u}^{\prime}=\pm k\neq 0

shows that
\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon}
, for
\epsilon
sufficiently small, can only turn
around, changing
+k
by
-k
or viceversa, near those contact
points. As a matter of fact, critical closed paths
\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon}
do have to
turn around whenever one such point is reached and is a single root of system (
1.2
). To emphasize this point, we explicitly introduce the following.

Definition 5.3
.

A point
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a single (or simple) contact point of our differential system, if

\det\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})\\
\nabla\operatorname{Div}(\mathbf{u})\end{pmatrix}

vanishes at
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{p}
, but it changes sign in every neighborhood of
\mathbf{p}
in
\operatorname{Div}=0
.

We can now establish in a precise way the role played by single, contact points.

Lemma 5.15
.

Let
\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon}=(x_{\epsilon},y_{\epsilon})
be a branch of critical closed paths of
E_{\epsilon}
such that
E_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon})
stays away from zero.
Suppose that in a certain subinterval

[t^{-}_{\epsilon},t^{+}_{\epsilon}]\subset[0,1],\quad t_{\epsilon}^{+}-t_{\epsilon}^{-}\to 0,

we know that

|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(t^{\pm}_{\epsilon})|\to+\infty,\quad\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon}(t^{\pm}_{\epsilon})\to\mathbf{p},

as
\epsilon\to 0
, where
\mathbf{p}
is a single, contact point of the system. Then
\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon}
must turn around at
\mathbf{p}
for
\epsilon
sufficiently small, in the sense

(5.26)

\frac{\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(t^{-}_{\epsilon})}{|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(t^{-}_{\epsilon})|}+\frac{\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(t^{+}_{\epsilon})}{|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(t^{+}_{\epsilon})|}\to\mathbf{0}

as
\epsilon\to 0
.

Figure 1.
Situation around a contact point: top, a multiple contact point; bottom, a single contact point.

Proof.

Figure
1
can help in understanding the situation.
The single, contact point
\mathbf{p}
is the intersection of both axes. Keep in mind that

Z^{2}=(F^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{u}^{\prime})^{2}\to k^{2},\quad Z\to\pm k,

a constant uniformly away from zero, as
\mathbf{u}\to\mathbf{p}
.
Z^{2}
must be continuous through
\mathbf{p}
, but
Z
might change sign abruptly.
The possibility for
\mathbf{u}
to turn around at
\mathbf{p}
, jumping from
k
to
-k
, is always there. The thing is that if
\mathbf{p}
is a single contact point, a simple solution of system (
1.2
), then this is the only possibility.
Indeed, the only way for
\mathbf{u}
to cross over a contact point
\mathbf{p}
is for the inner product

\mathbf{F}^{\perp}(\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(=Z)

to retain its sign as to avoid a jump discontinuity. This is impossible at a single, contact point (bottom picture of Figure
1
corresponding to a simple contact point), while it is coherent with a double (or even-order) contact point (top picture in the same figure).
∎

Our above discussion can be summarized in the following statement
that classifies all possible asymptotic behaviors for critical closed
paths.

Theorem 5.16
.

Assume that all the components of the curve
\operatorname{Div}=0
are topologically
straight lines or ovals. The possible limit behaviors as
\epsilon\to 0
of branches of critical closed
paths of
E_{\varepsilon}
, with critical value uniformly away from zero, can be necessarily
identified with arcs of the connected
components of the curve
\operatorname{Div}=0
in one of the following
possibilities:

(a)

If the component is homeomorphic to a straight line, then

(a.1)

the limit behavior is an arc whose endpoints are two contact
points and no additional contact point lies in its interior;

(a.2)

the limit behavior is an arc whose endpoints are one contact
point and the infinity, and no additional contact point can be found in its
interior;

(a.3)

the limit behavior is the whole component without contact points.

(b)

If the component is homeomorphic to an oval, then

(b.1)

the limit behavior is an arc whose endpoints are two contact
points and no additional contact point lies in its interior;

(b.2)

the limit behavior is an arc covering the full oval whose
endpoints have to be a single contact point, and no additional
contact point is to be found in the oval;

(b.3)

the limit behavior is the whole oval without contact points.

5.7.
Multiplicity

We are concerned in this subsection about the possibility that various
branches of the set of critical closed paths, for
\epsilon

positive, may coalesce into the same limit behavior as
\epsilon\searrow 0
,
and how they can possibly contribute to the
number of critical paths. We will need to exploit very precisely the information that the number of any such group of critical paths share, at least locally, the same limit behavior as
\epsilon\to 0
.

As a consequence of Definition
5.2
, and comments made after it, the set of possible singular pairs
(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})
where maps

\mathbf{u}(t;\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q},\epsilon)

are not well-defined because there might be more than one critical path with

\mathbf{u}(0;\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q},\epsilon)=\mathbf{u}(1;\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q},\epsilon)=\mathbf{p},\quad\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(0;\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q},\epsilon)=\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(1;\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q},\epsilon)=\mathbf{q},

is isolated, for each fixed
\epsilon
, as a consequence of analyticity. This is in fact the only reason to stress the analytic dependence of this map.
For a given (countable) sequence of values for
\epsilon
, we can always find a certain point
\mathbf{p}
in the part of the curve
\operatorname{Div}=0
, with tangent vector
\mathbf{q}
, identifying the particular asymptotic limit we are focusing on, and away from any contact point of the system limiting the asymptotic behavior considered according to Theorem
5.16
, in such a way that the family of maps

(5.27)

\mathbf{u}(t;\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z},\epsilon):[0,1]\times{\mathbb{B}}_{\rho}(\mathbf{p})\times{\mathbb{B}}_{\rho}(\mathbf{q})\to\mathbb{R}^{2}

is well-defined, smooth (even analytic) in all variables
(t,\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})
, for every value of
\epsilon
from the selected sequence, and some positive radius
\rho
. The interesting point is that the domain of this family of maps can be taken independently of
\epsilon
, at least for a sequence of values of
\epsilon
converging to zero. We will not make the distinction between a countable set of values of
\epsilon
and the full continuum since this distinction is irrelevant for our purposes.
Recall that, in addition to verifying (
5.19
) in Proposition
5.13
, we also have

\mathbf{u}(0;\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z},\epsilon)=\mathbf{u}(1;\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z},\epsilon)=\mathbf{y},\quad\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(0;\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z},\epsilon)=\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(1;\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z},\epsilon)=\mathbf{z},

for

(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})\in{\mathbb{B}}_{\rho}(\mathbf{p})\times{\mathbb{B}}_{\rho}(\mathbf{q}).

We are going to proceed in two successive steps.

(1)

Suppose
\mathbf{y}
is kept fixed in a neighborhood of
\mathbf{p}
, so that we would work in the space

n-1

for suitable velocity vectors

\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{z}_{i}(\mathbf{y}),\quad i=1,2,\dots,n-1,

that is to say

5.12
for each fixed
\mathbf{y}
. The maps
\mathbf{z}_{i}(\mathbf{y})

(2)

For each given

, how many values of
\mathbf{y}
in a neighborhood of
\mathbf{p}
can there be such that
\mathbf{u}_{i}(t;\mathbf{y},\epsilon)

5.28

5.11

Our strategy is led by two main points.

(1)

We would like to replace vector variables

and
\mathbf{z}
in (

r
and
s

(2)

Use the full power of being a critical path to localize arguments through suitable test paths, and reduce the counting procedure to a question about one-variable polynomials.

Since paths
\mathbf{u}(t;\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z},\epsilon)
will intersect the straight line

\{\mathbf{p}+r\mathbf{q}^{\perp}:r\in\mathbb{R}\},

at least for small
\epsilon
and small

and reparametrizing the path conveniently around

\mathbf{p}+r\mathbf{q}^{\perp}
in the above discussion in such a way that we have a single variable

to label the starting point of our critical paths. To make things easier, we will replace the unit interval

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{p}+r\mathbf{q}^{\perp},\quad\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{q}+s\mathbf{q}^{\perp},

P_{\epsilon}(s)=\operatorname{Div}[\mathbf{p}+r\mathbf{q}^{\perp}+\alpha(\epsilon)(\mathbf{q}+s\mathbf{q}^{\perp})].

Let
N\leq n-1
be the degree of such polynomials in the variable
s
(recall that

is being kept frozen here), and take

(5.36)

\tilde{\mathbf{W}}_{\epsilon}(t)\equiv\frac{1}{\alpha(\epsilon)^{N}}\mathbf{q}^{\perp},\quad\mathbf{W}_{\epsilon}=\chi_{\epsilon}(t)\tilde{\mathbf{W}}_{\epsilon}.

Keep in mind that
\operatorname{Div}(x,y)
is a polynomial of at most degree
n-1
in two variables.
Assume that, for fixed
r
, we could find at least
N+1
branches of values for
s
that are roots of

in (

), including possibly multiplicities, i.e.

f_{\epsilon}(r,s_{j}(\epsilon,r))=0,\quad s=s_{j}(\epsilon,r),j=1,2,\dots,N,N+1.

Because each
f_{\epsilon}
is smooth, there would be a certain value
s(\epsilon)
,
s(\epsilon)\to 0
, such that

\frac{\partial^{N}f_{\epsilon}}{\partial s^{N}}(r,s(\epsilon))=0\hbox{ for all }\epsilon.

We have dropped the dependence of

on

here for the sake of notational simplicity.
If we go back to our formula for
f_{\epsilon}(r,s)
, and take into account that all functions involved are smooth and variables move in intervals where everything is bounded, we can take differentiation under the integral sign, and find that

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\int_{J_{\epsilon}}\chi_{\epsilon}(t)\left.\frac{\partial^{N}}{\partial s^{N}}\right|_{s=s(\epsilon)}[\operatorname{Div}(\mathbf{u}(r,s,\epsilon))\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(r,s,\epsilon)^{\perp}\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{W}}_{\epsilon}]\,dt

\displaystyle-\frac{\epsilon}{k_{\epsilon}}\left.\frac{\partial^{N}}{\partial s^{N}}\right|_{s=s(\epsilon)}\langle\mathbf{u}(r,s,\epsilon),\mathbf{W}_{\epsilon}\rangle.

For the second term, we can write, after several integrations by parts,

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{u}(r,s,\epsilon),\mathbf{W}_{\epsilon}\rangle=

\displaystyle\int_{J_{\epsilon}}\sum_{i=0}^{2}\mathbf{u}^{i)}(t;r,s,\epsilon)\cdot\mathbf{W}_{\epsilon}^{i)}(t)\,dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{J_{\epsilon}}\chi_{\epsilon}(t)\sum_{i=0}^{2}(-1)^{i}\mathbf{u}^{2i)}(t;r,s,\epsilon)\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{W}}_{\epsilon}(t)\,dt.

By the second part of Proposition
4.17
, if
\alpha(\epsilon)
is chosen sufficiently small, we can conclude that

(5.37)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial^{N}}{\partial s^{N}}\right|_{s=s(\epsilon)}[\operatorname{Div}(\mathbf{u}(\alpha(\epsilon);r,s,\epsilon))\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(\alpha(\epsilon);r,s,\epsilon)^{\perp}\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{W}}_{\epsilon}]

\displaystyle-\frac{\epsilon}{k_{\epsilon}}\left.\frac{\partial^{N}}{\partial s^{N}}\right|_{s=s(\epsilon)}\sum_{i=0}^{2}(-1)^{i}\mathbf{u}^{2i)}(\alpha(\epsilon);r,s,\epsilon)\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{W}}_{\epsilon}\to 0.

We would like to argue that this vanishing limit contradicts the choice of the degree for polynomials
P_{\epsilon}(s)
made in (
5.35
).

Again, because of smoothness,

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}(\alpha(\epsilon);r,s,\epsilon))=\mathbf{u}(0;r,s,\epsilon)+\alpha(\epsilon)\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(0;r,s,\epsilon)+\alpha(\epsilon)^{2}\mathbf{R}(t;r,s,\epsilon),

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(\alpha(\epsilon);r,s,\epsilon))=\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(0;r,s,\epsilon)+\alpha(\epsilon)\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime}(0;r,s,\epsilon)+\alpha(\epsilon)^{2}\mathbf{R}^{\prime}(t;r,s,\epsilon)

with bounded remainders

\mathbf{R}(t;r,s,\epsilon),\quad\mathbf{R}^{\prime}(t;r,s,\epsilon)

in the domain where variables move. Even more explicitly

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}(\alpha(\epsilon);r,s,\epsilon))=\mathbf{p}+r\mathbf{q}^{\perp}+\alpha(\epsilon)(\mathbf{q}+s\mathbf{q}^{\perp})+\alpha(\epsilon)^{2}\mathbf{R}(t;r,s,\epsilon),

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(\alpha(\epsilon);r,s,\epsilon))=(\mathbf{q}+s\mathbf{q}^{\perp})+\alpha(\epsilon)\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime}(0;r,s,\epsilon)+\alpha(\epsilon)^{2}\mathbf{R}^{\prime}(t;r,s,\epsilon).

If we take these expressions into the partial derivatives in (
5.37
), and recall (
5.36
),
we see that the first term becomes

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial^{N}}{\partial s^{N}}\right|_{s=s(\epsilon)}\frac{1}{\alpha(\epsilon)^{N}}[\operatorname{Div}(\mathbf{p}+r\mathbf{q}^{\perp}+\alpha(\epsilon)(\mathbf{q}+s\mathbf{q}^{\perp})+\alpha(\epsilon)^{2}\mathbf{R}(t;r,s,\epsilon))]

\displaystyle[(\mathbf{q}+s\mathbf{q}^{\perp})^{\perp}+\alpha(\epsilon)\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime}(0;r,s,\epsilon)^{\perp}+\alpha(\epsilon)^{2}\mathbf{R}^{\prime}(t;r,s,\epsilon)^{\perp}]\cdot\mathbf{q}^{\perp}

or

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial^{N}}{\partial s^{N}}\right|_{s=s(\epsilon)}\frac{1}{\alpha(\epsilon)^{N}}[\operatorname{Div}(\mathbf{p}+r\mathbf{q}^{\perp}+\alpha(\epsilon)(\mathbf{q}+s\mathbf{q}^{\perp})+\alpha(\epsilon)^{2}\mathbf{R}(t;r,s,\epsilon))]

\displaystyle[|\mathbf{q}|^{2}+(\alpha(\epsilon)\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime}(0;r,s,\epsilon)^{\perp}+\alpha(\epsilon)^{2}\mathbf{R}^{\prime}(t;r,s,\epsilon)^{\perp})\cdot\mathbf{q}^{\perp}].

Because of the way the polynomials
P_{\epsilon}
and their degree
N
were selected in (
5.35
), on the one hand; and the freedom we have to choose
\alpha(\epsilon)
converging to zero as rapidly as necessary, on the other, we see that the previous derivative will not vanish as
\epsilon\to 0
since terms affected by a negative power of
\alpha(\epsilon)
will vanish when differentiation is performed, while the coefficient corresponding to power
s^{N}
does not vanish, by our manner of choosing the degree
N
in (
5.35
), and is independent of
\alpha(\epsilon)
(and of
\epsilon
). The terms affected by the remainders
\mathbf{R}
will vanish too because of the presence of the higher power
\alpha(\epsilon)^{2}
.
The second term in (
5.37
) does not spoil our argument because of the presence of
\epsilon
in front of it. This is easily checked. Note how the reason for the choice in (
5.36
) is to have that the additional variable
s
in
(\mathbf{q}+s\mathbf{q}^{\perp})^{\perp}\cdot\mathbf{q}^{\perp}
drops out.

This contradiction between (
5.37
) and polynomials in (
5.35
) enables us to conclude that there cannot be more than
n-1
branches of solutions

f_{\epsilon}(r,s_{j}(\epsilon,r))=0,\quad j=1,2,\dots,n-1.

This is the end of the first step.

For the second step, fix one of those
n-1
branches

(r,s_{j}(r,\epsilon)),\quad 1\leq j\leq n-1,

and put

(5.38)

\mathbf{u}_{j}(t;r,\epsilon)\equiv\mathbf{u}(t;r,s_{j}(r,\epsilon),\epsilon),\quad\mathbf{u}_{j}(r,\epsilon)=\mathbf{u}_{j}(t;r,\epsilon),

for
t\in[-1/2,1/2]
, and small

and
\epsilon
. We go back to the family of functions in (

) setting

, to define the smooth functions

(5.39)

f_{j,\epsilon}(r)=\int_{J_{\epsilon}}\chi_{\epsilon}(t)\operatorname{Div}(\mathbf{u}_{j}(r,\epsilon))\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{j}(r,\epsilon)^{\perp}\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{W}}_{\epsilon}\,dt-\frac{\epsilon}{k_{\epsilon}}\langle\mathbf{u}_{j}(r,\epsilon),\mathbf{W}_{\epsilon}\rangle-\frac{1}{k_{\epsilon}}\langle\mathbf{v}_{0},\mathbf{W}_{\epsilon}\rangle.

This time however, since we are looking for critical paths in Proposition
5.11
, the value of test paths

\mathbf{W}_{\epsilon}=\chi_{\epsilon}\tilde{\mathbf{W}}_{\epsilon}

need not vanish at

, and hence mollifier
\chi_{\epsilon}
can be taken to be even and with support
[-\alpha(\epsilon),\alpha(\epsilon)]
with
\alpha(\epsilon)
, as in the first step, at our disposal. Our goal is, again, to argue that for each fixed

, the family of functions

in (

) cannot have more than

branches of roots as
\epsilon\to 0
. If we succeed in showing this, our claim in the theorem will be proved.

Our job, after the first step, is now much easier. We follow exactly along the same lines. Consider the polynomial

P(r)=\operatorname{Div}(\mathbf{p}+r\mathbf{q}^{\perp})

whose degree

is at most
n-1
. Take, almost as above,

\tilde{\mathbf{W}}_{\epsilon}(t)\equiv\mathbf{q}^{\perp},\quad\mathbf{W}_{\epsilon}=\chi_{\epsilon}(t)\tilde{\mathbf{W}}_{\epsilon}.

Suppose that there could be

f_{j,\epsilon}(r_{i,j}(\epsilon))=0,\quad i=1,2,\dots,N,N+1.

Recall that

of the

f^{N)}_{j,\epsilon}(r_{j}(\epsilon))=0,\quad r_{j}(\epsilon)\to 0\hbox{ as }\epsilon\to 0.

Through (

\displaystyle\int_{J_{\epsilon}}\chi_{\epsilon}(t)\left.\frac{d^{N}}{dr^{N}}\right|_{r=r_{j}(\epsilon)}\operatorname{Div}(\mathbf{u}_{j}(r,\epsilon))\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{j}(r,\epsilon)^{\perp}\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{W}}_{\epsilon}\,dt

\displaystyle-\frac{\epsilon}{k_{\epsilon}}\left.\frac{d^{N}}{dr^{N}}\right|_{r=r_{j}(\epsilon)}\langle\mathbf{u}_{j}(r,\epsilon),\mathbf{W}_{\epsilon}\rangle=0.

We focus on the important first term, since the second is tackled as in the first step through the first part of Proposition

\left.\frac{d^{N}}{dr^{N}}\right|_{r=r_{j}(\epsilon)}\operatorname{Div}(\mathbf{u}_{j}(0;r,\epsilon))\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{j}(0;r,\epsilon)^{\perp}\cdot\mathbf{q}^{\perp}\to 0.

∎

5.8.
Another form to deal with asymptotic behaviors of critical paths

The localized way in which we have dealt with multiplicity in the previous section, permits to argue about our upper bound without Lemma
5.15
.

By Lemma
5.14
, we have that for sequences of critical paths

\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon}=(x_{\epsilon},y_{\epsilon}),

we know that

(Z_{\epsilon}^{2})^{\prime}\to 0,\quad Z_{\epsilon}^{2}\operatorname{Div}_{\epsilon}\to 0.

Since we are interested in those families of critical paths for which
Z_{\epsilon}^{2}
stays uniformly away from zero, we can conclude that

\operatorname{Div}_{\epsilon}\to 0,\quad Z_{\epsilon}^{2}-k_{\epsilon}^{2}\to 0,

for a family of constants
k_{\epsilon}
, uniformly away from zero. These two convergences suffice to go back to the beginning of Section
5.7
, and start with the discussion there exactly in the same terms with limit point, and corresponding tangent vector,

\mathbf{p}\in\{\operatorname{Div}=0\},\quad\mathbf{q},\hbox{ tangent to }\{\operatorname{Div}=0\}\hbox{ at }\mathbf{p},

respectively, to conclude that there cannot be more than
(n-1)^{2}
critical paths

\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon,i,j},\quad i,j\in\{1,2,\dots,n-1\},

with

\mathbf{u}_{\epsilon,i,j}(0)\to\mathbf{p},\quad\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{\epsilon,i,j}(0)\to\mathbf{q},

regardless of the limit of their full images. If some of these images go over contact points, that would mean less critical paths. The maximum possible number is the one counted through Theorem
5.16
, and this would lead to the same upper bound.

5.9.
Counting method

Once we have proved all of the main preceding steps, it is easy to describe how to organize the counting procedure for an upper bound of the number
M_{cri}
in Problem
2.6
.
We will express our bound in terms of the following parameters, in addition to the degree
n
of the system:

•

M
: the number of connected components of the curve
\operatorname{Div}=0
;

•

N
: the number of contact points of the differential system.

Theorem 5.18
.

Under the assumptions and notation of Theorem
1.1
and Problem
2.6
,

M_{cri}\leq(n-1)^{2}(M+N),

and so
our differential system cannot have more than

1+(n-1)^{2}(M+N)

limit cycles.

Proof.

From Theorem
5.16
, we must
compute the maximum number of limit behaviors which are contained in the
components of the curve
\operatorname{Div}=0
.

Let
L\geq 0
be the number of connected components of
\operatorname{Div}=0
homeomorphic to a straight line.
Assume that the connected component
i
, for

i\in\{0,1,\ldots,L\},

contains
x_{i}\geq 0
contact
points (
x_{0}=0
always). Then it can have at most
x_{i}+1
limit behaviors. Therefore
the number of limit critical closed paths contained in the components
of
\operatorname{Div}=0
homeomorphic to a straight line is at most

L+\sum_{i=0}^{L}x_{i}.

Let
O\geq 0
be the number of connected components of
\operatorname{Div}=0
homeomorphic to an oval.
Suppose
y_{j}\geq 0
is the number of contact points in the
j
-th component, for

j\in\{0,1,\ldots,O\}
.
Note that
y_{0}=0
always. Then we can have at most

\sum_{j=0}^{O}y_{j}

different limit behaviors, all of which are bounded.

In summary, the number of limit critical closed paths contained in
the components of
\operatorname{Div}=0
is at most

L+\sum_{i=0}^{L}x_{i}+\sum_{j=0}^{O}y_{j}\leq M+N.

By Theorem
5.17
, each such possible limit behavior must
be multiplied by the corresponding multiplicity factor
(n-1)^{2}
. Hence, we
will have at most

1+(n-1)^{2}(M+N)

critical closed paths of
E_{\epsilon}
for every
\epsilon

sufficiently small.
∎

Note that in fact, the upper bound can be slightly improved to

1+(n-1)^{2}(L+N)

where
L
is the number of connected components of the curve
\operatorname{Div}=0
homeomorphic to a line.

5.10.
Non-generic situation

In this final section we treat the case of polynomial differential
systems (
1.1
) for which either the curve
\operatorname{Div}=0
has singular
points, i.e. the system

(5.40)

P_{xx}+Q_{yx}=P_{xy}+Q_{yy}=0,\quad P_{x}+Q_{y}=0

has some solutions; our initial differential system (
1.1
) has non-countable, infinitely many contact points,
i.e. system (
1.2
) has a continuum of solutions; or there are multiple solutions to the same system. Note that
equations (
5.40
) and (
1.2
) involve the partial
derivatives of
\operatorname{Div}
. Our argument revolves around the idea that vector fields
\mathbf{F}
for such systems can be uniformly approximated by a sequence
\mathbf{F}_{\delta}
of
non-singular polynomial vector fields without increasing the degree, and in
such a way that the divergence curve for
\mathbf{F}_{\delta}
has no
singularities, and finitely many simple contact points with system
(
1.1
).

We can definitely apply our previous results to the family of
functionals

\displaystyle E_{\epsilon,\delta}(\mathbf{u})=\int_{0}^{1}

\displaystyle\left[\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{F}^{\perp}_{\delta}(\mathbf{u}(t))\cdot\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t))^{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}(|\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime}(t)|^{2}+|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)|^{2}+|\mathbf{u}(t)|^{2})\right.

\displaystyle\left.+(\mathbf{u}(t)\cdot\mathbf{v}_{\delta}(t)+\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)\cdot\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{\delta}(t)+\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime}(t)\cdot\mathbf{v}^{\prime\prime}_{\delta}(t))\right.

\displaystyle\left.\frac{1}{2\epsilon}(|\mathbf{v}^{\prime\prime}_{\delta}(t)|^{2}+|\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{\delta}(t)|^{2}+|\mathbf{v}_{\delta}(t)|^{2})\right]\,dt,

where the dependence of the smooth paths
\mathbf{v}_{\delta}
on

\delta
is as regular as necessary. It is clear that the convergence

E_{\epsilon,\delta}\to E_{\epsilon}\hbox{ as }\delta\to 0,

takes place in the Haussdorf sense since parameter
\delta
is only involved in lower-order terms (
\epsilon
is fixed in this argument).

Our first relevant observation is that the discussion in Subsection
2.4
is valid regardless of whether our initial differential system (
1.1
) is generic. This means that

H(n)\leq\lim_{a\to 0}\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\#(\{E_{\epsilon}\leq a\}\cap\mathbb{O})

where, as usual

\#(\{E_{\epsilon}\leq a\}\cap\mathbb{O})

designates the number of connected components of the corresponding set.
Because of the announced convergence
E_{\epsilon,\delta}\to E_{\epsilon}
, we
would also have the convergence

\{E_{\epsilon,\delta}\leq a\}\to\{E_{\epsilon}\leq a\},\quad\delta\to 0,

in the Hausdorff distance for bounded sets.
The operator
\#
, number of connected components of sub-level sets, is lower semicontinuous with respect to this convergence, and hence

\#(\{E_{\epsilon}\leq a\}\cap\mathbb{O})\leq\lim_{\delta\to 0}\#(\{E_{\epsilon,\delta}\leq a\}\cap\mathbb{O}).

In this way,

H(n)\leq\lim_{a\to 0}\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\#(\{E_{\epsilon}\leq a\}\cap\mathbb{O})\leq\lim_{a\to 0}\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\lim_{\delta\to 0}\#(\{E_{\epsilon,\delta}\leq a\}\cap\mathbb{O}).

If approximating polynomials
\mathbf{F}_{\delta}
are generic, all of our work implies that

\lim_{a\to 0}\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\lim_{\delta\to 0}\#(\{E_{\epsilon,\delta}\leq a\}\cap\mathbb{O})\leq C(n)

where
C(n)
is our upper bound for generic polynomial vector fields of degree
n
,
and we have the same bound in terms of the degree of the system for a non-generic differential vector field
\mathbf{F}
.

Notice that we are not claiming anything about the relationship
between limit cycles of
\mathbf{F}
and limit cycles of
\mathbf{F}_{\delta}
, and their relative positions, or of
critical closed paths for
E_{\epsilon}
and of
E_{\epsilon,\delta}
.
