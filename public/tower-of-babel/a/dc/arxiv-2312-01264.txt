Zeros of the Goss zeta function
Joe Kramer-Miller and James Upton

Abstract
Let X X be a smooth proper curve over a finite field and let ∞ ∈ X \infty\in X be a closed point. Let A A be the ring of functions on X − ∞ X-\infty . The Goss zeta function ζ A \zeta_{A} of A A is an equicharacteristic analogue of the Riemann zeta function. In this article we study the zeros of ζ A \zeta_{A} under the generic condition that X X is ordinary. We prove an analogue of the Riemann hypothesis, which verifies a corrected version of a conjecture of Goss. We also show that the zeros of ζ A \zeta_{A} at negative even integers are ‘simple’ and that ζ A \zeta_{A} is nonzero at negative odd integers. This answers questions posed by Goss and Thakur. Both of these results were previously only known under the restrictive hypothesis that A A has class number one. Finally, we prove versions of these results for v v -adic interpolations of the Goss zeta function.

Introduction

The Riemann zeta function
\zeta(s)=\sum n^{-s}
is an object of fundamental importance in number theory. The question of the distribution of its zeros is the content of the famous Riemann hypothesis, which is arguably the most important question in classical number theory. Given the strong analogy between number fields and function fields, it is natural to seek analogues of
\zeta(s)
and the Riemann hypothesis in positive characteristic.

Let
X
be a smooth, projective, geometrically connected curve over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of characteristic
p
with genus
g
. Let
\infty
be a closed point in
X
, which we view as the ‘infinite place’, and let
d
be the degree of
\infty
. We let
A
denote the coordinate ring of the affine curve
X-\infty
. Let
K(X)
denote the function field of
X
and let
K_{\infty}
denote the completion of
K(X)
at the infinite place. We let
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
be the completion of an algebraic closure of
K_{\infty}
. The analogy between function-field arithmetic and classical arithmetic can be summarized as follows:

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow\mathbb{Z},

\displaystyle K(X)

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow\mathbb{Q},

\displaystyle K_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{\infty}\leftrightarrow\mathbb{C}.

With this analogy in mind, David Goss defined a
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
-valued zeta function that plays the role of
\zeta(s)
(see
[
12
]
*§8 or
[
11
]
). The Goss zeta function shares many features with the Riemann zeta function, including the existence of “trivial zeros” (see below), transcendence of certain special values for
A=\mathbb{F}_{q}[\theta]
(see
[
28
]
and
[
5
]
), and an analogue of the Herbrand-Ribet theorem
[
9
]
. This paper concerns the distribution of its zeros, and in particular the question of a
Riemann hypothesis
for the Goss zeta function.

1.1
Background on the zeta function

1.1.1
Character groups

Let
\mathbb{R}^{+}
denote the multiplicative group of positive real numbers. Every complex number
s\in\mathbb{C}
defines a character of
\mathbb{R}^{+}
by complex exponentiation
\alpha\mapsto\alpha^{s}
. This identifies the complex plane with the group
\Hom(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathbb{C}^{\times})
of continuous multiplicative characters of
\mathbb{R}^{+}
. We may regard the Riemann zeta function as a meromorphic function

\zeta:\Hom(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathbb{C}^{\times})\to\mathbb{C}.

Note that the absolute value on
\mathbb{R}
provides a natural identification
\mathbb{R}^{+}\cong\mathbb{R}^{\times}/\langle\pm 1\rangle
. This leads us to the following analogue in characteristic
p
:

Definition 1.1
.

The group of positive numbers of
K_{\infty}
is the quotient
K_{\infty}^{+}=K_{\infty}^{\times}/\mu_{\infty}
, where
\mu_{\infty}
denotes the group of roots of unity in
K_{\infty}^{\times}
.

The character theory of
K_{\infty}^{+}
is complicated by the presence of the subgroup
U_{\infty}
of
1
-units in
K_{\infty}^{\times}
. The character group of
U_{\infty}
is enormous, since
U_{\infty}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{\mathbb{Z}}
. The Goss zeta function is defined on a subset of characters of
K_{\infty}^{+}
having a particularly simple action on
U_{\infty}
: Fix a uniformizer
\pi\in K_{\infty}^{+}
. Then
\pi
determines a factorization

K_{\infty}^{\times}\cong\mu_{\infty}\times\pi^{\mathbb{Z}}\times U_{\infty},

and we may identify
K_{\infty}^{+}
with the subgroup
1\times\pi^{\mathbb{Z}}\times U_{\infty}
. Accordingly, every
\alpha\in K_{\infty}^{+}
factors uniquely

\alpha=\pi^{v_{\infty}(\alpha)}\cdot\langle\alpha\rangle_{\pi},

(1)

where
\langle\alpha\rangle_{\pi}\in U_{\infty}
. Let us write
\deg(\alpha)=\deg(\infty)\cdot v_{\infty}(\alpha)
.

Definition 1.2
.

The
\pi
-adic
Goss plane
is the topological group
\mathbf{S}_{\pi}=\mathbb{C}_{\infty}^{\times}\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. For
s=(x,y)\in\mathbf{S}_{\pi}
, the action of
s
on a positive number
\alpha\in K_{\infty}^{+}
is given by

\alpha^{s}=x^{\deg(\alpha)}\langle\alpha\rangle_{\pi}^{y}\in\mathbb{C}_{\infty}^{\times}.

1.1.2
The zeta function

Let
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq A
be a non-zero ideal. If
\mathfrak{a}
is principal, then every generator of
\mathfrak{a}
determines the same positive number
\alpha\in K_{\infty}^{+}
(analogously to the fact that every non-zero ideal
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
has a unique positive generator). Thus, we may regard
\mathbf{S}_{\pi}
as a group of characters for the group of non-zero principal ideals of
A
. This action of
\mathbf{S}_{\pi}
on principal ideals extends uniquely to an action on the group of
all
non-zero fractional ideals of
A
(see §2 below or
[
12
]
[§8.2]).

Definition 1.3
.

The
\pi
-adic Goss zeta function of
A
is the function on
\mathbf{S}_{\pi}
defined by the equivalent formal expressions

\zeta_{A,\pi}(s):=\sum_{\mathfrak{a}}\mathfrak{a}^{-s}=\prod_{\mathfrak{p}}\frac{1}{1-\mathfrak{p}^{-s}},

where the sum is taken over non-zero ideals
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq A
, and the product is taken over non-zero prime ideals
\mathfrak{p}\subset A
.
This sum is known to be convergent for all
s\in\mathbf{S}_{\pi}
. Furthermore, if
y\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, then
\zeta_{A,\pi}(-,y)
is analytic on
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
.

Remark 1.4
.

In §
2
we present a “coordinate-free” definition of the zeta function denoted by
\zeta_{A}
. Its domain is a topological group, which we denote by
\mathbf{S}_{A}
. The choice of uniformizer
\pi
provides an isomorphism
\mathbf{S}_{A}\cong\mathbf{S}_{\pi}
, which identifies
\zeta_{A}
with
\zeta_{A,\pi}
.

1.1.3
A theory of the zeros

The zeros of the Riemann zeta function
\zeta(s)
can be put into two camps: the trivial zeros at negative integers and those that have (conjecturally) real part
\frac{1}{2}
. It is therefore natural to ask about the ‘real parts’ of zeros of the Goss zeta function and to ask about vanishing at ‘special values’.

The real part of zeros

Recall that if
s\in\mathbb{C}
, then for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
we have
|\alpha^{s}|=|\alpha|^{\mathrm{Re}(s)}
. Similarly, if
s\in\mathbf{S}_{\infty}
then there exists a unique real number
r(s)\in\mathbb{R}
such that for any
\alpha\in K_{\infty}^{+}
, we have

|\alpha^{s}|=|\alpha|^{r(s)}.

We regard the parameter
r(s)
as the characteristic
p
analogue of the real part of a complex number. In coordinates
s=(x,y)
, we have

r(s)=dv_{\infty}(x).

(2)

Thus, for
y\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
it is natural to consider the multiset

R_{A}(y)=\{r(s):\zeta_{A}(s)=0\text{ and }y(s)=y\},

where
y(s)
denotes the
y
-coordinate of
s
. The set
R_{A}(y)

consists of “real parts” of the zeros of
\zeta_{A}
with a given
y
-parameter counted with multiplicity. In light of (
2
), knowledge of
R_{A}(y)
is equivalent to that of the
Newton polygon
of the entire function
\zeta_{A,\pi}(-,y)
on
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}^{\times}
. The absence of a functional equation for
\zeta_{A}
makes it unclear what a characteristic
p
Riemann hypothesis should look like.

Progress in this direction began with Wan
[
27
]
, who computed the
R_{A}(y)
for
A=\mathbb{F}_{p}[\theta]
, and showed that each “real part” occurs with multiplicity
1
. As a corollary to Wan’s theorem, one sees that the zeros of
\zeta_{A,\pi}(-,y)
are all simple and lie in
K_{\infty}
. In
[
13
]
Goss conjectured that, barring finitely many exceptions, the elements of
R_{A}(y)
should have multiplicity
1
. Evidence for this conjecture was given by work of Diaz-Vargas
[
8
]
, Poonen, and Sheats
[
23
]
, who verify the conjecture for the affine line over
any
finite field.

Beyond the genus
0
case, evidence is sparse: Böckle
[
4
]
studies the
R_{A}(y)
in the special case
A=\mathbb{F}_{2}[x,y]/(y^{2}+y+x^{3}+x+1)
, which corresponds to a supersingular elliptic curve with class number one. He shows that this set agrees exactly with the affine line case, except that the smallest “real part” occurs with multiplicity
2
. Similar calculations were carried out in the thesis of Yujia Qui in the case where
A
has class number one. Excluding the affine line, there are exactly eight such
A
in all characteristics.

Vanishing at special values

We may also consider special values of
\zeta_{A,\pi}(s)
at negative integers.
The question of vanishing at odd integers was posed by Thakur in
[
26
]
. In this article Thakur proved that the special values at odd negative integers are nonzero when
A
has class number one. Beyond this, little is known. The story for
even integers is more developed. David Goss discovered ‘trivial zeros’ occurring at negative even integers. These trivial zeros arise from congruences between the zeta functions
\zeta_{A,\pi}(-,y)
and certain
L
-series in characteristic
0
, which have an extra Euler factor at
\infty
. Thus, trivial zeros come from an extra factor at
\infty
, analogous to what happens with the Riemann zeta function.

Using a limiting argument with trivial zeros, Goss found that for a delicately constructed
y
, infinitely many elements of
R_{A}(y)
occur with multiplicity
\geq d
(the corresponding zeros of
\zeta_{A}
are called
near-trivial
). The existence of near-trivial zeros provides a counterexample to Goss’s original conjecture on multiplicities occuring in
R_{A}(y)
. To complicate matters further, in
[
26
]
Thakur observed that the order of vanishing at a trivial zero could exceed the order of vanishing coming from the “missing” Euler factor at
\infty
(see
[
7
]
for further examples of this phenomenon). Such trivial zeros are called
irregular
. The existence of irregular trivial zeros opens the possibility of the near-trivial zeros having high orders of vanishing. In
[
14
]
, Goss states a conjecture that would prohibit this type of degenerate behavior.

1.2
Main results

The purpose of this article is to study the
R_{A}(y)
under the generic assumption that
X
is an ordinary curve. Under this assumption, we completely determine the
R_{A}(y)
in terms of the genus
g
of
X
, the degree of
\infty
, the cardinality
r
of the residue field at
\infty
, and the
p
-adic digits of
y
. This result may be regarded as a corrected version of Goss’s original conjecture. Our calculation immediately implies that the trivial zeros always have the ‘correct multiplicity’. That is, we verify Goss’s conjecture on irregular trivial zeros under the generic assumption that
X
is ordinary. We also deduce nonvanishing at negative odd integers. Finally, we prove partial analogues of these results for the
v
-adic interpolations of
\zeta_{A}
at the finite places
v
of
A
.

1.2.1
Distribution of the zeros

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
r=q^{d}
. Fix
y\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. There exists a (possibly finite) increasing sequence of positive integers
\alpha_{r,1}<\alpha_{r,2}<\cdots
, depending only on
y
and
r
, such that for
any
ordinary curve
X
, we have

R_{A}(y)=\{\underbrace{0,\dots,0}_{g-1+d}\}\sqcup\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{\infty}\Bigl\{\underbrace{\alpha_{r,i}(r-1),\dots,\alpha_{r,i}(r-1)}_{d\text{ times}}\Bigr\}.

(3)

In the special case
A=\mathbb{F}_{q}[\theta]
, Theorem
1.5
is due to Wan, Diaz-Vargas, Poonen, and Sheats. In this previous work it is shown that the numbers
\alpha_{r,i}
are related to the
r
-adic digit expansion of
y
. One can deduce from their results that each “real part” is divisible by
r-1
, although it is not explicitly stated. We note that our proof of Theorem
1.5
is completely independent of this prior work. In particular, we give a new proof of the Riemann hypothesis for the affine line.

Remark 1.6
.

When
X
is non-ordinary, we expect that a similar result holds. In particular, we expect that:

R_{A}(y)=\{\underbrace{s_{1},\dots,s_{g-1+d}}\}\sqcup\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{\infty}\Bigl\{\underbrace{\alpha_{r,i}(r-1),\dots,\alpha_{r,i}(r-1)}_{d\text{ times}}\Bigr\},

where we have
0\leq s_{i}\leq\alpha_{r,1}
.
In fact, one can prove that the number of
s_{i}
’s that are equal to zero is

p(X)+d-1
, where
p(X)
denotes the
p
-rank of
X
. Our expectation is
in agreement with the calculations in
[
4
]
.

1.2.2

For

there is a natural
j
-th power character of
K_{\infty}^{+}
:
it sends
\alpha\in K_{\infty}^{+}
to
\alpha^{j}\in\mathbb{C}_{\infty}

K_{\infty}^{+}
to an element of our character group
\mathbf{S}_{\pi}

\pi_{*}
to be a
d
-th root of
\pi

s_{j}=(\pi_{*}^{-j},j)\in\mathbf{S}_{\pi}

\alpha\in K_{\infty}^{+}

\alpha^{s_{j}}=\alpha^{j}

s_{j}
is really an extension of the
j
-th power map on
K_{\infty}^{+}
. We say
s_{j}
is even if

and odd otherwise. We are concerned with
the vanishing of the special values
\zeta_{A,\pi}(s_{j})
.

1

1

For each

\frac{d}{p^{v_{p}(d)}}

Consider the function defined on

This is known to be a polynomial in

(1-x^{-d})|z_{\pi,s_{j}}(x).

This implies that

Corollary 1.7

Suppose that
X

Proof.

From Theorem

we know that each ‘real part’ of the zeros occurs with multiplicity

. For
j

we see
that these

Let

1.3
Outline and summary

1.3.1
Summary of the proof

The first step is to understand the zeta function as a family of
L
-functions
of Galois characters.
More precisely, for fixed
y\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
there exists a character

\rho_{A,\pi}^{\otimes y}:\pi_{1}^{\text{\'{e}t}}(\mathrm{Spec}(A))\to\mathbb{C}_{\infty}^{\times}

such that
\zeta_{A,\pi}(-,y)=L(\rho_{A,\pi}^{\otimes y},-)
.
This vantage has been utilized when studying questions of meromorphicity of equicharacteristic zeta functions (see e.g.
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
, and
[
24
]
).
An essential observation is that the localization of
\rho_{A,\pi}^{\otimes y}

to
\mathrm{Spec}(K_{\infty})
only depends
y
and the residue field at
\infty
(see Corollary
2.16
). In particular, we see that
\rho_{A,\pi}^{\otimes y}
and
\rho_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta],\pi}^{\otimes y}
have the same localizations at
\infty
. This allows us to relate
the zeta function of
A
to the zeta function of the affine line.

The next step is to give a new proof of the Riemann hypothesis for the affine line
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Using the Anderson-Monsky trace formula, for fixed
y\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
we can compute

\zeta_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta],\pi}(-,y)
in terms of the “Fredholm determinant”
\det(1-x\Theta_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta]}|M_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta]})

of a certain compact operator
\Theta_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta]}
on a
\pi
-adic Banach space
M_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta]}
. Both the operator
\Theta_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta]}
and the space
M_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta]}
are determined by
\rho_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta],\pi}^{\otimes y}
. To study this Fredholm determinant, we choose an orthonormal basis of
M_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta]}
indexed by a countable set
I
and write
\Theta_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta]}
as an
I\times I
matrix
\Psi
. The Fredholm determinant has an explicit description:

\displaystyle\det(1-s\Psi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}s^{n},

\displaystyle c_{n}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle S\subset I}}{{|S|=n}}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathrm{Sym}(S)}\text{sgn}(\sigma)\prod_{i\in S}\Psi_{i,\sigma(i)},

where
\Psi_{i,j}
denotes the
(i,j)
-th entry of
\Psi
.
The heart of the proof of Theorem
1.5
for
\mathbb{F}_{q}[\theta]
is
proving that there is a unique term in the sum defining
c_{n}
whose
\pi
-adic
valuation is minimal.
In the ‘toy case’ (i.e.
\mathbb{F}_{p}[\theta]
), this is Corollary
5.8
whose proof is straightforward. The general case is Theorem
6.11
and the proof
is a difficult combinatorial argument. The proof of Theorem
6.11
is the most technical part of this article . Once we establish there is a unique term of minimal valuation, we can pinpoint the
\pi
-adic valuation of
c_{n}
and determine the Newton polygon of
\zeta_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta],\pi}(-,y)
.

Finally, we address the zeta function for general
A
. As in the affine line case,
we compute
\zeta_{A,\pi}(-,y)
by computing
\det(1-x\Theta_{A}|M_{A})
via the trace formula. In general it is difficult to make sense of both the operator
\Theta_{A}
and the space
M_{A}
when
X
has positive genus.
To overcome this difficulty, we use a ‘local twisting’ trick, which is similar to a technique introduced by the first author in
[
19
]
and
[
20
]
to study exponential sums. The rough idea is that
M_{A}
and
M_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta]}
are both included in an overspace
V
.
Both operators
\Theta_{A}
and
\Theta_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta]}
extend to all of
V
in a natural way and are conjugate, i.e. there is an invertible operator
D
with
D\Theta_{A}D^{-1}=\Theta_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta]}
. The conjugacy of
\Theta_{A}
and
\Theta_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta]}
is a consequence of the fact that
\rho_{A,\pi}^{\otimes y}
and
\rho_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta],\pi}^{\otimes y}
have the same localizations at
\infty
.
Then we have

\det(1-x\Theta_{A}|M_{A})=\det(1-x\Theta_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta]}|DM_{A}).

Thus, to compute
\zeta_{A,\pi}(-,y)
we are studying the same operator
\Theta_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta]}
that we used to study
\zeta_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta],\pi}(-,y)
. The difference is that this operator is acting on different subspaces of
V
.
The matrix of
\Theta_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta]}
acting on
DM_{A}
looks very similar to the matrix of
\Theta_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta]}
acting on
M_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta]}
. We can then exploit our understanding of
\Theta_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta]}
acting on
M_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta]}
from studying
\zeta_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta],\pi}(-,y)
.

1.3.2
Outline of article

In §
2
we introduce the Goss zeta function
\zeta_{A,\pi}
and establish some basic properties. Our construction
is a modification of the standard one (see e.g.
[
11
]
), but has the advantage of being coordinate free. In particular, it does not depend on
the choice of parameter
\pi
. In §
2.2
we see that for any uniformizer
\pi\in K_{\infty}
, the function
\zeta_{A,\pi}(-,y)
is the
L
-function of a character

\rho_{A,\pi}^{\otimes y}:\pi_{1}^{\text{\'{e}t}}(\mathrm{Spec}(A))\to\mathbb{C}_{\infty}^{\times}.

Let
\rho_{\infty,\pi}^{\otimes y}
denote the localization of
\rho_{A,\pi}^{\otimes y}
along
\mathrm{Spec}(K_{\infty})\to\mathrm{Spec}(A)
. Our key observation is that
\rho_{\infty,\pi}^{\otimes y}
depends only on
\pi
and
r
–not on
A
. More precisely: let
\theta\in K_{\infty}
be any element of valuation
-1
, so that
K_{\infty}=\mathbb{F}_{r}(\!(\theta^{-1})\!)
. Associated to the zeta function
\zeta_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta],\pi}
of the affine line we have a character

\rho_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta],\pi}^{\otimes y}:\pi_{1}^{\text{\'{e}t}}(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{F}_{r}}^{1})\to\mathbb{C}_{\infty}^{\times}.

Then the localization of this character at
\infty
agrees with that of
\rho_{A,\pi}^{\otimes y}
. Finally, in §
2.3
we construct the
v
-adic zeta function on
\mathbf{S}_{A,v}^{0}
and prove Theorem
1.10

In §
3
we give a summary of the theory of
F
-modules and
\tau
-modules. We explain the Katz equivalence between étale
\tau
-modules and representations of the étale fundamental group. Finally, we review the Anderson-Monsky trace formula for curves. This formula allows us to express the
L
-function as the characteristic series of an operator on an infinite dimensional space.

The remainder of the paper is dedicated to proving Theorem
1.5
. In §
5
we first consider the ‘toy case’ where
X
is defined
over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
and
\infty
is a rational point. This is done solely for expository purposes and the later sections have no logical dependence on §
5
. The proof of the ‘toy case’ uses the same strategy and insights, while avoiding the technical combinatorial arguments present in the general case. In
§
6
we prove Theorem
1.5

for the affine line over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
using the Anderson-Monsky trace formula. This
section is the most technical part of the paper, although our proof is significantly shorter than Sheats’ proof of the Riemann hypothesis for
\mathbb{F}_{q}[\theta]
. Finally, in §
7
the proof
of Theorem
1.5
is proven in full generality.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\pi\in K_{P}
be a uniformizer. We define the
\pi
-adic
1
-unit character
to be the character
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\pi}:K_{P}^{+}\to U_{P}
defined by the formula

\langle\alpha\rangle_{\pi}=\frac{\alpha}{\pi^{v_{P}(\alpha)}}\in U_{P}.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let

be a positive integer. Let
L/K_{P}
be a finite extension. Let
\alpha\in K_{P}^{+}
such that

. Then there exists a finite purely inseparable extension

such that
\alpha^{1/n}\in L_{1}^{+}
.

Proof.

The condition on
v_{L}(\alpha)
ensures that
\alpha/\langle\alpha\rangle_{\pi}
has an
n
th root in
L
. It suffices then to consider
\alpha\in U_{P}
. Factor

. Since

and

is naturally a

-module, we have
\alpha^{1/n^{\mathrm{t}}}\in U_{P}
. The claim follows from:

\alpha^{1/n}=(\alpha^{1/n^{\mathrm{t}}})^{1/p^{v_{p}(n)}}\in K_{P}^{1/p^{v_{p}(n)}}.

∎

Definition 2.4
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{C}_{P}^{\times}

s_{x}\in\Hom(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}_{p}^{\times})

s_{x}(n)=x^{-n}.

The map

gives an isomorphism of
\mathbb{C}_{P}^{\times}
with
\Hom(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}_{P}^{\times})
. As discussed in the introduction, the character group
\Hom(U_{P},\mathbb{C}_{P}^{\times})
is very large. For this reason we restrict our attention to characters of a special type:

Definition 2.5
.

The
Goss plane
over
L
is the group
\mathbf{S}_{L}
of characters

with the following property: There exists a

such that the restriction of

to
U_{L}
is the
y(s)
-th power map. The group operation on
\mathbf{S}_{L}
will be written additively. For
s\in\mathbf{S}_{L}
, we will write
\alpha^{s}
for the value of
s
at a positive number
\alpha\in L^{+}
.

The next lemma introduces the “real part” map on
\mathbf{S}_{L}
as described in the introduction.

Lemma 2.6
.

There exists a unique homomorphism
r:\mathbf{S}_{L}\to\mathbb{Q}
with the following property: For any
\alpha\in L^{+}
, we have

v_{L}(\alpha^{s})=r(s)\cdot v_{L}(\alpha).

Proof.

Every character
s\in\mathbf{S}_{L}
maps
U_{L}
into the unit circle
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{C}_{P}}^{\times}
in
\mathbb{C}_{P}
. The valuation
v_{L}
induces isomorphisms
L^{+}/U_{L}\cong\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathbb{C}_{P}^{+}/\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{C}_{P}}^{\times}\cong\mathbb{Q}
. The desired map is:

\mathbf{S}_{L}\to\Hom(L^{+}/U_{L},\mathbb{C}_{P}^{+}/\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{C}_{P}}^{\times})\cong\Hom(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{Q})\cong\mathbb{Q}.

∎

2.1.3
Coordinates

Let
L
be a finite extension of
K_{P}
. We will now explain how one can obtain a “coordinate picture” of the Goss plane
\mathbf{S}_{L}
by fixing a choice of uniformizer
\pi\in K_{P}
. Recall that
\pi
determines a
1
-unit character
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\pi}\in\mathbf{S}_{P}
.

Proposition 2.8
.

The
1
-unit character extends uniquely to a character
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\pi}
in
\mathbf{S}_{L}
with values in
U_{\mathbb{C}_{P}}
.

Proof.

Note that any extension of
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\pi}
in
\mathbf{S}_{L}
must act as the identity on
U_{L}
. Let
e=e_{L/K_{P}}
. For any
\alpha\in L^{+}
, we have
v_{P}(\alpha^{e})\in\mathbb{Z}
. Since
\langle\pi\rangle_{\pi}=1
, we must have

\langle\alpha^{e}\rangle_{\pi}=\frac{\alpha^{e}}{\pi^{v_{P}(\alpha^{e})}}\in U_{L}.

Then
\langle\alpha\rangle_{\pi}=\langle\alpha^{e}\rangle_{\pi}^{1/e}\in U_{\mathbb{C}_{P}}
provides the unique extension of
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\pi}
.
∎

In light of Proposition
2.8
, the choice of
\pi
provides a section of the natural map
\mathbf{S}_{L}\to\mathbb{Z}_{p}
given by
y\mapsto\langle\cdot\rangle_{\pi}^{y}
. Thus, the choice of
\pi
determines a splitting

\mathbf{S}_{L}\cong\mathbb{C}_{P}^{\times}\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}.

Notation 2.9
.

For
s\in\mathbf{S}_{L}
, the corresponding pair
(x,y)\in\mathbb{C}_{P}^{\times}\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}
will be called the
\pi
-adic coordinates
of
s
. The action of
s
in terms of its coordinates is given by:

\alpha^{s}=x^{-v_{L}(\alpha)}\langle\alpha\rangle_{\pi}^{y}.

Remark 2.10
.

Although
\mathbf{S}_{L}
depends up to canonical isomorphism only on
e_{L/K_{P}}^{\mathrm{t}}

9
) and the
\pi
-adic coordinates on
\mathbf{S}_{L}
depend on
e_{L/K_{P}}
.

2.2
The Goss zeta function

2.2.1
Divisors

As a first step in defining the Goss zeta function, we will explain how for certain
L
, we can regard
\mathbf{S}_{L}
as a character groups on classes of divisors on
X
. Our starting point is the canonical divisor exact sequence:

0\to K^{\times}/\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\to\Div^{0}(X)\to\Pic^{0}(X)\to 0.

Since
\Pic^{0}(X)
is finite, we have an induced isomorphism between the
rational
divisor groups
\mathbb{Q}\otimes K^{\times}/\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\cong\mathbb{Q}\otimes\Div^{0}(X)
. Because
\mathbb{C}_{P}^{+}
is uniquely divisible, the natural embedding
K^{\times}/\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\hookrightarrow K_{P}^{+}
extends uniquely to a map

\mathbb{Q}\otimes\Div^{0}(X)\to\mathbb{C}_{P}^{+}.

(10)

The next observation follows immediately from Lemma
2.3
and the finiteness of
\Pic^{0}(X)
.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
L
be a finite extension of
K_{P}
. There is a finite purely inseparable extension
L_{1}/L
such that (
10
) restricts to a map

\psi:\frac{1}{e_{L/K_{P}}}\Div^{0}(X)\to L_{1}^{+}.

Suppose now that
D\in\Div(X-P)
is prime to
P
, but not necessarily of degree
0
. We can associate to
D
a rational divisor of degree
0
as follows:

D_{P}:=D-\frac{\deg(D)}{\deg(P)}P\in\frac{1}{\deg(P)}\Div^{0}(X).

In particular, if
L/K_{P}
is sufficiently ramified, by Lemma
2.11
we obtain a map
\Div^{0}(X-P)\to L_{1}^{+}
sending
D\mapsto\psi(D_{P})
, where
L_{1}
is a finite purely inseparable extension of
L
.
Then from Proposition
2.7
we can regard
\mathbf{S}_{L}

as a group of characters on
\Div^{0}(X-P)
.

2.2.2
Definitions

Recall that
A
denotes the coordinate ring of the affine curve
X-\infty
. We now introduce our main character group of interest:

Definition 2.12
.

We define the group
\mathbf{S}_{A}=\mathbf{S}_{L}
, where
L
is any finite extension of
K_{P}
with ramification index
d
.

Note that
\mathbf{S}_{A}
depends up to canonical isomorphism only on
d^{\mathrm{t}}=\frac{d}{p^{v_{p}(d)}}

d
. As explained above, the characters in
\mathbf{S}_{A}
act on the non-zero fractional ideals of
A
(regarded as divisors in
\Div(X-\infty)
). Explicitly: If
s\in\mathbf{S}_{A}
has
\pi
-adic coordinates

4.1

4.1.1

Let

be an

\mathbf{V}_{\infty}
. For any subsets

-th entry is

. In light of the product formula (

will mainly be concerned with the following notion:

Definition 4.1
.

An

of
I
is a pair
(S,\sigma)
, where
S\subseteq I
is a finite subset and
\sigma
is a permutation of
S
. When there is no risk of confusion, we will often omit the underlying subset and refer to
(S,\sigma)

\sigma

If
(S,\sigma)
is an enriched permmutation of

then we can extend

to a permutation of

for

. If
(T,\tau)
is a second enriched permutation of

, then we may form the composite enriched permutation

. We will say that
\sigma
and
\tau
are
disjoint
if
S
and
T
are disjoint.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
(S,\sigma)
be an enriched permutation of
I
. We define

v(\sigma,\Phi)=v_{\pi}\left(\prod_{i\in S}\Phi_{i,\sigma(i)}\right)=\sum_{i\in S}v_{\pi}(\Phi_{i,\sigma(i)}).

4.1.2
Rotational permutations

Let
X
be a set. We will frequently encounter matrices indexed by sets of the form

. For any such

let us write

\mathbf{i}({k_{2}})=\mathbf{i}({k_{1}})-1
in
\mathbb{Z}/b\mathbb{Z}
. If
\Phi
is block cyclic, then for many enriched permutations of

the corresponding term of (

.

We say that an enriched permuation

\mathbf{i}({\sigma(k)})=\mathbf{i}({k})-1

6.1
Digit Sequences

As we will see, the Newton slopes of
\zeta_{A,\pi}(-,y)
are closely connected to the
q
-adic digits of
y
. We collect here some basic definitions concerning digit sums and their growth.

6.1.1
Basic Definitions

Let
y\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. Let us decompose
y
as follows:

y=\sum_{i=1}^{b}p^{i-1}y_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{b}p^{i-1}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}y_{i,j}q^{j},

(22)

where
0\leq y_{i,j}<p
.

Definition 6.1
.

We say that
y
is
q
-full
if each
y_{i}
is not a positive integer.

Convention 1
.

We assume
y
is
q
-full unless explicitly stated otherwise.

The condition that
y
is
q
-full guarantees that the partial sums of the
q
-adic digits of each
y_{i}
tend to infinity. This allows us to introduce the following important sequence:

Definition 6.2
.

Let
n
be a positive integer. We set
d_{i}(n)=p^{i-1}q^{w}
, where
w
is the unique positive integer such that

\sum_{j=0}^{w-1}y_{i,j}<n\leq\sum_{j=0}^{w}y_{i,j}.

Note that each
d_{i}
is a non-decreasing sequence of powers of
p
that tend to infinity. Moreover, we have

y_{i}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}d_{i}(n).

Definition 6.3
.

Let
m
be an integer. We define the partial sums

y_{i}(m)=\sum_{n=1}^{m}d_{i}(n).

Note that if
m\leq 0
, the sum for
y_{i}(m)
is empty and we have
y_{i}(m)=0
. In addition, for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}
we define

y_{i}(m,k):=y_{i}(m+k)-y_{i}(m).

Finally, we make the following evident but important observation:

y_{i}(m,k_{1}+k_{2})\geq y_{i}(m,k_{1})+y_{i}(m,k_{2})\text{ if $k_{1},k_{2}\geq 0$ or $k_{1},k_{2}\leq 0$}.

(23)

6.1.2
Growth of Digit Sequences

The sequences
d_{i}(n)
grow very rapidly with
n
. The next few lemmas quantify this rapid growth.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
n
be a positive integer. Let
m
and
k
be non-negative integers. Then

1.

d_{i}(n+p-1)\geq qd_{i}(n)
.

2.

y_{i}(m+p-1,k)\geq qy_{i}(m,k)
.

3.

y_{i}(m+p-1)>qy_{i}(m)
.

Proof.

The first claim is clear from the definition of
d_{i}
. If
k
is non-negative, then

y_{i}(m+p-1,k)=\sum_{n=m+p}^{m+p-1+k}d_{i}(n)=\sum_{n=m+1}^{m+k}d_{i}(n+p-1)\geq q\sum_{n=m+1}^{m+k}d_{i}(m)=qy_{i}(m,k).

For the final claim, note that if
m=0
we have
y_{i}(m)=0
, which immediately gives the inequality. For
m>0
we have

y_{i}(m+p-1)=y_{i}(p-1,m)+y_{i}(p-1)\geq qy_{i}(0,m)+y_{i}(p-1)>qy_{i}(m).

∎

Lemma 6.5
.

Assume
b>1
Let
n
be a positive integer. Then

pd_{i}(n)>\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}y_{i}(n-k(p-1)).

Proof.

Write
d_{i}(n)=p^{i-1}q^{w}
. For each
0\leq m\leq w-k
, the term
p^{i-1}q^{m}
appears in the partial sum for
y_{i}(n-k(p-1))
at most
p-1
times. Thus:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}y_{i}(n-k(p-1))

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{w}\sum_{m=0}^{w-k}p^{i-1}(p-1)q^{m}

\displaystyle=p^{i-1}\frac{p-1}{q-1}\sum_{k=0}^{w}(q^{w-k+1}-1)

\displaystyle<p^{i-1}\frac{p-1}{(q-1)^{2}}q^{w+2}

\displaystyle\leq pp^{i-1}q^{w}=pd_{i}(n),

where we have used the fact that
\frac{p-1}{(q-1)^{2}}<\frac{p}{q^{2}}
for
b>1
. ∎

Proposition 6.24
.

Assume

\Sigma_{n}^{*}(\vec{m})
in

R
-value.
Furthermore, these permutations satisfy the recurrence

\Sigma_{n}^{*}(\vec{m})=\Sigma_{n-1}^{*}(\sigma_{n}^{*}(\vec{m})-\vec{1})\sigma_{n}^{*}(\vec{m}).

(33)

The proof of Proposition
6.24
proceeds by induction on

following from Corollary
6.22
. Let
\tau
be an element of

-value. It is clear that

\tau_{n}\neq\sigma_{n}^{*}(\vec{m})

\displaystyle R(\tau_{n})-R(\sigma_{n}^{*}(\vec{m}))>R(\Sigma)-R(\Sigma_{n-1}^{*}(\tau_{n}-\vec{1}))=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\Big[R(\sigma_{j})-R(\tau_{j})\Big].

6.5.3

For the estimates below it is convenient introduce some additional notation: Let

6.5.4
Bounding

Our first goal is to show that
\tau_{n}\geq\sigma_{n}^{*}(\vec{m})

Lemma 6.25

with equality if and only if
\tau_{n}=\tau_{n}^{+}
.

Proof.

Define
\vec{z}^{-}=\vec{z}-\vec{z}^{+}
and write
\vec{z}^{-}=(z^{-}_{1},\dots,z^{-}_{b})
. Note that all the coordinates of
\vec{z}^{-}
are non-positive. Set

, which is contained in

. Note that
\tau_{n}=\tau_{n}^{+}
if and only if the coordinates of
\vec{z}^{-}
are all

. Since
\sigma_{n}^{*}(\vec{m})
is the unique

-minimal
b
-cycle in

we have

\displaystyle R(\tau_{n}^{-})-R(\sigma_{n}^{*}(\vec{m}))\geq 0,

(36)

and equality occurs if and only if
\tau_{n}=\tau_{n}^{+}
.
We claim that for each

and

must either both be non-negative or both be non-positive. If

is positive,

Using (

\displaystyle R(\tau_{n})

\displaystyle=R(\sigma_{n}^{*}(\vec{m})+\vec{z})

\displaystyle=R(\sigma_{n}^{*}(\vec{m}))+\sum_{i=1}^{b}y_{i}(\sigma_{n}^{*}(\vec{m}),\vec{z}^{+}+\vec{z}^{-})

\displaystyle\geq R(\sigma_{n}^{*}(\vec{m}))+\sum_{i=1}^{b}\left(y_{i}(\sigma_{n}^{*}(\vec{m}),\vec{z}^{+})+y_{i}(\sigma_{n}^{*}(\vec{m}),\vec{z}^{-})\right)

\displaystyle=R(\sigma_{n}^{*}(\vec{m}))+[R(\tau_{n}^{+})-R(\sigma_{n}^{*}(\vec{m}))]+[R(\tau_{n}^{-})-R(\sigma_{n}^{*}(\vec{m}))]

\displaystyle=R(\tau_{n}^{+})+R(\tau_{n}^{-})-R(\sigma_{n}^{*}(\vec{m}))\geq R(\tau_{n}^{+}).

This gives the desired inequality. Next, observe that

and
\tau_{n}^{+}

is

with minimal

-value.

Proof of Claim

To prove the second claim we first need a quick lemma.

Lemma 6.29

Since

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in N^{\pm}(\vec{r})}y_{i}(\sigma_{n}^{*}(\vec{m}),\vec{r})

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in N^{+}(\vec{r})}y_{i}(\sigma_{n}^{*}(\vec{m}),\vec{r})

\displaystyle\beta(\vec{r})

\displaystyle=\beta(\vec{r}_{1})+\beta(\vec{r}_{2}).

This allows us to reduce to the case where
r_{j}
is positive for
k<j<k^{\prime}
and zero otherwise (i.e.
\vec{r}

has one contiguous sequence of positive numbers surrounded by all zeros). The proof will proceed by induction on
m=\max\{r_{i}\}
. We will only consider the case where
\vec{r}=(r_{1},\dots,r_{k},0,\dots,0)
and each
r_{i}>0
for
i=1,\dots,k
. The proof of the general case is identical but involves cumbersome notation.

Our base case
is when
m=1
, so that

\vec{r}=\vec{1}_{k}:=(r_{1},\dots,r_{k},0,\dots,0),

and each
r_{i}=1
.
We prove this by induction on
k
. When
k=1
we have

N^{+}(\vec{r})=\{1\}
and
N^{-}(\vec{r})=\{2\}
. Then by
Lemma
6.29
we have

d_{1}(pc_{1}-c_{0}+p)=d_{1}(\vec{c}+\vec{r})\geq pd_{2}(\vec{c}).

By definition of
d_{1}
we have

\displaystyle d_{1}(pc_{1}-c_{0}+p-1)

\displaystyle\geq qd_{1}(pc_{1}-c_{0})=qd_{1}(\vec{c}).

This implies (
40
).
Now consider
k>1
. Then we have
N^{-}(\vec{r})=\{k+1\}
and

N^{+}(\vec{r})=\{1,\dots,k\}
. By Lemma
6.29
there exists
i_{0}\in\{1,\dots,k\}
such that

d_{i_{0}}(\vec{c}+\vec{r})\geq pd_{k+1}(\vec{c})
. If
i_{0}=1
, since

y_{i}(\vec{c},p-1)>qd_{i}(\vec{c})
for
i=1,\dots,k
we have

\sum_{i=1}^{k}y_{i}(\vec{c},\vec{r})=d_{1}(pc_{1}-c_{0}+p)+\sum_{i=1}^{k}y_{i}(\vec{c},p-1)>\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}pd_{i}(\vec{c}),

which implies (
40
).
Finally, assume
1<i_{0}\leq k
. By our inductive hypothesis,
we know that (
40
) holds
for
\vec{1}_{i_{0}-1}
, so that

\sum_{i=1}^{i_{0}-1}y_{i}(\vec{c},\vec{r})=\sum_{i=1}^{i_{0}-1}y_{i}(\vec{c},\vec{1}_{i_{0}})>\sum_{i=1}^{i_{0}-1}pd_{i}(\vec{c}-\vec{1})+pd_{i_{0}}(\vec{c})>\sum_{i=1}^{i_{0}}pd_{i}(\vec{c}-\vec{1}).

For
i_{0}<i\leq k
we have
y_{i}(\vec{c},\vec{r})=y_{i}(\vec{c},p-1)
,
which gives

\sum_{i=i_{0}+1}^{k}y_{i}(\vec{c},\vec{r})=\sum_{i=i_{0}+1}^{k}y_{i}(\vec{c},p-1)>\sum_{i=i_{0}+1}^{k}pd_{i}(\vec{c}-\vec{1}).

Putting this all together we have

\sum_{i=1}^{k}y_{i}(\vec{c},\vec{r})=\sum_{i=1}^{i_{0}-1}y_{i}(\vec{c},\vec{r})+y_{i_{0}}(\vec{c},\vec{r})+\sum_{i=i_{0}+1}^{k}y_{i}(\vec{c},\vec{r})>\sum_{i=1}^{k}pd_{i}(\vec{c}-\vec{1})+pd_{k+1}(\vec{c}),

which gives (
40
).

Consider a general
\vec{r}=(r_{1},\dots,r_{k},0,\dots,0)
with
r_{i}>0
. Set
m=\max\{r_{i}\}
. Assume the claim holds for any
b
-tuple with smaller maximum. Let
\vec{s}=\vec{r}-\vec{1}_{k}
.
Note that
N^{+}(\vec{s})\subset N^{+}(\vec{r})
. In particular, for
i\in N^{+}(\vec{s})
we have

\begin{split}y_{i}(\vec{c},\vec{r})=y_{i}(\vec{c},\vec{s})+y_{i}(\vec{c}+\vec{s},\vec{1}_{k})\geq y_{i}(\vec{c},\vec{s})+d_{i}(\vec{c}+\vec{r}).\end{split}

(41)

If
1\leq i\leq k
and
i\not\in N^{\pm}(\vec{s})
, we have
i\in N^{+}(\vec{r})
. More precisely, we have
y_{i}(\vec{c},\vec{r})=y_{i}(\vec{c},p-1)
or
y_{i}(\vec{c},\vec{r})=y_{i}(\vec{c},p)
, the latter occurring when
i=1
.
Thus, we compute

\begin{split}y_{i}(\vec{c},\vec{r})-d_{i}(\vec{c}+\vec{r})\geq d_{i}(\vec{c})\geq qd_{i}(\vec{c}-\vec{1}).\end{split}

(42)

Again, by Lemma
6.29
we know that for some
i_{0}\in N^{+}(\vec{r})
we have

\displaystyle d_{i_{0}}(\vec{c}+\vec{r})

\displaystyle\geq pd_{k+1}(\vec{c}).

(43)

From our inductive hypotheses, (
41
), (
42
), and (
43
) we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in N^{+}(\vec{r})}y_{i}(\vec{c},\vec{r})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in N^{+}(\vec{r})}d_{i}(\vec{c}+\vec{r})+\sum_{i\in N^{+}(\vec{r})}\Big[y_{i}(\vec{c},\vec{r})-d_{i}(\vec{c}+\vec{r})\Big]

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i\in N^{+}(\vec{r})}d_{i}(\vec{c}+\vec{r})+\sum_{i\in N^{+}(\vec{s})}\Big[y_{i}(\vec{c},\vec{r})-d_{i}(\vec{c}+\vec{r})\Big]+\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1\leq i\leq k}}{{i\not\in N^{\pm}(\vec{s})}}}\Big[y_{i}(\vec{c},\vec{r})-d_{i}(\vec{c}+\vec{r})\Big]

\displaystyle>\sum_{i\in N^{+}(\vec{r})}d_{i}(\vec{c}+\vec{r})+\sum_{i\in N^{+}(\vec{s})}y_{i}(\vec{c},\vec{s})+\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1\leq i\leq k}}{{i\not\in N^{\pm}(\vec{s})}}}pd_{i}(\vec{c}-\vec{1})

\displaystyle>pd_{k+1}(\vec{c})+\sum_{i\in N^{-}(\vec{s})}pd_{i}(\vec{c})+\sum_{i\in N^{+}(\vec{s})}pd_{i}(\vec{c}-\vec{1})+\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1\leq i\leq k}}{{i\not\in N^{\pm}(\vec{s})}}}pd_{i}(\vec{c}-\vec{1})

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i\in N^{-}(\vec{s})\cup\{k+1\}}pd_{i}(\vec{c})+\sum_{i\in N^{+}(\vec{s})}pd_{i}(\vec{c}-\vec{1})+\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1\leq i\leq k}}{{i\not\in N^{\pm}(\vec{s})}}}pd_{i}(\vec{c}-\vec{1})

The claim follows by observing that
N^{-}(\vec{r})\subset N^{-}(\vec{s})\cup\{k+1\}
and because
d_{i}(\vec{c})>d_{i}(\vec{c}-\vec{1})
.∎

Corollary 6.30
.

Fix
y\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. Then every slope in the Newton polygon of
\zeta_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta],\pi}(x,y)
occurs with multiplicity
one and is a integer multiple of
r-1
.

Proof.

First, consider the case where
y
is
q
-full. Write

\displaystyle\det(1-x\Psi^{\circ})

\displaystyle=1+c_{1}x+c_{2}x^{2}+\dots

Using the definition of the Fredholm determinant of a matrix, we know by Theorem
6.11
and Lemma
6.10
that
c_{i}=0
if
b\nmid i
and that

v(c_{bn})=v(\Sigma_{n})
. Also, if we set
\nu_{n}=v(\Sigma_{n})-v(\Sigma_{n-1})
, we know
\nu_{n+1}>\nu_{n}
by Theorem
6.11
. Thus, the slopes of the Newton polygon of
\det(1-x\Psi^{\circ})
are

\frac{\nu_{1}}{b},\frac{\nu_{2}}{b},\frac{\nu_{3}}{b},\dots

and each slope occurs with multiplicity
b
. By (
25
) we know the slopes of the Newton polygon of
\zeta_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta],\pi}(x,y)
are

\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\nu_{3},\dots

and that each slope occurs with multiplicity one.

For the general case, let
y\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. The subset of
q
-full numbers is dense in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. Let
y_{1},y_{2},\dots

be a sequence of
q
-full
p
-adic numbers that converge to
y
such that

\displaystyle y-y_{i}

\displaystyle\in p^{i}\mathbb{Z}_{p}

This implies
\zeta_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta],\pi}(x,y)\equiv\zeta_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta],\pi}(x,y_{i})\mod\pi^{p^{i}}
. To see this, note that
\rho_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta],\pi}^{\otimes y}
and
\rho_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta],\pi}^{\otimes y_{i}}
are congruent modulo
\pi^{p^{i}}
.
In particular, every vertex of
\NP_{\pi}(\zeta_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta],\pi}(x,y_{i}))
whose
y
-coordinate is less than
p^{i}
is also a vertex of
\NP_{\pi}(\zeta_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta],\pi}(x,y))
. By taking
i
to be large, we see that every slope of
\NP_{\pi}(\zeta_{\mathbb{F}_{r}[\theta],\pi}(x,y))
occurs with multiplicity one.

To prove divisibility by
r-1
, it suffices to show
r-1|\nu_{n}
. This will follow from the much stronger statement: For any rotational
b
-cycle
\sigma
of
J_{1}
we have
r-1|v(\sigma,\Psi^{\circ})
.
Write
\sigma=(m_{1},\dots,m_{b})
, so that

\displaystyle v(\sigma,\Psi^{\circ})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{b}y_{i}(pm_{i}-m_{i-1}).

By the definition of
y_{i}
we know
y_{i}(k)\equiv p^{i-1}k\mod r-1
.
Thus, we compute

\displaystyle v(\sigma,\Psi^{\circ})

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{b}p^{i-1}(pm_{i}-m_{i-1})\mod r-1

\displaystyle\equiv(p^{b}-1)m_{b}\equiv 0\mod r-1.

∎

Remark 7.5

By Proposition

By construction we have
m_{i-1}(\sigma^{\prime})=m_{i-1}(\sigma)-1
and
m(\sigma^{\prime})=m(\sigma)-1
. We claim that

n(\sigma^{\prime})=n(\sigma)
. First, note that
L_{i}(\sigma^{\prime})=L_{i}(\sigma)\backslash\{k_{i}\}
, so
n_{i}(\sigma^{\prime})=n_{i}(\sigma)-1
. Also
L_{i^{\prime}}(\sigma)=L_{i^{\prime}}(\sigma^{\prime})
for all
i^{\prime}\neq i,i-1
from the definition of
\sigma^{\prime}
, so that

n_{i^{\prime}}(\sigma)=n_{i^{\prime}}(\sigma^{\prime})
.
Thus, we are reduced to showing
n_{i-1}(\sigma^{\prime})=n_{i-1}(\sigma)+1
. If

\sigma(k_{i-1})\in K_{i-2}(\sigma)
, then
R(k_{i-1},\sigma(k_{i-1}))=y_{i}(p|k_{i-1}|)>0
. Since
R(k_{i-1}^{\prime},\sigma(k_{i-1}))=0
, we must have

R(\sigma^{\prime})<R(\sigma)
, which contradicts the
R
-minimality of
\sigma
. Thus, we have
\sigma(k_{i-1})\in H_{i-2}(\sigma)
.
In particular, we see that
L_{i-1}(\sigma^{\prime})=L_{i-1}(\sigma)\cup\{k_{i-1}^{\prime}\}

and so
n_{i-1}(\sigma^{\prime})=n_{i-1}(\sigma)+1
.

Step 2
.

By repeating the first step we may replace
\sigma
with an
R
-minimal enriched permutation where for some
i\in\mathbb{Z}/b\mathbb{Z}
with
m_{i-1}(\sigma)=0
and
n_{i}(\sigma)>0
.

Step 3
.

We are now reduced to the case where
m_{i-1}(\sigma)=0
and
n_{i}(\sigma)>0
. There exists
k_{i}\in K_{i}(\sigma)
with
k_{i-1}=\sigma(k_{i})\in H_{i-1}(\sigma)
, as
n_{i}(\sigma)>0
. Also
K_{i-1}(\sigma)=\{i-1\}\times\{1,\dots,g+d-1\}
, since
m_{i-1}(\sigma)=0
. This means there exists
k_{i}^{\prime}\in H_{i}(\sigma)

such that
k_{i-1}^{\prime}=\sigma(k_{i}^{\prime})\in K_{i-1}(\sigma)
. If this were not true, then
\sigma
would map
K_{i}(\sigma)
bijectively to
K_{i-1}(\sigma)
, which we know is not the case. We define
(\sigma^{\prime},S)
by swapping
k_{i-1}
and
k_{i-1}^{\prime}
. More precisely,

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}(k)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}k_{i-1}^{\prime}&k=k_{i}\\
k_{i-1}&k=k_{i}^{\prime}\\
\sigma(k)&k\neq k_{i},k_{i}^{\prime}\end{cases}.

We now compare
R(\sigma)
and
R(\sigma^{\prime})
. First note that
R(k_{i},\sigma(k_{i}))=0
and
R(k_{i},\sigma^{\prime}(k_{i}))=0
, since
k_{i}\in I
. Next, note that

R(k_{i}^{\prime},\sigma(k_{i}^{\prime}))=y_{i}(p|k_{i}^{\prime}|)
and
R(k_{i}^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}(k_{i}^{\prime}))=y_{i}(p|k_{i}^{\prime}|-|k_{i-1}|)
. In particular, we have
R(k_{i}^{\prime},\sigma(k_{i}^{\prime}))>R(k_{i}^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}(k_{i}^{\prime}))
. As
R(k,\sigma(k))=R(k,\sigma^{\prime}(k))
for
k\neq k_{i},k_{i}^{\prime}
, we see that
R(\sigma^{\prime})<R(\sigma)
, which contradicts the fact that
\sigma
is
R
-minimal.

∎

Corollary 7.19
.

There exists an
R
-minimal permutation of
I\sqcup J_{bd}

that is strongly split.

Proof.

Let
\sigma
be an
R
-minimal permutation. By Lemma
7.18
we know that
\sigma
is split. Write

\sigma=\tau\omega
where
\tau
is a permutation of
I
and
\omega
is an enriched permutation of
J_{bd}
. By Proposition
6.15
there
exists
\omega^{\prime}
that is decomposible and
p
-bounded with
the same size as
\omega
satisfying
R(\omega^{\prime})\leq R(\omega)
.
We also know from Corollary
7.11
that there exists a permutation
\tau^{\prime}
of
I
with
v(\tau^{\prime},\Delta)=0
. Then
R(\tau^{\prime}\omega^{\prime})\leq R(\tau\omega)
, so that
\tau^{\prime}\omega^{\prime}
is
R
-minimal. We also see that
\tau^{\prime}\omega^{\prime}
is strongly split by construction.
∎

Lemma 7.20
.

Let
n=n_{0}+b^{2}(g+d-1)
with
n_{0}\geq 0
and let
\sigma
be an enriched permutation of
I\sqcup J_{bd}
. Then
\sigma
is distinguished if and only if it is
R
-minimal and strongly split.

Proof.

We first do the ‘if’ direction. Assume
\sigma=\tau\omega
is
R
-minimal and strongly split. We have

\omega=\omega^{(1)}\dots\omega^{(bd)}
,
were each
\omega^{(j)}
is a
p
-bounded permutation of
J_{bd}^{(j)}
. From Theorem
6.11
and Corollary
7.17
we must have
\sigma^{(j)}=\Sigma^{(j)}_{n_{j}}
, where
\sum n_{j}=\mathbf{s}({\omega})
. If
n_{j_{1}}>n_{j_{2}}+1
, then the inequality in Theorem
6.11

tells us that

v(\Sigma_{n_{j_{1}}-1},\Psi^{\circ})+v(\Sigma_{n_{j_{2}}+1},\Psi^{\circ})<v(\Sigma_{n_{j_{1}}},\Psi^{\circ})+v(\Sigma_{n_{j_{2}}},\Psi^{\circ}).

This combined with Corollary
7.17
gives

R(\Sigma^{(j_{1})}_{n_{j_{1}}-1})+R(\Sigma^{(j_{2})}_{n_{j_{2}}+1})<R(\Sigma^{(j_{1})}_{n_{j_{1}}})+R(\Sigma^{(j_{2})}_{n_{j_{2}}}).

This contradicts the
R
-minimality of
\sigma
. It
follows that
\sigma
is distinguished. For the ‘only if’ direction, note that all distinguished permutations are strongly split by definition. From Corollary
7.19
and the ‘if’ direction we know that there exists a distinguished permutation that is
R
-minimal. However, all distinguished permutations have the same
R
-value. Thus, they all are
R
-minimal.
∎

(Of Proposition

)
Let
\sigma_{0}

-minimal and strongly split by Lemma

we see that

R

7.4
Finishing the proof of Theorem
1.5

We can now finish the proof of Theorem
1.5
. Write

\displaystyle\det(1-x\Delta)

\displaystyle=1+a_{1}x+a_{2}x^{2}+\dots.

By the cyclic shape of
\Delta
we know
a_{i}=0
for
i
with
b\nmid i
.
Also, by Corollary
7.11
we know

v(a_{b^{2}(g+d-1)})=0
.
Let
n=n_{0}+b^{2}(g+d-1)
with
n_{0}\geq 1
and write
n_{0}=c\cdot bd+r
where
r<bd
. From Proposition
7.13

we know

\displaystyle v(c_{bn})

\displaystyle\geq(bd-r)v\Big(\Sigma_{c},\Psi^{\circ}\Big)+rv\Big(\Sigma_{c+1},\Psi^{\circ}\Big).

Now assume
bd|n_{0}
and let
S
be the set underlying the enriched permutation
\Sigma_{c}
of
J_{1}
. Then from Corollary
7.10
and Proposition
7.13
we have

\displaystyle c_{bn}

\displaystyle\equiv\det(\Delta_{I\times I})\Big(\prod_{k\in S}\Psi^{\circ}_{k,\Sigma_{c}(k)}\Big)^{bd}\mod\pi^{bd\cdot v(\Sigma_{c},\Psi^{\circ})+1}.

As
\det(\Delta_{I\times I})
is in
\mathbf{V}^{\times}
by Corollary
7.11
we see that

\displaystyle v(c_{bn})

\displaystyle=bd\cdot v\Big(\Sigma_{c},\Psi^{\circ}\Big).

Recall from Theorem
6.11
that
\nu_{n}=v(\Sigma_{n},\Psi^{\circ})-v(\Sigma_{n-1},\Psi^{\circ})
satisfies

\nu_{n+1}>\nu_{n}
for all
n\geq 1
.
Thus, we determine the Newton polygon of
\det(1-x\Delta)
to have slopes

\underbrace{\{0,\dots,0\}}_{b^{2}(g-1+d)}\sqcup\bigsqcup_{n\geq 1}\underbrace{\Big\{\frac{\nu_{n}}{b},\dots,\frac{\nu_{n}}{b}\Big\}}_{b^{2}d\text{ times}}.

By Proposition
7.7
we know that

L(\rho^{\otimes y}_{A_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}},x^{b})^{b}
and
\det(1-x\Delta)
have
the same Newton polygon. Therefore, the Newton polygon of

L(\rho^{\otimes y}_{A_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}},x)
has slopes

\underbrace{\{0,\dots,0\}}_{g-1+d}\sqcup\bigsqcup_{n\geq 1}\underbrace{\Big\{\nu_{n},\dots,\nu_{n}\Big\}}_{d\text{ times}}.

The zeros of
L(\rho^{\otimes y}_{A},x)
are
d
-th roots of the zeros of
L(\rho^{\otimes y}_{A_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}},x)
.
Thus, the Newton polygon of
L(\rho^{\otimes y}_{A},x)
has slopes

\underbrace{\{0,\dots,0\}}_{g-1+d}\sqcup\bigsqcup_{n\geq 1}\underbrace{\Big\{\frac{\nu_{n}}{d},\dots,\frac{\nu_{n}}{d}\Big\}}_{d}.

From Corollary
6.30
we may write
\nu_{n}=\alpha_{n}(r-1)
where
\alpha_{n}
is a positive integer. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.5
.
