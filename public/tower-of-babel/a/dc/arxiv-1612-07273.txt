Natural Transformations as Rewrite Rules and Monad Composition
Dexter Kozen

Abstract
. Eklund et al. (2002) present a graphical technique aimed at simplifying the verification of various category-theoretic constructions, notably the composition of monads. In this note we take a different approach involving string rewriting. We show that a given tuple ( T , μ , η ) (T,\mu,\eta) is a monad if and only if T T is a terminal object in a certain category of strings and rewrite rules, and that this fact can be established by proving confluence of the rewrite system. We illustrate the technique on the monad composition problem. We also give a characterization of adjunctions in terms of rewrite categories.

Introduction

As common constructions in the theory of data types and programming language semantics become better understood, there is a natural tendency toward generality. One desires to isolate common underlying principles, to unify related notions in a common framework, and to provide powerful abstract tools for reasoning and understanding.

A good example of one successful such enterprise is the use of monads in functional and logic programming
[

,
17

[

. They have been applied to parsing and type checking
[

, semantics of nondeterministic and probabilistic computation
[

, and unification in logic programming
[

Unfortunately, greater abstraction is often accompanied by reduced accessibility. Many abstract constructions, although well motivated by applications, may at times be difficult to navigate when presented in more abstract form. In particular, reasoning about the basic properties of monads—such as
monad composition

In this note we take a different approach involving string rewriting. We show that a given tuple
(T,\mu,\eta)
is a monad if and only if
T
is a terminal object in a certain category whose objects are strings and whose morphisms are rewrite rules, and the fact that

(1)

Categories, functors, and natural transformations form a 2-category
\mathsf{Cat}
in which the 0-cells (objects) are the categories, the 1-cells (morphisms) are the functors, and the 2-cells (morphisms of morphisms) are the natural transformations.

In a general 2-category, composition of 1-cells and composition of 2-cells are called
horizontal
and
vertical composition
, respectively, and are often denoted
\circ_{0}
and
\circ_{1}
, respectively; but we will usually write
\circ
for
\circ_{1}
and omit the
\circ_{0}
altogether. Horizontal composition also acts on 2-cells and satisfies the property:
If
F_{1},F_{2},F_{3}:\mathsf{C}\to\mathsf{D}
and
G_{1},G_{2},G_{3}:\mathsf{D}\to\mathsf{E}
are 1-cells and
\varphi:F_{1}\to F_{2}
,
\varphi^{\prime}:F_{2}\to F_{3}
,
\psi:G_{1}\to G_{2}
, and
\psi^{\prime}:G_{2}\to G_{3}
are 2-cells, then

\displaystyle(\psi^{\prime}\circ\psi)(\varphi^{\prime}\circ\varphi)

\displaystyle=(\psi^{\prime}\varphi^{\prime})\circ(\psi\varphi).

(2)

A special case of the action of horizontal composition on 2-cells is the following. If
S:\mathsf{D}\to\mathsf{E}
,
F,G:\mathsf{C}\to\mathsf{D}
, and
T:\mathsf{B}\to\mathsf{C}
are 1-cells and
\varphi:F\to G
is a 2-cell, there is a 2-cell
S\varphi T:SFT\to SGT
obtained from the horizontal composition of
\varphi
with the identities
\mathsf{id}_{S}
on the left and
\mathsf{id}_{T}
on the right. It is helpful to think of this operation as a kind of scalar multiplication. It satisfies the following properties:

\displaystyle I\varphi\ =\ \varphi\ =\ \varphi I

\displaystyle(SS^{\prime})\varphi\ =\ S(S^{\prime}\varphi)\qquad\varphi(TT^{\prime})\ =\ (\varphi T)T^{\prime}

\displaystyle S(\varphi\circ\psi)\ =\ S\varphi\circ S\psi\qquad(\varphi\circ\psi)T\ =\ \varphi T\circ\psi T.

(3)

Thus we can write
SS^{\prime}\varphi
and
\varphi TT^{\prime}
without ambiguity.

\mathsf{Cat}

S
and
T
are functors and
\varphi
is a natural transformation with components
\varphi_{A}
, then
S\varphi T
is the natural transformation with components
(S\varphi T)_{A}=S(\varphi_{TA})
.

\varphi:F\to G
can be applied in the context of a string
S\varphi T:SFT\to SGT

3.
Rewrite Categories

A
rewrite category
is like a string rewrite system, except the semantics of the derivations are taken into account in the definitions of confluence and local confluence.

Formally, a
rewrite category
is defined in terms of a finitely presented 2-category
(\mathcal{O},\mathcal{F},\mathcal{R},\mathcal{E})
, where
\mathcal{O}
,
\mathcal{F}
, and
\mathcal{R}
are finite sets of 0-cells, 1-cells, and 2-cells, respectively, and
\mathcal{E}
is a finite set of well-typed equations between 2-cell-valued expressions over
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{R}
generating an equational theory on 2-cells. The 1-cells do not satisfy any equations except those imposed by the axioms of category theory, thus form the free category (free typed monoid) freely generated by
\mathcal{F}
. Each 1-cell corresponds to a well-typed string
F_{1}\cdots F_{n}
, where
well-typed
means
\mathop{\mathsf{dom}}F_{i}=\mathop{\mathsf{cod}}F_{i+1}
,
1\leq i\leq n-1
. We denote by
\mathcal{F}^{*}
the set of all well-typed strings over
\mathcal{F}
. The elements of
\mathcal{R}
are called
rewrite rules
and generate the set of all 2-cells under horizontal (
\circ_{0}
) and vertical (
\circ_{1}
) composition.

The rewrite category itself is a substructure of the category of 1- and 2-cells of this finitely presented 2-category. It is specified by a tuple
(\mathcal{A};\mathcal{R}^{\prime})
, where
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\mathrel{\subseteq}\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{A}
is any subset of
\mathcal{F}^{*}
closed under the action of
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
.

Rewrite categories provide a syntax that can be interpreted in the 2-category
\mathsf{Cat}
of categories, functors, and natural transformations. A well-typed string
F_{1}\cdots F_{n}
is interpreted as a functor
\mathop{\mathsf{dom}}F_{n}\to\mathop{\mathsf{cod}}F_{1}
, where concatenation of strings is interpreted as composition of functors. The rewrite rules describe the action of natural transformations on these strings.

The equations
\mathcal{E}
generating the equational theory of the 2-category typically represent an equational axiomatization of the construct under study. However, from the perspective of string rewriting, they serve a different purpose: they represent local confluence conditions that can be used to establish global confluence in the rewrite category, leading to the existence of terminal objects.

3.1.
Local Confluence and Normal Forms

Given a 1-cell
SXT
and a rule
\varphi:X\to Y
of a rewrite category, the scalar multiple
S\varphi T:SXT\to SYT
can be viewed as rewriting the indicated occurrence of
X
to
Y
in the context of the string
SXT
. This is called a
reduction
. The
redex
of the reduction
S\varphi T
is the substring
X
of
SXT
. A sequence of reductions from
Y
to
Z
is called a
derivation
. We write
\pi:Y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\to}}Z
if
\pi
is such a derivation. A string is in
normal form
if no rules apply.

The rules
\mathcal{R}
of the rewrite category each have a type
X\to Y
for various
X,Y\in\mathcal{F}^{*}
, where
\mathop{\mathsf{dom}}X=\mathop{\mathsf{dom}}Y
and
\mathop{\mathsf{cod}}X=\mathop{\mathsf{cod}}Y
. Applying these rules to strings in
\mathcal{F}^{*}
, one can generate diagrams in the rewrite category. We say that the system
\mathcal{R}

•

is
confluent
if for any two derivations
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\to}}U
and
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\to}}V
, there is a word
W
and derivations
U\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\to}}W
and
V\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\to}}W
such that the resulting diagram commutes;

•

is
locally confluent
if for any two single reductions
X\to U
and
X\to V
, there is a word
W
and derivations
U\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\to}}W
and
V\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\to}}W
such that the resulting diagram commutes; and

•

has the
diamond property
if for any two reductions
X\to U
and
X\to V
, there is a word
W
and reductions
U\to W
and
V\to W
such that the resulting diagram commutes. This might be a pushout in the rewrite category, but not necessarily.

Local confluence does not imply confluence, but the diamond property does. See
[
2
]
for a thorough treatment of these concepts.

Note, however, that our reductions have semantic content as well as syntactic. In our definitions of confluence, local confluence, and the diamond property, it is not enough that two derivations derive the same word; the resulting diagrams must also commute in the rewrite category. We say that two derivations
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\to}}Y
are
equivalent
if the composition of the 2-cells along the two paths are equal.

4.
Applications

In this section we demonstrate the use rewrite categories in the verification of monad composition as presented by Eklund et al.
[
7
]
. We also give a characterization of monads and adjunctions in terms of rewrite categories.

The following lemma and its proof introduce our approach at a basic level.

Lemma 2
.

Let
T^{+}
denote the set of nonnull strings of
T
’s and
\mu
the rewrite rule
T^{2}\to T
. The following are equivalent:

(1)

\mu
satisfies the left-hand diagram of (
5
);

(2)

T
is a terminal object in the rewrite category
(T^{+};\mu)
.

Proof 4.1
.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2):
Combining Lemma
1
with the observation (
6
), we have that the rewrite system consisting of the single rule
\mu
on strings
T^{n}
for
n\geq 1
satisfies the diamond property and is therefore confluent. It follows that any diagram starting from
T^{n}
,
n\geq 1
, and ending with the normal form
T
commutes. Moreover, there exists a reduction sequence from any such
T^{n}
to
T
. Thus there is a unique morphism
T^{n}\to T
for
n\geq 1
, so
T
is a terminal object.

(2)
\Rightarrow
(1):
Conversely, if
T
is a terminal object, then the left-hand diagram of (
5
) must commute, since there is a unique morphism
T^{3}\to T
.

4.1.
Monads

Now we add the rewrite rule
\eta
to the mix. This rule can be used to introduce a new occurrence of
T
anywhere in the string. In the presence of
\eta
,
T
is no longer a normal form, and in fact normal forms in the strict sense of string rewriting no longer exist. Nevertheless,
T
is still a terminal object. Moreover,
T^{0}=I
can now be included, since there is an arrow
I\to T
.

Theorem 3
.

Consider a rewrite category on 1-cells
T^{*}
and rules
\mu:T^{2}\to T
and
\eta:I\to T
. The following are equivalent:

(1)

\mu
and
\eta
satisfy (
5
);

(2)

T
is a terminal object in the rewrite category
(T^{*};\mu,\eta)
.

Proof 4.2
.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2):
Suppose we have a rewrite system in which the rules can be classified as either
bad rules
or
good rules
(in our application, a rule is
bad
if it increases the length of the string, e.g.
\eta
). Call a derivation
good
if it uses only good rules. Suppose further that

1.

every pair of good reductions
X\to U
and
X\to V
can be completed to a diamond using only good reductions
U\to W
and
V\to W
; and

2.

every pair of reductions
X\to U
and
X\to V
, good or bad, are confluent using only good derivations
U\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\to}}W
and
V\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\to}}W
.

These conditions hold for
(T^{*};\mu,\eta)
under the assumption (1). We have already argued condition 1 for
\mu
in Lemma
2
. The right-hand diagram in (
5
) implies condition 2 by immediately inverting any application of
\eta
whenever it is applied to a nonnull string. For example, consider applications of
\eta
and
\mu
to a substring
T^{2}
, where
\eta
is applied between the two occurrences of
T
. The two redexes thus overlap.

Distributive laws are discussed in depth in
[
4
, §9.2]
. A typical application is the construction of the
complex algebra
of an algebra, whose elements are sets of elements of the original algebra. Here
P
would be the powerset monad and
T
the term monad of some variety of algebras, and
\theta
takes a term of sets
t(A_{1},\ldots,A_{n})
and turns it into a set of terms
\{t(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\mid a_{i}\in A_{i},\ 1\leq i\leq n\}
. These constructions are discussed in
[
5
,
9
]
. Another example would be the combination of the additive and multiplicative monoid structures in semirings. Here
P
would be the finite powerset monad and
T
the free monoid construction.

In light of the theme of this note, it should be clear that the conditions in (
4.2
) are nothing more than a way to establish local confluence in the case of overlapping redexes between
\theta
and the monad operations. For example, the top rectangle of the left-hand diagram of (
4.2
) handles overlapping redexes involving
\theta
and
\mu^{P}
.

The two monads
P
and
T
can be combined as follows. Define

\displaystyle\mu^{PT}

\displaystyle=\mu^{P}T\circ P^{2}\mu^{T}\circ P\theta T:(PT)^{2}\to PT

\displaystyle\eta^{PT}

\displaystyle=\eta^{P}T\circ\eta^{T}:I\to PT.

(11)

Then
(PT,\mu^{PT},\eta^{PT})
is again a monad
[
10
,
4
,
7
]
. We will verify this using Theorem
3
. Most of the work is contained in the following lemma.

Lemma 5
.

Consider a rewrite category on 1-cells
\{P,T\}^{*}
in a 2-category with a single 0-cell and rules
\mu^{P}:P^{2}\to P
,
\eta^{P}:I\to P
,
\mu^{T}:T^{2}\to T
,
\eta^{T}:I\to T
, and
\theta:TP\to PT
, such that
(\mu^{P},\eta^{P})
and
(\mu^{T},\eta^{T})
both satisfy the monad axioms (
5
)
(
with appropriate substitutions
)
. The following statements are equivalent:

(1)

\mu^{P},\eta^{P},\mu^{T},\eta^{T}
, and
\theta
satisfy the distributive laws (
4.2
).

(2)

PT
is a terminal object in the rewrite category
(\{P,T\}^{*};\mu^{P},\mu^{T},\eta^{P},\eta^{T},\theta)
.

Proof 4.3
.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2):
First we show that the rewrite system is confluent. Recall that a rule is
bad
if it increases length,
good
otherwise. The good rules are
\mu^{P}
,
\mu^{T}
, and
\theta
, and the bad rules are
\eta^{P}
and
\eta^{T}
.

Every pair of good reductions can be completed to a good confluent diagram. If the redexes do not overlap, this follows from Lemma
1
. For redexes that overlap, all cases are covered by the left-hand diagram of (
5
) and the left-hand diagram of (
4.2
).

Given any derivation
\pi:X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\to}}Y
, possibly containing bad reductions, produce a new derivation
\pi^{\prime}
as follows:

1.

Extend the derivation to derive
PT
.

2.

Rearrange the resulting derivation
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\to}}Y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\to}}PT
to obtain an equivalent derivation
\pi^{\prime}:X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\to}}PT
in which all the bad rules are applied after all the good rules.

Step 1 can be accomplished by first introducing an occurrence of
P
and/or
T
using
\eta^{P}
and
\eta^{T}
if necessary, then moving all occurrences of
P
to the left of all occurrences of
T
using
\theta
, then collapsing the
P
’s using
\mu^{P}
and the
T
’s using
\mu^{T}
.

Step 2 can be done without increasing the length of the derivation. For any bad reduction followed immediately by a good reduction, if the symbol introduced by the bad reduction is not part of the redex of the good reduction, then the two reductions can be switched by Lemma
1
.
