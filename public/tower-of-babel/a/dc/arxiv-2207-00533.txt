T-Tetrominos in Arithmetic Progression Journal: Discrete Mathematics
Emily Feller, Robert Hochberg

Abstract
A famous result of D. Walkup is that an m × n m\times n rectangle may be tiled by T-tetrominos if and only if both m m and n n are multiples of 4. The “if” portion may be proved by tiling a 4 × 4 4\times 4 block, and then copying that block to fill the rectangle; but this leads to regular, periodic tilings. In this paper we investigate how much “order” must be present in every tiling of a rectangle by T-tetrominos, where we measure order by length of arithmetic progressions of tiles.

Introduction

There is a decent-sized body of literature characterizing the rectangles that may be tiled by various polyominos, and the T-tetromino has received much attention. A well-known result of D. Walkup
[
1
]
is that the T-tetromino can tile a rectangle if and only if both sides of that rectangle are multiples of 4. This result has been extended in several ways. Mike Reid
[
2
]
generalized the T-tetromino to a class of
(8n-4)
-ominos, each having Walkup-like conditions on the rectangles that they tile. Authors
[
3
,
4
]
have enumerated tilings of
4m\times 4n
rectangles with
T
-tetrominos, and others
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
have characterized (partial) tilings of rectangles that do not meet Walkup’s condition.

(Left) A 4x4 unit. (Middle) A periodic tiling of a
20\times 20
square. (Right) A tiling without any 3-term AP of tiles.

Any
4m\times 4n
rectangle may be tiled by repeating the four-tile unit on the left in Figure
1
, yielding an orderly tiling of, for example, a
20\times 20
rectangle as shown in the middle of that figure. The
20\times 20
tiling shown on the right in that figure is visually less orderly. We can make this precise by introducing the notion of an
arithmetic progression
(AP) of tiles; that is, a sequence
T_{1},T_{2},\ldots,T_{l}
of tiles that are all in the same orientation, and such that the translation from
T_{i}
to
T_{i+1}
is the same for all
i,1\leq i\leq l-1
. The number
l
of tiles is called the
length
of the AP. The tiling in the middle of Figure
1
has several APs of length 5 (two are highlighted, along with one of length three), but the tiling on the right has no AP of length greater than two.

The natural Ramsey-type question to ask is, given a length
l
, must there exist numbers
M
and
N
so that whenever
m\geq M
and
n\geq N
, any tiling of a
4m\times 4n
rectangle with T-tetrominos must contain an AP of tiles of length at least
l
? And from a fixed rectangle’s point of view we can ask, given
m
and
n
, what is the largest length AP of tiles that every
4m\times 4n
tiling must contain?

In this paper, “tiling” will always refer to tiling by non-overlapping T-tetrominos, and tilings of rectangles will always mean completely covering the interior of a rectangle satisfying Walkup’s condition. We write
(m,n)\rightarrow l
to mean that every tiling of an
m\times n
rectangle must contain an arithmetic progression of tiles of length at least
l
. For a fixed width
w
, a multiple of 4, and
l
, any integer at least 1, we define
T=T_{w}(l)
to be the least value of
T
such that
(w,T)\rightarrow l
.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows:
In Section
2
we prove some basic results and make a connection with classical van der Waerden numbers. In Sections
3
and
4
we show that for
w
in
\{4,8,12,16\}
and
l\geq 3
,
T_{w}(l)
is exactly given by the two-color van der Waerden numbers, and that
T_{20}(l)
is not. We end with a section on exact values and further explorations.

2
Basic Results

We begin by considering
boundary tilings
, that is, coverings of unit squares along one side of a line, by tiles lying completely on that side of the line, as shown in Figure
2
. The letters in the left figure stand for
up, down, left
and
right
, the orientations of the tiles — wording and notation we will frequently use.

Figure 2:
Boundary tiling with no arithmetic progression of length 2.

We start with a simple theorem:

Theorem 1

Any boundary tiling covering seven consecutive boundary squares must contain an arithmetic progression of length two.

Proof:
If there are two tiles in the same orientation then we are done. But four tiles, one in each orientation, can cover at most six squares along that edge.
\Box

Theorem 2

For any length
l\geq 1
there exists some boundary length
B
such that any covering of
B
consecutive boundary squares must contain an arithmetic progression of length
l
.

The proof makes use of van der Waerden’s theorem. See
[
8
]
.

Theorem 3
(van der Waerden’s Theorem)

For any integers
r\geq 1,l\geq 1
there exists an integer
W=W(r,l)
such that any
r
-coloring of the initial segment of positive integers

\{1,2,3,\ldots,W\}
contains a monochromatic arithmetic progression of length
l
.

Proof of Theorem
2
:

Color the boundary squares using the colors
u
,
l
and
r
for squares covered by tiles in those three orientations, and
d
1,
d
2 and
d
3 for squares covered by the left, middle or right squares of a
d
tile. This yields a six-coloring of the boundary squares. If we let
B=W(6,l)
then there must be a monochromatic AP of length
l
, which corresponds to an AP of Ts of length
l
.

\Box

We will improve this bound following the discussion of the HV Lemma below. As a straightforward corollary we get a Ramsey-type theorem about APs of T-tetrominos in tilings of rectangles.

Corollary 4

For any length
l\geq 1
there exists some number
B
such that any tiling of a rectangle having some side of length at least
B
must contain an AP of T-tetrominos of length
l
.

3
Width 4

For rectangles of width 4, 8, 12 and 16, the numbers behave like the classic 2-color van der Waerden numbers. That is, for widths
w=
4, 8, 12, and 16, the lengths of the smallest width-
w
rectangle that forces an
l
-term AP of T-tetrominos is four times the length of the smallest initial segment of the natural numbers that forces a monochromatic
l
-term AP under any 2-coloring. We first concentrate on the case of width 4.

Note that all tilings of a
4\times l
rectangle consist of some concatenation of the units shown in Figure
3
. (This follows directly from the discussion of chain graphs in Section
4
.) Since long units contain long APs within themselves, we can make some easy observations: If a tiling of a
4\times l
rectangle contains no AP of length 2, then it can contain only A units and/or B units. If such a tiling contains no AP of length 3, then it must consist of only A, B, C and/or D units. And so on. We use a two-character code to describe the orientation and vertical placement of a T-tetromino, consisting of a letter for orientation (u, d, l, or r) and a number giving the highest row it covers, where the rows are numbered 1, 2, 3, 4 from the top down. We also number the columns
1,2,3,\ldots
from left to right.

Figure 3:
Tilings of
4\times l
rectangles are made of units like these.

Figure 4:
Names for the orientations and vertical locations of all T-tetrominos in a
4\times N
tiling.

We first formalize a simple observation about arithmetic progressions.

Lemma 5
(mod 4 Lemma)

Let
A
be an arithmetic progression of integers with at least 3 terms, and let
s
be some integer. If, for each
a\in A
, either
a\equiv s\pmod{4}
or
a\equiv s+1\pmod{4}
, then the terms of
A
are all congruent to each other
\pmod{4}
.

Proof:
It is sufficient to prove this in the case that
s=0
, since the AP can be translated by subtracting
s
from each term without affecting the hypothesis or conclusion of the theorem.

Suppose that
a,b
and
c
are three consecutive terms of the AP. Then
a+c=2b
. If
b\equiv 0\pmod{4}
, then
a
and
c
must both be
0\pmod{4}
in order to satisfy
a+c=2b
. And if
b\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, then
a
and
c
must both be
1\pmod{4}
for the same reason. In each case, the three terms must all be in the same congruence class. Since this holds for all consecutive triples, all elements of the AP must be in the same congruence class.
\Box

Next we show that in rectangles of width 4 and APs of length
\geq 3
it suffices to consider only type d1 T-tetrominos.

Lemma 6
(d1 Lemma)

Any tiling
\mathcal{T}
of a width-4 rectangle contains an
l
-term AP of T-tetrominos, for
l\geq 3
, if and only if it contains an
l
-term AP of d1 T-tetrominos.

Proof:
As shown in Figure
3
, the only T-tetrominos that occur in a
4\times N
tiling are d1, d3, r1, r2, l1, l2, u1, and u3, and we observe (without proof) that any AP of length
\geq 3
must consist of one of these types only. We will show that for each of those types, if some tiling contains an AP of length at least 3 of that type, then there is also an AP of d1 T-tetrominos of that length in that tiling. We make recourse to Figure
3
to justify the observations that follow. For the u1, r1, r2, l1 and l2 T-tetrominos there will always be a d1 T-tetromino joined to it in a consistent way, so that an AP in one of those types will give an AP in the joined d1 T-tetrominos. For example, every u1 has a d1 to its immediate left in the same two rows that it occupies.

Now suppose there is an AP of u3 T-tetrominos. Note that the u3 T-tetrominos in Type-I units have their left-most square in a column number that is congruent to
1\pmod{4}
, and u3 T-tetrominos in Type-II units have their left-most squares in a column number that is congruent to
2\pmod{4}
. So by the mod 4 Lemma these u3 T-tetrominos will have to be all in Type-I or all in Type-II units. If it is Type-I, then there will be d1 T-tetrominos above and one square to the right of each u3, giving an AP of d1 T-tetrominos of the same length. And if it is Type-II, then they will be above and one square to the left. In either case we get an AP of the same length as for the u3 T-tetrominos. Finally, we observe that a similar proof is obtained in the case of an AP of d3 T-tetrominos.
\Box

Every tiling
\mathcal{T}
of a
4\times k
rectangle can be naturally mapped to a tiling of that rectangle by A units and/or B units only. This is done by considering each unit
U
in the tiling.
U
’s first column matches the first column of either an A unit or a B unit. If it matches the A unit, then we replace
U
by enough A units to match the length of
U
, and if it matches the B unit, then we replace
U
by enough B units to match the length of
U
. For example, C would be replaced by AA, and F would be replaced by BBB. We denote the resulting tiling by
AB(\mathcal{T})
.

Lemma 7
(AB Lemma)

A tiling
\mathcal{T}
of a
4\times N
rectangle contains an
l
-term arithmetic progression of T-tetrominos, if and only if
AB(\mathcal{T})
contains an
l
-term arithmetic progression of T-tetrominos.

Proof:
We note that the mapping fixes the d1 T-tetrominos, and the result follows from the d1 Lemma.
\Box

Lemma 8

For all
l\geq 3
,
T_{4}(l)=4W(2,l)
.

Proof:

First we show that
T_{4}(l)\geq 4W(2,l)
. Consider any two-coloring of the natural numbers of length
W(2,l)-1
which does not contain an
l
-term monochromatic arithmetic progression. Such a coloring exists by the definition of
W(2,l)
. Map one of the colors to the A unit and the other color to the B unit to produce a tiling of a rectangle of size
4\times 4(W(2,l)-1)
. This tiling contains no
l
-term arithmetic progression of A units and no l-term arithmetic progression of B units. By Lemma
5
, this tiling contains no
l
-term arithmetic progression of d1 T-tetrominos, and so by the d1 Lemma, contains no arithmetic progression of any orientation of T-tetrominos. Thus
T(4,l)\geq 4W(2,l)
.

Next we show that
T_{4}(l)\leq 4W(2,l)
.
Consider a tiling
\mathcal{T}
of a rectangle of width 4 and length
T_{4}(l)-4
which has no
l
-term arithmetic progression of T-tetrominos. Such a rectangle exists by the definition of
T_{4}(l)
. By the AB Lemma,
AB(\mathcal{T})
contains no
l
-term arithmetic progression of T-tetrominos, and in particular, no
l
-term arithmetic progression of A units or B units. Map the rectangle tiled with A units and B units to a 2-coloring of the natural numbers
\{1,\ldots,T_{4}(l)/4-1\}
such that A gets mapped to one color and B to the other. The 2-coloring resulting from the mapping contains no
l
-term monochromatic arithmetic progression, so that
W(2,l)>T_{4}(l)/4-1
, or

T_{4}(l)<4W(2,l)+4
, which implies
T_{4}(l)\leq 4W(2,l)
.
\Box

4
Chain Graphs and Widths 8, 12, and 16

To address widths 8, 12 and 16 we introduce some notions from a paper of Korn and Pak
[
3
]
. Every tiling (Figure
5
a) gives rise to a directed graph as follows: Subdivide the rectangle into
2\times 2
blocks, as shown in Figure
5
b. Korn and Pak show, based on Walkup’s paper, that each T-tetromino must have three of its squares in one block (its “majority” block) and one square in an adjacent block (its “minority” block). Place a vertex into the center of each block, and for each T-tetromino, draw a directed edge from the vertex in its majority block to the vertex in its minority block, as shown in Figure
5
c. They call the resulting digraph the
chain graph
for that tiling. Korn and Pak call the
2\times 2
boxes inside the chain graph
antiblocks
, on which they impose a gray/white checkerboard coloring with gray in the corners, as shown in Figure
5
c. Each arrow has a gray block on its left or its right, as seen when walking along that arrow in its direction. See Figure
5
d. We define an AP of
shaded arrows
to be a sequence of equally-spaced arrows, pointing in the same direction, having the same left/right shading.

Figure 5:
A tiling (a), its
2\times 2
blocks (b), its chain graph (c), and the correspondence between tiles and shaded arrows (d). The two shaded tiles are discussed in the proof of Lemma
9
.

Lemma 9

For any
l\geq 3
, a tiling has an AP of T-tetrominos of length
l
if and only if its chain graph has an AP of shaded arrows of length
l
.

Proof:

It follows from the discussion in the proof of Lemma 5 in
[
3
]
that every right-pointing shaded arrow corresponds to a tile exactly as shown in Figure
5
d, depending on whether its left or right side is shaded. Those figures can be rotated to show the correspondence for the other three orientations. Thus any
l
-term AP of shaded arrows gives rise to a
l
-term AP in the associated T-tetrominos.

The other direction is slightly more subtle. For example, the two shaded T-tetrominos in Figure
5
a form a 2-term AP, but their corresponding shaded arrows (dashed in the Figure
5
c) do not. To show that this cannot happen with APs of length 3 or greater, we need another lemma.

Lemma 10
(The dx/dy Lemma)

Suppose we are given an AP of T-tetrominos of length 3 or greater, and let the pair
(dx,dy)
give the translation between consecutive tiles in the AP. Then
(dx,dy)
is congruent to either
(0,0)
or
(2,2)\pmod{4}
.

Proof:

Walkup’s proof makes use of
cut
and
cornerless
points, which are, respectively, grid points where four tile corners must meet, and grid points where no tile corner may lie, in any tiling of a rectangle. Figure
6
shows cut points as crosses and cornerless points as circles, in
4\times 4
blocks that fill the rectangle periodically. More precisely, if the lower-left corner of the rectangle has coordinates
(0,0)
, then the cut and cornerless points are exactly those points
(x,y)
where both
x
and
y
are even; and
x+y\equiv 0\pmod{4}
for cut points, and
x+y\equiv 2\pmod{4}
for cornerless points.
The two shaded tiles in that figure show the only two possible placements of a T-tetromino, up to rotation or reflection with respect to those
4\times 4
blocks. Consider the T-tetromino on the bottom left. Of the sixteen possible pairs
(dx,dy)\pmod{4}
, twelve can be immediately ruled out by considering cut and cornerless points. For example,
(dx,dy)=(1,0)
is ruled out, because that tile, slid one square to the right, results in a tile with a corner at a cornerless point, as well as cutting two arms of a cross. Only
(0,0),(0,1),(2,2)
and
(2,3)
permit a valid translation. But as
(0,1)
and
(2,3)
do not permit a
second
translation, they can be ruled out in any AP of length 3 or greater. The proof is similar for the other starting position.
\Box

Figure 6:
The cut and cornerless points.

We may now finish the proof of Lemma
9
. Suppose we have an AP of at least three T-tetrominos  and consider the associated shaded arrows. By the dx/dy Lemma, their corresponding arrows must have their shading on the same side, and therefore have the same orientation, giving an AP of shaded arrows of the same length.
\Box

We give one more lemma to help us finish the proof of Theorem
12
for widths 8, 12 and 16. This follows from the discussion in Section 4 of
[
3
]
, so we do not prove it here. It is illustrated in Figure
7
.

Lemma 11
(The HV Lemma)

Every chain graph can be constructed as follows from the gray/white antiblocks: 1. Add a chain edge along every side of a gray antiblock that does not have an adjacent white antiblock; 2. For each white antiblock, add either both horizontal edges, or both vertical edges; 3. Arbitrarily orient each resulting cycle.
\Box

Figure 7:
Illustration of steps 1 and 2 of the HV Lemma.

Theorem 12

For all
l\geq 3
,
T_{4}(l)=T_{8}(l)=T_{12}(l)=T_{16}(l)=4W(2,l)
.

Proof:
The case for
T_{4}(l)
was proved in Lemma
8
. This section deals with widths 8, 12, and 16.

Observe that for any width
w\geq 4,T_{w}(l)\leq 4W(2,l)
. For, given a tiling of a
w\times 4W(2,l)
rectangle, the HV Lemma says that each gray anti-block in the top row yields an arrow on its upper edge, and we may consider the left/right orientation of those arrows to be a 2-coloring of the set
\{1,2,\ldots,W(2,l)\}
. By the definition of
W(2,l)
this will contain an AP of length
l
of shaded arrows, and hence of T-tetrominos. (Note that this gives the desired improvement of the bound given in the proof of Theorem
2
.)

The proof of Lemma
8
showed that
T_{4}(l)>4W(2,l)-4
, by constructing a row of A blocks and B blocks. To show that
T_{8}(l)>4W(2,l)-4
we can take two copies of such a row, one placed above the other, each of which contains no AP of length
l
. Suppose this tiling contained an AP of T-tetrominos of length
l
and let
(dx,dy)
be its translation. We cannot have
dy=0
, for then the AP would lie in a single row whose A/B blocks were constructed to contain no such AP. The structure of A/B blocks does not permit
dy=2
. And if
dy\geq 4
there is not enough vertical space to fit the AP, since
l\geq 3
. This completes the proof for width
w=8
.

For widths 12 and 16 the situation is different. We can not use A/B blocks, for example, to show that
T_{12}(3)\geq 4W(2,3)=36.
This is because any 2-coloring of the squares of a
3\times 5
grid must contain a monochromatic 3-term AP, as we will now show by considering the
3\times 5
rectangle on the left in Figure
8
. Suppose there was a red/blue-coloring of those 15 squares with no 3-term AP. We may assume that square H is colored red. Then at least one of A or P must be blue, and at least one of E or K must also be blue. This gives two cases up to symmetry. In case 1, we assume that A and E are blue. Then C must be red, which forces M to be blue, which forces both G and I to be red, giving a contradiction. The other case is similar. This implies that any tiling of a
12\times 20
rectangle with A/B blocks must contain a 3-term AP, yet there does exist a tiling of a
12\times 20
rectangle, as shown on the right in that figure, containing no 3-term AP.

Figure 8:
Every 2-coloring of a
3\times 5
grid has a 3-term monochromatic AP, but not every tiling of a
12\times 20
grid.

So instead of using just A/B blocks, for width
w=12
we have constructed tilings of all
12\times 4b
rectangles containing no AP of length 3, for
4\leq 4b\leq 32=4W(2,3)-4
. (All tilings that we have constructed can be found in the supplementary materials
[
9
]
.) This proves that
T_{12}(l)\geq 4W(2,l)
when
l=3
. For
l>3
, we may use stacked A/B tilings as we did for width 8, but with three identical rows instead of two. Finally, for width
w=16
, we have constructed tilings of all
16\times 4b
rectangles containing no AP of length 3, for
4\leq 4b\leq 32=4W(2,3)-4
, as well as tilings of all
16\times 4b
rectangles containing no AP of length 4, for
4\leq 4b\leq 136=4W(2,4)-4
. (See Figure
9
.) For
l>4
we may use four stacked identical A/B tilings.
\Box

16\times 136
rectangle with no AP of length 4.

By contrast we note that while
T_{20}(4)=140=4W(2,4)
,
T_{20}(3)=24
, which is less than
36=4W(2,3)
. See Figure

. Theorem

may be generalized to arbitrary widths, as long as the lower bound on

is made suitably large, since for any width, a large enough

5.1
2D van der Waerden Numbers

There seems to be no literature on APs of colored squares in a colored rectangular grid, so we mention it briefly here. As mentioned in the discussion of Figure
8
, every 2-coloring of the squares of a
3\times 5
grid must contain a monochromatic 3-term AP. A coloring with alternating vertical stripes shows that a 4-term AP may be avoided. If we define
l=L_{vdW}(h,w)
to be the greatest value

such that every 2-coloring of an
h\times w
grid must contain a monochromatic AP of length
l
, then we’ve shown that
L_{vdW}(3,5)=3
. Because of the connection between
L
and

given by the AB Lemma we wished to compute more values of the latter. Thanks to the obvious monotonicity of
L_{vdW}
this was much easier, and the results are shown in Figure

. That figure shows all values where
L_{vdW}(h,w)=3
, and the following list of
(h,w)
pairs show all sizes we know where

(4,100),(10,50),(15,40),(20,32),(24,24)
, together with those implied by symmetry (
