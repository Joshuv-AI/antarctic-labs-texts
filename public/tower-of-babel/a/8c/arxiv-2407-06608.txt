Iteratively Refined Image Reconstruction with Learned Attentive Regularizers
Mehrsa Pourya, Sebastian Neumayer, Michael Unser

Abstract
We propose a regularization scheme for image reconstruction that leverages the power of deep learning while hinging on classic sparsity-promoting models. Many deep-learning-based models are hard to interpret and cumbersome to analyze theoretically. In contrast, our scheme is interpretable because it corresponds to the minimization of a series of convex problems. For each problem in the series, a mask is generated based on the previous solution to refine the regularization strength spatially. In this way, the model becomes progressively attentive to the image structure. For the underlying update operator, we prove the existence of a fixed point. As a special case, we investigate a mask generator for which the fixed-point iterations converge to a critical point of an explicit energy functional. In our experiments, we match the performance of state-of-the-art learned variational models for the solution of inverse problems. Additionally, we offer a promising balance between interpretability, theoretical guarantees, reliability, and performance. Keywords: Convex regularization, data-driven priors, fixed-point equations, inverse problems, majorization minimization, solution-driven models

Introduction

In biomedical imaging
[
35
]
, including magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and computed tomography, reconstructions are often achieved via the resolution of an inverse problem.
Its task is to recover an unknown signal
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
from noisy measurements
{\mathbf{y}}={\bf{H}}{\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{n}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
, where
{\bf{H}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M,N}
encodes the data-acquisition process and the noise
{\mathbf{n}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
accounts for imperfections in this description.
From a variational perspective
[
41
]
, one defines the reconstruction as the solution to the minimization problem

\argmin_{{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\bigl({\mathrm{E}}({\bf{H}}{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})+\lambda\mathcal{R}({\mathbf{x}})\bigr),

(1)

which involves a data-fidelity term
{\mathrm{E}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and a regularizer
\mathcal{R}\colon\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
In (
1
), the data fidelity term ensures the consistency of the reconstruction with the measurements, while the regularization, whose strength is controlled by
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, imposes some regularity constraints (prior information) on the solution.

For a large variety of data-acquisition and noise models, a well-studied zoo of data fidelities
{\mathrm{E}}
can be found in the literature.
While an instance-specific
{\mathrm{E}}
is natural, it is desirable that the regularizer
\mathcal{R}
is agnostic to
{\bf{H}}
and
{\mathbf{n}}
and solely depends on the properties of the underlying images.
Hence, a regularizer that captures these inherent properties would be of great interest.
Attempts can be traced as far back as to the Tikhonov regularization
[
44
]
, where images are modeled as smooth signals.
Later, this approach was outperformed by compressed sensing
[
13
]
.
Such models either assume that the signal is sparse in some latent space (e.g., wavelet decomposition
[
34
]
) or involve a filter-based regularizer
\mathcal{R}
such as the total variation (TV)
[
40
,
13
]
and its generalizations
[
28
]
.
These classic signal-processing approaches achieve a baseline performance with the advantage that they provide stability and robustness guarantees
[
12
]
.

With the emergence of deep-learning techniques for the solution of inverse problems
[
4
]
, the traditional approaches have been outperformed in many applications.
The end-to-end training achieves state-of-the-art performance in terms of quantitative metrics such as the peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR).
However, such models are often neither interpretable nor trustworthy for sensitive applications such as biomedical imaging
[
25
,
2
]
.
Therefore, a recent line of research
[
32
,
33
,
26
,
15
]
is focusing on the use of deep learning for the solution of inverse problems within the variational framework (
1
).
There, instead of learning the whole reconstruction pipeline in an end-to-end manner, one only learns the regularizer
\mathcal{R}
.
Up to now, these models have relied mostly on deep architectures to parameterize
\mathcal{R}
, which makes an interpretation difficult.
To bypass this issue, the authors in
[
18
]
have proposed to parameterize the learnable
\mathcal{R}
as

\mathcal{R}\colon{\mathbf{x}}\mapsto\sum_{c=1}^{N_{\mathrm{C}}}\bigl\langle\mathbf{1}_{N},\bm{\psi}_{c}({\bf{W}}_{c}{\mathbf{x}})\bigr\rangle,

(2)

with channel-wise data-driven convolutional matrices
{\bf{W}}_{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{N,N}
and
\bm{\psi}_{c}({\mathbf{x}})=(\psi_{c}(x_{k}))_{k=1}^{N}
, where the convex and symmetric profiles
\psi_{c}
are members of
\mathcal{C}_{\geq 0}^{1,1}(\mathbb{R})
, the space of nonnegative differentiable functions with Lipschitz-continuous derivatives.
Based on the architecture (
2
), the authors of
[
18
]
obtain the best performance among known convex regularizers in their experiments.
Moreover, (
2
) has a clear interpretation as a filter-based regularizer.
To further improve the reconstruction performance, we need to look beyond convexity.
As an extension of the model (
2
), the authors of
[
19
]
have proposed to learn symmetric potentials
\psi_{c}\in\mathcal{C}_{\geq 0}^{1,1}(\mathbb{R})
with
\psi_{c}^{\prime\prime}\geq-\rho
a.e., namely
\rho
-weakly convex ones.
This relaxation significantly improves over the convex setting.
In particular, it gets close to the performance of the DRUNet-based model
[
22
]
, which is among the best-performing methods with a (loose) energy interpretation.

If

is strictly convex and differentiable, then (

Its unique minimum can be computed using the forward-backward splitting (FBS) algorithm

]
.
Hence, we can rewrite (
4

{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1}=T_{{\bf{\Lambda}},{\bf{W}},{\mathbf{y}}}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}).

(6)

In Theorem

, we prove that the iterations (

In (
5
), we can interpret

as a channel-wise spatial adaption of the regularization strength, which is attentive to image structures.
This viewpoint of solution-driven spatial adaptivity

,

serves as a starting point for the generalization of the MMR model in Section

.
More precisely, we propose to replace
\bm{\Lambda}
in (

) with a more expressive convolutional neural network
\tilde{{\bf{\Lambda}}}
.
For this, we relax the constraints on the activation functions and linear operators in the mask generator

associated with (
3
), see Figure
1
.
This leads to
the solution-adaptive fixed-point iterations (SAFI) as reconstruction scheme, which involves the regularizers

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{R}_{\text{SAFI},k}}\colon{\mathbf{x}}\mapsto\sum_{c=1}^{N_{\mathrm{C}}}\bigl\langle\tilde{{\bf{\Lambda}}}_{c}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}),\left\lvert{\bf{W}}_{c}{\mathbf{x}}\right\rvert\bigr\rangle.

(7)

In (
7
)
,
\tilde{{\bf{\Lambda}}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{N}\to([0,1]^{N})^{N_{C}}
is a 3-layer network with
\tilde{{\bf{\Lambda}}}_{c}({\mathbf{x}})=\bm{\phi}_{3,c}(\hat{{\bf{B}}}_{c}{\bm{\phi}}_{2}(\tilde{{\bf{B}}}{\bm{\phi}}_{1}(\tilde{{\bf{W}}}{\mathbf{x}})))
.
Its convolutional operators have dimensions

,
\tilde{{\bf{B}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N_{C}\cdot N),(N_{C}\cdot N)}
, and
\hat{{\bf{B}}}_{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{N,(N_{C}\cdot N)}
.
The activation functions
\bm{\phi}_{1}({\mathbf{x}})=(\phi_{1,\lceil k/N\rceil}(x_{k}))_{k=1}^{N_{C}\cdot N}
and
\bm{\phi}_{2}({\mathbf{x}})=(\phi_{2,\lceil k/N\rceil}(x_{k}))_{k=1}^{N_{C}\cdot N}
share linear splines
\phi_{r,c}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R})
on input blocks of size

.
The final activation functions are
\bm{\phi}_{3,c}({\mathbf{x}})=(\phi_{3,c}(x_{k}))_{k=1}^{N}
, where each

The latter enforces that the entries of each

[0,1]

For the regularizers (
7
), the minimization problem (
4

Therefore, each update (
6
) in the pipeline is numerically tractable and gives rise to an update operator
T_{\tilde{{\bf{\Lambda}}},{\bf{W}},{\mathbf{y}}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
In Theorem

, we prove that

admits at least one fixed point.
In this relaxed setting, the convergence of the SAFI scheme to a fixed point is encouraged by the use of regularization techniques during training
[
1
]
.
The parameterization details for the architectures (
5
) and (
7
) are given in Section
3.3
.
The learning of the associated parameters on denoising problems is discussed in Section
4
.

Our numerical evaluation for both denoising and MRI reconstruction in Section
5
indicates that the learning of the parameters
{\bf{W}}
,
{\bf{B}}
, and
\bm{\psi}
in (

) leads to a reconstruction performance similar to that of the weakly convex model from

with

.
By setting

in (

), we obtain a weakly convex regularizer without a bound on

as special case.
Hence, our theoretical analysis, corroborated by the numerical results, leads to yet another reasonable explanation for the performance gain of the weakly convex model
[

]
over the convex one
[
18
]
.
With the more general regularizer (
7
) associated to SAFI, the performance gets similar to that of
[
37
]
, despite the much simpler mask generator

Relation to Previous Work

Our regularizer
\mathcal{R}_{\text{MMR}}
relies on the architecture (
2
) from
[
19
]
, where we add the inner activation
|\cdot|
and the nonnegative convolutional matrices
{\bf{B}}_{c}
.
The decomposition
\psi_{c}=\mu\psi_{c,\mathrm{cvx}}+\psi_{c,\mathrm{ccv}}
is proposed in
[
19
]
with a convex
\psi_{c,\mathrm{cvx}}\in\smash{\mathcal{C}_{\geq 0}^{1,1}(\mathbb{R})}
, a concave

.
The convex part
\psi_{c,\mathrm{cvx}}
of the learned

is necessary to maintain differentiability at

.
Since our

only takes positive inputs, we do not have this issue and can drop the term

.
Further, we relax
\rho
from
1
to
\infty
to fully explore the role of concavity.
Given that the experimental results are similar, we do not expect that the inclusion of a convex part
\psi_{c,\mathrm{cvx}}
in (
3
) leads to a significant gain in performance.

For the regularizer
\mathcal{R}_{\text{SAFI},k}
, we use the absolute value
|\cdot|
instead of non-convex potentials, as proposed in
[
37
,
36
]
. Hence, the subproblem (

This is stronger than the mere convergence to stationary points of
[
37
]

Since we learn

]

Moreover, in contrast to these approaches, we iteratively refine the mask in

.
This leads to implicit depth, which is a possible explanation for why complex generators

are not required in our framework.

The majorization-minimization (MM) perspective also shows up in
[
29
]
, which deploys MM iterations to minimize a spatially adaptive model that is similar to
[
37
,
36
]
.
To ensure closed-form solutions for the minimization problems (

), the authors deploy

as potentials instead of

in (

In contrast to the SAFI approach, their masks

for the MM iterations are induced completely by the underlying regularizer.

Preliminaries

Throughout this work,
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
denotes a closed convex set.

Concave Functions

A function
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be
concave
if it satisfies

f\bigl(\alpha{\mathbf{x}}_{1}+(1-\alpha){\mathbf{x}}_{2}\bigr)\geq\alpha f({\mathbf{x}}_{1})+(1-\alpha)f({\mathbf{x}}_{2}),\qquad\forall{\mathbf{x}}_{1},{\mathbf{x}}_{2}\in\mathcal{X},\quad\forall\alpha\in[0,1].

(8)

If
\mathcal{X}
is open and
f\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathcal{X})
, then
f
is concave if and only if its gradient
{\mathbf{\nabla}}f
satisfies

\bigl\langle{\mathbf{\nabla}}f({\mathbf{x}}_{1})-{\mathbf{\nabla}}f({\mathbf{x}}_{2}),{\mathbf{x}}_{1}-{\mathbf{x}}_{2}\bigr\rangle\leq 0,\qquad\forall{\mathbf{x}}_{1},{\mathbf{x}}_{2}\in\mathcal{X}.

(9)

In the special case
N=1
, condition (
9
) simply states that the derivative
f^{\prime}
is non-increasing on
\mathcal{X}
.
Another useful property is that any differentiable concave function
f
is upper-bounded by its first-order Taylor expansion

f({\mathbf{x}}_{1})\leq f({\mathbf{x}}_{2})+\langle{\mathbf{\nabla}}f({\mathbf{x}}_{2}),{\mathbf{x}}_{1}-{\mathbf{x}}_{2}\rangle,\qquad\forall{\mathbf{x}}_{1},{\mathbf{x}}_{2}\in\mathcal{X}.

(10)

A function
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is
convex
if and only if
(-f)
is concave.

Majorization-Minimization Algorithm

For a deeper exposition to MM algorithms, we refer to
[
21
,
16
,
43
]
.
Here, we only collect some basic definitions and the core results.
For a continuous
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, we investigate the problem

\argmin_{{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}f({\mathbf{x}}).

(11)

The idea behind MM algorithms is to replace
f
by a sequence of (approximating) majorizations
g(\cdot,{\mathbf{x}}_{k})
,
{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\in\mathcal{X}
for which the computation of a (global) minimizer is tractable.
A function
g\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be a
majorization
of
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
if it satisfies

i)

the upper-bound
f({\mathbf{x}})\leq g({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{x}}_{k}),\quad\forall{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\in\mathcal{X}
;

ii)

and the local tight bound
g({\mathbf{x}}_{k},{\mathbf{x}}_{k})=f({\mathbf{x}}_{k}),\quad\forall{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\in\mathcal{X}.

Next, we introduce the formal MM algorithm together with a convergence result
[
43
,
23
]
.

Theorem 1
.

For a continuous
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
with majorization
g\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
and a starting point
{\mathbf{x}}_{1}\in\mathcal{X}
, the MM sequence is given by

{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1}\in\argmin_{{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}g({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{x}}_{k}),

(12)

and the function values
f({\mathbf{x}}_{k})
are non-increasing.
If
g
is continuous,
f
and every
g(\cdot,{\mathbf{x}}_{k})
is continuously differentiable, and the sub-level set
\{{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{X}:f({\mathbf{x}})\leq f({\mathbf{x}}_{1})\}
is compact,
then all accumulation points of
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
are critical points of
f
.
Moreover, if the set
\mathcal{X}^{*}=\{{\mathbf{x}}:\langle{\mathbf{\nabla}}f({\mathbf{x}}),{\mathbf{z}}-{\mathbf{x}}\rangle\geq 0,\ \forall{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathcal{X}\}
is a singleton or if
\mathcal{X}^{*}
is discrete and
\lim_{k\to\infty}\|{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\|_{2}\to 0
, then the MM iterations
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converge to a critical point of
f
.

Remark 1
.

The condition that
\mathcal{X}^{*}
is a singleton is met if
f
is strongly convex, namely if
(f-\tfrac{\sigma}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}_{2})
is convex for some
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
Hence, we get in this setting global convergence guarantees that are similar to those of convex-minimization algorithms.

\Gamma
-Convergence

Here, we recall the basic concepts of
\Gamma
-convergence within our Euclidean framework and refer to
[
9
]
for a more detailed exposition.
A family of functions
\{J_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

with
J_{k}\colon\mathcal{X}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
is said to
\Gamma
-converge to
J\colon\mathcal{X}\rightarrow[0,\infty]

if the following two conditions are fulfilled for every
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{X}
:

i)

for all
{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\to{\mathbf{x}}
, it holds that
J({\mathbf{x}})\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}J_{k}({\mathbf{x}}_{k})
;

ii)

for every
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{X}
, there is a sequence
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
with
{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\to{\mathbf{x}}
and
\limsup_{k\to\infty}J_{k}({\mathbf{x}}_{k})\leq J({\mathbf{x}})
.

The importance of
\Gamma
-convergence is captured by Theorem
2
.
Recall that a family of functions
J_{k}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is equi-coercive if it is bounded from below by a coercive function.

Theorem 2

(Theorem of
\Gamma
-convergence
[
9
]
)
.

Let
\{J_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be an equi-coercive family of functions
J_{k}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
.
If
J_{k}

\Gamma
-converges to
J
, then it holds that

i)

the optimal function values converge
\lim_{k\to\infty}\inf_{{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}J_{k}({\mathbf{x}})=\inf_{{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}J({\mathbf{x}})
;

ii)

all accumulation points of the minimizers of
J_{k}
are minimizers of
J
.

In particular, if all the
J_{k}
and
J
have unique minimizers, then Theorem
2
directly implies convergence of the minimizers of the
J_{k}
to the one of
J
.

3.1
Majorization-Minimization Regularization

For the MMR model
, we specify
\mathcal{R}
in the generic problem (
1
) as (

1
1

1

is continuous.

\bm{\psi}_{c}({\bf{B}}_{c,n}|{\bf{W}}_{c}\cdot|)

critical points
of the non-convex minimization problem

\argmin_{{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}\Big(\frac{1}{2}\left\rVert{\bf{H}}{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lambda\sum_{c=1}^{N_{\mathrm{C}}}\bigl\langle\smash{\tilde{{\bf{\Lambda}}}_{c}}({\mathbf{x}}),|{\bf{W}}_{c}{\mathbf{x}}|\bigr\rangle\Big),

(34)

where one also minimizes over the input of
\smash{\tilde{{\bf{\Lambda}}}_{c}}
.

Following the discussed ideas, we proceed as outlined in Figure
1
and Section
1
, and replace
{\bf{\Lambda}}_{c}({\mathbf{x}})
by the richer architecture
\smash{\tilde{{\bf{\Lambda}}}_{c}}({\mathbf{x}})=\bm{\phi}_{3,c}(\hat{{\bf{B}}}_{c}{\bm{\phi}}_{2}(\tilde{{\bf{B}}}{\bm{\phi}}_{1}(\tilde{{\bf{W}}}{\mathbf{x}})))
.
As observed in
[
37
]
, one expects that
\smash{\tilde{{\bf{\Lambda}}}_{c}}
dampens the response of the
{\bf{W}}_{c}
to structure and leaves it unchanged for noise or artifacts.
Under some conditions, we can show that
T_{\tilde{{\bf{\Lambda}}},{\bf{W}},{\mathbf{y}}}
admits indeed at least one fixed point.
Hence, the definition of reconstructions as fixed points of the operator
T_{\tilde{{\bf{\Lambda}}},{\bf{W}},{\mathbf{y}}}
makes sense.

Theorem 5
.

Let
{\bf{H}}
be invertible and let
\sigma_{\min}
denote its smallest singular value.
Then,
T_{\tilde{{\bf{\Lambda}}},{\bf{W}},{\mathbf{y}}}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
maps
\mathcal{X}
into a ball centered at
{\mathbf{0}}
with radius
2\left\rVert{\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert_{2}/\sigma_{\min}
.
Further,
T_{\tilde{{\bf{\Lambda}}},{\bf{W}},{\mathbf{y}}}
admits a fixed point.

Proof.

First, we investigate the range of
T_{\tilde{{\bf{\Lambda}}},{\bf{W}},{\mathbf{y}}}
.
By definition of
T_{\tilde{{\bf{\Lambda}}},{\bf{W}},{\mathbf{y}}}
, it holds for any
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{X}
that

\frac{1}{2}\bigl\|{\bf{H}}T_{\tilde{{\bf{\Lambda}}},{\bf{W}},{\mathbf{y}}}({\mathbf{x}})-{\mathbf{y}}\bigr\|_{2}^{2}\leq J(T_{\tilde{{\bf{\Lambda}}},{\bf{W}},{\mathbf{y}}}({\mathbf{x}}),{\mathbf{x}})\leq J({\mathbf{0}},{\mathbf{x}})=\frac{1}{2}\left\rVert{\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

(35)

From this, we conclude that

\bigl\|T_{\tilde{{\bf{\Lambda}}},{\bf{W}},{\mathbf{y}}}({\mathbf{x}})\bigr\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\sigma_{\min}}\bigl\|{\bf{H}}T_{\tilde{{\bf{\Lambda}}},{\bf{W}},{\mathbf{y}}}({\mathbf{x}})\bigr\|_{2}\leq 2\frac{\left\rVert{\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert_{2}}{\sigma_{\min}}.

(36)

For the second part, we want to apply Brouwer’s fixed-point theorem.
To this end, we additionally need to prove that
T_{\tilde{{\bf{\Lambda}}},{\bf{W}},{\mathbf{y}}}
is continuous.
Due to Theorem
2
, it suffices to check equi-coercivity and the conditions for
\Gamma
-convergence of the family
J(\cdot,{\mathbf{x}})
parameterized by
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{X}
.
First, note that it holds for any
{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathcal{X}
that

\frac{\sigma_{\min}^{2}}{4}\|{\mathbf{z}}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4}\|{\bf{H}}{\mathbf{z}}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|{\bf{H}}{\mathbf{z}}-{\mathbf{y}}\|_{2}^{2}+\|{\mathbf{y}}\|_{2}^{2}\leq J({\mathbf{z}},{\mathbf{x}})+\|{\mathbf{y}}\|_{2}^{2},

(37)

which implies equi-coercivity.
Now, let
{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\to{\mathbf{x}}^{*}
and
{\mathbf{z}}_{k}\to{\mathbf{z}}^{*}
.
By the triangle inequality, we get that

|J({\mathbf{z}}^{*},{\mathbf{x}}^{*})-J({\mathbf{z}}_{k},{\mathbf{x}}_{k})|\leq|J({\mathbf{z}}^{*},{\mathbf{x}}^{*})-J({\mathbf{z}}^{*},{\mathbf{x}}_{k})|+|J({\mathbf{z}}^{*},{\mathbf{x}}_{k})-J({\mathbf{z}}_{k},{\mathbf{x}}_{k})|.

(38)

To obtain the
\liminf
inequality, it suffices to prove that the first two terms converge to
0
as
k\to\infty
.
For the first one, we have that

|J({\mathbf{z}}^{*},{\mathbf{x}}^{*})-J({\mathbf{z}}^{*},{\mathbf{x}}_{k})|\leq\lambda\sum_{c=1}^{N_{\mathrm{C}}}\bigl\langle|\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}_{c}({\mathbf{x}}^{*})-\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}_{c}({\mathbf{x}}_{k})|,|{\bf{W}}_{c}{\mathbf{z}}^{*}|\bigr\rangle\to 0

(39)

because
{\bf{\Lambda}}_{c}
is continuous.
For the second one, we have that

\displaystyle|J({\mathbf{z}}^{*},{\mathbf{x}}_{k})-J({\mathbf{z}}_{k},{\mathbf{x}}_{k})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\Bigr|\|{\bf{H}}{\mathbf{z}}^{*}-{\mathbf{y}}\|_{2}^{2}-\|{\bf{H}}{\mathbf{z}}_{k}-{\mathbf{y}}\|_{2}^{2}\Bigr|+\lambda\sum_{c=1}^{N_{\mathrm{C}}}\bigl\langle|\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}_{c}({\mathbf{x}}_{k})|,\bigl||{\bf{W}}_{c}{\mathbf{z}}^{*}|-|{\bf{W}}_{c}{\mathbf{z}}_{k}|\bigr|\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\Bigr|\|{\bf{H}}{\mathbf{z}}^{*}-{\mathbf{y}}\|_{2}^{2}-\|{\bf{H}}{\mathbf{z}}_{k}-{\mathbf{y}}\|_{2}^{2}\Bigr|+\lambda\sum_{c=1}^{N_{\mathrm{C}}}\bigl\langle{\mathbf{1}}_{N},|{\bf{W}}_{c}{\mathbf{z}}^{*}-{\bf{W}}_{c}{\mathbf{z}}_{k}|\bigr\rangle.

(40)

Again, we conclude that this quantity converges to zero.
Hence, we have established the
\liminf
inequality.
For the
\limsup
inequality, we use the constant recovery sequence
{\mathbf{x}}_{k}={\mathbf{x}}^{*}
, for which the claim follows as in (
3.2
).
In summary, this implies that
T_{\tilde{{\bf{\Lambda}}},{\bf{W}},{\mathbf{y}}}
is continuous and that a fixed point exists.
∎

3.3
Parameterization of the Learnable Parameters

We now provide details of the parameterization for our two solution-adaptive regularizers.

The regularization strength
\lambda
in (
13
) and (
32
) is learnable for the corresponding reconstruction models.

For the
MMR
model (
13
) from Section
3.1
, the remaining parameters are the linear operators
\{{\bf{W}}
,
{\bf{B}}\}
and the concave potentials in
{\bf{\Psi}}
.
For the
SAFI
problem (
32
) from Section
3.2
, the remaining parameters are the linear operators
\{\tilde{{\bf{W}}}
,
\tilde{{\bf{B}}}
,
\hat{{\bf{B}}}\}
and the activation functions
\{\bm{\phi}_{1}
,
\bm{\phi}_{2}
,
\bm{\phi}_{3}\}
.
Taking a closer look at Algorithms
1
-
3
, we observe that we actually only need access to
{\bf{\Psi}}^{\prime}
and not to
{\bf{\Psi}}
itself.
Hence, we directly parameterize the derivatives
{\bf{\Psi}}^{\prime}
instead.

Parameterization of Linear Operators

All linear operators are constructed with the
Conv2d
module from PyTorch.
Here, we only detail the construction for the output dimension
N_{C}
.
More specifically, we decompose each operator into
S
stacked
Conv2d
modules; each with
N_{C}
output channels, a kernel size
(k_{s}\times k_{s})
, and a group size
G
.
This was observed to be more effective than the direct use of a single
Conv2d
module with a larger kernel size
[
18
,
19
]
.
Here, the group size
G
controls the potential transfer of information across the different channels.
In particular, if
G=1
, then each kernel of the
k
th layer,
k\in{2,\ldots,S}
, is convolved with all the ones of the
(k-1)
th layer.
If
G={N_{C}}
, then each kernel is only convolved with the one of its channel.

Constrained Linear Operators

We impose constraints on some convolution kernels.
All the kernels of
{\bf{W}}
and
\tilde{{\bf{W}}}
should have zero mean.
To ensure this, let
{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{s}^{2}}
contain the vectorized elements of the respective kernel.
Then, we can use the parameterization
{\mathbf{w}}\mapsto({\mathbf{w}}-({\mathbf{1}}^{\top}{\mathbf{w}})/k_{s}^{2})
, and optimize over unconstrained variables.
For
{\bf{B}}
, we impose that the kernel elements are positive and sum to one.
Let
{\mathbf{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{s}^{2}}
be the vectorized kernel elements.
Here, the implementation of the constraint is nonnegative with the parameterization
{\mathbf{b}}\mapsto(\left\lvert{\mathbf{b}}\right\rvert({\mathbf{1}}^{\top}\left\lvert{\mathbf{b}}\right\rvert))
.
Note that
\left\lvert\cdot\right\rvert
is applied element-wise to
{\mathbf{b}}
.

Learnable Activation Functions

For the
\{\bm{\phi}_{1}
,
\bm{\phi}_{2}
,
\bm{\phi}_{3}\}
in
\tilde{{\bf{\Lambda}}}
, we rely on the learnable linear-spline framework introduced in
[
8
]
.
More precisely, we use a uniform grid centered at
0
with stepsize
\Delta
and
2M+1
points,
M\in\mathbb{N}
, and the B-spline of degree one defined as

\beta^{1}(x)=\begin{cases}1-|x|,&x\in[-1,1]\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(41)

Then, we parameterize each
\phi_{p,c}
based on the vector
\mathbf{d}_{p,c}\in\mathbb{R}^{2M+1}
of function values at the grid points as

\phi_{p,c}(x)=\begin{cases}d_{p,c,1}+\frac{d_{p,c,2}-d_{p,c,1}}{\Delta}\left(x+M\Delta\right),&x\in\left(-\infty,-M\Delta\right)\\
\sum_{k=-M}^{M}d_{p,c,k+M+1}\beta^{1}(x/\Delta-k),&x\in\left[-M\Delta,M\Delta\right]\\
d_{p,c,2M+1}+\frac{d_{p,c,2M+1}-d_{p,c,2M}}{\Delta}\left(x-M\Delta\right),&x\in\left(M\Delta,\infty\right).\end{cases}

(42)

In particular,
\phi_{p,c}
is nonlinear on
[-M\Delta,M\Delta]
and extrapolated linearly outside of this interval.

Concave Potentials

For the
MMR
model, we parameterize the
\psi_{c}^{\prime}
,
c=1,\ldots,N_{C}
, as

\psi_{c}^{\prime}(x)=\mathrm{clip}_{[0,1]}\big(\sigma_{c}(r_{c}x)\big),

(43)

where
\sigma_{c}\colon\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}
are learnable linear splines and
r_{c}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
are learnable scaling constants that adapt the range.
To parameterize
\{r_{c}\}_{c=1}^{N_{C}}
, we use the
nn.Parameter
module of
PyTorch
.
To ensure their positivity, we use
\left\lvert r_{c}\right\rvert
instead of
r_{c}
in the implementation.
As
\sigma_{c}
is only defined on
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, we parameterize it with its
M+1
values on the nonnegative part of the grid from (
42
) denoted by
{\mathbf{d}}_{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{M+1}
.
As
\psi_{c}
must be concave, its derivative
\psi_{c}^{\prime}
is constrained to be non-increasing on
\mathbb{R}
.
This can be achieved by using a non-increasing
\sigma_{c}
with
\sigma_{c}(0)=1
.
To enforce the condition
\sigma_{c}(0)=1
, it suffices to fix
d_{c,0}=1
.
Let
{\bf{D}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M,M+1}
be defined via
({\bf{D}}{\mathbf{d}}_{c})_{m-1}=(d_{c,m}-d_{c,m-1})
,
m=2,\ldots,M+1
.
If all elements of
{\bf{D}}{\mathbf{d}}_{c}
are non-positive, then
\sigma_{c}
is non-increasing.
To directly embed this constraint into the parameterization, we define

\bm{P}_{\downarrow}({\mathbf{d}}_{c})={\bf{S}}\textbf{clip}_{[-\infty,0]}({\bf{D}}{\mathbf{d}}_{c})+{\mathbf{1}}_{M+1},

(44)

where
{\bf{S}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M+1,M}
with
({\bf{S}}{\mathbf{d}}_{c})_{m}=\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}d_{c,k}
,
m=1,\ldots,M+1
.
By projecting the unconstrained coefficients
{\mathbf{d}}_{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{M+1}
to
\bm{P}_{\downarrow}({\mathbf{d}}_{c})
, we ensure that the corresponding
\sigma_{c}
is non-increasing.
With the proposed parameterization, the associated concave profiles
\psi_{c}\colon\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
satisfy the following properties.

i)

They are piecewise-quadratic, nonnegative, and increasing.

ii)

We have that
0\leq\psi^{\prime}_{c}(x)\leq 1
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
\psi^{\prime}_{c}(0)=1
.

4
Architecture and Training

For our regularizers (
5
) and (
7
), we now describe the learning of the parameters detailed in Section
3.3
.
For both architectures and their respective reconstruction routines Algorithm
3
and
4
, we learn them by solving a denoising problem with additive white Gaussian noise of standard deviation
\sigma\in\{5/255,15/255,25/255\}
.
Since the training procedure is exactly the same for both architectures, we restrict our discussion to (
5
).

Let
\{{\mathbf{x}}^{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
with
{\mathbf{x}}^{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{40\times 40}
be a set of clean patches from the grayscale BSD500 dataset
[
3
]
, and let
\{{\mathbf{y}}^{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}=\{{\mathbf{x}}^{m}+{\mathbf{n}}_{\sigma}^{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
be some noisy versions, where
{\mathbf{n}}_{\sigma}^{m}
is a realization of the noise.
For all experiments, we train with
M=238400
patches.
In the following, we collect all the learnable parameters of (
5
) in the variable
{\mathbf{\theta}}
.
Given a noisy patch
{\mathbf{y}}^{m}
, we obtain its denoised version
D^{n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}}_{{\mathbf{\theta}},\sigma}({\mathbf{y}}^{m})
by applying Algorithm
3
with
K_{\mathrm{out}}=n_{1}
steps.
As discussed in Section
3.1
, fixing
K_{\mathrm{FBS}}=n_{2}=1
for Algorithm
1
suffices to guarantee convergence in the denoising case.
For calculating the involved
\prox_{\gamma\|{\bf{L}}\cdot\|_{1}}
based on Algorithm
2
, we use
K_{\mathrm{FBS}}=n_{3}
steps.
During the training phase, all the tolerances are set to
(-1)
to ensure that the maximum number of steps is used.
Now, we propose to learn the optimal parameters
\hat{{\mathbf{\theta}}}
in
D^{n_{1},1,n_{3}}_{{\mathbf{\theta}},\sigma}
based on the empirical risk

\hat{{\mathbf{\theta}}}\in\argmin_{{\mathbf{\theta}}}\sum_{n_{1}=4}^{6}\sum_{n_{3}=10}^{12}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\bigl\|D^{n_{1},1,n_{3}}_{{\mathbf{\theta}},\sigma}({\mathbf{x}}^{m}+{\mathbf{n}}_{\sigma}^{m})-{\mathbf{x}}^{m}\bigr\|_{2}^{2}.

(45)

To solve (
45
), we use the ADAM optimizer
[
24
]
with a learning rate of
10^{-3}
and a batch size of
128
patches that are reconstructed for a single pair
(n_{1},n_{3})
.
This pair is uniformly drawn at random from one of the possible values for each batch.
As documented in
[
1
]
, using a random numbers of iterations has a regularizing effect when unrolling fixed-point iterations.
In particular, this prevents the models from getting overfitted to a specific number of iterations.
We perform
40
training epochs and reduce the learning rate by a factor of
0.1
at the
5
th and
10
th epoch.
After each epoch, we evaluate the performance of the model on the Set12 validation data, and choose the output model as the one with the best performance.
As a result, we obtain the regularization strength in (
13
) as well as the linear layers and the potentials that appear in (
5
).

Remark 4
.

Instead of pursuing an unrolling approach for training, one can also aim to minimize (
45
) for
n_{1}=n_{3}=\infty
with implicit-differentiation techniques
[
5
,
17
]
.
However, as already observed in
[

, it is usually unnecessary to fully compute the involved fixed points in

to learn good parameters

for the regularizer.
Moreover, as we have two nested fixed-point problems, namely the problems (

) and (
23
), this easily gets prohibitively expensive.

Fine Tuning

The interpretability of the learned denoiser
D^{n_{1},1,n_{3}}_{{\mathbf{\theta}},\sigma}
with the small
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
from the training stage is, however, limited.
In particular, we only perform a partial minimization (unrolling) of (
13
).
To remain within our theoretical setup, we need to iterate Algorithms
1
-
3
until convergence during the evaluation phase.
Doing so without modifying the regularization strength
\lambda
in (
13
) has led to over-smoothing in our experiments.
Moreover, the training of the model is for denoising only and not necessarily adapted to other inverse problems with
{\bf{H}}\neq\textbf{Id}
.
To deal with these issues, we propose to deploy (
5
) with the previously learned parameters for (
13
) and solely fine-tune
\lambda
on a small set of task-specific validation data with a coarse-to-fine grid search, as described in
[
18
]
.
Hence, we get two different denoisers for our numerical evaluation:
first, the unrolled version
D^{n_{1},1,n_{3}}_{{\mathbf{\theta}},\sigma}
, which is exactly what we have trained for, but which is not necessarily a fixed point of (
33
);
second, the
exact
fixed point
D^{\infty,1,\infty}_{{\mathbf{\theta}},\sigma}
with an adapted
\lambda
, which uses more iterations and for which our theoretical analysis holds.
For the inverse problems, we use the parameters of the denoising models that are trained with
\sigma=15/255
.
Here, we only have the fixed-point-based reconstruction operator as we do not train for the task.

Algorithm Hyperparameters for Evaluation

We aim to iterate Algorithms
1
-
3
until convergence, namely, up to machine precision.
Still, we enforce an upper bound on the number of iterations for all algorithms.
This ensures that we always remain within a reasonable computational budget.
Independent of
{\bf{H}}
, we set
K_{\mathrm{prox}}=500
and
K_{\mathrm{out}}=10
.
For the denoising case with
{\bf{H}}=\textbf{Id}
, we know that
K_{\mathrm{FBS}}=1
suffices for convergence.
There, we use the iteration-dependent tolerance

f_{\epsilon,{\mathrm{prox}}}(k_{\mathrm{out}},k_{\mathrm{FBS}})=\begin{cases}$1\text{\cdot}{10}^{-3}$(0.01)^{\frac{k_{\mathrm{out}}}{5}},&k_{\mathrm{out}}\leq 5\\
10^{-5},&k_{\mathrm{out}}>5.\\
\end{cases}

(46)

For
{\bf{H}}\neq\textbf{Id}
, we set
K_{\mathrm{FBS}}=1000
and use the iteration-dependent tolerances

f_{\epsilon,\mathrm{FBS}}(k_{\mathrm{out}})=\begin{cases}10^{-3}(0.01)^{\frac{k_{\mathrm{out}}}{5}},&k_{\mathrm{out}}\leq 5\\
10^{-5},&k_{\mathrm{out}}>5,\\
\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad f_{\epsilon,{\mathrm{prox}}}(k_{\mathrm{out}},k_{\mathrm{FBS}})=\begin{cases}3\epsilon_{\mathrm{FBS}}(\frac{1}{9})^{\frac{k_{\mathrm{FBS}}}{50}},&k_{\mathrm{FBS}}\leq 50\\
\frac{\epsilon_{\mathrm{FBS}}}{3},&k_{\mathrm{FBS}}>50.\\
\end{cases}

(47)

To summarize, for efficiency, the inner subproblems are solved with lower precision early on, while the precision for the later stages is higher to ensure convergence.
This is a common technique to accelerate majorization minimization models
[
43
]
and the FBS algorithm
[
42
,
48
]
.

Overall, this results in a regularizer for which the image structures are less penalized.
To conclude, the SAFI scheme leads to a better reconstruction performance than MMR model.

5.3
Algorithmic Aspects for MMR and SAFI

Initialization

In principle, the proposed MMR and SAFI reconstructions depend on the initialization of the schemes.
The initializations are required to compute the first masks
\bm{\Lambda}
and
\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}
for MMR and SAFI, respectively.
In Algorithms
3
and
4
, we initialize with the solution of the nonadaptive convex problems (
21
) and (
32
) with
\bm{\Lambda}_{c}=\bm{1}_{N}
and
\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}_{c}=\bm{1}_{N}
, respectively. These plain experiments are denoted by CVX.
To evaluate the sensitivity to this choice, we compare it against two alternatives.
First, we perturb the proposed initialization by additive white Gaussian noise with
\sigma=15/255
. These noisy experiments are denoted by Perturbed CVX.
As an even stronger deviation, we use a random initialization, where each entry is drawn from the standard normal distribution. These challenging experiments are denoted by Random.
The PSNR values of the respective reconstructions for the MRI experiment from Figure
8
are given in Table
3
.
Most importantly, we observe that all variants eventually lead to the same PSNR.
This indicates that the fixed point does not depend on the initialization.
Unsurprisingly, the convergence to this fixed point occurs faster with a better initialization.
We also observe the same behavior for other images.
Finally, as indicated in Algorithms
1
and
2
, the involved convex subproblems are always initialized with the minimizer of the previous one to accelerate the convergence.

Computational Complexity

For the discussed MRI setups, the iterative SAFI approach is on average five times slower than the Prox-DRUNet approach, which does not incorporate any refinement steps.
Reconstruction methods with a similar regularization architecture that do not incorporate a mask refinement (such as WCRR) can be even 50 times faster than SAFI.
Memory-wise, SAFI has almost 10 times fewer parameters than ProxDRUNet and about 100 times more than WCRR.
Since our approach brings valuable insights regarding weakly convex and spatially adaptive regularization, future work should focus on the improvement of the computational effectiveness of the approach.
Since the subproblems (
21
) and (
32
) are convex, we can choose from a rich pool of methods for this goal.
Moreover, we can draw from the literature on accelerating MM iterations
[
21
,
43
]
.

Conclusion

We have proposed to use an iterative majorization-minimization regularization (MMR) along with solution-adaptive-fixed-point iterations (SAFI) as new families of data-driven regularizers. They give rise to a sequence of convex reconstruction problems.
Numerically, the minimizers associated with this sequence converged to a fixed point in all of our experiments.
Overall, this leads to a robust, universal, and interpretable regularization method for inverse problems.
A benefit of our simple mask generator for SAFI is that it is well-suited to the enforcement of Lipschitz constraints, which are in turn important to obtain stability estimates.
Such constraints might be the key to the proof of the convergence of the fixed point iterations.
Finally, it could also be interesting to explore other architectural constraints for generating the masks to obtain theoretical guarantees.
