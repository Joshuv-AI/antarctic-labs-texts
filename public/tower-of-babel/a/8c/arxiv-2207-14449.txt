The subconvexity problem for Rankin–Selberg and triple product L-functions
Yueke Hu, Philippe Michel, Paul Nelson

Abstract
. In this paper we study the subconvexity problem for the Rankin-Selberg L-function and triple product L-function, allowing joint ramifications and conductor dropping range. We first extend the method of Michel-Venkatesh to reduce the bounds for L-functions to local conjectures on test vectors, then verify these local conjectures under certain conditions, giving new subconvexity bounds as long as the representations are not completely related.



1.
Introduction

Let
F
be a number field with ring of adeles
{\mathbb{A}}
, and let
\Pi
be an automorphic representation of a reductive group
G
. Let
L(\Pi,s)
be the L-function associated to
\Pi
. The subconvexity problem for the central value
L(\Pi,1/2)
is to obtain
a (non-trivial) bound of the shape

L(\Pi,1/2)\ll_{F}C(\Pi)^{1/4-\delta}

where
\delta>0
is some positive absolute constant (independent of
\Pi
) and
C(\Pi)
is the analytic conductor of
\Pi
which is a product over all places of the local analytic conductors
C_{v}(\Pi)
.

In the case
G={\text{GL}}_{1}
,
{\text{GL}}_{2}
, the subconvexity problem has now been solved completely through the efforts of many people over the years, starting with the work of Weyl
[
49
]
and ending with
[
34
]
with important intermediary results along the way such as
[
4
,
14
,
11
,
8
,
9
,
7
,
2
,
47
]
.

Beyond
{\text{GL}}_{2}
this problem is far from well-understood, even for the Rankin–Selberg case
{\text{GL}}_{2}\times{\text{GL}}_{2}
. Here we briefly summarize the progress for Rankin–Selberg L-functions.

The problem is solved if one assumes that the conductor of one of the representations,
C(\pi_{1})
say, is fixed, or more generally, is bounded by a small enough but fixed positive power of the conductor of the other (see
[
29
,
46
,
12
,
47
,
34
,
24
]
.
Some sporadic cases are known when both conductors vary simultaneously and have comparable sizes, as in
[
16
,
18
,
50
,
32
]
.
These works usually assume disjoint ramifications.

In contrast the problem remains open when
\pi_{2,v}\simeq\tilde{\pi}_{1,v}
at places with ramifications, as in the QUE case.
We shall refer to this case as the QUE-like case later on.

It is natural to ask if one can bridge the gap between the known cases and the QUE-like cases, allowing joint ramifications and some conductor dropping away from QUE-like cases for
\pi_{i}
.

Alternatively one can understand this problem using the Langlands functoriality and view, for example, the Rankin–Selberg L-function as a special case of standard L-functions on
{\text{GL}}_{4}
. There has been some important progresses in the subconvexity problem for L-functions on higher rank groups as in
[
30
,
36
]
, and most recently
[
39
]
. But the available results are specific to the “uniform growth” cases where the conductor does not drop. In practice, the techniques employed in these papers depend polynomially upon the extent to which the conductor drops, so they can be extended to the range where the conductor drops a little.
However, it is generally much more difficult to let the conductor drop substantially.

In this paper we obtain new cases of subconvexity bounds for the Rankin–Selberg and triple product L-functions of
{\text{GL}}_{2}
, allowing general number fields, joint ramifications and conductor dropping range away from the QUE-like cases.
In the non-archimedean aspect a special case of our main results states the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
be cuspidal automorphic representations of
{\text{GL}}_{2,F}
both with trivial central character whose archimedean components are bounded. Then

(1.1)

L(\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2},1/2)\ll C(\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2})^{1/4+\varepsilon}\left(\frac{C(\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2})}{C(\pi_{2}\times\tilde{\pi}_{2})}\right)^{-\delta}.

By re-indexing if necessary, the above upper bound provides a non-trivial power saving whenever
there exists fixed
\gamma>0
such that

(1.2)

\min_{i=1,2}\{C(\pi_{i}\times\tilde{\pi}_{i})\}\leq C(\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2})^{1-\gamma}.

Thus we obtain a subconvexity bound for the Rankin–Selberg L-function on
{\mathrm{PGL}}_{2}(F)
even when the conductor drops all the way down except the QUE-like cases.

Note that when
C_{v}(\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2})
is large enough, we have by
[
6
, Corollary 3.1]

C_{v}(\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2})\geq\max_{i=1,2}\{C_{v}(\pi_{i}\times\tilde{\pi}_{i})\},

with equality in the QUE-like case.

1.1.
Conductor dropping range

Here we specify the meaning of conductor dropping range locally in terms of the Langlands correspondence. We fix a local field for the discussion.

In general
if
\sigma
is an n-dimensional local Weil–Deligne representation which decomposes into irreducible subrepresentations as
\oplus\sigma_{i}
, then we say
\sigma
is in the conductor dropping range if any one of the normalized conductors
C(\sigma_{i})^{\frac{1}{\dim\sigma_{i}}}
has significantly different size compared with others. In the Rankin–Selberg/triple product case, let
\sigma_{i,v}
be associated to
\pi_{i,v}
via the local Langlands correspondence, and we say
\{\pi_{i,v}\}
are in the conductor dropping range if
\sigma=\bigotimes\limits_{i}\sigma_{i,v}
is so.
As we shall also consider horizontal aspect uniformly, any fixed power of
p
will also be considered as a significant difference.

Example 1.2
.

Consider the case where
\nu=p
is a finite prime, and for
i=1,2
,

\pi_{i,p}\simeq\pi(\chi_{i},\chi_{i}^{-1})

are principal series representations.
Suppose for simplicity that
p>2
and
\chi_{i}
are not quadratic.
The corresponding Weil–Deligne representation
\otimes_{i=1,2}\sigma_{i,p}
decomposes into a sum of four characters (of the Galois group of
{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
),
corresponding to the characters
\chi_{1}^{\pm 1}\chi_{2}^{\pm 1}
via Class Field Theory.
Given a character
\chi
of
{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}^{\times}
, let
c(\chi)
be its conductor exponent (that is the smallest integer such that
\chi
is trivial on
1+p^{c(\chi)}{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
, and
c(\chi)=0
when
\chi
is unramified).

The conductor dropping range occurs when

c(\chi_{1})=c(\chi_{2})>c(\chi^{\pm 1}_{1}\chi^{\pm 1}_{2})

for some
(\pm 1,\pm 1)
; for example it occurs when
\chi_{2}=\chi_{1}^{\pm 1}
. This is also the range where

(1.3)

C(\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2})<\max\limits_{i}\{C(\pi_{i})^{2}\}.

In comparison, when
c(\chi_{1})\neq c(\chi_{2})
or
c(\chi_{1})=c(\chi_{2})=c(\chi^{\pm 1}_{1}\chi^{\pm 1}_{2})
, we have

C(\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2})=\max\limits_{i}\{C(\pi_{i})^{2}\}.

There are similar examples when both
\pi_{1,p},\ \pi_{2,p}
are supercuspidal representations constructed from related datum.

Consider now the case where the ramification of
\pi
comes from essentially a single place, that is,

C(\pi_{i})\asymp C(\pi_{i,p})

for a large enough prime
p
, and that
\pi_{1,p},\ \pi_{2,p}
are principal series as above, then condition (
1.2
) is automatic if
C(\pi_{1,p})\neq C(\pi_{2,p})
. On the other hand if
c(\chi_{1})=c(\chi_{2})\geq 2
, (
1.2
) is satisfied if and only if

(1.4)

\min\{c(\chi_{1}\chi_{2}),\ c(\chi_{1}\chi_{2}^{-1})\}\geq\gamma^{\prime}c(\chi_{i}^{2})

for some constant
\gamma^{\prime}>0
. (Actually
\gamma^{\prime}=\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}
with
\gamma<1
in the current case.)
This is because we have
\max\{c(\chi_{1}\chi_{2}),\ c(\chi_{1}\chi_{2}^{-1})\}=c(\chi_{i}^{2})=\frac{1}{2}c(\pi_{i,p}\times\pi_{i,p})
, and
c(\pi_{1,p}\times\pi_{2,p})=2(c(\chi_{1}\chi_{2})+c(\chi_{1}\chi_{2}^{-1}))
. Here
c(\Pi_{p})
is the exponent of
C(\Pi_{p})
.

On the other hand
\min\{c(\chi_{1}\chi_{2}),\ c(\chi_{1}\chi_{2}^{-1})\}=0
corresponds to the QUE-like case.

We leave it to readers to check that
(
1.2
) and (
1.4
) remain equivalent when
c(\chi_{1})=c(\chi_{2})=1
, in which case it could happen that
c(\chi_{i}^{2})=0
for quadratic characters.

In practice, one can also use (
1.3
) as a working definition for the conductor dropping range when we work with representations of trivial central characters in this paper.

1.2.
Main result and strategy of proof

To state our results for both the Rankin–Selberg and the triple product L-functions, we denote

Q=Q_{\infty}Q_{f}=\prod_{\nu}Q_{\nu}=\prod_{\nu}C_{\nu}(\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2}\times\pi_{3}),

and

P=P_{\infty}P_{f}=\prod_{v}P_{v}=\prod_{v}\frac{Q_{v}^{1/2}}{\max\limits_{i=2,3}\{C_{v}(\pi_{i}\times\tilde{\pi}_{i})\}}.

Here in the Rankin–Selberg case, we take
\pi_{3}=\pi(1,1)
.
Note that
Q_{v}=P_{v}=1
for almost every
\nu
, and
P_{v}\geq 1
at least when
Q_{v}
is large enough.
The main result we shall prove in this paper is the following theorem:

Theorem 1.3
.

Suppose that
\pi_{1},\ \pi_{2}
are cuspidal automorphic representations with trivial central characters. Suppose further that
(C_{f}(\pi_{2}),C_{f}(\pi_{3}))=1
(this is automatic in the Rankin–Selberg case since
C_{f}(\pi_{3})=1
). Then there exists an absolute constant
\delta>0
such that for any
\varepsilon>0
, one has

\displaystyle L(\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2}\times\pi_{3},1/2)

\displaystyle\ll C(\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2}\times\pi_{3})^{1/4+\varepsilon}\frac{1}{P^{\delta}},

\displaystyle L(\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2},1/2)

\displaystyle\ll C(\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2})^{1/4+\varepsilon}\frac{1}{P^{\delta}}.

Here the implicit constants depend on
\varepsilon,F
and (continuously) on the archimedean conductors
C_{\infty}(\pi_{i})
for
i=1,2,3
.

In the proof below we actually start with more general situations and formulate a conjecture which potentially allows one to remove/relax the technical assumptions in this result. Currently we stick to the setting of Theorem
1.3
.

The main global strategy follows and extends that of
[
34
]
.
Note first that the cases of Rankin–Selberg L-function and triple product L-function can be put into a uniform framework in the sense that they have analogous integral representations:
for
G={\text{GL}}_{2}
or
B^{\times}
for
B
some suitable quaternion algebra, depending on the (global and local) root numbers
\epsilon(\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2}\times\pi_{3},1/2)
, the Rankin–Selberg theory/triple product formula provides an identity of the shape

(1.5)

\left|\int\limits_{[G]}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{3}\varphi_{i}(g)dg\right|^{2}=\left|\langle\varphi_{1},\overline{\varphi_{2}\varphi_{3}}\rangle\right|^{2}\sim L(\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2}\times\pi_{3},1/2)\cdot\prod_{v}I_{v}(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\varphi_{3}).

Here
[G]=Z_{G}({\mathbb{A}})G(F)\backslash G({\mathbb{A}})
;
\sim
means equality up to some unimportant factors;

\varphi_{i}\in\pi_{i}^{G}
are
L^{2}
-normalized automorphic forms in the image of
\pi_{i}
under the Jacquet-Langlands correspondence;
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the unitary integral pairing for automorphic forms on
G
with the same central characters; the local integral
I_{v}(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\varphi_{3})
is an integral of products of local matrix coefficients in case of triple product L-function, or the local Rankin–Selberg integrals in case of Rankin–Selberg L-function. Furthermore in the Rankin–Selberg case,
G={\text{GL}}_{2}
, the Jacquet-Langlands correspondence is the identity and
\varphi_{3}
is an Eisenstein series constructed out of
\pi(1,1)
.

On the one hand we need to control
I_{v}(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\varphi_{3})
from below; this is done by choosing appropriate factorable vectors
\varphi_{i}
which also captures the information of
Q=C(\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2}\times\pi_{3})
and detects the conductor dropping range.

On the other hand, we also have to control

|\langle\varphi_{1},\overline{\varphi_{2}\varphi_{3}}\rangle|^{2}

from above; we do this by applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, and then the Plancherel formula (the regularized version in the Rankin–Selberg case) to get

(1.6)

\displaystyle|\langle\varphi_{1},\overline{\varphi_{2}\varphi_{3}}\rangle|^{2}\leq\langle\varphi_{2}\overline{\varphi_{2}},\varphi_{3}\overline{\varphi_{3}}\rangle=\int\limits_{\pi^{\prime}}\sum\limits_{\psi\in B(\pi^{\prime})}\langle\varphi_{2}\overline{\varphi_{2}},\psi\rangle\overline{\langle\varphi_{3}\overline{\varphi_{3}},\psi\rangle}.

Note that the first inequality is the source of asymmetry in the definition of
P
.

When all
\pi_{i}
are varying, the length of spectral sum/integral could however become very long. Denote

M_{v}:=\min\{C_{v}(\pi_{2}),C_{v}(\pi_{3})\}\hbox{,\ \ }M:=\prod_{v}M_{v}.

We further assume that there exist test vectors
\varphi_{i}
such that for every place
v
, if
j_{v}=2
or
3
(which may depend on
v
) is
the index
i
of
\pi_{i,v}
which obtains the minimum
M_{v}
, that is,

C_{v}(\pi_{j_{v}})=M_{v},

then the complexity of
\varphi_{j_{v},v}
is controlled by
M_{v}
. See Assumption
3.10
for the precise meaning.
Then one can restrict the spectral sum/integral in (
1.6
) to those
\pi^{\prime}
and
\psi
that are also controlled in terms of
M_{v}
, in which range we control the contributions term-wisely.

To study the individual terms
\langle\varphi_{2}\overline{\varphi_{2}},\psi\rangle\overline{\langle\varphi_{3}\overline{\varphi_{3}},\psi\rangle}
for non-residual spectrum, we apply the period relation (
1.5
) again and the known convexity bound for the resulting
L-
functions
L(\pi_{i}\times\tilde{\pi}_{i}\times\pi^{\prime},1/2)
. One also needs a reasonable upper bound for
I_{v}(\varphi_{i},\overline{\varphi_{i}},\psi)
, by which we expect a power saving in terms of
P
for non-residual spectrum which, combined with the amplification method, should give a power saving for the initial global period
\langle\varphi_{1},\overline{\varphi_{2}\varphi_{3}}\rangle
.

To summarize, we expect
the global consideration above to reduce the subconvexity problem to the following
test vector problem in our setting:

Conjecture 1.4
.

There exist factorable (normalized) test vectors
\varphi_{i}\in\pi_{i}
satisfying the following three local requirements.

(0)

The local component
\varphi_{j_{v},v}
should be controlled by
M_{v}
, to control the length of the spectral sum/integral;

(1)

I_{v}(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\varphi_{3})
is bounded from below by
\frac{1}{Q_{v}^{1/4}};

(2)

I_{v}(\varphi_{i},\overline{\varphi_{i}},\psi)
is controlled from above to provide power saving in terms of
P_{v}
, while
\psi
is controlled in terms of
M
.

See Conjecture
3.17
below for more precise formulation. Note that scaling the test vectors would change the requirements for (1) and (2) simultaneously. So one can assume without loss of generality that
\varphi_{i}
are
L^{2}
-normalized.

The main challenge of the test vector problem is the lower bound in item (1).
The existence of such test vectors is partially supported by previous know cases away from the conductor dropping range.
Within the conductor dropping range however, the required lower bound for
I_{v}(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\varphi_{3})
becomes substantially larger compared with the non-conductor dropping range.
Whether this is possible is not clear at all from, for example,
[
34
]
or other literature.

There are two main sources of test vectors, coming from translates of classical newforms, or localized vectors (or minimal vectors) as used in
[
41
,
20
,
21
,
38
,
39
]
. The newforms are commonly used in the cases with disjoint ramifications, but they have more complicated matrix coefficients and Whittaker functions when involved in the conductor dropping range. On the other hand the localized vectors
have simpler description of matrix coefficients/Whittaker functions, but they do not work well for representations with small levels, requiring more case by case discussions.

To our surprise, in the setting of Theorem
1.3
(in level aspect, with trivial central character, and
M=1
), we are able to find test vectors uniformly using only diagonal translates of newforms.
The complicated nature of matrix coefficients/Whittaker functions of newforms from different
\pi_{i}
turns out to be reflecting the conductor dropping range in a relatively simple fashion.
The extent of diagonal translates would then have to match the extent of conductor dropping to achieve item (1) of Conjecture
1.4
.

We also have some sporadic evidences for the conjecture when
M
is square-free, or in certain archimedean aspects, but we shall skip these cases for the sake of conciseness. In more general situations, the localized vectors may also turn out to be useful.

1.3.
Main local ingredient

Here we briefly discuss the main local ingredient to verify item (1) of Conjecture
1.4
, in the conductor dropping range where
c=c(\pi_{1,v})=c(\pi_{2,v})
is the exponent of the local conductors at
v
.

Consider for simplicity the Rankin–Selberg case.
The local representations
\pi_{i,v},i=1,2
of
{\mathrm{PGL}}_{2}(F_{v})
, in most cases, can be associated to some characters
\theta_{i}
over some étale quadratic algebra
E_{i}/F_{v}
by the local Langlands correspondence. Then the evaluation of the local period integral
I_{v}
can be closely related to the following correlation between twisted Gauss sum/integrals:

P_{k}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=\sum\limits_{c(\chi)=c-k}\int\ \theta_{1}^{-1}(u)\chi_{E_{1}}(u)\psi_{E_{1}}(u)d^{\times}u\overline{\int\theta_{2}^{-1}(w)\chi_{E_{2}}(w)\psi_{E_{2}}(w)d^{\times}w}.

Here
\psi_{E_{i}}=\psi\circ\text{Tr}_{E_{i}/F_{v}}
is an additive character on
E_{i}
,
\chi
is a multiplicative character of
F_{v}^{\times}
with specified level, and
\chi_{E_{i}}=\chi\circ\text{Nm}_{E_{i}/F_{v}}
.

An interesting phenomenon we find is that this quantity
P_{k}
is exactly capturing the conductor dropping range: when
k\geq c(\pi_{1,v}\times\pi_{2,v})/2
, there is no cancellation in the sum over
\chi
. On the other hand when
c/2\leq k<c(\pi_{1,v}\times\pi_{2,v})/2
, there are heavy cancellations, and
P_{k}
becomes much smaller and equals zero in many cases. The choice of test vector is then reduced to maximizing the contributions from those
P_{k}
with
k\geq c(\pi_{1,v}\times\pi_{2,v})/2
.
This phenomenon may also have independent interest.

\langle\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}\rangle=\int\limits_{[G]}\varphi_{1}(g)\overline{\varphi_{2}(g)}dg;

For
\varphi_{i,v}\in\pi_{v}
and
W_{i,v}=W_{\varphi_{i},v}
the associated Whittaker functions in the case
B_{v}^{\times}={\text{GL}}_{2}({F}_{v})
,

\langle\varphi_{1,v},\varphi_{2,v}\rangle=\int\limits_{F_{v}^{\times}}W_{1,v}(a(x))\overline{W_{2,v}(a(x))}d^{\times}x.

The
L^{2}
-norms of
\varphi
and
\varphi_{v}
are defined with respect to these pairings. Inner products can also be defined for compactly induced representations when
B_{v}
is a division algebra, though we do not need the details in this paper.

We denote by
\tilde{\pi}\in\mathcal{A}({\mathrm{G}},\chi^{-1})
the contragredient representation of
\pi
,
and by
\pi^{JL}\in\mathcal{A}({\mathrm{GL}_{2}},\chi)
the automorphic representation corresponding to
\pi
under the Jacquet-Langlands correspondence (one has
\pi_{v}\simeq\pi^{JL}_{v}
for
v\not\in\mathrm{ram}(\pi)
).

Convention 1
.

Unless stated otherwise a local unitary representation of
{{\mathrm{G}}_{v}}
for some
v
will always be understood as a local constituent of some global automorphic representation. In particular, the Langlands parameters (when the representation is unramified) satisfy the best known approximation towards the Ramanujan-Petersson conjecture.

Convention 2
.

To ease notations (and as long as the context is clear) we will not distinguish between
\pi
and the Jacquet-Langlands constituent
\pi^{JL}
at least notationally: suppose we have a quantity, say
L(\pi^{JL})
, which a priori is constructed out of
\pi^{JL}
: we will denote it indifferently
L(\pi^{JL})
or
L(\pi)
. This convention will be in use especially to denote L-functions and related quantities like conductors.

2.2.
L-functions

Let
\Pi\simeq\bigotimes_{v}\Pi_{v}
be an automorphic representation of some reductive group
{\mathrm{G}}
and
\rho
a representation of its dual group
{\mathrm{G}}^{L}
of dimension
d
; to these data, one associates an L-function given by an Euler product of local
L-
factors of degree
d
converging in some halfspace
\{s,\ \operatorname{Re}s\gg 1\}

L(\Pi,\rho,s)=\prod_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{f}}L_{v}(\Pi,\rho,s),

where
L_{v}(\Pi,\rho,s)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}(1-\frac{\alpha_{v,i}(\Pi,\rho)}{q_{v}^{s}})^{-1}
at unramified places for
\alpha_{v,i}(\Pi,\rho)
the eigenvalues of
\rho(c(\Pi_{v}))
where
c(\Pi_{v})
is the Satake matrix associated to
\Pi_{v}
. At ramified places,
L_{v}
is often defined as the common denominator of the relevant local period integrals, or using the local Langlands correspondence.

The archimedean factors are defined similarly as

L_{\infty}(\Pi,\rho,s)=\prod_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{\infty}}L_{v}(\Pi,\rho,s),\ L_{v}(\Pi,\rho,s)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\Gamma_{F_{v}}(s+\alpha_{v,i}(\Pi,\rho)).

In several cases one can prove that
L(\Pi,\rho,s)
admits analytic continuation to
{\mathbb{C}}
with at most a finite number of poles located on the line
\operatorname{Re}s=1
and that it admits a functional equation of the shape

\Lambda(\Pi,\rho,s)=\varepsilon(\Pi,\rho)C_{f}(\Pi,\rho)^{\frac{1}{2}-s}\overline{\Lambda(\Pi,\rho,1-\overline{s})}

where

\Lambda(\Pi,\rho,s)=L_{\infty}(\Pi,\rho,s)L(\Pi,\rho,s),\ |\varepsilon(\Pi,\rho)|=1,\ C_{f}(\Pi,\rho)\in{\bf{N}}_{\geq 1}.

The factor
\varepsilon(\Pi,\rho)
is the root number and
C_{f}(\Pi,\rho)
is the arithmetic conductor. Both quantities can also be factored into a product of local root numbers (upon choosing a non-trivial additive character of
F\backslash{\mathbb{A}}
) and local conductors which are almost everywhere equal to
1
and arise from local functional equations:

\varepsilon(\Pi,\rho)=\prod_{v}\varepsilon_{v}(\Pi,\rho),\ C_{f}(\Pi,\rho)=\prod_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{F}}C_{v}(\Pi,\rho).

One define the
analytic conductor
of
(\Pi,\rho)
by completing the arithmetic conductor with archimedean local conductors:

C(\Pi,\rho)=C_{f}(\Pi,\rho)C_{\infty}(\Pi,\rho)=C_{f}(\Pi,\rho)\prod_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{\infty}}C_{v}(\Pi,\rho)

C_{v}(\Pi,\rho)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}(1+|\alpha_{v,i}(\Pi,\rho)|)^{[F_{v}:{\mathbb{R}}]},\ v\in\mathcal{V}_{\infty}.

2.2.1.
Convexity bound

The following bound is also sometimes known:
for any
\varepsilon>0
, one has

L(\Pi,\rho,1/2)\ll_{F,d,\varepsilon}C(\Pi,\rho)^{1/4+\varepsilon}.

The subconvexity problem consist in improving the exponent
1/4
to one strictly smaller.

All the properties mentionned above are known for the following L-functions

-

Hecke-Godement-Jacquet L-functions:
n\geq 1
,
{\mathrm{G}}={\text{GL}}_{n}
,
\rho=\mathrm{Std}
,
d=n
. The L-function will be noted
L(\Pi,s)
.

-

Rankin–Selberg L-functions:
{\mathrm{G}}={\mathrm{GL}_{2}}\times{\mathrm{GL}_{2}}
,
\rho=\mathrm{Std}\otimes\mathrm{Std}
,
d=4
. If
\Pi=\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2}
, L-function will be noted
L(\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2},s)
.

-

Adjoint L-functions:
{\mathrm{G}}={\mathrm{GL}_{2}}
,
\rho=\mathrm{Ad}
,
d=3
. The L-function will be noted
L(\Pi,\mathrm{Ad},s)
.

-

Triple product L-function:
{\mathrm{G}}={\mathrm{GL}_{2}}\times{\mathrm{GL}_{2}}\times{\mathrm{GL}_{2}}
,
\rho=\mathrm{Std}\otimes\mathrm{Std}\otimes\mathrm{Std}
,
d=8
. If
\Pi=\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2}\times\pi_{3}
, the L-function will be noted
L(\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2}\times\pi_{3},s)
.

In the following
we will write

(2.2)

C(\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2}\times\pi_{3})=\prod_{v}C_{v}(\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2}\times\pi_{3})=Q=\prod_{v}Q_{v}.

2.2.2.
Bound for adjoint L-functions

Given
\pi\in\mathcal{A}({\mathrm{GL}_{2}},\chi)
it follows from
[
15
]
that

L(\pi,\mathrm{Ad},1)=C(\pi)^{o(1)}\hbox{ as }C(\pi)\rightarrow\infty.

2.3.
Convention for inequalities

For two functions
f_{1}

f_{2}
of variables
a,b,c
,etc., by

f_{1}(a,b,\cdots)\ll_{a,b}f_{2}(a,b,\cdots),

we mean
f_{1}(a,b,\cdots)\leq C_{a,b}f_{2}(a,b,\cdots)
for some implied constant
C_{a,b}
depending only on
a
and
b
,
independent of other variables. This convention is commonly used among analytic number theorists.

In the following we will omit additional variables from notations without confusion. To deal with infinite products over all places, we also introduce the following notation:

Definition 2.2
.

Let
f_{1},f_{2}
be two functions of
v\in\mathcal{V}
and other variables
a,b,c
, etc. By

f_{1}\leq_{v,a}f_{2},

we mean
f_{1}\ll_{v,a}f_{2}
with implied constant
C_{v,a}
, and
C_{v,a}=1
if the cardinality of residue field
q_{v}\geq N_{a}
where
N_{a}
depends only on
a
.

We define
f_{1}\geq_{v,a}f_{2}
similarly. By
f_{1}\asymp_{v,a}f_{2}
, we mean
f_{1}\leq_{v,a}f_{2}
and
f_{1}\geq_{v,a}f_{2}
.

The following lemma is straightforward:

Lemma 2.3
.

If
f_{1}(v)=f_{2}(v)=1
for almost all
v
, and
f_{1}\leq_{v,a}f_{2}
, then

\prod\limits_{v}f_{1}\ll_{a}\prod\limits_{v}f_{2}.

The nuance here is that the number of local factors
f_{i}(v)
which are not
1
may depend on other variables.

Example 2.4
.

Let
f(v)=\zeta_{v}(1)
for
v|Q_{f}
and
f(v)=1
otherwise.
Then

Q_{v}^{-\epsilon}\leq_{v,\epsilon}f(v)\leq_{v,\epsilon}Q_{v}^{\epsilon},

Q^{-\epsilon}\ll_{\epsilon}\prod\limits_{v}f(v)\ll_{\epsilon}Q^{\epsilon}.

Same conclusions hold if
f(v)=C
some fixed constant for
v|Q_{f}
and
f(v)=1
otherwise.

3.
bounds for global periods

Let
\pi_{i},\ i=1,2,3
be three generic automorphic representations of
{\mathrm{GL}_{2}}({\mathbb{A}})
whose product of central characters is trivial; we will moreover assume that
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
are cuspidal.

The basic analytic properties stated in §
2.2
(including the convexity bound) are known for the associated triple product L-function
L(\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2}\times\pi_{3},s)
. If
\pi_{3}
is a principal series representation this is the Rankin–Selberg theory; when
\pi_{3}
is cuspidal these are non-trivial facts which are consequences of the work of several people including Garrett, Piatestsky-Schapiro–Rallis and Ramakrishnan
[
10
,
42
,
44
]
.
L(\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2}\times\pi_{3},s)
is holomorphic unless
\pi_{3}
is a principal series and
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}
are contragredient to one another up to a twist. Note that the convexity bound follows from Ramakrishnan’s proof that
L(\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2},s)
is
{\text{GL}}_{4}
-automorphic and from an argument of Molteni
[
35
]
.

The condition on the product of central characters being trivial implies that
L(\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2}\times\pi_{3},s)
has real coefficients and that the global and local root numbers

\varepsilon(\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2}\times\pi_{3})=\prod_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\varepsilon_{v}(\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2}\times\pi_{3})

are all
\pm 1
.

The
subconvexity problem
we are studying in this paper amounts to finding an absolute
\delta>0
such that

L(\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2}\times\pi_{3},1/2)\ll_{F}C(\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2}\times\pi_{3})^{1/4-\delta}.

3.1.
The triple product formula

We may assume that

(3.1)

\varepsilon(\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2}\times\pi_{3})=+1,

as otherwise
L(\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2}\times\pi_{3},1/2)=0
and we are done.
This implies that the set of places of
F
at which the local root number equals
-1
has even cardinality; this set determines a unique quaternion algebra
{\mathrm{B}}
(the one ramified precisely at this even set of places) and three
{{\mathrm{B}}^{\times}}
-automorphic representation
(\pi^{\mathrm{B}}_{1},\pi^{\mathrm{B}}_{2},\pi^{\mathrm{B}}_{3})
whose images under the Jacquet-Langlands correspondence are
(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\pi_{3})
. More precisely, at any place for which
{\mathrm{B}}
is unramified,
{\mathrm{B}}^{\times}_{v}={\text{GL}}_{2,v}
and
\pi_{i,v}^{\mathrm{B}}\simeq\pi_{i,v}
while for any place where
{\mathrm{B}}
is ramified,
\pi_{i,v}^{\mathrm{B}}
is finite dimensional and
\pi_{i,v}
is the image of
\pi_{i,v}^{\mathrm{B}}
under the local Jacquet-Langlands correspondence; note that in the Rankin–selberg case, when
\pi_{3}
is a principal series representation,
{{\mathrm{B}}^{\times}}={\mathrm{GL}_{2}}
and the correspondence is the identity.

From now on, the quaternion algebra
{\mathrm{B}}
will be considered
fixed
; we write

\pi=\pi^{{\mathrm{B}}}_{1}\in\mathcal{A}({\mathrm{G}},\chi_{1}),\ \Pi=\pi^{{\mathrm{B}}}_{2}\otimes\pi^{{\mathrm{B}}}_{3}\in\mathcal{A}({\mathrm{G}}\times{\mathrm{G}},\chi^{-1}_{1}).

The later is an automorphic representation of
{{\mathrm{G}}({\mathbb{A}})}\times{{\mathrm{G}}({\mathbb{A}})}
on which
{{\mathrm{G}}({\mathbb{A}})}
acts via the diagonal embedding
{\mathrm{G}}\hookrightarrow{\mathrm{G}}\times{\mathrm{G}}
.

It follows from the work of Prasad
[
43
]
that for any place
v
the space

\operatorname{Hom}_{{\mathrm{G}}_{v}}(\Pi_{v},\tilde{\pi}_{v})
of
{{\mathrm{G}}_{v}}
-invariant intertwiners is one dimensional and a generator is given by the functional,
\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi_{v}}_{\Pi_{v}}
say, well defined up to a scalar of modulus
1
satisfying for any
\varphi_{v}\otimes\varPhi_{v}\in\pi_{v}\otimes\Pi_{v}

\left|\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi_{v}}_{\Pi_{v}}(\varPhi_{v})(\varphi_{v})\right|^{2}=\frac{L_{v}(\pi,\mathrm{Ad},1)L_{v}(\Pi,\mathrm{Ad},1)}{\zeta_{F_{v}}(2)^{2}L_{v}(\pi\times\Pi,1/2)}\int_{[{{\mathrm{G}}_{v}}]}\langle\varphi_{v},g.\varphi_{v}\rangle\langle\varPhi_{v},g.\varPhi_{v}\rangle dg.

Remark 3.1
.

Observe that the
{{\mathrm{G}}_{v}}
-integral is absolutely converging due to the known bounds for matrix coefficient
[
28
,
3
]
and the assumption that
\pi_{v}
and
\Pi_{v}
are local components of global automorphic representations.

Furthermore by work of Garrett, Piatetsky-Shapiro–Rallis, Harris-Kudla, Watson and Ichino
[
10
,
42
,
13
,
48
,
27
]
, the space of global
{{\mathrm{G}}({\mathbb{A}})}
-equivariant intertwiners
\operatorname{Hom}_{{\mathrm{G}}({\mathbb{A}})}(\Pi,\tilde{\pi})

is non-zero (and therefore one dimensional) if an only if

L(\pi\times\Pi,1/2)\not=0

and then is generated by
\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi}_{\Pi}
given by

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi}_{\Pi}(\varPhi)(\varphi)

\displaystyle=\int_{[{\mathrm{G}}]}\varphi(g)\varPhi(g)dg

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi}_{\Pi}(\varPhi)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\varphi\in\mathcal{B}(\pi)}\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi}_{\Pi}(\varPhi)(\varphi)

where
\varPhi
is viewed as a function on
{{\mathrm{G}}({\mathbb{A}})}\times{{\mathrm{G}}({\mathbb{A}})}
,
g\mapsto\varPhi(g)
denotes the restriction of
\varPhi
to diagonally-embedded
{{\mathrm{G}}({\mathbb{A}})}
in
{{\mathrm{G}}({\mathbb{A}})}\times{{\mathrm{G}}({\mathbb{A}})}
and
\mathcal{B}(\pi)
denotes an orthonormal basis for
\pi
; note that the integral is converging since
\pi
is cuspidal.
More precisely the following period formula relating global and local intertwiners holds (
[
27
]
):

Theorem 3.2
.

For
\varphi\otimes\varPhi\simeq\bigotimes_{v}\varphi_{v}\otimes\varPhi_{v}
any non-zero factorable global vector in
\pi\otimes\Pi
, one has

(3.3)

\frac{\left|\int_{[{\mathrm{G}}]}\varphi(g)\varPhi(g)dg\right|^{2}}{\langle\varphi,\varphi\rangle\langle\varPhi,\varPhi\rangle}=C_{F}\frac{L(\pi\times\Pi,1/2)}{L(\pi,\mathrm{Ad},1)L(\Pi,\mathrm{Ad},1)}\prod_{v}\frac{\left|\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi_{v}}_{\Pi_{v}}(\varPhi_{v})(\varphi_{v})\right|^{2}}{\langle\varphi_{v},\varphi_{v}\rangle\langle\varPhi_{v},\varPhi_{v}\rangle},

where
C_{F}>0
is an absolute constant.

Remark 3.3
.

If
{\mathrm{G}}={\mathrm{GL}_{2}}
and
\pi_{3}
is a principal series representation the above formula amounts to the Rankin–Selberg theory, and
\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi_{v}}_{\Pi_{v}}(\varPhi_{v})(\varphi_{v})
becomes the standard local Rankin–Selberg integral.

Assuming that
\varphi,\varPhi
are both globally and locally
L^{2}
-normalized and using §
2.2.2
we rewrite this formula in the following less precise form

(3.4)

{\left|\int_{[{\mathrm{G}}]}\varphi(g)\varPhi(g)dg\right|^{2}}=C(\pi\times\Pi)^{o(1)}{L(\pi\times\Pi,1/2)}\prod_{v}{\left|\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi_{v}}_{\Pi_{v}}(\varPhi_{v})(\varphi_{v})\right|^{2}}.

3.2.
Test vectors

Following a method initiated in
[
47
]
, we use this last formula to get our hands on the size of the central value

L(\pi\times\Pi,1/2)
.

By (
2.2
)
we rewrite (
3.4
) in the following form

(3.5)

\frac{L(\pi\times\Pi,1/2)}{C(\pi\times\Pi)^{1/4+\epsilon}}=\frac{\left|\int_{[{\mathrm{G}}]}\varphi(g)\varPhi(g)dg\right|^{2}}{\prod_{v}\left|\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi_{v}}_{\Pi_{v}}(\varPhi_{v})(\varphi_{v})\right|^{2}Q_{v}^{1/4}}.

From this we see that in order to solve the subconvexity problem it will be sufficient to show the existence of factorable vectors
\varphi
,
\varPhi
for which the global period is small, that is,

\left|\int_{[{\mathrm{G}}]}\varphi(g)\varPhi(g)dg\right|^{2}\ll Q^{-\delta}

for some
\delta>0

and for which the product of local periods

(3.6)

\prod_{v}\left|\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi_{v}}_{\Pi_{v}}(\varPhi_{v})(\varphi_{v})\right|^{2}Q_{v}^{1/4}\gg Q^{-\epsilon}

is not too small.

Remark 3.4
.

Observe that this product is converging: when
v
is finite,
\pi_{v}
and
\Pi_{v}
are unramified principal series and
\varphi^{\circ}_{v},\ \varPhi^{\circ}_{v}
are the spherical vectors, we have

\left|\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi_{v}}_{\Pi_{v}}(\varPhi^{\circ}_{v})(\varphi^{\circ}_{v})\right|^{2}=Q_{v}^{1/4}=1.

We will search for vectors of the following form:

Definition 3.5
.

For
i=1,2,3
a test vector for
\pi^{\mathrm{B}}_{i}\simeq\bigotimes_{v}\pi^{\mathrm{B}}_{i,v}
is a factorable vector
\varphi_{i}=\otimes_{v}\varphi_{i,v}
such that

(1)

For all
v
,
\|\varphi_{i,v}\|=1
.

(2)

For all
d\in{\bf{N}}
,
{\mathcal{S}}_{d}^{\pi_{v}}(\varphi_{i,v})\ll_{d}Q_{v}^{A(d)}
for some
A
depending on
d
only.

(3)

For
v\not\in\bigcup_{i=1,2,3}\mathrm{ram}(\pi^{\mathrm{B}}_{i})
,
\varphi_{i,v}=\varphi^{\circ}_{i,v}
is a spherical element.

Here
{\mathcal{S}}_{d}^{\pi_{v}}
is the Sobolev norm defined as in, for example,
[
34
, Section 2.3.5]
.
A test vector of
\Pi=\pi_{2}^{\mathrm{B}}\otimes\pi_{3}^{\mathrm{B}}
is a tensor product of test vectors
\varPhi=\varphi_{2}\otimes\varphi_{3}
.

The control of local periods from below is essentially a local problem (finding for each ramified place
v
a quantitatively "good" vector for the intertwiner
\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi_{v}}_{\Pi_{v}}
), but the explicit choice of test vectors is also related to the global upper bound. We make the following definition:

Definition 3.6
.

Given
\varphi_{i},\ i=1,2,3
test vectors of
\pi_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}
, the feasibility index of
\varPhi=\varphi_{2}\otimes\varphi_{3}
with respect to
\varphi=\varphi_{1}
at the place
v
is defined as

\mathrm{Feas}_{v}(\varPhi|\varphi)=\left|\mathrm{Pr}_{\Pi_{v}}^{\pi_{v}}(\varPhi_{v})(\varphi_{v})\right|^{2}Q_{v}^{1/4},

measuring if (
3.6
) can be achieved for proper
L^{2}
-normalized local test vectors;
Define the total feasibility index to be the product

\mathrm{Feas}(\varPhi|\varphi)=\prod_{v}\mathrm{Feas}_{v}(\varPhi|\varphi).

Finally the
feasibility index
of
\varPhi
with respect to the representation
\pi_{1}^{\mathrm{B}}
is

\mathrm{Feas}(\varPhi|\pi):=\sup_{\varphi}\mathrm{Feas}(\varPhi|\varphi)

where the supremum is taken over test vectors in
\pi_{1}^{\mathrm{B}}
.

3.3.
The amplification method

To give global upper bound we proceed as in
[
34
]
by first convolving
\varphi
by some amplifier
\sigma
with the following properties:

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
L\geq 1
be some parameter (to be chosen for optimization)
, there exist a complex-valued measure
\sigma
on
{{\mathrm{G}}({\mathbb{A}})}

satisfying

(1)

\sigma
is compactly supported on
{\mathrm{G}}({\mathbb{A}}_{S_{L}})
where
S_{L}
is a set of finite places of norm
\leq L
at which
\varphi
,
\varPhi
and
\psi
are all unramified;

(2)

For any
u\in\mathrm{supp}(\sigma)
we have
\|u\|\leq L
where
\|u\|
is the operator norm as in (
2.1
).

(3)

Let
|\sigma|
denote the total variation measure. Then
the total mass of
|\sigma|
is bounded above by
L^{B}
, for some absolute constant
B
.
Moreover, with
|\sigma|^{(2)}=|\sigma|\star|\check{\sigma}|
, one has for any

\gamma>1/2

\int\|u\|^{-\gamma}d|\sigma|^{(2)}(u)\leq L^{-\eta},\ \hbox{for some }\eta=\eta(\gamma)>0.

(4)

\varphi\star\sigma=\lambda\varphi
with
\lambda\gg_{F,\varepsilon}Q^{-\varepsilon}
for any
\varepsilon>0
.

Since at least one of
\pi_{2}
and
\pi_{3}
is cuspidal,
\varPhi
is rapidly decreasing. As
\varphi
is
L^{2}
-normalized, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality implies that:

(3.7)

\displaystyle|\lambda|^{2}\left|\int_{[{\mathrm{G}}]}\varphi(h)\varPhi(h)dh\right|^{2}=

\displaystyle\left|\int_{[{\mathrm{G}}]}(\varphi\star\sigma)(g)\varPhi(g)dg\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{[{\mathrm{G}}]}\left|\varPhi\star\check{\sigma}(g)\right|^{2}dg=\langle(\varphi_{2}\varphi_{3})\star(\overline{\sigma}\star\check{\sigma}),\varphi_{2}\varphi_{3}\rangle.

The later inner product decomposes into a sum of terms of the shape

\langle u.\varphi_{2}\ \overline{\varphi_{2}},\varphi_{3}\ u.\overline{\varphi_{3}}\rangle=\langle\varPhi_{2,u}|_{\mathrm{G}},\varPhi_{3,u}|_{\mathrm{G}}\rangle

where
u\in\mathrm{supp}(\overline{\sigma}\star\check{\sigma})
,
\varPhi_{2,u},\varPhi_{3,u}
are the functions on
{{\mathrm{G}}({\mathbb{A}})}\times{{\mathrm{G}}({\mathbb{A}})}
given by

(3.8)

\varPhi_{2,u}(g_{1},g_{2})=u.\varphi_{2}(g_{1})\times\overline{\varphi}_{2}(g_{2}),\varPhi_{3,u}(g_{1},g_{2})=\varphi_{3}(g_{1})\times u.\overline{\varphi}_{3}(g_{3}),

and
|_{\mathrm{G}}
denote the restriction to
{{\mathrm{G}}({\mathbb{A}})}\hookrightarrow{{\mathrm{G}}({\mathbb{A}})}\times{{\mathrm{G}}({\mathbb{A}})}
(diagonally embedded). The function
\varPhi_{2,u}
and
\varPhi_{3,u}
are factorable vectors in the
{{\mathrm{G}}({\mathbb{A}})}^{2}
-automorphic representations

\Pi_{2}=\pi_{2}\otimes\tilde{\pi}_{2},\ \Pi_{3}=\pi_{3}\otimes\tilde{\pi}_{3}.

To simplify notations we will omit the restriction
|_{\mathrm{G}}
of
\varPhi_{2}
and
\varPhi_{3}
and write
\langle\varPhi_{2,u},\varPhi_{3,u}\rangle
in place of
\langle\varPhi_{2,u}|_{\mathrm{G}},\varPhi_{3,u}|_{\mathrm{G}}\rangle
. Thus our discussion so far gives

(3.9)

\displaystyle|\lambda|^{2}\left|\int_{[{\mathrm{G}}]}\varphi(h)\varPhi(h)dh\right|^{2}\leq\int\limits_{u}\langle\varPhi_{2,u},\varPhi_{3,u}\rangle d(\overline{\sigma}\star\check{\sigma})(u)

which is essentially a finite sum in
u
.

3.4.
Applying the Plancherel formula

Suppose first that
\pi_{3}
is a cuspidal automorphic representation, then the restrictions to
{{\mathrm{G}}({\mathbb{A}})}
of
\varPhi_{2}
and
\varPhi_{3}
are rapidly decreasing modulo
{\mathbb{A}}^{\times}{{\mathrm{G}}(F)}
and by the Plancherel formula the above inner product decomposes as

\displaystyle\langle\varPhi_{2,u},\varPhi_{3,u}\rangle=

\displaystyle\int_{\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}({\mathrm{G}},1)}\sum_{\psi\in\mathcal{B}(\pi^{\prime})}\langle\varPhi_{2,u},\psi\rangle\langle\psi,\varPhi_{3,u}\rangle\ d\mu_{\mathrm{Pl}}(\pi^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}({\mathrm{G}},1)}\sum_{\psi\in\mathcal{B}(\pi^{\prime})}\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi^{\prime}}_{\Pi_{2}}(\varPhi_{2,u})(\psi)\overline{\mathrm{Pr}_{\Pi_{3}}^{\pi^{\prime}}(\varPhi_{3,u})(\psi)}\ d\mu_{\mathrm{Pl}}(\pi^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}({\mathrm{G}},1)}\langle\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi^{\prime}}_{\Pi_{2}}(\varPhi_{2,u}),{\mathrm{Pr}_{\Pi_{3}}^{\pi^{\prime}}(\varPhi_{3,u})}\rangle d\mu_{\mathrm{Pl}}(\pi^{\prime})

where

3.5.1.
Triple product L-functions and Quantum unique ergodicity

We first look at the decay properties of the inner product

Our present problem is a slight variant of the above. The comment justifies the choice of notations in Definition

3.7.
The Rankin–Selberg case

In this paper we are also interested in the critical value of the Rankin–Selberg L-function

, in which case
\varphi_{3}
is an Eisenstein series in the above formulation.
In this section we show that Theorem

continue to hold in this case.
We use the regularization process as developed in
[

]
, and refer to §4.3 of that paper for the detailed definitions and notations.

We assume for simplicity that
\pi_{3}=1\boxplus\chi_{3}
(with
\chi_{3}=(\chi_{1}\chi_{2})^{-1}

\chi_{i}

\pi_{i}

and the Jacquet-Langlands correspondence is just the identity.

We take
\varphi_{3}
of the shape

for

an element in
\pi(1,\chi_{3})
. One can try to proceed exactly as in the beginning of this section up to §
3.4
and the evaluation of linear combination of inner products of the shape
\langle\varPhi_{2,u},\varPhi_{3,u}\rangle

\varphi_{3}
is not rapidly decreasing we have to use the regularized version of Plancherel’s formula proven in
[
34
, §4.3]
. In order to apply this formula, we have to deform slightly the vector

:
given

is the section whose restriction to the maximal compact subgroup

coincide with

.
Set
\Pi_{3}(t)=\pi_{3}(t)\otimes\tilde{\pi}_{3}
and define the vector

\displaystyle\varPhi_{3,u}(t):(g_{1},g_{2})\mapsto\varphi_{3}(t)(g_{1})\times u.\overline{\varphi}_{3}(g_{2}).

As
\varphi_{2}
is cuspidal, we observe that the function

is anti-holomorphic in the

{\mathcal{C}}
of fixed radius around
t=0
.

The set of exponents of

is

and the squares of the above exponents are not

for

for almost all radius of
{\mathcal{C}}
. We fix such

with small enough radius in the following.

Then as in the regularized version of Plancherel’s formula
[

we have the following:

(3.19)

\displaystyle\langle\varPhi_{2,u},\varPhi_{3,u}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\varPhi_{2,u},{\mathcal{E}}_{3}(t)\rangle+\int\limits_{\pi^{\prime}\ \mathrm{generic}}\sum_{\psi\in\mathcal{B}(\pi^{\prime})}\langle\varPhi_{2,u},\psi\rangle\langle\psi,\varPhi_{3,u}(t)\rangle_{reg}\ d\mu_{\mathrm{Pl}}(\pi^{\prime}),

Using

4.1.
Basics

We collect some basic definitions and results here.

Let
F
be a p-adic local field, with ring of integers
O_{F}
, uniformizer
\varpi
, valuation
v
, residue field
k_{F}
,
q=|k_{F}|
and
p
be the characteristic of
k_{F}
.
Let
E
be an étale quadratic algebra over
F
. When
E
is a field, we denote
O_{E}
to be the ring of integers,
\varpi_{E}
to be a uniformizer, and
e_{E}
to be the ramification index of
E
. We normalize the valuation on
E
such that
v_{E}(\varpi_{E})=1
. Define
U_{E}(i)=1+\varpi^{i}O_{E}
.
To be uniform, we also take the following conventions when working with the case where
E
splits.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
E
be a split quadratic extension over
F
identified with
F\times F
. Take
e_{E}=1
in this case. Define
O_{E}=O_{F}\times O_{F}
,
O_{E}^{\times}=O_{F}^{\times}\times O_{F}^{\times}
,
U_{E}(i)=1+\varpi^{i}O_{E}
for
i>0
. Also we write
v_{E}(x)=i
when
x\in\varpi^{i}O_{E}^{\times}
. (In particular
v_{E}
is not defined for all
E
in this case.)

For a non-trivial additive character
\psi
over
F
, denote

c(\psi)=i,

if
i\geq 0
is the smallest integer such that
\psi|_{\varpi_{F}^{i}O_{F}}=1.

This definition also works for additive characters over
E
.
For a multiplicative character
\chi
of
F^{\times}
, define

c(\chi)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{\ if }\psi(O_{F}^{\times})=1;\\
i,&\text{\ if $i>0$ is smallest integer such that }\chi|_{U_{F}(i)}=1.\end{cases}

Let
\psi
now be an additive character over
F
with
c(\psi)=0
.
Denote
\psi_{E}=\psi\circ\text{Tr}_{E/F}
. Then
c(\psi_{E})=-e_{E}+1
. For a multiplicative character
\chi
on
F^{\times}
, we can associate a character
\chi_{E}=\chi\circ N_{E/F}
on
E^{\times}
.

For multiplicative characters over
F^{\times}
, we introduce an equivalence relation here.

Definition 4.2
.

For any two characters
\chi_{i}
of
F^{\times}
,
i=1,2
,

\chi_{1}\sim_{j}\chi_{2}
if and only if
c(\chi_{1}\chi_{2}^{-1})\leq j
.

We collect the following straightforward lemmas.

Lemma 4.3
.

Suppose that
2\nmid q
.
Let
E,E^{\prime}
be two non-isomorphic étale quadratic algebra over
F
,
\alpha_{1}\in E
,
\alpha_{2}\in E^{\prime}
be trace 0 elements with
v(N_{E/F}(\alpha_{1}))=v(N_{E/F}(\alpha_{2}))
. Let
\alpha_{0}\in F
with
v(N_{E/F}(\alpha_{1}))\leq v(N_{E/F}(\alpha_{0}))
. Then we have

\frac{N_{E/F}(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{0})}{N_{E^{\prime}/F}(\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{0})}\not\equiv 1\mod\varpi.

Proof.

Since
\alpha_{i}
are trace 0 for
i=1,2
and
\alpha_{0}\in F
, we have

N(\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{0})=N(\alpha_{i})+N(\alpha_{0}).

By the condition
v(N_{E/F}(\alpha_{1}))\leq v(N_{E/F}(\alpha_{0}))
, we are reduced to prove

\frac{N_{E/F}(\alpha_{1})}{N_{E^{\prime}/F}(\alpha_{2})}\not\equiv 1\mod\varpi.

This follows directly from that
E,E^{\prime}
are not isomorphic and
2\nmid q
.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
m\in F
such that
v(m)=-j<0
, and
\mu
be a character of
O_{F}^{\times}
of level
k>0
. Then

(4.1)

\left|\int\limits_{v(x)=0}\psi(mx)\mu^{-1}(x)d^{\times}x\right|=\begin{cases}\sqrt{\frac{q}{(q-1)^{2}q^{k-1}}}=\zeta_{F}(1)q^{-k/2},&\text{\ if\ }j=k;\\
0,&\text{\ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\theta
be a character over an étale quadratic algebra
E/F
, such that
e_{E}=1
,
c(\theta)=1

\theta|_{F^{\times}}=1
.
Then for any trace 0 element
\alpha
with
v_{E}(\alpha)=0
,

\left|\sum\limits_{x\in k_{F}^{\times},x+\alpha\in O_{E}^{\times}}\theta(x+\alpha)\right|\leq 2.

Here we have identified
O_{F}^{\times}/U_{F}(1)
with
k_{F}^{\times}
without confusion.

Proof.

Consider the case
E
is a field first.
As
c(\theta)=1
, we have

\sum\limits_{x,y\in k_{F},(x,y)\neq(0,0)}\theta(x+y\alpha)=0.

Using that
\theta|_{F^{\times}}=1
, we get that

\sum\limits_{x\in k_{F}^{\times}}\theta(x+\alpha)=\frac{1}{q-1}\sum\limits_{x,y\in k_{F}^{\times}}\theta(x+y\alpha)=-1-\theta(\alpha).

The claim follows.
On the other hand if
E\simeq F\times F
,
\theta=(\mu,\mu^{-1})
,
\alpha=(y,-y)
, then

\sum\limits_{x\in k_{F}^{\times},x+\alpha\in O_{E}^{\times}}\theta(x+\alpha)=\sum\limits_{x\in k_{F}^{\times},x\not\equiv y,-y}\mu\left(\frac{x+y}{x-y}\right)=\sum\limits_{z\in k_{F}^{\times},z\not\equiv\pm 1}\mu(z)=-\mu(1)-\mu(-1).

Again the claim follows.
∎

Lemma 4.6
.

For a character

\mu
over
{F}^{\times}
with
c(\mu)\geq 2
, there exists
\alpha_{\mu}\in{F}^{\times}
with
v_{F}(\alpha_{\mu})=-c(\mu)+c(\psi_{F})
such that:

(4.2)

\mu(1+u)=\psi_{F}(\alpha_{\mu}u)\text{\ \ for any $u\in\varpi_{F}^{\lceil c(\mu)/2\rceil}O_{F}$}.

Here we keep track of
c(\psi_{F})
so we can also apply the lemma directly to a character
\theta
defined over an étale quadratic algebra
E
. Also note that if
\theta
is a character over an étale quadratic algebra
E/F
such that
\theta|_{F^{\times}}=1
, then the associated element
\alpha_{\theta}
can be chosen as a trace 0 element in
E
.

Corollary 4.7
.

Let
E/F
be an étale quadratic algebra.
\chi
is defined over
F^{\times}
with
c(\chi)\geq 2
, and is associated to
\alpha
by
\chi(1+x)=\psi(\alpha x)
for

and

.

Then
\alpha
is also associated to
\chi_{E}
, in the sense that

\chi\circ N_{E/F}(1+y)=\psi_{E}(\alpha y),\forall y\in\varpi_{E}^{\lceil\frac{e_{E}c(\chi)-e_{E}+1}{2}\rceil}O_{E}.

Note that when

,

c(\chi_{E})=e_{E}c(\chi)-e_{E}+1.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
\mu
,
\chi
be multiplicative characters on
F^{\times}
and
\psi
be an additive character on

.
Suppose that

, and

is associated to

(4.3)

\int\limits_{v(x)=-c(\mu)+c(\psi)}\mu(x)\chi(x)\psi(x)d^{\times}x=\chi(-\alpha_{\mu})\int\limits_{v(x)=-c(\mu)+c(\psi)}\mu(x)\psi(x)d^{\times}x.

This result follows immediately from the p-adic stationary phase analysis.

Let
K_{1}(\varpi^{c})
denote the compact subgroup of
{\text{GL}}_{2}
whose elements are congruent to
\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\text{mod}{(\varpi^{c})}
.
Now we record some basic facts about integrals on
{\text{GL}}_{2}(F)
.

Lemma 4.9
.

For every positive integer
c
,

{\text{GL}}_{2}(F)=\coprod\limits_{0\leq i\leq c}B\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
\varpi^{i}&1\end{pmatrix}K_{1}(\varpi^{c}).

Here

is the Borel subgroup of

.

We normalize the Haar measure on
{\text{GL}}_{2}({F})
such that its maximal compact open subgroup

has volume 1. Then we have the following easy result (see, for example,
[
23
, Appendix A]
).

Lemma 4.10
.

Locally let

be a

invariant function, on which the center acts trivially. Then

(4.4)

\int\limits_{F^{\times}\backslash{\text{GL}}_{2}({F})}f(g)dg=\sum\limits_{0\leq i\leq c}A_{i}\int\limits_{{F}^{\times}\backslash B({F})}f\left(b\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
\varpi^{i}&1\end{pmatrix}\right)db.

Here

is the left Haar measure on
{F}^{\times}\backslash B({F})
, and

A_{0}=\frac{p}{p+1}\text{,\ \ \ }A_{c}=\frac{1}{(p+1)p^{c-1}}\text{,\ \ \ }A_{i}=\frac{p-1}{(p+1)p^{i}}\text{\ \ for\ }0<i<c.

The unitary pairings on

For

a Whittaker function, denote

&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&\\

If

5.2.2.
Case

There are many possible situations in this case. We shall discuss case by case according to

Note that

(1)

Consider first the case where

. By the assumption

\frac{N_{E/F}(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{0})}{N_{E^{\prime}/F}(\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{0})}\not\equiv 1

5.1.2
,
5.1.3

(2)

Suppose now

, and

in this case.
From (

\chi
into a double sum of
\chi_{0}
modulo

, and the sum of characters
\eta
of level

\alpha_{0}
by Lemma

The last line of (

\displaystyle=(q-1)q^{c/2-2}\sum\limits_{\chi_{0}}\int\limits_{y\in O_{E}^{\times}}\psi_{E}((-\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{0})\varpi_{E}^{c(\theta_{1})-l}y)\theta_{1}\theta_{2}^{-1}(y)d^{\times}y

\displaystyle=(q-1)q^{c/2-2}\int\limits_{y\in\varpi_{E}^{-1}O_{E}^{\times}}\psi_{E}(y)\theta_{1}\theta_{2}^{-1}(y)d^{\times}y\sum\limits_{a_{0}}\theta_{2}\theta_{1}^{-1}\left((-\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{0})\varpi_{E}^{c(\theta_{1})-1}\right).

Here in the second equality we have used Corollary

\eta

Using Lemma

,

\left|\sum\limits_{c(\chi)=c-i}\int\limits_{y\in O_{E}^{\times}}\theta_{1}^{-1}\chi_{E}(1+\varpi_{E}^{c(\theta_{1})-l}y)\theta_{1}\theta_{2}^{-1}(y)d^{\times}y\right|\leq\frac{2q^{c/2-1}}{q+1},

\displaystyle\left|P_{c/2}(W_{1},W_{2})\right|

(3)

If
1<l<c(\theta_{1})
, we write

for

\displaystyle\int\limits_{y\in O_{E}^{\times}}\theta_{1}^{-1}\chi_{E}(1+\varpi_{E}^{c(\theta_{1})-l}y)\theta_{1}\theta_{2}^{-1}(y)d^{\times}y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-q_{E}^{-1}}\sum\limits_{y_{0}\in O_{E}^{\times}/1+\varpi_{E}^{l-1}O_{E}}\theta_{1}^{-1}\chi_{E}(1+\varpi_{E}^{c(\theta_{1})-l}y_{0})\theta_{1}\theta_{2}^{-1}(y_{0})

\displaystyle\int\limits_{y_{1}\in\varpi_{E}^{l-1}O_{E}}\psi_{E}\left((-\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{0})\frac{\varpi_{E}^{c(\theta_{1})-l}y_{0}y_{1}}{1+\varpi_{E}^{c(\theta_{1})-l}y_{0}}+\alpha_{3}y_{1}\right)dy_{1}.

As

We can then rewrite (

) as follows by imposing the condition on

\displaystyle P_{i}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=

\displaystyle\frac{\theta_{1}\theta_{2}^{-1}(\varpi_{E}^{c(\theta_{1})-l})}{q_{E}^{c(\theta_{1})-l}}\int\limits_{w}\theta_{2}\theta_{1}^{-1}\psi_{E}\sum\limits_{c(\chi)=c-i}

\displaystyle\int\limits_{y\equiv\frac{\alpha_{3}}{(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{0})\varpi_{E}^{c(\theta_{1})-l}}\mod\varpi_{E}}\theta_{1}^{-1}\chi_{E}(1+\varpi_{E}^{c(\theta_{1})-l}y)\theta_{1}\theta_{2}^{-1}(y)d^{\times}y.

(3i)

If

, which is equivalent to

, we can break the sum of

(3ii)

Using that

and

\displaystyle P_{i}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum\limits_{c(\chi)<c-i}\frac{\theta_{1}\theta_{2}^{-1}(\varpi_{E}^{c(\theta_{1})-l})}{q^{c(\theta_{1})-l}}\int\limits_{w}\theta_{2}\theta_{1}^{-1}\psi_{E}\int\limits_{y\equiv\frac{\alpha_{3}}{\alpha_{1}\varpi_{E}^{c(\theta_{1})-l}}\mod\varpi_{E}}\theta_{1}^{-1}(1+\varpi_{E}^{c(\theta_{1})-l}y)\theta_{1}\theta_{2}^{-1}(y)d^{\times}y.

By working backwards without

5.3.
Case
E=E^{\prime}

Most of the discussions in Section

In that case, we have

\displaystyle P_{c/2}(W_{1},W_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k\geq 0}q^{(1/2-\gamma)k}\sum\limits_{c(\chi)=c/2}\int\limits_{x\in O_{F}^{\times}}W_{1}^{(c/2)}(\varpi^{k}x)\chi(x)d^{\times}x\overline{\int\limits_{y\in O_{F}^{\times}}W_{2}^{(c/2)}(\varpi^{k}y)\chi(y)d^{\times}y}

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k\geq 0}q^{(1/2-\gamma)k}\frac{q^{-k}}{C_{0}\overline{C_{0}^{\prime}}(q-1)^{2}q^{c/2-2}}\sum\limits_{c(\chi)=c/2}\sum\limits_{i,j=0,k}\mu_{1}(\varpi^{k-2i})\overline{\mu_{2}(\varpi^{k-2j})}

\displaystyle\int\limits_{u\in\varpi_{E}^{-c_{0}}O_{E}^{\times}}\theta_{1}^{-1}(u)\chi_{E}(u)\psi_{E}(\varpi^{k-i,i}u)d^{\times}u\overline{\int\limits_{w\in\varpi_{E}^{-c_{0}}O_{E}^{\times}}\theta_{2}^{-1}(w)\chi_{E}(w)\psi_{E}(\varpi^{k-j,j}w)d^{\times}w}.

Here
\theta_{1}=(\mu_{1}^{-1},\mu_{1})
,
\theta_{2}=(\mu_{2}^{-1},\mu_{2})
. Denote

.
Denote by

5.3.1.
Case

If

, then
C_{0}=C_{0}^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle\int\limits_{u\in\varpi_{E}^{-c_{0}}O_{E}^{\times}}\theta_{2}^{-1}(u)\chi_{E}(u)\psi_{E}(\varpi^{k-i,i}u)d^{\times}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\theta_{2}\theta_{1}^{-1}(\varpi_{E})^{c_{0}}\int\limits_{w\in\varpi_{E}^{-c_{0}}O_{E}^{\times}}\theta_{1}^{-1}(w)\chi_{E}(w)\psi_{E}(\varpi^{k-i,i}w)d^{\times}w.

There is no cancellation in the summation over
\chi

Thus when

4.23

P_{c/2,0}(W_{1},W_{2})=L^{2}(W_{1},0)=\frac{q-3}{q-1},

When

In particular when

5.3.2.
Case

.

As
\pi(\mu,\mu^{-1})\simeq\pi(\mu^{-1},\mu)

(1)

One can similarly relate

as in Section

, with the difference that the contribution to

applies here and

(2)

When

, we use similar strategy as in Section
5.2.2
with slight modifications.
Note that the case

is excluded here.
By the proof of Corollary
4.23
, we can separate the contribution of
\chi

Since these two cases are parallel, we focus on the case

(5.10)

\displaystyle P_{c/2,k}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\chi}\int\limits_{u\in\varpi_{E}^{-c_{0}}O_{E}^{\times}}\theta_{1}^{-1}(u)\chi_{E}(u)\psi_{E}(\varpi^{0,k}u)d^{\times}u\overline{\int\limits_{w\in\varpi_{E}^{-c_{0}}O_{E}^{\times}}\theta_{2}^{-1}(w)\chi_{E}(w)\psi_{E}(\varpi^{0,k}w)d^{\times}w}.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\chi}\iint\limits_{x\in O_{E}^{\times},w\in\varpi_{E}^{-c_{0}}O_{E}^{\times}}\theta_{1}^{-1}(x)\theta_{2}\theta_{1}^{-1}(w)\chi_{E}\left(x\right)\psi_{E}(\varpi^{0,k}(x-1)w)d^{\times}wd^{\times}x.

For the integral in

(5.11)

\displaystyle P_{c/2,k}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=

\displaystyle\frac{\theta_{1}\theta_{2}^{-1}(\varpi_{E}^{c/2-l})}{q^{c-2l-k}}\int\limits_{w\in\varpi_{E}^{-l}O_{E}^{\times}}\theta_{2}\theta_{1}^{-1}(w)\psi_{E}(w)d^{\times}w\sum\limits_{c(\mu_{i}^{-1}\chi)=c/2-k}

\displaystyle\int\limits_{y=(y_{1},y_{2})\in O_{E}^{\times},y_{2}\not\equiv-\varpi^{c/2-k-l}}\theta_{1}^{-1}\chi_{E}(1+\varpi^{c/2-l,c/2-k-l}y)\theta_{1}\theta_{2}^{-1}(y)d^{\times}y.

first

(3)

5.3.3.
The case

(1)

For the remaining computations, we first prove the following lemma:

Lemma 5.7
.

Let

with

, where

\left|\sum\limits_{a\in k_{F}}\theta(1+a\sqrt{D})\right|=1.

Proof.

Indeed as

\sum\limits_{(a,b)\in k_{F}^{2},(a,b)\neq 0}\theta(a+b\sqrt{D})=0.

Using that

\sum\limits_{(a,b)\in k_{E}^{2},(a,b)\neq 0}\theta(a+b\sqrt{D})=(q-1)\sum\limits_{a\in k_{F}}\theta(1+a\sqrt{D})+(q-1)\theta(\sqrt{D}).

The lemma follows.
∎

We summarize the remaining computations in the following.

Lemma 5.8
.

Suppose that
p
is large enough.
Suppose that
\theta_{i}
are defined over étale quadratic algebras

and
E^{\prime}
with

, such that either

, or

\displaystyle P_{1}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{c(\chi)\leq 1}\int\limits_{v_{E}(u)=-1}\theta_{1}^{-1}(u)\chi_{E}(u)\psi_{E}(u)d^{\times}u\overline{\int\limits_{v_{E^{\prime}}(w)=-1}\theta_{2}^{-1}(w)\chi_{E^{\prime}}(w)\psi_{E^{\prime}}(w)d^{\times}w}\ll\frac{1}{q^{2}},

\displaystyle P_{1}(W_{1},W_{2})\ll\frac{1}{q}.

Note that if we directly apply the bound for each Gauss integrals, we will get

and
\theta_{1}=\theta_{2}^{\pm 1}

Note that as

\left|\int\limits_{v_{E}(u)=-1}\theta_{1}^{-1}(u)\psi_{E}(u)d^{\times}u\right|\ll\frac{1}{q}.

Suppose first that

. By a change of variable

\displaystyle P_{1}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{c(\chi)\leq 1}\int\limits_{v_{E}(w)=-1}\int\limits_{v_{E}(x)=0}\theta_{1}^{-1}\theta_{2}(w)\theta_{1}^{-1}(x)\chi_{E}(x)\psi_{E}((x-1)w)d^{\times}xd^{\times}w

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{v_{E}(w)=-1}\int\limits_{v_{E}(x)=0}\theta_{1}^{-1}\theta_{2}(w)\theta_{1}^{-1}(x)\sum\limits_{c(\chi)\leq 1}\chi_{E}(x)\psi_{E}((x-1)w)d^{\times}xd^{\times}w

\displaystyle=(q-1)\int\limits_{v_{E}(w)=-1}\int\limits_{Nm(x)\equiv 1\mod p}\theta_{1}^{-1}\theta_{2}(w)\theta_{1}^{-1}(x)\psi_{E}((x-1)w)d^{\times}xd^{\times}w.

Here

\chi
.
The integral in

, in which case we can apply a change of variable

and get

\displaystyle P_{1}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(q-1)\int\limits_{v_{E}(v)=-1}\theta_{1}^{-1}\theta_{2}(v)\psi_{E}(v)d^{\times}v\int\limits_{Nm(x)\equiv 1\mod p,x-1\in O_{E}^{\times}}\theta_{1}\theta_{2}^{-1}(x-1)\theta_{1}^{-1}(x)d^{\times}x.

Note that

\int\limits_{v_{E}(v)=-1}\theta_{1}^{-1}\theta_{2}(v)\psi_{E}(v)d^{\times}v\ll\frac{1}{q}.

Thus to verify the lemma in this case, it suffices to prove that

\int\limits_{Nm(x)\equiv 1\mod p,x-1\in O_{E}^{\times}}\theta_{1}\theta_{2}^{-1}(x-1)\theta_{1}^{-1}(x)d^{\times}x\ll\frac{1}{q^{2}}.

for

, we have

\displaystyle\int\limits_{Nm(x)\equiv 1\mod p,x-1\in O_{E}^{\times}}\theta_{1}\theta_{2}^{-1}(x-1)\theta_{1}^{-1}(x)d^{\times}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{Vol}(1+\varpi_{E}O_{E},d^{\times}x)\sum\limits_{y\in k_{F}^{\times}\backslash k_{E}^{\times},y-\overline{y}\in k_{E}^{\times}}\theta_{1}\theta_{2}^{-1}(y-\overline{y})\theta_{1}^{-1}(y)\theta_{2}(\overline{y}),

Now if

is a field,
for the sum in
y

,

On the other hand if

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{y\in k_{F}^{\times}\backslash k_{E}^{\times},y-\overline{y}\in k_{E}^{\times}}\theta_{1}\theta_{2}^{-1}(y-\overline{y})\theta_{1}^{-1}(y)\theta_{2}(\overline{y})

Suppose without loss of generality now that

with the above mentioned congruence requirement. Make a change of variable

\displaystyle P_{1}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{v_{E}(v)=0,v\equiv 1}\theta_{1}^{-1}(v)\overline{\mu_{2,E}^{-1}(v)}d^{\times}v-\frac{1}{q-1}\int\limits_{v_{E}(v)=0,v\not\equiv 1}\theta_{1}^{-1}(v)\overline{\mu_{2,E}^{-1}(v)}d^{\times}v

∎
