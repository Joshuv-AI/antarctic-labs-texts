Comparing Classes of Estimators: When Does Gradient Descent Beat Ridge Regression in Linear Models?
Dominic Richards, Edgar Dobriban, Patrick Rebeschini

Abstract
Methods for learning from data depend on various types of tuning parameters, such as penalization strength or step size. Since performance can depend strongly on these parameters, it is important to compare classes of estimators—by considering prescribed finite sets of tuning parameters— not just particularly tuned methods. In this work, we investigate classes of methods via the relative performance of the best method in the class . We consider the central problem of linear regression—with a random isotropic ground truth—and investigate the estimation performance of two fundamental methods, gradient descent and ridge regression. We unveil the following phenomena. (1) For general designs, constant stepsize gradient descent outperforms ridge regression when the eigenvalues of the empirical data covariance matrix decay slowly, as a power law with exponent less than unity. If instead the eigenvalues decay quickly, as a power law with exponent greater than unity or exponentially, we show that ridge regression outperforms gradient descent. (2) For orthogonal designs, we compute the exact minimax optimal class of estimators (achieving min-max- min optimality), showing it is equivalent to gradient descent with decaying learning rate. We find the sub-optimality of ridge regression and gradient descent with constant step size. Our results highlight that statistical performance can depend strongly on tuning parameters. In particular, while optimally tuned ridge regression is the best estimator in our setting, it can be outperformed by gradient descent by an arbitrary/unbounded amount when both methods are only tuned over finitely many regularization parameters.

Introduction

Least squares regression is a workhorse in modern statistics, machine learning, engineering, and signal processing, often lying at the heart of more complex algorithms and methodologies.
It is well known that various forms of shrinkage or regularization often improve estimation performance in the presence of noise
[
Ste56
,
JS61
,
Ste81
]
.
The optimal level of shrinkage required can depend upon
unknown
properties of the problem, such as the signal-to-noise ratio. As a result, in practice the regularization strength is routinely chosen through a two-step procedure: first, a
finite
class, or collection, of candidate models (estimators) is proposed by considering a range of regularization values.
Then a candidate model is chosen from the class through a criterion—such as prediction or estimation error—which itself is estimated, say, through cross-validation using held-out data
[
All74
,
Sto77
,
Gei75
]
.
This leads to a natural statistical question: How should
classes
of candidate models be chosen and compared?

We investigate this fundamental question when classes of candidate models are compared through the
best-in-class model
. Studying the best candidate model in a class allows us to understand the behavior and sensitivity of model classes with respect to their tuning parameters at a level of precision that has not been investigated before.
Indeed, the standard approach is to compare estimators that are optimally tuned with respect to hyperparameters, say by balancing bias and variance.
In contrast, our approach allows us to
evaluate model classes when their hyperparameters are restricted to a finite collection/grid; as they must be in practice.

We instantiate this approach for two popular classes of estimators, the iterates of gradient descent (GD), and ridge regression with a varying regularization strength. Each of these classes is extremely important and widely used.
Gradient descent is the canonical first-order optimization algorithm, and its variants are currently the method of choice for training state-of-the art machine learning models, including deep neural networks
[

,
Bub15
,
KSH12
,

,
LBH15
,

]
. Ridge regression, and more broadly

[

,
Ste56
,
JS61
,

,

]
. For modern deep neural networks, it is linked to the highly popular method of weight decay

Related Literature

From the early works of
[
Tik43
,
Ste56
,
JS61
,
HK70
,
Ste81
]
, a vast literature investigating the performance of shrinkage methods in the context of mean estimation, inverse problems, and least squares regression has developed.
The PhD thesis by Thisted
[
Thi77
]
, in particular, studies ridge regression, making connections with minimax estimation and empirical Bayes methods, including a thorough historical review.
[
Thi77
]
argues for the importance of making a distinction between ridge regression estimators for a fixed regularization, for adaptively chosen regularization, and for classes of models with varying regularization strengths. Our work makes steps towards rigorously formalizing such a distinction, focusing on the performance of classes of models defined by varying the regularization strength. General references on ridge regression include
[
Gru17
,
vW15
]
.

We highlight works related to inverse problems and kernel regression, such as
[
Lan51
,
EHN96
,
BHMR07
,
CDV07
,
YRC07
,
BPR07
,
RWY14
,
RV15
,
BM18
,
PR19
,
LRRC20
]
, where the shrinkage performance of iterative methods like gradient descent has gained much attention. Here statistical performance is encoded by an
optimal rate
at which the estimation or prediction error decays with respect to the sample size
[
CDV07
,
BM18
]
. The rate depends on both the regularity of the problem (e.g., smoothness of the optimal regression function), and the effective dimension (often referred to as the capacity) of the kernel. The guarantees for various estimators—e.g., ridge regression and early stopped gradient descent—aim to show that the optimal rate is achieved in a worst case sense. However, the bounds usually do not capture the dependence on the discretization, which is a key component of our results.

The concurrent work
[
ZWB+21
]
compares the sample inflation, in a Bahadur efficiency sense
[
Bah67
,
Bah71
]
, of single-pass stochastic gradient descent (SGD) to ridge regression i.e., the additional sample size required by SGD in order to have comparable or better performance than ridge regression, when each is optimally tuned. Notably, they observe (Corollary 5.1 within) when the population eigenvalues decay with a power law with exponent less than unity, then the sample inflation is mild (logarithmic). This finding is connected to our results (Theorem
1
), in that we find that gradient descent can outperform ridge regression when the empirical eigenvalues decay at this rate. However, our perspective and results are different and complementary. For instance, we consider a fixed-design setting instead of a random-design setting, and focus on the sensitivity to tuning parameters with respect to the underlying unknown signal-to-noise level, including the effects of discretization, instead of looking at optimal tuning.

The two concurrent works
[
SA22
,
WZF22
]
use an error decomposition similar to Lemma
1
to explore the excess risk of spectral filters arising from continuous time accelerated gradient descent. While our work is linked to excess risk (see the relative sub-optimality definition
3
and Section
2.4.1
), we study finite classes of estimators.
The continuous time limit could perhaps be associated to an infinitely sized class, but this is distinct from our work.
A natural future direction is to explore
using our problem dependent class bounds for ridge regression or gradient descent, to compare with accelerated gradient descent. For extended related literature see supplementary material

.

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{C}):=\min_{\widehat{\beta}\in\mathcal{C}}\left\{\mathbf{E}_{\beta_{\star},\epsilon}[L_{\beta_{\star}}(\widehat{\beta})]-\mathbf{E}_{\beta_{\star},\epsilon}[L_{\beta_{\star}}(\widehat{\beta}_{\lambda_{\star}})]\right\}.

We define the spectral measure
\widehat{H}
associated to the eigenvalues
\{s_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
,
to have a cumulative distribution function, also denoted as
\widehat{H}
, for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
,
given by

\widehat{H}(s):=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mathbbm{1}(s_{i}\leq s)+(1-\frac{r}{d})\mathbbm{1}(0\leq s)
where
\mathbbm{1}(\cdot)
is the indicator function.
The following results bound the sub-optimality for a given class
\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{C})
in terms of a function integrated against the the spectral measure
\widehat{H}
.
This approach is rather natural, as the estimation error of optimally tuned ridge regression can be written

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\beta_{\star},\epsilon}[L_{\beta_{\star}}(\widehat{\beta}_{\lambda_{\star}})]=\lambda_{\star}\int\frac{1}{s+\lambda_{\star}}d\widehat{H}(s).

(16)

In our case bounds on
\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{C})
take the form
\lambda_{\star}\int G(s)d\widehat{H}(s)
for an “error” function
G:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
that describes “where” on the spectrum the estimator is sub-optimal.
Precisely, the function
G
will depend upon
\lambda_{\star}
and encode properties of the class
\mathcal{C}
, e.g., the step size
\eta
and number of iterations
t
for gradient descent, or the discretization length
\delta
for ridge regression. Since
\widehat{H}(s)
is an atomic measure, we adopt the convention, for any
b>a
, that
\int_{b}^{a}d\widehat{H}(s)=\int\mathbbm{1}(s)_{(a,b]}d\widehat{H}(s)
i.e., the integration range is closed on the right and open on the left.

The remainder of this section is then structured as follows. Section
2.4.1
considers classes arising from ridge regression with a uniformly discretized regularization parameter. Section
2.4.2
considers classes arising from the iterations of gradient descent.

2.4.1
Ridge Regression

The following lemma provides upper and lower bounds on the class of estimators arising from ridge regression in the case of a
fine discretization
, i.e., where
\delta=(1-\lambda_{\min})/(k-1)\leq\lambda_{\star}
.

Proposition 1
(Ridge Regression Estimator, Fine Discretization)

Let
\delta=(1-\lambda_{\min})/(k-1)
.
Suppose
0\leq\lambda_{\min}\leq\lambda_{\star}\leq 1
,
\lambda_{\star}\geq\delta>0
and
\lambda_{\star}=\lambda_{\min}+(j+\varepsilon)\delta
for some
j\in\{0,1,\dots,k-2\}
and
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{5}}\!\min\!\{1\!-\!\varepsilon,\varepsilon\}^{2}\lambda_{\star}\!\!\!\int\!\!G^{\text{Ridge}}(s)d\widehat{H}(s)\!\leq\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{C}^{\text{Ridge}}(\lambda_{\min},k))\leq\!4\min\{1\!-\!\varepsilon,\varepsilon\}^{2}\lambda_{\star}\!\!\!\int\!\!G^{\text{Ridge}}(s)d\widehat{H}(s)

where

\displaystyle G^{\text{Ridge}}(s)=\delta^{2}\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\lambda_{\star}s^{2}}&\text{ if }s\in(\lambda_{\star},\infty)\\
\frac{s}{\lambda_{\star}^{4}}&\text{ if }s\in(0,\lambda_{\star}].\end{cases}

Results for tuning ridge regression with either a coarse discretization
\delta>\lambda_{\star}
or logarithmic grid are presented within Proposition
5
and
6
in Section
A.5.1
.

Now, let us rewrite the sub-optimality of ridge regression in terms of a multiplicative constant of optimally tuned ridge regression, so that for some constant
C>0
, and
G^{\text{Ridge}}
from Proposition
1
,

\displaystyle\min_{\widehat{\beta}\in\mathcal{C}^{\text{Ridge}}(\lambda_{\min},k)}\mathbf{E}_{\beta_{\star},\epsilon}[L_{\beta_{\star}}(\widehat{\beta})]=\lambda_{\star}\int\Big(1+CG^{\text{Ridge}}(s)(s+\lambda_{\star})\Big)\frac{1}{s+\lambda_{\star}}d\widehat{H}(s).

We can interpret
CG^{\text{Ridge}}(s)(s+\lambda_{\star})
as a form of spectral dependent
risk inflation
. Recalling the discretization length
\delta=1/k
, and using Propositions
1
,
5
and
6
, we can take a uniform bound to get the risk inflation of ridge regression with each discretization

\displaystyle\max_{s\in(0,s_{1}]}G^{\text{Ridge}}(s)(s+\lambda_{\star})

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{k^{2}}\frac{1}{\lambda_{\star}^{2}},\qquad\max_{s\in(0,s_{1}]}G^{\text{Coarse Ridge}}(s)(s+\lambda_{\star})=\frac{1}{k\lambda_{\star}}

\displaystyle\max_{s\in(0,s_{1}]}G^{\text{log-ridge}}(s)(s+\lambda_{\star})

\displaystyle=\frac{\log^{2}(1/\lambda_{\min})}{4(k-1)^{2}}.

(17)

We make two observations.
First, for the uniform discretizations, the risk inflation initially decays at the “slow” rate of
(k\lambda_{\star})^{-1}
for
k\leq\lambda_{\star}^{-1}
, and then moves to the fast
(k\lambda_{\star})^{-2}
rate for
k\geq\lambda_{\star}^{-1}
. This phase transition in the number of models
k
also reflects what is observed in Section
3.1
for ridge regression with an orthogonal design.
Second, as the log-grid relies on knowing a lower bound
\lambda_{\min}<\lambda_{\star}
we see trade-off as to when a log-grid is beneficial over a uniform grid. Precisely, as the log-grid is favored approximately when
\log(1/\lambda_{\min})\leq 1/\lambda_{\star}
or
\lambda_{\min}\geq e^{-1/\lambda_{\star}}
.

2.4.2
Gradient Descent

The following presents the upper and lower bounds for gradient descent. Recall the definition of
\mathcal{C}^{\text{GD}}(\eta,t)
provided at the beginning of Section
2
.

Proposition 2
(Gradient Descent Estimators, Fine Discretization)

Suppose
0<\lambda_{\star}\leq 1
and
t\geq\lceil 1/(\eta\lambda_{\star})\rceil
for a step size
0<\eta\leq 1/\max\{s_{1},\lambda_{\star}\}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{C}^{\text{GD}}(\eta,t))\leq 18\lambda_{\star}\int G^{\text{GD}}(s)d\widehat{H}(s)

where

\displaystyle G^{\text{GD}}(s)=\begin{cases}\frac{\lambda_{\star}}{s^{2}}&\text{ for }s\in(\lambda_{\star},1/\eta]\\
\frac{s^{3}}{\lambda_{\star}^{4}}&\text{ for }s\in(\eta\lambda_{\star}^{2},\lambda_{\star}]\\
\eta^{2}s&\text{ for }s\in(0,\eta\lambda_{\star}^{2}].\end{cases}

(18)

Furthermore, if
\frac{1}{\eta\lambda_{\star}}=\ell+\kappa
for some integer
\ell
and
\kappa\in(0,1)
, then for any
t\geq 1

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{C}^{\text{GD}}(\eta,t))\geq\frac{1}{16}\min\{1-\kappa,\kappa\}^{2}\lambda_{\star}\int_{0}^{\eta\lambda_{\star}^{2}\kappa/4}G^{\text{GD}}(s)d\widehat{H}(s).

For ridge regression
as studied in Proposition
1
, the discretization length tuning parameter
\delta
simply scales the error function.
In contrast, for gradient descent,
the stepsize
\eta
does not simply scale the error function
G^{\text{GD}}
.
One interpretation is that ridge regression converges
uniformly
as the number of models increases. However, for gradient descent a non-zero error remains regardless of how small the step size is (see Theorem
8
in Appendix
B.8
).

Following the discussion after Theorem
2
in Section
2.2
, we can see how the factor of
\lambda_{\star}^{4}/\lambda^{2}_{\min}
arises within the upper bound of the relative sub-optimality. Specifically, consider the step size
\eta=1/(k\lambda_{\min})
for gradient descent and discretization length
\delta=1/k
for ridge regression. Taking the ratio of error functions, we then have for eigenvalues
s<\eta\lambda_{\star}^{2}
that

G^{\text{GD}}(s)/G^{\text{Ridge}}(s)=\lambda_{\star}^{4}/\lambda_{\min}^{2}
. When considering relative sub-optimality, the corresponding ratio of expectations
\int G^{\text{GD}}(s)d\widehat{H}(s)/\int G^{\text{Ridge}}(s)d\widehat{H}(s)
arises, and therefore, we need to take into account where the spectrum
\widehat{H}(s)
“places mass”.

The lower bound in Proposition
2
holds over “part of the spectrum”, in that the integral is over the range up to
\eta\lambda_{\star}^{2}\kappa/4
.
This lower bound is sharp when there are many eigenvalues within the interval
[0,\eta\lambda_{\star}^{2}\kappa/4]
, e.g., when the eigenvalues decay at a slow power law rate. For lower bounds when there is a faster decay in the eigenvalues, it is natural to focus on the range
(0,\lambda_{\star}]
. In Theorem
8
in Appendix
B.8
, a similar lower bound is given for an integral over the interval
(u_{\min}(\eta),s_{1}]
, for a certain function
u_{\min}
of the stepsize, depending on finer notions of the spectrum.

For completeness a bound for gradient descent in the coarse regime
t\lesssim 1/(\eta\lambda_{\star})
is presented within Proposition
7
in Section
A.5.2
of the supplementary material.

3
Minimax Estimation under Orthogonal Designs

For orthogonal designs, we can obtain even more precise results.
We find the
exact minimax optimal
classes of estimators over a range of signal-to-noise ratios.
Further, we can find the
exact
sub-optimality of ridge regression and gradient descent (Section
3.1
).

To study the minimax optimal class of
k
estimators, we consider a parameter set
\Theta
of design matrices
X
, signal strengths
\psi
, noise levels
\sigma
, and a collection
E
of classes
T_{k}=\{\Phi_{1},\ldots,\Phi_{k}\}
of
k
spectral shrinkers
\Phi_{j}:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
,
j\in[k]
,
each determining a corresponding class of estimators

\widehat{T_{k}}=\{\widehat{\beta}_{\Phi_{1}},\ldots,\widehat{\beta}_{\Phi_{k}}\}
.

13
13

13

We will sometimes also call
T_{k}
a collection, a set, a
k
-tuple, or—in the scalar case—a grid of estimators.

Evaluating the
minimax optimal excess risk
over the class
T_{k}\in E
amounts to finding, for
[k]=\{1,\ldots,k\}
, the following
min-max-min
problem:

\inf_{T_{k}=\{\Phi_{1},\ldots,\Phi_{k}\}\in E}\,\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\,\min_{j\in[k]}\,\mathcal{E}(\widehat{\beta}_{\Phi_{j}}).

(19)

Here we are minimizing over classes
T_{k}\in E
the worst-case risk over the unknown parameters
\theta\in\Theta
of the
best estimator
\widehat{\beta}_{\Phi_{j}}
in the class
T_{k}
.
This is a generalization of the usual notion of minimax excess risk in statistical decision theory and learning, which corresponds to classes with size
k=1
, where the inner minimization is vacuous

[
LC98
,
LR05
,
Ber13
]
.

Since solving problem (
19
) could be very challenging in general, we consider parameter spaces
\Theta
such that all
s_{i}=s>0
are fixed to some known value, and
r=n
. This corresponds to an orthogonal
design where
X^{\top}X/n=sI_{d}
.
We assume that the noise level is fixed, and without loss of generality we consider
\sigma=1
.
Thus, for some lower and upper bounds
0\leq\psi_{-}\leq\psi_{+}
on the signal strength
\psi
, we study the parameter space

\Theta=\{\psi\in[\psi_{-},\psi_{+}],X^{\top}X/n=sI_{d},n=d\}.

(20)

We have the following result, proved in Section
C.1
.

Theorem 6
(Minimax Optimal Class for Orthogonal Designs)

Let the parameter set
\Theta
be defined in (
20
) and let
\sigma=1
.
Consider the collection
E
of all classes of
k
estimators

T_{k}=\{\Phi_{1},\ldots,\Phi_{k}\}
determined by spectral shrinkers.
There is a minimax optimal class, solving the min-max-min problem (
19
), of the form

\widehat{T_{k}}=\left\{[1-\Phi_{j}(s)]/s\cdot X^{\top}Y/n,j=1,\ldots,k\right\},

where

\Phi_{j}
are constant functions defined, for all
s\geq 0
, by

\displaystyle\Phi_{j}(s)=\left[\frac{1}{x_{+}}+\left(j-\frac{1}{2}\right)c\right]^{2}-\frac{c^{2}}{4},

and
c=\frac{1}{k}(\frac{1}{{x_{-}}}-\frac{1}{{x_{+}}})
, with
x_{\pm}=\sqrt{1+s\psi_{\pm}}
.
The minimax optimal excess risk, i.e., the optimal value of the objective in (
19
), is

\displaystyle M(\psi_{-},\psi_{+},s,k)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k^{2}s}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+s\psi_{-}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+s\psi_{+}}}\right)^{2}.

The proof is based on a combination of classical mathematical analysis and explicit calculations, including an “infinite descent” argument to find the minimizer.
Specifically, after some calculations, we can reduce the problem to evaluating the quantity given in equation (
65
) in the appendix. This is a minimum over
k+1
terms.
We argue by contradiction that the solution is achieved when the
k+1
quantities in (
65
) are equal.
To show this, we suppose that at the minimum, the
k+1
terms are not all equal, and choose the maximal term. Then, by careful analysis, we verify that by changing one of the optimization variables in that term, we can decrease the maximal term to equal its larger neighbor, which is a contradiction to our assumption that we were at the minimum. Surprisingly, the mini-max optimal grid aligns with gradient descent with a decreasing learning rate. For details on this see the discussion within Section
A.6
.

3.1
Sub-optimality compared to the Minimax Grid

It is of interest to characterize the sub-optimality of ridge regression and gradient descent to the minimax optimal grid. The following result is proved in Section
C.2
. Denote by
\{x\}
the fractional part of the scalar
x
, and by
\lfloor x\rfloor
its floor, the largest integer less than or equal to
x
. Denote by
C_{\text{Ridge}}(\delta)=\{\widehat{\beta}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\{\delta,2\delta,\dots,1\}}
the class of estimators equal to ridge regression with regularization over a grid
\{\delta,2\delta,\dots,1\}
.

Proposition 3
(Max-Min Excess Risk of Ridge Regression)

Under the conditions of Theorem
6
, let
\delta=1/k
,
\phi=k/\psi_{-}-(\lfloor k/\psi_{+}\rfloor+1)
, and
\psi_{-}>k/(k+1)
. Then, the worst-case excess risk of the best estimator in the class
C_{\text{Ridge}}(\delta)
of ridge regression estimators with regularization over the grid
\{\delta,2\delta,\dots,1\}

=

\{1/k,\ldots,1\}
equals

\sup_{\psi\in[\psi_{-},\psi_{+}]}\min\limits_{\widehat{\beta}\in C_{\text{Ridge}}(\delta)}\mathcal{E}(\widehat{\beta})=\frac{1}{s}\max\left\{Q_{\star}(j,\tau^{\star}_{1})^{2},Q_{\star}(j+1,\tau^{\star}_{2})^{2}\right\}

where
j=\lfloor k/\psi_{+}\rfloor
,
a=ks
, and

Q_{\star}(j,\tau)=\frac{a/[j+\tau]}{(1+a/[j+\tau])^{1/2}}\min\left(\frac{\tau}{j+a},\frac{1-\tau}{j+1+a}\right),

\displaystyle\tau^{\star}_{1}=\max\left(\left\{\frac{k}{\psi_{+}}\right\},\frac{\lfloor k/\psi_{+}\rfloor+a}{2(\lfloor k/\psi_{+}\rfloor+a)+1}\right)\!\!\quad\quad\!\!\text{and}\!\!\quad\quad\!\!\tau^{\star}_{2}=\min\left(\phi,\frac{\lfloor k/\psi_{+}\rfloor+1+a}{2(\lfloor k/\psi_{+}\rfloor+1+a)+1}\right).

The condition
\psi_{-}>k/(k+1)
requires that
\psi_{-}
is sufficiently large, while
\lfloor k/\psi_{+}\rfloor+1\leq k/\psi_{-}
requires that
\psi_{+}
is sufficiently large compared to
\psi_{-}
and
k
.

If
s=1
, we can verify
\tau_{1}^{*}\in[1/3,1]
, and
a/[j+\tau_{1}^{*}]=k/[\lfloor k/\psi_{+}\rfloor+\tau_{1}^{*}]=O(\psi_{+})
, where
A=O(B)
means that
A\leq CB
for a constant
C
not depending on
k
. Also

\frac{\tau_{1}^{*}}{j+a}=O\left(\frac{1}{\lfloor k/\psi_{+}\rfloor+k}\right)=O\left(\frac{1}{k}\right).

This shows that
Q_{\star}(j,\tau^{\star}_{1})=O(1/k)
.
The same reasoning also shows that
Q_{\star}(j+1,\tau^{\star}_{2})=O(1/k)
.
Thus, the upper bound on the excess risk of ridge is of order at most
O(1/k)
. As we will see numerically, the rate decays linearly in
1/k
in certain ranges of
k
, and linearly in
1/k^{2}
in other ranges. This is a subtle and perhaps unexpected behavior that is uncovered by our analysis.

A similar approach allows us to characterize the sub-optimality of gradient descent. We have the following result, proved in Section
C.3
.
Let
C_{\text{GD}}(\eta,k)=\{\hat{\beta}_{\eta,i}\}_{i=1,\dots,k}
denote the class of estimators associated to the first
k
iterations of gradient descent initialized at
\hat{\beta}_{\eta,0}=0
zero, with step size
\eta>0
. We denote
a\vee b=\max\{a,b\}
as well as
a\wedge b=\min\{a,b\}
.

Proposition 4
(Max-Min Excess Risk of Gradient Descent)

Under the conditions of Theorem
6
and Proposition
3
, consider gradient descent with step size
0<\eta<1/s
. Let

b=1-\eta s\in(0,1),\!\quad j=\lfloor\log_{1/b}(1+s\psi_{-})\rfloor,\!\quad\tau^{\star}=\log_{1/b}(2/(b+1)),\!\quad\tau_{-}=\{\log_{1/b}(1+s\psi_{-})\}

and suppose that

(1-\eta s)^{k}<(1+s\psi_{+})^{-1}.

Then, the worst-case excess risk of the best iterate of gradient descent out of the first
k
iterates equals

\sup_{\psi\in[\psi_{-},\psi_{+}]}\min\limits_{\widehat{\beta}\in C_{\text{GD}}(\eta,t)}\mathcal{E}(\widehat{\beta})=b^{j-\tau^{\star}}\max\big\{Q_{\star}(\tau_{-}\vee\tau^{\star})^{2},b\cdot Q_{\star}(\tau_{-}\wedge\tau^{\star})^{2}\big\},

where

\displaystyle Q_{\star}(\tau)=\min\{1-b^{\tau},b(b^{\tau-1}-1)\}.

The condition
(1-\eta s)^{k}<(1+s\psi_{+})^{-1}
requires
\eta
and
k
to be large enough. This assumption ensures that the gradient descent iterates reach far enough so that the optimal stopping point is before the last iteration. Relaxing this condition requires a more careful study of the behavior of the last iteration, which we do not expect to lead to major new insights. If
s=1
and
\eta=C/k
for some constant
C>0
, then
(1-\eta s)^{k}\leq\exp(-C)
, and thus the aforementioned assumption is satisfied for
C
sufficiently large with respect to
\psi_{+}
. From Proposition
4
we then find that the excess risk is
O(\eta^{2})=O(1/k^{2})
, since the maximum of
Q_{\star}(\tau)
is achieved at
\tau^{\star}
, and
b^{\tau^{\star}}=(1+b)/2=1-\eta/2
.

4
Conclusion

We have introduced a
framework to compare the statistical performance of
classes of estimators
.
In linear regression with a random ground truth,
we have compared gradient descent and ridge regression.
We have found that,
if the rank of the empirical covariance matrix is sufficiently large, the
rate
at which the eigenvalues decays influences whether gradient descent or ridge regression is better, with a
slower decay
leading to more favorable behavior for gradient descent over.
This is surprising, as for the infinitely large set of all regularization parameters, the situation is reversed.
Our work lays foundations to investigate how classes of estimators—e.g., those obtained by implicit or explicit regularization—should be constructed and adapted to a specific problem instance, instead of considering optimally tuned estimators.

A.1
Related Literature

Our work is related to the study of the asymptotic performance of ridge regression in a high-dimensional setting where both the ambient dimension and number of data points go to infinity in proportion to one another, e.g.,
[
TV04
,
EKK11
,
EK18
,
Dic13
,
Dic16
,
DW18
,
HMRT19
,
AKT19
,
ADT20
,
RMR21
,
WX20
,
LD19
]
etc. Assuming the data follows a linear model or certain nonlinear models, the works
[

,
DW18
,
HMRT19
,
AKT19
,
ADT20
,
RMR21
,
WX20
,
LD19
]
study the estimation and prediction error of ridge regression in terms of integrals against the eigenvalue distribution of the empirical covariance matrix. By making assumptions on the covariate structure, they leverage the convergence of the empirical eigenvalue distribution to variants of the Marchenko–Pastur law
[
MP67
,
BS10
]
to obtain results in terms of population quantities. While our results are in terms of the eigenvalue spectrum, we alternatively focus on the fixed design setting with spectral distributions that are distinct from those classically studied in random matrix theory, such as eigenvalues with a power law or exponential decay.
Such distributions have been observed empirically in many different applications, including in finance, biology, and signal processing. For examples, see Section 8 of the arXiv version of the paper
[
Dob17
]
.

Our focus differs from the literature on tuning regularization for least squares via cross-validation
[
AC10
,
KKV11
,
MM18
,
XMR19
,
HMRT19
,
PWRT21
]
, which considers empirical model selection criteria and their consistency. Similarly, the works
[
BM01
,
BGH09
,
AB09
,
AM09
,
BY20
]
investigate selection criteria aiming to recover the performance of the best-in-class model (often referred to as oracle within). In contrast, we focus on choosing the class of models which arises from, for instance, a

finite grid of regularization parameters
.

For an orthogonal design matrix, the least squares problem reduces to mean estimation. However, unlike the usual setting where the mean parameter is fixed, here we consider a random-effects model where the mean parameter is isotropically distributed, and are interested in the average-case behavior over this prior. In this sense, our work has a Bayesian interpretation
[
LC98
,
Ber13
]
.

There is a great deal of other work studying statistical properties of optimization methods.
[
SGB94
]
show that in linear models, early stopped gradient flow on the ridge regression objective with a sufficiently small positive regularization parameter can outperform ridge regression in estimation error for
a fixed design and regression parameters
. In contrast, for the random-effects models we consider, ridge regression is average-case (i.e., Bayes) optimal under certain assumptions. Other connections between algorithmic regularization and penalization have also been studied
[
QQ19
]
.

The related work
[
DFKU13
]
shows that the estimation risk of ridge regression and principal component regression are within a multiplicative factor of four. Our approach is distinctly different, beyond just considering a different pair of estimators (early stopped gradient descent in place of principal component regression). First,
[
DFKU13
]
compares estimators with the
same
hyperparameter choice (denoted
\lambda>0
), while we compare each estimator with their
best
hyperparameter choice from two given sets. This is motivated from the best hyperparameter choice for principal component regression (or any general estimator) potentially not aligning with that for ridge regression. Second, our analysis also accounts for finding the best regularization parameter (by searching over a grid, say). This depends upon the estimator’s sensitivity with respect to its hyperparameter, specifically, the change due to the grid resolution around the optimal hyperparameter choice, and can result in estimators outperforming ridge regression (Theorem
1
). As principal component regression can be represented via a spectral shrinker, one future research direction is to extend the results of
[

]
into the framework developed within our work.

A.3
Discussion of our framework: Gradient Descent versus Ridge Regression

Our framework aims to address the issue that hyperparameter tuning can strongly affect the behavior of algorithms. Already in a very simple setting, namely in random effects linear models, we show that this leads to a sophisticated behavior of the relative performance of two truly fundamental classes of regularization methods: ridge regression and gradient descent.
This discovery is only possible due to our framework of comparing best in class algorithms; which in our view demonstrates the power of our perspective.

There are a number of important further considerations to discuss.

1.

In future work, certain key algorithmic considerations need to be taken into account for a more thorough comparison between methods. For instance, as we have shown, our framework can be extended to incorporate that some algorithms are computationally more expensive than others, by simply changing the class of algorithms to match the computational cost. In addition, it will be important to apply it to study more sophisticated classes of algorithms, and for more general data generating models.

2.

At the moment, our framework compares methods according to their true expected performance on the test distribution. In practice, the methods would be compared
after having selected the best-in-class on a finite training dataset, and
according to their performance on a finite a test set.
This will introduce some amount of noise, and thus has to be taken into account in the analysis. However we expect that with sufficiently large training and test sets, our conclusions would largely be unchanged.
The analysis of the noise due to the finiteness of the training and test set remains an important future problem.

\lambda_{\min}=d\sigma_{\min}^{2}/n\leq d\sigma^{2}/(2n)=\lambda_{\star}/2\leq 1/2
and therefore
1\geq(1-\lambda_{\min})^{2}\geq 1/4
.
Meanwhile, since
\lambda_{\star}=\lambda_{\min}+(j+\varepsilon)\delta
for an integer
k-2\geq j\geq 0
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, we have
\text{Dist}(\lambda_{\star},\Gamma)=\delta\min\{\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon\}
and as such
\text{Dist}_{\delta}(\lambda_{\star},\Gamma)=\text{Dist}(\lambda_{\star},\Gamma)/\delta=\min\{\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon\}
.
This then gives us
\mathcal{S}\left(\mathcal{C}^{\text{GD}}(\eta,k),\mathcal{C}^{\text{Ridge}}(\lambda_{\min},k)\right)\simeq\text{Dist}_{\delta}(\lambda_{\star},\Gamma)^{-2}\lambda_{\star}^{4}/\lambda_{\min}^{2}
as required.

Fast Power Law or Exponential Decay
:
We now consider the case when the eigenvalues decay more quickly, beginning with the case of eigenvalues that decay exponentially, so
s_{i}=\exp(-\rho(i-1))
for some
\rho>0
for
i=1,\dots,r
. The condition on
k
from (
13
) in Theorem
4
is satisfied by assumption, whereas the upper bound on
\lambda_{\star}
from (
13
) is on the order of a constant.
The condition
r\geq 1+\rho^{-1}\log(1/\lambda_{\star})
in Theorem
4
holds due to the assumption
s_{r}\leq\lambda_{\star}
. Given this we have from Theorem
4
the lower bound

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\left(\mathcal{C}^{\text{GD}}(\eta,k),\mathcal{C}^{\text{Ridge}}(\lambda_{\min},k)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{C_{\rho}}{\min\{1-\varepsilon,\varepsilon\}^{2}}\left(\frac{\lambda_{\star}k}{1-\lambda_{\min}}\right)^{2}

(27)

for some constant
C_{\rho}>0
depending exponentially
\rho
, i.e.,
C_{\rho}=\Theta(\exp(-\rho))
, and independent of
n,p,k,\sigma
. Following the previous case, going from
\min\{1-\varepsilon,\varepsilon\}
to
\text{Dist}_{\delta}(\lambda_{\star},\Gamma)
, we arrive at the lower bound

\mathcal{S}\left(\mathcal{C}^{\text{GD}}(\eta,k),\mathcal{C}^{\text{Ridge}}(\lambda_{\min},k)\right)\gtrsim\text{Dist}_{\delta}(\lambda_{\star},\Gamma)^{-2}(k\lambda_{\star})^{2}
as
k
grows, provided
\rho\lesssim\log(k)
.

Let us now consider eigenvalues that decay at a faster power law rate so
s_{i}=i^{-\alpha}
for
\alpha>1
and
i=1,\dots,r
. From Theorem
5
we see once again that the condition (
14
)
on
k
is implied by the assumption of the theorem,
and the upper bound (
15
) on
\lambda_{\star}
is on the order of a constant. Similarly,
r\geq\lambda_{\star}^{-1/\alpha}
holds due to
s_{r}\leq\lambda_{\star}
. Given this, the lower bound matches (
27
) for a (different) constant
C_{\alpha}>0
depending expoentially on
\alpha
so
C_{\alpha}=\Theta(\exp(-\alpha))
, and thus, we have the desired lower bound provided
\alpha\lesssim\log(k)
.

B.3
Proofs for Relative Sub-optimality with Slow Power Law Decaying Eigenvalues

In this section we present proofs for the results associated to bounding the relative sub-optimality of gradient descent and ridge regression when the eigenvalues decay at a slow power law rate. Section
B.3.1
presents the proof of Theorem
2
which gives an upper bound, meanwhile Section
B.3.2
presents the proof of Theorem
3
which gives the lower bound.

B.3.1
Proof of Theorem
2

Plugging the expression for
\delta
in Proposition
1
from Section
2.4
, with
\delta=(1-\lambda_{\min})/(k-1)
(where the condition
\lambda_{\star}\geq\delta
holds because
\lambda_{\star}\geq\lambda_{\min}\geq(1-\lambda_{\min})/(k-1)=\delta
, as
\lambda_{\min}>1/k
) immediately yields the lower bound for ridge regression

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{C}^{\text{Ridge}}(\lambda_{\min},k))\geq\frac{1}{2^{5}}\min\{1-\varepsilon,\varepsilon\}^{2}\lambda_{\star}\int G^{\text{Ridge}}(s)d\widehat{H}(s),

where

\displaystyle\int G^{\text{Ridge}}(s)d\widehat{H}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-\lambda_{\min})^{2}}{(k-1)^{2}}\frac{1}{d}\left(\sum_{i:s_{i}>\lambda_{\star}}\frac{1}{\lambda_{\star}s_{i}^{2}}+\sum_{i:s_{i}\leq\lambda_{\star}}\frac{s_{i}}{\lambda_{\star}^{4}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(1-\lambda_{\min})^{2}}{k^{2}}\frac{1}{d}\frac{1}{\lambda_{\star}^{4}}\left(\lambda_{\star}^{3}\sum_{i:s_{i}>\lambda_{\star}}\frac{1}{s_{i}^{2}}+\sum_{i:s_{i}\leq\lambda_{\star}}s_{i}\right).

(28)

We now turn to gradient descent. Since
\eta=\frac{1}{k\lambda_{\min}}
, using Proposition
2
with
t=k
, where the condition
t\geq\lceil 1/(\eta\lambda_{\star})\rceil
holds because
t=k=1/(\eta\lambda_{\min})\geq\lceil 1/(\eta\lambda_{\star})\rceil
, we find

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{C}^{\text{GD}}(\eta,t))\leq 18\lambda_{\star}\int G^{\text{GD}}(s)d\widehat{H}(s),

where

\displaystyle\int G^{\text{GD}}(s)d\widehat{H}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\left(\sum_{i:s_{i}>\lambda_{\star}}\frac{\lambda_{\star}}{s_{i}^{2}}+\sum_{i:\lambda_{\star}^{2}/(k\lambda_{\min})<s_{i}\leq\lambda_{\star}}\frac{s_{i}^{3}}{\lambda_{\star}^{4}}+\frac{1}{k^{2}\lambda_{\min}^{2}}\sum_{i:s_{i}\leq\lambda_{\star}^{2}/(k\lambda_{\min})}s_{i}\right)

(29)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\frac{1}{\lambda_{\star}^{4}}\left(\lambda_{\star}^{3}\sum_{i:s_{i}>\lambda_{\star}}\frac{1}{s_{i}^{2}}+\sum_{i:\lambda_{\star}^{2}/(k\lambda_{\min})<s_{i}\leq\lambda_{\star}}s_{i}^{3}+\frac{\lambda_{\star}^{4}}{k^{2}\lambda_{\min}^{2}}\sum_{i:s_{i}\leq\lambda_{\star}^{2}/(k\lambda_{\min})}s_{i}\right).

Dividing this by the bound in (
28
) gives, using
\lambda_{\star}^{2}/(k\lambda_{\min})\leq\lambda_{\star}

\displaystyle\frac{\int G^{\text{GD}}(s)d\widehat{H}(s)}{\int G^{\text{Ridge}}(s)d\widehat{H}(s)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(1-\lambda_{\min})^{2}}\left[k^{2}\frac{\lambda_{\star}^{3}\sum_{i:s_{i}>\lambda_{\star}}\frac{1}{s_{i}^{2}}}{\left(\lambda_{\star}^{3}\sum_{i:s_{i}>\lambda_{\star}}\frac{1}{s_{i}^{2}}+\sum_{i:s_{i}\leq\lambda_{\star}}s_{i}\right)}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\left.+k^{2}\frac{\sum_{i:\lambda_{\star}^{2}/(k\lambda_{\min})<s_{i}\leq\lambda_{\star}}s_{i}^{3}}{\left(\lambda_{\star}^{3}\sum_{i:s_{i}>\lambda_{\star}}\frac{1}{s_{i}^{2}}+\sum_{i:s_{i}\leq\lambda_{\star}}s_{i}\right)}+\frac{\lambda_{\star}^{4}}{\lambda_{\min}^{2}}\frac{\sum_{i:s_{i}\leq\lambda_{\star}^{2}/(k\lambda_{\min})}s_{i}}{\left(\lambda_{\star}^{3}\sum_{i:s_{i}>\lambda_{\star}}\frac{1}{s_{i}^{2}}+\sum_{i:s_{i}\leq\lambda_{\star}}s_{i}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(1-\lambda_{\min})^{2}}\left[k^{2}\frac{\lambda_{\star}^{3}\sum_{i:s_{i}>\lambda_{\star}}\frac{1}{s_{i}^{2}}}{\sum_{i:s_{i}\leq\lambda_{\star}}s_{i}}+k^{2}\frac{\sum_{i:\lambda_{\star}^{2}/(k\lambda_{\min})<s_{i}\leq\lambda_{\star}}s_{i}^{3}}{\sum_{i:s_{i}\leq\lambda_{\star}}s_{i}}+\frac{\lambda_{\star}^{4}}{\lambda_{\min}^{2}}\right].

Since
s_{i}=i^{-\alpha}
for
i=1,\dots,r
, we have the following bounds:

\displaystyle\sum_{i:s_{i}>\lambda_{\star}}\frac{1}{s_{i}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{\star}^{-(2+1/\alpha)}

(30)

\displaystyle\sum_{i:s_{i}\leq\lambda_{\star}}s_{i}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{r^{1-\alpha}}{2}

(31)

\displaystyle\sum_{i:\lambda_{\star}^{2}/(k\lambda_{\min})<s_{i}\leq\lambda_{\star}}s_{i}^{3}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{J}_{\alpha,\lambda_{\star}}(k,\lambda_{\min}),

(32)

where
\mathcal{J}_{\alpha,\lambda_{\star}}
is defined in (
10
).
Plugging in we then find

\displaystyle\frac{\int G^{\text{GD}}(s)d\widehat{H}(s)}{\int G^{\text{Ridge}}(s)d\widehat{H}(s)}\leq\frac{2}{(1-\lambda_{\min})^{2}}\left[\frac{k^{2}}{r^{1-\alpha}}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{\star}^{1/\alpha-1}}+\mathcal{J}_{\alpha,\lambda_{\star}}(k,\lambda_{\min})\right)+\frac{\lambda_{\star}^{4}}{\lambda_{\min}^{2}}\right]

as required.

We now show the bounds (
30
), (
31
), (
32
). For (
30
) we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i:s_{i}>\lambda_{\star}}\frac{1}{s_{i}^{2}}=\sum_{i<\lambda_{\star}^{-1/\alpha}}i^{2\alpha}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\lambda_{\star}^{-1/\alpha}}i^{2\alpha}\leq\lambda_{\star}^{-1/\alpha}\lambda_{\star}^{-2}=\lambda_{\star}^{-(2+1/\alpha)}.

For (
31
) we have from
i^{-\alpha}\geq\int_{i}^{i+1}x^{-\alpha}dx
that

\displaystyle\sum_{i:s_{i}\leq\lambda_{\star}}s_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=\lceil\lambda_{\star}^{-1/\alpha}\rceil}^{r}i^{-\alpha}\geq\int_{\lceil\lambda_{\star}^{-1/\alpha}\rceil}^{r+1}x^{-\alpha}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\left((r+1)^{1-\alpha}-(\lceil\lambda_{\star}^{-1/\alpha}\rceil)^{1-\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\left(r^{1-\alpha}-(\lceil\lambda_{\star}^{-1/\alpha}\rceil)^{1-\alpha}\right)\geq\frac{r^{1-\alpha}}{2(1-\alpha)},

for the last inequality we used the assumption that
r\geq 2^{1/(1-\alpha)}(1+\lambda_{\star}^{-1/\alpha})
.

Finally for (
32
) we have with
a=\lambda_{\star}^{2}/(k\lambda_{\min})
,
b=\lambda_{\star}
as well as with
i^{-3\alpha}\leq\int_{i-1}^{i}x^{-3\alpha}dx
that

\displaystyle\sum_{i:a<s_{i}\leq b}s_{i}^{3}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=\lceil b^{-1/\alpha}\rceil}^{\lceil a^{-1/\alpha}\rceil-1}i^{-3\alpha}=\lceil b^{-1/\alpha}\rceil^{-3\alpha}+\sum_{i=\lceil b^{-1/\alpha}\rceil+1}^{\lceil a^{-1/\alpha}\rceil-1}i^{-3\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq\lceil b^{-1/\alpha}\rceil^{-3\alpha}+\int_{\lceil b^{-1/\alpha}\rceil}^{\lceil a^{-1/\alpha}\rceil-1}x^{-3\alpha}dx\leq\lceil b^{-1/\alpha}\rceil^{-3\alpha}+\int_{b^{-1/\alpha}}^{a^{-1/\alpha}}x^{-3\alpha}dx

\displaystyle\leq 1+\begin{cases}3\log(k\lambda_{\min}/\lambda_{\star})&\text{ if }\alpha=1/3\\
\frac{\lambda_{\star}^{3-1/\alpha}}{1-3\alpha}\left[\left(\frac{\lambda_{\star}}{k\lambda_{\min}}\right)^{3-1/\alpha}-1\right]&\text{ if }\alpha\not=1/3\end{cases}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{J}_{\alpha,\lambda_{\star}}(k,\lambda_{\min}),

where we used that
\lceil\lambda_{\star}^{-1/\alpha}\rceil^{-3\alpha}\leq\lambda_{\star}^{3}\leq 1
. This yields the desired inequality.

B.3.2
Proof of Theorem
3

Following the steps in Theorem
2
but applying the upper bound for ridge regression from Proposition
1
with
\delta=(1-\lambda_{\min})/(k-1)
gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{C}^{\text{Ridge}}(\lambda_{\min},k))\leq 4\min\left\{1-\varepsilon,\varepsilon\right\}^{2}\lambda_{\star}\int G^{\text{Ridge}}(s)d\widehat{H}(s).

(33)

Moreover (
30
), (
31
) and the condition

yield

\displaystyle\sum_{i:s_{i}\leq\lambda_{\star}}s_{i}\geq\frac{r^{1-\alpha}}{2}\geq\lambda_{\star}^{1-1/\alpha}=\lambda_{\star}^{3}\lambda_{\star}^{-(2+1/\alpha)}\geq\lambda_{\star}^{3}\sum_{i:s_{i}>\lambda_{\star}}\frac{1}{s_{i}^{2}}.

(34)

Therefore

\displaystyle\int G^{\text{Ridge}}(s)d\widehat{H}(s)\leq\frac{(1-\lambda_{\min})^{2}}{(k-1)^{2}}\frac{1}{d}\frac{1}{\lambda_{\star}^{4}}2\sum_{i:s_{i}\leq\lambda_{\star}}s_{i}.

For gradient descent, Proposition
2
yields the lower bound

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{C}^{\text{GD}}(\eta,k))

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{16}\min\{1-\kappa,\kappa\}^{2}\lambda_{\star}\int_{0}^{\eta\lambda_{\star}^{2}\kappa/4}G^{\text{GD}}(s)d\widehat{H}(s).

(35)

Plugging in that
\eta=\frac{1}{k\lambda_{\min}}
as well as that
G^{\text{GD}}(s)=\eta^{2}s
for

by definition (

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\eta\lambda_{\star}^{2}\kappa/4}G^{\text{GD}}(s)d\widehat{H}(s)=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i:s_{i}\leq\eta\lambda_{\star}^{2}\kappa/4}\eta^{2}s_{i}=\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{\min}k}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i:s_{i}\leq\frac{\lambda_{\star}^{2}\kappa}{4k\lambda_{\min}}}s_{i}.

(36)

Taking the ratio with the ridge regression bound and plugging in the above bounds yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{C}^{\text{GD}}(\eta,k),\mathcal{C}^{\text{Ridge}}(\lambda_{\min},k))\geq\frac{1}{64}\frac{\min\{1-\kappa,\kappa\}^{2}}{\min\{1-\varepsilon,\varepsilon\}^{2}}\frac{\int_{0}^{\eta\lambda_{\star}^{2}\kappa/4}G^{\text{GD}}(s)d\widehat{H}(s)}{\int G^{\text{Ridge}}(s)d\widehat{H}(s)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{128}\frac{\min\{1-\kappa,\kappa\}^{2}}{\min\{1-\varepsilon,\varepsilon\}^{2}}\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{(1-\lambda_{\min})^{2}}\frac{\lambda_{\star}^{4}}{\lambda_{\min}^{2}}\frac{\sum_{i:s_{i}\leq\frac{\lambda_{\star}^{2}\kappa}{4k\lambda_{\min}}}s_{i}}{\sum_{i:s_{i}\leq\lambda_{\star}}s_{i}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{512}\frac{\min\{1-\kappa,\kappa\}^{2}}{\min\{1-\varepsilon,\varepsilon\}^{2}}\frac{1}{(1-\lambda_{\min})^{2}}\frac{\lambda_{\star}^{4}}{\lambda_{\min}^{2}}\frac{\sum_{i:s_{i}\leq\frac{\lambda_{\star}^{2}\kappa}{4k\lambda_{\min}}}s_{i}}{\sum_{i:s_{i}\leq\lambda_{\star}}s_{i}}.

We bound

\displaystyle\sum_{i:s_{i}\leq\frac{\lambda_{\star}^{2}\kappa}{4k\lambda_{\min}}}s_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i:s_{i}\leq\lambda_{\star}}s_{i}-\sum_{i:\lambda_{\star}\geq s_{i}>\frac{\lambda_{\star}^{2}\kappa}{4k\lambda_{\min}}}s_{i}\geq\sum_{i:s_{i}\leq\lambda_{\star}}s_{i}-\sum_{i:s_{i}>\frac{\lambda_{\star}^{2}\kappa}{4k\lambda_{\min}}}s_{i}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i:s_{i}\leq\lambda_{\star}}s_{i}-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\left(\frac{4k\lambda_{\min}}{\lambda_{\star}^{2}\kappa}\right)^{1/\alpha-1},

where we have used that
\sum_{i:s_{i}>b}s_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{\lceil b^{-1/\alpha}\rceil-1}i^{-\alpha}\leq 1+\int_{1}^{b^{-1/\alpha}}x^{-\alpha}dx\leq\frac{1}{1-\alpha}b^{1-1/\alpha}
.

Therefore, using (
31
) we find

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i:s_{i}\leq\frac{\lambda_{\star}^{2}\kappa}{4k\lambda_{\min}}}s_{i}}{\sum_{i:s_{i}\leq\lambda_{\star}}s_{i}}\geq 1-\frac{2}{1-\alpha}\left(\frac{4k\lambda_{\min}}{\lambda_{\star}^{2}\kappa}\right)^{1/\alpha-1}\frac{1}{r^{1-\alpha}}.

(37)

This then leads to the required lower bound.

B.4.2

We now consider Theorem

B.8
Proofs for Relative Sub-optimality with Fast Decaying Eigenvalues

In this section we present the proofs of the lower bounds on the relative sub-optimality when the eigenvalues decay either exponentially or as a power law with a power greater than one. We begin with a series of technical results. The first shows that the error of gradient descent is decreasing for a particular number of iterations.

Lemma 2

Let for
i=1,\dots,r
,
b_{i}=\frac{\lambda_{\star}}{\lambda_{\star}+s_{i}}

as well as

\displaystyle u_{\min}(\eta):=\arg\inf_{u>2\lambda_{\star}}\left\{A(u,\eta)\leq 1-\exp\left(-\frac{1}{64}\right)\right\},

(49)

where

A(u,\eta):=\frac{\sum_{i:s_{i}>u}b_{i}+\eta/2\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{r}b_{i}s_{i}(1+\frac{s_{i}}{\lambda_{\star}})^{2}}{\sum_{i:\lambda_{\star}<s_{i}\leq u/2}b_{i}}.

(50)

If
\eta\leq\frac{1}{64s_{1}\log(1+\frac{s_{1}}{\lambda_{\star}})}
and
u_{\min}(\eta)\leq s_{1}
, then the expected estimation error of gradient descent is decreasing up to
t=\lceil t^{\star}(u_{\min}(\eta))\rceil
iterations so that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\beta_{\star},\epsilon}[L_{\beta_{\star}}(\widehat{\beta}_{\eta,t+1})]\leq\mathbf{E}_{\beta_{\star},\epsilon}[L_{\beta_{\star}}(\widehat{\beta}_{\eta,t})]\quad\quad\text{ for }t\leq t^{\star}(u_{\min}(\eta)).

With slight abuse of notation, we will often abbreviate
A(u)=A(u,\eta)
.

A consequence of this lemma is that the optimal number of iterations is at least
t^{\star}(u_{\min}(\eta))
. This allows us to prove that gradient descent essentially overfits the large eigenvalue directions, which provides the required lower bounds.

Theorem 8

Suppose
\eta\leq\frac{1}{64s_{1}\log(1+s_{1}/\lambda_{\star})}
and
t\geq\frac{1}{\eta\lambda_{\star}}
. If
u_{\min}(\eta)<s_{1}/2
,
then the error of gradient descent is lower bounded as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{C}^{\text{GD}}(\eta,t))\geq\frac{1}{4}(1-\exp(-32^{-1}))^{2}\lambda_{\star}\int^{s_{1}}_{2u_{\min}(\eta)}G^{\text{GD}}(s)d\widehat{H}(s),

where

is defined in Lemma
2
.

Given this, it is clear that an upper bound on
u_{\min}(\eta)
is sufficient, which then depends upon the stepsize and eigenvalue spectrum. We now present two propositions, which provide upper bounds when the eigenvalues decay exponentially or as a power law.

Proposition 9

Suppose
s_{i}=\exp(-\rho(i-1))
for
i=1,\dots,r
and
\rho>0
. If

\displaystyle\eta\leq\frac{\lambda_{\star}}{8e}\frac{e^{\rho}}{1+e^{\rho}}(1-e^{-\frac{1}{64}}),

(51)

then
u_{\min}(\eta)\leq 2e\lambda_{\star}\left(1+2e^{\rho}(1-e^{-\frac{1}{64}})^{-1}\right).

We now consider the case of eigenvalues decaying at a fast power law rate.

Proposition 10

Suppose
s_{i}=i^{-\alpha}
for
i=1,2,3,\dots,r
and
\alpha>1

\lambda_{\star}\leq 2^{-\alpha}

\displaystyle\eta\leq\frac{\lambda_{\star}^{1-1/\alpha}}{17\cdot 2^{\alpha}}\frac{\alpha-1}{1+\alpha}(1-e^{-\frac{1}{64}}).

(52)

Then

(64)

Define the function

In the interval

(66)

, with

achieving the maximum, and at least one inequality is strict. Suppose without loss of generality that

Then,

must be strictly positive (otherwise all terms must be equal, which is a contradiction).

This shows that if not all

are equal, then we can decrease

has a minimum that is attained. Since the minimum cannot be attained when any

are unequal, at the minimizing
