BD-KD: Balancing the Divergences for Online Knowledge Distillation
Ibtihel Amara, Nazanin Sepahvand, Brett H. Meyer, Warren J. Gross, James J. Clark

Abstract
Knowledge distillation (KD) has gained a lot of attention in the field of model compression for edge devices thanks to its effectiveness in compressing large powerful networks into smaller lower-capacity models. Online distillation, in which both the teacher and the student are learning collaboratively, has also gained much interest due to its ability to improve on the performance of the networks involved. The Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence ensures the proper knowledge transfer between the teacher and student. However, most online KD techniques present some bottlenecks under the network capacity gap. By cooperatively and simultaneously training, the models the KL distance becomes incapable of properly minimizing the teacher’s and student’s distributions. Alongside accuracy, critical edge device applications are in need of well-calibrated compact networks. Confidence calibration provides a sensible way of getting trustworthy predictions. We propose BD-KD B alancing of D ivergences for online K nowledge D istillation. We show that adaptively balancing between the reverse and forward divergences shifts the focus of the training strategy to the compact student network without limiting the teacher network’s learning process. We demonstrate that, by performing this balancing design at the level of the student distillation loss, we improve upon both performance accuracy and calibration of the compact student network. We conducted extensive experiments using a variety of network architectures and show improvements on multiple datasets including CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, Tiny-ImageNet, and ImageNet. We illustrate the effectiveness of our approach through comprehensive comparisons and ablations with current state-of-the-art online and offline KD techniques.



1
Introduction

Knowledge distillation (KD)
hinton2015distilling
;
bucilu2006model
is a model compression technique where a low capacity network, a.k.a student, trains to mimic the behaviour of a higher capacity network, a.k.a teacher. KD is a powerful strategy as it does not put any constraints on the architecture of either teacher or student network. Traditionally, the distillation process is done in an offline fashion
romero2014fitnets
;
zhao2022decoupled
;
li2017learning
;
tian2019contrastive
, in that the student network is trained using the labels provided by a pre-trained teacher network. In this scenario, the pre-trained teacher network serves as an expert that provides
soft
labels which are shown to be more informative than the original
hard
labels.

Figure 1
:

An overview of our proposed objective distillation function. We adopt an adaptive balancing of the KL divergences (forward and backward KL) at the student optimization level. This balancing is performed sample-wise based on the quality of uncertainty (i.e, entropy) of both the student and teacher networks. The loss of the teacher’s objective function is also re-designed to become a reverse KL distillation loss.

Despite their impressive success
tian2019contrastive
;
park2019relational
;
zhao2022decoupled
;
jin2019knowledge
, offline KD methods are known to have a few shortcomings. First of all, more accurate teachers are not necessarily better teachers
mirzadeh2020improved
. As reported by Mirzadeh and colleagues, the performance of the student starts to degrade when the
capacity gap
between the student and teacher gets large, even though the teacher’s performance continues to improve. One possible explanation for this behaviour is the fact that larger networks are known to be more miscalibrated
guo2017calibration
. They are often overconfident and less uncertain in their prediction. Overconfident networks are shown to be worst teachers
menon2021statistical
;
wang2022robust
. That could possibly be due to the fact that labels provided by such networks are less soft and therefore less informative
mirzadeh2020improved
. Secondly, offline training is a two-stage process that requires the training of the teacher first. This makes the training of the teacher independent of the student and therefore inflexible. Finally, the entire training process is inefficient and can be computationally expensive.

More recently, an alternative way of performing KD, called online distillation, is proposed where, unlike offline learning, both networks are trained mutually
dml
;
chung2020feature
;
twolevelonlinekd
. Most of the online methods are inspired by the Deep Mutual Learning (DML) model
dml
; an online distillation technique in which two networks are trained simultaneously while exploiting a feedback signal from each other via Kullback Leibler (KL) divergence loss. This feedback signal encourages both networks to learn from each other and stay close to each other and as a result leads to improved performance of both networks. However, most proposed online techniques utilize similar or equal capacity networks for training, which is not the ideal setting for model compression. A recent study by Qian et. al demonstrates that increasing the capacity gap between the two networks leads to the same problem observed in offline learning, which is the deterioration of the smaller network’s performance
switokd
. Their results suggest that although online learning can mitigate the problem it does not fully resolve it. The authors argue that the reason behind the poor performance of the student in these methods is the lack of focus on student training. None of the prior distillation methods make a concerted effort to reduce the
accuracy gap
during the
training
. As a result, the higher capacity network learns and converges faster and the KL signal from this network converges to cross entropy loss.

In this work, we propose an online KD method where the focus of the training is mostly on the student. In our method, not only do we encourage the student to reduce the
accuracy gap
, but also put emphasis on
distribution alignment
. Better matched logit distributions means increased networks’
fidelity
, which in turn results in better calibration as well as improved generalization
stanton2021does
. We achieve this by (1) replacing the forward KL term that has traditionally been used in the student objective function with a weighted symmetric KL loss and (2) adjusting the weight according to sample-wise softmax entropy values. In other words, the weighted symmetric KL loss is a weighted sum of a forward and a reverse term and the weight is calculated in a sample-wise manner based on the gap between the entropy of student and teacher’s softmax distribution for that sample. In summary, our main contribution are as follows:

•

We propose an online KD method with focus on student model training where the forward KL loss in the student objective function is replaced with a sample-wise weighted symmetric KL.

•

We demonstrate that balancing the KL term enhances the student’s performance via better output uncertainty estimation, which can be seen as a simple metric to measure networks’ fidelity.

•

We show that our method outperforms the state-of-the-art models, both online and offline, for four major computer vision datasets and across multiple architecture choices.

•

We illustrate that our network improve the student network calibration, compared to both online and offline methods. To the best of our knowledge we are the first to report the relationship between online distillation and model confidence calibration.

•

We demonstrate that our student performance indeed gets better in the presence of larger capacity gaps.

2
Related Work

Knowledge distillation (KD) is a simple and yet powerful technique for model compression
hinton2015distilling
. It has become an accessible tool, which facilitates the creation of compact student networks for deployment onto limited memory and computation edge devices. While distillation is traditionally performed offline, in recent years online methods have gained popularity for their improved performance and computational efficiency. Deep mutual learning
dml
is one of the first techniques to propose collaborative learning of two or more networks This technique has opened an opportunity for distillation methods to reduce the training process to a single one-time simultaneous training of teacher and student. Each network is minimizing an objective function which consists of the traditional Cross-Entropy (CE) loss and a forward KL loss which encourages the network to mimic the other network’s behaviour. Online Knowledge Distillation with Diverse Peers (OKDDip) is another online method proposed by Chen et al.
twolevelonlinekd
; a teacher-free two-level distillation method where the first-level happens among multiple students with identical architectures. The group knowledge of these diverse students is then transferred to the ”group leader” in the second level. In a recent work by Chung et. al.
chung2020feature
the distillation goes beyond class logit matching and is used to match the feature maps of two networks using an adversarial framework.

None of these online methods study the impact of a large capacity gap. Guo and colleagues were the first ones to show large gap harm the performance of the networks. To combat this phenomenon, they present Distillation Collaborative learning (KDCL)
kdcl
where a series of ensembling methods is employed to generate high-quality soft targets at once for distillation. Although, KDCL improves the performance of both networks, due to the nature of the loss the focus is mostly on the larger network and therefore the improvement is more enhanced for the teacher. The first work that introduces an online distillation model that attempts to close the accuracy gap during training is Switchable Knowledge distillation model (SWITOKD)
switokd
. This model mitigates the accuracy gap problem by slowing down the training of the teacher. The idea is to maximize networks’ fidelity by minimize the
distillation gap
, which is defined as the absolute error between teacher and student’s softmax. This is done by introducing an
expert phase
to the training process during which teacher’s training is temporally put on pause as an attempt to gives the student the opportunity to catch-up.

Although this method achieves the current state of the art for online KD techniques, it is not without its caveats. It prolongs the training process by slowing down the training of the teacher. In addition, switching between the two modes of learning is done in an ad-hoc fashion. In this paper, we propose a new online distillation method where, just like SwitOKD, the networks goal is to decrease distillation gap by increasing network’s fidelity to each other. Here we define fidelity as the similarity between softmax probability of student and teacher networks and measure it by calculating the entropy of the softmax. Unlike SwitOKD, our method does not hinder the teacher’s ability to learn nor does it prolong the training process. It mainly involves modification to the online KD objective functions where both forward and reverse KL divergence losses are employed to get a more accurate estimate of teacher’s uncertainty.

Method

3.1
Classic distillation divergence and its limitation

Hinton et al.
hinton2015distilling
introduce the vanilla knowledge distillation method where soft labels obtained from a pre-trained teacher is used to train a student network with lower capacity. The objective function consists of two terms (Eq.
1
); classic CE loss ,
L_{CE}
, that minimizes the distance between student’s prediction and hard labels and the KL divergence loss,
D_{KL}
, whose goal is to bring student’s logit closer to the one of teacher.

\begin{split}L_{KD}=\alpha L_{CE}+(1-\alpha)D_{KL}\\
L_{CE}=-\sum_{k=1}^{c}y_{k}\log\hat{y}_{k}^{t}\\
D_{KL}(\hat{y}_{k}^{t}||\hat{y}_{k}^{s})=\sum_{k=1}^{c}\hat{y}_{k}^{t}log(\frac{\hat{y}_{k}^{t}}{\hat{y}_{k}^{s}})\end{split}

(1)

D_{KL}

One major shortcoming of forward KL divergence is its potential tendency to overestimate the uncertainty of the original distribution. For the remaining of this section, we describe this problem in more details and propose a simple solution.

Given the asymmetric nature of
KL
divergence, there are two ways to measure the statistical distance between two a fixed distribution,
p
, and a random one denoted as
q

2
) and reverse KL (Eq.
4
).

D_{KL}(p||q_{\theta})=\sum_{x}p(x)log(\frac{p(x)}{q_{\theta}(x)})

(2)

Forward KL is known to exhibits
mean-seeking
behaviour. what that means is that minimizing the forward KL divergence forces
q(x)
to have high probability values wherever
p(x)
has high values (Eq.
3
). To achieve that,
q(x)
ends up covering all the modes which can come at the cost of over-estimating the uncertainty of the original distribution
p
.

\argmin_{\theta}D_{KL}(p(x)||q_{\theta}(x))=\\
\argmin_{\theta}E_{p(x)}[\log p(x)-\log q_{\theta}(x)]=\\
\argmax_{\theta}E_{p(x)}[\log q_{\theta}(x)]

(3)

An alternative to forward KL is reverse KL divergence expressed in Eq.
4
. Unlike forward KL, reverse KL is known to have
mode-seeking
property. To understand why, we break the reversed KL into two terms (see Eq.
5
); the first term on the right pushes
q(x)
to have high values where
p(x)
is high. There is, however, no penalty for missing a mode and as a result
q
might end up fitting to only one of the modes of
p
which can cause the uncertainty of
q
to be less than that of
p
.

D_{KL}(q_{\theta}||p)=\sum_{x}q_{\theta}(x)log(\frac{q_{\theta}(x)}{p(x)})

(4)

Fig.
2
illustrates how, for an identical
p

q

q
to assign non-zero values to all
x
s, reverse KL can lead to a
q
that only covers the largest mode in
p
and avoids others. It is therefore not far-fetch to assume that minimizing forward KL can result in distributions that are prone to have higher uncertainty than the original
p
and minimizing reverse KL can have the exact opposite effect.

\argmin_{\theta}D_{KL}(q_{\theta}(x)||p(x))=\\
\argmin_{\theta}E_{q_{\theta}(x)}[\log q_{\theta}(x)-\log p(x)]=\\
\argmax_{\theta}E_{q_{\theta}(x)}[\log p(x)]+H[q_{\theta}(x)]

(5)

Given the mentioned properties of forward and reverse KL and to ensure better alignment and higher fidelity between student and teacher softmax distributions, two major changes are made in the original KD loss: 1. instead using the conventional forward KL in the student’s objective function, we use a balanced symmetrical KL divergence and 2. reverse KL divergence is added to the original student objective function (see Eq.
6
and Eq.
7
).

3.2
Adaptive balancing of KL divergence for online KD

To further improve the performance of the student, instead of simply adding both forward and reverse KL to the objective function

6

L_{KD}^{t}=\alpha_{t}\sum_{i}L_{CE}(\hat{y}_{i}^{t},y_{i})+\tau^{2}\beta_{t}\sum_{i}D_{KL}(\hat{y}_{i,\tau}^{t}||\hat{y}_{i,\tau}^{s})

(6)

where
(x_{i},y_{i})

L_{KD}^{s}=\alpha_{s}\sum_{i}L_{CE}(\hat{y}_{i}^{s},y_{i})\\

where,

Unlike other online methods, the reverse KL terms used in our teacher objective function simply acts like a regularizer which in turn can help combat the overconfidence problem commonly observed in large over-parametrized network. Better calibrated teacher leads to better soft labels and therefore ensure better distillation performance.

If this error is negative, the student is underestimating the teacher’s uncertainty, then we use

Experiments

In this section, we perform a series of experiments to demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed Online distillation training BD-KD. We mainly compare our method to current state-of-the art approaches using various network architectures and on different classification datasets.

Dataset and network architectures.
We performed our experiments on four different datasets CIFAR10, CIFAR100
krizhevsky2009learning
, Tiny-ImageNet
le2015tiny
, and ImageNet
deng2009imagenet
. We use standard data augmentation such as Random crop and Random horizontal flip. We normalized all images channel-wise using the means and standard deviations.
We used state-of-the art convolution-based networks.

Baselines and SOTA comparisons.
Aside from the vanilla KD, we compare our method with recent works on online distillation such as DML
dml
, KDCL
kdcl
, and SwitOKD
switokd
. We also contarst our findings with current offline KD methods, such as CRD
tian2019contrastive
, DKD
zhao2022decoupled
, FitNet
romero2014fitnets
, etc.

Training Details.
We follow the training procedure of previous works
switokd
;
tian2019contrastive
and we report the performance on multiple datasets. We mainly use SGD optimizer with 0.9 momentum for all datasets. For CIFAR100 the total number of epochs is set to 240. The initial learning rate is set to 0.05 for all networks, and we perform a scheduler on the learning rate. The latter is divided by 10 every 150th, 180th, and 210th epochs.
For CIFAR-10, the total number of epochs is also set to 300, the initial learning rate is taken to be 0.01 for all networks. We divide the learning rate by 10 every 100th, 150th, and 200th epochs. As for Tiny-ImageNet, the total number of epochs is set to 150 epochs and the initial learning rate is at 0.01. We set the temperature
\tau
for KD to 2.
We evaluate the performance of the models using classification accuracy. As for the confidence calibration we evaluate our models using the expected calibration error (ECE)
guo2017calibration
.

Understanding the effects of temperature
T
on BD-KD.

We vary the value of the temperature
\tau
when training with our proposed method and report the performances in terms of accuracies of both teacher and student networks.
The temperature parameter
\tau
softens the output probability distribution ensuring that th confidence gap is balanced to be able to make the student network match the teacher’s confidence via distillation. We find that T=2 to be the optimal value for
\tau
. As we increase
\tau
, we observe a decrease of performance in both networks. This could be linked to the fact that higher temperatures contribute in maximizing the entropy gap between the teacher and the student, hence making it very difficult for the student to follow the teacher.

4.6
BD-KD with multiple networks

Similar to DML and SwitchOKD, BD-KD can be extended for multiple networks. Since the loss functions between the teacher and the student are different, we adopt the same multiple-teacher setting as SwitchOKD. For instance, in a three-network training, there are two approaches to training the student model: the one-teacher-two-student setting (1T2S) and the two-teacher-one-student setting (2T1S). As can be seen in Table
5
, we achieve a higher accuracy student across all methods using the 2T1S scenario, whereas the student for 1T2S is a little lower. The best explanation for this is that for 2T1S, the compact student learns an ensemble-like and diverse knowledge from multiple teachers. As for our teacher networks, our largest teacher with BD-KD achieve comparable results to the vanilla KD at 79.45% and KDCL at 80.71% but much higher than DML and SwitOKD 1T2S and 2T1S.

5
Conclusion

This paper provides a new viewpoint on training online distillation under the network capacity gap for model compression. By adaptively balancing the KL divergences in the student’s loss function, we are able to improve both student accuracy and calibration as compared to other online distillation techniques. We highlighted the effectiveness of our method throughout different extensive experiments. We showed that our proposed method does not need complicated training to achieve well-performing student and teacher models and that it achieves better-calibrated compact student networks.

More details on the adaptive weighting

Let’s assume
f_{s}
and
f_{t}
are the predictor that the student and the teacher networks learn respectively, where the teacher’s logit,
z_{i}^{t}
, for the
i

x_{i},y_{i}
), is:
{z_{i}}^{t}=f_{t}(x_{i},y_{i})
. Similarly the student’s logit is defined as
{z_{i}}^{s}=f_{s}(x_{i},y_{i})
. The output of the
two networks is categorical predictive distribution over the
c

p_{s}(y_{i}=j|x_{i})=\sigma(z_{i}^{s,\tau})={\exp({z_{i}^{s}}/{\tau})}/{\sum_{j=1}^{c}{\exp({z_{j}^{s}}/{\tau})}}
and
p_{t}(y_{i}=j|x_{i})=\sigma(z_{i}^{t,\tau})={\exp({z_{i}^{t}}/{\tau})}/{\sum_{j=1}^{c}{\exp({z_{j}^{t}}/{\tau})}}
. The per-sample entropy of the predictive distribution is calculated as follow:

\displaystyle H_{t}(x_{i},y_{i})=-\sum_{j=0}^{c}p_{t}(y_{i}=j|x_{i})\log(p_{t}(y_{i}=j|x_{i}))

\displaystyle H_{s}(x_{i},y_{i})=-\sum_{j=0}^{c}p_{s}(y_{i}=j|x_{i})\log(p_{s}(y_{i}=j|x_{i}))

these entropy values are used to weigh the symmetric KL loss:

\displaystyle\delta_{i}^{r}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ c l }1&\quad\textrm{if }{H_{s}(x_{i},y_{i})-H_{t}(x_{i},y_{i})}<0\\
v&\quad\textrm{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\delta_{i}^{f}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ c l }v&\quad\textrm{if }{H_{s}(x_{i},y_{i})-H_{t}(x_{i},y_{i})}<0\\
1&\quad\textrm{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

where
\delta_{i}^{f}
and
\delta_{i}^{r}
are respectively the per-sample weights of the forward and reserve KL loss in Eq.
7
and
v
is a hyper-parameter with values greater than one.

Appendix C
Expected Calibration Error and Reliability Curves

In order to evaluate the degree of mis-calibration of the student network we use Calibration Curves and Expected Calibration Error measures.

Calibration Curves.
To assess if a model is well calibrated, we often plot the
calibration curve
, also referred to as
reliability diagram

guo2017calibration
. The diagrams is one way of depicting model calibration where the expected accuracy of the network is plotted as a function of its confidence (i.e. softmax probabilities). A ”perfectly” calibrated model is represented by the identity function. A deviation above (under-confidence) or below (over-confident) the diagonal is an indication of a mis-calibrated model. To obtain this curve, first, we partition the confidence estimates into
M=10
equal interval bins
I_{m}
. Then, we proceed to calculate the accuracy of each bin as follows:

acc(B_{m})=\frac{1}{|{B_{m}}|}\sum_{i\in B_{m}}\mathbbm{1}(\hat{y}_{i}=y_{i})

where
\hat{y_{i}}
and
y_{i}
are the predicted and true class labels of sample
i
respectively, and
B_{m}
is the set of samples indices whose confidence falls into interval
m
.
Finally, we compute the average confidence within each bin:

B_{m}:conf(B_{m})=\frac{1}{|{B_{m}}|}\sum_{i\in B_{m}}\hat{p}_{i}

where
\hat{p}_{i}
is the confidence for sample
i
.

Expected Calibration Error.

In contrast to the calibration curves, which are visual diagrams capturing the degree of model calibration, the Expected Calibration Error (ECE) is a function that returns a scalar value measuring the degree of miscalibration of the model.
Similar to the calibration curves, confidence values are partitioned into
M=10
equal bins. ECE is defined as the weighted average of the difference between the accuracy and confidence at each bin:

ECE=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{|{B_{m}}|}{n}|{acc(B_{m})-conf(B_{m})}|
