Short time existence of a quasi-stationary fluid-structure interaction problem for plaque growth Thanks: Y. Liu is supported by the RTG 2339 “Interfaces, Complex Structures, and Singular Limits” of the German Science Foundation (DFG). The support is gratefully acknowledged.
Helmut Abels, Yadong Liu

Abstract
. We address a quasi-stationary fluid-structure interaction problem coupled with cell reactions and growth, which comes from the plaque formation during the stage of the atherosclerotic lesion in human arteries. The blood is modeled by the incompressible Navier–Stokes equation, while the motion of vessels is captured by a quasi-stationary equation of nonlinear elasticity. The growth happens when both cells in fluid and solid react, diffuse and transport across the interface, resulting in the accumulation of foam cells, which are exactly seen as the plaques. Via a fixed-point argument, we derive the local well-posedness of the nonlinear system, which is sustained by the analysis of decoupled linear systems.

1.2.
Previous work

Our main goal is to investigate the short time existence of strong solutions to (
1.1
)–(
1.16
). Before discussing the technical details, let us recall some literature related to our work.

In the case of 3d-3d fluid-structure interaction problem with a free interface, the strong solution results can be tracked back to Coutand–Shkoller
[
12

, who addressed the interaction problem between the incompressible Navier–Stokes equation and a linear Kirchhoff elastic material. The results were extended to fluid-elastodynamics case by them in
[
13
]
, where they regularized the hyperbolic elastic equation by a particular parabolic artificial viscosity and then obtained the existence of strong solutions by delicate a priori estimates. Thereafter, systems consisting of an incompressible Navier–Stokes equation and (damped) wave/Lamé equations were continuously investigated in e.g.
[
20
,
21
,
23
,
34
]
with further developments.
Besides the models above, one refers to
[
8
]
for a compressible barotropic fluid coupled with elastic bodies and to
[
37
]
for the magnetohydrodynamics (MHD)-structure interaction system, where the fluid is described by the incompressible viscous non-resistive MHD equation and the structure is modeled by the wave equation of a superconductor material.

In the context of 3d-2d/2d-1d models, various kinds of models and results were established during the last twenty years. The widely focused case is the fluid-beam/plate systems where the beam/plate equations were imposed with different mechanical mechanism (rigidity, stretching, friction, rotation, etc.), readers are refer to e.g.
[
9
,
16
,
31
,
42
]
for weak solution results and to e.g.
[
5
,
14
,
17
,
25
,
28
,
30
]
for strong solutions respectively. Besides, the fluid-shells interaction problems were studied as well in e.g.
[
6
,
24
]
for weak solutions and in e.g.
[
10
,
11
,
27
]
for strong solutions. It is worth mentioning that in recent works
[
14
,
28
]
, a maximal regularity framework, which requires lower initial regularity and less compatibility conditions compared to the energy method, was employed.

Recently inspired by
[
43
]
, the authors considered the local well-posedness of a fluid-structure interaction problem for plaque growth in a smooth domain
[
2
]
and a cylindrical domain
[
3
]
respectively. By the maximal regularity theory, one obtains the well-posedness of linearized systems and then derives the existence of a unique strong solution to the nonlinear system via a fixed-point argument. However, concerning the origin of the model, which consists of blood flow and blood vessels, it is reasonable that the movement of vessels is supposed to be far slower than the blood, namely, the time scales of motions of the fluid and the solid are distinguished and the kinetic energy of the vessel is neglected. Thus, in this paper, we take an incompressible quasi-stationary hyperelastic equation into account, i.e., for every time
t
the elastic stresses are in equilibrium. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first result concerning on the strong solutions to a quasi-stationary fluid-structure interaction problem with growth.

1.3.
Technical discussions

Under the above setting, the fluid-structure part is of parabolic-elliptic type, while the cells part is similar to the ones in
[
2
,
3
]
. To solve the nonlinear problem, our basic strategy is still a fixed-point argument in the framework of maximal regularity theory, while more issues come up when we consider the linearized systems. More precisely, for the linearization of the fluid-structure part, it is hard to solve it directly by the maximal regularity theory due to the parabolic-elliptic type coupling, which results in the unmatched regularity between
\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{f}
and
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{s}
on the sharp interface
\Gamma
. To overcome the problem, one tries to decouple the system to a nonstationary Stokes equation with respect to fluid velocity
\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{s}
and a quasi-stationary Stokes-type equation with regard to the solid displacement
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{s}
. Note that one key point is to separate the kinetic and dynamic condition on the interface correctly. Specifically, we impose the Neumann boundary condition for
\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{s}
and a Dirichlet boundary condition for
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{s}
, see Section
4
below. Otherwise if
\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{f}|_{\Gamma}=\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{s}
, one may face the problem that there is no regularity information about the velocity of solid
\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{s}=\partial_{t}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{s}
, since the solid equation is quasi-stationary without any damping.

Another issue is the choice of function spaces for the elastic equation, i.e., how to assemble suitable function spaces for the data in the linearized solid equation so that the regularity of
\hat{\nabla}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{s}
matches with
\hat{\nabla}\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{s}
of fluid on the interface in the nonlinear system. Our choice is

\mathbf{f}\in H^{1/2}_{q}(0,T;W_{q,\Gamma}^{-1}(\Omega_{s})^{3})\cap L^{q}(0,T;L^{q}(\Omega_{s})^{3}).

This space is motivated by the observation that the nonstationary Stokes equation (
4.1
) is uniquely solvable if the Neumann boundary data

\mathbf{h}\in W^{1/2-1/2q}_{q}(0,T;L^{q}(\Gamma)^{3})\cap L^{q}(0,T;W^{1-1/q}_{q}(\Gamma)^{3})

holds, which implies that

D^{2}W(\mathbb{I})\hat{\nabla}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{s}\in W^{1/2-1/2q}_{q}(0,T;L^{q}(\Gamma)^{3})\cap L^{q}(0,T;W^{1-1/q}_{q}(\Gamma)^{3})

as well. Here
H^{s}_{q}
and
W^{s}_{q}
are Bessel potential space and Sobolev–Slobodeckij space respectively, which will be given later in Section
2.2
. In fact, the anisotropic Bessel potential space we assigned is a sharp regularity if one goes back to the anisotropic trace operator, see Lemma
2.7
below, and it is natural to equip
\mathbf{f}
with the regularity above. Because of this sharp regularity setting, one can not expect the Lipschitz estimates of nonlinear terms only with small time, which gives birth to an additional smallness assumption (
3.7
) on the initial solid displacement and pressure. Detailed discussion can be found in Remark
3.2
and Proposition
5.7
later.

To solve the quasi-stationary (linearized) elastic equation, one treated it as a Stokes-type problem with respect to the displacement
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{s}
and the pressure
\hat{\pi}_{s}
, due to the incompressibility. However, as we assigned the certain regularity space for it as above and the Stokes operator is not a standard one (namely,
\Div(D^{2}W(\mathbb{I})\nabla\cdot)
), one needs to consider a generalized stationary Stokes equation with
\mathbf{f}
in
L^{q}
and
W_{q,\Gamma_{1}}^{-1}
respectively, for which the maximal regularity of analytic C
0
semigroups is applied, as well as a complex interpolation method with a
very weak solution
in
L^{q}
of a mixed-boundary Stokes-type equation, which can be solved by a duality argument.

For the cells part there is a problem of the positivity for the concentrations, compared to
[
2
]
. The idea in
[
2
]
to prove it is to apply the maximum principle to the original equation and deduce a contradiction with the help of Hopf’s Lemma. However, due to the lack of regularity of
\mathbf{v}_{s}=\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}_{s}
, one can not expect it to be Hölder continuous in space-time, even continuous. To deal with this trouble, we make use of the idea of mollification, i.e., approximating
\mathbf{v}_{s}
by sufficient smooth functions
\mathbf{v}_{s}^{\epsilon}
such that
\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{v}_{s}^{\epsilon}\rightarrow\mathbf{u}_{s}
in certain space. Then arguing by a similar procedure in
[
2
]
, we obtain an approximate nonnegative solution
c^{\epsilon}
. Finally one can show that it converges to a nonnegative function
c
, which exactly satisfies the original equations of cell concentrations.

W_{q,\Gamma}^{m}(M)=\left\{\psi\in W^{m}_{q}(M):\psi|_{\Gamma}=0\right\},\quad W_{q,\Gamma}^{-m}(M):=[W_{q^{\prime},\Gamma}^{m}(M)]^{\prime},

The vector-valued variants are denoted by

and
W^{m}_{q}(M;X)
, where

is a Banach space. In particular,
L^{q}(M)=W^{0}_{q}(M)
,

.

Moreover, for

and

the standard Besov space

and Bessel potential space

coincide with the real and complex interpolation of Sobolev spaces respectively (see e.g. Lunardi

B^{s}_{q,p}(M)=\left(W^{k}_{q}(M),W^{m}_{q}(M)\right)_{\theta,p},\quad H^{s}_{q}(M)=\left[W^{k}_{q}(M),W^{m}_{q}(M)\right]_{\theta},

where
s=(1-\theta)k+\theta m
,
\theta\in(0,1)
,
k,m\in\mathbb{N}
,
k<m
. In particular,
W^{l}_{q}=H^{l}_{q}
for

and setting

above yields the Sobolev–Slobodeckij space

W^{s}_{q}(M)=B^{s}_{q,q}(M)=\left(W^{k}_{q}(M),W^{m}_{q}(M)\right)_{\theta,q},\text{ if }s\notin\mathbb{N}.

Then we define the seminorm of

as

\left[f\right]_{W^{s}_{q}(M)}=\left(\int_{M\times M}\frac{\left|f(x)-f(y)\right|^{q}}{\left|x-y\right|^{n+sq}}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\right)^{\frac{1}{q}},

for

with
0<s<1
,
1<q<\infty
.

For an interval

,
0<s<1
,

,

with norm

\left\|f\right\|_{K^{s}_{q}(I;X)}=\left(\left\|f\right\|_{L^{q}(I;X)}^{q}+\left[f\right]_{K^{s}_{q}(I;X)}^{q}\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}.

For convenience, with
T>0
we define the corresponding space with vanishing initial trace at

as

{{}_{0}K}^{s}_{q}(0,T;X)=\left\{f\in K^{s}_{q}(0,T;X):\left.f\right|_{t=0}=0\right\}\text{ for }s>\frac{1}{q}.

Now we recall several embedding results and properties for Banach space-valued spaces that will be frequently used later.

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose
0<r<s\leq 1
and
1\leq q<\infty
.
X
is a Banach space and
I=(0,T)\subset\mathbb{R}
is a bounded interval for

. Then
K^{s}_{q}(I;X)\hookrightarrow W^{r}_{q}(I;X)
and for some
\delta>0

\left[f\right]_{W^{r}_{q}(I;X)}\leq\left|I\right|^{\delta}\left[f\right]_{K^{s}_{q}(I;X)}\text{ for all }f\in K^{s}_{q}(I;X).

In particular, we have

W^{1}_{q}(I;X)\hookrightarrow W^{\theta}_{q}(I;X)\ \text{for all }0<\theta<1

and

\left[f\right]_{W^{\theta}_{q}(I;X)}\leq T^{1-\theta}\left\|\partial_{t}f\right\|_{L^{q}(I;X)}\text{ for all }f\in W^{1}_{q}(I;X).

Proof.

The case
K=W
was shown in Simon
[
39
, Corollary 17]
. Then taking

satisfying
r<t<s
, one has

\left[f\right]_{W^{r}_{q}(I;X)}\leq\left|I\right|^{t-r}\left[f\right]_{W^{t}_{q}(I;X)}\leq C\left|I\right|^{t-r}\left[f\right]_{K^{s}_{q}(I;X)},

where the last inequality holds in virtue of
H^{r}_{q}(I;X)\hookrightarrow W^{t}_{q}(I;X)
for

. The second assertion can be easily derived by means of the observation

f(t)-f(t-h)=h\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{t}f(t+(\tau-1)h)\mathrm{d}\tau

and the definition of Sobolev–Slobodeckij space, so we omit it here.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Let

,

satisfying

with

Remark 3.5

(Discussions on the linearization)
.

(1)

The linearization can be derived as follows. Let

. Then
h(0)=DW(\mathbb{I}),h(1)=DW(\mathbf{F})
.
Since

it follows from
(H3)
that

DW(\mathbf{F})=D^{2}W(\mathbb{I})(\mathbf{F}-\mathbb{I})+\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{F}),

where

\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{F}):=\int_{0}^{1}D^{3}W((1-s)\mathbb{I}+s\mathbf{F})(1-s)\,\mathrm{d}s(\mathbf{F}-\mathbb{I})(\mathbf{F}-\mathbb{I}).

(2)

The linearization is similar to the one in
[
2
]
, but with several modifications, one of which is deduced above. It is possible to have other kinds of linearizations but we remark that in the present paper a divergence structure of
\hdiv\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{s}
plays an essential role when we prove the linear theory and estimate it in a particular function space, see Corollary
4.4
and Proposition
5.7
later. Moreover, for the solid mass balance equation (
1.6
) (equivalently

since the density is constant), we integrate it over
(0,t)
as (
3.9e
) to keep the Stokes-type structure for the elastic equation with respect to the displacement
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{s}
.

(3)

Noticing that the continuity conditions (
3.5f
) on the interface are separated to (
3.9c
) and (
3.9f
) formally after the linearization, we remark here that this is for the sake of analysis due to the mismatch of the regularity on

. For instant, if one replaces (

) with the boundary condition

, it has no chance to solve the fluid part since we have no first-order temporal derivative information for the solid displacement

Analogously to

\mathcal{A}_{q}(\mathbf{u}):=\mathbb{P}_{q}\big(-\Div(D^{2}W(\mathbb{I})\nabla\mathbf{u})\big)\text{ for all }\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{q}),

with

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{q})=\left\{\mathbf{u}\in W^{2}_{q}(\Omega)^{3}\cap L^{q}_{\sigma}(\Omega):\left.\mathbf{u}\right|_{\Gamma_{1}}=0,\ \left.\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{n}}((D^{2}W(\mathbb{I})\nabla\mathbf{u})\mathbf{n})\right|_{\Gamma_{2}}=0\right\},

where
\mathbb{P}_{q}
denotes the
Helmholtz–Weyl projection
onto
L^{q}_{\sigma}(\Omega)
, see e.g.
[
1
, Appendix A]
for the existence of the projection with mixed boundary conditions.
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{n}}:=\mathbb{I}-\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}
is the tangential projection onto
\partial\Omega
. By the result of
s=0
we see that

\mathcal{A}_{q}:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{q})\rightarrow L^{q}_{\sigma}(\Omega)

is well-defined and bijective. Then one knows that its dual operator

\mathcal{A}_{q^{\prime}}^{*}:L^{q^{\prime}}_{\sigma}(\Omega)^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{q^{\prime}})^{\prime}

is bijective as well, which gives rise to the very weak solution. Note that
\mathcal{A}_{q}
and
\mathcal{A}_{q^{\prime}}^{*}
are consistent, namely,

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathcal{A}_{q^{\prime}}^{*}\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{q^{\prime}})^{\prime}\times\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{q^{\prime}})}=\left\langle\mathbf{u},\mathcal{A}_{q^{\prime}}\mathbf{w}\right\rangle_{L^{q}_{\sigma}(\Omega)\times L^{q^{\prime}}_{\sigma}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\nabla\mathbf{u}:D^{2}W(\mathbb{I})\nabla\mathbf{w}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}D^{2}W(\mathbb{I})\nabla\mathbf{u}:\nabla\mathbf{w}\,\mathrm{d}x=\left\langle\mathcal{A}_{q}\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w}\right\rangle_{L^{q}_{\sigma}(\Omega)\times L^{q^{\prime}}_{\sigma}(\Omega)},

for
\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{q})\subseteq L^{q}_{\sigma}(\Omega)
,
\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{q^{\prime}})\subseteq L^{q^{\prime}}_{\sigma}(\Omega)
, where
(D^{2}W(\mathbb{I}))_{ij}^{kl}=(D^{2}W(\mathbb{I}))_{kl}^{ij}
,
i,j,k,l=1,2,3
.
Then by the complex interpolation of operators, e.g.
[
38
, Theorem 2.6]
, we record that

\mathcal{A}_{q}:\big(L^{q}_{\sigma}(\Omega),\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{q})\big)_{\left[\frac{1}{2}\right]}\rightarrow\big(L^{q}_{\sigma}(\Omega),\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{q^{\prime}})^{\prime}\big)_{\left[\frac{1}{2}\right]}

is bijective. Since
\mathcal{A}_{q}
admits a bounded
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}
-calculus and has bounded imaginary powers, see e.g.
[
32
, Theorem 1.1]
, complex interpolation methods can be used to describe domains of fractional power operators. By virtue of
[
32
, Theorem 1.1]
and
[
38
, Theorem 2.6]
, one obtains

\displaystyle\big(L^{q}_{\sigma}(\Omega),\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{q})\big)_{[\frac{1}{2}]}=\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{q}^{1/2})=W_{\sigma,\Gamma_{1}}^{1,q}(\Omega),

\displaystyle\big(L^{q}_{\sigma}(\Omega),\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{q^{\prime}})^{\prime}\big)_{\left[\frac{1}{2}\right]}=(L^{q}_{\sigma}(\Omega)^{\prime},\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{q^{\prime}}))_{\left[\frac{1}{2}\right]}^{\prime}=\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{q^{\prime}}^{1/2})^{\prime}=W_{\sigma,\Gamma_{1}}^{-1,q}(\Omega).

Consequently,

\mathcal{A}_{q}:W_{\sigma,\Gamma_{1}}^{1,q}(\Omega)\rightarrow W_{\sigma,\Gamma_{1}}^{-1,q}(\Omega)

is bijective, which implies there exists a unique solution
\mathbf{u}\in W_{\sigma,\Gamma_{1}}^{1,q}(\Omega)
such that

\left\langle\mathcal{A}_{q}\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w}\right\rangle=\left\langle\mathcal{F},\mathbf{w}\right\rangle\text{ for all }\mathbf{w}\in W_{\sigma,\Gamma_{1}}^{1,q^{\prime}}(\Omega),

with
\mathcal{F}\in W_{\sigma,\Gamma_{1}}^{-1,q}(\Omega)
defined by

\left\langle\mathcal{F},\mathbf{w}\right\rangle:=\left\langle\mathbf{f},\mathbf{w}\right\rangle_{W_{q,\Gamma_{1}}^{-1}(\Omega)\times W_{q^{\prime},\Gamma_{1}}^{1}(\Omega)}+\left\langle\mathbf{h}^{2},\left.\mathbf{w}\right|_{\Gamma_{2}}\right\rangle_{W^{-\frac{1}{q}}_{q}(\Gamma_{2})\times W_{q^{\prime}}^{1-\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}}(\Gamma_{2})},

for all
\mathbf{w}\in W_{\sigma,\Gamma_{1}}^{1,q^{\prime}}(\Omega)
. Moreover, by means of the open mapping theorem, one immediately deduce the estimate

\left\|\mathbf{u}\right\|_{W_{\sigma,\Gamma_{1}}^{1,q}(\Omega)^{3}}\leq C\left|\kern-1.29167pt\left|\kern-1.29167pt\left|\mathcal{F}\right|\kern-1.29167pt\right|\kern-1.29167pt\right|_{-1},

in which

\left|\kern-1.29167pt\left|\kern-1.29167pt\left|\mathcal{F}\right|\kern-1.29167pt\right|\kern-1.29167pt\right|_{-1}:=\sup_{\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|_{W_{q^{\prime},\Gamma_{1}}^{1}(\Omega)^{3}}=1}\Big(\left\langle\mathbf{f},\mathbf{w}\right\rangle_{W_{q,\Gamma_{1}}^{-1}(\Omega)^{3}\times W_{q^{\prime},\Gamma_{1}}^{1}(\Omega)^{3}}+\left\langle\mathbf{h}^{2},\left.\mathbf{w}\right|_{\Gamma_{2}}\right\rangle_{W^{-\frac{1}{q}}_{q}(\Gamma_{2})^{3}\times W_{q^{\prime}}^{1-\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}}(\Gamma_{2})^{3}}\Big).

Up to now, one still needs to recover the pressure in the very weak sense, i.e., solving

(4.3)

\int_{\Omega}\pi\Delta\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x=\left\langle F,\varphi\right\rangle,\quad\forall\,\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(\Delta_{q^{\prime},DN}),

where

\displaystyle\left\langle F,\varphi\right\rangle:=-\left\langle\mathbf{f},\nabla\varphi\right\rangle+\int_{\Omega}D^{2}W(\mathbb{I})\nabla\mathbf{u}:\nabla^{2}\varphi+\left\langle\mathbf{h}^{2}\cdot\mathbf{n},\left.\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\varphi\right|_{\Gamma_{2}}\right\rangle,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\Delta_{q^{\prime},DN}):=\left\{\psi\in W_{q^{\prime}}^{2}(\Omega):\left.\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\psi\right|_{\Gamma_{1}}=0,\ \left.\psi\right|_{\Gamma_{2}}=0\right\}.

Since
\mathbf{f}\in W_{q,\Gamma_{1}}^{-1}(\Omega)^{3}
,
\mathbf{u}\in W_{q,\Gamma_{1}}^{1}(\Omega)^{3}
and
\mathbf{h}^{2}\in W^{-1/q}_{q}(\Gamma_{2})^{3}
, it is easy to verify that functional
F
defined above is well-defined in
\mathcal{D}(\Delta_{q^{\prime},DN})^{\prime}
. For every
u\in L^{q^{\prime}}(\Omega)
, it follows from
[
33
, Corollary 7.4.5]
that there exists a unique solution
\varphi(u)\in\mathcal{D}(\Delta_{q^{\prime},DN})
satisfying
\Delta\varphi=u
. Now we define
\pi\in L^{q}(\Omega)
by duality as a linear functional on
L^{q^{\prime}}(\Omega)
acting for every
u
as

(4.4)

\left\langle\pi,u\right\rangle=\left\langle F,\varphi\right\rangle.

Indeed
\pi
is the very weak solution we are looking for, since for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(\Delta_{q^{\prime},DN})
, we have

\left\langle\pi,\Delta\varphi\right\rangle=\left\langle\pi,u\right\rangle=\left\langle F,\varphi\right\rangle.

The uniqueness can be showed by letting
F=0
in (
4.4
) so that for all
u\in L^{q^{\prime}}(\Omega)
,
\left\langle\pi,u\right\rangle=0
, which implies
\pi=0
a.e. in
\Omega
. Then we have the estimate

\left\|\pi\right\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}\leq C\left\|F\right\|_{\mathcal{D}(\Delta_{q^{\prime},DN})^{\prime}}\leq C\Big(\left\|\mathbf{u}\right\|_{W^{1}_{q}(\Omega)^{3}}+\left|\kern-1.29167pt\left|\kern-1.29167pt\left|\mathcal{F}\right|\kern-1.29167pt\right|\kern-1.29167pt\right|_{-1}\Big).

This completes the proof.
∎

In Theorem
4.3
, we consider the general case of data. In fact, for applications the right-hand side terms sometimes have special structure, which is of much help to derive the estimate in a concise form.

5.1.
Auxiliary lemmas

In this section, we give some lemmas which we shall use later on.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
f,g\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}_{q}(\mathbb{R};L^{q}(\Omega))\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};L^{\infty}(\Omega))\cap W^{\alpha}_{2q}(\mathbb{R};L^{2q}(\Omega))
with
q>1
and
1/4<\alpha<1/2
, then
fg\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}_{q}(\mathbb{R};L^{q}(\Omega))
and

\displaystyle\left\|fg\right\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2}}_{q}(\mathbb{R};L^{q}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\left\|f\right\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2}}_{q}(\mathbb{R};L^{q}(\Omega))}\left\|g\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};L^{\infty}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\quad+\left\|g\right\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2}}_{q}(\mathbb{R};L^{q}(\Omega))}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};L^{\infty}(\Omega))}+\left\|f\right\|_{W^{\alpha}_{2q}(\mathbb{R};L^{2q}(\Omega))}\left\|g\right\|_{W^{\alpha}_{2q}(\mathbb{R};L^{2q}(\Omega))}\Big).

Moreover, if additionally
f|_{t=0}=g|_{t=0}=0
, the assertion is true as well for all
\mathbb{R}
substituted by an interval
(0,T)
and the constant
C>0
in the estimate is independent of
T>0
.

Proof.

First let us recall the equivalent definition of Bessel potential space

\|f\|_{H^{s}_{q}(\mathbb{R};L^{q}(\Omega))}=\|f\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R};L^{q}(\Omega))}+\|(-\Delta)^{\frac{s}{2}}f\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R};L^{q}(\Omega))},

where the fractional Laplace operator is represented by the singular integral

(-\Delta)^{\frac{s}{2}}f(t)=C_{s}\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{\left|h\right|\geq\epsilon}\frac{\Delta_{h}f(t)}{\left|h\right|^{1+s}}\mathrm{d}h,

with
C_{s}>0
a constant depending on
s
,
0<s<2
and
\Delta_{h}f(t):=f(t)-f(t-h)
, see e.g.
[
40
, Chapter V, Section 6.10]
.
For
f,g\in H^{1/2}_{q}(\mathbb{R};L^{q}(\Omega))\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};L^{\infty}(\Omega))\cap W^{\alpha}_{2q}(\mathbb{R};L^{2q}(\Omega))
with
q>1
and
1/4<\alpha<1/2
, we see that the integrand has an algebraic decay rate greater than one with respect to
\left|h\right|
, which means the singular integral is actually integrable and one can omit the “
\lim
” in the following. Then we have

\displaystyle\left\|(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}(fg)(t)\right\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}=C\bigg\|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\Delta_{h}(fg)(t)}{\left|h\right|^{1+\frac{1}{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}h\bigg\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=C\bigg\|g(t)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\Delta_{h}f(t)}{\left|h\right|^{1+\frac{1}{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}h+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f(t)\Delta_{h}g(t)}{\left|h\right|^{1+\frac{1}{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}h+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\big(\Delta_{h}f(t)\big)\big(\Delta_{h}g(t)\big)}{\left|h\right|^{1+\frac{1}{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}h\bigg\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\underbrace{\left(\left\|g(t)(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}f(t)\right\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}+\left\|f(t)(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}g(t)\right\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}\right)}_{\leq\left\|g(t)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega))}\|(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}f(t)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}+\left\|f(t)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega))}\|(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}g(t)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+C\underbrace{\bigg\|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\big(\Delta_{h}f(t)\big)\big(\Delta_{h}g(t)\big)}{\left|h\right|^{1+\frac{1}{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}h\bigg\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}}_{=:I(t)}.

Dividing the region
\mathbb{R}
into a neighborhood of the origin and its complement, we have

\displaystyle I(t)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\left|h\right|\leq 1}\left\|\big(\Delta_{h}f(t)\big)\big(\Delta_{h}g(t)\big)\right\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}\frac{1}{\left|h\right|^{1+\frac{1}{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}h

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{\left|h\right|>1}\left\|\big(\Delta_{h}f(t)\big)\big(\Delta_{h}g(t)\big)\right\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}\frac{1}{\left|h\right|^{1+\frac{1}{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}h=:I_{1}(t)+I_{2}(t),

where

\displaystyle I_{1}(t)

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\left|h\right|\leq 1}\left\|\Delta_{h}f(t)\right\|_{L^{2q}(\Omega)}\left\|\Delta_{h}g(t)\right\|_{L^{2q}(\Omega)}\frac{1}{\left|h\right|^{1+\frac{1}{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}h

\displaystyle=C\int_{\left|h\right|\leq 1}\left\|\Delta_{h}f(t)\right\|_{L^{2q}(\Omega)}\left\|\Delta_{h}g(t)\right\|_{L^{2q}(\Omega)}\left(\frac{1}{\left|h\right|^{1+\frac{q}{2}+\varepsilon\frac{q}{q^{\prime}}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}\left(\frac{1}{\left|h\right|^{1-\varepsilon}}\right)^{\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}}\,\mathrm{d}h

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\int_{\left|h\right|\leq 1}\left\|\Delta_{h}f(t)\right\|_{L^{2q}(\Omega)}^{q}\left\|\Delta_{h}g(t)\right\|_{L^{2q}(\Omega)}^{q}\frac{\mathrm{d}h}{\left|h\right|^{1+\frac{q}{2}+\varepsilon\frac{q}{q^{\prime}}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}\underbrace{\left(\int_{\left|h\right|\leq 1}\left|h\right|^{-1+\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}h\right)^{\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}}}_{\leq C_{\varepsilon}},

for every
\varepsilon>0
and
1/q+1/q^{\prime}=1
. By the Hölder’s inequality,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|I_{1}(t)\right|^{q}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq C_{\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\left|h\right|\leq 1}\left\|\Delta_{h}f(t)\right\|_{L^{2q}(\Omega)}^{q}\left\|\Delta_{h}g(t)\right\|_{L^{2q}(\Omega)}^{q}\frac{\mathrm{d}h\mathrm{d}t}{\left|h\right|^{1+\frac{q}{2}+\varepsilon\frac{q}{q^{\prime}}}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\varepsilon}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\left|h\right|\leq 1}\frac{\left\|\Delta_{h}f(t)\right\|_{L^{2q}(\Omega)}^{2q}}{\left|h\right|^{1+\frac{q}{2}+\varepsilon\frac{q}{q^{\prime}}}}\,\mathrm{d}h\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\left|h\right|\leq 1}\frac{\left\|\Delta_{h}g(t)\right\|_{L^{2q}(\Omega)}^{2q}}{\left|h\right|^{1+\frac{q}{2}+\varepsilon\frac{q}{q^{\prime}}}}\,\mathrm{d}h\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\varepsilon}\left\|f\right\|_{W^{\frac{1}{4}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2q^{\prime}}}_{2q}(\mathbb{R};L^{2q}(\Omega))}^{q}\left\|g\right\|_{W^{\frac{1}{4}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2q^{\prime}}}_{2q}(\mathbb{R};L^{2q}(\Omega))}^{q}.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|I_{2}(t)\right|^{q}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\|f(t)\right\|_{L^{2q}(\Omega)}^{q}\left\|g(t)\right\|_{L^{2q}(\Omega)}^{q}\left(\int_{\left|h\right|>1}\frac{1}{\left|h\right|^{1+\frac{1}{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}h\right)^{q}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\|f(t)\right\|_{L^{2q}(\Omega)}^{q}\left\|g(t)\right\|_{L^{2q}(\Omega)}^{q}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2q}(\mathbb{R};L^{2q}(\Omega))}^{q}\left\|g\right\|_{L^{2q}(\mathbb{R};L^{2q}(\Omega))}^{q}.

Combining the estimate

\left\|fg\right\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R};L^{q}(\Omega))}\leq\left\|f\right\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R};L^{q}(\Omega))}\left\|g\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega))}\leq C\left\|f\right\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2}}_{q}(\mathbb{R};L^{q}(\Omega))}\left\|g\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};L^{\infty}(\Omega))},

we conclude that for
1/4<\alpha<1/2
,

\displaystyle\left\|fg\right\|_{H^{1/2}_{q}(\mathbb{R};L^{q}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\left\|f\right\|_{H^{1/2}_{q}(\mathbb{R};L^{q}(\Omega))}\left\|g\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};L^{\infty}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\quad+\left\|g\right\|_{H^{1/2}_{q}(\mathbb{R};L^{q}(\Omega))}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};L^{\infty}(\Omega))}+\left\|f\right\|_{W^{\alpha}_{2q}(\mathbb{R};L^{2q}(\Omega))}\left\|g\right\|_{W^{\alpha}_{2q}(\mathbb{R};L^{2q}(\Omega))}\Big).

Now denoting by
\mathcal{E}
the extension operator for Bessel potential spaces with vanishing initial value from
[
29
, Lemma 2.5]
, one arrives at

\displaystyle\left\|fg\right\|_{{{}_{0}H}^{\frac{1}{2}}_{q}(0,T;L^{q}(\Omega))}=\left\|\mathcal{E}(f)\mathcal{E}(g)\right\|_{{{}_{0}H}^{\frac{1}{2}}_{q}(0,T;L^{q}(\Omega))}\leq C\left\|\mathcal{E}(f)\mathcal{E}(g)\right\|_{{{}_{0}H}^{\frac{1}{2}}_{q}(\mathbb{R};L^{q}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\left\|\mathcal{E}(f)\right\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2}}_{q}(\mathbb{R};L^{q}(\Omega))}\left\|\mathcal{E}(g)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};L^{\infty}(\Omega))}+\left\|\mathcal{E}(g)\right\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2}}_{q}(\mathbb{R};L^{q}(\Omega))}\left\|\mathcal{E}(f)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};L^{\infty}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\quad\qquad+\left\|\mathcal{E}(f)\right\|_{W^{\alpha}_{2q}(\mathbb{R};L^{2q}(\Omega))}\left\|\mathcal{E}(g)\right\|_{W^{\alpha}_{2q}(\mathbb{R};L^{2q}(\Omega))}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\left\|f\right\|_{{{}_{0}H}^{\frac{1}{2}}_{q}(0,T;L^{q}(\Omega))}\left\|g\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\quad\qquad+\left\|g\right\|_{{{}_{0}H}^{\frac{1}{2}}_{q}(0,T;L^{q}(\Omega))}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega))}+\left\|f\right\|_{W^{\alpha}_{2q}(0,T;L^{2q}(\Omega))}\left\|g\right\|_{W^{\alpha}_{2q}(0,T;L^{2q}(\Omega))}\Big),

where the constant
C>0
is independent of
T
.
∎

Lemma 5.2

Appendix A
Stokes resolvent problem

In this section, we give a short proof the solvability of the following Stokes resolvent problem with mixed boundary conditions. Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a bounded domain of class
C^{3-}
with boundary
\partial\Omega=\Gamma_{1}\cup\Gamma_{2}
consisting of two closed, disjoint, nonempty components. Consider the resolvent problem

(A.1)

\displaystyle\lambda\mathbf{u}-\Div(D^{2}W(\mathbb{I})\nabla\mathbf{u})+\nabla\pi

\displaystyle=\mathbf{f},

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

\displaystyle\Div\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma_{1},

\displaystyle(D^{2}W(\mathbb{I})\nabla\mathbf{u}-\pi\mathbb{I})\mathbf{n}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma_{2},

where
\mathbf{n}
is the outer unit normal on the boundary,
W:\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a scalar function with Assumption
H
holding.

Theorem A.1
.

Let
1<q<\infty
. Assume that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the domain defined above.
Given
\mathbf{f}\in L^{q}(\Omega)^{3}
, there exists some
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
such that for all
\lambda>\lambda_{0}
, (
A.1
) admits a unique solution
(\mathbf{u},\pi)
satisfying

\mathbf{u}\in W^{2}_{q}(\Omega)^{3},\quad\pi\in W^{1}_{q}(\Omega).

Moreover,

\lambda\left\|\mathbf{u}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{3}}+\left\|\mathbf{u}\right\|_{W^{2}_{q}(\Omega)^{3}}+\left\|\pi\right\|_{W^{1}_{q}(\Omega)}\leq C\left\|\mathbf{f}\right\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)^{3}}.

Proof.

The proof is based on the maximal regularity of a generalized Stokes equation, see e.g. Bothe–Prüss
[
7
, Theorem 4.1]
, Prüss–Simonett
[
33
, Theorem 7.3.1]
. Let us recall the definition of solenoidal space

L^{q}_{\sigma}(\Omega):=\{\mathbf{u}\in L^{q}(\Omega)^{3}:\Div\mathbf{u}=0,\left.\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{u}\right|_{\Gamma_{1}}=0\}.

Then we define a Stokes-type operator as in Section
4.2
that

\mathcal{A}_{q}(\mathbf{u}):=\mathbb{P}_{q}\big(-\Div(D^{2}W(\mathbb{I})\nabla\mathbf{u})\big)\text{ for all }\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{q}),

with

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{q})=\left\{\mathbf{u}\in W^{2}_{q}(\Omega)^{3}\cap L^{q}_{\sigma}(\Omega):\left.\mathbf{u}\right|_{\Gamma_{1}}=0,\ \left.\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{n}}((D^{2}W(\mathbb{I})\nabla\mathbf{u})\mathbf{n})\right|_{\Gamma_{2}}=0\right\},

where
\mathbb{P}_{q}
denotes the
Helmholtz–Weyl projection
on
L^{q}_{\sigma}(\Omega)
, see e.g.
[
1
, Appendix A]
for the existence of the projection with mixed boundary conditions.
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{n}}:=\mathbb{I}-\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}
is the tangential projection onto
\partial\Omega
. As in Remark
1.1
, the operator
-\Div(D^{2}W(\mathbb{I})\nabla\cdot)
is strongly normally elliptic. By e.g. Prüss–Simonett
[
33
, Theorem 7.3.2]
, one knows that
\lambda+\mathcal{A}_{q}\in\mathcal{M}\mathcal{R}_{q}(L^{q}_{\sigma}(\Omega))
for all
\lambda>\lambda_{0}:=s(-\mathcal{A}_{q})
, where
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{R}_{q}(L^{q}_{\sigma}(\Omega))
means the class of maximal
L^{q}
-regularity in
L^{q}_{\sigma}(\Omega)
and
s(-\mathcal{A}_{q})
denotes the spectral bound of
-\mathcal{A}_{q}
. Consequently, for
\lambda>\lambda_{0}
and
\mathbf{f}\in L^{q}_{\sigma}(\Omega)
,

\lambda\mathbf{u}+\mathcal{A}_{q}\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{f},

is uniquely solvable with
\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{q})
, which implies that of (
A.1
).

Now it remains to recover the pressure
\pi
. To this end, we solve the Dirichlet–Neumann problem

(A.2)

\displaystyle\Delta\pi

\displaystyle=\Div(\mathbf{f}-\lambda\mathbf{u}+\Div(D^{2}W(\mathbb{I})\nabla\mathbf{u})),

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

\displaystyle\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\pi

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{f}-\lambda\mathbf{u}+\Div(D^{2}W(\mathbb{I})\nabla\mathbf{u}))\cdot\mathbf{n},

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma_{1},

\displaystyle\pi

\displaystyle=(D^{2}W(\mathbb{I})\nabla\mathbf{u})\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{n},

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma_{2},

weakly, which is equivalent to the following weak formation

(A.3)

\int_{\Omega}\nabla\pi\cdot\nabla\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\underbrace{(\mathbf{f}-\lambda\mathbf{u}+\Div(D^{2}W(\mathbb{I})\nabla\mathbf{u}))}_{=:\tilde{\mathbf{f}}}\cdot\nabla\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x,\quad\forall\varphi\in W_{q^{\prime},\Gamma_{2}}^{1}(\Omega).

Since
\mathbf{f}\in L^{q}(\Omega)^{3}
and
\mathbf{u}\in W^{2}_{q}(\Omega)^{3}\cap L^{q}_{\sigma}(\Omega)
, we have
\tilde{\mathbf{f}}\in L^{q}(\Omega)^{3}
. Then (
A.3
) admits a unique solution
\pi\in W^{1}_{q}(\Omega)
, with the aid of
[
33
, Theorem 7.4.3]
. The boundary regularity is easy due to the third equation of (
A.2
), combining the trace theorem.
∎

Remark A.2
.

As
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{q})
embeds compactly into
L^{q}_{\sigma}(\Omega)
, the Stokes-type operator
\mathcal{A}_{q}
has compact resolvent. Therefore, its spectrum consists only of eigenvalues of finite algebraic multiplicity by spectral theory for compact operators (see e.g. Alt
[
4
]
), and is independent of
q
by Sobolev embeddings. So it is enough to investigate these eigenvalues for the case
q=2
. Let
\omega
be the eigenvalue of
-\mathcal{A}_{2}
. Employing the energy method, Lemma
1.1
and the Korn’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\omega\int_{\Omega}\left|\mathbf{u}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=-\left\langle\mathcal{A}_{2}\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}\right\rangle_{L^{2}_{\sigma}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}D^{2}W(\mathbb{I})\nabla\mathbf{u}:\nabla\mathbf{u}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq-C\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\mathbf{u}+\nabla^{\top}\mathbf{u}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq-C\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\mathbf{u}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x,

which shows that
\omega
is real and nonpositive. Since
\Omega
is bounded and
\Gamma_{1}
is assumed to be nonempty, the Poincaré’s inequality is valid and one obtains

\omega\int_{\Omega}\left|\mathbf{u}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq-C\int_{\Omega}\left|\mathbf{u}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x,

which implies
