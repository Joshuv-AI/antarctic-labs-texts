Weighted theta functions for non-commutative graphs
Dan Stahlke

Abstract
Grötschel, Lovász, and Schrijver generalized the Lovász ϑ \vartheta function by allowing a weight for each vertex. We provide a similar generalization of Duan, Severini, and Winter’s ϑ ~ \tilde{\vartheta} on non-commutative graphs. While the classical theory involves a weight vector assigning a non-negative weight to each vertex, the non-commutative theory uses a positive semidefinite weight matrix. The classical theory is recovered in the case of diagonal weight matrices. Most of Grötschel, Lovász, and Schrijver’s results generalize to non-commutative graphs. In particular, we generalize the inequality ϑ ⁡ ( G , w ) ​ ϑ ​ ( G ¯ , x ) ≥ ⟨ w , x ⟩ \vartheta(G,w)\vartheta({\overline{G}},x)\geq\langle w,x\rangle with some modification needed due to non-commutative graphs having a richer notion of complementation. Similar to the classical case, facets of the theta body correspond to cliques and if the theta body anti-blocker is finitely generated then it is equal to the non-commutative generalization of the clique polytope. We propose two definitions for non-commutative perfect graphs, equivalent for classical graphs but inequivalent for non-commutative graphs.



I
Introduction

Lovász
lovasz79
introduced the
\vartheta
function of a graph as an upper bound on the Shannon capacity
– the independence number regularized under the strong graph product.
The
\vartheta
quantity is an upper bound on independence number, a lower bound on fractional
chromatic number, and is multiplicative under the strong and the disjunctive graph products.
It is a semidefinite program, hence efficiently computable both in theory and in practice.
It is monotone under graph homomorphisms
de2013optimization
; in fact its bound on
independence and chromatic number follow from this.

Further insight into
\vartheta
is gained by allowing vertices to be
weighted
Grtschel1986
;
knuth94
.
Weights are basically equivalent to duplicating vertices
knuth94

except that weights don’t have to be whole numbers.
Aside from only being defined for non-negative weights, the weighted
\vartheta
of a graph resembles a
norm on the weight vector: it scales linearly and is convex.
In that language,
\vartheta
of the complement graph is the dual norm.
The set of weights
w
for which
\vartheta({\overline{G}},w)\leq 1
is investigated
in
Grtschel1986
, where facets of this convex body are shown to correspond to clique constraints.
This set is polyhedral if and only if the graph is perfect.

Lovász’s bound can be adapted to quantum channels via a suitable generalization of
graphs where an operator subspace takes the place of the adjacency matrix
dsw2013
.
These so called
non-commutative graphs
have since drawn interest in connection with
quantum channels but also independently of any application.
Several classical graph definitions and results carry over to non-commutative graphs,
including
homomorphisms
Stahlke2016QuantumZS
;
weaver_quantum_relations
;
ortiz2016quantum
;
brannan2020quantumtoclassical
,
chromatic numbers
Stahlke2016QuantumZS
;
KIM2019291
;
levene_paulsen_todorov_complexity
,
Ramsey and Turán theorems
weaver_ramsey
;
weaver_qm_turan
,
asymptotic spectrum
li2019quantum
,
a Haemers bound
gribling2020haemers
,
and connectivity
chavezdominguez2019connectivity
.
It can happen that there are multiple ways to generalize a particular
concept:
btw2019
presents two generalizations of
\vartheta
distinct from the one
in
dsw2013
(though possibly the same as each other).

The present work investigates a weighted version of the
\tilde{\vartheta}
of
dsw2013
, generalizing most
of the results from
Grtschel1986
.
We note that
btw2019
defined a weighted version of their
\vartheta
generalization,
and investigated the corresponding theta body.
It is not known whether it supports the sort of duality relations we find in this paper.

We will cover basic notation in
section

II
,
introduce our weighted
\tilde{\vartheta}
in
section

III
,
prove a duality relation in
section

IV
,
extend this to
S_{0}
-graphs in
section

V

(with the core proof deferred to
appendix

A
),
and in
section

VI
explore the geometry of the theta body for non-commutative graphs and its
relation to perfect graphs.
The presentation is self-contained, not requiring any background in quantum mechanics
or quantum channels.

II
Notation and basic definitions

Hilbert spaces will be denoted by the symbols
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B},\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{Z}
.
These will always be finite dimensional.
Dual spaces are denoted
{{\mathcal{A}}^{\dagger}}
, etc.
Linear operators on
\mathcal{A}
are denoted
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A})
; linear maps from

\mathcal{A}
to
\mathcal{B}
by
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{B})
.
The set of positive semidefinite operators on
\mathcal{A}
is denoted
\textnormal{Pos}(\mathcal{A})
,
the set of Hermitian operators by
\textnormal{Herm}(\mathcal{A})
.
For operators
X,Y\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A})
,
X\leq Y
means
Y-X\in\textnormal{Pos}(\mathcal{A})
.
For spaces
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
we write the quotient space as

\mathcal{A}/\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{B}^{\perp}
.

We will use Dirac’s bra-ket notation where
|{x}\rangle
is a vector,

\langle{x}|
is its dual,
\langle{x}|{y}\rangle
is an inner product, and

\left\langle{x}\middle|{M}\middle|{y}\right\rangle
is an inner product between
\langle{x}|
and

M|{y}\rangle
with
M
an operator.
The adjoint of an operator is written
M^{\dagger}
.
Basis vectors are denoted
|{i}\rangle
or
|{j}\rangle
with
i,j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.

For Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
of equal dimension,
we choose an isomorphism
{{\mathcal{A}}^{\dagger}}\to\mathcal{B}
, denoted
|{\Phi}\rangle
.
There is a canonical choice once an orthonormal basis has been chosen:

\displaystyle|{\Phi}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}|{i}\rangle_{\mathcal{A}}\otimes|{i}\rangle_{\mathcal{B}}.

(1)

For an operator
M\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A})
we define its vectorization

|{M}\rangle=(M\otimes I)|{\Phi}\rangle\in\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{B}
.
We also use this isomorphism to define the transpose, taking it to move
an operator from
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A})
to
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{B})
so that

(M\otimes I)|{\Phi}\rangle=(I\otimes M^{T})|{\Phi}\rangle
.
Complex conjugate is defined similarly:
\overline{M}=(M^{\dagger})^{T}
and

(M^{\dagger}\otimes I)|{\Phi}\rangle=(I\otimes\overline{M})|{\Phi}\rangle
.

We take the definition of a non-commutative graph from
dsw2013
.

Definition 1
.

A
non-commutative graph
is an operator subspace
S\subseteq\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A})
satisfying

S=S^{\dagger}
and
I\in S
.

In the above definition and throughout we use the shorthand notation

S^{\dagger}=\textnormal{span}\{x^{\dagger}:x\in S\}
,

S+S^{\prime}=\textnormal{span}\{x+y:x\in S,y\in S^{\prime}\}
,

\mathbb{C}I=\textnormal{span}\{xI:x\in\mathbb{C}\}
, etc.

Non-commutative graphs are analogous to adjacency matrices,
but taken as subspaces rather than 0-1 matrices.
Indeed, given any classical graph
G
we can define a corresponding
non-commutative graph.

Definition 2
.

For a graph
G
define
{S_{G}}=\textnormal{span}\{|{i}\rangle\langle{j}|:i\simeq_{G}j\}
.

Note that
Stahlke2016QuantumZS
breaks from the above definition,
taking non-commutative graphs to be trace free,
I\perp S
.
That works better for graph homomorphisms, which require vertices to not be self adjacent.
But for the present work the convention of
definitions

1
and
2

is more appropriate.

We take the following definition from
btw2019
.
This is an extension to operators of the convex corners defined in
Fulkerson1971
;
Csiszr1990
,
which in turn are recovered by restricting to diagonal operators.

Definition 3
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a Hilbert space.
A convex corner in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A})
is a non-empty closed convex subset

\mathcal{C}\subseteq\textnormal{Pos}(\mathcal{A})
such that

\displaystyle A\in\mathcal{C}\textrm{ and }0\leq B\leq A\implies B\in\mathcal{C}.

(2)

Condition (
2
) is called
herditarity
.
For a general subset
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\textnormal{Pos}(\mathcal{A})
we denote by
\textnormal{her}(\mathcal{C})
the

hereditary closure
, the smallest hereditary set containing
\mathcal{C}
:

\displaystyle\textnormal{her}(\mathcal{C})=\{A\in\textnormal{Pos}(\mathcal{A}):\exists B\in\mathcal{C}\textrm{ s.t. }A\leq B\}.

(3)

The smallest convex corner containing a given subset
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\textnormal{Pos}(\mathcal{A})
of positive
semidefinite operators is obtained by taking the hereditary closure of the closure of the convex
hull,

\displaystyle\textnormal{her}(\overline{\textnormal{conv}}(\mathcal{C})).

(4)

We then say this convex corner is
generated
by
\mathcal{C}
. We say a convex corner is

finitely generated
if it can be generated by a finite set.

Also from
btw2019
, and again an extension of a concept from
Fulkerson1971
;
Csiszr1990
, we take the
definition of anti-blockers.

Definition 4
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a Hilbert space.
The anti-blocker of
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\textnormal{Pos}(\mathcal{A})
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\sharp}=\{B\in\textnormal{Pos}(\mathcal{A}):\textnormal{Tr}(AB)\leq 1\textrm{ for all }A\in\mathcal{C}\}.

(5)

The following theorem is from
(
borelandthesis
, lemma 2.2.10 and theorem 2.3.12)
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a Hilbert space.
Convex corners satisfy the following basic facts.

(i)

If
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\textnormal{Pos}(\mathcal{A})
is non-empty then
\mathcal{C}^{\sharp}
is a convex corner.

(ii)

If
\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C}\subseteq\textnormal{Pos}(\mathcal{A})
and
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
then

\mathcal{C}^{\sharp}\subseteq\mathcal{B}^{\sharp}

(iii)

A non-empty set
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\textnormal{Pos}(\mathcal{A})
satisfies
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}^{\sharp\sharp}
if and only if
\mathcal{C}
is a convex corner
(the second anti-blocker theorem).

Following
btw2019
, we will occasionally refer to
diagonal convex corners
, analogous
to
definition

3
but restricted to diagonal matrices.
Note this is equivalent to the classical definition from
Fulkerson1971
;
Csiszr1990
, just with
the elements of the corner being positive semidefinite diagonal matrices rather than entrywise
non-negative vectors.
If
\mathcal{C}
is a convex corner then
\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{D}
, with
\mathcal{D}
being the subspace
of diagonal matrices, is a diagonal convex corner.
Again following
btw2019
we define the diagonal anti-blocker

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\flat}

\displaystyle=\{B\in\mathcal{D}:B\geq 0,\textnormal{Tr}(AB)\leq 1\textrm{ for all }A\in\mathcal{C}\}

(6)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}^{\sharp}\cap\mathcal{D}.

(7)

This is equivalent to the classical notion of anti-blocker from
Fulkerson1971
;
Csiszr1990
.
We will only be referencing diagonal convex corners and diagonal anti-blockers when comparing
our theory to the previously established theory of classical (commutative) graphs.

III
Weighted
\tilde{\vartheta}

The following definition of weighted
\vartheta
for classical graphs is derived in
a straightforward way from
(
knuth94
, section 6)
. It is presented here for background
and to motivate a similar definition for non-commutative graphs. We will not be using it
directly.

Definition 6
.

Let
G
be a graph with
n
vertices and
|{w}\rangle\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an entrywise positive vector.
Let
|{r}\rangle
be the entrywise square root of
|{w}\rangle
. Define

\displaystyle\vartheta(G,w)=\min\{\lambda:Y\geq|{r}\rangle\langle{r}|,Y_{ii}=\lambda,Y_{ij}=0\textrm{ for }i\not\simeq j\}.

(8)

The unweighted
\vartheta
is recovered by taking
|{w}\rangle
to be the all ones vector.

The non-weighted
\tilde{\vartheta}
has been defined for non-commutative graphs
by
dsw2013
, which presents the following equivalent definitions.

Definition 7
.

Let
S
be a non-commutative graph.

\displaystyle\tilde{\vartheta}(S)

\displaystyle=\max\{\left\lVert T+I\otimes I\right\rVert:T\in S^{\perp}\otimes\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{B}),T+I\otimes I\geq 0\}

(9)

\displaystyle=\max\{\left\langle{\Phi}\middle|{T+I\otimes\rho}\middle|{\Phi}\right\rangle:T\in S^{\perp}\otimes\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{B}),T+I\otimes\rho\geq 0,\rho\geq 0,\textnormal{Tr}\rho=1\}

(10)

\displaystyle=\min\{\left\lVert\textnormal{Tr}_{\mathcal{A}}Y\right\rVert:Y\in S\otimes\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{B}),Y\geq|{\Phi}\rangle\langle{\Phi}|\}

(11)

\displaystyle=\min\{\lambda:Y\in S\otimes\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{B}),\textnormal{Tr}_{\mathcal{A}}Y\leq\lambda I,Y\geq|{\Phi}\rangle\langle{\Phi}|\}

(12)

If
G
is a graph and
{S_{G}}=\textnormal{span}\{|{i}\rangle\langle{j}|:i\simeq_{G}j\}
then

\tilde{\vartheta}({S_{G}})=\vartheta(G)
.

It is worth mentioning that for classical graphs there are many alternate
forms for
\vartheta

Grtschel1986
;
knuth94
.
While these are all equivalent for classical graphs, they are in general different
when generalized to non-commutative graphs.
Two quantities different from
definition

7
are studied in
btw2019
.

We now construct a weighted version of
\tilde{\vartheta}(S)
.
Observing the similarity between (
12
) and

definition

6
, it seems reasonable that the weights should be absorbed
into
|{\Phi}\rangle
.
The weights will be a positive semidefinite operator rather than an entrywise positive vector,
which is common for quantum generalizations of classical concepts (cf. density operators vs. probability distributions).

Definition 8
.

Let
S
be a non-commutative graph and
W\in\textnormal{Pos}(A)
.
Let

|{W}\rangle=(W\otimes I)|{\Phi}\rangle\in\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{B}
and

|{\sqrt{W}}\rangle=(\sqrt{W}\otimes I)|{\Phi}\rangle
.
Note that
\textnormal{Tr}_{\mathcal{B}}\left({|{\sqrt{W}}\rangle\langle{\sqrt{W}}|}\right)=W
and

\textnormal{Tr}_{\mathcal{A}}\left({|{\sqrt{W}}\rangle\langle{\sqrt{W}}|}\right)=W^{T}
.
Define

\displaystyle\tilde{\vartheta}(S,W)

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\lambda:Y\in S\otimes\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{B}),\textnormal{Tr}_{\mathcal{A}}Y\leq\lambda I,Y\geq{|{\sqrt{W}}\rangle\langle{\sqrt{W}}|}\right\}.

(13)

Note that for
W=I
, (
13
) reduces
to (
12
).
And for a classical graph with diagonal weight matrix it reduces to

definition

6
:

Theorem 9
.

For any classical graph
G
and weight vector
|{w}\rangle
,

\displaystyle\tilde{\vartheta}({S_{G}},\textnormal{diag}(w))=\vartheta(G,w)

(14)

where
{S_{G}}=\textnormal{span}\{|{i}\rangle\langle{j}|:i\simeq_{G}j\}

and
\textnormal{diag}(w)
is the diagonal matrix with
\textnormal{diag}(w)_{ii}=w_{i}
.

Proof.

Straightforward generalization of the proof of
(
dsw2013
, corollary 12)
.
∎

Theorem

9
invites a question: for classical graphs, does our
\tilde{\vartheta}
,
by allowing non-diagonal weight matrices, provide additional information about the graph?
In
section

V
we will find the answer is no.
For classical graphs,
\tilde{\vartheta}
with non-diagonal weights is a function of
\tilde{\vartheta}
with
diagonal weights (
theorem

29
).

Being a semidefinite program, (
13
) is efficiently computable.
It is more computationally expensive than the classical
\vartheta
because
Y

is of size
n^{2}\times n^{2}
rather than
n\times n
where
n=\dim(\mathcal{A})={\lvert G\rvert}
.
Experiments with the SCS solver
scs2016
;
convexjl
show
n=9
takes one minute (i7-6820HQ CPU,
circa 2015), with runtime scaling at about
O(n^{6})
.
Much insight can be gained through numerical experiments even with
n=3,4
.
And, crucially, semidefinite programs have dual formulations.

Theorem 10
.

The dual of the semidefinite program (
13
) is

\displaystyle\tilde{\vartheta}(S,W)

\displaystyle=\max\Big\{\left\langle{\sqrt{W}}\middle|{T+I\otimes\rho}\middle|{\sqrt{W}}\right\rangle:T\in S^{\perp}\otimes\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{B}),

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad T+I\otimes\rho\geq 0,\rho\geq 0,\textnormal{Tr}\rho=1\Big\}

(15)

Proof.

Set
M={|{\sqrt{W}}\rangle\langle{\sqrt{W}}|}
and rewrite (
13
),

\displaystyle\tilde{\vartheta}(S,W)

\displaystyle=\min\Big\{\lambda:Y\in S\otimes\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{B}),\textnormal{Tr}_{\mathcal{A}}Y-\lambda I\leq 0,M-Y\leq 0\Big\}.

(16)

The Lagrangian is

\displaystyle L(\lambda,Y;R,\rho)

\displaystyle=\lambda+\langle{\rho,\textnormal{Tr}_{\mathcal{A}}Y-\lambda I}\rangle+\langle{R,M-Y}\rangle

(17)

\displaystyle=\langle{I\otimes\rho-R,Y}\rangle+\lambda(1-\textnormal{Tr}\rho)+\langle{R,M}\rangle.

(18)

The dual program is then

\displaystyle d^{*}

\displaystyle=\max\Big\{\langle{R,M}\rangle:I\otimes\rho-R\in S^{\perp}\otimes\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{B}),\textnormal{Tr}\rho=1,R\geq 0,\rho\geq 0\Big\}

(19)

Defining
T=R-I\otimes\rho
gives the right hand side of (
15
).
The point
T=0,\rho=I_{\mathcal{B}}/\dim(\mathcal{B})
is in the relative interior of the
feasible region so Slater’s condition holds and
d^{*}=\tilde{\vartheta}(S,W)
.
∎

The main result of the previous section,

of the classical
\vartheta

It has two problems: an extra factor of

The purpose of this section is to remedy both of these problems, which as we will see are related.
If

{S_{G}}=\textnormal{span}\{|{i}\rangle\langle{j}|:i\simeq_{G}j\}

non-commutative graph.
The non-commutative graph for

where

is the space of diagonal matrices.
This differs from the inverse

used in

In general, how are we to know whether

or

is the appropriate complement? Or something different from either of these?

The answer comes from
arxiv:1002.2514
, the extended arXiv version of
dsw2013
.
The non-commutative graph associated with a classical graph has a special linear algebraic structure.
We take the following definition from
arxiv:1002.2514
but omit discussion of the
S_{0}
-valued
inner product, which we will not need.

\mathcal{A}

(iii)

\displaystyle 0

(iii)

Lemma 27

Every convex corner

Lemma 28
.

Let

\mathcal{D}

(assuming

Let

\displaystyle p^{*}=\max\{\textnormal{Tr}((W+E)^{\dagger}X):W\in\mathcal{C},E\in\mathcal{D}^{\perp},W+E\geq 0\}.

This is a convex optimization problem on the convex domain

We will find the dual by the recipe in
(

The Lagrangian is

\displaystyle L(W,E,Q)

\displaystyle=\textnormal{Tr}((W+E)^{\dagger}X)+\textnormal{Tr}((W+E)^{\dagger}Q)

\displaystyle=\textnormal{Tr}(W^{\dagger}(X+Q))+\textnormal{Tr}(E^{\dagger}(X+Q))

(94)

\displaystyle g(Q)

\displaystyle=\max\{L(W,E,Q):(W,E)\in\mathcal{G}\}

\displaystyle=\max\{\textnormal{Tr}(W^{\dagger}(X+Q))+\textnormal{Tr}(E^{\dagger}(X+Q)):(W,E)\in\mathcal{G}\}

(96)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\max\{\textnormal{Tr}(W^{\dagger}(X+Q)):W\in\mathcal{C}\}&\textrm{ if }X+Q\in\mathcal{D}\\
\infty&\textrm{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

(97)

\displaystyle d^{*}

\displaystyle=\min\{g(Q):Q\geq 0\}

(98)

\displaystyle=\min\{\max\{\textnormal{Tr}(W^{\dagger}(X+Q)):W\in\mathcal{C}\}:X+Q\in\mathcal{D},Q\geq 0\}

(99)

\displaystyle=\min\{\max\{\textnormal{Tr}(W^{\dagger}Y):W\in\mathcal{C}\}:Y\in\mathcal{D},Y\geq X\}.

(100)

VI
A sandwich theorem and perfect graphs

For classical graphs the theta body is sandwiched between the vertex packing polytope
\textnormal{vp}(G)
,
defined as the convex hull of indicator functions of independent sets, and the fractional
vertex packing polytope, equal to
\textnormal{vp}^{\flat}({\overline{G}})
:
Grtschel1986

\displaystyle\textnormal{vp}(G)\subseteq\textnormal{TH}(G)\subseteq\textnormal{vp}^{\flat}({\overline{G}}).

(166)

Or, considering the complement graph and making use of the fact that

\textnormal{TH}({\overline{G}})=\textnormal{TH}^{\flat}(G)
,

\displaystyle\textnormal{vp}({\overline{G}})\subseteq\textnormal{TH}^{\flat}(G)\subseteq\textnormal{vp}^{\flat}(G).

(167)

We will generalize this latter statement to non-commutative graphs.
Note that
\textnormal{vp}({\overline{G}})
is the polytope of indicator functions of cliques of
G
.
Given that

\displaystyle\alpha(G)

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\sum w_{i}:w\in\textnormal{vp}(G)\right\}

(168)

\displaystyle\vartheta(G)

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\sum w_{i}:w\in\textnormal{TH}(G)\right\}

(169)

\displaystyle\chi^{*}({\overline{G}})

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\sum w_{i}:w\in\textnormal{vp}^{\flat}({\overline{G}})\right\}

(170)

(where
\alpha
is independence number and
\chi^{*}
is fractional chromatic
number), (
166
) can be seen as a more granular version of the well known
sandwich inequality
\alpha(G)\leq\vartheta(G)\leq\chi^{*}({\overline{G}})
.

Also from
Grtschel1986
comes the marvelous result that the following are equivalent:

1.

G
is a perfect graph.

2.

\textnormal{TH}(G)
is a polytope.

3.

\textnormal{TH}(G)=\textnormal{vp}(G)
.

4.

\textnormal{TH}(G)=\textnormal{vp}^{\flat}({\overline{G}})
.

We will show this partially generalizes to non-commutative graphs.
There is currently no definition of “perfect” for non-commutative graphs, so we may take the
non-commutative generalization of
\textnormal{vp}(G)=\textnormal{vp}^{\flat}({\overline{G}})
to be the definition.
Since we are using an operator generalization of convex corners and anti-blockers, there are
(at least) two possible analogues of “polytope”: we could consider convex corners
generated by finitely many vertices, or those bounded by finitely many inequalities.
We will find that if
\textnormal{TH}^{\sharp}(S)
is finitely generated then it is equal to the non-commutative
generalization of
\textnormal{vp}({\overline{G}})
, but that the converse is not true.
This can be considered a first dipping of the toes into the theory of non-commutative perfect
graphs, with many questions remaining open.

The following definition from
btw2019
gives the non-commutative generalization of

\textnormal{vp}({\overline{G}})
that we will be using.
They also define
S
-abelian and
S
-clique projectors, the former being analogous to independent
sets and the latter being a looser definition of clique, but we will not be using those here.

Definition 41
.

Let

We define the convex corner generated by these projections,

\displaystyle\textnormal{fp}(S)=\textnormal{her}(\overline{\textnormal{conv}}\{P:P\textnormal{ an $S$-full projection}\})

Theorem 42
.

Let

be an
S_{0}
-graph. If

is a maximal

-full projection then

. Consequently, the maximal elements of

Let

be a maximal

-full projection. Then

collection of normalized vectors

By the definition of an

It must be that

Consider a unitary

VII
Conclusion and open questions

Broadly speaking, there were three interrelated topics explored in this paper:
the weighed theta function, the geometry of convex corners, and perfect graphs.
While the basic theory of weighted theta functions is essentially complete,
the theory of perfect graphs stands as little more than an invitation to the topic.

There are numerous open questions surrounding all aspects of this work.
For the weighted theta function, the biggest question is whether there is any application.
In terms of what may be generalized to non-commutative graphs, the most tantalizing application
is the result of
ACIN2017489
that
independence number approaches
\vartheta
when activated through the strong product:

\displaystyle\sup_{H}\frac{\alpha(G\boxtimes H)}{\vartheta(G\boxtimes H)}=1.

(242)

The first step of the proof uses
\alpha(G\boxtimes{\overline{G}})=n
or, rather, the weighted version of
this. This is trivial for classical graphs since
\{(v,v):v\in V(G)\}
is an independent
set. The analogous statement for non-commutative graphs seems not so easy.
Possibly the rest of the proof of (
242
) could be made to work.

The entropy of the theta body is investigated in
Marton1993
.
Entropy of non-commutative convex corners is explored in
(
borelandthesis
, section 2.4.3)
.
Is there application for the entropy of the theta body for non-commutative graphs?
Using the techniques of
Fawzi2018
, such a quantity can be estimated to arbitrary precision
via an SDP.

An alternate definition of weighted theta was presented in
btw2019
. Does this support
a duality relation like
theorem

19
or
theorem

32
?
Are maximal elements of the corresponding theta body anti-blocker in
S_{0}^{\prime}
like we have in

theorem

29
?

While we have made much progress on understanding the geometry of vertices and facets for
non-commutative convex corners, a few questions remain open.
Is it true that vertices of a convex corner always correspond to facets of the anti-blocker?
Is a convex corner defined by finitely many inequalities if and only if its anti-blocker is finitely
generated?
If a convex corner is generated by its vertices, does this mean it is finitely generated?

As for perfect graphs, we have offered nothing beyond a couple definitions and some examples.
The most pressing question is whether there is some analogue to the strong perfect graph theorem.
That is to say, can
fp
- or
fg
-perfection be characterized by forbidden subgraphs?
If not, is there any other characterization?

It seems unsatisfactory that
S=\mathbb{C}I
,
S_{0}=\mathbb{C}I
is not

fp
-perfect.
Can this be remedied by finding a tighter version of the sandwich theorem
(
theorem

43
)?
For instance, perhaps we should replace
\textnormal{fp}(S)
, the non-commutative analogue of the clique
polytope, with a convex corner generated by entanglement assisted cliques (however those
may be defined).
Expanding the definition of perfect graphs by tightening the sandwich theorem has some precedent,
e.g., circular-perfect graphs
PECHER2021103224
.

Source code in the Julia language for computing weighted thetas on non-commutative graphs
is available
githubncgraphs
.

Lemma 60
