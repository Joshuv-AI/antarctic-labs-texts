On Long Orbit Empty Value (LOEV) principle
M. Ivanov, D. Kamburova, N. Zlateva

Abstract
We consider an useful in Variational Analysis tool – Long Orbit or Empty Value (LOEV) principle – in different settings, starting from more abstract to more defined. We prove, using LOEV principle, a number of basic results in Variational Analysis, including some novel. We characterize Σ g \Sigma_{g} -semicompleteness for a generalized metric function g g which is neither symmetric nor satisfies the triangle inequality, in terms of validity of Ekeland Theorem for this g g . We present an interesting application to perturbability to minimum in a G δ G_{\delta} subset of a complete metric space.

Further, the article

At present Ekeland Theorem is considered the basic one and all others are derived from it. Note that essentially Ekeland Theorem is an optimization result, while LOEV principle is inherently dynamical and can be applied where Ekeland Theorem is hard to apply. The origin of LOEV principle is in discretization of differential inclusions, see

Our aim here, however is somewhat different. To put it in context, LOEV principle can be considered as a non-idempotent counterpart to the famous Brézis-Browder Principle,
[

4

The advantage of not requiring

2
LOEV principle in a topological framework

First, we set the formal framework for the present study.

We work in a first countable topological space, usually denoted by
(X,\tau)
. We consider a set-valued map
S:X\rightrightarrows X
with domain

\mathop{\rm dom}S:=\{x\in X:\ S(x)\neq\varnothing\}.

Recall that a (finite or infinite) succession of points in
X
satisfying
x_{i+1}\in S(x_{i})
for
i=0,1,2,\dots
is called an orbit of
S
, or
S
-orbit, starting at
x_{0}
. We say that an
S
-orbit ends at
x\in X
if the orbit is finite and
x_{n}=x
, or the orbit is infinite and the sequence
\{x_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
converges to
x
. If an
S
-orbit is infinite and the sequence
\{x_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
diverges, we say that it is a divergent
S
-orbit.

For convenience, we impose
non-stationary
condition:

x\not\in S(x),\quad\forall x\in X.

(1)

The meaning of this condition is that the stationary sequence
x,x,x,\ldots
is never an
S
-orbit. Obviously, the latter can be easily ensured when necessary, by considering instead of
S
the map
x\rightrightarrows S(x)\setminus\{x\}
.

Recall, see
[
1
]
, that
S
satisfies property
(\ast)
if (
1
) is fulfilled, and for each
y\in S(x)
and each sequence
x_{i}\to x
, there is a subsequence
\{x_{i_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
such that
y\in S(x_{i_{k}})
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

The property
(\ast)
is very practical, as it can be seen from the examples in
[
1
]
, but it can be easily relaxed in several directions, most obvious of which is to consider only sequences starting from a certain fixed point.

Definition 2.1
.

The set-valued map
S:X\rightrightarrows X
, where
(X,\tau)
is a first countable topological space, satisfies the property
(\ast_{1})
at
x_{0}\in X
, if (
1
) is fulfilled, and for each infinite
S
-orbit
\{x_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
starting at
x_{0}
and ending at
x
(that is,
x_{i}\to x
) and each
y\in S(x)
, there is a subsequence
\{x_{i_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
such that

y\in S(x_{i_{k}}),\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}.

Next, we define what we require from the spaces we work on.

Definition 2.2
.

We will call the triple
(X,\tau,h)

is a first countable Hausdorff space and the generalized distance function

(i)

(ii) if
h(x,x_{n})\to 0

x_{n}\to x
;

(iii) if
x_{n}\to x
, then
h(x_{n+1},x_{n})\to 0
.

(Of course, all convergences are as

What follows is our first main result, which is a far-reaching generalization of LOEV principle.

Let

-orbit starting at

(b) There is an

-orbit starting at

and ending at

(2)

Next, we will analyze a bit the definition of an
h
-space.It is remarkable that generalized distance function

actually defines the topology.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
(X,\tau,h)
be an h-space. Then

x_{n}\to x\iff h(x,x_{n})\to 0.

(4)

Proof.

Because of Definition
2.2
(ii), we have to prove only that
x_{n}\to x
implies
h(x,x_{n})\to 0
. Assume this were not the case and let
x_{n}
be such a sequence that
x_{n}\to x
, but
\limsup_{n\to\infty}h(x,x_{n})>0

It also converges to
x
, but Definition
2.2
(iii) fails. Contradiction.
∎

Because of Proposition
2.6
, in an
h
-space
(X,\tau,h)
, for each positive integer

and each
x\in X
, the level set

V_{n}(x):=\{y\in X:\ h(x,y)<1/n\}

(5)

is a neighbourhood of
x
, that is, there is an open set

such that
x\in U
and
U\subset V_{n}(x)
, or, in other words,

is in the interior of

. In the terminology of

6
]
, the set-valued mapping

, so

has a countable family of neighbournets that define the topology in the sense that

if and only if for each
x\in U
there is

such that
V_{n}(x)\subset U
.

Since the function

is not a part of the conclusion of Theorem

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
(X,\tau)
be a first countable Hausdorff space. Then there exists a generalized distance function

such that
(X,\tau,h)

is an

-space if and only if there exists a countable nested family of neighbournets
\{V_{n}(x)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N},x\in X}
,
which defines
\tau
, that is,

\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}V_{n}(x)=\{x\},\quad\forall x\in X,

(6)

and if
x_{n}\to x
and
i_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
are such that
x_{n}\notin V_{i_{n}}(x_{n+1})
, then

\lim_{n\to\infty}i_{n}=\infty.

(7)

Proof.

Assume that
(X,\tau,h)
is an
h
-space and define

via (
5
). It is clear that these are nested and we have already seen that they define

, but let’s check (
6

is in the intersection in the left hand side of (

for all

and

by Definition
2.2
(i).

Assume now that (
7
) is false, so there is
x_{n}\to x
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
x_{n}\notin V_{k}(x_{n+1})
for infinitely many
n

h(x_{n+1},x_{n})\geq 1/k
for infinitely many
n
’s, see (
5
). This contradicts Definition

(iii).

For the opposite direction, let
\{V_{n}(x)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N},x\in X}
be a nested countable family of neighbournets in
(X,\tau)
satisfying (
6
) and (
7
). Set

and define

by

Now, (

(i).

If

then for each fixed

’s, so, by definition, all but finitely many

, and Definition

(ii) is verified.

Consider an arbitrary convergent sequence
x_{n}\to x
. Fix

. If

There is much more to be considered with respect to topology. For example, we have not touched the dual topology. However, we do not have space for that here, so we proceed to our next topic.

Often an intermediate property – between

Definition 2.8
.

The set-valued map
S:X\rightrightarrows X
, where
(X,\tau)
is a first countable topological space, satisfies the property
(\ast_{2})
at
x_{0}\in X
, if (

) is fulfilled for
S
, and for each infinite
S
-orbit
\{x_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
starting at
x_{0}
, each subsequence
\{x_{i_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
converging to
x
, and each

(\ast_{2})

, while

Under this stronger assumption

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
(X,\tau,h)
be an
h
-space. Let
S:X\rightrightarrows X
be a set-valued map satisfying
(\ast_{2})
at
x_{0}
. Then at least one of the following is true:

(a) There is an

-orbit starting at
x_{0}
, say
\{x_{i}\}_{i\geq 0}
, such that its closure is not entirely contained in

, i.e.,

\overline{\{x_{i}\}_{i\geq 0}}\not\subset\mathop{\rm dom}S.

(b) There is a divergent
S
-orbit starting at
x_{0}
, say
\{x_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
, such that

\limsup_{i\to\infty}h(x_{i+1},x_{i})>0.

(c) There is an infinite discrete
S
-orbit starting at

.

Proof.

Let
x_{0},x_{1},\dots
be the

-orbit given by Lemma

. If it is finite, then its end point is outside of

and (

) holds. So, assume that it is an infinite sequence:

. If it has no convergent subsequences, we are done, because (

) holds.

So, let
x_{i_{k}}\to x
, as
k\to\infty
. If
S(x)=\varnothing
then (

) holds, so let

. From

there is further subsequence
\{x_{i_{k_{j}}}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}

. If (

) is not true, then

\min\{1,h(y,x_{i_{k_{j}}})\}\leq 2h(x_{i_{k_{j}}+1},x_{i_{k_{j}}})\to 0,

. By Definition

As it is evident from

, and as we will see again later on here, many useful maps are

3
LOEV principle in
\Sigma_{g}
-semicomplete premetric space

Let
(X,\tau)
be a topological space. A generalized distance function
g:X\times X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
with the following properties:

(P1)

g(x,x)=0
for any
x\in X
and
g(x,y)>0
whenever
x\neq y
;

(P2)

g(x,\cdot)
is continuous for every fixed
x\in X

will be called a
premetric function on

X
. Obviously, (P1) is the same as Definition
2.2
(i), but we repeat it here for ease of reference. If there exists a premetric function
g
on a topological space
X
, then necessarily
X
is a Hausdorff space. A first countable topological space
(X,\tau)
with premetric function
g
on it will be called
premetric space
and will be denoted by
(X,\tau,g)
.

Premetric functions exist on very large classes of spaces. For example,
let
X
be a completely regular and first countable space. Fix a point
x\in X
and let
\{U_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a nested local base at
x
. For a
k\in\mathbb{N}
let
p_{k}:X\rightarrow[0,1]
be a continuous function such that
p_{k}(x)=0
and
p_{k}|_{X\setminus U_{k}}\equiv 1
. Define

g(x,y):=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{p_{k}(y)}{2^{k}}.

(9)

The function
g:X\times X\rightarrow[0,1]
is a premetric function that, moreover, satisfies Definition
2.2
(ii).

\Sigma
-Cauchy sequences and
\Sigma
-semicompleteness are considered in Suzuki
[
5
]
. We extend these notions to a premetric space
(X,\tau,g)
in the following way.

For a sequence
\{x_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
in a premetric space
(X,\tau,g)
,
\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}g(x_{i+1},x_{i})
can be considered as the
g
-length of the sequence. If a sequence
\{x_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
has a finite
g
-length, it will be called
\Sigma_{g}
-Cauchy sequence. The notion of
\Sigma_{g}
-Cauchy sequence also appears as absolutely convergent sequence in
[
7
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

A first countable premetric space
(X,\tau,g)
is called
\Sigma_{g}
-semicomplete if every
\Sigma_{g}
-Cauchy sequence in
X
has a convergent subsequence.

Next we will prove a variant of LOEV principle in a
\Sigma_{g}
-semicomplete space.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
(X,\tau,g)
be a
\Sigma_{g}
-semicomplete space, let
S:X\rightrightarrows X
satisfy
(\ast_{2})
at
x_{0}\in X
. Then at least one of (a) and (b) below is true:

(a) There is an
S
-orbit starting at
x_{0}
with an infinite
g
-length;

(b) There is an
S
-orbit starting at
x_{0}
with a finite
g
-length and such that its closure is not contained in the domain of
S
.

Proof.

Let
x_{0},x_{1},\dots
be the orbit given by Lemma
2.4
for
S
and
g
. If it is finite, or of infinite
g
-length, we are done. So, assume that it constitutes a
\Sigma_{g}
-Cauchy sequence
\{x_{i}\}_{i\geq 0}
. Obviously then
g(x_{i+1},x_{i})\to 0
, as
i\to\infty
. From (
2
) it follows that

\lim_{i\to\infty}\sup g(S(x_{i}),x_{i})=0.

(10)

From the
\Sigma_{g}
-semicompleteness of the space
(X,\tau,g)
it follows that there is a convergent subsequence
\{x_{i_{k}}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
. Let
x_{i_{k}}\rightarrow x\in X
, as
k\rightarrow\infty
. If
S(x)=\varnothing
, we are done, so assume there is
y\in S({x})
. By (
1
) we have
y\neq x
. Since
g
has property (P1), we have that
g(y,x)>0
.

By the property
(\ast_{2})
of
S
at
x_{0}
, there is a further subsequence
\{x_{i_{k_{j}}}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
y\in S(x_{i_{k_{j}}})
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
. By (P2)
g(y,\cdot)
is continuous, so when
j\rightarrow\infty
,
g(y,x_{i_{k_{j}}})\rightarrow g(y,x)>0
, . But then
\sup g(S(x_{i_{k_{j}}}),x_{i_{k_{j}}})\geq g(y,x_{i_{k_{j}}})>g(y,x)/2>0
for all large enough

’s, which contradicts (

).
∎

The next result shows that if LOEV holds in a premetric space
(X,\tau,g)
, then necessarily the space is
\Sigma_{g}

Theorem 3.3

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
(X,\tau,g)
be a first countable premetric space which is not
\Sigma_{g}
-semicomplete. Then there exists a countable discrete set

M=\{x_{n}:\ n\in\mathbb{N}\},

such that the sequence
\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
has a finite
g
-length.

Moreover, there is a strictly positive lower semicontinuous function
f:X\to\mathbb{R}^{+}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\mathop{\rm dom}f\equiv M
,
\inf f=0
, and

f(x_{n})-f(x_{n+1})=g(x_{n+1},x_{n}),\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}.

(11)

Proof.

By definition, there is a
\Sigma_{g}
-Cauchy sequence
\{z_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
with no convergent subsequences. Let for
n\in\mathbb{N}

\sigma(n):=\max\{k:\ z_{k}=z_{n}\}.

Note that the maximum is well defined, because if there were infinitely many equal
z_{k}

n\leq\sigma(n)<\infty.

Clearly,

z_{\sigma(n)}=z_{n}\text{ and }z_{k}\neq z_{n},\ \forall k>\sigma(n).

(12)

We construct by induction a subsequence
\{z_{k_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in the following way:

k_{1}:=1,\quad k_{n+1}:=\sigma(k_{n})+1.

Clearly,
\{k_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is strictly increasing, because
\sigma(n)\geq n
, so
\{z_{k_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a subsequence of
\{z_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
, thus it has no convergent subsequences. Set

x_{n}:=z_{k_{n}},\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}.

Then
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has no convergent subsequences and also

x_{i}\neq x_{j},\quad\forall i\neq j\in\mathbb{N}.

Indeed, let
i<j
. Since
\{k_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is increasing,
k_{j}\geq k_{i+1}=\sigma(k_{i})+1>\sigma(k_{i})
, so
x_{j}=z_{k_{j}}\neq z_{k_{i}}=x_{i}
, see (
12
). This means that the range
M
of the sequence
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is exactly as defined in the Lemma, or, in other words, the map
n\to x_{n}
is injective. The set
M
is discrete, because
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has no convergent subsequences.

To estimate the

\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, note that

x_{n}=z_{k_{n}}=z_{\sigma(k_{n})},\text{ and }x_{n+1}=z_{\sigma(k_{n})+1},

so

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}g(x_{n+1},x_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}g(z_{\sigma(k_{n})+1},z_{\sigma(k_{n})})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}g(z_{k+1},z_{k})<\infty,

therefore the
g
-length of
\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is finite.

Define the function

f(x):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\sum_{i=n}^{\infty}g(x_{i+1},x_{i}),&x=x_{n};\\
+\infty,&x\notin M.\end{array}\right.

(13)

It is obviously a strictly positive function. Since the sums are finite,
\mathop{\rm dom}f\equiv M
. The equality (
11
) is satisfied by definition. Clearly,
\lim_{n\to\infty}f(x_{n})=0
, so
\inf f=0
. Since
M
consists of isolated points,
f
is also lower semicontinuous.
∎

Proof.

of Theorem

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
(X,\tau,g)
be a
\Sigma_{g}
-semicomplete space. Let the set-valued map
T:X\rightrightarrows X
satisfy
T(x)\neq\varnothing
for all

be proper, lower semicontinuous and bounded below. Assume that for any
x\in X
there exists

such that

g(y,x)\leq f(x)-f(y).

(15)

Then
T
has a fixed point, that is, there exists
\bar{x}\in X
such that
\bar{x}\in T(\bar{x})
.

Proof.

Assume that
x\notin T(x)
for all
x\in X
. Consider the map

S(x):=\{y\in X:2^{-1}g(y,x)<f(x)-f(y)\}.

We claim that
S
satisfies
(\ast)
. Indeed,
S
satisfies (
1
) by definition. Let
x_{n}\to\bar{x}
and
y\in S(\bar{x})
, so

f(\bar{x})-f(y)-g(y,\bar{x})/2>0.

Since
f
is lower semicontinuous and
g
is continuous on its second argument by (P2), the function
x\to f(x)-f(y)-g(y,x)/2>0
is lower semicontinuous at
\bar{x}
. So,
f(x_{n})-f(y)-g(y,x_{n})/2>0
for all but finitely many
n
’s, that is,
y\in S(x_{n})
for all but finitely many
n
’s.

Now, since for each
x\in X
there is some
y\in T(x)
,
y\neq x
, satisfying (
15
) and by (P1),
g(y,x)>0
, we have that
S(x)\neq\varnothing
for all
x\in X
.

We apply Theorem
3.2
to get an
S
-orbit
\{x_{i}\}_{i\geq 0}
with an
infinite
g
-length.

From the the definition of
S
it follows that
2^{-1}g(x_{i+1},x_{i})<f(x_{i})-f(x_{i+1})
for all

. Summing the inequalities we obtain that

2^{-1}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}g(x_{i+1},x_{i})<f(x_{0})-f(x_{n}),\quad\forall n\geq 1.

Letting
n
to infinity, we get
f(x_{n})\rightarrow-\infty
which contradicts the boundedness below of
f
. The proof is then complete.
∎

Theorem 3.7
.

Let

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
(X,\tau,g)
be a
\Sigma_{g}
-semicomplete space. Let the function

be proper, lower semicontinuous and bounded below. Then for every

there exists

, such that

\displaystyle f(v)\leq f(x)+\varepsilon g(x,v)\text{ for all }x\in X.

(17)

Proof.

Assume that for some
{\varepsilon}>0
there is no point
v\in X
such that (
17
) holds. Then for every

there should be some

such that

f(x)-{\varepsilon}g(y,x)>f(y)

Consider the set-valued map

defined as

S(x):=\{y\in X:f(y)<f(x)-\varepsilon g(y,x)\},

and observe that
S(x)\neq\varnothing
for all

. By (P1) property of the function

,

for all

4
LOEV principle in complete metric space

In this section we work in a complete metric space
(X,d)
which is the original setting of LOEV principle. Obviously, if
\tau_{d}
is the canonical topology generated by the metric, then
(X,\tau_{d},d)
is both a
h
-space and a
\Sigma_{d}
-semicomplete premetric space (it is, of course, complete and there is no difference between completeness and semicompleteness here) so, all results so far work in a complete metric space
(X,d)
.

However, the presence of the triangle inequality and the full continuity of
d
as a function from
X\times X
to
\mathbb{R}^{+}
, add more structure, so we can prove a stronger result. We will also demonstrate how someone interested only in complete metric spaces could work somewhat easier than in the previous sections.

To put what follows into context, recall that a set-valued map
S:X\rightrightarrows X
is lower semicontinuous at
x\in X
if for each
y\in S(x)
there exists a sequence
x_{n}\to x
and
y_{n}\in S(x_{n})
such that
y_{n}\to y
, see
[
14
, p.39]
. Naturally,
S
is lower semicontinuous if it is lower semicontinuous at each
x\in X
. We see that the property
(\ast)
trivially implies lower semicontinuity. So, if we restrict the lower semicontinuity to the orbits starting at certain point, we obtain a property weaker than
(\ast_{1})
, but also more symmetric than
(\ast_{1})
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
(X,d)
be a complete metric space. We say that the set-valued map
S:X\rightrightarrows X
satisfies the condition
\left(\widehat{\ast_{1}}\right)
at
x_{0}\in X
if it satisfies (
1
), and for each infinite
S
-orbit
\{x_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
starting at
x_{0}
and ending at
x
, that is,
x_{n}\to x
, as
n\to\infty
; and each
y\in S(x)
, there exist a subsequence
\{x_{n_{k}}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\{x_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
and
y_{k}\in S(x_{n_{k}})
such that
y_{k}\to y
, as
k\to\infty
.

The following is a localization of the version of LOEV principle found in
[
15
]
. The reader is advised to check
[
15
]
for a very specific application of LOEV principle, which apparently cannot be done by Ekeland Theorem.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
(X,d)
be a complete metric space. Let the set-valued map
S:X\rightrightarrows X
satisfy the condition
\left(\widehat{\ast_{1}}\right)
at
x_{0}\in X
. Then at least one of the following two holds:

(a)

There is an
S
-orbit of finite length starting at
x_{0}
and ending outside of
\mathop{\rm dom}S
;

(b)

There is an
S
-orbit of infinite length starting at
x_{0}
.

Proof.

Let
\{x_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be the orbit given by Lemma
2.4
. If its length is infinite, we are done.

If, on the other hand, the
d
-length of

is finite then, since

is complete, it converges to, say,
x
. Since
d(x_{n+1},x_{n})\to 0
, we have by (

) that

\sup d(S(x_{n}),x_{n})\to 0,\text{ as }n\to\infty.

If we assume that
y\in S(x)
then

by (

). By
\left(\widehat{\ast_{1}}\right)
at

we have a subsequence

and

such that

, as

. Then eventually

\sup d(S(x_{n_{k}}),x_{n_{k}})\geq d(y_{k},x_{n_{k}})>d(y,x)/2>0,

If we want to repeat the proof of Theorem
3.9

, which would be:

Theorem 4.3

Let
(X,d)

x_{0}\in X

(a) There is an

Let

(18)

Ekeland Theorem is the only statement we will repeat from the previous section, because of its prominence.

Theorem 4.4
.

(Ekeland)

Let
(X,d)
be a complete metric space.
If
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is proper, lower semicontinuous, and bounded below, and
x_{0}\in\mathop{\rm dom}f
, then for each
\lambda>0
there exists
x_{\lambda}
such that

\lambda d(x_{\lambda},x_{0})\leq f(x_{0})-f(x_{\lambda}),

(19)

and

f(x)+\lambda d(x_{\lambda},x)>f(x_{\lambda}),\quad\forall x\neq x_{\lambda}.

(20)

Proof.

Fix
x_{0}\in\mathop{\rm dom}f
and
\lambda>0
. Define

S(x):=\{y:\ f(y)\leq f(x)-\lambda d(y,x)\}.

Because
f
is lower semicontinuous,
S(x)
is closed for all
x\in X
. It is easy to check that
S
is idempotent. Indeed, if
y\in S(x)
and
z\in S(y)
this means by definition that

f(z)\leq f(y)-\lambda d(z,y)\leq f(x)-\lambda d(y,x)-\lambda d(z,y)\leq f(x)-\lambda d(z,x),

the latter by triangle inequality.
Next, let
\{x_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
be any finite
S
-orbit starting at
x_{0}
with
n\geq 1
. By definition,
d(x_{i+1},x_{i})\leq\lambda^{-1}(f(x_{i})-f(x_{i+1}))
, and summing these we get

\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}d(x_{i+1},x_{i})\leq\lambda^{-1}(f(x_{0})-f(x_{n})).

(21)

Since the latter is
\leq\lambda^{-1}(f(x_{0})-\inf f)
, we see that each orbit starting at
x_{0}
has a length at most
\lambda^{-1}(f(x_{0})-\inf f)
, that is, (
b
) of Theorem
4.3
is impossible for this
S
. So, there is an
S
-orbit starting at
x_{0}
and ending at
x_{\lambda}
such that
S(x_{\lambda})\subset\{x_{\lambda}\}
. The latter implies (
20
).

Taking, if necessary, a limit as
n\to\infty
in (
21
), and using the lower semicontinuity of
f
and the triangle inequality, we get (
19
).
∎

We conclude this section with a demonstration how LOEV principle can be used for proving other two famous theorems.

Theorem 4.5
.

(Oettli and Théra
[
16
]
)

Let
(X,d)
be a complete metric space. Let

p:X\times X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}

be a generalized pseudometric on
X
, that is
p(x,x)=0
for all
x\in X
, and

p(x,z)\leq p(x,y)+p(y,z),\quad\forall x,y,z\in X,

(22)

which is lower semicontinuous on its second argument and also for some
x_{0}\in X

\inf_{x\in X}p(x_{0},x)>-\infty.

(23)

Denote
A:=\{x\in X:\ p(x_{0},x)+d(x_{0},x)\leq 0\}
.
Let
\Psi\subset X
be such that for each
x\in A\setminus\Psi
there exists
y\in X
such that
y\neq x
and

p(x,y)+d(x,y)\leq 0.

(24)

Then
A\cap\Psi\neq\varnothing
.

Proof.

Here condition (
24
) directly suggests the definition of
S
:

S(x):=\{y:\ p(x,y)+d(x,y)\leq 0\}.

Because
p+d
satisfies the triangle inequality,
S
is idempotent, and because the function
y\to p(x,y)+d(x,y)
is lower semicontinuous,
S(x)
is closed for all
x
. We apply Theorem
4.3
to
S
at
x_{0}
.

If
x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
, where
n\geq 1
, is an
S
-orbit, we have
d(x_{i},x_{i+1})\leq-p(x_{i},x_{i+1})
for all
i=0,\dots,n-1
, so, using (
22
) and (
23
) we get

\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}d(x_{i},x_{i+1})\leq-\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}p(x_{i},x_{i+1})\leq-p(x_{0},x_{n})\leq-\inf_{x\in X}p(x_{0},x)<\infty,

so, (
b
) is impossible. Therefore, there is some
S
-orbit of finite length
\{x_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
ending at some
\bar{x}\in X
such that
S(\bar{x})\subset\{\bar{x}\}
.

Since
x_{0}\in A
, we get by induction that
\{x_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}\subset A
. Since
A
is closed, we have that
\bar{x}\in A
. But then (
24
) shows that
\bar{x}\in\Psi
.
∎

Theorem 4.6
.

(Fabián and Preiss
[
17
]
)

Let
(X,d)
be a complete metric space. Let
I
be an index set. Let
\{p_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be pseudometrics on
X
continuous as functions from
X\times X
to
\mathbb{R}
, and let
\{f_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be lower semicontinuous functions from
X
to
\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
with the following properties:

\exists i_{0}\in I:\quad p_{i_{0}}=d.

(25)

For some fixed

x_{0}\in\bigcap_{i\in I}\mathop{\rm dom}f_{i}

and for the set

\Phi:=\{x:\ p_{i}(x_{0},x)\leq f_{i}(x_{0})-f_{i}(x),\ \forall i\in I\},

it is fulfilled that

\forall x\in\Phi:\ f_{i_{0}}(x)>0,\ \exists y\neq x:\ p_{i}(x,y)\leq f_{i}(x)-f_{i}(y),\ \forall i\in I.

(26)

Then there exists
\bar{x}\in\Phi
such that
f_{i_{0}}(\bar{x})\leq 0
.

Proof.

The lower semicontinuity of
f_{i}
ensures that the set
\Phi
is closed. Also, the
y
in (
26
) is actually in
\Phi
. Indeed,
p_{i}(x_{0},y)\leq p_{i}(x_{0},x)+p_{i}(x,y)\leq(f_{i}(x_{0})-f_{i}(x))+(f_{i}(x)-f_{i}(y))=f_{i}(x_{0})-f_{i}(y)
for all
i\in I
. Thus we can restrict our considerations to the complete metric space
(\Phi,d)
.

Define for
x\in\Phi

S(x):=\{y\in\Phi:\ p_{i}(x,y)\leq f_{i}(x)-f_{i}(y),\ \forall i\in I\}.

It is easy to check as we did above, that
S
is idempotent and has closed values. Clearly, for each finite
S
-orbit
\{x_{j}\}_{j=0}^{n}
with
n\geq 1
, using that
p_{i_{0}}=d
, we have that

\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}d(x_{j},x_{j+1})\leq f_{i_{0}}(x_{0})-f_{i_{0}}(x_{n}),

so if there is an infinite length
S
-orbit starting at
x_{0}
then
f_{i_{0}}
will become negative at some point. If, on the other hand, some finite length
S
-orbit ends at
\bar{x}
such that
S(\bar{x})\subset\{\bar{x}\}
, then (
26
) shows that
f_{i_{0}}(\bar{x})\leq 0
.
∎

5
Application: perturbed minimization on a
G_{\delta}
set

This section is inspired by certain classical results of Čoban, Kenderov and Revalski, see e.g.
[
18
]
. Those concern the question of when it is possible to achieve minimization by perturbing with a continuous function. Actually the focus is on when the latter can be achieved by a generic set of continuous functions, but we leave this aspect aside and rather construct something more explicit in a special case.

Let
(X,d)
be a complete metric space and let
Y\subset X
be nonempty and
G_{\delta}
:

Y=\bigcap_{i=1}^{\infty}U_{n},\text{ where }U_{n}\text{'s are open.}

Since Ekeland Theorem is equivalent to completeness, unless
Y
is closed, it is pointless to search for a Lipschitz minimizing perturbation in general, but finding – for a given proper and bounded below lower semicontinuous function
f:Y\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
– a continuous function
g
with a small uniform norm and such that
f+g
attains minimum is easy. Indeed, one can just pick an
\varepsilon
-minimum to
f
and use the modulus of semicontinuity of
f
at that point to construct
g
. However, in this construction the continuity of
g
at the minimum point will depend on the function
f
. Instead, we will provide below a premetric
g
on
Y
, such that
(Y,\tau_{d},g)
is a
\Sigma_{g}
-complete premetric space and use Theorem
3.9
. In this way the local continuity of the perturbing function depends only on the local geometry of the set
Y
, but not on the specific function to be perturbed. Here are the details.

First, by considering instead of
d
the equivalent metric
(x,y)\to d(x,y)/(1+d(x,y))
we may and do assume that the metric is bounded. We do this in order to have a bounded perturbation. Next, set

F_{n}:=X\setminus U_{n}.

Define for
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in U_{n}

\varphi_{n}(x):=\frac{1}{d(x,F_{n})}.

Obviously,
\varphi_{n}
is continuous on
U_{n}\supset Y
.
Define for
x,y\in Y

g(x,y):=d(x,y)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{|\varphi_{n}(x)-\varphi_{n}(y)|}{2^{n}(1+|\varphi_{n}(x)-\varphi_{n}(y)|)}.

(27)

As an uniform limit of continuous functions,
g
is continuous. So it satisfies (P2) from Section
3
, while (P1) is obvious. Thus,
g
is a premetric on
Y
. Obviously,
g
is also bounded.

To prove that
(Y,\tau_{d},g)
is a
\Sigma_{g}
-complete premetric space we need two preliminary lemmas.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
a_{i}\geq 0
. Then

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{i}}{1+a_{i}}<\infty\iff\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}<\infty.

Proof.

Since
a_{i}/(1+a_{i})\leq a_{i}
, the
(\Leftarrow)
part is clear.

If
\sum a_{i}/(1+a_{i})<\infty
, then eventually
a_{i}/(1+a_{i})<1/2
, which means that
a_{i}<1
, and, therefore,
a_{i}/(1+a_{i})>a_{i}/2
for all but finitely many
i
’s, so
\sum a_{i}<\infty
.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
K>0
the level set

L_{n,K}:=\{x\in U_{n}:\ \varphi_{n}(x)\leq K\}

is a closed subset of
X
.

Proof.

Fix
n
and
K
and let
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}\subset L_{n,K}
be such that
x_{i}\to x
as
i\to\infty
. If we assume that
x\in F_{n}
, then
d(x_{i},F_{n})\leq d(x_{i},x)\to 0
, as
i\to\infty
, so
\varphi_{n}(x_{i})=1/d(x_{i},F_{n})\to\infty
, as
i\to\infty
, which contradicts
x_{i}\in L_{n,K}
, that is,
\varphi_{n}(x_{i})\leq K
, for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
.

So,
x\in U_{n}
, and, since
\varphi_{n}
is continuous at
x
, we have that
\varphi_{n}(x)\leq K
.
∎

Now we can prove the promised statement.

Proposition 5.3
.

(Y,\tau_{d},g)
is a
\Sigma_{g}
-complete premetric space.

Proof.

Let
\{x_{i}\}_{i\geq 0}
be such sequence that

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}g(x_{i+1},x_{i})<\infty.

By (
27
) we get

\displaystyle\infty

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\left(d(x_{i+1},x_{i})+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{|\varphi_{n}(x_{i+1})-\varphi_{n}(x_{i})|}{2^{n}(1+|\varphi_{n}(x_{i+1})-\varphi_{n}(x_{i})|)}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}d(x_{i+1},x_{i})+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(2^{-n}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{|\varphi_{n}(x_{i+1})-\varphi_{n}(x_{i})|}{1+|\varphi_{n}(x_{i+1})-\varphi_{n}(x_{i})|}\right).

In particular,
\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}d(x_{i+1},x_{i})<\infty
, so
\{x_{i}\}_{i\geq 0}
is a Cauchy sequence, thus
x_{i}\to x\in X
, as
i\to\infty
. We have to show that
x\in Y
.

For each fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have from the above inequality

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{|\varphi_{n}(x_{i})-\varphi_{n}(x_{i+1})|}{1+|\varphi_{n}(x_{i})-\varphi_{n}(x_{i+1})|}<\infty,

so, Lemma
5.1
gives

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}|\varphi_{n}(x_{i})-\varphi_{n}(x_{i+1})|<\infty,

which means that the sequence
\{\varphi_{n}(x_{i})\}_{i\geq 0}
is bounded by, say,
K
. In other words, the sequence
\{x_{i}\}_{i\geq 0}
is fully contained in the closed level set
L_{n,K}
, see Lemma
5.2
. But then its limit point
x
is also in
L_{n,K}\subset U_{n}
, implying that
x\in U_{n}
. Since
n
was arbitrary,
x\in Y
.
∎

By applying Theorem
3.9
, we immediately get our perturbation.

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
(X,d)
be a complete metric space and
Y\subset X
be a nonempty
G_{\delta}
set. Let
g
be given by (
27
). Let
f:Y\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be proper and bounded below lower semicontinuous function. Then for each
\varepsilon>0
there is
x_{\varepsilon}\in Y
such that

f(x)+\varepsilon g(x_{\varepsilon},x)\geq f(x_{\varepsilon}),\quad\forall x\in Y.
