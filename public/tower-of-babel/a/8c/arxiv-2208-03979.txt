A correlatively sparse Lagrange multiplier expression relaxation for polynomial optimization
Zheng Qu, Xindong Tang

Abstract
In this paper, we consider polynomial optimization with correlative sparsity. We construct correlatively sparse Lagrange multiplier expressions (CS-LMEs) and propose CS-LME reformulations for polynomial optimization problems using the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker optimality conditions. Correlatively sparse sum-of-squares (CS-SOS) relaxations are applied to solve the CS-LME reformulation. We show that the CS-LME reformulation inherits the original correlative sparsity pattern, and the CS-SOS relaxation provides sharper lower bounds when applied to the CS-LME reformulation, compared with when it is applied to the original problem. Moreover, the convergence of our approach is guaranteed under mild conditions. In numerical experiments, our new approach usually finds the global optimal value (up to a negligible error) with a low relaxation order for cases where directly solving the problem fails to get an accurate approximation. Also, by properly exploiting the correlative sparsity, our CS-LME approach requires less computational time than the original LME approach to reach the same accuracy level.

polynomial optimization, correlative sparsity, Lagrange multiplier expressions, Moment-SOS relaxations

Introduction

Let
n
be a positive integer, and let
x:=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
be the variable in the
n
-dimensional Euclidean space.
Denote by
\mathbb{R}[x]
be the ring of real coefficient polynomials in
n
indeterminates.
We consider the polynomial optimization problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}&\displaystyle\min_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}&f(x)\\
&\mathrm{s.t.}&g(x)\geq 0,\quad h(x)=0.\end{array}\right.

(1)

In the above,
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]
is a polynomial,
and
g\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]^{m}
and
h\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]^{\ell}
are tuples of polynomial functions.
In
[
10
]
, Lasserre introduced a hierarchy of semidefinite programming (SDP) relaxations to provide a sequence of lower bounds for (
1
), which converges to the global optimal value of (
1
), under some compactness assumptions. This approach is known as
the Moment-SOS relaxations
and has been intensively explored in the last two decades for global solutions of polynomial optimization problems.
For (
1
), Nie introduced the
Lagrange multiplier expressions
(LMEs)
[
23
]
,
whose existence is guaranteed when
g(x)
and
h(x)
are given by generic polynomial functions.
LMEs can be applied to construct the
LME reformulation
of (
1
) using the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) optimality conditions,
which guarantees the moment relaxation being exact when the relaxation order is big enough and the global minimum for (
1
) is attainable.
However, these approaches are usually computationally expensive.
Indeed, even for unconstrained polynomial optimization problems, i.e.,
m=\ell=0
,
the moment relaxation for (
1
) is an SDP problem with matrices of size up to
\binom{n+d}{n}\times\binom{n+d}{n}
, where
d\in\mathbb{N}
is the relaxation order such that
2d\geq{\mathrm{deg}}(f)
.

Given the polynomial optimization problem (
1
),
let
(\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{s})
be subsets of
[n]:=\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that
\bigcup_{i=1}^{s}\mathcal{I}_{i}=[n]
,
and denote
x^{(i)}:=(x_{j})_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{i}}
.
Equation (
1
) is said to follow the
correlative sparsity pattern
(csp)
(\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{s})
if

(1)

there exist
f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{s}
such that every
f_{i}\in\mathbb{R}[x^{(i)}]
and
f(x)=f_{1}(x^{(1)})+\dots+f_{s}(x^{(s)})
;

(2)

there exist partitions
I=(I_{1},\dots,I_{s})
of
[m]
and
E=(E_{1},\dots,E_{s})
of
[\ell]
, such that for all
i\in[s]
,
we have
g_{j_{1}}\in\mathbb{R}[x^{(i)}]
and
h_{j_{2}}\in\mathbb{R}[x^{(i)}]
for every
j_{1}\in I_{i}
and
j_{2}\in E_{i}
.

For convenience, we let
m_{i}:=|I_{i}|
and
\ell_{i}:=|E_{i}|
,
and denote

g^{(i)}\,\coloneqq\,\left(g_{j}:j\in I_{i}\right),\quad h^{(i)}\,\coloneqq\,\left(h_{j}:j\in E_{i}\right).

Then, both
g^{(i)}
and
h^{(i)}
are subsets of
\mathbb{R}[x^{(i)}]
, and the polynomial optimization (
1
) with csp
(\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{s})
can be written in the following way:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}&\min\limits_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}&f_{1}(x^{(1)})+f_{2}(x^{(2)})+\dots+f_{s}(x^{(s)})\\
&\kern 5.0pt\mathrm{s.t.}&g^{(1)}(x^{(1)})\geq 0,\ldots,g^{(s)}(x^{(s)})\geq 0,\\
&&h^{(1)}(x^{(1)})=0,\ldots,h^{(s)}(x^{(s)})=0.\end{array}\right.

(2)

In this paper, we are interested in problems with csp
\{{\mathcal{I}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathcal{I}}_{s}\}
that satisfies the
running intersection property
(RIP),
meaning that for each
1\leq i\leq s-1
,
{\mathcal{I}}_{i+1}\cap\left({\mathcal{I}}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup{\mathcal{I}}_{i}\right)\subset{\mathcal{I}}_{t}
for some
t\in\{1,\ldots,i\}
; see
Definition

2
.
The Moment-SOS relaxation with correlative sparsity is studied in
[
34
]
,
and the convergence results are proved in
[
5
,
9
,
11
,
25
]
for the case when the RIP holds.
Recently, Magron and Wang developed the software
TSSOS

[
15
]
that implements correlative and term sparse SOS relaxations for polynomial optimization (see also
[
16
,
36
,
37
]
),
and it has been used in many applications
[
17
,
35
]
.

Note that for any polynomial optimization problem,
the trivial csp, i.e.,
s=1
with
\mathcal{I}_{1}=[n]
, always exists.
Our primary interests lie in the cases where
n
is much bigger than
\max_{i\in[s]}|\mathcal{I}_{i}|
.
For polynomial optimization (
1
) with the given csp,
we aim to construct reformulations similar to Nie’s LME reformulation introduced in
[
23
]
, while maintaining the correlative sparsity of (
1
).
Our main contributions are as follows:

•

For polynomial optimization with the given csp,
we provide a systematic way to construct correlatively sparse LMEs (CS-LMEs),
which are polynomial functions in
x
and some auxiliary variables.

•

Based on CS-LMEs, we proposed correlatively sparse reformulations using the KKT optimality conditions.
We show that under some general conditions, the reformulation inherits the csp and the RIP from the original polynomial optimization,
and their optimal values are identical.

•

We show that for a given relaxation order, correlatively sparse SOS (CS-SOS) relaxations always provide tighter lower bounds for the optimal value of the polynomial optimization problem when the CS-LME reformulation is applied.
The asymptotic convergence of our approach is proved under some standard assumptions.
Numerical experiments are given to show the superiority of our CS-LME approach.

Some preliminaries for polynomial optimization and Lagrange multiplier expressions are given in

.
In
Section

3
, CS-LMEs are studied, and reformulations based on CS-LMEs are proposed.

Section

Numerical experiments are presented in
Section

Section

Appendix

2.1
Notation and definitions

Let

When the dimension is clear, we use

Given two vectors

,
we denote by

and

, and

. For

and

the subvector formed by the elements of

to

.

Let

be a tuple of variables. Denote by
{\mathbb{R}}[z]
the ring of polynomials in variables

with real coefficients,
and let
{\mathbb{R}}[z]^{r\times k}
(resp.,

) be the set of all

matrices (resp.,
r
-dimensional vectors) whose entries are polynomials in

. For a polynomial

, denote by
{\mathrm{deg}}(p)

.
For an integer

, let
{\mathbb{R}}[z]_{d}
be the
{\mathbb{R}}
-vector space of real polynomials in
r
variables of degrees at most
d
. A polynomial

is a sum-of-squares (SOS) if there exist
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}[z]

.
Denote by
\Sigma[z]
the set of SOS polynomials in
z
, and let
\Sigma[z]_{d}:=\Sigma[z]\cap{\mathbb{R}}[z]_{d}
.
For
p\in{\mathbb{R}}[z]
and

, we define

p\cdot{\mathcal{R}}\,:=\,\{p\cdot q:q\in{\mathcal{R}}\}

\mathcal{R}+\mathcal{S}\,\coloneqq\,\{r+s:r\in\mathcal{R},\,s\in\mathcal{S}\}.

Given a tuple

, the
quadratic module
of
{\mathbb{R}}[z]
generated by
g
is the set

{\operatorname{Qmod}}(g):=\Sigma[z]+g_{1}\cdot\Sigma[z]+\dots+g_{m}\cdot\Sigma[z],

(3)

and the
2d
th truncation of
{\operatorname{Qmod}}(g)
is the set

{\operatorname{Qmod}}(g)_{2d}:=\Sigma[z]_{2d}+g_{1}\cdot\Sigma[z]_{2d-{\mathrm{deg}}(g_{1})}+\dots+g_{m}\cdot\Sigma[z]_{2d-{\mathrm{deg}}(g_{m})}.

(4)

For a tuple
h=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{\ell})\subset{\mathbb{R}}[z]
, the

of
{\mathbb{R}}[z]
generated by

is the set

{\operatorname{Ideal}}(h):=h_{1}\cdot{\mathbb{R}}[z]+\dots+h_{\ell}\cdot{\mathbb{R}}[z],

and the

th truncation of

{\operatorname{Ideal}}(h)_{2d}:=h_{1}\cdot{\mathbb{R}}[z]_{2d-{\mathrm{deg}}(h_{1})}+\dots+h_{\ell}\cdot{\mathbb{R}}[z]_{2d-{\mathrm{deg}}(h_{\ell})}.

For two polynomial tuples

and
g
,
denote

{\operatorname{IQ}}(h,g)\,\coloneqq\,{\operatorname{Ideal}}(h)+{\operatorname{Qmod}}(g),\quad{\operatorname{IQ}}(h,g)_{2d}\,\coloneqq\,{\operatorname{Ideal}}(h)_{2d}+{\operatorname{Qmod}}(g)_{2d}.

(5)

Then, it is clear that every polynomial
p\in{\operatorname{IQ}}(h,g)\subseteq\mathbb{R}[z]
is nonnegative over the set
{\mathcal{K}}:=\{z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r}:h(z)=0,\kern 5.0ptg(z)\geq 0\}
.
Conversely,
when
{\operatorname{IQ}}(h,g)
is
archimedean
, i.e., when there exists
p\in{\operatorname{IQ}}(h,g)
such that
\{z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r}:p(z)\geq 0\}
is compact (see
[
12
]
),
all positive polynomials over

are in
{\operatorname{IQ}}(h,g)
.
This result is referred to as Putinar’s Positivstellensatz
[
32
]
.
Moreover, when
h=0
has finitely many real roots,
or when some general optimality conditions hold,
a polynomial
f\in\mathbb{R}[z]
is nonnegative over
{\mathcal{K}}
if and only if
f\in{\operatorname{IQ}}(h,g)_{2d}
for all
d
that is sufficiently large (see
[
19
,
21
]
).

Throughout this paper,
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
is the tuple of
n
variables.
Given the csp
({\mathcal{I}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathcal{I}}_{s})
, for each
i\in[s]
, we fix a certain ordering for elements in
{\mathcal{I}}_{i}
and denote by
x^{(i)}
the tuple of variables
(x_{k}:k\in{\mathcal{I}}_{i})
.
The

th variable of
x^{(i)}
, denoted by

, corresponds to the variable

if

is the order of

in
{\mathcal{I}}_{i}
.
For example, if
{\mathcal{I}}_{1}
is ordered as
(1,3,5,6)
, then

.
For polynomial
p\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]
, denote by
\nabla p\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]^{n}
the gradient of

and

\nabla_{i}p\,\coloneqq\,\left[\begin{array}[]{lll}\frac{\partial p}{\partial x^{(i)}_{1}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial p}{\partial x^{(i)}_{n_{i}}}\end{array}\right]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}[x]^{n_{i}}.

(6)

When the dimension of the ambient space is clear,
we use
e_{i}
to denote the
i
th standard basis vector whose
i
th entry is
1
while all other entries are zeros.
For
k\in{\mathcal{I}}_{i}
, denote

e^{(i)}_{k}\,\coloneqq\,e_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}},

where

.

2.2
Moment-SOS relaxation

Denote by
f_{\min}
the optimal value of the polynomial optimization problem (
1
).
Denote by
\mathcal{K}
the feasible set of (
1
), i.e.,
\mathcal{K}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:h(x)=0,\ g(x)\geq 0\}
.
Then finding the global minimum of (

) is equivalent to

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}&\displaystyle\max&\gamma\\
&\kern 5.0pt\mathrm{s.t.}&f-\gamma\in\mathcal{P}_{d_{0}}(\mathcal{K}).\end{array}\right.

(8)

In the above,
d_{0}
is the degree of
f
, and
\mathcal{P}_{d_{0}}(\mathcal{K})
is the cone of nonnegative polynomials over

with degrees not greater than

.
A computationally tractable relaxation for (

Given the relaxation order

such that
2d\geq\max\{{\mathrm{deg}}(f),{\mathrm{deg}}(g),{\mathrm{deg}}(h)\}
,
the
d

) (and (

)) is

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}&\displaystyle\max&\gamma\\
&\kern 5.0pt\mathrm{s.t.}&f-\gamma\in{\operatorname{IQ}}(h,g)_{2d}.\end{array}\right.

(9)

Its dual problem corresponds to the so-called
d
th order moment relaxation of (
1
),
and this primal-dual pair is referred to as the Moment-SOS relaxation.
Both (
9
) and its dual problems can be written as SDP problems.
We refer to
[
6
,
10
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
20
,
22
,
24
]
for more references about polynomial optimization and moment problems.

For a relaxation order

denote by
\theta_{d}
the optimal value of (

.
Convergence of the Moment-SOS relaxation relies on Putinar’s Positivestellenstaz

]
.

Theorem 1
(
[

]
).

If

is archimedean, then

\lim_{d\rightarrow+\infty}\theta_{d}=f_{\min}.

We would like to remark that under some conditions,
the Moment-SOS relaxations have finite convergence,

\theta_{d}=f_{\min}
for all

that is big enough.
We refer to
[
2
,
3
,
8
,

]

The Moment-SOS relaxations have been implemented in the software

[

]
.
In this paper, we also call Moment-SOS relaxations ”dense relaxations” or ”dense SOS relaxations” to distinguish them from SOS relaxations exploiting the sparsity.

Correlatively sparse SOS relaxation

Let us
consider the problem (
2
) with csp
({\mathcal{I}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathcal{I}}_{s})
.
For polynomial tuples

, we
denote by

{\operatorname{IQ}}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{i}}(h^{(i)},g^{(i)})

the set given by (

.
To exploit the correlative sparsity of problem (

), we consider the following relaxation for problem (

):

\qquad\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}&\displaystyle\max&\gamma\\
&\kern 5.0pt\mathrm{s.t.}&f-\gamma\in\displaystyle{\operatorname{IQ}}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{1}}\left(h^{(1)},g^{(1)}\right)_{2d}+\dots+{\operatorname{IQ}}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{s}}\left(h^{(s)},g^{(s)}\right)_{2d}.\end{array}\right.

(10)

We refer to (

) as the

th order

of (
2
)
[

,

,

,

]
,
and denote its optimal value by

.
To demonstrate the convergence results for CS-SOS relaxations, we need the following property of csps.

Definition 2
.

We say that the csp
({\mathcal{I}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathcal{I}}_{s})
satisfies the RIP if for every
i\in[s-1]
, there exists

such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{i+1}\bigcap\bigcup_{j=1}^{i}\mathcal{I}_{j}\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{t}.

(11)

Convergence of the CS-SOS relaxation is derived from the following sparse version of Putinar’s Positivestellenstaz.

(
[
5
,
9
,
11
]
).

Suppose

({\mathcal{I}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathcal{I}}_{s})
satisfies the RIP property, and
{\operatorname{IQ}}_{{{\mathcal{I}}_{i}}}(g^{(i)},h^{(i)})
is archimedean for each

.
If

is positive on the semialgebraic set

, then

Therefore, under the same conditions as that in

, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{d\rightarrow+\infty}\rho_{d}=f_{\min}.

(12)

Aside from the correlative sparsity,

]
.
Since this paper mainly concerns correlative sparsity,
we refer to

]

]

2.4
Optimality conditions and Lagrange multiplier expressions

For the polynomial optimization problem (
1
),
the KKT conditions can be described by the following polynomial system in
(x,\lambda)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+m+\ell}
:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\nabla f(x)=\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}\nabla g_{j}(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\lambda_{m+j}\nabla h_{j}(x),\\
h(x)=0,\ 0\leq\lambda_{1:m}\perp g(x)\geq 0.\end{array}\right.

The pair
(x,\lambda)
satisfying (
2.4
) is called a KKT pair,
and the first component
x
of a KKT pair is called a KKT point of (
1
).
Under some constraint qualification conditions,
every minimizer of (
1
), if it exists, must be a KKT point.
In this case,
minimizing
f
over the KKT system (
2.4
) returns the same optimal value and optimal solutions as the original problem (
1
).
Moreover, conditions guaranteeing the convergence of the dense SOS relaxations are milder for the minimization over the KKT ideal than that for the original problem (
1
).
In particular, convergence can still occur even when the semialgebraic set given by (
2.4
) is noncompact
[
4
,
26
]
.

A drawback, however, of working on the KKT system (
2.4
) rather than on the original feasible region
{\mathcal{K}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is the augmentation of the number of variables from
n
to
n+m+\ell
,
which causes a significant increase on the computational cost.
To deal with this undesired complexity growth,
Nie
[
23
]
proposed polynomial Lagrange multipliers expressions.
For the polynomial optimization problem (
1
),
let
{\hat{m}}:=m+\ell
,
and let
c:=(c_{1},\dots,c_{\hat{m}})
be an enumeration for the constraining pair
(g,h)
.
We denote

{G}(x):=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccccc}\nabla c_{1}(x)&\nabla c_{2}(x)&\cdots&\nabla c_{{\hat{m}}}(x)\\
c_{1}(x)&0&\cdots&0\\
0&c_{2}(x)&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&c_{{\hat{m}}}(x)\\
\end{array}\right],\quad\mathbf{f}(x):=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\nabla f(x)\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0\end{array}\right].

(15)

Then, the following equation holds at every KKT pair
(x,\lambda)
:

\displaystyle{G}(x)\cdot\lambda=\mathbf{f}(x).

(16)

If there exists a matrix of polynomials
L\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]^{{\hat{m}}\times n}
and
D\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]^{{\hat{m}}\times{\hat{m}}}
such that

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}L(x)&D(x)\end{array}\right]{G}(x)={\bf{I}}_{{\hat{m}}},\kern 5.0pt\forall x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},

then the Lagrange multipliers
\lambda
can be expressed as polynomials in
x
:

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\lambda_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\lambda_{{\hat{m}}}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}p_{1}(x)\\
\vdots\\
p_{{\hat{m}}}(x)\end{array}\right]:=L(x)\nabla f(x).

(18)

The polynomial vector
p(x):=(p_{1}(x),\ldots,p_{{\hat{m}}}(x))
is called the
Lagrange multiplier expression
(LME).
Denote

c_{eq}(x):=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\nabla f(x)-\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{j}(x)\nabla g_{j}(x)-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}p_{m+j}(x)\nabla h_{j}(x)\\
h(x)\\
p_{1:m}(x)\circ g(x)\end{array}\right],

and

c_{in}(x):=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}p_{1:m}(x)\\
g(x)\end{array}\right].

Then,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a KKT point if and only if
x
satisfies
c_{eq}(x)=0,\ c_{in}(x)\geq 0.

Based on the LME (
18
), Nie
[
23
]
proposed the following reformulation of (
1
):

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}&\displaystyle\min_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}&f(x)\\
&\kern 5.0pt\mathrm{s.t.}&c_{eq}(x)=0,\quad c_{in}(x)\geq 0\end{array}\right.

(19)

It is clear that when the minimum of (
1
) is attained at some KKT points,
the optimal values of (
1
) and (
19
) are identical.
In fact, the existence of LMEs guarantees that every minimizer of (
1
), if it exists, must be a KKT point
[
23
, Proposition 5.1]
,
thus solving (
1
) is equivalent to solving the reformulation (
19
).
When Moment-SOS relaxations are applied,
finite convergence is guaranteed under some generic conditions:

Theorem 4
.

[
23
, Theorem 3.3]

Suppose LMEs exist and (
19
) has a nonempty feasible set.
Denote by
\theta_{d}
the optimal value of the

3
Correlatively sparse LMEs and reformulations

We consider the polynomial optimization problem (
2
) with the csp
({\mathcal{I}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathcal{I}}_{s})
satisfying the RIP.
For
i\in[s]

the polynomial matrix

given in (

) associated with

\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\

\end{array}\right].

(24)

As mentioned in Section
2.4
,
one can reformulate polynomial optimization problems with LMEs,
from which the Moment-SOS relaxation gives a tighter lower bound for the polynomial optimization.
To apply LMEs,
the KKT system of (
2
) corresponds to the following semialgebraic set on
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
\lambda^{(1)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\hat{m}_{1}},\ldots,\lambda^{(s)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\hat{m}_{s}}

\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\nabla f_{1}(x)+\dots+\nabla f_{s}(x)=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{s}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\lambda^{(i)}_{j}\nabla g^{(i)}_{j}(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\lambda^{(i)}_{m_{i}+j}\nabla h^{(i)}_{j}(x)\right),\\

0\leq\lambda_{1:m_{i}}^{(i)}\perp g^{(i)}(x)\geq 0,\kern 5.0pti\in[s].\end{array}\right.

(25)

we additionally assume that the nonsingularity condition holds for the constraining pair
(g^{(i)},h^{(i)})
within every
{\mathcal{I}}_{i}
.
That is:

Assumption 1
.

For each
i\in[s]
, there exist polynomial matrices
L^{(i)}(x^{(i)})\in{\mathbb{R}}[x^{(i)}]^{\hat{m}_{i}\times n_{i}}
and

\left[\begin{array}[]{ll}L^{(i)}(x^{(i)})&D^{(i)}(x^{(i)})\end{array}\right]{G}^{(i)}(x^{(i)})={\bf{I}}_{\hat{m}_{i}}.

(26)

By
[
23
, Proposition 5.2]
,
(
26

{G}^{(i)}(x^{(i)})
have a full column rank for all

.
For such cases, we say the pair
(g^{(i)},h^{(i)})
is
nonsingular
.
This is satisfied if all polynomials in
g^{(i)}
and
h^{(i)}
are generic polynomials in
x^{(i)}
.

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}

\displaystyle=L^{(2)}(x_{2},x_{3})\left(\nabla_{2}f_{2}(x_{2},x_{3})-\begin{bmatrix}\nu\\

To summarize,
the LME approach proposed by Nie

allows for tightening the classical Moment-SOS relaxation by incorporating necessary polynomial constraints, provided that certain nonsingularity conditions hold.
However, usually this approach cannot keep the csp from the original polynomial optimization problem.

In contrast, one can try to find the CS-LME instead by adding some new variables.
In the above, we demonstrate how to find CS-LMEs for the two-block cases.
In the next subsection, we provide a systematic way to construct CS-LMEs for an arbitrary number of blocks.

Remark 22
.

The archimedean condition of

3
3

3

It is important to note that there are two ways to replicate existing constraints.
For the constraint

we may add its replication to

and obtain a new constraining tuple

We refer to

) is

N+2k.

In contrast,
the maximal clique size in the original LME reformulation (

(N-k)\left\lfloor\frac{N-1}{N-k}\right\rfloor+N.

We give in

Remark 25
.

To end this section, we would like to point out that for small-scale problems, the LME approach has outstanding performance, especially when the SOS approach cannot find the global minimum with a low relaxation order; see
[
23
]
.
For small-scale problems with csp, the LME approach may still be faster than the CS-LME approach because the latter needs to add auxiliary variables to maintain the csp. See
Example

26
for a numerical example of a small-scale problem.

In general, we expect CS-LME to perform better than the other three approaches when the cliques in the csp graph of the CS-LME reformulation are not much larger than that of the LME reformulation. Since

|\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{i}|=|{\mathcal{I}}_{i}|+|{\mathcal{J}}_{i}|
, this occurs when

1.

the number of overlapping variables between any two blocks
{\mathcal{I}}_{i}
and
{\mathcal{I}}_{j}
is small;

2.

each node in the directed clique tree
G(V,A)
returned by
Algorithm

1
has a small number of children.

These two conditions ensure that only a small number of auxiliary variables
|{\mathcal{J}}_{i}|
must be added to each block.

Conclusions and discussions

We consider correlatively sparse polynomial optimization problems.
We introduce CS-LMEs to construct CS-LME reformations for polynomial optimization problems.
Under some general assumptions, we show that correlative SOS relaxations can get tighter lower bounds when solving the CS-LME reformulation instead of the original optimization problem.
Moreover, asymptotic convergence is guaranteed if the sequel of CS-SOS relaxations for the original polynomial optimization is convergent.
Numerical examples are presented to show the superiority of our new approach.

For future work, one wonders if the CS-SOS relaxation has finite convergence for solving CS-LME reformulations. Indeed, finite convergence for the original LME reformulation in
[
23
]
is guaranteed under mild conditions.
As demonstrated in Section
5
, the CS-LME approach usually finds the global minimum (up to a negligible numerical error) for polynomial optimization problems with a low relaxation order.
However, it is still open that if the finite convergence is guaranteed theoretically or not, even for generic cases.
Moreover, when the correlatively sparse polynomial optimization (
2
) is given by generic polynomials,
its KKT ideal is zero-dimensional. Thus the real variety given by equality constraints in (
63
) is a finite set.
For the classical Moment-SOS relaxations,
finite convergence is theoretically guaranteed when equality constraints of the polynomial optimization give a zero-dimensional real variety,
as shown in
[
19
]
.
So, it is interesting to ask whether the analogous is true for CS-SOS relaxations.
Besides that, our numerical experiments indicate that the CS-LME approach can usually find the global minimum for polynomial optimization problems even if some
{\operatorname{IQ}}_{{\mathcal{I}}^{(i)}}(h^{(i)},g^{(i)})
is not archimedean.
Therefore, an interesting question is whether the CS-LME approach has guaranteed asymptotic or finite convergence without the archimedean condition for every
{\operatorname{IQ}}_{{\mathcal{I}}^{(i)}}(h^{(i)},g^{(i)})
.

At last, we would like to remark that LMEs have broad applications in many polynomial-defined problems.
Therefore, a natural question is how to apply CS-LMEs to these applications.
For example, when a saddle point problem is given by polynomials with correlative sparsity, can we apply CS-LMEs to construct polynomial optimization reformulation similar to the one in
[
31
]
for finding saddle points?

Appendix A
Computing LMEs and CS-LMEs

We introduce how to find LMEs and CS-LMEs for practical implementation.
As mentioned in
Section

2.4
and
Section

3
, finding LMEs (resp., CS-LMEs) is equivalent to finding matrices of polynomials
L(x),\,D(x)
(resp.,
L^{(i)}(x),\,D^{(i)}(x)
) such that (
2.4
) (resp., (
26
)) holds.
Note that the matrices
{G}(x)
and
{G}^{(i)}(x)
only depend on constraints,
and LMEs can be viewed as special cases of CS-LMEs that there only exists one block, i.e.,
s=1
.
Here we only introduce how to get CS-LMEs, and the methodology for finding LMEs is similar.

Suppose the matrix of polynomial
{G}^{(i)}(x^{(i)})
has full column rank over
\mathbb{C}^{n_{i}}
.
In general, (
26
) gives a linear equation system.
Denote
{\hat{m}}_{i}:=m_{i}+\ell_{i}
, and

L^{(i)}(x^{(i)}):=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}L_{1,1}(x^{(i)})&L_{1,2}(x^{(i)})&\dots&L_{1,n_{i}}(x^{(i)})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
L_{{\hat{m}}_{i},1}(x^{(i)})&L_{{\hat{m}}_{i},2}(x^{(i)})&\dots&L_{{\hat{m}}_{i},n_{i}}(x^{(i)})\end{array}\right],

D^{(i)}(x^{(i)}):=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}D_{1,1}(x^{(i)})&D_{1,2}(x^{(i)})&\dots&D_{1,{\hat{m}}_{i}}(x^{(i)})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
D_{{\hat{m}}_{i},1}(x^{(i)})&D_{{\hat{m}}_{i},2}(x^{(i)})&\dots&D_{{\hat{m}}_{i},{\hat{m}}_{i}}(x^{(i)})\end{array}\right].

Suppose all entries in
L^{(i)}(x^{(i)})
and
D^{(i)}(x^{(i)})
are polynomials whose degrees are not greater than
d
.
For each
j,k
, let (here for the
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n_{i}})\in\mathbb{N}^{n_{i}}_{d}
, we denote
{x^{(i)}}^{\alpha}:={x^{(i)}_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}{x^{(i)}_{2}}^{\alpha_{2}}\dots{x^{(i)}_{n_{i}}}^{\alpha_{n_{i}}}
)

L_{j,k}(x^{(i)})=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n_{i}}_{d}}L_{j,k,\alpha}\cdot{x^{(i)}}^{\alpha},\quad D_{j,k}(x^{(i)})=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n_{i}}_{d}}D_{j,k,\alpha}\cdot{x^{(i)}}^{\alpha}.

(76)

Then (
26
) can be written as the following linear equation system in variables
L_{j,k,\alpha}
and
D_{j,k,\alpha}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{n_{i}}\left(\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n_{i}}_{d}}L_{j,l,\alpha}\cdot{x^{(i)}}^{\alpha}\right)\cdot\frac{\partial c^{(i)}_{k}}{\partial x^{(i)}_{l}}(x^{(i)})+\left(\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n_{i}}_{d}}D_{j,k,\alpha}\cdot{x^{(i)}}^{\alpha}\right)c^{(i)}_{k}(x^{(i)})

(77)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}1\quad\mbox{if}\quad j=k,\\
0\quad\mbox{if}\quad j\neq k,\end{array}\right.\qquad(j\in[{\hat{m}}_{i}],\ k\in[{\hat{m}}_{i}]).

We remark that in (
77
), the equality means that the polynomials on both sides are identically equaled.
By
[
23
, Proposition 5.2]
,
since
{G}^{(i)}(x)
has full column rank over
\mathbb{C}^{n_{i}}
,
the system (
77
) must have solutions when
d
is large enough.
Therefore, for each
i\in[s]
,
we solve the linear system (
77
) for solutions with a given degree
d
.
If we get a solution to (
77
), then we recover polynomial matrices
L^{(i)}(x^{(i)})
and
D^{(i)}(x^{(i)})
(hence CS-LMEs) using this solution;
otherwise, we let
d\leftarrow d+1
and solve (
77
) with the updated degree
d
, until a solution is obtained.

Sometimes, one may get CS-LMEs without actually computing polynomial matrices
L^{(i)}(x^{(i)})
and
D^{(i)}(x^{(i)})
.
Instead, CS-LMEs can be directly obtained using the ‘‘multiplication-cancellation’’ trick

6
6

6

This trick was introduced by Professor Jiawang Nie in his research group discussions. It is also mentioned in Section 6.3 of his new book
Moment and Polynomial Optimization

[
24
]
.
.
This is shown in the following example.

Example 33
.

Consider the case that

g^{(i)}(x^{(i)})=\left(1-{x^{(i)}}^{\top}x^{(i)},\ x^{(i)}_{1},\ldots,x^{(i)}_{n_{i}}\right).

Then the KKT-typed system (
50
) for the
i
th block implies that

\displaystyle F^{(i)}(z^{(i)})=-2\lambda^{(i)}_{1}\cdot x^{(i)}+\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\lambda^{(i)}_{j+1}\cdot e_{j},

(78)

\displaystyle\lambda^{(i)}_{1}\perp 1-{x^{(i)}}^{\top}x^{(i)},\quad\lambda^{(i)}_{j+1}\perp x^{(i)}_{j}\ (j\in[n_{i}]).

(79)

By multiplying
{x^{(i)}}^{\top}
on both sides of (
78
),
we get

{x^{(i)}}^{\top}F^{(i)}(z^{(i)})=-2\lambda^{(i)}_{1}\cdot{x^{(i)}}^{\top}x^{(i)}+\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\lambda^{(i)}_{j+1}\cdot x^{(i)}_{j}.

Note that (
79
) implies that
\lambda^{(i)}_{1}\cdot{x^{(i)}}^{\top}x^{(i)}=\lambda^{(i)}_{1}

and
\lambda^{(i)}_{j+1}\cdot x^{(i)}_{j}=0
.
So we further have

{x^{(i)}}^{\top}F^{(i)}(z^{(i)})=-2\lambda^{(i)}_{1}.

Therefore, again by (
78
), we get CS-LMEs that

\lambda^{(i)}_{1}=-{x^{(i)}}^{\top}F^{(i)}(z^{(i)})/2,\quad\lambda^{(i)}_{j+1}=F^{(i)}_{j}(z^{(i)})+2\lambda^{(i)}_{1}\cdot x^{(i)}_{j}\ (j\in[n_{i}]).

We remark that though we do not get explicit expressions for
L^{(i)}(x^{(i)})
and
D^{(i)}(x^{(i)})
, essentially, this trick is equivalent to finding solutions for (
26
).
For instance, the step of multiplying
{x^{(i)}}^{\top}
on both sides of (
78
) means that the first row of
L^{(i)}(x^{(i)})
is
{x^{(i)}}^{\top}
.
Besides that, for some commonly used constraints (e.g., box, ball, simplex, etc.), LMEs are explicitly given in
[
23
]
,
and they can be similarly applied to the construction of CS-LMEs.
