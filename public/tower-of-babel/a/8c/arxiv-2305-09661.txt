Abstract
Phase retrieval is a prevalent problem in digital signal processing and experimental physics that consists of estimating a complex signal from magnitude measurements. This paper expands the classical phase retrieval framework to electric power systems with unknown network models and limited access to observations of voltage magnitudes , active power injections, and reactive power injections. The proposed method recovers the phase angles and the power-phase angle submatrices of the AC power flow Jacobian matrix. This is made possible by deriving topology and parameter-free expressions for the structural symmetries of the power flow Jacobian that do not depend on the phase angles. These physical laws provide structural constraints for the proposed phase retrieval method. The paper then presents sufficient conditions for guaranteed recovery of the voltage phase angles, which also depend solely on voltage magnitudes, active power injections, and reactive power injections. The method offers two significant benefits: both estimating the voltage phase angles and recovering the power flow Jacobian matrix—a basis for approximating the power flow equations. Simulations on widely studied open-source test networks validate the findings.

Introduction

Flows of active and reactive power in AC electric power systems are directly related to the nodal voltage phase angles. Thus, there is great interest in knowing the phase angles for many network planning, control, and protection applications. To this end, broad efforts have taken place to deploy phasor measurement units (PMUs), which use communication networks and protocols to synchronize measurements of phase angles.

Nonetheless, due to their complexity and cost, PMU deployment remains limited in some regions and grid types, such as in transmission system boundaries, transmission systems in rural environments, and distribution systems. Another method to determine the voltage phase angles is through state estimation. However, conventional state estimation techniques require detailed and complete knowledge of the network topology and model parameters. This is rarely possible in practice
[
1
,
2
]
, particularly for combined transmission and distribution systems. In addition, network models are often inaccurate and difficult to maintain.

Therefore, we propose a method that enables the recovery of the voltage phase angles in electric power systems where the network model is unknown or unavailable. We extend previous works on recovering sensitivity matrices that relate the bus complex injections to bus voltage phasors to show that the structure of the AC power flow Jacobian enables the recovery of the power-to-phase angle sensitivities—and consequently, the phase angles—when neither observations of the phase angles nor the network topology are available.

2.2

Review of the phase retrieval problem

Optimization problems of the form (
1
) are known as
phase retrieval
problems. Classically, the goal of phase retrieval is to recover the phase of a complex signal
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}

given a design matrix
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
and
real-valued magnitude measurements
|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}|=\boldsymbol{b}
, where
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and
|\cdot|
denotes the elementwise magnitudes of a vector. In this paper, we make the noteworthy distinction that the design matrix (the power flow Jacobian) is not assumed to be known, in contrast with the classical approach to phase retrieval.

One of the earliest investigations of this problem was in experimental physics, where
[
5
]
developed an iterative method for recovering the phase of a wave function from the magnitude of its Fourier Transform coefficients, provided by intensity measurements. The authors of
[
5
]
note wide applications for their method in X-ray crystallography, which has been discussed more recently in
[
7
]
. Since then, the phase retrieval problem and its various formulations have seen applications in electron microscopy
[
6
]
; optical and medical imaging
[
7
]
; experimental physics
[
8
,
10
]
; and digital signal processing. We refer the interested reader to
[
10
]
and
[
13
]
for multidisciplinary surveys of the formulations and applications of phase retrieval.

2.2.1

Classical phase retrieval

The classical phase retrieval problem,
where
\boldsymbol{A}
is assumed to be known, (in our case, it will be recovered)
can be understood as a quadratic optimization problem with complex decision variables:

\minimize_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{n},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}}\quad\left|\left|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\right|\right|_{2}^{2}\quad\text{subject to:}\quad|\boldsymbol{y}|=\boldsymbol{b}.

(2)

The introduction of the decision variables
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
in (
2
) is sometimes referred to as “lifting” the magnitude measurements
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
to
\mathbb{C}^{m}
, which allows us to recover the complex vector
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
. It is noted in
[
9
]
that the problem (
2
) can be recast as solving for the complex signal
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and for the phases of the magnitude measurements
\boldsymbol{b}
. The phases are modeled as rectangular complex numbers
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
that are constrained to lie on the unit circle, i.e.,
|u_{i}|=1\ \ \forall i=1,\dots,n
. With this framework, the phase retrieval program (
2
) can be rewritten as

\minimize_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{n},\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}}\quad\left|\left|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{b})\boldsymbol{u}\right|\right|^{2}_{2}\quad\text{subject to:}\quad|\boldsymbol{u}|=\mathds{1}.

(3)

The authors of
[
9
]
note that, for any fixed guess of the phase
\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
of the magnitude measurements
\boldsymbol{b}
, an estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
of the complex signal is unique and is given as

\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}=\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{b})\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}=(\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{A})^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{b})\hat{\boldsymbol{u}},

(4)

where
(\cdot)^{*}
is the complex conjugate transpose and
(\cdot)^{\dagger}
is the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse.

With this in hand, we can substitute
\boldsymbol{x}
with
\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{b})\boldsymbol{u}
, and solve the quadratic program

\minimize_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}:|\boldsymbol{u}|=\mathds{1}}\boldsymbol{u}^{*}\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{u},\ \ \text{subject to:}\ \ \boldsymbol{M}=\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{b})(\mathbf{I}-\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger})\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{b}),

(5)

where
\boldsymbol{M}\succeq 0
. However, the modulus constraints in (
5
) are computationally challenging. They are equivalent to the quadratic equality constraints
\operatorname{Re}\left\{u_{i}\right\}^{2}+\operatorname{Im}\left\{u_{i}\right\}^{2}=1
for all
i
, which produces a non-convex feasible region.

2.2.2

The PhaseCut algorithm

This paper is inspired by a well-known approach to the phase retrieval problem known as
PhaseCut
. The algorithm, developed in
[
9
]
, casts the phase retrieval problem in terms of a semidefinite programming (SDP) relaxation of the maximum cut problem, which finds the maximum number of edges in a network that form a partition of the network into two disjoint subsets. The purpose of PhaseCut essentially boils down to the fact that the intractable modulus constraints in (
5
) can be avoided via a relaxed SDP. This is achieved by modeling the phase
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
of the signal
|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}|
as a rank-one matrix
\boldsymbol{X}\triangleq\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{u}^{*}
. With this matrix decision variable, (
5
) can be written as

\minimize_{\boldsymbol{X}\succeq 0}\operatorname{trc}\left(\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{M}\right),\quad\text{subject to:}\quad\operatorname{rank}(\boldsymbol{X})=1,\quad\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{X})=\mathds{1},

(6)

which is relaxed by dropping the
\operatorname{rank}(\boldsymbol{X})=1
constraint. Although we do not explicitly use this SDP relaxation in this work, its precursor quadratic formulation, (
3
) is central to the forthcoming results.

2.3

Review of Newton-Raphson power flow

2.3.1

Power flow equations

We consider an arbitrary electric power network with
n
“PQ” buses—i.e., buses where the complex power injections are specified. The well-known power flow equations in polar form for buses
i=1,\dots,n
are

\displaystyle p_{i}

\displaystyle=v_{i}\sum_{k=1}^{n}v_{k}(G_{ik}\cos\theta_{ik}+B_{ik}\sin\theta_{ik})=p^{g}_{i}-p^{d}_{i},

(7a)

\displaystyle q_{i}

\displaystyle=v_{i}\sum_{k=1}^{n}v_{k}(G_{ik}\sin\theta_{ik}-B_{ik}\cos\theta_{ik})=q^{g}_{i}-q^{d}_{i},

(7b)

where
\theta_{ik}\triangleq\theta_{i}-\theta_{k}
is the phase angle differences between buses
i,k
,
p^{g}_{i},q^{g}_{i}
are the generated active and reactive powers at bus
i
,
p^{d}_{i},q^{d}_{i}
are the demanded active and reactive powers at bus
i
,
v_{i},v_{k}
are the voltage magnitudes at buses
i,k
, and
G_{ik},B_{ik}\in\mathbb{R}
are the real and imaginary components of the
(i,k)
-th entry
Y_{ik}\triangleq G_{ik}+jB_{ik}\in\mathbb{C}
of the nodal admittance matrix
\boldsymbol{Y}=\boldsymbol{G}+j\boldsymbol{B}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
.

By convention, the state of the system is defined as
\boldsymbol{x}\triangleq[\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T},\boldsymbol{v}^{T}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, where
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are the voltage magnitudes and
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in(-\pi,\pi]^{n}
are the voltage phase angles. The complex voltage at each bus takes the form
\overline{v}_{i}\triangleq v_{i}e^{j\theta_{i}}=v_{i}(\cos(\theta_{i})+j\sin(\theta_{i}))\in\mathbb{C}
. The bus active and reactive power injection vector
\boldsymbol{g}(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{2n}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, i.e., the vectorization of the power flow equations (
7
), is defined as
\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{x})\triangleq[\boldsymbol{p}(\boldsymbol{x})^{T},\boldsymbol{q}(\boldsymbol{x})^{T}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
. The solution
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
of the non-linear system of equations (
7
) satisfies
\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})-\left[(\boldsymbol{p}^{g}-\boldsymbol{p}^{d})^{T},(\boldsymbol{q}^{g}-\boldsymbol{q}^{d})^{T}\right]^{T}=\boldsymbol{0}.

2.3.2

Newton-Raphson power flow in polar coordinates

The Newton-Raphson power flow algorithm is a well-studied iterative approach to solving the non-linear power flow equations (
7
). This method consists of repeatedly solving the linear system of equations

\begin{bmatrix}\Delta\boldsymbol{p}\\
\Delta\boldsymbol{q}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{p}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x})&\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{p}}{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{x})\\
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{q}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x})&\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{q}}{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{x})\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}\\
\Delta\boldsymbol{v}\end{bmatrix}=\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{x})\Delta\boldsymbol{x},

(8)

where—
around
a specified operating point—
\Delta\boldsymbol{p},\Delta\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are small perturbations in the active and reactive power injections respectively, and
\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta},\Delta\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are small perturbations in the voltage phase angles and magnitudes, respectively. The matrix
\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}
in (
8
) is the
power flow Jacobian
, which contains the partial derivatives of the power flow equations (
7
) with respect to the voltage magnitudes
\boldsymbol{v}
and phase angles
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.

In this work, we consider an
estimate
of the power flow Jacobian
\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})
computed at a time point
t
. We use
\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})
to provide an approximation
[
29
, (5.8)]
of the AC power flow equations (
7
) around the operating point
measured
at a time point
t
as

\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t+1})\approx\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})+\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})(\boldsymbol{x}_{t+1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{t}).

(9)

Here, the power flow Jacobian
\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})
denotes the
sensitivity
of the injections to changes in the state from the operating point measured at time
t
. We consider the notion of observed or predicted perturbations in the injections and state around the operating point measured at time point
t
. Denote these perturbations as
\Delta\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})\triangleq\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t+1})-\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})=[\boldsymbol{p}_{t+1}^{T},\boldsymbol{q}_{t+1}^{T}]^{T}-[\boldsymbol{p}_{t}^{T},\boldsymbol{q}_{t}^{T}]^{T}
and
\Delta\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\triangleq\boldsymbol{x}_{t+1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{t}=[\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}^{T},\boldsymbol{v}_{t+1}^{T}]^{T}-[\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}^{T},\boldsymbol{v}_{t}^{T}]^{T}
.

In this paper, the network model is
unknown
, and PMU measurements as in (
Phasors
) are
entirely
unavailable. Thus, we can neither measure nor solve for the phase angles, and the quantities
\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}
,
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{p}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})
, and
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{q}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})
are unknown. We will assume that the power-voltage magnitude sensitivities
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{p}}{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})
and
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{q}}{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})
are known in the sense that they can be estimated from prior measurements, which is a well-studied problem (e.g., see
[
3
,
4
]
).

In this paper, we ask the following question: can we recover the phase angles
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}
as in (
Phasors
) and the power flow Jacobian submatrices
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{p}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}),\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{q}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})
as in (
8
), using only the measurements
\boldsymbol{v}_{t},\boldsymbol{p}_{t},\boldsymbol{q}_{t}
as in (
Magnitudes

2.4

The structure of the power flow Jacobian

2.4.1

Power-voltage magnitude sensitivity matrices

Forming the entries of the block submatrices of the power flow Jacobian requires that we differentiate the power flow mismatch equations (
7
) with respect to the voltage magnitudes and phase angles at each bus. First, consider the derivatives of the net active power and reactive power injections at bus
i
with respect to the voltage magnitudes at bus
k
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p_{i}}{\partial v_{k}}=\begin{cases}v_{i}(G_{ik}\cos\theta_{ik}+B_{ik}\sin\theta_{ik})&i\neq k,\\
2v_{i}G_{ii}+\sum_{k:k\neq i}v_{k}(G_{ik}\cos\theta_{ik}+B_{ik}\sin\theta_{ik})&i=k.\end{cases}

(10a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial q_{i}}{\partial v_{k}}=\begin{cases}v_{i}(G_{ik}\sin\theta_{ik}-B_{ik}\cos\theta_{ik})&i\neq k,\\
-2v_{i}B_{ii}+\sum_{k:k\neq i}v_{k}(G_{ik}\sin\theta_{ik}-B_{ik}\cos\theta_{ik})&i=k.\end{cases}

(10b)

The diagonal terms can be alternatively expressed as
\frac{\partial p_{i}}{\partial v_{i}}=\frac{p_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})}{v_{i}}+G_{ii}v_{i}
and
\frac{\partial q_{i}}{\partial v_{i}}=\frac{q_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})}{v_{i}}-B_{ii}v_{i}
.

2.4.2

Power-voltage phase angle sensitivity matrices

Similarly to the power-voltage magnitude sensitivities in Section
2.4.1
, by differentiating the net active and reactive power injections with respect to the voltage phase angles, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p_{i}}{\partial\theta_{k}}=\begin{cases}v_{i}v_{k}(G_{ik}\sin\theta_{ik}-B_{ik}\cos\theta_{ik})&i\neq k,\\
-v_{i}\sum_{k:k\neq i}v_{k}(G_{ik}\sin\theta_{ik}-B_{ik}\cos\theta_{ik})&i=k,\end{cases}

(11a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial q_{i}}{\partial\theta_{k}}=\begin{cases}-v_{i}v_{k}(G_{ik}\cos\theta_{ik}+B_{ik}\sin\theta_{ik})&i\neq k,\\
v_{i}\sum_{k:k\neq i}v_{k}(G_{ik}\cos\theta_{ik}+B_{ik}\sin\theta_{ik})&i=k.\end{cases}

(11b)

The diagonal terms can alternatively be expressed as
\frac{\partial p_{i}}{\partial\theta_{i}}=-q_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})-B_{ii}v_{i}^{2}
and
\frac{\partial q_{i}}{\partial\theta_{i}}=p_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})-G_{ii}v_{i}^{2}
.

Analytical results

3.1

Model-free recovery of the power flow Jacobian

There are well-known mathematical symmetries that relate the power flow Jacobian block submatrices. These symmetries arise due to the physical laws of the power flow equations
[
30
, Ch. 9]
. The canonical expressions for these symmetries
[
30
, (9.5.4), (9.5.7)]
depend on the network topology and electrical parameters. In this section, we propose a topology and parameter-free extension of these known symmetries that:

1.

Has a valuable engineering implication, supporting system identification without voltage phase angles.

2.

Has a valuable theoretical implication, by providing structural constraints for applying the phase retrieval framework—informed by the physics of Newton-Raphson power flow.

Specifically, we show that we can represent
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{p}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{q}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}
as a function of the power-to-voltage magnitude submatrices of the power flow Jacobian
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{p}}{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}
and
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{q}}{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}
, the voltage magnitudes
\boldsymbol{v}
, and the active and reactive power injections
\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q}
. Representing
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{p}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{q}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}
in this way is valuable because:

1.

It removes dependence on direct measurements of the voltage phase angles, as in (
Phasors
).

2.

It requires only measurements as in (
Magnitudes
), and
estimates
for the power-voltage magnitude sensitivities; which is a well-studied problem
[
3
,
4
]
.

This result is achieved through the following Lemma:

Lemma 1

Consider an electric power network with PQ buses
\{1,\dots,n\}
. Let
\boldsymbol{x}=[\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T},\boldsymbol{v}^{T}]^{T}
be a guess for the network state, producing power injections
\boldsymbol{p}(\boldsymbol{x}),\boldsymbol{q}(\boldsymbol{x})

Phase retrieval via power flow Jacobian recovery

3.2.2

Phase retrieval by exploiting the structure of the power flow Jacobian

Importantly, the formulation (

The structural symmetries of the power flow Jacobian (

and

\begin{split}\minimize_{\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t},\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{p}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}},\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{q}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\quad\left|\left|\begin{bmatrix}\Delta\boldsymbol{p}_{t}\\

3.2.3

Recovering the current phase angles

For any guess for the voltage phase
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in(-\pi,\pi]^{n}

3.3

Recovery guarantees based on Gershgorin discs

The Gershgorin Circle Theorem provides regions of the complex plane where the eigenvalues of a matrix are guaranteed to lie. It has been applied in voltage stability assessment for siting and control of distributed energy resources
[
32
]
, in an impedance-based stability requirement
[
33
]
, in small signal stability of swing equations
[
34
]
, and many other electric power systems applications.

The motivations of using the Gershgorin Circle Theorem to provide phase retrieval guarantees are twofold. First, the Theorem’s structure is intuitively similar to the power flow Jacobian symmetry expression (
12
). Second, the power flow Jacobian submatrices are empirically diagonally dominant, as we show numerically in Fig.
4
of the subsequent section. This property is favorable for applying the Gershgorin Circle Theorem.

Below, in Section
3.3.1
, we apply Gershgorin to yield sufficient conditions for the guaranteed recovery of the voltage phase angles. In Section
3.3.2
, we apply a block matrix variant of Gershgorin to yield a sufficient condition for invertibility of the power flow Jacobian. The sufficient conditions of both Theorems provide these guarantees
without
requiring any knowledge of the voltage phase angles
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.

Modeling asynchronous measurements and non-ideal sampling rates

In practice, measurements of the form (
Magnitudes

d_{i}\sim\textsf{Bern}(p)
for nodes
i=1,\dots,n

Results of a survey of 31 distribution utilities in 2021 found that
>

[

4.4

Baseline comparison with known topology

We compare the method with a standard state estimation approach where the topology is known. We achieve this comparison by using exact closed form expressions
[
29
, 5.2.1]
for the complex-power to voltage magnitude and complex power-voltage phase angle sensitivities that depend on knowledge of the topology
\boldsymbol{Y}
. These matrices
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{s}}{\partial\boldsymbol{v}},\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{s}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
are defined as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{s}}{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}

\displaystyle\triangleq\operatorname{diag}(\overline{\boldsymbol{v}})\left(\operatorname{diag}(\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mu\boldsymbol{Y}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mu\overline{\boldsymbol{v}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)+\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mu\boldsymbol{Y}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\operatorname{diag}(\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mu\overline{\boldsymbol{v}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)\right)\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{v})^{-1},

(34)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{s}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\displaystyle\triangleq j\operatorname{diag}(\overline{\boldsymbol{v}})\left(\operatorname{diag}(\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mu\boldsymbol{Y}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mu\overline{\boldsymbol{v}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)-\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mu\boldsymbol{Y}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\operatorname{diag}(\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mu\overline{\boldsymbol{v}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)\right),

(35)

where
\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mu(\cdot)\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
denotes the complex conjugate. Using (
34
) and (
35
), closed form expressions for the power flow Jacobian submatrices are given as

\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{p}}{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}=\operatorname{Re}\left\{\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{s}}{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}\right\}
,
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{q}}{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}=\operatorname{Im}\left\{\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{s}}{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}\right\}
,
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{p}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\operatorname{Re}\left\{\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{s}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right\}

\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{q}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\operatorname{Im}\left\{\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{s}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right\}

The above matrices can then be used to build a deterministic (model-based) Jacobian

.
This stands in contrast with the program (

are
unknown
and are included as decision variables.

5

Discussion

The proposed extension of the phase retrieval framework to electric power systems has several potential consequences, key limitations, and directions for future work. Below, we discuss these items in Section
5.1
, Section
5.2
, and Section
5.3
, respectively.

5.1

Commentary on results

The proposed methods are a useful development from both a practical and theoretical perspective. These methods have important applications for numerous data-driven modeling scenarios in electric power systems where we only have access to measurements of the form (
Magnitudes
). The most obvious is in distribution systems, where it is typical to only have access to data like (
Magnitudes
). Applications in transmission systems are also appropriate, as large-scale PMU deployment remains an ongoing infrastructure upgrade challenge.

The results in Lemma
1
and its application in the program (
26
) are valuable results for data-driven power flow analysis. This is because they show that the state evolution matrices of the unmeasured voltage phase angle states can be recovered without knowledge of the voltage phase angles. The method can also be shown to be provably correct via the conditions based on spectral theory developed in Theorem
1
and Theorem
2
.

5.2

Limitations

The largest limitation of this research is that it primarily assumes a balanced power system that can be represented as a single-phase equivalent system.
2
2

2

Appendix
0.C.1
develops a preliminary extension to multiphase unbalanced networks.
An additional limitation is the fact that the phase retrieval program (
26
) relies on the structure of the power flow Jacobian, which is a time-varying matrix. While the matrix can be recursively estimated, e.g., as in
[
45
,
46
]
, or via Kalman filtering; this is a key computational limitation. A potential solution to this problem is to reformulate it in terms of the bus admittance matrix. Furthermore, the algorithm is only suitable for the PQ buses of the system or a system that only has PQ buses.

5.3

Future work

There are several key directions for future work to improve the practical application of the proposed methods.

5.3.1

Multiphase extensions

One of the most clear research needs is generalizing the proposed algorithms to multiphase unbalanced networks. This can facilitate practical implementation beyond single-phase equivalent models in distribution systems and in other unbalanced systems. We provide preliminary results that indicate that the proposed algorithms can be generalized to multiphase unbalanced networks in Appendix
0.C.1
.

5.3.2

Additional phase retrieval formulations

Furthermore, recent advances in the phase retrieval literature can be synthesized with the problem setting described in this paper. For example,
[
12
]
and
[
11
]
develop convex formulations for the phase retrieval problem. These formulations may provide numerical and theoretical advantages in comparison to the approach provided by PhaseCut
[
9
]
, which our work is largely based on. However,
[
12
,
11
]
requires an appropriate guess for the phase vector, and the conditions for the effectiveness of the algorithms are based on the quality and construction of this initialization vector, which may be difficult to obtain in this application setting. An important direction for future work is to determine how other aspects of the phase retrieval framework
[
10
,
13
]
can be applied to electric power systems and related to the AC power flow equations.

5.3.3

Partial/estimated topology knowledge

The limitation imposed by the time-varying nature of the power flow Jacobian could potentially be resolved by developing an extension of this work based on the nodal admittance matrix. This would require estimation of the network topology and parameters, e.g., as in
[
1
,
21
,
22
]
. The nodal admittance matrix is, in principle, not time-varying. This could possibly allow measurements of the form (
Magnitudes
) to be recovered without updating the power flow Jacobian.

5.3.4

Uncertain reactive power measurements

If reactive power measurements
\boldsymbol{q}
are unavailable, remotely readable reactive power control parameters—which are mandated to be available in the IEEE 1547-2018 standard
[
47
]
—can be used to infer reactive power measurements when only the
\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{p}
variables of (
Magnitudes
) are available. Alternatively, distribution operators can use historical power factors and other heuristics, which are widely used in practice
[
44
]
, to infer the reactive power injections. There is an opportunity for future work to develop methods to exploit knowledge of known/estimated reactive power control parameters to obtain this knowledge.

6

Conclusion

This work proposed theory and algorithms to recover voltage phasors, current phasors, and power-voltage phase angle sensitivity matrices in electric power systems where the network model is unknown. The method requires only the voltage magnitudes, active power injections, and reactive power injections. The methods are directly inspired by the classical phase retrieval framework from the signal processing literature.

To achieve these outcomes, this research developed a novel representation of the structure of the power flow Jacobian matrix (Lemma
1
) that depends on neither the topology of the network nor the voltage phase angles. The proposed representation of the structure of the power flow Jacobian was then used in Section
3.2
to provide a novel approach to recovering the voltage phase angles and a basis for an approximation of the power flow equations as a function of the phase angles. The method relies neither on partial observability of the phase angles nor on topological information. The proposed representation of the power flow Jacobian was also used to construct sufficient conditions (Theorem
1
, Theorem
2
) that certify when the method will work well. Consistent with the method itself, the conditions only require the measurements in (
Magnitudes
).

Foreseeably, the proposed theory and algorithms could have significant practical engineering implications. The results of this research can enable engineers to “fill in the information gap” in electric power networks that lack deployment of PMU measurements, as in (
Phasors
). It could also potentially save significant cost, by allowing fewer PMU measurements to be placed. As PMU deployment continues, these results can assist network control paradigms by retrieving comparable information to that provided by PMUs in areas that lack access to them.

\boldsymbol{M}=[\boldsymbol{A}_{ij}]\quad\textsf{with blocks}\quad\boldsymbol{A}_{ij}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m},\quad i,j=1,\dots,n,

(47)

where
\operatorname{rank}(\boldsymbol{A}_{ii})=m
for all

. According to

36

, if
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
is an eigenvalue of

(48)

where
\left|\left|\boldsymbol{A}\right|\right|_{2}
is the matrix 2-norm, or spectral norm, which is defined as
\left|\left|\boldsymbol{A}\right|\right|_{2}\triangleq\max_{\boldsymbol{x}\neq\boldsymbol{0}}\frac{\left|\left|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\right|\right|}{\left|\left|\boldsymbol{x}\right|\right|_{2}}=\sigma_{\sf max}(\boldsymbol{A})
, where
\sigma_{\sf max}(\boldsymbol{A})
is the largest singular value of
\boldsymbol{A}
.

Furthermore, every eigenvalue
\lambda
of
\boldsymbol{M}
lies in the unions of these discs.

\lambda(\boldsymbol{M})\in\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\left\{w\in\mathbb{C}:\sum_{j\neq i}^{n}\left|\left|(\boldsymbol{A}_{ii}-w\mathbf{I})^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}_{ij}\right|\right|_{2}\geq 1\right\}.

(49)

If all the blocks
\boldsymbol{A}_{ij}
have dimension
1\times 1
, then this statement reduces to the classical Gershgorin Circle Theorem. Suppose that
\boldsymbol{M}
takes the form of a
2n\times 2n
block matrix that satisfies Assumption
1
, which we write as

\boldsymbol{M}\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{A}_{11}&\boldsymbol{A}_{12}\\
\boldsymbol{A}_{21}&\boldsymbol{A}_{22}\end{bmatrix},

(50)

where
\operatorname{rank}(\boldsymbol{A}_{11})=\operatorname{rank}(\boldsymbol{A}_{22})=n
.
Then, applying (
48
), if 0 is an eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{M}
, then

\left|\left|\boldsymbol{A}_{11}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}_{12}\right|\right|_{2}\geq 1\quad\textsf{or}\quad\left|\left|\boldsymbol{A}_{22}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}_{21}\right|\right|_{2}\geq 1.

(51)

The contraposition is that if

\left|\left|\boldsymbol{A}_{11}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}_{12}\right|\right|_{2}<1\quad\textsf{and}\quad\left|\left|\boldsymbol{A}_{22}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}_{21}\right|\right|_{2}<1,

(52)

then 0 is guaranteed to
not
be an eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{M}
. That is, (
52
) is sufficient to certify the invertibility of the block matrix
\boldsymbol{M}
.

We can complete the proof by applying the above logic to the power flow Jacobian. Let
\boldsymbol{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{2|\mathcal{B}|\times 2|\mathcal{B}|}
be the power flow Jacobian for PQ buses
\mathcal{B}
under study. Recall that we defined

\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{x})\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{p}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x})&\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{p}}{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{x})\\
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{q}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x})&\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{q}}{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{x})\end{bmatrix}

(53)

is invertible—with
\boldsymbol{\theta}
unknown. This is equivalent to showing that
0
is not an eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{J}
. By Assumption
1
,
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{p}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{q}}{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}
are invertible

Consider a three-phase unbalanced distribution network with
n
buses. The
compound admittance matrix
of the network,
\boldsymbol{Y}\in\mathbb{C}^{3n\times 3n}

\boldsymbol{Y}=\left[\boldsymbol{M}_{i,j}\right]_{i,j=1}^{n}

\boldsymbol{M}_{i,j}\in\mathbb{C}^{3\times 3}

i\to j

\mathcal{F}_{ij}
denotes the phases of the branch

i

[
49
