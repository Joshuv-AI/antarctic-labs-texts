Abstract
. In this paper we continue the analysis of non-diagonalisable hyperbolic systems initiated in [ 25 , 26 ] . Here we assume that the system has discontinuous coefficients or more in general distributional coefficients. Well-posedness is proven in the very weak sense for systems with singularities with respect to the space variable or the time variable. Consistency with the classical theory is proven in the case of smooth coefficients.

1.
Introduction

This paper continues the analysis of hyperbolic systems with non-diagonalisable principal part and variable multiplicities started by Gramchev and Ruzhansky in
[
27
]
for
2\times 2
hyperbolic systems and continued by the first author, Jäh and Ruzhansky in
[
25
,
26
]
for systems of arbitrary size. Here we assume that the system coefficients are singular, i.e., distributional in
x
but still continuous with respect to
t
or when smoothness is assumed in the space variable we allow singularities in time. Let us briefly summarise what is known about hyperbolic systems with non-diagonalisable principal part. Let

\begin{cases}D_{t}u=A(t,x,D_{x})u+L(t,x,D_{x})u+f(t,x),\qquad(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\\
u(0,x)=g(x),\end{cases}

where
A
is an upper triangular
m\times m
matrix with real eigenvalues and
L
is the matrix of the lower order terms. Note that the eigenvalues might coincide and therefore we are dealing here in general with a hyperbolic system with multiplicities and non-diagonalisable principal part. It is also not restrictive to assume that the matrix
A
is upper-triangular since conditions are given in
[
25
]
which allow the reduction into upper-triangular form of the system above. For a non exhausting overview on hyperbolic problems with multiplicities we refer the reader to
[
1
,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

]
.

Combining the results proven in
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
we have that the Cauchy problem above is well-posed in anisotropic Sobolev spaces of any order provided that the entries of
A=(a_{ij})
are symbols in
C([0,T],S^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}))
and the entries of
L=(\ell_{ij})
are symbols in
C([0,T],S^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
fulfilling the following Levi type condition:

\ell_{ij}\in C([0,T],S^{j-i}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}))

for
i>j
. In addition, when
A
depends only on
x
and assuming intersection of finite order at points of multiplicity one can obtain a representation formula for the solution which leads to results of propagation of singularities. See
[
26
]
for more details. The results above heavily rely on the fact that the system coefficients are smooth in space and at least continuous on time. This is due to the fact that the system is solved making use of symbolic calculus and Fourier integral operators methods
[
30
]
.

When the system coefficients are less than continuous in time or less than smooth in space it might be difficult even to define a notion of solution. Indeed, as a consequence of Schwartz’s impossibility result such system might fail to have a distributional solution
[
31
,
34
,
35
]
. For this reason, we want to prove here well-posedness in a
very weak sense
. Note that the desire to drop the regularity assumption of the system coefficients has physical motivation, since this kind of systems naturally arise in wave propagation into a multi-layered medium, like the Subsoil, or in conical refraction in crystals, see
[
19
]
and references therein.

The notion of a
very weak solution
has been introduced by the first author and Ruzhansky in
[
19
]
. It is based on the idea of replacing the original problem with a regularised one, where the coefficients and initial data are regularised via convolution with suitable mollifiers. This generates a net of hyperbolic problems with smooth coefficients depending on the regularising parameter
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. The corresponding net of solutions
(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
is said to be a very weak solution if it satisfies some specific behaviour with respect to the parameter, that we will refer to as
moderate
. Note that the notion of very weak solution is inspired by the theory of algebras of generalised functions
[
14
,
15
,
23
,
28
,
35
]
however, here we work with nets of functions rather than equivalence classes and we have full flexibility of regularising coefficients and initial data with different mollifiers and scales, basing our choice on the intrinsic properties of the problem we are studying. Moderateness will also be measured according to different norms or seminorms which are determined by the function space where we aim to prove well-posedness:
C^{\infty}
, Sobolev spaces, Gevrey classes, etc.

In the sequel we list the main results obtained in this paper.

We begin by focusing on the Cauchy problem

D_{t}u=A(t,x,D_{x})u+L(t,x,D_{x})u+f(t,x),\qquad(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},

with initial data
u(0,x)=g(x)
, where,

A(t,x,D_{x})=\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{1j}(t,x)D_{x_{j}}&a_{12}(t,x,D_{x})\\
0&\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{2j}(t,x)D_{x_{j}}\end{bmatrix},

and

L(t,x,D_{x})=\begin{bmatrix}\ell_{11}(t,x)&\ell_{12}(t,x)\\
\ell_{21}(t,x)\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}&\ell_{22}(t,x)\end{bmatrix}.

We assume that the matrices
A
and
L
are defined by pseudo-differential operators of order
1
and
0
respectively, with symbols which are not smooth in
x
. Namely, we assume that the system coefficients are continuous in
t
but distributional with compact support in
x
. As a toy model one could think about a discontinuous function in
x
or a Dirac distribution in
x
. After regularisation, i.e. convolution with a suitable mollifier

\psi_{\varepsilon}(x)=\omega(\varepsilon)^{-n}\psi(\omega(\varepsilon)^{-1}x),

where
\omega(\varepsilon)\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, we work on

\begin{cases}D_{t}u_{\varepsilon}=A_{\varepsilon}(t,x,D_{x})u_{\varepsilon}+L_{\varepsilon}(t,x,D_{x})u_{\varepsilon}+f_{\varepsilon}(t,x),&\quad(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\\
u_{\varepsilon}(0,x)=g_{\varepsilon}(x),&\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\end{cases}

where
u_{\varepsilon}(0,x)=[g_{1,\varepsilon}(x),g_{2,\varepsilon}(x)]^{T}
and
f_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=[f_{1,\varepsilon}(t,x),f_{2,\varepsilon}(t,x)]^{T}
, and the system matrices are

A_{\varepsilon}(t,x,D_{x})=\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{1j,\varepsilon}(t,x)D_{x_{j}}&a_{12,\varepsilon}(t,x,D_{x})\\
0&\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{2j,\varepsilon}(t,x)D_{x_{j}}\end{bmatrix},

and

L_{\varepsilon}(t,x,D_{x})=\begin{bmatrix}\ell_{11,\varepsilon}(t,x)&\ell_{12,\varepsilon}(t,x)\\
\ell_{21,\varepsilon}(t,x)\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}&\ell_{22,\varepsilon}(t,x)\end{bmatrix}.

By the theory of Fourier integral operators
[
25
,
26
]
we know that the solution
u_{\varepsilon}=[u_{1,\varepsilon},u_{2,\varepsilon}]^{T}
can be written explicitly via action of FIOs or integrated FIOs on the initial data, that we will denote here with
G^{0}_{i,\varepsilon}
and
G_{i,\varepsilon}
, respectively, with
i=1,2
. The corresponding phase functions,
\phi_{i,\varepsilon}
can be also explicitly calculated by solving the Eikonal equation determined by the system eigenvalues. It turns out that to guarantee Sobolev mapping properties of the operators involved in our proof (see
[
38
]
) we need nets of symbols of order
0
as well as some bound on the phase function, namely,

\displaystyle|\partial_{\xi}\phi_{i,\varepsilon}(t,x,\xi)-\partial_{\xi}\phi_{i,\varepsilon}(t,y,\xi)|

\displaystyle\geq C(\varepsilon)|x-y|\,\,\,\,\text{ for }\,\,x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\,\,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

\displaystyle|\partial_{y}\phi_{i,\varepsilon}(t,y,\xi)-\partial_{y}\phi_{i,\varepsilon}(t,y,\eta)|

\displaystyle\geq C(\varepsilon)|\xi-\eta|\,\,\,\,\text{ for }\,\,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\,\,\xi,\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

for
i=1,2
. In particular, it is crucial to study the
\varepsilon
-behaviour of the composition operator

\mathcal{G}^{0}_{1,\varepsilon}:=G_{1,\varepsilon}\circ(a_{12,\varepsilon}+\ell_{12,\varepsilon})\circ G_{2,\varepsilon}\circ\ell_{21,\varepsilon}\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}.

Summarising, we identify the following set of hypotheses which guarantees the hyperbolicity of the system as well as its solvability in the very weak sense:

(H1)

the coefficients
(\lambda_{1j,\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
and
(\lambda_{2j,\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
are real valued and
C([0,T],C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
-moderate for
j=1,\ldots,n
with compact support in
x
;

(H2)

(\partial_{k}\lambda_{1j,\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
,
(\partial_{k}\lambda_{2j,\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
and
(\ell_{ii,\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
are logarithmic moderate for
k,j=1,\ldots,n
and
i=1,2
;

(H3)

the nets of constants
(C(\varepsilon))_{\varepsilon}
and
(C^{-1}(\varepsilon))_{\varepsilon}
are moderate and

||\mathcal{G}^{0}_{1,\varepsilon}||_{H^{s}\rightarrow H^{s}}=O(1).

In detail, we prove that

•

if (H1), (H2) and (H3) hold then the Cauchy problem above has a very weak solution (a solution
(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
which is
C([0,T],C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
-moderate) and it is well-posed in the very weak sense (Theorem
3.2
, Corollary
3.3
);

•

if
A
is diagonal and
L
upper-triangular then (H1) and (H2) are sufficient to get very weak well-posedness (Corollary
3.4
);

•

if
A
and
L
are both upper-triangular then the Cauchy problem is well-posed in the very weak sense provided that (H1), (H2) hold and the nets of constants
(C(\varepsilon))_{\varepsilon}
and
(C^{-1}(\varepsilon))_{\varepsilon}
are moderate (Corollary
3.5
);

•

the last two results are easily extendable to matrices of size
m\times m
.

This paper is organised as follows: in Section 2 we collect some preliminaries on regularisation and very weak solutions for hyperbolic equations. The main results are proven in Section 3. In Section 4 we prove that very weak solutions recover the classical solutions in the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
in the case of smooth coefficients. We also discuss some examples and we show how to adapt our methods to coefficients which are singular in time rather than in space. The paper ends with an Appendix devoted to the
L^{2}
-boundedness of Fourier integral operators
[
38
]
. In particular, we formulate precise bounds for FIOs depending on
\varepsilon
of the type encountered in the paper.

2.
Preliminaries: regularisation and very weak solutions

In this section we recall the notion of very weak solution as introduced in
[
19
]
. The main idea, which goes back to algebras of generalised functions, consists in regularising the equation coefficients via a Friedrichs type mollifier
\psi
(
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
\psi\geq 0
with
\int\psi=1)
. In other words, given a compactly supported distribution
u
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we generate a net of smooth functions
(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
, where
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
, by writing

u_{\varepsilon}=u*\psi_{\omega(\varepsilon)},

where

\psi_{\omega(\varepsilon)}(x)=\omega(\varepsilon)^{-n}\psi(x/\omega(\varepsilon)),

and
\omega(\varepsilon)
is a positive function converging to
0
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
with
\omega(\varepsilon)\geq c\varepsilon^{a}
for some
c,a>0
, uniformly with respect to
\varepsilon
. It is clear that
u_{\varepsilon}
converges to
u
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. In addition, the net
(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
fulfills some extra properties with respect to the parameter
\varepsilon
that are illustrated in the proposition below. For a detailed proof of we refer the reader to
[
19
,
28
,
35
]
and reference therein.

Proposition 2.1
.

(i)

If
u\in\mathcal{E^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
then there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
there exists
c>0
such that

|\partial^{\alpha}(u*\psi_{\omega(\varepsilon)})(x)|\leq c\omega(\varepsilon)^{-N-|\alpha|},

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
.

(ii)

If
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
then for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
there exists
c>0
such that

|\partial^{\alpha}(f*\psi_{\omega(\varepsilon)})(x)|\leq c,

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
.

(iii)

If
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and mollifier
\psi
has all the moments vanishing, i.e.,
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\psi(x)dx=1
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}x^{\alpha}\psi(x)dx=0
for all multi-index
\alpha
with
|\alpha|>1
, then for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{\alpha}
and for all
q\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
there exists
c>0
such that

|\partial^{\alpha}(f*\psi_{\omega(\varepsilon)}(x)-f(x))|\leq c\omega(\varepsilon)^{q},

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
.

It follows that the nets obtained via convolution with a mollifier are
moderate
or
negligible
with respect to the seminorms of
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
(see Definition
2.1
). Analogously one can replace
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
C^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
. In the sequel,
K\Subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
stands for
K
is a compact set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Definition 2.1
.

(i)

A net
(v_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{(0,1]}
is
C^{\infty}
-moderate if for all
K\Subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
c>0
such that

|\partial^{\alpha}v_{\varepsilon}(x)|\leq c\varepsilon^{-N},

uniformly in
x\in K
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
.

(ii)

A net
(v_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{(0,1]}
is

-negligible if for all
K\Subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
and
q\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
there exists
c>0
such that

|\partial^{\alpha}v_{\varepsilon}(x)|\leq c\varepsilon^{q},

uniformly in
x\in K
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
.

(iii)

A net
(v_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n})^{(0,1]}
is
C^{\infty}
-moderate if for all
K\Subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and for all
l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
c>0

|\partial^{k}_{t}\partial^{\alpha}v_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|\leq c\varepsilon^{-N},

uniformly in
t\in[0,T]
,
x\in K
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
.

(iv)

A net
(v_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n})^{(0,1]}
is
C^{\infty}
-negligible if for all
K\Subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
and
q\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
there exists
c>0
such that

|\partial^{k}_{t}\partial^{\alpha}v_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|\leq c\varepsilon^{q},

uniformly in
t\in[0,T]
,
x\in K
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
.

(v)

A net
(v_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}\in C([0,T],C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{(0,1]}
is
C([0,T],C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
-moderate if for all
K\Subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and

|\partial^{\alpha}_{x}v_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|\leq c\varepsilon^{-N},

uniformly in
t\in[0,T]
,

and

.

(vi)

A net
(v_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n})^{(0,1]}

-negligible if for all
K\Subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
and

there exists

,

\varepsilon\in(0,1]
.

Remark
.

If we are dealing with nets of numbers then we will measure moderateness and negligibility in terms of the module in
\mathbb{C}
or
\mathbb{R}
and we will simply talk of moderate and negligible nets. Analogously, we can state the definitions above for vectors by replacing

with

. We will still talk of

-moderatenss and

-negligibility.

2.1.
State of the art on hyperbolic equations with low regular coefficients

In this subsection we give a brief state of the art on hyperbolic equations with low regular coefficients, with particular focus on the very weak well-posedness of the corresponding Cauchy problem.

We begin by recalling that the Cauchy problem for ordinary differential equations has been investigated already in
[
28
]
in the context of Colombeau algebras of generalised functions. The results obtained there (Theorem 1.5.2
[
28
]
) can be rewritten at the net level as follows, for the Cauchy problem

\begin{split}\dot{x_{\varepsilon}}(t)&=F_{\varepsilon}(t,x_{\varepsilon}(t)),\\
x_{\varepsilon}(t_{0})&=x_{0,\varepsilon},\end{split}

(1)

where
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
(F_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
is a net in
(C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n}))^{n}
and
(x_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
is a net of initial conditions. In the sequel we are making use of the standard norm in
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
that for the sake of simplicity we will continue to denote with
|\cdot|
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
(F_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
be a net in
(C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n}))^{n}
such that

(i)

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
c>0
such that

|\partial^{k}_{t}\partial^{\alpha}_{x}F_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|\leq c\varepsilon^{-N}(1+|(t,x)|)^{N},

for all
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
.

(ii)

there exists
c>0
such that

\|\nabla_{x}F_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n})}\leq c|\log\varepsilon|,

for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
.

For all
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
the Cauchy problem (
1
) has a unique solution
x_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n})^{n}
such that if
(x_{0,\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
is moderate then
(x_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
is
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
-moderate as well. The solution
(x_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
is unique modulo, negligible perturbation, i.e., if we perturb the initial data with a negligible net (i.e.,
x_{0,\varepsilon}+n_{\varepsilon}
, where
(n_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
is negligible) then the corresponding solution will differ from
(x_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
by a
C^{\infty}
-negligible net.

For the sake of simplicity we say that
(\nabla_{x}F_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
is logarithmic moderate. Note that one can reformulate the proposition above on a finite time interval
[0,T]
and replace the polynomial bound in (i) with an assumption of compact support with respect to the
x
-variable.

Proposition
2.2
can also be stated for nets that are only continuous with respect to
t
. This means to set
k=0
and replace
C^{\infty}
with
C([0,T],C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
. Continuity with respect to
t\in[0,T]
and smoothness with respect to
x
will be the standard assumptions throughout this paper for our nets of functions.

Let us now consider the scalar case, i.e., the Cauchy problem for first order hyperbolic equations. In detail, let

\displaystyle D_{t}w_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j,\varepsilon}(t,x)D_{j}w_{\varepsilon}+a_{0,\varepsilon}(t,x)w_{\varepsilon},

(2)

\displaystyle w_{\varepsilon}(0,x)

\displaystyle=w_{0,\varepsilon},

where
D_{j}=D_{x_{j}}
, the coefficients
a_{j,\varepsilon}
are real-valued for all
j=1,\ldots,n
. The equation coefficients are compactly supported distributions with respect to
x
regularised by convolution with a mollifier
\psi
that we assume non-negative and with integral
1
. In detail,

-

a_{j}\in C([0,T]\times\mathcal{E^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
with
a_{j}\geq 0
for all
j=1,\ldots,n
,

-

a_{0}\in C([0,T]\times\mathcal{E^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))

-

w_{0}\in\mathcal{E^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

and

-

a_{j,\varepsilon}(t,x)=(a_{j}(t,\cdot)*\psi_{\omega(\varepsilon)})(x)
for
j=1,\ldots,n
,

-

a_{0,\varepsilon}(t,x)=(a_{0}(t,\cdot)*\psi_{\omega(\varepsilon)})(x)
,

-

w_{0},\varepsilon(x)=w_{0}*\psi_{\varepsilon}(x)
.

Definition 2.2
.

We say that the Cauchy problem

\begin{split}D_{t}w&=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}(t,x)D_{j}w+a_{0}(t,x)w,\\
w(0,x)&=w_{0}\end{split}

admits a
very weak solution

(w_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
if the net
(w_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
solves the regularised problem (
2
) and it is
C([0,T],C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
-moderate.

The following result, is the net-version of the well-posedness results obtained in
[
14
,
29
,
34
]
. It shows that a transport equation with singular coefficients admits a very weak solution under suitable choice of the regularising scale. In the sequel we say that a net of function is logarithmic moderate if its
L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
-norm is estimated by a logarithmic scale.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let the coefficients
(a_{j,\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
be
C([0,T],C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
-moderate for
j=1,\ldots,n
with compact support in
x
. Suppose that
(a_{j,\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
are real valued and
(\partial_{k}a_{j,\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
as well as
(a_{0,\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
are logarithmic moderate
(k,j=1,\ldots,n)
. Then,

(i)

the Cauchy problem above admits a very weak solution
(w_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
which is compactly supported with respect to
x
.

(ii)

If
a_{0,\varepsilon}
as well as
w_{0,\varepsilon}
are real-valued then the solution
(w_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
is real valued as well.

(iii)

Negligible perturbations of the coefficients and initial data lead to a negligible perturbation in the very weak solution.

In
[
14
]
the authors investigated further the properties of the very weak solution
(w_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
and proved that it can be written as the action of a Fourier integral operator (FIO) on the initial data
(w_{0,\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
. We summarise here the main steps of their argument, mainly to solve the Eikonal equation to determine the phase function of the FIO and to solve a certain transport equation to determine its symbol. As expected, we will work with nets of FIO’s, phase functions and symbols depending on the regularising parameter
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
.

2.2.
Nets of phase functions

We begin by constructing the phase function of the FIO making use of the characteristic curves associated to the principal part of the equation. This means to solve the linear Cauchy problem

\begin{split}\partial_{t}\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,x,\xi)&=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j,\varepsilon}(t,x)\partial_{j}\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,x,\xi)\\
\phi_{\varepsilon}(0,x,\xi)&=x\cdot\xi.\end{split}

(3)

Under the assumptions on coefficients
(a_{j,\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
in Proposition
2.3
, the Cauchy problem above is solved by a
C([0,T],C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
-moderate net
(\phi_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
which can be written as

\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,x,\xi)=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\omega_{l,\varepsilon}(t,x)\xi_{l},

where
\omega_{l,\varepsilon}
with
l=1,\ldots,n
are solutions of the Cauchy problem

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\omega_{l,\varepsilon}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j,\varepsilon}(t,x)\partial_{j}\omega_{l,\varepsilon}(t,x),

(4)

\displaystyle\omega_{l,\varepsilon}(0,x)

\displaystyle=x_{l}.

We recall that the characteristic equations associated with the Cauchy problem (
4
) are given by

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}\gamma_{l,\varepsilon}(x,t;s)

\displaystyle=-a_{l,\varepsilon}(s,\gamma_{1,\varepsilon}(x,t;s),\ldots,\gamma_{n,\varepsilon}(x,t;s)),

(5)

\displaystyle\gamma_{l,\varepsilon}(x,t;t)

\displaystyle=x_{l}\quad\text{ for }\quad l=1,\ldots,n,

The solutions
\gamma_{1,\varepsilon},\ldots,\gamma_{n,\varepsilon}
are the components of the characteristic curve

\gamma_{\varepsilon}=(\gamma_{1,\varepsilon},\ldots,\gamma_{n,\varepsilon})

associated the differential operator
\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j,\varepsilon}(t,x)D_{j}
. The following proposition has been proven in
[
14
]
in the context of generalised functions of Colombeau type. For the advantage of the reader we present here a detailed proof at the net level.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let the coefficients
(a_{j,\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
be real valued
C([0,T],C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
-moderate, compactly supported in
x
and let
(\partial_{k}a_{j,\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
be logarithmic moderate
(k,j=1,\ldots,n)
. Then, the solution
\omega_{l,\varepsilon}(t,x)
of the Cauchy problem (
4
) can be written as

\omega_{l,\varepsilon}(x,t)=\gamma_{l,\varepsilon}(x,t;0)=x_{l}+\int_{0}^{t}a_{l,\varepsilon}(\tau,\gamma_{1,\varepsilon}(x,t;\tau),\ldots,\gamma_{n,\varepsilon}(x,t;\tau))d\tau,

for
l=1,\ldots,n
.

Proof.

From Proposition
2.3
it is easy to show that there exists a real valued solution
\omega_{l,\varepsilon}
. It remains to prove that
\omega_{l,\varepsilon}(t,x)=\gamma_{l,\varepsilon}(x,t;0)
. This comes from the fact that
\omega_{l,\varepsilon}
is constant along the characteristic curves
\gamma_{\varepsilon}(x,t;s)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}\omega_{l,\varepsilon}(s,\gamma_{1,\varepsilon}(x,t;s),\ldots,\gamma_{n,\varepsilon}(x,t;s))=0.

Hence,

\displaystyle\omega_{l,\varepsilon}(t,\gamma_{1,\varepsilon}(x,t;t),\ldots,\gamma_{n,\varepsilon}(x,t;t))=\omega_{l,\varepsilon}(0,\gamma_{1,\varepsilon}(x,t;0),\ldots,\gamma_{n,\varepsilon}(x,t;0))

for each
t\in\mathbb{R}
. This implies
\omega_{l,\varepsilon}(t,x)=\gamma_{l,\varepsilon}(x,t;0)
. By solving directly the characteristic equations and making use of Proposition
2.2
we know that
\gamma_{l,\varepsilon}(x,t;0)
is
C^{\infty}
-moderate and it has the following form

\gamma_{l,\varepsilon}(x,t;s)=x_{l}-\int_{t}^{s}a_{l,\varepsilon}(\tau,\gamma_{1,\varepsilon}(x,t;\tau),\ldots,\gamma_{n,\varepsilon}(x,t;\tau))d\tau,

then

\omega_{l,\varepsilon}(x,t)=\gamma_{l,\varepsilon}(x,t;0)=x_{l}+\int_{0}^{t}a_{l,\varepsilon}(\tau,\gamma_{1,\varepsilon}(x,t;\tau),\ldots,\gamma_{n,\varepsilon}(x,t;\tau))d\tau.

This completes the proof.
∎

As a straightforward consequence we obtain the following result for the Eikonal Cauchy problem (
3
)

Corollary 2.5
.

If the coefficients
(a_{j,\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
are real valued
C([0,T],C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
-moderate with compact support in
x
with
(\partial_{k}a_{j,\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
is logarithmic moderate
(k,j=1,\ldots,n)
then the phase function

\displaystyle\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,x,\xi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\omega_{l,\varepsilon}(t,x)\xi_{l}=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\gamma_{l,\varepsilon}(x,t;0)\xi_{l},

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{n}x_{l}\xi_{l}+\xi_{l}\int_{0}^{t}a_{l,\varepsilon}(\tau,\gamma_{1,\varepsilon}(x,t;\tau),\ldots,\gamma_{n,\varepsilon}(x,t;\tau))d\tau,

solves the Eikonal Cauchy problem (
3
).

2.3.
The transport equation in this context

We now pass to solve the transport equation, in other words to solve the Cauchy problem (
2

\displaystyle\begin{cases}D_{t}b_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j,\varepsilon}(t,x)D_{j}b_{\varepsilon}(t,x)+a_{0,\varepsilon}(t,x)b_{\varepsilon}(t,x),\\
b_{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot)=1.\end{cases}

(6)

The characteristic equations of the Cauchy problem (

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}\gamma_{l,\varepsilon}(x,t;s)

\displaystyle=-a_{l,\varepsilon}(s,\gamma_{1,\varepsilon}(x,t;s),\ldots,\gamma_{n,\varepsilon}(x,t;s)),

\displaystyle\gamma_{l,\varepsilon}(x,t;t)

\displaystyle=1.

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}z(s)

\displaystyle z(0)

\displaystyle=1,

where

\displaystyle z(s)=b_{\varepsilon}(s,\gamma_{1,\varepsilon}(x,t;s),\ldots,\gamma_{n,\varepsilon}(x,t;s)),

we get

b_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=e^{i\int_{0}^{t}a_{0,\varepsilon}(\tau,\gamma_{1,\varepsilon}(x,t;\tau),\ldots,\gamma_{n,\varepsilon}(x,t;\tau))d\tau}.

(7)

Using Proposition
2.3
and integrating along the characteristics we have the following result.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let the coefficients
(a_{j,\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
be

and is

2.4.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let the coefficients

The solution of the inhomogeneous Cauchy problem

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j,\varepsilon}(t,x)D_{j}w_{\varepsilon}(t,x)+a_{0,\varepsilon}(t,x)w_{\varepsilon}(t,x)+f_{\varepsilon}(t,x),

(10)

\displaystyle w_{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot)

\displaystyle=w_{0,\varepsilon}(x),

\displaystyle=b_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{i\gamma_{\varepsilon}(x,t;0)\cdot\xi}\hat{w}_{0,\varepsilon}(\xi)d\xi

It defines, for each

.

Let the coefficients
(a_{j,\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
be

-moderate for

In the rest of the paper we will focus on hyperbolic systems with irregular coefficients. More precisely, we will formulate conditions on the principal part and the lower order terms matrix (Levi conditions) which will guarantee the existence of a very weak solution. Our analysis will also investigate different levels of singularity of the system coefficients and how they relate to the corresponding very weak solution.

3.

We want to study the Cauchy problem for

\ell_{21}(t,x)\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}&\ell_{22}(t,x)\end{bmatrix},

\begin{cases}D_{t}u_{\varepsilon}=A_{\varepsilon}(t,x,D_{x})u_{\varepsilon}+L_{\varepsilon}(t,x,D_{x})u_{\varepsilon}+f_{\varepsilon}(t,x),&\quad(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\\

-moderate and

\displaystyle||G_{1,\varepsilon}\circ a_{12,\varepsilon}\circ G_{2,\varepsilon}\circ\ell_{21,\varepsilon}\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}||_{H^{s}\rightarrow H^{s}}\leq TC_{1}(\varepsilon)A_{1}(\varepsilon)a_{12}(\varepsilon)C_{2}(\varepsilon)A_{2}(\varepsilon)L_{21}(\varepsilon)=O(1),

\displaystyle a_{12}(\varepsilon)

(H3)

the nets of constants
(C(\varepsilon))_{\varepsilon}
and
(C^{-1}(\varepsilon))_{\varepsilon}
are moderate and

If (H3) holds, then we will apply Banach’s fixed point theorem in the space

for

with the norm

||u_{1,\varepsilon}||_{X(t)}=\sup_{0\leq\tau\leq t}||u_{1,\varepsilon}(\tau,\cdot)||_{H^{s}}.

Note that we can rewrite the equation for

Using composition of Fourier integral operators and hypothesis (H3) we have that the 0-order Fourier integral operator

maps

||\mathcal{G}^{0}_{1,\varepsilon}(u-v)||_{X(T^{*})}\leq CT^{*}||u-v||_{X(T^{*})},

(20)

for the map

is invertible on

for all values of

(u_{1,\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
inherits the moderateness properties of
({\tilde{U}}_{1,\varepsilon}^{0})_{\varepsilon}

as already observed in
[

the constant

We have therefore proven the following general result.

Theorem 3.2

Let us consider the Cauchy problem

\begin{cases}D_{t}u=A(t,x,D_{x})u+L(t,x,D_{x})u+f(t,x),&\quad(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\\

Remark

Corollary 3.3

If (H1) and (H2), (H3) hold then the Cauchy problem (

(ii) The existence of a very weak solution of anisotropic Sobolev type is obtained from Theorem
3.2
. Note that since the coefficients are smooth the hypotheses (H1)-(H3) are fulfilled with constants independent of
\varepsilon
.

(iii) Let
(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
and
u
be a very weak solution and the classic solution of our Cauchy problem, respectively. Our argument is independent of the choice of the mollifier and the scale we are using in our regularisation, so we namely prove that every very weak solution produced in this way is convergent to the unique classical solution.

We now compare the classical Cauchy problem with solution
u
, with the regularised one with solution
(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
. It follows that

\begin{cases}D_{t}(u_{\varepsilon}-u)=A_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon}-u)+L_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon}-u)+(f_{\varepsilon}-f)+R_{\varepsilon},&\quad(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\\
(u_{\varepsilon}-u)(0,x)=(g_{\varepsilon}-g)(x),&\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\end{cases}

where

R_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=[(A_{\varepsilon}-A)(t,x,D_{x})+(L_{\varepsilon}-L)(t,x,D_{x})]u(t,x).

Repeating the argument in Theorem
3.2
, we arrive at

\displaystyle V_{1,\varepsilon}=U^{0}_{1,\varepsilon}+G_{1,\varepsilon}\circ(a_{12,\varepsilon}+\ell_{12,\varepsilon})\circ G_{2,\varepsilon}\circ\ell_{21,\varepsilon}\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}V_{1,\varepsilon},

where

\displaystyle V_{1,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=u_{1,\varepsilon}-u_{1},

\displaystyle U^{0}_{1,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=G_{1,\varepsilon}^{0}(g_{1,\varepsilon}-g_{1})+G_{1,\varepsilon}([f_{1,\varepsilon}-f_{1}]+R_{1,\varepsilon})

\displaystyle+G_{1,\varepsilon}\circ(a_{12,\varepsilon}+\ell_{12,\varepsilon})\circ[G_{2,\varepsilon}^{0}(g_{2,\varepsilon}-g_{2})+G_{2,\varepsilon}(f_{2,\varepsilon}-f_{2})+G_{2,\varepsilon}R_{2,\varepsilon}],

and

\displaystyle R_{1,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle:=[(\lambda_{1,\varepsilon}-\lambda_{1})(t,x,D_{x})u_{1}+(a_{12,\varepsilon}-a_{12})u_{2}+(\ell_{11,\varepsilon}-\ell_{11})u_{1}+(\ell_{12,\varepsilon}-\ell_{12})u_{2}],

\displaystyle R_{2,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle:=[(\lambda_{2,\varepsilon}-\lambda_{2})(t,x,D_{x})u_{2}+(\ell_{21,\varepsilon}-\ell_{21})\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}u_{1}+(\ell_{22,\varepsilon}-\ell_{22})u_{2}].

By using the hypothesis (H3) and the Banach fixed-point theorem with
CT\leq 1
, we get

\displaystyle||V_{1,\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{x})}

\displaystyle=||U^{0}_{1,\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{x})}+||G_{1,\varepsilon}\circ(a_{12,\varepsilon}+\ell_{12,\varepsilon})\circ G_{2,\varepsilon}\circ\ell_{21,\varepsilon}\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}V_{1,\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{x})}

\displaystyle\leq||U^{0}_{1,\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{x})}+CT||V_{1,\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{x})}

\displaystyle\leq||U^{0}_{1,\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{x})}.

Note that because of the regularity of the coefficients the operators involved above fulfill estimates which are independent of the parameter
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
. We therefore have that
||U^{0}_{1,\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{x})}\rightarrow 0
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
since
||g_{1,\varepsilon}-g_{1}||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{x})}\rightarrow 0
,
||f_{1,\varepsilon}-f_{1}||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{x})}\rightarrow 0
,
||\lambda_{i,\varepsilon}-\lambda_{i}||_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{x})}\rightarrow 0
,
\langle{x}\rangle^{-1}||a_{12,\varepsilon}-a_{12}||_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{x})}\rightarrow 0
, and
||\ell_{ij,\varepsilon}-\ell_{ij}||_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{x})}\rightarrow 0
for
i,j=1,2
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
. This gives that
||u_{1,\varepsilon}-u_{1}||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{x})}\rightarrow 0
. From the continuity assumption we have that all the limits above hold uniformly with respect to
t\in[0,T]
. This yields to
\|u_{\varepsilon}-u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{x})}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
uniformly with respect to
t\in[0,T]
.
∎

We conclude this section with the analysis of few examples where the system coefficients are less than continuous or in general distributions with compact support.

4.1.
Examples

For the sake of simplicity we work in space dimension 1. Let us consider the Cauchy problem

\begin{cases}D_{t}u=\begin{bmatrix}H(x)D_{x}&a_{12}(t,x,D_{x})\\
0&H(x)D_{x}\end{bmatrix}u+\begin{bmatrix}\ell_{11}(t,x)&\ell_{12}(t,x)\\
\ell_{21}(t,x)\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}&\ell_{22}(t,x)\end{bmatrix}u,\\
u(0,x)=g(x),\end{cases}

where
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}
,
u(0,x)=[g_{1}(x),g_{2}(x)]^{T}
with
g_{1},g_{2}\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
and the operators
a_{12}
and
\ell_{ij}
are pseudo-differential operators of order
1
and
0
respectively continuous with respect to
t
.
For the moment we assume that only the coefficients on the diagonal of the matrix
A
are non-regular, i.e.,
H
is the Heaviside function (
H(x)=1
for
x\geq 0
and
H(x)=0
for
x<0
). One could in principle replace
H
with any discontinuous but bounded function. Note that already in this situation the system is not treatable within the classical theory of hyperbolic systems with multiplicities that as in
[
25
,
26
]
requires smoothness with respect to the variable
x
. It is therefore meaningful to look at the regularised problem and at the net
(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
of its solutions. In detail,

\begin{cases}D_{t}u_{\varepsilon}=\begin{bmatrix}H_{\varepsilon}(x)D_{x}&a_{12}(t,x,D_{x})\\
0&H_{\varepsilon}(x)D_{x}\end{bmatrix}u_{\varepsilon}+\begin{bmatrix}\ell_{11}(t,x)&\ell_{12}(t,x)\\
\ell_{21}(t,x)\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}&\ell_{22}(t,x)\end{bmatrix}u_{\varepsilon},\\
u_{\varepsilon}(0,x)=g_{\varepsilon}(x),\end{cases}

where

\displaystyle H_{\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle=(H*\psi_{\omega(\varepsilon)})(x),

\displaystyle g_{1,\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle=(g_{1}*\psi_{\varepsilon})(x),

\displaystyle g_{2,\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle=(g_{2}*\psi_{\varepsilon})(x).

We will choose the scale
\omega(\varepsilon)
later in order to make sure that the hypothesis (H2) holds.

We can reformulate the regularised problem in terms of Fourier integral operators follows

\displaystyle u_{1,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=G_{1,\varepsilon}^{0}g_{1,\varepsilon}+G_{1,\varepsilon}((a_{12}+\ell_{12})u_{2,\varepsilon}),

\displaystyle u_{2,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=G_{2,\varepsilon}^{0}g_{2,\varepsilon}+G_{2,\varepsilon}(\ell_{21}\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}u_{1,\varepsilon}).

where

\displaystyle G_{j,\varepsilon}^{0}g_{j,\varepsilon}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm e}^{i(x+H_{\varepsilon}(x)t)\xi}{\rm e}^{\int_{0}^{t}\ell_{jj}(\tau,x+H_{\varepsilon}(x)\tau)d\tau}\hat{g}_{j,\varepsilon}(\xi)d\xi

\displaystyle={\rm e}^{\int_{0}^{t}\ell_{jj}(\tau,x+H_{\varepsilon}(x)\tau)d\tau}g_{j,\varepsilon}(x+H_{\varepsilon}(x)t),

\displaystyle G_{j,\varepsilon}f_{\varepsilon}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm e}^{i(x+H_{\varepsilon}(x)t)\xi}{\rm e}^{\int_{s}^{t}\ell_{jj}(\tau,x+H_{\varepsilon}(x)\tau)d\tau}\hat{f}_{\varepsilon}(s,\xi)d\xi ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}{\rm e}^{i(x+H_{\varepsilon}(x)t)\xi-iy\xi}A_{j,\varepsilon}(s,t,x)f_{\varepsilon}(s,y)\,dyd\hskip-3.99994pt{}^{-}\hskip-1.99997pt\xi ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}{\rm e}^{\int_{s}^{t}\ell_{jj}(\tau,x+H_{\varepsilon}(x)\tau)d\tau}f_{\varepsilon}(s,x+H_{\varepsilon}(x)(t-s))ds,

for
j=1,2
.

Let us now set
\omega^{-1}(\varepsilon)=\log(\varepsilon^{-1})
. Note that in this case the assumptions (H1) and (H2) are immediately fulfilled. Indeed,
(H_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
is real-valued and moderate and
H^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}=\psi_{\omega(\varepsilon)}
is logarithmic moderate. In addition
\|H_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 1
.

In the special case when both the matrices of the principal part and the lower order terms are diagonal the assumption (H3) is not needed. In detail, if
a_{12}=0
and
\ell_{12}=\ell_{21}=0
, then

\displaystyle u_{1,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=e^{\int_{0}^{t}\ell_{11}(\tau,x+H_{\varepsilon}(x)\tau,D_{x})d\tau}g_{1,\varepsilon}(x+H_{\varepsilon}(x)t),

\displaystyle u_{2,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=e^{\int_{0}^{t}\ell_{22}(\tau,x+H_{\varepsilon}(x)\tau,D_{x})d\tau}g_{2,\varepsilon}(x+H_{\varepsilon}(x)t).

In general for a full matrix
L
one would need the assumption (H3) to be fulfilled as well. This is investigated in the following example where we also handle singularities in time.

Let

\begin{cases}D_{t}u=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{1}(t)D_{x}&a_{12}(t,x,D_{x})\\
0&\lambda_{2}(t)D_{x}\end{bmatrix}u+\begin{bmatrix}\ell_{11}(t,x)&\ell_{12}(t,x)\\
\ell_{21}(t,x)\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}&\ell_{22}(t,x)\end{bmatrix}u,\\
u(0,x)=g(x),\end{cases}

where
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}
,
\lambda_{i}
,
i=1,2
, are distributions with compact support in
[0,T]
, and the coefficients of the matrix of the lower order terms are distributions with compact support in
t
and smooth in
x
. For the sake of simplicity we assume that
a_{12}\in C([0,T],S^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}))
. As usual we have that
u(0,x)=[g_{1}(x),g_{2}(x)]^{T}
with
g_{1},g_{2}\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
. Instead of regularising in
x
this time we regularise in
t
and restrict the domain of our nets to the interval
[0,T]
after regularisation. We obtain

\begin{cases}D_{t}u_{\varepsilon}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{1,\varepsilon}(t)D_{x}&a_{12}(t,x,D_{x})\\
0&\lambda_{2,\varepsilon}(t)D_{x}\end{bmatrix}u_{\varepsilon}+\begin{bmatrix}\ell_{11,\varepsilon}(t,x)&\ell_{12,\varepsilon}(t,x)\\
\ell_{21,\varepsilon}(t,x)\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}&\ell_{22,\varepsilon}(t,x)\end{bmatrix}u_{\varepsilon},\\
u_{\varepsilon}(0,x)=g_{\varepsilon}(x),\end{cases}

where

\lambda_{i,\varepsilon}(t)=\lambda_{i}\ast\psi_{\varepsilon},

for
t\in[0,T]
. In order to guarantee that (H1) and (H2) are fulfilled we assume that the coefficients
\lambda_{i}
are real valued for
i=1,2
and we regularise the coefficients
\ell_{ii}
,
i=1,2
via a logarithmic scale, i.e.,
\omega^{-1}(\varepsilon)=\ln(\varepsilon^{-1})
. It is easy to solve the corresponding Eikonal equation and compute the phase functions
\phi_{i,\varepsilon}
. Indeed,

\phi_{i,\varepsilon}(t,x,\xi)=x\xi+\xi\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{i,\varepsilon}(s)\,ds.

It follows that

\displaystyle|\partial_{\xi}\phi_{i,\varepsilon}(t,x,\xi)-\partial_{\xi}\phi_{i,\varepsilon}(t,y,\xi)|

\displaystyle=|x-y|\,\,\,\,\text{ for }\,\,x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\,\,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

\displaystyle|\partial_{y}\phi_{i,\varepsilon}(t,y,\xi)-\partial_{y}\phi_{i,\varepsilon}(t,y,\eta)|

\displaystyle=|\xi-\eta|\,\,\,\,\text{ for }\,\,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\,\,\xi,\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

and therefore the net
C(\varepsilon)
required in (H3) is identically equal to
1
. In addition, since the regularisation is not effecting the variable
x
all the constants involved in (H3) are independent of the parameter
\varepsilon
are therefore (H3) is trivially satisfied. It follows that our Cauchy problem is well-posed in the very weak sense.

Appendix:
L^{2}
-boundedness of Fourier integral operators

In this appendix we state an important theorem by Ruzhansky-Sugimoto
[
38
]
on the global
L^{2}
boundedness of FIO for amplitudes which are independent of the variable
x
. This theorem will be later applied to integrated FIOs.

Theorem 5.1

(Ruzhansky-Sugimoto
[
38
]
)
.

Let operator
T
be defined by

Tu(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{i(x\cdot\xi+\phi(y,\xi))}A(y,\xi)u(y)dyd\xi,

(23)

where
A(y,\xi)\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{y}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\xi})
, and
\phi(y,\xi)\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{y}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\xi})
is a real-valued function. Assume that
|\partial_{y}^{\alpha}\partial_{\xi}^{\beta}A(y,\xi)|
is uniformly bounded
for
|\alpha|,|\beta|\leq 2n+1
. Also assume that

\displaystyle|\partial_{\xi}\phi(x,\xi)-\partial_{\xi}\phi(y,\xi)|

\displaystyle\geq C|x-y|\,\,\,\,\text{ for }\,\,x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\,\,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(24)

\displaystyle|\partial_{y}\phi(y,\xi)-\partial_{y}\phi(y,\eta)|

\displaystyle\geq C|\xi-\eta|\,\,\,\,\text{ for }\,\,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\,\,\xi,\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

and that

|\partial_{y}^{\alpha}\partial_{\xi}\phi(y,\xi)|\leq C_{\alpha},\,\,|\partial_{y}\partial_{\xi}^{\beta}\phi(y,\xi)|\leq C_{\beta},

(25)

for
1\leq|\alpha|,|\beta|\leq 2n+2
. Then the operator
T
is
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
-bounded, and satisfies

||T||_{L^{2}\rightarrow L^{2}}\leq C^{\prime}\sup_{|\alpha|,|\beta|\leq 2n+1}||\partial_{y}^{\alpha}\partial_{\xi}^{\beta}A(y,\xi)||_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{y}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\xi})},

where
C^{\prime}
depends on the constants in (
24
) and (
25
).

Note that since
\Gamma_{x}=\Gamma_{y}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
in Theorem
5.1
, we may write the following assumption

|\det\partial_{x}\partial_{y}\phi(x,y)|\geq C>0,\,\,\,\text{ for all }x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

instead of (
24
).

Let us now consider operators of the type

\displaystyle Gu(t,x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{i\phi(t,x,\xi)}A(t,s,x)\hat{u}(s,\xi)d\xi ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{i\phi(t,x,\xi)-iy\cdot\xi}A(t,s,x)u(s,y)dyd\xi ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}Ku(t,s,x)ds,

where

\displaystyle Ku(s,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{i\phi(t,x,\xi)-iy\cdot\xi}A(t,s,x)u(s,y)dyd\xi.

It follows that

\displaystyle||Gu(t)||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{x})}=\int_{0}^{t}||Kg(s)||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{x})}ds

\displaystyle\leq t||K||_{L^{2}\rightarrow L^{2}}||u||_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{s})L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{x})}

\displaystyle=t||K^{*}||_{L^{2}\rightarrow L^{2}}||u||_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{s})L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{x})}.

In order to apply Theorem
5.1
, we compute the adjoint of operator
K

\displaystyle(Ku,v)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{i\phi(t,x,\xi)-iy\cdot\xi}A(t,s,x)u(s,y)dyd\xi\overline{v}(s,x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\overline{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{i\phi(t,x,\xi)-iy\cdot\xi}A(t,s,x)\overline{v}(s,x)dxd\xi}u(s,y)dy

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-i\phi(t,x,\xi)+iy\cdot\xi}A(t,s,x)v(s,x)dxd\xi u(s,y)dy

\displaystyle=(u,K^{*}v),

where

\displaystyle K^{*}v(t,s,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{ix\cdot\xi-i\phi(t,y,\xi)}A(t,s,y)v(s,y)dyd\xi.

Now the adjoint operator
K^{*}
has the same form as the operator
T
in Theorem
5.1
.

.
