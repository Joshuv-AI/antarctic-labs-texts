Permutons, meanders, and SLE-decorated Liouville quantum gravity
Jacopo Borga MIT Ewain Gwynne University of Chicago Xin Sun Peking University

Abstract
We study a class of random permutons which can be constructed from a pair of space-filling Schramm-Loewner evolution (SLE) curves on a Liouville quantum gravity (LQG) surface. This class includes the skew Brownian permutons introduced by Borga (2021), which describe the scaling limit of various types of random pattern-avoiding permutations. Another interesting permuton in our class is the meandric permuton, which corresponds to two independent SLE 8 curves on a γ \gamma -LQG surface with γ = OPEN 1 3 ​ ( 17 − 145 CLOSE ) \gamma=\sqrt{\frac{1}{3}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(17-\sqrt{145}}}\right)} . Building on work by Di Francesco, Golinelli, and Guitter (2000), we conjecture that the meandric permuton describes the scaling limit of uniform meandric permutations, i.e., the permutations induced by a simple loop in the plane which crosses a line a specified number of times. We show that for any sequence of random permutations which converges to one of the above random permutons, the length of the longest increasing subsequence is sublinear. This proves that the length of the longest increasing subsequence is sublinear for Baxter, strong-Baxter, and semi-Baxter permutations and leads to the conjecture that the same is true for meandric permutations. We also prove that the closed support of each of the random permutons in our class has Hausdorff dimension one. Finally, we prove a re-rooting invariance property for the meandric permuton and write down a formula for its expected pattern densities in terms of LQG correlation functions (which are known explicitly) and the probability that an SLE 8 hits a given set of points in numerical order (which is not known explicitly). We conclude with a list of open problems.

Introduction

1.1
Overview

A
permuton
is a probability measure
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
on
[0,1]^{2}
whose one-dimensional marginals are each equal to Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
.
Permutons are of interest because they describe the scaling limits of various types of random permutations (see
[
Bor23
, Section 1.1]
and the references therein) and they identify a natural space where one can study large deviations principles for permutations (as done in
[
Muk16
,
KKRW20
,
BDMW24
]
).
To be more precise, each permutation is associated to a permuton as follows.

Definition 1.1
.

For a permutation
\sigma
on
[1,n]\cap\mathbbm{Z}
, we define the
size
of
\sigma
by
|\sigma|=n
. We use the one-line notation to write permutations, that is, if
\sigma
is a permutation of size
n
then we write
\sigma=\sigma(1),\dots,\sigma(n)
.
We define the
permuton associated with
\sigma
to be the measure
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{\sigma}
on
[0,1]^{2}
which is equal to
|\sigma|
times Lebesgue measure on

\bigcup_{j=1}^{|\sigma|}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{j-1}{|\sigma|},\frac{j}{|\sigma|}}}\right]\times\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{\sigma(j)-1}{|\sigma|},\frac{\sigma(j)}{|\sigma|}}}\right].

Figure 1:
The permuton
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{\sigma}
associated with the permutation
\sigma=1,8,3,2,4,6,7,5
is equal to 8 times the Lebesgue measure on the gray subset of
[0,1]^{2}
.

See Figure
1
for an illustration.
We equip the space of permutons with the usual weak topology on measures, and we say that a sequence of permutations converges to a permuton
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
if their associated permutons converge weakly. We refer to
[
Bor21
, Remark 2.1.2]
for a brief history of the theory of permutons.

In this paper, we will study a certain class of random permutons which we define precisely in Section
1.2
. We will be especially interested in two special cases.

•

The
meandric permuton
is a random permuton which we define in Definition
1.7
and which we conjecture describes the scaling limit of uniform meandric permutations, i.e., the permutations induced by a pair of intersecting Jordan curves (see Section
1.3
for more details).

•

The
skew Brownian permutons
are a two-parameter family of random permutons introduced by Borga in
[
Bor23
]
which describe the scaling limits of various types of random pattern-avoiding permutations (see Section
1.4
for more details).

For various subsets of our class of permutons, including the two special cases listed above, we will show that the length of the longest increasing subsequence for any sequence of permutations converging to the permuton is sublinear (Theorem
1.13
); and that the dimension of the closed support of the permuton is one (Theorem
1.20
). We will also establish a re-rooting property for the meandric permuton (Theorem
1.22
) and a formula for its pattern densities in terms of hitting probabilities for SLE
8
and the correlation functions of the LQG area measure (Theorem
1.23
).

The common feature of all the permutons we consider is that they can be represented in terms of a
Liouville quantum gravity (LQG)
surface decorated by a pair of coupled
Schramm-Loewner evolution (SLE)
curves.
There is a vast literature concerning SLE and LQG, but not much prior knowledge of this literature is needed to understand this paper. All of the necessary background knowledge about SLE and LQG is explained in Section
2
. Our proofs will only use relatively simple statements about these objects which we re-state as necessary and which can be taken as black boxes.

1.2
Permutons constructed from SLEs and LQG

Let us now define the family of permutons which we will consider.
Fix parameters
\gamma\in(0,2)
and
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}>4
. Let
h
be a random generalized function on
\mathbbm{C}
corresponding to a singly marked unit area
\gamma
-Liouville quantum sphere, and let
\mu_{h}
be its associated
\gamma
-LQG area measure. We will review the definitions of these objects in Sections
2.2
and
2.3
, but for the time being the reader can just think of
\mu_{h}
as a random, non-atomic, Borel probability measure on
\mathbbm{C}
which assigns positive mass to every open subset of
\mathbbm{C}
.

Independently from
h
, let
(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
be a random pair consisting of a whole-plane space-filling SLE
{}_{\kappa_{1}}
curve and a whole-plane space-filling SLE
{}_{\kappa_{2}}
curve, each going from
\infty
to
\infty
. We will review the definition of these random curves in Section
2.4
, but for the time being the reader can just think of
(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
as a pair of random non-self-crossing space-filling curves in
\mathbbm{C}
which each visit almost every point of
\mathbbm{C}
exactly once. We parametrize each of
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
by
\mu_{h}
-mass, i.e., in such a way that
\mu_{h}(\eta_{1}([0,t]))=\mu_{h}(\eta_{2}([0,t]))=t
for each
t\in[0,1]
.

We emphasize that the coupling of our two SLE curves, viewed modulo time parametrization, is arbitrary. We will be interested in cases where the two curves determine each other as well as the case where the two curves are independent.

Let
\psi:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
be a Lebesgue measurable function such that

\eta_{1}(t)=\eta_{2}(\psi(t)),\quad\forall t\in[0,1].

(1.1)

The
permuton associated with
(h,\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
is the random probability measure
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
on
[0,1]^{2}
defined by

{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(A)={\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{\eta_{1},\eta_{2}}(A)=\operatorname{Leb}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{t\in[0,1]:(t,\psi(t))\in A}}\right\},\quad\text{$\forall$ Borel sets $A\subset[0,1]^{2}$},

(1.2)

where
\operatorname{Leb}
denotes Lebesgue measure. Using the fact that
\mu_{h}
-a.e. point in
\mathbbm{C}
is hit only once by each of
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
, is easy to see that the definition of
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
does not depend on the choice of
\psi
(see Lemma
2.8
).

Before we state our main results, we discuss the two special cases of the construction in this subsection which are particularly interesting. See Figure
2
for a Venn diagram of the different permutons we will consider in this paper.

Remark 1.2
.

Instead of parametrizing
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
by
\gamma
-LQG area, one could instead parametrize
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
by
\nu
-mass, where
\nu
is a non-atomic probability measure on
\mathbbm{C}
. Then the same construction as above would yield a different permuton which depends on
\nu
. In the case when
\nu
is mutually absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure on
\mathbbm{C}
, our proofs of Theorems
1.13
and
1.20
go through with minor modifications. However, we do not treat this case explicitly in this paper since we do not know of any natural permutation models which it is connected to.

Venn diagram of the different classes of random permutons considered in this paper. The outer grey oval represents all random permutons of the type constructed in (

). The meandric permuton and the skew Brownian permutons are special cases of this construction. In particular, the meandric permuton falls in the subclass of permutons constructed from independent SLEs.

1.3
Meanders, meandric permutations, and their conjectured connection to SLEs and LQG

Definition 1.3
.

A
meander
of size
n\in\mathbbm{N}
is a simple loop
\ell
(i.e., a homeomorphic image of the circle) in
\mathbbm{R}^{2}
which crosses the real line
\mathbbm{R}
exactly
2n
times and does not touch the line without crossing it, viewed modulo orientation-preserving homeomorphisms from
\mathbbm{R}^{2}
to
\mathbbm{R}^{2}
which take
\mathbbm{R}
to
\mathbbm{R}
.

See the left-hand sides of Figures
3
and
4
for some illustrations of meanders. A meander of size
n
can equivalently be thought of as a pair of Jordan curves in the sphere which intersect exactly
2n
times and which do not intersect without crossing, viewed modulo orientation-preserving homeomorphisms from the sphere to itself. The correspondence with Definition
1.3
is obtained by viewing
\mathbbm{R}
as a curve in the Riemann sphere. See the right-hand side of Figure
3
.

Figure 3:
Left:
A meander of size
6
, with the intersection points of the loop and the line labeled by the order in which they are hit by the loop (in black) and the line (in red) starting at the blue vertex. The associated meandric permutation is
\sigma_{\ell}=1,4,3,2,5,12,7,8,9,10,11,6
(in one-line notation); see Definition
1.6
below.
Right:
The same meander of size
6
seen as a pair of Jordan curves in the sphere.

The study of meanders dates back to at least the work of Poincaré
[
Poi12
]
, although the term “meander” was first coined by Arnold
[
Arn88
]
. Meanders are of interest in physics and computational biology as models of polymer folding
[
DFGG97
]
. There is a vast mathematical literature devoted to the enumeration of various types of meanders, which has connections to many different subjects, from combinatorics to theoretical physics and more recently to the geometry of moduli spaces
[
DGZZ20
]
. See
[
Zvo21
]
for a brief recent survey of this literature and
[
La 03
]
for a more detailed account. Additionally, meanders are the connected components of
meandric systems
, recently studied in
[
CKST19
,
FN19
,
Kar20
,
GNP20
,
FT23
,
BGP23
]
.

The problem of enumerating
all
meanders of size
n
is open and seemingly quite difficult. But, it was conjectured by Di Francesco, Golinelli, and Guitter
[
DFGG00
]
that the number of meanders of size
n
behaves asymptotically like
C\cdot A^{n}n^{-\alpha}
, where
\alpha=(29+\sqrt{145})/12
. This conjecture was later numerically tested by Jensen and Gutmann
[
JG00
]
, where they also proposed the numerical estimate of
A\approx 12.26
.
We will discuss this conjecture in more detail in Section
6
; see also Remark
2.6
. The best known rigorous bounds for the constant
A
are
11.380\leq A\leq 12.901
, as proved in
[
AP05
]
.

In this paper, we will be interested in the scaling limit of a uniform sample
\ell_{n}
from the set of all meanders of size
n
.
One way to formulate such a scaling limit is as follows.
We associate with
\ell_{n}
the planar map
M_{n}
whose vertices are the
2n
intersection points of the loop
\ell_{n}
with the real line, whose edges are the segments of the loop or the line
\mathbbm{R}
between these intersection points (where we consider the two infinite rays of the line
\mathbbm{R}
as being an edge from the leftmost to the rightmost intersection point), and whose faces are the connected components of
\mathbbm{R}^{2}\setminus(\ell_{n}\cup\mathbbm{R})
. The planar map
M_{n}
is equipped with two Hamiltonian paths
P_{n}^{1},P_{n}^{2}:[0,2n]\cap\mathbbm{Z}\rightarrow\{\text{vertices of $M_{n}$}\}
, one of which is induced by the left-right ordering of
\mathbbm{R}
and one of which is induced by
\ell_{n}
(these are the black and red paths in Figure
3
). We view each of these paths as starting and ending at the leftmost intersection point of
\ell_{n}
with
\mathbbm{R}
.

To talk about convergence, we can, e.g., view
(M_{n},P_{n}^{1},P_{n}^{2})
as a curve-decorated metric measure space (equipped with the graph metric and the counting measure on vertices) and ask whether it has a scaling limit with respect to the generalization of the Gromov-Hausdorff topology for curve-decorated metric measure spaces
[
GM17
]
. As we will explain in Section
6
, the arguments of
[
DFGG00
]
lead to the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1.4
.

Let
(M_{n},P_{n}^{1},P_{n}^{2})
be the random planar map decorated by two Hamiltonian paths associated with the uniform meander
\ell_{n}
as above.
Then
(M_{n},P_{n}^{1},P_{n}^{2})
converges under an appropriate scaling limit to a Liouville quantum gravity (LQG) sphere with matter central charge
{\mathbf{c}_{\mathrm{M}}}=-4
(equivalently, from (
2.12
), with coupling constant
\gamma=\sqrt{\frac{1}{3}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(17-\sqrt{145}}}\right)}
), decorated by a pair of independent whole-plane SLE
8
curves from
\infty
to
\infty
, each parametrized by LQG mass with respect to the quantum sphere.

An equivalent formulation of the conjecture is that
(M_{n},P_{n}^{1},P_{n}^{2})
should be in the same universality class as a uniform 3-tuple consisting of a planar map decorated by two spanning trees (and their associated discrete Peano curves).
See Section
6
for more details about the reasons behind the conjecture. We will not make any direct progress toward a proof of Conjecture
1.4
in this paper. Rather, we will study the conjectural limiting object directly.

Remark 1.5
.

Most results concerning SLE-decorated Liouville quantum gravity are specific to the case when
\kappa\in\{\gamma^{2},16/\gamma^{2}\}

Definition 1.7
.

The

is the permuton
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
defined as in (

\gamma=\sqrt{\frac{1}{3}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(17-\sqrt{145}}}\right)}

.

For

, let

(i.e., the permutation associated with a uniform meander of size

1.3.1
Cyclic meandric permutations

In various other literature, e.g.,
[
La 03
]
, the meandric permutation is instead defined as follows. Label the intersection points of the meander
\ell
from left to right as
1,\dots,2n
. For
j=1,\dots,2n
, let
\tau_{\ell}(j)
be the index of the intersection point hit by the loop (traversed counterclockwise) immediately after it hits
j
. Then
\tau_{\ell}
is a cycle of length
2n
. To distinguish
\tau_{\ell}
from the meandric permutation
\sigma_{\ell}
of Definition
1.6
, we call
\tau_{\ell}
the
cyclic meandric permutation
.

If we express in one-line notation the inverse
\sigma_{\ell}^{-1}
of our meandric permutation
\sigma_{\ell}
, then the same expression for
\sigma_{\ell}^{-1}
, interpreted as a permutation written in cyclic notation, is equal to
\tau_{\ell}
. The permutations
\tau_{\ell}
and
\sigma_{\ell}^{-1}
are related by

\sigma^{-1}_{\ell}(j)=\tau_{\ell}^{j-1}(1)\quad\text{and}\quad\tau_{\ell}(j)=\sigma_{\ell}^{-1}(\sigma_{\ell}(j)+1),\quad\forall j=1,\dots,2n,

(1.3)

where
2n+1
should be interpreted as
1
here. One reason why
\sigma_{\ell}
(or
\sigma_{\ell}^{-1}
) is more natural than
\tau_{\ell}
from our perspective is the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1.9
.

Let
\ell_{n}
be a uniform meander of size
n
and let
\tau_{\ell_{n}}
be its associated cyclic meandric permutation. The permuton associated with
\tau_{\ell_{n}}
converges in law to the identity permuton (i.e., the uniform measure on the diagonal in
[0,1]^{2}
) as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

See Section
6.6
for a heuristic justification of Conjecture
1.9
using Conjecture
1.4
.

1.4
Skew Brownian permutons

The skew Brownian permutons are a two-parameter family of random permutons, indexed by
(\rho,q)\in(-1,1)\times(0,1)
, which were first introduced in
[
Bor23
]
. The original construction of the skew Brownian permuton involves a pair of correlated Brownian excursions with correlation
\rho
. The parameter
q
controls, roughly speaking, the tendency of the permuton to be increasing. This can be made precise by looking at the probability that a pair of points
(X_{1},Y_{1}),(X_{2},Y_{2})
sampled from the permuton satisfy
X_{1}<X_{2}
and
Y_{1}>Y_{2}

[
BHSY23
, Proposition 1.14]
. The case
q=1/2
is symmetric, in the sense that this probability is
1/2
. We will not need the Brownian motion construction of the skew Brownian permuton in this paper.

It is shown in
[
Bor23
, Theorem 1.17]
that for each choice of parameters
(\rho,q)\in(-1,1)\times(0,1)
for the skew Brownian permuton, there exists
\gamma\in(0,2)
and a coupling of two whole-plane space-filling SLE
{}_{\kappa=16/\gamma^{2}}
curves from
\infty
to
\infty
such that the permuton (
1.2
) coincides with the skew Brownian permuton. See Theorem
2.7
below for a precise statement. This representation has previously been used to prove various properties of the skew Brownian permuton in
[
BHSY23
]
.

One of the main reasons to study skew Brownian permutons is that they describe the scaling limits of various types of pattern-avoiding random permutations, for example the following.

Definition 1.10
.

Let
\sigma
be a permutation of size
n
.

•

\sigma
is
semi-Baxter
if there does not exist
1\leq i<j<k\leq n
such that
\sigma(j+1)<\sigma(i)<\sigma(k)<\sigma(j)
.

•

\sigma
is
Baxter
if
\sigma
is semi-Baxter and additionally there does not exist
1\leq i<j<k\leq n
such that
\sigma(j)<\sigma(k)<\sigma(i)<\sigma(j+1)
.

•

\sigma
is
strong-Baxter
if
\sigma
is Baxter and additionally there does not exist
1\leq i<j<k\leq n
such that
\sigma(j+1)<\sigma(k)<\sigma(i)<\sigma(j)
.

We have the following convergence results toward skew Brownian permutons.

•

The permutons associated with uniform semi-Baxter permutations converge in law to the skew Brownian permuton with
\rho=-(1+\sqrt{5})/4
and
q=1/2

[
Bor22
]
.

•

The permutons associated with uniform Baxter permutations converge in law to the skew Brownian permuton with
\rho=-1/2
and
q=1/2

[
BM22
]
(this special case of the skew Brownian permuton is sometimes called the
Baxter permuton
).

•

The permutons associated with uniform strong-Baxter permutations converge in law to the skew Brownian permuton with parameters
\rho\approx-0.2151
and
q\approx 0.3008

[
Bor22
]
.

Remark 1.11
.

The boundary case
\rho=1
,
q\in(0,1)
for the skew Brownian permuton corresponds to the Brownian separable permuton
[
BBF+18
]
and biased versions thereof
[
BBF+20
,
BBFS20
]
, as proved in
[

. We will not consider the case

in the present paper since the representation in terms of SLE and LQG from
[

does not work the same way in this case. However, some of the results in this paper have already been proven for the Brownian separable permuton by other methods. It is conjectured in
[

1.5.1

1
1

1

We do not require that
\kappa_{i}=16/\gamma^{2}

\kappa_{1}=\kappa_{2}
.
for some choice of
(\rho,q)\in(-1,1)\times(0,1)
(Theorem
2.7
).

Let
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
be the associated permuton as in (

). Note that the possibilities for
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
include both the meandric permuton and the skew Brownian permutons. In this subsection we will consider the longest increasing subsequence for random permutations which converge to

Definition 1.12
.

For a permutation
\sigma
, we define the
length of the longest increasing (resp. decreasing) subsequence

\operatorname{LIS}(\sigma)
(resp.
\operatorname{LDS}(\sigma)
) to be the maximal cardinality of a set
L\subset[1,|\sigma|]\cap\mathbbm{Z}
such that the restriction of
\sigma
to
L
is monotone increasing (resp. decreasing).

There is a huge literature devoted to the asymptotic behavior of
\operatorname{LIS}(\sigma_{n})
and
\operatorname{LDS}(\sigma_{n})
for various types of large random permutations
\sigma_{n}
.
For uniform random permutations
\sigma_{n}
, the question of investigating
\operatorname{LIS}(\sigma_{n})
was raised in the 1960s by Ulam
[
Ula61
]
. In this case, one has
\operatorname{LIS}(\sigma_{n})\sim 2\sqrt{n}

[
Ham72
,
VK77
,
LS77
]
.
The strongest known result is due to Dauvergne and Virág
[
DV21
]
, who showed that the scaling limit of the longest increasing subsequence in a uniform permutation is the
directed geodesic
of the
directed landscape
.
The study of
\operatorname{LIS}(\sigma_{n})
is connected with many other problems in combinatorics and probability theory, such as last passage percolation and random matrix theory; we refer the reader to the book of Romik
[
Rom15
]
for an overview.

In recent years, many extensions beyond uniform permutations have been considered:

•

when
\sigma_{n}
is a uniform random pattern-avoiding permutation
[
DHW03
,
MY17
,
MY20
,
BBD+22
]
;

•

when
\sigma_{n}
is conjugacy-invariant with few cycles, in particular
Ewens-distributed

[
SK18
]
;

•

when
\sigma_{n}
follows the
Mallows distribution
, or is a product of such random permutations (
[

]
and references therein).

For the first two items the results are far from being complete, and our next result is a new contribution towards the understanding of the behavior of
\operatorname{LIS}(\sigma_{n})
for random permutations

which are not chosen uniformly from all possibilities.

Theorem 1.13
.

Assume that
(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
satisfy the hypotheses at the beginning of this subsection and let
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
be as in (

Let

be a sequence of random permutations of size

converge in law to
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
with respect to the weak topology.

is sublinear in

Theorem

will be a consequence of a general condition on a permuton which implies that any permutations converging to it have sublinear longest increasing subsequences (Proposition

Remark 1.16

The study of the length of the longest increasing subsequence in a Baxter permutation

Recall that a bipolar orientation is a planar map equipped with an acyclic orientation of the edges with exactly one source (i.e. a vertex with only outgoing edges) and one sink (i.e. a vertex with only incoming edges), both on the outer face. Uniform bipolar orientations have been studied, e.g., in

1.5.2
Dimension of the support is one

Definition 1.19
.

The
closed support
of a permuton
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
is the set

\operatorname{supp}{\boldsymbol{\pi}}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\text{intersection of all closed sets $K\subset[0,1]^{2}$ with ${\boldsymbol{\pi}}(K)=1$}}}\right).

(1.4)

For a general permuton, the Hausdorff dimension of its closed support can be any number in
[1,2]
. In particular, there exist permutons constructed from pairs of space-filling curves for which the Hausdorff dimension of the support is arbitrarily close to two (see Remark
4.4
). Our next theorem states that the closed supports of the permutons considered in this paper all have Hausdorff dimension one.

Theorem 1.20
.

Assume that we are in the setting of Section
1.2
with an
arbitrary
choice of
\gamma\in(0,2)
,
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}>4
, and coupling
(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
. Let
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
be as in (
1.2
). Let

\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2}):=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{(t,s)\in[0,1]^{2}:\eta_{1}(t)=\eta_{2}(s)}}\right\}.

(1.5)

Almost surely, both the closed support
\operatorname{supp}{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
and the set
\mathcal{T}
have Hausdorff dimension one.

It is easy to see that Hausdorff dimension of the support of every permuton is at least one. So, Theorem
1.20
says that
\operatorname{supp}{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
is in some sense as small as possible. Equivalently,
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
is as far as possible from being absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure.

It is easy to check that
\operatorname{supp}{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\subset\mathcal{T}
(Lemma
2.9
), but the reverse inclusion need not hold. For example, if
\eta_{1}=\eta_{2}
a.s. then
\operatorname{supp}{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
is the diagonal in
[0,1]^{2}
but
\mathcal{T}
contains off-diagonal points which arise from points in
\mathbbm{C}
which are hit more than once by
\eta_{1}
or
\eta_{2}
. It is in general a subtle question to determine which points of
\mathcal{T}
belong to
\operatorname{supp}{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
. This question was recently addressed in
[
BG24
]
.

The following corollary is a special case of Theorem
1.20
. It in particular answers a question from
[
Bor23
, Section 1.6]
.

Corollary 1.21
.

For each choice of parameters
(\rho,q)\in(-1,1)\times(0,1)
, the closed support of the skew Brownian permuton with parameters
\rho,q
has Hausdorff dimension 1. The same is true for the meandric permuton.

It is shown in
[
Maa20
, Theorem 1.5]
that the dimension of the closed support of the biased Brownian separable permuton (i.e. the skew Brownian permuton with
\rho=1
and
q\in(0,1)
; recall Remark
1.11
) is equal to 1. In fact, the one-dimensional Hausdorff measure of its support is bounded above by
\sqrt{2}
. The proof in
[
Maa20
]
is based on a description of the biased Brownian separable permuton in terms of Brownian motion, so it is possible (but not obvious) that a related proof could work for the skew Brownian permutons. However, arguments like those in
[
Maa20
]
would not work for the meandric permuton or for other permutons generated from a pair of independent space-filling SLE curves.

Our proof of Theorem
1.20
, given in Section
4

[
AFS20
,
GS24
]
.

Pattern density for the meandric permuton

Let
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
be a (possibly random) permuton and
n
be a positive integer. Let
(\vec{X},\vec{Y})=(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i\in[1,n]\cap\mathbbm{Z}}

n

[0,1]^{2}

{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
.

n

j\in[1,n]\cap\mathbbm{Z}

\tau(j)

j

\tau(j)

\sigma

2.1
Gaussian free field

In what follows, we use the notation

B_{r}(z):=\text{open Euclidean ball of radius $r$ centered at $z$}.

(2.1)

For
z,w\in\mathbbm{C}
, we also define

|z|_{+}:=\max\{|z|,1\}\quad\text{and}\quad G_{\mathbbm{C}}(z,w):=\log\frac{|z|_{+}|w|_{+}}{|z-w|}.

(2.2)

The
whole-plane Gaussian free field (GFF)
is the centered Gaussian random generalized function (distribution) on
\mathbbm{C}
with covariances

\operatorname{Cov}(h^{\mathbbm{C}}(z),h^{\mathbbm{C}}(w)):=G_{\mathbbm{C}}(z,w).

(2.3)

Note that
h^{\mathbbm{C}}
is not well-defined pointwise since the covariance kernel (
2.2
) diverges to
\infty
as
z\rightarrow w
.
Nevertheless, for
z\in\mathbbm{C}
and
r>0
, one can define the average of
h^{\mathbbm{C}}
over the circle
\partial B_{r}(z)
, which we denote by
h^{\mathbbm{C}}_{r}(z)
.
See
[
DS11
, Section 3.1]
for further discussion.

One often views the whole-plane GFF as being defined modulo additive constant. Our choice of covariance kernel in (
2.3
) corresponds to fixing the additive constant so that the circle average

is zero (see, e.g.,
[

). The law of the whole-plane GFF, viewed modulo additive constant, is invariant under complex affine transformations of

h^{\mathbbm{C}}\overset{d}{=}h^{\mathbbm{C}}(a\cdot+b)-h^{\mathbbm{C}}_{|a|}(b),\quad\forall a\in\mathbbm{C}\setminus\{0\},\quad\forall b\in\mathbbm{C}.

(2.4)

Let

be an open domain such that Brownian motion started at any point of

2.2
Liouville quantum gravity

In this subsection we give a brief review of the theory of Liouville quantum gravity (LQG). Our intention is to provide just enough background for the reader to understand the proofs in this paper. We refer to
[
Gwy20
,
She23
]
for brief introduction to LQG and to
[
BP24
]
for a detailed exposition.
Let
\gamma\in(0,2)
and let

Q:=\frac{2}{\gamma}+\frac{\gamma}{2}.

(2.5)

Definition 2.1
.

A
\gamma
-Liouville quantum gravity (LQG) surface with
k\in\mathbbm{N}_{0}
marked points
is an equivalence class of
k+2
-tuples
(U,h,z_{1},\dots,z_{k})
, where

•

U\subset\mathbbm{C}
is open and
z_{1},\dots,z_{k}\in U\cup\partial U
, with
\partial U
viewed as a collection of prime ends;

•

h
is a generalized function on
U
(which we will always take to be some variant of the Gaussian free field);

•

(U,h,z_{1},\dots,z_{k})
and
(\widetilde{U},\widetilde{h},\widetilde{z}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{z}_{k})
are declared to be equivalent if there is a conformal map
\phi:\widetilde{U}\rightarrow U
such that

\widetilde{h}=h\circ\phi+Q\log|\phi^{\prime}|\quad\text{and}\quad\phi(\widetilde{z}_{j})=z_{j},\quad\forall j=1,\dots k,

(2.6)

where
Q
is as in (
2.5
).

If
(U,h,z_{1},\dots,z_{k})
is an equivalence class representative, we refer to
h
as an
embedding
of the quantum surface into
(U,z_{1},\dots,z_{k})
.

In this paper, all of the LQG surfaces we consider will be random, and the generalized function
h
will be a
GFF plus a continuous function
, meaning that there is a coupling
(h,h^{U})
of
h
with the whole-plane or zero-boundary GFF
h^{U}
on
U
(as appropriate) such that a.s.
h-h^{U}
is a function which is continuous on
U
except possibly at finitely many points.

If
h
is a whole-plane GFF plus a continuous function, one can define the
LQG area measure

\mu_{h}
on
U
, which is a limit of regularized versions of
e^{\gamma h}\,d^{2}z
, where
d^{2}z
denotes Lebesgue measure on
U

[
Kah85
,
DS11
,
RV11
]
. Almost surely, the measure
\mu_{h}
assigns positive mass to every open set and zero mass to every point, but is mutually singular with respect to Lebesgue measure.

It was shown in
[
DDDF20
,
GM21b
]
that one can also define the
LQG metric

D_{h}
on
U
, which is a limit of regularized versions of the Riemannian distance function associated with the Riemannian metric tensor
e^{\gamma h}(dx^{2}+dy^{2})
, where
dx^{2}+dy^{2}
is the Euclidean metric tensor on
U
. Almost surely, the metric
D_{h}
induces the same topology on
U
as the Euclidean metric and is a length metric (i.e.,
D_{h}(z,w)
is the infimum of the
D_{h}
-lengths of paths in
U
from
z
to
w
). The Hausdorff dimension of
(U,D_{h})
is a.s. equal to a deterministic,
\gamma
-dependent constant
d_{\gamma}>2
which is not known explicitly
[
GP22
, Corollary 1.7]
. See
[
DDG23
]
for a survey of known results about the LQG metric.

Both
\mu_{h}
and
D_{h}
are compatible with coordinate changes of the form (
2.6
). That is, if
\phi:\widetilde{U}\rightarrow U
is a conformal map and
h
and
\widetilde{h}
are related as in (
2.6
), then a.s.

\mu_{h}(A)=\mu_{\widetilde{h}}(\phi^{-1}(A)),\quad\text{$\forall$ Borel set $A\subset U$}\quad\text{and}\quad D_{h}(z,w)=D_{\widetilde{h}}(\phi^{-1}(z),\phi^{-1}(w)),\quad\forall z,w\in U.

(2.7)

See
[
DS11
, Proposition 2.1]
for
\mu_{h}
and
[
GM21a
, Theorem 1.3]
for
D_{h}
.

Both
\mu_{h}
and
D_{h}
are also locally determined by
h
. In the case of
\mu_{h}
, this means that for each open set
V\subset\mathbbm{C}
, a.s.
\mu_{h}|_{V}
is a measurable function of
h|_{V}
(this follows, e.g., from the circle average construction of
\mu_{h}
in
[
DS11
]
).
In the case of
D_{h}
, the locality property says the following. For
V\subset\mathbbm{C}
, we define the
internal metric on
V
to be the metric
D_{h}(\cdot,\cdot;V)
on
V
defined by

D_{h}(z,w;V)=\inf\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\text{$D_{h}$-length of $P$}}}\right)\,:\,\text{$P$ is a path in $V$ from $z$ to $w$}}}\right\}.

(2.8)

Then a.s.
D_{h}(\cdot,\cdot;V)
is given by a measurable function of
h|_{V}

[
GM21b
, Axiom II]
.

2.3
The quantum sphere

For
\gamma\in(0,2)
, the unit area quantum sphere is the canonical LQG surface with the topology of the sphere. One sense in which this LQG surface is canonical is that it is expected, and in some cases proven, to describe the scaling limit of various types of random planar maps. There are a variety of different ways of defining the unit area quantum sphere. The first definitions appeared in
[
DMS21
,
DKRV16
]
and were proven to be equivalent in
[
AHS17
]
. The definition we give here is
[
AHS17
, Definition 2.2]
with
k=3
and
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=\alpha_{3}=\gamma
.

Definition 2.2
.

Using the notation (
2.2
), we define the
Liouville field

h_{\operatorname{L}}:=h^{\mathbbm{C}}+\gamma G_{\mathbbm{C}}(1,\cdot)+\gamma G_{\mathbbm{C}}(0,\cdot)-(2Q-\gamma)\log|\cdot|_{+}

(2.9)

and we let
\mu_{h_{\operatorname{L}}}
be its associated
\gamma
-LQG measure.

•

The
triply marked unit area quantum sphere
is the LQG surface
(\mathbbm{C},h,\infty,0,1)
, where the law
\mathbbm{P}_{h}
of
h
is obtained from the law
\mathbbm{P}_{\tilde{h}_{\operatorname{L}}}
of

\tilde{h}_{\operatorname{L}}:=h_{\operatorname{L}}-\frac{1}{\gamma}\log\mu_{h_{\operatorname{L}}}(\mathbbm{C})

(2.10)

weighted by a
\gamma
-dependent constant times
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mu_{h_{\operatorname{L}}}(\mathbbm{C})}}\right]^{4/\gamma^{2}-2}
, i.e.
\frac{d\mathbbm{P}_{h}}{d\mathbbm{P}_{\tilde{h}_{\operatorname{L}}}}=C(\gamma)\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mu_{h_{\operatorname{L}}}(\mathbbm{C})}}\right]^{4/\gamma^{2}-2}
.

•

The
doubly (resp. singly) marked unit area quantum sphere
is the doubly (resp. singly) marked quantum surface obtained from the triply marked unit area quantum sphere by forgetting the marked point at 1 (resp. the marked points at 1 and 0).

We note that subtracting
\frac{1}{\gamma}\log\mu_{h_{\operatorname{L}}}(\mathbbm{C})
in (
2.10
) makes it so that the unit area quantum sphere satisfies
\mu_{h}(\mathbbm{C})=1
a.s. The triply marked quantum sphere has only one possible embedding in
(\mathbbm{C},\infty,0,1)
(Definition
2.1
). This is because the only conformal automorphism of the Riemann sphere which fixes
\infty,0,1
is the identity. By contrast, the singly (resp. doubly) marked quantum sphere has multiple possible embeddings, obtained by applying (
2.6
) when
\phi
is a complex affine transformation (resp. a rotation and scaling). Any statements for a singly or doubly marked quantum sphere are assumed to be independent of the choice of embedding, unless otherwise specified. Many of the results in this paper are stated for a singly marked quantum sphere since we only need one marked point (i.e., the starting point of the SLE curves). However, it is sometimes convenient to work with a triply marked quantum sphere so that we can apply the exact description of the field from Definition
2.2
.

It is shown in
[
DMS21
, Proposition A.13]
that the marked points for the quantum sphere are independent samples from its associated LQG area measure.

Lemma 2.3

(
[
DMS21
]
)
.

Let
(\mathbbm{C},h,\infty,0,1)
be a triply marked unit area
\gamma
-Liouville quantum sphere. Conditional on

, let

be conditionally independent samples from the
\gamma
-LQG area measure

. Then

, and

The following absolute continuity statement allows us to transfer various results from the whole-plane GFF to the quantum sphere.

.

Let

be a triply marked quantum sphere, let

Let

be the Liouville field from (

), defined using the same whole-plane GFF

Remark 2.5

(Infinite measure on quantum spheres)
.

There is a natural infinite measure on (free area) quantum spheres which appears in

DKRV16
]
.

We then consider the quantum surface

Remark 2.6

(Physics interpretation and random planar maps connection)
.

A
\gamma
-LQG surface can be interpreted as a model of gravity (essentially, random geometry) coupled to matter, where the matter is represented by a conformal field theory (CFT). The central charge of this CFT is related to the coupling constant
\gamma\in(0,2)
by

{\mathbf{c}_{\mathrm{M}}}=25-6\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2}{\gamma}+\frac{\gamma}{2}}}\right)^{2}\in(-\infty,1).

(2.12)

This interpretation can be made rigorous by looking at random planar maps. Indeed, suppose that
(M_{n},S_{n})
is a random planar map with
n
edges decorated by a statistical physics model. We sample
M_{n}
and
S_{n}
simultaneously, so the marginal law of
M_{n}
is given by the uniform measure on some collection of planar maps weighted by the partition function of
S_{n}
. Suppose our statistical physics model
S_{n}
is such that the scaling limit of the version of
S_{n}
in a regular lattice (e.g.,
\mathbbm{Z}^{2}
) is described by a CFT with central charge
{\mathbf{c}_{\mathrm{M}}}
. Then in many cases the scaling limit of
M_{n}
(e.g., with respect to the Gromov-Hausdorff distance) should be described by
\gamma
-LQG, where
\gamma
and
{\mathbf{c}_{\mathrm{M}}}
are related as in (
2.12
). As an example, the scaling limit of the critical Ising model on
\mathbbm{Z}^{2}
is described by a CFT with central charge
{\mathbf{c}_{\mathrm{M}}}=1/2
, so random planar maps weighted by the critical Ising model partition function should converge to LQG with matter central charge
{\mathbf{c}_{\mathrm{M}}}=1/2
, equivalently,
\gamma=\sqrt{3}
. See
[
GHS23
, Section 3.1]
for more discussion. Using the formula for the law of the area for the infinite measure on single marked quantum spheres (Remark
2.5
), it is conjectured that the partition function of
(M_{n},S_{n})
grows like

where

and

\alpha=1+\frac{4}{\gamma^{2}}.

(2.13)

This explains why Conjecture

is related to the meander exponent

in Section

Almost surely, the permuton

Proof of Lemma

.

The formula (

For each

We also have the following basic lemma about the closed support of

Almost surely, for any choice of the function

Proof.

Both inclusions in (

Lemma 2.10

Almost surely, for any choice of the function

from (

), the following is true. For each time

is the unique

.
Furthermore, for each fixed

(t,s)\in\operatorname{supp}{\boldsymbol{\pi}}

Proof.

Since

is a permuton, we have

3
Longest increasing subsequence is sublinear

In this section we will prove Theorem
1.13
, which asserts that the longest increasing subsequence is sublinear for permutations which converge to
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
, where
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
is a permuton constructed from SLE and LQG which satisfies one of the two conditions in Section
1.5.1
.

3.1
Longest increasing subsequences and monotone sets for permutons

To prove Theorem
1.13
, we will use a general criterion on a permuton which implies a sublinear bound on the longest increasing subsequence for converging permutations, which we now state and prove.

Figure 8:
A set
A\subset[0,1]^{2}
is monotone if for any point
(t,s)\in A
, the set
A
is contained in the union of the green rectangles in the figure.

Definition 3.1
.

We say that a set
A\subset[0,1]^{2}
is
monotone
if

A\subset\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left([0,t]\times[0,s]}}\right)\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left([t,1]\times[s,1]}}\right),\quad\forall(t,s)\in A.

(3.1)

See Figure
8
for an illustration. An example of a monotone set is the graph of a non-decreasing function
[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
, or a subset of such a graph. The reason for our interest in monotone sets is the following deterministic result.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
be a permuton and let
\{\sigma_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbbm{N}}
be a sequence of permutations of size
|\sigma_{n}|\rightarrow\infty
whose associated permutons
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{\sigma_{n}}
converge weakly to
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
.
Assume that
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(A)=0
for every monotone set
A\subset[0,1]^{2}
(Definition
3.1
).
Then the longest increasing subsequence of
\sigma_{n}
is sublinear, i.e.,
\operatorname{LIS}(\sigma_{n})/|\sigma_{n}|\rightarrow 0
.

The proof of Proposition
3.2
is elementary, but we are not aware of a reference for the statement or proof, so we provide the proof here.

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

We will prove the contrapositive, i.e., we will assume that the largest increasing subsequence of
\sigma_{n}
is not sublinear and show that there is a monotone subset of
[0,1]^{2}
with positive
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
-mass.

Our assumption implies that after possibly replacing
\{\sigma_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbbm{N}}
by a subsequence, we can find
c>0
such that for each
n\in\mathbbm{N}
, there is an increasing subsequence of
\sigma_{n}
of length at least
c|\sigma_{n}|
.
That is, for each
n\in\mathbbm{N}
, there is a set
L_{n}\subset[1,|\sigma_{n}|]\cap\mathbbm{Z}
such that
\#L_{n}\geq c|\sigma_{n}|
and
\sigma_{n}|_{L_{n}}
is monotone increasing.
For
n\in\mathbbm{N}
, let

A_{n}:=\bigcup_{j\in L_{n}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{j-1}{|\sigma_{n}|},\frac{j}{|\sigma_{n}|}}}\right]\times\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{\sigma_{n}(j)-1}{|\sigma_{n}|},\frac{\sigma_{n}(j)}{|\sigma_{n}|}}}\right]\subset[0,1]^{2}.

(3.2)

By Definition
1.1
of
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{\sigma_{n}}
, we have
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{\sigma_{n}}(A_{n})\geq c
.

By compactness, after possibly passing to a further subsequence of
\{\sigma_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbbm{N}}
, we can arrange that
A_{n}
converges in the Hausdorff distance to a closed set
A\subset[0,1]^{2}
. We first argue that
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(A)>0
. Indeed, for each
\varepsilon>0
, it holds for each large enough
n\in\mathbbm{N}
that
A_{n}
is contained in the Euclidean
\varepsilon
-neighborhood
B_{\varepsilon}(A)
. Since
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{\sigma_{n}}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
weakly,

{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\overline{B_{\varepsilon}(A)})\geq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{\sigma_{n}}(\overline{B_{\varepsilon}(A)})\geq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{\sigma_{n}}(A_{n})\geq c.

(3.3)

Sending
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
gives
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(A)\geq c>0
.

We next claim that
A
is a monotone set in the sense of Definition
3.1
. To this end, let
(t,s)\in A
. Since
A_{n}\rightarrow A
in the Hausdorff distance, by (
3.2
) we can find a
j_{n}\in L_{n}
such that
(j_{n},\sigma_{n}(j_{n}))/|\sigma_{n}|\rightarrow(t,s)
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Since
\sigma_{n}
is monotone on
L_{n}
, it holds for each
n\in\mathbbm{N}
that

A_{n}\subset\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,\frac{j_{n}}{|\sigma_{n}|}}}\right]\times\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,\frac{\sigma_{n}(j_{n})}{|\sigma_{n}|}}}\right]}}\right)\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{j_{n}}{|\sigma_{n}|},1}}\right]\times\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{\sigma_{n}(j_{n})}{|\sigma_{n}|},1}}\right]}}\right).

(3.4)

Since
(j_{n},\sigma_{n}(j_{n}))/|\sigma_{n}|\rightarrow(t,s)
, for each
\varepsilon>0
it holds for each large enough
n\in\mathbbm{N}
that the set on the right side of (
4.11
) is contained in the
\varepsilon
-neighborhood of
([0,t]\times[0,s])\cup([t,1]\times[s,1])
. Since
A_{n}\rightarrow A
, it follows that for each
\varepsilon>0
,

A\subset\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\text{$\varepsilon$-neighborhood of $([0,t]\times[0,s])\cup([t,1]\times[s,1])$}}}\right).

(3.5)

Sending
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
shows that
A
is a monotone set.
∎

3.2
Monotone sets for permutons constructed from SLEs and LQG

Henceforth assume that we are in the setting of Theorem
1.13
. To prove the theorem, we need to check the condition of Proposition
3.2
for the random permuton
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
. This turns out to be straightforward using SLE and LQG theory.

Let
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
be the coupled or independent space-filling SLE curves of parameters
\kappa_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}
from Theorem
1.13
.
For
z\in\mathbbm{C}
, let
\mathcal{Q}_{z}
be the set of
w\in\mathbbm{C}
such that
\eta_{1}
hits
w
before it hits
z
and
\eta_{2}
hits
w
after it hits
z
. More precisely,
w\in\mathcal{Q}_{z}
if and only if

\exists t_{1},s_{1},t_{2},s_{2}\in[0,1]\quad\text{such that}\quad t_{1}\leq s_{1},\quad t_{2}\leq s_{2},\quad\eta_{1}(s_{1})=\eta_{2}(t_{2})=z,\quad\eta_{1}(t_{1})=\eta_{2}(s_{2})=w.

(3.6)

See Figure
9
for an illustration of
\mathcal{Q}_{z}
.

We claim that
\mathcal{Q}_{z}
is a closed set. Indeed, if
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
each hit
z
only once (say at times
s_{1}
and
t_{2}
, respectively) then
\mathcal{Q}_{z}=\eta_{1}([0,s_{1}])\cap\eta_{2}([t_{2},1])
is the intersection of closed sets, so is closed (note that this is the case for
\mu_{h}
-a.e.
z\in\mathbbm{C}
). In general, each of
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
hits
z
at most finitely many times (recall Section
2.4
), so
\mathcal{Q}_{z}
is the union of finitely many closed sets of the above type.
Furthermore,
\mathcal{Q}_{z}
depends only on
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
viewed modulo time parametrization, so
\mathcal{Q}_{z}
is independent from
h
.

To prove Theorem
1.13
, we will first show that for
\mu_{h}
-a.e.
z\in\mathbbm{C}
, the set
\mathcal{Q}_{z}
has positive density with respect to
\mu_{h}
, i.e., we will prove the following lemma.

and

are two space-filling counterflow lines of a GFF with angles

(Theorem

). This case is illustrated in the left and middle panels. The red, blue, green, and purple curves do not cross each other, but may hit each other and bounce off (as shown in the second panel). One can determine the values of

. In the case of two independent space-filling SLEs (right panel), the red and blue curves can cross the green and purple curves, so the region

is not connected. The three pictures in the case when

(resp.

.

Lemma 3.3
.

Assume that we are in the setting of Theorem
1.13
.
Almost surely,

\limsup_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{\mu_{h}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{Q}_{z}\cap B_{r}(z)}}\right)}{\mu_{h}(B_{r}(z))}>0,\quad\text{for $\mu_{h}$-a.e.\ $z\in\mathbbm{C}$},

(3.7)

where
B_{r}(z)
is a Euclidean ball as in (
2.1
).

We will eventually show that every monotone set for
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
has zero
\mu_{h}
-mass by combining Lemma
3.3
with the Lebesgue density theorem for the Radon measure
\mu_{h}
(see the proof of Proposition
3.6
).
We will prove Lemma
3.3
via a scaling argument. To do this, we need to work in an infinite-volume setting, i.e., we need to work with a field which satisfies an exact spatial scale invariance property in law (the unit area quantum sphere does not satisfy such a property). We choose to work with a whole-plane GFF with a
\gamma
-log singularity at 0 since the local behavior of this field near zero is the same as the local behavior of the quantum sphere field near a point sampled from its LQG area measure
\mu_{h}
(see Lemma
2.4
for a precise statement).

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\gamma\in(0,2)
and
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}>4
. Let
\widetilde{h}
be a whole-plane GFF plus
\gamma\log|\cdot|^{-1}
. Let
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
be two whole-plane space-filling SLE curves from
\infty
to
\infty
of parameters
\kappa_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}
, sampled independently from
\widetilde{h}
. Assume that
(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
are coupled together in one of the two possible ways listed just before Theorem
1.13
.
Define the set
\mathcal{Q}_{0}
as in (
3.6
).
Almost surely,

\limsup_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{\mu_{\widetilde{h}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{Q}_{0}\cap B_{r}(0)}}\right)}{\mu_{\widetilde{h}}(B_{r}(0))}>0.

(3.8)

Proof.

We first claim that a.s.
\mathcal{Q}_{0}
has non-empty interior.
For
i\in\{1,2\}
, let
\tau_{0}^{i}
be the (a.s. unique) time when
\eta_{i}
hits 0.
By the construction of space-filling SLE in Section
2.4
, the set
\partial\eta_{i}((-\infty,\tau_{0}^{i}])
is the union of two SLE
{}_{16/\kappa_{i}}
-type curves
\beta_{i}^{L}
and
\beta_{i}^{R}
from 0 to
\infty
, namely, the left and right boundaries of
\eta_{i}((-\infty,\tau_{0}^{i}])
.
The set
\mathcal{Q}_{0}
is the closure of the intersection of the region
\eta_{1}((-\infty,\tau_{0}^{1}])
which lies to the left of
\beta_{1}^{L}
and to the right of
\beta_{1}^{R}
; and the region
\eta_{2}([\tau_{0}^{2},\infty))
which lies to the right of
\beta_{2}^{L}
and to the left of
\beta_{2}^{R}
. See Figure
9
for an illustration.

In the case when
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
are coupled together as in the SLE/LQG description of the skew Brownian permuton, we recall from Section
2.4
that
\beta_{1}^{L},\beta_{1}^{R},\beta_{2}^{L}
, and
\beta_{2}^{R}
are the flow lines of a whole-plane GFF with angles
\pi/2
,
-\pi/2
,
\theta
, and
\theta-\pi
, respectively.
It follows from
[
MS17
, Proposition 3.28]
that a.s.
\beta_{2}^{R}\subset\eta_{1}((-\infty,\tau_{0}^{1}])
,
\beta_{2}^{R}
does not spend a non-trivial interval of time tracing
\partial\eta_{1}((-\infty,\tau_{0}^{1}])
, and the intersection of
\beta_{2}^{R}
with the closure of each connected component of the interior of
\eta_{1}((-\infty,\tau_{0}^{1}])
is a simple curve between two points of the boundary of the component. Each of these closures is homeomorphic to a closed Euclidean disk. By the Jordan curve theorem,
\beta_{2}^{R}
divides each connected component of the interior of
\eta_{1}((-\infty,\tau_{0}^{1}])
into two non-empty regions. The one of these regions to the left of
\beta_{2}^{R}
is contained in
\mathcal{Q}_{0}
; hence
\mathcal{Q}_{0}
has non-empty interior.

In the case when
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
are independent, modulo time parametrization, we first note that
\beta_{2}^{L}
has a positive chance to cross
\beta_{1}^{L}
(this follows, e.g., from
[
MS17
, Lemma 3.8]
applied under the conditional law given
\beta_{1}^{L}
). By the scale invariance of the joint law of
(\beta_{1}^{L},\beta_{2}^{L})
, we get that there exists
q>0
such that for every
\varepsilon>0
,
\beta_{1}^{L}
and
\beta_{2}^{L}
have probability at least
q
to cross before leaving
B_{\varepsilon}(0)
. By a tail triviality argument (using the Brownian motions that determine the Loewner driving functions for
\beta_{1}^{L}
and
\beta_{2}^{L}
), we get that in fact
\beta_{1}^{L}
and
\beta_{2}^{L}
a.s. cross infinitely many times. Consequently, a.s. there exist infinitely many open regions which lie to the left of
\beta_{1}^{L}
and to the right of
\beta_{2}^{L}
. Since the ranges of
\beta_{1}^{R}
and
\beta_{2}^{R}
are closed sets of empty interior, it follows that a.s. each of these regions contains an open subset of
\mathcal{Q}_{0}
. This concludes the proof of our claim.

The joint law of
(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
, viewed as curves modulo time parametrization, is invariant under scaling space. Hence the same is true of the law of
\mathcal{Q}_{0}
. Therefore, a.s.
\mathcal{Q}_{0}\cap B_{r}(0)
has non-empty interior for every
r>0
.
Since a.s.
\mu_{\widetilde{h}}
assigns positive mass to every open set, we get that a.s.
\mu_{\widetilde{h}}(\mathcal{Q}_{0}\cap B_{r}(0))>0
for every
r>0
.

By the LQG coordinate change formula (
2.7
), a.s. for each
r>0
,

\mu_{\widetilde{h}(r\cdot)+Q\log r}(A)=\mu_{\widetilde{h}}(rA),\quad\text{for each Borel set $A\subset\mathbbm{C}$.}

(3.9)

Furthermore, by (
2.4
), the law of
\widetilde{h}
is scale invariant modulo additive constant, i.e., for each
r>0
there is a random
C_{r}>0
such that
\widetilde{h}(r\cdot)-C_{r}\overset{d}{=}\widetilde{h}
.
Adding a constant
C
to
\widetilde{h}
results in scaling
\mu_{\widetilde{h}}
by
e^{\gamma C}
, so does not change the ratios of the
\mu_{\widetilde{h}}
-masses of any two sets. From this, (
3.9
), and the scale invariance of the law of
\mathcal{Q}_{0}
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{\widetilde{h}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{Q}_{0}\cap B_{r}(0)}}\right)}{\mu_{\widetilde{h}}(B_{r}(0))}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{\widetilde{h}(r\cdot)+Q\log r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((r^{-1}\mathcal{Q}_{0})\cap B_{1}(0)}}\right)}{\mu_{\widetilde{h}(r\cdot)+Q\log r}(B_{1}(0))}\qquad\text{(by~\eqref{eqn-cone-coord})}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\gamma C_{r}}r^{\gamma Q}\mu_{\widetilde{h}(r\cdot)-C_{r}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((r^{-1}\mathcal{Q}_{0})\cap B_{1}(0)}}\right)}{e^{\gamma C_{r}}r^{\gamma Q}\mu_{\widetilde{h}(r\cdot)-C_{r}}(B_{1}(0))}\quad\text{(by the scaling property of $\mu_{\widetilde{h}}$)}

\displaystyle\overset{d}{=}\frac{\mu_{\widetilde{h}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{Q}_{0}\cap B_{1}(0)}}\right)}{\mu_{\widetilde{h}}(B_{1}(0))}\quad\text{(by scale invariance in law)}.

Thus, the law of
\mu_{\widetilde{h}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{Q}_{0}\cap B_{r}(0)}}\right)/\mu_{\widetilde{h}}(B_{r}(0))
does not depend on
r
. As we noted above, this random variable is a.s. positive.
Consequently, for each
p\in(0,1)
there exists
c>0
such that

\mathbbm{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{\mu_{\widetilde{h}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{Q}_{0}\cap B_{r}(0)}}\right)}{\mu_{\widetilde{h}}(B_{r}(0))}\geq c}}\right]\geq p,\quad\forall r>0.

(3.10)

Hence, with probability at least
p
, there exist arbitrarily small values of
r>0
for which

\frac{\mu_{\widetilde{h}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{Q}_{0}\cap B_{r}(0)}}\right)}{\mu_{\widetilde{h}}(B_{r}(0))}\geq c.

Since
p
is arbitrary, the limsup in (
3.8
) is a.s. positive.
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.3
.

Conditional on
(h,\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
, let
Z
be sampled from the probability measure
\mu_{h}
. It suffices to show that a.s.

\limsup_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{\mu_{h}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{Q}_{Z}\cap B_{r}(Z)}}\right)}{\mu_{h}(B_{r}(Z))}>0.

(3.11)

Let
W
be another point sampled from
\mu_{h}
in such a way that
Z,W
are conditionally independent given
h
.
By Lemma
2.3
, the law of
(\mathbbm{C},h,\infty,Z,W)
is that of a triply marked unit area quantum sphere.
That is, if
\phi:\mathbbm{C}\rightarrow\mathbbm{C}
is the complex affine map with
\phi(0)=Z
and
\phi(1)=W
, then the surface
(\mathbbm{C},h\circ\phi+Q\log|\phi^{\prime}|,\infty,0,1)
has the law described in Definition
2.2
.
By Lemma
2.4
, the restriction of
h\circ\phi+Q\log|\phi^{\prime}|
to
B_{1/2}(0)
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\widetilde{h}|_{B_{1/2}(0)}
, where
\widetilde{h}
is a whole-plane GFF plus
\gamma\log|\cdot|^{-1}
(as in Lemma
3.4
), with both random distributions viewed modulo additive constant. We note that adding a constant to the field does not change any of the ratios in (
3.11
).

The law of
(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
, viewed modulo time parametrization, is invariant under scaling and spatial translation, and
(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
is independent from
(Z,W)
, hence from
\phi
. Consequently,
\phi^{-1}(\mathcal{Q}_{Z})\overset{d}{=}\mathcal{Q}_{0}
and
\phi^{-1}(\mathcal{Q}_{Z})
is independent from
h
.
By combining this with the absolute continuity in the previous paragraph, we get that the joint law of
((h\circ\phi+Q\log|\phi^{\prime}|)|_{B_{1/2}(0)},\phi^{-1}(\mathcal{Q}_{Z}))
is absolutely continuous with respect to the joint law of
(\widetilde{h}|_{B_{1/2}(0)},\mathcal{Q}_{0})
, with the distributions viewed modulo additive constant.
We can therefore apply Lemma
3.4
to get that a.s.

\limsup_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{\mu_{h\circ\phi+Q\log|\phi^{\prime}|}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi^{-1}(\mathcal{Q}_{Z})\cap B_{r}(0)}}\right)}{\mu_{h\circ\phi+Q\log|\phi^{\prime}|}(B_{r}(0))}>0.

(3.12)

Since
\phi
is complex affine,
\phi(B_{r}(0))=B_{|\phi^{\prime}(0)|r}(Z)
. By this and the LQG coordinate change formula (
2.7
),

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{h\circ\phi+Q\log|\phi^{\prime}|}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi^{-1}(\mathcal{Q}_{Z})\cap B_{r}(0)}}\right)}{\mu_{h\circ\phi+Q\log|\phi^{\prime}|}(B_{r}(0))}=\frac{\mu_{h}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{Q}_{Z}\cap B_{|\phi^{\prime}(0)|r}(Z)}}\right)}{\mu_{h}(B_{|\phi^{\prime}(0)|r}(Z))},\quad\forall r>0.

Thus, (
3.12
) implies (
3.11
).
∎

We now prove an SLE version of the “monotone sets have zero mass” condition from Proposition
3.2
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Assume that we are in the setting of Theorem
1.13
. Almost surely, the following is true. Let
X\subset\mathbbm{C}
be a
\mu_{h}
-measurable set with the following property: for
\mu_{h}
-a.e. pair of points
z,w\in X
, the curves
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
hit
z
and
w
in the same order.
Then
\mu_{h}(X)=0
.

Proof.

For
z\in\mathbbm{C}
, let
\mathcal{Q}_{z}
be as in (
3.6
). By Lemma
3.3
, a.s.

\limsup_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{\mu_{h}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{Q}_{z}\cap B_{r}(z)}}\right)}{\mu_{h}(B_{r}(z))}>0,\quad\text{for $\mu_{h}$-a.e.\ $z\in\mathbbm{C}$}.

(3.13)

By the Lebesgue density theorem for general Radon measures on
\mathbbm{C}

[
Mat95
, Corollary 2.14]
, a.s. for every
\mu_{h}
-measurable set
X\subset\mathbbm{C}
,

\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{\mu_{h}(B_{r}(z)\cap X)}{\mu_{h}(B_{r}(z))}=1,\quad\text{for $\mu_{h}$-a.e.\ $z\in X$}.

(3.14)

If
z
and
w
are two points which are hit in the same order by
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
, and
z
and
w
are each hit at most once by each of
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
, then the definition (
3.6
) of
\mathcal{Q}_{z}
shows that
w\notin\mathcal{Q}_{z}
. From this observation and the fact that a.s.
\mu_{h}
-a.e.
z\in\mathbbm{C}
is hit at most once by each of
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
, we see that if
X
is a
\mu_{h}
-measurable set with the property in the proposition statement, then
\mu_{h}(X\cap\mathcal{Q}_{z})=0
for
\mu_{h}
-a.e.
z\in X
. By combining this with (
3.13
), we obtain that a.s., for every such set
X
,

\limsup_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{\mu_{h}(B_{r}(z)\cap X)}{\mu_{h}(B_{r}(z))}<1,\quad\text{for $\mu_{h}$-a.e.\ $z\in X$}.

(3.15)

By (
3.14
) and (
3.15
), we get that
\mu_{h}(X)=0
.
∎

Proposition 3.6
.

Assume that we are in the setting of Theorem
1.13
. Almost surely, for each monotone set
A\subset[0,1]^{2}
(Definition
3.1
), we have
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(A)=0
.

Proof.

Let
\psi:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
be a function as in the definition (
1.2
) of
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
, so that
\eta_{1}(t)=\eta_{2}(\psi(t))
for each

.
For a monotone set
A\subset[0,1]^{2}
, let

T_{A}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{t\in[0,1]:(t,\psi(t))\in A}}\right\}.

(3.16)

We claim that a.s. for every monotone set
A
, it holds that
\mu_{h}
-a.e. pair of points
z,w\in\eta_{1}(T_{A})
are hit in the same order by
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
.
Almost surely, the set of points in
\mathbbm{C}
which are hit more than once by either
\eta_{1}
or
\eta_{2}
has zero
\mu_{h}
-mass, so we can assume that each of

and

is hit exactly once by each of
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
.
Fix

and let

and
t_{w}\in T_{A}
be the times when
\eta_{1}
hits

and

.
By definition of

and

of a monotone set and since

.
Since

hits each of

and

and

are the unique times at which

and

and

and
\eta_{2}

By Proposition

and the preceding claim, it is a.s. the case that for every monotone set

is zero. Since

-mass, this implies that the one-dimensional Lebesgue measure of

) of

, this means that

.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.13
.

The statement for the longest increasing subsequence follows combining Propositions

and

. The statement for the longest decreasing subsequence can be obtained by the same argument. Alternatively, one can observe that if

and

.
∎

Remark 3.7
.

The proof of Theorem
1.13
did not use many properties which are specific to SLE and LQG.
Indeed, all we needed was some form of approximate scale invariance in a neighborhood of a point sampled from the LQG measure
\mu_{h}
(in our setting, this comes from the local absolute continuity statements in the proof of Lemma
3.3
) and the fact that for a point
Z
sampled from
\mu_{h}
, the set
\mathcal{Q}_{Z}
a.s. has non-empty interior.
Consequently, the arguments of this section could in principle be extended to prove analogs of Theorem
1.13
for other permutons constructed from pairs of random space-filling curves as in Section
1.2
.
However, we do not have in mind any important examples of such permutons where the curves are not SLE curves parametrized by LQG mass, so we do not pursue this point further here.

4
Dimension of the support is one

In this section we will prove Theorem
1.20
, which asserts that the dimension of the closed support
\operatorname{supp}{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
is one for
any
of the permutons defined in Section
1.2
. We recall from Theorem
1.20
that
\mathcal{T}=\{(t,s)\in[0,1]^{2}:\eta_{1}(t)=\eta_{2}(s)\}
.

Since
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
is a permuton, by definition it is immediate that the projection of
\operatorname{supp}{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
onto the first coordinate of
[0,1]^{2}
is all of
[0,1]
. Since the projection map is 1-Lipschitz, this implies that the Hausdorff dimension of
\operatorname{supp}{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
is at least one. From this and Lemma
2.9
, we get that a.s.

1\leq\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\operatorname{supp}{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\leq\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{T},

(4.1)

where
\dim_{\mathcal{H}}
denotes the Hausdorff dimension of a set.
It therefore suffices to show that a.s.
\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{T}\leq 1
.

4.1
Core argument

By the definition of
\mathcal{T}
, if
[a,b]\times[c,d]\subset[0,1]^{2}
is a rectangle, then
\mathcal{T}
intersects
[a,b]\times[c,d]
if and only if
\eta_{1}([a,b])\cap\eta_{2}([c,d])\not=\emptyset
. We therefore seek an upper bound for the probability that the images of two small time intervals under
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
intersect. The following lemma is the main input in the proof of Theorem
1.20
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Assume that we are in the setting of Theorem
1.20
.
Fix
\zeta\in(0,1)
and
\delta\in(0,1/2)
.
There exist
E_{\varepsilon}=E_{\varepsilon}(\zeta,\delta)\in\sigma(h,\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
for
\varepsilon>0
such that
\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\mathbbm{P}[E_{\varepsilon}]=1
and the following is true.
Let
S\in[\delta,1-\delta]
be sampled uniformly from Lebesgue measure on
[\delta,1-\delta]
, independently from everything else.
For each
t\in[\delta,1-\delta]
and each small enough
\varepsilon>0
(depending on
\delta
),

\mathbbm{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[E_{\varepsilon},\,\eta_{1}([t,t+\varepsilon])\cap\eta_{2}([S,S+\varepsilon])\not=\emptyset}}\right]\leq\varepsilon^{1-\zeta}.

(4.2)

When we apply Lemma
4.1
, we will take
\zeta
and
\delta
to be small. Note that the statement of the lemma would not be true if we took
S
to be a fixed deterministic time instead of a random time. The reason is that the coupling of
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
, viewed as curves modulo time parametrization, is arbitrary. For example, we allow
\eta_{1}=\eta_{2}
in which case the probability that
\eta_{1}([t,t+\varepsilon])\cap\eta_{2}([t,t+\varepsilon])\not=\emptyset
is 1.

The idea of the proof of Lemma
4.1
is as follows. Since
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
are parametrized by LQG mass, the set
\eta([t,t+\varepsilon])
has
\mu_{h}
-mass
\varepsilon
and the conditional law of
\eta_{2}(S)
given
(h,\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
is that of a sample from the restriction of
(1-2\delta)^{-1}\mu_{h}
to
\eta_{2}([\delta,1-\delta])
. Therefore, the probability that
\eta_{2}(S)\in\eta_{1}([t,t+\varepsilon])
is at most of order
\varepsilon
. To deduce an upper bound for the probability in (
4.2
), we will improve this estimate by showing that it is unlikely for
\eta_{2}(S)
to be “close” to
\eta_{1}([t,t+\varepsilon])
and that it is unlikely for
\eta_{2}([S,S+\varepsilon])
to be “large”.

It is convenient to measure “close” and “large” in terms of the
\gamma
-LQG metric
D_{h}
associated with
h
. This is because a space-filling SLE curve parametrized by LQG mass behaves nicely with respect to this metric (see Lemma
4.2
below). In what follows, we let
d_{\gamma}
be the Hausdorff dimension of
\mathbbm{C}
with respect to the metric
D_{h}
, which is a.s. equal to a finite deterministic constant
[
GP22
, Corollary 1.7]
.
We will need the following two lemmas.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\gamma\in(0,2)
and
\kappa>4
.
Let
(\mathbbm{C},h,\infty)
be a singly marked unit area
\gamma
-Liouville quantum sphere. Let
\eta:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbbm{C}
be a whole-plane space-filling SLE
κ
from
\infty
to
\infty
sampled independently from
h
, then parametrized by
\gamma
-LQG mass with respect to
h
.
Also fix
\zeta\in(0,1)
and
\delta\in(0,1/2)
. Almost surely, for each small enough
\varepsilon>0
(how small is random),

D_{h}(\eta(s),\eta(t))\leq\varepsilon^{1/d_{\gamma}-\zeta},\quad\forall s,t\in[\delta,1-\delta]\quad\text{such that}\quad|s-t|\leq\varepsilon.

(4.3)

For the next lemma, we introduce the notation

\mathcal{B}_{r}(z;D_{h}):=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\text{$D_{h}$-ball of radius $r$ centered at $z$}}}\right),\quad\forall z\in\mathbbm{C}\cup\{\infty\},\quad\forall r>0.

(4.4)

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
(\mathbbm{C},h,\infty)
be a singly marked unit area
\gamma
-Liouville quantum sphere. Also fix
\zeta\in(0,1)
. Almost surely, for each small enough
r>0
(how small is random),

r^{d_{\gamma}+\zeta}\leq\mu_{h}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{B}_{r}(z;D_{h})}}\right)\leq r^{d_{\gamma}-\zeta},\quad\forall z\in\mathbbm{C}\cup\{\infty\}.

(4.5)

Lemmas
4.2
and
4.3
are straightforward consequences of known estimates for the LQG metric
[
AFS20
,
GS24
]
, but some work is needed to transfer from the case of a whole-plane GFF (which is the case treated by the results we cite) to the case of a unit area quantum sphere. We postpone the proofs until after the proof of Theorem
1.20
.

Proof of Lemma
4.1
.

Fix
\widetilde{\zeta}\in(0,\zeta)
to be chosen later, in a deterministic manner depending only on
\gamma
and
\zeta
.
Let
E_{\varepsilon}=E_{\varepsilon}(\widetilde{\zeta},\delta)
be the event that the following is true:

(i)

For each
i\in\{1,2\}
, we have
D_{h}(\eta_{i}(s),\eta_{i}(t))\leq\varepsilon^{1/d_{\gamma}-\widetilde{\zeta}}
for each
s,t\in[\delta/2,1-\delta/2]
such that
|s-t|\leq\varepsilon
;

(ii)

\mu_{h}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{B}_{r}(z;D_{h})}}\right)\leq r^{d_{\gamma}-\widetilde{\zeta}}
for each
r\in(0,2\varepsilon^{1/d_{\gamma}-\widetilde{\zeta}}]
.

By Lemma
4.2
(applied once to each of
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
) and Lemma
4.3
, we have
\mathbbm{P}[E_{\varepsilon}]\rightarrow 1
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
.

We henceforth assume that
\varepsilon<\delta/2
. Let
t
and
S
be as in the lemma statement. If
E_{\varepsilon}
occurs, then by condition (
i
) in the definition of
E_{\varepsilon}
,

\eta_{1}([t,t+\varepsilon])\subset\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon^{1/d_{\gamma}-\widetilde{\zeta}}}(\eta_{1}(t);D_{h})\quad\text{and}\quad\eta_{2}([S,S+\varepsilon])\subset\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon^{1/d_{\gamma}-\widetilde{\zeta}}}(\eta_{2}(S);D_{h}).

(4.6)

By condition (
ii
) in the definition of
E_{\varepsilon}
, on this event the
\mu_{h}
-mass of the larger ball
\mathcal{B}_{2\varepsilon^{1/d_{\gamma}-\widetilde{\zeta}}}(\eta_{1}(t);D_{h})
is at most a
(\gamma,\widetilde{\zeta})
-dependent constant times
\varepsilon^{(d_{\gamma}-\widetilde{\zeta})(1/d_{\gamma}-\widetilde{\zeta})}
. Since
S
is sampled from Lebesgue measure on
[\delta,1-\delta]
, independently from everything else, and
\eta_{2}
is parametrized by
\mu_{h}
-mass, the conditional law of
\eta_{2}(S)
given
(h,\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
is given by the restriction of
(1-2\delta)^{-1}\mu_{h}
to
\eta_{2}([\delta,1-\delta])
. Therefore,

\mathbbm{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[E_{\varepsilon},\,\eta_{2}(S)\in\mathcal{B}_{2\varepsilon^{1/d_{\gamma}-\widetilde{\zeta}}}(\eta_{1}(t);D_{h})}}\right]\preceq\mathbbm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathbbm{1}_{E_{\varepsilon}}\mu_{h}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{B}_{2\varepsilon^{1/d_{\gamma}-\widetilde{\zeta}}}(\eta_{1}(t);D_{h})}}\right)}}\right]\preceq\varepsilon^{(d_{\gamma}-\widetilde{\zeta})(1/d_{\gamma}-\widetilde{\zeta})},

(4.7)

where
\preceq
denotes inequality up to a finite constant which depends only on
\gamma,\widetilde{\zeta},\delta
.

By (
4.6
) and the triangle inequality for
D_{h}
, if
E_{\varepsilon}
occurs and
\eta_{2}(S)\notin\mathcal{B}_{2\varepsilon^{1/d_{\gamma}-\widetilde{\zeta}}}(\eta_{1}(t);D_{h})
, then
\eta_{1}([t,t+\varepsilon])\cap\eta_{2}([S,S+\varepsilon])=\emptyset
. Therefore, (
4.7
) implies that

\mathbbm{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[E_{\varepsilon},\,\eta_{1}([t,t+\varepsilon])\cap\eta_{2}([S,S+\varepsilon])\not=\emptyset}}\right]\preceq\varepsilon^{(d_{\gamma}-\widetilde{\zeta})(1/d_{\gamma}-\widetilde{\zeta})}.

We now obtain (
4.2
) by choosing
\widetilde{\zeta}>0
to be small enough that
(d_{\gamma}-\widetilde{\zeta})(1/d_{\gamma}-\widetilde{\zeta})<1-\zeta
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.20
.

By (
4.1
), we only need to show that a.s.
\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{T}\leq 1
.
Fix
\zeta\in(0,1)
and
\delta\in(0,1/2)
. By the countable stability of Hausdorff dimension, it suffices to show that a.s.

\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{T}\cap[\delta,1-\delta]^{2}}}\right)\leq 1+\zeta.

(4.8)

To this end, let
E_{\varepsilon}
be the event of Lemma
4.1
with
\zeta/2
in place of
\zeta
, and recall that
\mathbbm{P}[E_{\varepsilon}]\rightarrow 1
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
. We will upper-bound the expected number of
\varepsilon\times\varepsilon
squares needed to cover the set in (
4.8
) on the event
E_{\varepsilon}
.

Since
\mathcal{T}=\{(t,s)\in[0,1]^{2}:\eta_{1}(t)=\eta_{2}(s)\}
, for
x,y\in[0,1-\varepsilon]^{2}
, the set
\mathcal{T}
intersects
[x,x+\varepsilon]\times[y,y+\varepsilon]
if and only if
\eta_{1}([x,x+\varepsilon])\cap\eta_{2}([y,y+\varepsilon])\not=\emptyset
. Therefore, the expected number of
\varepsilon\times\varepsilon
squares needed to cover
\mathcal{T}\cap[\delta,1-\delta]^{2}
, truncated on the event
E_{\varepsilon}
, is at most

\mathbbm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathbbm{1}_{E_{\varepsilon}}\sum_{x\in[\delta-\varepsilon,1-\delta]\cap\varepsilon\mathbbm{Z}}\sum_{y\in[\delta-\varepsilon,1-\delta]\cap\varepsilon\mathbbm{Z}}\mathbbm{1}_{\eta_{1}([x,x+\varepsilon])\cap\eta_{2}([y,y+\varepsilon])\not=\emptyset}}}\right].

(4.9)

For each
x,y\in[\delta-\varepsilon,1-\delta]\cap\varepsilon\mathbbm{Z}
, the square
[x,x+\varepsilon]\times[y,y+\varepsilon]
is contained in
[t,t+2\varepsilon]\times[s,s+2\varepsilon]
for each
t\in[x-\varepsilon,x]
and each
s\in[y-\varepsilon,y]
. Therefore, the quantity (
4.9
) is bounded above by

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{-2}\mathbbm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathbbm{1}_{E_{\varepsilon}}\int_{\delta-2\varepsilon}^{1-\delta}\int_{\delta-2\varepsilon}^{1-\delta}\mathbbm{1}_{\eta_{1}([t,t+2\varepsilon])\cap\eta_{2}([s,s+2\varepsilon])\not=\emptyset}\,ds\,dt}}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\varepsilon^{-2}\mathbbm{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[E_{\varepsilon},\,\eta_{1}([T,T+2\varepsilon])\cap\eta_{2}([S,S+2\varepsilon])\not=\emptyset}}\right],

(4.10)

where
S
and
T
are sampled uniformly and independently from
[\delta-2\varepsilon,1-\delta]
, independently from everything else.

By Lemma
4.1
(with
\zeta/2
in place of
\zeta
), for each small enough
\varepsilon>0
the right side of (
4.1
) is at most
\varepsilon^{-1-\zeta/2}
. Hence the expected number of
\varepsilon\times\varepsilon
squares needed to cover
\mathcal{T}\cap[\delta,1-\delta]^{2}
on the event
E_{\varepsilon}
is at most
\varepsilon^{-1-\zeta/2}
.
From this, Markov’s inequality, and the fact that
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbbm{P}[E_{\varepsilon}]=1
, we obtain that a.s. the lower Minkowski dimension of
\mathcal{T}\cap[\delta,1-\delta]^{2}
is at most
1+\zeta
. This implies (
4.8
). Note that our proof does not show that the Minkowski dimension of
\mathcal{T}
is at most 1 since Minkowski dimension is not countably stable.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

The dimension upper bound of Theorem
1.20
is not true in general for pairs of permutons constructed from space-filling curves. Indeed, for each
d\in(1,2)
, there exists a pair of space-filling curves
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}:[0,1]\to[0,1]^{2}
in the unit square, parametrized by Lebesgue measure, such that the following is true. If we let
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
be the permuton associated with
(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
as in (
1.2
), then the Hausdorff dimension of
\operatorname{supp}{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
is at least
d
.

To see this, let
N\geq M\geq 3
be odd integers. Via a straightforward iterative procedure, one can construct a space-filling curve
\eta_{1}:[0,1]\to[0,1]^{2}
such that for each integer
k\geq 1
and each
i\in\{1,\dots,(NM)^{k}\}
, the set
\eta_{1}([(i-1)(NM)^{-k},i(NM)^{-k}])
is an
N^{-k}\times M^{-k}
rectangle with corners in
N^{-k}\mathbbm{Z}\times M^{-k}\mathbbm{Z}
. Moreover,
\eta_{1}
hits Lebesgue-a.e. each point of
[0,1]^{2}
exactly once. One can similarly construct
\eta_{2}
so that it satisfies the same conditions but with
N
and
M
interchanged. Then for each

Definition 5.1

(Re-rooting a curve)

6
Physics background for the meander conjecture

In this section we review the developments in statistical physics that lead to Conjecture
1.4
, namely, the scaling limit of meanders is described by LQG with matter central charge
{\mathbf{c}_{\mathrm{M}}}=-4
, decorated by two independent SLE
8
curves. Section
6.1
gives a quick overview of the ideas that lead to Conjecture
1.4
, then the consecutive sections review in more detail the reasoning. At the end of the section (Section
6.6
), we will also give a heuristic justification of Conjecture
1.9
using Conjecture
1.4
.

6.1
Meanders, Hamiltonian cycles, and spanning trees

The scaling limits of a large number of 2D statistical physics models are expected to be described by conformal field theories (CFTs); see
[
ISZ98
]
for a review. A key characteristic of a CFT is its central charge
{\mathbf{c}}\in\mathbbm{R}
, which encodes how its partition function responds to deformations of the background metric.
Although the full field-theoretic description of the scaling limit has only been achieved for a restricted class of models such as the critical Ising model
[
BPZ84
]
, for a wide range of models, the central charge can be derived from the asymptotic behavior of the partition function of the model on periodic lattices.
For example, by Kirchhoff’s matrix tree theorem, the scaling limit of the uniform spanning tree on a large class of 2D lattices should be a CFT with central charge
{\mathbf{c}}=-2
.

The Peano curve of a spanning tree on a 2D lattice is equivalent to the Hamiltonian cycle on a related directed lattice
[
Kas63
]
, which means that the uniform directed Hamiltonian cycle on a large class of directed lattices should also have central charge
{\mathbf{c}}=-2
. However, for the uniform undirected Hamiltonian cycle on the honeycomb lattice, the central charge turns out to be
{\mathbf{c}}=-1
instead of
{\mathbf{c}}=-2

[
BN94
,
BSY94
]
.
The honeycomb lattice is an example of a cubic planar map whose dual graph is a triangulation.
As explained in
[
GKN99
]
, the central charge discrepancy between directed and undirected Hamiltonian cycles is due to a special property of the honeycomb lattice, that is, its dual graph is an Eulerian triangulation (i.e. each vertex has an even degree). Since this property does not hold for a typical cubic planar map (whose dual is a triangulation), the uniform random triangulation decorated by a Hamiltonian cycle on its dual should be in the same universality class as a uniform spanning tree decorated map. The scaling limit of this latter decorated map is expected to be
\gamma
-LQG with matter central charge
{\mathbf{c}_{\mathrm{M}}}=-2
(equivalently,
\gamma\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eqn:lqg-ccM}}}{{=}}\sqrt{2}
; recall the discussion in Remark
2.6
), decorated by an SLE
8
curve; see, e.g.,
[
She16b
, Appendix A]
.

A meander can be viewed as a pair of Hamiltonian cycles on a planar map whose dual is an Eulerian quadrangulation, with the additional constraint that at each vertex the two cycles cross each other. Following the line of reasoning as in
[
GKN99
]
with a crucial new insight, Di Francesco, Golinelli, and Guitter
[
DFGG00
]

argued that the uniformly sampled meander should be in the same universality class as random planar maps decorated by a
pair
of independent spanning trees. Since the central charge is believed to be additive for independent models, such planar maps are expected to converge to LQG with matter central charge
{\mathbf{c}_{\mathrm{M}}}=(-2)+(-2)=-4
(equivalently,
\gamma\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eqn:lqg-ccM}}}{{=}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{3}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(17-\sqrt{145}}}\right)}
), decorated by a pair of independent SLE
8
curves; as stated in Conjecture
1.4
.

In the rest of this section, we review in more detail the reasoning of
[
DFGG00
]
. The key is a subtle relation between the fully packed and the dense O
(n)
loop model on different lattices. See Figure
11
for a summary of the models discussed in the following sections.

6.2
Positive temperature O(

) loop model and its scaling limit

For a fixed

(6.1)

For

{\mathbf{c}}_{\textrm{dense}}(n)=1-6\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{\kappa}}-\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}}{2}}}\right)^{2},\quad\text{where $\kappa\in[4,8)$ solves $n=2\cos\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{4}{\kappa}}}\right)\pi}}\right)$}.

(6.2)

6.3
Fully packed O(
n
) loop model and its scaling limit

As
T\to 0
, the O(
n
) loop model is supported on configurations that maximize
\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{L})
. This is the so-called
fully packed
O(
n
) loop model. In contrast to the dilute and dense phases, the scaling limit of the fully packed O(
n
) loop model highly depends on the underlying lattice.
In particular, on the honeycomb lattice, Blote and Nienhuis
[
BN94
]
argued that on the one hand, the scaling limit of the loops in the fully packed phase is in the same universality class as those in the dense phase, i.e., it should be described by the same CLE. On the other hand,
the CFT descibing the scaling limit has central charge

{\mathbf{c}}_{\textrm{fp}}(n)={\mathbf{c}}_{\textrm{dense}}(n)+1,

(6.3)

where
{\mathbf{c}}_{\textrm{dense}}
is as in (
6.2
).

The physics argument for (
6.3
) is the following. On the honeycomb lattice, a loop configuration
\mathcal{L}
maximizes
\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{L})
when it covers all vertices. Therefore, the fully packed O
(1)
loop model is the uniform measure on such configurations.
By
[
BN94
]
, a fully packed configuration
\mathcal{L}
on the honeycomb lattice can be mapped to an integer-valued function
h(\mathcal{L})
on the dual triangular lattice, which we call the height function.
Under this mapping, the fully packed O
(1)
loop model on the honeycomb lattice is equivalent to a solid-on-solid (SOS) model which produces the same height function. (Explaining the height function construction would be too much of a digression; see
[
GKN99
, Figure 1]
and
[
NHB84
, Figure 2-4]
for demonstrations.)
On the other hand, thanks to a connection to the zero-temperature triangular anti-ferromagnetic Ising model, it was already known from
[
BH82
,
BN93
]
that the SOS model has central charge
{\mathbf{c}}=1
, and the height function
h(\mathcal{L})
is believed to converge to the Gaussian free field. This means that
{\mathbf{c}}_{\textrm{fp}}(n=1)=1
. Since
{\mathbf{c}}_{\textrm{dense}}(n=1)=0
by (
6.2
), we get (
6.3
) when
n=1
.
For
n\neq 1
, although

is determined by
\mathcal{L}
, it is conjectured in
[

]
that
\mathcal{L}
and

are asymptotically decoupled and their joint scaling limit is equivalent to that of the the superposition of the dense O
(n)
loop model and the
{\mathbf{c}}=1
SOS model.
Since the central charge is additive for independent models, (
6.3
) holds for all
n\in[0,2]
. This relation is supported by some numerical evidence in
[
BN94
]
. It was put on a solid ground at the physics level of rigor via the exact Bethe ansatz techniques in
[
BSY94
]

For
n=0
, the fully packed loop model on the honeycomb lattice is supported on configurations where a single loop visits each vertex, namely, it produces a uniformly sampled Hamiltonian cycle on the honeycomb lattice. According to (

{\mathbf{c}}_{\textrm{fp}}(0)=\lim_{n\to 0}{\mathbf{c}}_{\textrm{dense}}(n)+1=-1

8
curve (which is the limit of CLE

It was observed by Guitter, Kristjansen, and Nielsen

We recall for later convenience the following equivalent characterizations of a Eulerian triangulation: (1) a planar map such that all its faces are triangles and the degree of each vertex is even; (2) a planar map such that all its faces are triangles, colored in black and white, so that no two adjacent faces have the same color; (3) a planar map such that its dual map is a planar bicubic map, i.e. all its vertices have degree three and these vertices are colored in black and white so that any two adjacent vertices have different colors.

Introducing some specific weights for the oriented configurations
(\vec{\mathcal{L}_{1}},\vec{\mathcal{L}_{2}})
, Jacobsen and Kondev also determined a general expression for the central charge of the scaling limit of this new weighted oriented model (see
[
JK98
, Eq. (6.4)]
). Using a simple relation (given in
[
JK98
, Eq. (4.1)]
) between the weights of the oriented configurations
(\vec{\mathcal{L}_{1}},\vec{\mathcal{L}_{2}})
and the weights
(n,m)
of the fully packed O
(n\times m)
loop model, i.e. of the unoriented configurations
(\mathcal{L}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{2})
, they finally conjectured that the central charge of the scaling limit of the fully packed O
(n\times m)
loop model on
\mathbbm{Z}^{2}
is given by:

{\mathbf{c}}_{\textrm{dfp}}(n,m)={\mathbf{c}}_{\textrm{dense}}(n)+{\mathbf{c}}_{\textrm{dense}}(m)+1.

(6.4)

They also conjectured that
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
asymptotically decouple, i.e., their scaling limits are independent.
The relation in (
6.4
) has also been justified at a physics level of rigor via the exact Bethe ansatz techniques
[
DCN04
,
JZJ04
]
. For
n=m=0
, the condition
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{L}_{1})=\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{L}_{2})=1
means that we have two Hamiltonian cycles on the square lattice whose edge sets are disjoint.
According to (
6.4
), the central charge of the limiting CFT is
{\mathbf{c}}_{\textrm{dfp}}(0,0)=2\,{\mathbf{c}}_{\textrm{dense}}(0)+1=-3
. Moreover, the two Hamiltonian cycles should converge to two independent SLE
8
curves (following the same reasoning in the discussion after (
6.2
)).

Recall from Section
1.3
that a meander can be viewed as a triplet
(M,P^{1},P^{2})
, where
P^{1},P^{2}
are two Hamiltonian cycles with disjoint edge sets on a 4-regular planar map
M
, i.e. a planar map with all vertices of degree four. Moreover, at each vertex the two cycles
P^{1}
and
P^{2}
cross each other. This implies that the edges on the boundary of each face of the dual map of
M
alternate between dual edges which cross edges of
P^{1}
and dual edges which cross edges of
P^{2}
. Hence, each face of
M
has even degree, and so the dual of
M
is an Eulerian quadrangulation.

As explained in
[
DFGG00
, Section 3]
, the height function mapping of Jacobsen and Kondev described in the previous section makes sense on a general planar map
G
(instead of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
) if and only if the dual map of
G
is an Eulerian quadrangulation. Since the dual of
M
is an Eulerian quadrangulation, one can associate to
(M,P^{1},P^{2})
a height function
(h_{1},h_{2},h_{3})
as above. However, the additional constraint that the two cycles must cross at each vertex yields that
(h_{1},h_{2},h_{3})
must lie on a 2D subspace. This effect exactly cancels the
+1
in (
6.4
). (See
[
DFGG00
, Figure 2]
and the discussion below that figure.)
Therefore, the scaling limit should be
\gamma
-LQG with
{\mathbf{c}_{\mathrm{M}}}=2\,{\mathbf{c}}_{\operatorname{dense}}(0)=-4
(equivalently,
\gamma\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eqn:lqg-ccM}}}{{=}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{3}(17-\sqrt{145})}
), decorated by two independent SLE
8
curves as in Conjecture
1.4
. This leads to several enumeration conjectures in
[
DFGG00
]
on meanders which are supported by numerical evidence in the follow-up works
[
DFGJ00
,
JG00
]
. In particular, they conjectured that the number of meanders grows as
A^{n}n^{-\alpha}
, where
A\approx 12.26
and
\alpha\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:exp}}}{{=}}1+4/\gamma^{2}=(29+\sqrt{145})/12

Although not mentioned explicitly in
[
DFGG00
]
, their argument naturally leads to the following scaling limit conjectures in addition to Conjecture
1.4
.
Consider a uniform 4-regular planar map decorated by a pair of edge-disjoint Hamiltonian cycles, without the constraint that the cycles cross at each vertex. Such configurations are called
tangent meanders
in
[
DFGJ00
]

\gamma

{\mathbf{c}_{\mathrm{M}}}=-4
(equivalently,
\gamma\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eqn:lqg-ccM}}}{{=}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{3}(17-\sqrt{145})}

\gamma
-LQG with
{\mathbf{c}_{\mathrm{M}}}=-3
(equivalently,
\gamma\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eqn:lqg-ccM}}}{{=}}\sqrt{\frac{4}{3}(4-\sqrt{7})}

Finally, consider a uniformly sampled pair of Hamiltonian cycles on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
with the condition that they form a meander. Then the central charge for the corresponding CFT should be
{\mathbf{c}}=-4
. In all cases, the two Hamiltonian cycles
should converge to two independent SLE
8
curves.

As in Remark

6.6
Convergence of the cyclic meandric permutation to the identity

Recall Conjecture
1.9
, which asserts that the cyclic meandric permutation
\tau_{\ell_{n}}
associated with a uniform meander
\ell_{n}
of size
n
converges to the identity permuton.
In this subsection we will give a heuristic justification of Conjecture
1.9
using Conjecture
1.4
. To prove Conjecture
1.9
, one would need to show that with high probability when
n
is large,
|\tau_{\ell_{n}}(j)-j|
is of order
o(n)
for all but all but a negligible fraction of the values of
j\in[1,2n]\cap\mathbbm{Z}
.

To see why this should be true, consider the setting of Section
1.2
with
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}
independent SLE
8
curves and
\gamma=\sqrt{\frac{1}{3}(17-\sqrt{145})}
, so that
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
is the meandric permuton. Let
\psi,\phi:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
be two Lebesgue measurable function chosen so that
\eta_{2}(t)=\eta_{1}(\psi(t))
and
\eta_{2}(\phi(t))=\eta_{1}(t)
. We claim that a.s. for Lebesgue-a.e.
t\in[0,1]
,

\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\sup_{s\in[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]}|\psi(\phi(t)+s)-t|=0.

(6.5)

If we think of
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
as the continuum limits of the loop and the line, respectively, for the meander
\ell_{n}
, then we can think of
\psi
and
\phi
as the continuum analogs of
\sigma_{\ell_{n}}^{-1}
and
\sigma_{\ell_{n}}
, respectively. Hence, if we assume Conjecture
1.4
, then (
6.5
) should imply that for a typical
t\in[0,1]
,

\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{k\in[-2\varepsilon n,2\varepsilon n]\cap\mathbbm{Z}}\frac{1}{2n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\sigma_{\ell_{n}}^{-1}(\sigma_{\ell_{n}}(\lfloor 2tn\rfloor)+k)-2tn}}\right|=0.

(6.6)

By the formula
\tau_{\ell_{n}}(j)=\sigma_{\ell_{n}}^{-1}(\sigma_{\ell_{n}}(j)+1)
from (
1.3
), the relation (
6.6
) for Lebesgue-a.e.
t\in[0,1]
implies the desired convergence of
\tau_{\ell_{n}}
to the identity permuton.

We now explain why (
6.5
) is true. For
\mu_{h}
-a.e.
z\in\mathbbm{C}
, each of
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
hits the point
z
exactly once (c.f. the proof of Lemma
2.8
). Equivalently, for Lebesgue-a.e.
t\in[0,1]
, then each of
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
hits
\eta_{1}(t)
exactly once. For such a time
t
, the point
\eta_{1}(t)
is not on the boundary of
\eta_{1}([t-\delta,t+\delta])
for any
\delta>0
(otherwise,
\eta_{1}
would have to hit
\eta_{1}(t)
at a time not in
[t-\delta,t+\delta]
). Hence,
\eta_{1}([t-\delta,t+\delta])
contains a neighborhood of
\eta_{1}(t)
. Since
\eta_{2}
is a continuous curve and
\eta_{2}
hits
\eta_{1}(t)
exactly once (namely, at the time
\phi(t)
), there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

\eta_{2}([\phi(t)-\varepsilon,\phi(t)+\varepsilon])\subset\eta_{1}([t-\delta,t+\delta])\quad\text{which implies}\quad\sup_{s\in[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]}|\psi(\phi(t)+s)-t|\leq\delta.

Since
\delta>0
is arbitrary, this gives (
6.5
).

7
Open problems

We state several additional open problems related to this work, besides proving the conjectures stated in Section
1
. At the end of the section, we comment briefly on how hard we expect the problems to be.

It is difficult to imagine how one would make a direct connection between a subsequential limit of meandric permutons and SLE-decorated LQG. Consequently, in order to prove Conjecture
1.8
one would likely first need to solve the following problem.

Problem 7.1
.

Is there a description of the meandric permuton which does not use SLE or LQG?

Possible solutions to Problem
7.1
might include an axiomatic characterization of the meandric permuton or a direct construction of this permuton in terms of Brownian motion (similar to the construction of the skew Brownian permutons in
[
Bor23
]
). We emphasize that Conjecture
1.8
is likely to be difficult to prove even if one finds a nice solution to Problem
7.1
, since meanders are notoriously difficult to analyze.

It seems quite difficult to get a lower bound for the length of the longest increasing subsequence for classes of random permutations which are known or expected to converge to the random permutons considered in this paper. Currently, the only available lower bounds are the essentially trivial ones, namely
\operatorname{LIS}(\sigma_{n})\geq\operatorname{const}\times|\sigma_{n}|^{1/2}
. We recall that the Erdös-Szekeres theorem
[
ES35
]
gives
\operatorname{LIS}(\sigma)\times\operatorname{LDS}(\sigma)\geq n
for every
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
.

Problem 7.2
.

For
n\in\mathbbm{N}
, let
\sigma_{n}
be a uniform Baxter, semi-Baxter, strong-Baxter, or meandric permutation of size
n
.
Show that there exists
\alpha>1/2
(depending only on the type of permutation, not on
n
) such that
\operatorname{LIS}(\sigma_{n})\geq n^{\alpha}
with high probability when
n
is large.

We note that the analog of Problem
7.2
in the case of permutations sampled from the Brownian separable permuton (the
\rho\to 1
limit of skew Brownian permutons) is solved in
[
BDSG24
]
.
Problem
7.2
is closely related to the following SLE/LQG problem.

Problem 7.3
.

Assume that we are in the setting of Section
1.2
. What is the maximal Hausdorff dimension (with respect to the LQG metric
D_{h}
) of a set
X\subset\mathbbm{C}
which is hit in the same order by
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
?

If
\Delta
is the maximal Hausdorff dimension in Problem
7.2
and
d_{\gamma}
is the Hausdorff dimension of
\mathbbm{C}
w.r.t.
D_{h}
, then it is natural to conjecture that
\operatorname{LIS}(\sigma_{n})\approx|\sigma_{n}|^{\Delta/d_{\gamma}}
for sufficiently “nice” sequences of permutations
\{\sigma_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbbm{N}}
which converge in law to
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
.

The following question is motivated by the formula for pattern densities in Theorem
1.23
.
We are especially interested in the case when
\kappa=8
, since this case corresponds to the meandric permuton.

Problem 7.4
.

Let
\kappa>4
and let
\eta
be a whole-plane space-filling SLE
κ
from
\infty
to
\infty
. Also let
z_{1},\dots,z_{n}\in\mathbbm{C}
be distinct points. Is there an exact formula for the function

P_{\kappa}(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})=\mathbbm{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\text{$\eta$ hits $z_{j}$ before $z_{j+1}$, $\forall j=1,\dots,n-1$}}}\right]?

(7.1)

Recall Conjecture
1.9
, which asserts that a uniform
cyclic
meandric permutation converges to the identity permuton. We explained in Section
6.6
why this conjecture should follow from Conjecture
1.4
.

Problem 7.5
.

Is there an unconditional proof of Conjecture
1.9
which does not use Conjecture
1.4
?

The techniques and many of the results of this paper apply to a wide range of different random permutons which can be described in terms of SLE and LQG. It is therefore natural to ask the following.

Problem 7.6
.

Are there any other interesting random permutons, besides the meandric permuton and the skew Brownian permutons, which can be described by SLE-decorated LQG in the manner of Section
1.2
?

The physics background provided in Section
6
motivates several conjectures about scaling limits. We highlight the following problem, which is motivated by the arguments of Sections
6.4
and
6.5
.

Problem 7.7
.

Prove that the scaling limit of a uniform Eulerian triangulation decorated by a Hamiltonian cycle on its dual is described by LQG with matter central charge
{\mathbf{c}_{\mathrm{M}}}=-1
(i.e.,
\gamma=\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}(7-\sqrt{13})}
) decorated by an SLE
8
curve. Moreover, prove the other scaling limit conjectures from Section
6.5
(see also Conjecture
6.2
and the two bottom boxes in the table in Figure
11
).

All of the conjectures and open problems stated in this paper seem to be quite difficult: we have spent some time on each of them and were unable to solve them. However, we expect that some are likely to be more accessible than others.

Probably the easiest of the open problems in this paper is Problem
7.2
, but it still seems to require some non-trivial new ideas. Of course, computing the exact exponent for the LIS (or the exact optimal dimension in Problem
7.3
) is likely to be much harder than just proving a non-trivial lower bound. Problem
7.4
seems like it could also conceivably be accessible with current techniques.

Typically, proving that a discrete model converges to SLE-decorated LQG requires some sort of combinatorial miracle to get a handle on the discrete model, plus a substantial amount of additional work to deduce convergence from such a combinatorial miracle. Consequently, we expect that Conjectures
1.4
,
1.8
, and
6.2
and Problem
7.7
are the most difficult of the conjectures and open problems stated in this paper. Proving convergence of meandric permutations (Conjecture
1.8
) might be easier than proving convergence of meanders to SLE-decorated LQG in, e.g., the Gromov-Hausdorff sense since one gets tightness for free, so one just has to identify the scaling limit. This would likely require solving Problem
7.1
, which seems hard, but which is a purely continuum problem so could conceivably be done without finding any combinatorial miracle for meanders. One would also need some new tools for analyzing uniform meanders in order to check the hypotheses of any hypothetical characterization theorem for the subsequential limits.

Conjecture
1.9
and Problem
7.5
appear to require some new insights about meanders, but could likely be done with much less “integrability” than what would be needed to get convergence to SLE and LQG.
