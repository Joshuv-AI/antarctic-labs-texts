Eigenstructure perturbations for a class of Hamiltonian matrices and solutions of related Riccati inequalities
Volker Mehrmann, Hongguo Xu

Abstract
The characterization of the solution set for a class of algebraic Riccati inequalities is studied. This class arises in the passivity analysis of linear time invariant control systems. Eigenvalue perturbation theory for the Hamiltonian matrix associated with the Riccati inequality is used to analyze the extremal points of the solution set.



Keywords
Kalman-Yacubovich-Popov-inequality, Riccati equation, Riccati inequality, Hamiltonian matrix, Lagrangian invariant subspace, eigenvalue perturbations.

AMS subject classification.
65F15, 65H10, 93D15

Introduction

We study the set of Hermitian solutions of a class of algebraic Riccati inequalities (ARI)

F^{H}X+XF+XGX+K\leq 0,

(1)

as well as the related
algebraic Riccati equations (ARE) of the form

F^{H}X+XF+XGX+K=0.

(2)

We consider the special class of problems with
0\leq G=G^{H}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
,
0\leq K=K^{H}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, and
F\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, where
\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}
denotes the set of complex
n\times m
matrices and
G>0
(
G\geq 0
) denotes that
G
is positive definite (semidefinite). The problem is studied in the setting of complex coefficients, but all our results also hold similarly in the real case replacing, if necessary, transformations concerning spectral properties by their real versions, like e.g. the Schur (or Jordan) form by the real Schur form (or real Jordan form).

The main goal of this paper is to characterize the set of positive
definite solutions of (
1
) and (
2
) and we do this by studying the perturbation theory for the eigenstructure of the associated
Hamiltonian matrix

H=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}F&G\\
-K&-F^{H}\end{array}\right],

(3)

formed from the coefficients, which satisfies
(JH)^{H}=JH
for

J=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&I\\
-I&0\end{array}\right].

Our study of the solvability of (
1
) and (
2
)
is motivated by the passivity analysis of linear time-invariant systems

\begin{array}[]{c}\dot{x}\ =\ A\,x+B\,u,\\[2.84526pt]
y\ =\ C\,x+D\,u,\end{array}

(4)

with
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
,
B\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}
,
C\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
, and
D\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
, as well as complex functions state
x
, input
u
, and output
y
.

A system of the form (
4
) is called
(impedance)
passive
, see e.g.
[
23
]
, if there exists a continuously differentiable
storage function

{\mathcal{H}}:\mathbb{C}^{n}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
such that the
dissipation inequality

{\mathcal{H}}(x(t_{1}))-{\mathcal{H}}(x(t_{0}))\leq\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}{\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits({y(t)^{H}}u(t))}\ dt,

(5)

holds for all admissible inputs
u
.

For the special case of systems of the form (
4
) that are
minimal
, i.e. both
controllable
and
observable
,
the classical theorem of
[
23
]
characterizes passivity.

Theorem 1
.

Assume that the system (
4
) is minimal.
The matrix inequality

\left[\begin{array}[]{cr}{A^{H}}X+XA&XB-C^{H}\\[5.69054pt]
B^{H}X-C&-(D+D^{H})\end{array}\right]\leq 0

(6)

has a solution
X=X^{H}>0
if and only if (
4
) is a
passive
system. In this case
{\mathcal{H}}(x)=\frac{1}{2}{x^{H}}Xx
defines a storage function for (
4
) associated
with the supply rate
{\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits({y^{H}}u)}
, satisfying (
5
).

Furthermore, for passive systems there exist maximum and minimum Hermitian solutions to (
6
) satisfying
0<X_{-}\leq X_{+}
(in the Loewner half-order of Hermitian matrices) such that
for all Hermitian solutions
X
to (
6
),
X_{-}\leq X\leq X_{+}
.

When the system is generated from purely data based interpolation approaches or from model reduction methods, then it typically cannot be guaranteed that the resulting system is minimal or it may happen that the system is close to an unobservable or uncontrollable system, see
[
3
,
7
]
. Also the minimality is only a sufficient condition for the existence of a positive definite solution, see
[
6
]
for a detailed characterization. We therefore do not assume minimality and study the existence and computation of positive definite solutions under weaker assumptions.

If the matrix
D+D^{H}
in (
6
) is positive definite, then (using Schur complements)
X
is a positive definite solution of (
6
) if and only if it is a positive definite solution of (
1
) with

F:=A-B(D+D^{H})^{-1}C^{H},\ G:=B(D+D^{H})^{-1}B^{H},\ K:=C^{H}(D+D^{H})^{-1}C.

(7)

The same argument applies to the equality case (
2
).

The solvability of (
1
) is well understood, see e.g.
[
5
]
,
and the same holds for
(
2
), see e.g.
[
12
]
. However, in most cases the study is motivated from linear quadratic optimal control theory
[
15
]
, where the quadratic term has a negative sign, while here we study the case where the sign is positive.

A second motivation for our work arises from energy based modeling of passive systems via
port-Hamiltonian (pH)
systems. See
[
17
]
for a detailed survey on pH systems covering also the case of descriptor systems.

A linear time invariant
port-Hamiltonian (pH) system
has the form

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\dot{x}&=&(J-R)Qx+(\hat{B}-\hat{P})u,\\
y&=&(\hat{B}+\hat{P})^{H}Qx+Du,\end{array}

(8)

with
Q=Q^{H}>0
,
J=-J^{H}
,

0\leq W^{H}=W:=\left[\begin{array}[]{lc}R&\hat{P}\\
\hat{P}^{H}&S\end{array}\right]
, with
S=\frac{1}{2}(D+D^{H})
.

It is well-known, see e.g.
[
2
,
6
]
, that if
X=X^{H}>0
is a solution of (
2
) then multiplication of (
4
) from the left by
X^{\frac{1}{2}}
(the principal square root of
X
)
and a change of variable
\xi=X^{\frac{1}{2}}x
leads to the pH formulation

\displaystyle\dot{\xi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X^{\frac{1}{2}}AX^{-\frac{1}{2}}\xi+X^{\frac{1}{2}}Bu=(J-R)\xi+(\hat{B}-\hat{P})u,

(9)

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle CX^{-\frac{1}{2}}\xi+Du=(\hat{B}+\hat{P})^{H}\xi+(S+N)u,

with
J=\frac{1}{2}(X^{\frac{1}{2}}AX^{-\frac{1}{2}}-X^{-\frac{1}{2}}A^{H}X^{\frac{1}{2}})
,

R=\frac{1}{2}(X^{\frac{1}{2}}AX^{-\frac{1}{2}}+X^{-\frac{1}{2}}A^{H}X^{\frac{1}{2}})
,

\hat{B}=\frac{1}{2}({X^{\frac{1}{2}}}B+X^{-\frac{1}{2}}C^{H})
,

\hat{P}=\frac{1}{2}(-{X^{\frac{1}{2}}}B+X^{-\frac{1}{2}}C^{H})
,

S=\frac{1}{2}(D+D^{H})
,
N=\frac{1}{2}(D-D^{H})
, and
Q=I
.

In this way any positive definite solution
X
of (
2
) leads to a pH representation of the system. This immediately raises the question which solution of (
2
) leads to a robust or even optimally robust representation (
9
). This has been the topic of several recent papers, where partial solutions are presented,
see
[
1
,
10
,
16
]
. To fully answer this question, it is essential to characterize the set of positive definite solutions of (
6
) and, in particular, the extremal solutions in this set, which is one of the goals of this paper.

A classical approach to solve (
2
) is to compute
n
-dimensional
Lagrangian invariant subspaces
of the Hamiltonian matrix (
3
), i.e.
subspaces satisfying

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}F&G\\
-K&-F^{H}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}W_{1}\\
W_{2}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}W_{1}\\
W_{2}\end{array}\right]{T_{11}},

(10)

where
{T_{11}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
and
W_{1}^{H}W_{2}=W_{2}^{H}W_{1}
.

If
W_{1}
is invertible then
X=W_{2}W_{1}^{-1}
is a Hermitian solution of (
2
) and the different solutions can be characterized by the choice of the Lagrangian invariant subspace which depends on the selection of the spectrum of
T_{11}
, see
[
9
]
for a complete characterization of the set of Lagrangian subspaces of
H
. This solution set is particularly complicated if the matrix
H
has purely imaginary eigenvalues, because in this case the approach to solve (
2
) via the numerical calculation of the Lagrangian invariant subspaces as in (
10
) may not be feasible. It may happen that either Lagrangian invariant subspaces do not exist or the matrix
W_{1}
is not invertible, see

[
9
,
12
,
15
]
for a detailed analysis under controllability, observability, stabilizability, and detectability conditions. An approach that can deal with possible limiting situations where eigenvalues of (
3
) are on the imaginary is the use of Newton’s method, see
[
4
,
11
]
.

In this paper we study the Riccati equations (
2
) and inequalities (
1
) arising in the passivity analysis, where both
G
and
K
are positive semidefinite. We derive the perturbation theory for the invariant subspaces in (
10
), and extend in particular the perturbation theory of
[
18
]
.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
we recall and extend some known results on the positive definiteness of solutions of the ARE (
2
) and the notation.

Section
3
then studies the behavior of the eigenvalues of the Hamiltonian matrix that is obtained when the ARE is perturbed by a positive semidefinite constant term.

2
Classical solution theory

In this section we present the notation and recall some well-known results on the solvability of (
1
) and (
2
).

A pair of matrices
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
,
B\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}

is called
controllable
if
\mathop{\operator@font rank}\nolimits\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}sI-A&B\end{array}\right]=n
for all
s\in\mathbb{C}
, and a pair of matrices
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
,
C\in\mathbb{C}^{p\times n}

is called
observable
if
\mathop{\operator@font rank}\nolimits\left[\begin{array}[]{c}sI-A\\
C\end{array}\right]=n
for all
s\in\mathbb{C}
.
For our analysis it will be very important to identify controllable as well observable subspaces, because the solution of (
1
) and (
2
) will turn out only to be nonsingular and therefore positive definite in these subspaces.

These subspaces can be obtained via a condensed form under unitary transformations due to
[
20
]
.
We present the result and a proof in our notation because it will be useful for our analysis.

Proposition 2
.

For matrices
F,G,K
as in (
7
) there exists a unitary matrix
U
such that

\displaystyle U^{H}FU

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc|c}\widetilde{F}_{11}&0&\widetilde{F}_{13}\\
\widetilde{F}_{21}&\widetilde{F}_{22}&\widetilde{F}_{23}\\
\hline\cr 0&0&\widetilde{F}_{33}\end{array}\right]=:\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}F_{11}&F_{12}\\
0&F_{22}\end{array}\right]

\displaystyle U^{H}GU

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc|c}\widetilde{G}_{11}&\widetilde{G}_{21}^{H}&0\\
\widetilde{G}_{21}&\widetilde{G}_{22}&0\\
\hline\cr 0&0&0\end{array}\right]=:\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}G_{11}&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right]

\displaystyle U^{H}KU

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc|c}\widetilde{K}_{11}&0&\widetilde{K}_{31}^{H}\\
0&0&0\\
\hline\cr\widetilde{K}_{31}&0&\widetilde{K}_{33}\end{array}\right]=:\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}K_{11}&K_{21}^{H}\\
K_{21}&K_{22}\end{array}\right],

where
(F_{11},G_{11})
and
(\widetilde{F}_{11},\widetilde{G}_{11})
are controllable,
and
(\widetilde{F}_{11},\widetilde{K}_{11})
is observable.

Proof.

The proof that
(F_{11},G_{11})
is controllable and
(\widetilde{F}_{11},\widetilde{K}_{11})
is observable follows from the computation of the staircase form of
\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}F&G\end{array}\right]
and
\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}F_{11}^{H}&K_{11}\end{array}\right]
, see
[
20
]
.

Since
G\geq 0
, so is
G_{11}
, which implies that
(\widetilde{F}_{11},\widetilde{G}_{11})
is controllable. To see this, suppose that
(\widetilde{F}_{11},\widetilde{G}_{11})
is not controllable. Then there exists a nonzero vector
x
and a scalar
\lambda
such that
x^{H}(\lambda I-\widetilde{F}_{11})=0
and

x^{H}\widetilde{G}_{11}=0
. Then
x^{H}{\widetilde{G}_{21}^{H}}=0
as well. If this was not the case, then for
y={\widetilde{G}_{21}}x
, we have

{\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}x&0\\
0&y\end{array}\right]^{H}}G_{11}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}x&0\\
0&y\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&|\!|y|\!|_{2}^{2}\\
|\!|y|\!|_{2}^{2}&y^{H}\widetilde{G}_{22}y\end{array}\right],

which is indefinite, contradicting the positive semi-definiteness of
G_{11}
. Thus

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}x\\
0\end{array}\right]^{H}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda I-F_{11}&G_{11}\end{array}\right]=0,

which contradicts the controllability of
(F_{11},G_{11})
.
∎

2.1
Solvability of algebraic Riccati equations

In order to characterize the existence of positive definite (positive semidefinite) solutions of (
1
) and (
2
), we have to identify what happens in the uncontrollable and unobservable subspaces.

To study this, let
U
be the unitary matrix in Proposition
2
and let
U^{H}XU=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}X_{11}&X_{21}^{H}\\
X_{21}&X_{22}\end{array}\right]
. Multiplying both (
1
) and (
2
) from the left by
U^{H}
and from the right by
U
, the Riccati operator on the left sides takes the form

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\Phi_{11}&\Phi_{21}^{H}\\
\Phi_{21}&\Phi_{22}\end{array}\right]
,
with

\displaystyle\Phi_{11}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F_{11}^{H}X_{11}+X_{11}F_{11}+X_{11}G_{11}X_{11}+K_{11},

\displaystyle\Phi_{21}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X_{21}(F_{11}+G_{11}X_{11})+F_{22}^{H}X_{21}+F_{12}^{H}X_{11}+K_{21},

\displaystyle\Phi_{22}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F_{22}^{H}X_{22}+X_{22}F_{22}+F_{12}^{H}X_{21}^{H}+X_{21}F_{12}+X_{21}G_{11}X_{21}^{H}+K_{22}.

For (
1
) (or (
2
)) to have a positive definite solution

it
is necessary that
\Phi_{11}\leq 0
(or
\Phi_{11}=0
) has a positive definite
solution
X_{11}
. The latter is equivalent to the condition that
\Psi\leq 0
(or

\Psi=0
)
has a positive definite solution
Y
, where
\Psi
is the
dual Riccati
operator
defined by

\Psi=F_{11}Y+YF_{11}^{H}+YK_{11}Y+G_{11}.

Using the block structures of
F_{11},K_{11},G_{11}
given in
Proposition
2

Y=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}Y_{11}&Y_{21}^{H}\\
Y_{21}&Y_{22}\end{array}\right],

one has

\Psi=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\Psi_{11}&\Psi_{21}^{H}\\
\Psi_{21}&\Psi_{22}\end{array}\right],

where

\Psi_{11}=\widetilde{F}_{11}Y_{11}+Y_{11}\widetilde{F}_{11}^{H}+Y_{11}\widetilde{K}_{11}Y_{11}+\widetilde{G}_{11}.

Its dual operator is

\widetilde{\Phi}_{11}=\widetilde{F}_{11}^{H}\widetilde{X}_{11}+\widetilde{X}_{11}\widetilde{F}_{11}+\widetilde{X}_{11}\widetilde{G}_{11}\widetilde{X}_{11}+\widetilde{K}_{11}.

As shown in
[

) (or (
2

\widetilde{\Phi}_{11}\leq 0
(or
\widetilde{\Phi}_{11}=0
)
has a positive definite solution. Recall that
(\widetilde{F}_{11},\widetilde{G}_{11})
is controllable
and
(\widetilde{F}_{11},\widetilde{K}_{11})
is observable.
In the following we therefore mainly consider the matrix triples
(F,G,K)
where these properties hold.

The ARE (

Hamiltonian matrix
(
3

W_{2}\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{C}^{2n\times n}

Lemma 3

(

Suppose that the Hamiltonian matrix (

Lemma 4
.

All solutions of the ARE (

the pair
(F,G)
is controllable, the pair
(F,K)

Proof.

Lemma

shows that the controllability of

and the observability of

ensure the existence of invertible solutions and the asymptotic stability of

guarantees the positive definiteness of the solutions. For necessity, without loss of generality we set

3.1
General perturbations

In order to study general perturbations to the Hamiltonian matrix that are associated with the perturbation (

For this we consider different subcases of perturbations, the perturbation of purely imaginary eigenvalues and the perturbation of non-imaginary eigenvalues. After studying the perturbation theory for purely imaginary eigenvalues, we move non-imaginary eigenvalues to the imaginary axis and then freeze them for further perturbation and in this way we successively move all eigenvalues to the imaginary axis without destroying the solvability of the ARI.

To proceed like this, we consider a Hamiltonian matrix that is already in the block upper triangular form
