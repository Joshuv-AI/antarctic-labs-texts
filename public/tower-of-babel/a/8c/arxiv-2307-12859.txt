Sums of proper divisors with missing digits
Kübra Benli, Giulia Cesana, Cécile Dartyge, Charlotte Dombrowsky, and Lola Thompson

Abstract
. Let s ⁡ ( n ) s(n) denote the sum of proper divisors of an integer n n . In 1992, Erdős, Granville, Pomerance, and Spiro (EGPS) conjectured that if 𝒜 \mathcal{A} is a set of integers with asymptotic density zero then s BINOP 1 ​ ( 𝒜 ) s^{-1}(\mathcal{A}) also has asymptotic density zero. In this paper we show that the EGPS conjecture holds when 𝒜 \mathcal{A} is taken to be a set of integers with missing digits. We also provide an overview of progress towards the EGPS conjecture and survey recent work on sets of integers with missing digits.

In the present paper, we consider the preimages under

The proofs of all of the aforementioned theorems make crucial use of the special forms that the numbers in these sets possess. The methods do not generalize to arbitrary sets with asymptotic density zero. In a different direction, the EGPS Conjecture has also been shown to hold for all sets of

.

[
25

Let

As a corollary, one can obtain (for example) that if

One can also deduce Theorem

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
m
be a positive integer. Then every positive integer
n
can be written uniquely as
n=ab
with
\gcd(a,b)=1
and

\displaystyle\mu^{2}(a)=1,\,\,\,p\mid a\text{ implies }p\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,m)\quad\text{ and }\quad p\mid b\text{ implies }p^{2}\mid b\text{ or }p\not\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,m).

Proof.

Let
n>1
as the result is vacuously true when
n=1
. Assume that
n
has the following prime factorization:

n=p_{1}^{e_{1}}p_{2}^{e_{2}}\dots p_{j}^{e_{j}},

with
p_{i}\not=p_{j}
if
i\not=j
. Changing the order if needed, without loss of generality assume that
p_{1},\dots,p_{J}\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,m)
with
e_{1}=\dots=e_{J}=1
and for
k=J+1,\dots,j
either
p_{k}\not\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,m)
or
e_{k}>1
. Then, we choose

a=p_{1}\dots p_{J}

and

b=\frac{n}{a}=p_{J+1}^{e_{J+1}}\dots p_{j}^{e_{j}}.

This finishes the proof.
∎

Remark.
If we remove the condition that
\gcd(a,b)=1
in Lemma
3.4
, then the decomposition
n=ab
is not unique. For example, if
n
has a prime divisor
q
with
q\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,m)
and
q^{3}\mid m
, then we can also choose

a=q\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}{p\equiv{-1}\ (\mathrm{mod}\,m)}\\
{p||n}\end{subarray}}p\quad\text{ and }\quad b=\frac{n}{a}.

We are now ready to prove our key lemma.

Proof of Lemma
1.9
.

Let
\ell\leq k
be chosen later. By Lemma
3.4
, an integer
n
can be written in a unique way as
n=ab
with
\gcd(a,b)=1
,
\mu^{2}(a)=1
and
p\mid a
implies
p\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{\ell})
and
b
such that

p\mid b\Rightarrow p^{2}\mid b\ \text{or }p\not\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{\ell}).

Suppose that
n=ab
, written in the above form, is a positive integer such that
g^{k}\nmid\sigma(n)
. Then we claim that
n
has at most
m\coloneqq\lfloor k/\ell\rfloor
prime factors
p
such that
p\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{\ell})
and
p^{2}\nmid n
. Indeed, if
n
has more than
k/\ell
prime factors
p\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{\ell})
with
p^{2}\nmid n
, say
p_{1},p_{2},\dots p_{m+1}
, then
\sigma(n)=(p_{1}+1)(p_{2}+1)\dots(p_{m+1}+1)K
, where
K
is a positive integer. Since each
p_{i}\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{\ell})
, this implies
\sigma(n)=c_{1}g^{\ell}c_{2}g^{\ell}\dots c_{m+1}g^{\ell}K=c_{1}c_{2}\dots c_{m+1}Kg^{(m+1)\ell}
with positive integers
c_{i}
. Since
(m+1)\ell\geq k
, this would imply that
g^{k}\mid\sigma(n)
, which contradicts our assumption. Thus for such
n
, we have
\omega(a)\leq m
. Combining what we noted above, we obtain

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{n\leq x}\\
{g^{k}\nmid\sigma(n)}\end{subarray}}1\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{ab\leq x}\\
{p\mid a\Rightarrow p\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{\ell})}\\
{\omega(a)\leq m}\\
{p\mid b\Rightarrow p^{2}\mid b\text{ or }p\not\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{\ell})}\end{subarray}}\mu^{2}(a).

To find an upper bound for the sum on the right-hand side, we first deal with the condition
\omega(a)\leq m.
To do that, we use Rankin’s method, replacing
1
with a nonnegative quantity that is at least
1
when
\omega(a)\leq m.
Namely, let
t\in(0,1)
be a parameter so that

t^{\omega(a)-m}>0
for any positive integer
a
and
t^{\omega(a)-m}\geq 1
if
\omega(a)\leq m
. Then we get

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{n\leq x}\\
{g^{k}\nmid\sigma(n)}\end{subarray}}1

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{ab\leq x}\\
{p\mid a\Rightarrow p\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{\ell})}\\
{p\mid b\Rightarrow p^{2}\mid b\text{ or }p\not\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{\ell})}\end{subarray}}\mu^{2}(a)t^{\omega(a)-m}

\displaystyle\leq t^{-m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{ab\leq x}\\
{p\mid a\Rightarrow p\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{\ell})}\\
{p\mid b\Rightarrow p^{2}\mid b\text{ or }p\not\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{\ell})}\end{subarray}}\mu^{2}(a)t^{\omega(a)}.

We can rewrite the sum on the right in terms of multiplicative functions

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{ab\leq x}\\
{p\mid a\Rightarrow p\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{\ell})}\\
{p\mid b\Rightarrow p^{2}\mid b\text{ or }p\not\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{\ell})}\end{subarray}}\mu^{2}(a)t^{\omega(a)}=\sum_{n\leq x}\sum_{ab=n}G(a)H(b)=\sum_{n\leq x}G\ast H(n),

where

G
and
H
are multiplicative functions defined by

\displaystyle G(p)\coloneqq\begin{cases}t,&\text{ if }p\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{\ell}),\\
0,&\text{ if }p\not\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{\ell}),\end{cases}

and
G(p^{\nu})\coloneqq 0
for all
\nu\geq 2
; and

\displaystyle H(p)\coloneqq\begin{cases}0,&\text{ if }p\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{\ell}),\\
1,&\text{ if }p\not\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{\ell}),\end{cases}

and
H(p^{\nu})\coloneqq 1
for all
\nu\geq 2
.

Let
f=G\ast H
. The function
f
is also multiplicative with

f(p)=G(p)+H(p)
, which gives

\displaystyle f(p)=\begin{cases}t,&\text{ if }p\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{\ell}),\\
1,&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

For
\nu\geq 2
we have

\displaystyle f\left(p^{\nu}\right)=\sum_{ab=p^{\nu}}G(a)H(b)=G(p)H(p^{\nu-1})+H\left(p^{\nu}\right)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{ if }\nu=2,\\
t+1,&\text{ if }\nu\geq 3\text{ and }p\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{\ell}),\\
1,&\text{ if }\nu\geq 3\text{ and }p\not\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{\ell}).\end{cases}

Now, we can apply Lemma
3.2
with
\lambda_{1}=2
and
\lambda_{2}=1
to
f
and obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{n\leq x}\\
{g^{k}\nmid\sigma(n)}\end{subarray}}1

\displaystyle\ll t^{-m}x\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}{p\leq x}\\
{p\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{\ell})}\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\left(1+\frac{t}{p}+\frac{1}{p^{2}}+(t+1)\sum_{\nu\geq 3}\frac{1}{p^{\nu}}\right)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}{p\leq x}\\
{p\not\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{\ell})}\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\sum_{\nu\geq 0}\frac{1}{p^{\nu}}.

Next, we note that for any prime
p
,

\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\sum_{\nu\geq 3}\frac{1}{p^{\nu}}=\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\frac{1}{p^{3}}\sum_{\nu\geq 0}\frac{1}{p^{\nu}}=\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\frac{1}{p^{3}}\frac{1}{(1-1/p)}=\frac{1}{p^{3}}

and

\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\sum_{\nu\geq 0}\frac{1}{p^{\nu}}=\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\frac{1}{(1-1/p)}=1

which yield

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{n\leq x}\\
{g^{k}\nmid\sigma(n)}\end{subarray}}1

\displaystyle\ll t^{-m}x\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}{p\leq x}\\
{p\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{\ell})}\end{subarray}}\left(1+\frac{t-1}{p}+\frac{1-t}{p^{2}}+\frac{t}{p^{3}}\right).

Furthermore, we see that

\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}{p\leq x}\\
{p\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{\ell})}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{t-1}{p}+\frac{1-t}{p^{2}}+\frac{t}{p^{3}}\right)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}{p\leq x}\\
{p\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{\ell})}\end{subarray}}\left(1+\frac{t-1}{p}\right)\left(1+\frac{1}{1+\frac{t-1}{p}}\frac{1-t}{p^{2}}+\frac{1}{1+\frac{t-1}{p}}\frac{t}{p^{3}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}{p\leq x}\\
{p\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{\ell})}\end{subarray}}\left(1+\frac{t-1}{p}\right)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}{p\leq x}\\
{p\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{\ell})}\end{subarray}}\left(1+\frac{1-t}{p(p+t-1)}+\frac{t}{p^{2}(p+t-1)}\right)

where the second product is bounded by a constant. So we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{n\leq x}\\
{g^{k}\nmid\sigma(n)}\end{subarray}}1

\displaystyle\ll t^{-m}x\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}{p\leq x}\\
{p\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{\ell})}\end{subarray}}\left(1+\frac{t-1}{p}\right).

In order to bound the product above, we use the Taylor–Young formula for
\log(1-X)
for
|X|\leq 1/2
, and the Siegel–Walfisz Theorem, stated in Lemma
3.1
. So, if
g^{\ell}
is less than a power of
\log x
, we have uniformly for
t\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\log\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}{p\leq x}\\
{p\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{\ell})}\end{subarray}}\left(1+\frac{t-1}{p}\right)\right)=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{p\leq x}\\
{p\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{\ell})}\end{subarray}}\log\left(1+\frac{t-1}{p}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{p\leq x}\\
{p\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{\ell})}\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{t-1}{p}+O\left(\frac{1}{p^{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(1-t)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{p\leq x}\\
{p\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{\ell})}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p}+O\left(\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{1}{p^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{(1-t)\log_{2}x}{\varphi(g^{\ell})}+O(1),

where the last equality above is deduced from the Siegel–Walfisz Theorem after partial summation, or more directly by the results of Norton
[
21
]
and Pomerance
[
28
]
mentioned just after Lemma
3.1
.
Thus, we obtain

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{n\leq x}\\
{g^{k}\nmid\sigma(n)}\end{subarray}}1\ll\frac{x}{t^{m}(\log x)^{(1-t)/\varphi(g^{\ell})}}.

It remains to choose
\ell
and
t
. Recall that
k\in\left[5(\log_{3}x),A(\log_{2}x)^{\gamma}\right]
. With our choices, we would like to have
t^{m}(\log x)^{(1-t)/\varphi(g^{\ell})}\rightarrow\infty.

Let
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
0<\alpha^{\prime}<\alpha<1
and
\gamma,\delta<\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}
. We let

\ell\coloneqq\left\lfloor(1-\alpha)\frac{\log_{3}x}{\log g}\right\rfloor,

t\coloneqq 1-\frac{1}{(\log_{2}x)^{\alpha^{\prime}}}.

Note that we then have
\ell\leq k
,
g^{\ell}\leq(\log_{2}x)^{1-\alpha}
and

1\leq m\leq\frac{k}{\ell}\leq A\frac{(\log_{2}x)^{\gamma}}{\Big\lfloor(1-\alpha)\frac{\log_{3}x}{\log g}\Big\rfloor}\leq 2A\frac{(\log_{2}x)^{\gamma}}{(1-\alpha)\frac{\log_{3}x}{\log g}}\leq(\log_{2}x)^{\gamma}

for
x
large enough.

With these choices, we get

\displaystyle t^{m}(\log x)^{(1-t)/\varphi(g^{\ell})}\geq

\displaystyle\,\,t^{m}(\log x)^{(1-t)/g^{\ell}}

\displaystyle\gg

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{1}{(\log_{2}x)^{\alpha^{\prime}}}\right)^{(\log_{2}x)^{\gamma}}(\log x)^{\frac{1}{(\log_{2}x)^{\alpha^{\prime}+1-\alpha}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left((\log_{2}x)^{\gamma}\log\left(1-\frac{1}{(\log_{2}x)^{\alpha^{\prime}}}\right)+\frac{\log_{2}x}{(\log_{2}x)^{\alpha^{\prime}+1-\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left((\log_{2}x)^{\gamma}\log\left(1-\frac{1}{(\log_{2}x)^{\alpha^{\prime}}}\right)+\frac{1}{(\log_{2}x)^{\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha}}\right).

Since
\gamma<\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}
, the first term in the exponential above in absolute value is at most one half of the second.
Hence for all
x\geq x_{0}
with
x_{0}=x_{0}(\alpha,\alpha^{\prime},\gamma)

we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{n\leq x}\\
{g^{k}\nmid\sigma(n)}\end{subarray}}1\ll x\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\log_{2}x)^{\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}}\right)\ll x\exp\left(-(\log_{2}x)^{\delta}\right),

where we have used the condition
\delta<\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}
. This
finishes the proof.
∎

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.8

Recall our basic setup: We have
g\in\mathbb{N}
,
g\geq 3
,
\mathcal{D}\subsetneq\{0,1,\dots,g-1\}
nonempty,
x
sufficiently large, and
0<\gamma<1
. For a positive integer

k\in\left[\frac{(\log_{2}x)^{\gamma}}{\log(g/|\mathcal{D}|)},2\frac{(\log_{2}x)^{\gamma}}{\log(g/|\mathcal{D}|)}\right]
, we write

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{W}_{s,\mathcal{D}}(x)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathcal{W}_{s,\mathcal{D}}(x)\\
\sigma(n)\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{k})\end{subarray}}1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathcal{W}_{s,\mathcal{D}}(x)\\
\sigma(n)\not\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{k})\end{subarray}}1\eqqcolon S_{1}+S_{2}.

We will now work on the upper bounds for
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
separately. To start with, we use a rather weak bound for
S_{2}
, where we drop the condition on the digit restriction on
s(n)
. Then we apply Lemma
1.9
with
\gamma=\delta
to obtain

\displaystyle S_{2}\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
g^{k}\nmid\sigma(n)\end{subarray}}1=O\left(x\exp\left(-\left(\log_{2}x\right)^{\gamma}\right)\right).

Next, we focus on finding an upper bound for
S_{1}
. Following a similar setting as in
[
23
]
, for
s(n)=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\varepsilon_{j}(s(n))g^{j}
, for some
N\geq 1
, we put

\displaystyle B\coloneqq\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\varepsilon_{j}(s(n))g^{j}

as the number formed by the
k
-rightmost digits of
s(n)
such that
s(n)\equiv B\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{k})
. Note that this implies
B\leq g^{k}-1.

Let
n\in\mathcal{W}_{s,\mathcal{D}}(x)
with
\sigma(n)\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{k})
. Then, since
g^{k}\mid\sigma(n)
, we have

\displaystyle n=\sigma(n)-s(n)\equiv-s(n)\equiv-B\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{k}).

So we can relax the condition on
S_{1}
with a congruence condition as follows

\displaystyle S_{1}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathcal{W}_{s,\mathcal{D}}(x)\\
\sigma(n)\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{k})\end{subarray}}1\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
n\equiv-B\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{k})\\
B\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{D}}(g^{k}-1)\end{subarray}}1=\sum_{B\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{D}}(g^{k}-1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
n\equiv-B\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g^{k})\end{subarray}}1\leq|\mathcal{D}|^{k}\Big\lfloor\frac{x}{g^{k}}\Big\rfloor+|\mathcal{D}|^{k},

where in the last inequality we used, for
M
a positive integer,
b\in\mathbb{Z}
and
X\geq M
, that

\#\{1\leq a\leq X:\,a\equiv b\ (\mathrm{mod}\,M)\}\leq\lfloor X/M\rfloor+1

along with (
2.2
).

Inserting our choice of
k
yields

S_{1}\ll x\exp\left(-k\log\left(g/|\mathcal{D}|\right)\right)\ll x\exp\left(-(\log_{2}x)^{\gamma}\right).

Thus, overall we obtain the following upper bound

\#\mathcal{W}_{s,\mathcal{D}}(x)=\#s^{-1}\left(\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{D}}\right)(x)\ll x\exp(-(\log_{2}x)^{\gamma})

as desired.∎

5.
Some remarks on a lower bound on
\#\mathcal{W}_{s,\mathcal{D}}(x)

As indicated earlier, as soon as we have
1\in\mathcal{D}
, then the elements in
\mathcal{W}_{s,\mathcal{D}}
are at least as frequent as the primes, since
s(p)=1
for all primes
p
. In the case when
{\mathcal{D}}=\{0,\ldots,g-1\}\setminus\{a_{0}\}
for some
a_{0}\in\{1,\ldots,g-1\}
and
g
large enough, we can prove that
s(n)
takes on infinitely many different values in
\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{D}}
by adapting the ideas used in
[
19
]
.

As already remarked in the introduction, if
p
and
q
are two distinct primes then
s(pq)=p+q+1
.
It is thus sufficient to prove that a positive proportion of ellipsephic integers can be expressed as
1
plus a sum of two primes. The arguments of
[
19
, Sections 6-8]
imply that

(5.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{D}}(g^{N}-1)}\sum_{p+q+1=n}\log p\log q=(c(\mathcal{D})+o(1))(g|\mathcal{D}|)^{N},

with

(5.2)

\displaystyle c(\mathcal{D})\coloneqq\frac{g}{\varphi(g)^{2}}\#\left\{(b_{1},b_{2})\in\{0,\ldots,g-1\}^{2}:\gcd(b_{1}b_{2},g)=1\text{ and }\exists\ d\in\mathcal{D}\text{ such that }b_{1}+b_{2}+1\equiv d\ (\mathrm{mod}\,g)\right\}.

When
g
is large enough,
c(\mathcal{D})>0
. Indeed, the set on the right-hand side of (
5.2
) is non-empty. It contains at least

(b_{1},b_{2})=(1,g-1)
if
a_{0}\not=1
, and
(b_{1},b_{2})=(1,1)
in the case
a_{0}=1
.

The proof of (
5.1
) consists of reproducing Sections 6-8 in
[
19
]
, with one additional prime variable which we handle trivially. The only small difference is in the computation of the main term coming from the major arcs at the end of the proof of
[
19
, Lemma 7.2]
.

Let
r(n)
denote the weight

r(n)=\sum_{p_{1}+p_{2}+1=n}\log p_{1}\log p_{2}.

We can apply an upper bound sieve to detect the primes
p_{1}
such that
n-1-p_{1}
is also prime.
By
[
14
, Theorem 3.11]
and the remark that appears afterwards,
for any odd integer
n\in[\sqrt{x},x]
we have that

(5.3)

\displaystyle r(n)\ll(\log x)^{2}\#\{p\leq n,\ n-1-p=p^{\prime}\}\ll x\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}{p>2}\\
{p|n-1}\end{subarray}}\frac{p-1}{p-2}\leq c_{1}x\log\log x,

for some absolute
c_{1}>0
.
By (
5.1
) and (
5.3
) we deduce that

(5.4)

\displaystyle\#\left\{n\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{D}}(x):\ n-1\text{ is a sum of two primes}\right\}\geq\frac{c(\mathcal{D})-o(1)}{c_{1}\log\log x}\#\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{D}}\left(x\right).

This implies that
s(n)
takes infinitely many different values in

\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{D}}
.
In this short section, we wanted to to highlight a result which is a direct consequence of the proofs presented in
[
19
]
. However, detecting ellipsephic primes is a considerably more difficult problem than detecting ellipsephic integers
n
such that
n-1
is a sum of two primes.
It is thus certainly possible to have a more precise lower bound than (
5.4
) for more general sets
\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{D}}
and with small base
g
. Also, we can probably remove the
\log\log x
in the denominator in (
5.4
) by combining the arguments in
[
32
, Section 3.2]
with
[
19
]
.
