1.
Introduction

The singularity of discrete random matrices is one of the fundamental problems of the non-asymptotic theory of random matrices. Let
B_{n}(p)
denote an
n\times n
random matrix with independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.)
\operatorname{Ber}(p)
entries (i.e. each entry is independently
1
with probability
p
and
0
with probability
1-p
). Let
q_{n}(p):=\mathbb{P}[B_{n}(p)\text{ is singular}]
. A classical result of Komlós
[
12
]
shows that
q_{n}(1/2)=o_{n}(1)
. Nearly 30 years later, the bound
q_{n}(1/2)\leq 0.999^{n}
was obtained by Kahn, Komlós, and Szemerédi
[
11
]
. The base of the exponent was improved to
0.939
by Tao and Vu
[
21
]
,
3/4+o_{n}(1)
also by Tao and Vu
[
22
]
, and
1/\sqrt{2}+o_{n}(1)
by Bourgain, Vu, and Wood
[
3
]
. In 2018, a breakthrough result of Tikhomirov
[
23
]
established that
q_{n}(p)=(1-p+o_{n}(1))^{n}
for fixed
p\in(0,1/2]
.

It is widely believed that the dominant reason for the singularity of discrete random matrices is, in a certain sense, local. A natural extension of a folklore conjecture (cf.
[
3
,
11
,
15
,
26
]
) in this direction is the following.

Conjecture 1.1
.

For fixed
p\in(0,1)
,

q_{n}(p)=(1+o_{n}(1))\mathbb{P}[B_{n}(p)\emph{hastwoequalrows,twoequalcolumns,azerorow,orazerocolumn}].

Note that this conjecture falls outside the scope of the aforementioned result of Tikhomirov
[
23
]
, since an analysis of the error term
(1+o_{n}(1))^{n}
appearing there reveals that it grows superpolynomially in
n
(cf.
[
15
]
).
Since for
p\in(0,1/2)
, the probability that a row or column is zero is exponentially more likely than the probability that two rows or two columns are equal, the following is a slight generalization of
1.1
in the case when
p\in(0,1/2)
, which is stated in a recent work of Litvak and Tikhomirov
[
15
]
.

Conjecture 1.2

(cf.
[
15
, Problem 8.2]
)
.

Let
p_{n}
be a sequence of real numbers satisfying
0<\liminf p_{n}\leq\limsup p_{n}<1/2
. Then

q_{n}(p_{n})=(1+o_{n}(1))\mathbb{P}[B_{n}(p)\emph{hasazeroroworcolumn}]=(2+o_{n}(1))n(1-p_{n})^{n}.

In recent work
[
15
]
, Litvak and Tikhomirov confirmed the validity of a sparse version of
1.2
. More precisely, they proved that the stated conclusion holds for all sequences of real numbers
p_{n}
satisfying
Cn^{-1}\ln{n}\leq p_{n}\leq c
, where
c>0
is a small universal constant, and
C>0
is a large universal constant. We note that in a certain range of sparsity limited to
p_{n}\leq n^{-1}\ln n+o_{n}(n^{-1}\ln\ln n)
, an analogous result was obtained in earlier work of Basak and Rudelson
[
1
]
. Very recently, using an intricate analysis of so-called ‘steep vectors’, Huang
[
6
]
was able to bridge the gap between the regimes covered by
[
1
]
and
[
15
]
, thereby completing the program of establishing
1.2
in all sparse regimes of interest.

In this paper, we establish a framework for addressing sharp questions about the singularity of random discrete matrices. As our first application, we resolve
1.2
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Fix
p\in(0,1/2)
. There exist constants
C_{p},\epsilon_{p},n_{p}>0
such that for all
n\geq n_{p}
and
t\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}[s_{n}(B_{n}(p))\leq t/\sqrt{n}]\leq C_{p}t+(2+(1-\epsilon_{p})^{n})n(1-p)^{n}.

Remark
.

It is readily seen from the proof that for any
\alpha\in(0,1/4)
, we may choose
\epsilon_{p}=\epsilon^{\prime}_{\alpha}
,
n_{p}=n^{\prime}_{\alpha}
, and
C_{p}=C^{\prime}_{\alpha}
for all
p\in(\alpha,1/2-\alpha)
. This uniformity in the choice of parameters is needed to resolve
1.2
.

Here,
s_{n}(B)
denotes the least singular value of the
n\times n
matrix
B
, which may be characterized as
s_{n}(B)=\inf_{x\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\lVert Bx\rVert_{2}
, where
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
denotes the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{2}
denotes the standard Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The techniques developed in this paper also lend themselves to the solution of the following problem, which has received attention from several researchers
[
17
,
5
,
7
,
24
]
in recent years as a test bed for (inverse) Littlewood–Offord type results in dependent settings. Let
Q_{n}
denote a random matrix with independent rows, each of which is chosen uniformly from among those vectors in
\{0,1\}^{n}
which have sum exactly
\lfloor n/2\rfloor
. The study of this model was initiated by Nguyen in
[
17
]
as a natural relaxation of the adjacency matrix of a uniformly random (directed) regular graph. He conjectured the following.

Conjecture 1.4

(
[
17
, Conjecture 1.4]
)
.

With notation as above,

\mathbb{P}[Q_{n}\emph{issingular}]=\left(\frac{1}{2}+o_{n}(1)\right)^{n}.

Nguyen showed that
\mathbb{P}[Q_{n}\text{ is singular}]=O_{C}(n^{-C})
for any
C>0
. After intermediate work
[
5
,
7
]
, an exponential upper bound on the singularity probability was only very recently obtained in work of Tran
[
24
]
. Our next result settles
1.4
, using completely different techniques compared to
[
17
,
5
,
7
,
24
]
.

Theorem 1.5
.

For every
\epsilon>0
, there exists
C_{\epsilon}
depending on
\epsilon
such that for all sufficiently large
n
, and for all
t\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}[s_{n}(Q_{n})\leq t/\sqrt{n}]\leq C_{\epsilon}t+(1/2+\epsilon)^{n}.

Remark
.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first instance where
\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{-1}\cdot\log\mathbb{P}[M_{n}\text{ is singular}]
has been determined for any natural model of random discrete matrices
M_{n}
with non-trivially dependent entries.

1.1.
Overview of techniques

Since the seminal works of Litvak, Pajor, Tomczak-Jaegermann, and Rudelson
[
13
]
, Rudelson
[
19
]
, and Rudelson and Vershynin
[
20
]
, upper bounds on the lower tail of the least singular value have been established by decomposing the unit sphere into ‘structured’ and ‘unstructured’ vectors, and analyzing invertibility on the two components separately. A high-level strategy of this nature underpins all recent sharp results on the invertibility of discrete random matrices
[
1
,
15
]
, and will also be used in our work. However, our definition and treatment of both the ‘structured’ and the ‘unstructured’ components departs significantly from
[
1
,
15
]
, as we now briefly discuss.

Structured vectors:
The structured vectors in our work are the so-called almost-constant vectors, which are those vectors on the unit sphere which have a
(1-\delta)
fraction of their coordinates within
\rho/\sqrt{n}
of each other, where
\delta,\rho>0
are small constants. This class of structured vectors arises naturally in the consideration of the invertibility of random matrices with prescribed row (and/or column) sums (cf.
[
10
,
14
,
24
]
); the connection to our setting, where all the entries of the matrix are independent, will be evident from the discussion of unstructured vectors. We remark that in
[
15
]
a different and much more involved class of structured vectors is considered using different techniques.

Invertibility on almost-constant vectors has been considered previously in
[
10
,
14
,
24
]
. However, for the purpose of proving the sharp invertibility estimate in
Theorem

1.3
, the techniques in these works are insufficient. In
Section

2
, we develop a new technique to prove sharp invertibility on the set of almost-constant vectors. Roughly speaking, the only problematic almost-constant vectors are those which are very close to a standard basis vector (indeed, standard basis vectors correspond to the event that the matrix has a zero column). For concreteness, consider vectors which are very close to the first standard basis vector. We show that, if any such vector has exponentially small image under
B_{n}(p)
, then either the first column of
B_{n}(p)
is the zero vector, or it must belong to a universal subset of nonzero vectors of
\{0,1\}^{n}
of measure (under
\operatorname{Ber}(p)^{\otimes n}
) at most
(1-p+\epsilon)^{n}
. The first case corresponds to the term
(1-p)^{n}
; for the second case, we use the work of Rudelson and Vershynin to show that, on our event, the probability that any vector in this universal subset appears as the first column of
B_{n}(p)
is at most
\exp(-4\epsilon n)
, at which point we can conclude using the union bound.

Unstructured vectors:
The starting point of our treatment of unstructured vectors is the insight of Litvak and Tikhomirov
[
15
]
to exploit the exponential gap (for
p<1/2
) between
(1-p)^{n}
and
\binom{n}{pn}^{-1}
by considering, for
x\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
an unstructured vector, the anticoncentration of the random sum
b_{1}x_{1}+\dots+b_{n}x_{n}
, where
(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})
is a random
\{0,1\}^{n}
vector
conditioned to have sum close to

pn
. However, in order to do this, one needs a rather precise theory of anticoncentration on a slice of the Boolean hypercube, which has traditionally been quite challenging. For instance, despite much interest (cf.
[
4
,
5
,
7
,
14
,
17
]
), exponential singularity bounds for
\{0,1\}
-valued random matrices with constrained row sums
[
24
]
(respectively, row and column sums
[
10
]
) were only very recently established.

We note that both the works
[
10
,
24
]
as well as
[
15
]
use a proxy for the conditional anticoncentration function resulting from manipulating Esseen’s inequality (these are analogues of the essential least common denominator of Rudelson and Vershynin
[
20
]
for the slice). In the case of
[
10
,
24
]
, the modification is closer in spirit to
[
20
]
, but cannot give sharp results, whereas the version in
[
15
]
is significantly more involved, but is not powerful enough to handle slices of the Boolean hypercube which are not very far from the central slice.

In this work, we completely avoid the use of Esseen’s inequality, choosing instead to work directly with the conditional anticoncentration function. Our approach is very much influenced by
[
23
]
, where a similar strategy was employed to show that the probability of singularity of
B_{n}(p)
is at most
(1-p+o_{n}(1))^{n}
. Indeed, our key ingredient (
Theorem

3.1
) shows that the conditional anticoncentration of uniformly random points of certain generic lattices is sufficiently good, and is an analogue of
[
23
, Theorem B]
, with the dependence of random variables on the slice requiring a necessarily delicate treatment.

Finally, for the proof of
Theorem

1.5
, since our treatment of unstructured vectors (discussed above) is already for the anti-concentration with respect to a uniformly random element of a Boolean slice, it only remains to analyse structured vectors. Compared to
Theorem

1.3
, we need a less precise estimate and hence, no longer need to isolate ‘elementary’ vectors. However, an additional complication arises since the inner product of any row of
Q_{n}
with the all-ones vector is deterministically
\lfloor n/2\rfloor
. This requires decomposing structured vectors into those which have sufficient mass in the all-ones direction and those which do not, and then using a suitable argument for each case.

1.2.
Extensions

The ideas used to establish
Theorem

3.1
, as well as our treatment of structured vectors, turn out to be remarkably robust. Indeed, the present article should be viewed as a companion work to
[
9
]
, which proves sharp invertibility results for random matrices whose individual entries are identical copies of a fixed, discrete random variable which is not uniform on its support, as well as logarithmically-optimal invertibility estimates (as in
[
23
]
) for all discrete distributions. Although the results of
[
9
]
subsume
Theorem

1.3
in this work, we have chosen to isolate the sparse Bernoulli case due to the significant interest in it (cf.
[
15
, Problem 8.2]
,
[
1
,
6
]
) and because the simplified setting of Bernoulli random variables allows us to present the basic elements of our framework in a clearer fashion. Additionally, the present work contains
Theorem

1.5
as well as complete details of certain arguments which are only sketched in
[
9
]
.

2.
Invertibility on almost-constant vectors

2.1.
Almost-constant and almost-elementary vectors

We recall the usual notion of almost-constant vectors, a modification of compressible vectors (see, e.g.,
[
24
]
).

Definition 2.1

(Almost-constant vectors)
.

For
\delta,\rho\in(0,1)
, we define

\operatorname{Cons}(\delta,\rho)
to be the set of
x\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
for which there exists some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that
|x_{i}-\lambda|\leq\rho/\sqrt{n}
for at least
(1-\delta)n
values
i\in[n]
.

The main result of this section is the following.

Proposition 2.2
.

For any
c>0
, there exist
\delta,\rho,\epsilon,n_{0}>0
depending only on
c
, such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
,

\mathbb{P}[\exists x\in\operatorname{Cons}(\delta,\rho):\lVert B_{n}(p)x\rVert_{2}\leq 2^{-cn}]\leq n(1-p)^{n}+(1-p-\epsilon)^{n}.

For later use, we record the following simple property of non-almost-constant vectors.

Lemma 2.3
.

For
\delta,\rho\in(0,1/4)
, there exist
\nu,\nu^{\prime}>0
depending only on
\delta,\rho
, and a finite set
\mathcal{K}
of
positive
real numbers, also depending only only on
\delta,\rho
, such that if
x\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\setminus\operatorname{Cons}(\delta,\rho)
, then at least one of the following two conclusions is satisfied.

(1)

There exist
\kappa,\kappa^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}
such that

|x_{i}|\leq\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{n}}\text{ for at least }\nu n\text{ indices }i\in[n],\text{ and }

\frac{\kappa+\nu^{\prime}}{\sqrt{n}}<|x_{i}|\leq\frac{\kappa^{\prime}}{\sqrt{n}}\text{ for at least }\nu n\text{ indices }i\in[n].

(2)

There exist
\kappa,\kappa^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}
such that

\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{n}}<x_{i}<\frac{\kappa^{\prime}}{\sqrt{n}}\text{ for at least }\nu n\text{ indices }i\in[n],\text{ and }

-\frac{\kappa^{\prime}}{\sqrt{n}}<x_{i}<-\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{n}}\text{ for at least }\nu n\text{ indices }i\in[n].

Proof.

Let
I_{0}:=\{i\in[n]:|x_{i}|\leq 4/\sqrt{\delta n}\}
. Since
\lVert x\rVert_{2}=1
, it follows that
|I_{0}|\geq(1-\delta/16)n
. We consider the following cases.

Case I:
|\{i\in I_{0}:|x_{i}|<\rho/(10\sqrt{n})\}|\geq\delta n/16
. Since
x\notin\operatorname{Cons}(\delta,\rho)
, there are at least
\delta n
indices
j\in J
such that
|x_{j}|\geq\rho/\sqrt{n}
. Moreover, at least
\delta n/8
of these indices satisfy
|x_{j}|\leq 4/\sqrt{\delta n}
. Hence, in this case, the first conclusion is satisfied for suitable choice of parameters.

Case II:
|\{i\in I_{0}:|x_{i}|<\rho/(10\sqrt{n})\}|<\delta n/16
and
|\{i\in I_{0}:x_{i}\leq-\rho/(10\sqrt{n})\}|\geq\delta n/16
and
|\{i\in I_{0}:x_{i}\geq\rho/(10\sqrt{n})\}|\geq\delta n/16
. In this case, the second conclusion is clearly satisfied for suitable choice of parameters.

Case III:
Either
|\{i\in I_{0}:x_{i}\geq\rho/(10\sqrt{n})\}|\geq(1-\delta/4)n
or
|\{i\in I_{0}:x_{i}\leq-\rho/(10\sqrt{n})\}|\geq(1-\delta/4)n
. We assume that we are in the first sub-case; the argument for the second sub-case is similar. We decompose

\displaystyle[0,4/\sqrt{\delta n}]=\cup_{\ell=1}^{L}J_{\ell},

where
J_{\ell}:=[(\ell-1)\cdot\rho/(10\sqrt{n}),\ell\cdot\rho/(10\sqrt{n}))
, and
L=O_{\delta,\rho}(1)
. Let

\displaystyle\ell_{0}:=\min\{\ell\in L:|\{i\in[n]:x_{i}\in J_{1}\cup\dots\cup J_{\ell}\}|\geq\delta n/16\}.

Since
x\notin\operatorname{Cons}(\delta,\rho)
, there are at least
\delta n
indices
j\in[n]
such that
|x_{j}-\ell_{0}\cdot\rho/(10\sqrt{n})|>\rho/\sqrt{n}
. On the other hand, by the assumption of this case and the definition of
\ell_{0}
, there exist at least
\delta n-(\delta n/4)-(\delta n/16)-(\delta n/16)>\delta n/2
indices
j\in I_{0}
for which
x_{j}>(\ell_{0}+2)\cdot\rho/(10\sqrt{n})
. Thus, the first conclusion is satisfied for suitable choice of parameters.
∎

We also isolate the following set of almost-elementary vectors.

Definition 2.4

(Almost-elementary vectors)
.

For
\delta>0
and
i\in[n]
, let

\operatorname{Coord}_{i}(\delta):=\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2}^{n}(e_{i},\delta)=\{x\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}:\lVert x-e_{i}\rVert_{2}\leq\delta\}.

We define the set of
\delta
-almost-elementary vectors by

\operatorname{Coord}(\delta):=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{Coord}_{i}(\delta).

To prove
Proposition

2.2
, we will handle almost-elementary vectors and non-almost-elementary vectors separately. We begin with the easier case of non-almost-elementary vectors.

2.2.
Invertibility on non-almost-elementary vectors

We begin with a by now standard anticoncentration result due to Kolmogorov-Lévy-Rogozin, which will also be important later.

Lemma 2.5

(
[
18
]
)
.

Let
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}
be independent random variables. Then, for any real numbers
r_{1},\dots,r_{n}>0
and any real
r\geq\max_{i\in[n]}r_{i}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i},r\bigg)\leq\frac{C_{\ref{lem:LKR}}r}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-\mathcal{L}(\xi_{i},r_{i}))r_{i}^{2}}},

where
C_{\ref{lem:LKR}}>0
is an absolute constant.

Using this, we can prove the following elementary fact about sums of independent
\operatorname{Ber}(p)
random variables.

Lemma 2.6
.

Fix
p,\delta\in(0,1/2)
. There exists
\theta=\theta(\delta,p)>0
such that for all
x\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\setminus\operatorname{Coord}(\delta)
,

\mathcal{L}(b_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+b_{n}x_{n},\theta)\leq 1-p-\theta,

where
b=(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})
is a random vector whose coordinates are independent
\operatorname{Ber}(p)
random variables.

Proof.

Since
\operatorname{Coord}(\delta)
is increasing with
\delta
, it suffices to prove the statement for all sufficiently small
\delta
(depending on
p
).
We may assume that
|x_{1}|\geq|x_{2}|\geq\cdots\geq|x_{n}|
. The desired conclusion follows by combining the following two cases.

Case 1:
Suppose
|x_{2}|>\delta^{4}
.
We claim that there is some
\theta=\theta(\delta,p)
for which

\mathcal{L}(b_{1}x_{1}+\dots+b_{n}x_{n},\theta)\leq\mathcal{L}(b_{1}x_{1}+b_{2}x_{2},\theta)\leq 1-p-\theta.

We borrow elements from
[
15
, Proposition 3.11]
. The first inequality is trivial. For the second inequality, we note that the random variable
b_{1}x_{1}+b_{2}x_{2}
is supported on the four points
\{0,x_{1},x_{2},x_{1}+x_{2}\}
. Moreover, the sets
\{0,x_{1}+x_{2}\}
and
\{x_{1},x_{2}\}
are
\delta^{4}
-separated, and each of these two sets is attained with probability at most
\max\{p^{2}+(1-p)^{2},2p(1-p)\}<1-p-\theta
, where the final inequality uses
p<1/2
.

Case 2:
|x_{2}|\leq\delta^{4}
. Note that we must have
\lVert(x_{2},\dots,x_{n})\rVert_{2}\geq\delta/2
, since otherwise, we would have
\lVert x-e_{1}\rVert_{2}<\delta
, contradicting
x\notin\operatorname{Coord}(\delta)
. We claim that there is some
\theta=\theta(\delta,\rho)>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(b_{1}x_{1}+\dots+b_{n}x_{n},\theta)\leq\mathcal{L}(b_{2}x_{2}+\dots+b_{n}x_{n},\theta)\leq 1-p-\theta.

Once again, the first inequality is trivial. The second inequality follows from
Lemma

2.5
applied with
\xi_{i}=b_{i}x_{i}
for
i=2,\dots,n
,
r_{i}=|x_{i}|/4
, and
r=\delta^{4}
, and from our assumption that
\delta
was small enough in terms of

.
∎

By combining the preceding statement with a standard net argument exploiting the low metric entropy of almost-constant vectors and the well-controlled operator norm of random matrices with independent centered subgaussian entries (cf.
[
25
, Lemma 4.4.5]
), we obtain invertibility on non-almost-elementary vectors.

Proposition 2.7
.

Fix
p\in(0,1/2)
. Then, for any
\delta^{\prime}>0
, there exist
\delta,\rho,\epsilon^{\prime},n_{0}>0
(depending on
p,\delta^{\prime}
) such that for all

,

\mathbb{P}[\exists x\in\operatorname{Cons}(\delta,\rho)\setminus\operatorname{Coord}(\delta^{\prime}):\lVert B_{n}(p)x\rVert_{2}\leq\epsilon^{\prime}\sqrt{n}]\leq(1-p-\epsilon^{\prime})^{n}.

Proof.

The argument is closely related to the proof of
[
23
, Proposition 3.6]
, and we omit the standard details. The point is that we can first choose
\epsilon^{\prime}
depending on
\delta^{\prime},p
, then choose
\rho
sufficiently small depending on
\delta^{\prime},p,\epsilon^{\prime}
, and finally, choose
\delta
sufficiently small depending on all prior choices.
∎

2.3.
Invertibility on almost-elementary vectors

We now prove the much more delicate claim that
\operatorname{Coord}(\delta^{\prime})
contributes the appropriate size to the singularity probability.

Proposition 2.8
.

Fix
p\in(0,1/2)
. Given
\theta^{\prime}>0
, there exist
\delta^{\prime},\theta,n_{0}>0
depending on
p
and
\theta^{\prime}
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
,

\mathbb{P}[\exists x\in\operatorname{Coord}(\delta^{\prime}):\lVert B_{n}(p)x\rVert_{2}\leq\exp(-\theta^{\prime}n)]\leq n\cdot\bigg((1-p)^{n}+(1-p-\theta)^{n}\bigg).

Before proceeding to the proof, we need the following preliminary fact, which essentially follows from the seminal work of Rudelson and Vershynin
[
20
]
(although we were not able to locate the precise statement needed here in the literature).

Lemma 2.9
.

Fix
p\in(0,1)
. For any

, there exist

depending on
c
and
p
for which the following holds. For all

and for any

with
\lVert v\rVert_{2}\geq 1
, we have

\mathbb{P}[\exists x\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}:\lVert Ax-v\rVert_{2}\leq 2^{-cn}]\leq 2^{-c^{\prime}n},

where
A
is a random
n\times(n-1)
matrix with independent
\operatorname{Ber}(p)
entries.

Proof.

By reindexing the coordinates, we may write

A=\begin{bmatrix}R\\
B\end{bmatrix},\quad v=\begin{bmatrix}v_{1}\\
v^{\prime}\end{bmatrix},

where
B
is an
(n-1)\times(n-1)
matrix,
v^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
and
\lVert v^{\prime}\rVert_{2}\geq 1/2
. Let
\mathcal{E}=\{s_{n-1}(B)\leq 2^{-cn/2}\}
. Then, by an extension of the main result of Rudelson and Vershynin
[
20
]
(see, e.g.,
[
8
, Theorem 1.3]
for a concrete reference),
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}]\leq 2^{-c_{1}n}
for some

depending on
c
and
p
.

Moreover, on the event
\mathcal{E}^{c}
, if there exists some
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
such that

, then

\lVert Bx-v^{\prime}\rVert_{2}\leq 2^{-cn}\implies\lVert x-B^{-1}v^{\prime}\rVert_{2}\leq 2^{-cn/2},\quad\text{ and }

|Rx-v_{1}|\leq 2^{-cn}\implies|R(B^{-1}v^{\prime})-v_{1}|\leq 2^{-cn}+n2^{-cn/2}.

Let
x_{0}:=B^{-1}v^{\prime}/\lVert B^{-1}v^{\prime}\rVert_{2}
; this is a random vector depending on
B
. It follows from a straightforward modification of the argument of Rudelson and Vershynin that there exists a constant
c_{2}>0
, depending on
c
and
p
, such that with probability at least
1-2^{-c_{2}n}
,
\mathcal{L}(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}b_{i}\cdot(x_{0})_{i},2^{-cn/4})\leq c_{2}^{-1}2^{-c_{2}n}
, where
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}
are independent
\operatorname{Ber}(p)
random variables (again, one has to take into account that
\operatorname{Ber}(p)
does not have mean

, but this is not an issue, see, e.g.,
[
8
]
). Let
\mathcal{G}
denote the event (depending on
B
) that this occurs.

Finally, note that since
\lVert B\rVert\leq n
deterministically, we have
\lVert B^{-1}v^{\prime}\rVert_{2}\geq 1/(2n)
. Hence, we see that for all

sufficiently large depending on
c
and
p
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}^{c}\wedge\lVert Ax-v\rVert_{2}\leq 2^{-cn}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}^{c}\wedge|R(B^{-1}v^{\prime})-v_{1}|\leq 2n\cdot 2^{-cn/2}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}^{c}\wedge\mathcal{G}\wedge|R(B^{-1}v^{\prime})-v_{1}|\leq 2n\cdot 2^{-cn/2}]+2^{-c_{2}n}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{L}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}b_{i}\cdot(x_{0})_{i},4n^{2}\cdot 2^{-cn/2}\bigg)+2^{-c_{2}n}\leq 2^{-c_{2}n/2},

which completes the proof.
∎

Now we are ready to prove
Proposition

2.8
.

Proposition

By taking a union bound, and exploiting the permutation invariance of the distribution of the matrix, it suffices to prove the statement for

3.
The conditional threshold function of random lattice points

In this section, we establish the key ‘inversion of randomness’ estimate on vectors with poor anticoncentration on the slice of the Boolean hypercube. This follows a direction introduced by Tikhomirov
[
23
]
. In this approach, the relevant Lévy concentration function of a random vector is replaced with certain random averages of functions. One then shows that the random vectors with large values of the Lévy concentration function are
super-exponentially
rare, by first demonstrating a weaker notion of anticoncentration after revealing
(1-\epsilon)n
coordinates of the random vector, and then iterating a smoothing procedure on linear-sized pieces of the vector which allows one to bootstrap the strength of anticoncentration considered. Our major challenges lie in the non-independence of the coordinates of a vector on the Boolean slice, as the arguments in
[
23
]
rely strongly on the independence structure of the considered model.

3.1.
Statement and preliminaries

Let
N,n\geq 1
be integers and let
0<\delta<1/4
,
K_{3}>K_{2}>K_{1}>1
be real parameters. We say that
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is
(N,n,K_{1},K_{2},K_{3},\delta)

•

\mathcal{A}=A_{1}\times\cdots\times A_{n}

is a subset of
\mathbb{Z}

•

,

•

,

•

A_{i}
is an integer interval of size at least
2N+1
for

, and either (P1) and (P2) or (Q1) and (Q2) hold:

(P1)

A_{2i}
is an integer interval of size at least

and is contained in
[-K_{1}N,K_{1}N]
for

,

(P2)

for

.

for

.

Remark
.

The conditions on sets up to

-almost constant, with (P1), (P2) corresponding to the first conclusion of

and (Q1), (Q2) corresponding to the second conclusion.

Let
\mathcal{A}=A_{1}\times\cdots\times A_{n}

f_{\mathcal{A},s,\ell}(t):=\mathbb{E}_{b}\bigg[f\bigg(t+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}b_{j}X_{j}\bigg)\bigg|\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}b_{j}=s\bigg]=\frac{1}{\binom{\ell}{s}}\sum_{v\in\{0,1\}_{s}^{\ell}}f(t+v_{1}X_{1}+\cdots+v_{\ell}X_{\ell}),

where

The main result of this section is the following.

Theorem 3.1

For

Definition 3.7

(Step record and averaging sequence)

Fix

•

•

Since

(3.2)

In order to remove the dependence of

5.
Proof of
Theorem

1.3

We now have all the ingredients needed to prove
Theorem

1.3
. The proof uses the insight from
[
15
]
of exploiting the exponential gap between
\binom{n}{pn}
and
(1-p)^{n}
for
p<1/2
by using a ‘row boosting’ argument to reduce to an anticoncentration problem on a well-conditioned slice.

Proof of
Theorem

1.3
.

Throughout, we fix functions
x(A),y(A)
which take as input a matrix
A
and output (fixed, but otherwise arbitrary) right and left least singular unit vectors, respectively. Let
B=B_{n}(p)
for simplicity. Fix
\epsilon>0
such that
\binom{n}{pn}\exp(\epsilon n)\leq(1-p-\epsilon)^{n}
.

Step 1:
By the work of Rudelson and Vershynin
[
20
]
, there is some
c_{p}>0
so that for all
t>2^{-2c_{p}n}
,

\mathbb{P}[s_{n}(B_{n}(p))\leq t/\sqrt{n}]\leq C_{p}t;

note that there is a slight complication since
\operatorname{Ber}(p)
is not centered, but this can be handled using standard techniques (see, e.g.,
[
8
, Theorem 1.3]
) Therefore, it suffices to consider the case
t\leq 2^{-2c_{p}n}
.

Step 2:
For
\delta,\rho\in(0,1)
, we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{L}(\delta,\rho)

\displaystyle=\{\exists y\in\operatorname{Cons}(\delta,\rho):\lVert y(B)^{T}B\rVert_{2}\leq 2^{-c_{p}n}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{R}(\delta,\rho)

\displaystyle=\{\exists x\in\operatorname{Cons}(\delta,\rho):\lVert Bx(B)\rVert_{2}\leq 2^{-c_{p}n}\}.

Applying
Proposition

2.2
with
c_{p}>0
, we find that there exist
\delta,\rho,\epsilon^{\prime}>0
such that for all sufficiently large
n
,

\mathbb{P}[s_{n}(B)\leq t/\sqrt{n}]\leq 2n(1-p)^{n}+2(1-p-\epsilon^{\prime})^{n}+\mathbb{P}[s_{n}(B)\leq t/\sqrt{n}\wedge\mathcal{E}_{L}(\delta,\rho)^{c}\wedge\mathcal{E}_{R}(\delta,\rho)^{c}].

Here, we have used that the distribution of
B
is invariant under transposition.

Step 3:
Let
\gamma=\gamma_{\ref{prop:structure}}(\delta,\rho,p,\epsilon)
. Let
W_{\gamma}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
denote the set of vectors
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\in[pn-\gamma n,pn+\gamma n]
. As in the proof of
Proposition

4.2
, let
Q\geq 1
be a constant such that the event

\mathcal{W}_{Q}:=\{|\{i\in[n]:B_{i}\notin W_{\gamma}|\leq Q\}

holds with probability at least
1-2^{-1729n}
. Then, it suffices to bound
\mathbb{P}[s_{n}(B)\leq t/\sqrt{n}\wedge\mathcal{E}_{L}^{c}\wedge\mathcal{E}_{R}^{c}\wedge\mathcal{W}_{Q}]
, where for simplicity, we have omitted the parameters
\delta,\rho
fixed in the previous step.

Let
B_{1},\ldots,B_{n}
denote the rows of
B
, and for simplicity, let
y=y(B)
. On the event that
s_{n}(B)\leq t/\sqrt{n}
, we have

\lVert y_{1}B_{1}+\cdots+y_{n}B_{n}\rVert_{2}\leq t/\sqrt{n}.

Moreover, on the event
\mathcal{E}_{L}^{c}
, using
Lemma

2.3
, there is a set
I\subseteq[n]
such that
|I|\geq\nu n
and such that for all
i\in I
,
|y_{i}|\geq\kappa/\sqrt{n}
, for some
\kappa:=\kappa(\delta,\rho)>0
. In particular, since for any
i\in[n]
,

\lVert y_{1}B_{1}+\dots+y_{n}B_{n}\rVert_{2}\geq|y_{i}|\operatorname{dist}(B_{i},H_{i}),

where
H_{i}
denotes the span of rows
B_{1},\dots,B_{i-1},B_{i+1},\dots B_{n}
, it follows that

\operatorname{dist}(B_{i},H_{i})\leq\frac{t}{\kappa}\text{ for all }i\in I.

Also, on the event
\mathcal{W}_{Q}
, there are at least
\nu n/2
indices
i\in I
such that
B_{i}\in W_{\gamma}
. Thus, we see that

\mathbb{P}[s_{n}(B)\leq t/\sqrt{n}\wedge\mathcal{E}_{L}^{c}\wedge\mathcal{E}_{R}^{c}\wedge\mathcal{W}_{Q}]\leq\frac{2}{\nu n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}[\operatorname{dist}(B_{i},H_{i})\leq t/\kappa\wedge\mathcal{E}_{L}^{c}\wedge\mathcal{E}_{R}^{c}\wedge B_{i}\in W_{\gamma}].

Step 4:
By symmetry, it suffices to bound
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}_{1}]
, where

\mathcal{B}_{1}:=\operatorname{dist}(B_{1},H_{1})\leq t/\kappa\wedge\mathcal{E}_{R}^{c}\wedge B_{1}\in W_{\gamma}.

Let
v(H_{1})
be a unit vector normal to
H_{1}
. Then, by
Proposition

4.2
, except with probability
4^{-n}
(over the randomness of
H_{1}
), exactly one of the following holds.

•

v(H_{1})\in\operatorname{Cons}(\delta,\rho)
, or

•

\mathcal{T}_{p,\gamma}(v(H_{1}),L)\leq\binom{n}{pn}^{-1}\exp(\epsilon n),

where
L:=L_{\ref{prop:structure}}(\delta,\rho,p,\epsilon)
. If the first possibility occurs, then
\mathcal{B}_{1}
cannot hold, since then,
v(H_{1})\in\operatorname{Cons}(\delta,\rho)
satisfies

\lVert Bv(H_{1})\rVert_{2}=|\langle B_{1},v(H_{1})\rangle|\leq\operatorname{dist}(B_{1},H_{1})\leq t/\kappa\leq 2^{-2c_{p}n}/\kappa,

which contradicts
\mathcal{E}_{R}^{c}
for all
n
sufficiently large. Hence, the second possibility must hold. But then, using
\operatorname{dist}(B_{1},H_{1})\geq|\langle B_{1},v(H_{1})\rangle|
, we have that (over the randomness of
B_{1}
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\operatorname{dist}(B_{1},H_{1})\leq t/\kappa\wedge B_{1}\in W_{\gamma}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}[|\langle B_{1},v(H_{1})\rangle|\leq t/\kappa\mid B_{1}\in W_{\gamma}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{Lt}{\kappa}+\binom{n}{pn}^{-1}\exp(\epsilon n)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{Lt}{\kappa}+(1-p-\epsilon)^{n}.

This completes the proof.
∎

6.
Singularity of random combinatorial matrices

In this section, we discuss the proof of
Theorem

1.5
. Given
Proposition

4.2
, by a similar argument as in the previous section, we see that the only additional ingredient required is the following estimate for invertibility on almost constant vectors.

Proposition 6.1
.

For any
\epsilon>0
, there exist
\delta,\rho,c,n_{0}
depending on
\epsilon
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
,

\mathbb{P}\bigg[\inf_{x\in\operatorname{Cons}(\delta,\rho)}\lVert Q_{n}x\rVert_{2}\leq c\sqrt{n}\vee\inf_{y\in\operatorname{Cons}(\delta,\rho)}\lVert yQ_{n}\rVert_{2}\leq c\sqrt{n}\bigg]\leq\bigg(\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon\bigg)^{n}.

We begin with the easier case of
\lVert yQ_{n}\rVert_{2}
.

Lemma 6.2
.

For any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
c,n_{0}
depending on
\epsilon
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
and for any
y\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
,

\mathbb{P}[\lVert yQ_{n}\rVert_{2}\leq c\sqrt{n}]\leq(1/2+\epsilon)^{n}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
|y_{1}|\geq\dots\geq|y_{n}|
. We divide the proof into two cases depending on
|y_{1}|
. Let
\delta>0
be a constant to be chosen at the end of the proof.

Case I:
|y_{1}|<\delta
. Note that any entry in the first
n/4
columns of
Q_{n}
, conditioned on all the remaining entries in the first
n/4
columns of
Q_{n}
, is distributed as
\operatorname{Ber}(p)
for some
p\in[1/3,2/3]
. Moreover, by
Lemma

2.5
, it follows that for independent random variables
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}
, where
\xi_{i}\sim\operatorname{Ber}(p_{i})
for some
p_{i}\in[1/3,2/3]
,

\mathcal{L}(y_{1}\xi_{1}+\dots+y_{n}\xi_{n},\delta)\leq 3C_{\ref{lem:LKR}}\delta.

Therefore, a slight conditional generalization of the second part of the tensorization lemma
[
23
, Lemma 3.2]
(which has the same proof) shows that

\mathbb{P}[\lVert yQ_{n}\rVert_{2}\leq\delta\sqrt{n/8}]\leq(20C_{\ref{lem:LKR}}\delta)^{n/8}\leq(1/4)^{n},

provided that
\delta
is chosen sufficiently small depending on
C_{\ref{lem:LKR}}
.

Case II:
|y_{1}|\geq\delta
. Let
R_{1},\dots,R_{n}
denote the rows of
Q_{n}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\lVert yQ_{n}\rVert_{2}\leq\delta c\sqrt{n}]

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{R_{2},\dots,R_{n}}\mathbb{P}[\lVert y_{1}R_{1}+y_{2}R_{2}+\dots+y_{n}R_{n}\rVert_{2}\leq\delta c\sqrt{n}|R_{2},\dots,R_{n}]

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbb{P}[\lVert R_{1}-v\rVert_{2}\leq c\sqrt{n}]\leq\binom{n}{n/2}^{-1}\binom{n}{2c^{2}n}\leq\bigg(\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon\bigg)^{n},

provided that
c>0
is chosen to be sufficiently small depending on
\epsilon>0
. This completes the proof.
∎

Next, we deal with the harder case of
\lVert Q_{n}x\rVert_{2}
. We will need the following analogue of
[
23
, Lemma 3.5]

Lemma 6.3
.

For any
\epsilon\in(0,1/8)
, there exist
\theta=\theta(\epsilon)>0
and
n_{0}
depending on
\epsilon
for which the following holds. For all
n\geq n_{0}
and for all
x\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
such that
|\langle x,1_{n}/\sqrt{n}\rangle|\leq 1/2
, we have

\mathcal{L}(q\cdot x,\theta)\leq 1/2+\epsilon,

where
q
is distributed uniformly on
\{0,1\}^{n}_{n/2}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
|x_{1}|\geq\dots\geq|x_{n}|
. Again, we divide the proof into two cases depending on
|x_{1}|
. Let
\delta>0
be a constant to be chosen at the end of the proof.

Case I:
|x_{1}|<\delta
. Let
\mu:=\mathbb{E}[q\cdot x]
and
\sigma^{2}:=\operatorname{Var}(q\cdot x)
. Since
\langle x,1_{n}\rangle\leq\sqrt{n}/2
, a direct computation shows that
\sigma^{2}\geq 3/16
. Moreover, a quantitative combinatorial central limit theorem due to Bolthausen
[
2
]
shows that the
L^{\infty}
distance between the cumulative distribution function of
(q\cdot x-\mu)/\sigma
and that of the standard Gaussian is at most
C\delta
, where
C
is an absolute constant. Hence, for all
\delta
sufficiently small, we have
\mathcal{L}(q\cdot x,\delta)\leq 1/4
whenever
|x_{1}|<\delta
.

Case II:
|x_{1}|\geq\delta
. Let
\mathcal{G}
denote the event (depending on
q
) that

(n-\epsilon^{2}n-1)^{-1}\sum_{i=2}^{n-\epsilon^{2}n}q_{i}\in[1/2-\epsilon^{4}/2,1/2+\epsilon^{4}/2].

Then for all sufficiently large
n
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{r\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}[|q\cdot x-r|\leq\theta]

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{r\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}[|q\cdot x-r|\leq\theta\wedge\mathcal{G}]+\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{G}^{c}]

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{r\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}[|q\cdot x-r|\leq\theta\wedge\mathcal{G}]+2\exp(-\epsilon^{8}n/128),

where the final inequality is by a standard large deviation estimate. It remains to control
\mathbb{P}[|q\cdot x-r|\leq\theta\wedge\mathcal{G}]
. For this, fix any realization
q^{\prime}:=(q_{2},\dots,q_{n-\epsilon^{2}n})
satisfying
\mathcal{G}
. Note that

1/2-2\epsilon^{2}\leq\inf_{q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}}\mathbb{P}[q_{1}=0\mid q^{\prime}]\leq\sup_{q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}}\mathbb{P}[q_{1}=0\mid q^{\prime}]\leq 1/2+2\epsilon^{2}.

Note also that, since
\sum_{i\geq n-\epsilon^{2}n}x_{i}^{2}\leq\epsilon^{2}
(this uses
\lVert x\rVert_{2}=1
and
|x_{1}|\geq|x_{2}|\geq\dots\geq|x_{n}|
), it follows that

\sup_{q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G},q_{1}}\operatorname{Var}\bigg[\sum_{i\geq n-\epsilon^{2}n}q_{i}x_{i}\bigg|q_{1},q^{\prime}\bigg]\leq\epsilon^{2},

so that by Markov’s inequality,

\sup_{q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G},q_{1}}\mathbb{P}\bigg[\bigg|\sum_{i\geq n-\epsilon^{2}n}q_{i}x_{i}-f(q^{\prime},q_{1})\bigg|\geq\frac{\delta}{8}\bigg|q^{\prime},q_{1}\bigg]\leq\frac{32\epsilon^{2}}{\delta^{2}},

where
f(q^{\prime},q_{1})
denotes the mean of
\sum_{i\geq n-\epsilon^{2}n}q_{i}x_{i}
conditioned on
q^{\prime},q_{1}
.
Finally, since
|x_{1}|\geq\delta
, and since

\sup_{q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}}|f(q^{\prime},0)-f(q^{\prime},1)|\leq|x_{n-\epsilon^{2}n}|\leq 2/\sqrt{n},

it follows by putting everything together that

\displaystyle\sup_{r\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}[|q\cdot x-r|\leq\theta\wedge\mathcal{G}]

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{r\in\mathbb{R}}\sup_{q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}}\mathbb{P}[|q\cdot x-r|\leq\theta\mid q^{\prime}]\leq 1/2+2\epsilon^{2}+64\epsilon^{2}/\delta^{2},

provided that
\theta
is chosen sufficiently small compared to
\delta
, and
n
is sufficiently large. Indeed, the two values of
q_{1}x_{1}
(for
q_{1}=1
and
q_{1}=0
) differ by
|x_{1}|
, which is at least
\delta
by assumption, and the above discussion shows that given
q^{\prime}
and
q_{1}
,
\sum_{i\geq n-\epsilon^{2}n}q_{i}x_{i}
is localized in an interval of length
\delta/2+2/\sqrt{n}
except with probability at most
32\epsilon^{2}/\delta^{2}
. Since
\delta
is an absolute constant coming from
Case I
, this gives the desired conclusion for all sufficiently small
\epsilon
, which completes the proof.
∎

Given the previous two lemmas, the proof of
Proposition

6.1
is by now standard.

Proof of
Proposition

6.1
.

The estimate for
\inf_{y\in\operatorname{Cons}(\delta,\rho)}\lVert yQ_{n}\rVert_{2}\leq c\sqrt{n}
(for a suitable choice of
\delta,\rho,c
) follows immediately by combining
Lemma

6.2
with the low metric entropy of
\operatorname{Cons}(\delta,\rho)
. To exploit the latter, one could either use a randomized rounding based net construction due to Livshyts
[
16
, Theorem 4]
, which uses that
\lVert Q_{n}\rVert_{\operatorname{HS}}^{2}\leq n^{2}
, or one could use the fact that there exists a constant
K
such that with probability at least
1-4^{-n}
, all singular values of
Q_{n}
except for the top singular value are at most
K\sqrt{n}
(see
[
24
, Proposition 2.8]
).

For the estimate on
\inf_{x\in\operatorname{Cons}(\delta,\rho)}\lVert Q_{n}x\rVert_{2}\leq c\sqrt{n}
(for suitable
\delta,\rho,c
), we begin by using the fact
[
24
, Proposition 2.8]
noted above that there exists a constant
K>0
such that with probability at least
1-4^{-n}
, the operator norm of
Q_{n}
restricted to the subspace perpendicular to
1_{n}
is at most
K\sqrt{n}
. Let us denote this event by
\mathcal{E}_{K}
. Then, on
\mathcal{E}_{K}
, for any
x\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
such that
\langle x,1_{n}/\sqrt{n}\rangle\geq 1/2
, we have
\lVert Q_{n}x\rVert_{2}\geq n/4-K\sqrt{n}
.
Hence, on the event
\mathcal{E}_{K}
, and for all
n
sufficiently large, it suffices to consider the infimum over those vectors
x\in\operatorname{Cons}(\delta,\rho)
which also satisfy
\langle x,1_{n}/\sqrt{n}\rangle<1/2
. For this, we can use
Lemma

3.5
followed by the tensorization lemma (cf.
[
23
, Lemma 3.2]
), and then exploit the low metric entropy of
\operatorname{Cons}(\delta,\rho)
as above. We leave the details to the interested reader.
∎
