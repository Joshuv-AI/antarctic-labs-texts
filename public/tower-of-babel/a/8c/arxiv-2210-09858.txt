The vanishing of excess heat for nonequilibrium processes reaching zero ambient temperature
Faezeh Khodabandehlou, Christian Maes, Irene Maes, Karel Netočný

Abstract
We present the mathematical ingredients for an extension of the Third Law of Thermodynamics (Nernst heat postulate) to nonequilibrium processes. The central quantity is the excess heat which measures the quasistatic addition to the steady dissipative power when a parameter in the dynamics is changed slowly. We prove for a class of driven Markov jump processes that it vanishes at zero environment temperature. Furthermore, the nonequilibrium heat capacity goes to zero with temperature as well. Main ingredients in the proof are the matrix-forest theorem for the relaxation behavior of the heat flux, and the matrix-tree theorem giving the low-temperature asymptotics of the stationary probability. The main new condition for the extended Third Law requires the absence of major (low-temperature induced) delays in the relaxation to the steady dissipative structure.

Introduction

The Nernst Postulate (1907) states that the change of entropy in any isothermal process approaches zero as the temperature reaches absolute zero

Its historical origin lies in the variational principle of Thomsen and Berthelot, which was an empirical precursor of the Gibbs variational principle,
callen
.
. It evolved into the Planck version of the Third Law of Thermodynamics, stating that the entropy of a perfect crystal of a pure substance vanishes at absolute zero. That Third Law differs from the First and Second Laws, as it does not automatically follow from more microscopic considerations. In fact, it sometimes fails, as exemplified by ice models in
paul

aL
:
for quantum and classical lattice systems, the entropy density at absolute zero temperature is directly related to the degeneracy of the ground state
corresponding to boundary conditions with the highest degeneracy. The Third Law holds for example for any ferromagnetic Ising system. Counterexamples include dimer systems and (spin) ice models,
kast
;
temp
;
lieb
;
paul
.

It is natural to ask about thermal properties of nonequilibria as well. Then appears the question of low-temperature asymptotics and a possible formulation of an extended Third Law.
Note that the temperature (which goes to zero) need not be physically associated to the degrees of freedom of the system but rather to a thermal bath or large equilibrium environment weakly coupled to the system. The main point to remember is that steady nonequilibrium systems are open (and possibly small) and constantly dissipate heat into the (much larger) environment. When parameters change, such as the temperature of that heat bath or the volume of the system, the heat flux may change. In other words, when connecting two nonequilibrium conditions via a quasistatic transformation, an excess heat may flow. The present paper rigorously defines that notion of excess heat, and we prove, under certain conditions, that this excess heat vanishes at zero ambient temperature. We do that for irreducible (continuous time) Markov jump processes with finite state space. To connect their mathematics with thermal properties, we require the interpretation of local detailed balance, which identifies the heat during a transition with the logarithmic ratio of forward to backward rates.

As physical orientation, we think of the vertices as states or configurations of an open system. The transitions between states are possibly accompanied by exchanges of energy or particles with the environment. The

We assume that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}\textrm{d}t\,[\langle{\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda}(X_{t})\,|\,X_{0}=x\rangle_{\lambda}-\langle\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}\rangle^{s}_{\lambda}]

For the rest of the paper we can thus concentrate on the boundedness of

V

In the illustrations of heat capacities in the literature so far, it was already observed how their behavior as function of temperature may inform us about dynamical aspects as well. The main change from equilibrium is indeed that nonequilibrium heat capacities are able to pick up the dependence of the relaxation of heat and dissipated power on temperature and other parameters. The low-temperature behavior of heat capacities is therefore not only or no longer only informing us about static fluctuations but information about

enters as well, which is naturally expressed as a weighted graph-property. That is seen most spectacularly in the behavior of heat capacities in the immediate neighborhood of absolute zero. As we will prove and illustrate, all states must remain “relatively well-connected,” which is a dynamical condition. Even when the invariant measure at zero temperature is concentrating on one unique state, the heat capacity can still be different from zero (and even diverge). That happens at parameter values where the relaxation behavior of the heat gets pathological; we can speak here of a localization-phenomenon where there is a local delay in the relaxation to the stationary dissipation. Such “accessibility” and “no-delay” can be captured more precisely by the graphical elements that we next introduce, and will be illustrated via examples also in Appendix

V.1
Quasistatic analysis

Note that always
\sum_{x}L^{\dagger}\rho(x)=\sum_{x}L1(x)\,\rho(x)=0
because
L1(x)=0
. Hence, when
L^{\dagger}\rho=\nu
, then
\sum_{x}\nu(x)=0
must be true necessarily. It means that the (nonexisting) inverse
1/L^{\dagger}
can only be defined, if at all, on those
\nu
such that
\sum_{x}\nu(x)=0
. It implies

(V.1)

\sum_{x}\int_{0}^{\infty}\textrm{d}t\,e^{tL}[f(x)-\langle f\rangle^{s}]\,\nu(x)=\sum_{x}\int_{0}^{\infty}\textrm{d}t\,e^{tL}f(x)\,\nu(x).

Therefore, we can actually define
(1/L^{\dagger})\,\nu
via the requirement that for all functions
f
,

(V.2)

\sum_{x}f(x)\,(\frac{1}{L^{\dagger}}\nu)(x)=-\sum_{x}\int_{0}^{\infty}\textrm{d}t\,e^{tL}[f(x)-\langle f\rangle^{s}]\,\nu(x)

or

(V.3)

\frac{1}{L^{\dagger}}\nu(x)=-\sum_{y}\int_{0}^{\infty}\textrm{d}t\,\big[\text{Prob}[X_{t}=x|X_{0}=y]-\rho^{s}(x)\big]\,\nu(y)

which is indeed well-defined (and we do not bother introducing new notation for that restricted inverse).

We want to solve the time-dependent master equation perturbatively,

(V.4)

\rho^{\varepsilon}_{t}=\rho^{s}_{\lambda(\varepsilon t)}+\varepsilon\,F_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}+O(\varepsilon^{2})

as everything is smooth around
\varepsilon=0
. Since time gets rescaled by
\varepsilon
we know automatically that
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\,F_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}=O(\varepsilon)
. On the other hand,

(V.5)

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\,F_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\,[\rho^{\varepsilon}_{t}-\rho^{s}_{\lambda(\varepsilon t)}]=L^{\dagger}_{\lambda(\varepsilon t)}F_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}-\varepsilon\,\dot{\lambda}(\varepsilon t)\cdot\nabla_{\lambda}\rho^{s}_{\lambda(\varepsilon t)}.

Hence,

(V.6)

F_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}=\dot{\lambda}(\varepsilon t)\cdot\frac{1}{L^{\dagger}_{\lambda}}\nabla_{\lambda}\rho^{s}_{\lambda}.

As a consequence,

(V.7)

\int_{0}^{1/\varepsilon}(\rho^{\varepsilon}_{t}-\rho^{s}_{\lambda(\varepsilon t)})\textrm{d}t=\int_{\Gamma}\textrm{d}\lambda\cdot\frac{1}{L_{\lambda}^{\dagger}}\nabla_{\lambda}\rho^{s}+O(\varepsilon)

where the integral on the right-hand side is purely geometrical, invariant under a reparametrization of time
t\mapsto\gamma(t)
for a smooth function
\gamma
with
\gamma^{\prime}(t)>0
.

Suppose we have a function
f_{\lambda}
on
\mathcal{V}
. We can always write
f_{\lambda}=-L_{\lambda}V_{\lambda}+\langle f_{\lambda}\rangle^{s}_{\lambda}
. Define the excess

f_{\lambda}^{\text{exc}}:=f_{\lambda}-\langle f_{\lambda}\rangle^{s}_{\lambda}
, with time-integral

(V.8)

I_{\varepsilon}:=\int_{0}^{1/\varepsilon}f^{\text{exc}}_{\lambda(\varepsilon t)}(x_{t})\,\textrm{d}t.

In the case of
f_{\lambda}={\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda}
, from (
II.7
) we have
Q_{\varepsilon}=\langle I_{\varepsilon}\rangle^{\varepsilon}
.

Proof of Proposition
II.1
.

From (
V.7
),

(V.9)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\langle I_{\varepsilon}\rangle^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{0}^{1/\varepsilon}\textrm{d}t\,\sum_{x}\big(f_{\lambda(\varepsilon t)}(x)\rho^{\varepsilon}_{t}(x)-f_{\lambda(\varepsilon t)}(x)\rho^{s}_{\lambda(\varepsilon t)}(x)\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\textrm{d}\lambda\cdot\sum_{x}f_{\lambda}(x)\,\frac{1}{L_{\lambda}^{\dagger}}\nabla_{\lambda}\rho^{s}_{\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int\textrm{d}\lambda\cdot\sum_{x}V_{\lambda}(x)\,\nabla_{\lambda}\rho^{s}_{\lambda}(x)

(V.10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int\textrm{d}\lambda\cdot\left[\nabla_{\lambda}\sum_{x}V_{\lambda}(x)\,\rho^{s}_{\lambda}(x)-\sum_{x}\rho^{s}_{\lambda}(x)\nabla_{\lambda}V_{\lambda}(x)\right]

and

(V.11)

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\Big\langle\int_{0}^{1/\varepsilon}f^{\text{exc}}_{\lambda(\varepsilon t)}(x_{t})\,\textrm{d}t\Big\rangle^{\varepsilon}=\langle V_{a}\rangle_{a}-\langle V_{b}\rangle_{b}+\int\textrm{d}\lambda\cdot\langle\nabla_{\lambda}V_{\lambda}\rangle_{\lambda}

when

are respectively the initial and final points in the protocol. But,

so that we end up with

which indeed means that the left-hand side is geometrical and in the case

, that identifies the thermal-response coefficient

as wished.
∎

∎

Now we can finish the proof of the uniform boundedness of the quasipotential.

Proposition V.5
.

Given Condition 2, the quasipotential
V_{\lambda}=V
of (

) is uniformly bounded in
\beta\uparrow\infty
.

Proof.

From Proposition
V.4
and by the finiteness of the graph

, there exists

such that

\sum_{\bar{e}\in\mathcal{E}}|V_{\lambda}(\bar{e})|\leq B
, uniformly in
\lambda
for

, for any

there exist configurations

and

such that

. Therefore, for any

,

V_{\lambda}(x)\leq V_{\lambda}(x)-V_{\lambda}(x_{0})\leq\sum_{\bar{e}:\,x_{0}\rightarrow x}|V_{\lambda}(\bar{e})|\leq B

where the sum is over an arbitrary path in

and

. Analogously,

That proves the statement.

∎

VI
Summary and conclusion

We have considered nonequilibrium Markov jump processes on a finite connected graph under the interpretation of local detailed balance. The main object is the
excess heat

Q_{\varepsilon}
which is computed in the quasistatic limit
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
. We prove it gives rise to an expression in terms of the quasipotential
V_{\lambda}
defined in (
II.4
): see (
II.11
),

(VI.1)

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}Q_{\varepsilon}=\int\textrm{d}\lambda\cdot\sum_{x}V_{\lambda}(x)\,\nabla_{\lambda}\rho^{s}_{\lambda}(x).

where
\rho^{s}_{\lambda}
is the unique stationary distribution.

Our main result is the formulation of sufficient conditions for that quasistatic excess heat (
VI.1
) to vanish as the temperature goes to zero. First, the low-temperature asymptotics of the stationary distribution ensures that
\nabla_{\lambda}\rho^{s}_{\lambda}(x)\rightarrow 0
. Secondly, we need the boundedness of the quasipotential
V_{\lambda}
, uniformly in
\beta\uparrow\infty
. For that boundedness, it suffices to consider the differences of
V_{\lambda}
over an edge. The result can be inferred by explicit inspection, where the condition arises more specifically from the analysis of tree-loop and tree-loop-tree contributions at low temperatures. The main idea is however contained in the matrix-forest theorem, which we discuss in Appendix
A
. It gives a graphical representation of the quasipotential, and it allows the evaluation of the zero-temperature limit.

As a conclusion, a proof is obtained for the validity of the Nernst postulate to be extended to nonequilibrium Markov jump processes.

\displaystyle V_{\text{loop}}(\overline{e})=\frac{1}{W}\,\sum_{H\in\mathcal{H}_{e}}\,\sum_{\overline{H}\in O(H^{(e)})}\sigma_{H}(\overline{e})\,q(\overline{\ell})\,w(\overline{H}),\quad e\in\text{trees}

where
\mathcal{H}_{e}
is a set of all spanning tree-loops including the edge
e
in a tree. If
H\in\mathcal{H}_{e}
, then
H^{(e)}
denotes a tree-loop-tree which is created by removing the edge
e
from the tree-loop
H
.
O(H^{(e)})
is the set of all oriented tree-loop-trees made by giving all possible orientations to
H^{(e)}
(remember Definition
III.2
).
Finally, the difference of the quasipotential over a directed edge
\overline{e}
is

\displaystyle V(\overline{e})

\displaystyle=V_{\text{tree}}(\overline{e})+V_{\text{loop}}(\overline{e})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{W}\;\sum_{u\in\mathcal{V}}\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}w(T_{u})\,q_{T}(x\rightarrow y)

(A.37)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{W}\,\sum_{H\in\mathcal{H}_{e}}\,\sum_{\overline{H}\in O(H^{(e)})}\sigma_{H}(\overline{e})\,q(\overline{\ell})\,w(\overline{H}),\quad e\in\text{trees}.

Appendix B
Proof of Lemma
V.2

Lemma B.1
.

For any directed edge
\overline{e}=(x_{e},y_{e})
, the average of
\mathcal{P}(x_{e})=\sum_{y_{e}}k(\overline{e})q(\overline{e})
is

(B.1)

\left\langle\mathcal{P}\right\rangle=\sum_{x_{e}}\sum_{y_{e}}k(\overline{e})q(\overline{e})\rho(x_{e})=\frac{1}{W}\sum_{\overline{H}\in O(\mathcal{H})}w(\overline{H})q(\overline{\ell})

where
w(\overline{H})=\prod_{(z,z^{\prime})\in\overline{H}}k(z,z^{\prime})
.

Proof.

Take a rooted spanning tree
T_{x_{e}}
and the edge
\overline{e}=(x_{e},y_{e})
. We consider two cases:

First case,
if the edge
\overline{e}^{\prime}=(y_{e},x_{e})\in T_{x_{e}}
, then

(B.2)

k(\overline{e})w(T_{x_{e}})q(\overline{e})=k(\overline{e}^{\prime})w(T_{y_{e}})q(\overline{e})=-k(\overline{e}^{\prime})w(T_{y_{e}})q(\overline{e}^{\prime}).

From here we can conclude that

(B.3)

\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\sum_{\overline{e},\overline{e}^{\prime}\in T_{x_{e}}}k(\overline{e})w(T_{x_{e}})q(\overline{e})=0.

Second case,
if the edge
\overline{e}^{\prime}=(y_{e},x_{e})\notin T_{x_{e}}
, then

k(\overline{e})w(T_{x_{e}})

is the same as the weight of a graphical object made by adding the edge
\overline{e}
to the rooted tree
T_{x_{e}}
. That graphical object is a tree-loop. As a consequence,

(B.4)

k(\overline{e})w(T_{x_{e}})q(\overline{e})=w(\overline{H})q(\overline{e})

where the loop of
\overline{H}
is in the same direction as
\overline{e}
. Summing over the edges in loop
\overline{\ell}
(in clockwise or counter clockwise direction) in the underlying graph
G
gives

(B.5)

\sum_{\overline{e}\in\overline{\ell}}k(\overline{e})w(T_{x_{e}})q(\overline{e})=w(\overline{H}_{\ell})q(\overline{\ell}).

Finally, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{x_{e}}\sum_{y_{e}}k(\overline{e})q(\overline{e})\rho(x_{e})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{W}\sum_{x_{e}}\sum_{y_{e}}\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}k(\overline{e})q(\overline{e})w(T_{x_{e}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{W}\sum_{\overline{e}}\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}k(\overline{e})q(\overline{e})w(T_{x_{e}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{W}\sum_{\overline{e}}\sum_{T\ni\overline{e}^{\prime}}k(\overline{e})q(\overline{e})w(T_{x_{e}})+\sum_{\overline{e}}\sum_{T\not\owns\overline{e}^{\prime}}k(\overline{e})q(\overline{e})w(T_{x_{e}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{W}\sum_{\ell}w(\overline{H}_{\ell})q(\overline{\ell})

(B.6)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{W}\sum_{\overline{H}\in O(\mathcal{H})}w(\overline{H})q(\overline{\ell}).

∎
