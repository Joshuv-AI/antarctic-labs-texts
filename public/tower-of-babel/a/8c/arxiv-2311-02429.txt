Backward Uniqueness for 3D Navier-Stokes Equations with Non-trivial Final Data and Applications
Zhen Lei, Zhaojie Yang, Cheng Yuan

Abstract
Presented is a backward uniqueness result of bounded mild solutions of 3D Navier-Stokes Equations in the whole space with non-trivial final data. A direct consequence is that a solution must be axi-symmetric in [ 0 , T ] [0,T] if it is so at time T T . The proof is based on a new weighted estimate which enables to treat terms involving Calderon-Zygmund operators. The new weighted estimate is expected to have certain applications in control theory when classical Carleman-type inequality is not applicable.



1
Introduction

In this paper, we consider the backward uniqueness problem for the following 3D Navier-Stokes equations in
\mathbb{R}^{3}\times[0,T]
:

\begin{cases}&\partial_{t}u-\Delta u+(u\cdot\nabla)u+\nabla p=f,\\
&\nabla\cdot u=0,\\
&u|_{t=T}=g(x),\end{cases}

(1.1)

where
u
denotes the velocity field,
p
is the pressure, and
f
is the forcing term. Here, we are interested in bounded mild solutions. Hence, it is assumed that the final data
g
is a bounded function, and the existence of bounded mild solution
u
is a known fact. More precisely, one has

p=\mathcal{K}:(u\otimes u-F),

and

u(t)=e^{(t-s)\Delta}u(s)+\int_{s}^{t}e^{(t-\tau)\Delta}\mathbb{P}\big[\nabla\cdot(u\otimes u)(\cdot,\tau)+f(\cdot,\tau)\big]d\tau,\quad 0\leq s\leq t\leq T,

with
f=\nabla\cdot F
, where
\mathcal{K}=(-\Delta)^{-1}\nabla^{2}
is the standard Riesz operator and
\mathbb{P}
is the Leray projector. For more details, see Section 2.

The backward uniqueness (BU) problem asks whether there are two different bounded mild solutions
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
to (
1.1
) with the same final data
g
at some time
T\in(0,+\infty)
. In this article we are interested in the case of non-trivial final data
g
. We say that the (BU) property holds if
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
must coincide.

1.1
Background

The (BU) property is closely connected to
internal control theory. In fact, if
u_{1},u_{2}
are two different bounded mild solutions to (
1.1
), denote
u:=u_{2}-u_{1}
. It is clear that
u
satisfies the following modified Navier-Stokes equation with internal control:

\begin{cases}&\partial_{t}u+u\cdot\nabla u-\Delta u+\nabla\pi=-u_{1}\cdot\nabla u-u\cdot\nabla u_{1},\\
&\nabla\cdot u=0,\\
&u|_{t=t_{0}}=u_{0}.\end{cases}

(1.2)

Indeed, by forgetting about the relation between
u
and
u_{1}
, one can think of
u_{1}
as an internal control function.
That the (BU) property holds means that under suitable constraints on the control functions
u_{1}
, the
null controllability
for (
1.2
) is impossible, that is, for any nontrivial data
u(t_{0})
, (
1.2
) can not achieve the trivial state
u(\cdot,T)\equiv 0
for any
T>t_{0}
.

Besides, the study of backward uniqueness has its own significance in the PDE’s field, especially in the regularity theory of parabolic equations. For instance, the regularity theory of Navier-Stokes equations in critical spaces (
[
11
]
,
[
12
]
,
[
22
]
,
[
19
]
,
[
20
]
), regularity of harmonic maps
[
23
]
, and blow up for semi-linear heat equation
[
18
]
.

In the literature, the backward uniqueness & unique continuation properties of linear parabolic equations have been widely considered. Lin
[
17
]
proved the strong unique continuation for linear parabolic equations of the form
\partial_{t}u-\Delta u+V(x)u=0
, where time-independent coefficients
V(x)\in L_{x}^{(n+1)/2}
. See also
[
4
]
,
[
7
]
for results on time-dependent potentials. For equations of the form
\partial_{t}u-\Delta u+a(t,x)u+b(t,x)\cdot\nabla u=0
, Poon
[
21
]
proved that if
a,b
are bounded, the bounded solution
u
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
is identically zero if it vanishes of infinite order at any point.
See also
[
3
]
for improved results allowing a suitable growth condition on
u
and
[
1
]
,
[
5
]
for analogous results on divergence-form backward operator
P=\partial_{t}+\partial_{i}(a^{ij}(x,t)\partial_{j})
.

In the remarkable work
[
11
]
, Escauriaza, Seregin and Sverák proved the global regularity of
L^{\infty}L^{3}
solutions of (
1.1
) with null force, where backward uniqueness arguments (proved in
[
6
]
) played a crucial role. Indeed, they proved that if a function
u
satisfies
|\Delta u+\partial_{t}u|\lesssim(|u|+|\nabla u|)
and a certain growth condition in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R}\times[0,T]:=Q_{R,T}
, then
u(x,0)\equiv 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R}
implies
u(x,t)\equiv 0
in
Q_{R,T}
. The result can be applied to prove the vanishing of the vorticity
\omega\triangleq\nabla\times u
in
Q_{R,T}
for large
R
provided
\omega
vanishes at
t=T
. Their proof relies on some Carleman-type inequalities, which have also been widely used in control theory. See also
[
16
]
for the use of Carleman estimates proving backward uniqueness of parabolic equations in a cone and
[
22
]
,
[
19
]
,
[
20
]
for proving quantitative regularity and blowup criterion for Navier-Stokes equations.

In addition to the important role of (BU) property played in the regularity theory of Navier-Stokes Equations, we point out that the (BU) property with
non-trivial final data
studied in this article has its own obvious significance. For instance, one may ask the following question:

(Q)
Suppose that
f=0
and
u(t,x)
is a bounded mild solution to the Navier-Stokes equations with
u(T,x)
being an axi-symmetric vector without swirl. Must
u(t,x)
be axi-symmetric and swirl-free?

To answer this question, one has to first answer whether
u
is axi-symmetric and then apply standard backward uniqueness result after various surgeries. The proof will be presented after stating the main Theorem
1.1
. Let us emphasis that a crucial difference from the trivial final data case is that one has to treat terms involving Calderon-Zygmund operators. Since the classical Carleman type inequality involves the exponential weight which does not belong to
\mathcal{A}_{p}
class for any
p\in[1,\infty]
, we need find new weight to overcome this essential difficulty. The new weighted estimate is expected to have certain applications in control theory when classical Carleman-type inequality is not applicable.

1.2
Main Result

In this paper, we will prove the backward uniqueness of forced Navier-Stokes equations (
1.1
) for bounded mild solutions, with nontrivial final data. Our main result is the following theorem:

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume
u_{1}(x,t),u_{2}(x,t)
are two bounded mild solutions of (
1.1
), such that
\nabla\times u_{1}
and
\nabla\times u_{2}
are also bounded. If
u_{1}(x,T)=u_{2}(x,T)
for some
T>0
, then
(u_{1},\nabla p_{1})
must coincide with
(u_{2},\nabla p_{2})
at any time
0\leq t\leq T
.

Remark 1.1
.

Our result only requires the vorticity to be bounded, this assumption is weaker than several classical results, such as in
[
21
]
. It is clear that the boundedness condition on vorticity can be removed if
f
vanishes or has certain regularity. For instance, if
f=\nabla\cdot F
with
F\in L^{\infty}_{t}W^{1,p}_{x}
for
3<p\leq\infty
, then one can easily derive that
u_{1},u_{2}\in\mathbf{C}\big((0,T],W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\big)
(see
[
13
]
).

Note that the
mild
assumption on the solutions in Theorem
1.1
is necessary. In view of the following simple counter-example, one can not expect that the (BU) properties holds true for general
L^{\infty}
solutions:

Example 1.2
.

Let
u(x,t)=(h(t),0,0)
, with
h(T)=0,h^{\prime}(T)=0
, and
h(t)\not\equiv 0
for
t\neq T
. Let
p(t,x)=-h^{\prime}(t)x_{1}
, then
(u,p)
is a classical solution of (
1.1
) vanishing at
t=T
, and is obviously nontrivial.

The backward uniqueness holds for a large class of solutions of (
1.1
) such that the Ladyzhenskaya–Prodi–Serrin regularity criteria holds. Indeed, the boundedness of
u
is a (non-trivial) consequence of these conditions stated below. For instance, see
[
2
]
,
[
11
]
, or
[
15
]
for proofs.

Corollary 1.3
.

Assume
u(x,t),v(x,t)
are mild solutions of (
1.1
) with
f=0
such that
u(x,t),v(x,t)\in L^{p}\big([0,T];L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\big)
for
2/p+3/q=1,3\leq q\leq\infty
Then,
u(x,t)
must coincide with
v(x,t)
at any time
0\leq t\leq T
, provided that
u(x,T)\equiv v(x,T)
for some
T>0
.

Remark 1.2
.

The regularity assumptions on
u,v
and
f
in Corollary
1.3
can be replaced by various functional frameworks. See
[
15
]
for a comprehensive survey on the regularity criteria of (
1.1
).

Now let us first assume the validity of Theorem
1.1
and present the answer of
(Q)
.

Proof.

First of all, we show that
u(t,x)
is also axi-symmetric for all
t\in[0,T]
. Recall that a vector
u
is called axi-symmetric if

u(x)=u^{r}e_{r}+u^{\theta}e_{\theta}+u^{z}e_{z},

where
u^{r},u^{\theta},u^{z}
are all functions of
(r,z)
but not depend on
\theta
, with
r=\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}}
,
\theta=\arctan\frac{x_{2}}{x_{1}}
, and
z=x_{3}
.
Then any rotation by
\phi
degrees around z-axis can be described by the matrix

S_{\phi}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\phi&\sin\phi&0\\
-\sin\phi&\cos\phi&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.

It is easy to check that the Navier-Stokes equation is invariant under axi-symmetric transformation. Hence if
u(x,t)
is a solution of the N-S equation, then so does
u_{\phi}:=S_{\phi}u(S_{-\phi}x,t)
. Since
u(x,T)
is an axi-symmetric vector, it holds that

S_{\phi}u(S_{-\phi}x,T)=u(x,T),

or equivalently
u_{\phi}(x,T)=u(x,T)
for any
\phi\in[0,2\pi]
. By Theorem
1.1
, we have
u_{\phi}(x,t)=u(x,t)
for any
t<T
, which implies that
u(x,t)
is axi-symmetric for all
\phi\in[0,2\pi]
and
t<T
.

Next, denote
G=\frac{u^{\theta}}{r}
. It is clear that
G
is governed by (
[
10
,
9
]
)

G_{t}-\Delta G-\frac{2}{r}\partial_{r}G+b\cdot\nabla G+\frac{2u^{r}}{r}G=0,

with
b=u^{r}e_{r}+u^{z}e_{z}.
Since here we are dealing with bounded mild axi-symmetric solutions, it is clear that
b
and
\frac{u^{r}}{r}
are bounded.
Similarly as in
[
9
,
14
]
, one may look on the above equation as a five-dimensional one and derive that

|G_{t}-\Delta_{5}G|\leq C(|G|+|\nabla G|).

Here

\Delta_{5}=\Delta+\frac{2}{r}\partial_{r},\quad\Delta=\partial_{r}^{2}+\frac{1}{r}\partial_{r}+\partial_{z}^{2}.

Then one may apply classical backward uniqueness result (
[
21
]
) to derive that
G\equiv 0
.

Hence, the answer of (Q) is yes.
∎

1.3
Main Ingredients

Now let us explain the main difficulties of this paper and the strategies used to overcome them.

(a) Invalidity of Carleman estimates.
We emphasize that those above-mentioned literature do not need to treat terms involving the Calderon-Zygmund operator and hence Carleman-type inequality can be applied. Since exponential-type weights appeared in Carleman type estimates do not belong to any
\mathcal{A}_{p}
-class for
1<p<\infty
, one can not apply Carleman type estimates to treat the terms involving Calderon-Zygmund operator. Unfortunately, for (
1.1
) when the final data is nontrivial, the Calderon-Zygmund operator will inevitably comes in.

To overcome it, we establish a new weighted estimate (see Theorem
3.1
). Compared with the Carleman estimate in
[
6
]
, we use the weights with polynomial spatial decay. This idea behind is the following: the Carleman inequality in
[
6
]
is applied to the heat equation whose kernel has exponential decay. Hence, weights with exponential decay appear naturally in
[
6
]
in view of the heat kernel. In the current setting, due to the presence of the Stokes operator, whose corresponding semi-group only enjoys an algebraic decay rate, it is natural to choose a polynomial type weight
\langle x\rangle^{-k}
.

(b) Invalidity of
\mathcal{A}_{p}
weights.
Since the solutions we dealt with here are bounded mild solutions and may not have any decay when
x
tends to infinity, the spatial weight
\langle x\rangle^{-k}
in our proof must be integrable over the whole space, which implies
k>3
. On the other hand, due to the appearance of the pressure term, which produces non-local effects, we have to control terms like

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\langle x\rangle^{-k}|\mathcal{R}f|^{2}dx\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\langle x\rangle^{-k}|f|^{2}dx,

(1.3)

where
\mathcal{R}
is a Calderon-Zygmund operator. Unfortunately, when
k>3
,
\langle x\rangle^{-k}
does not belong to any
\mathcal{A}_{p}
class (see
[
8
]
), which causes extra technical difficulties in estimating (
1.3
) . To overcome it, we exploit the inherent divergence structure in nonlinear terms and use elementary integration by parts techniques, see Lemma
4.1
.

2
Preliminary

In this section, we will give a brief definition of
L^{\infty}
mild solutions of (
1.1
).

Definition 2.1
.

u(t)
is defined to be an
L^{\infty}
mild solution of the Cauchy problem (
1.1
), if
u\in L^{\infty}_{t,x}
satisfies (
1.1
) in the distributional sense and the following integral equation

u(t)=e^{(t-s)\Delta}u(s)+\int_{s}^{t}e^{(t-\tau)\Delta}\mathbb{P}\big[\nabla\cdot(u\otimes u)(\cdot,\tau)+f(\cdot,\tau)\big]d\tau,\quad 0\leq s\leq t\leq T.

(2.1)

Remark 2.1
.

Here
e^{t\Delta}
is the standard heat semigroup, and
\mathbb{P}
denotes the Leray projection on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Cleary,
\mathbb{P}
is a Calderon-Zugmund type singular integral operator and can be formally written by

\mathbb{P}=I+\nabla(-\Delta)^{-1}(\nabla\cdot).

Remark 2.2
.

From the definition of mild solution (
2.1
), the pressure term can be recovered by

\nabla p=\nabla(-\Delta)^{-1}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}u^{i}u^{j}-\nabla(-\Delta)^{-1}(\nabla\cdot f),

therefore we are able to estimate the pressure in terms of the velocity filed.

Remark 2.3
.

It is clear that (for instance, see
[
13
]
) (
2.1
) can be written in a convolution form. Explicitly,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)\Delta}\mathbb{P}\big[(u\cdot\nabla)u(\cdot,s)\big]ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\Big(\nabla O(x-y,t-s)::u\otimes u(y,s)\Big)dyds,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)\Delta}\mathbb{P}(f)(\cdot,s)ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}O(x-y,t-s)\cdot f(y,s)dyds,

(2.3)

where the kernel satisfies following estimates:

|\nabla^{k}O(x,t)|\lesssim(|x|+\sqrt{t})^{-3-|k|},

(2.4)

which implies (
2.1
) is well defined for
u\in L^{\infty}_{t,x}.

3
Weighted
L^{2}
Estimates

In this section, we will prove the following weighted inequality.

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume
u(x,t)\in C_{c}^{\infty}\big(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(T_{-},T^{+})\big)
, where
[T_{-},T^{+}]\subset(0,\infty)
,
T^{+}
is sufficiently small. Then for any large number
a>0
and any
k\geq 0
, the following inequality holds:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(T_{-},T^{+})}h(t)^{-(2a+1)}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}\Big((a+1)u^{2}+|\nabla u|^{2}\Big)dxdt

\displaystyle\quad\quad\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(T_{-},T^{+})}h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}|\partial_{t}u+\Delta u|^{2}dxdt,

(3.1)

where
h(t)=te^{-t}
.

Remark 3.1
.

The above inequality is a type of (time-space) weighted
L^{2}
estimate. The corresponding weight in Theorem
3.1
satisfies the following facts:

(i) The temporal weight
h(t)
inspired from
[
6
]
is sufficiently large when time is small enough.

(ii) For
k>\frac{3}{2}
, the spatial weight is integrable on the whole space.

These two facts are crucial in our proof.

To prove Theorem
3.1
, we need the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Assume
A
is a densely defined operator on a real Hilbert space. Then for each
x\in\mathcal{D}(A^{2})
,the following holds,

\|Ax\|^{2}\geqslant\langle[J,K]x,x\rangle,

(3.2)

where
J=\frac{A+A^{t}}{2}
is the symmetric part of
A
, and
K=\frac{A-A^{t}}{2}
is the skew symmetric part of
A
,
[J,K]=JK-KJ
.

Proof.

By direct computation,

\displaystyle\|Ax\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|(J+K)x\|^{2}=\langle(J+K)x,(J+K)x\rangle

\displaystyle=\|Jx\|^{2}+\|Kx\|^{2}+\langle Jx,Kx\rangle+\langle Kx,Jx\rangle

\displaystyle\geqslant\langle Jx,Kx\rangle+\langle Kx,Jx\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle[J,K]x,x\rangle.

∎

Now we are ready to prove Theorem
3.1
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

We consider

\displaystyle L:

\displaystyle=h(t)^{-(a+1)}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}(\partial_{t}+\Delta)h^{a+1}(t)(1+|x|^{2})^{k}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}+\Delta+(a+1)\frac{h^{\prime}(t)}{h(t)}+\frac{4kx}{1+|x|^{2}}\cdot\nabla+\frac{2kn}{1+|x|^{2}}+\frac{4k(k-1)|x|^{2}}{(1+|x|^{2})^{2}}.

Let
A=tL
. Then we can decompose
A
as:

\displaystyle A=

\displaystyle t\partial_{t}+t\Delta+(a+1)(1-t)+\frac{4ktx}{1+|x|^{2}}\cdot\nabla+\frac{2knt}{1+|x|^{2}}+\frac{4k(k-1)t|x|^{2}}{(1+|x|^{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigg(t\Delta+\frac{4k^{2}t|x|^{2}}{(1+|x|^{2})^{2}}+(a+1)(1-t)-\frac{1}{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle+\bigg(t\partial_{t}+\frac{4ktx}{1+|x|^{2}}\cdot\nabla+2kt\Big(\frac{n}{1+|x|^{2}}-\frac{2|x|^{2}}{(1+|x|^{2})^{2}}\Big)+\frac{1}{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle:J+K,

where
J
is symmetric and
K
is skew-symmetric.
Furthermore, the commutator of
J,K
can be computed as follows:

\displaystyle[J,K]=

\displaystyle(a+1)t-t\Delta-\frac{4k^{2}t|x|^{2}}{(1+|x|^{2})^{2}}+\frac{8kt^{2}}{1+|x|^{2}}\Delta

\displaystyle-\frac{16kt^{2}}{(1+|x|^{2})^{2}}\Big[(x\otimes x)::\nabla^{2}\Big]+t^{2}V_{1}(x)x\cdot\nabla+t^{2}V_{2}(x),

where

\displaystyle V_{1}(x)=\frac{64k|x|^{2}}{(1+|x|^{2})^{3}}-\frac{16kn+32k}{(1+|x|^{2})^{2}},

\displaystyle V_{2}(x)=-\frac{(4n+8)kn}{(1+|x|^{2})^{2}}+\frac{(32kn+64k-32k^{3})|x|^{2}}{(1+|x|^{2})^{3}}+\frac{(64k^{3}-96k)|x|^{4}}{(1+|x|^{2})^{4}}.

Let
v(x,t)=h(t)^{-(a+1)}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}u(x,t)
, and applying lemma
3.2
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(T_{-},T^{+})}h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-2k}|\partial_{t}u+\Delta u|^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle\approx\|Av\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\geqslant\langle[J,K]v,v\rangle

\displaystyle=:\sum_{j=1}^{6}I_{j},

where

\displaystyle I_{1}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(T_{-},T^{+})}(a+1)tv^{2}dxdt,

\displaystyle I_{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(T_{-},T^{+})}-tv\Delta vdxdt,

\displaystyle I_{3}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(T_{-},T^{+})}-\frac{4k^{2}t|x|^{2}}{(1+|x|^{2})^{2}}v^{2}dxdt,

\displaystyle I_{4}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(T_{-},T^{+})}\frac{8kt^{2}}{1+|x|^{2}}v\bigg(\Delta v-\Big[\frac{2(x\otimes x)}{1+|x|^{2}}::\nabla^{2}\Big]v\bigg)dxdt,

\displaystyle I_{5}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(T_{-},T^{+})}t^{2}V_{1}(x)v(x\cdot\nabla)vdxdt,

\displaystyle I_{6}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(T_{-},T^{+})}t^{2}V_{2}(x)v^{2}dxdt.

It is easy to check that

\displaystyle I_{1}\gtrsim(a+1)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(T_{-},T^{+})}h(t)^{-(2a+1)}(1+|x|^{2})^{-2k}|u|^{2}dxdt;

\displaystyle I_{3}\gtrsim-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(T_{-},T^{+})}h(t)^{-(2a+1)}(1+|x|^{2})^{-2k}|u|^{2}dxdt.

For
I_{2}
, integrating by parts, we have

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(T_{-},T^{+})}h(t)^{-(2a+1)}\bigg|\nabla\Big((1+|x|^{2})^{-k}u\Big)\bigg|^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle\gtrsim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(T_{-},T^{+})}h(t)^{-(2a+1)}(1+|x|^{2})^{-2k}|\nabla u|^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle\quad-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(T_{-},T^{+})}h(t)^{-(2a+1)}(1+|x|^{2})^{-2k}|u|^{2}dxdt.

For
I_{4}
, integrating by parts again, using the fact that
T^{+}
is small, we have

\displaystyle|I_{4}|

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(T_{-},T^{+})}h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-1}\bigg|\nabla\Big((1+|x|^{2})^{-k}u\Big)\bigg|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(T_{-},T^{+})}h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-2k}|\nabla u|^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(T_{-},T^{+})}h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-2k}|u|^{2}dxdt,

Similarly, integrating by parts, and applying Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, we can also obtain

|I_{5}+I_{6}|\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(T_{-},T^{+})}h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-2k}|\nabla u|^{2}dxdt+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(T_{-},T^{+})}h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-2k}|u|^{2}dxdt.

Combining the above all estimates, using the fact that
a
is large enough and
T^{+}
is small enough, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(T_{-},T^{+})}h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-2k}|\partial_{t}u+\Delta u|^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle\quad\gtrsim(a+1)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(T_{-},T^{+})}h(t)^{-(2a+1)}(1+|x|^{2})^{-2k}|u|^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(T_{-},T^{+})}h(t)^{-(2a+1)}(1+|x|^{2})^{-2k}|\nabla u|^{2}dxdt.

∎

By approximating with compactly support smooth functions, it holds that

Corollary 3.3
.

Assume
u\in H_{0}^{1}\big((T_{-},T^{+});W^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\big)
, where
2\leq p\leq\infty
, then (
3.1
) still holds for
k>3\Big(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}\Big)
.

Proof.

Let
\eta
be a smooth spatial cut-off function such that
\eta\equiv 1
for
|x|\leq 1
, and
\eta\equiv 0
for
|x|\geq 2
. Let
\eta_{m}(x)=\eta(\frac{x}{m})
. Replace
u
by
\eta_{m}u
, and applying Theorem
3.1
we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(T_{-},T^{+})}h(t)^{-(2a+1)}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}\Big((a+1)u^{2}+|\nabla u|^{2}\Big)dxdt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(T_{-},T^{+})}h(t)^{-(2a+1)}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}\Big((a+1)(\eta_{m}u)^{2}+|\nabla(\eta_{m}u)|^{2}\Big)dxdt

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(T_{-},T^{+})}h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}|\partial_{t}\eta_{m}u+\Delta(\eta_{m}u)|^{2}dxdt.

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(T_{-},T^{+})}h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}|\eta_{m}|^{2}|\partial_{t}u+\Delta u|^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle\quad+\lim_{m\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(T_{-},T^{+})}h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}\big(|\Delta\eta_{m}u|^{2}+|\nabla\eta_{m}\nabla u|^{2}\big)dxdt

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(T_{-},T^{+})}h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}|\partial_{t}u+\Delta u|^{2}dxdt.

∎

4
Proof of the Main result

In this section, we will prove the main theorem of this paper.

Proof of Theorem 1.1.

Assume
u_{1},u_{2}
satisfy the assumption of Theorem 1.1. Here, as in section
3
, we choose
T_{-},T^{+}
sufficiently small. Let
u(x,t)=(u_{1}-u_{2})(x,T+T_{-}-t)
, then
u
satisfies the following equation:

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u+\Delta u+\mathbb{P}\nabla\cdot(u_{1}\otimes u+u\otimes u_{2})=0,\quad(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times[T_{-},T+T^{-}],\\
\nabla\cdot u=0,\\
u(\cdot,T_{-})=0.\end{cases}

(4.1)

Then by Section 3 in
[
13
]
,
u
is sufficiently smooth. Choose a temporal cut-off function
\chi\in C^{\infty}[T_{-},T^{+}]
such that
\chi(t)=1
, when
t\in[T_{-},T_{-}+\delta]
, and
\chi(t)\equiv 0
when
t\in[T_{-}+2\delta,T^{+}]
, where
3\delta<T^{+}-T_{-}
. For
\chi(t)u(x,t)
, applying Corollary
1.3
with
\frac{3}{2}<k<\frac{5}{2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\chi^{2}(t)h(t)^{-(2a+1)}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}\big((a+1)|u|^{2}+|\nabla u|^{2}\big)dxdt

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\chi^{2}(t)h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}|\partial_{t}u+\Delta u|^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\chi^{\prime}(t)|^{2}h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}|u|^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\chi^{2}(t)h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}\Big|\mathbb{P}\nabla\cdot(u_{1}\otimes u+u\otimes u_{2})\Big|^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\chi^{\prime}(t)|^{2}h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}|u|^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle A+B.

First, we estimate
A
. It turns out that
A
can be absorbed in the left-hand side. Clearly,

\displaystyle A\lesssim

\displaystyle\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\chi^{2}(t)h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}|u_{1}\cdot\nabla u+u\cdot\nabla u_{2}|^{2}dxdt

(4.2)

\displaystyle+\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\chi^{2}(t)h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}|\mathcal{R}\nabla\cdot(u_{1}\otimes u)|^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle+\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\chi^{2}(t)h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}|\mathcal{R}\nabla\cdot(u\otimes u_{2})|^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle A_{1}+A_{2}+A_{3},

where
\mathcal{R}=\nabla(-\Delta)^{-1}\nabla\cdot
is the zeroth-order singular integral operator. Note that
A_{1}
can be estimated directly,

\displaystyle A_{1}\lesssim

\displaystyle\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\chi^{2}(t)h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}|u_{1}|^{2}|\nabla u|^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle+\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\chi^{2}(t)h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}|u|^{2}|\nabla u_{2}|^{2}dxdt:=A_{11}+A_{12}.

Clearly,

A_{11}\lesssim\|u_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}^{2}\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\chi^{2}(t)h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}|\nabla u|^{2}dxdt.

To ensure
\nabla u_{2}
is not involved in the estimate of
A_{12}
, we have to rewrite the term
|\nabla u_{2}|^{2}
. Indeed,

\displaystyle A_{12}

\displaystyle=\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\chi^{2}(t)h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}|u|^{2}|\nabla u_{2}|^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle=\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\chi^{2}(t)h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}|u|^{2}\big(\Delta(|u_{2}|^{2})-u_{2}\cdot\Delta u_{2}\big)dxdt:=A_{13}+A_{14},

Firstly, we estimate the term
A_{14}
. Note that
-\Delta u_{2}=\nabla\times\nabla\times u_{2}
since
\nabla\cdot u_{2}=0
, integrating by parts, we have

\displaystyle A_{14}=

\displaystyle\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\chi^{2}(t)h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}|u|^{2}u_{2}\cdot(\nabla\times\nabla\times u_{2})dxdt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\chi^{2}(t)h(t)^{-2a}\nabla\times\big((1+|x|^{2})^{-k}|u|^{2}u_{2})\cdot\nabla\times u_{2}dxdt

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\chi^{2}(t)h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-(k+1/2)}|u|^{2}|u_{2}|\cdot|\nabla\times u_{2}|dxdt

\displaystyle+\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\chi^{2}(t)h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}|u|\cdot|\nabla u|\cdot|u_{2}|\cdot|\nabla\times u_{2}|dxdt

\displaystyle+\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\chi^{2}(t)h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}|u|^{2}|\nabla\times u_{2}|^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle(\|u_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}^{2}+\|\nabla\times u_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}^{2})\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\chi^{2}(t)h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}(|u|^{2}+|\nabla u|^{2})dxdt.

For
A_{13}
, integrating by parts again we have

\displaystyle A_{13}=

\displaystyle\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\chi^{2}(t)h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}|u|^{2}\big(\Delta(|u_{2}|^{2})\big)dxdt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\chi^{2}(t)h(t)^{-2a}\Big|\nabla\big((1+|x|^{2})^{-k}|u|^{2}\big)\Big|\cdot\Big|\nabla\big(|u_{2}|^{2}\big)\Big|dxdt

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\chi^{2}(t)h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-(k+1)}|u|^{2}|u_{2}|\cdot|\nabla u_{2}|dxdt

\displaystyle+\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\chi^{2}(t)h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}|u|\cdot|\nabla u|\cdot|u_{2}|\cdot|\nabla u_{2}|dxdt

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\epsilon\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\chi^{2}(t)h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}|u|^{2}|\nabla u_{2}|^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle+C_{\epsilon}\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\chi^{2}(t)h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}|u_{2}|^{2}(|u|^{2}+|\nabla u|^{2})dxdt

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\epsilon A_{12}+C_{\epsilon}\|u_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}^{2}\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\chi^{2}(t)h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}(|u|^{2}+|\nabla u|^{2})dxdt,

where
\epsilon
is sufficiently small and
C_{\epsilon}
is a constant only depends on
\epsilon
. Combining above computations, we have

\displaystyle A_{12}

\displaystyle\lesssim(\|u_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}^{2}+\|\nabla\times u_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}^{2})\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\chi^{2}(t)h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}(|u|^{2}+|\nabla u|^{2}).

To estimate
A_{2}
and
A_{3}
, we prove the following lemma:

Lemma 4.1
.

for
0\leq k<\frac{5}{2}
, the following inequality holds:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}|\mathcal{R}\nabla\cdot f|^{2}dx\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}\Big(|\nabla f|^{2}+|f|^{2}\Big)dx.

(4.3)

Remark 4.1
.

Note that for
k\geq\frac{3}{2}
,
(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}
does not belong to any
\mathcal{A}_{p}
class, however, the divergence structure enable us to improve the inequality to
k<\frac{5}{2}
, which enables
f\in L^{\infty}.

By the definition of
\mathcal{R}
and divergence, for
1\leq i,j,l\leq 3,
we define

\mathcal{R}_{ijl}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\frac{\xi_{i}\xi_{j}}{|\xi|^{2}}\mathcal{F}\partial_{x_{l}}

via Fourier transform. Then with a slightly abuse of notations, we shall only estimate

I_{ijl}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}|\mathcal{R}_{ijl}f|^{2}dx.

By the representation formula of homogeneous singular integral operator, for each
1\leq i,j\leq 3
, there exists a smooth function
\Omega_{ij}
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
with mean zero such that

\mathcal{R}_{ijl}f=\frac{\Omega(y/|y|)}{|y|^{3}}*\partial_{x_{l}}f,

where the convolution is defined in a limit sense.
Choosing
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(B_{2})
and
\varphi\equiv 1
in
B_{1}
, and integral by parts, we can decompose
\mathcal{R}_{ijl}
into

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{ijl}f

\displaystyle=\bigg[\frac{\Omega(y/|y|)}{|y|^{3}}\varphi(y)\bigg]*\partial_{x_{k}}f-\partial_{y_{l}}\bigg[\frac{\Omega(y/|y|)}{|y|^{3}}(1-\varphi(y))\bigg]*f

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{R}_{ijl1}f+\mathcal{R}_{ijl2}f.

The virtue of this decomposition is that
\mathcal{R}_{ijl1}
is a localized operator while the kernal of
\mathcal{R}_{ijl2}
is integrable. Hence we compute

\displaystyle I_{ijl}=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}\big|\mathcal{R}_{ijl1}f+\mathcal{R}_{ijl2}f\big|^{2}dx

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}\bigg|\mathcal{R}_{ijl1}f\bigg|^{2}dx

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}\bigg|\mathcal{R}_{ijl2}f\bigg|^{2}dx

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle I_{ijl1}+I_{ijl2}.

We will estimate
I_{ijl1},I_{ijl2}
separately.

Estimates of
I_{ijl1}
.
First, note that

\displaystyle(1+|x|^{2})^{-k/2}

\displaystyle=(1+|x-y|^{2})^{-k/2}-\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{dt}(1+|x-ty|^{2})^{-k/2}dt

\displaystyle=(1+|x-y|^{2})^{-k/2}-\int_{0}^{1}(1+|x-ty|^{2})^{-(k+2)/2}(x-ty)\cdot ydt.

We decompose

\displaystyle I_{ijl1}\lesssim

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1+|x-y|^{2})^{-k/2}\frac{\Omega(y/|y|)}{|y|^{3}}\varphi(y)\nabla f(x-y)dy\bigg|^{2}dx

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{0}^{1}(1+|x-ty|^{2})^{-(k+1)/2}dt\frac{\Omega(y/|y|)}{|y|^{2}}\varphi(y)\nabla f(x-y)dy\bigg|^{2}dx

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle I_{11}+I_{12}.

By the
L^{2}
boundness of singular integral integral,

I_{11}\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}|\nabla f|^{2}dx.

For
I_{12}
, since for
|y|\leq 2,
we have

1+|x-y|\lesssim 1+|x-ty|,

by Minkowski’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle I_{12}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\bigg|(1+|x-y|^{2})^{-(k+1)/2}\partial_{x_{k}}f(x-y)\bigg|^{2}\frac{\varphi(y)}{|y|^{2}}dydx

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}|\nabla f|^{2}dx.

Estimates of
I_{ijl2}
.

Note that

\partial_{y_{k}}\bigg[\frac{\Omega(y/|y|)}{|y|^{3}}(1-\varphi(y))\bigg]\lesssim(1+|y|^{2})^{-2},

we have

\displaystyle I_{ijl2}\lesssim

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(x-y)(1+|y|^{2})^{-2}dy\bigg|^{2}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(x-y)(1+|x|^{2})^{-\frac{k}{2}}(1+|y|^{2})^{-2}dy\bigg|^{2}dx

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(x-y)(1+|x-y|^{2})^{-\frac{k}{2}}\bigg((1+|y|^{2})^{-2}+(1+|y|^{2})^{-(2-\frac{k}{2})}(1+|x|^{2})^{-\frac{k}{2}}\bigg)dy\bigg|^{2}dx

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(x-y)(1+|x-y|^{2})^{-\frac{k}{2}}(1+|y|^{2})^{-2}dy\bigg|^{2}dx

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(x-y)(1+|x-y|^{2})^{-\frac{k}{2}}(1+|y|^{2})^{-(2-\frac{k}{2})}(1+|x|^{2})^{-\frac{k}{2}}dy\bigg|^{2}dx

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle I_{21}+I_{22},

where in the second inequality we used the fact that

(1+|x|^{2})^{-\frac{k}{2}}(1+|y|^{2})^{-2}\lesssim(1+|x-y|^{2})^{-\frac{k}{2}}\bigg((1+|y|^{2})^{-2}+(1+|y|^{2})^{-(2-\frac{k}{2})}(1+|x|^{2})^{-\frac{k}{2}}\bigg).

For
I_{21}
, using Young’s inequality we have

I_{21}\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}f^{2}(x)dx.

For
I_{22}
, since
k<\frac{5}{2}
, using Cauchy-Schwartz inequality we have

\displaystyle I_{22}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(x-y)(1+|x-y|^{2})^{-\frac{k}{2}}(1+|y|^{2})^{-(2-\frac{k}{2})}dy\bigg)^{2}dx

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1+|x|^{2})^{-\frac{5}{2}}dx\right)^{\frac{2k}{5}}\bigg\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(x-y)(1+|x-y|^{2})^{-\frac{k}{2}}(1+|y|^{2})^{-(2-\frac{k}{2})}dy\bigg\|_{L_{x}^{\frac{10}{5-2k}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{2}.

Then using Young’s inequality, since
k<\frac{5}{2}
implies
\frac{5(4-k)}{5-k}>3
, we have

\displaystyle I_{22}

\displaystyle\lesssim\bigg\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(x-y)(1+|x-y|^{2})^{-\frac{k}{2}}(1+|y|^{2})^{-(2-\frac{k}{2})}dy\bigg\|_{L_{x}^{\frac{10}{5-2k}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}f^{2}(x)dx\bigg\|(1+|y|^{2})^{-(2-\frac{k}{2})}\bigg\|_{L_{x}^{\frac{5}{5-k}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}f^{2}(x)dx.

Combining above estimates the lemma
4.1
is proved.

The estimates of
A_{2}
and
A_{3}
now follows from
f=u_{1}\otimes u
and
f=u\otimes u_{2}
. We have

A_{2}\lesssim\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\chi^{2}(t)h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}(|u_{1}|^{2}|u|^{2}+|u_{1}|^{2}|\nabla u|^{2}+|\nabla u_{1}|^{2}|u|^{2})dxdt.

Similarly,

A_{3}\lesssim\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\chi^{2}(t)h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}(|u_{2}|^{2}|u|^{2}+|u_{2}|^{2}|\nabla u|^{2}+|\nabla u_{2}|^{2}|u|^{2})dxdt.

Using the same techniques as in estimating
A_{12}
, we also have

\displaystyle A_{2}+A_{3}

\displaystyle\lesssim\big(\|u_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}^{2}+\|\nabla\times u_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}^{2}+\|u_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}^{2}+\|\nabla\times u_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}^{2}\big)\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\chi^{2}(t)h(t)^{-2a}(|u|^{2}+|\nabla u|^{2})dxdt.

All in all, we have

\displaystyle A=A_{1}+A_{2}+A_{3}

\displaystyle\lesssim\big(\|u_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}^{2}+\|\nabla\times u_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}^{2}+\|u_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}^{2}+\|\nabla\times u_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}^{2}\big)\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\chi^{2}h^{-2a}(|u|^{2}+|\nabla u|^{2})dxdt

\displaystyle\lesssim T^{+}\big(\|u_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}^{2}+\|\nabla\times u_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}^{2}+\|u_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}^{2}+\|\nabla\times u_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}^{2}\big)\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\chi^{2}h^{-(2a+1)}(|u|^{2}+|\nabla u|^{2})dxdt.

Since
T^{+}
can be chosen sufficenlty small, we have

A\ll\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\chi^{2}(t)h(t)^{-(2a+1)}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}\big((a+1)|u|^{2}+|\nabla u|^{2}\big)dxdt.

As a result, we have

\displaystyle\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\chi^{2}(t)h(t)^{-(2a+1)}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}\big((a+1)|u|^{2}+|\nabla u|^{2}\big)dxdt

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\chi^{\prime}(t)|^{2}h(t)^{-2a}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}|u|^{2}dxdt.

By the definition of
\chi
,

\displaystyle h(T_{-}+\delta/2)^{-(2a+1)}\int_{(T_{-},T_{-}+\frac{\delta}{2})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}|u|^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\chi^{2}(t)h(t)^{-(2a+1)}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}|u|^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\int_{(T_{-},T^{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\chi^{\prime}(t)|^{2}h(t)^{-(2a+1)}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}|u|^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle h(T_{-}+\delta)^{-(2a+1)}\int_{(T_{-}+\delta,T_{-}+2\delta)\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1+|x|^{2})^{-k}|u|^{2}dxdt.

Letting
a\to\infty
, we obtain
u\equiv 0
on
[T_{-},T_{-}+\delta]
, or equivalently,
u_{1}\equiv u_{2}
on
[T-\delta,T]
. Clearly,
\delta
is independent of the terminal time
T
. Repeating the procedure for finite times, we obtain
u_{1}
must coincide with
u_{2}
at any time
t<T
.
∎
