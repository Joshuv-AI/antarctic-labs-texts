The Amenability Framework: Rethinking Causal Ordering Without Estimating Causal Effects
Carlos Fernández-Loría, Jorge Loría

Abstract
Who should we prioritize for intervention when we cannot estimate intervention effects? In many applied domains—such as advertising, customer retention, and behavioral nudging—prioritization is guided by predictive models that estimate outcome probabilities rather than causal effects. This paper investigates when these predictions (scores) can effectively rank individuals by their intervention effects, particularly when direct effect estimation is infeasible or unreliable. We propose a conceptual framework based on amenability —an individual’s latent proclivity to be influenced by an intervention—and formalize conditions under which predictive scores serve as effective proxies for amenability. These conditions justify using non-causal scores for intervention prioritization, even when the scores do not directly estimate effects. We further show that, under plausible assumptions, predictive models can outperform causal effect estimators in ranking individuals by intervention effects. Empirical evidence from an advertising context supports our theoretical findings, demonstrating that predictive modeling can offer a more robust approach to targeting than effect estimation. Our framework suggests a shift in focus—from estimating effects to inferring who is amenable—as a practical and theoretically grounded strategy for prioritizing interventions in resource-constrained environments.

Introduction

Predictive models are central to decision making in applications like advertising
(
Radcliffe and Surry 2011
)
, customer retention
(
Ascarza 2018
)
, and behavioral nudging
(
Athey et al. 2025
)
. These models produce predictive scores (henceforth, simply
scores
), which guide interventions by identifying individuals most likely to exhibit some behavior (e.g., purchase, churn). Organizations routinely use these scores to select recipients for advertisements, retention incentives, and nudges.

This approach assumes that higher scores correspond to individuals who are more easily influenced by the intervention. In advertising, for instance, interventions are often directed toward individuals with the highest estimated purchase probabilities. This is particularly common when interest in a product is low—say, less than 1% of consumers buy it—so even the highest-scoring individuals remain unlikely to purchase (e.g., 10%). Since identifying potential buyers is already challenging, it seems reasonable to focus on those who have at least some level of interest in the product, as they are also the ones most likely to be persuadable. Scores capture this underlying interest, and because interest likely correlates with amenability, targeting those most interested may also mean targeting those most likely to be influenced by the ad.

However, scores do not directly reflect causal effects. A higher purchase probability may not imply a larger advertisement effect. Research on uplift modeling and causal targeting has challenged the use of predictive scores for decision making
(
Radcliffe and Surry 2011
,
Ascarza 2018
)
, arguing that targeting decisions should be based on estimated intervention effects rather than proxies that may not align with causal impact. While this argument is compelling, it comes with challenges. We highlight two key issues—one concerning effectiveness and the other feasibility.

1.1
Effectiveness of Causal Modeling for Targeting

The first issue is effectiveness. Causal targeting relies on
estimates
of effects, but estimating causal effects is notoriously difficult. In domains like advertising, retention, or nudging, intervention effects are often small. For instance, an ad campaign might influence only 200 or 300 people out of every 100,000 consumers
(
Stitelman et al. 2011
)
. With such weak signals relative to noise, estimating how effects vary across individuals becomes extremely challenging.

A potentially more useful approach is to ask:
why
do intervention effects vary? Rather than focusing on estimating causal effects directly, we propose modeling
amenability
—an individual’s proclivity to be influenced by an intervention. This perspective shifts the focus from effect estimation to identifying behavioral signals that indicate how amenable someone is.

For instance, in advertising, amenability can naturally be captured through interest in the advertised product. If someone has no interest, even a steep discount is unlikely to change their decision. Conversely, if someone is already fully committed to buying, an intervention is unnecessary. The most promising targets, then, are those who show the highest level of interest short of committing to a purchase
(
Athey et al. 2025
)
. These individuals may still be unlikely to buy but are among the most amenable, as they are the ones most likely to be influenced by a nudge.

Importantly, interest is not an abstract concept—it manifests in behavior. Consumers who make organic purchases, browse similar products, or engage with related content are signaling interest, and this interest likely correlates with their likelihood of being persuaded. If interest moderates the intervention effect, then targeting based on strong behavioral indicators of interest—rather than noisy estimates of causal effects—may be both more effective and more practical
(
Fernández-Loría and Provost 2022a
,
Fernández-Loría and Provost 2022b
)
.

1.2
Feasibility of Causal Modeling for Targeting

The second issue is feasibility. Uplift modeling and causal targeting assume experiments can be run to estimate effects, but this is often unrealistic. Running an experiment large enough to estimate
variation
in effects—rather than just the average effect—may not be cost-effective
(
Fernández-Loría and Provost 2019
)
. Even in advertising, many organizations lack the resources to conduct such experiments at scale
(
Gordon et al. 2023a
,
Johnson 2023
)
. In some cases, experimentation is not just impractical but impossible. A company with the budget to target only 1% of its customers may not be able to allocate that limited budget to experimentation. In other settings, experiments face ethical, political, or operational constraints. A government agency distributing limited subsidies, for example, may lack the flexibility to randomize allocations for causal inference.

Even when experiments are possible, long-term effects introduce additional challenges. In customer retention, for instance, the full impact of an intervention may take months or years to unfold. A growing literature addresses this by using surrogate outcomes
(
Yang et al. 2024
,
Huang and Ascarza 2024
)
. The key idea is that an intervention’s effect on a long-term outcome often operates through one or more intermediate outcomes. Surrogates are commonly justified as appropriate when they serve this role
(
Prentice 1989
,
Athey et al. 2019
)
, providing a practical alternative when waiting for long-term effects is infeasible—assuming a suitable surrogate can be identified.

Our work builds on this idea and takes it a step further: surrogates may not just be a workaround—they might be the best thing to model. If an intervention’s effect on the long-term outcome operates through the surrogate, then the surrogate isn’t just a proxy—it captures the underlying reason why effects vary across individuals. That means changes in the surrogate provide a clearer signal of amenability than changes in the long-term outcome. And in practice, that’s exactly what we see: targeting decisions based on surrogates can lead to better results than targeting based on noisy estimates of long-term effects
(
Huang and Ascarza 2024
)
.

This perspective suggests a broader principle: what matters for targeting is modeling amenability. The key question is not whether an intervention affects the primary outcome through one or more surrogates, but rather to what extent surrogates—or any other observable behavior—help model how amenable someone is. In some cases, the effect on a surrogate serves this purpose, while in others, the best approach may be to model an entirely different characteristic, such as product interest. Just as a surrogate effect can provide a stronger signal than a long-term effect, other behavioral signals may offer better indicators of amenability than direct effect estimates.

1.3
Key Contributions

Our work examines two key research questions:

•

Under what conditions can scores reliably rank individuals by intervention effects?

•

When can scores outperform direct effect estimation for ranking by effect size?

Our contributions are threefold. First, we identify general conditions under which predictive scores can reliably rank individuals by their intervention effects—even without directly estimating them. These conditions center on amenability, an individual’s proclivity to be influenced by an intervention. Scores can rank individuals by their intervention effects when they effectively capture amenability. A key implication of these conditions is that scores may actually rank individuals by their intervention effects better than direct effect estimation when they provide a less noisy signal of amenability than the effect estimates themselves.

Second, we explain why predictive models often work well for targeting and provide practical guidelines to determine when they should be used to rank individuals by intervention effects. In settings like targeted advertising and customer retention, amenability is closely tied to utility—a consumer’s underlying preference for a product or service
(
Train 2009
)
. Since utility is naturally embedded in models that predict consumer choices, these models can, under specific conditions, rank individuals by their intervention effects without explicitly estimating them.
Prior work provides empirical support for this phenomenon
(
Radcliffe and Surry 2011
,
Fernández-Loría and Provost 2022a
,
Athey et al. 2025
)
, and we establish conditions that explain when and why it holds.

We extend this reasoning to other causal signals, such as surrogate outcomes and potentially confounded causal estimates. Specifically, we show that surrogate effects can serve as a useful proxy for ranking individuals by primary effects when both the primary and surrogate effects are influenced in similar ways by the same factors that determine amenability. Similarly, confounding bias can contribute to the causal signal when the confounding itself is related to amenability.

Finally, we provide empirical evidence from advertising, showing that predictive models can outperform direct effect estimation for ranking individuals by their intervention effects. Counterintuitively, we show that biases in predictive scores can sometimes offset estimation error due to sampling variance, leading to better ranking performance. This highlights an often-overlooked advantage of predictive models in targeting: if scores provide a stronger signal of amenability than direct effect estimates, they can be more reliable for targeting interventions.

In summary, targeting can leverage effect heterogeneity without direct causal estimation by modeling amenability instead. This approach can be more practical than running large-scale experiments or correcting for confounding, particularly when the primary outcome is hard to measure. While randomized experiments remain the gold standard for causal inference, they are not always feasible. Our work broadens the range of viable targeting strategies, showing that interventions can be optimized without direct causal estimates—and that in certain cases, the added value of experimentation may be marginal.

2
Problem Setup: Causal Ordering

Organizations often need to prioritize who receives an intervention based on how likely they are to be influenced by it. In marketing, public policy, and behavioral interventions, limited resources—such as budget and time—make it essential to target individuals where the expected impact is greatest. However, direct measurement of intervention effects at the individual level is often unavailable or imprecise, creating challenges for effective prioritization.

In this work, we focus on the problem of inferring
causal ordering
: determining which individuals experience a greater effect from an intervention. Rather than estimating the absolute effect of an intervention on each individual, the goal is to rank individuals by their causal effects, emphasizing relative differences rather than precise effect sizes. This focus on ranking aligns with the practical demands of prioritization. Throughout this paper, we use the terms intervention and treatment interchangeably to refer to any action intended to influence an outcome. We also use advertising as a motivating and recurring example, but the insights generalize to other domains.

Ideally, prioritization would be based on each individual’s
conditional average treatment effect (CATE)
—the expected impact of the intervention on their outcome of interest, given their observed characteristics (features). Formally, the CATE is defined in terms of potential outcomes, which describe what would happen to an individual with and without the intervention
(
Rubin 1974
)
:

\beta(x)=\mathbb{E}[Y^{1}-Y^{0}\mid X=x]=\mathbb{E}[Y^{\Delta}\mid X=x],

(1)

where
X
is the set of observed covariates (features),
Y^{1}
and
Y^{0}
are the potential outcomes with and without the intervention, and
Y^{\Delta}=Y^{1}-Y^{0}
is the individual-level treatment effect.

If we could observe and reliably estimate
\beta(x)
, prioritization would be straightforward: rank individuals by their CATE and target those with the largest effects. But in many cases, this is either impossible or highly unreliable.

First, we may lack the necessary data to estimate intervention effects. The intervention may be new, preventing the collection of past data. Running experiments might be too expensive or unethical, or we may struggle to measure the outcome we aim to influence. Even when observational data is available, strong confounding can make causal inference unreliable.

Second, even when data is available, estimating causal effects remains extremely challenging. Sample sizes are often too small, treatment effects are weak relative to noise, and even well-designed experiments may suffer from issues like limited statistical power, noncompliance, or imperfect randomization. These challenges make direct estimation of
\beta(x)
not just difficult, but often unreliable for guiding real-world decisions.

As a result, decision makers often rely on scores that estimate alternative quantities. Common examples include:

•

Baseline outcomes:
The expected outcome without the intervention,
\mathbb{E}[Y^{0}\mid X=x]
. In advertising, this could be the probability of a purchase without seeing an ad.

•

Surrogate effects:
The CATE on a surrogate outcome. For instance, advertisers might track website visits instead of purchases, assuming more visits imply more sales.

We frame these scores as estimates of a
Conditional Average Signal (CAS)
, defined as:

\theta(x)=\mathbb{E}[S\mid X=x],

(2)

where is
S
is a
signal variable
(e.g., the baseline outcome or surrogate effect). A predictive model estimates
\theta(x)
, producing scores that guide prioritization decisions.

The key question is whether
\theta(x)
—which is presumed to be much easier to estimate than
\beta(x)
—can serve as a useful proxy for ranking individuals by their intervention effects. If the CAS successfully prioritizes those with the highest CATE, then it can be an effective tool for targeting, even if it does not directly estimate causal effects.

To formalize this idea, we introduce two key notions of unbiasedness: unbiased classification and unbiased ordering.
Unbiased classification (UC)
evaluates whether the CAS can correctly classify individuals whose CATE exceeds a given threshold. This is relevant in settings where interventions are limited to a top
k
subgroup. If the
k
-th largest CATE is
\tau_{k}
, and the
k
-th largest CAS is
\tilde{\tau}_{k}
, unbiased classification requires:

\theta(x)>\tilde{\tau}_{k}\Leftrightarrow\beta(x)>\tau_{k},\forall x.

(3)

This ensures consistency in selecting the top
k

individuals prioritized for intervention.
Unbiased ordering (UO)
is a stricter condition. It requires the ranking produced by the CAS to be identical to the ranking by the CATE:

\theta(x_{i})>\theta(x_{j})\Leftrightarrow\beta(x_{i})>\beta(x_{j}),\forall x_{i},x_{j}.

(4)

Proposition 1

Unbiased ordering implies unbiased classification for any
k
.

The proof is provided in Appendix
9
.

In summary, we aim to understand when scores can serve as reliable proxies for ranking individuals by their intervention effects. Since directly estimating causal effects is often infeasible, decision makers frequently rely on alternative causal signals, such as baseline outcomes or surrogate effects, to guide targeting. Ideally, these scores should satisfy unbiased classification or ordering, meaning that even if they do not precisely estimate causal effects, they still order individuals in a way that aligns with prioritization goals. Whether and when this holds is the key question. The rest of this paper investigates the conditions under which scores provide reliable rankings and how decision makers can assess their suitability for prioritization.

Modeling Causal Signals Without Estimating Effects

Intervention effects vary across individuals because people differ in how easily they can be influenced. In causal inference, these differences are often attributed to
moderators
—variables that shape how strongly an intervention affects an outcome. Moderators are central to explaining effect heterogeneity, as they determine why the same intervention has a strong impact on some individuals but little to no effect on others.

In practice, we do not always observe these moderators directly. Instead, we rely on observed features in
X
, hoping they provide information about who is more or less susceptible to an intervention. That is, we infer variations in intervention effects from variations in
X
, allowing us to identify individuals with larger effects and prioritize them for treatment. However, observed features may not directly determine effect heterogeneity. Instead, they often act as signals for deeper, unobserved mechanisms that shape how susceptible an individual is to an intervention.

For instance, a marketing team might find that users who recently browsed bag-related websites are more responsive to bag advertisements. While browsing history appears to moderate ad effectiveness, the underlying factor at play is likely latent interest in bags—an unobserved trait that influences both browsing behavior and responsiveness to the ad. Browsing history itself does not cause greater ad responsiveness; rather, it provides a behavioral clue about a deeper characteristic that actually determines how much the intervention will affect the individual.

A compelling illustration of this idea comes from
Bauer et al. 2024
, who conducted an experiment with a fashion retailer using loss-framing discounts (e.g., “Don’t lose your discount of up to 70% on items on sale”). They found that their estimated intervention effects correlated with loss aversion—a behavioral trait that was not directly observed but inferred from customer behavior. This suggests that at least some of the variation in treatment effects was related to latent differences in loss aversion, highlighting how observable features can act as proxies for deeper, unobserved factors that make some individuals more susceptible to influence than others.

While multiple behavioral traits may contribute to variation in effects, our key argument is that these tendencies can be thought of as part of a single underlying construct:
amenability
. Amenability captures an individual’s proclivity to be influenced by an intervention. When we estimate CATEs, we are not just measuring intervention effects; we are capturing a signal of amenability. The CATE is not a fundamental property of an individual but rather a behavioral manifestation of how amenable they are in the context of a specific intervention.

This perspective shifts the focus away from estimating intervention effects directly and toward identifying reliable signals of amenability. If amenability is what truly drives variation in effects, then the goal of prioritization is not to estimate CATEs with precision but rather to find the individuals who are most amenable. This distinction is important: even when causal effects are difficult to estimate, prioritization remains possible if we can identify strong behavioral signals of amenability. While causal effect estimation is one way to infer amenability, other behavioral patterns may also indicate how amenable someone is.

If the signal variable
S
captures a behavior that reflects amenability, then rather than estimating the average effect
\beta(x)
, we could instead estimate the signal
\theta(x)
. The scores would then allow us to prioritize individuals based on their likelihood of being amenable—without needing precise effect estimation. In this way, focusing on amenability rather than causal effects provides a practical alternative when effect estimation is infeasible or too challenging due to issues like small sample sizes, measurement noise, or confounding.

3.1
Amenability as the Source of Effect Heterogeneity

We define
amenability
as a latent factor
A
that explains why intervention effects vary across individuals. The idea is simple: if the effect of an intervention is larger for some people than for others, it’s because they’re more amenable. We capture this idea formally with the
Amenability Dominance Assumption
:

\mathbb{E}[Y^{\Delta}\mid x_{i}]>\mathbb{E}[Y^{\Delta}\mid x_{j}]\Leftrightarrow A\mid x_{i}\succ A\mid x_{j},

(5)

for any two individuals
i
and
j
with observed features
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
, respectively. Here,
A\mid x_{i}\succ A\mid x_{j}
means that the distribution of amenability given
x_{i}
stochastically dominates that given
x_{j}
.

Put differently: if the intervention tends to have a larger effect for people with features
x_{i}
than for those with
x_{j}
, it must be because individuals with
x_{i}
are more likely to have higher amenability. That’s the assumption we start from—and everything else builds on it.

Given that amenability is unobserved, the key challenge in prioritization is identifying observable signals in
X
that reflect it. This is where the signal variable
S
and predictive modeling come into play. If we have a strong theoretical basis for why
S
provides a meaningful signal of amenability, then we can use it to approximate
A
. Predictive modeling then allows us to identify features that are predictive of
S
, and by extension, informative of amenability.

In Section
2
, we introduced unbiased ordering to formally define when the ranking produced by the CAS exactly aligns with the ranking by the CATE. Here, we establish conditions under which unbiased ordering holds, using the amenability framework.

To illustrate these conditions, consider again our advertisement example, where an individual’s level of interest in a product is the primary driver of amenability---meaning that individuals who are more amenable correspond directly to those most interested in the product.

, we discuss scenarios where greater interest does not necessarily imply greater amenability.
With this setup, we now introduce the key conditions.

The first condition,
Amenability Mediation
, requires that the signal variable
S
be a pure signal of
A
, meaning that it reflects variation in
A
without picking up extraneous correlations with
X
:

\mathbb{E}[S\mid A,X]=\mathbb{E}[S\mid A].

(6)

Returning to our example, suppose we use purchases in the absence of an ad as
S
, so that scores correspond to the estimated purchase probability without an ad. If purchase probability is driven primarily by the latent interest captured in
A
, then it serves as a good proxy for amenability. However, if
S
also picks up factors that relate to purchasing behavior but not to how amenable the person is, then it no longer serves as a pure proxy for
A
, violating Amenability Mediation.

The second condition,
Amenability Monotonicity
, implies that as amenability increases, the expected value of
S
must either increase or decrease in a consistent direction. Formally:

\mathbb{E}[S\mid A]\text{ is strictly increasing or strictly decreasing in }A.

(7)

In our ad example, Amenability Monotonicity holds if, as the latent interest captured in
A
increases, the probability of purchasing a bag (in the absence of an ad) also increases. If
S
were instead strictly decreasing in
A
—a case we will discuss later in Section
4
—the correct amenability ranking could still be recovered by reversing the order of scores.

With these conditions in place, we can now state one of our key results.

Proposition 2

Amenability Mediation and Amenability Monotonicity imply unbiased ordering.

(Proof in Appendix
10
.) The intuition is straightforward: if Amenability Mediation holds, then all variation in the CAS is attributable to
A
. Moreover, Amenability Monotonicity ensures that the CAS captures directional changes in
A
, which is sufficient to recover the correct ranking of individuals by treatment effects.

3.2
Signal Strength Versus Structural Alignment

A central strength of the amenability framework is its theoretical clarity: if we can identify a target variable that serves as a pure and consistent signal of amenability, then the score ranking aligns with the effect ranking. However, in practice, the conditions required for this guarantee—Amenability Mediation and Amenability Monotonicity—may be too strict.

Does this invalidate the framework? Not necessarily. Even when the conditions fail, the CAS may still serve as a useful proxy for amenability if it is strongly correlated with it. This highlights a key distinction between theoretical identifiability and practical recoverability. In applied settings, we do not observe amenability or treatment effects directly. We model them using data, and our ability to recover the correct ordering depends not only on structural properties, but also on the signal-to-noise ratio of our estimates.

These two dimensions—structural alignment and signal strength—often pull in opposite directions. To illustrate this trade-off more precisely, consider a stylized comparison between two estimators: one based on the CATE and one based on the CAS. Both are noisy:

\hat{\beta}(X)=\beta(X)+\epsilon_{1},\quad\hat{\theta}(X)=\theta(X)+\epsilon_{2},

(8)

where
\epsilon_{1}
and
\epsilon_{2}
are independent noise terms with standard deviation 1.

To understand how they compare in practice, we introduce a simple formulation. Let the CATE be proportional to amenability
A
, scaled down by a factor
1/\lambda
, where
\lambda>0
:

\beta(X)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\cdot\mathbb{E}[A|X].

(9)

Similarly, let the CAS be proportional to a different latent variable
\tilde{A}
:

\theta(X)=\lambda\cdot\mathbb{E}[\tilde{A}\mid X].

(10)

Here,
\tilde{A}
is correlated with
A
but not identical to it. This captures the idea that the latent factors driving the score signal may align imperfectly with those moderating causal effects. In other words, the CAS reflects a different projection of amenability—potentially noisier or biased—while still retaining some informative structure.

We assume
\mathbb{E}[A\mid X]
and
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{A}\mid X]
each have standard deviation 1, and that their correlation is
\rho
. The correlation
\rho
captures the degree of structural alignment between the CAS and the CATE: a lower
\rho
implies a greater violation of the structural assumptions. The parameter
\lambda
determines the relative strength of the signals. A larger
\lambda
weakens the signal-to-noise ratio of the CATE estimate
\hat{\beta}
, while strengthening the signal-to-noise ratio of the CAS estimate
\hat{\theta}
. This formulation lets us explore how performance depends jointly on how well the score signal aligns with amenability (
\rho
) and how strongly the signal stands out from the noise (
\lambda
).

In this setting, the CAS estimate
\hat{\theta}(X)
is more strongly correlated with the CATE
\beta(X)
than the CATE estimate
\hat{\beta}(X)
when:

\lambda\rho>1.

(11)

(See Appendix
11
for the derivation.) This inequality captures the central insight: a misaligned but high-quality signal can outperform a noisier estimate of the correct target. In practical terms, this means that a well-estimated proxy may be more useful for prioritization than a theoretically ideal quantity that is difficult to estimate reliably. For example, if
\lambda=2
, it suffices for the correlation
\rho
between
A
and
\tilde{A}
to be greater than 0.5 for the CAS estimates to be more strongly correlated with the true CATE than the CATE estimates themselves.

This trade-off mirrors a broader principle in predictive modeling: when estimation is noisy, correlation often trumps theoretical alignment. In practice, high-quality estimates of imperfect proxies often outperform noisy estimates of ideal targets. This perspective supports a pragmatic approach to designing and evaluating prioritization strategies: rather than insisting on perfect alignment with causal effects, we should prioritize reliable, informative signals—even if imperfect.

3.3
Implications for Ranking Estimation

A key insight from modeling amenability is that it can lead to better causal ordering than directly estimating causal effects. This section explores why and when this can happen, focusing on the role of bias, variance, and their interaction for ranking estimation.

In practice, individuals are not ranked using the CAS itself. Instead, rankings are based on an
estimated score
, denoted as
\hat{\theta}(x)
, which differs from the CAS due to bias and variance in the estimation process. We express it as:

\begin{split}&\hat{\theta}(x)=\beta(x)+\alpha(x)+\epsilon,\\
&\mathbb{E}[\epsilon\mid X]=0.\end{split}

(12)

Here,
\alpha(x)
represents bias arising from two sources. The first is an inherent bias that exists regardless of how well we estimate the scores—it reflects the fundamental difference between the CAS and the CATE, meaning that
\theta(x)\neq\beta(x)
, regardless of how well we estimate the scores. The second source is model misspecification, such as incorrect functional form assumptions, leading to systematic errors in estimating
\theta(x)
, so that
\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}(x)]\neq\theta(x)
.

The term
\epsilon
captures estimation error due to variance in the training sample. This randomness affects the estimated score even when the model is correctly specified.

If the goal were to estimate causal effects precisely, minimizing both bias and variance would be ideal. However, reducing one often increases the other, creating a bias-variance trade-off. For example, methods that correct for confounding often introduce additional variance, making effect estimation noisier
(
Gordon et al. 2023b
)
.

But bias and variance do not affect ranking estimation in the same way they affect effect estimation. In fact, bias can somtimes
improve
the ranking. Prior work has shown that in certain cases, bias can offset variance-driven errors, leading to better classification of high- vs. low-effect individuals
(
Fernández-Loría et al. 2023
,
Fernández-Loría and Provost 2019
,
Fernández-Loría and Provost 2022a
)
. We extend this argument and show bias can also improve causal ordering by mitigating variance-driven errors.

To formally assess ranking quality, we use the
Kendall rank correlation coefficient

(
Kendall 1938

&=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i<j}\text{sgn}(\hat{\theta}(x_{i})-\hat{\theta}(x_{j})).\end{split}

(13)

4
Baseline Choices as Causal Signals

We now explore how modeling amenability—rather than directly estimating treatment effects—offers a practical and effective way to rank individuals by intervention effects in discrete choice settings. These settings are common in marketing applications such as customer retention and targeted advertising, where individuals make binary decisions (e.g., stay or leave, purchase or not).

Interventions in these domains are often targeted based on an individual’s baseline propensity to take an action rather than on explicitly estimated treatment effects. For instance, advertisers prioritize customers most likely to purchase, and retention teams focus on those at high risk of churning. This approach reflects the difficulty of estimating individual-level causal effects, particularly when intervention effects are small relative to noise, precisely estimating effect heterogeneity is prohibitively expensive, or experimentation is simply infeasible.

Empirical evidence shows that, although counterintuitive, targeting individuals based on baseline propensity rather than estimated effects can still prioritize those with larger causal effects. For example,
Stitelman et al. 2011
found that targeting prospective new customers produced a 40% larger causal effect compared to targeting the general population—and up to 100% larger when focusing on past converters. Similarly,
Athey et al. 2025
provide evidence that ranking individuals by their natural propensity to take specific actions is highly effective for targeting behavioral interventions. Their study on nudging students to renew financial aid finds that predictive models of behavior can outperform direct causal-effect models for intervention prioritization.

The logic underlying these findings is straightforward: if amenability is systematically related to an individual’s likelihood of acting in certain way, then the same models used to predict actions can also identify individuals most likely to be influenced. We now formalize this intuition by drawing on discrete choice theory, showing that an individual’s utility for taking an action provides a meaningful measure of amenability.

4.1
Utility as a Proxy for Amenability

In discrete choice models, utility represents an individual’s underlying preference for a particular action, such as making a purchase or renewing a subscription. Decisions follow a threshold-based structure: an individual chooses the action if their utility exceeds a decision threshold; otherwise, they do not. This threshold-based nature of decision making has a direct connection to amenability—how easily an intervention can change someone’s decision.

To formalize this, we follow the standard discrete choice framework
(
Train 2009
)
:

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}\{U+\delta T>0\},\textnormal{~where~}\delta>0.

(16)

Here,
Y
denotes the decision (e.g., purchasing a product), and
T
indicates whether the individual receives the intervention.
Baseline utility

U
captures the individual’s inherent inclination to take the action, while the
utility shift

\delta
represents the change in utility induced by the intervention. The intervention persuades someone only if the utility shift moves their total utility from below the threshold (zero) to above it.

Amenability then depends on two key forces. The first is baseline utility—how close an individual already is to taking the action. The second is the utility shift caused by the intervention. If an individual’s baseline utility is far from the decision threshold, the intervention must induce a large shift to change the decision. Conversely, if the person is already near the threshold, even a small shift may be enough to tip the outcome.

A useful way to illustrate this is through a simple analogy. Imagine a group of friends lounging by a swimming pool. One of them, feeling mischievous, wants to push someone into the water but has only one attempt. Who should they choose? The answer depends on two factors: how close each person is to the edge and how forceful the push is. Someone standing at the edge may need only a light tap, while someone sitting several feet away would require a much stronger shove. Similarly, in decision making, individuals closer to the decision threshold are more susceptible to intervention, while those far from it require a stronger utility shift to be persuaded.

This analogy maps naturally onto our model. Baseline utility determines proximity to the edge—an inherent characteristic of the individual that dictates how much of a shift is needed for persuasion. The utility shift produced by the intervention, in contrast, represents the strength of the push—a factor that depends on both the intervention itself and how it interacts with the individual’s preferences. If the utility shift is uniform across individuals, as in Equation (
16
), then amenability is determined entirely by baseline utility: those closest to the threshold are the most responsive. However, if the shift varies across individuals, amenability depends on both baseline utility and how strongly the intervention affects the utility of each individual.

This distinction is crucial. In many practical settings, targeting strategies rank individuals based on their baseline propensity to act, implicitly assuming that those nearest the decision threshold are also the most persuadable. However, a key caveat arises when an intervention can have both positive and negative effects. In such cases, variation in the utility shift may become more important than baseline propensity. For example, in efforts to encourage voting, the impact of a message may depend more on how well it aligns with an individual’s party affiliation than on their baseline likelihood of voting.

Conversely, when the intervention is expected to have predominantly non-negative effects, assuming a fixed utility shift offers a reasonable approximation. In Section
4.4
, we return to this point and explore how heterogeneity in utility shifts affects targeting strategies, and to what extent baseline utility alone can serve as a sufficient proxy for amenability. For now, however, we proceed with the fixed utility shift formulation.

4.2
Applying the Amenability Framework

In discrete choice settings, the challenge of targeting interventions is not about predicting individual responses to treatment but about prioritizing individuals who are amenable—those for whom an intervention is most likely to change their decision. Amenability in this context is fundamentally tied to baseline utility: an intervention succeeds in altering a decision only if it moves an individual’s utility from below the decision threshold to above it. However, since utility is unobserved, targeting requires an indirect approach. Rather than estimating treatment effects explicitly, the goal is to estimate the probability that an individual’s utility falls within the amenable range.

Formally, an individual is more amenable the more likely their baseline utility lies in the interval
(-\delta,0)
, where
\delta
is the utility shift induced by the intervention. So we define amenability as the conditional probability:

A=\mathbb{P}(-\delta<U<0\mid X).

(17)

This formulation makes explicit that the central problem is not to estimate treatment effects, but to infer how likely is an individual’s baseline utility to fall within this critical range. This shift in perspective is crucial: rather than predicting intervention effects directly, we aim to rank individuals based on their likelihood of being amenable.

A natural candidate for approximating
A
is an individual’s baseline probability of taking the action without intervention, which serves as our CAS:

\begin{split}\theta(X)&=\mathbb{E}[Y^{0}\mid X]=\mathbb{P}(U>0\mid X),\end{split}

(18)

Because utility determines an individual’s baseline decision, this probability carries key information about amenability. When
\theta(X)
is very low, most individuals with the same
X
have negative utility and are unlikely to take the action on their own. When it is very high, most have positive utility and would act regardless. Amenable individuals—those whose utility is close to but below zero—tend to be concentrated in the middle range of probabilities, where utility shifts could plausibly change their decision.

To formalize this intuition, consider a setting where the conditional baseline utility
U\mid X
follows a logistic distribution. In this case, the CAS can be expressed as a function of expected utility:

\theta(X)=\Lambda\big(\mathbb{E}[U\mid X]\big)=\left(1+e^{-\mathbb{E}[U\mid X]}\right)^{-1},

(19)

where
\Lambda
denotes the logistic CDF.

We use the logistic distribution for ease of exposition and because it is commonly used to model unobserved utility in discrete choice settings. However, the key insights about when baseline outcomes can serve as proxies for amenability do not depend on this choice. What matters is that baseline utility depends on
X
through shifts in a location parameter, and that the distribution of
U\mid X
is symmetric and unimodal around that parameter—these are standard assumptions in discrete choice models.

This formulation clarifies how baseline probabilities relate to amenability. Since the CAS is a monotonic function of expected utility, it captures shifts in the location of the utility distribution. These shifts, in turn, affect the likelihood that utility falls within the amenable range
(-\delta,0)
.

Because conditional baseline utility
U\mid X
is assumed to follow a logistic distribution, it is symmetric and unimodal. Under these assumptions, the probability of falling within the amenable range
(-\delta,0)
is highest when the expected utility is centered within that range—that is, when
\mathbb{E}[U\mid X]=-\delta/2

\theta(X)=\Lambda(-\delta/2)

As a result, amenability increases monotonically with the CAS for individuals with scores below
\Lambda(-\delta/2)
and decreases monotonically for those above it. This gives rise to a bell-shaped relationship between amenability and expected utility, as illustrated in Figure

4.4
Violation of Amenability Mediation

Thus far, we have assumed that the intervention causes a constant utility shift
\delta
across individuals. Under this assumption, amenability is fully determined by baseline utility. In reality, however, interventions do not increase the attractiveness of an action equally for everyone. Some individuals may find the intervention highly compelling, experiencing a large utility shift, while others may see little to no added value. This heterogeneity implies that amenability depends not only on baseline utility but also on how the intervention affects the action’s appeal.

We consider next the setting where the utility shift varies across individuals:

Y=\mathbf{1}\{U+U_{\delta}T>0\}.

(20)

Here, individual-specific utility shifts are represented by the random variable
U_{\delta}
. This formulation has important implications: If
X
predicts
U_{\delta}
, then
X
may correlate with baseline outcomes through mechanisms unrelated to amenability, potentially violating Amenability Mediation.

We say
potentially
because the extent to which this breakdown occurs depends on the relationship between baseline utility
U
and the utility shift
U_{\delta}
. If the expected utility shift is a monotonic function of expected baseline utility—meaning that individuals with higher baseline utility systematically experience stronger or weaker utility shifts—then baseline utility may remain monotonically related to amenability. In this case, the CAS would still be ordering unbiased.

Crucially, the direction of this monotonicity matters. When baseline utility is generally low, amenability increases with baseline utility, meaning that the CAS should increase with amenability. The ordering remains unbiased if greater baseline utility corresponds to a larger utility shift. Conversely, when baseline utility is generally high, amenability decreases with baseline utility, meaning the CAS should decrease with amenability. Here, the ordering is unbiased if higher baseline utility consistently corresponds to a smaller utility shift. In other words, even though the utility shift varies, the CAS remains a strong proxy for amenability as long as the shift is monotonic in baseline utility in a way that aligns with the amenability pattern.

However, if baseline utility and utility shifts are not monotonically related—so that individuals with higher expected baseline utility may experience either larger or smaller utility shifts—then amenability is no longer solely determined by baseline utility and scores are not ordering unbiased. Statistically, this means that the features in
X
may now predict the signal variable
S
through pathways unrelated to amenability.

In practice, however, the primary concern is how well the scores capture the causal signal, regardless of whether Amenability Mediation holds exactly.

The strength of the scores as a causal signal depends on two factors: the degree of variation in utility shifts and how these shifts relate to baseline utility. If utility shifts exhibit only small variation, amenability remains largely determined by baseline utility, and the CAS continues to serve as a good causal proxy. However, if utility shifts vary substantially, their relationship with baseline utility becomes crucial. When utility shifts consistently increase or decrease with baseline utility in a way that preserves the underlying amenability pattern, the CAS remains informative. But if utility shifts vary in a way that disrupts this relationship, the CAS may no longer provide a reliable ranking of amenability.

4.5
Empirical Evidence and Practical Considerations

Our discussions have primarily focused on advertising. In this setting, most individuals will not purchase a product, so scores are used to identify those with greater inferred interest—those who are closest to be “pushed over” by the advertisement. Here, it is natural to expect
\rho>0
: ads tend to be more compelling for individuals who have greater underlying interest in the product. As a result, large variations in utility shifts do not necessarily disrupt causal ordering because those shifts remain systematically related to baseline utility. It is therefore not surprising that even proponents of causal-effect models with extensive industry experience acknowledge that conventional predictive models can perform well for causal ordering in this setting:

“A situation we commonly see in retail environments is that direct marketing activity appears to have the most positive effect on high-spending customers. Where this is the case,
[causal effects]
and sales are positively correlated, and therefore a conventional model and
[a causal]
model are more likely to rank the population in similar ways.”
(
Radcliffe and Surry 2011
)

Now, consider a different setting: customer retention. In businesses where most customers tend to stay, baseline utility is generally high. This supports the standard practice of prioritizing individuals with the lowest baseline scores (i.e., those least likely to stay), as it aligns with our framework: when baseline utilities are high, amenability increases as baseline utility decreases. This reasoning helps explain the long-standing use of churn prediction models for targeting retention incentives
(
Neslin et al. 2006
)
.

However, our framework also highlights a key limitation: if there is large variation in utility shifts and those shifts are positively correlated with baseline utility (i.e.,
\rho>0
), then prioritizing low-score individuals may not work well. This is a concern in retention settings because incentives (e.g., discounts, loyalty rewards) often appeal more to customers already predisposed to staying. More generally, when attempting to persuade someone to take an action, interventions tend to be more attractive to individuals already inclined toward that action.

This suggests that in customer retention, utility shifts are more likely to disrupt causal ordering because
\rho
is likely to be positive, rather than negative. Whether this fully breaks the CAS as a causal signal depends on the relative variation in utility shifts compared to baseline utility. However, studies have shown that high-risk customers are not always the best targets for proactive retention programs
(
Ascarza 2018
)
, aligning with our observation. Furthermore, industry practitioners have reported that causal models tend to work better for retention than for advertising
(
Radcliffe and Surry 2011
)
, reinforcing the idea that utility shifts play a more disruptive role in retention contexts.

Another important consideration is that our framework assumes the intervention generally has a positive effect. In other words, we assume the intervention generally pushes individuals toward an action, rather than pulling them away from it. This is crucial because the CAS is used to infer amenability based on how close individuals are to the decision boundary. If the intervention harms most individuals but is highly beneficial for a few, then ranking based on baseline utility could backfire. Therefore, ensuring that the intervention has an overall non-negative effect is another fundamental requirement for applying this approach effectively.

4.6
A Practical Tool for Prioritizing Interventions Using Baseline Scores

To translate these theoretical insights into practice, we propose an analytical tool to help practitioners determine whether causal ordering can be inferred from baseline outcomes. The tool is particularly relevant in settings where:

1.

The intervention is expected to have a predominantly non-negative effect, but only a limited subset of individuals can be targeted due to resource constraints.

2.

Direct estimation of intervention effects is infeasible due to data limitations.

While the tool relies on simplifying assumptions, it provides a practical benchmark for assessing whether a CAS based on the baseline outcome model in Equation (
18
) offers a meaningful improvement over random selection in causal ordering. Since the tool is designed for settings where direct effect estimation is infeasible—leaving random selection as the alternative—the objective is not to achieve perfect ranking but to assess whether the model provides a systematic advantage in prioritizing individuals for intervention.

5
Surrogate Effects as Causal Signals

Another common strategy for inferring variation in treatment effects is to use surrogate effects—effects derived from a different, but related, intervention–outcome pair. This might involve estimating the effect of the same treatment on a related outcome (e.g., an intermediate or downstream behavior), the effect of a related treatment on the same outcome, or the effect of a related treatment on a related outcome. Surrogate effects are often used in practice due to data limitations, measurement costs, or the need for timely decisions.

Surrogate outcomes have long played a central role in clinical research, where long-term outcomes like survival or quality of life may take years to observe. In such cases, alternative outcomes like tumor shrinkage serve as timely indicators of treatment efficacy
(
Prentice 1989
,
VanderWeele 2013
,
Buyse et al. 2016
)
. The logic carries over to marketing and digital platforms, where short-term metrics such as site visits or immediate purchases are used in place of harder-to-measure goals like customer lifetime value or sustained engagement. For example,
Dalessandro et al. 2015
demonstrate that brand site visits can be used to optimize advertising campaigns when purchase data are sparse, and
Yang et al. 2024
,
Huang and Ascarza 2024
discuss the use of short-term behavioral signals to predict long-term retention and performance outcomes.

While much of the literature on surrogate outcomes focuses on detecting the presence of effects or estimating their magnitude, our interest lies elsewhere. The key question is:
When are surrogate effects useful signals for inferring the causal ordering of different intervention–outcome pairs?

This shift in focus—from effect estimation to causal ordering—has important implications. In many real-world settings, prioritization and targeting are critical, so ranking individuals correctly matters more than estimating the magnitude of the intervention’s effect on them. From this perspective, a surrogate effect can be useful even if it fails traditional identifiability criteria. What matters is whether it provides a reliable signal for amenability.

This framing broadens what counts as a valid surrogate. We do not require that the surrogate lies on the causal pathway from treatment to the outcome of interest, or even that it involves the same treatment or outcome. A related treatment affecting a related outcome can still provide a useful signal, as long as its effect correlates with amenability to the primary intervention–outcome pair.

This perspective also helps reinterpret existing work. For example,
Huang and Ascarza 2024
and
Yang et al. 2024
study surrogate effects for targeting but emphasize conditions for valid identification. Our goal is different: to understand when surrogate effects are good enough to rank individuals, even if they are not good enough to estimate effects precisely.

To be clear, our framework does provide conditions—Amenability Mediation and Amenability Monotonicity—under which surrogate effects are ordering unbiased. But more broadly, we argue that what makes a surrogate useful is not structural validity, but correlation with amenability.

These structural conditions hold when the surrogate and primary intervention–outcome pairs are governed by the same underlying latent trait. Figure
6
illustrates this structure: a shared latent trait shapes both surrogate and primary amenability, linking the effects of different intervention–outcome pairs through a common source of heterogeneity.

Let’s consider online advertising again. The effect of an ad on website visits might serve as a surrogate for its effect on purchases—not necessarily because visits lie on the causal pathway (though they might), but because both effects are shaped by a common latent factor such as consumer interest or intent. In this view, features do not predict intervention effectiveness directly; instead, they provide a noisy signal of the latent trait that determines amenability. When this is the case, visits and purchases become alternative reflections of the same underlying disposition. Amenability Mediation holds when amenability in the surrogate effect (e.g., on visits) is driven by the same latent trait that explains amenability in the primary effect (e.g., on purchases). Amenability Monotonicity holds when both surrogate and primary effects increase with that latent trait. Under these conditions, the surrogate effect is ordering unbiased.

5.2
Practical Guidelines for Using Surrogate Models

So far, our analysis has offered a conceptual framework for understanding when surrogate effects can support causal ordering. In practice, however, the key consideration—whether the surrogate effect reflects the same latent traits that govern the primary effect—is not directly testable without data on the primary intervention–outcome pair or on the latent traits themselves. As a result, practitioners must often rely on theoretical reasoning and domain knowledge to judge whether surrogate effects are likely to provide a meaningful causal signal.

To help bridge theory and practice, we offer a structured set of guiding questions. These are not meant to serve as definitive identifiability conditions, nor can they be used as empirical tests. Instead, they are designed to support theoretical reasoning when direct estimation of the treatment effect on the primary outcome is infeasible.

Practitioners can use these questions sequentially to evaluate whether a surrogate-based score is likely to preserve causal ordering. Importantly, our framework is intended to highlight conditions under which surrogate effects are recommended—not to categorically rule them out when those conditions are unmet. A “not recommended” outcome should be interpreted as a lack of theoretical support, not as evidence that the surrogate effect will necessarily fail.

1.

Common latent trait(s).
Is it reasonable to believe that the surrogate and primary effects are governed by the same underlying trait(s)? If not, there is no clear rationale for expecting the surrogate effect to reflect amenability to the primary effect, so it is not recommended.

2.

Directional alignment.
Do the relevant traits predict both effects in the same direction? If not, the surrogate effect may systematically misorder individuals relative to the primary effect and is not recommended.

3.

Dominant trait.
Is there a single dominant trait that explains variation in both effects? If so, the surrogate effect is likely to offer a reliable signal for causal ordering. If not, continue.

4.

Trait correlation.
If multiple traits explain variation in both effects, are those traits positively correlated (e.g., individuals high on one are likely high on the others)? If yes, surrogate effects are likely to track amenability well enough for ranking and are recommended. If not, continue.

5.

Relative importance.
Are the traits that most strongly predict the surrogate effect also the ones that most strongly predict the primary effect? That is, do the most important sources of variation in one tend to be the most important in the other? If yes, the surrogate is likely to reflect the relevant signal for causal ordering. If not, our insights suggest a greater risk of misalignment, and the surrogate effect is not recommended based on the available theoretical rationale.

6
Confounded Estimates as Causal Signals

A final strategy for inferring causal ordering involves using confounded effect estimates derived from observational data. These estimates compare average outcomes between treated and untreated individuals, conditional on observed covariates. While widely used in practice, they are often dismissed in causal inference due to the threat of unobserved confounding.

Our goal here is not to defend these estimates for effect estimation, but to explore conditions under which they might still provide useful signals for causal ordering. As discussed in Section
3.3
, this distinction matters: bias can degrade effect estimation while improving causal ordering, particularly when it reinforces the separation between high- and low-effect individuals.

Formally, when using a confounded target, the CAS corresponds to:

\theta(X)=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid T=1,X]-\mathbb{E}[Y\mid T=0,X],

(30)

where
T
is the treatment indicator and
Y
is the observed outcome. This can be decomposed as:

\theta(X)=\beta(X)+\alpha(X),

(31)

where
\beta(X)
is the CATE and
\alpha(X)
is the confounding bias.

To evaluate whether
\theta(X)
is ordering unbiased, consider two individuals
i
and
j
. If:

\beta(x_{i})>\beta(x_{j}),

(32)

ordering is unbiased if:

\beta(x_{i})-\beta(x_{j})>-\left(\alpha(x_{i})-\alpha(x_{j})\right).

(33)

This condition shows that what matters for ordering is not the size or sign of the bias, but how it varies across individuals. If bias is constant, ordering is unbiased. More generally, as shown in Proposition
3
, ordering improves when bias increases with effect size (i.e.,
\alpha(x_{i})-\alpha(x_{j})>0
) because it offsets variance-related errors. Conversely, if bias decreases with effect size, it degrades ranking performance.

How, then, can we reason about whether confounding pushes bias in a helpful or harmful direction? The amenability framework offers a useful starting point. If the latent traits that govern amenability are also the unobserved confounders driving bias, then we can begin to reason about how bias varies with treatment effects.

In particular, if bias increases with amenability, then the confounded target may actually reflect the underlying causal ordering more strongly than the treatment effect itself. In this case, bias acts not as noise but as an additional source of signal—improving ranking accuracy even while compromising effect estimation.

Conversely, if bias decreases with amenability, it can distort the ranking by amplifying variance-related errors or even reversing the ordering in expectation. Still, if the signal from
\beta(X)
is strong enough to counteract the misaligned confounding signal, ordering may remain unbiased.

These insights align with prior theoretical and empirical results in
Fernández-Loría and Provost 2019
, which show that confounded models can outperform unbiased CATE models in causal classification. While that work does not rely on the amenability framework, it analyzes closely related conditions under which confounded targets remain ordering unbiased. For a more complete discussion of confounded effect estimates as causal signals—including detailed conditions under which confounding does not distort causal ordering—we refer interested readers to that study.

Key takeaways:

•

What matters for causal ordering is not the magnitude or sign of the confounding bias, but how it varies across individuals.

•

If bias increases with amenability, it amplifies the causal signal and can improve ordering.

•

If bias decreases with amenability, it weakens the signal—but ordering may still be unbiased if the CATE signal is stronger than the misaligned confounding signal.

7.2
Application of Guidelines

Although our benchmark relies on data from a randomized experiment, the motivating use case is one in which such data are unavailable. In Sections
4.6
and
5.2
, we provided theoretical guidelines for evaluating proxy models under those conditions. Here, we illustrate how these guidelines can be applied in practice.

Conversion rate model.

We begin with the model that predicts conversion in the absence of an advertisement (Section
4.6
). The first consideration is whether the ad is expected to have primarily non-negative effects on conversion. While we lack specific details about the ad, it is generally reasonable to assume that targeted ads do not reduce conversion likelihood.

In this case, because conversions are rare, we fall under Scenario A: the desired action is typically unlikely. Under this scenario, whether baseline outcomes can provide a valid signal for causal ordering or classification depends on two quantities: (i) the maximum predicted conversion probability and (ii) a reasonable upper bound on the maximum CATE.

Suppose we assume that even the most responsive individuals are unlikely to have an expected effect larger than 1%—a plausible bound in advertising settings and consistent with our observed ATE of 0.1%. The 99th percentile of conversion rates predicted by our model is 16%, which satisfies the inequality in Equation (
22
) even under conservative assumptions (e.g., a CATE as large as 68%). This implies that causal ordering is theoretically plausible for 99% of the population.

However, the 99.99th percentile of scores is 70%, which violates the condition. This suggests that Amenability Monotonicity may not hold for a small subset of individuals. Even so, causal classification remains feasible: the inequality in Equation (
23
) supports the use of the model to identify top responders (e.g., the top 1%). Thus, while caution is warranted at the extreme tail, the model remains justifiable for broader prioritization.

CATE-on-Visits Model.

We now consider the surrogate model using the guidelines in Section
5.2
. The first question is whether both the surrogate and primary effects are governed by a common latent trait. Although the feature names are anonymized, it is plausible that they capture consumer interest in the advertised product, moderating both the effect on website visits and conversions.

Next, we assess whether the same trait influences both outcomes in the same direction. This also appears reasonable, since higher interest would likely increase the ad effectiveness in both domains. Finally, is there a single dominant latent trait? While multiple traits may be involved, consumer interest plausibly dominates variation in both outcomes. Thus, our guidelines support the use of the surrogate model for causal ordering.

In summary, our theoretical framework supports the use of both proxy models in this setting. While neither model directly estimates the effect of interest, both are likely to offer useful signals for causal ordering. Empirical results further support this conclusion.

8
Discussion and Limitations

This paper introduces a conceptual framework for intervention prioritization based on
amenability
—a latent trait that governs heterogeneity in intervention effects. We argue that in many applied settings, targeting decisions can be guided more effectively by reasoning about amenability than by estimating effects directly. This shift reflects a broader insight: causal ordering can be implemented even when causal effect estimation is noisy, infeasible, or unreliable.

Our core contribution is to formalize conditions under which predictive scores—such as those from baseline outcome models, surrogate effect models, or even confounded effect estimates—can serve as effective proxies for causal-effect rankings. When these scores align with amenability, they enable accurate prioritization without requiring causal-effect identification.

This perspective provides a theoretical foundation for a pattern observed in practice across domains such as advertising, customer retention, and behavioral nudging: predictive models can outperform causal effect estimators in identifying high-effect individuals. This advantage arises when effect estimation is noisy and the model’s target variable aligns more closely with the latent traits that determine amenability than the treatment effect does. In such cases, predictive scores lead to better rankings because they capture clearer signals of who is likely to be influenced, even if they do not estimate effects directly. Our empirical analysis supports this: in a large-scale advertising dataset, a simple non-causal model predicting baseline outcomes outperformed causal estimators in identifying those most amenable.

The practical implications are significant. In resource-constrained environments where randomized experiments are expensive, impractical, or unethical, predictive modeling can offer a reliable alternative. Even when the assumptions behind our framework hold only approximately, scores that reflect amenability may still provide better rankings than effect estimators, especially when they exhibit higher signal-to-noise ratios.

This work also bridges a longstanding divide between predictive modeling and causal inference. While causal inference traditionally emphasizes effect estimation under strong identification assumptions, predictive modeling focuses on flexibility, scalability, and utility. Our framework unifies these perspectives by showing that predictive models can support causal decision making, not by estimating effects, but by recovering a causal signal.

It also offers a new lens for interpreting prior empirical findings. For instance, work showing that baseline outcome models outperform causal estimators in targeting applications
(
Fernández-Loría and Provost 2022b
,
Athey et al. 2025
)
, or that short-term proxies can outperform estimates of long-term outcomes
(
Huang and Ascarza 2024
)
, can be explained through the lens of amenability: these models succeed not by estimating effects more precisely, but by more accurately capturing who is more likely to be amenable.

Naturally, the framework has limitations. It is best suited to settings where a single latent trait largely drives heterogeneity in intervention effects, making it easier to assess whether predictive scores are capturing that trait. When multiple uncorrelated traits contribute to effect variation, or when scores violate monotonicity with respect to amenability, predictive models may become misleading rather than helpful. Additionally, the assumptions underlying the framework are structural and not directly testable without experimental data—though they can be supported by theory, domain knowledge, or indirect validation.

We also focus here on a simple prioritization task: selecting individuals with the highest expected effect. In practice, organizations face more complex constraints, including fairness, heterogeneous costs, dynamic rollout, and strategic experimentation. A promising direction for future work is to embed amenability-based ranking into richer decision-making frameworks, including those that optimize downstream outcomes or incorporate constraints via policy learning
(
Elmachtoub and Grigas 2022
,
Athey and Wager 2021
)
.

Finally, while our framework is conceptual, it opens empirical and methodological questions:

•

In which domains is it more effective to model amenability than to estimate causal effects?

•

What kinds of data-generating processes support amenability inference?

•

Can we develop methods that directly estimate amenability rather than effects?

By shifting the focus from estimating effects to identifying who is amenable, we propose a more flexible and practically grounded approach to causal decision making. Rather than asking “What is the effect of this intervention?”, we may often be better served by asking “Who is most likely to be influenced?”—a shift in mindset that opens up new possibilities for both research and practice.

14
Proof of Proposition
5

This proof does not assume that
U\mid X
follows a logistic distribution. It applies more generally to any distribution where
U
depends on
X
only through a location parameter, and where the conditional distribution
U\mid X
is symmetric and unimodal around that parameter (e.g., logistic, normal, Laplace, or even Cauchy).

Let

A=\mathbb{P}(-\delta<U<0\mid X).

Suppose
U\mid X=\mu+\epsilon
, where
\mu=\mu(X)
is a location parameter that depends on
X
, and
\epsilon
is a random variable that is independent of
X
, symmetric around 0, and unimodal. Let
F
and
f
denote the CDF and PDF of
\epsilon
, respectively.

Then:

A=\mathbb{P}(-\delta<\mu+\epsilon<0\mid X)=\mathbb{P}(-\delta-\mu<\epsilon<-\mu)=F(-\mu)-F(-\mu-\delta).

Using the symmetry of
\epsilon
, which implies
F(-a)=1-F(a)
, we can rewrite:

A=(1-F(\mu))-(1-F(\mu+\delta))=F(\mu+\delta)-F(\mu).

So
A
is fully determined by
\mu
, and we aim to show that Amenability Mediation and Monotonicity are met.

To analyze how
A=F(\mu+\delta)-F(\mu)
behaves with respect to
\mu
, we differentiate:

\frac{dA}{d\mu}=f(\mu+\delta)-f(\mu).

Setting this derivative to zero gives the condition:

f(\mu+\delta)=f(\mu).

Since
\epsilon
is symmetric and unimodal around 0, the equality holds if and only if
\mu=-\delta/2
, which centers the interval
[\mu,\mu+\delta]
at the mode of
\epsilon
. Because
\epsilon
is unimodal, this is the
unique global maximum
of
A
.

It follows that:

•

A
increases with
\mu
when
\mu<-\delta/2
,

•

A
decreases with
\mu
when
\mu>-\delta/2
.

These two cases correspond to the scenarios where all individuals have CAS values below or above
\Lambda(-\delta/2)
(or more generally, below or above
F(-\delta/2)
).

Hence,
A
is strictly monotonic (and invertible) on either side of
-\delta/2
. If
\mu(X)
lies entirely on one side of this maximum, there exists a monotonic inverse function
g
such that
\mu=g(A)
.

Finally, since the outcome
S=\mathbb{I}\{U>0\}=\mathbb{I}\{\mu+\epsilon>0\}
, we have:

\mathbb{E}[S\mid X]=\mathbb{P}(U>0\mid X)=\mathbb{P}(\epsilon>-\mu)=1-F(-\mu)=F(\mu),

where the last equality follows from the symmetry of
\epsilon
.

Putting it all together:

\mathbb{E}[S\mid A,X]=\mathbb{E}[S\mid\mu(X)]=F(\mu(X))=F(g(A))=\mathbb{E}[S\mid A],

which establishes Amenability Mediation. Since
g
is monotonic, Amenability Monotonicity also holds. ∎

Derivation of Analytical Tool for Causal Ordering and Classification from Baseline Choices

We derive here Equations (
22
), (
23
), (
24
), and (
25
).

Let
\beta_{\text{max}}
be the maximum CATE. Based on Equation (
16
) and the logistic distribution of
U
, the CATE is largest when the expected value of
U
given
X
is
\mu_{\beta}=-\delta/2
. This implies that:

\displaystyle\beta_{\max}

\displaystyle=\tanh(\delta/4).

Given the above, we can use the properties of the inverse of the hyperbolic tangent to obtain
\delta=2\ln((1+\beta_{\max})/(1-\beta_{\max}))
. For simplicity of notation, we call
{r=(1+\beta_{\max})/(1-\beta_{\max})}
.

The logit response model implies that
\theta(x)=(1+\exp(-\mu(x)))^{-1}
. Let
\theta_{\beta}
be the score of individuals with CATE
\beta_{\max}
. This score is then:

\theta_{\beta}=(1+\exp(-\mu_{\beta}))^{-1}=(1+r)^{-1}=\frac{1-\beta_{\text{max}}}{2},

which corresponds to the right-hand side of Equations (
22
) and (
24
). CATEs increase for scores less than
\theta_{\beta}
and decrease for scores greater than
\theta_{\beta}
. Thus, latent monotonicity is satisfied if either
\theta_{\text{max}}<\theta_{\beta}
or
\theta_{\text{min}}>\theta_{\beta}
. In the former scenario, larger scores imply larger effects, while in the latter, smaller scores imply smaller effects.

We now examine the case where latent monotonicity is violated, so
\theta_{\text{max}}>\theta_{\beta}
and
\theta_{\text{min}}<\theta_{\beta}
. As depicted in Figure
3
, the CATE
\beta
is a function of
\mu
, which is unimodal and symmetrical around
\mu_{\beta}
.

Let
\mu_{\text{max}}
and
\mu_{\text{min}}
be the expected utility for the scores
\theta_{\max}
and
\theta_{\min}
, respectively. If
\theta
is concentrated at lower values, we want to find a score
\tilde{\tau}^{+}
that corresponds to a utility
\mu_{\tilde{\tau}}^{+}
such that
\mu_{\tilde{\tau}}^{+}<\mu_{\beta}
and
\tilde{\tau}^{+}=\beta(\mu_{\tilde{\tau}}^{+})=\beta(\mu_{\text{max}})
. The reasoning is as follows:

1.

For scores smaller than
\tilde{\tau}^{+}
, the CATE will be smaller. This is because monotonicity holds for any
\mu
smaller than
\mu_{\tilde{\tau}}^{+}
(given that
\mu_{\tilde{\tau}}^{+}<\mu_{\beta}
).

2.

For scores larger than
\tilde{\tau}^{+}
, the CATE will be larger. This is because
\beta(\mu_{\tilde{\tau}}^{+})=\beta(\mu_{\text{max}})
represents the smallest CATE for any
\mu
greater than
\mu_{\tilde{\tau}}^{+}
. The CATE increases from
\mu_{\tilde{\tau}}^{+}
to
\mu_{\beta}
and then decreases until
\mu_{\text{max}}
.

Therefore, unbiased classification holds for any
k
such that
\tilde{\tau}_{k}<\tilde{\tau}^{+}
.

Alternatively, if
\theta
is concentrated at higher values, smaller scores should be interpreted as corresponding to larger effects. In this case, we want to find a score
\tilde{\tau}^{-}
such that
\mu_{\tilde{\tau}}^{-}>\mu_{\beta}
and
\tilde{\tau}^{-}=\beta(\mu_{\tilde{\tau}}^{-})=\beta(\mu_{\text{min}})
. The rationale is:

1.

For scores larger than
\tilde{\tau}^{-}
, the CATE will be smaller. Monotonicity holds for any
\mu
larger than
\mu_{\tilde{\tau}}^{-}
because
\mu_{\tilde{\tau}}^{-}>\mu_{\beta}
.

2.

For scores smaller than
\tilde{\tau}^{-}
, the CATE will be larger. This is because
\beta(\mu_{\tilde{\tau}}^{-})=\beta(\mu_{\text{min}})
is the smallest CATE for any
\mu
smaller than
\mu_{\tilde{\tau}}^{-}
. The CATE increases from
\mu_{\text{min}}
to
\mu_{\beta}
and then decreases monotonically.

Therefore, unbiased classification holds for any
k
such that
\tilde{\tau}_{k}^{-}>\tilde{\tau}^{-}
, meaning the
k
individuals with the smallest scores correspond to those with the
k
largest CATEs.

To find
\tilde{\tau}^{+}
we first obtain
\mu_{\tilde{\tau}}^{+}
using the symmetry of the CATE around
\mu_{\beta}
. Specifically, if
\mu_{\tilde{\tau}}^{+}<\mu_{\beta}
and
\beta(\mu_{\tilde{\tau}}^{+})=\beta(\mu_{\text{max}})
, then:

\displaystyle\mu_{\tilde{\tau}}^{+}=\mu_{\beta}-(\mu_{\text{max}}-\mu_{\beta})=2\mu_{\beta}-\mu_{\text{max}}.

Similarly, if
\mu_{\tilde{\tau}}^{-}>\mu_{\beta}
and
\beta(\mu_{\tilde{\tau}}^{-})=\beta(\mu_{\text{min}})
, then:

\displaystyle\mu_{\tilde{\tau}}^{-}=\mu_{\beta}+(\mu_{\beta}-\mu_{\text{min}})=2\mu_{\beta}-\mu_{\text{min}}.

The definition of
\theta
implies:

\mu=\log\left(\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}\right).

We can then obtain
\tilde{\tau}^{+}
as follows:

\displaystyle\tilde{\tau}^{+}

\displaystyle=(1+\exp(-\mu_{\tilde{\tau}}^{+}))^{-1}

\displaystyle=(1+\exp(-2\mu_{\beta}+\mu_{\text{max}}))^{-1}

\displaystyle=(1+\exp(\delta+\mu_{\text{max}}))^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left(1+\left(\frac{1+\beta_{\text{max}}}{1-\beta_{\text{max}}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{\theta_{\text{max}}}{1-\theta_{\text{max}}}\right)\right)^{-1},

where the first line comes from the definition of
\theta
, the second from the definition of
\mu_{\tilde{\tau}}^{+}
, the third from the definition of
\mu_{\beta}
, and the last from the definition of
\delta
and
\mu_{\text{max}}
. This expression corresponds to Equation (
23
) and is the largest value that
\tau_{k}^{+}
can have while still maintaining unbiased classification. This expression is decreasing in
\beta_{\text{max}}
, so the range of
k
values for which unbiased classification holds is more conservative when
\beta_{\text{max}}^{+}>\beta_{\text{max}}
.

Using the same line of reasoning as above, we can obtain
\tilde{\tau}^{-}
:

\displaystyle\tilde{\tau}^{-}

\displaystyle=\left(1+\left(\frac{1+\beta_{\text{max}}}{1-\beta_{\text{max}}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{\theta_{\text{min}}}{1-\theta_{\text{min}}}\right)\right)^{-1}.

This expression corresponds to Equation (
25
) and is the smallest value that
\tau_{k}^{-}
can have while still maintaining unbiased classification. This expression is decreasing in
\beta_{\text{max}}
, so the range of
k
values for which unbiased classification holds is more conservative when
\beta_{\text{max}}^{-}<\beta_{\text{max}}
.
