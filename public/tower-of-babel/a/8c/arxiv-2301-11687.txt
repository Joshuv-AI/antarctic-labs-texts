Theoretical Analysis of Offline Imitation With Supplementary Dataset
Ziniu Li, Tian Xu ∗, Yang Yu, Zhi-Quan Luo †

Abstract
Behavioral cloning (BC) can recover a good policy from abundant expert data, but may fail when expert data is insufficient. This paper considers a situation where, besides the small amount of expert data, a supplementary dataset is available, which can be collected cheaply from sub-optimal policies. Imitation learning with a supplementary dataset is an emergent practical framework, but its theoretical foundation remains under-developed. To advance understanding, we first investigate a direct extension of BC, called NBCU, that learns from the union of all available data. Our analysis shows that, although NBCU suffers an imitation gap that is larger than BC in the worst case, there exist special cases where NBCU performs better than or equally well as BC. This discovery implies that noisy data can also be helpful if utilized elaborately. Therefore, we further introduce a discriminator-based importance sampling technique to re-weight the supplementary data, proposing the WBCU method. With our newly developed landscape-based analysis , we prove that WBCU can outperform BC in mild conditions. Empirical studies show that WBCU simultaneously achieves the best performance on two challenging tasks where prior state-of-the-art methods fail.

Introduction

Imitation learning (IL) methods train a good policy from expert demonstrations
(
Argall et al. 2009
;
Osa et al. 2018
)
. One popular approach is behavioral cloning (BC)
(
Pomerleau 1991
)
, which imitates the expert via supervised learning. Specifically, in IL, samples usually refer to state-action pairs/sequences from trajectories in the given dataset. Both the quality and quantity of trajectories are crucial to achieving satisfying performance. For instance, it is found that BC performs well when the dataset has a large amount of expert-level trajectories; see, e.g.,
(
Spencer et al. 2021
)
. Nevertheless, due to the compounding errors issue
(
Ross and Bagnell 2010
)
, any offline IL algorithm, including BC, will fail when the number of expert trajectories is small
(
Rajaraman et al. 2020
;
Xu et al. 2021a
)
. To address the compounding errors issue, a naive solution is to ask the expert to collect more trajectories. However, querying the expert is expensive and intractable in applications such as healthcare and industrial control.

To address the mentioned failure mode, we follow an alternative and emergent framework proposed in
(
Kim et al. 2022b
;
Xu et al. 2022a
)
, which assumes, in addition to the expert dataset, a supplementary dataset is relatively cheap to obtain. In particular, this supplementary dataset could be previously collected by certain behavior policies; as such, it has lots of expert and sub-optimal trajectories. The key challenge here is to figure out which supplemental samples are helpful. We realize that a few advances have been achieved in this direction
(
Kim et al. 2022b
;
Kim et al. 2022a
;
Xu et al. 2022a
;
Ma et al. 2022
)
. To leverage the supplementary dataset, a majority of algorithms train a discriminator to distinguish expert-style and sub-optimal samples, upon which a weighted BC objective is optimized to learn a good policy. For instance,
Kim et al. 2022b
proposed the DemoDICE algorithm, which learns the discriminator via a regularized state-action distribution matching objective. As for the DWBC algorithm in
(
Xu et al. 2022a
)
, the policy and discriminator are jointly trained under a cooperation framework.

Though prior algorithms are empirically shown to perform well in some scenarios, the theoretical foundation of this new imitation problem remains under-developed. Specifically, researchers have a sketchy intuition that noisy demonstrations in the supplementary dataset may hurt the performance when we directly apply BC, and researchers hope to develop algorithms to overcome this challenge. However, the following questions have not been carefully answered yet: (
Q1
) precisely, what kind of noisy samples is harmful? (or what kind of supplementary samples is helpful?)
(Q2)
how to design algorithms in a principled way? Answers could deepen our understanding and provide insights for future advances.

In this paper, we explore the above questions by investigating two (independent) variants of BC. To learn a policy, both algorithms apply a BC objective on the
union
dataset (the concatenation of the expert and supplementary dataset), but they differ in assigning weights of samples that appear in the loss function. To qualify the benefits of the supplementary dataset, we treat the BC
only
with the expert dataset as a baseline to compare.

The first algorithm, called NBCU (naive BC with union dataset), assigns uniform weights for all samples. As a direct extension of BC, the theoretical study of NBCU provides answers to (Q1). In particular, we show that NBCU suffers an imitation gap that is larger than BC in the

Related Work

Adversarial Imitation Learning.
Unlike BC, adversarial imitation learning (AIL) methods (e.g., GAIL
(
Ho and Ermon 2016
)
) do not suffer the compounding errors issue when the expert trajectories are limited; see the empirical evidence in
(
Ho and Ermon 2016
;
Ghasemipour et al. 2019
;
Kostrikov et al. 2019
)
and theoretical support in
(
Xu et al. 2020
;
Xu et al. 2022b
)
. In particular, AIL methods train a discriminator to perform the state-action distribution matching, which differs from BC. Nevertheless, AIL methods naturally work in the online setting (i.e., the interaction is allowed). If the transition function is not available, BC is minimax optimal
(
Rajaraman et al. 2020
;
Xu et al. 2021b
)
and offline counterparts of AIL are not better than BC
(
Li et al. 2022
)
.

Perhaps surprisingly, we find that the discriminator used in our WBCU algorithm plays a different role than AIL methods. We provide a detailed explanation in
Section

5
.

Imitation Learning with Imperfect Demonstrations.
The problem considered in this paper is closely related to imitation learning (IL) with imperfect demonstrations
(
Wu et al. 2019
;
Brown et al. 2019
;
Tangkaratt et al. 2020
;
Wang et al. 2021
;
Sasaki and Yamashina 2021
;
Liu et al. 2022
)
in the sense that the supplementary dataset can also be viewed as imperfect demonstrations. However, our problem setting differs from IL with imperfect demonstrations in two key aspects. First, in IL with imperfect demonstrations, they either pose strong assumptions
(
Tangkaratt et al. 2020
;
Sasaki and Yamashina 2021
;
Liu et al. 2022
)
or require auxiliary information (e.g., confidence scores on imperfect trajectories) on the imperfect dataset
(
Wu et al. 2019
;
Brown et al. 2019
)
. In contrast, we assume access to a small number of expert trajectories, which could be more practical. Second, most works
(
Wu et al. 2019
;
Brown et al. 2019
;
Tangkaratt et al. 2020
;
Wang et al. 2021
)
in IL with imperfect demonstrations require online environment interactions while we focus on the offline setting.

Finally, we point out that the problem of offline imitation learning with a supplementary dataset is first considered in
(
Kim et al. 2022b
;
Xu et al. 2022a
)
. Subsequently,
Ma et al. 2022
;
Kim et al. 2022a
study a related setting: learning from observation, where actions are missing and only states are observed. Existing works mainly focus on empirical studies, and the theoretical foundation remains under-developed.

Imitation Learning.
The goal of imitation learning is to learn a high quality policy directly from expert demonstrations. To this end, we often assume there is a nearly optimal expert policy
\pi^{\operatorname{E}}
that could interact with the environment to generate a dataset (i.e.,
N_{\operatorname{E}}
trajectories of length
H
):

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}=\big\{

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}=\left(s_{1},a_{1},s_{2},a_{2},\cdots,s_{H},a_{H}\right);s_{1}\sim\rho,a_{h}\sim\pi^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}(\cdot|s_{h}),s_{h+1}\sim P_{h}(\cdot|s_{h},a_{h}),\forall h\in[H]\big\}.

Then, the learner can use
{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}
to mimic the expert. From a theoretical perspective, the quality of imitation is measured by the

:
\mathbb{E}\left[V(\pi^{\operatorname{E}})-V(\pi)\right]
, where
\pi
is the learned policy and the expectation is taken over the randomness of
{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}
. We hope that a good learner can mimic the expert perfectly and thus the imitation gap is small. In this paper, we assume that the expert policy is deterministic, which is common in the literature
(
Rajaraman et al. 2020
;
Rajaraman et al. 2021
;
Xu et al. 2020
;
Xu et al. 2021b
)
. Note that this assumption holds for tasks including MuJoCo locomotion control.

Behavioral Cloning.
Given an
expert

{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}
, the behavioral cloning (BC) algorithm takes the maximum likelihood estimation:

\displaystyle\pi^{\operatorname{BC}}\in\max_{\pi}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}}\widehat{d^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}}(s,a)\log\pi_{h}(a|s),

(1)

where
\widehat{d^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}}(s,a)
is the empirical state-action distribution. By definition,
\widehat{d^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}}(s,a)=n_{h}^{\operatorname{E}}(s,a)/N_{\operatorname{E}}

refers to the number of expert trajectories such that their state-action pairs are equal to

in time step

\displaystyle\pi^{\operatorname{BC}}_{h}(a|s)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{n_{h}^{\operatorname{E}}(s,a)}{n_{h}^{\operatorname{E}}(s)}&\text{if }n_{h}^{\operatorname{E}}(s)>0\\
\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{A}}|}&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

Imitation with Supplementary Dataset.
BC will fail when the number of expert trajectories is small due to the mentioned compounding errors issue. A naive solution is to collect more expert trajectories. In this paper, we follow an alternative and emergent framework in
(
Kim et al. 2022b
;
Xu et al. 2022a
)
, where we assume that an offline

(i.e.,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{S}}=\big\{

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}=\left(s_{1},a_{1},s_{2},a_{2},\cdots,s_{H},a_{H}\right);s_{1}\sim\rho,a_{h}\sim\pi^{\beta}_{h}(\cdot|s_{h}),s_{h+1}\sim P_{h}(\cdot|s_{h},a_{h}),\forall h\in[H]\big\},

where
\pi^{\beta}
is a behavioral policy that could be a mixture of certain base policies, i.e.,
\pi^{\beta}=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\alpha_{i}\pi^{i}

\alpha_{i}\geq 0
for

Analysis of Weighted Behavioral Cloning with Union Dataset

In this section, we explore an alternative approach to leverage the expert and supplementary datasets. In light of
(
Kim et al. 2022b
;
Xu et al. 2022a
)
, a discriminator is trained to score samples, upon which a weighted BC objective is used for policy optimization:

\displaystyle\pi^{\operatorname{WBCU}}\in\mathop{\rm argmax}_{\pi}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}}\bigg\{\widehat{d^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}}(s,a)\times\left[w_{h}(s,a)\log\pi_{h}(a|s)\right]\times{\mathbb{I}}\left[w_{h}(s,a)\geq\delta\right]\bigg\},

(8)

where
\widehat{d^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}}(s,a)
is the empirical state-action distribution estimated from
{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}
,
w_{h}(s,a)\in[0,\infty)
is the weight decided by the discriminator, and
\delta\in[0,\infty)
is a hyper-parameter. We point out that
\delta
is introduced for theoretical analysis, and in practice we set
\delta=0
. We call this approach WBCU (weighted BC with the union dataset).

Our key idea is the importance sampling technique
(
Shapiro 2003
, Chapter 9)
, which can transfer samples in the union dataset under the expert policy distribution. In this way, WBCU is expected to address the failure mode of NBCU. We elaborate on this point as follows. In the population level (i.e., there are infinitely samples), we would have
\widehat{d^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}}=d^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}
, which is jointly determined by the expert policy and the behavioral policy. In this case, if
w_{h}(s,a)=d^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}(s,a)/d^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}(s,a)
, we would have
\widehat{d^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}}(s,a)w_{h}(s,a)=d^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}(s,a)
. Accordingly, the objective (
8
) is to learn a policy as if samples were collected by the expert policy. In practice,
d^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}(s,a)
and
d^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}(s,a)
are unknown; instead, we only have finite samples from these two distributions. Therefore, we need to estimate the grounded importance sampling ratio
d^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}(s,a)/d^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}(s,a)
.

We emphasize that a simple two-step idea—first estimating
d^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}(s,a)
and
d^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}(s,a)
separately and then calculating their quotient—is intractable. This is because it is difficult to accurately estimate the probability density of high-dimensional distributions. Following the seminal idea in
(
Goodfellow et al. 2014
)
, we take a one-step approach: we directly train a discriminator to estimate
d^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}(s,a)/d^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}(s,a)
. Concretely, we consider time-dependent parameterized discriminators
\{c_{h}:{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow(0,1)\}_{h=1}^{H}
, each of which has an objective

\displaystyle\max_{c_{h}}

\displaystyle\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}}\widehat{d^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}}(s,a)\left[\log\left(c_{h}(s,a)\right)\right]+\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}}\widehat{d^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}}(s,a)\left[\log\left(1-c_{h}(s,a)\right)\right].

(9)

The above problem amounts to training a binary classifier (i.e., the logistic regression). In the population level, with the first-order optimality condition, we have

\displaystyle c^{\star}_{h}(s,a)=\frac{{d^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}}(s,a)}{{d^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}}(s,a)+{d^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}}(s,a)}.

(10)

Then, we can obtain the importance sampling ratio
d^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}(s,a)/d^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}(s,a)
in the following way:

\displaystyle w_{h}(s,a)=\frac{c^{\star}_{h}(s,a)}{1-c^{\star}_{h}(s,a)}.

(11)

5.1
Negative Result of WBCU With Tabular Representation

In this section, we consider parameterizing the discriminator

). We present a surprising counter-intuitive result.

Proposition 2

In the tabular case (with
\delta=0

\pi^{\operatorname{WBCU}}=\pi^{\operatorname{BC}}
.

2

We highlight that this failure mode is because the discriminator has no extrapolation ability in this case. Specifically, for an expert-style sample
(s,a)
that
only
appears in the supplementary dataset, we have
\widehat{d^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}}(s,a)=0
and
\widehat{d^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}}(s,a)>0

. That is, such a good sample does not contribute to the learning objective (
8
). Intuitively, the tabular representation simply treats samples in a discrete way, which ignores the correlation between samples.

5.2
Positive Result of WBCU With Function Approximation

To bypass the hurdle in the previous section, we investigate WBCU with certain function approximation in this section. To avoid the tabular/discrete representation, we will consider “smooth” function approximators, which can model the internal correlation between samples. Specifically, we consider the discriminator to be parameterized by

\displaystyle c_{h}(s,a;\theta_{h})=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-\langle\phi_{h}(s,a),\theta_{h}\rangle)},

(13)

where
\phi_{h}(s,a)\in\real^{d}
is the feature vector (that can be learned by neural networks), and
\theta_{h}\in\real^{d}
is the parameter to train. Accordingly, the optimization problem becomes:

\displaystyle\min_{\theta_{h}}{\mathcal{L}}_{h}(\theta_{h})\triangleq\bigg\{\sum_{(s,a)}\widehat{d^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}}(s,a)\left[\log\left(1+\exp\left(-\langle\phi_{h}(s,a),\theta_{h}\rangle\right)\right)\right]+\sum_{(s,a)}\widehat{d^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}}(s,a)\left[\log\left(1+\exp\left(\langle\phi_{h}(s,a),\theta_{h}\rangle\right)\right)\right]\bigg\}.

(14)

Let

be the optimal solution obtained from
Equation

We clarify that to be consistent with the previous results, the policy is still based on the tabular representation. We discuss the general function approximation of policy in

Conclusion

We theoretically explore imitation learning with a supplementary dataset, and empirical results corroborate our findings. While our results have several desirable features, they also have shortcomings. One limitation is that we consider the tabular representation in policy learning. However, our theoretical implications may remain unchanged if function approximation is used. Please see
Appendix

C
for discussion, which deserves further investigation.

Exploring more applications of NBCU and WBCU is an interesting future work. For example, for large language models
(
Radford et al. 2018

, we may have massive supplementary samples from the Web, while examples with human annotations are limited. Compared with the existing reinforcement-learning-based methods (see, e.g.,
(
Stiennon et al. 2020
)
), training good policies may be easier and more efficient by the developed imitation learning approaches.

\pi^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}(a^{1}|s)=1,\forall s\in{\mathcal{S}},h\in[H],\;\pi^{\beta}_{h}(a^{2}|s)=1,\forall s\in{\mathcal{S}},h\in[H]
. It is direct to calculate that
V(\pi^{\operatorname{E}})=H
and
V(\pi^{\beta})=0
. The supplementary dataset
{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{S}}
and the expert dataset
{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}
are collected according to
1
. The mixture state-action distribution can be calculated as
\forall s\in{\mathcal{S}},h\in[H]
,

\displaystyle d^{\operatorname{mix}}_{h}(s,a^{1})=\eta d^{\pi^{\operatorname{E}}}_{h}(s,a^{1})+(1-\eta)d^{\pi^{\beta}}_{h}(s,a^{1})=\eta d^{\pi^{\operatorname{E}}}_{h}(s,a^{1})=\eta\rho(s),

\displaystyle d^{\operatorname{mix}}_{h}(s,a^{2})=\eta d^{\pi^{\operatorname{E}}}_{h}(s,a^{2})+(1-\eta)d^{\pi^{\beta}}_{h}(s,a^{2})=(1-\eta)d^{\pi^{\beta}}_{h}(s,a^{2})=(1-\eta)\rho(s).

Note that in the population level, the marginal distribution of the union dataset
{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{U}}
in time step
h
is exactly
d^{\operatorname{mix}}_{h}
. The mixture policy induced by
d^{\operatorname{mix}}
can be formulated as

\displaystyle\pi^{\operatorname{mix}}_{h}(a^{1}|s)=\eta,\pi^{\operatorname{mix}}_{h}(a^{2}|s)=1-\eta,\forall s\in{\mathcal{S}},h\in[H].

Just like before, we have
d^{\pi^{\operatorname{mix}}}_{h}(s,a)=d^{\operatorname{mix}}_{h}(s,a)
. The policy value of
\pi^{\operatorname{mix}}
can be calculated as

\displaystyle V(\pi^{\operatorname{mix}})=\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}}d^{\operatorname{mix}}_{h}(s,a)r_{h}(s,a)=\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}}d^{\operatorname{mix}}_{h}(s,a^{1})=\eta H.

The policy
\pi^{\operatorname{NBCU}}
can be formulated as

\displaystyle\forall h\in[H],\quad\pi^{\operatorname{NBCU}}_{h}(a|s)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{n^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}(s,a)}{\sum_{a^{\prime}}n^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}(s,a^{\prime})}&\text{if}\sum_{a^{\prime}}n^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}(s,a^{\prime})>0\\
\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{A}}|}&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

We can view that the BC’s policy learned on the union dataset mimics the mixture policy
\pi^{\operatorname{mix}}
. In the following part, we analyze the lower bound on the imitation gap of
\pi^{\operatorname{NBCU}}
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[V(\pi^{\operatorname{E}})-V(\pi^{\operatorname{NBCU}})\right]

\displaystyle=V(\pi^{\operatorname{E}})-V(\pi^{\operatorname{mix}})+\mathbb{E}\left[V(\pi^{\operatorname{mix}})-V(\pi^{\operatorname{NBCU}})\right]

\displaystyle=H-\eta H+\mathbb{E}\left[V(\pi^{\operatorname{mix}})-V(\pi^{\operatorname{NBCU}})\right]

\displaystyle=(1-\eta)(V(\pi^{\operatorname{E}})-V(\pi^{\beta}))+\mathbb{E}\left[V(\pi^{\operatorname{mix}})-V(\pi^{\operatorname{NBCU}})\right].

Then we consider the term
\mathbb{E}\left[V(\pi^{\operatorname{mix}})-V(\pi^{\operatorname{NBCU}})\right]
.

\displaystyle V(\pi^{\operatorname{mix}})-V(\pi^{\operatorname{NBCU}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}}\left(d^{\pi^{\operatorname{mix}}}_{h}(s,a)-d^{\pi^{\operatorname{NBCU}}}_{h}(s,a)\right)r_{h}(s,a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}}\rho(s)\left(\pi^{\operatorname{mix}}_{h}(a|s)-\pi^{\operatorname{NBCU}}_{h}(a|s)\right)r_{h}(s,a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}}\rho(s)\left(\pi^{\operatorname{mix}}_{h}(a|s)-\pi^{\operatorname{NBCU}}_{h}(a|s)\right)r_{h}(s,a)\mathbb{I}\{n^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}(s)>0\}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}}\rho(s)\left(\pi^{\operatorname{mix}}_{h}(a|s)-\pi^{\operatorname{NBCU}}_{h}(a|s)\right)r_{h}(s,a)\mathbb{I}\{n^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}(s)=0\}.

We take expectation over the randomness in
{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{U}}
on both sides and obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[V(\pi^{\operatorname{mix}})-V(\pi^{\operatorname{NBCU}})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}}\rho(s)\left(\pi^{\operatorname{mix}}_{h}(a|s)-\pi^{\operatorname{NBCU}}_{h}(a|s)\right)r_{h}(s,a)\mathbb{I}\{n^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}(s)>0\}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}}\rho(s)\left(\pi^{\operatorname{mix}}_{h}(a|s)-\pi^{\operatorname{NBCU}}_{h}(a|s)\right)r_{h}(s,a)\mathbb{I}\{n^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}(s)=0\}\right].

(18)

For the first term in RHS, we have that

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}}\rho(s)\left(\pi^{\operatorname{mix}}_{h}(a|s)-\pi^{\operatorname{NBCU}}_{h}(a|s)\right)r_{h}(s,a)\mathbb{I}\{n^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}(s)>0\}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}}\rho(s)r_{h}(s,a)\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\pi^{\operatorname{mix}}_{h}(a|s)-\pi^{\operatorname{NBCU}}_{h}(a|s)\right)\mathbb{I}\{n^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}(s)>0\}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}}\rho(s)r_{h}(s,a){\mathbb{P}}\left(n^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}(s)>0\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\pi^{\operatorname{mix}}_{h}(a|s)-\pi^{\operatorname{NBCU}}_{h}(a|s)\right)\bigg|n^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}(s)>0\right]

\displaystyle=0.

The last equation follows the fact that when
n^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}(s)>0
,
\pi^{\operatorname{NBCU}}_{h}(a|s)
is an unbiased maximum likelihood estimation of
\pi^{\operatorname{mix}}_{h}(a|s)
. For the remaining term, we have that

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}}\rho(s)\left(\pi^{\operatorname{mix}}_{h}(a|s)-\pi^{\operatorname{NBCU}}_{h}(a|s)\right)r_{h}(s,a)\mathbb{I}\{n^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}(s)=0\}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}}\rho(s)r_{h}(s,a)\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\pi^{\operatorname{mix}}_{h}(a|s)-\pi^{\operatorname{NBCU}}_{h}(a|s)\right)\mathbb{I}\{n^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}(s)=0\}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}}\rho(s)r_{h}(s,a){\mathbb{P}}\left(n^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}(s)=0\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\pi^{\operatorname{mix}}_{h}(a|s)-\pi^{\operatorname{NBCU}}_{h}(a|s)\right)\bigg|n^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}(s)=0\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}}\rho(s)r_{h}(s,a){\mathbb{P}}\left(n^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}(s)=0\right)\left(\pi^{\operatorname{mix}}_{h}(a|s)-\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{A}}|}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}}\rho(s){\mathbb{P}}\left(n^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}(s)=0\right)\left(\eta-\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{A}}|}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{=}H\left(\eta-\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{A}}|}\right)\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}}\rho(s){\mathbb{P}}\left(n^{\operatorname{U}}_{1}(s)=0\right).

In the equation
(a)
, we use the fact that
r_{h}(s,a^{1})=1,r_{h}(s,a)=0,\forall a\in{\mathcal{A}}\setminus\{a^{1}\}
. In the equation
(b)
, since each state is an absorbing state, we have that
{\mathbb{P}}\left(n^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}(s)=0\right)={\mathbb{P}}\left(n^{\operatorname{U}}_{1}(s)=0\right),\forall h\in[H]
.

We consider two cases. In the first case of
\eta\geq 1/|{\mathcal{A}}|
, we directly have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}}\rho(s)\left(\pi^{\operatorname{mix}}_{h}(a|s)-\pi^{\operatorname{NBCU}}_{h}(a|s)\right)r_{h}(s,a)\mathbb{I}\{n^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}(s)=0\}\right]\geq 0.

By
Equation

18
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[V(\pi^{\operatorname{mix}})-V(\pi^{\operatorname{NBCU}})\right]\geq 0,

which implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[V(\pi^{\operatorname{E}})-V(\pi^{\operatorname{NBCU}})\right]\geq(1-\eta)(V(\pi^{\operatorname{E}})-V(\pi^{\beta})).

In the second case of
\eta<1/|{\mathcal{A}}|
, we have that

\displaystyle H\left(\eta-\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{A}}|}\right)\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}}\rho(s){\mathbb{P}}\left(n^{\operatorname{U}}_{1}(s)=0\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\geq}-\left(\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{A}}|}-\eta\right)H\exp\left(-\frac{N_{\operatorname{tot}}}{|{\mathcal{S}}|}\right)

\displaystyle\geq-(1-\eta)H\exp\left(-\frac{N_{\operatorname{tot}}}{|{\mathcal{S}}|}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\geq}-\frac{(1-\eta)H}{2}.

In the inequality
(a)
, we use that

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}}\rho(s){\mathbb{P}}\left(n^{\operatorname{U}}_{1}(s)=0\right)=\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}}\rho(s)(1-\rho(s))^{N_{\operatorname{tot}}}=\left(1-\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{S}}|}\right)^{N_{\operatorname{tot}}}\leq\exp\left(-\frac{N_{\operatorname{tot}}}{|{\mathcal{S}}|}\right).

The inequality
(b)
holds since we consider the range where
N_{\operatorname{tot}}\geq|{\mathcal{S}}|\log(2)
. By
Equation

18
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[V(\pi^{\operatorname{mix}})-V(\pi^{\operatorname{NBCU}})\right]\geq-\frac{(1-\eta)H}{2}.

This implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[V(\pi^{\operatorname{E}})-V(\pi^{\operatorname{NBCU}})\right]

\displaystyle\geq(1-\eta)(V(\pi^{\operatorname{E}})-V(\pi^{\beta}))-\frac{(1-\eta)H}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-\eta)}{2}(V(\pi^{\operatorname{E}})-V(\pi^{\beta})).

In both cases, we prove that
\mathbb{E}\left[V(\pi^{\operatorname{E}})-V(\pi^{\operatorname{NBCU}})\right]\gtrsim(1-\eta)(V(\pi^{\operatorname{E}})-V(\pi^{\beta}))
and thus complete the proof.

A.5
Proof of Theorem

[
Kakade and Langford 2002

\pi

{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}

\displaystyle=\min\{\lambda_{i^{t}}(t):\forall t\in[0,1]\}

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\min\{\min\{\lambda_{i^{t}}(0),\lambda_{i^{t}}(1)\}:\forall t\in[0,1]\}

\displaystyle=\min\{\lambda_{i^{0}}(0),\lambda_{i^{1}}(1)\}

\displaystyle\min\{\lambda_{\min}(\nabla^{2}G(B_{h}\theta^{t})):\forall t\in[0,1]\}=\min\{\lambda_{\min}(\nabla^{2}G(B_{h}\theta^{0})),\lambda_{\min}(\nabla^{2}G(B_{h}\theta^{1}))\},

(21)

which proves the previous claim.

Further, we consider

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}\left(\nabla^{2}{\mathcal{L}}_{h}(\theta_{h})\right)

\displaystyle=\inf_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:\left\|x\right\|=1}x^{\top}\nabla^{2}{\mathcal{L}}_{h}(\theta_{h})x

B.4
Proof of Theorem

First, invoking

B.6
Additional Analysis of WBCU With Function Approximation

Here we provide an additional theoretical result for WBCU with function approximation when
d=1
.

Theorem 6
.

Consider
d=1
. For any
h\in[H]
, if the following inequality holds

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{S}}|}\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{S}}_{h}}\langle\phi_{h}(s,a),\bar{\theta}_{h}\rangle<\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|}\sum_{(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}}\langle\phi_{h}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}),\bar{\theta}_{h}\rangle.

(22)

then we have that
\Delta_{h}(\theta^{\star}_{h})>0
. Furthermore, the inequality (
22
) is also a necessary condition.

Theorem
6
provides a clean and sharp condition to guarantee that the learned discriminator can perfectly classify the high-quality samples from
{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}
and
{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{S},1}_{h}
, and low-quality samples from
{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{S},2}_{h}
. In particular, inequality (
22
) means that by the ground truth parameter
\bar{\theta}_{h}
, the average score of samples in supplementary dataset is lower than that of samples in expert dataset. This condition is easy to satisfy because the supplementary dataset contains bad samples from
{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{S},2}_{h}
whose scores are much lower. In contrast,
{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}
only contains expert-type samples with high scores.

Proof.

Recall the objective function

\displaystyle\min_{\theta_{h}}{\mathcal{L}}_{h}(\theta_{h})=\sum_{(s,a)}\widehat{d^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}}(s,a)\log\left(1+\exp\left(-\langle\phi_{h}(s,a),\theta_{h}\rangle\right)\right)+\sum_{(s,a)}\widehat{d^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}}(s,a)\log\left(1+\exp\left(\langle\phi_{h}(s,a),\theta_{h}\rangle\right)\right).

Consider the function
f(x)=\log(1+\exp(x))
. We have that
f^{\prime\prime}(x)=\sigma(x)(1-\sigma(x))\geq 0
, where
\sigma(x)=1/(1+\exp(-x))
. As such,
f^{\prime\prime}(x)
is a convex function. Notice that the operations of composition with affine function and non-negative weighted sum preserve convexity
[
Boyd et al. 2004
]
. Therefore,
{\mathcal{L}}_{h}(\theta_{h})
is a convex function with respect to
\theta_{h}
. Then for any
\widetilde{\theta}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, it holds that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{h}(\theta_{h})\geq{\mathcal{L}}_{h}(\widetilde{\theta})+\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{h}(\widetilde{\theta})\left(\theta_{h}-\widetilde{\theta}\right).

Setting
\theta_{h}=\theta^{\star}_{h}
and
\widetilde{\theta}=0
yields that

\displaystyle\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{h}(0)\theta^{\star}_{h}\leq{\mathcal{L}}_{h}(\theta^{\star}_{h})-{\mathcal{L}}_{h}(0).

(23)

The gradient of
{\mathcal{L}}_{h}(\theta_{h})
is of the form

\displaystyle\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{h}(\theta_{h})=-\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}}\widehat{d^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}}(s,a)\sigma(-\langle\phi_{h}(s,a),\theta_{h}\rangle)\phi_{h}(s,a)+\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}}\widehat{d^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}}(s,a)\sigma\left(\langle\phi_{h}(s,a),\theta_{h}\rangle\right)\phi_{h}(s,a).

Here
\sigma(x)=1/(1+\exp(-x))
is the sigmoid function. Then we calculate the gradient at
\widetilde{\theta}=0
.

\displaystyle\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{h}(0)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}}\widehat{d^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}}(s,a)\sigma(0)\phi_{h}(s,a)+\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}}\widehat{d^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}}(s,a)\sigma\left(0\right)\phi_{h}(s,a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}}\phi_{h}(s,a)}{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{U}}|}-\frac{\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}}\phi_{h}(s,a)}{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{U}}|}\left(\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{U}}_{h}}\phi_{h}(s,a)-\frac{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{U}}|}{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|}\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}}\phi_{h}(s,a)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{U}}|}\left(\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{S}}_{h}}\phi_{h}(s,a)+\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}}\phi_{h}(s,a)-\frac{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{U}}|}{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|}\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}}\phi_{h}(s,a)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{U}}|}\left(\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{S}}_{h}}\phi_{h}(s,a)-\frac{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{S}}|}{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|}\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}}\phi_{h}(s,a)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{S}}|}{2|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{U}}|}\left(\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{S}}|}\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{S}}_{h}}\phi_{h}(s,a)-\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|}\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}}\phi_{h}(s,a)\right).

Furthermore, with inequality (
22
), we know that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{S}}|}\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{S}}_{h}}\phi_{h}(s,a)-\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|}\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}}\phi_{h}(s,a)\not=0.

). Similarly, we get that

\displaystyle\Delta_{h}(\bar{\theta}_{h})

\displaystyle=\min_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}\cup{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{S},1}_{h}}\bar{\theta}_{h}\phi_{h}(s,a)-\max_{(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{S},2}_{h}}\bar{\theta}_{h}\phi_{h}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\bar{\theta}_{h}\left(\max_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}\cup{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{S},1}_{h}}\phi_{h}(s,a)-\min_{(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{S},2}_{h}}\phi_{h}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right).

Then
\Delta_{h}(\bar{\theta}_{h})>0
implies that

\displaystyle\max_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}\cup{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{S},1}_{h}}\phi_{h}(s,a)<\min_{(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{S},2}_{h}}\phi_{h}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}).

(25)

Similar to the analysis in case I, we claim that
\theta^{\star}_{h}<0
. Otherwise, it holds that

\displaystyle\Delta_{h}(\theta^{\star}_{h})

\displaystyle=\min_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}\cup{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{S},1}_{h}}\theta^{\star}_{h}\phi_{h}(s,a)-\max_{(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{S},2}_{h}}\theta^{\star}_{h}\phi_{h}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\theta^{\star}_{h}\left(\min_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}\cup{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{S},1}_{h}}\phi_{h}(s,a)-\max_{(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{S},2}_{h}}\phi_{h}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle<0.

The last inequality follows
\theta^{\star}_{h}>0
and
\min_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}\cup{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{S},1}_{h}}\phi_{h}(s,a)-\max_{(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{S},2}_{h}}\phi_{h}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})<0
due to inequality (
25
). Thus, the above inequality conflicts with
\Delta_{h}(\theta^{\star}_{h})>0
. The claim that
\theta^{\star}_{h}<0
is proved and
\bar{\theta}_{h}\theta^{\star}_{h}>0
.

To summarize, we have proved that
\bar{\theta}_{h}\theta^{\star}_{h}>0
. Similar to the proof of the sufficient condition, by the convexity of
{\mathcal{L}}_{h}(\theta_{h})
and optimality of
\theta^{\star}_{h}
, we can obtain that

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{S}}|}\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{S}}_{h}}\phi_{h}(s,a)-\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|}\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}}\phi_{h}(s,a)\right)\theta^{\star}_{h}<0.

This directly implies that

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{S}}|}\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{S}}_{h}}\phi_{h}(s,a)-\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|}\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}}\phi_{h}(s,a)\right)\bar{\theta}_{h}<0,

which completes the proof of necessity.

∎

Appendix C
Behavioral Cloning with General Function Approximation

In the main text, the theoretical analysis for BC-based algorithms considers the tabular setting in policy learning where a table function represents the policy. Here we provide an analysis of BC with
general function approximation
in policy learning. Notice that the algorithms considered in this paper (i.e., BC, NBCU and WBCU) can be unified under the framework of maximum likelihood estimation (MLE)
11
11

11

Among these algorithms, the main difference is the weight function in the MLE objective; see Equations (
1
), (
4
) and (
12
).
. Therefore, the theoretical results in the main text can also be extended to the setting of general function approximation by a similar analysis.

We consider BC with general function approximation, which is the foundation of analyzing NBCU and WBCU. In particular, we assume access to a policy class
\Pi=\{\pi=(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\ldots,\pi_{h}):\;\pi_{h}\in\Pi_{h},\;\forall h\in[H]\}
and
\Pi_{h}=\{\pi_{h}:{\mathcal{S}}\rightarrow\Delta({\mathcal{A}})\}
, where
\pi_{h}
could be any function (e.g., neural networks). For simplicity of analysis, we assume that
\Pi
is a finite policy class. The objective of BC is still

\displaystyle\pi^{\operatorname{BC}}\in\max_{\pi\in\Pi}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{(s,a)\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}}\widehat{d^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}}(s,a)\log\pi_{h}(a|s),

but we do not have the analytic solution as in
Section

3
.

Theorem 7
.

Under
1
. In the general function approximation, additionally assume that
\pi^{\operatorname{E}}\in\Pi
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[V(\pi^{\operatorname{E}})-V(\pi^{\operatorname{BC}})\right]\lesssim\min\left\{H,H^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log(|\Pi|HN_{\operatorname{E}})}{N_{\operatorname{E}}}}\right\}

Compared with
1
, we find that the only change in theoretical bound is that
{\mathcal{O}}(|{\mathcal{S}}|/N_{\operatorname{E}})
is replaced with
{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{\log(|\Pi|)/N_{\operatorname{E}}})
. We note that such a change also holds for other algorithms (e.g., NBCU and WBCU). Therefore, our theoretical implications remains unchanged.

Proof of
7
.

We apply the policy difference lemma in
[
Kakade and Langford 2002
]
and obtain that

\displaystyle V(\pi^{\operatorname{E}})-V(\pi^{\operatorname{BC}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{h=1}^{H}\mathbb{E}_{s_{h}\sim d^{\pi^{\operatorname{E}}}_{h}(\cdot)}\left[Q^{\pi^{\operatorname{BC}}}_{h}(s_{h},\pi^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}(s_{h}))-\sum_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}}\pi^{\operatorname{BC}}_{h}(\cdot|s_{h})Q^{\pi^{\operatorname{BC}}}_{h}(s_{h},a)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{h=1}^{H}\mathbb{E}_{s_{h}\sim d^{\pi^{\operatorname{E}}}_{h}(\cdot)}\left[Q^{\pi^{\operatorname{BC}}}_{h}(s_{h},\pi^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}(s_{h}))(1-\pi^{\operatorname{BC}}_{h}(\pi^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}(s_{h})|s_{h}))\right]

\displaystyle\leq H\sum_{h=1}^{H}\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d^{\pi^{\operatorname{E}}}_{h}(\cdot)}\left[\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi^{\operatorname{BC}}_{h}(\cdot|s)}\left[\mathbb{I}\left\{a\not=\pi^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}(s)\right\}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=H\sum_{h=1}^{H}\mathbb{E}_{s_{h}\sim d^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}(\cdot)}\left[\mathrm{TV}\left(\pi^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}(\cdot|s_{h}),\pi^{\operatorname{BC}}_{h}(\cdot|s_{h})\right)\right].

With Theorem 21 in
[
Agarwal et al. 2020
]
, when
|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|\geq 1
, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
over the randomness within
{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{s_{h}\sim d^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}(\cdot)}\left[\mathrm{TV}^{2}\left(\pi^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}(\cdot|s_{h}),\pi^{\operatorname{BC}}_{h}(\cdot|s_{h})\right)\right]\leq 2\frac{\log(|\Pi|/\delta)}{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|}.

With union bound, with probability at least
1-\delta
, for all
h\in[H]
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{s_{h}\sim d^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}(\cdot)}\left[\mathrm{TV}^{2}\left(\pi^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}(\cdot|s_{h}),\pi^{\operatorname{BC}}_{h}(\cdot|s_{h})\right)\right]\leq 2\frac{\log(|\Pi|H/\delta)}{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|},

which implies that

\displaystyle V(\pi^{\operatorname{E}})-V(\pi^{\operatorname{BC}})

\displaystyle\leq H\sum_{h=1}^{H}\mathbb{E}_{s_{h}\sim d^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}(\cdot)}\left[\mathrm{TV}\left(\pi^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}(\cdot|s_{h}),\pi^{\operatorname{BC}}_{h}(\cdot|s_{h})\right)\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}H\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{s_{h}\sim d^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}(\cdot)}\left[\mathrm{TV}^{2}\left(\pi^{\operatorname{E}}_{h}(\cdot|s_{h}),\pi^{\operatorname{BC}}_{h}(\cdot|s_{h})\right)\right]}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2}H^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log(|\Pi|H/\delta)}{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|}}.

Inequality
(a)
follows Jensen’s inequality. Taking expectation over the randomness within
{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}
yields that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}}\left[V(\pi^{\operatorname{E}})-V(\pi^{\operatorname{BC}})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\delta H+(1-\delta)\sqrt{2}H^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log(|\Pi|H/\delta)}{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|}}

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\frac{H}{2|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|}+\left(1-\frac{1}{2|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|}\right)\sqrt{2}H^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log(2|\Pi|H|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|)}{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sqrt{2}+1\right)H^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log(2|\Pi|H|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|)}{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|}}

\displaystyle\leq 4H^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log(4|\Pi|H|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|)}{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|}}.

Equation
(a)
holds due to the choice that
\delta=1/(2|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|)
. For
|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|=0
, we directly have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}}\left[V(\pi^{\operatorname{E}})-V(\pi^{\operatorname{BC}})\right]\leq H.

Therefore, for any
|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|\geq 0
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}}\left[V(\pi^{\operatorname{E}})-V(\pi^{\operatorname{BC}})\right]\leq 4H^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log(4|\Pi|H\max\{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|,1\})}{\max\{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|,1\}}}.

We consider a real-valued function
f(x)=\log(cx)/x,\;\forall x\geq 1
, where
c=4|\Pi|H
. Its gradient function is
f^{\prime}(x)=(1-\log(cx))/x^{2}\leq 0,\;\forall x\geq 1
. Then we know that
f(x)
is decreasing as
x
increases. Furthermore, we have that
\max\{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|,1\}\geq(|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|+1)/2,\;\forall|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|\geq 0
. Then we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}}\left[V(\pi^{\operatorname{E}})-V(\pi^{\operatorname{BC}})\right]

\displaystyle\leq 4H^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log(4|\Pi|H\max\{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|,1\})}{\max\{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|,1\}}}

\displaystyle\leq 4H^{2}\sqrt{\frac{2\log(4|\Pi|H(|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|+1))}{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|+1}}.

Taking expectation over the random variable
|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|\sim\text{Bin}(N_{\operatorname{tot}},\eta)
yields that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[V(\pi^{\operatorname{E}})-V(\pi^{\operatorname{BC}})\right]

\displaystyle\leq 4H^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\sqrt{\frac{2\log(4|\Pi|H(|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|+1))}{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|+1}}\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}4H^{2}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{2\log(4|\Pi|H(|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|+1))}{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|+1}\right]}.

Inequality
(a)
follows Jensen’s inequality. We consider the function
g(x)=-x\log(x/c),\;\forall x\in(0,1]
, where
c=4|\Pi|H
.

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(x)=-(\log(x/c)+1)\geq 0,\;g^{\prime\prime}(x)=-\frac{1}{x}\leq 0,\;\forall x\in(0,1].

Thus,
g(x)
is a concave function. By Jensen’s inequality, we have that
\mathbb{E}[g(x)]\leq g(\mathbb{E}[x])
. Then we can derive that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[V(\pi^{\operatorname{E}})-V(\pi^{\operatorname{BC}})\right]

\displaystyle\leq 4H^{2}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{2\log(4|\Pi|H(|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|+1))}{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|+1}\right]}

\displaystyle=4\sqrt{2}H^{2}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[g\left(\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|+1}\right)\right]}

\displaystyle\leq 4\sqrt{2}H^{2}\sqrt{g\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|+1}\right]\right)}

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}4\sqrt{2}H^{2}\sqrt{g\left(\frac{1}{N_{\operatorname{E}}}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq 4\sqrt{2}H^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log(4|\Pi|HN_{\operatorname{E}})}{N_{\operatorname{E}}}}.

In inequality
(a)
, we use the facts that
g^{\prime}(x)\geq 0
and
\mathbb{E}\left[1/(|{\mathcal{D}}^{\operatorname{E}}|+1)\right]\leq 1/N_{\operatorname{E}}
from
3
. We complete the proof.
∎
