1.
Introduction

An Hamiltonian system, from a geometric point of view, is a symplectic manifold
(M,\omega)
of dimension
2n
endowed with a smooth function
H:M\to\mathbb{R}
. It is called
completely integrable
if there exist
n
independent smooth functions
f_{1}:=H,f_{2},\dots,f_{n}
on the manifold
M
with vanishing Poisson bracket
\{f_{i},f_{j}\}=0,\forall i,j
(
[
AM08
]
). It was proved by Arnlod (see
[
AA68
]
and
[
MM74
]
), that smooth and compact components of the fibers
\{f_{1}=\text{constant},\dots,f_{n}=\text{constant}\}
are diffeomorphic to
n
-dimensional tori and that one can always find, in a neighborhood of such fibers,
angle-action coordinates
. These are coordinates
\{\theta_{1},\psi_{1},\dots,\theta_{n},\psi_{n}\}
which form a local Darboux frame for
\omega
and the motion in the variables
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n}
is
quasi-periodic
. In particular, the integral of motions
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
depends only on
\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{n}
but, in general, they are different.

Even though integrable Hamiltonian systems arise from a purely physical context, given the close connection with symplectic geometry, we are interested in understanding whether geometric spaces (e.g. deformation or moduli spaces) admit such a structure. Nowadays, the classic example is given by the so called
Hitchin system
(
[
Hit87
]
), that is the cotangent bundle of the moduli space of stable
G
-bundles on a compact algebraic curve, for
G
a reductive Lie group. A much more recent result (
[
CJK19
]
) showed that the Hitchin component of the
\SL(3,\mathbb{R})
-character variety has the structure of an integrable Hamiltonian system with respect to the Goldman symplectic form (
[
Gol84
]
). In particular, it was given a geometric description of the construction in terms of convex
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-structures.

On the other hand, a Lagrangian fibration
\pi:=(f_{1},\dots,f_{n}):(M,\omega)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is associated with every completely integrable Hamiltonian system
(M,\omega,f_{1},\dots,f_{n})
(
[
DD08
]
). Moreover, in the case in which the Hamiltonian vector fields
\mathbb{X}_{f_{1}},\dots,\mathbb{X}_{f_{n}}
are complete, the regular and connected fibers of
\pi
are diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{k}\times T^{n-k}
, for
1\leq k\leq n
(
[
Arn13
]
). Studying the topological and symplectic properties of this fibration, criteria have been found to be able to pick the integrals of motion
(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})
as action variables, trying to give them a geometric meaning (see
[
Dui80
]
for
k=0
and
[
CJK19
]
for
k=n
).

In this paper, we study some properties of a new family of pseudo-Kähler metrics, introduced by the authors (
[
RT21
]
), defined on the
\SL(3,\mathbb{R})
-Hitchin component of the torus. Such family of metrics can be restricted to the deformation space of properly convex
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-structures on the torus, which embeds as an open (but not closed) connected smooth manifold in the
\SL(3,\mathbb{R})
-character variety. First, we show that the aforementioned deformation space has the structure of a complete Hamiltonian integrable system. Using the theory of complete Lagrangian fibrations (see Definition
3.7
), we show that two Hamilotonian functions associated with two group actions on the system can be taken as action variables. As a general result, we show that on a broad class of Hamiltonian systems the associated Lagrangian fibration is complete (see Proposition
3.8
). Finally, we prove that, under suitable assumptions, the connected component of the identity of the isometry group is isomorphic to
\mathbb{P}\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})\times S^{1}
and, as a consequence, we deduce the expression of an arbitrary isometry of the space.

1.1.
Symplectic properties

Let
(M,\omega,H)
be a completely integrable Hamiltonian system with integral of motions
f_{1}:=H,f_{2},\dots,f_{n}
. By the well-know Arnold-Liouville theorem (see Theorem
3.4
), the existence of the so-called
action-angle
coordinates gives rise to a global Darboux frame
\{\theta_{1},\psi_{1},\dots,\theta_{n},\psi_{n}\}
for
\omega
and a Lagrangian fibration

\pi:=(f_{1},\dots,f_{n}):(M,\omega)\to B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\ .

Unfortunately, the action variables
\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{n}
are not necessarily equal to the integral of motions
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
and this does not allow, in general, to give a geometric description of the Darboux frame.

A first general strategy to overcome this problem was presented in
[
Dui80
]
, in the case in which the Lagrangian fibration
\pi:(M,\omega)\to B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
associated with a complete Hamiltonian integrable system has fiber diffeomorphic to an
n
-dimensional torus. The crucial point is the existence of a global Lagrangian section
\sigma:B\to(M,\omega)
, which is guaranteed as long as
H^{2}(B,\mathbb{R})\cong\{0\}
.

Recently, Choi-Jung-Kim have presented an adapted version of this result, in the case where each fiber of
\pi:(M,\omega)\to B
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(see Theorem 3.4.5 in
[
CJK19
]
). Their main application was the existence of a global Darboux frame for the Goldman symplectic form
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{G}
defined on the
\SL(3,\mathbb{R})
-Hitchin component of a surface of genus at least two.

In this paper, we are interested in studying completely integrable Hamiltonian systems whose associated Lagrangian fibration has non compact fibres. In order to get a similar result as the one in the compact or simply-connected case we need to focus on the so-called
complete Lagrangian fibrations
(see Definition
3.7
). Such fibrations arise as the ones associated with a broad class of Hamiltonian systems, namely the ones with complete Hamiltonian vector fields
\mathbb{X}_{f_{1}},\dots,\mathbb{X}_{f_{n}}
(see Proposition
3.8
). Our main result shows that, in the non compact case, the integral of motions
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
can be taken as action coordinates for
\omega
, whenever the base space
B
is a contractible open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the associated Lagrangian fibration
\pi:(M,\omega)\to B
is complete. Our strategy is adapted to that of the compact case (
[
Dui80
]
), which is based on the existence of global Lagrangian sections and it is proved using the theory of sheaf cohomology (see Corollary
3.23
).

As an application, we study the symplectic geometry of
(\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2}),\boldsymbol{\omega}_{f})
, which is a smooth manifold diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}^{*}
(Corollary
2.11
). There exists an action of
S^{1}
and
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
on
\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2})
which turns out to be Hamiltonian with respect to
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{f}
(Theorem
4.3
and Theorem
4.4
). In particular, by taking the action of the subgroup
\mathbb{R}^{*}<\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
generated by the diagonal matrices, it is possible to explicitly compute the Hamiltonian function
H_{2}
with respect to this restricted action. Together with the Hamiltonian function
H_{1}
of the circular action we get the existence of two commuting Hamiltonian vector fields
\mathbb{X}_{H_{1}},\mathbb{X}_{H_{2}}
on
\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2})
. In other terms, the space
(\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2}),\boldsymbol{\omega}_{f})
has the structure of a complete Hamiltonian integrable system (see Proposition
4.6
). The main issue is that each fiber of the associated Lagrangian fibration
H:=(H_{1},H_{2}):\big(\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2}),\boldsymbol{\omega}_{f}\big)\to B\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}\times S^{1}
(see Remark
4.7
). However, since the base space
B
is contractible and the Hamiltonian vector fields
\mathbb{X}_{H_{1}},\mathbb{X}_{H_{2}}
are complete, we can apply the theory developed in Section
3
to get the following:

Theorem A
.

The collection
\{\theta,H_{1},s,H_{2}\}
is a global Darboux frame for
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{f}
, where
(s,\theta)\in\mathbb{R}\times S^{1}\cong H^{-1}(b)
for each
b\in B
.

1.2.
Pseudo-Kähler geometry

A
pseudo-Riemannian
metric
\mathbf{g}
on a smooth
n
-manifold
M
is an everywhere non-degenerate, smooth, symmetric
(0,2)
-tensor. The
\emph{index}
of
\mathbf{g}
is the maximal rank
k
of the smooth distribution where it is negative-definite. For instance, if
k=0
then
\mathbf{g}
is a Riemannian metric. Now let
\mathbf{I}
be a complex structure on
M
, then
(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{I})
is a
pseudo-Hermitian structure
if

\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{I}X,\mathbf{I}Y)=\mathbf{g}(X,Y),\qquad\forall X,Y\in T_{p}M,p\in M\ .

Notice that, due to this last condition, the index of
\mathbf{g}
in this case is always even
k=2s
, where
s
is called the
complex index
and it satisfies
1\leq s\leq m=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}M
. If
k=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}M
we say the pseudo-metric has
neutral signature
. The
fundamental 2-form

\boldsymbol{\omega}
of a pseudo-Hermitian manifold
(M,\mathbf{g},\mathbf{I})
is defined by:

\boldsymbol{\omega}(X,Y):=\mathbf{g}(X,\mathbf{I}Y),\qquad\forall X,Y\in T_{p}M,p\in M\ .

A pseudo-Hermitian manifold
(M,\mathbf{g},\mathbf{I},\boldsymbol{\omega})
is called
pseudo-Kähler
if the fundamental
2
-form is closed, namely if
\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\omega}=0
. In this case the corresponding metric is called
pseudo-Kähler
.

1.3.
Metric properties

The new family of pseudo-Kähler metrics
(\mathbf{g}_{f},\mathbf{I},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{f})
is defined on the whole
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}
(see Section
2.3
) and it is parametrized by smooth functions
f:[0,+\infty)\to(-\infty,0]
satisfying the following properties:

(i)\ f(0)=0,\qquad(ii)\ f^{\prime}(t)<0,\ \forall t\geq 0,\qquad(iii)\ \displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}f(t)=-\infty\ .

In particular, they restrict to the hyperbolic Kähler metric on
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\{0\}
(see Theorem
2.15
).

Due to the complicated dependence of the metric and the symplectic form in terms of the function
f
, it is difficult to study basic properties of it. Nevertheless, as will be seen in Section
5
, exploiting the fact that a pseudo-Kähler metric with neutral signature enjoys the same symmetries as the positive-definite case, it is possible to greatly simplify the computation of the Ricci tensor and of the scalar curvature, even in the case of an arbitrary function
f
. Unfortunately, the final expression of these two quantities is slightly complicated. On the other hand, by choosing the functions
f(t)=-kt
with
k>0
, we will obtain an estimate on the scalar curvature that will allow us to compute the connected component of the identity in the isometry group of the metric for this special case.

Theorem B
.

If
f(t)=-kt
, with
k>0
, then any isometry
h:\big(\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{C},\mathbf{g}_{f}\big)\to\big(\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{C},\mathbf{g}_{f}\big)
isotopic to the identity, can be written as
h=P\circ e^{i\theta}
, for some
P\in\mathbb{P}\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
.

The Lie group
\mathrm{Isom}\big(\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{C},\mathbf{g}_{f}\big)
has four connected components. One is given by all the isometries preserving the orientations of both factors, one is given by all the isometries reversing the orientations of both factors (but preserving the orientation on the whole space) and the remaining two are formed by isometries reversing orientation on either
\mathbb{H}^{2}
or
\mathbb{C}
. In particular, the connected component of the identity is identified with those isometries preserving the orientations on both factors.

Corollary C
.

If
f(t)=-kt
, with
k>0
, then any isometry
h:\big(\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{C},\mathbf{g}_{f}\big)\to\big(\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{C},\mathbf{g}_{f}\big)
not isotopic to the identity, can be written as

h=P\circ e^{i\theta}\circ h_{1},\quad h=P\circ e^{i\theta}\circ h_{2},\quad h=P\circ e^{i\theta}\circ h_{1}\circ h_{2}\qquad P\in\mathbb{P}\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R}),e^{i\theta}\in S^{1}

where
h_{1}(z,w):=(-\bar{z},w)
and
h_{2}(z,w):=(z,\bar{w})
, according to which connected component it belongs.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Background on properly convex
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-structures

Here we give the standard definitions of
convex

\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-
structures
over a smooth surface
S
and we introduce the deformation space of such structures. For a more complete and detailed exposition see
[
Gol90
]
,
[
CG97
]
and
[
Gol18
]
.

An
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-
structure
on a smooth connected surface
S
is a maximal
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-atlas, i.e. an atlas in which the local charts take value in the real projective plane and the transition functions are induced by projective transformations. Once a maximal
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-atlas is given, we say that
S
has an
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-
structure
. A domain (open and connected)
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
is said to be
convex
if there exists a projective line
l
disjoint from
\Omega
such that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}\setminus l\cong\mathbb{A}^{2}
is convex in the usual sense.

Definition 2.1
.

An
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-surface
S
is
convex
if it is projectively isomorphic to a quotient
\Omega/_{\Gamma}
, where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
is a convex domain and
\Gamma\subset\Proj(\Omega)\subset\SL(3,\mathbb{R})
is a discrete group of projective transformations preserving
\Omega
acting freely and properly discontinuously on
\Omega
. The surface
S
is
\emph{properlyconvex}
if
\Omega
is bounded in some affine space.

If
S
is convex, then its universal cover
\widetilde{S}
can be identified with a convex domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
via the
developing map
and the discrete subgroup
\Gamma
can be identified with
\pi_{1}(S)
via the
holonomy homomorphism
. From this point on we will focus only on the case in which the surface is closed, orientable and of genus
g
, hence it will be denoted with
\Sigma
.

Definition 2.2
.

A
(properly) convex

\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-
structure
on
\Sigma
is a pair
(\phi,M)
, where
\phi:\Sigma\to M
is a diffeomorphism (called the
marking
) and
M\cong\Omega/_{\Gamma}
is a (properly) convex
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-surface.

One can define an equivalence relation on such pairs, namely
(\phi_{1},M_{1})\sim(\phi_{2},M_{2})
if and only if there exists a projective isomorphism
\Psi:M_{1}\to M_{2}
such that the new marking
\Psi\circ\phi_{1}
is isotopic to
\phi_{2}
. The
deformation space of (properly) convex
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-structures
is defined as:

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\Sigma):=\{(\phi,M)\ \text{convex}\ \mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}-\text{structure on}\ \Sigma\}_{/_{\sim}}

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{0}(\Sigma):=\{(\phi,M)\ \text{properly convex}\ \mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}-\text{structure on}\ \Sigma\}_{/_{\sim}}\ .

In the case of a hyperbolic Riemann surface, it is well-known that
\mathcal{B}(\Sigma)\equiv\mathcal{B}_{0}(\Sigma)
(see
[
Kui53
]
and
[
Ben60
]
).
In the case of the torus this is no longer true, for instance there are many convex
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-structures which are not properly convex: affine and Euclidean ones. They can not be properly convex since the developing map identifies the universal cover of
T^{2}
with a copy of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
inside
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
, which is convex but not bounded, see
[
Gol18
, §8.5]
. In particular, there is a strict inclusion
\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2})\subsetneq\mathcal{B}(T^{2})
and the natural space to study in this contest is
\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2})
, given that
\mathcal{B}(T^{2})
is not even a Hausdorff topological space.

2.2.
A natural isomorphism

Here we briefly introduce the theory of hyperbolic affine spheres in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, recalling also the most important result regarding their global geometry as hypersurfaces. Then, we will focus on the close relationship between these geometric objects and cubic holomorphic differentials defined on the surface. All this will lead us to a parameterization of properly convex
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-structures in terms of complex structures and holomorphic cubic differentials on
T^{2}
.

Let
f\!:M\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be an immersion and
\xi\!:M\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a transverse vector field to
f(M)
. This means that for all
x\in M
we have a splitting:

T_{f(x)}\mathbb{R}^{3}=f_{*}T_{x}M+\mathbb{R}\xi_{x}\ .

Let
D
be the standard flat connection on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and suppose the structure equations of the immersed surface are given by:

(2.3)

\displaystyle D_{X}Y

\displaystyle=\nabla_{X}Y+h(X,Y)\xi

\displaystyle D_{X}\xi

\displaystyle=-S(X)

where
\nabla
is a torsion-free connection on
M
called the
Blaschke connection
,
h
is a metric on
M
called the
Blaschke metric
and
S
is and endomorphism of
TM
called the
affine shape operator
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
M
be an immersed hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with structure equations given by (
2.3
). Then
M
is called a
hyperbolic affine sphere
if
S=-\mathrm{Id}_{TM}
.

Theorem 2.4

(
[
CY77
]
,
[
CY86
]
,
[
Gig81
]
,
[
Li90
]
)
.

Given a convex, bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, there is a unique properly embedded hyperbolic affine sphere
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
with center at
0
asymptotic to the boundary of the cone
\mathcal{C}(\Omega):=\{(tx,t)\ |\ x\in\Omega,t>0\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
. For any immersed hyperbolic affine sphere
f:M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
, properness of the immersion is equivalent to the completeness of the Blaschke metric, and any such
M
is a properly embedded hypersurface asymptotic to the boundary of the cone given by the convex hull of
M
and its center.

Remark 2.5
.

Theorem
2.4
induces a correspondence between equivariant hyperbolic affine spheres in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and properly convex
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-structures on a closed orientable surface
\Sigma
of genus
g\geq 1
.

Let
f:\!(M,h,\nabla)\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
be an immersed affine sphere, namely an immersed hypersurface satisfying equations (
2.3
) with
S=-\mathrm{Id}_{T}M
. If
\widehat{\nabla}
denotes the Levi-Civita connection of
h
, then
\nabla=\widehat{\nabla}+A
, where
A
is a section of
T^{*}(M)\otimes\End(TM)
. In particular, for every
X\in\Gamma(TM)
the quantity
A(X)
is an endomorphism of
TM
.

Proposition 2.6

(
[
BH13
, Lemma 4.3, Lemma 4.4]
)
.

The section
A
has the following properties:

(1)

A(X)Y=A(Y)X,\qquad\forall X,Y\in\Gamma(TM)

(2)

The endomorphism
A(X)
is trace-free and
h
-symmetric,
\forall X\in\Gamma(TM)

(3)

\mathrm{d}^{\widehat{\nabla}}A=0
, where
\mathrm{d}^{\widehat{\nabla}}A(X,Y)=\big(\widehat{\nabla}_{X}A\big)(Y)-\big(\widehat{\nabla}_{Y}A\big)(X),\qquad X,Y\in\Gamma(TM)

Definition 2.7
.

The
Pick tensor
is the
(0,3)
-tensor defined by

(2.4)

C(X,Y,Z):=h(A(X)Y,Z),\quad\forall X,Y,Z\in\Gamma(TM)

Proposition 2.8
.

If
f:\!(M,h,\nabla)\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
is an immersed affine sphere, then the Pick tensor is totally symmetric, namely in index notation
C_{ijk}
we have

C_{ijk}=C_{\sigma(ijk)},\quad\forall\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{3}\ .

Theorem 2.9

(
[
BH13
, Lemma 4.8]
)
.

Let
M
be a smooth orientable surface endowed with a metric
h
and a compatible complex structure
J
. Suppose the tensor
C(X,Y,Z)=h(A(X)Y,Z)
is totally symmetric, then
A(X)
is trace-free and
\mathrm{d}^{\widehat{\nabla}}A=0,\ \forall X\in\Gamma(TM)
if and only if
C=\real(q)
, where
q
is a cubic holomorphic differential on
(M,J)
. If this is the case, then
q=C(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)+iC(J\cdot,J\cdot,J\cdot)
.

Remark 2.10
.

Thanks to Proposition
2.8
we notice that if
M
is an immersed affine sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
its Pick tensor is totally symmetric, hence it can always be expressed as the real part of a cubic holomorphic differential on
(M,J)
.

We conclude this section by giving a parametrization of
\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2})
which will be useful for our discussion. First we recall that (one of the possible) definition of the Teichmüller space
\mathcal{T}(T^{2})
is the following:

\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2}):=\{J\in\End(\mathbb{R}^{2})\ |\ J^{2}=-\mathds{1},\ \rho(v,Jv)>0\ \text{for some}\ v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}\}\ ,

where

is the standard area form on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
(see
[
FM11
, §10.2]
). In particular, this set has a smooth manifold structure of real dimension two. Let
\pi:Q^{3}(\mathcal{T}(T^{2}))\to\mathcal{T}(T^{2})
be the holomorphic vector bundle of cubic differentials, i.e. a point in
Q^{3}(\mathcal{T}(T^{2}))
is represented by a pair
(J,q)
where
J\in\mathcal{T}(T^{2}),q\in H^{0}\big(T^{2},K^{\otimes^{3}}\big)\cong\mathbb{C}
and
\pi(J,q):=J
. Let
Q^{3}_{0}(\mathcal{T}(T^{2})):=Q^{3}(\mathcal{T}(T^{2}))\setminus\big(\mathcal{T}(T^{2})\times\{0\}\big)
be the complement of the zero section, then:

Corollary 2.11

(
[
RT21
]
)
.

There exists a bijection between
\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2})
and
Q^{3}_{0}(\mathcal{T}(T^{2}))
.

Proof.

The above bijection follows from Theorem
2.4
. In fact, by Remark
2.5
to any
\big[\Omega/_{\Gamma}\big]\in\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2})
we have an associated equivariant hyperbolic affine sphere
M
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
which is determined by its Blaschke metric and the Pick tensor (see Definition
2.7
and
[
NS94
]
). Hence, let
\chi:\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2})\to Q^{3}_{0}(\mathcal{T}(T^{2}))
be the map that associates to each
\big[\Omega/_{\Gamma}\big]
the pair
(J,q)
where
J
is the complex structure induced by the Blaschke metric and
q=c\mathrm{d}z^{3}
is a non-zero cubic holomorphic differential whose real part coincides with the Pick tensor of

. It can be shown (see
[
Wan90
]
,
[
RT21
, §2.3]
) that for any such
(J,q)
we can find a unique (up to unimodular affine transformations) hyperbolic affine sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
that is invariant under a subgroup
\Gamma<\SL(3,\mathbb{R})
isomorphic to
\pi_{1}(T^{2})
. This implies directly that the map
\chi
is a bijection.
∎

2.3.
A new family of metrics

In the following we want to introduce the new metrics defined on the space
Q^{3}(\mathcal{T}(T^{2}))
which can be restricted to

3.
Global Darboux coordinates for complete Lagrangian fibrations

We initially recall the basic notions of symplectic geometry and integrable systems that we will need later on (see for example
[
DD08
, §18]
). Then, we begin the study of complete Lagrangian fibrations and their connection with completely integrable Hamiltonian systems.

Definition 3.1
.

A
Hamiltonian system
is a triple
(M,\omega,H)
, where
(M,\omega)
is a symplectic manifold and
H\in C^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R})
is a function, called the
Hamiltonian
function.

If
(M,\omega)
is a symplectic manifold and
f\in C^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R})
, then the
Hamiltonian vector field

\mathbb{X}_{f}\in\Gamma(TM)
associated with
f
is defined by the following property

(3.1)

\omega(\mathbb{X}_{f},Y)=\mathrm{d}f(Y),\quad\forall Y\in\Gamma(TM)\ .

Definition 3.2
.

Let
(M,\omega,H)
be a Hamiltonian system. A function
f\in C^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R})
is called an
integral of motion
if

\omega(\mathbb{X}_{f},\mathbb{X}_{H})=0\ .

In other words, any integral of motion
f
is constant along the integral curves of
\mathbb{X}_{H}
.

Definition 3.3
.

A Hamiltonian system
(M,\omega,H)
is
completely integrable
if it possesses
n=\frac{1}{2}\dim(M)
integral of motions
f_{1}=H,f_{2},\dots,f_{n}
such that

(1)

The differentials
(\mathrm{d}f_{1})_{p},\dots,(\mathrm{d}f_{n})_{p}
are linearly independent for each
p\in M
.

(2)

They are pairwise in involution, i.e.
\omega(\mathbb{X}_{f_{i}},\mathbb{X}_{f_{j}})=0
for each
i,j=1,\dots,n
.

The first condition in the previous definition is called
independence
and the second one is called
involutivity
. Notice that one of the integral of motion can always be taken to be the Hamiltonian function of the system. Furthermore, at each
p\in M
, the Hamiltonian vector fields associated with the integral of motions span an isotropic subspace of
T_{p}M
.

One of the most relevant results of this theory is the following

Theorem 3.4

(Arnold-Liouville,
[
Arn13
]
)
.

Let
(M,\omega,H)
be a completely integrable Hamiltonian system of dimension
2n
and with integral of motions
f_{1}=H,f_{2},\dots,f_{n}
. Let
c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a regular value of the map
f=(f_{1},\dots,f_{n}):M\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then,

(1)

The level set
f^{-1}(c)
is a Lagrangian submanifold of
M
.

(2)

If the Hamiltonian vector fields
\mathbb{X}_{f_{1}},\dots\mathbb{X}_{f_{n}}
are complete on the level set
f^{-1}(c)
, then each connected component of
f^{-1}(c)
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{k}\times T^{n-k}
, for some
0\leq k\leq n
. Moreover, that component has coordinates
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n}
called angle coordinates, in which the flows of
\mathbb{X}_{f_{1}},\dots\mathbb{X}_{f_{n}}
are linear.

(3)

There are coordinates
\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{n}
, called action coordinates such that the manifold
(M,\omega)
is symplectomorphic to
(\mathbb{R}^{n+k}\times T^{n-k},\omega_{0})
, where
\omega_{0}=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i}\wedge\psi_{i}
.

Remark 3.5
.

From a geometric point of view, regular level sets
f^{-1}(c)
being lagrangian submanifolds implies that, in a neighborhood of a regular value, the map
f:M\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a
lagrangian fibration
, i.e. it is locally trivial and its fibers are lagrangian submanifolds. For more details see
[
DD08
]
.

On the other hand, one of the main issue of this result is that the action coordinates
\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{n}
are, in general, not the given integral of motions, since
\theta_{1},f_{1},\dots,\theta_{n},f_{n}
may not form a global Darboux chart for
\omega.
As we will see in Section
4.2
, this problem can be solved for a particular class of Hamiltonian systems, into which
\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2})
falls.

3.1.
Complete Lagrangian fibrations

Throughout this section a Lagrangian fibration is a triple
(\pi,M,B)
, where
(M,\omega)
is a symplectic manifold,
B
is an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
contained in the set of regular values of
\pi
, the map
\pi:(M,\omega)\to B
is a smooth surjective submersion and for each
b\in B
the sumbmanifold
\pi^{-1}(b)
is Lagrangian in
(M,\omega)
.

Let
\pi:(M,\omega)\to B
be a Lagrangian fibration and let
\alpha:B\to T^{*}B
be a
1
-form. Define a vector field
X_{\pi^{*}\alpha}\in\Gamma(TM)
by setting

(3.2)

\omega(X_{\pi^{*}\alpha},\cdot)=\pi^{*}\alpha\ .

Proposition 3.6
.

For all
\alpha,\beta\in\Gamma(T^{*}B)
and for all
f\in C^{\infty}(B)
we have:

(i)

X_{\pi^{*}(\alpha+\beta)}=X_{\pi^{*}\alpha}+X_{\pi^{*}\beta}

(ii)

X_{\pi^{*}(f\alpha)}=(\pi^{*}f)X_{\pi^{*}\alpha}

(iii)

X_{\pi^{*}\alpha}\in\mathrm{Ker}\pi_{*}

(iv)

\big[X_{\pi^{*}\alpha},X_{\pi^{*}\beta}\big]=0

Proof.

Properties (i) and (ii) follow directly form the defining equation (
3.2
). Let
q^{1},\dots,q^{n}
be local coordinates on
V\subset B
such that
\alpha=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\mathrm{d}q^{i}
for some functions
\alpha_{i}:V\to\mathbb{R},i=1,\dots,n
. Then, by properties (i) and (ii)

X_{\pi^{*}\alpha}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\pi^{*}\alpha_{i})X_{\pi^{*}\mathrm{d}q^{i}}\ .

Since condition (iii) is pointwise, it suffices to prove that for all functions
f\in C^{\infty}(V)
we get
X_{\pi^{*}\mathrm{d}f}\in\mathrm{Ker}\pi_{*}
. Let
f
be such a function and
Y\in\mathrm{Ker}\pi_{*}
. Since
\pi^{*}f
is constant along the fibre of
\pi:M\to B
, it follows
Y(\pi^{*}f)=0
. On the other hand,

0=Y(\pi^{*}f)=\big(\pi^{*}(\mathrm{d}f)\big)(Y)=\omega(X_{\pi^{*}\mathrm{d}f},Y)

by definition of
X_{\pi^{*}\mathrm{d}f}
. Since the last equality holds for all vertical fields
Y
, we have
X_{\pi^{*}\mathrm{d}f}\in(\mathrm{Ker}\pi_{*})^{\perp^{\omega}}
. The map
\pi
defines a Lagrangian fibration, which implies that
(\mathrm{Ker}\pi_{*})^{\perp^{\omega}}=\mathrm{Ker}\pi_{*}
and property (iii) follows.

For the last property, let
\alpha,\beta\in\Gamma(T^{*}B)
and locally write

\alpha=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\mathrm{d}q^{i},\qquad\beta=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\beta_{i}\mathrm{d}q^{i}

for smooth functions
\alpha_{i},\beta_{j}
. Then

\displaystyle[X_{\pi^{*}\alpha},X_{\pi^{*}\beta}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}[(\pi^{*}\alpha_{i})X_{\pi^{*}\mathrm{d}q^{i}},(\pi^{*}\beta_{j})X_{\pi^{*}\mathrm{d}q^{j}}]=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\bigg((\pi^{*}\alpha_{i})(\pi^{*}\beta_{j})[X_{\pi^{*}\mathrm{d}q^{i}},X_{\pi^{*}\mathrm{d}q^{j}}]

\displaystyle+(\pi^{*}\alpha_{i})\big(X_{\pi^{*}\mathrm{d}q^{i}}(\pi^{*}\beta_{j})\big)X_{\pi^{*}\mathrm{d}q^{j}}-(\pi^{*}\beta_{j})\big(X_{\pi^{*}\mathrm{d}q^{j}}(\pi^{*}\alpha_{i})\big)X_{\pi^{*}\mathrm{d}q^{i}}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(\pi^{*}\alpha_{i})(\pi^{*}\beta_{j})[X_{\pi^{*}\mathrm{d}q^{i}},X_{\pi^{*}\mathrm{d}q^{j}}]

where the first equality follows from properties (i)-(ii) and the the third one from property (iii). Thus, it suffices to show that for any
f,g\in C^{\infty}(B)
one has
[X_{\pi^{*}\mathrm{d}f},X_{\pi^{*}\mathrm{d}g}]=0
. Notice that the homomorphism

\displaystyle C^{\infty}(M)

\displaystyle\to\Gamma(TM)

\displaystyle f\mapsto X_{\mathrm{d}f}

is a Lie algebra homomorphism with respect to the Poisson bracket
\{\cdot,\cdot\}_{\omega}
and the Lie bracket
[\cdot,\cdot]
, where
\{f,g\}_{\omega}:=\omega(X_{\mathrm{d}f},X_{\mathrm{d}g})
. In particular, for each
f,g\in C^{\infty}(B)

[X_{\pi^{*}\mathrm{d}f},X_{\pi^{*}\mathrm{d}g}]=X_{\mathrm{d}\{\pi^{*}f,\pi^{*}g\}_{\omega}}=0\ .

The second equality follows from the fact that
\pi:(M,\{\cdot,\cdot\}_{\omega})\to(B,0)
is a Poisson morphism (see
[
Vai94
]
).
∎

Definition 3.7
.

A Lagrangian fibration
\pi:(M,\omega)\to B
is
complete
if for each compactly supported
1
-form
\alpha
on
B
, the vector field
X_{\pi^{*}\alpha}
defined by (
3.2
) is complete.

Recall that a Lagrangian fibration is naturally associated with a completely integrable Hamiltonian system (see Remark
3.5
). The next Proposition explains why the previous hypothesis of completeness on a Lagrangian fibration is on the one hand interesting from the point of view of geometry and on the other not too restrictive.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
\pi:(M,\omega)\to B
be a Lagrangian fibration associated with a completely integrable Hamiltonian system
(M,H,\omega)
with integral of motions given by
f_{1}=H,f_{2},\dots,f_{n}
. If the Hamiltonian vector fields
\mathbb{X}_{f_{1}},\dots,\mathbb{X}_{f_{n}}
are complete on
\pi^{-1}(b)
for each
b\in B
, then the Lagrangian fibration
\pi:(M,\omega)\to B
is complete.

Proof.

Since the Hamiltonian vector fields are vertical and linearly independent, they point-wise generate the tangent space to each fibre
\pi^{-1}(b)
. Moreover, there exist
1
-forms
\alpha_{i}:B\to T^{*}B
such that

\omega(\mathbb{X}_{f_{i}},\cdot)=\pi^{*}\alpha_{i},\qquad i=1,\dots,n

where
\omega(\mathbb{X}_{f_{i}},\cdot)=\mathrm{d}f_{i}
by (
3.1
). The
1
-forms
\alpha_{i}
are point-wise linearly independent on
B
, indeed if

a_{1}\alpha_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}\alpha_{n}=0,\ \text{for some}\ a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in C^{\infty}(B)

then the above sum is still equal to zero after taking the pullback via
\pi
. By using the defining property of the
\alpha_{i}
’s and the independence property of
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
we get
(\pi^{*}a_{1})(m)=\dots=(\pi^{*}a_{n})(m)=0,\ \forall m\in M
, i.e. the functions
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
are zero on the whole set
B
. Let
\alpha:U\subset B\to T^{*}U
be a locally defined compactly supported
1
-form. We need to prove that the vector field
X_{\pi^{*}\alpha}
, defined by (
3.2
), is complete. By the above argument, there exist
n
functions
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}
on
U
such that

\alpha=\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}\alpha_{i}\ .

Since
\alpha
has compact support on
U
, the functions
g_{i}
have compact support on the same set as well. In particular, they are bounded on
U
. By properties (i) an (ii) of Proposition
3.6
it follows that

X_{\pi^{*}\alpha}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\pi^{*}g_{i})X_{\pi^{*}\alpha_{i}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\pi^{*}g_{i})\mathbb{X}_{f_{i}}\ .

The vector field
X_{\pi^{*}(g_{j}\alpha_{j})}=(\pi^{*}g_{j})\mathbb{X}_{f_{j}}
is complete for all
j=1,\dots,n
by an application of the so-called "Escape Lemma" (see
[
Lee13
]
, Lemma 9.19), indeed the function
\pi^{*}g_{j}
is bounded and
\mathbb{X}_{f_{j}}
is complete. Moreover, since
[X_{\pi^{*}(g_{i}\alpha_{i})},X_{\pi^{*}(g_{j}\alpha_{j})}]=0
for all
i,j=1,\dots,n
by property (iv) of Proposition
3.6
, it follows that
X_{\pi^{*}(g_{i}\alpha_{i})}+X_{\pi^{*}(g_{j}\alpha_{j})}
defines a new complete vector field as it is the sum of two commuting complete vector fields. Applying in an iterative way the previous observation, we deduce that
X_{\pi^{*}\alpha}
is complete, as well. ∎

From now on, all Lagrangian fibrations will be complete.

Let
\alpha:U\to T^{*}U
be a compactly supported locally defined
1
-form on the base and let
\phi_{\alpha}^{t}:\pi^{-1}(U)\to\pi^{-1}(U)
be the flow of the vector field
X_{\pi^{*}\alpha}
defined for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
X_{\pi^{*}\alpha}
is vertical, its flow
\phi_{\alpha}^{t}
lies along the fibres of
\pi:(M,\omega)\to B
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Furthermore, for each
\alpha_{b}\in T^{*}B
there exists a compactly supported locally defined
1
-form
\alpha:U\to T^{*}U
such that
\alpha(b)=\alpha_{b}
and the value of
X_{\pi^{*}\alpha}
at a point
m\in M
only depends on
\alpha_{b}
and not on the choice of
\alpha
. Therefore, for each
\alpha_{b}\in T^{*}B
there is a well-defined diffeomorphism

\phi_{\alpha_{b}}^{1}:=\phi_{\alpha}^{1}{|_{\pi^{-1}(b)}}:\pi^{-1}(b)\to\pi^{-1}(b)

where
\alpha\in\Gamma(T^{*}U)
is a compactly supported form such that
\alpha(b)=\alpha_{b}
. In particular, for each
b\in B
the map

(3.3)

\displaystyle T^{*}_{b}B

\displaystyle\to\Diff(\pi^{-1}(b))

\displaystyle\alpha_{b}\mapsto\phi_{\alpha_{b}}^{1}

is a Lie group homomorphism, where
T^{*}_{b}B
has the structure of an abelian Lie group with respect to the sum of covectors. In other words, for each
b\in B
, the map in (
3.3
) defines a transitive action of
T^{*}_{b}B
on
\pi^{-1}(b)
. In general, this action is not free and for instance one can consider the associated isotropy group, namely

\Lambda_{b}:=\{\alpha_{b}\in T^{*}_{b}B\ |\ \phi_{\alpha_{b}}^{1}(m)=m,\ \forall m\in\pi^{-1}(b)\}

known as the
period lattice
. It can be proved that it is a discrete subgroup of
T^{*}_{b}B
isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{k}
, with
k=1,\dots,n
(see
[
Dui80
]
for the case
k=n
or
[
FGS02
]
in general).

Remark 3.9
.

In the case
M=T^{*}B
and
\omega=\Omega_{\text{can}}
, the transitive action is simply given by the sum of covectors and
\Lambda_{b}=0
for each
b\in B
.

Definition 3.10

(
[
Vai94
]
)
.

The subset

\Lambda:=\bigcup_{b\in B}\Lambda_{b}\subset T^{*}B

is called the
period net
associated with the complete Lagrangian fibration
\pi:(M,\omega)\to B
.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
\pi:(M,\omega)\to B
be a complete Lagrangian fibration and let
\alpha:U\to T^{*}U
be a locally defined
1
-form. Then,

(3.4)

\big(\phi_{\alpha}^{1}\big)^{*}\omega-\omega=\pi^{*}\mathrm{d}\alpha

Proof.

The proof relies on the following computation

\displaystyle\big(\phi_{\alpha}^{1}\big)^{*}\omega-\omega

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\big(\phi_{\alpha}^{t}\big)^{*}\omega\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\big(\phi_{\alpha}^{t}\big)^{*}\big(\mathcal{L}_{X_{\pi^{*}\alpha}}\omega\big)\mathrm{d}t

(Cartan’s magic formula)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\big(\phi_{\alpha}^{t}\big)^{*}\mathrm{d}\big(\omega(X_{\pi^{*}\alpha},\cdot)\big)\mathrm{d}t

(Equation (
3.2
))

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\big(\pi\circ\phi_{\alpha}^{t}\big)^{*}\mathrm{d}\alpha\mathrm{d}t

(
\pi\circ\phi_{\alpha}^{t}=\pi
, for all
t
)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\pi^{*}\mathrm{d}\alpha\mathrm{d}t=\pi^{*}\mathrm{d}\alpha\ .

∎

Theorem 3.12

(
[
Sep
]
)
.

Let
\pi:(M,\omega)\to B
be a complete Lagrangian fibration and let
\Lambda
be the associated period net. Then,

(1)

\Lambda
is a closed Lagrangian submanifold of
T^{*}B
.

(2)

The quotient
T^{*}B/_{\Lambda}
is a smooth manifold.

Proof.

Since
\pi:(M,\omega)\to B
is a surjective submersion, then for each
b\in B
there exists a local section
\sigma:U\to\pi^{-1}(U)
defined in an open neighbourhood
U
containing
b
(
[
GM+97
]
, Proposition 1.2.4). Fix such a section and consider the map

\displaystyle\psi_{\sigma}:T^{*}U

\displaystyle\to\pi^{-1}(U)

(3.5)

\displaystyle\alpha\mapsto\phi^{1}_{\alpha}\big(\sigma\circ p(\alpha)\big)

where
p:(T^{*}B,\Omega_{\text{can}})\to B
. We first want to prove that
\psi_{\sigma}
is a local diffeomorphism. Since dim
T^{*}U
=dim
\pi^{-1}(U)
it suffices to prove that
\mathrm{Ker}\mathrm{d}_{\alpha}\psi_{\sigma}=\{0\}
for all
\alpha\in T^{*}U
. Fix an element
\alpha_{0}\in T^{*}U
and notice that if
X\in T_{\alpha_{0}}T^{*}U
is tangent to the fibres of
p
, then
\mathrm{d}_{\alpha_{0}}\psi_{\sigma}(X)=0
if and only if
X=0
. Therefore, if
\mathrm{d}_{\alpha_{0}}\psi_{\sigma}(Y)=0
and
Y\neq 0
, then
\mathrm{d}_{\alpha_{0}}p(Y)\neq 0
. Any such vector

Definition 4.1

Let

(i)

The function

(4.1)

\mu_{g\cdot p}=\Ad^{*}(g)(\mu_{p}):=\mu_{p}\circ\Ad(g^{-1})\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}\ .

(ii)

Given

be the vector field on

\mathrm{d}\mu^{\xi}=\iota_{X_{\xi}}\omega=\omega(X_{\xi},\cdot)

where

\mu^{\xi}(p):=\mu_{p}(\xi)

A map

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\mathbb{R}^{*}
be a copy of the subgroup of diagonal matrices in

Proof.

By Lemma

Proposition 4.6

The Hamiltonian system

\displaystyle=\bigg(-1+f-3f^{\prime}y^{3}(u^{2}+v^{2})\bigg)\frac{\mathrm{d}x\wedge\mathrm{d}y}{y^{2}}-\frac{4}{3}f^{\prime}y^{3}\mathrm{d}u\wedge\mathrm{d}v

5.
Curvature properties

In this section we show that the copy of the hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\{0\}\subset\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}
is the only embedded submanifold with scalar curvature equal to
1
, whenever
f(t)=-kt
with
k>0
. Initially, this last estimate will allow us to compute the connected component of the identity of the isometry group and secondly, we will deduce the expression for an arbitrary isometry.

5.1.
The Ricci tensor and the scalar curvature

A
Kähler manifold
is a particular case of a pseudo-Kähler one, namely when the pseudo-Riemannian metric has index equal to zero. For this very reason it is natural to ask whether some properties of Kähler manifolds still holds in this more general setting. Now we briefly recall the definition of some curvature tensors defined on Kähler manifolds and we will explain how their formulae still hold in the pseudo-Riemannian setting as long as the pseudo-metric is of neutral signature.

Let
(M,g,I)
be a Kähler manifold of complex dimension
n
. The tensor
I
can be extended by
\mathbb{C}
-linearity on the complexified tangent bundle
T_{\mathbb{C}}M:=TM\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}
. Since
I^{2}=-\mathds{1}
there is an eigenbundle decomposition
T_{\mathbb{C}}M=T^{1,0}\oplus T^{0.1}M
, where

T^{1,0}M:=\{X\in T_{\mathbb{C}}M\ |\ I(X)=i\cdot X\},\qquad T^{0,1}M:=\{X\in T_{\mathbb{C}}M\ |\ I(X)=-i\cdot X\}\ .

The bundle
T^{1,0}M
is called the
holomorphic tangent bundle
and
T^{0,1}M
the
anti-holomorphic tangent bundle
of
M
, in particular they are the conjugate of each other. If
(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})
are local holomorphic coordinates on
M
, the
n
-dimensional complex vector space
T^{1,0}M
is generated by
\{\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{1}},\dots\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{n}}\}
. Since
z_{k}=x_{k}+iy_{k}
for each
k=1,\dots,n
we have

\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{k}}=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}-i\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{k}}\bigg),\qquad\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}_{k}}=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}+i\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{k}}\bigg),\quad\forall k=1,\dots n\ .

If we denote with
g^{\mathbb{C}}
the
\mathbb{C}
-linear extension of
g
to
T_{\mathbb{C}}M
, then locally it can be written as

g^{\mathbb{C}}:=\sum_{j,k}g^{\mathbb{C}}_{j\bar{k}}\big(\mathrm{d}z^{j}\otimes\mathrm{d}\bar{z}^{k}+\mathrm{d}\bar{z}^{k}\otimes\mathrm{d}z^{j}\big)

where
g^{\mathbb{C}}_{j\bar{k}}:=g^{\mathbb{C}}\big(\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{j}},\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}_{k}}\big)=\frac{1}{4}\bigg(g\big(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}},\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}\big)+g\big(\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{j}},\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{k}}\big)\bigg)
, since the Hermitian condition implies that
g^{\mathbb{C}}_{jk}=g^{\mathbb{C}}_{\bar{j}\bar{k}}=0
and the symmetry that
\overline{g^{\mathbb{C}}_{j\bar{k}}}=g^{\mathbb{C}}_{k\bar{j}}
for each
j,k=1,\dots,n
.

In the following, by abuse of notation, we will denote with
g
the metric extended by
\mathbb{C}
-linearity on
T_{\mathbb{C}}M
. If
\nabla
denotes the Levi-Civita connection of
g
, then the only non vanishing Christoffel symbols are

\Gamma_{jk}^{i}:=g^{i\bar{l}}\frac{\partial g_{k\bar{l}}}{\partial z_{j}},\qquad\Gamma_{\bar{j}\bar{k}}^{\bar{i}}:=\overline{\Gamma_{jk}^{i}}

where
g^{j\bar{k}}
denotes the inverse metric computed on
\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{j}},\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}_{k}}
. The Riemann curvature tensor
R\in\Gamma\big(T_{\mathbb{C}}^{*}M\otimes T_{\mathbb{C}}M\otimes\End(T_{\mathbb{C}}M)\big)
of
\nabla
is given by

{{R_{i}}^{j}}_{k\bar{l}}=-\frac{\Gamma_{ki}^{j}}{\partial\bar{z}_{l}},\qquad R_{\alpha\bar{\beta}\gamma\bar{\delta}}={{R_{\alpha}}^{j}}_{\gamma\bar{\delta}}g_{j\bar{\beta}}\ .

As a consequence of the Bianchi identity, the Riemann tensor enjoys the following symmetries

R_{i\bar{j}k\bar{l}}=R_{i\bar{l}k\bar{j}}=R_{k\bar{j}i\bar{l}}=R_{k\bar{l}i\bar{j}}\ .

Finally, the Ricci tensor and the scalar curvature are given, respectively, by:

R_{i\bar{j}}=g^{k\bar{l}}R_{i\bar{j}k\bar{l}},\qquad\text{scal}(g)=g^{i\bar{j}}R_{i\bar{j}}\ .

Remark 5.1
.

All the properties listed so far hold in the case of pseudo-Kähler manifolds, indeed they are only a consequence of the fact that the metric is non-degenerate and that
\nabla g=\nabla I=0
(see
[
Zhe01
]
).

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
(M,g,I)
be a pseudo-Kähler manifold of complex dimension
n
and of neutral signature, then

R_{i\bar{j}}=-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z_{i}\partial\bar{z}_{j}}\log\big(\det(g)\big)

Proof.

First, notice that
\log\big(\det(g)\big)
is well-defined since the pseudo-metric
g
is of neutral signature, hence
\det(g)>0
. Then, by using the formulae above, we get

(symmetry of
R_{i\bar{j}k\bar{l}}
)

\displaystyle R_{i\bar{j}}

\displaystyle=g^{k\bar{l}}R_{k\bar{l}i\bar{j}}

(
R_{k\bar{l}i\bar{j}}=g_{p\bar{l}}{{R_{k}}^{p}}_{i\bar{j}}
)

\displaystyle={{R_{p}}^{p}}_{i\bar{j}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial\Gamma_{ip}^{p}}{\partial\bar{z}_{j}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}_{j}}\big(g^{p\bar{q}}\frac{\partial g_{p\bar{q}}}{\partial z_{i}}\big)

(Jacobi’s formula)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z_{i}\partial\bar{z}_{j}}\log\big(\det(g)\big)\ .

∎

Before computing the Ricci tensor and the scalar curvature of the new metrics, it should be noted that it is sufficient to do the computation at points
(i,u)\in\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}
. In fact, the
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
-action introduced in Section
2.3
allows us to reduce to the points
(i,w)
and the natural
S^{1}
-action on
\mathbb{C}
, presented in (
2.6
), to the points
(i,u)
, since both actions are isometric (Theorem
2.15
and Theorem
4.3
). Furthermore, we need to write
\det(\g_f)
and the inverse of the metric
\mathbf{g}_{f}
, extended by
\mathbb{C}
-linearity on
T_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{C})
, in terms of the coordinates
(z,w)
. We have:

\displaystyle(\mathbf{g}_{f}^{z\bar{z}})_{(i,u)}=\frac{1}{2(1-f)},\quad(\mathbf{g}_{f}^{w\bar{w}})_{(i,u)}=\frac{3(1-f+3f^{\prime}u^{2})}{8f^{\prime}(1-f)}

\displaystyle(\mathbf{g}_{f}^{z\bar{w}})_{(i,u)}=i\frac{3u}{4(1-f)},\quad\det(\g_f)_{(z,w)}=\frac{16}{9}\Imm(z)^{2}(f^{\prime})^{2}(1-f)^{2}\ .

Proposition 5.3
.

The Ricci tensor and the scalar curvature of the pseudo-Kähler metrics
(\mathbf{g}_{f},\mathbf{I},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{f})
are given by:

\displaystyle(R_{z\bar{z}})_{(i,u)}=\frac{1}{2}+3u^{2}\bigg(\frac{f^{\prime}}{1-f}-\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime}}\bigg)+\frac{9}{2}u^{4}G_{f}

\displaystyle(R_{w\bar{w}})_{(i,u)}=-2\bigg(\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime}}-\frac{f^{\prime}}{1-f}\bigg)+2u^{2}G_{f}

\displaystyle(R_{z\bar{w}})_{(i,u)}=i\bigg(3u\bigg(\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime}}-\frac{f^{\prime}}{1-f}\bigg)-3u^{3}G_{f}\bigg)

\displaystyle{\text{scal}}(\mathbf{g}_{f})_{(i,u)}=\frac{1}{1-f}-\frac{3}{4}\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{(f^{\prime})^{2}}+\frac{3}{2}\frac{u^{2}}{1-f}\bigg(6u^{2}G_{f}+\frac{11}{2}\bigg(\frac{f^{\prime}}{1-f}-\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime}}\bigg)+\frac{G_{f}(1-f)}{2f^{\prime}}\bigg)

where
\displaystyle G_{f}:=\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(1-f)+(f^{\prime})^{2}}{(1-f)^{2}}-\frac{f^{\prime\prime\prime}\cdot f-(f^{\prime\prime})^{2}}{(f^{\prime})^{2}}\ .

Proof.

Using the formulae above and the symmetries
\overline{R_{i\bar{j}}}=R_{j\bar{i}}
, the Ricci tensor is given by

\text{Ric}_{\mathbf{g}_{f}}=R_{z\bar{z}}\mathrm{d}z\otimes\mathrm{d}\bar{z}+R_{w\bar{w}}\mathrm{d}w\otimes\mathrm{d}\bar{w}+2\mathcal{R}e(R_{z\bar{w}})\mathrm{d}z\otimes\mathrm{d}\bar{w}\ .

According to Lemma
5.2
, the components can be computed as

R_{z\bar{z}}=-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z\partial\bar{z}}\log\big(\det(\g_f)\big),\quad R_{w\bar{w}}=-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial w\partial\bar{w}}\log\big(\det(\g_f)\big),\quad R_{z\bar{w}}=-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z\partial\bar{w}}\log\big(\det(\g_f)\big)\ .

Using the expression of
\det(\g_f)
found above we get

\log\big(\det(\g_f)\big)=\log\bigg(\frac{16}{9}\bigg)+2\log\big(\Imm(z)\big)+\log\big((f')^2\big)+2\log\big(1-f\big)\ .

Finally, recalling that the functions
f,f^{\prime},f^{\prime\prime},f^{\prime\prime\prime}
are all evaluated at
\Imm(z)^{3}|w|^{2}
and using the formula
\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\Imm(z)^{l}=\frac{l}{(2i)^{l}}\Imm(z)^{l-1}
we obtain the desired expression for the components of the Ricci tensor.

The scalar curvature is given, by definition, by

\text{scal}(\mathbf{g}_{f})=\mathbf{g}^{z\bar{z}}_{f}R_{z\bar{z}}+\mathbf{g}^{w\bar{w}}_{f}R_{w\bar{w}}+\mathbf{g}^{z\bar{w}}_{f}R_{z\bar{w}}+\mathbf{g}^{w\bar{z}}_{f}R_{w\bar{z}}\ .

Since
\mathbf{g}^{w\bar{z}}_{f}R_{w\bar{z}}=\overline{\mathbf{g}^{z\bar{w}}_{f}R_{z\bar{w}}}
, the final expression is

\text{scal}(\mathbf{g}_{f})=\mathbf{g}^{z\bar{z}}_{f}R_{z\bar{z}}+\mathbf{g}^{w\bar{w}}_{f}R_{w\bar{w}}+2\mathcal{R}e(\mathbf{g}^{z\bar{w}}_{f}R_{z\bar{w}})\ .

Now, we can directly compute the scalar curvature at the points
(i,u)
. By a simple, but long enough, direct calculation, one gets the desired formula.
∎

As explained at the beginning of the paper, these expressions are too complicated to be able to make any estimates on the scalar curvature. On the other hand, on
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\{0\}\subset\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}
the expression is considerably simplified, indeed given that
f(0)=0
, it follows that

(5.1)

\text{scal}(\mathbf{g}_{f})_{(i,0)}=1-\frac{3}{4}\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(0)}{f^{\prime}(0)^{2}}\ .

In particular, if we pick the function
f
to be of the form
f(t)=-kt
, with
k>0
, it becomes clear that the scalar curvature on
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\{0\}
is constant and equal to
1
.

Corollary 5.4
.

For any
(i,u)\in\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}^{*}
and for
f(t)=-kt
, with
k>0
, the scalar curvature
scal
(\mathbf{g}_{f})_{(i,u)}
is strictly less then
1
.

Proof.

For this choice of
f
, at the point
(i,u)
we have

f^{\prime}(t)=-k,\quad f^{\prime\prime}=f^{\prime\prime\prime}=0,\quad G_{f}(t)=\frac{k^{2}}{(1+kt^{2})},\qquad t=u^{2}\neq 0\ .

Thanks to Proposition
5.3
it follows that

{\text{scal}}(\mathbf{g}_{f})_{(i,u)}=\frac{1}{1+kt}\bigg(1+\frac{3}{2}t\bigg(\frac{6tk^{2}}{(1+kt)^{2}}-\frac{6k}{1+kt}\bigg)\bigg)\ .

Using that
\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+kt}<1
for all
k>0
and
t>0
, we obtain

{\text{scal}}(\mathbf{g}_{f})_{(i,u)}<1+\frac{9tk}{1+kt}\bigg(\frac{tk}{1+kt}-1\bigg)\ .

The last quantity is strictly less then
1
since

\frac{9tk}{1+kt}>0,\quad\frac{tk}{1+kt}-1<0,\quad\forall t,k>0\ .

∎

5.2.
The isometry group

At this point we have all the tools necessary to prove the last result of the paper. It is clear from Theorem
2.15
that any matrix in
\mathbb{P}\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
and any rotation of the fiber generated by
S^{1}
, is an isometry of
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}
with respect to
\mathbf{g}_{f}
. Plus, the two actions commute. We will show that, whenever
f(t)=-kt
, with
k>0
, any other isometry
h
, isotopic to the identity, can be written as composition
h=P\circ e^{i\theta}
for some
(P,e^{i\theta})\in\mathbb{P}\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})\times S^{1}
.

We recall the following standard result, the proof of which can be found in any text on Riemannian geometry.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
h_{1},h_{2}:(M_{1},g_{1})\to(M_{2},g_{2})
be two isometries between Riemannian smooth connected manifolds. If there is a point
p\in M_{1}
such that
h_{1}(p)=h_{2}(p)
and
\mathrm{d}_{p}h_{1}\equiv\mathrm{d}_{p}h_{2}
, then
h_{1}\equiv h_{2}
.

Remark 5.6
.

It is not difficult to see that the proof of this lemma can also be extended to the pseudo-Riemannian case, since only the existence of the exponential map and the connectedness hypothesis on the manifolds are used.

Theorem B
.

Let
\mathrm{Isom}_{0}\big(\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{C},\mathbf{g}_{f}\big)
be the connected component of the identity of the isometry group
\mathrm{Isom}\big(\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{C},\mathbf{g}_{f}\big)
. If
f(t)=-kt
, with
k>0
, then
\mathrm{Isom}_{0}\big(\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{C},\mathbf{g}_{f}\big)\cong\mathbb{P}\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})\times S^{1}
.

Proof.

First notice that each isometry
h\in\mathrm{Isom}_{0}\big(\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{C},\mathbf{g}_{f}\big)
preserves the copy of the hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\{0\}
. In fact, if there was an isometry
\widetilde{h}
with
\widetilde{h}(z,0)=(z^{\prime},w)
for some
(z^{\prime},w)\in\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}^{*}
, then we would get the following contradiction

\displaystyle=\text{scal}(\mathbf{g}_{f})_{(z,0)}

(
\widetilde{h}
is an isometry)

\displaystyle=\big(\widetilde{h}^{*}\text{scal}(\mathbf{g}_{f})\big)_{(z,0)}

\displaystyle=\text{scal}(\mathbf{g}_{f})_{(z^{\prime},w)}

(Corollary
5.4
)

\displaystyle<1\ .

Pick any
h\in\mathrm{Isom}_{0}\big(\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{C},\mathbf{g}_{f}\big)
such that
h(z,0)=(z^{\prime},0)
for some
z,z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{H}^{2}
. We can always assume that
h(i,0)=(i,0)
, indeed thanks to Remark
2.14
there exist two matrices
P,P^{\prime}\in\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
such that
(z,0)=P\cdot(i,0),\ (z^{\prime},0)=P^{\prime}\cdot(i,0)
, hence the isometry
(P^{\prime})^{-1}\circ h\circ P
would fix the point
(i,0)
. If we consider the linear map
\mathrm{d}_{(i,0)}h:T_{i}\mathbb{H}^{2}\times T_{0}\mathbb{C}\to T_{i}\mathbb{H}^{2}\times T_{0}\mathbb{C}
restricted to horizontal directions, we can again assume, up to pre- and post-composition with elements in
\mathbb{P}\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
as before, that
\mathrm{d}_{(i,0)}h(Z,0)=(Z,0)
, for all
Z\in T_{i}\mathbb{H}^{2}
. This implies that

\mathrm{d}_{(i,0)}h_{|_{T_{i}\mathbb{H}^{2}}}=\mathrm{Id}_{T_{i}\mathbb{H}^{2}}\ .

Now if

is a real vertical direction, then
\mathrm{d}_{(i,0)}h(0,U)=(0,W)

∎

During the proof of the theorem we used that each isometry isotopic to the identity preserves the orientations on both
\mathbb{H}^{2}
and
\mathbb{C}
. There are other three possibilities for an arbitrary isometry
h\in\mathrm{Isom}\big(\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{C},\mathbf{g}_{f}\big)
:

(1)

h
reverses the orientation on
\mathbb{H}^{2}
and preserves the orientation on
\mathbb{C}
. Then, by composing with
h_{1}(z,w):=(-\bar{z},w)
we get an isometry preserving both orientations. Hence, the proof of Theorem
B
holds for
h\circ h_{1}
.

(2)

h
preserves the orientation on
\mathbb{H}^{2}
and reverses the orientation on
\mathbb{C}
. Then, by composing with
h_{2}(z,w)=(z,\bar{w})
we get an isometry preserving both orientations. Hence, we have the same conclusion as above for
h\circ h_{2}
.

(3)

Finally,
h
reverses both the orientations. Then, the same argument applies to
h\circ h_{1}\circ h_{2}
.

In the end, we proved the following

Corollary C
.

If
f(t)=-kt
, with
k>0
, then any isometry
h:\big(\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{C},\mathbf{g}_{f}\big)\to\big(\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{C},\mathbf{g}_{f}\big)
can be written as

h=P\circ e^{i\theta},\quad h=P\circ e^{i\theta}\circ h_{1},\quad h=P\circ e^{i\theta}\circ h_{2},\quad h=P\circ e^{i\theta}\circ h_{1}\circ h_{2}

for some
P\in\mathbb{P}\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
and
e^{i\theta}\in S^{1}
.
