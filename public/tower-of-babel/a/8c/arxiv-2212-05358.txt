Abstract
Numerous analysis methods for quantitative attack tree analysis have been proposed. These algorithms compute relevant security metrics, i.e. performance indicators that quantify how good the security of a system is; typical metrics being the most likely attack, the cheapest, or the most damaging one. However, existing methods are only geared towards specific metrics or do not work on general attack trees. This paper classifies attack trees in two dimensions: proper trees vs. directed acyclic graphs (i.e. with shared subtrees); and static vs. dynamic gates. For three out of these four classes, we propose novel algorithms that work over a generic attribute domain, encompassing a large number of concrete security metrics defined on the attack tree semantics; dynamic attack trees with directed acyclic graph structure are left as an open problem. We also analyse the computational complexity of our methods.

Static trees

The simplest category of Attack Trees are tree-structured static
AT
s.
As shown in a seminal paper by Mauw & Oosdijk
[
14
]
, metrics can be computed for these
AT
s in a bottom-up fashion, using appropriate operators
\mathbin{\triangledown}
and
\mathbin{\vartriangle}
on a set
V
, resp. for the
OR
and and gates in the tree.
We show this as
Algorithm

1
.
A key insight in
[
14
]
is that this procedure works whenever the algebraic structure
(\text{\scalebox{.92}{$V$}},\mathbin{\vartriangle},\mathbin{\triangledown})
constitutes a semiring, i.e.
\mathbin{\vartriangle}
must distribute over
\mathbin{\triangledown}
.

We provide an alternative proof of correctness for this result: while
[
14
]
deploys rewriting rules for attack trees, we show in
Section

IX
that it directly follows from the validity of
modular analysis
on DAG-structured ATs.
Furthermore, we propose new categories of attribute domains, which extend the application of the bottom-up algorithm to compute popular security metrics. Ordered semiring domains constitute an important category of metrics, for which we show that derived metrics such as Pareto fronts and
k
-top values also fall within the semiring attribute domain framework.

Static DAGs

It is well-known that static
AT
s with
DAG
structure cannot be studied with bottom-up procedures
[
23
,
29
]
.
Many algorithms exist to tackle such
AT
s, mostly geared to specific metrics
[
9
,
17
,
12
,
21
,
18
]
.

Section

V
presents a generic algorithm that works for any semiring attribute domain
(\text{\scalebox{.92}{$V$}},\mathbin{\triangledown},\mathbin{\vartriangle})
that is absorbing, i.e.
x\mathbin{\triangledown}(x\mathbin{\vartriangle}y)=x
, and has neutral elements
1_{\!\mathbin{\triangledown}}
and
1_{\!\mathbin{\vartriangle}}
for operators
\mathbin{\triangledown}
and
\mathbin{\vartriangle}
resp.

Concretely, we exploit a binary decision diagram representation (
BDD
) of the attack tree.
Our algorithm visits each
BDD
node once and is thus linear in its size.
The caveat is that
BDD
s can be of exponential size in the number of basic attack steps (
BAS
es), but one cannot hope for faster algorithms: as we show, computing a minimal attack is an NP-hard problem. However, in practice the
BDD
approach still yields computational gains: we show that we can split up the calculation according to the
modules
of the
AT
, i.e. sub
DAG
s only connected to the rest of the
AT
by their root.
This speeds up calculations considerably.
Moreover,
BDD
s are known to be compact in practice
[
30
]
, and allow parallel traversals
[
31
]
, making them an overall efficient choice.
Furthermore, we show that the bottom-up algorithm—of linear runtime on any static tree—works also when applied to
DAG

AT
s, if the operators
\mathbin{\triangledown}
and
\mathbin{\vartriangle}
are idempotent, i.e.
x\mathbin{\triangledown}x=x\mathbin{\vartriangle}x=x
.

Dynamic trees

Metrics for dynamic attack trees (DATs) are usually decoupled from semantics, and defined either on the syntactic
AT
structure, or ad hoc for the selected computation method
[
10
,
5
,
32
,
18
]
.
The main obstacle to a semantics-based approach is to choose semantics for
DAT
s in a way that supports a proper definition of metric, i.e. that is compatible with the notion of metric of static
AT
s, and that is as generic as the attribute domains from
[
14
]
.
In particular, the interaction among multiple
SAND
gates is nontrivial, because they may impose conflicting execution orders on the
BAS
of the tree.

We follow
[
26
,
8
]
in giving semantics to
DAT
s via partially ordered sets (
posets
).
Each poset
\langle{\text{\scalebox{.92}{$A$}}},{\prec}\rangle
represents an attack scenario, where

A

collects all attacks steps to be performed, and
a\prec b
indicates that step
a
must be completed before step
b
starts.
This set up enables us to define a notion of metric for
DAT
s based on their semantics.

A key contribution is defining general metrics for dynamic attack trees, and showing that tree-structured DATs are analysable by extending the bottom-up algorithm with an additional operator (see
Algorithm

4
)
.
Concretely, we use attribute domains with three operators:
\mathbin{\triangledown}
,
\mathbin{\vartriangle}
,
\mathbin{\vartriangleright}
, where
\mathbin{\vartriangleright}
distributes over
\mathbin{\triangledown}
and
\mathbin{\vartriangle}
, and
\mathbin{\vartriangle}
distributes over
\mathbin{\triangledown}
.
We prove this
algorithm
correct in our formal semantics.
This is relevant and non-trivial because
(a) earlier algorithms do not provide explicit correctness results in terms of semantics, and
(b) the metrics are formally defined on the poset semantics of a
DAT
, while the algorithm works on its syntactic
AT
structure.

Dynamic DAGs

Efficient computation of metrics for
DAG
-structured
DAT
s is left as future research challenge.
A naïve, inefficient algorithm would enumerate all posets in the semantics.
Instead, one could extend
BDD
-algorithms for static
DAG
s to dynamic
AT
s.
This is non-trivial, as
BDD
s ignore the order of attack steps.
Thus, efficient analysis of
DAG
-structured DATs is an important open problem.
Yet we do show how modular analysis works here as well, and can be used to speed up any algorithm.

II
Attack Trees

II-A

Attack tree models

Syntactically, an attack tree is a rooted
DAG
that models an undesired event caused by a malicious party, e.g. a theft.

AT
s show a top-down decomposition of a top-level attack—the root of the
DAG
—into simpler steps.
The leaves are basic steps carried out by the attacker.
The nodes between the basic steps and the root are intermediate attacks, and are labelled with gates to indicate how its input nodes (children) combine to make the intermediate attack succeed.

Basic Attack Steps

The leaves of the
AT
represent indivisible actions carried out by the attacker, e.g. smash a window,
decrypt a file, etc.
These
BAS
nodes can be enriched with attributes, such as its execution time, the cost incurred, and the probability with which the
BAS
occurs.
We model attributes via an attribution function
\attrOp\colon\text{BAS}\to\text{\scalebox{.92}{$V$}}
.

Gates

Non-leaf nodes serve to model intermediate steps that lead to the top-level goal.
Each has a logical
gate
that describes how its children combine to make it succeed:
an
OR
gate means that the intermediate attack will succeed if any of its child nodes succeeds;
an and gate indicates that all children must succeed, in any order or possibly in parallel;
a
SAND
gate (viz. sequential- and ) needs all children to succeed sequentially in a left-to-right order.

The pilfering is considered atomic, while the cryptoattack consists of two steps which must both succeed: intercepting transactions, and abusing weak

Note that

Instead,

\text{\scalebox{.92}{$T$}}_{\hskip-2.0pt\text{{$d$}}}

Its

In both cases the attacker must walk next to the victim, so these gates share the
BAS

\text{\scalebox{.92}{$T$}}_{\hskip-2.0pt\text{{$d$}}}
not a tree.
In the case of “luck” the order of events matters: if the attacker first walks next to the victim and then a traffic accident happens, the pick-pocket succeeds.
Thus, this intermediate attack is modelled with a
SAND

Instead, “fastest fingers” is an inherent attacker flair that is always present.
It is thus meaningless to speak of an order w.r.t. the attacker-victim encounter, so an and gate is used.

Security metrics

A key goal in quantitative security analysis is to compute relevant
security metrics
, which quantify how well a system performs in terms of security.
Typical examples are the cost of the cheapest attack, the probability of the most likely one, the damage produced by the most harmful one, and combinations thereof.
Security metrics for
AT
s are typically obtained by combining the attribute values
\attrOp(a)\in\text{\scalebox{.92}{$V$}}
assigned to each
a\in\text{BAS}
.
For instance, the cheapest attack is that for which the sum of the cost of its BASes is minimal.

The topic of this paper is how to compute large classes of security metrics in generic and efficient ways
.

II-B

Attack tree syntax

AT
s are rooted
DAG
s with typed nodes: we consider types
\mathbb{T}=\{\mathtt{BAS},\mathtt{OR},\mathtt{AND},\mathtt{SAND}\}
.
The edges of an
AT
are given by a function
\chOp
that assigns to each node its (possibly empty) sequence of children.
We use set notation for sequences, e.g.
e\in(e_{1},\ldots,e_{m})
means
\exists i.\,e_{i}=e
, and we denote the empty sequence by
\varepsilon
.

Definition II.2
.

An
attack tree
is a tuple

\text{\scalebox{.92}{$T$}}=\left(\text{\scalebox{.92}{$N$}},\typOp,\chOp\right)
where:

•

N

is a finite set of
nodes
;

•

\typOp\colon\text{\scalebox{.92}{$N$}}\to\mathbb{T}

gives the
type
of each node;

•

\chOp\colon\text{\scalebox{.92}{$N$}}\to\text{\scalebox{.92}{$N$}}^{\ast}

gives the sequence of
children
of a node.

Moreover,

T

satisfies the following constraints:

•

(\text{\scalebox{.92}{$N$}},\text{\scalebox{.92}{$E$}})
is a connected
DAG
, where

.

\text{\scalebox{.92}{$E$}}=\left\{(v,u)\in\text{\scalebox{.92}{$N$}}^{2}\mid u\in\chOp({v})\right\}
;

•

T

has a unique root, denoted
\text{\scalebox{.92}{$R$}}_{\text{\scalebox{.92}{$T$}}}
:

.

\exists!\,\text{\scalebox{.92}{$R$}}_{\text{\scalebox{.92}{$T$}}}\in\text{\scalebox{.92}{$N$}}.\penalty\ \forall v\in\text{\scalebox{.92}{$N$}}.\penalty\ \text{\scalebox{.92}{$R$}}_{\text{\scalebox{.92}{$T$}}}\not\in\chOp({v})
;

•

\text{BAS}_{\text{\scalebox{.92}{$T$}}}
nodes are the leaves of

T

:

.

\forall v\in\text{\scalebox{.92}{$N$}}.\penalty\ {\typOp({v})=\mathtt{BAS}}\Leftrightarrow{\chOp({v})=\varepsilon}
.

We omit the subindex

T

if no ambiguity arises, e.g. an attack tree
\text{\scalebox{.92}{$T$}}=(\text{\scalebox{.92}{$N$}},\typOp,\chOp)
defines a set
\text{BAS}\subseteq\text{\scalebox{.92}{$N$}}
of basic attack steps.
If
u\in\chOp({v})
then
u
is called a
child
of
v
, and
v
is a
parent
of
u
.
We write
v=\and(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})
if
{\typOp({v})=\mathtt{AND}}
and
{\chOp({v})=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})}
, and analogously for
OR
and
SAND
, denoting
\chOp({v})_{i}=v_{i}
.
Moreover we denote the universe of
AT
s by
\mathscr{T}
and call
\text{\scalebox{.92}{$T$}}\in\mathscr{T}

tree-structured
if

{\forall v,u\in\text{\scalebox{.92}{$N$}}.\chOp({v})\cap\chOp({u})=\varepsilon}
;
else we say that

T

is
DAG-structured
.
If
\mathtt{SAND}\notin\typOp({N})
we say that

T

is a
static attack tree
(
SAT
); else it is a
dynamic attack tree
(
DAT
).

III
Static Attack Trees

In the absence of
SAND
gates the order of execution of the BASes is irrelevant.
This allows for simple semantics given in terms of a Boolean function called
structure function
.
The computation of security metrics, however, crucially depends on whether the AT is tree- or DAG-structured.

III-A

Semantics for static attack trees

The semantics of a
SAT
is defined by its successful attack scenarios, in turn given by its structure function.
First, we define the notions of attack and attack suite.

Definition III.1
.

An
attack scenario
, or shortly an
attack
,
of a static
AT

T

is a subset of its basic attack steps:

\text{\scalebox{.92}{$A$}}\subseteq\text{BAS}_{T}
.
An
attack suite
is a set of attacks

\text{$\pazocal{S}$}\subseteq 2^{\text{BAS}_{T}}
.
We denote by

\mathscr{A}_{T}={2^{\text{BAS}_{T}}}

the universe of attacks of

T

, and by

\rotatebox[origin]{-15.0}{$\mathscr{S}$\!}_{T}=2^{2^{\text{BAS}_{T}}}

the universe of attack suites of

T

.
We omit the subscripts when there is no confusion.

Intuitively, an attack suite
\text{$\pazocal{S}$}\in\rotatebox[origin]{-15.0}{$\mathscr{S}$\!}
represents different ways in which the system can be compromised.
From those, one is interested in attacks
\text{\scalebox{.92}{$A$}}\in\text{$\pazocal{S}$}
that actually represent a threat.
For instance for
\text{\scalebox{.92}{$T$}}_{\hskip-2.0pt\text{{$s$}}}
in
Example

II.1
one such attack is
\{t,p\}
.
In contrast,
\{t\}
is an attack that does not succeed, i.e. it does not reach the top-level goal.
The structure function
\sfun_{\!\text{\scalebox{.92}{$T$}}}(v,\text{\scalebox{.92}{$A$}})
indicates whether the attack
\text{\scalebox{.92}{$A$}}\in\mathscr{A}
succeeds at node
v\in\text{\scalebox{.92}{$N$}}
of

T

.
For Booleans we use
\mathbb{B}=\{\mathtt{{1}},\mathtt{{0}}\}
.

Definition III.2
.

The
structure function

\sfun_{\!\text{\scalebox{.92}{$T$}}}\colon\text{\scalebox{.92}{$N$}}\times\mathscr{A}\to\mathbb{B}

of a static attack tree

is given by:

\displaystyle\sfun_{\!\text{\scalebox{.92}{$T$}}}(v,\text{\scalebox{.92}{$A$}})=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathtt{{1}}&\parbox{55.0pt}{if\penalty\ $\typOp({v})=\mathtt{OR}$}\penalty\ \text{and}\penalty\ \exists u\in\chOp({v}).\sfun_{\!\text{\scalebox{.92}{$T$}}}(u,\text{\scalebox{.92}{$A$}})=\mathtt{{1}},\\
\mathtt{{1}}&\parbox{55.0pt}{if\penalty\ $\typOp({v})=\mathtt{AND}$}\penalty\ \text{and}\penalty\ \forall u\in\chOp({v}).\sfun_{\!\text{\scalebox{.92}{$T$}}}(u,\text{\scalebox{.92}{$A$}})=\mathtt{{1}},\\
\mathtt{{1}}&\parbox{55.0pt}{if\penalty\ $\typOp({v})=\mathtt{BAS}$}\penalty\ \text{and}\penalty\ v\in\text{\scalebox{.92}{$A$}},\\
\mathtt{{0}}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

An attack

A

is said to
reach
a node
v
if
\sfun_{\!\text{\scalebox{.92}{$T$}}}(v,\text{\scalebox{.92}{$A$}})=\mathtt{{1}}
, i.e. it makes

is a

Stochastic analyses

Semirings form a bicomplete category, so finite and infinite products exist
[

This allows to propagate not only tuples of attribute values, but also functions over them.
In particular,

Such functions serve e.g. to consider attack probabilities (and cost, and damage) as functions that evolve on time.

Absorbing semirings

Although in this paper we calculate metrics by considering all
minimal
attacks, one could also simply consider all attacks.
For many metrics this does not make a difference: for example, the successful attack with minimal cost will always be a minimal attack, since adding
BAS

Therefore, in the calculation of min cost we may as well take the minimum over all successful attacks, rather than just minimal attacks.
On the other hand, when calculating max damage one will get a different answer when taking all attacks into account, as the full attack of all
BAS
es will do more damage than a smaller minimal attack.
The difference between these metrics can be described mathematically as follows.
We call a semiring attribute domain

Dynamic Attack Trees

In

VI-A

Partially-ordered attacks

As for the static case, the semantics of a dynamic attack tree (
DAT
) is given by its successful attack scenarios.
However,
DAT
s necessitate a formal notion of order, because a sequential gate
\text{SAND}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})
succeeds only if every
v_{i}
child is completely executed before
v_{i+1}
starts.
This models dependencies in the order of events.
For example, in Håstad’s broadcast attack,
n
messages must first be intercepted, from which an
n
-th root (the secret key) may be computed.
This standard interpretation is
ordered
, and an activated
BAS
is uninterruptedly completed.
This rules out constructs that introduce circular dependencies such as
\text{SAND}(a,b,a)
.​
5
5

5

Cf. Kumar et al. (2015), who separate activation from execution of a
BAS
and can therefore operate with
\text{SAND}(a,b,a)

[
5
]
.

Thus, an attack scenario that operates with
SAND
gates is not just a set
\text{\scalebox{.92}{$A$}}\subseteq\text{BAS}
, but rather a partially-ordered set (poset)
\langle{\text{\scalebox{.92}{$A$}}},{\prec}\rangle
: here
\prec
is a strict partial order, where
a\prec b
indicates that
a\in\text{\scalebox{.92}{$A$}}
must be carried out strictly before
b\in\text{\scalebox{.92}{$A$}}
. Incomparable basic attack steps can be executed in any order, or in parallel.
Thus, the attack
\langle{\text{\scalebox{.92}{$A$}}},{\prec}\rangle
indicates that all
BAS
in

A

must be executed, and their execution order will respect
{\prec}
.
This succinct construct can represent combinatorially many execution orders of
BAS
.
For instance
\langle{\{a,b\}},{\varnothing}\rangle
allows three executions: the sequence
(a,b)
, and
(b,a)
, and the parallel execution
a\|b
.
Instead,
\langle{\{a,b\}},{\{(a,b)\}}\rangle
only allows the execution sequence
(a,b)
.
Note that strict partial orders are irreflexive and transitive, so e.g.
\text{SAND}(a,b,c)
gives rise to
{\prec}=\{(a,b),(b,c),(a,c)\}
.

Satisfiability of DATs

Contrary to the static case, a dynamic

Our definition of semantics avoids this issue:

.

The

The same fundamental concepts of metric for static

1.

finally, the metric
\metrOp

\mathbin{\triangledown}

{O=\langle{\text{\scalebox{.92}{$A$}}},{\prec}\rangle}

\metrSOp

\mathbin{\vartriangleright}

\metrAOp
uses
\mathbin{\vartriangle}

\metrOp

\pazocal{S}

XII
Conclusions

This paper presents algorithms to compute quantitative security metrics on attack trees.
This is done in two steps: first, we revise and consolidate semantics in line with the literature, and we define metrics on these semantics, providing formal grounds on which to demonstrate the correctness of any devised computation method.
A key contribution here is the adaptation of non-restrictive poset semantics for dynamic attack trees (
Section

VI-B
), which allows for a formal definition of general metrics on a wide range of
DAT
s.

Second, we introduce efficient and unifying algorithms that can compute many popular metrics, including sets of metrics (i.e. several metrics simultaneously as in
k
-top and Pareto analyses).
Here, the
BDD
-based approach for general metrics of
Algorithm

2
is a prominent result, together with
Lemmas

X.3
and
X.4
that show how to use single-value algorithms for the computation of set metrics.

We noted — in
Section

VI-B
— that our
DAT
semantics rules out some interleavings in the execution of
SAND
gates, e.g.
(b,a,c)
for
{\text{SAND}\big(a,\and(b,c)\big)}
, even when these sequences would arguably result in a succesful attack.
To allow such sequences it is necessary to use formulae — rather than individual
BAS
es— as elements of the partial order.
For the
DAT
above, this would yield the relation
a\prec(b\land c)
, which allows
(b,a,c)
because the formulae in that sequence are satisfied in the order “first
a
, then
b\land c
.” Such ordering graphs are a promising research direction.

Further lines for future work also include:
developing efficient algorithms to compute metrics
on
DAG
-structured dynamic
AT
s;
extending our
AT
syntax to include
sequential-
OR
gates

[
5
,
60
]
;
and extending our general metrics to Attack–Defense Trees
[
35
,
61
]
.
Other important future work is to implement the methods and algorithms from this paper in real-life case studies. Interesting future work in the opposite direction would be to frame attack tree metrics in a wider, category-theoretical framework. Operad algebras may form a useful tool for research in this direction, as attribute domains can be regarded as algebras of the operad of (dynamic) attack trees.

Proofs for modular analysis

Because of

We start off with a definition that expresses a useful way of combining two posets into one.

Definition B.1
.

Let
O_{1}=\langle{X_{1}},{\prec_{1}}\rangle
and
O_{2}=\langle{X_{2}},{\prec_{2}}\rangle
be two nonempty posets, and let
x_{1}\in X_{1}
. Define the
insertion

O_{1}[x_{1}/O_{2}]
to be the poset
\langle{X},{\prec}\rangle
given by

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=X_{1}\setminus\{x_{1}\}\cup X_{2}

\displaystyle x\prec x^{\prime}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\begin{cases}x\prec_{1}x^{\prime}&\textrm{if $x,x^{\prime}\in X_{1}$}\\
x\prec_{2}x^{\prime}&\textrm{if $x,x^{\prime}\in X_{2}$}\\
x\prec_{1}x_{1}&\textrm{if $x\in X_{1}$ and $x^{\prime}\in X_{2}$}\\
x_{1}\prec_{1}x^{\prime}&\textrm{ if $x\in X_{2}$ and $x^{\prime}\in X_{1}$}.\end{cases}

As the notation suggests,
O_{1}[x_{1}/O_{2}]
is the poset obtained by replacing
x_{1}
in
O_{1}
by the entire poset
O_{2}
.

For a poset
O
, let
\operatorname{MC}_{O}
be its set of maximal chains (which are posets themselves). We can find the maximal chains of an insertion as follows:

Lemma B.2
.

One has

\displaystyle\operatorname{MC}_{O_{1}[x_{1}/O_{2}]}

\displaystyle=\{C_{1}\in\operatorname{MC}_{O_{1}}\mid x_{1}\notin C_{1}\}

\displaystyle\ \ \cup\{C_{1}[x_{1}/C_{2}]\mid x_{1}\in C_{1}\in\operatorname{MC}_{O_{1}},C_{2}\in\operatorname{MC}_{O_{2}}\}.

Proof.

Let
C=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in\operatorname{MC}_{O_{1}[x_{1}/O_{2}]}
. If
X_{2}\cap C=\varnothing
, then
C
is also a maximal chain of
O_{1}
not containing
x_{1}
. Now suppose
C\cap X_{2}\neq\varnothing
. By the nature of the poset
O_{1}[x_{1}/O_{2}]
, if
c_{i}\in X_{1}
, and
j<i<k
, then it is not possible that both
c_{j},c_{k}\in X_{2}
. It follows that all the elements of
X_{2}\cap C
form a single block
C_{2}=(c_{i},\ldots,c_{j})
; then
C_{2}
is a chain in
X_{2}
. Furthermore,
C_{1}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{i-1},x_{1},c_{j+1},\ldots,c_{n})
is a chain in
X_{1}
, and

. Furthermore, if
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
were not maximal, then at least one of them could be extended. This would also extend
C_{1}[x_{1}/C_{2}]
, so
C
would not be maximal. This shows “
\subset
”.

Conversely, let
C_{1}
be a maximal chain of
O_{1}
not containing
x_{1}
; this is also a chain of
O_{1}
. Suppose
C_{1}
can be extended by an

, then

could be extended by

; if not, then
C_{1}
could be extended by

. Either way,
C_{1}
is not maximal, which is a contradiction. Similarly, if
C_{1}
is a maximal chain of
O_{1}
containing
x_{1}
, and
C_{2}
is a maximal chain of
O_{2}
, then one can show that
C_{1}[x_{1}/C_{2}]
is maximal. This shows “
\supset

∎

Theorem
V.7
.

Let
T=(N,E)
be a SAT, let
D=(V,\mathbin{\triangledown},\mathbin{\vartriangle})
be an attribute domain, and let
\alpha\colon N\rightarrow V
be an attribution. Suppose that
D
is a both idempotent and absorbing. Then
\metrOp(T)=\mathtt{BU_{SAT}}(T,R_{T},\alpha,D)
.

Proof.

For a node
v
, let
\operatorname{Suc}_{v}\in\rotatebox[origin]{-15.0}{$\mathscr{S}$\!}
be the suite of attacks that activate
v
; then
\llbracket{v}\rrbracket
is the set of minimal elements in
\operatorname{Suc}_{v}
under the partial order
\subseteq
. It follows from the absorption axiom that

\bigoperOR_{A\in\llbracket{v}\rrbracket}\bigoperAND_{a\in A}\alpha(a)=\bigoperOR_{A\in\operatorname{Suc}_{v}}\bigoperAND_{a\in A}\alpha(a).

We now prove the theorem by showing that
\widecheck{\alpha}(v)=\mathtt{BU_{\mkern 1.0muSAT}}(T,v,\alpha,D)
by induction over
T
. It is clear for BASes. Suppose
v=\text{OR}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})
; then
\operatorname{Suc}_{v_{i}}=\bigcup_{i}\operatorname{Suc}_{v_{i}}
. It follows that

\displaystyle\widecheck{\alpha}(v)

\displaystyle=\bigoperOR_{A\in\bigcup_{i}\operatorname{Suc}_{v_{i}}}\widehat{\alpha}(A)

\displaystyle=\bigoperOR_{A\in\bigsqcup_{i}\operatorname{Suc}_{v_{i}}}\widehat{\alpha}(A)

(14)

\displaystyle=\bigoperOR_{i\leq n}\bigoperOR_{A\in\operatorname{Suc}_{v_{i}}}\widehat{\alpha}(A)

\displaystyle=\bigoperOR_{i\leq n}\mathtt{BU_{\mkern 1.0muSAT}}(T,v_{i},\alpha,D)

(15)

\displaystyle=\mathtt{BU_{\mkern 1.0muSAT}}(T,v,\alpha,D).

Here (
14
) follows from the idempotence of
\mathbin{\triangledown}
, and (
15
) follows from the induction hypothesis.

Now suppose that
v=\and(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})
; then
\operatorname{Suc}_{v}=\bigcap_{i}\operatorname{Suc}_{v_{i}}
. We claim that

\bigcap_{i}\operatorname{Suc}_{v_{i}}=\left\{\bigcup_{i}A_{i}\middle|\forall i\colon A_{i}\in\operatorname{Suc}_{v_{i}}\right\}.

The claim is proven as follows. On one hand, if
A\in\bigcap_{i}\operatorname{Suc}_{v_{i}}
, then we can write
A=\bigcap_{i}A
, which shows that it is part of the right hand side. On the other hand, each
\operatorname{Suc}_{v_{i}}
is upward closed: if
A\in\operatorname{Suc}_{v_{i}}
and
A\subseteq A^{\prime}
, then
A^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Suc}_{v_{i}}
. It follows that if
A_{i}\in\operatorname{Suc}_{v_{i}}
for all
i
, then
\bigcup_{i}A_{i}\in\bigcap_{i}\operatorname{Suc}_{v_{i}}
. This proves the claim. From the claim we find

\displaystyle\widecheck{\alpha}(v)

\displaystyle=\bigoperOR_{A\in\cap_{i}\operatorname{Suc}_{v_{i}}}\bigoperAND_{a\in A}\alpha(a)

\displaystyle=\bigoperOR_{\forall i\colon A_{i}\in\operatorname{Suc}_{v_{i}}}\bigoperAND_{a\in\bigcup_{i}A_{i}}\alpha(a)

\displaystyle=\bigoperOR_{\forall i\colon A_{i}\in\operatorname{Suc}_{v_{i}}}\bigoperAND_{a\in\bigsqcup_{i}A_{i}}\alpha(a)

(16)

\displaystyle=\bigoperOR_{\forall i\colon A_{i}\in\operatorname{Suc}_{v_{i}}}\bigoperAND_{i\leq n}\bigoperAND_{a\in A_{i}}\alpha(a)

\displaystyle=\bigoperAND_{i\leq n}\bigoperOR_{A_{i}\in\operatorname{Suc}_{v_{i}}}\bigoperAND_{a\in A_{i}}\alpha(a)

\displaystyle=\bigoperAND_{i\leq n}\mathtt{BU_{\mkern 1.0muSAT}}(T,v_{i},\alpha,D)

(17)

\displaystyle=\mathtt{BU_{\mkern 1.0muSAT}}(T,v,\alpha,D).

Here we use the idempotence of
\mathbin{\vartriangle}
in (
16
) and the induction hypothesis in (
17
).
∎

Theorem
V.2
.

Given a DAG-structured static AT, the problem of computing any successful attack of minimal size is NP-hard.

Proof.

A static attack tree

T

is equivalent to a logical formula whose atoms are the elements of
BAS
: denote this formula
\logicformula_{\text{\scalebox{.92}{$T$}}}
and note that none of its atoms appears negated.
The problem of finding the smallest minimal attack in

T

can thus be reformulated as finding the smallest
A\subseteq\text{BAS}
whose elements must evaluate to
\mathtt{{1}}
to satisfy
\logicformula_{\text{\scalebox{.92}{$T$}}}
.
Denote this problem
minSAT
.
We now reduce
CNFSAT
, the satisfiability problem for arbitrary logic formulae in conjunctive normal form, to solving
minSAT
.
Let
\varphi=\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}=\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}\bigvee_{j=1}^{N_{i}}\ell_{i}^{j}
be one such arbitrary formula with atoms
\mathrm{A}=\{a_{k}\}_{k=1}^{m}
.
Define
\pos(\ell)\doteq\tilde{a}
if the literal
\ell=\neg a
, and
\pos(\ell)\doteq a
otherwise, where
\tilde{a}
is a fresh non-negated (“positive”) atom.
Now let
\hat{c}_{i}\doteq\bigvee_{j=1}^{N_{i}}\pos(\ell_{i}^{j})
and
\hat{a}\doteq(a\lor\tilde{a})
, and consider the formula
\widehat{\varphi}=\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}\hat{c}_{i}\,\bigwedge_{k=1}^{m}\hat{a}_{k}
.
Since no atom of
\widehat{\varphi}
is negated, by
minSAT
we can find some
\operatorname{\mathit{g}}\colon\mathrm{A}\cup\tilde{\mathrm{A}}\to\mathbb{B}
that satisfies
\widehat{\varphi}
, mapping to
\mathtt{{1}}
a
minimum amount of atoms
from
\mathrm{A}\cup\tilde{\mathrm{A}}
.
Now consider the second part of the conjunction in
\widehat{\varphi}
: satisfying
\bigwedge_{k=1}^{m}\hat{a}_{k}
requires, minimally, mapping
m
atoms to
\mathtt{{1}}
, e.g. all the
\mathrm{A}
, or all the
\tilde{\mathrm{A}}
.
But then:

•

if
\operatorname{\mathit{g}}
maps
exactly
m
atoms
to
\mathtt{{1}}
, then for every
\hat{a}_{k}
it mapped either
a_{k}
or
\tilde{a}_{k}
to
\mathtt{{1}}

\therefore

\varphi
is satisfiable;

•

else
\exists\penalty\ \hat{c}_{i},a_{k}
s.t.
a_{k}\in\hat{c}_{i}
and
\tilde{a}_{k}\in\hat{c}_{i}
and
\operatorname{\mathit{g}}(a_{k})=\operatorname{\mathit{g}}(\tilde{a}_{k})=\mathtt{{1}}

\therefore

\varphi
is unsatisfiable. ∎

Proof of Lemma

Lemma
V.3
.

Let
(\text{\scalebox{.92}{$V$}},\mathbin{\triangledown},\mathbin{\vartriangle})
be a semiring attribute domain, and
\attrOp\colon\text{BAS}_{T}\to\mathfrak{M}(\text{BAS}_{\text{\scalebox{.92}{$T$}}})
be given by
\attrOp(a)=\{\mskip-5.0mu\{a\}\mskip-5.0mu\}
. Then the multiset
\metrOp(\text{\scalebox{.92}{$T$}})
is a set, and it is a succesful attack of minimal size.

Proof.

It is clear that
\mathbin{\triangledown}
and
\mathbin{\vartriangle}
are commutative and associative. Let
a,b,c\in V=\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}
; for distributivity we need to show that
\min(a+b,a+c)=a+\min(b,c)
. Assume
b\prec c
; then either
\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}<\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}
, or
\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}
and
\mu(b)<\mu(c)
. In the former case one has

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a+b)_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}

\displaystyle<\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a+c)_{i}

and so
a+b\prec a+c
. In the latter case one has
\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a+b)_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a+c)_{i}
. Furthermore,
\mu
is multiplicative, and so

\displaystyle\mu(a+b)\,=\,\mu(a)\mu(b)\,<\,\mu(a)\mu(c)\,=\,\mu(a+c).

Again we conclude
a+b\prec a+c
; hence
\min(a+b,a+c)=a+b=a+\min(b,c)
in both cases, and
(V,\mathbin{\triangledown},\mathbin{\vartriangle})
is a semiring domain. Furthermore, for
A\in\llbracket{T}\rrbracket
one has
\metrAOp(A)=A\in\mathscr{M}(\text{BAS}_{T})
, since
\mathbin{\vartriangle}
is just
\cup
on disjoint sets. Hence
\bigoperOR_{A\in\llbracket{T}\rrbracket}\metrAOp(A)=\min\{A\in\llbracket{T}\rrbracket\}
. By definition of
\preceq
this is an element of
\llbracket{T}\rrbracket
of minimal size.
∎
