Dimensional reduction from magnetic field in moiré superlattices
Nisarga Paul, Philip J.D. Crowley, Liang Fu

Abstract
Moiré materials provide a highly tunable platform in which novel electronic phenomena can emerge. We study strained moiré materials in a uniform magnetic field and predict highly anisotropic electrical conductivity which switches easy-axis as magnetic field or strain is varied. The dramatic anisotropy reflects one-dimensional localization (dimensional reduction) of the electron wavefunctions along a crystal axis due to quantum interference effects. This can be understood in an effective one-dimensional quasiperiodic Aubry-André-like models, or in a complementary semiclassical picture. This phenomenon should be observable in strained moiré materials at realistic fields and low strain disorder, as well as unstrained systems with anisotropic Fermi surfaces.

Dimensional reduction from strain and field.
First, let us review our expectations for magnetotransport in the 2D electron systems. In a clean system, when the Fermi level is between Landau levels, we expect familiar quantum Hall plateaus with vanishing
\sigma_{ii}
(
i=x,y
) and
\sigma_{xy}
quantized to
e^{2}\nu/h
. As the Fermi level sweeps through a Landau level, plateau transitions occur in
\sigma_{xy}
and
\sigma_{ii}
exhibits a peak. Our focus is on these peaks, and in particular oscillations of their heights. Specifically, we predict drastic anisotropy in the longitudinal conductivities in moiré systems due to the (generic) presence of strain.

To proceed, our starting point is the continuum Hamiltonian
(\hbar=1)

H=H_{0}({\boldsymbol{p}}-e{\boldsymbol{A}})+V({\boldsymbol{r}})

(1)

which describes electrons minimally coupled to a uniform magnetic field in the presence of a moiré potential. We consider a moiré potential

V({\boldsymbol{r}})=\sum_{j}V_{j}e^{i\boldsymbol{Q}_{j}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}}

(2)

built out of long-wavelength harmonics with
Q_{i}\sim 1/a_{M}
. Let us specialize for now to a quadratic dispersion
H_{0}({\boldsymbol{p}})=p^{2}/2m
. When
V=0
, the spectrum is described by Landau levels with energy
E_{n}=\omega_{c}(n+1/2)
, where
\omega_{c}=eB/m
. For small
V_{0}/\omega_{c}
, the Landau levels acquire a bandwidth of order
V_{0}
and we can work within a low-energy effective theory. The effective Hamiltonian in the
n
th Landau level is given by
\tilde{V}=P_{n}VP_{n}
where
P_{n}
is the corresponding projector. Adopting a symmetric gauge, we find

\tilde{V}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}})=\sum_{j}\tilde{V}_{j}e^{i\boldsymbol{Q}_{j}\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}}}

(3)

to leading order, where
\tilde{V}_{j}=V_{j}e^{-Q_{j}^{2}\ell^{2}/4}L_{n}(Q_{j}^{2}\ell^{2}/2)
and
L_{n}
is the
n
’th Laguerre polynomial. Here
\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}}=(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})
are the projected position operators, which satisfy

[\tilde{x},\tilde{y}]=i\ell^{2}.

(4)

Consider first the example of a
square
superlattice potential,
V({\boldsymbol{r}})=2V_{0}[\cos Q_{x}x+\cos Q_{y}y]
. The projected potential is

\tilde{V}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}})=2\tilde{V}_{x}\cos Q_{x}\tilde{x}+2\tilde{V}_{y}\cos Q_{y}\tilde{y}.

(5)

Let
\beta=2\pi/Q_{x}Q_{y}\ell^{2}
be the magnetic flux per unit cell in units of
\hbar/e
. Then we may write

\tilde{V}=2\tilde{V}_{y}(\cos Y+\lambda\cos X),\qquad[X,Y]=2\pi i/\beta

(6)

where
\lambda=\tilde{V}_{x}/\tilde{V}_{y}
. Let
X|x\rangle=x|x\rangle
and let
c_{n}^{\dagger}
create the state
|\phi_{n}/Q_{x}\rangle
where
\phi_{n}=\phi_{0}+2\pi n/\beta
. For each
\phi_{0}
, we may regard
\tilde{V}
as a 1D tight-binding model:

\tilde{V}=\sum_{n}2\tilde{V}_{x}\cos(\phi_n)c_{n}^{\dagger}c_{n}+\tilde{V}_{y}(c_{n+1}^{\dagger}c_{n}+\text{h.c.}).

(7)

This is equivalent to the Aubry-André-Harper (AAH) model
Aubry and André 1980
, a canonical model which exhibits a localization-delocalization transition at
|\lambda|=1
. This is consistent with the duality of Eq. (
6
) under
X\leftrightarrow Y
and
\lambda\leftrightarrow 1/\lambda
. In the presence of strict
C_{4}

and the model is at its critical point. However, any superlattice strain results in
Q_{x}\neq Q_{y}
and can have stark effects, as
\lambda

Rauh et al. 1974
.

Previous studies have shown that this transition is sharp if and only if
\beta
is a “Diophantine number”
Jitomirskaya 1999
, which is a dense subset of the irrationals which excludes Liouville numbers (for which the wavefunctions never localize
Avron and Simon 1982
). The existence of localized and delocalized phases at generic
\beta
, possibly separated by a mobility edge near
\lambda=1
, has been verified numerically
Modugno 2009
.

Consider next the more realistic case of a
triangular
superlattice cosine potential,
V({\boldsymbol{r}})=\sum_{j=1}^{3}2V_{j}\cos{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{j}\cdot{\boldsymbol{r}}
, with
{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{j+1}=Q(\cos(2\pi j/3),\sin(2\pi j/3)
. The projected potential can be written

\tilde{V}=\sum_{j=1}^{3}2\tilde{V}_{j}\cos X_{j},\qquad[X_{j},X_{j+1}]=2\pi i/\beta

(8)

where
X_{j}=\boldsymbol{Q}_{j}\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}}
and
\beta=2\pi/|{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{1}\times{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{2}|\ell^{2}
. For
\tilde{V}_{2}=\tilde{V}_{3}
, consider an
X_{1}
eigenbasis
|x_{1}\rangle
and let
c_{n}^{\dagger}
create
|\phi_{n}/Q_{1}\rangle
where
\phi_{n}=\phi_{0}+2\pi n/\beta
. Then for each
\phi_{0}
, we may again regard
\tilde{V}
as a 1D tight-binding model:

\tilde{V}=\sum_{n}2\tilde{V}_{1}\cos(\phi_n )c_{n}^{\dagger}c_{n}+2\tilde{V}_{2}\cos(\phi_{n+1/2}/2)(c_{n+1}^{\dagger}c_{n}+\text{h.c.}).

(9)

In both of the examples Eq. (
6

This implies a 1D localization in the 2D model along a direction which switches with varying magnetic field. For instance, in the square lattice case, for
|\tilde{V}_{y}|>|\tilde{V}_{x}|
we expect wavefunctions localized along the
y

|\tilde{V}_{x}|>|\tilde{V}_{y}|
, we expect wavefunctions localized along

So far, we have discussed the square and triangular lattice cosine potentials in the perturbative regime. In fact, this perturbative result holds for any 2D periodic potential
V(\boldsymbol{r})
. In the absence of strain,
V(\boldsymbol{r})
and
\tilde{V}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}})
possess rotational symmetry. In the presence of strain, the eigenmodes of
\tilde{V}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}})
are generically delocalized in one direction and localized in the perpendicular direction. The localization direction
may be simply read off from the functional form of
\tilde{V}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}})
: it is the direction in which
\tilde{V}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}})
has extended level sets (i.e. open orbits). This direction is uniquely defined for generic
\tilde{V}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}})
, as the extended level sets of a 2D periodic function necessarily all run parallel to the same lattice vector
\boldsymbol{b}_{\mathrm{ext.}}
. As
\tilde{V}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}})
is varied by tuning the strain, the direction of the extended level sets
\boldsymbol{b}_{\mathrm{ext}}
can discretely switch along crystal directions
Wilkinson 1984
;
Wilkinson 1987
;
Han et al. 1994
;
Yeo and Crowley 2022

While the discussion has so far dealt with the perturbative regime, we now demonstrate that this phenomenon is nonperturbative, and moreover holds for quite general energy dispersions. Our starting point is the semiclassical equations of motion for a Bloch wavepacket:

\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}=-e\dot{\boldsymbol{r}}\times\boldsymbol{B},\quad\dot{\boldsymbol{r}}=\mathbf{\nabla}E({\boldsymbol{p}}),

(10)

The relevant degrees of freedom at low temperatures are the electronic states near the Fermi surface. Wavepackets formed of these states can be thought of as propagating in a network made of copies of the
original

(12)

Magnetotransport.
To reveal the observable effects, we turn to a study of magnetotransport in strained moiré superlattices. Conductivity can be taken as
\sigma_{ab}=D_{ab}\tau

Discussion.
We have discussed a series of transitions observable as highly anisotropic and switching longitudinal conductivities, in the same universality class as the localization-delocalization transition of the AAH model, which can be readily observed in magnetotransport experiments on strained moiré materials. This is a manifestation of “dimensional reduction” due to the fundamental quantum noncommutativity of position operators in the presence of a magnetic field.

In fact, this phenomenon can be observed more broadly in 2D materials with anisotropic Fermi surfaces and in a nonperturbative regime
V_{0}\gtrsim\omega_{c}
, as we have demonstrated using a semiclassical approach. However, the magnetic length should be on the order of the lattice constant, making this difficult to realize outside moiré materials.

Our predictions are remarkably robust to strain disorder, which is omnipresent in moiré systems. At large fields
(B\gtrsim\sqrt{\rho}Q)
, no reasonable strain disorder disrupts homogeneous 1D localization. At small fields
(B\ll\sqrt{\rho}Q)
a strain disorder up to
\delta\alpha\sim\sqrt{B/\sqrt{\rho}Q}
is tolerable. For instance at
B=5
T,
Q=2\pi/10
nm
-1
and one electron per unit cell, up to
30\%
is permissible (far larger than the strain disorder in many clean moiré graphene samples). This is consistent with the fact that the switching frequency
\omega_{0}
depends quite weakly on strain.

We remark that evidence for unusual magnetotransport in moiré systems has been previously found in related contexts. Ref.
Finney et al. 2022
observed unusual magnetotransport in twisted bilayer graphene which was captured well by an anisotropic Hofstadter model. Such a model was also studied in Ref.
Barelli et al. 1999
, which noted enhanced
\sigma_{xx}/\sigma_{yy}
, but no “switching”.

One intriguing question for future work is whether this effect can be achieved in a general Chern band or in the absence of magnetic field. Indeed, the fundamental noncommutativity of the projected position operators depends only on the quantum geometry of the band. Other interesting directions for future studies include possible technological applications of this phenomenon, such as for “moiré transistors” or magnetic sensors.

III
Landau level projection

Here we review the method of Landau level projection used in the main text. We stress that this method is equivalent to evaluating the transport quantities to leading order in
V_{0}/\omega_{c}
. We consider the Hamiltonian of a 2D system lying in the
x,y
plane

H=H_{0}(\boldsymbol{\pi})+V(\boldsymbol{r}),\quad H_{0}=\frac{p^{2}}{2m},\quad V=\sum_{\boldsymbol{Q}}V_{\boldsymbol{Q}}\mathrm{e}^{i\boldsymbol{Q}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}}

(S12)

where
\boldsymbol{\pi}=\boldsymbol{p}-e\boldsymbol{A}
, the background field is
\boldsymbol{A}=(B/2)(y,-x,0)
, and
V(\boldsymbol{r})
is a weak periodic potential with reciprocal lattice vectors
\boldsymbol{Q}
and Fourier coefficients satisfying
V_{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{*}=V_{-\boldsymbol{Q}}
. We introduce the dual momenta
\boldsymbol{\tilde{\pi}}=\boldsymbol{p}+e\boldsymbol{A}
, and choose the symmetric gauge so that
\boldsymbol{\pi}
and
\boldsymbol{\tilde{\pi}}
obey
[\pi_{x},\pi_{y}]=-i/\ell^{2},[\tilde{\pi}_{x},\tilde{\pi}_{y}]=i/\ell^{2}
and
[\pi_{i},\tilde{\pi}_{j}]=0
, where we’ve defined
\ell^{2}=1/eB
. We define the annihilation operators

a=\frac{\ell}{\sqrt{2}}(\pi_{x}-i\pi_{y}),\quad b=\frac{\ell}{\sqrt{2}}(\tilde{\pi}_{x}+i\tilde{\pi}_{y})

(S13)

which satisfy
[a,a^{\dagger}]=1,[b,b^{\dagger}]=1,
and
[a,b]=0
. We define a basis satisfying
a^{\dagger}a|n,m\rangle=n|n,m\rangle,b^{\dagger}b|n,m\rangle=m|n,m\rangle
. Then
H_{0}=\omega_{c}(a^{\dagger}a+\frac{1}{2})
(where
\omega_{c}=eB/m
) and
V({\boldsymbol{r}})
can be expressed in terms of the raising and lowering operators via

\boldsymbol{r}=i\sigma_{y}\ell^{2}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\pi}}-\boldsymbol{\pi}).

(S14)

For small
V/\omega_{c}
, the leading term in the effective Hamiltonian for the
n
’th Landau level is
\tilde{V}\equiv P_{n}VP_{n}
(up to constant shift), where
P_{n}=\sum_{m}\outerproduct{n,m}{n,m}
. This takes the form

\tilde{V}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{Q}}\tilde{V}_{\boldsymbol{Q}}e^{i\boldsymbol{Q}\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}}}

(S15)

where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}}=(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})=i\sigma_{y}\ell^{2}\boldsymbol{\tilde{\pi}}
, which satisfy
[\tilde{x},\tilde{y}]=i\ell^{2}
, and
\tilde{V}_{\boldsymbol{Q}}=V_{\boldsymbol{Q}}D_{nn}((Q_{x}+iQ_{y})\ell/\sqrt{2})
with
D_{nn}(\eta):=\langle n|\exp\left[i(\eta a+\eta^{*}a^{\dagger})\right]|n\rangle=\mathrm{e}^{-|\eta|^{2}/2}L_{n}(|\eta|^{2})
.
L_{n}(x)
is the
n
’th Laguerre polynomial.

To obtain the projected current density operator, we first review the general form of projected observables. For any Hamiltonian
H=T+V
with
T=\sum_{n}E_{n}P_{n}
and some operator
M
, we seek the projected operator
\tilde{M}
which satisfies
\langle\psi_{1}|M|\psi_{2}\rangle=\langle\tilde{\psi}_{1}|\tilde{M}|\tilde{\psi}_{2}\rangle
for all energy eigenstates
|\psi_{i}\rangle
, and with
|\tilde{\psi}_{i}\rangle=P_{n}|\psi_{i}\rangle
. Expanding perturbatively in
V
, this is given by

\tilde{M}=P_{n}MP_{n}+\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{P_{n}MP_{m}VP_{n}-P_{n}VP_{m}MP_{n}}{E_{n}-E_{m}}+O(V^{2}).

(S16)

In the case at hand, the current operator is

\boldsymbol{J}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial\boldsymbol{A}}=-\frac{e\boldsymbol{\pi}}{m}.

(S17)

We can write this in the form
\boldsymbol{J}=ie\omega_{c}\left(a^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{d}^{*}-a\boldsymbol{d}\right)
, which makes clear that
\boldsymbol{J}
only couples neighboring Landau levels. We obtain

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tilde{J}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\omega_{c}}P_{n}\boldsymbol{J}(P_{n-1}-P_{n+1})VP_{n}+\text{h.c.}

(S18a)

\displaystyle=-ie(P_{n}(a^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{d}^{*}-a\boldsymbol{d})P_{n-1}VP_{n}-P_{n}(a^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{d}^{*}-a\boldsymbol{d})P_{n+1}VP_{n})+\text{h.c.}

(S18b)

\displaystyle=-ie(P_{n}a^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{d}^{*}VP_{n}+P_{n}a\boldsymbol{d}VP_{n})+\text{h.c.}

(S18c)

\displaystyle=-ieP_{n}[\boldsymbol{d}a+\boldsymbol{d}^{*}a^{\dagger},V]P_{n}

(S18d)

\displaystyle=ieP_{n}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}},V]P_{n}

(S18e)

\displaystyle=ie[\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}},\tilde{V}]

(S18f)

IV
Transport in perturbative regime

Let us define and derive relevant quantities for transport. The most general form for conductivity can be written as
Resta 2018
;
Scalapino et al. 1993

\displaystyle\sigma_{ab}(\omega)

\displaystyle=D_{ab}\left[\delta(\omega)+\frac{i}{\pi\omega}\right]+\sigma^{\text{reg}}_{ab}(\omega)

(S19a)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{ab}^{\text{Drude}}(\omega)+\sigma^{\text{reg}}_{ab}(\omega)

(S19b)

where
D_{ab}
is the Drude weight and
\sigma^{\text{reg}}_{ab}(\omega)
is the regular (or absorptive) part of the conductivity, sensitive to
\omega\neq 0
transitions. Within a single relaxation time approximation with scattering time
\tau
, the DC conductivity is
\sigma_{ab}=D_{ab}\tau
.

There are several methods to derive
D_{ab}

Resta 2018
;
Scalapino et al. 1993
, and we review one method here. In the absence of scattering the current response at short times to a DC electric field
E
turned on a time
t=0
is given by

j_{a}(t)=D_{ab}E_{b}t+O(E^{2})

(S20)

where
j_{a}
is the current density. Within the single relaxation time approximation the current response in an imperfect metal is given by truncating this at the scattering time
\tau

j_{a}(t)=D_{ab}E_{b}\tau+O(E^{2})

(S21)

yielding a conductivity

\sigma_{ab}=D_{ab}\tau.

(S22)

This yields a straightforward way of calculating the Drude tensor at the level of linear response:

D_{ab}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\left.\frac{1}{t}\frac{\partial\langle j_{a}\rangle}{\partial E_{b}}\large\right|_{E=0}.

(S23)

Here
j_{a}
is the current operator
j_{a}(t)=e^{iHt}j_{a}e^{-iHt}
and
H
is the Hamiltonian including an electric field, which we here include in Coulomb gauge

H=H_{0}-q\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}

(S24)

with
\boldsymbol{x}
the position operator. Differentiation yields

\left.\partialderivative{\cexp{j_a(t)}}{E_b}\large\right|_{E=0}=iq\int_{0}^{t}ds\,\langle[j_{a}(t),x_{b}(s)]\rangle_{0}

(S25)

Here we’ve defined
\langle O\rangle_{0}=\mathrm{tr}\left(f_{\beta}(H_{0}-\mu)O\right)
where
f_{\beta}(x)=1/(1+e^{\beta x})
is the Fermi-Dirac distribution. In Coulomb gauge the current density operator has only a paramagnetic term, and we may consequently use the relation

j_{a}=q\dot{x}_{a}/A
where
A
is the system area. Hence by integration
qx_{a}(t)-qx_{a}(s)=A\int_{s}^{t}ds^{\prime}\,j_{a}(s^{\prime})
and consequently

\displaystyle q\int_{0}^{t}ds\,x_{a}(s)

\displaystyle=tqx_{a}(t)-A\int_{0}^{t}ds\,\int_{s}^{t}ds^{\prime}\,j_{a}(s^{\prime})

(S26)

\displaystyle=tqx_{q}(t)-A\int_{0}^{t}ds\,sj_{a}(s).

Substituting back, we have

\displaystyle\left.\partialderivative{\cexp{j_a}}{E_b}\large\right|_{E=0}

\displaystyle=iqt\langle[j_{a}(t),x_{b}(t)]\rangle_{0}-iA\int_{0}^{t}ds\,s\langle[j_{a}(t),j_{b}(s)]\rangle_{0}

(S27)

\displaystyle=iqt\langle[j_{a},x_{b}]\rangle_{0}-iA\int_{0}^{t}ds\,s\langle[j_{a}(t-s),j_{b}(0)]\rangle_{0}.

Using
\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}ds\,sf(t-s)=\lim_{\eta\to 0^{+}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{-\eta t}f(t)
, we arrive at

D_{ab}=iq\langle[j_{a},x_{b}]\rangle_{0}-iA\lim_{\eta\to 0^{+}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{-\eta t}\langle[j_{a}(t),j_{b}(0)]\rangle_{0}.

(S28)

The second term can be rewritten in frequency space (leaving the
\eta\to 0^{+}
implicit):

\displaystyle-iA\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{-\eta t}\langle[j_{a}(t),j_{b}(0)]\rangle_{0}

\displaystyle=-iA\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{-\eta t}\sum_{n}\frac{1}{1+e^{\beta(E_{n}-\mu)}}\langle n|e^{iE_{n}t}j_{a}e^{-iH_{0}t}j_{b}-j_{b}e^{iH_{0}t}j_{a}e^{-iE_{n}t}|n\rangle

(S29)

\displaystyle=-iA\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{-\eta t}(\sum_{n,m}\frac{e^{i(E_{n}-E_{m})t}[j_{a}]_{nm}[j_{b}]_{mn}}{1+e^{\beta(E_{n}-\mu)}}-\sum_{n,m}\frac{e^{i(E_{m}-E_{n})t}[j_{b}]_{nm}[j_{a}]_{mn}}{1+e^{\beta(E_{n}-\mu)}})

\displaystyle=A\sum_{n,m}\left(\frac{1}{1+e^{\beta(E_{n}-\mu)}}-\frac{1}{1+e^{\beta(E_{m}-\mu)}}\right)\frac{[j_{a}]_{nm}[j_{b}]_{mn}}{E_{n}-E_{m}+i\eta}

yielding (for an electron with charge
q=-e
)

D_{ab}=-ie\langle[j_{a},x_{b}]\rangle_{0}+A\sum_{n\neq m}f_{nm}\frac{[j_{a}]_{nm}[j_{b}]_{mn}}{E_{n}-E_{m}}

(S30)

where
f_{nm}=f_{n}-f_{m}
and
f_{n}=(e^{\beta(E_{n}-\mu)}+1)^{-1}
. Note this obtains the correct Drude weight for a Fermi gas, in which case
\boldsymbol{j}=-e\boldsymbol{p}/mA
and

D_{ab}=\frac{ie^{2}}{mA}\langle[p_{a},x_{b}]\rangle_{0}=\frac{ne^{2}}{m}\delta_{ab}

(Fermi gas)

where we’ve used
\langle[p_{a},x_{b}]\rangle_{0}=-iN\delta_{ab}
and
[j_{a},j_{b}]=[j_{a},H_{0}]=0
. We also obtain the correct (vanishing) Drude weight for a 2D electron gas in a magnetic field. The first term yields an identical contribution
(ne^{2}/m)\delta_{ab}
, while the second term can be simplified using

[j_{a}]_{cq;dq^{\prime}}=e\delta_{qq^{\prime}}(\sqrt{c+1}\delta_{c+1,d}+s\sqrt{c}\delta_{c-1,d})/(\sqrt{2s}\ell mA)

(S31)

where
s=\pm 1
for
a=x,y
and
q
is the intra-Landau level quantum number (e.g. angular momentum if symmetric gauge is chosen). We then obtain

\displaystyle D_{aa}

\displaystyle=\frac{ne^{2}}{m}+\sum_{c\neq d,q,q^{\prime}}f_{cd}\frac{[j_{a}]_{cq;dq^{\prime}}[j_{b}]_{dq^{\prime};cq}}{E_{c}-E_{d}}

(Uniform B field)

\displaystyle=\frac{ne^{2}}{m}+\frac{e^{2}}{2s\ell^{2}m^{2}A}\sum_{c\neq d,q,q^{\prime}}f_{cd}\delta_{qq^{\prime}}\frac{sd\delta_{c+1,d}+sc\delta_{c-1,d}}{E_{c}-E_{d}}

\displaystyle=\frac{ne^{2}}{m}+\frac{e^{2}}{2\ell^{2}m^{2}A}\left(\sum_{d,q}f_{d-1,d}\frac{d}{-\omega_{c}}+\sum_{c,q}f_{c,c-1}\frac{c}{\omega_{c}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{ne^{2}}{m}-\frac{ne^{2}}{m}=0.

This is the familiar fact that longitudinal conductivity vanishes for Landau levels at any chemical potential or temperature. Indeed, this is a consequence of the
f
-sum rule and Kohn’s theorem
Kohn 1961
, which dictates that the total optical absorption in Landau levels is saturated by the absorption peak at
\omega=\omega_{c}
.

Applying Eq. (
S30
) to the model studied in the main text we make the replacements
H_{0}\to\tilde{V}
,
j_{a}\to ie[\tilde{x}_{a},\tilde{V}]/A
,
x_{a}\to\tilde{x}_{a}
,
q\to-e
where
\tilde{V},\tilde{x}_{a}
are the projected potentials and position operators, respectively. This projected method is ideally suited to our calculations because the Drude weight is a DC response which is not sensitive to matrix elements in states outside the low-energy subspace.

Numerics.
Our numerical calculations are done for each
Q_{x}Q_{y}\ell^{2}/2\pi\equiv\beta^{-1}=p/q
on a finite system of
\sim N\times q
unit cells (thus enclosing
N\times p
flux quanta and maintaining moiré translation symmetry) for

We framed the discussion in the main text around uniaxial heterostrain. In this section, for completeness, we show that shear heterostrain leads to unchanged main results. Let’s consider a shear strain
\alpha
on a square moiré superlattice acting as

\begin{pmatrix}{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{x}^{\prime}\\
{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{y}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\alpha\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{x}\\
{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{y}\end{pmatrix}.

(S32)

Here
{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{x}=Q\hat{x}
,
{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{y}=Q\hat{y}
. Let’s define
Q^{\prime}=\sqrt{1+\alpha^{2}}Q
. The moiré potential is
V({\boldsymbol{r}})=2V_{0}[\cos{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{x}^{\prime}\cdot{\boldsymbol{r}}+\cos{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{y}^{\prime}\cdot{\boldsymbol{r}}].

The potential projected into the
n
’th Landau level is

\tilde{V}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}})=2\tilde{V}_{x}\cos{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{x}^{\prime}\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}}+2\tilde{V}_{y}\cos{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{y}^{\prime}\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}}

(S33)

where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}}=(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})
,
[\tilde{x},\tilde{y}]=i\ell^{2}
,
\tilde{V}_{x}=V_{0}e^{-Q^{\prime}\ell^{2}/4}L_{n}(Q^{\prime 2}\ell^{2}/2)
, and
\tilde{V}_{y}=V_{0}e^{-Q^{2}\ell^{2}/4}L_{n}(Q^{2}\ell^{2}/2)
. It can be written

\displaystyle\tilde{V}

\displaystyle=2\tilde{V}_{x}(\cos X+\lambda\cos Y)

(S34a)

\displaystyle[X,Y]

\displaystyle=iQ^{2}\ell^{2},\quad\lambda=\tilde{V}_{y}/\tilde{V}_{x}

(S34b)

This again is an AAH model. Eigenmodes are generically localized in the diagonal basis of
X
or
Y
depending on whether
|\lambda|<1
or
|\lambda|>1
.
