The Catalan numbers occur in numerous counting problems as well as their generalizations, the Fuss-Catalan numbers with one parameter,
p

A_{n}^{p}:=\frac{1}{pn+1}\binom{pn+1}{n}.

The Catalan numbers correspond to the case

A

2.1

In the context of planar partitions, the numbers

turn out in different situations, some of which we highlight here. The set of integers

R1.
The Catalan number counts the number of planar partitions of
[n]
.

R2.
[
6
]

R3.
[
4
]
The Fuss-Catalan number

counts the number of planar partitions of

We recall that a double planar partition of
[n]

In

]

In

2.
Counting planar
p

2.1.
Planar partitions and arc diagrams

A partition of
[n]
is represented here by a diagram, consisting of
n
points on a line, labeled 1 to
n
, and arcs connecting pairs of points. If two points are connected by an arc, then they belong to the same block of the partition. The diagram representing a partition is evidently non unique. A diagram is said
standard
if for every pair of points
(i<j)
belonging to the same block, there is an arc
(i,j)
if and only if in the same block there is no some
k
such that
i<k<j
. The standard diagram is unique. In what follows we deal only with standard diagrams, unless it be specified, see Figure
2
b.

Notation 1
.

A partition is said planar if its diagram is planar, i.e., its arcs do no cross each other. The set of planar partitions of
[n]
is denoted by
\mathcal{P}_{n}
, and the set of planar
p
-partitions of
[pn]
is denoted by
\mathcal{P}^{p}_{n}
.

Figure 2.
a) Diagram of a non planar partition; b) the standard diagram of a planar 4-partition; c) a partition of
[11]
with 3 boxes

Here we prove Theorem
1
, generalizing R1.

Remark 1
.

For
p=1
a planar 1-partition of
[n]
consists of
n
blocks, each one with 1 element. Therefore it is unique and indeed
A_{n}^{1}=1
for every
n
.

2.2.
Proof of Theorem
1

In
[
2
]
I have introduced, for every integer
p>0
, a triangle
T^{p}(n,k)
, whose rows add to
A^{p}_{n}
.

Here we need to consider, for every
p
, the triangle
F^{p}(n,k)
, obtained by
T^{p}(n,k)
by:

(1)

F^{p}(n,k):=T^{p}(n,n-k),\quad k=0,\dots,n.

The integers
F^{p}(n,k)
for
n\geq 0
,
0\leq k\leq n
, result to be given by the initial conditions

(2)

F^{p}(0,0)=1;\quad F^{p}(n,0)=0\quad\text{ for}\quad n>0

and the recurrence, for
n>0
and
0<k\leq n
:

(3)

F^{p}(n,k)=\sum_{j=k-1}^{n-1}\binom{j-k+p-1}{p-2}F^{p}(n-1,j).

We recall also the notion of
box
in a diagram of a planar partition. Observe that two blocks
A,B
, satisfying
min(A)<min(B)
may satisfy either
max(A)<min(B)
or
max(A)>min(B)
. In the last case we have also, by planarity, that
max(A)>max(B)
, and we say that
B
is nested in
A
. A box is a block which is not nested.
A planar partition of
[n]
with
m

1\leq k\leq m
boxes, see Figure
2
.

We denote by
N^{p}(n,k)
the number of
p
-partitions of
[pn]
having
k
boxes. We see that
N^{p}(n,k)
fulfills (
2
): indeed, the void partition has no boxes, so
N^{p}(0,0)=1
; also, there are no
p
-partitions of
[pn]
with no boxes, so
N^{p}(n,0)=0
.

We see now that
N^{p}(n,k)
satisfies recurrence (
3
).
We consider the set of partitions of
\mathcal{P}^{p}_{n}
having
k
boxes. We observe that each one of them can be obtained from a unique partition
Q\in\mathcal{P}^{p}_{n-1}
having
j
boxes if
j\geq k-1
and
j\leq n-1
, by adding a new block
B
to
Q
that has the last element at right of
Q
.
On the other hand, given such a partition
Q
with
j
boxes, we may get different partitions with
k
boxes. Indeed, the first
k-1
boxes of
Q
remain unaltered in the partition of
\mathcal{P}^{p}_{n}
, say
P
, while
j-k+1
boxes must be nested in the new block
B
of
P
. So, the first point of
B
is necessarily between the first
k-1
boxes and the nested
j-k+1
boxes of
Q
. Now, between the first and the last point of the new block, there are
j-k+1
boxes and
p-2
points of
B
, see Figure
3
. The partition
P
is uniquely defined by the ordered set containing the
p-2
points and the
j-k+1
boxes: for a fixed
Q
, the number of possibilities is given by

\binom{j-k+1+p-2}{p-2}
. This gives the recurrence (
3
). Therefore
N^{p}(n,k)=F^{p}(n,k)
. Since
|\mathcal{P}^{p}_{n}|
is the sum of the numbers
N^{p}(n,k)
for

, this sum is

by (
1
) and (
2
).

3.
Counting double planar partitions

3.1.
Double planar partitions and tied arc diagrams

In this section we consider special double planar partitions
(P_{1},P_{2})
of
[pn]
in which
P_{1}\in\mathcal{P}^{p}_{n}
and
P_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{pn}
. The set of such double partitions is denoted by
\mathcal{P}^{II,p}_{n}

A double partition is represented by a sole diagram of arcs and ties in the following way, see also
[
3
]
. The partition
P_{1}
is represented by
n
blocks. Remember that each block of
P_{1}
consists of
p-1
consecutive arcs. A block is labeled by its point at left, so that the blocks of
P_{1}
are ordered by this labeling. A tie is an arc, drawn as a dotted line, with endpoints on two blocks of
P_{1}
. Suppose that
B_{k_{1}},B_{k_{2}},\dots,B_{k_{m}}
are the ordered blocks of
P_{1}
contained in a same block of
P_{2}
. Then we put a tie from
B_{k_{i}}
to
B_{k_{i+1}}
, for

. The planarity of
P_{2}
guarantees that the ties can be drawn without crossing neither the arcs nor each other. Moreover, a double partition is uniquely represented by arcs and ties in this way, see Figure
4
for an example.

Now we will prove that, for
p>0
, the cardinality of the set
\mathcal{P}^{II,p}_{n}
is the Fuss Catalan number

.

Proposition 2

Proof.

Consider the map
\tau

-th child of the root, and we give to it
