Blow-up of strong solutions of the Thermal Quasi-Geostrophic equation
Dan Crisan, Prince Romeo Mensah

Abstract
The Thermal Quasi-Geostrophic (TQG) equation is a coupled system of equations that governs the evolution of the buoyancy and the potential vorticity of a fluid. It has a local in time solution as proved in crisan2021theoretical . In this paper, we give a criterion for the blow-up of solutions to the Thermal Quasi-Geostrophic equation, in the spirit of the classical Beale–Kato–Majda blow-up criterion (cf. beale1984remarks ) for the solution of the Euler equation.

Introduction

The Thermal Quasi-Geostrophic (TQG) equation is a coupled system of equations governed by the evolution of the buoyancy
b:(t,\mathbf{x})\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\mapsto b(t,\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}
and the potential vorticity
q:(t,\mathbf{x})\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\mapsto q(t,\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}
in the following way:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}b+(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)b=0,

(1.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}q+(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)(q-b)=-(\mathbf{u}_{h}\cdot\nabla)b,

(1.2)

\displaystyle b(0,x)=b_{0}(x),\qquad q(0,x)=q_{0}(x),

(1.3)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}=\nabla^{\perp}\psi,\qquad\mathbf{u}_{h}=\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{\perp}h,\qquad q=(\Delta-1)\psi+f.

(1.4)

Here,
\psi:(t,\mathbf{x})\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\mapsto\psi(t,\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}
is the streamfunction,
h:\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mapsto h(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}
is the spatial variation around a constant bathymetry profile and
f:\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mapsto f(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}
is the Coriolis parameter. Since we are working on the whole space, we can supplement our system with the far-field condition

\displaystyle\lim_{|\mathbf{x}|\rightarrow\infty}(b(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}))=0.

Our given set of data is
(\mathbf{u}_{h},f,b_{0},q_{0})
with regularity class:

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{h}\in W^{3,2}_{\mathrm{div}}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2}),\quad f\in W^{2,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),\quad b_{0}\in W^{3,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),\quad q_{0}\in W^{2,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}).

(1.5)

The TQG equation models the dynamics of a submesoscale geophysical fluid in thermal geostrophic balance, for which the Rossby number, the Froude number and the stratification parameter are all of the same asymptotic order.
For a historical overview, modelling and other issues pertaining to the TQG equation, we refer the reader to
crisan2021theoretical
.

In the following, we are interested in
strong solutions
of the system (
1.1
)–(
1.4
)

which can naturally be defined in terms of just
b
and
q
although the unknowns in the evolutionary equations (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) are
b
,
q
and
\mathbf{u}
. This is because for a given
f
, one can recover the velocity
\mathbf{u}
from the vorticity
q
by solving the equation

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}=\nabla^{\perp}(\Delta-1)^{-1}(q-f)

derived from (
1.4
). Also note that a consequence of the equation
\mathbf{u}=\nabla^{\perp}\psi
in (
1.4
) is that
\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u}=0
. This means that the fluid is incompressible.

With these information in hand, we now make precise, the notion of a strong solution.

Definition 1
(Local strong solution)

Let
(\mathbf{u}_{h},f,b_{0},q_{0})
be of regularity class (
1.5
). For some
T>0
, we call the triple
(b,q,T)

a strong solution to the system (
1.1
)–(
1.4
) if the following holds:

•

The buoyancy
b
satisfies
b\in C([0,T];W^{3,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))

and the equation

\displaystyle b(t)

\displaystyle=b_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{div}(b\mathbf{u})\,\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

holds for all
t\in[0,T]
;

•

the potential vorticity
q
satisfies
q\in C([0,T];W^{2,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))

and the equation

\displaystyle q(t)

\displaystyle=q_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}\Big[\mathrm{div}((q-b)\mathbf{u})+\mathrm{div}(b\mathbf{u}_{h})\Big]\,\,\mathrm{d}\tau

holds for all
t\in[0,T]
.

Such local strong solutions exist on a maximal time interval. We define this as follows.

Definition 2
(Maximal solution)

Let
(\mathbf{u}_{h},f,b_{0},q_{0})
be of regularity class (
1.5
). For some
T>0
, we call
(b,q,T_{\max})

a maximal solution to the system (
1.1
)–(
1.4
) if:

•

there exists an increasing sequence of time steps
(T_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
whose limit is
T_{\max}\in(0,\infty]
;

•

for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the triple
(b,q,T_{n})
is
a local strong solution to the system (
1.1
)–(
1.4
) with initial condition
(b_{0},q_{0})
;

•

if
T_{\max}<\infty
, then

\displaystyle\limsup_{T_{n}\rightarrow T_{\max}}\|b(T_{n})\|_{W^{3,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}+\|q(T_{n})\|_{W^{2,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}=\infty.

(1.6)

We shall call
T_{\max}>0
the maximal time.

The existence of a unique local strong solution of (
1.1
)–(
1.4
) has recently been shown in
(
crisan2021theoretical
, Theorem 2.10)
on the torus. A unique maximal solutions also exist
(
crisan2021theoretical
, Theorem 2.14)
and the result also applies to the whole space
(
crisan2021theoretical
, Remark 2.1)
. We state the result here for completeness.

Theorem 1.1

For
(\mathbf{u}_{h},f,b_{0},q_{0})
of regularity class (
1.5
), there exist a unique maximal solution
(b,q,T)
of the system (
1.1
)–(
1.4
).

Before we state our main result, let us first present some notations used throughout this work.

1.1
Notations

In the following, we write
F\lesssim G
if there exists a generic constant
c>0
(that may vary from line to line) such that
F\leq c\,G
.
Functions mapping into
\mathbb{R}^{2}
are
boldfaced
(for example the velocity
\mathbf{u}
) while those mapping into
\mathbb{R}
are not (for example the buoyancy
b
and vorticity
q
).
For
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
and
p\in[1,\infty]
,
W^{k,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is the usual Sobolev space of functions mapping into
\mathbb{R}
with a natural modification for functions mapping into
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. For
p=2
,
W^{k,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is a Hilbert space with inner product

\langle u,v\rangle_{W^{k,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=\sum_{|\beta|\leq k}\langle\partial^{\beta}u\,,\,\partial^{\beta}v\rangle,

where
\langle\cdot\,,\,\rangle
denotes the standard
L^{2}
-inner product.
For general
s\in\mathbb{R}
, we use the norm

\displaystyle\|v\|_{W^{s,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\equiv\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\big(1+|\xi|^{2}\big)^{s}|\widehat{v}(\xi)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}

(1.7)

defined in frequency space. Here,
\widehat{v}(\xi)
denotes the Fourier coefficients of
v
.
For simplicity, we write
\|\cdot\|_{s,2}
for
\|\cdot\|_{W^{s,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
.
When
k=s=0
, we get the usual
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
space whose norm we will simply denote by
\|\cdot\|_{2}
.
A similar notation will be used for norms
\|\cdot\|_{p}
of general
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
spaces for any
p\in[1,\infty]
as well as for the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{k,2}:=\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{W^{k,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
when
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Additionally,
W^{k,p}_{\mathrm{div}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
represents the space of divergence-free vector-valued functions in
W^{k,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

With respect to differential operators, we let

\nabla_{0}:=(\partial_{x_{1}},\partial_{x_{2}},0)^{T}
and
\nabla_{0}^{\perp}:=(-\partial_{x_{2}},\partial_{x_{1}},0)
be the three-dimensional extensions of the two-dimensional differential operators
\nabla=(\partial_{x_{1}},\partial_{x_{2}})^{T}
and
\nabla^{\perp}:=(-\partial_{x_{2}},\partial_{x_{1}})
by zero respectively. The Laplacian
\Delta=\mathrm{div}\nabla=\partial_{x_{1}x_{1}}+\partial_{x_{2}x_{2}}
remains two-dimensional.

1.2
Main result

Our main result is to give a blow-up criterion, of Beale–Kato–Majda-type
beale1984remarks
, for a strong solution
(b,q,T)
of (
1.1
)–(
1.4
).
In particular, we show the following result.

Theorem 1.2

Suppose that
(b,q,T)
is a local strong solution of (
1.1
)–(
1.4
). If

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\Big(\|q(t)\|_{\infty}+\|\nabla b(t)\|_{\infty}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t\equiv K<\infty,

(1.8)

then there exists a solution
(b^{\prime},q^{\prime},T^{\prime})
with
T^{\prime}>T
, such that
(b^{\prime},q^{\prime})=(b,q)
on
[0,T]
.
Moreover, for all
t\in[0,T]
,

\|b(t)\|_{3,2}+\|q(t)\|_{2,2}\leq\big[\mathrm{e}+\|b_{0}\|_{3,2}+\|q_{0}\|_{2,2}\big]^{\exp(cK)}\exp[cT\exp(cK)].

An immediate consequence of the above theorem is the following:

Corollary 1

Assume that
(b,q,T)
is a maximal solution.
If
T<\infty
, then

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\Big(\|q(t)\|_{\infty}+\|\nabla b(t)\|_{\infty}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t=\infty

and in particular,

\displaystyle\sup_{t\uparrow T}\Big(\|q(t)\|_{\infty}+\|\nabla b(t)\|_{\infty}\Big)=\infty.

2
Blow-up

We devote the entirety of this section to the proof of Theorem
1.2
. In order to achieve our goal, we first derive a suitable exact solution for what is referred to as the modified Helmholtz equation. Some authors also call it the Screened Poisson equation
bhat2008fourier
while others rather mistakenly call it the Helmholtz equation. Refer to
arfken2005mathematical
for the difference between the Helmholtz equation and modified Helmholtz equation.

2.1
Estimate for the
2D
modified Helmholtz equation or the screened Poisson equation

In the following, we want to find an exact solution
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
of

\displaystyle(\Delta-1)\psi(\mathbf{x})=w(\mathbf{x}),\qquad\lim_{|\mathbf{x}|\rightarrow\infty}\psi(\mathbf{x})=0

(2.1)

for a given function
w\in W^{2,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

. The corresponding two-dimensional free space Green’s function
G^{\text{free}}(\mathbf{x})
for (
2.1
) must therefore solve

\displaystyle(\Delta-1)G^{\text{free}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})=\delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}),\qquad\lim_{|\mathbf{x}|\rightarrow\infty}G^{\text{free}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})(\mathbf{x})=0

(2.2)

in the sense of distributions.
Indeed, one can verify that the Green’s function is given by

\displaystyle G^{\text{free}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})=\frac{1}{2\pi}K_{0}(|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|)

(2.3)

see
(
arfken2005mathematical
, Table 9.5)
, where

\displaystyle K_{0}(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\sqrt{z^{2}+r^{2}}}}{\sqrt{z^{2}+r^{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}r

is the modified Bessel function of the second kind, see
equation (8.432-9), page 917 of

gradshteyn2007table
with
\nu=0
and
x=1
.
However, since the integral above is an even function, it follows that

\displaystyle G^{\text{free}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})=\frac{i}{4}H^{(1)}_{0}(i|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{e^{-\sqrt{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{2}+r^{2}}}}{\sqrt{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{2}+r^{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}r

(2.4)

which is the zeroth-order Hankel function of the first kind, see
equation (11.117) in
arfken2005mathematical
and equation (8.421-9) of
gradshteyn2007table
on page 915. Therefore,

\displaystyle\psi(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{e^{-|(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r)|}}{|(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r)|}w((\mathbf{y},0))\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\mathrm{d}r

(2.5)

\displaystyle=:\psi((\mathbf{x},0))

(2.6)

where we have used the identity
\sqrt{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{2}+r^{2}}=|(\mathbf{x},0)-(\mathbf{y},r)|=|(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r)|
.
We can therefore view the argument of the streamfunction
\psi
as a
3D
-vector with zero vertical component.

2.2
Log-Sobolev estimate for velocity gradient

Our goal now is to find a suitable estimate for the Lipschitz norm of
\mathbf{u}
that solves

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}=\nabla^{\perp}\psi,\qquad(\Delta-1)\psi=w

(2.7)

where
w\in W^{2,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is given. In particular, inspired by
beale1984remarks
, we aim to show Proposition
1
below. This log-estimate is the crucial ingredient that allow us to obtain our blow-up criterion in terms of just the buoyancy gradient and the vorticity although preliminary estimate may have suggested estimating the velocity gradient as well.

Proposition 1

For a given
w\in W^{2,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, any
\mathbf{u}
solving (
2.7
) satisfies

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{u}\|_{1,\infty}\lesssim 1+(1+2\ln^{+}(\|w\|_{2,2}))\|w\|_{\infty}

(2.8)

where
\ln^{+}a=\ln\,a
if
a\geq 1
and
\ln^{+}a=0
otherwise.

Proof

To show (
2.8
), we fix
L\in(0,1]
and for
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we let
\zeta_{L}(\mathbf{z})
be a smooth cut-off function satisfying

\displaystyle\zeta_{L}(\mathbf{z})=\begin{cases}1&:|\mathbf{z}|<L,\\
0&:|\mathbf{z}|>2L\end{cases}

and
|\partial\zeta_{L}(\mathbf{z})|\lesssim L^{-1}
where
\partial:=\nabla^{\perp}_{0}
or
\nabla_{0}
as well as
|\nabla_{0}\nabla^{\perp}_{0}\zeta_{L}(\mathbf{z})|\lesssim L^{-2}
. This latter requirement ensures that the point of inflection of the graph of the cut-off, the portion that is constant, concave upwards and concave downwards are all captured.
We now define the following

\displaystyle B_{1}:=\big\{(\mathbf{y},r)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,:\,|(\mathbf{x},0)-(\mathbf{y},r)|=|(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r)|<2L\big\},

\displaystyle B_{2}:=\big\{(\mathbf{y},r)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,:\,L\leq|(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r)|\leq 1\big\},

\displaystyle B_{3}:=\big\{(\mathbf{y},r)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,:\,|(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r)|>1\big\},

so that by adding and subtracting
\zeta_{L}
, we obtain

\displaystyle|\nabla\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})|=|\nabla_{0}\nabla^{\perp}_{0}\psi((\mathbf{x},0))|

\displaystyle\leq|\nabla_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{1}((\mathbf{x},0)),0)|+|\nabla_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{2}^{1}((\mathbf{x},0)),0)|

\displaystyle+|\nabla_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{2}^{2}((\mathbf{x},0)),0)|+|\nabla_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{2}^{3}((\mathbf{x},0)),0)|

\displaystyle+|\nabla_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{2}^{4}((\mathbf{x},0)),0)|+|\nabla_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{3}((\mathbf{x},0)),0)|

\displaystyle=:|\nabla_{0}\mathbf{u}_{1}|+|\nabla_{0}\mathbf{u}_{2}^{1}|+|\nabla_{0}\mathbf{u}_{2}^{2}|+|\nabla_{0}\mathbf{u}_{2}^{3}|

\displaystyle+|\nabla_{0}\mathbf{u}_{2}^{4}|+|\nabla_{0}\mathbf{u}_{3}|

where

\displaystyle\nabla_{0}\mathbf{u}_{1}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{B_{1}}\zeta_{L}((\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r))\frac{e^{-|(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r)|}}{|(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r)|}\nabla_{0}\nabla^{\perp}_{0}w((\mathbf{y},0))\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\mathrm{d}r,

\displaystyle\nabla_{0}\mathbf{u}_{2}^{1}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{B_{2}}\big[1-\zeta_{L}((\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r))\big]\nabla_{0}\nabla^{\perp}_{0}\bigg[\frac{e^{-|(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r)|}}{|(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r)|}\bigg]w((\mathbf{y},0))\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\mathrm{d}r,

\displaystyle\nabla_{0}\mathbf{u}_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{B_{2}}\nabla_{0}\nabla^{\perp}_{0}\big[1-\zeta_{L}((\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r))\big]\frac{e^{-|(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r)|}}{|(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r)|}\,w((\mathbf{y},0))\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\mathrm{d}r,

\displaystyle\nabla_{0}\mathbf{u}_{2}^{3}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{B_{2}}\nabla^{\perp}_{0}\big[1-\zeta_{L}((\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r))\big]\nabla_{0}\bigg[\frac{e^{-|(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r)|}}{|(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r)|}\bigg]\,w((\mathbf{y},0))\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\mathrm{d}r,

\displaystyle\nabla_{0}\mathbf{u}_{2}^{4}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{B_{2}}\nabla_{0}\big[1-\zeta_{L}((\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r))\big]\nabla^{\perp}_{0}\bigg[\frac{e^{-|(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r)|}}{|(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r)|}\bigg]\,w((\mathbf{y},0))\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\mathrm{d}r,

\displaystyle\nabla_{0}\mathbf{u}_{3}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{B_{3}}\nabla_{0}\nabla^{\perp}_{0}\bigg[\big[1-\zeta_{L}((\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r))\big]\frac{e^{-|(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r)|}}{|(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r)|}\bigg]w((\mathbf{y},0))\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\mathrm{d}r.

For
L\in(0,1]
, we have that

\displaystyle|\nabla_{0}\mathbf{u}_{1}|

\displaystyle\lesssim\bigg(\int_{B_{1}}\frac{e^{-2|(\mathbf{x},0)-(\mathbf{y},r)|}}{|(\mathbf{x},0)-(\mathbf{y},r)|^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\mathrm{d}r\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla_{0}\nabla^{\perp}_{0}w((\mathbf{y},0))\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\bigg(\int_{0}^{2L}\frac{e^{-2s}}{s^{2}}\,s^{2}\mathrm{d}s\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|w\|_{2,2}\lesssim\big(1-e^{-4L}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|w\|_{2,2}\lesssim\pi L^{\frac{1}{2}}\|w\|_{2,2}.

Now note that

\displaystyle\nabla_{0}\mathbf{u}_{2}^{1}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{B_{2}}\big[1-\zeta_{L}((\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r))\big]\bigg\{\frac{2(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})^{T}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})^{\perp}}{(|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{2}+r^{2})^{2}}+\frac{3(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})^{T}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})^{\perp}}{(|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{2}+r^{2})^{\frac{5}{2}}}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{2}+r^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
-1&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{(|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{2}+r^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
-1&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle+\frac{(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})^{T}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})^{\perp}}{(|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{2}+r^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}+\frac{(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})^{T}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})^{\perp}}{(|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{2}+r^{2})^{2}}\bigg\}e^{-|(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r)|}w(\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=:\sum_{i=1}^{6}\mathbb{K}_{i}((\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r)).

Clearly,

|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{2}\leq|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{2}+r^{2}=|(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r)|^{2}
and for any
L\in(0,1]
, the inequalities

\displaystyle{(e^{-L}-e^{-1})\leq(1-e^{-1})\leq(1-e^{-1})(1-\ln(L))\lesssim(1-\ln(L))}

holds independent of
L
. Therefore, for
L\in(0,1]
, it follows that

\displaystyle|\mathbb{K}_{1}((\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r))|

\displaystyle+|\mathbb{K}_{3}((\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r))+|\mathbb{K}_{6}((\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r))|

\displaystyle\lesssim\|w\|_{\infty}\int_{B_{2}}\frac{e^{-|(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r)|}}{|(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r)|^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|w\|_{\infty}\int_{L}^{1}\frac{e^{-s}}{s^{2}}s^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\lesssim\|w\|_{\infty}(1-\ln(L)).

Again, we can use
|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{2}\leq|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{2}+r^{2}
and the fact that the inequalities

\displaystyle{(e^{-L}(L+1)-2e^{-1})\leq(1-2e^{-1})\leq(1-2e^{-1})(1-\ln(L))\lesssim(1-\ln(L))}

holds independent of any
L\in(0,1]
to obtain

\displaystyle|\mathbb{K}_{5}((\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r))|

\displaystyle\lesssim\|w\|_{\infty}\int_{B_{2}}\frac{e^{-|(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r)|}}{|(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r)|}\,\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|w\|_{\infty}\int_{L}^{1}\frac{e^{-s}}{s}s^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\lesssim\|w\|_{\infty}(1-\ln(L)).

Finally, for
\mathbb{K}_{2}
and
\mathbb{K}_{4}
, we also obtain

\displaystyle|\mathbb{K}_{2}((\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r))|+|\mathbb{K}_{4}((\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r))

\displaystyle\lesssim\|w\|_{\infty}\int_{B_{2}}\frac{e^{-|(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r)|}}{|(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r)|^{3}}\,\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|w\|_{\infty}\int_{L}^{1}\frac{e^{-s}}{s^{3}}s^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\lesssim\|w\|_{\infty}e^{-L}\int_{L}^{1}\frac{1}{s}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\lesssim\|w\|_{\infty}e^{-0}\big(-\ln(L)\big).

We have shown that

\displaystyle|\nabla_{0}\mathbf{u}_{2}^{1}|\lesssim\|w\|_{\infty}(1-\ln(L))

(2.9)

for
L\in(0,1]
.
Also, the quantity
(1/L^{2})[e^{-L}(L+1)-e^{-2L}(2L+1)]
is uniformly bounded for any
L\in(0,1]
and as such,

\displaystyle|\nabla_{0}\mathbf{u}_{2}^{2}|

\displaystyle\lesssim\bigg(\int_{L}^{2L}\frac{e^{-s}}{sL^{2}}\,s^{2}\mathrm{d}s\bigg)\|w\|_{\infty}\lesssim\|w\|_{\infty}.

(2.10)

Next, we note that the estimate for
\nabla_{0}\mathbf{u}_{2}^{3}
and
\nabla_{0}\mathbf{u}_{2}^{4}
will be the same where in particular,

\displaystyle\nabla_{0}\mathbf{u}_{2}^{3}

\displaystyle:=\frac{-1}{4\pi}\int_{B_{2}}\nabla_{0}^{\perp}\big[1-\zeta_{L}((\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r))\big]\bigg\{\frac{(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})^{T}}{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{2}+r^{2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})^{T}}{(|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{2}+r^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\bigg\}e^{-|(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r)|}w(\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=:\mathbb{K}_{7}((\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r))+\mathbb{K}_{8}((\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r)).

Since
|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|\leq 1
holds on
B_{2}
, it follows from the condition
|\nabla_{0}^{\perp}\zeta_{L}(\mathbf{z})|\lesssim L^{-1}
that

\displaystyle|\mathbb{K}_{7}((\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r))|

\displaystyle\lesssim\bigg(\int_{L}^{2L}\frac{e^{-s}}{s^{2}L}s^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\bigg)\|w\|_{\infty}\lesssim\|w\|_{\infty}

since
(1/L)[e^{-L}-e^{-2L}]
is uniformly bounded in
L
. Similarly, we can use the fact that
|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|\leq\sqrt{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{2}+r^{2}}
to obtain

\displaystyle|\mathbb{K}_{8}((\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r))|

\displaystyle\lesssim\bigg(\int_{L}^{2L}\frac{e^{-s}}{s^{2}L}s^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\bigg)\|w\|_{\infty}\lesssim\|w\|_{\infty}.

We can therefore conclude that,

\displaystyle|\nabla_{0}\mathbf{u}_{2}^{3}|+|\nabla_{0}\mathbf{u}_{2}^{4}|\lesssim\|w\|_{\infty}.

(2.11)

Similar to the estimate for
\nabla\mathbf{u}_{2}^{1}
, we have that

\displaystyle|\nabla_{0}\mathbf{u}_{3}|\lesssim\|w\|_{\infty}.

(2.12)

It follows by summing up the various estimates above that

\displaystyle\|\nabla\mathbf{u}\|_{\infty}\lesssim L^{\frac{1}{2}}\|w\|_{2,2}+(1-\ln(L))\|w\|_{\infty}.

(2.13)

It remains to show that the estimate (
2.13
) also holds for
\mathbf{u}
.

For this, we first recall that

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\nabla^{\perp}_{0}\bigg[\frac{e^{-|(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r)|}}{|(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r)|}\bigg]w((\mathbf{y},0))\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\mathrm{d}r.

(2.14)

We now use the inequalities

\displaystyle|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|\leq\big(|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{2}+r^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}

(2.15)

and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\bigg|\nabla^{\perp}_{0}\frac{e^{-|(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r)|}}{|(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r)|}\bigg|\lesssim\bigg[\frac{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|}{(|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{2}+r^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}+\frac{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|}{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{2}+r^{2}}\bigg]e^{-|(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r)|}

to obtain

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|w\|_{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\bigg[\frac{1}{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{2}+r^{2}}+\frac{1}{(|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{2}+r^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}}\bigg]e^{-|(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},-r)|}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\mathrm{d}r

(2.16)

\displaystyle\lesssim\|w\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-s}}{s^{2}}s^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\|w\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-s}}{s}s^{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\lesssim\|w\|_{\infty}.

Therefore, it follows from (
2.13
) and (
2.16
) that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{u}\|_{1,\infty}\lesssim L^{\frac{1}{2}}\|w\|_{2,2}+(1-\ln(L))\|w\|_{\infty}.

(2.17)

If
\|w\|_{2,2}\leq 1
, we choose
L=1
and if
\|w\|_{2,2}>1
, we take
L=\|w\|_{2,2}^{-2}
so that (
2.8
) holds. This finishes the proof.

Before we end the subsection, we also note that a direct computation using the definition of Sobolev norms in frequency space (
1.7
) immediately yield

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{u}\|_{k+1,2}\lesssim\|w\|_{k,2}

(2.18)

for any
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
where
w\in W^{k,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is a given function in (
2.7
).

2.3
A priori estimate

In order to prove Theorem
1.2
,
we first need some preliminary estimates for
(b,q)
. In the following, we define

\displaystyle\|(b,q)\|:=\|b\|_{3,2}+\|q\|_{2,2}.

Lemma 1

A strong solution of (
1.1
)–(
1.4
) satisfies the bound

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\|(b,q)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\big(1+\|\mathbf{u}\|_{1,\infty}+\|\nabla b\|_{\infty}+\|q\|_{\infty}\big)\big(1+\|(b,q)\|^{2}\big).

(2.19)

Proof

Since the space of smooth functions is dense in the space
W^{3,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\times W^{2,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
of existence, in the following, we work with a smooth solution pair
(b,q)
.
To achieve our desired estimate, we apply
\partial^{\beta}
to (
1.1
) for
|\beta|\leq 3
to obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\partial^{\beta}b+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\partial^{\beta}b=R_{1}

(2.20)

where

\displaystyle R_{1}:=\mathbf{u}\cdot\partial^{\beta}\nabla b-\partial^{\beta}(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla b).

Now since
\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u}=0
, if we multiply (
2.20
) by
\partial^{\beta}b

and integrate over space, the second term on the left-hand side of (
2.20
) vanishes after integration by parts. On the other hand, we can use the commutator estimate (see for instant
(
crisan2021theoretical
, Section 2.2)
) to estimate the residual term
R_{1}
.
Consequently, by multiplying (
2.20
) by
\partial^{\beta}b
, integrating over space, and
summing over the multiindices
\beta
so that
|\beta|\leq 3
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\|b\|_{3,2}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\Big(\|\nabla\mathbf{u}\|_{\infty}\|b\|_{3,2}+\|\nabla b\|_{\infty}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{3,2}\Big)\|b\|_{3,2}

(2.21)

\displaystyle\lesssim(\|\nabla\mathbf{u}\|_{\infty}+\|\nabla b\|_{\infty})(1+\|(b,q)\|^{2})

where we have used (
2.18
) for
w=q-f
and
k=2
.

Next, we find a bound for
\|q\|^{2}_{2,2}
. For this, we apply
\partial^{\beta}
to (
1.2
) for
|\beta|\leq 2
and we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\partial^{\beta}q+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\partial^{\beta}(q-b)+\mathbf{u}_{h}\cdot\nabla\partial^{\beta}b=R_{2}+R_{3}+R_{4}

(2.22)

where

\displaystyle R_{2}

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{u}\cdot\partial^{\beta}\nabla q-\partial^{\beta}(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla q),

\displaystyle R_{3}

\displaystyle:=-\mathbf{u}\cdot\partial^{\beta}\nabla b+\partial^{\beta}(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla b),

\displaystyle R_{4}

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{u}_{h}\cdot\partial^{\beta}\nabla b-\partial^{\beta}(\mathbf{u}_{h}\cdot\nabla b).

Now notice that for
\mathbb{U}:=\nabla\mathbf{u}
, it follows from interpolation that

\displaystyle\|\nabla\mathbb{U}\|_{4}\lesssim\|\mathbb{U}\|_{\infty}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla^{2}\mathbb{U}\|_{2}^{\frac{1}{2}}

and so,

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{2}\mathbf{u}\|_{4}\lesssim\|\nabla\mathbf{u}\|_{\infty}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{3,2}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Similarly

\displaystyle\|\nabla q\|_{4}\lesssim\|q\|_{\infty}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|q\|_{2,2}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\|\nabla q\|_{4}\|\nabla^{2}\mathbf{u}\|_{4}\lesssim\|q\|_{\infty}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{3,2}+\|\nabla\mathbf{u}\|_{\infty}\|q\|_{2,2}.

By using this estimate, we deduce from (
2.18
) and commutator estimates that

\displaystyle\|R_{2}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\nabla\mathbf{u}\|_{\infty}\|q\|_{2,2}+\|q\|_{\infty}(1+\|q\|_{2,2}).

(2.23)

The commutators
R_{3}
and
R_{4}
are easy to estimate and are given by

\displaystyle\|R_{3}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\nabla\mathbf{u}\|_{\infty}\|b\|_{3,2}+\|\nabla b\|_{\infty}(1+\|q\|_{2,2}),

(2.24)

\displaystyle\|R_{4}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|b\|_{3,2}+\|\nabla b\|_{\infty},

(2.25)

respectively, for a given
\mathbf{u}_{h}\in W^{3,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
Next, by using
\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u}=0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\big\langle(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\partial^{\beta}q)\,,\,\partial^{\beta}q\big\rangle=0.

(2.26)

Additionally, the following estimates holds true

\displaystyle\Big|\big\langle(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\partial^{\beta}b)\,,\,\partial^{\beta}q\big\rangle\Big|

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\infty}\|b\|_{3,2}^{2}+\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\infty}\|q\|_{2,2}^{2},

(2.27)

\displaystyle\Big|\big\langle(\mathbf{u}_{h}\cdot\nabla\partial^{\beta}b)\,,\,\partial^{\beta}q\big\rangle\Big|

\displaystyle\lesssim\|b\|_{3,2}^{2}+\|q\|_{2,2}^{2}

(2.28)

since
\mathbf{u}_{h}\in W^{3,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
If we now collect the estimates above (keeping in mind that
f\in W^{2,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
\mathbf{u}_{h}\in W^{3,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2})
), we obtain by multiplying (
1.2
) by
\partial^{\beta}q
and then summing over
|\beta|\leq 2
, the following

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\|q\|_{2,2}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\big(1+\|\mathbf{u}\|_{1,\infty}+\|\nabla b\|_{\infty}+\|q\|_{\infty}\big)\big(1+\|(b,q)\|^{2}\big).

(2.29)

Summing up (
2.21
) and (
2.29
) yields the desired result.

We now have all in hand to prove our main theorem, Theorem
1.2
.

Proof
(Proof of Theorem
1.2
)

In the following, we define the time-dependent function
g
as

\displaystyle g(t):=\mathrm{e}+\|(b,q)(t)\|,\quad\text{for}\quad t\in[0,T].

(2.30)

Next, without loss of generality, we assume that
f=0
so that from Proposition
1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{u}(t)\|_{1,\infty}\lesssim 1+(1+\ln\|q(t)\|_{2,2})(\|\nabla b(t)\|_{\infty}+\|q(t)\|_{\infty})

(2.31)

for
t\in[0,T]
.
Using the monotonic properties of logarithms, it follows from the above that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{u}(t)\|_{1,\infty}\lesssim 1+\ln[g(t)](\|\nabla b(t)\|_{\infty}+\|q(t)\|_{\infty}).

(2.32)

Furthermore, since
1\leq\ln(\mathrm{e}+|x|)
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we can deduce from the inequality above that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{u}(t)\|_{1,\infty}+\|\nabla b(t)\|_{\infty}+\|q(t)\|_{\infty}\lesssim 1+\ln[g(t)](\|\nabla b(t)\|_{\infty}+\|q(t)\|_{\infty}).

(2.33)

On the other hand, it follows from Lemma
1
that

\displaystyle g(t)\leq g(0)\exp\bigg(c\int_{0}^{t}\big(1+\|\mathbf{u}(s)\|_{1,\infty}+\|\nabla b(s)\|_{\infty}+\|q(s)\|_{\infty}\big)\,\mathrm{d}s\bigg)

(2.34)

for any
t\in[0,T]
.
Combining (
2.33
) and (
2.34
) yields

\displaystyle g(t)\leq g(0)\exp\bigg(c\int_{0}^{t}\big(1+\ln[g(s)](\|\nabla b(s)\|_{\infty}+\|q(s)\|_{\infty})\big)\,\mathrm{d}s\bigg).

(2.35)

We can now take logarithm of both sides and apply Grönwall’s lemma to the resulting inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\ln[g(t)]\leq\big(\ln[g(0)]+cT\big)\exp\bigg(c\int_{0}^{t}(\|\nabla b(s)\|_{\infty}+\|q(s)\|_{\infty})\,\mathrm{d}s\bigg).

(2.36)

At this, point, we can now utilize (
1.8
), take exponentials in (
2.36
) and obtain

\displaystyle\|(b,q)(t)\|\leq[g(0)]^{\exp(cK)}\exp[cT\exp(cK)]

(2.37)

for any
t\in[0,T]
. Since the right-hand side is finite, it follows that the solution
(b,q)
can be continued on some interval
[0,T^{\prime})
for some
T^{\prime}>T

.
This finishes the proof.
