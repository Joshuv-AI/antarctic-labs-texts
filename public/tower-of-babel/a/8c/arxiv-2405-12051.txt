Abstract
. We study the amount of nonhyperbolicity within a broad class of (nonhyperbolic) partially hyperbolic diffeomorphisms with a one-dimensional center. For that, we focus on the center Lyapunov exponent and the entropy of its level sets. We show that these entropies vary continuously and can be expressed in terms of restricted variational principles. In this study, no dynamical coherence is required. Of particular interest is the case where the exponent is zero. To study this level set, we construct a compact set foliated by curves tangent to the central direction. Within this set, the entropy attains the maximal possible (and positive) value. Moreover, finite-time Lyapunov exponents converge uniformly to zero. In this construction, we introduce a mechanism to concatenate center curves. The class studied consists of those robustly transitive diffeomorphisms that have a pair of blender-horseshoes with different types of hyperbolicity and possess minimal strong stable and unstable foliations. This classes includes flow-type and circle-fibered diffeomorphisms as well as some derived from Anosov diffeomorphisms. It also includes the so-called anomalous examples which are dynamically incoherent.

1.
Introduction

We describe the “amount of nonhyperbolicity” of several classes of robustly transitive and nonhyperbolic diffeomorphisms. This is done in terms of entropy and restricted variational principles. Here we deal with those that are partially hyperbolic with a one-dimensional center bundle. For that, we develop a thermodynamical formalism suited for this setting.

Central to the thermodynamical formalism are (1) Lyapunov exponents (a special type of Birkhoff averages), (2) level sets of points with equal exponent, and (3) their metric and topological entropies. Frequently, the interrelation between these quantifiers is expressed through pressure functions and variational principles. In hyperbolic settings, one of the striking features of this formalism, one may highlight, is that there are uncountably many highly irregular, intertwined, fractal level sets whose quantifiers change in an even analytic way and can be described by employing Gibbs measures. To show this, one heavily relies on symbolic dynamics (see, for example,
[
B08
,
R04
,
PW97
]
). Some of the major difficulties to go beyond uniform hyperbolicity come from the non-existence of symbolic codings or lack of specification-like properties up to the occurrence of nonhyperbolic measures (for which “Pesin theory” is unavailable).

To go beyond uniform hyperbolicity is a challenge, which this paper addresses. To do so, a minimum amount of hyperbolicity seems natural to require. Following the motto of Pugh–Shub “a little hyperbolicity goes a long way”
[
PS97
]
, here we assume
partially hyperbolicity with a one-dimensional center bundle
. According to
[
BDV05
, Preface]
, this is a noncritical setting where two types of hyperbolicity are “intermingled” and “interact” and share a common center bundle. The case of higher-dimensional centers is beyond scope. We will see that there is a huge class of examples that fall into the setting studied here.

To be more precise, assume that
f\in\Diff^{1}(M)
is
partially hyperbolic
with a
Df
-invariant splitting with three nontrivial directions
TM=E^{\mathrm{ss}}\oplus E^{\mathrm{c}}\oplus E^{\mathrm{uu}}
, where
E^{\mathrm{ss}}
and
E^{\mathrm{uu}}
are the uniformly contracting and expanding bundles, respectively, and
E^{\mathrm{c}}
is the one-dimensional center bundle. Recall that there exist (uniquely defined) foliations tangent to the bundles
E^{\mathrm{ss}}
and
E^{\mathrm{uu}}
, called
strong stable
and
strong unstable foliations
and denoted by
\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{ss}}
and
\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{uu}}
, respectively,
[
BP74
,
HPS77
]
. Our hypotheses require that
f
belongs to the class
\BM^{1}(M)
of maps satisfying: the simultaneous minimality of strong foliations and the existence of two blender-horseshoes (for discussion see
[
BCDW16
]
) of different types of hyperbolicity. These conditions provide precisely the “interaction” between two regions with different types of hyperbolicity mentioned above. We observe that these hypotheses are fairly common among partially hyperbolic diffeomorphisms which are nonhyperbolic and robustly transitive.
We emphasize that our methods do not require
dynamical coherence
, that is, the existence of invariant foliations tangent to
E^{\mathrm{ss}}\oplus E^{\mathrm{c}}
and
E^{\mathrm{c}}\oplus E^{\mathrm{uu}}
(and hence, the existence of a center foliation tangent to
E^{\mathrm{c}}
). We neither require the existence of a center foliation tangent to
E^{\mathrm{c}}
.
Dynamically coherent examples of diffeomorphisms satisfying our hypotheses include
flow-type ones (this includes perturbations of the time-one map of transitive Anosov flows),
circle-fibered ones (partially hyperbolic diffeomorphisms with a one-dimensional compact center foliation),
and some DA (derived from Anosov) diffeomorphisms. Dynamically incoherent ones include the so-called
anomalous
examples in
[
BGHP20
]
. See Section
2.4
for further details.

Under the above partially hyperbolic hypothesis, the center bundle is among those provided by the Oseledets splitting
[
O68
]
and the absence of hyperbolicity is reflected by a zero Lyapunov exponent associated with this bundle. Three distinct types of nonhyperbolic behavior can be identified:

(P1)

occurrence of ergodic measures with zero Lyapunov exponent,

(P2)

existence of points having zero Lyapunov exponent,

(P3)

existence of ergodic measures whose (nonzero) Lyapunov exponents converge to 0.

Clearly, (P1) implies (P2), but the other implications
a priori
do not hold. For
f\in\BM^{1}(M)
, all three properties simultaneously hold (see
[
BBD16
,
BZ19
,
DGS20
,
YZ20
]
) and therefore we are in a genuinely nonhyperbolic setting.

To state our main result, let us introduce some notations. Consider the function

(1.1)

\varphi^{\mathrm{c}}\colon M\to\mathbb{R},\quad\varphi^{\mathrm{c}}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\log\,\lVert D^{\mathrm{c}}f(x)\rVert,\quad\text{ where }\quad D^{\mathrm{c}}f(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}Df|_{E^{\mathrm{c}}(x)},

which, by our hypotheses, is continuous. A point
x\in M
is
\varphi^{\mathrm{c}}
-regular
if

\chi^{\mathrm{c}}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\lim_{n\to\pm\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\varphi^{\mathrm{c}}\circ f^{k}(x)=\lim_{n\to\pm\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\,\lVert D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{n}(x)\rVert.

Given
\alpha
, consider the
level set
of points with exponent
\alpha
,

(1.2)

\mathcal{L}(\alpha)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\big\{x\in M\colon\chi^{\mathrm{c}}(x)=\alpha\big\}.

One key feature of our setting is that these sets are nonempty for a closed interval of values
\alpha
containing zero in its interior.

By the Birkhoff ergodic theorem, given
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\rm erg}(f)
(the set of
f
-ergodic measures),
\mu
-almost every point
x
is
\varphi^{\mathrm{c}}
-regular and verifies

\chi^{\mathrm{c}}(x)=\chi^{\mathrm{c}}(\mu)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\int\varphi^{\mathrm{c}}\,d\mu.

We call this number the
(center) Lyapunov exponent
of
\mu
. We can also consider the “level sets” of ergodic measures having exponent
\alpha
and the numbers

(1.3)

\mathcal{H}(\alpha)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\sup\big\{h_{\mu}(f)\colon\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\rm erg}(f),\chi^{\mathrm{c}}(\mu)=\alpha\big\}.

Let

(1.4)

\alpha_{\rm min}^{f}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\rm erg}(f)}\chi^{\mathrm{c}}(\mu),\quad\alpha_{\rm max}^{f}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\rm erg}(f)}\chi^{\mathrm{c}}(\mu).

If the map
f
is clear from the context, we drop the super-index
f
from the notation. It is straightforward to check that the above infimum and supremum are both attained by ergodic measures (taking limit measures and considering ergodic decompositions).

Our main theorem extends classical results in multifractal analysis for uniformly hyperbolic dynamics. For that, as a quantifier, we use the concept of topological entropy on a set
X\subset M
,
h_{\rm top}(f,X)
, introduced by Bowen
[
B73
]
.

Main Theorem
.

Let
f\in\BM^{1}(M)
. Then the function
\alpha\in[\alpha_{\rm min},\alpha_{\rm max}]\mapsto h_{\rm top}(f,\mathcal{L}(\alpha))
is continuous and

(1.5)

0<h_{\rm top}(f,\mathcal{L}(0))=\lim_{\alpha\nearrow 0}\mathcal{H}(\alpha)=\lim_{\beta\searrow 0}\mathcal{H}(\beta).

Moreover, there is a set
\Lambda(0)\subset\mathcal{L}(0)
consisting of non-degenerate curves tangent to the center bundle
E^{\mathrm{c}}
such that

(1)

h_{\rm top}(f,\Lambda(0))=h_{\rm top}(f,\mathcal{L}(0))
,

(2)

\lim_{n\to\pm\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{n}(x)\|=0
, uniformly on
x\in\Lambda(0)
.

This theorem is a consequence of Theorems
1
and
3
stated in Section
2
. In Theorem
1
, we deal with the continuity of the entropy spectrum for the hyperbolic part (i.e. for
\alpha\neq 0
) and in Theorem
3
, we deal with the nonhyperbolic part (i.e. at
\alpha=0
).

In the particular case of step skew-products with symbolic base dynamics (a full shift in finitely many symbols) and circle fibers, a precursor of this theorem is given in
[
DGR19
]
. The proofs in
[
DGR19
]
strongly rely on a symbolic description that is unavailable here. Moreover, we deal with dynamics that do have a one-dimensional center bundle, but this bundle may not give rise to a center foliation (see, for instance, the examples in
[
BGHP20
]
) nor a foliation by circles (for instance the perturbations of time one-map of geodesic flows on negatively curved surfaces). This is a major difficulty overcome in this paper and will be further discussed in Section
2
.

One of the main difficulties is to deal with points with zero Lyapunov exponent. By
[
LY85a
,
LY85b
]
, from measure-theoretical viewpoint, when the center exponent is zero then any entropy is “created in the strong foliations” (see also
[
T21
]
). Inspired by this fact, we introduce a flexible symbol-free strategy working directly with the strong foliations. Our proofs combine two tools. The first one is the use of pressure functions (and their Legendre-Fenchel transforms) and gradual approximation by (uniformly hyperbolic) exhausting families. This part implements and extends the formalism introduced in
[
DGR17
,
DGR19
]
. The second one is a method to concatenate center curves, which has intrinsic interest. It is used to construct the set
\Lambda(0)
in the Main Theorem. For that, we use the weakly integrable property which holds for partially hyperbolic diffeomorphisms with one-dimensional center (see
[
BBI04
, Proposition 3.4]
, where this property is called
weakly dynamically coherent
).

In Section
2
, we provide more detailed statements towards the Main Theorem as well as a discussion of the context and techniques. We also describe the organization of this paper.

2.1.
Context

We endow the space
\Diff^{1}(M)
of
C^{1}
-diffeomorphisms of
M
with the
C^{1}
-topology. Recall that
f\in\Diff^{1}(M)
is
partially hyperbolic
, if there exist a
Df
-invariant splitting
TM=E^{\mathrm{ss}}\oplus E^{\mathrm{c}}\oplus E^{\mathrm{uu}}
and an integer
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every point
x\in M
,

•

\|Df^{N}|_{E^{\mathrm{ss}}(x)}\|<1
and
\|Df^{-N}|_{E^{\mathrm{uu}}(x)}\|<1
;

•

\max\big\{\|Df^{N}|_{E^{\mathrm{ss}}(x)}\|\cdot\|Df^{-N}|_{E^{\mathrm{c}}(f^{N}(x))}\|,\|Df^{N}|_{E^{\mathrm{c}}(x)}\|\cdot\|Df^{-N}|_{E^{\mathrm{uu}}(f^{N}(x))}\|\big\}<1.

Up to changing the metric, one can assume that
N=1
(see
[
G07
]
) and we do so throughout this paper. Denote by
\PH^{1}_{\mathrm{c}=1}(M)
the set of
C^{1}
-partially hyperbolic diffeomorphisms on
M
with a one-dimensional center bundle. By definition, this set is open in
\Diff^{1}(M)
. In what follows, we assume
f\in\PH^{1}_{\mathrm{c}=1}(M)
.

Given any
f\in\PH^{1}_{\mathrm{c}=1}(M)
its space of ergodic measures naturally splits as follows

\mathcal{M}_{\rm erg}(f)=\mathcal{M}_{\rm erg,<0}(f)\cup\mathcal{M}_{\rm erg,>0}(f)\cup\mathcal{M}_{\rm erg,0}(f),

into the sets of measures with negative, positive, and zero center Lyapunov exponents, respectively. The former we call
hyperbolic
ergodic measures of
contracting
and
expanding type
, respectively. The ones in
\mathcal{M}_{\rm erg,0}(f)
are
nonhyperbolic
.
A priori
, without further hypotheses, any of these components (and also any pair of them) can be empty. The most interesting case, and the focus of this paper, occurs when all three components are nonempty. This happens when
f\in\BM^{1}(M)
as we proceed to describe.

We start introducing some terminology. Given a
saddle
, that is, a hyperbolic periodic point, its
\mathrm{u}
-index
is the dimension of its unstable bundle. Any saddle has either
\mathrm{u}
-index
d^{\mathrm{uu}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\dim(E^{\mathrm{uu}})
or
\mathrm{u}
-index
d^{\mathrm{uu}}+1
.
Transitivity
of
f
means that this map has a dense orbit. A property of a diffeomorphism is
C^{1}
-robust
if it holds in a neighborhood of it in
\Diff^{1}(M)
. We define
\RTPH^{1}(M)
as the subset of
\PH^{1}_{\mathrm{c}=1}(M)
of diffeomorphisms which are
C^{1}
-robustly transitive and have saddles of different
\mathrm{u}
-indices. By its very definition,

\RTPH^{1}(M)
is open in
\Diff^{1}(M)
and every
f\in\RTPH^{1}(M)
is not hyperbolic. A key property of
\RTPH^{1}(M)
is that there is a
C^{1}
-open and -dense subset formed by diffeomorphisms which possess
stable
and
unstable blender-horseshoes
, simultaneously (see Section
2.4.3
). Blender-horseshoes are important local plugs for obtaining topological and ergodic properties. However, they alone are insufficient for describing the (nonempty) sets
\mathcal{M}_{\rm erg,0}(f)
and
\mathcal{L}(0)
. To achieve this one needs to “dynamically connect” these blender-horseshoes. This connection is obtained under the assumption of (simultaneous) minimality of the strong stable and unstable foliations. Recall that a foliation of
M
is
minimal
if every one of its leaves is dense in
M
. This approach is reminiscent of
[
GIKN05
]
, where the systems have “center-contracting” and “center-expanding” regions with orbits going from one region to the other one.
This discussion provides the context for our two key hypotheses.

Definition 2.1

(The set
\BM^{1}(M)
)
.

The set
\BM^{1}(M)
consists of diffeomorphisms
f\in\PH^{1}_{\mathrm{c}=1}(M)
satisfying the following two properties:

(M)

the foliations
\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{uu}}
and
\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{ss}}
are both minimal,

(B)

there are
n^{\pm}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f^{n_{-}}
has a stable blender-horseshoe and
f^{n_{+}}
has an unstable blender-horseshoe.

We postpone the precise definitions of the blender-horseshoes in (B) to Section
6
. The interrelation between properties (B) and (M), and their relation with robust transitivity are discussed in Sections
2.4.2
and
2.4.3
. We discuss examples in Section
2.4
.

Remark 2.2

(
\BM^{1}(M)
is
C^{1}
-open)
.

Condition (B) is
C^{1}
-open, while condition (M) itself
a priori
is not
C^{1}
-open. However, if (M) and (B) hold for a diffeomorphism, then both hold in any small
C^{1}
-neighborhood of it. This is essentially a consequence of
[
BDU02
]
, see also Lemma
6.8
. Note that
\BM^{1}(M)\subset\RTPH^{1}(M)
.

Remark 2.3

(Topology of the space of ergodic measures)
.

Given
f\in\BM^{1}(M)
, the occurrence of un-/stable blender-horseshoes implies that the spectrum
\{\chi^{\mathrm{c}}(\mu)\colon\mathcal{M}_{\rm erg}(f)\}
contains negative and positive values. Moreover, the interaction between saddles of different indices (provided by the minimality of the foliations and the blender-horseshoes) implies that this spectrum also contains the value 0. Moreover, the value
0
is accumulated by negative and positive values (see
[
BZ19
, Theorem A]
and also
[
DGS20
,
YZ20
]
). These properties have their counterpart on the space of ergodic measures: every measure in
\mathcal{M}_{\rm erg,0}(f)
can be approximated in the weak
\ast
-topology and in entropy by measures in
\mathcal{M}_{\rm erg,<0}(f)
and in
\mathcal{M}_{\rm erg,>0}(f)
(see
[
DGS20
,
YZ20
]
). Moreover, there are measures in
\mathcal{M}_{\rm erg,0}(f)
with positive entropy
[
BBD16
]
.

0<\mathcal{H}(0)\leq h_{\rm top}(f,\mathcal{L}(0))=\mathfrak{h}(f).

Moreover, there is a set
\Lambda(0)\subset\mathcal{L}(0)
formed by non-degenerate pairwise disjoint center curves such that

(1)

h_{\rm top}(f,\Lambda(0))=h_{\rm top}(f,\mathcal{L}(0))=\mathfrak{h}(f)
,

(2)

\lim_{n\to\pm\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{n}(x)\|=0
, uniformly on
x\in\Lambda(0)
.

In Section
2.3
, we explain the main heuristic underlying the proof of Theorem
3
: a concatenation of center curves.

2.3.
Concatenation and outbranching of center curves

First, Theorem
1
guarantees that there are ergodic measures
\mu_{k}
in
\mathcal{M}_{\rm erg,<0}(f)
whose center Lyapunov exponents converge to
0
. Each ergodic measure has a (plenty of) generic point
y_{k}
with the corresponding exponent (the cardinality growing exponentially fast, according to its entropy). We fix a sequence
(y_{k})_{k}
of such points whose exponents tend to zero. We also choose for every
k
some center curve
\zeta_{k}
centered at
y_{k}
and sufficiently small.

Let us now start by concatenating two center curves, say
\zeta_{1}
and
\zeta_{2}
. We consider what we call a
\mathrm{u}
-strip
\Delta^{\mathrm{cu}}
associated to
\zeta_{1}
, that is the union of local strong unstable leaves through
\zeta_{1}
. Analogously, we consider an
\mathrm{s}
-strip
\Delta^{\mathrm{cs}}
associated to
\zeta_{2}
. Taking forward iterates of the former and backward iterates of the latter, we obtain images of them that enter the region of a stable blender-horseshoe. The number of iterates needed to enter only depends on the size of the local strong un-/stable leaves. Using the blender, after some further iterations, we can make both strips intersect through some center curve
\check{\zeta}
. Pulling back
\check{\zeta}
to
\Delta^{\mathrm{cu}}
, we get a center curve
\widehat{\zeta}
that stretches completely over the
\mathrm{u}
-strip of the initial curve
\zeta_{1}
. Moreover,
\widehat{\zeta}
accompanies it during the initial iterations and eventually ends close to
\zeta_{2}
. Compare Figure
3
in Section
7
.

This concatenation can now be applied to the sequence of center curves
(\zeta_{k})_{k}
from above. In the limit, we get a center curve
\zeta_{\infty}^{+}
with forward exponent
0
.

To produce
many
center curves
\zeta_{\infty}^{+}
as above (which form a set of positive topological entropy), at each step we choose between exponentially many curves from the next step. This technique is somewhat reminiscent of the one of “outbranching sequences” in
[
DGR19
, Section 8]
. However, here we perform this in a much more efficient and transparent way. In this process, we get a fractal set produced as a limsup set, which is fibered by center curves of uniform length and entirely contained in some initial
\mathrm{u}
-strips. Its entropy is estimated by employing an entropy distribution principle. The underlying idea is that “entropy is seen in the strong unstable leaves only”.

The concatenation produces only a set with
forward
exponent zero. Using once more a concatenation for
f^{-1}
(this involves a sequence of curves with positive exponents tending to zero and an unstable blender-horseshoe), one gets the set
\Lambda(0)
.

2.4.
The set
\BM^{1}(M)

In this section, we discuss the definition of
\BM^{1}(M)
and provide examples.

2.4.1.
Robustly transitive nonhyperbolic diffeomorphisms

The first examples of robustly transitive diffeomorphisms were obtained in
[
S71
]
on
\mathbb{T}^{4}
. Such examples are partially hyperbolic with a two-dimensional center. Thereafter,
[
M78
]
provided the first examples in
\RTPH^{1}(M)
(see Section
2.1
) falling in the category of so-called DA diffeomorphisms on
\mathbb{T}^{3}
. New methods for constructing maps in
\RTPH^{1}(M)
were provided in
[
BD96
]
using blenders (a precursor of the blender-horseshoes with less structure). Key ingredients in
[
BD96
]
are the coexistence of some saddles of different
\mathrm{u}
-indices and the density of their invariant manifolds (the latter is a correlative of the minimality of the strong foliations). These methods were used to prove that appropriate perturbations of product maps
F\times\mathrm{Id}
on
M_{1}\times\mathbb{T}^{1}
, where
F
is a
C^{1}
-Anosov diffeomorphism on
M_{1}
, belong to
\RTPH^{1}(M_{1}\times\mathbb{T}^{1})
. Further classes are obtained in
[
BD96
]
considering manifolds
M
supporting transitive Anosov flows
\varphi^{t}
. It is proved that suitable small
C^{1}
-perturbations of the time-
T
map
\varphi^{T}
belong to
\RTPH^{1}(M)
. All these examples are dynamically coherent. A common feature of the latter two sorts of examples is the existence of circles tangent to the center bundle. In the perturbations of product maps, there is a center foliation formed by circles. In the case of time-
T
maps, the circles arise from the periodic orbits of the flow. We refer to these classes of examples as
circle-fibered diffeomorphisms
and
diffeomorphisms of flow-type
(following the terminology in
[
BFT22
]
), respectively.

Examples of a rather different nature are the
anomalous diffeomorphisms
obtained in
[
BGHP20
]
by considering maps in the unitary tangent bundle
M=T^{1}S
of a surface
S
of genus
g\geq 2
. For these maps the union of the circles that are tangent to the center direction is dense in
M
, however, none of those circles is periodic. These examples are not dynamically coherent. Another sort of examples can be found in
[
Y21
]
perturbing accessible volume-preserving diffeomorphisms whose volume measure is hyperbolic.

2.4.2.
Occurrence of blender-horseshoes

By the
connecting lemmas

[
H97
,
WX00
,
BC04
]
, robust transitivity implies
that for any pair of saddles of different
\mathrm{u}
-indices of
f\in\RTPH^{1}(M)
, there is
g
arbitrarily
C^{1}
-close to
f
with a
heterodimensional cycle
associated to them (i.e., the invariant manifolds of the saddles intersect cyclically). Therefore, the set of diffeomorphisms with heterodimensional cycles is
C^{1}
-dense in
\RTPH^{1}(M)
. These cycles are associated with saddles of
\mathrm{u}
-indices
d^{\mathrm{uu}}
and
d^{\mathrm{uu}}+1
(they have co-index
1
). Moreover, partial hyperbolicity implies that these saddles have real center eigenvalues (i.e., the eigenvalues associated with
E^{\mathrm{c}}
). After arbitrarily small
C^{1}
-perturbations, this sort of heterodimensional cycle yields blender-horseshoes (for some iterate of the map). They can be chosen as either stable or unstable, according to the type of perturbation, see
[
BD08
, Theorems 3.3 and 4.1]
. Even though the terminology blender-horseshoe was not used in
[
BD08
]
, the construction corresponds exactly to the blender-horseshoes in
[
BD12
, Section 5.1]
. By
[
BD12
, Lemma 3.9]
, the existence of (stable or unstable) blender-horseshoes is a
C^{1}
-open property. Therefore, property (B) in Definition
2.1
is
C^{1}
-open and -dense in
\RTPH^{1}(M)
.

2.4.3.
Minimality of the strong foliations

The results in
[
BDU02

RRU07
]

provide a criterion for a map

\RTPH^{1}_{0}(M)

In the case of a three-dimensional DA diffeomorphism, by

3.
Pre-skeletons

In this section, we construct basic sets based on so-called skeletons. Skeletons are a collection of orbit segments whose cardinality mimics entropy and whose finite-time-exponents “approximate” a given exponent. This idea is reminiscent of a common strategy (see, for example,
[
KH95
, Supplement S.5]
) to “ergodically approximate hyperbolic ergodic measures by horseshoes” and plays a key role in this paper.
This discussion will be continued in Section
4.3
.

Here we introduce the
pre-skeleton property
that is a reformulation of analogous concepts in skew-products and in a partially hyperbolic context (see
[
DGR17
, Section 4]
and
[
DGS20
, Section 5.2]
, respectively). The latter coincides with our setting. Note that the skeleton property in
[
DGS20
, Section 5.2]
is associated with an ergodic measure and guaranteed entirely by ergodic properties (see
[
DGR17
, Section 4.2]
, where it is called skeleton
\ast
property). Here, we present a purely topological version, which requires the existence of sufficiently many “approximating” orbit segments of arbitrary length (we call it
pre-skeleton
).

Given
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon>0
, two points
x,y
are
(m,\varepsilon)
-separated
(with respect to
f\in\Homeo(M)
) if

(3.1)

d_{n}(x,y)>\varepsilon,\quad\text{ where }\quad d_{n}(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\max_{j=0,\ldots,n-1}d(f^{j}(x),f^{j}(y)).

A subset
X\subset M
is
(m,\varepsilon)
-separated
if it consists of points which are mutually
(m,\varepsilon)
-separated.
By compactness, any such set is finite.

Definition 3.1

(Pre-skeleton)
.

Given
h>0
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, we say that
f\in\PH^{1}_{\mathrm{c}=1}(M)

has the pre-skeleton property
relative to
h
and
\alpha
, if for every
\varepsilon_{H}>0
and
\varepsilon_{E}>0
there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for every
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
there are constants
K_{0}\geq 1
and
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
m\geq n_{0}
there is a
(m,\varepsilon)
-separated set
X=X(h,\alpha,\varepsilon_{H},\varepsilon_{E},m)=\{x_{i}\}\subset M
, called
pre-skeleton
, satisfying:

(1)

the set
X
has cardinality
\card X\geq e^{m(h-\varepsilon_{H})}
;

(2)

for every
\ell=0,\ldots,m
and every
i
one has

K_{0}^{-1}e^{\ell(\alpha-\varepsilon_{E})}\leq\lVert D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{\ell}(x_{i})\rVert\leq K_{0}e^{\ell(\alpha+\varepsilon_{E})}.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
f\in\PH^{1}_{\mathrm{c}=1}(M)
. Consider
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
with
h_{\rm top}(f,\mathcal{L}(\alpha))>0
. Then for every
h\in(0,h_{\rm top}(f,\mathcal{L}(\alpha)))
, the map
f
has the pre-skeleton property relative to
h
and
\alpha
.

Proof.

Fix
\varepsilon_{H}\in(0,h)
and
\varepsilon_{E}\in(0,\alpha)
. Given
N\in\mathbb{N}
, let

\mathcal{L}_{N}(\alpha)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\big\{x\in\mathcal{L}(\alpha)\colon e^{n(\alpha-\varepsilon_{E})}\leq\lVert D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{n}(x)\rVert\leq e^{n(\alpha+\varepsilon_{E})}\text{ for every }n\geq N\big\}.

Note that
\mathcal{L}_{N}(\alpha)\subset\mathcal{L}_{N+1}(\alpha)
and

\mathcal{L}(\alpha)=\bigcup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{L}_{N}(\alpha).

As topological entropy is countably stable and monotone (see Remark
A.3
), one has

h_{\rm top}\big(f,\mathcal{L}(\alpha))=h_{\rm top}\big(f,\bigcup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{L}_{N}(\alpha)\big)=\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}h_{\rm top}(f,\mathcal{L}_{N}(\alpha))=\lim_{N\to\infty}h_{\rm top}(f,\mathcal{L}_{N}(\alpha)).

Hence, there is
N_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

h_{\rm top}(f,\mathcal{L}_{N_{1}}(\alpha))>h-\frac{1}{3}\varepsilon_{H}.

Note that, by item (2) of Lemma
A.2
,
\underline{Ch}_{\rm top}(f,\mathcal{L}_{N_{1}}(\alpha))\geq h_{\rm top}(f,\mathcal{L}_{N_{1}}(\alpha))
. It follows from the definition in (
A.1
) that for every
\varepsilon_{0}>0
sufficiently small and
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
, there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
m\geq n_{0}
, there exists a
(m,\varepsilon)
-separated set
\{x_{i}\}_{i\in I}
in
\mathcal{L}_{N_{1}}(\alpha)
of cardinality

\card I\geq e^{m(h-\varepsilon_{H})}.

This implies property (1) of a pre-skeleton.

To get property (2) of a pre-skeleton, it is enough to take

K_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\max_{\ell=0,\ldots,N_{1}-1}\left\{\lVert D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{\ell}\rVert^{-1}e^{\ell(\alpha-\varepsilon_{E})},\lVert D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{\ell}\rVert e^{-\ell(\alpha+\varepsilon_{E})},1\right\}.

This proves the proposition.
∎

For the next result, recall that a set is
basic
if it is compact,

-invariant, locally maximal, topologically transitive, and hyperbolic. The
\mathrm{u}
-index
of a basic set is the
\mathrm{u}
-index of any of its saddles. Note that for

or

of contracting
or

, respectively. The
\mathrm{u}
-index of a hyperbolic ergodic measure is, by definition, the number of its positive Lyapunov exponents counted with multiplicities. When
\mu
is equidistributed on a hyperbolic periodic orbit, then its
\mathrm{u}
-index coincides with the
\mathrm{u}
-index of the orbit. Given an
f
-invariant subset
\Lambda\subset M
, we denote by
\mathcal{M}_{\rm erg}(f|_{\Lambda})
the corresponding subset of ergodic measures.

Proposition
3.3
below is similar to
[

, where

is the entropy of a nonhyperbolic ergodic measure

is based on essentially two ingredients: the pre-skeleton property relative to

and

Proposition 3.3
.

Assume that
f\in\MB^{1}(M)

and

Remark 4.3

(The general shape of the graph of

\alpha_{\rm max}
as

\mathcal{P}_{>0}

\begin{split}\frac{h_{\mu}(f)}{1+K(f)\cdot\beta}&\leq\limsup_{i\to\infty}h_{\mu_{i}}(f)\\

\text{\small{(by Theorem \ref{theorem1})}}\quad&\leq\lim_{\alpha\nearrow 0}\mathcal{H}(\alpha)=\lim_{\alpha\nearrow 0}\mathcal{P}^{\ast}_{>0}(\alpha)=\mathfrak{h}(f).\end{split}

was arbitrary, we get

\frac{\mathcal{H}(\beta)}{1+K(f)\cdot\beta}=\frac{\mathcal{P}_{>0}^{\ast}(\beta)}{1+K(f)\cdot\beta}\leq\mathfrak{h}(f)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\frac{\mathcal{P}_{>0}^{\ast}(\beta)-\mathfrak{h}(f)}{\beta}\leq K(f)\mathfrak{h}(f).

Taking limits
\beta\searrow 0
, implies that the right hand-side derivative satisfies
D_{R}\mathcal{P}_{>0}^{\ast}(0)\leq K(f)\mathfrak{h}(f)
. Hence, the assertion about the slope zero follows from classical properties of the Legendre-Fenchel transform.

Note that taking
\mu
in (
4.6
) satisfying
h_{\mu}(f)>0
, one immediately gets that
\mathfrak{h}(f)\geq\mathcal{H}(0)>0
.

At the current state of the art, it is unknown if
\mathcal{H}(\alpha_{\rm min})
and
\mathcal{H}(\alpha_{\rm max})
are positive or zero or even equal to
h_{\rm top}(f)
; this type of question is studied in the context of
ergodic optimization
(see, for example,
[
J19
]
).

4.1.
General properties of the restricted pressure

We recall some general properties of the restricted variational pressure and its Legendre-Fenchel transform (see
[
DGR19
, Section 5]
for proofs and further properties, here we keep the numeration in
[
DGR19
]
).

Given
\mathcal{N}\subset\mathcal{M}_{\rm erg}(f)
, let

(4.7)

\varphi^{\mathrm{c}}_{\mathcal{N}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\Big\{\int\varphi^{\mathrm{c}}\,d\mu\colon\mu\in\mathcal{N}\Big\},\quad\underline{\varphi}^{\mathrm{c}}_{\mathcal{N}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\inf\varphi^{\mathrm{c}}_{\mathcal{N}},\quad\overline{\varphi}^{\mathrm{c}}_{\mathcal{N}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\sup\varphi^{\mathrm{c}}_{\mathcal{N}}.

We have the following properties:

(P1)

For every
\mu\in\mathcal{N}
, the function
\mathcal{P}_{\{\mu\}}
is affine and satisfies
\mathcal{P}_{\{\mu\}}\leq\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{N}}
.

(P2)

\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{N}^{\prime}}\leq\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{N}}
for every
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{N}
.

(P4)

The function
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{N}}
is uniformly Lipschitz continuous.

(P5)

The function
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{N}}
is convex.

(E1)

The function
\mathcal{P}^{\ast}_{\mathcal{N}}
is concave (and hence continuous). Hence, it is differentiable at all but at most countably many
\alpha
and the left and right derivatives are defined for all
\alpha\in D(\mathcal{P}^{\ast}_{\mathcal{N}})
.

(E2)

D(\mathcal{P}^{\ast}_{\mathcal{N}})
is an interval and
D(\mathcal{P}^{\ast}_{\mathcal{N}})\supset(\underline{\varphi}^{\mathrm{c}}_{\mathcal{N}},\overline{\varphi}^{\mathrm{c}}_{\mathcal{N}})
.

(E4)

It holds

\max_{\alpha\in D(\mathcal{P}^{\ast}_{\mathcal{N}})}\mathcal{P}^{\ast}_{\mathcal{N}}(\alpha)=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{N}}(0).

(E5)

For every
\alpha\in D(\mathcal{P}^{\ast}_{\mathcal{N}})
,

\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{N}}(\alpha)\leq\mathcal{P}^{\ast}_{\mathcal{N}}(\alpha).

When in (E5) we have equality, then this is also called a
variational principle restricted to
\mathcal{N}
or simply
restricted variational principle
. For that, in general, some further properties are required, see Section
4.4
.

4.2.
Intersection and homoclinic classes

Homoclinic relations between saddles play a pivotal role in this paper. Let us recall some key facts. Denote by
\Per_{<0}(f)
and
\Per_{>0}(f)
the sets of all saddles of
\mathrm{u}
-index
d^{\mathrm{uu}}
and
d^{\mathrm{uu}}+1
, respectively.
Two saddles of the same
\mathrm{u}
-index are
homoclinically related
if the unstable manifold of one saddle has non-empty transverse intersections with the stable manifold of the other saddle, and vice versa. Note that this defines an equivalence relation on the sets
\Per_{<0}(f)
and
\Per_{>0}(f)
. We call
intersection classes
the equivalence classes for this relation.
The
homoclinic class
of a saddle
P
is the closure of its intersection class, denoted by
H(P,f)
. Note that if
P,Q
are homoclinically related saddles, then
H(P,f)=H(Q,f)
. Note that
H(P,f)
may contain saddles whose
\mathrm{u}
-index is different from the one of
P
. Indeed, we see below that this feature occurs for
f\in\BM^{1}(M)
, see Remark
4.4
.

Remark 4.4

(Homoclinically related saddles)
.

Given
f\in\BM^{1}(M)
, any pair of saddles with the same
\mathrm{u}
-index are homoclinically related. Furthermore,
\Per_{<0}(f)
and
\Per_{>0}(f)
are intersection classes and satisfy

\overline{\Per_{<0}(f)}=\overline{\Per_{>0}(f)}=M.

Remark
4.4
follows from the fact that for
f\in\BM^{1}(M)
, the strong stable manifold of a saddle of
\mathrm{u}
-index
d^{\mathrm{uu}}+1
is its stable manifold, the strong unstable manifold of a saddle of
\mathrm{u}
-index
d^{\mathrm{uu}}
is its unstable manifold, and the strong foliations
\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{ss}}
and
\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{uu}}
both are, by hypothesis, minimal.

4.3.
Ergodic approximation property

We now resume the ideas in Section
3
. The goal is to “gradually approximate” the restricted pressure functions (and the corresponding Legendre-Fenchel transforms).

Definition 4.5
.

Given
f\in\PH^{1}_{\mathrm{c}=1}(M)
, a compact
f
-invariant set
\Lambda\subset M
has the
\mathrm{u}
-index
d
-ergodic approximation property
or simply

-ergodic approximation property
if the following properties hold:

(1)

Every pair of saddles of
\mathrm{u}
-index
d
in
\Lambda
are homoclinically related.

(2)

For every hyperbolic measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\rm erg}(f|_{\Lambda})
of
\mathrm{u}
-index
d
and every

there is a basic set
\Xi
of
\mathrm{u}
-index
d

(i)

\Xi\subset\Lambda
,

(ii)

\Xi
is contained in the
\varepsilon
-neighborhood of the support of
\mu
,

(iii)

h_{\rm top}(f,\Xi)\geq h_{\mu}(f)-\varepsilon
,

(iv)

\lvert\chi^{\mathrm{c}}(\mu^{\prime})-\chi^{\mathrm{c}}(\mu)\rvert\leq\varepsilon\text{ for every }\mu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{\rm erg}(f|_{\Xi})
.

For maps in
\PH^{1}_{\mathrm{c}=1}(M)
, the existence of “ergodically approximating basic sets”, in the sense of satisfying properties (ii)–(iv), for hyperbolic ergodic measures was shown in
[
G16
]
(see also
[

, Proposition 1.4]
and compare
[

, Lemma 6.6]

The following result is the main step towards the proof of Theorem
4.2
.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let

-invariant set having the

Lemma 4.7

&=\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{N}\colon\chi^{\mathrm{c}}(\mu)=\alpha_{\rm min}}h_{\mu}(f)=\mathcal{H}(\alpha_{\rm min})\geq 0>-\infty.\end{split}

.

To consider

,

Thus, for every

, it follows

Thus, we get

\begin{split}\inf_{r\geq 1/|\alpha_{\rm min}|}\mathcal{P}_{<0}(r)&=\inf_{r\geq 1/|\alpha_{\rm min}|}\,\,\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{N}}\big(h_{\mu}(f)+r\chi^{\mathrm{c}}(\mu)\big)\geq-1.\end{split}

On the other hand,

\inf_{q\leq 0}\mathcal{P}_{<0}(q)\geq\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{N}}h_{\mu}(f)\geq 0\quad\text{ and }\quad\inf_{0\leq q\leq 1/|\alpha_{\rm min}|}\mathcal{P}_{<0}(q)\geq\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{N}}h_{\mu}(f)-1>-\infty.

Hence, one concludes that

∎

5.
Weak integrability and center distortion

In this section, we introduce two objects (disks and strips) that will be used when introducing blender-horseshoes in Section
6
. We also prove a distortion result along the center direction.
Throughout this section, we assume that
f\in\PH_{\mathrm{c}=1}^{1}(M)
and consider its partially hyperbolic splitting

E^{\mathrm{ss}}\oplus E^{\mathrm{c}}\oplus E^{\mathrm{uu}}
. Let
E^{\mathrm{cs}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}E^{\mathrm{ss}}\oplus E^{\mathrm{c}}
and
E^{\mathrm{cu}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}E^{\mathrm{c}}\oplus E^{\mathrm{uu}}
. Recall that the strong stable and strong unstable bundles give rise to the strong stable and strong unstable foliations
\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{ss}}
and
\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{uu}}
, respectively. Given
r>0
and
\star\in\{\mathrm{ss},\mathrm{uu}\}
, denote by
\mathcal{W}^{\star}(x,r)
the ball centered at
x
and of radius
r
(relative to the induced distance on
\mathcal{W}^{\star}(x)
).

The case for the center bundle is delicate. As
E^{\mathrm{c}}
is one-dimensional, and hence defines a continuous vector field, by Peano’s theorem, at each point, there are
C^{1}
-curves tangent to
E^{\mathrm{c}}
.
Note that these center curves
a priori
do not form a foliation, see for instance
[
BGHP20
]
. The cases of the (higher-dimensional) bundles
E^{\mathrm{cu}}
and
E^{\mathrm{cs}}
are even more delicate. However, we have the following.

Remark 5.1

(Weak integrability)
.

Assume
f\in\PH^{1}_{\mathrm{c}=1}(M)
. By
[
BBI04
]
, the bundles
E^{\mathrm{cu}}
and
E^{\mathrm{cs}}
are
weakly integrable
in the sense that at each point
x\in M
there exist two immersed complete
C^{1}
sub-manifolds
\mathcal{W}_{\rm loc}^{\mathrm{cu}}(x)
and
\mathcal{W}_{\rm loc}^{\mathrm{cs}}(x)
that contain
x
and are everywhere tangent to
E^{\mathrm{cu}}
and
E^{\mathrm{cs}}
, respectively. Moreover, there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for every
C^{1}
-curve
\gamma
tangent to
E^{\mathrm{c}}
and with length at most
\varepsilon_{0}
, the sets

(5.1)

\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{uu}}(\gamma,\varepsilon_{0})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\bigcup_{z\in\gamma}\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{uu}}(z,\varepsilon_{0})\quad\textrm{~and~}\quad\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{ss}}(\gamma,\varepsilon_{0})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\bigcup_{z\in\gamma}\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{ss}}(z,\varepsilon_{0})

are
C^{1}
-submanifolds tangent to
E^{\mathrm{cu}}
and
E^{\mathrm{cs}}
, respectively (
[
BBI04
, proof of Proposition 3.4]
). In particular, for every basic set of contracting type
\Lambda
, there is
r>0
such that for every

x
and of radius

is contained in the stable manifold of

of inner radius
r
, denoted as
\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{s}}(x,r)
. Analogously for basic sets of expanding type.

The objects in (
5.1
) are particular cases of the ones in the following definition.

Definition 5.2

(Disks)
.

Given
\star\in\{\mathrm{ss},\mathrm{cs},\mathrm{c},\mathrm{cu},\mathrm{uu}\}
, a
\star

\Delta
is a (connected)

-submanifold tangent to

of

\Delta
contains a ball of radius
\delta
(in the corresponding metric of

.

Definition 5.3

(Strips and complete center curves)
.

A
\mathrm{u}
-strip

is a
\mathrm{cu}
-disk that is foliated by
\mathrm{uu}
-disks. More precisely, there is a center curve
\gamma
such that

where
\Delta^{\mathrm{uu}}(x)
is a
\mathrm{uu}
-disk containing

\mathrm{uu}

-boundary

The next lemma is an immediate consequence of the uniform expansion/contraction along un-/stable foliations (see, for example,
[

, Lemma 2.4]
).

Lemma 5.4

(Center distortion control-time)
.

For every
\delta>0
and
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\ell^{\flat}=\ell^{\flat}(\delta,\varepsilon)\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
i\geq\ell^{\flat}
, every
x\in M
and every
y\in f^{-i}(\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{uu}}(f^{i}(x),\delta))
, one has

\big|\log\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{i}(x)\|-\log\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{i}(y)\|\big|<i\,\varepsilon.

Proof.

Given
\varepsilon>0
, by the uniform continuity of
\log\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f\|
, there exists
\eta>0
such that

(5.2)

\big|\log\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f(x)\|-\log\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f(y)\|\big|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2},\quad\text{for every $x,y\in M$ with $d(x,y)<\eta$.}

As
f^{-1}
is uniformly contracting along the foliation
\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{uu}}
, there exists
n_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

d(f^{-j}(x),f^{-j}(y))<\eta,\quad\textrm{for every $x\in M$, $y\in\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{uu}}(x,\delta)$, and $j>n_{1}$.}

Take
\ell^{\flat}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(5.3)

\frac{2n_{1}}{\ell^{\flat}}\max|\log\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f\||<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Then for every

and

,

, one has

for every

, and thus

\begin{split}\Big|\log\frac{\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{i}(x)\|}{\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{i}(y)\|}\Big|&=\Big|\sum_{j=0}^{i-n_{1}-1}\log\frac{\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f(f^{j}(x))\|}{\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f(f^{j}(y))\|}+\sum_{j=i-n_{1}}^{i-1}\log\frac{\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f(f^{j}(x))\|}{\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f(f^{j}(y))\|}\Big|\\
\text{\small{(by \eqref{more1})}}\quad&\leq(i-n_{1})\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+2n_{1}\max|\log\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f\||\\
\text{\small{(by \eqref{more2})}}\quad&<i\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+i\frac{2n_{1}}{\ell^{\flat}}\max|\log\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f\||<i\varepsilon,\end{split}

proving the lemma.
∎

8.
Approximation by basic sets

In this section, we start by stating some finer properties of basic sets. In particular, we state an approximation result of hyperbolic measures by basic sets (see Lemma
8.3
). We also formalize a concatenation procedure for separated orbit segments which will be a key step to “produce entropy” in our constructions (see Proposition

To start, with a slight abuse of notation, given two subsets

For

-Bowen ball centered at

B_{n}(x,\varepsilon)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\big\{y\in X\colon d_{n}(x,y)<\varepsilon\big\},\quad\text{ where }\quad d_{n}(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\max_{k=0,\ldots,n-1}d(f^{k}(x),f^{k}(y)).

Remark 8.1

(Constant of entropy expansiveness)

For a partially hyperbolic diffeomorphism

The following result we state without proof.

Let

The following one shows the importance of basic sets to study hyperbolic measures.

Lemma 8.3
.

Let

\varepsilon>0

K^{-1}\cdot e^{n(\chi^{\mathrm{c}}(\nu)-\varepsilon)}\leq\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{n}(y)\|\leq K\cdot e^{n(\chi^{\mathrm{c}}(\nu)+\varepsilon)}.

We now state the main result in this section.

Proposition 8.4
.

Let
f\in\PH^{1}_{\mathrm{c}=1}(M)
and
\delta_{0}>0
be as in Remark
8.1
. Let
\Lambda
be a basic set of contracting type of

Then there exists
\ell^{\sharp}=\ell^{\sharp}(\Lambda)\in\mathbb{N}
with the following properties.
For every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
b^{\sharp}=b^{\sharp}(\Lambda,\varepsilon)>a^{\sharp}
such that for every

, there exist an
(n,\delta_{0})
-separated subset
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}(n)\subset\Lambda
and an integer
\ell\in\{0,\ldots,\ell^{\sharp}-1\}
such that

(a)

\card(\mathcal{S})\geq e^{n(h_{\rm top}(f,\Lambda)-\varepsilon)},

(b)

for every
N\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists an injective map

\Psi=\Psi^{n,N}\colon\mathcal{S}^{N}\to\Lambda

having the following properties: given two points

\mathfrak{X}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}),\quad\mathfrak{X}^{\prime}=(x_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{N}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{S}^{N},

let

be the smallest integer such that
x_{i_{0}}\neq x_{i_{0}}^{\prime}

(b1)

for every

and
(i-1)(n+\ell)\leq j<(i-1)(n+\ell)+n

(b2)

there exist
(i_{0}-1)(n+\ell)+a^{\sharp}<j_{0}<(i_{0}-1)(n+\ell)+n

(c)

for every pair of different points
x,y\in\Image(\Psi)
and

, one has

\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{s}}(x,r)\cap\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{s}}(y,r)=\emptyset.

Proof.

We collect two classical facts without proof.

Claim 8.5

(Spectral decomposition)
.

There exist
p^{\sharp}\in\mathbb{N}
and a decomposition

\Lambda=\widehat{\Lambda}\cup f(\widehat{\Lambda})\cup\ldots\cup f^{p^{\sharp}-1}(\widehat{\Lambda})

f^{p^{\sharp}}
-invariant compact sets, and for each

is topologically mixing.

The following result, that we state without proof (see, for example,
[
KH95
]
), is a consequence of the fact that

has the specification property for each
i
.

Claim 8.6

(Specification property)
.

There exists
\ell_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
so that every finite family of orbit segments which start and end in
\widehat{\Lambda}
are
\delta_{0}/10
-shadowed by a true orbit segment in
\Lambda
with spacing time
\ell_{1}
. Analogous result holds for orbit segments which start and end in
f^{i}(\widehat{\Lambda})
,

.

Let
a^{\sharp}=a^{\sharp}(\Lambda)
as in Lemma
8.2
. Let

(8.4)

\ell^{\sharp}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\ell_{1}+p^{\sharp}.

The following claim provides the set
\mathcal{S}(n)
and proves property (a) in the proposition.

Claim 8.7

(The set
\mathcal{S}(n)
)
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
b^{\sharp}>a^{\sharp}
such that for every
n>b^{\sharp}
, there exists a
(n,\delta_{0})
-separated set

which satisfies

•

•

\mathcal{S}(n)\subset f^{j}(\widehat{\Lambda})

is contained in

for some point

Proof.

Fix some
(a^{\sharp},\delta_{0}/4)
-separated set of maximal cardinality

. The fact that
\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}\subset\Lambda
is an
(a^{\sharp},\delta_{0}/4)
-separated set of maximal cardinality, together with Claim

\Lambda=\bigcup_{i=0}^{p^{\sharp}-1}f^{i}(\widehat{\Lambda})\subset\bigcup_{y\in\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}}B_{a^{\sharp}}(y,\delta_{0}/4).

By the compactness of

, the set

As

is
\delta_{0}
-entropy expansive, by

Hence, it follows straightforwardly from (

such that for every

Let

Consider now

-separated set with maximal cardinality

Without loss of generality, we can assume that in Claim

. The following is a consequence of Claim

.

.

.

Recall our notation (

Lemma 9.14

(Separation of curves in
\mathcal{D}_{k}
)
.

For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
and distinct curves
\xi,\xi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}_{k}
there are numbers

and

,

(9.18)

1\leq N\leq N_{k}\quad\text{ and }\quad(N-1)(n_{k}+\ell_{k})+a^{\sharp}_{k}<j<(N-1)(n_{k}+\ell_{k})+n_{k},

such that for every

and

d(f^{j}(x),f^{j}(x^{\prime}))>4\delta_{0}/5-2r_{k}.

Moreover, the curves
\xi,\xi^{\prime}
are
(T_{k},4\delta_{0}/5-2r_{k})
-separated.

Proof.

Consider the curves
\xi=\xi(x_{k},r_{k})
and
\xi^{\prime}=\xi(x_{k}^{\prime},r_{k})
in
\mathcal{D}_{k}
, where
x_{k},x_{k}^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Im}(\Psi_{k})
. By item (b2) in Proposition
8.4
, there exist
N
and
j
as in (
9.18
) such that

d(f^{j}(x_{k}),f^{j}(x_{k}^{\prime}))>4\delta_{0}/5.

Let
x\in\xi
and
x^{\prime}\in\xi^{\prime}
. Note that (
9.18
) implies
j>a^{\sharp}_{k}
and hence, as we assume

, by Lemma

item (2), one has

d(f^{j}(x),f^{j}x_{k}))<r_{k}\quad\text{~and~}\quad d(f^{j}(x^{\prime}),f^{j}(x_{k}^{\prime}))<r_{k}.

Therefore, one has

(9.19)

\begin{split}d(f^{j}(x),f^{j}(x^{\prime}))&\geq d(f^{j}(x_{k}),f^{j}(x_{k}^{\prime}))-d(f^{j}(x),f^{j}(x_{k}))-d(f^{j}(x^{\prime}),f^{j}(x_{k}^{\prime}))\\
&>4\delta_{0}/5-2r_{k},\end{split}

that is,
\xi,\xi^{\prime}
are
(j,4\delta_{0}/5-2r_{k})
-separated.

Finally, recall that

and hence

implies

. This together with (

) implies that
\xi,\xi^{\prime}
are
(T_{k},4\delta_{0}/5-2r_{k})
-separated. This proves the lemma.
∎

Lemma 9.15

(Separation of curves in
\mathcal{E}_{k}
)
.

For every
k\geq 1
, the curves in
\mathcal{E}_{k}
are pairwise

\big(t_{k-1}+T_{k},4\delta_{0}/5-\sum_{j=0}^{k-2}\delta/2^{j}-2\sum_{j=1}^{k}r_{j}\big)\text{-separated},

where for

(t_{k-1}+T_{k},3\delta_{0}/5)

By definition,
\mathcal{E}_{1}=\mathcal{D}_{1}

.

Assume that the assertion holds for
\mathcal{E}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{E}_{k}

and
\gamma_{k+1}^{\prime}=\phi_{k}(\gamma_{k}^{\prime},\xi_{k+1}^{\prime})

\mathcal{E}_{k+1}

Case

9.14

Throughout this section, we assume the hypotheses of Theorem

For each

\mu_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\frac{1}{\card\mathcal{E}_{k}}\sum_{\gamma_{k}\in\mathcal{E}_{k}}\Leb_{\gamma_{k}},

Proposition 10.1
.

The sequence

10.1.
Convergence of the sequence of measures
(\mu_{k})_{k}

To show convergence, it suffices to prove that for any continuous function
\varphi\colon M\to\mathbb{R}
, the limit
\lim_{k\to\infty}\int\varphi\,d\mu_{k}
exists. In what follows, we fix
\varphi
and prove this assertion.

By item (5) in Proposition
9.3
, each curve
\gamma_{k}\in\mathcal{E}_{k}
is
(\mathrm{c},\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{uu}}(\xi,\delta))
-complete for some
\xi\in\mathcal{D}_{1}
. In particular, all the curves in
\bigcup_{k}\mathcal{E}_{k}
have lengths uniformly bounded away from zero and also from above. By the uniform continuity of the center and the strong unstable bundles, and the uniform continuity of
\varphi
, for every
\omega>0
, there exists
\eta>0
such that for every pair of
(\mathrm{c},\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{uu}}(\xi,\delta))
-complete curves
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}
(for some
\xi\in\mathcal{D}_{1}
) with
\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{uu}}(\gamma,\eta)\cap\gamma^{\prime}\neq\emptyset

(10.3)

\Big|\int\varphi\,d\Leb_{\gamma}-\int\varphi\,d\Leb_{\gamma^{\prime}}\Big|<\omega.

Given integers
\ell>k
, by Corollary
9.13
, for any curve
\gamma\in\mathcal{E}_{\ell}
, there exists a
\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}_{k}
such that
\gamma
is
(\mathrm{c},\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{uu}}(\gamma^{\prime},\delta/2^{k-1}))
-complete.
On the other hand, inductively applying Lemma
9.16
, it follows that every curve
\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}_{k}
gives rise to exactly
(\prod_{j=k+1}^{\ell}\card\mathcal{D}_{j})
distinct curves in
\mathcal{E}_{\ell}\cap\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{uu}}(\gamma^{\prime},\delta/2^{k-1})
.

Consider
k\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large such that
\delta/2^{k-1}<\eta
. Thus, for every
\ell>k
, by the choice of
\eta
, one has

\begin{split}&\Big|\int\varphi\,d\mu_{\ell}-\int\varphi\,d\mu_{k}\Big|\\
&=\Big|\frac{1}{\card\mathcal{E}_{\ell}}\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{E}_{\ell}}\int\varphi\,d\Leb_{\gamma}-\frac{1}{\card\mathcal{E}_{k}}\sum_{\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}_{k}}\int\varphi\,d\Leb_{\gamma^{\prime}}\Big|\\
&\leq\frac{1}{\card\mathcal{E}_{k}}\sum_{\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}_{k}}\frac{\card\mathcal{E}_{k}}{\card\mathcal{E}_{\ell}}\left(\sum_{\textrm{$\gamma$ is $(\ell-k)$ successor of $\gamma^{\prime}$}}\Big|\int\varphi\,d\Leb_{\gamma}-\int\varphi\,d\Leb_{\gamma^{\prime}}\Big|\right)\\
\text{\small{(by \eqref{refertoothiis})}}\quad&\leq\omega.\end{split}

This proves that the limit
\mu\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\lim_{k}\mu_{k}
indeed exists.

Recall that
(L_{k})_{k}
is a nested sequence of compact sets and
\mu_{k}(L_{k})=1
, thus for any
\ell>k

\mu_{\ell}(L_{k})=1
. Taking the limit

. As

was arbitrary, this gives
\mu(L)=1
. This proves the first part of Proposition
10.1

∎

Hence, by Claim

and applying (

) to

This finishes the proof for
\ell=k+1

inductively, each curve

Claim 10.6
.

It holds

\frac{1}{n}\log\big(\card\mathcal{E}_{k}(\card\mathcal{S}_{k+1})^{N}\big)>\mathfrak{h}(f)-\theta.

Proof.

It follows from Corollary

and Remark

By our choice of

in (

h_{k}-\varepsilon_{k}\geq\mathfrak{h}(f)-\varepsilon^{\prime},\quad t_{k_{0}-1}\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}t_{k_{0}},\quad\text{ and }\quad(n_{k+1}+\ell_{k+1})\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}t_{k}.

Hence, one has the following estimation

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{n}\log\big(\card\mathcal{E}_{k}(\card\mathcal{S}_{k+1})^{N}\big)=\frac{1}{n}\log\big(\prod_{i=1}^{k}\big(\card\mathcal{S}_{i}\big)^{N_{i}}\cdot(\card\mathcal{S}_{k+1})^{N}\big)\\
\text{\small(by \eqref{eqcSkcardinality})}&\geq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\big(N_{i}n_{i}(h_{i}-\varepsilon_{i})+Nn_{k+1}(h_{k+1}-\varepsilon_{k+1})\big)\\
&\geq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=k_{0}}^{k}\big(N_{i}n_{i}(h_{i}-\varepsilon_{i})+Nn_{k+1}(h_{k+1}-\varepsilon_{k+1})\big)\\
\text{\small{(by~\eqref{eq:choice-of-kepsilon})}}\quad&\geq\frac{1-\varepsilon^{\prime}}{n}\sum_{i=k_{0}}^{k}\bigg(\big(N_{i}(n_{i}+\ell_{i})+m_{i}\big)\cdot(h_{i}-\varepsilon_{i})+N(n_{k+1}+\ell_{k+1})(h_{k+1}-\varepsilon_{k+1})\bigg)\\
\text{\small{(by~\eqref{eq:choice-of-kepsilon})}}\quad&\geq\frac{1-\varepsilon^{\prime}}{n}(\mathfrak{h}(f)-\varepsilon^{\prime})\sum_{i=k_{0}}^{k}\bigg(\big(N_{i}(n_{i}+\ell_{i})+m_{i}\big)+N(n_{k+1}+\ell_{k+1})\bigg)\\
&=(\mathfrak{h}(f)-\varepsilon^{\prime})(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\frac{1}{n}\big(t_{k}-t_{k_{0}-1}+N(n_{k+1}+\ell_{k+1})\big)\\
\end{split}

By (

), we get

\begin{split}\frac{1}{n}\log\big(\card\mathcal{E}_{k}(\card\mathcal{S}_{k+1})^{N}\big)&\geq(\mathfrak{h}(f)-\varepsilon^{\prime})(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\frac{1}{n}\big(n-t_{k_{0}-1}-(n_{k+1}+\ell_{k+1})\big)\\
\text{\small(by \eqref{eqsomethingnewer})}\quad&\geq(\mathfrak{h}(f)-\varepsilon^{\prime})(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\frac{1}{n}\big(n-\varepsilon^{\prime}t_{k_{0}}-\varepsilon^{\prime}t_{k}\big)\\
\text{\small{(by \eqref{eq:choice-of-j0}, \eqref{eq:choice-of-eprime})}}\,&>(\mathfrak{h}(f)-\varepsilon^{\prime})(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})(1-2\varepsilon^{\prime})>\mathfrak{h}(f)-\theta.\end{split}

This proves the claim.
∎

By Lemma

.

9.1
: Forward exponents

Throughout this section, we assume the hypotheses of Theorem
9.1
.
Consider
L=L\big(\delta,(t_{k})_{k},(T_{k})_{k},(\mathcal{E}_{k})_{k}\big)
as in (

,
(t_{k})_{k}

The following proposition is item (
3
) in Theorem

.

Proposition 11.1
.

For every
x\in L
,
\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{n}(x)\|=0
and this convergence is uniform on
L.

Proof.

Recall the quantifiers in Section
9.2.1
. Recall
C_{\rm max}
in (
9.5
) and note that
|\chi_{i}|\leq C_{\rm max}/2
for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
.
By item (ii) in Section
9.2.1
,
\chi_{i}
and
\varepsilon_{i}
are chosen monotonically tending to zero. Hence there exists
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(11.1)

|\chi_{i}|+6\varepsilon_{i}<\varepsilon,\quad\textrm{~ for every $i\geq k_{0}$.}

Recall that (
9.7
) and (
9.15
) imply that for all
i\in\mathbb{N}

(11.2)

m_{i}C_{\rm max}<n_{i}\varepsilon_{i}<T_{i}\varepsilon_{i}<t_{i}\varepsilon_{i}\quad\text{ and }\quad t_{i}C_{\rm max}<t_{i+1}\varepsilon_{i+1}.

Fix
n>t_{k_{0}+1}
. As in (
10.6
), we consider the associated index
k\geq k_{0}+1
and
N\in\{0,\ldots,N_{k+1}\}
such that

(11.3)

\begin{split}&t_{k}\leq n<t_{k+1},\\
&t_{k}+N(n_{k+1}+\ell_{k+1})\leq n<t_{k}+(N+1)(n_{k+1}+\ell_{k+1}).\end{split}

From now on,
k
and
N
are fixed with the above properties.

Observe that

(11.4)

\begin{split}\log\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{n}(x)\|=\log\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{t_{k}}(x)\|+\log\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{n-t_{k}}(f^{t_{k}}(x))\|.\end{split}

For the next two lemmas, recall the distortion control-time
\ell_{k}^{\flat}=(\delta,\varepsilon_{k})
in Lemma
5.4
.

Lemma 11.2
.

For every
x\in L
, one has

\big|\log\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{t_{k}}(x)\|\big|<t_{k}(|\chi_{k_{0}}|+5\varepsilon_{k_{0}}).

Proof.

Fix
x\in L
. Recalling the definition of
t_{k}
in (
9.13
),

t_{k}=t_{k_{0}-1}+\sum_{j=k_{0}}^{k}(T_{j}+m_{j}),

let us first write

(11.5)

\begin{split}\log\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{t_{k}}(x)\|&=\log\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{t_{k_{0}-1}}(x)\|\\
&+\sum_{j=k_{0}}^{k}\Big(\log\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{T_{j}}(f^{t_{j-1}}(x))\|+\log\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{m_{j}}(f^{t_{j}-m_{j}}(x))\|\Big).\end{split}

As a first step, for every
k_{0}\leq j\leq k+1
, we choose points
y_{j}\in\Lambda_{j}
that have controlled finite-time Lyapunov exponents approximating the one of
x
.
By the definition of
L
in (
9.1
), for every
k_{0}\leq j\leq k+1
, there exists a (unique) center curve
\gamma_{j}\in\mathcal{E}_{j}
such that

x\in B^{\mathrm{cu}}_{t_{j-1}+T_{j}}\big(\gamma_{j},\delta/2^{j-1}\big),

and thus there exists a point
y_{j}\in\gamma_{j}
such that

(11.6)

~f^{i}(x)\in\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{uu}}\big(f^{i}(y_{j}),\delta/2^{j-1}\big)\subset\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{uu}}\big(f^{i}(\gamma_{j}),\delta/2^{j-1}\big)\quad\textrm{~~for $t_{j-1}\leq i\leq t_{j-1}+T_{j}-1$}.

On the other hand, by item (3) in Proposition
9.3
, there exists a curve
\xi_{j}\in\mathcal{D}_{j}
such that
f^{t_{j-1}}(\gamma_{j})\subset\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{ss}}(\xi_{j},\delta/2^{j-1})
and in particular,

d(f^{i}(\gamma_{j}),f^{i}(\xi_{j}))\leq\delta/2^{j-1}\quad\textrm{~~for $t_{j-1}\leq i\leq t_{j-1}+T_{j}-1$}.

By the definition of
\mathcal{D}_{j}
(see Lemma
9.7
) and item (iv) in Section
9.2.1
for
\Lambda_{j}
and the point
f^{t_{j-1}}(y_{j})\in\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{ss}}(\xi_{j},\delta/2^{j-1})
, one has

(11.7)

\big|\log\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{T_{j}}(f^{t_{j-1}}(y_{j}))\|-T_{j}\chi_{j}\big|\leq\log K_{j}+T_{j}\varepsilon_{j}.

By (
9.6
), we have
\ell_{j}^{\flat}\leq n_{j}<T_{j}
and hence, together with (
11.6
), we can apply Lemma
5.4
to the points
f^{t_{j-1}}(x)
and
f^{t_{j-1}}(y_{j})
, and get

(11.8)

\begin{split}&\big|\log\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{T_{j}}(f^{t_{j-1}}(x))\|-T_{j}\chi_{j}\big|\\
&\leq\big|\log\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{T_{j}}(f^{t_{j-1}}(x))\|-\log\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{T_{j}}(f^{t_{j-1}}(y_{j}))\|\big|\\
&\hskip 8.53581pt+\big|\log\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{T_{j}}(f^{t_{j-1}}(y_{j}))\|-T_{j}\chi_{j}\big|\\
\text{\small{(by Lemma~\ref{lemsomecount} and \eqref{thiseq})}}\quad&\leq T_{j}\varepsilon_{j}+\big(T_{j}\varepsilon_{j}+\log K_{j}\big)\\
&=2T_{j}\varepsilon_{j}+\log K_{j}.\end{split}

In particular, recalling that
T_{j}=N_{j}(n_{j}+\ell_{j})
, we get

(11.9)

\begin{split}\big|\frac{1}{T_{j}}\log\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{T_{j}}(f^{t_{j-1}}(x))\|-\chi_{j}\big|&\leq 2\varepsilon_{j}+\frac{\log K_{j}}{T_{j}}=2\varepsilon_{j}+\frac{\log K_{j}}{N_{j}(n_{j}+\ell_{j})}\\
\text{\small{(by \eqref{eqexp1bisbis})}}\quad&\leq 3\varepsilon_{j}.\end{split}

Hence, recalling (
11.5
) and putting all steps together, one gets

\begin{split}\big|\log\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{t_{k}}(x)\|\big|&=\big|\log\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{t_{k_{0}-1}}(x)\|\\
&+\sum_{j=k_{0}}^{k}\big(\log\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{T_{j}}(f^{t_{j-1}}(x))\|+\log\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{m_{j}}(f^{t_{j}-m_{j}}(x))\|\big)\big|\\
\text{\small{(by \eqref{defCmax} and \eqref{eqB})}}\quad&\leq t_{k_{0}-1}C_{\rm max}+\sum_{j=k_{0}}^{k}\big(T_{j}(|\chi_{j}|+3\varepsilon_{j})+m_{j}C_{\rm max}\big)\\
\text{\small{(by \eqref{useful})}}\quad&\leq t_{k_{0}}\varepsilon_{k_{0}}+\sum_{j=k_{0}}^{k}T_{j}(|\chi_{j}|+4\varepsilon_{j})\\
&\leq t_{k}(|\chi_{k_{0}}|+5\varepsilon_{k_{0}}),\end{split}

where the last inequality follows from
t_{k_{0}}+\sum_{j=k_{0}}^{k}T_{j}<t_{k}
, and
\varepsilon_{j}\leq\varepsilon_{k_{0}}
and
|\chi_{j}|\leq|\chi_{k_{0}}|
for every
j\geq k_{0}
(see the property (ii) in Section
9.2.1
). This proves the lemma.
∎

With Lemma
11.2
at hand, we are now ready to estimate
\log\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{n}(x)\|
in (
11.4
). There are three cases to consider, according to the position of

in (

).

where the last inequality follows from (

).

Case (2)

.

Recall again that
\varepsilon_{j}\searrow 0
monotonically and

due to (

Note that

. Hence, arguing as in (

) for

, we get

\big|\log\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{n-t_{k}}(f^{t_{k}}(x))\|\big|\leq(n-t_{k})(|\chi_{k+1}|+2\varepsilon_{k_{0}})+t_{k}\varepsilon_{k_{0}}<(n-t_{k})(|\chi_{k_{0}}|+2\varepsilon_{k_{0}})+t_{k}\varepsilon_{k_{0}}.

Combining with Lemma

and (

), one gets

\frac{1}{n}\log\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{n}(x)\|<\frac{1}{n}\big(t_{k}(|\chi_{k_{0}}|+5\varepsilon_{k_{0}})+(n-t_{k})(|\chi_{k_{0}}|+2\varepsilon_{k_{0}})+t_{k}\varepsilon_{k_{0}}\big)<|\chi_{k_{0}}|+6\varepsilon_{k_{0}}<\varepsilon.

Case (3)
t_{k}+T_{k+1}\leq n<t_{k+1}
.
Note that in this case Equation (
9.13
) implies

(11.10)

n-t_{k}-T_{k+1}<m_{k+1}.

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{n}\log\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{n-t_{k}}(f^{t_{k}}(x))\|\\
&=\frac{1}{n}\log\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{T_{k+1}}(f^{t_{k}}(x))\|+\frac{1}{n}\log\|D^{\mathrm{c}}f^{n-t_{k}-T_{k+1}}(f^{t_{k}+T_{k+1}}(x))\|.\end{split}

11.9
) for

\text{\small(by \eqref{eq:bound-LE-varepsilon})}\quad&\leq|\chi_{k_{0}}|+6\varepsilon_{k_{0}}<\varepsilon.\end{split}

To summarize, for every

, one has

which implies that the point

has zero center Lyapunov exponent and the convergence is uniform. This finishes the proof of the proposition.

Appendix A
Topological entropy

A.1.
Definition of topological entropy and a distribution principle

Let
(X,d)
be a compact metric space,
f\colon X\to X
be a continuous map, and
Y\subset X
. We recall the definition of topological entropy of
f
on
Y
. For that, we rely on
[
P97
]
.
We also introduce its capacitive versions
\underline{Ch}_{\rm top}
and
\overline{Ch}_{\rm top}
.

Recall the definition of a set of
(n,\varepsilon)
-separated points in (
3.1
) and the definition of a Bowen ball in (
8.1
).
Denote by
r_{n}(Y,\varepsilon)
the smallest cardinality of a family of sets
\{B_{n}(x_{i},\varepsilon)\}_{i}
whose union covers
Y
and denote by
s_{n}(Y,\varepsilon)
the maximum cardinality of a
(n,\varepsilon)
-separated set in
Y
. Recall that
r_{n}(Y,\varepsilon)\leq s_{n}(Y,\varepsilon)
. Given
h\geq 0
let

\begin{split}m_{\varepsilon,h}(Y)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\lim_{N\to\infty}\Big(\inf\Big\{\sum_{i}e^{-hn_{i}}\colon Y\subset\bigcup_{i}B_{n_{i}}(x_{i},\varepsilon),n_{i}\geq N\text{ for every }i\Big\}\Big),\\
\underline{Cm}_{\varepsilon,h}(Y)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\liminf_{n\to\infty}~e^{-nh}\cdot r_{n}(Y,\varepsilon),\quad\overline{Cm}_{\varepsilon,h}(Y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\limsup_{n\to\infty}e^{-nh}\cdot s_{n}(Y,\varepsilon).\end{split}

Fixing
\varepsilon
, this function jumps from
\infty
to
0
at a unique critical parameter. Let

(A.1)

\begin{split}h_{\rm top}(f,Y,\varepsilon)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\inf\big\{h\colon m_{\varepsilon,h}(Y)=0\big\},\\
\overline{Ch}_{\rm top}(f,Y,\varepsilon)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\inf\big\{h\colon\underline{Cm}_{\varepsilon,h}(Y)=0\big\},\quad\underline{Ch}_{\rm top}(f,Y,\varepsilon)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\inf\big\{h\colon\overline{Cm}_{\varepsilon,h}(Y)=0\big\}.\end{split}

Lemma A.1
.

For every
Y\subset X
,
h>0
, and
0<\varepsilon^{\prime}<\varepsilon
, it holds
m_{\varepsilon,h}(Y)\leq m_{\varepsilon^{\prime},h}(Y)
. Hence, in particular,
h_{\rm top}(f,Y,\varepsilon)\leq h_{\rm top}(f,Y,\varepsilon^{\prime})
.

Proof.

Note that if
Y\subset\bigcup_{i}B_{n_{i}}(x_{i},\varepsilon^{\prime})
, then also
Y\subset\bigcup_{i}B_{n_{i}}(x_{i},\varepsilon)
. Thus,

\inf\Big\{\sum_{i}e^{-hn_{i}}\colon Y\subset\bigcup_{i}B_{n_{i}}(x_{i},\varepsilon),n_{i}\geq N\Big\}\leq\inf\Big\{\sum_{i}e^{-hn_{i}}\colon Y\subset\bigcup_{i}B_{n_{i}}(x_{i},\varepsilon^{\prime}),n_{i}\geq N\Big\}.

Taking the limit
N\to\infty
, we get
m_{\varepsilon,h}(Y)\leq m_{\varepsilon^{\prime},h}(Y)
. This proves the lemma.
∎

Denote by
\widehat{h}_{\rm top}(f,Y)
the
topological entropy
of
f
on
Y
as defined in
[
B73
]
.

Lemma A.2

(
[
P97
, Chapter 4 Section 11]
)
.

For every
Y\subset X
, one has that

(1)

the following limit exists:

\begin{split}h_{\rm top}(f,Y)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}h_{\rm top}(f,Y,\varepsilon).,\\
\overline{Ch}_{\rm top}(f,Y)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\overline{Ch}_{\rm top}(f,Y,\varepsilon),\quad\underline{Ch}_{\rm top}(f,Y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\underline{Ch}_{\rm top}(f,Y,\varepsilon).\end{split}

(2)

\overline{Ch}_{\rm top}(f,Y)\geq\underline{Ch}_{\rm top}(f,Y)\geq\widehat{h}_{\rm top}(f,Y)=h_{\rm top}(f,Y)
.

Remark A.3
.

We recall some further properties which are relevant in our context:

(E1)

(monotonicity) if
Z\subset Y\subset X
, then
h_{\rm top}(f,Z)\leq h_{\rm top}(f,Y)
,

(E2)

(countable stability)
h_{\rm top}(f,\bigcup_{i}Y_{i})=\sup_{i}h_{\rm top}(f,Y_{i})
.

The following can be considered as an analogy with the classical
Mass Distribution Principle
to estimate Hausdorff dimension (see, for example,
[
P97
, Chapter 2]
). For completeness, we add its short proof.

Theorem A.4

(Entropy Distribution Principle,
[
TV03
, Theorem 3.6]
)
.

Let
f
be a continuous map on a metric space
X
and
Y\subset X
. Assume that there exist constants
h\geq 0
,
\delta>0
, and a Borel probability measure
\mu_{\delta}
such that

•

\mu_{\delta}(Y)>0
;

•

there is
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mu_{\delta}(B_{n}(x,\delta))\leq e^{-nh}
for every
n\geq n_{0}
and every Bowen ball
B_{n}(x,\delta)
intersecting the set
Y
.

Then
h_{\rm top}(f,Y,\delta)\geq h.

Proof.

By definition, it suffices to show that
\widehat{m}_{\delta,h}(Y)>0
. Consider any countable family of Bowen balls
\{B_{n_{i}}(x_{i},\delta)\}_{i}
,
n_{i}\geq n_{0}
, which covers
Y
. Up to removing some Bowen balls, one can assume that each Bowen ball
B_{n_{i}}(x_{i},\delta)
intersects
Y
.
Then

0<\mu_{\delta}(Y)\leq\mu_{\delta}\big(\bigcup_{i}B_{n_{i}}(x_{i},\delta)\big)\leq\sum_{i}e^{-n_{i}h}.

Thus,

m_{\delta,h}(Y)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\inf\Big\{\sum_{i}e^{-hn_{i}}\colon Y\subset\bigcup_{i}B_{n_{i}}(x_{i},\delta),n_{i}\geq N\text{ for every }i\Big\}\geq\mu_{\delta}(Y)>0.

This implies
h_{\rm top}(f,Y,\delta)\geq h.

∎

A.2.
“Stable shearing” preserves topological entropy

By definition, the concept of entropy only relies on the
forward
iterates of a set, and, indeed, one can show that for a partially hyperbolic diffeomorphism, if one “shears” a set along its strong stable leaves, the entropy is unchanged. Let us make this more precise. Given a subset
Y\subset M
and
\ell>0
, in analogy to our notation in (
5.1
), let

\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{ss}}(Y,\ell)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\bigcup_{Y\in Y}\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{ss}}(Y,\ell).

Theorem A.5
.

Let
f
be a
C^{1}
-partially hyperbolic diffeomorphism. Assume that
Y,Y^{\prime}\subset M
are two subsets and
\ell>0
such that
Y^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{ss}}(Y,\ell)
and
Y\subset\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{ss}}(Y^{\prime},\ell)
. Then

h_{\rm top}(f,Y)=h_{\rm top}(f,Y^{\prime}).

Proof.

As
Y^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{ss}}(Y,\ell)
and
Y\subset\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{ss}}(Y^{\prime},\ell)
, by monotonicity of entropy, it suffices to show that
h_{\rm top}(f,Y)\geq h_{\rm top}(f,\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{ss}}(Y,\ell))
.

By the uniform contraction of
f
along the strong stable bundle, for every
\varepsilon>0,
there is
k_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(A.2)

f^{k}(\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{ss}}(x,\ell))\subset\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{ss}}(f^{k}(x),{\varepsilon/2})\quad\text{ for every $x\in M$ and $k\geq k_{\varepsilon}$.}

Given any
\varepsilon>0
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
, fix now a countable collection of Bowen balls
\{B_{n_{i}}(x_{i},\varepsilon)\}
whose union covers
Y
and which satisfy
n_{i}\geq N+k_{\varepsilon}
for each
i
.

Claim A.6
.

f^{k_{\varepsilon}}(\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{ss}}(Y,\ell))\subset\bigcup_{i}B_{n_{i}-k_{\varepsilon}}\big(f^{k_{\varepsilon}}(x_{i}),3\varepsilon/2\big)
.

Proof.

For every
x\in\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{ss}}(Y,\ell)
, there exists
y\in Y
such that
x\in\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{ss}}(y,\ell)
. Since there exists
x_{i}
such that
y\in B_{n_{i}}(x_{i},\varepsilon)
, thus for every
n_{i}-k_{\varepsilon}>j\geq 0
, one has that

d(f^{j+k_{\varepsilon}}(x),f^{j+k_{\varepsilon}}(x_{i}))\leq d(f^{j+k_{\varepsilon}}(x),f^{j+k_{\varepsilon}}(y))+d(f^{j+k_{\varepsilon}}(y),f^{j+k_{\varepsilon}}(x_{i}))<3\varepsilon/2.

This proves the claim.
∎

Fixing some quantifiers, let

(A.3)

C\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\sup_{x\in M}\big\{\|Df(x)\|,\|Df^{-1}(x)\|\big\}.

The following fact is straightforward to prove.

Claim A.7
.

There is a constant

such that for every

c\cdot C^{k_{\varepsilon}\cdot\dim(M)}

\varepsilon\cdot C^{-k_{\varepsilon}}
-dense in

.

Claim A.8
.

There exists a collection of sets

such that

\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{ss}}(Y,\ell)\subset\bigcup_{i}\bigcup_{y\in Y_{i}}B_{n_{i}}(f^{-k_{\varepsilon}}(y),3\varepsilon)\quad\text{ and }\quad\card(Y_{i})\leq c\cdot C^{k_{\varepsilon}\cdot\dim(M)}.

Proof.

Note that for each
x_{i}
, one has

B_{n_{i}-k_{\varepsilon}}\big(f^{k_{\varepsilon}}(x_{i}),3\varepsilon/2\big)\subset B\big(f^{k_{\varepsilon}}(x_{i}),3\varepsilon/2\big).

Hence, by Claim
A.6
,

\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{ss}}(Y,\ell)\subset\bigcup_{i}f^{-k_{\varepsilon}}\big(B_{n_{i}-k_{\varepsilon}}(f^{k_{\varepsilon}}(x_{i}),3\varepsilon/2)\big)\subset\bigcup_{i}f^{-k_{\varepsilon}}\big(B(f^{k_{\varepsilon}}(x_{i}),3\varepsilon/2)\big).

By Claim
A.7
, for each
i
, we can choose a finite set of points
Y_{i}\subset B(f^{k_{\varepsilon}}(x_{i}),3\varepsilon/2)
which is
\varepsilon\cdot C^{-k_{\varepsilon}}
-dense in
B(f^{k_{\varepsilon}}(x_{i}),3\varepsilon/2)
and has cardinality at most
c\cdot C^{k_{\varepsilon}\cdot\dim(M)}
. Without loss of generality, we can assume that
Y_{i}\subset B_{n_{i}-k_{\varepsilon}}(f^{k_{\varepsilon}}(x_{i}),3\varepsilon/2)
.

Now fix
x\in\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{ss}}(Y,\ell)
, then there exists
x_{i}
such that

(A.4)

f^{k_{\varepsilon}}(x)\in B_{n_{i}-k_{\varepsilon}}(f^{k_{\varepsilon}}(x_{i}),3\varepsilon/2).

Then take
y\in Y_{i}
such that
d(f^{k_{\varepsilon}}(x),y)<\varepsilon\cdot C^{-k_{\varepsilon}}.
Now, we have the following estimates:
By Equation (

), for every

d(f^{-j}(y),f^{k_{\varepsilon}-j}(x))\leq C^{j}d(y,f^{k_{\varepsilon}}(x))\leq C^{j}\cdot\varepsilon\cdot C^{-k_{\varepsilon}}\leq\varepsilon.

Moreover, as
