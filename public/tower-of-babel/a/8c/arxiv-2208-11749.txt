Quantization dimension for self-similar measures of overlapping construction
MRINAL KANTI ROYCHOWDHURY, Károly Simon

Abstract
. Quantization dimension has been computed for many invariant measures of dynamically defined fractals having well separated cylinders, that is, in the cases when the so-called Open Set Condition (OSC) holds. To attack the same problem in case of heavy overlaps between the cylinders, we consider a family of self-similar measures, for which the underlying Iterated Function System satisfies the so-called Weak Separation Property (WSP) but does not satisfy the OSC since complete overlaps occur in between the cylinders. The work in this paper also shows that the quantization dimension determined for the set of overlap self-similar construction satisfying the WSP has a relationship with the temperature function of the thermodynamic formalism.

1.
Introduction

The basic goal of quantization for probability distribution is to reduce the number of values, which is typically uncountable, describing a probability distribution to some finite set and thus approximation of a continuous probability distribution by a discrete distribution. It has broad applications in signal processing, telecommunications, data compression, image processing and cluster analysis. Over the years, many useful theorems have been proved and numerous other results and algorithms have been obtained in quantization. For a detailed survey on the subject and comprehensive lists of references to the literature one is referred to
[
B
,
BW
,
G
,
GG
,
GKL
,
GL1
,
GL2
,
GL4
,
GN
,
Z1
,
Z2
]
. Rigorous mathematical treatment of the fundamentals of the quantization theory is provided in Graf-Luschgy’s book (see
[
GL1
]
). In general, these theorems almost exclusively involve absolutely continuous probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Two main goals of the theory are:
(1)
finding the exact configuration of a so-called ‘
n
-optimal set’ which corresponds to the support of the quantized version of the distribution, and
(2)
estimating the rate at which some specified measure of the error goes to zero as
n
goes to infinity. This paper deals with the second kind of problem.

Given a Borel
probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, a number
r\in(0,+\infty)

and a natural number
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the
n
th
quantization
error
of order
r
for
\mu
, is defined by

V_{n,r}:=V_{n,r}(\mu)=\text{inf}\left\{\int d(x,\alpha)^{r}d\mu(x):\alpha\subset\mathbb{R}^{d},\,1\leq\text{card}(\alpha)\leq n\right\},

where
d(x,\alpha)
denotes the distance from the point
x
to the set

\alpha
with respect to a given norm
\|\cdot\|
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. If
\alpha
is a finite set, the error
\int d(x,\alpha)^{r}d\mu(x)
is often referred to as the
cost,
or
distortion error
of order
r
for
\mu
and
\alpha
. It is known that for a Borel probability measure
\mu
, if its support contains infinitely many elements and
\int\|x\|^{r}d\mu(x)
is finite, then an optimal set of
n
-means always has exactly
n
-elements (see
[
AW
,
GKL
,
GL
,
GL1
]
). This set
\alpha
can then be used to give a best approximation of

\mu
by a discrete probability supported on a set with no more than

n
points. Such a set
\alpha
for which the infimum occurs and contains no more than
n
points is called an
optimal set of
n
-means
, or
optimal set of
n
-quantizers
(of order
r
). Under suitable conditions this can be done by giving
each point
a\in\alpha
a mass corresponding to
\mu(A_{a})
, where

A_{a}
is the set of points
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
d(x,\alpha)=d(x,a)
. So,
\{A_{a}:a\in\alpha\}
is the ‘Voronoi’ partition of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
induced by
\alpha
. Of course, the idea of ‘best approximation’ is, in general,
dependent on the choice of
r
.
For some recent work in the direction of optimal sets of
n
-means, one can see
[
CR
,
DR1
,
DR2
,
GL5
,

,

,

,

. The set of all optimal sets of
n
-means for a Borel probability measure
\mu
is denoted by
\mathcal{C}_{n,r}(\mu)
, i.e.,

(1.1)

\mathcal{C}_{n,r}(\mu):=\{\alpha\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}:1\leq\text{card}(\alpha)\leq n\text{ and }V_{n,r}(\mu)=\int d(x,\alpha)^{r}d\mu(x)\}.

Write
e_{n,r}(\mu):=V_{n,r}^{\frac{1}{r}}(\mu)
.
The numbers

(1.2)

\underline{D}_{r}(\mu):=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log n}{-\log e_{n,r}(\mu)},\text{ and }\overline{D}_{r}(\mu):=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log n}{-\log e_{n,r}(\mu)},

are called the
lower
and the
upper quantization dimensions
of
\mu
of order
r
, respectively. If
\underline{D}_{r}(\mu)=\overline{D}_{r}(\mu)
, the common value is called the
quantization dimension
of
\mu
of order
r
and is denoted by
D_{r}(\mu)
. Quantization dimension measures the speed at which the specified measure of the error goes to zero as
n
tends to infinity.
For any
\kappa>0
, the numbers
\liminf_{n}n^{\frac{r}{\kappa}}V_{n,r}(\mu)
and
\limsup_{n}n^{\frac{r}{\kappa}}V_{n,r}(\mu)
are called the
\kappa
-dimensional lower
and
upper quantization coefficients
for
\mu
, respectively. The quantization coefficients provide us with more accurate information about the asymptotics of the quantization error than the quantization dimension. Compared to the calculation of quantization dimension, it is usually much more difficult to determine whether the lower and the upper quantization coefficients are finite and positive. It follows from
[
GL1
, Proposition 11.3]
that if

(1.3)

0\leq t<\underline{D}_{r}<s,\text{ then }\lim_{n\to\infty}ne^{t}_{n,r}=+\infty\text{ and }\liminf_{n\to\infty}ne^{s}_{n,r}=0,

and if

(1.4)

0\leq t<\overline{D}_{r}<s,\text{ then }\limsup_{n\to\infty}ne^{t}_{n,r}=+\infty\text{ and }\lim_{n\to\infty}ne^{s}_{n,r}=0.

For probabilities with non-vanishing absolutely continuous parts the numbers
D_{r}
are all equal to the dimension
d
of the underlying space, but for singular probabilities the family
\{D_{r}\}_{r>0}
gives an interesting description of their geometric (multifractal) structures. A detailed account of this theory can be found in
[
GL1
]
. There the quantization dimension is introduced as a new type of fractal dimension spectrum and a formula for its determination is derived in the case of self-similar probabilities with the strong separation property. In
[

, Graf and Luschgy extended the above result and determined the quantization dimension

of self-similar probabilities with the weaker open set condition, but there it remained open whether the

-dimensional lower quantization coefficient is positive. Later they answered it in
[

]
. Under the open set condition, Lindsay and
Mauldin (see
[

]
) determined the quantization dimension function

, where

, of an

with a conformal iterated function system determined by finitely
many conformal mappings. Subsequently, quantization

[

In a very recent paper,
among many other interesting applications, S. Zhu
[
Zhu
]
has solved the problem of the computation of quantization dimension for the complete overlapping case in the following very special situation: Let
\mathcal{S}=\{S_{k}\}_{k\leq m}

be a self-similar IFS with the following properties:
(1)
there are distinct

and

S_{i}=S_{j}
.
(2)
The self-similar IFS
\mathcal{S}=\{S_{k}\}_{k\leq m,k\neq j}
satisfies the so-called strong separation property. That is, for any two distinct

, where
\Lambda
is the attractor of the IFSs
\mathcal{S}
.
In general, if we are given a self-similar IFS which satisfies the so-called Weak Separation Property (WSP), but does not satisfy the OSC we cannot get rid of the overlapping feature of the system just by throwing out some of the mappings of the IFS. Even if we get rid of one of the mappings which causes the total overlapping feature, we still have overlaps in the remaining system (which are not complete overlaps).

In this paper, we make a step towards our goal to determine the quantization dimension for self-similar measures on the line in the case when the underlying self-similar system satisfies the WSP (for the definition and basic properties see
[
Zer
]
).
Namely, we solve this problem for a special family which has the above mentioned properties. Our work also shows that the quantization dimension determined for a set of overlap self-similar construction satisfying the WSP has a relationship with the temperature function of the thermodynamic formalism.

\displaystyle V_{n,r}(\nu)

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\widehat{\Gamma}_{E}(\varepsilon)}3^{-|\mathbf{i}|r}\psi(\mathbf{i})V_{m,r}(\nu)

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\widehat{\Gamma}_{E}(\varepsilon)}\underbrace{\left(3^{-|\mathbf{i}|r}\psi(\mathbf{i})\right)^{t_{r}}}_{\leq\widehat{\phi}(\mathbf{i})}\underbrace{\left(3^{-|\mathbf{i}|r}\psi(\mathbf{i})\right)^{1-t_{r}}}_{\leq\varepsilon^{\frac{1-t_{r}}{t_{r}}}}V_{m,r}(\nu)

\displaystyle\underbrace{\varepsilon^{\frac{1-t_{r}}{t_{r}}}}_{\varepsilon^{\frac{r}{\chi_{r}}}}V_{m,r}(\nu)\underbrace{\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\widehat{\Gamma}_{E}(\varepsilon)}\widehat{\phi}(\mathbf{i})}_{\leq 2C_{4}}

∎

Fact 6.1
.

For every

\nu=\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}\psi(\mathbf{i})\nu_{\mathbf{i}}.

Proof.

.

Using that

\displaystyle\int f(x)d\nu(x)

Let

Lemma 6.2

There exists a set

Proof.

Define

It is easy to see that this function is continuous. Hence, it attains its infimum on

I^{n}
. Any place where the infimum is attained can be chosen as

.
∎

Lemma 6.3
.

Fix an
m
. Then, there exists an
n_{0}

there exists a sequence

\left\{n_{\mathbf{i}}\right\}_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{m}}

1\leq n_{\mathbf{i}}<n,\quad\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{m}}n_{\mathbf{i}}\leq 2n,\quad u_{n,r}(\nu)\geq\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{m}}\psi(\mathbf{i})3^{-|\mathbf{i}|r}u_{n_{\mathbf{i}},r}(\nu).

Proof.

Fix an
m
and to shorten the notation we write

\Gamma:=\mathcal{T}_{m}
.
We consider any
\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\Sigma^{*}
such that

S_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(I)\subset U
. For example
\boldsymbol{\tau}=(1)
will do since
I_{1}=S_{1}(I)=\left[1,\frac{5}{2}\right]\subset\left(0,\frac{9}{2}\right)
. Let

. Let
\delta:=3^{-m}

(6.7)

d(x,U^{c})\geq d(x,S_{\mathbf{i}}(U^{c}))\geq\delta\varepsilon,\quad\text{ if }x\in I_{\mathbf{i}\boldsymbol{\tau}}\text{ and }\mathbf{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{m}.

For each
n
let
\alpha_{n}
be the optimal set for

6.2
. We define

(6.8)

\delta_{n}:=\max\left\{d(x,\alpha_{n}\cup U^{c}):x\in\Lambda\right\}.

Since
\delta_{n}\to 0
we can choose an
n_{0}
such that

\delta_{n}<\delta\varepsilon
if
n\geq n_{0}

Let
x\in\Lambda_{\mathbf{i}\boldsymbol{\tau}}
.
Then,
x\in S_{\mathbf{i}}(U)\subset U

\mathbf{i}\in\Gamma
. By compactness,

where the one but last inequality holds since

a\not\in S_{\mathbf{i}}(U^{c})

,

\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\Gamma}n_{\mathbf{i}}\leq 2n.

Finally we prove that

u_{n,r}(\nu)\geq\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\Gamma}\psi(\mathbf{i})3^{-mr}u_{n_{\mathbf{i}},r}(\nu).

To verify this

\alpha_{n}\cup S_{\mathbf{i}}(U^{c})=\alpha_{n}\cup(S_{\mathbf{i}}(U))^{c}=\left(\alpha_{n}\cap S_{\mathbf{i}}(U)\right)\cup\left(S_{\mathbf{i}}(U)\right)^{c}.

\displaystyle=

at the third step we used that

and

for every

\displaystyle\geq

7.
Checking Properties 1-4

7.1.
Good blocks and bad blocks

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
.
For arbitrary
1\leq k<\ell\leq n
and we use the shorthand notation

[n]:=\left\{1,\dots,n\right\},\quad[k,\ell]:=\left\{k,\dots,\ell\right\}.

Definition 7.1
.

Bad blocks, Good blocks

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}=(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{n})
.
For a
k<\ell\leq n

we say that
[k,\ell]
is a
bad block
of
[n]

with respect to an
\boldsymbol{\eta}=(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{n})\in\mathcal{A}^{n}

if

(\eta_{k},\dots,\eta_{\ell})=(0,3,3,\dots,3)
. We say that
[k,\ell]
is a
maximal bad block
if either
\ell=\infty
or
\eta_{\ell+1}\neq 3
. Similarly,
[k,\ell]

is a
good block
of
[n]
with respect to
\boldsymbol{\eta}

if
(\eta_{k},\dots,\eta_{\ell})=(1,1,1,\dots,1,0)
.
We say that
[k,\ell]
is a
maximal good block
if either
k=1
or
\eta_{k-1}\neq 1
.
We write
B(\boldsymbol{\eta})
, (
G(\boldsymbol{\eta})
) for the collection of maximal bad (good) blocks with respect to
\boldsymbol{\eta}
, respectively. That is,

(7.1)

B(\boldsymbol{\eta}):=\left\{[k,\ell]:1\leq k<\ell<\infty,\ \eta_{k}=0,\ \eta_{k+1}=\cdots=\eta_{\ell}=3,\eta_{\ell+1}\neq 3\right\}\\
\bigcup\left\{[k,\infty]:1\leq k,\ \eta_{k}=0,\ \eta_{k+1}=\eta_{k+2}=\eta_{k+3}=\cdots=3\right\}.

Similarly,

(7.2)

G(\boldsymbol{\eta}):=\left\{[k,\ell]:1\leq k<\ell<\infty,\ \eta_{k}=\cdots=\eta_{\ell-1}=1,\ \eta_{\ell}=0,\eta_{k-1}\neq 1\right\}\\
\bigcup\left\{[k,\infty]:1\leq k,\ \eta_{k}=\eta_{k+1}=\eta_{k+2}=\eta_{k+3}=\cdots=1\right\}.

Definition 7.2
.

A "bad" and a "good" partition of
[n]

Given an
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
and a

\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathcal{A}^{n}
, the following definitions are meant to be with respect to
\boldsymbol{\eta}
.

(1)

A_{\text{Good}}(\boldsymbol{\eta}):=[n]\setminus\bigcup\limits_{[k,\ell]\in G(\boldsymbol{\eta})}[k,\ell],\text{ and }A_{\text{Bad}}(\boldsymbol{\eta}):=[n]\setminus\bigcup\limits_{[k,\ell]\in B(\boldsymbol{\eta})}[k,\ell],

(2)

B_{\text{Good}}(\boldsymbol{\eta}):=\bigcup\limits_{[k,\ell]\in G(\boldsymbol{\eta})}\left\{\ell\right\},\text{ and }B_{\text{Bad}}(\boldsymbol{\eta}):=\bigcup\limits_{[k,\ell]\in B(\boldsymbol{\eta})}\left\{\ell\right\},

(3)

C_{\text{Good}}(\boldsymbol{\eta}):=\bigcup\limits_{[k,\ell]\in G(\boldsymbol{\eta})}[k,\ell-1],\text{ and }C_{\text{Bad}}(\boldsymbol{\eta}):=\bigcup\limits_{[k,\ell]\in B(\boldsymbol{\eta})}[k,\ell-1]
,

(4)

D_{\text{Good}}(\boldsymbol{\eta}):=A_{\text{Good}}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\cup B_{\text{Good}}(\boldsymbol{\eta})
,
and

D_{\text{Bad}}(\boldsymbol{\eta}):=A_{\text{Bad}}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\cup B_{\text{Bad}}(\boldsymbol{\eta})
.

We use most frequently
C_{\text{Bad}}(\boldsymbol{\eta})
and

D_{\text{Bad}}(\boldsymbol{\eta})
, so we also explain their meaning in words:

C_{\text{Good}}(\boldsymbol{\eta})
is the collection of
indices which are in a good box of
[n]
with respect to
\boldsymbol{\eta}
but not as a right endpoint of a maximal good box (with respect to
\boldsymbol{\eta}
).

D_{\text{Good}}(\boldsymbol{\eta})
is the collection of
indices which are either the right endpoint of a maximal good box of
[n]
or
not contained in any good boxes of
[n]
(with respect to
\boldsymbol{\eta}
).

In this way we partition
[n]
into

C_{\text{Good}}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\cup D_{\text{Good}}(\boldsymbol{\eta})
. The indices in
D_{\text{Good}}(\boldsymbol{\eta})

cause less problem than the ones in
C_{\text{Good}}(\boldsymbol{\eta})
. This is indicated by the following fact. Before stating it
recall that
\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{i}}
was defined in (
3.2
). Using this definition we get that
for an
\mathbf{i}\in\Sigma_{A}^{*}

\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{i}}=\left\{\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathcal{A}^{|\mathbf{i}|}:\Pi(\boldsymbol{\eta})=\Pi(\mathbf{i})\right\}.

.

\frac{\psi(\mathbf{k}^{*})}{\psi(\mathbf{i}^{*})}=\frac{\psi(\mathbf{i}|_{\xi})\frac{p_{0}}{p_{1}}p_{1}^{n-\xi+m}\sum\limits_{\ell=0}^{n-\xi+m-1}\left(\frac{p_{1}}{p_{3}}\right)^{\ell}}{\psi(\mathbf{i}|_{\xi})p_{1}^{n-\xi}}=\frac{p_{0}}{p_{1}}p_{1}^{m}\sum\limits_{\ell=0}^{n-\xi+m-1}\left(\frac{p_{1}}{p_{3}}\right)^{\ell}.

(I)

If
m\leq q_{0}
, then
\widehat{\psi}(\mathbf{j})=p_{1}^{m}
. Then,
n-\xi+m<2q_{0}
. Hence, (
7.52
) holds in this case.

(II)

If
m>q_{0}
, then
\widehat{\psi}(\mathbf{j})=\widehat{\psi}(\overline{1}^{m})=a_{m}=\frac{p_{0}}{p_{1}}p_{1}^{m}\sum_{\ell=0}^{m-1}\left(\frac{p_{3}}{p_{1}}\right)^{\ell}
. Using this, (
7.57
) and the fact that
n-\xi<q_{0}
(which holds since
\mathbf{i}^{*}=\mathbf{i}
) we obtain that (
7.52
) holds also in this case.

(c)

\mathbf{i}^{*}=\mathbf{i}
and
\mathbf{k}^{*}=\mathbf{k}
(this implies that
\mathbf{j}^{*}=\mathbf{j}
since
\xi(\mathbf{k})\leq n-1
).
Then,

\widehat{\psi}(\mathbf{i})=\psi(\mathbf{i})
,

\widehat{\psi}(\mathbf{j})=\psi(\mathbf{j})
and

\widehat{\psi}(\mathbf{k})=\psi(\mathbf{k})
. Hence,

\frac{\widehat{\psi}(\mathbf{k})}{\widehat{\psi}(\mathbf{i})}=\frac{\psi(\mathbf{i}|_{\xi})p_{1}^{n+m-\xi}}{\psi(\mathbf{i}|_{\xi})p_{1}^{n-\xi}}=p_{1}^{m}=\widehat{\psi}(\mathbf{j}).

This verifies (
7.52
) for this case.

Case 2

\xi(\mathbf{k})\geq n
.
In this case
m-\xi(\mathbf{j})=n+m-\xi(\mathbf{k})
. This means that
\mathbf{k}^{*}\neq\mathbf{k}
if and only if
\mathbf{j}^{*}\neq\mathbf{j}
. In particular,
\xi(\mathbf{k})\geq n
implies that

(7.58)

\mathbf{k}^{*}=\mathbf{i}\mathbf{j}^{*}.

Now we distinguish two cases:

(a)

n\in D_{\text{Good}}(\mathbf{k}^{*})
.
Then, it follows from Lemma
7.9
that

\psi(\mathbf{k}^{*})=\psi(\mathbf{i})\psi(\mathbf{j}^{*})
. Here we used that by (
7.58
), we have
\sigma^{n}(\mathbf{k}^{*})=(\sigma^{n}\mathbf{k})^{*}
. Using that
\psi(\mathbf{i})\leq\widehat{\psi}(\mathbf{i})
we obtain that (
7.52
) holds in this case.

(b)

n\in C_{\text{Good}}(\mathbf{k}^{*})
.
This means that there is a good block
[u,v+1]\in\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{k}^{*})
such that

(7.59)

1\leq u\leq n\leq v\leq n+m-1,\quad\text{and}\quad k_{v+1}=0.

Using that either
\xi(\mathbf{k}^{*})=n+m
or
k_{\xi(\mathbf{k}^{*})}\neq 1
, we get that

(7.60)

\xi(\mathbf{k}^{*})\in D_{\text{Good}}(\mathbf{k}^{*}).

Moreover,

(7.61)

u-1<v+1\leq\xi(\mathbf{k}^{*}),\quad\text{ and }\quad u-1,v+1\in D_{\text{Good}}(\mathbf{k}^{*}).

Hence, by Lemma
7.9
we have

(7.62)

\psi(\mathbf{k}^{*})=\psi(\mathbf{i}|_{u-1})\psi(\overline{1}^{v-u+1}0)\psi(\sigma^{v+1}\mathbf{k}|_{\xi(\mathbf{k})})\psi\left(\left(\overline{1}^{n+m-\xi(\mathbf{k})}\right)^{*}\right),

where we remark that the first and last words in (
7.62
),

\mathbf{i}|_{u-1}
and

(\overline{1}^{n+m-\xi(\mathbf{k})})^{*}
, respectively, can be the empty words
\flat
. In this case we recall that
\psi(\flat)=1
.
Using Fact
7.10
in the third step we get

(7.63)

\displaystyle\psi(\overline{1}^{v-u+1}0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi(\overline{1}^{n-u+1}\overline{1}^{v-n}0)=a_{(n-u+1)+(v-n+1)}\leq C_{6}a_{n-u+1}a_{v-n+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{6}\psi(\overline{1}^{n-u}0)\psi(\overline{1}^{v-n}0)\leq C_{6}\psi\left(\left(\overline{1}^{n-u+1}\right)^{*}\right)\psi(\overline{1}^{v-n}0).

Now we substitute this into (
7.62
) and get

(7.64)

\psi(\mathbf{k}^{*})\leq C_{6}\underbrace{\psi(\mathbf{i}|_{u-1})\psi\left(\left(\overline{1}^{n-u+1}\right)^{*}\right)}_{\psi(\mathbf{i}^{*})}\underbrace{\psi(\overline{1}^{v-n}0)\psi(\sigma^{v+1}\mathbf{k}|_{\xi(\mathbf{k})})\psi\left(\left(\overline{1}^{n+m-\xi(\mathbf{k})}\right)^{*}\right)}_{\psi(\mathbf{j}^{*})},

where we used Fact
7.11
and
in the last step we used that
n+m-\xi(\mathbf{k})=m-\xi(\mathbf{j})
. That is, (
7.52
) holds also in this last possible case.

∎

We will need the following Claim:

Claim 7.16
.

For an arbitrary
\mathbf{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
we have

(7.65)

\psi(\mathbf{i}0)\geq p_{0}\cdot\widehat{\psi}(\mathbf{i}).

Proof.

Fix an
\mathbf{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
.

(a)

Assume that
i_{n}\neq 1
.
Then,
\mathbf{i}^{*}=\mathbf{i}
and
n\in D_{\text{Good}}(\mathbf{i}0)
. Then, by Lemma
7.9
, we have

\psi(\mathbf{i}0)=\psi(\mathbf{i})\psi(0)=p_{0}\widehat{\psi}(\mathbf{i})
. So, (
7.65
) holds in this case.

(b)

Assume that
i_{n}=1
. Then,
\xi:=\xi(\mathbf{i})\leq n-1

and
\xi\in D_{\text{Good}}(\mathbf{i}0)
. Then, by Lemma
7.9
we have

(7.66)

\psi(\mathbf{i}0)=\psi(\mathbf{i}|_{\xi})\psi\left(\sigma^{\xi}(\mathbf{i}0)\right)=\psi(\mathbf{i}|_{\xi})\psi\left(\overline{1}^{n-\xi}0\right)=\psi(\mathbf{i}|_{\xi})\cdot a_{n-\xi+1}.

On the other hand, by Fact
7.11

(7.67)

\widehat{\psi}(\mathbf{i})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\psi(\mathbf{i}|_{\xi})p_{1}^{n-\xi},&\hbox{if $n-\xi<q_{0}$;}\\
\psi(\mathbf{i}|_{\xi})a_{n-\xi},&\hbox{if $n-\xi\geq q_{0}$.}\end{array}\right.

Putting together (
7.66
) and (
7.67
) we get that (
7.65
) holds.

∎

Claim 7.17
.

Let
\mathbf{j}\in\mathcal{T}_{m}
for an
m\geq 1
. Then, we have

(7.68)

\widehat{\psi}(1\mathbf{j})\geq p_{1}\widehat{\psi}(\mathbf{j}).

Proof.

First we consider the case when
\mathbf{j}=\overline{1}^{m}
. This case can be subdivided into the three cases when
m<q_{0}-1
,
m=q_{0}-1
and
m\geq q_{0}
. Using formula (
7.21
), one can easily point out in each of these three cases that (
7.68
) holds. So, from now on we may assume that there exists a
p\in\left\{1,\dots,m\right\}

such that
j_{p}\neq 1
. Using this, one can easily see in the same way as in Case 2 of the proof of Lemma
7.15

that

(7.69)

\left(1\mathbf{j}\right)^{*}=1\mathbf{j}^{*}.

Observe that

(7.70)

\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{j}^{*}}\Longrightarrow 1\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathcal{I}_{1\mathbf{j}^{*}}.

Namely,

(7.71)

\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{j}^{*}}\Longleftrightarrow\Pi(\boldsymbol{\eta})=\Pi(\mathbf{j}^{*})\Longrightarrow S_{1}(\Pi(\boldsymbol{\eta}))=S_{1}(\Pi(\mathbf{j}^{*}))\\
\Longrightarrow\Pi(1\boldsymbol{\eta})=\Pi(1\mathbf{j}^{*})\Longrightarrow 1\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathcal{I}_{1\mathbf{j}^{*}}.

By definition

(7.72)

\psi(1\mathbf{j}^{*})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathcal{I}_{1\mathbf{j}^{*}}}p_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\geq\sum_{\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{j}^{*}}}p_{1\boldsymbol{\eta}}=p_{1}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{j}^{*}}}p_{1\boldsymbol{\eta}}=p_{1}\psi(\mathbf{j}^{*}).

∎

Lemma 7.18
.

There exist a
C_{11}>0
such that

(7.73)

\widehat{\psi}(\mathbf{i}01\mathbf{j})\geq C_{11}\widehat{\psi}(\mathbf{i})\widehat{\psi}(\mathbf{j}),\quad\forall\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}\in\Sigma_{A}^{*}.

Proof.

Given
\mathbf{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{n}

\mathbf{j}\in\mathcal{T}_{m}

7.15
, we obtain that

(7.74)

\widetilde{\mathbf{k}}^{*}=\mathbf{i}0(1\mathbf{j})^{*}.

Observe that

.
Hence, first using Lemma
7.9

\widehat{\psi}(\widetilde{\mathbf{k}})=\psi(\widetilde{\mathbf{k}}^{*})=\psi(\mathbf{i}0)\psi\left(\left(1\mathbf{j}\right)^{*}\right)\geq p_{0}\widehat{\psi}(\mathbf{i})p_{1}\widehat{\psi}(\mathbf{j}).

∎
