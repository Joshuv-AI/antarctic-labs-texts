José G. Alvim, Hugo L. Mariano, Caio de A. Mendes

Abstract
This work is largely focused on extending D. Higgs’ Ω \Omega -sets to the context of quantales, following the broad program of [ 7 ] , we explore the rich category of 𝒬 \mathscr{Q} -sets for strong, integral and commutative quantales, or other similar axioms. The focus of this work is to study the different notion of “completeness” a 𝒬 \mathscr{Q} -set may enjoy and their relations, completion functors, resulting reflective subcategories, their relations to relational morphisms. We establish the general equivalence of singleton complete 𝒬 \mathscr{Q} -sets with functional morphisms and the category of 𝒬 \mathscr{Q} -sets with relational morphisms; we provide two characterizations of singleton completeness in categorical terms; we show that the singleton complete categorical inclusion creates limits.

Definition 2.1
:

A

\mathscr{Q}=(|\mathscr{Q}|,\leq,\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}})
for which

(|\mathscr{Q}|,\leq)

(|\mathscr{Q}|,\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}})

1
1

1

\displaystyle a\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\left(\bigvee_{b\in B}b\right)

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{b\in B}\left(a\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}b\right)

\displaystyle\left(\bigvee_{b\in B}b\right)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}a

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{b\in B}\left(b\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}a\right)

We denote by
\operatorname{E}\mathscr{Q}
the subset of

\mathscr{Q}
comprised of its idempotent elements.

Remark 2.1
:

1.

In any quantale

2.

Since

Since

Example 2.1

Locales are – perhaps – the best example of quantales that

Remark 3.1

Hereon we are working exclusively with commutative

Given a quantale

Definition 3.1
:

A
\mathscr{Q}
-set is a set endowed with a

\mathscr{Q}
-distance operation usually denoted by

.
i.e

1.

\delta(x,y)=\delta(y,x)
;

2.

\delta(x,y)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\delta(y,z)\leq\delta(x,z)
;

3.

\delta(x,x)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\delta(x,y)=\delta(x,y)
.

and it is usual to denote

by simply the

\operatorname{E}x
.

A couple of things might jump out to reader in the above definition.

(i)

\delta
is symmetric, even though we have thrown out
all but the vaguest notions tying ourselves to metric

(ii)

Why is the triangle inequality upside down?

(iii)

\operatorname{E}x\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\delta(x,y)=\delta(x,y)

why not just ask that
\operatorname{E}x=\top
?

Those questions are all valid – and answering the first and last ones
differently has been done in the past and is the main difference

-sets and
\mathscr{Q}
-enriched categories
from a definitional perspective. The question of order being inverse is
more one of sanity: since we treat a

-valued equality, it makes sense to think that

\operatorname{E}x
is the maximally valid equality to

triangular inequality needs to be turned upsidedown – and turned into
the transitivity of equality.

:

Proposition 3.1
:

It should be observed that both notions of morphism actually do
form a category. For functional morphisms, we take composition
to be the usual function composition – since functional
morphisms are obviously closed under it – and the identity
function as the idendity. For relational morphisms, the
identity on
X
becomes
\delta_{X}
and composition is the
(perhaps obvious) relational composition:

[\psi\circ\varphi](x,z)=\bigvee_{y\in Y}\varphi(x,y)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\psi(y,z)

Since proving that functional morphisms do indeed form a
category would be trivial, we shall instead prove a stronger
result afterwards.

Proof.

Firstly we should observe that
\delta
is indeed a
relational morphism. This is easy enough, as the first three
axioms are direct applications of the triangular inequality,
the fourth and fifth are direct applications of the
extension axiom of
\mathscr{Q}
-sets, and the last one is
trivially true once we realize that

\delta(x,y)\leq\operatorname{E}x=\delta(x,x)

Once we have that
\delta
is indeed a morphism, we can
wonder about the action of (pre-)composing with it. In which
case we obtain

\displaystyle[\delta\circ\varphi](x,y)

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{y^{\prime}\in Y}\varphi(x,y^{\prime})\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\delta(y^{\prime},y)

\displaystyle\geq\varphi(x,y)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\delta(y,y)

\displaystyle=\varphi(x,y)

on the other hand, we could have applied the
appropriate relational morphism axiom inside
the
\bigvee
thus getting that the composite
was smaller than
\varphi(x,y)
instead. This
proves they were in fact equal all along. The
same goes for the other composite:

\displaystyle[\varphi\circ\delta](x,y)

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{x^{\prime}\in X}\delta(x,x^{\prime})\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\varphi(x^{\prime},y)

\displaystyle\geq\delta(x,x)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\varphi(x,y)

\displaystyle=\varphi(x,y)

Hence it only remains to see that
\circ
as defined for
relational morphisms is indeed a composition, in that it is
associative and that the composite of two morphisms is a
morphism. Firstly suppose that
\varphi
and
\psi
are
composable morphisms – we ought to show that their composite
is also a morphism:

\displaystyle[\psi\circ\varphi](x,z)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\delta(z,z^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{y}\varphi(x,y)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\psi(y,z)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\delta(z,z^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\bigvee_{y}\varphi(x,y)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\psi(y,z^{\prime})

\displaystyle=[\psi\circ\varphi](x,z^{\prime})

And likewise,
mutatis mutandis
one can
show that axioms 1-2 and 4-5 hold. Axiom 6 can
be seen to hold easily as well:

\displaystyle\bigvee_{z}[\psi\circ\varphi](x,z)

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{y}\varphi(x,y)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\bigvee_{z}\psi(y,z)

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{y}\varphi(x,y)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\operatorname{E}y

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{y}\varphi(x,y)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{E}x

Axiom 3 actually requires commutativity, which
is unfortunately the first of many times it is
very necessary to make use of it;

\displaystyle[\psi\circ\varphi](x,z)\mathbin{\overset{\psi\circ\varphi}{\otimes}}(x,z^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{y}\bigvee_{y^{\prime}}\varphi(x,y)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\psi(y,z)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\varphi(x,y^{\prime})\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\psi(y^{\prime},z)

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{y}\bigvee_{y^{\prime}}\psi(y,z)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\varphi(x,y)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\varphi(x,y^{\prime})\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\psi(y^{\prime},z)

\displaystyle\leq\bigvee_{y}\bigvee_{y^{\prime}}\psi(y,z)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\delta(y,y^{\prime})\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\psi(y^{\prime},z)

\displaystyle\leq\bigvee_{y}\bigvee_{y^{\prime}}\psi(y,z)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\psi(y,z)

\displaystyle\leq\bigvee_{y}\bigvee_{y^{\prime}}\delta(z,z^{\prime})=\delta(z,z^{\prime})

Interlude

In the general course of studying
{\Omega\text{-}\mathbf{Set}}
for a frame/locale

\Omega
one is bound to eventually come across a certain notion
called “completeness”. Completeness is, largely speaking, a property
necessary to transition between

-world to

\mathbf{Sh}\Omega
-world

[
3

. This notion

Sheaves, almost by definition, are about gluing partial data – in that

Definition 4.1

(Compatible Family)

Given a

-set

In light of our usual interpretation for

(Gluing Element)

Given a

If a family

Proof.

Suppose

\displaystyle\delta(x_{A},a)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{E}a

\displaystyle\delta(x_{A},b)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{E}b

hence

\displaystyle\operatorname{E}a\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\operatorname{E}b

\displaystyle=\delta(x_{A},a)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\delta(x_{A},b)

but then,

\displaystyle\operatorname{E}a\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\operatorname{E}b

\displaystyle\leq\delta(a,b)

but we know the following always holds

\displaystyle\delta(a,b)

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{E}a\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\operatorname{E}b

therefore they are the same.

This suffices for compatibility, now for the second claim:

and let us compare two possible gluings

\displaystyle\delta(x,y)

\displaystyle=\delta(x,y)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\operatorname{E}x

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{a\in A}\delta(x,y)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\operatorname{E}a

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{a\in A}\delta(x,y)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\operatorname{E}a\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\operatorname{E}a

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{a\in A}\delta(x,y)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\delta(x^{\prime},a)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\delta(x,a)

\displaystyle\leq\bigvee_{a\in A}\delta(x,y)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\delta(x^{\prime},x)

since the same holds swapping

∎

Definition 4.3

(
\delta
-equivalence)
:

Remark 4.1
:

It is evident that this is an equivalence relation.

Two
\delta

morphisms can’t distinguish them. This is codified in the following
lemma

:

The relation of

-equivalence is ‘congruential’

\displaystyle\operatorname{E}x=\operatorname{E}x^{\prime}=\delta(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\delta(f(x),f(x^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{E}f(x)=\operatorname{E}x

\displaystyle=\operatorname{E}x^{\prime}=\operatorname{E}f(x^{\prime})

As for relational morphisms,

\displaystyle=\varphi(x,y)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\delta(x,x^{\prime})

The dual inequality holds for the same reasons; and similarly,
the same equality can be obtained for

.

Remark 4.2

\displaystyle\delta(A,B)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\delta(B,C)

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{a\in A}\bigvee_{b\in B}\bigvee_{\beta\in B}\bigvee_{c\in C}\delta(a,b)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\delta(\beta,c)

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{a\in A}\bigvee_{b\in B}\bigvee_{\beta\in B}\bigvee_{c\in C}\delta(a,b)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\operatorname{E}b\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\operatorname{E}\beta\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\delta(\beta,c)

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{a\in A}\bigvee_{b\in B}\bigvee_{\beta\in B}\bigvee_{c\in C}\delta(a,b)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\delta(b,\beta)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\delta(\beta,c)

\displaystyle\leq\bigvee_{a\in A}\bigvee_{c\in C}\delta(a,c)

\displaystyle=\delta(A,C)

Which suffices for transitivity/triangular
inequality. It is obvious that the definition is
symmetric, so it only remains to show that extents
behave as they should.

\displaystyle\delta(A,A)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\delta(A,B)

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{a,a^{\prime}\in A}\bigvee_{\alpha\in A,b\in B}\delta(a,a^{\prime})\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\delta(\alpha,b)

\displaystyle\geq\bigvee_{\alpha\in A,b\in B}\delta(\alpha,\alpha)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\delta(\alpha,b)

\displaystyle=\delta(A,B)

and thus

\displaystyle\operatorname{E}A\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\delta(A,B)

\displaystyle=\delta(A,B)

(semi-cartesian)

Thus proving it is actually a
\mathscr{Q}
-set as desired.
∎

Definition 5.1

(Singleton)
:

A
singleton
on
X
is a map between the underlying set
and
\mathscr{Q}
:
\sigma:|X|\to\mathscr{Q}
. This map is
expected to satisfy the following axioms

1.

\sigma(x)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\operatorname{E}x=\sigma(x)
;

2.

\sigma(x)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\delta(x,y)\leq\sigma(y)
;

3.

\sigma(x)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\sigma(y)\leq\delta(x,y)
;

4.

\sigma(x)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\bigvee_{y}\sigma(y)=\sigma(x)
;

the first two being called the “subset axioms” the third being
the “singleton condition” and the last axiom being called the
“strictness condition”.

Intuitively, singletons represent a membership relation to a set –
this is governed by the subset axioms; the singleton condition then
states that the simultaneous membership of two different points implies
their similitude; the last axiom, strictness, is more technical in
nature.

It has been known in the literature for quite a few years

[
7
, p. 30]
– but its meaning is somewhat obscure, in
that it is a necessary condition for a singleton to be representable
(to be defined next), but it doesn’t have a neat interpretation. The
best we have arrived at is that strictness gives us that the supremum

\sup_{y}\sigma(y)
“behaves like an extent” in that it is idempotent.

Definition 5.2

(Representable Singleton)
:

A singleton
\sigma
is said to be representable
(or represented by an element
x_{\sigma}
) when there is one
such element
x_{\sigma}
such that

\sigma(y)=\delta(y,x_{\sigma})

Proposition 5.1
:

If
A
is a compatible family, then
\delta(\{{\underline{\hskip 5.0pt}}\},A)

is a singleton. In particular,
\delta({\underline{\hskip 5.0pt}},x)
is always
a singleton (by taking
A=\{x\}
).

Proof.

The subset axioms are immediately satisfied by the fact that

\operatorname{\mathfrak{G}}(X)
is always a
\mathscr{Q}
-set. The Singleton
condition is trivial too:

\sigma_{A}(x)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\sigma_{A}(y)=\delta(\{x\},A)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\delta(\{y\},A)\leq\delta(\{x\},\{y\})=\delta(x,y)

obviously, it is also strict considering that

\bigvee_{y\in X}\sigma_{A}(y)\geq\bigvee_{a\in A}\sigma_{A}(a)=\bigvee_{a\in A}\operatorname{E}a=\operatorname{E}A\geq\bigvee_{y\in X}\sigma_{A}(y)

∎

Remark 5.1
:

Quite obviously, if the singleton above is representable,
then the representing element glues
A
. And if an element
glues
A
, then it is bound to represent the singleton.

Proposition 5.2
:

If
A
is a compatible family, and
\sigma
is some
singleton over
A
’s
\mathscr{Q}
-set, we have that
if
x_{A}
glues
A
, then

\sigma(x_{A})=\bigvee_{a\in A}\sigma(a)

Proof.

\displaystyle\sigma(a)=

\displaystyle\sigma(a)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\operatorname{E}a

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma(a)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\delta(a,x_{A})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sigma(x_{A})

\displaystyle\sigma(x_{A})=

\displaystyle\sigma(x_{A})\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\operatorname{E}x_{A}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma(x_{A})\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\bigvee_{a\in A}\operatorname{E}a

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma(x_{A})\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\bigvee_{a\in A}\delta(a,x_{A})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigvee_{a\in A}\sigma(x_{A})\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\delta(a,x_{A})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\bigvee_{a\in A}\sigma(a)

∎

Definition 5.3

(Scott-Completeness)
:

We say that
X
is Scott-complete, or singleton-complete,
when every one of its singletons have exactly one representing
element.

Example 5.1
:

The terminal object is Scott-complete. An indirect way of
seeing this is to skip through the paper to where we prove
that the inclusion of Scott-complete
\mathscr{Q}
-sets
creates limits. A more direct answer comes from the fact that
since
\top\in\operatorname{E}\mathscr{Q}
is the gluing of the
compatible family
\operatorname{E}\mathscr{Q}
, thanks to
prop.

5.2
,

\sigma(\top)=\bigvee_{e}\sigma(e)
. And hence:

\sigma(e)=\sigma(e)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}e\leq\sigma(\top)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}e=\sigma(\top)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\delta(e,\top)\leq\sigma(e)

And therefore,
\sigma(\top)
represents
\sigma
.
Uniqueness is quite obvious: if for some
x
,

\forall e:\sigma(e)=e\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}x
, in particular

\sigma(\top)=\top\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}x=x
by integrality.

Example 5.2
:

1.

If
A
is a commutative ring and
M
is a left
A
-module, then the set over
\mathscr{Q}=Ideals(A)
,
X_{M}=(M,\delta)
, is such that for each
x,y\in M
,
x\sim_{\delta}y
iff
A.x=A.y
, so in general, is not a extensional
\mathscr{Q}
-set.

Remark 5.2
:

Scott-completeness is invariant under isomorphisms in

{\mathscr{Q}\text{-}\mathbf{Set}}_{f}
– but not in
{\mathscr{Q}\text{-}\mathbf{Set}}_{r}
, this will be explained
in a later subsection.

The first claim is easy to show, as functional isomorphisms are
simply bijections preserving
\delta
.

Theorem 5.1
:

Scott-Completness implies Gluing-Completeness

Proof.

Take a compatible family
A
, consider the singleton defined
in the previous proposition; suppose
x_{A}
represents

\sigma_{A}
; thus,

\bigvee_{a\in A}\delta(x_{A},a)=\sigma_{A}(x_{A})=\delta(x_{A},x_{A})=\operatorname{E}x_{A}

\delta(a,x_{A})=\sigma_{A}(a)=\bigvee_{a^{\prime}\in A}\delta(a,a^{\prime})=\operatorname{E}a

and hence
x_{A}
glues
A
. Now suppose that
x_{A}
glues

A
, then

\displaystyle\delta(y,x_{A})

\displaystyle\geq\delta(x_{A},a)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\delta(a,y)

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{E}a\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\delta(a,y)=\delta(a,y)

\displaystyle\delta(y,x_{A})

\displaystyle\geq\bigvee_{a\in A}\delta(y,a)

\displaystyle\geq\bigvee_{a\in A}\delta(a,x_{A})\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\delta(y,x_{A})

\displaystyle=\delta(y,x_{A})\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\bigvee_{a\in A}\delta(a,x_{A})

\displaystyle=\delta(y,x_{A})\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\bigvee_{a\in A}\operatorname{E}a

\displaystyle=\delta(y,x_{A})\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\operatorname{E}x_{A}

\displaystyle=\delta(y,x_{A})

hence
x_{A}
represents
\sigma_{A}
. Thus, if all singletons
have exactly one representing element, then all compatible
families have exactly one gluing.
∎

Theorem 5.2
:

There are gluing complete
\mathscr{Q}
-sets over

\mathscr{Q}=\operatorname{\mathscr{P}}(2)
which
are not
Scott-complete
– so those two concepts
are not
logically equivalent.

Proof.

Let
\mathscr{Q}
be the following partial order

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\top}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces a}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\neg a}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bot}

First, let
S\subseteq\mathscr{Q}
and let
\delta=\wedge
;

S
is gluing-complete if and only if
S
is complete as a
lattice:

If
A
is a subset of
S
, it must be compatible (!) since

\operatorname{E}x=x
and
\delta(x,y)=x\wedge y
and thus

\delta(x,y)=\operatorname{E}x\wedge\operatorname{E}y
. Moreover, if

x_{A}
glues
A
, then

\operatorname{E}x_{A}=x_{A}=\bigvee_{a\in A}\operatorname{E}a=\bigvee_{a\in A}a

and hence
x_{A}
is
\sup A
. It’s easy to verify that the
supremum is – if it exists in
S
– the gluing of
A
.

So, the set
S=\{\bot,a,\top\}
is gluing complete,
obviously. But the singleton
\sigma(x)=\neg a\wedge x

(which is the restriction of the singleton represented by

\neg a
) has no representing element.

\sigma(\bot)=\bot\qquad\sigma(a)=\bot\qquad\sigma(\top)=\neg a

And so
S
is gluing-complete but not Scott-complete.
∎

Definition 5.4

(The
\mathscr{Q}
-set of Singletons)
:

It is, in general, not possible to form a
\mathscr{Q}
-set
of singletons over a
\mathscr{Q}
-set
X
. There is some
trouble in defining
\delta
for singletons in a way akin to

[
3
, thm. 2.9.5]
. This is due to the
fact that
\bigvee_{x}\sigma(x)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\sigma(x)
not being
“extent-like” necessarily.

We must – seemingly – add the axiom of Strength to our
quantale
\mathscr{Q}
to guarantee we can form a

\mathscr{Q}
-set of singletons.
This enables us to define the following:

|\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(X)|=\left\{\sigma~\middle|~\text{$\sigma$ is a singleton over $X$}\right\}

\delta(\sigma,\xi)=\bigvee_{x\in X}\sigma(x)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\xi(x)

Proposition 5.3
:

Obviously, we must prove this is indeed a
\mathscr{Q}
-set.

Proof.

Firstly, we know that
\bigvee_{x}\sigma(x)
is idempotent,
and hence

\displaystyle\bigvee_{x}\sigma(x)

\displaystyle\leq\bigvee_{x}\sigma(x)

since
\mathscr{Q}
is hypothesized to be
strong, we have

\displaystyle\bigvee_{x}\sigma(x)

\displaystyle\leq\bigvee_{x}\sigma(x)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\sigma(x)

and since
\mathscr{Q}
is semi-cartesian

\displaystyle\bigvee_{x}\sigma(x)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\sigma(x)

\displaystyle\leq\bigvee_{x}\sigma(x)

(semi-cartesian)

and therefore,
\operatorname{E}\sigma=\bigvee_{x}\sigma(x)
. Now we
move onto the
\mathscr{Q}
-set axioms.
\delta
as defined
is obviously symmetric; let’s show extensionality:

\displaystyle\delta(\sigma,\xi)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\operatorname{E}\sigma

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{x}\sigma(x)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\xi(x)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\operatorname{E}\sigma

now recall
\sigma
’s strictness
condition

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{x}\sigma(x)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\xi(x)

Now we need only to show triangle
inequality/transitivity

\displaystyle\delta(\sigma,\xi)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\delta(\xi,\psi)

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{x}\bigvee_{y}\sigma(x)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\xi(x)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\xi(y)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\psi(y)

\displaystyle\leq\bigvee_{x}\bigvee_{y}\sigma(x)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\delta(x,y)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\psi(y)

\displaystyle\leq\bigvee_{y}\sigma(y)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\psi(y)

\displaystyle=\delta(\sigma,\psi)

∎

Lemma 5.1
:

A relational morphism
\varphi:X\to Y
induces a functional map

\check{\varphi}:X\to\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(Y)
given by

\check{\varphi}(x)=\varphi(x,{\underline{\hskip 5.0pt}})

Proof.

We ought to show that the map does indeed take every

to a
singleton
\check{\varphi}(x)
and that the map is indeed a
morphism.

\displaystyle\delta(x,x^{\prime})\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\check{\varphi}(x)

\displaystyle=\delta(x,x^{\prime})\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\varphi(x,{\underline{\hskip 5.0pt}})

\displaystyle\leq\varphi(x^{\prime},{\underline{\hskip 5.0pt}})

\displaystyle\operatorname{E}x\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\check{\varphi}(x)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{E}x\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\varphi(x,{\underline{\hskip 5.0pt}})

\displaystyle=\varphi(x,{\underline{\hskip 5.0pt}})

\displaystyle[\check{\varphi}(x)](y)\mathbin{\overset{\check{\varphi}(x)}{\otimes}}(y^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\varphi(x,y)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\varphi(x,y^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\delta(y,y^{\prime})

\displaystyle[\check{\varphi}(x)](y)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\bigvee_{y^{\prime}}[\check{\varphi}(x)](y^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\varphi(x,y)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\bigvee_{y}\varphi(x,y^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\varphi(x,y)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\operatorname{E}x

\displaystyle=\varphi(x,y)

\displaystyle=\check{\varphi}(x^{\prime})

Establishing that
\check{\varphi}(x)
is always
a singleton over
Y
. Now, with regards to it
being a morphism, just above we have also shown
that
\operatorname{E}\check{\varphi}(x)=\operatorname{E}x
, so
only one axiom remains:

\displaystyle\delta(\check{\varphi}(x),\check{\varphi}(x^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{y}\varphi(x,y)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\varphi(x^{\prime},y)

\displaystyle\geq\bigvee_{y}\varphi(x,y)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\varphi(x,y)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\delta(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq\delta(x,x^{\prime})\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\bigvee_{y}\varphi(x,y)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\varphi(x,y)

\displaystyle=\delta(x,x^{\prime})\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\operatorname{E}x

\displaystyle=\delta(x,x^{\prime})

∎

5.2
Scott Completion

Scott-completion is, unsurprisingly, going to be a reflector functor of
the from
{\mathscr{Q}\text{-}\mathbf{Set}}
to the full subcategory
\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\!{\mathscr{Q}\text{-}\mathbf{Set}}
; in this
subsection we therefore will show that
\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(X)
is indeed
Scott-complete and that object assignment can be made functorial – in
such a way to get a left adjoint to the fully faithful subcategory
inclusion.

Lemma 5.2

(
[
3
]
cf. p. 158)
:

If
\sigma_{x}
denotes
\delta({\underline{\hskip 5.0pt}},x)
, then

\delta(\sigma_{x},\xi)=\xi(x)

a result akin to the Yoneda lemma, wherein

\operatorname{\mathbf{hom}}(\operatorname{\!\text{\char 135\relax}}(x),F)\cong F(x)

Proof.

\displaystyle\delta(\sigma_{x},\xi)

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{y}\delta(y,x)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\xi(y)

\displaystyle\leq\xi(x)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{E}x\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\xi(x)

\displaystyle=\delta(x,x)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\xi(x)

\displaystyle\leq\bigvee_{z}\delta(z,x)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\xi(z)

\displaystyle=\delta(\sigma_{x},\xi)

∎

Remark 5.4
:

Notice that this means

\delta(\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y})=\delta(x,y)

and thus the mapping
x\mapsto\sigma_{x}
preserves

\delta
and hence is a morphism – it is in fact a regular
monomorphism.

Proposition 5.5
:

\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(X)
is, indeed, Scott-complete.

Proof.

In this proof, we shall refer to members of
\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(X)

as

-singletons and use lowercase
\xi,\psi,\varphi

and refer to members of
\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\circ\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(X)

2
-singletons and use uppercase
\Sigma

etc.

To go about doing this we must show two things: that two

1
-singletons cannot represent the same
2
-singleton; and
that every
2
-singleton comes from a
1
-singleton.

For the purpose of showing that
\xi\mapsto\sigma_{\xi}
is
injective, suppose that
\xi
and
\psi
are such that

\sigma_{\xi}=\sigma_{\psi}
. Let

and realize

\xi(x)=\delta(\sigma_{x},\xi)=\sigma_{\xi}(\sigma_{x})=\sigma_{\psi}(\sigma_{x})=\delta(\sigma_{x},\psi)=\psi(x)

thence they are extensionally equal.

Now take a
2
-singleton
\Sigma
, we shall define a

1
-singleton that we hope represents it; namely:

\varphi(x)=\Sigma(\sigma_{x})

one way to become convinced that
\varphi

is that
\sigma_{{\underline{\hskip 4.09723pt}}}

\delta
and

\Sigma\circ\sigma_{{\underline{\hskip 4.09723pt}}}

\displaystyle\Sigma(\xi)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{E}\xi\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\Sigma(\xi)

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{x}\xi(x)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\xi(x)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\Sigma(\xi)

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{x}\xi(x)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\delta(\sigma_{x},\xi)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\Sigma(\xi)

\displaystyle\leq\bigvee_{x}\xi(x)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\Sigma(\sigma_{x})

\displaystyle=\delta(\xi,\varphi)

Hence, if we can show strictness, we are done. So take

which we ought to show enjoys the property that

\varphi(x)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\bigvee_{y}\varphi(y)=\varphi(x)

it is clear that it would suffice if we proved that

\operatorname{E}\Sigma\leq\operatorname{E}\varphi
. For that would suffice for

\varphi(x)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\bigvee_{y}\varphi(y)=\varphi(x)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\operatorname{E}\varphi=\Sigma(\sigma_{x})\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\operatorname{E}\Sigma=\Sigma(\sigma_{x})=\varphi(x)

\displaystyle\operatorname{E}\Sigma

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{\xi}\Sigma(\xi)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\Sigma(\xi)

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{\xi}\Sigma(\xi)

(Strength)

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{\xi}\Sigma(\xi)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\operatorname{E}\xi

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{x}\bigvee_{\xi}\Sigma(\xi)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\xi(x)

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{x}\bigvee_{\xi}\Sigma(\xi)\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\delta(\xi,\sigma_{x})

\displaystyle\leq\bigvee_{x}\Sigma(\sigma_{x})

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{x}\Sigma(\sigma_{x})\mathbin{\overset{}{\otimes}}\Sigma(\sigma_{x})

(Strength)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{E}\varphi

∎

\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\!{\mathscr{Q}\text{-}\mathbf{Set}}_{r}

\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\!{\mathscr{Q}\text{-}\mathbf{Set}}_{r}(\operatorname{\mathcal{G}\!\mathcal{\scriptscriptstyle R}}({\underline{\hskip 5.0pt}}_{1}),\operatorname{\mathcal{G}\!\mathcal{\scriptscriptstyle R}}({\underline{\hskip 5.0pt}}_{2}))\cong\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\!{\mathscr{Q}\text{-}\mathbf{Set}}_{f}({\underline{\hskip 5.0pt}}_{1},{\underline{\hskip 5.0pt}}_{2})

if we fix
{\underline{\hskip 5.0pt}}_{2}=X
, we can forget
about the
\operatorname{\mathcal{G}\!\mathcal{\scriptscriptstyle R}}
on the second coordinate
(because it’s there just to act on morphisms),
thus getting

\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\!{\mathscr{Q}\text{-}\mathbf{Set}}_{r}(\operatorname{\mathcal{G}\!\mathcal{\scriptscriptstyle R}}({\underline{\hskip 5.0pt}}),X)\cong\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\!{\mathscr{Q}\text{-}\mathbf{Set}}_{f}({\underline{\hskip 5.0pt}},X)

Resuming our isomorphism chain:

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\!{\mathscr{Q}\text{-}\mathbf{Set}}_{f}(\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}({\underline{\hskip 5.0pt}}),X)

\displaystyle\cong\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\!{\mathscr{Q}\text{-}\mathbf{Set}}_{r}(\operatorname{\mathcal{G}\!\mathcal{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\circ\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}({\underline{\hskip 5.0pt}}),X)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\!\text{\char 135\relax}}_{r}(X)\circ\operatorname{\mathcal{G}\!\mathcal{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\circ\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}

For our claim to hold, we would need a natural
isomorphism
\operatorname{\mathcal{G}\!\mathcal{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\to\operatorname{\mathcal{G}\!\mathcal{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\circ\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}

– the reader should be happy to remember that
this isomorphism has already been proven in an
earlier lemma. We can then append one last
isomorphism then:

\displaystyle\operatorname{\!\text{\char 135\relax}}_{r}(X)\circ\operatorname{\mathcal{G}\!\mathcal{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\circ\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}

\displaystyle\cong\operatorname{\!\text{\char 135\relax}}_{r}\circ\operatorname{\mathcal{G}\!\mathcal{\scriptscriptstyle R}}

We can then put it all together and show there is some
twisted little natural isomorphism

\operatorname{\!\text{\char 135\relax}}_{f}(X)\cong\operatorname{\!\text{\char 135\relax}}_{r}\circ\operatorname{\mathcal{G}\!\mathcal{\scriptscriptstyle R}}=\operatorname{\!\text{\char 135\relax}}_{r}(X)\restriction\operatorname{\mathcal{G}\!\mathcal{\scriptscriptstyle R}}

The converse implication is, thankfully, easier; suppose there
is indeed an isomorphism as above; we ought to show that
X

was Scott-complete to begin with.

\displaystyle\operatorname{\!\text{\char 135\relax}}_{f}(i\circ\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(X))

\displaystyle\cong\operatorname{\!\text{\char 135\relax}}_{r}(i\circ\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(X))\circ\operatorname{\mathcal{G}\!\mathcal{\scriptscriptstyle R}}

(just proven)

\displaystyle\cong\operatorname{\!\text{\char 135\relax}}_{r}(X)\circ\operatorname{\mathcal{G}\!\mathcal{\scriptscriptstyle R}}

(
X\cong_{r}i\circ\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(X)
)

\displaystyle\cong\operatorname{\!\text{\char 135\relax}}_{f}(X)

(hypothesis)

thence, by Yoneda we obtain that

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(X)

\displaystyle\cong_{f}X

But Scott-completeness is invariant under functional
isomorphisms. So
X
was Scott-complete to begin with and
thus ends our proof.
∎

We then give a useful tool to show Scott-completeness in terms
of an object’s representable functor. Namely

Theorem 6.5
:

X\ \text{is Scott-complete}\iff{\mathscr{Q}\text{-}\mathbf{Set}}_{f}({\underline{\hskip 5.0pt}},X)\cong{\mathscr{Q}\text{-}\mathbf{Set}}_{f}(\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}({\underline{\hskip 5.0pt}}),X)

here again we actually wrote something slightly informal
and we really mean that,
{\mathscr{Q}\text{-}\mathbf{Set}}_{f}(i\circ\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}({\underline{\hskip 5.0pt}}),X)

is isomorphic to
{\mathscr{Q}\text{-}\mathbf{Set}}_{f}({\underline{\hskip 5.0pt}},X)
. We can rewrite it as

\operatorname{\!\text{\char 135\relax}}_{f}(X)\cong\operatorname{\!\text{\char 135\relax}}_{f}(X)\circ i\circ\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}

Proof.

Scott-completeness implies the existence of the isomorphism
since
X
as an object of
{\mathscr{Q}\text{-}\mathbf{Set}}_{f}
is just
i(X)
for

X
an object of
\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\!{\mathscr{Q}\text{-}\mathbf{Set}}
, and hence adjunction
applies:

\displaystyle{\mathscr{Q}\text{-}\mathbf{Set}}_{f}({\underline{\hskip 5.0pt}},X)

\displaystyle={\mathscr{Q}\text{-}\mathbf{Set}}_{f}({\underline{\hskip 5.0pt}},i(X))

\displaystyle\cong\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\!{\mathscr{Q}\text{-}\mathbf{Set}}(\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}({\underline{\hskip 5.0pt}}),X)

\displaystyle={\mathscr{Q}\text{-}\mathbf{Set}}_{f}(i\circ\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}({\underline{\hskip 5.0pt}}),i(X))

\displaystyle={\mathscr{Q}\text{-}\mathbf{Set}}_{f}(\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}({\underline{\hskip 5.0pt}}),X)

Now, provided with the isomorphism but no guarantee
that
X
is complete, we have to find sufficient
reason for
X
to be complete.

\displaystyle\operatorname{\!\text{\char 135\relax}}_{f}(X)

\displaystyle\cong\operatorname{\!\text{\char 135\relax}}_{f}(X)\circ i\circ\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\!{\mathscr{Q}\text{-}\mathbf{Set}}_{f}(\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}({\underline{\hskip 5.0pt}}),X)

\displaystyle\cong\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\!{\mathscr{Q}\text{-}\mathbf{Set}}_{r}(\operatorname{\mathcal{G}\!\mathcal{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\circ\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}({\underline{\hskip 5.0pt}}),\operatorname{\mathcal{G}\!\mathcal{\scriptscriptstyle R}}(X))

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\!{\mathscr{Q}\text{-}\mathbf{Set}}_{r}(\operatorname{\mathcal{G}\!\mathcal{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\circ\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}({\underline{\hskip 5.0pt}}),X)

\displaystyle\cong\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\!{\mathscr{Q}\text{-}\mathbf{Set}}_{r}(\operatorname{\mathcal{G}\!\mathcal{\scriptscriptstyle R}}({\underline{\hskip 5.0pt}}),X)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\!\text{\char 135\relax}}_{r}(X)\restriction\operatorname{\mathcal{G}\!\mathcal{\scriptscriptstyle R}}

∎

Proposition 6.1
:

The true force of the theorem above is that
i
in

\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\dashv i
not only preserves limits but
indeed it creates them.

Proof.

Take a diagram
D
on
\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\!{\mathscr{Q}\text{-}\mathbf{Set}}
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\!\text{\char 135\relax}}_{f}(\lim i\circ D)

\displaystyle\cong\lim\operatorname{\!\text{\char 135\relax}}_{f}(i\circ D)

\displaystyle\cong\lim\operatorname{\!\text{\char 135\relax}}(i\circ D)\circ i\circ\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}

\displaystyle\cong\operatorname{\!\text{\char 135\relax}}(\lim i\circ D)\circ i\circ\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}

This shows that the external (ie. in
{\mathscr{Q}\text{-}\mathbf{Set}}_{f}
) the limit
of complete
\mathscr{Q}
-sets is itself complete.

Since any cone for
D
– say, with vertex
X
– would

be
a cone for
i\circ D
(as
i
is simply an
inclusion, they literally are) – by universality there is
exactly one morphism
\lim i\circ D\to X
making
everything commute.

Since the external limit is now known to be complete, by
necessity, it also
is
the limit in
\operatorname{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\!{\mathscr{Q}\text{-}\mathbf{Set}}
as
well. Hence,
i
both preserves all limits from its
domain, and reflects all limits in its codomain.
∎
