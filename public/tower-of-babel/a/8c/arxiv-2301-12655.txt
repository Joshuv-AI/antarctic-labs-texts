Endomorphisms of a certain ring of rational functions
by Milo Moses

Abstract




1
Introduction

For any field
K
, define the ring
\mathcal{M}_{K}
as follows:

\begin{pmatrix}\text{elements: ratios of monic polynomials in $K(x)$}\\
\text{addition: multiplication of rational functions}\\
\text{multiplication: }\left[\prod_{i}(x-\alpha_{i})\right]\cdot\left[\prod_{j}(x-\beta_{j})\right]=\left[\prod_{i,j}(x-\alpha_{i}\beta_{j})\right]\end{pmatrix}

This sort of ring can naturally arise in the study of vector spaces. Endomorphisms of vector spaces have associated characteristic polynomials, and the direct sum/tensor product of endomorphisms corresponds to addition/multiplication in
\mathcal{M}_{K}
. We will put a topology on
\mathcal{M}_{K}
by letting Cauchy sequences be those in which the leading coefficients converge. We will later show that it makes
\mathcal{M}_{K}
into a topological ring. The following is the main theorem proved in this text:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
K/\mathbb{Q}
be an algebraic extension, and let
\phi:\mathcal{M}_{K}\to\mathcal{M}_{K}
be a continuous ring endomorphism. Then, either

1.

There exist integers
k,s\geq 0
and
\sigma\in\mathrm{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})
such that

\phi\left(\prod_{i}(x-\alpha_{i})\right)=x^{\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{s}-1}\cdot\prod_{i}\left(x-\sigma(\alpha_{i})^{k}\right)

2.

All roots of rational functions in the image of
\phi
are roots of unity. If
K/\mathbb{Q}
is finite, then there exists
N
such that all the roots are
N
th roots of unity.

The constant
N
appearing in part (2) of Theorem
1.1
is only effective when
K=\mathbb{Q}
. In that case one can easily compute
N
given (say,)
\phi([x-2])
using the methods of the proof. This can be turned into precise results, such as
N<\left(\#\text{ roots of }\phi([x-2])\right)^{1.8}
.

1
1

1

The result here is not proved explicitly, but it is a simple corollary of our methods. The constant 1.8 is not optimal, and can be readily improved to
1/\ln(2)+\epsilon

General Methods

In this section, we will establish some general results about
\mathcal{M}_{K}
, with
K
being an arbitrary field. Once convenient, we will specialize to
K\subseteq\mathbb{C}
. Our main tool will be the ring morphisms
t_{k}:\mathcal{M}_{K}\to K
defined on elements of the form
[x-\alpha]
by

t_{k}([x-\alpha])=\alpha^{k},

and extended to general elements by linearity. Note that we use the convention
0^{0}=1
, so
t_{0}
is the degree map. A first property of these maps is that they can be used to compute polynomial coefficients:

Theorem 2.1

(Newton’s Formulas)
.

For any polynomial
f\in K[x]
of degree
d
and
k\leq d
, we have that

ke_{k}(f)=\sum_{n=1}^{k}(-1)^{n+1}e_{k-n}(f)t_{n}([f]).

Here,
e_{n}(f)
are the elementary symmetric polynomial coefficients defined implicitly by

f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{d}(-1)^{d-n}e_{d-n}(f)x^{n}.

Proof.

These are classical.
∎

Corollary 2.1
.

If
K
has characteristic
0
, then for any monic polynomials
f,g\in K[x]
,
e_{k}(f)=e_{k}(g)
for all
k<N
if and only of
t_{k}([f])=t_{k}([g])
for all
k<N
.

Proof.

This is immediate from the Theorem.
∎

In particular, we have that the
t_{k}
define an injection

\mathcal{M}_{K}\xhookrightarrow{\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}t_{k}}\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}K.

This allows us to define the topology on
\mathcal{M}_{K}
as the subspace topology, with
\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}K
given the product topology, and
K
always given the discrete topology. It is clear that convergence in
\mathcal{M}_{K}
is equivalent to the convergence of the
t_{k}
, which in turn is equivalent to the convergence of the
e_{k}
, which are the leading coefficients of a polynomial. We will now show to what extent
t_{k}
are the only continuous ring morphisms
\mathcal{M}_{K}\to K
.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
S
is a subring of
K^{N}
for some natural number
N
, then the nonzero morphisms in
\mathrm{Hom}(S,K)
are projections of
S
onto a single index, followed by composition with some ring morphism
K\to K
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
N
. If
N=1
, then
S
is a subring of
K
. Thus, the map
S\to K
is automatically projection of
S
onto a single index followed by a morphism. Additionally, it is standard application of Zorn’s lemma that every map
S\to K
can be lifted to a map
K\to K
.

For our inductive step, suppose the result is proved for
N-1
. Fix a map
\phi:S\to\mathbb{Z}
. It is clear that
\phi
doesn’t factor through the projection
L\to L^{\prime}
forgetting the
1
st index if and only if there are two elements which are equal outside of the
1
st index, but give different elements under
\phi
. Equivalently, this means that there is a nonzero element
(a_{1},0,0...)
on which
\phi
does not act by
0
.

If
\phi
factors through the projection forgetting the
1
st or
2
nd index, then the induction hypothesis gives us the conclusion. Suppose it does not factor through forgetting either. This means there are elements
(a_{1},0,0...)
and
(0,a_{2},0...)
under which
\phi
does not act by
0
. However, these elements multiply to
0
. This implies there exist nonzero elements of
K
which multiply to
0
, which is a contradiction. This completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 2.1
.

The only continuous ring morphisms
\phi:\mathcal{M}_{K}\to K
are of the form
\sigma\circ t_{k}
, where
\sigma:K\to K
is a ring morphism.

Proof.

Since
K
is discrete, the kernel of
\phi
contains an open neighborhood of
0
. The ideals

I_{N}=\left\{r\,\,\mathrm{st}\,\,t_{k}(r)=0\,\,\forall\,\,k\geq N\right\}

form a basis of open neighborhoods of
0
under the topology for
\mathcal{M}_{K}
. Hence, we have that
I_{N}\in\ker\phi
for some
N
. Hence, any continuous map
\mathcal{M}_{K}\to K
factors through projection onto
\mathcal{M}_{K}/I_{N}
. This is a subring of
K^{N}
, hence Lemma
2.1
gives the desired result.
∎

This is extremely powerful, as it lets us define the following:

Definition 2.1
.

For any continuous endomorphisms
\phi:\mathcal{M}_{K}\to\mathcal{M}_{K}
, we define the functions
\phi^{*}:\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
\sigma^{\phi}:\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\to\left(\text{morphisms }K\to K\right)
to be the unique values such that

t_{n}(\phi(r))=\sigma^{\phi}_{n}(t_{\phi^{*}(n)}(r))

for all
r\in\mathcal{M}_{K}
.

We now define the most important endomorphisms of
\mathcal{M}_{K}
:

Proposition 2.2
.

For every
n\geq 1
, the map

\lambda_{n}:\mathcal{M}_{K}\to\mathcal{M}_{K}

sending
\prod_{i}(x-\alpha_{i})
to
\prod_{i}(x-\alpha_{i}^{n})
is a continuous endomorphism of
\mathcal{M}_{K}
. We have that
\lambda_{k}^{*}(n)=kn
for all
k,n
, and
\sigma_{n}^{\lambda_{k}}=1
.

Proof.

This is a straightforward computation.
∎

The following formula will underpin our use of cyclotomic methods throughout the proof.

Corollary 2.2
.

If
K
has characteristic
0
, then for any
f\in\overline{K}[x]
,

x^{d}f(1/x)=\prod_{n\geq 1}\left(1-x^{n}\right)^{c_{n}}

where

c_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{d|n}\mu(n/d)t_{d}([f]).

The quantities
(1+x)^{z}
are defined using Newton’s generalized binomial theorem.

Proof.

This is the so-called Cyclotomic identity, and can be found in many references.
∎

Let
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Z}}
be the subring of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Q}}
consisting of rational functions with integer coefficients.

Proposition 2.3
.

For any
r\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Z}}
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{d|n}\mu(n/d)t_{d}(r)\in\mathbb{Z}.

Proof.

We prove the result for
r=[f]
,
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
monic, and extend to a general element by linearity. Let
N
be the smallest number for which
c_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{d|N}\mu(n/d)t_{d}(r)
is not in
\mathbb{Z}
. Now, we look at the coefficient of
x^{N}
on both sides of

x^{d}f(1/x)=\prod_{n\geq 1}\left(1-x^{n}\right)^{c_{n}}.

The left hand side’s coefficient is an element of
\mathbb{Z}
by assumption. On the right hand side, terms with
n>N
do not contribute since they are too large. By assumption, all terms with
n<N
contribute an element of
\mathbb{Z}
. Thus, the final answer is an element of
R
if and only if the coefficient of
x^{N}
in
\left(1-x^{N}\right)^{c_{N}}
is in
\mathbb{Z}
. The coefficient is exactly
-c_{N}
and hence
c_{N}\in\mathbb{Z}
which is a contradiction. This yields the result.
∎

The following lemmas are necessary to continue:

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
s_{k}
be any sequence of complex numbers given explicitly by

s_{k}=\sum_{j}c_{j}\theta_{j}^{k}

where
j
runs over a finite set of values,
c_{j}
are complex, and
\theta_{j}
are on the unit circle. There are infinitely many
q\geq 1
such that

|s_{k}-s_{k+q}|\leq\frac{4\pi}{q^{1/J}}\sum_{i}|c_{i}|

for every
k\geq 0
, where
J
is the number of values
j
runs over. For every
k_{0}
,
s_{k_{0}}
is a limit point of
s_{k}
. In particular, if
s_{k}
converges then it must be constant. The only way for
s_{k}
to be constant is for the coefficients of all roots other than
1
to be zero.

Proof.

To begin, we assume without loss of generality that all the
\theta_{j}
are distinct. Let
\theta_{j}=e^{2\pi ix_{j}}
. By Dirichlet’s Simultaneous Approximation theorem, we can find infinitely many values
q
for which there exists
p_{j}
such that

\left|x_{j}-\frac{p_{j}}{q}\right|\leq\frac{1}{q^{1+1/J}}.

Using the estimate
\left|e^{x}-1\right|\leq 2x
for
\left|x\right|<1
, we get

\displaystyle\left|\exp\left(2\pi iqx_{j}\right)-1\right|\leq\frac{4\pi}{q^{1/J}}.

Choosing any
k\geq 1
and summing over
j
, this immediately gives

\displaystyle\left|s_{k}-s_{k+q}\right|\leq\frac{4\pi}{q^{1/J}}\sum_{i}\left|c_{i}\right|.

Since this tends to
0
as
q
gets large, it is clear that every value of
s_{k}
must be a limit point.

Now, suppose that
s_{k}
is a constant. By subtracting off the coefficient of
1
, we can assume
s_{k}=0
uniformly. The average value of
|s_{k}|^{2}
is
\sum_{j}|c_{j}|^{2}
, and hence we must have
c_{j}=0
for all
j
, as desired.
∎

Sometimes the following will also be useful:

Proposition 2.5
.

If
r\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}}
is such that
t_{k}(r)
goes over finitely many values, then all roots of
r
are roots of unity.

Proof.

If
r
has any roots greater than
1
, then Proposition
2.4
gives that
t_{k}(r)
tends to infinity. Hence, we must have that all roots of
r
are in absolute value less than or equal to
1
. We thus have

r=\prod_{i}(x-\theta_{i})^{c}_{i}\cdot r^{\prime},

where
\theta_{i}
are distinct complex numbers of absolute value
1
, and all roots of
r^{\prime}
are less than
1
. Letting
E<1
be the largest root of
r^{\prime}
, we get that

t_{k}(r)=S_{k}+O(E^{n})

where
S_{k}=\sum_{i}c_{i}\cdot\theta_{i}^{k}
, and
O
denotes big-Oh notation. Choose
q
large so that
|S_{k+q}-S_{k}|
is uniformly small, which is possible by Proposition
2.4
. Then, we would have that

t_{k+q}(r)-t_{k}(r)

is uniformly small as
q
gets large, for large enough
k
. However, since
t_{k}(r)
goes over finitely values, this must imply that
t_{k}(r)=t_{k+q}(r)
for all large enough
k
. In particular, we must have that
S_{k+q}-S_{k}
grows small at an exponentially small rate. Proposition
2.4
says this means that
S_{k}
is uniformly zero. Hence, we get that
\theta_{i}^{q}=1
for all
q
.

Now, consider the roots of
r
smaller than
1
. Since
S_{k}
is periodic, we must have that the
t_{k}
of the smaller roots must go over finitely many values. However, the
t_{k}
of the smaller roots also tend to zero. The only way to go over finitely many values and tend to zero is if the
t_{k}
is zero for all large enough roots. The only way to be zero for large enough roots is if there are no roots of absolute value less than
1
, by repeating the same as above.
∎

The above work motivates the following definition:

Proposition 2.6
.

The following conditions on an element
r\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Z}}
are equivalent:

1.

r=[f/g]
, where the roots of
f
and
g
are all of absolute value
1
or
0

2.

r=[f/g]
, where the roots of
f
and
g
are all of absolute value
\leq 1

3.

\lim_{k\to\infty}t_{k}(r)/\left(1+\epsilon\right)^{k}=0
for all
\epsilon>0

4.

r=c_{0}[x]+\sum_{n\geq 1}c_{n}\left[1-x^{-n}\right]
, where
c_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
are constants only finitely of which are non-zero

The subring of elements
r\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Z}}
with any (all) of these equivalent properties is called
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Z},\Phi}
.

Proof.

We begin by showing that
2\implies 1
. To do this, we note that the smallest non-zero coefficient of
f
is equal to the product of its non-zero roots. This product must be a non-zero integer, and since none of the roots are larger than
1
they must all be of absolute value
1
.

Now we show that
3\implies 2
. Let
C
be the absolute value of the largest root of
r
. If
C\leq 1
, we are done. If
C>1
, write

t_{n}(r)=S_{n}\cdot C^{n}+O(E^{n})

,

where
S_{n}=\sum_{i}c_{i}\cdot\theta_{i}^{n}
,
|\theta_{i}|=1
, and
E<C
. Then,

S_{n}=\frac{t_{n}(r)}{C^{n}}+O((E/C)^{n}),

so
S_{n}\to 0
. Thus
S_{n}
meets the conditions of Lemma
2.4
, and hence is zero constant. This contradicts the fact that there is at least one root of absolute value
C
, since otherwise the sum would be non-trivial.

Now we show that
4\implies 3
. To do this, we note that

t_{k}\left(c_{0}[x]+\sum_{n\geq 1}c_{n}\left[1-x^{-n}\right]\right)

is periodic and hence tends to zero when divided by arbitrarily large values.

Finally we show that
1\implies 4
. We induct on
\deg(f)+\deg(g)
. If
\deg(f)\geq 1
with root
\zeta
, then
\zeta
is an algebraic number all of whose Galois conjugates are also of absolute value one and hence must be a primitive root of unity of degree
n
,
\zeta=\zeta_{n}
, by a classical theorem of Kronecker. Thus,
\Phi_{n}(x)|\deg(f)
where
\Phi_{n}
is the
n
th cyclotomic polynomial and hence

[f/g]=\left[\left(f/\Phi_{n}(x)\right)/g\right]+\sum_{d|n}\mu(n/d)\left[x^{d}-1\right].

Continuing until the only roots of
f
and
g
are all zero, we are done.
∎

We define
\widehat{\mathcal{M}_{K}}
to be the topological completion of
\mathcal{M}_{K}
.

Proposition 2.7
.

The subring
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Z},\Phi}
is dense in
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Z}}
. Every element
r\in\widehat{\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Z},\Phi}}=\widehat{\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Z}}}
can be written uniquely in the form

r=c_{0}[x]+\sum_{n\geq 1}c_{n}\left[1-x^{-n}\right],

for arbitrary
c_{n}
. Moreover, for
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}

t_{k}(r)=\sum_{d|k}c_{d}

and
t_{0}(r)=c_{0}
.

Proof.

To begin, we show that every element of the form
r=c_{0}[x]+\sum_{n\geq 1}c_{n}\left[1-x^{-n}\right]
is in
\widehat{\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Z},\Phi}}
. That follows from the fact that

r=\lim_{N\to\infty}\left(c_{0}[x]+\sum_{n=1}^{N}c_{n}\left[1-x^{-n}\right]\right),

where each term in the limit is in
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Z},\Phi}
by Proposition
2.6
part 3. Next, we show that these sums follow the
t_{k}
formula stated. Namely, we begin by checking

\displaystyle t_{k}\left([x^{n}-1]\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}e^{2\pi ji/k}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}n&n|k\\
0&\mathrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Hence, dividing through by
x^{n}
, we get the same formula for
t_{k}\left([1-x^{-n}]\right)
except that it has no contribution at
0
. Extending by linearity we get the formula. Uniqueness follows from Mobius inversion.

For the last part of the proposition, we check that every element of
\widehat{\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Z}}}
really can be written in this form. let
r_{N}
be a sequence in
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Z}}
with
r
as a limit point. Let

c_{n}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{d|n}\mu(n/d)t_{d}(r_{N}),

which converges since
t_{k}
are continuous. Moreover,
c_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
since by Proposition
2.3
they are at every step in the limit. It is now clear that
r=c_{0}[x]+\sum_{n\geq 1}c_{n}[1-x^{-n}]
by Möbius inversion.
∎

We now prove an important multiplication formula on
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Z},\Phi}
:

Proposition 2.8
.

For any
n,m
it holds that

\left[x^{n}-1\right]\left[x^{m}-1\right]=\gcd(n,m)\left[x^{\mathrm{lcm}(n,m)}-1\right]

Proof.

This is a trivial computation that follows directly from the definition of multiplication in
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Z}}
.
∎

3
Endomorphisms of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Q}}

In this section, we study
\mathrm{End}\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Q}}\right)
. A natural place to start is to study maps on the smaller domain
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Z},\Phi}
:

Proposition 3.1
.

If
\phi\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Z},\Phi}\to\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}}
, is continuous then for all rational prime powers
p^{\alpha}
we have that

\phi\left(\left[x^{p^{\alpha}}-1\right]\right)=s_{-\infty}[x]+\sum_{\beta=0}^{\alpha}s_{\beta}\cdot p^{\alpha-\beta}\cdot\left[x^{p^{\beta}}-1\right],

where
s_{\beta}\in\{-1,0,1\}
.

Proof.

Seeing as
t_{k}(\phi(r))
must go over finitely values, Proposition
2.5
gives us that
\phi(r)
lies in
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Z},\Phi}
. Let
r=\phi\left(\left[x^{p^{\alpha}}-1\right]\right)
, and let
c_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{d|n}\mu(n/d)t_{d}(r)
. Choose
N
such that
c_{N}\neq 0
. Write
N=p^{\gamma}N^{\prime}
, with
p
not dividing
N^{\prime}
. We begin by showing that
\gamma\leq\alpha
. Namely we compute

\displaystyle p^{\gamma}N^{\prime}\leq p^{\gamma}N^{\prime}|c_{N}|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{d|p^{\gamma}N^{\prime}}\mu(p^{\gamma}N^{\prime}/d)t_{d}(r)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{d|N^{\prime}}\mu(N^{\prime}/d)\left(t_{p^{\gamma}d}(r)-t_{p^{\gamma-1}d}(r)\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{d|N^{\prime}}p^{\alpha}\leq p^{\alpha}N^{\prime}.

Hence,
\gamma\leq\alpha
as desired. Note that in the case
\gamma=0
the formula written is not strictly correct (i.e, there is not
p^{\gamma-1}d
correction term), but since
\alpha\geq 1
our proposition is true a posteriori in this case. Now, we have that
Nc_{N}
is a multiple of
p^{\alpha}
. In particular,
c_{N}
must be a multiple of
p^{\alpha-\gamma}
. We use this to bound
N^{\prime}
. Namely, from before we have the inequality

p^{\gamma}N^{\prime}|c_{N}|\leq\sum_{d|N^{\prime}}p^{\alpha}.

Seeing as
|c_{N}|\geq p^{\alpha-\gamma}
, we have equality if and only if
c_{N}=\pm p^{\alpha-\gamma}
and
N^{\prime}=\sum_{d|N^{\prime}}1
. This means
N^{\prime}=1
or
N^{\prime}=2
.

We now show that
N^{\prime}=2
is impossible. Let
\gamma
now be the smallest value (if it exists) such that
c_{2p^{\gamma}}\neq 0
. Then,

\displaystyle t_{2p^{\gamma}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{d|2p^{\gamma}}dc_{d}

\displaystyle=\sum_{d|p^{\gamma}}dc_{d}+2p^{\gamma}c_{2p^{\gamma}}

\displaystyle=t_{p^{\gamma}}\pm 2p^{\alpha}.

Since
t_{p^{\gamma}}
and
t_{2p^{\gamma}}
both equal
0
or
p^{\alpha}
, it is impossible. Hence,
c_{2p^{\gamma}}=0
for all
\gamma
. This is the desired result.
∎

An important element to study in endomorphisms is
\left[x\right]
. We classify the possible places it can go:

Proposition 3.2
.

For all
\phi\in\mathrm{End}\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Q}}\right)
, we must have that
\phi\left(\left[x\right]\right)
is one of the following:
[x]
,
0
,
[x-1]
, or
[(x-1)/x]
.

Proof.

As before,
t_{k}([x])
takes on only the values
0
or
1
. If
\phi([x])=c_{0}[x]+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n}\left[x^{n}-1\right]
, then

\left|nc_{n}\right|=\left|\sum_{d|n}\mu(n/d)t_{d}(\phi([x]))\right|\leq\sum_{d|n}1

and so if
c_{n}\neq 0
then
n\in\{0,1,2\}
. Moreover, in those cases we must have
c_{n}=1
is in those cases. Looking over the finite number of options, we get the desired result
∎

The cases
\phi([x])=[x-1]
or
\phi([x])=[(x-1)/x])
can be treated with ease:

Proposition 3.3
.

The following properties of an endomorphism in
\mathrm{End}\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Q}}\right)
are equivalent:

1.

\forall

k\geq 1
,
\phi^{*}(k)=0

2.

\exists

k\geq 1
s.t
\phi^{*}(k)=0

3.

\phi\left(\left[x\right]\right)=[x-1]
or
[(x-1)/x]

4.

\exists

k
s.t
\phi(r)=t_{0}(r)[x-1]+\left(t_{k}(r)-t_{0}(r)\right)[x]

\forall

r\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Q}}

Proof.

(1)\implies(2)
. Trivial.

(2)\implies(3)
. Let
k\geq 1
be the value such that
\phi^{*}(k)=0
. Then,
t_{k}\left(\phi\left(\left[x\right]\right)\right)=t_{\phi^{*}(k)}\left(\left[x\right]\right)=1
. Hence, we cannot have
\phi\left(\left[x\right]\right)
be
0
or
[x]
and so by Proposition
3.2
we get the result.

(3)\implies(4)
. If
\phi([x])=[(x-1)/x]
, then

\displaystyle\phi\left([x]r\right)

\displaystyle=\phi\left(t_{0}(r)[x]\right)

\displaystyle=t_{0}(r)\left[(x-1)/x\right]=t_{0}(r)[x-1]-t_{0}(r)[x]

\displaystyle=\phi(r)[(x-1)/x]=\phi(r)-t_{0}(\phi(r))[x]

so collecting we get that

\phi(r)=t_{0}(r)[x-1]+\left(t_{\phi^{*}(0)}(r)-t_{0}(r)\right)[x].

We proceed similarly when
\phi([x])=[x-1]
.

(4)\implies(1)
. It is an unenlightening computation that every
\phi\in\mathrm{End}\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Q}}\right)
of the form in (4) is an endomorphism. For any
j\geq 1
,

t_{j}\left(t_{0}(r)[x-1]+\left(t_{\phi^{*}(0)}(r)-t_{0}(r)\right)[x]\right)=t_{0}(r)

so
\phi^{*}(j)=0
and hence
(1)
is implied. Combining these implications, we obtain our result.
∎

We now analyze the remaining case,
\phi^{*}(1)\neq 0
. The first step is to study the behavior of
\phi^{*}
more closely.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
\phi\in\mathrm{End}\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Q}}\right)
with
\phi^{*}(1)\neq 0
, then
v_{p}(\phi^{*}(k))
depends only on
v_{p}(k)
.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.3
,
\phi^{*}(1)\neq 0
implies
\phi^{*}(k)\neq 0
for all
k
and hence
v_{p}(\phi^{*}(k))
will all be well defined. By the classification given in Lemma
3.1
, we get that
t_{k}(\phi([x^{p^{\alpha}}-1]))
depends only on
v_{p}(k)
. In turn,
t_{k}(\phi([x^{p^{\alpha}}-1]))
as
\alpha
varies determines
v_{p}(\phi^{*}(k))
and hence we are done.
∎

Proposition 3.4
.

If
\phi^{*}(1)\neq 0
,
\phi^{*}(n)/\phi^{*}(1)
is multiplicative, and sends powers of
p
to powers of
p
.

Proof.

If
n
is a power of
p
, then
v_{q}(n)=v_{q}(n)
for all
q\neq 0
, and hence
v_{q}(\phi^{*}(n))=v_{q}(\phi^{*}(1))
for all
q\neq p
and hence
\phi^{*}(n)/\phi^{*}(1)
is a power of
p
. Thus, in general,

\displaystyle\phi^{*}(n)

\displaystyle=\prod_{p}p^{v_{p}(\phi^{*}(n))}

\displaystyle=\prod_{p^{\alpha}||n}p^{v_{p}(\phi^{*}(p^{\alpha}))}\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p^{\alpha}||\phi^{*}(1)\\
p\nmid n\end{subarray}}p^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\phi^{*}(1)\cdot\prod_{p^{\alpha}||n}p^{v_{p}(\phi^{*}(p^{\alpha}))-v_{p}(\phi^{*}(1))}

and hence
\phi^{*}(n)/\phi^{*}(1)
is multiplicative.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose
\phi^{*}(1)\neq 0
, and
\phi\left(\left[x-k\right]\right)
has a root of absolute value greater than
1
for some
k\geq 2
. Then,

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{\phi^{*}(n)}{n}

is a nonzero rational number.

Proof.

Let
C
be the absolute value of the largest root of
r=\phi([x-k])
. By assumption,
C>1
. Letting
E<C
be the absolute value of the second largest root of
\phi([x-k])
, we find that

\displaystyle k^{\phi^{*}(n)}

\displaystyle=t_{\phi^{*}(n)}\left([x-k]\right)

\displaystyle=t_{n}\left(r\right)

\displaystyle=S_{n}\cdot C^{n}+O\left(E^{n}\right),

where
S_{n}=\sum_{j}c_{j}\theta_{j}^{n}
,
c_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}\neq 0
, and
|\theta_{j}|=1
are all distinct. If
S_{N}
were negative or complex for some
N
, then
S_{N}
would be a limit point of
S_{n}
by Lemma
2.4
. This is a contradiction since then we would have that
k^{\phi^{*}(n)}
is negative or complex for some
n
. We can thus take logarithms, and get that

\phi^{*}(n)=\log(C)n+\log(S_{n}+O((E/C)^{n})).

Here, by
\log
we mean logarithm base
k
. It is standard that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n<N}|S_{n}|^{2}=\sum_{j}|c_{j}|^{2}.

Hence,
S_{n}\geq 1
for a positive proportion of
n
since
S_{n}
is bounded above. In particular, we find that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\phi^{*}(n)}{n}=\log(C)>0.

Choose
N\geq 1
such that
S_{N}\neq 0
. Choose
q
large such that

\left|S_{n}-S_{n+q}\right|\ll\frac{1}{q^{1/J}},

where
J\geq 1
is a fixed integer. There are infinitely many such
q
by Lemma
2.4
. We find uniformly over
m\ll q^{1/J}

\displaystyle\left|\phi^{*}(N+mq)-\log(C)\cdot mq\right|

\displaystyle\ll_{N}

\displaystyle\left|\phi^{*}(N+mq)-\log(C)\cdot(N+mq)+\log(S_{N+mq})\right|

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left|\log(S_{N+mq})-\log(S_{N})\right|

\displaystyle\ll_{N}

\displaystyle 1.

We now manipluate to find that when
N^{2}m<q^{1/J}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\phi^{*}(N)}\left|\phi^{*}(N+(N^{2}m)q)-\log(C)\cdot(N^{2}m)q\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{\phi^{*}(1+m(Nq))}{\phi^{*}(1)}-\frac{N\log(C)}{\phi^{*}(N)}\cdot m(Nq)\right|

\displaystyle\ll_{N}1.

Now, we observe the following. By Proposition
3.4
,
\phi^{*}(p^{a})/\phi^{*}(1)
is a power of
p
for each prime
p
and
a\geq 1
. If
\phi^{*}(p^{a})/\phi^{*}(1)\geq p^{a+1}
, then
\phi^{*}(p^{a})/p^{a}\geq\phi^{*}(1)p
.Since
\phi^{*}(n)\leq\log(C)n+O(1)
, this can only happen for finitely many
p
. In particular, let
m
be the product of all primes for which
\phi^{*}(p^{a})/\phi^{*}(1)\geq p^{a+1}
for some
a\geq 1
. Then
1+m(Nq)
is relatively prime to all of these primes, and hence

\frac{\phi^{*}(1+m(Nq))}{\phi^{*}(1)(1+m(Nq))}

will be the reciprocal of an integer. Hence, since

\left|\frac{\phi^{*}(1+m(Nq))}{\phi^{*}(1)(1+m(Nq))}-\frac{N\log(C)}{\phi^{*}(N)}\right|\ll_{N}\frac{1}{q}

for large enough
q
, we have that
\frac{N\log(C)}{\phi^{*}(N)}
can be approximated aribitarily well by reciprocals of integers. Seeing as the set of reciprocals of integers is discrete, this means that
\frac{N\log(C)}{\phi^{*}(N)}
itself is the reciprocal of an integer. In particular,
\log(C)
is rational and we get our result.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

In the language of Lemma
3.2
,
S_{n}=1
for all
n
.

Proof.

By the discussion of Lemma
3.2
,
S_{n}\geq\sqrt{\sum_{j}|c_{j}|^{2}}
for a positive proportion of
n
. Suppose that
\sum_{j}|c_{j}|^{2}=1
. Then, clearly,
S_{n}=\theta^{n}
for a single value
\theta
. To make
S_{n}
a positive real number for each
n
, we must have
\theta=1
. This gives the desired result.

The other case is that
\sum_{j}|c_{j}|^{2}\neq 1
. Here, we get that
S_{n}\geq\sqrt{2}
for a positive proportion of
n
. For these values, we have

\phi^{*}(n)=\log(C)n+\log(S_{n})+O((E/C)^{n}).

Let
D
be the denominator of
\log(C)
, which exists by Lemma
3.2
. We have

D\phi^{*}(n)=D\log(C)n+D\log(S_{n})+O((E/C)^{n}).

Since
S_{n}\geq\sqrt{2}
,
D\log(S_{n})
is bounded away from zero. Hence,

D\phi^{*}(n)=D\log(C)n+O(1),

where
O(1)
is a bounded size non-zero integer. Every prime dividing
\phi^{*}(n)
must either divide
\phi^{*}(1)
or
n
by Proposition
3.4
. If a prime divides both
\phi^{*}(n)
and
n
it must also divide
O(1)
, and hence be of bounded size. Hence, we get that there exists
B>0
such that
\phi^{*}(n)
is
B
-smooth for all
n
. The set of
B
-smooth numbers is of proportion zero in the naturals. In particular,
n/\log(C)
is always a bounded distance from a
B
-smooth number and hence must be running over a set of proportion zero in the naturals. However, we assumed that
n
was running over a set of positive density. This is a contradiction. Hence
S_{n}=1
for all
n
and we are done.
∎

Proposition 3.5
.

If
\phi^{*}(1)\neq 0
and
\phi\left(\left[x-k\right]\right)
has a root of absolute value greater than
1
for some

, then
\phi=\lambda_{\phi^{*}(1)}+(t_{\phi^{*}(0)}-t_{0})[x]
.

Proof.

In the languages of Lemmas
3.2
and
3.3
, we have that

\phi^{*}(n)=\log(C)n+O((E/C)^{n}).

Seeing as
\phi^{*}(n)
and
\log(C)n
are rational, this means that
\phi^{*}(n)=\log(C)n
for large enough

. By the multiplicativity of
\phi^{*}(n)/\phi^{*}(1)
, this means that
\log(C)=\phi^{*}(1)
, so
\phi^{*}(n)=\phi^{*}(1)n
for large enough
n
. Again by the multiplicativity of
\phi^{*}(n)/\phi^{*}(1)
, this means that
\phi^{*}(n)=\phi^{*}(1)n

. Introducing the
\phi^{*}(0)=0
, and hence
\phi^{*}(n)=\phi^{*}(1)n
for all

It is clear that
\lambda_{\phi^{*}(1)}^{*}(n)=\phi^{*}(1)n

to get the correct value of

∎

Lemma 3.4
.

If
\phi^{*}(1)\neq 0
and all the roots of

\phi\left(\left[1-x^{-n}\right]\right)=0

We find that

is bounded. Seeing as it is integral, we get that it ranges over finitely many values. Hence,

ranges over finitely many values. Thus, we have that

for all

, for all but finitely many

, by Proposition

. For these

, we find that

is zero for all

\phi(\left[x^{N}-1\right])=N[x]

∎

Theorem 3.1
.

If
\phi\in\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Q}})
, then either
\phi=\lambda_{k}+(t_{j}-t_{0})
for some

\phi\left(\left[1-x^{-n}\right]\right)=0
for all but finitely many

.

Proof.

If
\phi^{*}(1)=0
, then
\phi\left(\left[1-x^{-n}\right]\right)=0
for all but finitely many

by the
(2)\iff(3)
equivalence in Proposition
2.7
. Since every

can be written as an infinite sum of elements of the form
[1-x^{-n}]
by Proposition [ref], this means that
\phi(r)
will be an

4
Endomorphisms of
\mathcal{M}_{K}
,
K/\mathbb{Q}
algebraic

Let
\phi:\mathcal{M}_{K}\to\mathcal{M}_{K}
be a continuous ring endomorphism. By Proposition
2.1
, for every
n
there exist
\sigma^{\phi}_{n}\in\mathrm{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})
and
\phi^{*}(n)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
so that
t_{n}\circ\phi=\sigma^{\phi}_{n}\circ t_{\phi^{*}(n)}
. In particular, the property of
t_{n}
being rational for every
n
is preserved by
\phi
, and hence in turn the property of having rational coefficients is preserved, by Newton’s formulas.

Thus,
\phi
restricts to a map
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Q}}\to\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Q}}
. Theorem
3.1
says then that either
\phi^{*}(k)
is bounded uniformly with respect to
k
, or it is linear of the form
\phi^{*}(1)k
for
k\geq 1
. We now treat these two cases:

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\phi:\mathcal{M}_{K}\to\mathcal{M}_{K}
be a continuous ring endomorphism for which
\phi^{*}(k)
is bounded. Then all roots of elements in the image of
\phi
are roots of unity. If
K/\mathbb{Q}
is finite, then all the roots are
N
th roots for a fixed
N
.

Proof.

To begin, choose
r\in\mathcal{M}_{K}
. Then
t_{k}(\phi(r))=\sigma_{k}(t_{\phi^{*}(k)}(r))
runs over finitely many values, since
t_{\phi^{*}(k)}(r)
runs over finitely many values and
\mathrm{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})
can send those values to some subset of their (finitely many) Galois conjugates. By Proposition
2.5
, we get that all the roots of
\phi(r)
are roots of unity.

Suppose
K/\mathbb{Q}
is finite. Then,
t_{k}(\phi(r))
goes over finitely many values, and that finite quanity is now uniform as
r
varies. Suppose there were no
N
such that every
\phi(r)
were periodic mod
N
. Then, by taking weighted linear combinations of these values we could create elements
r
for which
\phi(r)
takes on arbitrarily many values. This is a contradiction, hence
\phi(r)
is periodic mod
N
for some uniform period
N
.

We now show that
r
being periodic mod
N
implies all of its nonzero roots are
N
th roots of unity, which in turn will imply our result. Write
t_{k}(r)=\sum_{i}c_{i}\alpha_{i}^{k}
. Seeing as this is periodic mod
N
, we find that

\sum_{i}c_{i}\alpha_{i}^{k_{0}}\left(\alpha_{i}^{N}\right)^{k}

is constant with respect to
k
for every
k_{0}
. Hence, we find that

\sum_{\alpha_{i}^{N}=\alpha}c_{i}\alpha_{i}^{k_{0}}=0

for all
\alpha\neq 1
. Thus, again by Proposition
2.5
, we get that
c_{i}=0
whenever
\alpha_{i}^{N}\neq 1
. In particular, all
\alpha_{i}
are
N
th roots of unity, so we are done.
∎

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
be an algebraic number. For all large enough integers
N
, none of the Galois conjugates of
\alpha^{\prime}=N\alpha+1
differ in ratio by a root of unity.

Proof.

Suppose that there are infinitely many
N
for which
N\alpha+1
and
N\sigma(\alpha)+1
differ in ratio by a root of unity, for some automorphism
\sigma
. Then there is an infinite sequence
\frac{\alpha+1/N}{\sigma(\alpha)+1/N}
of roots of unity. All of these terms have bounded algebraic degree over
\mathbb{Q}
, and hence they must all be
J
th roots of unity for some
J
. The set of
J
th roots of unity is discrete, and hence a non-constant convergent sequence of
J
th roots of unity is impossible. Thus, we get a contradiction and the desired result is obtained.
∎

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\phi:\mathcal{M}_{K}\to\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}}
be a continuous ring endomorphism for which
\phi^{*}(n)=\phi^{*}(1)n
for all
n\geq 1
. Then,
\sigma_{n}^{\phi}=\sigma_{1}^{\phi}
for all
n\geq 1
.

Proof.

Choose
\alpha\in K
. We wish to show that
\sigma^{\phi}_{n}(\alpha)=\sigma^{\phi}_{1}(\alpha)
. By Proposition
4.1
, we can assume without loss of generality no Galois conjugates of
\alpha
differ in ratio by root of unity. Considering where
\phi([x-\alpha])
goes, we find that

\sigma^{\phi}_{n}(\alpha)^{\phi^{*}(1)n}=t_{n}(\phi([x-\alpha]))=S_{n}\cdot C^{n}+O(E^{n}),

where
C
is a a real number,
E<C
, and
S_{n}=\sum_{i}c_{i}\cdot\theta_{i}^{n}
is a weighted sum of complex numbers of the unit circle. Taking absolute values and a logarithm, we get that uniformly for
n
such that
|S_{n}|\geq 6/5

\log|\sigma_{n}^{\phi}(\alpha)|\cdot\phi^{*}(1)n=\log(C)\cdot n+\log|S_{n}|+O((E/C)^{n}).

Taking ratios, we get

\log|\sigma_{n}^{\phi}(\alpha)|=\frac{\log(C)}{\phi^{*}(1)}+\frac{\log|S_{n}|}{\phi^{*}(1)n}+O((E/C)^{n}).

Seeing as
\sigma_{n}^{\phi}(\alpha)
go over a finite set as
n
varies, the only way to be arbitrarily well approximated by
\log(C)/\phi^{*}(1)
is if
\log|\sigma_{n}^{\phi}(\alpha)|=\frac{\log(C)}{\phi^{*}(1)}
for large enough
n
. Hence, for large enough
n
with
|S_{n}|\geq 6/5
we have

\frac{\log|S_{n}|}{\phi^{*}(1)n}=O((E/C)^{n}).

Seeing as
\log|S_{n}|
is bounded away from
0
, this can only happen for finitely many
n
. Hence,
|S_{n}|\geq 6/5
for only finitely many
n
. Hence,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}|S_{n}|^{2}=\sum_{i}|c_{i}|^{2}\leq(6/5)^{2}<2.

Seeing as the
|c_{i}|
are integers, for
\sum_{i}|c_{i}|^{2}
to be less than
2
we must have
c_{i}=1
for some
i
, and
c_{i}=0
for all other
i
. Hence,
S_{n}=\theta^{n}
for some
|\theta|=1
. In particular, we find that

\sigma^{\phi}_{n}(\alpha)^{\phi^{*}(1)n}=(\theta C)^{n}+O(E^{n})

for all large enough
n
. Hence,

\sigma^{\phi}_{n}(\alpha)^{\phi^{*}(1)n}=(\theta C)^{n}

for all
n\geq 1
. Hence,

\left(\frac{\sigma_{n}^{\phi}(\alpha)}{\sigma_{1}^{\phi}(\alpha)}\right)^{\phi^{*}(1)n}=1

for all
n\geq 1
. By our assumption on
\alpha
, we thus get that
\sigma_{n}^{\phi}(\alpha)=\sigma_{1}^{\phi}(\alpha)
. We get the desired conclusion.
∎

Combining the two cases, we get the desired result.
