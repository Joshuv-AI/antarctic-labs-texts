High-contrast random systems of PDEs: homogenisation and spectral theory
Matteo Capoferri, Mikhail Cherdantsev, Igor Velčić

Abstract
We develop a qualitative homogenisation and spectral theory for elliptic systems of partial differential equations in divergence form with highly contrasting (i.e., non uniformly elliptic) random coefficients. The focus of the paper is on the behaviour of the spectrum as the heterogeneity parameter tends to zero; in particular, we show that in general one doesn’t have Hausdorff convergence of spectra. The theoretical analysis is complemented by several explicit examples, showcasing the wider range of applications and physical effects of systems with random coefficients, when compared with systems with periodic coefficients or with scalar operators (both random and periodic). Keywords: high-contrast media, random media, stochastic homogenisation, systems of PDEs. 2020 MSC classes: primary 74S25, 74A40; secondary 35B27, 74Q15, 35B40, 60H15, 35P05.



1
Introduction

Homogenisation theory addresses the problem of determining effective properties of heterogeneous materials (
composites
). Over time, this has become a well-established branch of mathematics, and a large number of classical monographs are now available. Yet, due to breadth and relevance of the subject, mathematical and physical understanding of the properties of composites remains at the forefront of modern research in science and industry.

A very interesting class of problems — often referred to as
high-contrast problems
— is obtained by considering near-resonant inclusions dispersed in a base medium (often referred to as “matrix”), in such a way that the macroscopic properties of the material are accounted for by resonances occurring at the microscopic scale. Mathematically, an important family of such problems is modelled by second order elliptic operators of the form

\mathcal{D}^{\varepsilon}=-\nabla\cdot a_{\varepsilon}\nabla\vskip-5.69054pt

(1.1)

defined on bounded or unbounded domains of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where the coefficients
a_{\varepsilon}\in[L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})]^{d\times d}
, describing the physical properties of a material with scale of heterogeneity
\varepsilon
, are of order
\varepsilon^{2}
in the inclusions and of order
1
in the surrounding base medium. This critical
double-porosity
type scaling results in “micro-resonances”, which ultimately gives rise to numerous novel effects that are not present in the classical (uniformly elliptic) setting.
One such effect, and possibly the most important, is the band-gap structure of the spectrum, which makes high-contrast problems particularly relevant from the point of view of applications.

One of the mathematical challenges of working with high-contrast media is that the family of operators (
1.1
) is not uniformly elliptic as
\varepsilon\to 0
, which, in turn, results in a loss of compactness in the limit. In pioneering work, Allaire
[
2
]
showed, in the
periodic
setting, that the limiting
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{hom}}
, analogue of the homogenised operator, possesses a
two-scale
nature, that is, it accounts for both macroscopic and microscopic properties, coupled in a particular way. Zhikov
[
42
,
43
]
later defined and analysed the two-scale limit operator, and established spectral convergence. By decoupling the macroscopic and microscopic scales in the two-scale spectral problem
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{hom}}u=\lambda u
, one obtains a spectral problem for the macroscopic component
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{hom}}_{\mathrm{mac}}u_{1}(x)=\beta(\lambda)u_{1}(x)
with highly nonlinear dependence on the spectral parameter
\lambda
expressed via
Zhikov’s
\beta
-function
. The latter, being defined explicitly in terms of the resonant frequencies of the inclusions, characterises the macroscopic behaviour of the composite by virtue of capturing length-scale interactions. In particular, the spectrum of the limit operator is described by

\sigma(\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{hom}})=\{\lambda:\ \beta(\lambda)\geq 0\}\cup\{\text{eigenfrequencies of the inclusions}\},\vskip-5.69054pt

and, as inferred by the explicit representation of
\beta(\lambda)
, has infinitely many gaps.

The literature in the high-contrast periodic setting is very rich. The seminal results by Zhikov have been extended in numerous directions, encompassing both scalar operators and systems of PDEs, rigorously demonstrating new non-trivial properties, such as, for instance, memory effects and spatial nonlocality
[
15
,
6
]
. Note that even in the absence of high-contrast in the constituents, by carefully choosing the frequency of a Bloch wave type solution in relation to the wavenumber along the fibres one can
artificially
obtain a high-contrast operator on the cross-section of the material
[
18
]
. Amongst recent advancements, Cherednichenko, Ershova and Kiselev
[
13
]
established a correspondence between homogenisation limits of micro-resonant periodic composites and a class of time-dispersive media. Their interesting and new techniques, based on norm-resolvent estimates
(see also
[
12
]
for earlier work in this regard in the high-contrast setting)
, offer a recipe for the
design
of media with prescribed dispersive properties from periodic composites whose individual components are non-dispersive, opening the way to the possibility of interpreting high-contrast media as frequency-converting devices. These scalar results were generalised to the case of systems in
[
14
]
. Very recently, significant advancement in the subject was made by Cooper, Kamotski and Smyshlyaev: in
[
19
]
they developed a generic approach to study abstract family of asymptotically degenerating variational problems including, while going far beyond,
periodic
high-contrast highly oscillatory PDEs. Their asymptotic analysis yields uniform operator-type error estimate, and their novel approach provides approximations of the spectra of the associated spectral problems in terms of the spectrum of a certain “bivariate” operator. The latter arises as an abstract generalisation of the two-scale limiting operators traditionally
emerging
in the high-contrast literature.

Since fabricating perfectly periodic composites may be challenging from a practical point of view, it is natural to ask whether the properties of high-contrast materials desirable in applications (e.g., the band-gap structure of the spectrum) persist, in a quantitatively controllable manner, when the geometry of the inclusions, the coefficients of
the
operator, or both involve an element of randomness. Stochastic homogenisation, qualitatively set out in
[
31
,
35
,
32
]
, is nowadays a very lively field of research, with a big community concerned mainly with uniformly elliptic (non-high contrast) operators. A most challenging and delicate aspect of the stochastic theory is to obtain growth estimates in the appropriate norms for the
corrector
, a quantity that appears in the first-order term of the asymptotic expansion ansatz for the homogenisation problem. Comprehensive
quantitative
results were first obtained by Gloria, Neukamm and Otto in the early 2010’s in a series of ground-breaking papers
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
in which, building upon earlier results by Yurinskii
[
41
]
and Naddaf–Spencer
[
33
]
, they proved that the corrector is essentially of bounded growth. Similar results were obtained using a different (variational) argument by Armstrong, Smart, Kuusi and Mourrat, see e.g.
[
5
]
,
[
4
]
and references in the latter. These developments prompted an intensive research activity, which resulted in a number of new elegant ideas whose potential has not yet been fully explored.

For
high-contrast
operators with random coefficients the picture is completely different, in that much less is known and even the
qualitative
theory is still under development. Indeed, moving from high-contrast periodic to high-contrast stochastic PDEs involves a step change in both conceptual and technical difficulty. Stochastic two-scale resolvent convergence for high-contrast scalar operators on bounded domains and Hausdorff convergence of spectra were established by Cherdantsev, Cherednichenko and Velčić in
[
9
]
, building upon earlier results by Zhikov and Piatnitski
[
45
]
. While stochastic two-scale convergence works equally well in unbounded domains, the same authors showed
[
10
]
that in the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
the lack of compactness combined with the presence of infinitely many areas with “atypical” distributions of inclusions allows one to construct quasimodes for the random operator
\mathcal{D}^{\varepsilon}
“escaping to infinity”, namely, weakly converging to zero, as
\varepsilon\to 0
. The latter entails that generically the spectrum
\sigma(\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{hom}})
of the two-scale limiting operator, characterised by a stochastic version of
\beta(\lambda)
, is a proper subset of the limiting spectrum
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\sigma(\mathcal{D}^{\varepsilon})
, in turn characterised by
\beta_{\infty}(\lambda)
, a “local” modification of the stochastic
\beta(\lambda)
. In fact, it can be shown that the limiting spectrum has infinitely many gaps for a range of physically meaningful examples
[
10
, Section 4.6]
. Also in the scalar setting, the authors of the current paper studied defect modes for operators with random coefficients
[
8
]
. Finally, we should mention the paper
[
3
]
, in which the authors study the large time behaviour of a Markov process associated with a symmetric diffusion in a high-contrast random environment and characterise the limit semigroup and the limit process under the diffusive scaling. Although there are certain similarities with
[
10
]
, the setting of
[
3
]
is somewhat less general and the focus
is
quite different.

The properties of high-contrast
systems
with random coefficients are, to date, completely unexplored. Already in the periodic setting, high-contrast systems exhibit additional novel effects, compared with scalar operators.
For example, in the macroscopic component of the limiting spectral problem for systems the
\beta
-function is replaced by a
\bm{\beta}
-matrix, which can be thought of as an “effective density”. It has been shown that for high-contrast periodic systems the
\bm{\beta}
-matrix is frequency dependent, and may be anisotropic as well as non-positive definite
[
7
,
6
,
44
]
, and even wavenumber dependent
[
37
]
. What determines the structure of the spectrum is the signature of the
\bm{\beta}
-matrix, which no longer has just a binary nature (positive/negative), thus resulting in a richer (and more complex) theory. Indeed, on top of “strong” bands (
maximal number of propagation modes
permitted) and gaps (propagation completely forbidden), one can have the intermediate situation of “weak” bands. This means that the number of macroscopic propagating modes may vary with frequency
[
6
]
and, in the case of interconnected inclusions, with direction as well
[
37
]
.

Due to their multidimensional nature, systems also allow for the intermediate situation of
partial degeneracy
(or
partial high contrast
), in which the coefficients in the inclusions pick up an additional term of order
1
which is non-degenerate
for certain components
in certain directions. Examples of this are, for instance, linear elasticity with inclusions hard in compression and soft in shear or Maxwell’s equations with high contrast in the electric permittivity. The introduction of partial high contrast leads to a constrained microscopic kinematics, which needs to be accounted for in the homogenisation process
[
17
,
11
]
.
Typically, one needs additional assumptions on the coefficients to ensure the well-posedness of the corrector equation in the space of admissible microscopic two-scale limit fields. A general theory for high-contrast partially degenerate
systems of
PDEs in the periodic setting was developed by Kamotski and Smyshlyaev in
[
29
]
.

Given the above rich picture in the periodic setting alongside the recent developments
[
9
,
10
,
8
]
for operators with random coefficients, it is very natural and timely to ask what new phenomena are brought about by allowing high-contrast
systems
to have
random
coefficients. Due to the additional freedom given by the random setting, one could examine scenarios precluded in the periodic or scalar settings, such as having a mixture of fully degenerate and partially degenerate inclusions or allowing the direction of partial degeneracy to vary randomly from inclusion to inclusion, to name just a couple of examples. Moreover, it may be possible to devise random media with spectral properties highly desirable in applications, such as eigenvalues embedded into the weak spectral bands.

In the current paper we carry out the first step of this programme, setting out a general homogenisation and spectral theory for high-contrast systems with random coefficients, thus laying firm theoretical foundations for a more specialised future analysis of the range of problems outlined above. In particular, random systems with partial degeneracy will be the subject of a separate paper.

Structure of the paper

Our paper is structured as follows.

In Section

. After defining it, we study its spectrum and the relation thereof with the spectrum of

In Section

, which is shown in Subsection

.

|\nabla\eta_{L}(x)|\leq\frac{C}{L},\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(2.4)

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):=[L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})]^{d},\qquad\mathbf{W}^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):=[W^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})]^{d},\qquad\mathbf{C}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):=[C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})]^{d}\,.

(2.5)

Here

Our probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
is defined as follows.

We define
\Omega
to be the set of all possible collections of randomly distributed

in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

Assumption 2.1
.

For all
\omega\in\Omega

the set

is connected, and
\omega

\omega=\bigsqcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\omega^{k}

(a)

For every

(b)

\operatorname{diam}\omega^{k}<\frac{1}{2}

(c)

There exists

There exist an integer

, and universal positive constants

(i)

For all

(ii)

For every

(a)

(b)

\varphi_{k}(\overline{P_{1}^{j}})=\overline{\omega^{k}},

Remark 2.3
.

Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
warrant a number of remarks.

(i)

Assumption
2.1
requires inclusions to be approximately of same size and at a “safe” uniform distance from one another. The restriction on the size of the inclusions is merely conventional and, at the same time, unimportant: one can recover our setting from more general boundedness assumptions on the inclusions by elementary scaling arguments.

(ii)

Assumption
2.2
requires that all inclusions are
C^{2}
diffeomorphic to a finite number of shapes, together with a small neighbourhood thereof, where the constants associated with these diffeomorphisms are uniformly bounded. Observe that Assumption
2.2
automatically implies that the inclusions are Lipschitz.

(iii)

The papers
[
10
,
8
]
feature an analogue of Assumption
2.1
with Lipschitzness (see item (ii) above) replaced by the stronger assumption of minimal smoothness
[
38
, Chapter VI, Section 3.3]
. Indeed, minimal smoothness was sufficient there to ensure the uniformity of the constants in the relevant inequalities needed in the proofs. In the current paper, Lipschitzness is enough for Theorem
2.7
for each individual inclusion.
However, since we will be dealing with symmetric gradients — and, hence, Korn-type inequalities — in order to have uniform (in
k
) constants of the extension operator stronger conditions (such as, e.g., Assumption
2.2
) are needed to close the argument.

(iv)

Let us emphasise that our regularity assumptions on the inclusions are
not
optimal. We need the regularity properties from Assumption
2.2
in exactly two places: for the uniformity of Korn’s constant given by Proposition
2.8
, and in the proof of Theorem
A.4
on the higher regularity of corrector, which, in turn, is used in the proof of Theorem
5.7
— see (

) and (

As far as the former is concerned, our assumptions are sufficient (although not necessary) to ensure the uniformity of the Korn constant on the domain. Note that Assumption
2.2
is a considerably stronger than minimal smoothness assumption adopted for scalar operators in
[
10
,
8
]
and, hence, takes us quite far from optimality. We should like to point out, however, that the study of the dependence of Korn’s inequality on the domain and its uniformity with respect to certain classes of domains is an active area of current research (see, e.g., the relatively recent result in
[
24

for star-shaped domains); in this perspective, pursuing optimal conditions for the uniformity of the Korn constant in our setting goes beyond the scope of the current paper.

As for the latter, in Theorem
A.4

one realises that a uniform

Let

(\mathsf{C}_{j})_{\alpha\beta\mu\nu}=(\mathsf{C}_{j})_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta},\qquad(\mathsf{C}_{j})_{\alpha\beta\mu\nu}=(\mathsf{C}_{j})_{\alpha\beta\nu\mu},

(b)

(2.16)

Remark 2.10
.

Conditions (

For every

Remark 2.12
.

Our setting can be generalised in a straightforward manner to cover the following.

(i)

3
Main results

In this section we summarise, for the convenience of the reader, the main results of our paper.

Theorem
4.5
establishes that the family of operators
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}
converges (that is, their resolvents converge in the sense of weak/strong stochastic two-scale convergence), as
\varepsilon\to 0
, to a limiting operator
\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{hom}}
(see Definition
4.1
). Our
first main result
is Theorem
4.11
, which characterises the spectrum of the limiting operator
\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{hom}}
. The latter is comprised of two parts: the micro-resonances — i.e., the spectrum of a microscopic limiting operator
\mathcal{A}_{0}
— and the nonnegative real numbers
\lambda
for which the
matrix
function
\bm{\beta}(\lambda)
(
4.19
) has at least one nonnegative eigenvalue. Furthermore, as a consequence of the above convergence result,
\sigma(\mathcal{A}^{\rm hom})
is contained in the limiting spectrum
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\sigma(\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon})
(Corollary
4.13
).

Our
second main result
, Theorem
5.5
, provides an outer bound for the limiting spectrum
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\sigma(\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon})
in terms of the set
\mathcal{G}
(
5.3
), in turn defined by a nonlocal
scalar
function
\beta_{\infty}(\lambda)
, deterministic almost surely, which is sensitive to areas of space with atypical arrangements of inclusions.

As it turns out, under the additional assumption of finite-range dependence,
\mathcal{G}
is also an inner bound for the limiting spectrum. This is the subject of our
third main result
, Theorem
5.7
, which, in conjunction with Theorem
5.5
, yields
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\sigma(\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon})=\mathcal{G}
. Since, in general,
\sigma(\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{hom}})
is a proper subset of
\mathcal{G}
, this implies one does not have Hausdorff convergence of spectra, so that the limiting operator
\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{hom}}
captures only partially information about the spectrum of
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}
for arbitrary small but finite
\varepsilon
. This is a distinctive feature of the stochastic setting, already observed in
[
10
]
in the scalar case, which persists for systems with random coefficients.

Lastly, our
fourth main result
is Proposition
6.1
, which, together with the other examples from Section
6
, showcases the wider range of applications and physical effects of stochastic systems, when compared with periodic ones, thus paving the way for the further analysis outlined in Section 1.

Let us emphasise that, although the strategy is broadly similar to recent works
[
9
,
10
]
dealing with the scalar case, its technical implementation is not. For instance, in addition to the fact that one must work with a matrix — as opposed to scalar — “macroscopic spectral parameter” (the Zhikov
\beta
-matrix), it is not
a priori
clear what the right quantity to characterise the set
\mathcal{G}
for random systems would be, or how such quantity would be related to the stochastic
\beta
-matrix. As it turns our, the set
\mathcal{G}
is described by a
scalar
quantity even in the case of systems, which calls for a few new tricks to make the proofs work (e.g., in Theorem
5.7
). The differences between the scalar case and systems is also transparent from our set of examples. By means of these examples, we expose new spectral features, to be fully explored in subsequent works, which distinguish the setting of the present paper from either periodic high-contrast systems or stochastic high-contrast scalar problems. We refer the reader to Section 6 for further comments and insight.

Consider the ‘two-scale’ space

\mathbf{H}:=\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})+L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbf{L}^{2}_{0}(\Omega)),

(4.1)

which is a subspace of
\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega))

The spaces

are comprised of vector functions of the form

Let us recall that a tensor field

•

such that

•

See, e.g.,
[
27
]
, for further details.
By analogy, one defines a tensor field
\overline{\bm{p}}\in[\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)]^{d}
to be potential (respectively solenoidal) if for a.e.
\omega
its stationary extension

is such. The classical Weyl’s decomposition theorem holds for tensor fields in
[\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)]^{d}
as well, see e.g.
[
27
, Chapter 12.7]
, see also
[
8
, Theorem 2.14]
.

Let
1_{\Omega}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega\to\{0,1\}
be the stationary random function defined in accordance with

1_{\Omega}(y,\omega):=\mathbbm{1}_{\omega}(y)\,.

(4.3)

Let
\mathsf{C}^{\mathrm{hom}}
be the homogenised tensor of coefficients defined in accordance with

\mathsf{C}^{\mathrm{hom}}\xi\cdot\xi:=\inf_{\overline{\bm{\psi}}\in\bm{\mathcal{V}}^{2}_{\mathrm{pot}}(\Omega)}\mathbb{E}\left[(1-1_{\Omega})\,\mathsf{C}_{1}(\bm{\psi}+\xi)\cdot(\bm{\psi}+\xi)\right],\quad\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{2}},

(4.4)

where

\bm{\mathcal{V}}^{2}_{\mathrm{pot}}(\Omega):=\{\overline{\bm{\psi}}\in[\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)]^{d}\ |\ \bm{\psi}\text{ is potential and }\ \mathbb{E}[\overline{\bm{\psi}}]=0\}\,.

(4.5)

Consider the following bilinear forms:

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{C}_{0}\nabla_{y}\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla_{y}\bm{\varphi}\right]\,,\qquad\overline{\mathbf{u}},\ \overline{\bm{\varphi}}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega),

(4.6)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathsf{C}^{\mathrm{hom}}\nabla\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\bm{\varphi}\,,\qquad{\mathbf{u}},\ {\bm{\varphi}}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

(4.7)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathsf{C}^{\mathrm{hom}}\nabla\mathbf{u}_{1}\cdot\nabla\bm{\varphi}_{1}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{C}_{0}\nabla_{y}\mathbf{u}_{0}\cdot\nabla_{y}\bm{\varphi}_{0}\right]\,,\qquad\mathbf{u}_{1}+\overline{\mathbf{u}_{0}},\ \bm{\varphi}_{1}+\overline{\bm{\varphi}_{0}}\in\mathbf{V}\,.

\operatorname{dist}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H}}\left(\mathcal{H}_{\square_{0}^{R}}(\omega-x_{j}),\mathcal{H}_{\square_{0}^{R}}(\omega-x_{k})\right)<\delta\qquad\forall j,k\in\{1,\ldots,N^{d}\},

(5.36)

\left|\beta_{\infty}(\lambda)-\sup_{k\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{1}{|\square_{x_{n}}^{R+\kappa}|}\int_{\square_{x_{n}}^{R+\kappa}}\left(\lambda+\lambda^{2}\,k\cdot\mathbf{b}_{\lambda}(y,\omega)k\right)dy\right|<\delta\qquad\forall n\in\{1,\dots,N^{d}\},

(5.37)

\left|\frac{1}{|\square_{x_{n}}^{R+\kappa}|}\int_{\square_{x_{n}}^{R+\kappa}}\left(\lambda\,\mathrm{I}+\lambda^{2}\,\mathbf{b}_{\lambda}(y,\omega)\right)dy-\frac{1}{|\square_{x_{m}}^{R+\kappa}|}\int_{\square_{x_{m}}^{R+\kappa}}\left(\lambda\,\mathrm{I}+\lambda^{2}\,\mathbf{b}_{\lambda}(y,\omega)\right)dy\right|<\delta\\
\qquad\forall n,m\in\{1,\dots,N^{d}\}\,.

(5.38)

Remark 5.9
.

Note that the effect of the range of dependence manifests itself explicitly in the bound (
5.36
), which is only true when one removes a boundary layer of width
\kappa
from our cubes
\square_{x_{n}}^{R+\kappa}
. Indeed, distributions of inclusions in
\square_{x_{j}}^{R}
and
\square_{x_{k}}^{R}
are independent for all
j\neq k
,
j,k\in\{1,\ldots,N^{d}\}
by Assumption
5.6
.

Proof of Theorem
5.7
.

Suppose we are given
\lambda_{0}\in\mathcal{G}
. The task at hand is to show that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\operatorname{dist}(\lambda_{0},\sigma(\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}))=0.

(5.39)

Due to Corollary
4.13
and the continuity of
\beta_{\infty}
, see Proposition
5.3
, it is sufficient to prove this for
\lambda_{0}
such that
\beta_{\infty}(\lambda_{0})>0
.
The proof, rather long and technical, will be broken into several steps for the sake of clarity.

Step 1
:
Approximating
\beta_{\infty}(\lambda_{0})
on nearly-periodic cubes
.

Let us fix sufficiently small
\delta>0
and let
R_{0},L>0
be sufficiently large positive numbers and suppose we have fixed
\varepsilon>0
. Then by Proposition
5.8
there exists
R\geq R_{0}
such that the following holds: there exist
\overline{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\{x_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N^{d}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
for which (
5.34
)–(
5.37
) are satisfied, where
N=N(\varepsilon)
is chosen to be the smallest positive integer such that

\varepsilon N(R+\kappa)\geq L.

(5.40)

Note that
N(\varepsilon)=O(\varepsilon^{-1})
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, and the choice of the centres of the cubes
\overline{x}
,
\{x_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N^{d}}
depends on
\varepsilon
, which we, however, do not reflect in the notation for the sake of brevity.
The above condition (
5.40
) ensures that

\square_{\varepsilon\overline{x}}^{L}\subseteq\square_{\varepsilon\overline{x}}^{\varepsilon N(R+\kappa)}=\bigcup_{k}\square_{\varepsilon{x_{k}}}^{\varepsilon(R+\kappa)}.

(5.41)

We pick one of the cubes as the approximation basis for the construction that follows. Since it is not important which cube we use, we pick
\square_{x_{1}}^{R+\kappa}
. Let

\bm{\ell}:=\frac{1}{|\square_{x_{1}}^{R+\kappa}|}\int_{\square_{x_{1}}^{R+\kappa}}(\lambda_{0}\,\mathrm{I}+\lambda_{0}^{2}\,\mathbf{b}_{\lambda_{0}}(y,\omega))\,dy\,.

Then (
5.37
) can be recast as

|\beta_{\infty}(\lambda_{0})-\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\bm{\ell})|<\delta,

where
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\bm{\ell})
denotes the greatest eigenvalue of the
d\times d
matrix
\bm{\ell}
. Without loss of generality, we can assume that
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\bm{\ell})>0
. This can always be achieved by choosing
\delta
small enough.

Step 2
:
Auxiliary periodic problem
.

Let

\widetilde{\square}_{x_{1}}^{R+\kappa}:=\square_{x_{1}}^{R+\kappa}\setminus\left(\bigcup_{\omega^{k}\subset\subset\square_{x_{1}}^{R}}\omega^{k}\right)

(5.42)

be the set obtained from
\square_{x_{1}}^{R+\kappa}
by removing the inclusions fully contained within the smaller cube
\square_{x_{1}}^{R}
.

For
j,k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, let
\widehat{\mathbf{N}}^{(j,k)}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\mathrm{per}}(\widetilde{\square}_{x_{1}}^{R+\kappa})
be the unique zero-mean solution to

\int_{\widetilde{\square}_{x_{1}}^{R+\kappa}}(\mathsf{C}_{1})_{\alpha\beta\mu\nu}\left(\delta_{\mu j}\delta_{\nu k}+\nabla_{\mu}\widehat{N}_{\nu}^{(j,k)}\right)\,\nabla_{\alpha}\varphi_{\beta}=0,\qquad\forall\bm{\varphi}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\mathrm{per}}(\widetilde{\square}_{x_{1}}^{R+\kappa})\,.

(5.43)

The quantity
\widehat{\mathbf{N}}
is a tensor of order
d^{2}
valued in vector functions over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and it has the meaning of
homogenisation corrector
for the auxiliary periodic problem obtained by covering
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with copies of the perforated cubical domain
\widetilde{\square}_{x_{1}}^{R+\kappa}
.
Let us first extend
\widehat{\mathbf{N}}^{(j,k)}
to the whole of
\square_{x_{1}}^{R+\kappa}
by Theorem
2.7
, and then to the whole of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by periodicity. With slight abuse of notation, we denote the resulting object
\widehat{\mathbf{N}}^{(j,k)}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\mathrm{per}}(\square_{x_{1}}^{R+\kappa})
by the same symbol.

We define the periodic homogenised tensor
\widehat{\mathsf{C}}^{\mathrm{hom}}
via

\widehat{\mathsf{C}}^{\mathrm{hom}}\xi:=\frac{1}{|\square_{x_{1}}^{R+\kappa}|}\int_{\widetilde{\square}_{x_{1}}^{R+\kappa}}\mathsf{C}_{1}(\xi+\xi_{jk}\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{N}}^{(j,k)})\qquad\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{2}},\ \xi=\operatorname{sym}\xi\,.

(5.44)

Step 3
:
Estimates for the periodic corrector
.

Formula (
5.43
) immediately gives us

\int_{\square_{x_{1}}^{R+\kappa}}\mathsf{C}_{1}\,\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{N}}^{(j,k)}\cdot\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{N}}^{(j,k)}=-\int_{\square_{x_{1}}^{R+\kappa}}(\mathsf{C}_{1})_{\alpha\beta jk}\,\nabla_{\alpha}\widehat{N}_{\beta}^{(j,k)},\qquad j,k\in\{1,\ldots,d\},

(in the above formula there is no summation over
j
and
k
). The latter and (
2.16
) imply

\|\operatorname{sym}\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{N}}^{(j,k)}\|_{[\mathbf{L}^{2}(\square_{x_{1}}^{R+\kappa})]^{d}}\leq C(R+\kappa)^{\frac{d}{2}}.

(5.45)

The Korn and Poincaré inequalities then give us

\|\widehat{\mathbf{N}}^{(j,k)}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\square_{x_{1}}^{R+\kappa})}\leq C(R+\kappa)^{\frac{d}{2}+1}.

(5.46)

The above estimates are rather straightforward. However, further on in the proof, in order to close the estimates, we will need higher (than
\mathbf{H}^{1}
) regularity of the periodic corrector. The key technical result in this regard is stated and proved in Appendix
A.3
in the form of Theorem
A.4
, which states that there exists a constant
C>0
and
p>2
, both independent of
R
, such that

\|\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{N}}^{(j,k)}\|_{[\mathbf{L}^{p}(\square_{x_{1}}^{R+\kappa})]^{d}}\leq C(R+\kappa)^{\frac{d}{p}}.

(5.47)

Step 4
:
Construction of “quasimodes”
.

Let
k_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
be given and let us define a
d\times d
matrix
\widetilde{\mathsf{C}}=\widetilde{\mathsf{C}}(k_{0})
component-wise as

\widetilde{\mathsf{C}}_{\alpha\beta}:=(k_{0})_{\mu}\,\widehat{\mathsf{C}}^{\mathrm{hom}}_{\alpha\mu\nu\beta}\,(k_{0})_{\nu}\,,\qquad\alpha,\beta\in\{1,\dots,d\}\,.

(5.48)

Observe that
\widetilde{\mathsf{C}}
is symmetric and positive definite. In the wave equation context the direct analogue of
k_{0}
has the meaning of the direction of propagation of a flat wave, cf. (
5.49
) below. Its particular choice is unimportant, and it won’t affect the final conclusion.

Let us choose
\varpi=\varpi(k_{0})\in\mathbb{R}
and
A=A(k_{0})\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
in accordance with

\varpi^{2}=\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\widetilde{\mathsf{C}}^{-1/2}\,\bm{\ell}\,\widetilde{\mathsf{C}}^{-1/2}),

A\in\ker(\varpi^{2}\widetilde{\mathsf{C}}-\bm{\ell}),

and let us put

\mathbf{u}(x):=A\cos(\varpi\,k_{0}\cdot x).

(5.49)

A direct calculation shows that formulae (
5.48
)–(
5.49
) imply

-\operatorname{div}\widehat{\mathsf{C}}^{\mathrm{hom}}\nabla\mathbf{u}=\bm{\ell}\,\mathbf{u}\,.

(5.50)

Let
\eta_{L,\varepsilon}(x):=\eta(x/L+\varepsilon\overline{x})
, where the cut-off
\eta
is defined as in (
2.2
). Then the normalised vector-functions

\mathbf{u}_{L,\varepsilon}:=\frac{\eta_{L,\varepsilon}\mathbf{u}}{\|\eta_{L,\varepsilon}\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}}

are approximate solutions of (
5.50
), that is, they satisfy

\|\operatorname{div}\widehat{\mathsf{C}}^{\mathrm{hom}}\nabla\mathbf{u}_{L,\varepsilon}+\bm{\ell}\,\mathbf{u}_{L,\varepsilon}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\frac{C}{L}.

(5.51)

Note that the constant
C
in the above estimate is independent of
\varepsilon
. The vector-functions
\mathbf{u}_{L,\varepsilon}
can be thought of as “quasimodes” as
L\to\infty
, where quotation marks are needed because the “spectral parameter” here is a matrix. Also note that (cf. (
5.41
))

\operatorname{supp}\mathbf{u}_{L,\varepsilon}\subseteq\square_{\varepsilon\overline{x}}^{L}\subseteq\square_{\varepsilon\overline{x}}^{\varepsilon N(R+\kappa)}\,.

(5.52)

Out of
\mathbf{u}_{L,\varepsilon}
one can construct candidates for “quasimodes” for the operator
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}
as follows. Let
\mathbf{b}_{\lambda_{0}}^{\varepsilon}(x):=\mathbf{b}_{\lambda_{0}}(x/\varepsilon,\omega)
(recall that
\mathbf{b}_{\lambda_{0}}
is defined by (
4.14
)) and define

\mathbf{u}_{L}^{\varepsilon}:=(1+\lambda_{0}\mathbf{b}^{\varepsilon}_{\lambda_{0}})\mathbf{u}_{L,\varepsilon}.

(5.53)

While the functions
\mathbf{u}_{L}^{\varepsilon}
are in the domain of the form (
2.20
), they are not in the domain of
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}
. Therefore, we define

\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\varepsilon}_{L}:=(\lambda_{0}+1)(\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}+1)^{-1}\mathbf{u}_{L}^{\varepsilon}

so that

(\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}-\lambda_{0})\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\varepsilon}_{L}=(\lambda_{0}+1)(\mathbf{u}^{\varepsilon}_{L}-\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\varepsilon}_{L}).

(5.54)

The task at hand reduces to show that for every
\sigma>0
one can choose
\varepsilon_{0}>0
,
\delta>0
and
L>0
such that

\|\mathbf{u}^{\varepsilon}_{L}-\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\varepsilon}_{L}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}<\sigma\qquad\forall\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}.

(5.55)

The latter, combined with (
5.54
) and the fact that, clearly,

\|\mathbf{u}^{\varepsilon}_{L}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\geq C>0

uniformly in
\varepsilon
and
L
, gives us (
5.39
).

Step 6
:
Adding the corrector
.

Rather than estimating
\|\mathbf{u}^{\varepsilon}_{L}-\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\varepsilon}_{L}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
directly, it is convenient to estimate

\|\mathbf{u}^{\varepsilon}_{LC}-\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\varepsilon}_{L}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

instead, where

\mathbf{u}^{\varepsilon}_{LC}(x):=\mathbf{u}_{L}^{\varepsilon}(x)+\varepsilon\partial_{k}[\mathbf{u}_{L,\varepsilon}]_{j}(x)\widehat{\mathbf{N}}^{(j,k)}(x/\varepsilon)

(5.56)

is a modification of
\mathbf{u}_{L}^{\varepsilon}
by adding the homogenisation corrector. (Recall that
\widehat{\mathbf{N}}^{(j,k)}
is not the actual stochastic corrector for the operator
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}
, but the auxiliary periodic corrector introduced in Step 2.)
Doing so will yield the result we are after because, on account of the estimate (
5.46
) for the corrector, we have

\left|\|\mathbf{u}^{\varepsilon}_{L}-\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\varepsilon}_{L}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}-\|\mathbf{u}^{\varepsilon}_{LC}-\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\varepsilon}_{L}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\right|\leq\|\mathbf{u}^{\varepsilon}_{L}-\mathbf{u}^{\varepsilon}_{LC}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C\varepsilon(R+\kappa).

(5.57)

Step 7
:
Key bilinear form estimate
.

Let us denote by
a^{\varepsilon}
the bilinear form associated with the operator
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}+\mathrm{Id}
:

a^{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\mathsf{C}^{\varepsilon}\nabla\mathbf{u}\,\cdot\nabla\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}\right)\,.

Following a strategy proposed in
[
28
]
(which exploits, in turn, a general idea found, e.g., in
[
40
]
), we claim that the task at hand reduces to proving the key estimate

|a^{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}^{\varepsilon}_{LC}-\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\varepsilon}_{L},\mathbf{v})|\leq C\,\mathcal{E}(\varepsilon,\delta,L)\sqrt{a^{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v})}\qquad\forall\mathbf{v}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

(5.58)

for some ‘error’
\mathcal{E}(\varepsilon,\delta,L)
satisfying

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\lim_{L\to+\infty}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathcal{E}(\varepsilon,\delta,L)=0.

(5.59)

Suppose (
5.58
) holds. Then for
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{u}^{\varepsilon}_{LC}-\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\varepsilon}_{L}
one obtains

\|\mathbf{u}^{\varepsilon}_{LC}-\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\varepsilon}_{L}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\mathcal{E}(\varepsilon,\delta,L).

(5.60)

Formulae (
5.60
), (
5.59
) and (
5.57
) imply (
5.55
). The remainder of the proof is devoted to proving (
5.58
).

Given an arbitrary
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, let
\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{\varepsilon}
be the extension of
\mathbf{v}|_{\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}}
into
\mathcal{I}^{\varepsilon}
via Theorem
2.7
and put
\mathbf{v}_{0}^{\varepsilon}=\mathbf{v}-\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{\varepsilon}
.

By the properties of the extension, we have:

\|\mathbf{v}_{0}^{\varepsilon}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C\varepsilon\|\operatorname{sym}\nabla\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,,

(5.61)

\|\operatorname{sym}\nabla\mathbf{v}_{0}^{\varepsilon}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C\,\|\operatorname{sym}\nabla\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{\varepsilon}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

(5.62)

and

\|\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{\varepsilon}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\|\operatorname{sym}\nabla\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{\varepsilon}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\varepsilon\|\operatorname{sym}\nabla\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C\sqrt{a^{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v})}\,.

(5.63)

Let us consider the quantity

a^{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}^{\varepsilon}_{LC},\mathbf{v})=\int_{\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}}\mathsf{C}_{1}\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\varepsilon}_{LC}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{v}+\varepsilon^{2}\int_{\mathcal{I}^{\varepsilon}}\mathsf{C}_{0}\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\varepsilon}_{LC}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{v}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbf{u}^{\varepsilon}_{LC}\cdot\mathbf{v}.

(5.64)

Step 8
:
Estimates: part 1
.

By adding and subtracting
\widehat{\mathsf{C}}^{\mathrm{hom}}\nabla\mathbf{u}_{L,\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}
, the first term in the RHS of (
5.64
) can be equivalently recast as

\int_{\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}}\mathsf{C}_{1}\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\varepsilon}_{LC}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{v}=\int_{\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}}\left[\widehat{\mathsf{C}}^{\mathrm{hom}}\nabla\mathbf{u}_{L,\varepsilon}+(\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}}\mathsf{C}_{1}(\delta_{j\cdot}\delta_{k\cdot}+\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{N}}^{(j,k)}(\cdot/\varepsilon))-\widehat{\mathsf{C}}^{\mathrm{hom}}\delta_{j\cdot}\delta_{k\cdot})\partial_{k}[\mathbf{u}_{L,\varepsilon}]_{j}\right.\\
\left.+\varepsilon\,\mathsf{C}_{1}(\nabla\partial_{k}[\mathbf{u}_{L,\varepsilon}]_{j})\otimes\widehat{\mathbf{N}}^{(j,k)}(\cdot/\varepsilon)\right]\cdot\nabla\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{\varepsilon}.

(5.65)

In view of (
5.46
), the elementary estimate

\|\nabla^{2}\mathbf{u}_{L,\varepsilon}\|_{[L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})]^{d^{3}}}\leq CL^{-d/2}

(5.66)

and (
5.40
), the last term in the RHS of (
5.65
) can be estimated as

\left|\int_{\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}}\left[\varepsilon\,\mathsf{C}_{1}(\nabla\partial_{k}[\mathbf{u}_{L,\varepsilon}]_{j})\otimes\widehat{\mathbf{N}}^{(j,k)}(\cdot/\varepsilon)\right]\cdot\nabla\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}\right|\leq C\varepsilon R\,\|\operatorname{sym}\nabla\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{\varepsilon}\|_{\mathbf{L}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,.

(5.67)

Next, let us denote by
\widehat{\mathbbm{1}}_{\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}}
the characteristic function of the set
\widetilde{\square}_{x_{1}}^{R+\kappa}
,
cf. (
5.42
), extended by periodicity to the whole of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let us rewrite

\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}}\mathsf{C}_{1}(\delta_{j\cdot}\delta_{k\cdot}+\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{N}}^{(j,k)}(\cdot/\varepsilon))-\widehat{\mathsf{C}}^{\mathrm{hom}}\delta_{j\cdot}\delta_{k\cdot}\\
=(\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}}-\widehat{\mathbbm{1}}_{\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}})\mathsf{C}_{1}(\delta_{j\cdot}\delta_{k\cdot}+\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{N}}^{(j,k)}(\cdot/\varepsilon))+\widehat{\mathbbm{1}}_{\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}}\mathsf{C}_{1}(\delta_{j\cdot}\delta_{k\cdot}+\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{N}}^{(j,k)}(\cdot/\varepsilon))-\widehat{\mathsf{C}}^{\mathrm{hom}}\delta_{j\cdot}\delta_{k\cdot}\,.

(5.68)

In view of (
5.52
), we will need to estimate
\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}}-\widehat{\mathbbm{1}}_{\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}}
in
\square_{\varepsilon\overline{x}}^{L}
. We claim that, for
R
large enough, we have

\int_{\square_{\varepsilon\overline{x}}^{L}}|\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}}-\widehat{\mathbbm{1}}_{\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}}|\leq C\delta L^{d}.

(5.69)

The inequality (
5.69
) follows from:

(i)

Formula (
5.41
);

(ii)

The estimate

\int_{\square_{\varepsilon x_{j}}^{\varepsilon R}}|\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}}-\widehat{\mathbbm{1}}_{\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}}|\leq C\delta(\varepsilon\,R)^{d},\qquad j\in\{1,\ldots,N^{d}\},

which is a consequence of (
5.36
) and the fact that the surface area of inclusions in each
\square_{\varepsilon x_{j}}^{\varepsilon R}
,
j\in\{1,\ldots,N^{d}\}
, is bounded above by the volume of the cube, up to a constant (this follows immediately from Assumption
2.2
);

(iii)

The fact that

\frac{\left|\square_{\varepsilon x_{j}}^{\varepsilon(R+\kappa)}\setminus\square_{\varepsilon x_{j}}^{\varepsilon R}\right|}{|\square_{\varepsilon\overline{x}}^{L}|}

can be made arbitrarily small by choosing
R
(at the start of the proof) sufficiently large.

When substituting (
5.68
) into (
5.65
), the first term on the RHS of (
5.68
) gives rise to two contributions:

(\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}}-\widehat{\mathbbm{1}}_{\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}})\mathsf{C}_{1}\nabla\mathbf{u}_{L,\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{\varepsilon}

and

(\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}}-\widehat{\mathbbm{1}}_{\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}})\mathsf{C}_{1}(\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{N}}^{(j,k)}(\cdot/\varepsilon)\,\partial_{k}(\mathbf{u}_{L,\varepsilon})_{j})\cdot\nabla\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{\varepsilon}
. The former can be estimated via (
5.66
) and (
5.69
) as

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}}-\widehat{\mathbbm{1}}_{\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}})\mathsf{C}_{1}\nabla\mathbf{u}_{L,\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}\right|\leq C\,\delta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\|\operatorname{sym}\nabla\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{\varepsilon}\|_{[\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})]^{2}}\,.

(5.70)

Combining again (
5.66
), (
5.69
) and the higher regularity of the corrector (
5.47
) by means of Hölder’s inequality, and applying Korn’s inequality to
\nabla\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{\varepsilon}
, we can estimate the latter as

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}}-\widehat{\mathbbm{1}}_{\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}})\mathsf{C}_{1}(\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{N}}^{(j,k)}(\cdot/\varepsilon)\,\partial_{k}[\mathbf{u}_{L,\varepsilon}]_{j})\cdot\nabla\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{\varepsilon}\right|\leq C\,\delta^{\frac{p-2}{2p}}\,\|\operatorname{sym}\nabla\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{\varepsilon}\|_{[\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})]^{2}}\,

(5.71)

for some
p>2
.

We are left to deal with the last two terms on the RHS of (
5.68
). To this end, we observe that, for fixed
\beta
, the vector field

\widehat{\mathbbm{1}}_{\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}}(\mathsf{C}_{1})_{\alpha\beta\mu\nu}(\delta_{j\mu}\delta_{k\nu}+\nabla_{\mu}N^{(j,k)}_{\nu})-(\widehat{\mathsf{C}}^{\mathrm{hom}})_{\alpha\beta\mu\nu}\,\delta_{j\mu}\delta_{k\nu}

(whose components are labelled by the index
\alpha
) is divergence free — because of (
5.43
) — and has zero mean — because of (
5.44
). Hence, by a classical construction, see e.g.
[
27
]
, there exist
d^{3}
skew-symmetric, zero-mean matrix functions
\mathbf{G}_{\cdot\,\cdot;\beta}^{(j,k)}\in[\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\mathrm{per}}(\square_{x_{1}}^{R+\kappa})]^{2}
,
\beta,j,k\in\{1,\dots,d\}
, such that

\partial_{\gamma}\mathbf{G}_{\gamma\alpha;\beta}^{(j,k)}=\widehat{\mathbbm{1}}_{\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}}(\mathsf{C}_{1})_{\alpha\beta\mu\nu}(\delta_{j\mu}\delta_{k\nu}+\nabla_{\mu}N^{(j,k)}_{\nu}(\cdot/\varepsilon))-(\widehat{\mathsf{C}}^{\mathrm{hom}})_{\alpha\beta\mu\nu}\,\delta_{j\mu}\delta_{k\nu}\,.

(5.72)

Observe that the RHS of (
5.72
) is symmetric in the pair of indices
\alpha
and
\beta
.
Moreover, the explicit construction of
\mathbf{G}_{\gamma\alpha;\beta}^{(j,k)}
implies

\|\mathbf{G}_{\cdot\,\cdot;\beta}^{(j,k)}\|_{[\mathbf{L}^{2}(\square_{x_{1}}^{R+\kappa})]^{2}}\\
\leq CR\|\widehat{\mathbbm{1}}_{\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}}(\mathsf{C}_{1})_{\cdot\,\cdot\mu\nu}(\delta_{j\mu}\delta_{k\nu}+\nabla_{\mu}N^{(j,k)}_{\nu}(\cdot/\varepsilon)-(\widehat{\mathsf{C}}^{\mathrm{hom}})_{\cdot\,\cdot\mu\nu}\,\delta_{j\mu}\delta_{k\nu}\|_{[\mathbf{L}^{2}(\square_{x_{1}}^{R+\kappa})]^{2}}\\
\overset{\eqref{proof part2 equation 9}}{\leq}CR^{\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}+1}\,.

(5.73)

In view of (
5.72
), integrating by parts and using the antisymmetry of
\mathbf{G}_{\cdot\,\cdot;\beta}^{(j,k)}
, we get

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left[(\widehat{\mathbbm{1}}_{\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}}\mathsf{C}_{1}(\delta_{j\cdot}\delta_{k\cdot}+\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{N}}^{(j,k)}(\cdot/\varepsilon))-\widehat{\mathsf{C}}^{\mathrm{hom}}\delta_{j\cdot}\delta_{k\cdot})\,\partial_{k}[\mathbf{u}_{L,\varepsilon}]_{j}\right]\cdot\nabla\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{\varepsilon}\\
=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left[\varepsilon\,\partial_{\gamma}\mathbf{G}_{\gamma\alpha;\beta}^{(j,k)}\,\partial_{k}[\mathbf{u}_{L,\varepsilon}]_{j}\right]\,\partial_{\alpha}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{\varepsilon}_{\beta}\\
=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varepsilon\,\mathbf{G}_{\gamma\alpha;\beta}^{(j,k)}\,\partial_{\gamma}\partial_{k}[\mathbf{u}_{L,\varepsilon}]_{j}\,\partial_{\alpha}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{\varepsilon}_{\beta}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\underset{=0}{\underbrace{\varepsilon\,\mathbf{G}_{\gamma\alpha;\beta}^{(j,k)}\,\partial_{k}[\mathbf{u}_{L,\varepsilon}]_{j}\,\partial_{\gamma}\partial_{\alpha}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{\varepsilon}_{\beta}}}\\
=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varepsilon\,\mathbf{G}_{\gamma\alpha;\beta}^{(j,k)}\,\partial_{\gamma}\partial_{k}[\mathbf{u}_{L,\varepsilon}]_{j}\,\left(\partial_{\alpha}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{\varepsilon}_{\beta}+\partial_{\beta}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}\right)\,.

(5.74)

Hence, by combining (
5.67
), (
5.68
), (
5.70
), (
5.71
), (
5.74
), (
5.66
), (
5.73
), and (
5.63
), we obtain that (
5.65
) can be rewritten as

\int_{\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}}\mathsf{C}_{1}\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\varepsilon}_{LC}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{v}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widehat{\mathsf{C}}^{\mathrm{hom}}\nabla\mathbf{u}_{L,\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{v}+\mathcal{R}_{1},

(5.75)

where

|\mathcal{R}_{1}|\leq C\left[R\,\varepsilon+\delta^{\frac{p-2}{2p}}\right]\|\operatorname{sym}\nabla\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{\varepsilon}\|_{[\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})]^{2}}\,.

6
Examples

In this section we will discuss some explicit examples, to showcase the different properties of
\bm{\beta}(\lambda)
and
\beta_{\infty}(\lambda)
, as well as to compare and contrast the stochastic and the periodic settings.

Note that, in general,
\beta(\lambda)
(recall (
4.21
)) and
\beta_{\infty}(\lambda)
are quite different: as the examples will show,
\bm{\beta}(\lambda)
results from “averaging” over the inclusions, whereas
\beta_{\infty}(\lambda)
from taking a “supremum”. This means, in particular, that
\sigma(\mathcal{A}^{\rm hom})
is generally a proper subset of
\mathcal{G}
— cf. Example 3. Remarkably, this is not the case in Example 1, where the randomness (in the final part of the example) is designed in such a way that
\beta_{\infty}(\lambda)=\beta(\lambda)
, which implies
\sigma(\mathcal{A}^{\rm hom})=\mathcal{G}
.

Let us emphasise that in Examples 1 and 3 Assumption
5.6
is satisfied, and hence the limiting spectrum coincides with the set
\mathcal{G}
— see also Remark
6.2
(ii).

6.2
Example 2: statistical macro-symmetries

Whilst obtaining an isotropic homogenised tensor
\mathsf{C}^{{\rm hom}}
in the purely periodic setting is not easy to achieve (if at all possible), this is something that can be done very naturally in the stochastic setting by means of statistical macro-symmetries. Here “macro” (as opposed to “micro” from Subsection
6.1
) refers to the fact that symmetries apply to the collection of inclusions as a whole. This is the subject of this short subsection.

Suppose that
\mathsf{C}_{1}
is isotropic and that the inclusions possess the following additional statistical macro-symmetries:

P(\mathfrak{f})=P(R\mathfrak{f}),\quad\forall\mathfrak{f}\in\mathcal{F},\,\,\forall R\in SO(3).

(6.12)

Here
R\mathfrak{f}
denotes the set obtained by applying the transformation
R
to each element
\omega
of
\mathfrak{f}
. Recall that
\mathcal{F}
is the
\sigma
-algebra introduced in subsection
2.1
.

It then follows easily from (
4.4
) and (
6.12
) that in this case we have

C^{\rm hom}(R\xi R^{T})\cdot(R\xi R^{T})=\mathsf{C}^{\rm hom}\xi\cdot\xi,\quad\forall R\in SO(3),

which, in turn, implies that the tensor
\mathsf{C}^{\rm hom}
is isotropic (see, e.g.,
[
16
, Chapter 4]
).

Note that this general example is not concerned with the spectral properties. In fact, one may come up with models satisfying the assumption (

\sigma(\mathcal{A}^{\rm hom})=\mathcal{G}
or
\sigma(\mathcal{A}^{\rm hom})

\mathcal{G}

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\sigma(\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon})

6.3
Example 3: random scaling

As our last example, let us consider a distribution of inclusions obtained from a single shape

Let

\mathcal{A}_{0}^{Q}

Let

Remark 6.2
.

(i)

One could also consider the case where geometry of the inclusions is fixed, but the tensor
\mathsf{C}_{0}

(ii)

Alternatively, one can rely on appropriate point processes (e.g., the random parking model) to position the inclusions randomly in space, without reference to periodic lattice. The additional technical aspects arising from doing so can be dealt with similarly to the scalar case
