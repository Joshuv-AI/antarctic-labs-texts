Improved classical shadows from local symmetries in the Schur basis
Daniel Grier, Sihan Liu, Gaurav Mahajan

Abstract
We study the sample complexity of the classical shadows task: what is the fewest number of copies of an unknown state you need to measure to predict expected values with respect to some class of observables? Large joint measurements are likely required in order to minimize sample complexity, but previous joint measurement protocols only work when the unknown state is pure. We present the first joint measurement protocol for classical shadows whose sample complexity scales with the rank of the unknown state. In particular we prove 𝒪 ⁡ ( r ​ B / ϵ 2 ) \mathcal{O}(\sqrt{rB}/\epsilon^{2}) samples suffice, where r r is the rank of the state, B B is a bound on the squared Frobenius norm of the observables, and ϵ \epsilon is the target accuracy. In the low-rank regime, this is a nearly quadratic advantage over traditional approaches that use single-copy measurements. We present several intermediate results that may be of independent interest: a solution to a new formulation of classical shadows that captures functions of non-identical input states; a generalization of a “nice” Schur basis used for optimal qubit purification and quantum majority vote; and a measurement strategy that allows us to use local symmetries in the Schur basis to avoid intractable Weingarten calculations in the analysis.

Introduction

One of the most fundamental questions in quantum state learning is as follows: given access to many copies of some unknown state
\chi\in\mathbbm{C}^{d\times d}
, what is the fewest number of copies you can measure to learn
\chi
or some property of it? Of course, the number of copies—known as the
sample complexity
—depends on many specifics of the question. That said, in essentially every variant, the optimal strategy is to perform entangling measurements on large groups of copies simultaneously. Evidently, single-copy measurements are less efficient than joint measurements on
\chi^{\otimes n}
at extracting information about the unknown state.

Consider the shadow tomography task (originally introduced in
[
Aar18
]

\tr(O_{i}\chi)
to additive imprecision
\epsilon

O_{1},O_{2},\ldots,O_{M}

While

[

In this paper, we consider the well-known communication variant of the shadow tomography task called

[

This constraint models a scenario in which one party has access to the unknown quantum states and wants to send a classical snapshot (i.e., the “shadow”) of the state for another party to analyze. With single-copy measurements,

1.1
Proof overview and technical contributions

Our solution starts by reducing the classical shadow task for mixed states to another problem we call the
Population Classical Shadow Task
.

Population Classical Shadows

To motivate the definition of this task, let’s start by diagonalizing
\chi
, so that
\chi=U(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}\left|i\right\rangle\!\left\langle i\right|)U^{\dagger}
. Therefore, we can expand
\chi^{\otimes n}
as

\chi^{\otimes n}=\sum_{e\in[d]^{n}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{e_{i}}\right)U^{\otimes n}\left|e\right\rangle\!\left\langle e\right|(U^{\otimes n})^{\dagger}.

In other words, we can view
\chi^{\otimes n}
as a statistical mixture of pure states of the form
U^{\otimes n}\left|e\right\rangle
such that every
\left|e_{i}\right\rangle
is distributed i.i.d. according to the eigenvalues of
\chi
.
Consider the “average” state

P(\left|e\right\rangle,U):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}U\left|e_{i}\right\rangle\left\langle e_{i}\right|U^{\dagger},

and notice that an unbiased estimator of
P(\left|e\right\rangle,U)
immediately implies an unbiased estimator of
\chi
by linearity. When clear, we suppress the explicit dependence on
\left|e\right\rangle
and
U
, and simply write
P
for this state.

Motivated by this, we define a variant of the classical shadow task in which one builds a “population” classical description
\hat{P}
for the joint state
U^{\otimes n}\left|e\right\rangle
such that
\tr(O\hat{P})
approximates
\tr\left(OP\right)
(see
Definition

5
).
We show that this population classical shadow task can be used as a stepping stone (in a black-box manner) for the original classical shadow task for mixed states with a small
1/\epsilon^{2}
additive overhead in the sample complexity (see
Lemma

24
).
Moreover, the model is interesting in its own right—it generalizes the notion of classical shadow to settings where the input registers contain states that are not necessarily all identical.

From now on, we focus on solving the population classical shadow task where the input is a pure state of the form
U^{\otimes n}\left|e\right\rangle
.
At the highest level, our classical shadow scheme has the usual basic outline: design a joint measurement on the input state
U^{\otimes n}\left|e\right\rangle
; from the outcome, construct an unbiased estimator
\hat{P}
such that
{\mathsf{E}}[\hat{P}]=P
(where the expectation is over the randomness in the measurement outcome); finally, for any observable
O
, bound the variance of
\tr(O\hat{P})
to show that it is a good estimate of the true expectation
\tr(OP)
for sufficiently large
n
.

Measurements in Schur Basis

To construct the measurement, we first need to understand the structure of the space
(\mathbbm{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}
in which
U^{\otimes n}\left|e\right\rangle
lives. Our starting point will be the famous Schur-Weyl decomposition:

(\mathbbm{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}\cong\bigoplus_{\lambda\vdash n}Q_{\lambda}\otimes P_{\lambda}

where subspaces
Q_{\lambda}
and
P_{\lambda}
are irreps

4

4

[
EGH+11

of the unitary group
\mathrm{U}(d)
and symmetric group
\mathrm{S}_{n}
, respectively.
In other words, the decomposition gives a
Schur basis
for
(\mathbbm{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}
: vectors
\left|(\lambda,i,j)\right\rangle
indexed by
\lambda\vdash n
,
i\in[\dim Q_{\lambda}]
, and
j\in[\dim P_{\lambda}]

such that
\{\left|(\lambda,i,j)\right\rangle\}_{i}
spans an irrep of
\mathrm{U}(d)
isomorphic to
Q_{\lambda}
and
\{\left|(\lambda,i,j)\right\rangle\}_{j}
spans an irrep of
\mathrm{S}_{n}
isomorphic to
P_{\lambda}
.
The main advantage of this basis is that it “decouples” the actions of the unitary and the permutation group on the space.

To see why this is helpful, we remark that a key difficulty in handling

U^{\otimes n}\left|e\right\rangle
is that it contains a lot of “noisy” permutation information irrelevant to the shadow task (i.e., permuting the elements of
e
leads to a population state with the same average state
P
).
When
\left|e\right\rangle
contains only one type of symbol, this joint state lives in the symmetric subspace, and therefore contains no permutation information.
In general,
\left|e\right\rangle
could be far from a symmetric state.
Nonetheless, measuring in the Schur basis allows us to “symmetrize” the input state, and thereby discard the irrelevant permutation information.
This effectively distills the most important information about the unknown state—the partition
\lambda
, which is related to the frequencies of each symbol
i\in[d]
in
\left|e\right\rangle
, and the vector in
Q_{\lambda}
, which is related to the unitary
U
.

The key question is how we formally leverage measurements in the Schur basis
to create an unbiased estimator.
Unfortunately, while there are Schur basis measurement protocols used for full state tomography that output a
\hat{\chi}
that concentrates around
\chi

[
HHJ+16
,
OW16
]
,
their analysis requires the sample size
n
to be at least
d
, which is prohibitively expensive for classical shadows.
Furthermore, Schur bases are not unique and previous constructions tend to be fairly abstract, making rigorous analysis of the measurement’s behavior in the low-sample regime difficult, i.e.,
n\ll d
. A central challenge in our approach is to construct a
concrete
Schur basis and a corresponding measurement protocol
such that the calculation of the expectation/variance of the induced estimator is tractable.

Nice Schur Basis and Local Symmetries

As Schur bases are
not
unique, the specific choice that we make will affect the usefulness of the measurement result for analysis.
A particularly useful property that has appeared before in various quantum information processing tasks
[
BLM+22
,
CEM99
]

on
qubits
(i.e.,
d=2
) is the following: each Schur basis vector completely lives in some subspace spanned by the standard basis vectors with the same
Hamming weight
.
At a high-level, the property helps ensure that measuring in the Schur basis will not disturb the information encoded in the relative frequencies of the symbols in
\left|e\right\rangle
(which are related to the eigenvalues of
\chi
).
To our knowledge, this “nice” Schur basis has never been generalized to arbitrary local dimension
d
. A key technical and conceptual contribution of this work, which may be of independent interest, is to construct one:

Theorem 2
.

There is an algorithm to construct an orthonormal Schur basis
\{\left|(\lambda,i,j)\right\rangle\}_{\lambda,i,j}
for
(\mathbbm{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}
such that each
\left|(\lambda,i,j)\right\rangle\in\Span\{P_{\pi}\left|v\right\rangle\mid\pi\in\mathrm{S}_{n}\}
,
where
\left|v\right\rangle
is some standard basis vector of
(\mathbbm{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}
and
P_{\pi}
permutes the tensor factors of
(\mathbbm{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}
according to
\pi
.

We briefly discuss the ideas behind the construction.
Our starting point is a procedure (for qubits) used in
[
BLM+22
,
CEM99
]
that can extend an arbitrary basis for an irrep
Q_{\lambda}
(viewed as a subspace of
(\mathbbm{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
) to a full Schur basis.
Using a representation-theoretic argument, we show the same procedure works in
(\mathbbm{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}
as well (see
Lemma

15
).
The more involved part is to ensure that the resulting Schur basis respects the additional weight structure and is
orthonormal
.
Towards this goal, we use the fact that the Young Symmetrizer
Y_{\lambda}
(see
Definition

18
) is (proportional to) a projector onto some irrep
Q_{\lambda}
.
This allows us to construct our initial
Q_{\lambda}
basis by running the Gram-Schmidt process on the projected weight subspace
Y_{\lambda}\;\text{span}\{P_{\pi}\left|e\right\rangle\mid\pi\in S_{n}\}
for each individual standard basis vector
\left|e\right\rangle
.
This effectively ensures orthonormality and the weight structure on the initial
Q_{\lambda}
basis.
Lastly, we exploit irreducibility of
Q_{\lambda}
and properties of the Young symmetrizer to show orthonormality of the full induced Schur basis (see
Lemma

22
).

Using this nice Schur basis,
we obtain a general “pre-processing” routine (inspired by
[
BLM+22
]
) for
U^{\otimes n}\left|e\right\rangle
which produces a state
U^{\otimes n}\left|\tau\right\rangle

where
\left|\tau\right\rangle\in\Span\{P_{\pi}\left|e\right\rangle\mid\pi\in S_{n}\}
, and a partition
\lambda\vdash n
(arising from Schur sampling).
Moreover,
\left|\tau\right\rangle
has local symmetries reflecting the measured partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{r})
.
Specifically, if we partition the qudits of
\left|\tau\right\rangle
according to
\lambda
(i.e., into parts of size
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r}
in order), then the state is invariant under all local permutations within each part.

We can then exploit these local symmetries by measuring our processed stated
U^{\otimes n}\left|\tau\right\rangle
with a continuous POVM of the form

\{(\left|\psi_{1}\right\rangle\!\left\langle\psi_{1}\right|)^{\otimes\lambda_{1}}\otimes(\left|\psi_{2}\right\rangle\!\left\langle\psi_{2}\right|)^{\otimes\lambda_{2}}\otimes\cdots\otimes(\left|\psi_{r}\right\rangle\!\left\langle\psi_{r}\right|)^{\otimes\lambda_{r}}\}_{\psi_{1},\psi_{2},\ldots,\psi_{r}}\,,

where each
\psi_{1},\psi_{2},\ldots,\psi_{r}
are drawn independently from the Haar measure. This construction ensures that the POVM elements conveniently integrate to a uniform sum of permutations that also respect the
\lambda
partition.
As a direct consequence,
the measurement is guaranteed to succeed due to the invariance of
\left|\tau\right\rangle
under these local permutations.
After the measurement, our estimator
\hat{P}
will be a simple linear combination of the
\psi_{i}
.
Moreover, the integrals involved in the calculation of the expectation/variance of
\hat{P}
will simplify into a uniform sum of permutations as well.
This allows us to avoid complicated calculations with the Weingarten calculus that are often required for Haar integrals over general states
[
RY17
]
.

Putting all these techniques together (and brushing over some details), we arrive at the following theorem:

Theorem 3

(Informal)
.

From the output of the POVM on the pre-processed state of
U^{\otimes n}\left|e\right\rangle
, there is an unbiased estimator
\hat{P}
such that
{\mathsf{E}}[\hat{P}]=P
and

\displaystyle\Var[\tr(O\hat{P})]=\mathcal{O}\!\left(1+\frac{rB}{n^{2}}\right)

for any observable
O
with
\left\|O\right\|_{\infty}=1,\tr(O^{2})\leq B
and for any
e\in[d]^{n}
with at most
r
different symbols.

In particular, we will have that
\Var[\tr(O\hat{P})]\leq O(1)
whenever
n\gg\sqrt{rB}
.
Unfortunately, the variance bound from
Theorem

3
stays constant even as
n
goes to infinity.

Hence, we circumvent the issue with a repetition trick: partition the input state
U^{\otimes n}\left|e\right\rangle
into
T=\Theta(1/\epsilon^{2})

U^{\otimes n}\left|e\right\rangle=\bigotimes_{t=1}^{T}U^{\otimes(n/T)}|e^{(t)}\rangle
; compute a shadow estimator
\hat{P}^{(t)}
for each segment separately; finally, take the empirical average.

6

6

If we are in the common setting where the input is given by
\chi^{\otimes n}

1/\epsilon^{2}

It is not hard to see that the resulting estimator is still unbiased and the variance now shrinks by a factor of

The result then follows directly from applying Chebyshev’s inequality with this variance bound.

1.2
Discussion and open problems

Our work leaves open many possible future directions. Perhaps the most straightforward is to find optimal bounds for classical shadows that scale with the rank of the unknown state. For example, for full state quantum tomography, the sample-complexity of the optimal joint measurement procedure scales linearly with the rank
r
—precisely,
\mathcal{O}(dr/\epsilon^{2})

[
OW16
,
HHJ+16
]
. Interestingly, our
Theorem

1
shows that sample complexity of classical shadows scales at most with the square root of
r
. Could this be improved? Technically, the only lower bound we have comes from the pure state setting, where it was shown that
\Omega(\sqrt{B}/\epsilon+1/\epsilon^{2})
samples are required
[
GPS22
]
.

There are two reasons one might suspect that our algorithm is not yet achieving optimal sample complexity. First, when
\chi
has full rank, our measurement can be reinterpreted as
\mathcal{O}(d)

independent
measurements on
\chi^{\otimes n}
with high probability. In other words, there are no large entangling measurements, which are crucial for achieving an advantage in sample complexity. Indeed, this is why we achieve the same sample complexity as
[
HKP20
]
in the high rank regime.

Second, our sample complexity does not scale smoothly with rank. In particular, for rank 1 states, our measurement is identical to that of
[
GPS22
]
, and so we achieve the optimal sample complexity:
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{B}/\epsilon+1/\epsilon^{2})
. However, for rank 2 states, our sample complexity jumps to
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{B}/\epsilon^{2})
. Technically, this happens because of a cross term in the variance that does not appear in the pure state case. Unfortunately, we show that this jump in scaling is inherent with our current measurement and analysis (see
Appendix

D
), suggesting that the measurement may need to be changed if one believes that the scaling with rank should be smooth.

Finally, we ask if there is a version of our algorithm which is robust to small perturbations. Rank is an inherently fragile measure, and one might suspect that when
\chi
has large overlap with a low-rank subspace, then the sample complexity should be small, even if
\chi
is technically full rank.

Preliminaries

Given a vector space
V
, we denote by
L(V)
the set of all linear operators on
V
, and
\mathrm{GL}(V)
the set of all invertible linear operators on
V
(also known as the
general linear group
on
V
).
Given a linear operator
H\in L(\mathbbm{C}^{d})
, we write
\left\|H\right\|_{p}
to mean the Schatten
p
-norm. Specifically,
\left\|H\right\|_{1}=\tr(|H|)
is the trace norm,

\left\|H\right\|_{2}=\sqrt{\tr(HH^{\dagger})}
is the Frobenius norm, and

\left\|H\right\|_{\infty}
is the spectral norm.
For any
B\in(0,d]
, we define
\mathrm{Obs}(B)
as the set of Hermitian observables
O\in\mathbbm{C}^{d\times d}
with
\left\|O\right\|_{\infty}=1
and
\tr(O^{2})\leq B

The task of classical shadow is formulated as follows:

Definition 4

(Classical Shadows Task)
.

We say an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is a valid protocol for classical shadows task with sample complexity
n_{\textrm{c}}(\epsilon,r,B)
if for any unknown state
\chi
with rank
\leq r
and for all
\epsilon,B>0
, algorithm
\mathcal{A}
takes
n_{\textrm{c}}(\epsilon,r,B)
many copies of state
\chi
and outputs a classical representation
\hat{\chi}
satisfying the following: for any observable
O\in\mathrm{Obs}(B)
, with probability
>2/3
,

\left|\tr(O\hat{\chi})-\tr(O\chi)\right|\leq\epsilon.

where the probability is over the randomness of algorithm
\mathcal{A}
.

For simplicity, we have given a definition of the classical shadow task which is more stringent than the classical shadows task introduced by
[
HKP20
]
or
[
GPS22
]
. Technically, the shadow can have any form which allows us to approximate
\tr(O\chi)
. In particular, the standard median-of-means procedure used to boost probability of success does not fall into this framework. That said, any algorithm satisfying our procedure can be boosted in the same way: repeat the shadow procedure
k
times to produce a list of shadows
\hat{\chi}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\chi}_{k}
; then, given an observable
O
, output the median of
\{\tr(O\hat{\chi}_{i})\}_{i\in k}
. By standard analysis
[
HKP20
]
, the probability of failure decreases exponentially in
k
.

A mixed state
\chi^{\otimes n}
can be written as a statistical mixture of pure states
U^{\otimes n}\left|e\right\rangle
where
\left|e\right\rangle
is some standard basis vector in
(\mathbbm{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}
and
U
is some unitary matrix. Motivated by this, we define a variant of classical shadow task, where the input is given by a list of non-identical but independent pure states.

Definition 5

(Population Classical Shadow Task)
.

We say an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is a valid protocol for population classical shadows task with sample complexity
n=n_{\textrm{p}}(\epsilon,r,B)
if the following holds. Let
U
be any unknown unitary and
\left|e\right\rangle
be any unknown standard basis vector in
(\mathbbm{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}
. Assume
\left|e\right\rangle
contains at most
r
symbols.
Then, the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
takes state
U^{\otimes n}\left|e\right\rangle
as input, and outputs a classical representation
\hat{P}
satisfying: for any observable
O\in\mathrm{Obs}(B)
, with probability
>9/10
,

\left|\tr(O\hat{P})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tr(OU\left|e_{i}\right\rangle\left\langle e_{i}\right|U^{\dagger})\right|\leq\epsilon\,,

where the probability is over the randomness of algorithm
\mathcal{A}
.

2.1
Representation theory

In this section, we discuss the representation theory of the permutation group
\mathrm{S}_{n}
and the unitary group
\mathrm{U}(d)
in the space
(\mathbbm{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}
.
For a more thorough walk through of the subject, we refer the readers to
[
FH13
]
.
For simplicity, we assume all vector spaces have finite dimensions, and are over the field of complex numbers
\mathbbm{C}
.

Definition 6

(Group Representation)
.

A
representation
of a group
G
on a vector space
V
is a group homomorphism
\rho\colon G\to\mathrm{GL}(V)
. The space
V
is called the
representation space
. But when the homomorphism is clear from context, it is also customary to refer to
V

A
subrepresentation space

W\subseteq V

V

3.1
Reduction to Population Classical Shadows

Using standard probability theory, we can show that an algorithm for population classical shadow task implies an algorithm for the (original) classical shadow task for mixed states.

Lemma 24

(Classical Shadows using Population Classical Shadows)
.

Suppose algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is a valid protocol for population classical shadows task with sample complexity
n_{\textrm{p}}(\epsilon,r,B)
.
Then it is also a valid for classical shadows task with sample complexity
n_{\textrm{c}}(\epsilon,r,B)=\max(n_{\textrm{p}}(\epsilon/2,r,B),\epsilon^{-2})
.

Proof.

Say we run algorithm
\mathcal{A}
on
n
copies of an unknown state
\chi
. We will show that this means, with probability at least
9/10
, we run algorithm
\mathcal{A}
on the joint state
U^{\otimes n}\left|e\right\rangle
satisfying that

\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tr(OU\left|e_{i}\right\rangle\left\langle e_{i}\right|U^{\dagger})-\tr(O\chi)\right|\leq\epsilon.

This will be enough for our main claim, because
\mathcal{A}
is a valid protocol for population classical shadows task implies conditioned on running algorithm
\mathcal{A}
on the state
U^{\otimes n}\left|e\right\rangle
, the algorithm outputs
\hat{P}
which satisfies: for any observable
O
, with probability
9/10
,

\left|\tr(O\hat{P})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tr(OU\left|e_{i}\right\rangle\left\langle e_{i}\right|U^{\dagger})\right|\leq\epsilon

Therefore, together this implies: on running algorithm
\mathcal{A}
on joint state
\chi^{\otimes n}
, it outputs
\hat{P}
such that for any observable
O
, with probability
>4/5
,

\left|\tr(O\hat{P})-\tr(O\chi)\right|\leq 2\epsilon\,.

We now prove our claim. Suppose the unknown
1
-qudit rank
r
quantum state
\chi
can be written as
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_{i}U\left|i\right\rangle\left\langle i\right|U^{\dagger}
. Then, the state
\chi^{\otimes n}
can be written as a statistical mixture of the form:

\chi^{\otimes n}=U^{\otimes n}\sum_{w\in[n]^{d}:|w|=n}\Pr[X=w]\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}P_{\pi}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{d}\left(\left|i\right\rangle\left\langle i\right|\right)^{\otimes w_{i}}P_{\pi}^{\dagger}{U^{\dagger}}^{\otimes n}\,,

where
X
follows the multinomial distribution

\text{Mul}(n,\sigma)
.
Define the random variable

\displaystyle S:=\sum_{i=1}^{d}X_{i}\tr\left(OU\left|i\right\rangle\left\langle i\right|U^{\dagger}\right)\,,

where
X
follows the multinomial distribution
\text{Mul}(n,\sigma)
.
We note that

{\mathsf{E}}[S]=\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\mathsf{E}}[X_{i}]\tr\left(OU\left|i\right\rangle\left\langle i\right|U^{\dagger}\right)=n\;\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma_{i}U\left|i\right\rangle\left\langle i\right|U^{\dagger}=n\tr(O\chi)

For convenience, we define

o_{i}=:\tr\left(OU\left|i\right\rangle\!\left\langle i\right|U^{\dagger}\right)
.
Then
we can bound the variance of
S
from above by

\displaystyle\Var[S]={\mathsf{E}}[S^{2}]-{\mathsf{E}}^{2}[S]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}o_{i}^{2}\Var[X_{i}]+\sum_{i\neq j}o_{i}\;o_{j}\;\left({\mathsf{E}}[X_{i}X_{j}]-{\mathsf{E}}[X_{i}]{\mathsf{E}}[X_{j}]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Var[X_{i}]\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}n\sigma_{i}=n\,,

where in the first inequality
we use the fact that
\tr(OU\left|i\right\rangle\left\langle i\right|U^{\dagger})\leq 1
, and that the covariance between any two coordinates

X_{i},X_{j}
of a multinomial distribution is non-positive,
in the second inequality we use the fact that
\Var[X_{i}]=n\sigma_{i}(1-\sigma_{i})
.
Therefore, by Chebyshev’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\left|S/n-\tr(O\chi)\right|\leq\epsilon\right]\geq 9/10\,,

as long as
n\gg\epsilon^{-2}
. This concludes our proof.
∎

(\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{k})
proportional to the product Haar measure
\mu_{H}^{k}
, plus a “fail” outcome
I-\Pi_{\mathrm{sym}}^{(\lambda)}
for states in orthogonal complement of the row symmetric subspace. Here
\kappa_{\lambda_{i}}
is the dimension of symmetric subspace of an
\lambda_{i}
-qudit system (see
Equation

6
for the definition of
\kappa_{\lambda_{i}}
).

The validity of our measurement follows from the validity of the standard joint measurement over the symmetric subspace.

Lemma 32

(Orthogonal projector on row symmetric subspace)
.

For any partition
\lambda=\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k}\}
, the expectation of
F^{(\lambda)}(\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{k})
under the Haar measure is a projection onto the row symmetric subspace:

{\mathsf{E}}_{(\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\ldots,\psi_{k})\sim\mu_{H}^{k}}\left[F^{(\lambda)}(\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{k})\right]=\Pi_{\mathrm{sym}}^{(\lambda)}.

Proof.

The lemma follows from

Equation

5
and
the following standard equality (see
[
Sco06
]
) concerning integral over the Haar measure:

\kappa_{s}{\mathsf{E}}_{\psi\sim\mu_{H}}\left[\psi^{\otimes s}\right]=\Pi_{\text{sym}}^{(s)}.

∎

The row symmetric joint measurement is appealing to us for the following reason.
The generic pre-processing
\Gamma
produces a state that lives in the canonical irrep
\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}:=Y_{\lambda}(\mathbbm{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}
, which is included in the
\lambda
-row symmetric subspace.
Thus, the
\lambda
-row symmetric joint measurement
always outputs meaningful outcome of the form
F^{(\lambda)}(\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{k})
for some pure states
\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{k}
instead of the “failure” outcome
I-\Pi_{\mathrm{sym}}^{(\lambda)}
.
This is much more efficient than the naive strategy of directly performing the standard joint measurement over the symmetric subspace on the initial input
\chi^{\otimes n}
, which produces “meaningful” outcome with negligible probability as
\chi^{\otimes n}
may have trivial components within the symmetric subspace when
\chi
has rank
>1
.

Lemma 33
.

Let
U^{\otimes n}\left|\tau\right\rangle
be a state obtained via the pre-processing step from
Lemma

27
.
Then we have

\Pi_{\mathrm{sym}}^{(\lambda)}U^{\otimes n}\left|\tau\right\rangle=U^{\otimes n}\left|\tau\right\rangle.

Proof.

Recall that we define
\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}
to be the image of
Y_{\lambda}
in
(\mathbbm{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}
.
Thus, we have

{\left|\tau\right\rangle}=Y_{\lambda}\left|u\right\rangle
for some state
\left|u\right\rangle\in(\mathbbm{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}
.
From the definition of row symmetric subspace above,
Definition

29
, we have

\Pi_{\mathrm{sym}}^{(\lambda)}=\frac{1}{\left|A_{\lambda}\right|}\sum_{\pi\in A_{\lambda}}P_{\pi}.

Recall the definition of Young symmetrizer
Y_{\lambda}
,
Definition

18
, implies for any permutation
\pi\in A_{\lambda}
,

P_{\pi}Y_{\lambda}=P_{\pi}\sum_{a\in A_{\lambda}}P_{a}\sum_{b\in B_{\lambda}}\mathrm{sgn}(b)\;P_{b}=Y_{\lambda}.

Since
\Pi_{\mathrm{sym}}^{(\lambda)}
commutes with
U^{\otimes n}
, the lemma hence follows.
∎

3.5
Expected value of estimator
\Psi

Suppose the generic pre-processing
\Gamma
produces a partition
\lambda\vdash_{d}n
that has exactly
k
parts and a state
\mathrm{U}^{\otimes n}\left|\tau\right\rangle
(see
Lemma

27
).
We then proceed to run the POVM
\mathcal{M}^{(\lambda)}
(see
Definition

31
), which gives a measurement outcome
F^{(\lambda)}(\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{k})
. We then combine
\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{k}
into a single
d\times d
shadow matrix description
\Psi
.
In particular, we consider the following linear combination parametrized by the partition
\lambda
:

\displaystyle\Psi=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(d+\lambda_{i}\right)\psi_{i}.

(7)

In this subsection, we compute the expected value of the shadow matrix
\Psi
conditioned on the post-processing state
U^{\otimes n}\left|\tau\right\rangle
and partition
\lambda
.
From there, it is not hard to see that it can be processed into an unbiased estimator of the initial mixed state
\chi
with some elementary algebraic operations.

Lemma 34
.

Suppose the output of our pre-processing step
\Gamma
is a state
U^{\otimes n}\left|\tau\right\rangle
and some partition
\lambda
with exactly
k
parts
(see
Lemma

27
for description of the pre-processing step). Moreover, assume that
\left|\tau\right\rangle\in V(w)
for some weight vector
w\in[n]^{d}
.
Let
F^{(\lambda)}(\psi_{1},\cdots,\psi_{k})
be the measurement outcome after performing the joint measurement
\mathcal{M}^{(\lambda)}
,
and
\Psi
be defined as in
Equation

7
.
Then we have

{\mathsf{E}}[\Psi]=kI+\sum_{i=1}^{d}w_{i}U\left|i\right\rangle\left\langle i\right|U^{\dagger}\,,

where the expectation is over the randomness of the measurement
\mathcal{M}^{(\lambda)}
.

Proof.

In this proof, we will be working with an
(n+1)
qudit quantum state, where we will refer to the first qudit as the
output
qudit, and associate a Young diagram with partition
\lambda\vdash_{d}[n]
to the remaining
n
-qudit system.
Specifically, we use
(j,p)
to index the qudit corresponding to the
p
-th cell in the
j
-th row of the Young diagram.

The expectation of
\Psi
is given by

\displaystyle{\mathsf{E}}[\Psi]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}(d+\lambda_{j}){\mathsf{E}}_{\mu_{H}^{k}}\left[\psi_{j}\tr\left(\bigg(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{k}\kappa_{\lambda_{i}}\;\psi_{i}^{\otimes\lambda_{i}}\bigg)\cdot\bigg(U^{\otimes n}\left|\tau\right\rangle\left\langle\tau\right|U^{\dagger\otimes n}\bigg)\right)\right]\;

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}(d+\lambda_{j}){\mathsf{E}}_{\mu_{H}^{k}}\left[\tr_{-1}\left(\bigg(\psi_{j}\otimes\bigotimes_{i=1}^{k}\kappa_{\lambda_{i}}\;\psi_{i}^{\otimes\lambda_{i}}\bigg)\cdot\bigg(I\otimes(U^{\otimes n}\left|\tau\right\rangle\left\langle\tau\right|U^{\dagger\otimes n})\bigg)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}(d+\lambda_{j})\tr_{-1}\left({\mathsf{E}}_{\mu_{H}^{k}}\bigg[\psi_{j}\otimes\bigotimes_{i=1}^{k}\kappa_{\lambda_{i}}\;\psi_{i}^{\otimes\lambda_{i}}\bigg]\cdot\bigg(I\otimes(U^{\otimes n}\left|\tau\right\rangle\left\langle\tau\right|U^{\dagger\otimes n})\bigg)\right).

(8)

A key step in the computation is to tackle the following expectation expression

{\mathsf{E}}_{\mu_{H}^{k}}\left[\psi_{j}\otimes\bigotimes_{i=1}^{k}\kappa_{\lambda_{i}}\psi_{i}^{\otimes\lambda_{i}}\right]\,,

where
(\psi_{1},\cdots,\psi_{k})\sim\mu_{H}^{k}
, the
k
-fold Haar measure over a
(n+1)
-qudit system. Similar to the proof of
Lemma

32
, this can be simplified into the form of
\bigotimes_{i=1}^{k}L_{i}
, where
L_{i}
is the average of all permutations over the qudits lying in the
i
-th row if
i\neq j
, and
L_{j}
is the average of all permutations over the qudits lying in the
j
-th row and the output qudit.
One can see that this is almost the definition of
\frac{1}{|A_{\lambda}|}\sum_{\pi\in A_{\lambda}}P_{\pi}
except for the fact that
L_{j}
contains permutations involving the output qudit. To simplify this, note that we can further decompose each permutations in
L_{j}
into two parts: a permutation over the qudits in the
j
-th row and an operation that swaps the output qudit with some qudit from the
j
-th row or itself.
We formalize the swap between the output qudit and an arbitrary qudit in
j
th row or itself with the operation below.

Definition 35

(Output Swap Permutation,
\mathrm{Swap}_{o}
)
.

We use
\mathrm{Swap}_{o}((i,j))
to denote the permutation which swaps the output qudit with the
(i,j)
qudit. We overload this definition to define
\mathrm{Swap}_{o}((i,\lambda_{i}+1))
to be the identity matrix.

The observation leads to the following equality:

\displaystyle{\mathsf{E}}_{\mu_{H}^{k}}\left[\psi_{j}\otimes\bigotimes_{i=1}^{k}\kappa_{\lambda_{i}}\;\psi_{i}^{\otimes\lambda_{i}}\right]=\frac{\kappa_{\lambda_{j}}}{\kappa_{\lambda_{j}+1}}\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}+1}\left(\sum_{p=1}^{\lambda_{j}+1}\mathrm{Swap}_{o}((j,p))\right)\;\left(I\otimes\frac{1}{|A_{\lambda}|}\sum_{\pi\in A_{\lambda}}P_{\pi}\right).

(9)

To simplify this further, note that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\lambda_{j}}\;\frac{\kappa_{\lambda_{j}}}{\kappa_{\lambda_{j}+1}}=\frac{1}{d+\lambda_{j}}.

(10)

Moreover, an important property (
Lemma

33
) of our post-processed state
U^{\otimes n}\left|\tau\right\rangle
is that:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|A_{\lambda}|}\sum_{\pi\in A_{\lambda}}P_{\pi}U^{\otimes n}\left|\tau\right\rangle=U^{\otimes n}\left|\tau\right\rangle\,.

(11)

Substitute
Equation

10
into
Equation

9
, then substitute
Equation

9
into
Equation

8
, and lastly simplify the expression using
Equation

11
.
We thus reach the identity

\displaystyle{\mathsf{E}}[\Psi]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{p=1}^{\lambda_{j}+1}\tr_{-1}\left(\mathrm{Swap}_{o}((j,p))\Big(I\otimes(U^{\otimes n}\left|\tau\right\rangle\left\langle\tau\right|U^{\dagger\otimes n})\Big)\right).

(12)

By the property of our pre-processing step (
Lemma

27
),
we know
\left|\tau\right\rangle
lives in the same weight subspace as
\left|e\right\rangle
.
Hence, it can be written as the following linear combination:

\displaystyle\left|\tau\right\rangle=\sum_{\left|a\right\rangle\in E_{n}(w)}c_{a}\left|a\right\rangle\,,

where we denote by
E_{n}(w)
the set of standard basis vectors on
n
qudits that
lie in the weight subspace of
V(w)
, i.e.,
\left|a\right\rangle\in E_{n}(w)
have
w_{i}
many qudits equal to
\left|i\right\rangle
for
i\in[d]
.
Therefore, we can expand
Equation

12
:

\displaystyle{\mathsf{E}}[\Psi]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\left|a\right\rangle,\left|a^{\prime}\right\rangle\in E_{n}(w)}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{p=1}^{\lambda_{j}+1}c_{a}\;c_{a^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\;\tr_{-1}\left(\mathrm{Swap}_{o}((j,p))\;\bigg(I\otimes(U^{\otimes n}\left|a\right\rangle\left\langle a^{\prime}\right|U^{\dagger\otimes n})\bigg)\right).

Fix some
a,a^{\prime}
.
We claim that the inner expression vanishes if
\left|a\right\rangle\neq\left|a^{\prime}\right\rangle
.
Assume
\left|a\right\rangle\neq\left|a^{\prime}\right\rangle
.
Since the weight vectors of
\left|a\right\rangle
and
\left|a^{\prime}\right\rangle
are the same,
\left|a\right\rangle
must disagree with
\left|a^{\prime}\right\rangle
in at least two qudits.
Hence, in the end, there must be at least one qudit where
\left|a\right\rangle
disagrees with
\left|a^{\prime}\right\rangle
that has been traced out, making the partial trace vanish.
It then follows that

\displaystyle{\mathsf{E}}[\Psi]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\left|a\right\rangle\in E_{n}(w)}|c_{a}|^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{p=1}^{\lambda_{j}+1}\tr_{-1}\left(\mathrm{Swap}_{o}((j,p))\;\bigg(I\otimes(U^{\otimes n}\left|a\right\rangle\left\langle a\right|U^{\dagger\otimes n})\bigg)\right).

Now, the partial trace

\tr_{-1}\left(\mathrm{Swap}_{o}((j,p))\;\bigg(I\otimes(U^{\otimes n}\left|a\right\rangle\left\langle a\right|U^{\dagger\otimes n})\bigg)\right)

can be evaluated for a fixed
j
and
p
.
In particular, it evaluates to
I
whenever
p=\lambda_{j}+1
, and evaluates to
U\left|a_{j,p}\right\rangle\left\langle a_{j,p}\right|U^{\dagger}
otherwise.
Since
\left|a\right\rangle\in E_{n}(w)
, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{p=1}^{\lambda_{j}+1}\tr_{-1}\left(\mathrm{Swap}_{o}((j,p))\;\bigg(I\otimes U^{\otimes n}\left|a\right\rangle\left\langle a\right|U^{\dagger\otimes n}\bigg)\right)=k\;I+\sum_{i=1}^{d}w_{i}\;U\left|i\right\rangle\left\langle i\right|U^{\dagger}.

Since the above sum is the same for each
\left|a\right\rangle\in E_{n}(w)
and
\sum_{\left|a\right\rangle\in E_{n}(w)}|c_{a}|^{2}=1\,,

we reach the conclusion

\displaystyle{\mathsf{E}}[\Psi]=kI+\sum_{i=1}^{d}w_{i}U\left|i\right\rangle\left\langle i\right|U^{\dagger}.

∎

3.6
Variance of
\tr(O\Psi)

In this subsection, we show how to bound the variance of
\tr(O\Psi)
conditioned on the partition
\lambda
.

Lemma 36
.

Let
O
be the observable received and

\Psi
be the shadow matrix defined as in
Lemma

34
. Then we have

\Var[\tr(O\Psi)]={\mathsf{E}}[\tr(O\Psi)^{2}]-{\mathsf{E}}[\tr(O\Psi)]^{2}\leq k\;\tr(O^{2})+\mathcal{O}(n)\left\|O^{2}\right\|_{\infty}+\mathcal{O}(n^{2})\left\|O\right\|_{\infty}^{2}\,,

where the variance is over the randomness of the measurement
\mathcal{M}^{(\lambda)}
.

Before we dive into the proof, we briefly comment on the term
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})\left\|O\right\|_{\infty}^{2}
in our upper bound.
In order to process
\Psi
into an unbiased estimator of the unknown state
\chi
, we will scale it down by a factor of
n
.
The “scaled” variance then becomes

\frac{k}{n^{2}}\;\tr(O^{2})+\mathcal{O}(1/n)\left\|O^{2}\right\|_{\infty}+\mathcal{O}(1)\left\|O\right\|_{\infty}^{2}.

One can see that while the remaining terms approach
0
as
n
increases, the last term remains a constant for all
n
.
Thus, in order to make sure that our final estimator has its variance bounded from above by
\epsilon^{2}
, we need to repeat the entire process
1/\epsilon^{2}
many times, and take the empirical average.
This leads to the multiplicative factor
\epsilon^{-2}
in our algorithm’s final sample complexity.

One may wonder whether the term is simply an artifact of our analysis. To answer this, we show in
Appendix

D
that there is a natural barrier in improving the variance bound.
In particular, we demonstrate a concrete setup of quantum state
\left|\tau\right\rangle=\sum_{\pi}\alpha_{\pi}P_{\pi}\left|0\right\rangle^{\otimes p}\left|1\right\rangle^{\otimes q}
, partition
\lambda=(n)
and observable
O
, where we show that the scaled variance

\frac{1}{n^{2}}\Var\left[O\Psi\right]

is indeed lower bounded by some constant
regardless of
the size of
n
due to an unexpected “cross-term”.

Surprisingly, this indicates that the output of the measurement goes through a “phase-change” as the quantum state
\left|\tau\right\rangle
evolves from a highly symmetric pure state

\left|0\right\rangle^{\otimes n}
to another non-symmetric pure state
\sum_{\pi}\alpha_{\pi}P_{\pi}\left|0\right\rangle^{\otimes p}\otimes\left|1\right\rangle^{\otimes q}
— a state that naturally arises from rank-
2
classical shadow.
This is because for
\lambda=(n)
,
the measurement
\mathcal{M}^{(\lambda)}
is identical to
the standard symmetric subspace joint measurement
from
[
GPS22
]
. Thus, for the symmetric state
\left|\tau\right\rangle=\left|0\right\rangle^{\otimes n}
, the distribution of the estimator is identical to that of the classical shadow estimator from
[
GPS22
]
built for the pure state
U\left|0\right\rangle\left\langle 0\right|U^{\dagger}
, implying that its variance will go to
0
as
n
increases. But as we discussed above, the variance for the non-symmetric pure state
\sum_{\pi}\alpha_{\pi}P_{\pi}\left|0\right\rangle^{\otimes p}\otimes\left|1\right\rangle^{\otimes q}
always stays lower bounded by a constant.

We now present the proof of
Lemma

36
.

Proof.

First, we argue that we can assume that
O
is a traceless observable without loss of generality, i.e.,
\tr(O)=0
.
In particular, consider

O_{0}=O-\tr(O)I/d
.

O_{0}
is a traceless observable.
Assume that we can show
Lemma

36
for
O_{0}
, then we will have

\Var[\tr(O\Psi)]=\Var[\tr(O_{0}\Psi)]\leq k\tr(O_{0}^{2})+\mathcal{O}(n)\left\|O_{0}^{2}\right\|_{\infty}+\mathcal{O}(n^{2})\left\|O_{0}\right\|_{\infty}^{2}.

It then suffices to show that

\tr(O_{0}^{2})\leq\tr(O^{2})
,

\left\|O_{0}^{2}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\mathcal{O}(1)\left\|O^{2}\right\|_{\infty}
, and

\left\|O_{0}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\mathcal{O}(1)\left\|O\right\|_{\infty}
, which has been shown in Corollary 15 of
[
GPS22
]
.

In this proof, we will be working with an
(n+2)
-qudit quantum system.
We will refer to the first and the second qudits as the first and the second output qudits respectively, and again associate a Young diagram with partition
\lambda\vdash_{d}n
to the remaining qudits.

Suppose the post-processed state is
U^{\otimes n}\left|\tau\right\rangle
. Then, the second moment of the estimator
\Psi
is given by

\displaystyle{\mathsf{E}}\left[\Psi\otimes\Psi\right]

\displaystyle=\hskip-6.00006pt\sum_{j,j^{\prime}\in[k]}\left(\lambda_{j}+d\right)\left(\lambda_{j^{\prime}}+d\right)\tr_{-1,-2}\left({\mathsf{E}}_{\mu_{H}^{k}}\left[\psi_{j}\otimes\psi_{j^{\prime}}\otimes\bigotimes_{i=1}^{k}\kappa_{\lambda_{i}}\psi_{i}^{\otimes\lambda_{i}}\right]\cdot\bigg(I\otimes I\otimes(U^{\otimes n}\left|\tau\right\rangle\left\langle\tau\right|U^{\dagger\otimes n})\bigg)\right).

(13)

Again, the key step is in simplifying the quantity

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}&\frac{\kappa_{\lambda_{j}}}{\kappa_{\lambda_{j}+1}}\frac{\kappa_{\lambda_{j^{\prime}}}}{\kappa_{\lambda_{j^{\prime}}+1}}\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}+1}\frac{1}{\lambda_{j^{\prime}}+1}\left(\sum_{p=1}^{\lambda_{j}+1}\sum_{p^{\prime}=1}^{\lambda_{j^{\prime}}+1}\mathrm{Swap}_{o}((j,p),(j^{\prime},p^{\prime}))\right)\;\left(I^{\otimes 2}\otimes\frac{1}{|A_{\lambda}|}\sum_{\pi\in A_{\lambda}}P_{\pi}\right)\text{ for }j\neq j^{\prime}\\

(15)

\frac{\lambda_{j}+d}{\lambda_{j}+1}\frac{\lambda_{j^{\prime}}+d}{\lambda_{j^{\prime}}+1}\frac{\kappa_{\lambda_{j}}}{\kappa_{\lambda_{j}+1}}\frac{\kappa_{\lambda_{j^{\prime}}}}{\kappa_{\lambda_{j^{\prime}}+1}}=1\,,

,

\displaystyle\left|\tau\right\rangle=\sum_{\left|a\right\rangle\in E_{n}(w)}c_{a}\left|a\right\rangle\,,

Proof.

\left|v\right\rangle

\displaystyle\sum_{k,\ell\in[d]}\tr_{-(1,2)}\left(U^{\otimes(n+2)}\mathrm{OrderedSwap}_{o}(j,p,p^{\prime})\cdot\left(\Pi_{\mathrm{sym}}^{(2)}\otimes I^{\otimes n}\right)\cdot\bigg(\left|k\;\ell\;a\right\rangle\left\langle k\;\ell\;a^{\prime}\right|\bigg)U^{\dagger\otimes(n+2)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k,\ell\in[d]}U^{\otimes 2}\tr_{-(1,2)}\left(\mathrm{OrderedSwap}_{o}(j,p,p^{\prime})\cdot\bigg(\left|k\;\ell\;a\right\rangle\left\langle k\;\ell\;a^{\prime}\right|\bigg)\right)U^{\dagger\otimes 2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k,\ell\in[d]}U^{\otimes 2}\tr_{-(1,2)}\left(\mathrm{OrderedSwap}_{o}(j,p,p^{\prime})\cdot\bigg(\left|\ell\;k\;a\right\rangle\left\langle k\;\ell\;a^{\prime}\right|\bigg)\right)U^{\dagger\otimes 2}.

For the first term, we note that the partial-trace is non-vanishing only if

\left|k\right\rangle=\left|a^{\prime}_{j,p}\right\rangle

and
\left|\ell\right\rangle=\left|a^{\prime}_{j^{\prime},p^{\prime}}\right\rangle
.
Thus, it can be simplified into

\frac{1}{2}U^{\otimes 2}\left|a_{j,p}\;a_{j^{\prime}p^{\prime}}\right\rangle\left\langle a^{\prime}_{j,p}\;a^{\prime}_{j^{\prime}p^{\prime}}\right|U^{\dagger\otimes 2}.

For the second term, we note that the partial trace is non-vanishing only if
\left|\ell\right\rangle=\left|a^{\prime}_{j,p}\right\rangle

and
\left|k\right\rangle=\left|a^{\prime}_{j^{\prime},p^{\prime}}\right\rangle
.
Thus, it can be simplified into

\frac{1}{2}U^{\otimes 2}\left|a_{j,p}\;a_{j^{\prime},p^{\prime}}\right\rangle\left\langle a^{\prime}_{j^{\prime},p^{\prime}}\;a^{\prime}_{j,p}\right|U^{\dagger\otimes 2}=\frac{1}{2}U^{\otimes 2}\left|a_{j,p}\;a_{j^{\prime},p^{\prime}}\right\rangle\left\langle a^{\prime}_{j,p}\;a^{\prime}_{j^{\prime},p^{\prime}}\right|U^{\dagger\otimes 2}\text{Swap}^{(2)}

where
\text{Swap}^{(2)}
is the permutation that swaps the two qudits.
Combining the two terms then concludes the proof of the claim.
∎

Fact 61
.

Let
\left|x\right\rangle,\left|y\right\rangle
be two unit vectors.
Furthermore, assume
\tr(O)=0
.
Then we have

\displaystyle\tr\left(\left(O\otimes O\right)\;\left(\left|x\right\rangle\left\langle x\right|\otimes I\right)\Pi_{\mathrm{sym}}^{(2)}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\|O^{2}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\tr\left(\left(O\otimes O\right)\;\left(\left|x\right\rangle\left\langle x\right|\otimes\left|y\right\rangle\left\langle y\right|\right)\Pi_{\mathrm{sym}}^{(2)}\right)\leq\left\|O\right\|_{\infty}^{2}.

Proof.

To show the first inequality, we note that

\tr\left(\left(O\otimes O\right)\;\left(\left|x\right\rangle\left\langle x\right|\otimes I\right)\Pi_{\mathrm{sym}}^{(2)}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\tr(O)\;\left\langle x\right|O\left|x\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\left\langle x\right|O^{2}\left|x\right\rangle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\|O^{2}\right\|_{\infty}.

To show the second inequality, we note that

\tr\left(\left(O\otimes O\right)\;\left(\left|x\right\rangle\left\langle x\right|\otimes\left|y\right\rangle\left\langle y\right|\right)\Pi_{\mathrm{sym}}^{(2)}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle x\right|O\left|x\right\rangle\left\langle y\right|O\left|y\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\left\langle y\right|O\left|x\right\rangle\left\langle y\right|O\left|x\right\rangle\leq\left\|O\right\|_{\infty}^{2}.

∎

Appendix D
Variance Lower Bound

In this section, we present a concrete setup of
the post-processing state
U^{\otimes n}\left|\tau\right\rangle
, partition outcome
\lambda\vdash n
, and observable
O
such that the bound from
Lemma

36
on
\Var[O\Psi]
is tight up to a constant factor.

First, assume the generic pre-processing
\Gamma
returns the partition
\lambda=(n)
and a state of the form

U^{\otimes n}\left|(\lambda,i,0)\right\rangle
, where
\left|(\lambda,i,0)\right\rangle
lies in the same weight subspace as
\left|0\right\rangle^{\otimes p}\otimes\left|1\right\rangle^{\otimes q}
for some
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
with
p+q=n
.
We note that this is a valid output for our generic pre-processing
\Gamma
, because in this case each subspace
Y_{\lambda}V(w)
is at most
1
-dimensional. Hence, without loss of generality, we can assume that the output state is of the form mentioned above (with a change in unitary).

We proceed to compute the variance of the shadow matrix
\Psi
after running the POVM
\mathcal{M}^{\lambda}
on state
U^{\otimes n}\left|(\lambda,i,0)\right\rangle
.
To simplify the analysis even further, we assume that
U=I
and
O
is a traceless observable.

Assumption 62
.

The generic pre-processing step returns

\lambda=(n)
, and the state
\left|(\lambda,i,0)\right\rangle
, where
\left|(\lambda,i,0)\right\rangle\in\Span\{P_{\pi}\left|0\right\rangle^{\otimes p}\left|1\right\rangle^{\otimes q}\mid\pi\in S_{n}\}
for some natural numbers
p+q=n
.
Moreover, the observable
O
is traceless, i.e.,
\tr(O)=0
.

Lemma 63
.

Let
O
be the observable received and

\Psi
be the shadow matrix defined as in
Lemma

34
.
Under
Assumption

62
, it holds that

\displaystyle\Var[O\Psi]=

\displaystyle\frac{n+d}{n+d+1}\bigg(\tr(O^{2})+2p\left\langle 0\right|O^{2}\left|0\right\rangle+2q\left\langle 1\right|O^{2}\left|1\right\rangle+2pq\;\left(\left\langle 0\right|O\left|1\right\rangle\right)^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n+d+1}\left(+p^{2}\left(\left\langle 0\right|O\left|0\right\rangle\right)^{2}+q^{2}\left(\left\langle 1\right|O\left|1\right\rangle\right)^{2}+2pq\;\left\langle 0\right|O\left|0\right\rangle\;\left\langle 1\right|O\left|1\right\rangle\right).

Moreover, whenever

O=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&\cdots\\
1&-1&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{bmatrix}
,
p,q=\Theta(n)
, we have that

\frac{1}{n^{2}}\Var[O\Psi]\geq\Omega\left(\frac{pq}{n^{2}}\;\left(\left\langle 0\right|O\left|1\right\rangle\right)^{2}\right)\geq\Omega(1).

for all sufficiently large
n
.

Proof.

We begin by computing the second moment
{\mathsf{E}}[\Psi\otimes\Psi]
in a fashion similar to the proof of
Lemma

36
.
The computation is easier as we now assume
\lambda
only has one part.
In particular, following identical steps, we reach the expression

\displaystyle{\mathsf{E}}[\Psi\otimes\Psi]

\displaystyle=\frac{2(n+d)^{2}}{n(n+1)}\frac{\kappa_{n}}{\kappa_{n+2}}\sum_{p^{\prime}=1}^{n+2}\sum_{p=1}^{p^{\prime}-1}\tr_{-1,-2}\left(\mathrm{OrderedSwap}_{o}(1,p,p^{\prime})\left(\Pi_{\mathrm{sym}}^{(2)}\otimes I^{\otimes 2}\right)\left(I^{\otimes 2}\otimes\left|(\lambda,i,0)\right\rangle\left\langle(\lambda,i,0)\right|\right)\right)

\displaystyle=2\frac{n+d}{n+d+1}\sum_{p^{\prime}=1}^{n+2}\sum_{p=1}^{p^{\prime}-1}\tr_{-1,-2}\left(\mathrm{OrderedSwap}_{o}(1,p,p^{\prime})\left(\Pi_{\mathrm{sym}}^{(2)}\otimes I^{\otimes 2}\right)\left(I^{\otimes 2}\otimes\left|(\lambda,i,0)\right\rangle\left\langle(\lambda,i,0)\right|\right)\right)\,,

where in the second line we use

\frac{1}{n(n+1)}\frac{\kappa_{n}}{\kappa_{n+2}}=\frac{n+d}{n+d+1}.

Let
\left|a\right\rangle,\left|a^{\prime}\right\rangle
be two arbitrary standard basis vector.
Define

\displaystyle T_{p,p^{\prime}}(\left|a\right\rangle,\left|a^{\prime}\right\rangle):=\tr_{-1,-2}\left(\left(\Pi_{\mathrm{sym}}^{(2)}\otimes I^{\otimes n}\right)\cdot\bigg(I\otimes I\otimes(U^{\otimes n}\left|a\right\rangle\left\langle a^{\prime}\right|U^{\dagger\otimes n})\bigg)\right)\,,

\displaystyle T_{p,p^{\prime}}:=\tr_{-1,-2}\left(\left(\Pi_{\mathrm{sym}}^{(2)}\otimes I^{\otimes n}\right)\cdot\bigg(I\otimes I\otimes\left|(\lambda,i,0)\right\rangle\left\langle(\lambda,i,0)\right|\bigg)\right).

To simplify the partial trace, we again break into four cases depending on the values of
p,p^{\prime}
.

Case I
:
p=n+1,p^{\prime}=n+2
. Recall that in this case we have the following more general expression:

\displaystyle T_{p,p^{\prime}}(\left|a\right\rangle,\left|a^{\prime}\right\rangle)=\begin{cases}\Pi_{\mathrm{sym}}^{(2)}\left(I\otimes I\right)&\text{ if }a=a^{\prime}\,,\\
0&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

where
\left|a\right\rangle,\left|a^{\prime}\right\rangle
are two standard basis vectors that lie in the same weight subspace.
In the current setting, this implies that

\displaystyle T_{n+1,n+2}=\tr_{-1,-2}\left(\left(\Pi_{\mathrm{sym}}^{(2)}\otimes I^{\otimes n}\right)\cdot\bigg(I\otimes I\otimes\left|(\lambda,i,0)\right\rangle\left\langle(\lambda,i,0)\right|\bigg)\right)=\Pi_{\mathrm{sym}}^{(2)}\left(I\otimes I\right).

Thus, its contribution to variance is

{\mathsf{E}}[O\otimes OT_{n+1,n+2}]=\frac{1}{2}\tr(O^{2}).

Case II
:
p\in[n],p^{\prime}=n+2
. Recall that in this case we have the following more general expression:

\displaystyle T_{p,p^{\prime}}(\left|a\right\rangle,\left|a^{\prime}\right\rangle)=\begin{cases}\left((U\left|a_{j,p}\right\rangle\left\langle a_{j,p}\right|U^{\dagger})\otimes I\right)\Pi_{\mathrm{sym}}^{(2)}.&\text{ if }a=a^{\prime}\,,\\
0&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

In the current setting, this implies that

\displaystyle\sum_{p=1}^{n}T_{p,n+2}=\left(\left(p\left|0\right\rangle\left\langle 0\right|+q\left|1\right\rangle\left\langle 1\right|\right)\otimes I\right)\Pi_{\mathrm{sym}}^{(2)}.

Thus, its contribution to variance is

{\mathsf{E}}\left[O\otimes O\sum_{p=1}^{n}T_{p,n+2}\right]=\frac{1}{2}\left(p\left\langle 0\right|O^{2}\left|0\right\rangle+q\left\langle 1\right|O^{2}\left|1\right\rangle\right).

Case III
:
p\in[n],p^{\prime}=n+1
. Recall that the general expression in this case is identical to Case II. So its contribution to the variance is also given by

{\mathsf{E}}[O\otimes O\sum_{p=1}^{n}T_{p,n+1}]=\frac{1}{2}\left(p\left\langle 0\right|O^{2}\left|0\right\rangle+q\left\langle 0\right|O^{2}\left|0\right\rangle\right).

Case IV
:

p\in[n],p^{\prime}=[n]
.
Recall that in this case we have the following more general expression:

\displaystyle T_{p,p^{\prime}}(\left|a\right\rangle,\left|a^{\prime}\right\rangle)=\begin{cases}U^{\otimes 2}\left|a_{j,p}a_{j^{\prime},p^{\prime}}\right\rangle\left\langle a^{\prime}_{j,p}a^{\prime}_{j^{\prime},p^{\prime}}\right|U^{\dagger\otimes 2}\Pi_{\mathrm{sym}}^{(2)}&\text{ if }a=a^{\prime}\text{ or }\left|a^{\prime}\right\rangle=\mathrm{Swap}((1,p),(1,p^{\prime}))\left|a\right\rangle\,,\\
0&\text{ otherwise }.\end{cases}

Note that when
\lambda=(n)
and
\left|(\lambda,i,0)\right\rangle
lies in the the same weight subspace as
\left|0\right\rangle^{\otimes p}\otimes\left|1\right\rangle^{\otimes q}
,
\left|(\lambda,i,0)\right\rangle
,
\left|(\lambda,i,0)\right\rangle
has a particularly nice structure — it is the uniform super position of all permutations of

\left|0\right\rangle^{\otimes p}\otimes\left|1\right\rangle^{\otimes q}
.
This then implies that

\sum_{p^{\prime}=2}^{n}\sum_{p=1}^{p^{\prime}-1}T_{p,p^{\prime}}=\frac{p(p-1)}{2}\left(\left|0\right\rangle\left\langle 0\right|\right)^{\otimes 2}+\frac{q(q-1)}{2}\left(\left|1\right\rangle\left\langle 1\right|\right)^{\otimes 2}+2\;pq\left|0\right\rangle\left\langle 0\right|\otimes\left|1\right\rangle\left\langle 1\right|\Pi_{\mathrm{sym}}^{(2)}.

Hence, the contribution to the variance is given by

\displaystyle{\mathsf{E}}\left[O\otimes O\sum_{p^{\prime}=2}^{n}\sum_{p=1}^{p^{\prime}-1}T_{p,p^{\prime}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{p(p-1)}{2}\left(\left\langle 0\right|O\left|0\right\rangle\right)^{2}+\frac{q(q-1)}{2}\left(\left\langle 1\right|O\left|1\right\rangle\right)^{2}+pq\;\left(\left\langle 0\right|O\left|1\right\rangle\right)^{2}+pq\;\left\langle 0\right|O\left|0\right\rangle\;\left\langle 1\right|O\left|1\right\rangle.

Overall, we thus have

\displaystyle{\mathsf{E}}[\tr\left(\left(O\otimes O\right)\;\left(\Psi\otimes\Psi\right)\right)]

\displaystyle=\frac{n+d}{n+d+1}\bigg(\tr(O^{2})+2p\left\langle 0\right|O^{2}\left|0\right\rangle+2q\left\langle 1\right|O^{2}\left|1\right\rangle+p^{2}\left(\left\langle 0\right|O\left|0\right\rangle\right)^{2}+q^{2}\left(\left\langle 1\right|O\left|1\right\rangle\right)^{2}

\displaystyle+2pq\;\left(\left\langle 0\right|O\left|1\right\rangle\right)^{2}+2pq\;\left\langle 0\right|O\left|0\right\rangle\;\left\langle 1\right|O\left|1\right\rangle\bigg).

By
Lemma

34
, we have

{\mathsf{E}}[\Psi]=(I+p\left|0\right\rangle\left\langle 0\right|+q\left|0\right\rangle\left\langle 0\right|).

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\Var[O\Psi]=

\displaystyle\frac{n+d}{n+d+1}\bigg(\tr(O^{2})+2p\left\langle 0\right|O^{2}\left|0\right\rangle+2q\left\langle 1\right|O^{2}\left|1\right\rangle+

\displaystyle 2pq\;\left(\left\langle 0\right|O\left|1\right\rangle\right)^{2}-p\left(\left\langle 0\right|O\left|0\right\rangle\right)^{2}-q\left(\left\langle 1\right|O\left|1\right\rangle\right)^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n+d+1}\left(p^{2}\left(\left\langle 0\right|O\left|0\right\rangle\right)^{2}+q^{2}\left(\left\langle 1\right|O\left|1\right\rangle\right)^{2}+2pq\;\left\langle 0\right|O\left|0\right\rangle\;\left\langle 1\right|O\left|1\right\rangle\right).

It is not hard to see that all negative terms are dominated by the positive term

\frac{n+d}{n+d+1}\;2pq\;\left(\left\langle 0\right|O\left|1\right\rangle\right)^{2}
when
p,q=\Theta(n)
.
Thus, if we set
O=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&\cdots\\
1&-1&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{bmatrix}
, the variance will be
bounded from below by
\Theta(pq/n^{2})\geq\Omega(1)
.
∎
