Deducibility in the full Lambek calculus with weakening is HAck -complete
Vitor Greati, Revantha Ramanayake

Abstract
We show that the problem of deciding the consequence relation (deducibility) for the full Lambek calculus with weakening ( 𝐅𝐋 𝐰 \mathbf{FL}_{\mathbf{w}} ) is complete for the class HAck of hyper-Ackermannian problems (i.e., 𝐅 ω ω \mathbf{F}_{\omega^{\omega}} in the ordinal-indexed hierarchy of fast-growing complexity classes). Provability was already known to be PSpace -complete. We prove that deducibility is HAck -complete even for the multiplicative fragment. Lower bounds are obtained via a novel reduction from reachability in lossy channel systems. Upper bounds are obtained via structural proof theory (forward proof search over sequent calculi) and well-quasi-order theory (length theorems for Higman’s ordering on words over a finite alphabet).

Substructural logics are obtained from

The upper bound argument

We define a forward proof procedure extending the construction in Balasubramanian et al.
[
13
]
. The idea is to start from the set
D_{0}
of initial sequent instances and form an increasing chain
D_{0}\subset D_{1}\subset\ldots
of finite sets of deducible sequents (each step corresponds to limited weakening and then a single rule application), taking care that each
D_{i}
contains only minimal elements under the Higman ordering on words (omit words that can be obtained from another by inserting letters); the ordering is a well-quasi-order (wqo)
[
14
]
so the chain must be finite. By construction, the bad sequence underlying the chain is controlled (no element in
D_{i+1}
is much larger than some element in
D_{i}
), and the complexity is dominated by the maximum length of a controlled bad sequence. This is a function in
HAck
as shown by Schmitz and Schnoebelen
[
6
]
. The argument applies to infinitely-many structural rule extensions of
\mathbf{FL}_{\mathbf{w}}{}
.

Hyper-Ackermannian lower bounds

We obtain hyper-Ackermannian lower bounds
for the sequent-deducibility problem
in
\Sigma
-fragments of

{\mathbf{FL}_{\mathbf{w}}}

where
\otimes\in\Sigma
, via a reduction from the
reachability problem in lossy channel systems.
The result extends to the problem
of deducibility if

\Sigma
also contains
{\setminus}
(or
{/}
),
0
and
1
, in view of Lem.
2
.
We begin by introducing lossy channel systems
and their reachability problem,
and then show how to encode it
into sequent-deducibility
in
{\mathbf{FL}_{\mathbf{w}}^{\otimes}}
.

3.1
Lossy channel systems

A channel system is a computational model with unbounded FIFO channels (queues), defined as follows.

Definition 6
.

A
channel system
(CS)
is a structure
\mathcal{L}:=\left\langle{\mathtt{Q},\mathtt{C},\mathtt{M},\mathtt{I}}\right\rangle
, where

1.

\mathtt{Q}:=\left\{{q_{1},\ldots,q_{m}}\right\}
is a finite set of
states
;

2.

\mathtt{C}:=\left\{{c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}}\right\}

is a finite set of
channels
;

3.

\mathtt{M}:=\left\{{\mathtt{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathtt{a}_{k}}\right\}
is a finite
message alphabet
; and

4.

\mathtt{I}\subseteq\mathtt{Q}\times\mathtt{C}\times\mathtt{M}\times\{!,?\}\times\mathtt{Q}
is a finite set of
instructions
.

We denote by
\mathsf{Conf}(\mathcal{L})
the set
\mathtt{Q}\times(\mathtt{M}^{\ast})^{n}
of
configurations
of
\mathcal{L}
,
where

\mathtt{M}^{\ast}

is the set of all finite sequences
of elements in
\mathtt{M}
.

Definition 7
.

The
perfect steps
of a CS
\mathcal{L}
are given by a binary relation
\to_{\mathcal{L}\;\mathsf{perf}}\;\subseteq\mathsf{Conf}(\mathcal{L})\times\mathsf{Conf}(\mathcal{L})
such that

\left(q_{i},u_{1},\ldots,u_{r},\ldots,u_{n}\right)\to_{\mathcal{L}\;\mathsf{perf}}\left(q_{j},u_{1},\ldots,v_{r},\ldots,u_{n}\right)
if either

1.

u_{r}=\mathtt{a}_{p}v_{r}

and
\left(q_{i},c_{r},\mathtt{a}_{p},?,q_{j}\right)\in\mathtt{I}
(read and remove
\mathtt{a}_{p}
from the
front
of channel
c_{r}
and change state from
q_{i}
to
q_{j}
)

2.

v_{r}=u_{r}\mathtt{a}_{p}
and
\left(q_{i},c_{r},\mathtt{a}_{p},!,q_{j}\right)\in\mathtt{I}
(write
\mathtt{a}_{p}
at the
back
of channel
c_{r}
and change state from
q_{i}
to
q_{j}
).

Let
\to_{\mathcal{L}\;\mathsf{perf}}^{?}

and

\to_{\mathcal{L}\;\mathsf{perf}}^{!}

denote the perfect steps given by items 1 and 2 respectively. Also let
\to_{\mathcal{L}\;\mathsf{perf}}^{\ast}
denote the reflexive
and transitive closure of
\to_{\mathcal{L}\;\mathsf{perf}}
.

Clearly,
u\to_{\mathcal{L}\;\mathsf{perf}}^{\ast}v

iff there is a finite sequence
w_{1},\ldots,w_{\eta}
(
\eta\geq 1
) of configurations such that

u=w_{1}
,
v=w_{\eta}

and
w_{i}\to_{\mathcal{L}\;\mathsf{perf}}w_{i+1}
for each
i
(
1\leq i<\eta
).
This sequence is called a
perfect computation in
\mathcal{L}

of length
\eta
and it witnesses
that
u

reaches

v
in
\mathcal{L}
.

Reachability (as well as many other verification problems for CSs) is undecidable
[
17
]
, but
adding lossy behaviour makes
it decidable.
This behaviour is introduced
in the operational semantics
of a CS as follows.

Definition 8
.

The
lossy semantics
of a CS
\mathcal{L}

is given by a relation
\to_{\mathcal{L}\;\mathsf{lossy}}\;\subseteq\mathsf{Conf}(\mathcal{L})\times\mathsf{Conf}(\mathcal{L})

that extends
\to_{\mathcal{L}\;\mathsf{perf}}

with all transitions
(called
lossy steps
)
of the form

\left(q_{i},u_{1},\ldots,u_{r},\ldots,u_{n}\right)\to_{\mathcal{L}\;\mathsf{lossy}}^{l}\left(q_{i},u_{1},\ldots,v_{r},\ldots,u_{n}\right)

such that

u_{r}=w_{1}\mathtt{a}_{p}w_{2}
and
v_{r}=w_{1}w_{2}
. Let
\to_{\mathcal{L}\;\mathsf{lossy}}^{\ast}
be the reflexive
and transitive closure of
\to_{\mathcal{L}\;\mathsf{lossy}}
.
A channel system with a lossy semantics
is called a
lossy channel system (LCS)
.

Intuitively,
in an LCS the
lossy steps permit any channel to lose a message (a symbol) from any position at any moment without changing the state. No instruction in the machine is required for this.
The relation
\to_{\mathcal{L}\;\mathsf{lossy}}^{\ast}
induces a
notion of
lossy computation in
\mathcal{L}
and reachability is defined analogously to perfect computations
with
\to_{\mathcal{L}\;\mathsf{perf}}^{\ast}
.
Since a step can be either read, write, or lossy, we use
\to_{\mathcal{L}\;\mathsf{lossy}}^{?}
,
\to_{\mathcal{L}\;\mathsf{lossy}}^{!}

or
\to_{\mathcal{L}\;\mathsf{lossy}}^{l}
to indicate precisely which step was performed
in a lossy computation.

Definition 9

(Reachability problem in LCSs)
.

Given a LCS
\mathcal{L}

and configurations

u,v\in\mathsf{Conf}(\mathcal{L})
,
does
u\to_{\mathcal{L}\;\mathsf{lossy}}^{\ast}v
?

Remark 10
.

The relation
\to_{\mathcal{L}\;\mathsf{lossy}}^{\ast}
has an equivalent definition in the literature
[
18
, Sec. 2]
as

u\rightsquigarrow_{\mathcal{L}}^{\ast}v

if, and only if,
there are

u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\in\mathsf{Conf}(\mathcal{L})

such that
u\to_{\mathcal{L}\;\mathsf{lossy}}^{l\ast}u^{\prime}\to_{\mathcal{L}\;\mathsf{perf}}v^{\prime}\to_{\mathcal{L}\;\mathsf{lossy}}^{l\ast}v
(the
\ast
denotes the reflexive transitive closure).
That is, messages may be lost before and after
performing a perfect step.
It can be shown that

\to_{\mathcal{L}\;\mathsf{lossy}}^{\ast}\;=\;\to_{\mathcal{L}\;\mathsf{lossy}}^{l\ast}\circ\to_{\mathcal{L}\;\mathsf{perf}}\circ\to_{\mathcal{L}\;\mathsf{lossy}}^{l\ast}\;=\;\rightsquigarrow_{\mathcal{L}}^{\ast}
.
The latter relation is shown
[
12
, App. A]
to have no impact on the complexity of reachability compared to the “write-lossy" mechanism employed in
the proof of Thm.
11

[
12
]
stated below.

Theorem 11

(
[
12
, Thm. 5.5, Obs. 6.1, App. A]
)
.

Reachability in LCSs
is

HAck
-complete.

3.2
Encoding reachability in LCSs into sequent-deducibility

We code each instruction of a lossy channel
system into a sequent. The collection of these sequents
is the
theory
associated to
the system.

Definition 12
.

For an LCS

\mathcal{L}=\left\langle{\mathtt{Q},\mathtt{C},\mathtt{M},\mathtt{I}}\right\rangle
,
define these sets of
propositional variables:

1.

\mathsf{Q}:=\{Q_{i}\mid q_{i}\in\mathtt{Q}\}
(state variables)

2.

\#:=\{\#^{s}_{i}\mid c_{i}\in\mathtt{C}\}\cup\{\#^{e}_{i}\mid c_{i}\in\mathtt{C}\}
(channel markers)

3.

\mathsf{M}:=\{P_{a}\mid a\in\mathtt{M}\}
(alphabet variables)

Moreover, define

\mathsf{Props}(\mathcal{L}):=\mathsf{Q}\cup\#\cup\mathsf{M}
.

Definition 13

(Theory of an LCS)
.

Given an LCS

\mathcal{L}=\left\langle{\mathtt{Q},\mathtt{C},\mathtt{M},\mathtt{I}}\right\rangle
,
the
theory of
\mathcal{L}
,
denoted by
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{L})
,
is defined as the union of the following finite sets
of sequents:

1.

\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{L})^{!}:=\{{\#^{e}_{l},Q_{i}}\Rightarrow{P_{a}\otimes(\#^{e}_{l}\otimes Q_{j})}\mid\left(q_{i},c_{l},a,!,q_{j}\right)\in\mathtt{I}\}
;

2.

\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{L})^{?}:=\{{\#^{s}_{l},P_{a},Q_{i}}\Rightarrow{\#^{s}_{l}\otimes Q_{j}}\mid\left(q_{i},c_{l},a,?,q_{j}\right)\in\mathtt{I}\}
;

3.

\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{L})^{\mathtt{Q}}:=\bigcup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\in\mathsf{Q}\\
R\in\mathsf{Props}(\mathcal{L}){\setminus}\mathsf{Q}\end{subarray}}\{{R,Q}\Rightarrow{Q\otimes R}\}\cup\{{Q,R}\Rightarrow{R\otimes Q}\}
.

Note that

\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{L})

is a regular theory in the sense of Def.
3
.

Recall that

\bigotimes{\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{m}}:=\varphi_{1}\otimes(\varphi_{2}\otimes(\ldots(\varphi_{m-1}\otimes\varphi_{m})\ldots))
, where
m\geq 1

and
\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{m}

is a sequence of formulas.
We now translate a reachability instance
in a fixed LCS
\mathcal{L}=\left\langle{\mathtt{Q},\mathtt{C},\mathtt{M},\mathtt{I}}\right\rangle

to a sequent-deducibility instance
in
\mathbf{FL}_{\mathbf{w}}^{\otimes}
.
We begin by coding a configuration as a sequence.

Definition 14
.

Given
u:=\left(q_{i},u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}\right)\in\mathsf{Conf}(\mathcal{L})
,
define the sequence

E(u):={Q_{i},\#^{s}_{1},U_{1},\#^{e}_{1},\ldots,\#^{s}_{n},U_{n},\#^{e}_{n}}
, where, for
u_{i}=a_{1}\cdots a_{k_{i}}\in\mathtt{M}^{\ast}
,
U_{i}
is the sequence
P_{a_{1}},\ldots,P_{a_{k_{i}}}
of propositional variables.

Definition 15
.

For
u:=\left(q_{i},u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}\right)
,
v:=\left(q_{j},v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\right)\in\mathsf{Conf}(\mathcal{L})
,
define the sequent

S_{{u}\rightarrow{v}}:={E(u)}\Rightarrow{\bigotimes E(v)}.

Define also
S_{{u}\rightarrow{v}}^{\bowtie}

as
the set of sequents of the above form
where the occurrence of

Q_{i}
in the antecedent may appear in any position (i.e., not necessarily at the front as in
E(u)
).

We note that sequents in standard deductions in
\mathbf{FL}_{\mathbf{w}}^{\otimes}
from a regular theory have a nonempty succedent coming from the observation that every leaf has this property:

Lemma 16
.

Let
\mathcal{T}

be a regular theory.
It is never the case that

\mathcal{T}\vdash_{{\mathbf{FL}_{\mathbf{w}}^{\otimes}}\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}^{\mathsf{std}}{\Gamma}\Rightarrow{}
.

Proof.

It follows because
no axiomatic rule
has conclusion with empty
succedent
and no other rule
allows to derive a
sequent with empty succedent
from one with nonempty succedent.
∎

The
flattening
[\varphi]
is the sequence
of propositional variables
obtained from a formula
\varphi

by replacing ‘
\otimes
’ with ‘,’.
E.g.,
[p\otimes((q\otimes s)\otimes r)]=p,q,s,r
.
The flattening
[\Gamma]
of a sequence

\Gamma
of formulas is the concatenation of the flattening
of the formulas in
\Gamma
in the order they appear in the sequence.
Let
\#\Delta
denote the subsequence of
\Delta
having only variables from the set
\#

(that is, only channel markers).
The
\mathsf{Q}
-free subsequence
of a sequence of propositional variables
is obtained by deleting all
occurrences of state variables.

The following lemmas express that in standard deductions from a regular theory, in various situations, propositional variables occurring positively must also occur negatively.
They abstract what is needed for the right-to-left direction of Lem.
20
below.
The proofs are by structural induction on standard deductions.

Lemma 17
.

Let
\Gamma
be a sequence of
formulas, none having a state variable.
If
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{L})\vdash_{{\mathbf{FL}_{\mathbf{w}}^{\otimes}}\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}^{\mathsf{std}}{\Gamma}\Rightarrow{\varphi}
, then
the
\mathsf{Q}
-free subsequence of

[\varphi]
is a subsequence of

[\Gamma]
.

Proof.

Structural induction on a standard deduction witnessing

\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{L})\vdash_{{\mathbf{FL}_{\mathbf{w}}^{\otimes}}\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}{\Gamma}\Rightarrow{\varphi}
.

Base case
. For elements from

\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{L})
it holds vacuously since each sequent contains a state variable in the antecedent; for an identity sequent the claim is immediate.

Inductive step
. Suppose that the last rule instance applied is
\mathrm{\mathrm{(lw)}}
,
\mathrm{({R}{\otimes})}
or
\mathrm{({L}{\otimes})}
. Applying the IH to its premise(s), we have that the
\mathsf{Q}
-free subsequence of
[\varphi]
is a subsequence of the flattening of the premise antecedent(s), and hence also of
[\Gamma]
. The last rule instance cannot be a standard cut as the antecedent of each element in

\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{L})
contain a state variable hence
\Gamma
would contains a state variable, contradicting the hypotheses.
The rule
\mathrm{(rw)}

cannot occur due to Lem.
16
.
∎

Lemma 18
.

Let
\Gamma
be a sequence of
formulas,
u\in\mathsf{Conf}(\mathcal{L})

and
L
a nonempty subsequence of
E(u)
.
Then

\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{L})\vdash_{{\mathbf{FL}_{\mathbf{w}}^{\otimes}}\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}^{\mathsf{std}}{\Gamma}\Rightarrow{\bigotimes L}

implies that
the variables in

\#L

appear at least once in

[\Gamma]
.

Proof.

Induction on the structure of a
standard
deduction witnessing

\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{L})\vdash_{{\mathbf{FL}_{\mathbf{w}}^{\otimes}}\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}{\Gamma}\Rightarrow{\bigotimes L}
.

Base case.

Straightforward: identity sequent
{p}\Rightarrow{p}
(trivial) and an element from
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{L})
(by inspection, it contains no channel variables or the same channel variable occurs in the antecedent and succedent).

Inductive step
. Consider the last rule
instance in the standard deduction.
For
\mathrm{({R}{\otimes})}
,
\mathrm{({L}{\otimes})}
and
\mathrm{\mathrm{(lw)}}
, we have by IH that each variable in
\#L
appears in
[\Gamma^{\prime}]
for the antecedent
\Gamma^{\prime}
of some premise of the rule instance.
Those variables will be carried to the conclusion antecedent. If the last rule applied is a standard cut,
its right premise has the form
{\Gamma_{1},\varphi,\Gamma_{2}}\Rightarrow{\bigotimes L}
where
\varphi
is the cut formula. By IH, every variable in

\#L
appears in
[\Gamma_{1},\varphi,\Gamma_{2}]
.
Moreover, since the left premise is from
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{L})
, any channel variable occurring in the cut formula will occur in its antecedent, and hence also in
[\Gamma]
.
The case of
\mathrm{(rw)}

does not need to be considered in view of Lem.
16
.
∎

Lemma 19
.

Let
\Gamma
be a sequence of formulas
and

\varphi
be
a formula.
If
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{L})\vdash_{{\mathbf{FL}_{\mathbf{w}}^{\otimes}}\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}^{\mathsf{std}}{\Gamma}\Rightarrow{Q_{i}\otimes\varphi}

or

\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{L})\vdash_{{\mathbf{FL}_{\mathbf{w}}^{\otimes}}\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}^{\mathsf{std}}{\Gamma}\Rightarrow{Q_{i}}
,
then
[\Gamma]
must contain
a state variable.

Proof.

Structural induction on a
derivation witnessing

\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{L})\vdash_{{\mathbf{SL_{w}^{\cdot}}}\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}{\Gamma}\Rightarrow{Q_{i}\otimes\varphi}
.

Base case
. The case of an initial sequent is trivial, and the antecedent of any sequent from
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{L})
contains a state variable.

Inductive step
. Suppose that the last rule is
\mathrm{({R}{\otimes})}
. The left premise
has
Q_{i}
as succedent, and hence by
IH, its antecedent will contain a state variable
that is carried down to the conclusion. The argument
is analogous for
\mathrm{\mathrm{(lw)}}
and
\mathrm{({L}{\otimes})}
.
If the last rule instance is a standard cut, its left premise is a sequent in
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{L})
. As already observed, the antecedent of such a sequent contains a state variable and that will be carried down to the conclusion.
The case of
\mathrm{(rw)}

does not need to be considered in view of Lem.
16
.

The case of

\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{L})\vdash_{{\mathbf{FL}_{\mathbf{w}}^{\otimes}}\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}{\Gamma}\Rightarrow{Q_{i}}
is similar.
∎

For all LCS

In the left-to-right direction,

Base case

Inductive step

By cases on the last rule applied in

It cannot be

Then it could be:

1.

\mathrm{(rw)}

2.

\mathrm{({R}{\otimes})}

4.1
Just enough on well-quasi-order theory

A
quasi-ordered set (qo-set)

is a structure
\mathbf{W}:=\left\langle{W,\preccurlyeq_{\mathbf{W}}}\right\rangle
,
where

W
is a set
and
\preccurlyeq_{\mathbf{W}}\,\subseteq W\times W
is reflexive and transitive (a
quasi order
, for short).
Abusing notation, we write
a\in\mathbf{W}

for
a\in W
.
We denote by

\mathbb{N}
the
set of natural numbers;
by

\mathbb{N}^{+}

the set
\mathbb{N}{\setminus}\{0\}
;
and, given

\ell\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\omega\}
, we write

\mathbb{N}_{<{\ell}}
for the set

\{a\in\mathbb{N}\mid a<\ell\}
.
Given a qo-set
\mathbf{W}
,
a
bad sequence over
\mathbf{W}
(of length
\ell
)
is a sequence
(a_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{<{\ell}}}
of elements of
\mathbf{W}

such that, for all
i<j
,

a_{i}\not\preccurlyeq_{\mathbf{W}}a_{j}
.
A qo-set
\mathbf{W}
is a
well-quasi-ordered set (wqo-set)

if every bad sequence over it is finite (see
[
14
, Sec. 2]
for equivalent definitions).
Examples include

\mathbb{N}^{k}
under component-wise ordering (Dickson’s Lemma
[
19
]
), and
sequences under the subword embedding ordering:

Theorem 23

(Higman’s Lemma
[
14
]
)
.

For any finite set
\Omega
,

\left\langle{\Omega^{\ast},\preccurlyeq_{\ast}}\right\rangle

is a wqo, where, recall,

\Omega^{\ast}

is the set of all finite sequences
of elements from
\Omega

and

w_{1}\preccurlyeq_{\ast}w_{2}
iff

w_{1}
is obtainable from
w_{2}
by deleting some
elements.

Bad sequences over a wqo, though finite, do not in general have a maximum length. For example,
(1,0),(0,k),(0,k-1),\ldots,(0,0)
is a bad sequence on
\mathbb{N}^{2}
under the component-wise ordering with length
k+2
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Nevertheless, a maximum length can be ensured by limiting the size of each element in the sequence to some (fixed) function of the size of the preceding element. To achieve this, the wqo is enriched with more structure.
A
normed
(well-)quasi-ordered

set (n(w)qo-set)

[
20
]

is a structure
\mathbf{W}:=\left\langle{W,\preccurlyeq_{\mathbf{W}},{\llbracket}{\cdot}{\rrbracket}_{\mathbf{W}}}\right\rangle
,
where

\left\langle{W,\preccurlyeq_{\mathbf{W}}}\right\rangle
is a (w)qo-set and

{\llbracket}{\cdot}{\rrbracket}_{\mathbf{W}}:W\to\mathbb{N}
is a
proper norm
,
meaning that, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\left\{{a\in W:{\llbracket}{a}{\rrbracket}_{\mathbf{W}}<n}\right\}
is finite.
It is easy to check that the following is a nwqo.

Definition 24
.

Given a finite set
\Omega
,
let
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{\ast}:=\left\langle{\Omega^{\ast},\preccurlyeq_{\ast},{\llbracket}{\cdot}{\rrbracket}_{\ast}}\right\rangle
,
where
{\llbracket}{w}{\rrbracket}_{\ast}
is
the length of
w

(and
\left\langle{\Omega^{\ast},\preccurlyeq_{\ast}}\right\rangle
is as in Thm.
23
).

Indeed, the

disjoint sum
of these nwqos, defined below, is also an nwqo
[
20
]
.

Definition 25
.

Given a finite set
\Omega

and
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
let
m\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{\ast}:=\left\langle{m\cdot\Omega^{\ast},\preccurlyeq_{m\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{\ast}},{\llbracket}{\cdot}{\rrbracket}_{m\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{\ast}}}\right\rangle
.
Here,
m\cdot\Omega^{\ast}:=\{1,\ldots,m\}\times\Omega^{\ast}

is the disjoint sum of
m
copies of
\Omega^{\ast}
, and

(i,w_{1})\preccurlyeq_{m\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{\ast}}(j,w_{2})

iff
i=j
and
w_{1}\preccurlyeq_{\ast}w_{2}
. Also, define

{\llbracket}{(i,w)}{\rrbracket}_{m\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{\ast}}:={\llbracket}{w}{\rrbracket}_{\ast}
.

A
control function
is a mapping
g:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}

that is strictly increasing (for all
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
, if
a<b
, then
g(a)<g(b)
)
and strictly inflationary
(
a<g(a)
for all
a\in\mathbb{N}
).

For an nwqo-set
\mathbf{W}
,
control function
g
,
and
initial parameter

t\in\mathbb{N}
,
a
(g,t)
-controlled bad sequence over
\mathbf{W}

is a bad sequence
a_{0},\ldots,a_{\ell-1}
over
\mathbf{W}
where

{\llbracket}{a_{i}}{\rrbracket}_{\mathbf{W}}<g^{i}(t)
for all
0\leq i<\ell
.
The
(g,t)
-controlled bad sequences have a maximum length
[
21
]

using König’s Lemma.
Denote by
L_{\mathbf{W}}^{g}(t)

the maximum length
of a
(g,t)
-controlled
bad sequence over
\mathbf{W}
.
We call

L_{\mathbf{W}}^{g}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}

the
length function

of
\mathbf{W}
(for
g
).
Bounds for
(g,t)
-controlled bad sequences over
\mathbf{W}

reduce to finding bounds for
L_{\mathbf{W}}^{g}
(‘
length theorems
’),
expressed in terms of the ordinal-indexed
extended Grzegorczyk hierarchy

\{\mathfrak{F}_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha}
, from which
the hierarchy
\{\mathbf{F}_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha}

of
fast-growing complexity classes
are defined
(the reader is referred to
[
10
, Sec. 2]
for detailed definitions).
What matters here is that to classify a problem in
\mathbf{F}_{\alpha}
, it suffices to show that the complexity of every instance is upper bounded by a function in
\mathfrak{F}_{\beta}
for some
\beta<\alpha
.
Schmitz and Schnoebelen obtained
the following length theorem for Higman’s lemma.

Theorem 26

(
[
6
, Thm. 5.3]
)
.

For any finite set
\Omega
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
and primitive recursive control function

g
, the length function

L_{m\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{\ast}}^{g}

is upper bounded by a function
in

\mathfrak{F}_{\omega^{|{\Omega}|-1}}
.

A
reflection
is a map between nqo-sets that preserves bad sequences. Hence, its existence implies the reverse transfer of the wqo-property and upper bounding of length theorem.
In the next subsection, we obtain a length theorem for a nwqo over sequents in this way.

Definition 27

(Reflection)
.

Let
\mathbf{W}_{1}:=\left\langle{W_{1},\preccurlyeq_{1},{\llbracket}{\cdot}{\rrbracket}_{1}}\right\rangle

and
\mathbf{W}_{2}:=\left\langle{W_{2},\preccurlyeq_{2},{\llbracket}{\cdot}{\rrbracket}_{2}}\right\rangle
be nqo-sets.
A
reflection
between
\mathbf{W}_{1}
and
\mathbf{W}_{2}
is a mapping

f:W_{1}\to W_{2}

such that,
for all
a,b\in W_{1}
,

\text{if }f(a)\preccurlyeq_{2}f(b),\text{then }a\preccurlyeq_{1}b

and, for all
a\in W_{1}
,

{\llbracket}{f(a)}{\rrbracket}_{2}\leq{\llbracket}{a}{\rrbracket}_{1}.

We
write
\mathbf{W}_{1}{\;\hookrightarrow^{f}\;}\mathbf{W}_{2}

(sometimes omitting the superscript
f
).

Lemma 28

(
[
6
, p. 446]
)
.

Whenever

\mathbf{W}_{1}{\;\hookrightarrow\;}\mathbf{W}_{2}
,
if
\mathbf{W}_{2}
is a nwqo, then so is
\mathbf{W}_{1}
; and
if
g
is a control function, then
L_{\mathbf{W}_{1}}^{g}(t)\leq L_{\mathbf{W}_{2}}^{g}(t)

for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
.

Observe that the antecedent of the premise of the left-weakening rule

In what follows, let
\Sigma

Definition 29
.

For finite

,

\Omega

is a nwqo; and

Fix an enumeration

of the formulas in
\Omega
.
Then the mapping

such that

and

and

.

4.4
Complexity analysis

The fast-growing complexity classes are closed under exponentiation so the distinction
between space and time (also determinism and
non-determinism) is unimportant
[
10
]
.
We undertake a space analysis of the algorithm based on Thm.
33

and Thm.
35
.
It suffices to write down each
D_{i}
in turn at the same location, computing each rule instance in turn; when the stabilization index
D_{N}
is reached, check whether

s^{\prime}\rightsquigarrow_{\Sigma}^{\Omega}s

for some
s^{\prime}\in D_{N}
.
Each
D_{i}\subseteq D_{N}
, and writing down a rule instance takes space bounded by the size of an element from
D_{N}^{\langle{\mathbf{S}}\rangle}\times D_{N}
.
So the space requirements are an elementary function in
|{D_{N}}|
and the size of an element from
D_{N}
.
We upper bound each of these.

Lemma 36
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a regular theory,
s
be a sequent
and
\Omega:=\mathsf{subf}(\mathcal{T}\cup\{s\})
.

1.

|{\Omega}|\leq\langle{s}\rangle+\left\langle{\mathcal{T}}\right\rangle
;

2.

if

s^{\prime}\in D_{i}

then
{\llbracket}{s^{\prime}}{\rrbracket}<(\left\langle{\mathcal{T}}\right\rangle+1)\langle{\mathbf{S}}\rangle^{2i+1}

and
{\llbracket_{{\mathsf{max}}}}{D_{i}}{\rrbracket}<(\left\langle{\mathcal{T}}\right\rangle+1)\langle{\mathbf{S}}\rangle^{2i+1}

if

then
\langle{s^{\prime}}\rangle<4(\left\langle{\mathcal{T}}\right\rangle+\langle{s}\rangle)^{2}\langle{\mathbf{S}}\rangle^{2N+1}
;

4.

for all
i\geq 0
,

|{D_{i}}|<E(\left\langle{\mathcal{T}}\right\rangle+\langle{s}\rangle,N)

for
E
an elementary function (i.e., in
\mathfrak{F}_{2}
).

Proof.

1.

It is easy to see by
structural induction that

|{\mathsf{subf}(\varphi)}|\leq\langle{\varphi}\rangle
for all formulas
\varphi
.
Then, for any sequent
t:={\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{m}}\Rightarrow{\Pi}

we have

|{\mathsf{subf}(t)}|\leq|{\mathsf{subf}(\Pi)}|+\sum_{i=1}^{m}|{\mathsf{subf}(\psi_{i})}|\leq\langle{\Pi}\rangle+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\langle{\psi_{i}}\rangle\leq\langle{t}\rangle
.
The result then follows because

|{\Omega}|\leq|{\mathsf{subf}(s)}|+\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}|{\mathsf{subf}(t)}|\leq\langle{s}\rangle+\left\langle{\mathcal{T}}\right\rangle
.

2.

By induction on
i\geq 0
.
Note that
if
s^{\prime}\in D_{0}
, since
D_{0}
is a subset of the union of
\mathcal{T}
and instantiations from
\Omega
of initial sequents in
\mathbf{S}
,

{\llbracket}{s^{\prime}}{\rrbracket}<{\llbracket_{{\mathsf{max}}}}{\mathcal{T}}{\rrbracket}+\langle{\mathbf{S}}\rangle\leq({\llbracket_{{\mathsf{max}}}}{\mathcal{T}}{\rrbracket}+1)\langle{\mathbf{S}}\rangle\leq(\left\langle{\mathcal{T}}\right\rangle+1)\langle{\mathbf{S}}\rangle
.
Inductive step:
if
s^{\prime}\in D_{i+1}
,
then
{\llbracket}{s^{\prime}}{\rrbracket}<\langle{\mathbf{S}}\rangle^{2}{\llbracket_{{\mathsf{max}}}}{D_{i}}{\rrbracket}<\langle{\mathbf{S}}\rangle^{2}(\left\langle{\mathcal{T}}\right\rangle+1)\langle{\mathbf{S}}\rangle^{2i+1}=(\left\langle{\mathcal{T}}\right\rangle+1)\langle{\mathbf{S}}\rangle^{2(i+1)+1}

Since
{\llbracket_{{\mathsf{max}}}}{D}{\rrbracket}=\max_{s\in D}{\llbracket}{s}{\rrbracket}
, it follows that
{\llbracket_{{\mathsf{max}}}}{D_{i}}{\rrbracket}<(\left\langle{\mathcal{T}}\right\rangle+1)\langle{\mathbf{S}}\rangle^{2i+1}
.

3.

It is enough to observe that

\langle{s^{\prime}}\rangle

is upper bounded by
the sum of the sizes of each formula
in the antecedent, plus 1 for each comma,
plus 1 to account for the sequent symbol,
plus the size of the succedent.
This gives us

\langle{s^{\prime}}\rangle\leq{\llbracket}{s^{\prime}}{\rrbracket}\langle{\Omega}\rangle+{\llbracket}{s^{\prime}}{\rrbracket}+1+\langle{\Omega}\rangle\leq 4({\llbracket}{s^{\prime}}{\rrbracket}+1)\langle{\Omega}\rangle
. From the previous items, the latter is

\leq 4((\left\langle{\mathcal{T}}\right\rangle+1)\langle{\mathbf{S}}\rangle^{2N+1}+1)(\langle{s}\rangle+\left\langle{\mathcal{T}}\right\rangle)\leq 4((\left\langle{\mathcal{T}}\right\rangle+\langle{s}\rangle)\langle{\mathbf{S}}\rangle^{2N+1})(\langle{s}\rangle+\left\langle{\mathcal{T}}\right\rangle)
where we have used that
\langle{s}\rangle\geq 2
and
\langle{\mathbf{S}}\rangle\geq 1
.

4.

Let
\eta,\rho\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then define

\big[\begin{smallmatrix}{\eta}\\
{\rho}\end{smallmatrix}\big]_{\mathsf{seq}}:=(\sum_{l=0}^{\rho}\eta^{l})\cdot(\eta+1)\leq(\eta+1)^{\rho+1}\cdot(\eta+1)

over
\eta

\leq\rho
.
Note that

|{D_{0}}|\leq\big[\begin{smallmatrix}{\langle{s}\rangle+\left\langle{\mathcal{T}}\right\rangle}\\
{\langle{s}\rangle+\left\langle{\mathcal{T}}\right\rangle}\end{smallmatrix}\big]_{\mathsf{seq}}

|{D_{i+1}}|\leq\big[\begin{smallmatrix}{|{\Omega}|}\\
{\langle{\mathbf{S}}\rangle^{2}{\llbracket_{{\mathsf{max}}}}{D_{i}}{\rrbracket}}\end{smallmatrix}\big]_{\mathsf{seq}}<\big[\begin{smallmatrix}{\langle{s}\rangle+\left\langle{\mathcal{T}}\right\rangle}\\
{\langle{\mathbf{S}}\rangle^{2}(\left\langle{\mathcal{T}+1)}\right\rangle\langle{\mathbf{S}}\rangle^{2N+1}}\end{smallmatrix}\big]_{\mathsf{seq}}\leq\big[\begin{smallmatrix}{\langle{s}\rangle+\left\langle{\mathcal{T}}\right\rangle}\\

In view of the algebraizability

-hard.
The quasiequational theory
is in

Since the word problem reduces to the quasiequational theory,

-complete.
This also applies to integral FL-algebras and integral residuated lattices since
the constant
