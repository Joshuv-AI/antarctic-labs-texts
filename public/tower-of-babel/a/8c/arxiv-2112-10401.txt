Quasi-uniform designs with optimal and near-optimal uniformity constant
Luc Pronzato, Anatoly Zhigljavsky

Abstract
A design is a collection of distinct points in a given set 𝒳 {\mathscr{X}} , which is assumed to be a compact subset of ℝ d \mathds{R}^{d} , and the mesh-ratio of a design is the ratio of its fill distance to its separation radius. The uniformity constant of a sequence of nested designs is the smallest upper bound for the mesh-ratios of the designs. We derive a lower bound on this uniformity constant and show that a simple greedy construction achieves this lower bound. We then extend this scheme to allow more flexibility in the design construction. AMS subject classifications: Primary 65D17, 05B30; secondary 65D15

Introduction

Let
{\mathscr{X}}
be a compact subset of
\mathds{R}^{d}
, for some
d\geq 1
, with
{\rm vol}({\mathscr{X}})>0
.

Let
\|\cdot\|
denote a norm, not necessarily the Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|_{2}
, on
\mathds{R}^{d}
.
The ball of radius
r
and center
\mathbf{x}
is
{\mathscr{B}}(\mathbf{x},r)=\{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathds{R}^{d}:\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}\|\leq r\}
. The volume of
the unit ball
{\mathscr{B}}(\mathbf{0},1)
is denoted by
V_{d}
. If the norm
\|\cdot\|
is Euclidean, then
V_{d}=\pi^{d/2}/\Gamma(d/2+1)
.

A collection
\mathbf{X}_{n}=\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}\}
of
n
distinct points in
{\mathscr{X}}
will be called an
n
-point design (in the modern literature on approximation theory, designs are often called “data sets”, see e.g.
[
14
,
16
]
).
We start with several definitions of well-known characteristics of designs.

FD,
the fill distance
(also known as mesh norm, covering radius, dispersion, or minimax-distance criterion), of the
n
-point design
\mathbf{X}_{n}
for
{\mathscr{X}}
is

\displaystyle h(\mathbf{X}_{n})=h_{\mathscr{X}}(\mathbf{X}_{n}):=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}}\min_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbf{X}_{n}}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{i}\|\,,\ n\geq 1\,.

A design
\mathbf{X}_{n,FD}^{*}
will be called FD-optimal if

h_{n}^{*}:=h(\mathbf{X}_{n,FD}^{*})=\min_{\mathbf{X}_{n}\in{\mathscr{X}}}h(\mathbf{X}_{n})\,.

SR,
the separation radius
(also called packing radius or maximin-distance criterion), of
\mathbf{X}_{n}
is

\displaystyle q(\mathbf{X}_{n})=\frac{1}{2}\,\min_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\neq\mathbf{x}_{j}\in\mathbf{X}_{n}}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\|\,,\ n\geq 2\,.

A design
\mathbf{X}_{n,q}^{*}
will be called SR-optimal if

q_{n}^{*}:=q(\mathbf{X}_{n,SR}^{*})=\max_{\mathbf{X}_{n}\in{\mathscr{X}}}q(\mathbf{X}_{n})\,.

The mesh-ratio
of
\mathbf{X}_{n}
for
{\mathscr{X}}
is

\displaystyle\mathsf{MR}(\mathbf{X}_{n})=\mathsf{MR}_{\mathscr{X}}(\mathbf{X}_{n}):=\frac{h_{\mathscr{X}}(\mathbf{X}_{n})}{q(\mathbf{X}_{n})}\,,\ n\geq 2\,.

The mesh-ratio provides a measure of how
uniformly points in
\mathbf{X}_{n}
are distributed in
{\mathscr{X}}
, see e.g.
[
14
, p. 573]
and
[
4
, p. 129]
; it is sometimes called the uniformity constant of
\mathbf{X}_{n}
, see
[
2
]
.
The mesh-ratio is commonly used to asses the stability of approximations constructed on the base of observations at
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbf{X}_{n}
, see e.g.

[
14
]
and
[
16
, Chapter 12]
.
According to Guideline 7.10 in
[
14
, p. 579]
, the best approximation error with the most stable system is achieved by using quasi-uniform designs (data sets) with the smallest mesh-ratio. The mesh-ratio is fundamental in estimation of stability of approximations through the approach involving the Lebesgue constant, see
[
2
, Th. 1]
and
[
7
, Sect. 8.5]
. Moreover, the mesh-ratio plays an an important role in the derivation of upper-bounds on the quality of kernel approximations in the so-called ‘escape theorems’, when the approximated function is less smooth than the kernel, see
[
9
,
10
]
as well as
[
4
, Th. 1, p. 129]
and
[
14
, Th. 7.8]
.

Let
\mathbf{X}_{\infty}=\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\ldots\}\subset{\mathscr{X}}
be a sequence of points in
{\mathscr{X}}
. There is a one-to one correspondence between such point sequence
\mathbf{X}_{\infty}
and the sequence
\{\mathbf{X}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
of nested designs
\mathbf{X}_{n}=\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}\}
.
A sequence
\{\mathbf{X}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
of nested designs
\mathbf{X}_{n}
in a compact set
{\mathscr{X}}\subset\mathds{R}^{d}
is called quasi-uniform if there exists a constant
b<\infty
such that
\mathsf{MR}(\mathbf{X}_{n})\leq b
for all
n
. The smallest such
b={\mathsf{MR}}(\mathbf{X}_{\infty})
is called the
uniformity constant
of the corresponding sequence of nested designs
\{\mathbf{X}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
.
Quasi-uniform sequences of designs with small uniformity constants are the main sources of designs (point sets) in the meshless (or “mesh-free”) methods of computational mathematics; see e.g.
[
4
,
14
,
16
]

A sequence
\mathbf{X}_{\infty}^{*}=\{\mathbf{x}_{1}^{*},\mathbf{x}_{2}^{*},\ldots\}
will be called
\mathsf{MR}
-optimal if its uniformity constant is minimal:

\displaystyle{\mathsf{MR}}(\mathbf{X}_{\infty}^{*})=\min_{\mathbf{X}_{\infty}\subset{\mathscr{X}}}{\mathsf{MR}}(\mathbf{X}_{\infty})\,.

(1.1)

It is well known that when
{\mathscr{X}}
is connected,
\mathsf{MR}(\mathbf{X}_{n})\geq 1
for any
n
-point design
\mathbf{X}_{n}
in
{\mathscr{X}}
(as the
n
-balls
{\mathscr{B}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathsf{CR}(\mathbf{X}_{n}))
must cover
{\mathscr{X}}
).
One of the main results of the paper is Theorem
1.1
below, which states that in fact
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\,\mathsf{MR}(\mathbf{X}_{n})\geq 2
for any compact
{\mathscr{X}}
with positive volume. The proof is rather elementary but the result does not seem to be known. It implies in particular that the classical greedy packing algorithm is
\mathsf{MR}
-optimal.

Theorem 1.1

For any sequence of nested designs
\mathbf{X}_{n}
in a compact set
{\mathscr{X}}\subset\mathds{R}^{d}
with
{\rm vol}({\mathscr{X}})>0
, we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\,\mathsf{MR}(\mathbf{X}_{n})\geq 2\,.

In particular,
{\mathsf{MR}}(\mathbf{X}_{\infty})\geq 2\,
for any
\mathbf{X}_{\infty}\subset{\mathscr{X}}
.

Theorem
1.1
is proved in Section
2
.
The greedy-packing (or coffee-house) algorithm is presented in Section
3.1
; it constructs a sequence
\mathbf{X}_{\infty}
with
\mathsf{MR}(\mathbf{X}_{n})\leq 2
for all
n\geq 2
and hence
{\mathsf{MR}}(\mathbf{X}_{\infty})=2
. In Section
3.2
, we generalize the greedy-packing algorithm to the construction of other quasi-uniform sequences with bounded
{\mathsf{MR}}(\mathbf{X}_{\infty})
. In Section
3.3
we use the results of Section
3.2
to establish properties of an implementable version of the greedy-packing algorithm where, at every iteration, the next design point
\mathbf{x}_{n+1}
is chosen among a finite set of candidates
{\mathscr{X}}_{N}\subset{\mathscr{X}}
rather than within the whole
{\mathscr{X}}
. In Section
3.4
we consider a boundary-phobic version of greedy packing, which provides designs with worse (larger) mesh-ratio but better (smaller) fill distance.

2
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Before providing a proof of Theorem
1.1
, we prove two simple lemmas, both of them presenting independent interest.

Lemma 2.1

For any design
\mathbf{X}_{n}
in a compact set
{\mathscr{X}}\subset\mathds{R}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\left[\mathrm{vol}({\mathscr{X}})/V_{d}\right]^{1/d}\,n^{-1/d}\leq h(\mathbf{X}_{n})\,,\ n\geq 1\,.

Moreover, for any
m
such that
n\geq m\geq 2
, we have

\displaystyle q(\mathbf{X}_{n})\leq\left[\mathrm{vol}({\mathscr{X}}_{0})/V_{d}\right]^{1/d}\,n^{-1/d}\,,

where
{\mathscr{X}}_{0}={\mathscr{X}}\oplus{\mathscr{B}}(\mathbf{0},q(\mathbf{X}_{m}))
,
\mathbf{X}_{m}
is a sub-design of
\mathbf{X}_{n}
consisting of
m
points and
\oplus
denotes the Minkowski sum.

Proof.
The
n
balls
{\mathscr{B}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},h(\mathbf{X}_{n}))
cover
{\mathscr{X}}
; this yields the first inequality. The second inequality follows from
q(\mathbf{X}_{n})\leq q(\mathbf{X}_{m})
, which implies that all the balls
{\mathscr{B}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},q(\mathbf{X}_{n}))
are fully inside
{\mathscr{X}}_{0}
(
i=1,\ldots,n
).

Lemma
2.1
has the following consequence concerning the rate of decrease of the fill distance and separation radius of quasi-uniform sequences of nested designs.

Corollary 2.1

For any quasi-uniform sequence of nested designs
\mathbf{X}_{n}

with uniformity constant
\rho

in a compact set
{\mathscr{X}}\subset\mathds{R}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle c_{1}\,n^{-1/d}\leq h(\mathbf{X}_{n})\leq\rho\,q(\mathbf{X}_{n})\leq c_{2}\,n^{-1/d}\,,\ \forall n\geq 2\,,

(2.1)

where
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are some positive constants.

In the case of Euclidean norm, the statement of Corollary
2.1
is proved in
[
16
]
; see Proposition 14.1 and the discussion just after it.

Lemma 2.2

Let, for any given
n\in\mathds{N}
,
\mathbf{X}_{n}=\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}\}
and
\mathbf{X}_{n+1}^{\prime}=\{\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n+1}^{\prime}\}
be arbitrary
n
-point and
(n\!+\!1)
-point designs in
{\mathscr{X}}
. Then

\displaystyle q(\mathbf{X}_{n+1}^{\prime})\leq h(\mathbf{X}_{n})\,.

Proof.
Since the
n
balls
{\mathscr{B}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},h(\mathbf{X}_{n}))
cover
{\mathscr{X}}
, the pigeon-hole principle implies that at least one of them must contain at least two points
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{x}_{j}^{\prime}
from
\mathbf{X}_{n+1}^{\prime}
. Therefore,
\|\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}_{j}^{\prime}\|\leq 2\,h(\mathbf{X}_{n})
, implying
q(\mathbf{X}_{n+1}^{\prime})\leq h(\mathbf{X}_{n})
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
. Assume that
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathsf{MR}(\mathbf{X}_{n})<2
. This would yield that there exists
r<2
and
n_{0}
such that
\mathsf{MR}(\mathbf{X}_{n})\leq r
for all
n\geq n_{0}
.

Consider all such
n\geq n_{0}
.
The definition of
h(\mathbf{X}_{n})
and
\mathsf{MR}(\mathbf{X}_{n})
imply the existence of
\mathbf{x}_{j}\in\mathbf{X}_{n}
such that

\|\mathbf{x}_{n+1}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\|\leq h(\mathbf{X}_{n})\leq r\,q(\mathbf{X}_{n})\,.

Therefore,

\displaystyle q(\mathbf{X}_{n+1})\leq(1/2)\,\min_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbf{X}_{n}}\|\mathbf{x}_{n+1}-\mathbf{x}_{i}\|\leq(r/2)\,q(\mathbf{X}_{n})\,.

This implies the exponential decrease of
q(\mathbf{X}_{n})
to zero (as
n\to\infty
), which contradicts (
2.1
).

Construction of sequences of quasi-uniform designs

3.1
Greedy packing

Let us first describe the greedy-packing algorithm (called “geometric greedy method” in
[
3
]
), which
achieves the lower bound of Theorem
1.1
and hence constructs an
\mathsf{MR}
-optimal sequence of points

and nested designs

.
This algorithm is sometimes called the “coffee-house” algorithm, due to the analogy with the behavior of customers in large coffee shops, where new clients tend to seat as far as possible from occupied tables

Algorithm 1

(Greedy packing)

1:

{\mathscr{X}}
compact subset of
\mathds{R}^{d}
,

2:

set
n=1

\mathbf{X}_{1}=\{\mathbf{x}_{1}\}

3:

4:

5:

set
\mathbf{X}_{n+1}=\mathbf{X}_{n}\cup\{\mathbf{x}_{n+1}\}

For arbitrary
\mathbf{x}_{1}\in{\mathscr{X}}

Lemma 3.1

For all
n\geq 2

\mathbf{X}_{n}
generated by Algorithm

q(\mathbf{X}_{n})=h(\mathbf{X}_{n-1}){/2}

Proof.
The inequality

Assume that

\displaystyle q(\mathbf{X}_{n+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min\left\{q(\mathbf{X}_{n}),(1/2)\,\min_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbf{X}_{n}}\|\mathbf{x}_{n+1}-\mathbf{x}_{i}\|\right\}

\displaystyle\min\left\{q(\mathbf{X}_{n}),h(\mathbf{X}_{n})/2\right\}

\displaystyle\min\left\{h(\mathbf{X}_{n-1})/2,h(\mathbf{X}_{n})/2\right\}=h(\mathbf{X}_{n})/2\,.\hskip 113.81102pt\mbox{}~\hfill\rule{5.69054pt}{5.69054pt}

Theorem 3.2

For all
n\geq 2
, the designs
\mathbf{X}_{n}
generated by Algorithm

\displaystyle h(\mathbf{X}_{n})\leq 2\,h_{n}^{*}\,,\;\;q(\mathbf{X}_{n})\geq\frac{1}{2}\,q_{n}^{*}\,,\;\;\mathsf{MR}(\mathbf{X}_{n})\leq 2\,.

Proof.
By Lemma

applied to the designs
\mathbf{X}_{n+1}

Theorem 3.3

For any

, define

with

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{llll}q(\mathbf{X}_{n})=\gamma_{m}\sqrt{2}/4\,,&\!\!h(\mathbf{X}_{n})=\gamma_{m}/2\,,&\!\!\mathsf{MR}(\mathbf{X}_{n})=\sqrt{2}\,,&\!\!\mbox{for }n=n_{m}\,,\\

For the sake of brevity, we only give a sketch of the full proof. It is based on the self-replicating pattern of the construction. The first five points in
{\mathscr{X}}=[0,1]^{2}
correspond to the corners and the center of the square. This gives the initialization for the beginning of the initial cycle, indexed by
m=0
, with
m
denoting the cycle number. Define the initialization of cycle
m
as the replication of the initial design of cycle
0
into
4^{m}
squares of side length
\gamma_{m}=2^{-m}
, which form a regular partition of
[0,1]^{2}
. The initial design for cycle
m
has thus
n_{m}=(2^{m}+1)^{2}+4^{m}=2^{2m+1}+2^{m+1}+1

(2^{m}+1)^{2}
of them form a regular grid of width
\gamma_{m}
(i.e., a
(2^{m}+1)^{2}
full factorial design); the other
4^{m}
points are the centers of the small squares. When moving to the next cycle, the algorithm first (
i
) adds the midpoints of the sides of all small squares (in arbitrary order), then (
ii
) adds the
4^{m+1}

\ell_{m}=(2^{m+1}+1)^{2}-[(2^{m}+1)^{2}+4^{m}]=2^{m+1}(2^{m}+1)

For any

is the unique integer satisfying

points are the centers of the small hypercubes. We thus have
2^{4m}
replications of the initial 17-point initial design, but in smaller hypercubes. In each of them, the selections made by the algorithm are similar to those of the cycle
m=0
.

From the description above, we can observe that the design
\mathbf{X}_{n_{m}}
re-scaled by a factor
2^{m}
gives the integer lattice
\mathds{Z}_{4}

truncated to
(i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},i_{4})\in\{0,\ldots,2^{m}\}^{4}
. Moreover, when
n=n_{m}+\ell_{m}
, the design
\mathbf{X}_{n}
re-scaled by
2^{m+1}
gives the
so-called checkerboard lattice
D_{4}
(the subset of the integer lattice
\mathds{Z}_{4}
consisting of quadruples whose sum is even), truncated to
(i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},i_{4})\in\{0,\ldots,2^{m+1}\}^{4}
; note that
D_{4}
is the densest packing lattice in the 4-dimensional space
[
1
, p. 9]
.

The regular behaviour of Algorithm
1
observed for
d=1
, and
d=2
and 4 where the properly re-scaled design
\mathbf{X}_{n}
oscillates between the integer point lattice and the checkerboard lattice, does not hold for other dimensions
d

3.2
Relaxed greedy packing

We consider now a generalization of Algorithm
1
, where the next point at a given iteration is not necessarily the furthest away from current design points, but is guaranteed to be far enough from them. The bounds obtained in Theorem
3.6
are worse than those in Theorem
3.2
; however, it can be shown that the relaxation introduced may improve the covering properties of the design sequence generated; see Section
3.4
.

Algorithm 2

(Relaxed greedy packing)

1:

{\mathscr{X}}
compact subset of
\mathds{R}^{d}
,
\mathbf{x}_{1}\in{\mathscr{X}}
,
a\in(0,1]
,
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots\in[a,1]
;

2:

set
n=1
,
\mathbf{X}_{1}=\{\mathbf{x}_{1}\}
;

3:

for
n=1,2,\ldots
do the following:

4:

take any
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
such that
\min_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbf{X}_{n}}\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}_{i}\|\geq\alpha_{n}\,h(\mathbf{X}_{n})
and set
\mathbf{x}_{n+1}=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
;

5:

set
\mathbf{X}_{n+1}=\mathbf{X}_{n}\cup\{\mathbf{x}_{n+1}\}
.

At step 3, the choice of
\mathbf{x}_{n+1}
is arbitrary provided it satisfies the condition indicated. Due to this flexibility, several existing algorithms form particular cases of Algorithm
2
, which is fact defines a whole family of algorithms. In particular, one may first select

\mathbf{x}^{*}\in\mathrm{Arg}\max_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}}\min_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in{\mathscr{X}}}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{i}\|
and then take any point
\mathbf{x}_{n+1}\in{\mathscr{B}}(\mathbf{x}^{*},(1-\alpha_{n})h(\mathbf{X}_{n}))
.

Theorem 3.6

For all
n\geq 2
, the designs
\mathbf{X}_{n}
generated by any version of Algorithm
2
satisfy

\displaystyle h(\mathbf{X}_{n})\leq\frac{2}{a}\,h_{n}^{*}\,,\;\;q(\mathbf{X}_{n})\geq\frac{a}{2}\,q_{n}^{*}\,,\;\;\mathsf{MR}(\mathbf{X}_{n})\leq\frac{2}{a}\,.

Proof.
We first prove by induction that for all
n\geq 2
,
q(\mathbf{X}_{n})\geq(a/2)\,h(\mathbf{X}_{n-1})
.

For
n=2
, by construction we have
q(\mathbf{X}_{2})\geq(\alpha_{1}/2)\,h(\mathbf{X}_{1})\geq(a/2)\,h(\mathbf{X}_{1})
.

Assume that
q(\mathbf{X}_{n})\geq(a/2)\,h(\mathbf{X}_{n-1})
and consider
q(\mathbf{X}_{n+1})
.
The induction assumption gives

\displaystyle q(\mathbf{X}_{n+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min\left\{q(\mathbf{X}_{n}),(1/2)\,\min_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbf{X}_{n}}\|\mathbf{x}_{n+1}-\mathbf{x}_{i}\|\right\}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\min\left\{q(\mathbf{X}_{n}),(\alpha_{n}/2)\,h(\mathbf{X}_{n})\right\}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\min\left\{(a/2)\,h(\mathbf{X}_{n-1}),(a/2)\,h(\mathbf{X}_{n})\right\}=(a/2)\,h(\mathbf{X}_{n})\,.

The inequality proved by induction implies

\mathsf{MR}(\mathbf{X}_{n})=h(\mathbf{X}_{n})/q(\mathbf{X}_{n})\leq h(\mathbf{X}_{n-1})/q(\mathbf{X}_{n})\leq 2/a\,.

Next, by Lemma
2.2
,
h(\mathbf{X}_{n-1})\geq q_{n}^{*}
, and therefore

q(\mathbf{X}_{n})\geq(a/2)\,h(\mathbf{X}_{n-1})\geq(a/2)\,q_{n}^{*}
.
The same lemma implies
h_{n-1}^{*}\geq q(\mathbf{X}_{n})\geq(a/2)\,h(\mathbf{X}_{n-1})
.

Theorem
3.2
follows from Theorem
3.6
by taking
a=1
. As in Theorem
3.2
, the choice of the norm in
{\mathscr{X}}
is irrelevant
and
{\mathscr{X}}
does not have to be a subset of
\mathds{R}^{d}
. In the next section, Theorem
3.6
is used for assessing properties of an easily implementable version of Algorithm
1
, where
\mathbf{x}_{n+1}
at step 3 is chosen from a finite set.

3.3
Greedy packing for a finite candidate set

Consider a version of Algorithm
1
where
\mathbf{x}_{n+1}
is chosen among a finite set of candidates
{\mathscr{X}}_{N}\subset{\mathscr{X}}
rather than from the whole
{\mathscr{X}}
. This assumption
makes the implementation of Algorithm
1
much simpler but naturally deteriorates its performance.
Such implementation of Algorithm
1
can be considered as a special case of Algorithm
2
, and hence, as we show below in Theorem
3.8
, its performance over entire
{\mathscr{X}}
can be assessed.
Note that the total number of iterations must be smaller than
N
, the number of candidate points: indeed, for
n\geq N
, the algorithm degenerates as several points necessarily coincide in
\mathbf{X}_{N+j}
,
j\geq 1
.

Lemma 3.7

For any
n
-point design
\mathbf{X}_{n}
and any
N
-point set
{\mathscr{X}}_{N}\subset{\mathscr{X}}
we have

\displaystyle h_{{\mathscr{X}}_{N}}(\mathbf{X}_{n})\leq h_{\mathscr{X}}(\mathbf{X}_{n})\leq h_{{\mathscr{X}}_{N}}(\mathbf{X}_{n})+h_{\mathscr{X}}({\mathscr{X}}_{N})\,.

Proof.
The inequality
h_{{\mathscr{X}}_{N}}(\mathbf{X}_{n})\leq h_{\mathscr{X}}(\mathbf{X}_{n})
follows from
{\mathscr{X}}_{N}\subset{\mathscr{X}}
. Next, denoting
{\mathscr{X}}_{N}=\{\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(N)}\}
, we have

\displaystyle h_{\mathscr{X}}(\mathbf{X}_{n})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}}\min_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbf{X}_{n}}\min_{\mathbf{x}^{(j)}\in{\mathscr{X}}_{N}}\left(\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{(j)}\|+\|\mathbf{x}^{(j)}-\mathbf{x}_{i}\|\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}}\left[\min_{\mathbf{x}^{(j)}\in{\mathscr{X}}_{N}}\left(\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{(j)}\|+\min_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbf{X}_{n}}\|\mathbf{x}^{(j)}-\mathbf{x}_{i}\|\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}}\left[\min_{\mathbf{x}^{(j)}\in{\mathscr{X}}_{N}}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{(j)}\|+\max_{\mathbf{x}^{(j)}\in{\mathscr{X}}_{N}}\min_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbf{X}_{n}}\|\mathbf{x}^{(j)}-\mathbf{x}_{i}\|\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}}\left[\min_{\mathbf{x}^{(j)}\in{\mathscr{X}}_{N}}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{(j)}\|+h_{{\mathscr{X}}_{N}}(\mathbf{X}_{n})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h_{\mathscr{X}}({\mathscr{X}}_{N})+h_{{\mathscr{X}}_{N}}(\mathbf{X}_{n})\,.\hskip 184.9429pt\mbox{}~\hfill\rule{5.69054pt}{5.69054pt}

Theorem 3.8

When Algorithm
1
uses a finite set of candidates
{\mathscr{X}}_{N}\subset{\mathscr{X}}
and
n<N
, its performance satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{lcll}h_{\mathscr{X}}(\mathbf{X}_{n})&\leq&(2/\alpha_{n})\,h_{n}^{*}\,,&\forall n\geq 1\,,\\
q(\mathbf{X}_{n})&\geq&(\alpha_{n}/2)\,q_{n}^{*}\,,&\forall n\geq 2\,,\\
\mathsf{MR}_{\mathscr{X}}(\mathbf{X}_{n})&\leq&2/\alpha_{n}\,,&\forall n\geq 2\,,\end{array}

with
\alpha_{n}=1-h_{\mathscr{X}}({\mathscr{X}}_{N})/h_{\mathscr{X}}(\mathbf{X}_{n})
.

Proof.
Denote
\epsilon=h_{\mathscr{X}}({\mathscr{X}}_{N})
, so that Lemma
3.7
gives
h_{{\mathscr{X}}_{N}}(\mathbf{X}_{n})\leq h_{\mathscr{X}}(\mathbf{X}_{n})\leq h_{{\mathscr{X}}_{N}}(\mathbf{X}_{n})+\epsilon
. At step 3 of Algorithm
1
, we have

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbf{X}_{n}}\|\mathbf{x}_{n+1}-\mathbf{x}_{i}\|=h_{{\mathscr{X}}_{N}}(\mathbf{X}_{n})\geq h_{\mathscr{X}}(\mathbf{X}_{n})-\epsilon=\alpha_{n}\,h_{\mathscr{X}}(\mathbf{X}_{n})\,,

with
\alpha_{n}=1-\epsilon/h_{\mathscr{X}}(\mathbf{X}_{n})
. Since
h_{\mathscr{X}}(\mathbf{X}_{n})
is non-increasing with
n
,
\alpha_{n}
is non-increasing too (it reaches zero when
\mathbf{X}_{n}
has exhausted
{\mathscr{X}}_{N}
, that is, when
k=N
). Theorem
3.6
with
\alpha_{n}
substituted for
a
implies (
3.8
).

As we do not know
h_{\mathscr{X}}(\mathbf{X}_{n})
and thus
\alpha_{n}
, we can use the inequality
h_{\mathscr{X}}(\mathbf{X}_{n})\geq h_{{\mathscr{X}}_{N}}(\mathbf{X}_{n})
, which gives
\alpha_{n}\geq a_{n}=1-h_{\mathscr{X}}({\mathscr{X}}_{N})/h_{{\mathscr{X}}_{N}}(\mathbf{X}_{n})
. The inequalities (
3.8
) then remain true with
a_{n}
substituted for
\alpha_{n}
, as long as
a_{n}>0
.

A result similar to Theorem
3.8
holds when the performance of Algorithm
1
is evaluated on a finite set
{\mathscr{X}}_{N^{\prime}}\supset{\mathscr{X}}_{N}
instead of
{\mathscr{X}}
: we simply substitute
{\mathscr{X}}_{N^{\prime}}
for
{\mathscr{X}}
and
\alpha_{n}=1-h_{{\mathscr{X}}_{N}^{\prime}}({\mathscr{X}}_{N})/h_{{\mathscr{X}}_{N}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{X}_{n})
is evaluated easily.

3.4
Boundary-phobic greedy packing

Versions of the greedy packing algorithm that enforce boundary avoidance have been proposed in
[
12
,
15
]
. There, at iteration
n\geq 2
, the next point
\mathbf{x}_{n+1}
is chosen in
\mathrm{Arg}\max_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}}D_{\beta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{X}_{n},{\mathscr{X}})
,
where

\displaystyle D_{\beta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{X}_{n},{\mathscr{X}})=\min\left\{\min_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbf{X}_{n}}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{i}\|,\,\beta\,d(\mathbf{x},\partial{\mathscr{X}})\right\}\,,\ \beta\in(0,\infty)\,,

(3.7)

with
d(\mathbf{x},\partial{\mathscr{X}})
the distance from
{\mathscr{X}}
to the boundary of
{\mathscr{X}}
. Note that this quantity is easily determined if
{\mathscr{X}}
has a simple shape, like a hypercube or a ball, but may be difficult to evaluate otherwise. For
\beta=\infty
, we define
D_{\infty}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{X}_{n},{\mathscr{X}})=\min_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbf{X}_{n}}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{i}\|
by continuity; the algorithm then coincides with Algorithm
1
. For
\beta=1
,
\mathbf{x}_{n+1}
is the center of (one of) the largest ball included in
{\mathscr{X}}
and not intersecting
\mathbf{X}_{n}
. For
\beta=2
, the algorithm corresponds to a greedy method for the solution of the traditional packing problem, for which the
n
balls do not intersect and are constrained to be fully inside
{\mathscr{X}}
. For
\beta>2
, the larger
\beta
is, the more the balls are allowed to overshoot
{\mathscr{X}}
, with their centers remaining inside
{\mathscr{X}}
. When
{\mathscr{X}}=[0,1]^{d}
and
\|\cdot\|=\|\cdot\|_{2}
, the value
\beta=2\,\sqrt{2d}
is recommended in
[
15
]
, while
[
12
]
recommends to let
\beta
depend on the targeted number
n_{\max}
of design points and suggests taking

\displaystyle\beta=\beta(n_{\max},d)=\frac{d}{2\,(n_{\max}V_{d})^{-1/d}}-\sqrt{d}\,,

with
V_{d}=\pi^{d/2}/\Gamma(d/2+1)
. Both references illustrate the interest of using
\beta<\infty
instead of Algorithm
1
in terms of fill distance
h(\mathbf{X}_{n})
. As shown below, for
{\mathscr{X}}=[0,1]^{d}
the boundary-phobic version of greedy packing becomes a particular case of Algorithm
2
.

Theorem 3.9

For
{\mathscr{X}}=[0,1]^{d}
and
\|\cdot\|=\|\cdot\|_{2}
, the boundary-phobic algorithm that chooses
\mathbf{x}_{n+1}
in
\mathrm{Arg}\max_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}}D_{\beta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{X}_{n},{\mathscr{X}})
at iteration
n
, with

D_{\beta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{X}_{n},{\mathscr{X}})
defined by (
3.7
) and
\beta\in(0,\infty)
, forms a particular instance of Algorithm
2
with
\alpha_{n}=a=1/(1+\sqrt{d}/\beta)
.

Proof.
Let
{\mathscr{X}}=[0,1]^{d}
and
\beta\in(0,\infty)
,
r_{n}=D_{\beta}(\mathbf{x}_{n+1},\mathbf{X}_{n},{\mathscr{X}})=\max_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}}D_{\beta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{X}_{n},{\mathscr{X}})
. Any
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}
satisfies at least one of the two inequalities

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbf{X}_{n}}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{i}\|\leq r_{n}\,,\ d(\mathbf{x},\partial{\mathscr{X}})\leq r_{n}/\beta\,.

This implies that
{\mathscr{X}}\setminus\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathds{R}^{d}:d(\mathbf{x},\partial{\mathscr{X}})\leq r_{n}/\beta\}\subset\cup_{i=1}^{k}{\mathscr{B}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},r_{n})
. The inequalities
r_{n}\leq\beta\,d(\mathbf{x}_{n+1},\partial{\mathscr{X}})\leq\beta/2
imply that
2\,r_{n}/\beta\leq 1
, and the set
{\mathscr{X}}\setminus\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathds{R}^{d}:d(\mathbf{x},\partial{\mathscr{X}})\leq r_{n}/\beta\}
is a hypercube
{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
with side length
1-2\,r_{n}/\beta
. This hypercube is covered by the
n
balls
{\mathscr{B}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},r_{n})
, implying that

\displaystyle h_{\mathscr{X}}(\mathbf{X}_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}}\min_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbf{X}_{n}}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{i}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}}\left[\inf_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{n}}\left(\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|+\min_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbf{X}_{n}}\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}_{i}\|\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}}\inf_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{n}}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|+r_{n}\leq\sqrt{d}\,(r_{n}/\beta)+r_{n}\,.

Since, by definition,
r_{n}\leq\min_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbf{X}_{n}}\|\mathbf{x}_{n+1}-\mathbf{x}_{i}\|
, we have

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbf{X}_{n}}\|\mathbf{x}_{n+1}-\mathbf{x}_{i}\|\geq\frac{h_{\mathscr{X}}(\mathbf{X}_{n})}{1+\sqrt{d}/\beta}\,,

and the algorithm is a particular instance of Algorithm
2
with
\alpha_{n}=a=1/(1+\sqrt{d}/\beta)
.

Theorem
3.9
implies that the performance of this algorithm satisfies the bounds indicated in Theorem
3.6
.
