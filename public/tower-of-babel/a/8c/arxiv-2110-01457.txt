General Relativistic Non-Neutral White Dwarf Stars
Érik Amorim, Parker Hund

Abstract
We generalize the recent Newtonian two-component charged fluid models for white dwarf stars of Krivoruchenko, Nadyozhin and Yudin and of Hund and Kiessling to the context of general relativity. We compare the equations and numerical solutions of these models. We extend to the general relativistic setting the non-neutrality results and bounds on the stellar charge obtained by Hund and Kiessling.

I
Introduction

In
KNY
, a two-component system of cold fluids in hydrostatic equilibrium with polytropic pressure density relation under Newtonian gravity was introduced and studied. This system is formed of electrons and ions and was first written

\displaystyle\mu_{i}+m_{i}\phi_{G}+Ze\phi_{E}

\displaystyle=\text{const}\ ,

(1)

\displaystyle\mu_{e}+m_{e}\phi_{G}-e\phi_{E}

\displaystyle=\text{const}\ ,

(2)

where
\mu_{i}
is the chemical potential of the ions,
\mu_{e}
is the chemical potential of the electrons,
\phi_{G}
is the gravitational potential,
\phi_{E}
is the electric potential,
m_{i}
and
m_{e}
are the masses of the ion particles and electrons, and
Ze
and
-e
are their charges. To close the system, a polytropic equation of state was taken. The usual assumption
Chandra
of local neutrality was not made, and this led to solutions which are not globally neutral.

HK1
was interested in applying this model to white dwarfs, in particular using the nonrelativistic ‘5/3’ polytropic pressure law derived from Fermi-Dirac statistics. However, they noted that, as the heavier ions in a white dwarf are bosons, such a pressure law does not apply to them.
HK1
subsequently studied in greater detail the two-fluid model consisting of only electrons and protons, noting that such a model would be an approximation to a small range of ‘brown dwarf’ stars. In that paper, it was noted that, for a wide range of pressure laws including the ‘5/3’ and special relativistic laws discussed in
Chandra
, there is a simple set of bounds on the total amount of charge such a star can hold in its ground state configuration. In CGS units, this is

\frac{1-\frac{Gm_{p}^{2}}{e^{2}}}{1+\frac{Gm_{e}m_{p}}{e^{2}}}\leq\frac{N_{e}}{N_{p}}\leq\frac{1+\frac{Gm_{e}m_{p}}{e^{2}}}{1-\frac{Gm_{e}^{2}}{e^{2}}}\ ,

(3)

where
N_{e}
and
N_{p}
are the total number of electrons and protons, and
G
is Newton’s gravitational constant. See also
HK2
for more details on these bounds. It was speculated that such bounds would also hold in the general relativistic case.

In the present paper, we determine the general relativistic version of these bounds, as well as present the general relativistic analogue of system (
1
) and (
2
). To do this, we apply the framework developed by Olson and Bailyn in a series of papers
OB1
,
OB2
, and
OB3
. However, in the course of our study, we also noticed that an algebra error had been made in
OB3
, the work most directly related to ours. Subsequently, the system presented there is incorrect, although it appears the correct system was used for the numerical computations. In section II we review this framework and present the corrected system of equations, in section III compare this system to the special relativistic version of (
1
) and (
2
), in section IV we extend the charge bounds results of
HK1
, in section V we present the result of numerical calculations, in section VI we discuss how these results apply to neutron stars, and in section VII we conclude.

II
The Olson-Bailyn approach

In
OB1
, it was noted that, in a multicomponent Einstein-Maxwell system of spherically symmetric fluids, there is one more unknown than equation. It is further noted that there are a variety of approaches to closing the system, and one of the more common is to assume local charge neutrality. Instead of making such an assumption, the authors of
OB1
minimize the energy of the system to determine the last equation. In doing so, they derive an equation for every component in the fluid, which they call the “species TOV” equations (their sum is equivalent to the Bianchi identity for the stress-energy tensor):

\mu_{i}^{\prime}=\frac{q_{i}}{r^{2}}\mathcal{E}e^{\lambda/2}+\mu_{i}\left(\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{2}-\frac{4\pi G}{c^{4}}re^{\lambda}\sum_{j}n_{j}\mu_{j}\right)\ ,

(4)

where the spacetime metric is taken as

ds^{2}=e^{\nu}dt^{2}-e^{\lambda}dr^{2}-r^{2}(d\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta d\phi^{2})\ ,

(5)

primes mean derivative with respect to the radial coordinate, the constant
q_{i}
is the charge of each particle of the species labelled by
i
(note that
i
does not mean “ion” here, but is instead a label),
n_{i}(r)
is the corresponding number density at radius
r
,
\mathcal{E}(r)
is the total charge below radius
r
, defined via

\mathcal{E}(r)=\int_{0}^{r}\left(\sum_{i}q_{i}n_{i}(s)\right)e^{\lambda(s)/2}s^{2}ds\ ,

(6)

and
\mu_{i}(r)
is the chemical potential of the fluid of particle species
i
, defined via

\mu_{i}(r)=c^{2}\frac{\partial\rho_{m}}{\partial n_{i}}(r)\ ,

(7)

where
\rho_{m}(r)
is the total mass-energy density. It is also noted that, except under very specific circumstances, (
4
) precludes local charge neutrality.

In
OB2
, a white dwarf star was modelled by applying these results to a two-component system formed of a perfect Fermi gas of electrons and a perfect Bose gas of nuclei. Since the Bose gas contributes no pressure and has mass-energy density of

\rho_{m_{N}}=m_{N}n_{N}

(8)

(
N
for “nucleon”), they are able to reduce the system down to a single second-order ODE, somewhat similar to the one found by Chandrasekhar in the locally neutral case.

In
OB3
, the results of
OB1
were applied to a model of a neutron star consisting of three perfect Fermi gases of electrons, protons, and neutrons. The system of equations used is

\mu_{i}^{\prime}=\frac{q_{i}\mathcal{E}}{r^{2}(1-2m/r)^{1/2}}+\frac{\mu_{i}}{1-2m/r}\left[-\frac{m}{r^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{G\mathcal{E}^{2}}{c^{4}r^{3}}+\frac{4\pi Gr}{c^{4}}(\rho_{m}c^{2}-\sum_{j=e,p,n}n_{j}\mu_{j})\right]\ ,

(9)

where the label
i
can be
e,p,n
for “electron”, “proton” and “neutron” and where
m(r)
is defined via

e^{-\lambda}=1-\frac{2m(r)}{r}\ ,

(10)

together with the equations

\displaystyle m^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{4\pi Gr^{2}}{c^{2}}\left[\rho_{m}+\frac{\mathcal{E}^{2}}{8\pi c^{2}r^{4}}\right]\ ,

(11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=4\pi r^{2}\frac{q_{e}n_{e}+q_{p}n_{p}}{\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right)^{1/2}}\ .

(12)

Note that the usual mass function is related to
m
by
M(r)=\frac{Gm(r)}{c^{2}}
. The mass-energy density function
\rho_{m}
that appears in equation (
11
) is assumed to be a known expression of the
n_{i}
, and thus also each
\mu_{i}
is determined by
n_{i}
. In that paper and also here for us, it is assumed that
\rho_{m}=\rho_{m_{e}}+\rho_{m_{p}}+\rho_{m_{n}}
, with

\rho_{m_{i}}=\frac{\pi m_{i}^{4}c^{3}}{h^{3}}(2\overline{n}_{i}+\overline{n}_{i}^{1/3})(\overline{n}^{2/3}_{i}+1)^{1/2}\\
-\ln(\overline{n}_{i}^{1/3}+(\overline{n}_{i}^{2/3}+1)^{1/2})\ ,

(13)

where

\overline{n}_{i}=\frac{3h^{3}n_{i}}{8\pi m_{i}^{3}c^{3}}\ .

(14)

This is the same energy density term derived in
Chandra

\mu_{i}
and

\mu_{i}=m_{i}c^{2}\sqrt{1+\overline{n_{i}}^{2/3}}\ .

(15)

The strategy in
OB3

11
) and (
12

9
) for

and

K=1-2r\frac{q_{p}\mu_{e}^{\prime}-q_{e}\mu_{p}^{\prime}}{q_{p}\mu_{e}-q_{e}\mu_{p}}\ ,

a=\frac{G^{1/2}r}{c^{2}}\frac{\mu_{e}\mu^{\prime}_{p}-\mu^{\prime}_{e}\mu_{p}}{q_{p}\mu_{e}-q_{e}\mu_{p}}\ ,

b=\frac{8\pi Gr^{2}}{c^{4}}(\rho_{m}c^{2}-\sum_{i}n_{i}\mu_{i})

(18)

(19)

\mathcal{E}=\frac{c^{2}ra}{G^{1/2}}\left(\frac{K-b}{K+a^{2}}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dr}\left(r\frac{b+a^{2}}{K+a^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{8\pi Gr^{2}}{c^{2}}\rho_{m}+a^{2}\frac{K-b}{K+a^{2}}\ ,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dr}\left(r\frac{b+a^{2}+K-1}{K+a^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{8\pi Gr^{2}}{c^{2}}\rho_{m}+a^{2}\frac{1-b}{K+a^{2}}\ ,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^{2}a^{2}\frac{1-b}{K+a^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{8\pi G^{1/2}ar^{3}}{c^{2}}(q_{e}n_{e}+q_{p}n_{p})\ .

(26)

Note that the corrected equations would not differ from the ones they reported if

Next, it is explained in

There are two remarks that we would like to make about this procedure. The first is that it appears that the numerical calculations in

The second remark is that the system comprised of equations (

III.1
The post-Minkowski approximation

A natural question to ask is whether the SR and GR models coincide in low orders of a power series expansion in
G
. We shall see here that they match only in zeroth order, which is to be expected: on the one hand, when
G=0
, the flat space of special relativity also solves the Einstein equations of general relativity, thus explaining the match at zeroth order; on the other hand, the SR model contains terms of degree 1 in
G
that are nonrelativistic in nature, derived from the Newtonian potential, and there’s no reason to hope that they will result from any approximation scheme performed from a fully relativistic model.

For
G=0
, equations (
29

\displaystyle(r^{2}\mu_{p}^{\prime})^{\prime}

\displaystyle=+4\pi q_{p}^{2}r^{2}(n_{p}-n_{e})\ ,

(32)

\displaystyle(r^{2}\mu_{e}^{\prime})^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-4\pi q_{p}^{2}r^{2}(n_{p}-n_{e})\ ,

(33)

while equation (

\displaystyle r^{2}\mu_{p}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=q_{p}\mathcal{E}\ ,

III.2
The weak field limit of the GR model

A different type of approximation scheme often performed in general relativity is the
weak field limit
, in which the metric is assumed to be close to the flat metric of special relativity. One might ask whether the GR model reduces to the SR model under this approximation, but we shall see here that the answer is also no, although the discrepancy is small.

Taking into account the definition (
10
) of the mass function, we see that, in order to find the weak field limit of the GR model, one must assume that the expression
GM/c^{2}r
and its derivatives are small. So let us study what the equations of the model become when neglecting all occurrences of

and its derivative in (
12

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=4\pi r^{2}(q_{e}n_{e}+q_{p}n_{p})\ ,

(42)

\displaystyle r^{2}\mu_{i}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=q_{i}\mathcal{E}-\frac{4\pi G\mu_{i}r^{3}}{c^{4}}(n_{e}\mu_{e}+n_{p}\mu_{p})\ .

(43)

Therefore, taking the derivative of (
43

42

(r^{2}\mu_{i}^{\prime})^{\prime}=4\pi q_{i}r^{2}(q_{e}n_{e}+q_{p}n_{p})\\
-\frac{4\pi G}{c^{4}}\bigg(\mu_{i}r^{3}(n_{e}\mu_{e}+n_{p}\mu_{p})\bigg)^{\prime}\ .

(44)

This only matches the SR equations (
29
) and (
30

\phi_{G}
as well as
GM/c^{2}r
, as well as let
1/c^{2}=0
in order to obtain the Newtonian limit, then the equation above turns into the usual Newtonian equation

\phi_{G}^{\prime}=\frac{GM}{r^{2}}\ ,

(52)

where
M
now satisfies

M^{\prime}(r)=4\pi r^{2}(m_{e}n_{e}+m_{p}n_{p})\ .

(53)

We also find that the electric potential reduces to

\phi_{E}^{\prime}=\frac{\mathcal{E}}{r^{2}}\ ,

(54)

which is the same as its usual classical equation, considering the weak field form (
42
) of the
\mathcal{E}
equation. Now replace
e^{\nu/2}
by
1+\phi_{G}/c^{2}
and
\mu_{i}
by
m_{i}c^{2}+\mu_{i}^{NR}
in (
47
), multiply out, ignore the ensuing term
\mu_{i}^{NR}/c^{2}
and differentiate; we find the Newtonian model equations

(\mu_{i}^{NR})^{\prime}+m_{i}\phi_{G}^{\prime}+q_{i}\phi_{E}^{\prime}=0\ .

(55)

IV
Bounds on the charge

In this section we find charge bounds analogous to (
3
) for the GR model of a system composed of electrons and protons. Assume that we have a solution of the system and suppose that the support of
n_{p}
is larger than that of
n_{e}
and is bounded (if it has unbounded support, much of the next section can be skipped, although we do need to assume sufficient decay of
\mu_{p}^{\prime}
). Then multiply the proton version of (
9
) by
r^{2}
and evaluate at
R
, the radius of the star, which by definition is the smallest
r
at which
n_{e}(r)=n_{p}(r)=0
. We get

\frac{q_{p}\mathcal{E}(R)}{\left(1-\frac{2m(R)}{R}\right)^{1/2}}-\frac{m_{p}c^{2}m(R)}{1-\frac{2m(R)}{R}}+\frac{m_{p}c^{2}}{1-\frac{2m(R)}{R}}\frac{G\mathcal{E}^{2}(R)}{2c^{4}R}=R^{2}\mu^{\prime}_{p}(R)\ .

(56)

Let
Q=\mathcal{E}(R)
be the total charge and
M=\frac{c^{2}}{G}m(R)
the total mass. Then (
56
) can be written as

\frac{q_{p}Q}{\left(1-\frac{2GM}{c^{2}R}\right)^{1/2}}-\frac{m_{p}GM}{1-\frac{2GM}{c^{2}R}}+\frac{m_{p}c^{2}}{1-\frac{2GM}{c^{2}R}}\frac{GQ^{2}}{2c^{4}R}=R^{2}\mu^{\prime}_{p}(R)\ .

(57)

If it was the electron support which was larger, the same argument would give us

\frac{q_{e}Q}{\left(1-\frac{2GM}{c^{2}R}\right)^{1/2}}-\frac{m_{e}GM}{1-\frac{2GM}{c^{2}R}}+\frac{m_{e}c^{2}}{1-\frac{2GM}{c^{2}R}}\frac{GQ^{2}}{2c^{4}R}=R^{2}\mu^{\prime}_{e}(R)

(58)

(notice that now the first term is negative). These equations are the analogues of equations (67) and (70) from
HK1
.

Since (
3
) gives bounds on the
most extreme
charges a configuration can hold, to make a direct comparison we do the same. However, since there are two degrees of freedom in the GR model (in both cases the central densities), what we call “the most extreme charge” is only most extreme under the assumption of some other quantity being fixed. In the nonrelativistic case, this quantity was
N_{p}=N_{p}(R)
, which was an easy quantity to work with since it was directly related to both
M
and
Q
via
M=m_{e}N_{e}+m_{p}N_{p}
and
Q=q_{e}N_{e}+q_{p}N_{p}
. It was noted in
HK1
that the density
n_{p}
or
n_{e}
must be decreasing at the boundary of the star so that the right hand sides of (67) and (70) in that paper are both nonpositive, which gives

\displaystyle q_{e}Q-m_{e}GM

\displaystyle\leq 0\ ,

(59)

\displaystyle q_{p}Q-m_{p}GM

\displaystyle\leq 0\ ,

(60)

and plugging the definitions of
M
and
Q
in terms of
N_{e}
and
N_{p}
into these equations yields the charge bounds (
3
). In the GR model, however,
M
is not defined as a simple linear combination of
N_{e}
and
N_{p}
, so
N_{p}
is not easy to work with. We therefore seek to make a comparison to (
59
) and (
60
), which we can see already somewhat resemble (
57
) and (
58
).

To this end, we first also make the observation as in
HK1
that
\mu_{p}^{\prime}(R)\leq 0
, since
\mu_{p}
decreases to
m_{p}c^{2}
close to the surface of the star. Now assume that we have a positively charged configuration, that is,
Q>0
. We are interested in the most charged configurations, so we consider what happens as we increase
N_{p}
and hold
N_{e}
fixed. If we knew that the radius
R
diverges faster than
Q
or
M
as
N_{p}
increases, we could apply this to (
57
) to give us (
60
). We therefore seek to prove this.

As explained in section III of
OB1
, the special TOV equations are obtained from the condition
\frac{\partial\Lambda}{\partial N_{i}}=0
for

\Lambda=M+\frac{GQ^{2}}{Rc^{4}}\ .

(61)

Differentiating this with respect to
N_{i}
and taking into account
Q=q_{e}N_{e}+q_{p}N_{p}
, we can compute that

\frac{\partial R}{\partial N_{i}}=\frac{2Rq_{i}}{Q}+\frac{c^{4}R^{2}}{GQ^{2}}\frac{\partial M}{\partial N_{i}}\ .

(62)

Since we are assuming
Q>0
and are increasing
N_{p}
, the first term on the right is positive. So, if we can show that
\frac{\partial M}{\partial N_{p}}>0
, we show that the radius is increasing with increasing
N_{p}
. We show in the appendix that

\frac{\partial M}{\partial N_{p}}=m_{p}e^{-\lambda(R)/2}\ .

(63)

Therefore we can conclude that, given
Q>0
, as
N_{p}
increases, so too does
R
.

Now we would like to conclude that
R
increases faster than
Q
and
M
. First note that, by (
62
),
R
grows faster than
M
since
\frac{c^{4}R^{2}}{GQ^{2}}>1
. By (
10
) we have

e^{-\lambda(R)}=1-\frac{2M}{R},

(64)

so as
R
increases,
e^{-\lambda(R)/2}
increases, this allows us to say that as we increase
R
, we have a lower bound on
e^{-\lambda(R)/2}
, let us call it
e^{-\overline{\lambda}/2}
. So we can say that in this situation,

\frac{\partial M}{\partial N_{p}}\geq m_{p}e^{-\overline{\lambda}/2}>0\ .

(65)

Since
Q
grows linearly in
N_{p}
, we can write that
Q(N_{p})=Q_{0}+q_{p}N_{p}
for an appropriate
Q_{0}
. Define
\hat{R}
through

\frac{\partial\hat{R}}{\partial N_{p}}=\frac{c^{4}\hat{R}^{2}m_{p}}{G(Q_{0}+q_{p}N_{p})^{2}}e^{-\overline{\lambda}/2}\ .

(66)

Comparing (
62
) and (
66
), we can see that
R>\hat{R}
, as long as we choose the correct initial condition for
\hat{R}
. (
66
) can be solved by a separation of variables to give

\frac{1}{\hat{R}}=\frac{c^{4}m_{p}e^{-\overline{\lambda}}}{Gq_{p}(Q_{0}+N_{p}q_{p})}+K\ .

(67)

Now
K
is negative if

\hat{R}(0)>\frac{Gq_{p}Q_{0}}{c^{4}m_{p}e^{-\overline{\lambda}/2}}\ .

(68)

Since we can “start” the differential equation when
Q_{0}=q_{p}>0
, this inequality will be true as the right hand side is very small, but the left is larger than the radius of a neutral configuration, which is still thousands of kilometers. Then

\hat{R}=\frac{Gq_{p}(Q_{0}+N_{p}q_{p})}{c^{4}m_{p}e^{-\overline{\lambda}/2}+KGq_{p}(Q_{0}+N_{p}q_{p})}\ .

(69)

Since
K
is negative,
\hat{R}
blows up at a finite
N_{p}
, which implies that so does
R
. We may then conclude that, for fixed
N_{e}
,
Q
is bounded above and
R
grows faster than
Q
for large enough
N_{p}
.

We can get another lower bound on
R
by following the same procedure with the first term on the right in (
62
). Define
\bar{R}
by

\frac{\partial\bar{R}}{\partial N_{p}}=\frac{2\bar{R}q_{p}}{Q_{0}+N_{p}q_{p}}\ .

(70)

Solving this we get

\bar{R}=(Q_{0}+q_{p}N_{p})^{2}+K

(71)

for
K
the integration constant. We can therefore conclude that
R>Q
.

Thus we can finally conclude that, as
N_{p}
grows,
R
grows faster than
M
and
Q
, and further that there is some finite
N_{p}^{\infty}
such that
R\rightarrow\infty
as
N_{p}\rightarrow N_{p}^{\infty}
. Therefore, by taking this limit, we can determine that, in the most positively charged cases, (
57
) reduces to (
60
). The exact same argument works to show that, in the most negatively charged cases, (
58
) reduces to (
59
). So we conclude that, in terms of
Q
and
M
, the relativistic bounds on charge are the same as (
59
) and (
60
).

VI
Neutron Stars

Everything we have done above applies as well when we consider neutron stars. The only difference in the neutron star model is that a third equation of the form (
9
) is added for the Fermi gas of neutrons. This third equation is implied by the equation for
\beta
-equilibrium

\mu_{n}=\mu_{p}+\mu_{e}\ .

(74)

Figures
8
,
9
, and
10
illustrate, using the science-fiction values, some possible configurations given by this model. Immediately one notices that these science fiction values produce some rather extreme looking configurations: for a central proton density of 100, the largest the central electron density can be for a stable configuration is about 6.5. We have kept these same values for consistency, and also because we are interested in how the solutions change as we change the central densities, and, as in the white dwarf case, these science fiction values dramatically exaggerate the changes. Compare, for example, these plots to figure 2 in
Ruffini2011a
.

Another thing one notices is that, as one approaches the extremely charged cases, the neutron density has a larger support than one of the electron or proton density. This would seem to indicate a limitation of the model.

VII
Conclusion

We have applied the model developed by Olson and Bailyn in
OB1
,
OB2
and
OB3
to write the equations of a general relativistic model of a star composed of fluids of electrons, protons and (optionally) neutrons. It generalizes the Newtonian model from
KNY
and the special relativistic model from
HK1
in the sense that all three make the same predictions in the Newtonian limit (large
c
), but we have also mentioned some differences between them in the post-Minkowski (small
G
) and weak field (flat metric) approximations. After pointing out an arithmetic error in the Olson-Bailyn papers which was inconsequential in their numeric calculations but relevant in ours, we showed how the equations of the model can be used to rigorously prove the bounds (
72
) on the total charge that the star can hold. The bounds proved were the same as in
HK1
for the special relativistic model. We then plotted numerical solutions that do not assume local charge neutrality or approximate neutrality, as was the case in
OB3
. The graphs obtained behave similarly to the special relativistic ones appearing in
HK1
, and they also provide evidence that our charge bounds are close to begin sharp.

VII.1
Singularity of (
23
)

One could rewrite equation (
23
) in terms of the unknown

s=\frac{h^{2}}{m_{e}^{2}c^{2}}\left(\frac{3n_{e}}{8\pi}\right)^{2/3}

(75)

in the following form:

\displaystyle\frac{b+a^{2}}{K+a^{2}}+\frac{r}{K+a^{2}}\left(\frac{\partial b}{\partial r}+\frac{\partial b}{\partial s}s^{\prime}+2a\left(\frac{\partial a}{\partial r}+\frac{\partial a}{\partial s}s^{\prime}+\frac{\partial a}{\partial s^{\prime}}s^{\prime\prime}\right)\right)

(76)

\displaystyle-r\frac{b+a^{2}}{(K+a^{2})^{2}}\left(\frac{\partial K}{\partial r}+\frac{\partial K}{\partial s}s^{\prime}+\frac{\partial K}{\partial s^{\prime}}s^{\prime\prime}+2a\left(\frac{\partial a}{\partial r}+\frac{\partial a}{\partial s}s^{\prime}+\frac{\partial a}{\partial s^{\prime}}s^{\prime\prime}\right)\right)=\frac{8\pi Gr^{2}}{c^{2}}\rho_{m}+a^{2}\frac{K-b}{K+a^{2}}

or, solving for
s^{\prime\prime}
,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{2ar}{K+a^{2}}-r\frac{b+a^{2}}{(K+a^{2})^{2}}\left(\frac{\partial K}{\partial s^{\prime}}+2a\frac{\partial a}{\partial s^{\prime}}\right)\right)s^{\prime\prime}=-\frac{b+a^{2}}{K+a^{2}}-\frac{r}{K+a^{2}}\left(\frac{\partial b}{\partial r}+\frac{\partial b}{\partial s}s^{\prime}+2a\left(\frac{\partial a}{\partial r}+\frac{\partial a}{\partial s}s^{\prime}\right)\right)

(77)

\displaystyle+r\frac{b+a^{2}}{(K+a^{2})^{2}}\left(\frac{\partial K}{\partial r}+\frac{\partial K}{\partial s}s^{\prime}+2a\left(\frac{\partial a}{\partial r}+\frac{\partial a}{\partial s}s^{\prime}\right)\right)+\frac{8\pi Gr^{2}}{c^{2}}\rho_{m}+a^{2}\frac{K-b}{K+a^{2}}\ .

A simple heuristic way to see the singularity at
r=0
is to consider the asymptotic behavior of these terms as
r\rightarrow 0
. We assume
s(0)\neq 0
, but that
s^{\prime}(0)=0
, although we do not know the rate at which
s^{\prime}
goes to zero: by L’Hopital’s rule we would need to know
s^{\prime\prime}
. Thus we have the following behaviors near zero:
a\sim rs^{\prime}
,
b\sim r^{2}
,
K\sim 1
,
\frac{\partial a}{\partial s}\sim rs^{\prime}
,
\frac{\partial a}{\partial r}\sim s^{\prime}
,
\frac{\partial a}{\partial s^{\prime}}\sim r
,
\frac{\partial b}{\partial s}\sim r^{2}
,
\frac{\partial b}{\partial r}\sim r
,
\frac{\partial K}{\partial r}\sim s^{\prime}
,
\frac{\partial K}{\partial s}\sim rs^{\prime}
, and
\frac{\partial K}{\partial s^{\prime}}\sim r
.

One can check that the coefficient of
s^{\prime\prime}
has leading order term behaving like
r^{3}s^{\prime}
or
r^{4}
: it is possible these terms could cancel, but then the coefficient would go to zero even faster. On the right side of (

) however, the leading order terms are

and

which do not cancel and behave like

near zero. So there is no way to remove the singularity: to leading order we have either

(78)

or

s^{\prime\prime}=\frac{c}{rs^{\prime}}

(79)

In the former case,
s
has a singularity at
r=0
from a logarithmic term. In the latter case, one can consider
T(r)=\frac{(s^{\prime}(r))^{2}}{2}
so that

, and

. Fixing

, we compute

(80)

which implies that

(81)

So we must have

become unbounded around

.

\displaystyle\delta m(r)

where we defined the integrating factor

(93)

which implies that

\frac{\partial m(R)}{\partial N_{p}}=\frac{G}{c^{2}}e^{-\lambda(R)/2}\frac{\partial\rho_{m}}{\partial n_{p}}(R)\ .

(94)

Taking into account
