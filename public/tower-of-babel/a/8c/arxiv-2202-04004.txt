Abstract
. Two related questions are discussed. The first is when reflection symmetry in a finite set of i i -dimensional subspaces, i ∈ { 1 , … , n − 1 } i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\} , implies full rotational symmetry, i.e., the closure of the group generated by the reflections equals O ⁡ ( n ) O(n) . For i = n − 1 i=n-1 , this has essentially been solved by Burchard, Chambers, and Dranovski, but new results are obtained for i ∈ { 1 , … , n − 2 } i\in\{1,\dots,n-2\} . The second question, to which an essentially complete answer is given, is when (full) rotational symmetry with respect to a finite set of i i -dimensional subspaces, i ∈ { 1 , … , n − 2 } i\in\{1,\dots,n-2\} , implies full rotational symmetry, i.e., the closure of the group generated by all the rotations about each of the subspaces equals S ​ O ​ ( n ) SO(n) . The latter result also shows that a closed set in ℝ n {\mathbb{R}}^{n} that is invariant under rotations about more than one axis must be a union of spheres with their centers at the origin.

1.
Introduction

The question of when symmetry in a restricted set of subspaces implies full rotational symmetry arises naturally in several contexts. Radial functions are particularly amenable to analysis, and a function on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is radial if and only if it is symmetric with respect to reflection in any hyperplane through the origin. A basic geometrical or mechanical problem asks whether a body that is rotationally symmetric in more than one axis must be a ball. Our motivation comes from the widely applied method of Steiner symmetrization, in which iterated Steiner symmetrals of a compact set, if taken with respect to a suitable sequence of hyperplanes, converge to a ball. For more information about the latter, see
[
2
]
, where we find use for all the main results obtained here.

Let
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
. We say that a set
G
of reflections in
i
-dimensional subspaces of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
generates
O(n)
if the closure of the group
\langle G\rangle
generated by
G
equals
O(n)
. For the applications we have in mind, it is particularly desirable to find finite sets
G
with this property, and this is the focus of most of the paper, in Section 3. In Section 4, we address the corresponding question as to when
G
generates
SO(n)
if
G
is the set of all the rotations about each of several subspaces, each with dimension between
1
and
n-2
.

In the case of reflections, prior work mainly addresses the case when
i=n-1
, i.e., of reflections in hyperplanes through the origin. When
G
is infinite, Eaton and Perlman
[
4
]
proved that
G
generates
O(n)
if it is irreducible, i.e., there is no nontrivial proper subspace
S
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that
gS\subset S
for
g\in G
, and it is clear that this sufficient condition is also necessary. The problem for arbitrary sets
G
was solved by Burchard, Chambers, and Dranovski
[
3
]
. In
[
3
, Proposition 4.2]
, they show that for a set of reflections to generate
O(n)
, it suffices that the unit normal vectors to the corresponding hyperplanes satisfy three conditions: (i) at least two are at an angle that is an irrational multiple of
\pi
; (ii) they span
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
; and (iii) they cannot be partitioned into two mutually orthogonal subsets. As these authors point out on
[
3
, p. 1189]
, the first condition can be replaced by (i
′
): the set of reflections do not belong a finite Coxeter group. The latter is weaker than (i), since, when
n>2
, it is enough that at least two unit normal vectors are at an angle which is not an integer multiple of
\pi/3
,
\pi/4
, or
\pi/5
. Conditions (i
′
), (ii), and (iii) are necessary as well as sufficient.

We obtain the following results for reflections. Theorem
3.2
deals with the relatively simple case when
i=1
, and proves that the conditions (i), (ii), and (iii) stated above suffice, where the unit vectors are now taken parallel to the subspaces. When
i\in\{2,\dots,n-2\}
, the situation is inherently more complicated. One of the main ingredients in our approach is the quite difficult but general Lemma
3.7
, which we call the symmetry extension lemma. This assumes that
H
and
L
are subspaces of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that
H\cap L^{\perp}=\{o\}
and
\dim H<\dim L
, and that
G
is a closed subgroup of
O(n)
that acts transitively on
S^{n-1}\cap(L+x)
for each
x\in S^{n-1}
. (Here
+
denotes the Minkowski or vector sum; see Section
2
for this and other notation and terminology.) The conclusion is that the closure of the subgroup generated by
G
and the reflection
R_{H}
in
H
acts transitively on
S^{n-1}\cap(H+L+x)
for each
x\in S^{n-1}
. Using the symmetry extension lemma and other tools such as Kronecker’s approximation theorem, we are able in Theorem
3.8
to provide sufficient conditions for a finite set of reflections in
i
-dimensional subspaces to generate
O(n)
. In Corollary
3.9
, we conclude that if

k=\begin{cases}n,&\text{if $i=1$ or $i=n-1$,}\\
\lceil n/i\rceil+1,&\text{if $1<i\leq n/2$,}\\
\lceil n/(n-i)\rceil+1,&\text{if $n/2\leq i<n-1$,}\end{cases}

then there are
i
-dimensional subspaces
H_{1},\dots,H_{k}
, such that the reflections
R_{H_{1}},\dots,R_{H_{k}}
generate
O(n)
, or, equivalently, are
such that if
E\subset S^{n-1}
is nonempty, closed, and satisfies
R_{H_{j}}E=E
for
j=1,\dots,k
, then
E=S^{n-1}
. This in turn implies that if
K
is a convex body in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
satisfying
R_{H_{j}}K=K
for
j=1,\dots,k
, then
K
is a ball with center at the origin.

The previous result prompts the question as to what happens if reflections in subspaces are replaced by (full) rotational symmetries in subspaces, and we answer this in Section 4. (See
[
6
]
for a result in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
in the same spirit.) There are easy examples of nonempty, closed, proper subsets of
S^{n-1}
that are left invariant under all rotations about more than one subspace; for example, the Cartesian product in
{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
of two orthogonal copies of the circle with center at the origin and radius
1/\sqrt{2}
is invariant under any rotation about each of the two-dimensional subspaces orthogonal to the circles. Similarly, the Cartesian product in
{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
of two orthogonal disks centered at the origin (a so-called duocylinder) is a nonspherical convex body of revolution about each of the two-dimensional subspaces orthogonal to the disks. Theorem
4.1
states a necessary and sufficient pair of conditions for subspaces
H_{1},\dots,H_{k}
with
1\leq\dim H_{j}\leq n-2
to be such that if a nonempty closed subset of
S^{n-1}
is invariant under any rotation about each
H_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,k
, then
E=S^{n-1}
. The latter is equivalent to saying that a closed set in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
that is invariant under rotations that fix
H_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,k
, must be a union of spheres with their centers at the origin, or to saying that if a convex body
K
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is rotationally symmetric in
H_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,k
, then
K
must be a ball with center at the origin. The conditions are (a)
H_{1}^{\perp}+\cdots+H_{k}^{\perp}={\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and (b)
\{H_{1}^{\perp},\dots,H_{k}^{\perp}\}
cannot be partitioned into two mutually orthogonal nonempty subsets. Corollary
4.2
draws the conclusion that if
1\leq i\leq n-2
and
k=\lceil n/(n-i)\rceil
, then there are
i
-dimensional subspaces

H_{1},\dots,H_{k}
, that satisfy (a) and (b). Corollary
4.3
shows that among closed sets in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
n\geq 3
, only unions of spheres with their centers at the origin are invariant under rotations about two different axes.

3.
Full rotational symmetry via reflections in finitely many subspaces

This section focuses on finding finite sets of
i
-dimensional subspaces such that reflections in these subspaces generate full rotational symmetry.

Our first main result, Theorem

to be rotationally symmetric with respect to

Lemma 3.1
.

of

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
H_{j}\in{\mathcal{G}}(n,1)
,
j=1,\dots,n
, be such that

(i)
at least two of them form an angle that is an irrational multiple of
\pi
,

(ii)

H_{1}+\cdots+H_{n}={\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, and

(iii)

\{H_{1},\dots,H_{n}\}
cannot be partitioned into two mutually orthogonal nonempty subsets.

If
E\subset S^{n-1}
is nonempty, closed, and such that
R_{H_{j}}E=E
,
j=1,\dots,n
, then
E=S^{n-1}
.

Proof.

We claim that there is an ordering of
H_{1},\dots,H_{n}
so that for
m=2,\dots,n
and all

, we have

(2)

S^{n-1}\cap(H_{1}+\cdots+H_{m}+v)\subset E.

When

, this yields the theorem, by hypothesis (ii).

We argue by induction on

. By reordering, if necessary, we may, by hypothesis (i), assume that
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
form an angle that is an irrational multiple of

. The composition

. By reordering, if necessary, we may assume that this line is

Let

Lemma 3.7
.

(Symmetry extension lemma.)
Let
H
and
L
be subspaces of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that
H\cap L^{\perp}=\{o\}
and
\dim H<\dim L
. If

is nonempty, closed, and invariant under
R_{H}
and under any element of
O(n)_{L^{\perp}}
, then
E
is invariant under any element of
O(n)_{(H+L)^{\perp}}
. The same conclusion holds if invariance under
R_{H}
is replaced by invariance under
R_{H^{\perp}}
.

Proof.

Let
G
be the closure of the subgroup of
O(n)
generated by
O(n)_{L^{\perp}}
and
R_{H}
. The first conclusion of the lemma is equivalent to the statement that for all
x\in E
, the orbit
G(x)
contains the orbit
O(n)_{(H+L)^{\perp}}(x)
. The latter orbit is
S^{n-1}\cap(H+L+x)
, and we claim that it coincides with
S_{x}=S^{n-1}\cap(M+x)
, where
M=L+R_{H}L
. (See Figure 2, where for the sake of illustration, both
H
and
L
are 1-dimensional.) It suffices to prove that
M=H+L
. To this end, we use the easily-proved identity
A^{\perp}\cap B^{\perp}=(A+B)^{\perp}
, where
A
and
B
are subspaces of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, to conclude that the assumption
H\cap L^{\perp}=\{o\}
is equivalent to
H^{\perp}+L={\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Then
L|H=(H^{\perp}+L)|H={\mathbb{R}}^{n}|H=H
. Hence, if
z\in H+L
, then
z=x|H+y
for some
x,y\in L
. Thus

z=(x/2+y)+(x|H-x/2)=(x/2+y)+R_{H}(x/2)\in L+R_{H}L.

This shows that
H+L\subset M
. On the other hand, the definition of
R_{H}
implies that
R_{H}L\subset(L|H)+L\subset H+L
and hence that
M\subset H+L
. This proves the claim.

Figure 2.

Let
x\in E
. We have to show that
S_{x}\subset G(x)
. If
x\in M^{\perp}
, then
S_{x}=\{x\}
and there is nothing to prove. Therefore we may assume that
x\notin M^{\perp}
, in which case
S_{x}
is of dimension
\dim M-1
. We may also assume that
\dim H>0
, since otherwise
H+L=L
and the first conclusion of the lemma is trivially true. Then
\dim L>1
, which in turn yields
\dim M=\dim\,(H+L)>1
, so
S_{x}
is connected. In order to prove that
G(x)
contains
S_{x}
, we shall prove that
G(x)\cap S_{x}
is both closed and open relative to
S_{x}
.

To prove that
G(x)\cap S_{x}
is closed relative to
S_{x}
, let
\{y_{k}\}
be a sequence of points in
G(x)\cap S_{x}
converging to
y
. By the definition of

, for each
k
, there is a
\phi_{k}\in O(n)
such that
\phi_{k}(x)=y_{k}
. Since
O(n)
is compact, there exists a subsequence
\{\phi_{k_{m}}\}
of
\{\phi_{k}\}
converging to
\phi\in O(n)
. Clearly
\phi(x)=y
, so

, and

because
S_{x}
is closed.

It remains to show that
G(x)\cap S_{x}
is open relative to
S_{x}
. By the closed-subgroup theorem (see, for example,
[
9
, Theorem 3.42]
),
G
is a Lie group, because it is a closed subgroup of a Lie group. Since
G
is a
C^{\infty}
manifold and the map
a:G\to G(x)
given by

is
C^{\infty}
, the orbit
G(x)
is also a

manifold. Moreover,
G(x)\cap S_{x}
is also a manifold, because
G(x)\subset S_{x}\cup R_{H}S_{x}=S_{x}\cup S_{R_{H}x}
, and the manifolds
S_{x}
and
S_{R_{H}x}
either coincide or are disjoint. Since

is a submanifold of
S_{x}
, it is open relative to
S_{x}
if (and only if) the dimension of
G(x)\cap S_{x}
equals the dimension
\dim M-1
of
S_{x}
.

Let
p\in G(x)\cap S_{x}
. The orbit of
p
with respect to each closed subgroup of

is a submanifold of

. Two such subgroups are natural for our purposes:
O(n)_{L^{\perp}}
and its conjugate
R_{H}O(n)_{L^{\perp}}R_{H}

just mentioned. The tangent space to a

manifold at any point is a vector space whose dimension equals that of the manifold; see, for example,

9

and

, while if

, then

Thus we need to prove that if

then

(19)

Suppose that (

) is false. Then

contains all vectors which are differences of points in

. The assumption

implies that

has dimension

. It follows that

contains

. Therefore

is closed and

for

and

for

is a union of

-symmetric spheres (or

-symmetric ball, respectively).

Proof.

Let

satisfy the stated hypotheses. If

is a consequence of Theorem

and

. Suppose that

∎

4.
Full rotational symmetry via rotational symmetries in finitely many subspaces

This section focuses on finding finite sets of
i

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
H_{1},\dots,H_{k}
be subspaces in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that
1\leq\dim H_{j}\leq n-2
for
j=1,\dots,k
. The following statements are equivalent.

(i)

H_{1}^{\perp}+\cdots+H_{k}^{\perp}={\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
\{H_{1}^{\perp},\dots,H_{k}^{\perp}\}
cannot be partitioned into two mutually orthogonal nonempty subsets.

(ii)
If
E\subset S^{n-1}
is nonempty and closed, and for each
j=1,\dots,k
and
x\in E
, we have
S^{n-1}\cap(H_{j}^{\perp}+x)\subset E
, then
E=S^{n-1}
.

(iii)

If
F\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is closed and invariant under any rotation that fixes
H_{j}
for each
j=1,\dots,k
, then
F
is a union of
o
-symmetric spheres.

(iv)

If
K\in{\mathcal{K}}^{n}_{n}
is rotationally symmetric with respect to
H_{j}
for each
j=1,\dots,k

K
is an
o
-symmetric ball.

The hypothesis on

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
1\leq i\leq n-2
and let
k=\lceil n/(n-i)\rceil
. There exist
H_{j}\in{\mathcal{G}}(n,i)
,
j=1,\dots,k
, such that the statements in Theorem
4.1
hold.

Proof.

It suffices to ensure that Theorem
4.1
(i) holds. This is easily accomplished by choosing
H_{j}\in{\mathcal{G}}(n,i)
inductively such that for
k=1,2,\dots
, we have

H_{k+1}^{\perp}\not\subset(H_{1}^{\perp}+\dots+H_{k}^{\perp})^{\perp}
and

\dim(H_{1}^{\perp}+\cdots+H_{k}^{\perp})\geq\min\{k(n-i),n\},

until
k(n-i)\geq n
, i.e.,
k=\lceil n/(n-i)\rceil
.
∎

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
n\geq 3
. If
H_{1},H_{2}\in{\mathcal{G}}(n,1)
with
H_{1}\neq H_{2}
, the statements in Theorem
4.1
hold.

No finite
k
exists such that if
H_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,k
, are different subspaces with
2\leq\dim H_{j}\leq n-2
for
j=1,\dots,k
, the statements in Theorem
4.1
hold.

Proof.

Let
H_{1},H_{2}\in{\mathcal{G}}(n,1)
with
H_{1}\neq H_{2}
. Since
\dim H_{1}^{\perp}=\dim H_{2}^{\perp}=n-1
and
H_{1}^{\perp}\neq H_{2}^{\perp}
, Theorem
4.1
(i) holds.

Suppose that
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. The assumption that

for
j=2,\dots,k
allows us to choose different
H_{j}
’s such that

are all contained in the same

-dimensional subspace. The first condition in Theorem

(i) then fails.

With these observations in hand, the result follows from Theorem
4.1
.
∎
