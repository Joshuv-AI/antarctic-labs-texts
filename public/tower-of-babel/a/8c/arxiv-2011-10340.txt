Abstract
. For a finite-dimensional representation V V of a group G G we introduce and study the notion of a Lie element in the group algebra k ⁡ [ G ] k[G] . The set ℒ ( V ) ⊂ k ⁡ [ G ] \mathop{\mathcal{L}}\nolimits(V)\subset k[G] of Lie elements is a Lie algebra and a G G -module acting on the original representation V V . Lie elements often exhibit nice combinatorial properties. Thus, for a G = S n G=S_{n} and V V , a permutation representation, we prove a formula for the characteristic polynomial of a Lie element similar to the classical matrix-tree theorem.

1.
Introduction: Lie elements in the group algebra

Let
V
be a finite-dimensional representation of a group
G
over a field

k
. For every
g\in G
and every
m
define linear operators
\mathop{\mathcal{G}}\nolimits_{m}\langle g\rangle,\mathop{\mathcal{A}}\nolimits_{m}\langle g\rangle:V^{\wedge m}\to V^{\wedge m}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathcal{G}}\nolimits_{m}\langle g\rangle(v_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{m})

\displaystyle=g(v_{1})\wedge\dots\wedge g(v_{m})

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathcal{A}}\nolimits_{m}\langle g\rangle(v_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{m})

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{m}v_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge g(v_{p})\wedge\dots\wedge v_{m}.

(here and below
v_{1},\dots,v_{m}
are arbitrary vectors in
V
). Also
take by definition

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathcal{G}}\nolimits_{0}\langle g\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathcal{I}}\nolimits

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathcal{A}}\nolimits_{0}\langle g\rangle

\displaystyle=0

for every
g\in G
. Here and below
\mathop{\mathcal{I}}\nolimits
means the identity operator.

Denote by
k[G]
the group algebra of
G
; extend
\mathop{\mathcal{G}}\nolimits_{m}
and

\mathop{\mathcal{A}}\nolimits_{m}
by linearity to operators
k[G]\to\mathop{\rm End}\nolimits(V^{\wedge m})
. In particular,
\mathop{\mathcal{A}}\nolimits_{0}=0
and
\mathop{\mathcal{G}}\nolimits_{0}\langle\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}g\rangle=\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}
(a constant regarded as an operator
k\to k
).

Definition 1.1
.

An element
x\in k[G]
satisfying
\mathop{\mathcal{G}}\nolimits_{m}\langle x\rangle=\mathop{\mathcal{A}}\nolimits_{m}\langle x\rangle
for all
m=0,1,\dots,\dim V
is called a Lie element (with respect to the representation

V
). The set of Lie elements is denoted by
\mathop{\mathcal{L}}\nolimits(V)\subset k[G]
.

Remark 1.2
.

In particular, if
x=\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}g
is a Lie element then
\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}=\mathop{\mathcal{G}}\nolimits_{0}\langle x\rangle=\mathop{\mathcal{A}}\nolimits_{0}\langle x\rangle=0
.

Example 1.3
.

Let
G=S_{n}
(a permutation group),
k={\mathbb{C}}
and
V={\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, the permutation representation of
S_{n}
(an element of
the group permutes the coordinates of a vector
v=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
).

Lemma 1.4
.

\varkappa_{ij}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize def}}}{{=}}1-(ij)\in{\mathbb{C}}[S_{n}]
is a Lie element.

Here
(ij)\in S_{n}
means a transposition of
i
and
j
; more
generally, we will use notation like
(i_{1}\dots i_{k})
for a cyclic
element in
S_{n}
, that is, a permutation sending
i_{1}\mapsto i_{2}\mapsto\dots\mapsto i_{k}\mapsto i_{1}
and leaving all other elements of
\{1,\dots,n\}
intact.

We call
\varkappa_{ij}\in{\mathbb{C}}[S_{n}]
a
Kirchhoff difference

as a tribute to G. Kirchhoff’s seminal paper
[
5
]
(1847);
see Theorem
2.4
below.

Proof of Lemma
1.4
.

The proof is a direct computation. First,
\mathop{\mathcal{G}}\nolimits_{m}\langle 1\rangle=\mathop{\mathcal{I}}\nolimits
and
\mathop{\mathcal{A}}\nolimits_{m}\langle 1\rangle=m\mathop{\mathcal{I}}\nolimits
. Obviously (cf. Proposition
1.7
below), one can assume

i=1
,
j=2
without loss of generality. Denote by
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
the
standard basis in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
; by linearity, it is enough to
consider the action of
\mathop{\mathcal{G}}\nolimits_{m}\langle(12)\rangle
and
\mathop{\mathcal{A}}\nolimits_{m}\langle(12)\rangle
on
v=v_{i_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{i_{m}}
where
1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{m}\leq n
.

Consider now three cases:

•

i_{1}\geq 3
: here
\mathop{\mathcal{G}}\nolimits_{m}\langle(12)\rangle v=v
and
\mathop{\mathcal{A}}\nolimits_{m}\langle(12)\rangle v=mv
, and
therefore
\mathop{\mathcal{G}}\nolimits_{m}\langle 1-(12)\rangle v=0=\mathop{\mathcal{A}}\nolimits_{m}\langle 1-(12)\rangle v
.

•

i_{1}=2
: here
\mathop{\mathcal{G}}\nolimits_{m}\langle(12)\rangle v=v_{1}\wedge v_{i_{2}}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{i_{m}}
and
\mathop{\mathcal{A}}\nolimits_{m}\langle(12)\rangle v=(v_{1}+(m-1)v_{2})\wedge v_{i_{2}}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{i_{m}}
, so that
\mathop{\mathcal{G}}\nolimits_{m}\langle 1-(12)\rangle v=\mathop{\mathcal{A}}\nolimits_{m}\langle 1-(12)\rangle v=(v_{2}-v_{1})\wedge v_{i_{2}}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{i_{m}}
. The case
i_{1}=1
and
i_{2}\geq 3
is similar.

•

finally,
i_{1}=1,i_{2}=2
: here
\mathop{\mathcal{G}}\nolimits_{m}\langle(12)\rangle v=-v
, so

\mathop{\mathcal{A}}\nolimits_{m}\langle(12)\rangle v=(m-2)v
, and therefore
\mathop{\mathcal{G}}\nolimits_{m}\langle 1-(12)\rangle v=2v=\mathop{\mathcal{A}}\nolimits_{m}\langle 1-(12)\rangle v
.

Lemma is proved.
∎

For any

and

the

and

Proposition 1.5
.

Maps
\mathop{\mathcal{G}}\nolimits_{m},\mathop{\mathcal{A}}\nolimits_{m}:k[G]\to\mathop{\rm End}\nolimits(V^{\wedge m})
are Lie algebra
homomorphisms.

Proof.

Obviously,
\mathop{\mathcal{G}}\nolimits_{m}:k[G]\to\mathop{\rm End}\nolimits(V^{\wedge m})
is an associative
algebra homomorphism, hence a Lie algebra homomorphism. For
\mathop{\mathcal{A}}\nolimits_{m}
take
x=\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}g
,
y=\sum_{h\in G}b_{h}h
, to obtain

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathcal{A}}\nolimits_{m}\langle x\rangle

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathcal{A}}\nolimits_{m}\langle y\rangle v_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{m}=\sum_{h\in G,1\leq p\leq m}b_{h}\mathop{\mathcal{A}}\nolimits_{m}\langle x\rangle v_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge h(v_{p})\wedge\dots\wedge v_{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{g,h\in G,1\leq p\leq m}a_{g}b_{h}v_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge g(h(v_{p}))\wedge\dots\wedge v_{m}

\displaystyle\hphantom{=}+\sum_{g,h\in G,1\leq p,q\leq m,p\neq q}a_{g}b_{h}v_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge h(v_{p})\wedge\dots\wedge g(v_{q})\wedge\dots\wedge v_{m},

implying
\mathop{\mathcal{A}}\nolimits_{m}\langle[x,y]\rangle=[\mathop{\mathcal{A}}\nolimits_{m}\langle x\rangle,\mathop{\mathcal{A}}\nolimits_{m}\langle y\rangle]
.
∎

Corollary 1.6
.

The set of Lie elements
\mathop{\mathcal{L}}\nolimits(V)\subset k[G]
is a Lie subalgebra.

Proposition 1.7
.

Operators
\mathop{\mathcal{G}}\nolimits_{m}
and
\mathop{\mathcal{A}}\nolimits_{m}
are conjugation-invariant: if
x\in k[G]
and
y\in k[G]
is invertible then for any
m
one has
y\mathop{\mathcal{G}}\nolimits_{m}\langle x\rangle y^{-1}=\mathop{\mathcal{G}}\nolimits_{m}\langle yxy^{-1}\rangle
and
y\mathop{\mathcal{A}}\nolimits_{m}\langle x\rangle y^{-1}=\mathop{\mathcal{A}}\nolimits_{m}\langle yxy^{-1}\rangle
.

The proof is straightforward.

Corollary 1.8
.

The Lie algebra
\mathop{\mathcal{L}}\nolimits(V)\subset k[G]
is a representation of
G

where elements of the group act by conjugation.

2.
Lie elements in the relection representation of the permutation group

2.1.
Kirchhoff differences and their commutators

Let
G=S_{n}
(a permutation group), and
V
be its reflection
(a.k.a. Coxeter or geometric) representation;
\dim V=n-1
. The
permutation representation

is a sum of

1.2

For a finite-dimensional representation

element

(

4
]

3.
Shuffle determinant

Here are basic properties of the shuffle determinant of Definition

2.7
:

Theorem 3.1
.

(1)

\mathop{\rm sdet}\nolimits(A,B)
is a polynomial of variables

a_{ij}
and
b_{ij}
,
1\leq i,j\leq n
, with integer coefficients,
bihomogeneous of degree
n
(thus, its total degree is
2n
).

(2)

\mathop{\rm sdet}\nolimits(B,A)=\mathop{\rm sdet}\nolimits(A,B)
.

(3)

Let
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize def}}}{{=}}\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
be a
diagonal matrix with
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
as diagonal entries. Then

\mathop{\rm sdet}\nolimits(A,B)=[x_{1}\dots x_{n}:\det(A+BX)^{2}]
(that is,
\mathop{\rm sdet}\nolimits(A,B)

is equal to the coefficient at the monomial
x_{1}\dots x_{n}
in the
polynomial
\det(A+BX)^{2}
).

(4)

\mathop{\rm sdet}\nolimits(CA,CB)=\mathop{\rm sdet}\nolimits(A,B)\det C^{2}
for any
n\times n
-matrix
C
. In particular, if
B
is invertible then
\mathop{\rm sdet}\nolimits(A,B)=\mathop{\rm sdet}\nolimits(B^{-1}A,\mathop{\mathcal{I}}\nolimits)\det B^{2}
.

(5)

\mathop{\rm sdet}\nolimits(A,\mathop{\mathcal{I}}\nolimits)=(-1)^{n}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}(-2)^{\nu(\sigma)}a_{1\sigma(1)}\dots a_{n\sigma(n)}
where

\nu(\sigma)
the number of independent cycles in
