A quantum annealing approach to the minimum distance problem of quantum codes
Refaat Ismail, Ashish Kakkar, Anatoly Dymarsky

Abstract
Quantum error-correcting codes (QECCs) are at the heart of fault-tolerant quantum computing. As the size of quantum platforms is expected to grow, one of the open questions is to design new optimal codes of ever-increasing size. A related challenge is to “certify” the quality of a given code by evaluating its minimum distance, a quantity characterizing code’s capacity to preserve quantum information. This problem is known to be NP-hard. Here we propose to harness the power of contemporary quantum platforms to address this question, and in this way help design quantum platforms of the future. Namely, we introduce an approach to compute the minimum distance of quantum stabilizer codes by reformulating the problem as a Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO) problem and leveraging established QUBO algorithms and heuristics as well as quantum annealing (QA) to address the latter. The reformulation as a QUBO introduces only a logarithmic multiplicative overhead in the required number of variables. We demonstrate the formulation using the D-Wave Advantage 4.1 quantum annealer and the hybrid quantum-classical algorithm Qbsolv, and benchmark the resulting QUBO-based implementations against specialized classical distance-finding methods. The QA- and SA-assisted implementations show broadly comparable performance, while the classical comparison clarifies the relative strengths of heuristic optimization and exact distance certification. Our results establish a solver-independent QUBO formulation with only a logarithmic multiplicative overhead in the number of variables and characterize connectivity and minor embedding as central limitations of its present implementation on quantum-annealing hardware.



I
Introduction

The field of quantum error correction has been the focus of intense research in recent decades due to its role in the development of fault-tolerant quantum computing. Quantum fault tolerance offers a trade-off between efficiency in storing and manipulating information and robust protection against errors
[
34
]
. One of the central theoretical goals is to design QECCs that minimize the required resources while enhancing the protective properties.

Quantum algorithms operate on an abstract Hilbert space, known as the logical Hilbert space. When employing a QECC, we restrict the logical Hilbert space to a specific subspace of the full computational (also referred to as physical) Hilbert space. Errors that send the computation outside the code subspace could be detected and corrected. In contrast, errors that map the code subspace into itself are undetectable. The code’s minimum distance

is defined as the smallest weight of an undetectable error, i.e. how many physical qubits this error affects
[
26
]
.

A QECC can be characterized by three parameters: the number of physical qubits

. Corresponding codes are conventionally denoted as
((n,K,d))
. Constructing QECCs of ever-increasing

, with a fixed ratio

and the best (largest)

is an open problem. Our emphasis will be on stabilizer codes, the most common class of quantum codes introduced in
[
26
]
and widely considered the best strategy to achieve fault tolerance in the near term.
A binary stabilizer code, i.e. over qubits, protects the
K=2^{k}
states, where

. Various other code constructions have been presented

, including those generated by computational methods

,

,

]
.

An optimal code is the one with the highest known value of

. In practice to certify that code is optimal or close to optimal is a difficult problem.
Depending on how the code is defined, determining

[

]
. There exist multiple approaches for computing the minimum distance of quantum codes. One of the best deterministic algorithms was developed by G. White and M. Grassl and has been incorporated into MAGMA

In this paper, we introduce a new computational technique to evaluate or bound from above the minimum distance of binary quantum stabilizer codes, by reformulating the question as a Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO) problem. A QUBO is a general framework to represent combinatorial optimization problem as a problem to minimize quadratic functions of binary variables

25
]
. To reformulate the minimum distance problem as a QUBO, one needs to introduce auxiliary variables, e.g. to implement mod
2

Reformulating the problem in terms of QUBO introduces a promising pathway to harness power of quantum computational schemes such as Adiabatic Quantum Computing (AQC) and Quantum Annealing (QA), alongside a spectrum of established classical algorithms and heuristics designed specifically for QUBO problems. There is an ongoing effort to reformulate many NP-hard problems of theoretical and practical value within a unified framework of QUBO, with such problems as SAT, Knapsack, Maximum Cut, and Graph Coloring already being worked out in the literature

,

[
17
,
21
,
39
,
45

To investigate the efficiency of our approach, implemented on contemporary quantum hardware, we focus on quantum annealing (QA) as a method to solve QUBO. QA is a quantum heuristic approach, inspired by the principles of Adiabatic Quantum Computing (AQC)
[
27
,
22

To investigate the viability of QA in our case, we tested our method using both the D-Wave Advantage 4.1 quantum annealer and Qbsolv, a hybrid quantum-classical algorithm developed by D-Wave and running on their platform
[
40

[

Our results show broadly comparable performance between the QA- and

here. The quantum demonstrations therefore serve primarily as a

II.2
Minimum distance

The weight of a Pauli operator – an element from the Pauli group – is the number of qubits on which it acts non-trivially. The weight of a Pauli operator can be calculated using the binary representation

\displaystyle d=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{c}=G\,\boldsymbol{x}\pmod{2},\quad\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n+k},\quad\boldsymbol{c}\notin\ker(G^{T}\Lambda).

Here the constraint

\boldsymbol{c}\notin\text{Im}(H^{T})
which ensures that we minimize only over the set of logical Pauli operations. A stabilizer code with minimum distance

II.3
Self-dual stabilizer codes

Self-dual codes represent a special class of stabilizer codes with

By initializing a quantum processor in a stabilizer state and evolving in time such that it should return to the original state, we can detect and identify any errors that may have accumulated during this evolution. For self-dual codes, the minimum distance of the code is the smallest weight among the weights of all non-trivial elements of the stabilizer group,

\displaystyle d=

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{c}}w(\boldsymbol{c}),

(6)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{c}=G\boldsymbol{x}\ \text{(mod 2)},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n},\quad\boldsymbol{c}\neq 0^{2n}.

For self-dual codes, we can chose

. An

III
Algorithm

In this section, we reformulate the problem of finding the minimum distance of a stabilizer code as a QUBO
(quadratic unconstrained binary optimization) problem.

III.1
Quadratic binary cost function

The transpose of the normalizer matrix can be partitioned as
G^{T}=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\end{pmatrix}
, where
A
and
B
are

binary matrices corresponding to Pauli X and Pauli Z support, respectively. Throughout this section, matrix

\mathbb{P}(m):=m\bmod 2,

(7)

and apply

\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\mathbb{P}(A\boldsymbol{x}),\qquad\boldsymbol{\beta}=\mathbb{P}(B\boldsymbol{x}).

(8)

Using the definition of the Pauli weight in Eq. (
4
), the minimum distance in Eq. (
5
) can be written as

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\notin\mathcal{C}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\alpha_{i}^{2}+\beta_{i}^{2}-\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}\right),\qquad\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n+k},

(9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\{\boldsymbol{0}\},&k=0,\\[5.69054pt]
\left\{\boldsymbol{x}\;|\;G^{T}\Lambda G\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{0}\right\},&k\neq 0.\end{array}\right.

The minimization in Eq. (
9
) already has a quadratic cost function in the binary variables
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{i}

\boldsymbol{x}
are constrained by

III.2
Reformulation of the mod 2 summation

The cost function (
9

\alpha_{i}
and

\beta_{i}

variables

. We can express it one of two ways, which

\alpha_{i}=\sum_{j}A_{ij}x_{j}\bmod 2=\frac{1}{2}\left[1-\prod_{j}\left(1-2A_{ij}x_{j}\right)\right].

(13)

The first form is linear in the

Decomposition of the Pauli weight into parities.

The first step is to rewrite the Pauli-weight contribution in a form that involves only sums of parity functions. The original expression

\alpha_{i}^{2}+\beta_{i}^{2}-\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}

\alpha_{i}^{2}=\alpha_{i}

It follows that

\alpha_{i}^{2}+\beta_{i}^{2}-\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i}+\mathbb{P}(\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i})\right].

(16)

Using Eq. (
8

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[\mathbb{P}\left((A\boldsymbol{x})_{i}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left((B\boldsymbol{x})_{i}\right)\right]

(17)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left((A\boldsymbol{x})_{i}+(B\boldsymbol{x})_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\left[(A+B)\boldsymbol{x}\right]_{i}\right).

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}^{2}+\beta_{i}^{2}-\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\Big[

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}\left(\left[(A+B)\boldsymbol{x}\right]_{i}\right)\Big].

III.3
Reformulating the constraints

The construction above removes the modular arithmetic from the objective, but the minimization is still restricted by
\boldsymbol{x}\notin\mathcal{C}

III.3.1
Constraint as a penalty term

Different stabilizer codes are called equivalent if their stabilizer generators can be mapped to each other using conjugation by local Clifford operations

4

4

Local Clifford operations are tensor products of single-qubit unitaries that map Pauli operators to themselves under conjugation.
, and qubit permutations. The definition of code equivalence ensures that equivalent codes have the same minimum distance. Code equivalences, together with the freedom to select generators of the normalizer, can be used to bring the normalizer matrix

For

to be brought to the form

G=\begin{pmatrix}L&H^{T}\end{pmatrix},

(40)

where

contains

contains generators of the stabilizer group
[

is equivalent to requiring that at least one of the first

is nonzero. The self-dual and non-self-dual cases can be written uniformly by defining

\mathcal{I}=\begin{cases}\{1,\ldots,2k\},&k\neq 0,\\
\{1,\ldots,n\},&k=0,\end{cases}\qquad L_{\mathcal{I}}:=|\mathcal{I}|,

(41)

and

S(\boldsymbol{x}):=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}x_{i}.

(42)

The desired constraint is simply

S(\boldsymbol{x})\geq 1.

(43)

Introduce an auxiliary integer

1\leq h\leq L_{\mathcal{I}}

and add the penalty

\Pi(\boldsymbol{x})=\lambda\min_{1\leq h\leq L_{\mathcal{I}}}\left(S(\boldsymbol{x})-h\right)^{2}.

(45)

If

makes the penalty vanish. If

, the smallest possible value is obtained for

\Pi(\boldsymbol{x})=\begin{cases}0,&S(\boldsymbol{x})\geq 1,\\
\lambda,&S(\boldsymbol{x})=0.\end{cases}

(46)

Since the Pauli weight cannot exceed

ensures that every forbidden configuration has a larger total cost than any admissible configuration. The integer

(48)

s_{h}=\left\lceil\log_{2}L_{\mathcal{I}}\right\rceil.

(49)

The constrained minimum-distance problem is therefore replaced by

(50)

The constraint introduces only

) belongs to exactly one such sector, according to the position of its first nonzero component. Therefore,

d=\min_{1\leq j\leq L_{\mathcal{I}}}\left[\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega_{j}\\
\boldsymbol{y}\end{subarray}}F(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\right].

(52)

The original constrained problem can thus be replaced by
L_{\mathcal{I}}=O(n)
unconstrained QUBOs, with some of the original variables fixed in each one. Although this generally requires more QUBO solves than the penalty approach, it can be advantageous when symmetries make the different sectors equivalent.

Circulant self-dual codes.

Consider a self-dual code with

G^{T}=\begin{pmatrix}I_{n}&B\end{pmatrix},

(53)

where
B
is circulant. A cyclic permutation of the components of
\boldsymbol{x}
induces the same cyclic permutation of
B\boldsymbol{x}
and therefore leaves the Pauli weight invariant. Any nonzero configuration can consequently be cyclically shifted so that a nonzero component occupies the first position. The constraint
\boldsymbol{x}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
can therefore be imposed simply by fixing

x_{1}=1.

(54)

Hence, for circulant self-dual codes, only a single unconstrained QUBO needs to be solved.

IV
Adiabatic Quantum Computing and Quantum Annealing

Adiabatic Quantum Computing (AQC) is a universal model for quantum computation based on the adiabatic theorem. AQC starts by initializing the quantum processor in an easy-to-prepare ground state of some chosen initial Hamiltonian,
H(0)=H_{I}
. Subsequently, the system’s Hamiltonian is slowly evolved over an extended time,
t_{a}
, transitioning towards a problem Hamiltonian,
H(t_{a})=H_{p}
, whose ground state encodes the solution to a problem of interest. The adiabatic theorem guarantees that the evolved state
\ket{\Psi(t_a)}
will have a large overlap with the ground state of the problem Hamiltonian provided that the evolution was done slow enough
[
23
]
. In fact, this overlap, often termed the probability of success,
P_{s}:=\absolutevalue{\braket{0,t_a}{\Psi(t_a)}}^{2}
satisfies

\lim\limits_{t_{a}\to\infty}P_{s}=1.

(55)

Here, the state
\ket{m,t}
satisfies
H(t)\ket{m,t}=E_{m}(t)\ket{m,t}
with
E_{m}(t)
denoting the
m
-th lowest eigenvalue of
H(t)
.

For Hamiltonians that can be written as
H(t)=\tilde{H}(\gamma)
, where
\gamma:=t/t_{a}\in[0,1]
, and
\tilde{H}
has no explicit dependence on
t_{a}
, the adiabatic condition can be expressed as

\displaystyle t_{a}\gg

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar\ \mathcal{E}}{g^{2}_{\text{min}}},

(56)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}:=\max\limits_{\gamma}\absolutevalue{\left\langle 1,\gamma\middle| \partial_{\gamma} \Tilde{H} \middle| 0,\gamma\right\rangle},

\displaystyle g_{\text{min}}:=\min\limits_{\gamma}\absolutevalue{E_1(\gamma) - E_0(\gamma)}.

This condition ensures that
P_{s}
is finite for finite values of
t_{a}

[
23
]
. The performance of AQC depends on the chosen adiabatic path, specifically the choice of the initial Hamiltonian and the trajectory of evolution towards the problem Hamiltonian. For Hamilotnians exhibiting final ground state degeneracy,
g_{\text{min}}
is calculated between the instantaneous ground state and the first excited state that does not evolve eventually into the final ground state subspace
[
18
]
. In this scenario, we define the probability of success,
P_{s}
, as the overlap between the final evolved state and the subspace of final degenerate ground-states. Notably, AQC has been shown to be polynomially equivalent, in terms of the resource overhead, to universal gate-based quantum computation
[
1
]
.

Quantum Annealing (QA) is a heuristic approach that follows the same process as AQC but relaxes the condition of adiabaticity. The departure from adiabatic evolution introduces excitations in the system’s state, voiding the guarantee of convergence to the ground state of the problem Hamiltonian. To mitigate the limitations of nonadiabaticity, the algorithm repeats the entire evolution multiple times, generating various candidate final states. The algorithm then reports the best overall result. The computational power of QA and whether it offers a speedup over classical computation is currently an open area of research
[
27
]
, with studies comparing the performance of QA against classical algorithms like Simulated Annealing (SA) and Simulated Quantum Annealing (SQA)
[
11
]
.

Quantum annealers are devices designed to run the QA algorithm. D-Wave systems have introduced multiple commercially available quantum annealers
[
13
]
. In this paper, we focus on the D-wave Advantage 4.1 quantum annealer, which operates by evolving a mixed-field quantum Ising Hamiltonian of the form

\displaystyle H(\gamma)=

\displaystyle\underbrace{-\frac{\mathcal{A}(\gamma)}{2}\sum_{i}X_{(i)}}_{\text{initial Hamiltonian}}

(57)

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\frac{\mathcal{B}(\gamma)}{2}\left(\sum_{i}h_{i}Z_{(i)}+\sum_{i>j}J_{ij}Z_{(i)}Z_{(j)}\right)}_{\text{problem Hamiltonian}}.

Here,
\mathcal{A}(\gamma)
and
\mathcal{B}(\gamma)
are functions that control the transition between the initial and problem Hamiltonians, satisfying
\mathcal{A}(0)\gg\mathcal{B}(0)
and
\mathcal{A}(1)\ll\mathcal{B}(1)
. The parameters
\gamma
and
t_{a}
are referred to as the annealing parameter and the annealing time, respectively. The existence of a map between any QUBO cost function and the problem Hamiltonian of the quantum Ising model
[
37
]
makes the D-Wave Advantage 4.1 quantum annealer a natural tool to solve the QUBOs constructed in the previous section. The exact functional forms of
\mathcal{A}(\gamma)
and
\mathcal{B}(\gamma)
as implemented on the device, can be found in
[
13
]
. More information about the device is provided in the appendix.

IV.1
Necessary runtime analysis

The runtime necessary to evaluate the optimum for the worst-case instance of an NP-complete optimization problem is believed to be exponentially large in problem size for both classical and quantum algorithms. Hypothetically however, it could be that quantum algorithms offer a modest (sub-exponential) speedup. It is also conceivable that quantum algorithms may offer systematically better performance on average, i.e. for typical instances
[
2
]
. In practice there are many factors affecting AQC runtime necessary to solve a QUBO problem e.g. associated with the circulant self-dual optimal stabilizer codes.
We discuss the most important of them below, assuming for simplicity
that the quantum device possesses sufficient connectivity to enable direct mapping of QUBO variables onto the physical qubits. The effects of limited connectivity, which are by far the most important, will be discussed in later sections.

Figure 1:
Estimation of necessary annealing time.
Both panels show the annealing time required to achieve
P_{s}=0.9
(
P_{s}
stands for probability of success), calculated using the form of
\mathcal{A}(s)
and
\mathcal{B}(s)
utilized by the D-Wave Advantage 4.1 quantum annealer. a) depicts the relationship between this annealing time and the ground-state degeneracy of the problem Hamiltonian. b) shows the same annealing time against the code size. For larger code sizes, the dimensions of the corresponding Ising Hamiltonian exceeded computational feasibility.

It is well-known that the degeneracy of the ground state, even in absence of quantum noise and decoherence, may significantly influence necessary annealing time
t_{a}
.
Given that circulant codes are cyclic, our problem Hamiltonian
H(\gamma=1)
always exhibits multiple ground states. Ground state degeneracy can both increase and decrease
t_{a}
, depending on the degeneracy of the excited states
[
38
,
32
,
18
]
. To address this question in our case we numerically simulated the time-dependent Schrodinger equation describing the annealing process for codes of sizes
n=3-8
. In these simulations annealing time was varied until the success rate
P_{s}=0.9
was achieved. The results, shown in the panel a) of Fig. 1 are consistent with the inverse correlation between the ground state degeneracy and minimal necessary
t_{a}
.

This conclusion is very preliminary, as the degeneracy of final Hamiltonian is merely one of the factors. Another important factor is the smallest value of gap of
H(\gamma)
, which may change significantly for large
n
(we checked that for codes of sizes
n=3-7
it does not). For the Hamiltonian employed, it can be demonstrated that
\mathcal{E}
scales polynomially with the problem size, as follows from the observation that the eigenvalues of
H_{I}
and
H_{P}
are also at most polynomial. Yet, understanding the scaling of
g_{\text{min}}
with
n
is usually a very difficult problem, see
[
2
]
.

Our classical simulations of the annealing dynamics, shown in Fig. 1, panel b), suggest that theoretically required annealing times for the small code sizes
n\sim 3-8
fall within the nanosecond range. In practice, as we discussed in the next section, reasonable necessary annealing time on the D-Wave Advantage 4.1 platform for these problems is of the order of microseconds. This suggests
theoretically quantum annealer is operating firmly within the adiabatic regime, but in practice its quality of performance is dominated by other factors related to quantum noise. The factors discussed above are much less important, at least for moderate problem sizes
n
. This instructs our approach in the next section to optimize the annealing time
t_{a}
empirically.

V
Results of Quantum Demonstrations on the D-Wave QA Platform

V.1
Quantum-only implementation

We tested our QUBO-based algorithm by running implementations on the D-Wave Advantage 4.1 quantum annealer. We ran instances of optimal circulant self-dual codes of small length, taken from the database composed by M. Grassl
[
28
]
. The constraint
\boldsymbol{x}\neq 0^{n}
was imposed by setting
x_{1}=1
as described in Sec.
III.3.2
. To quantify how close the quantum annealer got to the optimal value, we calculated the so-called approximation ratio for various code sizes. The approximation ratio is the ratio between the smallest value generated by the quantum annealer and the actual minimum distance. As shown in Figure
2
, the quantum annealer was able to successfully find exact minimum distance for codes of sizes up to
12
. For larger code sizes, despite numerous re-runs, QA could only find conservative upper bounds. Our results strongly suggest that QA alone can not currently compete with purely classical algorithms/implementations, but there could be a room to use QA as a tool to obtain rigorous upper bounds.

This figure plots the Approximation ratio (AR) of best output generated by runs of quantum annealer solving the QUBO associated with optimal circulant codes of length

. AR equals one means the algorithm found the global minimum (exact value of the minimum code distance). Larger AR means that despite numerous runs, the algorithm failed to find the global minimum and only produced a conservative bound. We performed 40 runs (one run is a 100 anneals) for each code size.

To systematically analyze the performance of quantum annealer,
we study how the quality of outcome depends on the annealing time
t_{a}
,
a free parameter of implementation of the quantum algorithm. At least naively, longer annealing time would make time evolution more adiabatic. But since the platform is noisy, a longer annealing time can lead to an accumulation of errors. Thus, we can expect that certain values of
t_{a}
would yield optimal performance.

To study
t_{a}
dependence, we plot the rate of success for the QUBO associated with the given code of length
n=9
using various annealing times. The rate of success is defined as the proportion of runs where the optimal result was obtained out of the total number of runs conducted. As shown in Figure
3
, the rate of success initially increases as we increase the annealing time, reaching its peak at approximately
4.5\ \mu s
. However, increasing the annealing time beyond this point resulted in deteriorating performance. Considering the results from the previous section, which suggested that, at least theoretically, we are operating within the adiabatic regime, this deterioration in performance is likely due to accumulated noise. Empirically, we find that an annealing time of
t_{a}=4.5\ \mu s
is close to optimum for most code sizes, and therefore, we use this value in all of our quantum demonstrations.

This figure depicts the success rate of the QUBO-based algorithm run on D-Wave Advantage 4.1 as a function of different annealing times

Suboptimal performance of quantum annealer for relatively small code sizes
n\geq 13
can likely be attributed to limited connectivity between physical qubits of the platform. On the annealer, a quadratic interaction between two QUBO variables requires
a programmable coupling between their corresponding physical qubits.
Because the connectivity of the hardware graph is limited, not every pair of
interacting logical variables can be represented directly. Minor embedding
addresses this by representing a single logical variable by a chain of
physical qubits
[
6
]
, thereby enlarging the physical
optimization problem relative to the logical QUBO. This leads to an overhead in the number of qubits employed by the platform. Figure
4
shows that the number of necessary physical qubits increases approximately
quadratically
with the size of the original QUBO problem, associated with the code. The increase in the number of necessary qubits significantly complicates successful implementation of the QA algorithm. As the number of physical qubits grows, the corresponding search space also expands. Furthermore, since current quantum devices are noisy, each additional qubit introduces additional sources of noise that can affect the final result of the algorithm.

V.2
Hybrid implementation

To mitigate challenges of limited connectivity, we shift our focus to hybrid quantum-classical implementation. Specifically, we use Qbsolv algorithm, a hybrid algorithm developed by the D-Wave Systems. Qbsolv employs a classical algorithm to partition QUBO problems into smaller and more manageable pieces. These “subproblems” can be solved using QA, or any other suitable QUBO solving algorithm, before stitching them together
[
3
]
. This approach offers two significant benefits. First, it allows solving problems of bigger size, exceeding current limits of quantum platforms. Second, the subproblems have fewer connectivity constraints, which reduces the number of necessary physical qubits, and in this way decreases the source of errors.

To compare the hybrid QA-assisted implementation with a fully classical one,
we solved the Qbsolv subproblems using either quantum annealing on the D-Wave
platform or classical simulated annealing (SA). We refer to these as the
“QA-assisted” and “SA-assisted” implementations, respectively. At each
code length, the two implementations were assigned the same end-user time
budget and the same number of Qbsolv runs, although these quantities varied
with the code size
n
.

This is a comparison of the two implementations from the perspective of a
user of the current platforms, rather than a controlled comparison of two
solvers performing identical computational work. In particular, the binary encodings supplied to the two implementations were not always identical in size, with the QA-assisted
instances generally being larger. In addition, the QA implementation incurs
the minor-embedding overhead described above, so that its logical QUBO is
represented by a substantially larger physical Ising problem. Remote execution of QA on the D-Wave platform also introduces latency. These effects all act against the present QA implementation and should be distinguished from the
intrinsic difficulty of the underlying logical QUBO. Since the subproblems are of similar size to those considered in the previous
subsection, we use an annealing time of
t_{a}=4.5\,\mu\mathrm{s}
for the
QA-assisted demonstrations.

The two QUBO-based implementations show broadly comparable performance.
Across the 31 code lengths retained in Fig.
5
, the QA-assisted
implementation reaches the tabulated minimum distance at 18 lengths and the
SA-assisted implementation at 17. Neither implementation consistently
dominates the other, and both return progressively looser upper bounds for
some of the larger instances. Figure
5
also places these results alongside specialized
classical distance-finding methods, which we discuss in the following
subsection.

V.3
Comparison with classical minimum distance algorithms

To place the QUBO formulation in context, we performed a direct numerical
comparison with specialized classical distance-finding methods. We used
BZDistMW as a Brouwer–Zimmermann exact method and QDistRndMW as
a heuristic method, using the implementations in the
codeDistance

package (version 0.0.8)
[
42
]
. Brouwer–Zimmermann certifies the
minimum distance when allowed to terminate, whereas the heuristic methods
return upper bounds that need not in general be optimal.

For a present-day end-user comparison, we imposed on each classical method a
wall-clock cap equal to the total budget of the QUBO runs at the same code length. This gives a meaningful comparison of what the
available implementations return within the same user-visible time allowance,
but it should not be interpreted as a hardware-independent measure of
computational work. Classical processors and software are highly mature,
whereas the present QA implementation incurs additional architectural costs, most notably minor embedding of the logical QUBO into a substantially larger
physical Ising problem.

QDistRnd returned the tabulated minimum distance at all tested lengths within the imposed budget. In contrast, the QA- and SA-assisted QUBO implementations reach the
tabulated minimum distance at 18 and 17 of the 31 lengths, respectively.
Thus, on the instances studied here, the present QUBO implementations do not
provide a computational advantage over specialized classical heuristic
distance-finding methods.

The exact Brouwer–Zimmermann comparison illustrates a different tradeoff.
In the capped sweep shown in Fig.
5
, Brouwer–Zimmermann
reaches the tabulated distance at 18 of the 31 tested lengths and returns an
approximation ratio of
2
at the remaining 13. When allowed
to complete, Brouwer–Zimmermann certified the tabulated minimum distance at
all tested lengths from
n=20
through
n=40
.

VI
Discussion

In this work we introduced a reformulation of the minimum-distance problem for
binary quantum stabilizer codes as a quadratic unconstrained binary
optimization problem. The formulation uses
O(n\log n)
binary variables and
can be supplied without modification to classical, quantum, or hybrid QUBO
solvers. This solver-independent representation is the main conceptual
advantage of the approach and can benefit directly from future developments
in general-purpose QUBO optimization.

We tested the formulation using the D-Wave Advantage 4.1 quantum annealer and
the hybrid Qbsolv framework. The purely quantum implementation ceases to
reliably reach the optimum at relatively small code lengths, while the
QA-assisted and SA-assisted Qbsolv implementations perform substantially
better and are broadly comparable to one another. The direct classical
benchmark of Sec.
V.3
, however, shows that
specialized classical distance-finding methods remain more effective on the
instances considered here. In particular, QDistRnd return the
tabulated minimum distance throughout the tested data set under the same
end-user wall-clock budgets. We therefore find no evidence of a computational
advantage for the present quantum-annealing implementation.

The wall-clock comparison answers a practical present-day question: given the
currently available implementations and hardware, what result can an end user
obtain within a fixed amount of time? It should not be interpreted as a
hardware-neutral comparison of intrinsic computational work; the present work
does not attempt such a comparison. Classical processors and optimization software are highly mature, whereas
current quantum-annealing hardware is at a much earlier stage and incurs
substantial implementation overhead. In particular, limited connectivity
requires minor embedding, so the annealer operates on a physical Ising problem
that can be considerably larger than the logical QUBO. Improvements in
connectivity and hardware quality could reduce this disadvantage in future
generations.

For the current Advantage 4.1 platform, the dominant hardware limitation is
minor embedding rather than the logical variable count alone. The QUBO
formulation requires only
O(n\log n)
logical binary variables, whereas
Fig.
4
shows that the number of physical qubits
required after embedding grows approximately quadratically with the number of
QUBO variables. Combining these observed scalings gives an effective physical
qubit requirement of roughly
O(n^{2}\log^{2}n)
for the present architecture. Higher
connectivity would reduce the embedding overhead directly, while lower noise
would make the resulting physical embeddings more reliable.

Although the present implementation does not outperform specialized classical
distance-finding methods, the QUBO reformulation remains applicable to other
optimization platforms. Future work could explore improved classical QUBO
solvers, later generations of quantum annealers, or gate-based approaches such
as the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA)

[
24
]
.

To conclude, we proposed a reformulation of a theoretically and practically important NP-hard problem as a QUBO problem, contributing to an ongoing effort to reformulate various problems in a form suitable for quantum platforms. Looking ahead, an important step would be to extend our approach to other problems of classical and quantum information theory and discrete mathematics. In particular, the shortest lattice vector problem, a known NP-hard problem, which is conceptually similar to code minimum distance problem discussed in this work, would be a natural next candidate.
