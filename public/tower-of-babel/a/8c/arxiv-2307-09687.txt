Global well-posedness for a two-dimensional Navier-Stokes-Cahn-Hilliard-Boussinesq system with singular potential
Lingxi Chen

Abstract
We study a general Navier-Stokes-Cahn-Hilliard-Boussinesq system that describes the motion of a mixture of two incompressible Newtonian fluids with thermo-induced Marangoni effects. The Cahn-Hilliard dynamics of the binary mixture is governed by aggregation/diffusion competition of the free energy with a physically-relevant logarithmic potential. The coupled system is studied in a bounded smooth domain Ω ⊂ ℝ 2 \Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2} and is supplemented with a no-slip condition for the fluid velocity, homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions for the order parameter and the chemical potential, homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition for the relative temperature, and suitable initial conditions. For the corresponding initial boundary value problem, we first prove the existence of global weak solutions and their continuous dependence with respect to the initial data. Under additional assumptions on the initial data, we prove the existence and uniqueness of a global strong solution and the validity of the strict separation property.



keywords

Navier-Stokes equations; Cahn-Hilliard equation; Boussinesq equation; logarithmic potential; weak solution; strong solution, existence, continuous dependence.

Introduction

In this study, we consider the following Navier-Stokes-Cahn-Hilliard-Boussinesq system with temperature-dependent viscosity, thermal conductivity and surface tension in
\Omega\times(0,T)
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}_{t}+\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{u}-\nabla\cdot(2\nu(\theta)\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{u})+\nabla p

\displaystyle=-\nabla\cdot\sigma+(\text{Ra}\theta-\text{Ga})g\mathbf{e}_{2},

(1.1)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=0,

(1.2)

\displaystyle\phi_{t}+\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla\phi

\displaystyle=\Delta\mu,

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=-\Delta\phi+W^{\prime}(\phi),

(1.4)

\displaystyle\theta_{t}+\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla\theta-\nabla\cdot(\kappa(\theta)\nabla\theta)

\displaystyle=0.

(1.5)

Here,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a bounded domain with smooth boundary and
T>0
is the final time.
The symmetric gradient is defined by
\mathcal{D}=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla+\nabla^{t})
. This system is completed with the following initial and boundary conditions:

\displaystyle\left.\boldsymbol{u}\right|_{t=0}=\boldsymbol{u}_{0}(x),\left.\quad\phi\right|_{t=0}=\phi_{0}(x),\left.\quad\theta\right|_{t=0}=\theta_{0}(x),\quad\text{in}\ \Omega,

(1.6)

\displaystyle\left.\boldsymbol{u}\right|_{\partial\Omega}=\mathbf{0},\left.\quad\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi\right|_{\partial\Omega}=\left.\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mu\right|_{\partial\Omega}=0,\left.\quad\theta\right|_{\partial\Omega}=0,\quad\text{on}\ \partial\Omega\times(0,T),

(1.7)

where
\mathbf{n}
is the unit outward normal vector on
\partial\Omega
, and
\partial_{\mathbf{n}}
denotes the outer normal derivative on
\partial\Omega
. The unknown variables of the system are denoted by
(\boldsymbol{u},p,\phi,\mu,\theta)
. The vectorial function
\boldsymbol{u}
is the (volume) averaged velocity of the binary fluids, admitting the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations, where
p
is the pressure. The scalar function
\phi
refers to the so-called order parameter (or phase-field), together with chemical potential
\mu
consisting the Cahn-Hilliard equation. The scalar function
\theta
represents the relative temperature and satisfies the Boussinesq system.

The coupled system (
1.1
)-(
1.5
) describes the dynamics of a two-phase flow of incompressible viscous Newtonian fluids, where surface tension inhomogeneity on the interface caused by the temperature difference is considered. This phenomenon is referred to as the (thermal) Marangoni effect in the literature
[
38
,
39
]
. The system adopts the diffuse interface framework and contains a (convective) Cahn-Hilliard equation as a subsystem, where sharp interface (see e.g.,
[
35
,
7
]
) of macroscopically immiscible fluids are substituted by a capillary layer. Over this transition layer, physical quantities have steep but smooth changes, which is convenient for both mathematical and numerical analysis (see e.g.,
[
3
,
12
,
5
,
10
]
for advantages of the diffuse interface models). The model under consideration was first derived in
[
39
,
24
]
by the energetic variational approach. For a more complicated model with temperature-dependent singular potential, we refer to the recent work
[
4
]
.

In the above equations, the temperature-dependent coefficients
\nu(\theta)
and
\kappa(\theta)
denote the fluid viscosity and the thermal conductivity, respectively.
In (
1.1
),
\mathbf{e}_{2}=(0,1)^{\mathrm{T}}
is the unit vector along
y

(\text{Ra}\theta-\text{Ga})g\mathbf{e}_{2}
stands for the Rayleigh-Galileo approximation of the buoyancy force (see e.g.,
[

,
34
]

The Cauchy stress tensor
\sigma
is given by

\displaystyle\sigma=\lambda(\theta)(\nabla\phi\otimes\nabla\phi)+\lambda(\theta)\left(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\phi|^{2}+W(\phi)\right)\mathbb{I}_{2},

(1.8)

where
\mathbb{I}_{2}
is the two-dimensional unit matrix.
In (
1.8
), the function
\lambda(\theta)
denotes the temperature-dependent surface tension approximated by the
E\ddot{o}tv\ddot{o}s
rule

\lambda(\theta)=\lambda_{0}(a-b\theta),

with
a
and
b
being two constants (see
[
11
]
). Besides,

is the physically-relevant logarithmic potential
[
8
]
(also known as the Flory-Huggins potential) such that

\displaystyle W(\phi)=\frac{A}{2}\big[(1+\phi)\ln(1+\phi)+(1-\phi)\ln(1-\phi)\big]-\frac{B}{2}\phi^{2},\quad\phi\in(-1,1),

(1.9)

where
A
and
B
are constants satisfying
0<A<B
. It’s easy to check that
W^{\prime\prime}(\phi)\geqslant-\alpha
, for some
\alpha>0
(see e.g.
[
23
]
). In this paper, we denote the convex part by

\displaystyle F(\phi):=\dfrac{A}{2}\big[(1+\phi)\ln(1+\phi)+(1-\phi)\ln(1-\phi)\big].

(1.10)

In the literature, there is little investigation for the well-posedness of the initial boundary value problem (
1.1
)-(
1.7
). The inviscid case with a regular potential has been analyzed
in
[
45
,
44
,
46
]
in a two-dimensional bounded domain.
In
[
45
]
, the author proved the global existence of classical solutions with smooth initial data. Moreover, in
[
44
,
46
]
, he considered the long time behavior with the same assumption for the initial data, and proved the exponential convergence rate to the equilibrium. The former considered the case where the mobility is constant, and the latter extended this to the case where the mobility depends on the order parameter. In a recent work
[
23
]
, the authors considered a similar system with a singular potential, constant surface tension and thermal conductivity. They proved the existence of strong solutions by a standard Faedo-Galerkin method, which was based on a suitable regularization of the singular potential.
In
[
43
,
42
]
, the authors considered an alternative system, where the Cahn-Hilliard equation with a singular potential was replaced by the Allen-Cahn equation with a regular potential. In this manner, possible difficulties related to the singular potential can be avoided, while the phase function still satisfies the maximum principle. In
[
43
]
, the authors considered the case where the thermal conductivity
\kappa
is a constant. In
[
42
]
, the results were extended to the case with temperature-dependent thermal conductivity. With suitable initial and boundary conditions, both works proved the existence of global weak and strong solutions in two dimensions with a suitably small initial temperature. The bound of the temperature ensures the system to be a dissipative one. Under the same assumptions on the initial temperature, the former proved that every global weak solution converges to a single equilibrium as time goes to infinity. In the situation with a smaller initial temperature, the latter derived uniform-in-time estimates for weak and strong solutions in corresponding spaces.

In this study, we consider a general diffuse interface model for incompressible two-phase flows with thermal Marangoni effects, where the viscosity, surface tension and thermal conductivity are all temperature-dependent.
Our aim is to establish well-posedness for the initial boundary value problem (
1.1
)-(
1.7
) in a two-dimensional setting, without any smallness assumption on the initial temperature.
The results are listed as follows:

(1)

(Theorem
1
) existence of a global weak solution and uniform-in-time estimates;

(2)

(Theorem
2
) continuous dependence with respect to the initial data and uniqueness of the weak solution;

(3)

(Theorem
3
) existence and uniqueness of the global strong solution and the validity of strict separation property for the phase function
\phi
.

To achieve our goal, several difficulties due to the highly nonlinear structure of the system and the singular potential have to be overcome.
For instance, we shall take advantage of a novel interpolation inequality (
2.25
). Some techniques for handling the singular potential originate from
[
22
,
30
]
. Besides, for the continuous dependence estimate, we apply a useful estimate inspired by
[
14
]
.

It is worth mentioning that we are able to prove the existence of global weak and strong solutions without assuming that the initial temperature should be properly small. The proof relies on a new attempt of the so-called semi-Galerkin scheme, in which only part of the unknown variables are approximated (see e.g.,
[
27
,
42
,
43
,
25
,
26
,
18
]
).
Comparing with the previous results in
[
43
,
42
]
, where the Allen-Cahn equation was considered, we observe that the Cahn-Hilliard equation can improve the dissipation of the system. More precisely, the fourth order Cahn-Hilliard equation provides us a higher order dissipative term involving
\|\nabla\mu\|_{L^{2}}
(see (
3.44
)), while the second order Allen-Cahn equation only presents a lower order dissipation term involving
\|\mu\|_{L^{2}}
(see
[
43
,
42
]
). The latter is not enough to control the change of energy when the temperature
\theta
is large. Next, with the novel interpolation inequality (
2.25
), we can derive uniform-in-time estimates for the solutions and remove the previous requirement on the smallness of the initial temperature as in
[
42
]
. Uniqueness of the weak solution is in virtue of a recent
L^{4}
-estimate for the pressure in the two-dimensional Stokes problem (see
[
14
]
). Taking advantage of this, we can handle the most difficult term
I_{8}
in the subsequent proof (see Section
3
). We also note that although the Cahn-Hilliard is a fourth order parabolic equation, which in general does not enjoy a maximum principle, the singular potential still yields an
L^{\infty}
-estimate for
\phi
. This together with the maximum principle for
\theta
(see (
3.43
)) plays a crucial role to control highly nonlinear terms like
\lambda(\theta)(\nabla\phi\otimes\nabla\phi)
.

Plan of the paper.
In Section

Lemma 2.1

(Gagliardo-Nirenberg Inequality)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded domain with smooth boundary. Let

,

satisfy
0\leqslant j<m
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}-\frac{j}{n}=a\left(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{m}{n}\right)+(1-a)\frac{1}{q}.

Then there are two positive constants

, the following inequality holds:

\displaystyle\|D^{j}u\|_{L^{p}}\leqslant C_{1}\|D^{m}u\|_{L^{r}}^{a}\|u\|_{L^{q}}^{1-a}+C_{2}\|u\|_{L^{q}}.

(2.24)

In particular, for any

(

-estimates for

.

Let

\displaystyle\|\phi\|_{H^{2}}^{2}\leqslant C\|\phi\|^{2}+C(\nabla\mu,\nabla\phi)\leqslant C\|\phi\|^{2}+C\|\nabla\mu\|\|\nabla\phi\|,

(2.26)

\displaystyle=C(\|\boldsymbol{u}\|^{2}+\|\nabla\phi\|^{2}+\|\nabla\theta\|^{2}),

Since

\int_{t}^{t+1}\beta(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{t}^{t+1}\mathcal{G}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leqslant C,\quad\forall\,t\geqslant 0,

the classical Gronwall’s lemma together with the uniform Gronwall’s lemma implies that
\beta(t)\leqslant C
for all
t\geqslant 0
. Recalling (
4.112
), we have

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{u}(t)\|_{\boldsymbol{V}_{\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sigma}}^{2}+\|\mu(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+\|\theta_{t}(t)\|^{2}\leqslant C,\quad\forall\,t\geqslant 0.

(4.124)

Here and hereafter, positive constants
C
not only depend on
\Omega
, parameters of the system and
C_{1}
(see (
4.91
)), but also depend on norms of the initial data.

Concerning
\Gamma(t)
, integrating (
4.123
) from
t
to
t+1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{t\geqslant 0}\int_{t}^{t+1}\|\nabla\theta_{t}\|^{2}+\|\nabla\phi_{t}\|^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{u}_{t}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leqslant C.

(4.125)

The estimate
\sup\limits_{t\geqslant 0}\int_{t}^{t+1}\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{\boldsymbol{H}^{2}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leqslant C
then
follows from (
4.108
). Further regularity of
\phi
and
\mu
can be deduced like in
[
26
]
. According to Remark
C.2
, since
\mu\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty;H^{1})
, there exists some small
\delta>0
, such that

\|\phi(t)\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\leqslant 1-\delta,\quad\forall\,t\geqslant 0.

As a consequence, the singular potential
W^{\prime}(\phi)
becomes a smooth function. Applying standard elliptic estimates to (
3.39
), we see that
\phi\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty;H^{3})
. Subsequently, taking the gradient to (
3.38
), we can prove

\sup\limits_{t\geqslant 0}\int_{t}^{t+1}\|\mu\|_{H^{3}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leqslant C
. Applying Laplacian to (
3.39
), we further get

\sup\limits_{t\geqslant 0}\int_{t}^{t+1}\|\phi\|_{H^{4}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leqslant C
. Next, differentiating (
3.39
) by
t
, by comparison, we can deduce that
\sup\limits_{t\geqslant 0}\int_{t}^{t+1}\|\mu_{t}\|_{(H^{1})^{\prime}}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leqslant C
.
Concerning the estimate for
\theta
, we follow the strategy in
[
42
]
. Taking the transform
\Theta(x,t)=\int_{0}^{\theta(x,t)}\kappa(s)\,\mathrm{d}s
, and estimating
\|\Delta\Theta\|
by using
\|\Theta_{t}\|
, we have
\Theta\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty;H^{2})
. Therefore,
\theta\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty;H^{2})
. To estimate
\|\theta\|_{H^{3}}
, we can apply gradient to the equation (
3.40
), and estimate term by term as in the
[
42
]
. In this manner, we obtain

\sup\limits_{t\geqslant 0}\int_{t}^{t+1}\|\theta\|_{H^{3}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leqslant C
.

Collecting the above estimates, we can prove the existence of a global strong solution by the standard compactness argument. Uniqueness again follows from the continuous estimate (
2.35
).

The proof of Theorem
3
is complete.

Appendix A
Semi-Galerkin Scheme for Weak Solutions

The semi-Galerkin scheme (
3.37
)-(
3.42
) can be solved by a fixed point argument. In this scheme, the unknown variables
\phi
and
\theta
are not approximated, and hence we cannot directly use the ODE theory to derive the existence of approximate solutions. To this end, we first fix some function
\boldsymbol{v}^{m}\in C([0,T];\mathbf{H}_{m})
, and consider the subsystem for
(\phi^{m},\mu^{m},\theta^{m})
. When a solution
(\phi^{m},\mu^{m},\theta^{m})
is obtained, we solve the subsystem for
\boldsymbol{u}^{m}
and thus construct a mapping from
\boldsymbol{v}^{m}
to
\boldsymbol{u}^{m}
. Under suitable assumptions, we will show that the fixed point argument can be applied.

A.1
Solve
(\phi^{m},\mu^{m},\theta^{m})
with a given velocity
\boldsymbol{v}^{m}
.

Let
T>0
. We
fix the velocity
\boldsymbol{v}^{m}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}g_{i}^{m}(t)\boldsymbol{w}_{i}(x)

\in C([0,T];\mathbf{H}_{m})
.
Then we consider the subsystem with convection:

\displaystyle\phi_{t}^{m}+\boldsymbol{v}^{m}\cdot\nabla\phi^{m}=\Delta\mu^{m},\quad\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),

(1.126)

\displaystyle\mu^{m}=-\Delta\phi^{m}+W^{\prime}(\phi^{m}),\quad\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),

(1.127)

\displaystyle\theta^{m}_{t}+\boldsymbol{v}^{m}\cdot\nabla\theta^{m}-\nabla\cdot(\kappa(\theta^{m})\nabla\theta^{m})=0,\quad\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),

(1.128)

\displaystyle\left.\phi^{m}\right|_{t=0}=\phi_{0},\quad\left.\theta^{m}\right|_{t=0}=\theta_{0},\quad\text{in}\ \Omega,

(1.129)

\displaystyle\left.\theta^{m}\right|_{\partial\Omega}=0,\left.\quad\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi^{m}\right|_{\partial\Omega}=\left.\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mu^{m}\right|_{\partial\Omega}=0,\quad\text{on}\ \partial\Omega\times(0,T).

(1.130)

Since the equations for
(\phi^{m},\mu^{m})
and
\theta^{m}
are decoupled in the above system, we can handle them separately.

Solvability of
\theta^{m}
.
Consider the system for
\theta^{m}
:

\begin{cases}\theta_{t}^{m}+\boldsymbol{v}^{m}\cdot\nabla\theta^{m}=\nabla\cdot\left(\kappa\left(\theta^{m}\right)\nabla\theta^{m}\right),\quad\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\theta^{m}=0,\quad\text{ on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\left.\theta^{m}\right|_{t=0}=\theta_{0}(x),\quad\text{ in }\Omega.\end{cases}

(1.131)

We have

Lemma A.1
.

Assume that
\boldsymbol{v}^{m}\in C\left([0,T];\mathbf{H}_{m}\right)
,
\theta_{0}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Problem (
1.131
) admits a unique solution
\theta^{m}\in L^{\infty}\left(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)\right)\cap L^{2}\left(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega)\right)
, which satisfies

\displaystyle\left\|\theta^{m}(t)\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leqslant\left\|\theta_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}},\quad\forall\,t\in[0,T],

(1.132)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\|\theta^{m}(t)\right\|^{2}+2\underline{\kappa}\int_{0}^{T}\left\|\nabla\theta^{m}(t)\right\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\leqslant\left\|\theta_{0}\right\|^{2},

(1.133)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\|\theta^{m}(t)\right\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\left\|\theta^{m}(t)\right\|_{H^{2}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\leqslant C.

(1.134)

Furthermore, we have the continuous dependence estimate:

\left\|\theta_{1}^{m}(t)-\theta_{2}^{m}(t)\right\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla(\theta_{1}^{m}-\theta_{2}^{m})(\tau)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leqslant C\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{m}_{1}(\tau)-\boldsymbol{v}^{m}_{2}(\tau)\right\|_{\boldsymbol{V}_{\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sigma}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

(1.135)

for all
t\in[0,T]
, where
\theta_{1}^{m}
,
\theta_{2}^{m}
are two solutions to problem (
1.131
) corresponding to velocities
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}^{m}
,
\boldsymbol{v}_{2}^{m}
.

Since
\boldsymbol{v}^{m}
is sufficiently regular, the existence of a global weak solution
\theta^{m}
satisfying the maximum principle follows from that in
[
29
]
. Besides, we refer to
[
42
]
for the continuous dependence estimate (
1.135
) and to
[
40
]
for the higher order estimate (
1.134
) (via the transform
\Theta^{m}(x,t)=\int_{0}^{\theta^{m}(x,t)}\kappa(s)\,\mathrm{d}s
).

Solvability of
(\phi^{m},\mu^{m})
.

Consider the system for
(\phi^{m},\mu^{m})
:

\begin{cases}\phi_{t}^{m}+\boldsymbol{v}^{m}\cdot\nabla\phi^{m}=\Delta\mu^{m},\quad\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\mu^{m}=-\Delta\phi^{m}+W^{\prime}(\phi^{m}),\quad\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\left.\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi^{m}\right|_{\partial\Omega}=\left.\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mu^{m}\right|_{\partial\Omega}=0,\quad\text{on}\ \partial\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\left.\phi^{m}\right|_{t=0}=\phi_{0}.\end{cases}

(1.136)

We have

Lemma A.2
.

Assume that
\boldsymbol{v}^{m}\in C\left([0,T];\mathbf{H}_{m}\right)
and
\phi_{0}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
with
\left\|\phi_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leqslant 1
,

\left|\overline{\phi_{0}}\right|<1
. Then problem (
1.136
) admits a unique weak solution

\phi^{m}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))\cap L^{4}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;W^{2,p}(\Omega))\cap H^{1}(0,T;(H^{1}(\Omega))^{\prime}),

for any
p\geqslant 2
,
\mu^{m}\in{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}
, with

\phi^{m}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega\times(0,T))
,
|\phi^{m}|<1
almost everywhere in
\Omega\times(0,T)
and
\sup\limits_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\|\phi^{m}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leqslant 1
. Moreover, we have the continuous dependence estimate:

\left\|\phi_{1}^{m}(t)-\phi_{2}^{m}(t)\right\|_{V_{0}^{\prime}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla(\phi_{1}^{m}-\phi_{2}^{m})(\tau)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leqslant C\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{m}_{1}(\tau)-\boldsymbol{v}^{m}_{2}(\tau)\right\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

(1.137)

for all
t\in[0,T]
, where
\phi_{1}^{m}
,
\phi_{2}^{m}
are two solutions to problem (
1.136
) corresponding to velocities
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}^{m}
,
\boldsymbol{v}_{2}^{m}
.

Since
\boldsymbol{v}^{m}
is sufficiently regular, we refer to
[

, Theorem 6]
for the existence and uniqueness of weak solution, see also
[
21
]
for the regularity

. The continuous dependence estimate follows from the argument in
[
25
]
, in which a more general system was studied.

A.2
Solvability of
\boldsymbol{u}^{m}
with given
(\phi^{m},\mu^{m},\theta^{m})

Thanks to Lemmas
A.1
,
A.2
, we have
\theta^{m}\in L^{\infty}\left(0,T;H_{0}^{1}\cap L^{\infty}\right)\cap L^{2}\left(0,T;H^{2}\right)
,
\phi^{m}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1})\cap L^{4}(0,T;H^{2})\cap H^{1}(0,T;(H^{1})^{\prime})
, such that
\sup\limits_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\left\|\theta^{m}(t)\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leqslant\left\|\theta_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}
and

\sup\limits_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\|\phi^{m}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leqslant 1
. Given the pair
(\phi^{m},\theta^{m})
, we consider the system for
\boldsymbol{u}^{m}
:

\begin{cases}(\boldsymbol{u}_{t}^{m},\boldsymbol{w}^{m})+(\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{m},\boldsymbol{w}^{m})+(2\nu(\theta^{m})\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{u}^{m},\nabla\boldsymbol{w}^{m})\\

\left.\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\right|_{t=0}=\Pi_{m}\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\end{cases}

(1.138)

for any
\boldsymbol{w}^{m}\in\mathbf{H}_{m}
.
The system (
1.138
) is equivalent to a Cauchy problem of ordinary differential equations. Hence, for any
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\in\boldsymbol{L}^{2}_{\sigma}(\Omega)
, the classical Cauchy-Lipschitz Theorem guarantees the existence and uniqueness of a local solution
\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\in H^{1}\left(0,\mathcal{T}_{m};\mathbf{H}_{m}\right)
.
Using the fact that
\boldsymbol{u}^{m}(\cdot,t)
lies in a finite-dimensional space, we can apply the argument in
[
42
]
to obtain the following estimate:

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{t\in[0,\mathcal{T}_{m}]}\|\boldsymbol{u}^{m}(t)\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{\mathcal{T}_{m}}\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{m}(t)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant C\left(\|\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{\mathcal{T}_{m}}\|\nabla\phi^{m}(t)\|^{4}\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{0}^{\mathcal{T}_{m}}\|\theta^{m}(t)\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\,\tau\right),

(1.139)

which enables us to extend the solution
\boldsymbol{u}^{m}
to the interval
[0,T]
.

For completeness, we show the continuous dependence of
\boldsymbol{u}^{m}
with respect to
(\phi^{m},\theta^{m})
. Let
\boldsymbol{u}^{m}_{1}
,
\boldsymbol{u}^{m}_{2}
be two solutions with the same initial data corresponding to
(\phi^{m}_{1},\theta^{m}_{1})
and
(\phi^{m}_{2},\theta^{m}_{2})
, respectively.
Let

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}^{m}=\boldsymbol{u}^{m}_{1}-\boldsymbol{u}^{m}_{2},\quad\phi^{m}=\phi^{m}_{1}-\phi^{m}_{2},\quad\theta^{m}=\theta^{m}_{1}-\theta^{m}_{2}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\left(\boldsymbol{u}_{t}^{m},\boldsymbol{w}^{m}\right)+\int_{\Omega}\left(\boldsymbol{u}_{1}^{m}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{m}+\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{2}^{m}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{w}^{m}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}2\nu\left(\theta_{1}^{m}\right)\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{u}^{m}:\nabla\boldsymbol{w}^{m}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}2\left(\nu\left(\theta_{1}^{m}\right)-\nu\left(\theta_{2}^{m}\right)\right)\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{u}_{2}^{m}:\nabla\boldsymbol{w}^{m}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(\lambda\left(\theta_{1}^{m}\right)\nabla\phi_{1}^{m}\otimes\nabla\phi_{1}^{m}-\lambda\left(\theta_{2}^{m}\right)\nabla\phi_{2}^{m}\otimes\nabla\phi_{2}^{m}\right):\nabla\boldsymbol{w}^{m}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\theta^{m}\mathbf{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{w}^{m}\,\mathrm{d}x,

(1.140)

for any
\boldsymbol{w}^{m}\in\mathbf{H}_{m}
.
In view of the regularity of
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}^{m}
, we can take the test function
\boldsymbol{w}^{m}=\boldsymbol{u}^{m}
. Thanks to the Korn’s inequality (
2.22
), we deduce that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\|^{2}+\int_{\Omega}\nu(\theta_{1}^{m})|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{m}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant-\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{2}^{m})\cdot\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}2(\nu(\theta_{1}^{m})-\nu(\theta_{2}^{m}))\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{u}_{2}^{m}:\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{\Omega}(\lambda(\theta_{1}^{m})\nabla\phi_{1}^{m}\otimes\nabla\phi_{1}^{m}-\lambda(\theta_{2}^{m})\nabla\phi_{2}^{m}\otimes\nabla\phi_{2}^{m}):\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\theta^{m}\mathbf{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Thanks to the finite-dimensional property of
\mathbf{H}_{m}
, we have for
i=1,2
,

\|\boldsymbol{u}^{m}_{i}\|_{\boldsymbol{L}^{\infty}}\leq C\|\boldsymbol{u}^{m}_{i}\|,\quad\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{m}_{i}\|_{\boldsymbol{L}^{\infty}}\leq C\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{m}_{i}\|\leq C\|\boldsymbol{u}^{m}_{i}\|,

where the positive constant
C
depends on
m
. Similar results also hold for
\boldsymbol{u}^{m}
,
(\boldsymbol{u}_{i}^{m})_{t}
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{t}^{m}
. Then we can deduce that

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{2}^{m})\cdot\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leqslant\|\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\|\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{2}^{m}\|_{\boldsymbol{L}^{\infty}}\|\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\|\leqslant C\|\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\|^{2},

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}2(\nu(\theta_{1}^{m})-\nu(\theta_{2}^{m}))\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{u}_{2}^{m}:\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leqslant C\|\theta^{m}\|\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{2}^{m}\|_{\boldsymbol{L}^{\infty}}\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\|

\displaystyle\leqslant\varepsilon\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\|^{2}+C\|\theta^{m}\|^{2},

and

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\theta^{m}\mathbf{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\mathrm{d}x\leqslant C\|\theta^{m}\|^{2}+C\|\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\|^{2}.

Next, by the Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality and Lemmas
A.1
,
A.2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\lambda(\theta_{1}^{m})\nabla\phi_{1}^{m}\otimes\nabla\phi_{1}^{m}-\lambda(\theta_{2}^{m})\nabla\phi_{2}^{m}\otimes\nabla\phi_{2}^{m}):\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{\Omega}\big[(\lambda(\theta_{1}^{m})-\lambda(\theta_{2}^{m}))\nabla\phi_{2}^{m}\otimes\nabla\phi_{2}^{m}+\lambda(\theta_{1}^{m})(\nabla\phi^{m}\otimes\nabla\phi_{2}^{m}+\nabla\phi_{1}^{m}\otimes\nabla\phi_{1}^{m})\big]:\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant C\|\theta^{m}\|\|\nabla\phi_{2}^{m}\|_{L^{4}}^{2}\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\|_{\boldsymbol{L}^{\infty}}+C(\|\nabla\phi_{1}^{m}\|+\|\nabla\phi_{2}^{m}\|)\|\nabla\phi^{m}\|\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\|_{\boldsymbol{L}^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant C\|\theta^{m}\|^{2}+C\|\phi_{2}^{m}\|_{H^{2}}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\|^{2}+C\|\nabla\phi^{m}\|^{2}+C\|\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\|^{2}.

Combining the above estimates and taking
\varepsilon
sufficiently small, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\|^{2}+\frac{\underline{\nu}}{2}\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\|^{2}\leqslant C(1+\|\phi_{2}^{m}\|_{H^{2}}^{2})\|\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\|^{2}+C(\|\nabla\phi^{m}\|^{2}+\|\theta^{m}\|^{2}),

(1.141)

which together with Gronwall’s lemma implies

\left\|\boldsymbol{u}^{m}(t)\right\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{m}(\tau)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leqslant C\left(\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla\phi^{m}(\tau)\|^{2}+\|\theta^{m}(\tau)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right)e^{Ct},

(1.142)

for all
t\in[0,T]
.
Next, taking
\boldsymbol{w}^{m}=\boldsymbol{u}_{t}^{m}
in (
1.140
), we get

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{u}_{t}^{m}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\left(\boldsymbol{u}_{1}^{m}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{m}+\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{2}^{m}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{u}_{t}^{m}\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}2\nu\left(\theta_{1}^{m}\right)\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{u}^{m}:\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{t}^{m}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\Omega}2\left(\nu\left(\theta_{1}^{m}\right)-\nu\left(\theta_{2}^{m}\right)\right)\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{u}_{2}^{m}:\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{t}^{m}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\theta^{m}\mathbf{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}_{t}^{m}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Omega}\left(\lambda\left(\theta_{1}^{m}\right)\nabla\phi_{1}^{m}\otimes\nabla\phi_{1}^{m}-\lambda\left(\theta_{2}^{m}\right)\nabla\phi_{2}^{m}\otimes\nabla\phi_{2}^{m}\right):\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{t}^{m}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leqslant\|\boldsymbol{u}_{1}^{m}\|_{\boldsymbol{L}^{\infty}}\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\|\|\boldsymbol{u}_{t}^{m}\|+\|\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\|\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{2}^{m}\|_{\boldsymbol{L}^{\infty}}\|\boldsymbol{u}_{t}^{m}\|

\displaystyle\quad+C\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\|\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{t}^{m}\|+C\|\theta^{m}\|\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{2}^{m}\|_{\boldsymbol{L}^{\infty}}\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{t}^{m}\|+\|\theta^{m}\|\|\boldsymbol{u}_{t}^{m}\|

\displaystyle\quad+C\|\theta^{m}\|\|\nabla\phi_{2}^{m}\|_{L^{4}}^{2}\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{t}^{m}\|_{\boldsymbol{L}^{\infty}}+C(\|\nabla\phi_{1}^{m}\|+\|\nabla\phi_{2}^{m}\|)\|\nabla\phi^{m}\|\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{t}^{m}\|_{\boldsymbol{L}^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{u}_{t}^{m}\|^{2}+C\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\|^{2}+C\|\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\|^{2}+C(1+\|\phi_{2}^{m}\|_{H^{2}}^{2})\|\theta^{m}\|^{2}+C\|\nabla\phi^{m}\|^{2}.

Integrating over time, we deduce from (
1.142
) that

\int_{0}^{t}\|\boldsymbol{u}_{t}^{m}(\tau)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leqslant C\left(\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla\phi^{m}(\tau)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\sup\limits_{\tau\in[0,t]}\|\theta^{m}(\tau)\|^{2}\right),\quad\forall\,t\in[0,T].

(1.143)

A.3
The fixed point argument

Combining the continuous dependence estimates (
1.135
), (
1.137
), (
1.142
), (
1.143
), we have

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{t\in[0,T]}\|\boldsymbol{u}_{1}^{m}(t)-\boldsymbol{u}_{2}^{m}(t)\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\|\nabla(\boldsymbol{u}_{1}^{m}(t)-\boldsymbol{u}_{2}^{m}(t))\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\|\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}_{1}^{m}(t)-\boldsymbol{u}_{2}^{m}(t))\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant C_{T}\int_{0}^{T}\left\|\nabla(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}^{m}(t)-\boldsymbol{v}_{2}^{m}(t))\right\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t.

(1.144)

Let

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{X}=

\displaystyle L^{\infty}\left(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)\right)\cap L^{2}\left(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega)\right)

\displaystyle\times L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1})\cap L^{4}(0,T;H^{2})\cap H^{1}(0,T;(H^{1})^{\prime}).

We infer from (
1.144
) that the mapping

\begin{array}[]{rlccc}&&&&\\
\Phi_{T}^{m}:\ C\left([0,T];\mathbf{H}_{m}\right)&\rightarrow&\textbf{X}&\rightarrow&H^{1}\left(0,T;\mathbf{H}_{m}\right)\\
\boldsymbol{v}^{m}&\mapsto&\left(\theta^{m},\phi^{m}\right)&\mapsto&\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\end{array}

is continuous from
C\left([0,T];\mathbf{H}_{m}\right)
to
H^{1}\left(0,T;\mathbf{H}_{m}\right)
and thus compact from
C\left([0,T];\mathbf{H}_{m}\right)
into itself.
Let
\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{m}\right\|^{2}_{C([0,T];\mathbf{H}_{m})}=\sup\limits_{t\in[0,T]}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\left|g_{i}^{m}(t)\right|^{2}\leqslant M
.
Taking
M>0
sufficiently large and
T_{m}
sufficiently small, in view of (
1.139
), we have

\left\|\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\right\|^{2}_{C\left([0,T_{m}];\mathbf{H}_{m}\right)}\leqslant M
.
Set

\boldsymbol{Y}_{M}=\big\{\boldsymbol{u}\in C\left([0,T_{m}];\mathbf{H}_{m}\right)\ :\ \left\|\boldsymbol{u}\right\|^{2}_{C([0,T_{m}];\mathbf{H}_{m})}\leqslant M\big\},

which is a bounded closed convex subset of
C\left([0,T_{m}];\mathbf{H}_{m}\right)
.
Therefore, the restriction of
\Phi_{T}^{m}
on
\boldsymbol{Y}_{M}
is compact and maps
\boldsymbol{Y}_{M}
into itself. Hence, we can apply Schauder’s fixed point theorem to obtain an approximate solution
(\boldsymbol{u}^{m},\phi^{m},\mu^{m},\theta^{m})
of the semi-Galerkin scheme (
3.37
)-(
3.42
) on
[0,T_{m}]
with mentioned regularity.

The proof of Theorem
4
is complete.

Appendix B
Semi-Galerkin Scheme for Strong Solutions

The semi-Galerkin scheme (
3.37
)-(
3.42
) is also valid for strong solutions (see
[
26
,
18
]
for a similar strategy). Compared with the regularity for

Appendix C
An Elliptic Problem with Singular Nonlinearity

We consider the following Neumann problem

\begin{cases}-\Delta\phi+F^{\prime}(\phi)=\widetilde{\mu},\quad\text{in}\ \Omega,\\
\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi=0,\quad\text{on}\ \partial\Omega.\end{cases}

(3.145)

Here,
\widetilde{\mu}=\mu+B\phi
(see (
1.9
)).
Then we have the following results (see e.g.,
[
22
,
20
,
26
,
31
]
):

Lemma C.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded domain with smooth boundary. Assume that
\mu\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, then problem (
3.145
) admits a unique solution
\phi\in H^{2}(\Omega)
such that
F^{\prime}(\phi)\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. Moreover,

(i) It holds
\tilde{\mu}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and

\displaystyle\|\phi\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\|F^{\prime}(\phi)\right\|\leqslant C(1+\|\widetilde{\mu}\|).

(3.146)

(ii) If
\mu\in L^{p}(\Omega)
, where
2\leqslant p\leqslant\infty
, then
\widetilde{\mu}\in L^{p}(\Omega)
, and we have

\displaystyle\left\|F^{\prime}(\phi)\right\|_{L^{p}}\leqslant\|\widetilde{\mu}\|_{L^{p}}.

(3.147)

(iii) If
\mu\in H^{1}(\Omega)
, then
\widetilde{\mu}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
, and we have

\displaystyle\|\Delta\phi\|\leqslant\|\nabla\phi\|^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla\widetilde{\mu}\|^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(3.148)

(iv) Assume
\mu\in H^{1}(\Omega)
. For any
p\in[2,\infty)
, there exists a positive constant
C
such that

\displaystyle\|\phi\|_{W^{2,p}}+\left\|F^{\prime}(\phi)\right\|_{L^{p}}\leqslant C\left(1+\|\widetilde{\mu}\|_{H^{1}}\right),

(3.149)

\displaystyle\left\|F^{\prime\prime}(\phi)\right\|_{L^{p}}\leqslant C\left(1+e^{C\|\widetilde{\mu}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}}\right).

(3.150)

Remark C.2
.

Lemma
C.1
provides critical insights into the so-called strict separation property of
\phi
. Namely, the supreme of
\phi
is controlled by the supreme of
F^{\prime}(\phi)
, while the latter can be controlled by the
H^{1}
-norm of the chemical potential
\mu
. Suppose
\mu\in H^{1}(\Omega)
, it follows from (
3.149
) and (
3.150
) that
F^{\prime}(\phi)\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)
for arbitrary
p\geq 2
in two dimensions (see e.g.,
[
26
, Lemma 3.2]
). This combined with the Sobolev embedding theorem entails
F^{\prime}(\phi)\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, and thus
\phi
is strictly separated from the pure states
\pm 1
due the singular nature of
F
. For further discussions on the separation property of the phase variable
\phi
satisfying the Cahn-Hilliard equation, we refer to
[
13
,
20
,
26
,
31
]
and the references therein.

Appendix D
Proof of Lemma
2.2

Below we prove the interpolation inequality (
2.25
). This relies on an embedding from the Hölder spaces into the Sobolev spaces with fractional order
[
19
]
.

Proof D.1
.

(
Proof of Lemma
2.2
.
)
Let
u\in H^{2}(\Omega)\cap C^{\gamma}(\overline{\Omega})
,
\gamma\in(0,1)
. First, applying the Gagliardo-Nirenberg interpolation inequality for the fractional-order Sobolev spaces (see e.g.,
[
6
, Theorem 1]
), we have

\displaystyle\|u\|_{W^{1,4}}\leqslant C\|u\|_{W^{s,p}}^{\xi}\|u\|_{H^{2}}^{1-\xi},

where

1=s\xi+2(1-\xi),\qquad\frac{1}{4}=\frac{\xi}{p}+\frac{1-\xi}{2},\qquad\xi\in(0,1).

Let us require
s=2-\frac{1}{\xi}\in(0,\gamma)\subset(0,1)
. Then it follows that

\xi\in\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2-\gamma}\right)\subset\left(\frac{1}{2},1\right),\qquad p=\frac{4\xi}{2\xi-1}>\frac{4}{\gamma}.

Next, we show that
C^{\gamma}(\overline{\Omega})\hookrightarrow W^{s,p}(\Omega)
when
s\in(0,\gamma)
. To this end, we only need to estimate the Gagliardo seminorm (see e.g.,
[
6
,
32
]
). Indeed, the condition
s-\gamma<0
implies
n+(s-\gamma)p<n
so that

\displaystyle[u]_{W^{s,p}}^{p}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{p}}{|x-y|^{n+sp}}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\|u\|_{C^{\gamma}(\overline{\Omega})}^{p}|x-y|^{\gamma p}}{|x-y|^{n+sp}}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leqslant\|u\|_{C^{\gamma}(\overline{\Omega})}^{p}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{n+(s-\gamma)p}}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leqslant C\|u\|_{C^{\gamma}(\overline{\Omega})}^{p}.

The proof is complete.
