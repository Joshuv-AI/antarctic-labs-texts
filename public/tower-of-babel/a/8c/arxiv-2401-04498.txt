Efficient designs for multivariate crossover trials
Shubham Niphadkar, Siuli Mukhopadhyay

Abstract
This article aims to study efficient/trace optimal designs for crossover trials with multiple responses recorded from each subject in the time periods. A multivariate fixed effects model is proposed with direct and carryover effects corresponding to the multiple responses. The corresponding error dispersion matrix is chosen to be either of the proportional or the generalized Markov covariance type, permitting the existence of direct and cross-correlations within and between the multiple responses. The corresponding information matrices for direct effects under the two types of dispersions are used to determine efficient designs. The efficiency of orthogonal array designs of Type I I and strength 2 2 is investigated for a wide choice of covariance functions, namely, Mat( 0.5 0.5 ), Mat( 1.5 1.5 ) and Mat( ∞ \infty ). To motivate these multivariate crossover designs, a gene expression dataset in a 3 × 3 3\times 3 framework is utilized.



Keywords—
Completely symmetric; Correlated response; Markov-type covariance; Orthogonal arrays; Proportional covariance; Trace optimal.

1
Introduction

In biomedical and clinical studies, we often come across crossover or repeated measure setups with
g
responses, where
g>1
. For instance,
Grender and Johnson, 1993
studies the effect of caffeine on the mathematical ability of boys aged 14 and 16 years in a crossover framework. In this study, two responses diastolic and systolic blood pressures, of each boy are recorded from each of the two periods in a
2\times 2
crossover design setup. More recently,
Leaker et al., 2017
considered a randomized, placebo controlled, double-blind
3\times 3
crossover trial, where in each time period gene expression values corresponding to multiple genes were recorded for each subject, resulting in a multiple response crossover trial. Due to more than one response being measured from the same subject at the same and different time periods, there is a necessity to account for both between and within response correlations in the covariance structure. Using the gene experiment study of
Leaker et al., 2017
, we motivate a multivariate response model for a
p,t\geq 3
crossover setup with correlation within and between responses. Our main objective is to find
p
-period and
t
-treatment trace optimal/efficient crossover designs for
g
correlated responses. Though these correlated response crossover trials are quite popular in the statistical literature
(
Grender and Johnson, 1993
;
Chinchilli and Esinhart, 1996
;
Tudor et al., 2000
;
Pareek et al., 2023
, see)
, mostly the topics of estimation and testing of hypothesis for such trials have been studied without any attention being paid to the actual designing of such studies. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work in optimal designs for correlated response crossover experiments.

The key difficulty in finding optimal designs for such crossover models is specifying the direct and cross-covariance for the multiple responses. In the direct covariance, correlations arising from within responses measured in different time periods from each subject are acknowledged. This closely resembles the covariance pattern we see in usual longitudinal/repeated measures studies. To consider the relations between different responses measured in the same and different time periods we use cross-correlations. Two popular covariance structures from the statistical literature, resembling the proportional covariance and the generalized Markovian-type covariance
(
Journel, 1999
;
Chiles and Delfiner, 2012
;
Dasgupta et al., 2022
, see)
, are utilized to model the correlations within (direct covariance) and between (cross-covariance) responses. Under the proportional structure, the between response covariance is assumed to be proportional to the within response covariance,
\bm{V}
, where
\bm{V}
is positive definite. The Markov-type covariance has been proposed in the statistical literature solely for
g=2
or the bivariate response case, with the within and between response covariances formed by two respective matrices,
\bm{V}_{1}
and
\bm{V}_{R}
, where
\bm{V}_{1}
is a positive definite matrix and
\bm{V}_{R}
is a valid correlation matrix. Possible choices of covariance functions, for example the Exponential (Mat(
0.5
)), Gaussian (Mat(
\infty
)) and Matern are used in this article for
\bm{V}
and (
\bm{V}_{1}
,
\bm{V}_{R}
) (see Section
2.1
for details). Thus, it is important to note that the proportional and Markovian-type covariances are not at all restrictive in nature, in fact they allow us to explore a wide range of covariance functions for modelling the within and between response correlations.

For obtaining optimal designs, information matrices under the proportional and generalized Markovian-type covariance assumptions are determined. We show that these information matrices fail to satisfy the usual sufficient conditions
(
Kiefer, 1975
)
and the conditions by
Yeh, 1986
for universal optimal designs, leading us to instead search for trace optimal or efficient crossover designs
(
Bate and Jones, 2006
)
. For the proportional structure, an orthogonal array design represented by
OA_{I}\left(n=\lambda t\left(t-1\right),p=t,t,2\right)
, where
\lambda
is a positive integer and
t\geq 3
, is proved to be trace optimal/efficient. Under the more complicated Markovian structure, for
g=2
, i.e., a bivariate model, we show that the same orthogonal array design is highly efficient for various choices of within and between response covariance matrices. Further, we utilize the gene experiment dataset to illustrate the choice of a highly efficient design in a multivariate crossover setup. To reiterate, it is very important to understand that in this article we are able to determine efficient designs theoretically, for a wide range of covariance structures as opposed to obtaining optimal designs only for the exponential covariance case
(
Dasgupta et al., 2022
)
.

Various researchers, namely,
Matthews, 1987
;
Matthews, 1990
,
Kunert, 1991
,
Stufken, 1991
,
Kushner, 1997b
;
Kushner, 1998
,
Bate and Jones, 2006
and
Singh and Kunert, 2021
have studied efficient/highly efficient crossover designs. While, universally optimal crossover designs have been discussed by
Hedayat and Afsarinejad, 1978
,
Cheng and Wu, 1980
,
Kunert, 1984
,
Kunert and Martin, 2000
,
Hedayat and Yang, 2003
;
Hedayat and Yang, 2004
, to name a few. For a detailed review of crossover designs, we would like to refer to the paper by
Bose and Dey, 2013
and books by
Senn, 2002
,
Bose and Dey, 2009
and
Kenward and Jones, 2014
. However, all of these works concentrate only on single responses measured in each period. Very recently,
Niphadkar and Mukhopadhyay, 2023
have determined universal optimality results for multiple response crossover trials, where the responses are assumed to be uncorrelated with a homoscedastic covariance matrix.

The original contributions of this article include (i) a multivariate response crossover model with proportional and Markovian covariance structures to model correlations, (ii) information matrices for direct effects, (iii) theoretical results for efficient designs for a wide range of covariance functions, particularly -trace optimal design under the proportional covariance structure and highly efficient designs for the Markovian structure.

2
A multivariate crossover model

We consider the class,
\Omega_{t,n,p}
, of crossover designs with
t
treatments,
n
subjects and
p
periods, where
t,p\geq 3
. It is assumed that measurements are recorded on
g\geq 1
response variables from every subject in each period. Suppose for a design
d\in\Omega_{t,n,p}
,
Y_{dijk}
represents the
k^{th}
response measurement from the
j^{th}
subject in the
i^{th}
period of the experiment, where
1\leq k\leq g
,
1\leq i\leq p
and
1\leq j\leq n
. We model
Y_{dijk}
as:

\displaystyle Y_{dijk}=\mu_{k}+\alpha_{i,k}+\beta_{j,k}+\tau_{d\left(i,j\right),k}+\rho_{d\left(i-1,j\right),k}+\epsilon_{ijk},

(2.1)

where corresponding to the
k^{th}
response variable,
\mu_{k}
is the intercept effect,
\alpha_{i,k}
is the
i^{th}
period effect,
\beta_{j,k}
is the
j^{th}
subject effect,
\tau_{s,k}
and
\rho_{s,k}
are respectively the direct effect and the first order carryover effect due to the
s^{th}
treatment where
1\leq s\leq t
, and
d(i,j)
denotes the treatment allocated to the
j^{th}
subject in the
i^{th}
period. The assumptions of varying effects are in accordance with the observations made in the gene expression study in Subsection
1.1
. We assume that (
2.1
) is a fixed effect model with a zero mean error term,
\epsilon_{ijk}
, and no carryover effect in the first period. If
g=1
, then the above model becomes an univariate response model similar to those found in the statistical literature
(
Bose and Dey, 2009
, see)
.

Using matrix notations we rewrite the above model as:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\bm{Y}^{{}^{\prime}}_{d1}&\cdots&\bm{Y}^{{}^{\prime}}_{dg}\end{bmatrix}^{{}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\bm{Z}_{d}\bm{\eta}+\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\epsilon}^{{}^{\prime}}_{1}&\cdots&\bm{\epsilon}^{{}^{\prime}}_{g}\end{bmatrix}^{{}^{\prime}},

(2.2)

where

\bm{Y}_{dk}=\left(Y_{d11k},\cdots,Y_{dp1k},\cdots,Y_{d1nk},\cdots,Y_{dpnk}\right)^{{}^{\prime}}
and
\bm{\epsilon}_{k}=\left(\epsilon_{11k},\cdots,\epsilon_{pnk}\right)^{{}^{\prime}},
represent the observations and the error terms, respectively, corresponding to the
k^{th}
response, and

\bm{\eta}=

\begin{bmatrix}\mu_{1}&\cdots&\mu_{g}&\bm{\alpha}^{{}^{\prime}}_{1}&\bm{\beta}^{{}^{\prime}}_{1}&\cdots&\bm{\alpha}^{{}^{\prime}}_{g}&\bm{\beta}^{{}^{\prime}}_{g}&\bm{\tau}^{{}^{\prime}}_{1}&\bm{\rho}^{{}^{\prime}}_{1}&\cdots&\bm{\tau}^{{}^{\prime}}_{g}&\bm{\rho}^{{}^{\prime}}_{g}\end{bmatrix}^{{}^{\prime}}
is the parameter vector. The design matrix is given by

\displaystyle\bm{Z}_{d}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}_{g}\otimes\bm{{1}}_{np}&\bm{Z_{1}}&\bm{Z_{2}}\end{bmatrix},

(2.3)

where
\bm{Z_{1}}=\bm{I}_{g}\otimes\bm{X_{1}}
,
\bm{Z_{2}}=\bm{I}_{g}\otimes\bm{X_{2}}
,
\bm{X_{1}}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{P}&\bm{U}\end{bmatrix}
and
\bm{X_{2}}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{T}_{d}&\bm{F}_{d}\end{bmatrix}
. For the periods and subjects, we have
\bm{P}=\bm{{1}}_{n}\otimes\bm{I}_{p}
,
\bm{U}=\bm{I}_{n}\otimes\bm{{1}}_{p}
, while
\bm{T}_{d}
and
\bm{F}_{d}
are the matrices for treatment and carryover effects, respectively. Note that
\bm{F}_{d}=\left(\bm{I}_{n}\otimes\bm{\psi}\right)\bm{T}_{d}
, where
\bm{\psi}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{{0}}^{{}^{\prime}}_{p-1\times 1}&0\\
\bm{I}_{p-1}&\bm{{0}}_{p-1\times 1}\end{bmatrix}
. The vector of errors is assumed to have multivariate normal distribution with
E(\bm{\epsilon})=\bm{{0}}_{np\times 1}
and the dispersion matrix as

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathit{\Sigma}}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathit{\Sigma}}_{11}&\cdots&\bm{\mathit{\Sigma}}_{1g}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\bm{\mathit{\Sigma}}_{g1}&\cdots&\bm{\mathit{\Sigma}}_{gg}\end{bmatrix},

(2.4)

where
\bm{\mathit{\Sigma}}_{k^{\prime}k}=\bm{\mathit{\Sigma}}_{kk^{\prime}}^{{}^{\prime}}
, for
k\neq k^{\prime}=1,\cdots,g
. Here, the symmetric matrices,
\bm{\mathit{\Sigma}}_{kk}
represent the variance of
\bm{\epsilon}_{k}
, while
\bm{\mathit{\Sigma}}_{kk^{\prime}}
represents the covariance between
\bm{\epsilon}_{k}
and
\bm{\epsilon}_{k^{\prime}}
. The matrix
\bm{\mathit{\Sigma}}
is assumed to be positive definite. Also, observations from different subjects are taken to be uncorrelated, as is the usual practice
(
Bose and Dey, 2009
, see)
.

The investigation made regarding the presence of correlation between genes (see
Fig. 3
) in Subsection
1.1
, motivates the use of the covariance structure with cross-covariances. Two different structures are considered to model the covariances between responses, namely, the proportional structure and the generalized Markov-type structure. A generalized version of the Markov-type covariance structure for spatial data
(
Journel, 1999
;
Chiles and Delfiner, 2012
)
was proposed recently by
Dasgupta et al., 2022
. The proportional structure is simpler than the Markovian one and is used for the general
g>1
case. However, in the statistical literature, the Markovian structure has been studied for only the
g=2
or bivariate response case. We also restrict our search for optimal designs for a Markovian covariance structure to
g=2
or bivariate responses.

Proportional Structure:

Under the proportional structure the dispersion matrix
\bm{\mathit{\Sigma}}
is taken as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathit{\Sigma}}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathit{\Gamma}}\otimes\left(\bm{I}_{n}\otimes\bm{V}\right),

where
\bm{\mathit{\Gamma}}
is assumed to be a
g\times g
positive definite and symmetric matrix having non-zero off-diagonal elements, and
\bm{V}
a known positive definite and symmetric matrix. For
g=2
case, the matrix
\bm{\mathit{\Gamma}}
is given as

\displaystyle\bm{\mathit{\Gamma}}=\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_{11}&\gamma_{12}\\
\gamma_{12}&\gamma_{22}\end{bmatrix},

where
\gamma_{11},\gamma_{22}>0
and
\gamma_{11}\gamma_{22}>\gamma_{12}^{2}
. Under the proportional structure, the within and between responses have similar covariance matrices differing by some constants.

Generalized Markov-Type Structure:

In the statistical literature
(
Journel, 1999
;
Chiles and Delfiner, 2012
;
Dasgupta et al., 2022
, see)
, this structure is available to model the
g=2
case. Let
\sigma_{11}
and
\sigma_{22}
, where
\sigma_{11},\sigma_{22}>0
, be the respective variances of the first and second responses and
\rho
,
0<|\rho|<1
, denotes the correlation coefficient between the observations from the two responses measured from the same period. Under the generalized Markov-type structure the dispersion matrix
\bm{\mathit{\Sigma}}
is as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathit{\Sigma}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathit{\Sigma}}_{11}&\bm{\mathit{\Sigma}}_{12}\\
\bm{\mathit{\Sigma}}_{21}&\bm{\mathit{\Sigma}}_{22}\end{bmatrix},

where
\bm{\mathit{\Sigma}}_{11}=\bm{I}_{n}\otimes\bm{V}_{1}
,
\bm{\mathit{\Sigma}}_{12}=\bm{\mathit{\Sigma}}_{21}=\rho\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{22}}{\sigma_{11}}}\bm{\mathit{\Sigma}}_{11}
,
\bm{\mathit{\Sigma}}_{22}=\rho^{2}\frac{\sigma_{22}}{\sigma_{11}}\bm{\mathit{\Sigma}}_{11}+\sigma_{22}(1-\rho^{2})\bm{\mathit{\Sigma}}_{R}
and
\bm{\mathit{\Sigma}}_{R}=\bm{I}_{n}\otimes\bm{V}_{R}
. Here,
\bm{V}_{1}=\sigma_{11}\bm{V}_{C}
,
\bm{V}_{C}
is a known positive definite and symmetric matrix, and
\bm{V}_{R}
is a known correlation matrix.

3
Search for efficient designs

We next determine a trace optimal/efficient design for the direct effects in model (
2.2
). Using the idea as provided by
Bate and Jones, 2006
, we say that a design
d^{*}\in\mathcal{D}
, where
\mathcal{D}
is a subclass of designs, is a trace optimal/efficient design for the parameters of interest over
\mathcal{D}
if
d^{*}
maximizes the trace of the corresponding information matrix over
\mathcal{D}
.

For any design
d\in\Omega_{t,n,p}
, let
\bm{C}_{d(s1)}
and
\bm{C}_{d(s2)}
represent the information matrices of the direct effects for the proportional and generalized Markov-type structures, respectively.
Lemma S.1
and
Lemma S.3
show that the two information matrices have the following representations:

\displaystyle\bm{C}_{d(s1)}

\displaystyle=\bm{C}_{d(s1)(11)}-\bm{C}_{{d(s1)}(12)}\bm{C}^{-}_{{d(s1)}(22)}\bm{C}_{{d(s1)}(21)},

(3.1)

\displaystyle\bm{C}_{d(s2)}

\displaystyle=\bm{C}_{{d(s2)}(11)(1)}-\bm{C}_{{d(s2)}(12)(1)}\bm{C}^{-}_{{d(s2)}(22)(1)}\bm{C}_{{d(s2)}(21)(1)},

(3.2)

where
\bm{C}_{d(s1)(11)}=\left(\bm{I}_{g}\otimes\bm{T}_{d}^{{}^{\prime}}\right)\bm{A}^{*}\left(\bm{I}_{g}\otimes\bm{T}_{d}\right)
,
\bm{C}_{d(s1)(12)}=\bm{C}_{d(s1)(21)}^{{}^{\prime}}=\left(\bm{I}_{g}\otimes\bm{T}_{d}^{{}^{\prime}}\right)\bm{A}^{*}\left(\bm{I}_{g}\otimes\bm{F}_{d}\right)
,

\bm{C}_{{d(s1)}(22)}=\left(\bm{I}_{g}\otimes\bm{F}^{{}^{\prime}}_{d}\right)\bm{A}^{*}\left(\bm{I}_{g}\otimes\bm{F}_{d}\right)
,
\bm{C}_{{d(s2)}(11)(1)}=\left(\bm{I}_{2}\otimes\bm{T}_{d}^{{}^{\prime}}\right)\bm{A}^{*}\left(\bm{I}_{2}\otimes\bm{T}_{d}\right)
,
\bm{C}_{{d(s2)}(12)(1)}=

\bm{C}^{{}^{\prime}}_{{d(s2)}(21)(1)}=\left(\bm{I}_{2}\otimes\bm{T}_{d}^{{}^{\prime}}\right)\bm{A}^{*}\left(\bm{I}_{2}\otimes\bm{F}_{d}\bm{H}_{t}\right)
,
\bm{C}_{{d(s2)}(22)(1)}=\left(\bm{I}_{2}\otimes\bm{F}^{{}^{\prime}}_{d}\bm{H}_{t}\right)\bm{A}^{*}\left(\bm{I}_{2}\otimes\bm{F}_{d}\bm{H}_{t}\right)
, and the matrix
\bm{A}^{*}
is
\bm{\mathit{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}pr^{\perp}\left(\bm{\mathit{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}\bm{Z_{1}}\right)\bm{\mathit{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}
. Here,
\bm{H}_{t}=\bm{I}_{t}-\frac{1}{t}\bm{{1}}_{t}\bm{{1}}{t}^{{}^{\prime}}
. Note that
\bm{A}^{*}
depends on the structure of
\bm{\mathit{\Sigma}}
and
\bm{C}_{d(s1)}
is a
gt\times gt
matrix, while
\bm{C}_{d(s2)}
is a
2t\times 2t
matrix.

3.1
Proportional covariance structure

Under the proportional covariance, we prove that for the
g>1
setup, a design represented by
OA_{I}\left(n=\lambda t\left(t-1\right),p=t,t,2\right)
, where
\lambda
is a positive integer and
t\geq 3
, maximizes the
tr\left(\bm{C}_{d(s1)}\right)
and hence is a trace optimal/efficient design.

We know from (
3.1
), that the information matrix for the direct effects depends on the expression of
\bm{A}^{*}
. However, till date, there has been no work discussing the explicit expression of
\bm{A}^{*}
for bivariate/multivariate responses in a crossover framework. Using
Lemma S.2
, the expression of the matrix
\bm{A}^{*}
under the proportional structure for the
g>1
case is obtained as

\displaystyle\bm{A}^{*}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathit{\Gamma}}^{-1}\otimes\left(\bm{H}_{n}\otimes\bm{V}^{*}\right),

where
\bm{V}^{*}=\bm{V}^{-1}-\left(\bm{{1}}_{p}^{{}^{\prime}}\bm{V}^{-1}\bm{{1}}_{p}\right)^{-1}\bm{V}^{-1}\bm{J}_{p\times p}\bm{V}^{-1}
,
\bm{J}_{p\times p}=\bm{{1}}_{p}\bm{{1}}_{p}^{{}^{\prime}}
and
\bm{H}_{n}=\bm{I}_{n}-\frac{1}{n}\bm{{1}}_{n}\bm{{1}}_{n}^{{}^{\prime}}
. In the next theorem, the expression of
\bm{A}^{*}
is used to obtain the information matrix for the direct effects under the proportional structure for the multivariate setup.

Theorem 3.1
.

The information matrix for the direct effects under
g>1
case can be expressed as

\displaystyle\bm{C}_{d(s1)}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathit{\Gamma}}^{-1}\otimes\bm{C}_{d(uni)},

(3.3)

where
\bm{C}_{d(uni)}=\bm{C}_{d(uni)(11)}-\bm{C}_{d(uni)(12)}\bm{C}_{d(uni)(22)}^{-}\bm{C}_{d(uni)(21)}
,
\bm{C}_{d(uni)(11)}=\bm{T}_{d}^{{}^{\prime}}\left(\bm{H}_{n}\otimes\bm{V}^{*}\right)\bm{T}_{d}
,

\bm{C}_{d(uni)(12)}=\bm{C}_{d(uni)(21)}^{{}^{\prime}}=\bm{T}_{d}^{{}^{\prime}}\left(\bm{H}_{n}\otimes\bm{V}^{*}\right)\bm{F}_{d}
, and
\bm{C}_{d(uni)(22)}=\bm{F}_{d}^{{}^{\prime}}\left(\bm{H}_{n}\otimes\bm{V}^{*}\right)\bm{F}_{d}
. Here
\bm{V}^{*}=\bm{V}^{-1}-\left(\bm{{1}}_{p}^{{}^{\prime}}\bm{V}^{-1}\bm{{1}}_{p}\right)^{-1}\bm{V}^{-1}\bm{J}_{p\times p}\bm{V}^{-1}
.

Proof.

The details of the proof are given in
Appendix A.1
.
∎

It should be noted that
\bm{C}_{d(uni)}
is the information matrix for the direct effects when we have a univariate version of the model (
2.1
), i.e.,
g=1
. To check if
OA_{I}\left(n=\lambda t\left(t-1\right),p=t,\right.

\left.t,2\right)
, where
\lambda
is a positive integer and
t\geq 3
, is universally optimal, we explore complete symmetricity of the corresponding information matrix for
g>1
case.

Remark 3.1
.

Let
d^{*}
be a design given by
OA_{I}\left(n=\lambda t\left(t-1\right),p=t,t,2\right)
, where
\lambda

. Then for

, the information matrix for the direct effects corresponding to

,

, is not completely symmetric.

Proof.

The proof is given in
Appendix A.1
.
∎

From
Remark 3.1
, it is clear that the usual sufficient condition of complete symmetricity similar to the one given by
Kiefer, 1975
for finding a universally optimal design is not applicable here. Also, to satisfy the more general conditions similar to those given by
Yeh, 1986
, for
g>1
case, we need

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\gamma^{(11)}|\bm{E}|}{te_{22}}&=\cdots=\frac{\gamma^{(gg)}|\bm{E}|}{te_{22}},\text{ and}\\
\frac{\gamma^{(kk)}|\bm{E}|}{te_{22}}&=\frac{\gamma^{(kk^{\prime})}|\bm{E}|}{te_{22}},\end{split}

where
\gamma^{(kk^{\prime})}
is the
(k,k^{\prime})^{th}
element of the matrix
\bm{\mathit{\Gamma}}^{-1}
. However, this condition is also seen to be violated for the
g>1
setup (see Proof of
Remark 3.1
). Note, for a multivariate setup with no correlation between responses and a much simpler covariance structure,
Niphadkar and Mukhopadhyay, 2023
have successfully obtained universally optimal designs. However, this is not true when the responses are correlated. Hence for the multivariate response case, we resort to identifying an efficient design in the next theorem.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
d^{*}\in\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{t,n=\lambda t(t-1),p=t}
be a design given by
OA_{I}\left(n=\lambda t\left(t-1\right),p=t,t,2\right)
, where
\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{t,n=\lambda t(t-1),p=t}
is a class of binary designs with
p=t
,
\lambda
is a positive integer and
t\geq 3
. Then
d^{*}
is a trace optimal/efficient design for the direct effects over
\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{t,n=\lambda t(t-1),p=t}
for the
g>1
case.

Proof.

Here,
d^{*}\in\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{t,n=\lambda t(t-1),p=t}
is a design given by
OA_{I}\left(n=\lambda t\left(t-1\right),p=t,t,2\right)
, where

\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{t,n=\lambda t(t-1),p=t}
is a class of binary designs with
p=t
,
\lambda
is a positive integer and
t\geq 3
. It has been shown by
Kunert and Martin, 2000
that
d^{*}
is universally optimal for the direct effects over
\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{t,n=\lambda t(t-1),p=t}
in the univariate response case. Note that the universal optimality of a design over a subclass of designs implies that the design maximizes the trace of the corresponding information matrix over the same subclass of designs. Hence,
d^{*}
maximizes
tr\left(\bm{C}_{d(uni)}\right)
over
\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{t,n=\lambda t(t-1),p=t}
, where
\bm{C}_{d(uni)}
is the information matrix of the direct effects for the univariate response case. For
g>1
, using the expression of
\bm{C}_{d(s1)}
from
Theorem 3.1
, we get

\displaystyle tr\left(\bm{C}_{d(s1)}\right)

\displaystyle=tr\left(\bm{\mathit{\Gamma}}^{-1}\right)tr\left(\bm{C}_{d(uni)}\right).

(3.4)

Here,
\bm{\mathit{\Gamma}}^{-1}
is a positive definite matrix and thus
tr\left(\bm{\mathit{\Gamma}}^{-1}\right)>0
. So from (
3.4
), we get that for
g>1
,
d^{*}
maximizes
tr\left(\bm{C}_{d(s1)}\right)
over
\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{t,n=\lambda t(t-1),p=t}
. Thus
d^{*}
is trace optimal/efficient for the direct effects over
\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{t,n=\lambda t(t-1),p=t}
for the
g>1
case.
∎

3.2
Generalized Markov-type covariance structure

For the Markovian structure, we are able to show that the
OA_{I}\left(n=\lambda t\left(t-1\right),p=t,t,2\right)
, where
\lambda
is a positive integer and
t\geq 3
, is a highly efficient design. To evaluate the trace optimality/efficiency of these
OA_{I}\left(n=\lambda t\left(t-1\right),p=t,t,2\right)
designs, we obtain an upper bound of
tr\left(\bm{C}_{d(s2)}\right)
, and state a necessary and sufficient condition for a
OA_{I}\left(n=\lambda t\left(t-1\right),p=t,t,2\right)
, to attain this upper bound of
tr\left(\bm{C}_{d(s2)}\right)
. Note, that if this upper bound is attained then the design maximizes
tr\left(\bm{C}_{d(s2)}\right)
and hence is an efficient design. Also, for various choices of
\bm{V}_{1}
,
\bm{V}_{R}
and
\rho
, we study the trace of the information matrix for the
OA_{I}\left(n=\lambda t\left(t-1\right),p=t,t,2\right)
and the derived upper bound.

Proceeding as before, we see that the process of obtaining
\bm{A}^{*}
is more complicated as compared to the proportional structure. This is mainly because under the Markovian structure,
\bm{\mathit{\Sigma}}_{22}
is not proportional to
\bm{\mathit{\Sigma}}_{11}
. By laborious calculation, (see
Lemma S.4
), we obtain the expression of the matrix
\bm{A}^{*}
under the generalized Markov-type structure as

\displaystyle\bm{A}^{*}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{H}_{n}\otimes\bm{\mathit{\Omega}}_{1}&-\bm{H}_{n}\otimes\bm{\mathit{\Omega}}_{2}\\
-\bm{H}_{n}\otimes\bm{\mathit{\Omega}}_{2}&\bm{H}_{n}\otimes\bm{\mathit{\Omega}}_{4}\end{bmatrix},

where
\bm{\mathit{\Omega}}_{1}=\bm{V}_{1}^{*}+\frac{\bar{\rho}^{2}}{\sigma_{12}}\bm{V}_{R}^{*}
,
\bm{\mathit{\Omega}}_{2}=\frac{\bar{\rho}}{\sigma_{12}}\bm{V}_{R}^{*}
and
\bm{\mathit{\Omega}}_{4}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{12}}\bm{V}_{R}^{*}
. Here,
\bm{V}_{1}^{*}=\bm{V}_{1}^{-1}-\left(\bm{{1}}_{p}^{{}^{\prime}}\bm{V}_{1}^{-1}\bm{{1}}_{p}\right)^{-1}\bm{V}_{1}^{-1}\times

\bm{J}_{p\times p}\bm{V}_{1}^{-1}
,
\bm{V}_{R}^{*}=\bm{V}_{R}^{-1}-\left(\bm{{1}}_{p}^{{}^{\prime}}\bm{V}_{R}^{-1}\bm{{1}}_{p}\right)^{-1}\bm{V}_{R}^{-1}\bm{J}_{p\times p}\bm{V}_{R}^{-1}
,
\bm{H}_{n}=\bm{I}_{n}-\frac{1}{n}\bm{{1}}_{n}\bm{{1}}_{n}^{{}^{\prime}}
,
\bm{J}_{p\times p}=\bm{{1}}_{p}\bm{{1}}_{p}^{{}^{\prime}}
,
\bar{\rho}=\rho\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{22}}{\sigma_{11}}}
and
\sigma_{12}=\sigma_{22}\left(1-\rho^{2}\right)
.

Let
d^{*}
be a design given by
OA_{I}\left(n=\lambda t\left(t-1\right),p=t,t,2\right)
, where
\lambda
is a positive integer and
t\geq 3
. Using the above expression of
\bm{A}^{*}
, the information matrix for the direct effects and its trace under the generalized Markov-type structure, corresponding to
d^{*}
, are obtained.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
d^{*}
be a design given by

, where
\lambda
is a positive integer and
t\geq 3
. Then under the generalized Markov-type structure, the information matrix for the direct effects can be expressed as

\displaystyle\bm{{C}}_{d^{*}(s2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{t-1}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathit{\Lambda}}_{1}&\bm{\mathit{\Lambda}}_{2}\\
\bm{\mathit{\Lambda}}_{2}&\bm{\mathit{\Lambda}}_{4}\end{bmatrix},

(3.5)

where the matrices
\bm{\mathit{\Lambda}}_{1}
,
\bm{\mathit{\Lambda}}_{2}
and

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bm{\mathit{\Lambda}}_{1}&=\left(tr\left(\bm{V}_{1}^{*}\right)+\frac{\bar{\rho}^{2}}{\sigma_{12}}tr\left(\bm{V}_{R}^{*}\right)-\frac{\left(tr\left(\bm{V}_{1}^{*}\bm{\psi}\right)\right)^{2}}{tr\left(\bm{H}_{p}\bm{\psi}^{{}^{\prime}}\bm{V}_{1}^{*}\bm{\psi}\right)}-\frac{\bar{\rho}^{2}}{\sigma_{12}}\frac{\left(tr\left(\bm{V}_{R}^{*}\bm{\psi}\right)\right)^{2}}{tr\left(\bm{H}_{p}\bm{\psi}^{{}^{\prime}}\bm{V}_{R}^{*}\bm{\psi}\right)}\right)\bm{H}_{t},\\

(3.6)

\displaystyle tr\left(\bm{{C}}_{d^{*}(s2)}\right)

(3.7)

Proof.

The details of the proof are given in
Appendix A.2
.
∎

As in Subsection
3.1
, we show next that the sufficient conditions for universal optimality as similar to those discussed in
Kiefer, 1975
and
Yeh, 1986
are not applicable.

Remark 3.2
.

Let
d^{*}
be a design given by
OA_{I}\left(n=\lambda t\left(t-1\right),p=t,t,2\right)
, where
\lambda
is a positive integer and

,

Proof.

For the proof see
Appendix A.2
.
∎

From
Remark 3.2
, it is clear that as in the proportional covariance case, here also we cannot apply the usual sufficient condition for a universally optimal design. For conditions similar to
Yeh, 1986
, we require

\frac{1}{t}\left(tr\left(\bm{V}_{1}^{*}\right)+\frac{\bar{\rho}^{2}}{\sigma_{12}}tr\left(\bm{V}_{R}^{*}\right)-\frac{\left(tr\left(\bm{V}_{1}^{*}\bm{\psi}\right)\right)^{2}}{tr\left(\bm{H}_{p}\bm{\psi}^{{}^{\prime}}\bm{V}_{1}^{*}\bm{\psi}\right)}-\frac{\bar{\rho}^{2}}{\sigma_{12}}\frac{\left(tr\left(\bm{V}_{R}^{*}\bm{\psi}\right)\right)^{2}}{tr\left(\bm{H}_{p}\bm{\psi}^{{}^{\prime}}\bm{V}_{R}^{*}\bm{\psi}\right)}\right)=\\

is an efficient design, we obtain an upper bound of

attains this upper bound then we say that

Note,
d^{*}\in\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{t,n=\lambda t(t-1),p=t}

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{t,n=\lambda t(t-1),p=t}=\{d:d\in\Omega_{t,n=\lambda t(t-1),p=t},\text{ }t\geq 3\text{ and }d\text{ is a binary design}\}.

.

\displaystyle tr\left(\bm{{C}}_{d(s2)}\right)\leq u\left(t,n,p,\sigma_{11},\sigma_{22},\rho,\bm{V}_{1},\bm{V}_{R}\right),

Lemma S.5

\displaystyle tr\left(\bm{\tilde{C}}_{d(s2)}\right)\leq tr\left(\bm{\tilde{C}}_{d(s2)(11)}\right)-\left(tr\left(\bm{\tilde{C}}_{d(s2)(12)}\right)\right)^{2}\left(tr\left(\bm{\tilde{C}}_{d(s2)(22)}\right)\right)^{+},

6
Conclusion

In this article, we studied the trace optimality/efficiency of an orthogonal array of Type
I
and strength
2
when measurements are recorded on
g
responses, where
g>1
. To account for correlation between responses, we considered two different types of structure for the error dispersion matrix, namely, the proportional structure and the generalized Markov-type structure. Under the proportional covariance structure, we considered within response covariance structures, i.e, structures of
\bm{V}_{1}
to be Mat(
0.5
), Mat(
1.5
) and Mat(
\infty
). Under the generalized Markov-type covariance structure, we considered
7
different choices for structure of (
\bm{V}_{1}
,
\bm{V}_{R}
), namely (Mat(
0.5
), Mat(
1.5
)), (Mat(
0.5
), Mat(
\infty
)), (Mat(
1.5
), Mat(
0.5
)), (Mat(
1.5
), Mat(
\infty
)), (Mat(
\infty
), Mat(
0.5
)) and (Mat(
\infty
), Mat(
1.5
)). The main conclusions of this paper are:

1.

Under the proportional structure, for
g>1
and
p=t\geq 3
, a design given as an orthogonal array of type
I
and strength
2
is trace optimal/efficient for the direct effects over the class of binary designs.

2.

Under the generalized Markov-type structure, for the
7
different cases depending on the choices of matrices
\bm{V}_{1}
and
\bm{V}_{R}
, when
p=t=3
and
n=6
, and
p=t=4
and
n=12
, an orthogonal array of type
I
and strength
2
is highly efficient for the direct effects over the class of binary designs.

Although in this article, we have only considered universal optimality and trace optimality criteria, other criteria can also be investigated. In the future, for multivariate crossover designs, we plan to investigate
A
-,
D
-, and
E
-optimal designs, under the proportional and Markovian structures of the dispersion matrix.

A.1 Proportional structure

Proof of

.

From
Bose and Dey, 2009
, we know that for the
g=1
case with the dispersion matrix of error terms as a positive definite and symmetric matrix
\bm{I}_{n}\otimes\bm{V}
, the information matrix for the direct effects is given as

\displaystyle\bm{C}_{d(uni)}

\displaystyle=\bm{C}_{d(uni)(11)}-\bm{C}_{d(uni)(12)}\bm{C}_{d(uni)(22)}^{-}\bm{C}_{d(uni)(21)},

(A.1)

where
\bm{C}_{d(uni)(11)}=\bm{T}_{d}^{{}^{\prime}}\left(\bm{H}_{n}\otimes\bm{V}^{*}\right)\bm{T}_{d}
,
\bm{C}_{d(uni)(12)}=\bm{C}_{d(uni)(21)}^{{}^{\prime}}=\bm{T}_{d}^{{}^{\prime}}\left(\bm{H}_{n}\otimes\bm{V}^{*}\right)\bm{F}_{d}
and

\bm{C}_{d(uni)(22)}=\bm{F}_{d}^{{}^{\prime}}\left(\bm{H}_{n}\otimes\bm{V}^{*}\right)\bm{F}_{d}
. Here, the matrix
\bm{V}^{*}=\bm{V}^{-1}-\left(\bm{{1}}_{p}^{{}^{\prime}}\bm{V}^{-1}\bm{{1}}_{p}\right)^{-1}\bm{V}^{-1}\bm{J}_{p\times p}\bm{V}^{-1}
.

Using the expression of
\bm{A}^{*}
as given in
Lemma S.2
and the expression of the information matrix for the direct effects as given in
Lemma S.1
, we get that under the proportional structure for
g>1
,

\bm{C}_{d(s1)}=\bm{\mathit{\Gamma}}^{-1}\otimes\\
\left[\bm{T}_{d}^{{}^{\prime}}\left(\bm{H}_{n}\otimes\bm{V}^{*}\right)\bm{T}_{d}-\bm{T}_{d}^{{}^{\prime}}\left(\bm{H}_{n}\otimes\bm{V}^{*}\right)\bm{F}_{d}\left(\bm{F}_{d}^{{}^{\prime}}\left(\bm{H}_{n}\otimes\bm{V}^{*}\right)\bm{F}_{d}\right)^{-}\bm{F}_{d}^{{}^{\prime}}\left(\bm{H}_{n}\otimes\bm{V}^{*}\right)\bm{T}_{d}\right],

(A.2)

where
\bm{\mathit{\Gamma}}
is a
g\times g
positive definite and symmetric matrix with non-zero off-diagonal elements. Thus from (
A.1
) and (
A.2
), we can prove (
3.3
).
∎

Proof of
Remark 3.1
.

Here,
d^{*}
is a design given by
OA_{I}\left(n=\lambda t\left(t-1\right),p=t,t,2\right)
, where
\lambda
is a positive integer and
t\geq 3
. From
Theorem 3.1
, we get that for
g>1
, the information matrix for the direct effects corresponding to
d^{*}
is given as

\displaystyle\bm{C}_{d^{*}(s1)}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathit{\Gamma}}^{-1}\otimes\bm{C}_{d^{*}(uni)},

(A.3)

where
\bm{C}_{d^{*}(uni)}=\bm{C}_{d^{*}(uni)(11)}-\bm{C}_{d^{*}(uni)(12)}\bm{C}_{d^{*}(uni)(22)}^{-}\bm{C}_{d^{*}(uni)(21)}
, and
\bm{\mathit{\Gamma}}
is a
g\times g
positive definite and symmetric matrix with non-zero off-diagonal elements. Here,
\bm{C}_{d^{*}(uni)(11)}=\bm{T}_{d^{*}}^{{}^{\prime}}\left(\bm{H}_{n}\otimes\bm{V}^{*}\right)\bm{T}_{d^{*}}
,
\bm{C}_{d^{*}(uni)(12)}=\bm{C}_{d^{*}(uni)(21)}^{{}^{\prime}}=\bm{T}_{d^{*}}^{{}^{\prime}}\left(\bm{H}_{n}\otimes\bm{V}^{*}\right)\bm{F}_{d^{*}}
,
\bm{C}_{d^{*}(uni)(22)}=

\bm{F}_{d^{*}}^{{}^{\prime}}\left(\bm{H}_{n}\otimes\bm{V}^{*}\right)\bm{F}_{d^{*}}
and the matrix

.

Since
\bm{F}_{d^{*}}=\left(\bm{I}_{n}\otimes\bm{\psi}\right)\bm{T}_{d^{*}}
, where
\bm{\psi}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{{0}}^{{}^{\prime}}_{p-1\times 1}&0\\
\bm{I}_{p-1}&\bm{{0}}_{p-1\times 1}\end{bmatrix}
, and
\bm{V}
is a known positive definite and symmetric matrix, from
Martin and Eccleston, 1998
, we know

\displaystyle\bm{C}_{d^{*}(uni)}

\displaystyle=\left(|\bm{E}|/e_{22}\right)\bm{H}_{t},

(A.4)

where
\bm{E}=\frac{n}{t-1}\begin{bmatrix}e_{11}&e_{12}\\
e_{12}&e_{22}\end{bmatrix}
,

\bm{V}^{*}
, and
\bm{T}_{d^{*}1}
is the treatment incidence matrix for the first subject corresponding to

. For

\displaystyle\bm{\mathit{\Gamma}}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}\gamma^{(11)}&\cdots&\gamma^{(1g)}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\gamma^{(g1)}&\cdots&\gamma^{(gg)}\end{bmatrix}.

\bm{\mathit{\Gamma}}^{-1}

) and (

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\gamma^{(11)}|\bm{E}|}{te_{22}}&=\cdots=\frac{\gamma^{(gg)}|\bm{E}|}{te_{22}},\\
\frac{\gamma^{(kk)}|\bm{E}|}{te_{22}}&=\frac{\gamma^{(kk^{\prime})}|\bm{E}|}{te_{22}},\text{ and}\\

(A.5)

where

. Thus using the expression of the matrices
\bm{{C}}_{d(s2)}
and
\bm{\tilde{C}}_{d(s2)}
as given in
Lemma S.3
and
Lemma S.5
, respectively, we get that
\bm{{C}}_{d(s2)}=\bm{\tilde{C}}_{d(s2)}
.
∎

Lemma S.8
.

Let
\bm{V}_{1}^{*}=\bm{V}_{1}^{-1}-\left(\bm{{1}}_{p}^{{}^{\prime}}\bm{V}_{1}^{-1}\bm{{1}}_{p}\right)^{-1}\bm{V}_{1}^{-1}\bm{J}_{p\times p}\bm{V}_{1}^{-1}
and
\bm{V}_{R}^{*}=\bm{V}_{R}^{-1}-\left(\bm{{1}}_{p}^{{}^{\prime}}\bm{V}_{R}^{-1}\bm{{1}}_{p}\right)^{-1}\times

\bm{V}_{R}^{-1}\bm{J}_{p\times p}\bm{V}_{R}^{-1}
. Then
tr\left(\bm{H}_{p}\bm{\psi}^{{}^{\prime}}\bm{V}_{1}^{*}\bm{\psi}\right)>0
and
tr\left(\bm{H}_{p}\bm{\psi}^{{}^{\prime}}\bm{V}_{R}^{*}\bm{\psi}\right)>0
.

Proof.

Here,
\bm{V}_{1}
is a symmetric and positive definite matrix. Since
pr^{\perp}\left(.\right)
is a symmetric and idempotent matrix,
\bm{V}_{1}^{*}=\bm{V}_{1}^{-1}-\left(\bm{{1}}_{p}^{{}^{\prime}}\bm{V}_{1}^{-1}\bm{{1}}_{p}\right)^{-1}\bm{V}_{1}^{-1}\bm{J}_{p\times p}\bm{V}_{1}^{-1}
can be written as

\displaystyle\bm{V}_{1}^{*}

\displaystyle=\bm{L}_{1}^{{}^{\prime}}\bm{L}_{1},

(S.50)

where
\bm{L}_{1}=pr^{\perp}\left(\bm{V}_{1}^{-1/2}\bm{J}_{p\times p}\right)\bm{V}_{1}^{-1/2}
. Since
\bm{H}_{p}
is a symmetric and idempotent matrix,

tr\left(\bm{H}_{p}\bm{\psi}^{{}^{\prime}}\bm{V}_{1}^{*}\bm{\psi}\right)=tr\left(\bm{L}_{2}^{{}^{\prime}}\bm{L}_{2}\right)\geq 0
, where
\bm{L}_{2}=\bm{L}_{1}\bm{\psi}\bm{H}_{p}
. It can be easily seen that
tr\left(\bm{L}_{2}^{{}^{\prime}}\bm{L}_{2}\right)=\bm{{0}}_{p\times p}
if and only if
\bm{L}_{2}=\bm{{0}}_{p\times p}
. Now, suppose
tr\left(\bm{H}_{p}\bm{\psi}^{{}^{\prime}}\bm{V}_{1}^{*}\bm{\psi}\right)=0
. So,
\bm{L}_{1}\bm{\psi}\bm{H}_{p}=\bm{{0}}_{p\times p}
and thus from (
S.50
), we get

\displaystyle\bm{V}_{1}^{*}\bm{\psi}\bm{H}_{p}=\bm{{0}}_{p\times p}.

(S.51)

From the expression of
\bm{V}_{1}^{*}
, we get that the row sums of
\bm{V}_{1}^{*}
is
0
. Thus using (
S.51
), by calculation we get

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left(\bm{V}_{1}^{*}\right)_{1,2}&=\cdots=\left(\bm{V}_{1}^{*}\right)_{1,p}=-\frac{1}{p}\left(\bm{V}_{1}^{*}\right)_{1,1},\\
\vdots\\
\left(\bm{V}_{1}^{*}\right)_{p,2}&=\cdots=\left(\bm{V}_{1}^{*}\right)_{p,p}=-\frac{1}{p}\left(\bm{V}_{1}^{*}\right)_{p,1},\end{split}

(S.52)

where
\left(\bm{V}_{1}^{*}\right)_{l_{1},l_{2}}
denotes the
(l_{1},l_{2})^{th}
element of
\bm{V}_{1}^{*}
. Using the property that the row sums of
\bm{V}_{1}^{*}
is
0
in (
S.52
), we get that
\bm{V}_{1}^{*}=\bm{{0}}_{p\times p}
. Thus, by calculation we get

\displaystyle\left(\bm{{1}}_{p}^{{}^{\prime}}\bm{V}_{1}^{-1}\bm{{1}}_{p}\right)^{-1}\bm{J}_{p\times p}\bm{V}_{1}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\bm{I}_{p}.

(S.53)

The above equation (
S.53
) leads to a contradiction. Hence our assumption that

tr\left(\bm{H}_{p}\bm{\psi}^{{}^{\prime}}\bm{V}_{1}^{*}\bm{\psi}\right)=0
is wrong. Thus,
tr\left(\bm{H}_{p}\bm{\psi}^{{}^{\prime}}\bm{V}_{1}^{*}\bm{\psi}\right)>0
.

Since
\bm{V}_{R}
is a positive definite matrix, similarly we can prove that
tr\left(\bm{H}_{p}\bm{\psi}^{{}^{\prime}}\bm{V}_{R}^{*}\bm{\psi}\right)>0
.
∎
