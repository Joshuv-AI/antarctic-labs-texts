A practical guide to well roundedness
Tal Horesh, Yakov Karasik

Abstract
Let G G be a semisimple algebraic group. We develop a machinery for manipulation and manufacture of well-rounded families { ℬ T } T > 0 ⊂ G \left\{\mathcal{B}_{T}\right\}_{T>0}\subset G as they were defined in a work by A. Gorodnik and A. Nevo. The importance of these types of families is that one can asymptotically count lattice points in them and even obtain an error term. Lattice counting is highly effective for solving asymptotic problems from number theory and the geometry of numbers. The tools we develop are handy especially when the family is given w.r.t. some decomposition of G G (e.g. Iwasawa or Cartan) and also when it depends upon a sub-quotients of the form ℳ / H \mathcal{M}/H , where ℳ ⊂ G \mathcal{M}\subset G is a submanifold and H < G H<G is a closed subgroup.

Introduction

This text came to life out from the authors’ work
[
HK19
,
HK20b
,
HK20a
]

on equidistrbution problems in geometry of numbers. In the course
of our work we relied significantly on counting lattice point results
in semisimple algebraic groups. The classical setting for counting
lattice points problems is
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where in order to establish
an asymptotic formula for the number of lattice points inside an increasing
family of compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it is required that the boundary
of the sets satisfies certain regularity conditions (e.g. smoothness,
finite non vanishing curvature, etc.). With the rise of interest in
counting problems in semisimple groups and their affine spaces, e.g.
hyperbolic spaces (e.g.
[
EM93
]
), where the boundary of a set
has fundamentally different properties than in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, a new notion
of regularity for a family
\left\{\mathcal{B}_{T}\right\}
was born:

well roundedness
. It was introduced in
[
EM93
]
(although
a similar idea appeared already in
[
DRS93
]
), was used e.g. in

[
GW07
]
, and then defined again in
[
GN12
]
, for the general
setting of lcsc groups. In the latter, the concept of well roundedness
was further refined to Lipschitz well roundedness:

Definition 1.1

(
[
GN12
]
)
.

Let
G
be a locally compact second
countable group with a Borel measure
\mu
, and let
\left\{\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\right\}_{\epsilon>0}

be a family of identity neighborhoods in
G
. Assume
\left\{\mathcal{B}_{T}\right\}_{T>0}\subset G

is a family of measurable domains and denote

\mathcal{B}_{T}^{\left(+\epsilon\right)}:=\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\mathcal{B}_{T}\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}=\bigcup_{u,v\in\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}}u\,\mathcal{B}_{T}\,v,

\mathcal{B}_{T}^{\left(-\epsilon\right)}:=\bigcap_{u,v\in\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}}u\,\mathcal{B}_{T}\,v.

The family
\left\{\mathcal{B}_{T}\right\}
is
Lipschitz well-rounded
(LWR)
with (positive) parameters
\left(\mathcal{C},T_{0},\epsilon_{0}\right)

if for every
0<\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}
and
T>T_{0}
:

\mu\left(\mathcal{B}_{T}^{\left(+\epsilon\right)}\right)\leq\left(1+\mathcal{C}\epsilon\right)\>\mu\left(\mathcal{B}_{T}^{\left(-\epsilon\right)}\right).

(1.1)

The parameter
\mathcal{C}
is called the
Lipschitz constant

of the family
\left\{\mathcal{B}_{T}\right\}
.

The results in
[
GN12
]
show that under some conditions on
G
,
which originate from representation theory, one can get an estimate
with an error term of the size of the set
\Gamma\cap\text{$\mathcal{B}_{T}$}
,
where
\Gamma<G
is a lattice and
T\to\infty
. To be able to use
these results to solve concrete problems, one should have an ample
supply of LWR sets as well as a mechanism for checking that a certain
family is indeed well rounded. This is the first aim of this work.

Despite their name, simple (and semisimple) groups are quite complicated
objects, and they are often studied via their decompositions into
“less complicated” groups. A decomposition is when a group (or
some “big” subset of it) is written as a product of certain subgroups,
e.g. the Cartan, Iwasawa and Bruhat decompositions. In the context
of counting lattice points, many natural counting problems translate
into counting lattice points in families of increasing sets inside
semisimple Lie groups, that are defined via a decomposition of the
group. As a baby example, consider the Cartan decomposition
KAK

of
\operatorname{SO}_{1,n}^{0}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
, with
K=\operatorname{SO}_{n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)

and
A=\left\{a_{t}:t\in\mathbb{R}\right\}
,
a_{t}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\cosh t&0&\sinh t\\
0&I_{n-2}&0\\
\sinh t&0&\cosh t\end{smallmatrix}\right]
. The set
\mathcal{B}_{T}=\left\{Ka_{t}K:0\leq t\leq T\right\}
is the
lift of a hyperbollic ball of radius
T
in
\mathbb{H}^{n}\simeq K\backslash\operatorname{SO}_{1,n}^{0}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
,
and so counting lattice points in the family
\left\{\mathcal{B}_{T}\right\}_{T>0}

is essentially equivalent to the hyperbolic sphere problem
[
LP82
,
PR94
]
,
first stated by Selberg, which concerns counting lattice orbit points
in increasing hyperbolic balls. We refer to
[
GN12
]
(see also

[
GOS10
]
) for counting in sets that are defined via the
Cartan decomposition, to
[
MMO14
]
for counting in sets that are
defined via the Bruhat decomposition, and to
[
HN16
]

for counting in sets that are defined via the Iwasawa decomposition,
which is also the setting in our aforementioned ongoing work, for
which this text was written.

When verifying the well roundedness of a family of sets inside a semisimple
Lie group, it is therefore quite natural to consider a suitable decomposition
of the group, and then try and reduce the verification to the that
of well roundedness of the projections of the family in each one of
the subgroups that appear in the decomposition. The logic being that
these subgroups are easier to analyze since they are compact, or abelian,
or unipotent, etc. In the example with the lifts of hyperbolic balls,
one would like to reduce the well roundedness of the family
\left\{\mathcal{B}_{T}\right\}_{T>0}

to well roundedness of
\left\{a_{t}:0\leq t\leq T\right\}_{T>0}

inside the subgroup
A
(and of the constant family
\left\{K\right\}

inside
K
, which is trivial). However, it is false that well roundedness
in the components of the decomposition implies well roundedness of
the original family in the group, so this reduction cannot be implemented
without further thought. We have considered a systematic approach
to this problem, which can be also applied in other situation as well.
The idea is a categorical approach of defining morphisms between groups,
called
roundomorphisms
, which pull back a well rounded family
in the image into a well rounded family in the domain.

When it comes to counting problems in a semisimple group, many families
of interest have the property that their projections to one or more
of the components is a fixed set. A simple example is the the projection
to the
K
components of the lifted hyperbolic balls, but in fact
such families arise naturally in equidistribution problems (see
[
Tru13
,
HN16
]

and our aforementioned work in progress). Since our method relies
on well roundedness in the components, it is helpful to formulate
a condition that is easier to verify than the one of well roundedness
itself, yet implies well roundedness for a constant family of sets;
by that we mean
\mathcal{B}_{T}=\mathcal{B}
for all
T
. We propose the following:

Definition 1.2
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be an orbifold. A subset
B
of
\mathcal{M}
is
called a Boundary Controllable Set, or BCS, if for every
x\in\mathcal{M}

there is an open neighborhood
U_{x}
of
x
such that
U_{x}\cap\partial B

is contained in a finite union of embedded submanifolds of
\mathcal{M}
.

Remark 1.3
.

In most cases (e.g. when
\mathcal{M}
is a
manifold and
B
is bounded) the global version of the previous definition
is sufficient. That means one can take for every point
x\in\mathcal{M}

the open set
\mathcal{M}
, and so it is sufficient to check that
the boundary of
B
is contained in a finite number of embedded submanifolds
with dimension strictly smaller than
\dim\left(\mathcal{M}\right)
.

Orbifolds arise naturally as a quotient of a submanifold of
G
by
a group with almost free stabilizers. One example would be the space

\operatorname{SO}_{n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\backslash\operatorname{SL}_{n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)/\operatorname{SL}_{n}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)

which bears great significance in geometry of numbers, being the space
of
shapes
of lattices, and when
n=2
it is the modular curve.
Our aforementioned work in progress concerns equidistribution in spaces
of lattices that have the structure of an orbifold, e.g. the space
of shapes. Since our approach is lifting the counting problem from
the quotient to the group
G
, where we count in well rounded families,
we are forced to transfer BCS’s from the quotient space into some
convenient fundamental domain inside the group. It turns out that
not every fundamental domain is adequate, and in Section
6

we characterize the fundamental domains that are. We call them
spread
models
, since we think of them as fundamental domains that are obtained
by cutting the space open and spreading it - here one can think of
cutting a two dimensional torus into a parallelogram. We hope that
this section could be of further interest in the future, since it
is essentially a discussion on fundamental domains for which the quotient
map into the space the domain represents, pulls back differential
properties from the space to the domain. This investigation is the
second main goal of this work.

Finally in the last section
7.1
, we
provide some examples for spread models. In particular, we introduce
the well known (
[
Gre93
,
Sch98
]
) constructions of fundamental
domains coming from geometry of numbers and show that they are indeed
spread models for the spaces of lattices that they represent.

2
Coordinate balls

Definition
1.1
is w.r.t. a nested family

\left\{\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\right\}_{\epsilon>0}
of identity neighborhoods in
the group, where by “nested” we mean that
\epsilon_{1}<\epsilon_{2}
implies

\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon_{1}}\subset\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon_{2}}
. While the definition of
well roundedness allows any nested family of identity neighborhoods,
we shall work only with neighborhoods that are the images of small
balls in the Lie algebra under the exponent map — this is Assumption

2.7
, which concludes the current subsection. The
advantages of this choice follow from the fact that it is a special
case of
coordinate balls
(Definition
2.2
),
and this subsection is devoted to investigating the properties of
neighborhoods of this sort.

Definition 2.1

(Equivalence of identity neighborhoods)
.

Let
G
be a Lie group and consider
two families
\left\{\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\right\}_{\epsilon>0},\left\{\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}^{\prime}\right\}_{\epsilon>0}

of nested and symmetric identity neighborhoods. We say that these
families are
equivalent
if there exist
\epsilon_{1},c,C>0
such
that for every
0<\epsilon<\epsilon_{1}

\mathcal{O}_{c\epsilon}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{C\epsilon}.

Definition 2.2

(Coordinate balls)
.

A family
\left\{\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\right\}_{\epsilon>0}

of identity neighborhoods inside a Lie group
G
will be called a
family of
coordinate balls
if there exist a ball
B_{\epsilon}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{\dim\left(G\right)}:\|x\|<\epsilon\right\}

inside
\mathbb{R}^{\dim\left(G\right)}
, and a
C^{1}
chart

\phi:\underset{1_{G}\in}{\underbrace{U}}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}

of the identity, such that
\left\{\phi^{-1}\left(B_{\epsilon}\right)\right\}_{\epsilon>0}

is equivalent to
\left\{\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\right\}_{\epsilon>0}
.

Remark 2.3
.

All coordinate balls of a
given Lie group are equivalent. Indeed, if
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}

are two charts, then
\phi_{2}\phi_{1}^{-1}|_{B_{1}}
is a
bi-Lipschitz
map.
Hence,

\phi_{2}^{-1}\left(B_{c\epsilon}\right)\subseteq\underset{\phi_{1}^{-1}\left(B_{\epsilon}\right)}{\underbrace{\phi_{2}^{-1}\left(\phi_{2}\phi_{1}^{-1}\left(B_{\epsilon}\right)\right)}}\subseteq\phi_{2}^{-1}\left(B_{C\epsilon}\right)

for some
c,C>0
and
\epsilon<1
.

The following Lemma specifies two useful features of coordinate balls.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\left\{\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\right\}_{\epsilon>0}

be a family of coordinate balls inside a Lie group
G
. Then for
small enough
\epsilon
and
\delta
, the following two properties hold:

•

(
Connectivity
)
\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}
is a connected subset of
G
.

•

(
Additivity
) There exists
c>0
such that:

\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\mathcal{O}_{\delta}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{c\left(\epsilon+\delta\right)}.

Proof.

Connectivity holds since
\phi^{-1}
(
\phi
being the associated
chart) is continuous. Additivity holds for Riemannian left
G
-invariant
balls with
c=1
(triangle inequality); these Riemannian balls are
indeed coordinate balls, where the implied chart is the Riemannian
exponential map. Since all families of coordinate balls are equivalent
(Remark
2.3
), the statement follows.
∎

One last property of coordinate balls is the following.
2.4

Proposition 2.5
.

Let

\left\{\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\right\}_{\epsilon>0}
be a family of coordinate balls
inside a Lie group
G
, and assume

\phi:\underset{g\in}{\underbrace{U}}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}

is a chart that contains an element
g
. Then, there exist an open
set
g\in V\subset U
and positive
\epsilon\left(g\right),c\left(g\right)

such that for
\epsilon\leq\epsilon\left(g\right)
:

\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}V\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\subseteq U

and for every
h\in\overline{V}

\phi\left(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}h\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\right)\subseteq\phi\left(h\right)+B_{c\left(g\right)\epsilon}.

The proof requires an auxiliary lemma:

Lemma 2.6

(
[
HN16
]
)
.

Let
G
be
a Lie group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. For
\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}=\exp\left(B_{\epsilon}\right)

and every
g\in G
,

g^{-1}\,\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\,g\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon\cdot\left\|\operatorname{Ad}_{g}\right\|_{\text{op}}}=\exp\left\{Z\in\mathfrak{g}:\left\|Z\right\|\leq\epsilon\cdot\left\|\operatorname{Ad}_{g}\right\|_{\text{op}}\right\},

where
\|\cdot\|
is any euclidean norm on
\mathfrak{g}
and
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mbox{op}}

is the norm on the space of linear operators on
\mathfrak{g}
.

Proof of Proposition
2.5
.

Observe that by the previous lemma and the additivity property
in Lemma
2.4
, for
every
h\in G
there is a constant
c_{1}\left(h\right)
such that
for
0<\epsilon\leq\epsilon_{1}\left(h\right)
:

\phi\left(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}h\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\right)=\phi\left(h\cdot\underset{\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon\cdot\left\|\operatorname{Ad}_{h}\right\|_{\text{op}}}}{\underbrace{h^{-1}\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}h}}\cdot\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\right)\subseteq\phi\left(h\cdot\mathcal{O}_{c_{1}\left(h\right)\epsilon}\right)=\underset{\psi_{h}}{\underbrace{\phi\circ L_{h}\circ\exp}}\left(B_{c_{1}\left(h\right)\epsilon}\right),

where
L_{h}:G\to G
is the left translation by
h
. By compactness
of
D_{g}:=\overline{\mathcal{O}_{1}g\mathcal{O}_{1}}
and continuity of
\left\|\operatorname{Ad}\left(\cdot\right)\right\|_{\text{op}}

, there exist
c_{0}\left(g\right)
and
\epsilon_{0}\left(g\right)

for which the above holds uniformly on
D_{g}
, namely for every

h\in D_{g}
and
0<\epsilon\leq\epsilon_{0}\left(g\right):

\phi\left(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}h\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\right)\subseteq\psi_{h}\left(B_{c_{0}\left(g\right)\epsilon}\right).

We choose
\epsilon\left(g\right)>0
and
0<\delta<1
small enough so
that (using the additivity property again) for
V:=\mathcal{O}_{\delta}g\mathcal{O}_{\delta}

and
0<\epsilon<\epsilon\left(g\right)
we have

\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}V\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}=\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\mathcal{O}_{\delta}g\mathcal{O}_{\delta}\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\subset\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon_{0}\left(g\right)}g\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon_{0}\left(g\right)}.

We also assume
\epsilon_{0}\left(g\right)
is small enough such that
\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon_{0}\left(g\right)}g\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon_{0}\left(g\right)}\subset U
.

Since
\psi\left(h,x\right)=\psi_{h}\left(x\right)
is a differentiable
map defined on a compact domain
\overline{U}\times\overline{B_{c_{0}\left(g\right)\epsilon}}
,
there exists
c\left(g\right)=c\left(D_{g}\right)>0
such that
for every
h\in U
and
x\in B_{c_{0}\left(g\right)\epsilon}
:

\|\psi_{h}\left(x\right)-\psi_{h}\left(0\right)\|\leq c\left(g\right)\|x-0\|.

Hence,

\psi_{h}\left(B_{c_{0}\left(g\right)\epsilon}\right)\subseteq\psi_{h}\left(0\right)+B_{c\left(g\right)\epsilon}=\phi\left(h\right)+B_{c\left(g\right)\epsilon}.

∎

Finally, we fix a choice of coordinate balls that will be used from
now on.

Assumption 2.7
.

Unless specified otherwise we will assume that

\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}=\exp\left(B_{\epsilon}\right)
, where
\exp
is the Lie
exponent.

3
Well rounded sets - criteria and properties

This section is devoted to investigating the concept of well-roundedness
for
constant
families, which are just fixed subsets of
G
:

\mathcal{B}_{T}=\mathcal{B}
for all
T
. It turns out that in the constant
case, the LWR property can be reduced to a boundary condition. This
enables us to obtain a huge class of Lipschitz well rounded (fixed)
sets, which are in fact the BCS’s defined in the introduction.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose
\left\{\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\right\}_{\epsilon>0}

is a family of coordinate balls, and let
\mathcal{B}\subseteq G
. Then

\mathcal{B}^{+}\left(\epsilon\right)\setminus\mathcal{B}^{-}\left(\epsilon\right)=\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\,\partial\mathcal{B}\,\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}
,
or equivalently:

\mathcal{B}^{\left(+\epsilon\right)}=\mathcal{B}\cup\left(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\,\partial\mathcal{B}\,\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\right)

and

\mathcal{B}^{\left(-\epsilon\right)}=\mathcal{B}\setminus\left(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\,\partial\mathcal{B}\,\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\right).

Remark 3.2
.

In fact, Lemma
3.1
applies for any family
\left\{\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\right\}_{\epsilon>0}

of connected identity neighborhoods.

Proof.

We first show that

\mathcal{B}^{\left(+\epsilon\right)}\setminus\mathcal{B}^{\left(-\epsilon\right)}=\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\,\partial\mathcal{B}\,\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}.

For the inclusion
\supseteq
, we must show that
\mathcal{B}^{\left(+\epsilon\right)}\supseteq\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\cdot\partial\mathcal{B}\cdot\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}

and that
\left(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\cdot\partial\mathcal{B}\cdot\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\right)\cap\mathcal{B}^{\left(-\epsilon\right)}=\emptyset
.
For the first, assume
g\in\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\cdot\partial\mathcal{B}\cdot\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}
.
By symmetry of
\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}
, the open set
\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\cdot g\cdot\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}

intersects
\partial\mathcal{B}
non-trivially, and therefore meets
\mathcal{B}
,
say in a point
h
. Then (again by symmetry)
g\in\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\cdot h\cdot\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\subset\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\mathcal{B}\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}
.
For the latter, note that
h\in\mathcal{B}^{\left(-\epsilon\right)}
if and only
if
h\in u\mathcal{B}v
for all
u,v\in\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}
, i.e. if and only
if
u^{-1}hv^{-1}\in\mathcal{B}
for all
u,v\in\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}
, which by
symmetry of
\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}
is equivalent to
\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\cdot h\cdot\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\subset\mathcal{B}
.
Now if
g\in\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\cdot\partial\mathcal{B}\cdot\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}
then as before
the open set
\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\cdot g\cdot\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}
intersects
\partial\mathcal{B}

non-trivially, and in particular meets
\operatorname{\mathcal{B}}^{c}
; then
\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\cdot g\cdot\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\not\subset\mathcal{B}
,
namely
g\notin\mathcal{B}^{\left(-\epsilon\right)}
.

For the inclusion
\subseteq
, let
g\notin\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\partial\mathcal{B}\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}
,
and we show that
g\notin\mathcal{B}^{\left(+\epsilon\right)}\setminus\mathcal{B}^{\left(-\epsilon\right)}
.
Namely, that either
g\in\mathcal{B}^{\left(-\epsilon\right)}
or that
g\in\operatorname{(\mathcal{B}^{\left(+\epsilon\right)})}^{c}
.
Indeed,
g\notin\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\partial\mathcal{B}\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}
implies that
\left(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}g\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\right)\cap\partial\mathcal{B}=\emptyset
,
and since
\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}g\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}
is connected it follows that
either
\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}g\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
or
\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}g\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\subseteq\operatorname{\mathcal{B}}^{c}
.
The first implies (by the equivalence established in the first inclusion)
that
g\in\mathcal{B}^{\left(-\epsilon\right)}
. The latter implies that
g\notin\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\mathcal{B}\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}=\mathcal{B}^{\left(+\epsilon\right)}
.

The statement of the lemma now follows:

\mathcal{B}^{\left(+\epsilon\right)}=\mathcal{B}^{\left(-\epsilon\right)}\sqcup\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\partial\mathcal{B}\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\subseteq\mathcal{B}\cup\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\partial\mathcal{B}\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}

where the opposite inclusion holds as
\mathcal{B}^{\left(+\epsilon\right)}\supseteq\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\partial\mathcal{B}\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}
.
Furthermore,

\mathcal{B}^{\left(-\epsilon\right)}=\mathcal{B}^{\left(+\epsilon\right)}\setminus\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\partial\mathcal{B}\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}=\left(\mathcal{B}\cup\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\partial\mathcal{B}\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\right)\setminus\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\partial\mathcal{B}\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}=

=\left(\left(\mathcal{B}\setminus\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\partial\mathcal{B}\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\right)\sqcup\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\partial\mathcal{B}\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\right)\setminus\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\partial\mathcal{B}\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}=\mathcal{B}\setminus\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\partial\mathcal{B}\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}.

∎

From Lemma
3.1
we deduce the following simple
criterion for the Lipschitz well roundedness of a (fixed) set.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
G
be a Lie group with a Borel
measure
\mu
. If a subset
\mathcal{B}\subset G
satisfies that
0<\mu\left(\mathcal{B}\right)<\infty

and that there exists
c>0
such that

\mu(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\cdot\partial\mathcal{B}\cdot\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon})\leq c\epsilon

for every
0<\epsilon<\frac{\mu\left(\mathcal{B}\right)}{2c}
, then
\mathcal{B}

is LWR with

C=\frac{2c}{\mu\left(\mathcal{B}\right)}.

The converse also holds: suppose
\mathcal{B}
is LWR with positive measure
and parameter
C
. Then for
\epsilon<C^{-1}
,

\mu(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\cdot\partial\mathcal{B}\cdot\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon})\leq C\mu\left(\mathcal{B}\right)\epsilon.

Proof.

By our assumption, for
\epsilon<\frac{\mu\left(\mathcal{B}\right)}{2c}
,
and by Lemma
3.1
:

\displaystyle\mu\left(\mathcal{B}^{\left(+\epsilon\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu\left(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\mathcal{B}\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu\left(\mathcal{B}^{\left(-\epsilon\right)}\right)+\mu\left(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\cdot\partial\mathcal{B}\cdot\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mu\left(\mathcal{B}^{\left(-\epsilon\right)}\right)+c\epsilon

and

\displaystyle\mu(\mathcal{B}^{\left(-\epsilon\right)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu\left(\mathcal{B}\setminus\left(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\cdot\partial\mathcal{B}\cdot\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mu\left(\mathcal{B}\right)-\mu\left(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\cdot\partial\mathcal{B}\cdot\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mu\left(\mathcal{B}\right)-c\epsilon

{}_{\left(\epsilon<\frac{\mu\left(\mathcal{B}\right)}{2c}\right)}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\mu\left(\mathcal{B}\right)}{2}

As a result, for
\epsilon<\frac{\mu\left(\mathcal{B}\right)}{2c}
,

\frac{\mu(\mathcal{B}^{\left(+\epsilon\right)})-\mu(\mathcal{B}^{\left(-\epsilon\right)})}{\mu(\mathcal{B}^{\left(-\epsilon\right)})}\leq\frac{c\epsilon}{\frac{1}{2}\mu\left(\mathcal{B}\right)}=\frac{2c}{\mu\left(\mathcal{B}\right)}\cdot\epsilon.

Regarding the opposite direction, our assumption is that for
\epsilon<C^{-1}
,

\frac{\mu(\mathcal{B}^{\left(+\epsilon\right)})-\mu(\mathcal{B}^{\left(-\epsilon\right)})}{\mu(\mathcal{B}^{\left(-\epsilon\right)})}\leq C\epsilon.

Hence,

\frac{\mu(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\cdot\partial\mathcal{B}\cdot\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon})}{\mu\left(\mathcal{B}\right)}\leq\frac{\mu(\mathcal{B}^{\left(+\epsilon\right)})-\mu(\mathcal{B}^{\left(-\epsilon\right)})}{\mu(\mathcal{B}^{\left(-\epsilon\right)})}\leq C\epsilon.

In other words,

\mu(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\cdot\partial\mathcal{B}\cdot\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon})\leq\mu\left(\mathcal{B}\right)C\epsilon.

∎

One consequence of Lemma
3.3
is that finite
unions and intersections of LWR sets are in themselves LWR.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
G
be a Lie
group with a Borel measure
\mu
. If two subsets
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}

of
G
such that
0<\mu\left(\mathcal{B}\cap\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\right)
are
LWR, then
\mathcal{B}\cap\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{B}\cup\mathcal{B}^{\prime}

are also LWR with Lipschitz constant

C_{\mathcal{B}\cap\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}=2\max\left\{C,C^{\prime}\right\}\cdot\frac{\mu\left(\mathcal{B}\right)+\mu\left(\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\right)}{\mu\left(\mathcal{B}\cap\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\right)};\,\,C_{\mathcal{B}\cup\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}=2\max\left\{C,C^{\prime}\right\}\cdot\frac{\mu\left(\mathcal{B}\right)+\mu\left(\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\right)}{\mu\left(\mathcal{B}\cup\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\right)}.

Proof.

We prove the lemma only for the intersection
\mathcal{B}\cap\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
;
the proof for the union
\mathcal{B}\cup\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
is similar. By
Lemma
3.3
, for
\epsilon<\frac{1}{C_{\mathcal{B}\cap\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}}

(so
\epsilon<C^{-1},C^{\prime-1}
):

\displaystyle\mu\left(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\cdot\partial\mathcal{B}\cdot\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\>\mu\left(\mathcal{B}\right)\epsilon\,,

\displaystyle\mu\left(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\cdot\partial\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\cdot\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C^{\prime}\mu\left(\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\right)\epsilon.

Hence, by using the fact that the boundary of an intersection is contained
in the union of the boundaries, we obtain that for
\epsilon<\frac{1}{C_{\mathcal{B}\cap\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\mu\left(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\cdot\partial\left(\mathcal{B}\cap\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\right)\cdot\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mu\left(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\cdot\partial\mathcal{B}\cdot\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\right)+\mu\left(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\cdot\partial\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\cdot\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{C,C^{\prime}\right\}\cdot\left(\mu\left(\mathcal{B}\right)+\mu\left(\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\right)\right)\cdot\epsilon

The first direction of Lemma
3.3
yields
the desired conclusion.
∎

Using Lemma
3.3
, which provides us with
an if and only if criterion for Lipschitz well roundedness of a fixed
set, we will now obtain that the sets with controlled boundary are
indeed LWR.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
G
be a Lie group. Assume
that
\mu
is a measure on
G
that is absolutely continuous w.r.t. Haar measure, and has density that is bounded on compact sets. If

\mathcal{B}
is a compact BCS with
\mu\left(\mathcal{B}\right)>0
, then
\mathcal{B}

is Lipchitz well-rounded.

Proof.

The strategy is to apply Lemma
3.3
. This
will be done by showing that for a subset
Y
of
G
which is compact
and consists of a finite union of subsets of embedded submanifolds
of strictly smaller dimension (e.g. the boundary of
\mathcal{B}
) there
exist
c=c\left(Y\right),\epsilon\left(Y\right)>0
such that

\mu\left(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}Y\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\right)\leq c\epsilon

(3.1)

for some
0<\epsilon<\epsilon\left(Y\right)
.

It is clearly sufficient to assume that
Y
is contained in one submanifold.
For each point
g\in Y
, there is some chart
\phi_{g}:U_{g}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}

for which
g\in U_{g}
and
\phi\left(U_{g}\cap Y\right)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m-1}\times\left\{0\right\}
.
Let
V_{g}
be the open sets from Proposition
2.5

which satisfy:
g\in V_{g}\subseteq U_{g}
. By compactness, there
are
g_{1},\dots,g_{r}\in Y
for which
V_{g_{1}},\dots,V_{g_{r}}

cover
Y
entirely. In order to establish the inequality in Formula
(
3.1
), it is sufficient to prove it for each
Y\cap\overline{V}_{g_{i}}

separately. Consequentially, we may assume that
r=1
:
g_{1}=g
,

V_{g_{1}}=V
,
Y_{0}=Y\cap\overline{V}
and
\phi_{g_{1}}=\phi
.

By Proposition
2.5
,
there exist
c\left(g\right),\epsilon\left(g\right)>0
such that for
\epsilon<\epsilon\left(g\right)

and
h\in\overline{V}
,
\phi\left(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}h\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\right)\subseteq\phi\left(h\right)+B_{c\left(g\right)\epsilon}
.
In particular

\phi\left(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}Y_{0}\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\right)\subseteq\phi\left(Y_{0}\right)+B_{c\left(g\right)\epsilon}.

Hence it is sufficient to show that
\phi_{*}\mu\left(\phi\left(Y_{0}\right)+B_{c\left(g\right)\epsilon}\right)\leq c\epsilon
.

Let
\omega\in L^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{m}\right)
be such that
\phi_{*}\mu=\omega\cdot\mu_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}

where
\mu_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}
is the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Then,
since
\omega
is bounded on compact sets (and in particular on
\phi\left(\overline{\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon\left(g\right)}Y_{0}\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon\left(g\right)}}\right)
),
it is sufficient to show that

\mu_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left(\phi\left(Y_{0}\right)+B_{\epsilon}\right)\leq c\epsilon.

Indeed, since
Y_{0}
is an embedded submanifold, there exists a
bounded set
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m-1}
such that
\phi\left(Y_{0}\right)+B_{\epsilon}\subseteq E\times\left[-c_{2}\epsilon,c_{2}\epsilon\right]
,
which implies the desired result.
∎

4
Roundomorphisms

Roughly speaking, the difficulty in checking well roundedness inside
a simple non compact Lie group arises from the fact that well roundedness
is a
multiplicative
property, while simple Lie groups are “highly
non-abelian”. Nevertheless, simple Lie groups have several known
decompositions — Cartan, Iwasawa, etc. — which allow them to
be written as the product of more “convenient” subgroups. E.g.,
in the case of the Iwasawa decomposition, the subgroups
K,A,N
are
compact, abelian and nilpotent respectively, which makes it considerably
easier to prove well roundedness inside them. The goal of this section
is to reduce the question of whether a family
\mathcal{B}_{T}\subset G

is LWR, to verifying LWR of the projections of
\mathcal{B}_{T}
to each
of the components of
G
w.r.t. a given decomposition. E.g. when
considering the Iwasawa decomposition, the well roundedness of
\mathcal{B}_{T}

is reduced to the question of well roundedness of the image of
\mathcal{B}_{T}

in the direct product
K\times A\times N
. This can be achieved
if

the Iwasawa diffeomorphism
G\to K\times A\times N
preserves well
roundedness; maps with this property are the topic of the following
definition.

Definition 4.1

(Roundomorphism)
.

Let
G
and
H
be two topological
groups with measures
\mu_{G}
and
\mu_{H}
, and let
\left(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}^{G}\right)_{\epsilon>0}

and
\left(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}^{H}\right)_{\epsilon>0}
be two families of identity
neighborhoods in
G
and
H
respectively. A Borel measurable
map
r:G\to H
will be called an
f
-roundomorphism

if it is:

1.

Measure preserving:

r_{*}\left(\mu_{G}\right)=\mu_{H}
.

2.

Locally Lipschitz:

r\left(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}^{G}g\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}^{G}\right)\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{f\epsilon}^{H}r(g)\mathcal{O}_{f\epsilon}^{H}

for some continuous
f=f\left(g\right):G\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and for every

0<\epsilon<\frac{1}{f}
.

The following proposition reveals the motivation for defining roundomorphisms,
as well as the reason they are called that way: they pull back LWR
families to LWR families.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
r:G\to H

be an
f
-roundomorphism. Assume that
\left\{\mathcal{B}_{T}\right\}_{T>0}

is a family of measurable subsets of
H
such that
f
is bounded
uniformly on
r^{-1}\left(\mathcal{B}_{T}\right)
by a constant
F
.
If
\left\{\mathcal{B}_{T}\right\}
is LWR with parameters
\left(T_{0},C_{0}\right)
,
then the pre-image
r^{-1}\left(\mathcal{B}_{T}\right)
is LWR with
parameters
\left(T_{0},F\cdot\max\left\{C_{0},1\right\}\right)
.

Proof.

The strategy of the proof is to show that for
\epsilon<F^{-1}
,

\mu_{G}\left(\left(r^{-1}\left(\mathcal{B}_{T}\right)\right)^{\left(+\epsilon\right)}\right)\leq\mu_{H}\left(\mathcal{B}_{T}^{\left(+F\epsilon\right)}\right)

(4.1)

and

\mu_{H}\left(\mathcal{B}_{T}^{\left(-F\epsilon\right)}\right)\leq\mu_{G}\left(\left(r^{-1}\left(\mathcal{B}_{T}\right)\right)^{\left(-\epsilon\right)}\right).

(4.2)

It will then follow that for
T>T_{0}
and
\epsilon<\frac{1}{F\cdot\max\left\{C_{0},1\right\}}

(so that both
\epsilon<F^{-1}
and
\epsilon<\left(FC_{0}\right)^{-1}
: the
first for inequalities (
4.1
) and (
4.2
)
to hold, and the second for the LWR of
\left\{\mathcal{B}_{T}\right\}
),

\frac{\mu_{G}\left(\left(r^{-1}\left(\mathcal{B}_{T}\right)\right)^{\left(+\epsilon\right)}\right)}{\mu_{G}\left(\left(r^{-1}\left(\mathcal{B}_{T}\right)\right)^{\left(-\epsilon\right)}\right)}\leq\frac{\mu_{H}\left(\mathcal{B}_{T}^{\left(+F\epsilon\right)}\right)}{\mu_{H}\left(\mathcal{B}_{T}^{\left(-F\epsilon\right)}\right)}\leq 1+FC_{0}\epsilon.

Inequalities (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) follow
from measure preservation of
r
, along with the following inclusions:

\displaystyle\left(r^{-1}\left(\mathcal{B}_{T}\right)\right)^{\left(+\epsilon\right)}\subseteq

\displaystyle r^{-1}\left(\mathcal{B}_{T}^{\left(+F\epsilon\right)}\right),

\displaystyle\left(r^{-1}\left(\mathcal{B}_{T}\right)\right)^{\left(-\epsilon\right)}\supseteq

\displaystyle r^{-1}\left(\mathcal{B}_{T}^{\left(-F\epsilon\right)}\right),

that we now justify. For the first, note that by definition of a roundomorphism,

\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}^{G}g\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}^{G}\subseteq r^{-1}\left(\mathcal{O}_{f\epsilon}^{H}r(g)\mathcal{O}_{f\epsilon}^{H}\right)
.
Hence,
\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}^{G}\cdot r^{-1}\left(\mathcal{B}_{T}\right)\cdot\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}^{G}\subseteq r^{-1}\left(\mathcal{O}_{F\epsilon}^{H}\mathcal{B}_{T}\mathcal{O}_{F\epsilon}^{H}\right)
.
For the second inclusion, suppose
g\in r^{-1}\left(\mathcal{B}_{T}^{\left(-F\epsilon\right)}\right)
.
We want to show that if
u,v\in\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}^{G}
, then
ugv\in r^{-1}\left(\mathcal{B}_{T}\right)
.
Put differently,
r\left(ugv\right)\in\mathcal{B}_{T}
. This is indeed
the case, since
r\left(ugv\right)=u^{\prime}r\left(g\right)v^{\prime}

for some
u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{O}_{F\epsilon}^{H}
(local Lipschitzity
of
r
), and
u^{\prime}r\left(g\right)v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{T}
since

r\left(g\right)\in\mathcal{B}_{T}^{\left(-F\epsilon\right)}
.
∎

The most useful incident of Proposition
4.2

is when
H
(such that
r:G\to H
is a roundomorphism)
is a direct product of groups. This is what allows
us to reduce (under certain conditions) well roundedness in the group

G
to well roundedness in the components of a decomposition of
G
.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
r:G\to H=H_{1}\times\cdots\times H_{q}

be an
f
-roundomorphism and let
\mathcal{B}_{T}=\mathcal{B}_{T}^{1}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{B}_{T}^{q}\subseteq H
.
Set

1.

\mu_{H}=\mu_{H_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mu_{H_{q}}

2.

\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}^{H}=\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}^{H_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}^{H_{q}}

and assume that:

1.

For
j=1,\dots,q
:
\mathcal{B}_{T}^{j}\subseteq H_{j}
is
LWR w.r.t. the parameters
\left(T_{j},C_{j}\right)
;

2.

f
is bounded uniformly by
F
on the sets
r^{-1}\left(\mathcal{B}_{T}\right)
.

Then
r^{-1}\left(\mathcal{B}_{T}\right)
is LWR, w.r.t. the parameters

T=\max\left\{T_{1},\dots,T_{q}\right\},\;C\asymp_{q}F\cdot\max\left\{C_{1},\dots,C_{q},1\right\}.

Proof.

It is sufficient to prove the claim for
q=2
, where one
then proceeds by induction. According to the previous proposition
we only need to show that
\mathcal{B}_{T}
is Lipchitz well-rounded w.r.t. the parameters
\left(T,C/F\right)
. Indeed, since

\mu_{H}\left(\mathcal{B}_{T}^{\left(\pm\epsilon\right)}\right)=\mu_{H_{1}}\left(\left(\mathcal{B}_{T}^{1}\right)^{\left(\pm\epsilon\right)}\right)\cdot\mu_{H_{2}}\left(\left(\mathcal{B}_{T}^{2}\right)^{\left(\pm\epsilon\right)}\right),

we obtain

\frac{\mu_{H}\left(\mathcal{B}_{T}^{\left(+\epsilon\right)}\right)}{\mu_{H}\left(\mathcal{B}_{T}^{\left(-\epsilon\right)}\right)}\leq\left(1+C_{1}\epsilon\right)\left(1+C_{2}\epsilon\right)\leq\left(1+\max\left\{C_{1},C_{2}\right\}\epsilon\right)^{2}\leq 1+3\max\left\{C_{1},C_{2}\right\}\epsilon

for
\epsilon\prec\frac{1}{\max\left\{C_{1},C_{2}\right\}}
.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

One consequence of Corollary
4.3

is that a direct product of LWR families

\mathcal{B}_{T}^{1}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{B}_{T}^{q}\subseteq H_{1}\times\cdots\times H_{q}

is LWR. To see this, take
G=H_{1}\times\cdots\times H_{q}

and
r
that is the identity map on
G
; it is a roundomorphism
with
f\equiv 1
.

The content of the following lemma is that a composition of roundomorphisms
is a roundomorphism.

Lemma 4.5
.

Suppose that
r_{1}:G_{1}\to G_{2}

is an
f_{1}
-roundomorpism and
r_{2}:G_{2}\to G_{3}
is an
f_{2}
-roundomorphism.
Then,
r_{2}\circ r_{1}
is an
f=\left(f_{2}\circ r_{1}\right)\cdot f_{1}
-roundomorphism.

Proof.

Clearly we only need to check that
r_{2}\circ r_{1}
is locally
Lipchitz:

r_{2}r_{1}\left(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}^{G_{1}}\cdot g\cdot\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}^{G_{1}}\right)\subseteq r_{2}\left(\mathcal{O}_{f_{1}\epsilon}^{G_{2}}\cdot r_{1}\left(g\right)\cdot\mathcal{O}_{f_{1}\epsilon}^{G_{2}}\right)\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{f\epsilon}^{G_{3}}\cdot r_{2}r_{1}\left(g\right)\cdot\mathcal{O}_{f\epsilon}^{G_{3}}.

∎

Finally, any smooth map from
G_{1}
to
G_{2}
such that
r_{*}\left(\mu_{G_{1}}\right)=\mu_{H_{2}}

is a roundomorphism:

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
be
Lie groups and
r:G_{1}\to G_{2}
a sooth map. Then
r
is locally
Lipschitz.

Proof.

Let
g\in G_{1}
with
\phi_{1}:U\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
a chart at
g
.
By Proposition
2.5
,
there is an open neighborhood
V\subset U
of
g
and
\epsilon_{0},c_{1}>0

such that for every
0<\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}
,

\phi_{1}\left(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}g\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\right)\subseteq\phi\left(g\right)+B_{c_{1}\epsilon}.

Let
L_{r(g)^{-1}}:G_{2}\to G_{2}
be the left translation by
r(g)^{-1}
,
and let
W
be an open neighborhood of
1_{G_{2}}
such that
\ln_{G_{2}}|_{W}

is a diffeomorphism onto an open neighborhood of
\mathfrak{g}_{2}
.
We may assume that
L_{r(g)^{-1}}\circ r(U)\subseteq W
. We get that
there is
c_{2}>0
such that for every
\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}
:

\ln_{G_{2}}\circ L_{r(g)^{-1}}\circ r\left(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}g\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\right)\subseteq\ln_{G_{2}}\circ L_{r(g)^{-1}}\circ r\circ\phi^{-1}\left(\phi\left(g\right)+B_{c_{1}\epsilon}\right)\subseteq B_{c_{2}\epsilon}.

As a result,

r\left(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}g\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\right)\subseteq r(g)\cdot\exp_{G_{2}}\left(B_{c_{2}\epsilon}\right).

∎

5
Well roundedness of fibered
families

In the previous section we have developed a machinery to establish
whether a fixed set
\mathcal{B}\subset G
is LWR (Proposition
3.5
),
and whether a family of the form
\left\{P_{T}^{\prime}Q_{T}^{\prime}\right\}

is LWR (Corollary
4.3
), where
P_{T}^{\prime}\subset P
,

Q_{T}^{\prime}\subset Q
and
G=PQ
is a decomposition of
G

into subgroups
P
and
Q
. The latter applies for any number of
components in the decomposition, but for brevity we wrote it here
with two components only. In this section we will extend our machinery
to handle families of sets with the more complicated structure of
a fiber product, namely sets of the form
\cup_{z\in P_{T}^{\prime}}Q_{z}^{\prime}
,
where again
P_{T}^{\prime}\subset P
,
Q_{z}^{\prime}\subset Q

and
G=PQ
. The tool of roundomorphisms allows us to reduce to well
roundedness in
P
and in
Q
separately, namely to work in
P\times Q
,
which is what we will do.

We start by formulating a regularity condition on the fibers in
Q
.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
P
and
H
be Lie groups and
\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}^{P}

and
\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}^{H}
families of coordinate balls. Let
\mathcal{E}
be
a subset of
P
, and consider the family
\mathcal{\mathscr{D}}_{\mathcal{E}}=\left\{{\cal D}_{z}\right\}_{z\in\mathcal{E}}
,
where
{\cal D}_{z}\subseteq H
. We say that the family
\mathcal{\mathscr{D}}_{\mathcal{E}}

is
bounded Lipschitz continuous
(or
BLC
) w.r.t
\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}^{P}

and
\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}^{H}
and with parameters
\left(C_{\mathscr{D}},V_{\text{min}},B\right)
,
where
C_{\mathscr{D}},V_{\text{min}}
are positive real numbers and
B
is
a bounded subset of
H
, if for every
0<\epsilon<C_{\mathscr{D}}^{-1}
the
following hold:

1.

Every
D_{z}
is LWR with parameters
\left(C_{\mathscr{D}},1/C_{\mathscr{D}}\right)
.

2.

If
z^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}^{P}z\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}^{P}
for
z,z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}
,
then
\mathcal{D}_{z}^{\left(-C_{\mathscr{D}}\epsilon\right)}\subseteq{\cal D}_{z^{\prime}}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{z}^{\left(+C_{\mathscr{D}}\epsilon\right)}
.

3.

The volume of
{\cal D}_{z}
(w.r.t. a Haar measure of
H
) is bounded
uniformly from below by a positive constant
V_{\text{min}}
.

4.

{\cal D}_{z}\subseteq B
for some bounded set
B
and every
z\in\mathcal{E}
.

For convenience, we will always assume WLOG that
C_{\mathscr{D}}\geq 1
.

The following proposition and corollary are concerned with certain
manipulations that can be performed on fibered sets, while maintaining
the BLC property of the fibers. These manipulations include pulling
back the fibers by a locally-Lipschitz map, and enlarging the basis
set by taking a product with another set.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
P,P_{0}
be Lie groups
and suppose that
r:P_{0}\to P
is an
f
-locally Lipschitz map
(Definition
4.1
). Let
\mathcal{E}\subseteq P

and
\mathcal{\mathcal{E}}_{0}:=r^{-1}\left(\mathcal{E}\right)\subseteq P_{0}
.
If the family

\mathscr{D}_{\mathcal{E}}=\left\{{\cal D}_{z}\right\}_{z\in\mathcal{E}}

is BLC with parameters
\left(C,V_{\text{min}},B\right)
, then the family

\mathscr{D}_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}=\left\{{\cal D}_{r\left(z_{0}\right)}\right\}_{z_{0}\in\mathcal{E}_{0}}

is BLC with parameters
\left(FC,V_{\text{min}},B\right)
, where
F=\sup_{g\in r^{-1}\left(\mathcal{E}\right)}f(g)<\infty
.

Proof.

Since
\mathscr{D}_{\mathcal{\mathcal{E}}_{0}}\subset\mathscr{D}_{\mathcal{E}}
,
properties 1, 3, and 4 of BLC hold automatically in
\mathscr{D}_{\mathcal{\mathcal{E}}_{0}}
,
and it is only left to verify the second property. Indeed, if
z_{0}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}^{P_{0}}z_{0}\,\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}^{P_{0}}
,
then by local Lipschitzity and definition of
F
,
r\left(z_{0}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathcal{O}_{F\epsilon}^{P}r\left(z_{0}\right)\mathcal{O}_{F\epsilon}^{P}
.
Since
\mathscr{D}_{\mathcal{E}}
is BLC then for
\epsilon\leq\frac{1}{FC}

we obtain

{\cal D}_{r\left(z_{0}^{\prime}\right)}^{\left(-C\epsilon\right)}\subseteq{\cal D}_{r\left(z_{0}\right)}\subseteq{\cal D}_{r\left(z_{0}^{\prime}\right)}^{\left(+C\epsilon\right)}.

∎

Corollary 5.3
.

Let
P\times Q
be a product of Lie
groups and let
\mathcal{E}\subseteq P
,
\mathcal{\mathcal{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}}=\mathcal{E}\times Q
.
If
\mathscr{D}_{\mathcal{E}}=\left\{{\cal D}_{z}\right\}_{z\in\mathcal{E}}

is BLC w.r.t.
\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}^{P}
, then

\mathscr{D}_{\mathcal{E^{\prime}}}=\left\{{\cal D}_{\left(z,q\right)}\right\}_{\left(z,q\right)\in\mathcal{E^{\prime}}}\text{ such that ${\cal D}_{\left(z,q\right)}={\cal D}_{z}\>\forall q\in Q$}

is BLC with the same parameters and w.r.t.
\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}^{P}\times\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}^{Q}
.

Remark 5.4
.

Clearly we can replace the group
Q
in the definition of
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}

with any subset
\mathcal{B}\subseteq Q
.

Proof.

This follows from Proposition
5.2

using the projection map

r:P\times Q\to P

which is an
f
-local Lipschitz map with
f\equiv 1
.
∎

We now turn to the concluding result of this section:

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
\left\{\mathcal{E}_{T}\right\}_{T>0}

be an increasing family inside a Lie group
P
, and
\mathcal{E}:=\cup_{T>0}\mathcal{E}_{T}
.
Let
\mathscr{D}_{\mathcal{E}}=\left\{{\cal D}_{z}\right\}_{z\in\mathcal{E}}

where
{\cal D}_{z}\subset H
, and consider the family

\mathcal{B}_{T}=\bigcup_{z\in\mathcal{E}_{T}}z\times{\cal D}_{z}\subseteq P\times H.

If
\left\{\mathcal{E}_{T}\right\}_{T>0}
is LWR with parameters
\left(T_{0},C_{\mathcal{E}}\right)
,
and
\mathcal{\mathscr{D}}_{\mathcal{E}}
is BLC w.r.t. a family
\left\{\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}^{P},\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}^{H}\right\}_{\epsilon>0}

of coordinate balls and with parameters
\left(C_{\mathcal{\mathscr{D}}},V_{\text{min}},B\right)
,
then
\mathcal{B}_{T}
is LWR w.r.t the coordinate balls
\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}^{P}\times\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}^{H}\subset P\times H

and with parameters

6
Relation between fundamental domains and
quotient spaces

As mentioned in the Introduction, this note is meant to support our
work on equidistribution in various lattice spaces. All of these spaces
are of the form
\mathcal{M}/H
, where
\mathcal{M}
is a manifold
and
H
is a Lie group acting on it. We are interested in Boundary
Controllable Sets (and Bounded Lipschitz Continuous families of sets)
in these spaces, which is a differential property; as such, it is
easier to check it in concrete manifolds, than in abstract spaces.
This bring up the need in finding a set of representatives inside

\mathcal{M}
for the action of
H
, such that one can move the question
of verifying the BCS property from the space
\mathcal{M}/H
to this
subset of
\mathcal{M}
. In the case where
H
is discrete, one can
think of this desired set of representatives as a “nice” fundamental
domain in
\mathcal{M}
(see examples below). The precise definition
is the following:

Definition 6.1
.

Let
H
be a Lie group acting smoothly,
freely and properly on a manifold
\mathcal{M}
, and let
\pi:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}/H

denote the associated quotient map. A full set of representatives

F\subset\mathcal{M}
for
\mathcal{M}/H
is called a
spread model

for the quotient space
\mathcal{M}/H
if the following conditions
are met:

1.

F
is contained in a finite union of embedded submanifolds
\cup_{\alpha}V_{\alpha}

of
\mathcal{M}
such that the natural projection
\pi_{\alpha}^{0}:V_{\alpha}\to\mathcal{M}/H_{0}

(here
H_{0}
denotes the connected component of
H
) is an open
diffeomorphism onto its image;

2.

for each
\alpha
, there exists an open set (w.r.t.
V_{\alpha}
)

F_{\alpha}\subseteq F\cap V_{\alpha}
such that
\overline{F}\cap V_{\alpha}\subset\overline{F_{\alpha}}
;

3.

F_{\alpha}
is BCS w.r.t.
V_{\alpha}
. and

4.

the quotient map restricted to
\overline{F}
is proper, namely it
pulls back compact sets to compact sets.

We will denote
F\overset{{}_{\text{s.m.}}}{\simeq}\mathcal{M}/H
.

Remark 6.2
.

One can extend definition also to the case where
H
acts
almost
freely
(i.e. the point stabilizer subgroups are finite) on
\mathcal{M}

by considering the open submanifold
\mathcal{M}_{free}
on which
the action is free. In that case we add the extra condition that

F\subseteq\mathcal{M}
is a spread model if
F\cap\mathcal{M}_{free}

is a spread model for the action of
H
on
\mathcal{M}_{free}
.

Remark 6.3
.

If
H=\Gamma
is a discrete Lie
group, then the conditions in Definition
6.1

are satisfied when:
\Gamma
acts properly and almost freely on

\mathcal{M}
;
F\subseteq\overline{\operatorname{int}\left(F\right)}
; the boundary of

F
is contained in a finite union of lower dimensional submanifolds
of
\mathcal{M}
and the quotient map restricted to
\overline{F}

is proper. This is indeed the case since the quotient map
\pi:\mathcal{M}_{free}\to\mathcal{M}_{free}/\Gamma

restricted to
\operatorname{int}\left(F\right)
is a diffeomorphism.

Example 6.4
.

Let us mention some examples (an explanation follows).

1.

If
\Lambda
is a lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then any fundamental
domain of
\Lambda
which is a convex polygon with a finite number of
edges, is a spread model for
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\Lambda
. In particular, its fundamental
parallelepiped and its Dirichlet domain are spread models.

2.

If
\Gamma
is a discrete group acting properly discontinuously and
freely on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
by isometries, then any locally finite
fundamental polygon
F
is a spread model. In particular, the (generic)
Dirchilet domain of a Fuchsian group is a spread model.

3.

Suppose that
G=H\cdot P
, is a product of two closed subgroups having
trivial intersection. Then
P
is a spread model for the action of

H
. In particular, the group of upper triangular matrices in
\operatorname{SL}_{n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)

is a spread model for the symmetric space
\operatorname{SO}_{n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\backslash\operatorname{SL}_{n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
,
and a minimal parabolic group inside a non-compact simple algebraic
rank one Lie group is a spread model for the associated hyperbolic
space. E.g.  the upper triangular matrices in
\operatorname{SL}_{2}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)

(resp.
\operatorname{SL}_{2}\left(\mathbb{C}\right)
) form a spread model for the real
hyperbolic plane (resp. 3-space).

Indeed, we use Remark
6.3
to justify
the first two examples. All the conditions are easily seen to be satisfied
except that
\pi|_{\overline{F}}
is proper in the second example.
We check it here: suppose that
a_{n}\to\infty
in
\overline{F}
,
but modulo
\Gamma
, the set
\left\{\Gamma a_{n}\right\}
is
bounded. As a result, we may assume that
\gamma_{n}.a_{n}\to a

for some
\gamma_{n}\in\Gamma
. Let
K
be a compact neighborhood
of
a
. We have that
K\cap\gamma_{n}\overline{F}\neq\emptyset

for all
n
. Since
F
is locally finite, we may assume that
\gamma_{n}=\gamma

for all
n
. This is however an absurd, since we must have that
\gamma.a_{n}\to\infty
.

To check example 3 we use the original definition and notice that
we may choose
V_{\alpha}=F_{\alpha}=P
(only a single
\alpha
)
and since
P=\overline{P}
is diffeomorphic via
\pi
to
H\backslash G

and the boundary of
F_{\alpha}
w.r.t.
V_{\alpha}
is trivial,
we are done.

6.1
BCS’s in space and its spread model correspond

We start by claiming that a BCS in the spread model projects modulo

H
to a BCS in the space
\mathcal{M}/H
.

Proposition 6.5
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{M}

is a manifold and
\Gamma
is a discrete group acting on
\mathcal{M}

freely, properly and smoothly. Let

be a spread

The proof requires a lemma.

.

Let

1) Let

and a point of

. As a result,

We need to show that

U
of

Since

and

U\cap W

is a neighborhood of
b

b

B

A

B

Since

for a compact

we have that

\pi^{-1}|_{\overline{F}}\left(B\right)=\cup_{\alpha}\pi^{-1}|_{\overline{F}}\left(B\cap B_{\alpha}\right)=\cup_{\alpha}\theta_{\alpha}^{-1}\left(\left(B\cap B_{\alpha}\right)\times H\right)\cap\overline{F}=\cup_{\alpha}\theta_{\alpha}^{-1}\left(\left(B\cap B_{\alpha}\right)\times 1_{H}\right),

which is clearly compact.
∎

Remark 6.13
.

It is clear from the proof
that if
K^{\prime\prime}<K_{1}^{\prime\prime}
are closed Lie subgroups of
a Lie group
K
such that
K/K^{\prime\prime}
is compact, then one can
find
F_{1}\subset F
such that
F
is a spread model of
K
w.r.t.
K^{\prime\prime}
and
F_{1}
is a spread model of
K
w.r.t.
K_{1}^{\prime\prime}
.

Corollary 6.14
.

Let

K
be a compact Lie group and let
\Gamma<K
be a lattice (i.e.
a finite subgroup). There exists a fundamental domain
\mathcal{\mathcal{K}}\subseteq K

for
\Gamma
which is BCS.

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of Proposition
6.11
.
∎

6.4
Manipulations on spread models

The following result allows us to “compose” spread models in the
sense that a spread model for
\mathcal{M}/G
“times” a spread
model for
G/H
is a spread model for
\mathcal{M}/H
.

Proposition 6.15
.

Suppose that a Lie group
G

acts smoothly, freely and properly on a manifold
\mathcal{M}
. Let

H
be a closed subgroup of
G
.

1.

If
F^{\mathcal{M}}
is a spread model of
\mathcal{M}/G
in
\mathcal{M}

and
F^{G}
is a spread model of
G/H
in
G
(considered as a
manifold), then
F=F^{\mathcal{M}}\cdot F^{G}
is a spread model of

\mathcal{M}/H
in
\mathcal{M}
.

2.

If
B^{\mathcal{M}/G}\subset\mathcal{M}/G
and
B^{G/H}\subset G/H

are BCS’s, then so does

B^{\mathcal{M}/H}:=\pi_{\mathcal{M}/H}^{\mathcal{M}}\left(\mathcal{B}^{\mathcal{M}}\cdot\mathcal{B}^{G}\right)\subset\mathcal{M}/H,

where
\mathcal{B}^{\mathcal{M}},\mathcal{B}^{G}
are the representatives
in
F^{\mathcal{M}},F^{G}
of
B^{\mathcal{M}/G},B^{G/H}
respectively.

3.

If
\mu_{G/H},\mu_{\mathcal{M}/G},\mu_{\mathcal{M}/H}
are the measures
appearing in Proposition
6.10
, then

\mu_{\mathcal{M}/H}\left(B^{\mathcal{M}/H}\right)=\mu_{\mathcal{M}/G}\left(B^{\mathcal{M}/G}\right)\mu_{G/H}\left(B^{G/H}\right).

Proof.

We begin with the
first part
. Let
F_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{M}}\subseteq V_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{M}}\subseteq\mathcal{M}

and
F_{\beta}^{G}\subseteq V_{\beta}^{G}\subseteq G
be as in the
definition of a spread model. The sets
V_{\alpha\beta}=V_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{M}}V_{\beta}^{G}

are embedded manifolds as they are the diffeomorphic image of
V_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{M}}\times V_{\beta}^{G}

under the map
\theta_{\alpha}^{-1}
, which is defined on an open
submanifold of
\mathcal{M}
(see Lemma
6.8
). Moreover,
the composition of the diffeomorphisms

\textstyle{V_{\alpha\beta}\times H_{0}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\theta_{\alpha}^{-1}\times id_{H_{0}}}
\textstyle{V_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{M}}\times V_{\beta}^{G}\times H_{0}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{id_{V_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{M}}}\times\theta_{\beta}}
\textstyle{V_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{M}}\times V_{\beta}^{G}H_{0}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\theta_{\alpha}}
\textstyle{V_{\alpha\beta}H_{0}}

is a diffeomorphism, and it is
\theta_{\alpha\beta}
. Since
V_{\beta}^{G}H_{0}

is open in
G
, then
V_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{M}}\times V_{\beta}^{G}H_{0}

is open in
V_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{M}}\times G
; so
V_{\alpha\beta}

is open in
\mathcal{M}
as the image of the open map
\theta_{\alpha}
.
The first property of a spread model is now established.

Let
F_{\alpha\beta}=F_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{M}}F_{\beta}^{G}=\theta_{\alpha\beta}(F_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{M}}\times F_{\beta}^{G})
.
Since
\theta_{\alpha\beta}
is open
F_{\alpha\beta}
is also open.

\overline{F_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{M}}}\subseteq V_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{M}}

and
\overline{F_{\beta}^{G}}\subseteq V_{\beta}^{\mathcal{M}}
, hence

\theta_{\alpha\beta}(\overline{F_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{M}}}\times\overline{F_{\beta}^{G}})=\overline{F_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{M}}}\cdot\overline{F_{\beta}^{G}}\subseteq\overline{F_{\alpha\beta}}
.
As
\theta_{\alpha\beta}^{-1}
is continuous we conclude that
\overline{F_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{M}}}\cdot\overline{F_{\beta}^{G}}

is closed and hence
\overline{F_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{M}}}\cdot\overline{F_{\beta}^{G}}=\overline{F_{\alpha\beta}}
.
Furthermore,

\cup_{\alpha,\beta}\overline{F_{\alpha\beta}}=\cup_{\alpha,\beta}\overline{F_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{M}}}\cdot\overline{F_{\beta}^{G}}=(\cup_{\alpha}\overline{F_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{M}}})\cdot(\cup_{\beta}\overline{F_{\beta}^{G}})=\overline{F^{\mathcal{M}}}\cdot\overline{F^{G}}\supseteq F.

Note for future use that this shows that
\overline{F^{\mathcal{M}}}\cdot\overline{F^{G}}

is a closed set, hence
\overline{F^{\mathcal{M}}}\cdot\overline{F^{G}}\supseteq\overline{F}

(in fact we get an equality).

We check the injectivity of
\pi|_{F_{\alpha\beta}}
. Suppose that

\pi(p_{1}^{\mathcal{M}}p_{1}^{G})=\pi(p_{2}^{\mathcal{M}}p_{2}^{G})
,
or in other words,
p_{1}^{\mathcal{M}}p_{1}^{G}H=p_{2}^{\mathcal{M}}p_{2}^{G}H
.
Hence modulo
G
we have that
p_{1}^{\mathcal{M}}G=p_{2}^{\mathcal{M}}G
.
Since the projection to
\mathcal{M}/G
is injective when restricted
to
F_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{M}}
, then
p_{1}^{\mathcal{M}}=p_{2}^{\mathcal{M}}
.
Since the
G
action on
\mathcal{M}
is free, we conclude that

p_{1}^{G}H=p_{2}^{G}H
– but this forces
p_{1}^{G}=p_{2}^{G}
,
by the injectivity of the projection to
G/H
restricted to
F_{\beta}^{G}
.

Next we show that
\partial_{V_{\alpha\beta}}F_{\alpha\beta}
is contained
in a finite union of lower dimensional submanifolds of
\mathcal{M}
.
This follows from the analogous assumptions on
F_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{M}}

and
F_{\beta}^{G}
, along with the following:

\partial_{V_{\alpha\beta}}F_{\alpha\beta}=\left((id_{V_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{M}}}\times\theta_{\beta}^{-1})\circ\theta_{\alpha}^{-1}\right)\left(\partial(F_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{M}}\times F_{\beta}^{G})\times\left\{e\right\}\right).

Now we show that
\pi|_{\overline{F}}
is a proper map. Suppose that

p_{n}\to\infty
, where
p_{n}\in\overline{F}
. Since
\overline{F}=\overline{F^{\mathcal{M}}}\cdot\overline{F^{G}}
,
we may decompose
p_{n}=p_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}p_{n}^{G}
. Assume by
contradiction that
p_{n}H
does not tend to infinity. Consequentially,
there exist
h_{n_{k}}\in H
and
p\in\mathcal{M}
such that
p_{n_{k}}h_{n_{k}}\to p
.
For convenience we will write instead that
p_{n}h_{n}\to p
. Since

\overline{F}\subseteq\overline{F^{\mathcal{M}}}G\simeq\overline{F^{\mathcal{M}}}\times G
,
we can find some
\alpha
such that, for almost every
n
,
p_{n}h_{n}\in V_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{M}}G
.
We clearly have
p_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}\to p^{\mathcal{M}}
and
p_{n}^{G}h_{n}\to p^{G}
,
where
p=p^{\mathcal{M}}p^{G}
. Since
G
is assumed to act properly
on
\mathcal{M}
, we conclude from
p_{n}\to\infty
that
p_{n}^{G}\to\infty
.
However, the restriction to
F^{G}
of the projection
G\to G/H

is assumed to be proper, so we get a contradiction with
p_{n}^{G}h_{n}\to p^{G}
.

Finally, we need to check that
F
is a full set of representatives.
Let
x\in\mathcal{M}/H
and set
x^{\prime}=xG\in\mathcal{M}/G
. There
is
p^{\mathcal{M}}\in F^{\mathcal{M}}
which projects to
x^{\prime}
.
Since
p^{\mathcal{M}}H
and
x
both project to
x^{\prime}
,
there is some
g\in G
such that
p^{\mathcal{M}}gH=x
. By definition
of
F^{G}
, there is
p^{G}\in F^{G}
which projects to
gH
. As
a result,
p^{\mathcal{M}}p^{G}H=x
i.e.
\pi\left(p^{\mathcal{M}}p^{G}\right)=x
.

We turn to the
second part
of the Proposition. We know from
the first part that
\pi_{\mathcal{M}/G}^{\mathcal{M}}\left(F_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{M}}\right)

and
\pi_{G/H}^{G}\left(F_{\beta}^{G}\right)
are open submanifolds
of
\mathcal{M}/G
and
G/H
respectively, and that the maps

\rho_{\alpha\beta}:\pi_{\mathcal{M}/G}^{\mathcal{M}}\left(F_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{M}}\right)\times\pi_{GH}^{G}\left(F_{\beta}^{G}\right)\to F_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{M}}\times F_{\beta}^{G}\to F_{\alpha\beta}\to\mathcal{M}/H

are open diffeomorphisms onto their image. Denote
L_{\alpha\beta}:=\overline{\pi_{\mathcal{M}/G}^{\mathcal{M}}\left(F_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{M}}\right)}\times\overline{\pi_{GH}^{G}\left(F_{\beta}^{G}\right)}
.
Similarly to the proof of Proposition
6.5
,
we split each

B_{\alpha\beta}:=\left(B^{\mathcal{M}/G}\times B^{G/H}\right)\cap L_{\alpha\beta}

into two parts:
B\cap\partial L_{\alpha\beta}
and
B_{\text{int}}:=B\cap L_{\alpha\beta}^{\circ}
.
Since, by part (2) of Lemma
6.6
,

\partial\rho_{\alpha\beta}\left(B\right)\subseteq\rho_{\alpha\beta}\left(\partial L_{\alpha\beta}\right)\cup\partial_{\rho_{\alpha\beta}\left(L_{\alpha\beta}^{\circ}\right)}\rho_{\alpha\beta}\left(B_{\text{int}}\right),

it is enough to show that
\rho_{\alpha\beta}\left(B_{\text{int}}\right)

is BCS inside
\rho_{\alpha\beta}\left(L_{\alpha\beta}^{\circ}\right)

and that locally
\rho_{\alpha\beta}\left(\partial L_{\alpha\beta}\right)

is contained in a finite union of codimension
\geq 1
submanifolds.

The first statement is clear since
\rho_{\alpha\beta}|_{L_{\alpha\beta}^{\circ}}

is an open diffeomorphism onto its image
\rho_{\alpha\beta}\left(L_{\alpha\beta}^{\circ}\right)
.

For the second statement, first notice that

\rho_{\alpha\beta}\left(\partial L_{\alpha\beta}\right)=\pi_{\mathcal{M}/H}^{\mathcal{M}/H_{0}}\circ\pi_{\mathcal{M}/H_{0}}^{\mathcal{M}}\left(\theta_{\alpha}^{-1}\left(\partial_{V_{\alpha}\times V_{\beta}}\left(F_{\alpha}\times F_{\beta}\right)\right)\right).

By assumption,
\partial_{V_{\alpha}\times V_{\beta}}\left(F_{\alpha}\times F_{\beta}\right)

is locally contained in a finite union of codimension
\geq 1
submanifolds
(w.r.t.
V_{\alpha}\times V_{\beta}
). Hence, using the fact that

\pi_{\mathcal{M}/H_{0}}^{\mathcal{M}}\circ\theta_{\alpha}^{-1}
is an

\pi_{\mathcal{M}/H_{0}}^{\mathcal{M}}\left(\theta_{\alpha}^{-1}\left(\partial_{V_{\alpha}\times V_{\beta}}\left(F_{\alpha}\times F_{\beta}\right)\right)\right)

is also locally contained in a finite union of codimension
\geq 1

submanifolds w.r.t.
\mathcal{M}/H_{0}

\pi_{\mathcal{M}/H}^{\mathcal{M}/H_{0}}

It is left to prove the

of the proposition. Let

be the measures appearing in

, and assume

be a compact neighborhood of

so that
0<\mu_{H}\left(A\right)<\infty

By Proposition

we have the following

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\mathcal{B}^{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{B}^{G}A\right)

\displaystyle=\mu_{G}\left(\mathcal{B}^{G}A\right)\mu_{\mathcal{M}/G}\left(B^{\mathcal{M}/G}\right),

\displaystyle\mu_{G}\left(\mathcal{B}^{G}A\right)=

\displaystyle\mu_{G/H}\left(B^{G/H}\right)\mu_{H}\left(A\right),

\displaystyle=\mu_{H}\left(A\right)\mu_{\mathcal{M}/H}\left(B^{\mathcal{M}/H}\right).

It follows that

\mu_{\mathcal{M}/H}\left(B^{\mathcal{M}/H}\right)=\mu_{\mathcal{M}/G}\left(B^{\mathcal{M}/G}\right)\mu_{G/H}\left(B^{G/H}\right).

∎

The content of the following proposition is that if a space can be
written as a quotient in two ways,
\mathcal{M}/G
and
\mathcal{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}/H
,
where
\mathcal{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{M}
and

,
then a spread model for the latter a also a spread model for the first.

Proposition 6.16
.

Suppose
that
\mathcal{M}
is a manifold and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
is an
embedded closed submanifold of
\mathcal{M}
. Suppose that
G
is
a Lie group and
H
is a closed subgroup of
G
satisfying

.
Assume that
G

\mathcal{M}
and
H
is stabilizes

;

\iota:\mathcal{M}^{\prime}/H\to\mathcal{M}/G

is a spread model of

\mathcal{M}/G

in

.

Let
V_{\alpha},F_{\alpha}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{\prime}

modulo

.

7
Construction of fundamental domains for
\operatorname{SL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)

The goal of this section is to recall a construction for fundamental
domains of
\operatorname{SL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)
inside
\operatorname{SL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)

and inside
\operatorname{SO}_{m}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\backslash\operatorname{SL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
.
The motivation for this is that in the next section we will see that
these fundamental domains are spread models for two spaces of lattices
that we now turn to describe.

Let
\Lambda
be a (full rank) lattice inside
\mathbb{R}^{m}
having the
columns of
M\in\operatorname{GL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
as an ordered basis. It
is well known that any other basis of
\Lambda
appears as the columns
of a matrix obtained by multiplying
M
from the right by a matrix
from
\operatorname{GL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)
. As a result, the space of
m
-lattices
can be defined as
\operatorname{GL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)/\operatorname{GL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)
.
One can also consider a more crude space which is the space of
shapes

of lattices: two lattices
\Lambda_{1}=M_{1}\cdot\operatorname{GL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)

and
\Lambda_{2}=M_{2}\cdot\operatorname{GL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)
have the same
shape if
\Lambda_{1}
differs from
\Lambda_{2}
by an orthogonal
transformation and rescaling, namely there are
k\in\operatorname{O}_{m}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)

and
c>0
such that
ckM_{1}\cdot\operatorname{GL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)=M_{2}\cdot\operatorname{GL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)
.
As a result, the space of shapes can be defined as

\mathcal{X}_{m}=\operatorname{PO}_{m}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\backslash\operatorname{PGL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)/\operatorname{PGL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)\backsimeq\operatorname{SO}_{m}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\backslash\operatorname{SL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)/\operatorname{SL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right).

Notice that in the right hand side we consider unimodular lattices
(i.e. lattices with covolume
1
), since clearly every lattice can
be rescaled to a unimodular lattice. We let

\mathcal{L}_{m}:=\operatorname{SL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)/\operatorname{SL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)

denote the space of unimodular latices in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

In Subsection
7.1
we introduce a variant
of Siegel sets inside
\operatorname{SL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
, which contain a finite
number of representatives from every (right) orbit of
\operatorname{SL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)
,
and therefore a fundamental domain; in Subsection
7.2

we define the fundamntal domain
\widetilde{F_{m}}\subset\operatorname{SL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)

inside the Siegel set, as well as the resulting fundamental domain

F_{m}
inside
\operatorname{SO}_{m}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\backslash\operatorname{SL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
,
or more precisely in
P_{m}
, the group of upper triangular matrices
of determinant
1
with positive diagonal entries; indeed the Iwasawa
decomposition of
\operatorname{SL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
implies that
\operatorname{SO}_{m}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\backslash\operatorname{SL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)

is diffeomorphic to
P_{m}
.

Let us stress on the fact that the construction for
F_{m}

is not new (
[
Sch98
]
,
[
Gre93
]
), but we bring
it here for completeness, as well as for adding the way to obtain

\widetilde{F_{m}}
from
F_{m}
.

7.1
Reduced bases

Write
P_{m}=A_{m}N_{m}
where
A_{m}
is the subgroup of diagonal
matrices, and
N_{m}
the subgroup of unipotent matrices.

Let
\Lambda
be a lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
(not necessarily unimodular).
We describe an inductive method to construct an ordered basis
\left\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\right\}

for
\Lambda
as follows. Let
v_{1}
be a shortest nonzero element
of
\Lambda
. For future reference, we denote its length by
a_{1}

and its direction
v_{1}/a_{1}
by
\phi_{1}
. Next, write
V_{1}

for
\mbox{span}_{\mathbb{R}}(v_{1})_{\mathbb{R}}
and consider the projection of
\Lambda

to
V_{1}^{\perp}
, which is a lattice of dimension
m-1
. One can
find a vector
v_{2}\in\Lambda
whose projection to
V_{1}^{\perp}

is of nonzero minimal length
a_{2}
. Since actually all the elements
in
\left\{v_{2}+nv_{1}:n\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}
share this property of
having their projection to
V_{1}^{\perp}
be of length
a_{2}
,
we may assume that
v_{2}
also satisfies that its projection to

V_{1}
is
n_{1,2}a_{1}\cdot\phi_{1}
with
\left|n_{1,2}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}
.
We proceed by induction:

Definition 7.1

(
and notations
)
.

A
Reduced
basis for a lattice

\Lambda
is a basis
\left\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\right\}
in which
for all
j\in\left\{1,\ldots,m\right\}
, the basis element
v_{j}

is chosen such that:

1.

The projection of
v_{j}
to
V_{j-1}^{\perp}=(\mbox{span}_{\mathbb{R}}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{j-1}))^{\perp}

has minimal non-zero length
a_{j}
(here
V_{0}=\left\{0\right\}
);
denote this projection by
a_{j}\phi_{j}
, where
\phi_{j}
is a
unit vector.

2.

The projection of
v_{j}
to
V_{j-1}=\mbox{span}_{\mathbb{R}}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{j-1})=\mbox{span}_{\mathbb{R}}(\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{j-1})

is

\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\underset{\mbox{scalars}}{\underbrace{\left(n_{i,j}a_{i}\right)}}\phi_{i}\mbox{ with $\left|n_{ij}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}\;\,$for all $i=1,\ldots,j-1$}.

The matrix
M=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}v_{1}&\cdots&v_{m}\end{array}\right]
is called a
reduced matrix
of
\Lambda
.

We note that in the case of unimodular bases (bases of co-volume
1
),
one may need to replace
v_{1}
by
-v_{1}
in order for the reduced
matrix
M
to have determinant
1
(and not
-1
).

The parameters
\left\{a_{j}\right\}
,
\left\{n_{i,j}\right\}

and
\left\{\phi_{j}\right\}
involved in the process of constructing
a reduced basis
\left\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\right\}
are interpreted
via the
KAN
coordinates of the associated reduced matrix as follows.
Let

a=\operatorname{diag}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}),\;k=\left[\begin{matrix}\phi_{1}&\cdots&\phi_{m}\end{matrix}\right]

and

n=\begin{pmatrix}1&n_{1,1}&\dots&n_{1,m}\\
&1&&\vdots\\
&&\ddots&n_{m-1,m}\\
&&&1\end{pmatrix}.

Then, since the
i
-th column of
ka
is
a\phi_{i}=
the projection
of
v_{i}
to
V_{i-1}^{\perp}
, and the
i
-th column of
n

is exactly the coordinates of
v_{i}
w.r.t. the orthogonal set

\left\{a_{1}\phi_{1},\dots,a_{m}\phi_{m}\right\}
, we obtain that
the reduced matrix is

M=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}v_{1}&\cdots&v_{m}\end{array}\right]=kan.

Lemma 7.2
.

Suppose
M=k_{{}_{M}}a_{{}_{M}}n_{{}_{M}}

is -reduced w.r.t. some lattice
\Lambda
, where
k_{{}_{M}}
,
a_{{}_{M}}

and
n_{{}_{M}}
are as above.

1.

n_{M}
is
a unipotent upper triangular matrix whose entries are bounded in
\left[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right]

(in particular,
\|n_{{}_{M}}^{\pm 1}\|,\|n_{{}_{M}}^{\pm\mbox{t}}\|\prec 1
).

2.

a_{{}_{M}}=\operatorname{diag}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})

is a diagonal matrix which satisfies that
a_{1}\prec\cdots\prec a_{m}
.
Specifically,
\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}a_{j}\leq a_{j+1}
.

3.

If
\lambda\in\Lambda
(i.e.
\lambda=Mv

for some
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
) satisfies
\lambda\notin V_{j-1}
, then

\left\|\lambda\right\|\geq\text{dist}\left(\lambda,V_{j-1}\right)\geq\operatorname{dist}(v_{j},V_{j-1})=a_{j}.

4.

If
x\in V_{j}
, then
\left\|a_{{}_{M}}x\right\|\prec a_{j}\left\|x\right\|
.

Proof.

Parts
1
and
3

are immediate from the construction of
M
. For part
2
,
recall that
\left\{a_{1}\phi_{1},\dots,a_{m}\phi_{m}\right\}

is an orthogonal set in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and that
v_{j+1}=a_{j+1}\phi_{j+1}+\sum_{i=1}^{j}n_{i,j+1}a_{i}\phi_{i}
.
Then,

a_{j}^{2}\overset{\begin{subarray}{c}{}_{\mbox{part \ref{enu: norm out of E_(j-1)}}}\\
{}_{(v_{j+1}\notin V_{j-1})}\end{subarray}}{\leq}\text{dist}\left(v_{j+1},V_{j-1}\right)^{2}=\|n_{j,j+1}\left(a_{j}\phi_{j}\right)+\left(a_{j+1}\phi_{j+1}\right)\|^{2}=a_{j}^{2}\left|n_{j,j+1}\right|^{2}+a_{j+1}^{2}.

Now, since
\left|n_{j,j+1}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}
(by part
1
),
we obtain:

\frac{1}{4}a_{j}^{2}+a_{j+1}^{2}\geq a_{j}^{2}

and therefore

a_{j+1}\geq\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}a_{j}.

As for part
4
,

\|a_{{}_{\text{}}}x\|=\left\|\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a_{1}\\
&\ddots\\
&&a_{m}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x_{1}\\
\vdots\\
x_{j}\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\right\|=\left\|\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a_{1}x_{1}\\
\vdots\\
a_{j}x_{j}\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\right\|\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq j}\left|a_{i}\right|\|x\|\overset{{}_{\mbox{part \ref{enu: entries of a increasing}}}}{\asymp}a_{j}\|x\|.

∎

Definition 7.3
.

We refer to the sets

\left\{M\in\operatorname{GL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{R}\right):\>\begin{subarray}{c}\mbox{$M$ is reduced for the lattice }\\
\mbox{spanned by its columns}\end{subarray}\right\}

as
reduced Siegel sets
.

Remark 7.4
.

The reduced Siegel sets are contained in the well-known Siegel sets
(e.g.,
[
BM00
,
Rag72
, Chapter X]
).

We note that parts
1

and
3
of Lemma
7.2

are the defining conditions of the reduced Siegel sets (the inequalities
in parts
2
and
4

are redundant, since they follow from part
3
).
Observe that these defining inequalities depend only on the entries
of the
N
and
A
components of the matrix. Indeed, let
M=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}v_{1}&\cdots&v_{m}\end{array}\right]=kan
and
z=an
; the inequalities in
1

are on the entries of
n
, and the inequalities in
3

translate into

a_{j}\leq\left\|\begin{subarray}{c}\mbox{projection of $zv$}\\
\mbox{to }\mbox{span}_{\mathbb{R}}(e_{j},\ldots e_{m})\end{subarray}\right\|,

(7.1)

for every
v=\left(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}\right)^{\mbox{t}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}

and
j=1,\ldots,m
. This is because:

a_{j}=\operatorname{dist}(v_{j},V_{j-1})\leq\operatorname{dist}(\underset{Mv}{\underbrace{\sum_{i=j}^{m}\alpha_{i}v_{i}}},V_{j-1})\overset{{}_{\mbox{rotation by $k$}}}{=}\operatorname{dist}(\sum_{i=j}^{m}\alpha_{i}z_{i},E_{j-1})

where
E_{j-1}:=\mbox{span}_{\mathbb{R}}(e_{1},\ldots,e_{j-1})
and
z=\left[z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}\right]
,

=\operatorname{dist}(zv,E_{j-1})=\|\begin{subarray}{c}\mbox{projection of $zv$}\\
\mbox{to }\mbox{span}_{\mathbb{R}}(e_{j},\ldots e_{m})\end{subarray}\|.

We also note that the number of the defining inequalities for these
reduced Siegel sets is infinite: indeed, every
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
yields
an inequality in formula
7.1
(resp. part
3

of Lemma
7.2
). However, it
is shown in
[
Sch98
, p.49]
that it is actually sufficient
to consider the inequalities

for only
finitely
many

v\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. We state it for future reference:

Proposition 7.5
.

The reduced
Siegel sets are defined by a finite number of inequalities in (the
entries of) the
N
and
A
components of a matrix.

Remark 7.6
.

The finitely many integral vectors
u
that imply the sufficient
inequalities from
7.1
are as follows. For any
j=1,\ldots,m

one considers the
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
which satisfy

\max\left(\left|\alpha_{j}\right|,\dots,\left|\alpha_{m}\right|\right)\leq\frac{C}{a_{j}}\|\begin{subarray}{c}\mbox{projection of $zv$}\\
\mbox{to }\mbox{span}_{\mathbb{R}}(e_{j},\ldots e_{m})\end{subarray}\|

where
C
is some constant that depends only on
m
and can be computed
explicitly from
[
Sch98
]
; clearly, there is only a finite
number of integral vectors
u
which satisfy this condition. In particular,
the reduced Siegel sets can be computed explicitly.

7.2
Fundamental domains of
\operatorname{SL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)
inside

\operatorname{SL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
and
P_{m}

From now on we shall only consider unimodular lattices (resp. bases)
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Since these unimodular lattices are identified with
cosets in
\operatorname{SL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)/\operatorname{SL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)
, where a
representative for a coset is a choice of a basis for the corresponding
lattice, it follows that a fundamental domain for (the right action
of)
\operatorname{SL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)
inside
\operatorname{SL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
consists
of a unique choice of a basis for every unimodular lattice
\Lambda<\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

We know (by the construction presented in the previous subsection)
that every lattice has a reduced basis, and therefore the reduced
Siegel set contains a fundamental domain for
\operatorname{SL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)
.
We turn to describe a
closure
of such a fundamental domain,
namely a choice of a unique reduced basis for almost every unimodular
lattice
\Lambda<\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Remark 7.7
.

It is shown in
[
Sch98
]

that the number of reduced bases for a lattice
\mathbb{R}^{m}
is finite,
where a bound on this number depends only on
m
, and not on the
lattice. Intuitively, this is due to the fact that a given lattice
has only a finite number of shortest vectors (where the bound on this
number depends only on the dimension), and a reduced basis is constructed
such that in every step, one chooses a shortest vector from some lattice.

Given a reduced basis
\left\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\right\}
, one
can clearly obtain further reduced bases for the same lattice by alternating
the signs of the elements
v_{j}
. Note that the corresponding reduced
matrices
M
will have the same
A
components, and in fact they
will vary from each other only by the signs of the entries of
n

and
k
as follows:

•

replacing
\left\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\right\}
by
\left\{-v_{1},\ldots,-v_{m}\right\}

is done by multiplying from
M
from the left by
-I
(replacing

k
by
-k
);

•

replacing
v_{j}
by
-v_{j}
for
j=2,\ldots,m
is done by changing
the sign of the
j
-th row and column of
n
(above the diagonal)
and changing the sign of the
j
-th column of
k
,
\phi_{j}
.

In order to preserve the property
\det\left(M\right)=1
, one
is only allowed to alternate the sign of an
even
number among
the
v_{j}
’s. In particular, one is allowed to change all signs
simultaneously if and only if
-I\in K=\operatorname{SO}_{m}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
.

Definition 7.8
.

We let
\widetilde{F_{m}}\subset\operatorname{SL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)

denote the closed subset of the reduced Siegel set (Definition
7.3
)
which satisfies the following conditions on the
N
and
K
components:

1.

Condition on the sign of the first row of
n
:

\begin{array}[]{c}2|m\Longrightarrow n_{1,j}\geq 0\text{ for $j>2$}\Longrightarrow n_{1,j}\in\left[0,\frac{1}{2}\right]\text{ for $j>2$}\\
2\nmid m\Longrightarrow n_{1,j}\geq 0\text{ for $j>1$}\Longrightarrow n_{1,j}\in\left[0,\frac{1}{2}\right]\text{ for $j>1$}\end{array}.

2.

Condition on the
K
-components:
they lie in a closure of a fundamental domain of the lattice
Z\left(K\right)

in
K
.

We also denote by
F_{m}\subseteq P_{m}
the projection of
\widetilde{F_{m}}

mod
K
, namely the set of upper triangular matrices whose columns
are reduced bases for the lattice spanned by their columns, and whose

N
components satisfy condition
1
.

We state the following for future reference:

Corollary 7.9
.

The boundary of
\widetilde{F_{m}}
(resp.

F_{m}
) is contained in a finite union of lower-dimeansional
manifolds in
\operatorname{SL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
(resp.
P_{m}
).

Proof.

According to Proposition
7.5

and Definition
7.8
,
\widetilde{F_{m}}
and
F_{m}

are defined by a finite number of polynomial inequalities.
∎

The significance of
\widetilde{F_{m}}
and
F_{m}
stems from the
following:

Theorem 7.10
.

\widetilde{F_{m}}\subset\operatorname{SL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)

and
F_{m}\subset P_{m}
are the closures of fundamental domains
for the right actions of
\operatorname{SL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)
on
\operatorname{SL}_{m}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)

and on
P_{m}
respectively.

Proof.

Since
\widetilde{F_{m}}
is a subset of the reduced Siegel set, the
latter containing a basis for every lattice, defined by the additional
conditions from Definition
7.8
, which merely
impose a choice of signs for the basis elements — we conclude that

\widetilde{F_{m}}
contains a representative (basis) for every unimodular
lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

It is now sufficient to show that every
M\in\operatorname{int}\left(\widetilde{F_{m}}\right)

is the unique representative for the lattice spanned by its columns.
In other words, if a given lattice has more than one representative
in
\widetilde{F_{m}}
, then these representatives (that are reduced matrices
for the lattice) lie in the boundary of
\widetilde{F_{m}}
.

Let
M\in\operatorname{int}\left(\widetilde{F_{m}}\right)
. Then,
M
satisfies a strict
version of the inequalities in Formula
7.1
and in Definition

7.8
. Write
M=kan
. Using induction and (the
strict version of) inequality
7.1
, it is clear that
v_{1}

is uniquely determined up to a sign;
v_{2}
is uniquely determined
up to a sign and modulo
V_{1}
; and so forth, every
v_{j}
is
uniquely determined up to a sign and modulo
V_{j-1}
. As a result,

a
is uniquely determined, and the columns of
k
,
\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{m}
,
are determined up to a sign. Since
v_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{j}n_{i,j}\left(a_{i}\phi_{i}\right)
,
one can show using reverse induction (from
i=j-1
to
i=1
) that
there are unique
n_{i,j}
satisfying the strict version of condition

1
from Definition
7.8
so
that
v_{1},\dots,v_{m}
are determined up to a sign. According to
the explanation in the beginning of this section, the inequalities
in Definition
7.8
impose a unique choice of signs,
and therefore a unique representative in the interior of
\widetilde{F_{m}}
.
∎

Remark 7.11

(
Declaring abuse of notation
)
.

As mentioned in Theorem
7.10
,

\widetilde{F_{m}}
and
F_{m}
are
closures
of fundamental

and

, and not

Notation 7.12
.

For a matrix

, denote by

a fundamental domain in

.

The relation between the fundamental

\widetilde{F_{m}}

Corollary 7.15

From Proposition

is the measure of

8
Special examples of spread models
in

8.1
Space of
d
-dimensional lattices in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

In a similar fashion to the construction of the space of full dimensional
lattices in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we can construct the space of unimodular
rank
d
lattices with orientation in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

\mathcal{L}_{d,n}:=\operatorname{SL}_{n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)/T,

where

T=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\operatorname{SL}_{d}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)&\mathbb{R}^{d,n-d}\\
0_{n-d,d}&\operatorname{SL}_{n-d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\end{smallmatrix}\right]\times\left\{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha^{-\frac{1}{d}}\operatorname{I}_{d}&0_{d,n-d}\\
0_{n-d,d}&\alpha^{\frac{1}{n-d}}\operatorname{I}_{n-d}\end{smallmatrix}\right]:\alpha>0\right\}.

One can also get a fundamental domain for
\mathcal{L}_{d,n}
in
\operatorname{SL}_{n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)

using a variant of Iwasawa decomposition. For this denote by
P^{\prime\prime}

the subgroup
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}P_{d}&0_{d,n-d}\\
0_{n-d,d}&P_{n-d}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
in
\operatorname{SL}_{n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
,
K=\operatorname{SO}_{n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
,
K^{\prime\prime}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\operatorname{SO}_{d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)&0_{d,n-d}\\
0_{n-d,d}&\operatorname{SO}_{n-d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\end{smallmatrix}\right]
and let
K^{\prime}
be a spread model for
K/K^{\prime\prime}\simeq\operatorname{Gr}(d,n)
,
the Grassmanian of oriented
d
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}

(see Proposition
6.11
). It is easy to
conclude that

\mathcal{L}_{d,n}=\operatorname{SL}_{n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)/T\simeq KP^{\prime\prime}/\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\operatorname{SL}_{d}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)&0_{d,n-d}\\
0_{n-d,d}&\operatorname{SO}_{n-d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\end{smallmatrix}\right]

and that
K^{\prime}\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\widetilde{F_{d}}&0_{d,n-d}\\
0_{n-d,d}&I_{n-d}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
is a set of representatives for
T
inside
\operatorname{SL}_{n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
,
and for
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\operatorname{SL}_{d}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)&0_{d,n-d}\\
0_{n-d,d}&\operatorname{SO}_{n-d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\end{smallmatrix}\right]
inside
KP^{\prime\prime}
.

Next, we have the space of oriented normalized quotient latices of

\Lambda/\Lambda_{d}
, where
\Lambda
is a full lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

and
\Lambda_{d}
is a
d
-dimensional sub-lattice of
\Lambda
.
It is more convenient to identify
\Lambda/\Lambda_{d}
with the
orthogonal projection of
\Lambda
onto the subspace orthogonal to

\mbox{span}_{\mathbb{R}}(\Lambda)
. Now it is easy to see that this space, say
\mathcal{L}_{d,n}^{\pi}
,
can be presented as the quotient

\mathcal{L}_{d,n}^{\pi}=\operatorname{SL}_{n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)/T_{\#}=KP^{\prime\prime}/\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\operatorname{SO}_{d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)&0_{d,n-d}\\
0_{n-d,d}&\operatorname{SL}_{n-d}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)\end{smallmatrix}\right],

where

T^{\pi}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\operatorname{SL}_{d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)&\mathbb{R}^{d,n-d}\\
0_{n-d,d}&\operatorname{SL}_{n-d}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)\end{smallmatrix}\right]\times\left\{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha^{-\frac{1}{d}}\operatorname{I}_{d}&0_{d,n-d}\\
0_{n-d,d}&\alpha^{\frac{1}{n-d}}\operatorname{I}_{n-d}\end{smallmatrix}\right]:\alpha>0\right\}.

It is easy to see that
K^{\prime}\left[\begin{smallmatrix}I_{d}&0_{d,n-d}\\
0_{n-d,d}&\widetilde{F_{n-d}}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
is a set of representatives for
T^{\pi}
inside
\operatorname{SL}_{n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)

and for
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\operatorname{SO}_{d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)&0_{d,n-d}\\
0_{n-d,d}&\operatorname{SL}_{n-d}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)\end{smallmatrix}\right]
inside
KP^{\prime\prime}
.

In fact this space is diffeomorphic to
\mathcal{L}_{n-d,n}
. To see
this write
g\in\operatorname{SL}_{n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
as
g=\left(A|B\right)
,
where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n,d}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n-d,n}
and consider the map
from
\mathcal{L}_{d,n}^{\pi}
to
\mathcal{L}_{n-d,n}
given by
