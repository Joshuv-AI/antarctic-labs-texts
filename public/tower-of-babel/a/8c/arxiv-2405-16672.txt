Transfer Learning Under High-Dimensional Graph Convolutional Regression Model for Node Classification
Jiachen Chen, Danyang Huang, Liyuan Wang, Kathryn L. Lunetta, Debarghya Mukherjee, Huimin Cheng

Abstract
Node classification is a fundamental task, but obtaining node classification labels can be challenging and expensive in many real-world scenarios. Transfer learning has emerged as a promising solution to address this challenge by leveraging knowledge from source domains to enhance learning in a target domain. Existing transfer learning methods for node classification primarily focus on integrating Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) with various transfer learning techniques. While these approaches have shown promising results, they often suffer from a lack of theoretical guarantees, restrictive conditions, and high sensitivity to hyperparameter choices. To overcome these limitations, we propose a G raph C onvolutional Multinomial Logistic R egression (GCR) model and a transfer learning method based on the GCR model, called Trans-GCR. We provide theoretical guarantees of the estimate obtained under GCR model in high-dimensional settings. Moreover, Trans-GCR demonstrates superior empirical performance, has a low computational cost, and requires fewer hyperparameters than existing methods.

Our proposed

Under the
GCR

.
Specifically, we let
\bm{\beta}^{s}

\bm{\beta}^{t}=\bm{\beta}^{s}+\delta

\delta

In the first step, we obtain the estimate of source domain parameters, denoted as
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{s}
,
by minimizing the

-regularized negative likelihood function of the

\delta
by substituting
\bm{\beta}^{t}
with
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{s}+\delta
in the
GCR
negative likelihood function and minimizing it using the target data. This step leverages the knowledge learned from
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{s}

Finally, our estimate of the target domain parameters is given by
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{t}=\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{s}+\hat{\delta}
.

Our method enjoys the following advantages: (1) We demonstrate through extensive empirical studies that our proposed method achieves superior or comparable performance compared with existing complicated GCN-based transfer learning approaches for node classification.
(2)
We provide theoretical guarantees of the estimate obtained under our proposed
GCR
model in high-dimensional
settings under mild conditions.
(3) By leveraging the simplified model
GCR
, our method involves fewer parameters to be trained than more complex GCN-based models, resulting in reduced computational cost. (4) Our framework has only two hyperparameters, i.e., the number of graph aggregation layers and the
l_{1}-
norm penalty strength.

2
Graph Convolutional Regression Model

A graph with
n
nodes is represented by an adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}=(\mathbf{A}_{ij})\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
,
i,j=1,\ldots,n
, where
\mathbf{A}_{ij}=1
if there is an edge between nodes
i
and
j
, and
\mathbf{A}_{ij}=0
otherwise. We only consider a graph with no self-loops, so all diagonal entries of
\mathbf{A}
are 0.
In addition, each node is associated with a
d
-dimensional covariates and a
C
-dimensional one-hot class label. The entire covariate matrix is
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, and the entire classification label matrix is
\mathbf{Y}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times C}
. Node classification aims to predict
\mathbf{Y}
based on
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{A}
. The normalized adjacency matrix is

\mathbf{S}=\tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}},\mbox{ where }\tilde{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{I}_{n},

(2.1)

where
\mathbf{I}_{n}
is an
n\times n
identity matrix,
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
denote the adjacency matrix with added self-connections.
Here,
\tilde{\mathbf{D}}
is the degree matrix of
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
, with diagonal entry
\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{ii}
representing the degree of node
i
, and all off-diagonal elements being zero.

2.1
Related Work: Graph Convolutional Networks

GCNs and their variants have gained increasing popularity for node classification tasks
[
6
,
13
]
. Among these variants, Simple Graph Convolution (SGC)
[
27
]
is a popular and powerful simplified variant of GCNs.
The main idea behind SGC is to remove the nonlinear activation functions in the hidden layers of a GCN and only keep the softmax activation in the final layer to obtain probabilistic outputs.
An
M
-layer SGC trains a classifier

\hat{\mathbf{Y}}=\mbox{Softmax}(\mathbf{S}^{M}\mathbf{X}\hat{\bm{\beta}})
,
where
\hat{\bm{\beta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times C}
is the learned weight matrix. SGC enjoys the following advantages: (1) SGC reduces model complexity and computational burden, making SGC more efficient and scalable.
(2) SGC has only one hyperparameter, the number of layers
M
, reducing tuning complexity and hyperparameter sensitivity and thus preventing overfitting.
(3) Despite the simplicity, extensive experiments have shown that SGC maintains competitive or even slightly better performance than GCN
[
27
]
.

2.2
High-Dimensional Graph Convolutional Multinomial Logistic Regression Model

Motivated by the success of SGC, we propose the
GCR
model to model the relationship between
\mathbf{Y}
,
\mathbf{X}
, and
\mathbf{A}
.
GCR
assumes that the classification labels are not only affected by their own, but also their neighbors’ features based on the graph structure captured by the adjacency matrix. Usually, a normalized adjacency matrix is used; a common choice is
\mathbf{S}
in Eq.
2.1
. In what follows, we present the
GCR
model using
\mathbf{S}
.
In
GCR
, there is a vector of coefficients
\bm{\beta}_{c}=(\bm{\beta}_{1c},\ldots,\bm{\beta}_{dc})^{T}
for each category
c=1,\ldots,C
. To ensure the identifiability of
\bm{\beta}_{c}
,
we take the last category as the reference category, i.e.,
\bm{\beta}_{C}=\textbf{0}_{d}
.
In
GCR
model, given predictors
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{A}
, for each node
i=1\ldots,n
, the probability of node
i
’s classification label

\mathbf{Y}_{i}
belonging to category
c
is

\textstyle\mathbf{P}_{ic}=\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{Y}_{i}=c|\mathbf{X},\mathbf{A})=\frac{{\sf exp}^{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{S}^{M}_{ij}\mathbf{X}_{j}\bm{\beta}_{c}}}{1+\sum_{c=1}^{C-1}{\sf exp}^{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{S}^{M}_{ij}\mathbf{X}_{j}\bm{\beta}_{c}}},c=1,\ldots,C,

(2.2)

where
\mathbf{X}_{j}=(\mathbf{X}_{j1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{jd})
is the
j
th row of
\mathbf{X}
,

\mathbf{S}^{M}_{ij}
is the
(i,j)
th element in
\mathbf{S}^{M}
,
\mathbf{S}
is defined in Eq.
2.1
, and
M
is the number of convolution layer. The sum
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{S}^{M}_{ij}\mathbf{X}_{j}
aggregates neighboring nodal features, encouraging neighboring nodes to have similar aggregated features, thus having similar classification labels. A larger value of
M
enables the model to capture higher-order neighborhood dependencies.
In the high-dimensional setting, where
d
can be larger than
n
, we assume
\bm{\beta}_{c}
to be sparse such that the number of nonzero elements of
\bm{\beta}_{c}
, denoted by
s<<d
.

Parameter estimation.
Given observed data
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})
, the parameter estimate
\hat{\bm{\beta}}=(\hat{\bm{\beta}_{1}},\ldots,\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{C-1})
is obtained by minimizing the following negative log-likelihood with
l_{1}
regularization,

l(\bm{\beta};\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{X},\mathbf{A})=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{c=1}^{C}(\mathbf{Y}_{ic}\log\mathbf{P}_{ic}+(1-\mathbf{Y}_{ic})\log(1-\mathbf{P}_{ic}))+\lambda\sum_{c=1}^{C-1}||\bm{\beta}_{c}||_{1},

where
\mathbf{P}_{ic}
is defined in Eq.
2.2
,

\bm{\beta}_{c}
is the
c
th column of
\bm{\beta}
,

||\cdot||_{1}
is the
l_{1}-
norm,
\lambda\geq 0
is a regularization hyperparameter controlling the trade-off between the log-likelihood and the penalty.

l(\bm{\beta};\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{X},\mathbf{A})
can be further simplified as

\textstyle l(\bm{\beta};\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{X},\mathbf{A})=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{c=1}^{C}(\mathbf{Y}_{ic}(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{S}_{ij}^{M}\mathbf{X}_{j})\bm{\beta}_{c}-\log(1+e^{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{S}_{ij}^{M}\mathbf{X}_{j}\bm{\beta}_{c}}))+\lambda\sum_{c=1}^{C-1}||\bm{\beta}_{c}||_{1}

(2.3)

To minimize Eq.
2.3
, we use the standard coordinate descent algorithm
[
28
]
, which iteratively minimizes the objective function with respect to each coordinate of
\bm{\beta}
, while keeping the others fixed.

3
Transfer Learning

In this paper, we consider the following transfer learning problem. Let
(\mathbf{A}^{(0)},\mathbf{X}^{(0)},\mathbf{Y}^{(0)})
denote the target data, where
\mathbf{A}^{(0)}\in\{0,1\}^{n_{0}\times n_{0}}
,
\mathbf{X}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}\times d}
,
\mathbf{Y}^{(0)}\in\{0,1\}^{n_{0}\times C}
. Let
\bm{\beta}^{(0)}=(\bm{\beta}_{1}^{(0)},\ldots,\bm{\beta}_{C-1}^{(0)})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times(C-1)}
denote the true coefficient matrix associated with target data under
GCR
model.
Let
(\mathbf{A}^{(k)},\mathbf{X}^{(k)},\mathbf{Y}^{(k)})
denote the
k
th source data,

k=1,\ldots,K
, where
\mathbf{A}^{(k)}\in\{0,1\}^{n_{k}\times n_{k}}
,
\mathbf{X}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}\times d}
,
\mathbf{Y}^{(k)}\in\{0,1\}^{n_{k}\times C}
.
Let
\bm{\beta}^{(k)}=(\bm{\beta}_{1}^{(k)},\ldots,\bm{\beta}_{C-1}^{(k)})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times(C-1)}
denote the true coefficient matrix associated with the
k
th source data.

The difference between the
k
th source domain’s coefficient and the target domain’s coefficient is
\delta^{(k)}=\bm{\beta}^{(0)}-\bm{\beta}^{(k)}
. Let
(C-1)^{-1}\sum_{c=1}^{C-1}||\delta_{c}^{(k)}||_{1}
measure the domain shift level of the
k
th source data, where
\delta_{c}^{(k)}
is the
c
th column of
\delta^{(k)}
. A source sample is defined as
h-
level transferable if its domain shift level is lower than a threshold
h
. The set of
h-
level transferable source data is
\mathcal{A}_{h}=\{k:(C-1)^{-1}\sum_{c=1}^{C-1}||\delta_{c}^{(k)}||_{1}\leq h\}
.
To ensure that transferring sources within the set
\mathcal{A}_{h}
is beneficial,
h
should be reasonably small.
In the subsequent sections, we will abbreviate the notation
\mathcal{A}_{h}
as
\mathcal{A}
for brevity without special emphasis.

3.1
Transfer Learning When the Transferable Source Set is Known

In this subsection, we present a transfer learning method under
GCR
(abbreviated as

Trans-
GCR
) for the node classification task when the transferable source set
\mathcal{A}
is known, i.e., we have prior knowledge of which source data to utilize. Our method Trans-
GCR
is motivated by the transfer learning literature in the conventional regression model (without considering graph structure)
[
29
,
30
,
31
]
.
Figure
1
shows the workflow of Trans-
GCR
, which works in the following steps. We first preprocess
\mathbf{A}^{(k)}
to obtain the normalized adjacency matrices
\mathbf{S}^{(k)}
,
k\in\{0,\mathcal{A}\}
.

Source sample pooling.
We then pooled all source domain data in
\mathcal{A}
into a single source sample with pooled normalized adjacency matrix
\mathbf{S}^{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathcal{A}}\times n_{\mathcal{A}}}
, node features
\mathbf{X}^{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathcal{A}}\times d}
, and labels
\mathbf{Y}^{\mathcal{\mathcal{A}}}\in\{0,1\}^{n_{\mathcal{A}}\times C}
, where
n_{\mathcal{A}}=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{A}}n_{k}
.
\mathbf{S}^{\mathcal{A}}
has a block structure with diagonal blocks corresponding to the individual
\mathbf{S}^{(k)}
matrices,
k\in\mathcal{A}
. The pooled node features
\mathbf{X}^{\mathcal{A}}
and labels
\mathbf{Y}^{\mathcal{A}}
are obtained by concatenating the respective
\mathbf{X}^{(k)}
and
\mathbf{Y}^{(k)}
matrices row-wise,
k\in\mathcal{A}
. The rationale behind pooling the source samples in
\mathcal{A}
together is as follows. Since the source domains in
\mathcal{A}
are assumed to be similar to the target domain, with only small domain shifts, this naturally implies that the source domains in
\mathcal{A}
have similar underlying model parameters. By pooling the source samples, the algorithm gains access to a larger effective sample size from the same distribution, which leads to a more accurate and stable estimate.

Source domain parameter estimation.
Let
\bm{\beta}^{\mathcal{A}}
denote the latent parameter
in
GCR
that are used to generate
(\mathbf{Y}^{\mathcal{A}})
.
To obtain
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\mathcal{A}}
, we minimize the aforementioned
negative log-likelihood with
l_{1}-
norm penalty in Eq.
2.3
using the pooled source samples.
Domain shift estimation.
Let
\delta^{\mathcal{A}}
denote the difference between the pooled source parameters
\bm{\beta}^{\mathcal{A}}
and the target domain parameters
\bm{\beta}^{(0)}
, i.e.,
\bm{\beta}^{(0)}=\bm{\beta}^{\mathcal{A}}+\delta^{\mathcal{A}}
.
We then estimate
\delta^{\mathcal{A}}
by minimizing the following loss function,
which essentially replaces
\bm{\beta}^{(0)}
with
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\mathcal{A}}+\delta^{\mathcal{A}}
in the likelihood function of

GCR
model using the target data,

\textstyle-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{0}}\sum_{c=1}^{C}[\mathbf{Y}^{(0)}_{ic}(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n_{0}}s_{ij}\mathbf{X}^{(0)}_{j})(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{c}^{\mathcal{A}}+\delta_{c}^{\mathcal{A}})-\psi(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n_{0}}s_{ij}\mathbf{X}^{(0)}_{j}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{c}^{\mathcal{A}}+\delta_{c}^{\mathcal{A}}))]+\lambda\sum\limits_{c=1}^{C-1}||\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{c}^{\mathcal{A}}+\delta_{c}^{\mathcal{A}}||_{1},

(3.1)

where
\psi(x)=\log{(1+e^{x})},s_{ij}
is the
(i,j)
th element of
(\mathbf{S}^{(0)})^{M}
,
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{c}^{\mathcal{A}}
and
\delta_{c}^{\mathcal{A}}
are the
c
th column of
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\mathcal{A}}
and
\delta^{\mathcal{A}}
, respectively. This reformulation transforms the unknown parameter from
\bm{\beta}^{(0)}
to
\delta^{\mathcal{A}}
.
Finally, the target domain parameter estimate is
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{(0)}=\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\mathcal{A}}+\hat{\delta}^{\mathcal{A}}
, combining the estimated source domain parameters and the learned domain shift.

We summarize our procedure in
Algorithm
1
in Appendix
F
, which involves two hyperparameters: the graph convolution layers
M
and the
l_{1}-
norm penalty strength
\lambda
. When implementing Trans-
GCR
, we apply cross-validation procedures to select these hyperparameters.

Workflow of Trans-
GCR
. We first pool all source domains to get an estimate
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\mathcal{A}}
. We then use target data and the knowledge from
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\mathcal{A}}
to estimate domain shift
\hat{\delta}^{\mathcal{A}}
. The final estimate for the target data is
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{(0)}=\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\mathcal{A}}+\hat{\delta}^{\mathcal{A}}

3.2
Transferable Source Detection

In section
3.1
, we presented a method when the transferable set
\mathcal{A}
is known. Nevertheless, in real applications, such prior knowledge might be unavailable.
When the source domain differs significantly from the target domain, negative transfer can occur, leading to decreased performance on the target task
[
30
,
31
]
. To address this issue, we propose a data-driven cross-validation approach to select the transferable set
\mathcal{A}
automatically.

Our method begins by partitioning nodes in the target data into
V
folds, i.e.,
s_{1},\ldots,s_{V}
. For each fold
v
, the labels of nodes in
s_{v}
in the target data (denoted as
\mathbf{Y}_{s_{v}}^{(0)}
) are held out as the testing target data, while the labels in the remaining folds
\mathbf{Y}_{-s_{v}}^{(0)}
serve as the training target data.
We then apply the transfer learning Algorithm
1
using the
k
th source data
\{\mathbf{A}^{(k)},\mathbf{X}^{(k)},\mathbf{Y}^{(k)}\}
and training target data

\{\mathbf{A}^{(0)},\mathbf{X}^{(0)},\mathbf{Y}_{-s_{v}}^{(0)}\}
to obtain the estimate
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{vk}^{(0)}
for the target data.
Note that we use the entire network structure of the target data
\mathbf{A}^{(0)}
and all node covariates
\mathbf{X}^{(0)}
to obtain aggregated features. However, we only use the classification labels of the training nodes
\mathbf{Y}_{-s_{v}}^{(0)}
when minimizing the negative log-likelihood in Eq.
2.3
during the estimation process.

Using the model estimate
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{vk}^{(0)}
, we
predict labels for the testing nodes in the target data,
calculating
\hat{\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{Y}_{i}^{(0)}=c)
,
i\in s_{v}
.
The model’s performance is assessed through the negative log-likelihood,
\mbox{NL}_{v}^{(k)}=-\sum_{i\in s_{v}}\sum_{c=1}^{C}(\mathbf{Y}^{(0)}_{ic}\log\hat{\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{Y}^{(0)}_{i}=c)+(1-\mathbf{Y}^{(0)}_{ic})\log(1-\hat{\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{Y}^{(0)}_{i}=c))
.
We then average
\mbox{NL}_{v}^{(k)}
across all
V
folds, yielding
\mbox{NL}^{(k)}=V^{-1}\sum_{v=1}^{V}\mbox{NL}_{v}^{(k)}
. A lower score of
\mbox{NL}^{(k)}
indicates higher transferability of the
k
th source data.
We rank the
K
sources by their corresponding
\mbox{NL}^{(k)}
values and select the top
L
sources with the lowest scores
as the estimated transferable set
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
, where
L
is a user-defined hyperparameter specifying the number of source data to include. We summarize our procedure in Algorithm
2
in the Appendix
F
.

4
Theoretical Properties

In this section, we present our main theoretical results.
Recall that in this paper, we proposed a
GCR
model to account for the network dependency between observations, and a transfer learning method Trans-
GCR
to promote the estimation of the target data utilizing knowledge from source data. Establishing theoretical guarantees for the GCR model under network dependency alone is extremely challenging as our data i) is high-dimensional (i.e., the number of covariates can be much larger than the number of samples) and ii) admits a network dependency among the observations.
This dependency prevents the application of standard concentration results, valid only for i.i.d. data.
Consequently, a careful mathematical analysis is necessary.
In this paper, we take a step towards that goal by rigorously analyzing the theoretical properties of
\hat{\bm{\beta}}

obtained by minimizing the loss function in Eq.
2.2

when
\mathbf{A}
is generated from an Erdős–Rényi (ER) random graph model with parameter
p
,
M=1
(i.e., one convolutional layer),
given observed data
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})
. For simplicity and ease of presentation, this section focuses on results for the case where
C=2
corresponds to a two-class classification problem.
Nevertheless, our theoretical results can be easily generalized to the multiclass cases. Under ER model, the expected degree of each node is
np
. In what follows, we build theoretical guarantees under the normalized adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}/\sqrt{np}
.
To ease presentations, we let

\mathbf{Z}=(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X})/\sqrt{np}
(replace
p
by
\hat{p}
when uknown).
We would like to highlight that rows of
\mathbf{Z}
are
not
independent.
Let
\|{\bm{\beta}}^{(0)}\|_{0}=s
for some
s\ll d
. The estimate

\hat{\bm{\beta}}
is obtained by minimizing the loss function in Eq.
2.2
, which can further rewritten using
\mathbf{Z}
, as shown below,

\textstyle\hat{\bm{\beta}}={\arg\min}_{\bm{\beta}}\left\{-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\mathbf{Y}_{i}\mathbf{Z}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}-\log{(1+{\sf exp}{(\mathbf{Z}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta})})}\right]+\lambda\|\bm{\beta}\|_{1}\right\},

(4.1)

where
\mathbf{Z}_{i}
is the
i
th row of
\mathbf{Z}
.
The key difference between our analysis of
\hat{\bm{\beta}}
and that of a standard high dimensional generalized linear model (e.g.,
[
32
]
) is precisely the dependence among observations.
Below, we present the assumptions required to establish the theoretical guarantees of our penalized estimator
\hat{\bm{\beta}}
:

Assumption 4.1
.

We assume
\mathbf{X}_{i}
’s are generated independently from a sub-gaussian distribution with parameter
\sigma_{\mathbf{X}}
, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[{\sf exp}{(\lambda a^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i})}]\leq{\sf exp}{(\lambda^{2}\sigma_{\mathbf{X}}^{2}/2)}
for all
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
and for all
a\in S^{p-1}
. Let
\Sigma_{\mathbf{X}}
be the covariance matrix of
\mathbf{X}_{i}
. Assume that
\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{\mathbf{X}})\geq\kappa_{l}>0
and
\max_{j}\Sigma_{\mathbf{X},jj}\leq\sigma_{+}
for some constant
(\kappa_{l},\sigma_{+})
.

Assumption 4.2

(Network connectivity)
.

The network connectivity parameter
p
of the Erdős–Rényi random graph
\mathbf{A}
satisfies
(np)/\log{n}\to\infty
, and
p\log{d}\to 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

Assumption 4.3

(Sparsity)
.

The sparsity parameter
s
of
{\bm{\beta}}^{(0)}
satisfies:

\textstyle s\frac{\log^{2}{d}}{n}\frac{(1-2p)}{4p\log{\left((1-p)/p\right)}}=o(1)
.

All three assumptions above are quite standard in the literature on the analysis of high-dimensional linear and generalized linear models. Assumption
4.1
requires the covariates to be sub-gaussian, a ubiquitous assumption, which is often needed to deal with ultra-dimensional setup (i.e.,
d
can be as large as
e^{n^{\gamma}}
for some
\gamma<1
) (see Chapter 6 of
[
33
]
for details). One may relax this assumption as the cost of the trade-off between
d
and
n
; the thicker the tail of
\mathbf{X}
, the more stringent condition is needed on
d
for estimation, or one may use robust loss function (
[
34
]
). Assumption
4.2
is also a standard assumption in the literature of network estimation, which merely assumes
p
to be (slightly) larger than
n^{-1}
in order
[
35
]
. As for Assumption
4.3
, in standard i.i.d. setup we require
s\log{d}/n\to 0
. However, here, our condition is slightly different; we have an additional factor involving a factor of
p
. Whether this dependence is optimal is out of the scope of this paper. Given these assumptions, we are now ready to state our main theorem:

Theorem 4.4
.

Under Assumption
4.1
-
4.3
, the
\ell_{1}
-penalized estimator
\hat{\bm{\beta}}
obtained in (
4.1
) satisfies;

\textstyle\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}-{\bm{\beta}}^{(0)}\|_{2}^{2}\leq c\frac{s\log{d}}{n}

for some constant
c>0
with probability
1-g(n,p,d)
, where
g(n,p,d)
goes to
0

The proof of the theorem is deferred to the Appendix.
One of the main technical challenges lies in establishing a condition equivalent to the standard restricted strong convexity (RSC) or restricted eigenvalue (RE) condition in the presence of network dependency.
This task is difficult even in an i.i.d. setup; see
[
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
]
for related references. The modified RSC condition is presented in Lemma
B.1
, which is of independent interest.

Remark 4.5
.

It may seem surprising that the convergence rate of
\hat{\bm{\beta}}
, as established in Theorem
4.4
does not depend on
p
. This is precisely because we have appropriately scaled
\mathbf{Z}=(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X})/\sqrt{np}
by
p
. If we change the scale, i.e., say we use symmetric normalized Laplacian
\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}
, then the effective calling would be
\mathbf{Z}=(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X})/np
. In that case, the estimation rate of
\hat{\bm{\beta}}
would depend on
p
, and this dependence can be easily quantified by carefully tracking the steps of our proof.
However, as this involves technical algebra without adding further insight, we refrain from pursuing it here.

Remark 4.6
.

Our theorem precisely quantifies the estimation rate of
\bm{\beta}^{(0)}
in a single domain, which can be viewed as the estimation rate using only data from the target domain. Recently, estimating
\bm{\beta}^{(0)}
using related source samples under the high-dimensional generalized linear model setup has been explored by
[
31
,
40
]
. As previously mentioned, extending these ideas to incorporate network dependency is quite challenging. However, we have conjectured an estimation rate for
\bm{\beta}^{(0)}
obtained using Trans-
GCR
. See Appendix
D
for details.

Remark 4.7
.

Although in our theory we have assumed all the entries of
\mathbf{A}
are generated independently from
{\sf Ber}(p)
, Theorem
4.4
continues to hold under the self-loop (resp. no self-loop), i.e. if we set
\mathbf{A}_{ii}=1
(resp.
\mathbf{A}_{ii}=0
) and generate off-diagonal elements from
{\sf Ber}(p)
, with different constant
c^{\prime}
instead of
c
. In the proof, we have pointed out the required modifications precisely to adapt proof when i) we have self-loop, and ii)
p
is unknown and estimated from the data.

Discussion

In this paper, we introduce the
GCR
model to capture the relationship between node classification labels (
\mathbf{Y}
), network structure (
\mathbf{A}
), and node covariates (

\mathbf{X}
). We then propose Trans-
GCR
, a transfer learning method that enhances estimation in the target domain using knowledge from the source domain. Despite its strong empirical performance and theoretical benefits, our method has limitations that need further research. Firstly, our theoretical results are limited to the ER random graph model. While we show empirical success with other models like SBM and graphon, theoretical validation for these models is still needed. Secondly, the
GCR
model assumes a linear relationship between graph convolutional features and the log odds ratio; extending this to nonlinear models would be valuable. Thirdly, our source detection algorithm only provides a transferability score, and developing a hypothesis testing method for source domain transferability would be useful. Lastly, our algorithm currently transfers only point estimations of model parameters, and extending it to include confidence interval estimation would provide a measure of uncertainty.

In this Appendix, we show (1) the notation table to briefly summarize important notations in this manuscript, (2) proofs of theoretical guarantees,
(3) additional experimental results, and (4) pseudo code of algorithms.

Appendix B
Proof of Theorem
4.4

For notational simplicity, define
\psi(x)=\log{(1+e^{x})}
. Recall that our loss function is negative log-likelihood:

L_{n}({\bm{\beta}})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\left\{-{\mathbf{Y}}_{i}{\mathbf{Z}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{\beta}}+\phi({\mathbf{Z}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{\beta}})\right\}\,,

and our estimator is:

\hat{\bm{\beta}}={\arg\min}_{{\bm{\beta}}}\left[L_{n}({\bm{\beta}})+\lambda\|{\bm{\beta}}\|_{1}\right]\,.

Furthermore, define the Bregman divergence
\delta L_{n}({\bm{\beta}})
as:

\delta L_{n}({\bm{\beta}})=L_{n}({\bm{\beta}})-L_{n}({\bm{\beta}}^{(0)})-\langle{\bm{\beta}}-{\bm{\beta}}^{(0)},\nabla L_{n}({\bm{\beta}}^{(0)})\rangle\,.

(B.1)

As
L_{n}(\cdot)
is a convex loss function, it is immediate that
\delta L_{n}({\bm{\beta}})\geq 0
for all
{\bm{\beta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Furthermore, define
\hat{v}=\hat{\bm{\beta}}-{\bm{\beta}}^{(0)}
and the penalized excess risk function
R_{n}
as:

R_{n}(v)=L_{n}({\bm{\beta}}^{(0)}+v)+\lambda\|{\bm{\beta}}^{(0)}+v\|_{1}-L_{n}({\bm{\beta}}^{(0)})-\lambda\|{\bm{\beta}}^{(0)}\|_{1}\,.

As both the loss and penalty functions are convex,
R_{n}(\cdot)
is a convex function. Furthermore, as
\hat{\bm{\beta}}
minimizes the penalized loss function, we have:

R_{n}(\hat{v})\leq 0,\text{ or equivalently
}L_{n}(\hat{\bm{\beta}})+\lambda\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}\|_{1}\leq L_{n}({\bm{\beta}}^{(0)})+\lambda\|{\bm{\beta}}^{(0)}\|_{1}\,.

(B.2)

Recall that we need to show
\|\hat{v}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}-{\bm{\beta}}^{(0)}\|_{2}^{2}\leq C(s\log{d})/n
.
We prove this by reductio ad absurdum.
As
s=o(\log{d}/n)
, we know
C(s\log{d})/n<1
. Suppose that the claim of the theorem is not true. Then there exists
t\in(0,1)
such that
C(s\log{d})/n<\|t\hat{v}\|_{2}^{2}\leq 1
. Call
t\hat{v}=\tilde{v}
. By the convexity of
R_{n}(\cdot)
we have:

R_{n}(\tilde{v})=R_{n}(t\hat{v})=R_{n}(t\hat{v}+(1-t)0)\leq t\underbrace{R_{n}(\hat{v})}_{\leq 0}+(1-t)\underbrace{R_{n}(0)}_{=0}\leq 0\,.

The above chain of inequalities provides an immediate upper bound on
R_{n}(\tilde{v})
. For the lower bound, we use Bregman divergence:

\displaystyle 0\geq R_{n}(\tilde{v})

\displaystyle=L_{n}({\bm{\beta}}^{(0)}+\tilde{v})+\lambda\|{\bm{\beta}}^{(0)}+\tilde{v}\|_{1}-L_{n}({\bm{\beta}}^{(0)})-\lambda\|{\bm{\beta}}^{(0)}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\langle\tilde{v},\nabla L_{n}({\bm{\beta}}^{(0)})\rangle+\delta L_{n}(\tilde{v})+\lambda\|{\bm{\beta}}^{(0)}+\tilde{v}\|_{1}-\lambda\|{\bm{\beta}}^{(0)}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\geq\delta L_{n}(\tilde{v})-\|\nabla L_{n}({\bm{\beta}}^{(0)})\|_{\infty}\|\tilde{v}\|_{1}+\lambda\|{\bm{\beta}}^{(0)}+\tilde{v}\|_{1}-\lambda\|{\bm{\beta}}^{(0)}\|_{1}

Therefore, we have:

\displaystyle\delta L_{n}(\tilde{v})+\lambda\|{\bm{\beta}}^{(0)}+\tilde{v}\|_{1}\leq\|\nabla L_{n}({\bm{\beta}}^{(0)})\|_{\infty}\|\tilde{v}\|_{1}+\lambda\|{\bm{\beta}}^{(0)}\|_{1}\,.

Now suppose we choose
\lambda
such that
\|\nabla L_{n}({\bm{\beta}}^{(0)})\|_{\infty}\leq\lambda/2
with high probability. We will specify the choice of
\lambda
later in the proof. Under this choice we have:

\displaystyle\delta L_{n}(\tilde{v})+\lambda\|{\bm{\beta}}^{(0)}+\tilde{v}\|_{1}\leq\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\tilde{v}\|_{1}+\lambda\|{\bm{\beta}}^{(0)}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\delta L_{n}(\tilde{v})+\lambda\|\tilde{v}_{S^{c}}\|_{1}\leq\frac{3\lambda}{2}\|\tilde{v}_{S}\|_{1}\,.

(B.3)

As
\delta L_{n}(\tilde{v})\geq 0
, we further have
\|\tilde{v}_{S^{c}}\|_{1}\leq 3\|\tilde{v}_{S}\|_{1}
, i.e.
\tilde{v}\in\mathcal{C}(s,3)
,
where
\mathcal{C}(s,\alpha)
is defined as:

\mathcal{C}(s,\alpha)=\left\{v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}:\|v_{S^{c}}\|_{1}\leq\alpha\|v_{S}\|_{1},\text{ for some }S\text{ with }|S|=s\right\}\,.

We next present a lemma, which establishes a lower bound on
\delta L_{n}(\cdot)
with high probability:

Lemma B.1
.

Under Assumptions 1 and 2, there exists some positive constants
\kappa_{l}
and
C_{4}
such that,

\delta L_{n}(u)\geq L_{\psi}(T)\|u\|_{2}^{2}\left\{\kappa_{l}-\left(C_{4}\log{d}\sqrt{\frac{\Psi(p)}{n}}\right)\frac{\|u\|_{1}}{\|u\|_{2}}\right\},\ \ \forall\ \|u\|_{2}\leq 1\,.

with probability at least
1-({\sf exp}{\left(\frac{1}{2}\log{d}+1-c_{4}\log^{2}{d}\Psi(p)\right)}+2{\sf exp}{\left(1-(c_{1}-3/2)\log{d}\right)}+2{\sf exp}{\left(\frac{3}{2}\log{d}+1-c_{2}n\right)})
, where

\Psi(p)=\frac{1-2p}{4p\log{((1-p)/p)}}\,,

and
L_{\psi}(T)
is a constant, same as in the proof of Proposition 2 of
[
39
]
.

We defer the proof the lemme to the end to maintain the flow. Consider the event when the upper bound of Lemma
B.1
holds. As by definition
\|\tilde{v}\|_{2}\leq 1
, applying Lemma
B.1
on
\tilde{v}
yields:

\delta L_{n}(\tilde{v})\geq L_{\psi}(T)\kappa_{l}\|\tilde{v}\|_{2}^{2}-L_{\psi}(T)\left(C_{4}\log{d}\sqrt{\frac{\Psi(p)}{n}}\right)\|\tilde{v}\|_{1}\|\tilde{v}\|_{2}\,.

Using this lower bound on the Bregman divergence in equation (
B.3
), we have:

\displaystyle L_{\psi}(T)\kappa_{l}\|\tilde{v}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|\tilde{v}_{S^{c}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{9}{8L_{\psi}(T)\kappa_{l}}s\lambda^{2}+\frac{L_{\psi}(T)\kappa_{l}}{2}\|\tilde{v}_{S}\|_{2}+4L_{\psi}(T)\left(C_{4}\log{d}\sqrt{\frac{\Psi(p)}{n}}\right)(\|\tilde{v}_{S}\|_{1})\|\tilde{v}\|_{2}\,.

Changing sides we have:

\displaystyle\frac{L_{\psi}(T)\kappa_{l}}{2}\|\tilde{v}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|\tilde{v}_{S^{c}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{9}{8L_{\psi}(T)\kappa_{l}}s\lambda^{2}+4L_{\psi}(T)\left(C_{4}\log{d}\sqrt{\frac{\Psi(p)}{n}}\right)(\|\tilde{v}_{S}\|_{1})\|\tilde{v}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{9}{8L_{\psi}(T)\kappa_{l}}s\lambda^{2}+4L_{\psi}(T)\sqrt{s}\left(C_{4}\log{d}\sqrt{\frac{\Psi(p)}{n}}\right)\|\tilde{v}\|^{2}_{2}

As under Assumption
4.3
, we have

, eventually it will be smaller than

(B.4)

If we can show that a proper choice of

Now we bound

(C.16)

Finding a bound on
\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\|_{F}^{2}
:
As before, we first find the expected value of
\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\|_{F}^{2}
. For any

:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})_{ij}^{2}]=\frac{1}{n^{2}p^{2}}\mathbb{E}[(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})_{ij}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}p^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbf{A}_{ki}\mathbf{A}_{kj}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}p^{2}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[(\mathbf{A}_{ki}\mathbf{A}_{kj})^{2}]+\sum_{k\neq l}\mathbb{E}[(\mathbf{A}_{ki}\mathbf{A}_{kj})(\mathbf{A}_{li}\mathbf{A}_{lj})]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}p^{2}}(np^{2}+n^{2}p^{4})=\frac{1}{n}+p^{2}\,.

Now for
1\leq i=j\leq n
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})_{ii}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}p^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbf{A}^{2}_{ki}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}p^{2}}\left(\sum_{k}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{A}^{4}_{ki}]+\sum_{k\neq l}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{A}_{ki}^{2}\mathbf{A}_{li}^{2}]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}p^{2}}(np+n^{2}p^{2})=\frac{1}{np}+1\,.

Therefore, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\|_{F}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\mathbb{E}[(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})_{ii}^{2}]+\sum_{i\neq j}\mathbb{E}[(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})_{ij}^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq n\left(\frac{1}{np}+1\right)+n^{2}\left(\frac{1}{n}+p^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq n+\frac{1}{p}+n^{2}p^{2}\leq n+n^{2}p^{2}\,.

The last inequality follows from
p\geq n^{-1}
. Next, we establish a bound on the variance:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}\left(\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\|_{F}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{4}p^{4}}\operatorname{Var}(\|\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\|_{F}^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{4}p^{4}}\operatorname{Var}\left(\sum_{i,j}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{2}_{i,j}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{4}p^{4}}\left[\sum_{i,j}\operatorname{Var}\left((\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{2}_{i,j}\right)+\sum_{(i,j)\neq(k,l)}\operatorname{Cov}((\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{2}_{i,j},(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{2}_{k,l})\right]

\displaystyle\triangleq\frac{1}{n^{4}p^{4}}(T_{1}+T_{2})\,.

We bound
T_{1}
and

For that, we use some basic bounds on the raw moments of a binomial random variable; if
X\sim\operatorname{Ber}(n,p)
, then
\mathbb{E}[X^{k}]\leq Cn^{k}p^{k}
for all
k\in\{1,2,3,4\}
, for some universal constant
C
as long as
np\uparrow\infty
. Observe that
({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}{\mathbf{A}})_{ii}\sim\operatorname{Ber}(n-1,p)
and
({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}{\mathbf{A}})_{ij}\sim\operatorname{Ber}(n-2,p^{2})
for
i\neq j

For

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\operatorname{Var}\left((\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{2}_{i,j}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{Var}\left((\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{2}_{ii}\right)+\sum_{i\neq j}\operatorname{Var}\left((\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{2}_{ij}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i}\mathbb{E}[(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{4}_{ii}]+\sum_{i\neq j}\mathbb{E}[(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{4}_{ij}]

\displaystyle\leq C(n^{5}p^{4}+n^{6}p^{8})\,.

Next, we bound

, i.e., the covariance term. Note that if
(i,j,k,l)

0
as the terms are independent. Therefore, we only consider the cases when there are three or two distinct indices. We first deal with the terms of two distinct indices, i.e.,
\operatorname{Cov}(({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}{\mathbf{A}})^{2}_{ii},({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}{\mathbf{A}})^{2}_{ij})
where
i\neq j
. There are almost
n^{2}
many terms of this form. For each of these types of terms:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}((\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{2}_{ii},(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{2}_{ij})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{2}_{ii}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{2}_{ij}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{2}_{ii}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{2}_{ij}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{2}_{ii}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{2}_{ij}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{k,k^{\prime}=1}^{n}\mathbf{A}^{2}_{ki}\mathbf{A}_{k^{\prime}i}\mathbf{A}_{k^{\prime}j}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{k}\mathbf{A}^{3}_{ki}\mathbf{A}_{kj}+\sum_{k\neq k^{\prime}}\mathbf{A}^{2}_{ki}\mathbf{A}_{k^{\prime}i}\mathbf{A}_{k^{\prime}j}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{k}\mathbf{A}_{ki}\mathbf{A}_{kj}+\sum_{k\neq k^{\prime}}\mathbf{A}_{ki}\mathbf{A}_{k^{\prime}i}\mathbf{A}_{k^{\prime}j}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{k}\mathbf{A}_{ki}\mathbf{A}_{kj}\right)^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{k\neq k^{\prime}}\mathbf{A}_{ki}\mathbf{A}_{k^{\prime}i}\mathbf{A}_{k^{\prime}j}\right)^{2}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2C(n^{2}p^{4}+n^{4}p^{6})\,.

Therefore, we have:

\sum_{i\neq j}\operatorname{Cov}(({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}{\mathbf{A}})^{2}_{ii},({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}{\mathbf{A}})^{2}_{ij})\leq 2C(n^{4}p^{4}+n^{6}p^{6})\,.

(C.17)

Next, we bound the covariance terms of the form
\operatorname{Cov}(({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}{\mathbf{A}})^{2}_{ij},({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}{\mathbf{A}})^{2}_{jk})
, i.e. two terms share an index with
i\neq j\neq k
. There are almost

such terms. For each term:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}((\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{2}_{ij},(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{2}_{jk})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{2}_{ij}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{2}_{jk}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{l,l^{\prime}}\mathbf{A}_{li}\mathbf{A}_{lj}\mathbf{A}_{l^{\prime}i}\mathbf{A}_{l^{\prime}k}\right)^{2}\right]

with
\kappa_{1}=L_{\psi}(T)\kappa_{l}
,
\kappa_{3}=C_{4}/\kappa_{l}
.

We showed in the proof of Step 1 that
\left\|\hat{{\bm{u}}}_{S^{c}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}\leq 3\left\|\hat{{\bm{u}}}_{S}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}+4C_{l}h
. Next we discuss about bounding
\frac{1}{n_{0}}\left\|{\mathbf{Z}}^{(0)}\hat{{\bm{u}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{2}^{2}
by
\left\|\hat{{\bm{u}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{2}^{2}
using this fact.

If
3\left\|\hat{{\bm{u}}}_{S}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}\geq 4C_{l}h
, then
\left\|\hat{{\bm{u}}}_{S^{c}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}\leq 6\left\|\hat{{\bm{u}}}_{S}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}
. Then by Lemma
D.9
(the extension on Theorem 1.6 of
[
37
]
for network dependency), we have

\frac{1}{n_{0}}\left\|{\mathbf{Z}}^{(0)}\hat{{\bm{u}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim\left\|\hat{{\bm{u}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim\frac{s\log d}{n_{\mathcal{A}}+n_{0}}+h\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{n_{\mathcal{A}}+n_{0}}}

(D.11)

with probability at least
1-d^{-\tilde{K}}-n_{0}^{-1}-e^{\log{n_{0}}-\frac{n_{0}p}{c}}
.

If
3\left\|\hat{{\bm{u}}}_{S}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}<4C_{l}h
, then
\left\|\hat{{\bm{u}}}_{S^{c}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}\leq 8C_{l}h\leq\sqrt{s}
. Also
\left\|\hat{{\bm{u}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{2}\leq 1
with probability
1-(c_{12}d^{-c_{13}}+\sum_{k}n_{k}^{-1}+\sum_{k}e^{\log{n_{k}}-\frac{n_{k}p_{k}}{c_{14}}})
. We denote

\displaystyle\Pi_{0}(s)=\left\{{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\|{\bm{u}}\|_{2}\leq 1,\|{\bm{u}}\|_{0}\leq s\right\}

\displaystyle\Pi_{1}(s)=\left\{{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\|{\bm{u}}\|_{2}\leq 1,\|{\bm{u}}\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{s}\right\}

Due to Lemma 3.1 of
[
53
]
,
\Pi_{1}(s)\subseteq 2\overline{\operatorname{conv}}\left(\Pi_{0}(s)\right)
, where
\overline{\operatorname{conv}}\left(\Pi_{0}(s)\right)
is the closure of convex hull of
\Pi_{0}(s)
. Similarly, an extension for network dependency on the proof of Theorem 2.4 in
[
54
]
will also conclude (
D.11
).

Next we bound
\frac{1}{n_{0}}\left\|\bm{\mathbf{Z}}^{(0)}\tilde{{\mathbf{v}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{2}^{2}
by
\left\|\tilde{{\mathbf{v}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{2}^{2}
. From basic inequality, we establish

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\hat{L}^{(0)}\left(\hat{{\bm{\beta}}}^{(0)},\mathcal{D}^{(0)}\right)-\hat{L}^{(0)}\left({\bm{\beta}}^{(0)},\mathcal{D}^{(0)}\right)-\nabla\hat{L}^{(0)}\left({\bm{\beta}}^{(0)},\mathcal{D}^{(0)}\right)^{T}(\hat{{\bm{\beta}}}^{(0)}-{\bm{\beta}}^{(0)})

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{{\delta}}\left(\left\|{\bm{\beta}}^{(0)}-\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}-\left\|\hat{{\bm{\delta}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}\right)+\left\|\nabla\hat{L}^{(0)}\left({\bm{\beta}}^{(0)},\mathcal{D}^{(0)}\right)\right\|_{\infty}\|\hat{{\bm{\beta}}}^{(0)}-{\bm{\beta}}^{(0)}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{{\delta}}\left(\left\|{\bm{\beta}}^{(0)}-\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}-\left\|\hat{{\bm{\delta}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{{\delta}}\|\hat{{\bm{\beta}}}^{(0)}-{\bm{\beta}}^{(0)}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{2}\lambda_{{\delta}}\left\|{\bm{\beta}}^{(0)}-\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{{\delta}}\left\|\hat{{\bm{\delta}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{2}\lambda_{{\delta}}C_{l}h+\frac{3}{2}\lambda_{{\delta}}\left\|\hat{{\bm{u}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{{\delta}}\left\|\hat{{\bm{\delta}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}

(D.12)

implying

\left\|\hat{{\mathbf{v}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}\leq\left\|\hat{{\bm{\delta}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}+C_{l}h\leq 3\left\|\hat{{\bm{u}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}+4C_{l}h

Combined with results by (
D.7
), we have
\left\|\tilde{{\mathbf{v}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}\leq\left\|\hat{{\mathbf{v}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{s}
when
s\log d/\left(n_{\mathcal{A}}+n_{0}\right)
and
h
are small enough. We can see
\delta\hat{L}^{(0)}\left(\hat{{\bm{\delta}}}^{\mathcal{A}},\mathcal{D}\right)>0
from the strict convexity, which leads to
\left\|\hat{{\bm{\delta}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}\leq

3\left\|\hat{{\bm{u}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}+3h
. Then we get

\left\|\hat{{\mathbf{v}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}\leq\left\|{\bm{\beta}}^{(0)}-\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}+\left\|\hat{{\bm{\delta}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}\leq 4\left\|\hat{{\bm{u}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}+4h\leq\sqrt{s}

(D.13)

Similar to the analysis considering
3\left\|\hat{{\bm{u}}}_{S}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}<4C_{1}h
above, we establish

c_{0}\cdot\frac{1}{n_{0}}\left\|{\mathbf{Z}}^{(0)}\tilde{{\mathbf{v}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq c_{0}\cdot C\left\|\tilde{{\mathbf{v}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

holds with probability at least
1-d^{-\tilde{K}}-n_{0}^{-1}-e^{\log{n_{0}}-\frac{n_{0}p}{c}}
. As long as
c_{0}C<c_{9}/2
, by (
D.10
), we have

\displaystyle\kappa_{1}\left\|\tilde{{\mathbf{v}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\kappa_{1}\kappa_{3}\left(\log{d}\sqrt{\frac{\Psi(p)}{{n_{0}}}}\right)\cdot\left\|\tilde{{\mathbf{v}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\lambda_{{\delta}}\left\|{\mathcal{\bm{\delta}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{{\delta}}\left\|\tilde{{\mathbf{v}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}+C\frac{s\log d}{n_{\mathcal{A}}+n_{0}}+Ch\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{n_{\mathcal{A}}+n_{0}}}+c_{3}/2\left\|\tilde{{\mathbf{v}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

(D.14)

with probability at least
1-C^{\prime}n_{0}^{-1}
,
\kappa_{1}=L_{\psi}(T)\kappa_{l}
,
\kappa_{3}=C_{4}/\kappa_{l}
.

If satisfying
\lambda_{{\delta}}\left\|{\bm{\delta}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}\leq C\frac{s\log d}{n_{\mathcal{A}}+n_{0}}+Ch\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{n_{\mathcal{A}}+n_{0}}}
, then

\displaystyle\left\|\tilde{{\mathbf{v}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}\lesssim\left[\frac{s\log d}{n_{\mathcal{A}}+n_{0}}+h\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{n_{\mathcal{A}}+n_{0}}}\right]\cdot\sqrt{\frac{1}{\log d}\sqrt{\frac{n_{0}}{\Psi(p)}}}+\left\|\tilde{{\mathbf{v}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

Because
\left\|\tilde{{\mathbf{v}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{2}\leq 1
, by (
D.14
), the following inequality holds

\left\|\tilde{{\mathbf{v}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim\frac{s\log d}{n_{\mathcal{A}}+n_{0}}+h\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{n_{\mathcal{A}}+n_{0}}}\lesssim\frac{s\log d}{n_{\mathcal{A}}+n_{0}}+\left[h\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{n_{0}}}\right]\wedge h^{2}

with probability at least
1-C^{\prime}n_{0}^{-1}
.

If
\lambda_{\delta}\left\|{\bm{\delta}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}>C\frac{s\log d}{n_{\mathcal{A}}+n_{0}}+Ch\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{n_{\mathcal{A}}+n_{0}}}
, then
\left\|\tilde{{\mathbf{v}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}\lesssim h+\left\|\tilde{{\mathbf{v}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{2}^{2}
, leading to

\left\|\tilde{{\mathbf{v}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim 2\lambda_{{\delta}}\left\|{\bm{\delta}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{{\delta}}\left\|\tilde{{\mathbf{v}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}

which implies
\left\|\tilde{{\mathbf{v}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}\leq 4\left\|{\bm{\delta}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}\leq 4C_{l}h
. By plugging this result into (
D.14
), we obtain

\left\|\tilde{{\mathbf{v}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim\frac{s\log d}{n_{\mathcal{A}}+n_{0}}+\left[h\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{n_{0}}}\right]\wedge h^{2}

(D.15)

with probability at least
1-C^{\prime}n_{0}^{-1}
.

When
s\log d/\left(n_{\mathcal{A}}+n_{0}\right)
and
h
is small enough, due to
h\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{{n_{0}}}}=o(1)
, the right side of (
D.15
) can be very small, implying
\left\|\tilde{{\mathbf{v}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{2}\leq c<1
with probability at least
1-C^{\prime}n_{0}^{-1}
. We should notice that this result holds for any
t\in(0,1]
such that
\left\|\tilde{{\mathbf{v}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{2}\leq 1
. Finally let’s consider the vector of interest:
\hat{{\mathbf{v}}}^{\mathcal{A}}
. Suppose
\left\|\tilde{{\mathbf{v}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\geq\frac{s\log d}{n_{\mathcal{A}}+n_{0}}+\left[h{\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{{n_{0}}}}\right)}\right]\wedge h^{2}
for some constant
C>0
with probability at least
C^{\prime}n_{0}^{-1}
, then there exists
t\in(0,1]
such that
c<\left\|\tilde{{\mathbf{v}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{2}\leq 1
. This contradicts with the fact
\left\|\tilde{{\mathbf{v}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{2}\leq c
with probability at least
1-C^{\prime}n_{0}^{-1}
. Hence we establish

\left\|\hat{{\mathbf{v}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim\frac{s\log d}{n_{\mathcal{A}}+n_{0}}+\left[h\sqrt{\left({\frac{\log d}{{n_{0}}}}\right)}\right]\wedge h^{2}\lesssim 1

with probability at least
1-C^{\prime}n_{0}^{-1}
.

Similarly, the
\ell_{1}
-bound on
\hat{{\mathbf{v}}}^{\mathcal{A}}
will be obtained by going over the analysis procedure of
\tilde{{\mathbf{v}}}^{\mathcal{A}}

\left\|\hat{{\mathbf{v}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}\lesssim\left[s\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{n_{\mathcal{A}}+n_{0}}}+h\right]\cdot\sqrt{\sqrt{\frac{1}{\Psi(p)}}}

with probability at least
1-C^{\prime}n_{0}^{-1}
.

Lastly, we combine the conclusions in this Step 2 with the upper bounds on
\left\|\hat{{\bm{u}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{2}
and
\left\|\hat{{\bm{u}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{1}
in Step 1, to complete the proof.

Combining the above inequalities, we obtain:

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{(0)}-\bm{\beta}^{(0)}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\hat{\bm{u}}\right\|_{2}+\left\|\hat{\mathbf{v}}\right\|_{2}\lesssim h\log{d}\sqrt{\frac{\Psi(p)}{n_{\mathcal{A}}+n_{0}}}+\sqrt{\frac{s\log d}{n_{\mathcal{A}}+n_{0}}}+\left(\frac{\log d}{n_{\mathcal{A}}+n_{0}}\right)^{1/4}\sqrt{h}.

And

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{(0)}-\bm{\beta}^{(0)}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\hat{\bm{u}}\right\|_{1}+\left\|\hat{\mathbf{v}}\right\|_{1}\lesssim s\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{n_{\mathcal{A}}+n_{0}}}+\left(\frac{\log d}{n_{\mathcal{A}}+n_{0}}\right)^{1/4}\sqrt{sh}+

\displaystyle h\left(1+\log d\sqrt{\frac{s\Psi(p)}{n_{\mathcal{A}}+n_{0}}}\right)+\left[s\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{n_{\mathcal{A}}+n_{0}}}+h\right]\cdot\sqrt{\sqrt{\frac{1}{\Psi(p)}}}.

Appendix F
Pseudo Code for Algorithms

Here, we summarize the procedure of Trans-
GCR
in Algorithm
1
, and the procedure of source domain selection in
Algorithm
2
.

Algorithm 1

Trans-
GCR
Algorithm

Target data
(\mathbf{A}^{(0)},\mathbf{X}^{(0)},\mathbf{Y}^{(0)})
; Source data
(\mathbf{A}^{(k)},\mathbf{X}^{(k)},\mathbf{Y}^{(k)}),k\in\mathcal{A}
; Hyperparameter
M
and
\lambda
.

Step 1.
Preprocessing.

Calculate the normalized adjacency matrix
\mathbf{S}^{(k)}
based on
\mathbf{A}^{(k)}
,
k\in\{0,\mathcal{A}\}
.

Step 2.
Pooled source samples.

Get pooled source sample with
\mathbf{S}^{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathcal{A}}\times n_{\mathcal{A}}}
,

\mathbf{X}^{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathcal{A}}\times d}
,

\mathbf{Y}^{\mathcal{A}}\in\{0,1\}^{n_{\mathcal{A}}\times C}
.

Step 3.

Source domain parameter estimation.
Get
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\mathcal{A}}
by Eq.
2.3
, using the pooled source samples
(\mathbf{S}^{\mathcal{A}},\mathbf{X}^{\mathcal{A}},\mathbf{Y}^{\mathcal{A}})
.

Step 4.
Domain shift estimation
. Obtain domain shift estimate
\hat{\delta}^{\mathcal{A}}
using Eq.
3.1
and target data.

Step 5.
Target domain parameter estimation.

Obtain
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{(0)}=\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\mathcal{A}}+\hat{\delta}^{\mathcal{A}}
.

Output
:
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{(0)}
.

Algorithm 2

Source Domain Transferability Score Calculation Algorithm

Data: Target data
(\mathbf{A}^{(0)},\mathbf{X}^{(0)},\mathbf{Y}^{(0)})
; Source data
(\mathbf{A}^{(k)},\mathbf{X}^{(k)},\mathbf{Y}^{(k)}),k=1,\ldots,K
;

Hyperparameters: Number of layers
M
; Cross-validation folds
V
; Number of selected source data
L
.

Step 1.

Target data partition.
Randomly partition data points in the target domain
\{1,\ldots,n_{0}\}
into
V
subsets of approximately equal size
s_{1},\ldots,s_{V}
.

Step 2.

Training and testing target data construction.

Construct testing target data
(\mathbf{A}^{(0)},\mathbf{X}^{(0)}
,
\mathbf{Y}_{s_{v}}^{(0)})
, where we only use the label information of nodes in
s_{v}
. Similarly, construct training target data
(\mathbf{A}^{(0)}
,
\mathbf{X}^{(0)}
,
\mathbf{Y}_{-s_{v}}^{(0)})
by excluding the label information of nodes in
s_{v}
.

Step 3.

Cross-validation based score.
For
k
th source data,
k=1,\ldots,K
, repeat the following procedure.

For
v=1,\ldots,V
,

•

Model estimation.
Apply the transfer learning Algorithm
1
using the source data
\{\mathbf{A}^{(k)},\mathbf{X}^{(k)},\mathbf{Y}^{(k)}\}
and training target data

\{\mathbf{A}^{(0)},\mathbf{X}^{(0)},\mathbf{Y}_{-s_{v}}^{(0)}\}
to obtain the estimate
\hat{{\bm{\beta}}}_{vk}^{(0)}
for the target data after transfer learning.

•

Model evaluation
Using the learned
\hat{{\bm{\beta}}}_{vk}^{(0)}
from the previous step, evaluate its prediction performance in the
target domain testing data
\{\mathbf{A}^{(0)},\mathbf{X}^{(0)},\mathbf{Y}_{s_{v}}^{(0)}\}
by calculating the negative log-likelihood value
\mbox{NL}_{v}^{(k)}
for nodes in
s_{v}
.

Averaged score over folds.
Calculate averaged negative log-likelihood over
V
folds for each
k
source data,
\mbox{NL}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{v=1}^{V}\mbox{NL}_{v}^{(k)}

Step 4.

Selection.
Rank the
K
sources according to
\mbox{NL}^{(k)}
and select among the top
L
lowest sources as
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
.

Output
:
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
and
Transferability score
\mbox{NL}^{(k)}
,
k=1,\ldots,K
.
