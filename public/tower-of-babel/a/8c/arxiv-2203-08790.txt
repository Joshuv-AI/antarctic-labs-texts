Analysis of a Gray-Box Operator for Vertex Cover
Samuel Baguley, Tobias Friedrich, Timo Kötzing, Xiaoyue Li, Marcus Pappik, Ziena Zeif

Abstract
Combinatorial optimization problems are a prominent application area of evolutionary algorithms, where the (1+1) EA is one of the most investigated. We extend this algorithm by introducing some problem knowledge with a specialized mutation operator which works under the assumption that the number of 1s of a solution is critical, as frequently happens in combinatorial optimization. This slight modification increases the chance to correct wrongly placed bits while preserving the simplicity and problem independence of the (1+1) EA. As an application of our algorithm we examine the vertex cover problem on certain instances, where we show that it leads to asymptotically better runtimes and even finds with higher probability optimal solutions in comparison with the usual (1+1) EA. Precisely, we compare the performance of both algorithms on paths and on complete bipartite graphs of size n n . Regarding the path we prove that, for a particular initial configuration, the (1+1) EA takes in expectation Θ ⁡ ( n 4 ) \Theta(n^{4}) iterations while the modification reduces this to Θ ⁡ ( n 3 ) \Theta(n^{3}) , and present experimental evidence that such a configuration is reached. Concerning the complete bipartite graph our modification finds the optimum in polynomial time with probability 1 − 1 / 2 Ω ⁡ ( n ξ ) 1-1/2^{\Omega(n^{\xi})} for every positive constant ξ < 1 \xi<1 , which improves the known probability of 1 − 1 / poly ​ ( n ) 1-1/\text{poly}(n) for the (1+1) EA.

Introduction

Evolutionary algorithms (EAs) are a diverse and highly versatile class of randomized search heuristics, and their effectiveness at solving combinatorial optimization problems has been recognized and widely studied since the nineties
[
19
, Chap. 14]
.
Typically, EAs are implemented with naïve mutation behavior, and adapt to a particular problem only via an associated fitness function.
This simplicity has lead to success in many applications, but leaves room for improvement on specific problems.

The minimum vertex cover problem is an ideal target for evolutionary algorithms because it is NP-complete
[
10
]
,
and appears in a broad range of practical applications.
Already in 1994 Bäck and Khuri
[
1
]
defined the canonical
fitness function for EAs on vertex cover and provided experimental evidence that on some graph instances EAs can produce better approximate solutions than bespoke solvers.
In the years that followed runtime analysis of EAs tended to be heuristic and experimental, but recently rigorous theoretical analyses
have gained prominence.
The various approaches to this include proving runtime bounds for approximate solutions
[
9
,
15
]
, analyzing performance on best- or worst-case graph instances
[
13
,
14
]
,
and implementing genetic mechanisms like crossover
[
6
]
. It is also common for new research to define variations of well-studied EAs, like Olivieto et al.’s (1+1) EA with an additional ‘diversity maintenance mechanism’
[
14
]
, or a fitness function with multiple objectives
[
5
,
11
]
.

In the application of evolutionary algorithms to real world problems, the full black-box view of the fitness function (ignoring all additional information about its inner workings) is at most the initial prototype;
significant improvements can be made by applying problem knowledge for designing tailored variation operators
[
17
]
.
The resulting view is called gray-box optimization, and research tries to understand what general structural properties can be usefully employed in evolutionary computation
[
20
]
.
We are interested in combinatorial problems where a certain subset has to be selected; typically, the number of elements in the chosen set is rather important.
For example, a minimum spanning tree contains exactly
n-1
edges, the vertex cover problem tries to minimize the number of vertices picked, and the independent set problem maximizes the selected vertices.
While
[
12
]
argued that variation operators should be unbiased with respect to whether a
0
or a
1
encodes a specific property of a solution, the gray-box view of combinatorial problems wants the
1
to mean ‘in’ and
0
to mean ‘out’, and then design operators that can make use of this meaning.
From the very general work of Rowe and Vose
[
18
]
we can see that only for problems which have symmetrical meaning in
1
and
0
can the operators be assumed unbiased.

In this paper we augment
the (1+1) EA by allowing a second kind of mutation called a ‘balanced flip’, in which a bit is chosen uniformly at random, and then swapped with the bit of a neighboring vertex
of opposite value, if one exists.
At every iteration the augmented algorithm chooses between a balanced flip and a regular step of the (1+1) EA with equal probability.
The ‘balanced flip’ behavior is already exhibited by the (1+1) EA on nearly-optimal bit-strings, the difference being that it takes an order of magnitude longer to perform each flip.
Thus the evolutionary algorithm presented here refines
the (1+1) EA by incorporating its emergent behavior on inputs close to the optimum directly into the mutation operator; this is somewhat in the spirit of Giel and Wegener’s ‘local (1+1) EA’
[
7
]
.
The immediate benefit of our augmentation is
a tighter upper runtime bound for the Balanced (1+1) EA on paths.

In
Section

3
we compare the runtime of the (1+1) EA solving vertex cover on paths to that of the Balanced (1+1) EA. Our analysis of the (1+1) EA uses techniques
similar to
those in
[
7
]
to obtain an expected runtime of
O(n^{4})
, and we then apply those same techniques to the Balanced (1+1) EA to prove an expected runtime of
O(n^{3})
. We also give a partial proof of
\Omega(n^{4})

expected runtime for the (1+1) EA, assuming that the algorithm at some point produces a bit-string with a long
consecutive sequence of incorrectly assigned bits. We provide experimental evidence to motivate this condition, and delay a full proof to future work.

In
Section

4
we consider the complete bipartite graph
K_{L,R}
, which is a common instance class of interest when it comes to evolutionary algorithms.
Under the assumption that the ratio
R/L
is at least
2
, the (1+1) EA is known to have exponential runtime with at least polynomial probability
[
5
, Theorem 5]
.
In contrast, the Balanced (1+1) EA has far better performance on this graph instance, with exponentially small probability of not reaching the optimum in polynomial time.

2.1
Feasible bit strings

In order to analyze the expected runtimes of the (1+1) EA and Balanced (1+1) EA, we use the following multiplicative drift theorem to bound the time it takes for
X
to become feasible.

Theorem 1

(Doerr et. al
[
4
]
)
.

Let
(X_{t},t\in\mathds{N})
be a
stochastic process on

S\cup\{0\}
, where
S\varsubsetneq\mathds{R}^{+}
is a set positive numbers with minimum
s_{\min}
,
and let
T=\inf\{t\in\mathds{N}\mid X_{t}=0\}
.
Suppose there exists a real number

such that for all

\mathds{E}\left[X_{t}-X_{t+1}\;\middle|\;X_{t}=s\right]\geq\delta s
.
Then, for all

\mathds{E}\left[T\mid X_{0}=s_{0}\right]\leq\frac{\ln(s_{0})-\ln(s_{\min})+1}{\delta}.

Lemma 2
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph with
n=|V|
vertices, and let

be the first time that the (1+1) EA samples a feasible string.
Then

\mathds{E}\left[T\right]\leq\mathrm{e}n(\ln(n)+1/2)

and for

\mathds{P}\left[T>2\mathrm{e}kn(\ln\left(n\right)+1/2)\right]\leq 2^{-k}

Further, if

is the first time that the Balanced (1+1) EA samples a feasible string, then

\mathds{E}\left[S\right]\leq 2\mathrm{e}n(\ln(n)+1/2)

and for
k>0

\mathds{P}\left[S>4\mathrm{e}kn(\ln\left(n\right)+1/2)\right]\leq 2^{-k}
.

Proof.

We start by analysing the (1+1) EA.
Let
X_{t}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
denote
the state of the (1+1) EA after
t
iterations, where
X_{0}
is the initial random configuration.
We shall apply
Theorem

1
to the process
u(X_{t})
,
t\geq 0
.
For all
t\in\mathds{N}
, let
F_{t}\subseteq E
be the set of edges that are not covered by
X_{t}
and let
U_{t}\coloneqq\bigcup_{e\in F_{t}}e
.
Note that the algorithm attempts to flip exactly one bit with probability
(1-1/n)^{n-1}\geq 1/\mathrm{e}
.
Whenever a single bit is flipped, this bit is chosen uniformly at random.
Consider the (not necessarily induced) subgraph
(U_{t},F_{t})
of
G
.
If the flipped bit corresponds to a vertex
v\in U_{t}
, then
u(X_{t})-u(X_{t+1})
is equal to the degree of
v
in
(U_{t},F_{t})
.
Thus, given a uniformly random bit from
U_{t}
is flipped, the expected difference
u(X_{t})-u(X_{t+1})
is the average degree of
(U_{t},F_{t})
, which is
2\left\lvert F_{t}\right\rvert/\left\lvert U_{t}\right\rvert=2u(X_{t})/\left\lvert U_{t}\right\rvert

Let us denote the event that exactly one bit in

is flipped, and no other, by

Since

is non-increasing,
u(X_{t})-u(X_{t+1})
is non-negative.
Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[u(X_{t})-u(X_{t+1})\;\middle|\;u(X_{t})\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathds{E}\left[(u(X_{t})-u(X_{t+1}))\mathds{1}_{C}\;\middle|\;u(X_{t})\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2u(X_{t})}{\left\lvert U_{t}\right\rvert}\mathds{P}\left[C\;\middle|\;u(X_{t})\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2u(X_{t})}{\left\lvert U_{t}\right\rvert}\frac{\left\lvert U_{t}\right\rvert}{\mathrm{e}n}\geq\frac{2u(X_{t})}{\mathrm{e}n}.

So the drift condition of

1
is satisfied by

. Since
u(X)\in\{0,1,\dots,n^{2}\}
, we conclude that

\mathds{E}\left[T\right]\leq\mathrm{e}n(\ln(n)+1/2)
.

To bound the expected time for the Balanced (1+1) EA to find a feasible solution, note that with probability
1/2
it performs the same actions as the (1+1) EA.
Moreover, the balanced flip operations cannot increase

due to the fitness function

Thus we can essentially use the same proof as for the (1+1) EA but with drift
\mathds{E}\left[u(X_{t})-u(X_{t+1})\;\middle|\;u(X_{t})\right]\geq u(X_{t})/\mathrm{e}n

.

In order to prove the second part of the statement, we start once again with the (1+1) EA.
We prove our claim via induction over
k\in\mathds{N}
.
The base case
k=0
is trivial.
Now, consider some
k>0
and assume the claim holds for
k-1

Let
A
be the event that no feasible solution was found within the first
2\mathrm{e}(k-1)n\left(\ln(n)+1/2\right)
iterations and let
B
be the event that no feasible solution is found within the last

Then the desired probability can be written as
\mathds{P}\left[A\text{ and }B\right]=\mathds{P}\left[B\;\middle|\;A\right]\mathds{P}\left[A\right]
.
By our induction hypothesis, we have
\mathds{P}\left[A\right]\leq 2^{-(k-1)}

Further, note that the bound on the expected number of iterations for finding a feasible solution, proven above, holds for arbitrary starting states.
Thus, by Markov’s inequality, we have
\mathds{P}\left[B\;\middle|\;A\right]\leq\frac{1}{2}
, which proves the upper bound.
The proof for the Balanced (1+1) EA is obtained analogously, using the respective expected hitting time.
∎

2.2
Lower Bound Probability for Symmetric Random Walks

The technical core of our proof for a lower bound on the running time for solving vertex cover on paths is the following general lemma.
It essentially lower bounds the hitting time of a process that is dominated by a symmetric random walk.
There are two main difference between
Lemma

3
and most bounds that can be found in the literature.
Firstly, the presented bound does not only hold in expectation but at least with constant probability and, secondly, the description of the process allows for arbitrarily large jumps, as long as their probability is bounded.

Lemma 3
.

Let
d\in\mathds{N}
with
d\geq 4\ln(10)
and let
Z_{t}\in\mathds{N}
be a stochastic process such that, for some
p\in(0,1]
and
q\in[0,1)
with
q+2p\leq 1
, it holds that

(i)

\forall s\in\mathds{N}\cap[0,d-1]:\mathds{P}\left[Z_{t+1}=s+1\;\middle|\;Z_{t}=s\right]\leq p
,

(ii)

\forall s\in\mathds{N}\cap[1,d-1]:

\mathds{P}\left[Z_{t+1}=s+1\;\middle|\;Z_{t}=s\right]\leq\mathds{P}\left[Z_{t+1}=s-1\;\middle|\;Z_{t}=s\right]
and

(iii)

\forall s\in\mathds{N}\cap[0,d]:\mathds{P}\left[\left\lvert Z_{t+1}-Z_{t}\right\rvert>1\;\middle|\;Z_{t}=s\right]\leq q
.

Then, for
Z_{0}=0
and
T=\inf\{t\in\mathds{N}\mid Z_{t}\geq d\}
, it holds that

\mathds{P}\left[T\geq\min\left\{\frac{1}{5q},\frac{d^{2}(1-q)}{4\ln(10)p}\right\}\right]\geq\frac{16}{25}.

To prove
Lemma

3
, we need two additional ingredients.
The first one is an elementary bound on the lower tail of a geometric random variable, which will allow us to lower bound the time until a certain event appears.

Lemma 4
.

Let
X
be a geometric random variable with success probability
p\in(0,1)
.
For all
c\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
it holds that
\mathds{P}\left[X\geq\frac{c}{p}\right]\geq 1-c
.

Proof.

If
\frac{c}{p}\leq 1
then
\mathds{P}\left[X\geq\frac{c}{p}\right]=1\geq 1-c
holds trivially.
Assume
\frac{c}{p}>1
and let
k=\left\lceil\frac{c}{p}\right\rceil\geq 2
.
By the definition of a geometric random variable and Bernoulli’s inequality, we have

\mathds{P}\left[X\geq\frac{c}{p}\right]=\mathds{P}\left[X\geq k\right]=\left(1-p\right)^{k-1}\geq 1-p\cdot(k-1).

Observing that
\left\lceil\frac{c}{p}\right\rceil-1\leq\frac{c}{p}
concludes the proof.
∎

The second ingredient is a concentration result on the sum of independent geometric random variables, which is similar to the Chernoff bound.
More specifically, we are interested in obtaining a lower bound on such a sum.

Theorem 5
.

[Doerr
[
3
]
, Theorem
1.10.32
(b)]

Let
X_{1},\dots X_{n}
be independent geometric random variables with common success probability
p>0
.
Then, for
X=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
,
\mu=\frac{n}{p}
and all
\delta\in(0,1)
it holds that

\mathds{P}\left[X\leq(1-\delta)\mu\right]\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\delta^{2}}{2-\frac{4}{3}\delta}n}.

Given these two result, we are able to prove

3
.

Note that, as long as

with probability

\tau_{N}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\tau_{i}-\tau_{i-1})

Recall that

almost surely.

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left[T_{Y}\geq m\right]

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tau_{i}-\tau_{i-1}\geq m\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathds{P}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tau_{i}-\tau_{i-1}\geq m\text{ and }N\geq b\right]

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tau_{i}-\tau_{i-1}\geq m\;\middle|\;N\geq b\right]\cdot\mathds{P}\left[N\geq b\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathds{P}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{b}\tau_{i}-\tau_{i-1}\geq m\right]\cdot\frac{9}{10}.

Next, observe that each of the differences

Using
Theorem

5
with

Therefore, we get

3.1
Upper Bound Running Time

By

2

Once a feasible solution is found, all states in following iterations are feasible as well.

If

We define the

The expected number of iterations that the (1+1) EA spends at level

The expected number of iterations that the Balanced (1+1) EA spends at level

Let

be a path of length

Proof.

In either case,

Theorem 17
.

Let

be a complete bipartite graph with vertex partitions

of size

of size
R

\mathcal{O}\left((c+1)L^{2}\log((c+1)L)\right)

with probability at least
1-2^{-\Omega\left(L^{\xi}\right)}
.

Proof.

We prove this statement in two steps.
At first, we argue that, with sufficiently high probability, we reach a state where all vertices in
V_{L}
are selected and more than
L
vertices from
V_{R}
are not selected in at most
\mathcal{O}\left((c+1)L^{2}\log((c+1)L)\right)

In the second step, we argue that, once such a state is reached, we go to the optimum in at most
\mathcal{O}\left((c+1)L^{2}\log((c+1)L)\right)

For the first part, let
X_{t}
denote the states of the Balanced (1+1) EA and let
T_{L}
and

be defined as in

16
.
Formally, we want to lower bound
\mathds{P}\left[T_{L}\leq\tau\text{ and }T_{L}<T^{\prime}_{R}\right]

\tau\in\mathcal{O}\left((c+1)L^{2}\log((c+1)L)\right)

Let

and observe that, if

, then

.
Thus, we have

\mathds{P}\left[T_{L}\leq\tau\text{ and }T_{L}<T^{\prime}_{R}\right]=\mathds{P}\left[T\leq\tau\text{ and }T_{L}<T^{\prime}_{R}\right].

Using De Morgan’s laws and union bound, we get

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left[T\leq\tau\text{ and }T_{L}<T^{\prime}_{R}\right]

\displaystyle=1-\mathds{P}\left[T>\tau\text{ or }T_{L}\geq T^{\prime}_{R}\right]

\displaystyle\geq 1-\mathds{P}\left[T>\tau\right]-\mathds{P}\left[T_{L}\geq T^{\prime}_{R}\right].

By

16
, we have
\mathds{P}\left[T_{L}\geq T^{\prime}_{R}\right]\leq 2^{-\Omega\left(L^{\xi}\right)}

Setting
\tau=4\mathrm{e}L(L+R)\left(\ln(L+R)+1/2\right)

2

selected and more than
L

V_{R}
are not selected in at most

with

and
\left\lvert X_{0}\cap V_{R}\right\rvert<R-L

Note that adding vertices from

, we distinguish between three different cases.
If
v\in V_{R}\setminus S_{t-1}
, then also
v\in V_{R}\setminus R_{t-1}
and nothing happens.
On the other hand, if
v\in R_{t-1}
, then
t\leq T_{L}
implies
t-1<T_{L}
and
L\setminus L_{t}\neq\emptyset
.
Thus, the bit corresponding to
v
gets flipped to
0
and a vertex from the left partition is added to the solution.
It holds that
R_{t}=R_{t-1}\setminus\{v\}\subseteq S_{t-1}\setminus\{v\}=S_{t}
.
Finally, assume that
v\in S_{t-1}\setminus R_{t-1}
.
Note that the existence of such a vertex
v
implies
R_{t-1}\subseteq S_{t-1}\setminus\{v\}
.
In this case, the Balanced (1+1) EA does nothing and
v
is removed from
S_{t-1}
.
Thus, we have
R_{t}=R_{t-1}\subseteq S_{t-1}\setminus\{v\}=S_{t}
.
In all cases,
R_{t}\subseteq S_{t}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

We are aiming to use the stopping time
T_{S}=\inf\{t\in\mathds{N}\mid\left\lvert S_{t}\right\rvert\geq R-L\}
as a lower bound for
T^{\prime}_{R}
.
The following lemma justifies this.

Lemma 20
.

For
T^{\prime}_{R}
and
T_{S}
as above it holds that
T^{\prime}_{R}\geq T_{S}
.

Proof.

We distinguish between two cases.
If
T_{L}\geq T^{\prime}_{R}
, then
Lemma

19
implies that
R_{t}\subseteq S_{t}
for all
t\leq T^{\prime}_{R}
.
Thus,
\left\lvert R_{t}\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert S_{t}\right\rvert
for all such
t
and especially
R-L\leq\left\lvert R_{T^{\prime}_{R}}\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert S_{T^{\prime}_{R}}\right\rvert
, which implies
T^{\prime}_{R}\geq T_{S}
.
Now, assume
T_{L}<T^{\prime}_{R}
.
Note that this implies that
X_{T_{L}}
is feasible and has a fitness value of
f\left(X_{T_{L}}\right)<R
.
Observe that increasing the number of selected vertices in the right partition would require removing vertices from the left partition.
However, to remain feasible, all vertices from the right partition must be added, resulting in a fitness value of at least
R
.
Thus, we have for all
t>T_{L}
that
\left\lvert R_{t}\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert R_{T_{L}}\right\rvert<R-L
.
Therefore,
T^{\prime}_{R}=\infty
and
T_{S}\leq T^{\prime}_{R}
holds trivially.
∎

Based on
Lemma

20
, the following statement is essentially derived by applying a negative drift argument to
\left\lvert S_{t}\right\rvert
.

Lemma 21
.

Let
T^{\prime}_{R}
be defined as above.
If
c>2
, then, for every positive constant
\xi<1
and all
L\geq\left(\max\left\{2,4/(c-2)\right\}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\xi}}
, there is a constant
\alpha>0
and a function
g(L)\in\Omega(L^{\xi})
such that

\mathds{P}\left[T^{\prime}_{R}\leq 2^{\alpha L^{\xi}}\right]\leq 2^{-g(L)},

given the initial configuration
X_{0}
is chosen uniformly at random.

Proof.

We start by assuming that the initial configuration is chosen such that
\left\lvert R_{0}\right\rvert\leq R-L-L^{\xi}
.
By
Lemma

20
it is sufficient to prove the statement for
T_{S}
instead.
To simplify notation, set
s_{t}=\left\lvert S_{t}\right\rvert
.
Observe that
s_{t}
has the following transition probabilities:

•

\mathds{P}\left[s_{t+1}=s_{t}-1\;\middle|\;s_{t}\right]=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{s_{t}}{L+R}

•

for all
j\in\mathds{N}\cap[1,R-r_{t}]
:

\mathds{P}\left[s_{t+1}=s_{t}+j\;\middle|\;s_{t}\right]=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\binom{R-r_{t}}{j}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{L+R}\right)^{j}\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{L+R}\right)^{R-r_{t}-j}

•

with the remaining probability mass,
s_{t+1}=s_{t}

Note that the second case (i.e.,
s_{t}
increases) can be described more intuitively as follows.
With a probability of
\frac{1}{2}
, we draw a binomial random variable
Q
with
R-r_{t}
trials and success probability
\frac{1}{L+R}
, and
s_{t+1}=s_{t}+Q
.

To bound
T_{S}
, we apply
Theorem

18
to the transformed stochastic process
Y_{t}=R-L-s_{t}
with its natural filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}
and stopping time
T_{Y}=\inf\{t\in\mathds{N}\mid Y_{t}\leq 0\}=T_{S}
.
Note that, for
s_{t}\leq R-L-L^{\xi}
, we have
Y_{t}\geq L^{\xi}
.
To this end, we argue that, for any fixed constant
c>2
and all
L\geq(\frac{4}{c-2})^{\frac{1}{1-\xi}}
, the requirements
(a)
and
(b)
of
Theorem

18
are satisfied for
a=0
,
b=L^{\xi}
,
r(d)=\mathrm{e}^{1/3}\geq 1
,
\delta=\mathrm{e}^{1/3}-1>0
and
\epsilon=\frac{c-2}{4(c+1)}>0
.

Theorem

18
condition
(a)
:

Note that this condition is trivially satisfied for
Y_{t}\leq a=0
and
Y_{t}\geq b=L^{\xi}
due to the indicator functions.
To simplify notation, we assume from now on that
Y_{t}\in(0,L^{\xi})
and omit those indicator functions.
By the definition of
Y_{t}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[Y_{t+1}-Y_{t}\;\middle|\;\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=-\mathds{E}\left[s_{t+1}-s_{t}\;\middle|\;\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left(\frac{R-s_{t}}{R+L}-\frac{s_{t}}{R+L}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2s_{t}-R}{2(R+L)}.

Substituting
s_{t}=R-L-Y_{t}
and
R=cL
, and upper bounding
Y_{t}
by
L^{\xi}
yields

\mathds{E}\left[Y_{t+1}-Y_{t}\;\middle|\;\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]=\frac{R-2L-2Y_{t}}{2(R+L)}\geq\frac{(c-2)}{2(c+1)}-\frac{L^{\xi-1}}{c+1}.

Thus, for
L\geq\left(\frac{4}{c-2}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\xi}}
we have
\mathds{E}\left[Y_{t+1}-Y_{t}\;\middle|\;\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\geq\frac{c-2}{2(c+1)}=\epsilon
.

Theorem

18
condition
(b)
:

Again, we omit the indicator functions and assume
Y_{t}>a=0
.
We do a case distinction based on
j\in\mathds{N}
.
First, observe that for
j\in\{0,1\}
it trivially holds that

\mathds{P}\left[\left\lvert Y_{t+1}-Y_{t}\right\rvert=j\leq 1\right]\leq\frac{\mathrm{e}^{1/3}}{\mathrm{e}^{j/3}}=\frac{r(d)}{(1+\delta)^{j}}

Thus, the condition is satisfied.
For
j\geq 2
, note that such jumps can only appear when
Y_{t}
decreases.
Therefore, we focus on the distribution
Y_{t}-Y_{t+1}=s_{t+1}-s_{t}
.
As discussed earlier, given
s_{t}
,
s_{t+1}-s_{t}
is dominated by a binomial random variable
Q
with
R-s_{t}
trials and success probability
\frac{1}{L+R}
.

Q
in turn is dominated by a binomial random variable
Q^{\prime}
with the same success probability but
L+R
trials.
By Chernoff’s bound (see Theorem
1.10.1
in
[
3
]
) and the fact that
\mathds{E}\left[Q^{\prime}\right]=1
we obtain

\mathds{P}\left[\left\lvert Y_{t+1}-Y_{t}\right\rvert=j\;\middle|\;Y_{t}\right]\leq\mathds{P}\left[Q^{\prime}\geq j\right]\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{(j-1)}{3}}=\frac{r(d)}{(1+\delta)^{j}}.

To save space, we write
\lambda
for
R-L-L^{\xi}
.
Applying
Theorem

18
, we obtain that there is a constant
z>0
and a function
m(d)\in\Omega\left(d\right)
such that
\mathds{P}\left[T_{Y}\leq 2^{\frac{zd}{\mathrm{e}^{1/3}}}\;\middle|\;R_{0}\leq\lambda\right]\leq 2^{-m(d)}
.
Noting that
d=L^{\xi}
and setting
\alpha=\frac{z}{\mathrm{e}^{1/3}}
proves that, for
g^{\prime}(L)=m(L^{\xi})
we obtain

\mathds{P}\left[T^{\prime}_{R}\leq 2^{\alpha L^{\xi}}\;\middle|\;\left\lvert R_{0}\right\rvert\leq\lambda\right]\leq\mathds{P}\left[T_{S}\leq 2^{\alpha L^{\xi}}\;\middle|\;\left\lvert R_{0}\right\rvert\leq\lambda\right]\leq 2^{-g^{\prime}(L)}.

Next, assume we start with an initial configuration
X_{0}
that is chosen uniform at random.
Observe that
\left\lvert R_{0}\right\rvert
follows a binomial distribution with success probability
\frac{1}{2}
and
R
trials.
Next, observe that for
L\geq 2^{\frac{1}{1-\xi}}
and
c>2
it holds that

\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\right)\geq\left(2-\frac{1}{c}\right)\geq\left(2-\frac{2-2L^{\xi-1}}{c}\right)=\frac{cL-L-L^{\xi}}{\frac{c}{2}L}.

As
\mathds{E}\left[\left\lvert R_{0}\right\rvert\right]=\frac{1}{2}R=\frac{c}{2}L
, we have
\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\right)\mathds{E}\left[\left\lvert R_{0}\right\rvert\right]\geq cL-L-L^{\xi}
and by Chernoff’s bound (see Theorem
1.10.1
in
[
3
]
)

\mathds{P}\left[\left\lvert R_{0}\right\rvert>\lambda\right]\leq\mathds{P}\left[\left\lvert R_{0}\right\rvert>\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\right)\mathds{E}\left[\left\lvert R_{0}\right\rvert\right]\right]\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{c}{24}L}.

By using the law of total probability we obtain

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left[T^{\prime}_{R}\leq 2^{\alpha L^{\xi}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}\left[T^{\prime}_{R}\leq 2^{\alpha L^{\xi}}\text{ and }\left\lvert R_{0}\right\rvert\leq\lambda\right]+\mathds{P}\left[T^{\prime}_{R}\leq 2^{\alpha L^{\xi}}\text{ and }\left\lvert R_{0}\right\rvert>\lambda\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{P}\left[T^{\prime}_{R}\leq 2^{\alpha L^{\xi}}\;\middle|\;\left\lvert R_{0}\right\rvert\leq\lambda\right]+\mathds{P}\left[\left\lvert R_{0}\right\rvert>\lambda\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-g^{\prime}(L)}+\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{c}{24}L}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-g(L)}

for some function
g(L)\in\Omega\left(L^{\xi}\right)
, which proves the claim.
∎

Having
Lemma

21
at hand, we are ready to prove
Lemma

16
and hence our main theorem of this section (
Theorem

17
).

Proof of
Lemma

16
.

By
Lemma

21
we know that for
L
sufficiently large and
c>2
there is a constant
\alpha>0
and a function
g(L)\in\Omega(L^{\xi})
such that
\mathds{P}\left[T^{\prime}_{R}\leq 2^{\alpha L^{\xi}}\right]\leq 2^{-g(L)}
.

We know that
\mathds{P}\left[T_{L}<T^{\prime}_{R}\right]\geq\mathds{P}\left[T_{L}\leq\tau\text{ and }T^{\prime}_{R}>\tau\right]
holds for every
\tau\in\mathds{N}
.
Let
T=\inf\{t\in\mathds{N}\mid X_{t}\text{ is feasible }\}
and observe that, if
T^{\prime}_{R}>\tau
, then
T_{L}\leq\tau
if and only if
T\leq\tau
.
Consequently, we have
\mathds{P}\left[T_{L}\leq\tau\text{ and }T^{\prime}_{R}>\tau\right]=\mathds{P}\left[T\leq\tau\text{ and }T^{\prime}_{R}>\tau\right]
.
Moreover, using union bound, we get

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left[T\leq\tau\text{ and }T^{\prime}_{R}>\tau\right]

\displaystyle=1-\mathds{P}\left[T>\tau\text{ or }T^{\prime}_{R}\leq\tau\right]

\displaystyle\geq 1-\mathds{P}\left[T>\tau\right]-\mathds{P}\left[T^{\prime}_{R}\leq\tau\right].

By choosing
\tau=4\mathrm{e}(c+1)L^{2}\left(\ln((c+1)L)+1/2\right)
,
Lemma

2
yields
\mathds{P}\left[T>\tau\right]\leq 2^{-L}
.
Moreover, for
L
sufficiently large, we have
\tau\leq 2^{\alpha L^{\xi}}
.
Thus,
\mathds{P}\left[T^{\prime}_{R}\leq\tau\right]\leq 2^{-g(L)}
for a function
g(L)\in\Omega(L^{\xi})
.
Consequently,
\mathds{P}\left[T_{L}<T^{\prime}_{R}\right]\geq 1-2^{-\Omega\left(L^{\xi}\right)}
and
\mathds{P}\left[T_{L}\geq T^{\prime}_{R}\right]\leq 2^{-\Omega\left(L^{\xi}\right)}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Besides
Theorem

17
, a variety of other properties that might be of independent interest can be derived from
Lemma

16
.
For example, the following corollary shows that the probability of
X
filling the larger side before all vertices in the smaller partition are selected, and the probability to ever select the entire larger side at all, both decay exponentially in
L
as well.

Corollary 22
.

Consider the setting of
Lemma

16
and let
T_{R}=\inf\{t\in\mathds{N}\mid V_{R}\subseteq X_{t}\}
.
If
c>2
, then, for all positive constants
\xi<1
and
L
sufficiently large, it holds that

(1)

\mathds{P}\left[T_{L}\geq T_{R}\right]\leq 2^{-\Omega\left(L^{\xi}\right)}

(2)

\mathds{P}\left[T_{R}<\infty\right]\leq 2^{-\Omega\left(L^{\xi}\right)}
.

Proof.

Part
(1)
follows trivially from
T_{R}\geq T^{\prime}_{R}
.
For part
(2)
, note that, as soon as all vertices in
V_{L}
are selected, adding vertices to the right partition requires removing at least as many vertices on the left.
To remain feasible, however, all free vertices in the right partition must be added at once.
If there are more than
L
vertices in the right partition not selected, this is not possible as there are at most
L
vertices in the left partition in total that could be removed.
Thus, from this point on, no more vertex from the right partition can be added and
T_{L}<T^{\prime}_{R}
implies
T_{R}=\infty
.
∎
