On some convexity and monotonicity inequalities of Elliott Lieb


Abstract
Zusammenfassung A fundamental paper of Elliott Lieb from 1973 has been the basis for much beautiful work on matrix inequalities by many people over the following years. We review a well-connected set of these developments. Some new proofs are provided. Dedicated to Elliott Lieb on the occasion of his 90th birthday.

1.1
Introduction

Elliott Lieb’s work on matrix inequalities has inspired a great many mathematicians and physicists, and the starting point of this paper
is an account of some of the developments relating to the first three theorems in his fundamental 1973 paper
Convex trace functions and the
Wigner-Yanase-Dyson conjecture
. Lieb’s paper contains many results, and not only did it solve what has become known as the Wigner-Yanase-Dyson conjecture, it provided the mathematical tools that were used to resolve another important conjecture made by Lanford and Robinson that was explicitly written out in
[
58
]
, the
strong subadditivity of the quantum mechanical entropy
(SSA).
This conjecture was proved by Lieb and Ruskai
[
62
]
, also in 1973.

However,
[
60
]
also contained theorems that would answer questions that nobody had yet asked, and in some cases,
when
years later
the questions were asked, it was not recognized that the answers were to be found in Lieb’s paper
[
60
]
.
The present paper begins by discussing Theorems 2 and 3 of
[
60
]
, along with Theorem 1, the
Lieb Concavity Theorem, which proved the Wigner-Yanase-Dyson conjecture. These three theorems were all stated
as convexity or concavity theorems, but they have equivalent formulations in terms of monotonicity of certain matrix functionals under
certain classes of completely positive maps. These formulations have turned out to be important to many more recent applications of these results.

These three theorems, as well as two more in the same paper, have been the starting point for many other developments since 1973. We cannot go into all of these developments here – that would require a much longer paper. Instead, we will focus on a selection of developments with a fairly close relation to the first three theorems in
[
60
]
, eventually arriving at the latter two. This paper is essentially expository although there are more than a few new and simpler proofs.

The following notation will be used throughout this paper.
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
denotes the set of
n\times n
complex matrices,
M_{n}^{+}(\mathbb{C})
the set of positive semidefinite
n\times n
complex matrices, and

M_{n}^{++}(\mathbb{C})
the set of positive definite
n\times n
complex matrices. In some cases it is convenient to refer instead to bounded operators on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. To keep the proofs simple, we shall always assume that
\mathcal{H}
is finite dimensional.
This is the heart of the matter. The reader familiar with the necessary technical tools will readily see how to make extensions. For the generalization to a general von Neumann algebra setting that also makes a connection with the matrix algebra case treated here, see Araki’s paper
[
4
]
, and the references it contains.

The paper consists of four parts. Part One consists of Sections 2 and 3, in which the theorems that are our focus are introduced in both their convexity/concavity forms and their monotonicity forms. Some simple but important consequences are noted, and the equivalence of their convexity/concavity and monotonicity formulations is proved.

Part Two consists of Sections 4 through 8, in which a number of applications of the theorems from Part One are made. All of the applications discussed here have some sort of connection with SSA, for which several different proofs are given.

Part Three consists of sections 9 through 11, and these recall some well known material on the Lieb-Ruskai Monotonicity Theorem, the theory of operator monotone and convex functions, the application of the GNS representation to proving matrix inequalities.

Part Four consists of sections 12 and 13, and these are devoted – finally – to the proof of monotonicity theorems discussed in Section 2. Section 12 in particular presents an important theorem of Hiai and Petz
[
45
]
whose proof is amazingly simple, and which yields all of the first three theorems in
[
60
]
as special cases.

1.2
The first three theorems in Lieb’s 1973 matrix inequality paper and their monotonicity formulations

The first three theorems in
[
60
]
are:

Theorem 1.1

(Lieb, Theorem 1).

For
0\leq s,t
and
s+t\leq 1
, and any fixed
K\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, the function

(X,Y)\mapsto\operatorname{Tr}[K^{*}Y^{s}KX^{t}]

(1.2.1)

is jointly concave on
M_{n}^{+}(\mathbb{C})\times M_{n}^{+}(\mathbb{C})
.

The special case
s=t=1/2
had been proved earlier by Wigner and Yanase
[
100
,
101
]
; see the remarks at the end of the section for more information.

Theorem 1.2

(Lieb, Theorem 2).

For all
s,t\geq 0
,
s+t\leq 1
,

(X,Y,K)\mapsto\operatorname{Tr}[K^{*}Y^{-s}KX^{-t}]

(1.2.2)

is jointly convex on
M_{n}^{++}(\mathbb{C})\times M_{n}^{++}(\mathbb{C})\times M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
.

Theorem 1.3

(Lieb, Theorem 3).

(X,Y,K)\mapsto\operatorname{Tr}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}K^{*}\frac{1}{sI+Y}K\frac{1}{sI+X}{\rm d}s\right]\

(1.2.3)

is jointly convex on
M_{n}^{++}(\mathbb{C})\times M_{n}^{++}(\mathbb{C})\times M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
.

Lieb proved Theorem
1.1
using an interesting interpolation argument
and he then deduced Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.3
from it by variational arguments.
However, in the final section of
[
60
]
he proved that these three theorems – along with
two others – are “equivalent” to one another, meaning that once any one of them has been proved, short, elementary arguments yield each of the others.
Lieb also proved certain corollaries of these theorems; e.g., in Theorem
1.1
, one may replace the right side of (
1.2.1
) by
(\operatorname{Tr}[K^{*}Y^{s}KX^{t}])^{1/q}

for all
q
such that
0<q(s+t)\leq 1
, and the concavity still holds. However, this generalization comes rather easily once one has Theorem
1.1
, and we shall not use it in any of the applications discussed here.

In Theorem
1.1
, the Lieb Concavity Theorem, the case in which
s=1-t
is particularly important. In fact, one can easily deduce the general case from this special case, as was pointed out by Araki
[
4
]
: Suppose
s<1-t
and let
r
be such that
r(1-t)=s
. Then for positive invertible
X_{1},X_{2},Y_{1},Y_{2}
,

K^{*}(Y_{1}+Y_{2})/2)^{s}K=K^{*}(((Y_{1}+Y_{2})/2)^{r})^{1-t}K\geq K^{*}((Y_{1}^{r}+Y^{r}_{2})/2)^{1-t}K

where we have used the well known fact that of
Y\mapsto Y^{r}
is monotone increasing and concave in
Y
for
0<r<1
. (See
[
17
]
for the standard integral representation that provides a simple proof.)
Now take the trace against
(X_{1}+X_{2})/2)^{t}
to conclude from the
s=1-t
case of (
1.2.1
) that the general case is valid.
In the same way, one readily shows that (
1.2.2
) follows in general from the special case in which
s=1-t
.

An even more special case of (
1.2.1
) arises when
K
is the identity
I
, and
X
and
Y
are density matrices; i.e.,
\operatorname{Tr}[X]=\operatorname{Tr}[Y]=1
. The

Umegaki relative entropy of
X
with respect to
Y

[
98
]
,

D(X||Y):=\operatorname{Tr}[X(\log X-\log Y)]
,
satisfies

D(X||Y)=\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\operatorname{Tr}[Y^{1-t}X^{t}]\bigg|_{t=1}=\lim_{t\uparrow 1}\frac{1}{1-t}\left(1-\operatorname{Tr}[Y^{1-t}X^{t}]\right)\ .

(1.2.4)

By the Lieb Concavity Theorem, for each
0<t<1
, the right of (
1.2.4
) side is jointly convex in
X
and
Y
, and this yields a
result of Lindblad
[
64
, Lemma 2]
, who used exactly this differentiation argument:

Theorem 1.4

(Lindblad).

The map

(X,Y)\mapsto D(X||Y)

(1.2.5)

is jointly convex on
M_{n}^{+}(\mathbb{C})\times M_{n}^{+}(\mathbb{C})

There are several useful notions of relative entropy for two density matrices, but the one that comes up most frequently is the one introduced by Umegaki, and in the following, when we refer to relative entropy using this short phrase, we mean the Umegaki relative entropy.

Lindblad had been working on proving Thereom
1.4
, and recognized that
[
60
]
provided the solution. A much simpler but still useful fact about the relative entropy is the non-negativity of
D(X||Y)
when
\operatorname{Tr}[X]=\operatorname{Tr}[Y]

For all
X,Y\in M_{n}^{+}(\mathbb{C})
with
\operatorname{Tr}[X]=\operatorname{Tr}[Y]

D(X||Y)\geq 0\quad{\rm and}\quad D(X||Y)=0\ \iff\ X=Y\ .

(1.2.6)

This is an easy consequence of Klein’s inequality
[
54
]
, an elementary proof of which may be found in Appendix A of
[
24
]
, though we shall give another very simple proof of (
1.2.6
) in Section 10.

Theorem
1.2
has an important consequence:

Theorem 1.5

(Ando’s Convexity Theorem).

For all
K\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, and all
1\leq q\leq 2
and
0\leq r\leq 1
with
q-r\geq 1
,

(X,Y)\mapsto\operatorname{Tr}(K^{*}X^{q}KY^{-r})

(1.2.7)

is convex on
M_{n}^{+}(\mathbb{C})\times M_{n}^{++}(\mathbb{C})
.

Beweis.

Note that

\operatorname{Tr}(K^{*}X^{q}KY^{-r})=\operatorname{Tr}((XK)^{*}X^{q-2}(XK)Y^{-r})

and that
0\leq(2-q),r\leq 1
and
(2-q)+r=2-(q-r)\leq 1
. Now apply Theorem
1.2
.
∎

Theorem
1.5
was proved in
[
2
]
by a different argument. The connection with Theorem
1.2
was not noted.

There are by now many simple proofs of the Lieb Concavity Theorem, and hence of Theorems
1.1
,
1.2
and
1.3
; see for instance
[
9
,
37
,
70
]
. However, a 2012 theorem of Hiai and Petz
[
45
]
that is stated and proved in Section 12 is definitive in this direction: It has a very simple proof that directly yields the monotonicity versions of these theorems, as we discuss next, and much more, all at once.

The convexity/concavity theorems, Theorems
1.1
,
1.2
,
1.3
and
1.4
, all have
equivalent
formulations as
monotonicity theorems
, as we now explain, and not only have these been important to a number of their more recent applications, it turns out to be easiest to directly prove the monotonicity variants, and then to deduce the original convexity/concavity results from them.

Let
\Phi:M_{m}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be a linear transformation.
\Phi
is
positive
when
\Phi(A)\geq 0
for all
A\geq 0
.
\Phi
is
2
-positive if the block matrix

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\Phi(A)&\Phi(B)\\
\Phi(C)&\Phi(D)\end{array}\right]\geq 0
whenever
\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}A&B\\
C&D\end{array}\right]\geq 0
. For each integer
k>2
, the condition of
k
-positivity is defined in the analogous manner, and
\Phi
is
completely
positive if it is
k
positive for all
k
. For example, it is easy to see that for any
m\times n
matrix
V
,
the map
\Phi:X\mapsto V^{*}XV
is completely positive, and it follows that for any set
\{V_{1},\dots,V_{\ell}\}
of
m\times n
matrices,

\Phi:X\mapsto\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}V_{j}^{*}XV_{j}\ .

(1.2.8)

is completely positive. By a theorem of Kraus
[
56
]
and Choi
[
29
,
31
]
, every completely positive map
\Phi
from
M_{m}(\mathbb{C})
to
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
has this form, and in fact, one can always find such a representation with
\ell\leq mn
. The map
\Phi
is
unital
if it takes the identity to the identity; i.e.,
\Phi(I)=I
. The map
\Phi
is
trace preserving
if
\operatorname{Tr}[\Phi(X)]=\operatorname{Tr}[X]
for all
X
. For each
n
, we equip
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
with the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product, making it a Hilbert space. For any linear transformation
\Phi:M_{m}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, we write
\Phi^{\dagger}
to denote the adjoint with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product. It is easy to see that if
\Phi
is given by (
1.2.8
), then

\Phi^{\dagger}:X\mapsto\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}V_{j}XV_{j}^{*}\ .

(1.2.9)

In any case, whether
\Phi
is completely positive or not,
\Phi
is unital if and only if
\Phi^{\dagger}
is trace preserving.

Unital completely positive maps
\Phi
play a fundamental role in quantum information theory and the quantum theory of open systems
[
35
]
; these maps update the observables in the time evolution and partial measurement of such a system
[
56
]
. The state of a finite dimensional quantum system may be identified with a
density matrix
, i.e., an
X\in M_{n}^{+}(\mathbb{C})
with
\operatorname{Tr}[X]=1
. If
\Phi
is completely positive and unital, its adjoint

\Phi^{\dagger}
is a completely positive trace preserving map, and so evidently it takes density matrices to density matrices. Such maps
\Phi^{\dagger}
are known as
quantum operations
.

The following example is fundamental: Let
m,n\in{\mathord{\mathbb{N}}}
and identify
\mathbb{C}^{mn}
with the
m
-fold direct sum of
m
copies of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. We may then think of matrices in
M_{mn}(\mathbb{C})
as
m\times m
block matrices each of whose entries is in
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. Then, suppressing the
n
dependence, for each
m
we define

\Psi_{m}(X)=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}X&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&X\end{array}\right]\ ,

(1.2.10)

where the matrix on the right is the
m\times m
block diagonal matrix each of whose diagonal entries is
X
. This is evidently completely positive and unital. Its adjoint,
\Psi_{m}^{\dagger}
, is therefore trace preserving and completely positive. It is easy to see that

\Psi^{\dagger}_{m}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}X_{1,1}&\cdots&X_{1,m}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
X_{m,1}&\cdots&X_{m,m}\end{array}\right]\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}X_{j,j}\ ,

(1.2.11)

is the
partial trace
.
Some of the theorems that follow involve negative powers. When
\Phi:M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{m}(\mathbb{C})
is unital and completely positive,
\Phi(A)\in M_{m}^{++}(\mathbb{C})
whenever
A\in M_{n}^{++}(\mathbb{C})
, However, the image of
M_{m}^{++}(\mathbb{C})
under the completely positive trace preserving map
\Phi^{\dagger}
may lie entirely in
M_{n}^{+}(\mathbb{C})\backslash M_{n}^{++}(\mathbb{C})
. For example, take
n=2m
and think of elements of
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
as
2\times 2
block matrices with entries in
M_{m}(\mathbb{C})
. Then define

\Phi\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}A&B\\
C&D\end{array}\right]\right)=A\qquad{\rm so\ that}\qquad\Phi^{\dagger}(A)=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}A&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right]\ .

We may now state the monotonicity versions of Theorems
1.1
,
1.2
,
1.3
and
1.4
.

Theorem 1.6

(Monotonicity version of Theorem
1.1
).

For all
0\leq t\leq 1
, all
m,n\in{\mathord{\mathbb{N}}}
, all
X,Y\in M_{m}^{+}(\mathbb{C})
, all
K\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, and all completely positive unital maps
\Phi:M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{m}(\mathbb{C})
,

\operatorname{Tr}[\Phi(K^{*})Y^{1-t}\Phi(K)X^{t}]\leq\operatorname{Tr}[K^{*}\Phi^{\dagger}(Y)^{1-t}K\Phi^{\dagger}(X)^{t}]\ .

(1.2.12)

Theorem 1.7

(Monotonicity version of Theorem
1.2
).

For all
0\leq t\leq 1
, all
m,n\in{\mathord{\mathbb{N}}}
, all

X,Y\in M_{m}^{++}(\mathbb{C})
and all
K\in M_{m}(\mathbb{C})
, and all completely positive unital maps
\Phi:M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{m}(\mathbb{C})
such that
\Phi^{\dagger}(X),\Phi^{\dagger}(Y)\in M_{n}^{++}(\mathbb{C})

\operatorname{Tr}[\Phi^{\dagger}(K^{*})\Phi^{\dagger}(Y)^{t-1}\Phi^{\dagger}(K)\Phi^{\dagger}(X)^{-t}]\leq\operatorname{Tr}[K^{*}Y^{t-1}KX^{-t}]\ .

(1.2.13)

Theorem 1.8

(Monotonicity version of Theorem
1.3
).

For all
0\leq t\leq 1
, all
m,n\in{\mathord{\mathbb{N}}}
, all
X,Y\in M_{m}^{++}(\mathbb{C})
, all
K\in M_{m}(\mathbb{C})
and all completely positive unital maps
\Phi:M_{m}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{m}(\mathbb{C})
,

\operatorname{Tr}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi^{\dagger}(K^{*})\frac{1}{sI+\Phi^{\dagger}(Y)}\Phi^{\dagger}(K)\frac{1}{sI+\Phi^{\dagger}(X)}{\rm d}s\right]\leq\operatorname{Tr}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}K^{*}\frac{1}{sI+Y}K\frac{1}{sI+X}{\rm d}s\right]\ .

(1.2.14)

Theorem 1.9

(Monotonicity version of Theorem
1.4
).

For all
m,n\in{\mathord{\mathbb{N}}}
, all
X,Y\in M_{m}^{+}(\mathbb{C})
, and all completely positive unital maps
\Phi:M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{m}(\mathbb{C})
,

D(\Phi^{\dagger}(X)||\Phi^{\dagger}(Y))\leq D(X||Y)\ .

(1.2.15)

The inequality (
1.2.15
) is known as the
Data Processing Inequality
, and is due to Lindblad
[
64
]
. It implies that all quantum operations
performed on density matrices
X
and
Y
reduce their relative entropy, and this has the effect of making the states harder to
experimentally distinguish. It is one of the cornerstones of quantum information theory; see the introduction of
[
19
]
for more information.
The first proof of Theorem
1.6
in this form is due to Uhlmann
[
97
, Proposition 17]
.

The passage from Theorems
1.6
,
1.7
,
1.8
,
1.9
and
1.10
back to Theorems
1.1
,
1.2
,
1.3
,
1.4
and
1.5

is easy to explain: In each case, one takes
\Phi^{\dagger}
to be the partial trace map
\Psi_{2}^{\dagger}
where
\Psi_{m}
is defined in (
1.2.10
). For instance, suppose we have proved Theorem
1.6
.
Let
X_{1}
,
X_{2}
,
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
be positive definite in
M_{m}(\mathbb{C})
, and define

X=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}X_{1}&0\\
0&X_{2}\end{array}\right]\qquad{\rm and}\qquad Y=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}Y_{1}&0\\
0&Y_{2}\end{array}\right]\ .

Then for any
K\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
,

Y^{1-t}\Psi_{2}(K)X^{t}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}Y_{1}^{1-t}KX_{1}^{t}&0\\
0&Y_{2}^{1-t}KX_{2}^{t}\end{array}\right]

so that
\operatorname{Tr}[\Psi_{2}(K^{*})Y^{1-t}\Psi_{2}(K)X^{t}]=\operatorname{Tr}[K^{*}Y_{1}^{1-t}KX_{1}^{t}]+\operatorname{Tr}[K^{*}Y_{2}^{1-t}KX_{2}^{t}]

\operatorname{Tr}[K^{*}\Psi_{2}^{\dagger}(Y)^{1-t}K\Psi_{2}^{\dagger}(X)^{t}]=\operatorname{Tr}[K^{*}(Y_{1}+Y_{2})^{1-t}K(X_{1}+X_{2})^{t}]\ .

Then by (

\operatorname{Tr}[K^{*}Y_{1}^{1-t}KX_{1}^{t}]+\operatorname{Tr}[K^{*}Y_{2}^{1-t}KX_{2}^{t}]\leq\operatorname{Tr}[K^{*}(Y_{1}+Y_{2})^{1-t}K(X_{1}+X_{2})^{t}]\ .

Since
(X,Y)\mapsto\operatorname{Tr}[K^{*}Y^{1-t}KX^{t}]
is homogeneous of degree one, this is equivalent to (

) for

, and we have already explained how this implies the general case. This proves that Theorem

The passage from Theorems
1.1
,

,

and

,

,

and

is
almost as simple. It relies on the Stinespring factorization

of any completely positive trace preserving map

into the composition of three

Then, a very useful lemma of Uhlmann
[
95

that expresses partial traces as averages over unitary

One might expect, in analogy with Theorem
1.7
, that there would be a monotonicity variant of the Ando Concavity Theorem
1.5
stating that
for all
0\leq t\leq 1
, all
m,n\in{\mathord{\mathbb{N}}}
, all completely positive unital maps
\Phi:M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{m}(\mathbb{C})
,
all
Y\in M_{m}^{+}(\mathbb{C})
and
X\in M_{m}^{++}(\mathbb{C})
with
\Phi^{\dagger}(X)\in M_{n}^{++}(\mathbb{C})
, and all
K\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
,

\operatorname{Tr}[\Phi(K^{*})Y^{1+t}\Phi(K)X^{-t}]\geq\operatorname{Tr}[K^{*}\Phi^{\dagger}(Y)^{1+t}K\Phi^{\dagger}(X)^{-t}]\ .

(1.2.16)

However, (
1.2.16
) is false
. Consider the completely positive map
\Phi:M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
given by
\Phi(A)=\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Tr}[A]I
. Note that this is both unital and trace preserving,
and
\Phi^{\dagger}=\Phi
. Choosing

with

, the left side of (

\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Tr}[K^{*}K](\operatorname{Tr}[Y])^{1-t}(\operatorname{Tr}[X])^{-t}>0
.
However, we do have monotonicity under partial traces, which is shown to follow from the convexity in the next section:

Theorem 1.10

(Restricted monotonicity version of Theorem
1.5
).

For all
0\leq t\leq 1
, all
m,n\in{\mathord{\mathbb{N}}}
,
all
Y\in M_{mn}^{+}(\mathbb{C})
and
X\in M_{mn}^{++}(\mathbb{C})
, with
\Psi_{m}
given by (

\operatorname{Tr}[\Psi_{m}(K^{*})Y^{1+t}\Psi_{m}(K)X^{-t}]\geq\operatorname{Tr}[K^{*}\Psi_{m}^{\dagger}(Y)^{1+t}K\Psi_{m}^{\dagger}(X)^{-t}]\ .

(1.2.17)

We close this section with some remarks on the work of Wigner and Yanase
[
100
,
101
]
. A 1960 paper of Araki and Yanase
[
7
]
showed that the extent to which a quantum observable fails to commute with a conserved quantity limits the precision with which it can be measured. A few years later, when Yanase was visiting the I.A.S. in Princeton, he and Wigner investigated the implications for measuring the amount of information in a quantum state
\rho
; i.e., a density matrix. They presented a list of axioms that a good “measure of information” should satisfy, and in the case of a quantum mechanical density matrix
\rho
and a self-adjoint operator
H
, representing some conserved quantity such as the energy, they proposed
[
100
, Equation 2]
what they called the
skew information
:

I(\rho,H):=-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}[[\rho^{1/2},H]^{2}]=\operatorname{Tr}[H^{2}\rho]-\operatorname{Tr}[H\rho^{1/2}H\rho^{1/2}]

Wigner and Yanase proved that for fixed
H
,
\rho\mapsto I(\rho,H)
is convex, and this is evidently equivalent to the concavity of
\rho\mapsto\operatorname{Tr}[H\rho^{1/2}H\rho^{1/2}]
.
At first glance, this may look like a special case of (
1.2.1
) for
s=t=\tfrac{1}{2}
, but notice that since the convexity proved by Wigner and Yanase does not depend on the normalization of
\rho
, we may consider arbitrary
X,Y\in M_{n}^{++}
and
K\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
and form the block matrices

\rho:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}Y&0\\
0&X\end{array}\right]\quad{\rm and}\quad H:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&K\\
K^{*}&0\end{array}\right]

and then

\operatorname{Tr}[H\rho^{1/2}H\rho^{1/2}]=2\operatorname{Tr}[K^{*}Y^{1/2}KX^{1/2}]\ ,

(1.2.18)

and hence the convexity result of Wigner and Yanase is equivalent to the special case
s=t=\frac{1}{2}
of the Lieb Concavity Theorem.

In the final paragraph of
[
100
]
, Wigner and Yanase wrote that they were not sure that their proposed measure of information is the only one satisfying the axioms they specified, and they reported that they had also considered
-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}[[\log\rho,H][\rho,H]]
. They also remark that Freeman Dyson pointed out that these cases fell into a one parameter family of candidates

-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}[[\rho^{t},H][\rho^{1-t},H]]\ ,

(1.2.19)

0<t\leq 1/2
since

\operatorname{Tr}[[\log\rho,H][\rho,H]]=\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{t}\operatorname{Tr}[[\rho^{t},H][\rho^{1-t},H]]\ .

However, it is not clear that Wigner, Yanase or even Dyson conjectured anything about concavity or convexity in these cases,
and Wigner and Yanase wrote that these other candidates have “undesirable” features, and appear to have been dismissive of their further study.

Nonetheless, Res Jost gave a partially alternate proof
[
51
]
of the theorem of Wigner and Yanase that was written during a visit he made to the I.A.S. in Princeton during Fall 1968. He thanks his friend Freeman Dyson for many discussions on the subject, and mentions the generalized cases proposed by Dyson. Jost does not ascribe any explicit conjecture to Dyson, but it certainly would appear that five years after the work of Wigner and Yanase, Dyson felt that his proposed functionals were worthy of investigation.

From Convexity or Concavity to Monotonicity

A theorem of Stinespring
[
87

provides a factorization of any unital completely positive map
\Phi

One of the factors has already been introduced: For
m\in{\mathord{\mathbb{N}}}

be defined by (

\Psi_{m}^{\dagger}
is defined by

Next, for

be the map from
M_{q}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{p}(\mathbb{C})
that sends

to upper left

Then evidently,
\Xi^{\dagger}_{q,p}
is the map sending

to the matrix in
M_{q}(\mathbb{C})
whose upper left
p\times p

It is easy to write down the Kraus representation of
\Xi_{q,p}

Let

be linear transformation by which the first

are the same as those of

, in the same order,
and the rest, if any, are zero. Then for all

\Xi_{q,p}(X)=W^{*}XW
. Evidently,

\Xi_{q,p}
is completely positive and unital, and
\Xi^{\dagger}_{q,p}
is completely positive and trace preserving.

The following is a version of the Stinespring Representation Theorem for unital and completely positive maps in the finite dimensional case. It is well-known to people who work on quantum information theory, but I do not know of an accessible written reference in this convenient form. For a thorough discussion of the standard operator-algebraic formulation, see
[
71
]
.

Theorem 1.11

(Stinespring Representation).

Let
\Phi:M_{p}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{q}(\mathbb{C})
be completely positive and unital. Then there is a natural number
m
such that
mp\geq q
, and a unitary
U\in M_{mp}(\mathbb{C})
such that for all
X\in M_{p}(\mathbb{C})

\Phi(X)=\Xi_{mq,q}(U^{*}\Psi_{m}(X)U)\ ,

(1.3.1)

and consequently for all
Y\in M_{q}(\mathbb{C})
,

\Phi^{\dagger}(Y)=\Psi_{m}^{\dagger}(U\Xi_{mp,q}^{\dagger}(Y)U^{*})\ .

(1.3.2)

In other words, by (
1.3.1
) every unital completely positive map from
M_{p}(\mathbb{C})
to
M_{q}(\mathbb{C})
acting on
X\in M_{p}(\mathbb{C})
consists of
first
sending

X
to the block diagonal
m\times m
matrix each of whose diagonal entries is
X
,
second
applying a unitary conjugation to the

mp\times mp
matrix,
and third
picking off the upper left
q\times q
corner of the result.

Likewise, by (
1.3.2
) every trace preserving completely positive map from
M_{q}(\mathbb{C})
to
M_{p}(\mathbb{C})
acting on
Y\in M_{q}(\mathbb{C})
consists of
first
embedding
Y
as the upper left
q\times q
corner of matrix in
M_{mp}(\mathbb{C})
whose other entries are all zero,
second
, applying a unitary conjugation, and the regarding the result as an
m\times m
block matrix with entries in
M_{p}(\mathbb{C})
,
and third
, taking the partial trace.

The following lemma due to Uhlmann
[
96
]
connects partial traces with convexity.

Lemma 1.12
.

There is an explicit set
\{U_{1},\dots,U_{m^{2}}\}
of unitaries in
M_{mn}(\mathbb{C})
such that for all

,

\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{m^{2}}U_{k}^{*}YU_{k}=\frac{1}{m}\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\Psi_{m}^{\dagger}(Y)&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&\Psi_{m}^{\dagger}(Y)\end{array}\right]=\frac{1}{m}\Psi_{m}(\Psi_{m}^{\dagger}(Y))=\frac{1}{m}I_{\mathbb{C}^{m}}\otimes\Psi_{m}^{\dagger}(Y)\ ,

(1.3.3)

where we have used the obvious identification of
\mathbb{C}^{mn}
with
\mathbb{C}^{m}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

We provide a simple proof for completeness.

Beweis.

The second and third equalities in (
1.3.3
) are evident, and we need only prove the first. Write vectors
v\in\mathbb{C}^{mn}
in the form

v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{m})
where each entry is in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
For each
j=1,\dots,m
, let
P_{j}
be the orthogonal projection on
\mathbb{C}^{mn}
leaves the
\ell
th entry of
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{m})
as it is, and sends the others to
0
. That is,

P_{1}(v)=(v_{1},0,\dots 0)
,
P_{2}(v)=(0,v_{2},0,\dots,)
, etc.
For
k=1,\dots,m
, define
{\displaystyle U_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}e^{i2\pi jk/m}J_{j}}
.
Since
J_{k}J_{\ell}=\delta_{k,\ell}P_{\ell}
, each

is unitary. Moreover,

\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}U_{k}^{*}YU_{k}=\sum_{j,\ell=1}^{m}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}(e^{i2\pi(j-\ell)/m})^{k}\right)P_{\ell}YP_{j}=\sum_{j,\ell=1}^{m}\delta_{j,\ell}P_{\ell}YP_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}P_{j}YP_{j}\ .

That is,

\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}U_{k}^{*}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}Y_{1,1}&\cdots&Y_{1,m}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
Y_{m,1}&\cdots&Y_{m.m}\end{array}\right]\right)U_{k}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}Y_{1,1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&Y_{m,m}\end{array}\right]

where the right hand side denotes the block diagonal matrix whose
j
th diagonal block is
Y_{j,j}
. Now for
1\leq\ell\leq m
define
C_{\ell}
to be the
\ell
th cyclic permutation matrix acting on the
m
-fold direct sum of
\mathbb{C}^{m}
by

C_{j}(v_{1},\dots,v_{m})=(v_{j},\dots,v_{m},v_{1},\dots)\ .

Then evidently the
m^{2}
unitary matrices of the form
C_{\ell}U_{k}
,
1\leq k,\ell\leq m
have the desired property.
∎

Remark 1.13
.

Suppose

&\ddots&\\

Proof of Theorem

&A_{2}\end{array}\right]

&B_{2}\end{array}\right]

and the fact that the relative entropy is homogeneous of degree one.

Therefore, for all

\displaystyle D(\Psi_{m}^{\dagger}(X)\ ||\ \Psi_{m}^{\dagger}(Y))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D\left(\frac{1}{m}\Psi_{m}\Psi_{m}^{\dagger}(X)\ \bigg|\bigg|\ \frac{1}{m}\Psi_{m}\Psi_{m}^{\dagger}(Y)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D\left(\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{m^{2}}U_{k}^{*}XU_{k}\ \bigg|\bigg|\ \frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{m^{2}}U_{k}^{*}YU_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{m^{2}}D(U_{k}^{*}XU_{k}||U_{k}^{*}YU_{k})=D(X||Y)\ .

The first equality is the additivity and homogeneity discussed above, the second it Uhlmann’s Lemma. The inequality is the joint convexity, and the final equality is the unitary invariance of the relative entropy. This proves the monotonicity of the relative entropy under partial traces.

The rest is an easy consequence of Theorem
1.11
It is evident that for

since the extra zero entries change nothing. Again using the unitary invariance,

That is, the first two components in the factorization of

\Psi_{m}^{\dagger}

1.4
Strong subadditivity of the quantum entropy

The first application of the inequalities proved in
[
60
]
was the proof of the
strong subadditivity of the quantum entropy
(SSA) conjecture. To explain,
recall that if
\rho
is a density matrix on a Hilbert space, its von Neumann entropy,
S(\rho)
, is defined by

S(\rho)=-\operatorname{Tr}[\rho\log\rho]\ .

Now consider a density matrix
\rho_{12}
on a bipartite Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2}
. Let
\rho_{1}
be the partial trace of
\rho_{12}
over
\mathcal{H}_{2}
; i.e.,
\rho_{1}=\operatorname{Tr}_{2}[\rho_{12}]
, and define
\rho_{2}
in the analogous manner. Then
\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2}
is a density matrix on
\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2}
, and

\displaystyle 0\leq D(\rho_{12}||\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{12}(\log\rho_{12}-\log{\rho_{1}}\otimes I-I\otimes\log\rho_{2})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-S(\rho_{12})+S(\rho_{1})+S(\rho_{2})\ ,

where the initial inequality is (
1.2.6
).
Going forward, it will be useful to simplify our notation and write
S_{12}=S(\rho_{12})
,
S_{1}=S(\rho_{1})
, etc., and to simply write
\log\rho_{1}
in place of
\log{\rho_{1}}\otimes I
, etc. Then from the calculation just made

S_{12}\leq S_{1}+S_{2}\quad{\rm and}\quad S_{12}=S_{1}+S_{2}\ \iff\ \rho_{12}=\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2}\ .

This relatively elementary, but physically important, inequality is known as the
subadditivity of the quantum entropy
.

SSA concerns a density matrix
\rho_{123}
on a tripartite Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{3}
. The 1968 conjecture of Lanford and Robinson
[
58
]
was that

S_{12}+S_{23}\geq S_{123}+S_{2}\ .

(1.4.1)

If
\mathcal{H}_{2}
is one dimensional, then (
1.4.1
) reduces to
S_{1}+S_{3}\geq S_{13}
; i.e., to subadditivity, and this justifies the name strong subadditivity.

The name SSA is generally used in the statistical mechanics literature for what is known in classical information theory as
positivity of the conditional mutual information
,
as discussed at the end of this section. The term SSA was introduced in the context of classical statistical mechanics by Robinson and Ruelle
[
79
, Proposition 1]
who gave a proof in this context,
and applied it to prove of the existence of the thermodynamic limit in classical statistical mechanics. Then in 1968, Lanford and Robinson attempted to apply the methods
of
[
79
]
in the quantum setting, but fell short as they could not prove quantum SSA. They explicitly stated the conjecture that (
1.4.1
) was valid in the quantum setting
[
58
, p. 1125]
,
writing, in reference to classical SSA, that: “One could believe, and even support one’s belief by heuristic physical arguments that the same condition holds for the quantum entropy".
For an overview of the physical context, see the review article of Wehrl
[
99
]
.

The classical analog is elementary to prove, and this is done below, but the
usual
classical proof uses conditional probabilities. Recall that if
\rho(x,y)
is a joint probability
distribution for two random variables, and
\rho(x)
is the marginal distribution for one of them, the corresponding conditional probability density
\rho(x|y)
is
\rho(x,y)/\rho(y)
. Unfortunately in the quantum setting there are many ways one might try divide
\rho_{12}
by
\rho_{2}
, but none of them yields a
satisfactory notion of conditional quantum probability, and none of them provide a basis for adapting the easy proof of classical SSA to the quantum case.

However, once the results from
[
60
]
were available, Lieb and Ruskai
[
62
]
proved SSA, also in 1973. Moreover, they gave several equivalent reformulations of SSA that have turned out to be very important in their own right. One of these involves the notion of
conditional entropy
:

Definition 1.14
.

Given a density matrix
\rho_{12}
on
\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2}
, considered as a state on a bipartite system, the conditional entropy of system 1 with respect to system 2 is defined to be
S_{12}-S_{1}
.

At the end of this section, we explain the connection with the concept of conditional entropy in classical probability theory,
but since the whole notion of conditional probability is problematic in quantum mechanics, the origins of the name are largely irrelevant for present purposes.

Theorem 1.15

(Lieb Ruskai 1973, SSA and two equivalent formulations).

The following statements are all true, and equivalent in that once any one of them is proven, simple arguments yield the other two:

(1) For all density matrices
\rho_{123}
on
\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{3}
, (
1.4.1
) is satisfied.

(2) The map

\rho_{12}\mapsto S_{2}-S_{12}

is convex and homogeneous of degree one on the set of density matrices
\rho_{12}
on
\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2}
.

(3) The relative entropy functional
(\rho,\sigma)\mapsto D(\rho||\sigma)
is montone under partial traces; i.e., for all density matrices
\rho_{1,2},\sigma_{1,2}
on
\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2}
,
D(\rho_{1}||\sigma_{1})\leq D(\rho_{12}||\sigma_{12})
.

As in
[
62
]
, we use an elementary Lemma from Lieb’s 1973 paper,
[
60
, Lemma 5]
, whose simple statement and proof we recall. (It shall be applied several times in what follows.)

Lemma 1.16

(Lieb 1973).

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a convex cone in a vector space, and let
F:\mathcal{C}\to{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}
be homogeneous of degree one. Define

G(x,y):=\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{F(x+ty)-F(x)}{t}\ .

If
F
is convex, then for all
x,y\in\mathcal{C}
,
G(x,y)\leq F(y)
. Conversely, if
F
is continuously differentiable and
G(x,y)\leq F(y)
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{C}
,
then
F
is convex.

Beweis.

Assume first that
F
is convex, and compute

\displaystyle F(x+ty)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1+t)F\left(\frac{1}{1+t}x+\frac{t}{1+t}y\right)\leq(1+t)\left(\frac{1}{1+t}F(x)+\frac{t}{1+t}F(y)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F(x)+tF(y)\ .

Next assume
F
is continuously differentiable and
G(x,y)\leq F(y)
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{C}
. For
0<\lambda<1
,
x,y\in\mathcal{C}
,
let
z:=\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y
. Then

F(z)-\lambda F(x)=F(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)-F(\lambda x)=\int_{0}^{1-\lambda}G(\lambda x+sy,y){\rm d}s\leq(1-\lambda)F(y)\ .

∎

Proof of Theorem
1.15
.

First we show that
(3)

\Rightarrow

(1)
:
Let
\tau_{3}
denote the density matrix on

\mathcal{H}_{3}
that is a multiple of the identity, i.e.
\tau_{3}=(d_{3})^{-1}I_{3}
where
d_{3}
is the dimension of
\mathcal{H}_{3}
.
Since
D(X||Y)=\operatorname{Tr}[X(\log X-\log Y)]
,

D(\rho_{123}||\rho_{12}\otimes\tau_{3})=-S_{123}+S_{12}+\log d_{3}\quad{\rm and}\quad D(\rho_{23}||\rho_{2}\otimes\tau_{3})=-S_{23}+S_{2}+\log d_{3}\ .

Therefore

S_{12}+S_{23}-S_{123}-S_{2}=D(\rho_{123}||\rho_{12}\otimes\tau_{3})-D(\rho_{23}||\rho_{2}\otimes\tau_{3})\ .

(1.4.2)

Let
\Phi^{\dagger}
denote the partial trace over
\mathcal{H}_{1}
so that
\Phi^{\dagger}

is completely positive and trace preserving. Then since

\Phi^{\dagger}(\rho_{123})=\rho_{23}
and
\Phi^{\dagger}(\rho_{12}\otimes\tau_{3})=\rho_{2}\otimes\tau_{3}
,

D(\rho_{123}||\rho_{12}\otimes\tau_{3})-D(\rho_{23}||\rho_{2}\otimes\tau_{3})\geq 0
as a consequence of the monotonicity of the relative entropy under partial traces, and then by (
1.4.2
), this implies (
1.4.1
).

We next prove that
(1)

\Rightarrow

(2)
.
For two density matrices
\rho_{12}
and
\sigma_{12}
on

\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2}
, take
\mathcal{H}_{3}
to be the
\mathbb{C}^{2}
with the usual inner product and define the tripartite state

\frac{1}{2}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\rho_{12}&0\\
0&\sigma_{12}\end{array}\right]
.
Then

S_{123}=\log 2-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{12}\log\rho_{12}]-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}[\sigma_{12}\log\sigma_{12}]\ ,

S_{23}=\log 2-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{2}\log\rho_{2}]-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}[\sigma_{2}\log\sigma_{2}]\ ,\quad S_{12}=-\operatorname{Tr}\left[\frac{\rho_{12}+\sigma_{12}}{2}\log\left(\frac{\rho_{12}+\sigma_{12}}{2}\right)\right]\ ,

and

S_{2}=-\operatorname{Tr}\left[\frac{\rho_{2}+\sigma_{2}}{2}\log\left(\frac{\rho_{2}+\sigma_{2}}{2}\right)\right]\ .

Now (
1.4.1
) yields

S\left(\frac{\rho_{2}+\sigma_{2}}{2}\right)-S\left(\frac{\rho_{12}+\sigma_{12}}{2}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(-S(\rho_{2})-S(\rho_{12}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(S(\sigma_{2})-S(\sigma_{12}\right)\ .

This proves the convexity, and the homogeneity is obvious.

We next prove that
(2)

\Rightarrow

(3)
.
Fix two density matrices
\rho_{12}
and
\sigma_{12}
on
\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2}
, and compute

\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{t}(S(\sigma_{12}+t\rho_{12})-S(\sigma_{12}))=-1-\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{12}\log\sigma_{12}]\ .

By Lemma
1.16
,

(-1-\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{2}\log\sigma_{2}])-\left(-1-\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{12}\log\sigma_{12}]\right)\leq-\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{2}\log\rho_{2}]+\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{12}\log\rho_{12}]
.
Rearranging terms,

D(\rho_{12}||\sigma_{12})\geq D(\rho_{2}||\sigma_{2})
,
which is the monotonicity of the relative entropy under partial traces.

Finally, since the monotonicity of the relative entropy under partial traces is a special case of the DPI (from which the general case can be deduced), all three of the equivalent statements are valid.
∎

Remark 1.17
.

Because the joint convexity of the relative entropy, and hence the DPI, is a simple consequence of the Lieb Concavity Theorem, one can view all of these equivalent statements as fairly direct consequences of Theorem
1.1
. Furthermore, if one applies the converse of Lemma
1.16
, one easily obtains a direct proof that (3) implies (2). Likewise, another application of Uhlmann’s Lemma shows that
{\it(2)}
can be rephrased as saying that the conditional entropy is monotone under partial traces, and then it is easy to see that this yields SSA.

The proof that we have presented here is somewhat different from the proof in
[
62
]
. In
[
62
]
it is proved that (1) and (2) are equivalent, both of which are also given direct proofs, and the paper ends by showing in the final paragraph that (2) implies (3) without mentioning the term “relative entropy” by name, and without noting the equivalence of (2) and (3) which follows directly from the second half of Lemma
1.16
, which was used for such purposes twice in
[
60
]
.

As explained in Section 3, the Data Processing Inequality (DPI) (
1.2.15
) is easily seen to be equivalent to the monotonicity of the relative entropy under partial traces, and hence the DPI is simply an equivalent formulation of SSA. It is in this form that SSA finds its widest use today, in quantum information theory, although as explained in Section 5, the original formulation has important applications there as well.

The conjecture of Lanford and Robinson attracted much attention, and many people had worked to prove it during the half decade it was open. Among the first published papers resulting from efforts in this direction is a paper of Araki and Lieb
[
6
]
. We quote Theorem 1 of that paper:

Theorem 1.18

(Araki-Lieb 1970).

Let
\rho_{123}
be a tripartite density matrix. Then

S_{12}+S_{23}\geq S_{123}-\log\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{2}^{2}]\ .

(1.4.3)

If
\rho_{2}
commutes with
\rho_{12}
or
\rho_{23}
, then (
1.4.1
) is valid.

By Jensen’s Inequality, and the concavity of the logarithm, if
\{\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}\}
are the eigenvalues of
\rho_{2}
, repeated according to multiplicity,

\log\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{2}^{2}]=\log\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}\lambda_{j}\right)\geq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}\log\lambda_{j}\ .

Therefore,

-\log\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{2}^{2}]\leq S(\rho_{2})
, and hence (
1.4.3
) is weaker than (
1.4.1
).

Nonetheless, Theorem
1.18
, together with the Araki-Lieb Triangle Inequality, also proved in
[
6
]
and discussed in the next section,
were sufficient
[
6
]
to solve the quantum thermodynamic limit problem that had motivated the conjecture of Lanford and Robinson in the first place.
Despite this, interest in proving the original conjecture remained strong, and the progress of Araki and Lieb influenced future work.
Hence it is worthwhile to recall the simple proof of Theorem
1.18
. The proof uses the well-known Golden-Thompson inequality
[
41
,
90
]
which states that
for all self-adjoint
H,K\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
,

\operatorname{Tr}[e^{H+K}]\leq\operatorname{Tr}[e^{H}e^{K}]\ ,

(1.4.4)

and the Peierls-Bogoliubov Inequality which states that for self-adjoint
H
and
K
with
\operatorname{Tr}[e^{H}]=1
,

\operatorname{Tr}[Ke^{H}]\leq\log\operatorname{Tr}[e^{H+K}]\ .

(1.4.5)

An elementary proof can be found in many places; e.g.,
[
24
, Appendix A]
. Note that if
\rho(x)
is a classical probability density on a measure space
(X,\mu)
, and
f(x)
is any bounded, real valued function on
X
,

\int_{X}f(x)\rho(x){\rm d}\mu\leq\log\left(\int_{X}e^{f(x)}\rho(x){\rm d\mu}\right)=\log\left(\int_{X}e^{f(x)+\log\rho(x)}{\rm d\mu}\right)\ ,

(1.4.6)

by Jensen’s inequality for the exponential function. Replacing integrals by traces,
f
by
K
and
\rho
by
e^{H}
, the left side of (
1.4.6
) becomes
the left side of (
1.4.5
). The two terms on the right hand side of (
1.4.6
) become
\log(\operatorname{Tr}[e^{K}e^{H}])
and
\log(\operatorname{Tr}[e^{H+K}])
, which are no longer equal. By the Golden-Thompson inequality,
\log(\operatorname{Tr}[e^{H+K}])\leq\log(\operatorname{Tr}[e^{K}e^{H}])
, and the utility of the Peierls-Bogoliubov Inequality lies in the fact that it is the
smaller
of these two quantities that appears on the right side of (
1.4.5
). This is essential in the application to follow.

Proof of Theorem
1.18
.

Assume that
\rho_{123}
is positive definite, and define

\Delta:=S_{123}-S_{12}-S_{23}=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{123}(-\log\rho_{123}+\log\rho_{12}+\log\rho_{23})]\ .

Now apply (
1.4.5
) taking
H=\log\rho_{123}
and
K=-\log\rho_{123}+\log\rho_{12}+\log\rho_{23}

e^{\Delta}\leq\operatorname{Tr}[\exp(\log\rho_{123}-\log\rho_{123}+\log\rho_{12}+\log\rho_{23})]=\\
\operatorname{Tr}[\exp(\log\rho_{12}+\log\rho_{23}))]\leq\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{12}\rho_{23}]=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{2}^{2}]

where the second inequality is (
1.4.4
). This proves (
1.4.3
).

Now suppose
\rho_{2}
commutes with
\rho_{23}
, and make a different definition of
\Delta
:

\Delta:=S_{123}+S_{2}-S_{12}-S_{23}=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{123}(-\log\rho_{123}-\log\rho_{2}+\log\rho_{12}+\log\rho_{23})]\ .

Using the Peierls-Bogoliubov Inequality as before,

e^{\Delta}\leq\operatorname{Tr}[\exp(-\log\rho_{2}+\log\rho_{12}+\log\rho_{23})]\ .

(1.4.7)

Then again by the Golden-Thompson Inequality, and then the fact that
\rho_{2}
commutes with
\rho_{23}
,

e^{\Delta}\leq\operatorname{Tr}[\exp(-\log\rho_{2}+\rho_{12}+\log\rho_{23}))]\leq\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{12}\exp(-\log\rho_{2}+\log\rho_{23})]=\\
\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{12}\rho_{2}^{-1}\rho_{23}]=1\ .

Therefore,
\Delta\leq 0
, and hence (
1.4.1
) is valid in this case.
∎

This result was very influential in the community of people working on proving the conjecture of Lanford and Ruelle. It put the focus on the need for a stronger form of the
Golden-Thompson inequality that would provide an upper bound on
\operatorname{Tr}[e^{H+K+L}]
for arbitrary self-adjoint matrices
H
,
K
and
L
without assuming that any of them
commute. Uhlmann in particular took up this line of investigation, and he proved
[
96
, Satz 8.2]
a mild generalization of part of Theorem
1.18
, namely that
(
1.4.1
) is valid when any two of the matrices
\rho_{12}
,
\rho_{23}
and
\rho_{2}
commute.

The fifth theorem in Lieb’s paper
[
60
]
is a generalization of the Golden-Thompson Inequality to three self-adjoint matrices. The naive generalization might be (depending on how naive one might be),

\operatorname{Tr}[e^{H+K+L}]\leq\operatorname{Tr}[e^{H}e^{K}e^{L}]
, but this would be complete nonsense; the right side is in general a complex number. Lieb’s
Triple Matrix Inequality
, the fifth theorem in
[
60
]
, states that

\operatorname{Tr}[e^{H+K+L}]\leq\operatorname{Tr}[e^{H}T_{e^{-K}}(e^{L})]]\ ,

where

T_{A}(B)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{s+A}B\frac{1}{s+A}{\rm d}s\ .

Notice that if
A
and
B
commute, we have
T_{A}(B)=A^{-1}B
, and hence if
K
and
L
commute,
e^{H}T_{e^{-K}}(e^{L})=e^{H}e^{K+L}
. A proof of this theorem is given in Section 7; see Theorem
1.34
.

Armed with this, return to (
1.4.7
), and observe that, using Lieb’s Triple Matrix Inequality in the second line, and taking the trace over
\mathcal{H}_{3}
in the next step, and then using cyclicity of the trace,

\displaystyle e^{\Delta}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}[\exp(-\log\rho_{2}+\log\rho_{12}+\log\rho_{23})]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\frac{1}{s+\rho_{2}}\rho_{12}\frac{1}{s+\rho_{2}}\rho_{23}\right]{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\frac{1}{s+\rho_{2}}\rho_{12}\frac{1}{s+\rho_{2}}\rho_{2}\right]{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\rho_{12}\rho_{2}\frac{1}{(s+\rho_{2})^{2}}\right]{\rm d}s=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{12}]=1\ .

This proves SSA following the line of the investigation started in
[
6
]
, more directly than in
[
62
]
, despite the fact that Lieb wrote in the abstract of
[
60
]
that the results were relevant to the proof of SSA, and had, as he has told me, SSA in mind when he proved the Triple Matrix Inequality. Only very recently have generalizations of the Golden-Thompson inequality to more than three matrices been proved. See
[
89
]
for these theorems and their application to questions in quantum information theory.

We close this section by discussing the classical analogs of the inequalities we have been considering.
Given two finite sets
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
, and a probability density
\rho(x,y)
on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, thought of as specifying the joint distributions of two discrete random variables
X
and
Y
, the
(classical discrete) conditional entropy

H(X|Y)
of
X
with respect to
Y
is defined by

H(X|Y)=-\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}\rho(x,y)\log\left(\frac{\rho(x,y)}{\rho(y)}\right)\ ,

(1.4.8)

where

\rho(y):=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\rho(x,y)

is the
marginal distribution
of
Y
.
If we denote

S_{12}=-\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}\rho(x,y)\log\rho(x,y)\qquad{\rm and}\qquad S_{2}:=-\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\rho(y)\log\rho(y)\ ,

(1.4.9)

we then find that

H(X|Y)=S_{12}-S_{2}\ .

(1.4.10)

Note that

H(X|Y)=-\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\left(\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left(\frac{\rho(x,y)}{\rho(y)}\right)\log\left(\frac{\rho(x,y)}{\rho(y)}\right)\right)\rho(y)\geq 0

(1.4.11)

since for all
x,y
,
0\leq\rho(x,y)/\rho(y)\leq 1
.
Thus, in the setting of classical discrete probability, it is always the case that
S_{2}\leq S_{12}
, and it is easy to see that there is equality if and only if
\rho(x,y)/\rho(y)\in\{0,1\}
for all
x,y
, and this is the case if and only if the random variable
X
is completely determined by the random variable
Y
; i.e., for some
f:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{X}
,
X=f(Y)
.

While there simply is not a good way to define an analog of the conditional density
\rho(x,y)/\rho(y)
out of
\rho_{12}
and
\rho_{2}
in the quantum case,
and hence no way to use an analog of (
1.4.8
) to define conditional entropy in the quantum case, the right hand side of (
1.4.10
) makes perfect sense in the quantum setting, and leads to Definition
1.14
.

One might hope that the inequality
S_{12}-S_{1}>0
; i.e., (
1.4.10
) and (
1.4.11
) in the classical discrete case, would extend to the quantum case,
but it does not
. In fact, let
\mathcal{H}
be an
n
-dimensional Hilbert space, and let
\{u_{1},\dots u_{n}\}
be an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{H}
. Define a state
\psi
on
\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}
by

\psi:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}u_{j}\otimes u_{j}\ ,

and then define
\rho_{12}:=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
.
Since
\rho_{12}
is a rank one projection; i.e., a
pure state
,
S(\rho_{12})=0
. However, simple computations show that

\rho_{1}=\rho_{2}=\frac{1}{n}I
, and hence
S_{1}=S_{2}=\log n
which is easily seen to be the maximum value of the entropy of any density matrix on
\mathcal{H}
.

Given three finite sets
\mathcal{X}
,
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{Z}
and a tripartite probability density on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Z}
form the
the conditional joint and marginal distributions of
X
and
Y
, given
Z=z
:

\rho(x,y|z):=\frac{\rho(x,y,z)}{\rho(z)}\,\qquad\rho(x|z):=\frac{\rho(x,z)}{\rho(z)}\quad{\rm and}\quad\rho(y|z):=\frac{\rho(y,z)}{\rho(z)}

(1.4.12)

By the convexity of
t\mapsto t\log t
and Jensen’s inequality, for each
z
,

\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}\rho(x|z)\rho(y|z)\left(\frac{\rho(x,y|z)}{\rho(x|z)\rho(y|z)}\right)\log\left(\frac{\rho(x,y|z)}{\rho(x|z)\rho(y|z)}\right)\geq 0\ ,

and therefore the
conditional mutual information of
X
and
Y
given

Z
,
I(X,Y|Z)
, defined by

I(X,Y|Z)=\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\rho(z)\left(\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}\rho(x|z)\rho(y|z)\left(\frac{\rho(x,y|z)}{\rho(x|z)\rho(y|z)}\right)\log\left(\frac{\rho(x,y|z)}{\rho(x|z)\rho(y|z)}\right)\right)

satisfies
I(X,Y|Z)\geq 0
. A simple calculation shows

I(X,Y|Z)=S_{13}+S_{23}-S_{123}-S_{3}\ ,

and this proves
S_{13}+S_{23}-S_{123}-S_{3}>0
, which is SSA in the classical setting. Specializing to the case in which
\mathcal{Z}
consists of a single point,

S_{3}=0
,
S_{13}=S_{1}
,
S_{23}=S_{s}
and
S_{123}=S_{12}
, and we obtain the classical subadditivity of the entropy,

S_{12}\leq S_{1}+S_{2}
which obviously can be proved directly using Jensen’s Inequality as above. We have just proved subadditivity and
strong subadditivity in the discrete probability setting, but unlike the positivity of conditional entropy (
1.4.11
), which is not true for continuous
random variables, both subadditivity and strong subadditivity are valid in the classical case for both discrete and continuous random variables.
Strong subadditivity is universal, holding in all contexts, unlike the positivity of conditional probability, which is valid only in the classical discrete setting.
For more information, see
[
61
]
.

One cannot even begin to adapt the above classical proof to the quantum setting because there is no reasonable way to form
analogs of the conditional densities (
1.4.12
) out of the marginals of a tripartite density matrix
\rho_{123}
. There is, however,
another proof
[
21
]
of classical SSA that does not make any reference to conditonal probability, and which does extend to the
quantum case, as we show in Section 7.

1.5
The Araki-Lieb inequality

This section recalls some results from
[
6
]
that were used together with the “good enough” SSA, Theorem
1.18
to solve the thermodynamic limit problem that had motivated the conjecture of Lanford and Ruelle.

One might think that this would have closed the subject, but many people realized that SSA was interesting far beyond its original motivation.
Both SSA and the inequalities discussed here have indeed proven to be fundamental in quantum information theory.
It even appears in black hole physics
[
48
]
.

Let
\rho_{12}
be a density matrix on the tensor product of finite dimensional Hilbert spaces,
\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2}
. Then using the natural orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2}
induced by orthonormal bases of
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
, we may write
\rho_{12}
as a matrix
with entries
[\rho_{12}]_{(i,k),(j,\ell)}
. Then with
d_{j}:={\rm dim}(\mathcal{H}_{j})
,
j=1,2
,

[\rho_{1}]_{i,j}=\sum_{k=1}^{d_{2}}[\rho_{12}]_{(i,k),(j,k)}\quad{\rm and}\qquad[\rho_{2}]_{k,\ell}=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{1}}[\rho_{12}]_{(i,k),(i,\ell)}\ .

(1.5.1)

Suppose that
\rho_{12}
is a pure state; i.e., for some unit vector
\psi\in\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2}
,
\rho_{12}=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
so that

[\rho_{12}]_{(i,k),(j,\ell)}=\overline{\psi_{i,k}}\psi_{j,\ell}\ .

(1.5.2)

Define a
d_{1}\times d_{2}
matrix
K
by
K_{j,\ell}=\psi_{j,\ell}
. Then from (
1.5.1
) and (
1.5.2
),

\rho_{1}=KK^{*}\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\rho_{2}=K^{*}K\ .

(1.5.3)

Since
K^{*}K
and
KK^{*}
have the same non-zero spectrum with the same multiplicities for non-zero eigenvalues, it follows that when
\rho_{12}
is a pure state, then
S_{1}=S_{2}
. It is also evident that
S_{12}=0
if and only if
\rho_{12}
is a pure state. Thus

S_{12}=0\ \Rightarrow\ S_{1}=S_{2}\ .

(1.5.4)

This discussion in this paragraph summarized Lemma 3 of
[
6
]
and its short proof.

The Araki-Lieb Theorem allows one to conclude that
S_{1}
is close to
S_{2}
if
S_{12}
is small:

Theorem 1.19

(Araki-Lieb Triangle Inequality).

Let
\rho_{12}
be any density matrix on the tensor product of finite dimensional Hilbert spaces,
\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2}
.
Then

|S_{1}-S_{2}|\leq S_{12}\ ;

(1.5.5)

The proof uses a very simple construction known as
purification
, which has many other uses, some of which will be discussed later in this paper. The following is Lemma 4 from
[
6
]
.

Lemma 1.20

(Purification Lemma).

Let
\rho
be any density matrix on a finite dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Then there is a pure state density matrix
|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
on
\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}
such that

\rho=\operatorname{Tr}_{1}[|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|]=\operatorname{Tr}_{2}[|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|]

(1.5.6)

where
\operatorname{Tr}_{1}
and
\operatorname{Tr}_{2}
denote the partial traces over
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
respectively.

Beweis.

Define
K=\rho^{1/2}
and using any orthonormal basis
\{u_{1},\dots,u_{d}\}
of
\mathcal{H}
, identify
K
with

\psi=\sum_{i,j}K_{i,j}u_{i}\otimes u_{j}\ .

Then (
1.5.6
) follows from (
1.5.2
) and (
1.5.3
).
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.19
.

Consider any purification
\rho_{123}
of
\rho_{12}
; this exists by Lemma
1.20
. By (
1.5.4
),
S_{12}=S_{3}
and

S_{1}=S_{23}\leq S_{2}+S_{3}
and hence

S_{12}\geq S_{1}-S_{2}
.
By symmetry, one then has (
1.5.5
). ∎

The cases of equality in the triangle inequality have been determined in
[
23
]
. The following theorem is the part of Theorem 1.3 in
[
23
]
that we shall need below.

Theorem 1.21
.

For arbitrary
a,b>0
, there exist bipartite states
\rho_{12}
such that

S_{12}=S_{1}-S_{2}

and

S_{12}=a\qquad{\rm and}\qquad S_{2}=b\ .

For the proof, see
[
23
]
. Note that the two conditional entropies associated to
\rho_{12}
are

S_{12}-S_{2}=a-b\qquad{\rm and}\qquad S_{12}-S_{1}=-b\ .

Thus, there exist bipartite states
\rho_{12}

that saturate the triangle inequality
for which one of the conditional entropies is an arbitrary negative number, and the other is an arbitrary positive number.

1.6
SSA and Entanglement

Let
\rho_{12}
be a density matrix
on the tensor product of two Hilbert spaces

{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
. Then
\rho_{12}
is
finitely
separable

if and only if it has a decomposition as a convex combination of
tensor products:

\rho_{12}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}\rho_{1}^{k}\otimes\rho_{2}^{k}

(1.6.1)

where the
\lambda_{k}
are positive and sum to
1
, and each

\rho_{\alpha}^{k}
is a density matrix on
{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha}
. A
bipartite state is
separable
if it is in the closure
of the set of finitely separable states.
A bipartite state that is not separable is

entangled
.

For many purposes in quantum information theory, it is important to be able to decide whether a given bipartite state is entangled or not, already a challenging problem, and beyond this, to quantify the degree of entanglement of entangled states. For more information in the importance entanglement in quantum information theory, see
[
49
]
. We now discuss the mathematics of one such measure of entanglement.

For any tripartite density matrix
\rho_{123}
on
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{2}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{3}
,
the
conditional mutual information of
1
and
2
given
3
,
I(1,2|3)
, is
defined by

I(1,2|3):=S_{13}+S_{23}-S_{123}-S_{3}\geq 0\ .

(1.6.2)

Note that SSA is equivalent to the fact that
I(1,2|3)\geq 0

Definition 1.22

(Squashed Entanglement).

The functional
{\rm E}_{\rm sq}
on bipartite density matrices
\rho_{12}
by

{\rm E}_{\rm sq}(\rho_{12})=\frac{1}{2}\inf\{\ I(1,2|3)\ :\rho_{123}\ {\rm is}\ any\ {\rm tripartite\ extension\ of}\ \rho_{12}\ \}\ .

(1.6.3)

is known as the
squashed entanglement of
\rho_{12}
,

This functional was first introduced by Tucci
[
94
]
, and was rediscovered
by Christandl
and Winter
[
33
]
who studied it and proved that it has many important
properties, such as additivity.

The cases of equality in SSA have been determined in
[
82
,
42
]
, and it follows from the results in the second of these papers that if
I(2,2|3)=0
, then

{\mathcal{H}}_{3}
has the form

{\mathcal{H}}_{3}=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{m}{\mathcal{H}}^{j}_{3\ell}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{3r}^{j}

and

\rho_{123}
has the form

\rho_{123}=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{m}\rho_{1,3\ell}^{j}\otimes\rho_{3r,2}^{j}\ .

(1.6.4)

Evidently, for any
\rho_{123}
of the form (
1.6.4
),

\rho_{12}:=\operatorname{Tr}_{3}(\rho_{123})
is separable. Thus, if one knew that the infimum in Definition
1.22
was attained,
it would follow that if
{\rm E}_{\rm sq}(\rho_{12})=0
, then
\rho_{12}
is separable. On the other hand, let

\rho_{12}
be separable, and have the decomposition
(
1.6.1
).
Take
\rho^{j}_{1,3\ell}
to be an
arbitrary
purification of
\rho_{1}^{j}
onto
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{3\ell}^{j}
, take
{\mathcal{H}}_{3r}
to be
one-dimensional with

\rho_{3r,2}^{j}=\rho_{2}^{j}
. Then

\rho_{123}
is an extension of the given separable
bipartite state
\rho_{12}
for which equality holds in
(
1.4.1
). Thus, whenever
\rho_{12}
is finitely
separable, then
{\rm E}_{\rm sq}(\rho_{12})=0
, and then a continuity argument shows that whenever
\rho_{12}
is separable,

{\rm E}_{\rm sq}(\rho_{12})=0
. However, since it is not known whether the infimum in (
1.6.3
) is attained, it is not so simple to see that if

{\rm E}_{\rm sq}(\rho_{12})=0
, then
\rho_{12}
is separable. Nonetheless, this has been proved in
[
16
]
, and thus it is now known that

{\rm E}_{\rm sq}(\rho_{12})=0
if and only if
\rho_{12}
is separable.
That is,
{\rm E}_{\rm sq}
provides a
faithful
measure of entanglement.

The following extension of SSA is proved in
[
23
]
:

Theorem 1.23

(Extended SSA).

For all tripartite states
\rho_{123}
,

S_{13}+S_{23}-S_{123}-S_{3}\geq 2\max\{S_{1}-S_{12}\ ,S_{2}-S_{12}\ ,0\ \}\ .

(1.6.5)

This has the immediate corollary that

{\rm E}_{\rm sq}(\rho_{12})\geq\max\{S_{1}-S_{12}\ ,S_{2}-S_{12}\ ,0\ \}\ .

(1.6.6)

Therefore if either of the conditional entropies
S_{12}-S_{1}
or
S_{12}-S_{2}
is strictly negative, then

{\rm E}_{\rm sq}(\rho_{12})>0
, and
\rho_{12}
is entangled.

It is also shown in
[
23
]
that the factor of
2
on the right side cannot be replaced by any larger value. The argument uses a tripartite extension of a bipartite state that saturates the Araki-Lieb Triangle inequality. This is natural since the proof of Theorem
1.23
, which we give below, makes use of the purification arguments used to prove the Araki-Lieb Triangle inequality. The construction yields a tripartite state that, when inserted into the definition (
1.6.3
), yields the upper bound

{\rm E}_{\rm sq}(\rho_{12})\leq S_{1}-S_{12}\qquad{\rm with}\qquad S_{1}-S_{12}>0\ .

Then from the lower bound, (
1.6.6
), we have
{\rm E}_{\rm sq}(\rho_{12})=S_{1}-S_{12}>0
. This proves that (
1.6.6
), and hence the factor of
2
in Theorem
1.23
, is sharp.
We refer to
[
23
]
for this construction.

A weaker form of the inequality (
1.6.6
) was given by
Christandl and Winter

[
33
]
.
Their lower bound involves the
averaged quantity

\frac{1}{2}(S_{1}+S_{2})-S_{12}

(1.6.7)

in place of

\max\{S_{1}-S_{12},S_{2}-S_{12}\}
. The difference can be significant: By the remarks following Theorem
1.21
,
there exist states
\rho_{12}
for which
\max\{S_{1}-S_{12}\ ,S_{2}-S_{12}\ ,0\ \}
is
arbitrarily large, but the quantity in (
1.6.7
) is negative.
Moreover, the argument in
[
33
]

relied on a lower bound for the the
one-way
distillable entanglement

in terms of the conditional entropy. This inequality, known
as the
hashing inequality
had been a long-standing
conjecture,
and its proofs remain complicated. Our contribution was to
show how this stronger lower bound follows in a relatively
simple manner from strong subadditivity, and to provide the
examples that prove the
sharpness of these bounds.

Proof of Theorem
1.23
.

Consider any purification
\rho_{1234}
of
\rho_{123}
.
Then since
\rho_{1234}
is pure,
S_{23}=S_{14}
and

S_{124}=S_{3}

Then

S_{12}+S_{23}-S_{1}-S_{3}=S_{12}+S_{14}-S_{124}-S_{1}\ ,

and the right hand side is non-negative by (
1.4.1
).
This proves

S_{12}+S_{23}\geq S_{1}+S_{3}\ ,

which may also be seen as a consequence of the convexity of the conditional entropy.

Next, adding
S_{12}+S_{23}\geq S_{1}+S_{3}
and
S_{13}+S_{23}\geq S_{1}+S_{2}
, we obtain

S_{12}+S_{13}+2S_{23}\geq 2S_{1}+S_{2}+S_{3}\ .

Again consider any purification
\rho_{1234}
of
\rho_{123}
. Then we obtain, using
S_{12}=S_{34}
,

S_{23}=S_{14}
, and
S_{2}=S_{134}
,

S_{13}+S_{34}-S_{134}-S_{3}\geq 2(S_{1}-S_{14})\ ,

which is (
1.6.5
) with different indices.
∎

1.7
Minkowski’s Inequality and SSA

This section begins with a second proof of SSA in the classical case taken from
[
21
]
. Recall that a standard statement of the Minkowski inequality is that for non-negative measurable
functions
f
on the Cartesian product of two measure spaces
(X,\mu)
and
(Y,\nu)
,

\biggl(\int_{X}\biggl(\int_{Y}f(x,y){\rm d}\nu\biggr)^{p}{\rm d}\mu\biggr)^{1/p}\leq\int_{Y}\biggl(\int_{X}f^{p}(x,y){\rm d}\mu\biggr)^{1/p}{\rm d}\nu

(1.7.1)

for
p\geq 1
, and that the opposite inequality holds for
0<p\leq 1
. If
Y=\{1,\dots,n\}
and
\nu
is counting measure and we write
f_{y}(x)
for
f(x,y)
,
this reduces to

\left(\int_{X}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}f_{j}(x)\right)^{p}{\rm d}\mu\right)^{1/p}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\int_{X}f_{j}^{p}{\rm d}\mu\right)^{1/p}\ .

Of course since
\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}f_{j}(x)\right|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}|f_{j}(x)|
, and likewise for the integral version, the assumption of non-negativity can by dropped by inserting absolute values, but for our purposes, the present statements are more useful. Note in particular that in (
1.7.1
), there is equality at
p=1
by Fubini’s Theorem.

The inequality (
1.7.1
) has a trivial extension to functions of three
variables, and this extension has an interesting consequence, namely classical SSA.
Consider a non-negative measurable function
f(x,y,z)
on the Cartesian
product of three measure spaces
(X,\mu)
,
(Y,\nu)
and
(Z,\rho)
, and simply
holding
z
fixed as a parameter, one gets

\biggl(\int_{X}\biggl(\int_{Y}f(x,y,z){\rm d}\nu\biggr)^{p}{\rm d}\mu\biggr)^{1/p}\leq\int_{Y}\biggl(\int_{X}f^{p}(x,y,z){\rm d}\mu\biggr)^{1/p}{\rm d}\nu

pointwise in
z
for
p\geq 1
.
Integrating in
z
then yields

\int_{Z}\biggl(\int_{X}\biggl(\int_{Y}f(x,y,z){\rm d}\nu\biggr)^{p}{\rm d}\mu\biggr)^{1/p}{\rm d}\rho\leq\int_{Z}\int_{Y}\biggl(\int_{X}f^{p}(x,y,z){\rm d}\mu\biggr)^{1/p}{\rm d}\nu{\rm d}\rho

(1.7.2)

for
p\geq 1
, and of course the inequality reverses for
0<p\leq 1
.

For any probability density
g
on any finite measure space
(X,\mu)
that belongs to
L^{p}(X,\mu)
for some
p>1
,
g\log g\in L^{1}(X,\mu)
and

\lim_{p\downarrow 1}\frac{1}{p-1}\left(\int_{X}g^{p}{\rm d}\mu-1\right)=\int_{X}g\log g{\rm d}\mu=-S(g)\ ,

(1.7.3)

a formula that is the basis Irving Segal’s approach to entropy
[
83
]
in the classical and quantum setting.

To avoid technical details that never arise in the quantum setting, where the entropy is always non-negative, let us suppose that the measures
\mu
,
\nu
and
\rho
in (
1.7.2
) are all finite. Let
f_{123}
be a probability density on
(X\times Y\times Z,\mu\otimes\nu\otimes\rho)
that belongs to
L^{p}
for some
p>1
. Then by (
1.7.1
), all of its marginals are probability densities whose
p
th powers are integrable.
Denote the various marginal densities of
f_{123}
as follows:

f_{23}(y,z)=\int_{X}f(x,y,z){\rm d}\mu\ ,\quad f_{13}(x,z)=\int_{Y}f(x,y,z){\rm d}\nu\ ,\quad f_{3}(z)=\int_{X}\int_{Y}f(x,y,z){\rm d}\mu{\rm d}\nu\ .

Then inserting
f_{123}
into (
1.7.2
), it becomes:

\int_{Z}\left(\int_{X}f_{13}^{p}{\rm d}\mu\right)^{1/p}\leq\int_{Y}\int_{Z}\left(\int_{X}f_{123}^{p}{\rm d}\mu\right)^{1/p}{\rm d}\nu{\rm d}\rho\ .

(1.7.4)

Since (
1.7.4
) is an equality at
p=1
, we get another inequality by taking the right derivative of both sides of (
1.7.2
) with respect to
p
at
p=1
, and by (
1.7.3
) this yields an entropy inequality which is, in the now familiar notation, with all terms finite,

S_{13}+S_{23}\geq S_{123}+S_{3}\ .

That is, differentiating Minkowski’s inequality for three measure spaces yields the strong subadditivity of the classical entropy. The condition that the measures are finite can be easily relaxed, but this is irrelevant for our present purposes. This proof of classical SSA has the merit of making no reference to conditional probability densities, and so one might hope that it would extend to the quantum setting. This is indeed the case.

The operator analog of (
1.7.1
) is elementary, though it appears the first proof was given in
[
21
]
:

Theorem 1.24
.

Let
A
be a positive operator on the tensor
product of two
Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2}
. Then for all
p\geq 1
,

\bigl(\operatorname{Tr}(\operatorname{Tr}_{1}A)^{p}\bigr)^{1/p}\leq\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(\bigl(\operatorname{Tr}_{2}A^{p}\bigr)^{1/p}\bigr)

(1.7.5)

and inequality (
1.7.5
) reverses for
0<p\leq 1
.

Beweis.

Take
p>1
, and let
1/q=1-1/p
. Then there is a positive operator
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2})
such that
\operatorname{Tr}_{2}B^{q}=1
and

\bigl(\operatorname{Tr}(\operatorname{Tr}_{1}A)^{p}\bigr)^{1/p}=\operatorname{Tr}B(\operatorname{Tr}_{1}A)=\operatorname{Tr}[(I\otimes B)A]=\sum_{i,j}\langle u_{i}\otimes v_{j},(I\otimes B)A\langle u_{i}\otimes v_{j}\rangle

for any orthonormal bases
\{u_{1},\otimes u_{m}\}
and
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}
of
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
respectively. (See the remarks on Schatten trace norms following Remark
1.29
below for the relevant facts about duality.) Choosing

\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}
to be an eigenbasis of
B
,
Bv_{j}=\lambda_{j}v_{j}
, we then have

\bigl(\operatorname{Tr}(\operatorname{Tr}_{1}A)^{p}\bigr)^{1/p}=\sum_{i,j}\lambda_{j}\langle u_{i}\otimes v_{j},A(u_{i}\otimes v_{j})\rangle\leq\sum_{i}\left(\sum_{j}\langle u_{i}\otimes v_{j},A(u_{i}\otimes v_{j})\rangle^{p}\right)^{1/p}

by Hölder’s inequality and
\operatorname{Tr}B^{q}=1
. Then by the Spectral Theorem and Jensen’s inequality, in its standard form for convex functions on
{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}
,

\langle u_{i}\otimes v_{j},A\langle u_{i}\otimes v_{j}\rangle^{p}\leq\langle u_{i}\otimes v_{j},A^{p}\langle u_{i}\otimes v_{j}\rangle\ ,

and thus
\bigl(\operatorname{Tr}(\operatorname{Tr}_{1}A)^{p}\bigr)^{1/p}\leq\sum_{i}\langle u_{i},\operatorname{Tr}_{2}A^{p}u_{i}\rangle^{1/p}
. Now choose
\{u_{1},\dots,u_{m}\}
to consist of eigenvectors of
\operatorname{Tr}_{2}A^{p}
to conclude that (
1.7.5
) is valid for all
p\geq 1
.

For
0<p\leq 1
, define
r=1/p
and
B=A^{p}
so that

A=B^{r}
. Since
r>1
, the inequality proved above says

\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(\bigl(\operatorname{Tr}_{2}B^{r}\bigr)^{1/r}\bigr)\geq\bigl(\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(\operatorname{Tr}_{1}B\bigr)^{r}\bigr)^{1/r}
.
This is (
1.7.5
) for
0<p<1
in other notation.
∎

The following theorem, also from
[
21
,
22
]
does not have such a simple proof:

Theorem 1.25
.

Let
A
be a positive operator on the tensor
product of three
Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{3}
. Then

\operatorname{Tr}_{3}\bigl(\operatorname{Tr}_{2}(\operatorname{Tr}_{1}A)^{p}\bigr)^{1/p}\leq\operatorname{Tr}_{13}\bigl(\bigl(\operatorname{Tr}_{2}A^{p}\bigr)^{1/p}\bigr)

(1.7.6)

for
1\leq p\leq 2
while the reverse inequality holds for
0<p\leq 1
, and neither inequality is valid for
p>2
.

Evidently there is equality at
p=1
, and if
A
is a tripartite density matrix
\rho_{123}
then for all
p>0
,

\frac{1}{p-1}\left(\operatorname{Tr}_{3}\bigl(\operatorname{Tr}_{2}(\operatorname{Tr}_{1}\rho_{123})^{p}\bigr)^{1/p}-1\right)\leq\frac{1}{p-1}\left(\operatorname{Tr}_{13}\bigl(\bigl(\operatorname{Tr}_{2}\rho_{123}^{p}\bigr)^{1/p}\bigr)-1\right)

Taking the limit
p\to 1
, we obtain
S_{13}+S_{23}\geq S_{123}+S_{3}
, giving another proof of SSA in the quantum case.

Note that while (
1.7.5
) is valid for all
p\geq 1
, (
1.7.6
) is valid only for
1\leq p\leq 2
. This is an indication of the fact that the
deeper notion of operator convexity comes into play in the proof of Theorem
1.25
, while the proof of
Theorem
1.24
only required the use of Jensen’s inequality for real valued functions on the real line. Two references,
[
21
,
22
]
, are given for
Theorem
1.25
because in
[
21
]
the theorem was proved only for
0<p\leq 1
and
p=2
. This is still enough to prove SSA – simply take the left derivative.

The fundamental input to the proof in
[
21
]
came from a theorem of Epstein
[
38
]
, who proved the one conjecture explicitly stated by Lieb in
[
60
, p. 282]
. Lieb’s conjecture was that: For each fixed
B\in M_{n}^{+}(\mathbb{C})
, and each
m\in{\mathord{\mathbb{N}}}
, the function

A\mapsto\operatorname{Tr}[(BA^{1/m}B)^{m}]

(1.7.7)

is concave on
M_{n}^{+}(\mathbb{C})
.

Of course the case
m=1
is trivial, but the case
m=2
is equivalent to Wigner-Yanase Theorem, by what has explained at the close of Section 2.
Thus, there had been two conjectured extensions of the Wigner-Yanase Theorem, namely the one that appears to have been put forward by
Dyson, and was confirmed with the proof of the Lieb Concavity Theorem, and then Lieb’s conjecture, and it was not immediately clear
that they were equivalent. After all, Lieb proved the Lieb Concavity Theorem, and made the other conjecture in the very same paper. As explained later in this section, the conjecture is not only true, but it is equivalent to the Lieb Concavity Thoerem.

The proof of Lieb’s conjecure, and somewhat more, is due to Epstein
[
38
]
:

Theorem 1.26

(Epstein’s Theorem).

For any
n\times\ell
matrix
B
, and all
0<p<1
, the function

A\mapsto\operatorname{Tr}[(B^{*}A^{p}B)^{1/p}]

(1.7.8)

is concave on
M_{n}^{+}(\mathbb{C})
.

Epstein’s proof relied on the theory of Herglotz functions; i.e., functions that are analytic on the upper half plane in
\mathbb{C}
with a positive imaginary part. This includes
z\mapsto z^{p}
, but only for
0\leq p\leq 1
. Using Epstein’s result, Theorem
1.25
was proven in
[
21
]
for
0\leq p\leq 1
and the special case
p=2
. The part of Theorem
1.25
referring to
1<p<2
was left as a conjecture in
[
21
]
.

Then in
[
22
]
, a duality method was introduced that led to the proof of Theorem
1.25
in full, and much more, including a new and simple proof of Epstein’s Theorem. One of the new tools that
[
22
]
brought to bear on matrix convexity and concavity theorems is the following:

Lemma 1.27
.

Let
X
and
Y
be vector spaces.
If
f(x,y)
is jointly convex in
(x,y)\in X\times Y
with values in
(-\infty,\infty]
, then
g(x):=\inf_{y\in Y}\{f(x,y)\}
is convex. If
f(x,y)
is jointly concave in

is concave.

Proof of Lemma

For any

and

with

, and any

,

, and choose

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad g((1-\lambda)x_{0}+\lambda x_{1})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle f((1-\lambda)x_{0}+\lambda x_{1},(1-\lambda)y_{0}+\lambda y_{1})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-\lambda)f(x_{0},y_{0})+\lambda f(x_{1},y_{1})

\displaystyle(1-\lambda)g(x_{0})+\lambda g(x_{1})+\varepsilon\ .\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad

There is another well-known general convexity/concavity lemma
[
80
]
that is useful in conjunction with Lemma
1.27
:

Lemma 1.28
.

Let
V
be a vector space and
f:V\to[0,\infty)
be homogeneous of degree
q>0
, so that for all
s>0
and all
x\in V
,
f(sx)=s^{q}f(x)
. Suppose also that
f(x)>0
for
x\neq 0
. Then if
f
is concave, so is
f^{1/q}
.

Beweis.

First consider the case
q=1
. We claim that
f
is concave if and only if
\mathcal{K}:=\{x:f(x)\geq 1\}
is convex. Suppose
f
is concave.
Let
x,y\in\mathcal{K}
, and
0<\lambda<1
. Then

f(\lambda x+(1-\lambda y)\geq\lambda f(x)+(1-\lambda)f(y)\geq 1\ ,

and hence
\mathcal{K}
is convex. Now suppose that
\mathcal{K}
is convex. Let
x,y\in V
. We must show
f(x+y)\leq f(x)+f(y)
. This is obvious if either
x=0
or
y=0
, so we suppose both are non-zero. Then

\frac{f(x+y)}{f(x)+f(y)}=f\left(\frac{f(x)}{f(x)+f(y)}\frac{x}{f(x)}+\frac{f(y)}{f(x)+f(y)}\frac{y}{f(y)}\right)\ .

Evidently
x/f(x)\in\mathcal{K}
and
y/f(y)\in\mathcal{K}
so by the convexity of
\mathcal{K}
,
f(x+y)\geq f(x)+f(y)
, and then since
f
is homogeneous of degree one,
f
is concave. This proves the initial claim.

Now suppose that
f
is homogeneous of degree
q
, and define
\mathcal{K}
as before. The first part of the argument given above did not involve the homogeneity, and hence it still yields the result that
\mathcal{K}=\{x:f(x)\geq 1\}
is convex. But evidently

\{x:f(x)\geq 1\}=\{x:f(x)^{1/q}\geq 1\}

and then since
f^{1/q}
is homogeneous of degree one, it is concave.
∎

Remark 1.29

The obvious analog of Lemma
1.28
for convex functions may be proved in the same manner.

1.8
Monotone metrics

In the Introduction, it was noted that Theorems 2 and 3 of
[
60
]
provided the answers to questions that would not be asked for years to come, and that when they were asked, it was not recognized
that the answers could be found in
[
60
]
. The questions concerned
monotone metrics
on the space of non-degenerate density matrices.

Let
\rho(t)
be a differentiable path in the space
\mathfrak{S}
of invertible
n\times n
density matrices; i.e., elements of

M_{n}^{++}(\mathbb{C})
with unit trace. Then the derivative
\rho^{\prime}(t)=K(t)
is a self-adjoint operator with
\operatorname{Tr}[K(t)]=0
. We may think of

\mathfrak{S}
as a differentiable manifold, and it is then of interest to equip it with various Riemannian metrics that have the property that the
distance between
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\in\mathfrak{S}
,
d(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
, decreases under the application of
any quantum operation; i.e.,

d(\Phi^{\dagger}(\rho_{1}),\Phi^{\dagger}(\rho_{1}))\leq d(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})\ .

(1.8.1)

In such a metric, any quantum operation performed on the states can only make it harder to distinguish between them. See the Introduction to
[
19
]
for further discussion of the problem of distinguishing between states, which is basic to quantum communication.

We may identify the tangent space to
\mathfrak{S}
at each point
\rho
with the space of traceless self-adjoint
n\times n
matrices. If we denote the quadratic form that specifies a Riemannian metric at
\rho
by
\gamma_{\rho}(K,K)
, then the contractive property (
1.8.1
) for such a metric is equivalent to

\gamma_{\Phi^{\dagger}(\rho)}(\Phi^{\dagger}(K),\Phi^{\dagger}(K))\leq\gamma_{\rho}(K,K)\ .

The same question had been of interest and answered in the classical case where in which the analog of
\mathfrak{S}
is the set
\mathfrak{S}_{c}
of
n
dimensional strictly positive probability vectors

p:=(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
with each
p_{j}>0
and
\sum_{j=1}^{n}p_{j}=1
. At each
p\in\mathfrak{S}_{c}
,
we may identify the tangent space with the subspace of
{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}
consisting of vectors
k=(k_{1},\dots,k_{n})

with

\sum_{j=1}^{n}k_{j}=0
. The analogs of quantum operations are
stochastic maps

P
; i.e., elements of

M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{R}}})
with non-negative and the property that for each
j
,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}P_{i,j}=1
. In this setting,
it is natural to ask for metrics
\gamma
on
\mathfrak{S}_{c}
with the property that

\gamma_{Pp}(Pk,Pk)\leq\gamma_{p}(k,k)

for all stochastic
P
at each
p\in\mathfrak{S}_{c}
and for each tangent vector
k
. In 1982, Cencov
[
28
]
, building on earlier work of Fisher
[
40
]
, proved that the unique such metric, up to a constant multiple, is the
Fisher Information

\gamma_{p}(k,k)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{k_{j}^{2}}{p_{j}}\ .

A decade later, Cencov together with Morozova
[
67
]
, took up the quantum problem. In the non-commutative setting, there are many possible
ways to “divide by” a non-degenerate density matrix
\rho
. Morozova and Cencov came up with several conjectures, all eventually shown to be correct,
that certain explicit metrics were in fact monotone metrics, although they did not resolve any of these conjectures. An account of their work can be found in
[
75
]
.

However, had they known of Theorems 2 and 3 of Lieb’s paper, and then recognized them as monotonicity theorems, they would have had positive solutions to the most important of their conjectures: These theorems, written in the monotonicity forms (
1.2.13
) and (
1.2.14
) show that for each
0<t<1

\gamma_{\rho}^{(t)}(K,K):=\operatorname{Tr}[K\rho^{t-1}K\rho^{-t}]

(1.8.2)

and

\widehat{\gamma}_{\rho}(K,K):=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}K\frac{1}{s+\rho}K\frac{1}{s+\rho}{\rm d}s\right]

(1.8.3)

are monotone metrics. Note that the right hand sides are always positive for non-zero
K
, so that these do define Riemannian metrics, and then the monotonicity is provided by (
1.2.13
) and (
1.2.14
). Note that when
\rho
and
K
commute, we have

\gamma_{\rho}^{(t)}(K,K)=\widehat{\gamma}_{\rho}(K,K)=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\frac{K^{2}}{\rho}\right]\ ,

as one might expect.
Thus the functionals studied by Lieb in 1973 are both natural quantum generalizations of the Fisher Information metric
.

However, none of the early writers on the subject made the connection with Lieb’s theorems, and his paper
[
60
]
is not cited in
[
75
]
which gives the first explicit proof of the fact that (
1.8.2
) and (
1.8.3
) do in fact define monotone metrics as had been conjectured by Morozova and Cencov. The fact that (
1.8.3
) is a monotone metric plays an important role in the author’s work
[
25
]
with Jann Maas on a geometric approach to inequalities for quantum Markov semigroups with detailed balance. In our paper, we do observe that the metric in (
1.8.3
) is monotone is a consequence of Lieb’s Theorem 3 in
[
60
]
, while we also give a reference to
[
75
]
for the explicit monotonicity statement. In pointing out the lack of early references to
[
60
]
by people working on monotone metrics, the intention is not to criticize, but rather to emphasize how prescient Lieb was in pointing out both the validity and equivalence of first three theorems in the 1973 paper.

The right hand side of (
1.8.3
) is well known to be the negative of the Hessian of the entropy
S(\rho)
. That is,

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial s\partial t}\operatorname{Tr}[(\rho+sK+tK)\log(\rho+sK+tK)]\bigg|_{s=0,t=0}=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}K\frac{1}{s+\rho}K\frac{1}{s+\rho}{\rm d}s\right]\ ,

as one can verify using the integral representation for the logarithm (
1.13.1
) that is used below. Lesniewski and Ruskai
[
59
]
showed that all monotone metrics arise in this manner from one of the
quasi entropies
that had been introduced by Petz
[
73
,
74
]
.

1.9
The Lieb-Ruskai Monotonicity Theorem

The Lieb-Ruskai Monotonicity Theorem is the main result of
[
63
]
, and it asserts the monotonicity of an operator function in two variables under completely positive maps. However it is not stated in
[
63
]
as a theorem,
but only discussed as an example, after Theorem 2 of that paper, and with an extraneous hypothesis, namely that the completely positive map in question be unital. Their argument does not require this at all, and hence their result, stated for matrices, is:

Theorem 1.35

(Lieb and Ruskai 1974).

Let
\Phi:M_{m}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{m}(\mathbb{C})
be completely positive. Then for all
A,B\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
,

\Phi(A^{*}B)\frac{1}{\Phi(B^{*}B)}\Phi(B^{*}A):=\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\Phi(A^{*}B)\frac{1}{\Phi(B^{*}B)+\epsilon I}\Phi(B^{*}A)\in M_{m}(\mathbb{C})\ ,

and

\Phi(A^{*}A)\geq\Phi(A^{*}B)\frac{1}{\Phi(B^{*}B)}\Phi(B^{*}A)\ .

(1.9.1)

Some years later, Choi proved:

Theorem 1.36

(Choi 1980).

The Lieb-Ruskai Monotonicity Theorem is valid for all
2
positive maps
\Phi:M_{m}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{m}(\mathbb{C})
.

This is an extension of Theorem
1.35
since every completely positive map is
2
positive, but the converse is not true; see, e.g.,
[
29
]
. Choi’s proof uses the following well known lemma (He refers to
[
86
]
for a more general result):

Lemma 1.37
.

For
X\in M_{n}^{++}(\mathbb{C})
and
Y,Z\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
,

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}X&Z\\
Z^{*}&Y\end{array}\right]\geq 0\quad\iff\quad Y\geq Z^{*}X^{-1}Z\ .

(1.9.2)

Beweis.

For any
v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
,

\left\langle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-X^{-1}Zv\\
v\end{array}\right)\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}X&Z\\
Z^{*}&Y\end{array}\right]\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-X^{-1}Zv\\
v\end{array}\right)\right\rangle=\langle v,Yv\rangle-\langle v,Z^{*}X^{-1}Zv\rangle\ .

On the other hand,

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}X&Z\\
Z^{*}&Z^{*}X^{-1}Z\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}X^{1/2}&0\\
Z^{*}X^{-1/2}&0\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}X^{1/2}&X^{-1/2}Z\\
0&0\end{array}\right]\geq 0\ .

∎

Proof of Theorem
1.36
.

For all
A,B\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
,

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}B^{*}B&B^{*}A\\
A^{*}B&A^{*}A\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}B^{*}&0\\
0&A^{*}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}B&A\\
0&0\end{array}\right]\geq 0\ ,

and if
\Phi
is
2
-positive, then for all
\epsilon>0
,

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\Phi(B^{*}B)+\epsilon I&\Phi(B^{*}A)\\
\Phi(A^{*}B)&\Phi(A^{*}A)\end{array}\right]\geq 0
. By Lemma
1.37
,

\Phi(A^{*}A)\geq\Phi(A^{*}B)(\Phi(B^{*}B)+\epsilon I))^{-1}\Phi(B^{*}A)\ .

∎

Corollary 1.38
.

Let
\Phi:M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{m}(\mathbb{C})
be
2
-positive.
Then for all
C\in M_{n}^{++}(\mathbb{C})
and all
X\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})

\Phi\left(X^{*}\frac{1}{C}X\right)\geq\Phi(X)^{*}\frac{1}{\Phi(C)}\Phi(X)\ .

(1.9.3)

Beweis.

Let
B=C^{1/2}
and let
A=C^{-1/2}X
. Then (
1.9.1
) becomes (
1.9.3
).
∎

Example 1.39

(Kiefer’s Inequality).

For example, take
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{C}^{n}
so that
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
can be identified with
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
.
Let
\mathcal{K}
the direct sum of
m
copies of
\mathcal{H}
, so that we may identify
\mathcal{A}:=\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K})
with the
m\times m
block matrices
whose entries are in
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. The partial trace map
\Psi_{m}^{\dagger}:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K})\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
defined in (
1.2.11
)
is, as we have seen, completely positive and trace preserving.

Consider any sets

\{X_{1},\dots,X_{m}\}
of
n\times n\subset M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
and
\{C_{1},\dots,C_{m}\}\subset M_{n}^{++}(\mathbb{C})
.
Define

X:=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}X_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&X_{m}\end{array}\right]\qquad{\rm and}\qquad C:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}C_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&C_{m}\end{array}\right]

Then with
\Phi
taken to be the partial trace, (
1.9.3
) becomes

\sum_{j=1}^{m}X_{j}^{*}\frac{1}{C_{j}}X_{j}\geq\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}X_{j}\right)^{*}\frac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}C_{j}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}X_{j}\right)

(1.9.4)

This is due to Kiefer
[
53
]
, though it was also noted by Lieb and Ruskai, who were unaware of Kiefer’s work. However, it is a very special case of their main result.

Example 1.40

(The Kadison-Choi Schwarz Inequality).

Taking
B=I
and supposing that
\Phi
is unital and
2
-positive; (
1.9.1
) becomes

\Phi\left(A^{*}A\right)\geq\Phi(A)^{*}\Phi(A)\ .

(1.9.5)

for all
X\in\mathord{\mathcal{A}}
. This was proved by Kadison for self-adjoint
A

[
52
]
, and in general by Choi
[
30
]
.

In fact, Kadison proved his inequality for
A
self-adjoint, but only assumed that
\Phi
was positive, not even
2
-positive. However,
Theorem 4 of Stinespring’s fundamental paper
[
87
]
states that if
\Phi:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is a positive map on any commutative

C^{*}
algebra
\mathcal{A}
, then
\Phi
is completely positive. If
A
is self-adjoint, or even normal, take
\mathcal{C}(A)
to be the
commutative
C^{*}
algebra that is the norm closure of all polynomials in
A
. Thus, Corollary
1.38
and Theorem 4 of
[
87
]
yield a short proof of
Kadison’s inequality. (Stinespring’s proof of Theorem 4 is short and elementary). Størmer has proved a complementary result: A positive maps from one
C^{*}
algebra into another commutative
C^{*}
algebra is completely positive. Both theorems are discussed in
[
88
]
.

Unital maps that satisfy (
1.9.5
) are known as
Schwarz maps
.
(The terminology is nearly, but not completely, standard. Petz
[
74
, p. 62]
calls any map satisfying (
1.9.5
) a Schwarz map.)
One might hope that Corollary
1.38
would be valid whenever
\Phi
is a Schwarz map. Choi proved
[
32
, Proposition 4.1]

that the inequality (
1.38
) essentially characterizes the set of
2
-positive maps: Under the condition that
\Phi(I)
is invertible,

\Phi:M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{m}(\mathbb{C})
is
2
-positive if and only if (
1.38
) is valid for all
C\in M_{n}^{++}(\mathbb{C})
and all
X\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
.
He also showed in
[
32
, Appendix A]
, that there exist Schwarz maps that are not
2
-positive; e.g., the map
map
\Phi
on
M_{2}(\mathbb{C})
given by

\Phi(X)=\frac{1}{2}X^{T}+\frac{1}{4}\operatorname{Tr}[X]I\ ,

(1.9.6)

where
X^{T}
is the transpose of
X
. His construction was further developed in
[
91
]
and
[
46
, Example 3.6]
.

Corollary
1.38
is the source of many monotonicity inequalities, and it will be the only inequality used in Section 12 when
we prove, following
[
45
]
, the monotonicity versions of Lieb’s first three theorems.
However, in that proof we will need Corollary
1.38
only in the
tracial
form:

\operatorname{Tr}\left[\Phi\left(X^{*}\frac{1}{C}X\right)\right]\geq\operatorname{Tr}\left[\Phi(X)^{*}\frac{1}{\Phi(C)}\Phi(X)\right]\ .

(1.9.7)

One may expect that (
1.9.7
) is valid for a wider class of maps
\Phi
than
2
-positive maps, and this is the case. For a recent result in this direction, see
[
26
]
.

We close this section with a further extension of the Lieb-Ruskai Monotonicity Theorem. Let

\Phi:M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{m}(\mathbb{C})
be a non-zero positive map. As noted earlier, even if
X\in M_{n}^{++}(\mathbb{C})
,
\Phi(X)
need not be invertible. The map
\Xi^{\dagger}_{m,n}
for
m>n
is completely positive and trace preserving even, but
\Xi^{\dagger}_{m,n}(X)
is not invertible for any
X\in M_{n}^{++}(\mathbb{C})
. (See the second paragraph of Section 3).

The following was proved by Ando
[
2
, Corollary 3.1]
who assumed both
H
and
X
to be positive definite, and using a more restrictive definition of positivity that entailed the invertibility of
\Phi(X)
. Choi observed
[
32
, Proposition 4.3]
that the same proof allows for
H
to be merely self-adjoint.

Theorem 1.41
.

(Ando, Choi) Let

\Phi:M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{m}(\mathbb{C})
be a positive map such that
\Phi(I)
is invertible. Then for all self-adjoint
H\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
and all
X\in M_{n}^{++}(\mathbb{C})
,

\Phi(HX^{-1}H)\geq\Phi(H)\Phi(X)^{-1}\Phi(H)\ .

(1.9.8)

Beweis.

Define
\Psi:M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{m}(\mathbb{C})
by

\Psi(Y)=\Phi(X)^{-1/2}\Phi(X^{1/2}YX^{1/2})\Phi(X)^{-1/2}\ ,

(1.9.9)

and note the
\Psi
is positive an unital. Then taking
Y:=X^{-1}HX^{-1/2}
, which is self-adjoint, Kadison’s inequality yields
\Psi(Y^{2})\geq\Psi(Y)^{2}
, and this is equivalent to (
1.9.8
).
∎

The condition that
\Phi(I)
be invertible can be relaxed as follows: If
\Phi(I)
is not invertible, then
\Phi(I)
has the block matrix structure

\Phi(I)=U^{*}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}B&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right]U

(1.9.10)

where
U\in M_{m}(\mathbb{C})
is unitary and for some
1\leq\ell<m
,
B\in M_{\ell}^{++}(\mathbb{C})
. If
X\in M_{n}^{++}(\mathbb{C})
, there are numbers
0<c_{1},c_{2}
such that
c_{1}I\leq X\leq c_{2}I
and hence
c_{1}\Phi(I)\leq\Phi(X)\leq c_{2}\Phi(I)
. It follows that

\Phi(X)=U^{*}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\widehat{X}&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right]U

(1.9.11)

where
U
is the same unitary figuring in (
1.9.10
) and
\widehat{X}\in M_{\ell}^{++}(\mathbb{C})
, again with the same
\ell
as in (
1.9.10
).
The
Moore-Penrose generalized inverse
of
\Phi(X)
, denoted
\Phi(X)^{+}
is given by

\Phi(X)^{+}=U^{*}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\widehat{X}^{-1}&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right]U\ .

(1.9.12)

Every self adjoint
H\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
can be written as the difference of two matrices in
M_{n}^{++}(\mathbb{C})
, and it follows that
\Phi(H)
has the form

\Phi(H)=U^{*}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\widehat{H}&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right]U

(1.9.13)

with
U
and
\ell
as before and
\widehat{H}\in M_{\ell}(\mathbb{C})
. Given such a positive map
\Phi
we may define
\widehat{\Phi}:M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{\ell}(\mathbb{C})
by

\widehat{\Phi}(Z)=\Xi_{m,\ell}(U\Phi(Z)U^{*})

(1.9.14)

for all
Z\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, where, as in Section 3,
\Xi_{m,\ell}
“picks off” the upper-left
\ell\times\ell
block of the matrix in
M_{m}(\mathbb{C})
to which it is applied.
For seld-adjoint
H
, we may then recover
\Phi(H)
from
\widehat{\Phi}(H)
through

\Phi(H)=U^{*}\Xi^{\dagger}_{m,\ell}(\widehat{\Phi}(H))U\ .

(1.9.15)

Since
\widehat{\Phi}(I)
is invertible, we have the following corollary of Theorem
1.41
:

Corollary 1.42
.

Let

\Phi:M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{m}(\mathbb{C})
be a non-zero positive map. Then for all self-adjoint
H\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
and all
X\in M_{n}^{++}(\mathbb{C})
,

\Phi(HX^{-1}H)\geq\Phi(H)\Phi(X)^{+}\Phi(H)\ .

(1.9.16)

Beweis.

With
\widehat{\Phi}
defined as in (
1.9.14
), we may apply Theorem
1.41
to conclude

\widehat{\Phi}(HX^{-1}H)\geq\widehat{\Phi}(H)\widehat{\Phi}(X)^{-1}\widehat{\Phi}(H)

The claim then follows from (
1.9.15
) together with the fact that

W\mapsto U^{*}\Xi^{\dagger}_{m,\ell}(W)U

(1.9.17)

is a
*
-homomorphism from
M_{\ell}(\mathbb{C})
into
M_{m}(\mathbb{C})
, together with (
1.9.12
).
∎

1.10
The GNS construction for
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})

Uhlmann made an important contribution to our subject
[
97
]
by bringing the Gelfand-Naimark-Segal representation (GNS) of

M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
associated to the trace into the tool-kit.
The GNS representation is explicitly mentioned at the end of Section 5 in
[
97
]

where he explains that a construction he has been using in these terms.
Simon
[
84
]
made a simpler and more direct development of this point of view, and used it to prove the Lieb Concavity Theorem,
and Donald
[
36
]
had used it to prove the joint convexity of the relative entropy. It also plays a role in the work of
Pusz and Woronowicz
[
78
]
who cite Uhlmann
[
97
]
.

Regard

M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
equipped with the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product
\langle X,Y\rangle:=\operatorname{Tr}[X^{*}Y]
as a Hilbert space. For
X\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
,
define
L_{X}
to be the operator on
{\mathcal{H}}
given by
L_{X}A:=XA
. That is,
L_{X}
is left multiplication by
X
. It is readily checked that

X\mapsto L_{X}
is a
*
-homomorphism from
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
into the linear operators on
M_{n}(C)
regarded as a Hilbert space,
and this is the GNS representation of
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
induced by the trace.

Likewise, define
R_{X}
to be the operator of right multiplication by
X
; that is,
R_{X}B=BX
. Notice that

\langle C,R_{X}B\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}[C^{*}BX]=\operatorname{Tr}[(CX^{*})^{*}B]=\langle R_{X^{*}}C,B\rangle
, and hence
(R_{X})^{\dagger}=R_{X^{*}}
. A similar computation shows that

(L_{X})^{\dagger}=L_{X^{*}}
. In particular, if
X
is self-adjoint so are both
R_{X}
and
L_{X}
, and then it is easy to see that if

X
is strictly positive so are
R_{X}
and
L_{X}
,
and indeed when
X
is self-adjoint,
X
,
L_{X}
and
R_{X}
all have the same spectrum, and for any real valued function
f
defined on the spectrum of
X
,

f(L_{X})=L_{f(X)}
and
f(R_{X})=R_{f(X)}
. Since
R_{X}
and
L_{Y}^{-1}
are positive and commute,
R_{X}L_{Y}^{-1}
is positive, and hence for any

f:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
,
f(R_{X}L_{Y}^{-1})
is well defined by the Spectral Theorem.

For example, consider the function
f(x)=x\log x-x+1
. It is evident that
f(x)\geq 0
with equality only at
x=0
Hence
X,Y\in M_{n}^{++}(\mathbb{C})
,

f(R_{X}L_{Y}^{-1})
is a positive operator on
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, and then so is

f(R_{X}L_{Y}^{-1})L_{Y}=R_{X}(\log R_{x}-\log L_{Y})-R_{X}+L_{Y}\ .

But then

0\leq\langle I,f(R_{X}L_{Y}^{-1})L_{Y}I\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}[X(\log X-\log Y)]-\operatorname{Tr}[X]+\operatorname{Tr}[Y]\ .

If
X
and
Y
are density matrices, then the right side is
D(X||Y)
, and this shows that for density matrices,
D(X||Y)\geq 0
, and in fact, expanding
I
in a basis
\{|u_{i}\rangle\langle v_{j}|\}_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
where the
u_{i}
’s are eigenvectors of
Y
and the
v_{j}
’s are eigenvectors of
X
, one see that

D(X||Y)=0
if and only if
X=Y
. This provides the second elementary proof of (
1.2.6
) promised in Section 2, this time without using Klein’s inequality.
The second proof of (
1.2.6
) provides only the barest hint of the utility of writing trace functionals in terms of the GNS representation. To get the most out of this approach, we need one more tool, namely integral representations for operator monotone and convex functions.

1.11
Operator monotonicity and convexity

A function
f:(0,\infty)\to{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}
is said to be
operator monotone increasing
in case for all
A,B\in M_{n}^{++}(\mathbb{C})
, any
n
,

A\geq B
implies
f(A)\geq f(B)
, and
f
is said to
operator monotone decreasing
if
-f
is operator monotone increasing.

Consider
f(x)=x^{-1}
. For
A,B>0
, define
X:=(A+B)^{-1/2}A(A+B)^{-1/2}
and
Y:=(A+B)^{-1/2}B(A+B)^{-1/2}
.
Evidently,
A\geq B\iff X\geq Y
, and
Y^{-1}\geq X^{-1}\iff B^{-1}\geq A^{-1}
. Since
X+Y=1
,
X
and
Y
commute, and then by the spectral theorem
X\geq Y
implies
Y^{-1}\ \geq X^{-1}
simply because
f(x)=x^{-1}
is monotone decreasing in
x
. Thus, the function
f(x)=x^{-1}
is operator monotone decreasing. This one example leads to many others: For instance, let
0<t<1
. Then there is the integral representation

x^{t}=\frac{\sin(\pi t)}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda^{t}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{\lambda+x}\right){\rm d}{\lambda}\ ,

and now it follows from what we have just proved that
f(x)=x^{t}
is operator monotone increasing for all
0<t<1
. By a theorem of Löwner
[
65
]
, every operator monotone increasing functions has an integral representation of this general form (see below), and this is the deep part of the theory. Simon’s book
[
85
]
contains a beautiful account, with many proofs, some new, of Löwner’s Theorem. However, for the specific examples that arise in this paper, elementary arguments suffice, such as the ones provided just above. For the generalizations that follow, more is required.

We quote Löwner’s Theorem
[
65
]
as stated in Ando and Hiai
[
3
]
; see also
[

]
and
[

]
:

Theorem 1.43

(Löwner’s theorem).

For
x,\lambda\in[0,\infty)
, define the function
\phi(x,\lambda)
by

\phi(x,\lambda):=(1+\lambda)\frac{x}{\lambda+x}\ ,

(1.11.1)

and define
\phi(x,\infty):=x=\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\infty}\phi(\lambda,x)
,
and notice that for each
x
,
\phi(x,\lambda)
is a bounded function of
\lambda
, so that for any finite positive Borel measure
\mu
on
(0,\infty)
, all
\beta\in{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}
and all
\gamma\geq 0
,

h(x):=\beta+\gamma x+\int_{(0,\infty]}\phi(x,\lambda){\rm d}\mu\ ,

(1.11.2)

is a well defined function on
{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}_{+}
. The mapping
(\beta,\gamma,\mu)\mapsto h
is an affine isomorphism onto the class of operator monotone increasing functions.

Note that

\frac{x}{\lambda+x}=1-\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+x}\ ,

(1.11.3)

from which it is clear that for each
\lambda
,
\phi(\lambda,x)
is concave and monotone increasing in
x
, not only as a function of a real variable, but also in the operator sense. Thus all operator monotone functions
h
that are real valued on
[0,\infty)
are also operator concave, and thus
-h
is operator convex, and monotone decreasing.

By Theorem 3.3 of Bendat and Sherman
[
14
, Theorem 3.3]
, a real valued function
f
on
(0,\infty)
is operator convex if and only if for each
x_{0}\in(0,\infty)
,

h(x):=\frac{f(x)-f(x_{0})}{x-x_{0}}

is an operator monotone increasing function. An operator convex function is necessarily convex in the ordinary sense, and hence
f
has a right deriviative
f^{\prime}_{+}(x)
at each
x>0
, and this is an increasing function of
x
. Therefore

f^{\prime}_{+}(0):=\lim_{x\downarrow 0}f^{\prime}_{+}(x)
exists, though it may take the value
-\infty
.
It may be deduced from the result of Bendata and sherman that every operator convex function
f
on
[0,\infty)
with
f^{\prime}_{+}(0)\in{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}
has an integral representation of the form

f(x)=\alpha+\beta x+\gamma x^{2}+\int_{(0,\infty)}(\lambda+1)\frac{x^{2}}{\lambda+x}{\rm d}\mu

(1.11.4)

where
\mu
is a finite Borel measure and where
\alpha,\beta\in{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}
and
\gamma\geq 0
. It will often be convenient to extend the integrand to
(0,\infty]
by continuity, and then replace
\mu
with the finite Borel measure
\nu
on
(0,\infty]
that agrees with
\mu
on
(0,\infty)
and with
\nu(\{\infty\})=\gamma
. Then we can rewrite (
1.11.4
) as

f(x)=\alpha+\beta x+\int_{(0,\infty]}(\lambda+1)\frac{x^{2}}{\lambda+x}{\rm d}\nu

(1.11.5)

The condition
f^{\prime}_{+}(0)>-\infty
excludes certain cases such as
f(x)=-\log x
and
f(x)=x\log x
that come up in the next sections, but these cases are dealt with by using the elementary integral representation for the logarithm given below in (
1.13.1
). Alternatively, one has the integral representation
(
1.11.4
) for
f(x+\epsilon)
for any
\epsilon>0
.

Recall that if
f
is operator monotone increasing, then
f
is operator concave, and hence if
f
is operator monotone decreasing, then
f
is operator convex. For this special class of operator convex functions, there is another integral representation due to Hansen
[
43
]
:

Lemma 1.44

(Hansen).

A function
f(x):(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
is monotone decreasing if and only if

f(x)=\beta+\int_{[0,\infty)}\frac{\lambda+1}{\lambda+x}{\rm d}{\nu}

(1.11.6)

where
{\nu}
is a finite Borel measure on
[0,\infty)
and
\beta\geq 0
.

For the reader’s convenience, here is the short proof of this due to Ando and Hiai
[
3
]
.

Beweis.

Note that
f(x)
is operator monotone decreasing if and only if
f(1/x)
is operator monotone increasing, and hence

f(1/x)=\beta+\int_{(0,\infty]}(\lambda+1)\frac{x}{\lambda+x}{\rm d}\mu

for some finite Borel measure
\mu
on
(0,\infty)
, and some
\beta\in{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}
.

Define
{\nu}
to the be the push-forward of
{\mu}
under the map
\lambda\mapsto\lambda^{-1}
.
Then

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta+\int_{(0,\infty]}(\lambda+1)\frac{1/x}{\lambda+1/x}{\rm d}\mu=\beta+\int_{(0,\infty]}(\lambda+1)\frac{1}{\lambda x+1}{\rm d}\mu

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta+\int_{[0,\infty)}(1/\lambda+1)\frac{1}{x/\lambda+1}{\rm d}{\nu}=\beta+\int_{[0,\infty)}\frac{\lambda+1}{\lambda+x}{\rm d}{\nu}\ .

Since
\beta=\lim_{x\uparrow\infty}f(x)
,

. Evidently (

) defines an operator monotone decreasing function.
∎

The following theorem is due to Hansen
[
44
, Proposition 4,3, Remark 4.6]
who proved the “if” part, and to
Ando and Hiai
[
3
]
who proved the “only if” part. Their proof is provided in the context of an investigation of operator log-convexity, and it is a
consequence of a theorem asserting the equivalence of 13 conditions. The following short and direct proof of the “only if” part of this theorem was provided to me by Frank Hansen in recent correspondence, and I am thankful to him for permission to present it here.

Theorem 1.45

(Hansen, Ando and Hiai).

Let
f:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
. Then

(X,v)\mapsto\langle v,f(X)v\rangle

is jointly convex on
M_{n}^{++}(\mathbb{C})\times\mathbb{C}^{n}
if and only if
f
is operator monotone decreasing.

Beweis.

Suppose first that
f(x):(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
is operator monotone decreasing.
Hansen
[
44
]

showed that
(v,X)\mapsto\langle v,(\lambda+X)^{-1}v\rangle

is jointly convex. In
[
44
, Remark 4.5]
he gave a proof of this fact that
was suggested to him by Lieb: Choose any
fixed
unit vector
u
, and then for each vector
v
,
define
Z_{v}
to be the rank one operator
Z_{v}:=|v\rangle\langle u|
. Then for each
\lambda
,

\langle v,(\lambda+X)^{-1}v\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}[Z_{v}^{*}(\lambda+X)^{-1}Z_{v}]\ ,

and now the joint convexity of
(v,X)\mapsto\langle v,(\lambda+X)^{-1}v\rangle
follows from Kiefer’s Inequality (
1.9.4
), and the
joint convexity of
(v,X)\mapsto\langle v,f(X)v\rangle
then follows from the integral representation (
1.11.6
).

Suppose next that
(X,v)\mapsto\langle v,f(X)v\rangle
is jointly convex. Define
g(x):=x^{2}f(x)
.
Then for
X,Y\in M_{n}^{++}(\mathbb{C})
and
0<s<1
, and any
v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}

\displaystyle\langle v,g((1-s)X+sY)v\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle((1-s)X+sY)v,f((1-s)X+sY)((1-s)X+sY)v\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-s)\langle Xv,f(X)Xv\rangle+s\langle Yv,f(Y)Yv\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-s)\langle v,g(X)v\rangle+s\langle v,g(Y)v\rangle\ .

Thus,
g
is operator convex, and evidently
f
is operator convex. It is not hard to see that
g^{\prime}_{+}(0)>-\infty
, but one may replace
f(x)
by
f(x+\epsilon)
for
\epsilon>0
, and then take
\epsilon
to
0
at the end. Either way,
g
has an integral representation of the form

g(x)=\alpha+\beta x+\gamma x^{2}+\int_{(0,\infty)}(\lambda+1)\frac{1}{\lambda+x}{\rm d}\mu\ ,

where
\mu
is a finite Borel measure on
(0,\infty)
. Therefore

f(x)=\alpha x^{-2}+\beta x^{-1}+\gamma+\int_{(0,\infty)}(\lambda+1)\frac{1}{\lambda+x}{\rm d}\mu\ ,

and since
f
is operator convex and non-negative
\alpha=0
and
\beta,\gamma\geq 0
. It follows immediately that
f
is operator monotone decreasing.
∎

1.12
Inequalities relating to operator monotonicity

The following fundamental theorem of Hiai and Petz

(Hiai and Petz).

(1) The function

is operator monotone increasing.

(2) For all positive definite

Lemma 1.48
.

For positive invertible

1.13
Trace inequalities associated to operator convex functions

The Data Processing Inequality

D(\Phi^{\dagger}(X)||\Phi^{\dagger}(Y))\leq D(X||Y)

lies outside the direct scope of Theorem
1.46
because
D(X||Y)
cannot be written in terms of
G_{f}(X,Y)
for any operator monotone function
f
from
(0,\infty)
to
(0,\infty)
. However, one does have
D(X||Y)=\langle I,G_{f}(X,Y)I\rangle
for
f(x)=x\log x
and also

D(Y||X)=\langle I,G_{f}(X,Y)I\rangle
for
f(x)=-\log x
. Both of these functions are operator convex, as may be seen from the integral representation

\log(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda+1}-\frac{1}{\lambda+x}\right){\rm d}\lambda\ .

(1.13.1)

Alternatively, in 1986 Donald
[
36
]
gave an entirely elementary proof of the joint convexity of
D(X||Y)
taking as his starting point the integral formula

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(y-x)^{2}t}{(1-t)x+ty}{\rm d}t=x(\log x-\log y)+y-x

(1.13.2)

valid for all
x,y>0
.
Applying this with
R_{X}
in place of
x
and
L_{Y}
in place of
y
, where
X
and
Y
are two non-degenerate density matrices, he obtained

D(X||Y)=\int_{0}^{1}\left\langle{\mathds{1}},(L_{Y}-R_{X})\frac{1}{(1-t)R_{X}+tL_{Y}}(L_{Y}-R_{X}){\mathds{1}}\right\rangle t{\rm d}t\ .

(1.13.3)

Now the joint convexity is an immediate consequence of Kiefer’s inequality. However, Donald did something
even more elementary
: He used this formula to deduce an expression

D(X||Y)=\sup\{\operatorname{Tr}[XC+YD]\ :\ (X,Y)\in\Omega\}\ ,

where
\Omega
is specific convex set in
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\times M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
consisting of pairs of self-adjoint matrices. This effectively displays the relative entropy as a Legendre transform, and specifies the Legendre transform of the relative entropy. His proof built on ideas of Pusz and Woronowicz
[
77
,
78
]
who also gave a proof of the Lieb Concavity Theorem and the joint convexity of the relative entropy by giving explicit Legendre transform representations in
[
78
, Section 4]
. The variational formula of Pusz and Woronowicz was rediscovered by Kosaki in 1986
[
55
]
. (Kosaki wrote that his result is implicit in
[
77
]
, but he does not cite
[
78
]
in which his formula is explicitly proved.)

We return to the study of operators of the form

G_{f}(X,Y):=f(R_{X}L_{Y}^{-1})L_{Y}\ ,

(1.13.4)

where now we shall take
f
to be operator convex. Some useful monotonicity theorems in this setting can be obtained from integral representations and the Lieb-Ruskai Monotony Theorem by making somewhat use of the
perspective function
construction
[
34
]
which associates the function

g_{f}(x,y):=f(x/y)y

to the function
f(x)
.
Note that for
f(x)=x^{2}
, the associated perspective function is
x^{2}/y
. The integral representation of a general operator convex function
allows it to be expressed in functions of this type, opening the way to the application of the Lieb-Ruskai Monotony Theorem.

Theorem 1.50
.

Let
f
be an operator convex function on
[0,\infty)
with
f^{\prime}_{+}(0)>-\infty
. Let
g_{f}(x,y)
be the perspective function of

Then for some finite Borel measure
\nu
on

, and some

(1.13.5)

Beweis.

From the integral representation (

) for

, and the identity

{\displaystyle\frac{x^{2}}{\lambda+x}=x-\frac{\lambda x}{\lambda+x}}
,

\displaystyle g_{f}(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha y+\beta x+\int_{(0,\infty]}\left(x-\lambda\frac{x}{\lambda+x/y}\right)(1+\lambda){\rm d}\nu

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha y+\beta x+\int_{(0,\infty]}\left(x-\lambda\frac{1}{(\lambda^{-1}x)^{-1}+y^{-1}}\right)(1+\lambda){\rm d}\nu\ .

Now using the identity, valid for all

,
which follows easily from

if need be, we have
the integral representation (
1.13.5
), which is equivalent to

g_{f}(x,y)=\alpha y+\beta x+\int_{(0,\infty]}\left(x\frac{1}{x+\lambda y}x\right)(1+\lambda){\rm d}\nu\ ,

(1.13.7)

G_{f}(X,Y)=\alpha L_{Y}+\beta R_{X}+\int_{(0,\infty]}\left(R_{X}\frac{1}{R_{X}+\lambda L_{Y}}R_{X}\right)(1+\lambda){\rm d}\nu\ .

(1.13.8)

Then (
1.13.6
) follows directly from the corollary of the Lieb-Ruskai Theorem asserting that
(A,Z)\mapsto Z^{*}A^{-1}Z
is sub-additive so that for all positive invertible

,

G_{f}(X_{1}+X_{2},Y_{1}+Y_{2})\leq G_{f}(X_{1},Y_{1})+G_{f}(X_{2},Y_{2})\ ,

(1.13.9)

and then since
(X,Y)\mapsto G_{f}(X,Y)
is homogeneous of degree one, it is also jointly convex.
∎

Corollary 1.52
.

For all operator convex functions
f
on
{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}_{+}

is jointly convex on
M_{n}^{++}(\mathbb{C})
.

Beweis.

Evidently
\operatorname{Tr}[K^{*}f(R_{X}L_{Y}^{-1})YK]=\langle K,G_{f}(X,Y)K\rangle
.
∎

Likewise, taking

Let
f

[0,\infty)

invertible operators

\mathcal{H}

2
-positive maps
\Phi

\Phi(g_{f}(X,Y))\geq g_{f}(\Phi(X),\Phi(Y))\ .

Suppose that
f

. Then from the integral representation (

\displaystyle f(Y^{-1/2}XY^{-1/2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha+\beta Y^{-1/2}XY^{-1/2}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{(0,\infty]}Y^{-1/2}XY^{-1/2}\frac{1}{\lambda+Y^{-1/2}XY^{-1/2}}Y^{-1/2}XY^{-1/2}{\rm d}(\lambda+1)\nu\ .

g_{f}(X,Y)=\alpha Y+\beta X+\int_{(0,\infty]}X\frac{1}{\lambda Y+X}X(\lambda+1){\rm d}\nu\ ,

\Phi(g_{f}(X,Y))=\alpha\Phi(Y)+\beta\Phi(X)+\int_{(0,\infty]}\Phi\left(X\frac{1}{\lambda Y+X}X\right)(\lambda+1){\rm d}\nu\ .

to the Lieb-Ruskai Monotonicity Theorem,

\Phi\left(X\frac{1}{\lambda Y+X}X\right)\geq\Phi(X)\frac{1}{\lambda\Phi(Y)+\Phi(X)}\Phi(X)\ .

∎

The coda to this paper is a timeline. We have discussed many theorems that have been proven to be equivalent; in some sense
these theorems are the many faces of SSA.
The discussion has not been chronological, but rather focused on the connections between the ideas that have emerged in the 60 years since the
1962 paper of Wigner and Yanase
[
100
]
. The SSA conjecture of Lanford and Robinson
[
58
]
was published in 1968. The 1970 paper of Araki and
Lieb accelerated the progress. It identified the need for a generalization of the Golden-Thompson inequality to three matrices. At least Lieb and Uhlmann
were working on this problem, completely independently of one another. They both realized that proving that

X\mapsto\operatorname{Tr}[\exp(H+\log X)]
is concave on
M_{n}^{++}(\mathbb{C})
would lead to a proof of SSA; as explained above, this is
equivalent through the simple Lemma
1.16
to Lieb’s Triple Matrix Theorem, which, as explained in Section 4, can be used to close the gap between the partial result of Araki and Lieb and SSA.

The parallel but partial insights of Uhlmann are recorded in the final section of

[
96
]
, in which he proved a mild generalization of Theorem 1 in
[
6
]
, and then he conjectured
[
96
, Vermutung III]
the concavity of

X\mapsto\operatorname{Tr}[\exp(H+\log X)]
. Presumably, by the time he made this conjecture, Lieb had already proved it, but in any case when Lieb proved the result, he was unaware of any such conjecture by anybody else, as he has told me. Uhlmann
[
96
]
did not explain how the concavity of
X\mapsto\operatorname{Tr}[\exp(H+\log X)]
would lead to the proof of SSA. He also noted, again without explanation, that his Conjecture III
(Lieb’s Theorem
1.33
) would follow from the generalization of the Wigner-Yanase result that became Epstein’s Theorem. While he did not prove his
conjecture, he was following the trail blazed by Araki and Lieb
[
6
]
and had realized the connection with the work of Wigner and Yanase
[
100
,
101
]
. However,
Lieb
[
60
]
was the first to seal the connection between the work of Wigner and Yanase and the SSA conjecture, and to prove the missing
Triple Matrix Theorem, Theorem
1.34
. All of the many deep results
[
60
]
are derived by simple arguments from the Lieb Concavity Theorem, Theorem
1.1
, which had settled the Wigner Yanase Dyson conjecture. In this sense, all of the deep theorems in
[
60
]
owe their depth to the Lieb Concavity Theorem.

The 1973 paper of Lieb
[
60
]
opened the floodgates. The 1973 proof of SSA by Lieb and Ruskai quickly followed. By what has been explained in the last paragraph, all of the results in their paper
ultimately depended on the Lieb Concavity Theorem. The paper
[
60
]
was written while Elliott Lieb was visiting I.H.E.S., and after
Henri Epstein, then a Professor at I.H.E.S., learned of the results, he provided not only a proof of Theorem
1.26
, the one conjecture in
[
60
]
,
but also a new proof of the concavity of

X\mapsto\operatorname{Tr}[\exp(H+\log X)]
, Theorem
1.33
, and his proof was independent of the Lieb Concavity Theorem. Epstein does not discuss SSA,
but with his proof of Theorem
1.33
, one has the first route to the proof of SSA,
building on
[
6
]
, that does not depend upon the Lieb Concavity Theorem; Theorem
1.1
.

The next important breakthrough came in 1977 and is due to Uhlmann
[
97
]
. He proved, building on work of Pusz and Woronowicz
[
77
]
,
that the DPI was valid not only for all completely positive trace preserving maps, but for all adjoints of unital Schwarz maps. He also introduced the monotonicity version of the Lieb Concavity Theorem, and even proved it for the wider class of unital Schwarz maps
[
97
, Proposition 17]
.

The next progress came in 1978 with the work of Pusz and Woronowicz
[
78
]
who gave a new proof of the Lieb Concavity Theorem
and the joint convexity of the relative entropy by displaying the relevant functionals as explicit Legendre transforms. They did not discuss SSA directly,
but their results provided an alternate route to SSA on account of the equivalence with joint convexity of the relative entropy which was well understood by then. The approach of Pusz and Woronowicz
was greatly simplified in 1986 by Donald
[
36
]
who provided the first truly elementary proof of the joint convexity of the relative entropy,
using nothing but direct computation. Again, he didnot discuss SSA, but his results provided the first completely elementary path to
SSA. Finally, in
[
21
]
, a proof of classical SSA that does not refer
to conditional probabilities was provided, and it was shown, using Epstein’s Theorem, that this proof extended to the quantum case.

As noted above, since the work of Uhlmann, it has been known that complete positivity was not required for the DPI; it is valid for all trace preserving Schwarz maps. In
[
76
]
Petz asked an important question: Is the DPI
valid for all positive maps trace preserving maps? It was recently proved by Müller-Hermes and Reeb
[
68
]
that this is the case. They drew on work of Beigi
[
11
]
who proved the monotonicity under quantum operations of certain sandwiched Rényi entropies that are discussed in Section 8 of this paper. His method relied on interpolation for certain non-commutative
L^{p}
spaces. Although he invoked complete positivity in his proof, Müller-Hermes and Reeb noted that positivity alone would have sufficed, and carried this through, and also in an infinite dimensional setting. Shortly afterwards, Jencova
[
50
]
extended the proof to a general von Neumann algebra setting.
