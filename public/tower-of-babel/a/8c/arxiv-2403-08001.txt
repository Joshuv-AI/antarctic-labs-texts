1.
Introduction

1.1.
The model

We study flows of incompressible fluids with elastic properties governed by the incompressible generalized Navier-Stokes-Voigt (NSV) equations in a bounded domain

\mathcal{O}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 2
, with its boundary denoted by
\partial\mathcal{O}
, during the time interval
[0,T]
, with
0<T<\infty
.
The momentum equation is assumed here to be perturbed by a stochastic term, that is, infinite-dimensional multiplicative Gaussian noise which is the product of
\Phi(\bm{u})
and a Wiener process
\mathrm{W}(\cdot)
.
The problem under consideration is posed by the following system of equations:

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bm{u}-\kappa\Delta\bm{u}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\bm{f}+\operatorname{div}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\pi\mathbf{I}+\nu|\mathbf{D}(\bm{u})|^{p-2}\mathbf{D}(\bm{u})-\bm{u}\otimes\bm{u}}}\right)}}\right]\mathrm{d}t+\Phi(\bm{u})\mathrm{d}\mathrm{W}(t),\text{ in }\mathcal{O}_{T},

(1.2)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}\bm{u}

\displaystyle=0,\text{ in }\mathcal{O}_{T},

(1.3)

\displaystyle\bm{u}

\displaystyle=\bm{u}_{0},\text{ in }\mathcal{O}\times\{0\},

(1.4)

\displaystyle\bm{u}

\displaystyle=\bm{0},\text{ on }\Gamma_{T},

where
\mathcal{O}_{T}:=\mathcal{O}\times(0,T)
and
\Gamma_{T}:=\partial\mathcal{O}\times[0,T]
. Here,
\bm{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{d})
and
\bm{f}=(f_{1},\dots,f_{d})
are vector-valued functions,
\pi
is a scalar-valued function, whereas
\nu
and
\kappa
are positive constants. In real-world applications, space dimensions of interest are
d=2
and
d=3
, and in that case
\bm{u}
accounts for the velocity field,
\pi
is the pressure,
\bm{f}
represents an external forcing field. Greek letters
\nu
and
\kappa
stay for positive constants that, in the dimensions of physical interest, account for the kinematics viscosity and for the relaxation time, that is, the time required for a viscoelastic fluid relax from a deformed state back to its equilibrium configuration.
The power-law index
p
is a constant that characterizes the flow and is assumed to be such that
1<p<\infty
.
In particular, for
1<p<2,
the model describes shear-thinning fluids, when
p=2,
we recover the model that governs Newtonian fluids, while for
p>2,
the model describes shear-thickening fluids.
The capital letters stay for tensor-valued functions, in particular
\mathbf{I}
is the unit tensor and
\mathbf{D}(\bm{u})
is the symmetric part of the velocity gradient, that is,
\mathbf{D}(\bm{u}):=\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nabla\bm{u}+(\nabla\bm{u})^{\top}}}\right)
.
The idea behind the presence of the noise
\Phi(\bm{u})
is an interaction between the solution
\bm{u}
and the random perturbation caused by the Wiener process
\mathrm{W}(\cdot)
, and the product of these two terms represents the stochastic part in the momentum equation (
1.1
). This stochastic part can be interpreted in different ways. It can be understood as a turbulence in the fluid motion or it can be interpreted as a perturbation from the physical model. On the other hand, apart from the forcing term
\bm{f},
there might be further quantities with influence on the motion which can be characterized by this stochastic part as well.

1.2.
Literature review

We start with the physical significance of NSV equations, also named Kelvin-Voigt (KV) equations, as we have considered the generalized stochastic NSV equations (
1.1
)-(
1.4
). KV equations are used in the applications to model the response of materials that exhibit all intermediate range of properties between an elastic solid and a viscous fluid, as, for instance, polymers. These materials have some memory in the sense that they can come back to some previous state when the shear stress is removed (see
[
10
]
). The deterministic KV equations were extensively studied in the article
[
54
]
, whose author coined the name Kelvin-Voigt for the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations perturbed by the relaxation term
\kappa\partial_{t}\Delta\bm{u}
(see
[
50
]
also). However, and as observed in the work
[
66
]
, neither Kelvin nor Voigt have suggested any stress-strain relation, or system of governing equations for viscoelastic fluids (see
[
36
]
). Currently, the Navier-Stokes-Voigt name for the associated system of equations seems to be the most accepted by the researchers in this field. Mathematically speaking, and was noticed by Ladyzhenskaya (see
[
41
]
), the most important property of NSV equations is that the term
\kappa\partial_{t}\Delta\bm{u}
works as a regularization of the 3D incompressible Navier-Stokes (NS) equations ensuring the unique global solvability of the corresponding problem. The existence and uniqueness of the weak and strong solutions of NSV equations have been established in the work
[
54
]
(cf.
[
42
]
). A local in time existence and uniqueness result of an inverse problem for NSV equations is established in
[
40
]
. Several other authors have also studied some variants of the NSV equations, in many settings and under different assumptions, with respect to the well-posedness and long time behavior. It is worth to mention that the works
[
39
,
55
]
established a connection between NSV and the Oldroyd models, and proved the existence of classical global solutions for the models. Also, in the works
[
52
,
66
,
67
]
, the authors proved the existence and uniqueness of weak solutions in case of domain varying with time and with the optimal control. The authors in
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
8
,
9
]
discussed the existence, uniqueness and some qualitative properties of the solutions to some variants of NSV equations, more specifically the case with power-laws and anisotropic diffusion and several other properties. In
[
3
]
, the authors considered the generalized NSV equations for non-homogeneous and incompressible fluid flows with the diffusion and relaxation terms described by two distinct power-laws and proved the existence of weak solutions as well as large-time behavior of the solutions. The works
[
20
,
37
,
45
,
58
]
established a clear connection between NSV equations and the turbulence modeling, to be more specific, with Bardina turbulence models. The same type of connection was firstly studied in the work
[
43
]
and followed by the work
[
44
]
. The authors in
[
1
]
connects NSV equations with the Prandtl-Smagorinsky and turbulent kinematic energy models in order to compute the turbulent viscosity. Several questions on NSV equations as a regularization of both NS and Euler equations have been tackled in
[
12
,
13
,
37
]
. Also, the author in the work
[
25
]
considered generalized NS equations (NS equations with power-law) with the presence of damping term in the momentum equation which governs isothermal flows of incompressible and homogeneous non-Newtonian fluids and established the existence of weak solutions using regularization techniques, the monotone theory, compactness arguments and truncation method. In a recent work
[
7
]
, the authors considered the classical NSV problem for incompressible fluids with unknown non-constant density and established the existence of weak solutions (velocity and density), unique pressure and some regularity results of the solutions on the smoothness of the given data.

We are interested in the generalized stochastic NSV equations (
1.1
)-(
1.4
).
As mentioned above, from several physical point of view it is important to have a stochastic part in the equation of motion:

(1)

It gives a clear understanding of turbulence in the fluid motion (see
[
49
]
).

(2)

It could be viewed as a deviation from the physical model.

(3)

We are seeing that there may be additional values, generally minor ones, that have an impact on the motion in addition to the force
\bm{f}
.

Several works are available in the literature dealing with the existence of weak solutions of stochastic NS equations starting from the article
[
11
]
. One can see the work
[
28
]
for an overview. However, only a few works are available dealing with the generalized (
p\not=2
) NS problem. In the article
[
21
]
, the authors discussed the bipolar shear thinning fluid. In that work, the presence of additional bi-Laplacian term
\Delta^{2}\bm{u}
makes the computations easy. Since, the additional term gives enough initial regularity which leads to the proof using monotone operators without any truncation arguments.

In
[
62
,
64
]
, the authors considered the stochastic power-law fluids and established the existence and uniqueness of weak solutions and strong convergence of the Galerkin approximation and energy equality, respectively. There were no interaction between the solution and Brownian motion in
[
62
,
64
]
(additive noise). Further, in
[
14
]
, the author extended the existence theory of the stochastic power-law fluids and improved the results of
[
62
,
64
]
. He proved the existence of weak solutions (in both analytic and probabilistic sense), using the standard procedure as the pressure term disappear in the weak formulation and later it reconstructed into the formulation. He borrowed the idea from
[
63
]
(for deterministic setting), which relates each term in the equation a pressure part. Moreover, he stabilized the auxiliary equations by adding a large power of
\bm{u}
and the approach was based on Galekin method. Later, he extended the deterministic approach of the work
[
63
]
to the stochastic case and combined the techniques from nonlinear partial differential equations with stochastic calculus for martingales. Since the author cannot apply test functions in this situation, he instead employed Itô’s formula to obtain energy estimates and other stochastic tools. Finally, in order to justify the limit in the nonlinear terms, he used the monotone operator theory together with the
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
–truncation.

Let us mention some works available for stochastic NSV equations. In
[
46
]
, the authors considered 3D stochastic NSV equations in bounded domains perturbed by a finite-dimensional Brownian motion and established an LDP for the solution using weak convergence approach. The existence and long-time behavior of the solutions (more specifically mean square exponentially stability and the almost sure exponential stablility of the stationary solutions) to 3D stochastic NSV equations in bounded domains perturbed by infinite-dimensional Wiener process obtained in
[
2
]
. In a recent work
[
65
]
, the author studied the stability of pullback random attractors for 3D stochastic NSV equations with delays.

1.3.
Objectives and novelties of the paper

The main aim of this work is to prove the existence of martingale solution and unique probabilistic strong solution of generalized stochastic NSV equations perturbed by infinite-dimensional Wiener process. There seems to be a very scarce knowledge about the model presented in this work, and to the authors best knowledge the analysis presented here has not yet been investigated in the available literature.

•

As we have mentioned above, there are only a few works available in the literature dealing with stochastic power-law fluids
[
14
,
62
,
64
]
. In the first work
[
14
]
, the author proved the existence theory for stochastic power-law fluids driven by an infinite-dimensional multiplicative Gaussian noise. The latter two works
[
62
,
64
]
discussed the existence and uniqueness of weak solutions for stochastic power-law fluids driven by a finite-dimensional Brownian motion.

•

In this work, we are following the stochastic approach considered in the work
[
14
]
, which in turn was an extension to the stochastic case of the deterministic problem studied in the work
[
63
]
. We are also considering the perturbation by an infinite-dimensional multiplicative Gaussian noise.

In addition to the stochastic situation, the main conclusion of this work is that the Voigt term
\kappa\partial_{t}\Delta\bm{u}
regularizes the power-law model of the NS equations in such a way that the existence result can be proven up to the smallest possible value (in the sense that the Gelfand triple can still be used) of the power-law exponent
p
(
p>\frac{2d}{d+2}
), (see Theorem
2.6
below).
The NS power-law model, which corresponds to considering
\kappa=0
in the momentum equation (
1.1
), was considered in
[
14
]
.
But there the author used the
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
–truncation method, and obtained the existence result for values of
p>\frac{2(d+1)}{d+2}
.

1.4.
Organization

This article is organized as follows: We start with the basic formulation of the stochastic background, as well as Definitions
2.3
and
2.4
of martingale solution and probabilistically strong solution, respectively, and then we state our main Theorem
2.6
in Section
2
.
In Section
3
, we discuss the decomposition of pressure terms.
As in the weak formulation of the problem, the pressure term disappears (see Definition
2.3
), later it can be recovered by using a version of de Rham’s theorem. The idea of decomposing the pressure term that we use in the sequel is borrowed from the work
[
14
]
, but its first application dates back to
[
63
]
. In Section
4
, we consider an approximate system with a stabilization term (a large power of
\bm{u}
), and, by using Galerkin approximations, we obtain the uniform energy estimate, followed by the existence of martingale solution for this approximate system, and for which we still have to use the Skorokhod representation theorem. Later, we reconstruct the pressure term and using monotone operator theory leads to existence of martingale solution to the system (
1.1
)-(
1.4
). We wind up the article by establishing the pathwise uniqueness of the solution to the system (
1.1
)-(
1.4
) and hence the existence of a unique probabilistic strong solution by an application of the classical Yamada-Watanabe theorem.

2.2.
Stochastic setting

In this subsection, we start by recalling some notions of stochastic processes necessary to study stochastic partial differential equations. We consider the scalar-valued case, being the vectorial case a straightforward generalization. Let
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space, where
\Omega
denotes the set of all possible outcomes
\omega
,
\mathscr{F}
is the set of events and
\mathbb{P}:\mathscr{F}\longrightarrow[0,1]
accounts for the probability measure function. We assume that
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P})
is equipped with a filtration
\{\mathscr{F}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
, that is, with a nondecreasing family of sub-sigma fields of
\mathscr{F}
(
\mathscr{F}_{s}\subset\mathscr{F}_{t}
whenever
s<t
), such that
\mathscr{F}_{0}
contains all the null elements (elements
A\in\mathscr{F}
with
\mathbb{P}(A)=0
) and
\{\mathscr{F}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
is right-continuous (
\mathscr{F}_{t}=\mathscr{F}_{t+}=\cap_{s>t}\mathscr{F}_{s}
for all
t\in[0,T]
).
In this case, we denote a filtered probability space by
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\{\mathscr{F}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]},\mathbb{P})
.
Letting
1\leq p\leq\infty
and given a Banach space
\mathbf{B}
, we denote by
\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P};\mathbf{B}),
the space of all measurable mappings
v:(\Omega,\mathscr{F})\longrightarrow(\mathbf{B},\mathscr{B}(\mathbf{B}))
, where
\mathscr{B}(\mathbf{B})
denotes the Borel
\sigma-
algebra on
\mathbf{B}
, such that

\mathbb{E}\big[\|v\|_{\mathbf{B}}^{p}\big]<\infty,

where the expectation
\mathbb{E}
is taken with respect to
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P})
.
A
\mathbf{B}-
valued stochastic process can be interpreted as a mapping
X:[0,T]\times\Omega\longrightarrow(\mathbf{B},\mathscr{B}(\mathbf{B}))
, denoted by
X(t,\omega)
.
For a fixed
\omega\in\Omega
, the mapping
t\mapsto X(t,\omega)
is called the
path or trajectory
of
X
.
We say that a
\mathbf{B}-
valued stochastic process
X
is
adapted to a filtration

\{\mathscr{F}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
, or just
\mathscr{F}_{t}-
adapted, if
the mapping
X(t):\Omega\longrightarrow(\mathbf{B},\mathscr{B}(\mathbf{B}))
, defined by
\omega\mapsto X(t,\omega)
, is measurable for all
t\in[0,T]
.
The
\mathbf{B}-
valued stochastic process
X
is
progressively measurable
on the probability space
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P})
, with respect to the filtration
\{\mathscr{F}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
, that is, is
\{\mathscr{F}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}-
measurable, if the mapping

(s,\omega)\longmapsto X(s,\omega)

is measurable on
\mathscr{B}([0,t])\otimes\mathscr{F}_{t}
for all
t\in[0,T]
. Note that every progressively measurable stochastic process is
\mathscr{F}_{t}-
adapted.

We are now ready to define the notion of integral of a stochastic process with values in specific Banach spaces. To this end, we first consider a bounded linear operator
\mathrm{Q}\in{\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{U})}
, where
\mathbf{U}
is a separable Hilbert space with inner product denoted by
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\mathbf{U}}
and associated norm by
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{U}}
.
If
\mathrm{Q}
is supposed to be nonnegative, symmetric and with finite trace, then there exists an orthonormal basis
\{\mathbf{e}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\mathbf{U}
such that

\mathrm{Q}\mathbf{e}_{k}=\lambda_{k}\mathbf{e}_{k},\qquad\lambda_{k}\geq 0,\quad\forall\ k\in\mathbb{N}

and so that
0
is the only accumulation point of the sequence
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
.
For a fixed
\mathrm{Q}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{U})
nonnegative, symmetric and with finite trace, a
{\mathbf{U}}-
valued stochastic process
\mathrm{W}
on the probability space
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P})
is a standard
\mathrm{Q}-
Wiener process if and only if

\mathrm{W}(t)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}\beta_{k}(t)\mathbf{e}_{k},\qquad t\in[0,T],

where
\beta_{k}
, with
k\in\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\lambda_{n}>0\}
, are independent real-valued Brownian motions on
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P})
.
A
\mathrm{Q}-
Wiener process
\mathrm{W}
is said to be adapted to a filtration
\{\mathscr{F}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
, if
\mathrm{W}(t)\in\mathscr{F}_{t}
for every
t\in[0,T]
and
\mathrm{W}(t)-\mathrm{W}(s)
is independent of
\mathscr{F}_{s},
for all
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
.
Let us now set
\mathbf{U}_{0}=\mathrm{Q}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{U}
, where
\mathrm{Q}^{\frac{1}{2}}
is the operator defined by

\mathrm{Q}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{e}_{k}=\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}\mathbf{e}_{k}.

Then
\mathbf{U}_{0}
is a Hilbert space with inner product

(\bm{u},\bm{v})_{0}=\big(\mathrm{Q}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{u},\mathrm{Q}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{v}),\qquad\bm{u},\bm{v}\in{\mathbf{U}_{0}},

and associated norm denoted by
\|\cdot\|_{0}
, where
\mathrm{Q}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
is the pseudo-inverse operator of
\mathrm{Q}^{\frac{1}{2}}
.
If
\{\mathbf{e}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is an orthonormal basis of
{\mathbf{U}}_{0}
and
\{\beta_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a family of independent real-valued Brownian motions on
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P})
, then the series
\sum\limits_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}\beta_{k}(t)\mathbf{e}_{k}
is convergent in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P};{\mathbf{U}})
, because
the inclusion
{\mathbf{U}}_{0}\subset{\mathbf{U}}
defines a Hilbert-Schmidt embedding from
{\mathbf{U}}_{0}
onto
{\mathbf{U}}
.
Recall that by
\mathcal{L}_{2}({\mathbf{U}}_{0},{\mathbf{U}})
, we denote the set of all linear operators
\mathrm{Q}:\mathbf{U}_{0}\longrightarrow\mathbf{U}
such that for every orthonormal basis
\{\mathbf{e}_{k}^{0}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of
{\mathbf{U}}_{0},
one has
\sum\limits_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\|\mathrm{Q}\mathbf{e}_{k}^{0}\|_{\mathbf{U}}^{2}<\infty.

In the case that
\mathrm{Q}
is no longer of finite trace, one looses this convergence, but it is possible to define the Wiener process.
For that, we consider a further Hilbert space
{\mathbf{U}}_{1}
and a Hilbert-Schmidt embedding

.
Let us set
\mathrm{Q}_{1}=JJ^{\ast}
, where

is the adjoint operator of

.
Then

\displaystyle C({\mathcal{O}})\|\operatorname{div}\Phi\mathbf{e}_{k}\|_{-1,2}\|\bm{\psi}\|_{{2}}\leq C({\mathcal{O}})\|\Phi\mathbf{e}_{k}\|_{{2}}\|\bm{\psi}\|_{{2}}\leq C({\mathcal{O}})\|\Phi\mathbf{e}_{k}\|_{{2}}\|\bm{\psi}\|_{{2}},

for
\bm{\psi}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{O})^{d}
. Similarly, we can conclude the final part as follows:

\displaystyle\|(\Phi_{\pi}(\bm{u}_{1})-\Phi_{\pi}(\bm{u}_{2}))\mathbf{e}_{k}\|_{{2}}

\displaystyle=\|\nabla\Delta\Delta^{-2}_{\mathcal{O}}\operatorname{div}(\Phi(\bm{u}_{1})-\Phi(\bm{u}_{2}))\mathbf{e}_{k}\|_{{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C({\mathcal{O}})\|(\Phi(\bm{u}_{1})-\Phi(\bm{u}_{2}))\mathbf{e}_{k}\|_{{2}}=C({\mathcal{O}})\|\phi_{k}(\bm{u}_{1})-\phi_{k}(\bm{u}_{2})\|_{{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C({L,\mathcal{O}})\|\bm{u}_{1}-\bm{u}_{2}\|_{{2}},

where we have used (
2.11
).
∎

4.
Solvability of The Approximate System

In this section, we establish the solvability of an auxiliary problem that regularizes the problem (
1.1
)-(
1.4
) with the following stabilization term in the momentum equation:

(4.1)

\alpha\mathbf{a}(\bm{u}),\qquad\mathbf{a}(\bm{u}):=|\bm{u}|^{q-2}\bm{u},\qquad\alpha>0,\qquad 1<q<\infty.

Given
\alpha>0
, we consider the problem

(4.2)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathrm{d}(\mathrm{I}-\kappa\Delta)\bm{u}=\big\{\operatorname{div}\mathbf{A}(\bm{u})-\operatorname{div}(\bm{u}\otimes\bm{u})+\nabla\pi-\alpha\mathbf{a}(\bm{u})+\bm{f}\big\}\mathrm{d}t+\Phi(\bm{u})\mathrm{d}\mathrm{W}(t),\\
&\bm{u}(0)=\bm{u}_{0},\end{aligned}}}\right.

depending on the initial
\Lambda_{0}
and forcing
\Lambda_{\bm{f}},
laws in the conditions of (
2.9
) and (
2.10
), respectively, and for
the operator
\mathbf{A}(\bm{u})
defined in (
2.5
).
The exponent
q
in (
4.1
) is chosen in such a way that the convective term becomes a compact perturbation (see (
4.23
) below).
For that purpose, we choose

(4.3)

q\geq\max\{2p^{\prime},3\},

and thus a solution
\bm{u}
is expected in the following space:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{p,q}:=\mathrm{L}^{2}({\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P}};\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,T;\mathbf{V}))\cap\mathrm{L}^{p}({\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P}};\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;{\mathrm{W}_{0}^{1,p}(\mathcal{O})^{d}})\cap\mathrm{L}^{q}({\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P}};\mathrm{L}^{q}(0,T;{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathcal{O})^{d}})).

4.1.
Approximate solutions

We construct a solution to the problem (
4.2
) as a limit of suitable Galerkin approximations.
Let
s
be the smallest positive integer such that
{\mathrm{W}^{s,2}_{0}(\mathcal{O})^{d}}\hookrightarrow{\mathrm{W}^{1,\infty}_{0}(\mathcal{O})}^{d}
, for
s>1+\frac{d}{2}
, and let us consider the space
\mathbf{V}_{s}
associated to
{\mathrm{W}^{s,2}_{0}(\mathcal{O})^{d}}
defined in Section
2
. By means of separability, there exists a basis
\big\{\mathds{\bm{\psi}}_{k}\big\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\mathbf{V}_{s}
, formed by the eigenfunctions of a suitable spectral problem, that is, orthogonal in
{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{O})^{d}}
and that can be made orthonormal in
{\mathrm{W}^{s,2}_{0}(\mathcal{O})^{d}}
(see
[
48
, Theorem A.4.11]
).
Given

n\in\mathbb{N}
, let us consider the
n-
dimensional space
\mathbf{X}^{n}=\mathrm{span}\{\bm{\psi}_{1},\dots,\bm{\psi}_{n}\}
.
For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we search for approximate solutions of the form

(4.4)

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{n}(x,t)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}^{n}(t)\bm{\psi}_{k}(x),\quad\bm{\psi}_{k}\in\mathbf{X}^{n},

where the coefficients
c_{1}^{n}(t),\dots,c_{n}^{n}(t)

are solutions of the following
n
stochastic ordinary differential equations

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\Big[\big(\bm{u}_{n}(t),\bm{\psi}_{k}\big)+\kappa\big(\nabla\bm{u}_{n}(t),\nabla\bm{\psi}_{k}\big)\Big]

\displaystyle=\Big[\big(\bm{u}_{n}(t)\otimes\bm{u}_{n}(t):\nabla\bm{\psi}_{k}\big)-\nu\big<|\mathbf{D}(\bm{u}_{n}(t))|^{p-2}\mathbf{D}(\bm{u}_{n}(t)):\mathbf{D}(\bm{\psi}_{k})\big>+\big(\bm{f}(t),\bm{\psi}_{k}\big)\Big]\mathrm{d}t

(4.5)

\displaystyle\qquad-\alpha\big(\mathbf{a}(\bm{u}_{n}(t)),\bm{\psi}_{l}\big)+{\Phi(\bm{u}_{n}(t))}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{W}_{n}(t),\bm{\psi}_{k}\big),\qquad k=1,\dots,n,

supplemented with the initial conditions

(4.6)

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{n}(0)=\bm{u}_{0}^{n},\quad\mbox{in}\ \mathcal{O},

where
\bm{u}_{0}^{n}=P^{n}(\bm{u}_{0})
, with
P^{n}
denoting the orthogonal projection
P^{n}:\mathbf{V}\longrightarrow\mathbf{X}^{n}
so that

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{n}(0,x)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}^{n}(0)\bm{\psi}_{k}(x),\quad c_{k}^{n}(0)=c_{k,0}^{n}:=(\bm{u}_{0},\bm{\psi}_{k}),\quad k=1,\dots,n.

In (
4.1
), we assume that the approximate cylindrical Wiener process
\big\{\mathrm{W}_{n}(\cdot)\big\}
has the form

\mathrm{W}_{n}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbf{e}_{k}\beta_{k}(t),

and note that (
4.1
) is to be understood
\mathbb{P}-
a.s., and for all
t\in(0,T]
.
Observe that the stochastic system (
4.1
)-(
4.6
) can be written in the matrix form as follows,

(4.7)

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{c}(t)=\mathbf{b}(t)\mathrm{d}t+\mathbf{G}(t)\mathrm{d}\bm{\beta}(t),\quad\mathbf{c}(0)=\mathbf{c}_{n,0},

where

\mathbf{C}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{a^{n}_{lm}}}\right\}_{l,m=1}^{n}
,
\mathbf{b}(t)=\{b_{l}^{n}(t)\}_{l=1}^{n}
,
\mathbf{c}(t)=\{c_{m}^{n}(t)\}_{m=1}^{n}
,

\mathbf{G}(t)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{g^{n}_{lm}(t)}}\right\}_{l,m=1}^{n}
and
\bm{\beta}(t)=\{\beta_{m}^{n}(t)\}_{m=1}^{n}
, with

\displaystyle a_{lm}^{n}

\displaystyle:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bm{\psi}_{l},\bm{\psi}_{m}}}\right)+\kappa\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nabla\bm{\psi}_{l},\nabla\bm{\psi}_{m}}}\right),

\displaystyle b_{l}^{n}(t)

\displaystyle:=\big(\bm{f}(t),\bm{\psi}_{l}\big)+\big(\bm{u}^{n}_{n}(t)\otimes\bm{u}^{n}_{n}(t):\nabla\bm{\psi}_{l}\big)-\nu\big<|\mathbf{D}(\bm{u}_{n}(t))|^{p-2}\mathbf{D}(\bm{u}_{n}(t)):\mathbf{D}(\bm{\psi}_{l})\big>

\displaystyle\quad-\alpha\big(\mathbf{a}(\bm{u}_{n}(t)),\bm{\psi}_{l}\big),

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}(t)

\displaystyle:=(c_{1}^{n}(t),\dots,c_{n}^{n}(t))\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\mathbf{c}_{0}={(c_{1,0}^{n},\dots,c_{n,0}^{n})},

\displaystyle g^{n}_{lm}(t)

\displaystyle:=\big(\Phi(\bm{u}_{n}(t))\mathbf{e}_{l},\bm{\psi}_{m}\big),

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}(t)

\displaystyle:=(\beta_{1}^{n}(t),\dots,\beta_{n}^{n}(t)).

Taking into account that the family
\big\{\bm{\psi}_{k}\big\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is linearly independent in
\mathbf{V}
,

\big(\mathbf{C}\bm{\xi},\bm{\xi}\big)>0
for all
\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, we can write (
4.7
) in the form

(4.8)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathbf{c}(t)=\overline{\mathbf{b}(t)}\mathrm{d}t+\overline{\mathbf{G}(t)}\mathrm{d}\bm{\beta}(t),\quad\mathbf{c}(0)=\mathbf{c}_{n,0},

where
\overline{\mathbf{b}(t)}=\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{b}(t)
and
\overline{\mathbf{G}(t)}=\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{G}(t)
.

If both the coefficients
\mathbf{b}(\cdot)
and
\mathbf{G}(\cdot)
are globally Lipschitz-continuous, one can use the classical existence results for stochastic differential equations. Our case does not fall in that category, so we have to use the monotonicity method and verify the conditions of the vectorial version of Lemma
2.1
as follows:

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{b}(\cdot,\bm{u}_{n})-\mathbf{b}(\cdot,\bm{v}_{n}),\bm{u}_{n}-\bm{v}_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=\big(\bm{u}_{n}\otimes\bm{u}_{n}-\bm{v}_{n}\otimes\bm{v}_{n}:\nabla(\bm{u}_{n}-\bm{v}_{n})\big)

\displaystyle\quad-\nu\langle|\mathbf{D}(\bm{u}_{n})|^{p-2}\mathbf{D}(\bm{u}_{n})-|\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}_{n})|^{p-2}\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}_{n}):\mathbf{D}(\bm{u}_{n}-\bm{v}_{n})\rangle

\displaystyle\quad-\alpha\big(|\bm{u}_{n}|^{q-2}\bm{u}_{n}-|\bm{v}_{n}|^{q-2}\bm{v}_{n},\bm{u}_{n}-\bm{v}_{n}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\big(\bm{u}_{n}\otimes\bm{u}_{n}-\bm{v}_{n}\otimes\bm{v}_{n}:\nabla(\bm{u}_{n}-\bm{v}_{n})\big),

where we have used (
2.4
). If
\|\bm{u}_{n}\|_{2}\leq R
and
\|\bm{v}_{n}\|_{2}\leq R
, there exists a constant
C(R,n)>0
such that

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{b}(t,\bm{u}_{n})-\mathbf{b}(t,\bm{v}_{n}),\bm{u}_{n}-\bm{v}_{n}\rangle\leq C(R,n)\|\bm{u}_{n}-\bm{v}_{n}\|^{2}_{2}.

This, together with the Lipschitz-continuity of
\mathbf{G}
(cf. (
2.11
)), gives us the weak monotonicity property in the sense of Lemma
2.1
-(2). Using the fact
\langle\bm{u}_{n}\otimes\bm{u}_{n}:\nabla\bm{u}_{n}\rangle=0
, we find

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{b}(\cdot,\bm{u}_{n}),\bm{u}_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\nu\langle|\mathbf{D}(\bm{u}_{n})|^{p-2}\mathbf{D}(\bm{u}_{n}):\mathbf{D}(\bm{u}_{n})\rangle-\alpha\big(|\bm{u}_{n}|^{q-2}\bm{u}_{n},\bm{u}_{n}\big)+\big(\bm{f},\bm{u}_{n}\big)

\displaystyle\leq C(1+\|\bm{f}\|_{2}\|\bm{u}_{n}\|_{2})\leq C(1+\|\bm{f}\|_{2})(1+\|\bm{u}_{n}\|^{2}_{2}).

Using the linear growth of
\mathbf{G}
cf. (
2.11
), we find

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{b}(t,\bm{u}_{n}),\bm{u}_{n}\rangle+\|\mathbf{G}\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}}^{2}\leq C(1+\|\bm{f}\|_{2})(1+\|\bm{u}_{n}\|^{2}_{2}).

Since the term
\int_{0}^{T}(1+\|\bm{f}\|_{2})\mathrm{d}t
is finite,
\mathbb{P}-
a.s., this yields the weak coercivity property in the sense of Lemma
2.1
-(3).
By Lemma
2.1
, we obtain the existence of a unique strong solution
\mathbf{C}\mathbf{c}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P};\mathrm{C}([0,T^{*}];\mathbb{R}^{n}))
to the stochastic system (
4.7
), which implies

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T^{*}]}\|\mathbf{C}\mathbf{c}(t)\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}<+\infty.

Our aim is to show that
\sup\limits_{t\in[0,T^{*}]}\|\mathbf{c}(t)\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}<+\infty
, which we obtain as follows:

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T^{*}]}\|\mathbf{c}(t)\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{t\in[0,T^{*}]}\|\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{c}(t)\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\leq\|\mathbf{C}^{-1}\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})}\sup_{t\in[0,T^{*}]}\|\mathbf{C}\mathbf{c}(t)\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}<+\infty,

since
\mathbf{C}^{-1}
is a bounded operator.
Thus, we obtain the existence of a unique strong solution to the stochastic system (
4.6
).

4.2.
Uniform estimates

In this subsection, we establish the uniform energy estimate for the solution of the finite-dimensional approximate system corresponding to the system (
4.2
).

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
p\in(1,\infty)
and assume that (
2.11
) and (
4.3
) are verified.
Assume, in addition, that (
2.9
) and (
2.10
) hold with
\gamma=2
.
Then there exists a positive constant
C,
neither depending on
n
nor on
\alpha
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sup_{t\in(0,T)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\|\bm{u}_{n}(t)\|^{2}_{2}+2\kappa{\|\nabla\bm{u}_{n}(t)\|_{2}^{2}}}}\right\}+4C({p,\mathcal{O}})\nu\int_{0}^{T}{\|\nabla\bm{u}_{n}(t)\|_{p}^{p}}\mathrm{d}t+2\alpha\int_{0}^{T}\|\bm{u}_{n}(t)\|^{q}_{q}\mathrm{d}t}}\right]

(4.9)

\displaystyle\leq C(\kappa)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\bigg(\frac{1}{\eta_{1}}+\kappa\bigg)\int_{\mathbf{V}}\|\bm{z}\|_{\mathbf{V}}^{2}\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{0}(\bm{z})+\int_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathcal{O}_{T})}\|\mathbf{g}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathcal{O}_{T})}^{2}\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{\bm{f}}(\mathbf{g})+C(K)T}}\right\}e^{\frac{C(K)T}{\eta_{1}}},

where
C
is independent of
\alpha
and
\eta_{1}
is the first eigenvalue of the Dirichlet Laplacian.

Proof.

Define a sequence of stopping times
\{\tau_{N}^{n}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
as follows:

(4.10)

\displaystyle\tau_{N}^{n}:=\inf_{t\in[0,T]}\{t:\|\nabla\bm{u}_{n}(t)\|_{2}\geq N\}.

Applying finite-dimensional Itô’s formula to the process
\|(\mathrm{I}-\kappa\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{u}_{n}(\cdot)\|^{2}_{2}
, we obtain,
\mathbb{P}-
a.s.,

\displaystyle\|\bm{u}_{n}(t\wedge\tau_{N}^{n})\|^{2}_{2}+\kappa\|\nabla\bm{u}_{n}(t\wedge\tau_{N}^{n})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\bm{u}_{n}(0)\|^{2}_{2}+\kappa\|\nabla\bm{u}_{n}(0)\|_{2}^{2}-2\nu\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{N}^{n}}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\mathbf{A}(\bm{u}_{n}):\mathbf{D}(\bm{u}_{n})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad-2\alpha\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{N}^{n}}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\mathbf{a}(\bm{u}_{n})\cdot\bm{u}_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}s+2\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{N}^{n}}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\Phi(\bm{u}_{n})\mathrm{d}\mathrm{W}_{n}(s)\cdot\bm{u}_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{N}^{n}}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\mathrm{d}\bigg\langle\int_{0}^{\cdot}\Phi(\bm{u}_{n})\mathrm{d}\mathrm{W}_{n}\bigg\rangle_{s}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\frac{1}{\eta_{1}}+\kappa\bigg)\|\nabla\bm{u}_{n}(0)\|_{2}^{2}-2\nu\int_{0}^{t}\|\mathbf{D}(\bm{u}_{n}(s))\|_{p}^{p}\mathrm{d}s-2\alpha\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{N}^{n}}\|\bm{u}_{n}(s)\|^{q}_{q}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+2\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{N}^{n}}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\bm{f}\cdot\bm{u}_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}s+2\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{N}^{n}}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\Phi(\bm{u}_{n})\mathrm{d}\mathrm{W}_{n}(s)\cdot\bm{u}_{n}\mathrm{d}\bm{x}

(4.11)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{N}^{n}}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\mathrm{d}\bigg\langle\int_{0}^{\cdot}\Phi(\bm{u}_{n})\mathrm{d}\mathrm{W}_{n}\bigg\rangle_{s}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x},

where we have used Poincaré’s inequality. Taking expectation on both sides and then using Korn’s inequality (cf.
[
38
]
or
[
3
, Lemma 2.1]
), we find

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\|\bm{u}_{n}(t\wedge\tau_{N}^{n})\|^{2}_{2}+\kappa\|\nabla\bm{u}_{n}(t\wedge\tau_{N}^{n})\|_{2}^{2}+2C({p,\mathcal{O}})\nu\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{N}^{n}}\|\nabla\bm{u}_{n}(s)\|_{p}^{p}\mathrm{d}s\bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad+2\alpha\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{N}^{n}}\|\bm{u}_{n}(s)\|^{q}_{q}\mathrm{d}s\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(\frac{1}{\eta_{1}}+\kappa\bigg)\|\nabla\bm{u}_{n}(0)\|_{2}^{2}+2\underbrace{\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{N}^{n}}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\bm{f}\cdot\bm{u}_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}s}_{:=I_{1}(t\wedge\tau_{N}^{n})}+\underbrace{\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{N}^{n}}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\mathrm{d}\bigg\langle\int_{0}^{\cdot}\Phi(\bm{u}_{n})\mathrm{d}\mathrm{W}_{n}\bigg\rangle_{s}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}_{:=I_{2}(t\wedge\tau_{N}^{n})}

\displaystyle\quad+2\underbrace{\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{N}^{n}}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\Phi(\bm{u}_{n})\mathrm{d}\mathrm{W}_{n}(s)\cdot\bm{u}_{n}\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}_{:=I_{3}(t\wedge\tau_{N}^{n})}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\frac{1}{\eta_{1}}+\kappa\bigg)\mathbb{E}\big[\|\nabla\bm{u}_{n}(0)\|_{2}^{2}\big]+2\mathbb{E}\big[I_{1}(t\wedge\tau_{N}^{n})\big]+\mathbb{E}\big[I_{2}(t\wedge\tau_{N}^{n})\big]+2\mathbb{E}\big[I_{3}(t\wedge\tau_{N}^{n})\big].

We estimate
\mathbb{E}\big[|I_{1}(t\wedge\tau_{N}^{n})|\big]
using Young’s and Poincaré’s inequalities as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[|I_{1}(t\wedge\tau_{N}^{n})|\big]

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{N}^{n}}\|\bm{u}_{n}(s)\|^{2}_{2}\mathrm{d}s\bigg]+C(\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{N}^{n}}\|\bm{f}(s)\|^{2}_{2}\mathrm{d}s\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\eta_{1}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{N}^{n}}{\|\nabla\bm{u}_{n}(s)\|_{2}^{2}}\mathrm{d}s\bigg]+C(\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{N}^{n}}\|\bm{f}(s)\|^{2}_{2}\mathrm{d}s\bigg].

We know that
\mathbb{E}\big[I_{3}(t\wedge\tau_{N}^{n})\big]=0
, since the term

is a martingale with zero expectation.
Now, we consider the term
\mathbb{E}\big[I_{2}(t\wedge\tau_{N}^{n})\big]
and estimate it with the help of Itô’s isometry, (
2.11
) and Poincaré’s inequality as,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[I_{2}(t\wedge\tau_{N}^{n})\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{N}^{n}}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\big|\Phi(\bm{u}_{n})\mathbf{e}_{k}\big|^{2}\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}s\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{N}^{n}}\int_{\mathcal{O}}|\phi_{k}(\bm{u}_{n})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}s\bigg]\leq C(K)\mathbb{E}\bigg[T+{\frac{1}{\eta_{1}}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{N}^{n}}\|\nabla\bm{u}_{n}(s)\|^{2}_{2}}\mathrm{d}s\bigg].

Combining the above estimates and using it in (

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}(\overline{\bm{u}}_{n})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\mathbf{D}(\overline{\bm{u}}),\ \ \overline{\mathbb{P}}\otimes\lambda^{d+1}-\text{a.e.}

The above convergence justifies the limit procedure in the energy estimate, that is,
\overline{\mathbf{S}}=\mathbf{A}(\overline{\bm{u}})
, which completes the proof of Claim
5
(for more details, see
[
63
, Appendix A]
).
∎

This completes the proof of Theorem
2.6
.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

Since
\overline{\bm{u}}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,T;\mathbf{V})\cap\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;{\mathrm{W}_{0}^{1,p}(\mathcal{O})^{d}})
is regular, we note that in order to obtain the Claim
5
, neither Lipschitz truncation (
[
25
]
) nor
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
-truncation (
[
14
,
63
]
) are required.

4.8.
Pathwise uniqueness of solution

We rewrite the equation (
1.1
) as follows:

(4.116)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}(\mathrm{I}-\kappa\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{u}

\displaystyle=(\mathrm{I}-\kappa\Delta)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\big\{\operatorname{div}\big(\nu\mathbf{A}(\bm{u})-(\bm{u}\otimes\bm{u})-\pi\mathrm{I}-\mathbf{F}\big)\big\}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+(\mathrm{I}-\kappa\Delta)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Phi(\bm{u})\mathrm{d}\mathrm{W}(t),

where
\mathbf{F}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;{\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(\mathcal{O})^{d\times d}})
such that
\operatorname{div}\mathbf{F}=-\bm{f}
.

The existence of a solution
\bm{u}
is already established in Theorem
2.6
and
\bm{u}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,T;\mathbf{V})\cap\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;{\mathrm{W}_{0}^{1,p}(\mathcal{O})^{d}})
,
\mathbb{P}-
a.s., for
p>\frac{2d}{d+2}
. Let us set
\bm{v}(\cdot):=(\mathrm{I}-\kappa\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{u}(\cdot)
. In order to establish the energy equality (Itô’s formula), in view of
[
32
, Theorem 2.1, Eqn. (1.2)]
, we only need to show that

\displaystyle\bm{v}^{*}=(\mathrm{I}-\kappa\Delta)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\big\{\operatorname{div}\big(\nu\mathbf{A}(\bm{u})-(\bm{u}\otimes\bm{u})-\pi\mathrm{I}-\mathbf{F}\big)\big\}\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(0,T;{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{O})^{d}}).

In order to verify
\bm{v}^{*}\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(0,T;{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{O})^{d}})
, we consider

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\|\bm{v}^{*}(s)\|_{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}\|(\mathrm{I}-\kappa\Delta)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\operatorname{div}\mathbf{F}(s)\|_{2}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\|(\mathrm{I}-\kappa\Delta)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\operatorname{div}\pi\mathrm{I}\|_{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\nu\int_{0}^{t}\|(\mathrm{I}-\kappa\Delta)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\operatorname{div}\mathbf{A}(\bm{u}(s))\|_{2}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\|(\mathrm{I}-\kappa\Delta)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\operatorname{div}(\bm{u}(s)\otimes\bm{u}(s))\|_{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=:\sum_{i=1}^{4}I_{i}.

We consider the term
I_{1}
and estimate it using Hölder’s inequality, to find

\displaystyle I_{1}\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\|\mathbf{F}(s)\|_{2}\mathrm{d}s\leq C\|\mathbf{F}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;{\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(\mathcal{O})^{d\times d}})}<\infty.

Similarly, we can estimate the term
I_{2}
as

\displaystyle I_{2}\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\|\pi(s)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\mathrm{d}s\leq C\|\pi\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{O}))}<\infty.

Now, we consider the term
I_{3}
and estimate it in the following way:

\displaystyle I_{3}\leq C\nu\int_{0}^{t}\||\mathrm{D}(\bm{u}(s))|^{p-2}\mathrm{D}(\bm{u}(s))\|_{2}\mathrm{d}s\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla\bm{u}(s)\|_{2(p-1)}^{p-1}\mathrm{d}s\leq C\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\nabla\bm{u}(t)\|^{p-1}_{2}<\infty,

for
p\leq 2
.

We estimate the final term
I_{4}
, with the help of the embedding
\mathrm{W}^{1,2}_{0}(\mathcal{O})^{d}\hookrightarrow\mathrm{L}^{4}(\mathcal{O})^{d}
for
2\leq d\leq 4
, and Hölder’s inequality as

\displaystyle I_{4}\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\|\bm{u}(s)\otimes\bm{u}(s)\|_{2}\mathrm{d}s\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\|\bm{u}(s)\|^{2}_{4}\mathrm{d}s\leq C\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\nabla\bm{u}(s)\|^{2}_{2}<\infty.

Combining the estimates of
I_{1}-I_{4}
, we deduce that
\bm{v}^{*}\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(0,T;{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{O})^{d}})
and hence we can use Itô’s formula (see
[
32
, Theorem 2.1]
) to obtain the following energy equality,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.,

\displaystyle\|\bm{u}(t)\|^{2}_{2}+{\kappa\|\nabla\bm{u}(t)\|_{2}^{2}}+2\nu\int_{0}^{t}\|\mathrm{D}(\bm{u}(s))\|^{p}_{p}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\|\bm{u}_{0}\|^{2}_{2}+{\kappa\|\nabla\bm{u}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}}+2\int_{0}^{t}\big(\bm{f}(s),\bm{u}(s)\big)\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\|\Phi(\bm{u}(s))\|^{2}_{2}\mathrm{d}s

(4.117)

\displaystyle\quad+2\int_{0}^{t}\big(\Phi(\bm{u}(s))\mathrm{d}\mathrm{W}(s),\bm{u}(s)\big),

for all
t\in[0,T]
, where
p\in\big(\frac{2d}{d+2},2\big]
.

For
p\geq 2
, we consider the Gelfand triplet
{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathcal{O})^{d}}\subset\mathbf{H}\subset{\mathrm{L}^{p^{\prime}}(\mathcal{O})^{d}}
. In view of
[
32
, Theorem 2.1, Eqn. (1.2)]
, we only need to show the following:

(4.118)

\displaystyle\nu\int_{0}^{t}\|(\mathrm{I}-\kappa\Delta)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\operatorname{div}\mathbf{A}(\bm{u}(s))\|^{p^{\prime}}_{p^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}s<\infty,

and the remaining terms can be estimated with the help of the embedding
\mathrm{L}^{p^{\prime}}(0,T;{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{O})^{d}})\subset\mathrm{L}^{p^{\prime}}(0,T;{\mathrm{L}^{p^{\prime}}(\mathcal{O})^{d}})
, for
p\geq 2
. Let us verify
(
4.118
)
in the following way:

\displaystyle\nu\int_{0}^{t}\|(\mathrm{I}-\kappa\Delta)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\operatorname{div}\mathbf{A}(\bm{u}(s))\|^{p^{\prime}}_{p^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}s\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\|\mathbf{A}(\bm{u}(s))\|^{p^{\prime}}_{p^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}s\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla\bm{u}(s)\|^{p}_{p}\mathrm{d}s<\infty.

Combining the estimates (
4.118
),
I_{1},I_{2}
and
I_{4}
imply the required energy equality (
4.8
) for the case
p\in[2,\infty)
.

Now, we discuss the pathwise uniqueness of the solution to the system (
1.1
)-(
1.4
).

Theorem 4.5

(Uniqueness)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2.6
, solution of the system (
1.1
)-(
1.4
) is
pathwise unique
.

Proof.

Let
\bm{u}_{1}(\cdot),\bm{u}_{2}(\cdot)
be any two solutions of the system (
4.116
) with the initial data
\bm{u}_{0}^{1}
and
\bm{u}_{0}^{2}
, respectively. For
M>0
, let us define

\displaystyle\tau_{M}^{1}=\inf_{t\in[0,T]}\big\{t:\ {\|\nabla\bm{u}_{1}(t)\|_{2}}\geq M\big\},\ \text{ and }\ \tau_{M}^{2}=\inf_{t\in[0,T]}\big\{t:\ {\|\nabla\bm{u}_{2}(t)\|_{2}}\geq M\big\}.

Set
\tau_{M}:=\tau_{M}^{1}\wedge\tau_{M}^{2}
. Let us define
\bm{w}(\cdot):=\bm{u}_{1}(\cdot)-\bm{u}_{2}(\cdot)
and
\tilde{\Phi}(\cdot):=\Phi(\bm{u}_{1}(\cdot))-\Phi(\bm{u}_{2}(\cdot))
. Then,
\bm{w}(\cdot)
satisfies the following system:

(4.119)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathrm{d}(\mathrm{I}-\kappa\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{w}&=(\mathrm{I}-\kappa\Delta)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\operatorname{div}\big(\mathbf{A}(\bm{u}_{1})-\mathbf{A}(\bm{u}_{2})\big)-\operatorname{div}\big((\bm{u}_{1}\otimes\bm{u}_{1})-(\bm{u}_{2}\otimes\bm{u}_{2})\big)}}\right.\\
&\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.-\operatorname{div}\big((\pi_{1}-\pi_{2})\mathrm{I}\big)}}\right]\mathrm{d}t+(\mathrm{I}-\kappa\Delta)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{\Phi}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{W},\\
\bm{w}(0)&=\bm{u}_{0}^{1}-\bm{u}_{0}^{2},\end{aligned}}}\right.

for all
t\in[0,T]
in
\mathbf{L}^{p^{\prime}}
.

Let us define

\displaystyle\varphi(t):=\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-C_{1}\int_{0}^{t}{\|\nabla\bm{u}_{2}(s)\|_{2}}\mathrm{d}s}}\right),

where
C_{1}
is the constant appearing in (
4.121
) below. Applying infinite-dimensional Itô’s formula (see
[
32
, Theorem 2.1]
) to the process
\varphi(\cdot)\|(\mathrm{I}-\kappa\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{w}(\cdot)\|^{2}_{2}
, we find
\mathbb{P}-
a.s.,

\displaystyle\varphi(t\wedge\tau_{M})\big\{\|\bm{w}(t\wedge\tau_{M})\|^{2}_{2}+{\kappa\|\nabla\bm{w}(t\wedge\tau_{M})\|_{2}^{2}}\big\}

\displaystyle=\|\bm{w}(0)\|^{2}_{2}+{\kappa\|\nabla\bm{w}(0)\|_{2}^{2}}-2\nu\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{M}}\varphi(s)\big\langle\mathbf{A}(\bm{u}_{1}(s))-\mathbf{A}(\bm{u}_{2}(s)),\mathbf{D}(\bm{w})\big\rangle\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+2\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{M}}\varphi(s)\bigg(\big\langle\operatorname{div}\big\{(\bm{u}_{1}(s)\otimes\bm{u}_{1}(s))-(\bm{u}_{2}(s)\otimes\bm{u}_{2}(s))\big\},\bm{w}(s)\big\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad-C_{1}{\|\nabla\bm{u}_{2}(s)\|_{2}}{\|\bm{w}(s)\|_{2}^{2}}\bigg)\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{M}}\varphi(s)\|\tilde{\Phi}(s)\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s

(4.120)

\displaystyle\quad+2\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{M}}\varphi(s)\big(\tilde{\Phi}(s)\mathrm{d}\mathrm{W}(s),\bm{w}(s)\big).

Using the following facts:

(4.121)

\displaystyle\big|\big\langle\operatorname{div}\big\{(\bm{u}_{1}\otimes\bm{u}_{1})-(\bm{u}_{2}\otimes\bm{u}_{2})\big\},\bm{w}\big\rangle\big|\leq{\|\nabla\bm{u}_{2}\|_{2}\|\bm{w}\|_{4}^{2}\leq C_{1}\|\nabla\bm{u}_{2}\|_{2}\|\nabla\bm{w}\|_{2}^{2}},

for
2\leq d\leq 4
,
and

\displaystyle\big\langle\mathbf{A}(\bm{u}_{1})-\mathbf{A}(\bm{u}_{2}),\mathbf{D}(\bm{w})\big\rangle\geq 0,

and
\operatorname{div}\bm{w}=0,

we arrive at
\mathbb{P}-
a.s.,

\displaystyle\varphi(t\wedge\tau_{M})\big\{\|\bm{w}(t\wedge\tau_{M})\|^{2}_{2}+{\kappa\|\nabla\bm{w}(t\wedge\tau_{M})\|_{2}^{2}}\big\}

\displaystyle\leq\|\bm{w}(0)\|^{2}_{2}+{\kappa\|\nabla\bm{w}(0)\|_{2}^{2}}+\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{M}}\varphi(s)\|\tilde{\Phi}(s)\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+2\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{M}}\varphi(s)\big(\tilde{\Phi}(s)\mathrm{d}\mathrm{W}(s),\bm{w}(s)\big).

Taking expectation, using Hypothesis
2.11
, Poincaré’s inequality and the fact that the final term is a martingale, we deduce

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\varphi(t\wedge\tau_{M})\big\{\|\bm{w}(t\wedge\tau_{M})\|^{2}_{2}+{\kappa\|\nabla\bm{w}(t\wedge\tau_{M})\|_{2}^{2}}\big\}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\frac{1}{\eta_{1}}+\kappa\bigg){\|\nabla\bm{w}(0)\|_{2}^{2}}+\frac{C(K)}{\eta_{1}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{M}}\varphi(s){\|\nabla\bm{w}(s)\|_{2}^{2}}\mathrm{d}s\bigg].

An application of Gronwall’s inequality yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\varphi(t\wedge\tau_{M}){\|\nabla\bm{w}(t\wedge\tau_{M})\|_{2}^{2}}\bigg]\leq C(\eta_{1},\kappa,K){\|\nabla\bm{w}(0)\|_{2}^{2}},

where we have used that fact that
\int_{0}^{t}{\|\nabla\bm{u}_{2}(s)\|_{2}}\mathrm{d}s<\infty,\ \mathbb{P}-
a.s.

Thus the initial data
\bm{u}_{0}^{1}=\bm{u}_{0}^{2}=\bm{u}_{0}
leads to

\bm{w}(t\wedge\tau_{M})=0,\ \mathbb{P}-
a.s. But using the fact that
\tau_{M}\to T
,
\mathbb{P}-
a.s., implies
\bm{w}(t)=0
and hence
\bm{u}_{1}(t)=\bm{u}_{2}(t)
,
\mathbb{P}-
a.s., for all
t\in[0,T]
, which completes the proof.∎

Proof of Theorem
2.7
.

We have already established the existence of a probabilistically weak solution and pathwise uniqueness in Theorems
2.6
and
4.5
, respectively. Therefore, combining Theorems
2.6
,
4.5
and an application of Yamada-Watanabe theorem (see
[
60
, Theorem 2.1]
) leads to the proof of this theorem.
∎
