An Empirical Study of Using ChatGPT for Fact Verification Task
Mohna Chakraborty, Adithya Kulkarni, Qi Li

Abstract
ChatGPT has recently emerged as a powerful tool for performing diverse NLP tasks. However, ChatGPT has been criticized for generating nonfactual responses, raising concerns about its usability for sensitive tasks like fact verification. This study investigates three key research questions: (1) Can ChatGPT be used for fact verification tasks? (2) What are different prompts performance using ChatGPT for fact verification tasks? (3) For the best-performing prompt, what common mistakes does ChatGPT make? Specifically, this study focuses on conducting a comprehensive and systematic analysis by designing and comparing the performance of three different prompts for fact verification tasks on the benchmark FEVER dataset using ChatGPT.



1
Introduction

With recent advancements in scalable attention-based architectures
Vaswani et al. 2017
, language models encompass phenomenal generalization and exhibit unprecedented performance at generating novel human-like content. One such large language model based on GPT-3.5-TURBO or GPT-4 architecture is ChatGPT
OpenAI 2023
, capable of generating text responses nearly indistinguishable from those written by humans. The generated text reads remarkably convincing, but several factors should be considered while evaluating the model’s response. Specifically, we need to check whether the response is opinionated, contains toxic aspects, or is accurate. Recent studies
Ray 2023
;
Zhuo et al. 2023
have evaluated and analyzed the ethical aspects of ChatGPT; however, there has yet to be a comprehensive study that investigates the accuracy of ChatGPT for the fact verification task.

Fact verification requires the model to verify a given textual claim based on data from reliable sources. ChatGPT is known to generate convincing responses; however, the response may not be accurate. Due to the sensitive nature of the fact verification task, it is quintessential to empirically validate the accuracy of the response, including the common mistakes made by ChatGPT. In this study, we conduct a systematic empirical analysis to validate the usability of ChatGPT for the fact verification task.

Another observation made by many users is that the performance of ChatGPT is sensitive to the choice of prompts
Li et al. 2023b
. A well-designed prompt can significantly improve the quality and relevance of ChatGPT’s output, while a poorly crafted one may lead to incorrect responses. Prompt designing of ChatGPT for the fact verification task requires understanding the task objectives, the output format, and the strengths and limitations of ChatGPT. To design the prompt, we can directly query ChatGPT for the prompt or design it manually. In this study, we design and compare the performance of ChatGPT on the fact verification task with three different prompts. Specifically, we aim to understand how well the prompt suggested by ChatGPT works in practice and if there is a better prompt that can achieve better performance.

In summary, this study investigates three key research questions:
RQ1:
Can ChatGPT be used for fact verification tasks?
RQ2:
What are the different prompts performance using ChatGPT for fact verification tasks?
RQ3:
For the best-performing prompt, what common mistakes does ChatGPT make?

We conduct experiments on the development and test splits of the non-binary FEVER 1.0 benchmark dataset to answer the research questions. For the experiments, we design three non-binary prompts

Related Works

Prompts are widely used to employ LLMs for various NLP tasks. In this section, we discuss the related studies that explored prompt design for LLMs like ChatGPT.

Prompts are designed either manually
Schick and Schütze 2021
or automatically
Shin et al. 2020
;
Gao et al. 2021
. Several prompt engineering methods are proposed to guide the model to generate better intermediate reasoning explanations to solve a complex task. For instance,
Cai et al. 2023
proposed the Chain-of-Thought (CoT) method to improve the reasoning abilities of LLMs by decomposing complicated problems into multiple intermediate steps and solving them individually, and using their results to solve the final problem.

Specifically for ChatGPT,
Han et al. 2021
proposed strategies for designing prompts for classification tasks.
Sobania et al. 2023
;
Xia and Zhang 2023
examined prompts for software bug fixing.
Wang et al. 2023
utilized ChatGPT to generate effective boolean prompts for systematic review literature search. In several domains, various studies examined the positive usage of ChatGPT results. Specifically,
Sallam 2023
;
Gilson et al. 2023
observed surprisingly good performance of ChatGPT in medical licensing exams.
Choi et al. 2023
and
Frieder et al. 2023
explored using ChatGPT in Law School and for mathematical reasoning abilities, respectively.

More related to our work, several studies also consider the security risks and ethics of ChatGPT. To test for severe privacy concerns,
Li et al. 2023a
developed a multi-step jailbreaking attack against ChatGPT to steal confidential information. Jailbreak prompts are used to remove the restrictions on ChatGPT and facilitate its usage beyond its intended capacity.
Liu et al. 2023

Huang et al. 2023
did an empirical evaluation to study the performance of ChatGPT in classifying and generating explanations for implicit hate speech.

Prompts for Fact Verification Task

3.1
FEVER Challenge

The Fact Extraction and VERification (FEVER) Shared Task
Thorne et al. 2018
conducted in 2018 challenged participants to classify if the human-written factoid claims can be SUPPORTED or REFUTED using the evidence retrieved from Wikipedia June 2017 dump
2
2

2

https://fever.ai/dataset/fever.html
. If sufficient evidence is absent in Wikipedia, such claims are labeled Not Enough Information (NEI).

3.2
Prompt Designing

We prompted ChatGPT with the following three prompts:

Prompt-1:
The claim is "<claim>". Select whether the claim is (a.) – Support, (b.) – Refute, (c.) – NEI?

Prompt-2:
The claim is "<claim>". Provide evidence for the claim, and based on the evidence, select whether the claim is (a.) - Support, (b.) - Refute, (c.) - NEI?

Prompt-3:
The claim is "<claim>". Can you generate 5 prompts for this claim and the answers for each of the prompts? For each of the answers, select whether that prompt is (a.) - Support, (b.) – Refute, (c.) – NEI? Based on the prompts and the answers provided, select finally whether the claim is (a.) - Support, (b.) – Refute, (c.) – NEI?

Prompt-1
asks ChatGPT to directly provide the label for the claim.
Prompt-2
asks ChatGPT to provide evidence for the claim along with the label.
Prompt-2
is the suggested prompt by ChatGPT for the fact verification task. Comparing performances of ChatGPT on
Prompt-2
and
Prompt-1
provides insights into the importance of evidence for the claim in ChatGPT’s verdict for the claim.

Prompt-3
asks ChatGPT to generate more prompts related to the claim and answers them. Based on the answers to the generated prompts, ChatGPT is asked to provide the label for the claim. ChatGPT is known to change its response based on the conversation, and in most scenarios, the change is positive. We utilize this observation to design
Prompt-3
. Empirically, we test if asking ChatGPT to generate multiple prompts improves its performance for the fact verification tasks. For each prompt, we constrain the output to be the labels of the task.

4.3
Baseline Methods

For the evaluation of
setting-2
, we compare the performance of ChatGPT on the three non-binary prompts designed in Section
3
with the binary prompt designed by HOES
Hoes et al. 2023
. The binary prompt designed by HOES is as follows:

HOES-prompt:
Can you fact-check a claim "<claim>" for me? When fact checking use clear language such as ‘true’ and ‘false’, and avoid negations.

The binary prompt has an advantage over the non-binary prompt since ChatGPT should only choose between two labels of ‘true’ (Support) and ‘false’ (Refute), whereas the non-binary prompt requires ChatGPT to choose among the three labels of Support, Refute, and NEI.

4.6
Results: RQ3

To investigate the common mistakes made by ChatGPT for fact verification tasks, we examined the prediction of ChatGPT on
prompt-3
. For the experiment, we randomly sampled
200
claims for which ChatGPT made mistakes. We categorized these claims into eight categories depending on the type of mistake. Each claim is placed into one of the eight categories; if the claim belongs to more than one category, it is placed into the most prominent category. Among the error categories, the most prominent is
Miscellaneous
, where ChatGPT correctly answers and predicts the labels for each of the five prompts it generates but makes a mistake in the overall prediction (
48\%
). We suspect the primary reason for the error is that ChatGPT could not correctly reason the importance of prompt outputs to the claim. The second most prominent error category is
Misunderstanding the claim
, where ChatGPT failed to correctly understand the context of the claim resulting in an incorrect response (
21\%
). The third most prominent error category is
Factual errors
, where ChatGPT made factual errors in the answers provided for the generated prompts resulting in incorrect judgment (
16\%
). The fourth most prominent error category is
Irrelevant prompts
, where some prompts generated by ChatGPT are irrelevant to the claim, leading to a mistake in the output label (
9\%
). The remaining four categories that account for less than
8\%
of the errors are
Additional information
,
Missing knowledge
,
Incorrect answers
, and
Time sensitive claim
.
Incorrect answers
error category contains claims where ChatGPT made non-factual mistakes in the answers for the generated prompts. The errors in the categories of
Additional information
,
Missing knowledge
, and
Time sensitive claim
are not true errors and are caused due to the data available to ChatGPT. The evidence for the claims in the FEVER 1.0 dataset are obtained from Wikipedia’s June 2017 dump, whereas ChatGPT may or may not have access to Wikipedia or may have access to additional websites resulting in incorrect responses for these categories. Addressing the evaluation to remove these false errors will not increase ChatGPT performance much. We provide examples for each error category in Appendix
A.1
.

Figure 1:
Categories of errors by ChatGPT. The error percentage is rounded off to the nearest integer.

From the comprehensive error analysis, we find that the primary cause for erroneous responses by ChatGPT lies in incorrect logical reasoning and contextual misunderstanding of the claim. ChatGPT failed to properly reason the generated prompt answers to the claim and failed to better understand the context of the claim and instead focused on the tokens used in the claim.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we empirically investigate three key research questions related to the usability of ChatGPT for the fact verification task. We find that ChatGPT can perform well for fact verification tasks on datasets such as FEVER with a correct prompt. From the comprehensive error analysis, we find that near to
70\%
of the mistakes by ChatGPT are due to incorrect logical reasoning and contextual misunderstanding of the claim.

6
Limitations

In this work, we investigate the usability of ChatGPT for fact-verification tasks on FEVER 1.0 shared task on a specific version
gpt-3.5-turbo (version March 23, 2023)
of the ChatGPT model, and our conclusions cannot be extended to other versions of the model. Furthermore, our conclusions are based on empirical results on FEVER 1.0 shared task and cannot be extended to other fact verification datasets. The ground truth labels of FEVER 1.0 shared task datasets are based on Wikipedia’s June 2017 dump, and in our evaluation, we observed that this could lead to some errors. Therefore, our evaluation metric has some limitations.
