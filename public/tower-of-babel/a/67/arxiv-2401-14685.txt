Data-dependent density estimation for the Fokker-Planck equation in higher dimensions
Max Jensen, Fabian Merle, Andreas Prohl

Abstract
We present a new strategy to approximate the global solution of the Fokker-Planck equation efficiently in higher dimension and show its convergence. The main ingredients are the Euler scheme to solve the associated stochastic differential equation and a histogram method for tree-structured density estimation on a data-dependent partitioning of the state space ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} . Keywords: Fokker-Planck equation, numerical approximation, tree-structured density estimation, data-dependent partitioning

Introduction

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
, fix
T>0
, and choose a probability density function (pdf, for short)
p_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
with
\lVert p_{0}\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{1}}=1
. In this work, we propose and study a new method that uses probabilistic and statistical tools to approximate the
global
solution
\big\{p(t,\cdot)\,;\,t\in[0,T]\big\}
of the
Fokker-Planck equation

\begin{split}\partial_{t}p(t,\mathbf{x})&=\mathcal{L}^{\ast}p(t,\mathbf{x})\quad\text{for all }\;\,(t,{\bf x})\in(0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
p(0,\mathbf{x})&=p_{0}(\mathbf{x})\quad\text{for all}\;\,\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,,\end{split}

(1.1)

in higher dimensions
d
, where

\mathcal{L}^{\ast}p(t,\mathbf{x})=-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{x_{i}}\left(b_{i}(\mathbf{x})\cdot p(t,\mathbf{x})\right)+\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{i,j,k=1}^{d}\partial_{x_{i}}\partial_{x_{j}}\left(\sigma_{ik}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\sigma_{jk}(\mathbf{x})\cdot p(t,\mathbf{x})\right)\,

(1.2)

is a second-order differential operator, in general, with non-constant coefficients. Under the assumptions stated in Subsection
2.1
, there exists a unique classical solution
p:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
of (
1.1
) which coincides with the pdf of the related stochastic differential equation (SDE) process
\mathbf{X}\equiv\big\{\mathbf{X}_{t}\,;\,t\in[0,T]\big\}
, which is given by

\begin{split}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{t}&=\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{X}_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\mathbf{X}_{t})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{t}\qquad\text{for all }t\in(0,T]\\
\mathbf{X}_{0}&=\mathbf{X}^{0}\,;\end{split}

(1.3)

see e.g.
[
22
, Prop. 3.3]
and
[
14
, Chap. 2, Thm. 1.1]
. Here,
\mathbf{b}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}:\mathbf{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
are smooth bounded functions with entries
b_{i}(\cdot)
resp.
\sigma_{ij}(\cdot)
, and
\mathbf{W}\equiv\{\mathbf{W}_{t}\,;\,t\in[0,T]\}
is a
\mathbb{R}^{d}-
valued Wiener process on a filtered probability space
\bigl(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P}\bigr)
, and
\mathbf{X}^{0}
is random and drawn according to
p_{0}
.

To numerically solve (
1.1
) in higher dimensions
d\gg 1
, different
deterministic
and
probabilistic
methods have been developed in the last two decades and are the subject of active research. For example, well-known deterministic methods to simulate (
1.1
) in dimensions
d\gg 1
, e.g. include ‘sparse grid’ methods
[
9
]
, and ‘low-rank approximation’ methods
[
1
,
2
]
, but their efficient use (and theory) is restricted to ‘tensor-sparsity respecting data’
\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
and
p_{0}
in (
1.1
); see the recent survey in
[
21
, Sec. 2]
. A different numerical strategy is to use the probabilistic reformulation (
1.3
) as a starting point for discretisation and statistical sampling via Monte-Carlo simulations; see e.g.
[
16
]
; while the analysis there is exempted from the assumption on ‘tensor-sparsity respecting data’, its straightforward application only yields approximations of values for
p
from (
1.1
) at selected space-time points. This work aims at constructing numerical methods for (
1.1
) and
d\gg 1
that are based on the probabilistic reinterpretation (
1.3
) and approximate the ‘entire’ function
p(T,\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to(0,\infty)
.

From a practical viewpoint, the new Algorithm
1.1
below in this work stands out due to its simple implementability, its intuitive interpretability, and efficiency; from a theoretical viewpoint, its convergence theory covers
non-constant

\mathcal{L}^{\ast}\equiv\mathcal{L}^{\ast}(\mathbf{x})
in higher dimensions
d\gg 1
, which allows broader potential applicability, including the simulation of polymer models, for example, see e.g.
[
4
,
5
]
. Conceptually, its construction combines

a)

the (explicit) Euler scheme for (
1.3
) on a mesh
\{t_{j}\}_{j=0}^{J}\subset[0,T]
of fixed step size
\tau=\frac{T}{J}
,
J\in\mathbb{N}
, which determines the
\mathbb{R}^{d}-
valued random variable
\mathbf{Y}^{j+1}
from

\begin{split}\mathbf{Y}^{j+1}&=\mathbf{Y}^{j}+\tau\cdot\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{Y}^{j})+\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\mathbf{Y}^{j})(\mathbf{W}_{t_{j+1}}-\mathbf{W}_{t_{j}})\qquad(j=0,...,J-1)\\
\mathbf{Y}^{0}&=\mathbf{X}^{0}\end{split}

(1.4)

to approximate
\mathbf{X}_{t_{j+1}}
from (
1.3
) at time
t_{j+1}=t_{j}+\tau
. The density
p^{J}
of the induced measure
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{Y}^{J}}
approximates
p(T,\cdot)
from (
1.1
); see Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
.

b)

a
data-dependent
partitioning
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
based on an
\mathtt{M}
-sample
\mathbf{D}^{J}_{\mathtt{M}}:=\{\mathbf{Y}^{J,m}\}_{m=1}^{\mathtt{M}}
from (
1.4
), and
statistically equivalent cells

[
17
, Ch. 13]
on which the histogram-based estimator
\widehat{\mathtt{p}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}
for the pdf
p^{J}
from (
1.4
) at time
t_{J}=T
is constructed; see Subsection
3.1
.

Such an estimator
\widehat{\mathtt{p}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}
is advantageous for several reasons:

(a)

suitability to approximate solutions of (
1.1
) for
d\gg 1
, where uniform partitioning strategies tend to exhaust even extensive computational resources in the pre-asymptotic range and therefore lose competitivity.

(b)

automatic construction of
adaptive
meshes, where locally appearing finer cells
\mathbf{R}_{r}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}
adjust to local features of the solution
p^{J}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
. The proposed ‘
data-dependent
’ estimators

\widehat{\mathtt{p}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
below aim to meet two goals at the same time with the help of a single
\mathtt{M}-
sample

\mathbf{D}^{J}_{\mathtt{M}}
: to generate an
adaptive
mesh
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}
, and to compute an accurate approximation
\widehat{\mathtt{p}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}
of
p^{J}
on that mesh. In approximation theory, generating an underlying partition which depends on the solution itself is referred to as ‘nonlinear approximation’
[
11
, Section 3.2]
;
well-known deterministic methods in this direction are ‘adaptive finite elements’ that are applicable in
small dimensions
1\leq d\leq 4
, where criteria trigger a repeated automatic local refinement/coarsening of elements with the help of computed local residuals that leads to a final adapted mesh.

In the vein of (b), Algorithm
1.1
below may be seen as a new probabilistic method for the parabolic problem (
1.1
), which is adaptive in space and whose convergence for general non-constant operators
\mathcal{L}^{\ast}\equiv\mathcal{L}^{\ast}(\mathbf{x})
follows from Theorem
3.5
in Subsection
3.2
. Moreover, the Algorithm
1.1
is applicable in higher dimensions
d\gg 1
– a property that adaptive finite element methods do not possess.

We now outline the basic steps of our algorithm to solve (
1.1
) with the help of the tools that were motivated in (a) and (b) above.

Algorithm 1.1
.

Let
J\in\mathbb{N}
. Fix an equidistant mesh
\{t_{j}\}_{j=0}^{J}\subset[0,T]
of mesh-size
\tau=\frac{T}{J}
.

1)

(Initialization)
For
\mathtt{M}\in\mathbb{N}
, generate an
i.i.d.

\mathtt{M}-
sample
\mathbf{D}_{\mathtt{M}}^{0}:=\{\mathbf{Y}^{0,m}\}_{m=1}^{\mathtt{M}}
through sampling via
p_{0}
.

2)

(Discretization & Sampling)
Generate a
\mathtt{M}-
sample
\mathbf{D}_{\mathtt{M}}^{J}:=\{\mathbf{Y}^{J,m}\}_{m=1}^{\mathtt{M}}
via
i.i.d.
sample iterates from (
1.4
).

3)

(Splitting rule)
Use
\mathbf{D}_{\mathtt{M}}^{J}
to get a
data-dependent
partitioning
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}=\{\mathbf{R}_{r};\,r=0,...,\mathtt{R}-1\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
into
\mathtt{R}\in\mathbb{N}
many cells ‘
\mathbf{R}_{r}
’, where each cell contains
k_{\mathtt{M}}\leq\mathtt{M}
sample iterates.

4)

(Estimator)
Define

\widehat{\mathtt{p}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}(\mathbf{x}):=\sum\limits_{r=0}^{\mathtt{R}-1}\frac{k_{\mathtt{M}}}{\mathtt{M}\cdot\mathtt{vol}(\mathbf{R}_{r})}\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{R}_{r}\}}\,,\qquad\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,,

(1.5)

where
\mathtt{vol}(\mathbf{R}_{r})
denotes the (possibly infinite) volume of the cell
\mathbf{R}_{r}
.

Two specific splitting rules to generate partitions
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}
will be detailed in Section
3
: the first rule is due to Gessaman
[
15
]
(
cf.
Gessaman’s rule
3.1
), the second one is a modification of the
Binary Tree Cuboid
(BTC)-splitting rule taken from
[
13
]
(
cf.
BTC-rule
3.3
). Both splitting rules generate partitions of statistically equivalent cells, meaning that each cell contains the same number of realisations of
\mathbf{D}_{\mathtt{M}}^{J}
. Our main theoretical result in this work is to prove
\mathbb{P}-a.s.
convergence of Algorithm
1.1
for Gessaman’s rule
3.1
; see Theorem
3.5
. More precisely, we prove that for
\widehat{\mathtt{p}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}\equiv\widehat{\mathtt{p}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}_{\mathtt{Ges}}
in (
1.5
),

\displaystyle\lVert p(T,\cdot)-\widehat{\mathtt{p}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}_{\mathtt{Ges}}\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{1}}\to 0\qquad\mathbb{P}-a.s.\qquad(\text{for }\mathtt{M}\to\infty\text{ and }\tau\to 0)\,.

(1.6)

\mathcal{L}^{\ast}\equiv\mathcal{L}^{\ast}(\mathbf{x})

In this work, we are interested in complex data

in the sense of

•

•

Throughout this work,
\bigl(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P}\bigr)
is a given filtered probability space with natural filtration generated by the Wiener process
\mathbf{W}
in (
1.3
). For
1\leq q<\infty
, and a given integrable function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, we denote by
\lVert f\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{q}}:=\Big(\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,\lvert f(\mathbf{x})\rvert^{q}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\Big)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}}
the
\mathbb{L}^{q}-
norm of
f
, and by
\lVert f\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}:=\esssup\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lvert f(\mathbf{x})\rvert
the
\mathbb{L}^{\infty}-
norm of
f
. For two sets
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
, we denote by
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}};\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}})
the space of all smooth functions
f:\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\to\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
. If
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}=\mathbb{R}
, we write
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}})
. Furthermore, we denote by
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the space of all smooth functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
vanishing at infinity. Moreover, we denote by
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
the Euclidean norm, and by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
we denote the corresponding scalar product in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In addition, we denote by
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}}
the spectral matrix norm. Throughout this work, we assume for simplicity:

(A1)

\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
, with bounded derivatives of any order.

(A2)

\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
, with bounded derivatives, and moreover,
\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\cdot)\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\top}(\cdot)
satisfies the
uniform ellipticity condition
, i.e., there exists a constant
\theta>0
, such that

\langle\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\mathbf{x})\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\top}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{y}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\geq\theta\cdot\lVert\mathbf{y}\rVert_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{2}\qquad\text{for all }\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,.

(A3)

p_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
is a pdf; in particular
\lVert p_{0}\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{1}}=1
.

It is well-known that by the assumptions above, there exists a unique classical solution
p:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
with
p(t,\cdot)\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for all
t\in(0,T]
of (
1.1

, i.e.,

on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
; see e.g.
[
19

.

In this subsection, we prove the
\mathbb{P}-a.s.
convergence of
p^{J}
to
p(T,\cdot)
in
\mathbb{L}^{q}
(
1\leq q\leq\infty
) by extending
[
3
, Corollary 2.7]
to include random initial conditions; see Theorem Theorem
2.2
below.
First, we introduce some notation: for
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote by
(t,\mathbf{x})\mapsto p_{\mathbf{y}}(t,\mathbf{x})
the pdf of the associated SDE process
\mathbf{X}^{\mathbf{y}}\equiv\{\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\mathbf{y}}\,;\,t\in[0,T]\}
, which solves (
1.3
) with
\mathbf{X}_{0}=\mathbf{y}
. Similarly, we denote by
\mathbf{x}\mapsto p_{\mathbf{y}}^{J}(\mathbf{x})
the pdf associated with the Euler iterate
\mathbf{Y}^{J}
from (
1.4
), where
\mathbf{Y}^{0}=\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. With this notation we have
p(T,\cdot)\equiv p_{\mathbf{X}^{0}}(T,\cdot)
, and
p^{J}(\cdot)\equiv p_{\mathbf{X}^{0}}^{J}(\cdot)
.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
3
, Corollary 2.7]
)
.

Let
J\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\{t_{j}\}_{j=0}^{J}\subset[0,T]
be a mesh with uniform step size
\tau=\frac{T}{J}
. For all
\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have

\lvert p_{\mathbf{y}}(T,\mathbf{x})-p_{\mathbf{y}}^{J}(\mathbf{x})\rvert\leq C(T)\cdot\tau\cdot\exp\left(-c\frac{\lVert\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\rVert_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{2}}{T}\right)\,,

where
c>0
, and
C(T)>0
is a constant that depends on
T
.

For the proof of Theorem
2.1
see
[
3
]
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
J\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\{t_{j}\}_{j=0}^{J}\subset[0,T]
be a mesh with uniform step size
\tau=\frac{T}{J}
. Let
p(T,\cdot)
and
p^{J}
be the pdfs of
\mathbf{X}_{T}
and
\mathbf{Y}^{J}
from (
1.3
) resp. (
1.4
). Then, for
1\leq q\leq\infty
,

\lVert p(T,\cdot)-p^{J}\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{q}}\leq C(T,d,q)\cdot\tau\,,

where
C(T,d,q)>0
is a constant which depends on
T
,
d
, and
q
.

Consequently, for
1\leq q\leq\infty
,

\lVert p(T,\cdot)-p^{J}\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{q}}\to 0\qquad\mathbb{P}-a.s.\qquad(\text{as }\tau\to 0)\,.

Proof.

By the
Riesz-Thorin interpolation theorem
for
\mathbb{L}^{q}
spaces, it suffices to prove the statement for
q=1
and
q=\infty
.

Step 1:
(
q=1
) By using the
Chapman-Kolmogorov equation
for the transition density, a calculation yields

\displaystyle\lVert p(T,\cdot)-p^{J}\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{1}}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,\lvert p_{\mathbf{X}^{0}}(T,\mathbf{x})-p_{\mathbf{X}^{0}}^{J}(\mathbf{x})\rvert\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,\bigg\lvert\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,p_{\mathbf{y}}(T,\mathbf{x})\cdot p_{0}(\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}-\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,p_{\mathbf{y}}^{J}(\mathbf{x})\cdot p_{0}(\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\,\bigg\rvert\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,\bigg\lvert\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,\left(p_{\mathbf{y}}(T,\mathbf{x})-p_{\mathbf{y}}^{J}(\mathbf{x})\right)\cdot p_{0}(\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\,\bigg\rvert\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\leq\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,\big\lvert p_{\mathbf{y}}(T,\mathbf{x})-p_{\mathbf{y}}^{J}(\mathbf{x})\big\rvert\cdot p_{0}(\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,.

Application of Theorem
2.1
then leads to

\displaystyle\lVert p(T,\cdot)-p^{J}\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq C(T)\cdot\tau\cdot\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,\exp\left(-c\frac{\lVert\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\rVert_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{2}}{T}\right)\cdot p_{0}(\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=C(T)\cdot\tau\cdot\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,p_{0}(\mathbf{y})\,\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,\exp\left(-c\frac{\lVert\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\rVert_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{2}}{T}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\,.

Since

\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,\exp\left(-c\frac{\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{2}}{T}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\left(\frac{T\pi}{c}\right)^{\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}}\,,

we further conclude

\displaystyle\lVert p(T,\cdot)-p^{J}\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq C(T)\cdot\tau\cdot\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,p_{0}(\mathbf{y})\cdot\left(\frac{T\pi}{c}\right)^{\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}=C(T)\cdot\left(\frac{T\pi}{c}\right)^{\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}}\cdot\tau\,,

from which the convergence statement in the theorem follows for
q=1
.

Step 2:
(
q=\infty
) Similar to Step 1, we obtain

\displaystyle\lVert p(T,\cdot)-p^{J}\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}

\displaystyle=\sup\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,\lvert p_{\mathbf{X}^{0}}(T,\mathbf{x})-p_{\mathbf{X}^{0}}^{J}(\mathbf{x})\rvert\leq\sup\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,\big\lvert p_{\mathbf{y}}(T,\mathbf{x})-p_{\mathbf{y}}^{J}(\mathbf{x})\big\rvert\cdot p_{0}(\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle\leq C(T)\cdot\tau\cdot\sup\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,\underbrace{\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,\exp\left(-c\frac{\lVert\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\rVert_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{2}}{T}\right)\cdot p_{0}(\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}}_{\leq 1}\leq C(T)\cdot\tau\,,

from which the convergence assertion follows for
q=\infty
.
∎

do:

For

q=0,...,2^{p}-1
do:

(I)

Set
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}:=\mathcal{S}_{p,q}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}:=\mathcal{R}_{p,q}^{\mathtt{M}}
.

(II)

Find the index of the coordinate axes
\ell\in\{1,...,d\}
parallel to a largest edge of the cell
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}
; denote this component by
\ell_{p,q}\in\{1,...,d\}
. If two or more edges have equal largest length, choose the edge with the lowest index
\ell
. Edge lengths in a direction in which the cell is unbounded are considered infinite.

(III)

Compute the median
\mathtt{med}_{p,q}\in\mathbb{R}
along the
\ell_{p,q}-
axis of all sample iterates in
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}
.

(IV)

Divide
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}=\mathcal{R}_{p+1,2q}^{\mathtt{M}}\cup\mathcal{R}_{p+1,2q+1}^{\mathtt{M}}
into two disjoint cells by a hyperplane perpendicular to the
\ell_{p,q}-
axis at
\mathtt{med}_{p,q}
, and set
\mathcal{S}_{p+1,2q}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}\cap\mathcal{R}_{p+1,2q}^{\mathtt{M}}
,
\mathcal{S}_{p+1,2q+1}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}\cap\mathcal{R}_{p+1,2q+1}^{\mathtt{M}}
.

Note that ‘
\mathcal{S}_{p,q}
’ contains the samples in ‘
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}
’ which are contained in ‘
\mathcal{R}_{p,q}^{\mathtt{M}}
’. The partition
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathtt{BTC}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}
generated via this procedure has a total number of
\mathtt{R}_{\mathtt{BTC}}:=2^{\kappa}=2^{N-n}=\frac{\mathtt{M}}{k_{\mathtt{M}}}
cells.

3.2
Strong consistency of the density estimator
\widehat{\mathtt{p}}_{\mathtt{Ges}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}

The goal of this subsection is to prove the strong
\mathbb{L}^{1}-
consistency of the estimator
\widehat{\mathtt{p}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}_{\mathtt{Ges}}
from Algorithm
1.1
with Gessaman’s rule
3.1
; see Theorem
3.5
below. The combination with Theorem
2.2
then settles convergence of the estimator towards
p(T,\cdot)
through (
1.6
).

The density estimator
\widehat{\mathtt{p}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}_{\mathtt{Ges}}
was first proposed in
[
15
]
, where its strong
\mathbb{L}^{1}-
consistency was motivated. This section aims to provide a proof of this property. It rests on the verification of three criteria given in
[
20
, Theorem 1]
to guarantee strong
\mathbb{L}^{1}-
consistency for a general density estimator; see also Theorem
3.4
below.

Let
\mathtt{M}\in\mathbb{N}
. We write
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}(\mathbf{x}_{1},...,\mathbf{x}_{\mathtt{M}})
for a partition of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
which is realised based upon given points
\mathbf{x}_{1},...,\mathbf{x}_{\mathtt{M}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Note that with this notation we have
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}\equiv\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}(\mathbf{Y}^{J,1},...,\mathbf{Y}^{J,\mathtt{M}})
based on the sample iterates
\mathbf{D}^{J}_{\mathtt{M}}
from Algorithm
1.1
. We denote by
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathtt{M}}:=\{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}(\mathbf{x}_{1},...,\mathbf{x}_{\mathtt{M}})\,:\,\mathbf{x}_{1},...,\mathbf{x}_{\mathtt{M}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
the collection of nonrandom partitions following the same underlying partitioning strategy. Moreover, we denote by
\mathtt{m}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathtt{M}}):=\sup\limits_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathtt{M}}}\#\{\text{cells in }\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}\}
the maximal cell count of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathtt{M}}
.

Fix
\mathtt{M}
points
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1},...,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathtt{M}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}:=\{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1},...,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathtt{M}}\}
. We denote by
\Delta\bigl(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathtt{M}},(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1},...,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathtt{M}})\bigr)
the number of distinct partitions

\{\mathbf{R}_{0}\cap\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}},...,\mathbf{R}_{\mathtt{R}-1}\cap\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}\}

of the finite set
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
that are induced by partitions
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}:=\{\mathbf{R}_{0},...,\mathbf{R}_{\mathtt{R}-1}\}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathtt{M}}
. Then, we define the growth function of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathtt{M}}
as

\Delta_{\mathtt{M}}^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathtt{M}}):=\max\limits_{(\mathbf{x}_{1},...,\mathbf{x}_{\mathtt{M}})\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathtt{M}\cdot d}}\Delta\bigl(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathtt{M}},(\mathbf{x}_{1},...,\mathbf{x}_{\mathtt{M}})\bigr)\,,

which is the largest number of distinct partitions of any
\mathtt{M}
point subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that can be induced by the partitions in
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathtt{M}}
.

Finally, for every
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote by
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}[\mathbf{x}]
the unique cell in the partition
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}
that contains the point
\mathbf{x}
.

Theorem 3.4

(
[
20
, Theorem 1]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{Y}^{J,1},\mathbf{Y}^{J,2},...
be
i.i.d.
random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
whose common distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{Y}^{J}}
has density
p^{J}
. Let
\mathtt{M}\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathtt{M}}:=\{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}(\mathbf{x}_{1},...,\mathbf{x}_{\mathtt{M}})\,:\,\mathbf{x}_{1},...,\mathbf{x}_{\mathtt{M}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
be the collection of partitions following the same underlying partitioning strategy. If, as
\mathtt{M}
tends to infinity

(a)

\mathtt{M}^{-1}\cdot\mathtt{m}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathtt{M}})\to 0

(b)

\mathtt{M}^{-1}\cdot\ln\bigl(\Delta_{\mathtt{M}}^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathtt{M}})\bigr)\to 0

(c)

\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{Y}^{J}}\big[\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,:\,\mathtt{diam}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}[\mathbf{x}]\bigr)>\gamma\}\big]\to 0\quad\mathbb{P}-a.s.
for every
\gamma>0
,

then the estimator
\widehat{\mathtt{p}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}
given in (
1.5
) is strongly
\mathbb{L}^{1}-
consistent, i.e.,

\lVert p^{J}-\widehat{\mathtt{p}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{1}}\to 0\qquad\mathbb{P}-a.s.\qquad\text{(as }\mathtt{M}\to\infty)\,.

The next theorem states the strong
\mathbb{L}^{1}-
consistency of the estimator
\widehat{\mathtt{p}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}_{\mathtt{Ges}}
of Algorithm
1.1
, where step 3) is realised via Scheme
3.1
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
J\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\{t_{j}\}_{j=0}^{J}\subset[0,T]
be a mesh with uniform step size
\tau=\frac{T}{J}
. Let
\mathtt{M}\in\mathbb{N}
and
k_{\mathtt{M}}\in\mathbb{N}
with
k_{\mathtt{M}}<\mathtt{M}
. Let
\mathbf{D}_{\mathtt{M}}^{J}:=\{\mathbf{Y}^{J,m}\}_{m=1}^{\mathtt{M}}
be the sample set from (
1.4
). If
k_{\mathtt{M}}\to\infty
and
\frac{k_{\mathtt{M}}}{\mathtt{M}}\to 0
as
\mathtt{M}\to\infty
, then
Gessaman’s
estimator
\widehat{\mathtt{p}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}_{\mathtt{Ges}}
in Algorithm
1.1
, where step 3) is realised via Scheme
3.1
, is strongly
\mathbb{L}^{1}-
consistent, i.e.,

\lVert p^{J}-\widehat{\mathtt{p}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}_{\mathtt{Ges}}\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{1}}\to 0\qquad\mathbb{P}-a.s.\qquad\text{(as }\mathtt{M}\to\infty)\,.

We prove Theorem
3.5
by verifying the conditions (a), (b), and (c) in Theorem
3.4
. As we will see, the conditions (a) and (b) automatically follow from the concept of
statistically equivalent cells
in combination with an appropriate choice of
\mathtt{M}
and
k_{\mathtt{M}}
. Here, we closely follow the arguments of
[
20
, Theorem 4]
, which verifies the conditions (a), (b), and (c) of Theorem
3.4
in the case of
d=1
, and therefore proves Theorem
3.5
for
d=1
.
The verification of condition (c) for
d\geq 2
, however, is more delicate, and we are not aware of any results in the literature where this part is rigorously proved for Gessaman’s rule
3.1
.

Proof of Theorem
3.5
.

Let
d\geq 2
.

Verification of (a) in Theorem
3.4
:
Since we are in the setting of statistically equivalent cells, the number of cells in every partition in
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathtt{M}}:=\{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}_{\mathtt{Ges}}(\mathbf{z}_{1},...,\mathbf{z}_{\mathtt{M}})\,:\,\mathbf{z}_{1},...,\mathbf{z}_{\mathtt{M}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
is equal to
\mathtt{R}_{\mathtt{Ges}}:=\mathtt{N}^{d}:=\Big\lceil\left(\frac{\mathtt{M}}{k_{\mathtt{M}}}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{d}}}\Big\rceil^{d}
; see Gessaman’s rule
3.1
. Hence, due to the growth of
k_{\mathtt{M}}
related to
\mathtt{M}
, we get

\mathtt{M}^{-1}\cdot\mathtt{m}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathtt{M}})=\frac{1}{\mathtt{M}}\cdot\Big\lceil\left(\frac{\mathtt{M}}{k_{\mathtt{M}}}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{d}}}\Big\rceil^{d}\to 0\qquad(\text{as }\mathtt{M}\to\infty)\,.

Verification of (b) in Theorem
3.4
:
A combinatorial argument immediately yields (
cf.

[
20
, Subsec. 6.3]
)

\Delta_{\mathtt{M}}^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathtt{M}})=\binom{\mathtt{M}+\mathtt{R}_{\mathtt{Ges}}}{\mathtt{M}}^{d}\,.

(3.1)

We consider the
binary entropy function

h(x)=-x\ln(x)-(1-x)\ln(1-x)
,
x\in(0,1)
. We see that
h
is increasing on
(0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}]
, symmetric about
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
, and
h(x)\to 0
as
x\to 0
. Using (
3.1
), the inequality

\ln\left(\binom{s}{t}\right)\leq sh\left(\nicefrac{{t}}{{s}}\right)\,,\qquad s,t\in\mathbb{N}

stated in
[
10
]
, and the fact that
h
is symmetric about
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
, we get

\displaystyle\ln\bigl(\Delta_{\mathtt{M}}^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathtt{M}})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\ln\left(\binom{\mathtt{M}+\mathtt{R}_{\mathtt{Ges}}}{\mathtt{M}}^{d}\right)=d\cdot\ln\left(\binom{\mathtt{M}+\mathtt{R}_{\mathtt{Ges}}}{\mathtt{M}}\right)\leq d\cdot(\mathtt{M}+\mathtt{R}_{\mathtt{Ges}})\cdot h\left(\frac{\mathtt{M}}{\mathtt{M}+\mathtt{R}_{\mathtt{Ges}}}\right)

\displaystyle=d\cdot(\mathtt{M}+\mathtt{R}_{\mathtt{Ges}})\cdot h\left(\frac{\mathtt{R}_{\mathtt{Ges}}}{\mathtt{M}+\mathtt{R}_{\mathtt{Ges}}}\right)\,.

Since
\mathtt{M}\geq\mathtt{R}_{\mathtt{Ges}}
,
\frac{\mathtt{R}_{\mathtt{Ges}}}{\mathtt{M}+\mathtt{R}_{\mathtt{Ges}}}\leq\frac{1}{k_{\mathtt{M}}}
, we obtain

\ln\bigl(\Delta_{\mathtt{M}}^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathtt{M}})\bigr)\leq 2d\cdot\mathtt{M}\cdot h\left(\frac{1}{k_{\mathtt{M}}}\right)\,.

Hence, using the properties of
h
and that
k_{\mathtt{M}}\to\infty
as
\mathtt{M}\to\infty
, we conclude

\mathtt{M}^{-1}\cdot\ln\bigl(\Delta_{\mathtt{M}}^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathtt{M}})\bigr)\leq 2d\cdot h\left(\frac{1}{k_{\mathtt{M}}}\right)\to 0\qquad(\text{as }\mathtt{M}\to\infty)\,.

Verification of (c) in Theorem
3.4
:
Fix
\gamma>0
,
\varepsilon>0
, and let
L\equiv L(\varepsilon)>0
be such that
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{Y}^{J}}[\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus[-L,L]^{d}]\leq\varepsilon
.
Because all cells in the partition
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}_{\mathtt{Ges}}
are disjoint excluding the boundary, and due to the choice of
L>0
above, we find

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{Y}^{J}}\big[

\displaystyle\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,:\,\mathtt{diam}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}_{\mathtt{Ges}}[\mathbf{x}]\bigr)>\gamma\}\big]\leq\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{Y}^{J}}\big[\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,:\,\mathtt{diam}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}_{\mathtt{Ges}}[\mathbf{x}]\bigr)>\gamma\}\cap[-L,L]^{d}\big]+\varepsilon

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{Y}^{J}}\Bigg[\bigcup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in\{0,...,\mathtt{R}_{\mathtt{Ges}}-1\}:\\
\mathtt{diam}(\mathbf{R}_{r})\geq\gamma\end{subarray}}\mathbf{R}_{r}\cap[-L,L]^{d}\Bigg]+\varepsilon

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{r=0}^{\mathtt{R}_{\mathtt{Ges}}-1}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathtt{diam}(\mathbf{R}_{r})\geq\gamma\}}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{Y}^{J}}\big[\mathbf{R}_{r}\cap[-L,L]^{d}\big]+\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in\{0,...,\mathtt{R}_{\mathtt{Ges}}-1\}:\\
\mathbf{R}_{r}\cap[-L,L]^{d}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathtt{diam}(\mathbf{R}_{r})\geq\gamma\}}\cdot\left(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{Y}^{J}}[\mathbf{R}_{r}]-\frac{k_{\mathtt{M}}}{\mathtt{M}}+\frac{k_{\mathtt{M}}}{\mathtt{M}}\right)+\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leq\frac{k_{\mathtt{M}}}{\mathtt{M}}\cdot\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in\{0,...,\mathtt{R}_{\mathtt{Ges}}-1\}:\\
\mathbf{R}_{r}\cap[-L,L]^{d}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathtt{diam}(\mathbf{R}_{r})\geq\gamma\}}+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in\{0,...,\mathtt{R}_{\mathtt{Ges}}-1\}:\\
\mathbf{R}_{r}\cap[-L,L]^{d}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathtt{diam}(\mathbf{R}_{r})\geq\gamma\}}\cdot\Big\lvert\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{Y}^{J}}[\mathbf{R}_{r}]-\frac{k_{\mathtt{M}}}{\mathtt{M}}\Big\rvert+\varepsilon\,.

(3.2)

Since each cell ‘
\mathbf{R}_{r}
’ is a hyper-rectangle, we can write
\mathbf{R}_{r}=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d}[a_{i}^{(r)},b_{i}^{(r)}]
for
a_{i}^{(r)}<b_{i}^{(r)}
. If
\mathtt{diam}(\mathbf{R}_{r}):=\sqrt{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\lvert b_{i}^{(r)}-a_{i}^{(r)}\rvert^{2}}
is greater or equal to
\gamma
, then its longest side is greater or equal to
\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{d}}
, i.e.,
\max\limits_{i=1,...,d}b_{i}^{(r)}-a_{i}^{(r)}\geq\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{d}}
.
Because the second sum on the right-hand side of (
3.2
) is bounded from above by

\mathbf{E}_{\mathtt{M}}:=\sum\limits_{r=0}^{\mathtt{R}_{\mathtt{Ges}}-1}\Big\lvert\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{Y}^{J}}[\mathbf{R}_{r}]-\frac{k_{\mathtt{M}}}{\mathtt{M}}\Big\rvert\,,

we therefore conclude from (
3.2
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{Y}^{J}}\big[

\displaystyle\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,:\,\mathtt{diam}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}_{\mathtt{Ges}}[\mathbf{x}]\bigr)>\gamma\}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{k_{\mathtt{M}}}{\mathtt{M}}\cdot\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in\{0,...,\mathtt{R}_{\mathtt{Ges}}-1\}:\\
\mathbf{R}_{r}\cap[-L,L]^{d}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\mathbbm{1}_{\Big\{\max\limits_{i=1,...,d}b_{i}^{(r)}-a_{i}^{(r)}\geq\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{d}}\Big\}}+\mathbf{E}_{\mathtt{M}}+\varepsilon\,.

(3.3)

In the following, we estimate the first sum on the right-hand side of (
3.3
). Standard calculations lead to

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in\{0,...,\mathtt{R}_{\mathtt{Ges}}-1\}:\\
\mathbf{R}_{r}\cap[-L,L]^{d}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{\big\{\max\limits_{i=1,...,d}b_{i}^{(r)}-a_{i}^{(r)}\geq\tfrac{\gamma}{\sqrt{d}}\big\}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in\{0,...,\mathtt{R}_{\mathtt{Ges}}-1\}:\\
\mathbf{R}_{r}\subseteq[-L,L]^{d}\end{subarray}}\mathbbm{1}_{\big\{\max\limits_{i=1,...,d}b_{i}^{(r)}-a_{i}^{(r)}\geq\tfrac{\gamma}{\sqrt{d}}\big\}}+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in\{0,...,\mathtt{R}_{\mathtt{Ges}}-1\}:\\
\mathbf{R}_{r}\cap\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus[-L,L]^{d}\right)\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\mathbbm{1}_{\big\{\max\limits_{i=1,...,d}b_{i}^{(r)}-a_{i}^{(r)}\geq\tfrac{\gamma}{\sqrt{d}}\big\}}

\displaystyle\leq\#\Big\{r=0,...,\mathtt{R}_{\mathtt{Ges}}-1\,:\,\mathbf{R}_{r}\subseteq[-L,L]^{d},\,\max\limits_{i=1,...,d}b_{i}^{(r)}-a_{i}^{(r)}\geq\tfrac{\gamma}{\sqrt{d}}\Big\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\#\Big\{r=0,...,\mathtt{R}_{\mathtt{Ges}}-1\,:\,\mathbf{R}_{r}\cap\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus[-L,L]^{d}\right)\neq\emptyset\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\#\Big\{r=0,...,\mathtt{R}_{\mathtt{Ges}}-1\,:\,\mathbf{R}_{r}\subseteq[-L,L]^{d},\,b_{i}^{(r)}-a_{i}^{(r)}\geq\tfrac{\gamma}{\sqrt{d}}\Big\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\#\Big\{r=0,...,\mathtt{R}_{\mathtt{Ges}}-1\,:\,\mathbf{R}_{r}\cap\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus[-L,L]^{d}\right)\neq\emptyset\Big\}\,.

(3.4)

Since there are at most
\frac{2L}{\nicefrac{{\gamma}}{{\sqrt{d}}}}
disjoint intervals of length greater than
\nicefrac{{\gamma}}{{\sqrt{d}}}
in
[-L,L]
, and the number of cuts along any coordinate axis in Gessaman’s rule
3.1
is at most
\mathtt{N}:=\Big\lceil\left(\frac{\mathtt{M}}{k_{\mathtt{M}}}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{d}}}\Big\rceil
, we can bound the first sum in (
3.2
) by
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\frac{2L}{\nicefrac{{\gamma}}{{\sqrt{d}}}}\cdot\mathtt{N}=d\cdot\sqrt{d}\cdot\frac{2L}{\gamma}\cdot\Big\lceil\left(\frac{\mathtt{M}}{k_{\mathtt{M}}}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{d}}}\Big\rceil
from above. Furthermore, since
[-L,L]^{d}
has
2d
sides, we can bound the second summand in (
3.2
) by
2d\cdot\Big\lceil\left(\frac{\mathtt{M}}{k_{\mathtt{M}}}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{d}}}\Big\rceil
from above. Thus, we conclude

\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in\{0,...,\mathtt{R}_{\mathtt{Ges}}-1\}:\\
\mathbf{R}_{r}\cap[-L,L]^{d}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\mathbbm{1}_{\big\{\max\limits_{i=1,...,d}b_{i}^{(r)}-a_{i}^{(r)}\geq\tfrac{\gamma}{\sqrt{d}}\big\}}\leq\left(\sqrt{d}\cdot d\cdot\frac{2L}{\gamma}+2d\right)\cdot\Big\lceil\left(\frac{\mathtt{M}}{k_{\mathtt{M}}}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{d}}}\Big\rceil\,.

(3.5)

Hence, combining (
3.5
) with (
3.3
), we get

\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{Y}^{J}}\big[\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,:\,\mathtt{diam}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}_{\mathtt{Ges}}[\mathbf{x}]\bigr)>\gamma\}\big]\leq\frac{k_{\mathtt{M}}}{\mathtt{M}}\cdot\left(\sqrt{d}\cdot d\cdot\frac{2L}{\gamma}+2d\right)\cdot\Big\lceil\left(\frac{\mathtt{M}}{k_{\mathtt{M}}}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{d}}}\Big\rceil+\mathbf{E}_{\mathtt{M}}+\varepsilon\,.

(3.6)

Since (a) and (b) in Theorem
3.4
are verified, an application of
[
20
, Corollary 1]
yields

\mathbf{E}_{\mathtt{M}}\to 0\quad\mathbb{P}-a.s.\quad(\text{ as }\mathtt{M}\to\infty)\,.

(3.7)

Finally, by (
3.6
) and (
3.7
) we conclude

\lim\limits_{\mathtt{M}\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{Y}^{J}}\big[\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,:\,\mathtt{diam}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{J,\mathtt{M}}_{\mathtt{Ges}}[\mathbf{x}]\bigr)>\gamma\}\big]=\varepsilon\quad\mathbb{P}-a.s.\,,

which verifies condition (c) of Theorem
3.4
.

∎

All simulations are conducted via
\mathtt{PYTHON}
(version 3.11) on a
HP ProBook 455 15.6 inch G9 Notebook PC (Processor: AMD Ryzen 7 5825U with Radeon Graphics, 2000 MHz)
and on a
Dual Xeon 6242 Workstation with 768 GB RAM
. In all computational experiments, the samples of the initial conditions are generated with Numpy’s
\mathtt{random.multivariate\_normal}
command.
For the computational evaluation of errors in the
\mathbb{L}^{1}-
norm we use
Monte Carlo integration
:

\lVert f\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{1}}:=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lvert f(\mathbf{x})\rvert\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\approx\sum\limits_{r=0}^{\mathtt{R}-1}\mathtt{vol}(\mathbf{R}_{r})\cdot\frac{1}{\mathsf{N}}\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\mathsf{N}}\lvert f(\mathbf{x}^{(r)}_{i})\rvert\,,

where
\mathsf{N}\in\mathbb{N}
is sufficiently large, and
\mathbf{x}^{(r)}_{i}
is a randomly drawn point in the cell
\mathbf{R}_{r}
.

In order to approximate the
\mathbb{L}^{\infty}-
norm, we evaluate the error on each cell
\mathbf{R}_{r}
at randomly drawn points
\mathbf{x}^{(r)}_{i}\in\mathbf{R}_{r}
in it, i.e.,

\lVert f\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}:=\esssup\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lvert f(\mathbf{x})\rvert\approx\max\limits_{r=0,...,\mathtt{R}-1}\max\limits_{i=1,...,\mathsf{N}}\lvert f(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(r)})\rvert\,,

for some
\mathsf{N}\in\mathbb{N}
.
