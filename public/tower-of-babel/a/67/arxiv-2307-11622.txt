A Benchmarking Study on Vision-Based Grasp Synthesis Algorithms
Bharath K Rameshbabu, Sumukh S Balakrishna, Berk Calli

Abstract
In this paper, we present a benchmarking study of vision-based grasp synthesis algorithms, each with distinct approaches, and provide a comparative analysis of their performance under different experimental conditions. In particular, we compare two machine-learning-based and two analytical algorithms to determine their strengths and weaknesses in different scenarios. In addition, we provide an open-source benchmarking tool developed from state-of-the-art benchmarking procedures and protocols to systematically evaluate different grasp synthesis algorithms. Our findings offer insights into the performance of the evaluated algorithms, which can aid in selecting the most appropriate algorithm for different scenarios.

Motivation

The lack of benchmarking studies for assessing grasping performance makes it challenging to compare the effectiveness of different grasping systems, to evaluate the impact of different parameters or design choices, and to systematically test and improve grasp synthesis algorithms. Researchers generally rely on ad-hoc metrics and evaluation methods, which makes it difficult to determine the best approaches or to reproduce and build upon existing work.

Technical Approach

Figure 1:
The left image represents the architecture of the benchmarking tool. The right image shows the grasps synthesized by different grasping algorithms for Medium Clamp (Mask based, ResNet, Top Surface, GG-CNN inorder from top-left to bottom-right.)

We evaluated two learning-based algorithms, i.e. GG-CNN
bib7
and ResNet
bib8
; and two analytical algorithms in our benchmarking study. The implementation of the learning-based algorithms were directly adopted from the public repositories of the papers. The analytical algorithms are based on
bib9
bib10
, which segments and utilizes the top surfaces of the objects, and
bib11
, which adopts a mask-based approach to determine the grasp poses. The analytical approaches were developed in 2008 and 2011, when computational power was comparatively limited. In our work, we take advantage of today’s improved computational resources and enhance the implementation of these algorithms as follows. The top surface-based algorithm
bib9
bib10
extracts the point cloud corresponding to the object’s top surface by applying distance filtering to the points along the table plane. Instead of using the original paper’s ad-hoc grasp search approach, we apply a thorough grasp search on the filtered data. Firstly, we determine the concave hull of the top surface. Then, we calculate force/moment balance across densely sampled normals along the hull to identify the most stable grasping location. The mask-based algorithm
bib11
relies on a convolution mask that is designed to search for geometric features on the object that closely resembles the gripper’s structure. Multiple masks of different sizes and rotations are sampled and convoluted across the entire depth image and the segment with the best similarity score is chosen. While the original algorithm does relatively sparse sampling and requires a grasp pose refinement step, we skip this step by applying dense sampling. In addition, we developed a benchmarking software designed to evaluate the performance of grasping algorithms, both in simulation and on real systems, based on the benchmarking procedure from
bib3
(Fig.
1
). Our software is capable of automatically benchmarking and calculating scores in a simulation environment. In the real world experiments, the software streamlines the process and prompts the user to place the object in specific locations, after which the tool automatically initiates the grasping procedure.

Experiments

The top-left image depicts the objects utilized for the benchmarking experiments. The bottom-left image illustrates the robot executing the task of picking up an object. Our benchmarking study utilized a Franka Panda robot with a paralell jaw gripper (Fig.
2
). We also designed and implemented a simulator for mimicking the real experimental setup. To capture the RGB and depth information of the objects, we mounted an eye-in-hand Intel Realsense D435 camera onto the robot’s end effector. We set the exposure to 8500 ms, gain to 16 db, and laser power to 150 mW for the Realsense camera. The camera is positioned in a downward-facing vertical orientation, 0.8 meters away from the table plane, to capture RGB/depth information. Please note that all the algorithms we assess are for planar grasping, and are designed for top-down view only.

To conduct our experiments, we selected 10 objects from the YCB object dataset
bib12
depicted in Fig.
2
. To evaluate and compare different grasping algorithms, we adopted the benchmarking procedure described in
bib3
. We conducted the experiments in six different (but consistent) locations for each object. In each location, we attempted a pick for the object based on the output of the grasping algorithm. If the pick is successful, we performed yaw movements of +/- 45 degrees and shaking motions with an angle of +/- 45 degrees to test grasp stability. All movements were executed at a joint velocity of 1.0175 rad/s. We used RRTConnect algorithm from MoveIt for motion planning of the robot.

To thoroughly assess the performance of the grasping algorithms, we conducted evaluations and comparisons in diverse experimental conditions: The algorithms were tested in both simulated and real-world environments to evaluate their effectiveness in different contexts. We also tested the algorithms in the two different background textures as shown in Fig.
2
. Additionally, we tested the algorithms under varying lighting conditions (60 lux and 340 lux) to determine their robustness in real-world scenarios. In total, we did

6
Main Experimental Insights

In Fig.
3
, we presented a comparison of the performance of all four algorithms in a non-textured background, real-world environment with a lighting of 340 lux. The results indicate that the analytical algorithms, specifically the adapted versions of the mask-based
bib11
and the top surface-based
bib9
bib10
algorithms, exhibit better performance compared to the learning algorithms for majority of the objects with an aggregated score of 155 and 145 respectively, out of a maximum score of 180. Notably, Top Surface algorithm encounters difficulties only when dealing with objects that have irregular top surfaces, i.e pear, mustard, and lopsided small clamp. Whereas the mask-based algorithm consistently delivers satisfactory results across all the objects. On the other hand, GG-CNN and ResNet algorithms display a mixed performance, scoring 50 and 103 respectively out of a total score of 180. They perform exceptionally well for a specific set of objects but demonstrate poor performance for the remaining set. This discrepancy can primarily be attributed to the specific objects the models were trained on, as we utilized pretrained models for our evaluation.

Another notable finding is that, as shown in Fig.
4
, GG-CNN demonstrates considerably better performance in the simulation environment. This indicates that GG-CNN grasping algorithm is more sensitive to noise and environmental factors. On the other hand, ResNet excels in performance when the objects are placed against a non-textured background. This is due to ResNet being an RG-D based algorithm, getting significantly affected by the features in the background. The presence of glare over the objects in the RGB image also had a notable impact on the success of grasps at specific poses for ResNet. Our observations revealed consistently low scores for these poses across multiple objects and algorithms. This implies that ResNet doesn’t perform very good when the color contrast near the object is less.

Through our research, we have identified several other common factors that influence the performance of grasping algorithms. These factors encompass the weight, friction, and shape properties of the objects being grasped. Even when the algorithms generate satisfactory grasp outputs, objects may still slip due to the inherent characteristics of weight, friction, and shape. The lighting conditions also play a crucial role in determining the quality of input data. Algorithms reliant on high-quality input data are particularly susceptible to variations in lighting conditions, which in turn can affect their performance.

In addition, the parameters involved in the depth calculation algorithm, which is implemented in our benchmarking tool to calculate the z-coordinate of the grasp based on the gripper shape, significantly impacts the overall performance of grasping algorithms. Additionally, it is crucial to acknowledge the limitations inherent in simulators, specifically when simulating the manipulation of small parts within the grasping process, the grasp plugin utilized in our benchmarking tool, referenced in
bib13
, has exhibited instances of occasional failure in grasping.
