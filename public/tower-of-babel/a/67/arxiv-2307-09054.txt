K-Divergent lattices
Guy Lachman, Anurag Rao, Uri Shapira, Yuval Yifrach

Abstract
. We introduce a novel concept in topological dynamics, referred to as k k -divergence, which extends the notion of divergent orbits. Motivated by questions in the theory of inhomogeneous Diophantine approximations, we investigate this notion in the dynamical system given by a certain flow on the space of unimodular lattices in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} . Our main result is the existence of k k -divergent lattices for any k ≥ 0 k\geq 0 . In fact, we utilize the emerging theory of parametric geometry of numbers and calculate the Hausdorff dimension of the set of k k -divergent lattices.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
k
-divergence

In this article we investigate a novel concept in topological dynamics. We are motivated by a particular example of a diagonal flow acting on the space of lattices with specific implications to questions in Diophantine approximation. As a consequence, our analysis in this paper is restricted to this particular example. Nevertheless, we start the introduction abstractly.

Let
X
be a topological space and
H
a topological (semi) group acting continuously on
X
. The pair
(X,H)
is referred to as a topological dynamical
system. Given
x\in X
, the set

Hx:=\{hx\in X:h\in H\}

is called the
orbit
of
x
. Broadly speaking, topological dynamics concerns itself with investigating the topological properties of orbits. A basic notion which is highly relevant to our discussion is that of divergence:
An orbit
Hx
is said to be
divergent
if the map

h\mapsto hx
is a proper map from
H
to
X
; that is, the preimage of any compact set in
X
is compact in
H
. Clearly, it is of interest to discuss
divergent orbits only when both
H
and
X
are non-compact.
It will be convenient to use the following terminology
1
1

1

For the sake of simplicity all topological spaces are assumed to be metric with the Heine-Borel property: compact sets are those which are closed and bounded. Examples are manifolds equipped with a complete Riemannian metric.
: If
Y
is a topological space, we say that a sequence
(y_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\subset Y
is
divergent
and write
y_{n}\to\infty
if
y_{n}
eventually
leaves any compact set in
Y
. Using this terminology an orbit
Hx
is divergent if and only if

h_{n}\to\infty\implies h_{n}x\to\infty.

It is convenient to use the following terminology.

Definition 1.1

(Asymptotic accumulation points)
.

Given a topological dynamical system
(X,H)
and a point
x\in X
, we have its set of asymptotic accumulation points:

D(x):=\{y\in X:\text{ There exists }(h_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\subset H\text{ with }h_{n}\to\infty\text{ and }h_{n}x\to y\}.

Since the acting (semi)-group
H
is fixed we do not record it in the notation, although strictly speaking this set should have been denoted
D_{H}(x).
For example,
Hx
is divergent if and only if
D(x)=\varnothing
.
The following is the main concept which will interest us in the rest of this paper.

Definition 1.2

(
k
-divergent orbits, or points)
.

Given a point
x\in X
, a finite sequence

(y_{0}:=x,y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{k})\in X^{k+1}\ \text{ with }\ y_{i+1}\in D(y_{i})\text{ for }0\leq i<k

is called an accumulation sequence of length
k
for
x
. The point
x
is said to be
k
-divergent if
k
is the supremum over all lengths of accumulation sequences for
x
. Finally,
the orbit
Hx
is said to be
k
-divergent if any of its points are such (note that this is well defined).

In the above definition,
k
takes values in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}
.
Let

\mathbf{Div}(k):=\{x\in X:\text{$x$ is $k$-divergent}\}

so that these sets give a partition of
X
.

Remark 1.3
.

We note that from the statistical point of view the sets
\mathbf{Div}(k)
for
k
finite are very small in the sense that they are null sets
with respect to any
H
-invariant probability measure. This is because: if
x
is
k
-divergent
for finite
k
, then
x\notin D(x)
, and so
x
is not a recurrent point.
(cf. the proof of Theorem
6.2
below.)

As mentioned above, any point
x\in\mathbf{Div}(0)
only has accumulation sequences of length
0
which implies that
Hx
is divergent (and conversely).
Any point
x\in\mathbf{Div}(1)
has an accumulation sequence of length
1
and none which are longer. This shows that it is not divergent but only has asymptotic accumulation points which are themselves divergent.
Indeed, Definition
1.2
is recursive in nature and the reader can check that, for any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

(1.1)

\mathbf{Div}(k)=\left\{x\in X:D(x)\subset\cup_{j=0}^{k-1}\mathbf{Div}(j)\right\}\smallsetminus\cup_{j=0}^{k-1}\mathbf{Div}(j).

In words,
x
is
k
-divergent if its asymptotic accumulation points are all
j
-divergent for
j<k
and if
x
itself is not
j
-divergent for any
j<k.

1.2.
Results in the space of lattices

Let
m
and
n
be positive integers and let
d=m+n
.
Let
G
denote the Lie group
\operatorname{SL}_{d}({\mathbb{R}})
, let
\Gamma
denote
\operatorname{SL}_{d}({\mathbb{Z}})
, and consider the noncompact homogeneous space
X_{d}:=G/\Gamma
. The space
X_{d}
is the moduli space of lattices in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
of covolume 1 (with
g\Gamma
identified with the
{\mathbb{Z}}
-span of the columns of
g
).
For the acting semi-group we take the one-parameter diagonal semi-group

\{a_{t}\in G:t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\}
where

(1.2)

a_{t}:=\text{diag}(e^{t/m},\dots,e^{t/m},e^{-t/n},\dots,e^{-t/n})

(where the number of positive eigenvalues is
m
and the number of negative eigenvalues is
n
). Henceforth, whenever we refer to Definition
1.2
we keep
(X_{d},(a_{t})_{t\geq 0})
as the underlying dynamical system.

Understanding this dynamical system is tightly related to Diophantine approximation via the so-called Dani correspondence. See for example
[
Dan85
, Theorem 2.14]
where divergent orbits under this flow are shown to be in correspondence with singular matrices. See
[
Dan85
, Definition 2.13]
for the definition of singular matrices or see (
1.4
) where singular vectors are defined.
Thus, the set
\mathbf{Div}(0)
has received a lot of attention from both the dynamical and number theoretic perspectives.
There are certain obvious lattices in
\mathbf{Div}(0)
; the so called
degenerate
divergent orbits which correspond to lattices having a rational subspace whose volume form goes to zero under
a_{t}
(see
[
Dan85
, Definition 2.8]
). These lattices are easy to construct. On the other hand, the existence of nondegenerate divergent lattices was first demonstrated by Khintchine in 1926
[
Khi26
]
. Much more recent work involves the computation of the exact Hausdorff dimension of
\mathbf{Div}(0)
(see
[
Che11
,
CC16
,
KKLM17
,
DFSU
,
Sol
]
) which turns out to be

\dim X_{d}-\frac{nm}{n+m}
, and as this number is strictly bigger than the dimension of the degenerate divergent trajectories, one obtains a refinement of Khintchine’s result showing the abundance of non-degenerate divergent lattices.

As we will explain shortly (see §
1.3
), our motivation is to prove existence of
k
-divergent lattices for

k>0
finite.
The situation seems to differ from the
\mathbf{Div}(0)
case because there are seem to be no lattices
which are
k
-divergent for obvious reasons. When
n=m=1
and continued fractions are available, it is not hard to construct explicit examples of
k
-divergent lattices in the plane.
In an unpublished work, the third named
author was able to construct examples of
1
-divergent lattices building on the results in
[
Sha17
]
. However, given the sophisticated theory of parametric geometry of numbers, one can establish existence in a far more systematic manner.

This theory was developed in
[
SS09
,
Roy15
,
DFSU
,
Sol
]
and guarantees existence of lattices whose orbit’s behaviour is controlled by certain combinatorial graphs called templates. The following, which is the main result
of this paper, establishes that indeed, for any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\mathbf{Div}(k)
is nonempty. Moreover, we show that (unless
n=m=1
) their dimensions coincide with that of
\mathbf{Div}(0)
.
This result should be regarded as a demonstration of the strength and flexibility of the emerging tools from the theory of parametric geometry of numbers.

Theorem 1.4
.

For any
k\geq 1
,

(1.3)

\dim(\mathbf{Div}(k))=\dim(X_{d})-\frac{mn}{m+n}

where
\dim
refers to the Hausdorff dimension.

Note, we prove Theorem
1.4
by establishing tight upper and lower bounds on the dimension of
\mathbf{Div}(k)
, for each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, with the lower bound coming from the formulas of
[
DFSU
]
and the upper bound arising as an easy corollary of the main result in
[
KKLM17
]
. (See Corollaries
5.3
,
6.3
below.)

1.3.
Inhomogeneous Diophantine approximation

This subsection is not needed for the rest of the paper and is included for motivational purposes and to put the discussion in context.
Our original motivation for proving the existence of
k
-divergent lattices for
k\geq 1

[
Sha13
]
. In order to explain things in more detail we need to fix some terminology. Since this is a motivational discussion, we do not strive to describe things in utmost generality and restrict our attention to the case
n=1

a_{t}=\operatorname{diag}(e^{t/m},\dots,e^{t/m},e^{-t})\text{ for }t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}.

2.
Invariance properties of
k
-divergent lattices

In this section we prove certain invariance properties of
k
-divergent lattices.
Let
\mathfrak{g}
denote the Lie algebra of
G
and consider the diagonal element
{\bf a}\in\mathfrak{g}
for which
\exp({\bf a})=a_{1}\in G
.
Consider the derivation
\text{ad}_{\bf a}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
. Clearly, this map is diagonalizable.
We define

(2.1)

\mathfrak{h}^{+}:=\text{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\left\{{\bf b}\in\mathfrak{g}:\text{ad}_{\bf a}({\bf b})=\lambda{\bf b}\text{ with }\lambda>0\right\},

(2.2)

\mathfrak{h}^{-}:=\text{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\left\{{\bf b}\in\mathfrak{g}:\text{ad}_{\bf a}({\bf b})=\lambda{\bf b}\text{ with }\lambda<0\right\},

(2.3)

\mathfrak{h}^{0}:=\left\{{\bf b}\in\mathfrak{g}:\text{ad}_{\bf a}({\bf b})=0\right\}.

We thus have a vector space direct sum

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{h}^{+}\oplus\mathfrak{h}^{-}\oplus\mathfrak{h}^{0}.

Moreover, each of the above summands is a Lie subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
.
Let
H^{+},H^{-},H^{0}
be the corresponding connected Lie subgroups of
G
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
. Let
x\in\mathbf{Div}(k)
and let
g^{-}
and
g^{0}
belong to
H^{-}
and
H^{0}
respectively.
We then have that

(2.4)

\left(x\in\mathbf{Div}(k)\right)\iff\left(g^{-}g^{0}x\in\mathbf{Div}(k)\right).

Proof.

We work with elements
g^{-}=\exp({\bf b})
and
g^{0}=\exp({\bf c})
along with the assumptions
\operatorname{ad}_{\bf a}{\bf b}=\lambda{\bf b}
for
\lambda<0
and
\operatorname{ad}_{\bf a}{\bf c}=0
.
The reader is left to deduce the general case from this.
We then have, for
t>0
,

(2.5)

\begin{split}a_{t}\exp({\bf b})\exp({\bf c})x&=\left(a_{t}\exp({\bf b})a_{-t}\right)\left(a_{t}\exp({\bf c})a_{-t}\right)a_{t}x\\
&=\exp\left(\operatorname{Ad}(\exp(t{\bf a})){\bf b}\right)\exp\left(\operatorname{Ad}(\exp(t{\bf a})){\bf c}\right)a_{t}x\\
&=\exp\left(e^{t\operatorname{ad}_{\bf a}}{\bf b}\right)\exp\left(e^{t\operatorname{ad}_{{\bf a}}}{\bf c}\right)a_{t}x\\
&=\exp\left(e^{t\lambda}{\bf b}\right)\exp\left({\bf c}\right)a_{t}x.\end{split}

The key observation is that
\lim_{t\to\infty}e^{t\lambda}=0
.
Thus, for a divergent positive sequence
({t_{n}})
,

(2.6)

\left(\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{t_{n}}g^{-}g^{0}x=y\right)\iff\left(\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{t_{n}}x=\left(\exp(-{\bf c}\right)y\right).

This immediately proves the proposition for the
\mathbf{Div}(0)
case.
For the
\mathbf{Div}(k)
case, we proceed by induction, assuming that the proposition has been proved for all
\mathbf{Div}(j)
with
j<k
.
Then, we can use the equivalence (
2.6
) along with the induction hypothesis that

\exp({\bf c})\mathbf{Div}(j)=\mathbf{Div}(j)\text{ for }j<k.

This completes the proof and shows the so-called
weak stable

invariance property of
\mathbf{Div}(k)
.
∎

Using the fact that
a_{t}
has the form of (
1.2
), one can check that the groups defined above are actually given by

H^{-}=\left\{\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}I_{m}&0\\
A&I_{n}\end{array}}\right]:A\in\text{Mat}_{n\times m}({\mathbb{R}})\right\},

H^{0}=\left\{\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}A&0\\
0&B\end{array}}\right]:A\in\text{Mat}_{m\times m}({\mathbb{R}}),B\in\text{Mat}_{n\times n}({\mathbb{R}})\right\}

and

H^{+}=\left\{\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}I_{m}&A\\
0&I_{n}\end{array}}\right]:A\in\text{Mat}_{m\times n}({\mathbb{R}})\right\}.

For future use, we use the notation

(2.7)

u_{A}:=\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}I_{m}&A\\
0&I_{n}\end{array}}\right]\text{ and }x_{A}:=u_{A}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}.

Our second invariance property arises from looking at the log-minima functions of a lattice.
We consider the supremum norm
\|\cdot\|
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Let
B(r)
denote the supremum norm ball with radius
r
and center at the origin. If
x
is a lattice,
the
d
successive minima functions of
x
with respect to the supremum norm are defined as

\lambda_{i}(x):=\inf\left\{r\in{\mathbb{R}}:x\cap B(r)\text{ contains $i$ independent vectors}\right\}.

The functions
\lambda_{i}:X_{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
are in fact continuous. We consider, for a fixed lattice
x\in X_{d}
and the flow
(a_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, the log-minima functions

(2.8)

f_{x}(t):{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d};\ t\mapsto\left(\log(\lambda_{1}(a_{t}x)),\dots,\log(\lambda_{d}(a_{t}x))\right).

We denote the components of
f_{x}
by
f_{1},\dots,f_{d}
.
We also need to use the supremum norm to compare such functions on subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}
. We use the convention that, for
E\subset{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
and
f,g:{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

(2.9)

\|f-g\|_{E}:=\sup_{t\in E}\|f(t)-g(t)\|=\sup_{t\in E}\left(\max_{i=1,\dots,d}|f_{i}(t)-g_{i}(t)|\right).

For repeated implicit use in the future, we note the following reformulation of Mahler’s compactness criterion:

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
x\in X_{d}
be a lattice with log-minima function
f_{x}=(f_{i})_{i=1,\dots,d}
. Then, for any sequence of positive times
(t_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, the sequence
(a_{t_{i}}x)_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\subset X_{d}
has a convergent subsequence if and only if

\limsup_{i\to\infty}f_{1}(t_{i})>-\infty.

Proof.

We simply note that, for any
M\in{\mathbb{R}}
, Mahler’s compactness criterion gives that

(2.10)

\left\{y\in X_{d}:\log(\lambda_{1}(y))\geq M\right\}

is a compact set.
Moreover, every compact subset of
X_{d}
is contained in a set of the form (
2.10
).
∎

Definition 2.3
.

We define an equivalence relation on the set of continuous functions
C({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0},{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
by writing
f\sim g
if

(2.11)

\|f-g\|_{{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}}<\infty.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
, let
x\in\mathbf{Div}(k)
and let
y
be another lattice in
X_{d}
.
Further, let
f_{x}
and
f_{y}
denote the log-minima functions for
x
and
y
respectively.
Then,

(2.12)

\left(f_{x}\sim f_{y}\right)\implies\left(y\in\mathbf{Div}(k)\right).

Proof.

The proposition consists of
{\mathbb{N}}
-many assertions and we use induction (on
k
) to prove them all.

Assume
k=0
. By the definition of
\mathbf{Div}(0)
and Lemma
2.2
,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\log(\lambda_{1}(a_{t}x))=-\infty.

Since
f_{x}\sim f_{y}
, we see that
\lim_{t\to\infty}\log(\lambda_{1}(a_{t}y))=-\infty
as well.
Again, an application of Lemma
2.2
shows that
y\in\mathbf{Div}(0)
.

Now assume
k>0
and suppose our claim holds for every
j<k
. Let
C
be a constant such that

(2.13)

\|f_{x}-f_{y}\|_{{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}}<C.

Given that
x\in\mathbf{Div}(k)
, we are to show that
y\in\mathbf{Div}(k)
. Suppose
(t_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is an unbounded positive sequence such that

(2.14)

\lim_{i\to\infty}a_{t_{i}}y=w

for some
w\in X_{d}
.
Using the joint continuity of the action of
(a_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on
X_{d}
, we may assume (perhaps after passing to a subsequence) that

(2.15)

\|f_{a_{t_{i}}y}-f_{w}\|_{[0,i]}\leq 1\ \text{ for every }i\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Since
\{a_{t_{i}}y\in X_{d}:i\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
is precompact and since
f_{x}\sim f_{y}
, Lemma
2.2
shows that a subsequence of
(a_{t_{i}}x)_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges to some
z\in\mathbf{Div}(j)
(for
j<k
).
Again, passing to a subsequence, we can assume in addition to (
2.15
) that

(2.16)

\|f_{a_{t_{i}}x}-f_{z}\|_{[0,i]}\leq 1.

Applying (
2.13
), (
2.15
) and (
2.16
), we get

(2.17)

\begin{split}\|f_{w}-f_{z}\|_{[0,i]}&=\|f_{w}-f_{a_{t_{i}}y}+f_{a_{t_{i}}y}-f_{a_{t_{i}}x}+f_{a_{t_{i}}x}-f_{z}\|_{[0,i]}\\
&\leq 1+C+1\end{split}

for every

.
Thus
\|f_{z}-f_{w}\|_{{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}}<\infty
and the induction hypothesis gives us that

. Thus,

D(y)\subset\bigcup_{j<k}\mathbf{Div}(j).

The argument to show that

is similar.
We are then finished in light of the characterization of

as in (

3.
Templates

Our proof of the non-emptiness and, furthermore, of a lower bound on the Hausdorff dimension of the set of
k
-divergent lattices is based on the results appearing in
[
DFSU
]
. In order to present and then use their results, we first need to define the notion of templates.

Definition 3.1

(Templates)
.

For any real interval
I
, we use
I_{\mathbb{Z}}
to denote its intersection with
{\mathbb{Z}}
.
An
m\times n
template is a continuous piecewise linear function
3
3

3

f
is piecewise linear means its domain is divided into a locally finite collection of closed intervals with disjoint interiors such that
f
is linear on each one.

f:[0,\infty)\xrightarrow{}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
(where
d=m+n
) that satisfies the following properties:

(a)

f_{1}\leq\dots\leq f_{d}
.

(b)

For all
i=1,\dots,d
, we have
-\frac{1}{n}\leq f^{\prime}_{i}\leq\frac{1}{m}
.

(c)

For all
j=0,\dots,d
and for every interval
I
such that
f_{j}<f_{j+1}
we have that the function

\sum_{0<i\leq j}f_{i}

is convex and piecewise linear on
I
with slopes in the set

Z(j):=\left\{\frac{L_{+}}{m}-\frac{L_{-}}{n}:L_{+}\in[0,m]_{{\mathbb{Z}}},\ L_{-}\in[0,n]_{\mathbb{Z}},\ L_{+}+L_{-}=j\right\}.

As a convention we set
f_{0}=-\infty,f_{d+1}=\infty
.

Templates are meant to model the log-minima functions of lattices. See
[
DFSU
, Lemma 32.7]
where a standard exterior algebra argument explains the relevance of condition (c) above.
We have the following theorem of fundamental importance:

Theorem 3.2

(Theorem 4.2
[
DFSU
]
)
.

For

let
f_{A}
denote the log-minima function defined for the lattice

defined in (

).
Then there exists a template
f
such that
f\sim f_{A}
.
Conversely, given a template
f
, there exists an
A\in\text{Mat}_{m\times n}({\mathbb{R}})
for which
f_{A}\sim f
.

Definition 3.3

(Score of a template)
.

Let
f
be a template and let
I
be an interval on which it is linear. An interval of equality for
f
on
I
is a set of integers
(p,q]_{\mathbb{Z}}
where
0\leq p<q\leq d
and where the components of

satisfy

f_{p}<f_{p+1}=\dots=f_{q}<f_{q+1}\text{ on }I.

We define
M_{\pm}(p,q,I)
to be the unique real numbers satisfying

(3.1)

M_{+}(p,q,I)+M_{-}(p,q,I)=q-p\ \text{ and }\ \frac{M_{+}(p,q,I)}{m}-\frac{M_{-}(p,q,I)}{n}=\sum_{i=p+1}^{q}f^{\prime}_{i}.

It follows from part (c) of Definition
3.1
that

are positive integers. (See also the footnote on page 24 of
[

]
.)
Further define

(3.2)

S_{+}(f,I):=\bigcup\left\{\left(p,p+M_{+}(p,q,I)\right]_{\mathbb{Z}}\subset(p,q]_{\mathbb{Z}}:(p,q]_{\mathbb{Z}}\text{ is an interval of equality}\right\}

and

(3.3)

S_{-}(f,I):=[1,d]_{\mathbb{Z}}\smallsetminus S_{+}(f,I).

We also have

(3.4)

\delta(f,I):=\#\left\{(i_{+},i_{-})\in S_{+}(f,I)\times S_{-}(f,I):i_{+}<i_{-}\right\}.

We take
\delta(f,t)
to be the piecewise constant function with value
\delta(f,I)
on
I
.
Finally,

(3.5)

\underline{\delta}(f):=\liminf_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\delta(f,t)dt.

We aim to compute the Hausdorff dimension of the set of matrices
A\in\text{Mat}_{m\times n}({\mathbb{R}})
for which
x_{A}
is
k
-divergent.
Given a template
f
, we define the set

(3.6)

\mathcal{D}(f):=\left\{A\in\text{Mat}_{m\times n}({\mathbb{R}}):f_{A}\sim f\right\}.

We say that a collection of templates
\mathcal{F}
is
closed under finite perturbation
if

(3.7)

\left(f\in\mathcal{F}\text{ and }f^{\prime}\text{ is a template with }f^{\prime}\sim f\right)\implies\left(f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}\right).

For such a
\mathcal{F}
, we define

(3.8)

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{F}):=\bigcup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\mathcal{D}(f)\subset\operatorname{Mat}_{m\times n}({\mathbb{R}}).

A tool to compute dimension is:

Theorem 3.4

(Theorem 4.3
[
DFSU
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a Borel
4
4

4

Under the compact-open topology.
collection of templates closed under finite perturbation.
Then we have that the Hausdorff dimension of

is

(3.9)

\sup\left\{\underline{\delta}(f):f\in\mathcal{F}\right\}.

We define a piecewise linear function

5.
Computations for a lower bound on dimension

We can now turn to computing the Hausdorff dimension of
\mathbf{Div}(k)
.
Let
\mathcal{F}^{k}
be the set of templates which are equivalent to
f^{(k)}
. It is clearly closed under finite perturbations and Borel in the compact-open topology on
C({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0},{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.
Each
A\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{F}^{k})
gives rise to a lattice
x_{A}
which is necessarily in
\mathbf{Div}(k)
, by Theorem
4.7
.
Thus, in light of Proposition
2.1
, computing the dimension of
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{F}^{k})\subset\text{Mat}_{m\times n}({\mathbb{R}})
will lead to a lower bound for the dimension of
\mathbf{Div}(k)\subset X_{d}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

The score
\underline{\delta}(f^{(k)})
is equal to
\delta_{m,n}
which is defined by

(5.1)

\delta_{m,n}:=mn-\frac{mn}{m+n}.

Proof.

We note that the first part of this computation has already been done in
[
DFSU
, Figure 4]
.
We proceed by induction and begin with the case
k=1
. We must contend with the function
\delta(f^{(1)},t)
and thus also the quantities
S_{+},M_{+}
and
M_{-}
from Definition
3.3
.
Since
f^{(1)}
is defined by repeating the same pattern over larger and larger scales, it suffices to compute
\delta(f^{(1)},t)
over the interval
[0,d]
.
First consider the interval
K_{1}:=[0,n]
. There are two intervals of equality here,
(0,1]_{\mathbb{Z}}
and
(1,d]_{\mathbb{Z}}
.
Solving the system of equations

M_{+}(0,1,K_{1})+M_{-}(0,1,K_{1})=1\ \text{ and }\ \frac{M_{+}(0,1,K_{1})}{m}-\frac{M_{-}(0,1,K_{1})}{n}=-\frac{1}{n},

we see that
M_{+}(0,1,K_{1})=0
.
Solving

M_{+}(1,d,K_{1})+M_{-}(1,d,K_{1})=d-1\ \text{ and }\ \frac{M_{+}(1,d,K_{1})}{m}-\frac{M_{-}(1,d,K_{1})}{n}=\frac{1}{n},

we see that
M_{+}(1,d,K_{1})=m
.
We thus have

S_{+}(f^{(1)},K_{1})=[2,m+1]_{\mathbb{Z}}\ \text{ and }\ S_{-}(f^{(1)},K_{1})=\{1\}\cup[m+2,m+n]_{\mathbb{Z}}.

This shows that

(5.2)

\delta(f^{(1)},t)=m(n-1)\ \text{ for }\ t\in[0,n].

We pause here to note that the interval
[m+2,m+n]_{\mathbb{Z}}
could be empty; for example, in the case when
m=n=1
.

Now consider the interval
K_{2}:=[n,d]
. We again have two intervals of equality,
(0,1]_{\mathbb{Z}}
and
(1,d]_{\mathbb{Z}}
.
One can perfom a similar computation as above to see that
M_{+}(0,1,K_{2})=1
and
M_{+}(1,d,K_{2})=m-1
.
We thus have

S_{+}(f^{(1)},K_{2})=[1,m]_{\mathbb{Z}}\ \text{ and }\ S_{-}(f^{(1)},K_{2})=[m+1,m+n]_{\mathbb{Z}}.

This shows that

(5.3)

\delta(f^{(1)},t)=mn\ \text{ for }\ t\in[n,d].

Recall that
f^{(1)}
was defined over a specific collection of intervals which we now call

\left\{I_{p}=[b_{p},b_{p+1}]:p\in{\mathbb{N}}\right\}.

Each
I_{p}
has length
pd
.
The definition in (
4.4
) and equations (

) and (

) show that, for any
q\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

(5.4)

\int_{I_{q}}\delta(f^{(1)},t)dt=qn(mn-m)+qm(mn).

Thus, for any
T\in I_{p}
with
p>1
, we see that

(5.5)

\begin{split}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\delta(f^{(1)},t)dt&=\frac{1}{T}\left(\int_{0}^{b_{p}}\delta(f^{(1)},t)dt+\int^{T}_{b_{p}}\delta(f^{(1)},t)dt\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{b_{p}}\delta(f^{(1)},t)dt+\frac{1}{T}\int^{T}_{b_{p}}\delta(f^{(1)},t)dt\\
&=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{q=1}^{p-1}\left(qmn^{2}+qm^{2}n-qmn\right)+\frac{1}{T}\int_{b_{p}}^{T}\delta(f^{(1)},t)dt\\
&=\frac{mn^{2}+m^{2}n-mn}{d}\cdot\frac{1}{T}\sum_{q=1}^{p-1}qd+\frac{1}{T}\int_{b_{p}}^{T}\delta(f^{(1)},t)dt\\
&=\delta_{m,n}\cdot\frac{1}{T}\sum_{q=1}^{p-1}qd+\frac{1}{T}\int_{b_{p}}^{T}\delta(f^{(1)},t)dt.\end{split}

Since

(5.6)

\frac{\text{length}\left(\bigcup^{p-1}_{q=1}\ I_{q}\right)}{\text{length}\left(\bigcup^{p}_{q=1}\ I_{q}\right)}=\frac{p-1}{p+1}

\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\delta(f^{(1)},t)dt=\delta_{m,n}.

Second, we leave it to the reader as an exercise to prove the limit

6.
Full escape of mass and an upper bound on dimension

Let
\mathcal{M}(X_{d})
and
\mathcal{P}(X_{d})
denote the set of Borel complex measures on
X_{d}
and the set of Borel probability measures on
X_{d}
respectively. Every open set in
X_{d}
is
\sigma
-compact and so, if we consider the
C^{*}
-algebra of continuous complex-valued functions vanishing at infinity,
C_{0}(X_{d})
, the Riesz Representation theorem
[
Rud87
, Theorem 6.19]
asserts that
\mathcal{M}(X_{d})
is in bijection with the continuous dual
C_{0}(X_{d})^{\vee}
, via

(6.1)

\nu\mapsto\left(f\mapsto\int fd\nu\right).

We induce the weak
∗
-topology on
\mathcal{M}(X_{d})
and the corresponding subset topology on
\mathcal{P}(X_{d})
.
Given
x\in X_{d}
, we define the family of probabilities
\{\mu_{x,T}\in\mathcal{P}(X_{d}):T>0\}
by

(6.2)

\int fd\mu_{x,T}:=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}f(a_{t}x)dt

where
f\in C_{0}(X_{d})
.
The following notion of divergence on average is crucial for us:

Definition 6.1

(cf.
[
KKLM17
]
Theorem 1.1)
.

We say
x\in X_{d}
is divergent on average if

\lim_{T\to\infty}\mu_{x,T}=0\ \text{ in }\mathcal{M}(X_{d}).

Theorem 6.2
.

Fix
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Let
x\in\mathbf{Div}(k)
. Then,
x
is divergent on average.

Proof.

Note that the Banach-Aloaglu theorem implies that every sequence
(\mu_{x,T_{i}})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
has a limit point in
\mathcal{M}(X_{d})
.
Thus, to prove the theorem it suffices to show that for any sequence
(T_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of divergent times,

(6.3)

\left(\lim_{i\to\infty}\mu_{x,T_{i}}=\mu\ \text{ in }\mathcal{M}(X_{d})\right)\implies\left(\mu=0\right).

Note that the limit measure
\mu
in the equation above is necessarily non-negative, and also invariant under the action of
(a_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
.
Thus, if we have a limit
\mu
which is nonzero, we may scale to obtain an
(a_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
-invariant probability measure
\nu
.
Assume we have such a
\nu
for sake of contradiction.

First observe that
\text{supp}(\nu)=\text{supp}(\mu)
and so any
y\in\text{supp}(\nu)
must belong to
D(x)
. In particular, there must be some
j<k
for which

y\in\mathbf{Div}(j).

On the other hand, we claim that we can find a
y\in\text{supp}(\nu)
for which there exists a sequence of unbounded positive times
(t_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
with

(6.4)

\lim_{t\to\infty}a_{t_{i}}y=y.

To see this, consider a countable base for the topology of
X_{d}
,
\{U_{l}\subset X_{d}:l\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
, and consider the sets

A_{l}:=\left\{z\in U_{l}:a_{j}z\notin U_{l}\text{ for all }j\in{\mathbb{N}}\right\}.

Poincare recurrence shows that
\nu\left(\cup A_{l}\right)=0
.

Thus, we can choose any
y\in\text{supp}(\nu)\smallsetminus\cup A_{l}
and get the claim in (
6.4
).
This contradicts the fact that
y\in\mathbf{Div}(j)
.
∎

Applying
[
KKLM17
, Theorem 1.1]
, we immediately get:

Corollary 6.3
.

For each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the Hausdorff dimension of
\mathbf{Div}(k)
is bounded above by

\dim(X_{d})-\frac{mn}{m+n}.
