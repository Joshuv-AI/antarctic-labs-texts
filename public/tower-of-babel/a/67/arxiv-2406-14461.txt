Engineering interactions by collective coupling of atom pairs to cavity photons for entanglement generation
Sankalp Sharma, Jan Chwedeńczuk, Tomasz Wasak

Abstract
Engineering atom-atom interactions is essential both for controlling novel phases of matter and for efficient preparation of many-body entangled states, which are key resources in quantum communication, computation, and metrology. In this work, we propose a scheme to tailor these interactions by coupling driven atom pairs to optical cavity photons via a molecular state in the dispersive regime, resulting in an effective photon-field-dependent potential. As an illustrative example, by analyzing the quantum Fisher information, we show that such induced interactions can generate robust many-body entanglement in two-mode ultracold bosons in an optical cavity. By tuning the photon-induced interactions through the cavity drive, we identify conditions for preparing highly entangled states on timescales that mitigate decoherence due to photon loss. Our results show that entanglement formation rate scales strongly with both photon and atom number, dramatically reducing the timescale compared to bare atomic interactions. We also identify an optimal measurement for exploiting the metrological potential of the atomic state in an interferometric protocol with significant photon losses, saturating the quantum Cramer-Rao lower bound. Furthermore, we show that despite these losses the atomic state exhibits strong Bell correlations. Our results pave the way for engineering atom-atom interactions to study novel phases of light and matter in hybrid atom-photon systems, as well as for tailoring complex quantum states for new quantum technology protocols and fundamental tests of quantum mechanics.



Introduction
— Scalable many-body entangled states are essential resources for quantum technologies of the future, such as quantum communication, computation and metrology. The generation of these states relies on precisely controlled interactions between the particles. They should enable fast creation of quantum correlations in order to mitigate the effects of decoherence, which is a fundamental obstacle even in most advanced equipments operating in well-isolated environments. In degenerate quantum gases, for instance, the formation of entanglement can be significantly accelerated by exploiting Feshbach resonances which increase the rate of two-body collisions

A promising approach for generating entangled states with tailored properties is based on cavity quantum electrodynamics (QED) with ultracold atoms. This platform is particularly advantageous because it enables independent control over the light and matter components of the hybrid system. Such control allows for a strong coupling regime of the atom-photon interaction and the engineering of entangled states with specific desired properties
[

Moreover, cavity QED systems can be probed in real time via transmission spectra

These unique possibilities critically depend on tunable-range photon-mediated atom-atom interactions

The quantum dynamics of self-organization of atoms in an optical cavity was shown to create strong atom-photon entanglement that is, however, fragile to photon losses

Recently, such photon-induced interactions in a cavity have been used experimentally to generate spin- and momentum-correlated atom pairs in a Bose gas

This mechanism enabled a real-time weakly destructive probe of the pair correlation function of the atoms, and it holds promise for the development of novel methods to engineer atomic interaction potentials.

Here, we exploit this light-matter coupling to derive the form of the photon-induced atom-atom interactions under different system driving schemes. Based on this approach, we propose a novel method for the fast generation of many-body entangled states on a time scale controlled by the strength of the drive. The setup consists of ultracold bosons trapped in a double-well potential immersed in an optical cavity. Cavity-assisted bosonic Josephson junction
—
To illustrate how the interaction from Eq. (
4
) influences the buildup of atomic correlations, we consider a bosonic Josephson junction (BJJ) with
N
atoms, without transverse driving, i.e.,
\eta_{\mathrm{t}}=0
. We expand the field operator
\hat{\Psi}(\mathbf{r})
into two modes,
\hat{b}_{1}
and
\hat{b}_{2}

1

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}=-\tilde{\Delta}_{c}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}-J\hat{J}_{x}+U\hat{J}_{z}^{2}+2W_{0}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\hat{J}_{z}^{2}
, where the last term originates from Eq. (
4
) and the detuning is renormalized as
\tilde{\Delta}_{c}=\Delta_{c}-W_{0}N^{2}/2

[
29
]
; by
\hat{J}_{i}
, where

\hat{b}_{1/2}

[
34
,
29

is the tunneling amplitude, and

is the on-site energy while

determines the strength of the photon-induced atomic interactions

29

Generation of many-body entanglement in BJJ
—We now demonstrate that the effective Hamiltonian,
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}
, generates strong entanglement between atoms. To illustrate this, we consider a scenario analogous to the one-axis twisting (OAT) protocol, which has been successfully employed to create many-body entangled states in two-mode atomic configurations
[
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
]
. Here, all atoms are initially placed in a single mode,
and then the bare Josephson oscillation (governed by tunneling and in the absence of other terms) creates a separable state in which all
atoms are in coherent spin state
[
34
]
with a symmetric superposition of the two modes, namely,

\mbox{$|\psi_{0}\rangle$}\propto(\hat{b}_{1}^{\dagger}+\hat{b}_{2}^{\dagger})^{N}\mbox{$|\mathrm{vac}\rangle$}
.

Next, the Josephson term is suppressed by raising the inter-well barrier (and thus setting
J=0
) and the atomic interactions correlate the particles.
Without the cavity, this procedure is the standard OAT, because the Hamiltonian
\propto\hat{J}_{z}^{2}
reduces atomic fluctuations, creating spin-squeezing
[
40
,
41
,
27
]
.

The standard OAT process is governed by two time scales.
First, after
t\propto N^{-2/3}
, the Gaussian spin-squeezed states are created which yield sub-shot-noise sensitivity
[
29
]
, i.e.,
\Delta\theta<1/\sqrt{N}
, of phase estimation in the Mach-Zehnder interferometer (MZI)
[
36
,
42
]
. Later, when
t\propto N^{-1/2}
the system enters a non-Gaussian regime
[
43
,
44
,
45
]
, where
the many-body entangled states have to be characterized by the quantum Fisher information (QFI)
[
46
]
, i.e.,

\displaystyle F_{Q}=2\sum_{i,j}\frac{\left(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}\right)^{2}}{\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}}\left|\langle\Psi_{i}|\hat{J}_{y}|\Psi_{j}\rangle\right|^{2},

(5)

which sets
the Cramer-Rao lower bound (CRLB):
\Delta\theta\geqslant F_{Q}^{-1/2}

\hat{J}_{y}
is the generator of the MZI transformation
[
34
,
36
]
, and the sum runs over the spectrum of the atomic density matrix, i.e.,
\hat{\varrho}_{A}=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}|\Psi_{i}\rangle\!\langle\Psi_{i}|

47
]
.

We now demonstrate that even in the presence of photon losses, the light-induced atom-atom interaction from Eq. (
4

can drastically accelerate the generation of strong entanglement compared to the above-mentioned rates characterising the OAT procedure.
Initially, atoms are in a state

Impact of photon losses
— We now investigate the impact of photon lossess on atomic entanglement. The QFI in Eq. (
8
) is given by the average number of photons, suggesting that on the time scale considered,
F_{Q}
is weakly sensitive to photonic decoherence.

We model the photon losses by the Lindblad master equation
[
53
]
governing the evolution of the system:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\hat{\varrho}=-i\left[\hat{H}_{\rm eff},\hat{\varrho}\right]+\kappa\left(\hat{a}\hat{\varrho}a^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\hat{\varrho},\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\right\}\right),

(10)

where
\kappa
is the loss rate and
\{\,,\,\}
stands for the anticommutator
[
54
]
.
For the same initial state as in the lossless case, we now use
\kappa\tau_{Q}=0.1,1,10
, so that losses span three orders of magnitude of the scale
\tau_{Q}
that sets the entanglement build-up time. Fig.
2

\kappa\tau_{Q}\lesssim 1
,
F_{Q}
remains roughly unaffected
[
29
]
.
For the strong loss
\kappa\tau_{Q}\gg 1
, the initial growth slows down — the leakage of photons leaves the system in a bare OAT mode.
Decoherence in the photonic subspace does not hinder the build-up of atomic entanglement, as Heisenberg scaling of
F_{Q}\sim N^{2}
is preserved even when
\kappa\tau_{Q}\sim 1
. However, it does influence the timescale over which entanglement is generated. Despite significant losses, the cavity-assisted OAT protocol remains effective in producing strong, genuine multi-particle entanglement. Moreover, after the entanglement is generated, the atomic state can be exploited for metrological purposes. Our calculations for
\kappa\tau_{Q}\sim 1
show that
F_{Q}
remains large over a long timescale after the pump is switched off at

, despite the rapid depletion of cavity photons
[
29
]
.

Finally, we note that recent observations of pair-polaritons in the strong coupling regime
[
26
]
for long observation times of the order of
0.5

Also, since the photons are far off-resonance from the molecular transition, the molecular population is significantly suppressed minimizing the heating of the atomic gas due to spontaneous emission. We expect that photon loss from the cavity is the dominating source of noise, and we leave the impact of the heating for future research.

Summary and conclusions
—
We have investigated a system’s dynamics with interactions induces by coupling photons to pairs of atoms. Based on the example of the bosonic Josephson junction immersed into an optical cavity, in which photons are pumped through the cavity mirrors, we have shown how the strength of the atom-atom interaction, resulting from single-photon–single-pair coupling, can be controlled by injecting photons into the cavity. This allows us to fine-tune the time required for atoms to become entangled without having to rely on the potentially noisy magnetic fields that
are typically used to control the strength of the atom-atom interaction. Our analytical results show that the strength of entanglement, as measured by the quantum Fisher information, depends only on the intensity of the cavity light and not on the precise structure of the photonic state. The configuration is, therefore, robust to noise from photon losses.
We analytically predicted the characteristic time-scale for the generation of entanglement, which for realistic parameters can be orders of magnitude shorter than of bare atom-atom collisions. In turn, the short time scale implies that photon losses during the preparation of the atomic state can be overcome, since strong entanglement can be generated even with large losses. Additionally, we found the optimal measurement which saturates the quantum Cramer-Rao lower bound. Also, the atoms exhibit strong nonlocal many-body Bell correlations, which demonstrates that the photon-induced interaction can lead to strongly entangled state that can serve for quantum technological purposes as well as for fundamental tests of quantum mechanics
[
65
,
59
]
.

As a result, many-body entangled states of atoms, with their enormous potential for quantum applications, could be generated very quickly and reliably. In this way, one obstacle to the implementation of quantum solutions can be overcome.
Our setup can be used in future quantum sensors or other devices where controllable and scalable many-body entanglement is required.

We have demonstrated that the photon–atom-pair coupling adds an important ingredient to the techniques of engineering the interactions
[
66
,
67
,
68
,
10
]
.
Due to the vanishing tunneling rate, the dynamics in the protocol do not generate nonclassical light states, see SM
[
29
]
.
However, since the photon-assisted atom-atom interaction depends in general on the quantum state of cavity photons, an intriguing direction for future research would be to explore the interplay of atomic and photonic nonclassicality, and how the non-classical nature of photonic states could give rise to novel quantum phases of matter and light
[
69
]
.
For instance, since the interaction is position-dependent, it may lead to instabilities signalling novel types of self-ordering of atoms driven by correlations
[
70
,
48
,
10
]
, such as supersolidity
[
71
]
. Finally, a promising research direction is to explore the potential for generating
n
-body photon-mediated interactions with
n>2

[

.

Appendix A
Dynamics of the system

A.1
Derivation of the effective Hamiltonian

To derive the effective Hamiltonian without the transversal pumping (
\eta_{t}=0
), we first take the cavity mode function in the form of a standing-wave Gaussian, i.e.,

f(\mathbf{r})=\cos(ky){e^{-\left(x^{2}+z^{2}\right)/(2\sigma^{2})}},

(11)

where
k=\omega_{c}/c
is the wave number of the cavity mode,
L
is the distance between the mirrors, and
\sigma
is the width of the Gaussian profile in the
x
-
z
plane.
Furthermore, we switch to a frame rotating with
\omega_{L}
as follows

\displaystyle\hat{a}=\hat{\tilde{a}}e^{-i\omega_{L}t}

(12)

\displaystyle\psi=\tilde{\psi}

(13)

\displaystyle\hat{\Psi}_{\Delta}=\hat{\tilde{\psi}}_{\Delta}e^{-i\omega_{L}t}.

(14)

In this scenario, we obtain the set of Heisenberg equations, where the total Hamiltonian is
\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{A}\!+\!\hat{H}_{\Delta}\!+\!\hat{H}_{C}\!+\!\hat{H}_{CA}\!+\!\hat{H}_{P}

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\hat{\tilde{a}}=\left[\hat{\tilde{a}},\hat{H}-\omega_{L}\hat{\tilde{a}}^{\dagger}\hat{\tilde{a}}\right]=-\Delta_{c}\hat{\tilde{a}}-i\eta+i\Omega_{R}\int d^{3}rf(r)\left(\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}^{2}_{g}(r)\right)^{\dagger}\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{\Delta}(r)

(15a)

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{g}(r)=\left[\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{g}(r),\hat{H}\right]=\left[\frac{-\nabla^{2}}{2m}+V(r)\right]\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{g}(r)+g\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{g}^{\dagger}(r)\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{g}(r)\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{g}(r)+2i\Omega_{R}f(r)\hat{\tilde{a}}^{\dagger}\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{g}^{\dagger}(r)\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{\Delta}(r)

(15b)

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{\Delta}(r)=\left[\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{\Delta},\hat{H}-\omega_{L}\int d^{3}r\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{\Delta}^{\dagger}(r)\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{\Delta}(r)\right]=\left[\frac{-\nabla^{2}}{2M}+V(r)-\Delta_{A}\right]\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{\Delta}(r)-i\Omega_{R}f(r)\hat{\tilde{a}}\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{g}^{2}(r)

(15c)

The above equations of motions can be derived by commuting the corresponding operators with the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}^{\prime}
,
where

\hat{H}^{\prime}=\hat{H}-\omega_{L}\hat{\tilde{a}}^{\dagger}\hat{\tilde{a}}-\omega_{L}\int d^{3}r\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{\Delta}^{\dagger}(r)\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{\Delta}(r).

(16)

In the limit, when the atomic detuning
\Delta_{A}=\omega_{L}-\omega_{B}
is much larger than the other characteristic frequency scales of the system, the population of the molecular state is small and follows the ground state adiabatically. The molecular excited-field operator thus can be eliminated.
Under these conditions the square bracket in Eq. (
15c
) is dominated by the detuning, and its stationary value can approximate the molecular-state field operator:

\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{\Delta}\simeq-i\frac{\Omega_{R}f(r)}{\Delta_{A}}\hat{\tilde{a}}\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{g}^{2}(r).

(17)

The resulting dynamics of the photon field and the ground-state atoms are given by

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\hat{\tilde{a}}=-\Delta_{c}\hat{\tilde{a}}-i\eta+\frac{\Omega_{R}^{2}}{\Delta_{A}}\hat{\tilde{a}}\int d^{3}rf^{2}(r)\left(\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}^{2}_{g}(r)\right)^{\dagger}\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{g}^{2}(r),

(18a)

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{g}(r)=\left[\frac{-\nabla^{2}}{2m}+V(r)\right]\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{g}(r)+g\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{g}^{\dagger}(r)\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{g}(r)\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{g}(r)+2\frac{\Omega_{R}^{2}}{\Delta_{A}}f^{2}(r)\hat{\tilde{a}}^{\dagger}\hat{\tilde{a}}\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{g}^{\dagger}(r)\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{g}^{2}(r),

(18b)

where we have neglected interactions between molecular state atoms since the population of the molecular state is much smaller than that of the ground state.

The approximate equations of motions given in Eq. (
18a
) and (
18b
) can be derived from the effective Hamiltonian, which reads

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}

\displaystyle=-\Delta_{c}\hat{\tilde{a}}^{\dagger}\hat{\tilde{a}}-i\eta\left(\hat{\tilde{a}}^{\dagger}-\hat{\tilde{a}}\right)+\int d^{3}r\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{g}^{\dagger}(r)\left[\frac{-\nabla^{2}}{2m}+V(r)\right]\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{g}(r)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int d^{3}r\,g\,\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{g}^{\dagger}(r)\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{g}^{\dagger}(r)\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{g}(r)\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{g}(r)+U_{0}\hat{\tilde{a}}^{\dagger}\hat{\tilde{a}}\int d^{3}rf^{2}(r)\left(\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}^{2}_{g}(r)\right)^{\dagger}\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{g}^{2},

(19)

where with
U_{0}=\frac{\Omega_{R}^{2}}{\Delta_{A}}
we denote the strength of the atom-atom interaction per photon induced by cavity photons.

As a result of the adiabatic elimination of the molecular state, atom-photon interaction stems from the pair-photon scattering by the intermediate process of molecule creation. As a consequence, a new optical potential has appeared with position dependence
f^{2}(r)
and an effective amplitude
U_{0}\hat{\tilde{a}}^{\dagger}\hat{\tilde{a}}
which depends on the state of the photon in the cavity and on detuning from molecular state. Since these parameters can be controlled in the experiments, new accessible types of interactions are allowed inside the cavity.

A.2
Two-mode approximation for bosonic atoms

In order to arrive at a simplified, two-mode description of the system, we assume that the double-well potential from Eq. (1) defines localized states
w_{1}(x)
and
w_{2}(x)
centered around the minima of the potential
V_{\mathrm{DW}}(x)
. The atomic field operator truncated to the lowest lying modes is then approximated as

\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{g}(\mathbf{r})\approx\phi_{1}(\mathbf{r})\hat{b}_{1}+\phi_{2}(\mathbf{r})\hat{b}_{2},

(20)

where the localized wavefunctions are

\phi_{i}(\mathbf{r})=w_{i}(x)\frac{e^{-\left(y^{2}+z^{2}\right)/\left(2l_{H}^{2}\right)}}{\sqrt{\pi}l_{H}},

(21)

with
\hat{b}_{i}
,
i\in\{1,2\}
, the bosonic annihilation operators of the localized modes, and
l_{H}=\sqrt{{\hbar}/({m\omega_{H}})}
is the harmonic oscillator length of the potential in
y
-
z
plane.

By substituting Eq. (
20
) into the effective Hamiltonian from Eq. (
19
), and since the double well is symmetric, one arrives at the light-matter Hamiltonian

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx\hat{H}_{L}+\hat{H}_{A}+\hat{H}_{AL},

(22)

where the bare cavity field with pump is described by

\hat{H}_{L}=-\Delta_{c}\hat{\tilde{a}}^{\dagger}\hat{\tilde{a}}-i\eta\left(\hat{\tilde{a}}^{\dagger}-\hat{\tilde{a}}\right)

(23)

The atoms are now described by

\hat{H}_{A}=\epsilon\hat{N}_{A}-J\left(\hat{b}_{1}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{2}+\hat{b}_{2}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{1}\right)+\frac{U}{2}\left(\hat{b}_{1}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{1}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{1}\hat{b}_{1}+\hat{b}_{2}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{2}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{2}\hat{b}_{2}\right)

(24)

where
\hat{N}_{A}=\hat{b}_{1}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{1}+\hat{b}_{2}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{2}
is the total number of atoms. The parameter
\epsilon
is the on-site energy of a single well,
J
represents the tunneling amplitude, and
U
is the on-site interaction energy to the bare ground-state atom interactions.
The dispersive interaction between the atomic pairs and the cavity photons takes the following form:

\hat{H}_{AL}=W_{0}\hat{\tilde{a}}^{\dagger}\hat{\tilde{a}}\left[\hat{b}_{1}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{1}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{1}\hat{b}_{1}+\hat{b}_{2}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{2}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{2}\hat{b}_{2}\right],

(25)

where
W_{0}
is the cavity-induced atom-atom coupling strength per photon.
The new parameters are the following:

\displaystyle\epsilon=\omega_{H}+\int dxw_{j}^{*}(x)\left[-\frac{1}{2m}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+V_{\mathrm{DW}}(x)\right]w_{j}(x)

(26a)

\displaystyle J=-\int dxw_{1}^{*}(x)\left[-\frac{1}{2m}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+V_{\mathrm{DW}}(x)\right]w_{2}(x),

(26b)

\displaystyle U=\frac{g}{2\pi l_{H}^{2}}\int dx\left|w_{j}(x)\right|^{4}.

(26c)

\displaystyle W_{0}=\frac{U_{0}\left(1+e^{-\frac{k^{2}l_{H}^{2}}{2}}\right)}{L\pi^{2}\sigma l_{H}^{2}\sqrt{2\left(l_{H}^{2}+2\sigma^{2}\right)}}\int dx\lvert\omega_{j}(x)\rvert^{4}e^{\frac{-x^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}}.

(26d)

Finally, by means of the Schwinger representation of the angular momentum operators

\displaystyle\hat{J}_{x}=\frac{b_{1}^{\dagger}b_{2}+b_{2}^{\dagger}b_{1}}{2},\ \ \hat{J}_{y}=\frac{b_{1}^{\dagger}b_{2}-b_{2}^{\dagger}b_{1}}{2i},\ \ \hat{J}_{z}=\frac{b_{1}^{\dagger}b_{1}-b_{2}^{\dagger}b_{2}}{2},

(27)

we obtain the effective BJJ Hamiltonian provided in the main text.

A.3
The atomic density operator

The first step of the derivation of the atomic density operator is to rotate out the pump term. This is done by means of the displacement operator

\displaystyle\hat{D}(\hat{\beta})=e^{\hat{\beta}(\hat{a}-\hat{a}^{\dagger})},\ \ \ \hat{\beta}=\eta\hat{\omega}^{-1},\ \ \ [\hat{\beta},\hat{\omega}]=0,

(28)

where
\hat{\omega}=-\Delta_{c}\hat{\mathds{1}}_{A}+2W_{0}\hat{J}_{z}^{2}
and
\hat{\mathds{1}}_{A}
is the identity in the atomic subspace.
The action of this operator onto the photon-number operator is

\displaystyle\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\hat{\beta})\hat{\omega}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\hat{D}(\hat{\beta})=\hat{\omega}(\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\hat{\beta})(\hat{a}-\hat{\beta})=\hat{\omega}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}-\hat{\omega}\hat{\beta}(\hat{a}^{\dagger}+\hat{a})+\omega\beta^{2}=\hat{\omega}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}-\eta(\hat{a}^{\dagger}+\hat{a})+\hat{\omega}\hat{\beta}^{2}.

(29)

Hence, by means of this expression, our starting Hamiltonian from Eq. (5) of the main text (note that we set
J=0
) can be written as

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\hat{\beta})\hat{\omega}\hat{n}\hat{D}(\hat{\beta})+U\hat{J}_{z}^{2}-\eta\hat{\beta}.

(30)

Therefore, and since
\hat{\beta}
commutes with the remaining operators, we obtain the evolution operator in the form

\displaystyle\hat{U}(t)=\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\hat{\beta})e^{-i\hat{\omega}t\hat{n}}\hat{D}(\hat{\beta})e^{-it(U\hat{J}_{z}^{2}-\eta\hat{\beta})}.

(31)

We now consider the action of this operator on a generic atom-light input state in the form

\displaystyle\hat{\varrho}(0)=\sum_{nn^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}\sum_{mm^{\prime}=-N/2}^{N/2}\varrho_{nn^{\prime}}^{mm^{\prime}}|nm\rangle\!\langle n^{\prime}m^{\prime}|,

(32)

where
n/n^{\prime}
stands for the photon number and
m/m^{\prime}
are the eigenvalues of
J_{z}
and
\mbox{$|nm\rangle$}=\mbox{$|n\rangle$}_{L}\otimes\mbox{$|m\rangle$}_{A}
.

The action of the last (atom-only) term of the evolution operator is straightforward, namely

\displaystyle e^{-it(U\hat{J}_{z}^{2}-\eta\hat{\beta})}\mbox{$|nm\rangle$}=e^{-it(Um^{2}-\eta\beta_{m})}\mbox{$|nm\rangle$},\ \ \ \beta_{m}=\frac{\eta}{-\Delta_{c}+2W_{0}m^{2}}.

(33)

Moreover, also in the first part of the evolution operator
\hat{\beta}
can be replaced by
\beta_{m}
and
\hat{\omega}
by
\omega_{m}
.
Hence, our goal now is to calculate

\displaystyle\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\beta_{m})e^{-i\omega_{m}t\hat{n}}\hat{D}(\beta_{m})|n\rangle\!\langle n^{\prime}|\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\beta_{m})e^{i\omega_{m}t\hat{n}}\hat{D}(\beta_{m}).

(34)

To this end, we notice that

\displaystyle\mbox{$|\psi_{1}\rangle$}\equiv\hat{D}(\beta_{m})\mbox{$|n\rangle$}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n!}}\hat{D}(\beta_{m})(\hat{a}^{\dagger})^{n}\mbox{$|0\rangle$}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n!}}\hat{D}(\beta_{m})(\hat{a}^{\dagger})^{n}\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\beta_{m})\hat{D}(\beta_{m})\mbox{$|0\rangle$}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n!}}(\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\beta_{m})^{n}\mbox{$|\beta_{m}\rangle$},

(35)

where
|\beta_{m}\rangle
stands for a coherent state of light with the amplitude
\beta_{m}
. Next is the action of the phase-factor that gives

\displaystyle\mbox{$|\psi_{2}\rangle$}\equiv e^{-i\omega_{m}t\hat{n}}\mbox{$|\psi_{1}\rangle$}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n!}}e^{-i\omega_{m}t\hat{n}}(\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\beta_{m})^{n}e^{i\omega_{m}t\hat{n}}e^{-i\omega_{m}t\hat{n}}\mbox{$|\beta_{m}\rangle$}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n!}}(\hat{a}^{\dagger}e^{-i\omega_{m}t}-\beta_{m})^{n}\mbox{$|\beta_{m}e^{-i\omega_{m}t}\rangle$}.

(36)

Finally, the second displacement operator, in analogy to Eq. (
35
), yields

\displaystyle\mbox{$|\psi_{3}\rangle$}\equiv\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\beta_{m})\mbox{$|\psi_{2}\rangle$}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n!}}\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\beta_{m})(\hat{a}^{\dagger}e^{-i\omega_{m}t}-\beta_{m})^{n}\hat{D}(\beta_{m})\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\beta_{m})\mbox{$|\beta_{m}e^{-i\omega_{m}t}\rangle$}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n!}}((\hat{a}^{\dagger}e^{-i\omega_{m}t}+\beta_{m})-\beta_{m})^{n}\hat{D}(-\beta_{m})\hat{D}(\beta_{m}e^{-i\omega_{m}t})\mbox{$|0\rangle$}.

(37)

Using the property
\hat{D}(x)\hat{D}(y)=\hat{D}(x+y)e^{\frac{1}{2}(xy^{*}-x^{*}y)}
, we obtain the final expression

\displaystyle\mbox{$|\psi_{3}\rangle$}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n!}}e^{-i\beta^{2}_{m}\sin(\omega_{m}t)}((\hat{a}^{\dagger}e^{-i\omega_{m}t}+\beta_{m})-\beta_{m})^{n}\mbox{$|\beta_{m}e^{-i\omega_{m}t}-\beta_{m}\rangle$}.

(38)

In order to simplify the above expression, we introduce

\gamma_{m}(t)\equiv\beta_{m}\big(e^{-i\omega_{m}t}-1\big),

(39)

which then results in

\displaystyle\mbox{$|\psi_{3}\rangle$}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n!}}e^{-i\beta^{2}_{m}\sin(\omega_{m}t)}(\hat{a}^{\dagger}e^{-i\omega_{m}t}+\gamma_{m})^{n}\mbox{$|\gamma_{m}\rangle$}.

(40)

In the next step, we observe that

\displaystyle(\hat{a}^{\dagger}e^{-i\omega_{m}t}+\gamma_{m})^{n}\mbox{$|\gamma_{m}\rangle$}=\hat{D}(\gamma_{m})\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\gamma_{m})(\hat{a}^{\dagger}e^{-i\omega_{m}t}+\gamma_{m})^{n}\hat{D}(\gamma_{m})\mbox{$|0\rangle$}=\hat{D}(\gamma_{m})((\hat{a}^{\dagger}+\gamma^{*})e^{-i\omega_{m}t}+\gamma_{m})^{n}\mbox{$|0\rangle$}.

(41)

Using the identity

\displaystyle\gamma^{*}e^{-i\omega_{m}t}+\gamma_{m}=\beta_{m}-\beta_{m}e^{-i\omega_{m}t}-\beta_{m}+\beta_{m}e^{-i\omega_{m}t}=0

(42)

we have

\displaystyle(\hat{a}^{\dagger}e^{-i\omega_{m}t}+\gamma_{m})^{n}\mbox{$|\gamma_{m}\rangle$}=\hat{D}(\gamma_{m})\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\gamma_{m})(\hat{a}^{\dagger}e^{-i\omega_{m}t}+\gamma_{m})^{n}\hat{D}(\gamma_{m})\mbox{$|0\rangle$}=\hat{D}(\gamma_{m})(\hat{a}^{\dagger})^{n}e^{-i\omega_{m}nt}\mbox{$|0\rangle$}.

(43)

Hence

\displaystyle\mbox{$|\psi_{3}\rangle$}=e^{-i\beta^{2}_{m}\sin(\omega_{m}t)}e^{-i\omega_{m}nt}\hat{D}(\gamma_{m})\mbox{$|n\rangle$}.

(44)

This way, after all these manipulations, we arrive at the complete expression for the atom-photon density matrix

\displaystyle\hat{\varrho}(t)=\hat{U}(t)\hat{\varrho}(0)\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{nn^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}\sum_{mm^{\prime}=-N/2}^{N/2}\varrho_{nn^{\prime}}^{mm^{\prime}}e^{-i(\beta^{2}_{m}\sin(\omega_{m}t)-\beta^{2}_{m^{\prime}}\sin(\omega_{m^{\prime}}t))}e^{-i(\omega_{m}n-\omega_{m^{\prime}}m^{\prime})t}e^{-iU(m^{2}-m^{\prime 2})t}\times

\displaystyle\times\,e^{-i\eta t(\beta_{m}-\beta_{m^{\prime}})}\hat{D}(\gamma_{m})|nm\rangle\!\langle n^{\prime}m^{\prime}|\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\gamma_{m}).

(45)

The results of the main text uses this atomic density operator, which is obtained by assuming that initially atoms and photons form a separable state pure state, i.e.,

\displaystyle\varrho_{nn^{\prime}}^{mm^{\prime}}=C_{m}C^{*}_{m^{\prime}}d_{n}d^{*}_{n^{\prime}}

(46)

and tracing-out the photonic degrees of freedom, which gives

\displaystyle\hat{\varrho}_{A}(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\langle n|\psi(t)\rangle\langle\psi(t)|n\rangle=\sum_{mm^{\prime}}C_{m}(t)C^{*}_{m^{\prime}}(t)|m\rangle\!\langle m^{\prime}|e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\left|\gamma_{m}\right|^{2}+\left|\gamma_{m^{\prime}}\right|^{2})}e^{\gamma_{m}\gamma^{*}_{m^{\prime}}},

(47)

with

\displaystyle C_{m}(t)=C_{m}e^{-i\beta^{2}_{m}\sin(\omega_{m}t)}e^{-iUm^{2}t}e^{-i\eta t\beta_{m}},

(48)

and
\gamma_{m}(t)
is given in Eq. (
39
).

Appendix B
Derivation of the QCRB

B.1
Verifying the
{\rm Tr}\left[(\hat{\varrho}_{A}(t)\hat{J}_{y})^{2}\right]=0
condition

For the generator
\hat{J}_{y}
of transformation, the QFI is given by

F_{Q}=2\sum_{i,j}\frac{(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j})^{2}}{\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}}|\langle\Psi_{i}|\hat{J}_{y}|\Psi_{j}\rangle|^{2},

(49)

where the eigen-decomposition of the atomic state is
\hat{\varrho}_{A}=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}|\Psi_{i}\rangle\langle\Psi_{i}|
.
In general, the following inequality holds:

F_{Q}\leqslant 4\big(\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{\varrho}_{A}\hat{J}_{y}^{2}]-\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{\varrho}_{A}\hat{J}_{y}]^{2}\big).

(50)

According to Ref.
[
46
]
, the sufficient and necessary condition for the equality is

\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}|\langle{\Psi_{i}}|\hat{J}_{y}-\langle\hat{J}_{y}\rangle|\Psi_{j}\rangle|^{2}=0

(51)

for all
i
and
j
; here
\langle\hat{J}_{y}\rangle={\rm Tr}\left[\varrho_{A}\hat{J}_{y}\right]
.
Now, we observe that in Eq. (
51
), for all
i
and
j
the terms are non-negative. Therefore, after summation we obtain an equvalent statement, that Eq. (
51
) is satisfied if and only if

\sum_{i,j}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}|\langle{\Psi_{i}}|\hat{J}_{y}-\langle\hat{J}_{y}\rangle|\Psi_{j}\rangle|^{2}=0

(52)

is satisfied. Using the hermiticity of the operator
\hat{J}_{y}-\langle\hat{J}_{y}\rangle
and the decomposition of the state
\hat{\varrho}_{A}
, the sum can be rewritten as

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}\left[(\hat{\varrho}_{A}(t)\hat{J}_{y})^{2}\right]=0.

(53)

This is the condition for the equality in Eq. (
50
).

Below, we demonstrate that the condition from Eq. (
53
) is satisfied. With the symmetry of the initial state, we obtain
\langle\hat{J}_{y}(t)\rangle=0
. These two observations imply that that the QCRB from Eq. (5) simplifies to the formula

\displaystyle F_{Q}=4{\rm Tr}\left[\hat{\varrho}_{A}(t)\hat{J}_{y}^{2}\right].

(54)

To proceed with showing Eq. (
53
), we use the expression for the atomic density matrix

\displaystyle\hat{\varrho}_{A}(t)=\sum_{mm^{\prime}=-N/2}^{N/2}C_{m}C_{m^{\prime}}^{*}e^{-i(\beta^{2}_{m}\sin(\omega_{m}t)-\beta^{2}_{m^{\prime}}\sin(\omega_{m^{\prime}}t))}e^{-iU(m^{2}-m^{\prime 2})t}e^{+i\eta t(\beta_{m}-\beta_{m^{\prime}})}e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\left|\gamma_{m}\right|^{2}+\left|\gamma_{m^{\prime}}\right|^{2})}e^{\gamma_{m}\gamma_{m^{\prime}}^{*}}|m\rangle\!\langle m^{\prime}|,

(55)

and write it in a compact form

\displaystyle\hat{\varrho}_{A}(t)=\sum_{mm^{\prime}=-N/2}^{N/2}C_{m}C_{m^{\prime}}^{*}e^{-i\varphi_{mm^{\prime}}}e^{\Gamma_{mm^{\prime}}}|m\rangle\!\langle m^{\prime}|,\ \ \ \mathrm{with}\ \ \

(56)

\displaystyle\varphi_{mm^{\prime}}=\beta^{2}_{m}\sin(\omega_{m}t)-\beta^{2}_{m^{\prime}}\sin(\omega_{m^{\prime}}t)+U(m^{2}-m^{\prime 2})t-\eta t(\beta_{m}-\beta_{m^{\prime}}),\ \ \

(57)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{mm^{\prime}}=-\frac{1}{2}(\left|\gamma_{m}\right|^{2}+\left|\gamma_{m^{\prime}}\right|^{2})+\gamma_{m}\gamma_{m^{\prime}}^{*}.

(58)

The action of the
\hat{J}_{y}
operator on the density matrix results in

\displaystyle\hat{J}_{y}\hat{\varrho}_{A}(t)=\frac{1}{2i}\sum_{mm^{\prime}=-N/2}^{N/2}C_{m}C_{m^{\prime}}^{*}e^{-i\varphi_{mm^{\prime}}}e^{\Gamma_{mm^{\prime}}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{N}{2}+m+1}\sqrt{\frac{N}{2}-m}\mbox{$|m+1\rangle$}-\sqrt{\frac{N}{2}+m}\sqrt{\frac{N}{2}-m+1}\mbox{$|m-1\rangle$}\right)\mbox{$\langle m^{\prime}|$}

(59)

Hence, the square of this operator yields

\displaystyle(\hat{J}_{y}\hat{\varrho}_{A}(t))^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{mm^{\prime};nn^{\prime}=-N/2}^{N/2}C_{m}C_{m^{\prime}}^{*}C_{n}C_{n^{\prime}}^{*}e^{-i(\varphi_{mm^{\prime}}+\varphi_{nn^{\prime}})}e^{\Gamma_{mm^{\prime}}+\Gamma_{nn^{\prime}}}\times

\displaystyle\left(\sqrt{\frac{N}{2}+m+1}\sqrt{\frac{N}{2}-m}\mbox{$|m+1\rangle$}-\sqrt{\frac{N}{2}+m}\sqrt{\frac{N}{2}-m+1}\mbox{$|m-1\rangle$}\right)\mbox{$\langle m^{\prime}|$}\times

\displaystyle\left(\sqrt{\frac{N}{2}+n+1}\sqrt{\frac{N}{2}-n}\mbox{$|n+1\rangle$}-\sqrt{\frac{N}{2}+n}\sqrt{\frac{N}{2}-n+1}\mbox{$|n-1\rangle$}\right)\mbox{$\langle n^{\prime}|$}.

(60)

We now perform the inner multiplication and get rid of the sum over
m^{\prime}
, to obtain

\displaystyle(\hat{J}_{y}\hat{\varrho}_{A}(t))^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{m;nn^{\prime}=-N/2}^{N/2}

(61)

B.2
The QCRB for the
\eta=0
case

When Eq. (
53
) is satisfied and
\langle\hat{J}_{y}(t)\rangle=0
, which is our case, we can use the
following formula for the QFI

\displaystyle F_{Q}=4\Delta^{2}\hat{J}_{y}.

(68)

Using the photonic coherent state and the spin-coherent atomic state

\displaystyle F_{q}=

\displaystyle 4\langle\hat{J}_{y}^{2}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}N(N+1)-2\mathrm{Re}\left[I\right],

(69)

where

\displaystyle I=\sum_{m}C_{m}C_{m-2}^{*}e^{-iU(m^{2}-(m-2)^{2})t}e^{\left|\alpha\right|^{2}{e^{-i(\omega_{m}-\omega_{m-2})t}-1}}\sqrt{\left(\frac{N}{2}+m+1\right)\left(\frac{N}{2}+m+2\right)\left(\frac{N}{2}-m\right)\left(\frac{N}{2}-m-1\right)}.

(70)

We now
assume that
N
is sufficiently large
to replace the summation over
m
by an integral.
For as long as the only quickly changing terms are the phase-factors, the
term under the square-root can be assumed to be slowly varying, hence,

\displaystyle I\simeq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi N}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!dm\,e^{-\frac{2}{N}m^{2}}e^{4iUt(m+1)}e^{|\alpha|^{2}\left[e^{i8W_{0}t(m+1)}-1\right]}\left(\frac{N^{2}}{4}-m^{2}\right).

(71)

In the next step, we make an additional assumption, that the times at which this integral is considered are sufficiently short so that
e^{4iW_{0}t(m+1)}-1\simeq 4iW_{0}t(m+1)
.
To this end, we notice that the Gaussian function yields the maximal range of
m
’s to be of the order of
\sqrt{N}
, hence “short times” implies that
the condition
4W_{0}t\sqrt{N}\ll 1

must be fulfilled. In this case, we obtain a simple Gaussian integral

\displaystyle I=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi N}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!dm\,e^{-m^{2}\left(\frac{2}{N}+32\bar{n}(W_{0}t)^{2}\right)}e^{4iUt(m+1)}e^{\bar{n}4iW_{0}t(m+1)-32\bar{n}(W_{0}t)^{2}(2m+1)}\left(\frac{N^{2}}{4}-m^{2}\right),

(72)

where
\bar{n}=|\alpha|^{2}
. This can be written as

\displaystyle I=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi N}}e^{i\alpha}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!dm\,e^{-m^{2}\sigma}e^{i\beta m}\left(\frac{N^{2}}{4}-m^{2}\right),

(73)

where

\displaystyle\sigma=\frac{2}{N}+32\bar{n}(W_{0}t)^{2},\ \ \ \alpha=a+ib,\ \ \ \beta=-a-2ib,\ \ \ a=-8W_{0}t\bar{n}-4Ut,\ \ \ b=32\bar{n}(W_{0}t)^{2}.

(74)

The outcome of this Gaussian integral is

\displaystyle I=\sqrt{\frac{2}{N\sigma}}e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}\sigma}e^{i\alpha}\left(\frac{\beta^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}+\frac{N^{2}}{4}-\frac{1}{2\sigma}\right).

(75)

Hence the QFI from Eq. (
69
) is approximated by

\displaystyle F_{Q}\simeq\frac{1}{2}N(N+1)-2\sqrt{\frac{2}{\sigma N}}e^{-\frac{1}{4\sigma}(a^{2}-4b^{2})}e^{-b}\left[\cos\left(a-\frac{ab}{\sigma}\right)\left(\frac{N^{2}}{4}-\frac{1}{2\sigma}+\frac{a^{2}-4b^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}\right)-\frac{ab}{\sigma}\sin\left(a-\frac{ab}{\sigma}\right)\right].

(76)

It is now our goal to identify the dominant terms.
In order to observe the positive impact of the cavity photons,
it must hold that
W_{0}\bar{n}\gg U
, so
a\simeq-4W_{0}t\bar{n}
.
Moreover
when the mean number of photons is large,
\bar{n}\gg 1
, then
b\simeq a/{\bar{n}}
and can be neglected as compared to
a
.
In such case, the QFI can be approximated as follows

\displaystyle F_{Q}\simeq\frac{1}{2}N(N+1)-2\sqrt{\frac{2}{\sigma N}}e^{-\frac{1}{4\sigma}a^{2}}e^{-b}\left[\cos\left(a-\frac{ab}{\sigma}\right)\left(\frac{N^{2}}{4}-\frac{1}{2\sigma}+\frac{a^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}\right)-\frac{ab}{\sigma}\sin\left(a-\frac{ab}{\sigma}\right)\right].

(77)

We now focus on the exponents. We have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\sigma}a^{2}=\frac{16(W_{0}\bar{n}t)^{2}}{\frac{2}{N}+32\bar{n}(W_{0}t)^{2}}=\frac{8(W_{0}\bar{n}t)^{2}N}{1+16\frac{(W_{0}\bar{n}t)^{2}N}{\bar{n}}}.

(78)

The characteristic time-scale of the numerator is
\tau_{c}=\frac{1}{2W_{0}\bar{n}\sqrt{N}}
and is
\bar{n}
shorter than of the denominator, hence, at the times when the exponent is non-negligible, the denominator can be taken as constant, giving

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\sigma}a^{2}\simeq 2\frac{t^{2}}{\tau_{c}^{2}}.

(79)

At these times
b\ll 1
, and, hence, another step of the approximation is

\displaystyle F_{Q}\simeq\frac{1}{2}N(N+1)-2\sqrt{\frac{2}{\sigma N}}e^{-\frac{1}{4\sigma}a^{2}}\left[\cos\left(a-\frac{ab}{\sigma}\right)\left(\frac{N^{2}}{4}-\frac{1}{2\sigma}+\frac{a^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}\right)-\frac{ab}{\sigma}\sin\left(a-\frac{ab}{\sigma}\right)\right].

(80)

Also, when
t\simeq\tau_{c}
, it holds that
a\ll 1
and
\sigma\simeq 2/N
, hence, we obtain the final expression

\displaystyle F_{Q}\simeq\frac{1}{2}N(N+1)-e^{-2\frac{t^{2}}{\tau_{c}^{2}}}\left(\frac{N^{2}}{2}-\frac{N}{2}+4\frac{t^{2}}{\tau_{c}^{2}}\frac{N}{2}\right).

(81)

This last term in the parenthesis can be safely neglected, as for short times it is small, while when
\tau\sim\tau_{c}
, the Gaussian nullifies the whole term inside the parenthesis.
Therefore, under the above assumptions, the QFI is

\displaystyle F_{Q}\simeq\frac{1}{2}N\left(1+f(t)\right)+\frac{1}{2}N^{2}\left(1-f(t)\right),\ \ \ f(t)=e^{-2\frac{t^{2}}{\tau_{c}^{2}}},\ \ \ \tau_{c}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{N}W_{0}\bar{n}}.

(82)

B.3
The QCRB for the
\eta\neq 0

Employing the same approach as above but with photons initially in a vacuum state we obtain again

\displaystyle F_{q}=

\displaystyle 4\langle\hat{J}_{y}^{2}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}N(N+1)-2\mathrm{Re}\left[I\right],

(83)

with the term
I
given by

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}C_{m}C_{m+2}e^{-iU(m^{2}-(m+2)^{2})t}e^{-i(\beta^{2}_{m}\sin(\omega_{m}t)-\beta^{2}_{m+2}\sin(\omega_{m+2}t))}

(84)

\displaystyle\times e^{i\eta t(\beta_{m}-\beta_{m+2})}e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\left|\gamma_{m}\right|^{2}+\left|\gamma_{m+2}\right|^{2})}e^{\gamma_{m}\gamma^{*}_{m+2}}\sqrt{\bigg(\frac{N}{2}+m+1\bigg)\bigg(\frac{N}{2}+m+2\bigg)\bigg(\frac{N}{2}-m\bigg)\bigg(\frac{N}{2}-m-1\bigg)}\Big].

The
condition for short times requires now that
\Delta_{c}t\ll 1
is satisfied
.
In this case we can expand the trigonometric functions and to the leading orded we obtain:

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\left|\gamma_{m}\right|^{2}+\left|\gamma_{m+2}\right|^{2})}e^{\gamma_{m}\gamma^{*}_{m+2}}\simeq e^{i\eta^{2}W_{0}t^{3}4(m+1)},

(85a)

(85b)

The approximate expression for the integral is then

\displaystyle I=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi N}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!dm\,e^{-\frac{2}{N}m^{2}}e^{4iUt(m+1)}e^{i\frac{8}{3}\eta^{2}W_{0}(m+1)t^{3}}\left(\frac{N^{2}}{4}-m^{2}\right).

This, in turn, yields the QFI equal to

\displaystyle F_{Q}\simeq\frac{1}{2}N(N+1)-e^{-\frac{\chi(t)^{2}N}{8}}\left(\frac{N^{2}}{2}(1+\frac{\chi(t)^{2}}{4})-\frac{N}{2}\right)\cos(\chi(t)),

where

Hence, we arrive at the final expression for the QFI

\displaystyle F_{Q}\simeq\frac{1}{2}N\left(1+f(t)\right)+\frac{1}{2}N^{2}\left(1-f(t)\right),\ \ \ f(t)=e^{-2N(Ut+\frac{2}{3}\eta^{2}W_{0}t^{3})^{2}}.

(88)

When
the mean number of photons
\bar{n}(t)=\eta^{2}t^{2}

Appendix C
Parameters

Our effective Hamiltonian for the photon-pair coupling is:

\hat{H}_{CA}=-i\Omega_{R}\int d^{3}rf(\mathbf{r})\hat{\Psi}_{\Delta}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r})\hat{\Psi}_{g}^{2}(\mathbf{r})\hat{a}+\mathrm{H.c.},

(89)

where
\Omega_{R}
is the strength of the light-matter coupling and
f(\mathbf{r})
is the cavity photon mode function,
\hat{\Psi}_{\Delta}
(
\hat{\Psi}_{g}
) is the molecule (ground-state atom) annihilation field operator, and
\hat{a}
is the annihilation operator of the single-mode cavity photon.

In Ref.
[
26
]
, the microscopic Hamiltonian that is used for the description of the single-photon–single-pair interaction has the following structure:

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{pair}}=-i\frac{\Omega_{0}}{2}\int d^{3}R\int d^{3}rf(\mathbf{R})\phi_{m}(\mathbf{r})\hat{\Psi}_{\Delta}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{R})\hat{\Psi}_{g}\bigg(\mathbf{R}-\frac{\mathbf{r}}{2}\bigg)\hat{\Psi}_{g}\bigg(\mathbf{R}+\frac{\mathbf{r}}{2}\bigg)\hat{a}+\mathrm{H.c.},

(90)

where
\phi_{m}(\mathbf{r})
is a wavefunction that describes the relative motion of atoms in the target molecule state. Here,
\Omega_{0}
is the single-photon–single-pair Rabi coupling.
Assuming that the dependence on
\mathbf{r}
of the field operators changes weakly on the characteristic scales of the problem, we approximate:

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{pair}}\approx-i\frac{\Omega_{0}}{2}\xi\int d^{3}Rf(\mathbf{R})\Psi_{\Delta}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{R})\Psi_{g}(\mathbf{R})\Psi_{g}(\mathbf{R})a+\mathrm{H.c.},

(91)

where

\xi=\int d^{3}r\phi_{m}(\mathbf{r}),

(92)

which matches Eq. (4) with

\Omega_{R}=\frac{\Omega_{0}\xi}{2}.

(93)

We note that the units of
\Omega_{R}
are
[\mathrm{frequency}][\mathrm{length}]^{3/2}
and
\Omega_{0}\sim[\mathrm{frequency}]
with
\hbar=1
.
Therefore, we find that, after adiabatic elimination of the molecular state in the low saturation regime, i.e., when the number of molecules is vanishingly small, the atomic interaction Hamiltonian takes the form:

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}=\int d^{3}r\bigg(\frac{g}{2}+U_{0}a^{\dagger}af^{2}(\mathbf{r})\bigg)\Psi_{g}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r})\Psi_{g}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r})\Psi_{g}(\mathbf{r})\Psi_{g}(\mathbf{r}),

(94)

where the cavity-assisted atom-atom interaction strength per photon is

U_{0}=\frac{\Omega_{R}^{2}}{\Delta_{A}},

(95)

where
\Delta_{A}=\omega_{L}-\omega_{B}
is the detuning of the pump laser with respect to the molecular state.

Critical, therefore, is the ratio of
U_{0}
and
g
. In order to estimate its value, we make a simple assumption about the molecular wavefunction, i.e.,
\phi_{m}(\mathbf{r})\propto\exp(-|\mathbf{r}|^{2}/R_{0}^{2})/|\mathbf{r}|
, where
R_{0}
is the characteristic length scale of the molecular state.
In such a case, we obtain
\xi=2\sqrt{2\pi}R_{0}^{3/2}
.

Finally, for an order-of magnitude estimation, we take
\Omega_{0}\sim 2\pi\times 750
kHz, which is an order of magnitude reported in Ref.
[
26
]
, detuning
\Delta_{a}\sim 2\pi\times 2
MHz, atomic mass
m\sim 87
u, where u is the atomic mass unit, the scattering length
a\sim 100a_{0}
, with
a_{0}
the Bohr radius. We are assuming that the
\Delta_{a}
is much larger than the decay rate of the molecular state. For the scale
R_{0}\sim R_{C}
, where the Condon point of the molecular state
R_{C}\sim 250a_{0}
, as reported in Ref.
[
26
]
. For these parameters, we obtain
U_{0}/g\sim 0.5
. The cavity-assisted atom-atom interaction strength per cavity photon,
U_{0}
is, thus, of the order of the bare coupling
g
.

For the double-well geometry,
\hat{\Psi}_{g}(\mathbf{r})=\phi_{1}(\mathbf{r})\hat{b}_{1}+\phi_{2}(\mathbf{r})\hat{b}_{2}
, where
\hat{b}_{i}
, with
i=1,2
, are the modes that are centred in the tight traps. The couplings that enter into the two-mode Hamiltonian are:

U=g\int d^{3}r|\phi(\mathbf{r})|^{4}

(96)

for the bare atom-atom interaction, and

W_{0}=U_{0}\int d^{3}r[f(\mathbf{r})]^{2}|\phi(\mathbf{r})|^{4}

(97)

for the cavity-induced atom-atom interaction strength. We see that the ratio

\frac{W_{0}}{U}=\frac{U_{0}}{g}\times\frac{\int d^{3}r[f(\mathbf{r})]^{2}|\phi(\mathbf{r})|^{4}}{\int d^{3}r|\phi(\mathbf{r})|^{4}}

(98)

is of the order
U_{0}/g
times a factor that takes into account the mode function of the cavity.
Assuming that the tight atomic traps are centered in the region
f\sim 1
, we obtain
W_{0}/U\sim U_{0}/g\sim 1
. For lighter atoms (like
7
Li) we may have
W_{0}/g\sim 0.1\ll 1
if we set the scattering length
a\sim 25a_{0}
. We note that the value
U_{0}/g
strongly depends on
R_{C}
, and for small
R_{C}
one may have
U_{0}/g\ll 1
. In such a case, more photons or stronger pumps are needed in order to overcome the bare atom-atom interaction strength in Eq. (
94
).
