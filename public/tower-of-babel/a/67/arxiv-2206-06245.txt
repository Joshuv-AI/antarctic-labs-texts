Upper bounds on key rates in device-independent quantum key distribution based on convex-combination attacks
Karol Łukanowski, Maria Balanzó-Juandó, Máté Farkas, Antonio Acín, Jan Kołodyński

Abstract
The device-independent framework constitutes the most pragmatic approach to quantum protocols that does not put any trust in their implementations. It requires all claims, about e.g. security, to be made at the level of the final classical data in hands of the end-users. This imposes a great challenge for determining attainable key rates in device-independent quantum key distribution (DIQKD), but also opens the door for consideration of eavesdropping attacks that stem from the possibility of a given data being just generated by a malicious third-party. In this work, we explore this path and present the convex-combination attack as an efficient, easy-to-use technique for upper-bounding DIQKD key rates. It allows verifying the accuracy of lower bounds on key rates for state-of-the-art protocols, whether involving one-way or two-way communication. In particular, we demonstrate with its help that the currently predicted constraints on the robustness of DIQKD protocols to experimental imperfections, such as the finite visibility or detection efficiency, are already very close to the ultimate tolerable thresholds.

Quantum key distribution (QKD) protocols are based on the setting in which two distant honest parties, Alice and Bob, aim at sharing a cryptographic key, while assuring it to be unknown to any potential eavesdropper. In QKD, this can be achieved by distributing entangled quantum states between Alice and Bob in each round of the protocol, during which they then measure their corresponding part of the state
[
12
]
. Within the DIQKD framework, however, the “black-box” approach depicted schematically in Fig.
1
is pursued. From the perspective of the users, they are just provided with devices that allow to vary the type of measurement being implemented in each round, the measurement
setting
, whose
outcome
is then outputted by the device. Still, by revealing some of the results between each other, Alice and Bob can verify what is the probability distribution—the
correlation
—describing the operation of their devices (boxes), i.e. specifying the probabilities with which the outcomes occur for each of the chosen setting. Note that, for the sake of simplicity, we adopt here a terminology in terms of the observed correlation that is meaningful in a scenario consisting of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) realisations of the experiment. In a general security proof, one should consider the estimated frequencies of all the observed events for a given finite number of rounds.

Crucially, within the DI paradigm the users do not assume anything about the origin of the correlation, apart from the fact that it must be compliant with the laws of quantum mechanics. Nonetheless, if the observed correlation violates a Bell inequality
[
4
]
, Alice and Bob can estimate the information that any potential eavesdropper may have about their recorded outcomes—the
raw data
—opening up the possibility for the parties to extract a secure key. Although establishing performance when only finite amount of data is available is important for real-life implementations
[
13
,
14
,
15
]

—can be even positive, i.e. the number of secret bits being finally shared by Alice and Bob divided by the number of protocol rounds employed (the size of the raw data), in the limit of the latter going to infinity.

The task of estimating key rates and proving the security of DIQKD protocols constitutes a great challenge, being a subject of intensive theoretical research. In the
one-way

In parallel, complementary methods of upper-bounding the DIQKD key rates have been proposed

particular
DIQKD scenario. As a result, the upper bounds on key rates we obtain account for the special features of the DIQKD protocol considered, e.g.: whether it involves one-way or two-way communication, the type of preprocessing used, or the postselection stage.

In particular, our approach is to propose concrete strategies a malicious third-party can play when distributing and controlling the devices, so that the data in hands of the users is consistently recovered, but some information about it—which can be explicitly quantified—remains in possession of the eavesdropper, Eve. We consider
individual attacks

[
12
]
that yield a tripartite (Alice, Bob and Eve) classical (i.i.d.) model that in case of one-way scenarios describes a broadcast (wiretap) channel

]

Moreover, as allowing for more powerful eavesdroppers (collective or coherent attacks) can only decrease the attainable key rate, such an upper bound remains valid beyond individual attacks. In particular, in case it vanishes, it is assured that no secure key can be distilled by the honest parties from a given data set.

In our work, we focus on a class of individual attacks—dubbed
convex-combination
(CC) attacks—in which Eve randomly alternates between distributing either devices that yield stronger non-local correlations than the ones exhibited by the data being shared, or devices that yield classical (local) correlations with Eve possessing full knowledge about the output data of both Alice and Bob. We show that, while correctly reproducing the shared data on average, the CC attack can be optimised by means of linear programming to maximise the probability of the local correlation being shared. As a result, it provides a direct method of upper-bounding the key rates that turns out to be very effective in predicting the zero-key regions, in which no DIQKD is possible. Although this is not our motivation here, let us note that in the non-zero regions our technique could also be merged with the other methods
[
38
,
39
,
40
]
to determine the tightest overall upper bounds on the key rate
[
46

In contrast to our previous work

In particular, we use the CC attack to determine thresholds on experimentally motivated parameters:

2
DIQKD under individual attacks

2.1
Standard DIQKD protocols

In a DIQKD protocol two parties, Alice and Bob, have access to a bipartite quantum state,
\rho_{\mathrm{AB}}\in\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{A}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{B}}})
, defined on the tensor product of their corresponding Hilbert spaces. The protocol consists of several rounds, in each of which Alice and Bob choose a particular quantum measurement to measure their part of a fresh copy of
\rho_{\mathrm{AB}}
. In particular, Alice chooses her measurement according to a random variable
X
, whose instance
x
labels the (measurement)
setting
selected out of
|X|=m_{\mathrm{A}}
possibilities. Similarly, Bob chooses his measurement according to
Y=y
with
|Y|=m_{\mathrm{B}}
. The (measurement)
outcome

a
(
b
) recorded then by Alice (Bob) corresponds to an instance of the random variable
A
(
B
) that we assume, without loss of generality, to take the same number of
|A|=n_{\mathrm{A}}
(
|B|=n_{\mathrm{B}}
) values for any setting,
x
(
y
), chosen.

According to quantum theory, each of the
m_{\mathrm{A}}
(
m_{\mathrm{B}}
) measurements of Alice (Bob) is described by a positive-operator-valued measure
\{M^{x}_{a}\}_{a=1}^{n_{\mathrm{A}}}
(
\{N^{y}_{b}\}_{b=1}^{n_{\mathrm{B}}}
), so that the
correlation
shared by the parties generally reads

p_{AB}^{\textrm{obs}}(a,b|x,y)=\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{{\,\rho_{\mathrm{AB}}\,(M^{x}_{a}\otimes N^{y}_{b})}\right\},

(1)

specifying the probability of obtaining the outcomes
a
and
b
, given that the measurements
x
and
y
were selected. We say that
p_{AB}^{\textrm{obs}}
in Eq. (
1
) is the
observed
correlation within the
m_{\mathrm{A}}m_{\mathrm{B}}n_{\mathrm{A}}n_{\mathrm{B}}
-scenario

[
50
]
.

After each protocol round, Alice and Bob store both the observed measurement outcomes,
a
and
b
, as well as the measurement settings,
x
and
y
, they employed. The records constitute then the
raw data
, out of which Alice and Bob distil a secret key with help of public communication, so that at the end of the procedure they aim at holding identical strings that appear perfectly random to any third party. In this work, we focus on estimating the asymptotic
key rate
, i.e. the length of such secret strings divided by the overall number of protocol rounds, in the limit of the latter going to infinity.

We consider here
standard
DIQKD protocols, i.e. ones in which both parties announce publicly the measurement settings employed in each round
[
41
]
. Although we primarily focus on protocols that further use the outcomes of a pre-agreed fixed pair of settings to extract the key, let us emphasise already that the CC attack, which is our main interest, can be applied to any scenario by following step-by-step every stage of a given protocol within the attack, e.g. see
[
41
]
for its application to the scheme of
[
15
]
involving multiple key-settings. In standard protocols, Alice and Bob firstly record strings of outcomes and settings over sufficiently many protocol rounds. Since individually they only have access to the marginal distributions, they publicly reveal part of their data in order to estimate the full correlation
p_{AB}^{\textrm{obs}}(a,b|x,y)
. This part of the dataset is then discarded. They also reveal the settings for the remaining dataset, and keep those outcomes that correspond to a pre-agreed
key setting
pair, (
x^{*},y^{*}
), distributed according to
p_{AB}^{\textrm{obs}}(a,b|x^{*},y^{*})
. If estimation shows that the error probability is low enough, they extract the final key from this dataset, using either two-way or one-way public communication schemes known as
privacy amplification
and
error correction

[
43
,
45
,
44
]
—and abort the protocol otherwise.

2.2
Individual attacks

In this work, we consider
individual attacks

[
12
]
of the eavesdropper, Eve, in which her register at the end of each protocol round corresponds to a random variable,
E
, being somehow correlated with the outcomes of Alice and Bob, determined by
A
and
B
, respectively. As
E
may take as many values as required, it may, for example, consist of doubles (ordered pairs), i.e.
e=(\tilde{a},\tilde{b})
where
|E|=n_{\mathrm{A}}n_{\mathrm{B}}
, and
\tilde{a}
and
\tilde{b}
stand for Eve’s guesses of Alice’s and Bob’s outcomes, respectively. In such a case, the situation in which Eve knows perfectly both the outcomes corresponds simply to the (tripartite) correlation
p_{ABE}(a,b,e\!=\!(\tilde{a},\tilde{b}))=\delta_{a\tilde{a}}\delta_{b\tilde{b}}/(n_{\mathrm{A}}n_{\mathrm{B}})
with
\delta_{\alpha\beta}
denoting the Kronecker delta function. Note that, generalising naturally Eq. (
1
) to
p_{ABE}
, such attacks “force” Eve to measure her part of now a tripartite state
\rho_{\mathrm{ABE}}
in the same way at the end of each protocol round
[
12
]
, and exclude the possibility of her possessing a quantum memory
[
51
]
.

As a result, each round of the protocol and, hence, the protocol on the whole, is then completely described by a tripartite correlation incorporating also the eavesdropper:

\displaystyle p_{ABE}(a,b,e|x,y)

(2)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\quad\forall_{a,b,x,y}:\;\sum_{e}p_{ABE}(a,b,e|x,y)=p_{AB}^{\textrm{obs}}(a,b|x,y),

where the above constraint assures that a given quantum correlation observed by Alice and Bob is indeed recovered on average, despite the presence of Eve.

In general, in order to consistently define the attack one should specify the form of the correlation (
2
), in particular, its quantum origin, i.e. the state being shared between all three parties and the measurements they perform
[
12
]
. However, for our purposes we consider individual attacks in which the strategy of Eve is to simply distribute different boxes—bipartite correlations shared by Alice and Bob (known to her and labelled by
\lambda
)—in each protocol round, so that Eq. (
2
) takes the
form:

\displaystyle p_{ABE}(a,b,e|x,y)

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)\,p(e|\lambda)\,p_{AB}(a,b|x,y,\lambda)

(3)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\quad\forall_{x,y}:\;\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)\,p_{AB}(a,b|x,y,\lambda)=p_{AB}^{\textrm{obs}}(a,b|x,y).

Such an attack is then specified by the probabilities,
q(\lambda)
, of Eve distributing each bipartite correlation
p_{AB}(a,b|x,y,\lambda)
, each of which must be decomposable as in Eq. (
1
) to be consistent with quantum theory, and her knowledge
p(e|\lambda)
about the outcomes of Alice and Bob for each of these correlations.

Note that this individual attack can be implemented by Eve via sharing the same tripartite state in each measurement round and measuring her part of the state, producing the outcome
e
. Importantly, this can be done such that
e
preserves all information about
\lambda
that can later be used by Eve to post-process the variable
e
. This will be important in standard DIQKD protocols, in which Alice and Bob at some point reveal their measurement settings for each round, and this information—together with
\lambda
—can be used by Eve to improve her guess on Alice’s and Bob’s outcomes. The knowledge of
\lambda
practically means that Eve always knows which term in the convex decomposition of
p_{AB}^{\textrm{obs}}
in Eq. (
3
) is used, whereas Alice and Bob have access only to the average distribution
p_{AB}^{\textrm{obs}}
. For an explicit construction of the tripartite state and the measurements of Alice, Bob and Eve, see App.
A
.

2.3
Upper bounds on one-way key rates

Formally, the key rate for all QKD and, hence, also DIQKD protocols assisted by one-way communication (say from Alice to Bob) includes a maximisation over all

r_{\text{1-way}}(A\to B)\coloneqq\max\limits_{p_{A^{\prime}|A},\;p_{M|A^{\prime}}}r_{\text{1-way}}(A\to B|A^{\prime},M),

(4)

where
A
and
B
are the outcome variables of Alice and Bob when they both select the key settings,
x^{*}
and
y^{*}
, respectively. The mapping
A\rightarrow A^{\prime}
described by the stochastic map
p_{A^{\prime}|A}
is applied by Alice on her outcome, followed by

(described by

For any given preprocessing strategy, however, the one-way key rate may be generally lower-bounded by the so-called

, which is valid for all

(more powerful than individual

\displaystyle r_{\text{1-way}}(A\to B|A^{\prime},M)\;\geq\;

\displaystyle\qquad{r}_{\text{DW}}\coloneqq H(A^{\prime}|\mathrm{E},M)-H(A^{\prime}|B,M),

(5)

where

\mathrm{E}
to explicitly distinguish quantum side-information from random variables signified throughout the text by italic characters—while

is the conditional (Shannon) entropy between Alice’s and Bob’s outcomes for the key settings, both conditioned also on the classical data

The DW rate (
5

H(A^{\prime}|\mathrm{E},M)
, represents the fraction of bits that are at least available to Alice after she compresses her bit-string sufficiently to ensure that it is no longer correlated anyhow with any eavesdropper. The EC-term,
H(A^{\prime}|B,M)

p_{AB}^{\textrm{obs}}(a,b|x^{*},y^{*})

[
12

{r}_{\text{DW}}=H(A^{\prime}|\mathrm{E})-H(A^{\prime}|B)

[

19
]

On the other hand, by considering any particular

2.4

3
The convex-combination attack

We consider a subclass of individual attacks taking the form (
3
), in particular, the
convex-combination (CC) attacks
introduced by us in
[
41
]
, being inspired by the considerations of
[
7

6
]
in which, however, Eve is allowed to possess even stronger than quantum, but still non-signalling, correlations with the raw data.

In short, within the CC attack, Eve mimics the ‘observed’ non-local correlation (pair of boxes) being shared between Alice and Bob,
p_{AB}^{\textrm{obs}}(a,b|x,y)
, by distributing interchangeably ‘local’ (exhibiting a local-hidden-variable model
[
4
]
) and ‘non-local’ correlations, in such a way that on average the ‘observed’ correlation is recovered and the attack proceeds unnoticed by the parties. Here we are interested in protocols involving two parties, but such a strategy may be analogously generalised to scenarios in which more parties are involved
[
57
]
.

An (overpessimistic
4
4

4

If Eve possesses any information about the outcomes also in the ‘non-local’ rounds, this may only improve the CC attack further—yield even tighter upper bounds on the key rates.
) assumption is then made, restricting Eve to possess no knowledge about the outcomes of the honest parties whenever she distributes any ‘non-local’ correlation. This contrasts strongly the case of distributing ‘local’ correlations, for each of which Eve can be shown to possess full knowledge about all the outcomes. Motivated by this difference, it is further assumed within the CC attack that it is best for Eve to maximise the overall probability of using local boxes. As a result, once the ‘non-local’ boxes to be used by Eve are specified, the optimal ‘local’ correlation to be distributed most frequently by her can always be found by means of linear programming.

In what follows, we first provide a geometrical interpretation of the CC attack, in order then to describe its optimisation in terms of a linear program, which we subsequently employ in Sec.
5
to find the tightest upper bounds on the DIQKD key rates that the CC attack can provide.

3.1
Geometric formulation of the CC attack

As stated above, the CC attack constitutes an example of the individual attack described by Eq. (
3
). In particular, in its simplest form, Eve distributes either a local or a non-local correlation, denoted by
p_{AB}^{\mathcal{L}}
or
p_{AB}^{\mathcal{N\!L}}
, respectively, such that the tripartite correlation (

\displaystyle p_{ABE}(a,b,e|x,y)

\displaystyle=q^{\mathcal{L}}\,p_{AB}^{\mathcal{L}}(a,b|x,y)\,\delta_{e,(a,b)}

\displaystyle\qquad+q^{\mathcal{N\!L}}\,p_{AB}^{\mathcal{N\!L}}(a,b|x,y)\,\delta_{e,?},

(11)

\lambda=0

Recall from Eq. (

q^{\mathcal{L}}\,p_{AB}^{\mathcal{L}}(a,b|x,y)+q^{\mathcal{N\!L}}\,p_{AB}^{\mathcal{N\!L}}(a,b|x,y)=p_{AB}^{\textrm{obs}}(a,b|x,y).

3.2
Optimisation of the CC attack via a linear program

For brevity, we drop within this section the subscript

The extremal correlations defining the local polytope,
\mathcal{L}

the set of all such extremal local correlations, and by
\textbf{q}^{\mathcal{L}}=\left(q_{i}^{\mathcal{L}}\right)_{i}
the vector of the corresponding probabilities that Eve assigns to each of them within the CC attack. On the other hand, we assume the average non-local correlation that she distributes to be a mixture of pre-chosen non-local quantum correlations forming a vector
\textbf{p}^{\mathcal{N\!L}}=\left(p^{\mathcal{N\!L}}_{j}\right)_{j}
, each of which is distributed by Eve with the corresponding probability from the vector
\textbf{q}^{\mathcal{N\!L}}=\left(q_{j}^{\mathcal{N\!L}}\right)_{j}
. Finally, let us recall that for the attack to succeed Eve must reproduce on average the true correlation observed by Alice and Bob, i.e.
p^{\textrm{obs}}\equiv p_{AB}^{\textrm{obs}}(a,b|x,y)
of Eq. (
1

In order to optimise the CC attack, Eve seeks a probability vector
\textbf{q}=\textbf{q}^{\mathcal{L}}\oplus\textbf{q}^{\mathcal{N\!L}}

\displaystyle\textbf{q}_{\text{CC}}\!\left[\textbf{p}^{\mathcal{N\!L}},p^{\textrm{obs}}\right]=\;

\displaystyle\underset{\textbf{q}}{\text{argmax}}\;\sum_{i}q_{i}^{\mathcal{L}}

(13)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\quad\textbf{q}^{\mathcal{L}}\cdot\textbf{p}^{\mathcal{L}}+\textbf{q}^{\mathcal{N\!L}}\cdot\textbf{p}^{\mathcal{N\!L}}=p^{\textrm{obs}},

\displaystyle\qquad\sum_{i}q_{i}^{\mathcal{L}}+\sum_{j}q_{j}^{\mathcal{N\!L}}=1,

\displaystyle\qquad\;\forall_{i,j}\!:\quad 0\leq q_{i}^{\mathcal{L}},q_{j}^{\mathcal{N\!L}}\leq 1,

where the first constraint is just the generalisation of Eq. (

\textbf{p}^{\mathcal{N\!L}}

The above construction requires to specify multiple local correlations,

(extremal points of the local polytope), and for generality we have also allowed for multiple non-local boxes,
\textbf{p}^{\mathcal{N\!L}}

p^{\mathcal{L}}\coloneqq\textbf{q}^{\mathcal{L}}\cdot\textbf{p}^{\mathcal{L}}

4.1
Finite visibility

Although within the DI framework we are restricted to perform the analysis at the level of correlations, the noise models associated with particular implementations are typically defined assuming certain form of quantum states and measurements employed. The finite
visibility
, in particular, is associated with the probability
V\in[0,1]
with which Alice and Bob succeed in sharing the intended bipartite state
\rho_{\mathrm{AB}}

1-V
it is the maximally mixed state that is rather distributed. As a result, the actual state they share becomes

\rho_{\mathrm{AB}}(V):=V\,\rho_{\mathrm{AB}}+\frac{1-V}{d_{\mathrm{A}}d_{\mathrm{B}}}\,\mathbbm{1}_{d_{\mathrm{A}}d_{\mathrm{B}}},

(14)

where
d_{\mathrm{A}}=\dim\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{A}}
and
d_{\mathrm{B}}=\dim\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{B}}

However, given that Alice and Bob perform projective (von Neumann) measurements for which
n_{\mathrm{A}}=d_{\mathrm{A}}
and
n_{\mathrm{B}}=d_{\mathrm{B}}

1

p_{AB}^{\textrm{obs}}(a,b|x,y)=V\,Q_{AB}(a,b|x,y)+\frac{1-V}{n_{\mathrm{A}}n_{\mathrm{B}}},

(15)

where by

we denote the ideal correlation shared at
V=1
. Hence, the finite visibility model is then equivalent to the uniform noise being admixed with all the
n_{\mathrm{A}}n_{\mathrm{B}}
outcomes occurring with equal probability, so that at
V=0
a uniformly random distribution of the outcomes is always observed by the parties, i.e. independently of the measurement settings chosen.

5
Applications to DIQKD protocols

In the following, we apply the CC attack to derive upper bounds on the one-way and two-way key rates in noisy scenarios, i.e. as functions of detection efficiency
\eta
and visibility
V
, for a range of DIQKD protocols. Most importantly, as a result, we determine
critical
visibilities
{V}_{\mathrm{crit}}
and detection efficiencies
{\eta}_{\mathrm{crit}}
, below which our upper bounds on the key rates become negative and preclude a secure experimental realisation of a given protocol. As these critical values signify then
lower bounds on minimal robustness parameters that the protocol can tolerate—below these values there exists an explicit attack, the CC attack, that invalidates the security—by comparing them with the ones obtained from the state-of-the-art security proofs, one can judge how much room there exists for potential improvement of the latter.

In order to apply the CC attack and upper-bound the key rate in a noisy one-way (
6
) or two-way (
7
) DIQKD protocol, one must first specify the correlation
Q_{AB}(a,b|x,y)
that would be shared by the parties in the absence of imperfections. The true noisy correlation being observed,
p_{AB}^{\textrm{obs}}(a,b|x,y)
, is then decomposed within the attack into the local and nonlocal parts. Although the local contribution is determined via the linear program (
13
), the eavesdropper must specify in advance the set of nonlocal correlations
\textbf{p}^{\mathcal{N\!L}}
to be used within the convex decomposition. In this work, we choose
\textbf{p}^{\mathcal{N\!L}}
to consist of only one correlation, namely, the noiseless
Q_{AB}(a,b|x,y)
. We find this choice to be optimal for our purposes by heuristic methods, however, we leave it open whether the upper bounds on key rates derived under this choice can be further improved by performing a rigorous optimisation of
\textbf{p}^{\mathcal{N\!L}}
.

In this section, we consider the application of the CC attack to particular DIQKD protocols, which exhibit state-of-the-art robustness to noise. In particular, we summarise the experimentally relevant bounds on critical visibilities and detection efficiencies below which the protocols become vulnerable to the CC attack and thus insecure. As an example, an explicit derivation of the CC-based upper bound on the key rate and the resulting lower bounds on tolerable noise levels are presented for the CHSH-based protocol with deterministic binning of the non-detection events, while similar derivations applicable to the other protocols considered are relegated to the
Appendices

6
Conclusions

We have introduced the convex-combination (CC) attack as an easy-to-use tool to compute upper bounds on asymptotic rates in one-way and two-way DIQKD protocols. This in turn allows one to quickly establish critical noise parameters (here, detection efficiency and visibility) below which these upper bounds vanish and, hence, the CC attack disallows any DIQKD to be possible.

By applying the CC attack to one-way and two-way protocols involving either maximally or partially entangled states, as well as ones including a postselection stage or relying on correlations with more than two measurement settings and outcomes, we have demonstrated that despite its simple construction—decomposition of a given quantum correlation into a ‘local’ and a ‘more non-local’ part—the CC attack turns out to be very efficient in proving that the current thresholds on noise tolerance established with help of state-of-the-art security proofs are already very close to critical noise values, below which the CC attack invalidates the security. It is worth stressing that computing the upper bounds on the key rates, or equivalently, on Eve’s entropy, in the CC attack is very simple, in particular, much simpler than computing lower bounds. In light of the heuristic results derived in this work, the CC attack appears to be also a versatile tool to benchmark lower bounds to entropies obtained with existing techniques, such as the hierarchies of
[
35
,
36
]
.

We have successfully applied the CC attack in its simplest form, in which the ‘non-local’ (but quantum) correlation within the convex decomposition is fixed, while the ‘local’ contribution can then be chosen to maximise its weight within the decomposition by means of a linear program. On one hand, it may not be generally true that the strategy of maximising the probability of distributing the local correlation, for which the eavesdropper perfectly knows the outcomes, is actually optimal from the point of view of providing the tightest upper bound on the key rate. On the other, we have pessimistically assumed the eavesdropper not to possess any information about the outcomes in case the ‘more non-local’ correlation is distributed. Although the linear program can be straightforwardly adapted to utilise multiple non-local point within the decomposition, it would be desirable to generalise the construction, so that it actually includes optimisation over the non-local points e.g. by approximating the quantum set of correlations with sufficient accuracy from outside by means of a convergent hierarchy of relaxations
[
31
,
32
]
. We leave the above interesting developments of our CC attack for future work.

Appendix A
Explicit form of the state and measurements in individual attacks

In this section, we provide an explicit construction for a shared tripartite state, and measurements for Alice, Bob and Eve for achieving the individual attack in Eq. (
3
). We start from the observed correlation, and write it as a convex combination of quantum correlations:

p_{AB}^{\textrm{obs}}(a,b|x,y)=\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)p_{AB}(a,b|x,y,\lambda).

(49)

That is, there exist a state
\rho_{AB}
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
and measurements
\{M^{x}_{a}\}
and
\{N^{y}_{b}\}
on
\mathcal{H}_{A}
and
\mathcal{H}_{B}
, respectively, such that

p_{AB}^{\textrm{obs}}(a,b|x,y)=\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{{\rho_{AB}(M^{x}_{a}\otimes N^{y}_{b})}\right\}.

(50)

Moreover,
q(\lambda)
is a probability distribution and there exist states
\rho^{\lambda}_{AB}
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}^{\lambda}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{\lambda}_{B}
and measurements
\{(M^{\lambda})^{x}_{a}\}
and
\{(N^{\lambda})^{y}_{b}\}
on
\mathcal{H}^{\lambda}_{A}
and
\mathcal{H}^{\lambda}_{B}
, respectively, such that

p_{AB}(a,b|x,y,\lambda)=\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{{\rho^{\lambda}_{AB}[(M^{\lambda})^{x}_{a}\otimes(N^{\lambda})^{y}_{b}]}\right\}.

(51)

It is clear from the convex structure of the quantum set of correlations that for every valid convex decomposition of the form Eq. (
49
), if
q(\lambda)
, the states
\rho^{\lambda}_{AB}
, and the measurements
\{(M^{\lambda})^{x}_{a}\}
and
\{(N^{\lambda})^{y}_{b}\}
are known, then one can build the state
\rho_{AB}
and the measurements
\{M^{x}_{a}\}
and
\{N^{y}_{b}\}
. In particular, one can pick the Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{A}=\bigoplus_{\lambda}\mathcal{H}^{\lambda}_{A}
and
\mathcal{H}_{B}=\bigoplus_{\lambda}\mathcal{H}^{\lambda}_{B}
, and define the state
\rho_{AB}=\bigoplus_{\lambda}q(\lambda)\rho^{\lambda}_{AB}
on
\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
. This state together with the measurements
M^{x}_{a}=\bigoplus_{\lambda}(M^{\lambda})^{x}_{a}
on
\mathcal{H}_{A}
and
N^{y}_{b}=\bigoplus_{\lambda}(N^{\lambda})^{y}_{b}
on
\mathcal{H}_{B}
gives rise to the correlation
p_{AB}^{\textrm{obs}}(a,b|x,y)
.

We now construct a tripartite state
\rho_{ABE}
on
\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E}
and measurement operators
E_{e}
on
\mathcal{H}_{E}
such that the resulting tripartite correlation in the individual attack reads

p_{ABE}(a,b,e|x,y)=\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{{\rho_{ABE}(M^{x}_{a}\otimes N^{y}_{b}\otimes E_{e})}\right\}=\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)p(e|\lambda)p_{AB}(a,b|x,y,\lambda),

(52)

for some arbitrary distributions
p(e|\lambda)
. We simply choose the state

\rho_{ABE}=\bigoplus_{\lambda}q(\lambda)\rho^{\lambda}_{AB}\otimes|\hskip 0.7pt\lambda\rangle\langle\lambda\hskip 0.7pt|_{E},

(53)

where
\{|\hskip 0.7pt\lambda\rangle\}
is an orthonormal basis on
\mathcal{H}_{E}
, and the measurement operators

E_{e}=\sum_{\lambda}p(e|\lambda)|\hskip 0.7pt\lambda\rangle\langle\lambda\hskip 0.7pt|

(54)

on
\mathcal{H}_{E}
. It follows that

\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{{\rho_{ABE}(M^{x}_{a}\otimes N^{y}_{b}\otimes E_{e})}\right\}=p_{ABE}(a,b,e|x,y)=\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)p(e|\lambda)p_{AB}(a,b|x,y,\lambda)

(55)

as required, and clearly,
\sum_{e}p_{ABE}(a,b,e|x,y)=p_{AB}^{\textrm{obs}}(a,b|x,y)
for all
a,b,x,y
. Last, we note that
p(e|\lambda)
can be chosen in a way that
e
preserves the information about
\lambda
(given that the alphabet size of
e
is large enough).

and the noise-free correlation
Q_{AB}(a,b|x,y)
, such that the observed correlation is of the form:

p_{AB}^{\textrm{obs}}(a,b|x,y)=q^{\mathcal{L}}\,p_{AB}^{\mathcal{L}}(a,b|x,y)+(1-q^{\mathcal{L}})\,Q_{AB}(a,b|x,y).

(58)

From equating the above expression for
p_{AB}^{\textrm{obs}}(a,b|x,y)
it follows that

p_{AB}^{\mathcal{L}}(a,b|x,y)=\tilde{V}\,Q_{AB}(a,b|x,y)+\frac{1-\tilde{V}}{4},

(59)

where
\tilde{V}\in[0,1]
and
q^{\mathcal{L}}=(1-V)/(1-\tilde{V})
. Therefore, maximising
q^{\mathcal{L}}
simply corresponds to maximising
\tilde{V}
in Eq. (
59
), such that
p_{AB}^{\mathcal{L}}(a,b|x,y)
is local. The result of this maximisation is the
local visibility

V^{\mathcal{L}}
, which in turn fully characterises the CC attack for this protocol with
q^{\mathcal{L}}=(1-V)/(1-V^{\mathcal{L}})
when
V\geq V^{\mathcal{L}}
and otherwise
q^{\mathcal{L}}=1
. Even though the maximisation is a linear program, it is possible to solve it explicitly as all the facets of the polytope in the 2322-scenario are known analytically
[
50
]
. In particular, all the facets that correspond to non-trivial constraints (i.e. do not correspond to the positivity and normalisation of the probabilities) are of the CHSH-type:

-2\leq\langle A_{x_{0}}B_{y_{0}}\rangle+\langle A_{x_{0}}B_{y_{1}}\rangle+\langle A_{x_{1}}B_{y_{0}}\rangle-\langle A_{x_{1}}B_{y_{1}}\rangle\leq 2\quad x_{0},x_{1}\in\{0,1\},\,y_{0},y_{1}\in\{0,1,2\},

(60)

where
\langle A_{x}B_{y}\rangle=p(0,0|x,y)+p(1,1|x,y)-p(0,1|x,y)-p(1,0|x,y)
are the correlators of a given
p(a,b|x,y)
. That is, a correlation in the 2322-scenario is local if and only if it satisfies all the inequalities in Eq. (
60
).

Let us denote the correlators of
Q_{AB}(a,b|x,y)
by
\langle A_{x}B_{y}\rangle^{\mathcal{N\!L}}
, and those of
p_{AB}^{\mathcal{L}}(a,b|x,y)
by
\langle A_{x}B_{y}\rangle^{\mathcal{L}}
. It is easy to see that

\langle A_{x}B_{y}\rangle^{\mathcal{L}}=\tilde{V}\,\langle A_{x}B_{y}\rangle^{\mathcal{N\!L}}.

(61)

Therefore, finding the maximal
\tilde{V}
such that
p_{AB}^{\mathcal{L}}(a,b|x,y)
is local corresponds to finding the maximal
\tilde{V}
such that

-\frac{2}{\tilde{V}}\leq\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{N\!L}}_{x_{0},x_{1},y_{0},y_{1}}\leq\frac{2}{\tilde{V}}\quad\forall x_{0},x_{1}\in\{0,1\},\,\forall y_{0},y_{1}\in\{0,1,2\},

(62)

where we have defined

\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{N\!L}}_{x_{0},x_{1},y_{0},y_{1}}=\langle A_{x_{0}}B_{y_{0}}\rangle^{\mathcal{N\!L}}+\langle A_{x_{0}}B_{y_{1}}\rangle^{\mathcal{N\!L}}+\langle A_{x_{1}}B_{y_{0}}\rangle^{\mathcal{N\!L}}-\langle A_{x_{1}}B_{y_{1}}\rangle^{\mathcal{N\!L}}.

(63)

A straightforward computation yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{N\!L}}_{0,1,0,1}

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{2}

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{N\!L}}_{0,1,0,2}

\displaystyle=1+\sqrt{2}

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{N\!L}}_{0,1,1,0}

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{2}

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{N\!L}}_{0,1,1,2}

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{N\!L}}_{0,1,2,0}

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{N\!L}}_{0,1,2,1}

\displaystyle=1+\sqrt{2}

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{N\!L}}_{1,0,0,1}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{N\!L}}_{1,0,0,2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}-1

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{N\!L}}_{1,0,1,0}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{N\!L}}_{1,0,1,2}

\displaystyle=-1

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{N\!L}}_{1,0,2,0}

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{N\!L}}_{1,0,2,1}

\displaystyle=1-\sqrt{2}.

(64)

It is clear from the first equation that the maximal
\tilde{V}
(denoted by
V^{\mathcal{L}}
) is bounded by
V^{\mathcal{L}}\leq 1/\sqrt{2}
. Furthermore, substituting
\tilde{V}=1/\sqrt{2}
into Eq. (
62
) also implies that
p_{AB}^{\mathcal{L}}(a,b|x,y)
with visibility
\tilde{V}=1/\sqrt{2}
is local, and therefore
V^{\mathcal{L}}\geq 1/\sqrt{2}
. Hence, we get that
V^{\mathcal{L}}=1/\sqrt{2}
, fully characterising the CC attack for this protocol and determinig the local weight as

q^{\mathcal{L}}=\min\left\{1,\frac{1-V}{1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}}\right\}.

(65)

B.2
Correlations yielding maximal CHSH-violation subject to finite detection efficiency

In this section, we determine explicitly the maximal local weights in the CC attack for the CHSH-based 2333-protocol subject to finite detection efficiency. As in Eq. (
4.2
) of the main text, the lossy observed correlation is given by

\displaystyle p_{AB}^{\textrm{obs}}(a,b|x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr a\setminus b&0&1&\varnothing\\
\hline\cr 0&\eta^{2}\mathsf{Q}_{00}^{xy}&\eta^{2}\mathsf{Q}_{01}^{xy}&\eta\bar{\eta}\mathsf{Q}_{0}^{x}\\
\hline\cr 1&\eta^{2}\mathsf{Q}_{10}^{xy}&\eta^{2}\mathsf{Q}_{11}^{xy}&\eta\bar{\eta}\mathsf{Q}_{1}^{x}\\
\hline\cr\varnothing&\bar{\eta}\eta\mathsf{Q}_{0}^{y}&\bar{\eta}\eta\mathsf{Q}_{1}^{y}&\bar{\eta}^{2}\\
\hline\cr\end{array},

where
\mathsf{Q}_{ab}^{xy}\coloneqq Q(a,b|x,y)
denotes the ideal, noiseless correlation with marginals
\mathsf{Q}_{a}^{x}=\sum_{b}\mathsf{Q}_{ab}^{xy}
and
\mathsf{Q}_{b}^{y}=\sum_{a}\mathsf{Q}_{ab}^{xy}
for Alice and Bob, respectively. Specifically, we calculate the maximal local weight
q^{\mathcal{L}}(\theta=\pi/2,\eta)
for protocols involving maximally entangled states, discussed in Secs.
5.2
&
5.3
, for which
Q(a,b|x,y)
is given by Eq. (
56
) above, as well as
q^{\mathcal{L}}(\theta\to 0,\eta)
for protocols involving partially entangled states, discussed in Sec.
5.4
, where the
\mathsf{Q}
-probabilities take a more complicated form discussed in App.
B.2.2
below.

Consider first the 2233-scenario. The complete characterisation of the local polytope in terms of facet (Bell) inequalities becomes more complicated than in the 2222-scenario as the number of such inequalities is 1116
[
50
]
. However, they may still be checked for violation with the help of some symbolic computation software. In general, the corresponding facet inequalities can be cast into three categories
[
50
]
: 36 “trivial” inequalities ensuring non-negativity of probabilities, 648 CHSH-like inequalities (resulting from the original CHSH inequality by some relabelling of measurements, outcomes and parties), and 432 CGLMP-like inequalities
[
68
]
(also all equivalent under some choice of relabelling). All of them impose constraints on conditional probabilities, assuring the resulting correlation to admit a local hidden-variable model.

On the other hand, we note that for a given correlation
p_{AB}^{\textrm{obs}}(a,b|x,y)
observed by Alice and Bob, and a particular lossless correlation
p_{AB}^{\mathcal{N\!L}}(a,b|x,y)
distributed by Eve in the “nonlocal” rounds of the CC attack, the local correlation in Eq. (
12
) must satisfy the following equality:

p_{AB}^{\mathcal{L}}(a,b|x,y)=\frac{p_{AB}^{\textrm{obs}}(a,b|x,y)-(1-q^{\mathcal{L}})\,Q_{AB}(a,b|x,y)}{q^{\mathcal{L}}},

(71)

where
q^{\mathcal{L}}
is to be maximised and we set
p_{AB}^{\mathcal{N\!L}}=Q_{AB}
. Although the maximal value of
q^{\mathcal{L}}
can always be determined numerically by the linear program, one may equivalently treat
q^{\mathcal{L}}
as a free parameter and verify what is its maximal value such that none of the aforementioned inequalities is violated by the correlation in Eq. (
71
). Importantly, in this way not only the maximal value of
q^{\mathcal{L}}
is determined, but also the particular (facet) inequality may be identified, i.e. the facet of the local polytope on which the correlation (
71
) then resides in the correlation space when
q^{\mathcal{L}}
is maximal. As all the Bell inequalities are linear in probabilities
[
4
]
, i.e.

\sum_{a,b,x,y}b_{a,b,x,y}\;p_{AB}^{\mathcal{L}}(a,b|x,y)\leq 0,

(72)

with
b_{a,b,x,y}\in\mathbb{R}
, it can be rearranged into an inequality for
q^{\mathcal{L}}
using Eq. (
71
), and expressing
p_{AB}^{\textrm{obs}}(a,b|x,y)
and
Q_{AB}(a,b|x,y)
as functions of
\eta
and
\theta
.

B.2.1
Maximally entangled states

Focusing first on the 2233-scenario with
\theta=\pi/2
in (
21
) and standard CHSH-optimal measurements
x,y\in\{0,1\}
(
40
), we observe that for all values of
\eta

in Eq. (

-p_{A}^{\mathcal{L}}(1|0)-p_{B}^{\mathcal{L}}(1|0)+p_{AB}^{\mathcal{L}}(1,1|0,0)+p_{AB}^{\mathcal{L}}(1,1|0,1)\\
+p_{AB}^{\mathcal{L}}(1,1|1,0)-p_{AB}^{\mathcal{L}}(1,1|1,1)\leq 0

(73)

with
p_{A}^{\mathcal{L}}
and
p_{B}^{\mathcal{L}}
being the marginal distributions. We can evaluate the relevant conditional probabilities by calculating the corresponding terms for the correlation
p_{AB}^{\textrm{obs}}(a,b|x,y)
observed by Alice and Bob from (
B.2

\displaystyle p_{A}^{\textrm{obs}}(1|0)=p_{B}^{\textrm{obs}}(1|0)=

\displaystyle\frac{\eta}{2},

(74)

\displaystyle p_{AB}^{\textrm{obs}}(1,1|0,0)=p_{AB}^{\textrm{obs}}(1,1|0,1)=p_{AB}^{\textrm{obs}}(1,1|1,0)=

\displaystyle\eta^{2}\frac{2+\sqrt{2}}{8},

\displaystyle p_{AB}^{\textrm{obs}}(1,1|1,1)=

\displaystyle\eta^{2}\frac{2-\sqrt{2}}{8}.

Hence, substituting for the local distribution
p_{AB}^{\mathcal{L}}
and its marginals into Eq. (
73

71

q^{\mathcal{L}}=\min\left\{1,(1-\eta)\left(1+\left(3+2\sqrt{2}\right)\eta\right)\right\}.

(75)

In the 2333-scenario Bob uses an additional measurement
B_{2}=\sigma_{z}
identical to
A_{0}
, so that these are correlated and, hence, most efficient in generating the key. Although the dimensionality of the correlation space is then formally increased, such an added setting does not impose any further locality constraints on the resulting shared correlation. In particular, as the inequality (
73

q^{\mathcal{L}}
according to Eq. (
75
) also in the 2333-scenario considered.

B.2.2

Moreover, focusing further on the 2233-scenario but examining correlations determined by the partially entangled states (

\displaystyle p_{A}^{\textrm{obs}}(1|0)=

\displaystyle p_{B}^{\textrm{obs}}(1|0)=

\displaystyle\frac{\eta}{2}(1-z_{+}\cos\theta),

\displaystyle p_{AB}^{\textrm{obs}}(1,1|0,0)=

\displaystyle P:

\displaystyle\!=\alpha\eta+\alpha\bar{\eta}\cos\theta,

\displaystyle Q:

\displaystyle\!=\eta\cos\phi_{A}+\bar{\eta}\cos\theta,

\displaystyle R:

and

In this section, we calculate the

\varnothing

p_{A^{\prime}|B}(a^{\prime}|b)_{\mathrm{det}}\quad=\quad\frac{p_{A^{\prime}B}(a^{\prime},b)}{p_{B}(b)}\quad=\quad\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr a^{\prime}\setminus b&0&1&\varnothing\\
\hline\cr 0&\begin{tabular}[]{@{}c@{}}$\eta\,\frac{\mathsf{P}_{00}}{\mathsf{P}^{\mathrm{B}}_{0}}+\bar{\eta}$\end{tabular}&\begin{tabular}[]{@{}c@{}}$\eta\,\frac{\mathsf{P}_{01}}{\mathsf{P}^{\mathrm{B}}_{1}}+\bar{\eta}$\end{tabular}&\begin{tabular}[]{@{}c@{}}$\eta\mathsf{P}^{\mathrm{A}}_{0}+\bar{\eta}$\end{tabular}\\
\hline\cr 1&\eta\,\frac{\mathsf{P}_{10}}{\mathsf{P}^{\mathrm{B}}_{0}}&\eta\,\frac{\mathsf{P}_{11}}{\mathsf{P}^{\mathrm{B}}_{1}}&\eta\mathsf{P}^{\mathrm{A}}_{1}\\
\hline\cr\end{array}\,,

(92)

which allows to directly compute the relevant conditional entropy in case Alice bins deterministically:

H(A^{\prime}|B)_{\text{det}}=\eta\,\mathsf{P}^{\mathrm{B}}_{0}\,h\!\left[\frac{\eta\,\mathsf{P}_{10}}{\mathsf{P}^{\mathrm{B}}_{0}}\right]+\eta\,\mathsf{P}^{\mathrm{B}}_{1}\,h\!\left[\frac{\eta\,\mathsf{P}_{11}}{\mathsf{P}^{\mathrm{B}}_{1}}\right]+\bar{\eta}\,h\!\left[\eta\mathsf{P}^{\mathrm{A}}_{1}\right],

(93)

where
h[x]\coloneqq-x\log_{2}x-(1-x)\log_{2}(1-x)
is the binary entropy function.

C.2
Calculation of the PA-term
H(A^{\prime}|E)

As within the CC attack Eve knows whether a local or a nonlocal correlation is being distributed to Alice and Bob, the entropy of the variable
A
(which describes the output of the measurement used by Alice for key distribution) conditioned on Eve’s knowledge is given by the convex mixture of local and non-local contributions. Moreover, Eve not only knows when a local distribution is shared by the parties, but also knows then perfectly the outcomes
A
and
B
of Alice and Bob, respectively. Hence, the contribution of the local distribution to the conditional entropy of
A
is zero, unless Alice performs a non-deterministic preprocessing
p_{A^{\prime}|A}\equiv\mathcal{S}
of the outcome
A
that introduces some randomness, so that the knowledge of Eve about the resulting variable
A^{\prime}
is no longer perfect.

In order to determine the conditional entropy
H(A^{\prime}|E)
, we must only track Eve’s knowledge of Alice’s outputs. Hence, without loss of generality, we can assume Eve to hold a random variable
E
taking four values
e\in\{\{\tilde{e}\},?\}
, where
?
means that she distributed a nonlocal correlation and has no knowledge of Alice’s output, while values
\tilde{e}\in\{0,1,\varnothing\}
correspond to the perfect knowledge of Alice’s output
A
, which Eve possesses after distributing a local correlation (so that always
\tilde{e}=a
).

As a consequence, we can generally write the PA-term for the CC attack as

\displaystyle H(A^{\prime}|E)

\displaystyle=\sum_{e}p(E=e)H\left(A^{\prime}|E=e\right)=(1-q^{\mathcal{L}})H(A^{\prime}|E=\,?)+q^{\mathcal{L}}\sum_{\tilde{e}}p(E=\tilde{e}|\mathcal{L})H\left(A^{\prime}|E=\tilde{e}\right),

(104)

where
q^{\mathcal{L}}
is the local weight, so that
p(E=\,?)=1-q^{\mathcal{L}}
and
p(E=\tilde{e})=q^{\mathcal{L}}\,p(E=\tilde{e}|\mathcal{L})
, with
p(E=\tilde{e}|\mathcal{L})
denoting the probability of Eve recording
\tilde{e}
given she has distributed a local correlation.

As Eve has perfect knowledge of Alice’s outcome, the conditional entropy within the “local rounds” is

\displaystyle H\left(A^{\prime}|E=\tilde{e}\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{a^{\prime}}p_{A^{\prime}}(a^{\prime}|E=\tilde{e})\log_{2}\!\left[p_{A^{\prime}}(a^{\prime}|E=\tilde{e})\right]=-\sum_{a^{\prime},a}\mathcal{S}_{a^{\prime}a}\,p_{A}(a|E=\tilde{e})\,\log_{2}\!\left[\sum_{a}\mathcal{S}_{a^{\prime}a}\,p_{A}(a|E=\tilde{e})\right]

\displaystyle=-\sum_{a^{\prime}}\mathcal{S}_{a^{\prime}\tilde{e}}\log_{2}\mathcal{S}_{a^{\prime}\tilde{e}}=:H\!\left(\mathcal{S}_{\cdot\tilde{e}}\right),

(105)

where we have used the fact that
p_{A}(a|E=\tilde{e})=\delta_{a,\tilde{e}}
, and defined above
H\!\left(\mathcal{S}_{\cdot\tilde{e}}\right):=H[A^{\prime}]_{p(A^{\prime}|A=\tilde{e})}
as the entropy of the distribution described by the
\tilde{e}
-column of the stochastic matrix
\mathcal{S}
, which equivalently represents the randomness (entropy) of the preprocessed variable
A^{\prime}
when
A=\tilde{e}
.

We can further simplify the expression (
104
) by expanding the probability
p(E=\tilde{e}|\mathcal{L})
, after realising that
p(E=\tilde{e}|\mathcal{L})=p_{A}^{\mathcal{L}}(a=\tilde{e})
, where
p_{A}^{\mathcal{L}}(a)=\sum_{b}p_{AB}^{\mathcal{L}}(a,b|x^{*},y^{*})
is the Alice’s marginal of the local correlation. As the convex decomposition of the observed correlation (
12
) naturally carries over onto the marginal, i.e.:

p_{A}(a)=q^{\mathcal{L}}\,p_{A}^{\mathcal{L}}(a)+(1-q^{\mathcal{L}})\,p_{A}^{\mathcal{N\!L}}(a),

(106)

we can then explicitly compute

p_{A}^{\mathcal{L}}(a)=\begin{cases}\eta_{\mathcal{L}}\,\mathsf{P}^{\mathrm{A}}_{a}-\frac{1-q^{\mathcal{L}}}{q^{\mathcal{L}}}(1-V)\left(\mathsf{Q}^{\mathrm{A}}_{a}-\frac{1}{2}\right)&\text{if }a\in\{0,1\}\\
1-\eta_{\mathcal{L}}=:\bar{\eta}_{\mathcal{L}}&\text{if }a=\varnothing\end{cases},

(107)

after substituting for the observed Alice’s marginal,
p_{A}(a)=\sum_{b}p_{AB}(a,b)
, according to the lossy correlation (
85
), while the nonlocal contribution in Eq. (
106
) corresponds to the noiseless
p_{A}^{\mathcal{N\!L}}(a)=\mathsf{Q}^{\mathrm{A}}_{a}
. We also define
\eta_{\mathcal{L}}
as above in Eq. (
80
), which should be understood as the effective “local” detection efficiency.

Finally, we arrive at the expression for the PA-term as

H(A^{\prime}|E)=(1-q^{\mathcal{L}})\,H(A^{\prime}|E=\,?)+q^{\mathcal{L}}\left[p_{A}^{\mathcal{L}}(0)\,H\!\left(\mathcal{S}_{\cdot 0}\right)+p_{A}^{\mathcal{L}}(1)\,H\!\left(\mathcal{S}_{\cdot 1}\right)+p_{A}^{\mathcal{L}}(\varnothing)\,H\!\left(\mathcal{S}_{\cdot\varnothing}\right)\right],

(108)

where we should recall that
H\left(A^{\prime}|E=?\right)
is the conditional entropy of Alice’s outputs applicable whenever Eve distributes the nonlocal correlation within the CC attack, i.e.
p_{AB}^{\mathcal{N\!L}}(a)=\mathsf{Q}^{\mathrm{A}}_{a}
, and Alice preprocesses the outcome
A
onto
A^{\prime}
according to the map
\mathcal{S}
.

In what follows, we calculate in detail the PA-term (
108
) for the preprocessing strategies of Alice listed in Tab.
1
, as considered above in the evaluation of the EC-term, as well as the other two cases of random binning with and without noisy preprocessing.

C.2.1
No preprocessing or any deterministic binning

Whenever the matrix
\mathcal{S}
describes a stochastic map that is deterministic, i.e. contains only 0s or 1s as its entries, the whole second term in Eq. (
108
) identically vanishes. On the other hand, as within nonlocal rounds inconclusive outcomes never occur, and so any operations on
\varnothing
-outcomes are never performed, any binning strategy does not affect the first term in Eq. (
108
). Thus, we can write the PA-term in absence of preprocessing or for any deterministic binning as

H(A^{\prime}|E)_{\text{no-prep}}=H(A^{\prime}|E)_{\text{det}}=(1-q^{\mathcal{L}})\,H(A^{\prime}|E=\,?)=(1-q^{\mathcal{L}})\,h\!\left[\mathsf{Q}_{0}^{\mathrm{A}}\right].

(109)

C.2.2
Deterministic binning with noisy preprocessing (bit-flip)

In case Alice decides to further “noisy preprocess” her outcomes after having binned
\varnothing
to
0
, then the overall preprocessing map she applies corresponds to the
\mathcal{S}
-matrix introduced in Eq. (
94
). As a result, the first term in Eq. (
108
) can be obtained from Eq. (
109
) after including a bit-flip occurring with probability
\mathsf{p}
, while the second term in Eq. (
108
) is then no longer zero, as the entropy for each column of
\mathcal{S}
equals now
h[\mathsf{p}]
. Thus, the full PA-term (
108
) then reads

H(A^{\prime}|E)_{\text{det+n.p.}}=(1-q^{\mathcal{L}})\,h\!\left[(1-\mathsf{p})\,\mathsf{Q}_{0}^{\mathrm{A}}+\mathsf{p}\,\mathsf{Q}_{1}^{\mathrm{A}}\right]+q^{\mathcal{L}}\,h[\mathsf{p}].

(110)

C.2.3
Random binning

As before, the first term in Eq. (
108
) is unaffected by any binning of the
\varnothing
-outcomes and, hence, also when Alice bins these randomly. However, the second term in Eq. (
108
) must now be evaluated based on the stochastic matrix
\mathcal{S}
given in Eq. (
97
), which is no longer deterministic—its last column yields a non-trivial contribution. Hence, for random binning of inconclusive outcomes we obtain

H(A^{\prime}|E)_{\text{rand}}=(1-q^{\mathcal{L}})\,h\!\left[\mathsf{Q}_{0}^{\mathrm{A}}\right]+q^{\mathcal{L}}\bar{\eta}_{\mathcal{L}}\,h\!\left[\frac{1}{2}\right]=(1-q^{\mathcal{L}})\,h\!\left[\mathsf{Q}_{0}^{\mathrm{A}}\right]+1-\eta.

(111)

C.2.4
Random binning with noisy preprocessing (bit-flip)

In case Alice decides to further “noisy preprocess” her outcomes after having binned
\varnothing
randomly to 0 and 1, she, in fact, implements the stochastic matrix
\mathcal{S}
given in Eq. (
101
). Within the first term of Eq. (
108
) one has to account for the bit-flip occurring with probability
\mathsf{p}
and arrives at the same expression as in Eq. (
110
). Whereas for the second term, we note that the entropy of the first two columns of
\mathcal{S}
in Eq. (
101
) is then
h[\mathsf{p}]
, while the entropy of the last column is 1. Therefore, we have

H(A^{\prime}|E)_{\text{rand+n.p.}}=(1-q^{\mathcal{L}})h[(1-\mathsf{p})\,\mathsf{Q}_{0}^{\mathrm{A}}+\mathsf{p}\,\mathsf{Q}_{1}^{\mathrm{A}}]+(\eta-1+q^{\mathcal{L}})\,h[\mathsf{p}]+1-\eta.

(112)

Appendix D
CC-based upper bounds on key rates and the resulting noise thresholds

D.1
One-way protocols involving maximally entangled states

In this section we utilise the formulae derived in App.
C

83

The goal is to determine analytically the tolerable noise thresholds below which no key rate can be distilled, some of which are listed in Table
1

In this section we assume that the parties ideally measure the pure, maximally entangled state with
\theta=\pi/2
in (

\eta

, we consider here specifically two cases of purely lossy correlations (with

D.1.1
Finite detection efficiency

Given perfect visibility (

) but imperfect detection efficiency (
\eta<1
), the correlation (
85
) used for the key generation simplifies to the purely lossy one (
B.2
) with all
\mathsf{P}_{ab}=\mathsf{Q}_{ab}

\mathsf{Q}_{ab}=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{a,b}
with marginals
\mathsf{Q}^{\mathrm{A}}_{a}=\mathsf{Q}^{\mathrm{B}}_{b}=\frac{1}{2}
, whereas for the 2233-protocol
\mathsf{Q}_{00}=\mathsf{Q}_{11}=(2-\sqrt{2})/8
and
\mathsf{Q}_{01}=\mathsf{Q}_{10}=(2+\sqrt{2})/8
if
x^{*}=y^{*}=1
, and
\mathsf{Q}_{00}=\mathsf{Q}_{11}=(2+\sqrt{2})/8
,
\mathsf{Q}_{01}=\mathsf{Q}_{10}=(2-\sqrt{2})/8
otherwise (with marginal probabilities also always equal to
1/2
).

No preprocessing.

In absence of any preprocessing map, we use Eqs. (
89
) and (
109
) to calculate
r_{\text{1-way}\!,\text{no-prep}}^{\uparrow}=H(A^{\prime}|E)_{\text{no-prep}}-H(A^{\prime}|B)_{\text{no-prep}}
, which after substituting also for the
\mathsf{Q}
-probabilities of the 2333-protocol and the optimal local weight (
75

r_{\text{1-way}\!,\text{no-prep}}^{\uparrow}(\eta)=\eta\left[4\eta+\log_{2}\eta-2\sqrt{2}(1-\eta)\right]+2\log_{2}(1-\eta)-3\eta,

(113)

and leads to the critical detection efficiency

{\eta}_{\mathrm{crit}}^{\text{no-prep}}\approx 91.85\%.

(114)

Following the same steps for the 2233-protocol one arrives at the formula for
r_{\text{1-way}\!,\text{no-prep}}^{\uparrow}
with a zero at
{\eta}_{\mathrm{crit}}^{\text{no-prep}}\approx 96.90\%

irrespectively of the particular choice of key settings
x^{*},y^{*}\in\{0,1\}
.

Deterministic binning.

In case Alice applies deterministic binning as her preprocessing strategy, we use Eqs. (
93
) and (
109
) to calculate
r_{\text{1-way}\!,\text{det}}^{\uparrow}=H(A^{\prime}|E)_{\text{det}}-H(A^{\prime}|B)_{\text{det}}
instead, which after substituting for the
\mathsf{Q}
-probabilities of the 2333-protocol and the optimal local weight (
75

r_{\text{1-way}\!,\text{det}}^{\uparrow}(\eta)=\eta\left(3\eta+2\sqrt{2}\eta+\frac{\log_{2}\eta}{2}-2\sqrt{2}\right)-\frac{1-\eta}{2}\left[-(2-\eta)\log_{2}(2-\eta)-\eta\log_{2}(1-\eta)\right]-1-\eta,

(115)

and leads to the critical detection efficiency

{\eta}_{\mathrm{crit}}^{\text{det}}\approx 89.16\%.

(116)

The fact that

r_{\text{1-way}\!,\text{det}}^{\uparrow}

{\eta}_{\mathrm{crit}}^{\text{det}}\approx 94.80\%

Deterministic binning with noisy preprocessing.

If Alice decides to apply noisy preprocessing apart from binning deterministically her inconclusive outcomes
\varnothing

r_{\text{1-way}\!,\text{det+n.p.}}^{\uparrow}=H(A^{\prime}|E)_{\text{det+n.p.}}-H(A^{\prime}|B)_{\text{det+n.p.}}

r_{\text{1-way}\!,\text{det+n.p.}}^{\uparrow}(\eta)=1-q^{\mathcal{L}}+q^{\mathcal{L}}\,h[\mathsf{p}]-\frac{\eta}{2}\,h\!\left[\mathsf{p}\right]-\frac{\eta}{2}\,h\!\left[(1-\mathsf{p})\left(1-\eta\right)+\mathsf{p}\eta\right]-(1-\eta)\,h\!\left[(1-\mathsf{p})\left(1-\frac{\eta}{2}\right)+\mathsf{p}\left(\frac{\eta}{2}\right)\right].

(117)

Substituting then for the optimal local weight,
q^{\mathcal{L}}
in Eq. (
75

D.1.3
Optimisation over all preprocessing maps

In this section, we discuss the results obtained for the computation of the
general
upper bounds applicable to one-way key rates (
4
)—the asymptotic one-way key rates optimised over all preprocessing strategies including not just the stochastic mapping applied by Alice,
p_{A^{\prime}|A}
, on her variable
A
, but also the extra message
M
she prepares by applying
p_{M^{\prime}|A^{\prime}}
on
A^{\prime}
and sends publicly to Bob, i.e.:

r_{\text{1-way}}(A\to B)\qquad\leq\qquad r_{\text{1-way}}^{\uparrow}\coloneqq\max\limits_{p_{A^{\prime}|A},\;p_{M|A^{\prime}}}I(A^{\prime}\!:\!B|M)-I(A^{\prime}\!:\!E|M),

(130)

where the so-defined
r_{\text{1-way}}^{\uparrow}
corresponds to the upper bound (
6
) being now crucially maximised over all the
p_{A^{\prime}|A}
and
p_{M|A^{\prime}}
preprocessing maps, including ones with
|A^{\prime}|>2
or
|M|>2
. In particular, we perform the maximisation in Eq. (
130
) numerically by means of heuristic methods, despite dealing with a non-convex optimisation problem. This allows us to, at least numerically, determine
r_{\text{1-way}}^{\uparrow}
while firstly accounting for finite detection efficiency (
\eta<1
with purely lossy correlation (
B.2
) being shared) within the CHSH-based 2333- and 2233-protocols.
In a similar manner, we then consider the visibility to be finite instead (
V<1
), and determine
r_{\text{1-way}}^{\uparrow}
for the corresponding CHSH-based 2322- and 2222-protocols.
These upper bounds can then be used to determine
universal
noise thresholds
\check{\eta}_{\mathrm{crit}}
and
\check{V}_{\mathrm{crit}}
, below which
no
key can be distilled with one-way communication—these appear in Table
1
(column ‘
any
’) and in Fig.
4
of the main text. However, in order to perform the numerical optimisation, we must decide on the number of outcomes for the discrete random variables
A^{\prime}
and
M
, which, in principle, can be as large as possible. We proceed phenomenologically, i.e. in each case we raise the outcome-number by one, until the moment we can conclude that no further increase is necessary.

Finally, let us emphasise that we perform the optimisation here over preprocessing strategies for the same CHSH-optimal correlations, for which the thresholds in App.
D.1
were derived, utilising maximally entangled states
|\hskip 0.7pt\Phi^{+}\rangle
and standard CHSH measurements (
40
).

Finite detection efficiency.

In our optimisation we directly seek the minimal detection efficiency,
\eta
, such that
r_{\text{1-way}}^{\uparrow}=0
, which then constitutes the desired universal threshold,
\check{\eta}_{\mathrm{crit}}
, below which no key can be extracted with one-way communication. We first consider the 2333-protocol with measurement settings
x^{*},y^{*}=\{0,2\}
, and then briefly discuss the 2233-protocol case with
x^{*},y^{*}\in\{0,1\}
.

We start by optimising the bound (
130
) only over the
p_{A^{\prime}|A}
maps, while disregarding the
p_{M|A^{\prime}}
maps. We perform the maximisation over all corresponding stochastic matrices
\mathcal{S}_{A\to A^{\prime}}
of size
|A^{\prime}|\times|A|
, where we vary the outcome number
2\leq|A^{\prime}|\leq 7
(
|A|=3
is fixed with
A\in\{0,1,\varnothing\}
by the lossy correlation
p_{AB}(a,b)
in Eq. (
B.2
) considered). Independently of the outcome number
|A^{\prime}|
, we always arrive at the critical detection efficiency:

\check{\eta}_{\mathrm{crit}}^{A\to A^{\prime}}\approx 87.0105\%,

(131)

which coincides up to our best-achieved numerical precision with the critical efficiency attainable with random binning followed by noisy preprocessing, i.e.
{\eta}_{\mathrm{crit}}^{\text{rand+n.p.}}
in Eq. (
124
). Hence, as the critical efficiency (
131
) applies to all preprocessing strategies of Alice
p_{A^{\prime}|A}
, we conjecture that the strategy of random binning combined with noisy preprocessing is the optimal form of defense against the CC attack, when Alice is not utilising the message
M
sent to Bob. However, we observe that the numerical optimisation converges to different stochastic matrices leading to the critical efficiency (
131
), suggesting that the optimal preprocessing strategy is then not unique.

Secondly, we incorporate into the maximisation of (
130
) the optimisation over both
p_{A^{\prime}|A}
and
p_{M|A^{\prime}}
, which correspond to some choice of
\mathcal{S}_{A\to A^{\prime}}
and
\mathcal{S}_{A^{\prime}\to M}
stochastic matrices of dimensions
|A|\times|A^{\prime}|
and
|A^{\prime}|\times|M|
, respectively. Allowing the outcome numbers to range in
3\leq|A^{\prime}|\leq 5
and
2\leq|M|\leq 5
, we surprisingly observe that the upper bound
r_{\text{1-way}}^{\uparrow}
can be increased thanks to inclusion of the
p_{M|A^{\prime}}
mapping. This, in turn, allows to lower the required critical efficiency to

\check{\eta}_{\mathrm{crit}}^{A\to A^{\prime}\to M}=\check{\eta}_{\mathrm{crit}}^{A\to M}\approx 85.3553\%.

(132)

Furthermore, we observe this to be possible when considering already
|A^{\prime}|=3
,
|M|=2
(but not for
|A^{\prime}|=2
,
|M|=2
for which the value (
131

However, as noted in Eq. (
132
), we find that the inclusion of the map
p_{A^{\prime}|A}
is then unnecessary—it is sufficient for Alice to use the “raw”
A
-variable for the key and send some
A

M
to Bob as the best preprocessing strategy. In particular, we establish the same critical efficiency (
132

\mathcal{S}_{A\to M}
with
2\leq|M|\leq 5
, implying that the outcome number
|M|=2
is already sufficient, as the optimal maps always possess the crucial feature of effectively “singling out” the
\varnothing
-outcome of Alice within the message send to Bob. In particular, it is always optimal for Alice to simply announce to Bob whether she has a conclusive outcome or not, which is the bit of information to be encoded in
M

A=\varnothing
never occurs in the latter case. Hence, as Eve perfectly knows the outcomes of Alice whenever a local correlation is shared, she also always knows whenever Alice records any inconclusive outcome
\varnothing
. Note also that Alice announcing the
\varnothing

As we now show, the critical value (
132

A\in\{0,1,\varnothing\}
the stochastic matrix

\mathcal{S}_{A\to M}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&1&0\\

(133)

Still, note that the binning procedure does not change the nature of the protocol, which remains

or
2233

\mathsf{Q}_{00}=\cos^{2}\frac{\theta}{2},\quad\mathsf{Q}_{11}=\sin^{2}\frac{\theta}{2},\quad\mathsf{Q}_{01}=\mathsf{Q}_{10}=0,\quad\mathsf{Q}_{0}^{\mathrm{A}}=\mathsf{Q}_{0}^{\mathrm{B}}=\cos^{2}\frac{\theta}{2},\quad\mathsf{Q}_{1}^{\mathrm{A}}=\mathsf{Q}_{1}^{\mathrm{B}}=\sin^{2}\frac{\theta}{2}.

(171)

However, let us emphasise that other measurement settings, which are chosen to maximise the CHSH functional (

Deterministic binning.

In case Alice applies deterministic binning as her preprocessing strategy, we use again Eqs. (

r_{\text{1-way}\!,\text{det}}^{\uparrow}=H(A^{\prime}|E)_{\text{det}}-H(A^{\prime}|B)_{\text{det}}
. However, in contrast to Eq. (

\mathsf{Q}
-probabilities (

in Eq. (

r_{\text{1-way}\!,\text{det}}^{\uparrow}(\eta,\theta)\;\underset{\theta\to 0}{=}\;\eta(3\eta-2)\;h\!\left[\cos^{2}\frac{\theta}{2}\right]-\eta\sin^{2}\frac{\theta}{2}h[\eta]-(1-\eta)h\!\left[\eta\sin^{2}\frac{\theta}{2}\right],

(172)

D.4
One-way protocols involving partially entangled states with postselection

In this last section, we derive an upper bound on the DW rate, Eq. (
5
) of the main text, that now incorporates a postselection (PS) step, being used to certify the security of the protocol considered in Ref.
[
33
]
, i.e.:

{r}_{\text{DW}}^{\text{PS}}:=p_{\mathcal{V}_{p}}\left[H(A|\mathrm{E},\mathcal{V}_{p})-H(A|B,\mathcal{V}_{p})\right]\geq p_{\mathcal{V}_{p}}\left[H_{\mathrm{min}}(A|\mathrm{E},\mathcal{V}_{p})-H(A|B,\mathcal{V}_{p})\right],

(178)

where
\mathcal{V}_{p}
indicates successful postselection occurring with probability
p_{\mathcal{V}_{p}}
;
H(A|\mathrm{E},\dots)
denotes the von Neumann entropy conditioned on the information possessed by the most general quantum eavesdropper Eve (hence the roman letter
\mathrm{E}
instead of an italic
E
that would correspond to a classical random variable), which can in turn be lower-bounded by the min-entropy
H_{\mathrm{min}}(A|\mathrm{E},\dots)
. The CC attack, in which Eve holds a classical variable
E
, allows us to directly compute the upper bound on the PA-term, i.e.:

H_{\mathrm{min}}(A|\mathrm{E},\mathcal{V}_{p})\leq H(A|\mathrm{E},\mathcal{V}_{p})\leq H(A|E,\mathcal{V}_{p}),

(179)

however, we drop for simplicity the conditioning on the postselected subset
\mathcal{V}_{p}
in what follows.

In particular, we write the upper bound determined by the CC attack as just
\frac{{r}_{\text{DW}}^{\text{PS}}}{p_{\mathcal{V}_{p}}}\leq H(A|E)-H(A|B)
, and determine the analytic form of the corresponding EC- and PA-terms,
H(A|B)
and
H(A|E)
, respectively. These may then be evaluated explicitly given a particular form of the observed correlation
p_{AB}^{\textrm{obs}}(a,b|x,y)
—here, see Sec.
5.4.1
of the main text, we consider the purely lossy correlation (
4.2
,
B.2
) with
\eta<1
and
V=1
in Eq. (
85
).

D.4.1
The EC-term
H(A|B)

We adopt the notation of Ref.
[
33
]
and consider the 2333-protocol with the honest users, Alice and Bob, ideally sharing a partially entangled two-qubit state:

\left|\psi(\theta)\right\rangle_{\mathrm{AB}}=\cos\theta\,\left|00\right\rangle+\sin\theta\,\left|11\right\rangle.

(180)

Alice uses two measurement settings
x\in\{1,2\}
, while Bob three settings
y\in\{1,2,3\}
, each corresponding to a projective measurement
\{\Pi(\phi),\mathbf{1}-\Pi(\phi)\}
parametrised by the angle
\phi
, where

\Pi(\phi)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\cos\left(\phi/2\right)^{2}&\cos\left(\phi/2\right)\sin\left(\phi/2\right)\\
\cos\left(\phi/2\right)\sin\left(\phi/2\right)&\sin\left(\phi/2\right)^{2}\end{array}\right).

(181)

This is equivalent to considering projective measurements
\{\frac{\mathbf{1}\pm\Pi(\phi)}{2}\}
and
\Pi(\phi)=\cos\phi\,\sigma_{z}+\sin\phi\,\sigma_{x}
. For future reference, the key settings are set to
x^{*}=1
,
y^{*}=3
.

The lossy correlation shared by Alice and Bob is defined in Eqs. (
4.2
) and (
B.2
) for finite detection efficiency
\eta
(and
\bar{\eta}\coloneqq 1-\eta)
as—dropping the
(x,y)
-dependence and the ‘obs’ superscript for convenience, as in Eq. (
85
):

p_{AB}(a,b)\quad=\quad\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr a\setminus b&0&1&\varnothing\\
\hline\cr 0&\eta^{2}\,\mathsf{Q}_{00}&\eta^{2}\,\mathsf{Q}_{01}&\eta\bar{\eta}\mathsf{Q}^{\mathrm{A}}_{0}\\
\hline\cr 1&\eta^{2}\,\mathsf{Q}_{10}&\eta^{2}\,\mathsf{Q}_{11}&\eta\bar{\eta}\mathsf{Q}^{\mathrm{A}}_{1}\\
\hline\cr\varnothing&\bar{\eta}\eta\mathsf{Q}^{\mathrm{B}}_{0}&\bar{\eta}\eta\mathsf{Q}^{\mathrm{B}}_{1}&\bar{\eta}^{2}\\
\hline\cr\end{array}\,,

(182)

where
\varnothing
denotes the no-click event, while
\mathsf{Q}_{ab}
are the ideal probabilities observed in the absence of losses, i.e.:

\mathsf{Q}_{ab}=\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{{A_{a|x^{*}}\otimes B_{b|y^{*}}\;|\hskip 0.7pt\psi(\theta)\rangle_{\mathrm{AB}}\langle\psi(\theta)\hskip 0.7pt|}\right\}

(183)

with

A_{a|x^{*}}=\delta_{a,0}\Pi(\phi_{x^{*}})+\delta_{a,1}\left(1-\Pi(\phi_{x^{*}})\right),\quad B_{b|y^{*}}=\delta_{b,0}\Pi(\phi_{y^{*}})+\delta_{b,1}\left(1-\Pi(\phi_{y^{*}})\right).

(184)

The marginals are defined as
\mathsf{Q}^{\mathrm{A}}_{0}=\mathsf{Q}_{00}+\mathsf{Q}_{01}
,
\mathsf{Q}^{\mathrm{A}}_{1}=\mathsf{Q}_{10}+\mathsf{Q}_{11}
, and similarly for Bob.

After Alice bins deterministically
\varnothing\to 1
(but not Bob
[
52
]
), the shared correlation reads

p_{AB}^{\text{det}}(a,b)\quad=\quad\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr a\setminus b&0&1&\varnothing\\
\hline\cr 0&\eta^{2}\,\mathsf{Q}_{00}&\eta^{2}\,\mathsf{Q}_{01}&\eta\bar{\eta}\mathsf{Q}^{\mathrm{A}}_{0}\\
\hline\cr 1&\eta^{2}\,\mathsf{Q}_{10}+\bar{\eta}\eta\mathsf{Q}^{\mathrm{B}}_{0}&\eta^{2}\,\mathsf{Q}_{11}+\bar{\eta}\eta\mathsf{Q}^{\mathrm{B}}_{1}&\eta\bar{\eta}\mathsf{Q}^{\mathrm{A}}_{1}+\bar{\eta}^{2}\\
\hline\cr\end{array}\,.

(185)

She then performs the postselection step, i.e. she keeps the events when she measured ‘1’ with probability
\mathsf{q}
and discards them with probability
1-\mathsf{q}
. Bob does the same but, importantly, he postselects both ‘1’ and
\varnothing
results, because of
\varnothing
being equivalent to ‘1’ with respect to postselection (which should be done as if Bob also binned his data
[
52
]
). This corresponds to both of them defining a new variable ‘D’ signifying discarded bits. We will ignore these discarded outcomes, but let us for completeness write down the full correlation including the ‘D’ outcome. In order to do so, let us express (
185
) for short as

p_{AB}^{\text{det}}(a,b)\quad\coloneqq\quad\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr a\setminus b&0&1&\varnothing\\
\hline\cr 0&q_{00}&q_{01}&q_{0\varnothing}\\
\hline\cr 1&q_{10}&q_{11}&q_{1\varnothing}\\
\hline\cr\end{array}\,,

(186)

so that the ‘full’ postselected (fPS) correlation then reads

p_{AB}^{\text{fPS}}(a,b)\quad=\quad\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr a\setminus b&0&1&\varnothing&\mathrm{D}\\
\hline\cr 0&q_{00}&\mathsf{q}\,q_{01}&\mathsf{q}\,q_{0\varnothing}&(1-\mathsf{q})\,(q_{01}+q_{0\varnothing})\\
\hline\cr 1&\mathsf{q}\,q_{10}&\mathsf{q}^{2}\,q_{11}&\mathsf{q}^{2}\,q_{1\varnothing}&\mathsf{q}(1-\mathsf{q})(q_{11}+q_{1\varnothing})\\
\hline\cr\mathrm{D}&(1-\mathsf{q})\,q_{10}&\mathsf{q}(1-\mathsf{q})\,q_{11}&\mathsf{q}(1-\mathsf{q})\,q_{1\varnothing}&(1-\mathsf{q})^{2}(q_{11}+q_{1\varnothing})\\
\hline\cr\end{array}\,.

(187)

Throwing away the discarded bits, i.e. columns and rows of Tab. (
187
) labelled by ‘D’, we arrive at the desired postselected (PS) correlation:

p_{AB}^{\text{PS}}(a,b)\quad=\quad\frac{1}{P(\mathcal{V}_{p})}\;\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr a\setminus b&0&1&\varnothing\\
\hline\cr 0&q_{00}&\mathsf{q}\,q_{01}&\mathsf{q}\,q_{0\varnothing}\\
\hline\cr 1&\mathsf{q}\,q_{10}&\mathsf{q}^{2}\,q_{11}&\mathsf{q}^{2}\,q_{1\varnothing}\\
\hline\cr\end{array}\,,

(188)

where the probability of successful postselection is then given by

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{V}_{p})

\displaystyle\coloneqq q_{00}+\mathsf{q}(q_{01}+q_{0\varnothing}+q_{10})+\mathsf{q}^{2}(q_{11}+q_{1\varnothing})

\displaystyle=\eta^{2}\,\mathsf{Q}_{00}+\mathsf{q}\left[\eta^{2}\left(\mathsf{Q}_{01}+\mathsf{Q}_{10}\right)+\bar{\eta}\eta\left(\mathsf{Q}^{\mathrm{A}}_{0}+\mathsf{Q}^{\mathrm{B}}_{0}\right)\right]+\mathsf{q}^{2}\left[\eta^{2}\,\mathsf{Q}_{11}+\eta\bar{\eta}\left(\mathsf{Q}^{\mathrm{A}}_{1}+\mathsf{Q}^{\mathrm{B}}_{1}\right)+\bar{\eta}^{2}\right],

(189)

with the full expression obtained after substituting explicitly for
p_{AB}^{\text{det}}(a,b)
according to Eq. (
185
).

In general, the postselected marginals of Bob read

p_{B}^{\text{PS}}(b)=\frac{1}{P(\mathcal{V}_{p})}\begin{cases}q_{00}+\mathsf{q}\,q_{10}&\text{if }b=0\\
\mathsf{q}\,q_{01}+\mathsf{q}^{2}\,q_{11}&\text{if }b=1\\
\mathsf{q}\,q_{0\varnothing}+\mathsf{q}^{2}\,q_{1\varnothing}&\text{if }b=\varnothing\end{cases},

(190)

and the relevant conditional probability distribution is obtained by dividing the columns of (
188
) by the corresponding terms in (
190
), i.e.:

p_{AB}^{\text{PS}}(a|b)\quad=\quad\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr a\setminus b&0&1&\varnothing\\
\hline\cr 0&q_{00}/(q_{00}+\mathsf{q}\,q_{10})&\mathsf{q}\,q_{01}/(\mathsf{q}\,q_{01}+\mathsf{q}^{2}\,q_{11})&\mathsf{q}\,q_{0\varnothing}/(\mathsf{q}\,q_{0\varnothing}+\mathsf{q}^{2}\,q_{1\varnothing})\\
\hline\cr 1&\mathsf{q}\,q_{10}/(q_{00}+\mathsf{q}\,q_{10})&\mathsf{q}^{2}\,q_{11}/(\mathsf{q}\,q_{01}+\mathsf{q}^{2}\,q_{11})&\mathsf{q}^{2}\,q_{1\varnothing}/(\mathsf{q}\,q_{0\varnothing}+\mathsf{q}^{2}\,q_{1\varnothing})\\
\hline\cr\end{array}\,.

(191)

Hence, we can now evaluate explicitly the conditional entropy
H(A|B)
, i.e. the EC-term, as:

H(A|B)=\sum_{b}p_{B}^{\text{PS}}(b)\,H(A|B=b)=p_{B}^{\text{PS}}(0)\,h\!\left[\frac{q_{00}}{q_{00}+\mathsf{q}\,q_{10}}\right]+p_{B}^{\text{PS}}(1)\,h\!\left[\frac{\mathsf{q}\,q_{01}}{\mathsf{q}\,q_{01}+\mathsf{q}^{2}\,q_{11}}\right]+p_{B}^{\text{PS}}(\varnothing)\,h\!\left[\frac{\mathsf{q}\,q_{0\varnothing}}{\mathsf{q}\,q_{0\varnothing}+\mathsf{q}^{2}\,q_{1\varnothing}}\right],

(192)

where
h[x]
is again the binary entropy function.

D.4.2
The PA-term
H(A|E)

Let us recall again that within the CC attack here considered Eve distributes a noiseless correlation in each non-local round that occur with probability
q^{\mathcal{N\!L}}\coloneqq 1-q^{\mathcal{L}}
. In contrast, whenever she distributes a local correlation with probability
q^{\mathcal{L}}
(the local weight), she perfectly knows the outcome of both Alice and Bob. Hence, denoting generally by
\mathsf{P}_{ab}^{\mathcal{L}}
the resulting correlation shared by Alice and Bob within the local rounds after they bin their
\varnothing
-outcomes onto ‘1’, we may write the overall tripartite correlation specifying the CC attack as

p_{ABE}(a,b,e)=q^{\mathcal{N\!L}}\begin{bmatrix}\mathsf{Q}_{00}&\mathsf{Q}_{01}\\
\mathsf{Q}_{10}&\mathsf{Q}_{11}\end{bmatrix}\delta_{e,?}+q^{\mathcal{L}}\begin{bmatrix}\mathsf{P}_{00}^{\mathcal{L}}\delta_{e,00}&\mathsf{P}_{01}^{\mathcal{L}}\delta_{e,01}\\
\mathsf{P}_{10}^{\mathcal{L}}\delta_{e,10}&\mathsf{P}_{11}^{\mathcal{L}}\delta_{e,11}\end{bmatrix},

(193)

where the random variable of Eve,
E
, consists of two bits (one for Alice and one for Bob) and an extra outcome ‘?’ representing her lack of knowledge. Note that, as it will be clear from the calculation below, without loss of generality we could have also ignored the second bit of Eve, because in the following we are interested only in the correlations between her and Alice—assumed to be the party performing PA within the one-way paradigm of key distribution. Moreover, recall that, after tracing out Eve, Alice and and Bob must recover their correct shared correlation that corresponds the case of finite detection efficiency
\eta
and binning
\varnothing
-outcomes onto ‘1’—obtained by adding the last column onto the second one and similarly then for the rows in Eq. (
182
). Hence, we also have a constraint that:

p_{AB}(a,b)=q^{\mathcal{N\!L}}\begin{bmatrix}\mathsf{Q}_{00}&\mathsf{Q}_{01}\\
\mathsf{Q}_{10}&\mathsf{Q}_{11}\end{bmatrix}+q^{\mathcal{L}}\begin{bmatrix}\mathsf{P}_{00}^{\mathcal{L}}&\mathsf{P}_{01}^{\mathcal{L}}\\
\mathsf{P}_{10}^{\mathcal{L}}&\mathsf{P}_{11}^{\mathcal{L}}\end{bmatrix}\;\equiv\;\begin{bmatrix}\eta^{2}\mathsf{Q}_{00}&\eta^{2}\mathsf{Q}_{01}+\eta\bar{\eta}\mathsf{Q}^{\mathrm{A}}_{0}\\
\eta^{2}\mathsf{Q}_{10}+\bar{\eta}\eta\mathsf{Q}^{\mathrm{B}}_{0}&\eta^{2}\mathsf{Q}_{11}+\bar{\eta}\eta\left(\mathsf{Q}^{\mathrm{B}}_{1}+\mathsf{Q}^{\mathrm{A}}_{1}\right)+\bar{\eta}^{2}\end{bmatrix},

(194)

which specifies unambiguously the correlation
\mathsf{P}_{ab}^{\mathcal{L}}
distributed by Eve in the local rounds.

Now, we consider the tripartite correlation (
193
) after both Alice and Bob postselect their ‘1’ outcomes with probability
\mathsf{q}
, as in Eq. (
188
), i.e.:

p_{\mathrm{ABE}}^{\text{PS}}(a,b,e)=\frac{1}{P(\mathcal{V}_{p})}\left(q^{\mathcal{N\!L}}\begin{bmatrix}\mathsf{Q}_{00}&\mathsf{q}\,\mathsf{Q}_{01}\\
\mathsf{q}\,\mathsf{Q}_{10}&\mathsf{q}^{2}\,\mathsf{Q}_{11}\end{bmatrix}\delta_{e,?}+q^{\mathcal{L}}\begin{bmatrix}\mathsf{P}_{00}^{\mathcal{L}}\delta_{e,00}&\mathsf{q}\,\mathsf{P}_{01}^{\mathcal{L}}\delta_{e,01}\\
\mathsf{q}\,\mathsf{P}_{10}^{\mathcal{L}}\delta_{e,10}&\mathsf{q}^{2}\,\mathsf{P}_{11}^{\mathcal{L}}\delta_{e,11}\end{bmatrix}\right),

(195)

where the probability of successful postselection,
P(\mathcal{V}_{p})
, can be naturally decomposed as

P(\mathcal{V}_{p})=P(\mathcal{V}_{p},\mathcal{N\!L})+P(\mathcal{V}_{p},\mathcal{L})=P(\mathcal{N\!L})\,P(\mathcal{V}_{p}|\mathcal{N\!L})+P(\mathcal{L})\,P(\mathcal{V}_{p}|\mathcal{L}),

(196)

with
P(\mathcal{L})=q^{\mathcal{L}}
,
P(\mathcal{N\!L})=q^{\mathcal{N\!L}}
by the definition of local weight, and

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{V}_{p}|\mathcal{N\!L})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Q}_{00}+\mathsf{q}\,(\mathsf{Q}_{01}+\mathsf{Q}_{10})+\mathsf{q}^{2}\,\mathsf{Q}_{11},

(197)

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{V}_{p}|\mathcal{L})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{P}_{00}^{\mathcal{L}}+\mathsf{q}\,(\mathsf{P}_{01}^{\mathcal{L}}+\mathsf{P}_{10}^{\mathcal{L}})+\mathsf{q}^{2}\,\mathsf{P}_{11}^{\mathcal{L}},

(198)

being the probabilities of successful postselection given that, respectively, the non-local or the local box is distributed. In particular, we have that

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{V}_{p})

\displaystyle=q^{\mathcal{N\!L}}\left(\mathsf{Q}_{00}+\mathsf{q}\,(\mathsf{Q}_{01}+\mathsf{Q}_{10})+\mathsf{q}^{2}\,\mathsf{Q}_{11}\right)+q^{\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathsf{P}_{00}^{\mathcal{L}}+\mathsf{q}\,(\mathsf{P}_{01}^{\mathcal{L}}+\mathsf{P}_{10}^{\mathcal{L}})+\mathsf{q}^{2}\,\mathsf{P}_{11}^{\mathcal{L}}\right)

\displaystyle=\eta^{2}\mathsf{Q}_{00}+\mathsf{q}\,\left[\eta^{2}\left(\mathsf{Q}_{01}+\mathsf{Q}_{10}\right)+\bar{\eta}\eta\left(\mathsf{Q}^{\mathrm{A}}_{0}+\mathsf{Q}^{\mathrm{B}}_{0}\right)\right]+\mathsf{q}^{2}\,\left[\eta^{2}\mathsf{Q}_{11}+\bar{\eta}\eta\left(\mathsf{Q}^{\mathrm{A}}_{1}+\mathsf{Q}^{\mathrm{B}}_{1}\right)+\bar{\eta}^{2}\right],

(199)

where we consistently reproduce the expression (
189
) after imposing the constraint (
194
) on
\mathsf{P}_{ab}^{\mathcal{L}}
.

Tracing out Bob from (
195
), we obtain

p_{AE}^{\text{PS}}(a,e)\quad=\quad\frac{1}{P(\mathcal{V}_{p})}\;\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr a\setminus e&00&01&10&11&?\\
\hline\cr 0&q^{\mathcal{L}}\mathsf{P}_{00}^{\mathcal{L}}&q^{\mathcal{L}}\mathsf{q}\,\mathsf{P}_{01}^{\mathcal{L}}&0&0&q^{\mathcal{N\!L}}\left(\mathsf{Q}_{00}+\mathsf{q}\,\mathsf{Q}_{01}\right)\\
\hline\cr 1&0&0&q^{\mathcal{L}}\mathsf{q}\,\mathsf{P}_{10}^{\mathcal{L}}&q^{\mathcal{L}}\mathsf{q}^{2}\,\mathsf{P}_{11}^{\mathcal{L}}&q^{\mathcal{N\!L}}\left(\mathsf{q}\,\mathsf{Q}_{10}+\mathsf{q}^{2}\,\mathsf{Q}_{11}\right)\\
\hline\cr\end{array}\,,

(200)

where, as expected, Eve is perfectly correlated with Alice in the local rounds, so that
\forall e\neq?:\;H(A|E=e)=0
.

As a result, the desired entropy of Alice’s outcomes conditioned on Eve is fully determined by the case when Eve distributes a non-local correlation, which occurs with probability

\displaystyle p_{E}(?)

\displaystyle=P(\mathcal{N\!L}|\mathcal{V}_{p})=\frac{P(\mathcal{N\!L},\mathcal{V}_{p})}{P(\mathcal{V}_{p})}=q^{\mathcal{N\!L}}\frac{P(\mathcal{V}_{p}|\mathcal{N\!L})}{P(\mathcal{V}_{p})},

(201)

so that the PA-term reads

\displaystyle H(A|E)=p_{E}(?)\;H(A|E=?)=q^{\mathcal{N\!L}}\frac{P(\mathcal{V}_{p}|\mathcal{N\!L})}{P(\mathcal{V}_{p})}\;h\!\left[\frac{\mathsf{Q}_{00}+\mathsf{q}\,\mathsf{Q}_{01}}{P(\mathcal{V}_{p}|\mathcal{N\!L})}\right],

(202)

being defined completely by the last column of the correlation (
200
).
