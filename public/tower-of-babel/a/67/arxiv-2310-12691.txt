Discrete-to-Continuum Rates of Convergence for p p -Laplacian Regularization
Adrien Weihs, Jalal Fadili, Matthew Thorpe

Abstract
Higher-order regularization problem formulations are popular frameworks used in machine learning, inverse problems and image/signal processing. In this paper, we consider the computational problem of finding the minimizer of the Sobolev W 1 , p \mathrm{W}^{1,p} semi-norm with a data-fidelity term. We propose a discretization procedure and prove convergence rates between our numerical solution and the target function. Our approach consists of discretizing an appropriate gradient flow problem in space and time. The space discretization is a nonlocal approximation of the p p -Laplacian operator and our rates directly depend on the localization parameter ε n \varepsilon_{n} and the time mesh-size τ n \tau_{n} . We precisely characterize the asymptotic behaviour of ε n \varepsilon_{n} and τ n \tau_{n} in order to ensure convergence to the considered minimizer. Finally, we apply our results to the setting of random graph models.



Keywords and phrases.
convergence rates, evolution problems, nonlocal variational problems,
p
-Laplacian, random graph models, asymptotic consistency, regularization

Introduction

In machine learning, inverse problems and image/signal processing, one is often confronted with finding smooth solutions to regression problems. This leads to the introduction of regularization terms in the problem formulation (see for example
[

]
,
[

(1)

for some

, linear operator
\mathcal{A}
, data function
\ell
,
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

.
The first term on the right-hand side of the latter enforces some regularization upon the functions
v
while the second term is a data-fidelity term. For
\mathcal{A}=\mathrm{Id}
, this is also called a fully supervised machine learning problem or a denoising problem in image processing. More precisely, in this paper, we prove convergence rates between our numerical discrete solution and
u_{\infty}
. This type of regularization has been considered for example in
[

]
,
[

]
,
[

]
and
[

In particular, this means that in the former case, emphasis is partly placed on having as few constraints as possible on different parameters as the latter are usually inferred from the data at hand: in our case, as we are choosing all the parameters, this concern is less relevant.

The approach we choose in this paper is to discretize the gradient flow (

-Laplacian operator and its discrete counterpart.

The plan for the discretization follows loosely the strategy in
[
39
]
and
[
61
]
: after choosing an appropriate kernel

p
-Laplacian operator in the continuum and then discretize the latter. The former is inspired by
[
13
]
,
[

]
,
[

where finite-difference approximations of Sobolev norms are discussed in the continuum, while the latter step allows us to use existing results on convergence results in the nonlocal setting as in
[

]
,
[
44
]
,
[
43
]
.

Passing from the nonlocal setting to the local one requires a kernel that is appropriately scaled. For some length-scale
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
, it is shown for example in
[
61
]
,
[
18
]
and
[
2
]
that, ignoring regularity assumptions,

\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{n}^{d+p}}\int K\left(\frac{|y-x|}{\varepsilon_{n}}\right)|u(y)-u(x)|^{p-2}(u(y)-u(x))\,\mathrm{d}y\to\Delta_{p}u(x).

The finite-difference structure of the above nonlocal approximation is essential for obtaining rates in the continuum: indeed, as in
[
18
]
, for smooth enough functions, we pass from finite-differences to derivatives and the rates follow from a conceptually basic Taylor expansion.

The discrete-to-continuum step requires more subtle techniques. In fact, one needs a way to compare functions
\bar{u}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{n}}
and
u:\Omega\mapsto\mathbb{R}
. We discuss various alternatives in Section
1.2
. In this paper, we choose to partition our space
\Omega
into
D_{n}
cells and elements of
\mathbb{R}^{D_{n}}
are injected through the operator
\mathcal{I}:\mathbb{R}^{D_{n}}\to\mathrm{L}^{1}(\Omega)
in the continuum through piecewise constant approximations while continuum functions are projected through the operator
\mathcal{P}:\mathrm{L}^{1}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}^{D_{n}}
onto our cells by averaging on each cell. Using this method, establishing rates between a continuum function and its injected discrete approximation relies on tools from approximation theory, depending on the partition chosen and the regularity assumption of the continuum function. This topic is discussed in greater detail in Section
2.4
. The other central tool for the discrete-to-continuum rates, where the discretization is both in space and time, is the semigroup structure of our solutions to the nonlocal gradient flows. Indeed, relying on some favourable properties of the nonlocal
p
-Laplacian, we are able to deduce strong contraction properties as discussed in Section

.

Combining the discrete-to-continuum rates in the nonlocal setting to the nonlocal-to-local rates in the continuum, we obtain in Corollary

that for

,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{I}_{n}\bar{u}_{n}^{N}-u_{\infty}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\Bigg(\varepsilon_{n}\log(\varepsilon_{n}^{-\kappa})+\varepsilon_{n}^{\kappa/4}(\mathcal{F}(u_{0})-\mathcal{F}(u_{\infty}))^{1/2}

(2)

\displaystyle+\varepsilon_{n}^{-\kappa}\left[n^{-\alpha_{1}}+n^{-\alpha_{2}}+\frac{\log(\varepsilon_{n}^{-\kappa})^{(p-1)}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{d+p+\alpha_{3}}n^{\alpha_{3}}}+\tau_{n}\frac{\log(\varepsilon_{n}^{-\kappa})^{2p-3}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{2(d+p)}}\right]\Biggr)

where

is the solution to (

Second, while we give the precise statement of this result in Section

Third, we note that (

Lastly, coming back to a more data-centric approach, we apply the above-mentioned results to a random graph model. The graph models appear in several applications and we obtain results equivalent to the ones displayed above in Corollary

Functions

Asymptotics

A function

Definition 2.2

(Accretive operator)
.

We say that an operator A on

is accretive if

\|v-w\|\leq\|v-w+\lambda(\hat{v}-\hat{w})\|

for all
\lambda>0

If we assume that

\|v-w\|\leq\lambda\left\|\left(A+\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathrm{Id}\right)(v)-\left(A+\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathrm{Id}\right)(v)\right\|

which implies that

(

.

We say that an operator

\range(\mathrm{Id}+\lambda A)=V

Accretivity of operators can be viewed as a generalization of monotony in Hilbert spaces (see
[
1

\langle v-w,\hat{v}-\hat{w}\rangle_{H}\geq 0

Proposition 2.5

(Equivalence of accretivity and monotony)
.

Let
A
be an operator in a Hilbert space

We specialize our discussion by considering Banach spaces that are spaces of functions defined on some
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
\mathrm{L}^{0}(\Omega)
be the set of measurable functions on
\Omega
that map to

and define the two set of functions

\mathcal{H}=\{h\in\mathrm{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\,|\,0\leq h^{\prime}\leq 1,\text{ $\support(h^{\prime})$ is compact, }h(0)=0\}

and

\mathcal{J}=\{j:\mathbb{R}\mapsto[0,\infty]\,|\,\text{$j$ is convex, lower semi-continuous and satisfies $j(0)=0$}\}.

Then, we can use the following notation for two functions

:

v\llSgt w\quad\text{if and only if }\int_{\Omega}j(v(x))\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\Omega}j(w(x))\,\mathrm{d}x\text{ for all $j\in\mathcal{J}$}.

Definition 2.6

(Completely accretive operators)
.

We say that an operator
A
on
\mathrm{L}^{0}(\Omega)

v-w\llSgt v-w+\lambda(\hat{v}-\hat{w})

for all
\lambda>0

In Section

(3)

Definition 2.9

(Hemicontinuity)

Let

, the function

A weak version of

Lemma 2.10

(Continuity implies hemicontinuity)
.

Let
A:V\mapsto V^{*}
be an operator. If

is continuous, then
A
is hemicontinuous.

The next result deals with the existence of a solution to a nonlinear stationary problem
[
50
, Chapter 2, Theorem 2.1]
. This will be an essential step in the proof of the range condition of our operators in Proposition
3.3
.

Theorem 2.11

(Solutions to nonlinear stationary problems)
.

Let
V
be a separable reflexive Banach space and
A:V\mapsto V^{*}
an operator which is bounded, hemicontinuous and
satisfies

\langle A(v)-A(w),v-w\rangle_{V^{*}}\geq 0\qquad\text{for all $v,w\in V$ as well as}

\frac{\langle A(v),v\rangle_{V^{*}}}{\|v\|_{V}}\to\infty\qquad\text{when $\|v\|_{V}\to\infty$.}

Then, for every
f\in V^{*}
, there exists
u\in V
such that

A(u)=f.

For the existence of the local problem, we will use the following result on nonlinear evolution problems which can be found in
[
50
, Chapter 2, Theorem 1.4 and Remark 1.13]
.

Theorem 2.12

(Solutions to nonlinear evolution problems)
.

Let
H
be an Hilbert space and
V_{i}
reflexive Banach spaces with
V_{i}\subseteq H
and
V_{i}
dense in
H
for
1\leq i\leq q
. Assume that
\cap_{i=1}^{q}V_{i}
is seperable and dense in
H
. For
1\leq i\leq q
, let
A_{i}:V_{i}\mapsto V_{i}^{*}
be an operator which is bounded, hemicontinuous and, for some
1<p_{i}<\infty
and
c_{i}>0
, satisfies:

1.

\|A_{i}(v)\|_{V_{i}^{*}}\leq c_{i}\|v\|_{V_{i}}^{p_{i}-1}
;

2.

\langle A_{i}(v)-A_{i}(w),v-w\rangle_{V_{i}^{*}}\geq 0
for all
v,w\in V_{i}
;

3.

there exists a seminorm
[\cdot]_{i}
on
V_{i}
with constants
\alpha_{i},\lambda_{i},\beta_{i}>0
such that
[\cdot]_{i}+\lambda_{i}\|\cdot\|_{H}\geq\beta_{i}\|\cdot\|_{V_{i}}
on
V_{i}
and
\langle A_{i}(v),v\rangle_{V_{i}^{*}}\geq\alpha_{i}[v]_{i}^{p_{i}}
for all
v\in V_{i}
.

Then, for
u_{0}\in H
and
f\in\sum_{i=1}^{q}\mathrm{L}^{p_{i}^{*}}(0,T;V_{i}^{*})
where
1/p_{i}+1/p_{i}^{*}=1
, there exists a unique function
u
such that

u\in\bigcap_{i=1}^{q}\mathrm{L}^{p_{i}}(0,T;V_{i}),\quad u\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,T;H)

that satisfies

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\sum_{i=1}^{q}A_{i}(u)=f\quad\text{and}\quad u(0)=u_{0}.

2.4
Piecewise constant approximations

We start by introducing a space of functions which will be relevant for our discrete-to-continuum approximations. We refer to
[
64
]
for a detailed discussion.

Definition 2.13

(Lipschitz spaces)
.

Let
\Omega
be an open bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For
g\in\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Omega)
with
q\in[1,+\infty)
, we define the (first-order)
\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Omega)
modulus of smoothness by

\omega(g,h)_{q}=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d},|z|<h}\left(\int_{x,x+z\in\Omega}|g(x+z)-g(x)|^{q}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/q}.

For
0<s\leq 1
, the Lipschitz spaces
\mathrm{Lip}(s,\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Omega))
consist of all functions
g\in\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Omega)
for which

|g|_{\mathrm{Lip}(s,\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Omega))}=\sup_{h>0}h^{-s}\omega(g,h)_{q}<+\infty.

Lipschitz spaces contain functions with, roughly speaking,
s
"derivatives" in
\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Omega)
. We also note that we restrict ourselves to
0<s\leq 1
since for
s\geq 1
, the only functions in
\mathrm{Lip}(s,\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Omega))
are constants by
[
30
, Chapter 2, Proposition 7.1]
. Lipschitz spaces allow for a broad range of functions and namely,
\mathrm{Lip}(1,\mathrm{L}^{1}(\Omega))
contains functions of bounded variation, for example
[
30
, Chapter 2, Lemma 9.2]
.

We will be considering the error rate between a function and its piecewise constant approximation. The results presented below are part of the broader literature on approximation theory and in particular, spline approximations (we refer to
[
30
]
for a review of such topics). We begin by defining operators that will allow us to connect discrete and continuum spaces.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be some bounded set and let
\Pi=\{\pi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{|\Pi|}
be a disjoint partition of
\Omega
with cardinality
|\Pi|
. We define the projection operator
\mathcal{P}_{\Pi}:\mathrm{L}^{1}(\Omega)\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{|\Pi|}
and the injection operator
\mathcal{I}_{\Pi}:\mathbb{R}^{|\Pi|}\mapsto\mathrm{L}^{1}(\Omega)
as

(\mathcal{P}_{\Pi}u)_{i}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{x}(\pi_{i})}\int_{\pi_{i}}u(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{and}\quad(\mathcal{I}_{\Pi}\bar{u})(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{|\Pi|}\bar{u}_{i}\chi_{\pi_{i}}(x)

respectively for
u\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(\Omega)
and
i=1,\dots,|\Pi|
,
\bar{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\Pi|}
and
x\in\Omega
. When using the partition
\{\pi_{i}\times\pi_{j}\}_{i,j=1}^{|\Pi|}
of
\Omega\times\Omega
, by an abuse of notation, we have

(\mathcal{P}_{\Pi}v)_{i,j}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{x}(\pi_{i})\lambda_{x}(\pi_{j})}\int_{\pi_{i}}\int_{\pi_{j}}v(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{and}\quad(\mathcal{I}_{\Pi}\bar{v})(x,y)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{|\Pi|}\bar{v}_{i,j}\chi_{\pi_{i}}(x)\chi_{\pi_{j}}(y)

for
v\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(\Omega\times\Omega)
,
\bar{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\Pi|\times|\Pi|}
and
(x,y)\in\Omega\times\Omega
.

For a function
u
, in order to obtain quantitative rates for
\|u-\mathcal{I}_{\Pi}\mathcal{P}_{\Pi}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}
the choice of
\Pi
and the regularity of
u
are essential. We now describe one construction of a partition
\Pi
but refer to
[
28
]
and references therein for more examples. In order to simplify the discussion, we consider
\Omega=(0,1)^{d}
although some of the results described below hold in more general cases.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define
[n]={1,\dots,n}
and let
{{{\boldsymbol{i}}}}=(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{d})\in[n]^{d}
be a multi-index. We partition
\Omega
into
n^{d}
hypercubes with sides of length
n^{-1}
and denote this partition by
\Pi_{\mathrm{uni},n}=\{\Omega_{n,{{\boldsymbol{i}}}}\}_{{{\boldsymbol{i}}}\in[n]^{d}}
. We have the following approximation lemma whose proof can be found in Section
5.2
.

Lemma 2.14

(Approximation on the uniform partition)
.

Let
\Omega=(0,1)^{d}
,
g\in\mathrm{Lip}(s,\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Omega))
and assume the partition
\Pi_{\mathrm{uni},n}
on
\Omega
. For
0<s\leq 1
and
1\leq q\leq\infty
, we have

\|g-\mathcal{I}_{\Pi_{\mathrm{uni},n}}\mathcal{P}_{\Pi_{\mathrm{uni},n}}g\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}}\leq C|g|_{\mathrm{Lip}(s,\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Omega))}n^{-s},

for some constant
C>0
depending only on
d
.

Furthermore, if
g
is in

\mathrm{C}^{0,\alpha}
for
0<\alpha\leq 1
and
\varepsilon>0
, then:

\|g(\cdot/\varepsilon)-\mathcal{I}_{\Pi_{\mathrm{uni},n}}\mathcal{P}_{\Pi_{\mathrm{uni},n}}g(\cdot/\varepsilon)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}}\leq C\varepsilon^{-\alpha}n^{-\alpha}

for some
C>0
dependant on
d
and
\Omega
.

Remark 2.15

(Rates for functions on the product space)
.

We note that the conclusions of Lemma
2.14
also apply to functions
g
defined on
\Omega\times\Omega
.

2.5
Random graph models

Whenever we deal with random graph models, we will assume that we have the uniform partition
\Pi_{\mathrm{uni},n}=\{\Omega_{n,i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
of
(0,1)
and shall therefore write
\mathcal{P}_{n}=\mathcal{P}_{\Pi_{\mathrm{uni},n}}
and
\mathcal{I}_{n}=\mathcal{I}_{\Pi_{\mathrm{uni},n}}
.

In some applications, data is represented in the form of an undirected graph. One approach to understanding the underlying structure is to analyse the convergence properties of the graph as the number of vertices goes to infinity. We therefore consider graphs through their weight function defined on
[0,1]^{2}
: for a graph
G
with vertices labelled by
[n]
and weight matrix
\{\bar{K}_{n,ij}\}_{i,j=1}^{n}
with
\bar{K}_{n,ii}=0
and
\bar{K}_{n,ji}=\bar{K}_{n,ij}\geq 0
for
i\neq j
, for
(x,y)\in\Omega_{n,i}\times\Omega_{n,j}
, we define
\tilde{K}_{n}(x,y)=\bar{K}_{n,ij}
. The objective is now to analyze the limit of the step-functions
\tilde{K}_{n}
as
n\to\infty
: this is well-known to be a graphon, i.e. a symmetric kernel function in
\mathrm{L}^{1}([0,1]^{2})
(see
[
12
]
and references therein). Given a sequence of graphs from a certain graph model, i.e. a sequence of graphs for which there is a systematic way to determine the graph weights
\{\bar{K}_{n,ij}\}_{i,j=1}^{n}
, we can establish the convergence of the sequence to the corresponding graphon in an appropriate metric.
In the data-centric approach, one tries to fit graph models to data therewith estimating the underlying graphon
(see for example
[
11
]
,
[
74
]
): the intuition here is that graph sequences that have related graphons as limiting points should share similarities.

In this paper, we consider a general sparse random graph model originally introduced and studied by
[
12
]
.

Definition 2.16

(Random graph models)
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\rho_{n}>0
and
\bar{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a symmetric matrix with non-negative entries.
Assume
\rho_{n}\bar{K}_{ij}\leq 1
for all
i,j\in[n]
and
\bar{K}_{ii}=0
for all
i\in[n]
.
Let

\left(\bar{\Lambda}_{n}\right)_{ij}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{\rho_{n}}&\text{with probability }\rho_{n}\bar{K}_{ij}\\
0&\text{else.}\end{array}\right.

We define the random graph
G(n,\bar{\Lambda}_{n})
to be the random graph with vertices
[n]
and weight matrix
\bar{\Lambda}_{n}
.

As a simple example, consider the case where
\rho_{n}=1
and the entries of
\bar{K}
are
\bar{K}_{ij}=p\in(0,1)
for
1\leq i,j\leq n
. Then, vertices
i
and
j
are connected with probability
p
and
G(n,\bar{\Lambda}_{n})
is the Erdős-Rényi graph model
G(n,p)
.

Note that in previous papers (for example
[
33
, Section 3.2]
), the weight matrix only has 0-1 weights but, when the graphs are used in evolution problems, the quantities are normalized by
\rho_{n}^{-1}
which is related to the average degree of the graph. Altering the definition as we did in Definition
2.16
leads to more a straight-forward and clearer problem setting.

In our paper, we will be considering the limit to a local problem and are therefore less concerned with statements related to the convergence of our random graph models. We refer to
[
12
]
and
[
33
, Proposition 3.1]
for more details on the topic however.

Remark 2.17

(Extension of graph models)
.

We mention at the beginning of Section
2.5
that we consider the uniform partition
\Pi_{\mathrm{uni},n}=\{\Omega_{n,i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
of
\Omega=(0,1)
in the setting of random graph models. The reason for choosing
d=1
follows from a standard data-centric approach (see
[
12
, Section 2.7]
): to model the relationships between
n
data points, given a graphon
\tilde{K}
, we usually sample
n
points
x_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\text{Unif}([0,1])
and set graph weights
\bar{K}_{ij}=\tilde{K}(x_{i},x_{j})
for
i\neq j
and
\bar{K}_{ii}=0
. We then create the appropriate graph models (for example as in Definition
2.16
) with the goal of showing that the step-functions associated to these models converge to the initial graphon
\tilde{K}
. We note that the first part of the procedure, i.e. setting the weights
\bar{K}_{ij}
, could work equally well with a function
\tilde{K}:[0,1]^{2d}\mapsto[0,\infty)
with
d\geq 1
; however, the second part of the procedure only makes sense for
d=1
since the convergence of step-functions associated to graphs supposes that the limiting function is also defined on
[0,1]^{2}
(and is therefore a graphon).

As we are not concerned with showing the convergence of our graph models to graphons, we could therefore formulate everything for
d\geq 1
. This would however not be consistent with the standard data-centric approach where random graph models are generated from graphons. An interesting research direction would be to generate random hypergraph models (a hypergraph is a graph that also includes non-pairwise relationships, for example faces connecting three vertices instead of pairwise edges) from functions
\tilde{K}:[0,1]^{2d}\to[0,\infty)
, and show the convergence of step-functions associated to these hypergraphs
to
\tilde{K}
. Our results in Section
4.3
should naturally extend to such models.

3.2
Setting

3.2.1
Discrete problem

Given a partition
\Pi
of
\Omega
and a symmetric
\bar{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\Pi|\times|\Pi|}
, we define the discrete nonlocal
p
-Laplacian operator for
\bar{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\Pi|}
and
1\leq i\leq|\Pi|
as follows:

(\Delta_{p,\Pi}^{\bar{K}}\bar{u})_{i}=-\sum_{j=1}^{|\Pi|}\lambda_{x}(\pi_{j})\bar{K}_{i,j}|(\bar{u})_{j}-(\bar{u})_{i}|^{p-2}((\bar{u})_{j}-(\bar{u})_{i}).

Let
\bar{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\Pi|}
and
\bar{u}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\Pi|}
. We will consider the fully discrete nonlocal evolution problem

\begin{cases}\frac{\bar{u}^{k}-\bar{u}^{k-1}}{\tau^{k-1}}+\mu\Delta_{p,\Pi}^{\bar{K}}\bar{u}^{k}+\bar{G}(\bar{u}^{k})=\bar{f},&\text{for}\quad 1\leq k\leq N\\
\bar{u}(0)=\bar{u}_{0}\end{cases}

(4)

for some
\mu>0
, a positive sequence
\{\tau^{k}\}_{k=1}^{N-1}
(with
\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\tau^{k}=T
)
and linear operator
\bar{G}:\mathbb{R}^{|\Pi|}\to\mathbb{R}^{|\Pi|}
.
We also define
\tau=\max_{k=1,\dots,N}\tau^{k}
and will write
\tau_{n}
for
\tau
when
our sequence
\{\tau^{k}\}_{k=1}^{N-1}
will be indexed by
n
.
We say that
\bar{u}
solves (
4
) with parameters
\bar{K}
,
\bar{f}
and
\bar{u}_{0}
.

Later we choose
\tau^{k}=t^{k+1}-t^{k}
for a discretisation
0=t^{0}<t^{1}<\dots<t^{N}=T
of
[0,T]
and we will need the following two quantities. First, we define the time interpolated version of the injected vectors
\{\bar{u}^{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}
:

\uTimeInter(t,x)=\frac{t^{k}-t}{\tau^{k-1}}\left(\mathcal{I}_{\Pi_{n}}\bar{u}^{k-1}\right)(x)+\frac{t-t^{k-1}}{\tau^{k-1}}\left(\mathcal{I}_{\Pi_{n}}\bar{u}^{k}\right)(x)\text{ for }(t,x)\in(t^{k-1},t^{k}]\times\Omega

and
\uTimeInter(0,x)=\mathcal{I}_{\Pi_{n}}\bar{u}_{0}
.
Second, we define the time injected version of the injected vectors
\{\bar{u}^{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}
:

\uTimeInject(t,x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\mathcal{I}_{\Pi_{n}}\bar{u}^{k}\right)(x)\chi_{(t^{k-1},t^{k}]}(t).

Most of our results will holds for any partition
\Pi
but, keeping in mind that we are ultimately interested in convergence results, meaning the case where
\max_{j\leq|\Pi|}\lambda_{x}(\pi_{j})\to 0
, we will index our partition with a parameter

. We denote the latter by
\Pi_{n}=\{\pi_{j}^{n}\}_{j=1}^{|\Pi_{n}|}
. As an example, one can consider
\Pi_{\mathrm{uni},n}
. For ease of notation we will write
\Delta^{\bar{K}}_{p,n}=\Delta_{p,\Pi_{n}}^{\bar{K}}
,
\mathcal{I}_{n}=\mathcal{I}_{\Pi_{n}},\mathcal{P}_{n}=\mathcal{P}_{\Pi_{n}}
and similarly for other quantities.

3.2.2
Nonlocal problem

Given a function a kernel function
K:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
, we define the nonlocal
p
-Laplacian operator
\Delta_{p}^{K}
for a function
u\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(\Omega)
and

as follows:

\Delta_{p}^{K}u(x)=-\int_{\Omega}K(|x-y|)|u(y)-u(x)|^{p-2}(u(y)-u(x))\,\mathrm{d}y.

For a kernel
K
, we define the function
\tilde{K}:\Omega\times\Omega\mapsto[0,\infty)
by
\tilde{K}(x,y)=K(|x-y|)
. For a general function
v:\Omega\times\Omega\mapsto[0,\infty)

\Delta_{p}^{v}u(x)=-\int_{\Omega}v(x,y)|u(y)-u(x)|^{p-2}(u(y)-u(x))\,\mathrm{d}y.

Furthermore, for
f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
,
p\geq 2
and
q
such that
p^{-1}+q^{-1}=1
, we define the following evolution operator
\mathcal{E}^{K}_{\mathcal{A},f}:\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega)\mapsto\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Omega)
for
\mu>0
, a function
u\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega)
and some linear operator
\mathcal{A}:\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\mapsto\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
:

\mathcal{E}^{K}_{\mathcal{A},f}(u)=\mu\Delta_{p}^{K}u+\mathcal{A}^{*}\mathcal{A}u-f.

The well-posedness of this evolution operator will be discussed in Theorem
3.4
.

We will consider the following nonlocal evolution problem:

\begin{cases}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}u(t,x)+\mathcal{E}^{K}_{\mathcal{A},f}(u(t,x))=0&\text{on}\quad(0,T)\times\Omega,\\
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x).\end{cases}

(5)

We say that
u
solves (
5
) with parameters
K
,
f
and
u_{0}
. In particular, in order to link the above problem with (
1
), we will be interested in (
5
) when
\mathcal{E}^{K}_{\mathcal{A},f}(u)=\mathcal{E}^{K}_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{A}^{*}\ell}(u)
for some
\ell\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
.

We are interested in the following solution which is just of Definition
2.1
using the evolution operator appearing in (

).

Definition 3.1

(Nonlocal problem solution)
.

Assume that Assumptions
S.1
,

,

and

,

,

\lambda_{x}
-a.e. on

and

If we are further given a positive sequence

(6)

3.2.3

The local
p
-Laplacian operator is defined as

\Delta_{p}u=-\diver(|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u).

For

,

,

for some linear operator

Definition 3.2

(Local weak solution)

and

3.3

3.3.1
Well-posedness of the nonlocal continuum gradient flow

In order to prove well-posedness, we loosely follow the strategy in

In the nonlocal case, we require the solutions to be in the same space as the local solution, namely
\mathrm{W}^{1,p}(\Omega)
. This is a natural requirement for establishing rates which is fulfilled when approximating local problems by finite elements as in
[

. This is in contrast with
our finite-differences approach for which conservation of regularity properties of the local problem is not inherent. For a similar problem, regularity of a nonlocal solution is studied in
[
3

.

In the local case, we extend the regularity from
\mathrm{W}^{1,p}(\Omega)
to some fractional Sobolev space

with continuous first, second and third derivatives. In order to align for example with

where functions are taken in

, and due to the regularity of Sobolev spaces
we would like to have

for
k=2,3
. As it will turn out, this uncovers a subtle interplay between regularity and dimension of the underlying space.

Remark 4.11

(Embeddings of
\mathrm{W}^{k+\delta,p}(\Omega)
into
\mathrm{C}^{k}(\Omega)
)
.

In view of Remark
4.10
, we are interested in the embedding of

embeds into

needs to be.

Definition 5.2

(

-convexity)
.

Let

be a normed space.
We say that a function

and

Lemma 5.3

(

-convexity of
\mathcal{F}
)
.

Assume that Assumption
S.1
holds.
Further, assume that
\mathcal{A}
is such that
\mathcal{A}^{*}\mathcal{A}
is coercive with

for all

\mathcal{F}
is

-convex.

Proof.

Since
t\mapsto|t|^{p}
is convex we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(tu+(1-t)v)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu}{p}\int_{\Omega}|t\nabla u+(1-t)\nabla v|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\|t\mathcal{A}u+(1-t)\mathcal{A}v-\ell\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mu}{p}\int_{\Omega}t|\nabla u|^{p}+(1-t)|\nabla v|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}[t^{2}\|\mathcal{A}u-\ell\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}^{2}+(1-t)^{2}\|\mathcal{A}v-\ell\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+2t(1-t)\langle\mathcal{A}v-\ell,\mathcal{A}u-\ell\rangle]

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu}{p}\left[t\|\nabla u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}^{p}+(1-t)\|\nabla v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}^{p}\right]+\frac{1}{2}[t\|\mathcal{A}u-\ell\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}^{2}+(1-t)\|\mathcal{A}v-\ell\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+t(1-t)\left[\langle\ell-\mathcal{A}u,\mathcal{A}u-\ell\rangle+\langle\ell-\mathcal{A}v,\mathcal{A}v-\ell\rangle+2\langle\mathcal{A}v-\ell,\mathcal{A}u-\ell\rangle\right]]

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu}{p}\left[t\|\nabla u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}^{p}+(1-t)\|\nabla v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}^{p}\right]+\frac{1}{2}[t\|\mathcal{A}u-\ell\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}^{2}+(1-t)\|\mathcal{A}v-\ell\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad-t(1-t)\|\mathcal{A}(u-v)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}^{2}]

\displaystyle=t\mathcal{F}(u)+(1-t)\mathcal{F}(v)-\frac{1}{2}t(1-t)\|\mathcal{A}(u-v)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}^{2}.

Now,
\|\mathcal{A}(u-v)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}^{2}=\langle\mathcal{A}^{*}\mathcal{A}(u-v),u-v\rangle\geq c_{\mathcal{A}}\|u-v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}^{2}
and so
we have that
