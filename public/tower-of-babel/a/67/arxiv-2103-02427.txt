Abstract




Abstract:

We consider formal power series
f(x)=a_{1}x+a_{2}x^{2}+\cdots

(a_{1}\neq 0)
, with
coefficients in a field. We revisit the classical subject of iteration of formal power series, the n-fold composition
f^{(n)}(x)=f(f(\cdots(f(x)\cdots))=f^{(n-1)}(f(x))=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}f_{k}^{(n)}x^{k}
for
n=2,3,\ldots
, where
f^{(1)}(x)=f(x)
. The study of this was begun, and the coefficients
f_{k}^{(n)}
where calculated assuming
a_{1}=1
, by Schröder in 1871. The major result of this paper, Theorem
7.1.1
, generalizes Schröder
[
15
]
. It gives explicit formulas for the coefficients
f_{k}^{(n)}
when
a_{1}\neq 0
and it is viewed as an analog to the
n^{th}
Multinomial Theorem Under Multiplication. We prove Schröder’s Theorem using a new and shorter approach. The Recursion Lemma, Lemma
3.1.1
,
which sharpens Cohen’s lemma (Lemma
2.4.2
) is our key tool in the proof of Theorem
7.1.1
and it is one of the most useful recurrence relations for the composition of formal power series. Along the way we develop numerical formulas for
f_{k}^{(n)}~{\rm where}~1\leq k\leq 5
.

Keywords:
Formal Power Series, Multinational, Composition, Cayley, Iteration Schröder’s Theorem, Combinatorics.

1.
Introduction

In 1871, the problem of finding the nth iterative power of a formal power series,
f^{(n)}(x)
, was introduced and solved by the famous mathematician Ernst Schröder in the case
a_{1}=1

[
15
]
. He left the general case open saying that ”the formula when
a_{1}=1
can be found much more easily than in the general case”. He thus asserted that the case
a_{1}\neq 1
was much harder, and this has not been done till now.

A main challenge in Mathematics is to give closed formulas for quantities which are defined recursively.
A formal power series
f
(x) is an expression of the form

f(x)=a_{0}+a_{1}x+a_{2}x^{2}+\cdots
. The word ”formal” indicates that this is actually just the sequence of its coefficients. The composition of
f(x)
and
g(x)
is defined by

(f\circ g)(x)=f(g(x))=a_{1}\cdot g(x)+a_{2}\cdot(g(x))^{2}+\cdots+a_{n}(g(x))^{n}+\cdots

The condition
a_{0}=0
insures that the coefficient of
x^{n}
in
f(g(x))
is a finite sum. In this paper we assume
a_{0}=0
. If
f(x)=a_{1}x+a_{2}x^{2}+\cdots
, our main purpose is to study the recursively defined n-fold composition by,
f^{(1)}(x)=f(x)
and

f^{(n)}(x)=f(f(\cdots f(x))\cdots)=f^{(n)}_{1}\cdot x+\cdots+f^{(n)}_{k}\cdot x^{k}+\cdots~\quad\forall n,~n~>1.

This paper investigates the coefficient
f_{k}^{(n)}
for almost unit (
a_{1}\neq 0
) formal power series
f
and improves the results obtained by Schröder
[
15
]
and Cayley
[
1
]
by giving a recursive and non-recursive formulation of the composition of formal power series whenever
a_{1}\neq 0
. The major result of this paper, Theorem
7.1.1
, provides a non-recursive formula for
f^{(n)}_{k}
, for
k\geq 1
and

n\in\mathbb{N}
. This major result may be viewed as an analogue of the well-known Multinomial
Theorem (Theorem
2.2.2
), which gives the coefficients
a_{k}^{[n]}
of the product
\big(f(x)\big)^{n}
.

2.
Background on Formal Power Series.

2.1.
Preliminaries

Definition 2.1.1
.

( From
[
5
]
)

Let
S
be a ring, a formal power series in one variable on
S
is defined to be a mapping
from
{\mathbb{N}}
to
S
, where
\mathbb{N}
represents the natural numbers. We denote the set of all such mappings by
{\mathbb{X}}(S)
, or
\mathbb{X}
. A formal power series
f
in
x

from
\mathbb{N}
to
S
is usually denoted by

f(x)=a_{1}x+\cdots+a_{n}x^{n}+\cdots
, where
\{a_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}\subset S
.

In this thesis, we only consider formal power series

f(x)\in{\mathbb{X}}
.

Definition 2.1.2
.

If
f(x)\ {\rm and}~g(x)
are two formal power series such that:

f(x)=a_{1}x+a_{2}x^{2}+\cdots\ {\rm and}\quad g(x)=g_{1}x+g_{2}x^{2}+\cdots

then

(1) The composition of
f(x)
and
g(x)
is defined by:

(f\circ g)(x)=f(g(x))=a_{1}\cdot g(x)+a_{2}\cdot(g(x))^{2}+\cdots+a_{n}(g(x))^{n}+\cdots

The condition that
g_{0}=0
insures that the coefficient of
x^{n}
in
(f\circ g)(x)
is finitely calculable as the coefficient of
x^{n}
in
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}(g(x))^{i}
.

(2) The multiplication of
f(x)
and
g(x)
is defined by:

(f\cdot g)(x)=g(x)\cdot f(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\,c_{n}\,x^{n},\ c_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}g_{j}a_{n-j},\ n=1,2,\cdots

.

2.2.
The Multinomial Theorem for Formal Power Series Under Multiplication

We first investigate the coefficients of
(f(x))^{i}
if
f(x)
is
a formal power series. Mathematical induction or the multinomial
coefficients can be used to initiate the investigation of the coefficients of
(f(x))^{i}
.

Definition 2.2.1
.

Let
f(x)=a_{1}x+a_{2}x^{2}+\dots+a_{k}x^{k}+\dots
be a formal
power series. For every
i\in\mathbb{N}
, we write

(f(x))^{i}=\underbrace{(f(x)\cdot~f(x)\cdot~\cdots~\cdot f(x))}_{i~times}

\hskip 14.45377pt=a_{1}^{i}x^{i}+ia_{1}^{i-1}a_{2}x^{i+1}+\dots=a_{i}^{[i]}x^{i}+a_{i+1}^{[i]}x^{i+1}+\dots+a_{k}^{[i]}x^{k}+\cdots

Thus
a_{k}^{[i]}
is the coefficient of
x^{k}~in

(f(x))^{i}
.
Note:
a_{k}^{[i]}=0
for
k<i
and

a_{k}^{[1]}=a_{k}\quad{\rm for~all}\ k\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Theorem 2.2.2
.

The Multinomial Theorem

(i)

If f(x) is defined as previously then for any
i,k\in\mathbb{N}
.

a_{k}^{[\,i\,]}=\sum_{(r_{1},\ldots,r_{k})}\frac{i\,!}{(r_{1}!)(r_{2}!)\cdots(r_{j}!)\cdots(r_{k}!)}\,a_{1}^{r_{1}}a_{2}^{r_{2}}\cdots a_{j}^{r_{j}}\cdots a_{k}^{r_{k}}

where the sum is taken for all possible nonnegative integers
r_{1},\cdots,r_{k}
such that
r_{1}+r_{2}+\cdots+r_{j}+\cdots+r_{k}=i
and
r_{1}+2r_{2}+\cdots+jr_{j}+\cdots+kr_{k}=k
.

(ii)

If
i=2
, then for
k\geq 3
we have

a_{k}^{[2]}=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}a_{i}a_{k-i}

(iii)

a_{k}^{[i]}
is a polynomial in the variable
\{a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{k-i+1}\}
for
k\geq i\geq 2.

2.3.
History of the Composition of Formal Power Series.

The composition of formal power series, or functional composition, is a significant feature of formal power series which generates a lot of mathematical
results. Some popular applications of formal series and the composition of them can be seen in composition of analytic functions, in Riordan Arrays,
in numerical analysis, in differential equations,
etc.
(see
[
6
]
,
[
17
]
, and
[
11
]
).

Schröder proposed that iteration of any function could be studied by iterating a simple conjugate of the function:

f=\phi~\circ~g~\circ~\phi^{-1}\implies f^{(n)}=\phi~\circ~g^{(n)}~\circ~\phi^{-1}

. Scheinberg, Schwaiger, and Reich used the method proposed by Schröder. Scheinberg
[
14
]
classified formal power series up to conjugation and used the notion of conjugacy to study the composition of formal fower series. Schwaiger
[
16
]
and Reich
[
13
]
used conjugacy to study roots of formal power series . Muckenhoupt
[
8
]
use direct computation to compute
the exact expression of
f_{2}^{(n)}
when
a_{1}\neq 1.

Cohen (
[
3
]
, Lemma 2.4) below is the first for having written the n-fold compositional power
f^{(n)}=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}f_{k}^{(n)}x^{k}
of
f(x)=\sum\limits_{m=1}^{\infty}a_{m}x^{m}
, when
a_{1}\neq 1
, so as to give the coefficient of
a_{k}
in the polynomial
f_{k}^{(n)}=f_{k}^{(n)}(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{k}).

The Main Recursive Lemma

3.1.
Statement and Proof.

Lemma 3.1.1
.

(
Main Recursive Lemma )

Suppose that

f(x)=a_{1}x+\cdots+a_{k}x^{k}+\cdots\in\mathbb{X}
, and

f^{(n)}(x)=f^{(n)}_{1}\cdot x+\cdots+f^{(n)}_{k}\cdot x^{k}+\cdots

then
f^{(n)}_{k}
can be written as

f^{(n)}_{k}=a_{k}~\overbrace{a^{n-1}_{1}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}a^{i(k-1)}_{1}}^{C_{k,n}}~+\overbrace{\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}a^{ki}_{1}\cdot\left[\ \sum_{j=2}^{k-1}f^{(n-i-1)}_{j}a^{[~j~]}_{k}~\right]}^{P^{(n)}_{k}}

Remark 3.1.2
.

•

f_{k}^{(n)}=a_{k}C_{k,n}+P_{k}^{(n)}

•

P_{k}^{(n)}(a_{1},0\cdots,0)=0.
see Cohen (
[
3
]
, Lemma 2.4)

\displaystyle\bullet

\displaystyle C_{k,n}=\begin{cases}n&~\rm{if}\ ~a_{1}=1\\
&\\
0&~{\rm if}\ ~a_{1}^{k-1}\neq 1~{\rm and}~a_{1}^{(k-1)n}=1\\
&\\
\frac{a_{1}^{(k-1)n}-1}{a_{1}^{k-1}-1}\neq 0,&~\rm{if}\ ~a_{1}^{(k-1)n}\neq 1\end{cases}

Proof of Lemma
3.1.1
:

By definition for
s\geq 2
we have,

f^{(s)}(x)=f^{(s-1)}_{1}\cdot f(x)+f^{(s-1)}_{2}\cdot(f(x))^{2}+\cdots+f_{k-1}^{(s-1)}\cdot(f(x))^{k-1}+f_{k}^{(s-1)}\cdot(f(x))^{k}+\dots

\displaystyle\noindent\implies

\displaystyle f_{k}^{(s)}=f^{(s-1)}_{1}\cdot a_{k}^{[1]}+f^{(s-1)}_{2}\cdot a_{k}^{[2]}+\cdots+f_{k-1}^{(s-1)}\cdot a_{k}^{[k-1]}+f_{k}^{(s-1)}\cdot a_{k}^{[k]}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle f_{k}^{(s)}-a_{1}^{k}\cdot f_{k}^{(s-1)}=f^{(s-1)}_{1}\cdot a_{k}^{[1]}+f^{(s-1)}_{2}\cdot a_{k}^{[2]}+\cdots+f_{k-1}^{(s-1)}\cdot a_{k}^{[k-1]}\hskip 14.45377pt\ ~~(L_{s-1})

Taking a linear combination of both sides of these equations for
s\in[2,n]
we get

\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}L_{i}(a_{1}^{k})^{n-i-1}\iff f_{k}^{(n)}-a_{1}^{k(n-1)}\cdot f^{(1)}_{k}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}a_{1}^{ki}\cdot\left[\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}f_{j}^{(n-1-i)}a_{k}^{[j]}\right]

so it follows that

\displaystyle f_{k}^{(n)}=

\displaystyle\ a_{1}^{k(n-1)}\cdot a_{k}+\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}a_{1}^{ki}\cdot\left[f_{1}^{(n-1-i)}\cdot a_{k}^{[1]}+\sum_{j=2}^{k-1}f_{j}^{(n-1-i)}a_{k}^{[j]}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ a_{1}^{n-1}a_{k}\left[a_{1}^{(k-1)(n-1)}+\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}a_{1}^{(k-1)i}\right]+\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}a_{1}^{ki}\cdot\left[\sum_{j=2}^{k-1}f_{j}^{(n-1-i)}a_{k}^{[j]}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ a_{1}^{n-1}a_{k}\left[~\sum_{i=n-1}^{n-1}a_{1}^{(k-1)i}+\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}a_{1}^{(k-1)i}\right]+\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}a_{1}^{ki}\cdot\left[\sum_{j=2}^{k-1}f_{j}^{(n-1-i)}a_{k}^{[j]}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ a_{1}^{n-1}a_{k}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}a_{1}^{(k-1)i}+\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}a_{1}^{ki}\cdot\left[\sum_{j=2}^{k-1}f_{j}^{(n-1-i)}a_{k}^{[j]}\right]

Thus we have proved Lemma
3.1.1
.
\square

The proof can also be done by induction.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{\alpha+1}\prod_{p=1}^{1+\alpha}p

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\frac{1\cdot 2\cdots\alpha(\alpha+1)}{\alpha+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\alpha!

Thus, for

Now, we suppose that (1) holds for some

\displaystyle\sum_{p=1}^{n+1}p(p+1)\cdots(p+\alpha-1)=

\displaystyle(n+1)(n+2)\cdots(n+\alpha)+\sum_{p=1}^{n}p(p+1)\cdots(p+\alpha-1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \prod_{p=n+1}^{n+\alpha}p+\frac{1}{\alpha+1}\prod_{p=n}^{n+\alpha}p

\displaystyle\ \left(1+\frac{n}{\alpha+1}\right)\prod_{p=n+1}^{n+\alpha}p

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \left(\frac{n+\alpha+1}{\alpha+1}\right)\prod_{p=n+1}^{n+\alpha}p

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{\alpha+1}\prod_{p=n+1}^{n+\alpha+1}p\,.~\qquad~\square

\quad f_{k}^{(n)}=a_{k}\cdot\binom{n}{1}+\underbrace{\sum_{\alpha=2}^{k-1}\left[\overbrace{\binom{n}{\alpha}\cdot\sum a_{k}^{[j_{1}]}\cdot~a_{j_{1}}^{[j_{2}]}\cdot a_{j_{2}}^{[j_{3}]}\cdot\,\cdots\,\cdot a_{j_{\alpha-2}}^{[j_{\alpha-1}]}\cdot a_{j_{\alpha-1}}^{[\,1\,]}}^{A_{\alpha,k}}\right]}_{P_{k}^{(n)}}~{\bf\cdot}

For
k=5
,

f_{5}^{(n)}=a_{5}\cdot\binom{n}{1}+\sum_{\alpha=2}^{4}\left[\underbrace{\binom{n}{\alpha}\cdot\sum a_{5}^{[j_{1}]}\cdot~a_{j_{1}}^{[j_{2}]}\cdot a_{j_{2}}^{[j_{3}]}\cdot\,\cdots\,\cdot a_{j_{\alpha-2}}^{[j_{\alpha-1}]}\cdot a_{j_{\alpha-1}}^{[\,1\,]}}_{A_{\alpha,5}}\right]~{\bf\cdot}

If
\alpha=2
, then

\displaystyle A_{2,5}=

\displaystyle\ \binom{n}{2}\cdot\sum a_{5}^{[j_{1}]}\cdot a_{j_{1}}^{[\,1\,]}\quad{\rm where}~j_{1}\,\in\{2,3,4\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \binom{n}{2}\cdot\left(a_{5}^{[\,2\,]}\cdot a_{2}^{[\,1\,]}+a_{5}^{[\,3\,]}\cdot a_{3}^{[\,1\,]}+a_{5}^{[\,4\,]}\cdot a_{4}^{[\,1\,]}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \binom{n}{2}\cdot\left(~5a_{2}^{2}+6a_{2}a_{4}+3a_{3}^{2}~\right)\,{\bf\cdot}

If
\alpha=3
, then

\{j_{1},j_{2}\}
consists of all possible 2–element subsets of
\{2,3,4\}
, where
j_{2}<j_{1}
, so we have

\{j_{1},j_{2}\}\in\{\{4,3\},\{4,2\},\{3,2\}\},
and

\displaystyle A_{3,5}=

\displaystyle\ \binom{n}{3}\cdot\sum a_{5}^{[j_{1}]}\cdot a_{j_{1}}^{[j_{2}]}\cdot a_{j_{2}}^{[\,1\,]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \binom{n}{3}\cdot\left(a_{5}^{[\,4\,]}\cdot a_{4}^{[\,3\,]}\cdot a_{3}^{[\,1\,]}+a_{5}^{[\,4\,]}\cdot a_{4}^{[\,2\,]}\cdot a_{2}^{[\,1\,]}+a_{5}^{[\,3\,]}\cdot a_{3}^{[\,2\,]}\cdot a_{2}^{[\,1\,]}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \binom{n}{3}\cdot\left(10a_{2}^{4}+26a_{2}^{2}a_{3}\right)~{\bf\cdot}

If
\alpha=4
, then

\{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}\}
consists of all possible 3–element subsets of
\{2,3,4\}
,

where
j_{3}<j_{2}<j_{1}
. That is,

\{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}\}=\{4,3,2\}
. We have,

\displaystyle\binom{n}{\alpha}\cdot\sum a_{5}^{[j_{1}]}\cdot~a_{j_{1}}^{[j_{2}]}\cdot a_{j_{2}}^{[j_{3}]}\cdot\,\cdots\,\cdot a_{j_{\alpha-2}}^{[j_{\alpha-1}]}\cdot a_{j_{\alpha-1}}^{[1]}=

\displaystyle\ \binom{n}{4}\cdot\sum a_{5}^{[j_{1}]}\cdot a_{j_{1}}^{[j_{2}]}\cdot a_{j_{2}}^{[j_{3}]}\cdot a_{j_{3}}^{[2]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \binom{n}{4}\cdot\left(a_{5}^{[4]}\cdot a_{4}^{[3]}\cdot a_{3}^{[1]}\cdot a_{2}^{[\,1\,]}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \binom{n}{4}\cdot\left(24a_{2}^{4}\right)~{\bf\cdot}

So, it follows that

\displaystyle f_{5}^{(n)}=

\displaystyle\ ~a_{5}\cdot\binom{n}{1}+\sum_{\alpha=2}^{4}\left[\binom{n}{\alpha}\cdot\sum a_{5}^{[j_{1}]}\cdot~a_{j_{1}}^{[j_{2}]}\cdot a_{j_{2}}^{[j_{3}]}\cdot\,\cdots\,\cdot a_{j_{\alpha-2}}^{[j_{\alpha-1}]}\cdot a_{j_{\alpha-1}}^{[1]}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ a_{5}\cdot\binom{n}{1}+A_{2,5}+A_{3,5}+A_{4,5}~{\bf\cdot}

Thus,

f_{5}^{(n)}=~a_{5}\cdot\binom{n}{1}+\binom{n}{2}\cdot\left(~5a_{2}^{2}+6a_{2}a_{4}+3a_{3}^{2}~\right)+\binom{n}{3}\cdot\left(~10a_{2}^{4}+26a_{2}^{2}a_{3}~\right)+24~\binom{n}{4}\cdot a_{2}^{4}~~{\bf\cdot}
