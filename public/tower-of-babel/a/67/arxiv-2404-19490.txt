Fokker-Planck equation for McKean-Vlasov SPDEs driven by time-space Brownian sheet
Nacira Agram , Bernt Øksendal, Frank Proske, Olena Tymoshenko

Abstract
In this paper, we consider a McKean-Vlasov (mean-field) stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs) driven by a Brownian sheet. We study the propagation of chaos for a space-time Ornstein-Uhlenbeck SPDE type. Subsequently, we prove the existence and uniqueness of a nonlinear McKean-Vlasov SPDE. Finally, we establish a Fokker-Planck equation for the law of the solution of the McKean-Vlasov type SPDE driven by a time-space Brownian sheet, and we provide some examples to illustrate the results obtained.

1
Introduction

In the one-parameter case, McKean-Vlasov equations were originally used in the kinetic theory of gases to model the dynamics of large particle systems in a medium. A crucial concept in kinetic theory is the propagation of chaos, which provides a description of the distribution of particles in a (monoatomic) gas medium as the number of particles tends to infinity. This concept, known as the ”Stosszahlansatz,” was assumed by Boltzmann (see
[
Ehr
]
) to assert that each pair of particles in such a system moves independently. In this context, we also mention the work of Vlasov
[
Vl
]
, which studied the propagation of chaos of charged particles in an electron gas or plasma. Regarding the investigation of the connection between Markov processes and nonlinear parabolic equations such as the Boltzmann equation in kinetic theory, we refer to McKean
[
McK
]
.

The purpose of this paper is to study the law of the solution of McKean-Vlason type SPDEs driven by the Brownian sheet. We shall study the law
\mu_{t,x}=P_{Y(t,x)}
of solutions
Y(t,x),t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}
of SPDEs of the form

Y(t,x)=Y(t_{0},x_{0})+\int_{R(t,x)}\alpha(s,a,Y(s,a),\mu_{s,a})dsda+\int_{R(t,x)}\beta(s,a,Y(s,a),\mu_{s,a})B(ds,da),

(1.1)

where

R(t,x)=R^{(t_{0},x_{0})}(t,x)=[t_{0},t]\times[x_{0},x],t\geq t_{0},x\geq x_{0},

and
\alpha
and
\beta
are Lipschitz continuous vector fields of linear growth,

B
is a Brownian sheet.

The differential form of (
1.1
) in terms of time-space white noise
\overset{\bullet}{B}
and Wick product
\diamond
is

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t\partial x}Y(t,x)=\alpha(t,x,Y(t,x),\mu_{t,x})+\beta(t,x,Y(t,x),\mu_{t,x})\diamond\overset{\bullet}{B}(t,x).

(1.2)

The identity of (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) comes from the fact that

\int_{R(t,x)}\varphi(s,a)B(ds,da)=\int_{R(t,x)}\varphi(s,a)\diamond\overset{\bullet}{B}(s,a)dsda,\text{ }\hskip 9.24994pt\text{for all}\hskip 9.24994pt\varphi,t,x.

See, for example, Holden et al.
[
HØUZ
]
for more details.

The one-parameter case of McKean-Vlasov stochastic differential equations (SDEs) in infinite dimensions have been studied recently by Hong et al
[
HLL
]
.

It is important to find the Fokker-Planck equation, denoted by
\mu_{t,x}
, for the McKean-Vlasov SPDE driven by a Brownian sheet as described above. It is worth noting that the specific type of Fokker-Planck equation for SPDEs driven by a Brownian sheet has not been previously addressed in the existing literature. Moreover, it is interesting to see the application of these concepts to understand the behavior of complex systems described by such equations.

However, in the one-parameter case, Fokker-Planck equations in infinite dimensions have been studied by Bogachev et al.
[
BPR
]
. The authors developed a general technique to prove uniqueness of solutions for Fokker-Planck equations on infinite-dimensional spaces. They have been also studied by Agram et al.
[
AØ
,
AØPu
,
AR
]
including various applications to optimal control and even deep learning.

Recently, Agram et al.
[
AØPT
]
have considered various applications including the optimal control of time-space SPDEs driven by a Brownian sheet.

The paper is organized as follows: In Section 2, we review some preliminary concepts that will be used throughout this work. Specifically, we introduce background information about the stochastic calculus of time-space white noise. In Section 3, we investigate the propagation of chaos for a space-time Ornstein-Uhlenbeck SDE as a motivating factor for considering mean-field SDEs in the context of time-space. Section 4 is devoted to proving the existence and uniqueness of solutions to a McKean-Vlasov SPDE driven by a Brownian sheet. Finally, in Section 5, we state and prove the Fokker-Planck equation and illustrate the results for some time-space SPDE.

Propagation of Chaos for space time Ornstein-Uhlenbeck SDE

In order to motivate mean-field SDEs in the two-parameter case from the
viewpoint of propagation of chaos, consider now the following linear
N-
particle system,
i=1,...,N
,

Y^{i,N}(t,x)=Y_{i}(t_{0},x_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}Y^{j,N}(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})-Y^{i,N}(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})\right)d\zeta_{1}d\zeta_{2}+B^{i}(t,x),

where
a_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
,
j=1,...,N
and
B=(B^{1},...,B^{N})
a
N-
dimensional Brownian sheet.

So the latter system of equations, which could be e.g. used to describe the
dynamics of interacting waves in an ocean, can be written as

Y(t,x)=Y(t_{0},x_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}\left(\frac{1}{N}A-I_{N}\right)Y(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})d\zeta_{1}d\zeta_{2}+B(t,x),

where
0\leq t,x\leq T
,
Y(t_{0},x_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
Y(t,x):=(Y^{1,N}(t,x),...,Y^{N,N}(t,x))
,
I_{N}
is the unit matrix
and

A:=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&\cdots&a_{N}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
a_{1}&\cdots&a_{N}\end{pmatrix}.

By applying the Malliavin derivative
D_{u,v}
to both sides of the latter
equation, for
0\leq u\leq t
,
0\leq v\leq x
, we obtain that

D_{u,v}Y(t,x)=\int_{u}^{t}\int_{v}^{x}\left(\frac{1}{N}A-I_{N}\right)D_{u,v}Y(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})d\zeta_{1}d\zeta_{2}+\chi_{R(t,x)}(u,v)I_{N}.

Using Picard iteration we see that

D_{u,v}Y(t,x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\int_{R(u,v,t,x)}\int_{R(u,v,t_{1},x_{1})}...\int_{R(u,v,t_{n-1},x_{n-1})}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{N}A-I_{N}\right)dt_{n}dx_{n}...dt_{1}dx_{1}

=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{1}{(n!)^{2}}\left((t-u)(x-v)t\left(\frac{1}{N}A-I_{N}\right)\right)^{n}=f\left((t-u)(x-v)\left(\frac{1}{N}A-I_{N}\right)\right)\text{,}

where
f
is a function, which is related to the Bessel function of order
zero and given by

\displaystyle f(y)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{1}{(n!)^{2}}y^{n}\text{.}

Then it follows from the Clark-Ocone theorem for a Brownian sheet that

Y(t,x)=E\left[Y(t,x)\right]+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}f\left((t-u)(x-v)\left(\frac{1}{N}A-I_{N}\right)\right)B(du,dv)\text{.}

Similarly, we find that

E\left[Y(t,x)\right]=f\left(tx\left(\frac{1}{N}A-I_{N}\right)\right)Y(t_{0},x_{0})\text{.}

Hence,

Y(t,x)=f\left(tx\left(\frac{1}{N}A-I_{N}\right)\right)Y(t_{0},x_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}f\left((t-u)(x-v)\left(\frac{1}{N}A-I_{N}\right)\right)B(du,dv)\text{.}

Define

\displaystyle\left\|A\right\|=\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}\text{.}
Require that
Y_{i}(t_{0},x_{0})=y
for all
i=1,...,N
, the sequence
a_{j},j\geq 1
is
bounded and that

\frac{1}{N}\left\|A\right\|=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}\underset{N\longrightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}a>0\text{.}

On the other hand, we observe for
n\geq 0
that

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{N}A-I_{N}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom{n}{j}\left(\frac{1}{N}A\right)^{j}(-I_{N})^{n-j}=(-1)^{n}I_{N}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\binom{n}{j}\left(\frac{1}{N}A\right)^{j}(-I_{N})^{n-j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{n}I_{N}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\binom{n}{j}\left\|\frac{1}{N}A\right\|^{j-1}\frac{1}{N}A(-I_{N})^{n-j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{n}I_{N}+\left(-(-1)^{n}+(-1)^{n}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\binom{n}{j}\left\|\frac{1}{N}A\right\|^{j}(-1)^{n-j}\right)\frac{1}{\left\|\frac{1}{N}A\right\|}\frac{1}{N}A

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{n}(I_{N}-\frac{1}{\left\|A\right\|}A)+(\frac{1}{N}\left\|A\right\|-1)^{n}\frac{1}{\left\|A\right\|}A\text{.}

So

\displaystyle f\left((t-u)(x-v)(\frac{1}{N}A-I_{N})\right)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{1}{(n!)^{2}}((t-u)(x-v)(\frac{1}{N}A-I_{N}))^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{1}{(n!)^{2}}((t-u)(x-v))^{n}\left\{(-1)^{n}(I_{N}-\frac{1}{\left\|A\right\|}A)+(\frac{1}{N}\left\|A\right\|-1)^{n}\frac{1}{\left\|A\right\|}A\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(-(t-u)(x-v))(I_{N}-\frac{1}{\left\|A\right\|}A)+f((t-u)(x-v)(\frac{1}{N}\left\|A\right\|-1))\frac{1}{\left\|A\right\|}A\text{.}

Hence

\displaystyle Y(t,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(-tx)(I_{N}-\frac{1}{\left\|A\right\|}A)y+f(tx)\left(\frac{1}{N}\left\|A\right\|-1\right)\frac{1}{\left\|A\right\|}Ay

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}\left\{f(-(t-u)(x-v))\left(I_{N}-\frac{1}{\left\|A\right\|}A\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.+f((t-u)(x-v)\left(\frac{1}{N}\left\|A\right\|-1\right)\frac{1}{\left\|A\right\|}A\right\}B(du,dv)\text{.}

The latter entails that

\displaystyle X^{i,N}(t,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(-tx)\left(1-\frac{\left\|A\right\|}{\left\|A\right\|}\right)y+f(tx)\left(\frac{1}{N}\left\|A\right\|-1\right)y

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}f(-(t-u)(x-v))dB^{i}(du,dv)+I_{i,N}\text{,}

where

\displaystyle I_{i,N}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}f(-(t-u)(x-v))\frac{a_{j}}{\left\|A\right\|}dB^{j}(du,dv)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}f(-(t-u)(x-v))\left(\frac{1}{N}\left\|A\right\|-1\right)\frac{a_{j}}{\left\|A\right\|}dB^{j}(du,dv),

for all
1\leq i\leq N
. Using the Itô isometry, our assumptions on
a_{j},j\geq 1
and dominated convergence, we find that

\displaystyle E\left[\left|I_{i,N}\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\frac{N}{\left\|A\right\|^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}(f(-(t-u)(x-v)))^{2}(\frac{1}{N}\left\|A\right\|-2)^{2}dudv

\displaystyle\underset{N\longrightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}0\text{,}

where
C
is a constant.
Thus, for
N\longrightarrow\infty

Y^{i,N}(t,x)\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}Y(t,x):=f(tx)(a-1)y+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}f(-(t-u)(x-v))dB(du,dv),

for all
i\geq 1
, where
Y(t,x)
,
0\leq t
,
x\leq T
solves (by the same
reasoning as above) the mean-field hyperbolic SPDE

Y(t,x)=y+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}aE\left[Y(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})\right]-Y(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})d\zeta_{1}d\zeta_{2}+B(t,x).

4
McKean-Vlasov SPDE

In this section, we prove the existence and uniqueness of the solution to a McKean-Vlasov SPDE driven by a Brownian sheet. To this end, we denote by
z=(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
,
{R}_{z}
will denote the rectangle
[0,t]\times[0,x]
. The object of our study is an SPDE of the type

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t\partial x}Y(t,x)=\alpha(t,x,Y(t,x),\mu_{t,x})+\beta(t,x,Y(t,x),\mu_{t,x})\diamond\overset{\bullet}{B}(t,x),\hskip 9.24994ptY(0,0)=y.

(4.1)

Here
\mu_{t,x}
represents the probability distribution of
Y(t,x)
. We shall define the space where these probability distributions reside.

Definition 4.1
(Special weighted Sobolev space)

Let
\mathbb{M}
be the pre-Hilbert space of random measures
\mu
on

\mathbb{R}
equipped with the norm

\displaystyle\left\|\mu\right\|_{\mathbb{M}}^{2}:=\mathbb{E[}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\hat{\mu}(y)|^{2}e^{-y^{2}}dy]\text{,}

(4.2)

where
\hat{\mu}
is the Fourier transform of the measure
\mu
, i.e.

\begin{array}[c]{lll}\hat{\mu}(y)&:=&{\int_{\mathbb{R}}}e^{-ixy}\mu(dx);\hskip 9.24994pty\in\mathbb{R}.\end{array}

If
\mu,\eta\in\mathbb{M}
, we define the inner product
\left\langle\mu,\eta\right\rangle_{\mathbb{M}}
by

\left\langle\mu,\eta\right\rangle_{\mathbb{M}}=\mathbb{E}{[}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\operatorname{Re}(\overline{\hat{\mu}}(y)\hat{\eta}(y))e^{-y^{2}}dy],

where,
\operatorname{Re}(z)
denotes the real part and
\bar{z}
denotes the complex conjugate of the complex number
z
.

The space
\mathbb{M}
equipped with the inner
product
\left\langle\mu,\eta\right\rangle_{\mathbb{M}}

is a pre-Hilbert space. For not having ambiguity, we will also use the notation
\mathbb{M}
for the completion of this pre-Hilbert space.
Moreover, we have the following estimate:
Let
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
be two
d
-dimensional random variables in

L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
with associated probability distributions
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
, respectively. Thus

\begin{array}[c]{lll}\left\|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\right\|_{\mathbb{M}}^{2}&\leq&\pi\ \mathbb{E}[(Y_{1}-Y_{2})^{2}]\text{.}\end{array}

To study the well-posedness of the McKean-Vlasov SPDE (
4.1
) driven by Brownian sheet, we impose the following set of assumptions on the coefficients
\alpha
and
\beta
for which we aim, they will insure the well-posedness:

(a)

\alpha(z,y,\mu):[0,T]^{2}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\beta(z,y,\mu):[0,T]^{2}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are locally bounded and Borel-measurable functions.

(b)

There exists a constant
C
, such that for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+},y,y^{\prime},\mu,\mu^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\alpha(z,y,\mu)-\alpha(z,y^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})\right|

\displaystyle+\left|\beta(z,y,\mu)-\beta(z,y^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})\right|\leq C(\left|y-y^{\prime}\right|+\left|\left|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\right|\right|_{\mathbb{M}}),

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int_{{R}_{z}}\left|\alpha(\zeta,0,\delta_{(0,0)})\right|^{2}+\left|\beta(\zeta,0,\delta_{(0,0}))\right|^{2}d\zeta\leq\infty,

where
\delta
is the Dirac measure.

Let
f_{0}
be the Bessel function of order zero and
r_{0}\approx 1.4458
be the first nonnegative zero of
J_{0}
:

r_{0}=inf\left\{t>0:f_{0}(2\sqrt{t})=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{{j!}^{2}}t^{j}=0\right\}.

We shall recall the two-parameter version of Gronwall’s
Lemma in
[
ZN
]
.

Lemma 4.2
(Two-parameter Gronwall’s Lemma)

Let
f
be a non-negative and bounded function. There exists
C_{0}>0
satisfies
C_{0}|z|\leq r_{0}
, such that

f(z)\leq C_{0}\int_{{R}_{z}}f(\zeta)d\zeta.

Moreover,
f
vanishes on
R_{z}
.

Theorem 4.3
(Existence and uniqueness)

Under the above assumptions (a)-(b), the conditional McKean-Vlasov SPDE (
4.1
) has a unique strong solution.

Proof.  The proof is based on the Picard iteration argument as in the proof of the Propagation of Chaos for Space time Ornstein-Uhlenbeck SPDE in Section 3.

Step 1. Uniqueness

Suppose that we have two solutions
Y,Y^{\prime}
and set
\tilde{Y}=Y-Y^{\prime}
, such that
\tilde{Y}
satisfies

\displaystyle\tilde{Y}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\int_{{R}_{z}}\{\alpha(\zeta,Y(\zeta),\mu_{\zeta})-\alpha(\zeta,Y^{\prime}(\zeta),\mu^{\prime}_{\zeta})\}d\zeta

\displaystyle+\int_{{R}_{z}}\{\beta(\zeta,Y(\zeta),\mu_{\zeta})-\beta(\zeta,Y^{\prime}(\zeta),\mu^{\prime}_{\zeta})\}dB(\zeta).

Taking the mean square yields

\displaystyle E|\tilde{Y}(t,x)|^{2}

\displaystyle=E|\int_{{R}_{z}}\{\alpha(\zeta,Y(\zeta),\mu_{\zeta})-\alpha(\zeta,Y^{\prime}(\zeta),\mu^{\prime}_{\zeta})\}d\zeta

\displaystyle+\int_{{R}_{z}}\{\beta(\zeta,Y(\zeta),\mu_{\zeta})-\beta(\zeta,Y^{\prime}(\zeta),\mu^{\prime}_{\zeta})\}dB(\zeta)|^{2}.

Triangle inequality together with the linearity of the expectation, leads

\displaystyle E|\tilde{Y}(t,x)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq E|\int_{{R}_{z}}\{\alpha(\zeta,Y(\zeta),\mu_{\zeta})-\alpha(\zeta,Y^{\prime}(\zeta),\mu^{\prime}_{\zeta})\}d\zeta|^{2}

\displaystyle+E|\int_{{R}_{z}}\{\beta(\zeta,Y(\zeta),\mu_{\zeta})-\beta(\zeta,Y^{\prime}(\zeta),\mu^{\prime}_{\zeta})\}dB(\zeta)|^{2}.

We use the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for the
d\zeta
-integral and the isometry for the
dB
-integral, we get

\displaystyle E|\tilde{Y}(t,x)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq|z|^{2}E\int_{{R}_{z}}|\alpha(\zeta,Y(\zeta),\mu_{\zeta})-\alpha(\zeta,Y^{\prime}(\zeta),\mu^{\prime}_{\zeta})|^{2}d\zeta

\displaystyle+E\int_{{R}_{z}}|\beta(\zeta,Y(\zeta),\mu_{\zeta})-\beta(\zeta,Y^{\prime}(\zeta),\mu^{\prime}_{\zeta})|^{2}d\zeta,

where we have used the notation
|z|=tx.

Jensen inequality combined with the Lipschitz condition, give

\displaystyle E|\tilde{Y}(t,x)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq|z|^{2}(C+C\pi)^{2}\int_{{R}_{z}}E|Y(\zeta)-Y^{\prime}(\zeta)|^{2}d\zeta+(C+C\pi)^{2}\int_{{R}_{z}}E|Y(\zeta)-Y^{\prime}(\zeta)|^{2}d\zeta

\displaystyle=(C+C\pi)^{2}(|z|^{2}+1)\int_{{R}_{z}}E|\tilde{Y}(\zeta)|^{2}d\zeta.

By Gronwall’s Lemma , we get

E|\tilde{Y}(t,x)|^{2}=0.

Step 2. Existence

Define
Y^{0}(z)=y
and
Y^{n}(z)
inductively with corresponding probability distributions
\delta_{y}
and
\mu^{n}_{\zeta}=P_{Y^{n}(\zeta)}
respectively, as follows

\displaystyle Y^{n+1}(z)

\displaystyle=y+\int_{{R}_{z}}\alpha(\zeta,Y^{n}(\zeta),\mu^{n}_{\zeta})d\zeta+\int_{{R}_{z}}\beta(\zeta,Y^{n}(\zeta),\mu^{n}_{\zeta})dB(\zeta).

Similar computations as in the uniqueness, for some constant
K>0
depending on the lipschitz constant, lead to

\displaystyle E|Y^{n+1}(z)-Y^{n}(z)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq K^{2}|z|^{2}\int_{{R}_{z}}E|Y^{n}(\zeta)-Y^{n-1}(\zeta)|^{2}d\zeta.

Repeating this procedure
n
-times, we get

\displaystyle E|Y^{n+1}(z)-Y^{n}(z)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq K^{2n}|z|^{2}\int_{{R}_{z}}\int_{{R}_{z_{n}}}\cdot\cdot\cdot(\int_{{R}_{z_{n}}}E|Y^{1}(\zeta_{n+1})-Y^{0}(\zeta_{n+1})|^{2}d\zeta_{n+1})d\zeta_{{n}}\cdot\cdot\cdot d\zeta_{z_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq K^{2n}|z|^{2n}sup_{u\in{R}_{z}}E|Y^{1}(u)|^{2}x_{n}.

Taking the sum, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}E|Y^{n+1}(z)-Y^{n}(z)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq sup_{u\in{R}_{z}}E|Y^{1}(u)|^{2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(K|z|)^{2n}x_{n}<\infty,

with
x_{n}=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{(j!)^{2}}x_{n-j}
and
K|z|<\sqrt{r_{0}}
. Thus
(Y^{n})_{n}
converges.

\square

5
The Fokker-Planck equation

In this section we state and prove our main result.

In the following we let
D=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}
denote the derivative in the sense of distributions meaning in the space
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
of tempered distributions on
\mathbb{R}
.

We first prove the following auxiliary result:

Lemma 5.1

Suppose
F
has the form

\displaystyle F(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime},y,\mu)=D^{p}[f(\zeta,y,\mu)g(\zeta^{\prime},y,\mu)\mu_{\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime}}];\hskip 9.24994ptp=1,2,3,4.

(5.1)

Then

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t\partial x}\int_{R_{z}}\int_{R_{z}}I(\zeta\bar{\wedge}\zeta^{\prime})F(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime},y,\mu)d\zeta d\zeta^{\prime}=D^{p}\Big[\Big(\int_{0}^{t}f((\zeta_{1},x),y,\mu)d\zeta_{1}\int_{0}^{x}g((t,\zeta^{\prime}_{2}),y,\mu))d\zeta^{\prime}_{2}\Big)\mu_{t,x}\Big].

In particular, if
f(\zeta,y,\mu)=f(\zeta)
and
g(\zeta,y,\mu)=g(\zeta)
do not depend on
y
and
\mu
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t\partial x}\int_{R_{z}}\int_{R_{z}}I(\zeta\bar{\wedge}\zeta^{\prime})F(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime},y,\mu)d\zeta d\zeta^{\prime}=\Big(\int_{0}^{t}f(\zeta_{1},x)d\zeta_{1}\int_{0}^{x}g(t,\zeta^{\prime}_{2})d\zeta^{\prime}_{2}\Big)D^{p}\mu_{t,x},

and if
f,g
are a constants, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t\partial x}\int_{R_{z}}\int_{R_{z}}I(\zeta\bar{\wedge}\zeta^{\prime})F(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime},y,\mu)d\zeta d\zeta^{\prime}=txfgD^{p}\mu_{t,x}.

Proof.  By the definition of
I(\zeta\bar{\wedge}\zeta^{\prime})
we have

\displaystyle H:=\int_{R_{z}}\int_{R_{z}}I(\zeta\bar{\wedge}\zeta^{\prime})F(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime})d\zeta d\zeta^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{R_{z}}\int_{0}^{x}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{1}_{\zeta_{1}\leq\zeta^{\prime}_{1}\&\zeta_{2}\geq\zeta^{\prime}_{2}}F(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime})d\zeta d\zeta^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\int_{0}^{\zeta_{2}}\int_{\zeta_{1}}^{t}F(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime})d\zeta^{\prime}_{1}d\zeta^{\prime}_{2}\right)d\zeta_{1}d\zeta_{2}.

Taking derivatives, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}H=\int_{0}^{x}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\int_{0}^{\zeta_{2}}F(\zeta,(t,\zeta^{\prime}_{2}))d\zeta^{\prime}_{2}\right)d\zeta_{1}d\zeta_{2},

and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x\partial t}H

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}F((\zeta_{1},x),(t,\zeta^{\prime}_{2}))d\zeta^{\prime}_{2})d\zeta_{1}=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}D^{p}[f(\zeta_{1},x)g(t,\zeta^{\prime}_{2})\mu_{(\zeta^{\prime},x)\vee(t,\zeta^{\prime}_{2})}]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}D^{p}[f(\zeta_{1},x)g(t,\zeta^{\prime}_{2})\mu_{t,x}]d\zeta^{\prime}_{2}d\zeta_{1}=D^{p}\Big[\Big(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}f(\zeta_{1},x)g(t,\zeta^{\prime}_{2})d\zeta^{\prime}_{2}d\zeta_{1}\Big)\mu_{t,x}\Big]

\displaystyle=D^{p}\Big[\Big(\int_{0}^{t}f(\zeta_{1},x)d\zeta_{1}\int_{0}^{x}g(t,\zeta^{\prime}_{2})d\zeta^{\prime}_{2}\Big)\mu_{t,x}\Big]

\square

Theorem 5.2
(The Fokker-Planck equation)

Let
Y(t,x)
be the solution of a mean-field SPDE of the form

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t\partial x}Y(t,x)

\displaystyle=\alpha(t,x,Y(t,x),\mu_{t,x})+\beta(t,x,Y(t,x),\mu_{t,x})\diamond\overset{\bullet}{B}(t,x),\,\,t,x>0;\,\,Y(0,0)=y.

•

(Integral version) Then the law
\mu_{t,x}=\mu_{t,x}(dy)=\mathcal{L}(Y(t,x))(dy)
of
Y(t,x)
satisfies the following integral (Fokker-Planck) equation:

\displaystyle\mu_{z}=\delta_{y}+\int_{R_{z}}\Big\{A_{1}[\mu_{\zeta}]+\int_{R_{z}}L_{\zeta^{\prime}}[\mu_{\zeta}]d\zeta^{\prime}\Big\}d\zeta,

(5.2)

where the operators
A_{1}
and
L_{\zeta^{\prime}}
are given by

\displaystyle A_{1}[\mu_{\zeta}]=-D[\alpha(\zeta,y,\mu)\mu_{\zeta}]+\tfrac{1}{2}D^{2}[\beta^{2}(\zeta,y,\mu_{\zeta})\mu_{\zeta}]

(5.3)

and, with
z=(t,x)
,

\displaystyle L_{\zeta^{\prime}}[\mu_{\zeta}]

\displaystyle=I(\zeta\bar{\wedge}\zeta^{\prime})\Big(D^{2}\Big[\alpha(\zeta,y,\mu)\alpha(\zeta^{\prime},y,\mu)\mu_{\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime}}\Big]

\displaystyle-\tfrac{1}{2}D^{3}\Big[\Big(\alpha(\zeta,y,\mu)\beta^{2}(\zeta^{\prime},y,\mu)+\alpha(\zeta^{\prime},y,\mu)\beta^{2}(\zeta,y,\mu)\Big)\mu_{\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime}}\Big]

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{4}D^{4}\Big[\beta^{2}(\zeta,y,\mu)\beta^{2}(\zeta^{\prime},y,\mu)\mu_{\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime}}\Big]\Big).

(5.4)

•

(Differential form) Equivalently, in differential form the Fokker-Planck equation states that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t\partial x}\mu_{t,x}

\displaystyle=-D[\alpha(z,y,\mu)\mu_{z}]+\tfrac{1}{2}D^{2}[\beta^{2}(z,y,\mu)\mu_{z}]

\displaystyle+D^{2}\Big[\Big(\int_{0}^{t}\alpha((\zeta_{1},x),y,\mu)d\zeta_{1}\int_{0}^{x}\alpha((t,\zeta^{\prime}_{2}),y,\mu)d\zeta^{\prime}_{2}\Big)\mu_{t,x}\Big]

\displaystyle-\tfrac{1}{2}D^{3}\Big[\Big(\int_{0}^{t}\alpha((\zeta_{1},x),y,\mu)d\zeta_{1}\int_{0}^{x}\beta^{2}((t,\zeta^{\prime}_{2}),y,\mu)d\zeta^{\prime}_{2}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{x}\alpha((t,\zeta^{\prime}_{2}),y,\mu)d\zeta^{\prime}_{2}\int_{0}^{t}\beta^{2}((\zeta_{1},x),y,\mu)d\zeta_{1}\Big)\mu_{t,x}\Big]

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{4}D^{4}\Big[\Big(\int_{0}^{t}\beta^{2}((\zeta_{1},x),y,\mu)d\zeta_{1}\int_{0}^{x}\beta^{2}((t,\zeta^{\prime}_{2}),y,\mu)d\zeta^{\prime}_{2}\Big)\mu_{t,x}\Big].

(5.5)

In particular, if
\alpha
and
\beta
are constants, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t\partial x}\mu_{t,x}=-\alpha D\mu_{t,x}+\tfrac{1}{2}\beta^{2}D^{2}\mu_{t,x}+tx\Big(\alpha^{2}D^{2}\mu_{t,x}+\alpha\beta^{2}D^{3}\mu_{t,x}+\tfrac{1}{4}\beta^{4}D^{4}\mu_{t,x}\Big).

(5.6)

Proof.  The proof follows the idea of the proof of a similar result in
[
AØ
]
.

By the Dynkin formula we have, for a given locally bounded smooth function
\varphi
,

\displaystyle E[\varphi(Y(z))]-\varphi(y)=E\left[\int_{R_{z}}A\varphi(Y(\zeta))d\zeta\right]

where

\displaystyle A\varphi(Y(\zeta))

\displaystyle=\varphi^{\prime}(Y(\zeta))\alpha(\zeta)+\tfrac{1}{2}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(Y(\zeta))\beta^{2}(\zeta)+\int_{R_{z}}I(\zeta\bar{\wedge}\zeta^{\prime})\Big\{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(Y(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime}))\alpha(\zeta)\alpha(\zeta^{\prime})

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{2}\varphi^{(3)}(Y(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime}))\big(\alpha(\zeta)\beta^{2}(\zeta^{\prime})+\alpha(\zeta^{\prime})\beta^{2}(\zeta)\big)+\tfrac{1}{4}\varphi^{(4)}(Y(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime}))\beta^{2}(\zeta)\beta^{2}(\zeta^{\prime})\Big\}d\zeta^{\prime}.

Applying this to the function

\varphi(y)=\varphi_{w}(y)=e^{-iwy};\hskip 9.24994pty,w\in\mathbb{R},

where
i=\sqrt{-1}
, and with
\varphi^{\prime}(y)=-iwe^{-iwy}
,
\varphi^{\prime\prime}(y)=-w^{2}e^{-iwy}
,
\varphi^{(3)}(y)=iw^{3}e^{-iwy}
,
\varphi^{(3)}(y)=w^{4}e^{-iwy}

we get

\displaystyle E\left[e^{-iwY(z)}\right]-e^{-iwy}=E\left[\int_{R_{z}}\Big\{-iwe^{-iwY(\zeta)}\alpha(\zeta)+\tfrac{1}{2}(-w^{2})e^{-iwY(\zeta)}\beta^{2}(\zeta)\right.

\displaystyle+\int_{R_{z}}I(\zeta\bar{\wedge}\zeta^{\prime})\left(-w^{2}e^{-iwY(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime})}\alpha(\zeta)\alpha(\zeta^{\prime})\right.+\tfrac{1}{2}iw^{3}e^{-iwY(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime})}\big(\alpha(\zeta)\beta^{2}(\zeta^{\prime})+\alpha(\zeta^{\prime})\beta^{2}(\zeta)\big)

\displaystyle\left.\left.+\tfrac{1}{4}w^{4}e^{-iwY(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime})}\beta^{2}(\zeta)\beta^{2}(\zeta^{\prime})\right)d\zeta^{\prime}\Big\}d\zeta\right].

(5.7)

Note that

Y(\zeta)=Y(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime})\mathbf{J}(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime}),\hskip 9.24994ptY(\zeta^{\prime})=Y(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime})\mathbf{K}(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime})

where

\mathbf{J}(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime})=\mathbf{1}_{\zeta_{1}\geq\zeta_{1}^{\prime}\&\zeta_{2}\geq\zeta_{2}^{\prime}}(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime}),\hskip 9.24994pt\mathbf{K}(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime})=\mathbf{1}_{\zeta_{1}^{\prime}\geq\zeta_{1}\&\zeta^{\prime}_{2}\geq\zeta_{2}}(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime}).

Therefore (
5.7
) can be written

\displaystyle E\left[e^{-iwY(z)}\right]-e^{-iwy}=E\Big[\int_{R_{z}}\int_{R_{z}}\Big\{-iwe^{-iwY(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime})}(\alpha(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime})\mathbf{J}

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{2}(-w^{2})e^{-iwY(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime})}(\beta^{2}(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime})\mathbf{J}+\int_{R_{z}}I(\zeta\bar{\wedge}\zeta^{\prime})\Big(-w^{2}e^{-iwY(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime})}\alpha(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime})\mathbf{J}\alpha(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime})\mathbf{K}

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{2}iw^{3}e^{-iwY(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime})}\big(\alpha(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime})\mathbf{J}\beta^{2}(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime})\mathbf{K}+\alpha(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime})\mathbf{J}(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime})\beta^{2}(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime})\mathbf{K}(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime})\big)

\displaystyle\left.\left.+\tfrac{1}{4}w^{4}e^{-iwY(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime})}\beta^{2}(\zeta)\beta^{2}(\zeta^{\prime})\right)d\zeta^{\prime}\Big\}d\zeta\right].

(5.8)

Note that if
F
denotes the Fourier transform operator, and
\mu_{\zeta}(dy)
denotes the law of
Y(\zeta)
, we have

\displaystyle E\left[e^{-iwY(\zeta)}\right]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-iwy}\mu_{\zeta}(dy)=F[\mu_{\zeta}](w),

and similarly

\displaystyle E[e^{-iwY(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime})}]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-iwy}\mu_{\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime}}(dy)=F[\mu_{\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime}}](w),

Using this, and noting that
e^{-iwy}=F[\delta_{y}(\cdot)](w)
, we see that (
5.7
) can be written

\displaystyle F[\mu_{z}](w)-F[\delta_{y}(\cdot)](w)=

\displaystyle\int_{R_{z}}\Big\{-iwF[\alpha\mu_{\zeta}](w)-\tfrac{1}{2}w^{2}F[\beta^{2}\mu_{\zeta}](w)

\displaystyle+\int_{R_{z}}I(\zeta\bar{\wedge}\zeta^{\prime})\Big(-w^{2}F[\alpha(\zeta)\alpha(\zeta^{\prime})\mu_{\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime}}](w)

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{2}iw^{3}F[\big(\alpha(\zeta)\beta^{2}(\zeta^{\prime})+\alpha(\zeta^{\prime})\beta^{2}(\zeta)\big)\mu_{\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime}}](w)

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{4}w^{4}F[\beta^{2}(\zeta)\beta^{2}(\zeta^{\prime})\mu_{\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime}}](w)\Big)d\zeta^{\prime}\Big\}d\zeta.

(5.9)

Now we use that

iwF[\alpha\mu_{\zeta}](w)=F[D(\alpha\mu_{\zeta})](w)\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994pt-w^{2}F[\beta^{2}\mu_{\zeta}](w)=F[D^{2}({\beta^{2}\mu_{\zeta})](w)}],

where
D=\frac{\partial}{\partial y},D^{2}=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial y^{2}}
denote derivatives with respect to
y
, in the sense of distribution. Using (3.12) and (3.13), we can define terms with third and fourth-order derivatives.

-iw^{3}F[\alpha\beta^{2}\mu_{\zeta}](w)=F[D^{3}({\alpha\beta^{2}\mu_{\zeta})](w)}]\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994ptw^{4}F[\beta^{2}\beta^{2}\mu_{\zeta}](w)=F[D^{4}({\beta^{2}\beta^{2}\mu_{\zeta})](w)}].

Then (
5.9
) can be written

\displaystyle F[\mu_{\zeta}-\delta_{y}](w)

\displaystyle=F\Big[\int_{R_{z}}\Big\{-D[\alpha\mu_{\zeta}]+\tfrac{1}{2}D^{2}[\beta^{2}\mu_{\zeta}]

\displaystyle+\int_{R_{z}}I(\zeta\bar{\wedge}\zeta^{\prime})\Big(D^{2}[\alpha{(\zeta)\alpha(\zeta^{\prime})}\mu_{\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime}}]-\tfrac{1}{2}D^{3}[\big(\alpha(\zeta)\beta^{2}(\zeta^{\prime})+\alpha(\zeta^{\prime})\beta^{2}(\zeta)\big)\mu_{\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime}}]

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{4}D^{4}[\beta^{2}(\zeta)\beta^{2}(\zeta^{\prime})\mu_{\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime}}\Big)d\zeta^{\prime}\Big\}d\zeta\Big](w).

By uniqueness of the Fourier transform we conclude that
\mu
satisfies the equation

\displaystyle\mu_{z}=\delta_{y}+\int_{R_{z}}\Big\{A_{1}[\mu_{\zeta}]+\int_{R_{z}}L_{\zeta^{\prime}}[\mu_{\zeta}]d\zeta^{\prime}\Big\}d\zeta,

which is (
5.2
).

The rest of the theorem, i.e. (
5.5
) and (
5.6
) follow by Lemma
5.1
.

\square

Example 5.3

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{3}}{\partial y^{3}}m_{(t,x)}(y)

\displaystyle=m_{(t,x)}(y)\left[-\frac{(y-y_{0})^{3}}{t^{3}x^{3}}+\frac{3(y-y_{0})}{t^{2}x^{2}}\right],

and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{4}}{\partial y^{4}}m_{(t,x)}(y)

\displaystyle=-m_{(t,x)}(y)\frac{y-y_{0}}{tx}\left[-\frac{(y-y_{0})^{3}}{t^{3}x^{3}}+\frac{3(y-y_{0})}{t^{2}x^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle+m_{(t,x)}(y)\left[\frac{-3(y-y_{0})^{2}}{t^{3}x^{3}}+\frac{3}{t^{2}x^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t^{2}x^{2}}m_{(t,x)}(y)\left[\frac{(y-y_{0})^{4}}{t^{2}x^{2}}-\frac{6(y-y_{0})^{2}}{tx}+3\right].

(5.14)

Next, we compute

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}m_{(t,x)}(y)

\displaystyle=m_{(t,x)}(y)\left[-\frac{1}{2t}+\frac{(y-y_{0})^{2}}{2xt^{2}}\right],

and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t\partial x}m_{(t,x)}(y)

\displaystyle=m_{(t,x)}(y)\left[-\frac{1}{2x}+\frac{(y-y_{0})^{2}}{2x^{2}t}\right]\left[-\frac{1}{2t}+\frac{(y-y_{0})^{2}}{2xt^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle+m_{(t,x)}(y)\left(-\frac{(y-y_{0})^{2}}{2x^{2}t^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=m_{(t,x)}(y)\left[\frac{1}{4tx}-\frac{(y-y_{0})^{2}}{x^{2}t^{2}}+\frac{(y-y_{0})^{4}}{4x^{3}t^{3}}\right].

(5.15)
