Quantum electrodynamics in the null-plane causal perturbation theory II
O.A. Acevedo, B.M. Pimentel

Abstract
We develop a complete formulation of quantum gauge invariance in light-front dynamics for interacting theories with massless vector gauge fields in the framework of null-plane causal perturbation theory. We apply the general results to quantum electrodynamics, showing that the so-called “gauge terms” present in the photon commutation distribution when quantized under the null-plane gauge condition have no contribution in the calculation of the physical S S -operator matrix elements at any order. We use this result to prove the normalizability of the theory, and to calculate the electron’s self-energy at second order.

Introduction

Light-front dynamics is one of the three forms of relativistic dynamics discovered by Dirac in 1949
Dirac 1949
, complemented in 1978 with two additional forms by Leutwyler and Stern
Leutwyler and Stern 1978

x^{+}\propto x^{0}+x^{3}

Perry et al. 1990

Bakker et al. 2014

In spite of this, the equivalence between the instant and the light-front formulations of quantum field theory is not firmly established yet. This problem for perturbative quantum electrodynamics (QED) was first addressed by Ten Eyck and Rohrlich

;

;
Pimentel and Suzuki 1991

Aiming to shed light to the subtleties of perturbative light-front field theory, the authors developed the framework of null-plane causal perturbation theory (CPT)

This program was successfully applied to obtain the radiative corrections for Yukawa’s model

II
Quantum gauge invariance

As we have seen in Ref.
Acevedo and Pimentel 2022b
, the gauge terms of the radiation field commutation distribution do not contribute to Møller’s scattering, which is an expression of gauge invariance. It is our aim in this section to prove that they in fact do not contribute to any process, in other words, that the scattering operator can be constructed with the covariant terms only. To do that, we will get back to the quantization procedure of the massless vector field –see Ap. A in Ref.
Acevedo and Pimentel 2022a
–. Such a procedure had the meaning of constructing Fock’s space and the operators as well as operator-valued distributions acting on it. This Fock’s space consists on physical states, only, whose wavefunctions are the positive-frequency part of the classical field solutions. As a consequence, only physical polarization states are quantized, leading to the instantaneous term in the commutation distribution of the field operator. If we want to use a covariant commutation distribution, it will be necessary to quantize the non-physical polarization states as well, and, accordingly, to extend Fock’s space in order to contain the non-physical states created by their corresponding field operators. This extension must be done in such a way that the physical content of the theory is not altered, as will be shown in the following paragraphs.

II.1
Fock’s space extension

Vector massless fields have only two degrees of freedom, identified with the transversal polarizations
\varepsilon_{\alpha}(x)^{a}
(
\alpha=1,2
). Fock’s space of these physical states will be called the physical subspace,
\mathcal{F}_{\text{phys}}
, of the complete Fock’s space,
\mathcal{F}
, because the physical potentials will be those which satisfy both the null-plane and Lorenz’s gauge conditions. All the sectors of
\mathcal{F}
different from the physical subspace are inaccessible to experimental observation; therefore, there are no reasons to expect that the expression of the quantized field operator has the same form as its classical version: Fock’s space extension is an eminently mathematical process.

If the gauge conditions are not imposed to the quantized field operator, then we must quantize all the four polarization states
\lambda=+,1,2,-

a_{\lambda}^{\dagger}(f)

a_{\lambda}(f)

\left[a_{\lambda}(\bm{p});a_{\lambda}^{\dagger}(\bm{q})\right]=2p_{-}\delta(\bm{p}-\bm{q}).

(21)

As a consequence, all states, including the non-transverse ones, have positive-definite norm. The most natural extension of the quantized radiation field operator would be:

\displaystyle A^{a}(x)=(2\pi)^{-3/2}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{\lambda}\int d\mu(\bm{p})\varepsilon_{\lambda}(\bm{p})^{a}\left(a_{\lambda}(\bm{p})e^{-ipx}+a_{\lambda}^{\dagger}(\bm{p})e^{ipx}\right),

\displaystyle\left[A^{a}(x);A^{b}(y)\right]=(2\pi)^{-3}\int d^{4}p\delta(p^{2})\Theta(p_{-})

Poincaré’s invariance of the physical subspace

\varepsilon_{+}(\bm{p})^{a}
, is null [see Eq. (
2
)], we have that:

\displaystyle A^{+}(x)=(2\pi)^{-3/2}

\displaystyle\times\int d\mu(\bm{p})\varepsilon_{+}(\bm{p})^{+}\left(a_{+}(\bm{p})e^{-ipx}-a_{-}^{\dagger}(\bm{p})e^{ipx}\right).

(35)

Also, the divergence of the quantized field operator is:

\displaystyle\partial_{a}A^{a}(x)=-i(2\pi)^{-3/2}\sum_{\lambda}\int d\mu(\bm{p})p_{a}\varepsilon_{\lambda}(\bm{p})^{a}

\displaystyle\times\left(a_{\lambda}(\bm{p})e^{-ipx}+\sum_{\tau}g_{\lambda\tau}a_{\tau}^{\dagger}(\bm{p})e^{ipx}\right).

(36)

But, as it can be seen in Eq. (

), the polarization vectors are chosen in such a way that:

\varepsilon_{1,2}(\bm{p})_{-}=0\ ,\ \varepsilon_{1,2}(\bm{p})_{+}=-\frac{p_{\alpha}\varepsilon_{1,2}(\bm{p})^{\alpha}}{p_{-}},

(37)

which imply that they are orthogonal to the momentum
p^{a}
:
p_{a}\varepsilon_{1,2}(\bm{p})^{a}=0
. Therefore, in the sum in Eq. (
II.2
) only the polarization states
\lambda=+,-
contribute:

\displaystyle\partial_{a}A^{a}(x)=-i(2\pi)^{-3/2}\sum_{\lambda=+,-}\int d\mu(\bm{p})p_{a}\varepsilon_{\lambda}(\bm{p})^{a}

\displaystyle\times\left(a_{\lambda}(\bm{p})e^{-ipx}+\sum_{\tau}g_{\lambda\tau}a_{\tau}^{\dagger}(\bm{p})e^{ipx}\right),

(38)

and only the emission and absorption field operators
a_{\pm}^{\dagger}(\bm{p})
and
a_{\pm}(\bm{p})
appear. Since every state in
\mathcal{F}_{\text{phys}}
has polarizations
\lambda=1,2
, Eqs. (
II.2
) and (
II.2
) mean that this subspace can be characterized by the accomplishment of the gauge conditions as matrix elements:

\forall\Phi,\Psi\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{phys}}:\ \left(\Phi;A^{+}(x)\Psi\right)=0\ \wedge\ \left(\Phi;\partial_{a}A^{a}(x)\Psi\right)=0.

(39)

Let
f=(f_{a})
be a wave-function in the extended one-particle Hilbert’s space; it is transformed under a Poincaré’s transformation by the classical field law:

\left(U(a;\Lambda)f\right)(x)=\Lambda^{-1}f\left(\Lambda^{-1}(x-a)\right).

(40)

Over the space of these functions we define the operator
A(f)
according to:

A(f):=\int d^{4}xf_{a}(x)A^{a}(x);

(41)

this is the smearing of the operator-valued distribution
A^{a}(x)
over the test-function
f_{a}
. The operator of Poincaré’s transformation which acts on the extended Fock’s space is the operator
\bm{\mathsf{U}}(a;\Lambda)
such that:

\bm{\mathsf{U}}(a;\Lambda)A(f)\bm{\mathsf{U}}(a;\Lambda)^{-1}=A\left(U(a;\Lambda)f\right).

(42)

Hence, using Eqs. (
40
) and (
41
) we obtain the transformation law of the quantized field operator:

\bm{\mathsf{U}}(a;\Lambda)A^{a}(x)\bm{\mathsf{U}}(a;\Lambda)^{-1}=\left(\Lambda^{-1}\right)^{{{a}\mathchoice{\makebox[3.51666pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[3.51666pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.1029pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.50208pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[4.33765pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.33765pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.59009pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.85005pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}{b}}}A^{b}(\Lambda x+a).

(43)

The application of the “
\dagger
” adjoint to this equation allows us to see that the operator
\bm{\mathsf{U}}(a;\Lambda)
is not unitary because
A^{a}(x)
is not Hermitian. This does not violate Wigner’s theorem: Poincaré’s transformations are symmetries in the real world, so that they must be symmetries in the physical subspace only. In order to show that this is the case, denote by
\mathcal{F}_{L}
the subspace of
\mathcal{F}
which satisfies Lorenz’s gauge condition. In Ref.
Rohrlich 1971
it is shown that this gauge condition is compatible with the null-plane one in the free case –which is always the case in CPT–, and, equally important, that these two conditions determine the gauge completely –
i.e.
, there is no remnant gauge symmetry after the imposition of them–; therefore, we can affirm:

\displaystyle\forall\Phi^{\prime}\in

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{L}:\ \exists!\Phi\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{phys}}:

\displaystyle\Phi^{\prime}_{a}(x)=\Phi_{a}(x)+\partial_{a}\Lambda(x),\ \square\Lambda(x)=0.

(44)

We denote the projection onto the physical subspace by:
\Phi=P\Phi^{\prime}
. Now, the inner product in the one-particle space is:

\displaystyle(f;g)_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a}i\int f_{a}(x)^{*}\overleftrightarrow{\partial}_{-}g_{a}(x)d^{3}\bm{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a}\int d\mu(\bm{p})\hat{f}_{a}(\bm{p})^{*}\hat{g}_{a}(\bm{p}).

(45)

Let
\Phi,\Psi\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{phys}}
be one-particle states, with wave-functions
\Phi_{a}
and
\Psi_{a}
, respectively. They satisfy the null-plane gauge:
\Phi^{+}=0=\Psi^{+}
, hence their inner product can be put in Poincaré’s invariant form:

\displaystyle(\Phi;\Psi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}i\int\Phi_{\alpha}(x)^{*}\overleftrightarrow{\partial}_{-}\Psi_{\alpha}(x)d^{3}\bm{x}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\alpha}i\int\Phi_{\alpha}(x)^{*}\overleftrightarrow{\partial}_{-}\Psi^{\alpha}(x)d^{3}\bm{x}

\displaystyle=-(\Phi_{a};\Psi^{a}).

(46)

Let us apply now a reference frame transformation. In the general case the value of
\Phi^{+}=0
changes, and the transformed states
\Phi^{\prime}
and
\Psi^{\prime}
are no more in
\mathcal{F}_{\text{phys}}
, but they are in
\mathcal{F}_{L}
yet. Applying the operator
P
to obtain again states in
\mathcal{F}_{\text{phys}}
:

\widetilde{\Phi}=P\bm{\mathsf{U}}(a;\Lambda)\Phi=:\widetilde{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}(a;\Lambda)\Phi,\ \widetilde{\Psi}=\widetilde{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}(a;\Lambda)\Psi.

(47)

Therefore, the operator which transforms the states in
\mathcal{F}_{\text{phys}}
is not
\bm{\mathsf{U}}(a;\Lambda)
, but
\widetilde{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}(a;\Lambda)
, and it is this one that must be unitary on
\mathcal{F}_{\text{phys}}
. Effectively it is: Since
\widetilde{\Phi}
and
\Phi^{\prime}
are related by a gauge transformation, as well as
\widetilde{\Psi}
and
\Psi^{\prime}
, we write:

\Phi^{\prime}_{a}=\widetilde{\Phi}_{a}+\partial_{a}\chi,\ \Psi^{\prime}_{a}=\widetilde{\Psi}_{a}+\partial_{a}\Lambda.

(48)

Then the inner product in Eq. (
II.2
), which is Poincaré’s invariant, is equal to:

\displaystyle(\Phi;\Psi)=-(\Phi^{\prime}_{a};\Psi^{\prime a})

\displaystyle=\left(\widetilde{\Phi};\widetilde{\Psi}\right)-\left(\partial_{a}\chi;\widetilde{\Psi}^{a}\right)-\left(\widetilde{\Phi}_{a};\partial^{a}\Lambda\right)-\left(\partial_{a}\chi;\partial^{a}\Lambda\right).

(49)

It is easy to see that the second and third terms in the last equality are null, because
\widetilde{\Phi}
and
\widetilde{\Psi}
satisfy the gauge conditions and because they are assumed to vanish at infinity (asymptotic conditions). Using again the asymptotic conditions, the last term takes the form:

\displaystyle(\partial_{a}\chi;\partial^{a}\Lambda)=(\partial_{+}\chi;\partial_{-}\Lambda)+(\partial_{-}\chi;\partial_{+}\Lambda)-(\partial_{\alpha}\chi;\partial_{\alpha}\Lambda)

\displaystyle=-(\partial_{+}\partial_{-}\chi;\Lambda)-(\chi;\partial_{+}\partial_{-}\Lambda)+\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\perp}^{2}\chi;\Lambda)+\frac{1}{2}(\chi;\partial_{\perp}^{2}\Lambda)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}(\square\chi;\Lambda)-\frac{1}{2}(\chi;\square\Lambda),

which are null as established in Eq. (
II.2
). In conclusion:

(\Phi;\Psi)=\left(\widetilde{\Phi};\widetilde{\Psi}\right)=\left(\widetilde{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}(a;\Lambda)\Phi;\widetilde{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}(a;\Lambda)\Psi\right),

(50)

and Poincaré’s transformations operator in
\mathcal{F}_{\text{phys}}
,
\widetilde{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}(a;\Lambda)
, is a unitary operator. This shows that the physical subspace is the same in all reference frames.

II.3
Quantum gauge transformations

As we have said, the extension of Fock’s space is not a physical procedure, but a mathematical one. Accordingly, we now have to impose that the physical content of the theory is independent of this extension.

Definition.—
A quantum gauge transformation is a transformation of the quantized field, or equivalently, of the quantized field operator, which depends continuously on a constant parameter
\lambda
and has the form

A^{\prime}(f)=e^{-i\lambda Q}A(f)e^{i\lambda Q}\ \Leftrightarrow\ A^{\prime a}(x)=e^{-i\lambda Q}A^{a}(x)e^{i\lambda Q},

(51)

form which warrants (1) that the commutation distribution of the transformed field operator does not change, and (2) that the quantized field operator still satisfies the equation of motion
\square A^{\prime a}(x)=0
. Also, we impose the condition that
e^{i\lambda Q}
is an operator which leaves invariant the states of
\mathcal{F}_{\text{phys}}
. The operator
Q
, generator of the quantum gauge transformations, is called the gauge charge operator.

The most immediate consequence of this definition is that the gauge charge operator annihilates physical states, hence:

\mathcal{F}_{\text{phys}}\subseteq\text{Ker}(Q).

(52)

But we do not know yet under what conditions the equality holds in Eq. (
52
). Let us start by establishing:

Lemma:

The gauge charge operator
Q
, as well as its adjoint
Q^{\dagger}
, is constructed with non-physical emission and absorption operators, only.

Proof:
In first place, let us see that the non-physical states are orthogonal to the physical ones; in effect, let
a_{\text{phys}}^{\dagger}
and
a_{\text{nph}}^{\dagger}
be emission operators of physical and non-physical particles, respectively. Since they commute:

\displaystyle\left(a_{\text{phys}}^{\dagger}\Omega;a_{\text{nph}}^{\dagger}\Omega\right)=\left(\Omega;a_{\text{phys}}a_{\text{nph}}^{\dagger}\Omega\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\left(\Omega;a_{\text{nph}}^{\dagger}a_{\text{phys}}\Omega\right)=0.

(53)

Consider now two states
\Phi,\Psi\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{phys}}
:

0=(\Phi;Q\Psi)=\left(Q^{\dagger}\Phi;\Psi\right)\ \Rightarrow\ Q^{\dagger}\Phi\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{phys}}^{\perp}.

(54)

Now, every operator acting on Fock’s space can be written as a function of emission and absorption operators. Particularly, in order to
Q
to annihilate every physical state it is mandatory that all the terms in it have an absorption operator of a non-physical particle at the right. Let us suppose that one of the terms has a physical emission operator at the left:

Q\sim a_{\text{phys}}^{\dagger}a_{\text{nph}}+\ldots

(55)

Then, applying the adjoint to a physical state
\Phi
:

Q^{\dagger}\Phi\sim a_{\text{nph}}^{\dagger}a_{\text{phys}}\Phi+\ldots,

(56)

which in general still contains physical particles, in contradiction with Eq. (
54
). Hence,
Q
could not contain physical emission operators, and the same is clearly true for physical absorption operators. From this, it follows that
Q^{\dagger}
also contains non-physical emission and absorption operators, hence
\forall\Phi\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{phys}}
:
Q^{\dagger}\Phi=0
.
\blacksquare

As a consequence of this lemma, Eq. (
52
) must be substituted by:

\mathcal{F}_{\text{phys}}\subseteq\text{Ker}(Q)\cap\text{Ker}\left(Q^{\dagger}\right)=\text{Ker}\left(\left\{Q;Q^{\dagger}\right\}\right).

(57)

Expanding the exponentials in Eq. (
51
) as a series in the parameter
\lambda
, we will find that:

\displaystyle A^{\prime a}(x)=

\displaystyle A^{a}(x)-i\lambda\left[Q;A^{a}(x)\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\left[Q;[Q;A^{a}(x)]\right]+\mathscr{O}(\lambda^{3}).

(58)

By virtue of the lemma, all the commutators in Eq. (
II.3
) are non-null only for the non-physical part of the quantized field operator
A^{a}(x)
; in other words, the quantum gauge transformation does not modify the dynamical (physical) part of the quantized field, as required, and all the quantities constructed with
A^{a}(x)
will have the same matrix elements on the physical subspace: This is quantum gauge invariance.

Now, every operator
Q
, constructed with non-physical emission and absorption operators in such a way that an emission operator is at the left and an absorption operator is at the right of every term, can be used as a generator of a quantum gauge transformation. However, such a general gauge charge could originate a transformed gauge field which is a composite operator. We must impose that
Q
is a quadratic operator in order to maintain
A^{a}(x)
as a simple field operator.

For a general gauge charge, Eq. (
57
) is satisfied with the symbol “
\subset
”; the equality holds if
\left\{Q;Q^{\dagger}\right\}
is an operator in which all the non-physical absorption operators appear. In order to obtain such a gauge charge, note that
[Q;A^{a}(x)]
is always a linear combination of non-physical emission and absorption operators. Therefore, regarding Eq. (
II.3
), define the field operator
u(x)
by:

i\partial_{a}u(x):=\left[Q;A_{a}(x)\right].

(59)

Eq. (
II.3
) adopts the form:

A^{\prime a}(x)=A^{a}(x)+\lambda\partial^{a}u(x)-\frac{i\lambda^{2}}{2}\left[Q;\partial^{a}u(x)\right]+\mathscr{O}(\lambda^{3}).

(60)

But, by definition,
A^{\prime a}(x)
must satisfy Klein-Gordon-Fock’s equation, which is possible if
u(x)
satisfies:

\square u(x)=0.

(61)

The solution for this equation is:

u(x)=\int\limits_{y^{+}=y_{0}^{+}}u(y)\overleftrightarrow{\partial}_{-}^{y}D_{0}(y-x).

(62)

Deriving Eq. (
62
) and recognizing the commutation distribution of the extended gauge field operator of Eq. (
30
):

i\partial_{a}u(x)=\left[\int\limits_{y^{+}=y_{0}^{+}}\partial_{b}A^{b}(y)\overleftrightarrow{\partial}_{-}u(y)d^{3}\bm{y};A_{a}(x)\right],

(63)

from which we identify, by comparison with Eq. (
59
), that the gauge charge operator is:

Q=\int\limits_{x^{+}=x_{0}^{+}}\partial_{a}A^{a}(x)\overleftrightarrow{\partial}_{-}u(x)d^{3}\bm{x}.

(64)

On the other hand, Eq. (
61
) implies that the field operator
u(x)
has the form:

u(x)=(2\pi)^{-3/2}\int d\mu(\bm{p})\left(c_{2}(\bm{p})e^{-ipx}+c_{1}^{\dagger}(\bm{p})e^{ipx}\right).

(65)

The field operators
c_{1}(\bm{p})
and
c_{2}(\bm{p})
are, at the moment, unknown, but they belong to non-physical ‘‘particles’’
2
2

2

This is, of course, an abuse of language, because the concept of particle can only be defined in the asymptotically free region, whereas a “non-physical particle” cannot reach that region.
. Joining Eqs. (
II.2
) and (
65
) into Eq. (
64
) and by an elemental integration, we find:

\displaystyle Q=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\lambda=+,-}\int d\mu(\bm{p})p_{a}\varepsilon_{\lambda}(\bm{p})^{a}

\displaystyle\times\left(a_{\lambda}(\bm{p})c_{1}^{\dagger}(\bm{p})-\sum_{\tau}g_{\lambda\tau}a_{\tau}^{\dagger}(\bm{p})c_{2}(\bm{p})\right).

(66)

By construction, this is the most general form that a quadratic gauge charge operator can have. This formula reveals: Since
Q
must have absorption operators to the right, and this does not occur in the first term of Eq. (
II.3
), we must commute
a_{\lambda}(\bm{p})
with
c_{1}^{\dagger}(\bm{p})
; but, in doing that, if
c_{1}(\bm{p})
were one of
a_{+}(\bm{p})
or
a_{-}(\bm{p})
, then
Q
would gain a constant term and does not annihilate the physical states. This is impossible. In consequence, it is necessary to extend Fock’s space even more in order to contain field operators
c_{1}(\bm{p})
and
c_{2}(\bm{p})
and their adjoints, associated to non-physical particles and which are different from
a_{+}(\bm{p})
and
a_{-}(\bm{p})
. The quantized field operator
u(x)
, as a new non-physical field, is called ghost field. As we see, the ghost field is indispensable for quantum gauge invariance. Since
c_{1}^{\dagger}(\bm{p})
corresponds to a new particle, it can be simply commutated with
a_{\lambda}(\bm{p})
. From this in Eq. (
II.3
) it follows that:

\displaystyle Q=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\lambda=+,-}\int d\mu(\bm{p})p_{a}\varepsilon_{\lambda}(\bm{p})^{a}

\displaystyle\times\left(c_{1}^{\dagger}(\bm{p})a_{\lambda}(\bm{p})-\sum_{\tau}g_{\lambda\tau}a_{\tau}^{\dagger}(\bm{p})c_{2}(\bm{p})\right).

(67)

The explicit form of the polarization vectors of the massless vector field are given in Eq. (
2
), from which it follows that:

p_{a}\varepsilon_{+}(\bm{p})^{a}=p_{+}-\frac{p_{\perp}^{2}}{2p_{-}}=\frac{p^{2}}{2p_{-}},\ p_{a}\varepsilon_{-}(\bm{p})^{a}=p_{-}.

(68)

Introducing this into Eq. (
II.3
) and taking in mind that for the massless field it is
p^{2}=0
, we arrive at the final expression:

Q=\int d\mu(\bm{p})p_{-}\left(c_{1}^{\dagger}(\bm{p})a_{-}(\bm{p})-a_{+}^{\dagger}(\bm{p})c_{2}(\bm{p})\right).

(69)

The adjoint operator is:

Q^{\dagger}=\int d\mu(\bm{p})p_{-}\left(a_{-}^{\dagger}(\bm{p})c_{1}(\bm{p})-c_{2}^{\dagger}(\bm{p})a_{+}(\bm{p})\right).

(70)

In order to characterize the physical subspace we are interested in the anticommutator of
Q
and
Q^{\dagger}
[see Eq. (
57
)]. Using Eqs. (
69
) and (
70
):

\displaystyle\left\{Q;Q^{\dagger}\right\}=\int d\mu(\bm{p})d\mu(\bm{q})p_{-}q_{-}\Big(2p_{-}\delta(\bm{p}-\bm{q})c_{1}^{\dagger}(\bm{p})c_{1}(\bm{p})

\displaystyle+2p_{-}\delta(\bm{p}-\bm{q})c_{2}^{\dagger}(\bm{p})c_{2}(\bm{p})+\left\{c_{1}(\bm{q});c_{1}^{\dagger}(\bm{p})\right\}a_{-}^{\dagger}(\bm{q})a_{-}(\bm{p})

\displaystyle+\left\{c_{2}(\bm{p});c_{2}^{\dagger}(\bm{q})\right\}a_{+}^{\dagger}(\bm{p})a_{+}(\bm{q})\Big).

(71)

The first two terms in this equation have the desired form. For the other two, if
c_{1}(\bm{p})
and
c_{2}(\bm{p})
were boson operators, then the anticommutator
\left\{Q;Q^{\dagger}\right\}
would have terms of the form
a_{-}^{\dagger}(\bm{q})c_{1}^{\dagger}(\bm{p})c_{1}(\bm{q})a_{-}(\bm{p})
, and there will be non-physical states annihilated by
\left\{Q;Q^{\dagger}\right\}
, for example, a one particle state with longitudinal polarization. As this is not required and contradicts our hypothesis on the physical degrees of freedom of the gauge field, the quantized field operator
u
must be a fermion field, with emission and absorption field operators subjected to the anticommutation rules:

\displaystyle\left\{c_{1}(\bm{p});c_{1}^{\dagger}(\bm{q})\right\}=2p_{-}\delta(\bm{p}-\bm{q}),

\displaystyle\left\{c_{2}(\bm{p});c_{2}^{\dagger}(\bm{q})\right\}=2p_{-}\delta(\bm{p}-\bm{q}).

(72)

And, hence:

\left\{u(x);\widetilde{u}(y)\right\}=-iD_{0}(x-y).

(73)

The field
\widetilde{u}(x)
is called antighost field, and is given by:

\widetilde{u}(x)=(2\pi)^{-3/2}\int d\mu(\bm{p})\left(-c_{1}(\bm{p})e^{-ipx}+c_{2}^{\dagger}(\bm{p})e^{ipx}\right).

(74)

Being that way, Eq. (
71
) adopts the final form:

\displaystyle\left\{Q;Q^{\dagger}\right\}

\displaystyle=\int d\mu(\bm{p})p_{-}^{2}\Big(a_{-}^{\dagger}(\bm{p})a_{-}(\bm{p})+a_{+}^{\dagger}(\bm{p})a_{+}(\bm{p})

\displaystyle+c_{1}^{\dagger}(\bm{p})c_{1}(\bm{p})+c_{2}^{\dagger}(\bm{p})c_{2}(\bm{p})\Big).

(75)

And now we can affirm:

\mathcal{F}_{\text{phys}}=\text{Ker}\left(\left\{Q;Q^{\dagger}\right\}\right).

(76)

Also, as a consequence of Eq. (
69
), and by direct calculation, it is immediate to see that the gauge charge operator is nilpotent:

Q^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left\{Q;Q\right\}=0.

(77)

The fermion statistics of the ghost particles implies, in Eq. (
60
), that the parameter
\lambda
is a number following a Grassmannian multiplication rule, so the series ends in the term linear in
\lambda
: The quantum gauge transformation of the operator
F(x)
, containing gauge, ghosts and anti-ghosts field operators, is
exactly
:

F^{\prime}(x)=e^{-i\lambda Q}F(x)e^{i\lambda Q}=F(x)-i\lambda QF(x)+iF(x)\lambda Q.

(78)

Since
\lambda
is a Grassmann’s number, we cannot simply commute it with
F(x)
in the last term of Eq. (
78
); in putting it at the left of
F(x)
we must write a factor
(-1)^{n_{F}}
, with
n_{F}
the so-called ghost number of the operator
F(x)
, equal to the difference between the number of ghosts and anti-ghost fields contained in it. Therefore, we define the gauge variation of the operator
F(x)
as:

d_{Q}F(x):=\left[Q;F(x)\right]_{(-1)^{1+n_{F}}}.

(79)

From Eq. (
78
) it follows that the exact gauge transformation is given by:

F^{\prime}(x)=F(x)-i\lambda d_{Q}F(x).

(80)

And from this equation we see that, necessarily,
d_{Q}
changes the statistics of the operator
F(x)
, in such a way that
\lambda d_{Q}F(x)
maintains the one of
F(x)
.

Let us enumerate some properties of the gauge variation
d_{Q}
. Firstly, it satisfies the following rule for the product of operators:

d_{Q}(F(x)G(y))=(d_{Q}F(x))G(y)+(-1)^{n_{F}}F(x)(d_{Q}G(y)).

(81)

Secondly, it is nilpotent:

d_{Q}^{2}=0.

(82)

Thirdly, from Eq. (
59
) they follow the gauge variations of the field operators:

d_{Q}A^{{{a}\mathchoice{\makebox[6.25002pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[6.25002pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[3.81111pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[2.72223pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[4.33765pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.33765pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.59009pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.85005pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}{\pm}}}=i\partial^{a}u_{\pm},

(83)

d_{Q}u=0,

(84)

d_{Q}\widetilde{u}_{\pm}=-i\partial_{a}A^{{{a}\mathchoice{\makebox[6.25002pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[6.25002pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[3.81111pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[2.72223pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[4.33765pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.33765pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.59009pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.85005pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}{\pm}}}.

(85)

And fourthly, it satisfies the following result, proved in Ref.
Scharf 2016
:

Lemma:

Let
G
be a Wick’s monomial containing gauge fields
A
, ghosts
u
and anti-ghosts
\widetilde{u}
. The gauge variation of
G
commutes with its normal ordering:

d_{Q}\;\bm{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}}\,\mathrel{G}\,\bm{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}}\;=\;\bm{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}}\,\mathrel{d_{Q}G}\,\bm{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}}\;.

(86)

II.4
Quantum gauge invariance of the scattering operator

The concept of quantum gauge invariance was already introduced in the commentaries after Eq. (
II.3
): It is the independence of the physical quantities from the mathematical operation of extending Fock’s space. This kind of symmetries frequently occurs in physics; examples of it are the principle of general covariance, the one of classical gauge symmetry,
et cetera
. In the
S
-matrix program the fundamental physical quantity is the transition amplitude between two physical states. Then
Scharf 2016
:

Definition.—
Let
P
be the projection operator onto the physical subspace
\mathcal{F}_{\text{phys}}
. Two scattering operators
S(g)
and
S^{\prime}(g)
are called physically equivalent if for every states
\Phi,\Psi\in\mathcal{F}
:

\lim_{g\to 1}\left(\Phi;PS(g)P\Psi\right)=\lim_{g\to 1}\left(\Phi;PS^{\prime}(g)P\Psi\right),

(87)

if the adiabatic limit exists.

The condition of existence of the adiabatic limit is essential, because only in that case the bilinear form
\left(\Phi;PS(g)P\Psi\right)
has physical meaning; such existence will be assumed in the present discussion. Inserting in this definition the formal series of Eq. (
6
) we obtain a perturbative version of it:

Definition.—
Two scattering operators
S(g)
and
S^{\prime}(g)
are (perturbatively) physically equivalent at the
n
-th order if their corresponding
n
-point distributions satisfy –the limit is weak in the sense of Eq. (
87
)–:

\text{w-}\lim_{g\to 1}\int dX\left(PT_{n}(X)P-PT^{\prime}_{n}(X)P\right)g(X)=0,

(88)

if the adiabatic limit exists for the order
n
of the perturbation series.

Eq. (
88
) has the general solution
PT_{n}(X)P=PT^{\prime}_{n}(X)P+\partial(\ldots)
, with
\partial(\ldots)
meaning the divergence of the quantity
(\ldots)
. In effect, the integration by parts implies that in such case the left-hand-side of Eq. (
88
) is equal to:

\text{w-}\lim_{g\to 1}\int dX(\ldots)\partial g(X),

(89)

which is null in the adiabatic limit in which
g
turns to a constant. On the other hand, in the expression
PT_{n}(X)P
,
T_{n}(X)
is defined modulo a gauge variation, because Eq. (
76
) implies that:
PQ=0=QP
, and, as a consequence:
Pd_{Q}XP=PQX-XQP=0
. As a result, we have that the distributions
T_{n}
and
T^{\prime}_{n}
are physically equivalent if they differ by terms which are divergences or gauge variations. This we denote by the symbol “
\sim
”:

T^{\prime}_{n}\sim T_{n}\ :\Leftrightarrow\ T^{\prime}_{n}=T_{n}+d_{Q}(\ldots)+\partial(\ldots).

(90)

Being that way, the matrix elements of the
S(g)
operator between states of the physical subspace, at any order, are trivially quantum gauge invariant, because
d_{Q}T_{n}
is physically equivalent to the null distribution:
0\sim d_{Q}T_{n}
. Quantum gauge invariance of
T_{1}(x)
, therefore, is assured whatever it was. But this is not sufficient:
T_{1}(x)
must be constructed in such a way that the second order distribution
T_{2}(x_{1};x_{2})
is also physically equivalent to
T^{\prime}_{2}(x_{1};x_{2})
constructed with
T^{\prime}_{1}(x)=T_{1}(x)-i\lambda d_{Q}T_{1}(x)
. This is a consistency condition. And, clearly, the same must be true for the higher order
T_{n}
distributions. In a word, we must impose that quantum gauge invariance is not destroyed in the inductive procedure of CPT.

Definition.—
An interacting theory generated by
T_{1}(x)
is a quantum gauge theory if it is physically equivalent to the theory generated by
T_{1}(x)-i\lambda d_{Q}T_{1}(x)
at all orders of the perturbation series.

Let us start by studying the second order distribution,
T_{2}(x_{1};x_{2})
. We introduce the following notation:
T_{1}(x)=T_{1}^{0}(x)-i\lambda d_{Q}T_{1}^{0}(x)
;
T_{2}^{0}(x_{1};x_{2})
is the two-points distribution coming from
T_{1}^{0}(x)
;
T_{2}(x_{1};x_{2})
is the one corresponding to
T_{1}(x)
, and so on. As we have already known, the construction of the transition distribution starts by determining the subsidiary distributions:

\displaystyle A^{\prime}_{2}(x_{1};x_{2})=-T_{1}(x_{1})T_{1}(x_{2}),

\displaystyle R^{\prime}_{2}(x_{1};x_{2})=-T_{1}(x_{2})T_{1}(x_{1}).

(91)

Substituting in them the expression of
T_{1}(x)
we have that the second order causal distribution,
D_{2}=R^{\prime}_{2}-A^{\prime}_{2}
, is:

\displaystyle D_{2}(x_{1};x_{2})=D_{2}^{0}(x_{1};x_{2})-i\lambda d_{Q}D_{2}^{0}(x_{1};x_{2})

\displaystyle+2i\lambda T_{1}^{0}(x_{1})d_{Q}T_{1}^{0}(x_{2})-2i\lambda T_{1}^{0}(x_{2})d_{Q}T_{1}(x_{1}).

(92)

From it we extract the retarded part,
R_{2}(x_{1};x_{2})
, by means of the splitting procedure:

\displaystyle R_{2}(x_{1};x_{2})=R_{2}^{0}(x_{1};x_{2})-i\lambda d_{Q}R_{2}^{0}(x_{1};x_{2})

\displaystyle+2i\lambda\text{ret}\left\{T_{1}^{0}(x_{1})d_{Q}T_{1}^{0}(x_{2})-T_{1}^{0}(x_{2})d_{Q}T_{1}^{0}(x_{1})\right\},

(93)

with
\text{ret}\left\{\ldots\right\}
meaning the retarded part of
\left\{\ldots\right\}
. The two-points distribution
T_{2}=R_{2}-R^{\prime}_{2}
is then:

\displaystyle T_{2}(x_{1};x_{2})=T_{2}^{0}(x_{1};x_{2})-i\lambda d_{Q}T_{2}^{0}(x_{1};x_{2})

\displaystyle+2i\lambda\Big(\text{ret}\left\{T_{1}^{0}(x_{1})d_{Q}T_{1}^{0}(x_{2})-T_{1}^{0}(x_{2})d_{Q}T_{1}^{0}(x_{1})\right\}

\displaystyle-T_{1}^{0}(x_{2})d_{Q}T_{1}^{0}(x_{1})\Big).

(94)

From here it follows that
T_{2}
will be physically equivalent to
T_{2}^{0}
if and only if the second line in Eq. (
94
) is a divergence or a gauge variation. The first possibility is realized if
T_{1}^{0}(x)
is a gauge variation:
T_{1}^{0}(x)=d_{Q}(\ldots)
; this case has no physical interest. The second possibility holds when
T_{1}^{0}(x)
is a divergence:

d_{Q}T_{1}^{0}(x)=i\partial_{a}T^{a}_{1/1}(x).

(95)

And, in that way, the theory maintains quantum gauge invariance at second order:

T_{2}\sim T_{2}^{0}.

(96)

The
T_{1/1}^{a}(x)
distribution is called Q-vertex. Hence, Eq. (
95

T_{1}(x)
is not only gauge invariant as a bilinear form in the physical subspace, but in the whole Fock’s space.

In order to see if Eq. (
95
) is also a sufficient condition in order to the theory to be quantum gauge invariant we must study the following orders in the perturbation series, which will be easier after establishing the consequences of Eq. (
95
). As we know, the
n

distributions –ordered according to the null-plane time

T_{n}(x_{1};\ldots;x_{n})=\mathfrak{T}_{+}\left\{T_{1}(x_{1})\ldots T_{1}(x_{n})\right\}.

Since every

has boson character, when we apply the gauge variation operator to

d_{Q}T_{n}=\sum\limits_{l=1}^{n}\mathfrak{T}_{+}\left\{T_{1}(x_{1})\ldots d_{Q}T_{1}(x_{l})\ldots T_{1}(x_{n})\right\}.

(98)

And with Eq. (
95
):

d_{Q}T_{n}=i\sum\limits_{l=1}^{n}\partial_{a}^{x_{l}}T^{a}_{n/l}(x_{1};\ldots;x_{n});

(99)

T^{a}_{n/l}:=\mathfrak{T}_{+}\left\{T_{1}(x_{1})\ldots T_{1/1}^{a}(x_{l})\ldots T_{1}(x_{n})\right\}.

Now,
T_{1}(x)
is in general a Wick’s polynomial:

T_{1}(x)=T_{1}^{0}(x)+T_{1}^{1}(x)+\ldots+T_{1}^{p}(x),

(100)

with every
T_{1}^{j}(x)
a Wick’s monomial. Considering Eq. (

T_{n}(x_{1};\ldots;x_{n})=\sum\limits_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}\in\left\{0;\ldots;p\right\}}T_{n}^{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}(x_{1};\ldots;x_{n});

(101)

with the superindex
i_{j}
indicating the one-point distribution
T_{1}^{i_{j}}
corresponding to the point
x_{j}
, this is to say:

\displaystyle T_{n}^{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}(x_{1};\ldots;x_{n}):=

\displaystyle\quad\mathfrak{T}_{+}\left\{T_{1}^{i_{1}}(x_{1})\ldots T_{1}^{i_{j}}(x_{j})\ldots T_{1}^{i_{n}}(x_{n})\right\}.

(102)

With this we can already establish the following results, which are general and contain, as a particular case, the sufficiency of Eq. (

Theorem:

Let
T_{1}(x)
be a distribution of the form:

T_{1}(x)=T_{1}^{0}(x)+T_{1}^{2}(x);\ T_{1}^{2}(x)=\partial_{a}T_{1}^{4a}(x).

(103)

If the gauge variation of

is a divergence,

d_{Q}T_{1}^{0}(x)=i\partial_{a}T_{1}^{1a}(x),

(104)

then, in Eq. (
II.4
) notation:

T_{n}(x_{1};\ldots;x_{n})=T_{n}^{0\ldots 0}(x_{1};\ldots;x_{n})+\partial(\ldots).

(105)

According to this theorem, any term in
T_{1}(x)
which is a divergence can be disregarded without altering the physics at any order in the perturbation series. In particular, this is valid for the divergence of the Q-vertex, which means that Eq. (
95
) is a sufficient condition for quantum gauge invariance at all orders.

Theorem:

Let
T_{1}(x)
be a distribution of the form:

T_{1}(x)=T_{1}^{0}(x)+T_{1}^{3}(x)\quad;\quad T_{1}^{3}(x)=d_{Q}T_{1}^{5}(x).

(106)

If for all order

the gauge variation

is a divergence,

d_{Q}T_{n}^{0\ldots 0}=\partial(\ldots),

(107)

then
T_{n}
and

are physically equivalents:

T_{n}(x_{1};\ldots;x_{n})=T_{n}^{0\ldots 0}(x_{1};\ldots;x_{n})+\partial(\ldots)+d_{Q}(\ldots).

(108)

Note that Eq. (

) is always respected once
T_{1}(x)
is appropriately chosen [see Eq. (
99
)],
when all the coordinates correspond to different times
. On the other hand, Eq. (

) could be invalid only because of instantaneous terms –this is to say, when there are some points corresponding to the same time–, but they can always be cancelled by a convenient choice of the normalization terms. Therefore, all the terms in

which are gauge variations can be neglected without any physical implication.

II.5
Unitarity of the scattering operator in
\mathcal{F}_{\text{phys}}

There is, still, one point that must be clarified, which is if the extension of Fock’s space and quantum gauge invariance is compatible with the axiom of unitarity of the scattering operator. Remember that such unitarity, being a physical property, must be imposed on
\mathcal{F}_{\text{phys}}
only. As this issue has already appeared in our study of Poincaré’s transformations, let us start by considering Eq. (
43
), from which it follows that there is a possibility for
\bm{\mathsf{U}}(a;\Lambda)
to be pseudo-unitary on the whole Fock’s space according to some adjoint “
K
”, in the sense:

\bm{\mathsf{U}}(a;\Lambda)^{K}=\bm{\mathsf{U}}(a;\Lambda)^{-1},

(109)

if the extended quantized field operator
A^{a}(x)
is Hermitian according to it:
A^{a}(x)^{K}=A^{a}(x)
. This is:

\displaystyle A^{a}(x)=(2\pi)^{-3/2}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{\lambda}\int d\mu(\bm{p})\varepsilon_{\lambda}(\bm{p})^{a}\left(a_{\lambda}(\bm{p})e^{-ipx}+a_{\lambda}^{K}(\bm{p})e^{ipx}\right).

(110)

Comparison with Eq. (
II.1
) leads to the equalities:

a_{\lambda}^{K}(\bm{p})=-\sum_{\tau}g_{\lambda\tau}a_{\tau}^{\dagger}(\bm{p}),

(111)

this is to say:
\quad a_{1,2}^{K}(\bm{p})=a_{1,2}^{\dagger}(\bm{p})
and
a_{\pm}^{K}(\bm{p})=-a_{\mp}^{\dagger}(\bm{p})
. In instant dynamics with the physical space containing photon wavefunctions in the radiation gauge, one can find explicitly that a similar adjoint exists by using the definition
B^{K}=(-1)^{\mathsf{N}_{0}}B^{\dagger}(-1)^{\mathsf{N}_{0}}
, with
\mathsf{N}_{0}
the number of scalar particles operator
Scharf 2014
;
Scharf 2016
. In light-front dynamics, instead, while not finding its explicit expression, we will prove that such an adjoint “
K
” does exist, by considering the following theorem
Prugovečki 2006
:

Theorem:

Let
(\bullet;\bullet)_{K}
be a bilinear form in Hilbert’s space
\mathcal{H}
. If it is bounded,
i.e.
:

\exists C\in\mathbb{R}_{+}:\ \forall f,g\in\mathcal{H}:\ \left|(f;g)_{K}\right|\leq C\|f\|\|g\|\ ,

(112)

then there is a bounded linear operator
\eta:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
, with
\|\eta\|\leq C
and
\text{Dom}(\eta)=\mathcal{H}
, such that:

(f;g)_{K}=(f;\eta g).

(113)

Moreover,
(\bullet;\bullet)_{K}
is Hermitian
3
3

3

This is to say, for every two vectors
f,g\in\mathcal{H}
:
(f;g)_{K}=(g;f)^{*}_{K}
.

if and only if the operator
\eta
is self-adjoint,
\eta^{\dagger}=\eta
.

We start by defining the following operators, for test functions
f\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
:

\displaystyle a^{a}_{\lambda}(f):=\int d\mu(\bm{p})\hat{f}(\bm{p})^{*}\varepsilon_{\lambda}(\bm{p})^{a}a_{\lambda}(\bm{p}),

\displaystyle a^{a}_{\lambda}(f)^{\dagger}:=\int d\mu(\bm{p})\hat{f}(\bm{p})\varepsilon_{\lambda}(\bm{p})^{a}a_{\lambda}^{\dagger}(\bm{p}).

(114)

They generate the states:

\Phi^{a_{1}\cdots a_{n}}_{\lambda_{1}\cdots\lambda_{n}}(f_{1};\cdots;f_{n}):=a^{a_{1}}_{\lambda_{1}}(f_{1})^{\dagger}\cdots a^{a_{n}}_{\lambda_{n}}(f_{n})^{\dagger}\Omega.

(115)

Similarly, having Eq. (
111
) as inspiration, we define the operators:

a^{a}_{\lambda}(f)^{K}:=-\int d\mu(\bm{p})\hat{f}(\bm{p})\varepsilon_{\lambda}(\bm{p})^{a}\sum_{\tau}g_{\tau\lambda}a_{\tau}^{\dagger}(\bm{p}),

(116)

and the states generated by them are denoted:

\widetilde{\Phi}^{a_{1}\cdots a_{n}}_{\lambda_{1}\cdots\lambda_{n}}(f_{1};\cdots;f_{n}):=a^{a_{1}}_{\lambda_{1}}(f_{1})^{K}\cdots a^{a_{n}}_{\lambda_{n}}(f_{n})^{K}\Omega.

(117)

We define now the bilinear form:

\displaystyle\left(\Phi_{\lambda_{1}\cdots\lambda_{n}}(f_{1};\cdots;f_{n});\Phi_{\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{m}}(g_{1};\cdots;g_{m})\right)_{K}:=

\displaystyle\quad\left(\Phi_{\lambda_{1}\cdots\lambda_{n}}(f_{1};\cdots;f_{n});\widetilde{\Phi}_{\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{m}}(g_{1};\cdots;g_{m})\right).

(118)

This definition, given only for the states of Eq. (

), is suficient because they generate Fock’s space and because it is defined with regard to the inner product; its evaluation for more general states is defined by linear continuation.

Attending at first at the one-particle states, the direct calculus shows that:

\displaystyle\left(\Phi_{\lambda}(f);\Phi_{\sigma}(g)\right)_{K}=

\displaystyle-g_{\lambda\sigma}\int d\mu(\bm{p})\hat{f}(\bm{p})^{*}\hat{g}(\bm{p})\left(\sum_{a}\varepsilon_{\lambda}(\bm{p})^{a}\varepsilon_{\sigma}(\bm{p})^{a}\right).

(119)

By comparison, the inner product between the same states is:

\displaystyle\left(\Phi_{\lambda}(f);\Phi_{\sigma}(g)\right)=

\displaystyle\delta_{\lambda\sigma}\int d\mu(\bm{p})\hat{f}(\bm{p})^{*}\hat{g}(\bm{p})\left(\sum_{a}\varepsilon_{\lambda}(\bm{p})^{a}\varepsilon_{\sigma}(\bm{p})^{a}\right).

(120)

So we see that for the physical polarizations
\lambda=1,2
, the bilinear form
(\bullet;\bullet)_{K}
reduces to the inner product, which satisfies Eq. (
112
) with

because of Cauchy-Schwarz’s inequality. The other case in which the bilinear product is non-null (modulo symmetry operations) is:

\displaystyle\left(\Phi_{+}(f);\Phi_{-}(g)\right)_{K}=-\int d\mu(\bm{p})\hat{f}(\bm{p})^{*}\hat{g}(\bm{p})

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\times\left(\sum_{a}\varepsilon_{+}(\bm{p})^{a}\varepsilon_{-}(\bm{p})^{a}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=-\int d\mu(\bm{p})\frac{p_{\perp}^{2}}{2p_{-}^{2}}\hat{f}(\bm{p})^{*}\hat{g}(\bm{p}),

(121)

in which we have used the explicit form of the polarization vectors. On the other hand, from Eq. (

\displaystyle\left\|\Phi_{+}(f)\right\|^{2}=\int d\mu(\bm{p})\left(1+\frac{p_{\perp}^{2}}{2p_{-}^{2}}\right)^{2}\left|\hat{f}(\bm{p})\right|^{2},

\displaystyle\left\|\Phi_{-}(g)\right\|^{2}=\int d\mu(\bm{p})\left|\hat{g}(\bm{p})\right|^{2}.

(122)

Eqs. (
121
) and (
II.5
), together with Cauchy-Schwarz’s inequality in

, imply that:

\displaystyle\left|\left(\Phi_{+}(f);\Phi_{-}(g)\right)_{K}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int d\mu(\bm{p})\left(\frac{p_{\perp}^{2}}{2p_{-}^{2}}\hat{f}(\bm{p})\right)^{*}\hat{g}(\bm{p})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\left(\frac{p_{\perp}^{2}}{2p_{-}^{2}}\hat{f}(\bm{p});\hat{g}(\bm{p})\right)_{L^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\frac{p_{\perp}^{2}}{2p_{-}^{2}}\hat{f}\right\|\left\|\hat{g}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\Phi_{+}(f)\right\|\left\|\Phi_{-}(g)\right\|.

(123)

Therefore, also in this case Eq. (
112
) is satisfied with
C=1
. Now we can generalize to the case in which the states are linear combinations of different polarizations, as for the same polarization:
\Phi^{a}_{\lambda}(f_{1})+\Phi^{a}_{\lambda}(f_{2})=\Phi^{a}_{\lambda}(f_{1}+f_{2})
. Hence, since each polarization has only one corresponding polarization such that the bilinear form is non-null, as indicated by Eq. (
II.5
), we will have, for example:

\displaystyle\left|\left(\Phi_{\lambda_{1}}+\Phi_{\lambda_{2}};\Phi_{\sigma_{1}}+\Phi_{\sigma_{2}}\right)_{K}\right|

\displaystyle\quad=\left|\left(\Phi_{\lambda_{1}};\Phi_{\sigma_{1}}\right)_{K}+\left(\Phi_{\lambda_{2}};\Phi_{\sigma_{2}}\right)_{K}\right|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left|\left(\Phi_{\lambda_{1}};\Phi_{\sigma_{1}}\right)_{K}\right|+\left|\left(\Phi_{\lambda_{2}};\Phi_{\sigma_{2}}\right)_{K}\right|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left\|\Phi_{\lambda_{1}}\right\|\left\|\Phi_{\sigma_{1}}\right\|+\left\|\Phi_{\lambda_{2}}\right\|\left\|\Phi_{\sigma_{2}}\right\|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left(\left\|\Phi_{\lambda_{1}}\right\|^{2}+\left\|\Phi_{\lambda_{2}}\right\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\left\|\Phi_{\sigma_{1}}\right\|^{2}+\left\|\Phi_{\sigma_{2}}\right\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

The last line contains the norm of the states
\Phi^{a}_{\lambda_{1}}+\Phi^{a}_{\lambda_{2}}
and
\Phi^{b}_{\sigma_{1}}+\Phi^{b}_{\sigma_{2}}
, because the polarization vectors –and then the corresponding states– are mutually orthogonal –see Eq. (
II.5
)–. The same applies when other polarizations are present, so even in the most general linear combination:

\left|\left(\Phi;\Psi\right)_{K}\right|\leq\|\Phi\|\|\Psi\|.

(124)

As the constant is
C=1
, its value will not be modified when tensor products are considered, so Eq. (
124
) is valid for any
\Phi,\Psi\in\mathcal{F}
. The above theorem then implies that there is an operator
\eta
, defined over the entire Fock’s space, such that:

(\Phi;\Psi)_{K}=(\Phi;\eta\Psi),\ \|\eta\|\leq 1.

(125)

Even more, from Eq. (
II.5
) it is clear that the bilinear form
(\bullet;\bullet)_{K}
is Hermitian,

\left(\Phi_{\lambda}(f);\Phi_{\sigma}(g)\right)^{*}_{K}=\left(\Phi_{\sigma}(g);\Phi_{\lambda}(f)\right)_{K},

from which it follows that the operator

\eta^{\dagger}=\eta.

(127)

\left(\widetilde{\Phi}_{\lambda}(f);\widetilde{\Phi}_{\sigma}(g)\right)=\left(\Phi_{\lambda}(f);\Phi_{\sigma}(g)\right),

\displaystyle\quad=\left(\eta\Phi_{\sigma}(g);\eta\Phi_{\lambda}(f)\right)^{*}=\left(\Phi_{\lambda}(f);\eta^{2}\Phi_{\sigma}(g)\right).

The comparison of Eqs. (

\eta^{2}=1.

(130)

Other important properties are the following. Since the bilinear form

\forall\Phi\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{phys}}:\ \eta\Phi=\Phi.

This is valid, particularly, for the vacuum state:
\eta\Omega=\Omega

\left(\Phi;\eta a^{\dagger}_{\lambda_{1}}(f_{1})\cdots a^{\dagger}_{\lambda_{n}}(f_{n})\Omega\right)=\left(\Phi;a^{K}_{\lambda_{1}}(f_{1})\cdots a^{K}_{\lambda_{n}}(f_{n})\Omega\right).

B

(A+B)^{K}=A^{K}+B^{K},\ (AB)^{K}=B^{K}A^{K},

Theorem:

Let
T_{1}(x)
be the one-point distribution of a quantum gauge theory. If

\widetilde{T}_{1}(x)=T_{1}(x)^{K},

(138)

Since the adjoints “

” and “

, the above theorem implies that the unitarity in the physical subspace is not destroyed in the extension of Fock’s space process.

To finish this section, we want to say a word about the bilinear form
(\bullet;\bullet)_{K}
. It corresponds to the “undefinite metric” used in Gupta-Bleuler’s quantization procedure. If one adopts it as the “inner product” (and therefore uses pseudo-Hilbert or Krein spaces), the non-transverse states have zero norm, as can be seen from Eq. (
II.5
). This is similar to what happens in instant dynamics, but in that case the metrics is
\text{diag}(+1;-1;-1-1)
, hence one non-transverse mode has negative norm while the other has a positive one. We think that it is very important to show that pseudo-Hilbert spaces are unnecessary in the sense that there is no reason to say that the bilinear form
(\bullet;\bullet)_{K}
is the inner product: The extended Fock’s space is a perfectly defined Hilbert space and the bilinear form
(\bullet;\bullet)_{K}
, which is not an inner product, simply defines a different involution, which coincides with the “
\dagger
” adjoint in the physical subspace. In this sense, both physics and mathematics are put on a solid and safe ground.

III
Ward-Takahashi’s identities for QED

Now we turn to the application of the general results just founded to null-plane QED. The procedure to obtain Ward-Takahashi’s identities is as in the instant dynamics formulation
(
Scharf 2016
)
. Recall
Acevedo and Pimentel 2022b
that the one-point distribution for QED only contains the interaction between the gauge field and the matter fields:

T_{1}(x)=iej^{a}(x)A_{a}(x),

(139)

with
j^{a}(x)
the matter fields current. In that case, the gauge variation of
T_{1}
is equal to

\displaystyle d_{Q}T_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=-ej^{a}(x)\partial_{a}u(x)

\displaystyle=\partial_{a}\left(-ej^{a}(x)u(x)\right)+e\left(\partial_{a}j^{a}(x)\right)u(x),

(140)

which reduces to a divergence if the current
j^{a}
is conserved; in such case, the second term in the above equation is null. Hence: The field-current coupling in CPT holds with the conserved current of the free matter fields. For a fermion field, it is:

j^{a}(x)=\;\bm{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}}\,\mathrel{\overline{\psi}(x)\gamma^{a}\psi(x)}\,\bm{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}}\;.

(141)

The Q-vertex for QED is thus:

T_{1/1}^{a}(x)=iej^{a}(x)u(x)=ie\;\bm{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}}\,\mathrel{\overline{\psi}(x)\gamma^{a}\psi(x)}\,\bm{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}}\;u(x).

(142)

Now we want to explore the consequences of gauge invariance in the higher order terms
T_{n}
. Since there is no selfinteraction term of the radiation field in
T_{1}
, at each point only a gauge field can appear, and the
T_{n}
distribution will have the general form:

V
Conclusions

We have shown an explicit construction of a quantum gauge invariant theory in light-front dynamics, which proves the independence of the physical scattering operator of the gauge terms in the massless gauge field commutation distribution. For such a task it was necessary to extend Fock’s space to contain also non-physical states, not only coming from the non-physical polarization states of the gauge field, but also from massless fermion ghost fields. The scattering operator matrix elements between physical states are then independent of the gauge terms of the commutation distribution whenever the normalization terms in the splitting of the causal distribution are chosen so as to satisfy Ward-Takahashi’s identities. In particular, this imposes a restriction over the one-point distribution
T_{1}
, similarly as classical gauge invariance dictates the form of the Lagrangian interaction density by Utiyama’s minimal coupling prescription.

With the aid of the result just commented, we have shown (see the appendix) the normalizability of the physical scattering matrix of null-plane QED, as well as that no selfinteraction terms for the fields will appear at any order. Additionally, we have calculated electron’s self-energy by utilizing the covariant part of the radiation field commutation distribution, showing by direct comparison the equivalence with instant dynamics. Its normalization has been done by studying its insertions into Compton’s scattering. In that way, a relation between the two undetermined constants which appear in its expression follows from the imposition of the physical value of the mass of the lepton. On the other hand, the physical value of the coupling constant cannot be imposed on the mass shell because of the infrared divergences, hence one imposes a condition at a different normalization point. To show that such a change in the normalization point does not affect the physical value of the electric charge needs a study of the vertex function of null-plane QED, to which the self-energy is tied by Ward-Takahashi’s identities. Such a study of the vertex function, including the derivation of the electron’s gyromagnetic ratio, will be done in the third part of this series.
