A Cyclical Fast Iterative Method for Simulating Reentries in Cardiac Electrophysiology Using an Eikonal-Based Model
Cristian Barrios Espinosa, Jorge Sánchez, Stephanie Appel, Silvia Becker, Jonathan Krauß, Patricia Martínez Díaz, Laura Unger, Marie Houillon, Axel Loewe

Abstract
Background: Computer models for simulating cardiac electrophysiology are valuable tools for research and clinical applications. Traditional reaction-diffusion (RD) models used for these purposes are computationally expensive. While eikonal models offer a faster alternative, they are not well-suited to study cardiac arrhythmias driven by reentrant activity. The present work extends the diffusion-reaction eikonal alternant model (DREAM), incorporating conduction velocity (CV) restitution for simulating complex cardiac arrhythmias. Methods: The DREAM modifies the fast iterative method to model cyclical behavior, dynamic boundary conditions, and frequency-dependent anisotropic CV. Additionally, the model alternates with an approximated RD model, using a detailed ionic model for the reaction term and a triple-Gaussian to approximate the diffusion term. The DREAM and monodomain models were compared, simulating reentries in 2D manifolds with different resolutions. Results: The DREAM produced similar results across all resolutions, while experiments with the monodomain model failed at lower resolutions. CV restitution curves obtained using the DREAM closely approximated those produced by the monodomain simulations. Reentry in 2D slabs yielded similar results in vulnerable window and mean reentry duration for low CV in both models. In the left atrium, most inducing points identified by the DREAM were also present in the high-resolution monodomain model. DREAM’s reentry simulations on meshes with an average edge length of 1600 µm were 40x faster than monodomain simulations at 200 µm. Conclusion: This work establishes the mathematical foundation for using the accelerated DREAM simulation method for cardiac electrophysiology. Cardiac research applications are enabled by a publicly available implementation in the openCARP simulator.

Introduction

Computer models have provided meaningful contributions to better understand the mechanisms of cardiac arrhythmia
Grandi et al. (2019)
;
Trayanova
et al. (2023)
. An emerging application of cardiac modeling are tissue-level simulations to guide treatments such as ablation procedures
Jacquemet (2016)
. Tissue-level simulations can be performed using reaction diffusion or eikonal models
Nagel
et al. (2023)
;
Pernod et al. (2011)
. reaction diffusion models accurately capture the complex interplay between ion channels, cellular, and tissue-level behavior
Nagel
et al. (2023)
;
Bishop and Plank (2011)
. However, they often require significant computing time, even on high performance computing systems
Trayanova
et al. (2023)
. Therefore, these models are hardly compatible with clinical time frames for intraprocedural decision support. Eikonal models are an alternative to investigate cardiac arrhythmias
Pernod et al. (2011)
;
Loewe et al. (2019)
;
Jacquemet (2012)
;
Wallman et al. (2012)
. They can be 3 orders of magnitude faster than reaction diffusion models and thus better suited for use in clinical settings (e.g. planning ablation procedures) or uncertainty quantification
Neic et al. (2017)
;
Niederer
et al. (2019)
.
However, various eikonal-based models encounter distinct challenges that impede their ability to accurately simulate cardiac arrhythmias. These challenges include the absence of repolarization and reactivation phenomena, inadequate representation of conduction velocity and action potential duration restitution (i.e., their dependence on activation frequency), and the utilization of numerical methods unsuitable for anisotropic propagation
Pernod et al. (2011)
;
Neic et al. (2017)
;
Corrado and
Zemzemi (2018)
. While not all eikonal-based models face all these limitations, a model capable of addressing these challenges simultaneously could provide a more suitable tool for studying arrhythmia compatible with clinical time frames.

Numerical solutions for the eikonal equation are computationally inexpensive due to its simple formulation and the low mesh resolution requirements. The simplest formulations of the eikonal model are only capable of simulating one activation per node. Consequently, these versions do not account for recovery or reactivation of the cardiomyocytes
Neic et al. (2017)
;
Pullan et al. (2002)
. This shortcoming hinders the simulation of reentrant activity, which is a major limitation in the context of simulating arrhythmia. To overcome this problem, Pernod et al. modified the fast marching method to allow reactivation of the nodes while solving the eikonal equation
Pernod et al. (2011)
. Later, Gassa et al. extended the method to enable the simulation of rotors
Gassa et al. (2021)
. Nonetheless, when simulating anisotropic propagation in cardiac tissue using single pass methods like the regular fast marching method, numercial errors can arise because the gradient directions of the eikonal equation solution do not align with the characteristic directions (i.e., the optimal trajectories). This discrepancy affects the accurate depiction of anisotropic wave propagation dynamics, particularly in regions where these directions do not lie within the same simplex in the mesh discretization. Further limitations of single pass methods like fast marching method in anisotropic media are addressed in more detail by Sethian and Vladimirsky
Sethian and
Vladimirsky (2003)
. Alternative methods have been proposed to solve the anisotropic eikonal equation such as the fast iterative method, the buffered fast marching method, and the anisotropic fast marching method
Fu et al. (2013)
;
Cristiani (2009)
;
Pernod et al. (2011)
.

Neic et al. used the fast iterative method to develop the reaction eikonal model, which incorporates repolarization by linking the eikonal equation with a detailed ionic model
Neic et al. (2017)
. However, it lacks the capability to simulate reactivation and reentry. Later, Campos et al. employed the reaction eikonal model in virtual induction and treatment of arrhythmias, a method to investigate ventricular tachycardia
Campos et al. (2022)
. While virtual induction and treatment of arrhythmias can identify areas in the heart susceptible to isthmus-dependent reentry, it can only simulate the first reentry cycle and disregards conduction velocity restitution and functional reentry.

Iterative methods to solve the anisotropic eikonal equation are challenging when simulating reactivation and reentry phenomena. There are 2 main factors hinder the accurate simulation of reentries using iterative solution methods. First, activation times can undergo multiple changes while solving the eikonal equation iteratively unlike in single pass methods. Second, activation times and repolarization times, i.e. the time after which a node can be reactivated, have a mutual dependency. The repolarization time of every node in a given activation cycle depends on the effective refractory period and the activation time in the same activation cycle. Similarly, the activation time depends on whether a node has fully recovered from the previous cycle’s activation before the next activation attempt. Managing these conditions becomes intricate when computing the activation time for the next activation cycle while constantly updating the activation time from the previous activation cycle. Another important factor to reproduce physiological reentries is to incorporate conduction velocity restitution. This phenomenon adds additional complexity as conduction velocity becomes dependent on the previous activation time, rather than remaining constant. Therefore, a way to calculate the frequency-dependent conduction velocity in the eikonal model must be included in the method. This can be achieved by taking the diastolic interval of previous activation cycles in the node that is being activated, or by considering the diastolic interval of the neighboring nodes
Corrado and
Zemzemi (2018)
.

This study builds on an initial version of the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model enabling reactivation in anisotropic media through the solution of the eikonal equation using the fast iterative method
Espinosa et al. (2022)
. The diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model introduced a new strategy that alternates between the eikonal and reaction diffusion models. In the proposed update of the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model, a novel approach to conduction velocity restitution ensures a coherent set of conduction velocity, diastolic interval, and activation time values for each revised node during the current activation cycle. This work extends the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model by conduction velocity restitution properties while preserving the model’s other advantages.

Propagation Models

2.1
Monodomain Model

reaction diffusion models faithfully represent the propagation of the electrical wavefront through the cardiac tissue
Tung (1978)
;
Franzone
et al. (2014)
;
Keener and
Sneyd (2009)
. These models are the most detailed because they incorporate more physiological mechanisms than other available models. However, numerical methods used to solve the reaction diffusion equations rely on high resolution meshes, which is the main cause for their high computational cost
Vigmond et al. (2008)
;
Nagel
et al. (2023)
. The most common examples of reaction diffusion models are the bidomain and the monodomain models
Potse et al. (2006)
. In this work, the latter is used as control to benchmark the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model.

Derived from the bidomain equations, the monodomain model assumes equal intracellular and extracellular anisotropy ratios and is, therefore, computationally more efficient. This assumption does not hold true particularly in scenarios such as the simulation of defibrillation, where the dynamics in the extracellular space play a significant role
Kandel (2015)
. However, for the majority of cardiac electrophysiology simulations, the monodomain model proved to be adequate due to its ability to capture various electrophysiological mechanisms accurately, such as source-sink mismatch effects

et al. (2023)
;

2.3
Diffusion Reaction Eikonal Alternant Model (DREAM)

The diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model is a mixed model combining an approximation of the reaction diffusion model and the eikonal model. The goal of this model is to simulate reactivation patterns on meshes with lower resolutions than required for comparable simulations with reaction diffusion models, thereby increasing computational efficiency. The diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model is inspired by the reaction eikonal model
Neic et al. (2017)
and the multi-frontal fast marching method
Pernod et al. (2011)
.
A modified version of the fast iterative method, named cyclical FIM, was implemented to solve the anisotropic eikonal equation allowing reactivations by alternating with the approximated reaction diffusion model (Section
2.3.1
).

2.3.1
Cyclical Fast Iterative Method

The cyclical FIM is called in step
\mathrm{A}_{n}
of the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model algorithm with
n\in\mathbb{N}
representing the number of the cycle. Algorithm
2
describes this cyclical FIM. Some modifications were made to the single-thread fast iterative method to allow alternation between the eikonal and reaction diffusion modelss. Firstly, the boundary conditions for the eikonal equation need to be dynamically applied as their effect on the system varies depending on the refractory state of the nodes receiving the stimuli. Secondly, list iterations should handle repolarization time and manage multiple activation times per node. Thirdly, the local solver must ensure coherence among the conduction velocity, diastolic interval, and activation time for each node. If
L
is emptied before all the boundary conditions are computed, further aspects must be considered since
t_{\mathrm{min}}
does no longer align with multiples of
\tau_{\mathrm{inc}}
(see Section
2.3.5
).

Dynamic Boundary Conditions

Dynamic boundary conditions handle stimuli applied to specific areas of the tissue. These conditions vary dynamically as they depend on the refractory state of stimulated nodes when stimuli are applied. Unlike conventional eikonal models where boundary conditions are computed a priori irrespective of their timing, the cyclical FIM computes boundary conditions (i.e, stimuli) progressively during the simulation. For this reason, to compute boundary conditions in the cyclical FIM the order in which stimuli are applied must be considered. Let
T_{1}<T_{2}<\ldots<T_{n_{s}}
be the times when stimuli are applied, and let
\Gamma_{1},\ldots,\Gamma_{n_{s}}\subset\Omega_{\mathcal{T}}
be the respective subsets of nodes where this stimuli are applied. At the beginning of the
n
-th cyclical FIM cycle, let
T_{s}
, for
s\in\mathbb{N}
and
1\leq s\leq n_{s}
, be the time of the
s-th
stimulus such that
T_{1},\ldots,T_{s-1}
have already been computed during the previous cyclical FIM cycles. Moreover, assume that
T_{s},\ldots,T_{n_{s}}
have not yet been computed. Therefore,
T_{s}
denotes the time of the earliest stimulus that has yet to be processed. Boundary conditions might be computed in 2 scenarios: at the beginning of each cycle if
L
is empty, or at the beginning of each list iteration if
L
is not empty.

In the first case,
L
can be empty for 2 reasons: the simulation has just started and
n=s=1
(i.e., the first stimulus must be computed during the first cyclical FIM cycle), or all activation times (activation times) associated with activation waves triggered by stimuli applied before
T_{s}
have converged, and
L
was emptied during the
(n-1)
-th cyclical FIM cycle. To incorporate new nodes into
L
and start a new list iteration, the
s
-th stimulus must be computed. In the second case,
L
is not empty and new stimuli might be applied. For instance, if

Coherence Between Conduction Velocity, Diastolic Interval and Activation Time

The local solver for the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model seeks to calculate the activation time for each node. Unlike the standard eikonal model, the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model accounts for the conduction velocity restitution at each node in each activation cycle instead of a fixed conduction velocity. To implement conduction velocity restitution, the function UPDATE() in Algorithm
1
is replaced by a new function COHERENCE() in Algorithm
2
. The conduction velocity restitution is incorporated in the model by providing a template restitution curve, for example calculated from monodomain model simulations or inferred from clinical data
Loewe et al. (2019)
. To integrate this conduction velocity restitution phenomenon into the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model, the feedback loop among activation time, conduction velocity, and diastolic interval must be acknowledged. This feedback loop means that calculating the activation time depends on the conduction velocity, which relies on the diastolic interval, which in turn is influenced by the activation time.

When applying the COHERENCE() function to a given node
X
, the reference conduction velocity of
X
, the repolarization time of
X
from the previous activation, and activation times of the neighboring nodes in the current activation are known. The reference conduction velocity corresponds to the maximum possible conduction velocity, i.e.
v_{\mathrm{l}}(X)
when the diastolic interval is sufficiently long and conduction velocity becomes unaffected by restitution effects. Restitution curves with biphasic behavior, where the maximum conduction velocity is reached at intermediate diastolic intervals instead of long diastolic intervals, are excluded from this method. If an initial activation time is estimated using the highest value of the reference conduction velocity, this activation time will have the smallest (i.e., earliest) possible value, resulting in the shortest possible diastolic interval. If this diastolic interval is short, when applying the restitution curve, this leads to a slower conduction velocity than the reference conduction velocity and therefore in a higher (i.e., later) activation time than the initially estimated activation time. To calculate a potential activation time for
X
, the COHERENCE() function is utilized to iteratively recalculate conduction velocity, diastolic interval, and activation time until a stable state of coherence between these 3 variables is reached. Note that these temporary values of the 3 variables are referring to the current activation of
X
. These 3 values are recalculated in each COHERENCE() iteration (not to be confused with list iteration see Fig.
1
b). Each application of the local solver involves one or more COHERENCE() iterations, starting with the reference conduction velocity and ending with a potential value for
T(X)
to be used in cyclical FIM. Additionally, the term conduction velocity can refer to the speed of the wavefront itself, the longitudinal conduction velocity when the wavefront moves in the preferential cardiomyocyte orientation, or the transversal conduction velocity when the wavefront moves perpendicularly to this orientation. Although the COHERENCE() function utilizes the longitudinal conduction velocity (i.e.,
v_{\mathrm{l}}(X)
) for its computation and equilibrium is reached among activation time, diastolic interval, and longitudinal conduction velocity, this equilibrium also extends to the wavefront’s conduction velocity.

COHERENCE() calculates the potential activation time of
X
using the functions
F
,
G
and
H
. Each of these functions also depends on
X
. To simplify notation,
X
as an argument of these functions will be omitted. Let
F:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}
be the function that computes the activation time from the conduction velocity:

\begin{split}F(v^{*})=t^{*},\end{split}

(10)

where
v^{*}
is a temporary value for
v_{\mathrm{l}}(X)
and
t^{*}
is a temporary solution to the eikonal equation (Eq.
3
) at node
X\in\Omega
providing a potential activation time for this node.
To solve the eikonal equation, Eq.
6
is used to collect all potential candidates for
t^{*}
.
F
is similar to the UPDATE() function of fast iterative method but considers additional conditions. Propagation block can occur in cyclical FIM, implying that

. On the other hand, when taking into account the neighboring nodes

6

Now, let

\begin{split}G(t^{*})=d^{*}=t^{*}-R(X),\end{split}

(11)

where

Finally, let

H(d^{*})=v^{*}=\begin{cases}v_{\mathrm{ref}}\cdot\left(1-\rho\cdot e^{-\frac{\log(\rho)}{\psi}(d^{*}+\kappa)}\right)&\text{if }d^{*}>\theta,\\

A recursive sequence

\begin{split}v_{0}&=v_{\mathrm{ref}},\\

The COHERENCE() function approximates the activation time that corresponds to the conduction velocity value which is the limit of the sequence defined in Eq.

\begin{split}\mathrm{COHERENCE}(X)\approx F(\lim_{n\to\infty}v_{n}).\end{split}

\begin{split}\mathrm{COHERENCE}(X)=F(v_{m})\text{ such that }|F(v_{m})-F(v_{m-1})|<\varepsilon\text{ OR }m=\mu_{\mathrm{CHR}},\end{split}

The proof of convergence of the sequence described in Eq.

2.3.2
Approximation of the Diffusion Current

To allow for
V_{\mathrm{m}}
calculation in coarse meshes, a current
I_{\mathrm{diff}}
was introduced following the idea of the reaction eikonal model
Neic et al. (2017)
.
I_{\mathrm{diff}}
is computed in step
\mathrm{B}_{n}
and approximates
\nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\mathrm{m}}\nabla V_{\mathrm{m}})
in Eq.
1
. A similar approach is used in the eikonal model by Gassa et al.
Gassa et al. (2021)
. In the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model,
I_{\mathrm{diff}}
is defined as a triple Gaussian function:

\nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\mathrm{m}}\nabla V_{\mathrm{m}})\Big|_{X}\approx I_{\mathrm{diff}}(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\alpha_{i}\exp\left(-\frac{(t-T(X)-\beta_{i})^{2}}{\gamma_{i}^{2}}\right),

(16)

where the parameters
\alpha_{i}
,
\beta_{i}
and,
\gamma_{i}
are optimized to reduce the difference between
I_{\mathrm{diff}}
and the diffusion current specific to the used ionic model. A high-resolution mesh (i.e., average edge length 200 µm) was used to run a monodomain simulation in the optimization process. A planar wavefront was simulated by stimulating at the mesh border. The diffusion current was calculated at a node located far from the boundaries and the stimulus location.
I_{\mathrm{diff}}
serves as a trigger for an action potential, regardless of whether the activation is initiated directly by a stimulus or through diffusion from neighboring nodes.

2.3.3
Approximated Reaction Diffusion Model

After the
n
-th cyclical FIM cycle is finished and
I_{\mathrm{diff}}
is computed, the approximated reaction diffusion model will run (i.e, step
\mathrm{C}_{n}
). The time interval for this step is:
[t_{\mathrm{min,init}}-\tau_{\mathrm{s}},\mathrm{min}(t_{\mathrm{min}}-\tau_{\mathrm{s}},t_{\mathrm{end}})]
ensuring that the nodes that trigger an action potential during this step have converged activation times. The diffusion term in the parabolic equation of the reaction diffusion system is replaced by
I_{\mathrm{diff}}
:

\beta C_{\mathrm{m}}\frac{\partial V_{\mathrm{m}}}{\partial t}=I_{\mathrm{diff}}-\beta I_{\mathrm{ion}}(V_{\mathrm{m}},\overrightarrow{\eta}).

(17)

In this case, the diffusion current is approximated by
I_{\mathrm{diff}}
and only the reaction (ODE) part of the reaction diffusion system remains to be solved. Solving this ODE system independently for each node in
\Omega_{\mathcal{T}}
will yield the values for
V_{\mathrm{m}}
within the interval
[t_{\mathrm{min,init}}-\tau_{\mathrm{s}},\mathrm{min}(t_{\mathrm{min}}-\tau_{\mathrm{s}},t_{\mathrm{end}})]
.

2.3.4
Identification of Repolarization Times

Before the
(n+1)
-th cyclical FIM cycle starts, it is important to identify which nodes and when are available for reactivation. Ideally,
V_{\mathrm{m}}
of every activated node would reach its repolarization time during step
\mathrm{C}_{n}
. However, recently activated nodes will not have recovered at the end of this reaction diffusion iteration (i.e., at
t_{\mathrm{min}}-\tau_{\mathrm{s}}
). The aim in step
\mathrm{D}_{n}
is to compute repolarization times of these nodes. To obtain these times, the ODEs of the single cell models are integrated in time until the threshold
-40
mV is crossed:

C_{\mathrm{m}}\frac{\mathrm{d}V_{\mathrm{m}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-I_{\mathrm{ion}}(V_{\mathrm{m}},\overrightarrow{\eta}).

(18)

This process is implemented for each node that was depolarized but not yet repolarized in the previous step.
The initial conditions are defined by the state variables at
t=t_{\mathrm{min}}-\tau_{\mathrm{s}}
for each node. After the repolarization time is calculated,
V_{\mathrm{m}}
and
\overrightarrow{\eta}
are reset to the values they had at
t=t_{\mathrm{min}}-\tau_{\mathrm{s}}
.

Additional considerations are required if

Benchmarking

To assess the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model, it was compared with the monodomain model in 3 numerical experiments.
First, multiple simulations were run on a 2D rectangular tissue mesh, each with 2 consecutive planar wavefronts per pacing cycle length (i.e., interval between wavefronts). The pacing cycle length was varied across simulations to analyze conduction velocity restitution curves.
In the second numercial experiment, functional reentry was examined in absence of structural abnormalities, i.e. their occurrence attributed solely to slow conduction velocitys and short effective refractory periods. These reentries were induced using an S1-S2 protocol in a 2D square mesh. In the third numerical experiment, the pacing at the end of the effective refractory period protocol was used to investigate reentry in a realistic geometry of a left atrium

To facilitate the comparison between different meshes, each node in the lower resolution meshes was mapped to a corresponding node at the same position in the higher resolution meshes. To execute monodomain simulations, tissue conductivities were adjusted to match the conduction velocity values desired for each scenario
Plank et al. (2021)
;
Costa et al. (2013)

In this numerical experiment, 2 planar wavefronts were generated for each pacing cycle length by stimulating the left side of the mesh with different frequencies. For monodomain simulations, the stimulus was applied as a transmembrane current density with an amplitude of 300 µA/cm
2
for a duration of 2 ms. For the healthy atrial electrophysiology condition with the ionic model of Courtemanche et al., the pacing cycle length was decreased in 50 ms intervals from 950 ms to 400 ms, then in 10 ms intervals from 390 ms to 320 ms, and finally in 1 ms intervals from 320 ms to 318 ms. The different intervals were chosen to cover critical changes in electrophysiological behavior, as CV and effective refractory period drastically decrease towards the last propagating pacing cycle length. For the ionic models of Bueon-Orovio et al. and Mitchell & Schaeffer, also shorter pacing cycle lengths propagated on the mesh. Therefore, the pacing cycle length was further decreased in 1 ms intervals to 250 ms. In the atrial fibrillation condition, the pacing cycle length was decreased for all ionic models from 950 ms to 200 ms in 50 ms intervals, and from 199 ms to 118 ms in 1 ms intervals. The considered pacing cycle length values varied across conditions due to differences in the restitution curves, requiring a range of pacing cycle length values tailored to each condition.

After the experiments, the conduction velocity restitution curves were determined. To calculate the conduction velocity, the activation times of the mesh points (–15000 µm, 0 µm, 0 µm) and (15000 µm, 0 µm, 0 µm) were used. conduction velocity was normalized over
v_{\mathrm{ref}}
. The effective refractory period was defined as the time difference between activation times and repolarization times from the second activation cycle plus
\theta
from Eq.
12
(i.e., shortest propagating diastolic interval).

3.2
Reentry in 2D Slabs

To evaluate the impact of mesh resolution on reentry properties in both models, a 2D slab geometry was subjected to S1-S2 protocols. The geometry was represented by squared unstructured meshes of size 51.2 mm
\times
51.2 mm
\times
0, with the centroid of the mesh located at (25600 µm, 25600 µm, 0 µm) for each of the 4 resolutions. Preferential cardiomyocyte orientation was set as constant everywhere in the geometry and aligned with the
x
-axis (i.e.,
\overrightarrow{l}=(1,0,0)
). Experiments were conducted for homogeneous reference conduction velocity values of 200, 600 and 1000 mm/s.
The Courtemanche et al. ionic model was pre-paced at 250 ms pacing cycle length for 50 activation cycles.
The tissue was pre-paced 5 times with planar stimuli (S1) from the left border of the slab. Subsequently, a single cross-field stimulus (S2) was applied in the bottom left quadrant at various times to induce reentry. After each simulation, it was assessed whether S2 was applied too early and got completely blocked, applied too late and propagated to all directions without any block, or if unidirectional block occurred as a prerequisite to induce reentry. Reentry duration was defined as the period from S2 application to the last activation time in the simulation.
\Delta S
was defined as the difference between the times when S2 and the last S1 were applied. The vulnerable window duration was determined as the difference between the earliest and latest
\Delta S
values that induced reentry. The sample frequency for
\Delta S
values was 1 ms. The mean and standard deviation of the local reentry cycle length were calculated at 4 nodes, each located at the same coordinates in all 4 mesh resolutions:
P_{\mathrm{left}}
= (12800 µm, 25600 µm, 0 µm),
P_{\mathrm{down}}
= (25600 µm, 12800 µm, 0 µm),
P_{\mathrm{right}}
= (38400 µm, 25600 µm, 0 µm), and
P_{\mathrm{up}}
= (25600 µm, 38400 µm, 0 µm). To calculate the local reentry cycle length, reentries were induced using diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model and monodomain simulations with a reference conduction velocity (conduction velocity) of 200 mm/s and a stimulus interval (
\Delta S
) of 225 ms in the 4 resolutions. The local reentry cycle length was defined as the difference between the activation times corresponding to 2 consecutive activation cycles of the same node after reentry was induced.

3.3
Reentry in the Left Atrium

The pacing at the end of the effective refractory period method
Azzolin
et al. (2021)
was used to induce reentries in a realistic human left atrial geometry from a publicly available dataset
Azzolin et al. (2021)
. This realistic geometry is derived from an instance of a statistical shape model
Nagel et al. (2021)
. Preferential cardiomyocyte orientation was assigned using a rule-based method
Azzolin et al. (2023)
. The endocardial surface was extracted and remeshed at 4 different resolutions to achieve the desired average edge lengths. To benchmark the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model on coarse meshes, the adaptations detailed below were introduced; the remaining parameters were sourced from Azzolin et al.
Azzolin
et al. (2021)
. Modifications were applied equally to both diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model and monodomain experiments. From the coarsest mesh (1600 µm average edge length), 21 points that were evenly spaced approximately 2 cm apart were selected. Since the lower resolution meshes were embedded in the higher resolution ones, each of the 21 points were located at positions where each of the 4 meshes had a corresponding node. The reference conduction velocity was reduced to 200 mm/s, and the conductances of the ionic model were adapted to represent atrial fibrillation to increase the likelihood of inducing reentry without the need for an additional arrhythmogenic substrate. Pre-pacing on single cell and tissue level was performed for 50 and 5 activation cycles, respectively, at a pacing cycle length of 250 ms. From the action potentials triggered by the last tissue pre-pacing, the repolarization time was calculated and the effective refractory period was defined as repolarization time
+\theta
. Afterwards, the tissue was stimulated at the end of the effective refractory period to try to induce reentry at each of the 21 chosen locations. Stimulation comprised all nodes within a 5 mm-radius sphere centered at this location. Stimulations were stopped if a reentry was induced or the maximum number of 4 stimuli per location was reached.

3.4
Computing Time

The simulations were executed on a 2017 iMac, equipped with a 3.5 GHz Quad-Core Intel Core i5 processor and 64 GB 2400 MHz DDR4 memory. Computing times were benchmarked in 2 experiments across 4 different resolutions using Courtemanche et al. as ionic model. In the first experiment, one single planar wavefront was stimulated in each of the 2D slabs described in Section
3.2
. The computing time was measured from the onset of the stimulus until 500 ms of simulated time thereafter. The selected duration encompassed the repolarization phases of all nodes. In the second experiment, a reentry was induced using the S1-S2 protocol presented in Section
3.2
. The computing time was measured starting 200 ms of simulated time after delivering the S2 stimulus and ending 300 ms of simulated time after delivering the S2 stimulus, i.e., in the interval [S2+200 ms, S2+300 ms].

Discussion

5.1
Advantages and Novel Aspects of the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model

The diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model benefits from the consistency of the eikonal model across mesh resolutions. Across all experiments, it was possible to obtain similar results with all tested resolutions. Unlike the monodomain model that requires fine mesh resolutions, hence more computational effort, the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model yields similar results at lower resolutions. This characteristic allows the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model to perform faster simulations.

Most of the eikonal models that have attempted to incorporate reentry phenomena use the fast marching method as a numerical method
Gassa et al. (2021)
;
Pernod et al. (2011)
and implementations are not publicly available. Moreover, the regular fast marching method struggles with anisotropic propagation because this algorithm assumes the characteristic direction to be always colinear with the wavefront gradient, which is not true for anisotropic cases
Sethian and
Vladimirsky (2003)
. To address this limitation, Pernod et al. developed the anisotropic fast marching method, which incorporates an additional “CHANGED” list to enable recursive corrections
Pernod et al. (2011)
. Similarly, Cristiani proposed a similar method, introducing the “BUFFER” list with the same recursive correction concept
Cristiani (2009)
. In his study, he conducted various tests comparing the buffered fast marching method against the regular fast marching method, focusing on anisotropy problems. His findings revealed that the regular fast marching method calculated incorrect solutions with varying degrees of error across different anisotropic scenarios and resolutions. For instance, in an experiment with an anisotropy ratio of approximately 5 on a 2D grid of varying resolutions, the regular fast marching method was evaluated for its accuracy in solving the anisotropic eikonal equation, which in our case corresponds to find activation times. The errors in the solution of the eikonal equation were 40 to 50 times larger compared to those of the buffered fast marching method, with the fast sweeping method, which is a classical iterative method, used as the reference solution
Qian et al. (2007)
.

Another constraint of the regular fast marching method is the inability to simulate reentry and reactivation. Pernod et al. also developed the multifrontal fast marching method, integrating a REFRACTORY list to include reentry
Pernod et al. (2011)
. However, this adaptation omitted the “CHANGED” list essential for managing anisotropic propagation, thereby compromising its efficacy in such scenarios. Although no reason for this omission is provided, it is possible that they faced similar challenges to those motivating this work. Specifically, changes in the solution of the eikonal equation make it difficult to determine definitive activation times for calculating repolarization times. Gassa et al. later incorporated ideas from the multifrontal fast marching method, the Dijkstra’s algorithm and the Mitchell & Schaeffer membrane model, reproducing spiral reentries in the atria
Corrado and
Zemzemi (2018)
;
Gassa et al. (2021)
;
Mitchell and
Schaeffer (2003)
;
Wallman et al. (2012)
. Notably, this adaptation neglected the use of recursive corrections or any other mechanism for better handling of anisotropy, possibly due to similar challenges encountered in Pernod et al.’s work.

The numerical solution of the anisotropic eikonal equation with regular FMM-based methods without recursive corrections does not lose stability under grid refinement
Sethian and
Vladimirsky (2003)
. The error from neglecting recursive corrections can be small, especially in experiments with small geometries, low anisotropy, and homogeneous properties
Sethian and
Vladimirsky (2003)
;
Cristiani (2009)
and errors may be less apparent due to the stability of the solution
Sethian and
Vladimirsky (2003)
. However, arrhythmia simulations of multiple reentry cycles in realistic geometries with heterogeneous properties and high anisotropy may deviate significantly from the viscosity solution. For these complex cases, some of the adaptations made in the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model might be particularly useful.

The fast iterative method is better suited to solve systems with anisotropic conditions
Fu et al. (2013)
. However, the iteration process hinders the implementation of reactivation patterns and reentry. The diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model uses the cyclical FIM framework to manage anisotropic propagation through correction mechanisms. The diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model also addresses reentry by incorporating a “safety margin in time”
\tau_{\mathrm{s}}
, allowing for corrections before calculating repolarization time based on a reliable activation time. This approach could potentially also be applied for merging the anisotropic fast marching method and the multifrontal fast marching method, leveraging the strengths of both methods
Pernod et al. (2011)
. However, the computational cost of buffered fast marching method and the anisotropic fast marching method can exceed that of iterative methods in worst-case scenarios with high anisotropy. This is due to the increase in the “BUFFER” and “CHANGED” list sizes, respectively
Pernod et al. (2011)
;
Cristiani (2009)
. Additionally, an fast iterative method-based algorithm is more favorable because it can be parallelized more easily while maintaining computational density
Capozzoli
et al. (2013)
;
Jeong and Whitaker (2008)
;
Fu et al. (2011)
;
Fu et al. (2013)
. The diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model is the first model to use an iterative method such as cyclical FIM, ionic models and conduction velocity restitution to simulate functional reentry to the best of our knowledge.

Another method that uses the fast iterative method in an alternative approach is the virtual induction and treatment of arrhythmias method. This method employs the reaction eikonal method based on the fast iterative method approach
Campos et al. (2022)
;
Neic et al. (2017)
;
Fu et al. (2013)
. Initially intended for studying ventricular tachycardia, virtual induction and treatment of arrhythmias explores reentries using the fast iterative method. While virtual induction and treatment of arrhythmias relies on scar tissue with an isthmus to simulate anatomical reentry, the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model is capable of simulating reentry in the absence of structural abnormalities, including functional and anatomical reentries. Additionally, the cyclical FIM within the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model framework can simulate multiple activation times per node per reentry, considering conduction velocity restitution and effective refractory period values to analyze reentry maintenance and average cycle length. Conversely, virtual induction and treatment of arrhythmias does not compute multiple activation times per node during reentry. Instead, virtual induction and treatment of arrhythmias calculates 2 types of activation times using the eikonal-based model. The first activation time map identifies isochrone splitting and merging points, revealing isthmus exits where then elements are decoupled to induce unidirectional block for the second activation. This unidirectional block does not consider the electrophysiological properties at that location during repolarization. Subsequently, a second activation time map is calculated for each isthmus exit, stimulating where the elements were decoupled. This second activation time is the only one calculated during reentry. Then, the round-trip time is defined as the time the wavefront takes to go around and reach the isthmus exit again. The round-trip time serves as a surrogate marker for potential ablation targets if its value is longer than 50 ms. One argument for using round-trip time as a surrogate instead of personalized conduction velocity restitution and effective refractory period values is that the latter are rarely available in clinical settings. While most in silico studies do not personalize these variables but rather rely on literature references, some clinical and in silico studies have explored the effect of personalized conduction velocity restitution and effective refractory period values on reentry patterns
Unger et al. (2021)
;
Deng et al. (2017)
;
Corrado et al. (2018)
;
Martínez Díaz
et al. (2024)
. The diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model in contrast can incorporate personalized conduction velocity restitution and effective refractory period values extracted from patient measurements when available by adjusting parameters in the embedded ionic model and the COHERENCE() function.

While implementing
\tau_{\mathrm{s}}
allows to have reentries with multiple activation cycles in iterative methods, it also entails a new challenge successfully addressed by the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model. The cyclical FIM in step
\mathrm{A_{n}}
of the
n
-th diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model cycle and the reaction diffusion portion in step
\mathrm{C}_{n-1}
of the
(n-1)
-th diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model cycle operate within different, non-overlapping time windows. Consequently, in scenarios such as reentry, it is common for the activation time of a node to be calculated in step
\mathrm{A}_{n}
without the previous repolarization time for that node having been calculated by the reaction diffusion model during step
\mathrm{C}_{n-1}
, as required by the cyclical FIM. The diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model overcomes this limitation in step
\mathrm{D}_{n-1}
by briefly running the ionic model on the activated nodes until the voltage threshold is reached. This enables the effective refractory period restitution to be directly obtained from the ionic model instead of relying on provided phenomenological curves
Loewe et al. (2019)
;
Pernod et al. (2011)
;
Serra et al. (2022)
.

This study also demonstrated how the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model could adapt to different ionic models by adjusting the corresponding parameters. This adaptability allows for the selection of the most suitable ionic model for a specific research question, depending on the required levels of computational speed and physiological detail. For instance, the Courtemanche et al. model is ideal when detailed biophysical information is needed, although it comes at the cost of computational speed
Courtemanche
et al. (1998)
. In contrast, the simplified Bueno-Orovio et al. model, which includes the main ionic currents that modify the action potential morphology and basic calcium dynamics, offers intermediate complexity with improved computational efficiency
Bueno-Orovio et al. (2008)
. At the other end of the spectrum, the simplified model of Mitchell & Schaeffer combines fast inward and outward currents to represent basic action potential dynamics, providing higher computational speed
Mitchell and
Schaeffer (2003)
.
The diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model was able to reproduce restitution behaviors very similar to the monodomain model when reducing the level of detail in the embedded ionic model for the sake of computational efficiency, as shown in the comparison between the Courtemanche et al. and Bueno-Orovio et al. ionic models. This demonstrates that the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model is flexible enough to be used in various research scenarios where computational resources are a constraint without significantly compromising on accuracy.

Another novel aspect of the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model lies in its approach to incorporate conduction velocity restitution. Previous methods typically involved incorporating the diastolic interval at every node from the previous activation cycle or the diastolic interval of neighboring nodes in the current activation cycle
Loewe et al. (2019)
;
Corrado and
Zemzemi (2018)
. In this study, conduction velocity is calculated using the diastolic interval of the current activation cycle, allowing for better adaptation to sudden changes in activation frequency and diastolic interval. Furthermore, as conduction velocity is assigned per node rather than per element, the minimization of activation time is facilitated using analytical formulas such as Eq.
6
. This eliminates the need for the Dijkstra’s algorithm and additional pathways within the triangles can be considered
Wallman et al. (2012)
. In contrast, Corrado et al. proposed incorporating the diastolic interval within the Dijkstra’s algorithm
Corrado and
Zemzemi (2018)
which considers trajectories solely along the edges.

5.2
Limitations and Future Work

Several attempts have been made to incorporate additional properties such as curvature and diffusion effects into the pure eikonal model
Sermesant et al. (2005)
;
Gander et al. (2023)
;
Colli Franzone
et al. (1990)
;
Keener (1991)
. However, existing eikonal models still struggle to accurately represent complex activation patterns, such as multi-wavelet reentry, which are influenced by high wavefront curvatures, bath loading, wave collisions and other source-sink mismatch effect. These properties are typical of propagation in fibrotic and heterogeneous tissue, a key factor in sustaining reentry
Gander et al. (2023)
. To simulate these complex activation patterns accurately, it is necessary to accurately model diffusion and curvature effects, as well as reactivation and repolarization.

In this work, the approximation of the diffusion current was incorporated as a function that depends solely on the activation times with constant amplitude and duration. This function is unaffected by electrophysiological properties of the surrounding tissue. This limitation implies that the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model at this moment does not consider source-sink mismatch effects in the diffusion current and in the conduction velocity. Most of the differences observed between the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model and monodomain model in the experiments regarding reentries can be attributed to this phenomenon. In the reaction eikonal
+
model described by Neic et al.,
I_{\mathrm{foot}}
(analogous to
I_{\mathrm{diff}}
in the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model) is added to the parabolic portion of the reaction diffusion model. On the other hand, the reaction eikonal
-
model replaces the diffusion term
\nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\mathrm{i}}\nabla V_{\mathrm{m}})
with
I_{\mathrm{foot}}
. By doing this, the reaction eikonal
+
model managed to obtain similar repolarization phases as those obtained in the reaction diffusion models when the tissue had heterogeneous action potential duration. In the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model,
I_{\mathrm{diff}}
is implemented as in the reaction eikonal
-
model, resulting in sharp repolarization gradients that preserve the action potential duration differences between neighboring cells. When implementing the reaction eikonal
+
approach in the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model, one needs to ensure that the reaction diffusion wavefront is never ahead of the eikonal wavefront to prevent major artifacts in the membrane models and their refractory behavior. This is more difficult for complex activation patterns with spatially heterogeneous curvature than for planar or radial excitation spread. For instance, during the repolarization phase, new activations (i.e., not predicted by the eikonal model) occurred when there was a strong gradient between cells with short action potential duration and neighboring cells with longer action potential duration.

While this paper is limited to triangles, the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model algorithm can be executed in tetrahedra by employing a similar approach as described in
Fu et al. (2013)
and by implementing diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model’s adaptations to the tetrahedra equation within the local solver, as performed in Eq.
6
. Nevertheless, further analysis of the computational efficiency is required for diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model simulations in volumetric meshes. While the simulations in this paper were conducted in serial code, parallelization can further enhance the efficiency of the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model. Another potential improvement to decrease the computing time of the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model would be to increase the integration time step when calculating the repolarization time in cases where they were not yet provided by the ionic model. Further improvements could be achieved in calculating the repolarization times. Currently, repolarization times are calculated twice in some nodes: once to provide the eikonal model with the necessary information and a second time when the transmembrane voltage is calculated.

In this work, atrial cell models and conduction velocity values were tested in the context of atrial fibrillation. Nonetheless, other atrial or ventricular models can also be used with the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model as long as the parameters of conduction velocity restitution and action potential properties are tuned accordingly. Investigating the tissue effects of additional changes in ionic models, such as pharmacological effects or channel mutations, represents another valuable area for exploration. On the other hand, the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model with the embedded ionic model of Mitchell & Schaeffer demonstrated challenges in reproducing the restitution behavior at short pacing cycle lengths observed in the corresponding monodomain model. Therefore, a better adjustment of the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model parameters is required to further improve the faithful representation of conduction velocity restitution curve and simulation of reentrant scenarios.

More systematic analysis of the new parameters introduced in the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model is required to further understand the optimal tuning that allows for a good balance between accuracy and computational efficiency. We expect that
\tau_{\mathrm{s}}
should increase when the anisotropy ratio increases as more changes are required. On the other hand,
\tau_{\mathrm{inc}}
must be smaller than the sum of the longest possible action potential duration and the minimum diastolic interval, i.e, parameter
\theta
in Eq.
12
. Moreover, further investigation of the COHERENCE() function could enhance the approximation of conduction velocity restitution, particularly near propagation failure.

The cyclical FIM embedded in the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model could potentially also be used as an alternative method to simulate other applications of reaction diffusion models. In this case, changing the ionic model according to the reaction part of the potential application would be necessary. Potential use cases include the reaction diffusion model simulating cyclical phenomena like the Belousov-Zhabotinsky reaction, which can also exhibit spiral propagation patterns
Keener and Tyson (1986)
.

5.3
Conclusion

The diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model presents several advancements in simulating cardiac arrhythmias compared to existing eikonal-based models while retaining their main advantages and making it accessible as part of the openCARP simulator
Plank et al. (2021)
. By inheriting consistency across mesh resolutions from the eikonal model, the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model achieves faster computing times compared to reaction diffusion models for a given desired accuracy. Additionally, the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model faithfully represents functional reentry without the need for structural abnormalities like scar tissue. The use of cyclical FIM enables multiple activation times per node, allowing for better analysis of reentry patterns in anisotropic media. Moreover, the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model permits modification of conduction velocity restitution and effective refractory periods, enhancing the personalization of cardiac computer models. Ongoing work aims to overcome limitations in representing source-sink balance. Overall, the diffusion reaction eikonal alternant model offers promising prospects for advancing our understanding and improving treatments of cardiac arrhythmias compatible with clinical time frames. Finally, the cyclical FIM may find applications beyond cardiac modeling in simulating cyclical phenomena.
