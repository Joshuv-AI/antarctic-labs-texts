Assortment Optimization for Conference Goodies with Indifferent Attendees
Fernanda Gutiérrez † and Bernardo Subercaseaux ⋆

Abstract
. Conferences such as FUN with Algorithms routinely buy goodies (e.g., t-shirts, coffee mugs, etc) for their attendees. Often, said goodies come in different types, varying by color or design, and organizers need to decide how many goodies of each type to buy. We study the problem of buying optimal amounts of each type under a simple model of preferences by the attendees: they are indifferent to the types but want to be able to choose between more than one type of goodies at the time of their arrival. The indifference of attendees suggests that the optimal policy is to buy roughly equal amounts for every goodie type. Despite how intuitive this conjecture sounds, we show that this simple model of assortment optimization is quite rich, and even though we make progress towards proving the conjecture (e.g., we succeed when the number of goodie types is 2 2 or 3 3 ), the general case with K K types remains open. We also present asymptotic results and computer simulations, and finally, to motivate further progress, we offer a reward of $ 100 \$100 usd for a full proof.



1.
Introduction

Organizing a good conference can be a challenging task involving a wide variety of sub-problems. Common subroutines of conference organization include scheduling problems concerning the design of a good program, matching problems for assigning reviewers to papers, and even routing problems to provide indications for the attendees to arrive at the conference venue. In this paper we focus on an often overlooked aspect of conference organization, that of buying the right goodies to congratulate attendees.

The conference organizers must buy

How many of each type should the organizers buy?
This is the central question of

For any integers

such that

Another reason to believe in

Context and Related Work

Conjecture

“You have to distribute

Several similar models can be analyzed, and their behavior is often more complicated than the one studied in this article. For more realistic preference models we refer the reado to e.g.,

2.
The simple case,

.

A natural idea when attacking this problem is to consider first the case of two colors of goodies
(K=2)
. This case happens to admit a very simple induction proof.

2.1.
A simple proof by induction

Consider that instead of minimizing the number of unhappy visitors, we maximize the number of happy visitors, which is clearly equivalent.
As
K=2
, we only have
n_{1},n_{2}\geq 0
. Let
h(n_{1},n_{2})
be the expected number of happy visitors starting with the amounts
n_{1},n_{2}
. Now consider the following intuitive idea.

Idea 1
.

As long as the values
n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{K}
are not equal, we can improve (or at least not decrease the objective value) by moving
1
unit from color
\arg\max\{n_{1},\ldots,n_{K}\}
to
\arg\min\{n_{1},\ldots,n_{K}\}
.

We will see that
Idea

1
works in the case
K=2
.
First it is easy to see that we have the following recursive equations for

:

(1)

h(n_{1},0)=h(0,n_{2})=0,

(2)

h(n_{1},n_{2})=1+\frac{h(n_{1}-1,n_{2})}{2}+\frac{h(n_{1},n_{2}-1)}{2},\quad\text{if }n_{1},n_{2}\geq 0.

These recursive equations naturally allow for the following proof by induction.

Lemma 1
.

Let
K=2
, and let
s\coloneqq\min(n_{1},n_{2}),l\coloneqq\max(n_{1},n_{2})
3
3

3

We will use the letter

. If

, then

h(s+1,l)\geq h(s,l+1).

Proof.

By induction on
N=s+l
. The base case is

(with

), where
h(2,2)\geq h(1,3)
can be easily checked using
Equations

1
and
2
. Then, for the inductive case, consider

\displaystyle h(s+1,l)

\displaystyle=1+\frac{h(s,l)}{2}+\frac{h(s+1,l-1)}{2}

\displaystyle\geq 1+\frac{h(s-1,l+1)}{2}+\frac{h(s+1,l-1)}{2},

where the inequality is trivial in case

, and follows from the inductive hypothesis otherwise. Note as well that

as if

then we have equality, and otherwise we can apply the inductive hypothesis. Putting the last two equations together we conclude

h(s+1,l)\geq 1+\frac{h(s-1,l+1)}{2}+\frac{h(s,l)}{2}=h(s,l+1).

∎

Before we can finish the proof for

Definition 2

For any initial assortment

Intuitively,

2.2.
Why does this not scale to any

If we tried to recreate

\sum_{i\in\operatorname{support}(\vec{n})}h(\vec{n}+\vec{e}_{s}-\vec{e}_{i})\geq\sum_{i\in\operatorname{support}(\vec{n})}h(\vec{n}+\vec{e}_{l}-\vec{e}_{i}).

It is now tempting to use induction over each

h\left((\vec{n}-\vec{e}_{i})+\vec{e}_{s}\right)\geq h\left((\vec{n}-\vec{e}_{i})+\vec{e}_{l}\right),\quad\forall i\in\operatorname{support}(\vec{n}).

This issue suggests that the

or yet,
h(2,7,8)>h(3,6,8)

Definition 4

We are now ready to introduce the inductive lemmas:

(1)

Don’t Increase the Lonely Tower
(DILT). Intuitively, if there is a color that starts with more goodies than any other color, then that is the worst color to buy an additional goodie from. In other words, if the goodies from one color are building a
“lonely tower”
, then don’t increase it!

Lemma 6

(DILT)
.

Let

h(l,m+1,s)\geq h(l+1,m,s)\quad\text{and}\quad h(l,m,s+1)\geq h(l+1,m,s).

(2)

Don’t Increase the Twin Towers
(DITT).
Intuitively, if two different colors have both the same amount of goodies and more than the third color, then don’t increase the size of the
twin towers
and buy from the third color instead.

Lemma 7

(DITT)
.

Let
l>s
be non-negative integers. Then

h(l,l,s+1)\geq h(l+1,l,s).

(3)

Dual Transfer to the Poor
(DTP).
Intuitively, if the color with the fewest goodies has way fewer than the other two, then it is convenient to exchange 1 goodie from each of the top 2 colors for 2 goodies of the bottom color.

Let

4.
Asymptotic Bounds and Approximations

The random process studied (i.e., Model
1
) can be seen as a sequence of
K-1

first emptying events
that are analogous to
\tau
, as described next.
Throughout the entire process, there are
K-1
time steps at which a goodie runs out, which we can denote by
\tau,\tau_{2},\tau_{3},\ldots,\tau_{K-1}
. Each
\tau_{i}
is a random variables depending on the initial assortment
n_{1},\ldots,n_{K}
, and moreover, we have that
\tau_{K-1}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{K})=h(n_{1},\ldots,n_{K})
. For an example, imagine an initial assortment
\vec{n}^{(0)}:=(300,300,300,300)
, and that at time
\tau=1000
the first emptying event occurs, leaving the stocks at
\vec{n}^{(1000)}=(24,30,0,14)
. Then, if the next emptying event is at time
\tau_{2}=1039
, leaving the stocks at
\vec{n}^{(1039)}=(x,y,0,0)
, what should we expect
x
and
y
to be?
We should expect that
x\approx 24-\frac{39}{3}
and
y\approx 30-\frac{39}{3}
, as the
39
rounds between
\tau
and
\tau_{2}
should be split roughly equally between goodie types. An even better approximation, given we know the last goodie type emptied at
\tau_{2}
, would be to consider
x\approx 24-\frac{25}{2},y\approx 39-\frac{25}{2}
, as
14
of the
39
rounds must have removed from the last goodie type.
More in general, consider the time elapsed between
\tau_{i}
and
\tau_{i+1}
for some
i
, given there were
K-i
nonzero values
n^{(\tau_{i})}_{j}
at time
\tau_{i}
. Let us define random variable
\Delta_{i}
as
\tau_{i+1}-\tau_{i}
. Then, we would expect each of the
k
type of goodies to decrease roughly as:

(4)

n^{(\tau_{i+1})}_{j}\leftarrow n^{(\tau_{i})}_{j}-\frac{\Delta_{i}}{K-i}.

We can use this idea to approximate the general expected duration of the process
h
based on approximations for the expectations of
\tau
, as we show next.
Consider we have a function
\gamma
that approximates
\mathbb{E}[\tau]
, meaning that

(5)

\mathbb{E}\left[\tau\left(n_{1}^{(0)},\ldots,n_{K}^{(0)}\right)\right]\approx\gamma\left(n_{1}^{(0)},\ldots,n_{K}^{(0)}\right).

Then, by linearity of expections we have

(6)

h\left(n_{1}^{(0)},\ldots,n_{K}^{(0)}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left[\tau\left(n_{1}^{(0)},\ldots,n_{K}^{(0)}\right)\right]+h\left(n_{1}^{(\tau)},\ldots,n_{K}^{(\tau)}\right),

from where we will define a recursive approximation
\hat{h}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{K})
for
h\left(n_{1}^{(0)},\ldots,n_{K}^{(0)}\right)
.
Using notation
\gamma:=\gamma\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{K}\right)
,
S:=\left|\left\{i\mid x_{i}\geq 1\right\}\right|
, and
a\mathbin{\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{.}\cr$-$\cr}}}}b:=\max\{0,a-b\}
, we pose:

\displaystyle\hat{h}\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{K}\right)

\displaystyle=\gamma+\hat{h}\left(x_{1}\mathbin{\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{.}\cr$-$\cr}}}}\frac{\gamma}{S},\ldots,x_{K}\mathbin{\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{.}\cr$-$\cr}}}}\frac{\gamma}{S}\right),

(11)

\displaystyle\hat{h}\left(n_{1}^{(0)},\ldots,n_{K}^{(0)}\right)

\displaystyle\approx h\left(n_{1}^{(0)},\ldots,n_{K}^{(0)}\right).

As a result, obtaining better approximations for
\tau
will result in better approximations for
h
. As illustrated in
Figure

3
, using the approximation
\gamma(x_{1},\ldots,x_{K}):=K\cdot\min\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{K}\}
, we can see that
\hat{h}
is a good approximation for
h
.
We devote the rest of this section to argue that the approximation
\gamma(n_{1},\ldots,n_{K}):=K\cdot\min_{c}n_{c}
is asymptotically correct, by using Chernoff-Hoeffding bounds.

The core idea is showing that the type of goodie
m:=\arg\min_{c}n_{c}
is the most important one to approximate
\mathbb{E}[\tau]
.
As a first step, we will need a lemma stating that types that start with fewer items are more likely to empty first.

Lemma 13
.

Let
(n_{1},\ldots,n_{K})
be an initial assortment, and let
i,j
be indices such that
n_{i}<n_{j}
. Let
p
the random variable defined as the type of goodie (i.e., a number in
\{1,\ldots,K\}
) that is emptied first. Then, for all
t
we have

\Pr[p=i~\,\mathrm{and}\,~\tau\leq t]\geq\Pr[p=j~\,\mathrm{and}\,~\tau\leq t].

Despite it being intuitive, our proof of
Lemma

13
uses some non-trivial calculations and is deferred to
Appendix

A
.

We now give a lower bound for
\mathbb{E}[\tau]
in terms of
n_{m}
and
K
.

Lemma 14
.

For any
K>1
and positive integers
n_{1},\dots,n_{K}
, we have

\mathbb{E}[\tau(n_{1},\dots,n_{K})]\geq(K-2)\cdot n_{m}-\delta,

where
\delta
is a real number such that

0\geq\delta\geq\left(K-2\right)\left(\sqrt{3\ln K\cdot(2n_{m}+3\ln K)}-3\ln K\right).

Proof.

Consider the sequence of independent Bernoulli random variables defined as

A_{n}:=\begin{cases}1&\text{with prob. }1/K\\
0&\text{ with prob.}(K-1)/K\end{cases}

and let
Y_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}
. Thus
Y_{n}
is a finite sum. By the Chernoff-Hoeffding bound we get that for any
d\geq 0
and
\mu=\mathrm{E}[Y_{n}]
, we have

(12)

\Pr[\left|Y_{n}-\mu\right|\geq d]\leq 2\cdot\exp{\left(\frac{-d^{2}}{3\mu}\right)}.

Let
0\leq x\leq K\cdot n_{m}
be a real number we will determine later. We will take
n:=\lfloor K\cdot n_{m}-x\rfloor
. Given that
\mathbb{E}[A_{i}]=\frac{1}{K}
for every
i
, we have
\mu=\mathbb{E}[Y_{n}]=\frac{n}{K}
.
Replacing into
Equation

12
with
d=x/K
, we obtain

(13)

\Pr\left[\left|Y_{n}-\frac{n}{K}\right|\geq\frac{x}{K}\right]\leq 2\cdot\exp\left(\frac{-x^{2}/K^{2}}{\left(\frac{3\lfloor K\cdot n_{m}-x\rfloor}{K}\right)}\right)\leq 2\cdot\exp\left(\frac{-x^{2}}{3K\left(K\cdot n_{m}-x\right)}\right).

On ther other hand, using that
(n+x)/K\leq(K\cdot n_{m}-x+x)/K=n_{m}/K
, we have

(14)

\Pr\left[\left|Y_{n}-\frac{n}{K}\right|\geq\frac{x}{K}\right]\geq\Pr\left[Y_{n}\geq\frac{n}{K}+\frac{x}{K}\right]\geq\Pr[Y_{n}\geq n_{m}].

We now find a value of
x
for which the
RHS
is lower than
\frac{2}{K^{2}}
. This is equivalent to
x
verifying the inequality

(15)

\frac{x^{2}}{3K(K\cdot n_{m}-x)}\geq 2\ln K.

By the quadratic formula, we obtain that the positive solutions are

x\geq K\left(\sqrt{3\ln K\cdot(2n_{m}+3\ln K)}-3\ln K\right).

Then, combining the previous calculation with
Equation

14
, and recalling that
p
is the random variable denoting the type that runs off for the first time, for any value of
x
that satisfies
Equation

15
, we have

\frac{2}{K^{2}}\geq\Pr[Y_{n}\geq n_{m}]\geq\Pr[p=m~\text{and}~\tau\leq n].

Let us consider from now on a fixed value
x^{*}
that satisfies
Equation

15
.

Using the abbreviation
P_{t}^{i}:=\Pr[p=i~\text{and}~\tau\leq t]
. In this notation,
Lemma

13
establishes that
P_{t}^{m}\geq P_{t}^{i}
for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
and
t\in\mathbb{N}
. Now, note that

(16)

\Pr[\tau\leq n]=\sum_{i=1}^{K}P_{n}^{i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{K}P_{n}^{m}=K\cdot P_{n}^{m}\leq K\cdot\frac{2}{K^{2}}=\frac{2}{K},

from where

(17)

1-\frac{2}{K}\leq 1-\Pr[\tau\leq n]=\Pr[\tau>n].

We are now ready to conclude by the following chain of inequalities:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\tau(n_{1},\dots,n_{K})]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}t\cdot\Pr[\tau=t]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\leq n}t\cdot\Pr[\tau=t]+\sum_{t>n}t\cdot\Pr[\tau=t]

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{t>n}t\cdot\Pr[\tau=t]

\displaystyle\geq n\cdot\sum_{t>n}\cdot\Pr[\tau=t]

\displaystyle\geq n\cdot\Pr[\tau>n]

(By
Equation

17
)

\displaystyle\geq n\cdot\left(1-\frac{2}{K}\right)

\displaystyle\geq(K-2)\cdot n_{m}-x^{*}\left(1-\frac{2}{K}\right).

Note that the last inequality holds for any such fixed
x^{*}
, therefore we get

\mathbb{E}[\tau(n_{1},\dots,n_{K})]\geq(K-2)\cdot n_{m}-x^{*}\left(1-\frac{2}{K}\right)=(K-2)\cdot n_{m}-\delta

for all
\delta
such that

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\frac{2}{K}\right)\cdot K\left(\sqrt{3\ln K(2n_{m}+3\ln K)}-3\ln K\right)

\displaystyle=\left(K-2\right)\left(\sqrt{3\ln K\cdot(2n_{m}+3\ln K)}-3\ln K\right).

This concludes the proof.
∎

As a direct corollary of the previous lemma we get

Corollary 15
.

Asymptotically, we have
\mathbb{E}[\tau(n_{1},\ldots,n_{K})]\in\Omega\left(K\cdot n_{m}\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{\ln K}{n_{m}}}\right)\right)
, and thus as long as
\ln K\ll n_{m}
, the bounds are asymptotically tight.

An Algebraic Approach

We now present an algebraic proof that does not quite work for the case
K=2

Banach’s matchbox problem
.

Theorem 16
.

Let
N=n_{1}+n_{2}
be even. Then
\mathbb{E}[u(n_{1},n_{2})]
is minimized when
n_{1}=n_{2}=N/2
.

An algebraic approach to proving
Theorem

16
.

To prove this, we will find an explicit formula for
\mathbb{E}[u(n_{1},n_{2})]
, and then show that the pair
(N/2,N/2)
is a minimizer.
Let
\downarrow_{1}
be the event denoting that all unhappy visitors get goodies of color
1
.
Similarly, let
\downarrow_{2}
be the event when the unhappy visitors get goodies of color
2
.
By the law of total probabilities, we have

(18)

\mathbb{E}[u(n_{1},n_{2})]=\mathbb{E}[u(n_{1},n_{2})\mid\,\downarrow_{1}]\cdot\Pr[\downarrow_{1}]+\mathbb{E}[u(n_{1},n_{2})\mid\,\downarrow_{2}]\cdot\Pr[\downarrow_{2}].

To simplify our notation, let

E_{1}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[u(n_{1},n_{2})\mid\,\downarrow_{1}]\cdot\Pr[\downarrow_{1}]\;\quad;\quad E_{2}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[u(n_{1},n_{2})\mid\,\downarrow_{2}]\cdot\Pr[\downarrow_{2}].

Also, let
\downarrow_{1}^{r}
denote the event when the unhappy visitors get goodies of color
1
and there are exactly
r\geq 1
unhappy visitors.
Note that

(19)

E_{1}=\sum_{r=1}^{n_{1}}r\cdot\Pr[\downarrow_{1}^{r}\mid\;\downarrow_{1}]\cdot\Pr[\downarrow_{1}]=\sum_{r=1}^{n_{1}}r\cdot\Pr[\downarrow_{1}^{r}],

and analogously,

(20)

E_{2}=\sum_{r=1}^{n_{2}}r\cdot\Pr[\downarrow_{2}^{r}].

Now let us compute
\Pr[\downarrow_{1}^{r}]
(the same calculations will hold for
\Pr[\downarrow_{2}^{r}]
). Indeed, for
\downarrow_{1}^{r}
to occur we need that right before the last happy visitor, there are
r
goodies remaining of color
1
and
1
single goodie remaining of color
2
. After that, we simply need the event where the next visitor takes the last goodie of color
2
, which occurs with probability
1/2
. Therefore,

(21)

\Pr[\downarrow_{1}^{r}]={N-r-1\choose n_{1}-r}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{N-r-1}\cdot\frac{1}{2}={N-r-1\choose n_{1}-r}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{N-r},

as we need that exactly
n_{1}-r
visitors among the initial
N-r-1
choose goodie of color
1
, and each such sequence of visitor choices has probability
\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{N-r-1}
.
Now note that by rewriting the combinatorial number in terms of factorial we obtain for
r\geq 1
that

(22)

\Pr[\downarrow_{1}^{r+1}]=\frac{2\cdot(n_{1}-r)}{N-r-1}\Pr[\downarrow_{1}^{r}],

and thus

(N-r-1)\Pr[\downarrow_{1}^{r+1}]=2\cdot(n_{1}-r)\Pr[\downarrow_{1}^{r}],

which in turn gives us

(23)

N\Pr[\downarrow_{1}^{r+1}]-(r+1)\Pr[\downarrow_{1}^{r+1}]=2n_{1}\Pr[\downarrow_{1}^{r}]-2r\Pr[\downarrow_{1}^{r}].

By summing over
Equation

23
from
r=1
to
r=n_{1}
, and using that
\Pr[\downarrow_{1}]=\sum_{r=1}^{n_{1}}\Pr[\downarrow^{r}_{1}]
we obtain

N(\Pr[\downarrow_{1}]-\Pr[\downarrow_{1}^{1}])-(E_{1}-\Pr[\downarrow_{1}^{1}])=2n_{1}\Pr[\downarrow_{1}]-2E_{1},

from where we get

(24)

E_{1}=(2n_{1}-N)\Pr[\downarrow_{1}]+(N-1)\Pr[\downarrow_{1}^{1}],

and analogously,

(25)

E_{2}=(2n_{2}-N)\Pr[\downarrow_{2}]+(N-1)\Pr[\downarrow_{2}^{1}].

Now from
Equation

21
we have that

\Pr[\downarrow_{1}^{1}]={N-2\choose n_{1}-1}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{N-1}={N-2\choose n_{2}-1}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{N-1}=\Pr[\downarrow_{2}^{1}].

Recalling that
\mathbb{E}[c(n_{1},n_{2})]=E_{1}+E_{2}
,
and up adding
Equation

24
and
Equation

25
, we get

(26)

\mathbb{E}[c(n_{1},n_{2})]=(n_{1}-n_{2})(\Pr[\downarrow_{1}]-\Pr[\downarrow_{2}])+(N-1){N-2\choose n_{1}-1}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{N-2}.

Let us take a moment to inspect the previous equation, as it is the crux of our approach;
the term

(n_{1}-n_{2})(\Pr[\downarrow_{1}]-\Pr[\downarrow_{2}])

is clearly
0
when
n_{1}=n_{2}
. Moreover, it is not hard to see that it is always non-negative as we prove next.

Claim 17
.

For all values
n_{1},n_{2}
we have that
(n_{1}-n_{2})(\Pr[\downarrow_{1}]-\Pr[\downarrow_{2}])\geq 0
.

Proof of
Claim

17
.

Assume without loss of generality that
n_{1}\geq n_{2}>0
, and let us see that
\Pr[\downarrow_{1}]\geq\Pr[\downarrow_{2}]
. Indeed, the events
\downarrow_{1}
and
\downarrow_{2}
depend on the values of
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
, so we can write

\Pr[\downarrow_{1}]=\Pr[\downarrow_{1}\mid n_{1},n_{2}]\quad;\quad\Pr[\downarrow_{2}]=\Pr[\downarrow_{2}\mid n_{1},n_{2}].

We will now prove the claim by induction on
N=n_{1}+n_{2}
. The base case
N=2
with
n_{1}=n_{2}
is trivial.
For the inductive case, if
n_{1}=n_{2}
, then the claim holds trivially, so we can safely assume
n_{1}>n_{2}
. Now note that

\Pr[\downarrow_{1}\mid n_{1},n_{2}]=\frac{\Pr[\downarrow_{1}\mid n_{1}-1,n_{2}]}{2}+\frac{\Pr[\downarrow_{1}\mid n_{1},n_{2}-1]}{2},

and as
n_{1}>n_{2}
we have
n_{1}-1\geq n_{2}
, which allows us to use the inductive hypothesis on both terms of the previous equation, thus yielding

\displaystyle\Pr[\downarrow_{1}\mid n_{1},n_{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{\Pr[\downarrow_{1}\mid n_{1}-1,n_{2}]}{2}+\frac{\Pr[\downarrow_{1}\mid n_{1},n_{2}-1]}{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\Pr[\downarrow_{2}\mid n_{1}-1,n_{2}]}{2}+\frac{\Pr[\downarrow_{2}\mid n_{1},n_{2}-1]}{2}

\displaystyle=\Pr[\downarrow_{2}\mid n_{1},n_{2}],

thus concluding the proof.
∎

Concluding Remarks

We have studied
Conjecture

1
, a natural conjecture regarding a very simple model of assortment optimization. Despite our best efforts, we have not succeeded in fully proving the conjecture. Nonetheless, we have provided several results representing partial steps toward a full proof.
Theorem

16
proved the conjecture for
K=2
and
Theorem

12
proved the conjecture for
K=3
. Both proofs are based on
Idea

1
, and proceed by induction. A full proof by induction seems challenging, as explained in
Section

2
. We believe the proof of
Theorem

12
could be expanded to
K=4
with significant effort by designing an even more sophisticated induction scheme, but we currently do not know how such an approach could be generalized enough to proof
Conjecture

1
for all values of
K
at once.
Then, in
Section

4
, we have presented asymptotic bounds for the time at which a type of goodies runs out for the first time, which can be used to provide bounds for
\mathbb{E}[u]
. We evaluated our bounds computationally in
Section

5
.

On the other hand,
Section

6
presented an inconclusive algebraic approach for proving the conjecture for
K=2
. With significant more effort, similar but more complicated equations can be derived for
K=3
, but once again we do not know how such an approach could be extended for general
K
.
We hope a future reader of this article might put
Conjecture

1
to an end, and in order to motivate progress, we offer a reward of
100
US dollars to the first person or group that proves or refutes the conjecture, in the style of greater mathematicians like Paul Erdős, Donald Knuth or Ronald Graham.

Proof of
Lemma

5
.

Wald’s lemma establishes that if
X_{n}
is a countable sequence of random variables over
\mathbb{R}
with finite mean, and
\tau
is a random variable taking values over
\mathbb{N}
, where for every natural
n
it holds that

\mathbb{E}_{X_{n},\tau}\left[X_{n}\mathbf{1}_{\tau>n}\right]=\mathbb{E}[X_{n}]\cdot\Pr_{\tau}[\tau>n],

and the infinite series satisfies

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}_{X_{n},\tau}\left[|X_{n}|\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\tau>n}\right]<\infty,

then

\mathbb{E}_{X_{n},\tau}\left[\sum_{n=1}^{\tau}X_{n}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\tau}\left[\sum_{n=1}^{\tau}\mathbb{E}[X_{n}]\right].

In our case, let us define
K
different sequences of random variables
X^{(1)},\ldots,X^{(K)}
, where each
X^{(c)}
is sequence of i.i.d Bernoulli distributions of parameter
p=1/K
, corresponding to whether a goodie of color
c
gets taken, assuming there is an infinite supply of every color.
It is clear that each
X^{(c)}_{n}
has mean
1/K
. Now let
\tau
be exactly the smallest
j
such that for some
c
we have

\sum_{n=1}^{j}X^{(c)}_{n}=n_{c},

Then, note that for every
c\in[K]
we have

\mathbb{E}_{X_{n},\tau}\left[X^{(c)}_{n}\mathbf{1}_{\tau>n}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[X^{(c)}_{n}\right]\cdot\Pr_{\tau}[\tau>n],

even though
\mathbf{1}_{\tau>n}
depends on the
X^{(c)}_{n}
variables, as by total probabilities we have

\mathbb{E}_{X_{n},\tau}\left[X^{(c)}_{n}\mathbf{1}_{\tau>n}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{X_{n}}\left[X^{(c)}_{n}\mathbf{1}_{\tau>n}\mid\mathbf{1}_{\tau>n}\right]\Pr_{\tau}[\tau>n]+\mathbb{E}_{X_{n}}\left[X^{(c)}_{n}\mathbf{1}_{\tau>n}\mid\mathbf{1}_{\tau\leq n}\right]\Pr_{\tau}[\tau\leq n],

but

\mathbb{E}_{X_{n}}\left[X^{(c)}_{n}\mathbf{1}_{\tau>n}\mid\mathbf{1}_{\tau>n}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{X_{n}}\left[X^{(c)}_{n}\right]\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbb{E}_{X_{n}}\left[X^{(c)}_{n}\mathbf{1}_{\tau>n}\mid\mathbf{1}_{\tau\leq n}\right]=0,

from where the condition is satisfied. Given that
\tau\leq N\leq n_{1}+\ldots+n_{K}
, we trivially have that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}_{X_{n},\tau}\left[|X^{(c)}_{n}|\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\tau>n}\right]<\infty,

from where we obtain that

\sum_{c=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{X_{n},\tau}\left[\sum_{n=1}^{\tau}X^{(c)}_{n}\right]=\sum_{c=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{\tau}\left[\sum_{n=1}^{\tau}\mathbb{E}\left[X^{(c)}_{n}\right]\right]=\sum_{c=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{\tau}\left[\tau/K\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\tau}[\tau]=g(n_{1},\ldots,n_{K}).

Let
S_{c}=\sum_{n=1}^{\tau}X^{(c)}_{n}
be the total number of goodies of color
c
taken right after the first pile gets emptied (that is, within the first
\tau
rounds). Then, given that
S_{c}\leq n_{c}
, we have

\displaystyle E_{X_{n},\tau}\left[S_{c}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X_{n},\tau}\left[\sum_{n=1}^{\tau}X^{(c)}_{n}\right]

(Wald’s equation)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\tau}\left[\sum_{n=1}^{\tau}\mathbb{E}\left[X^{(c)}_{n}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\tau}\left[\tau/K\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\tau(n_{1},\ldots,n_{K})]/K.

and given that
n_{c}\geq S_{c}
, we have

n_{c}\geq E_{X_{n},\tau}\left[S_{c}\right]=\mathbb{E}[\tau(n_{1},\ldots,n_{K})]/K.

As we have
\mathbb{E}[\tau(n_{1},\ldots,n_{K})]\leq K\cdot n_{c}
for every
c
, we indeed conclude that

\mathbb{E}[\tau(n_{1},\ldots,n_{K})]\leq K\cdot\min_{c}n_{c}.\qed

. In particular, the proof is by induction over
n_{i}

n_{1}
while identifying
n_{j}
with
n_{2}

n_{1}=1

Lemma 18
.

(1 versus
n_{2}
)
For any
K>2
and positive integers
n_{3},\dots,n_{K}
. Let
n_{1}=1
,
n_{2}>1
,
p
the random variable defined as the index in
\{1,\ldots,K\}
of the pile which is first emptied and
\tau
the random variable defined as the amount of rounds until the first pile gets emptied. Then,

\Pr[p=1\mid\tau=t]\geq\Pr[p=2\mid\tau=t]

for all
t
.

Proof.

Note that if
t<n_{2}
then
\Pr[p=2\mid\tau=t]=0
, and hence the lemma holds. We therefore focus on
t\geq n_{2}
from now on. Let
h_{i}(m_{1},\dots,m_{j})
denote the number of ways of coloring
i
objects using
j
colors such that the amount of objects getting color
c
is less than
m_{c}
. Note now that we can use the
h
function to understand our problem as follows: each round until
t
corresponds to an object, and the color
c(t)
of round
t
corresponds to which pile gets an item removed in that round.
This way
h_{t}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{K})
is the number of possible sequences for the first
t
rounds. But how many of those have
p=i
and
\tau=t
? To answer this, let
\overline{n_{i}}
denote the sequence
(n_{1},\ldots,n_{i-1},n_{i+1},\ldots,n_{K})
, or in other words, all the pile numbers except for
n_{i}
. Then, there are exactly

c_{i}=h_{t-n_{i}}(\overline{n_{i}})\cdot{t-1\choose n_{i}-1}

such sequences, we need to choose where the
n_{i}-1
will occur within the first
t-1
rounds (note that the
t
-th round removes from pile
i
by definition), and make sure through the
h
function that no other pile has emptied.
Consequently, the total number of sequences with
\tau=t
is simply

C=\sum_{i=1}^{K}c_{i}.

Therefore, we have

(27)

\Pr[p=1\mid\tau=t]=\frac{c_{1}}{C}=\frac{h_{t-1}(n_{2},n_{3},\dots,n_{K})}{C}

(28)

\Pr[p=2\mid\tau=t]=\frac{c_{2}}{C}=h_{t-n_{2}}(n_{3},\dots,n_{K})\cdot{t-1\choose n_{2}-1}\cdot\frac{1}{C}.

By the definition of
h_{i}
it follows that

(29)

h_{t-1}(n_{2},n_{3},\dots,n_{K})=\sum_{r=0}^{n_{2}-1}h_{t-1-r}(n_{3},\dots,n_{K})\cdot{t-1\choose r}.

Note that the term with
r=n_{2}-1
in the RHS of the sum is exactly
c_{2}
, and as
h_{i}
is always non-negative, we can conclude that
\Pr[p=1\mid\tau=t]\geq\Pr[p=2\mid\tau=t]
for all
t
.
∎

In order to analyze the general case of comparing piles
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
(i.e.,
n_{1}
not necessarily equal to
1
) we will use a relationship between
h_{i}(m_{1}+x,\dots,m_{j})
and
h_{i}(m_{1},\dots,m_{j})
. Indeed, note that Equation
29
does not depend on the values of
t,n_{2},\dots,n_{K}
, then in general it leads to

\displaystyle h_{i}(m_{1}+x,m_{2},\dots,m_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=0}^{m_{1}+x-1}h_{i-r}(m_{2},\dots,m_{j})\cdot{i\choose r}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=0}^{m_{1}-1}h_{i-r}(m_{2},\dots,m_{j})\cdot{i\choose r}+\sum_{r=0}^{x-1}h_{i-m_{1}-r}(m_{2},\dots,m_{j})\cdot{i\choose m_{1}+r},

for all
x,m_{1},\dots,m_{j}
. By rewriting the first term of the RHS according to Equation
29
, we obtain

(30)

h_{i}(m_{1}+x,m_{2},\dots,m_{j})=h_{i}(m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{j})+\sum_{r=0}^{x-1}h_{i-m_{1}-r}(m_{2},\dots,m_{j})\cdot{i\choose m_{1}+r}.

Lemma 19
.

(
n_{1}
versus
n_{2}
)
The previous lemma holds for all positive numbers
n_{1},n_{2}
such that
n_{1}<n_{2}
.

Proof.

For every positive number
d:=n_{2}-n_{1}
, the proof is by induction on
n_{1}
. The previous lemma makes the base case for all
d
, therefore we now argue that if the lemma holds for
(n_{1},n_{2})
, with
n_{2}-n_{1}=d
, then it must hold for
(n_{1}+1,n_{2}+1)
. Our inductive hypothesis is therefore that
\Pr[p=1\mid\tau=t]\geq\Pr[p=2\mid\tau=t]
for all
t
, which by Equations
27
and
28
, is equivalent to

(31)

h_{t-n_{1}}(n_{2},n_{3},\dots,n_{K})\cdot{t-1\choose n_{1}-1}\geq h_{t-n_{2}}(n_{1},n_{3},\dots,n_{K})\cdot{t-1\choose n_{2}-1},

for all values of
t
.
We now proceed to show that for any
t
it holds that

h_{t-(n_{1}+1)}(n_{2}+1,n_{3},\dots,n_{K})\cdot{t-1\choose n_{1}}\geq h_{t-(n_{2}+1)}(n_{1}+1,n_{3},\dots,n_{K})\cdot{t-1\choose n_{2}}.

In order to improve legibility, we abbreviate “
n_{3},\dots,n_{K}
” as
n_{3}^{K}
. Using the Equation
30
with
x=1
, the inequality to be proven is equivalent to

(32)

\left[h_{t-n_{1}-1}(n_{2},n_{3}^{K})+h_{t-n_{1}-1-n_{2}}(n_{3}^{K})\cdot{t-n_{1}\choose n_{2}}\right]\cdot{t-1\choose n_{1}}\\
\geq\left[h_{t-n_{2}-1}(n_{1},n_{3}^{K})+h_{t-n_{2}-1-n_{1}}(n_{3}^{K})\cdot{t-n_{2}\choose n_{1}}\right]\cdot{t-1\choose n_{2}}.

If we use our inductive hypothesis of Equation
31
for
t-1
, we get

h_{t-1-n_{1}}(n_{2},n_{3}^{K})\cdot{t-2\choose n_{1}-1}\geq h_{t-1-n_{2}}(n_{1},n_{3}^{K})\cdot{t-2\choose n_{2}-1}.

Now, we use this as follows:

\displaystyle h_{t-n_{1}-1}(n_{2},n_{3}^{K})\cdot{t-1\choose n_{1}}

\displaystyle=h_{t-n_{1}-1}(n_{2},n_{3}^{K})\cdot{t-2\choose n_{1}-1}\cdot\frac{t-1}{n_{1}}

(Inductive Hyp.,
n_{1}<n_{2}
)

\displaystyle\geq h_{t-n_{2}-1}(n_{1},n_{3}^{K})\cdot{t-2\choose n_{2}-1}\cdot\frac{t-1}{n_{2}}

(33)

\displaystyle=h_{t-n_{2}-1}(n_{1},n_{3}^{K})\cdot{t-1\choose n_{2}}

From Equation

h_{t-n_{1}-1-n_{2}}(n_{3}^{K})\cdot{t-n_{1}\choose n_{2}}\cdot{t-1\choose n_{1}}\geq h_{t-n_{2}-1-n_{1}}(n_{3}^{K})\cdot{t-n_{2}\choose n_{1}}\cdot{t-1\choose n_{2}},

But

\left[{t-n_{1}\choose n_{2}}\cdot{t-1\choose n_{1}}\right]\div\left[{t-n_{2}\choose n_{1}}\cdot{t-1\choose n_{2}}\right]=\frac{t-n_{1}}{t-n_{2}},

which is greater than

, and thus our lemma is finally proven.
