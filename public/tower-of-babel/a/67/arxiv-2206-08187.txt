Approximating optimization problems in graphs with locational uncertainty
Marin Bougeret

Abstract




Many combinatorial optimization problems can be formulated as the search for a subgraph that satisfies certain properties and minimizes the total weight. We assume here that the vertices correspond to points in a metric space and can take any position in given uncertainty sets. Then, the cost function to be minimized is the sum of the distances for the worst positions of the vertices in their uncertainty sets. We propose two types of polynomial-time approximation algorithms. The first one relies on solving a deterministic counterpart of the problem where the uncertain distances are replaced with maximum pairwise distances.
We study in details the resulting approximation ratio, which depends on the structure of the feasible subgraphs and whether the metric space is Ptolemaic or not. The second algorithm is a fully-polynomial time approximation scheme for the special case of
s-t
paths.

Key words:
robust optimization, approximation algorithms, dynamic programming

Introduction

Given a graph
\mathbb{G}=(\mathbb{V},\mathbb{E})
and a family of feasible subgraphs
\mathcal{G}
of
\mathbb{G}
, many discrete optimization problems amount to find the cheapest subgraph in
\mathcal{G}
. In this paper, we assume that
\mathbb{G}
is a graph embedded into a given metric space
(\mathcal{M},d)
so that every vertex
i\in\mathbb{V}
is associated to a point
u_{i}\in\mathcal{M}
and the weight of edge
\{i,j\}\in\mathbb{E}
is equal to the distance
d(u_{i},u_{j})
. Then, the cost of a subgraph
G\in\mathcal{G}
is defined as the sum of the weights of its edges. Denoting by
E[G]
the set of edges of
G
, the cost of graph
G\in\mathcal{G}
is given by

c(u,G)=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E[G]}d(u_{i},u_{j}),

leading to the combinatorial optimization problem

\min_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E[G]}d(u_{i},u_{j}).

(
\Pi
)

An example of problem

Π

arises in data clustering, where one wishes to partition a given set of data points
\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{M}^{n}
into at most
K
sets so as to minimize the sum of all dissimilarities between points that belong to the same element of the partition. In this example,
\mathbb{G}
is a complete graph, every vertex
i
of
\mathbb{V}
representing one of the data point
u_{i}
, and
\mathcal{G}
consists of all disjoint unions of cliques covering
\mathbb{G}
. Furthermore, the distance
d(u_{i},u_{j})
between any two vertices
i,j\in\mathbb{V}
measures the dissimilarity between the corresponding data points. This dissimilarity may involve Euclidean distances, for instance when comparing numerical values, as well as more ad-hoc distances when comparing ordinal values instead.

Problem

Π

encompasses other applications, such as subway network design or facility location. In the the network design problem, one wishes to find a cheapest feasible subgraph of
\mathbb{G}
connecting a certain number of vertices. Thus,
\mathcal{G}
consists of Steiner trees covering a given set of terminals and the distance used is typically Euclidean
[
14
]
. In facility location problems, set
\mathcal{G}
consists of unions of disjoint stars, the centers and the leaves of the stars respectively representing the facility and the clients. In that example, the distance typically involves shortest paths in an underlying road network
[
20
]
, represented by an auxiliary weighted graph
G_{\mathcal{M}}
, leading to a graph-induced metric space.

More formally, we are interested in the class
\mathcal{S}
of deterministic combinatorial optimization problems that can be formulated as

Π

.
Any
\Pi\in\mathcal{S}
represents a specific problem, such as the shortest path or the minimum spanning tree.
An instance of problem
\Pi
is defined by its input
I=(\mathbb{G},\alpha,u,D)
, where
\mathbb{G}
is an undirected simple graph,
\alpha
contains problem-specific additional inputs,
u\in\mathcal{M}^{n}
denotes the position of the vertices, and
D
is a (symmetric) matrix recording the distance between each pair
u,u^{\prime}\in\bigcup_{i\in\mathbb{V}}\{u_{i}\}
. Thus,
\mathcal{G}
contains the subsets of edges of
\mathbb{G}
that satisfy the constraints specific to
\Pi
for input
I
.
As an example, for the Shortest Path problem (
sp
), the additional input
\alpha=\{s,t\}
consists of the origin and destination vertices, so the objective is to find an
s-t
path
P
minimizing
c(u,P)
.
In the Minimum Spanning Tree problem (
mst
),
\alpha=\emptyset
, and the objective is to find a spanning tree
T
minimizing
c(u,T)
.
Notice that providing the distance matrix explicitly in the input avoids (i) providing
(\mathcal{M},d)
as part of the input, which may not be straightforward in some cases (e.g., if
(\mathcal{M},d)
is a Riemannian manifold), (ii) discussing the complexity of computing the distances which again might involve solving difficult optimization problems.

It has been assumed so far that the position of the vertices are known with precision, which is often not a realistic assumption. In data clustering, uncertainty on the values is common, be it because of a measurement error, or because of a lack of information – in which case the corresponding coordinate is often replaced by the full interval
[
19
]
. Similar uncertainties arise in network design or facility location because the exact location of the stations and facilities must satisfy technical requirements as well as political considerations as local officials are never happy to let their citizens face the inconvenience of heavy civil engineering. In this paper, we address this issue through the lenses of robust optimization and introduce for each vertex a finite set of
{\sigma_{i}}
possible locations
\mathcal{U}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
.
Using the notations
u=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{|V|})
and
\mathcal{U}=\times_{i\in V}\mathcal{U}_{i}
, the general problem considered in this paper can be cast as

\min_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\max_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E[G]}d(u_{i},u_{j}).

(
robust
-
\Pi
)

An instance of
robust
-
\Pi
is given by
I=(\mathbb{G},\alpha,\mathcal{U},\mathcal{D})
where
\mathbb{G}
and
\alpha
are as before,
\mathcal{U}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
contains the possible positions for vertex
i
, and
\mathcal{D}
is a (symmetric) matrix recording the distance between each pair
u,u^{\prime}\in\bigcup_{i\in\mathbb{V}}\mathcal{U}_{i}
.
The objective of
robust
-
\Pi
is to find
G\in\mathcal{G}
that minimizes

c(G)=\max_{u\in\mathcal{U}}c(u,G).

We see that
robust
-
\Pi
is a generalization of
\Pi
as it corresponds to the case where
{\sigma_{i}}=1
for any
i
. We have provided above two examples of metric spaces, namely, the Euclidean ones and those induced by an auxiliary weighted graph
G_{\mathcal{M}}
. Notice that in the former example, the convexity of the distance function implies that considering finite sets
\mathcal{U}_{i}
is equivalent to considering their convex hulls. Hence, our results also apply to the uncertainty polytopes used in recent works on robust optimization involving distance minimization
[
24
]
.

Problem
robust-

Π

is closely related to min-max robust combinatorial optimization pioneered in
[
18
]
. In that framework, one searches for the best solution to a combinatorial optimization problem given that the adversary chooses the worst possible cost vector in a given uncertainty set, see the surveys
[
2
,
8
,
16
]
. The complexity and approximation ratios available for the resulting problems typically depend on the underlying combinatorial optimization problem (herein represented by

Π

) and the structure of the uncertainty set. While some algorithms have been proposed to handle arbitrary finite uncertainty sets (e.g.
[
9
,
15
]
), simpler sets typically benefit from stronger results, such as axis-parallel ellipsoids
[
4
,
21
]
or budget uncertainty sets
[
5
,
6
]
. The latter results have been extended to more general polytopes in
[
22
]
and to problems featuring integer decision variables
[
13
]
. Specific combinatorial optimization problems, such as the shortest path or the spanning tree, have also benefited from stronger results, e.g.
[
1
,
23
]
.

An important specificity of problem
robust-

Π

is that its cost function is in general non-concave, hardening the inner maximization problem.
In fact, we have shown in our companion paper
[
7
]
that computing the cost function
c(G)
of a given subgraph
G\in\mathcal{G}
is in general
\mathcal{NP}
-hard.
We have further shown in that paper that problem
robust-

Π

is
\mathcal{NP}
-hard for the shortest path and the minimum spanning tree, even for simplistic uncertainty sets
\mathcal{U}_{i}
.
That work also proposes exact and approximate solution algorithms based on mixed-integer formulations.
Another paper addressing
robust-

Π

is
[
11
]
, which relies on computational geometry techniques to provide constant-factor approximation algorithms in the special case where

Π

is the traveling salesman problem, meaning that
\mathcal{G}
consists of all Hamiltonian cycles of
\mathbb{G}
. The main result of that paper is a polynomial-time approximation scheme for the special case in which each
\{\mathcal{U}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{U}_{n}\}
is a set of disjoint unit disks in the plane. They also propose an approximation algorithm that amounts to solve a deterministic counterpart of
robust-

Π

where the uncertain distances are replaced by the maximum pairwise distances

d^{max}_{ij}=\max_{u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}_{i},u_{j}\in\mathcal{U}_{j}}d\left(u_{i},u_{j}\right),

for each
(i,j)\in V^{2},i\neq j
.

In Section
2
, we deal with the main purpose of this paper, which is to extend the approximation algorithm based on
d^{max}
and suggested by
[
11
]
to general sets
\mathcal{G}
and metric spaces more general than the Euclidean one.
First, Theorem
2
transfers any approximation ratio known for problem
\Pi\in\mathcal{S}
to
robust
-
\Pi
. We prove that, in general, the transfer involves a multiplicative constant equal to 9 (Theorem
4
).
Then, we dig into smaller multiplicative constants, assuming that
\mathcal{G}
and
(\mathcal{M},d)
satisfy additional assumptions. Regarding
\mathcal{G}
, we obtain smaller constants for special families of graphs such as cycles, stars, trees, and graphs that can be composed as disjoint unions of these graphs. As a special case of our results, we find the constant of 3 for Hamiltonian cycles previously provided by
[
11
]
. Concerning the structure of
(\mathcal{M},d)
, we show that metric spaces that satisfy Ptolemy’s inequality
[
3
]
benefit from stronger results.

We complement these results by Section
3
where we provide a dynamic programming algorithm for the special case where

Π

is the Shortest Path problem, so
\mathcal{G}
consists of all
s-t
paths. The algorithm is then turned into a fully-polynomial time approximation scheme by rounding the input appropriately.

Additional notations and definitions

Given a simple undirected graph
G
,
V[G]
denotes its set of vertices and
E[G]
its set of edges.
When clear from context we use notations
n=|V(G)|
and
m=|E(G)|
, where
|S|
denotes the cardinality of any finite set
S
.
For any
i\in V[G]
, we denote by
N(i)=\{j\in V[G]\mid\{i,j\}\in E[G]\}
the neighborhood of
i
. We say that a graph
G
is a clique if for any two disjoint vertices
i,j
,
\{i,j\}\in E
, and we say that
G
is a star if
G
is a tree of maximum diameter
2
. For any positive integer
k
, we denote
[k]=\{1,\dots,k\}
. The diameter of a set
S\subseteq\mathcal{M}
is given by
\max_{u,v}d(u,v)
.

Algorithm 1

Solving a deterministic counterpart based on some representative location

A natural approach to
robust
-
\Pi
would be to choose a relevant vector
u^{*}
and reduce to the corresponding deterministic problem
\Pi
given by

\min_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E[G]}d\left(u^{*}_{i},u^{*}_{j}\right),

and use any known approximation algorithm for
\Pi

1

Unfortunately, choosing such a representative
u^{*}
is not easy. For instance, a natural choice might be to consider the geometric median of each set, i.e.,
u^{*}=u^{gm}
where

u_{i}^{gm}\in\argmin_{u_{1}\in\mathcal{U}_{i}}\sum_{u_{2}\in\mathcal{U}_{i}}d\left(u_{1},u_{2}\right).

Although the choice of geometric median may appear natural at first glance, the cost of the solution
F^{gm}
returned by Algorithm
1
for

may actually be arbitrarily larger than the optimal solution cost.

Observation 1
.

Let
\Pi\in\mathcal{S}
such that each
G
of
\mathcal{G}
consists of a unique edge. Let
e^{gm}
be the solution returned by Algorithm
1
for
u^{*}=u^{gm}
. The ratio
c(e^{gm})/\mbox{{opt}}
is unbounded, even when
(\mathcal{M},d)
is included in the one-dimensional Euclidean metric space.

Proof.

For any
\epsilon>0
small enough, consider
\mathbb{V}=\{1,2,3\}
and
\mathbb{E}=\{\{1,2\},\{2,3\}\}
with
\mathcal{U}_{1}=\{\epsilon\}
,
\mathcal{U}_{2}=\{0\}
, and
\mathcal{U}_{3}=\{-1,0,1\}
. We have that
u_{1}^{gm}=\epsilon
and
u_{2}^{gm}=u_{3}^{gm}=0
so Algorithm
1
picks edge
\{2,3\}
having a cost of

. In contrast edge
\{1,2\}
has a cost of
\epsilon

1/\epsilon
.
∎

We proceed by using a different approach for constructing the deterministic problem
\Pi{}
. For each
\{i,j\}\in\mathbb{E}
, we define the worst-case distances as
d^{max}_{ij}=\max_{u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}_{i},u_{j}\in\mathcal{U}_{j}}d\left(u_{i},u_{j}\right)
, and for any
G\in\mathcal{G}
, we define similarly the value

c^{max}(G)=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E[G]}d^{max}_{ij}.

Notice that, in general, there is no

such that

for any

and

\mathcal{U}_{j}

Then, rather than choose a particular vector

is indeed a distance.
In particular,
d^{\prime}(\mathcal{U}_{i},\mathcal{U}_{j})=0
means that
\mathcal{U}_{i}=\mathcal{U}_{j}
and that both sets are reduced to a singleton.
Consequently,
\min_{G\in\mathcal{G}}c^{max}(G)=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E[G]}d^{\prime}(\mathcal{U}_{i},\mathcal{U}_{j})
is an instance of
\Pi

2.2
Bounding the approximation ratio on general graphs

We divide our study of
c^{max}(G)/c(G)
for arbitrary graphs
G
into special metric spaces that satisfy an important property, called Ptolemy’s inequality, and metric spaces that do not satisfy this property.

2.2.1
Ptolemaic metric spaces

Let us recall Ptolemy’s inequality (see e.g.
[
3
]
).
A metric space
(\mathcal{M},d)
is
Ptolemaic
if for any four points
A,B,C,D
in
\mathcal{M}
,

d\left(A,C\right)\cdot d\left(B,D\right)\leq d\left(A,B\right)\cdot d\left(C,D\right)+d\left(B,C\right)\cdot d\left(A,D\right).

We consider throughout the section that
(\mathcal{M},d)
is Ptolemaic, which includes for instance Euclidean spaces. A direct consequence of the definition is given by the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

Let
ABC
be a triangle in a Ptolemaic metric space
(\mathcal{M},d)
such that
d\left(B,C\right)\geq\max\{d\left(A,B\right),d\left(A,C\right)\}.
Then, for any
O\in\mathcal{M}
,

d(O,A)\leq d(O,B)+d(O,C).

(1)

Proof.

Using
d\left(B,C\right)\geq\max\{d\left(A,B\right),d\left(A,C\right)\}
and Ptolemy’s inequality, we get

d\left(B,C\right)\cdot d\left(O,A\right)\leq d\left(B,C\right)\cdot d\left(O,B\right)+d\left(B,C\right)\cdot d\left(O,C\right),

and the result follows.
∎

Using the above inequality, we can get a constant bound on the approximation ratio by focusing on the extremities of a diameter of each uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}_{i},i\in\mathbb{V}
. It results in the following ratio.

Theorem 3
.

Let
(\mathcal{M},d)
be a Ptolemaic metric space. Then,
c^{max}(G)\leq 4\>c(G)
.

Proof.

For all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\},
let
[u_{i}^{1},u_{i}^{2}]
be a diameter of
\mathcal{U}_{i},
i.e.,
u_{i}^{1}\in\mathcal{U}_{i},u_{i}^{2}\in\mathcal{U}_{i}
and
d\left(u_{i}^{1},u_{i}^{2}\right)=\diam(\mathcal{U}_{i})
.
Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}_{i}=\{u_{i}^{1},u_{i}^{2}\}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}
be the cross product of the
\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}_{i}
.
Let
\tilde{c}(G)=\max_{u\in\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}}\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E[G]}d(u_{i},u_{j})
.
As
\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
, we get
c(G)\geq\tilde{c}(G)
.
Let us now prove that
\tilde{c}(G)\geq\frac{c^{max}(G)}{4}
.

Define the random variable
\tilde{u}_{i}
taking any value of
\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}_{i}
with equal probability
1/2
.
The worst-case length of the graph is not smaller than its expected edge length, i.e.,

\tilde{c}(G)=\max_{u\in\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}}\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E[G]}d\left(u_{i},u_{j}\right)\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E[G]}d\left(\tilde{u}_{i},\tilde{u}_{j}\right)\right],

where, by linearity of expectation,

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E[G]}d\left(\tilde{u}_{i},\tilde{u}_{j}\right)\right]=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E[G]}\mathbb{E}\left[d\left(\tilde{u}_{i},\tilde{u}_{j}\right)\right].

We then consider some arbitrary edge
\{i,j\}\in E[G]
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[d\left(\tilde{u}_{i},\tilde{u}_{j}\right)\right]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\left(d\left(u_{i}^{1},u_{j}^{1}\right)+d\left(u_{i}^{1},u_{j}^{2}\right)+d\left(u_{i}^{2},u_{j}^{1}\right)+d\left(u_{i}^{2},u_{j}^{2}\right)\right).

Let
\bar{u}_{i}\in\mathcal{U}_{i}
and
\bar{u}_{j}\in\mathcal{U}_{j}
such that
d\left(\bar{u}_{i},\bar{u}_{j}\right)=d^{max}_{ij}
.
As
u_{j}^{1}u_{j}^{2}
is a diameter of
\mathcal{U}_{j}
, we have
d(u_{j}^{1},u_{j}^{2})\geq\max(d(u_{j}^{1},\bar{u}_{j}),d(u_{j}^{2},\bar{u}_{j}))
, and we can apply Lemma
1
twice in triangle
u_{j}^{1}u_{j}^{2}\bar{u}_{j}
to get

\left\{\begin{aligned} d\left(u_{i}^{1},u_{j}^{1}\right)+d\left(u_{i}^{1},u_{j}^{2}\right)&\geq d\left(u_{i}^{1},\bar{u}_{j}\right)\\
d\left(u_{i}^{2},u_{j}^{1}\right)+d\left(u_{i}^{2},u_{j}^{2}\right)&\geq d\left(u_{i}^{2},\bar{u}_{j}\right).\end{aligned}\right.

One last application of the lemma in
u_{i}^{1}u_{i}^{2}\bar{u}_{i}
then yields

d\left(u_{i}^{1},\bar{u}_{j}\right)+d\left(u_{i}^{2},\bar{u}_{j}\right)\geq d\left(\bar{u}_{i},\bar{u}_{j}\right)=d^{max}_{ij}.

Summarizing the above, we get
to
c(G)\geq\tilde{c}(G)\geq\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E[G]}\mathbb{E}\left[d\left(\tilde{u}_{i},\tilde{u}_{j}\right)\right]\geq\frac{1}{4}c^{max}(G)
.
∎

2.2.2
Arbitrary metric spaces

Lemma
1
does not apply to non-Ptolemaic metric spaces, as illustrated in the following example.

Figure 1:
Counter example of Lemma
1
for non-Ptolemaic metric spaces.

Example 1
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a metric space on four points
A,B,C,O
, and consider a distance
d
such that for
X,Y\in\mathcal{M},X\neq Y
(see also Figure
1
)

d(X,Y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\mbox{if }\{X,Y\}\subseteq\{A,B,C\}\\
0.5&\mbox{if }\{X,Y\}\in\{\{O,B\},\{O,C\}\}\\
1.5&\mbox{if }\{X,Y\}=\{O,A\}\\
\end{array}\right.,

One readily verifies that
d(O,A)=\frac{3}{2}(d(O,B)+d(O,C))
.

In fact, multiplying the right-hand-side of (
1
) by 3/2, as in Example
1
, is enough for any metric space.

Lemma 2
.

Let
ABC
be a triangle of
\mathcal{M}
such that
d\left(B,C\right)\geq\max\{d\left(A,B\right),d\left(A,C\right)\}.
Then, for any
O\in\mathcal{M}
,

d(O,A)\leq\frac{3}{2}(d(O,B)+d(O,C)).

Proof.

Using
d\left(B,C\right)\geq\max\{d\left(A,B\right),d\left(A,C\right)\}
and applying the triangle inequality at each step, we get

\displaystyle d(O,A)\leq d(O,B)+d\left(A,B\right)\leq d(O,B)+d\left(B,C\right)\leq 2d(O,B)+d(O,C)

\displaystyle d(O,A)\leq d(O,C)+d\left(A,C\right)\leq d(O,C)+d\left(B,C\right)\leq d(O,B)+2d(O,C)

Adding the above two inequalities provides the result.
∎

Using the above result, we can obtain a weaker counterpart of Theorem
3
for non-Ptolemaic metric spaces.

Theorem 4
.

For any metric space
(\mathcal{M},d)
,
c^{max}(G)\leq 9\>c(G)
.

Proof.

The proof follows exactly the approach followed in the proof of Theorem
3
, but we use Lemma
2
instead of Lemma
1
when needed.
We thus use the same notations as in the proof of Theorem
3
.
As
u_{j}^{1}u_{j}^{2}
is a diameter of
\mathcal{U}_{j}
, we have
d(u_{j}^{1},u_{j}^{2})\geq\max(d(u_{j}^{1},\bar{u}_{j}),d(u_{j}^{2},\bar{u}_{j}))
, and we can apply Lemma
2
twice in triangle
u_{j}^{1}u_{j}^{2}\bar{u}_{j}
to get

\left\{\begin{aligned} d\left(u_{i}^{1},u_{j}^{1}\right)+d\left(u_{i}^{1},u_{j}^{2}\right)&\geq\frac{2}{3}d\left(u_{i}^{1},\bar{u}_{j}\right)\\
d\left(u_{i}^{2},u_{j}^{1}\right)+d\left(u_{i}^{2},u_{j}^{2}\right)&\geq\frac{2}{3}d\left(u_{i}^{2},\bar{u}_{j}\right).\end{aligned}\right.

One last application of Lemma
2
in
u_{i}^{1}u_{i}^{2}\bar{u}_{i}
then yields

d\left(u_{i}^{1},\bar{u}_{j}\right)+d\left(u_{i}^{2},\bar{u}_{j}\right)\geq\frac{2}{3}d\left(\bar{u}_{i},\bar{u}_{j}\right)=\frac{2}{3}d^{max}_{ij}.

Summarizing the above, we get
to

c(G)\geq\tilde{c}(G)\geq\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E[G]}\mathbb{E}\left[d\left(\tilde{u}_{i},\tilde{u}_{j}\right)\right]\geq\frac{1}{4}\frac{4}{9}c^{max}(G)=\frac{c^{max}(G)}{9}.

∎

2.3
Bounding the approximation ratio on specific structures

In what follows, we assume that the structure of the subragph induced by
G
can be leveraged to obtain stronger bounds than in the previous section.
We first describe how graph decomposition can be used to obtain such bounds.
We then address the special graphs that have been singled out in our introductory applications, namely: paths, cycles, trees (subway network design), cliques (clustering), and stars (facility location). Unless stated otherwise, we assume throughout the section that
(\mathcal{M},d)
is an arbitrary metric space, non-necessarily Ptolemaic.

2.3.1
Building blocks

We study below how the bounds obtained for distinct subgraphs can be combined to obtain a bound on their union.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a graph,
G_{t}
be a subgraph of
\mathbb{G}
, and
\rho_{t}\geq 1
such that
c^{max}(G_{t})\leq\rho_{t}\>c(G_{t})
for each
t=1,\ldots,T
. Then:

•

c^{max}\left(\cup_{t=1}^{T}G_{t}\right)\leq T\times\max\limits_{t=1,\ldots,T}\rho_{t}\>c\left(\cup_{t=1}^{T}G_{t}\right)
, and

•

c^{max}(\cup_{t=1}^{T}G_{t})\leq\max\limits_{t=1,\ldots,T}\rho_{t}\>c(\cup_{t=1}^{T}G_{t})
if, in addition,
G_{t}\cap G_{t^{\prime}}=\emptyset
for each
t\neq t^{\prime}\in[T]
.

Proof.

Let
G=\cup_{t=1}^{T}G_{t}
.
In the first case we have

\displaystyle T\cdot c(G)\geq\sum_{t=1}^{T}c(G_{t})\geq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{\rho_{t}}c^{max}(G_{t})\geq\frac{c^{max}(G)}{\max\limits_{t=1,\ldots,T}\rho_{t}}.

In the second case we have

\displaystyle c(G)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}c(G_{t})\geq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{\rho_{t}}c^{max}(G_{t})\geq\frac{c^{max}(G)}{\max\limits_{t=1,\ldots,T}\rho_{t}}.

∎

The above results are particularly useful when combining sets
G_{t}
having low values of
\rho_{t}
. The simplest example of such a set is a single edge.

Observation 2
.

For any
e\in E[G]
,
c^{max}(e)=c(e)
.

From the above observation and Proposition
1
, we obtain immediately that matchings also satisfy the equality.

Corollary 1
.

For any matching
G
,
c^{max}(G)=c(G)
.

Matchings can be further combined to obtain general bounds that depend on the characteristics of
G
.
In the remainder,
\chi(G)
denotes the edge chromatic number of
G
and
\Delta(G)
denotes its maximum degree.

Corollary 2
.

c^{max}(G)\leq\chi(G)\>c(G)
.

Proof.

We can cover
G
by
\chi(G)
disjoint matchings.
∎

2.3.2
Graphs with small maximum degree

Recall that Vizing’s theorem states that
\chi(G)\leq\Delta(G)+1
. Combining this with Corollary
2
implies that
c^{max}(G)\leq(\Delta(G)+1)c(G)
. Actually, the bound can be decreased to
\Delta(G)
, as stated below.

Proposition 2
.

For any graph
G
,
c^{max}(G)\leq\Delta(G)\>c(G)
.

Proof.

We follow the proof of Theorem
3
(and re-use same notations), but using different sets
\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}_{i}
.
For any edge
\{i,j\}
of
G
, define
u^{j}_{i}\in\mathcal{U}_{i}
and
u^{i}_{j}\in\mathcal{U}_{j}
such that
d\left(u^{i}_{j},u^{j}_{i}\right)=d^{max}_{ij}
.
For any vertex
i
, let
\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}_{i}=\{u_{i}^{\ell},\ell\in N(i)\}
, and
\Delta_{i}=|N(i)|
be the degree of
i
.
Let
\tilde{c}(G)=\max_{u\in\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}}\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E[G]}d\left(u_{i},u_{j}\right)
.
As
\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
, we know that
c(G)\geq\tilde{c}(G)
.
Let us now prove that
\tilde{c}(G)\geq\frac{c^{max}(G)}{\Delta(G)}
.

For any vertex
i
, consider random variable
\tilde{u}_{i}
taking any value of
\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}_{i}
with equal probability. As in the proof of Theorem
3
, it is enough to lower bound the following quantity for an arbitrary edge
\{i,j\}\in E[G]
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[d\left(\tilde{u}_{i},\tilde{u}_{j}\right)\right]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Delta_{i}\Delta_{j}}\left(\sum\limits_{\ell_{1}\in N(i),\ell_{2}\in N(j)}d\left(u_{i}^{\ell_{1}},u_{j}^{\ell_{2}}\right)\right).

Suppose without loss of generality that
\Delta_{j}\leq\Delta_{i}
and let
X_{i}=N(i)\setminus\{j\}
and
X_{j}=N(j)\setminus\{i\}
(
X_{i}
or
X_{j}
may be empty).
Let us define an arbitrary injective mapping
\phi:X_{j}\rightarrow X_{i}
, so that

\sum\limits_{\ell_{1}\in X_{i}}\sum_{\ell_{2}\in X_{j}}d\left(u_{i}^{\ell_{1}},u_{j}^{\ell_{2}}\right)\geq\sum_{\ell_{1}\in X_{j}}\sum_{\ell_{2}\in X_{j}}d\left(u_{i}^{\phi(\ell_{1})},u_{j}^{\ell_{2}}\right)\geq\sum_{\ell_{2}\in X_{j}}d\left(u_{i}^{\phi(\ell_{2})},u_{j}^{\ell_{2}}\right).

(2)

Using (
2
), we have

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\ell_{1}\in N(i)\ell_{2}\in N(j)}d\left(u_{i}^{\ell_{1}},u_{j}^{\ell_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq d^{max}_{ij}+\sum\limits_{\ell\in X_{j}}d\left(u_{i}^{j},u_{j}^{\ell}\right)+\sum\limits_{\ell_{1}\in X_{i},\ell_{2}\in X_{j}}d\left(u_{i}^{\ell_{1}},u_{j}^{\ell_{2}}\right)+\sum\limits_{\ell\in X_{i}}d\left(u_{i}^{\ell},u_{j}^{i}\right)

\displaystyle\geq d^{max}_{ij}+\sum\limits_{\ell\in X_{j}}d\left(u_{i}^{j},u_{j}^{\ell}\right)+\sum\limits_{\ell\in X_{j}}d\left(u_{i}^{\phi(\ell)},u_{j}^{\ell}\right)+\sum\limits_{\ell\in X_{i}}d\left(u_{i}^{\ell},u_{j}^{i}\right)

\displaystyle\geq d^{max}_{ij}+\sum\limits_{\ell\in X_{j}}d\left(u_{i}^{j},u_{j}^{\ell}\right)+\sum\limits_{\ell\in X_{j}}d\left(u_{i}^{\phi(\ell)},u_{j}^{\ell}\right)+\sum\limits_{\ell\in X_{j}}d\left(u_{i}^{\phi(\ell)},u_{j}^{i}\right)

\displaystyle=d^{max}_{ij}+\sum\limits_{\ell\in X_{j}}\left(d\left(u_{i}^{j},u_{j}^{\ell}\right)+d\left(u_{i}^{\phi(\ell)},u_{j}^{\ell}\right)+d\left(u_{i}^{\phi(\ell)},u_{j}^{i}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq d^{max}_{ij}+\sum\limits_{\ell\in X_{j}}d^{max}_{ij}=\Delta_{j}d^{max}_{ij}.

We obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[d\left(\tilde{u}_{i},\tilde{u}_{j}\right)\right]\geq\frac{1}{\Delta_{i}\Delta_{j}}\Delta_{j}d^{max}_{ij}=\frac{1}{\Delta_{i}}d^{max}_{ij}\geq\frac{1}{\Delta(G)}d^{max}_{ij}.

∎

Proposition
2
immediately implies the following Corollary, which had an ad-hoc proof for cycles in
[
11
]
.

Corollary 3
.

Let
G
be a path or a cycle, then
c^{max}(G)\leq 2\>c(G)
.

We verify in the two propositions below that the above bound is tight.

Proposition 3
.

For any path
G
of length at least three, there exists an uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}
such that
c^{max}(G)=2\>c(G)
.

Proof.

We consider a path
G
composed of
n\geq 3
vertices, where
E[G]=\{\{i,i+1\}:i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}.

The vertices are located on a one-dimensional line where
\mathcal{U}_{1}=\{0\}
,
\mathcal{U}_{2}=\{0,1\}
and
\mathcal{U}_{3}=\dots=\mathcal{U}_{n}=\{1\}
.
We have
d^{max}_{12}=d^{max}_{23}=1
and
d^{max}_{i,i+1}=0,\forall i=3,\dots,n-1
, so
c^{max}(G)=2
.
There are only two feasible solutions depending on whether
u_{2}=1
or
u_{2}=0
, and they have the same cost
c(G)=1
.
∎

Proposition 4
.

For any cycle
G
of length at least four, there exists an uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}
such that
c^{max}(G)=2\>c(G)
.

Proof.

We consider a cycle
G
composed of
n\geq 4
vertices, where
E[G]=\{\{i,i+1\}:i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}\cup\{\{n,1\}\}.

The vertices are located on a one-dimensional line where
\mathcal{U}_{1}=\{0\}
,
\mathcal{U}_{2}=\{0,1\}
,
\mathcal{U}_{3}=\{1\}
,
\mathcal{U}_{4}=\{0,1\}
and
\mathcal{U}_{5}=\dots=\mathcal{U}_{n}=\{0\}
if
n\geq 5
.
We verify that
c^{max}(G)=4
, and there are four feasible solutions depending on whether
u_{2}=1
or
u_{2}=0
and
u_{4}=1
or
u_{4}=0
.
These four solutions all have the same cost
c(G)=2
.
∎

The above proposition considers cycles that contain at least four vertices, so one can wonder what happens in the case of smaller cycles. We show next that for cycles that contain only 3 vertices, the bound can be reduced to
3/2
.

Remark 1
.

Consider the 3-cycle
G
.
Applying the triangle inequality three times yields:

\displaystyle c(G)=\max_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\left(d(u_{1},u_{2})+d(u_{2},u_{3})+d(u_{1},u_{3})\right)

\displaystyle\geq\max_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\left(2\max\{d(u_{1},u_{2}),d(u_{2},u_{3}),d(u_{1},u_{3})\}\right)

\displaystyle\geq 2\max\{d^{max}_{12},d^{max}_{23},d^{max}_{13}\}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2}{3}c^{max}(G),

so the maximum worst-case factor is bounded by 3/2.
This bound is tight.
To see this, one can look at the case exhibited in the proof of Proposition
3
(
\mathcal{U}_{1}=\{0\}
,
\mathcal{U}_{2}=\{0,1\}
and
\mathcal{U}_{3}=\{1\}
).
For the 3-cycle,
c^{max}(G)=3
and
c(G)=2
.

2.3.3
Cliques

We now turn to the special case where
\mathcal{G}
contains only cliques.
One specificity of a clique
G
is that for any matching
M
of size
\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
, every edge of
G
belongs to a triangle including one edge of
M
.
Applying the triangle inequality repeatedly for a well chosen matching provides the following ratio.

Proposition 5
.

Let
G
be a clique. Then,
c^{max}(G)\leq 2\>c(G)
.

Proof.

Recall that
n
denotes
|V[G]|
. It is folklore that
\chi(G)=n-1
when
n
is even, and
\chi(G)=n
when
n
is odd, leading to
\chi(G)\leq n
for any
n
. This implies that
E[G]
can be partitioned into
n
matchings
M_{i}
, each of size
\lfloor n/2\rfloor
, and thus that
c^{max}(G)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c^{max}(M_{i})
. Therefore, there is a matching of
G
, denoted
M^{*}
, such that

c^{max}(M^{*})\geq\frac{1}{n}c^{max}(G).

(3)

Next, we define
u^{*}
as any element from
\argmax\limits_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\sum\limits_{\{i,j\}\in M^{*}}d(u_{i},u_{j})
, and we use the shorter notation
d^{*}_{ij}=d(u^{*}_{i},u^{*}_{j})
, and
d^{*}_{e}=d^{*}_{ij}
for
any edge
e=\{i,j\}
. Observe that because
M^{*}
is a matching

d^{*}_{e}=d^{max}_{e}

(4)

for each
e\in M^{*}
. For any
E^{\prime}\subseteq E[G]
, let
d^{*}(E^{\prime})=\sum_{e\in E^{\prime}}d^{*}_{e}
. Observe that
c(G)\geq d^{*}(G)
.
Our objective is to prove that
d^{*}(G)\geq\frac{1}{2}c^{max}(G)
.

Assume without loss of generality that
M^{*}=\{\{2i-1,2i\}\mid i\in[\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor]\}
, so
n
is the only vertex not belonging to any edge of
M^{*}
when
n
is odd. For any
i\in[\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor]
, let
X(2i-1,2i)=\{\{2i-1,l\}\cup\{2i,l\}\mid l\in V[G]\setminus\{2i-1,2i\}\}
.
As a consequence,
X(2i-1,2i)\cup\{2i-1,2i\}
gathers all the edges of the triangles of
G
including
\{2i-1,2i\}
. Observe that the triangle inequality yields

d^{*}(X(2i-1,2i))=\sum_{l\in V[G]\setminus\{2i-1,2i\}}(d^{*}_{(2i-1)l}+d^{*}_{(2i)l})\geq(n-2)d^{*}_{(2i-1)(2i)}.

(5)

Summing up (
5
) for all
i\in[\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor]
, we obtain

\sum_{i\in[\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor]}d^{*}(X(2i-1,2i))\geq(n-2)d^{*}(M^{*})=(n-2)c^{max}(M^{*}),

where the last equality follows from (
4
). What is more, any edge
e\in E[G]\setminus M^{*}
belongs to at most two sets
X(2i-1,2i)
, so that

2d^{*}(E[G]\setminus M^{*})\geq\sum_{i\in[\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor]}d^{*}(X(2i-1,2i)).

We obtain

2d^{*}(G)=2\bigg[d^{*}((E[G]\setminus M^{*}))+d^{*}(M^{*})\bigg]\geq(n-2)c^{max}(M^{*})+2c^{max}(M^{*})=nc^{max}(M^{*}).

From (
3
), we finally get
c(G)\geq d^{*}(G)\geq\frac{n}{2}c^{max}(M^{*})\geq\frac{1}{2}c^{max}(G)
.
∎

We show below that the above bound is asymptotically tight, even for very simple metric spaces.

Proposition 6
.

If
G
is a
k
-clique, there exists an uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}
such that
c^{max}(G)=\frac{2(k-1)}{k}\>c(G)
if
k
is odd and
c^{max}(G)=\frac{2k}{k+1}\>c(G)
if
k
is even.

Proof.

Let us define
\mathcal{U}_{i}=\{0,1\}
for any
i\in V[G]
, and the distance
d
by
d(x,y)=|x-y|
. Hence,
c^{max}(G)=m
, where
m=|E[G]|=\frac{n(n-1)}{2}
.

On the other hand, we see that computing
c(G)
is equivalent to partitioning
V
into
\{V_{1},V_{2}\}
, and defining
u_{i}=0
if
i\in V_{1}
, and
1
if
i\in V_{2}
. Hence,
c(G)
is equal to the optimal cost of a max-cut for
G
. That cost is equal to
\frac{n^{2}}{4}
and
\frac{(n-1)}{2}\frac{(n+1)}{2}
when
n
is even and odd, respectively, leading in both cases to the claimed ratio.
∎

2.3.4
Stars

In what follows, we consider stars whose center is vertex
1
(meaning that for any
i\neq 1
,
|N(i)|=1
).

Proposition 7
.

Let
G
be a star. Then, assuming that
(\mathcal{M},d)
is a Ptolemaic space,
c^{max}(G)\leq 2\>c(G)
.

Proof.

Let
\{u_{1}^{1},u_{1}^{2}\}
such that
d\left(u_{1}^{1},u_{1}^{2}\right)=\diam(\mathcal{U}_{1})
. Let
\bar{u}_{1}\in\mathcal{U}_{1}
and For all
i\in\{2,\dots,n\}
, let
\bar{u}_{i}\in\mathcal{U}_{i}
such that there exists
\bar{u}_{1}\in\mathcal{U}_{1}
with
d(\bar{u}_{1},\bar{u}_{i})=d^{max}_{1i}
.
We then follow the same approach as in the proof of Theorem
3
: we set
\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{1}=\{u_{1}^{1},u_{1}^{2}\}
,
\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{i}=\{\bar{u}_{i}\},\forall i>1,
and for
i\in V
, we consider the random variables
\tilde{u}_{i}
uniformly distributed on
\mathcal{U}_{i}
.
Since
\{u_{1}^{1},u_{1}^{2}\}
is a diameter of
\mathcal{U}_{1}
, we can apply Lemma

to get

d(u_{1}^{1},\bar{u}_{i})+d(u_{1}^{2},\bar{u}_{i})\geq d(\bar{u}_{1},\bar{u}_{i})=d^{max}_{1i}.

This implies
\mathbb{E}\left[d\left(\tilde{u}_{1},\tilde{u}_{i}\right)\right]\geq\frac{1}{2}d^{max}_{1i}
for any

, and thus the claimed ratio.
∎

Corollary 4
.

Let
G
be a star. Then,
c^{max}(G)\leq 3\>c(G)
.

Proof.

The result is obtained with the exact same proof as Proposition
7
where we apply Lemma
2
instead of Lemma

.
∎

We show below that the bound from Corollary
4
is asymptotically tight.

Proposition 8
.

Let
G
be a star on
n
vertices.
There is an uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}
such that
c^{max}(G)=\frac{3(n-1)}{n+1}\>c(G)
.

Proof.

We consider the uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}=\varprod_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{U}_{i}
where
\mathcal{U}_{1}=\{u_{1}^{2},\dots,u_{1}^{n}\}
and
\mathcal{U}_{i}=\{u_{i}\},i=2,\dots,n,
such that:

•

for all
(i,j)\in\{2,\dots,n\}^{2},i\neq j
,
d(u_{1}^{i},u_{1}^{j})=2/3
,

•

for all
(i,j)\in\{2,\dots,n\}^{2},i\neq j
,
d(u_{i},u_{j})=2/3
,

•

for all
i=2,\dots,n
,
d(u_{1}^{i},u_{i})=1
and
\forall j\neq i
,
d(u_{1}^{j},u_{i})=1/3
.

The triangle inequality is verified, so

is a distance.

By symmetry of the star graph and of the uncertainty set, every solution

is optimal and has the same value
1+\frac{1}{3}(n-2)
.
Moreover, the maximum distance between
\mathcal{U}_{1}
and
\mathcal{U}_{i}
is equal to
1
for all

so

.
As a result,
c^{max}(G)=\frac{3(n-1)}{n+1}c(G)
.
∎

2.3.5
Trees

We conclude our study of specific structures with trees.
Our first result combines the bounds obtained for stars in the previous section with the composition results presented in Section
2.3.1
to improve the ratio of
9

obtained in Theorem

for general graphs and metric spaces.

Proposition 9
.

Let
G
be a tree. Then
c^{max}(G)\leq 6\>c(G)
.

Proof.

Observe first that we can partition

into
E_{1}
and

such that each

induces a star forest (a graph where any connected component is a star).
Indeed, to obtain such a partition we root the tree at vertex
1
, and define

as the star whose central vertex is
i
and whose leaves are the children vertices of
i
in
G
.
Then, we define
E_{1}
(resp.
E_{2}
) as the union of
E(S^{i})
for vertices

which are an odd (resp. even) distance from vertex

.
By Proposition

and Corollary

, we get the claimed ratio.
∎

Next, we show how the bound can be further tightened when considering Euclidean metric spaces and spherical uncertainty sets.

Proposition 10
.

Assume that
\mathcal{M}
is a Euclidean space with
d
the associated Euclidean distance, and assume that for all
i\in V
,
\mathcal{U}_{i}
is a closed ball.
Then, for any tree

c^{max}(G)\leq 2\sqrt{2}\>c(G)
.

Proof.

We assume without loss of generality that
G
is rooted at vertex

.
The proof is made by induction on the height of the tree.
For this, we consider the following induction statement:

{{P}(h)}:

If
G
has height
h
, there are two solutions
u^{1},u^{2}\in\mathcal{U}
such that

•

c^{max}(G)\leq 2\sqrt{2}\>\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E[G]}d(u_{i}^{1},u_{j}^{1})=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E[G]}d(u_{i}^{2},u_{j}^{2})
,

•

u_{i}^{1}=u_{i}^{2}
for each vertex
i
with level
l<h
,

•

[u_{i}^{1},u_{i}^{2}]
is a diameter of
\mathcal{U}_{i}
for each vertex
i
with level
h
.

If
h=0
,
G
has only one vertex which is the root of the tree, so the induction statement is trivially satisfied with any diameter
[u^{1},u^{2}]
of
\mathcal{U}_{1}
.

Assume that
P(h)
is true for some
h\geq 1
, and let
G
be a rooted tree with height
h+1
.
Without loss of generality, we assume that the vertices of
V[G]
are sorted by increasing level and let
n_{h-1}
,
n_{h}
and
n_{h+1}
be such that the vertices with level
h
are
V_{h}=\{n_{h-1}+1,\dots,n_{h}\}
and those with level
h+1
are
V_{h+1}=\{n_{h}+1,\dots,n_{h+1}\}
.
Let
G_{\leq h}=(V_{\leq h},E_{\leq h})
be the subtree of
G
induced by vertices
\{1,\dots,n_{h}\}
.
Tree
G_{\leq h}
has height
h
so we can apply the induction statement to get two solutions
u^{1},u^{2}\in\mathcal{U}
as described in
{P(h)}
.

Now, for all
i\in V_{h}
, let
S_{i}\subset E[G]
be the set of edges of the star graph whose internal vertex is
i
and whose leaves are the children vertices of
i
(which all belong to
V_{h+1}
).
Similarly to what was done in the proof of Proposition
7
, for all
\{i,j\}\in S_{i}
, we can set
u_{j}^{1}\in\argmax_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{j}}d(u,u_{i}^{1})
and
u_{j}^{2}\in\argmax_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{j}}d(u,u_{i}^{2})
, which yields
d(u_{i}^{1},u_{j}^{1})+d(u_{i}^{2},u_{j}^{2})\geq d^{max}_{ij}
.
We can then assume without loss of generality that
\sum_{\{i,j\}\in S_{i}}d(u_{i}^{1},u_{j}^{1})\geq\sum_{\{i,j\}\in S_{i}}d(u_{i}^{2},u_{j}^{2})
, i.e.,

c^{max}(S_{i})\leq 2\sum_{\{i,j\}\in S_{i}}d(u_{i}^{1},u_{j}^{1}).

(6)

Given that we are considering the Euclidean distance with spherical uncertainty sets, the above implies that segment
[u_{i}^{1},u_{j}^{1}]
goes through the center
o_{j}
of
\mathcal{U}_{j}
(direct application of the triangle inequality).
Then, let
[\bar{u}_{j}^{1},\bar{u}_{j}^{2}]
be the diameter of
\mathcal{U}_{j}
that is orthogonal to
[u_{i}^{1},u_{j}^{1}]
; it exists because
\mathcal{U}_{j}
is spherical.
Denoting the radius of
\mathcal{U}_{j}
by
r_{j}
, we then compute

\left\{\begin{aligned} &d(u_{i}^{1},u_{j}^{1})^{2}=d(u_{i}^{1},o_{j})^{2}+r_{j}^{2}+2r_{j}d(u_{i}^{1},o_{j})\leq 2(d(u_{i}^{1},o_{j})^{2}+r_{j}^{2})\\
&d(u_{i}^{1},\bar{u}_{j}^{1})^{2}=d(u_{i}^{1},\bar{u}_{j}^{2})^{2}=d(u_{i}^{1},o_{j})^{2}+r_{j}^{2}\end{aligned}\right.

As a consequence,
d(u_{i}^{1},\bar{u}_{j}^{1})=d(u_{i}^{1},\bar{u}_{j}^{2})\geq 1/\sqrt{2}d(u_{i}^{1},u_{j}^{1})
.
Using (
6
), we then get

c^{max}(S_{i})\leq 2\sqrt{2}\sum_{\{i,j\}\in S_{i}}d(u_{i}^{1},\bar{u}_{j}^{1})=2\sqrt{2}\sum_{\{i,j\}\in S_{i}}d(u_{i}^{1},\bar{u}_{j}^{2}).

The same applies to all
i
, so we build two solutions
\tilde{u}^{1},\tilde{u}^{2}\in\mathcal{U}
such that

•

\tilde{u}_{i}^{1}=\tilde{u}_{i}^{2}=u_{i}^{1}
for all
i\in V_{\leq h}
,

•

[\tilde{u}_{i}^{1},\tilde{u}_{i}^{2}]=[\bar{u}_{i}^{1},\bar{u}_{i}^{2}]
is a diameter of
\mathcal{U}_{i}
for all
i\in V_{h+1}.

To conclude, we observe that
E[G]=E_{\leq h}\cup\left(\bigcup_{i\in V_{h}}S_{i}\right)
, so

\displaystyle\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E[G]}d(\tilde{u}_{i}^{1},\tilde{u}_{j}^{1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E_{\leq h}}d(\tilde{u}_{i}^{1},\tilde{u}_{j}^{1})+\sum_{i\in V_{h}}\sum_{\{i,j\}\in S_{i}}d(\tilde{u}_{i}^{1},\tilde{u}_{j}^{1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E_{\leq h}}d(u_{i}^{1},u_{j}^{1})+\sum_{i\in V_{h}}\sum_{\{i,j\}\in S_{i}}d(u_{i}^{1},\bar{u}_{j}^{1})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}c^{max}(G_{\leq h})+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\sum_{i\in V_{h}}c^{max}(S_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}c^{max}(G).

∎

Remark 2
.

Notice that Proposition
10
does not fit into the framework described so far as the sets
\mathcal{U}_{i}
are infinite. Nevertheless, a finite input for such problems can be defined as
I^{ball}=(\mathbb{G},\alpha,c,r,\ell)
, where
\mathbb{G}
and
\alpha
are as before,

and

denote the center and radii of the balls, respectively, and
\ell
denotes their dimension. Furthermore in such problems, we consider that the distance between any two points in

can be computed in constant time by calling the function
\|u-v\|_{2}
.

3
FPTAS for robust shortest path

Up to now, we have mostly focused on general problems
robust
-
\Pi
and provided constant-factor approximation algorithms. The purpose of this section is to focus on a specific problem,
robust
-
sp

, known to be
\mathcal{NP}
-hard
[
7
]
.
We do this by providing a dynamic programming algorithm for
robust
-
gen-sp

(a generalization of
robust
-
sp

where
d
is not required to verify the triangle inequality) in Section
3.1
, which can also be used on its own when the appropriated parameters have small values. The algorithm is then used in Section
3.2
to derive the
\cal{FPTAS}
.

3.1
Dynamic programming algorithm

In the
robust
-
gen-sp

problem, the input
I=(G,s,t,\mathcal{U},\mathcal{D})
is the same as in the
robust
-
sp

problem, except that
\mathcal{D}
is now a non-negative matrix that is only assumed to be symmetric, e.g.,
\mathcal{D}(u,v)=\mathcal{D}(v,u)
for any
u,v\in\bigcup_{i\in V[G]}\mathcal{U}_{i}
.
In particular, the function associated with
\mathcal{D}
may not respect the triangle inequality. This level of generality will be useful in the next section when deriving the
\cal{FPTAS}
for
robust
-
sp

.

Next, we introduce further notations that are used to derive the dynamic programming algorithm. Let
\mathcal{G}^{i\rightarrow t}
denote the set of all
i-t
simple paths in
G
, and
\mathcal{G}^{i\rightarrow t}_{\kappa}
those having at most
\kappa
edges.
Given
i\in V[G]
and a path
P\in\mathcal{G}^{i\rightarrow t}
, we define the worst-case cost given that
u_{i}=u_{i}^{\ell}
for
\ell\in[{\sigma_{i}}]
(recall that
{\sigma_{i}}=\left\lvert\mathcal{U}_{i}\right\rvert
) as

c^{\ell}(P)=\max\{c(u,P)\mid u\in\mathcal{U},u_{i}=u_{i}^{\ell}\},

and
Pr(P)=(c^{\ell}(P))_{\ell\in[{\sigma_{i}}]}
as the profile of
P
.
We also introduce for any
\kappa\in[n]
the set of profiles of all
i-t
paths with at most
\kappa
edges as
\mathcal{P}^{(i,\kappa)}=\{Pr(P)\mid P\in\mathcal{G}^{i\rightarrow t}_{\kappa}\}
.
We denote

\Val(I)=\{c^{\ell}(P)\mid i\in V[G],P\in\mathcal{G}^{i\rightarrow t},\ell\in[{\sigma_{i}}]\},

n_{val}=|\Val(I)|
, and
n_{\mathcal{P}}=|\bigcup_{i\in V[G],\kappa\in[n]}\mathcal{P}^{(i,\kappa)}|
the total number of different profiles.

Our objective is to define an algorithm
A(i,\kappa)
that, given any
i\in V[G]
and
\kappa\in[n]
computes
(\mathcal{P}^{(i,\kappa)},Q^{(i,\kappa)})
such that

•

Q^{(i,\kappa)}\subseteq\mathcal{G}^{i\rightarrow t}_{\kappa}
,

•

|Q^{(i,\kappa)}|=|\mathcal{P}^{(i,\kappa)}|
,

•

for any
p\in\mathcal{P}^{(i,\kappa)}
, there exists
P\in Q^{(i,\kappa)}
such that
Pr(P)=p
.

Informally,
A(i,\kappa)
computes all profiles associated to
(i,\kappa)
as well as a representative path for each one of these profiles. Let us first verify that computing this is enough to solve
robust
-
gen-sp

optimally.

Lemma 3
.

Given
(\mathcal{P}^{(s,n)},Q^{(s,n)})
, we can find an optimal solution in time polynomial in
n
and linear in
n_{\mathcal{P}}
.

Proof.

For any
p\in\mathcal{P}^{(s,n)}
,
p=(p_{\ell})_{\ell\in[{\sigma_{s}}]}
, let
x_{p}=\max_{\ell\in[{\sigma_{s}}]}p_{\ell}
.
We define

p^{min}=\argmin_{p\in\mathcal{P}^{(s,n)}}x_{p}

and output
P^{min}\in Q^{(s,n)}
such that
Pr(P^{min})=p^{min}
.
Let
P^{*}
be an optimal solution and
p^{*}=Pr(P^{*})
.
As
p^{*}\in\mathcal{P}^{(s,n)}
, we have
c(P^{min})=x_{p^{min}}\leq x_{p^{*}}=c(P^{*})
.
∎

We provide next the dynamic programming recursion for
\mathcal{P}^{(i,\kappa)}
, leaving aside the computation of
Q^{(i,\kappa)}
to simplify the presentation.
Given
i\in V[G]
,
\kappa\in[n]
,
j\in N(i)
,
P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}^{j\rightarrow t}_{\kappa}
, and
p^{\prime}=Pr(P^{\prime})
, we consider the
i-t
path
P=iP^{\prime}
obtained by concatenating
i
with
P^{\prime}
. One readily verifies that
Pr(P)=p(i,\kappa,j,p^{\prime})
, where
p(i,\kappa,j,p^{\prime})=(y_{\ell})_{\ell\in[{\sigma_{i}}]}
, with
y_{\ell}=\max_{\ell^{\prime}\in[{\sigma_{j}}]}d\left(u_{i}^{\ell},u_{j}^{\ell^{\prime}}\right)+p^{\prime}_{\ell^{\prime}}
. We obtain that for any
i\neq s
and
\kappa>0

\mathcal{P}^{(i,\kappa)}=\{p(i,\kappa,j,p^{\prime})\mid j\in N(i),p^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}^{(j,\kappa-1)}\},

(7)

and
\mathcal{P}^{(s,0)}=(0)_{\ell\in[{\sigma_{s}}]}
. Recall that
\sigma=\max_{i\in V[G]}{\sigma_{i}}
. We provide in the next lemma the complexity of the resulting dynamic programming algorithm.

Lemma 4
.

Let
i\in V[G]
,
\kappa\in[n]
. Given
\mathcal{P}^{(j,\kappa-1)}
and
Q^{(j,\kappa-1)}
for any
j\in N(i)
, we can compute

(\mathcal{P}^{(i,\kappa)},Q^{(i,\kappa)})
in time
\mathcal{O}(n\sigma^{2}n_{\mathcal{P}})
and space
\mathcal{O}(n_{\mathcal{P}}n(log(n)))
.

Proof.

For complexity issues we assume that
\mathcal{P}^{(j,\kappa-1)}
and
Q^{(j,\kappa-1)}
are represented as arrays
A_{P}
and
A_{Q}
indexed by profiles,
where given a profile
p
,
A_{P}[p]
is true iff
p\in\mathcal{P}^{(j,\kappa-1)}
, and
A_{Q}[p]
contains a path
P
such that
Pr(P)=p
if
p\in\mathcal{P}^{(j,\kappa-1)}
, and
\emptyset
otherwise.
This explains the
\mathcal{O}(n_{\mathcal{P}}n(log(n)))
required space for storing
Q
.
As profiles are vectors of length at most
\sigma
, we consider that it takes
\mathcal{O}(\sigma)
to obtain the value stored at index
p
of array
A_{P}
or
A_{Q}
.

We compute
\mathcal{P}^{(i,\kappa)}
following the recursion relation (
7
), and compute
Q^{(i,\kappa)}
along the way. More precisely, we start by initializing two arrays
A^{\prime}_{P}
and
A^{\prime}_{Q}
of size
n_{\mathcal{P}}
.
Then, for all
j\in N(i)
and
p^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}^{(j,\kappa-1)}
, we compute
p(i,\kappa,j,p^{\prime})
in time
\mathcal{O}(\sigma^{2})
.
Now, we perform the following operations in
\mathcal{O}(1)
.
If
p(i,\kappa,j,p^{\prime})
is not already in
\mathcal{P}
, we add it to
\mathcal{P}
, we find a path
P^{\prime}
in
Q^{(j,\kappa-1)}
such that
Pr(P^{\prime})=p^{\prime}
, and we add the path
iP^{\prime}
to
Q
.
∎

We are now ready to state the main result of this section.

Theorem 5
.

robust
-
gen-sp

can be solved in time
\mathcal{O}(n^{3}\sigma^{2}n_{\mathcal{P}})
and space
\mathcal{O}(n_{\mathcal{P}}n^{3}log(n))
.

Proof.

We compute
(\mathcal{P}^{(s,n)},Q^{(s,n)})
using a DP algorithm based on (
7
), and obtain an optimal solution following Lemma
3
.
As the total number of entries of the associated memoïzation table has size
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
, we get the claimed complexity.
∎

Let us further elaborate on the value of
n_{\mathcal{P}}
that arises in Theorem
5
. First of all, we see that
n_{\mathcal{P}}\leq(n_{val})^{\sigma}
, leading to the observation below, used in the next section to derive the FPTAS.

Observation 3
.

robust
-
gen-sp

can be solved in time
\mathcal{O}(n^{3}\sigma^{2}(n_{val})^{\sigma})

From a more theoretical viewpoint, notice that the reduction of
[
7
]
[Proposition 1] proving the hardness of
robust
-
gen-sp

involves a “large” metric space, so a natural question is whether
robust
-
gen-sp

becomes polynomial for “small” metric space, either in terms of diameter or number of elements. It so happens that the two questions can be answered positively. Namely, observe that for any value
c^{\ell}(P)
, we can find some
n_{v,v^{\prime}}\in[n]
for each
(v,v^{\prime})\in\mathcal{M}^{2}
such that
c^{\ell}(P)=\sum_{(v,v^{\prime})\in\mathcal{M}^{2}}n_{v,v^{\prime}}d\left(v,v^{\prime}\right)
. Therefore,
n_{val}
can be bounded by
n^{\frac{|\mathcal{M}|(|\mathcal{M}|-1)}{2}}
, so that
robust
-
gen-sp

is polynomially solvable if
|\mathcal{M}|
is constant. Alternatively, if all distances are integer, meaning
d
has integer values, then
n_{\mathcal{P}}\leq n\times\mbox{diam}(\mathcal{M})
, so
robust
-
gen-sp

can be solved in
\mathcal{O}(n^{3}\sigma^{2}(n\times\mbox{diam}(\mathcal{M}))^{\sigma})
in that case.

3.2
FPTAS

We now consider solving
robust
-
sp

approximately. More precisely, we provide an algorithm that, given an input
I
of
robust
-
sp

and
\epsilon>0
, outputs an
(1+\epsilon)
-approximated solution in time polynomial both in n and
1/\epsilon
.
More precisely, given any
\epsilon>0
, we want to provide an
(1+\epsilon)
-approximated solution. Let
I=(G,s,t,\mathcal{U},\mathcal{D})
be an input to
robust
-
sp

and
A
be an upper bound to
\mbox{{opt}}(I)
and
\epsilon^{\prime}>0
. We define a matrix
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
by rounding each element of
\mathcal{D}
to the closest value of the form
\epsilon^{\prime}\ell A
for some
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. We obtain an instance
I^{\prime}=(G,s,t,\mathcal{U},\mathcal{D}^{\prime})
to
robust
-
gen-sp

. Having Observation
3
in mind, a straightforward application of the DP from the previous section to
I^{\prime}
would yield too many values
n_{val}
. Hence, we show in Section
3.2
how to adapt the DP, and choose
A
and
\epsilon^{\prime}
(depending on
\epsilon
) appropriately to obtain the result below.

Theorem 6
.

robust
-
sp

admits an
\cal{FPTAS}
for fixed
\sigma
: for any
\epsilon
, we can compute a
(1+\epsilon)
-approximated solution
in time
\mathcal{O}(n^{3}\sigma^{2}(\frac{n^{2}}{\epsilon})^{\sigma})
.

Proof.

Let
I=(G,s,t,\mathcal{U},\mathcal{D})
be an instance of
robust
-
sp

.
Our objective is to provide a solution of cost at most
(1+\epsilon)\mbox{{opt}}(I)
.
Let
\epsilon^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
Using the
2
-approximation obtained combining Theorem
1
and Proposition
2
, we start by computing an
s-t
path
P_{A}
of cost
c(P_{A})=A
, where
\mbox{{opt}}(I)\leq A\leq 2\mbox{{opt}}(I)
.
For any
x,y\in\bigcup_{i\in V[G]}\mathcal{U}_{i}
, we define
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(x,y)
by rounding up
\mathcal{D}(x,y)
to the closest value of the form
\epsilon^{\prime}\ell A
for some
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
.
For any path
P
and
u\in\mathcal{U}
we denote by
c^{\prime}(u,P)=\sum_{{i,j}\in P}\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(u_{i},u_{j})
,
c^{\prime}(P)=\max_{u\in\mathcal{U}}c^{\prime}(u,P)
.
Let
I^{\prime}=(G,s,t,\mathcal{U},\mathcal{D}^{\prime})
be the instance of
robust
-
gen-sp

obtained when using
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
instead of
\mathcal{D}
.

Observe that

•

the function
d^{\prime}(u,v)=\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(u,v)
may not be a distance,

•

for any
x,y\in\bigcup_{i\in V[G]}\mathcal{U}_{i}
, we have
\mathcal{D}(u,v)\leq\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(u,v)\leq\mathcal{D}(u,v)+\epsilon^{\prime}A

•

for any path
P
,
c(P)\leq c^{\prime}(P)\leq c(P)+n\epsilon^{\prime}A

•

\mbox{{opt}}^{\prime}\leq\mbox{{opt}}+n\epsilon^{\prime}A
.

Let
i\in V[G]
and
P
be an
i-t
path. We say that
P
is
useless
if it verifies
c^{\prime}(P)>A(1+n\epsilon^{\prime})
; otherwise,
P
is said to be
good
. According to previous observations, we see that
c^{\prime}(P_{A})\leq A(1+n\epsilon^{\prime})
.
Thus, for any
i\in V[G]
and useless
i-t
path
P
, we have
c^{\prime}(P)>c^{\prime}(P_{A})
. This implies that
P
cannot be the suffix of an optimal solution to input
I^{\prime}
(meaning that there is no optimal solution of
I^{\prime}
that first goes from
s
to
i
, and then uses
P
).
As a consequence, in the DP algorithm provided in Section
3.1
, we can restrict our attention to the profiles of good paths, without loosing optimality in
I^{\prime}
.
More formally, for any
i\in V[G]
and
\kappa\in[n]
, we adapt the previous definition
\mathcal{P}^{(i,\kappa)}
to

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{good}}^{(i,\kappa)}=\{Pr(P)\mid P\in\mathcal{G}^{i\rightarrow t}_{\kappa},P\mbox{ is good}\},

and we now consider that the DP algorithm

A_{g}(i,\kappa)
computes
(\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{good}}^{(i,\kappa)},Q^{(i,\kappa)})
instead of
(\mathcal{P}^{(i,\kappa)},Q^{(i,\kappa)})
.
We now compute
P^{*}
an optimal solution on instance
I^{\prime}
(for cost function
c^{\prime}
) using Theorem
5
(with DP algorithm
A_{g}
), and output
P^{*}
.

We have
c(P^{*})\leq c^{\prime}(P^{*})=\mbox{{opt}}(I^{\prime})\leq\mbox{{opt}}(I)+n\epsilon^{\prime}A\leq\mbox{{opt}}(I)(1+2n\epsilon^{\prime})
.
Let us now consider the complexity of computing
P^{*}
.
As
c^{\prime}(P)=\max_{\ell\in[{\sigma_{i}}]}c^{\prime\ell}(P)
, observe that for any good path
P
we have
c^{\prime\ell}(P)\leq A(1+n\epsilon^{\prime})
.
Moreover, as for any
x,y\in\bigcup_{i\in V[G]}\mathcal{U}_{i}
,
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(u,v)
is a multiple of
\epsilon^{\prime}A
, we get that for any good path
P
,

c^{\prime\ell}(P)=\ell\epsilon^{\prime}A
for
0\leq l\leq n+\lceil\frac{1}{\epsilon^{\prime}}\rceil
.
This implies that
n_{val}\leq(n+2+\frac{1}{\epsilon^{\prime}})=\mathcal{O}(\frac{n}{\epsilon^{\prime}})
, so Observation
3
leads to the desired complexity of
\mathcal{O}(n^{3}\sigma^{2}(\frac{n}{\epsilon^{\prime}})^{\sigma})

to get a ratio
1+2n\epsilon^{\prime}
. Finally, given a target ratio
1+\epsilon
, we set
\epsilon^{\prime}=\frac{\epsilon}{2n}
,
obtaining the claimed complexity.
∎
