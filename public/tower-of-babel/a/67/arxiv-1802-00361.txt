Tailoring T c T_{c} by symmetry principles: The concept of Superconducting Fitness
Aline Ramires, Daniel F. Agterberg, Manfred Sigrist

Abstract
We propose a generalization of the concept of superconducting fitness , which allows us to make statements analogous to Anderson’s theorems concerning the stability of different superconducting states. This concept can be applied to complex materials with several orbital, layer, sublattice or valley degrees of freedom. The superconducting fitness parameters F A ​ ( 𝕜 ) F_{A}({\mathbb{k}}) and F C ​ ( 𝕜 ) F_{C}({\mathbb{k}}) give a direct measure of the robustness of the weak coupling instability and of the presence of detrimental terms in the Hamiltonian, respectively. These two parameters can be employed as a guide to engineer normal state Hamiltonians in order to favour or suppress superconducting order parameters with different symmetries and topological properties. To illustrate the applicability and power of this concept we study three cases: the non-centrosymmetric heavy fermion CePt 3 Si, the hole doped iron pnictide KFe 2 As 2 and the doped topological insulator Cu x Bi 2 Se 3 .

This paper generalizes the concept of superconducting fitness previously introduced by some of the authors

The generalization of the concept of superconducting fitness also allows us to better understand the role of each term in the normal state Hamiltonian, and how it supports or suppresses different kinds of superconducting order parameters. With this concept in hand, we have a very portable and inexpensive tool to, in principle, engineer the normal electronic state in order to support the emergence of specific superconducting order parameters, which can be exotic or topological. We focus here on a generic two-orbital model, a minimal model for the discussion of multi-band superconductivity. The Hamiltonian can be written in terms of an ‘orbital
\otimes
spin’ basis,
\Psi_{OS}^{\dagger}=(c_{1\uparrow}^{\dagger},c_{1\downarrow}^{\dagger},c_{2\uparrow}^{\dagger},c_{2\downarrow}^{\dagger})
, and parametrized as:

\displaystyle H_{0}({\mathbb{k}})=\sum_{a,b}h_{ab}({\mathbb{k}})\tau_{a}\otimes\sigma_{b},

(1)

where the Pauli matrices
\tau_{a}
and
\sigma_{b}
correspond to the extra (orbital) and spin DOF, respectively.
h_{ab}({\mathbb{k}})
are real functions of momenta
{\mathbb{k}}
due to the hermiticity of the Hamiltonian. In presence of parity (
P:{\mathbb{k}}\rightarrow-{\mathbb{k}}
, for orbitals of same parity) and time-reversal symmetry (TRS) defined as
\Theta=K\tau_{0}\otimes(i\sigma_{2})
, the only pairs allowed for
(a,b)
are
(0,0),(1,0),(3,0),(2,1),(2,2),(2,3)
. For orbitals with opposite parity (
P:\tau_{3}\otimes\sigma_{0}
and
{\mathbb{k}}\rightarrow-{\mathbb{k}}

(0,0),(2,0),(3,0),(1,1),(1,2),(1,3)

P:\tau_{1}\otimes\sigma_{0}
and
{\mathbb{k}}\rightarrow-{\mathbb{k}}
), and as a consequence the allowed pairs are
(0,0),(1,0),(2,0),(3,1),(3,2),(3,3)
. In all cases, the set of matrices
\tau_{a}\otimes\sigma_{b}
is completely anti-commuting, an important property to simplify the equations below.

In order to introduce a more concise notation, we substitute the
\tau_{a}\otimes\sigma_{b}
matrices by
T_{i}
-operators labelled by

T_{0}=\tau_{0}\otimes\sigma_{0}
. The generators follow the relation:

T_{i}T_{j}=\delta_{ij}I_{0}+\sum_{c}(if_{ijk}+d_{ijk})T_{k},

where
f_{ijk}=Tr[[T_{i},T_{j}]T_{k}]
and
d_{ijk}=Tr[\{T_{i},T_{j}\}T_{k}]
are completely anti-symmetric and symmetric structure constants, respectively. We distinguish the special subset of 5 completely anti-commuting generators by a different letter
O_{i}

T_{1...5}
are identified with
O_{1...5}
. These follow
\{O_{i},O_{j}\}=2\delta_{ij}I_{0}

\displaystyle H_{0}({\mathbb{k}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{0}({\mathbb{k}})T_{0}+\textbf{c}({\mathbb{k}})\cdot\textbf{O},

(2)

where
c_{0}({\mathbb{k}})
is an even function of
{\mathbb{k}}
,
\textbf{O}=(O_{1},...,O_{5})
and
\textbf{c}({\mathbb{k}})
is a 5-dimensional vector. In presence of parity and TRS the problem is doubly degenerate, so we can parametrize the Green’s functions (GF) in terms of two poles in a convenient way:

\displaystyle G_{0}(k)=G_{a}(k)\frac{(I_{0}+\hat{\textbf{c}}({\mathbb{k}})\cdot\textbf{O})}{2}+G_{b}(k)\frac{(I_{0}-\hat{\textbf{c}}({\mathbb{k}})\cdot\textbf{O})}{2},

where
G_{a,b}(k)=G_{a,b}({\mathbb{k}},i\omega_{n})
and
\hat{\textbf{c}}({\mathbb{k}})=\textbf{c}({\mathbb{k}})/|\textbf{c}({\mathbb{k}})|
. From
(i\omega_{n}T_{0}-H_{0})G_{0}(k)=T_{0}

G_{a,b}(k)=(i\omega_{n}-\epsilon_{a,b}({\mathbb{k}}))^{-1}
, where
\epsilon_{a,b}({\mathbb{k}})=c_{0}({\mathbb{k}})\pm|\textbf{c}({\mathbb{k}})|
.

We can also write the order parameter in terms of the generators of SU(4) as a gap matrix
\Delta({\mathbb{k}})=\mathbb{d}({\mathbb{k}})\cdot\textbf{T}(i\Sigma_{2})
, where
\mathbb{d}({\mathbb{k}})
is a 16-dimensional vector,
\textbf{T}=(T_{0},T_{1},...,T_{15})
and
i\Sigma_{2}=\tau_{0}\otimes(i\sigma_{2})

\Sigma_{2}O_{i}\Sigma_{2}=O_{i}^{T}

\Sigma_{2}T_{i}\Sigma_{2}=-T_{i}^{T}

We can then insert the parametrized GF and gap matrix in the linearized gap equation

\displaystyle 1=-Tv\sum_{{\mathbb{k}},n}Tr[\hat{\Delta}^{\dagger}({\mathbb{k}})G_{0}(k)\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})G^{T}_{0}(-k)],

(4)

where
v
is the magnitude of the attractive interaction in the symmetry channel of the respective
\Delta({\mathbb{k}})=d\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})
, where
\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})
is the normalized gap matrix satisfying
\langle|\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})|^{2}\rangle_{FS}=1

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{Tv}{8}\sum_{{\mathbb{k}},n}\Big[(G_{a}\bar{G}_{a}+G_{b}\bar{G}_{b})Tr[|F_{A}({\mathbb{k}})|^{2}]

(5)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle(G_{a}\bar{G}_{b}+G_{b}\bar{G}_{a})Tr[|F_{C}({\mathbb{k}})|^{2}]\Big],

where
G_{a,b}=G_{a,b}(k)
and
\bar{G}_{a,b}=G_{a,b}(-k)

\displaystyle F_{A}({\mathbb{k}})(i\sigma_{2})=\tilde{H}_{0}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})+\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})\tilde{H}_{0}^{*}(-{\mathbb{k}}),

(6)

\displaystyle F_{C}({\mathbb{k}})(i\sigma_{2})=\tilde{H}_{0}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})-\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})\tilde{H}_{0}^{*}(-{\mathbb{k}}),

where
\tilde{H}_{0}({\mathbb{k}})=(H_{0}({\mathbb{k}})-c_{0}({\mathbb{k}})T_{0})/|\textbf{c}({\mathbb{k}})|

Tr[|F_{A}({\mathbb{k}})|^{2}]
quantifies the presence of
intra-band pairing
, therefore guaranteeing the robustness of the SC instability in the weak-coupling limit; while
Tr[|F_{C}({\mathbb{k}})|^{2}]
quantifies the presence of
inter-band pairing

The first line in Eq.
5
can be treated as the usual BCS equation, leading to the familiar form, now carrying the superconducting fitness parameter
F_{A}({\mathbb{k}})
:

\displaystyle-2v\alpha ln\left(\frac{4e^{\gamma}}{\pi}\frac{\omega_{C}}{2k_{B}T}\right),

(7)

where

\displaystyle 16\alpha=\left[N_{a}(0)\langle Tr[|F_{A}({\mathbb{k}})|^{2}]\rangle_{FS_{a}}+(a\rightarrow b)\right],

(8)

\omega_{C}
is a characteristic cutoff energy,
N_{a,b}(0)

\langle...\rangle_{FS_{a,b}}

The second line, after the sum over the Matsubara frequencies, can be written as:

\displaystyle-\frac{v}{8}\sum_{{\mathbb{k}}}\frac{\text{tanh}\left(\frac{\beta\epsilon_{a}({\mathbb{k}})}{2}\right)}{(\epsilon_{a}({\mathbb{k}})+\epsilon_{b}({\mathbb{k}}))}Tr[|F_{C}({\mathbb{k}})|^{2}]+(a\rightarrow b).

(9)

At this point, for the single band scenario, one usually turns the integral over momenta into an integral over energy, introducing a DOS and integrating over a narrow range over the FS between
\pm\omega_{C}
. Note, though, that this integral is different because its denominator is written as a sum of two dispersions, and the integral is dominated by the region where
\epsilon_{a}+\epsilon_{b}=0
, but for well separated bands, this condition is normally satisfied far away from the FS. We introduce the quantity
q({\mathbb{k}})=\epsilon_{a}({\mathbb{k}})-\epsilon_{b}({\mathbb{k}})
and assume well separated bands, such that
q({\mathbb{k}})>>\omega_{C}
in the range of energies close to the FS. Within this approximation, we find that the second line in Eq.

2v\delta
, where

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!16\delta=\frac{\omega_{C}^{2}}{2}\left[N_{a}(0)\left\langle\frac{Tr[|F_{C}({\mathbb{k}})|^{2}]}{q({\mathbb{k}}_{Fa})^{2}}\right\rangle_{\!\!FS_{a}}\!\!\!\!\!+(a\rightarrow b)\right].

(10)

Now the full gap equation can be concisely written as:

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2v\alpha ln\left(\frac{4e^{\gamma}}{\pi}\frac{\omega_{C}}{2k_{B}T}\right)+2v\delta,

(11)

and the critical temperature is explicitly obtained as:

\displaystyle k_{B}T_{C}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4e^{\gamma}}{\pi}\frac{\omega_{C}}{2}e^{-1/2|v|\alpha}e^{-\delta/\alpha},

which makes clear that the larger the

Note that this closed form for

Within this non-perturbative formalism one can also find a closed form for

for the single band scenario. Starting from

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2vN(0)ln\left(\frac{4e^{\gamma}}{\pi}\frac{\omega_{C}}{2k_{B}T_{C}}\right)

(13)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{v}{8}N(0)\langle 2f(\rho_{\mathbb{k}})Tr[|F_{C}({\mathbb{k}})|^{2}]\rangle_{FS},

\displaystyle ln\left(\frac{T_{C}}{T_{C}^{Max}}\right)

Now we apply the analysis of the two superconducting fitness parameters to several superconducting materials in order to show the power of this concept and what kind of robust statements one can make concerning the stability of different superconducting states, or how to better engineer the normal state in order to support the most interesting or exotic order parameter.

In conclusion, we have introduced the concept of superconducting fitness and the parameters
F_{A}({\mathbb{k}})
and
F_{C}({\mathbb{k}})
which quantify the robustness of the superconducting instability and the presence of detrimental effects, respectively. These two parameters provide a very handy and useful computational tool to evaluate the effects of each symmetry allowed term in the Hamiltonian and how it supports or suppresses different superconducting order parameters. Ultimately, this scheme can be used as a first guide to engineer complex materials in order to support the desired exotic superconducting states.
