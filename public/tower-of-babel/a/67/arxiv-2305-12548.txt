The connectivity of the complex of homologous curves
Daniel Minahan

Abstract
. We show that the complex of homologous curves of a closed, orientable surface of genus g g is ( g − 3 ) (g-3) –acyclic. This is a key step in Putman and the author’s proof that the second rational of the Torelli group is finite dimensional for surfaces of sufficiently large genus.

Primary 54C40, 14E20; Secondary 46E25, 20C20

1.
Introduction

Let
S_{g}
be a connected, orientable, closed surface of genus
g
. For the remainder of this paper, a
curve
on
S_{g}
will be a homotopy class of oriented essential embedded circles
S^{1}\rightarrow S_{g}
. A
multicurve

M
will be a set of distinct curves in
S_{g}
with pairwise disjoint representatives. A curve
c\subseteq S_{g}
is
nonseparating
if
c
has a nonseparating representative. Likewise, a multicurve
M\subseteq S_{g}
is
nonseparating
if every curve in
M
can be simultaneously represented by disjoint embedded circles whose union is nonseparating in
S_{g}
. We let
\mathcal{C}(S_{g})
denote the
curve complex
of
S_{g}

[
FM12
, pg 92]
. The
k
–cells of this complex are multicurves
M\subseteq S_{g}
containing
k+1
curves.

Complex of homologous curves
. If
c\subseteq S_{g}
is a curve, we let
[c]\in\HH_{1}(S_{g};\mathbb{Z})
denote the homology class represented by
c
. Let
\vec{x}\in\HH_{1}(S_{g};\mathbb{Z})
be a
primitive homology class
, i.e., a class for which if
\vec{x}=m\vec{y}
for some
\vec{y}\in\HH_{1}(S_{g};\mathbb{Z})
and
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, then
m=\pm 1
. The
complex of homologous curves
, defined by Putman
[
Put08
]
and denoted
\mathcal{C}_{\vec{x}}(S_{g})
, is the full subcomplex of
\mathcal{C}(S_{g})
generated by curves
c
that satisfy
[c]=\vec{x}
. Putman used the work of Johnson
[
Joh85
]
to show that when
g\geq 3
, the complex
C_{\vec{x}}(S_{g})
is connected
[
Put08
]
. Recall that if
X
is a path–connected, locally path–connected, semi–locally simply connected topological space, then
X
is
n
–acyclic
if
\HH_{k}(X;\mathbb{Z})
vanishes for all
k\leq n
. By convention, we say that
X
is
(-1)
–acyclic
if
X
is non–empty, and we say that the empty topological space is
(-2)
–acyclic. Our first main result is the following.

Theorem A
.

Let
g\geq 2
. Let
\vec{x}\in\HH_{1}(S_{g};\mathbb{Z})
be a primitive homology class. The complex
\mathcal{C}_{\vec{x}}(S_{g})
is
(g-3)
–acyclic.

If
\vec{x}=0
, then the resulting complex is called the
complex of separating curves
, and is denoted
\mathcal{C}_{\sep}(S_{g})
. Looijenga has proven that
\mathcal{C}_{\sep}(S_{g})
is
\left(g-3\right)
–connected
[
Loo13
, Theorem 1.1]
.

Application of Theorem
A
to the Torelli group
. The
Torelli group
, denoted
\mathcal{I}_{g}
, is the subgroup of the mapping class group
\Mod(S_{g})
consisting of mapping classes
\varphi
that act trivially on
\HH_{1}(S_{g};\mathbb{Z})
. The Torelli group
\mathcal{I}_{g}
acts on
\mathcal{C}_{\vec{x}}(S_{g})
for any choice of primitive
\vec{x}\in\HH_{1}(S_{g};\mathbb{Z})
. The author and Putman use Theorem
A
in a key way to compute the second rational homology of the Torelli group in a stable range
[
MP25
]
. The complex
\mathcal{C}_{\vec{x}}(S_{g})
has been used by Hatcher and Margalit to give a new proof that
\mathcal{I}_{g}
is generated by bounding pair maps
[
HM12
]
. Gaster, Greene and Vlamis also related the chromatic number of
\mathcal{C}_{\vec{x}}(S_{g})
to the Chillingworth homomorphism
[
GGV18
]
.

1.1.
The strategy of the proof of Theorem
A

Let
g\geq 2
and let
\vec{x}\in\HH_{1}(S_{g};\mathbb{Z})
be a primitive homology class. Bestvina, Bux and Margalit defined a complex called the complex of minimizing cycles, denoted
\mathcal{B}_{\vec{x}}(S_{g})

[
BBM10
]
. The complex of minimizing cycles has two important properties:

•

\mathcal{C}_{\vec{x}}(S_{g})
is a subcomplex of
\mathcal{B}_{\vec{x}}(S_{g})
, and

•

\mathcal{B}_{\vec{x}}(S_{g})
is contractible
[
BBM10
, Theorem E]
.

Hatcher and Margalit
[
HM12
]
prove that the relative homotopy group

vanishes when
g\geq 3
. Along with the contractibility of
\mathcal{B}_{\vec{x}}(S_{g})
, this implies that
\mathcal{C}_{\vec{x}}(S_{g})
is connected when
g\geq 3
. We will use their PL–Morse function to prove
\HH_{k}(\mathcal{B}_{\vec{x}}(S_{g}),\mathcal{C}_{\vec{x}}(S_{g});\mathbb{Z})=0
for
k\leq g-2
. The contractibility of
\mathcal{B}_{\vec{x}}(S_{g})
then implies Theorem
A
.

The complex of splitting curves

\Sigma=(S,P)

]

\mathfrak{B}_{+}
and

\mathcal{C}_{\sep}(\Sigma)

Question 1

Do the relative homotopy groups

If the answer were affirmative, then

2.
Some terminology and algebraic topology facts

Let
X
be a topological space with a basepoint
x\in X
and let
Y\subseteq X
be a subspace with
x\in Y
. Let
n\geq 0
be a non–negative integer. We say that
X
is
n
–connected
if
\pi_{k}(X,x)=0
for every
k\leq n
. We say that
X
is
n
–acyclic
if
\widetilde{\HH}_{k}(X;\mathbb{Z})=0
for every
k\leq n
. We say that the pair
(X,Y)
is
relatively
n
–connected
if
\pi_{k}(X,Y)=0
for every
k\leq n
. We say that the pair
(X,Y)
is
relatively
n
–acyclic
if
\HH_{k}(X,Y)=0
for every
k\leq n
. By convention, a non–empty space is both
(-1)
–connected and
(-1)
–acyclic. If
X
is contractible, we will say that
X
is
\infty
–connected. Likewise, if
X
has the integral homology of a point, we say that
X
is
\infty
–acyclic. Note also that an
n
–connected space is always
k
–connected for
k\leq n
, and similarly for acyclic.

Notation
. We will let
\mathfrak{c}(X)
and
\mathfrak{a}(X)
denote the connectivity and acyclicity respectively of the space
X
, i.e., the maximal
n
for which
X
is
n
–connected (resp.
n
–acyclic). Similarly,
\mathfrak{c}(X,Y)
and
\mathfrak{a}(X,Y)
will denote the connectivity and acyclicity resp. of the pair
(X,Y)
.

Convention
. When we say that a space
X
is connected, we assume that
X
is
0
–connected, i.e., we assume that all connected spaces are nonempty.

We require the following fact from algebraic topology (see
[
Loo13
, Lemma 2.1]
).

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be a collection of topological spaces.

(a)

If each
X_{i}
is
k_{i}
–connected, then the join

X_{1}*\ldots*X_{n}

is
\left(-2+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(k_{i}+2\right)\right)
–connected.

(b)

If each
X_{i}
is
k_{i}
–acyclic, then the join

X_{1}*\ldots*X_{n}

is
\left(-2+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(k_{i}+2\right)\right)
–acyclic.

3.
Covers indexed by simplicial complexes

The main output of this section is Lemma
3.1
, which is a result about the connectivity of complexes given as the union of certain simplicial subcomplexes. Lemma
3.1
is a rephrasing of a result of Mirzaii and van der Kallen
[
MvdK02
]
. Lemma
3.1
can also be phrased using the homotopy colimit spectral sequence
[
Dug
]
.

Bi–cellular covers
. Let
X
and
Y
be two simplicial complexes. Let
\mathfrak{L}_{X}
be a function taking a vertex
y\in Y^{(0)}
to a subcomplex of
X
. We will always assume that
\mathfrak{L}_{X}(y)\neq\emptyset
. If
\sigma
is a
k
-cell of
Y
with vertices
y_{0},\ldots,y_{k}
, we denote

\mathfrak{L}_{X}(\sigma)=\mathfrak{L}_{X}(y_{0})\cap\ldots\cap\mathfrak{L}_{X}(y_{k}).

If
\bigcup_{y\in Y^{(0)}}\mathfrak{L}_{X}(y)=X
, we will call such an
\mathfrak{L}_{X}
a
Y
-indexed simplicial cover of
X
. If
\mathfrak{L}_{X}
is a
Y
-indexed cover of
X
, then there is an associated
X
-indexed cover of
Y
given by setting
\mathfrak{L}_{Y}(x)
to be the full subcomplex of
Y
generated by vertices
y
such that
x\in\mathfrak{L}_{X}(y)
. We call this cover
\mathfrak{L}_{Y}
the
dual cover
. We say that a
Y
-indexed cover of
X
is
cellularly
n
–acyclic
if for each
k
-cell
\sigma
of
Y
, the complex
\mathfrak{L}_{X}(\sigma)
is
(n-k)
–acyclic. We say
\mathfrak{L}_{X}
is
bi-cellularly
n
–acyclic
if
\mathfrak{L}_{X}
and
\mathfrak{L}_{Y}
are both cellularly
n
–acyclic.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
X
and
Y
be finite–dimensional, countable simplicial complexes. Let
\mathfrak{L}_{X}
be a
Y
-indexed simplicial cover of
X
. Suppose there is an integer
n
such that the following hold:

•

\mathfrak{L}_{X}
is bi-cellularly
n
–acyclic, and

•

Y
is
n
–acyclic.

Then
X
is
n
–acyclic.

We defer this proof for a moment. We will introduce the main object that we use to prove Lemma
3.1
.

Bi–cellular spectral sequence
. Let
X
and
Y
be simplicial complexes, and let
\mathfrak{L}_{X}
be a
Y
–indexed simplicial cover of
X
. We have a bi-graded complex called the
bi-cellular complex
given by

C_{p,q}=\bigoplus_{\sigma\in Y^{(p)}}C_{q}(\mathfrak{L}_{X}(\sigma))

where
C_{*}
denotes the complex of simplicial chains on a simplicial complex.

The leftward versus downward strategy
. Let
C_{p,q}
be a double complex. There are two spectral sequences associated to
C_{p,q}
, which are the leftward and downward spectral sequences. We denote these
\mathbb{E}_{*,*}^{*,\leftarrow}
and
\mathbb{E}_{*,*}^{*,\downarrow}
. These two spectral sequences are each constructed out of
C_{p,q}
by two different filtrations of the total complex
C_{p+q}
. The key point is that both
\mathbb{E}_{*,*}^{*,\leftarrow}
and
\mathbb{E}_{*,*}^{*,\downarrow}
both converge to filtrations of the total homology of
C_{p,q}
. Denote the total homology by
\HH_{p+q}(C_{*,*})
. Suppose that we want to compute some of the groups
\mathbb{E}_{p,q}^{2,\leftarrow}
. The strategy is as follows:

(1)

Show that
\mathbb{E}_{p,q}^{*,\downarrow}
converges to
0
in a range
0<p+q<n
. This implies that
\HH_{p+q}(C_{*,*})
converges to
0
for
0<p+q<n
.

(2)

Use the fact that
\mathbb{E}_{p,q}^{*,\leftarrow}
must also converge to
0
for
0<p+q<n
to say something about the groups
\mathbb{E}_{p,q}^{2,\leftarrow}
.

This is a standard technique, used for example to show that the
G
–equivariant homology of a contractible CW–complex
X
converges to the group homology of
G
when
G
acts on
X
without rotations
[
Bro94
, Section VII]
.

Proof of Lemma
3.1
.

We will apply the leftward versus downward strategy discussed above. In particular, we will show that the downward bi-cellular spectral sequence converges to
\mathbb{Z}
for
p+q=0
and converges to 0 for
0<p+q\leq n
, and that the leftward bi-cellular spectral sequence converges to
\HH_{p+q}(X;\mathbb{Z})
for
0\leq p+q\leq n
. This completes the proof since the leftward and downward sequence both converge to the total homology of
C_{*,*}
.

The downward sequence
. On page 1 of
\mathbb{E}_{*,*}^{*,\downarrow}
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{p,q}^{1,\downarrow}=\bigoplus_{\sigma\in Y^{(p)}}\HH_{q}(\mathfrak{L}_{X}(\sigma);\mathbb{Z}).

By hypothesis, for
0\leq p+q\leq n
and
q>0
we have

\mathbb{E}_{p,q}^{1,\downarrow}=0.

Then for
q=0
in this range we have
H_{0}(\mathfrak{L}_{X}(\sigma);\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z}
, so

\mathbb{E}_{p,0}^{1,\downarrow}=C_{p}(Y).

Therefore, we have
\mathbb{E}_{p,0}^{2,\downarrow}=\HH_{p}(Y;\mathbb{Z})
. Hence
\mathbb{E}_{p,q}^{*,\downarrow}
converges to
\HH_{p+q}(Y;\mathbb{Z})
, which by hypothesis is
\mathbb{Z}
when
p+q=0
and
0
for
0<p+q\leq n
.

The leftward sequence
. By the definition of the dual cover, we have

C_{*,*}=\bigoplus_{\sigma\in X^{(q)}}C_{p}(\mathfrak{L}_{Y}(\sigma)).

Then by the same argument as the downward case, the sequence
\mathbb{E}_{*,*}^{*,\leftarrow}
converges to
\HH_{p+q}(X;\mathbb{Z})
for
p+q\leq n
. Hence
\HH_{0}(X;\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}
and
\HH_{p+q}(X;\mathbb{Z})=0
for
0<p+q\leq n
, so
X
is
n
–acyclic.
∎

4.
The Complex of splitting curves

Our main goal in this section is to prove Proposition
4.1
, which says that the complex of splitting curves has the homotopy type of a wedge sum of spheres.

Homologically spherical.
Let
X
be an
n
-dimensional simplicial complex. We say that
X
is homologically spherical if
X
is
(n-1)
–acyclic. In particular, this means that
X
has the homology of a wedge sum of
n
–spheres.

Cutting curves on surfaces
. Let
S
be a surface and
S^{\prime}\subseteq S
an isotopy class of compact submanifolds. The notation
S\ssearrow S^{\prime}
denotes Farb and Margalit’s notion of cutting surfaces
[
FM12
]
.

Partitioned surface.
Following Putman
[
Put07
]
, a
partitioned surface

\Sigma=(S,P)
is a pair consisting of a compact, connected, oriented surface
S
and a partition
P
of the set of boundary components of
S
. A
block of a partition
is one set in the partition. There is a category
\mathfrak{Sur}
called the
Torelli surface category
of partitioned surfaces. There is a morphism
(S,P)\leq(S^{\prime},P^{\prime})
if there is an embedding
\iota:S\rightarrow S^{\prime}
satisfying the following conditions. Let
T
denote a connected component of
S^{\prime}\ssearrow\iota(S)
. Let
\mathfrak{B}_{T}
denote the boundary components of
T
that are boundary component of
S
, and similarly
\mathfrak{B}^{\prime}_{T}
. We require that the following hold:

•

for all
T\in\pi_{0}(S^{\prime}\ssearrow\iota(S))
, the set
\mathfrak{B}_{T}
is contained in some
\mathfrak{b}\in P
, and

•

for each
p_{1},p_{2}\in\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}
for some
\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}\in P^{\prime}
, if
p_{i}\in\mathfrak{B}_{T_{i}}
for two connected components
T_{1}\neq T_{2}
, then there is a
\mathfrak{b}\in P
with
\mathfrak{B}_{T_{1}},\mathfrak{B}_{T_{2}}\subseteq\mathfrak{b}
.

We will work with a poset
\mathfrak{TSurPos}
consisting of objects in
\mathfrak{Sur}
. This has the same objects as
\mathfrak{Sur}
, but we require additionally that the inclusion map
\iota:\Sigma\rightarrow\Sigma^{\prime}
is not given by filling in boundary components of
\Sigma
with discs. For our purposes this is a more convenient object to work with than
\mathfrak{Sur}
.

Complex of separating curves
. The
complex of separating curves

\mathcal{C}_{\sep}(\Sigma)
is the full subcomplex of
\mathcal{C}(S)
generated by separating curves
\delta
such that each block
\mathfrak{b}\in P
is contained entirely in one connected component of
S\ssearrow\delta
. A theorem of Looijenga
[
Loo13
, Theorem 1.3]
tells us that this complex is
(g-2)
–connected when
\left|\pi_{0}(\partial S)\right|\geq 2
.

Vertex complement.
Following Hatcher and Margalit
[
HM12
]
, a
vertex complement
is a partitioned surface
\Sigma=(S,P)
such that:

•

|P|\geq 2
, and

•

there are two distinguished blocks in
P
. One is labeled
\mathfrak{B}_{+}
and the other is labeled
\mathfrak{B}_{-}
.

We will denote a vertex complement by

(S,P,\mathfrak{B}_{+},\mathfrak{B}_{-}).

The terminology “vertex complement” refers to
\Sigma
being the complement of a vertex of a certain complex called the complex of minimizing cycles, which we discuss in Section
6
. We will say that
\Sigma
is a
vertex complement on a surface
S
if the underlying surface of
\Sigma
is
S
. The set of vertex complements is a poset which we will denote
\mathfrak{VerCom}
. We have
\Sigma\leq\Sigma^{\prime}
if:

•

\Sigma\leq\Sigma^{\prime}
in
\mathfrak{TSurPos}
, and

•

there is an inclusion
\iota:S\rightarrow S^{\prime}
realizing
\Sigma\leq\Sigma^{\prime}
in
\mathfrak{TSurPos}
such that there are at least two connected components of
S^{\prime}\ssearrow\iota(S)
. The two connected components are labeled
S_{+}
and
S_{-}
and satisfy
\partial S_{+}=\mathfrak{B}_{+}\sqcup\mathfrak{B}_{+}^{\prime}
and

5.
PL–Morse theory for cell complexes

Recall from the introduction that the proof of Theorem
A
proceeds by using PL–Morse theory on the complex of minimizing cycles
\mathcal{B}_{\vec{x}}(S_{g})

[
BBM10
]
. A reference for PL–Morse theory in general can be found in the work of Bestvina
[

]
. The complex
\mathcal{B}_{\vec{x}}(S_{g})
is not a simplicial complex, so we must explain how to use PL–Morse theory on certain types of non–simplicial complexes. The main goal is to prove Lemma
5.6
, which describes a method for proving that a complex is highly acyclic.

Convex hulls
. Let
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}=\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{R}
. Let
S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
be two subsets. The convex hull of
S_{1}
, denoted
\Hull(S_{1})
, is the set

\{t_{0}s_{0}+\ldots+t_{n}s_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},s_{0},\ldots,s_{n}\in S,\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}=1,t_{i}\geq 0\text{ for all }0\leq i\leq n\}.

The convex join of
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
, denoted
\ConvJoin(S_{1},S_{2})
, is the set

\{t_{1}s_{1}+t_{2}s_{2}:s_{i}\in S_{i},t_{1}+t_{2}=1,t_{i}\geq 0\}.

Locally linear cell complex
. A finite dimensional cell complex
X
is
locally linear
if there is an inclusion
\iota:X\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that each cell
\sigma
of
X
is the convex hull of its vertices and
\partial\sigma
is the union of the faces of
\iota(\sigma)
. We will conflate
X
with its image under this map. Let
W:X^{(0)}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
be a PL–Morse funtion. We say that
W
is a
linear PL–Morse function
if
W
is the restriction of some linear function
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, which by abuse of notation we will refer to as
W
as well. Linear PL–Morse functions have the following property.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\sigma
be a cell of a locally linear cell complex
X
and let
W
be a linear PL–Morse function on
X
. The set of all points
x\in\sigma
with
W(x)
of maximal weight among the points of
\sigma
is a face of
\sigma
, and is the convex hull of the vertices of
\sigma
of maximal weight.

Proof.

Let
M\subseteq\sigma
denote the set of points of maximal weight. By definition this is a face of
\sigma
since
M
is the set of points maximizing the linear function
W
. Then
M
must be the convex hull of its vertices as well, so the result follows.
∎

Sharp PL–Morse functions
. Let
X
be a locally linear cell complex. We say that a function
W:X^{(0)}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
is
sharp
if, for every cell
\rho\subseteq X
where
W|_{\rho^{0}}\neq 0
, the function
W|_{\rho^{0}}
has a unique maximum.

Quasi–linear PL–Morse functions
. We say that a function
W:X^{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
is
quasi–linear
if
W
is either linear or sharp, and we will refer to these as PL–Morse functions. We say that a cell
\sigma\subseteq X
is
W
–constant
if the following hold:

•

W(v)=W(w)
for every
v,w\in\sigma^{(0)}
; and

•

W(v)>0
for all
v\in\sigma^{0}
.

Descending links in locally linear cell complexes
. Let
X
be a locally linear cell complex and let
W
be a quasi–linear PL–Morse function on
X
. Let
\sigma\subseteq X
be a
W
–constant cell. We will define three different versions of the descending link
d_{W}(\sigma)
, and then show that all are homotopy equivalent. Let
\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}
denote the set of all cells
\rho\subseteq X
such that

•

\sigma
is a face of
\rho
, and

•

W(r)\leq W(\sigma)
for all
r\in\rho^{(0)}
, with equality if and only if
r\in\sigma
.

The variants of the descending link are as follows.

•

The facial descending link
d_{W}^{\face}(\sigma)
.
Let
\rho\in\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}
be a cell and let
\mathcal{T}(\rho)
be the set of all faces
\tau\subseteq\rho
such that
\tau\cap\sigma=\emptyset
. We define

d_{W}^{\face}(\sigma)=\bigcup_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}}\mathcal{T}(\rho).

•

The adjacent descending link
d_{W}^{\adj}(\sigma)
.
For all
\rho\in\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}
, define
V(\rho)
to be the set of all vertices

.
This is given by

Note that there are two inclusions

We first prove the following.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
W
be a quasi–linear PL–Morse function on a locally linear cell complex

. Let
\sigma\subseteq X
be a
W
–constant cell. Let

be a cell. Then

is contractible for any choice of
\dagger=\face,\tot,\adj
.

Proof.

We prove this for each choice of
\dagger
in turn.

\dagger=\adj
. We will show that
d_{W}^{\adj}(\sigma)\cap\rho=\Hull(V(\rho))
, where
V(\rho)
is as in the definition of
d_{W}^{\face}
.
The only potentially non–obvious point here is that if
\rho
is a face of another cell

, then it may be the case that
\Hull(V(\rho^{\prime}))\cap\rho\supsetneq\Hull(V(\rho))
. Suppose in fact that this does happen, so there is a point

. Then

is on a line connecting two points

. These

and
t_{2}
cannot lie
\rho
, since otherwise

. But no such
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
can exist since
\rho
is a face of
\rho^{\prime}
, so we have
\Hull(V(\rho))=d_{W}^{\adj}(\sigma)\cap\rho
.

\dagger=\tot
. This follows by a similar line of reasoning to
\dagger=\adj
.

\dagger=\face
. As for
\adj
, define
\mathcal{T}(\rho)
as in the definition of
d_{W}^{\face}(\sigma)
. Then
\mathcal{T}(\rho)
is given by the union of faces of
\rho
. If

were a point in the interior of
\rho
that was contained in
\mathcal{T}(\rho^{\prime})
for some other cell
\rho^{\prime}
, then
\rho
would be a face of
\rho^{\prime}
. But then
\rho\subseteq\mathcal{T}(\rho^{\prime})
implies that

by definition of

, which contradicts the definition of
\mathcal{R}_{\rho}
.
∎

We now have the following lemma that describes the relationships between the three types of descending links.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
W
be a quasi–linear PL–Morse function on a locally linear cell complex
X
. Let
\sigma\subseteq X
be a
W
–constant cell. Then each inclusion

d_{W}^{\face}(\sigma)\hookrightarrow d_{W}^{\tot}(\sigma)\hookleftarrow d_{W}^{\adj}(\sigma)

is a weak homotopy equivalence.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}
be as above. For each choice of
\dagger=\face,\tot,\adj
and for each
\rho\in\mathcal{R}^{\max}
let

U_{\rho}^{\dagger}=\rho\cap d_{W}^{\dagger}(\sigma).

Let
\mathcal{R}^{\max}_{\sigma}
denote the set of maximal cells in
\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}
, i.e., the set of all
\rho
such that there is no
\rho^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}
with
\rho\subsetneq\rho^{\prime}
. Let
\mathcal{N}(U_{\rho}^{\dagger})
denote the nerve of the set
\{U_{\rho}^{\dagger}\}_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}^{\max}}
. Each of these spaces and their non–empty intersections are contractible by Lemma

. The nerve lemma implies that we have isomorphisms of homology groups

\HH_{*}(\mathcal{N}(U_{\rho}^{\dagger}))\cong\HH_{*}(d_{W}^{\dagger}(\sigma)).

It now remains to show that for a choice of
\dagger=\adj,\face
, the induced map of chain complexes

C_{*}(\mathcal{N}(U_{\rho}^{\dagger});\mathbb{Z})\rightarrow C_{*}(\mathcal{N}(U_{\rho}^{\tot});\mathbb{Z})

is a homotopy equivalence. In fact, we will show that the above map induces an isomorphism of chain complexes. It is clear that the induced map is a well–defined injection for either choice of
\dagger\in\{\face,\adj\}
, so it remains to prove that this induced map is a surjection.

Let
\rho_{0},\ldots,\rho_{n}\in\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}^{\max}
be a collection of cells with

. Let
\rho=\rho_{0}\cap\ldots\cap\rho_{n}
, which is necessarily a cell of
X
. Since
\rho
is a cell of
X
and
\sigma\subsetneq\rho
, there is a cell
\tau\subseteq\rho
with
\tau\cap\sigma=\emptyset
and
\tau\subseteq U_{\rho}^{\tot}
. We can choose
\tau
to contain a vertex adjacent to
\sigma
, since
\rho
is a cell. Therefore
U_{\rho}^{\adj}
and
U_{\rho}^{\face}
are both nonempty and hence contractible by Lemma

, so the induced map of chain complexes is surjective, and hence an isomorphism.
∎

We will say that a quasi–linear PL–Morse function

–acyclic. Let
\sigma
be a

denote the union of all cells

•

\tau\subseteq\rho

Lemma 5.4
.

Let

be a locally linear complex and

a quasi–linear PL–Morse function on

Proof.

If

is sharp, then

, so this follows by definition. Therefore, we may assume that

is linear. As in the proof of Lemma

, recall the set

. For each

and let

. From Lemma

, we see that for any

. Furthermore, if

U_{\rho_{1}}\cap\ldots\cap U_{\rho_{n}}\simeq U^{\prime}_{\rho_{1}}\cap\ldots\cap U^{\prime}_{\rho_{n}}

Now, consider the topological space

, let

denote the topological space

Therefore

is a compact convex set, so

is homotopy equivalent to a sphere of dimension

Lemma 6.4
.

The PL–Morse function
W
is a linear PL–Morse function.

Proof.

Let
\sigma\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{\vec{x}}(S_{g})
be a cell corresponding to a multicurve
M
. By definition,
\sigma
is the convex hull of its vertices
v_{0},\ldots,v_{m}
. Hence every point
v\in\sigma
is a linear combination

v=\sum_{i=0}^{m}t_{i}v_{i}

with
\sum_{i=0}^{m}t_{i}=1
. Set

W^{\sigma}(v)=\sum_{i=0}^{m}t_{i}W(v_{i}).

It suffices to show that
W
is well-defined on points in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{S}}
, i.e., independent of the choice
\sigma
. To see this, suppose that

v=\sum_{i=0}^{m}t^{\prime}_{i}v_{i}

is another linear combination with
\sum_{i=0}^{m}t^{\prime}_{i}=1
. If
a_{0},\ldots,a_{n}
are the underlying simple closed curves in the multicurve
M
corresponding to
\sigma
, then each
v_{i}
is by definition a formal sum

\sum_{j=0}^{n}\lambda_{i,j}a_{j}

such that

\vec{x}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\lambda_{i,j}[a_{j}]

in
\HH_{1}(S_{g};\mathbb{Z})
. Since

\sum_{i=0}^{m}t_{i}v_{i}=\sum_{i=0}^{m}t^{\prime}_{i}v_{i}

there is a relation for each
a_{j}
given by

\sum_{i=0}^{m}t_{i}\lambda_{i,j}=\sum_{i=0}^{m}t^{\prime}_{i}\lambda_{i,j}.

Hence we have

\sum_{j=0}^{n}\sum_{i=0}^{m}t_{i}\lambda_{i,j}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\sum_{i=0}^{m}t_{i}^{\prime}\lambda_{i,j}.

But by the definition of
W
, this is precisely

\sum_{i=0}^{m}t_{i}W(v_{i})=\sum_{i=0}^{m}t^{\prime}_{i}W(v_{i})

so the claim holds.
∎
