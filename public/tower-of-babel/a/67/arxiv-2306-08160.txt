Degenerate homoclinic bifurcations in complex dimension 2
Romain Dujardin

Abstract
. Unfolding homoclinic tangencies is the main source of bifurcations in 2-dimensional (real or complex) dynamics. When studying this phenomenon, it is common to assume that tangencies are quadratic and unfold with positive speed. Adapting to the complex setting an argument of Takens, we show that any 1-parameter family of 2-dimensional holomorphic diffeomorphisms unfolding an arbitrary non-persistent homoclinic tangency contains such quadratic tangencies. Combining this with recent results of Avila-Lyubich-Zhang and former results in collaboration with Lyubich, this yields the abundance of robust homoclinic tangencies in the bifurcation locus for complex Hénon maps. We also study bifurcations induced by families with persistent tangencies, which provide another approach to the complex Newhouse phenomenon.



1.
Introduction

Bifurcation theory is the study of the mechanisms creating instability in smooth dynamics. For
surface diffeomorphisms, the most basic such mechanism is the unfolding of a homoclinic tangency, and a long standing conjecture of Palis predicts that homoclinic tangencies are the building block of
all bifurcations. Recall that a homoclinic tangency is a tangency between the stable manifold and the unstable manifold of a saddle periodic point.
We refer the reader to the classical monograph of Palis and Takens
[
29
]

for an introduction to this topic.

When studying the unfolding of a homoclinic tangency,
it is common to restrict to a 1-parameter family (or more generally a finite dimensional parameter family) where the unfolding is “as transverse as it can be”, namely that
the tangency is quadratic and detaches with positive speed. Without these assumptions, the
analysis of the bifurcation becomes much more delicate –the situation is somehow parallel to the difference between the quadratic family and a general multimodal family in one-dimensional dynamics.
In the smooth (
C^{k}
) category this restriction is essentially harmless since one can always ensure
these properties in a generic family –hence the usual terminology

generic homoclinic tangencies
. On the other hand,
going to the analytic, or even algebraic, category,
where the parameter spaces are typically much smaller, the genericity of such tangencies become
an interesting problem. In
[
17
]
, the prevalence of homoclinic tangencies in the space of complex Hénon mappings of a given degree was studied by M. Lyubich and the author, and the Palis conjecture was confirmed under mild dissipativity assumptions.
However, the question of the genericity of these tangencies was left open.

In a remarkable, but seemingly not so well-known,
paper, Takens
[
36
]
proved that in any family of
real-analytic surface diffeomorphisms presenting an “inevitable tangency” (that is, in which
a tangency must happen for topological reasons), then under a non-degeneracy assumption on the
eigenvalues at the saddle point,
generic tangencies are dense in the tangency locus. Other relevant references include
Robinson
[
30
]
and Davis
[
13
]
,
where cascades of sinks are created from tangencies of
arbitrary order for real-analytic diffeomorphisms of surfaces (in
[
30
]
) and for
C^{\infty}
-
diffeomorphisms under a
C^{\infty}
linearizability condition (in
[
13
]
).
Many of the techniques developed in these papers take advantage of plane topology and
geometry, so non-trivial work needs to be carried out to adapt them to the complex setting.
Note that conversely, obtaining degenerate tangencies from non-degenerate ones is also interesting
and leads to rich dynamical phenomena (see e.g.
[
21
,
22
]
).

The first main result in this paper is a generalization of Takens’ theorem
to holomorphic diffeomorphisms.

Theorem A.1
.

Let
(f_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
be a holomorphic family of holomorphic diffeomorphisms
defined in some domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{C}^{2}}
, parameterized by a complex manifold
\Lambda
.
Assume that in the neighborhood of
some
\lambda_{0}\in\Lambda
,
f_{\lambda}
possesses a saddle fixed point
p_{\lambda}

with a non persistent homoclinic tangency at
{\lambda_{0}}
. Assume furthermore that there is no persistent relation of the form
u_{\lambda}^{a}s_{\lambda}^{b}=1
, where

s_{\lambda}
and
u_{\lambda}
are the respective stable and unstable multipliers of
p_{\lambda}
and
a
and
b
are positive integers.

Then there exists
\lambda_{1}

arbitrary close to
{\lambda_{0}}
such that
p_{\lambda_{1}}
has
a quadratic homoclinic tangency, unfolding with positive speed.

Note that the notion of “unfolding with positive speed” really makes sense only if
\Lambda
is 1-dimensional (see §
3
for a thorough discussion).
For higher dimensional families, this means that there is a
1-dimensional family though
{\lambda_{0}}
in which this property holds. Hence
the result is strongest when
\Lambda
is 1-dimensional, and we will prove it in this case. We note that in the quadratic case, the “positive speed” assertion of the theorem was independently established in
[
3
]
.

To understand the subtlety of this result we have to recall how secondary
tangencies are produced from the unfolding of an initial homoclinic tangency. The mechanism is of course very classical.
Assume that
(f_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
is a family of local diffeomorphisms of
{\mathbb{C}^{2}}
, and

p=(p_{\lambda})
is a fixed saddle point such that for
\lambda={\lambda_{0}}
,
W^{s}(p_{\lambda_{0}})
is tangent to
W^{u}(p_{\lambda_{0}})

at
\tau
. We can work in local coordinates
(x,y)
where
p_{\lambda}=(0,0)

W^{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}(p_{\lambda})=\left\{x=0\right\}
and
W^{u}_{\mathrm{loc}}(p_{\lambda})=\left\{y=0\right\}
. Iterating
\tau
if necessary, we may
assume that it belongs to
W^{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}(p_{\lambda_{0}})
, so there is a branch
\Delta^{u}_{\lambda_{0}}\subset W^{u}({\lambda_{0}})

tangent to
\left\{x=0\right\}
at
\tau
. Assume that
p_{\lambda_{0}}
belongs to some horseshoe (in the complex case
this is automatic, see Proposition
3.2
), whose stable lamination accumulates

\left\{x=0\right\}
. When
\lambda
moves in parameter space, the horseshoe persists and the branch
\Delta^{u}_{\lambda}
is pulled across
the stable lamination, so new tangencies are created (see Figure
1
).

Figure 1.
Creation of a secondary tangency

Now imagine a toy model for this situation where the stable manifolds of the horseshoe are just vertical
lines and
\Delta^{u}_{\lambda}
moves under a horizontal translation.
In the holomorphic case one can easily imagine that the speed of motion of

\Delta^{u}_{\lambda}
cannot vanish on a Cantor set of parameters (of course the reality is more
complicated because the Cantor set of vertical lines moves with the parameter), so most tangencies should occur with positive speed. On the other hand,
on this toy model it is unclear why, if we start with a tangency of high order,
the order of the secondary tangencies would generically decrease.
The point of Takens’ proof is to understand how the stable lamination of the horseshoe
differs
from a Cantor set of vertical lines, in order to lower the order of tangency. In this respect, a key
notion is that of
dynamical slope
(see §
4.2
). The argument also requires delicate

C^{k}
estimates for these vertical graphs, for large
k
(see §
4.1
).
The structure of the proof in the complex case is roughly the same as that of
[
36
]
,
but the technical details differ in many ways.

For polynomial automorphisms of

Theorem A.2
.

Let
(f_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
be a holomorphic family of
dissipative polynomial
automorphisms of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
of constant dynamical degree,
parameterized by a complex manifold
\Lambda
.
Assume that
f_{\lambda_{0}}
admits a non-persistent homoclinic tangency.
Then there exists
\lambda_{1}\in\Lambda

arbitrary close to
{\lambda_{0}}
such that
f_{\lambda_{1}}
has
a quadratic homoclinic tangency, unfolding with positive speed.

If
\Lambda
is an open subset of
the space of all generalized Hénon maps of a given degree, by
Buzzard-Hruska-Illyashenko
[
11

there is no persistent resonance between the multipliers of a given periodic point,

applies directly. In particular no dissipativity assumption is required in this case.
Let us also point out that a weaker version of this result was recently established by Araujo and Moreira in
[
1

, in which
f_{{\lambda_{0}}}
is perturbed in an infinite dimensional space of entire mappings.

For families of polynomial diffeomorphisms of
{\mathbb{C}^{2}}

it was shown in
[

that in the moderately dissipative regime

\left|\jac(f)\right|<\deg(f)^{-2}
, homoclinic tangencies are dense in the bifurcation locus.
Theorem

thus implies that these homoclinic tangencies
can be chosen to be quadratic with positive speed.
Putting this together with the results of

we obtain:

Corollary A.3
.

In any holomorphic family of moderately dissipative polynomial diffeomorphisms of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
of a given degree, the bifurcation locus is the closure of its interior.

The second main result of the paper is that for families of
polynomial automorphisms of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, persistent homoclinic tangencies also induce bifurcations.

Theorem B.1
.

Let
(f_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
be a substantial family of
polynomial automorphisms of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
of constant dynamical degree,
parameterized by a connected complex manifold
\Lambda
. Assume that there is
a persistent homoclinic tangency associated to some saddle periodic point
p

(with multipliers
u
and
s
) such that
the function
\lambda\mapsto\frac{\ln\left|u_{\lambda}\right|}{\ln\left|s_{\lambda}\right|}
is non-constant.
Then
(f_{\lambda})
is not weakly
J^{\varstar}
-stable.

We refer to the Appendix for
the meaning of the word “substantial” and the notion of weak
J^{\varstar}
-stability from
[
17
]
, which is a weak form of
structural stability on the Julia set. Here we content ourselves with
pointing out that any dissipative family is substantial by definition, and that in this case the
failure of weak
J^{\varstar}
-stability means that some saddle bifurcates to a sink (which implies the creation of new tangencies when
\left|\jac(f)\right|<\deg(f)^{-2}
).

Since this result applies to any open subset of
\Lambda
, in the dissipative regime
it follows from
[
17
, Cor. 4.5]

that Newhouse parameters, that is parameters displaying infinitely many sinks,
are dense in
\Lambda
. Thus we obtain an
alternate approach to the existence of such parameters
which does not involve stable intersections of Cantor sets
(see Yampolsky-Yang
[
37
]
for yet another approach, also using
[
17
]
).

It is quite simple to find examples of families satisfying the assumptions of
Theorem
B.1
. For instance, it is classical that
in the space of quadratic Hénon mappings with parameters

(a,c)\in\mathbb{C}^{\varstar}\times\mathbb{C}
,

f_{a,c}(z,w)=(aw+z^{2}+c,az)
, any (degenerate) parameter of the form

(0,c)
where
c
is strictly post-critically finite can be continued (in infinitely many ways)
as a 1-parameter family of Hénon maps with a persistent tangency.
It follows that
\left\{0\right\}\times\partial M
(where
M
is the Mandelbrot set)
lies in the closure of the set of Newhouse parameters. In this case the assumption on the multipliers
is easy to check because the Jacobian tends to zero along the parameter curve, so
s_{\lambda}\to 0
while

u_{\lambda}
is bounded away from 0 and infinity.

It is worth mentioning that two such curves (one homoclinic, one heteroclinic), landing at
(0,-2)
,
were studied in detail by Bedford and Smillie in
[
5
,
6
]
, in the real setting. Along these families, the Julia set
J^{\varstar}
is contained in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and no sink nor additional tangency is created as the Jacobian varies. This shows that Theorem
B.1
is really about complex parameters.

To get further and prove the abundance of Newhouse parameters in the bifurcation locus,
we have to check that the assumption on the multipliers is generically satisfied, up to a change of periodic point. This is similar in spirit to Theorem
A.2
,
but also more delicate, and requires to work in the space of all
polynomial automorphisms of a given degree.

Theorem B.2
.

Let
\Lambda
be an irreducible component of the space of generalized
Hénon mappings of degree
d\geq 2
, and
{\lambda_{0}}\in\Lambda
a
parameter displaying a homoclinic tangency. Then in any neighborhood of

{\lambda_{0}}
there is a hypersurface
\Lambda_{1}\subset\Lambda

satisfying the assumptions of Theorem
B.1
. In particular if
\left|\jac f_{\lambda_{0}}\right|\leq 1
, then

{\lambda_{0}}
belongs to
the closure of the set of Newhouse parameters.

To prove this, we have to rule out the unlikely phenomenon that as soon as a
tangency is created near
{\lambda_{0}}
, then,
along the corresponding hypersurface where this new tangency
persists, the non-resonance condition of Theorem
B.1
fails.
Even if such a coincidence is hardly plausible, excluding it requires some non-trivial
arguments. In particular we make heavy use the genericity
results of
[
11
]
.

Together with
[
17
]
, Theorem
B.2

gives an alternate argument for the following weak
version of Corollary
A.3
.

Corollary B.3
.

In the family of all moderately dissipative polynomial diffeomorphisms of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
of a given degree,
the bifurcation locus is contained in
the closure of the set of Newhouse parameters.

This result is much weaker than Corollary
A.3
because, instead of
open sets where Newhouse parameters are residual, it only provides codimension 1 laminations.
On the other hand, the proof is simpler since it does not
resort to the results of
[
3
]
.
We also note that
another approach to the construction of
Newhouse parameters was devised by
Martens, Palmisano and Tao
[

]
.

It is natural to wonder whether Theorem
B.1
admits a local version as in Theorem
A.1
. The statement would be:
in any family of diffeomorphisms of
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{C}^{2}}
with a persistent homoclinic tangency, and such that

\frac{\ln\left|u_{\lambda}\right|}{\ln\left|s_{\lambda}\right|}
is non-constant, non-persistent tangencies are created
. There is a simple mechanism for this in the real setting,
going back to
the work of Gavrilov and Shil’nikov
[
20
]
,
which depends on the signs of certain auxiliary parameters (such restrictions are
necessary in view of the above mentioned examples of Bedford-Smillie
[
6
]
).

To prove Theorem
B.1
, we take a different path
and use the notion of
moduli of stability
,
introduced by Palis
[
28
]
and further developed e.g. by
Newhouse, Palis and Takens
[
27
]
and also by
Buzzard
[
10
]
for diffeomorphisms of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
.
In all these references, the authors start with a
topological conjugacy between two diffeomorphisms
in a neighborhood of an orbit of tangency to deduce a differentiable rigidity of the multipliers (
[
28
]
further relies on plane topology considerations).
We show that this notion can be adapted to the context of the weak
J^{\varstar}
-stability
theory of
[
17
]
, which does
not
yield topological conjugacies. As observed above, the results of
[
5
,
6
]

show that it is essential here to work in the complex setting.
A key idea is that the
holomorphic motion of saddle periodic points admits a natural extension to stable and unstable manifolds, which satisfy good distortion properties.
This provides a reasonably simple proof of Theorem
B.1
, which takes
advantage of the global geometric structure of complex Hénon mappings and showcases the techniques of
[
17
]
.

Note that it is actually also possible to adapt the Gavrilov-Shil’nikov mechanism to the complex setting, at least for quadratic tangencies. The details (which are a bit technical) will appear elsewhere.

Remark 1.1
.

Theorems
B.1
and
B.2

bear some similarity with
recent work of Gauthier, Taflin and Vigny
[
19
]
,
where “higher bifurcations” are studied
in spaces of regular endomorphisms of
\mathbb{P}^{k}(\mathbb{C})
(see also
[
2
]
, as well as
[
16
]
for a brief account on this topic).
These theorems can actually be interpreted from the perspective of higher bifurcations (at least in the moderately dissipative regime):
indeed the bifurcation locus has some codimension 1 structure given by the hypersurfaces
of persistent homoclinic tangencies, and inside these hypersurfaces
Theorem
B.1

can recursively be used to construct new tangencies.
One might expect that the analogue of Theorem
B.2
holds
recursively, so that if
\Lambda
is a component of the space of polynomial automorphisms of given degree,
then on a dense subset of the bifurcation locus there should be
\dim(\Lambda)

“independent” tangencies.

Preliminary geometric lemmas

2.1.
Transversality lemma

The following basic lemma is very useful.

Lemma 2.1

(see
[
4
, Lem. 6.4]
)
.

Let
\Delta
and
\Delta^{\prime}
be two holomorphic disks with an isolated tangency of order
h
at
p
. Then if
\Delta^{\prime\prime}
is a holomorphic disk disjoint from
\Delta
and sufficiently
C^{1}
close to it,
it intersects
\Delta^{\prime}
transversally in
h+1
points close to
p
.

Observe that this result does not hold in higher dimension, due to the possibility of non-proper intersections: this is precisely the reason why Theorem
A.1
is non-trivial (cf. the formalism of §
3.2
).
An explicit example where the higher dimensional version of this lemma fails
is obtained by lifting the basic toy model from the Introduction to the projectivized tangent bundle.

2.2.
Horizontal and vertical varieties in
\mathbb{B}

Recall that
\mathbb{B}
is the unit bidisk. We let
\partial^{h}\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{D}\times\partial\mathbb{D}
(resp.
\partial^{v}\mathbb{B}=\partial\mathbb{D}\times\mathbb{D}
) be its horizontal (resp. vertical) boundary. A subvariety
V
in
some neighborhood of
\overline{\mathbb{B}}
is
horizontal
(resp.
vertical
) in
\mathbb{B}
if

V\cap\partial^{h}\mathbb{B}=\emptyset
(resp.
V\cap\partial^{v}\mathbb{B}=\emptyset
). A horizontal (resp. vertical) subvariety is a branched cover over the unit disk for the first (resp. second)
projection so it has a
degree
, which is the degree of this cover. If
V_{1}
(resp.
V_{2}
is a horizontal (resp. vertical) variety of degree
d_{1}
(resp.
d_{2}
), then
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
intersect in
d_{1}d_{2}
points, counting multiplicity
(see e.g.
[
14
]
for more details on these notions).

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\Delta
be a horizontal submanifold in
\mathbb{B}
of degree
d

which is a union of holomorphic disks, and
(W_{i})_{i\in I}
an arbitrary collection of disjoint vertical disks. Then the total number of tangencies between
\Delta
and the
W_{i}
, counting multiplicities,
is bounded by
d-1

Proof.

The union of the
W_{i}
and
\partial^{v}\mathbb{B}=\bigcup_{\zeta\in\partial\mathbb{D}}\left\{\zeta\right\}\times\mathbb{D}
is a lamination
by vertical graphs. By Lemma
2.1
,
by slightly perturbing the radius of the bidisk, we may assume that

\Delta
is transverse to
\partial^{v}\mathbb{B}
. If we fix a horizontal slice, for instance
L:=\mathbb{D}\times\left\{0\right\}
,
which we identify to
\mathbb{D}
, this lamination can be viewed as a holomorphic motion of

\partial\mathbb{D}\cup(\bigcup W_{i}\cap L)
, which can be extended to a motion of
\overline{\mathbb{D}}

by Slodkowski’s theorem
[
32
]
. Note that since the motion is the identity on
\partial\mathbb{D}
,
it must preserve
\mathbb{D}
.
The corresponding lamination of
\mathbb{B}
by vertical graphs fills up the whole
bidisk.

Let
\pi:\mathbb{B}\to\mathbb{D}
be the projection along this lamination. Since
\Delta
intersects any vertical graph in

d
points,
\pi\arrowvert_{\Delta}:\Delta\to\mathbb{D}
is a branched cover of degree
d
.
If we can show that this branched cover
satisfies the Riemann-Hurwitz formula, then the total number of tangencies, counting
multiplicity, is at most
(d-1)
, and the lemma follows. To prove this fact, we first note that by
Lemma
2.1
, only finitely many
W_{i}
are tangent to
\Delta
. They correspond to the
critical points of
\pi\arrowvert_{\Delta}
.
Then we argue as in the
usual proof of the Riemann-Hurwitz formula,
by pulling back a triangulation of the base whose vertex set
contains the critical values, and computing the Euler characteristic of the pulled-back triangulation (which is equal to the number of components of
\Delta
). The only delicate point is to show that at the critical points,
\pi
behaves topologically like a holomorphic map: this follows from the fact that near such a point,
\pi
is of the form

u\circ k
, where
u
is a holomorphic map and
k
is a quasiconformal homemorphism
(see
[
15
, p. 590]
).
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\Delta
be a horizontal submanifold in
\mathbb{B}
of degree
d

which is a union of holomorphic disks.
Assume that there is a vertical graph
W
in
\mathbb{B}

which is tangent to order
d-1
to
\Delta
.
Then
\Delta
has
d
vertical tangencies in
\mathbb{B}

Proof.

Fix
R<1
such that
W
is vertical in
R\mathbb{D}\times\mathbb{D}
.
Since the number of vertical tangencies of
\Delta
is finite, there exists
R\leq R^{\prime}\leq 1
such that

\Delta
is transverse to
\partial(R^{\prime}\mathbb{D})\times\mathbb{D}
. Without loss of generality we replace
\mathbb{D}
by
R^{\prime}\mathbb{D}
.
Each component of
\Delta
is a horizontal submanifold intersecting

.
Since
W\cap\Delta
admits
d
points counting multiplicities, we infer that

W\cap\Delta
is reduced to the tangency point, of multiplicity
d
. Hence

\Delta
is made of a single component, and the result follows from the Riemann-Hurwitz formula applied to the first projection.

3.
Complex geometry of homoclinic tangencies

3.1.
Conventions

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
be an open set and
f:\Omega\to f(\Omega)\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
be a diffeomorphism with a saddle fixed point
p
, with local stable and unstable manifolds

W^{s/u}_{\mathrm{loc}}(p)
(we use the notation
W^{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}(p)
for the component of

W^{s}(p)\cap\Omega
containing
p
).
In such a semi-local setting, the global stable
manifold
W^{s}(p)
is the set of points
x\in\Omega
such that
f^{n}(x)
belongs to
\Omega
for every
n\geq 0
and eventually falls into
W^{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}(p)
, and likewise for
W^{u}(p)
.

Assume that there is a tangency between
W^{s}(p)
and
W^{u}(p)
at
\tau
.
We will generally
use the following normalization: upon iteration one may assume that
\tau\in W^{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}(p)
;
we pick local coordinates
(x,y)\in\mathbb{B}

such that
p=0
,
f(x,y)=(ux,sy)+{\mathrm{h.o.t.}}
and

W^{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}(p)=\left\{x=0\right\}
, so that
\tau=(0,y_{0})
. The branch of
W^{u}(p)
tangent to

W^{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}(p)
at
\tau
, denoted
\Delta^{u}
, is locally of the form
x=\varphi(y)
, where

\varphi
a holomorphic function defined in a neighborhood of
y_{0}
, with

\varphi(y_{0})=\varphi^{\prime}(y_{0})=0
(see Figure
1
).
We say that the tangency is
of order
h
if the order of contact –which must be finite–
equals
h+1
, that is

\varphi(y)\cong(y-y_{0})^{h+1}
as
y\to y_{0}
(recall that this means
\varphi(y)\sim c(y-y_{0})^{h+1}
for some
c\neq 0
). So a quadratic tangency is a tangency of order 1.

Now let
f
depend holomorphically on a parameter
\lambda\in\Lambda
, so that all the above defined
objects depend holomorphically on
\lambda
,
and are denoted by
f_{\lambda}
,
W^{s/u}_{\lambda}
, etc.
The parameter space
\Lambda
is typically the unit disk,
but for clarity we keep the notation
\Lambda
.
The tangency parameter is denoted by
\lambda_{0}
, and without loss of generality
we assume
\lambda_{0}=0
.
Our study is local near
\lambda_{0}
, so we may reduce
\Lambda
without further notice.
Again we choose local
coordinates
(x,y)
such that
p_{\lambda}=(0,0)
and
W^{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}(p_{\lambda})=\left\{x=0\right\}
.

The tangency is
non-persistent
if for
\lambda\neq\lambda_{0}
close to
\lambda_{0}
,

\Delta^{u}_{\lambda}
is not tangent to
W^{s}_{\lambda}(p_{\lambda})
, hence in a holomorphic setting it must intersect it transversally. It is not so easy in the smooth category
to define a formal notion
of “speed of motion” for the tangency: first, when
\lambda\neq\lambda_{0}
, there is no homoclinic
tangency to work with.
A common idea is to consider vertical tangencies of
\Delta^{u}_{\lambda}
as a kind of
‘‘virtual’’ tangency, and look at the speed of motion of these tangencies (
3
3

3

Then, an additional argument would be required to show that this notion is intrinsic, in a given regularity
class for the family
(f_{\lambda})
.
).
Next, in the complex setting, when
h>1
the tangency usually splits into
h
vertical
tangencies, which may or may not be followed holomorphically. The good news is that there is
still a unequivocal notion of a
generically unfolding
or “positive speed” tangency,
as we explain in §
3.3
below.

Note that for persistent tangencies, it may be the case
that for
\lambda={\lambda_{0}}
the tangency is of order
h
, while for
\lambda\neq{\lambda_{0}}

it splits off into a persistent tangency of order
h^{\prime}<h
together with
h-h^{\prime}
vertical tangencies.

3.2.
Lifting to the projectivized tangent bundle

The following viewpoint was developed in
[
17
, §9.1]
. Consider a family of two holomorphically varying smooth complex submanifolds

(V_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
and
(W_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
in
\mathbb{B}
, with
\Lambda=\mathbb{D}
.
For every
\lambda
, we denote by
\mathbb{P}TV_{\lambda}
(resp.
\mathbb{P}TW_{\lambda}
) the lift
of
V_{\lambda}
(resp.
W_{\lambda}
) to the projectivized tangent bundle
\mathbb{P}T\mathbb{B}\simeq\mathbb{B}\times{\mathbb{P}^{1}}
. Finally, we define a 2-dimensional subvariety

\widehat{\mathbb{P}TV}
of
\Lambda\times\mathbb{B}\times{\mathbb{P}^{1}}
by putting together the
\mathbb{P}TV_{\lambda}
, that
is
\widehat{\mathbb{P}TV}\cap\left\{\lambda\right\}\times\mathbb{B}\times{\mathbb{P}^{1}}=\mathbb{P}TV_{\lambda}
, and likewise
for
\widehat{\mathbb{P}TW}
A non-persistent tangency between
V_{0}
and
W_{0}
then corresponds to an isolated intersection of
\widehat{\mathbb{P}TV}
and
\widehat{\mathbb{P}TW}
. Let us denote by
\widehat{0}
the corresponding intersection point Since in this
case

\codim_{\widehat{0}}\left(\widehat{\mathbb{P}TV}\cap\widehat{\mathbb{P}TW}\right)=\codim_{\widehat{0}}\left(\widehat{\mathbb{P}TV}\right)+\codim_{\widehat{0}}\left(\widehat{\mathbb{P}TW}\right),

this intersection is proper so it has nice properties, in particular the intersection multiplicity
\mult_{\widehat{0}}\left(\widehat{\mathbb{P}TV},\widehat{\mathbb{P}TW}\right)
is well-defined (see
[
12
, §12]
). By definition this number is the
multiplicity
of the tangency.

Lemma 3.1
.

With notation as in §
3.1
,
\widehat{\mathbb{P}TW^{s}}
and

\widehat{\mathbb{P}T\Delta^{u}}
are smooth, and

(1)

\mult_{\widehat{0}}\left(\widehat{\mathbb{P}TW^{s}},\widehat{\mathbb{P}T\Delta^{u}}\right)\geq h,

where
h
is the order of tangency.

Proof.

The multiplicity in (
1
) can be computed by intersecting two curves, as follows. Recall that we fixed local coordinates such that
W^{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}(p_{\lambda})=\left\{x=0\right\}
, and

\Delta^{u}_{\lambda}=\left\{x=\varphi_{\lambda}(y)\right\}
. Write
y=y_{0}+t
and
\varphi_{y_{0}}(\lambda,t):=\varphi(\lambda,y_{0}+t)
so that near the point of tangency,
\Delta^{u}_{\lambda}
is parameterized by
(t,\varphi_{y_{0}}(\lambda,t))
, whose tangent vector
is
(1,\dot{\varphi}_{y_{0}}(\lambda,t))
,
where the dot denotes derivation with respect to the
t
-variable. In

\mathbb{P}T\mathbb{C}^{2}\simeq\mathbb{P}^{1}
, denote by
w
the coordinate in
the affine chart containing
[1,0]
. Thus we have local coordinates

(\lambda,t,x,w)
in

\Lambda\times\mathbb{B}\times{\mathbb{P}^{1}}
, in which we have the equations

\begin{cases}x=\varphi_{y_{0}}(\lambda,t)\\
w=\dot{\varphi}_{y_{0}}(\lambda,t)\end{cases}\text{ for }\widehat{\mathbb{P}T\Delta^{u}}\ \text{ and }\begin{cases}x=0\\
w=0\end{cases}\text{ for }\widehat{\mathbb{P}TW^{s}},

and it follows that both varieties are smooth and

\mult_{\hat{0}}\left(\widehat{\mathbb{P}TW^{s}},\widehat{\mathbb{P}T\Delta^{u}}\right)
is equal to
the intersection multiplicity of the curves

\mathcal{C}:=\left\{\varphi_{y_{0}}(\lambda,t)=0\right\}
and
\dot{\mathcal{C}}:=\left\{\dot{\varphi}_{y_{0}}(\lambda,t)=0\right\}
, which
admit an isolated intersection at
(0,0)
. This is clear if we think of
the multiplicity as the number of intersections of generic local translations of

\widehat{\mathbb{P}T\Delta^{u}}
and
\widehat{\mathbb{P}TW^{s}}
(see
[
12
, §12]
).
Recall that the classical definition of multiplicity of the intersection of two curves
is
\mult_{0}(\mathcal{C},\dot{\mathcal{C}})=\dim\mathcal{O}({\mathbb{C}^{2}},0)/\langle\varphi_{y_{0}},\dot{\varphi}_{y_{0}}\rangle
.
In the situation at hand we have

(2)

\begin{cases}\varphi_{y_{0}}(\lambda,t)=ct^{h+1}+O({t^{h+2}})+O\left(\lambda\right)\\
\dot{\varphi}_{y_{0}}(\lambda,t)=cht^{h}+O({t^{h+1}})+O\left(\lambda\right)\end{cases}

hence

\mathcal{O}({\mathbb{C}^{2}},0)/\langle\varphi_{y_{0}},\dot{\varphi}_{y_{0}}\rangle
contains all monomials

1,t,\ldots,t^{h-1}
and its dimension is at least
h
.
∎

3.3.
Speed of motion

We now explain how the multiplicity defined above takes into account both the order
and the speed of motion of the tangency. Recall from the proof of Lemma
3.1

that the multiplicity of tangency equals

\mult_{0}(\mathcal{C},\dot{\mathcal{C}})
, where

\mathcal{C}=\left\{\varphi_{y_{0}}(\lambda,t)=0\right\}
and
\dot{\mathcal{C}}=\left\{\dot{\varphi}_{y_{0}}(\lambda,t)=0\right\}
.

For simplicity , let us first assume that
\dot{\mathcal{C}}
is irreducible at
(0,0)
. For a
fixed small
\lambda\neq 0
, the equation
\dot{\varphi}_{y_{0}}(\lambda,t)=0
admits
h
solutions
(that is, there are
h
vertical tangencies)
so
\dot{\mathcal{C}}
can be parameterized by a Puiseux series in
t^{1/h}
.
In usual complex geometric language, there is a coordinate
\mu
on the normalization
of
{\dot{\mathcal{C}}}
such that the expression of the composition of the
normalization map and the first projection
(\lambda,t)\mapsto\lambda
is

\mu\mapsto\mu^{h}
, hence
\dot{\mathcal{C}}
admits a local (injective)
parameterization of the form

\mu\mapsto(\mu^{h},\theta(\mu))
, for some holomorphic function
\theta
with

\theta(0)=0
.
Writing

(3)

\varphi_{y_{0}}(\lambda,t)=ct^{h+1}+O({t^{h+2}})+\lambda\psi(\lambda,t)

and
substituting, we infer that

(4)

\varphi_{y_{0}}(\mu^{h},\theta(\mu))=c\theta(\mu)^{h+1}+O({\theta(\mu)^{h+2}})+\mu^{h}\psi(\mu^{h},\theta(\mu)),

and the multiplicity
m
of tangency is the order of vanishing of this expression at
\mu=0
(since the tangency is non-persistent,
\mu\mapsto\varphi_{y_{0}}(\mu^{h},\theta(\mu))
has an isolated zero at the origin).
This yields another proof that
m\geq h
, with equality if and only if

\psi(0,0)\neq 0
, that is,

(5)

\varphi_{y_{0}}(\lambda,t)=ct^{h+1}+d\lambda+{\mathrm{h.o.t.}},\text{ with }d=\psi(0,0).

In the language of Puiseux series, this reads

(6)

\varphi_{y_{0}}(\lambda,\theta(\lambda^{1/h}))=c\theta(\lambda^{1/h})^{h+1}+O({\theta(\lambda^{1/h})^{h+2}})+\lambda\psi(\lambda,\theta(\lambda^{1/h})).

The speed of motion
\sigma
of the vertical tangencies is characterized by the
exponent of
\lambda
in
\varphi_{y_{0}}(\lambda,\theta(\lambda^{1/h}))
,
that is,
\varphi_{y_{0}}(\lambda,\theta(\lambda^{1/h}))\cong\lambda^{\sigma}
, where

\sigma=m/h\geq 1
(which is typically not an integer),
and we see that
non-vanishing speed of motion at
\lambda=0

(i.e. non-degenerate unfolding)
corresponds to
m=h
, as expected from (
5
).
Note that for quadratic tangencies (
h=1
), this simply
means that
\widehat{\mathbb{P}TW^{s}}
and
\widehat{\mathbb{P}T\Delta^{u}}
are transverse at
\widehat{0}
.

Beware, however, that non-vanishing speed of motion (i.e.
\sigma=1
)
does
not

imply that the vertical tangencies can be followed holomorphically, as shown for instance
by the example
\varphi_{y_{0}}(\lambda,t)=t^{h+1}+\lambda(1+t)
, for
which the abscissae of vertical tangencies are given by
\lambda+c\lambda^{1+1/h}
for some
explicit
c
.

In the general case where
\dot{\mathcal{C}}
is reducible, write

\dot{\varphi}_{y_{0}}(\lambda,t)\cong\prod_{j=1}^{q}\xi_{j}(\lambda,t)
(up to some invertible element of

\mathcal{O}({\mathbb{C}^{2}},0)
), where
\xi_{j}(0,t)=t^{h_{j}}+{\mathrm{h.o.t.}}
, and
\sum_{j}h_{j}=h
. Each branch

\dot{\mathcal{C}}_{j}
is injectively parameterized by
\mu\mapsto(\mu^{h_{j}},\theta_{j}(\mu))
,
\theta_{j}(0)=0
,
and substituting this expression in
\varphi_{y_{0}}
as in (
4
)
gives

(7)

\varphi_{y_{0}}(\mu^{h_{j}},\theta_{j}(\mu))=c\theta_{j}(\mu)^{h+1}+O({\theta_{j}(\mu)^{h+2}})+\mu^{h_{j}}\psi(\mu^{h_{j}},\theta_{j}(\mu)).

This shows that
m_{j}\geq h_{j}
, where

m_{j}=\mult_{0}(\mathcal{C},\dot{\mathcal{C}}_{j})
, with equality if and only if
\psi(0,0)\neq 0
(this condition does not depend on
j
), and

\sigma_{j}:=m_{j}/h_{j}

is the speed exponent of the block of
h_{j}

tangencies corresponding to
\dot{\mathcal{C}}_{j}
. By the additivity of multiplicity, we conclude that

m=\sum_{j=1}^{q}h_{j}\sigma_{j}
. Combining this relation with
\sum_{j}h_{j}=h
, shows that

m=h
if and only if

\sigma_{j}=1
for every
j
, so again
the equality
m=h
characterizes a non-degenerate unfolding
.

3.4.
Secondary intersections

The following result is specific to complex diffeomorphisms. Note that for polynomial automorphisms
of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
it also follows from global arguments (see
[
4
, §9]
).

Proposition 3.2
.

If
f:\Omega\to{\mathbb{C}^{2}}
is a diffeomorphism with a homoclinic tangency associated to
p
,
then there are also
transverse homoclinic intersections between
W^{s}(p)
and
W^{u}(p)
.

To get an intuition of what is going on,
let us explain the argument (which is classical) in the oversimplified case
where the dynamics in linearizable at
p
. In this case we can simply write
f(x,y)=(ux,sy)
. With notation as in §
3.1
we have a branch
\Delta^{u}
of
W^{u}(p)
tangent to

W_{\mathrm{loc}}^{s}(p)
at
(0,y_{0})
, with equation
x=\varphi(y)\cong(y-y_{0})^{h+1}
. Pulling back
this pair of curves by for some
iterate
f^{k}
, we get a branch
\Delta^{s}\subset W^{s}(p)
tangent
to
W^{u}_{\mathrm{loc}}(p)
at
(x_{0},0)
, with equation
y=\psi(x)\cong(x-x_{0})^{h+1}
. If
(x,y)\in\Delta^{u}
with

\left|x\right|\ll 1
, then a tangent vector to
\Delta^{u}
at
(x,y)
is

v\asymp(x^{h/(h+1)},1)
, whose slope is
\asymp x^{-h/(h+1)}
. Likewise, if

(x,y)\in\Delta^{s}
with
\left|y\right|\ll 1
, then the slope of
\Delta^{s}
at
(x,y)
is

\asymp y^{h/(h+1)}
.
Now let us assume for the moment that some version of the argument principle guarantees that
f^{n}(\Delta^{u})
intersects
\Delta^{s}
for large
n
, so there exists

(x_{0},y_{0})\in\Delta^{u}
such that

(x_{n},y_{n})=(u^{n}x_{0},s^{n}y_{0})\in\Delta^{s}
. Then with notation as above,
v\asymp(u^{-nh/(h+1)},1)
so its image under
df^{n}
is
\asymp(u^{n}u^{-nh/(h+1)},s^{n})

whose slope is
s^{n}u^{-nh/(h+1)}
. On the other hand the slope of
T\Delta^{s}
at

(x_{n},y_{n})
is
\asymp s^{nh/(h+1)}\gg s^{n}u^{-nh/(h+1)}
so

f^{n}\Delta^{u}
is transverse to
\Delta^{s}
. Making this argument rigorous in the non-linearizable case is quite technical, and it is remarkable that in the complex setting
all these estimates can be replaced by geometric analysis considerations.

Proof.

We keep notation as above, and use the formalism of
horizontal/vertical objects
and crossed mappings from
[
23
]

(or Hénon-like mappings of degree 1 in the language of

[
14
]
).
Consider a thin vertical bidisk around
W^{s}(p)
of the form

D(0,\delta)\times\mathbb{D}
. Then for small enough
\delta
,
\Delta^{u}
is a horizontal disk of degree
h+1
in
D(0,\delta)\times\mathbb{D}
. Likewise, reducing
\delta
if necessary,

\Delta^{s}
is a vertical disk of degree
h+1
in
\mathbb{D}\times D(0,\delta)
.
By the Inclination lemma, for large
n
,

f^{n}
defines a crossed mapping of degree 1 from

D(0,\delta)\times\mathbb{D}
to
\mathbb{D}\times D(0,\delta)
. Therefore the graph transform

\mathcal{L}^{n}\Delta^{u}
of
\Delta^{u}
(that is, its image under the crossed mapping) is
a horizontal disk of degree
h+1
in
\mathbb{D}\times D(0,\delta)
, so it intersects
\Delta^{s}
in

(h+1)^{2}
points, counting multiplicities. We claim that for large
n
all these intersections are transverse. Indeed when
\delta
is small enough,
\Delta^{s}\cap(\mathbb{D}\times D(0,\delta))
is contained
in
D(x_{0},r)\times D(0,\delta)
, where
r<\left|x_{0}\right|/2
. Now, since
\mathcal{L}^{n}\Delta^{u}

admits a tangency of order
h
with

W^{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}(p)
and since by the maximum principle it is a topological disk, by the
Riemann-Hurwitz formula it admits no other vertical tangency (see also Lemma
2.2
). So

\mathcal{L}^{n}\Delta^{u}\cap(D(x_{0},r)\times D(0,\delta))
is the union of
h+1
horizontal graphs disjoint from

D(x_{0},r)\times\left\{0\right\}
, and close to it. Then by Lemma
2.1
these graphs must be transverse to
\Delta^{s}
, and we are done.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

Since there are homoclinic intersections,

W^{u}(p)
must accumulate itself. In particular there are disks contained in
W^{u}(p)
arbitrary
C^{1}
close to
\Delta^{u}
. Applying Lemma
2.1
again then produces transverse
intersections between these disks and
W^{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}(p)
, such that the angle
between the tangent spaces at the intersection is arbitrary small. This observation will be crucial later.

3.5.
Comments on higher dimensional families

Still working under the conventions of §
3.1
, assume in this paragraph only
that
k:=\dim(\Lambda)>1
. Let
\mathcal{T}\subset\Lambda
be the locus of tangency between

W^{s}_{\lambda}
and
\Delta^{u}_{\lambda}
. Our purpose is to study the basic properties of
\mathcal{T}
.

Arguing exactly as in Lemma
3.1
, we see that

\widehat{\mathbb{P}TW^{s}}
and
\widehat{\mathbb{P}T\Delta^{u}}
are smooth and of codimension 2 in

\Lambda\times\mathbb{B}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
, so
[
12
, §3.5]

\dim(\widehat{\mathbb{P}TW^{s}}\cap\widehat{\mathbb{P}T\Delta^{u}})\geq 2(k+1)-(k+3)=k-1
.
It is clear that the natural projection

\pi_{\Lambda}:\Lambda\times\mathbb{B}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}\to\Lambda
is finite and locally proper in restriction to

\widehat{\mathbb{P}TW^{s}}\cap\widehat{\mathbb{P}T\Delta^{u}}
, so
it preserves dimensions
[
12
, §3.3]
and
\mathcal{T}
is an analytic subset of dimension
at least
k-1
.
Since the tangency is not persistent,
\dim(\mathcal{T})<k
, and we conclude that

\dim(\mathcal{T})=k-1
, that is,

\mathcal{T}
is an analytic hypersurface
.

Slightly abusing terminology, we say that
the homoclinic tangency has
positive speed
if there exists a smooth 1-dimensional subfamily

\Lambda_{1}\ni\lambda_{0}
along which this property holds. Then, in the 4-dimensional subspace

\Lambda_{1}\times\mathbb{B}\times{\mathbb{P}^{1}}
, the
intersection
\widehat{\mathbb{P}TW^{s}}\cap\widehat{\mathbb{P}T\Delta^{u}}

(which is reduced to a point) is transverse.
Counting dimensions in the tangent space, it is easy to see that
it implies the corresponding transversality in
\Lambda\times\mathbb{B}\times{\mathbb{P}^{1}}
. And since
the fibers of the projections
\pi_{\Lambda}
and
\pi_{\Lambda_{1}}
coincide, we also deduce that
in
\Lambda\times\mathbb{B}\times{\mathbb{P}^{1}}
,
\widehat{\mathbb{P}TW^{s}}\cap\widehat{\mathbb{P}T\Delta^{u}}
is transverse to

\pi_{\Lambda}^{-1}(\left\{{\lambda_{0}}\right\})
. This implies that
if the unfolding has positive speed,

\mathcal{T}
is smooth at
{\lambda_{0}}
.

4.
Unfolding degenerate tangencies

4.1.
Linearization and graph transform estimates

As in
[
36
, §3]
, a key technical fact in the argument of Theorem
A.1
is that in the graph transform,
higher derivatives converge faster and faster to zero. This is obvious for the linear map

f(x,y)=(ux,sy)
: indeed the forward iterate of the graph
y=\gamma(x)
is
y=\mathcal{L}^{n}\gamma(x)
,
with
\mathcal{L}^{n}\gamma:x\mapsto s^{n}\gamma(x/u^{n})
so
\|(\mathcal{L}^{n}\gamma)^{(\ell)}\|=O((su^{-\ell})^{n})
.
It is unclear to us whether such an
estimate holds in general, and to achieve this, as in
[
36
]

we use
C^{\ell}
linearization, which
imposes some conditions on the multipliers
u
and
s
.
Even if this belongs to real dynamics, we want to salvage as much complex geometry as possible
–in particular we need to be able to talk about the
complex
multipliers
u
and
s
, and not only their moduli, which is important in the parameter exclusion in §
4.3
–
so the presentation is different from that of Takens (and since this matter is quite delicate
we actually give more details).
In particular we will not switch between different coordinate systems in the proof of the main theorem,
and always stay in holomorphic coordinates,
which in our opinion makes the argument neater.

An important remark is that to achieve

\|(\mathcal{L}^{n}\gamma)^{(j)}\|=O((su^{-j})^{n})
for all
j\leq\ell
it is not enough to merely know the existence of
a system of
C^{\ell}
linearizing coordinates: we also need this coordinate system
to be flat up to a high order
K=K(\ell)
along the separatrices, to
prevent lower derivatives of the chart to spoil the estimate

\|(\mathcal{L}^{n}\gamma)^{(j)}\|=O((su^{-j})^{n})
.
Finally, we need some uniformity of these estimates with respect to parameters.

4.1.1.
Normal form

The first stage is to put
f
in an appropriate normal form, under a non-resonance condition.
Denote by
\mathfrak{M}
the maximal ideal of the local ring of germs of holomorphic functions in
({\mathbb{C}^{2}},0)
,
that is, the
ideal of functions vanishing at the origin.
Recall that if
f(x,y)=(ux,sy)+{\mathrm{h.o.t.}}
, a
resonance of order
k
is a relation of the form

u=u^{a}s^{b}
or
s=u^{a}s^{b}
, where
a
and
b
are positive integers with
a+b=k
. When

\left|s\right|<1<\left|u\right|
, this can be rewritten as
u^{a}s^{b}=1
, with
a+b=k-1
.
It is well-known that resonances are obstructions to holomorphic (and even formal) linearization.
More precisely, a resonance of the form

u=u^{a}s^{b}
(resp.
s=u^{a}s^{b}
)
prevents from killing the term
x^{a}y^{b}
in the first (resp. second)
component of
f
. Conversely, if
f
has no resonance up to order
k
,
a holomorphic (actually polynomial) change of coordinates brings
f
to the form

(8)

f(x,y)=(ux+g_{1}(x,y),sy+g_{2}(x,y)),\text{ with }g_{1},g_{2}\in\mathfrak{M}^{k+1}.

The following more precise normal form is presumably
known to some experts. We include the proof for completeness.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
f\in\Diff({\mathbb{C}^{2}},0)
with a saddle fixed point at the origin,
with eigenvalues
u
and
s
. If there is no resonance up to order
k+1
,
f
can be brought to the form

(
\star_{k}
)

f(x,y)=(ux(1+yg_{1}(x,y)),sy(1+xg_{2}(x,y))),\text{ with }g_{1},g_{2}\in\mathfrak{M}^{k}.

by a holomorphic change of coordinates.

Proof.

We review the proof of the existence of the local stable and unstable manifolds, by checking that the corresponding change of coordinates are sufficiently tangent to identity at the origin. Let us first deal with the local unstable manifold. We follow Sternberg
[
33
, Thms 7 to 9, §9]
. To stick with the notation of
[
33
, p. 823]
,
we put
T=f^{-1}
. Thanks to the non-resonance assumption, we can assume that

(9)

T(x,y)=(u^{-1}x+\tilde{g}_{1}(x,y),s^{-1}y+\tilde{g}_{2}(x,y)),\text{ with }\tilde{g}_{1},\tilde{g}_{2}\in\mathfrak{M}^{k+2}.

We first look for a change of coordinates
R:(x,y)\mapsto(x,y-\phi(x))
with
\phi^{\prime}(0)=0

such that
RTR^{-1}(x,0)=(*,0)
, so that the axis
\left\{y=0\right\}
is invariant, hence it is
the local stable manifold of
T
(unstable manifold of
f
). The existence (and uniqueness) of such a
\phi
is guaranteed by the Stable Manifold Theorem. Here we only need to check that
\phi(x)=O(x^{k+2})
.
With
T
as in (
9
),
the relation
RTR^{-1}(x,0)=(*,0)
is equivalent to

(10)

s^{-1}\phi(x)+\tilde{g}_{2}(x,\phi(x))=\phi(u^{-1}x+\tilde{g}_{1}(x,\phi(x))).

In other words,
\phi
is a fixed point of the operator
\mathcal{D}
defined by

(11)

\mathcal{D}\phi:x\longmapsto s\left(\phi(u^{-1}x+\tilde{g}_{1}(x,\phi(x)))-\tilde{g}_{2}(x,\phi(x))\right).

This is a contracting operator in a suitable Banach space of holomorphic functions on
r\mathbb{D}
for small
r
, and since
g_{1},g_{2}\in\mathfrak{M}^{k+2}
, it preserves the closed subspace of functions vanishing to order

k+2
, and the fixed point belongs to this subspace, as desired.

Then we do the same with the stable direction, by a change of coordinate of the form
(x,y)\mapsto(x-\psi(y),y)
, therefore we have shown that there is a change of coordinate tangent to the identity to the order
k+1
such that the stable and unstable manifold are the coordinate axes. In these new coordinates,
f
is of the form

(12)

f(x,y)=(f_{1}(x,y),f_{2}(x,y))=(ux(1+h_{1}(x,y)),sy(1+h_{2}(x,y)),\text{ with }h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathfrak{M}^{k+1}.

To reach the desired form
(\star_{k})
we linearize
f
inside
W^{s/u}_{\mathrm{loc}}(0)
.
More precisely, we consider the one-dimensional map
h:x\mapsto f_{1}(x,0)=ux(1+h_{1}(x,0))=ux+O(x^{k+2})
. By Koenigs’ theorem it is locally conjugate to
x\mapsto ux
by some
local diffeomorphism
\phi
. Moreover, examining the proof (see e.g.
[
26
]
) reveals that

\phi(x)=x+O(x^{k+2})
. So if we conjugate
f
by
(x,y)\mapsto(\phi(x),y)
, in the new coordinates we get

(13)

f(x,y)=(ux(1+\tilde{h}_{1}(x,y)),sy(1+\tilde{h}_{2}(x,y)),\text{ with }\tilde{h}_{1},\tilde{h}_{2}\in\mathfrak{M}^{k+1},

and furthermore
\tilde{h}_{1}(x,0)=0
, hence
\tilde{h}_{1}(x,y)=y\hat{h}_{1}(x,y)
, with

\hat{h}_{1}(x,y)\in\mathfrak{M}^{k}
. Repeating this operation in the stable direction (which does not affect the form
of the first coordinate of
f
) concludes the proof.
∎

Proposition 4.2
.

For every integer
\ell\geq 2
, there exists
k=k(\ell,\rho)
such that if

f
is a diffeomorphism defined in
2\mathbb{B}
of the form
(\star_{k})
satisfying
(
14
) and (
15
), then
there exists a constant
C=C(\ell,\rho,\left\|g_{1}\right\|_{2\mathbb{B}},\left\|g_{1}\right\|_{2\mathbb{B}})

such that for every
j\leq\ell
,
\left\|(\mathcal{L}^{n}\gamma)^{(j)}\right\|_{\mathbb{D}}\leq C\left|su^{-j}\right|^{n}
.

Proof.

The proof proceeds by constructing a
C^{r}
-diffeomorphism linearizing
f
, which is
tangent to the identity up to order
r
along the axes, for a sufficiently large
r
. In a first stage we estimate the required value of
r
, and then we follow Sternberg
[
34
]
to show that if
k
is large enough, such a linearization exists.

Step 1. Estimation of the order of differentiability
r
.

Here we show that there exists
r=r(\ell,\rho)
such that
if there exists a
C^{r}
-diffeomorphism
R
whose image contains a neighborhood
of
\overline{\mathbb{D}}\times\left\{0\right\}
,
linearizing
f
, i.e.

R\circ L=f\circ R
with
L(x,y)=(ux,sy)
(for convenience the
notation here is as in
[
34
]
, except that
f
is denoted by
T
there), and
which is tangent to the identity up to order
r
along

\left\{y=0\right\}
, then
\left\|(\mathcal{L}^{n}\gamma)^{(j)}\right\|_{\mathbb{D}}\leq C\left|su^{-j}\right|^{n}
for every
j\leq\ell
.

Write

R=(R_{1},R_{2})
and denote by
\pi_{1}
,
\pi_{2}
the coordinate projections, so that
R_{1}-\pi_{1}
and
R_{2}-\pi_{2}
have vanishing first
r
derivatives along
\left\{y=0\right\}
.
Start with
R^{-1}(\left\{y=\gamma(x)\right\})
which is of the form
y=\psi(x)

and iterate
L
to get
a sequence of graphs
y=\psi_{n}(x)=s^{n}\psi(xu^{-n})
, whose image under
R

is
\left\{y=\gamma_{n}(x)\right\}
, where
\gamma_{n}=\mathcal{L}^{n}\gamma
.
Unwinding the definitions gives

R_{2}(x,\psi_{n}(x))=\gamma_{n}(R_{1}(x,\psi_{n}(x)))
,
and we have to estimate the derivatives of
\gamma_{n}
.
A caveat is in order here:
x
is a complex variable and
\gamma_{n}
is holomorphic,
but
R
is not, so formally we have to write
x=x_{1}+ix_{2}
and deal with the partial derivatives

\partial_{x_{1}}^{i_{1}}\partial_{x_{2}}^{i_{2}}
.
For notational ease, we not dwell on this point and do as if everything was holomorphic
(this does not change the structure of the estimates).

Write
\widetilde{\gamma}_{n}=\gamma_{n}(R_{1}(x,\psi_{n}(x)))
and let us estimate the derivatives of

\widetilde{\gamma}_{n}-\psi_{n}
. The
j^{\rm th}
-derivative of

\widetilde{\gamma}_{n}(x)-\psi_{n}=(R_{2}-\pi_{2})(x,\psi_{n}(x))
is a sum of terms involving
the partial derivatives of
R_{2}-\pi_{2}
multiplied by
polynomial expressions in the derivatives of
\psi_{n}

(which can be
computed exactly using the Faa Di Bruno formula). Analyzing this expression and using

\partial^{j}(R_{2}-\pi_{2})(x,y)=O({y}^{r-j})
and
\psi_{n}^{(j)}=O((su^{-j})^{n})
, it is not
difficult to convince oneself that the dominant term is the one obtained
by differentiating with respect to the
first variable (i.e. the first two real variables) of
(R_{2}-\pi_{2})
, that is, the only term which comes with no additional
multiplicative factor. This term
is of order of magnitude
O(\left|\psi_{n}\right|^{r-j})=O(\left|s\right|^{n(r-j)})
, and we conclude that

\big|{\widetilde{\gamma}_{n}^{(j)}-\psi_{n}^{(j)}}\big|\lesssim\left|s\right|^{n(r-j)}
.

We now choose the order
r
such that for every
j\leq\ell
,
{s}^{n(r-j)}=o((su^{-j})^{n})
. For this it is enough that

\left|s\right|^{r-j}<\left|su^{-j}\right|
, that is
\left|s\right|^{r}<\left|s(su^{-1})^{j}\right|
, hence it suffices that

r>(1+\alpha)\ell+1
, where
\alpha
is such that
\left|u\right|^{-1}=\left|s\right|^{\alpha}
, and the choice

(16)

r(\ell,\rho)=2+\left(1+\frac{\ln(1+\rho^{-1})}{\ln(1+\rho)}\right)\ell

works.

Now let us estimate
\big|{\gamma_{n}^{(j)}}\big|
. Recall that
\widetilde{\gamma}_{n}=\gamma_{n}(R_{1}(x,\psi_{n}(x)))
, and that
at this stage we know that
\big|{\widetilde{\gamma}_{n}^{(j)}}\big|=O((su^{-j})^{n})
. Write
h_{n}(x)=R_{1}(x,\psi_{n}(x))
. This is
a diffeomorphism such that
h_{n}(x)-x=O(s^{rn})
. Arguing as above shows that

\partial h_{n}(x)=1+O(s^{n(r-1)})
and for
2\leq j\leq r
,
\partial^{j}h_{n}(x)=O(s^{n(r-j)})
. It follows that the inverse diffeomorphism

h_{n}^{-1}
satisfies the same estimates. Indeed, the expression for the
j^{\rm th}
partial derivatives of
h_{n}^{-1}
is a rational function whose denominator is a power of
\partial h_{n}(y)
,
y=h_{n}^{-1}(x)
,
and whose numerator is a polynomial expression in
the
\partial^{i}h_{n}(y)
,
i\leq j
, with a single term of order
j
. Plugging in the estimate
\partial^{i}h_{n}(x)=O(s^{n(r-i)})

shows that
\partial^{j}h_{n}^{-1}(x)=O(s^{n(r-j)})
.

Now we write
\gamma_{n}=\widetilde{\gamma}_{n}\circ h_{n}^{-1}
.
Taking the
j^{\rm th}
derivative of this expression gives a sum of terms of the form

P_{i}(\partial h_{n}^{-1},\ldots,\partial^{j}h_{n}^{-1})\partial^{i}\widetilde{\gamma}_{n}(h_{n}^{-1})
, with

i\leq j
. When
i=j
,
P_{i}
depends only on
\partial h_{n}^{-1}
, so this term is of order of magnitude
O(\partial^{j}\widetilde{\gamma}_{n})=O((su^{-j})^{n})
, and all the other terms involve higher derivatives of

h_{n}^{-1}
, so they are bounded by
O(s^{n(r-j)})
. Our choice of
r
guarantees that
s^{n(r-j)}=o((su^{-j})^{n})
so we conclude
that
\big|{\gamma_{n}^{(j)}}\big|\leq C\left|su^{-j}\right|^{n}
, as announced.

Step 2. Construction of a
C^{r}
-linearization.

We follow step by step the proof of Theorem 1 pp. 628-629 in
[
34
]

to show that there exists

such that if

is of the form
(\star_{k})
and satisfies (

) and (

4.2.
Dynamical slope

In
[
36
, §3.4]
, Takens defines a notion of
angle of crossing
, that we prefer to call
dynamical slope
, whose purpose is to make the estimates in the Inclination Lemma more precise, and plays an important role in the main argument. To understand the idea, let us consider the real
linear case:
if
f(x,y)=(ux,sy)
and
v
is a tangent vector at
(0,y_{0})
,
y_{0}\neq 0
,
with slope
m
, then its
n^{\rm th}

image is a tangent vector at
(0,y_{n})=(0,s^{n}y_{0})
with slope
m_{n}=(su^{-1})^{n}m
.
So if
\alpha>1
is such that
su^{-1}=s^{\alpha}
, we see that
{m_{n}}{y_{n}^{-\alpha}}

is an invariant quantity, which by definition is the dynamical slope. In particular, if the dynamical slope is non-zero, then
m_{n}\cong(su^{-1})^{n}
.
This definition can be extended to the non-linear case by linearization,
under an appropriate non-resonance
assumption.

In the complex setting, we cannot directly extend this definition (because
y_{n}^{-\alpha}
does not make sense),
so we content ourselves with explaining that there is a well-defined notion of having non-zero dynamical slope, which suffices for our needs (see Remark
4.6
for further discussion).

For concreteness, assume that
f\in\Diff(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
is of the form
(\star_{k})
and consider the action
of
f
(denoted by
f_{\varstar}
)
on projective tangent vectors
[v]\in\mathbb{P}T{\mathbb{C}^{2}}\arrowvert_{W^{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}(0)}
. If
[v]=[v_{1}:v_{2}]
is not tangent
to
W^{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}(0)=\left\{x=0\right\}
its slope is by definition
\slope([v])=v_{2}/v_{1}
. We work under a non-resonance condition, and to allow for some uniformity it is convenient to express it in terms
of the constant
\rho
of (
14
).

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
f\in\Diff(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
be of the form
(\star_{k})
for some
k\geq 1
,
with a saddle point at the origin, and assume
that there is no resonance up to order

k^{\prime}(\rho)=2+\frac{\ln(1+\rho^{-1})}{\ln(1+\rho)}
, where
\rho
is as in (
14
).

Then there exists an invariant holomorphic section
Z
of the bundle
of
\mathbb{P}T\mathbb{C}^{2}\arrowvert_{W^{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}(0)}\to W^{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}(0)
, disjoint from
[TW^{s}]
,
such that if

[v]\in\mathbb{P}T\mathbb{C}^{2}\arrowvert_{W^{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}(0)}
neither belongs to the image of
Z
nor to
[TW^{s}]
, then

\slope(f_{\varstar}^{n}[v])\cong(u^{-1}s)^{n}
.

By definition
Z
is the
zero dynamical slope section
. Since
Z
is uniformly transverse to

W^{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}
, it follows that any tangent vector which is sufficiently close to
TW^{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}
, but not tangent to it, has non-zero dynamical slope.

Proof.

We study the dynamics of
f_{\varstar}
on
\mathbb{P}T\mathbb{C}^{2}\arrowvert_{W^{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}(0)}
, which is a
\mathbb{P}^{1}
-bundle over the disk. The “central fiber”
\mathbb{P}T_{0}{\mathbb{C}^{2}}
is globally attracting and contains two fixed points, one saddle

(0,[E^{s}])
corresponding to the stable direction and one attracting
(0,[E^{u}])

corresponding to the unstable direction. Fix local coordinates
(y,m)
near
(0,[E^{u}])

(
m
is the slope); the action of
f_{\varstar}

is
f_{\varstar}(y,m)=(sy,u^{-1}sm)+{\mathrm{h.o.t.}}

The non-resonance assumption implies
that there cannot be any resonance of the form
s^{k}=(su^{-1})
for
k\leq 1+\frac{\ln\left|u\right|}{\ln\left|s^{-1}\right|}
so there is no resonance at all since for higher values of
k
we have

\left|s\right|^{k}<\left|su^{-1}\right|
.
It follows that the attracting fixed point
(0,[E^{u}])
is holomorphically linearizable.
In the linearizing coordinates, the dynamics becomes
L:(y^{\prime},m^{\prime})\mapsto(sy^{\prime},(su^{-1})m^{\prime})
, so if

m^{\prime}\neq 0
, the second coordinate of
L^{n}(y^{\prime},m^{\prime})
decays like
c(su^{-1})^{n}
with
c\neq 0
. Back to the intial coordinates, we see that there is an
invariant holomorphic curve
Z=W^{ws}(0,[E^{u}])
transverse to the central fiber (associated to the “slow” eigenvalue
s
) such that if

(y,m)\notin Z
then
m_{n}\cong(su^{-1})^{n}
. Thus we have defined the announced
section
Z
in some neighborhood of
(0,[E^{u}])
, and we extend it to
\mathbb{P}T\mathbb{C}^{2}\arrowvert_{W^{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}(0)}

by pulling back. Finally, to conclude the proof, it is enough to observe that
any
v\in\mathbb{P}T\mathbb{C}^{2}\arrowvert_{W^{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}(0)}
not
tangent to
\left\{x=0\right\}
is eventually attracted by the unstable direction, so the previous analysis applies.
∎

Remark 4.6
.

Even if it is not clear how to define the dynamical slope as a number in the complex case,
the notion of two tangent directions
having the same dynamical slope does make sense. Indeed, with notation as in the previous proof, we can pull back the foliation
\left\{m^{\prime}=\mathrm{C}^{\mathrm{st}}\right\}
by the linearizing coordinates, which defines sections of constant dynamical slope in
\mathbb{P}T\mathbb{C}^{2}\arrowvert_{W^{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}(0)}
. Still, it is not obvious to decide whether two
tangent directions at different points of
W^{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}(0)
have the same slope or not. Since the idea of taking distinct slopes plays an important role in
[
36
]
, we have to modify the concluding
argument in the proof of Proposition
4.8
so that considering one non-zero slope is sufficient.

Unfolding the tangency thus locally produces countably many disjoint

Since
\bigcup_{n}\mathcal{T}_{n}

by

{\lambda_{0}}

Lemma 5.6
.

The Jacobian
\jac(f_{\lambda})
is not constant along
\mathcal{T}
.

Proof.

Indeed by assumption
\frac{\ln\left|u_{\lambda}(p)\right|}{\ln\left|s_{\lambda}(p)\right|}=c
along
\mathcal{T}
. Since
f_{\lambda}
is dissipative, it follows that
c\in(-1,0)
. Assume by contradiction that
\jac(f_{\lambda})\equiv j
on

\mathcal{T}
. Then from
u_{\lambda}s_{\lambda}=j
we get

\frac{\ln\left|u_{\lambda}(p)\right|}{\ln\left|j/u_{\lambda}(p)\right|}=c\text{, hence }\ln\left|u_{\lambda}(p)\right|=\frac{c}{1+c}\ln\left|j\right|,

and it follows that

\left|u_{\lambda}(p)\right|
is constant along
\mathcal{T}
, hence so is
u_{\lambda}(p)
. Likewise,

s_{\lambda}(p)
is constant along
\mathcal{T}
because

u_{\lambda}s_{\lambda}=j
. This is a contradiction
because
\lambda\mapsto(s_{\lambda}(p),u_{\lambda}(p))
has rank 2 and
\mathcal{T}
has codimension 1.
∎

The same reasoning shows that
u_{\lambda}(p)
is not constant along
\mathcal{T}
. Indeed otherwise
\ln\left|s_{\lambda}\right|

would be constant, hence so would be

s_{\lambda}
, leading to the same contradiction. Therefore, moving
{\lambda_{0}}
slightly and reducing
U
again,
we may assume that
u_{\lambda}(p)\notin\mathbb{R}
for every
\lambda\in U
.
Recall the set of disjoint hypersurfaces
\mathcal{T}_{n}
in
U
constructed above.

Lemma 5.7
.

\bigcup_{n}\mathcal{T}_{n}
is
\mathbb{R}
-Zariski dense in
U
.

Proof.

We start by showing that for every
\lambda\in U
,

\bigcup_{n}\Gamma_{n,\lambda}
is
\mathbb{R}
-Zariski dense in
\mathbb{B}
. For this, it is enough to show that
for any
y\in\mathbb{D}
,
E^{s}_{\lambda}(y):=W^{s}_{\mathbb{B}}(E)\cap(\mathbb{D}\times\left\{y\right\})
is
\mathbb{R}
-Zariski dense in
\mathbb{D}\times\left\{y\right\}
. Identify

\mathbb{D}\times\left\{y\right\}
with
\mathbb{D}
. Since
u_{\lambda}(p)\notin\mathbb{R}
and
\bigcup_{n}\Gamma_{n,\lambda}
is dense in
W^{s}(E_{\lambda})
which is invariant,

F(y)
contains a sequence of points spiraling to 0 (here we use the fact that we have a sequence of graphs in
W^{s}(E_{\lambda})
of the form
x=\alpha u^{-n}+o(u^{-n})
). Since
E
is self similar, there is also such a spiral at each point of

\Gamma_{n,\lambda}\cap(\mathbb{D}\times\left\{y\right\})
. Thus
E^{s}_{\lambda}(y)
cannot be contained in a real-analytic curve, because such a curve would have to be smooth at one of the points of

\Gamma_{n,\lambda}
which is impossible because of the spiraling phenomenon.

To complete the proof,
consider any one-parameter family
\Lambda_{1}
in
U
in which the tangency unfolds with positive speed.
It is enough to show that

\bigcup_{n}\mathcal{T}_{n}\cap\Lambda_{1}
is
\mathbb{R}
-Zariski dense in
\Lambda_{1}
.
We use the basic idea of homoclinic renormalization theory, which says that, near any of its points,

\bigcup_{n}\mathcal{T}_{n}\cap\Lambda_{1}

(which is now a countable set) contains a set which is arbitrarily close to a scaled copy of

E^{s}_{{\lambda_{0}}}(0)
. The argument is similar to
that of §
3.3
, in a simpler setting since
m=1
: we leave the details to the reader (see also
[
29
]

\bigcup_{n}\mathcal{T}_{n}\cap\Lambda_{1}
cannot be contained in a real-analytic subvariety, and we are done.
∎

Consider the foliation
\mathcal{F}(p)
in
U
whose leaves are the level sets of

\lambda\mapsto\frac{\ln\left|u_{\lambda}(p)\right|}{\ln\left|s_{\lambda}(p)\right|}\in\mathbb{R}
. Observe that
the leaves are the complex hypersurfaces defined by

u_{\lambda}(p)=s_{\lambda}(p)^{c}e^{i\alpha}
for
(c,\alpha)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, but the foliation is only real-analytic. Note also that
since
\lambda\mapsto(s_{\lambda}(p),u_{\lambda}(p))
has rank 2,
\mathcal{F}(p)
is a smooth foliation.
Our contradiction hypothesis, together with Lemma
5.7

implies that
\bigcup_{n}\mathcal{T}_{n}
is a
\mathbb{R}
-Zariski dense
set of leaves of
\mathcal{F}(p)
.

Since
{\lambda_{0}}
belongs to the bifurcation locus, as in Theorem
A.2
we can
fix another periodic point
q
, which is a saddle at
{\lambda_{0}}
and bifurcates to a sink in
U
. Shifting
{\lambda_{0}}
slightly
we may assume
\lambda\mapsto(s_{\lambda}(q),u_{\lambda}(q))
has rank 2 at
{\lambda_{0}}
.
Fix a smaller connected open neighborhood

of
{\lambda_{0}}
in which

remains
a saddle and this submersion property persists.

Lemma 5.8
.

The foliations
\mathcal{F}(p)
and
\mathcal{F}(q)
coincide in
V
.

Proof.

It is enough to prove the result in some possibly smaller
V^{\prime}
.
Since
p_{\lambda_{0}}
and
q_{\lambda_{0}}
admit
transverse homoclinic intersections, by the Inclination Lemma there exists a
countable set
(\widetilde{\Gamma}_{m})
of disjoint vertical graphs contained in

W^{s}(q)\cap\mathbb{B}
, whose closure contains
W^{s}_{\mathbb{B}}(E)
, which can be followed holomorphically in some
V^{\prime}
.
Fix also a sequence of branches
\widetilde{\Delta}^{u}_{k}
of
W^{u}(q)
which converges to

\Delta^{u}
. Restricting to sufficiently large
k
, we may assume that
\widetilde{\Delta}^{u}_{k,\lambda}
remains close to

\Delta^{u}_{\lambda}

throughout
V^{\prime}
, so in particular it is of horizontal degree 2 and its unique vertical tangency moves with positive speed.
The tangency locus
between
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{m,k,\lambda}
and
\widetilde{\Delta}^{u}_{k,\lambda}
is then
a hypersurface
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{m,k}
,
and our contradiction hypothesis implies that each
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{m,k}
is contained in a leaf of
\mathcal{F}(q)
.
Let
V^{\prime}
be small enough so that the leaves of
\mathcal{F}(q)
in

are contained in a foliation chart,
so they form a uniformly bounded family of graphs.

The key observation is that any
\Gamma_{n}
is the limit of a sequence
(\widetilde{\Gamma}_{m_{j}})

is, the convergence holds for the corresponding fibered objects in

\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{m_{j},k_{j}}
converges to

in the Hausdorff topology in

.
This follows easily from the fact that the lifts of
\widetilde{\Delta}^{u}

and
\Gamma_{n}
to the projectivized tangent bundle are transverse (see §

).
Since the
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{m_{j},k_{j}}

it follows that
\mathcal{T}_{n}
is a leaf of
\mathcal{F}(q)

\mathcal{F}(p)
and
\mathcal{F}(q)
coincide on a

\mathbb{R}
-Zariski dense subset, and

∎

Now recall that
q
changes type in
U
, and consider a connected open subset
U^{\prime}\subset U
in
which

can be followed holomorphically,
but its unstable multiplier crosses the unit circle.
(Recall that by the Implicit Function Theorem,

can be locally followed holomorphically unless some eigenvalue equals 1.) Consider a parameter

at which

and
{u_{\lambda_{1}}(q)}\neq 1

Then the leaf of

and

Lemma 5.9
.

W_{\beta}
extends to an irreducible algebraic hypersurface in
the extended
parameter space
\widetilde{\Lambda}
, which intersects the zero Jacobian locus.

Let us admit this result for the moment and conclude the proof of the theorem. Let

\Lambda_{\mathrm{dissip}}
be the region of the parameter space
made of dissipative maps.
Let us first show that the coincidence between

\mathcal{F}(p)
and
\mathcal{F}(q)
propagates along

W_{\beta}\cap\Lambda_{\mathrm{dissip}}
. Since these foliations are defined only in terms of the eigenvalues of
p
and
q
, for this it is enough to show that we can follow these periodic points along
W_{\beta}\cap\Lambda_{\mathrm{dissip}}
. For
q
this is obvious because along
W_{\beta}

one multiplier is

e^{i\beta}\neq 1
and the other one is smaller than 1 in modulus
by dissipativity.
For
p
, since
W_{\beta}
is a leaf of
\mathcal{F}(p)
near
\lambda_{1}
of the form

u_{\lambda}(p)=s_{\lambda}(p)^{c}e^{i\alpha}
,
by (real) analytic continuation, this property persists as long as we can follow
p
.
But this relation implies that if one eigenvalue hits 1, then the other one has modulus 1 as well, which is impossible in the dissipative regime, and we are done.

Now, let
\lambda\in W_{\beta}
converge
to some parameter
\lambda_{2}\in\widetilde{\Lambda}

with Jacobian 0. For
q
, this means that the stable eigenvalue tends to 0. For
p
, the
fact that
-1<c<0
in the relation
u_{\lambda}(p)=s_{\lambda}(p)^{c}e^{i\alpha}
forces

\left|s_{\lambda}(p)\right|^{-1}\geq\left|u_{\lambda}(p)\right|
, so
s_{\lambda}(p)
remains the stable eigenvalue.
Thus, as the Jacobian tends to 0,
s_{\lambda}(p)

must tend to zero, hence
\left|u_{\lambda}(p)\right|
tends to infinity, which is a contradiction
because
f_{\lambda}
converges to some well-defined 1-dimensional map in
\widetilde{\Lambda}
.
This finishes the proof of Theorem
B.2
.∎

Proof of Lemma
5.9
.

Basic elimination theory shows that

W_{\beta}
is defined by an algebraic condition (see e.g.
[
11
, §2.3]
), so it
defines an algebraic hypersurface
in
\widetilde{\Lambda}
. The non-trivial fact is that

W_{\beta}
hits the zero Jacobian locus.

For concreteness let us first explain the argument in
the space
\left\{f_{a,c},(a,c)\in\mathbb{C}^{\varstar}\times\mathbb{C}\right\}
of quadratic Hénon maps, where

f_{a,c}(z,w)=(z^{2}+c+aw,z)
. Since by Lemma
5.6

the Jacobian (which equals
-a
)
is non-constant along
W_{\beta}
, it must take arbitrary small values along
W_{\beta}
(indeed, a bounded holomorphic function on a quasiprojective variety is constant).
On the other hand, there exists
C
such that the set
\left\{(a,c),\ \left|c\right|>C\text{ and }\left|a\right|<1\right\}
is contained in the horseshoe locus, and in this region
all periodic points are saddles.
It follows that
W_{\beta}\cap\Lambda_{\mathrm{dissip}}=W_{\beta}\cap\left\{(a,c),\ \left|a\right|<1\right\}\subset\left\{(a,c),\ \left|c\right|\leq C\right\}
, so any sequence

(a_{n},c_{n})\in W_{\beta}
such that

a_{n}\to 0
must stay bounded in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, and we conclude that
W_{\beta}
must accumulate
\left\{a=0\right\}
, as asserted.

This argument can be transposed to the general case.
Indeed, in
[
11
, Prop. 5.1]
, given
any
f=h_{P_{1},a_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ h_{P_{k},a_{k}}\in\Lambda
,
the authors construct an explicit algebraic
2-parameter family
(f_{a,c})_{(a,c)\in\mathbb{C}^{\varstar}\times\mathbb{C}}
,
with
\jac f_{a,c}\to 0
when
a\to 0
and such that for any
A>0
,
there exists
C>0
such that if

\left|c\right|>C
and
\left|a\right|<A
, all periodic points are saddles.
So as before we conclude that
W_{\beta}
accumulates the zero Jacobian locus in
a bounded part of the parameter space, and we are done.
∎

Appendix A
Weak stability for polynomial automorphisms of
\mathbb{C}^{2}

Here we briefly review the notion of weak (
J^{\varstar}
-)stability from
[
17
]

(and further developed in
[
8
]
).
First, recall the usual vocabulary of complex Hénon maps:

K^{+}
and
K^{-}
are respectively the sets of bounded forward and backward orbits;

J^{+}=\partial K^{+}
and
J^{-}=\partial K^{-}
are the forward and backward Julia sets, and

J^{\varstar}
is the closure of the set of saddle periodic points.

Any family of polynomial automorphisms of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
of constant dynamical degree
is conjugate to a family of compositions of Hénon mappings
[
17
, Prop. 2.1]
. A family of polynomial automorphisms of dynamical degree
d\geq 2
is said to be
substantial
if:
either all its members are dissipative or for any periodic point with eigenvalues
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
,
no relation of the form
\alpha_{1}^{a}\alpha^{b}_{2}=c
holds persistently in parameter space,
where
a
,
b
,
c
are complex numbers and
\left|c\right|=1
.

A
branched holomorphic motion
is a family of holomorphic graphs over
\Lambda
in
\Lambda\times\mathbb{C}^{2}
.
It is said
normal
if these graphs form a normal family. As the “branched” terminology
suggests, these graphs are allowed to intersect, while in a holomorphic motion they are not.

A substantial family
(f_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
of
polynomial automorphisms is said to be
weakly
J^{\varstar}
-stable
if every periodic point stays of
constant type (attracting, saddle, indifferent, repelling) in the family.
Equivalently,
(f_{\lambda})
is weakly
J^{\varstar}
-stable if the periodic points
move under a holomorphic motion. Then the
sets
J^{\varstar}_{\lambda}
move under an equivariant branched holomorphic motion
[
17
, Thm. 4.2]
. This motion is unbranched at periodic points, heteroclinic intersections
[
17
, Prop. 4.14]
, and more generally points with some hyperbolicity
[
8
]
. In a weakly
J^{\varstar}
-stable family, every heteroclinic or homoclinic tangency must be persistent
[
17
, Prop. 4.14]
. Note the contraposite statement: if in a family
(f_{\lambda})
there is a non-persistent tangency
at
{\lambda_{0}}
, then some saddle point must change type near
{\lambda_{0}}
.

Using the automatic extension properties of plane holomorphic motions and the density of
stable and unstable manifolds of saddles, the motion of
J^{\varstar}
can be extended to a branched holomorphic motion of
J^{+}\cup J^{-}
. It is important that this extended motion is normal in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
and respects saddle points and their
stable and unstable manifolds, as well as the sets
K^{\pm}
and their complements
[
17
, Lem. 5.10, Thm. 5.12 and Cor. 5.14]
. Thus we are entitled to simply call such a family
weakly stable
.
