Hele-Shaw flow as a singular limit of a Keller-Segel system with nonlinear diffusion Thanks: Partially supported by NSF Grant DMS-2009236.
Antoine Mellet

Abstract
. We study a singular limit of the classical parabolic-elliptic Patlak-Keller-Segel (PKS) model for chemotaxis with non linear diffusion. The main result is the Γ \Gamma convergence of the corresponding energy functional toward the perimeter functional. Following recent work on this topic, we then prove that under an energy convergence assumption, the solution of the PKS model converges to a solution of the Hele-Shaw free boundary problem with surface tension, which describes the evolution of the interface separating regions with high density from those with low density. This result complements a recent work by the author with I. Kim and Y. Wu, in which the same free boundary problem is derived from the incompressible PKS model (which includes a density constraint ρ ≤ 1 \rho\leq 1 and a pressure term): It shows that the incompressibility constraint is not necessary to observe phase separation and surface tension phenomena.



Keywords:
Chemotaxis, Singular limit, Gamma-convergence, Free boundary problems, Hele-Shaw flow, Mean-curvature.

35K55, 35R35, 35A15, 53E10, 76D27

1.
Introduction

We consider the classical parabolic-elliptic Keller-Segel model for chemotaxis with nonlinear diffusion (see
[
20
,
34
,
19
,
17
]
):

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\rho-\Delta\rho^{m}+\beta\mathrm{div}\,(\rho\nabla\phi)=0,\\
-\varepsilon^{2}\Delta\phi=\rho-\sigma\phi.\end{cases}

In this model

\rho(t,x)
is the density function of a population of bacteria (or other type of cells, amoebae or even animals)
which diffuses and is advected by a drift
\nabla\phi
toward the region of higher concentration of a chemoattractant.
This chemical is being secreted by the bacteria themselves and its
concentration
\phi(t,x)
solves an elliptic equation.
The parameter
\beta
is the cell sensitivity and
\sigma>0
represents the degradation of the chemical.
Importantly, the diffusion is nonlinear and we assume that
m>2
(we will also consider more general diffusion term - see Assumptions
(H1)
-
(H3)
below).
Finally, we note that the chemical’s diffusivity, denoted here by
\varepsilon^{2}
is destined to go to zero.

The system (
1.1
) is set on an open bounded subset
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with smooth boundary in dimension
d\geq 2
.

In this model, the diffusion of the bacteria competes with the attracting potential
\phi
which causes the cells to aggregate.
This competition has been well studied in the linear diffusion case
m=1
.
In particular, it is well-known that, for some initial conditions, the concentration of the bacteria will lead to
finite time blow-up of the density (
\limsup_{t\to t^{*}}\|\rho(t)\|_{\infty}=\infty
- see e.g.
[
19
]
,
[
15
]
).
These blow-ups can be interpreted as the formation of saturated regions with very high value of the density function,
but from a biological point of view, arbitrary large density of bacteria should not be allowed.
Various modifications of the classical Keller-Segel model have been proposed to prevent blow-up. There are two main strategies: Enhancing the diffusion or inhibiting the attraction when the density is large.
Our model (
1.1
) with
m>1
is an example of the former strategies and has been studied by several authors
[
5
,
23
,
38
]
(we refer to
[
16
]
for models with inhibited attraction).

In some recent papers
[
22
,
21
]
(in collaboration with I. Kim and Y. Wu), we took a slightly different approach:
We considered a similar chemotaxis system, with linear diffusion (
m=1
) but with a hard incompressibility constraint
\rho\leq 1
. This constraint requires the introduction of a pressure term in the density equation, as is common in fluid mechanics - see
[
22
]
for details.
This incompressible model can actually be obtained as the limit
m\to\infty
of (
1.1
) - but this is not our purpose here and
m
will be fixed in the present paper.
The main result of
[
22
,
21
]
is the fact that the constraint
\rho\leq 1
leads to the formation of saturated patches
\{\rho(t)=1\}
(regions of maximal densities) and that the evolution of these patches can be described by classical Hele-Shaw free boundary problems.
In particular,
we characterized (in
[
21
]
) the limiting behavior of the density when
\varepsilon\ll 1
and
\beta\sim\varepsilon^{-1}
:
In that regime, the attractive effect of the potential
\phi
together with the constraint
\rho\leq 1
leads to the convergence of the density toward a characteristic function
\chi_{E(t)}
(a phenomena known as phase separation). Furthermore, the localization of the potential
\phi
when
\varepsilon\ll 1
leads to surface tension phenomena.
More precisely, under some assumptions on the convergence of the energy, we proved that the evolution of
E(t)
could be described by the Hele-Shaw problem with surface tension (see (
1.4
) below).

The goal of the present paper is to show that this convergence occurs for the solutions of (
1.1
) as well, without the hard constraint
\rho\leq 1
, but with nonlinear diffusion
m>2
. In other words,
when the diffusion is strong enough for large values of
\rho
the balance between the attractive potential and the diffusion leads, in a time scale of order
\varepsilon^{-1}
to phase separation and surface tension phenomena.

Let us now write the rescaled boundary value problem that we will be working with in this paper: We are interested in the asymptotic behavior of the solutions of (
1.6
)
when
\varepsilon\ll 1
at time scale
\varepsilon^{-1}
.
Rescaling the time variable accordingly and introducing the relevant boundary (null flux) and initial conditions, we are led to the following system of equations:

(1.2)

\begin{cases}\varepsilon\partial_{t}\rho-\Delta\rho^{m}+\beta\mathrm{div}\,(\rho\nabla\phi)=0,\quad&\mbox{ in }\Omega\times(0,\infty),\\
(-\nabla\rho^{m}+\beta\rho\nabla\phi)\cdot n=0,\quad&\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega\times(0,\infty)\\
\rho(x,0)=\rho_{in}(x)\quad&\mbox{ in }\Omega\end{cases}

with
\phi(\cdot,t)
solution of

(1.3)

\begin{cases}\sigma\phi-\varepsilon^{2}\Delta\phi=\rho&\mbox{ in }\Omega\\
\nabla\phi\cdot n=0&\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Remark 1.1
.

The scaling of (
1.2
)-(
1.3
) can also be interpreted as follows:
Let
\bar{\rho}(\bar{x},\bar{t})
be solution of the system (
1.2
)-(
1.3
) with
\varepsilon=1
(here
\bar{x}
,
\bar{t}
denotes the microscopic variables) and a large number of bacteria:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\bar{\rho}(\bar{x},\bar{t})\,d\bar{x}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\bar{\rho}_{in}(\bar{x})\,d\bar{x}\gg 1.

We then introduce
\varepsilon\ll 1
such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\bar{\rho}_{in}(\bar{x})\,d\bar{x}=\varepsilon^{-n}
and
rescale the time and space variables

x=\varepsilon\bar{x},\qquad t=\varepsilon^{3}\bar{t}.

The function
\rho^{\varepsilon}(x,t)=\bar{\rho}(\bar{x},\bar{t})
then solves (
1.2
)-(
1.3
) and satisfies
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho^{\varepsilon}(x,t)\,dx=1
.

The first part of our result concerns the phenomena of phase separation.
We note that the natural energy for (
1.2
)-(
1.3
) is

E_{\varepsilon}(\rho)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{m-1}\rho(x)^{m}-\frac{\beta}{2}\rho(x)\phi(x)\,dx&\mbox{ if }f(\rho)\in L^{1}(\Omega),\rho\geq 0\\
\infty&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases}

(where
\phi
is given by (
1.3
)).
We will prove (see Theorem
2.1
for a precise statement) that there is a constant
A_{\varepsilon}
such that the functional
\rho\mapsto E_{\varepsilon}(\rho)+A_{\varepsilon}
(which is also an energy for the system)

\Gamma
-converges when
\varepsilon\to 0
to

E_{0}(\rho)=\begin{cases}\gamma\rho_{c}P(E)&\mbox{ if }\rho=\rho_{c}\chi_{E}\in\mathrm{BV}(\Omega)\\
\infty&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases}

for some constants
\gamma
and
\rho_{c}
. This implies that
if the initial condition
\rho_{in}^{\varepsilon}
is such that
E_{\varepsilon}(\rho^{\varepsilon}_{in})+A_{\varepsilon}
remains bounded (which holds in particular if the initial condition is of the form
\rho_{c}\chi_{E_{in}}
), then the solution

\rho^{\varepsilon}
of (
1.2
)-(
1.3
) converges to a characteristic function
\rho_{c}\chi_{E(t)}
.

The second part of our result (see Theorem
2.4
) describes the evolution of this set
E(t)
and shows that under a classical assumption on the convergence of the energy (
2.15
), this evolution can be described by the following Hele-Shaw free boundary problem with surface tension:

(1.4)

\begin{cases}\Delta p=0&\mbox{ in }E(t),\qquad\nabla p\cdot n=0\quad\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega\cap E(t)\\
p=\gamma_{0}\kappa&\mbox{ on }\partial E(t)\cap\Omega\\
V=-\nabla p\cdot\nu&\mbox{ on }\partial E(t)\cap\Omega.\end{cases}

where
\kappa(x,t)
denotes the mean-curvature of
\partial E(t)
(with the convention that
\kappa\geq 0
when
E
is convex) and
V
denotes the normal velocity of the interface
\partial E(t)
.
This problem must be supplemented with a contact angle condition at the triple junction
\partial E(t)\cap\partial\Omega
.
Since
\phi
satisfies Neumann boundary condition, the contact angle condition derived in
[
21
]
reads

(1.5)

\cos\theta=0

which simply states that the moving interface
\partial E(t)
must be orthogonal to the fixed boundary
\partial\Omega
(
\theta
denotes the angle formed by
\partial E(t)
and
\partial\Omega
).

We recall that (
1.2
)-(
1.3
) (respectively (
1.4
)) is the gradient flow for the energy
E_{\varepsilon}
(respectively
E_{0}
) with respect to the Wasserstein metric on the manifold of probability measures. This fact will not be used in this paper, but it provides a heuristic justification for the convergence result Theorem
2.4

and it underscores the importance of the
\Gamma
-convergence result, Theorem
2.1
.

The Hele-Shaw free boundary problem with surface tension (
1.4
) is a very classical model, classically introduced to model the
motion of the interface separating two immiscible fluids in an unbounded
Hele-Shaw cell.
It has been derived in various frameworks, in particular as the limit of the discrete time approximation of a gradient flow for the perimeter functional (see for instance
[
33
,
10
]
)
and as the sharp interface limit of a
Cahn-Hilliard equation with degenerate mobility (see
[
13
,
24
]
).
The derivation of (
1.4
) in
[
21
]
was the first to make a direct connection between this model and chemotaxis phenomena, but it should be noted that the energy functional used in that paper shares some similarities with the functional used in
[
18
,
25
]
to derive a related two-phase free boundary problems.

We point out that the density constrained model that we considered in
[
22
,
21
]
is the natural limit of our (unconstrained) model (
1.1
) when
m\to\infty
. One would thus expect to recover the result of
(
1.1
) in the regime
m\gg 1
and
\varepsilon\ll 1
.
However, and this is the main object of this paper, it turns out that it is not necessary to impose the hard constraint
\rho\leq 1
or to take the limit
m\to\infty
in order
to observe these phenomena of phase separation and surface tension since
we derive the Hele-Shaw free boundary problem with surface tension (
1.4
) in the limit
\varepsilon\ll 1
when
m
is fixed satisfying
m>2
.

To conclude this introduction, we mention that our result will be proved for general non-linear diffusion, not necessarily given by some power law. More precisely, we consider the system:

(1.6)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\rho+\mathrm{div}\,(\rho v)=0\quad&\mbox{ in }\Omega\times(0,\infty)\\
v=\varepsilon^{-1}\nabla[-f^{\prime}(\rho)+\beta\phi]&\mbox{ in }\Omega\times(0,\infty)\\
-\varepsilon^{2}\Delta\phi=\rho-\sigma\phi&\mbox{ in }\Omega\times(0,\infty)\\
\rho v\cdot n=0\,,\quad\nabla\phi\cdot n=0\qquad&\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega\times(0,\infty)\end{cases}

which corresponds to (
1.1
) when

(1.7)

f(\rho)=\frac{1}{m-1}\rho^{m}.

One way to understand the conditions that will be required on the nonlinearity
f(\rho)
, is to note that (
1.3
) gives
\phi=\frac{1}{\sigma}\rho+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})
and so the evolution of
\rho
when
\varepsilon^{2}\ll 0
is approximated by

(1.8)

\varepsilon\partial_{t}\rho=\mathrm{div}\,(\rho\nabla f^{\prime}(\rho))-\frac{\beta}{\sigma}\mathrm{div}\,(\rho\nabla\rho)=\mathrm{div}\,\left(\rho\left[f^{\prime\prime}(\rho)-\frac{\beta}{\sigma}\right]\nabla\rho\right).

If
f^{\prime\prime}(\rho)-\frac{\beta}{\sigma}\geq 0
, this is a well-behaved diffusion equation, but we are interested here in models for which
f^{\prime\prime}(\rho)-\frac{\beta}{\sigma}
is positive for large
\rho
but negative for small
\rho
. In such cases, (
1.8
) is a forward-backward diffusion equation, which typically leads to phase separation.
We will list later some general assumptions on
f
for our result to hold but we can already see that this applies when
f
is given by (
1.7
) with
m>2
.

While (
1.8
) explains why phase separation occurs in our limit, it does not lead to surface tension phenomena. These can be understood by looking at the next order approximation in (
1.3
):

\phi=\frac{1}{\sigma}\rho+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\sigma}\Delta\phi\sim\frac{1}{\sigma}\rho+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\Delta\rho+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4})
.
With this in mind, we see that the system (
1.2
) is formally approximated by the following Cahn-Hilliard equation with degenerate mobility coefficient:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon^{-1}\mathrm{div}\,\left(\rho\nabla\left(-f^{\prime}(\rho)+\frac{\beta}{\sigma}\rho+\frac{\beta}{\sigma^{2}}\varepsilon^{2}\Delta\rho\right)\right)

(1.9)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{div}\,\left(\rho\nabla\left(\varepsilon\frac{\beta}{\sigma^{2}}\Delta\rho-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}W_{0}^{\prime}(\rho)\right)\right)

where the function
W_{0}(\rho)=f(\rho)-\frac{\beta}{2\sigma}\rho^{2}
is a double well potential when
f
is given by (
1.7
) with
m>2
thanks to the constraint
\rho\geq 0
(see Figure
1
) - with wells at
0
and
\rho_{c}>0
.

Figure 1.
The double-well potential
W_{0}(\rho)=f(\rho)-\frac{\beta}{2\sigma}\rho^{2}
when
f(\rho)=\rho^{3}

The singular limit
\varepsilon\to 0

for the classical Cahn-Hilliard equation with constant mobility was formally derived by Pego in
[
35
]
and justified rigorously, for example, in
[
1
,
11
,
26
]
. This limit leads to phase separation and the free boundary separating the two phases evolves according to a two-phase Hele-Shaw free boundary problem with surface tension (also called Mullins-Sekerka). In the case of degenerate mobility, which we are considering here, a similar formal analysis was performed by Glasner in
[
13
]
and leads to the one-phase Hele-Shaw problem (
1.4
).
This convergence was justified rigorously more recently by Kroemer and Laux in
[
24
]
.
Of course, this approximation (
1.9
) is only valid if
\rho
is smooth, so it cannot be used to justify the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, but the result of
[
24
]
is similar to our result in many ways (in particular it also requires an energy convergence assumption).

Finally, we point out that the limiting free boundary problem (
1.4
) is a one-phase problem because of the degeneracy of the mobility coefficient
\rho
at one of the two wells
\rho_{-}=0
.
If we include in (
1.6
) a nondegenerate diffusion term
\Delta\rho
as well, it adds a term
\rho\log\rho
to the potential
W_{0}(\rho)
, which would then have two positive wells (
0<\rho_{-}<\rho_{+}
). While we do not pursue the corresponding analysis here, the singular limit would lead in that case to the two-phase Hele-Shaw problem.

2.
Notations and main results

2.1.
The pressure
f^{\prime}(\rho)
and the potential
W(\rho)

When
f
is given by (
1.7
) with
m>2
, the function

h:=\rho\mapsto\frac{1}{\rho}\left(f(\rho)-\frac{\beta}{2\sigma}\rho^{2}\right)

has a unique minimum for
\rho\geq 0
, attained when
\rho=\rho_{c}:=\left(\frac{\beta}{2\sigma}\right)^{\frac{1}{m-2}}
.
We also denote

a:=-h(\rho_{c})=\frac{m-2}{m-1}\left(\frac{\beta}{2\sigma}\right)^{\frac{1}{m-2}}

so that we have
h(\rho)\geq-a
for all
\rho\geq 0
with equality if and only if
\rho=\rho_{c}
.

We now introduce the function

(2.1)

W(\rho):=\rho(h(\rho)+a)=f(\rho)-\frac{\beta}{2\sigma}\rho^{2}+a\rho.

This is a
C^{2}
function on
[0,\infty)
and the definition of
a
implies that

(2.2)

W(\rho)\geq 0\mbox{ for all $\rho\geq 0\quad$ and }\quad W(\rho)=0\Leftrightarrow\rho=0\mbox{ or }\rho_{c}.

Because of the natural constraint
\rho\geq 0
in the problem, we can thus view
W
as a double-well potential (we can also define
W
in
\mathbb{R}
by setting
W(\rho)=+\infty
for
\rho<0
).
Furthermore, we clearly have

(2.3)

W(\rho)\geq\nu\rho^{2}\qquad\mbox{ for all }\rho\geq C.

As mentioned in the introduction, we do not need to assume that
f
is a power law of the form (
1.7
) in order to prove our result.
All we need is to be able to define the double-well potential
W
as in (
2.1
), satisfying
the properties listed above.
This is the case if we assume that
f
satisfies the following general conditions:

(H1)

The function

\rho\mapsto f(\rho)
is a
C^{2}
strictly convex function on
\mathbb{R}_{+}

satisfying
f^{\prime\prime}(\rho)>0
for all
\rho>0
and

f^{\prime\prime}(\rho)\geq\delta
for
\rho\geq\delta^{-1}
(for some
\delta>0
).

Since
f
is defined up to a linear part, we can assume (without loss of generality) that

f(0)=f^{\prime}(0)=0.

(H2)

The function

h:=\rho\mapsto\frac{1}{\rho}\left(f(\rho)-\frac{\beta}{2\sigma}\rho^{2}\right)

is bounded below on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and there exists a unique
\rho_{c}\in(0,\infty)
such that

(2.4)

h(\rho_{c})=\inf_{\rho\geq 0}h(\rho)=:-a.

(H3)

There exists
\nu>0
and
C\geq 0
such that
the function
\rho\mapsto f(\rho)
satisfies

(2.5)

f(\rho)\geq\left(\frac{\beta}{2\sigma}+\nu\right)\rho^{2}\qquad\mbox{ for all }\rho\geq C.

Assumption
(H1)
says that the pressure
\rho\mapsto f^{\prime}(\rho)
must be monotone increasing and grow at least linearly for large
\rho
. In particular it implies that
f(\rho)\geq\frac{\delta}{2}\rho^{2}
for large
\rho
.
Assumption
(H2)
is also a condition on the quadratic growth of
f
, but it is more precise:
The fact that
h
is bounded below on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
is immediate if, for example, we have
f(\rho)\geq\frac{\beta}{2\sigma}\rho^{2}
for large enough
\rho
. This condition also implies that
\liminf_{\rho\to\infty}h(\rho)\geq 0=h(0)
so that the minimum is reached in
[0,\infty)
.
If we have in addition that
f(\rho)\leq\frac{\beta}{2\sigma}\rho^{2}
for small
\rho
, then this minimum is reached in
(0,\infty)
.
The uniqueness of
\rho_{c}
in
(H2)
ensures that we have phase separation with two (and only two) values of
\rho
(
0
and
\rho_{c}
).
Assumption
(H2)
implies that the potential
W
defined by (
2.1
) satisfies
W(\rho)\geq 0
for all
\rho\geq 0
, and
(H3)
gives (
2.3
) which will be used in the proof of Theorem
2.4
.

2.2.
Gamma convergence of the energy functional

As in
[
21
]
, the derivation of the Hele-Shaw free boundary problem in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
relies heavily on the properties of the energy functional associated to (
1.2
)-(
1.3
).
First, we recall that the solution
\phi
of (
1.3
) can be written as

\phi^{\varepsilon}(x,t)=\int_{\Omega}G_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\rho(y,t)\,dy

for some kernel
G_{\varepsilon}
satisfying in particular
G_{\varepsilon}(x,y)=G_{\varepsilon}(y,x)
.
We then define

\displaystyle\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[\int_{\Omega}f(\rho(x))+a\rho(x)\,dx-\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}G_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\rho(x)\rho(y)\,dy\,dx\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}f(\rho(x))+a\rho(x)-\frac{\beta}{2}\rho(x)\phi^{\varepsilon}(x)\,dx.

The system (
1.2
)-(
1.3
) is a gradient flow for this energy with respect to the Wasserstein distance
W_{2}
(the linear term
a\rho
plays no role here since the mass is preserved, but it will be important later - the same is true of the scaling
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
).
This expression makes apparent the competition between the repulsive effect of diffusion (the
f
term) and attractive effect of advection.
However, a simple but important computation shows that this energy can then also be written as follows:

(2.6)

\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon}(\rho)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}W(\rho)\,dx+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\beta}{2\sigma}(\rho-\sigma\phi^{\varepsilon})^{2}\,dx+\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\frac{\beta}{2}|\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,dx

with
W
given by (
2.1
).
Indeed, using (
1.3
), we can write

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\rho\phi^{\varepsilon}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma}\int_{\Omega}-\rho^{2}+(\rho-\sigma\phi^{\varepsilon})^{2}+\sigma\phi(\rho-\sigma\phi^{\varepsilon})\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma}\int_{\Omega}-\rho^{2}+(\rho-\sigma\phi^{\varepsilon})^{2}\,dx-\varepsilon^{2}\int_{\Omega}\phi\Delta\phi^{\varepsilon}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma}\int_{\Omega}-\rho^{2}+(\rho-\sigma\phi^{\varepsilon})^{2}\,dx+\varepsilon^{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,dx.

We deduce

\displaystyle\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}f(\rho)+a\rho-\frac{\beta}{2\sigma}\rho^{2}\,dx+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\beta}{2\sigma}(\rho-\sigma\phi^{\varepsilon})^{2}\,dx+\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\frac{\beta}{2}|\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,dx

which is (
2.6
) (see (
2.1
) for the definition of
W
).

We recall (see (
2.2
)) that
W
is a double-well potential. The functional (
2.6
) is thus reminiscent of the classical Modica-Mortola functional, except that the double-well potential involves
\rho
while the gradient term involves
\phi^{\varepsilon}
. But the middle term controls the
L^{2}
distance between
\rho
and
\sigma\phi^{\varepsilon}
. It is thus not unreasonable to think that this functional behaves, for small
\varepsilon
, like the Modica-Mortola functional and
\Gamma
-converges to the perimeter functional.
This will indeed be our first result.
To state this, we first recall that the solution of (
1.6
) satisfies
\rho\geq 0
and
\int_{\Omega}\rho\,dx=m_{0}
(with
m_{0}
the initial mass).
In what follows we take
m_{0}=1
and define

(2.7)

\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon}(\rho)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}W(\rho)\,dx+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\beta}{2\sigma}(\rho-\sigma\phi^{\varepsilon})^{2}\,dx+\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\frac{\beta}{2}|\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,dx&\mbox{ if }\rho\in L^{2}(\Omega),\;\rho\geq 0\mbox{ and }\int_{\Omega}\rho=1\\
\infty&\mbox{otherwise in $L^{1}(\Omega)$}\end{cases}

(we could also remove the condition
\rho\geq 0
and extend the definition of
W
by setting
W(\rho)=+\infty
for
\rho<0
).
The first result of the paper is:

Theorem 2.1
.

Assume that
\Omega
is an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with Lipschitz boundary and that
f
satisfies
(
H1
), (
H2
).
Then
the energy functional
\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon}

\Gamma
-converges to
\mathscr{J}_{0}
defined by

\mathscr{J}_{0}(\rho)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\gamma\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\rho|&\mbox{ if }\rho\in\mathrm{BV}(\Omega;\{0,\rho_{c}\})\mbox{ and }\int_{\Omega}\rho=1\\
\infty&\mbox{ otherwise in $L^{1}(\Omega)$}\end{cases}

for some constant
\gamma
defined by (
2.8
) below.
More precisely, we have

liminf property
: For all sequence
\rho^{\varepsilon}\in L^{1}(\Omega)
such that
\rho^{\varepsilon}\to\rho
in
L^{1}(\Omega)
, we have

\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon}(\rho^{\varepsilon})\geq\mathscr{J}_{0}(\rho).

limsup property
: For all
\rho\in L^{1}(\Omega)
, there exists a sequence
\rho^{\varepsilon}\in L^{1}(\Omega)
such that
\rho^{\varepsilon}\to\rho
in
L^{1}(\Omega)
and

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon}(\rho^{\varepsilon})\leq\mathscr{J}_{0}(\rho).

The definition of the constant
\gamma
appearing in the definition of
\mathscr{J}_{0}
requires the introduction of the function

g(s):=\inf_{\rho\geq 0}\left[W(\rho)+\frac{\beta}{2\sigma}(\rho-\sigma s)^{2}\right]

which plays an important role in the proof.
We note that
g(s)\geq 0
for all
s\geq 0
and
g(0)=g(\rho_{c}/\sigma)=0
(in fact
g
is a double-well potential).
The constant
\gamma
appearing in the definition of
\mathscr{J}_{0}
is then given by

(2.8)

\gamma:=\frac{1}{\rho_{c}}\int_{0}^{\rho_{c}/\sigma}\sqrt{2\beta g(s)}\,ds.

The proof of Theorem
2.1
is presented in Section
3
.
The key idea is to introduce the functional

\mathscr{G}_{\varepsilon}(\eta,\psi)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}W(\eta)+\frac{1}{2}(\eta-\psi)^{2}+\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\psi|^{2}\,dx

for all functions
\eta\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
\psi\in H^{1}(\Omega)
and notice (see Proposition
3.2
) that for a given
\rho
, the infimum of
\mathscr{G}_{\varepsilon}(\rho,\psi)
over all
\psi\in H^{1}(\Omega)
is actually achieved when
\psi=\phi^{\varepsilon}
solution of (
1.3
).

Remark 2.2
.

When
\rho(x)=\rho_{c}\chi_{E}\in\mathrm{BV}(\Omega;\{0,\rho_{c}\})
, we have
W(\rho(x))=0
a.e. and so
(
2.6
) reduces to

\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon}(\rho_{c}\chi_{E})=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\beta}{2\sigma}(\rho-\sigma\phi)^{2}\,dx+\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\frac{\beta}{2}|\nabla\phi|^{2}\,dx

This is the same functional that we studied in
[
30
]
(see
[
30
, Proposition 3.4]
).
It follows in particular from
[
30
, Proposition 2.3]
and its proof that

\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon}(\rho_{c}\chi_{E})\leq C\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\rho|=C\rho_{c}P(E,\Omega)

for some constant
C
independent of
\varepsilon
and

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon}(\rho_{c}\chi_{E})=\frac{\beta\rho_{c}}{4\sigma^{3/2}}\int_{\Omega}|D\rho|=\frac{\beta\rho_{c}^{2}}{4\sigma^{3/2}}P(E,\Omega).

The definition of
g(s)
implies
g(s)\leq\frac{\beta}{2\sigma}\min\{(\sigma s)^{2},(\rho_{c}-\sigma s)^{2}\}
(taking
\rho=0
and
\rho=\rho_{c}
in the infimum) which leads to

\gamma\leq\frac{\beta\rho_{c}}{4\sigma^{3/2}}

with, in general, a strict inequality. We thus expect to have
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon}(\rho_{c}\chi_{E})>\mathscr{J}_{0}(\rho_{c}\chi_{E})
.
This remark shows that we cannot simply take
\rho^{\varepsilon}=\rho
to prove the limsup property in Theorem

. Instead, the proof of Theorem
2.1
-(ii) will require the delicate construction of a recovery sequence
\rho^{\varepsilon}
.

2.3.
Weak solutions of (
1.2
)-(
1.3
)

The Patlak-Keller-Segel system of equations (
1.2
)-(
1.3
) has been
extensively studied.
Many results
concerning the existence of global in time solutions vs. finite time blow-up
have been obtained in the linear diffusion case
f(\rho)=\rho\log\rho
with
\sigma=0
(see for instance
[
19
,
3
,
6
,
4
]
).
It is also well known that nonlinear diffusion can prevent blow-up and guarantee the existence of bounded solutions globally in time (see
[
38
,
23
,
5
]
).
In dimension
2
, bounds on
\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{\infty}(\Omega))}
were established in
[
5
,
23
]
assuming that
\rho f^{\prime\prime}(\rho)\geq\kappa
for large
\rho
and when
\kappa\geq\kappa_{*}
(which holds under our assumption
(H1)
).
In dimension
d\geq 2
, and when
f^{\prime}(\rho)=\rho^{m-1}
,
solutions can be proved to be bounded
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
globally in time without any restriction on the size of the initial data in the subcritical case
m>2-\frac{2}{d}
, see
[
38
]
(while finite time blow-up may occur for some initial data when
1<m<2-\frac{2}{d}
).
Similar bounds have been established in
[
7
]
for very general nonlocal interaction potential with the same non-linear diffusion.

We will use the following result:

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
C^{1,\alpha}
boundary
and assume that
\sigma,\beta>0
and that
f(\rho)
satisfies (
H1
).
For any initial data
\rho_{in}\in L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, there exists a weak solution
\rho^{\varepsilon}
of (
1.2
)-(
1.3
) defined globally in time satisfying

\rho\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega))
,
\phi\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty;H^{1}(\Omega))

\rho
is a solution of the continuity equation
\partial_{t}\rho+\mathrm{div}\,(\rho v)=0
for some
v\in L^{2}((0,\infty)\times\Omega,d\rho)
, that is

(2.9)

\int_{\Omega}\rho_{in}(x)\zeta(x,0)\,dx+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega}\rho\,\partial_{t}\zeta+\rho v\cdot\nabla\zeta\,dx=0\qquad\forall\zeta\in C^{\infty}_{c}([0,\infty)\times\overline{\Omega}).

The flux
\rho v
satisfies

(2.10)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega}\rho v\cdot\xi-\varepsilon^{-1}\beta\rho\nabla\phi\cdot\xi-\varepsilon^{-1}[\rho f^{\prime}(\rho)-f(\rho)]\,\mathrm{div}\,\xi\,dx\,dt=0

for any vector field
\xi\in C^{\infty}_{c}((0,\infty)\times\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
\xi\cdot n=0
on
\partial\Omega
.

For all
t>0
, the function
\phi(\cdot,t)
is the unique solution of (
1.3
) in the following weak sense:

(2.11)

\phi\in H^{1}(\Omega),\quad\int_{\Omega}(\rho(\cdot,t)-\phi)\psi+\varepsilon^{2}\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla\psi\,dx=0\qquad\forall\psi\in H^{1}(\Omega).

The following energy dissipation property holds:

(2.12)

\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon}(\rho(t))+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\rho|v|^{2}\,dx\,dt\leq\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon}(\rho_{in})\qquad\forall t>0.

Equality (
2.9
) is the usual weak formulation for the continuity equation
\partial_{t}\rho+\mathrm{div}\,(\rho v)=0
with Neumann boundary conditions and initial condition
\rho_{in}
. Equation (
2.10
) is equivalent to the equality
\rho v=-\varepsilon^{-1}\rho\nabla f^{\prime}(\rho)+\varepsilon^{-1}\beta\rho\nabla\phi
.

Since
\rho\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega))
,
Inequality (
2.12
), implies
that
\rho v\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
and so the continuity equation gives
\rho\in C^{1/2}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))
. In particular, it make sense to have the (pointwise in time) function
\rho(\cdot,t)
in (
2.11
).

Some version of this result is proved for example in
[
38
]
(when
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
f^{\prime}(\rho)=\rho^{m-1}
with
m>2-\frac{2}{d}
) and
[
5
,
23
]
(when
\Omega
subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
).
Weak solutions can also be constructed using a JKO scheme much as in
[
22
]
(where a similar system, but with the density constraint
\rho\leq 1
, is considered) or
[
24
]
(where this gradient flow approach is developed for the degenerate Cahn-Hilliard model, which is closely related to our model).
We also point out that Theorem 4 in
[
9
]
shows that when
f^{\prime\prime}>0
and
\rho_{in}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
(with
\Omega
convex bounded domain) our equation (with Dirichlet boundary condition for
\phi
) has a weak bounded solution for some time
T=(\beta\|\rho_{in}\|_{L^{\infty}})^{-1}
.
Finally, we note that uniqueness of such weak solutions is proved in

[
8
]
.

Importantly, we point out that all the
L^{\infty}
bounds mentioned above are not expected to be uniform in
\varepsilon
and are therefore not useful for the purpose of this paper.
Only the natural bound in
L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{1}(\Omega))
and the bound in
L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\Omega))
provided by the energy inequality under assumption
(H3)
will be used in the proof of Theorem
2.4
below.

2.4.
Convergence results

Our second main result is the following convergence theorem:

Theorem 2.4
.

Assume
f
satisfies (
H1
), (
H2
) and (
H3
).
Given a sequence
\varepsilon_{n}
and initial data
\rho_{in}^{\varepsilon_{n}}
such that
\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon_{n}}(\rho_{in}^{\varepsilon_{n}})\leq M
and
\rho_{in}^{\varepsilon_{n}}\to\rho_{in}=\rho_{c}\chi_{E_{in}}\in BV(\Omega;\{0,\rho_{c}\})
, let
\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}}(x,t)
be the weak solution
of (
1.2
)-(
1.3
) (given by Theorem
2.3
). Then the followings hold:

Along a subsequence, the density
\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}}(x,t)
converges strongly in
L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{1}(\Omega))
to

\rho(x,t)=\rho_{c}\chi_{E(t)}\in L^{\infty}((0,\infty);\mathrm{BV}(\Omega;\{0,\rho_{c}\})).

There is a velocity function
v(x,t)
such that
t\mapsto\chi_{E(t)}
satisfies the continuity equation

(2.13)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\chi_{E(t)}+\mathrm{div}\,(\chi_{E(t)}v)=0&\mbox{ in }\Omega\\
\chi_{E}v\cdot n=0&\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega\\
\chi_{E(0)}=\chi_{E_{in}}&\mbox{ in }\Omega\end{cases}

as well as the
energy dissipation property

(2.14)

\mathscr{J}_{0}(\rho(t))+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}|v|^{2}\rho\,dx\,dt\leq\liminf\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon_{n}}(\rho_{in}^{\varepsilon_{n}}).

The pressure defined by

p^{\varepsilon_{n}}:=\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{n}}\left[\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}}(f^{\prime}(\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}})-f^{\prime}(\rho_{c}))+\frac{\beta}{\sigma}\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}}(\rho_{c}-\sigma\phi^{\varepsilon_{n}})\right]

converges to a function
p(x,t)
weakly-
*
in
L^{2}((0,T);(C^{s}(\Omega))^{*})
for any
s>0
.

If the following energy convergence assumption holds:

(2.15)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon_{n}}(\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}}(t))\,dt=\int_{0}^{T}\mathscr{J}_{0}(\rho(t))\,dt

then we have

(2.16)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega}\rho v\cdot\xi-p\,\mathrm{div}\,\xi\,dx\,dt=-\gamma\int_{\Omega}\left[\mathrm{div}\,\xi-\nu\otimes\nu:D\xi\right]|\nabla\rho|

for any vector field
\xi\in C^{\infty}_{c}((0,\infty)\times\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
\xi\cdot n=0
on
\partial\Omega
.
In other words,
E(t)
is a weak solution of the Hele-Shaw free boundary problem with surface tension (
1.4
)-(
1.5
) with
\gamma_{0}=\gamma\rho_{c}
and initial condition
E_{in}
.

The notion of weak solution of (
1.4
) that we recover with his theorem is similar to the definition considered for example in
[
18
,
21
,
24
]
and calls for a few classical comments:

(1)

The continuity equation (
2.13
) encodes the incompressibility condition
\mathrm{div}\,v=0
in
E(t)
and the free boundary condition
V=-v\cdot\nu
.

(2)

In (
2.16
),
\nu=\frac{\nabla\rho}{|\nabla\rho|}
stands for the
L^{\infty}
density of
\nabla\rho
with respect to the total variation
|\nabla\rho|
(which exists by Radon-Nikodym’s differentiation theorem).
In particular, the term

\left(\nu\otimes\nu:D\xi\right)|\nabla\rho|
is of the form

f(x,\lambda/|\lambda|)d|\lambda|
with
f
continuous and
1
-homogeneous and
\lambda=\nabla\rho
. The integral in (
2.16
) thus makes sense (see for example
[
14
]
).
Since
\rho=\chi_{E(t)}\in\mathrm{BV}
, we can also write

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega}\rho v\cdot\xi-p\,\mathrm{div}\,\xi\,dx\,dt=-\gamma_{0}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\partial^{*}E(t)}\left[\mathrm{div}\,\xi-\nu\otimes\nu:D\xi\right]d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}dt

where
\partial^{*}E(t)
denotes the reduced boundary of
E(t)
. In that case
\nu:\partial^{*}E(t)\to\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
is the measure theoretic unit normal vector.

(3)

By taking test functions
\xi
supported in either
E(t)
or
\Omega\setminus E(t)
, we see that
(
2.16
) implies
\nabla p=0
in
\{\rho(t)=0\}
and
v=-\nabla p
in
E(t)
.
For general test functions
\xi
, and taking into account the right hand side of (
2.16
) we further get
the surface tension condition
[p]=\gamma_{0}\kappa
on
\partial E(t)
and the normal contact angle condition.
This can be seen by using the classical formula (for a smooth interface
\Sigma
):

(2.17)

\int_{\Sigma}\mathrm{div}\,\xi-\nu\otimes\nu:D\xi d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}=\int_{\Sigma}\kappa\,\xi\cdot\nu d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}+\int_{\Gamma}\tilde{n}\cdot\xi d\mathcal{H}^{d-2}

where
\nu
is the normal vector to
\Sigma
,
\kappa
denotes the mean curvature of
\Sigma
and
\tilde{n}
is the conormal vector along
\Gamma=\partial\Sigma
(orthogonal to
\partial(\Sigma)
and tangential to
\Sigma
.
This last term must vanish in (
2.16
), which gives the condition
\tilde{n}=n
which means that
\partial E
and
\partial\Omega
must meet orthogonally.

(4)

We could remove the pressure
p
from this definition by taking divergence free vector field in (
2.16
). This approach actually makes sense from a variational point of view since such vector fields are the natural directions for the first order variations of the energy which preserve the mass. We choose this definition to be closer to the formulation (
1.4
), and so that one recognizes, in the right hand side of (
2.16
), the classical weak formulation of the mean-curvature operator.

2.5.
Setting the constants to
1

While it was important to state the results with the constants
\sigma
and
\beta
, it will be less cumbersome to make them equal to
1
in the proofs.
For that, we note that if we introduce

\bar{\phi}=\sigma\phi,\quad\bar{\varepsilon}=\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{\sigma}},\quad\bar{f}^{\prime}(\rho)=\frac{\sigma}{\beta}f^{\prime}(\rho)

and rescale the time variable by a factor
\frac{\sigma^{3/2}}{\beta}
, we are led to the system

\begin{cases}\partial_{\bar{t}}\rho-\mathrm{div}\,(\rho\nabla\bar{f}^{\prime}(\rho))+\mathrm{div}\,(\rho\nabla\bar{\phi})=0,\\
-\bar{\varepsilon}^{2}\Delta\bar{\phi}=\rho-\bar{\phi}\end{cases}

We can thus prove the result when
\sigma=\beta=1
and recover the mains results from there.

3.
\Gamma
-convergence: Proof of Theorem
2.1

3.1.
Preliminaries

When
\sigma=\beta=1
the energy functional is given by
(see (
2.6
))

(3.1)

\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon}(\rho)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}W(\rho)+\frac{1}{2}(\rho-\phi^{\varepsilon})^{2}\,dx+\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,dx,\quad W(\rho)=f(\rho)+a\rho-\frac{1}{2}\rho^{2}

where
\rho\in L^{2}(\Omega)
satisfies
\rho\geq 0
and
\int_{\Omega}\rho\,dx=1
, and

\phi^{\varepsilon}
solves (
1.3
) with
\sigma=1
, that is

(3.2)

\begin{cases}\phi^{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon^{2}\Delta\phi^{\varepsilon}=\rho&\mbox{ in }\Omega\\
\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}\cdot n=0&\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

An important role in the proof of Theorem
2.1
is played by the function

(3.3)

g(s)=\inf_{\rho\geq 0}\left[W(\rho)+\frac{1}{2}(\rho-s)^{2}\right]

(which is defined for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
).
In particular, we clearly have

(3.4)

\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon}(\rho)\geq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}g(\phi^{\varepsilon})\,dx+\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,dx\qquad\mbox{ where $\phi^{\varepsilon}$ solves \eqref{eq:phi0}}.

Our assumptions on
W
,
(H1)
,
(H2)
readily imply the following lemma:

Lemma 3.1
.

The function
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by (
3.3
) satisfies

(3.5)

g(s)\geq 0,\quad\forall s\in\mathbb{R},\qquad g^{-1}(0)=\{0,\rho_{c}\}

and

(3.6)

g(s)\leq\frac{1}{2}s^{2},\quad g(s)\leq\frac{1}{2}(s-\rho_{c})^{2}.

Proof.

The fact that
W(\rho)\geq 0
, and
W^{-1}(0)=\{0,\rho_{c}\}
yields (
3.5
).
Furthermore, we have
g(s)\leq W(\rho)+\frac{1}{2}(\rho-s)^{2}
for all
\rho\geq 0
and we get (
3.6
) by taking
\rho=0
and
\rho=\rho_{c}
in this inequality.
∎

Lemma
3.1
shows in particular that
g
is a double-well potential. The right-hand side of (
3.4
) is thus the classical Modica-Mortola functional

(3.7)

\mathscr{F}_{\varepsilon}(\psi)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}g(\psi)\,dx+\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\psi|^{2}\,dx&\mbox{ if }\psi\in H^{1}(\Omega),\mbox{ and }\int_{\Omega}\psi\,dx=1\\
\infty&\mbox{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

The
\Gamma
-convergence of
\mathscr{F}_{\varepsilon}
toward the perimeter functional has been known since the work on Modica and Mortola
[
32
]
, Modica
[
31
]
and Sternberg
[
37
]
when
g(s)\sim|s|^{p}
for large
|s|
,
p\geq 2
. The same result with weaker assumptions on the double-well potential (
g(s)\geq C|s|
for large
|s|
) was proved by Fonseca and Tartar
[
12
]
.
The proof of the liminf property of Theorem
2.1
, which is presented in Section
3.2
, relies on these results and inequality (
3.4
), which gives:

(3.8)

\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon}(\rho)\geq\mathscr{F}_{\varepsilon}(\phi^{\varepsilon})\qquad\mbox{ where $\phi^{\varepsilon}$ solves \eqref{eq:phi0}}.

The proof of the limsup property, given in Section
3.3
is more delicate and will require the introduction of the functional

\mathscr{G}_{\varepsilon}(\eta,\psi)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}W(\eta)+\frac{1}{2}(\eta-\psi)^{2}+\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\psi|^{2}\,dx

for all functions
\eta\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
\psi\in H^{1}(\Omega)
. This is similar to
\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon}(\eta)
, but we no longer assume that the potential
\psi
and the density
\eta
are related via (
3.2
).
The key observation is that the minimizer of
\psi\mapsto\mathscr{G}_{\varepsilon}(\eta,\psi)
for a fixed
\eta
, naturally solves the elliptic equation with Neumann boundary conditions (
3.2
) so that we can recover
\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon}(\eta)
.
More precisely, we will need the following proposition which we prove below:

Proposition 3.2
.

The following equalities hold:

(3.9)

\min_{\eta\in L^{2}(\Omega),\eta\geq 0}\mathscr{G}_{\varepsilon}(\eta,\phi)=\mathscr{F}_{\varepsilon}(\phi)\qquad\mbox{ for all $\phi\in H^{1}(\Omega)$ with $\int_{\Omega}\phi\,dx=1$}.

and

(3.10)

\min_{\psi\in H^{1}(\Omega)}\mathscr{G}_{\varepsilon}(\rho,\psi)=\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon}(\rho)\qquad\mbox{ for all $\rho\in L^{2}(\Omega)$, with $\rho\geq 0$ and $\int_{\Omega}\rho\,dx=1$.}

In order to prove this proposition, we need to establish a few additional properties of the function
\rho\mapsto g(\rho)
defined by (
3.3
):

Lemma 3.3
.

We have
g\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap C^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{a\})
and

(3.11)

g(s)=\frac{1}{2}s^{2}-f^{*}(s-a)

where
f^{*}
is the Legendre transform of the convex function
f
(which we extend to
\mathbb{R}
by setting
f(\rho)=+\infty
for
\rho<0
).
Furthermore, for all
s\geq 0
we have

(3.12)

g(s)=W(\rho)+\frac{1}{2}(\rho-s)^{2}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad s\in\partial f(\rho)+a\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\rho=s-g^{\prime}(s).

The Legendre transform:

We recall that the Legendre transform of the convex function
f
is defined by

f^{*}(s)=\sup_{\rho\in\mathbb{R}}\left(\rho s-f(\rho)\right).

This function
f^{*}
is convex and since
f\geq 0
,
f(0)=0
and
f(\rho)=+\infty
for
\rho<0
we easily check that

f^{*}(s)=0\qquad\mbox{ for all }s\leq 0.

Furthermore, (
H2
) implies that
f(\rho)\geq\frac{1}{2}\rho^{2}-a\rho
for all
\rho\geq 0
, and so
f^{*}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous and satisfies
f^{*}(s)\leq\frac{1}{2}(s+a)^{2}
.
We also have the classical property of the subdifferentials

s\in\partial f(\rho)\Leftrightarrow\rho\in\partial f^{*}(s)

where
\partial f(\rho)=f^{\prime}(\rho)
if
\rho>0
and
\partial f(0)=(-\infty,0]
.
We thus have

(3.13)

{f^{*}}^{\prime}(s)=0\quad\mbox{ for }s\leq 0,\quad{f^{*}}^{\prime}(s)=(f^{\prime})^{-1}(s)>0\quad\mbox{ for }s>0

and (
H1
) now implies that
f^{*}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap C^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})
.
Finally, (
H1
) also implies
f^{\prime}(\rho)\geq\delta\rho-1
for
\rho\geq\delta^{-1}
which gives

(3.14)

{f^{*}}^{\prime}(s)\leq\delta^{-1}(1+s)\qquad\mbox{ for all }s\in\mathbb{R}.

We now turn to the proof of Proposition
3.2
(the proof of Lemma
3.3
will be presented right after):

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

Given
\phi\in H^{1}(\Omega)
with
\int_{\Omega}\phi\,dx=1
,
(
3.3
) implies

\mathscr{G}_{\varepsilon}(\eta,\phi)\geq\mathscr{F}_{\varepsilon}(\phi)\qquad\mbox{ for all $\eta\in L^{2}(\Omega)$, with $\eta\geq 0$}.

Furthermore, if we define the function
\rho_{\phi}(x)
by

\rho_{\phi}(x):=\phi(x)-g^{\prime}(\phi(x))
for all
x\in\Omega
, then (
3.12
) implies

g(\phi(x))=W(\rho_{\phi}(x))+\frac{1}{2}(\rho_{\phi}(x)-\phi(x))^{2}

and so

\mathscr{G}_{\varepsilon}(\rho_{\phi},\phi)=\mathscr{F}_{\varepsilon}(\phi).

This implies (
3.9
) if we can show that
\rho_{\phi}\in L^{2}
and
\rho_{\phi}\geq 0
:
Using, (
3.11
) we get the alternative formula

\rho_{\phi}(x)={f^{*}}^{\prime}(\phi(x)-a)
which, together with
(
3.13
) and (
3.14
), implies that
\rho_{\phi}(x)\geq 0
and

\rho_{\phi}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
.
Equality (
3.9
) thus follows.

In order to prove (
3.10
), we note that for a given
\rho\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, we have

\min_{\psi\in H^{1}(\Omega)}\mathscr{G}_{\varepsilon}(\rho,\psi)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}W(\rho)\,dx+\min_{\psi\in H^{1}(\Omega)}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}(\rho-\psi)^{2}+\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\psi|^{2}\,dx\right)

This latest minimization problem is classical and the minimum is reached for the function
\phi^{\varepsilon}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
which satisfies the weak formulation (

We deduce that

\min_{\psi\in H^{1}(\Omega)}\mathscr{G}_{\varepsilon}(\rho,\psi)=\mathscr{G}_{\varepsilon}(\rho,\phi^{\varepsilon})

is the solution of (

\mathscr{G}_{\varepsilon}(\rho,\phi^{\varepsilon})=\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon}(\rho)
(when

). Equality (

Proof of Lemma
3.3
.

Recalling the fact that
W(\rho)=f(\rho)+a\rho-\frac{1}{2}\rho^{2}
,
we can write:

\displaystyle g(s)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\rho\geq 0}\left[f(\rho)+a\rho-\frac{1}{2}\rho^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(\rho-s)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\inf_{\rho\geq 0}\left[f(\rho)-\rho(s-a)+\frac{1}{2}s^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}s^{2}+\inf_{\rho\geq 0}\left(f(\rho)-\rho(s-a)\right)

(3.15)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}s^{2}-\sup_{\rho\in\mathbb{R}}\left(\rho(s-a)-f(\rho)\right)

(where we recall that
f(\rho)=+\infty
for
\rho<0
)
which gives (

).
The properties of the Legendre transform discussed above imply that
g\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap C^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{a\})
.

Given
s\geq 0
, we have

g(s)=W(\rho)+\frac{1}{2}(\rho-s)^{2}
if and only if the infimum in the definition of
g
, (
3.3
), is reached for
\rho
. This infimum is reached for the same
\rho
as the supremum
in (
3.15
), that is for all
\rho
such that
s-a\in\partial f(\rho)
(recall that
f
is convex).
This gives the first equivalence.
Furthermore, basic properties of the Legendre transform imply

s-a\in\partial f(\rho)\Longleftrightarrow\rho\in\partial f^{*}(s-a)

It remains to see that (

) gives
\partial f^{*}(s-a)=s-g^{\prime}(s)
to get the second equivalence.
∎

3.2.
The liminf property (Theorem
2.1
-(i))

The proof of the liminf property ((Theorem
2.1
-(i)) follows from (
3.8
) and the usual argument for Modica-Mortola’s convergence for
\mathscr{F}_{\varepsilon}
. Since this argument is relatively simple, we recall it here for convenience:
We introduce the function
F
such that

(3.16)

F^{\prime}(s)=\sqrt{2g(s)}\,,\quad s\in(0,\rho_{c}),\quad F(0)=0

which we extend by constant to
\mathbb{R}_{+}
by setting
F(s)=\int_{0}^{\rho_{c}}\sqrt{2g(s)}\,ds=\gamma\rho_{c}
for
s\geq\rho_{c}
.
We point out that (
3.6
) implies

F^{\prime}(s)\leq\sqrt{2g(s)}\leq\min\{s,\rho_{c}-s\}_{+}\qquad\forall s\geq 0

so that
F
is a Lipschitz function.

Given
\rho\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,
\rho\geq 0
and
\phi^{\varepsilon}\in H^{1}
the solution of (
3.2
) (which satisfies in particular
\phi^{\varepsilon}\geq 0
by the maximum principle), we have

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla F(\phi^{\varepsilon})|\leq\int_{\Omega}\sqrt{2g(\phi^{\varepsilon})}|\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}|\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}g(\phi^{\varepsilon})\,dx+\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,dx.

Together with (
3.4
), this implies

(3.17)

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla F(\phi^{\varepsilon})|\leq\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon}(\rho).

We now consider a sequence
\rho^{\varepsilon}
which converges to
\rho
in
L^{1}
.
If
\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon}(\rho^{\varepsilon})=\infty
, then the result is obviously true, so we assume that
\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon}(\rho^{\varepsilon})<\infty
and consider a subsequence
\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}}
such that
\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon}(\rho^{\varepsilon})=\lim\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon_{n}}(\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}})
and
\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon_{n}}(\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}})\leq C
.
In particular, we have
\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}}\geq 0
and
\int_{\Omega}\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}}\,dx=1
and so
\int_{\Omega}\rho(x)\,dx=1
.
Up to another subsequence, we can further assume that
\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}}\to\rho
a.e. in
\Omega
.
Formula (
2.6
) then gives

\int_{\Omega}W(\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}})\,dx+\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}(\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}}-\phi^{\varepsilon_{n}})^{2}\leq C{\varepsilon_{n}}.

This implies that
\phi^{\varepsilon_{n}}\to\rho
in
L^{1}
(and a.e. up to yet another subsequence) and
since
W(\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}})\to W(\rho)
a.e., Fatou’s lemma implies that

W(\rho)=0
a.e., that is
\rho=\rho_{c}\chi_{E}
for some set
E\subset\Omega
.

Since
\rho\mapsto F(\rho)
is continuous,
F(\phi^{\varepsilon_{n}})
converges a.e. and in
L^{1}(\Omega)
to
F(\rho_{c}\chi_{E})=F(\rho_{c})\chi_{E}=\gamma\rho_{c}\chi_{E}=\gamma\rho
(we can use Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem since
F(\phi^{\varepsilon_{n}})\leq\gamma\rho_{c}
in
\Omega
).
Finally, (
3.17
) and the lower semicontinuity of the BV norm implies

\liminf\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon_{n}}(\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}})\geq\liminf\int_{\Omega}|\nabla F(\phi^{\varepsilon_{n}})|\geq\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(\gamma\chi_{E})|=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(\gamma\rho)|=\mathscr{J}_{0}(\rho).

This concludes the proof of the liminf property.

The Limsup property (Theorem

-(ii))

First, we recall the classical result for the Modica-Mortola functional

Theorem 3.4

(

Assume that

Proof of Theorem

-(ii).

If

, the construction of the recovery sequence is trivial, so we can assume that

\rho\in BV(\Omega;\{0,\rho_{c}\})
and
\int_{\Omega}\rho(x)\,dx=1

\rho=\rho_{c}\chi_{E},\quad\mbox{ with }\quad\rho_{c}\int_{\Omega}\chi_{E}(x)\,dx=1.

As noted in Remark

, for such a function

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon}(\rho_{c}\chi_{E})=\frac{\rho_{c}}{4}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\rho|>\gamma H^{n-1}(\partial^{*}E)=\mathscr{J}_{0}(\rho)

so we cannot take
\rho^{\varepsilon}=\rho
as a recovery sequence.
Instead, we use

, which implies the existence

\psi^{\varepsilon}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
such that

with
\psi^{\varepsilon}_{0}\leq\rho_{c}\chi_{E}

\psi^{\varepsilon}_{1}\geq\rho_{c}\chi_{E}
and

The classical recovery sequence given by Theorem

For our proof, we will make a slightly different choice of

We claim that one can choose

\int_{\Omega}\psi^{\varepsilon}_{t_{\varepsilon}}-g^{\prime}(\psi^{\varepsilon}_{t_{\varepsilon}})\,dx=1.

Indeed, we can write (see (

which is a non decreasing function of

\psi^{\varepsilon}_{0}-g^{\prime}(\psi^{\varepsilon}_{0})\leq\rho_{c}\chi_{E}-g^{\prime}(\rho_{c}\chi_{E})=\rho_{c}\chi_{E},\qquad\psi^{\varepsilon}_{1}-g^{\prime}(\psi^{\varepsilon}_{1})\geq\rho_{c}\chi_{E}-g^{\prime}(\rho_{c}\chi_{E})=\rho_{c}\chi_{E}

(where we used the fact that

and has minimums at

\rho_{c}

A continuity argument implies that there exists

We then denote

and a.e. in

We now define the function

Using (

\rho^{\varepsilon}\geq 0,\quad\rho^{\varepsilon}\in L^{2}(\Omega),\quad\int_{\Omega}\rho^{\varepsilon}\,dx=1.

Next, we note that

Since

For this choice of

\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}}\to\rho\qquad\mbox{ strongly in }L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{1}(\Omega)).

It remains to show that
\rho(t)
is of the form
\rho_{c}\chi_{E(t)}
for all
t
: We note that given
t>0
, we can extract a subsequence which converges a.e. in
\Omega
so Formula (
3.1
) and the energy bound (see (
4.2
)) implies

\int_{\Omega}W(\rho(t))\,dx=0

hence
\rho(x,t)\in\{0,\rho_{c}\}
a.e.
x\in\Omega
(for all
t>0
) and so
\rho=\rho_{c}\chi_{\{\rho>0\}}
which completes the proof of Proposition
4.2
.
∎

5.
Proof of Theorem
2.4
- Part 2

5.1.
First variation of the energy

We proved in Theorem
2.1
that the energy functional
\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon}

\Gamma
-converges to
\mathscr{J}_{0}
.
We now want to prove that if

\rho^{\varepsilon}
is a sequence of densities that converge to
\rho
and if

\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon}(\rho^{\varepsilon})
converges to
\mathscr{J}_{0}(\rho)
, then we have the convergence of the first variations. This is the statement of the next proposition which plays a crucial role in the proof of the second part of Theorem
2.4
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Given a sequence of functions
\rho^{\varepsilon}\in L^{1}(\Omega)
and
\phi^{\varepsilon}
the corresponding solution of (
1.3
).
If
\rho^{\varepsilon}\to\rho
strongly in
L^{1}(\Omega)
and

(5.1)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon}(\rho^{\varepsilon})=\mathscr{J}_{0}(\rho),

then for all
\xi\in C^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying
\xi\cdot n=0
on
\partial\Omega
, we have:

(5.2)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{\Omega}[f(\rho^{\varepsilon})+a\rho^{\varepsilon}-\rho^{\varepsilon}\phi^{\varepsilon}]\mathrm{div}\,\xi-\rho^{\varepsilon}\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}\cdot\xi\,dx=\gamma\int_{\Omega}\left[\mathrm{div}\,\xi-\nu\otimes\nu:D\xi\right]|\nabla\rho|

where
f=\frac{\nabla\rho}{|\nabla\rho|}
and
n
denotes the outward normal unit vector to the fixed boundary
\partial\Omega
.

If
\rho^{\varepsilon}
is smooth enough, we can rewrite the left hand side of (
5.2
) as

-\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{\Omega}(f^{\prime}(\rho^{\varepsilon})+a-\phi^{\varepsilon})\nabla\rho^{\varepsilon}\cdot\xi\,dx

Since we have
\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon}(\rho)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}f(\rho)+a\rho-\frac{1}{2}\rho\phi^{\varepsilon}\,dx
, this is the first variation of
\frac{d}{ds}\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon}(\rho_{s})|_{s=0}
along the perturbation

(5.3)

\begin{cases}\partial_{s}\rho_{s}+\nabla\rho_{s}\cdot\xi=0\\
\rho_{s}|_{s=0}=\rho.\end{cases}

It might seem strange to the reader that we are using a perturbation which does not preserve the mass (unless we require that
\mathrm{div}\,\xi=0
). But this perturbation preserves characteristic functions, and is thus the appropriate perturbation when we are interested in the regime
\varepsilon\ll 1
.

Similar results have been proved for different energy functionals.
In particular, a classical result of Reshetnyak
[
36
]
gives that if
\chi_{E_{\varepsilon}}
converges to
\chi_{E}
strongly in
L^{1}
, then the convergence of the perimeter

P(E_{\varepsilon}):=\int|\nabla\chi_{E_{\varepsilon}}|\to\int|\nabla\chi_{E}|,

implies the convergence of the first variation to

\int(\mathrm{div}\,\xi-\nu\otimes\nu:D\xi)|D\chi_{E}|.

We refer to
[
28
,
25
,
18
]
for similar results for other approximations of the perimeter functional.

Proceeding as in
[
25
]
, Proposition
5.1
also implies the following time-dependent version:

Corollary 5.2
.

Given a sequence of functions
\rho^{\varepsilon}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))
such that
\rho^{\varepsilon}\to\rho
strongly in
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))
and
\phi^{\varepsilon}
the corresponding solution of (
1.3
).
If

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{0}^{T}\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon}(\rho^{\varepsilon}(t))\,dt=\int_{0}^{T}\mathscr{J}_{0}(\rho(t))\,dt,

then we have, for all
\xi\in C^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying
\xi\cdot n=0
on
\partial\Omega
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}[f(\rho^{\varepsilon})+a\rho^{\varepsilon}-\rho^{\varepsilon}\phi^{\varepsilon}]\mathrm{div}\,\xi-\rho^{\varepsilon}\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}\cdot\xi\,dx\,dt=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left[\mathrm{div}\,\xi-\nu\otimes\nu:D\xi\right]|\nabla\rho|

where
f=\frac{\nabla\rho}{|\nabla\rho|}
and
n
denotes the outward normal unit vector to the fixed boundary
\partial\Omega
.

A key tool in the proof of Proposition
5.1
is the following lemma:

Lemma 5.3
.

Given
\rho(x)
,
\phi^{\varepsilon}(x)
solution of (
3.2
) and
for all
\xi\in C^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying
\xi\cdot n=0
on
\partial\Omega
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}[f(\rho)+a\rho-\rho\phi^{\varepsilon}]\mathrm{div}\,\xi-\rho\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}\cdot\xi\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}[W(\rho)+\frac{1}{2}(\rho-\phi^{\varepsilon})^{2}]\mathrm{div}\,\xi\,dx

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\mathrm{div}\,\xi\,dx-\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}\otimes\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}:D\xi\,dx.

Proof.

The definition of
W(\rho)
(
2.1
) gives:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}[f(\rho)+a\rho-\rho\phi^{\varepsilon}]\mathrm{div}\,\xi-\rho\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}\cdot\xi\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}[W(\rho)+\frac{1}{2}\rho^{2}-\rho\phi^{\varepsilon}]\mathrm{div}\,\xi-\rho\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}\cdot\xi\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}[W(\rho)+\frac{1}{2}(\rho-\phi^{\varepsilon})^{2}]\mathrm{div}\,\xi-\frac{1}{2}|\phi^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\mathrm{div}\,\xi-\rho\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}\cdot\xi\,dx.

Next, we write (using the fact that
\rho-\phi^{\varepsilon}=-\varepsilon^{2}\Delta\phi^{\varepsilon}
):

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|\phi^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\mathrm{div}\,\xi+\rho\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}\cdot\xi\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(\rho-\phi^{\varepsilon})\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}\cdot\xi\,dx

\displaystyle=-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\mathrm{div}\,\xi+\varepsilon^{2}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}\otimes\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}:D\xi\,dx.

The lemma follows.
∎

Proof of Proposition
5.1
.

In view of Lemma
5.3
, it is enough to show, under the assumptions of Proposition
5.1
, that

(5.4)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}[W(\rho^{\varepsilon})+\frac{1}{2}(\rho^{\varepsilon}-\phi^{\varepsilon})^{2}]\mathrm{div}\,\xi\,dx+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\mathrm{div}\,\xi\,dx=\gamma\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{div}\,\xi|\nabla\rho|

and

(5.5)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}\otimes\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}:D\xi\,dx=\gamma\int_{\Omega}\nu\otimes\nu:D\xi|\nabla\rho|.

The proof largely follows the proof of Proposition 5.2 in
[
21
]
and is given here for the sake of completeness.
As in the proof of Theorem
2.1
-(i) (liminf property), we use the function
F(s)
defined by

F(0)=0,\quad F^{\prime}(s)=\sqrt{2g(s)}\quad\forall s\in(0,\rho_{c}),\quad F(s)=F(\rho_{c})\mbox{ for }s\geq\rho_{c}.

We also introduce the functions
u_{\varepsilon}
and
v_{\varepsilon}
defined by

u_{\varepsilon}^{2}:=\varepsilon^{-1}[W(\rho^{\varepsilon})+\frac{1}{2}(\rho^{\varepsilon}-\phi^{\varepsilon})^{2}]\quad\mbox{ and }\quad v_{\varepsilon}^{2}=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}|\nabla{\phi^{\varepsilon}}|^{2}.

We have in particular (see (
3.1
)):

\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon}(\rho^{\varepsilon})=\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}^{2}+v_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,dx

and the definition of the function
g
(see (
3.3
)) implies
\varepsilon^{-1}g(\phi^{\varepsilon})\leq u_{\varepsilon}^{2}
and so

(5.6)

|\nabla F(\phi^{\varepsilon})|\leq\sqrt{2g(\phi^{\varepsilon})}|\nabla{\phi^{\varepsilon}}|\leq 2u_{\varepsilon}v_{\varepsilon}=u_{\varepsilon}^{2}+v_{\varepsilon}^{2}-(u_{\varepsilon}-v_{\varepsilon})^{2}

Next, the strong convergence of
\rho^{\varepsilon}
and (
4.7
) imply that
F(\phi^{\varepsilon})
converges to
F(\rho)
strongly in
L^{1}
. Since
\rho
is a characteristic function and
F(0)=0
,
F(\rho_{c})=\gamma\rho_{c}
, we deduce
F(\phi^{\varepsilon})\to F(\rho)=\gamma\rho
strongly in
L^{1}
. The lower semi-continuity of the BV norm implies:

\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla F(\phi^{\varepsilon})|\,dx\geq\gamma\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\rho|.

On the other hand, the convergence assumption (
5.1
) implies

\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}^{2}+v_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,dx=\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon}(\rho^{\varepsilon})\to\gamma\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\rho|.

Inequality (
5.6
) thus implies:

(5.7)

\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}^{2}+v_{\varepsilon}^{2}-|\nabla F(\phi^{\varepsilon})|\to 0\quad\mbox{ in }L^{1}(\Omega)

(5.8)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla F(\phi^{\varepsilon})|\,dx\to\gamma\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\rho|

(5.9)

\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}-v_{\varepsilon}\to 0\quad\mbox{ in }L^{2}(\Omega).

These facts allow us to establish the limit (
5.4
).
Indeed, using first the definition of
u^{\varepsilon}
and
v^{\varepsilon}
, then the limit (
5.7
) and finally (
5.8
) (together with Proposition
A.1
), we can write:

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int[W(\rho^{\varepsilon})+\frac{1}{2}(\rho^{\varepsilon}-\phi^{\varepsilon})^{2}]\mathrm{div}\,\xi+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\int|\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\mathrm{div}\,\xi

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Omega}(u_{\varepsilon}^{2}+v_{\varepsilon}^{2})\mathrm{div}\,\xi\,dx

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla F(\phi^{\varepsilon})|\mathrm{div}\,\xi\,dx

\displaystyle=\gamma\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{div}\,\xi|\nabla\rho|.

Furthermore, (
5.7
) and (
5.9
) yields:

(5.10)

2u_{\varepsilon}^{2}-|\nabla F(\phi^{\varepsilon})|\to 0\quad\mbox{ in }L^{1}(\Omega)

which we use to pass to the limit in the term involving
\nabla{\phi^{\varepsilon}}\otimes\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}
. Indeed, we can write

2\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\partial_{i}{\phi^{\varepsilon}}\partial_{j}{\phi^{\varepsilon}}\partial_{i}\xi_{j}\,dx=2\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial_{i}{\phi^{\varepsilon}}}{|\nabla{\phi^{\varepsilon}}|}\frac{\partial_{j}{\phi^{\varepsilon}}}{|\nabla{\phi^{\varepsilon}}|}\partial_{i}\xi_{j}\,{|\nabla{\phi^{\varepsilon}}|^{2}}dx=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial_{i}{\phi^{\varepsilon}}}{|\nabla{\phi^{\varepsilon}}|}\frac{\partial_{j}{\phi^{\varepsilon}}}{|\nabla{\phi^{\varepsilon}}|}\partial_{i}\xi_{j}\,2u_{\varepsilon}^{2}dx

and since
\frac{\partial_{i}{\phi^{\varepsilon}}}{|\nabla{\phi^{\varepsilon}}|}\frac{\partial_{j}{\phi^{\varepsilon}}}{|\nabla{\phi^{\varepsilon}}|}\partial_{i}\xi_{j}
is bounded in
L^{\infty}
, (
5.10
) implies that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}2\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\partial_{i}{\phi^{\varepsilon}}\partial_{j}{\phi^{\varepsilon}}\partial_{i}\xi_{j}\,dx=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial_{i}{\phi^{\varepsilon}}}{|\nabla{\phi^{\varepsilon}}|}\frac{\partial_{j}{\phi^{\varepsilon}}}{|\nabla{\phi^{\varepsilon}}|}\partial_{i}\xi_{j}\,|\nabla F(\phi^{\varepsilon})|dx.

Using the fact that
F^{\prime}(\phi)\geq 0
, we can also write

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}2\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\partial_{i}{\phi^{\varepsilon}}\partial_{j}{\phi^{\varepsilon}}\partial_{i}\xi_{j}\,dx=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial_{i}F({\phi^{\varepsilon}})}{|\nabla F({\phi^{\varepsilon}})|}\frac{\partial_{j}F(\phi^{\varepsilon})}{|\nabla F(\phi^{\varepsilon})|}\partial_{i}\xi_{j}\,|\nabla F(\phi^{\varepsilon})|dx.

and using (
5.8
) and Proposition
A.2
we deduce

(5.11)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}2\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\partial_{i}{\phi^{\varepsilon}}\partial_{j}{\phi^{\varepsilon}}\partial_{i}\xi_{j}\,dx=\gamma\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial_{i}\rho}{|\nabla\rho|}\frac{\partial_{j}\rho}{|\nabla\rho|}\partial_{i}\xi_{j}\,|\nabla\rho|

which is (
5.5
).

∎

5.2.
The pressure equation

We now complete the proof of Theorem
2.4
:
We use the result of the previous section to pass to the limit in
(
2.10
), which we rewrite here using the notations of Proposition
4.2
:

(5.12)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega}j^{\varepsilon_{n}}\cdot\xi-\varepsilon_{n}^{-1}\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}}\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon_{n}}\cdot\xi-\varepsilon_{n}^{-1}[\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}}f^{\prime}(\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}})-f(\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}})]\,\mathrm{div}\,\xi\,dx\,dt=0

where
\xi\in C^{\infty}_{c}((0,\infty)\times\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{d})
is such that
\xi\cdot n=0
on
\partial\Omega
.

We now introduce the function

\displaystyle p^{\varepsilon_{n}}

\displaystyle:=\varepsilon_{n}^{-1}\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}}[f^{\prime}(\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}})+a-\phi^{\varepsilon_{n}}]+m^{\varepsilon_{n}}(t)

where
m^{\varepsilon_{n}}(t)
, independent of
x
, is chosen so that

\int_{\Omega}p^{\varepsilon_{n}}(x,t)\,dx=0\qquad\forall t>0.

With this notation, we can
rewrite (
5.12
) as

(5.13)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega}j^{\varepsilon_{n}}\cdot\xi\,dx\,dt=-\varepsilon_{n}^{-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega}[f(\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}})+a\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}}-\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}}\phi^{\varepsilon_{n}}]\mathrm{div}\,\xi-\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}}\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon_{n}}\cdot\xi\,dx\,dt+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega}p^{\varepsilon_{n}}\,\mathrm{div}\,\xi\,dx\,dt

where we recognize the middle term as the first variation of the energy appearing in (
5.2
).

We note that Lemma
5.3
implies

\left|\varepsilon_{n}^{-1}\int_{\Omega}[f(\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}})+a\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}}-\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}}\phi^{\varepsilon_{n}}]\mathrm{div}\,\xi-\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}}\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon_{n}}\cdot\xi\,dx\,\right|\leq C\|D\xi\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\mathscr{J}_{\varepsilon_{n}}(\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}})

so (
5.13
) together with Lemma
4.1
- (i) give

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}p^{\varepsilon_{n}}\,\mathrm{div}\,\xi\,dx\,dt\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\|D\xi\|_{L^{1}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega))}+C\|j^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))}\|\xi\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq C(T)\|\xi\|_{L^{2}(0,T;C^{1}(\Omega))}.

This implies that

\nabla p^{\varepsilon_{n}}
is bounded in
L^{2}((0,T);(C^{1}(\Omega))^{*})
and (proceeding as in
[
21
]
) that

p^{\varepsilon_{n}}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{2}((0,T);(C^{s}(\Omega))^{*})
and has a weak-* limit
p(x,t)
.

Finally, using Corollary
5.2
, we can pass to the limit in (
5.13
) to get

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega}j\cdot\xi\,dx\,dt=-\gamma\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega}\left[\mathrm{div}\,\xi-\nu\otimes\nu:D\xi\right]|\nabla\rho|+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega}p\,\mathrm{div}\,\xi\,dx\,dt

which is (
2.16
) and thus complete the proof of Theorem
2.4
.

Remark 5.4
.

Using the fact that
W^{\prime}(\rho_{c})=f^{\prime}(\rho_{c})-\rho_{c}+a=0
(since
\rho_{c}
is a minimum of
W
), we can also write the pressure
p^{\varepsilon_{n}}
as follows:

p^{\varepsilon_{n}}=\varepsilon_{n}^{-1}\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}}[f^{\prime}(\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}})-f^{\prime}(\rho_{c})+\rho_{c}-\phi^{\varepsilon_{n}}]+m^{\varepsilon_{n}}(t).

In this form, it is a bit easier to see why this function should converge despite the factor
\varepsilon_{n}^{-1}
, since
\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}}[f^{\prime}(\rho^{\varepsilon_{n}})-f^{\prime}(\rho_{c})+\rho_{c}-\phi^{\varepsilon_{n}}]
converges to

\rho[f^{\prime}(\rho)-f^{\prime}(\rho_{c})+\rho_{c}-\phi]
which vanishes when
\phi=\rho=\rho_{c}\chi_{E}
.
However, proving that
p^{\varepsilon_{n}}
is bounded directly, and obtaining a stronger notion of convergence than
L^{2}((0,T);(C^{s}(\Omega))^{*})
, does not seem realistic. Indeed, the properties of the limiting pressure
p(x,t)
are related to that of the mean curvature of
\partial E(t)
. Showing that
p(x,t)
is in some
L^{p}
space would thus be equivalent to establishing some regularity properties for the solution of the limiting Hele-Shaw flow with surface tension, a very delicate problem.

Appendix A
A couple of facts about
BV
functions

We recall here a couple of important results about
\mathrm{BV}
functions which we used in our proof (we refer the reader to
[
2
]
for details).
First we have

Proposition A.1
.

Let
f_{k}
be a sequence of functions such that
f_{k}\to f
in
L^{1}(\Omega)
when
k\to\infty
.
Then

\liminf_{k\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}\zeta(x)|\nabla f_{k}|\geq\int_{\Omega}\zeta(x)|\nabla f|

for all
\zeta\in C(\Omega)
with
\zeta\geq 0
.
Furthermore, if

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla f_{k}|\to\int_{\Omega}|\nabla f|,

then

\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}\zeta(x)|\nabla f_{k}|=\int_{\Omega}\zeta(x)|\nabla f|\qquad\mbox{ for all $\zeta\in C(\Omega)$}.

We also used the following particular case of Reshetnyak’s continuity theorem (see
[
2
]
Theorem 2.39)

Proposition A.2
.

Let
f_{k}
be a sequence of function such that
f_{k}\to f
in
L^{1}(\Omega)
and
\int_{\Omega}|\nabla f_{k}|\,dx\to\int_{\Omega}|\nabla f|
.
Then

\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}\zeta(x)\frac{\partial_{i}f_{k}}{|\nabla f_{k}|}\frac{\partial_{j}f_{k}}{|\nabla f_{k}|}|\nabla f_{k}|=\int_{\Omega}\zeta(x)\frac{\partial_{i}f}{|\nabla f|}\frac{\partial_{j}f}{|\nabla f|}|\nabla f|

for all
\zeta\in C(\overline{\Omega})
.
