Maurer-Cartan methods in perturbative quantum mechanics
Andrey Losev, Tim Sulimov

Abstract
. We reformulate the time-independent Schrödinger equation as a Maurer-Cartan equation on the superspace of eigensystems of the former equation. We then twist the differential so that its cohomology becomes the space of solutions with a set energy. A perturbation of the Hamiltonian corresponds to a deformation of the twisted differential, leading to a simple recursive relation for the eigenvalue and eigenfunction corrections.



1.
Introduction

In
[
1
]
we reformulated the perturbative one-dimensional quantum
scattering problem in the language of homological algebra. In particular, we
verified that the space of solutions of the free problem

(1.1)

H_{0}\psi=E\psi

with fixed
E>0
can be viewed as the cohomology group of the differential

(1.2)

Q_{0}=c(H_{0}-E),

where
c
is a Grassmann variable. We then proceeded to apply homotopy transfer
to a deformed differential

(1.3)

Q=Q_{0}+Q_{1}=c(H_{0}-E)+cV

using the homotopy
h
for
Q_{0}
. The induced differential turned out to contain
the on-shell scattering K-matrix.

This approach relies on the degeneracy of energy levels and the isospectrality
of scattering problems, i.e., for every positive energy
E
there exists a
solution of the full problem

(1.4)

H_{0}\psi+V\psi=E\psi

that tends to two separate solutions of (
1.1
) as
x\to\pm\infty
.
Thus, the treatment of bound states (especially non-degenerate ones) under small
perturbations requires a different method, as the energy levels shift and the
corrections to the original wave functions place them outside of the
Q_{0}

cohomology.

In this paper we continue the homological algebra approach to quantum mechanics
by tackling the time-independent Schrödinger equation

(1.5)

H\psi=E\psi,

where the energy
E
is no longer fixed but rather an unknown on par with the
wave function
\psi
.

The main idea is presented in Section
2
where we
combine the eigenvector with its eigenvalue into a single object
\Psi
and
view (
1.5
) as a quadratic equation in
\Psi
. Following the
Maurer-Cartan approach to deformation problems
[
2
]
we show that
this equation can be written as a Maurer-Cartan equation

(1.6)

Q\Psi+\tfrac{1}{2}\{\Psi,\Psi\}=0

in an appropriate differential graded Lie algebra, where the differential
Q

depends on the Hamiltonian
H
but the bracket does not. We also write down the
corresponding gauge group and apply the twisting procedure using a given
Maurer-Cartan element
\Psi_{0}
, i.e., a solution of (
1.5
).

In Section
3
we take the next step by deforming the
differential. This allows us to study the corrections to the eigensystem
under a small perturbation of
H
. In particular, we effortlessly acquire a
simple recurrence relation for these corrections. In addition, we show how these
corrections can be presented as a sum over tree diagrams.

Finally, in Section
4
we discuss how the Maurer-Cartan
approach can be applied to Hamiltonians with known symmetries and degenerate
spectra.

2.
Schrödinger equation in Maurer-Cartan form

2.1.
The superspace of eigensystems

Consider a quantum mechanical problem with Hamiltonian
H
acting on the Hilbert
space
\mathscr{H}
. The time-independent Schrödinger equation

(2.1)

H\psi=E\psi,\quad\psi\in\mathscr{H},E\in\mathbb{R},

is just an eigenproblem for a self-adjoint operator
H
.

For the purpose of unifying the eigenfunctions of
H
with the corresponding
eigenvalues we introduce the
superspace of eigensystems

(2.2)

\hat{\mathscr{H}}=\left(\mathbb{C}[\theta,c]\otimes\mathscr{H}\right)\oplus\mathbb{C}[c],

where
\theta
and
c
are Grassmann variables:

(2.3)

\theta^{2}=c^{2}=0,\quad[\theta,c]_{+}=\theta c+c\theta=0.

Despite looking contrived, this superspace is a natural extension of the
superspace used in
[
1
]
. In particular,
c
is the <<ghost>>
corresponding to
H
viewed as a symmetry of itself. However, the meaning of

\theta
is not clear yet.

In what follows <<hats>> over spaces will signify that they are in fact
superspaces. The general form of an element
\Psi\in\hat{\mathscr{H}}
is

(2.4)

\Psi=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{1}+\theta\psi_{2}+c\psi_{3}+c\theta\psi_{4}\\
E_{1}+cE_{2}\end{pmatrix}

and
\hat{\mathscr{H}}
thus has a natural
\mathbb{Z}
grading

(2.5)

\hat{\mathscr{H}}=\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{0}\oplus\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{1}\oplus\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{2}

based on total degree in
\theta
and
c
. To keep track of signs it is also
convenient to use the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
grading

(2.6)

\hat{\mathscr{H}}=\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathrm{even}}\oplus\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathrm{odd}}.

Next we turn
\hat{\mathscr{H}}
into a graded Lie algebra by defining the following bracket:

(2.7)

\{\Psi,\Phi\}=\begin{pmatrix}\Psi^{\mathrm{T}}\epsilon\Phi\\
0\end{pmatrix},\quad\epsilon=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}.

Example 1
.

A bracket of two generic elements in
\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{0}
is

(2.8)

\{\Psi,\Phi\}=\{\begin{pmatrix}\psi\\
E\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\varphi\\
\mathcal{E}\end{pmatrix}\}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{E}\psi-E\varphi\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

Example 2
.

A bracket of two generic elements in
\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{1}
is

(2.9)

\{\Psi,\Phi\}=\{\begin{pmatrix}\theta\psi_{1}+c\psi_{2}\\
cE\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\theta\varphi_{1}+c\varphi_{2}\\
c\mathcal{E}\end{pmatrix}\}=-\begin{pmatrix}c\theta(\mathcal{E}\psi_{1}+E\varphi_{1})\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

Remark 1
.

This bracket is
graded-antisymmetric
, i.e., it is symmetric if both
elements are odd and antisymmetric if at least one is even.

We also define

(2.10)

Q=\begin{pmatrix}cH&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\in\End{\hat{\mathscr{H}}}.

It can be easily checked that
Q
is a differential of degree
1
, i.e., it
squares to zero and is a derivation of the bracket
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
. Thus,

\hat{\mathscr{H}}
is a differential graded Lie algebra.

2.2.
Maurer-Cartan equation

We now restrict our attention to the subspace
\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{1}
and reformulate the
eigenvalue problem in Maurer-Cartan terms.

Definition 1
.

A Maurer-Cartan element is a solution of the Maurer-Cartan equation

(2.11)

Q\Psi+\tfrac{1}{2}\{\Psi,\Psi\}=0

in
\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{1}
. The set of Maurer-Cartan elements is denoted by
\mathrm{MC}(\hat{\mathscr{H}})
.

Statement 1
.

\Psi\in\mathrm{MC}(\hat{\mathscr{H}})
if and only if

(2.12)

\Psi=\begin{pmatrix}\theta\psi+c\phi\\
cE\end{pmatrix},

where
\phi
is any function and
H\psi=E\psi
.

The freedom in the choice of
\phi
in (
2.12
) seems irrelevant to
the eigenproblem. Additionally, different normalizations of
\psi
correspond
to different elements of
\mathrm{MC}(\hat{\mathscr{H}})
, which so far has no internal structure to
account for this multiplicity. Both of these issues are reconciled by the gauge
group associated to
\hat{\mathscr{H}}
. The following definition and theorem are adapted
directly from
[
2
]
.

Definition 2
.

The gauge group
\Gamma
associated to
\hat{\mathscr{H}}
is the group obtained from

\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{0}
via the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula. Namely,
1_{\Gamma}=0
and
for
\Psi,\Phi\in\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{0}
their product is

(2.13)

\Psi\cdot\Phi=\ln({\mathrm{e}}^{\Psi}{\mathrm{e}}^{\Phi})=\Psi+\Phi+\tfrac{1}{2}\{\Psi,\Phi\}+\tfrac{1}{12}\{\Psi,\{\Psi,\Phi\}\}+\ldots.

Remark 2
.

It can be easily seen that

(2.14)

\begin{pmatrix}\psi\\
E\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\varphi\\
\mathcal{E}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}f_{1}(E,\mathcal{E})\psi+f_{2}(E,\mathcal{E})\varphi\\
E+\mathcal{E}\end{pmatrix}.

Although
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
can be computed, they are of no direct interest
to us.

Theorem 1
.

The formula

(2.15)

\Phi.\Psi=\frac{\mathds{1}-{\mathrm{e}}^{\ad_{\Phi}}}{\ad_{\Phi}}(Q\Phi)+{\mathrm{e}}^{\ad_{\Phi}}\Psi,

where
\ad_{\Phi}=\{\Phi,-\}
, defines a left action of the gauge group

\Gamma
on
\mathrm{MC}(\hat{\mathscr{H}})
.

Given
\Phi=(\varphi~\mathcal{E})^{\mathrm{T}}
one readily gets

(2.16)

\ad_{\Phi}=\begin{pmatrix}-\mathcal{E}&\varphi\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad{\mathrm{e}}^{\ad_{\Phi}}=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\mathcal{E}}&\frac{1-{\mathrm{e}}^{-\mathcal{E}}}{\mathcal{E}}\varphi\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},

as well as

(2.17)

\frac{\mathds{1}-{\mathrm{e}}^{\ad_{\Phi}}}{\ad_{\Phi}}(Q\Phi)=\begin{pmatrix}c\frac{{\mathrm{e}}^{-\mathcal{E}}-1}{\mathcal{E}}H\varphi\\
0\end{pmatrix},

producing the formula for the gauge action

(2.18)

\Phi.\begin{pmatrix}\theta\psi+c\phi\\
cE\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\theta{\mathrm{e}}^{-\mathcal{E}}\psi+c\left({\mathrm{e}}^{-\mathcal{E}}\phi+\frac{{\mathrm{e}}^{-\mathcal{E}}-1}{\mathcal{E}}(H-E)\varphi\right)\\
cE\end{pmatrix}.

As we can see, using an appropriate gauge transformation we can fix the
normalization of
\psi
as well as remove all
\im(H-E)
components from

\phi
. This still leaves
\phi\in\ker(H-E)
not fixed. The meaning of this
freedom is unclear.

Once we have a solution of (
2.11
) we can twist the differential:

Corollary 1
.

Let
\Psi\in\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{1}
be a solution of (
2.11
), then

(2.19)

\widetilde{Q}=Q+\{\Psi,\cdot\}

is also a differential. Moreover, if

(2.20)

\Psi=\begin{pmatrix}\theta\psi\\
cE\end{pmatrix}

then

(2.21)

\widetilde{Q}=\begin{pmatrix}c(H-E)&\theta\psi\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

Corollary 2
.

Let
\Psi\in\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{1}
be a solution of (
2.11
) and let
\Phi\in\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{1}

be such that
\Psi+\Phi
is another solution of (
2.11
). Then

(2.22)

\widetilde{Q}\Phi+\tfrac{1}{2}\{\Phi,\Phi\}=0.

2.3.
Kernel and cohomology

Let us introduce some notation:

(2.23)

H_{E}=H-E,\qquad\mathscr{K}=\ker{H_{E}},\qquad\hat{\mathscr{K}}=\ker{\widetilde{Q}},

(2.24)

\mathscr{I}=\im{H_{E}},\qquad\hat{\mathscr{I}}=\im{\widetilde{Q}},

where
\widetilde{Q}
is constructed using a nonzero
\psi\in\mathscr{K}
:

(2.25)

\widetilde{Q}=\begin{pmatrix}c(H-E)&\theta\psi\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

Statement 2
.

\Psi\in\hat{\mathscr{K}}
if and only if there exist
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\in\mathscr{K}
and

\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}\in\mathscr{H}
such that

(2.26)

\Psi=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{1}+\theta\psi_{2}+c\varphi_{1}+c\theta\varphi_{2}\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

Proof.

The <<if>> part is trivial. Now consider
\widetilde{Q}
acting on a generic element
in
\hat{\mathscr{H}}
:

(2.27)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}cH_{E}&\theta\psi\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{1}+\theta\psi_{2}+c\varphi_{1}+c\theta\varphi_{2}\\
E_{1}+cE_{2}\end{pmatrix}

(2.28)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\theta E_{1}\psi+cH_{E}\psi_{1}+c\theta(H_{E}\psi_{2}-E_{2}\psi)\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

For this to be zero we necessarily have
E_{1}=0
,
H_{E}\psi_{1}=0
, and

H_{E}\psi_{2}=E_{2}\psi
. But the image of a self-adjoint operator is
orthogonal to its kernel. Thus,
H_{E}\psi_{2}=0
and consequently
E_{2}=0
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Next we describe the cohomology of
\widetilde{Q}
.
For simplicity and clarity we shall assume that
\mathscr{K}=\langle\psi\rangle
.
This means that the eigenstate
\psi
is non-degenerate — a common occurrence
for physical systems.

Corollary 3
.

The cohomology of
\widetilde{Q}
is

(2.29)

\mathcal{H}_{\widetilde{Q}}\cong\mathbb{C}[c]\otimes\mathscr{K}.

Proof.

Elements of
\hat{\mathscr{K}}
and
\hat{\mathscr{I}}
are described by (
2.26
)
and (
2.28
), respectively. Comparing the two forms we can see that
appropriate choice of
\psi_{1}
,
\psi_{2}
,
E_{1}
and
E_{2}

in (
2.28
) allows us to acquire any element of

(2.30)

\begin{pmatrix}\theta\mathscr{K}+c\mathscr{I}+c\theta\mathscr{H}\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

When we factor the closed elements (
2.26
) by the exact elements
of this space we are left with

(2.31)

\begin{pmatrix}\mathscr{K}+c\mathscr{K}\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

∎

2.4.
Homotopy

We introduce a homotopy

(2.32)

h=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{c}G&0\\
\partial_{\theta}Y&0\end{pmatrix},

where
G
is defined as

(2.33)

G\big|_{\mathscr{K}}=0,\qquad G\big|_{\mathscr{H}\setminus\mathscr{K}}=H_{E}^{-1},

and
Y
is a projection functional onto
\psi
in
\mathscr{H}
:

(2.34)

Y(\varphi)=(\psi,\varphi).

Here we assume that
\psi
is appropriately normalized using the gauge group.

Remark 3
.

This homotopy squares to zero, since
YG\varphi=0
for any
\varphi
.

Lemma 1
.

The anti-commutator of
\widetilde{Q}
and
h
is

(2.35)

[\widetilde{Q},h]_{+}=\mathds{1}-\Pi_{\mathcal{H}_{\widetilde{Q}}}.

Proof.

We have to keep track of what the individual components are acting on to do
the computation:

(2.36)

\widetilde{Q}h+h\widetilde{Q}=\begin{pmatrix}c\partial_{c}H_{E}G+\theta\partial_{\theta}\psi Y&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{c}cGH_{E}&\partial_{c}\theta G\psi\\
\partial_{\theta}cYH_{E}&\partial_{\theta}\theta Y\psi\end{pmatrix}.

In particular,

(2.37)

(G\psi)(\mathcal{E})=\mathcal{E}G(\psi)=0

by definition of
G
. For the same reason

(2.38)

H_{E}G=GH_{E}=1-\Pi_{\mathscr{K}},

where
\Pi
denotes the projection operator. Additionally

(2.39)

(YH_{E})(\varphi)=(\psi,H_{E}\varphi)=(H_{E}\psi,\varphi)=0,

and also

(2.40)

(\psi Y)(\varphi)=\psi(\psi,\varphi)=\Pi_{\psi}\varphi,\qquad(Y\psi)(\mathcal{E})=\mathcal{E}Y(\psi)=\mathcal{E}.

Combining all of this gives

(2.41)

[\widetilde{Q},h]_{+}=\begin{pmatrix}1-\Pi_{\mathscr{K}}+\theta\partial_{\theta}\Pi_{\psi}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},

which can be further simplified if we recall that
\Pi_{\psi}=\Pi_{\mathscr{K}}
,
producing

(2.42)

[\widetilde{Q},h]_{+}=\begin{pmatrix}1-\partial_{\theta}\theta\Pi_{\mathscr{K}}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}=\mathds{1}-\Pi_{\mathcal{H}_{\widetilde{Q}}}.

∎

3.
Perturbation theory

Let us now develop perturbation theory using the formalism above.
We would like to solve the quantum mechanical problem

(3.1)

H\psi=(H_{0}+\lambda V)\psi=E\psi

perturbatively in
\lambda
, assuming we can solve the non-perturbed problem

(3.2)

H_{0}\psi^{(0)}=E^{(0)}\psi^{(0)}.

Remark 4
.

The parameter
\lambda
can be thought of as small, but is actually
there only to keep track of the powers of
V
.

3.1.
Non-perturbed problem

In this section we only recall and adjust the notation from before. Let

(3.3)

\Psi^{(0)}=\begin{pmatrix}\theta\psi^{(0)}\\
cE^{(0)}\end{pmatrix}

be a solution of

(3.4)

Q_{0}\Psi^{(0)}+\tfrac{1}{2}\{\Psi^{(0)},\Psi^{(0)}\}=0,

where

(3.5)

Q_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}cH_{0}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

We embed
\Psi^{(0)}
into
Q_{0}
by defining

(3.6)

\widetilde{Q}_{0}=Q_{0}+\{\Psi^{(0)},\cdot\}=\begin{pmatrix}c(H_{0}-E^{(0)})&\theta\psi^{(0)}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

The corresponding homotopy is

(3.7)

h_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{c}G_{0}&0\\
\partial_{\theta}Y_{0}&0\end{pmatrix},

where
G_{0}
is defined as

(3.8)

G_{0}\big|_{\mathscr{K}_{0}}=0,\quad G_{0}\big|_{\mathscr{H}\setminus\mathscr{K}_{0}}=(H_{0}-E^{(0)})^{-1},\quad\mathscr{K}_{0}=\ker(H_{0}-E^{(0)})

and
Y_{0}
is a linear functional on
\mathscr{H}
:

(3.9)

Y_{0}(\varphi)=(\psi^{(0)},\varphi).

We also have

(3.10)

[\widetilde{Q}_{0},h_{0}]_{+}=\mathds{1}-\Pi_{\mathcal{H}_{\widetilde{Q}_{0}}},

where

(3.11)

\mathcal{H}_{\widetilde{Q}_{0}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathscr{K}_{0}+c\mathscr{K}_{0}\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

3.2.
Perturbation

It is clear that equation (
3.1
) can be rendered in Maurer-Cartan
form as

(3.12)

Q\Psi+\tfrac{1}{2}\{\Psi,\Psi\}=0,

where
\Psi\in\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{1}
,
Q=Q_{0}+\lambda Q_{1}
, and

(3.13)

Q_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}cV&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

In accordance with our original intention we search for
\Psi
in the form

(3.14)

\Psi=\Psi^{(0)}+\hat{\Psi},\qquad\hat{\Psi}=\begin{pmatrix}\theta\hat{\psi}\\
c\hat{E}\end{pmatrix},

(3.15)

\hat{\Psi}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda^{k}\Psi^{(k)}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda^{k}\begin{pmatrix}\theta\psi^{(k)}\\
cE^{(k)}\end{pmatrix}.

To fix the normalization we impose that

(3.16)

(\psi^{(0)},\psi^{(k)})=\delta_{k,0}.

Substituting (
3.14
) into (
3.12
) we get

(3.17)

\displaystyle{}Q_{0}\Psi^{(0)}+\lambda Q_{1}\Psi^{(0)}+Q_{0}\hat{\Psi}+\lambda Q_{1}\hat{\Psi}

(3.18)

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{2}\{\Psi^{(0)},\Psi^{(0)}\}+\tfrac{1}{2}\{\Psi^{(0)},\hat{\Psi}\}+\tfrac{1}{2}\{\hat{\Psi},\Psi^{(0)}\}+\tfrac{1}{2}\{\hat{\Psi},\hat{\Psi}\}=0,

which can be rearranged using (
3.4
), (
3.6
), and the
properties of the bracket:

(3.19)

\widetilde{Q}_{0}\hat{\Psi}=-\lambda Q_{1}\Psi^{(0)}-\lambda Q_{1}\hat{\Psi}-\tfrac{1}{2}\{\hat{\Psi},\hat{\Psi}\}.

Before dealing with
\widetilde{Q}_{0}
using the homotopy we must check that the
obstructions vanish, i.e., the r.h.s. is zero in
\mathcal{H}_{\widetilde{Q}_{0}}
. This is indeed
the case, as can be seen from the Grassmann structure: the r.h.s. has to be
proportional to
c\theta
, which puts it outside
\mathcal{H}_{\widetilde{Q}_{0}}
, given
by (
3.11
). Applying
h_{0}
to the l.h.s. gives

(3.20)

\displaystyle h_{0}\widetilde{Q}_{0}\hat{\Psi}

\displaystyle=(1-\Pi_{\mathcal{H}_{\widetilde{Q}_{0}}}-\widetilde{Q}_{0}h_{0})\hat{\Psi}

(3.21)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1-\partial_{\theta}\theta\Pi_{\mathscr{K}_{0}}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\theta\hat{\psi}\\
c\hat{E}\end{pmatrix}

(3.22)

\displaystyle\quad-\begin{pmatrix}c(H_{0}-E^{(0)})&\theta\psi^{(0)}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{c}G_{0}&0\\
\partial_{\theta}Y_{0}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\theta\hat{\psi}\\
c\hat{E}\end{pmatrix}

(3.23)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\theta\hat{\psi}\\
c\hat{E}\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}\theta\Pi_{\mathscr{K}_{0}}\hat{\psi}\\
0\end{pmatrix}

(3.24)

\displaystyle=(1-\Pi_{\hat{\mathscr{K}}_{0}})\hat{\Psi}=\hat{\Psi},

where the last equality holds due to (
3.16
). Consequently,

(3.25)

\hat{\Psi}=-\lambda h_{0}Q_{1}\Psi^{(0)}-\lambda h_{0}Q_{1}\hat{\Psi}-\tfrac{1}{2}h_{0}\{\hat{\Psi},\hat{\Psi}\}.

3.3.
Recurrence relation

We can now substitute the series expansion (
3.15
) for
\hat{\Psi}
and
gather all the terms of order
k
to acquire the following recurrence relation

(3.26)

\Psi^{(k)}=-h_{0}Q_{1}\Psi^{(k-1)}-\tfrac{1}{2}h_{0}\sum_{m=1}^{k-1}\{\Psi^{(m)},\Psi^{(k-m)}\}.

In particular, for
k=1,2,3
they read

(3.27)

\displaystyle\Psi^{(1)}

\displaystyle=-h_{0}Q_{1}\Psi^{(0)},

(3.28)

\displaystyle\Psi^{(2)}

\displaystyle=-h_{0}Q_{1}\Psi^{(1)}-\tfrac{1}{2}h_{0}\{\Psi^{(1)},\Psi^{(1)}\},

(3.29)

\displaystyle\Psi^{(3)}

\displaystyle=-h_{0}Q_{1}\Psi^{(2)}-h_{0}\{\Psi^{(1)},\Psi^{(2)}\}.

These equations can be solved, producing expressions for eigenfunction and
eigenvalue corrections. For
k=1
we have

(3.30)

\begin{pmatrix}\theta\psi^{(1)}\\
cE^{(1)}\end{pmatrix}=-\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{c}G_{0}&0\\
\partial_{\theta}Y_{0}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}cV&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\theta\psi^{(0)}\\
cE^{(0)}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-\theta G_{0}V\psi^{(0)}\\
c(\psi^{(0)},V\psi^{(0)})\end{pmatrix},

where we used the explicit form of
Y_{0}
. Skipping some computations, for
k=2
we get

(3.31)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\theta\psi^{(2)}\\
cE^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\theta G_{0}VG_{0}V\psi^{(0)}\\
-cY_{0}VG_{0}V\psi^{(0)}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}-\theta(Y_{0}V\psi^{(0)})G_{0}G_{0}V\psi^{(0)}\\
c(Y_{0}V\psi^{(0)})Y_{0}G_{0}V\psi^{(0)}\end{pmatrix}

(3.32)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\theta\left(G_{0}VG_{0}V\psi^{(0)}-(\psi^{(0)},V\psi^{(0)})G_{0}^{2}V\psi^{(0)}\right)\\
-c(\psi^{(0)},VG_{0}V\psi^{(0)})\end{pmatrix},

where in the second equality we used the fact that
Y_{0}G_{0}\varphi=0
for any

\varphi
.

Remark 5
.

It is easy to see that only the first term in (
3.26
)
provides a correction to the eigenvalue due to
Y_{0}G_{0}=0
.

The simple structure of the energy corrections is broken in order
k=3
. In components:

(3.33)

\displaystyle\psi^{(3)}

\displaystyle=-G_{0}VG_{0}VG_{0}V\psi^{(0)}+(\psi^{(0)},V\psi^{(0)})G_{0}VG_{0}^{2}V\psi^{(0)}

(3.34)

\displaystyle+(\psi^{(0)},VG_{0}V\psi^{(0)})G_{0}^{2}V\psi^{(0)}+(\psi^{(0)},V\psi^{(0)})G_{0}^{2}VG_{0}V\psi^{(0)}

(3.35)

\displaystyle-(\psi^{(0)},V\psi^{(0)})^{2}G_{0}^{3}V\psi^{(0)},

(3.36)

\displaystyle E^{(3)}

\displaystyle=(\psi^{(0)},VG_{0}VG_{0}V\psi^{(0)})-(\psi^{(0)},V\psi^{(0)})(\psi^{(0)},VG_{0}^{2}V\psi^{(0)}).

We can see that this procedure reproduces the familiar formulas found in every
textbook on quantum mechanics (cf.
[
3
]
).

3.4.
Corrections as sums over tree diagrams

Let us introduce the following graphical notation for the various elements in
the recurrence relation:

4.
Conclusion

Quantum mechanics provides a playground for homological methods which can
further be applied to quantum field theories and string
theories
[
4
]
. Thus, the utility of this work lies not in the
well-known formulas for perturbative corrections, but in paving the way for
further development of such methods — specifically in writing the
time-independent Schrödinger equation as a Maurer-Cartan equation and twisting
the differential. We see two directions inviting further investigation.

Firstly, energy level degeneracy makes the cohomology of
\widetilde{Q}_{0}

two-dimensional (four-dimensional, counting the ghost degrees of freedom),
and requires a different approach to calculate the corrections. We expect that
homotopy transfer and the perturbation lemma (see, for
instance,
[
5
]
) will play a crucial role there.

Secondly, in the spirit of our previous work
[
1
]
we can introduce
symmetries of the Hamiltonian (conserved charges) into the differential. This
would most likely result in relations describing how the perturbation breaks
these symmetries.
