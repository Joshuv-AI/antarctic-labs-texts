Sam M. Thompson, Dominik D. Freydenberger

Abstract
. Despite considerable research on document spanners, little is known about the expressive power of generalized core spanners. In this paper, we use Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé games to obtain general inexpressibility lemmas for the logic 𝖥𝖢 \mathsf{FC} (a finite-model variant of the theory of concatenation). Applying these lemmas give inexpressibility results for 𝖥𝖢 \mathsf{FC} that we lift to generalized core spanners. In particular, we give several relations that cannot be selected by generalized core spanners, thus demonstrating the effectiveness of the inexpressibility lemmas. As an immediate consequence, we also gain new insights into the expressive power of core spanners.



1.
Introduction

Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé games are a fundamental tool for establishing inexpressibility results in finite-model theory.
In this paper, we develop techniques to use Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé games for the logic
\mathsf{FC}
and use these to gain insights into the expressive power of generalized core spanners.

Document Spanners.

SystemT is a rule-based information extraction system, developed by IBM, which includes a declarative query language called Annotation Query Language (AQL) – see
(
Chiticariu et al. 2010
;
Krishnamurthy et al. 2009
;
Li et al. 2011
)
.
Fagin, Kimelfeld, Reiss, and Vansummeren
(
Fagin et al. 2015
)
developed a formal framework for information extraction called
document spanners
(or just
spanners
), which captures the core functionality of AQL.

One can define the process of querying a word with a spanner in a two-step process.
First, so-called
extractors
obtain relations of intervals (or
spans
) from the text.
For the purposes of this introduction, we assume these extractors to be
regex formulas
, which are regular expressions with capture variables.
For example, if someone wanted to find all occurrences of common misspellings in a text document, they could consider the regex formula
\gamma(x):=\Sigma^{*}\cdot x\{\mathtt{acheive}\lor\mathtt{begining}\lor\cdots\lor\mathtt{wether}\}\cdot\Sigma^{*}
.
Then,
\gamma
extracts a unary relation of spans within the given text document where one of the misspellings occur.

Secondly, the extracted relations are combined using a relational algebra.
This paper is concerned with the class of
generalized core spanners
, which allow for
\cup
(union),
\pi
(projection),
\bowtie
(natural join),
\setminus
(difference), and
\zeta^{=}
(equality selection).
While union, projection, natural join, and difference are defined as one might expect (assuming prior knowledge of relational algebra), equality selection is more text specific.
The equality selection
\zeta^{=}_{x,y}
only chooses those tuples for which
x
and
y
are mapped to spans that represent the same factor of the input word (at potential different locations).

A word relation
R\subseteq(\Sigma^{*})^{r}
, for some
r\in\mathbb{N}
, is
selectable
by generalized core spanners if we add an
R
selection operator (e.g.,
\zeta^{R}
) to our relational algebra without increasing the expressive power.
One fundamental question regarding generalized core spanners is what relations are selectable?
In other words, what is the expressive power of generalized core spanners?
For example, from Theorem 5.14 of
(
Freydenberger and Peterfreund 2021
)
, we know that length equality
\zeta^{\mathsf{len}}_{x,y}
(i.e., only keep those tuples for which the span that
x
is mapped to has the same length as the span that
y
is mapped to) cannot be expressed by generalized core spanners.

Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé Games and FC

For relational first-order logic (and therefore relational algebra), one of the fundamental tools for inexpressibility are
Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé Games
; for example, see
(
Ebbinghaus and Flum 1999
;
Immerman 1998
;
Libkin 2004
)
.
To use Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé games in order to yield inexpressibility results for generalized core spanners, we need a suitable logic.
For this task, we use
\mathsf{FC}
; a logic that was introduced by Freydenberger and Peterfreund
(
Freydenberger and Peterfreund 2021
)
as a finite-model variant on the theory of concatenation.
For this paper, we define this logic slightly differently to
(
Freydenberger and Peterfreund 2021
)
.
First, we treat every word
w\in\Sigma^{*}
as a relational structure consisting of a universe that contains all factors of
w
, a ternary concatenation relation
R_{\circ}
where
(x,y,z)\in R_{\circ}
if and only if
x
,
y
, and
z
are all factors of
w
where
x=y\cdot z
, and constant symbols for every
\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma
and for the emptyword
\varepsilon
.
Then,
\mathsf{FC}
is simply a first-order logic over these structures.
As syntactic sugar, we use
(x\mathbin{\dot{=}}y\cdot z)
rather than
R_{\circ}(x,y,z)
for atomic
\mathsf{FC}
-formulas.
As an example, consider the formula

\varphi:=\forall z\colon\bigl(\neg(z\mathbin{\dot{=}}\varepsilon)\rightarrow\neg\exists x,y\colon(x\mathbin{\dot{=}}z\cdot y)\land(y\mathbin{\dot{=}}z\cdot z)\bigr).

This formula states, that for all factors
z
where
z\neq\varepsilon
does not hold (all non-empty factors), we have that there does not exist a factor
x
where
x=zzz
.
In other words,
\varphi
defines those words
w\in\Sigma^{*}
that do not contain
uuu
where
u
is a non-empty word.

If we consider Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé games over two structures that represent a word
w\in\Sigma^{*}
, we can gain inexpressibility results for
\mathsf{FC}
.
Then, using techniques from combinatorics on words (namely,
commutation
), we are able to lift these inexpressibility results to generalized core spanners.

Related Work.

Fagin et al.
(
Fagin et al. 2015
)
introduces three main classes of document spanners.
The
regular spanners
(regex formulas extended with projection, union, and natural join) are perhaps the most intensively studied class with a sizeable amount of research on enumeration algorithms
(
Florenzano et al. 2020
;
Amarilli et al. 2020
;
Schmid and Schweikardt 2021b
)
.
When it comes to expressive power, Fagin et al.
(
Fagin et al. 2015
)
showed that regular spanners cannot go beyond
recognizable relations
.

Core spanners
extend regular spanners with equality selection, allowing for more expressive power at the cost of tractability, see e.g.
(
Freydenberger 2019
;
Freydenberger and Holldack 2018
;
Freydenberger and Peterfreund 2021
)
.
With regards to their expressive power,
(
Fagin et al. 2015
)
gave the
core-simplification lemma
, allowing one to simplify core spanners into a normal form in order to study their expressive power.
Freydenberger
(
Freydenberger 2019
)
uses inexpressibility techniques from word equations (namely, results in Karhumäki, Mignosi, and Plandowski
(
Karhumäki et al. 2000
)
) to obtain an inexpressibility lemma for core spanners, which yields relations that cannot be selected by core spanners.

When it comes to generalized core spanners, the techniques from core spanners do not work.
As far as the authors are aware, only two inexpressibility results for generalized core spanners exist in literature:
First, Peterfreund, ten Cate, Fagin, and Kimelfeld
(
Peterfreund et al. 2019
)
showed that over a unary alphabet, only
semi-linear languages
can be expressed by generalized core spanners.
Then, Freydenberger and Peterfreund
(
Freydenberger and Peterfreund 2021
)
used the
Feferman-Vaught theorem
to show that the language containing those words
w:=\mathtt{a}^{n}\mathtt{b}^{n}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
is not expressible by generalized core spanners.
However, the use of the Feferman-Vaught theorem in
(
Freydenberger and Peterfreund 2021
)
relies on the limited structure of the language
\mathtt{a}^{n}\mathtt{b}^{n}
, and it is unclear how to generalize this proof technique.
Note that from the data complexity of model checking
\mathsf{FC}
-formulas, we know that it cannot express languages outside of
\mathsf{LOGSPACE}
, see
(
Freydenberger and Peterfreund 2021
)
.
While this observation might not be considered particularly enlightening from an inexpressibility point of view, we state this for completeness sake.

The expressive power of other classes of spanners (that is, apart from regular spanners, core spanners, and generalized core spanners) has also been considered.
For example, see
(
Schmid and Schweikardt 2021a
;
Peterfreund et al. 2019
;
Peterfreund 2021
;
Thompson and Freydenberger 2023
)
.

Issues with Standard Techniques.

Beyond toy examples, Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé games are difficult to play, and usually require rather involved combinatorial arguments.
Therefore, many techniques have been developed in order to achieve sufficient criteria that give Duplicator a winning strategy (see
(
Fagin 1996
)
for a survey).
For example,
locality
is often used to show that Duplicator has a winning strategy.
Unfortunately, these locality results often fail for
non-sparse structures
.
The structures we look at in the present paper are very non-sparse, and therefore we cannot simply apply locality results in order to gain inexpressibility tools.

Another useful tool is the
Feferman-Vaught theorem
.
Freydenberger and Peterfreund
(
Freydenberger and Peterfreund 2021
)
used this result to prove that
\mathtt{a}^{n}\mathtt{b}^{n}
is not an
\mathsf{FC}
-language, which then implies that length selection is unattainable for generalized core spanners.
To this end,
(
Freydenberger and Peterfreund 2021
)
defines
\mathsf{FO}[\mathsf{EQ}]
, which extends first-order logic over a linear order with symbol predicates with a built-in equality relation.
Then, we can decompose these structures that represent words of the form
\mathtt{a}^{n}\mathtt{b}^{m}
into two substructures (one for
\mathtt{a}^{n}
and one for
\mathtt{b}^{m}
).
Finally, the Feferman-Vaught theorem can be invoked to prove that
\mathtt{a}^{n}\mathtt{b}^{n}
is not an
\mathsf{FC}
language.
However, this proof idea does not generalize beyond words which can be decomposed into disjoint sections (at least not without a sizeable amount of extra machinery).

Preliminaries

Let
\mathbb{N}:=\{0,1,2,\dots\}
and let
\mathbb{N}_{+}:=\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
where
\setminus
denotes set difference.
For
n\geq 1
, we use
[n]
for
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
.
The cardinality of a set
S
is denoted by
|S|
.
For a vector
\vec{a}\in A^{k}
for some set
A
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we write
x\in\vec{a}
to denote that
x
is a component of
\vec{a}
.

We use
\Sigma
for a fixed and finite alphabet of terminal symbols and we use
\Xi
for a countably infinite set of variables.
If
w=w_{1}\cdot w_{2}\cdot w_{2}
where
w,w_{1},w_{2},w_{3}\in\Sigma^{*}
, then we call
w_{1}
a prefix of
w
,
w_{2}
a factor
w
, and
w_{3}
a suffix of
w
.
If
w_{1}\neq w
, then
w_{1}
is a
strict prefix
of
w
; and likewise, if
w_{3}\neq w
, then
w_{3}
is a
strict suffix
of
w
.
We denote that
w_{2}
is a
factor
of
w
by
w_{2}\sqsubseteq w
and if
w_{2}\neq w
also holds, then
w_{2}\sqsubset w
.
For
w\in\Sigma^{*}
, we write the set of all factors of
w
as
\mathsf{Facs}(w):=\{u\in\Sigma^{*}\mid u\sqsubseteq w\}
.
We use
|w|
to denote the length of a word
w\in\Sigma^{*}
, and for some
\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma
, we use
|w|_{\mathtt{a}}
to denote the number of occurrences of
\mathtt{a}
within
w
.
For
w\in\Sigma^{*}
, let
w^{k}\in\Sigma^{*}
denote the word that consists of
k
repetitions of
w
.
We always assume
w^{0}=\varepsilon
.

A word
w\in\Sigma^{+}
is called
imprimitive
if
w=z^{k}
for some
z\in\Sigma^{*}
and
k>1
.
We always assume
\varepsilon
to be imprimitive.
If
w\in\Sigma^{+}
is not imprimitive, then
w
is
primitive
.
That is,
w\in\Sigma^{+}
is primitive if for all
z\in\Sigma^{+}
, we have that
w=z^{m}
implies
w=z
.

The logic FC

We introduce a definition of
\mathsf{FC}
that is slightly more technical than the original one from
(
Freydenberger and Peterfreund 2021
)
, and more suitable for defining games. The differences, and why they do no affect the expressive power, are discussed at the end of this section.

For the purposes of this paper, we consider one fixed signature
\tau_{\Sigma}:=\{R_{\circ},\mathtt{a}_{1},\dots,\mathtt{a}_{m},\varepsilon\}
for every terminal alphabet
\Sigma:=\{\mathtt{a}_{1},\dots,\mathtt{a}_{m}\}
, where
R_{\circ}
is a ternary relation symbol and where
\varepsilon
and
\mathtt{a}_{i}
for every
i\in[m]
are constant symbols.
Given a word
w\in\Sigma^{*}
, let
\mathfrak{A}_{w}:=(A,R_{\circ}^{\mathfrak{A}_{w}},\mathtt{a}_{1}^{\mathfrak{A}_{w}},\dots,\mathtt{a}_{m}^{\mathfrak{A}_{w}},\varepsilon^{\mathfrak{A}_{w}})
be the
\tau_{\Sigma}
-structure that represents
w\in\Sigma^{*}
as follows:

•

A:=\mathsf{Facs}(w)\mathrel{\cup}\{\perp\}
is the universe,

•

R_{\circ}^{\mathfrak{A}_{w}}:=\{(a,b,c)\in\mathsf{Facs}(w)^{3}\mid a=b\cdot c\}
is concatenation, restricted to factors of
w
,

•

\mathtt{a}^{\mathfrak{A}_{w}}:=\mathtt{a}
if
|w|_{\mathtt{a}}\geq 1
, and
\mathtt{a}^{\mathfrak{A}_{w}}=\perp
otherwise, and

•

\varepsilon^{\mathfrak{A}_{w}}:=\varepsilon
.

Note that if
|w|_{\mathtt{a}}=0
, then
\mathtt{a}^{\mathfrak{A}_{w}}=\perp
.
One can think of
\perp
as a null value; however, we usually deal with those words where
|w|_{\mathtt{a}}\geq 1
for all
\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma
.
Therefore, due to the nature of the constant symbols used in
\tau_{\Sigma}
, we often do not distinguish between a constant symbol
\mathtt{a}\in\tau_{\Sigma}
, and the terminal symbol
\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma
.
In other words, when the structure is clear from context, we use
\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma
rather than
\mathtt{a}^{\mathfrak{A}_{w}}\in A
.

An
\mathsf{FC}
-formula is a first-order formula, where the atomic formulas are of the form
R_{\circ}(x,y,z)
for variables or constants
x
,
y
, and
z
.
As syntactic sugar, we use
(x\mathbin{\dot{=}}y\cdot z)
as
\mathsf{FC}
atomic formulas, as we always interpret
R_{\circ}
as concatenation.
More formally:

Definition 2.1.

We define
\mathsf{FC}
, the set of all
\mathsf{FC}
-formulas, recursively as:

•

If
x,y,z\in\Xi\mathrel{\cup}\Sigma\mathrel{\cup}\{\varepsilon\}
, then
(x\mathbin{\dot{=}}y\cdot z)\in\mathsf{FC}
,

•

if
\varphi,\psi\in\mathsf{FC}
, then
(\varphi\land\psi)\in\mathsf{FC}
,
(\varphi\lor\psi)\in\mathsf{FC}
, and
\neg\varphi\in\mathsf{FC}
,

•

if
\varphi\in\mathsf{FC}
and
x\in\Xi
, then
\forall x\colon\varphi\in\mathsf{FC}
and
\exists x\colon\varphi\in\mathsf{FC}
.

An interpretation
\mathcal{I}:=(\mathfrak{A}_{w},\sigma)
consists of a
\tau_{\Sigma}
-structure
\mathfrak{A}_{w}
that represents some
w\in\Sigma^{*}
, and a mapping
\sigma\colon(\Sigma\mathrel{\cup}\Xi\mathrel{\cup}\{\varepsilon\})\rightarrow\mathsf{Facs}(w)\mathrel{\cup}\{\perp\}
, where
\sigma(\mathtt{a})=\mathtt{a}^{\mathfrak{A}_{w}}
for each constant symbol
\mathtt{a}\in\tau_{\Sigma}
.
Furthermore, we always assume
\sigma(x)\neq\perp
for all
x\in\Xi
.

Definition 2.2.

For any
\varphi\in\mathsf{FC}
, and any interpretation
\mathcal{I}:=(\mathfrak{A}_{w},\sigma)
, where
\mathfrak{A}_{w}
represents
w\in\Sigma^{*}
, we define
\mathcal{I}\models\varphi
as follows:
Let
\mathcal{I}\models(x\mathbin{\dot{=}}y\cdot z)
if
(\sigma(x),\sigma(y),\sigma(z))\in R_{\circ}^{\mathfrak{A}_{w}}
.
For the quantifiers, we have that
\mathcal{I}\models\exists x\colon\varphi
(or
\mathcal{I}\models\forall x\colon\varphi
) if
\sigma_{x\rightarrow u}\models\varphi
holds for an (or all)
u\in\mathsf{Facs}(w)
, where
\sigma_{x\rightarrow u}(x)=u
and
\sigma_{x\rightarrow u}(y)=\sigma(y)
for all
y\in\Xi\setminus\{x\}
.
Negation, disjunction, and conjunction are defined canonically.

We use the usual first-order logic definition of bound variables and free variables.
If
\varphi
is a sentence (that is, there are no free variables in
\varphi
), then we simply write
\mathfrak{A}_{w}\models\varphi
.
Furthermore, for any
w\in\Sigma^{*}
, there is a unique
\tau_{\Sigma}
-structure
\mathfrak{A}_{w}
that represents
w
.
We can therefore define the
language
of an
\mathsf{FC}
-sentence.

Definition 2.3.

The language defined by a sentence
\varphi\in\mathsf{FC}
is
\mathcal{L}(\varphi):=\{w\in\Sigma^{*}\mid\mathfrak{A}_{w}\models\varphi\}
.
Let
\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{FC})
be the class of languages definable by an
\mathsf{FC}
-sentence.

We write
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(w)
to denote the set of all mappings
\sigma
such that
(\mathfrak{A}_{w},\sigma)\models\varphi
, where
\mathfrak{A}_{w}
is the
\tau_{\Sigma}
-structure that represents
w\in\Sigma^{*}
.
Note that for simplicity, we assume that for any
\sigma\in\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(w)
, the domain of
\sigma
is exactly the free variables of
\varphi
.

For a word relation
R\subseteq(\Sigma^{*})^{k}
, we say that
R
is
definable in
\mathsf{FC}
if there exists
\varphi_{R}\in\mathsf{FC}
with free variables
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{k}
such that for any
w\in\Sigma^{*}
, we have
\sigma\in\llbracket\varphi_{R}\rrbracket(w)
if and only if

(\sigma(x_{1}),\sigma(x_{2}),\dots,\sigma(x_{k}))\in R\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{Facs}(w)^{k}.

We say that such a formula
\varphi_{R}

defines

R
.

Example 2.4.

Consider the following
\mathsf{FC}
-formula:

\varphi_{w}(x):=\neg\exists z_{1},z_{2}\colon\bigl(((z_{1}\mathbin{\dot{=}}z_{2}\cdot x)\lor(z_{1}\mathbin{\dot{=}}x\cdot z_{2}))\land\neg(z_{2}\mathbin{\dot{=}}\varepsilon)\bigr).

This formula states that there is no factor that is a concatenation of
\sigma(x)
and a non-empty word
\sigma(z_{2})
.
Thus, for
w\in\Sigma^{*}
, it must be that
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(w)=\{\sigma\}
where
\sigma(x)=w
.
Note that we use
(z_{2}\mathbin{\dot{=}}\varepsilon)
as a shorthand for
(z_{2}\mathbin{\dot{=}}\varepsilon\cdot\varepsilon)
.

We can use
\varphi_{w}(x)
to define the following sentence:

\varphi_{ww}:=\exists x\colon\exists y\colon\bigl(\varphi_{w}(x)\land(x\mathbin{\dot{=}}yy)\bigr).

It follows that
\mathfrak{A}\models\varphi_{ww}
if and only if
\mathfrak{A}
is a
\tau_{\Sigma}
-structure that represents
ww
for some
w\in\Sigma^{*}
.

Now consider
\varphi(x,y):=(x\mathbin{\dot{=}}yy)
.
If follows that
\varphi
defines the relation
R_{\mathsf{copy}}:=\{(u,v)\subseteq{(\Sigma^{*})}^{2}\mid u=vv\}
.
Since we know that
R_{\mathsf{copy}}
can be defined in
\mathsf{FC}
, we can use it as an additional atomic formula without changing the expressive power of
\mathsf{FC}
.
Furthermore, it is rather straightforward to generalize this relation to the
\mathsf{FC}
definable relation
R_{k\mathsf{\mathchar 45\relax copies}}:=\{(u,v)\in(\Sigma^{*})^{2}\mid u=v^{k}\}
.

Before moving on, we first make a brief note on the difference between the definition of
\mathsf{FC}
in this paper, and the definition given in
(
Freydenberger and Peterfreund 2021
)
.
First, the definition of
\mathsf{FC}
given in
(
Freydenberger and Peterfreund 2021
)
allows for an arbitrarily large right-hand side.
That is, atomic formulas of the form
x\mathbin{\dot{=}}\alpha
where
x\in\Xi
and
\alpha\in(\Sigma\mathrel{\cup}\Xi)^{*}
.
Whereas we use
(x\mathbin{\dot{=}}y\cdot z)
as atomic formulas, where
x,y,z\in\Xi\mathrel{\cup}\Sigma\mathrel{\cup}\{\varepsilon\}
.
It is clear that an arbitrarily large right-hand side is shorthand for a binary concatenation term (for example, see Freydenberger and Thompson
(
Freydenberger and Thompson 2022
)
).
The reason behind our choice, is that we have a finite signature
\tau_{\Sigma}
which more closely aligns with “traditional” finite-model theory.
The second difference is that we do not use a
universe variable

\mathfrak{u}
to denote the “input word” (see
(
Freydenberger and Peterfreund 2021
)
for more details).
However, referring back to
Example

2.4
, we can simply use a subformula to simulate the behaviour of
\mathfrak{u}
.
While there is also a small difference with the semantic definitions (such as the use of
\perp
), these differences are negligible and do not change the expressive power.

3.
Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé Games for FC.

In this section, we discuss the use of Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé games for
\mathsf{FC}
.
Although broader definitions of the concepts defined here exist (see e.g.
(
Ebbinghaus and Flum 1999
;
Immerman 1998
;
Libkin 2004
)
), we tailor our definitions to the logic
\mathsf{FC}
.

Definition 3.1 (Partial Isomorphism).

Let
\mathfrak{A}_{w}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{v}
be two
\tau_{\Sigma}
-structures that represent
w\in\Sigma^{*}
and
v\in\Sigma^{*}
, respectively.
Let
A:=\mathsf{Facs}(w)\mathrel{\cup}\{\perp\}
and
B:=\mathsf{Facs}(v)\mathrel{\cup}\{\perp\}
be the universes of these structures.
For
\vec{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
and
\vec{b}=(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})
where
a_{i}\in A
and
b_{i}\in B
for all
i\in[n]
, we say that
(\vec{a},\vec{b})
defines a
partial isomorphism
between
\mathfrak{A}_{w}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{v}
if:

•

For every
i\in[n]
, and constant symbol
c\in\tau_{\Sigma}
, we have
a_{i}=c^{\mathfrak{A}_{w}}
if and only if
b_{i}=c^{\mathfrak{B}_{v}}
,

•

for every
i,j\in[n]
, we have that
a_{i}=a_{j}
if and only if
b_{i}=b_{j}
, and

•

for every
i,j,k\in[n]
, we have that
a_{i}=a_{j}\cdot a_{k}
if and only if
b_{i}=b_{j}\cdot b_{k}
.

We now discuss Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé games.
This game is played between two players, called
Spoiler
and
Duplicator
.
For our purposes, Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé games are played on two
\tau_{\Sigma}
-structures
\mathfrak{A}_{w}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{v}
that represent some
w\in\Sigma^{*}
and
v\in\Sigma^{*}
respectively.
Let
A:=\mathsf{Facs}(w)\mathrel{\cup}\{\perp\}
and
B:=\mathsf{Facs}(v)\mathrel{\cup}\{\perp\}
.
The players play a fixed number (which we often denote with
k\in\mathbb{N}
) of rounds.
For any
i\leq k
, the
i
-th round is played as follows:

Spoiler’s Move::

Spoiler picks a structure
\mathfrak{A}_{w}
or
\mathfrak{B}_{v}
, and then picks some element
a_{i}\in A
if they chose
\mathfrak{A}_{w}
, or some
b_{i}\in B
if they chose
\mathfrak{B}_{v}
.

Duplicator’s Move::

Duplicator then responds by picking some element in the other structure.

For any
\tau_{\Sigma}
-structure
\mathfrak{A}
, where
\Sigma:=\{\mathtt{a}_{1},\dots,\mathtt{a}_{n}\}
, we use
\vec{c}^{\,\mathfrak{A}}
to denote
(\mathtt{a}_{1}^{\mathfrak{A}},\dots,\mathtt{a}_{n}^{\mathfrak{A}},\varepsilon^{\mathfrak{A}})
.
Let
\mathfrak{A}_{w}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{v}
be two
\tau_{\Sigma}
structures that represent
w\in\Sigma^{*}
and
v\in\Sigma^{*}
respectively.
For a
k
-round game
\mathcal{G}
over
\mathfrak{A}_{w}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{v}
, let
a_{i}\in A
and
b_{i}\in B
be the elements chosen in round
i
for each
i\in[k]
.
Furthermore, let
\vec{a}=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{k+|\Sigma|+1})
and
\vec{b}=(b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{k+|\Sigma|+1})
be tuples of the chosen elements, combined with the tuples
\vec{c}^{\,\mathfrak{A}_{w}}
and
\vec{c}^{\,\mathfrak{B}_{v}}
respectively.
That is, the tuples consisting of the last
|\Sigma|+1
components of
\vec{a}
and
\vec{b}
are
\vec{c}^{\,\mathfrak{A}_{w}}
and
\vec{c}^{\,\mathfrak{B}_{v}}
respectively.
Then, Duplicator has won the
k
-round game if and only if
(\vec{a},\vec{b})
is a partial isomorphism between
\mathfrak{A}_{w}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{v}
.

For a
k
-round game
\mathcal{G}
over
\mathfrak{A}_{w}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{v}
, we say that Duplicator has a
winning strategy
for
\mathcal{G}
if and only if there is a way for Duplicator to play such that no matter what Spoiler chooses, Duplicator can win
\mathcal{G}
.
Otherwise, Spoiler has a winning strategy for
\mathcal{G}
.
If Duplicator has a winning strategy for
\mathcal{G}
, then we write
\mathfrak{A}_{w}\equiv_{k}\mathfrak{B}_{v}
.
Since
\mathfrak{A}_{w}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{v}
are uniquely determined by
w,v\in\Sigma^{*}
, we can simply write
w\equiv_{k}v
when convenient.

Example 3.2.

Let
\Sigma=\{\mathtt{a}\}
.
For any
i\in\mathbb{N}
, Spoiler has a 2-round winning strategy on
\mathfrak{A}_{w}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{v}
where
w:=\mathtt{a}^{2i}
and
v:=\mathtt{a}^{2i-1}
:

Round One.:

Spoiler chooses the structure
\mathfrak{A}_{w}
and the factor
a_{1}=\mathtt{a}^{2i}
. Duplicator responds with some
b_{1}\sqsubseteq v
.

Round Two.:

The choice for Spoiler in this round is broken into two cases, depending on Duplicator’s choice in round one:

(1):

If Duplicator responds in round one with
b_{1}=\mathtt{a}^{2i-1}
, then Spoiler chooses the structure
\mathfrak{A}_{w}
and
a_{2}=\mathtt{a}^{i}
, as there does not exists a factor
b_{2}\sqsubseteq v
such that
v=b_{2}\cdot b_{2}
, Spoiler has won after two rounds.

(2):

If Duplicator responds in round one with
b_{1}\neq\mathtt{a}^{2i-1}
, then Spoiler chooses the structure
\mathfrak{B}_{v}
and
b_{2}=b_{1}\cdot\mathtt{a}
.
Duplicator must respond with some factor
a_{2}
, however a factor of the form
a_{1}\cdot\mathtt{a}
does not exist.

For either of the cases given in round two, we have that
(a_{1},a_{2},\mathtt{a},\varepsilon)
and
(b_{1},b_{2},\mathtt{a},\varepsilon)
does not form a partial isomorphism and thus, Spoiler has a winning strategy for the two round game.
Consequently,
\mathtt{a}^{2i}\not\equiv_{2}\mathtt{a}^{2i-1}
for any
i\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
.

Next, we connect Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé games to
\mathsf{FC}
.
Each
\varphi\in\mathsf{FC}
has a so-called
quantifier rank
, denoted by the function
\mathsf{qr}\colon\mathsf{FC}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
.
Let
\varphi,\psi\in\mathsf{FC}
.
We define
\mathsf{qr}
recursively as follows:

•

If
\varphi\in\mathsf{FC}
is an atomic formula, then
\mathsf{qr}(\varphi):=0
,

•

\mathsf{qr}(\neg\varphi):=\mathsf{qr}(\varphi)
,

•

\mathsf{qr}(\varphi\land\psi)=\mathsf{qr}(\varphi\lor\psi):=\mathsf{max}\left(\mathsf{qr}(\varphi),\mathsf{qr}(\psi)\right)
, and

•

\mathsf{qr}(Qx\colon\varphi):=\mathsf{qr}(\varphi)+1
for any
x\in\Xi
and
Q\in\{\forall,\exists\}
.

Let
\mathsf{FC}(k)
denote the set of sentences
\varphi\in\mathsf{FC}
such that
\mathsf{qr}(\varphi)\leq k
.

Note that the set of all
\tau_{\Sigma}
-structures
\mathfrak{A}_{w}
that represent
w\in\Sigma^{*}
are simply a subset of all structures over a signature that contains a ternary relational symbol, and
|\Sigma|+1
constant symbols.
Therefore, we can state an
\mathsf{FC}
-version of the the Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé theorem (see e.g.
(
Ebbinghaus and Flum 1999
;
Immerman 1998
;
Libkin 2004
)
for the general version).

Theorem 3.3.

Let
\mathfrak{A}_{w},\mathfrak{B}_{v}
be
\tau_{\Sigma}
-structures that represent
w\in\Sigma^{*}
and
v\in\Sigma^{*}
respectively. Then, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

\mathfrak{A}_{w}\equiv_{k}\mathfrak{B}_{w}
,

(2)

\mathfrak{A}_{w}\models\varphi
if and only if
\mathfrak{B}_{v}\models\varphi
for all
\varphi\in\mathsf{FC}(k)
.

Thus,
Theorem

3.3
is useful for proving inexpressibility for first-order logic over finite models.
A slight reformulation of
Theorem

3.3
yields the following observation:

Lemma 3.4.

Let
L\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
. If for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exist
w\in L
and
u\notin L
where
w\equiv_{k}u
, then
L
is not an
\mathsf{FC}
-language.

For our next step, we rely on an inexpressibility technique for languages over unary alphabets.
Over a unary alphabet
\{\mathtt{a}\}
, we can identify every unary word
\mathtt{a}^{n}
with the natural number
n
, which allows us to treat languages
L\subseteq\{\mathtt{a}\}^{*}
as sets
S_{L}
of natural numbers.

A set
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is
linear
if there exist an
r\geq 0
and
m_{0},\dots,m_{r}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
S=\{m_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{r}m_{i}n_{i}\mid n_{i}\geq 0\}
.
A set is
semi-linear
if it is a finite union of linear sets, and we call a unary language
L
semi-linear if the corresponding set
S_{L}
is semi-linear.

Over a unary alphabet, semi-linear languages are exactly the languages defined by
Presburger arithmetic
(Ginsburg and Spanier
(
Ginsburg and Spanier 1966a
)
), core spanners (Freydenberger and Holldack
(
Freydenberger and Holldack 2018
)
) or generalized core spanners (Peterfreund, ten Cate, Fagin, and Kimelfeld
(
Peterfreund et al. 2019
)
). By Freydenberger and Peterfreund
(
Freydenberger and Peterfreund 2021
)
, this means that
\mathsf{FC}
(over unary alphabets) defines exactly the semi-linear languages.

As
2^{n}
grows faster than any linear function, no finite union of linear sets can define
\{2^{n}\}
.
Hence,
L_{\mathsf{pow}}:=\{\mathtt{a}^{2^{n}}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is not semi-linear and, therefore, is not expressible in
\mathsf{FC}
.
From this, we almost directly infer the following:

Lemma 3.5.

Let
\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma
.
For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathtt{a}^{p}\equiv_{k}\mathtt{a}^{q}
where
p\neq q
.

Lemma

3.5
is one of our main building blocks for
\mathsf{FC}
inexpressibility.
That is, we shall utilize Duplicator’s winning strategy for the
k
-round game over structures that represent
\mathtt{a}^{p}
and
\mathtt{a}^{q}
in order to construct winning strategies for more general structures.

A binary relation
R\subseteq(\Sigma^{*})^{2}
is a
congruence relation
if
a\mathrel{R}b
and
c\mathrel{R}d
implies
(a\cdot c)\mathrel{R}(b\cdot d)
.
When considering the more common
\mathsf{FO}[<]
over words (where words are encoded as a linear order with symbol predicates), we have that
\equiv_{k}
is a congruence relation (see Section 3.2 of
(
Thomas 1993
)
).
Unfortunately, this does not hold for
\mathsf{FC}
.

Proposition 3.6.

There exists
u,u^{\prime},v,v^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{*}
where, for any
k\geq 5
, we have that
u\equiv_{k}v
and
u^{\prime}\equiv_{k}v^{\prime}
, but
u\cdot u^{\prime}\not\equiv_{k}v\cdot v^{\prime}
.

Proposition

3.6
rules out what would be a convenient technique for strategy composition.
Despite this, in
Section

4.1
we consider a special case where
\equiv
can be used as if it were a congruence relation.

4.
FC Inexpressibility

Our next goal is using Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé games in order to yield inexpressibility results for
\mathsf{FC}
.
To do so, we heavily utilize
strategy composition
.
That is, we “bootstrap” known winning strategies for Duplicator – gathered from inexpressibility results such as
Lemma

3.5
– and construct more general winning strategies for Duplicator.
The main technical contribution of this section is the Fooling Lemma for
\mathsf{FC}
that is derived from the
Pseudo-Congruence Lemma
and the
Primitive Power Lemma
.

Section

4.1
contains the Pseudo-Congruence Lemma (
Lemma

4.4
), and
Section

4.2
contains the Primitive Power Lemma (
Lemma

4.9
).
Both these sections also contain many useful lemmas on the way to proving the respective result.
Before looking at these lemmas, we first demonstrate the capabilities of
\mathsf{FC}
by giving a language that (somewhat surprisingly) is expressible in
\mathsf{FC}
.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define
F_{n}\in\{\mathtt{a},\mathtt{b}\}^{*}
recursively as follows:
F_{0}:=\mathtt{a}
,
F_{1}:=\mathtt{ab}
, and
F_{i}:=F_{i-1}\cdot F_{i-2}
for all
i\geq 2
.
The
Fibonacci word
is the limit
F_{\omega}
(see
(
Lothaire 2002
)
for details on
F_{\omega}
and its properties).
We show that the language
L_{\mathsf{fib}}
containing those words
\mathtt{c}F_{0}\mathtt{c}F_{1}\mathtt{c}\cdots\mathtt{c}F_{n}\mathtt{c}
for
\mathtt{c}\in\Sigma
and for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
is expressible in
\mathsf{FC}
.

Proposition 4.1.

L_{\mathsf{fib}}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{FC})
.

While the definition suggests that expressing
L_{\mathsf{fib}}
requires a logic that allows recursion, we can use the universal quantifier to simulate recursion in certain cases.
As a curious aside, this also shows that
\mathsf{FC}
does not have a pumping lemma in the sense that for sufficiently long words, some non-empty factor can be repeated an arbitrary number of times without falling out of the language.
This is due to the fact that
F_{\omega}
does not contain any factor of the form
u^{4}
with
u\neq\varepsilon
, see Karhumäki
(
Karhumäki 1983
)
.

4.1.
The Pseudo-Congruence Lemma

In this section, we first give some necessary lemmas, we then give the Pseudo-Congruence Lemma along with a proof idea.
To conclude this section, we consider some consequences.

First, we consider the case where Spoiler has chosen a factor that is so short (with respect to the number of remaining rounds) that Duplicator must respond with the identical factor or lose.

Lemma 4.2.

Let
\mathfrak{A}_{w}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{v}
be
\tau_{\Sigma}
-structures that represent
w\in\Sigma^{*}
and
v\in\Sigma^{*}
, where
\mathfrak{A}_{w}\equiv_{k}\mathfrak{B}_{v}
.
Let
\vec{a}=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{k+|\Sigma|+1})
and
\vec{b}=(b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{k+|\Sigma|+1})
be the tuple resulting from a
k
-round game over
\mathfrak{A}_{w}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{v}
where Duplicator plays their winning strategy.
If
r+|a_{r}|-1<k
or
r+|b_{r}|-1<k
for some
r\in[k]
, then
b_{r}=a_{r}
.

Next, we show that for rounds
r
with
r\leq k-2
, if Spoiler picks a prefix (or a suffix), then Duplicator must respond with a prefix (or suffix, respectively); assuming they play a winning strategy.

Lemma 4.3.

Let
\mathfrak{A}_{w}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{v}
be two
\tau_{\Sigma}
-structures that represent
w\in\Sigma^{*}
and
v\in\Sigma^{*}
, where
\mathfrak{A}_{w}\equiv_{k}\mathfrak{B}_{v}
.
Let
\vec{a}=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{k+|\Sigma|+1})
and
\vec{b}=(b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{k+|\Sigma|+1})
be the tuple resulting from a
k
-round game over
\mathfrak{A}_{w}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{v}
where Duplicator plays their winning strategy.
For any
r\leq k-2
, we have that
a_{r}
is a suffix (or prefix) of
w
if and only if
b_{r}
is a suffix (or prefix respectively) of
v
.

While we mainly use
Lemma

4.2
and
Lemma

4.3
to prove the Pseudo-Congruence Lemma, they also provide some insights into necessary conditions of Duplicator’s strategy.

In some of the subsequent proofs, we use strategy compositions that require quite technical proofs of correctness.
To avoid handwaving, we shall sometimes define Duplicator’s strategy using what we call
look-up games
.
If Spoiler and Duplicator are playing a
k
-round game
\mathcal{G}
over
\mathfrak{A}_{w}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{v}
, a
look-up
game is a
k^{\prime}
-round auxiliary game
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
over two (potentially different) structures
\mathfrak{C}
and
\mathfrak{D}
, where
\mathfrak{C}\equiv_{k^{\prime}}\mathfrak{D}
.
The idea is that in the
i
-th round in
\mathcal{G}
, each move by Spoiler corresponds to a move by Spoiler in
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
.
Then, Duplicator “looks up” what their response would be in
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
to form their response to Spoiler in
\mathcal{G}
.
Generalizing this idea, Duplicator can use multiple look-up games
\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2},\dots,\mathcal{G}_{n}
to form their response in
\mathcal{G}
.
In other words, if Duplicator has winning strategies in
\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2},\dots,\mathcal{G}_{n}
, then Duplicator has a winning strategy in
\mathcal{G}
.

While
\equiv_{k}
is not a congruence relation in general (see
Proposition

3.6
), we identify a special case where we can use
\equiv
as if it were a congruence relation.

Lemma 4.4 (Pseudo-Congruence Lemma).

Let
w_{1},w_{2},v_{1},v_{2}\in\Sigma^{*}
where
\mathsf{Facs}(w_{1})\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{Facs}(w_{2})=\mathsf{Facs}(v_{1})\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{Facs}(v_{2})

If
w_{1}\equiv_{k+r+2}v_{1}
and
w_{2}\equiv_{k+r+2}v_{2}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, then
w_{1}\cdot w_{2}\equiv_{k}v_{1}\cdot v_{2}
.

We consider two look-up games
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}

For

and

over

Freydenberger and Peterfreund
(
Freydenberger and Peterfreund 2021
)
showed that the language
\{\mathtt{a}^{n}\cdot\mathtt{b}^{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is not expressible in
\mathsf{FC}
.
Their proof uses an alternative logic
\mathsf{FO}[\mathsf{EQ}]
with equivalent expressive power, for which the Feferman-Vaught theorem can be invoked.
As this approach heavily relies on the restricted nature of words
\mathtt{a}^{n}\cdot\mathtt{b}^{m}
,
it seems to be very difficult to generalize.
Furthermore, even for
\mathsf{FO}[\mathsf{EQ}]
, the Feferman-Vaught theorem needs some additional reasoning.
Compare this to the following alternative proof.

Example 4.5.

From
Lemma

3.5
, we know that for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
where
\mathtt{a}^{p}\equiv_{k+2}\mathtt{a}^{q}
and, without loss of generality,
p<q
.
Trivially, we have that
\mathtt{b}^{p}\equiv_{k+2}\mathtt{b}^{p}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Furthermore,
\mathsf{Facs}(\mathtt{a}^{n})\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{Facs}(\mathtt{b}^{m})=\{\varepsilon\}
for any
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
.
We can now invoke
Lemma

4.4
, with
r=0
, to state that for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathtt{a}^{q}\cdot\mathtt{b}^{p}\equiv_{k}\mathtt{a}^{p}\cdot\mathtt{b}^{p}
and
p<q
.
Consequently,
\{\mathtt{a}^{i}\cdot\mathtt{b}^{j}\mid 0\leq i\leq j\}\notin\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{FC})
and
\{\mathtt{a}^{n}\cdot\mathtt{b}^{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}\notin\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{FC})
.

In addition to giving an alternative proof of an existing result, we can also use
Lemma

4.4
to prove new inexpressibility results.

Proposition 4.6.

\{\mathtt{a}^{n}\cdot(\mathtt{ba})^{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}\notin\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{FC})
.

Proof.

To prove this result, we show that for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
where
p<q
and
\mathtt{a}^{q}(\mathtt{ba})^{q}\equiv_{k}\mathtt{a}^{p}(\mathtt{ba})^{q}
.

From
Lemma

3.5
, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathtt{a}^{q}\equiv_{k+3}\mathtt{a}^{p}
where
q\neq p
.
Without loss of generality, assume that
p<q
.
Trivially,
(\mathtt{ba})^{q}\equiv_{k+3}(\mathtt{ba})^{q}
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Since,
\mathsf{Facs}(\mathtt{a}^{m})\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{Facs}((\mathtt{ba})^{n})=\{\varepsilon,\mathtt{a}\}
for any
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
, we can invoke
Lemma

4.4
with
r=1
.
It therefore follows that for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
where
p\neq q
and
\mathtt{a}^{q}(\mathtt{ba})^{q}\equiv_{k}\mathtt{a}^{p}(\mathtt{ba})^{q}
.
From
Lemma

3.4
we infer that
\{\mathtt{a}^{n}\cdot(\mathtt{ba})^{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}\notin\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{FC})
.
∎

Observing
Proposition

4.6
, we see that the Pseudo-Congruence Lemma by itself is a useful tool for inexpressibility.

4.2.
The Primitive Power Lemma

In this section, we look at the Primitive Power Lemma.
Before giving this result, we first consider a lemma for words that are primitive (that is, not a power of a shorter word).

For any
w\in\Sigma^{+}
, let
\mathsf{exp}_{w}\colon\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
be a function that maps
u\in\Sigma^{+}
to
m\in\mathbb{N}
if
w^{m}\sqsubseteq u
and there does not exist
m^{\prime}>m
such that
w^{m^{\prime}}\sqsubseteq u
.
Less formally,
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)
is the maximum value
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
w^{m}
is a factor of
u
.

Example 4.7.

Consider
u:=\mathtt{aaaabaabaab}
.
Then,
\mathsf{exp}_{\mathtt{a}}(u)=4
, and
\mathsf{exp}_{\mathtt{aab}}(u)=3
.

The following is due to some basic results on primitive words.

Lemma 4.8.

Let
w\in\Sigma^{+}
be a primitive word and let
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
If
u\sqsubseteq w^{m}
where
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)>0
, then there is a unique proper suffix
u_{1}\in\Sigma^{*}
of
w
and a unique proper prefix
u_{2}\in\Sigma^{*}
of
w
, such that
u=u_{1}\cdot w^{\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)}\cdot u_{2}
.

While perhaps not particularly interesting by itself,
Lemma

4.8
shall be an important part of the proof of the Primitive Power Lemma – a result that (in a sense) generalizes
Lemma

3.5
.

Lemma 4.9 (Primitive Power Lemma).

If
\mathtt{a}^{p}\equiv_{k+3}\mathtt{a}^{q}
for
\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma
and
p,q,k\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, then
w^{p}\equiv_{k}w^{q}
for any primitive word
w\in\Sigma^{+}
.

k

\mathcal{G}
over
w^{p}
and

.

k+3

\mathcal{G}_{l}
over
\mathtt{a}^{p}
and
\mathtt{a}^{q}
, where
\mathtt{a}^{p}\equiv_{k+3}\mathtt{a}^{q}
.

For each round

in
\mathcal{G}

where
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)=n
, then we let Spoiler choose
\mathtt{a}^{n}
in round

of
\mathcal{G}_{l}
in the corresponding word.
Duplicator’s response in
\mathcal{G}_{l}
is some
\mathtt{a}^{m}
. Then, we form Duplicator’s response to Spoiler in

•

If in
\mathcal{G}_{l}
, Duplicator responds with
\varepsilon

u
where
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)=0
.

•

If in
\mathcal{G}_{l}
, Duplicator responds with
\mathtt{a}^{m}
where
m\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, then we can show that in
\mathcal{G}

u=u_{1}\cdot w^{n}\cdot u_{2}

u_{1}

). We define Duplicator’s response in
\mathcal{G}
as
u_{1}\cdot w^{m}\cdot u_{2}

For any
w\in\Sigma^{+}
, the
primitive root
of
w
is the unique primitive word
z\in\Sigma^{*}
such that
w=z^{k}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Therefore, almost immediately from
Lemma

4.9
, we are able to show:

Proposition 4.10.

For any
w\in\Sigma^{+}
and any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
p\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and
v\in\Sigma^{*}
such that
w^{p}\equiv_{k}v
where
w^{p}\neq v
.

As an immediate consequence of
Lemma

4.9
, it follows that for any primitive word
w\in\Sigma^{+}
, the language
w^{2^{n}}
is not expressible in
\mathsf{FC}
.
While this may seem somewhat restrictive, due to the fact that this result holds for primitive words, any non-primitive word is a repetition of primitive words, hence
Proposition

4.10
.

4.3.
The Fooling Lemma for FC

The Fooling Lemma for
\mathsf{FC}
combines the Pseudo-Congruence Lemma with the Primitive Power Lemma.
In order to give a convenient formulation of this result, we first look at
co-primitive
words:
For two words
w,v\in\Sigma^{+}
, we say that
w
and
v
are
conjugate
if there exists
x,y\in\Sigma^{*}
such that
w=x\cdot y
and
v=y\cdot x
.
If
w
and
v
are not conjugate, and both
w
and
v
are primitive words, then we say that
w
and
v
are
co-primitive
.

Example 4.11.

It is clear that
u:=\mathtt{aabba}
and
v:=\mathtt{aaabb}
are both primitive words.
However,
u
and
v
are not co-primitive.
This can easily be observed by considering
x:=\mathtt{aabb}
and
y:=\mathtt{a}
.
Then,
u=xy
and
v=yx
.
Now consider
u^{\prime}:=\mathtt{aba}
and
v^{\prime}:=\mathtt{bba}
.
Again, both
u^{\prime}
and
v^{\prime}
are primitive.
Furthermore, from the fact that
|u^{\prime}|_{\mathtt{a}}\neq|v^{\prime}|_{\mathtt{a}}
, we can also see that
u^{\prime}
and
v^{\prime}
are co-primitive.

For any two words
w,v\in\Sigma^{*}
, let
w^{\omega}=w\cdot w\cdots
and
v^{\omega}:=v\cdot v\cdots
be the one-sided infinite word consisting of continuous repetitions of
w
and
v
respectively.

We now state the
periodicity lemma
.
For our purposes, we use the formulation of this result given in
(
Hadravová and Holub 2015
)
.
Further information regarding the periodicity lemma can be found in
(
Rozenberg and Salomaa 1997
)
.

Lemma 4.12 (Periodicity lemma).

Let
w
and
v
be primitive words.
If
w^{\omega}
and
v^{\omega}
have a common factor of length at least
|w|+|v|-1
, then
w
and
v
are conjugate.

The periodicity lemma and some further reasoning gives us:

Lemma 4.13.

For any primitive words
w,v\in\Sigma^{*}
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

w
and
v
are co-primitive,

(2)

there exists
n_{0},m_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathsf{Facs}(w^{n_{0}})\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{Facs}(v^{m_{0}})=\mathsf{Facs}(w^{n})\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{Facs}(v^{m})
for all
n>n_{0}
and all
m>m_{0}
, and

(3)

there exists
r\in\mathbb{N}
such that
r\geq\mathsf{max}\{|w|\in\mathbb{N}\mid w\in\mathsf{Facs}(u^{n})\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{Facs}(v^{m})\}
for all
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
.

We are almost ready to give the Fooling Lemma for
\mathsf{FC}
.
Before doing so, the Fooling Lemma is simply a possible way of combining the results we have given so far, and there could be a more general inexpressibility lemma for
\mathsf{FC}
just using the results from this paper.
However, for our purposes,
Lemma

4.14
is enough.

Lemma 4.14 (Fooling Lemma).

Let
w_{1},w_{2},w_{3}\in\Sigma^{*}
, let
u,v\in\Sigma^{+}
be two co-primitive words, let
\varphi\in\mathsf{FC}
, and let
f\colon\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
be an injective function.
If
w_{1}\cdot u^{p}\cdot w_{2}\cdot v^{f(p)}\cdot w_{3}\in\mathcal{L}(\varphi)
for all
p\in\mathbb{N}
, then
w_{1}\cdot u^{s}\cdot w_{2}\cdot v^{t}\cdot w_{3}\in\mathcal{L}(\varphi)
for some
s,t\in\mathbb{N}
, where
f(s)\neq t
.

Proof Sketch.

We use the Primitive Power Lemma along with the Pseudo-Congruence Lemma to determine that for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
such that
w_{1}\cdot u^{p}\cdot w_{2}\equiv_{k}w_{1}\cdot u^{q}\cdot w_{2}
where
p\neq q
.
Then, we again invoke the Pseudo-Congruence Lemma (using
Lemma

4.13
to ensure that the required prerequisites hold) to conclude that for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
where
p\neq q
and
w_{1}\cdot u^{p}\cdot w_{2}\cdot v^{f(p)}\cdot w_{3}\equiv_{k}w_{1}\cdot u^{q}\cdot w_{2}\cdot v^{f(p)}\cdot w_{3}
.
Since
f
is injective,
f(p)\neq f(q)
if
p\neq q
.
∎

Immediately from
Lemma

4.14
, we infer the following:

Proposition 4.15.

Let
w_{1},w_{2},w_{3}\in\Sigma^{*}
, and let
u,v\in\Sigma^{+}
be two co-primitive words.
Then, for any injective function
f\colon\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
, the language
\{w_{1}\cdot u^{p}\cdot w_{2}\cdot v^{f(p)}\cdot w_{3}\mid p\in\mathbb{N}\}

is not an
\mathsf{FC}
-language.

So far, we have developed several inexpressibility techniques.
Next, we look at using these tools to show that the following languages are not
\mathsf{FC}
languages.

Lemma 4.16.

The following languages are not expressible in
\mathsf{FC}
:

•

L_{1}:=\{\mathtt{a}^{n}\cdot(\mathtt{ba})^{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
,

•

L_{2}:=\{\mathtt{a}^{i}\cdot(\mathtt{ba})^{j}\mid 1\leq i\leq j\}
,

•

L_{3}:=\{\mathtt{b}^{n}\cdot\mathtt{a}^{m}\cdot\mathtt{b}^{n+m}\mid m,n\in\mathbb{N}\}
,

•

L_{4}:=\{\mathtt{b}^{n}\cdot\mathtt{a}^{m}\cdot\mathtt{b}^{n\cdot m}\mid m,n\in\mathbb{N}\}
,

•

L_{5}:=\{(\mathtt{abaabb})^{m}\cdot(\mathtt{bbaaba})^{m}\mid m\in\mathbb{N}\}
, and

•

L_{6}:=\{\mathtt{a}^{n}\cdot\mathtt{b}^{n}\cdot(\mathtt{ab})^{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
.

The authors note that it seems at least possible to show that
L_{3}
and
L_{4}
in
Lemma

4.16
are not
\mathsf{FC}
languages by using the Feferman-Vaught Theorem and the logic
\mathsf{FO}[\mathsf{EQ}]
(see
(
Freydenberger and Peterfreund 2021
)
for more details).
However, in order to do so, a sizeable amount of work would be needed.
In comparison, using the techniques developed in this work, it is rather straightforward to show
L_{3},L_{4}\notin\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{FC})
.

While
Lemma

4.16
seems somewhat “language theoretic”, in
Theorem

5.5
we shall use
Lemma

4.16
to show that several relations are not definable in
\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}]
.

5.
Generalized Core Spanner Inexpressibility

Freydenberger and Peterfreund
(
Freydenberger and Peterfreund 2021
)
proved that
\mathsf{FC}
, extended with so-called
regular constraints
captures the expressive power of generalized core spanners.
That is, a word relation
R\subseteq(\Sigma^{*})^{k}
is definable in
\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}]
if and only if
R
is
selectable
by generalized core spanners.
We do not give the definition of generalized core spanners here, since we are able to simply use
\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}]
instead.
See
(
Fagin et al. 2015
)
for the formal definitions of generalized core spanners
1
1

1

Note that in
(
Fagin et al. 2015
)
what we call generalized core spanners is called core spanners with difference. The term “generalized core spanner” was coined in
(
Peterfreund et al. 2019
)
.
.

Definition 5.1.

A
regular constraint
is an atomic formula
(x\mathbin{\dot{\in}}\gamma)
where
x\in\Xi
and
\gamma
is a regular expression.
Let
\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}]
be the logic that extends
\mathsf{FC}
with regular constraints.
The semantics are as follows: For a regular constraint
(x\mathbin{\dot{\in}}\gamma)
, and an
\mathsf{FC}
interpretation
\mathcal{I}:=(\mathfrak{A}_{w},\sigma)
, where
\mathfrak{A}_{w}
is a
\tau_{\Sigma}
-structure that represents
w\in\Sigma^{*}
, we write
\mathcal{I}\models(x\mathbin{\dot{\in}}\gamma)
if
\sigma(x)\sqsubseteq w
, and
\sigma(x)\in\mathcal{L}(\gamma)
.

Unfortunately, the introduction of regular constraints also introduces some difficulties when dealing with the quantifier rank of a formula.
Whilst one could easily adapt the definition of quantifier rank to
\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}]
, we run into the issue that there are infinite
\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}]
formulas of quantifier rank one.
Consider
\exists x\colon(x\mathbin{\dot{\in}}\gamma)
, along with the infinite number of regular languages.
This would mean that we cannot use
Theorem

3.3
for
\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}]
, which requires (up to logical equivalence) a finite number of formulas of quantifier rank
k
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
(see, e.g.,
(
Libkin 2004
)
for more details).

We now consider a way to handle the regular constraints in order to derive inexpressibility results for generalized core spanners.

Definition 5.2.

A language
L\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
is
bounded
if it is a subset of a language
w_{1}^{*}\cdot w_{2}^{*}\cdots w_{n}^{*}
where
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
w_{i}\in\Sigma^{*}
for all
i\in[n]
.

A
bounded regular language
is simply a language that is both bounded, and regular.
From previous literature (see
(
Freydenberger 2019
;
Freydenberger and Peterfreund 2021
)
for example), we know that every bounded regular language can be expressed in
\mathsf{FC}
.
Thus, using a very similar proof idea to Theorem 6.2 from
(
Freydenberger 2019
)
, we are able to show the following:

Lemma 5.3.

If
L\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
is a Boolean combination of bounded languages, then
L\in\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{FC})
if and only if
L\in\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}])
.

From Lemma 5.5 in
(
Freydenberger and Peterfreund 2021
)
, we know that if every regular constraint in an
\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}]
-formula is a so-called
simple regular expression
, then we can rewrite the
\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}]
-formula into an
\mathsf{FC}
-formula.
We can therefore replace every constraint that is a Boolean combination of simple regular expressions with an
\mathsf{FC}
-formula.
We do not go further into this, as
Lemma

5.3
is all that we require.

Before looking at relations that are not definable by
\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}]
, we first define some concepts.

For
x,y\in\Sigma^{*}
, we say that
x
is a
scattered subword
of
y
(denoted by
x\sqsubseteq_{\mathsf{scatt}}y
) if for some
n\geq 1
, there exists
x_{1},x_{2},\dots x_{n}\in\Sigma^{*}
and
y_{0},y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\in\Sigma^{*}
such that
x=x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{n}
and
y=y_{0}x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}y_{2}\cdots x_{n}y_{n}
.

A similar concept to scattered subwords is the
shuffle product
.
For
x,y\in\Sigma^{*}
, we define the shuffle product of
x
and
y
, denoted
x\shuffle y
, as the set of all words
x_{1}y_{1}\cdot x_{2}y_{2}\cdots x_{n}y_{n}
where
x=x_{1}\cdots x_{n}
and
y=y_{1}\cdots y_{n}
for
x_{i},y_{i}\in\Sigma^{*}
for all
i,j\in[n]
.

Example 5.4.

Consider the words
u:=\mathtt{abba}
and
v:=\mathtt{aa}
.
It is clear that
v\sqsubseteq_{\mathsf{scatt}}u
, and
\mathtt{ababaa}\in u\shuffle v
.

We are now ready to use
Lemma

4.16
to show that several relations are not selectable by generalized core spanners.

Theorem 5.5.

The following are not definable in
\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}]
:

•

\mathsf{Num}_{\mathtt{a}}:=\{(x,y)\in(\Sigma^{*})^{2}\mid|x|_{\mathtt{a}}=|y|_{\mathtt{a}}\}
for any
\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma
,

•

\mathsf{Add}:=\{(x,y,z)\in(\Sigma^{*})^{3}\mid|z|=|x|+|y|\}
,

•

\mathsf{Mult}:=\{(x,y,z)\in(\Sigma^{*})^{3}\mid|z|=|x|\cdot|y|\}
,

•

\mathsf{Scatt}:=\{(x,y)\in(\Sigma^{*})^{2}\mid x\sqsubseteq_{\mathsf{scatt}}y\}
,

•

\mathsf{Perm}:=\{(x,y)\in(\Sigma^{*})^{2}\mid x\text{ is a permutation of }y\}
,

•

\mathsf{Rev}:=\{(x,y)\in(\Sigma^{*})^{2}\mid x\text{ is the reverse of }y\}
,

•

\mathsf{Shuff}:=\{(x,y,z)\in(\Sigma^{*})^{3}\mid z\in x\shuffle y\}
,

•

\mathsf{Morph}_{h}:=\{(x,y)\in(\Sigma^{*})^{2}\mid y=h(x)\}
for a given morphism
h\colon\Sigma^{*}\rightarrow\Sigma^{*}
.

A
morphism
is a function
h\colon\Sigma^{*}\rightarrow\Sigma^{*}
with
h(xy)=h(x)\cdot h(y)
for all
x,y\in\Sigma^{*}
.

Proof Sketch.

Assuming some relation
R
from theorem statement is definable in
\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}]
, we can easily construct
\varphi\in\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}]
such that
\mathcal{L}(\varphi)
is a language given in
Lemma

4.16
.
This results in a contradiction due to
Lemma

5.3
.
A similar proof idea was used in Proposition 6.7 of
(
Freydenberger 2019
)
to show that several relations are not definable by core spanners.
∎

Immediately from
(
Freydenberger and Peterfreund 2021
)
, all the relations given in
Theorem

5.5
are not selectable by generalized core spanners.
Consequently, if one needed to express a query from
Theorem

5.5
, they would have to add a specialized operator to obtain this functionality.

For any relation
R\subseteq{(\Sigma^{*})}^{k}
, where
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have that
R
is definable in
\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}]
if and only if
{(\Sigma^{*})}^{k}\setminus R
is definable by
\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}]
.
This is simply due to the fact that
\mathsf{FC}
is closed under complementation.
Thus, the complement of all relations given in
Theorem

5.5
are also not selectable by generalized core spanners.

Core Spanners

Core spanners are a subclass of generalized core spanners (see
(
Fagin et al. 2015
)
).
While there are known inexpressibility results for core spanners (for example,
\mathsf{Num}_{\mathtt{a}}
,
\mathsf{Scatt}
,
\mathsf{Perm}
,
\mathsf{Rev}
, and
R_{<}:=\{(u,v)\mid|u|<|v|\}
were shown to be not selectable by core spanners in
(
Freydenberger 2019
)
, and
(
Fagin et al. 2015
)
proved that length equality is not selectable by core spanners), our results extend what is known about the expressive power of core spanners.
For example, since the complement of the relations given in
Theorem

5.5
are not selectable by generalized core spanners, they are also not selectable by core spanners.
Furthermore, we also show that
\mathsf{Add}
,
\mathsf{Mult}
,
\mathsf{Shuff}
and
\mathsf{Morph}_{h}
are not selectable by generalized core spanners, and thus are not selectable by core spanners.

6.
Conclusions

In
Section

3
, we established Ehrenfreucht-Fraïssé games for
\mathsf{FC}
.
Then, in
Section

4
, we gave two technical lemmas – the Pseudo-Congruence Lemma, and the Primitive Power Lemma – which serve the basis of the Fooling Lemma.
These lemmas are the main contributions of this work; however, in
Section

5
, we lifted our inexpressibility results to
\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}]
which immediately from
(
Freydenberger and Peterfreund 2021
)
yields inexpressibility results for generalized core spanners.

The main limitation of our results is that our generalized core spanner inexpressibility results require Boolean combinations of bounded languages.
It is possible to use closure properties in order to partially overcome this issue.
For example, consider the language
L:=\{w\in\Sigma^{*}\mid|w|_{\mathtt{a}}=|w|_{\mathtt{b}}\}
.
Since
\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}]
is closed under intersection with regular languages,
L\in\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}])
if and only if
L\mathrel{\cap}\mathtt{a}^{*}\mathtt{b}^{*}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}])
.
It is clear that
L\mathrel{\cap}\mathtt{a}^{*}\mathtt{b}^{*}
is the language
\mathtt{a}^{n}\mathtt{b}^{n}
and thus
L\notin\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}])
.

Such an approach can only get us so far, and therefore going beyond Boolean combinations of bounded languages is an important area for future research.
We note that it is still open as to whether
\mathsf{FC}
and
\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}]
have the same expressive power.
The authors believe that this is unlikely, although proving that
\mathsf{FC}
and
\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}]
do indeed have equivalent expressive power would remove the restriction of Boolean combinations of bounded languages.

As an intermediate step, one could look at more general regular languages that can be expressed by
\mathsf{FC}
.
This would close the gap between what we know can be expressed by
\mathsf{FC}
and what can be expressed by
\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}]
.
Then, a similar result to
Lemma

5.3
could gain further inexpressibility results for generalized core spanners.
A starting point for this direction would be to see whether regular constraints beyond the Boolean closure of
simple regular expressions
are achievable in
\mathsf{FC}
(see Lemma 5.5 in
(
Freydenberger and Peterfreund 2021
)
).

Another promising area of future work, is looking at related two player games.
For example, pebble games could be used to derive insights on
\mathsf{FC}
-formulas with a finite number of variables (for example, see Chapter 11 of
(
Libkin 2004
)
).
Alternatively, the restriction to existential Ehrenfreucht-Fraïssé games could yield further results regarding the inexpressibility of core spanners.

We note that there are many further open problems regarding
\mathsf{FC}
.

Proof of Lemma

Proof.

We prove this observation by a contradiction.
To that end, assume there exists
\varphi\in\mathsf{FC}
such that
\mathcal{L}(\varphi)=L
, and let
\mathsf{qr}(\varphi)=n
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Further assume that we have
w\equiv_{n}u
, where
w\in L
and
u\notin L
.
Since
w\in L
and
\mathcal{L}(\varphi)=L
, we know that
\mathfrak{A}_{w}\models\varphi
where
\mathfrak{A}_{w}
is the
\tau_{\Sigma}
-structure that represents
w
.
Furthermore, as
w\equiv_{n}u
, we know from
Theorem

3.3
that
\mathfrak{B}_{u}\models\varphi
where
\mathfrak{B}_{u}
is the
\tau_{\Sigma}
-structure that represents
u
.
Therefore,
u\in\mathcal{L}(\varphi)
which is a contradiction.
∎

A.2.
Proof of Lemma
3.5

Proof.

Working towards a contradiction, assume that there exists some
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
where
q
is not a power of two we have that
\mathtt{a}^{2^{p}}\not\equiv_{k}\mathtt{a}^{q}
.
Observing
Lemma

3.4
, there exists
\varphi\in\mathsf{FC}(k)
such that
\mathfrak{A}_{w}\models\varphi
if and only if
w=\mathtt{a}^{2^{n}}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
As
L_{\mathsf{pow}}:=\{\mathtt{a}^{2^{n}}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is not semi-linear, it is not definable in
\mathsf{FC}
. Thus, we have reached a contradiction.
∎

A.3.
Proof of Lemma
3.6

Proof.

For every
k
, there exists
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
where
p\neq q
and
\mathtt{a}^{p}\equiv_{k}\mathtt{a}^{q}
, see
Lemma

3.5
.
Trivially,
\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a}^{p}\equiv_{k}\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a}^{p}
.
But
\mathtt{a}^{p}\cdot\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a}^{p}\not\equiv_{k}\mathtt{a}^{q}\cdot\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a}^{p}
.
To show that this does indeed hold, we give an
\mathsf{FC}
-formula that accepts words of the form
v\cdot\mathtt{b}\cdot v
for
v\in\Sigma^{*}
, and thus can distinguish between
\mathtt{a}^{p}\cdot\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a}^{p}
and
\mathtt{a}^{q}\cdot\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a}^{p}
where
p\neq q
.

\varphi:=\exists x,y,z\colon\Bigl((y\mathbin{\dot{=}}x\cdot z)\land(z\mathbin{\dot{=}}\mathtt{b}\cdot x)\land\neg\exists z_{1},z_{2}\colon\bigl(((z_{1}\mathbin{\dot{=}}z_{2}\cdot y)\lor(z_{1}\mathbin{\dot{=}}y\cdot z_{2}))\land\neg(z_{2}\mathbin{\dot{=}}\varepsilon)\bigr)\Bigr).

For any
\tau_{\Sigma}
structure
\mathfrak{A}_{w}
that represents some word
w\in\Sigma^{*}
, the subformula

\neg\exists z_{1},z_{2}\colon\bigl(((z_{1}\mathbin{\dot{=}}z_{2}\cdot y)\lor(z_{1}\mathbin{\dot{=}}y\cdot z_{2}))\land\neg(z_{2}\mathbin{\dot{=}}\varepsilon)\bigr)

states that
\sigma(y)=w
for any substitution
\sigma
where
(\mathfrak{A}_{w},\sigma)\models\varphi
.
Thus,
\varphi
states that for
w\in\mathcal{L}(\varphi)
, we have that
w=x\mathtt{b}x
.
Consequently,
\mathcal{L}(\varphi)=\{v\cdot\mathtt{b}\cdot v\mid v\in\Sigma^{*}\}
and therefore
\mathtt{a}^{p}\cdot\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a}^{p}\not\equiv_{k}\mathtt{a}^{q}\cdot\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a}^{p}
for any

.
Since
\mathsf{qr}(\varphi)=5
, the stated lemma holds.
∎

Appendix B
Proof of Proposition
4.1

Proof.

Recall that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define
F_{n}\in\{\mathtt{a},\mathtt{b}\}^{*}
recursively as follows:
F_{0}:=\mathtt{a}
,
F_{1}:=\mathtt{ab}
, and
F_{i}:=F_{i-1}\cdot F_{i-2}
for all
i\geq 2
.

In this proof, we show that
L_{\mathsf{fib}}:=\{\mathtt{c}F_{0}\mathtt{c}F_{1}\mathtt{c}\cdots\mathtt{c}F_{n}\mathtt{c}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
over
\Sigma:=\{\mathtt{a},\mathtt{b},\mathtt{c}\}
is an
\mathsf{FC}
language.
For this proof, we use arbitrary concatenation as a shorthand for binary concatenation.

First, consider

\varphi_{w}(x):=\neg\exists z_{1},z_{2}\colon\Bigl(\neg(z_{1}\mathbin{\dot{=}}\varepsilon)\land\bigl((z_{2}\mathbin{\dot{=}}z_{1}\cdot x)\lor(z_{2}\mathbin{\dot{=}}x\cdot z_{1})\bigr)\Bigr).

This states that for any interpretation
(\mathfrak{A}_{w},\sigma)
where
\mathfrak{A}_{w}
represents
w\in\Sigma^{*}
, and
(\mathfrak{A}_{w},\sigma)\models\varphi_{w}(x)
, it holds that
\sigma(x)=w
.

Now consider:

\varphi_{\mathsf{struc}}:=\exists x_{1},\mathfrak{u}\colon\bigl(\varphi_{w}(\mathfrak{u})\land(\mathfrak{u}\mathbin{\dot{=}}\mathtt{cacabc}x_{1}\mathtt{c})\land\neg\exists x_{2}\colon(x_{2}\mathbin{\dot{=}}\mathtt{cc}).\bigr)

The formula
\varphi_{\mathsf{struc}}
states that any
w\in\mathcal{L}(\varphi_{\mathsf{struc}})
is of the form
\mathtt{c}\cdot\mathtt{a}\cdot\mathtt{c}\cdot\mathtt{ab}\cdot\mathtt{c}\cdot\bigl(\{\mathtt{a},\mathtt{b}\}^{+}\cdot\mathtt{c}\bigr)^{+}
.

Next, consider:

\varphi_{\mathsf{fib}}:=\exists\mathfrak{u}\colon\Bigl(\varphi_{\mathsf{struc}}\land\forall x,y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}\colon\bigl((x\mathbin{\dot{=}}\mathtt{c}y_{1}\mathtt{c}y_{2}\mathtt{c}y_{3}\mathtt{c})\rightarrow\bigl(\varphi_{\mathtt{c}}(y_{1})\lor\varphi_{\mathtt{c}}(y_{2})\lor\varphi_{\mathtt{c}}(y_{3})\lor(y_{3}\mathbin{\dot{=}}y_{2}\cdot y_{1})\bigr)\bigr)\Bigr),

where
\varphi_{\mathtt{c}}(x):=\exists y,z\colon(x\mathbin{\dot{=}}y\cdot\mathtt{c}\cdot z)
, and
\rightarrow
is logical implication.

The formula
\varphi_{\mathsf{fib}}
accepts those words that are in
\mathcal{L}(\varphi_{\mathsf{struc}})
, and for any factor of the form
\mathtt{c}y_{1}\mathtt{c}y_{2}\mathtt{c}y_{3}\mathtt{c}
for
y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}\in\Sigma^{*}
, either

•

for some
i\in[3]
, the word
y_{i}
contains a
\mathtt{c}
, or

•

y_{3}=y_{2}\cdot y_{1}
.

Thus, for all factors of the form
\mathtt{c}y_{1}\mathtt{c}y_{2}\mathtt{c}y_{3}\mathtt{c}
for
y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}\in\{\mathtt{a},\mathtt{b}\}^{*}
, we have that
y_{3}=y_{2}\cdot y_{1}
.

Consequently,
\mathcal{L}(\varphi_{\mathsf{fib}})=L_{\mathsf{fib}}
.
∎

.
After Spoiler has made their choice in

Let

Before moving on to define Duplicator’s strategy in

.

From the definition of Spoiler’s choice in

•

Duplicator derives their response in

To sum up Duplicator’s strategy for

Correctness.

Our next focus is showing that the strategy just defined for Duplicator is indeed a winning strategy for the game
\mathcal{G}
.
To that end, let
\vec{a}:=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{k+|\Sigma|+1})
and
\vec{b}:=(b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{k+|\Sigma|+1})
, where
a_{i}\in A
and
b_{i}\in B
for all
i\in[k+|\Sigma|+1]
, be the tuples that result from
\mathcal{G}
along with the interpreted constants, where Duplicator plays the strategy we have just defined.

Recall that
\vec{a}_{1}=(a_{1,1},a_{1,2},\dots,a_{1,k+|\Sigma|+1})
and
\vec{b}_{1}=(b_{1,1},b_{1,2},\dots,b_{1,k+|\Sigma|+1})
are the tuples generated from the first
k

\mathcal{G}_{1}
, and
\vec{a}_{2}=(a_{2,1},a_{2,2},\dots,a_{2,k+|\Sigma|+1})
and
\vec{b}_{2}=(b_{2,1},b_{2,2},\dots,b_{2,k+|\Sigma|+1})
are the tuples generated from the first
k
rounds of
\mathcal{G}_{2}
.
Also recall, that the last
|\Sigma|+1
components of these tuples are the interpretations of the constant symbols.
We know that
(\vec{a}_{1},\vec{b}_{1})
and
(\vec{a}_{2},\vec{b}_{2})
both form a partial isomorphism.

While Duplicator’s strategy for
\mathcal{G}
is based upon their strategy for the first
k
-rounds of
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}
, Duplicator can survive
k+r+2
-rounds of
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}
.
This ensures that Duplicator makes certain decisions in
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}
which ensures that Duplicator’s strategy for
\mathcal{G}
is indeed a winning strategy.
Thus, to prove that Duplicator’s strategy for
\mathcal{G}
is a winning strategy, we consider Spoiler’s choices in
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}
for rounds
k+1
up to
k+r+2
.
In fact, we only look at rounds
k+1
,
k+2
, and
k+3
, since we leave the last
r-1
rounds to ensure that if Spoiler chooses some
u
such that
|u|\leq r
, then Duplicator responds with
u
.

Let
\kappa:=k+|\Sigma|+1
.
Then, we use
a_{1,\kappa+i}
for
i\in[r+2]
to denote Spoiler’s choice in round
k+i
(assuming Spoiler chooses the structure
\mathfrak{A}_{w}|_{\mathsf{Facs}(w_{1})}
).
Thus, after all
k+r+2
rounds, we have that the first
k
-rounds of
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}
are in the first
k
-components of the corresponding tuples, the next
|\Sigma|+1
components are then the interpreted constants, and finally, the last
r+2
components are the chosen elements for the last
r+2
-rounds.
This is simply a permutation of the resulting tuples, and therefore
(\vec{a}_{1},\vec{b}_{1})
and
(\vec{a}_{2},\vec{b}_{2})
still form a partial isomorphism.

To help with some subsequent reasoning, we now prove the following claim:

Claim 1.

For every
p\in[k]
, we have that
a_{p}\in A_{\mathsf{other}}
if and only if
b_{p}\in B_{\mathsf{other}}
.

Proof of Claim.

Without loss of generality, assume that in round
p
of
\mathcal{G}
, Spoiler chose
a_{p}\in A_{\mathsf{other}}
.
Therefore, we have that
a_{p}=a_{1,p}\cdot a_{2,p}
where
a_{1,p}\in\mathsf{Facs}(w_{1})
and
a_{2,p}\in\mathsf{Facs}(w_{2})
.
It follows that Duplicator responds with
b_{p}=b_{1,p}\cdot b_{2,p}
, where
b_{1,p}\in\mathsf{Facs}(v_{1})
and
b_{2,p}\in\mathsf{Facs}(v_{2})
.
Working towards a contradiction, assume (without loss of generality) that
b_{p}\in\mathsf{Facs}(v_{1})
.
It follows that
b_{2,p}\in\mathsf{Facs}(v_{1})
holds, and therefore
b_{2,p}\in\mathsf{Facs}(w_{1})\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{Facs}(w_{2})
.
Since it therefore holds that
|b_{2,p}|\leq r
, we have that
a_{2,p}=b_{2,p}
, see
Lemma

4.2
.
Hence,
a_{2,p}\in\mathsf{Facs}(w_{1})\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{Facs}(w_{2})
holds.

We now look at the game
\mathcal{G}_{1}
for rounds
k+1
to
k+r+2
.
Recall that Duplicator plays
\mathcal{G}_{1}
with their winning strategy.

•

In round
k+1
, Spoiler can choose
\mathfrak{B}_{w}|_{\mathsf{Facs}(v_{1})}
and
b_{1,\kappa+1}:=a_{2,p}
.
Duplicator must respond with
a_{1,\kappa+1}=a_{2,p}
.
This is because
|a_{2,p}|\leq r
, since
a_{2,p}\in\mathsf{Facs}(w_{1})\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{Facs}(w_{2})
, and Duplicator must be able to survive the next
r
rounds, see
Lemma

4.2
..

•

In round
k+2
, Spoiler can choose
\mathfrak{B}_{w}|_{\mathsf{Facs}(v_{1})}
and
b_{1,\kappa+2}:=b_{1,p}\cdot b_{1,\kappa+1}
.
We note that Spoiler can choose
b_{1,\kappa+2}
since
b_{1,\kappa+2}=b_{p}
and we have assumed that
b_{p}\in\mathsf{Facs}(v_{1})
.
Duplicator must respond with
a_{1,\kappa+2}=a_{1,p}\cdot a_{1,\kappa+1}
.

However,
a_{1,\kappa+2}=a_{1,p}\cdot a_{2,p}
and therefore
a_{1,\kappa+2}=a_{p}
, which implies
a_{1,\kappa+2}\in\mathsf{Facs}(w_{1})
.
This is a contradiction since we have assumed
a_{p}\in A_{\mathsf{other}}
, and therefore
a_{p}\notin\mathsf{Facs}(w_{1})
.

\lhd

To show that
(\vec{a},\vec{b})
is indeed a partial isomorphism, we show that the following conditions hold:

•

For every
i\in[k+|\Sigma|+1]
, and constant symbol
c\in\tau_{\Sigma}
, we have
a_{i}=c^{\mathfrak{A}_{w}}
if and only if
b_{i}=c^{\mathfrak{B}_{v}}
,

•

for every
i,j\in[k+|\Sigma|+1]
, we have that
a_{i}=a_{j}
if and only if
b_{i}=b_{j}
, and

•

for every
i,j,l\in[k+|\Sigma|+1]
, we have that
a_{l}=a_{i}\cdot a_{j}
if and only if
b_{l}=b_{i}\cdot b_{j}
.

First note that directly from the definition of Duplicator’s strategy, we have that
a_{i}\in\Sigma
if and only if
b_{i}\in\Sigma
and
a_{i}=b_{i}
.
Furthermore, for every
i,j\in[k+|\Sigma|+1]
, we have that
a_{i}=a_{j}
if and only if
a_{i}=a_{j}\cdot\varepsilon
.
Symmetrically, for every
i,j\in[k+|\Sigma|+1]
, we have that
b_{i}=b_{j}
if and only if
b_{i}=b_{j}\cdot\varepsilon
.

Thus, if
a_{i}\cdot a_{j}=a_{l}
if and only if
b_{i}\cdot b_{j}=b_{l}
for all
a_{i},a_{j},a_{l}\in\vec{a}
and for all
b_{i},b_{j},b_{l}\in\vec{b}
, then
(\vec{a},\vec{b})
forms a partial isomorphism.
Hence, the following claim completes the proof of correctness for Duplicator’s strategy:

Claim 2.

For every
i,j,l\in[k+|\Sigma|+1]
, we have that
a_{i}\cdot a_{j}=a_{l}
if and only if
b_{i}\cdot b_{j}=b_{l}
.

Proof of Claim.

Without loss of generality, assume that
a_{i}\cdot a_{j}=a_{l}
.
We consider three cases based on
a_{l}
, and we shall prove that each case yields
b_{i}\cdot b_{j}=b_{l}
.
Before doing so, we state some facts.

From the definition of Spoiler’s and Duplicator’s choices in
\mathcal{G}
,
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}
, we have that the following conditions hold for any
p\in[k]
and any
\iota\in\{1,2\}
:

•

a_{p}\in\mathsf{Facs}(w_{\iota})
if and only if
b_{p}\in\mathsf{Facs}(v_{\iota})
,

•

if
a_{p}\in\mathsf{Facs}(w_{\iota})
, then
a_{p}=a_{\iota,p}
, and

•

if
b_{p}\in\mathsf{Facs}(v_{\iota})
, then
b_{p}=b_{\iota,p}
.

Likewise, for any
p\in[k]
we know that

•

a_{p}\in A_{\mathsf{other}}
if and only if
b_{p}\in B_{\mathsf{other}}
(see
1
),

•

if
a_{p}\in A_{\mathsf{other}}
, then
a_{p}=a_{1,p}\cdot a_{2,p}
, and

•

if
b_{p}\in B_{\mathsf{other}}
, then
b_{p}=b_{1,p}\cdot b_{2,p}
.

Case 1,
a_{l}\in\mathsf{Facs}(w_{1})
:

If
a_{l}\in\mathsf{Facs}(w_{1})
, then we know that
a_{i},a_{j}\in\mathsf{Facs}(w_{1})
, and from the definition of Duplicator’s strategy, we know that
b_{l},b_{i},b_{j}\in\mathsf{Facs}(v_{1})
.
Therefore, it follows that
a_{s}=a_{1,s}
and
b_{s}=b_{1,s}
for
s\in\{i,j,l\}
.
Since
(\vec{a}_{1},\vec{b}_{1})
forms a partial isomorphism, and
a_{1,l}=a_{1,i}\cdot a_{1,j}
, we know that
b_{1,l}=b_{1,i}\cdot b_{1,j}
.

Case 2,
a_{l}\in\mathsf{Facs}(w_{2})
:

This case is symmetric to Case 1.

Case 3,
a_{l}\in A_{\mathsf{other}}
:

Note that
a_{i},a_{j}\in A_{\mathsf{other}}
cannot hold.
This is because
a_{l}\in A_{\mathsf{other}}
, and therefore, there exists
x\in\mathsf{Facs}(w_{1})
and
y\in\mathsf{Facs}(w_{2})
such that
a_{l}=x\cdot y
.
Thus, from the fact that
a_{l}=a_{i}\cdot a_{j}=x\cdot y
, using a length argument we can determine that
a_{i}\in\mathsf{Facs}(w_{1})
or
a_{j}\in\mathsf{Facs}(w_{2})
.
Thus, we split Case 3 into the following subcases:

Case 3.1,:

a_{i}\in\mathsf{Facs}(w_{1})
and
a_{j}\in\mathsf{Facs}(w_{2})
.

Case 3.2,:

a_{i}\in A_{\mathsf{other}}
and
a_{j}\in\mathsf{Facs}(w_{2})
.

Case 3.3,:

a_{i}\in\mathsf{Facs}(w_{1})
and
a_{j}\in A_{\mathsf{other}}
.

We now prove that for each of the above mentioned subcases, our assumption that
a_{i}\cdot a_{j}=a_{l}
yields
b_{i}\cdot b_{j}=b_{l}
.

Case 3.1,
a_{i}\in\mathsf{Facs}(w_{1})
and
a_{j}\in\mathsf{Facs}(w_{2})
:

As
a_{i}\in\mathsf{Facs}(w_{1})
, it follows that
a_{i}=a_{1,i}
and consequently,
b_{i}\in\mathsf{Facs}(v_{1})
which implies that
b_{i}=b_{1,i}
.
Analogously, since
a_{j}\in\mathsf{Facs}(w_{2})
, we know that
a_{j}=a_{2,j}
and consequently,
b_{j}\in\mathsf{Facs}(v_{2})
which implies that
b_{j}=b_{2,j}
.

As per the definition of Duplicator’s strategy and the look-up games
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}
, we know that
a_{l}=a_{1,l}\cdot a_{2,l}
and
b_{l}=b_{1,l}\cdot b_{2,l}
.
Since
a_{l}=a_{i}\cdot a_{j}
, it follows that
a_{1,l}\cdot a_{2,l}=a_{1,i}\cdot a_{2,j}
.

Due to the fact that
a_{1,l}\cdot a_{2,l}=a_{1,i}\cdot a_{2,j}
, we have that either
a_{1,l}
is a (not necessarily strict) prefix of
a_{1,i}
, or
a_{1,i}
is a prefix of
a_{1,l}
.
This gives us two further subcases to look at.

Case 3.1.1,
a_{1,l}
is a prefix of
a_{1,i}
:

For this case, we can write
a_{1,i}=a_{1,l}\cdot x
for some
x\in\Sigma^{*}
.
Therefore

a_{1,l}\cdot a_{2,l}=a_{1,l}\cdot x\cdot a_{2,j}

and hence
a_{2,l}=x\cdot a_{2,j}
.
Since
a_{1,i}\in\mathsf{Facs}(w_{1})
and
x\sqsubseteq a_{1,i}
, we know that
x\in\mathsf{Facs}(w_{1})
.
Furthermore,
a_{2,l}\in\mathsf{Facs}(w_{2})
, and
x\sqsubseteq a_{2,l}
, and consequently
x\in\mathsf{Facs}(w_{2})
also holds.
Thus
x\in\mathsf{Facs}(w_{1})\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{Facs}(w_{2})
.

We now show that (for Case 3.1.1) if Duplicator plays their winning strategy for
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}
, and
a_{l}=a_{i}\cdot a_{j}
, then
b_{l}=b_{i}\cdot b_{j}
must hold.
To show this, we look at possible choices for Spoiler in round
k+1
up to round
k+r+2
for
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}
.
Recall that
\kappa:=k+|\Sigma|+1
, and we use the last
r+2
components of the tuples
\vec{a}_{1}
,
\vec{a}_{2}
,
\vec{b}_{1}
, and
\vec{b}_{2}
to denote the choices made in the last
r+2
rounds of
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}
respectively.

•

For round
k+1
of
\mathcal{G}_{1}
, Spoiler can choose
\mathfrak{A}_{w}|_{\mathsf{Facs}(w_{1})}
and
a_{1,\kappa+1}:=x
.
Since
x\in\mathsf{Facs}(w_{1})\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{Facs}(w_{2})
, we know that
|x|\leq r
, and therefore Duplicator must respond with
\mathfrak{B}_{v}|_{\mathsf{Facs}(v_{1})}
and
b_{1,\kappa+1}:=x
.
This is because Duplicator must be able to survive the next
r
rounds, see
Lemma

4.2
.
Since we know that
a_{1,i}=a_{1,l}\cdot a_{1,\kappa+1}
, and Duplicator plays their winning strategy for the
k+r+2
round game
\mathcal{G}_{1}
, we know that
b_{1,i}=b_{1,l}\cdot b_{1,\kappa+1}
must also hold.

•

For round
k+1
of
\mathcal{G}_{2}
, Spoiler can choose
\mathfrak{A}_{w}|_{\mathsf{Facs}(w_{2})}
and
a_{2,\kappa+1}:=x
.
Duplicator must respond with
\mathfrak{B}_{v}|_{\mathsf{Facs}(v_{1})}
and
b_{2,\kappa+1}:=x
.
Since
a_{2,l}=a_{2,\kappa+1}\cdot a_{2,j}
, we know that
b_{2,l}=b_{2,\kappa+1}\cdot b_{2,j}
must hold.

Since
b_{2,\kappa+1}=b_{1,\kappa+1}=x
, we can write:

b_{1,l}\cdot b_{2,\kappa+1}\cdot b_{2,j}=b_{1,l}\cdot b_{1,\kappa+1}\cdot b_{2,j}.

We know that
b_{2,l}=b_{2,\kappa+1}\cdot b_{2,j}
and
b_{1,i}=b_{1,l}\cdot b_{1,\kappa+1}
, we have that

b_{1,l}\cdot\underbrace{b_{2,\kappa+1}\cdot b_{2,j}}_{b_{2,l}}=\underbrace{b_{1,l}\cdot b_{1,\kappa+1}}_{b_{1,i}}\cdot b_{2,j}.

Furthermore,
b_{l}=b_{1,l}\cdot b_{2,l}
and
b_{i}\cdot b_{j}=b_{1,i}\cdot b_{2,j}
.
Consequently,
b_{l}=b_{i}\cdot b_{j}
.

All of the subsequent cases have a similar proof to the proof of Case 3.1.2.
Unfortunately, they are not similar enough to hand-wave away.
If the reader is convinced that the proof of Case of 3.1.2 can be adapted to the other various cases, they are invited to skip the rest of the proof.
However, we include proofs of all the cases for completeness sake (and to avoid any pitfalls).

Case 3.1.2,
a_{1,i}
is a prefix of
a_{1,l}
:

We can write
a_{1,l}=a_{1,i}\cdot x
for some
x\in\Sigma^{*}

Appendix D
Appendix for Section
4.2

Section

4.2
is dedicated to the Primitive Power Lemma.
Before giving the proof of this result, we first prove some lemmas regarding primitive words.
Most of these subsequent lemmas follow from the fact that a primitive word
w\in\Sigma^{+}
cannot be a non-trivial factor of
ww
.
That is, if
ww=pws
, then either
p=\varepsilon
or
s=\varepsilon
; for more details, see Chapter 6 of
(
Rozenberg and Salomaa 1997
)
.
For our purposes, we look at a slight generalization of this idea:

Lemma D.1.

A word
w\in\Sigma^{+}
is primitive if and only if for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we have that
w^{m}=u\cdot w\cdot v
for any
u,v\in\Sigma^{+}
, implies that
u=w^{n}
and
v=w^{n^{\prime}}
for some
n,n^{\prime}<m
.

Proof.

For this proof, we only show that
w\in\Sigma^{+}
is primitive if and only if for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we have that
w^{m}=u\cdot w\cdot v
for
u,v\in\Sigma^{+}
implies that
u=w^{n}
for some
n<m
.
The fact that
v=w^{n^{\prime}}
for some
n^{\prime}<n
also holds, follows immediately from a length argument.
Furthermore, since
u,v\in\Sigma^{+}
, it follows that
m\geq 2
.

If direction.

We show that if
w^{m}=u\cdot w\cdot v
implies that
u=w^{n}
for some
n<m
, then
w\in\Sigma^{+}
is primitive by proving the contraposition.
That is,
w\in\Sigma^{+}
is imprimitive implies that there exists
u,v\in\Sigma^{+}
such that
w^{m}=u\cdot w\cdot v
and
u\neq w^{n}
for some
n<m
.
Let
w\in\Sigma^{+}
and let
z\in\Sigma^{+}
where
w=z^{k}
for some
k>1
.
Thus,
w^{m}=z^{km}
, and therefore we can write
w^{m}=z\cdot w\cdot z^{km-k-1}
.
Since
z\neq w
, we have proven the contraposition.

Only if direction.

For this direction, we show that if
w\in\Sigma^{*}
is primitive, then for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we have that
w^{m}=u\cdot w\cdot v
for any
u,v\in\Sigma^{+}
implies that
u=w^{n}
for some
n<m
.
Working towards a contradiction, let
m\in\mathbb{N}
and assume that
w\in\Sigma^{*}
is primitive, but
w^{m}=u\cdot w\cdot v
where
u\neq w^{n}
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Trivially,
u\neq\varepsilon
must hold since
\varepsilon=w^{0}
.
Furthermore, since
u,v\in\Sigma^{+}
, it must hold that
m\geq 2
for
w^{m}=u\cdot w\cdot v
to hold.

First, we show that we can assume
u
is a strict prefix of
w
. Note that
|u|\neq w^{k}
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
If
|u|>|w|
, then because
w^{m}=u\cdot w\cdot v
, it follows that
u=w^{s}\cdot\bar{u}
for some
s\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and a proper prefix
\bar{u}\in\Sigma^{+}
of
w
.
Thus, we can consider the new equality

w^{m-s}=\bar{u}\cdot w\cdot v.\

For the rest of the proof, we simply use
u
to denote a strict prefix of
w
such that the equality
w^{m}=u\cdot w\cdot v
holds.

Since
u
is a strict prefix of
w
, we can write
w=u\cdot u^{\prime}
for some
u^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{+}
.
Furthermore, since
u\cdot w\cdot v=w^{m}
and
w^{m}=(u\cdot u^{\prime})^{m}
, we have that

u\cdot w\cdot v=(u\cdot u^{\prime})^{m},

which implies

\displaystyle w\cdot v

\displaystyle=u^{\prime}\cdot(u\cdot u^{\prime})^{m-1},

\displaystyle w\cdot v

\displaystyle=u^{\prime}\cdot u\cdot u^{\prime}\cdot(u\cdot u^{\prime})^{m-2}.

We know that
|w|=|u|+|u^{\prime}|
, and therefore
w=u\cdot u^{\prime}
and
w=u^{\prime}\cdot u
.
Proposition 1.3.2 in Lothaire
(
Lothaire 1997
)
states that if
vu=uv
for
u,v\in\Sigma^{*}
, then there is some
z\in\Sigma^{*}
and
k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
u=z^{k_{1}}
and
v=z^{k_{2}}
.
It follows that
w=z^{k}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and since
\varepsilon\sqsubset u\sqsubset w
and
\varepsilon\sqsubset u^{\prime}\sqsubset w
, we know that
k>1
.
Therefore
w
is imprimitive, which is a contradiction.
∎

\displaystyle w^{r}\cdot w

\displaystyle=u_{1}^{\prime}\cdot w^{r}\cdot u_{4}\cdot\bar{u}_{2},

\displaystyle w^{r+1}

\displaystyle=u_{1}^{\prime}\cdot w^{r}\cdot u_{4}\cdot\bar{u}_{2}.

.

\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)>r

\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)

Consequently, our assumption that

cannot hold.
Furthermore, using a simple length argument, we can conclude that

Lemma D.2.

Let
w\in\Sigma^{+}
be a primitive word and let
u,v\sqsubseteq w^{m}
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)>0

\mathsf{exp}_{w}(v)>0
.
If

n\in\mathbb{N}
, then there exists
u_{1},u_{2},v_{1},v_{2}\sqsubset w

u=u_{1}\cdot w^{s}\cdot u_{2}
and
v=v_{1}\cdot w^{t}\cdot v_{2}

and either
u_{2}\cdot v_{1}=\varepsilon

u_{2}\cdot v_{1}=w

Proof.

Let
w\in\Sigma^{+}
be a primitive word and let
u,v\sqsubseteq w^{m}
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
Due to the fact that
u,v\sqsubseteq w^{m}
where
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)>0
and
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(v)>0
it follows from
Lemma

4.8
that we can write

and
u=v_{1}\cdot w^{t}\cdot v_{2}

and where

and

To prove this lemma, we prove the contrapositive.
That is, we shall prove that if

and
u_{2}\cdot v_{1}\neq w
, then
u\cdot v\not\sqsubseteq w^{n}
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

To prove the contrapositive of the stated lemma, we work towards a contradiction.
Assume that
u_{2}\cdot v_{1}\neq\varepsilon
and
u_{2}\cdot v_{1}\neq w
and
u\cdot v\sqsubseteq w^{n}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
From what we have defined, we can write

Since

4.8

Furthermore, it follows that
w_{1}
is a proper suffix of
w
and
w_{2}
is a proper prefix of
w
, see
Lemma

4.8
.
Thus, there exists
\bar{w}_{1},\bar{w}_{2}\in\Sigma^{+}
such that
\bar{w}_{1}\cdot w_{1}=w

w_{2}\cdot\bar{w}_{2}=w

Therefore,

\displaystyle\bar{w}_{1}\cdot u_{1}\cdot w^{s}\cdot u_{2}\cdot v_{1}\cdot w^{t}\cdot v_{2}\cdot\bar{w}_{2}

\displaystyle=\bar{w}_{1}\cdot w_{1}\cdot w^{r}\cdot w_{2}\cdot\bar{w}_{2},

\displaystyle=w\cdot w^{r}\cdot w,

\displaystyle=w^{r+2}.

Lemma

D.1

\bar{w}_{1}\cdot u_{1}\cdot w^{s}\cdot u_{2}\cdot v_{1}=w^{p},

for some

D.2.
Proof of Lemma
4.9

Before giving the actual proof of
Lemma

4.9
, we first give a proof for the following lemma:

Lemma D.3.

If
u\cdot v\sqsubseteq w^{m}
for some primitive word
w\in\Sigma^{*}
, some
m\in\mathbb{N}
, and some
u,v\in\Sigma^{*}
, then either

(1)

\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u\cdot v)=\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)+\mathsf{exp}_{w}(v)
, or

(2)

\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u\cdot v)=\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)+\mathsf{exp}_{w}(v)+1
.

Proof.

We split this proof into four cases.

Case 1,
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)\geq 1
and
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(v)\geq 1
:

This case follows immediately follows from
Lemma

D.2
.
That is, we know
u=u_{1}\cdot w^{\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)}\cdot u_{2}
and
v=v_{1}\cdot w^{\mathsf{exp}_{w}(v)}\cdot v_{2}
where
u_{1},v_{2}\sqsubset w
and
u_{2}\cdot v_{1}\in\{\varepsilon,w\}
.
If
u_{2}\cdot v_{1}=\varepsilon
, then

u\cdot v=u_{1}\cdot w^{\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)}\cdot w^{\mathsf{exp}_{w}(v)}\cdot v_{2},

and hence
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u\cdot v)=\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)+\mathsf{exp}_{w}(v)
.
If
u_{2}\cdot v_{1}=w
, then

u\cdot v=u_{1}\cdot w^{\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)}\cdot w\cdot w^{\mathsf{exp}_{w}(v)}\cdot v_{2},

and hence
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u\cdot v)=\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)+\mathsf{exp}_{w}(v)+1
.

Case 2,
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)=0
and
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(v)=0
:

Working towards a contradiction, assume that
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u\cdot v)=r
where
r\geq 2
.
From
Lemma

4.8
, we know that
u\cdot v=w_{1}\cdot w^{r}\cdot w_{2}
for some
r\geq 2
where
w_{1},w_{2}\sqsubset w
.
Now, we have two cases: First case is if
|u|<|w_{1}|+|w|
. However, it then follows that
|v|>|w_{2}|+|w|
and therefore
w\sqsubseteq v
.
This is a contradiction, since then
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(v)\geq 1
.
The second case is if
|u|\geq|w_{1}|+|w|
. However, then
w\sqsubseteq u
.
This is a contradiction, since then
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)\geq 1
.

Case 3,
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)=0
and
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(v)\geq 1
:

Working towards a contradiction, assume that

(1)

\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u\cdot v)<\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)+\mathsf{exp}_{w}(v)
or

(2)

\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u\cdot v)>\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)+\mathsf{exp}_{w}(v)+1
.

Since
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)=0
, it follows that
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)+\mathsf{exp}_{w}(v)=\mathsf{exp}_{w}(v)
.
Therefore, since
w^{r}\sqsubseteq u\cdot v
where
r=\mathsf{exp}_{w}(v)
, it follows that
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u\cdot v)<\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)+\mathsf{exp}_{w}(v)
cannot hold.
Thus,
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u\cdot v)>\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)+\mathsf{exp}_{w}(v)+1
must hold.

Due to the fact that
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(v)\geq 1
, we can write
v=v_{1}\cdot w^{r}\cdot v_{2}
, where
v_{1}
is a proper suffix of
w
and
v_{2}
is a proper prefix of
w
, and where
r=\mathsf{exp}_{w}(v)
.
Furthermore, since
u\cdot v\sqsubseteq w^{m}
and
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u\cdot v)\geq 1
, we know from
Lemma

4.8
that
u\cdot v=w_{1}\cdot w^{\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u\cdot v)}\cdot w_{2}
where
w_{1}
is a proper suffix of
w
and
w_{2}
is a proper prefix of
w
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle u\cdot v

\displaystyle=w_{1}\cdot w^{\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u\cdot v)}\cdot w_{2},

\displaystyle=u\cdot v_{1}\cdot w^{r}\cdot v_{2},

and since
w_{2}
and
v_{2}
are proper prefixes, it follows that
w_{2}=v_{2}
, see
Lemma

4.8
.
We can thus write

which implies
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u\cdot v)=\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u\cdot v_{1})+r
.
Since

and

.
Therefore,
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u\cdot v_{1})\leq 1
.
Thus,
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u\cdot v)>\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)+\mathsf{exp}_{w}(v)+1
cannot hold.

Case 4,
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)\geq 1
and
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(v)=0
:

This follows analogously to Case 3.
Working towards a contradiction, assume that

(1)

\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u\cdot v)<\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)+\mathsf{exp}_{w}(v)
or

(2)

\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u\cdot v)>\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)+\mathsf{exp}_{w}(v)+1
.

Since
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(v)=0
, it follows that
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)+\mathsf{exp}_{w}(v)=\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)
.
Therefore,
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u\cdot v)<\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)+\mathsf{exp}_{w}(v)
cannot hold.
Thus,
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u\cdot v)>\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)+\mathsf{exp}_{w}(v)+1
must hold.

Since
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)\geq 1
, we can write
u=u_{1}\cdot w^{r}\cdot u_{2}
, where
u_{1}
is a proper suffix of
w
, and
u_{2}
is a proper prefix of
w
, and where

Therefore,
u\cdot v=u_{1}\cdot w^{r}\cdot u_{2}\cdot v

We can thus write

Since
|u_{2}|<|w|
and

, we know that

Therefore,
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u_{2}\cdot v)\leq 1

Consequently,
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u\cdot v)>\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u)+\mathsf{exp}_{w}(v)+1

∎

b_{j}
such that
b_{j}=b_{i}
.
The case where Spoiler chooses
b_{j}
follows analogously.

Therefore, it suffices to show that
a_{i}\cdot a_{j}=a_{l}
if and only if
b_{i}\cdot b_{j}=b_{l}
holds to prove that
(\vec{a},\vec{b})
is a partial isomorphism.

Without loss of generality, assume that
a_{i}\cdot a_{j}=a_{l}
.
We now look at the following cases, and for each case show that
a_{i}\cdot a_{j}=a_{l}
implies
b_{i}\cdot b_{j}=b_{l}

•

Case 1:

\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{i})=0
and
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{j})=0
,

•

Case 2:

\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{i})\geq 1
and
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{j})\geq 1
,

•

Case 3:

\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{i})=0
and
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{j})\geq 1
, and

•

Case 4:

\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{i})\geq 1
and
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{j})=0
.

Case 1,
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{i})=0
and
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{j})=0
:

First, note that from
Lemma

D.3
, we know that
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{l})\leq 1
must hold.
Thus, we continue Case 1 under the assumption that
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{l})\leq 1
.
Furthermore, we know from the definition of Duplicator’s strategy, that
a_{i}=b_{i}
and
a_{j}=b_{j}
.

•

If
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{l})=0
, then it follows directly from Duplicator’s strategy that
a_{l}=b_{l}
, and
a_{i}=b_{i}
and
a_{j}=b_{j}
.
Thus,
b_{l}=b_{i}\cdot b_{j}
.

•

If
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{l})=1
, then we can write
a_{l}=w_{1}\cdot w\cdot w_{2}
where
w_{1},w_{2}\sqsubset w
.
From the look-up game, we know that
a_{l}^{\prime}=\mathtt{a}
, and therefore
b_{l}^{\prime}=\mathtt{a}
must hold.
Thus, from Duplicator’s strategy, we know
b_{l}=w_{1}\cdot w\cdot w_{2}
.
Hence
b_{l}=b_{i}\cdot b_{j}
.

Case 2,
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{i})\geq 1
and
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{j})\geq 1
:

For this case, we know that we can write:

•

a_{i}=u_{i}\cdot w^{m_{i}}\cdot v_{i}
, where
u_{i}
is a suffix of
w
and
v_{i}
is a prefix of
w
, and
m_{i}=\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{i})
, and

•

a_{j}=u_{j}\cdot w^{m_{j}}\cdot v_{j}
, where
u_{j}
is a suffix of
w
and
v_{j}
is a prefix of
w
, and
m_{j}=\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{j})
.

Due to the definition of Duplicator’s strategy, we know that

•

b_{i}=u_{i}\cdot w^{n_{i}}\cdot v_{i}
, where
n_{i}=\mathsf{exp}_{w}(b_{i})
, and

•

b_{j}=u_{j}\cdot w^{n_{j}}\cdot v_{j}
, where
n_{j}=\mathsf{exp}_{w}(b_{j})
.

Since
a_{l}\sqsubseteq w^{q}
and
b_{l}\sqsubseteq w^{p}
, we know from
Lemma

D.2
that
v_{i}\cdot u_{j}=w
or
v_{i}\cdot u_{j}=\varepsilon
.
Thus,

a_{l}=u_{i}\cdot w^{m_{i}}\cdot w^{g}\cdot w^{m_{j}}\cdot v_{j},

where
g\in\{0,1\}
.
From the definition of Duplicator’s strategy,

b_{l}=u_{i}\cdot w^{n}\cdot v_{j}

for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Furthermore,

\displaystyle b_{i}\cdot b_{j}

\displaystyle=u_{i}\cdot w^{n_{i}}\cdot w^{g}\cdot w^{n_{j}}\cdot v_{j},

\displaystyle b_{i}\cdot b_{j}

\displaystyle=u_{i}\cdot w^{n_{i}+n_{j}+g}\cdot v_{j}.

Therefore,
b_{l}=b_{i}\cdot b_{j}
if and only if
n=n_{i}+n_{j}+g
.

Recall that the result
(\vec{a}^{\prime},\vec{b}^{\prime})
of the look-up game
\mathcal{G}_{l}
forms a partial isomorphism.
From the look-up game
\mathcal{G}_{l}
, we know that

(1)

a_{i}^{\prime}=\mathtt{a}^{m_{i}}
and
b_{i}^{\prime}=\mathtt{a}^{n_{i}}
,

(2)

a_{j}^{\prime}=\mathtt{a}^{m_{j}}
and
b_{j}^{\prime}=\mathtt{a}^{n_{j}}
, and

(3)

a_{l}^{\prime}=\mathtt{a}^{m_{i}+m_{j}+g}
and
b_{l}^{\prime}=\mathtt{a}^{n}
.

We consider two cases.

Case 2.1,
g=0
:

Since
g=0
, it follows that
a_{l}^{\prime}=\mathtt{a}^{m_{i}+m_{j}}
, and hence
a_{l}^{\prime}=a_{i}^{\prime}\cdot a_{j}^{\prime}
.
Furthermore, because
(\vec{a}^{\prime},\vec{b}^{\prime})
forms a partial isomorphism and
a_{l}^{\prime}=a_{i}^{\prime}\cdot a_{j}^{\prime}
, we have that
b_{l}^{\prime}=b_{i}^{\prime}\cdot b_{j}^{\prime}
.
Hence,
b_{l}^{\prime}=\mathtt{a}^{n_{i}}\cdot\mathtt{a}^{n_{j}}
, and consequently,

.
It therefore follows that
b_{l}=b_{i}\cdot b_{j}
holds.

Case 2.2,
g=1
:

Since
g=1
, it follows that
a_{l}^{\prime}=\mathtt{a}\cdot\mathtt{a}^{m_{i}+m_{j}}
, and thus
a_{l}^{\prime}=\mathtt{a}\cdot a_{i}^{\prime}\cdot a_{j}^{\prime}
.
It follows from
3
that
b_{l}^{\prime}=\mathtt{a}\cdot b_{i}^{\prime}\cdot b_{j}^{\prime}
, which means that
n=n_{i}+n_{j}+1
.
It therefore follows that
b_{l}=b_{i}\cdot b_{j}
holds.

Case 3,
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{i})=0
and
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{j})\geq 1
:

Since
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{j})\geq 1
and therefore
\mathsf{exp}(a_{l})\geq 1
, we can write

•

a_{j}=u_{j}\cdot w^{\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{j})}\cdot v_{j}
where
u_{j},v_{j}\sqsubset w
, and

•

a_{l}=u_{l}\cdot w^{\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{l})}\cdot v_{l}
where
u_{l},v_{l}\sqsubset w
.

From the definition of Duplicator’s strategy, we know that

•

b_{i}=a_{i}
,

•

b_{j}=u_{j}\cdot w^{\mathsf{exp}_{w}(b_{j})}\cdot v_{j}
, and

•

b_{l}=u_{l}\cdot w^{\mathsf{exp}_{w}(b_{l})}\cdot v_{l}
.

Since
a_{l}=a_{i}\cdot a_{j}
, we have that

u_{l}\cdot w^{\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{l})}\cdot v_{l}=a_{i}\cdot u_{j}\cdot w^{\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{j})}\cdot v_{j}.

Notice that
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{i})=0
and
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(u_{j})=0
, and thus from
Lemma

D.3
, we know that
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{i}\cdot u_{j})\leq 1
.
If
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{i}\cdot u_{j})=0
, then
a_{l}=a_{i}\cdot u_{j}\cdot w^{\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{l})}\cdot v_{l}
, and therefore

u_{l}\cdot w^{\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{l})}\cdot v_{l}=a_{i}\cdot u_{j}\cdot w^{\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{l})}\cdot v_{l},

which implies that
a_{i}\cdot u_{j}=u_{l}
.

If
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{i}\cdot u_{j})=1
, then from
Lemma

4.8
, we know that
a_{i}\cdot u_{j}=w^{\prime}\cdot w\cdot w^{\prime\prime}
, where
w^{\prime},w^{\prime\prime}\sqsubset w
.
Since,

(w^{\prime}\cdot w\cdot w^{\prime\prime})\cdot w^{\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{j})}\cdot v_{l}\sqsubseteq w^{q},

it immediately follows that
w^{\prime\prime}=\varepsilon
must hold, see
Lemma

D.1
.

Thus, either
a_{i}\cdot u_{j}=u_{l}
or
a_{i}\cdot u_{j}=u_{l}\cdot w
.
Therefore, we can write:

a_{l}=u_{l}\cdot w^{g}\cdot w^{\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{j})}\cdot v_{j},

where
g\in\{0,1\}
.

We know that:

b_{i}\cdot b_{j}=b_{i}\cdot u_{j}\cdot w^{\mathsf{exp}_{w}(b_{j})}\cdot v_{j}.

Furthermore,
b_{i}=a_{i}
, and
a_{i}\cdot u_{j}=u_{l}\cdot w^{g}
where
g\in\{0,1\}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle b_{i}\cdot b_{j}=

\displaystyle u_{l}\cdot w^{g}\cdot w^{\mathsf{exp}_{w}(b_{j})}\cdot v_{j},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u_{l}\cdot w^{\mathsf{exp}_{w}(b_{j})+g}\cdot v_{j},

for some
g\in\{0,1\}
.
Hence
b_{l}=b_{i}\cdot b_{j}
if and only if
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(b_{l})=\mathsf{exp}_{w}(b_{i})+g
for

.
From
3

\mathsf{exp}_{w}(b_{l})=\mathsf{exp}_{w}(b_{i})+g
must hold.
Consequently,
b_{l}=b_{i}\cdot b_{j}
.

Case 4,
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{i})\geq 1
and
\mathsf{exp}_{w}(a_{j})=0
:

This case is symmetric to Case 3.
We therefore omit a proof.

Concluding the proof.

We have shown that if
a_{l}=a_{i}\cdot a_{j}
, then
b_{l}=b_{i}\cdot b_{j}
must hold.
Due to the arbitrary nature of the notation
\vec{a}
and
\vec{b}
, it follows that if
b_{l}=b_{i}\cdot b_{j}
, then
a_{l}=a_{i}\cdot b_{j}
.
Thus, we have shown that
(\vec{a},\vec{b})
is a partial isomorphism.
We have therefore given a winning strategy for Duplicator over
\mathfrak{A}_{q}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{p}
– two structures that represent

and

respectively, whenever
\mathtt{a}^{q}\equiv_{k+3}\mathtt{a}^{p}
.
∎

E.1.
Proof of Lemma
4.13

Proof.

We prove this lemma in two parts.

(1) if and only if (2).

First, we show that the only if direction holds by proving the contrapositive.
In other words, if
v
and
w
conjugate, then there does not exist
n_{0},m_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\mathsf{Facs}(w^{n_{0}})\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{Facs}(v^{m_{0}})=\mathsf{Facs}(w^{n})\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{Facs}(v^{m})

for all
n>n_{0}
and all
m>m_{0}
.
To that end, assume that there exists
x,y\in\Sigma^{*}
such that
w=x\cdot y
and
v=y\cdot x
.
Then, for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we have that

\displaystyle w^{m}=

\displaystyle\underbrace{xy\cdot xy\cdot xy\cdots xy}_{m\text{ times}},\text{ and }

\displaystyle v^{m}=

\displaystyle\underbrace{yx\cdot yx\cdot yx\cdots yx}_{m\text{ times}}.

Thus
(xy)^{m-1}\in\mathsf{Facs}(w^{m})\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{Facs}(v^{m})
and
(xy)^{m-1}\notin\mathsf{Facs}(w^{m-d})\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{Facs}(v^{m-d})
for any
d\geq 2
where
d\leq m
.
Consequently, the only if direction holds.

Next, we work towards showing the if direction.
In order to prove this direction, we reformulate the statement.
Let

L(w^{\omega},v^{\omega}):=\{u\in\Sigma^{*}\mid u\sqsubseteq w^{\omega}\text{ and }u\sqsubseteq v^{\omega}\}.

Clearly, if
L(w^{\omega},v^{\omega})
is infinite, then there does not exist
n_{0},m_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathsf{Facs}(w^{n_{0}})\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{Facs}(v^{m_{0}})=\mathsf{Facs}(w^{n})\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{Facs}(v^{m})
for all
n>n_{0}
and all
m>m_{0}
.
We can therefore reformulate the contraposition of the if direction as: If
L(w^{\omega},v^{\omega})
is infinite then
w
and
v
conjugate.
To that end, assume
L(w^{\omega},v^{\omega})
is infinite.
Therefore, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
u\in L(w^{\omega},v^{\omega})
such that
|u|\geq n
.
Invoking the periodicity lemma, we conclude that
w
and
v
are conjugate.

(2) if and only if (3).

Let us first consider the if direction.
Note that for any
n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
where
n_{1}<n_{2}
, and any word
w\in\Sigma^{*}
, we have that
w^{n_{1}}\sqsubseteq w^{n_{2}}
.
Therefore,
\mathsf{Facs}(w^{n_{1}})\subseteq\mathsf{Facs}(w^{n_{2}})
.

Let
w,v\in\Sigma^{+}
be two primitive words such that there exists
r\in\mathbb{N}
where

r\geq\mathsf{max}\{|w|\in\mathbb{N}\mid w\in\mathsf{Facs}(w^{n})\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{Facs}(v^{m})\},

for all
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
.
We assume that
r\in\mathbb{N}
is the smallest such value.
Thus, for some
n^{\prime},m^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
, we have that

r=\mathsf{max}\{|w|\in\mathbb{N}\mid w\in\mathsf{Facs}(w^{n^{\prime}})\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{Facs}(v^{m^{\prime}})\}.

Furthermore, due to the fact that
\Sigma
is a fixed and finite alphabet, the number of words of in
\Sigma^{*}
with a length that is less than or equal to
r
is finite.

We now define the language

L_{\mathsf{intersect}}:=\{u\in\Sigma^{*}\mid u\in\mathsf{Facs}(w^{n})\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{Facs}(v^{m})\text{ for some }n,m\in\mathbb{N}\}.

Since the number of words in
\Sigma^{*}
with a length that is less than or equal to
r
is finite, it follows that
L_{\mathsf{intersect}}
is also finite.
Combining this with the fact that for any
n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
where
n_{1}<n_{2}
, and any word
w\in\Sigma^{*}
, we have that
\mathsf{Facs}(w^{n_{1}})\subseteq\mathsf{Facs}(w^{n_{2}})
,
there must exist some
n_{0},m_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
and all
m\geq m_{0}
, we have that

\mathsf{Facs}(w^{n_{0}})\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{Facs}(v^{m_{0}})=\mathsf{Facs}(w^{n})\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{Facs}(v^{m}).

Now let us consider the only if direction.
Assume that there exists
n_{0},m_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathsf{Facs}(w^{n_{0}})\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{Facs}(v^{m_{0}})=\mathsf{Facs}(w^{m})\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{Facs}(v^{m})
for all
n>n_{0}
and
m>m_{0}
.
Let
r:=\mathsf{max}\{\mathsf{Facs}(w^{n_{0}})\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{Facs}(v^{m_{0}})\}
.
Since for any
n>n_{0}
and
m>m_{0}
, there does not exist
u
such that
|u|>r
and
u\in\mathsf{Facs}(w^{m})\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{Facs}(v^{m})
, this concludes the proof.
∎

\{\mathtt{a}^{m}\cdot\mathtt{b}^{m}\mid m\in\mathbb{N}\}
.
From
Example

4.5
, we know that for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
such that
q\neq p
and
\mathtt{a}^{q}\cdot\mathtt{b}^{p}\equiv_{k}\mathtt{a}^{p}\cdot\mathtt{b}^{p}
.
Thus, invoking
Lemma

3.4
, we conclude that
L_{3}\notin\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{FC})
.

L_{4}
:

For
L_{4}
, let
n=1
. We therefore get the language
\{\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a}^{m}\cdot\mathtt{b}^{m}\mid m\in\mathbb{N}\}
.
From
Lemma

3.5
, we know that for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
such that
p\neq q
and
\mathtt{b}^{p}\equiv_{k+3}\mathtt{b}^{q}
.
It trivially holds that
\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a}^{p}\equiv_{k+3}\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a}^{p}
.
As
\mathsf{Facs}(\mathtt{b}^{m})\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{Facs}(\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a}^{n})=\{\varepsilon,\mathtt{b}\}
for any
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
, we can invoke
Lemma

4.4
with
r=1
and conclude that
\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a}^{p}\cdot\mathtt{b}^{p}\equiv_{k}\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a}^{p}\cdot\mathtt{b}^{q}
.
Thus, we know from
Lemma

3.4
that
L_{4}\notin\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{FC})
.

L_{5}
:
It is clear that
\mathtt{abaabb}
and
\mathtt{bbaaba}
are both co-primitive.
Thus, there exists some
r\in\mathbb{N}
where
r\geq\mathsf{max}\{|u|\in\mathbb{N}\mid u\in\mathsf{Facs}((\mathtt{abaabb})^{m})\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{Facs}((\mathtt{bbaaba})^{n})\}
for all

.
Therefore, we can invoke
Proposition

4.15
to conclude that
L_{5}\notin\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{FC})
.

L_{6}

We know that for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists

such that
\mathtt{a}^{p}\cdot\mathtt{b}^{p}\equiv_{k+2}\mathtt{a}^{q}\cdot\mathtt{a}^{p}
and
p\neq q
.
Furthermore, it is trivial that
(\mathtt{ab})^{p}\equiv_{k+2}(\mathtt{ab})^{p}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Since for any

\mathsf{Facs}(\mathtt{a}^{i}\cdot\mathtt{b}^{j})\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{Facs}((\mathtt{ab})^{l})=\{\varepsilon,\mathtt{a},\mathtt{b},\mathtt{ab}\}

F.1.
Proof of Lemma
5.3

Proof.

The proof of this result closely follows the proof of Theorem 6.2 from
(
Freydenberger 2019
)
.
We prove this result for completeness sake, as Theorem 6.2 from
(
Freydenberger 2019
)
does not directly deal with
\mathsf{FC}
and
\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}]
.
In this proof, we only show that if
L\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
is a bounded language, then
L\in\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{FC})
if and only if
L\in\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}])
.
Immediately from the fact that
\mathsf{FC}
has conjunction, disjunction, and negation, we are able to lift this to Boolean combinations of bounded languages.

The only if direction is trivial, and therefore this proof focuses on the if direction.
That is, if
L\in\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}])
, and
L
is bounded, then
L\in\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{FC})
.
Let
\varphi\in\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}]
such that
\mathcal{L}(\varphi)=L
, where
L
is bounded.
Most of the proof of this direction follows from the following claim:

Claim 6.

If
\gamma
is a regular expression that generates a bounded regular language, then there exists
\varphi\in\mathsf{FC}
such that
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(w)=\llbracket x\mathbin{\dot{\in}}\gamma\rrbracket(w)
for all
w\in\Sigma^{*}
.

Proof of Claim.

From Theorem 1.1 from
(
Ginsburg and Spanier 1966b
)
, we know that the class of bounded regular languages are exactly the class of languages that contain all finite languages, all languages
w^{*}
where
w\in\Sigma^{*}
, and is closed under finite union and finite concatenation.
We show that for any
w\in\Sigma^{*}
, the language
w^{*}
can be defined in
\mathsf{FC}
.

As syntactic sugar, we shall use atomic formulas that contain arbitrary concatenation.
It is clear that this can be rewritten into an
\mathsf{FC}
formula that only contains binary concatenation (for example, see
(
Freydenberger and Thompson 2022
)
).
Now, consider

\varphi_{w^{*}}(x):=\exists x\colon\Bigl((x\mathbin{\dot{=}}\varepsilon)\lor\bigl((x\mathbin{\dot{=}}w\cdot z)\land(x\mathbin{\dot{=}}z\cdot w)\bigr)\Bigr).

Thus, for any interpretation
\mathcal{I}:=(\mathfrak{A}_{w},\sigma)
such that
\mathcal{I}\models\varphi_{w^{*}}
, we have that either
\sigma(x)=\varepsilon
, or
\sigma(x)=w\cdot u
and
\sigma(x)=u\cdot w
for some
u\in\Sigma^{*}
.
Proposition 1.3.2 in Lothaire
(
Lothaire 1997
)
states that if
vu=uv
for
u,v\in\Sigma^{*}
, then there is some
z\in\Sigma^{*}
and
k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
u=z^{k_{1}}
and
v=z^{k_{2}}
.
Thus, if
\sigma(x)=w\cdot u
and
\sigma(x)=u\cdot w
for some
u\in\Sigma^{*}
, then
\sigma(x)=w^{k}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
.

Finite languages, finite union, and finite concatenation can easily be dealt with.
Thus, for any
\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}]
-formula of the form
x\mathbin{\dot{\in}}\gamma
, where
\gamma
generates a bounded regular language, there exists
\varphi\in\mathsf{FC}
such that
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(w)=\llbracket x\mathbin{\dot{\in}}\gamma\rrbracket(w)
for all
w\in\Sigma^{*}
.

\lhd

We now construct
\psi\in\mathsf{FC}
such that
\mathcal{L}(\psi)=\mathcal{L}(\varphi)
.
Note that every set of factors of a bounded language is also bounded (for example, see Lemma 5.1.1 in Ginsburg
(
Ginsburg 1966
)
).
Thus, since
\mathcal{L}(\varphi)
is bounded, it follows that
\mathcal{L}(\varphi)\subseteq B
, where
B:=w_{1}^{*}\cdots w_{n}^{*}
for some
w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\in\Sigma^{*}
.
Therefore, for any
w\in\Sigma^{*}
and any
x\in\mathsf{vars}(\varphi)
, if
\sigma\in\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(w)
, then
\sigma(x)\in L_{x}
, where

L_{x}\subseteq\{u\in\Sigma^{*}\mid u\sqsubseteq v\text{ and }v\in B\}

is a bounded regular language.
Therefore, we can replace any atomic formulas of the form
(x\mathbin{\dot{\in}}\gamma)
, with
(x\mathbin{\dot{\in}}\gamma^{\prime})
, where
\mathcal{L}(\gamma^{\prime})=\mathcal{L}(\gamma)\mathrel{\cap}L_{x}
.
Furthermore, it follows that
\mathcal{L}(\gamma^{\prime})
is a bounded regular language.
Then, we can invoke
6
to replace
(x\mathbin{\dot{\in}}\gamma^{\prime})
with
\varphi_{\gamma^{\prime}}(x)\in\mathsf{FC}
without altering the semantics of the formula.

After carrying out this process of replacing all regular constraints, we have a formula
\psi\in\mathsf{FC}
that is equivalent to
\varphi
.
∎
