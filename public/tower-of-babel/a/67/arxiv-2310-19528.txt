The history of the General Adjoint Functor Theorem
Hans - E. Porst

Abstract
Not only motivated by the fact that the publication of the GAFT first appeared 60 years ago in print we reconstruct its history and so show that it is no exaggeration to claim that it has appeared already 75 years ago! MSC 2020 : 18-03, 18A40 Keywords: Universal morphism, left adjoint functor.



Introduction

The General Adjoint Functor Theorem appeared in print for the first time in Freyd’s book
Abelian Categories
[
6
]
in 1964; insiders had been aware of it earlier, due to the fact that P. Freyd’s PhD thesis (1960) containing it was apparently distributed widely. Today it is often called Freyd’s Adjoint Functor Theorem, because it appeared under this name in the first edition of Mac Lane’s CWM
[
11
]
. Consequently, one may call 2024 the year when this theorem turns 60.

In fact — and that is the topic of this note — this theorem already turned 75 in 2023 and, moreover, rather should be called the Samuel-Freyd Adjoint Functor Theorem.
This suggestion may sound strange since in 1948 the notion of an adjoint functor wasn’t known at all.
However, in 1948 appeared P. Samuel’s paper On universal mappings and free topological groups
[
18
]
. And this paper
contains an existence theorem of what the author called universal mappings — and the core of the proof of the GAFT is the construction of universal arrows!

The author apparently was not yet familiar with the concepts of Category and Functor, introduced in the then recent
Eilenberg-Mac Lane-paper
[
5
]
. So he based his construction on an axiomatization of what later became known as concrete categories whose forgetful functors into the category of sets create limits — the first such axiomatization in the literature. It is somewhat irritating that in the later literature there seems to be no
reference to this fact.

One probably should recall in this context that the idea of the concept of a concrete category over the category of sets (though not that of concrete functors) was in the air already in the 1930s
(see Section
1.3
) and the annotation to Emmy Noether in Section
4
below, as was the idea of universal maps (see Section
1.3
).

1

The first examples of universal maps

1.1

Free groups (1920s)

The first known example of a universal map seems to be the embedding of the generating set
X
into the free group
F(X)
over
X
. This wasn’t really seen as an important observation since it was considered to be a property following quite obviously from the construction of
F(X)
as a group of (equivalence classes) of words in the letters
x
and
x^{-1}
with
x\in X
, “free” from any relations but those of the group axioms. (The terminology free group was introduced by Nielsen
[
15
]
in 1921). However, since the end of the 1920s algebraists started to use this characterization more often as it was considered more easy to use than the original construction.

1.2

The Stone-
\check{\rm\bf{C}}
ech-compactification (1930/1937)

The first non-trivial example of a universal map seems to be
what is called today the Stone-
\check{\rm{C}}
ech-compactification of a completely regular space.
{\rm\check{C}}
ech
[
4
]
attributes the origins of this to Tychonoff
[
19
]
by writing “ … Tychonoff proves … that, given a completely regular space
S
, there exists a bicompact Hausdorff space
\beta(S)
such that (i) S is dense in
\beta(S)
, (ii) any bounded continuous real function defined in the domain S admits of a continuous extension to the domain
\beta(S)
”.
He then proves (certainly without using categorical language) that this result can be generalized to saying that the embedding of
S
into
\beta(S)
is
E
-universal for the functor
E
embedding the category completely regular spaces into the category of compact Hausdorff spaces and that this property characterizes
\beta(S)
. In contrast to the algebraists view on the characterization of free groups by their universal property, the topologists considered this as an important result.
In
{\rm\check{C}}
ech’s paper it also becomes clear that there was a size problem to be solved in order to get a solution of the respective universal mapping problem.

1.3

Free topological groups (1941 - 1945)

As early as 1941
[
13
]
there appears the construction of a universal map where at least its extended version
[
14
]
, containing the motivation for this endeavour,
has a certain categorical flavour: Here Markov motivates his interest in free topological groups by the analogy between the relations between
groups with group homomorphisms and sets with maps on the one hand and topological groups with continuous homomorphisms and topological spaces with continuous maps contrariwise.
He interprets free groups
F(X)
over a set
X
as given by the universal property of the embedding of the generating set
X
into
F(X)
and, hence, asks for an analog property of the embedding of a “generating” topological space
X
into a topological group
F(X)
.
Since only Hausdorff topological groups were seen to be of interest (and these are completely regular automatically), and he wanted the generating space
X
to be a subspace of the respective topological group
F(X)
in analogy to the groups case, he assumed
X
to be completely regular.
He introduces sets of multinorms in order to solve the size problem.

Motivated by
[
13
]
Kakutani
[
7
]
gave a much simpler proof of the existence of free topological groups by solving the size problem in showing that, for any completely regular space
X
, the set of all topological groups
G
with
card(G)\leq max(card(X),\aleph_{0})
is a solution set and then using the by now standard construction of a universal embedding of
X
into
F(X)
only a couple of years later.

2

Samuel’s universal mapping problem (1948)

For Samuel, who already had successfully used an analog of
\check{\rm{C}}
ech’s solution of the size problem in his PhD thesis
[
17
]
, the appearance of Markov’s paper
[
14
]
seems to have been the decisive motivation to look for a more general approach to the constructions mentioned above and made him write the paper
[
18
]
.

2.1

Concrete categories

Not being aware of the then recent paper
[
10
]

he did not use the notions category and functor, but rather abstracts his axioms from Bourbaki’s notion of structure.
He, hence, was talking about
T
-sets and
T
-mappings for some “kind of structure”
T
.
This is in some detail:

1.

Given a “kind of structure”
T
then, for each set

there exists a set
T(Y)
of “
T
-structures on
Y

2.

T
-sets are pairs
(Y,\tau_{Y})
where
Y
is a set and
\tau_{Y}\in T(Y)
.

3.

T
-mappings
(Y,\tau_{Y})\xrightarrow{}(Y^{\prime},\tau_{Y^{\prime}})
are (certain) maps
Y\xrightarrow{}Y^{\prime}
.

4.

T
-isomorphisms
(Y,\tau_{Y})\xrightarrow{}(Y^{\prime},\tau_{Y^{\prime}})
are (certain) bijective
T
- mappings.

5.

For each
T
-set
(Y,\tau_{Y})
the identity

is a
T
-isomorphism.

He then assumes that
T
-sets and
T
-mappings satisfy certain axioms.
It is obvious, however, of how to translate this into the language of category theory — more precisely, into the language of concrete categories over the category
\mathit{Set}
of sets in the sense of
[

The category
\mathcal{T}
of

Given a kind of structure

and

form a concrete category

\mathcal{T}\xrightarrow{|-|}\mathit{Set}

acts as

Properties of
\mathcal{T}
:

The forgetful functor
\mathcal{T}\xrightarrow{|-|}\mathit{Set}
creates products, equalizers, and intersections and reflects isomorphisms.

One, hence, can form for each
T

and any subset

containing

(\bar{Z},\bar{\zeta})
of

S-T-mappings

(Axioms S-T):

Given structures

Remark 2

There is, however, an axiom missing. In the first step of his proof of the solution of the universal mapping problem Samuel uses the following fact, very much in line with his Axiom I
2

Fact 3

If
(X,\sigma)\xrightarrow{\phi}(Y,\tau)
is an
S-T-mapping and
\overline{\mathrm{Im}\,\phi}

\phi

(Y^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})
of

\phi

(S-T

\mathit{Set}

\phi=X\xrightarrow{\phi^{\prime}}Y^{\prime}\xrightarrow{i}Y

(X,\sigma)\xrightarrow{\phi^{\prime}}(Y^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})

This condition is satisfied an all examples given in
[

Note that in view of all other axioms this would be equivalent to

(S-T)

2.2

The problem of universal mappings

When translated into categorical language as suggested in Remark
4
, the problem of universal mappings then is the following.

Let
\mathcal{T}\xrightarrow{E}\mathcal{S}
be a concrete functor.
Given an object
(X,\sigma)
in
\mathcal{S}

(Y_{0},\tau_{0})

\mathcal{T}

\mathcal{S}
-morphism

\mathcal{S}

\mathcal{T}
-morphism
(Y_{0},\tau_{0})\xrightarrow{f}(Y,\tau)
with
\phi=E(f)\circ\psi

Samuel’s crucial (though simple) observation was that this problem essentially is a size problem: if the category
\mathcal{T}
were small one could, since
