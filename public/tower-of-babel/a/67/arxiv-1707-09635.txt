Metric-minimizing surfaces revisited
Anton Petrunin, Stephan Stadler

Abstract
A surface that does not admit a length-nonincreasing deformation is called metric-minimizing . We show that metric-minimizing surfaces in CAT ( 0 ) \mathop{\rm CAT}\nolimits(0) spaces are locally CAT ( 0 ) \mathop{\rm CAT}\nolimits(0) with respect to their length metrics.

Introduction

Main result.

Assume
s
is a Lipschitz embedding of the disc
\mathbb{D}
into the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
We say
s
is
metric-minimizing
if its intrinsic metric is minimal. Explicitly, this means that if a map
s^{\prime}\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
agrees with

on
\partial\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{S}^{1}
and fulfills

\mathop{\rm length}\nolimits s\circ\gamma\geqslant\mathop{\rm length}\nolimits s^{\prime}\circ\gamma

for all curves
\gamma
in
\mathbb{D}
, then equality holds for all curves
\gamma
.

As follows from the main theorem below, the induced metric on the disc for a metric-minimizing embedding is
\mathop{\rm CAT}\nolimits(0)
.

To formulate the theorem in full generality we need to extend the definition of metric-minimizing maps in two ways.
First, we will only assume continuity of
s
, so the map
s
might not be an embedding nor does it have to be Lipschitz;
this part is tricky — a straightforward generalization turns out to be too weak.
Secondly, we will have to allow arbitrary metric spaces as targets; this part is straightforward.

Let
Y
be a metric space
and
s\colon\mathbb{D}\to Y
be a continuous map.
Let
\gamma\colon\mathbb{S}^{1}\to Y

We say that

spans

\gamma

if

is an extension of
\gamma

s|_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}=\gamma
.

Consider the induced length-pseudometric on
\mathbb{D}
defined as

\langle x-y\rangle_{s}=\inf_{\alpha}\{\,\mathop{\rm length}\nolimits(s\circ\alpha)\,\},

where the greatest lower bound is taken over all paths
\alpha
from
x
to
y
in
\mathbb{D}
.
The distance
\langle x-y\rangle_{s}
might take infinite values.
We denote by
\langle\mathbb{D}\rangle_{s}
the corresponding metric space;
see the next section for a precise definition.

The space
\langle\mathbb{D}\rangle_{s}
comes with the projections
\mathbb{D}\xrightarrow{\hat{\pi}_{s}}\langle\mathbb{D}\rangle_{s}\xrightarrow{\hat{s}}Y
;
the restriction
\hat{\pi}_{s}|_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}
will be denoted by

Assume that

and

are two maps spanning the same curve.
We write

if there is a

meaning that

here and further

(Note that if

A map

will be called a

if

is rectifiable and

implies that the corresponding majorization

is distance-preserving.

A topological space

if the mapping cylinder of

W_{\delta}=W\bigsqcup_{\delta(u)\sim(u,0)}\mathbb{S}^{1}\times[0,1]

\mathbb{D}

The curve

An example of a disc-retract that is not a disc is shown in the picture;

The definition is motivated by the following observation:
if the boundary curve of a disc-retract

\mathbb{D}

1.1. Main theorem.

Assume
Y
is a
\mathop{\rm CAT}\nolimits(0)
space and
s\colon\mathbb{D}\to Y
is a metric-minimizing disc.
Then
\langle\mathbb{D}\rangle_{s}
is a
\mathop{\rm CAT}\nolimits(0)
disc-retract with boundary curve
\delta_{s}
.

In particular, if
s
spans a simple closed curve, then
\langle\mathbb{D}\rangle_{s}
is a
\mathop{\rm CAT}\nolimits(0)
disc.

Note that apart from continuity we did not make any regularity assumptions on the map
s
;
in particular, beforehand, the space
\langle\mathbb{D}\rangle_{s}
might have wild topology.

If we remove the condition that the boundary curve is rectifiable, then the space
\langle\mathbb{D}\rangle_{s}
might have points at infinite distance from each other.
However, our proof shows that all triangles with finite sides in
\langle\mathbb{D}\rangle_{s}
are still thin;
in particular, each metric component of
\langle\mathbb{D}\rangle_{s}
is a
\mathop{\rm CAT}\nolimits(0)
space.
So, one could consider
\langle\mathbb{D}\rangle_{s}
as a
\mathop{\rm CAT}\nolimits(0)
space where infinite distances between points are legal.

The class of metric-minimizing discs is huge — if the ambient space
Y
is
\mathop{\rm CAT}\nolimits(0)

\succcurlyeq

Metric-minimizing discs include many well-studied maps from
\mathbb{D}
to metric spaces.
The ruled discs considered by Alexandrov
[
1
]
are evidently metric-minimizing since one cannot shorten a geodesic.
In addition, harmonic maps in the sense of Korevaar–Schoen
[
13
]
from the disc to
\mathop{\rm CAT}\nolimits(0)
spaces are metric-minimizing.
Indeed, harmonic discs are solutions to the Dirichlet problem;
that is energy minimizing fillings of given loops.
Since the Korevaar–Schoen energy is convex, such solutions are unique. But decreasing the intrinsic metric will only decrease the energy of the corresponding map.
As a consequence, minimal discs in the sense of Lytchak–Wenger
[
16
]
are metric-minimizing;
these are Douglas–Rado solutions to the Plateau problem and therefore harmonic.

Let us list a few applications of metric-minimizing surfaces.
They were used by Alexander Lytchak
[

]
to study sets of positive reach (ruled surfaces).
In
[

, Stephanie Alexander and Richard Bishop used them to generalize the Gauss equation to non-smooth spaces (ruled surfaces).
In

In

In

Note that any smooth metric-minimizing disc in a Euclidean space is saddle.
The converse fails in general (see Section

1.2. Proposition.

Any smooth strictly saddle surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is locally metric-minimizing.

Structure of the paper.

In Section
2
we define a couple of metrics induced by a given continuous map.
This naturally leads to the monotone-light factorization theorem in a metric context.

In Section
3
we obtain topological control on the length space
associated to a metric-minimizing disc.
This part follows from Moore’s quotient theorem on cell-like maps.

Section
4
establishes compactness of a certain class of nonpositively curved surfaces.
This will be used later, when we approximate metric-minimizing maps.

In Section
6
we prove the key lemma which says that the restriction to a finite set of
a metric-minimizing disc factorizes as a composition of short maps over a nonpositively curved surface.
The proof uses the properties of
metric-minimizing graphs
discussed in Section
5
.

In Section
7
we prove an extension result which is of independent interest and logically detached
from the rest of the paper.
It gives a criterion that allows to extend maps from subsets
to the whole space, once extensions on finite subsets are guaranteed.

In Section
8
we assemble the proof of the main theorem.
We show that a metric-minimizing disc factorizes as a composition of short maps over a
nonpositively curved surface.
The proof is finished by showing that the first map of this factorization
induces an isometry.

In Section
9
, we provide rectified formulations of the main result in
[
21
]
and
show that they follow from the main theorem.

In the last section, we discuss the relation between saddle surfaces and metric-minimizing discs.

Definitions

Metrics and pseudometrics.

Let
X
be a set.
A
pseudometric
on
X

is a function
X\times X\to[0,\infty]
denoted as
(x,y)\mapsto|x-y|

such that

\diamond

|x-x|=0
, for any
x\in X
;

\diamond

|x-y|=|y-x|
, for any
x,y\in X
;

\diamond

|x-y|+|y-z|\geqslant|x-z|
for any
x,y,z\in X
.

If in addition
|x-y|=0
implies
x=y
, then the pseudometric
|{*}-{*}|
is called a
metric
; some authors prefer to call it an
\infty
-metric
to emphasize that the distance between points might be infinite.
The value
|x-y|
will also be called the
distance
form
x
to
y
.

A (pseudo)metric space
X
is the underlying set (also denoted by
X
) equipped with a (pseudo)metric which often will be denoted by
|{*}-{*}|_{X}
.
We will use
X
as an index if we want to emphasize that we are working in the space
X
;
for example, the ball of radius
R
centered at
z
in
X
can be denoted as

B(z,R)_{X}=\{\,{x\in X}\,:\,{|z-x|_{X}<R}\,\}.

For any pseudometric on a set there is an equivalence relation “
\sim
” such that

x\sim y\quad\iff\quad|x-y|=0.

The pseudometric induced on the set of equivalence classes

[x]=\{\,{x^{\prime}\in X}\,:\,{x^{\prime}\sim x}\,\}

becomes a metric.
The obtained metric space will be denoted as
[X]
;
it comes with the projection map
X\to[X]
defined as
x\mapsto[x]
.

For a metric space we can consider the equivalence relation “
\approx
” defined as

x\approx y\quad\iff\quad|x-y|<\infty.

Its equivalence classes are called
metric components
.
Note that by definition each metric component is a
genuine metric space
, meaning that distances between points are finite.
Consequently, any metric space is a disjoint union of genuine metric spaces.

Pseudometrics induced by a map.

Assume
X
is a topological space and
Y
is a metric space.
Let

Let us define the

Denote by
\langle X\rangle_{f}
the corresponding metric space; that is,

\langle X\rangle_{f}=[(X,\langle{*}-{*}\rangle_{f})].

Similarly, define a

|x-y|_{f}=\inf\{\mathop{\rm diam}\nolimits f(K)\},

The associated metric space will be

For the projections

\bar{x}=\bar{\pi}_{f}(x)\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{x}=\hat{\pi}_{f}(x).

Metrics induced by metrics.

If
X
is a metric space, the two constructions above can be applied to the identity map
\mathrm{id}\colon X\to X

In this case, the obtained spaces
\langle X\rangle_{\mathrm{id}}

The space

All three spaces
\langle X\rangle

have the same underlying set;
in other words, they can be considered as a single space with different metrics and tautological maps between them.
Both tautological maps

As a consequence of Lemma

is an isometry;
that is, for any continuous map

coincides with the length metric induced by

\bar{f}\colon|X|_{f}\to Y
.

Recall that a geodesic in a metric space is a curve whose length coincides with the distance between its endpoints.
A metric space is called

if any two points at a finite distance can be joined by a geodesic.

Let

Monotone-light factorization.
Let
f\colon X\to Y
be a map between topological spaces.
Recall that

\diamond

f
is called

if the inverse image of each point is connected,

\diamond

f
is called

if the inverse image of any point is totally disconnected.

Since a connected set has to be nonempty,
any monotone map is onto
.

2.4. Lemma.

Assume
X
is a locally connected compact metric space and
Y
is a metric space.
Let
f\colon X\to Y
be a continuous map.
Then the map
\bar{\pi}_{f}
is monotone and
\bar{f}
is light.
In particular,

f=\bar{f}\circ\bar{\pi}_{f}

is a monotone-light factorization.

Proof.

First, we prove the monotonicity of
\bar{\pi}_{f}
.

Assume the contrary;
that is, for some
x\in X
the equivalence class

K=\bar{\pi}_{f}^{-1}(\bar{x})=\{\,{x^{\prime}\in X}\,:\,{|x-x^{\prime}|_{f}=0}\,\}

is not connected. Since
X
is normal, we
can cover
K
by disjoint open sets
U,V\subset X
such that both intersections

K\cap U
and
K\cap V
are nonempty.

By Lemma
2
,
K
is closed.

Suppose that
x\in U
and pick
x^{\prime}\in K\cap V
.
Then there is a sequence of connected sets
K_{n}\ni x,x^{\prime}
such that
\mathop{\rm diam}\nolimits f(K_{n})<\tfrac{1}{n}
.
For each
n
we choose a point
k_{n}\in K_{n}\backslash(U\cup V)
.
Let
k
be a partial limit of the sequence
(k_{n})
.
It follows that
k\in K\backslash(U\cup V)
, a contradiction.

Assume
\bar{f}
is not light;
that is, the inverse image of some

contains a closed connected set
C\subset|X|_{f}
with more than one point.
Note that the inverse image
Z:=\bar{\pi}_{f}^{-1}C
is connected due to the monotonicity of
\bar{\pi}_{f}
.
It follows that
|z-z^{\prime}|_{f}=0
for any two points

, a contradiction.

Disc retracts

A disc-retract as defined above is nothing but the image of strong deformation retraction of
\mathbb{D}
;
the restriction of the retraction to the boundary can be taken as the corresponding boundary curve.
We will not need this statement, but it follows from Moore’s quotient theorem quoted below.

3.1. Proposition.

Let
Y
be a metric space and
s\colon\mathbb{D}\to\ Y
be a metric-minimizing disc.
Then
|\mathbb{D}|_{s}
is a disc-retract with boundary curve
\delta_{s}=\bar{\pi}_{s}|_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}
.
Moreover, the map
\tau_{s}\colon\langle\mathbb{D}\rangle_{s}\to|\mathbb{D}|_{s}
is injective and defines an isometry

\langle\mathbb{D}\rangle_{s}\to\penalty\langle|\mathbb{D}|\rangle_{s}
;
that is,
\tau_{s}
is an isometry from
\langle\mathbb{D}\rangle_{s}
to
|\mathbb{D}|_{s}
equipped with induced length metric.

We need a little preparation before giving the proof.

Let
Y
be a metric space and

s\colon\mathbb{D}\to Y
be a continuous map.
We say that
s
has
no bubbles

if for any point
p\in Y
every connected component of the complement
\mathbb{D}\backslash s^{-1}\{p\}
contains a point from
\partial\mathbb{D}
.

3.2. Lemma.

Let
Y
be a metric space and
s\colon\mathbb{D}\to Y
be a metric-minimizing disc.
Then
s
has no bubbles.

Proof.

Assume the contrary;
that is, there is

such that the complement
\mathbb{D}\backslash s^{-1}(y)
contains a connected component

with
\partial\mathbb{D}\cap\Omega=\varnothing
.

Let us define a new map

by setting
s^{\prime}(z)=y
for any

and
s^{\prime}(x)=s(x)
for any

.

By construction,
s^{\prime}
and
s
agree on
\partial\mathbb{D}
. Moreover,
s\succcurlyeq s^{\prime}

because of the majorization
\mu\colon\hat{\pi}_{s}(x)\mapsto\penalty\hat{\pi}_{s^{\prime}}(x)

Note that

\langle x-x^{\prime}\rangle_{s}>0=\langle x-x^{\prime}\rangle_{s^{\prime}}

for a pair of distinct points

In particular,

4
Compactness lemma

A sequence of pairs
(X_{n},\gamma_{n})
, where
X_{n}
is a metric space and
\gamma_{n}\colon\mathbb{S}^{1}\to X_{n}
is a
closed curve is said to

to
(X_{\infty},\gamma_{\infty})
if there is a convergence of
X_{n}
to
X_{\infty}

in the sense of Gromov–Hausdorff for which
\gamma_{n}
converges to
\gamma_{\infty}
pointwise.

More precisely, we ask the following.

(1)

There is a metric
\rho
on the disjoint union

\bm{X}=X_{\infty}\sqcup X_{1}\sqcup X_{2}\dots

that restricts to the given metric on each
X_{\alpha}
, for
\alpha\in\{1,2,\dots,\infty\}
,
and such that
X_{n}
converge to
X_{\infty}
in the sense of Hausdorff as subsets in
(\bm{X},\rho)
.

(2)

The sequence of compositions
\gamma_{n}\colon\mathbb{S}^{1}\to X_{n}\hookrightarrow\bm{X}

converges to
\gamma_{\infty}\colon\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\penalty X_{\infty}\hookrightarrow\bm{X}
pointwise.

Consider the class
\mathcal{K}_{\ell}
of
\mathop{\rm CAT}\nolimits(0)
disc-retracts whose marked
boundary curves have Lipschitz constant
\ell
.

4.1. Compactness lemma.

\mathcal{K}_{\ell}
is compact in the topology described above.

The lemma follows from the two lemmas below.

4.2. Lemma.

\mathcal{K}_{\ell}
is precompact in the topology described above.

Proof.

Let
K
be a metric space with the isometry class in
\mathcal{K}_{\ell}
.

Denote by
\mathop{\rm area}\nolimits A
the two-dimensional Hausdorff measure of
A\subset K
.
By the Euclidean isoperimetric inequality, we have

\mathop{\rm area}\nolimits K\leqslant\pi{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\hskip 0.5pt}\ell^{2}.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
.
Set
m=\lceil 10{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\hskip 0.5pt}\tfrac{\ell}{\varepsilon}\rceil
.
Choose
m
points
y_{1},\dots,y_{m}
on
\partial K
that divide
\partial K
into arcs of equal length.

Consider the maximal set of points
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
such that
d(x_{i},x_{j})>\varepsilon
and
d(x_{i},y_{j})>\varepsilon
.

Note that the set
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n},y_{1},\dots,y_{m}\}

is an
\varepsilon
-net in
(K,d)
.
Further, note that the balls
B_{i}=B_{\varepsilon/2}(x_{i})

do not overlap.

By comparison,

\mathop{\rm area}\nolimits B_{i}\geqslant\tfrac{\pi{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\hskip 0.5pt}\varepsilon^{2}}{4}.

It follows that
n\leqslant 4{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\hskip 0.5pt}\left(\tfrac{\ell}{\varepsilon}\right)^{2}
.
In particular, there is an integer-valued function
N(\varepsilon)
, such that any

as above contains an
\varepsilon
-net
with at most
N(\varepsilon)
points.

The latter means that the class
\mathcal{K}_{\ell}
is uniformly totally bounded.
By the selection theorem
[
6
, 7.4.15]
, the class of metrics with this property is precompact in the Gromov–Hausdorff topology.

Since the set of
\ell
-Lipschitz maps defined on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
with compact target is compact
with respect to pointwise convergence, we conclude that
\mathcal{K}_{\ell}
is precompact in the topology defined above.
∎

4.3. Lemma.

\mathcal{K}_{\ell}
is closed in the topology described above.

Proof.

Let
(X_{n},\gamma_{n})
be a sequence in

Assume

and

Choose a point

By comparison,

is a
(5{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\hskip 0.5pt}\ell)
-Lipschitz continuous ruled disc. The limit map

-Lipschitz continuous ruled disc.

5
Metric-minimizing graphs

Metric-minimizing graphs
are defined analogously to metric-minimizing discs.

Namely, let
Y
be a metric space,
\Gamma
be a finite graph and
A
be a subset of its vertexes.
Given two maps

, we write
f\succcurlyeq f^{\prime}\ ({\rm rel.}\ {A})
if
f
and
f^{\prime}
agree on
A

and there is a majorization
\mu\colon\langle\Gamma\rangle_{f}\to\langle\Gamma\rangle_{f^{\prime}}

such that
f(a)=\penalty f^{\prime}(\mu(a))
for any
a\in A
.

A map
f\colon\Gamma\to Y
is called
metric-minimizing relative to
A
if
f\succcurlyeq f^{\prime}\ ({\rm rel.}\ {A})
implies that the majorization
\mu
is distance-preserving.

5.1. Proposition.

Let
Y
be a
\mathop{\rm CAT}\nolimits(0)
space,

\Gamma
be a finite graph and
A
be a subset of its vertexes.

Given a continuous map

that is metric-minimizing relative to
A
and
f\succcurlyeq h\ ({\rm rel.}\ {A})
.

Proof.

Let us parametrize each edge of
\Gamma
by
[0,1]
.
A map

will be called
straight
if it
sends each edge of
\Gamma
to a constant-speed geodesic path in
Y
.

If

is straight, then

if and only if

|f(v)-f(w)|_{Y}\geqslant|h(v)-h(w)|_{Y}

for any two adjacent vertexes
v
and
w
in
\Gamma
.
In particular, we can assume that the given map
f
is straight.

By finiteness of the number of vertexes and Zorn’s lemma,
it is sufficient to prove that for any ordered sequence of straight maps
f_{1}\succcurlyeq f_{2}\succcurlyeq\dots
there exists a map
f\preccurlyeq f_{n}
for all

.

Assume the contrary; let us apply the
ultralimit+projection construction
.

Namely, fix an ultrafilter
\omega
; denote by

the ultrapower of

.
Then
Y^{\omega}

is a
\mathop{\rm CAT}\nolimits(0)
space that contains
Y
as a closed convex subset.
The
\omega
-limit
f_{\omega}\colon\Gamma\to Y^{\omega}
is well-defined since all
f_{n}
are Lipschitz continuous.
Denote by
f^{\prime}
the composition of
f_{\omega}
with the nearest point projection
Y^{\omega}\to Y
.

The nearest point projection to a closed convex set in
\mathop{\rm CAT}\nolimits(0)
space is short.
Therefore
f_{n}\succcurlyeq f^{\prime}
for any
n
.
Let
f^{\prime\prime}
denote the straightening of
f^{\prime}
.
Then
f^{\prime}\succcurlyeq f^{\prime\prime}
and the claim follows.

10
Saddle surfaces

In this section we will discuss the relation between metric-minimizing discs and saddle discs.

Recall that a map
s\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is called
saddle
if for any hyperplane
\Pi\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
each of the connected components of
\mathbb{D}\backslash s^{-1}\Pi
meets the boundary.

If
s
is a smooth embedding in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, then it is saddle if and only if the obtained surface has nonpositive Gauss curvature.
An old conjecture of Samuel Shefel states that any saddle disc in a
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is
\mathop{\rm CAT}\nolimits(0)
with respect to its length metric, see
[
26
]
.

It is evident that any metric-minimizing disc
s
in a Euclidean space is saddle.

Three-dimensional case.

In general, a saddle disc may not be globally metric-minimizing.
An example is shown in the picture.
It is a saddle polyhedral disc made from 10 triangles
with a hexagon boundary.
The boundary curve goes along the Y-shape marked with bold lines;
each segment of the Y-shape is traveled twice back and forth.

The picture is rotationally symmetric by the angle
\tfrac{2}{3}{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\hskip 0.5pt}\pi
.
A shortening deformation can be obtained by rotating the central triangle slightly counterclockwise and extending the map on the remaining 9 triangles linearly.

By smoothing this example one can produce a smooth saddle disc that is not metric-minimizing.

Proposition
1
states that there are no local examples of that type that are
smooth and
strictly saddle
meaning that the principal curvatures at each interior point have opposite signs.
To prove this proposition we need to introduce a certain energy functional.

Let
s\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a smooth map.

Fix an array of vector fields
\bm{v}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})
on
\mathbb{D}
.
Assume that each integral curve of vector fields
v_{i}
goes from boundary to boundary yielding sweep outs of the whole disc
\mathbb{D}
.

Consider the energy functional

E_{\bm{v}}s:=\sum_{i}\int\limits_{\mathbb{D}}|v_{i}s|^{2},

where
vs=ds(v)
denotes the derivative of
s
in the direction of the field
v
.
Set

\Delta_{\bm{v}}s=\sum_{i}v_{i}(v_{i}s).

It is convenient to think of the operator
s\mapsto\Delta_{\bm{v}}s

as an analog of the Laplacian.

Note that

(i)

E_{\bm{v}}
is well-defined for any Lipschitz map
s
.

(ii)

E_{\bm{v}}
is convex; that is,

E_{\bm{v}}s_{t}\leqslant(1-t){\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\hskip 0.5pt}E_{\bm{v}}s_{0}+t{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\hskip 0.5pt}E_{\bm{v}}s_{1},

where
s_{t}=(1-t){\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\hskip 0.5pt}s_{0}+t{\hskip 0.5pt\cdot\hskip 0.5pt}s_{1}
and
0\leqslant t\leqslant 1
.
Moreover, the equality holds for any

if and only if for any

v_{i}s_{0}=v_{i}s_{1}
almost everywhere.

(iii)

If
s_{0}
is a smooth

-minimizing map in the class of Lipschitz maps with given boundary data, then
s_{0}
is metric-minimizing.

Indeed, if
s_{1}\preccurlyeq s_{0}
, then
s_{1}
has to be Lipschitz.
It follows that
E_{\bm{v}}s_{1}\leqslant E_{\bm{v}}s_{0}
and from convexity
E_{\bm{v}}s_{t}\leqslant E_{\bm{v}}s_{0}
if
0\leqslant t\leqslant 1
.
Since
s_{0}
is
E_{\bm{v}}
-minimizing,
E_{\bm{v}}s_{t}=E_{\bm{v}}s_{0}
for any

.
Hence
v_{i}s_{0}=v_{i}s_{1}
almost everywhere.
Since
\mathbb{D}
is swept out by arcs of integral curves of

(iv)

A smooth map

is a

-minimizing map among the class of Lipschitz maps with given boundary if and only if

\Delta_{\bm{v}}s=0.

Four-dimensional case.

Except for constructing an energy as we did above,
we do not see any way to show that a given smooth surface is metric-minimizing.
Locally, the appropriate energy functional can be described by three functions defined on the disc.
These three functions are subject to certain differential equations.
Straightforward computations show that on generic smooth saddle surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{4}

there is no solution even locally.

For that reason we expect that generic smooth saddle surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
are not locally metric-minimizing.
That is, arbitrarily small neighborhoods of any point admit deformations that shrink
the length metric and keep the boundary fixed.
On the other hand, we do not have an example of a saddle surface for which this condition would hold at a single point.
