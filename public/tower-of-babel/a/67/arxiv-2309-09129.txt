L 1 L^{1} Estimation: On the Optimality of Linear Estimators
Leighton P. Barnes, Alex Dytso, Jingbo Liu, H. Vincent Poor

Abstract
Consider the problem of estimating a random variable X X from noisy observations Y = X + Z Y=X+Z , where Z Z is standard normal, under the L 1 L^{1} fidelity criterion. It is well known that the optimal Bayesian estimator in this setting is the conditional median. This work shows that the only prior distribution on X X that induces linearity in the conditional median is Gaussian. Along the way, several other results are presented. In particular, it is demonstrated that if the conditional distribution P X | Y = y P_{X|Y=y} is symmetric for all y y , then X X must follow a Gaussian distribution. Additionally, we consider other L p L^{p} losses and observe the following phenomenon: for p ∈ [ 1 , 2 ] p\in[1,2] , Gaussian is the only prior distribution that induces a linear optimal Bayesian estimator, and for p ∈ ( 2 , ∞ ) p\in(2,\infty) , infinitely many prior distributions on X X can induce linearity. Finally, extensions are provided to encompass noise models leading to conditional distributions from certain exponential families.



I
Introduction

The theory of linear estimation plays a central role in Bayesian estimation. Linear estimators are easy to deploy and are thus attractive from a practical point of view. From a theoretical point of view, linear estimators often serve as useful benchmarks for understanding the performance of other more complex estimators. Furthermore, they appear as key objects in the study of Bayesian topics such as exponential families and conjugate priors. Thus characterizing the optimality of linear estimators is important from both practical and theoretical points of view.

I-A

Outline and Contributions

The paper outline and contributions are as follows. In Section
II
, we start by making some preliminary observations about characterizing which prior distributions lead to linear conditional medians. In Section
II-C
, Proposition
2
, we show that a Gaussian prior distribution does indeed yield a linear conditional median. Section
II-D
, Proposition
5
provides an equivalent condition to linearity in terms of a convolution; and Section
II-E
discusses finding the nullspace of the corresponding linear operator. In Section
III
, we present our main results and show that the Gaussian distribution is the only prior distribution that induces a linear conditional median. The proof of the main theorem uses a Fourier approach to solve the convolution equation, with care being needed to establish growth estimates so that the Fourier transform of the relevant measures can be made sense of using the machinery of tempered distributions. The non-negativity of the measure plays a key role in establishing these growth estimates, and without it there are indeed counterexamples. Finally, in Section
IV
we conclude by discussing the Poisson noise case, other
L^{p}
losses, exponential families, and possible multidimensional extensions.

II
The Problem Setup and Some Preliminary Observations

In this section, we begin by providing preliminary observations about the problem, derive a necessary and sufficient condition for linearity of the conditional median to hold, and try to point out the reasons why solving the problem is a challenging task. Along the way, we also derive some results that might be of independent interest.

II-A

The Problem Setup

Despite considerable research into linear estimators, to the best of our knowledge, the question of identifying the set of prior distributions on
X
that ensure that
\mathsf{m}(X|Y)
is a linear function of

For a given

y\in\mathbb{R}

\mathsf{m}(X|Y=y)=ay\;?

(8)

Unless otherwise noted, we will focus on the Gaussian in (
1

It is well known that for the conditional expectation, under the model in (
1

\mathbb{E}[X|Y=y]=ay,\,\forall y\in\mathbb{R},

(9)

if and only if
a\in[0,1)

\mathcal{N}_{L^{1}}(T_{a})
is an infinite-dimensional subset of

. Moreover,

\displaystyle{\rm span}\left(\bigcup_{(\mu_{n},\omega_{n}):z_{n}=\frac{\frac{1-a}{\sqrt{a}}\mu_{n}+j\sqrt{a}\omega_{n}}{\sqrt{2}}}\hskip-11.38092ptf_{\mu_{n},\frac{a}{1-a},\omega_{n}}\right)\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{L^{1}}(T_{a}),

(57)

Proof.

Chose
\omega_{n}
and
\mu_{n}

\displaystyle z_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{\mu}{\sigma^{2}}+jw}{\sqrt{2b}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{\mu}{\frac{a}{1-a}}+jw}{\sqrt{2\frac{1}{a}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\frac{1-a}{\sqrt{a}}\mu_{n}+j\sqrt{a}\omega_{n}\right)

(59)

where

\mathrm{erf}
function. Then, by using (

since

∎

The above theorem says that the null space of
T_{a}
over

contains infinitely many Gabor wavelets. These Gabor wavelets are special in the sense that the location and frequency components correspond to the real and imaginary parts of zeros of the
\mathrm{erf}

\displaystyle\frac{f_{\mu_{n},\frac{a}{1-a},\omega_{n}}(x)+f_{\mu_{n},\frac{a}{1-a},\omega_{n}}(x)}{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\exp\left(-\frac{\left(x-\mu_{n}\right)^{2}}{2\frac{a}{1-a}}\right)\cos(x\omega_{n}),

(61)

\displaystyle\frac{f_{\mu_{n},\frac{a}{1-a},\omega_{n}}(x)-f_{\mu_{n},\frac{a}{1-a},\omega_{n}}(x)}{2j}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\exp\left(-\frac{\left(x-\mu_{n}\right)^{2}}{2\frac{a}{1-a}}\right)\sin(x\omega_{n}).

(62)

The above discussion shows that the non-negativity of probability measures plays a crucial role; otherwise, we get infinitely many solutions. Indeed, the non-negativity of the probability measures will also play a key role in our proof. An interesting implication of our results is that infinite linear combinations of the functions defined on the left-hand side of (
57
) can only result in a single non-negative function, which is the Gaussian pdf.
Finally, in Section
IV-B
, we will revisit functions similar to Gabor wavelets, where such functions will be used to demonstrate that infinitely many distributions can induce linearity in Bayesian estimators under other
L^{p}
loss.

III
Main Results

In this section, we present our main result.
Our main technique uses the theory of
distributions
and
tempered distributions
from functional analysis,
the background of which can be found in
[
27
]
.
In a nutshell, this is a rigorous framework for extending the concept of functions to include important examples such as the Heaviside delta function that arise naturally from Fourier analysis.

The main result of this work is the following theorem.

Theorem 9
.

The conditional median satisfies
\mathsf{m}(X|Y=y)=ay,\,\forall y\in\mathbb{R}
if and only if
a\in[0,1)
and
X\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{a}{1-a}\right)
.

Proof.

Recall that according to Proposition
4
, the linearity of the conditional median is equivalent to the following integral equation:

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g^{\prime}(t-x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=0,\quad\forall t\in\mathbb{R}.

(63)

Assuming that
\mu
satisfies (

below shows that
\mu
must be a tempered distribution, and therefore we can take its Fourier transform, denoted as
\widehat{\mu}

Next, observe that
\widehat{\mu}
is a (tempered) distribution supported at the origin,
as is shown in
Lemma

below.
Equivalently, we have that
\mu
can be represented as a polynomial function

\mu(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}x^{i}.

(64)

We next show that all coefficients but
a_{0}
are zero and that
\mu
is a constant.
Suppose that
\mu(t-x)=\sum_{i,j\colon i+j\leq k}b_{ij}t^{i}x^{j}
for some coefficients

a_{i+j}=0

Next, define

\displaystyle\epsilon_{j}:

\displaystyle=\int g^{\prime}(x)x^{j}=-\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{sign}(Z)Z^{j}]

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}0&j\text{ even}\\

Clearly, a polynomial of degree

IV
Discussion, Extensions and Future Directions

This work has focused on characterizing which prior distributions give an optimal estimator (with respect to
L^{1}
loss) that is linear, which is equivalent to the answering the question of when conditional medians are a linear function of the observation. We have focused on a Gaussian noise model and
L^{1}
loss, but the question can be considered more generally. In this section, we will discuss several interesting future directions and show several extensions.

IV-A

A Near Miss for Poisson Noise

One interesting direction is to consider the case of Poisson noise, where the input-output relationship is given by

P_{Y|X}(y|x)=\frac{1}{y!}x^{y}\mathrm{e}^{-x},\,x\in\mathbb{R}_{+},y\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

(79)

with the convention that
0^{0}=1
.

It is well-known that a linear conditional expectation is induced by gamma distribution prior, that is, when
X\sim{\rm Gam}(\alpha,\beta)
where the pdf of a gamma distribution is given by

(82)

(83)

IV-C

Extension to the Natural Exponential Family

In this section, we extend our results to members of certain exponential families of distributions. Recall that a
natural exponential family
, parameterized by
x\in\mathbb{R}
, is characterized by the pdf of the form

f_{Y|X}(y|x)=h(y)\mathrm{e}^{xy-\psi(x)},\,y\in\mathbb{R},\,x\in\mathbb{R},

(96)

where
h(y):\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
is known as the
base measure
and
\psi:\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is known as the
log-partition function
. The log-partition function is a convex function, and its convex conjugate is defined as

\psi^{*}(y)=\sup_{x}\left(xy-\psi(x)\right),\,y\in\mathbb{R}.

(97)

We now seek to characterize the prior that induces linearity of the conditional median of
X
when the noise distribution induces a conditional distribution for
Y
given
X=x
whose pdf is a member of a natural exponential family in (
96
).

Importantly, we note that prior literature on the exponential family, such as the canonical result of
[
2
]
, has typically focused on related but different questions. Specifically, the focus of
[
2
]
and related works is on the linearity of Bayesian estimators of
\psi^{\prime}(X)
; the motivation being that
\psi^{\prime}(X)=\mathbb{E}[Y|X]
(i.e., the mean of the exponential model). We, however, focus on the linearity of the Bayesian estimator of the natural parameter
X
and not the mean parameter
\psi^{\prime}(X)
. The two questions coincide only for the Gaussian case when
\psi(x)=\frac{x^{2}}{2}
. This alternative perspective, naturally, results in a different structure of our conjugate prior than the one derived in
[
2
]
.

For the natural exponential family, the condition for linearity of the conditional median of
X
is derived verbatim to (
27
) and is given by: for
a\in[0,1)

\mathbb{E}\left[{\rm sign}(X-ay)h(y)e^{Xy-\psi(X)}\right]=0,\,\forall y\in\mathbb{R}.

(98)

Our restriction to the
L^{1}
case here is done for ease of presentation. By using similar techniques to those in Section
IV-B
the results can be extended to
L^{p}
with
1\leq p\leq 2
.

While we do not have an answer for every exponential family, we show that our main result can be adapted to find the solution to (
98
) when
\psi^{*}
is finite. This answers the question for many
natural
exponential families, but answering this in full generality for any exponential family is outside of the scope of this work and would need to handle cases such as (
84
) discussed above.

Theorem 14
.

Suppose that the convex conjugate
\psi^{*}(y)
is finite for all
y\in\mathbb{R}
. Then,
the only solution to (
98

f_{X}(x)\propto\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2a}+\psi(x)},

(99)

provided that it is integrable.

Proof.

Suppose that a probability measure

satisfies (

) and induces linearity. Starting with (

\displaystyle\int\hskip-2.84544pt{\rm sign}(x-ay)h(y)\mathrm{e}^{xy-\psi(x)}\,\mathrm{d}P_{X}(x)=0,\forall y

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\int\hskip-2.84544pt{\rm sign}(x-ay)\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}+xy+\frac{x^{2}}{2}-\psi(x)}\mathrm{d}P_{X}(x)=0,\forall y

(100)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\int\hskip-2.84544pt{\rm sign}(x-ay)\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{(y-x)^{2}}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{x^{2}}{2}-\psi(x)}\,\mathrm{d}P_{X}(x)=0,\forall y

(101)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\int g^{\prime}(y-x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)=0,\forall y

(102)

where in the last implication we have used the same steps leading to the proof of Proposition
5

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\nu(x)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{x^{2}}{2}-\psi(\sqrt{a}x)}\mathrm{d}P_{X}(\sqrt{a}x),

(103)

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=-\mathsf{sign}(x)\phi(x).

(104)

To show that

since
\max\{|g^{\prime}(x)|,g(x)\}\lesssim e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}
, we have to show that the following quantity is finite for all

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{(x-y)^{2}}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{y^{2}}{2}}\int\mathrm{e}^{xy-\psi(\sqrt{a}x)}\mathrm{d}P_{X}(\sqrt{a}x)

(105)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{y^{2}}{2}+\sup_{x}\left(\frac{xy}{\sqrt{a}}-\psi(x)\right)\right)

(106)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{y^{2}}{2}+\psi^{*}\left(\frac{y}{\sqrt{a}}\right)\right)\;,

(107)

The situation with extending the results from the present paper to the multivariate case is more complex. the asymptotics of the Bayesian risk

.

To the best of our knowledge, the proofs based on Stein’s method and on the cumulant method are new.

B-A

Tweedie Formula Approach

Using Tweedie’s formula, the conditional expectation can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X|Y=y]=y+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}y}\log f_{Y}(y),\,y\in\mathbb{R}

is the pdf of

\displaystyle(a-1)y=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}y}\log f_{Y}(y),\,y\in\mathbb{R}.

\displaystyle f_{Y}(y)=\mathrm{e}^{(a-1)\frac{y^{2}}{2}+by+c}

(126)

for some
b
and

. Therefore,
f_{Y}
is Gaussian. Now using the standard characteristic function argument, the only distribution on
X
that induces
Y
to be Gaussian is Gaussian.
