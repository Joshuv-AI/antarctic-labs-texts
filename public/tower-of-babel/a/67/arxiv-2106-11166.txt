Abstract




Abstract

In this book chapter we address the problem of 3D shape registration and we propose a novel technique based on spectral graph theory and probabilistic matching. Recent advancement
in shape acquisition technology has led to the capture of large amounts of 3D data.
Existing real-time multi-camera 3D acquisition methods provide a frame-wise reliable visual-hull
or mesh representations for real 3D animation sequences
The task of 3D shape analysis involves tracking, recognition, registration, etc.
Analyzing 3D data in a single framework is still a challenging task considering
the large variability of the data gathered with different acquisition devices.
3D shape registration is one such challenging shape analysis task.
The main contribution of this chapter is to extend the spectral graph matching methods
to very large graphs by combining spectral graph matching with Laplacian embedding.
Since the embedded representation of a graph is obtained by dimensionality reduction
we claim that the existing spectral-based methods are not easily applicable.
We discuss solutions for the exact and
inexact graph isomorphism problems and recall the main spectral properties of the combinatorial graph Laplacian;
We provide a novel analysis of the commute-time embedding that allows us to interpret the latter in
terms of the PCA of a graph, and to select the appropriate dimension of the associated embedded
metric space;
We derive a unit hyper-sphere normalization for the commute-time embedding that allows us to register
two shapes with different samplings;
We propose a novel method to find the eigenvalue-eigenvector ordering and the eigenvector sign using the
eigensignature (histogram) which is invariant to the isometric shape deformations and fits well in
the spectral graph matching framework, and
we present a probabilistic shape matching formulation using an expectation maximization
point registration algorithm which alternates between aligning the eigenbases and finding a vertex-to-vertex assignment.

Introduction

In this chapter we discuss the problem of 3D shape matching. Recent advancement
in shape acquisition technology has led to the capture of large amounts of 3D data.
Existing real-time multi-camera 3D acquisition methods provide a frame-wise reliable visual-hull

Analyzing 3D data in a single framework is still a challenging task considering
the large variability of the data gathered with different acquisition devices.
3D shape registration is one such challenging shape analysis task.
The major difficulties in shape registration arise due to: 1) variation in the shape acquisition
techniques, 2) local deformations in non-rigid shapes, 3) large acquisition discrepancies
(
e.g.
, holes, topology change, surface acquisition noise), 4) local scale change.

Most of the previous attempts of shape matching can be broadly categorized as
extrinsic

or
intrinsic
approaches depending on how they analyze the properties of the underlying manifold.
Extrinsic approaches mainly focus on finding a global or local rigid transformation between two 3D shapes.

There is large set of approaches based on variations of the iterative closest point (ICP)
algorithm
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
that falls in the category of extrinsic approaches.
However, the majority of these approaches compute rigid transformations for shape registration and are
not directly applicable to non-rigid shapes.
Intrinsic approaches are a natural choice for finding dense correspondences between
articulated shapes, as they embed the shape in some canonical domain which preserves
some important properties of the manifold,
e.g.
, geodesics and
angles. Intrinsic approaches are preferable over extrinsic as they provide a global representation which
is invariant to non-rigid deformations that are common in the real-world 3D shapes.

Interestingly, mesh representation also enables the adaptation of well established
graph matching algorithms that use eigenvalues and eigenvectors of graph matrices,
and are theoretically well investigated in the framework of
spectral graph theory
(SGT)

e.g.
,
[
10
,
11
]
.
Existing methods in SGT are mainly theoretical results applied to small graphs and under the premise
that eigenvalues can be computed exactly.
However, spectral graph matching does not easily generalize to very large graphs due to
the following reasons: 1) eigenvalues are approximately computed using eigen-solvers, 2) eigenvalue multiplicity and
hence ordering change are not well studied, 3) exact matching is intractable for very large graphs.
It is important to note that these methods mainly focus on exact graph matching while majority
of the real-world graph matching applications involve graphs with different cardinality and for which only a
subgraph isomorphism can be sought.

The main contribution of this work is to extend the spectral graph methods
to very large graphs by combining spectral graph matching with
Laplacian embedding
.
Since the embedded representation of a graph is obtained by dimensionality reduction
we claim that the existing SGT methods (
e.g.

[
10
]
) are not easily applicable.
The major contributions of this work are the following: 1) we discuss solutions for the exact and
inexact graph isomorphism problems and recall the main spectral properties of the combinatorial graph Laplacian,
2) we provide a novel analysis of the commute-time embedding that allows us to interpret the latter in
terms of the PCA of a graph, and to select the appropriate dimension of the associated embedded
metric space,
3) we derive a unit hyper-sphere normalization for the commute-time embedding that allows us to register
two shapes with different samplings,
4) we propose a novel method to find the eigenvalue-eigenvector ordering and the eigenvector signs using the
eigensignatures (histograms) that are invariant to the isometric shape deformations and which fits well in
the spectral graph matching framework,
5) we present a probabilistic shape matching formulation using an

point registration algorithm which alternates between aligning the eigenbases and finding a vertex-to-vertex assignment.

In this chapter we present an intrinsic approach for unsupervised 3D shape registration
first proposed in
[

using the graph Laplacian which allows us to embed a 3D shape in an isometric subspace
invariant to non-rigid deformations. This leads to an embedded point cloud representation where
each vertex of the underlying graph is mapped to a point in a
K

Thus, the problem of non-rigid 3D shape registration is transformed into a

need to be correctly aligned. This alignment is critical for the spectral matching methods
because the two eigen spaces are defined up to the signs and the ordering of the eigenvectors of

eigenvectors as eigensignatures.
In the final step, a point registration method based on a variant of

[

is applied in order to register two sets of
points associated with the Laplacian embeddings of the two shapes. The proposed algorithm
alternates between the estimation of an orthogonal transformation matrix associated with
the alignment of the two eigen spaces and the computation of probabilistic vertex-to-vertex assignment.

Graph Matrices

A shape can be treated as a connected
undirected weighted graph

\mathscr{G}=\{\mathscr{V},\mathscr{E}\}
where
\mathscr{V}(\mathscr{G})=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
is the vertex set,

\mathscr{E}(\mathscr{G})=\{e_{ij}\}
is the edge set.
Let
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}
be the weighted adjacency matrixweighted of this graph. Each

(i,j)^{\text{th}}
entry of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}
matrix stores weight
w_{ij}
whenever there is an edge
e_{ij}\in\mathscr{E}(\mathscr{G})
between graph
vertices
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
and
0
otherwise with all the diagonal elements set to
0
.
We use the following notations:
The degree
d_{i}
of a graph vertex
d_{i}=\sum_{i\sim j}w_{ij}
(
i\sim j
denotes the set of vertices

v_{j}
which are adjacent to
v_{i}
),
the
degree
matrix

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}=\mbox{diag}[d_{1}\ldots d_{i}\ldots d_{n}]
, the
n\times 1

vector
\mathbbm{1}=(1\ldots 1)^{\top}
(the constant vector), the
n\times 1

degree
vector

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{d}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}\mathbbm{1}
, and the
graph volume

\mbox{Vol}(\mathscr{G})=\sum_{i}d_{i}
.

In spectral graph theory, it is
common
[
39
,
40
]
to use the following expression for the edge weights:

w_{ij}=e^{-\frac{\text{dist}^{2}(v_{i},v_{j})}{\sigma^{2}}},

(1)

where
\text{dist}(v_{i},v_{j})
denotes any distance metric between two vertices and
\sigma

is a free parameter.
In the case of a
fully connected graph
, matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}
is
also referred to as the
similarity matrix
.
The
normalized weighted adjacency matrix
writes

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}^{-1/2}
.
The
transition
matrix of the non-symmetric reversible Markov chain associated
with the graph is

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}}_{R}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}^{-1/2}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}^{1/2}
.

3
Spectral Graph Isomorphism

Let
\mathscr{G}_{A}
and
\mathscr{G}_{B}
be two
undirected weighted graphs
with the same
number of nodes,
n
, and let
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}_{A}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}_{B}
be their adjacency
matrices. They are real-symmetric matrices. In the general case, the
number
r
of distinct eigenvalues of these matrices is smaller than
n
. The standard spectral methods
only apply to those graphs whose adjacency matrices have
n

distinct eigenvalues (each eigenvalue has multiplicity one), which
implies
that the eigenvalues can be ordered.

Graph isomorphism
[
43
]
can be written as the following minimization problem:

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}^{\star}=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}}\|{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}_{A}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}_{B}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}^{\top}}\|_{F}^{2}

(8)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}
is an
n\times n
permutation matrix (see
appendix
A
) with
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}^{\star}
as the desired vertex-to-vertex permutation matrix and
\|{\bullet}\|_{F}
is the
Frobenius norm defined by (see appendix
B
):

\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}\|^{2}_{F}=\langle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{w}_{ij}^{2}=\mbox{tr}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}})

(9)

Let:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}_{A}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{A}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}_{A}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{A}^{\top}

(10)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}_{B}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{B}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}_{B}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{B}^{\top}

(11)

be the eigen-decompositions
of the two matrices with
n
eigenvalues
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}_{A}=\mbox{diag}[\alpha_{i}]
and

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}_{B}=\mbox{diag}[\beta_{i}]
and
n
orthonormal eigenvectors, the
column vectors of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{A}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{B}
.

3.1
An Exact Spectral Solution

If there exists a vertex-to-vertex correspondence that makes (
8
) equal to
0
, we have:

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}_{A}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}^{\star}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}_{B}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}^{\star}}^{\top}.

(12)

This implies that the adjacency matrices of the two graphs should have
the same eigenvalues. Moreover, if the eigenvalues are non null and,
the matrices
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{A}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{B}
have full rank and are
uniquely defined by their
n
orthonormal column vectors (which are
the eigenvectors of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}_{A}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}_{B}
), then

\alpha_{i}=\beta_{i},\forall i,\;1\leq i\leq n
and

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}_{A}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}_{B}
. From (
12
)
and using the eigen-decompositions of the two graph matrices we obtain:

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}_{A}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}^{\star}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\breve{U}}}_{B}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}_{B}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\breve{U}}}_{B}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}^{\star}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{A}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}_{B},

(13)

where the matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\breve{U}}}_{B}
is defined by:

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\breve{U}}}_{B}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{B}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}.

(14)

Matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}=\mbox{diag}[s_{i}]
, with
s_{i}=\pm 1
, is referred to
as a sign matrix with the property
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}^{2}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{I}}
. Post
multiplication of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{B}
with a sign matrix
takes into account the fact that the eigenvectors (the column vectors
of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{B}
) are only defined up to a sign.
Finally we obtain the following permutation matrix:

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}^{\star}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{B}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{A}^{\top}.

(15)

Therefore, one may notice that there are as many solutions as the cardinality
of the set of matrices
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{n}
, i.e.,
|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{n}|=2^{n}
, and that
not all of these solutions
correspond to a permutation matrix
.
This means that there exist some matrices
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}^{\star}
that exactly make

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}^{\star}
a permutation matrix. Hence, all those permutation
matrices that satisfy (
15
) are solutions of the exact
graph isomorphism problem.
Notice that once the permutation has been estimated, one can write that the rows of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{B}
can be
aligned with the rows of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{A}
:

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{A}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}^{\star}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{B}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}^{\star}.

(16)

The rows of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{A}
and of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{B}
can be interpreted as
isometric embeddings of the two graph vertices: A vertex
v_{i}
of

\mathscr{G}_{A}
has as
coordinates the
i^{\text{th}}
row of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{A}
. This means that the
spectral graph
isomorphism problem becomes a point registration problem, where graph
vertices are represented by points in
\mathbbm{R}^{n}
.
To conclude, the exact graph isomorphism problem has a spectral
solution based on: (i) the eigen-decomposition of the two graph matrices, (ii) the
ordering of their eigenvalues, and (iii) the choice of a sign for
each eigenvector.

3.2
The Hoffman-Wielandt Theorem

The Hoffman-Wielandt theorem
[
44
,
45
]
is
the fundamental building block of spectral graph isomorphism. The
theorem holds for normal matrices; Here, we restrict the analysis to real symmetric
matrices, although the generalization to Hermitian matrices is
straightforward:

Theorem 1

(Hoffman and Wielandt)
If
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}_{A}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}_{B}
are real-symmetric matrices, and
if
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{i}

are their eigenvalues arranged in increasing order,
\alpha_{1}\leq\ldots\leq\alpha_{i}\leq\ldots\leq\alpha_{n}
and
\beta_{1}\leq\ldots\leq\beta_{i}\leq\ldots\leq\beta_{n}
,
then

\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\alpha_{i}-\beta_{i})^{2}\leq\|{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}_{A}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}_{B}}\|_{F}^{2}.

(17)

Proof:

The proof is derived from
[
11
,
46
]
. Consider
the eigen-decompositions of matrices
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}_{A}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}_{B}
, (
10
), (
11
).
Notice that for the time being we are free to prescribe the ordering of the eigenvalues

\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{i}
and hence the ordering of the column vectors
of matrices
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{A}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{B}
. By combining (
10
) and (
11
) we write:

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{A}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}}}_{A}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{A}^{\top}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{B}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}}}_{B}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{B}^{\top}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}_{A}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}_{B}

(18)

or, equivalently:

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}}}_{A}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{B}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{B}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}}}_{B}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{A}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}_{A}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}_{B})\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{B}.

(19)

By the unitary-invariance of the Frobenius norm (see appendix
B
) and with the notation
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{B}
we obtain:

\|{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}}}_{A}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}}}_{B}}\|_{F}^{2}=\|{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}_{A}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}_{B}}\|_{F}^{2},

(20)

which is equivalent to:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\alpha_{i}-\beta_{j})^{2}z_{ij}^{2}=\|{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}_{A}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}_{B}}\|_{F}^{2}.

(21)

The coefficients
x_{ij}=z_{ij}^{2}
can be viewed as the entries of
a doubly-stochastic matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}
:
x_{ij}\geq 0,\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{ij}=1,\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{ij}=1
. Using these
properties, we obtain:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\alpha_{i}-\beta_{j})^{2}z_{ij}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\beta_{j}^{2}-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}z_{ij}^{2}\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}

(22)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\beta_{j}^{2}-2\max_{Z}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}z_{ij}^{2}\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}\right\}.

Hence, the minimization of (
21
) is
equivalent to the maximization of the last term in
(
22
). We can modify our maximization problem to
admit all the doubly-stochastic matrices. In this way we seek an
extremum over a convex compact set. The maximum over this compact set
is larger than or equal to our maximum:

\max_{Z\in\mathcal{O}_{n}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}z_{ij}^{2}\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}\right\}\leq\max_{X\in\mathcal{D}_{n}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{ij}\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}\right\}

(23)

where
\mathcal{O}_{n}
is the set of orthogonal matrices and

\mathcal{D}_{n}
is the set of doubly stochastic matrices (see appendix
A
).
Let
c_{ij}=\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}
and hence one can write that the right
term in the equation above as the dot-product of two matrices:

\langle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}\rangle=\mbox{tr}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{ij}c_{ij}.

(24)

Figure 2:
This figure illustrates the maximization of the dot-product

\langle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}\rangle
. The two matrices can be viewed
as vectors of dimension
n^{2}
. Matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}
belongs to a
compact convex set whose extreme points are the permutation
matrices
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}_{1},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}_{2},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}_{n}
. Therefore, the
projection of this set (i.e.,

) onto

has projected permutation matrices at its extremes, namely

and

Therefore, this expression can be interpreted as the projection of

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}
onto
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}

The Birkhoff theorem (appendix

We obtain that the extrema (minimum and maximum) of the projection of

occur at the projections of one of the extreme points of this convex set, which correspond to permutation
matrices. Hence, the maximum of

By substitution in (

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\alpha_{i}-\beta_{j})^{2}z_{ij}^{2}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\alpha_{i}-\beta_{\pi(i)})^{2}.

(26)

If the eigenvalues are in increasing order then

. Indeed, let’s assume that for some indices

and hence (

3.3
Umeyama’s Method

The exact spectral matching solution presented in section

Umeyama

addresses the problem of

for

and the second for

directed weighted graphs
. The adjacency matrix is used in
both cases. Let’s consider the case of undirected graphs.
The eigenvalues are (possibly with multiplicities):

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}_{A}:

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}\leq\ldots\leq\alpha_{i}\leq\ldots\leq\alpha_{n}

(30)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}_{B}:

\displaystyle\beta_{1}\leq\ldots\leq\beta_{i}\leq\ldots\leq\beta_{n}.

(31)

Theorem 2

(Umeyama)
If
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}_{A}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}_{B}
are real-symmetric matrices with
n
distinct eigenvalues (that can be ordered),

\alpha_{1}<\ldots<\alpha_{i}<\ldots<\alpha_{n}
and
\beta_{1}<\ldots<\beta_{i}<\ldots<\beta_{n}
, the minimum of :

J(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Q}})=\|{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}_{A}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Q}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}_{B}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Q}}^{\top}}\|_{F}^{2}

(32)

is achieved for:

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Q}}^{\star}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{A}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{B}^{\top}

(33)

and hence (
29
) becomes an equality:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\alpha_{i}-\beta_{i})^{2}=\|{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}_{A}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Q}}^{\star}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}_{B}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Q}}^{\star}}^{\top}}\|_{F}^{2}.

(34)

Proof:

The proof is straightforward. By corollary
1.2
, the Hoffman-Wielandt theorem applies to matrices
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}_{A}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Q}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}_{B}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Q}}}^{\top}
. By corollary
1.1
, the equality (
34
) is achieved for:

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Q}}^{\star}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{B}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}

(35)

and hence (
33
) holds.
\quad\blacksquare

Notice that (
33
) can be written as:

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{A}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Q}}^{\star}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{B}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}

(36)

which is a
relaxed
version of
(
16
): The permutation matrix in the exact
isomorphism case is replaced by an orthogonal matrix.

A Heuristic for Spectral Graph Matching:

Let us consider again the exact solution outlined in
section
3.1
. Umeyama suggests a heuristic
in order to avoid exhaustive search over all possible
2^{n}
matrices
that satisfy (
15
). One may easily notice that:

\|{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{A}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{B}^{\top}}\|_{F}^{2}=2n-2\mbox{tr}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{A}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{B})^{\top}).

(37)

Using Umeyama’s notations,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\bar{U}}}_{A}=[|u_{ij}|],\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\bar{U}}}_{B}=[|v_{ij}|]
(the entries of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\bar{U}}}_{A}
are the absolute values of the entries of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{A}
), one may further notice that:

\mbox{tr}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{A}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{B})^{\top})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}s_{j}u_{ij}v_{\pi(i)j}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|u_{ij}||v_{\pi(i)j}|=\mbox{tr}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\bar{U}}}_{A}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\bar{U}}}_{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}^{\top}).

(38)

The minimization of (
37
) is equivalent to the
maximization of (
38
) and the maximal value
that can be attained by the latter is
n
. Using the fact that both

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{A}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{B}
are orthogonal matrices, one can easily conclude
that:

\mbox{tr}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\bar{U}}}_{A}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\bar{U}}}_{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}^{\top})\leq n.

(39)

Umeyama concludes that when the two graphs are isomorphic, the optimum
permutation matrix maximizes

\mbox{tr}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\bar{U}}}_{A}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\bar{U}}}_{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}^{\top})
and this can be solved by the
Hungarian algorithm
[
47
]
.

When the two graphs are not exactly isomorphic,
theorem
1
and
theorem
2
allow us to relax the permutation matrices
to the group of orthogonal matrices. Therefore with similar arguments
as above we obtain:

\mbox{tr}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{A}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Q}}^{\top})\leq\mbox{tr}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\bar{U}}}_{A}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\bar{U}}}_{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Q}}^{\top})\leq n.

(40)

The permutation matrix obtained with the Hungarian algorithm can be
used as an initial solution that can then be improved by some
hill-climbing or relaxation technique

10
]
.

The spectral matching solution presented in this section is not directly applicable to large graphs.
In the next section we introduce the notion of dimensionality reduction for graphs which will lead
to a tractable graph matching solution.

4
Graph Embedding and Dimensionality Reduction

For
large
and
sparse
graphs, the results of
section
3
and Umeyama’s method (section
3.3
) hold
only
weakly
. Indeed, one cannot guarantee that all the eigenvalues
have multiplicity equal to one:
the presence of symmetries causes some of
eigenvalues to have an algebraic multiplicity greater than
one. Under these circumstances and due to numerical approximations,
it might not be possible to properly order the
eigenvalues. Moreover, for very large graphs with thousands of
vertices it is not practical to compute all its eigenvalue-eigenvector
pairs. This means that one has to devise a method that is able to
match shapes using a small set of eigenvalues and eigenvectors.

One elegant way to overcome this problem, is to reduce
the dimension of the eigenspace, along the line of spectral
dimensionality reductions techniques. The eigendecomposition
of graph Laplacian matrices (introduced in section
2.1
)
is a popular choice for the purpose of dimensionality reduction
[
39
]
.

4.2
Principal Component Analysis of a Graph Embedding

The Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse of the Laplacian can be written as:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}^{{\dagger}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}^{\top}

(49)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}^{\top})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}^{\top})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}

where
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}^{-1}=\mbox{diag}(0,1/\lambda_{2},\ldots,1/\lambda_{n})
.

The symmetric semi-definite positive matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}^{{\dagger}}
is a
Gram
matrix
with the same eigenvectors as those of the graph Laplacian. When omitting the null eigenvalue and associated constant eigenvector,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}
becomes
a
(n-1)\times n
matrix whose columns are the coordinates of the
graph’s vertices in an
embedded (or feature) space
, i.e.,

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}=[\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{1}\ldots\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{j}\ldots\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{n}]
. It is interesting to note that
the entries of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}^{{\dagger}}
may be viewed as
kernel

dot-products, or a Gram matrix
[
48
]
.
The Gram-matrix representation allows us to embed the graph in an Euclidean feature-space
where each vertex
v_{j}
of the graph is a feature point represented as
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{j}
.

The left pseudo-inverse operator of the Laplacian
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}
, satisfying
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}^{{\dagger}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}

for any
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}\bot\mathrm{null}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}})
, is also called the
Green function
of the heat equation.
Under the assumption that the graph is connected and thus
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}
has an
eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}=0
with multiplicity 1, we obtain:

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}^{{\dagger}}=\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}_{k}^{\top}.

(50)

The Green function is
intimately related to random walks on graphs, and can be interpreted probabilistically as follows.
Given a Markov chain such that each graph vertex is the state, and the transition
from vertex
v_{i}
is possible to any adjacent
vertex
v_{j}\sim v_{i}
with probability
w_{ij}/d_{i}
, the expected
number of steps required to reach vertex
v_{j}
from
v_{i}
, called the

access
or
hitting time

O(v_{i},v_{j})
.
The expected number of steps in a round trip from
v_{i}
to
v_{j}
is
called the
commute-time distance
:
\mbox{CTD}^{2}(v_{i},v_{j})=O(v_{i},v_{j})+O(v_{j},v_{i})
.
The commute-time distance
[
49
]
can be expressed in terms of the entries of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}^{{\dagger}}
:

\displaystyle\mbox{CTD}^{2}(v_{i},v_{j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{Vol}(\mathscr{G})(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}^{{\dagger}}(i,i)+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}^{{\dagger}}(j,j)-2\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}^{{\dagger}}(i,j))

(51)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{Vol}(\mathscr{G})\left(\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}_{ik}^{2}+\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}_{jk}^{2}-2\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}_{ik}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}_{jk}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{Vol}(\mathscr{G})\sum_{k=2}^{n}\left(\lambda_{k}^{-1/2}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}_{ik}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}_{jk})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{Vol}(\mathscr{G})\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{j}\|^{2},

where the volume of the graph,
\mbox{Vol}(\mathscr{G})
is the sum of the degrees of
all the graph vertices.
The
CTD
function is positive-definite and sub-additive, thus defining
a
metric
between the graph vertices, referred to as

commute-time
(or
resistance
)
distance

[
50
]
.
The CTD is inversely related to the number and length of paths connecting two vertices.
Unlike the shortest-path (geodesic) distance, CTD
captures the connectivity structure of the graph volume rather
than a single path between the two vertices. The great advantage of
the commute-time distance over the shortest geodesic path is that it
is robust to topological changes and therefore is well suited for
characterizing complex shapes.
Since the volume is a graph constant, we obtain:

\mbox{CTD}^{2}(v_{i},v_{j})\propto\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{j}\|^{2}.

(52)

Hence, the Euclidean distance between any two feature points
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{j}
is the commute time distance
between the graph vertex
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
.

Using the first

4.3
Choosing the Dimension of the Embedding

A direct consequence of theorem
3

is that the embedded graph representation is centered and the eigenvectors of the
combinatorial Laplacian are the directions of maximum variance. The
principal

eigenvectors correspond to the eigenvectors associated with the
K

largest
eigenvalues of the
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}^{{\dagger}}
, i.e.,

\lambda_{2}^{-1}\geq\lambda_{3}^{-1}\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{K}^{-1}
. The variance along
vector
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}_{k}
is
\lambda_{k}^{-1}/n
. Therefore, the total
variance can be computed from the trace of the
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}^{{\dagger}}
matrix :

\mbox{tr}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Sigma}}_{X})=\frac{1}{n}\mbox{tr}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}^{{\dagger}}).

(57)

A standard way of choosing the principal components is to use the

scree diagram
:

\theta(K)=\frac{\sum_{k=2}^{K+1}\lambda_{k}^{-1}}{\sum_{k=2}^{n}\lambda_{k}^{-1}}.

(58)

The selection of the first
K
principal eigenvectors therefore
depends on the spectral fall-off of the inverses of the eigenvalues.
In spectral graph theory, the dimension
K
is chosen on the basis of the existence of an
eigengap, such that
\lambda_{K+2}-\lambda_{K+1}>t
with
t>0
.
In practice it is extremely difficult to find such an eigengap, in
particular in the case of sparse graphs that correspond to a
discretized manifold. Instead, we propose to select the dimension of
the embedding in the following way. Notice that
(
58
) can be written as
\theta(K)=A/(A+B)
with

A=\sum_{k=2}^{K+1}\lambda_{k}^{-1}
and
B=\sum_{k=K+2}^{n}\lambda_{k}^{-1}
. Moreover, from the fact that the
\lambda_{k}
’s are
arranged in increasing order, we obtain
B\leq(n-K-1)\lambda_{K+1}^{-1}
. Hence:

\theta_{\min}\leq\theta(K)\leq 1,

(59)

with

\theta_{\min}=\frac{\sum_{k=2}^{K+1}\lambda_{k}^{-1}}{\sum_{k=2}^{K}\lambda_{k}^{-1}+(n-K)\lambda_{K+1}^{-1}}.

(60)

This lower bound can be computed from the
K
smallest non null eigenvalues
of the combinatorial Laplacian matrix. Hence, one can choose
K
such
that the sum of the first
K
eigenvalues of the
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}^{{\dagger}}
matrix is
a good approximation of the total variance, e.g.,

\theta_{\min}=0.95
.

4.4
Unit Hyper-sphere Normalization

One disadvantage of the standard embeddings is that,
when two shapes have large difference in sampling the embeddings
will differ by a significant scale factor.
In order to avoid this we can

normalize
the embedding such that the
vertex coordinates lie on a unit sphere of dimension

, which yields:

\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}_{i}=\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i}}{\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i}\|}.

(61)

In more detail, the

writes as:

\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}_{ik}=\frac{\lambda_{k}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}_{ik}}{\left(\sum_{l=2}^{K+1}\lambda_{l}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}_{il}^{2}\right)^{1/2}}.

(62)

A

given by
(
61
), i.e.,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}_{A}=[\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}_{1}\ldots\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}_{j}\ldots\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}_{n}]
(as well as

\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}_{B}=[\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}^{\prime}_{1}\ldots\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}^{\prime}_{j}\ldots\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}^{\prime}_{m}]
). We recall that each column
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}_{j}

(and respectively
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}^{\prime}_{j}
) is a
K
-dimensional vector
corresponding to a vertex
v_{j}
of
\mathscr{G}_{A}
(and respectively
v^{\prime}_{j}
of
\mathscr{G}_{B}
).

5.1
Maximum Subgraph Matching and Point Registration

Let’s apply the graph isomorphism framework of
Section
3
to the two
graphs. They are embedded into two congruent spaces of dimension

\mathbbm{R}^{K}
. If the smallest
K
non-null eigenvalues associated with the
two embeddings are distinct and can be ordered, i.e.:

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}<\ldots<\lambda_{k}<\ldots<\lambda_{K+1}

(65)

\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime}_{2}<\ldots<\lambda^{\prime}_{k}<\ldots<\lambda^{\prime}_{K+1}

(66)

then, the Umeyama method
could be applied. If one uses the unnormalized Laplacian embeddings
just defined, (
33
)
becomes:

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Q}}^{\star}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{n\times K}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{K}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}^{\prime}_{m\times K})^{\top}

(67)

Notice that here the sign matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}
defined in
33
became a
K\times K
matrix denoted by
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{K}
. We now assume that the eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{K+1}\}
and
\{\lambda^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,\lambda^{\prime}_{K+1}\}

cannot be reliably ordered
. This can be modeled by multiplication with a
K\times K
permutation matrix

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}_{K}
:

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Q}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{n\times K}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{K}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}_{K}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}^{\prime}_{m\times K})^{\top}

(68)

Pre-multiplication of
(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}^{\prime}_{m\times K})^{\top}
with
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}_{K}
permutes its rows such that
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}^{\prime}_{k}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}^{\prime}_{\pi(k)}
. Each entry
q_{ij}
of the
n\times m
matrix

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Q}}
can therefore be written as:

q_{ij}=\sum_{k=2}^{K+1}s_{k}u_{ik}u^{\prime}_{j\pi(k)}

(69)

Since both
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}_{n\times K}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}^{\prime}_{m\times K}
are column-orthonormal matrices, the
dot-product defined by
(
69
) is equivalent to the cosine of
the angle between two
K
-dimensional vectors. This means that each entry of

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Q}}
is such that
-1\leq q_{ij}\leq+1
and that two vertices

v_{i}
and
v^{\prime}_{j}
are matched if
q_{ij}
is
close to 1.

One can also use the normalized
commute-time coordinates and define an equivalent expression as above:

\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Q}}}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{K}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}_{K}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}^{\prime}

(70)

with:

\hat{q}_{ij}=\sum_{k=2}^{K+1}s_{k}\hat{x}_{ik}\hat{x}^{\prime}_{j\pi(k)}

(71)

Because both sets of points
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}^{\prime}
lie on a
K
-dimensional unit hyper-sphere, we also have

-1\leq\hat{q_{ij}}\leq+1
.

It should however be emphasized that the rank of the
n\times m

matrices
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Q}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Q}}}
is equal to
K
. Therefore, these
matrices cannot be viewed as
relaxed permutation matrices

between the two graphs. In fact they define many-to-many
correspondences between the vertices of the first graph and the
vertices of the second graph, this being due to the fact that the
graphs are embedded on a low-dimensional space. This is one of the
main differences between our method proposed in the next section and the Umeyama method, as well as
many other subsequent methods, that use all eigenvectors of the graph.
As it will be explained below, our formulation leads to a shape
matching method that will alternate between aligning their eigenbases
and finding a vertex-to-vertex assignment.

It is possible to extract a one-to-one assignment matrix
from
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Q}}
(or from
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Q}}}
) using either
dynamic programming or an assignment method technique such as the
Hungarian algorithm. Notice that this assignment is conditioned by the
choice of a sign matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{K}
and of a permutation matrix

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}_{K}
, i.e.,
2^{K}K!
possibilities, and that not all these
choices correspond to a valid sub-isomorphism between the two
graphs. Let’s consider the case of the normalized commute-time
embedding; there is an equivalent formulation for the unnormalized
Laplacian embedding. The two graphs are described by two sets of
points,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}^{\prime}
, both lying onto the

K
-dimensional unity hyper-sphere. The
K\times K
matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{K}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}_{K}
transforms one graph embedding onto the other graph
embedding. Hence, one can write
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{K}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}_{K}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}^{\prime}_{j}
if vertex
v_{i}
matches
v_{j}
. More generally
Let
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}_{K}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{K}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}_{K}
and let’s extend
the domain of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}_{K}
to all possible orthogonal matrices of size
K\times K
, namely
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}_{K}\in\mathcal{O}_{K}
or the
orthogonal group
of dimension
K
. We can now write the following criterion whose minimization over
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}_{K}
guarantees an optimal solution for registering the vertices of the first graph with the vertices of the second graph:

\min_{R_{K}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\hat{q}_{ij}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}_{K}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}_{j}^{\prime}\|^{2}

(72)

One way to solve minimization problems such as (
72
) is to use a point registration algorithm that alternates
between (i) estimating the
K\times K
orthogonal transformation
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}_{K}
,
which aligns the
K
-dimensional coordinates associated with
the two embeddings, and (ii) updating the assignment variables
\hat{q}_{ij}
. This can be done using either ICP-like methods (the
\hat{q}_{ij}
’s are binary variables), or EM-like methods (the
\hat{q}_{ij}
’s are posterior probabilities of assignment variables).
As we just outlined above, matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}_{K}
belongs to the orthogonal group
\mathcal{O}_{K}
.
Therefore this framework differs from standard implementations of ICP and EM algorithms that usually estimate a 2-D or 3-D
rotation
matrix which belong to the
special orthogonal group
.

It is well established that ICP algorithms are easily trapped in local minima. The EM algorithm recently proposed in
[
38
]
is able to converge to a good solution starting with a rough initial guess and is robust to the presence of outliers. Nevertheless, the algorithm proposed in
[
38
]
performs well under
rigid transformations
(rotation and translation), whereas in our case we have to estimate a more general orthogonal transformation that incorporates both rotations and reflections. Therefore, before describing in detail an EM algorithm well suited for solving the problem at hand, we discuss the issue of estimating an initialization for the transformation aligning the
K
eigenvectors of the first embedding with those of the second embedding and we propose a practical method for initializing this transformation (namely, matrices
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{K}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}_{K}
in (
70
)) based on comparing the histograms of these eigenvectors, or
eigensignatures
.

5.2
Aligning Two Embeddings Based on Eigensignatures

Both the unnormalized Laplacian embedding and the normalized commute-time embedding of a graph are represented in a metric space spanned by the eigenvectors of the Laplacian matrix, namely the n-dimensional vectors
\{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}_{2},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}_{k},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}_{K+1}\}
, where
n
is the number of graph vertices. They correspond to
eigenfunctions
and each such eigenfunction maps the graph’s vertices onto the real line. More precisely, the
k
-th eigenfunction maps a vertex
v_{i}
onto
u_{ik}
.
Propositions
1
and
3
revealed interesting statistics of the sets
\{u_{1k},\ldots,u_{ik},\ldots,u_{nk}\}_{k=2}^{K+1}
. Moreover, theorem
3
provided an interpretation of the eigenvectors in terms of principal directions of the embedded shape. One can therefore conclude that the probability distribution of the components of an eigenvector have interesting properties that make them suitable for comparing two shapes, namely
-1<u_{ik}<+1
,
\overline{u}_{k}=1/n\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{ik}=0
, and
\sigma_{k}=1/n\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{ik}^{2}=1/n
. This means that one can build a histogram for each eigenvector and that all these histograms share the same bin width
w
and the same number of bins
b

[
51
]
:

\displaystyle w_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3.5\sigma_{k}}{n^{1/3}}=\frac{3.5}{n^{4/3}}

(73)

\displaystyle b_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sup_{i}u_{ik}-\inf_{i}u_{ik}}{w_{k}}\approx\frac{n^{4/3}}{2}.

(74)

We claim that these histograms are eigenvector signatures which are invariant under graph isomorphism. Indeed, let’s consider the Laplacian
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}
of a shape and we apply the isomorphic transformation
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}^{\top}
to this shape, where
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}
is a permutation matrix. If
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}
is an eigenvector of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}
, it follows that
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}
is an eigenvector of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}^{\top}
and therefore, while the order of the components of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}
are affected by this transformation, their frequency and hence their probability distribution remain the same. Hence, one may conclude that such a histogram may well be viewed as an
eigensignature
.

We denote with
H\{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}\}
the histogram formed with the components
of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}
and let
C(H\{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}\},H\{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}^{\prime}\})
be a similarity
measure between two histograms. From the eigenvector properties just
outlined, it is straightforward to notice that
H\{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}\}\neq H\{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{-u}}\}
: These two histograms are mirror symmetric. Hence, the histogram is not invariant to the sign of an eigenvector.
Therefore one can use the eigenvectors’ histograms to estimate both the permutation matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}_{K}
and the sign matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{K}
in (
70
).
The problem of finding one-to-one assignments
\{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}_{k}\leftrightarrow s_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}^{\prime}_{\pi(k)}\}_{k=2}^{K+1}
between the two sets of eigenvectors associated with the two shapes is therefore equivalent to the problem of finding one-to-one assignments between their histograms.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}_{K}
be an assignment matrix between the histograms of the first shape and the histograms of the second shape. Each entry of this matrix is defined by:

a_{kl}=\sup[C(H\{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}_{k}\},H\{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}_{l}^{\prime}\});C(H\{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}_{k}\},H\{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{-u}}_{l}^{\prime}\})]

(75)

Similarly, we define a matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}_{K}
that accounts for the
sign assignments
:

b_{kl}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}+1&\mbox{if}&C(H\{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}_{k}\},H\{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}_{l}^{\prime}\})\geq C(H\{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}_{k}\},H\{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{-u}}_{l}^{\prime}\})\\
-1&\mbox{if}&C(H\{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}_{k}\},H\{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}_{l}^{\prime}\})<C(H\{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}_{k}\},H\{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{-u}}_{l}^{\prime}\})\end{array}\right.

(76)

Extracting a permutation matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}_{K}
from
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}_{K}
is an
instance of the bipartite maximum matching problem and the Hungarian
algorithm is known to provide an optimal solution to this assignment
problem
[
47
]
. Moreover, one can use the estimated

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}_{K}
to extract a sign matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{K}
from

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}_{K}
. Algorithm
1
estimates an alignment between two
embeddings.

.

5.3
An EM Algorithm for Shape Matching

As explained in section
5.1
, the maximum subgraph matching problem
reduces to a point registration problem in
K
dimensional metric space spanned by the eigenvectors of graph Laplacian
where two shapes are represented as point clouds.
The initial alignment of Laplacian embeddings can be obtained by matching the histogram of eigenvectors as
described in the previous section.
In this section we propose an EM algorithm for 3D shape matching that computes a probabilistic
vertex-to-vertex assignment between two shapes. The proposed method alternates between the step to estimate
an orthogonal transformation matrix associated with the alignment of the two shape embeddings and the step
to compute a point-to-point probabilistic assignment variable.

The method is based on a parametric probabilistic model, namely maximum likelihood with missing data. Let
us consider the Laplacian embedding of two shapes, i.e., (
53
)
:
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}=\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}^{\prime}=\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}^{\prime}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
, with

\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}^{\prime}\subset\mathbbm{R}^{K}
. Without loss of generality, we
assume that the points in the first set,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}
are cluster centers
of a Gaussian mixture model (GMM) with
n
clusters and an additional
uniform component that accounts for outliers and unmatched data. The
matching
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}\leftrightarrow\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}^{\prime}
will consist in fitting the
Gaussian mixture to the set
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}^{\prime}
.

Let this Gaussian mixture undergo a
K\times K

transformation
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}
(for simplicity, we omit the index
K
) with
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{I}}_{K},\det(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}})=\pm 1
, more precisely
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}\in\mathcal{O}_{K}
, the group of orthogonal
matrices acting on
\mathbbm{R}^{K}
. Hence, each cluster in the mixture
is parametrized by a prior
p_{i}
, a cluster mean

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\mu}}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}_{i}
, and a covariance matrix

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Sigma}}_{i}
. It will be assumed that all the clusters in the
mixture have the same priors,
\{p_{i}=\pi_{\text{in}}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, and the same
isotropic covariance matrix,

\{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Sigma}}_{i}=\sigma\boldsymbol{\mathsf{I}}_{K}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. This
parametrization leads to the following
observed-data
log-likelihood
(with
\pi_{\text{out}}=1-n\pi_{\text{in}}
and
\mathcal{U}
is
the uniform distribution):

\log P(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}^{\prime})=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\log\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{\text{in}}\mathcal{N}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}^{\prime}_{j}|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\mu}}_{i},\sigma)+\pi_{\text{out}}\mathcal{U}\right)

(77)

It is well known that the direct maximization of (
77
) is
not tractable and it is more practical to maximize the

expected complete-data log-likelihood
using the EM
algorithm, where “complete-data” refers to both the observed data
(the points
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}^{\prime}
) and the missing data (the data-to-cluster
assignments). In our case, the above expectation writes (see

[
38
]
for details):

\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}},\sigma)=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{ji}(\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}^{\prime}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}_{i}\|^{2}+k\log\sigma),

(78)

where
\alpha_{ji}
denotes the posterior probability of an assignment:

\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}^{\prime}_{j}\leftrightarrow\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}_{i}
:

\alpha_{ji}=\frac{\exp(-\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}^{\prime}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}_{i}\|^{2}/2\sigma)}{\sum_{q=1}^{n}\exp(-\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}^{\prime}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}_{q}\|^{2}/2\sigma)+\emptyset\sigma^{k/2}},

(79)

where
\emptyset
is a constant term associated with the uniform
distribution
\mathcal{U}
. Notice that one easily obtains the
posterior probability of a data point to remain unmatched,

\alpha_{jn+1}=1-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{ij}
. This leads to the shape matching
procedure outlined in Algorithm
2
.

Algorithm 2

EM for shape matching

0:

: Two embedded shapes
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}^{\prime}
;

0:

: Dense correspondences
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}\leftrightarrow\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}^{\prime}
between
the two shapes;

1:

Initialization:
Set
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}^{(0)}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{K}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}_{K}
choose a large value for the variance
\sigma^{(0)}
;

2:

E-step:
Compute the current posteriors
\alpha_{ij}^{(q)}
from the current parameters
using (
79
);

3:

M-step:
Compute the new transformation
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}^{(q+1)}
and the new variance
\sigma^{(q+1)}
using the current posteriors:

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}^{(q+1)}=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}}\sum_{i,j}\alpha_{ij}^{(q)}\|\mbox{{\boldmath$x$}}^{\prime}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}\mbox{{\boldmath$x$}}_{i}\|^{2}

Accept the assignment
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}^{\prime}_{j}\leftrightarrow\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}_{i}

if
\max_{i}\alpha_{ij}^{(q)}>0.5
.

7
Discussion

This chapter describes a 3D shape registration approach
that computes dense correspondences between two articulated objects.
We address the problem using spectral matching and unsupervised point registration method.
We formally introduce graph isomorphism using the Laplacian
matrix, and we provide an analysis of the matching problem
when the number of nodes in the graph is very large,
i.e.
of the
order of
O(10^{4})
. We show that there is a simple equivalence between
graph isomorphism and point registration under the
group of orthogonal transformations, when the dimension
of the embedding space is much smaller than the cardinality
of the point-sets.

The eigenvalues of a large sparse Laplacian cannot be reliably
ordered. We propose an elegant alternative to eigenvalue
ordering, using eigenvector histograms and alignment
based on comparing these histograms. The point registration
that results from eigenvector alignment yields an
excellent initialization for the EM algorithm, subsequently
used only to refine the registration.

However, the method is susceptible to large topology changes that
might occur in the multi-camera shape acquisition setup due to self-occlusion
(originated from complex kinematics poses) and shadow effects.
This is because Laplacian embedding is a global representation and
any major topology change will lead to large changes in embeddings causing
failure of this method. Recently, a new shape registration method proposed in
[
36
]

provides robustness to the large topological changes using the heat kernel framework.

Appendix A
Permutation and Doubly-stochastic Matrices

A matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}
is called a
permutation
matrix if exactly one
entry in each row and column is equal to
1
, and all other entries
are
0
. Left multiplication of a matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}
by a permutation
matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}
permutes the
rows
of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}
, while right
multiplication permutes the
columns
of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}
.

Permutation matrices have the following properties:
\det(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}})=\pm 1
,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}^{\top}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}^{-1}
, the identity is a permutation matrix,
and the product of two permutation
matrices is a permutation matrix. Hence the set of permutation
matrices
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
constitute a subgroup of the
subgroup
of orthogonal matrices, denoted by
\mathcal{O}_{n}
, and

\mathcal{P}_{n}
has finite cardinality
n!
.

A non-negative matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}
is a matrix such that all its entries
are non-negative. A non-negative matrix
with the property that all its row sums are
+1
is said to be a

(row) stochastic matrix
. A
column stochastic matrix

is the transpose of a row stochastic matrix. A stochastic matrix

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}
with the property that
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}^{\top}
is also stochastic is said
to be
doubly stochastic
: all row and column sums are
+1
and

a_{ij}\geq 0
.
The set of stochastic matrices is a compact convex set with the simple
and important property that
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}
is stochastic if and only if

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}\mathbbm{1}=\mathbbm{1}
where
\mathbbm{1}
is the vector with all components
equal to
+1
.

Permutation matrices are doubly stochastic matrices. If we denote by

\mathcal{D}_{n}
the set of doubly stochastic
matrices, it can be proved that
\mathcal{P}_{n}=\mathcal{O}_{n}\cap\mathcal{D}_{n}

[
52
]
.
The permutation matrices are the fundamental and prototypical
doubly stochastic matrices, for Birkhoff’s theorem states that any
doubly stochastic matrix is a linear convex combination of finitely many
permutation matrices
[
46
]
:

Theorem 4

(Birkhoff) A matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}
is a doubly stochastic matrix if and only
if for some
N<\infty
there are permutation matrices

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}_{N}
and positive scalars
s_{1},\ldots,s_{N}
such
that
s_{1}+\ldots+s_{N}=1
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}=s_{1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}_{1}+\ldots+s_{N}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}_{N}
.

A complete proof of this theorem is to be found in
[
46
]
[pages
526–528]. The proof relies on the fact that

\mathcal{D}_{n}
is a compact convex set and every point in such a set
is a convex combination of the extreme points of the set. First it is
proved that every permutation matrix is an extreme point of

\mathcal{D}_{n}
and second it is shown that a given matrix
is an extreme point of
\mathcal{D}_{n}
if an only if it is a
permutation matrix.

The Frobenius (or Euclidean) norm of a matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}_{n\times n}
is an
entry-wise
norm
that treats the matrix as a vector of size
1\times nn
. The standard norm properties hold:

\|{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}}\|_{F}>0\Leftrightarrow\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}\neq 0

\|{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}}\|_{F}=0\Leftrightarrow\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}=0

\|{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}\|_{F}\leq\|{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}}\|_{F}+\|{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}\|_{F}

\|{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}\|_{F}\leq\|{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}}\|_{F}\|{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}\|_{F}

(80)

as well as
unitarily-invariant

\|{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{V}}}\|_{F}=\|{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}}\|_{F}.

It immediately follows the following equalities:
