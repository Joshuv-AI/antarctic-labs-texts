Alexandrov-Fenchel inequalities for convex hypersurfaces in the half-space with capillary boundary II
Xinqun Mei, Guofang Wang, Liangjun Weng, Chao Xia

Abstract
. In this paper, we provide an affirmative answer to [ 16 , Conjecture 1.5] on the Alexandrov-Fenchel inequality for quermassintegrals for convex capillary hypersurfaces in the Euclidean half-space. More generally, we establish a theory for capillary convex bodies in the half-space and prove a general Alexandrov-Fenchel inequality for mixed volumes of capillary convex bodies. The conjecture [ 16 , Conjecture 1.5] follows as its consequence.

1.
Introduction

Let
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\,|\,x_{n+1}>0\}
be the upper half-space. Let
\Sigma\subset\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
be a properly embedded, smooth compact hypersurface with boundary in
\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
such that
\text{int}(\Sigma)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}
and
\partial\Sigma\subset\partial\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}.

We call
\Sigma\subset\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
a capillary hypersurface if
\Sigma
intersects
\partial\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}
at a constant contact angle
\theta\in(0,\pi)
.
Let
\widehat{\Sigma}
be the bounded closed region in
\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
enclosed by
\Sigma
and
\partial\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}
. The boundary of
\widehat{\Sigma}
consists of two parts: one is
\Sigma
and the other, which
will be denoted by
\widehat{\partial\Sigma}
, lies on
\partial\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}
.
In
[
16
]
, for a capillary hypersurface
\Sigma\subset\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
, we introduced a new family of geometric functionals as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{0,\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma})\coloneqq|\widehat{\Sigma}|,\qquad\mathcal{V}_{1,\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma})\coloneqq\frac{1}{n+1}\left(|\Sigma|-\cos\theta|\widehat{\partial\Sigma}|\right),

and for
1\leq k\leq n
,

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{k+1,\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma})\coloneqq\frac{1}{n+1}\left(\int_{\Sigma}H_{k}dA-\frac{\cos\theta\sin^{k}\theta}{n}\int_{\partial\Sigma}H_{k-1}^{\partial\Sigma}ds\right),

where
H_{k}
is the normalized
k
-th mean curvature of
\Sigma\subset\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
and
H_{k-1}^{\partial\Sigma}
is the normalized
(k-1)
-th mean curvature of
\partial\Sigma\subset\widehat{\partial\Sigma}\subset\partial\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}
(cf.
[
16
, Section 2.2]
). We use the convention that
H_{0}\equiv 1
and
H_{0}^{\partial\Sigma}\equiv 1
.

\mathcal{V}_{0,\theta}
is the volume of
\widehat{\Sigma}
and
\mathcal{V}_{1,\theta}
is the capillary area of
\Sigma
, up to a multiple constant.
\mathcal{V}_{k,\theta}
(
2\leq k\leq n+1)
are natural capillary counterparts of quermassintegrals for closed hypersurfaces. (For quermassintegrals we refer to
[
11
]
and for capillary hypersurfaces to
[
2
]
and also
[
7
]
.) Especially, as shown in
[
16
, Theorem 2.6]
, the first variation of
\mathcal{V}_{k,\theta}
gives rise to
H_{k}
. Moreover, the following conjecture was proposed in
[
16
, Conjecture 1.5]
.

Conjecture.

Let
\Sigma\subset\overline{{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}_{+}}

(n\geq 2)
be a convex capillary hypersurface with a contact angle
\theta\in(0,{{\pi}})
. Then there holds

(1.2)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{V}_{k,\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma})}{\mathbf{b}_{\theta}}\geq\left(\frac{\mathcal{V}_{l,\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma})}{\mathbf{b}_{\theta}}\right)^{\frac{n+1-k}{n+1-l}},\qquad\forall\penalty\ 0\leq l<k\leq n,

with equality holds if and only if
\Sigma
is a capillary spherical cap.

Here
(n+1)\mathbf{b}_{\theta}
is the capillary area of
\mathcal{C}_{\theta,1}
, the capillary spherical cap with radius
1
. One can check that
\mathbf{b}_{\theta}
is the volume of
\widehat{\mathcal{C}_{\theta,1}}
.
The family of capillary spherical caps lying entirely in
\overline{{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}_{+}}
and intersecting
\partial{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}_{+}
with a constant contact angle
\theta\in(0,\pi)
is given by

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\theta,r}\coloneqq\left\{\xi\in\overline{{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}_{+}}\big|\penalty\ |\xi-r\cos\theta e|=r\right\},\qquad r\in[0,\infty),

which is clearly a portion of a sphere of radius
r
and centered at
r\cos\theta e
. Here we denote by
e\coloneqq-E_{n+1}=(0,\cdots,0,-1)
the unit outward normal of
\partial{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}_{+}\subset\overline{{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}_{+}}
. For simplicity, we denote
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}\coloneqq\mathcal{C}_{\theta,1}
. In the following, we denote
\sigma
and
d\sigma
the round metric and its associated volume form on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
respectively.

For
k=1
and
l=0
, (
1.2
) is just the relative (or capillary) isoperimetric inequality which holds for any hypersurfaces in
\overline{{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}_{+}}
(see e.g.
[
7
, Theorem 19.21]
). Therefore, (
1.2
) are generalizations of the capillary isoperimetric inequality or capillary counterparts of the Alexandrov-Fenchel inequalities for closed convex hypersurfaces
(see e.g.
[
1
]
or
[
11
]
).

In
[
16
]
, we established (
1.2
) for
k=n
and any
0\leq l<n
, in the case
\theta\leq{\pi}/\penalty{2}
, by using a locally constrained inverse curvature flow. Subsequently, Hu-Wei-Yang-Zhou
[
5
]
used a similar locally constrained inverse type curvature flow to establish (
1.2
) for all
0\leq l<k\leq n
, again in the case
\theta\leq{\pi}/\penalty{2}
. The new key ingredient in
[
5
]
is to verify the convexity preservation along the flow via a boundary tensor maximum principle. One can also refer to
[
8
,
10
]
for using various locally constrained curvature-type flows to prove partial results of (
1.2
) for the case
l=0
. The contact angle range restriction
\theta\leq\pi/\penalty 2
is very crucial in the aforementioned papers
in order to obtain
C^{2}
estimates. It actually becomes a challenging problem how to obtain
C^{2}
estimates for those curvature flows in the case
\theta>{\pi}/\penalty 2
.
Recently, Wang-Weng-Xia proved the Minkowski-type inequality, i.e.,
k=2
and
l=1
in (
1.2
), for all
\theta\in(0,\pi)
in
[
15
]
, by assuming only the mean convexity and the star-shapedness of the hypersurface.
In
[
15
]
, the authors studied an inverse mean curvature type flow and explored the specific
divergence structure of the mean curvature to directly obtain Schauder’s estimate for the
radial function for star-shaped hypersurfaces. This method is not easy to be generalized for
k>2
.
Nevertheless, this result makes us believe that (
1.2
) is true for the whole range of
\theta
.
The objective of this paper is to confirm this belief and fully resolve the Conjecture for all
\theta\in(0,\pi)
using a different approach.

In the classical theory of convex bodies, the isoperimetric-type inequalities for quermassintegrals state that for a convex body (a compact convex set with non-empty interior)
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with
C^{2}
boundary, there holds

(1.4)

\displaystyle\frac{W_{k}(K)}{{\bf b}_{n+1}}\geq\left(\frac{W_{l}(K)}{{\bf b}_{n+1}}\right)^{\frac{n+1-k}{n+1-l}},\qquad\forall\penalty\ 0\leq l<k\leq n,

where
{\bf b}_{n+1}=|\mathbb{B}^{n+1}|
, the volume of unit ball
\mathbb{B}^{n+1}
, and
W_{k}(K)
is the
k
-th quermassintegral defined by

W_{k}(K)=\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{\partial K}H_{k-1}dA.

Moreover, equality in (
1.4
) holds if and only if
K
is a round ball. It is in fact a special case of a more general classical Alexandrov-Fenchel inequality for mixed volumes, which says that for a family of convex bodies
K_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
,
i=1,\cdots,n+1
, there holds

(1.5)

\displaystyle V^{2}(K_{1},K_{2},K_{3}\cdots,K_{n+1})\geq V(K_{1},K_{1},K_{3}\cdots,K_{n+1})V(K_{2},K_{2},K_{3}\cdots,K_{n+1}),

where
V(K_{1},K_{2}\cdots,K_{n+1})
is the so-called mixed volume.
When
K_{i},

i=1,\cdots,n+1
, has
C^{2}
boundary and
h_{i}:\mathbb{S}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is the support function of
K_{i}
, then it is known that

V(K_{1},K_{2}\cdots,K_{n+1})=V(h_{1},h_{2}\cdots,h_{n+1})=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}h_{1}Q(A[h_{2}],\cdots,A[h_{n+1}])d\sigma,

where
A[h]=\nabla^{2}h+h\sigma
and
Q
is the so-called mixed discriminant. In this case, equality in (
1.5
) holds if and only if
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
are homothetic. For the Alexandrov-Fenchel inequalities for closed convex hypersurfaces, see e.g.
[
1
, Section 20]
and
[
11
, Chapter 7]
. Recently, Guan-Ma-Trudinger-Zhu established in
[
4
]
a form of Alexandrov-Fenchel inequality for a larger class of closed hypersurfaces,
k^{*}
-convex hypersurfaces.

In this paper, we shall make a systematic study of a special class of convex bodies in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
, that is, capillary convex bodies in
\overline{{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}_{+}}
. By a capillary convex body
\widehat{\Sigma}\subset\overline{{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}_{+}}
, we mean that
\widehat{\Sigma}
is a convex body for a capillary hypersurface
\Sigma
. Denote by
\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
the family of capillary convex bodies in
\overline{{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}_{+}}
.
For
\widehat{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
, let
\nu
be the outer unit normal of
\Sigma
, i.e., the Gauss map of
\Sigma
. We
define the
capillary Gauss map
by
\tilde{\nu}=\nu+\cos\theta e
. It turns out
\tilde{\nu}:\Sigma\to\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
is a diffeomorphism and one can reparametrize
\Sigma
by its inverse on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
. Moreover, there is a one-to-one correspondence between a capillary convex body and a capillary support
function
h\in C^{2}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})
, which is a capillary convex function, that is,
A[h]>0
and

(1.6)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}h=\cot\theta h\penalty\ \penalty\ \hbox{ on }\partial\mathcal{C}_{\theta},

where
\mu
is the outward conormal of
\partial\mathcal{C}_{\theta}\subset\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
.
See Proposition
2.6
below or
[
9
, Section 2]
. Since
\widehat{\Sigma}
is not
C^{2}
at the points in
\partial\Sigma
, the classical support function of
\widehat{\Sigma}
, which is defined on whole
\mathbb{S}^{n}
, is not
C^{2}
. Therefore, we use instead the capillary support function which is only defined on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
and satisfies the boundary condition (
1.6
).
For example, the capillary support function of
\widehat{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}
is given by (see Section
2.1
)

\displaystyle\ell(\xi)=\sin^{2}\theta+\cos\theta\langle\xi,e\rangle,\quad\xi\in\mathcal{C}_{\theta}.

This is the main point of view different from the one in the classical case, where a convex body is determined by a (smooth or non-smooth) support function defined on the whole unit sphere.

For a family of capillary convex bodies
\{\widehat{\Sigma}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}\subset\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
, whose capillary support functions are given by
h_{i}
, the mixed volume

V(\widehat{\Sigma}_{i_{1}},\cdots,\widehat{\Sigma}_{i_{n+1}})
is defined in the classical way.
We will see that the Minkowski sum

K=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\widehat{\Sigma}_{i}
lies in
\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
and its capillary support function is given by
h_{K}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}h_{i}.

Using

(n+1)|K|=\int_{\partial K\cap\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}\langle X,\nu\rangle dA=\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}h_{K}\det(A[h_{K}])d\sigma,

one can check that the mixed volume is given by

\displaystyle V(\widehat{\Sigma}_{i_{1}},\cdots,\widehat{\Sigma}_{i_{n+1}})=V(h_{i_{1}},\cdots,h_{i_{n+1}})\coloneqq\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}h_{i_{1}}Q\left(A[h_{i_{2}}],\cdots,A[h_{i_{n+1}}]\right)d\sigma.

We first observe that the quermassintegrals introduced in
[
16
]
can be reinterpreted as a special case of the above mixed volume. Precisely,

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{n-k+1,\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma})=V(\underbrace{\widehat{\Sigma},\cdots,\widehat{\Sigma}}_{k\penalty\ \rm{copies}},\widehat{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}},\cdots,\widehat{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}})=V(\underbrace{h,\cdots,h}_{k\penalty\ \rm{copies}},\ell,\cdots,\ell),\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ 0\leq k\leq n+1,

where
h
and
\ell
are the capillary support functions of
\widehat{\Sigma}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}
respectively.
By virtue of this observation, the conjectured inequality (
1.2
) follows in fact directly from the classical Alexandrov-Fenchel inequality (
1.5
). It remains to consider the equality case. The characterization of the equality case of the Alexandrov-Fenchel inequality is actually a long-standing open problem, see e.g.
[
11
, Section 7.6]
. Very recently, Shenfeld-van Handel made a big breakthrough in
[
14
]
and classified the extremals of the Alexandrov-Fenchel inequality for convex polytopes. They actually solved the problem where the convex bodies
K_{3},K_{4},\cdots,K_{n+1}
in (
1.5
) are a combination of polytopes, zonoids, and smooth bodies, see
[
14
, Section 14]
. The case we study here is not directly included. In order to study the mixed volume for the class
\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
of capillary convex bodies, we will use capillary support functions defined only on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
. We prove the following capillary version of the Alexandrov-Fenchel inequality with rigidity characterization.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\widehat{\Sigma}_{i}\in\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
for
1\leq i\leq n+1
.
Then

(1.7)

\displaystyle V^{2}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{1},\widehat{\Sigma}_{2},\widehat{\Sigma}_{3},\cdots,\widehat{\Sigma}_{n+1})\geq V(\widehat{\Sigma}_{1},\widehat{\Sigma}_{1},\widehat{\Sigma}_{3},\cdots,\widehat{\Sigma}_{n+1})V(\widehat{\Sigma}_{2},\widehat{\Sigma}_{2},\widehat{\Sigma}_{3},\cdots,\widehat{\Sigma}_{n+1}).

Equality holds if and only if
the capillary support functions
h_{j}
of
\widehat{\Sigma}_{j},j=1,2
, satisfy

h_{1}=ah_{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\langle\cdot,E_{i}\rangle,\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ in }\penalty\ \penalty\ \mathcal{C}_{\theta}

for some constants
a,a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
,
i=1,\cdots,n
, and
\{E_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
the horizontal coordinate unit vectors of
\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
.

As a consequence of Theorem
1.1
, we confirm the above
Conjecture
.

Theorem 1.2
.

For any convex capillary hypersurface
\Sigma\subset\overline{{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}_{+}}
with a contact angle
\theta\in(0,\pi)
, (
1.2
) is true, with equality if and only if
\Sigma
is a spherical cap.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is inspired by a previous paper of Shenfeld-van Handel
[
12
]
, where a new proof of the classical Alexandrov-Fenchel inequality is provided by using its closed relationship with a spectral problem on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
and its associated Bochner formula. For our situation, we establish a similar relationship between the capillary Alexandrov-Fenchel inequality and a spectral problem on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
with a boundary condition. Precisely, the Alexandrov-Fenchel inequality (
3.1
) for the mixed volume of capillary convex bodies can be translated into a sharp lower bound for a spectral problem on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
associated with certain elliptic operator
\mathcal{A}
with a Robin boundary condition, (
3
). For our operator, we proved that it is self-adjoint and the corresponding positive eigenspace has dimension one (cf. (
3
) below), which in turn justifies the validity of the Alexandrov-Fenchel inequality (
3.1
).
The self-adjointness follows from the capillarity, namely, the intersection angle
\theta
is constant.

We also recommend
[
13
]
for a further application of this idea, which provides a new tool for the study of mixed volumes in the theory of convex bodies.

We emphasize that the capillary Gauss map
\tilde{\nu}:\Sigma\to\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
and the capillary support function
h:\mathcal{C}_{\theta}\to\mathbb{R}
play a crucial role in this paper. They were already crucially used in our previous work on studying the capillary Minkowski problem
[
9
]
.

The paper is organized as follows
: In Section
2
, we provide basic properties about the capillary convex body and its associated capillary convex function, the mixed volume of capillary convex bodies and its relation with the quermassintegrals. In Section
3
, we prove Theorem
1.1
on the Alexandrov-Fenchel inequalities for mixed volumes and thus confirm the Conjecture as a special case of Theorem
1.1
.

2.
Capillary convex bodies and mixed volume

2.1.
Capillary convex bodies and capillary convex functions

Definition 2.1
.

For
\theta\in(0,\pi)
, we say that
\Sigma
is a capillary hypersurface (with constant contact angle
\theta
) in
\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
if

(2.1)

\displaystyle\langle\nu,e\rangle=-\cos\theta,\quad\text{along}\penalty\ \partial\Sigma.

We call
\widehat{\Sigma}
a capillary convex body if
\widehat{\Sigma}
is a convex body (a compact convex set with non-empty interior) in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and
\Sigma
is a capillary hypersurface in
\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
.
The class of capillary convex bodies in
\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
is denoted by
\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
.

The simplest capillary convex body is given by
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\theta}\in\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
. Recall that
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
was defined by (
1.3
) above. Next for any
\widehat{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
we give a parametrization of
\Sigma
via the (capillary) Gauss map
\tilde{\nu}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

The Gauss map
\nu:\Sigma\to\mathbb{S}^{n}
has its image in

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{n}_{\theta}\coloneqq\{y\in\mathbb{S}^{n}\,|\,y_{n+1}\geq\cos\theta\},

and
\nu:\Sigma\to\mathbb{S}^{n}_{\theta}
is a diffeomorphism.

Proof.

Since
\Sigma
is a smooth, strictly convex capillary hypersurface, by
[
16
, Corollary 2.5]
, we see that
\partial\Sigma\subset\partial{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
is a strictly convex, closed hypersurface. In particular,
\partial\Sigma
is connected and topologically an
n-1
-sphere. By
[
3
, Corollary 3.1 and Notes 3.2]
, we see that
\nu
is one-to-one on
\partial\Sigma
and furthermore,
\nu:\Sigma\to\nu(\Sigma)\subset\mathbb{S}^{n}
is a diffeomorphism. On the other hand, it follows from (
2.1
) that
\nu(\partial\Sigma)=\partial\mathbb{S}_{\theta}^{n}
. Also, at least
E_{n+1}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{\theta}
lies in
\nu(\Sigma)
. It follows that
\nu(\Sigma)=\mathbb{S}_{\theta}^{n}
and the assertion follows.
∎

Let
T:\mathbb{S}^{n}_{\theta}\to\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
be the translation given by

T(y)=y+\cos\theta e.

Instead of using the usual Gauss map
\nu
, it is more convenient to use the following map

(2.2)

\displaystyle\tilde{\nu}\coloneqq T\circ\nu:\Sigma\to\mathcal{C}_{\theta},

which we call
capillary Gauss map
of
\Sigma
.

From Lemma
2.2
, we can parametrize
\Sigma
by the inverse capillary Gauss map, i.e.,
X:\mathcal{C}_{\theta}\to\Sigma
, given by

\displaystyle X(\xi)=\tilde{\nu}^{-1}(\xi)=\nu^{-1}\circ T^{-1}(\xi)=\nu^{-1}(\xi-\cos\theta e).

The support function of
\Sigma
is given by

\displaystyle h(X)=\langle X,\nu(X)\rangle.

By the above parametrization,
h
is regarded as a function on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
,

(2.3)

\displaystyle h(\xi)=\langle X(\xi),\nu(X(\xi))\rangle=\langle X(\xi),T^{-1}(\xi)\rangle=\langle\tilde{\nu}^{-1}(\xi),\xi-\cos\theta e\rangle.

To distinguish with the ordinary support function of the convex body
\widehat{\Sigma}
, we call
h
in (
2.3
)
capillary support function
of
\Sigma
(or of
\widehat{\Sigma}
).

Remark 2.3
.

In
[
9
]
, we called
u(\xi)\coloneqq{h(\xi)}/\penalty(\sin^{2}\theta+\cos\theta\langle\xi,e\rangle)
for
\xi\in\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
, as the capillary support function of
\Sigma
. It is easy to see that
\nabla_{\mu}u=0
on
\partial\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
.
u
has a clear geometric meaning (see
[
9
, Remark 2.3]
) and is more suitable for this name.

It is clear that the capillary Gauss map for
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
is the identity map
from
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}\to\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
and

\displaystyle h(\xi)=\langle\xi,\xi-\cos\theta e\rangle=\sin^{2}\theta+\cos\theta\langle\xi,e\rangle.

For simplicity, we denote

(2.4)

\displaystyle\ell(\xi)\coloneqq\sin^{2}\theta+\cos\theta\langle\xi,e\rangle.

Therefore
\ell
is the capillary support function of
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
(or of
\widehat{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}
) in the latter context.

Denote
D
the Euclidean covariant derivative,
\sigma
and
\nabla
the spherical metric and the covariant derivative on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
, then we have the following Lemma (see also
[
9
, Section 2]
).

Lemma 2.4
.

For the parametrization
X:\mathcal{C}_{\theta}\to\Sigma
,

(1)

X(\xi)=\nabla h(\xi)+h(\xi)T^{-1}(\xi)
.

(2)

\nabla_{\mu}h=\cot\theta h
along
\partial\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
, where
\mu
is the outward unit conormal to
\partial\mathcal{C}_{\theta}\subset\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
.

(3)

The principal radii of
\Sigma
at
X(\xi)
are given by the eigenvalues of
(\nabla^{2}h+h\sigma)
with respect to the metric
\sigma
. In particular,
(\nabla^{2}h+h\sigma)>0
on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
.

Proof.

Let
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be an orthonormal frame on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
. From (
2.3
), we see

\displaystyle\nabla_{e_{i}}h(\xi)=\langle D_{e_{i}}X,\xi-\cos\theta e\rangle+\langle X,e_{i}\rangle=\langle X,e_{i}\rangle,\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ 1\leq i\leq n.

Here the second equality follows from the fact that
D_{e_{i}}X
is tangential to
\Sigma
while
\xi-\cos\theta e
is normal to
\Sigma
. The first assertion follows.

Using (1), we get for
\xi\in\partial\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}h(\xi)=\langle\mu(\xi),X(\xi)\rangle,

since
\langle\mu(\xi),T^{-1}(\xi)\rangle=0
. On the other hand, by the capillary boundary condition,

\displaystyle\langle\mu(\xi),X(\xi)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\frac{1}{\sin\theta}(e+\cos\theta\nu(X(\xi))),X(\xi)\rangle=\cot\theta\langle\nu(X(\xi)),X(\xi)\rangle=\cot\theta h(\xi),

since
\langle X(\xi),e\rangle=0
when
\xi\in\partial\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
. The second assertion follows.

Next, using the Gauss formula

D_{e_{j}}D_{e_{i}}X=\hbox{ tangential part}-B_{ij}\nu,

where
B_{ij}
is the second fundamental form of
\Sigma
, we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{e_{j}}\nabla_{e_{i}}h(\xi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle D_{e_{j}}D_{e_{i}}X,\xi-\cos\theta e\rangle+\langle\nabla_{e_{i}}X,D_{e_{j}}\xi\rangle+\langle\nabla_{e_{j}}X,D_{e_{i}}\xi\rangle+\langle X,\nabla_{e_{j}}\nabla_{e_{i}}\xi\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle D_{e_{j}}D_{e_{i}}X,\xi-\cos\theta e\rangle-\langle X,\nabla_{e_{j}}\nabla_{e_{i}}\xi\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle-B_{ij}(\xi-\cos\theta e),\xi-\cos\theta e\rangle-\langle X,\sigma_{ij}(\xi-\cos\theta e)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-B_{ij}-\sigma_{ij}h(\xi).

Hence the second fundamental form
(B_{ij})
is given by
(\nabla^{2}h+h\sigma)
.
By the Weingarten formula
D_{e_{i}}\nu=g^{kl}B_{ik}D_{e_{l}}X
, we see

\displaystyle\sigma_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle D_{e_{i}}\xi,D_{e_{j}}\xi\rangle=\langle D_{e_{i}}(\xi-\cos\theta e),D_{e_{j}}(\xi-\cos\theta e)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle g^{kl}B_{ik}D_{e_{l}}X,g^{pq}B_{jp}D_{e_{q}}X\rangle=B_{ik}B_{jp}g^{kp}.

Hence

\displaystyle g_{kp}=\sigma^{ij}B_{ik}B_{jp}.

It follows that the principal radii, that are the eigenvalues of
(g_{kp}B^{jp})
, are given by the eigenvalues of
(\nabla^{2}h+h\sigma)
with respect to the metric
\sigma
. The third assertion follows.

∎

Definition 2.5
.

For
\theta\in(0,\pi)
,
f\in C^{2}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})
is called a capillary function if it satisfies the following Robin-type boundary condition

(2.5)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}f=\cot\theta f\quad\text{on}\quad\partial\mathcal{C}_{\theta},

If in addition,
(\nabla^{2}f+f\sigma)>0
on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
,
f
is called a capillary convex function on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
.

The next proposition says that a capillary convex function on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
yields a capillary convex body in
\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
, and vice versa. This can be compared to the one-to-one correspondence of convex bodies in
{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}}
and convex functions on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
h\in C^{2}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})
. Then
h
is a capillary convex function if and only if
h
is the capillary support function of a capillary convex body
\widehat{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
.

Proof.

We have already seen from Lemma
2.4
that
if
h\in C^{2}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})
is the capillary support function of a convex capillary hypersurface
\Sigma\subset\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
, then
h
is a capillary convex function.
It remains to show the converse.

Let
h\in C^{2}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})
be a capillary convex function. Consider the hypersurface
\Sigma
given by the map

\displaystyle X:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\theta}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1},

\displaystyle X(\xi)=\nabla h(\xi)+h(\xi)T^{-1}(\xi).

For
\xi\in\partial\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
, it is easy to see
\langle\xi,e\rangle=0
and
\langle\mu(\xi),e\rangle=\sin\theta
.
Then by (
2.5
), we have

\displaystyle\langle X(\xi),e\rangle=\langle\nabla h(\xi)+h(\xi)T^{-1}(\xi),e\rangle=\nabla_{\mu}h\cdot\langle\mu,e\rangle+h(\xi)\langle\xi-\cos\theta e,e\rangle=0.

which implies
X(\partial\mathcal{C}_{\theta})=\partial\Sigma\subset\partial{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}_{+}
.

For any tangent vector field
V
on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
, we have

(2.6)

\displaystyle D_{V}X(\xi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D_{V}(\nabla h+hT^{-1}(\xi))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla_{V}(\nabla h)-\langle V,\nabla h\rangle T^{-1}(\xi)+D_{V}hT^{-1}(\xi)+h(\xi)V

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\nabla^{2}h+h\sigma)|_{\xi}(V).

Since
(\nabla^{2}h+h\sigma)>0
, we see
X
is an immersion.
Also from (
2.6
), we see that for an orthonormal frame
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
at
\xi
,

T_{X(\xi)}\Sigma={\rm span}\{D_{e_{i}}X(\xi)\}_{i=1}^{n}={\rm span}\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}.

It follows that

\nu(X(\xi))=T^{-1}(\xi)=\xi-\cos\theta e
is the unit normal to
\Sigma
at
X(\xi)
.

On the other hand, for
\xi\in{\rm int}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})
, we claim that
\langle X(\xi),E_{n+1}\rangle>0
for each
\xi\in{\rm int}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})
. To see this, for each
\xi\in{\rm int}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})
, let
\xi_{0}\in\partial\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
be the nearest point in
\partial\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
to
\xi
and
\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
be the geodesic from
\xi_{0}
to
\xi
. Then

\displaystyle\langle X(\xi),E_{n+1}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle X(\xi),E_{n+1}\rangle-\langle X(\xi_{0}),E_{n+1}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\langle D_{\dot{\gamma}(t)}X(\gamma(t)),E_{n+1}\rangle dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}(\nabla^{2}h+h\sigma)|_{\gamma(t)}(\dot{\gamma}(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))\langle E_{n+1},\dot{\gamma}(t)\rangle dt.

The last equality holds since for
V\in T_{\gamma(t)}\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
such that
\langle V,\dot{\gamma}(t)\rangle=0
, it holds
\langle E_{n+1},V\rangle=0
.
Since
(\nabla^{2}h+h\sigma)>0
and
\langle E_{n+1},\dot{\gamma}(t)\rangle>0
, we obtain that
\langle X(\xi),E_{n+1}\rangle>0
.
Hence
X(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})\subset\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
.

Next, we prove
X(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})=\Sigma\subset\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
is (locally) strictly convex and capillary.
First, in particular,

\langle\nu(X(\xi)),e\rangle=\langle T^{-1}(\xi),e\rangle=-\cos\theta\quad\hbox{for}\penalty\ \xi\in\partial\mathcal{C}_{\theta},

which implies it is capillary.
Second by (
2.6
),

D_{e_{i}}\nu=D_{e_{i}}(\xi-\cos\theta e)=(\nabla^{2}h+h\sigma)^{-1}\cdot D_{e_{j}}X,

Hence the eigenvalues of
(\nabla^{2}h+h\sigma)^{-1}|_{\xi}
give the principal curvatures of
\Sigma
at
X(\xi)
. Thus
X:\mathcal{C}_{\theta}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
is strictly convex.

Finally, we prove that
X:\mathcal{C}_{\theta}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
is an embedding. For this, we look at the Gauss map
\nu:\Sigma\to\mathbb{S}^{n}
. From the strictly convexity and capillarity of
\Sigma
, we know from
[
3
, Corollary 3.1]
that

\nu
is one-to-one on
\partial\Sigma
and furthermore,
\nu
is a diffeomorphism on
\Sigma
(as in the proof of Lemma
2.2
). On the other hand, we know that

\nu(\Sigma)=\mathbb{S}^{n}_{\theta}=\mathcal{C}_{\theta}-\cos\theta e
. Hence
X(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})=\Sigma=\nu^{-1}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta}-\cos\theta e)
is an embedding.

This completes the proof of the lemma.

∎

Next, we derive a simple and crucial fact about the capillary functions.

Lemma 2.7
.

If
f
is a capillary function on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
, then for all
1\leq\alpha\leq n-1
,

(2.7)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}f(e_{\alpha},\mu)=0,\quad\text{on}\quad\partial\mathcal{C}_{\theta},

where
\{e_{\alpha}\}_{1\leq\alpha\leq n-1}
is an orthonormal frame of
\partial\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
.

Proof.

Note that
\partial\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
is a totally umbilical hypersurface in
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
with principal curvature
\cot\theta
, we have
\nabla_{e_{\alpha}}\mu=\cot\theta e_{\alpha}
on
\partial\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
. Together with (
2.5
), for all
1\leq\alpha\leq n-1
,

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}f(e_{\alpha},\mu)=\nabla_{e_{\alpha}}(\nabla_{\mu}f)-\nabla_{\nabla_{e_{\alpha}}\mu}f=0.

We complete the proof.
∎

2.2.
Mixed volume for capillary convex bodies

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
K_{i}\in\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
,
i=1,\cdots,m
. Then the Minkowski sum
K\coloneqq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}K_{i}\in\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
, where
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}>0
and
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m}\geq 0
. Moreover,

(2.8)

\displaystyle h_{K}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}h_{K_{i}},

where
h_{K}
and
h_{K_{i}}
are the capillary support functions of
K
and
K_{i}
respectively.

Proof.

Let
\widetilde{h}_{K_{i}}\coloneqq\widehat{h}_{K_{i}}(\cdot-\cos\theta e)
with
\widehat{h}_{K_{i}}
being the classical support function of
K_{i}
, viewed as a function on
\mathbb{S}^{n}+\cos\theta e
for
1\leq i\leq m
. Then
\widetilde{h}_{K}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\widetilde{h}_{K_{i}}
is the support function of
K
, which is defined on
\mathbb{S}^{n}+\cos\theta e
.
On the other hand, denote

h_{K_{i}}\in C^{2}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})
the capillary support function of
K_{i}\in\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
, then

h_{K_{i}}=\widetilde{h}_{K_{i}}\big|_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}
.
Define

\displaystyle h\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}h_{K_{i}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\widetilde{h}_{K_{i}}|_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}=\widetilde{h}_{K}|_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}.

From Lemma
2.4
(2) and (3), it is easy to see
h\in C^{2}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})
is a capillary convex function, together with Proposition
2.6
, it gives rise to a capillary convex body
L\in\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
with

h=\widetilde{h}_{L}|_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ in }\penalty\ \penalty\ \mathcal{C}_{\theta}.

From Lemma
2.4
(1), the position vector of
\partial L\cap{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
is given by

\displaystyle X(\xi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla h(\xi)+h(\xi)T^{-1}(\xi)=\nabla\widetilde{h}_{K}(\xi)+\widetilde{h}_{K}(\xi)T^{-1}(\xi),\quad\xi\in\mathcal{C}_{\theta}.

The right hand side is exactly the position vector of
K
restricting on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
, hence we conclude that
K=L\in\mathcal{K}_{\theta}.
The last assertion follows from
h_{K}=\widetilde{h}_{K}|_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}
.
∎

Recall that the mixed discriminant
Q:(\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by

\displaystyle\det(\lambda_{1}A_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{m}A_{m})=\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}=1}^{m}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{n}}Q(A_{i_{1}},\cdots,A_{i_{n}})

for
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m}\geq 0
and the real symmetric matrices
A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. If
(A_{k})_{ij}
denotes the
(i,j)
-element of the matrix
A_{k}
, then

\displaystyle Q(A_{1},\cdots,A_{n})=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{n},j_{1},\cdots,j_{n}}\delta_{j_{1}\cdots j_{n}}^{i_{1}\cdots i_{n}}(A_{1})_{i_{1}j_{1}}\cdots(A_{n})_{i_{n}j_{n}}.

It is clear that the mixed discriminant
Q
is a multilinear and symmetric function (see, e.g.,
[
1
, Chapter 4, Section 25.4]
).

For any
f\in C^{2}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})
, we denote
A[f]\coloneqq\nabla^{2}f+f\sigma
. We introduce the multi-variable function
V:(C^{2}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta}))^{n+1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
given by

(2.9)

\displaystyle V(f_{1},\cdots,f_{n+1})=\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}f_{1}Q\left(A[f_{2}],\cdots,A[f_{n+1}]\right)d\sigma.

for
f_{i}\in C^{2}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta}),1\leq i\leq n+1
.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
f_{1},f_{2},\cdots,f_{n+1}
be capillary functions, then
V
is a symmetric and multi-linear function.

Proof.

The multilinear property is obvious. By the symmetry of
Q
,
we just need to prove

(2.10)

\displaystyle V(f_{1},f_{2},\cdots,f_{n+1})=V(f_{2},f_{1},\cdots,f_{n+1}).

It is known that

(2.11)

\displaystyle Q(A[f_{2}],\cdots,A[f_{n+1}])=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}Q^{ij}\cdot A[f_{2}]_{ij},

where

\displaystyle Q^{ij}\coloneqq\frac{\partial Q}{\partial A[f_{2}]_{ij}}

satisfying

(2.12)

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\nabla_{i}Q^{ij}=0,\quad\text{for\penalty\ all }\penalty\ 1\leq j\leq n.

Using (
2.11
), (
2.12
) and integrating by parts twice, we get

\displaystyle V(f_{1},f_{2},\cdots,f_{n+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}f_{1}Q^{ij}\cdot(\nabla^{2}f_{2}(e_{i},e_{j})+f_{2}\delta_{ij})d\sigma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}f_{2}Q^{ij}\cdot(\nabla^{2}f_{1}(e_{i},e_{j})+f_{1}\delta_{ij})d\sigma

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{\partial\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}\left[f_{1}Q^{ij}\nabla_{e_{j}}f_{2}\cdot\langle\mu,e_{i}\rangle-f_{2}Q^{ij}\nabla_{e_{i}}f_{1}\cdot\langle\mu,e_{j}\rangle\right]ds.

Here we use an orthonormal basis
\{e_{i}\}
on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
such that
e_{n}=\mu
along
\partial\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
.
On
\partial\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
, from (
2.5
), there holds

\displaystyle f_{1}Q^{ij}\nabla_{e_{j}}f_{2}\langle\mu,e_{i}\rangle-f_{2}Q^{ij}\nabla_{e_{i}}f_{1}\langle\mu,e_{j}\rangle=Q^{nn}\left(f_{1}\nabla_{\mu}f_{2}-f_{2}\nabla_{\mu}f_{1}\right)=0.

Hence we complete the proof of (
2.10
).
∎

The following is a direct corollary of Proposition
2.9
.

Corollary 2.10
.

For any capillary function
f
, there holds

(2.13)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}fH_{k-1}(A[f])d\sigma=\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}\ell H_{k}(A[f])d\sigma,\quad 1\leq k\leq n,

where
H_{k}(A[f])
is the
k
-th normalized elementary symmetric function evaluated at
A[f]
.

Proof.

Recall
\ell(\xi)=\sin^{2}\theta+\cos\theta\langle\xi,e\rangle.
Note

\nabla^{2}(\xi-\cos\theta e)=-(\xi-\cos\theta e).

Hence
A[\ell]=\nabla^{2}\ell+\ell\sigma=\sigma
.
From Proposition
2.9
, we have

\displaystyle V(\underbrace{f,\cdots,f}_{k\penalty\ \text{copies}},\ell,\cdots,\ell)=V(\ell,\underbrace{f,\cdots,f}_{k\penalty\ \text{copies}},\ell,\cdots,\ell).

By definition, we have

\displaystyle V(\underbrace{f,\cdots,f}_{k\penalty\ \text{copies}},\ell,\cdots,\ell)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}fQ(\underbrace{A[f],\cdots,A[f]}_{(k-1)\penalty\ \text{copies}},A[\ell],\cdots,A[\ell])d\sigma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}fH_{k-1}(A[f])d\sigma,

and

\displaystyle V(\ell,\underbrace{f,\cdots,f}_{k\penalty\ \text{copies}},\ell,\cdots,\ell)=\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}\ell H_{k}(A[f])d\sigma.

The assertion follows from the previous Proposition.
∎

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
K_{1},\ldots,K_{m}\in\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
and
h_{i}

(2.14)

\displaystyle V(K_{i_{1}},\cdots,K_{i_{n+1}})=V(h_{i_{1}},\cdots,h_{i_{n+1}})=\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}h_{i_{1}}Q\left(A[h_{i_{2}}],\cdots,A[h_{i_{n+1}}]\right)d\sigma.

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}h_{K}\det\left(A[h_{K}]\right)d\sigma

We collect the following basic properties for

Proposition 2.13
.

Let

and

.

Finally, we demonstrate that the quermassintegral
\mathcal{V}_{k,\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma})
, which was introduced in

To see this, we need the following reinterpretation of
\mathcal{V}_{k,\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma})

Lemma 2.14
.

Let
\widehat{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
.
Then

(2.15)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{k+1,\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma})=\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{\Sigma}(1+\cos\theta\langle\nu,e\rangle)H_{k}dA,\quad 0\leq k\leq n.

Proof.

By integration by parts, we know

\displaystyle 0=\int_{\widehat{\Sigma}}{\rm div}(e)dx=|\widehat{\partial\Sigma}|+\int_{\Sigma}\langle\nu,e\rangle dA.

The assertion for
k=0
follows.

Next, we consider the case
1\leq k\leq n
. Let
B=(B_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
and
\widehat{B}=(\widehat{B}_{\alpha\beta})_{1\leq\alpha,\beta\leq n-1}
be the second fundamental form of
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}
and
\partial\Sigma\subset\partial\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n+1}}
respectively. Let
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be an orthonormal basis on
\Sigma
. It is known (cf.
[
16
, Proposition 2.4 (1) (2)]

\mu\coloneqq e_{n}

\Sigma

and

We notice that on
\Sigma
,

(2.16)

\displaystyle\sigma^{ij}_{k}\langle\nabla^{\Sigma}_{i}e^{T},e_{j}\rangle=-\sigma^{ij}_{k}B_{ij}\langle\nu,e\rangle=-k\sigma_{k}\langle\nu,e\rangle,

where
\sigma_{k}^{ij}=\frac{\partial\sigma_{k}}{\partial B_{ij}}
.
Integrating (
2.16
) over
\Sigma
and integrating by parts, we see that

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}k\sigma_{k}\langle\nu,e\rangle dA=-\int_{\partial\Sigma}\sigma^{\mu\mu}_{k}\langle e,\mu\rangle ds=-\sin^{k}\theta\int_{\partial\Sigma}\sigma_{k-1}^{\partial\Sigma}ds,

where

is

It follows that

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}H_{k}\langle\nu,e\rangle dA=-\frac{1}{n}\sin^{k}\theta\int_{\partial\Sigma}H^{\partial\Sigma}_{k-1}ds.

This completes the proof.

Proposition 2.15
.

Let

be a convex capillary hypersurface and

its capillary support function. Then for

\displaystyle V(\underbrace{\widehat{\Sigma},\cdots,\widehat{\Sigma}}_{k\penalty\ \rm{copies}},\widehat{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}},\cdots,\widehat{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}})=V(\underbrace{h,\cdots,h}_{k\penalty\ \rm{copies}},\ell,\cdots,\ell)=\mathcal{V}_{n-k+1,\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma}).

From Proposition

\displaystyle V(\underbrace{h,\cdots,h}_{k\text{ copies}},\ell,\cdots,\ell)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}\ell Q(\underbrace{A[h],\cdots,A[h]}_{k\text{ copies}},A[\ell],\cdots,A[\ell])d\sigma

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}\ell H_{k}(A[h])d\sigma

Remark 2.17
.

We make further comments on the Minkowski sum

\widehat{\Sigma}+t\widehat{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}

. For any

(2.18)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{t}\coloneqq\{x+t(\nu+\cos\theta e)\,|x\in\Sigma\}.

One observes that

\displaystyle\widehat{\Sigma_{t}}=\widehat{\Sigma}+t\widehat{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}.

This is because a point

y=\nu(y)+\cos\theta e
.
From this observation, one sees the unit outward normal
\nu_{t}

of
\Sigma_{t}
is given by

dA_{t}=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n}t^{k}\binom{n}{k}H_{k}dA.

Therefore, by virtue of (
2.15
), we get that

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{1,\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma_{t}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{\Sigma_{t}}(1+\cos\theta\langle\nu_{t},e\rangle)dA_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\int_{\Sigma}(1+\cos\theta\langle\nu,e\rangle)t^{k}\binom{n}{k}H_{k}dA

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}t^{k}\mathcal{V}_{k+1,\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma}).

We remark that recently the parallel hypersurfaces were used in
[

to prove the Heintze-Karcher inequality for capillary hypersurfaces.

3.
The Alexandrov-Fenchel inequalities for mixed volume

With preparation in the previous section, we prove Theorem
1.1
in this section, adapting the idea of Shenfeld-van Handel in
[
12
,
13
]
. For the reader’s convenience, we include
[
12
, Lemma 1.4]
below, as it will be crucially used in the subsequent analysis.

Lemma 3.1

(
[
12
]
)
.

Let
A
be a symmetric matrix. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

\langle x,Ay\rangle^{2}\geq\langle x,Ax\rangle\langle y,Ay\rangle
for all
x,y
such that
\langle y,Ay\rangle\geq 0
.

(2)

The positive eigenspace of
A
has dimension at most one.

The conclusion remains valid if
A
is a self-adjoint operator on a Hilbert space with a discrete spectrum, provided the vectors
x,y
are chosen in the domain of
A
.

We now proceed to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
f_{1},\cdots,f_{n}\in C^{2}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})
be capillary convex functions on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
and at least one of
f_{k}\penalty\ (2\leq k\leq n)
is positive. Then for any capillary function
f\in C^{2}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})
, there holds

(3.1)

\displaystyle V^{2}(f,f_{1},f_{2},\cdots,f_{n})\geq V(f,f,f_{2},\cdots,f_{n})V(f_{1},f_{1},f_{2},\cdots,f_{n}).

Equality holds if and only if
f=af_{1}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\langle\cdot,E_{i}\rangle
for some constants
a,a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\{E_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
being the horizontal coordinate unit vectors of
\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
f_{2}
is positive on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
.
Define
\omega
to be the following area measure on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}

d\omega\coloneqq\frac{1}{n+1}\frac{Q(A[f_{2}],A[f_{2}],\cdots,A[f_{n}])}{f_{2}}d\sigma.

Let
L^{2}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta},\omega)
be the Hilbert space associated with the inner product
\langle,\rangle_{L^{2}(\omega)}
given by

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle_{L^{2}(\omega)}\coloneqq\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}f\cdot gd\omega,\quad\forall f,g\in L^{2}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta},\omega).

W^{2,2}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta},\omega)
is similarly defined.
Define the following operator as in
[
12
]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}:\text{dom}(\mathcal{A})\subset L^{2}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta},\omega)\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta},\omega),

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(f)=\frac{f_{2}Q(A[f],A[f_{2}],\cdots,A[f_{n}])}{Q(A[f_{2}],A[f_{2}],\cdots,A[f_{n}])},

where

\text{dom}(\mathcal{A})
is given by

\displaystyle\text{dom}(\mathcal{A})\coloneqq\left\{f\in W^{2,2}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta},\omega):\nabla_{\mu}f=\cot\theta f\penalty\ \text{on}\penalty\ \partial\mathcal{C}_{\theta}\right\}.

Note that all the above objects are well-defined, due to
f_{2}>0
and
f_{1},\cdots,f_{n}
are capillary convex functions on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
.

By the very definition of
\mathcal{A}
, (
3.1
) is equivalent to

(3.2)

\displaystyle\left(\langle f,\mathcal{A}f_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}(\omega)}\right)^{2}\geq\langle f,\mathcal{A}f\rangle_{L^{2}(\omega)}\langle f_{1},\mathcal{A}f_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}(\omega)},

for functions
f,\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
that satisfy the assumptions in Theorem
1.1
. By Lemma
3.1
, we only need to prove that the positive eigenspace of
\mathcal{A}
has dimension at most one, which we are going to do.

Since
f_{1},\cdots,f_{n}
are capillary convex functions, we know that
\mathcal{A}
is a uniformly elliptic operator with a Robin boundary condition. Proposition
2.9
implies

\displaystyle\langle f,\mathcal{A}g\rangle_{L^{2}(\omega)}=\langle g,\mathcal{A}f\rangle_{L^{2}(\omega)},\quad\forall f,g\in\text{dom}(\mathcal{A}),

which means that
\mathcal{A}
is a self-adjoint operator on
\text{dom}(\mathcal{A})
. Consider the following eigenvalue problem

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rcll}\mathcal{A}f&=&\lambda f,&\quad\hbox{ in }\mathcal{C}_{\theta},\\
\nabla_{\mu}f&=&\cot\theta f,&\quad\hbox{ on }\partial\mathcal{C}_{\theta}.\end{array}

The classical spectral theory of compact, self-adjoint operators implies that the eigenvalue problem (
3
) admits a sequence of real eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots
, with
\lambda_{k}\rightarrow-\infty
, its first eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
is simple and its first eigenfunction has the same sign everywhere.
It is trivial to check that
f_{2}
satisfies (
3
) with
\lambda=1
. Since
f_{2}>0
,
f_{2}
is the first eigenfunction with the first eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}=1
.

On the other hand, by using Alexandrov’s mixed discriminant inequality (see e.g.
[
11
, Theorem 5.5.4]
)

(3.6)

\displaystyle\left(Q(A[{g}],A[f_{2}],\cdots,A[f_{n}])\right)^{2}\geq Q(A[f_{2}],A[f_{2}],\cdots,A[f_{n}])\cdot Q(A[{g}],A[{g}],A[f_{2}],\cdots,A[f_{n}]),\penalty\ \penalty\

together with Proposition
2.9
, for all function
{g}\in\text{dom}({\mathcal{A}})
we have

(3.7)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{A}{g},\mathcal{A}{g}\rangle_{L^{2}(\omega)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}\frac{f_{2}\left(Q(A[{g}],A[f_{2}],\cdots,A[f_{n}])\right)^{2}}{Q(A[f_{2}],A[f_{2}],\cdots,A[f_{n}])}d\sigma

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}f_{2}Q(A[{g}],A[{g}],A[f_{2}],\cdots,A[f_{n}])d\sigma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}{g}Q(A[{g}],A[f_{2}],\cdots,A[f_{n}])d\sigma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle{g},\mathcal{A}{g}\rangle_{L^{2}(\omega)}.

Therefore, if
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of the operator
\mathcal{A}
, the above inequality implies that
\lambda^{2}\geq\lambda
, which means
\lambda\geq 1
or
\lambda\leq 0
.
Consequently, the positive eigenspace of
\mathcal{A}
is of one dimension and is spanned by
f_{2}
. In view of Lemma
3.1
, we complete the proof of (
3.2
).

Next, we characterize equality. If equality holds in (
3.1
), then also equality holds in (
3.2
), that is

(3.8)

\displaystyle\left(\langle f,\mathcal{A}f_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}(\omega)}\right)^{2}=\langle f,\mathcal{A}f\rangle_{L^{2}(\omega)}\langle f_{1},\mathcal{A}f_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}(\omega)}.

From
[
12
, Lemma 2.9 and its proof]
, equality holds in (
3.8
) if and only if
\widetilde{f}\coloneqq f-af_{1}\in\text{Ker}(\mathcal{A})
for some constant
a\in\mathbb{R}
.

For
g=\tilde{f}
, equality holds in (
3.7
) and in turn in Alexandrov’s mixed discriminant inequality (
3.6
). It follows from
[
11
, Theorem 5.5.4]
that

A[\tilde{f}]=cA[f_{2}]
for some function
c:\mathcal{C}_{\theta}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Since
\tilde{f}\in\text{Ker}(\mathcal{A})
, we have

\displaystyle 0=\frac{f_{2}Q(A[\tilde{f}-cf_{2}],A[f_{2}],\cdots,A[f_{n}])}{Q(A[f_{2}],A[f_{2}],\cdots,A[f_{n}])}=\mathcal{A}\tilde{f}-c=-c.

This implies
A[\tilde{f}]=0
and
hence
\tilde{f}
is a linear function. The fact that
\tilde{f}
is a capillary function implies that

\tilde{f}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\langle\cdot,E_{i}\rangle
for some constants
a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
,
i=1,\cdots,n
. Therefore, equality in (
3.1
) holds if and only if
f=af_{1}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\langle\cdot,E_{i}\rangle.

∎

Taking
f
