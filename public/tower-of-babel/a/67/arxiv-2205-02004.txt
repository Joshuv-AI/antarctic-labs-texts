Geometric multiplicity of unitary non-backtracking eigenvalues
Leo Torres

Abstract
We completely characterize the conditions under which a complex unitary number is an eigenvalue of the non-backtracking matrix of an undirected graph. Further, we provide a closed formula to compute its geometric multiplicity and describe an algorithm to compute this multiplicity without making a single matrix computation. The algorithm has time complexity that is linear in the size of the graph. Keywords: spectral graph theory, non-backtracking, cycle structure, graph algorithms MSC: 05C50, 05C82, 05C85, 15A18, 15B99

Introduction

The non-backtracking matrix of a graph
G
was first introduced by Hashimoto in the study of Zeta functions of graphs
[
11
]
.
He and others proved that the poles of the Zeta function of a graph coincide with the reciprocals of the eigenvalues of the non-backtracking matrix
[
20
,
2
]
.
Subsequently, the non-backtracking (NB) matrix became a point of focus of modern spectral graph theory.
It is now known that the NB eigenvalues,

independently of their relationship to the Zeta function, contain rich information about the structure of the underlying graph, and have thus been used in multiple applications and theoretical settings
[

.

The NB matrix encodes the adjacency of oriented edges of an undirected graph, thus its entries are all
0
or
1
.
We give a formal definition in the next Section.
Being a non-negative matrix, Perron-Frobenius theory can be used to describe the eigenvalues
\lambda
that have maximal magnitude.
At the other end of the spectrum, the eigenvalue
\lambda=0
can be shown, via elementary methods, to be related to the existence of nodes of degree
1
, or more generally, of subgraphs that are tree-like.
Up to now, these were the only two families of NB eigenvalues whose properties have been fully described in the general case of arbitrary graphs.
The difficulty in making general claims about the NB eigenvalues is that, in contrast to the classical matrices studied in spectral graph theory, the NB matrix is not symmetric, in fact it is not even normal.
Thus most of the existing tools from spectral graph theory do not apply to it.

In this work, we study a third family of NB eigenvalues, namely those complex numbers on the unit circle, and fully describe how they are related to the structure of the graph.
In particular, we establish that only those unitary numbers that are also roots of unity can be NB eigenvalues of a graph.
Additionally, we characterize the subgraphs that constitute the support of an eigenfunction corresponding to a unitary NB eigenvalue, and describe all possible ways in which these subgraphs may be connected to the rest of the graph.
Finally, we provide closed formulas to compute the geometric multiplicity of unitary NB eigenvalues, and we show an algorithm that computes said formulas efficiently.

Background

Conventions and notation

A graph
G
is comprised of a set of nodes
V(G)
and a set of edges
E(G)
.
All graphs considered are finite, undirected, unweighted, connected, and possibly contain self-loops or multi-edges.
The degree of node

is the number of edges that contain it and is denoted

A self-loop
(v,v)\in E(G)
adds two units to the degree.

If a complex number

|z|=1

(a.k.a.

).
Given a unitary
\lambda

\lambda
is said to be a

th root of unity.
The smallest positive such

is the

of
\lambda
, and in this case
\lambda
is a
primitive
q
th root
of unity.

Multiplicity of eigenvalues

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a square matrix and let
(\lambda,\mathbf{v})
be a pair such that
\mathbf{Av}=\lambda\mathbf{v}
.
The
algebraic multiplicity
of
\lambda

\am_{\mathbf{A}}(\lambda)

as a root of the polynomial
\det\left(\mathbf{A}-t\mathbf{I}\right)

The

of
\lambda

\left(\mathbf{A}-\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)
.
It is always the case that
\am_{\mathbf{A}}(\lambda)\geq\gm_{\mathbf{A}}(\lambda)
.
If
G
is a graph, we write
\am_{G}(\lambda),\gm_{G}(\lambda)
to refer to the multiplicities of
\lambda
as an eigenvalue of the non-backtracking matrix of

We drop the subscript when clear from context.

NB walks and NB matrix

Consider a graph

A

The

is equal to the number of NB walks starting with

Remark 1

(
Circle graphs
)
.

The special case when

Suppose

In this case

We begin our discussion with the real roots of unity, namely

Proposition 3.1
.

Let

have at least two cycles. Then

Proof.

Define

This fact, together with

Thus, showing

First, note that

The so-called Jacobi formula shows

Finally, observe that

All together, we have

\displaystyle g(1)=f^{\prime}(1)

\displaystyle=\Tr\left[\adj\left(\mathbf{D-A}\right)\left(\mathbf{D-A+D}-2\mathbf{I}\right)\right]

(3.1)

\displaystyle=\eta\Tr\left[\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{D-A}\right)+\mathbf{11}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{D}-2\mathbf{I}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\eta\mathbf{1}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{D-A}\right)\mathbf{1}+\eta\mathbf{1}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{D}-2\mathbf{I}\right)\mathbf{1}

\displaystyle=\eta\left(2m-2n\right)\neq 0\qed

4.2
Support of an eigenfunction

We now characterize those unitary complex numbers that may possibly be NB eigenvalues, and then we characterize the graphs that may possibly be the support of an eigenfunction of unitary eigenvalue.
Some of the following arguments require the graph to have no nodes of degree

Definition 4.5
.

Given a NB eigenfunction
\mathbf{v}
of a graph

, its

is the set of edges

such that at least one of
\mathbf{v}_{k\to l}

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
G
be a graph with no nodes of degree
1
and NB matrix
\mathbf{B}
.
Suppose
\mathbf{Bv}=\lambda\mathbf{v}
with
\lambda\neq 0
and let

be the graph induced by the support of
\mathbf{v}
.
Then,

has no nodes of degree

.

Proof.

Assume there is a node

with degree
1
in
H
and degree

in
G
.
Suppose
y
is the only neighbor of
x
that exists in
H
.
We show that both
\mathbf{v}_{x\to y}=0
and
\mathbf{v}_{y\to x}=0
, which is a contradiction.

Since
\mathbf{v}
is an eigenfunction, we have
\lambda\mathbf{v}_{x\to y}=\sum_{i\neq y}a_{ix}\mathbf{v}_{i\to x}
.
But the right-hand side is zero since otherwise

would have degree greater than
1
in

.
Since
\lambda\neq 0
, we have
\mathbf{v}_{x\to y}=0
.

Let

be a neighbor of

.
We can always choose such

.
We now have

Most of the main results that follow are essentially corollaries of the upcoming Key Lemma, which determines the behavior of a NB eigenfunction on a specific type of subgraph called

Definition 4.7

(

Suppose

has a NB path of the form

.

of length

Note

and

.
If two nodes

Note every graph with minimum degree at least

Lemma 4.8

(

Let
G
have NB matrix
\mathbf{B}
with
\mathbf{Bv}=\lambda\mathbf{v}

Suppose

.

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{v}
is an eigenfunction of unitary eigenvalue, it satisfies

.
By Lemma

, this implies
\mathchoice{\vec{\mathbf{v}}^{{{{k}}}}}{\vec{\mathbf{v}}^{{{{k}}}}}{\vec{\mathbf{v}}^{{{{k}}}}}{\vec{\mathbf{v}}^{{{{k}}}}}=\mathchoice{\vec{\mathbf{v}}^{{{{l}}}}}{\vec{\mathbf{v}}^{{{{l}}}}}{\vec{\mathbf{v}}^{{{{l}}}}}{\vec{\mathbf{v}}^{{{{l}}}}}=0

.
To fix ideas, suppose

The fact that
\mathchoice{\vec{\mathbf{v}}^{{{{k}}}}}{\vec{\mathbf{v}}^{{{{k}}}}}{\vec{\mathbf{v}}^{{{{k}}}}}{\vec{\mathbf{v}}^{{{{k}}}}}=0

Now assume

.

Let

Theorem 4.10
.

Let

be a graph with at least two cycles, with NB matrix
\mathbf{B}
, and suppose
\mathbf{Bv}=\lambda\mathbf{v}

Let

Let

\mathbf{v}

Note Theorem

Now take an edge

If both

Otherwise, if

Proposition 5.3
.

Let

be the

\mathbf{v}
is an eigenfunction of

Let

Define the vector

as

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{k\to l}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbf{v}_{k\to k_{1}(l)},\quad\text{for each $k$ with $d_{k}>2$,}

(5.2a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{k\to j}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbf{u}_{i\to k}/\lambda^{r},\quad\text{for each $k$ with $d_{k}=2$ and distinct neighbors $i,j$.}

(5.2b)

Then,
\mathbf{u}
is a NB eigenfunction of
G

\lambda^{r}
.
We say that
\mathbf{u}
is the

, or simply the

.

Proof.

First we show that
\mathbf{u}
satisfies

\lambda^{r}\mathbf{u}_{k\to l}=-\mathbf{u}_{l\to k},\quad\text{for each edge $k\to l$ in $G$ where $d_{k}>2$.}

(5.2c)

We do so in three cases.

Case 1:
Assume
k,l
are neighbors with
d_{k},d_{l}>2
.
By

\mathbf{u}_{k\to l}=\mathbf{v}_{k\to k_{1}(l)}=-\lambda^{-1}\mathbf{v}_{k_{1}(l)\to k}
, where last equality comes from
\lambda\mathbf{v}_{k\to k_{1}(l)}+\mathbf{v}_{k_{1}(l)\to k}=\mathchoice{\vec{\mathbf{v}}^{{{{k}}}}}{\vec{\mathbf{v}}^{{{{k}}}}}{\vec{\mathbf{v}}^{{{{k}}}}}{\vec{\mathbf{v}}^{{{{k}}}}}=0
which holds since

is unitary.
Further, recall that each

has degree

5.1

Let

6
Gluing non-leaky functions

Consider a graph

that is the

How may we connect, or

The following construction formalizes the notion of

Definition 6.1

(

Let

Proposition 6.8

(

Suppose

With the notations of

Corollary 6.12

7
Geometric multiplicity

Let
X
be a connected graph possibly with multi-edges or self-loops, and let
\lambda
be a root of unity of order
q
.
In this section we compute the geometric multiplicity of
\lambda
as a NB eigenvalue of
X
.
It is clear by now that to do so, we must first find all NB chains in
X
of the appropriate length and then assemble them in subgraphs of
X
that may support an eigenfunction of
\lambda
.
In this section, by
H\subset G
we mean
E(H)\subset E(G)
.

Definition 7.1
.

For a fixed positive integer
q>2
, define

\mathcal{C}_{q}\coloneqq\{C_{q}\subset G\colon C_{q}\text{ is a NB chain whose length is a multiple of }q\text{ or }q/2\}.

(7.1)

Note the case when the length of a chain is a multiple of
q/2
is only relevant when
q
is even.
Furthermore, let
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{q}
be the subgraph assembled from all such chains,

E\left(\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{q}\right)\coloneqq\bigcup_{C_{q}\subset\mathcal{C}_{q}}E(C_{q}).

(7.2)

Note
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{q}
may be disconnected.
Therefore we define

\mathcal{S}_{q}\coloneqq\{S_{q}\subset\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{q}\colon S_{q}\text{ is a connected component of }\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{q}\}.

(7.3)

Each
S_{q}\in\mathcal{S}_{q}
that contains at least two cycles supports at least one eigenfunction of
\lambda
.
Since each
S_{q}
is maximal with respect to inclusion, there is no instance of partial support (see
section

6.2
).

If
q
is odd, we can directly use the graphs
S_{q}
to compute the multiplicity of
\lambda
.
However, if
q
is even, we need sometimes consider the graph of which
S_{q}
is a subdivision.

Definition 7.2
.

For a graph
S
that is the subdivision of some other graph, we write
\coll(S,q)
for the graph whose
q
-subdivision is
S
, if it exists.
In that case, we say
\coll(S,q)
is the
collapse
of
S
.

Theorem 7.3

(
Main Theorem
)
.

Let
X
be a connected graph with at least two cycles, possibly with multi-edges or self-loops, and let
\lambda
be a root of unity of order
q>2
.
If
\mathcal{S}_{q}
is empty, we have
\gm_{X}(\lambda)=0
.
If
\mathcal{S}_{q}
is non-empty and
q
is odd,

\gm_{X}(\lambda)=\sum_{S_{q}\in\mathcal{S}_{q}}|E(S_{q})|-|V(S_{q})|+1.

(7.4)

If
\mathcal{S}_{q}
is non-empty and
q
is even,

\gm_{X}(\lambda)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S_{q}\in\mathcal{S}_{q}:\\
q|r(S_{q})\end{subarray}}|E(S_{q})|-|V(S_{q})|+1\;\;+\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S_{q}\in\mathcal{S}_{q}:\\
q\,\nmid\,r(S_{q})\end{subarray}}|E(S_{q})|-|V(S_{q})|+\mathbf{1}\{\coll(S_{q},q)\textnormal{ is bipartite}\},

(7.5)

where
r(S)
is the maximum integer
r
such that
S
is the
r
-subdivision of some other graph.

Proof.

We claim that each eigenfunction of
\lambda
must be supported on
\mathcal{S}_{q}
.
Recall the components
S_{q}
of
\mathcal{S}_{q}
were assembled using chains whose length is a multiple of
q
or
q/2
, which guarantees that the distance between every pair of nodes with degrees greater than
2
is a multiple of
q/2
.
Thus, every such node in
S_{q}
is in
N(S_{q},q)
by Theorems
6.7
and
6.8
, which in turn means we may conceive of
S_{q}
as having been glued to
X\setminus S_{q}
to form
X
by a valid non-leaky gluing.
Therefore, every eigenfunction of
\lambda
in
S_{q}
is transferred to
X
, and there are no others (other than linear combinations thereof).

Since each
S_{q}
is a subdivision, we may use
Theorem

5.4
to compute the number of eigenfunctions transferred.
If
q
is odd, each
S_{q}
is always a
q
-subdivision of some other graph.
Thus, in this case the number of eigenfunctions transferred is
|E(S_{q})|-|V(S_{q})|+1
.

Now assume
q
is even.
In this case, each
S_{q}
may be a
q
-subdivision or not.
If it is not, then it must be a
q/2
-subdivision.
This is because
S_{q}
is assembled from chains whose lengths devide
q
or
q/2
.

If
S_{q}
is a
q
-subdivision, or equivalently if
q|r(S_{q})
, the
q
th roots of unity are eigenvalues of
S_{q}
corresponding to the eigenvalue
+1
in
\coll(S_{q},q)
.
Again due to
Theorem

5.4
, there are
E(\coll(S_{q},q))-V(\coll(S_{q},q))+1
of these eigenvalues.
This in turn equals
E(S_{q})-V(S_{q})+1
, as taking subdivisions does not change the number of cycles in a graph.

If
S_{q}
is not a
q
-subdivision, or equivalently if
q\nmid r(S_{q})
, it must be a
q/2
-subdivision, and the
q
th roots of unity are eigenvalues of
S_{q}
corresponding to the eigenvalue
-1
in
\coll(S_{q},q)
.
Using
Theorem

5.4
again finishes the proof.
∎

is bipartite and
False
otherwise.
Both these algorithms are well known and can be computed in a single depth-first traversal of the graph.

The function
GetChains
(
X,q
) returns a collection of NB chains of
X
whose length is a multiple of
q
or
q/2
.
This can be achieved in a single depth-first traversal of the graph, by keeping track of the current number of consecutive times that a node of degree
2
was observed.
When a node of degree greater than
2
is observed, the length of the current chain, if any, is checked for divisibility against
q
and
q/2
.
If the check is successful, the current chain is stored, otherwise it is discarded.

The function
MaximumSubdivisionNumber
(
X
) computes the maximum integer
r
such that
X
is the
r
-subdivision of some graph.
This is done by first obtaining the NB chains in
X
while keeping track of the lengths of each chain found, and then computing the greatest common divisor of all lengths.

The function
Collapse
(
X,q
) returns
\coll(X,q)
, the graph whose
q
-subdivision is
X
.
This is done in a single depth-first traversal of
X
, by adding a new edge to
\coll(X,q)
for every
q
edges of
X
visited.

Theorem 7.4
.

Fix a graph
X
with
m
edges and
n
nodes, and an integer
q>2
.
Let
k
be the number of NB chains in
X
whose lengths divide
q
or
q/2
.

Algorithm

1
computes formulas (
7.4
) and (
7.5
) in
O(m+k\log^{2}m)
time.

Proof.

Let
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}
be the lengths of the chains in
X
whose lengths divide
q
or
q/2
.
In the following we make the natural assumptions
q<m
and
n<m
, and note
p_{j}<\sum_{i}p_{i}<m
for each
j
.
Let us analyze
Algorithm

1
line by line.

Line 2 requires a depth-first traversal, whose worst case scenario time complexity is
O(m+n)
.
Additionally, Line 2 performs two integer divisions per chain.
Using the Euclidean algorithm for integer division on a chain with length
p
, this is upper bounded by
O\left((\log p+\log q)^{2}\right)
; see
[
24
,
14
]
.
Under our assumptions, this is
O(\log^{2}m)
.
Thus the total complexity of Line 2 is upper bounded by
O(m+n+k\log^{2}m)
.

Line 4 performs a depth-first traversal of
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{q}
, which is always faster than a traversal of
X
and is thus upper bounded by
O(m+n)
.

Line 6 takes constant time, as determining whether an integer is even can be done by looking at its last binary digit.

Lines 8, 14, 18, and 20 compute
|E(S_{q})|
and
|V(S_{q})|
, both of which can be computed in a single graph traversal taking
O(|E(S_{q})|+|V(S_{q})|)
time.
Since each of these lines is executed only once for each
S_{q}
, when the algorithm finishes running, the total time it takes to run these lines equals
O(|E(\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{q})|+|V(\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{q})|)
, upper bounded again by
O(m+n)
.

Line 12 computes the chains in
S_{q}
, taking
O(|E(S_{q})|+|V(S_{q})|)
time, and then computes the greatest common divisor among the lengths of said chains.
As it must perform this once for each
S_{q}
, the total time taken is upper bounded by
O(m+n+\sum_{i}(\log p_{i}+\log q)^{2})=O(m+n+k\log^{2}m)
.

Line 13 takes
O((\log r(S_{q})+\log q)^{2})
.
Accounting for each component
S_{q}
, this yields
O(k\log^{2}m)
.

Lines 16 and 17 take
O(|E(S_{q})|+|V(S_{q})|)
time each, for a total of
O(m+n)
when the algorithm finishes running.

All other lines take constant time.
The assumption
n<m
yields the result.
∎

Remark
.

Note that if the numbers
q,p_{i},m
fit in a single-precision variable, the time complexity for integer division becomes upper bounded by
O(\log m)
instead of
O(\log^{2}m)
.
Furthermore, when the number of nodes of degree
2
in
X
is small compared to
m
, or equivalently when
k\ll m
, the time complexity is essentially linear for large enough
m
.
