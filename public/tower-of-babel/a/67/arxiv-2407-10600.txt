Spectral Properties of Infinitely Smooth Kernel Matrices in the Single Cluster Limit, with Applications to Multivariate Super-Resolution
Nuha Diab, Dmitry Batenkov

Abstract
We study the spectral properties of infinitely smooth multivariate kernel matrices when the nodes form a single cluster. We show that the geometry of the nodes plays an important role in the scaling of the eigenvalues of these kernel matrices. For the multivariate Dirichlet kernel matrix, we establish a criterion for the sampling set ensuring precise scaling of eigenvalues. Additionally, we identify specific sampling sets that satisfy this criterion. Finally, we discuss the implications of these results for the problem of super-resolution, i.e. stable recovery of sparse measures from bandlimited Fourier measurements.



Keywords—
kernel matrices, flat limit, dirichlet kernel, eigenvalues, multivariate Vandermonde matrices, super-resolution

Introduction

Super-Resolution Problem.
The problem of super-resolution (SR) is to recover the fine details of a signal from inherently low resolution measurements in the frequency domain. The SR problem has a variety of applications in signal processing, imaging, optics, inverse scattering, and data analysis problems
[
9
,
28
]
. In the last years, a particular SR model has received considerable attention, where the signal is modelled as a sparse measure
[
6
]
. Let
\mathbb{T}^{d}:=[-\pi,\pi)^{d}\equiv\left(\mathbb{R}\mod 2\pi\right)^{d}
, let
\delta(\cdot)
denote the Dirac delta distribution
(in the sense of generalized functions
[
42
]
)
, and consider the signal

f(t):=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}\delta(t-x_{j}),\quad t,x_{j}\in\mathbb{T}^{d},\quad\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{C}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}.}

(1)

We refer to
\{x_{j}\}
as the ”nodes” and to
\{\alpha_{j}\}
as the ”coefficients” or “amplitudes”. The noisy measurements are given by

\hat{f}(\omega):=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}e^{\imath\langle\omega,x_{j}\rangle}+\epsilon(\omega),\quad\omega\in{\cal G}_{N}:=\{-N+1,\dots,-1,0,1,\dots,N-1\}^{d}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d},\quad|\epsilon(\omega)|\leq\epsilon.

(2)

The problem of super-resolution is, given discrete noisy Fourier measurements (
2
),
recover the parameters of model (
1
), i.e.
\{\alpha_{j},x_{j}\}
.

Moreover, define
\text{SRF}:=\frac{1}{N\Delta}
, where
\Delta
represents the smallest distance (e.g. in the infinity norm) between the nodes. It is well-known that, at least in the one-dimensional case, this quantity controls the numerical stability of the problem. The super-resolution regime is when
\text{SRF}\gg 1
; while the well-separated case is when
\text{SRF}\leq 1
. In the super-resolution regime, nodes are organized in clusters, with a distance of order
\Delta
between any two nodes within a cluster.

One primary objective in theory of super-resolution is to define optimal bounds for reconstruction errors, commonly referred to as min-max error bounds, or the computational resolution limits
[
14
,
29
,
26
,
12
,
6
]
. These bounds are achieved by the
(intractable) “oracle”
algorithm under the worst-case scenario. Furthermore, these stability bounds serve to validate the optimality of different tractable solution methods, offering assurances regarding the methods’ performance
[
27
,
21
,
22
]
. It has been shown previously that the min-max bounds are related to the smallest singular value of the Vandermonde matrix
U
of the system
[
12
]
, which is defined as

U(x_{1},\dots,x_{n};{\cal G}_{N}):=\big[\exp{\imath\langle\omega,x_{j}\rangle}\big]_{\omega\in{\cal G}_{N}}^{j=1,\dots,n}\in\mathbb{C}^{|{\cal G}_{N}|\times n}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0},\ \langle\omega,x_{j}\rangle:=\omega^{*}x_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\omega_{k}^{*}x_{j,k},}

(3)

where
\omega^{*}
denotes the conjugate transpose of
\omega
.

Extensive research has been conducted on the one-dimensional
(d=1)
scenario, covering both well-separated and super-resolution regimes
[
8
,
26
,
11
,
34
]
.
Let
\ell
be the number of nodes in the largest cluster. Then, in the super-resolution regime, the smallest singular value of the Vandermonde matrix scales like
\text{SRF}^{1-\ell}

whenever
N\Delta\geq c(n)
, resulting in “on-grid” min-max bounds to be of order
\text{SRF}^{2\ell-1}\epsilon

[
7
,
26
]
, (cf. similar off-grid bounds
[
6
,
30
]
).
In the multidimensional context, several results are available for the well-separated regime as well as the super-resolution regime
[
29
,
24
,
23
,
17
,
35
]
, under specific conditions related to the unknown nodes. A key distinction between the one-dimensional and multidimensional cases lies in the spectrum of the Vandermonde matrix, wherein the geometry of the nodes plays a crucial role. As we shall show in this paper, and consistent with the observations in
[
23
]
, it is not solely the distance between the nodes that determines the spectral properties of the Vandermonde matrix, at least in the near-colliding limit, but also the algebraic variety on which these nodes are situated.

Kernel matrices in the flat limit.
Following the previous discussion, let us consider the Gramian matrix
D_{N}:=\frac{1}{{(2N)}^{d}}U^{*}U
, which is the “kernel matrix” for the multidimensional Dirichlet kernel. Kernel matrices are of importance in various fields, including scattered data approximation and machine learning, see e.g.
[
1
,
44
,
43
]
and references therein. In the context of super-resolution, if all the nodes
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
form a single cluster and
\text{SRF}\gg 1
, then the kernel matrix
D_{N}
can be considered in the so-called “flat limit”, a term introduced in
[
15
]
in the context of radial basis function (RBF) interpolation. The flat limit was investigated recently in various publications, from among those
[
1
]
and
[
44
]
being most relevant to our work. While
[
44
]
deals exclusively with RBF kernels and derives asymptotic eigenvalue scaling,
[
1
]
extends those results to arbitrary smooth kernels, albeit under the assumption of the points not lying on any low-dimensional algebraic variety. The approach from
[
44
]
is primarily based on Micchelli’s lemma
[
31
]
, and we have adopted this approach previously in the one-dimensional case
[
8
]
.

Contributions.
The main result of this paper, Theorem
3.1

This generalizes
the corresponding result of
[
1
]
to arbitrary geometry,
and the result in Theorem 8 of

to arbitrary smooth kernels.
. As a corollary, we derive the asymptotic scaling of the eigenvalues of

In more detail, suppose that

with

which depends on the algebraic properties of the original nodes

\mathcal{S}

satisfying

), see Definition

or are ’lower sets’ as described in Definition

.

To demonstrate the relevance of our findings to the multivariate SR problem, in Section
6
we also report on a numerical study of reconstructing the signals from noisy measurements using the nonlinear least squares (NLS) method and the multidimensional ESPRIT method
[
37
]
. Our results indeed suggest that the asymptotic conditioning of the problem may vary from the “worst-case” single-line scenario (previously considered in
[
29
]
), to the “best-case” general position scenario. We are therefore confident that our findings will enhance the understanding of super-resolution stability in high-dimensional settings and can contribute to the analysis of current multivariate SR recovery methods such as
[
13
,

,
39
,

,
37
]
. Furthermore, we believe these results can serve as an important step towards establishing min-max error bounds in the general multidimensional scenario, when the nodes form multiple clusters (where some kind of multidimensional “confluent” Vandermonde matrices may be required, cf.
[
3
,
2

5

Preliminaries

We adapt some definitions and notation from
[
1
]
.
For a multi-index
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{+}
, denote

\big|\boldsymbol{\alpha}\big|:=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\alpha_{j},\quad\boldsymbol{\alpha}!:=\alpha_{1}!\cdot\cdot\cdot\alpha_{d}!,

and let for each
j=0,1,2,\dots

\mathbb{P}_{j}:=\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{+}:\big|\boldsymbol{\alpha}\big|\leq j\},\quad\mathbb{H}_{j}:=\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{+}:\big|\boldsymbol{\alpha}\big|=j\}=\mathbb{P}_{j}\setminus\mathbb{P}_{j-1},\quad\mathbb{P}_{-1}:=\emptyset.

The cardinalities of these sets are given by:

p_{j}:=\#\mathbb{P}_{j}={j+d\choose d},\quad h_{j}:=\#\mathbb{H}_{j}={j+d-1\choose d-1}=\#\mathbb{P}_{j}-\#\mathbb{P}_{j-1}.

(4)

For
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{+}
, the monomial
\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
is defined as
\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}:=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdot\cdot\cdot x_{d}^{\alpha_{d}}
, and its (total) degree is
|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|
. Let
\prec
be a fixed graded order on the set of multi-indices
\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{+}
(or, equivalently, on the set of monomials), for example a graded lexicographic order or a graded reverse lexicographic order.
The ordering
\prec
is used throughout the sequel.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a fixed open set. A kernel function
\mathcal{K}:\Omega\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is said to belong to the class
\mathcal{C}^{(r,r)}(\Omega)
(or
\mathcal{C}^{\mathbb{P}_{r}\times\mathbb{P}_{r}}(\Omega)
) when the partial derivatives

\mathcal{K}^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha,\beta})}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}):=\frac{\partial^{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|+|\boldsymbol{\beta}|}}{\partial x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdot\cdot\cdot\partial x_{d}^{\alpha_{d}}\partial y_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\cdot\cdot\cdot\partial y_{d}^{\beta_{d}}}\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})

exist and are continuous on
\Omega\times\Omega
, for all
\boldsymbol{\alpha,\beta}\in\mathbb{P}_{r}
. When
\mathcal{K}\in\mathcal{C}^{(r,r)}(\Omega)
for all
r\in\mathbb{N}
, we write
\mathcal{K}\in\mathcal{C}^{(\infty,\infty)}(\Omega)
.

Definition 2.2
.

For a kernel
\mathcal{K}\in\mathcal{C}^{(r,r)}(\Omega)
, a finite set
{\cal X}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\}}\subset\Omega
and
\epsilon>0
we define the scaled kernel
\mathcal{K}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}):=\mathcal{K}(\epsilon\boldsymbol{x},\epsilon\boldsymbol{y})
and the kernel matrix

K_{\epsilon}({\cal X}):=\big[\mathcal{K}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x^{\prime}})\big]_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x^{\prime}}\in{\cal X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n\times n}.

(5)

Definition 2.3

(Multivariate Vandermonde matrix)
.

For a finite set
{\cal X}=\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of nodes, and an
\prec
-ordered set of multi-indices
\mathcal{A}=\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{m}\}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{+}
(i.e.
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}\prec\dots\prec\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{m}
), we define the multivariate Vandermonde matrix as

V_{\mathcal{A}}=V_{\mathcal{A}}({\cal X})=\big[(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}}\big]_{1\leq i\leq n}^{{1\leq j\leq m}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}.

(6)

Note that
V
is a multivariate real Vandermonde matrix with monomial entries, in contrast to the complex Vandermonde matrix
U
defined in (
3
), whose entries are of the form
e^{\imath\langle\omega,x\rangle}
.

Let us further denote
V_{\leq k}:=V_{\mathbb{P}_{k}}({\cal X})
and
V_{k}:=V_{\mathbb{H}_{k}}({\cal X})
. The matrix
V_{\leq k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p_{k}}
can be partitioned into
k+1
matrices
V_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times h_{k}}
arranged by increasing degree:

V_{\leq k}=\big[V_{0}\quad V_{1}\quad\dots\quad V_{k}\big].

(7)

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\mathcal{K}\in\mathcal{C}^{(r,r)}(\Omega)
, with
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbf{0}\in\Omega
. Let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{+}
be two sets of multi-indices satisfying
|\alpha|,|\beta|\leq r
for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{A},\beta\in\mathcal{B}
. The Wronskian matrix of
\mathcal{K}
is defined as:

W^{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}=\bigg[{{\mathcal{K}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(0,0)}\over{\alpha!\beta!}}\bigg]_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A},\beta\in\mathcal{B}},

(8)

where the rows and columns are indexed by multi-indices in
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
according to the chosen ordering. In addition, denote by
W^{\mathcal{K}}_{\leq k}=W^{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathbb{P}_{k}}=W^{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathbb{P}_{k},\mathbb{P}_{k}}
.

The final piece of notation is central to our discussion, describing a geometric property of
{\cal X}
.

Definition 2.5

(Discrete moment order,
[
41
]
)
.

The
discrete moment order
of a set
{\cal X}
consisting of
n
pairwise distinct points, is the smallest number
m=\mu(\mathcal{X})
such that

\ker(V_{\leq m}({\cal X})^{T})=\emptyset

holds, or equivalently,

\rank(V_{\leq m}({\cal X}))=n

holds.

Note that such
m
exists (is finite). Indeed, if
{\cal X}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
, then
\mu({\cal X})\leq n-1
because the following Lagrange interpolation polynomials, each of total degree
n-1
, are linearly independent:

\biggl\{\ell_{i}(x)=\prod_{j\neq i}\frac{H_{j}(x)}{H_{j}(x_{i})}\biggr\}_{i=1}^{n},

where
H_{j}
is some affine hyperplane containing
x_{j}
and not containing every other
x_{k},k\neq j
.
Cf.
[
41
, Definition 3]
,
[
18
]
and
[
44
]
.

3
Eigenvalues of smooth kernel matrices in the flat limit

In this section, we consider the kernel matrix
K_{\epsilon}=K_{\epsilon}({\cal X})
for a fixed infinitely smooth kernel
\mathcal{K}
and a fixed set
{\cal X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of
n
pairwise distinct points. Our main interest is in the asymptotic order of the decay of the eigenvalues of
K_{\epsilon}
in the flat limit
\epsilon\to 0
. We employ the standard
O(\cdot)
notation.

Our main result gives an upper bound on the decay rates of all the eigenvalues.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
m=\mu({\cal X})
be the discrete moment order of the set
{\cal X}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}}
. For any infinitely smooth kernel
\mathcal{K}\in\mathcal{C^{(\infty,\infty)}}(\Omega)
with
\mathbf{0}\in\Omega
, and small enough
\epsilon
, the eigenvalues of
K_{\epsilon}({\cal X})
split into
m+1
groups

\displaystyle\lambda_{0,0}

\displaystyle=O(1),\quad\{\lambda_{1,j}\}_{j=1}^{t_{1}}=O(\epsilon^{2}),\quad\dots\quad,\quad\{\lambda_{m,j}\}_{j=1}^{t_{m}}=O(\epsilon^{2m}),

\displaystyle t_{k}

\displaystyle:=\rank(V_{\leq k}({\cal X}))-\rank(V_{\leq k-1}({\cal X})),\;k=1,2,\dots,m.

Remark 3.2
.

Theorem (
3.1
) is valid also for kernels in
\mathcal{C}^{(m+1,m+1)}
.

Proof.

We start by writing the Taylor expansion of
K_{\epsilon}
around 0 as equation (55) in
[
1
]
:

K_{\epsilon}=V_{\leq m}\Delta_{m}W\Delta_{m}V_{\leq m}^{T}+\epsilon^{m+1}(V_{\leq m}\Delta_{m}W_{1}(\epsilon)+W_{2}(\epsilon)\Delta_{m}V_{\leq m}^{T})+\epsilon^{2{(m+1)}}W_{3}(\epsilon)

(9)

where
W_{i}(\epsilon)=O(1)
,
W=W^{\mathcal{K}}_{\leq m}
and
\Delta_{m}(\epsilon)=\diag(1,\epsilon I_{h_{1}},\dots,\epsilon^{m}I_{h_{m}})\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{m}\times p_{m}}
.
For the full derivation of (
9
), see section
A
in the Appendix.

Let
V=V_{\leq m}
where
V=[V_{0}\dots V_{m}]=[V_{\leq m-1}\quad V_{m}]
, and
t_{k}:=\rank(V_{\leq k})-\rank(V_{\leq k-1})
as defined in the theorem.
In
[
1
]
only the generic case
t_{k}=h_{k}-h_{k-1}
is considered. Here we generalize Theorem 6.1 in
[
1
]
to the case where the multivariate Vandermonde matrices

are not necessarily full rank.

Consider the full QR decomposition of
V_{\leq m-1}
with column pivoting, as elaborated in Section 5.4.2, specifically, formula (5.4.6) in the book
[
19
]
:

V_{\leq m-1}P_{m-1}=[Q_{\leq m-1}\quad Q_{\perp}]\begin{bmatrix}R_{11}&R_{12}\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}=Q\begin{bmatrix}R\\
0\end{bmatrix}

where
Q_{\leq m-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r_{m-1}}
,
Q_{\perp}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times t_{m}}
,
R_{11}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{m-1}\times r_{m-1}}
,
R\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{m-1}\times p_{m-1}}
,
r_{m-1}+t_{m}=n
and
P_{m-1}
is a permutation matrix.

We have
[Q_{\leq m-1}\quad Q_{\perp}]^{T}V_{\leq m}P=\begin{bmatrix}R&Q_{\leq m-1}^{T}V_{m}\\
0&Q_{\perp}^{T}V_{m}\end{bmatrix}

and
Q_{\perp}^{T}V_{\leq m}P=[0\quad Q_{\perp}^{T}V_{m}]
where
P:=\begin{bmatrix}P_{m-1}&0\\
0&I_{h_{m}}\end{bmatrix}
. Note that for every permutation matrix, we have
P^{-1}=P^{T}
.

Thus we can rewrite the expansion (
9
) as follows:

\displaystyle K_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\quad V_{\leq m}PP^{T}\Delta_{m}PP^{T}WPP^{T}\Delta_{m}PP^{T}V_{\leq m}^{T}

\displaystyle\quad+\epsilon^{m+1}(V_{\leq m}PP^{T}\Delta_{m}PP^{T}W_{1}(\epsilon)+W_{2}(\epsilon)PP^{T}\Delta_{m}PP^{T}V_{\leq m}^{T})

\displaystyle\quad+\epsilon^{2{(m+1)}}W_{3}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle K_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\tilde{V}_{\leq m}\tilde{\Delta}_{m}\tilde{W}\tilde{\Delta}_{m}\tilde{V}_{\leq m}^{T}+\epsilon^{m+1}(\tilde{V}_{\leq m}\tilde{\Delta}_{m}\tilde{W}_{1}(\epsilon)+\tilde{W}_{2}(\epsilon)\tilde{\Delta}_{m}\tilde{V}_{\leq m}^{T})+\epsilon^{2{(m+1)}}W_{3}(\epsilon)

where
\tilde{V}_{\leq m}:=V_{\leq m}P
,
\tilde{\Delta}_{m}:=P^{T}\Delta_{m}P={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\diag(\epsilon^{i_{1}},...,\epsilon^{i_{q_{m-1}}},\epsilon^{m}I_{h_{m}})}
,

Multiplying by

\displaystyle Q_{\perp}^{T}K_{\epsilon}Q_{\perp}

\displaystyle=\quad[0\quad Q_{\perp}^{T}V_{m}]\tilde{\Delta}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}m}\tilde{W}\tilde{\Delta}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}m}[0\quad Q_{\perp}^{T}V_{m}]^{T}

\displaystyle\quad+\epsilon^{m+1}[0\quad Q_{\perp}^{T}V_{m}]\tilde{\Delta}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}m}\tilde{W}_{1}Q_{\perp}

\displaystyle\quad+\epsilon^{m+1}Q_{\perp}^{T}\tilde{W}_{2}\tilde{\Delta}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}m}[0\quad Q_{\perp}^{T}V_{m}]^{T}

\displaystyle Q_{\perp}^{T}K_{\epsilon}Q_{\perp}

\displaystyle=\quad O(\epsilon^{2m})Q_{\perp}^{T}V_{m}\hat{W}V_{m}^{T}Q_{\perp}

\displaystyle\quad+O(\epsilon^{2m+1})(Q_{\perp}^{T}V_{m}\hat{W_{1}}Q_{\perp}

\displaystyle\quad+Q^{T}_{\perp}\hat{W_{2}}V_{m}Q_{\perp})

is the lower right

For any
k=1,...,m-1
, let
V_{\leq k-1}P_{k-1}=[Q_{\leq k-1}\quad Q_{\perp,k-1}]\begin{bmatrix}R_{\leq k-1}\\
0\end{bmatrix}

with pivoting, where
Q_{\leq k-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times[n-(t_{k}+\dots+t_{m})]}

Q_{\perp,k-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times[t_{k}+\dots+t_{m}]}

R_{\leq k-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{[n-(t_{k}+\dots+t_{m})]\times p_{k-1}}

Q_{\perp,k-1}

\sum_{i=0}^{m}t_{i}=n

exactly

t_{k}
eigenvalues decaying (at least) like
\epsilon^{2k}
when
\epsilon
goes to 0.
∎

The bounds of Theorem

Definition 3.3
.

Let
{\cal X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Consider the matrices
Q_{\perp,k-1}

We define a sequence
\{\mathcal{M}_{k}\}_{k=0}^{m}

\dim\mathcal{M}_{k}=t_{k}

\mathcal{M}_{k}\subseteq\operatorname{range}(Q_{\perp,k-1})
and
\mathcal{M}_{k}\perp\operatorname{range}(Q_{\perp,k})
. By a slight abuse of notation, we also denote by
\mathcal{M}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times t_{k}}
a matrix whose columns span
\mathcal{M}_{k}
. For consistency we set
\operatorname{range}(Q_{\perp,-1})\equiv\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since
\sum_{i=0}^{m}t_{i}=n
, we have

\mathcal{Q}_{\perp}:=\big(\mathcal{M}_{0}\;\mathcal{M}_{1}\;\dots\mathcal{M}_{m}\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}.

The matrix
\mathcal{Q}_{\perp}^{T}V_{\leq m}
is upper triangluar. We denote its block-diagonal part by

\tilde{R}:=\operatorname{blkdiag}\left(\mathcal{Q}_{\perp}^{T}V_{\leq m}\right)=\diag\bigl\{\mathcal{M}_{0}^{T}V_{0},...,\mathcal{M}_{m}^{T}V_{m}\bigr\}.

Each diagonal block of
\tilde{R}
is full rank by definition, therefore
\tilde{R}
is full rank.

Lemma 3.4
.

For
\mathcal{K}\in\mathcal{C^{(\infty,\infty)}}(\Omega)
and
{\cal X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
\tilde{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p_{m}}
be as in Definition
3.3
. Define
C=\det\left(\tilde{R}W\tilde{R}^{T}\right)\in\mathbb{R}
where
W=W^{\mathcal{K}}_{\leq m}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{m}\times p_{m}}
is the Wronskian matrix of
\mathcal{K}
as in Definition
2.4
. Then

\det(K_{\epsilon}({\cal X}))=\epsilon^{2\sum_{i=0}^{m}it_{i}}(C+O(\epsilon)),\quad\epsilon\to 0.

Proof.

By
[
1
, Lemma 6.4]
we have

E_{n}^{-1}\mathcal{Q}_{\perp}^{T}V_{\leq m}\Delta_{m}=\tilde{R}+O(\epsilon)

where
E_{n}=\diag\{1,\epsilon I_{t_{1}},...,\epsilon^{m}I_{t_{m}}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
\tilde{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p_{m}}
.

Thus, using (
9
), we get that

\displaystyle E_{n}^{-1}\mathcal{Q}_{\perp}^{T}K_{\epsilon}\mathcal{Q}_{\perp}E_{n}^{-1}

\displaystyle=(\tilde{R}+O(\epsilon))W(\tilde{R}^{T}+O(\epsilon))

\displaystyle+\epsilon^{m+1}((\tilde{R}+O(\epsilon))W_{1}(\epsilon)\mathcal{Q}_{\perp}E_{n}^{-1}

\displaystyle+E_{n}^{-1}\mathcal{Q}_{\perp}^{T}W_{2}(\epsilon)(\tilde{R}^{T}+O(\epsilon)))

\displaystyle+\epsilon^{2{(m+1)}}E_{n}^{-1}\mathcal{Q}_{\perp}^{T}W_{3}(\epsilon)\mathcal{Q}_{\perp}E_{n}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\tilde{R}W\tilde{R}^{T}+O(\epsilon)

where the last equality follows from
\epsilon^{2{(m+1)}}E_{n}^{-1}=O(\epsilon)
and
\tilde{R}W\tilde{R}^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.
This implies that
\epsilon^{-2\sum_{j=0}^{m}jt_{j}}\det(K_{\epsilon})=\det(\tilde{R}W\tilde{R}^{T}+O(\epsilon))=\det(\tilde{R}W\tilde{R}^{T})+O(\epsilon)
(by Lemma (
B.2
) in the Appendix) with
C=\det(\tilde{R}W\tilde{R}^{T})
.

∎

Corollary 3.5
.

Suppose
\mathcal{K}
is symmetric and analytic (in both variables) in the neighborhood of
(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0})
. The scaling of the eigenvalues as given in Theorem
3.1
is exact, if and only if
\det(\tilde{R}W\tilde{R}^{T})\neq 0
.
By ’exact scaling’ we mean that there exists a constant
\tilde{\lambda}_{k,j}\neq 0
independent of
\epsilon
such that
\lambda_{k,j}=\epsilon^{2k}(\tilde{\lambda}_{k,j}+O(\epsilon))
.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.1
and
[
1
, Theorem 2.9]
, the eigenvalues of
K_{\epsilon}({\cal X})
satisfy

\lambda_{0,0}=\epsilon^{0}(\tilde{\lambda}_{0,0}+O(\epsilon)),\quad\{\lambda_{1,j}\}_{j=1}^{t_{1}}=\{\epsilon^{2}(\tilde{\lambda}_{1,j}+O(\epsilon))\}_{j=1}^{t_{1}},...,\{\lambda_{m,j}\}_{j=1}^{t_{m}}=\{\epsilon^{2m}(\tilde{\lambda}_{m,j}+O(\epsilon))\}_{j=1}^{t_{m}},

where
t_{k}:=\rank(V_{\leq k})-\rank(V_{\leq k-1})
and
m=\mu({\cal X})
, and
\tilde{\lambda}_{s,j}
do not depend on
\epsilon
(but may be zero). By Lemma
3.4
we have that

\big|K_{\epsilon}({\cal X})\big|=\epsilon^{2\sum_{i=0}^{m}it_{i}}\biggl\{\big|\tilde{R}W\tilde{R}^{T}\big|+O(\epsilon)\biggr\}.

On the other hand, the determinant is the product of eigenvalues, therefore

\displaystyle\big|K_{\epsilon}({\cal X})\big|

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}=\lambda_{0,0}\prod_{j=1}^{t_{1}}\lambda_{1,j}...\prod_{j=1}^{t_{m}}\lambda_{m,j}=(\tilde{\lambda}_{0,0}+O(\epsilon))\prod_{j=1}^{t_{1}}\epsilon^{2}(\tilde{\lambda}_{1,j}+O(\epsilon))...\prod_{j=1}^{t_{m}}\epsilon^{2m}(\tilde{\lambda}_{m,j}+O(\epsilon))

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{2\sum_{i=0}^{m}it_{i}}\biggl\{\prod_{s=1}^{m}\prod_{j=1}^{t_{s}}\tilde{\lambda}_{s,j}+O(\epsilon)\biggr\}.

Now we just compare the two expressions above. In one direction, if the scaling is exact then
\tilde{\lambda}_{s,j}\neq 0
for all
0\leq s\leq m
and
0\leq j\leq t_{s}
, which implies
\left|\tilde{R}W\tilde{R}^{T}\right|\neq 0
. In the other direction, if there are
\ell\geq 1
zeros
\tilde{\lambda}_{k}\in\{\tilde{\lambda}_{s,j}\}_{s=1,\dots,m}^{j=1,\dots,t_{m}}
and
\tilde{\lambda}_{k}=0
for
k=1,...,\ell
, then
\big|K_{\epsilon}\big|=\epsilon^{\ell+2\sum_{i=0}^{m}it_{i}}(\prod_{k=\ell+1}^{n}\tilde{\lambda}_{k}+O(\epsilon))
, forcing
\left|\tilde{R}W\tilde{R}^{T}\right|=0
.
∎

4
Eigenvalues of the multidimensional Dirichlet kernel

In this section we consider the specific case of the multidimensional Dirichlet kernel, which is directly motivated by the problem of super-resolution as outlined in the Introduction. Let
{\cal S}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote a finite set of frequencies, symmetric around the origin, where the spectral data (
2
) is available. We first derive a generic sufficient condition on
{\cal S}
which ensures that the scaling order of Theorem
3.1
is exact. Later we show that this condition is satisfied by
{\cal S}={\cal G}_{N}
, the regular symmetric grid in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of side length
2N+1
, for large enough
N
.

Definition 4.1
.

For
{\cal S}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, symmetric around
\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the (real-valued) Dirichlet kernel
\mathcal{D}_{{\cal S}}
is defined as follows:

\mathcal{D}_{{\cal S}}(x)=\sum_{\omega\in{\cal S}}\exp\left(\imath\langle x,\omega\rangle\right).

By a slight abuse of notation, we also denote by
\mathcal{D}_{{\cal S}}
the corresponding bi-multivariate kernel:

\mathcal{D}_{{\cal S}}(x,y)=\mathcal{D}_{{\cal S}}(x-y)=\sum_{\omega\in{\cal S}}\exp\left(\imath\langle x-y,\omega\rangle\right).

(10)

Given
{\cal X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\epsilon>0
, the corresponding Dirichlet kernel matrix and its flat limit version are denoted as:

\displaystyle\begin{split}D({\cal X},{\cal S})&:=\big[\mathcal{D}_{{\cal S}}(x,x^{\prime})\big]_{x,x^{\prime}\in{\cal X}},\\
D_{\epsilon}({\cal X},{\cal S})&:=D(\epsilon{\cal X},{\cal S})=\big[\mathcal{D}_{{\cal S}}(\epsilon x,\epsilon x^{\prime})\big]_{x,x^{\prime}\in{\cal X}}.\end{split}

(11)

In addition, if
{\cal S}={\cal S}_{1}\times...\times{\cal S}_{d}
is a tensor product grid, we have
\mathcal{D}_{{\cal S}}(x,y)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\mathcal{D}_{{\cal S}_{j}}(x_{j},y_{j})
.

4.1
A nondegeneracy condition

Note that
\mathcal{D}_{{\cal S}}
is a translation invariant, symmetric and analytic kernel. Recall the definition (
8
) of the Wronskian matrix for an arbitrary kernel. Set
W:=W^{\mathcal{D_{S}}}_{\leq m}
. By Corollary
3.5
, in order to show tightness, it is sufficient to demonstrate that
C:=\det(\tilde{R}W\tilde{R}^{T})\neq 0
. In the following lemma, we show that this holds under a certain (more or less natural) non-degeneracy assumption on the set
{\cal S}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For
W:=W_{\leq m}
as defined above,
\det(\tilde{R}W\tilde{R}^{T})>0
holds whenever
\rank(V_{\leq m}({\cal S}))\geq n
,
where
V_{\leq m}({\cal S})=\big[{(\omega)}^{\alpha}\big]_{\omega\in{\cal S}}^{\alpha\in\mathbb{P}_{m}}
.

Remark 4.3
.

Note that contrary to Section
3
, the Vandermonde matrix
V_{\leq m}
is here evaluated on the set
{\cal S}
(and not on
{\cal X}
), while the polynomial total degree is still
m=\mu({\cal X})
.

Proof of Lemma
4.2
.

We first show that
W
is positive semidefinite. Directly from (
8
) and (
10
), we have

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=\bigg[{{\mathcal{D}_{{\cal S}}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(0,0)}\over{\alpha!\beta!}}\bigg]_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{P}_{m}}=\bigg[\sum_{\omega\in{\cal S}}{{(i\omega)^{\alpha}(-i\omega)^{\beta}}\over{\alpha!\beta!}}\bigg]_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{P}_{m}}

\displaystyle=\bigg[\sum_{\omega=(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{d})\in{\cal S}}{{(i)^{|\alpha|}(-i)^{|\beta|}\omega_{1}^{\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1}}\cdot...\cdot\omega_{d}^{\alpha_{d}+\beta_{d}}}\over{\alpha!\beta!}}\bigg]_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{P}_{m}}

\displaystyle=FV^{T}_{\leq m}({\cal S})V_{\leq m}({\cal S})F^{*},

where
F:=\diag\left(\frac{i^{|\alpha|}}{\alpha!}\right)_{\alpha\in\mathbb{P}_{m}}
. Let
B:=FV^{T}_{\leq m}({\cal S})
, and consider
B=U\Sigma T^{*}
the singular value decomposition of
B
. We therefore have
W=BB^{*}=U\Sigma^{2}U^{*}
, implying that
W
is positive semidefinite (since
W
is Hermitian and has non-negative eigenvalues). Since
\tilde{R}
is full rank
(recall Definition
3.3
)
, it follows that
\tilde{R}W\tilde{R}^{T}
is positive semidefinite as well, implying that
\det(\tilde{R}W\tilde{R}^{T})\geq 0
. To ensure
\det(\tilde{R}W\tilde{R}^{T})>0
, we require that
\tilde{R}W\tilde{R}^{T}
be positive definite. The latter condition is satisfied when
\rank(\tilde{R}W\tilde{R}^{T})\geq n
, which is equivalent to
\rank(W)=\rank(V_{\leq m}({\cal S}))\geq n
.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

For
d=1
, we have that
m=n-1
and
p_{n-1}={n\choose 1}=n
, meaning that the condition
\rank(V_{\leq n-1}({\cal S}))\geq n
in Lemma
4.2
is satisfied as long as the sampling set
{\cal S}
contains at least
n
distinct points.

By applying Corollary
3.5
, we immediately obtain the following result.

Lemma 4.5
.

Assume that the sampling set
{\cal S}
satisfies the condition
\rank(V_{\leq m}({\cal S}))\geq n
. Then the eigenvalues of
D_{\epsilon}({\cal X},{\cal S})
split into
m+1
groups

\lambda_{0,0}=\epsilon^{0}(\tilde{\lambda}_{0,j}+O(\epsilon)),\quad\{\lambda_{1,j}\}_{j=1}^{t_{1}}=\{\epsilon^{2}(\tilde{\lambda}_{1,j}+O(\epsilon))\}_{j=1}^{t_{1}},...,\{\lambda_{m,j}\}_{j=1}^{t_{m}}=\{\epsilon^{2m}(\tilde{\lambda}_{m,j}+O(\epsilon))\}_{j=1}^{t_{m}},

where
t_{k}:=\rank(V_{\leq k}({\cal X}))-\rank(V_{\leq k-1}({\cal X}))
and
\tilde{\lambda}_{s,j}\neq 0
for all
0\leq s\leq m
and
0\leq j\leq t_{s}
.

4.2
Geometric characterization condition and the uniform grid

The rank condition in Lemma
4.2
is nontrivial to verify for a given set
{\cal S}
. In this section we provide an explicit example of such a set, namely, the uniform grid
{\cal G}_{N}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Our approach is a straightforward application of well-known results in multivariate polynomial interpolation, adapted to our setting.

We first recall some definitions and results from
[
38
]
.
For a set
\Theta
, let
\#\Theta
denote its cardinality. For a square matrix
M
, let
|M|
denote its determinant, i.e.
|M|:=\det(M)
.

Definition 4.6

(
[
38
]
, p.194)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a linear subspace of the polynomial ring
\Pi:=\mathbb{R}\left[x_{1},...,x_{d}\right]
. The polynomial interpolation problem with respect to a (finite) set of linearly independent functionals
\Theta\subset\Pi^{\prime}
(
\Pi^{\prime}
is the dual space
of
\Pi
) is said to be
poised
for
\mathcal{P}
, if for any
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{\#\Theta}
there exists a unique
f\in\mathcal{P}
such that
\Theta f=Y
.

Theorem 4.7

(
[
38
]
p.194)
.

For
\mathcal{P}\subset\Pi
, and a finite set
\Theta\subset\Pi^{\prime}
as above, the following statements are equivalent:

1.

The polynomial interpolation problem with respect to
\Theta
is poised for
\mathcal{P}
.

2.

\dim\mathcal{P}=\#\Theta
and
\Theta P=\big[\theta p:\theta\in\Theta,p\in P\big]
satisfies
|\Theta P|\neq 0
for any basis
P
of
\mathcal{P}
.

For
\Theta_{{\cal S}}:=\{\delta_{\xi}\}_{\xi\in{\cal S}}
the set of point evaluation functionals on
{\cal S}
, and the standard monomial basis
P_{n}
of polynomials of total degree
\leq n
, we have in particular
\dim P_{n}=\#{\cal S}={{n+d}\choose d}=p_{n}
, and the matrix
\Theta_{{\cal S}}P_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}=V:=V_{\leq n}({\cal S})
is our Vandermonde matrix. Therefore, if we can find a sample set
{\cal S}
such that the polynomial interpolation problem with respect to
\Theta_{{\cal S}}
is poised for
\mathcal{P}_{n}:=\text{span}\{P_{n}\}
, this would immediately imply that
|V|\neq 0
. Examples of such situations are provided in
[
10
,
18
]
, and it turns out that there is a general sufficient condition which ensures poisedness.

Definition 4.8

(
[
10
]
)
.

A lattice
\mathcal{J}:=\{x_{1},...,x_{N}\}
of
N=p_{n}
distinct nodes of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is said to satisfy the
Geometric Characterization Condition (GC)
(of degree
n
) if corresponding to each node
x_{i}
there exist
n
distinct hyperplanes
G_{i1},...,G_{in}
such that:

1.

x_{i}
does not lie on any of these hyperplanes, and

2.

all the other nodes in
\mathcal{J}
lie on at least one of these hyperplanes.

The following result is fundamental for our purposes. We give a short proof for completeness.

Theorem 4.9

(Theorem 1 in
[
10
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{J}:=\{x_{1},...,x_{N}\}
be a lattice of
N=p_{n}
distinct nodes of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. If
\mathcal{J}
satisfies the GC condition as in Definition
4.8
, then the polynomial interpolation problem with respect to
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta_{\mathcal{J}}}
is poised for
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{P}_{n}}
.

Proof.

Let
Y=\{y_{i}\}_{i=1,...,N}
be an arbitrary vector of values. It can be directly verified that the following polynomial of total degree
\leq n
interpolates
Y
on
\mathcal{J}
:

P(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}y_{i}\prod_{j=1}^{n}{{G_{ij}(x)}\over{G_{ij}(x_{i})}},

where
G_{i1},\dots,G_{in}
are distinct hyperplanes associated with
x_{i}\in\mathcal{J}
, and they satisfy the conditions stated in Definition
4.8
.
Since
Y
was arbitrary, uniqueness follows.
∎

Next, we recall the definition of
n
-th order principal lattice
[
32
]
.

Definition 4.10
.

Let
I_{n}=\{0,{1\over n},{2\over n},...,1\}
. Further, denote by
X_{i}:=e_{i}
the simplex vertices with
e_{i}
being the
i
-th element in standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and furthermore put
X_{0}:=\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then the
n
-th order principal lattice (corresponding to the coordinate simplex) is defined as

B(n,d):=\biggl\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\Big|x=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\gamma_{i}X_{i},\;\sum_{i=0}^{d}\gamma_{i}=1,\;\gamma_{i}\in I_{n}\biggr\}.

Note that
B(n,d)
contains precisely
p_{n}={n+d\choose n}
points.

It was proven in
[
10
]
Theorem 4, that
B(n,d)
satisfies the GC condition which, by Theorem
4.9
, guarantees poisedness
[
18
]
. This is almost what we want, and indeed as we will show, the uniform grid contains a scaled principal lattice.

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
J_{n}:=\{0,1,...,n\}
, and put

A(n,d):=n\cdot B(n,d)=\biggl\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\Big|x=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\gamma_{i}X_{i},\sum_{i=0}^{d}\gamma_{i}=n,\gamma_{i}\in J_{n}\biggr\}\subset{\cal G}_{n}.

(12)

Then the set
A(n,d)
satisfies the GC condition of degree
n
.

Proof.

The proof is very similar to the one in
[
10
]
for
B(n,d)
, and we provide it for completeness.

Let
\{\gamma_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\}_{k=0,...,d}
be the barycentric coordinate functions associated with the simplex
X_{0},...,X_{d}
. Define the following hyperplanes
H_{km}:=\{x|\gamma_{k}(x)=m\}
,
k=0,...,d
,
m=0,...,n
. These hyperplanes contain all the nodes in
A(n,d)
.

Now let
x\in A(n,d)
be a lattice node, and let
\beta_{k}:=\gamma_{k}(x)
. For each
k=0,..,d
with
\beta_{k}>0
, the union of the hyperplanes
\{H_{kj}\}_{k=0,...,d}^{j=0,...,\beta_{k}-1}
doesn’t contain the node
x
. Since
\sum_{k=0}^{d}\beta_{k}=n
we have exactly
n
hyperplanes in the set
\{H_{kj}\}_{k=0,...,d}^{j=0,...,\beta_{k}-1}
.

We still have to show why the union of
\{H_{kj}\}_{k=0,...,d}^{j=0,...,\beta_{k}-1}
contains all the other nodes. Let
y\neq x\in A(n,d)
. Again, we have
\sum_{k=0}^{d}\gamma_{k}(y)=n
, therefore there is at least one
k\in\{0,...,d\}
such that
\gamma_{k}(y)<\gamma_{k}(x)=\beta_{k}
. ∎

To clarify the proof, we give an example for
d=2,n=2
. For the node
x=(0,1)
we have
\gamma_{0}(x)=1,\gamma_{1}(x)=0,\gamma_{2}(x)=1
because
x=1\cdot(0,0)+0\cdot(1,0)+1\cdot(0,1)
. Following the proof,
H_{00}
and
H_{20}
are the hyperplanes which contain all the nodes except for
x
(see figure
1
).

4.11
.

Remark 4.12
.

For the scaled principal lattice
A(n,d)
, the interpolation polynomial
P(x)
of degree
\leq n
for any
\{y_{a}\}_{a\in A(n,d)}
is given by:

P(x)=\sum_{a\in A(n,d)}y_{a}\prod_{k=0}^{d}\prod_{m=0}^{\gamma_{k}(a)-1}{{\gamma_{k}(x)-m}\over{\gamma_{k}(a)-m}}.

For any
t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote by
A_{t}
the shifted set
A_{t}:=\{a+t:a\in A\}
.
The following result is immediate.

Proposition 4.13
.

If
A
satisfies the GC condition of degree
n
, then
A_{t}
satisfies the GC condition of degree
n
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

We are finally in a position to state and prove the main result of this section.

Theorem 4.14
.

Let
{\cal X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a finite set of
n
distinct points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
m=\mu({\cal X})
be its discrete moment order. Let
\ell
be the unique integer such that
p_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}2\ell-2}<n\leq p_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}2\ell-1}
. Then for every
N\geq\ell
we have

\rank(V_{\leq m}({\cal G}_{N}))\geq n,

so the conclusion of Lemma
4.5
holds for
{\cal S}={\cal G}_{N}
.

Proof.

Let
s=-(\ell-1,\dots,\ell-1)^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and define
A_{s}(2\ell-1,d):=A(2\ell-1,d)+s
. By Lemma
4.11
and Proposition
4.13
we have that
A_{s}(2\ell-1,d)
satisfies the GC condition of degree
2\ell-1
.

Using
Theorem
4.9
(or alternatively Corollary
4.18
) we get that the polynomial interpolation problem with respect to
\Theta_{A_{s}(2\ell-1,d)}
is poised for
\text{span}\{P_{2\ell-1}\}
, thus by Theorem
4.7
we get

\displaystyle\big|V_{\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}2\ell-1}}(A_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}2\ell-1},d))\big|\neq 0

\displaystyle\implies\rank(V_{\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}2\ell-1}}(A_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}2\ell-1},d)))={{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}2\ell-1}+d}\choose d}=p_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}2\ell-1}\geq n

\displaystyle A_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}2\ell-1},d)\subset{\cal G}_{\ell}

\displaystyle\implies\rank(V_{\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}2\ell-1}}({\cal G}_{\ell}))\geq{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}2\ell-1}+d}\choose d}\geq n.

Now notice that since
\rank V_{\leq m}({\cal X})=n
we must have
p_{m}\geq n
and therefore
m\geq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}2\ell-1}
. This implies

\rank(V_{\leq m}({\cal G}_{N}))\geq\rank(V_{\leq m}({\cal G}_{\ell}))\geq\rank(V_{\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}2\ell-1}}({\cal G}_{\ell}))\geq n,

finishing the proof.
∎

Remark 4.15
.

Theorem
4.14
can be reformulated without explicitly referring to the set
\mathcal{{\cal X}}
and its discrete moment order
m=\mu(\mathcal{{\cal X}})
by instead imposing the assumption that
p_{m}\geq n
for given
m,n
.

We now introduce an alternative condition for poisedness, following the work
[
16
]
.

Definition 4.16

(
[
16
]
)
.

A finite set
L\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
is a
lower set
if whenever
\mu\in L
and
0\leq\alpha\leq\mu
then
\alpha\in L
, where
\alpha\leq\beta
means that
\alpha_{j}\leq\beta_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,d
.

Theorem 4.17

(Theorem 1 in
[
16
]
)
.

Let
L
be a lower set and
F_{L}:=\{x_{\alpha}:\alpha\in L\}
. Then the polynomial interpolation problem with respect to
\Theta_{F_{L}}
is poised for
\mathcal{P}_{L}:=\text{span}\{x^{\alpha}:\alpha\in L\}
.

Corollary 4.18
.

The polynomial interpolation problem with respect to
\Theta_{A_{s}(2\ell-1,d)}
is poised for
\mathcal{P}_{2\ell-1}:=\text{span}\{P_{2\ell-1}\}
, where
P_{2\ell-1}
is the standard monomial basis of polynomials of total degree
\leq 2\ell-1
and
s:=-(\ell-1,\dots,\ell-1)^{T}
.

Proof.

It is easy to verify that the set
A(2\ell-1,d)
is a lower set as in Definition
4.16
. Now we show that
\mathcal{P}_{A(2\ell-1,d)}\equiv\mathcal{P}_{2\ell-1}
. By the definition of
A(2\ell-1,d)
in (
12
), any
\alpha\in A(2\ell-1,d)
has the form
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d})
where
\alpha_{i}\in\{0,1,\dots,2\ell-1\}
and
\sum_{i=0}^{d}\alpha_{i}=2\ell-1
which is equivalent to
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}\leq 2\ell-1
. Hence the set
\{x^{\alpha}:\alpha\in A(2\ell-1,d)\}
is precisely the set of monomials of total degree
\leq d
.
To complete the proof, let
F_{A(2\ell-1,d)}:=\{\alpha+s:\alpha\in A(2\ell-1,d)\}
. It is easy to see that
F_{A(2\ell-1,d)}\equiv A_{s}(2\ell-1,d)
, therefore, using Theorem
4.17
, we get the desired result.
∎

Note that corollary
4.18
, gives an alternative proof of the poisdness result in Theorem
4.14
.

5
Vandermonde matrices in the super-resolution regime

In this section we come back to the super-resolution problem, and consider the Vandermonde matrices
U(x_{1},\dots,x_{n};{\cal G}_{N})
in (
3
) in the single cluster setting. We prove the multidimensional analogue of
[
8
, Theorem 2.3]
, with the caveat that the geometry of the nodes stays fixed as
\text{SRF}\gg 1
. We start by defining the cluster geometry. Naturally, since the sampling set is an integer grid, we must restrict the nodes to
\mathbb{T}^{d}:=[-\pi,\pi)^{d}\equiv\left(\mathbb{R}\mod 2\pi\right)^{d}
to avoid aliasing.

Definition 5.1
.

For
x,y\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
, we denote the wrap-around distance by

\|x-y\|_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}:=\min_{r\in(2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{d}}\|x-y+r\|_{\infty}.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{1},...,x_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{T}^{d}
. If for some
\tau>1
and
0<\Delta<\pi/\tau
we have

\forall x,y\in\mathcal{X},x\neq y:\quad\Delta\leq\|x-y\|_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\leq\tau\Delta,

then
\mathcal{X}
is said to form an
(\Delta,\tau,n)
-cluster.

In what follows we fix a node set
{\cal Y}=\{y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{T}^{d}
such that the (non-wrapped) minimal distance between any two nodes is

\rho=\rho({\cal Y}):=\min_{i\neq j}\|y_{i}-y_{j}\|_{\infty}.

Now let
{\cal X}=\Delta{\cal Y}
with
\Delta<\frac{1}{2\pi}
, so that
{\cal X}
forms an
(\Delta^{\prime},\tau^{\prime},n)
-cluster for
\Delta^{\prime}=\rho\Delta
and some
\tau^{\prime}\leq\frac{2\pi}{\rho}
. (Otherwise, there exist
y,y^{\prime}\in{\cal Y}
such that
\|y-y^{\prime}\|_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\geq\frac{2\pi}{\rho}\Delta^{\prime}=2\pi\Delta
, a contradiction).

Further, let
{\cal G}_{N}=\{0,\pm 1,...,\pm N\}^{d}
be the uniform symmetric grid of side length
2N+1
.

Let
U:=U(x_{1},\dots,x_{n};{\cal G}_{N})
. Denote
\epsilon:=N\Delta
. We have

U^{*}U=D({\cal X},{\cal G}_{N})=D\Bigl(\epsilon\frac{{\cal Y}}{N},{\cal G}_{N}\Bigr)=D_{\epsilon}\Bigl(\frac{{\cal Y}}{N},{\cal G}_{N}\Bigr).

We further define the rescaled Dirichlet kernel

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{K}_{N}(x,y):=\frac{1}{(2N)^{d}}\mathcal{D}_{{\cal G}_{N}}(x/N,y/N)

\displaystyle\implies K_{\epsilon}({\cal Y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2N)^{d}}D_{\epsilon}\Bigl(\frac{{\cal Y}}{N},{\cal G}_{N}\Bigr)=D_{N}({\cal X}).

We have the following result.

Theorem 5.3
.

With the above notations, let
m=\mu({\cal Y})=\mu({\cal X})
be the discrete moment order of
{\cal Y}
.

For every
\epsilon_{0}>0,\alpha\in(0,1)
there exists
N_{0}
such that for all
N\geq N_{0}
and
\alpha\epsilon_{0}<N\Delta<\epsilon_{0}
,
the eigenvalues of
D_{N}
split into
m+1
groups

\lambda_{0,0}(D_{N})\asymp{(N\Delta)}^{0},\quad\{\lambda_{1,j}(D_{N})\}_{j=1}^{t_{1}}\asymp{(N\Delta)}^{2},...,\{\lambda_{m,j}(D_{N})\}_{j=1}^{t_{m}}\asymp{(N\Delta)}^{2m},

where
t_{k}:=\rank(V_{\leq k}({\cal X}))-\rank(V_{\leq k-1}({\cal X}))
,
k=1,\dots,m
.
Here
\asymp
means ’exact scaling’ as defined in Corollary
3.5
.

Proof.

With the identification
D_{N}({\cal X})=K_{\epsilon}({\cal Y})
as above, we would like to apply Theorem
3.1
and Corollary
3.5
to the kernel
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}_{N}
. However,
\mathcal{K}
depends on
N
, so we need to make sure that all the estimates in the proofs are uniform in
N
.

Definition 5.4

(sinc kernel)
.

Let
\sinc(x):=\begin{cases}\frac{\sin(x)}{x},&\text{if }x\neq 0,\\
1,&\text{if }x=0.\end{cases}
. Given
{\cal X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the corresponding sinc kernel matrix is defined as follows:

\Sinc({\cal X}):=\biggl[\prod_{i=1}^{d}\sinc(x_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime})\biggr]_{x,x^{\prime}\in{\cal X}}

(13)

For
\epsilon>0
, the flat limit version of the
\sinc
kernel matrix is:

\Sinc_{\epsilon}({\cal X}):=\biggl[\prod_{i=1}^{d}\sinc(\epsilon x_{i}-\epsilon x_{i}^{\prime})\biggr]_{x,x^{\prime}\in{\cal X}}

(14)

Outline of the proof: we aim to prove that the eigenvalues of
D_{N}
converge to
\Sinc
kernel when
N\to\infty
. The scaling of the eigenvalues of
\Sinc
can be obtained using Corollary
3.5
(the constants
\tilde{\lambda}_{k,j}
don’t depend on
N
). First we show element-wise convergence of
D_{N}
to
\sinc
and equicontinuity of
D_{N}
in
\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}
to show uniform convergence of
D_{N}
to
\sinc
(uniform in
\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}
and independent of
N
). Then using the Bauer–Fike Theorem, we prove convergence of the eigenvalues.

We start with some definitions.

Theorem 5.5

(The Bauer–Fike Theorem)
.

For a diagonalizable matrix
A
let
E
be it’s eigenvectors matrix,
A=E\Lambda{E}^{-1}
where
\Lambda
is a diagonal matrix. Let
\mu
be an eigenvalue of
A_{N}
, then there exists
\lambda\in\Lambda(A)
such that

|\lambda-\mu|\leq\kappa_{p}(E)\|A-A_{N}\|_{p}

(15)

where
\kappa_{p}(E):=\|E\|_{p}\|{E}^{-1}\|_{p}
is the condition number of
E
associated with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{p}
.

Definition 5.6

(Element-wise convergence)
.

Let
\{A_{N}\}_{N=1}^{\infty}
, where
A_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times s}
, be a sequence of matrices, and let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times s}
be a matrix. The sequence
\{A_{N}\}_{N=1}^{\infty}
is said to converge element-wise to
A
if the following condition is satisfied:

For every
\delta>0
, there exists a positive integer
M(\delta)
such that for all
N\geq M(\delta)
, the inequality

\big|(A_{N})_{i,j}-(A)_{i,j}\big|\leq\delta

holds for all
1\leq i,j\leq s
.

Definition 5.7

(Equicontinuity)
.

Let
\{f_{n}(x)\}
be a sequence of functions.
\{f_{n}(x)\}
is equicontinuous if for every
\delta>0
, there exists
\nu>0
(independent of
n
) such that for all
n
and all
x,y\in[a,b]
with
|x-y|<\nu
we have
|f_{n}(x)-f_{n}(y)|<\delta
.

Definition 5.8

(Uniform convergence)
.

A sequence of functions
\{f_{n}(x)\}
converges uniformly to
f(x)
on
S
if for every
\epsilon>0
, there exists an integer
N
such that for all
n\geq N
and for all
x\in S
,
|f_{n}(x)-f(x)|<\epsilon
.

Theorem 5.9
.

Let
C(J)
be the space of continuous functions on a closed interval
J\subset\mathbb{R}
. A sequence in
C(J)
is uniformly convergent if and only if it is equicontinuous and converges pointwise to a function.

Proof.

The first direction is an immediate corollary of
[
36
, Theorem 7.25]
. Since the sequence converges pointwise, we can replace the subsequence in the proof of (b) by the sequence itself. The second direction is Theorem 7.24.
∎

Lemma 5.10
.

For every
\epsilon>0
and
{\cal X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the Dirichlet kernel matrix
D_{N}({\cal X})
converges element-wise to the
\sinc
kernel matrix
\Sinc_{\epsilon}(\mathcal{Y})
when
N\to\infty
.

Proof.

\big(D_{N}({\cal X})\big)_{i,j}=\frac{1}{(2N)^{d}}\big(D_{\epsilon}\Bigl(\frac{{\cal Y}}{N},{\cal G}_{N}\Bigr)\big)_{i,j}=\frac{1}{(2N)^{d}}\mathcal{D}_{{\cal G}_{N}}(\epsilon({y_{i}\over N}-{y_{j}\over N}))=\frac{1}{(2N)^{d}}\sum_{\omega\in{\cal G}_{N}}\exp\left(\imath\langle\epsilon({y_{i}\over N}-{y_{j}\over N}),\omega\rangle\right)

(16)

Denote by
y:=y_{i}-y_{j}
and
y:=(y^{(1)},\dots,y^{(d)})
. First, we consider the case where
d=1
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{k=-N}^{N}\exp(\imath\epsilon k{y\over N})=\frac{1}{2N}(1+2\sum_{k=1}^{N}\cos{(\epsilon k{y\over N})})=\frac{1}{2N}\frac{\sin((N+{1\over 2}){\epsilon y\over N})}{\sin({\epsilon y\over 2N})}

\displaystyle\to_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{2N}\frac{\sin((1+{1\over 2N})\epsilon y)}{{\epsilon y\over 2N}}\to_{N\to\infty}{\sin(\epsilon y)\over\epsilon y}=\sinc(\epsilon y).

For
d>1
, using the above, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2N)^{d}}\sum_{\omega\in{\cal G}_{N}}\exp\left(\imath\langle\epsilon({y\over N}),\omega\rangle\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{k=-N}^{N}\exp(\imath\epsilon k{y^{(i)}\over N})\to_{N\to\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\sinc(\epsilon y^{(i)}).

∎

We proved that each entry of
D_{N}({\cal X})
converges point-wise to each entry of
\Sinc_{\epsilon}(\mathcal{Y})
. Now we show that this convergence is uniform for
\epsilon\in[0,\epsilon_{0}]
for a given
\epsilon_{0}
. We show that using Theorem
5.9
. First we need to show that the sequence
\{\big(D_{N}({\cal X})\big)_{i,j}\}
is equicontinuous.

For fixed
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, let
f_{N}(\epsilon):=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{k=-N}^{N}\exp(\imath\epsilon k{y\over N})
and
f(\epsilon):=\sinc(\epsilon y)
. We prove the following result.

Lemma 5.11
.

Let
\epsilon_{0}>0
be given. For every
\delta>0
there exists
\nu>0
(independent of
N
) such that for all
N
and all
\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime}\in[0,\epsilon_{0}]
with
|\epsilon-\epsilon^{\prime}|<\nu
we have
|f_{n}(\epsilon)-f_{n}(\epsilon^{\prime})|<\delta
.

Proof.

Let
\delta>0
. we want to find
\nu(\delta)>0
such that for all
N
and
\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime}\in[0,\epsilon_{0}]
with
|\epsilon-\epsilon^{\prime}|<\nu
we have that
|f_{N}(\epsilon)-f_{N}(\epsilon^{\prime})|<\delta
. Using that
|1-e^{ix}|=2|\sin({x\over 2})|
and
\sin(x)\leq x
for
x\in[0,{\pi\over 2}]
we get

\displaystyle|f_{N}(\epsilon)-f_{N}(\epsilon^{\prime})|

\displaystyle=|\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{k=-N}^{N}\exp(\imath\epsilon k{y\over N})-\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{k=-N}^{N}\exp(\imath\epsilon^{\prime}k{y\over N})|

\displaystyle=|\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{k=-N}^{N}\exp(\imath\epsilon k{y\over N})(1-\exp(\imath(\epsilon-\epsilon^{\prime})k{y\over N}))|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{k=-N}^{N}|1-\exp(\imath(\epsilon-\epsilon^{\prime})k{y\over N})|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{k=-N}^{N}2|\sin((\epsilon-\epsilon^{\prime})k{y\over 2N})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{2N}\sum_{k=0}^{N}2k|\epsilon-\epsilon^{\prime}|{y\over 2N}={1\over N^{2}}{N+N^{2}\over 2}\nu y

\displaystyle=({1\over 2N}+{1\over 2})y\nu\leq y\nu.

Thus we choose
\nu:={\delta\over y}
. The same holds for
f_{N}(\epsilon):=\frac{1}{(2N)^{d}}\sum_{\omega\in{\cal G}_{N}}\exp\left(\imath\langle\epsilon({y\over N}),\omega\rangle\right)
.
∎

Using Lemma
5.10
and
5.11
together with Theorem
5.9
we get the following Corollary.

Corollary 5.12
.

Let
\epsilon_{0}
be given. For every
\delta>0
there exists
M=M(\epsilon_{0},\delta)
such that for all
N>M

\big|\big(D_{N}({\cal X})\big)_{i,j}-\big(\Sinc_{\epsilon}(\mathcal{Y})\big)_{i,j}\big|\leq\delta,\quad\forall\epsilon\in[0,\epsilon_{0}].

(17)

Proof.

We take
\nu
in Definition
5.7
to be the minimum of all the
\nu_{i,j}^{(\ell)}
according to the proof of Lemma
5.11
, with
\ell=1,\dots,d
. Note that for
y_{i,j}^{(\ell)}=0
we have
\nu_{i,j}^{(\ell)}=\infty
.
∎

Corollary 5.13

(Uniform convergence of eigenvalues)
.

Let
\epsilon_{0}>0
be given. For every
\delta^{\prime}>0
there exists
M=M(\epsilon_{0},\delta^{\prime})
s.t. for all
N>M
there is an eigenvalue
\mu_{k}^{(N)}
of
D_{N}({\cal X})
with

|\lambda^{(\epsilon)}_{k}-\mu_{k}^{(N)}|\leq\delta^{\prime},\quad\forall\epsilon\in[0,\epsilon_{0}]

(18)

where
\lambda^{(\epsilon)}_{k}
is an eigenvalue of
\Sinc_{\epsilon}(\mathcal{Y})
.

Proof.

Since
\Sinc_{\epsilon}(\mathcal{Y})
is a symmetric matrix, the eigenvectors matrix
E
is orthogonal in the eigenvalue decomposition of
\Sinc_{\epsilon}(\mathcal{Y})
i.e
\Sinc_{\epsilon}(\mathcal{Y})=E\Lambda{E}^{-1}
. Thus we have
\kappa_{2}(E)=1
. Using the Bauer–Fike theorem
5.5
with Corollary
5.12
and
\delta:={\delta^{\prime}\over n\sqrt{n}}
, we get:

\displaystyle|\lambda-\mu|\leq\|\Sinc_{\epsilon}(\mathcal{Y})-D_{N}({\cal X})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{n}\|\Sinc_{\epsilon}(\mathcal{Y})-D_{N}({\cal X})\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{n}\max_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big|(\Sinc_{\epsilon}(\mathcal{Y}))_{i,j}-(D_{N}({\cal X}))_{i,j}\big|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{n}n\delta=\delta^{\prime}.

∎

To complete the proof of Theorem
5.3
, we choose
\delta^{\prime}=O\biggl((\alpha\epsilon_{0})^{2m}\biggr)
in (
18
) and apply Corollary
3.5
.
The only remaining question is to show that
\det(RWR)\neq 0
. We will show that the limit
N\to\infty
the rank of
W=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{(2N)^{d}}W_{N}
is full, and as a result,
\det\left(\tilde{R}W\tilde{R}^{T}\right)
is uniformly bounded away from zero. By definition of the Wronskian, we write

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2N)^{d}}W_{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2N)^{d}}\bigg[{{\mathcal{D}_{{\cal G}_{N}}^{(\alpha,\beta)}({x\over N},{y\over N})|_{x,y=0}}\over{\alpha!\beta!}}\bigg]_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{P}_{m}}=\bigg[\frac{1}{(2N)^{d}}\sum_{\omega\in{\cal G}_{N}}{1\over N^{|\alpha|+|\beta|}}{{(i\omega)^{\alpha}(-i\omega)^{\beta}}\over{\alpha!\beta!}}\bigg]_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{P}_{m}}

\displaystyle=\bigg[\frac{1}{(2N)^{d}}\sum_{\omega=(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{d})\in{\cal G}_{N}}{{(i)^{|\alpha|}(-i)^{|\beta|}}\over{\alpha!\beta!}}\big({\omega_{1}\over N}\big)^{\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1}}\cdot...\cdot\big({\omega_{d}\over N}\big)^{\alpha_{d}+\beta_{d}}\bigg]_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{P}_{m}}

\displaystyle=\bigg[\prod_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{k=-N}^{N}\frac{1}{2N}\frac{(i)^{\alpha_{j}}(-i)^{\beta_{j}}}{\alpha_{j}!\beta_{j}!}\big({k\over N}\big)^{\alpha_{j}+\beta_{j}}\bigg]_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{P}_{m}}.

We get that each entry of
\frac{1}{(2N)^{d}}W_{N}
is a Riemann sum, thus as
N\to\infty
we get a multiple integral:

\lim_{N\to\infty}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{k=-N}^{N}\frac{1}{2N}\frac{(i)^{\alpha_{j}}(-i)^{\beta_{j}}}{\alpha_{j}!\beta_{j}!}\biggl({k\over N}\biggr)^{\alpha_{j}+\beta_{j}}={1\over 2^{d}}\int_{[-1,1]^{d}}{{(i)^{|\alpha|}(-i)^{|\beta|}}\over{\alpha!\beta!}}x^{\alpha+\beta}\,dx

Thus we get:

W=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{(2N)^{d}}W_{N}=\bigg[{1\over 2^{d}}\int_{[-1,1]^{d}}{{(i)^{|\alpha|}(-i)^{|\beta|}}\over{\alpha!\beta!}}x^{\alpha+\beta}\,dx\bigg]_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{P}_{m}}.

It is immediately seen that
W
is none other than the Gram matrix for the monomials
\{\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{P}_{m}}
:

W_{\alpha,\beta}=\langle x^{\alpha},x^{\beta}\rangle={1\over 2^{d}}\int_{[-1,1]^{d}}{{(i)^{|\alpha|}(-i)^{|\beta|}}\over{\alpha!\beta!}}x^{\alpha+\beta}\,dx.

Since the set of monomials is linearly independent, we have that
W
is positive definite. Thus
W
is full rank, and as a result,
\det\left(\tilde{R}W\tilde{R}^{T}\right)>0
.
This completes the proof of Theorem
5.3
.
∎

6.2
Super-resolution

In this section, we consider the super-resolution problem for multidimensional sparse measures described in the Introduction, as it was the original motivation for our investigations. We reconstruct measures of the form (

2
). We consider the asymptotic regime of constant
N

6.2.1
Local Stability

Analogous to some previous studies in the one-dimensional case, e.g.
[
2
,
4
]
, we consider the “local stability” of the problem to be well-approximated by the error incurred by the nonlinear least squares (NLS) method applied to the noisy measurements:

\left\{\hat{x}_{j}^{NLS},\hat{\alpha}_{j}^{NLS}\right\}_{j=1}^{n}=\arg\min_{\hat{\alpha},\hat{x}}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\in{\cal G}_{N}}\left|\hat{f}(k)-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\hat{\alpha}_{j}e^{\imath\left\langle k,\hat{x}_{j}\right\rangle}\right|^{2}.

The initial values for the NLS method are taken to be the true parameter values (which are clearly unknown in practice). Our implementation of the NLS method is based on the Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm, with the complex residuals converted to real residuals by concatenating the real and imaginary parts. The results are shown in Figures
3
and
4
. To measure the accuracy, we fitted the scaled error

\kappa:=\max_{j=1,\dots,n}\|\hat{x}_{j}^{NLS}-x_{j}\|_{\infty}/\sigma

to the curve
\kappa\sim\Delta^{m}
, where
\Delta
is the minimal distance between the nodes. It can be seen that the accuracy crucially depends on the geometry, and is indeed best for the random configuration, and worst for the single-line configuration (for which the scaling is precisely
\kappa\sim\Delta^{2n-2}
for any
d
, as established in previous works on the subject).

Let

\Omega

m=\mu({\cal X})

{\cal X}

2.5

[
1

\epsilon x,\epsilon y

[0,\epsilon x],[0,\epsilon y]\subset\Omega
(where
[0,x]

0

x

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\epsilon}(x,y)=\mathcal{K}(\epsilon x,\epsilon y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{P}_{m}}\frac{{(\epsilon x)}^{\alpha}{(\epsilon y)}^{\beta}}{\alpha!\beta!}\mathcal{K}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(0,0)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{P}_{m},\beta\in\mathbb{H}_{m+1}}\frac{{(\epsilon x)}^{\alpha}{(\epsilon y)}^{\beta}}{\alpha!\beta!}\mathcal{K}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(0,\theta_{\epsilon y,\alpha}\epsilon y)+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{H}_{m+1},\beta\in\mathbb{P}_{m}}\frac{{(\epsilon x)}^{\alpha}{(\epsilon y)}^{\beta}}{\alpha!\beta!}\mathcal{K}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(\eta_{\epsilon x,\beta}\epsilon x,0)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{H}_{m+1}}\frac{{(\epsilon x)}^{\alpha}{(\epsilon y)}^{\beta}}{\alpha!\beta!}\mathcal{K}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(\psi_{\epsilon x,\epsilon y}\epsilon x,\xi_{\epsilon x,\epsilon y}\epsilon y),

\{\theta_{\epsilon y,\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathbb{P}_{m}}\subset[0,1]
depend on
\epsilon y

\{\eta_{\epsilon x,\beta}\}_{\beta\in\mathbb{P}_{m}}\subset[0,1]

\epsilon x

depend on both
\epsilon x
and
\epsilon y
.

Let
\mathbb{P}_{m}=\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{p_{m}}\}
. Then we can write the above expansion as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(\epsilon x,\epsilon y)=\big[{(\epsilon x)}^{\alpha_{1}},\dots,{(\epsilon x)}^{\alpha_{p_{m}}}\big]W_{\leq m}{\big[{(\epsilon y)}^{\alpha_{1}},\dots,{(\epsilon y)}^{\alpha_{p_{m}}}\big]}^{T}

\epsilon_{0}x,\epsilon_{0}y\in\Omega

Appendix B
Auxiliary Lemmas

Lemma B.1
.

Let
D\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
be a
k\times k
diagonal matrix and
P
a permutation matrix corresponding to a permutation
\sigma
, then

PDP^{T}=\diag\{d_{\sigma(1)},\dots,d_{\sigma(k)}\},\quad D=\diag\{d_{1},\dots,d_{k}\}.

Proof.

If
P
is a permutation matrix corresponding to a permutation
\sigma
, then for every vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{k}

Pv=\begin{bmatrix}v_{\sigma(1)}\\
\vdots\\
v_{\sigma(k)}\end{bmatrix}

and then for every matrix
A=\begin{bmatrix}R_{1}\\
\vdots\\
R_{k}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
, we have

PA=\begin{bmatrix}R_{\sigma(1)}\\
\vdots\\
R_{\sigma(k)}\end{bmatrix}.

Now let
A=D=\diag\{d_{1},\dots,d_{k}\}
, and take
j=1,\dots,k
. Then
R_{\sigma(j)}=d_{\sigma(j)}e_{\sigma(j)}^{T}
. Therefore

R_{\sigma(j)}P^{T}=d_{\sigma(j)}\left(Pe_{\sigma(j)}\right)^{T}=d_{\sigma(j)}e_{j}^{T}.

Stacking all the rows we get
PDP^{T}=\diag\{d_{\sigma(1)},\dots,d_{\sigma(k)}\}
.
∎

Lemma B.2
.

Let
M,B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. For
\epsilon>0
we have

\det(M+\epsilon B)=\det(M)+O(\epsilon),\quad\epsilon\ll 1.

(20)

Proof.

Recall formula (0.8.12.3) for sum of matrices in
[
20
]
:

\det(M+\epsilon B)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\epsilon^{k}\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{adj}_{k}(M)C_{k}(B)),

(21)

where
\operatorname{tr}(\cdot)
is the trace of matrix,
\operatorname{adj}_{k}(\cdot)
is the
k
-th adjugate of matrix (0.8.12) and
C_{k}(\cdot)
is the
k
-th compound (0.8.1). Using the following properties:
\operatorname{adj}_{0}(M)=\det(M)
and
C_{0}(M)=1
, we have

\det(M+\epsilon B)=\epsilon^{0}\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{adj}_{0}(M)C_{0}(B))+O(\epsilon)=\det(M)+O(\epsilon),

(22)

completing the proof.
∎
