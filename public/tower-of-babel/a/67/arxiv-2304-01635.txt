A False Sense of Privacy: Towards a Reliable Evaluation Methodology for the Anonymization of Biometric Data Volume: YYYY X DOI: XXXXXXX.XXXXXXX
Simon Hanisch, Julian Todt, Jose Patino, Nicholas Evans, Thorsten Strufe

Abstract
. Biometric data contains distinctive human traits such as facial features or gait patterns. The use of biometric data permits an individuation so exact that the data is utilized effectively in identification and authentication systems. But for this same reason, privacy protections become indispensably necessary. Privacy protection is extensively afforded by the technique of anonymization. Anonymization techniques obfuscate or remove the sensitive personal data to achieve high levels of anonymity. However, the effectiveness of anonymization relies, in equal parts, on the effectiveness of the methods employed to evaluate anonymization performance. In this paper, we assess the state-of-the-art methods used to evaluate the performance of anonymization techniques for facial images and gait patterns. We demonstrate that the state-of-the-art evaluation methods have serious and frequent shortcomings. In particular, we find that the underlying assumptions of the state-of-the-art are quite unwarranted. When a method evaluating the performance of anonymization assumes a weak adversary or a weak recognition scenario, then the resulting evaluation will very likely be a gross overestimation of the anonymization performance. Therefore, we propose a stronger adversary model which is alert to the recognition scenario as well as to the anonymization scenario. Our adversary model implements an appropriate measure of anonymization performance. We improve the selection process for the evaluation dataset, and we reduce the numbers of identities contained in the dataset while ensuring that these identities remain easily distinguishable from one another. Our novel evaluation methodology surpasses the state-of-the-art because we measure worst-case performance and so deliver a highly reliable evaluation of biometric anonymization techniques.



1.
Introduction

Biometric data is rich in sensitive personal information that can be used to identify individuals and infer private attributes. Examples of biometric data in use are face recognition
(
Deng et al. 2019
)
, gait recognition
(
Wan et al. 2018
)
, inference of medical conditions
(
Koktas et al. 2006
)
, and inference of character traits based on eye motion
(
Kröger et al. 2020
)
. Clearly, the use of biometrics poses a real and serious threat to personal privacy. However, the utility of the same data is also undeniable as users want to upload their images to social media, share videos with friends, or use online services for health tracking.
Therefore, in order to protect the privacy of the individuals whose biometrics are captured, techniques have been developed that perturb biometric data and so obfuscate or remove sensitive personal information while keeping the utility of the data. The representative technique for identity protection is anonymization.

The privacy-protecting function of any anonymization technique has its only guarantee in the reliable evaluation of the performance of the anonymization. A reliable evaluation methodology for anonymization will accurately assess the level of protection afforded against identification from the anonymized data.
Therefore, any assumptions made about an attacker must be robust, because otherwise the evaluation methodology will likely deliver grossly inaccurate estimates of anonymization performance. The result will be a false sense of privacy, and the consequence will be the erosion of user trust. Moreover, any inaccuracy or even error in an evaluation methodology will detrimentally affect advances in the research. Flaws in the methodology may feed into future research and thus hinder or even arrest the development of advanced anonymization techniques. The upshot is this: Only when a biometric anonymization technique has been convincingly evaluated can researchers improve on existing techniques or provide secure applications to users.

In this paper, we assess the state-of-the-art evaluation methodology for the anonymization of biometric data. In particular, we choose to assess the methods employed to evaluate techniques for face anonymization and gait anonymization. Our choice for face anonymization is based on the fact that there are many widely-employed techniques in application. On the other hand, we have chosen gait anonymization precisely because the techniques here are fewer and relatively seldom in applications. Moreover, the 3D time-series data in gait anonymization allow us to push the envelope of the simpler two-dimensional space of the face image. On a side note, we are entirely aware of the fact that the comprehensive evaluation of any anonymization technique is only possible when utility is also taken into consideration. However, for this paper, we have narrowed our scope to the improvement of the methods evaluating anonymization performance only.

Our assessment of the state-of-the-art in evaluation for the anonymization of biometric data shows that these methods often fail at convincingly evaluating the performance of the anonymization.

One reason is that the state-of-the-art methods have been uncritically adopted from the evaluation methodology for biometric recognition. In biometric recognition, problems are used that are difficult not for anonymization, but for recognition. The problems in biometric recognition employ many identities and difficult biometric samples (e.g., profile photos or nearly indistinguishable identities). In anonymization, on the other hand, a difficult problem has a small number of identities which are very diverse, thus making the identities easier to differentiate but more difficult to anonymize.

One further reason why the state-of-the-art methods provide unreliable evaluations of anonymization performance is that the methods rely on weak adversary models. The assumption behind state-of-the-art evaluation methods is that the attacker is unaware of the anonymization mechanisms in place. For example, a method will use pre-trained recognition models which perform well on clear data. However, such models prove incapable of adapting to a data-structural modifications performed by an anonymization technique. Consider this straightforward scenario: An anonymization technique for a face image performs consistently the same block permutation. This anonymization can easily be removed with the inverse permutation. However, the permutation will go unnoticed by a recognition model pre-trained in the structures of the clear data. Therefore, our recommendation is instead to assume that the attacker knows of the anonymization in place. Our recommendation to train on anonymized data allows us to learn the recognition model to fit the structures of the anonymized data.

Moreover, further consideration of the same scenario illustrates this inherent weakness of many works in biometric anonymization: The evaluation is performed using only a single recognition system. This will likely cause a serious problem for the evaluation reliability. Although a given anonymization technique may successfully degrade the performance of one recognition system,
other
systems classifying
other
feature vectors may be more robust or even largely unaffected. Therefore, we conclude that a robust approach to evaluation should consider multiple and different recognition systems.

Improvements to the evaluation methodology for the anonymization of biometric data seem necessary to us for two reasons. First, reliable evaluation is absolutely essential to the development of more robust biometric data anonymization. Second, methodological reliability is, in and of itself, a value of our research community. Therefore, we take the first steps towards a reliable evaluation methodology for the anonymization of biometric data. Our results all point to two substantial improvements to the state-of-the-art evaluation methodology. First, the methodology improves when it assumes a strong attacker; and second, the methodology improves when it uses challenging evaluation datasets.

The contributions of our paper are as follows:

•

We assess the current state-of-the-art evaluation methodology for biometric data anonymization and must point to fatal flaws in the evaluation methodology.

•

We update the state-of-the-art evaluation methodology. Our methodological improvements involve (1) retraining the recognition system on anonymized data, (2) using multiple recognition systems to evaluate the anonymization, and (3) generating evaluation datasets that are challenging to anonymize and consequently reliable for the evaluation of the anonymization performance.

•

We test our methodological improvements on the biometric traits face and gait. Our evidence supports the conclusion that our improved methodology delivers reliable evaluations of biometric data anonymization.

2.
Related Work

Biometric recognition spans dozens of biometric traits and hundreds of techniques, but the methodology for evaluating the performance of these techniques has been assessed by only a very few works.

Goga et al.
(
Goga et al. 2015
)
assess the methodology for evaluating matching techniques of profiles from different social media platforms. They find that evaluation commonly overestimates the performance of the approaches by using an unrealistic methodology. Decann and Ross
(
DeCann and Ross 2013
)
investigate how the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve can be related to the Cumulative Match Characteristic (CMC) curve, finding that both ROC and CMC curves should be reported for biometric recognition. Granger and Gorodnischy
(
Granger and Gorodnichy 2014
)
describe the methodology that should be applied to evaluate the performance of biometric recognition for video surveillance applications. For the evaluation of stylometric authorship attribution, Stolerman et al.
(
Stolerman et al. 2013
)
make the case that an open-set model should be applied since in a realistic scenario the actual author might not be on the suspect list. Brennan et al.
(
Brennan et al. 2012
)
propose adding attacks to the methodology of stylometry evaluation because most methods cannot defend against attacks.

More generally, Arp et al.
(
Arp et al. 2022
)
investigate the methodology of using machine learning in computer security. They describe common pitfalls of using the methodology for research purposes and also give advice on how to avoid the pitfalls.

Template protection is a very specific kind of privacy protection because it removes all possible attribute inferences while still allowing identity verification. One specialized evaluation methodology
(
Rane 2014
)
for template protection specifies such properties as irreversibility and unlinkability which any template protection scheme must achieve to be deemed secure.

Le et al.
(
Le et al. 2020
)
discuss how to evaluate privacy-utility trade-offs for face anonymization, but their focus is on measuring the utility.

Recent works
(
Tekli et al. 2019
;
Hao et al. 2020
;
Todt et al. 2022
)
propose attacks on biometric data anonymization that use machine learning to reverse the obfuscation of images. These results show that the method is highly effective even when a human observer cannot recognize anything at all in the image.

In the context of the VoicePrivacy challenge
(
Tomashenko et al. 2020a
)
, other recent works have investigated the evaluation methodology of speaker anonymization. The VoicePrivacy challenge, begun in 2020, aims at offering a common framework and methodology for evaluating speaker anonymization. Noé et al.
(
Noé et al. 2022
)
also propose a framework to evaluate and compare speech pseudonymization approaches using ZEBRA
(
Nautsch et al. 2020
)
and voice similarity matrices
(
Noé et al. 2020
)
. ZEBRA aims at creating a worst-case metric to evaluate speaker anonymization and voice similarity matrices allow to compare how well specific identities are anonymized. Bonastre et al.
(
Bonastre et al. 2021
)
propose a benchmarking methodology to test speaker recognition against spoofing and anonymization.

3.
Background

In this section, we define basic terminology required for our work.

3.1.
Biometrics, inference, and recognition

Biometric traits
(also called biometric characteristics
(
1 2017
)
) are properties of a human that specify either what a human is (e.g. face, iris, fingerprint) or how a human behaves (e.g. voice, gait, heartbeat). The traits which specify being are called biological biometric traits, and the traits which specify behavior are called behavioral biometric traits.
Soft biometric traits
(e.g. age, gender, weight) are insufficiently entropic to positively identify an individual. However, the combination of soft biometrics can suffice to positively identify an individual.

Due to the unique nature of biometric traits for each human being they can be used for privacy-invasive inferences.
We distinguish between two privacy threats,
identity inference
in which the identity of an individual is inferred and
attribute inference
in which a specific private attribute (e.g. age, gender, medical condition) is inferred.

In biometric recognition, identity and attribute inference are made operative in a system that learns an inference on representative samples for each class. For each biometric sample to be classified, a biometric recognition system returns a list of possible classes, where each class has been assigned its own separate likelihood. In closed-set recognition, the sample must belong to one of the classes in the dataset for training purposes, while in open-set recognition the sample may belong to an unknown class.

We use two different biometric recognition system architectures for our later introduced gait and face recognition systems. For our gait recognition systems we use an architecture which only uses data specific to the target identities, and for our face recognition systems we use an architecture that uses an additional background data not specific to the target identities (see Fig.
1
).
Both architectures split the samples of each identity contained in the evaluation dataset into
train set
and
test set
. The train set is then used to learn a representation for each identity which is used to infer the identity of the samples in the test set. Additionally to this architecture the face recognition systems are
pre-trained
before the training on the train set. During pre-training an additional background dataset, that seeks to represent the general population, is used to learn the features which can be used to differentiate between identities.

Figure 1.
Dataset use through the phases of our recognition systems for face and gait recognition.

3.2.
Anonymization

The aim of
anonymization
is to protect an individual’s identity. During the process of anonymization, information is removed or perturbed that is specific to an individual. Hence, anonymization prevents an adversary from using the data to infer the class either corresponding to an individual (i.e. identification). In contrast to anonymization,
pseudonymization
is aimed at retaining some connection between identity and data in order to link the data to an alternative identifier.
Besides preventing identification an anonymization also seeks to retain some
utility
(i.e. usefulness) of the data. Most often a trade-off between anonymization and utility must be made.

4.
Improving the evaluation methodology

In this section, we aim to achieve a reliable evaluation methodology for the anonymization of biometric data.
Our premise is that an evaluation methodology for anonymization techniques should be pessimistic and assume a strong adversary based on the worst-case performance of the anonymization technique. We proceed in two concrete steps.

First, we analyze the shortcomings of the state-of-the-art for evaluating biometric data anonymization. Overall, we find that the methods for recognition evaluation have uncritically been carried over into the evaluation of anonymization performance.

Second, we discuss our three suggestions for improvement. We suggest (1) that recognition systems be trained with anonymized data, (2) that anonymization performance be tested against different recognition systems, and (3) that evaluation datasets consist of recognition problems more challenging to anonymization performance.

4.1.
State-of-the-Art Evaluation Methods for the Anonymization of Biometric Data

We began by gaining an overview of the problems caused by state-of-the-art evaluation methods. To this end, we assessed the papers covered by two recent surveys
(
Hanisch et al. 2021
;
Ribaric et al. 2016
)
on the topic of biometric data anonymizations. Next, to gain a closer perspective on the field of face anonymization, we analyzed works
(
Rajabi et al. 2021
;
Fan 2018
;
John et al. 2020
;
Wang et al. 2019
;
Newton et al. 2005
;
Hukkelås et al. 2019
;
Maximov et al. 2020
;
Shan et al. 2020
)
published from 2018 (excepting
(
Newton et al. 2005
)
). We included as many works as we could find appearing at
USENIX Security
,
Privacy Enhancing Technologies Symposium
(PETs), and
Data and Applications Security and Privacy
. Another work
(
Le and Carlsson 2023
)
of 2023 testifies to how the methodological flaws we have found persist to this day.

Our main finding is the striking similarity between evaluation methods for anonymization and recognition. This similarity is striking because it is strong but also because it is unmotivated. Recognition performance will not be accurately evaluated by methods designed to evaluate the performance of anonymization; likewise, anonymization performance will not be accurately evaluated by methods designed to evaluate the performance of recognition. The evaluation methodology must suit the technique under evaluation.

In particular, our analysis of the state-of-the-art shows that the methods for evaluating either a biometric recognition or an anonymization technique both use the same recognition models, the same datasets, and the same evaluation scenarios. In biometric recognition, an evaluation method presents challenging scenarios to the recognition system. Identities are hard to distinguish from one another, and moreover, the number of identities to be distinguished is high, the biometric samples are poor in quality, a difficult recognition problem in the form of an open-set scenario, and imposters are introduced to mislead recognition systems. But these same conditions in biometric anonymization do not pose a challenge. In fact, the high number of identities makes the anonymization very much easier.

Our analysis shows that the mixing of evaluation methods from recognition and anonymization causes three main problems.

The first problem we identified is that the evaluation of recognition introduces an adversary model that is unrealistically weak to the evaluation of anonymization. Most papers implicitly assume that the adversary is not aware of the anonymization in place because the recognition system is trained on clear data and not on anonymized data (e.g.
(
Le and Carlsson 2023
;
Hukkelås et al. 2019
;
Rajabi et al. 2021
)
). An adversary which is aware of the anonymization can adapt to the anonymization and thus presents a greater threat. Consider, for example, an anonymization that performs a deterministic block permutation on a face image. The change to the data structure would most likely cause the trained recognition model break down, so the report back on the performance of the anonymization will be high. However, that report will be based on flawed premises and is very likely false.

The second problem we identified is that most evaluation methods assume that the recognition model which works best on clear data will also be the best model for recognizing people in anonymized data (e.g.
(
Tieu et al. 2019
;
Bahmaninezhad et al. 2018
;
Fang et al. 2019
)
). We challenge this assumption. Recognition models are developed on clear data, and so no consideration is given to tampering with the data. Therefore, we doubt whether the recognition model which works best on the clear data is also the best for the anonymized data.

The third problem we identified is that the same datasets are used to evaluate anonymization as are used to evaluate recognition (e.g.
(
Le and Carlsson 2023
;
Maximov et al. 2020
;
Rajabi et al. 2021
)
), and so it is that anonymization techniques are evaluated almost exclusively on large numbers of identities. We argue that it is more challenging for anonymization techniques when there are low numbers of identities in the dataset. Moreover, a low number of identities is also more realistic because any group of identities can usually be reduced by additionally individuating information.

4.2.
Our Improvements to State-of-the-Art Evaluation Methods

We use closed-set recognition for our general scenario. Any adversary in possession of a list of identities may simply test samples against the list in order then to be able to select the most likely identity for a given sample.

4.2.1.
Training recognition systems with anonymized data

In line with previous work
(
Newton et al. 2003
;
McPherson et al. 2016
;
Srivastava et al. 2020
;
Tomashenko et al. 2021
)
, we propose that recognition systems also be trained on anonymized data in order to get more reliable anonymization performance. The idea of retraining recognition systems was first proposed for face recognition by Newton et. al.
(
Newton et al. 2003
)
. Their model is trained with anonymized data and tested on anonymized data, a procedure they call parrot recognition. The authors report much better performance for parrot recognition compared to training with clear data, which is called naive recognition.

Parrot recognition is another term for the informed attacker, as defined by Srivastava et. al.
(
Srivastava et al. 2020
)
. In the evaluation of voice anonymization, Srivastava et. al.
(
Srivastava et al. 2020
)
propose three attackers who differ in their awareness of anonymization. The ignorant attacker is unaware of the anonymization (as in black-box assumptions), the semi-informed attacker knows the anonymization algorithm (as in gray-box assumptions), and the informed attacker knows the algorithm plus the given parameters (as in white-box assumptions).

The VoicePrivacy challenge
(
Tomashenko et al. 2021
;
Tomashenko et al. 2020b
)
used anonymized data to enroll identities in a speaker verification system and then tested the performance of the system against anonymized voice samples. It was found that enrollment by anonymized data already improved recognition performance; however, performance improvement was greater when the recognition was pre-trained on anonymized data. The results of the VoicePrivacy challenge show that training the recognition system on anonymized data leads to a much stronger evaluation of the privacy performance of a technique. Therefore, we recommend training, including pre-training, recognition systems on anonymized data. But even when a complete retraining of the model is not possible, the training with anonymized data alone can already pose a more difficult challenge to an anonymization.

4.2.2.
Test against different recognition systems

Most evaluation methods rely on the state-of-the-art recognition system currently available for the targeted biometric trait. However, during the design and development of recognition systems anonymization is not considered and as such recognition systems are not optimized to operate on anonymized data.
We, therefore, challenge the assumption that the state-of-the-art recognition systems will also be the one that performs best on the anonymized data.
For practical reasons, it is obvious that not all types of recognition systems can be used in an evaluation. However, at least a few conceptually different recognition systems should be tested in order to assess which techniques work best on the anonymized data. The aim here is to approximate worst-case performance of the anonymization.

4.2.3.
Use a more challenging evaluation dataset

As explained above, the datasets currently being used for the evaluation of biometric recognition are recorded and designed to pose challenging recognition problem. It is our proposition, though, that evaluators of anonymization use an easy recognition problem in order to create a challenging anonymization scenario. Since the recording of biometric datasets is time-consuming and expensive (not to mention complicated by legal regulations like GDPR), we propose that existing recognition datasets be reused so that the easy recognition problem becomes a hard anonymization problem. In particular, instead of using the entire dataset, we propose that all the identities in a dataset be drastically reduced in number and further, that the selection of identities be based on the criterion of easy distinguishability. In the reduced dataset, our strategies for identity selection are as follows:

•

Random: As our baseline selection strategy, we use a random selection of identities, which we repeat multiple times to account for the variability of the selection.

•

Classification: We use clear data on a biometric recognition system to classify the identities. This step enables us to select the identities which have the highest identification accuracy, as identities that perform well on clear data can be more easily identified in anonymized data.

•

Metadata: We operationalize the fact that most biometric datasets also contain metadata about the identities, such as age and gender, or that such metadata can be extracted via a recognition system. The rationale is that identities with diverse attributes can be more easily distinguished when images are anonymized. First, we normalize each point of metadata information between 0 and 1, and then we calculate the pair-wise Euclidean distance between the points. Second, we obtain a subset of identities by locating pairs of identities at the greatest distances from one another. And third, we calculate the average of distances between the identities in our subset, and then we consistently select the identity located at the maximum distance to the average.

•

Feature-space: Many recognition systems work by projecting the biometric data into a feature space and then calculating distances between the feature vectors. The rationale is that the recognition system is trained to project datapoints from the same identity onto similar features and as well, to project datapoints from different identities onto contrasting features. However, misclassification occurs when the feature of a datapoint belonging to one identity is farther from the correct feature and closer to a feature belonging to another identity. Therefore, it is our proposal that recognition performance be improved by the intentional selection of identities whose feature spaces are distant from one another. In other words, we choose identities who are very different to one another. Any one feature vector gets modified because the anonymization method has modified the datapoint, but if the correct identity is still closest to the feature vector, then the datapoint will be classified correctly. We use this idea to develop two selection strategies:

–

Distinctive: Inspired by the Biometric Menagerie
(
Yager and Dunstone 2010
)
, we calculate for each identity a genuine score and an imposter score (illustrated in Fig.
2
). The genuine score of an identity is the furthest Euclidean distance of any feature vector of this identity to the average of all feature vectors of this identity. Thus the score is effectively an intra-class distance; conversely, the imposter score is effectively an inter-class distance. The imposter score is the shortest Euclidean distance of the average of all feature vectors of this identity to a feature vector of any other identity. If this inter-class distance is high, then the identity is less likely to be misclassified because the features of other identities lie farther away. In sum, we select identities that have the best average of genuine and imposter scores.

–

Center: Our purpose is to create a subset of identities lying at the greatest distances from one another. As with the metadata vector above, we begin by selecting the two identities whose average feature vectors have the largest Euclidean distance. Then we consistently select the identities whose average feature vectors lie at maximum distances from the average feature vector of our subset of identities.

Simplified example for Genuine and Imposter scores of an identity A in a 2D projection of the feature space.

5.
Experiments

Our evaluation is based on the biological biometric of face and on the behavioral biometric of gait. We begin by stating our hypotheses, and then we describe the experiments and present the results.

5.1.
Hypotheses

Our aim in the evaluation is to test our three methodological proposals for improvements to the evaluation of biometric anonymization. We have proposed that recognition systems also be trained on anonymized data, that multiple recognition systems be used, and that a more challenging dataset be used. Therefore, we test whether training the recognition system on anonymized data achieves more reliable anonymization performance (H1, H2), whether a single recognition system simulates worst-case performance on all anonymizations (H3), and lastly whether a reduction in the number of identities in the evaluation dataset and the way the ids are chosen more robustly challenges the performance of the anonymization (H4, H5).

Our Hypotheses H1 and H2 hold that training recognition systems on anonymized data will achieve higher recognition performance. For our
H1
, we expect that (pre-)training recognition systems with anonymized data of the respective anonymization will result in higher recognition accuracies compared to (pre-)training on clear data. Further, for
H2
, we expect also that (pre-)training on partial anonymized datasets will perform better compared to (pre-)training on clear data. Further, we expect that increasing the amount of anonymized data in the train set will increase the recognition performance. We reason that the models we test must necessarily generalize more suitably to data that are noisier.

Our Hypothesis
H3
holds that no single recognition system will achieve the best performance on every anonymization. Our prediction for H3 is that, independent of results on clear data, some recognition systems will outperform others when using anonymized data. We reason that some recognition system will better represent the structure contained in the anonymized data.

Our Hypothesis
H4
holds that reducing the number of identities in the evaluation dataset will present a more robust challenge to the performance of the anonymization. Our H5 builds on H4. For
H5
, we expect that selecting an evaluation dataset with our proposed selection strategies will pose a bigger challenge to the anonymization, and hence result in higher recognition performance then selecting random identities.

5.2.
Experiments

We set an optimal performance bound by using chance-level performance of the anonymization as our baseline. We reason that perfect anonymization would leave adversaries with such a negligible advantage that their most effective strategy would be to guess identities at random. To approximate worst-case performance of the anonymization, we use the performance of clear level recognition, that is, the performance of the recognition system on clear data.

To test H1, we follow the same procedure for each anonymization technique: the recognition system is trained on the respective anonymized training data, and where possible by the model, the system is also pre-trained on the anonymized data. To test H2, we train the recognition system on different compositions of anonymized and clear training data using 25%, 50%, and 75% anonymized training data. Hence, we assess our H1 and H2 by parrot recognition and by naive recognition.

For our H3, we use different recognition systems and perform parrot recognition for each anonymization.

For our H4, we again perform parrot recognition. However, instead of using the full evaluation dataset we only use a random subset of identities for 50%, 25%, 12.5%, …, until three identities remain of the original identities. For each number of identities the sampling is repeated five times to account for the variability of the random selection. Finally, in our last experiment for H5, we use the same numbers of identities as in the experiments for H4, but instead of randomly selecting, we choose identities with the strategies described above in our methodology: Random, Classification, Metadata, Distinctive, and Center (see subsection
4.2.3
).

5.3.
Datasets

For the face recognition, we use the CelebA
(
Liu et al. 2015
)
dataset because it is popular for face recognition and for anonymization evaluation. We randomly select 1,000 identities with at least eight images, and limit the maximum number of images per identity to 20. We crop all images to the face region, with images containing multiple faces cropped to the largest one. We resize all images to 224x224 pixel and rotate them until eyes are level.

For gait we use the dataset of the gait patterns of 57 identities by Horst et. al.
(
Horst et al. 2019
)
. The dataset represents the most comprehensive publicly available dataset that contains multiple gait samples per identity, and this, in particular, recommends the dataset to the evaluation of anonymization performance. For each identity in the dataset there are 20 gait sequences, and we resample these to be 100 frames long. The dataset has used optical markers to capture motion. The motion capture covers 52 tracked points, each given as absolute 3D position (see Fig.
3
).

5.4.
Evaluation Framework

In order to run our experiments, we implemented the evaluation framework depicted in Fig.
4
.

First, the selector selects a subset of the dataset for our experiments for H4 and H5. This subset is then copied and anonymized with a specific anonymization technique. Second, the splitter splits the samples per identity into two sets, with 75% of samples going into the train set and 25% going into the test set. Depending on the configuration, either the clear samples or the anonymized samples go into the respective datasets.

Third and last, the recognition system is trained and evaluated with the test dataset. The resulting likelihood for a given test sample is recorded and saved for each identity.

5.5.
Recognition systems

For face recognition, we use the DeepFace
(
Serengil and Ozpinar 2020
)
library because it covers the entire face recognition process and includes pre-trained models for ArcFace
(
Deng et al. 2019
)
, Facenet
(
Schroff et al. 2015
)
, and VGG-Face
(
Parkhi et al. 2015
)
. Additionally, we use the face recognition model (frknn)
(
Geitgey 2021
)
, which uses a pre-trained feature extractor. We also pre-trained multiple models of ArcFace, then referred to as Retrained ArcFace, using the remaining identities in CelebA with the respective anonymization technique applied to the samples. For %-parrot recognition approaches only the respective percentage of the samples in the background dataset was anonymized.
We validated Retrained ArcFace on clear data and achieved similar identification accuracy than the regular pre-trained ArcFace.

For gait recognition, we use two types of feature vectors. The flatten feature vector simply flattens all poses of a gait sequence into a single vector, as proposed by Horst et al.
(
Horst et al. 2019
)
. The simple feature vector does a principal component analysis (PCA) over all poses of a walking sequence and then concatenates the 4 first components of the PCA with an average over all poses of the sequence. For classification, we use support vector machines (SVM), random forest, and k-nearest neighbors. Unless stated otherwise, we used the combination SVM+flatten for gait recognition.

5.6.2.
Simple Gait Anonymization

For gait recognition, we use simple anonymization techniques for gait to select precisely which information to perturb in the samples. First, we suppress parts of the samples:
Keep(legs)
and

both keep only the captured points for legs and head, respectively, while all other points are set to zero. Second, we perturb the samples:

Motion Extraction
captures the differences between each next pose in order to extract only the dynamic parts of the data. The structure of the walkers is, then, effectively removed.

6.
Results

We report here on the results of our experiments. We assess, in turn, the validity of each of our hypotheses: whether recognition systems trained on anonymized data improve evaluation performance (H1, H2), whether a single recognition system performs best on every anonymization (H3), and lastly whether a reduction in the number of identities and selection of identities in the evaluation dataset poses a real challenge to the performance of the anonymization (H4, H5).

6.1.
Evaluation techniques are trained on anonymized data

In Fig.
6
and Fig.
7
, we present the results of our experiments for H1 and H2 on the anonymization of face images and gait motion captures respectively.

For face images, we find that, except for CIAGAN and
k
-Same-Pixel, all parrot recognition systems perform better than naive recognition. For CIAGAN, naive recognition performs best, while for
k
-Same-Pixel, all recognition systems have nearly the same performance. This anomaly makes sense when we consider how CIAGAN performs the anonymization: every face is replaced by another face which shares the same soft biometrics. Therefore, we assume that CIAGAN’s replacement of the face on each training image makes it harder to learn useful feature vectors.

We find significant results for parrot recognition of face anonymization. The performance of both full parrot recognition and all %-parrot recognition cluster close together for most anonymizations. In fact, %-parrot recognition often achieves the same performance as the full parrot recognition, and for DP Snow, the 75%-parrot recognition even outperforms the full parrot recognition.

In contrast to our results for face anonymization, the results for gait anonymization show full parrot recognition outperforming %-parrot recognition, with the exception of all Noise anonymization (cf. Fig.
7
). For all anonymizations, naive recognition performs only at the level of chance. The %-parrot results for Noise(3) and Noise(10) are interesting because 25% performs best, 50% performs second best, 75% performs third best, and full parrot performs worst.

In our results for both face and gait anonymization, one thing defied our predictions. In the face and gait anonymization of Noise(3), Noise(10), and DP Snow, anonymization performance improves when the model is trained solely on a portion of anonymized images rather than on the full anonymized training set. We note here, though, that these anonymizations have in common that they perform noise injection either by adding noise to each datapoint or by randomly removing pixels from the image. That portion of noisy data samples in the training set enables Noise(3), Noise(10), and DP Snow to adapt while still learning the features required for the classification from the clear data. Our conclusion is this: there is a tipping point where more noisy data no longer improves training performance but begins impairing it.

6.4.
Summary of Results

•

For training, and especially pre-training: recognition performance increases when the system is trained or pre-trained on anonymized data.

•

For the evaluation dataset: recognition performance increases when the number of identities is reduced. This reduction in identities makes it more difficult to anonymize the biometric data.

•

For our Distinctive selection strategy: Distinctive provides reliable evaluation of anonymization. However, Distinctive might be outperformed by best-case random selections when the number of identities in the evaluation dataset is very small.

•

For anonymization techniques: Since we have shown that techniques perform differently across recognition systems, it remains unclear which anonymization techniques perform best in conjunction with which recognition system.

•

For some anonymization techniques using noise injection: it is crucial to determine the optimal proportion of anonymized data for both training and pre-training.

7.
Discussion, Limitations, and Future Work

The results of our three experiments confirm all five of our hypotheses. We see ourselves justified in drawing the overall conclusion that our recommendation for the evaluation of the anonymization of biometric data will improve the state-of-the-art in evaluation methodology.

Our results for H1 and H2 clearly show that training and also pre-training with anonymized data significantly improves the performance of the recognition and thus opens the door to improved evaluation of face and gait anonymization. As demonstrated for face anonymization, even a small amount of anonymized data greatly improves the training process. However, our results also indicate that an excess of noisy training data may decrease the performance. Therefore, for anonymization by noise injection (e.g. Laplace mechanism), we conclude that effort should be expended on determining the right amount of anonymized training data. Nonetheless, we draw the final conclusion that training with anonymized data significantly improves the validity of the evaluation methodology. Without anonymized data in the train set, the performance of the anonymization is bound to be overestimated.

Our results for H3 show that the recognition systems which perform comparably to one another on clear data may perform differently on anonymized data. Since the performance on clear data is not a good predictor of performance on anonymized data, we conclude that the recognition system which seems to perform at the state-of-the-art on clear data might not accurately evaluate anonymization performance. Our conclusion holds especially true for anonymizations which use noise injection, as demonstrated by our results for gait anonymization. Therefore, we consider it the minimum that multiple recognition systems be used with different model architectures. Furthermore, since for both face and gait anonymization, there is no single recognition system that outperforms another, we recommend designing recognition systems to be more resistant to anonymization. It seems obvious that any evaluation techniques which lack anonymization-resistance is going to fail to properly evaluate the performance of the anonymization.

Our results for H4 confirm that for most anonymization techniques a reduced number of identities in the evaluation dataset increases the recognition performance and thus presents a more challenging scenario for the anonymization. Our results here also show that, as the number of identities decreases, the run-to-run variation of possible results increases. We conclude that the selection of identities for the subset is a significant task in the evaluation of anonymization performance.

Our results for H5 show clear indication that a more challenging dataset is generated when our Distinctive selection strategy is used to select the identities for a reduced evaluation dataset. However, the advantages of our Distinctive strategy over repeated random sampling diminish with decreasing numbers of identities in the evaluation datasets. Furthermore, for very small sets (i.e., smaller than 15 identities), our Distinctive strategy delivers unreliable results for some anonymizations. Therefore in general we recommend our Distinctive strategy, but for very small numbers of identities, we recommend verifying on multiple random samples of identities.

All in all, we have proposed improvements that we deem necessary to convincingly evaluate biometric anonymization techniques. Further research, however, is clearly necessary. For example validating our methodology improvements on other biometric traits.
Another topic which deserves attention is the investigation which types of recognition systems perform best on which types of anonymization to learn if there is a systematic approach for building recognition systems that perform well on specific anonymizations.

8.
Conclusion

Biometric recognition technologies such as face recognition systems pose a real threat to privacy. Therefore, the technique of anonymization is crucial to privacy protection, and likewise, evaluation is crucial to anonymization. This paper assessed the state-of-the-art methodologies used for the evaluation of anonymization techniques, points out flaws, and makes proposals how to address them.
We find several major flaws in the state-of-the-art methodologies for the evaluation of biometric anonymization. The state-of-the-art evaluation is based on weak and unrealistic assumptions about the adversary. The adversaries of state-of-the-art evaluation act in ignorance of the anonymization in place and are accordingly unable to adapt their recognition systems. These are not real adversaries of anonymization techniques. Therefore, the state-of-the-art methodologies largely fail to assess accurately the performance of the recognition.

To begin the work of correcting such flaws, we have proposed to improve the evaluation methodology for the anonymization of biometric data. We recommend that recognition systems trained not only with clear data but also anonymized data should be used to evaluate anonymization performance. Furthermore, we argue that the use of a variety of different recognition systems will improve the rigor of the evaluation. The use of merely a single classifier which has been trained on clear data might result in unreliable, overoptimistic estimates of anonymization performance. Hence, we recommend using multiple recognition systems trained on anonymized data.
And lastly, we recommend to use a more challenging evaluation dataset to approximate worst-case performance. Our results indicate that such a dataset can be constructed by reducing the number of identities and selecting the easy to distinguish identities with our proposed Distinctive strategy. According to our experimental results, our recommendations lead to a more reliable evaluation as anonymization performance is not overestimated.
