On Estimating Rank-One Spiked Tensors in the Presence of Heavy Tailed Errors ∗
Arnab Auddy, Ming Yuan

Abstract
In this paper, we study the estimation of a rank-one spiked tensor in the presence of heavy tailed noise. Our results highlight some of the fundamental similarities and differences in the tradeoff between statistical and computational efficiencies under heavy tailed and Gaussian noise. In particular, we show that, for p p th order tensors, the tradeoff manifests in an identical fashion as the Gaussian case when the noise has finite 4 ​ ( p − 1 ) 4(p-1) th moment. The difference in signal strength requirements, with or without computational constraints, for us to estimate the singular vectors at the optimal rate, interestingly, narrows for noise with heavier tails and vanishes when the noise only has finite fourth moment. Moreover, if the noise has less than fourth moment, tensor SVD, perhaps the most natural approach, is suboptimal even though it is computationally intractable. Our analysis exploits a close connection between estimating the rank-one spikes and the spectral norm of a random tensor with iid entries. In particular, we show that the order of the spectral norm of a random tensor can be precisely characterized by the moment of its entries, generalizing classical results for random matrices. In addition to the theoretical guarantees, we propose estimation procedures for the heavy tailed regime, which are easy to implement and efficient to run. Numerical experiments are presented to demonstrate their practical merits.

Introduction

Singular value decomposition (SVD) and principal component analysis (PCA) are among the most commonly used procedures in multivariate data analysis. See, e.g.,
Anderson 1984
;
Jolliffe 2002
. By seeking low rank approximations to a data matrix, they allow us to reduce the dimensionality of the data, and oftentimes serve as a useful first step to capture the essential features in the data. While both were first developed for the analysis of data matrices, extensions to higher order tensors have also been developed in recent years. See, e.g.,
De Lathauwer et al. 2000b
;
Lu et al. 2008
;
Liu et al. 2017
. More generally, low rank tensor methods have exploded in popularity in numerous areas involving high dimensional data analysis. See
Kolda and Bader 2009
;
Anandkumar et al. 2014
;
Cichocki et al. 2015
;
Sidiropoulos et al. 2017
for recent reviews.

To fix ideas, consider a rank-one spiked tensor model

\mathscr{X}=\lambda\mathbf{u}_{1}\otimes\mathbf{u}_{2}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbf{u}_{p}+\mathscr{E},

(1)

\lambda\geq 0
is a scalar, and “singular vectors”
\mathbf{u}_{k}

\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\mathscr{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times\cdots\times d}
is a noise tensor whose entries are independent and identically distributed random variables with zero mean and unit variance. The goal is to estimate the singular vectors after observing
\mathscr{X}
in a high dimensional setting where
d
is large. In particular, the special case when the noise tensor
\mathscr{E}
consists of independent standard normal entries has attracted much attention in recent years, and an intriguing gap in statistical efficiencies with or without computational constraints is observed. It can be shown that tensor SVD that seeks the best rank-one approximation to

The Gaussian, or more generally subgaussian, assumption on the noise tensor

\mathscr{E}

Due to the suboptimality of tensor SVD, it is doubtful if power iteration would work when
\alpha<4
. To this end, we consider a different estimating strategy. More specifically, our techniques are based on recent developments in the theory of robust estimation of the mean in the presence of heavy tailed errors. These works derive estimators with subgaussian concentration, inspired from the pioneering work of
Catoni 2012
. The key idea is to reduce the adverse effect of heavy tails through an influence function, and can be extended to matrix estimation. For covariance matrix estimation,
Catoni 2016
and
Mendelson and Zhivotovskiy 2020
were some of the first works in this area, although both these approaches involved optimizing over a
d
-dimensional
\varepsilon
-net and thus having exponential time complexity.
Avella-Medina et al. 2018
have similar results with polynomial time, but they too require an extensive search for tuning parameters. We will instead use results on spectrum truncated estimators applied to covariance estimation.
Giulini 2015
described one such method for robust PCA through smooth truncation, based on which
Minsker 2018
and
Ke et al. 2019
provided more tractable procedures and general results.

Our results are obtained by exploiting close connections between estimating the rank-one spikes and the spectral norm of a random tensor of iid entries. We show that the order of the spectral norm of a random tensor can be precisely characterized by the moment of its entries, which might be of independent interest. In particular, our result indicates that, up to a logarithmic factor, the norm of the random tensor
\|\mathscr{E}\|
is of the order
\sqrt{d}
if and only if its entries have finite
4(p-1)
th moment. This can be viewed as a generalization of the classical results for random matrices
(
Bai et al. 1988
;
Silverstein 1989
, see, e.g.,)
. In deriving these bounds, we used techniques developed for random matrices by
Latała 2005
and improved moment bounds of random tensors established earlier by
Nguyen et al. 2015
.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. We first develop probabilistic bounds for the spectral norm of a random tensor of iid entries and use these tools to study the performance of the tensor SVD in Section 2. Polynomial time computable estimation schemes are given in Sections 3 and 4 for
\alpha\geq 4
and for
\alpha\geq 2
respectively. To corroborate our theoretical development, Section 5 provides simulation studies to further demonstrate the practical merits of the proposed methods. We conclude with a few remarks on the implications and future directions in Section 6. All proofs are relegated to Section 7.

2
Tensor SVD and Spectral Norm of Random Tensors

The most natural approach to estimating the singular vectors is via the tensor SVD. In particular, let

(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\rm SVD}:1\leq k\leq p)=\argmax_{\mathbf{a}_{k}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\langle\mathscr{X},\mathbf{a}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{a}_{p}\rangle,

(3)

Here
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
is the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. It is well known that the tensor SVD can be equivalently characterized the best rank-one apprpoximation to
\mathscr{X}
in that

(\widehat{\lambda}^{\rm SVD},\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\rm SVD}:1\leq k\leq p)=\argmax_{\gamma\in\mathbb{R},\mathbf{a}_{k}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\|\mathscr{X}-\gamma\mathbf{a}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{a}_{p}\|_{\rm HS},

where
\|\cdot\|_{\rm HS}
is the Hilbert-Schmidt or Frobenius norm. See, e.g.,
Zhang and Golub 2001
. The performance of these singular vector estimates is closely related to the spectral norm of the noise tensor:

\|\mathscr{E}\|:=\max_{\mathbf{a}_{k}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\langle\mathscr{E},\mathbf{a}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{a}_{p}\rangle.

For example, it is known that

\max_{1\leq k\leq p}\sin\angle(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\rm SVD},\mathbf{u}_{k})\lesssim{\|\mathscr{E}\|\over\lambda},

(4)

so that
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\rm SVD}
s are consistent whenever
\lambda\gg\|\mathscr{E}\|
. See, e.g.,
Auddy and Yuan 2020
. To this end, we shall first study the spectral norm of a random tensor consisting of independent and identically distributed entries.

2.1
Norm of Random Tensors

The problem of bounding the spectral norm of a random tensor is well-studied in the matrix case, i.e.,
p=2
. In particular,
Silverstein 1989
showed that if
\mathscr{E}
is an iid ensemble, then
\|\mathscr{E}\|
is of the order
\sqrt{d}
if and only if its entries have finite (weak) fourth moment. In other words, when
p=2
and the entries of
\mathscr{E}
have finite fourth moment,
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\rm SVD}
s are consistent if and only if
\lambda\gg\sqrt{d}
. A couple of questions arise naturally. First, is there similar characterization of
\|\mathscr{E}\|
for higher order tensors? And what happens if the entries of
\mathscr{E}
have only
\alpha
th moment for
2\leq\alpha<4
? The next result aims to settle the first question.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\mathscr{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times\cdots\times d}
be a
p
th order random tensor whose entries are independent copies of a random variable
E
with mean zero and variance
\sigma^{2}
. Then there exists a constant
C_{p}>0
depending on
p
only such that for any
\alpha\geq 4
,
\mathbb{E}|E|^{\alpha}<\infty
implies that, with probability at least
1-d^{-\alpha/4+1}
,

\|\mathscr{E}\|\leq C_{p}\sigma\left[\sqrt{d\log d}+d^{\tfrac{p-1}{\alpha}+\tfrac{1}{4}}(\log d)^{\tfrac{3}{2}}\right].

Conversely, there exists another constant
C_{p}^{\prime}>0
depending on
p
only such that
\mathbb{E}|E|^{\alpha}=\infty
implies that

\|\mathscr{E}\|\geq C^{\prime}_{p}\sigma d^{\max\left\{\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{p-1}{\alpha}+\tfrac{1}{4}\right\}},\qquad{\rm almost\ surely}.

The lower and upper bounds of Theorem
2.1
match up to the logarithmic factor. In particular,
\|\mathscr{E}\|
is of the order
\sqrt{d}
, up to a logarithmic factor, if and only if its entries have finite
4(p-1)
th moment. This can be viewed as a generalization of the classical result for
p=2
from
Silverstein 1989
. For higher order tensors (
p>2
), the precise order of
\|\mathscr{E}\|
depends on the value of
\alpha
for
4\leq\alpha<4(p-1)
. Consider, for example,
p=3
. Then
\|\mathscr{E}\|
is of the same order as that of an iid Gaussian ensemble, up to at most a logarithmic factor, as soon as
E
has finite eighth moment. Yet, if
E
only has finite
\alpha
th moment for
4\leq\alpha<8
, then
\|\mathscr{E}\|
depends on the exact value of
\alpha
, and decreases as
\alpha
increases.

The next result complements Theorem
2.1
and deals with the case when
2\leq\alpha<4
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
\mathscr{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times\cdots\times d}
be a
p
th order random tensor whose entries are independent copies of a random variable
E
with mean zero and variance
\sigma^{2}
. There exist constants
C_{p},C_{p}^{\prime}>0
depending on
p
only such that for any
2\leq\alpha<4
,
\mathbb{E}|E|^{\alpha}<\infty
implies that, with probability at least
1-d^{-\alpha/2+1}
,

\|\mathscr{E}\|\leq C_{p}\sigma d^{\tfrac{p-1}{\alpha}+\tfrac{1}{2}}(\log d)^{\tfrac{3}{2}}.

Conversely, if
\mathbb{E}|E|^{\alpha}=\infty
then

\|\mathscr{E}\|\geq C^{\prime}_{p}\sigma d^{\tfrac{p}{\alpha}},\qquad{\rm almost\ surely}.

Note that there is a gap between the upper bound and lower bound in Theorem
2.2
beyond the logarithmic factor. While it is plausible that this is the result of our proof technique, it remains a possibility that this may point to something more fundamental.

2.2
Convergence Rates for Tensor SVD

In light of (
4
), Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
immediately imply the consistency of
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\rm SVD}
s when

\lambda\gg\lambda_{\rm crit}(d;\alpha):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}d^{1/2}(\log d)^{1/2}&{\rm if\ }\alpha>4(p-1)\\
d^{{\tfrac{p-1}{\alpha}+\tfrac{1}{4}}}(\log d)^{3/2}&{\rm if\ }4\leq\alpha\leq 4(p-1)\\
d^{{\tfrac{p-1}{\alpha}+\tfrac{1}{2}}}(\log d)^{3/2}&{\rm if\ }2\leq\alpha<4\end{array}\right..

(5)

In fact, under this condition of the signal-to-noise ratio, much stronger statement can be made and in fact,
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\rm SVD}
s can be shown to be rate optimal:

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\mathscr{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times\cdots\times d}
be a
p
th order random tensor whose entries are independent random variable with mean zero, variance one, and finite
\alpha
th moment, e.g.,
\mathbb{E}|E|^{\alpha}<\infty
for some
\alpha\geq 2
. Then there exist a numerical constant
C>0
and another constant
C_{p}
depending on
p
only such that if
\lambda\geq C\lambda_{\rm crit}(d;\alpha)
, then

\max_{1\leq k\leq p}\sin\angle(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\rm SVD},\mathbf{u}_{k})\leq C_{p}{\sqrt{d}\over\lambda},

with probability tending to one as
d
increases.

For comparison, under Gaussian noise,
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\rm SVD}
converges to
\mathbf{u}_{k}
at the optimal rate of
\sqrt{d}/\lambda
as soon as
\lambda>C\sqrt{d}
for some constant
C>0
. Theorem
2.3
shows that the same is true, up to a logarithmic factor, when the entries of
\mathscr{E}
has finite
4(p-1)
th moment. However, when
\alpha<4(p-1)
, the rate
\sqrt{d}/\lambda
can only be achieved when
\lambda
is much larger than that required with Gaussian errors. Nonetheless the following result shows that when
\alpha>4
these requirements are indeed optimal, up to a logarithmic factor, and therefore highlight a fundamental difference in behavior of tensor SVD with heavy tailed and Gaussian noise.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
\mathscr{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times\cdots\times d}
be a
p
th order random tensor whose entries are independent copies of a random variable
E
such that
\mathbb{E}|E|^{\alpha}=\infty
for some
4<\alpha<4(p-1)
yet
\mathbb{E}|E|^{\beta}<\infty
for some
\beta>\max\{(p-2)\alpha/(p-1),4\}
. If
\lambda<C\max\{d^{\tfrac{(p-1)}{\alpha}+\tfrac{1}{4}},\,\sqrt{d}\}
for any constant
C>0
, then for any constant
0<C_{0}<1
,

\min_{1\leq k\leq p}\sin\angle(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\rm SVD},\mathbf{u}_{k})\geq C_{0},

with probability tending to one, as
d\to\infty
. Similarly, suppose that
\mathbb{E}|E|^{\alpha}=\infty
for some
2<\alpha<4
and
\mathbb{E}|E|^{\beta}<\infty
for some
\beta>\max\{(2p-4)\alpha/(2p-\alpha),2\}
. If
\lambda<Cd^{{p}/{\alpha}}
for any constant
C>0
, then for any constant
0<C_{0}<1,

\min_{1\leq k\leq p}\sin\angle(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\rm SVD},\mathbf{u}_{k})\geq C_{0}

with probability tending to one, as
d\to\infty
.

For concreteness, consider a continuous distribution symmetric about 0 whose survival function is given by

\bar{F}(x):=1-F(x)=x^{-\alpha}L(x),\qquad{x>0}

where
L(x)
is slowly varying function at
+\infty
in that
L(x)>0
and

\lim_{x\to\infty}{L(tx)\over L(x)}=1,\qquad t>0.

For such distributions,
\alpha
is often referred to as their tail index. It is clear that for
E\sim F
,
\mathbb{E}(|E|^{q})=\infty
if and only if
q\geq\alpha
. In light of Theorem
2.4
, when
\alpha>4
,
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\rm SVD}
is inconsistent if
\lambda\lesssim\max\{d^{\tfrac{(p-1)}{\alpha}+\tfrac{1}{4}},\,\sqrt{d}\}
; when
2<\alpha<4
,
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\rm SVD}
is inconsistent if
\lambda\lesssim d^{{p}/{\alpha}}
. Conversely as a result of Theorem
2.3
,
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\rm SVD}
converges to
\mathbf{u}_{k}
at the optimal rate if
\lambda\gtrsim\lambda_{\rm crit}(d;\alpha-\epsilon)
for any
\epsilon>0
.

Interestingly, perhaps also surprisingly at the first sight, the inferior signal strength requirement for estimating the singular vectors under heavy-tailed noise is only a limitation of the tensor SVD and not a fundamental barrier in general. We now show that it is possible to improve the tensor SVD via a different estimation strategy at least when the signal-to-noise ratio is sufficiently high.

3
Power Iteration with Spectral Initiation

One of the chief challenges with the tensor SVD is the computational cost. It is well known that computing the best rank-one approximation
(
3
) is NP hard
(
Hackbusch 2012
;
Hillar and Lim 2013
, e.g.,)
so that it is infeasible to compute
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\rm SVD}_{k}
s for large
d
. A common strategy to overcome this difficulty is to apply power iteration with spectral initialization, which has been shown to yield an estimator that is both polynomial time computable and rate optimal in the presence of Gaussian error. See, e.g.,
Richard and Montanari 2014
;
Liu et al. 2017
. We shall now show that this strategy continues to work whenever
\alpha>4
.

Recall that the first order condition yields that
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\rm SVD}_{k}
s satisfies

\mathscr{X}\times_{j\neq k}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\rm SVD}_{j}\propto\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\rm SVD}_{k},\qquad 1\leq k\leq p.

Motivated by this property, we shall consider estimating
\mathbf{u}_{k}
through power iteration:

\mathbf{x}_{k}^{[t+1]}={\mathscr{X}\times_{j\neq k}\mathbf{x}_{j}^{[t]}\over\left\|\mathscr{X}\times_{j\neq k}\mathbf{x}_{j}^{[t]}\right\|},

(6)

with initial estimates
\mathbf{x}_{j}^{[0]}
s. For this to work, we first need to be able to find a “reasonably good” initial estimate
\mathbf{x}_{j}^{[0]}
that can be efficiently computed. This is usually done through HOSVD.

More specifically, denote by
{\sf Mat}_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{d\times\cdots\times d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d^{p-1}}
the operator that collapses all indices other than the
k
th one of a
p
th order tensor and therefore converts it into a
d\times d^{p-1}
matrix. Write

\mathscr{T}=\lambda\mathbf{u}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{u}_{p}.

It is not hard to see that

{\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{T})=\lambda\mathbf{u}_{k}(\mathbf{u}_{1}\odot\cdots\odot\mathbf{u}_{k-1}\odot\mathbf{u}_{k+1}\odot\cdots\odot\mathbf{u}_{p})^{\top},

where
\odot
stands for the Kronecker product so that we can estimate
\mathbf{u}_{k}
by the leading left singular vector, denoted by
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\rm Mat}
, of
{\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{X})
. Observe that

\mathbb{E}\left[{\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{X}){\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{X})^{\top}\right]=\lambda^{2}\mathbf{u}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{u}_{k}+d^{p-1}I,

and
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\rm Mat}
is the leading eigenvectors of
{\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{X}){\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{X})^{\top}-d^{p-1}I
. By Davis-Kahan Theorem, we have

\displaystyle\sin\angle(\mathbf{u}_{k},\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\rm Mat})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{2\|{\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{E}){\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{E})^{\top}+{\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{T}){\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{E})^{\top}+{\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{E}){\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{T})^{\top}-d^{p-1}I\|\over\lambda^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{2\|{\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{E}){\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{E})^{\top}-d^{p-1}I\|+4\|{\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{T}){\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{E})^{\top}\|\over\lambda^{2}}.

Following Bai-Yin’s law, we then have

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\mathscr{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times\cdots\times d}
be a
p
th order random tensor whose entries are independent copies of a random variable
E
with mean zero, variance one and
\mathbb{E}|E|^{\alpha}<\infty
for some
\alpha\geq 4
. Then

\max_{1\leq k\leq p}\sin\angle(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\rm Mat},\mathbf{u}_{k})=O_{p}\left(\dfrac{d^{p/2}+\lambda\sqrt{d}}{\lambda^{2}}\right),\qquad{\rm as\ }d\to\infty.

Proposition
3.1
indicates that
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\rm Mat}
s are consistent as soon as
\lambda\gg d^{p/4}
. It is worth comparing this requirement with that of
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\rm SVD}
s:
\lambda\gg d^{\max\{1/2,1/4+(p-1)/\alpha\}}
. See Theorem
2.4
. The former is more restrictive since
\alpha\geq 4
. As in the Gaussian noise case, this gap is likely a display of the tradeoff between computational and statistical efficiencies:
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\rm Mat}
is computationally tractable yet
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\rm SVD}
in general is not. On the other hand, the convergence rate for
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\rm Mat}
is inferior to that of
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\rm SVD}
. However, we can improve upon
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\rm Mat}
s by using
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{j}^{\rm Mat}
s in place of
{\mathbf{x}}^{[0]}_{j}
s in (
6
) to get an updated estimate.

To see how this works, write

\mathbf{x}^{[t]}_{j}=\sqrt{1-\rho_{j}^{2}}\mathbf{u}_{j}+\rho_{j}\mathbf{v}_{j}

where
\mathbf{v}_{j}
is a unit length vector perpendicular to
\mathbf{u}_{j}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathscr{X}\times_{j\neq k}\mathbf{x}^{[t]}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda\left(\prod_{j\neq k}\sqrt{1-\rho_{j}^{2}}\right)\mathbf{u}_{k}

\displaystyle+\sum_{A\subset([p]\setminus\{k\})}\left(\prod_{j\in A}\sqrt{1-\rho_{j}^{2}}\right)\left(\prod_{j\notin A\cup\{k\}}\rho_{j}\right)\mathscr{E}\times_{j\in A}\mathbf{u}_{j}\times_{j\notin A\cup\{k\}}\mathbf{v}_{j}.

Note that the second term on the righthand side can be bounded by, up to a constant,
\|\mathscr{E}\|
. In light of Proposition
3.1
, this implies that, if
\rho_{j}
are uniformly bounded away from 1, then

\sin\angle\left(\mathbf{x}_{k}^{[t+1]},\mathbf{u}_{k}\right)=O_{p}\left({\|\mathscr{E}\|\over\lambda}\right).

In particular, in the case of Gaussian errors,
\|\mathscr{E}\|=O_{p}(\sqrt{d})
so that we can conclude that

\sin\angle\left(\mathbf{x}_{k}^{[1]},\mathbf{u}_{k}\right)=O_{p}\left({\|\mathscr{E}\|\over\lambda}\right)

suggesting that a single iteration with
\mathbf{x}_{k}^{[0]}=\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\rm Mat}
(
k=1,\ldots,p
) leads to rate optimal estimates of
\mathbf{u}_{k}
. The same technique can be applied whenever
\alpha>4(p-1)
thanks to Theorem
2.1
. The argument, however, breaks down when
\alpha<4(p-1)
and a single iteration no longer suffices. Nonetheless, a more careful analysis shows that the performance keeps improving with more iterations and
O(\log d)
number of iterations can yield a rate optimal of
\mathbf{u}_{k}
s.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\mathscr{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times\cdots\times d}
be a
p
th order random tensor whose entries are independent copies of a random variable
E
with mean zero, variance one and
\mathbb{E}|E|^{\alpha}<\infty
for some
\alpha>4
. There exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that if
\lambda>C_{1}d^{p/4}
and
\rho^{[t]}<1
, then

\rho^{[t+1]}\leq C_{2}(\rho^{[t]})^{2}{\|\mathscr{E}\|\over\lambda}+O_{p}\left({\sqrt{d}\over\lambda}\right),

where

\rho^{[t]}=\max_{1\leq k\leq p}\sin\angle(\mathbf{x}^{[t]}_{k},\mathbf{u}_{k}).

In light of Propositions
3.1
and
3.2
, we can estimate
\mathbf{u}_{k}
by running power iterations (
6
) with initialization

\mathbf{x}_{k}^{[0]}=\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\rm Mat},\qquad k=1,\ldots,p.

And

\max_{1\leq k\leq p}\sin\angle(\mathbf{x}_{k}^{[T]},\mathbf{u}_{k})=O_{p}\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{d}}{\lambda}\right),\qquad{\rm as\ }d\to\infty,

for
T\gtrsim\log d
provided that
\lambda\geq Cd^{p/4}
for a sufficiently large constant
C>0
. This proves that

Theorem 3.3
.

Assume that the entries of
\mathscr{E}
are independent and identically distributed with zero mean, unit variance and finite
\alpha
th moment for some
\alpha>4
. There exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that if
\lambda>C_{1}d^{p/4}
, then there is a polynomial time computable estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}
(
k=1,\ldots,p
) obeying

\max_{1\leq k\leq p}\sin\angle(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k},\mathbf{u}_{k})\leq{C_{2}\sqrt{d}\over\lambda},

with probability tending to one as
d\to\infty
.

For this strategy to work we need
\lambda>\|\mathscr{E}\|
. However, in light of Theorem
2.2
, this would require a higher signal-to-noise ratio than
d^{p/4}
when
\alpha<4
. It turns out that while the vanilla power iteration may not work for smaller
\alpha
s, it is possible to attain both statistical and computational efficiencies as long as
\lambda\gtrsim d^{p/4}
for any
\alpha\geq 2
.

4
Tractable Estimation for all
\alpha\geq 2

As indicated in Theorem
3.3
, HOSVD and power iteration yields a consistent estimator under the signal strength requirement
\lambda\gtrsim d^{p/4}
only if the entries of
\mathscr{E}
have finite fourth moment. This can no longer be successful when
\lambda\sim d^{p/4}
and
\alpha<4
, even without computational considerations, as shown by Theorem
2.4
. To resolve this issue, we need to modify both the initialization and the power iteration steps. We first describe a new way for initialization.

4.1
Initialization by Robust HOSVD

The rationale behind the spectral initialization presented in the previous section is that
{\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{X}){\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{X})^{\top}
is an unbiased estimate of
\lambda^{2}\mathbf{u}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{u}_{k}
. However, this incurs bounding
\|{\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{E}){\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{E})^{\top}-I\|
which requires finite fourth moment of the entries of
\mathscr{E}
. To relax this condition, we shall now proceed to estimate
\lambda^{2}\mathbf{u}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{u}_{k}
via a more robust approach that works as long as the entries of
\mathscr{E}
have finite variance.

In particular, we shall adopt a method first developed by
Catoni 2012
for estimating univariate mean, and later extended by
Minsker 2018
for estimating matrices. It is based on an M-estimation framework where we estimate the common mean
\mathbf{M}
from some independent, but not necessarily identically distributed, samples
\mathbf{S}_{i},i=1,\ldots,n
by

\widehat{\mathbf{S}}=\argmin_{\mathbf{M}}\left[\tr\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Psi(\theta(\mathbf{S}_{j}-\mathbf{M}))\right],

and
\theta
is a tuning parameter to be specified later. Here, for a function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and symmetric matrix
\mathbf{M}
with spectral decomposition
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{U}^{\top}
,

f(\mathbf{M})=\mathbf{U}\diag(f(\lambda_{1}),\dots,f(\lambda_{d}))\mathbf{U}^{\top}.

In particular, we shall take a
\Psi
so that its first derivative
\psi=\Psi^{\prime}
is operator Lipschitz and obeys

-\log(1-x+x^{2}/2)\leq\psi(x)\leq\log(1+x+x^{2}/2).

See
Catoni 2012
and
Minsker 2018
for further discussions and various examples.

Recall that

{\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{X}){\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{X})^{\top}=\sum_{i_{-k}\in[d]^{p-1}}\mathbf{X}_{i_{-k}}\mathbf{X}_{i_{-k}}^{\top}

where
i_{-k}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k-1},i_{k+1},\ldots,i_{p})
and
\mathbf{X}_{i_{-k}}
is the
k
th mode fiber of
\mathscr{X}
with all indices except for the
k
th one fixed. Note that

\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{X}_{i_{-k}}\mathbf{X}_{i_{-k}}^{\top})=\lambda^{2}w_{i_{-k}}^{2}\mathbf{u}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{u}_{k}+I,

where

w_{i_{-k}}=\prod_{l\neq k}u_{li_{l}}.

It is tempting to apply the aforementioned strategy directly to
\{\mathbf{X}_{i_{-k}}\mathbf{X}_{i_{-k}}^{\top}:i_{-k}\in[d]^{p-1}\}
to estimate
\lambda^{2}\mathbf{u}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{u}_{k}
. There are, however, a couple of obstacles in doing so. Firstly, bounding the variation of
\widehat{\mathbf{S}}
incurs the second moment of
\mathbf{S}_{j}
s which can be translated into a requirement on the fourth moment of
\mathscr{X}
. This is exactly what we try to avoid. To this end, we shall instead consider estimating

\mathbf{V}_{k}=\lambda^{2}\left[\mathbf{u}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{u}_{k}-{\rm diag}(\mathbf{u}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{u}_{k})\right].

Note that

\left\|\mathbf{V}_{k}-\lambda^{2}\mathbf{u}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{u}_{k}\right\|=\lambda^{2}\|\mathbf{u}_{k}\|_{\ell_{\infty}}^{2}.

By Davis-Kahan Theorem, we know that

\sin\angle(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u}_{k})\leq 2\|\mathbf{u}_{k}\|_{\ell_{\infty}}^{2}.

Therefore, by assuming that
2\|\mathbf{u}_{k}\|_{\ell_{\infty}}^{2}<\eta
, a “good” estimate of the leading eigenvector of
\mathbf{V}
may yield an initial value satisfying the requirement of Proposition
4.2
.

Another difficulty is that

\mathbf{Y}_{i_{-k}}=\mathbf{X}_{i_{-k}}\mathbf{X}_{i_{-k}}^{\top}-{\rm diag}(\mathbf{X}_{i_{-k}}\mathbf{X}_{i_{-k}}^{\top})

have different means. To this end, we randomly partition
[d]^{p-1}
into
n
groups, denoted by
I_{1},\ldots,I_{n}
. This sampling is done through
d^{p-1}
samples of
\mathrm{Multinomial}\left(n;\tfrac{1}{n},\dots,\tfrac{1}{n}\right).
Let

\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{k}={1\over n\theta}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\psi(\theta\mathbf{S}_{j}),

where

\mathbf{S}_{j}=\sum_{i_{-k}\in I_{j}}\mathbf{Y}_{i_{-k}}.

(7)

\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{k}
can be viewed as a one-step gradient descent for computing
\widehat{\mathbf{S}}
with initial value
0
.

Denote by

\mu_{1}=\max_{i_{-k}\in[d]^{p-1}}|w_{i_{-k}}|,\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\mu_{2}=\max_{1\leq k\leq p}\|\mathbf{u}_{k}\|_{\ell_{\infty}}.

And write
\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{k}
the leading eigenvector of
\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{k}
. Then

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume that
\lambda>Cd^{p/4}(\log d)^{1/4}
and
\mu_{1}\leq C^{-1}(\log d)^{-1}
for a sufficiently large constant
C>0
. If

\theta=\sqrt{\dfrac{8\log(d)}{\lambda^{4}/n+d^{p}}},

then

\max_{1\leq k\leq p}\sin\angle(\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{k},\mathbf{u}_{k})\leq 2\mu_{2}^{2}+\sqrt{\dfrac{32\log d}{n}}+\dfrac{(\lambda\sqrt{d}+d^{p/2})\sqrt{8\log d}}{\lambda^{2}}

with probability at least
1-Cd^{-1}\log d-n\exp(-1/Cn\mu_{1}^{2}).

The algorithm above effectively does a truncation around
\mathbf{0}
. It is natural that this causes significant bias and leads to a larger deviation term. With more gradient iterations,
\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{(t)}_{k}
becomes an increasingly better approximation to
\mathbf{V}_{k}
and reduces the second term of the deviation exponentially fast. We omit details since we intend to use this only for initialization and the performance guarantee given by Theorem
4.1
is sufficient for our purpose.

The theoretical choice of the truncation parameter
\theta
as given above, requires some knowledge of
\lambda
. If we instead have some preliminary bounds on
\lambda
, we define
\theta_{j}
as follows by the so-called Lepski method. Let
{\cal L}=\{l\in\mathbb{N}:\lambda_{\min}\leq\lambda_{l}=2^{l}\lambda_{\min}\leq 2\lambda_{\max}\}.
For each
\lambda_{l}
the corresponding truncated estimators
\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{(l)}
are defined as above. Then,

\theta=\sqrt{\dfrac{8\log d}{\lambda_{l}^{4}/n+d^{p}}}

and

l^{*}=\min\left\{l\in{\cal L}:\forall k\in{\cal L},\,k>l,\,\|\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{(l)}-\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{(k)}\|\leq\frac{\lambda_{k}^{4}/n+d^{p}}{12n}\right\}.

Using results from
Minsker 2018
, it can be shown that this scheme provides estimates that differ from Theorem
4.1
only by a constant. Notice that in our case we can get a crude upper bound for
\lambda
using the Frobenius norm of one of the tensor pieces. Moreover, our simulation results show that a fixed upper bound for
\lambda
often suffices and we do not need to estimate it.

4.2
One Step Power Iteration with Sample Splitting

In light of Theorem
4.1
, if
\lambda\sim d^{p/4}(\log d)^{1/4}
, then we can ensure that
\sin\angle(\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{k},\mathbf{u}_{k})\leq\eta
for some constant
\eta<1
by we take
n=C\log d
. We shall now consider using them in the power iteration. As suggested by Proposition
3.2
, for the accuracy to improve from iteration to iteration, it is important that we have
\lambda\gtrsim\|\mathscr{E}\|
. In light of Theorem
2.2
, the requirement that
\lambda\sim d^{p/4}
cannot ensure that is the case when
\alpha<4
. It turns out that this requirement is a mere consequence of the complicated nonlinear relationship between the singular vectors and
\mathscr{E}
induced by the iterations. If the initial values
\mathbf{x}^{[0]}_{k}
s are independent of
\mathscr{X}
, then running the power iteration (
6
) once would result in a rate optimal estimate.

Proposition 4.2
.

Assume that
\mathbf{x}_{k}^{[0]}
s are independent of
\mathscr{X}
and satisfy

\max_{1\leq k\leq p}\sin\angle(\mathbf{x}_{k}^{[0]},\mathbf{u}_{k})\leq\eta

for some constant
\eta<1
. Then for any
0<\delta<1
,

\max_{1\leq k\leq p}\sin\angle(\mathbf{x}_{k}^{[1]},\mathbf{u}_{k})\leq\max\left\{{C\sqrt{d}/\delta^{1/\alpha}\over\lambda(1-\eta^{2})^{(p-1)/2}},1\right\}

with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Proposition
4.2
immediately suggests a simple strategy to estimate
\mathbf{u}_{k}
s when we observe, in addition to
\mathscr{X}
, another independent copy of it, denoted by
\tilde{\mathscr{X}}
: first apply robust tensor SVD to
\tilde{\mathscr{X}}
, and then update the estimated singular vectors using (
6
). As a direct consequence of Theorem
4.1
and Proposition
4.2
, the resulting estimate
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{k}
s satisfy:

\max_{1\leq k\leq p}\sin\angle(\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{k},\mathbf{u}_{k})\lesssim_{p}{\sqrt{d}\over\lambda}.

(8)

if
\lambda\geq Cd^{p/4}\log d
for a sufficiently large constant
C>0
.

Of course, we do not have another copy of
\mathscr{X}
. To overcome this obstacle, we randomly partition the tensor into two halves along its
p
-th mode. Denote the two halves of indices by
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
. We use the tensor
\mathscr{X}_{1},
with indices
[d]^{p-1}\times J_{1}
for nontrivial initialization, and
\mathscr{X}_{2}
with indices
[d]^{p-1}\times J_{2}
for iteration. It can be derived from the scaled Chernoff bound that

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i\in J_{1}}u_{pi}^{2}\geq 0.25\right)\leq\exp\left(-1/16\mu_{2}^{2}\right).

See, e.g., Theorems 1, 2 and the subsequent remarks of
Raghavan 1988
. Note that we can write

\mathscr{X}_{1}=\lambda\left\lVert\mathbf{u}_{p,J_{1}}\right\rVert\mathbf{u}_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbf{u}_{p-1}\otimes{\mathbf{u}_{p,J_{1}}\over\left\lVert\mathbf{u}_{p,J_{1}}\right\rVert}.

The last two equations imply that
\mathscr{X}_{1}
has a signal strength of at least

0.5\lambda>Cd^{p/4}(\log d)^{1/4}.

Thus we can use Theorem
4.1
, assuming all the incoherence conditions are satisfied, to get estimates
\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{k}
such that

\max_{1\leq k\leq p-1}\sin\angle\left(\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{k},\,\mathbf{u}_{k}\right)\leq\eta,

(9)

for some constant
\eta<1
. Following (
6
), we can use
\mathbf{x}^{[0]}_{t}=\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{k}
with the second tensor
\mathscr{X}_{2}
to yield an improved estimate of
\mathbf{u}_{k}
, denoted by
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}
. Notice that
\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{k}
are independent of
\mathscr{X}_{2}.
In light of Proposition
4.2
,
we get

Theorem 4.3
.

Assume that the entries of
\mathscr{E}
are independent and identically distributed with zero mean, unit variance and
\mathbb{E}|E|^{\alpha}<\infty
for some
\alpha\geq 2
. There exist constants
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3}>0
such that if
\lambda>C_{1}d^{p/4}(\log d)^{1/4}
and
\max_{1\leq k\leq p}\|\mathbf{u}_{k}\|_{\ell_{\infty}}\leq C_{2}(\log d)^{-1}
, then there is a polynomial time computable estimate
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}
(
k=1,\ldots,p
) obeying

\mathbb{P}\left\{\max_{1\leq k\leq p}\sin\angle(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k},\mathbf{u}_{k})\leq{C_{3}\sqrt{d}\over\lambda t}\right\}\geq 1-t^{\alpha}

for any
0<t<1
.

Note that the additional requirement of
\max_{1\leq k\leq p}\|\mathbf{u}_{k}\|_{\ell_{\infty}}\leq C_{2}(\log d)^{-1}
ensures that the singular vectors are not too concentrated on a few coordinates and therefore allows us to capture the signal even after the sample splitting. In the event that this is not the case, our task can be effectively reduced to a problem of lower order. To see this, assume, without loss of generality, that
u_{11}=\|\mathbf{u}_{1}\|_{\ell_{\infty}}\gtrsim(\log d)^{-1}
. Denote by
\mathscr{X}_{j}
the
j
th slice of
\mathscr{X}
along its first mode. It is clear that

\mathscr{X}_{1}=\tilde{\lambda}\mathbf{u}_{2}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{u}_{p}+\mathscr{E}_{1},

where

\tilde{\lambda}=\lambda u_{11}\gtrsim d^{p/4}{\rm polylog}(d),

by assumption. Note that the signal strengths
\lambda
and
\tilde{\lambda}
are of the same order up to the logarithmic factor. However,
\mathscr{X}
is a
p
th order tensor and
\mathscr{X}_{1}
is of order
(p-1)
. It is therefore conceivable that estimating the singular vectors of
\mathscr{X}_{1}
could be easier because of the relative higher signal-to-noise ratio.

Finally, notice that the robust estimation method of the present section does not depend on
\alpha
, provided
\alpha\geq 2
. This allows the user to apply this method without any prior knowledge about the error distribution. The numerical experiments of Section 5 also support this claim. When the signal strength condition is satisfied, the performance of the robust estimators does not depend on the number of moments of the errors.

6
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we study the problem of estimating the rank-one spikes in the presence of heavy tailed noises. Our contributions are three-fold. First we investigate the performance of estimates from tensor SVD, perhaps the most natural approach especially if we neglect the computational cost. Our results identify the signal strength requirement for the tensor SVD to yield rate-optimal estimates. (Nearly) matching lower bounds are also given to show that these requirements are optimal in the sense that the tensor SVD is necessarily inconsistent if the signal strength is below these requirement.

Our analysis of the tensor SVD exploits its close connection with the spectral norm of random tensors, and our second contribution is to establish upper bounds and (nearly) matching lower bounds for a tensor consisting of independent mean zero random variables. Our bounds pinpoint the connection between spectral norm of a random tensor and the moment condition for its entries.

Finally, we develop procedures for estimating the singular vectors under heavy tailed noises that are tractable in that they are polynomial time computable, practical in that they are easy to implement, and yields estimates that converge to the true parameter at the optimal rate. In particular, we show that similar to the case with Gaussian noise, a single power iteration with spectral initialization suffices if the entries of the noise have finite
4(p-1)
th moment. If the entries have finite fourth moment but infinite
4(p-1)
th moment, then we need to do
O(\log d)
number of power iterations. If the entries do not have finite fourth moment, we need a different strategy. This new procedure combines robust matrix estimation and sample splitting, and can be shown as both tractable and rate optimal.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

We begin with the upper bound.

Upper bound.
For any
t>d,
and
\mathbb{E}\left\lvert E\right\rvert^{\alpha}=\kappa<\infty
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\underset{i_{1},i_{3},\dots,i_{p}}{\max}\sum_{i_{2}}\left(E^{2}_{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}-1\right)>t\right)\leq d^{p-1}\cdot\dfrac{\mathbb{E}\left\lvert\sum_{i_{2}}(E^{2}_{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}-1)\right\rvert^{\alpha/2}}{t^{\alpha/2}}\leq\dfrac{C_{p}d^{p-1+\frac{\alpha}{4}}\kappa}{t^{\alpha/2}}

by Khintchine and Rosenthal inequalities respectively. This means

\mathbb{E}\underset{i_{1},i_{3},\dots,i_{p}}{\max}\displaystyle\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{d}E^{2}_{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}\leq d+C_{p}d^{\tfrac{2(p-1)}{\alpha}+\tfrac{1}{2}}.

By Theorem
7.1
with
q=2,

\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\mathscr{E}\right\rVert\leq C\sqrt{d\log d}+C_{p}d^{\tfrac{p-1}{\alpha}+\tfrac{1}{4}}(\log d)^{3/2}.

Notice that we can get a constant
C>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(\max\left\lvert E_{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}\right\rvert>Cd^{\tfrac{p-1}{\alpha}+\tfrac{1}{4}}\right)\leq d^{p}\mathbb{E}\left\lvert E\right\rvert^{\alpha}/C^{\alpha}d^{p-1+\tfrac{\alpha}{4}}=d^{1-\tfrac{\alpha}{4}}.

It is well known that the function

f:\mathbb{R}^{d^{p}}\to\mathbb{R}
given by
f(\mathrm{vec}(\mathscr{E}))=\|\mathscr{E}\|
is convex and
1
-Lipschitz. Now using Talagrand’s concentration inequality for convex Lipschitz functions
(
Ledoux and Talagrand 2013
, see, e.g., Equation 1.4 of)
we obtain

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lvert\|\mathscr{E}\|-\mathbb{E}\|\mathscr{E}\|\right\rvert>C_{p}d^{\tfrac{p-1}{\alpha}+\tfrac{1}{4}}(\log d)^{3/2}\right)\leq d^{1-\tfrac{\alpha}{4}}

and thus the upper bound now follows.

Now consider the lower bound.

Lower bound.
It is clear that

\left\lVert\mathscr{E}\right\rVert^{2}\geq\underset{i_{2},\dots,i_{p}}{\max}\displaystyle\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{d}E_{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}^{2}.

Thus, for any constant
C>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lVert\mathscr{E}\right\rVert^{2}>d+C^{2}d^{\tfrac{2(p-1)}{\alpha}+\tfrac{1}{2}}\,\,\mathrm{i.o.}\right)\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\underset{i_{2},\dots,i_{p}}{\max}\displaystyle\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{d}E_{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}^{2}-d>C^{4}d^{\tfrac{2(p-1)}{\alpha}+\tfrac{1}{2}}\,\,\mathrm{i.o.}\right)

Notice that
\displaystyle\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{d}(E^{2}_{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}-1)
is a sum of independent mean zero random variables. Since
\mathbb{E}\left\lvert E_{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}\right\rvert^{\alpha/2}=\infty,

Latała 1997
along with Khintchine inequalities imply that for any finite

X_{i_{2}\dots i_{p}}=\displaystyle\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{d}(E_{i_{1}i_{2}\dots i_{p}}^{2}-1)/\sqrt{d}

satisfy

\mathbb{E}|X_{i_{2}\dots i_{p}}|^{\alpha/2}\asymp\max\{1,\,d^{1-\alpha/4}(\mathbb{E}|E_{i_{1}i_{2}\dots i_{p}}^{2}-1|)^{\alpha/2}\}=\infty.

For
k=1,2,\dots,
let

B_{k}=\{i_{2},\dots,i_{p}:2^{k-1}<i_{2}\leq 2^{k},1\leq i_{3},\dots,i_{p}\leq 2^{k}\}.

By Borel Cantelli theorem, it is enough to show that

\sum_{k}\mathbb{P}\left(\text{there exists }i_{2},\dots,i_{p}\in B_{k}\text{ s.t. }\left\lvert X_{i_{2}\dots i_{p}}\right\rvert\geq C\cdot 2^{2k(p-1)/\alpha}\right)=\infty.

In other words, we need

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\left[1-\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lvert X_{i_{2}\dots i_{p}}\right\rvert<C\cdot 2^{2k(p-1)/\alpha}\right)^{2^{k(p-1)}/2}\right]=\infty.

Again since
\mathbb{E}\left\lvert X_{i_{2}\dots i_{p}}\right\rvert^{\alpha/2}=\infty,
we have
\mathbb{P}(|X_{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}|>t)\gtrsim t^{-\alpha/2}
for large enough

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2^{k(p-1)}\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lvert X_{i_{2}\dots i_{p}}\right\rvert\geq C\cdot 2^{2k(p-1)/\alpha}\right)=\infty.

We use the well known implication

\sum_{k}\left[1-(1-a_{k})^{b_{k}}\right]<\infty\implies\sum_{k}a_{k}b_{k}<\infty\quad\text{ for }a_{k}\in[0,1],\,\,b_{k}\geq 0.

(14)

Notice that (
14
) implies, for
d=2^{k}
and any constant
C>0,
tensors
\mathscr{E}
of dimension
d\times d\times\dots\times d
satisfies

\mathbb{P}\left((\left\lVert\mathscr{E}\right\rVert^{2}-d)/\sqrt{d}\geq C\cdot d^{2(p-1)/\alpha}\text{ for infinitely many }d\right)=1.

The proof is now completed.
∎

The proof of Theorem
2.2
follows a similar strategy.

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

Upper bound.
Recall that
\mathbb{E}\left\lvert E\right\rvert^{\alpha}=\kappa<\infty.

By Markov inequality,

\mathbb{P}\left(\underset{i_{1},i_{3},\dots,i_{p}}{\max}\displaystyle\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{d}E^{2}_{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}>t\right)\leq d^{p-1}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left\lvert\displaystyle\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{d}E^{2}_{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}\right\rvert^{\alpha/2}/t^{\alpha/2}.

By Khintchine’s inequality, for independent Rademacher random variables
R_{i_{2}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\lvert\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{d}E^{2}_{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}\right\rvert^{\tfrac{\alpha}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\cdot\mathbb{E}\left\lvert\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{d}R_{i_{2}}E_{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}\right\rvert^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\cdot\max\left\{\left(\mathbb{E}\left\lvert\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{d}R_{i_{2}}E_{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}\right\rvert^{2}\right)^{\tfrac{\alpha}{2}},d\mathbb{E}\left\lvert E\right\rvert^{\tfrac{\alpha}{2}}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Cd^{\tfrac{\alpha}{2}},

where the second step is by Rosenthal’s inequality. Consequently

\mathbb{P}\left(\underset{i_{1},i_{3},\dots,i_{p}}{\max}\displaystyle\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{d}E^{2}_{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}>t\right)\leq C\cdot d^{p-1+\tfrac{\alpha}{2}}/t^{\tfrac{\alpha}{2}},

and so

\mathbb{E}\underset{i_{1},i_{3},\dots,i_{p}}{\max}\displaystyle\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{d}E^{2}_{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}\leq C_{p}d^{\tfrac{2(p-1)}{\alpha}+1}.

Similar to before, by Theorem
7.1
with
q=2,

\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\mathscr{E}\right\rVert\leq C\sqrt{d\log d}+C_{p}d^{\tfrac{p-1}{\alpha}+\tfrac{1}{2}}(\log d)^{3/2}.

Moreover, we can get a constant
C>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(\max\left\lvert E_{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}\right\rvert>Cd^{\tfrac{p-1}{\alpha}+\tfrac{1}{2}}\right)\leq d^{p}\mathbb{E}\left\lvert E\right\rvert^{\alpha}/C^{\alpha}d^{p-1+\tfrac{\alpha}{2}}=d^{1-\tfrac{\alpha}{2}}.

Again, using Talagrand’s concentration inequality for convex Lipschitz functions we obtain

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lvert\|\mathscr{E}\|-\mathbb{E}\|\mathscr{E}\|\right\rvert>C_{p}d^{\tfrac{p-1}{\alpha}+\tfrac{1}{2}}(\log d)^{3/2}\right)\leq d^{1-\tfrac{\alpha}{2}},

and the upper bound now follows.

Lower bound.
We will show that for any constant
C>0,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lVert\mathscr{E}\right\rVert^{2}>Cd^{2p/\alpha}\text{ i.o.}\right)=1.

Clearly,

\left\lVert\mathscr{E}\right\rVert\geq\underset{i_{1},\dots,i_{p}}{\max}\left\lvert E_{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}\right\rvert.

For
k=1,2,\dots,
let

A_{k}=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{p}:2^{k-1}<i_{1}\leq 2^{k},1\leq i_{2},\dots,i_{p}\leq 2^{k}\}.

By Borel Cantelli theorem, it is enough to show that

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\mathbb{P}\left(\text{there exists }i_{1},\dots,i_{p}\in A_{k}\text{ s.t. }\left\lvert E_{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}\right\rvert\geq C\cdot 2^{kp/\alpha}\right)=\infty.

As before, we need

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\left[1-\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lvert E_{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}\right\rvert<C\cdot 2^{kp/\alpha}\right)^{2^{kp}/2}\right]=\infty.

Notice now that

\mathbb{E}\left\lvert E_{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}\right\rvert^{\alpha}\leq C^{\alpha}+\displaystyle\sum_{k}\mathbb{E}(\left\lvert E_{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}\right\rvert^{\alpha}\mathbbm{1}_{C\cdot 2^{kp/\alpha}\leq\left\lvert E_{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}\right\rvert\leq C\cdot 2^{(k+1)p/\alpha}}).

Thus,

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2^{kp}\mathbb{P}(C\cdot 2^{kp/\alpha}\leq\left\lvert E_{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}\right\rvert\leq C\cdot 2^{(k+1)p/\alpha})=\infty

since
\mathbb{E}\left\lvert E_{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}\right\rvert^{\alpha}=\infty.

The conclusion now follows from equation (
14
).
∎

7.3
Bounds for Tensor SVD

We now turn our attention to bounds for the tensor SVD and prove Theorems
2.3
and
2.4
. The cases when
\alpha>4
and
2<\alpha<4
can be treated in an identical fashion and we shall focus on the case when
\alpha>4
for brevity.

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

Note that

\displaystyle\widehat{\lambda}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\mathscr{X}\times_{1}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\rm SVD}\dots\times_{p}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{p}^{\rm SVD}

(15)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathscr{X}\times_{1}\mathbf{u}_{1}\dots\times_{p}\mathbf{u}_{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda+\mathscr{E}\times_{1}\mathbf{u}_{1}\dots\times\mathbf{u}_{p}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\lambda-\sqrt{d}

with probability at least
1-d^{-1}
.

Write

\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\mathrm{SVD}}_{j}=\sqrt{1-\rho_{j}^{2}}\mathbf{u}_{j}+\rho_{j}\mathbf{v}_{j}

where
\|\mathbf{v}_{j}\|=1
and
\mathbf{v}_{j}\perp\mathbf{u}_{j}
, for
1\leq j\leq p
. Let
\rho:=\max_{j}|\rho_{j}|
. Using the upper bounds from Theorem
2.1
for
k\geq 2
, we can derive that

\displaystyle\widehat{\lambda}=

\displaystyle\mathscr{X}\times_{1}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\mathrm{SVD}}_{1}\dots\times_{p}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\mathrm{SVD}}_{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda\prod_{j=1}^{p}\sqrt{1-\rho_{j}^{2}}+\sum_{A\subset[p]}\left(\prod_{j\in A}\sqrt{1-\rho_{j}^{2}}\right)\left(\prod_{j\notin A}\rho_{j}\right)\mathscr{E}\times_{j\in A}\mathbf{u}_{j}\times_{j\notin A}\mathbf{v}_{j}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lambda\left(\prod_{j=1}^{p}\sqrt{1-\rho_{j}^{2}}\right)^{1/p}+\sum_{A\subset[p]}\left(\prod_{j\in A}\sqrt{1-\rho_{j}^{2}}\right)\left(\prod_{j\notin A}\rho_{j}\right)\left\lVert\mathscr{E}\times_{j\in A}\mathbf{u}_{j}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(\lambda+\sqrt{d})(1-\sum_{j}\rho_{j}^{2}/2)+C_{p}\rho\sqrt{d}+C_{p}\sum_{k=2}^{p}\rho^{k}\left(d^{\tfrac{k-1}{\alpha}+\tfrac{1}{4}}+\sqrt{d}\right)(\log d)^{3/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(\lambda+\sqrt{d})(1-\rho^{2}/2)+C_{p}\rho\sqrt{d}+C_{p}\rho^{2}\left(d^{\tfrac{p-1}{\alpha}+\tfrac{1}{4}}+\sqrt{d}\right)(\log d)^{3/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lambda+C_{p}\sqrt{d}+\left[C_{p}\left(d^{\tfrac{p-1}{\alpha}+\tfrac{1}{4}}+\sqrt{d}\right)(\log d)^{3/2}-\lambda/2\right]\rho^{2}

(16)

with probability at least
1-d^{-1}-d^{1-\alpha/4}
. Note that

|\mathscr{E}\times_{1}\mathbf{u}_{1}\dots\times_{p}\mathbf{u}_{p}|\leq\sqrt{d}\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\|\mathscr{E}\times_{k\neq j}\mathbf{u}_{k}\|\leq C\sqrt{d}

with probability at least
1-d^{-1}
, using Chebychev inequalities. We also use AM-GM inequality for the first term on the fourth line.

We can get a sufficiently large constant
C_{p}>0
, such that if

\lambda>C_{p}\left(d^{\tfrac{p-1}{\alpha}+\tfrac{1}{4}}+\sqrt{d}\right)(\log d)^{3/2},

and
\rho^{2}>\sqrt{d}/\lambda
the last line of (
16
) is at most
\lambda-2\sqrt{d}
, thus contradicting (
15
). We thus have

\rho^{2}\leq\sqrt{d}/\lambda.

(17)

It is also clear from (
16
) that
\widehat{\lambda}\leq\lambda+C_{p}\sqrt{d}
, which combined with (
15
) yields

|\widehat{\lambda}-\lambda|\leq C_{p}\sqrt{d}.

We will derive an improved upper bound on
\rho
by using the first order condition on
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{j}^{\rm SVD}
. In particular,
(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\rm SVD}_{1},\dots,\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\rm SVD}_{p})
is a local minimum of the function

F(\gamma,\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{p})=\|\mathscr{X}-\gamma\mathbf{a}_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbf{a}_{p}\|_{\rm HS}^{2}

for
\gamma\in\mathbb{R},\,\mathbf{a}_{j}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. Setting the derivative of the Lagrangian to zero, we have

\mathscr{X}\times_{k\neq j}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\rm SVD}_{k}=\widehat{\lambda}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\rm SVD}_{j}\quad\text{for }1\leq j\leq p.

For
j=1
,

\displaystyle\|\lambda(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\rm SVD}_{1}-\mathbf{u}_{1})\|=

\displaystyle\|(\lambda-\widehat{\lambda})\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\rm SVD}_{1}+(\widehat{\lambda}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\rm SVD}_{1}-\lambda\mathbf{u}_{1})\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|\widehat{\lambda}-\lambda|+\|(\mathscr{T}+\mathscr{E})\times_{k\neq 1}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\rm SVD}_{k}-\lambda\mathbf{u}_{1}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{p}\sqrt{d}+\left\lVert\lambda\big(\prod_{k\neq 1}\sqrt{1-\rho_{k}^{2}}-1\big)\mathbf{u}_{1}\right\rVert+\|\mathscr{E}\times_{k\neq 1}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\rm SVD}_{k}\|.

(18)

Since

\rho^{2}=\max_{j}\rho_{j}^{2}\leq\sqrt{d}/\lambda

by (
17
), it is not hard to see that

\left\lvert\prod_{k\neq 1}\sqrt{1-\rho_{k}^{2}}-1\right\rvert\leq 1-(1-\rho^{2})^{(p-1)/2}\leq C_{p}\sqrt{d}/\lambda.

On the other hand, following (
16
), we have

\displaystyle\|\mathscr{E}\times_{k\neq 1}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\rm SVD}_{k}\|=

\displaystyle\left\lVert\sum_{A\subset([p]\setminus\{1\})}\left(\prod_{k\in A}\sqrt{1-\rho_{j}^{2}}\right)\left(\prod_{k\notin A\cup\{1\}}\rho_{j}\right)\mathscr{E}\times_{j\in A}\mathbf{u}_{j}\times_{k\notin A\cup\{1\}}\mathbf{v}_{j}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{A\subset([p]\setminus\{1\})}\left(\prod_{k\in A}\sqrt{1-\rho_{j}^{2}}\right)\left(\prod_{k\notin A\cup\{1\}}\rho_{j}\right)\left\lVert\mathscr{E}\times_{j\in A}\mathbf{u}_{j}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{p}\sqrt{d}+C_{p}\rho\sqrt{d}+C_{p}\sum_{k=2}^{p-1}\rho^{k}\left(d^{\tfrac{k-1}{\alpha}+\tfrac{1}{4}}+\sqrt{d}\right)(\log d)^{3/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{p}\sqrt{d}+C_{p}\rho^{2}\left(d^{\tfrac{p-1}{\alpha}+\tfrac{1}{4}}+\sqrt{d}\right)(\log d)^{3/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{p}\sqrt{d}+C_{p}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\lambda}\cdot\left(d^{\tfrac{p-1}{\alpha}+\tfrac{1}{4}}+\sqrt{d}\right)(\log d)^{3/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{p}\sqrt{d},

with probability at least
1-d^{1-\alpha/4}
, once again using the upper bounds from Theorem
2.1
. The last line uses the facts
\rho^{2}\leq\sqrt{d}/\lambda
and

\lambda>C\left(d^{\tfrac{p-1}{\alpha}+\tfrac{1}{4}}+\sqrt{d}\right)(\log d)^{3/2}

for a sufficiently large constant
C>0
.

Plugging the last two bounds into (
18
) above implies

\sin\angle(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\rm SVD}_{1},\,\mathbf{u}_{1})\leq\sqrt{2}\|\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{\rm SVD}_{1}-\mathbf{u}_{1}\|\leq\frac{C_{p}\sqrt{d}}{\lambda}.

The bounds for
j=2,\dots,p
follow by an analogous argument. ∎

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

Consider for some constant
0<C_{0}<1
, a set of vectors

{\cal S}(C_{0})=\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{p}:\mathbf{x}_{j}=\sqrt{1-\rho_{j}^{2}}\mathbf{u}_{j}+\rho_{j}\mathbf{v}_{j},\,\|\mathbf{v}_{j}\|=1,\mathbf{v}_{j}\perp\mathbf{u}_{j},|\rho_{j}|<C_{0}\}.

By assumption, there exists a
\beta
such that
\mathbb{E}|E_{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}|^{\beta}<\infty
and

\beta>\max\{(p-2)\alpha/(p-1),4\}

and all
i_{1}\dots i_{p}\in[d].

Following the steps of (
16
), for any
\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{p}\in{\cal S}(C_{0})
,

\displaystyle\,\mathscr{E}\times_{1}\mathbf{x}_{1}\dots\times_{p}\mathbf{x}_{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{A\subset[p]}\left(\prod_{j\in A}\sqrt{1-\rho_{j}^{2}}\right)\left(\prod_{j\notin A}\rho_{j}\right)\mathscr{E}\times_{j\in A}\mathbf{u}_{j}\times_{j\notin A}\mathbf{v}_{j}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{p}{{p}\choose{k}}\max_{|A|=p-k}\prod_{j\notin A}\rho_{j}\left\lVert\mathscr{E}\times_{j\in A}\mathbf{u}_{j}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathscr{E}\times_{1}\mathbf{u}_{1}\dots\mathbf{u}_{p}\right\rvert+C_{p}\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}{{p}\choose{k}}C_{0}^{k}(\sqrt{d}+d^{\tfrac{k-1}{\beta}+\tfrac{1}{4}})(\log d)^{3/2}+C_{0}^{p}\left\lVert\mathscr{E}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{p}\sqrt{d}+C_{p}d^{\tfrac{p-2}{\beta}+\tfrac{1}{4}}(\log d)^{3/2}+C_{0}^{p}\left\lVert\mathscr{E}\right\rVert

(19)

with probability at least
1-d^{1-\beta/4}
. Once again, in the third inequality above, we have used the upper bounds from Theorem
2.1
for
k\geq 2
. Notice that

|\mathscr{E}\times_{1}\mathbf{u}_{1}\dots\times_{p}\mathbf{u}_{p}|\leq\sqrt{d}\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\|\mathscr{E}\times_{k\neq j}\mathbf{u}_{k}\|\leq C\sqrt{d}

with probability at least
1-d^{-1}
, using Chebychev inequalities. On the other hand,
\mathbb{E}\left\lvert E\right\rvert^{\alpha}=\infty
for some
\alpha<4(p-1)
. Then by the lower bounds in Theorems
2.1
, for any constant
C>0,

\left\lVert\mathscr{E}\right\rVert>Cd^{\tfrac{p-1}{\alpha}+\tfrac{1}{4}}

almost surely. We then have

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{x}_{j}\in{\cal S}(C_{0})}\mathscr{X}\times_{1}\mathbf{x}_{1}\dots\times_{p}\mathbf{x}_{p}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{x}_{j}\in{\cal S}(C_{0})}\mathscr{T}\times_{1}\mathbf{x}_{1}\dots\times_{p}\mathbf{x}_{p}+\sup_{\mathbf{x}_{j}\in{\cal S}(C_{0})}\mathscr{E}\times_{1}\mathbf{x}_{1}\dots\times_{p}\mathbf{x}_{p}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lambda+C_{p}\sqrt{d}+C_{p}d^{\tfrac{p-2}{\beta}+\tfrac{1}{4}}(\log d)^{3/2}+C_{0}^{p}\left\lVert\mathscr{E}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lambda+C_{p}d^{\tfrac{p-1}{\alpha}+\tfrac{1}{4}}+C_{0}^{p}\|\mathscr{E}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{p}d^{\tfrac{p-1}{\alpha}+\tfrac{1}{4}}+C_{0}^{p}\|\mathscr{E}\|-2\lambda

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\mathscr{E}\|-2\lambda,

(20)

since
\beta>(p-2)\alpha/(p-1)
and
\lambda<Cd^{\tfrac{p-1}{\alpha}+\tfrac{1}{4}}
. Again,

\displaystyle\mathscr{X}\times_{1}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\rm SVD}\times_{2}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{2}^{\rm SVD}\times_{3}\dots\times_{p}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{p}^{\rm SVD}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{p}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathscr{X}\times_{1}\mathbf{x}_{1}\dots\times_{p}\mathbf{x}_{p}\geq\left\lVert\mathscr{E}\right\rVert-\lambda,

which when compared to (
20
) shows that the global maximizer
(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\rm SVD},\dots,\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{p}^{\rm SVD})\notin{\cal S}(C_{0})
. In particular,
\|\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{j}^{\rm SVD}-\mathbf{u}_{j}\|>C_{0}
with probability at least
1-d^{1-\beta/4}
, for any
C_{0}<1.

The same proof goes through for the case
\alpha>4(p-1)
provided there is a small enough constant
C
such that
\lambda<C\sqrt{d}
. Similarly, the case where
2<\alpha<4
can be proved through the upper and lower bounds from Theorem
2.2
.
∎

7.4
Bounds for Spectral Initialization and Power Iteration

We now consider polynomial time computable estimates when
\alpha\geq 4
by establishing bounds for spectral initialization and power iterations.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Notice that
{\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{E})
is a
d\times d^{p-1}
matrix of i.i.d. random variables with mean
0
and variance
1
. Also,

{\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{T}){\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{E})^{\top}=\lambda\mathbf{u}_{k}(\mathbf{u}_{1}\odot\dots\odot\mathbf{u}_{k-1}\odot\mathbf{u}_{k+1}\odot\dots\odot\mathbf{u}_{p})^{\top}{\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{E})^{\top}=\lambda\mathbf{u}_{k}(\mathbf{E}^{\prime})^{\top},

where
\mathbf{E}^{\prime}
is a
d
length vector with independent random variables
\mathbb{E}\mathbf{E}^{\prime}_{i}=0,

\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{E}^{\prime}_{i})^{2}=1
and
\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{E}^{\prime}_{i})^{4}=\kappa<\infty.
Then

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{u}_{k}(\mathbf{E}^{\prime})^{\top}\right\rVert>2C\sqrt{d}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left((\sum E_{i}^{\prime 2}-1)^{2}>4Cd^{2}\right)\leq Cd\mathbb{E}((E^{\prime}_{i})^{4})/d^{2}\leq d^{-1}.

By Bai-Yin’s law,
\lambda_{\max}({\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{E}))=d^{(p-1)/2}+\sqrt{d}+o(\sqrt{d})
, meaning

\left\lVert{\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{E}){\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{E})^{\top}-d^{p-1}I_{d}\right\rVert=\lambda_{\max}({\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{E}))^{2}-d^{p-1}\leq Cd^{p/2}

almost surely. See, e.g., Theorem 2 of
Bai and Yin 2008
and Theorem 5.31 of
Vershynin 2010
.

Now using Davis-Kahan theorem,

\displaystyle\sin\angle\left(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\sf Mat},\,\mathbf{u}_{k}\right)\leq

\displaystyle\dfrac{2\left\lVert{\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{E}){\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{E})^{\top}-d^{p-1}I_{d}\right\rVert+4\lambda\left\lVert{\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{T}){\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{E})^{\top}\right\rVert}{\lambda^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\cdot\dfrac{d^{p/2}+\lambda\sqrt{d}}{\lambda^{2}}.

with probability at least
1-d^{-1}
. The proof for other modes follows similarly.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

The proof is by induction on
t
. The basis step holds by some nontrivial initialization, for example through the matricization estimator of Proposition
3.1
. We now assume that the induction hypothesis holds for some
t>0
and prove the induction step for
t+1
.

As before, we write

\mathbf{x}^{[t]}_{j}=\sqrt{1-\rho_{j}^{2}}\mathbf{u}_{j}+\rho_{j}\mathbf{v}_{j}

where
\mathbf{v}_{j}
is a unit length vector perpendicular to
\mathbf{u}_{j}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathscr{X}\times_{j\neq k}\mathbf{x}^{[t]}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda\left(\prod_{j\neq k}\sqrt{1-\rho_{j}^{2}}\right)\mathbf{u}_{k}

(21)

\displaystyle+\sum_{A\subset([p]\setminus\{k\})}\left(\prod_{j\in A}\sqrt{1-\rho_{j}^{2}}\right)\left(\prod_{j\notin A\cup\{k\}}\rho_{j}\right)\mathscr{E}\times_{j\in A}\mathbf{u}_{j}\times_{j\notin A\cup\{k\}}\mathbf{v}_{j}.

Notice that the entries of
\mathbf{E}^{\prime}=\mathscr{E}\times_{j\neq k}\mathbf{u}_{j}
are i.i.d. copies of a random variable
E^{\prime\prime}
with
\mathbb{E}(E^{\prime\prime})=0
,
\Var(E^{\prime\prime})=1
and
\mathbb{E}\left\lvert E^{\prime\prime}\right\rvert^{4}=\kappa<\infty
. By Chebyshev’s inequality, for any
1\leq k\leq p
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\|\mathscr{E}\times_{j\neq k}\mathbf{u}_{j}\|>C\sqrt{d}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left((\sum E_{i}^{\prime 2}-1)^{2}>4Cd^{2}\right)\leq d\,\mathbb{E}((E^{\prime}_{i})^{4})/Cd^{2}\leq d^{-1}.

Notice also that

\displaystyle\sum_{A\subset([p]\setminus\{k\}),\,|A|\leq p-2}\left(\prod_{j\in A}\sqrt{1-\rho_{j}^{2}}\right)\left(\prod_{j\notin A\cup\{k\}}\rho_{j}\right)\mathscr{E}\times_{j\in A}\mathbf{u}_{j}\times_{j\notin A\cup\{k\}}\mathbf{v}_{j}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{l=2}^{p-1}{{p}\choose{l}}(\rho^{[t]})^{l}\|\mathscr{E}\|\leq C_{p}(\rho^{[t]})^{2}\|\mathscr{E}\|.

The last two inequalities together imply that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathscr{E}\times_{j\neq k}\mathbf{x}_{j}^{[t]}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\lVert\sum_{A\subset([p]\setminus\{k\})}\left(\prod_{j\in A}\sqrt{1-\rho_{j}^{2}}\right)\left(\prod_{j\notin A\cup\{k\}}\rho_{j}\right)\mathscr{E}\times_{j\in A}\mathbf{u}_{j}\times_{j\notin A\cup\{k\}}\mathbf{v}_{j}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\sqrt{d}+C_{p}(\rho^{[t]})^{2}\|\mathscr{E}\|.

(22)

By the nontrivial initialization and the induction hypothesis, we have a constant
\rho^{*}<1
such that
\rho^{[t]}\leq\rho_{*}<1
. We then have

\displaystyle\sin\angle(\mathbf{x}_{k}^{[t]},\,\mathbf{u}_{k})=

\displaystyle\underset{\|\mathbf{w}\|=1,\mathbf{w}\perp\mathbf{u}_{k}}{\sup}{{\mathscr{X}\times_{j\neq k}\mathbf{x}_{j}^{[t]}\times_{k}\mathbf{w}}\over{\|\mathscr{X}\times_{j\neq k}\mathbf{x}_{j}^{[t]}\|}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underset{\|\mathbf{w}\|=1,\mathbf{w}\perp\mathbf{u}_{k}}{\sup}{{\mathscr{E}\times_{j\neq k}\mathbf{x}_{j}^{[t]}\times_{k}\mathbf{w}}\over{\|\mathscr{X}\times_{j\neq k}\mathbf{x}_{j}^{[t]}\|}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\left\lVert\mathscr{E}\times_{j\neq k}\mathbf{x}_{j}^{[t]}\right\rVert\over{\lambda\left(\prod_{j\neq k}\sqrt{1-\rho_{j}^{2}}\right)-\left\lVert\mathscr{E}\times_{j\neq k}\mathbf{x}_{j}^{[t]}\right\rVert}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\dfrac{C\sqrt{d}+C_{p}(\rho^{[t]})^{2}\|\mathscr{E}\|}{\lambda(1-\rho_{*}^{2}))^{(p-1)/2}-C\sqrt{d}-C_{p}(\rho^{[t]})^{2}\|\mathscr{E}\|}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\dfrac{\sqrt{d}}{\lambda}+C_{p}(\rho^{[t]})^{2}\dfrac{\|\mathscr{E}\|}{\lambda}

with probability at least
1-d^{1-\alpha/4}
.

We use (
21
) and (
22
) for the first and second inequalities respectively. The last line follows if
\lambda>C\|\mathscr{E}\|
for a sufficiently large constant
C>0
. Since we have
\lambda>d^{p/4}
and
\alpha>4
, this condition is satisfied with probability at least
1-d^{1-\alpha/4}
, using the upper bounds from Theorem
2.1
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Let us fix
k=1
as the other modes follow by symmetry.

We will denote the partition of
[d]^{p-1}
into
n

I:=(I_{1},\dots,I_{n}).
Let us also define

I

Minsker 2018
with

(23)

We need to calculate

We have the following lemma, the proof of which can be found in section

.

Lemma 1
.

\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{S}_{j}|I)=\left(\displaystyle\sum_{i_{-1}\in I_{j}}\mathbf{w}_{i_{-1}}^{2}\right)\mathbf{V}_{1}

(24)

To complete the proof, we now use the multinomial sample splitting scheme to get high probability bounds on the above quantities. We write

By our sampling scheme,

\mathbbm{1}(i_{-1}\in I_{j})\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}({1\over{n}})
so that
\mathbb{E}G_{j}=\tfrac{1}{n}.
Then, the scaled Chernoff bound
(
Raghavan 1988
, see, e.g., Theorems 1 and 2 and the subsequent remark of)

yields,

(25)

By the sample partition scheme

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}|I_{j}|=d^{p-1}\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i_{-1}\in I_{j}}\mathbf{w}^{2}_{i_{-1}}=\left\lVert\mathbf{w}\right\rVert^{2}=1.

\sigma^{2}\leq\dfrac{2\lambda^{4}}{n^{2}}+\dfrac{\lambda^{2}d}{n}+\dfrac{d^{p}}{n}

(26)

with probability at least

Notice also that the signal matrix is

Now applying (

) with

) and using Davis-Kahan theorem, we have

\displaystyle\sin\angle(\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{k},\,\mathbf{u}_{k})\leq

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\leq

Proof of Proposition

.

Since
\mathbf{x}_{k}^{[0]}

\mathbb{E}E_{i}^{2}=1
. Moreover,

where we use Rosenthal inequalities in the last step.
Therefore

\displaystyle\sin\angle\left(\mathbf{x}_{k}^{[1]},\mathbf{u}_{k}\right)=

\displaystyle\underset{\mathbf{v}:\|\mathbf{v}\|=1,\,\mathbf{v}\perp\mathbf{u}_{k}}{\sup}|\langle\mathbf{x}_{k}^{[1]},\mathbf{v}\rangle|\leq\underset{\mathbf{v}:\|\mathbf{v}\|=1,\,\mathbf{v}\perp\mathbf{u}_{k}}{\sup}\dfrac{\mathscr{E}\times_{j\neq k}\mathbf{x}_{j}^{[0]}\times_{k}\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathscr{X}\times_{j\neq k}\mathbf{x}_{j}^{[0]}\|}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\dfrac{\left\lVert\mathscr{E}\times_{j\neq k}\mathbf{x}_{j}^{[0]}\right\rVert}{\lambda\displaystyle\prod_{j\neq k}\left\lvert\langle\mathbf{x}_{j}^{[0]},\mathbf{u}_{j}\rangle\right\rvert-\left\lVert\mathscr{E}\times_{j\neq k}\mathbf{x}_{j}^{[0]}\times_{k}\mathbf{u}_{k}\right\rVert}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\dfrac{C\sqrt{d}/\delta^{1/\alpha}}{\lambda(1-\eta^{2})^{(p-1)/2}-\sqrt{d}/\delta^{1/\alpha}}\leq\dfrac{C\sqrt{d}/\delta^{1/\alpha}}{\lambda(1-\eta^{2})^{(p-1)/2}}.

with probability at least
1-\delta,
provided
\lambda(1-\eta^{2})^{(p-1)/2}\geq 2\sqrt{d}/\delta^{1/\alpha}
.
∎
