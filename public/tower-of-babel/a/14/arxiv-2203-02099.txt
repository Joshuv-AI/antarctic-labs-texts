Abstract
. Many entanglement measures are first defined for pure states of a bipartite Hilbert space, and then extended to mixed states via the convex roof extension. In this article we alter the convex roof extension of an entanglement measure, to produce a sequence of extensions that we call f f - d d extensions, for d ∈ ℕ d\in\mathbb{N} , where f : [ 0 , 1 ] → [ 0 , ∞ ) f:[0,1]\to[0,\infty) is a fixed continuous function which vanishes only at zero. We prove that for any such function f f , and any continuous, faithful, non-negative function, (such as an entanglement measure), μ \mu on the set of pure states of a finite dimensional bipartite Hilbert space, the collection of f f - d d extensions of μ \mu detects entanglement, i.e. a mixed state ρ \rho on a finite dimensional bipartite Hilbert space is separable, if and only if there exists d ∈ ℕ d\in\mathbb{N} such that the f f - d d extension of μ \mu applied to ρ \rho is equal to zero. We introduce a quantum variational algorithm which aims to approximate the f f - d d extensions of entanglement measures defined on pure states. However, the algorithm does have its drawbacks. We show that this algorithm exhibits barren plateaus when used to approximate the family of f f - d d extensions of the Tsallis entanglement entropy for a certain function f f and unitary ansatz U ⁡ ( θ ) U(\theta) of sufficient depth. In practice, if additional information about the state is known, then one needs to avoid using the suggested ansatz for long depth of circuits.

1.
Introduction

The detection and quantification of quantum entanglement is a fundamental problem in quantum information theory. A common method for such a quantification is via the use of entanglement measures and entanglement monotones
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
. However, entanglement measures are often difficult to compute for arbitrary density operators, as many of these measures are defined via the convex roof construction. There are some classical numerical algorithms
[
5
]
for computing the convex roof of entanglement measures, and even some quantum variational algorithms
[
6
]
for computing the logarithmic negativity of arbitrary densities. In this article
we define a family of extensions of entanglement measures defined on pure states, and we introduce a quantum variational algorithm
which aims to approximate these extensions. We prove that the family of certain extensions of the Tsallis entanglement entropy
exhibits barrens plateaus.

First, let us review the definitions of entanglement measures and the convex roof construction in general.
All Hilbert spaces that we consider throughout this article are finite dimensional. Also, if
\mathcal{H}
is a Hilbert space
then
\text{D}\,(\mathcal{H})
will denote the set of all density operators on
\mathcal{H}
, (i.e. positive semidefinite operators, of trace equal to
1
).

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a finite dimensional bipartite Hilbert space, i.e.
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{B}
for some finite dimensional Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
. Then a function
\mu:\text{D}\,(\mathcal{H})\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is called an entanglement measure if the following three properties hold for all
\rho\in\text{D}\,(\mathcal{H})
:

(1)

\mu(\rho)=0
if and only if
\rho
is separable, (i.e.
\mu
is
faithful
).

(2)

If
\Lambda
is a channel of Local Operations and Classical Communications,
(LOCC channel in short),
\rho\in\text{D}\,(\mathcal{H})
, and
\Lambda(\rho)=\sum_{i}p_{i}\rho_{i}
is a channel for some convex coefficients
(p_{i})_{i}
and some states
(\rho_{i})_{i})
, then

\sum_{i}p_{i}\mu(\rho_{i})\leq\mu(\rho)
. While the structure of LOCC channels is complicated in general, a thorough overview
of LOCC channels can be found in
[
7
]
. This property is referred as “LOCC monotonicity” or
“decrease on average under an LOCC channel”).

(3)

\mu((U_{1}\otimes U_{2})\rho(U_{1}^{*}\otimes U_{2}^{*}))=\mu(\rho)
where
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
are unitaries on

\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
respectively, (i.e.
\mu
is invariant under local unitaries).

If
\mu
is defined only on the set of pure states of
\mathcal{H}
and satisfies the above properties
(1)
-
(3)
for all pure states

\rho
of
\mathcal{H}
, then we say that
\mu
is an entanglement measure on the set of pure states of
\mathcal{H}
. If
\mu
is defined only
on the set of pure states of
\mathcal{H}
and satisfies the above property
(1)
for all pure states
\rho
of
\mathcal{H}
, then we say that

\mu
is a faithful non-negative function on the set of pure states.

If
\mathcal{H}
is a bipartite Hilbert space and
\mu
is an entanglement measure on the pure states of
\mathcal{H}
, then
one extends the domain of
\mu
to the set
\text{D}\,(\mathcal{H})
of density operators on
\mathcal{H}
,
using the
convex roof extension
which is defined by

(1)

\mu(\rho)=\inf\left\{\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}p_{i}\mu(\psi_{i}):\{\{p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty},\{\psi_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}\}\in\mathcal{C}\mathcal{D}(\rho)\right\}

where
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{D}(\rho)
is the set of all convex decompositions of
\rho
with respect to pure states,
often called
pure state ensembles
. More precisely,
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{D}(\rho)
is defined by

(2)

\mathcal{C}\mathcal{D}(\rho)=\left\{\{\{p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty},\{\ket{\psi_i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}\}:p_{i}\geq 0,\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}p_{i}=1,\ket{\psi_i}\in\mathcal{H},\|\ket{\psi_i}\|=1,\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}p_{i}\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i}=\rho\right\}

It can be shown that the function
\mu
given by Equation (
1
) is the largest convex function defined on the
set of all states of
\mathcal{H}
, which is less than
or equal to the function
\mu
on the set of pure states of
\mathcal{H}
.
While at first glance it’s not clear that the infimum in Equation (
1
) is attained, it has indeed been proven

[
8
,
9
]
that the infimum is attained as long as the measure
\mu
is norm continuous on the set of unit vectors of
\mathcal{H}
,
(i.e. on the set of pure states of
\mathcal{H}
).

Terminology 1.2
.

The elements of
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{D}(\rho)

at which the infimum is achieved are called optimal pure state ensembles or simply OPSEs.

Probably the most prevalent example of an entanglement measure formed in this way is the
entanglement of formation
, which is first defined on pure states by

(3)

\text{S}(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi})=-\text{Tr}\,\big(\text{Tr}\,_{\mathcal{A}}(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi})\log(\tTr_\cA\ketbra\psi)\big)=-\text{Tr}\,\big(\text{Tr}\,_{\mathcal{B}}(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi})\log(\tTr_\cB\ketbra\psi)\big)

This definition is then extended to the set of all density operators by the convex roof extension method described in
Equation (
1
). It has been proven that the entanglement entropy is norm continuous on
\mathcal{H}

[
10
]
, and so the infimum, (OPSE), is always achieved in this case.

Another example of entanglement measurement formed in the same way is the Tsallis entanglement entropy
T_{2}
, which is defined by

(4)

T_{2}(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi})=1-\text{Tr}\,((\text{Tr}\,_{\mathcal{A}}(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}))^{2})

for all pure states
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{B}
. It is then extended to general mixed states
\rho
of
\mathcal{H}
via the convex roof construction given in Equation (
1
), namely

(5)

T_{2}(\rho)=\inf\left\{\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}p_{i}T_{2}(\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i}):\{\{p_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty},\{\psi_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}\}\in\mathcal{C}\mathcal{D}(\rho)\right\}

The natural question that arises is how to find the optimal pure state ensembles that yield the infimum of the
extension of an entanglement measure which is defined on the pure state of a bipartite Hilbert space and is extended to
the mixed states using the convex roof extension. This is an important question because
once the OPSEs are known, then one can compute precisely the entanglement measure.

In this article we give a variational algorithm which aims at approximating the OPSE. It is well known that a main difficulty
in the convergence of variational optimization methods is the existence of
Barren Plateaus

[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
.
Unfortunately, even for
“simple” entanglement measures, such as the Tsallis entanglement measure, to determine whether or not a variational algorithm
has barren plateaus, is not an easy task.
In this article we study barren plateaus of a
variant of the convex roof extension method of the Tsallis entanglement
entropy,
instead of
studying barren plateaus of the Tsallis entanglement entropy itself, since the latter is significantly more technical.
Nevertheless in Section
4
we indicate how one can use the ideas presented here in order to examine barren plateaus of the Tsallis entanglement entropy. Additionally, we believe that our method adapted for the Tsallis entanglement entropy will only predict barren plateaus under
stronger assumptions on the depth of the ansatz than the depth assumptions used in this article. In practice, one will have to avoid using the ansatz
that we use this article for circuits of large depth. This can be possible
if additional information is known about the state whose entanglement is studied.
The variant of the convex roof extension method that we use here
does not yield an entanglement measure, but it is still allows us to detect
entanglement. It depends on a fixed function
f:[0,1]\to[0,\infty)
which vanishes only at zero,
and it yields a family of extensions indexed by the integers
d\in\mathbb{N}
. The domains of these extensions increase with
d
,
and they become equal to the set of density operators on the Hilbert space when
d
is large enough, (more precisely, when
d

strictly exceeds the dimension of the manifold of the density operators on the given bipartite Hilbert space).
These extensions decrease with respect to
d\in\mathbb{N}
at any fixed density operator, and their infimum for all
d
is equal to zero.
More precisely, we have the following definition.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a bipartite Hilbert space,
\mu
be an entanglement measure on the set of pure states of
\mathcal{H}
, and
f:[0,1]\to[0,\infty)

be a function that vanishes only at
0
.
For every
d\in\mathbb{N}
define the set
\text{D}\,(\mathcal{H})_{d}
of density operators of
\mathcal{H}
that can be written as a convex combination of at most
d

many pure states, i.e.

(6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\text{D}\,(\mathcal{H})_{d}=\bigg\{\rho\in\text{D}\,(\mathcal{H})&:\text{ there exists a family of pure states }(\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i})_{i=1}^{d}\\
&\text{ and }(p_{i})_{i=1}^{d}\subseteq[0,1]\text{ with }\sum_{i=1}^{d}p_{i}=1\text{ and }\rho=\sum_{i=1}^{d}p_{i}\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i}\bigg\}.\end{split}

Note that

(i)

\text{D}\,(\mathcal{H})_{1}
is equal to the set of pure states of
\mathcal{H}
, (in this case,
p_{1}=1
).

(ii)

\text{D}\,(\mathcal{H})_{d}\subseteq\text{D}\,(\mathcal{H})_{d+1}
for every
d\in\mathbb{N}
, (since the
p_{i}
’s are allowed to be equal to zero).

(iii)

There exists
d\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\text{D}\,(\mathcal{H})_{d}=\text{D}\,(\mathcal{H})
, (indeed, by Caratheodory’s theorem in convex analysis, this happens when
d\geq\text{dim}\,(\text{D}\,(\mathcal{H}))+1
).

Define a function
\mu_{f,d}:\text{D}\,(\mathcal{H})_{d}\to[0,\infty)
by

\mu_{f,d}(\rho)=\inf\bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}f(p_{i})\mu(\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i}):\rho=\sum_{i=1}^{d}p_{i}\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i}\in\text{D}\,(\mathcal{H})_{d}\text{ as in Equation~\eqref{DHt}}\bigg\}.

We call the function
\mu_{f,d}
the “
f
-
d
extension of
\mu
”. We call the sequence
(\mu_{f,d})_{d\in\mathbb{N}}

the “sequence of
f
-extensions of
\mu
”.

Remark 1.4
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a finite dimensional bipartite Hilbert space,
\mu
be a continuous, non-negative function on the set of pure states of
\mathcal{H}
,
and

f:[0,1]\to[0,\infty)
be a continuous function which vanishes only at
0
. Then, for every
d\in\mathbb{N}
the infimum in the
definition of
\mu_{f,d}
is attained, (i.e. it is a minimum).

Indeed, since
\mathcal{H}
is finite dimensional, the set of pure states on
\mathcal{H}
is a compact set. Let
\mathcal{P}
denote the set
of pure states on
\mathcal{H}
. Also, the set

\mathcal{C}=\bigg\{(p_{i})_{i=1}^{d}:p_{i}\geq 0\text{ for all }i=1,\ldots,d\text{ and }\sum_{i=1}^{d}p_{i}=1\bigg\}

is compact as well. Now the function defined on
\mathcal{P}^{d}\times\mathcal{C}
by

\mathcal{P}^{d}\times\mathcal{C}\ni(\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i})_{i=1}^{d}\times(p_{i})_{i=1}^{d}\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{d}f(p_{i})\mu(\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i}),

is a continuous function, since both
f
and
\mu
are assumed to be continuous. Hence it achieves its minimum on its compact domain,
which finishes the proof of Remark
1.4
.

Remark
1.4
justifies the next terminology.

Terminology 1.5
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a finite dimensional bipartite Hilbert space,
\mu
be a continuous non-negative function on the set of pure states of
\mathcal{H}
,
and
f:[0,1]\to[0,\infty)
be a continuous function which vanishes only at
0
. Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\rho\in\text{D}\,(\mathcal{H})_{d}
.
The tuple

((p_{i})_{i=1}^{d},(\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i})_{i=1}^{d})
with
p_{i}\geq 0
for all
i=1,\ldots,d
,
\sum_{i=1}^{d}p_{i}=1
,

\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i}
being pure states on
\mathcal{H}
, and
\mu_{f,d}(\rho)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}f(p_{i})\mu(\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i})

is called “Optimal Pure State Ensemble (OPSE) for

,
f
and

”.

In this article we study the sequence of
f
-extensions of Tsallis
entanglement entropy

, where

is defined by

We will denote by
(T_{f,d})_{d\in\mathbb{N}}
the sequence of

defined on the set of

(7)

T_{f,d}(\rho)=\inf\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}q_{i}^{2}T_{2}(\phi_{i}):\rho=\sum_{i=1}^{d}p_{i}\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i}\in\text{D}\,(\mathcal{H})_{d}\text{ as in \eqref{DHt}}\right\}.

Remark 1.6
.

The reason that we bound the length of the convex combinations in Definition
1.3
by a finite number
d
,
is because otherwise the infimum in Equation (
7
) would be equal to zero for every
\rho
.

Indeed, in order to verify the statement of Remark
1.6
, notice that if

\rho=\sum_{i=1}^{d}p_{i}\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i}\in\text{D}\,(\mathcal{H})_{d}
for some

(p_{i})_{i=1}^{d}\subset[0,1]
with
\sum_{i=1}^{d}p_{i}=1
and a sequence of pure states
(\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i})_{i=1}^{d}
,
then for every
n\in\mathbb{N}

we can write
\rho=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{p_{i}}{n}\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i}
(i.e. we divide each
p_{i}\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i}
by
n
and we repeat

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\frac{p_{i}}{n}\right)^{2}\mu(\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i})=n\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{p_{i}^{2}}{n^{2}}\mu(\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{d}p_{i}^{2}\mu(\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i}),

which tends to zero as
n
tends to infinity.

Given an entanglement measure
\mu
on the set of pure states of a finite dimensional bipartite Hilbert space,
and a continuous function
f:[0,1]\to[0,\infty)
which vanishes only at
0
, the
sequence of the
f
-extensions of
\mu
can be used in order to detect entanglement in the following sense:

Definition 1.7
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a bipartite Hilbert space, and for every
d\in\mathbb{N}
let
\text{D}\,(\mathcal{H})_{d}
be a subset of the set
\text{D}\,(\mathcal{H})
of density
matrices of
\mathcal{H}
, satisfying properties
(i)
,
(ii)
and
(iii)
of Definition
1.3
.
For every
d\in\mathbb{N}
let a function
\mu_{d}:\text{D}\,(\mathcal{H})_{d}\to[0,\infty)
. We say that the family

(\mu_{d})_{d\in\mathbb{N}}
detects entanglement, if for every density operator
\rho
on
\mathcal{H}
,

\rho
is separable if and only if there exists
d\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\rho\in\text{D}\,(\mathcal{H})_{d}
and
\mu_{d}(\rho)=0
.

Proposition 1.8
.

Let
\mu
be any continuous, faithful, non-negative function on the set of pure states of a finite dimensional bipartite Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{B}
. Let
f:[0,1]\to[0,\infty)
be a continuous function that vanishes only at zero.
Then, the
f
-
d
extensions
(\mu_{f,d})_{d\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\mu
to the density operators of
\mathcal{H}
, detect entanglement, as in the previous definition.

Proof.

(\Leftarrow)
Let
\rho
be a mixed state on
\mathcal{H}
such that
\mu_{f,d}(\rho)=0
for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
. Then by Remark
1.4
, there exists some pure state ensemble
\big\{(p_{i})_{i=1}^{d},(\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i})_{i=1}^{d}\big\}
with
\sum_{i=1}^{d}p_{i}\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i}=\rho
and

(8)

\mu_{f,d}(\rho)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}f(p_{i})\mu(\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i})=0.

If we assume without loss of generality that each
p_{i}>0
, then it must follow that

\mu(\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i})=0
for each
i\in\{1,\dots,d\}
. Since
\mu
is a faithful, non-negative function on the set of pure states,
it must be true that
\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i}
is factorable for each
i\in\{1,\dots,d\}
. Thus
\rho
can be written as a convex combination of factorable pure states, and is therefore separable.

(\Rightarrow)
Conversely, suppose that
\mu
is separable. Then there exists some ensemble
\big\{(p_{i})_{i=1}^{d}
,
(\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i})_{i=1}^{d}\big\}
of
\rho
such that each
\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i}
is factorable.
Therefore
\mu(\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i})=0
for each
i\in\{1,\dots,d\}
and so

(9)

\mu_{f,d}(\rho)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}f(p_{i})\mu(\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i})=0.

∎

2.
The Algorithm

It is difficult to compute optimal pure state ensebles in Equation (
1
) or in Terminology
1.5
, because the proof
for their existence, (given in Remark
1.4
), is not constructive. However, methods for approximating them on a quantum computer are heavily implied by the following theorem
[
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
.

Theorem 2.1

(Purification Theorem)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a finite dimensional Hilbert space and suppose that
\rho\in\text{D}\,(\mathcal{H})
can be expressed as both of the following pure state ensembles:

\sum_{i=1}^{d}p_{i}\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i}=\rho=\sum_{i=1}^{d}q_{i}\outerproduct{\varphi_i}{\varphi_i}

and define

(10)

\ket{\psi}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sqrt{p_{i}}\ket{\psi_i}\otimes\ket{i}

where
\{\ket{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
is an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{W}:=\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Then there exists a unitary
U\in\mathbb{U}(d)
such that
(I_{\mathcal{H}}\otimes U)\ket{\psi}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sqrt{q_{i}}\ket{\varphi_i}\otimes\ket{i}
.

This theorem tells us that any two pure state ensembles of the same length are related by a unitary map.
So if we already know one pure state ensemble of a given density, we can find all others simply by applying unitaries
on the ancilla space of a purification
\ket{\psi}
of
\rho
. Hence we can minimize the quantity

\sum_{i=1}^{d}q_{i}\mu(\varphi_{i})
over the unitary group, where the
q_{i}
and
\varphi_{i}
are as in the previous theorem, and
\mu
is an arbitrary entanglement measure. Moreover, the theorem hints at using the following quantum circuit to achieve our goal.

Figure 1
.
The quantum circuit implied by the purification theorem.

Here
h=\text{dim}\,(\mathcal{H})
and
\rho\in D(\mathcal{H})
, while
d=\text{dim}\,(\mathcal{W})
where
\mathcal{W}
is the ancilla space. In particular,
d
is also the length of the pure state decompositions output from the circuit. Usually
d
is taken to be greater than
h
.

Examining the circuit, notice that after preparing the system in the state
\ket{\psi}
and applying the unitary
U
, the system
is put into the superposition
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sqrt{q_{i}}\ket{\varphi_i}\otimes\ket{i}
. When measuring the system, if the state

\ket{j}
is observed on the ancilla space, then it must be the case that the top qubits are in the state
\ket{\varphi_j}

with certainty. Running this circuit a number of times allows the observer to experimentally reconstruct both
\ket{\varphi_j}
and
q_{j}
, and therefore compute the quantity
\sum_{i=1}^{d}q_{i}\mu(\varphi_{i})
. In order to find the optimal pure state ensemble we need
expressions for
\ket{\varphi_i}
and
q_{i}
in terms of
U
.
By examining the circuit step by step, we see that the circuit first prepares the purification
\ket{\psi}
. The unitary
U
is then
applied to the ancilla space resulting in the state
(I_{\mathcal{H}}\otimes U)\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}(I_{\mathcal{H}}\otimes U^{*})
. Lastly, measuring
the ancilla yields the states

(11)

\frac{1}{q_{i}}\Big(I_{\mathcal{H}}\otimes\outerproduct{i}{i}U\Big)\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}\Big(I_{\mathcal{H}}\otimes U^{*}\outerproduct{i}{i}\Big)

where

(12)

\begin{split}q_{i}&=\text{Tr}\,\Bigg(\Big(I_{\mathcal{H}}\otimes\outerproduct{i}{i}U\Big)\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}\Big(I_{\mathcal{H}}\otimes U^{*}\outerproduct{i}{i}\Big)\Bigg)\\
&=\bra{\psi}\Big(I_{\mathcal{H}}\otimes U^{*}\outerproduct{i}{i}U\Big)\ket{\psi}.\\
\end{split}

And to access the state on the space
\mathcal{H}
, we partial trace the ancilla and use the partial cyclicity of the partial trace to arrive at the following expression for the states
\outerproduct{\varphi_i}{\varphi_i}

(13)

\outerproduct{\varphi_i}{\varphi_i}=\frac{1}{q_{i}}\text{Tr}\,_{\mathcal{W}}\Bigg(\Big(I_{\mathcal{H}}\otimes U^{*}\outerproduct{i}{i}U\Big)\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}\Bigg)

for each
i
in
\{1,\dots,d\}
.

Convention 2.2
.

Since all of the sums that follow range from 1 to
d
, we will often write
\sum_{index}
to mean
\sum_{index=1}^{d}
.

The natural question that arises is how to search the unitary group for the unitary that minimizes the quantity
\sum_{i=1}^{d}q_{i}\mu(\varphi_{i})
where the
q_{i}
and
\ket{\varphi_i}
as in Equations (
12
) and (
13
) respectively, and
\mu
is an entanglement measure or related function. There are several ways to accomplish this task, the most common being the utilization of a parametrization of the unitary group
[
20
]
and applying geometric optimization techniques as in
[
5
]
, or the use of random parametrized quantum circuits
[
21
,
22
,
23
,
6
]
. We choose the latter, as this choice will greatly simplify calculations in the proof of our main result. Specifically, we will use parametrized unitaries of the following form:

(14)

U(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{L})=\prod_{l=1}^{L}exp\big(i\theta_{l}V_{l}\big)E_{l},

where
V_{l}
is a Hermitian operator,
\theta_{l}\in[0,2\pi)
for each
l\in\{1,\dots,L\}
, and
E_{l}
is an entangling gate
independent of the parameters
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{L}
. Common choices for
E_{l}
are a
CX
or
CZ
ladder. In applications,
the operators
V_{l}
are often taken to be a randomly chosen string of Pauli operators, which is the convention that we use. Indeed, it is well known that the gates
R_{x}(\theta)=exp(-i\theta X/2)
,
R_{y}(\theta)=exp(-i\theta Y/2)
,
R_{z}(\theta)=exp(-i\theta Z/2)
, the phase gate
Ph(\theta)=exp(i\theta)
and
CNOT
form a universal gate set, (see
[
24
, Section 2.6]
).
Since we have introduced the parameters
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{L}
into our unitaries, the ansatz we wish to train can be written
as
\sum_{i=1}^{d}q_{i}(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{L})\mu\Big(\varphi_{i}(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{L})\Big)
, thus allowing us to use common
optimization techniques to approximate the minimum. Thus a quantum algorithm to approximate optimal pure state ensembles can be
summarized as below:

Input:
An ensemble
\{(p_{i})_{i=1}^{d},(\psi_{i})_{i=1}^{d}\}
of a density matrix
\rho\in\text{D}\,(\mathbb{C}^{h})
,
and an optimization routine, (as in
[
25
]
).

Parameters:
Initial parameters
\theta_{1}^{0},\dots,\theta_{L}^{0}
.

while

Optimization routine has not converged

do

Prepare the purification
\ket{\psi}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sqrt{p_{i}}\ket{\psi_i}\otimes\ket{i}
on
\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{W}
.

Construct
U(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{L})
as above and apply
U(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{L})
to the ancilla,
\mathcal{W}
.

Measure.

Use measurements to compute
\sum_{i=1}^{d}q_{i}(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{L})\mu\Big(\varphi_{i}(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{L})\Big)
(or related quantities such as gradients as required by the chosen optimization technique).

Update
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{L}
according to optimization routine.

return

\bigg\{\Big(q_{i}(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{L})\Big)_{i=1}^{d},\Big(\varphi_{i}(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{L})\Big)_{i=1}^{d}\bigg\}
.

Algorithm 1

Variational Approximation of OPSE

An algorithm of this type is called a
variational quantum algorithm
(VQA)
[
26
]
. VQA has shown great success in various settings such as Hamiltonian Simulation and Quantum Machine Learning
[
21
,
26
,
27
,
28
]
where most cost functions are physically implementable on a quantum computer. In the algorithm described above, we instead train a nonlinear cost function on the measurements of a physically implementable ansatz. While VQA is believed, (not yet proven), to offer a possible quantum advantage over classical algorithms for certain problems
[
26
, Chapter 1 - Section C]
, there are still drawbacks, namely the issue of
Barren Plateaus

[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
. These are regions of the training landscape on which the partial derivatives with respect to the parameters decrease exponentially as the number of qubits in the experiment increase. More precisely, we give the following definition.

Consider the ansatz in Equation (
14
) on
k
qubits where
d=2^{k}
, with objective function

(15)

C(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{L})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}q_{i}(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{L})\mu\Big(\varphi_{i}(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{L})\Big).

Then the training landscape will contain barren plateaus in the parameter
\theta_{j}
for some
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, if

(16)

\text{Var}_{\mathbb{U}(2^{k})}\big[\partial_{\theta_{j}}C(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{L})\big]\sim\mathscr{O}(a^{k})

where the variance is taken with respect to the Haar measure on the Unitary group,
k
is the number of qubits, and

a\in(0,1)
is a real number
[
13
]
. When this variance is exponentially small, the ansatz can become untrainable in practice because an exponentially large amount of precision will be required on the classical hardware to evaluate the derivatives. For instance, if it were shown that the variance is on the order of
\frac{1}{2^{k}}
, where
k
is the number of qubits, then systems with only 100 qubits can start creating difficulties since the number of classical bits required to differentiate the values of the gradient from 0 will be in the order of
2^{-100}
.

In this article we show that the
f
-
d
-extension

T_{f,d}
of the Tsallis entanglement entropy
T_{2}
for
f(x)=x^{2}
exhibits
barren plateaus when using the ansatz given in Equation (
14
)
of large depth, (thus in particular, if no additional information is
available about the state whose entanglement is examined). In practice, if additional information about the state is known, then one needs to avoid using the suggested
ansatz for long depth of circuits. Recall that

T_{f,d}
is given by Equation (
7
).
While the OPSE of
T_{f,d}
will yield
T_{f,d}(\rho)=0
if we allow infinitely long decompositions as considered in
Remark
1.6
,
T_{f,d}
is still useful in
determining whether a state is entangled, as long as we consider decompositions of finite length, (by Proposition
1.8
).
Also, obviously,
T_{f,d}
is local unitary invariant (just as the Tsallis
entanglement entropy
T_{2}
), but is not LOCC monotonic.
In order to set the ground for Theorem
2.3
, first
define the cost function for the algorithm, that needs to be minimized, to be equal to
T_{f}(\rho;\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{L})

which is given by

(17)

T_{f,d}(\rho;\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{L})=\sum_{i}q_{i}^{2}(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{L})T_{2}(\varphi_{i}(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{L})).

To simplify notation somewhat, we define
\Phi_{i}
by

(18)

\begin{split}\Phi_{i}(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{L})&=q_{i}(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{L})\outerproduct{\varphi_i(\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_L)}{\varphi_i(\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_L)}\\
&=\text{Tr}\,_{\mathcal{W}}\Bigg(\Big(I_{\mathcal{H}}\otimes U^{*}\outerproduct{i}{i}U\Big)\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}\Bigg).\end{split}

For readability, we will usually suppress the parameters

(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{L})
and simply write
\Phi_{i}
.
Since, by Equation (
4
),
T_{f,d}
can be equivalently rewritten
from Equation (
7
) to

(19)

T_{f,d}(\rho)=\inf\bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}q_{i}^{2}(1-\text{Tr}\,((\text{Tr}\,_{\mathcal{A}}(\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i}))^{2})):\rho=\sum_{i=1}^{d}q_{i}\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i}\in\text{D}\,(\mathcal{H})_{d}\bigg\},

we can write
T_{f,d}
succinctly as

(20)

\sum_{i}\Big(\big(\text{Tr}\,\text{Tr}\,_{\mathcal{A}}\Phi_{i}\big)^{2}-\text{Tr}\,\big(\text{Tr}\,_{\mathcal{A}}\Phi_{i}^{2}\big)\Big)=\sum_{i}\Big(q_{i}^{2}-\text{Tr}\,\big(\text{Tr}\,_{\mathcal{A}}\Phi_{i}^{2}\big)\Big).

Notice that the last quantity is non-negative, since

\Big(\text{Tr}\,(P)\Big)^{2}\geq\text{Tr}\,(P^{2})
for all positive operators
P
. Indeed, let
P=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\Pi_{i}
be the spectral decomposition of
P
. Then all eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
are non-negative and the
\Pi_{i}
are mutually orthogonal projections with
\text{Tr}\,\Pi_{i}=1
for each
i
. Thus

(21)

\Big(\text{Tr}\,(P)\Big)^{2}=\sum_{i,j}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i\neq j}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}\geq\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}=\text{Tr}\,(P^{2}).

We show some modest limits on the trainability of
T_{f}(\rho;\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{L})
as summarized by the following theorem.

Theorem 2.3
.

If the parametrized circuit in Equation (
14
) is of sufficient depth, then for each
j\in\{1,\dots,L\}
,
\text{Var}[\frac{\partial}{\partial_{\theta_{j}}}T_{f}]\sim\mathscr{O}(\frac{1}{2^{k}})
, where the objective function is defined in Equation (
7
), the variance is taken with respect to the Haar measure on the unitary group, and
k
is the number of qubits.

Because of the length of the proof of the above theorem, its proof is presented in the appendix of this article.

Remark 2.4
.

As shown in the proof of Theorem
2.3
, the circuit must have a relatively large depth in order for a barren plateau to occur. Thus for most practical purposes, the ansatz should still be trainable since less expressive circuits are often used in practice. It is also possible to construct more “friendly”ansätze by taking into consideration properties of the given density matrix such as translation invariance.

Recall that for a two qubit state
\ket{\psi}=\sum_{i,j=0}^{1}c_{i,j}\ket{ij}

[

\text{C}(\psi)

(22)

\text{C}(\psi)=2|c_{0,0}c_{1,1}-c_{0,1}c_{1,0}|.

The concurrence is related to the von Neumann entanglement entropy via the formula

(23)

S(\psi)=h\Big(\frac{1+\sqrt{1-C(\psi)^{2}}}{2}\Big)

where
h
is the standard binary entropy defined by
h(x)=-x\log_{2}(x)-(1-x)\log_{2}(1-x)

[
1
]
. Interestingly enough, it is possible to directly measure the concurrence of a 2 qubit quantum system if there is access to two decoupled copies of the same state
[
29
]
. One can do this using the quantum circuit in Figure
3
, where the first two wires as well as the last two wires are in the state
\ket{\psi}
respectively, and
R
is the unitary

(24)

R=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
-1&1\\
\end{bmatrix}.

Figure 3
.
Quantum circuit for directly measuring concurrence in 2 qubit states.

After applying these operations to the state
\ket{\psi}\otimes\ket{\psi}
, the probability amplitude of the state
\ket{0000}
will be
\pm\frac{C(\psi)}{2\sqrt{2}}
.

Using the above ideas, we can append the circuit for measuring the concurrence of a state to the pure state ensemble circuit, so that we can measure the concurrence of a 2 qubit ensemble of states directly, and therefore efficiently compute the entanglement of formation for 2 qubit states. The entire circuit for this process is given in Figure

\rho

\ket{\psi}
of a two qubit density operator on the first four and last four quantum registers so that we can access two decoupled copies of the pure state ensemble. Next we apply

\ket{\varphi_i}

where the first two registers are in state

Approximating the Tsallis Entanglement Entropy Via The Swap Test

In this section we discuss some additional applications of the algorithm discussed in Section 2. Specifically, we show that it is possible to measure the convex roof extended Tsallis entanglement entropy directly using the swap test
[
32
]
.

Recall that the swap test can be used to estimate the quantity
|\bra{\psi}\ket{\phi}|^{2}
with
\varepsilon
error using the circuit in Figure
7
using
\mathscr{O}\big(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\big)
quantum queries. When running this circuit a large number of times, an observer will measure
\ket{0}
on the top wire with probability
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}|\bra{\psi}\ket{\phi}|^{2}
, and an observer will measure
\ket{1}
on the top wire with probability
\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}|\bra{\psi}\ket{\phi}|^{2}
.

Figure 7
.
Quantum circuit used for the swap test.

To see how this is useful for computing the Tsallis entropy of a state, consider a density operator
X=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\outerproduct{v_i}{v_i}
where
\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}=1
and the
\ket{v_i}
are pure states which are not necessarily orthonormal.
Then
\text{Tr}\,(X^{2})=\sum_{i,j}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}|\bra{v_i}\ket{v_j}|^{2}
. Thus, in order to compute the Tsallis entropy of
X
which is given by
T(X)=1-\text{Tr}\,(X^{2})
, (not to be confused with the Tsallis entanglement entropy
T_{2}
of a pure bipartite
state given in Equation (
4
)),
one needs to run the circuit in Figure
7
with inputs
\ket{\phi}=\ket{v_i}
and
\ket{\psi}=\ket{v_j}
for each
i
and
j
, measure the probability of measuring
\ket{1}
for each combination, and then weight the outputs by the appropriate probabilities
\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}
. In summary, if approximate values of
\lambda_{i}
’s can be computed,
then the Tsallis entropy
T(X)
of
X
can be approximated via

(26)

T(X)=2\sum_{i,j}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}\mathbb{P}\big[\text{top wire is }\ket{1}\big|\ket{\phi}=\ket{v_i},\ket{\psi}=\ket{v_j}\big].

Now we need to see how circuit is useful for approximating the Tsallis entanglement entropy, let

\rho\in\text{D}\,\Big(\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{B}\Big)
and let
\ket{\psi}
be a purification of
\rho
as in Equation (
10
). Next
let
\ket{\varphi_i}
and
q_{i}
be as in Equations (
13
) and (
12
). Then, the Tsallis
entanglement entropy of
\rho
can be approximated by minimizing the expression

(27)

\sum_{i}q_{i}T\Big(\Tr_{\mathcal{B}}\outerproduct{\varphi_i}{\varphi_i}\Big)

with respect to the
q_{i}
and
\ket{\varphi_i}
. However, the swap test requires two copies of the state, so we need to have two adjacent copies the pure state ensemble circuit, each of them as in Figure
1
, in order to access two copies the various ensembles of
\rho
and then append the quantum circuit for the swap
test. Thus we need a quantum circuit very similar to the circuit in Figure
4
. The full circuit
for this process is given in figure
8
.

Figure 8
.
Quantum circuit for approximating Tsallis Entanglement Entropy

Let’s go through this circuit one operation at a time to understand what’s going on, so that we can point out some loose
quantum query complexity constraints on this process. Before we begin, notice that in this circuit, we specified wires for the Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
instead of specifying one wire for
\mathcal{H}
. The reason for this is that we need to partial trace (measure and discard) either the
\mathcal{A}
or
\mathcal{B}
subsystems in order to approximate the Tsallis entanglement entropy.

First, we need to prepare two decoupled copies of the purification
\ket{\psi}
of a given density
\rho
and apply

U(\hat{\theta})
to each ancilla
\mathcal{W}
. Then we measure each ancilla
\mathcal{W}
. If we obtain measurements

i
and
j
on the two ancillas, then the state of the circuit will collapse into

\ket{0}\otimes\ket{\varphi_i}\otimes\ket{\varphi_j}
, (where
\ket{0}
is on the top wire, and

\ket{\varphi_i}
and
\ket{\varphi_j}
are on the wires representing the two copies of
\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{B}
).

If
i\neq j
, then all other operations will produce no useful information and we just use the observations to
approximate
q_{i}
and
q_{j}
.
Otherwise, if
i=j
, then the state of the circuit will be

\ket{0}\otimes\ket{\varphi_i}\otimes\ket{\varphi_i}
. In this case, we measure the space
\mathcal{B}
with respect
to a fixed basis (say the standard
Z
-basis) and collect all measurements weighted according to their probabilities
into one density matrix on the space
\mathcal{A}
.
The density matrix that is obtained that way is equal to

\text{Tr}\,_{\mathcal{B}}\outerproduct{\varphi_i}{\varphi_i}=\sum_{\alpha}\lambda_{\alpha,i}\outerproduct{\xi_{\alpha,i}}{\xi_{\alpha,i}}
for some probabilities

\lambda_{\alpha,i}
and some pure states
\ket{\xi_{\alpha,i}}
on
\mathcal{A}
for each
i
.
Therefore, upon measuring and discarding the wire(s) for the space
\mathcal{B}
, the first wire for
\mathcal{A}
will be in the state

\ket{\xi_{\alpha,i}}
, and the second wire for
\mathcal{A}
will be the state
\ket{\xi_{\alpha',i}}
for some
\alpha
and

\alpha^{\prime}
each appearing with probabilities
\lambda_{\alpha,i}
and
\lambda_{\alpha^{\prime},i}
respectively.
Then, the swap test circuit given in Figure
7
is appended with inputs
\ket{\phi}=\ket{\xi_{\alpha,i}}
and

\ket{\psi}=\ket{\xi_{\alpha',i}}
. As we noticed earlier, we will measure
\ket{1}
on the top wire with probability

\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}|\bra{\xi_{\alpha,i}}\ket{\xi_{\alpha',i}}|^{2}
.
Moreover, the state
\ket{\xi_{\alpha,i}}\otimes\ket{\xi_{\alpha',i}}
will occur with frequency
\lambda_{\alpha,i}\lambda_{\alpha^{\prime},i}N
where
N
is the number of shots. One can then run this experiment a desired number of times in order approximate
T(\text{Tr}\,_{\mathcal{B}}\outerproduct{\varphi_i}{\varphi_i})
which can seen to be equal to

(28)

T(\text{Tr}\,_{\mathcal{B}}\outerproduct{\varphi_i}{\varphi_i})=\sum_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}\lambda_{\alpha,i}\lambda_{\alpha^{\prime},i}\big(1-|\bra{\xi_{\alpha,i}}\ket{\xi_{\alpha',i}}|^{2}\big)

for each
i
. Thus, the Tsallis entanglement entropy of
\rho
is approximated by

(29)

T_{2}(\rho)\approx\min_{\hat{\theta}}\sum_{i}q_{i}\sum_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}\lambda_{\alpha,i}\lambda_{\alpha^{\prime},i}\big(1-|\bra{\xi_{\alpha,i}}\ket{\xi_{\alpha',i}}|^{2}\big).

Next we need to think about the algorithmic complexity of this approximation. In order for the quantum circuit to yield useful information both
ancillae must be in the same state
\ket{i}
for some
i
. By equation (
12
), this happens with probability
q_{i}^{2}
. Thus if we consider
the Bernoulli random variable
X
such that
X=
“i=j”occurs with probability
q_{i}^{2}
and
X=
“i
\neq
j”occurs with
probability
1-q_{i}^{2}
, then the expected number shots until the first success is given by
\frac{1}{q_{i}^{2}}
. Since
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{U}}[q_{i}]=\frac{1}{d}

for each
i
, we can see that the average number of shots until the first success,
i.e.
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{U}}[\frac{1}{q_{i}^{2}}]
, is then greater than or equal to
d^{2}
by applying Jensen’s inequality
using the convex function
1/x^{2}
for
x>0
.
And since
d=2^{k}
where
k
is the number of qubits in the system, we can see that the approximation will require exponentially many shots on average in order to compute
T(\text{Tr}\,_{\mathcal{B}}\outerproduct{\varphi_i}{\varphi_i})
within
some error
\varepsilon
for a fixed
\hat{\theta}
. Thus, while we are able to approximate the Tsallis entanglement entropy directly via the measurements of a quantum circuit, the process is inefficient.
While we do not prove it here, we suspect that there should be similar results for the von Neumann entanglement entropy
and related quantities using methods found in
[
33
,
34
]
. We also leave as an open to problem to determine
if this objective function suffers from barren plateaus.

5.
Concluding Remarks

We have shown how to access the different pure state ensembles of a given density operator using a quantum computer. We are thus able to approximate entanglement measures and related quantities that use convex roof constructions using a variational method and measurements from a parametrized quantum circuit. However we also show that the training landscape of a quantity related to the Tsallis entanglement entropy exhibits barren plateaus for a certain ansatz
and for circuits of large depth, effectively making the ansatz untrainable for large enough systems. In practice, if additional information about the state is known, then one needs to avoid using the suggested
ansatz for long depth of circuits. Lastly, we leave as an open problem the determination of barren plateaus of other entanglement measures and related quantities.

Appendix A
Integration Over The Unitary Group

In order to prove Theorem
2.3
, we’ll need the following results on integrals over the unitary group
[
35
,
36
]
:

Lemma A.1
.

For any strings of indices
i_{1},\dots,i_{p},j_{1},\dots,j_{p}
and
i_{1}^{\prime},\dots,i_{q}^{\prime},j_{1}^{\prime},\dots,j_{q}^{\prime}
whose values range from
1
to
d

(30)

\int_{\mathcal{U}(d)}U_{i_{1},j_{1}}\cdot\cdot\cdot U_{i_{p},j_{p}}\overline{U_{i_{1}^{\prime},j_{1}^{\prime}}}\cdot\cdot\cdot\overline{U_{i_{q}^{\prime},j_{q}^{\prime}}}dU=\delta_{p,q}\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in\text{S}_{p}}\text{Wg}(\sigma\tau^{-1})\delta_{i_{1},i_{\sigma(1)}^{\prime}}\delta_{j_{1},j_{\tau(1)}^{\prime}}\cdot\cdot\cdot\delta_{i_{p},i_{\sigma(p)}^{\prime}}\delta_{j_{p},j_{\tau(p)}^{\prime}}

where
dU
is the Haar-Measure on the Unitary group and
Wg
is the Weingarten function
[
37
,
38
]
.

While notation can make this lemma difficult to parse at first glance, notice that the integral is 0 when the number of complex conjugate entries of the matrix is different the number of non-conjugated entries. Unfortunately, the integrals used in this paper aren’t lucky enough to fall into this category and we must instead use the expression on the right hand side of the equation to evaluate our integrals. Since this expression can be difficult to understand at first, we supply a simple example of computing such an integral via this lemma.

Example A.2
.

Let
U,V
be unitary matrices on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Then the average gate fidelity between
U
and
V
,
F_{avg}(U,V)
, is defined by

(31)

F_{avg}(U,V):=\int_{\text{S}(\mathcal{H})}|\bra{\psi}U^{*}V\ket{\psi}|^{2}d\psi

where
\text{S}(\mathcal{H})
is unit ball of
\mathcal{H}
.

Such an integral over pure states is defined by

(32)

\int_{\text{S}(\mathcal{H})}|\bra{\psi}U^{*}V\ket{\psi}|^{2}d\psi:=\int_{\mathcal{U}(d)}|\bra{\psi_0}W^{*}U^{*}VW\ket{\psi_0}|^{2}dW

where each
\psi
is replaced by
W\ket{\psi_0}
for some arbitrary state
\ket{\psi_0}\in\mathcal{H}
. Now let
\{\ket{i}:1\leq i\leq d\}
be a basis for
\mathbb{C}^{d}
and without loss of generality, set
\ket{\psi_0}:=\ket{1}
. Next write
H=U^{*}V
and suppose that
H
has spectral decomposition

(33)

H=\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}P^{*}\outerproduct{i}{i}P

where
\sigma_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
with
|\sigma_{i}|=1
for each
1\leq i\leq d
and
P
is some unitary on
\mathcal{H}
. Using these substitutions, the integral in Equation(
32
) transforms as

(34)

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathcal{U}(d)}|\bra{\psi_0}W^{*}U^{*}VW\ket{\psi_0}|^{2}dW\\
&=\int_{\mathcal{U}(d)}\bra{1}W^{*}HW\ket{1}\overline{\bra{1}W^{*}HW\ket{1}}dW\\
&=\sum_{i,j}\sigma_{i}\overline{\sigma}_{j}\int_{\mathcal{U}(d)}\bra{1}W^{*}P^{*}\outerproduct{i}{i}PW\ket{1}\overline{\bra{1}W^{*}P^{*}\outerproduct{i}{i}PW\ket{1}}dW.\\
\end{split}

Now using the translation invariance of the Haar measure on
\mathcal{U}(d)
, we use a change of variable to simplify the above integral. Set
Q=PW
so that
dQ=d(PW)=dW
. Therefore

(35)

\begin{split}&\sum_{i,j}\sigma_{i}\overline{\sigma_{j}}\int_{\mathcal{U}(d)}\bra{1}W^{*}P^{*}\outerproduct{i}{i}PW\ket{1}\overline{\bra{1}W^{*}P^{*}\outerproduct{i}{i}PW\ket{1}}dW\\
&=\sum_{i,j}\sigma_{i}\overline{\sigma_{j}}\int_{\mathcal{U}(d)}\bra{1}Q^{*}\outerproduct{i}{i}Q\ket{1}\overline{\bra{1}Q^{*}\outerproduct{i}{i}Q\ket{1}}dQ\\
&=\sum_{i,j}\sigma_{i}\overline{\sigma_{j}}\int_{\mathcal{U}(d)}Q_{i,1}Q_{j,1}\overline{Q_{i,1}}\text{ }\overline{Q_{j,1}}dQ.\\
\end{split}

We can now use the integration formula given in (
A.1
) to evaluate the integral in the last expression of the previous equation and we arrive at

(36)

\begin{split}&\sum_{i,j}\sigma_{i}\overline{\sigma_{j}}\int_{\mathcal{U}(d)}Q_{i,1}Q_{j,1}\overline{Q_{i,1}}\text{ }\overline{Q_{j,1}}dQ\\
&=\sum_{i,j}\sigma_{i}\overline{\sigma_{j}}\Big(\text{Wg}((1))(1+\delta_{i,j})+\text{Wg}((1,2))(1+\delta_{i,j})\Big)\\
&=\frac{1}{d(d+1)}\sum_{i,j}\sigma_{i}\overline{\sigma_{j}}(1+\delta_{i,j}).\end{split}

where
\text{Wg}\big((1)\big)=\frac{1}{d^{2}-1}
and
\text{Wg}\big((1,2)\big)=\frac{-1}{d(d^{2}-1)}
. Since
H
is a unitary, each
\sigma_{i}
has absolute value 1, and so

(37)

\sum_{i,j}\sigma_{i}\overline{\sigma_{j}}\delta_{i,j}=\sum_{i}|\sigma_{i}|^{2}=d.

To compute the other sum, we use the fact that the sum of the eignvalues of a matrix is its trace, so that

(38)

\begin{split}\sum_{i,j}\sigma_{i}\overline{\sigma_{j}}=\Big(\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}\Big)\Big(\sum_{j}\overline{\sigma_{j}}\Big)=|\text{Tr}\,(H)|^{2}.\end{split}

Recalling that
H=U^{*}V
, we can see that
\text{Tr}\,(H)=\langle U,V\rangle_{HS}
where the inner product is taken to be the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product.
Thus it follows that

(39)

F_{avg}(U,V)=\frac{|\langle U,V\rangle|^{2}+d}{d(d+1)}.

We can see that in order to compute these integrals, we must be familiar with the Weingarten function on the unitary group. Proof of Theorem

Before computing the mean and variance of the derivatives of the cost function
T_{f}(\rho;\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{L})
, we must first discuss the probability distribution of unitaries produced by the circuits defined in Equation(
14
).

In order to make the notation more readable, suppose that
\theta=\theta_{j}
for some
j\in\{1,\dots,L\}
and that
V=V_{j}
. Then the derivative of
U
with respect to
\theta
can be written as

(40)

\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}U(\theta)=i\Big(\prod_{l_{1}=1}^{j}\exp(i\theta_{l_1}V_{l_1})E_{l_{1}}\Big)V\Big(\prod_{l_{2}=j+1}^{L}\exp(i\theta_{l_2}V_{l_2})E_{l_{2}}\Big).

The derivative splits the circuit into two “pieces”, the left side and the right side which we define accordingly

(41)

L=\prod_{l_{1}=1}^{j}\exp(i\theta_{l_1}V_{l_1})E_{l_{1}}\text{ and }R=\prod_{l_{2}=j+1}^{L}\exp(i\theta_{l_2}V_{l_2})E_{l_{2}}

which lets us write
\partial_{\theta}U(\theta)=iLVR
. Moreover, using this expression for the derivative of
U
lets us express
\partial_{\theta}U^{*}\outerproduct{i}{i}U
as

(42)

\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}U^{*}\outerproduct{i}{i}U=\sqrt{-1}R^{*}\big[V,L^{*}\outerproduct{i}{i}L\big]R.

We will often write
K_{i}=\sqrt{-1}\big[V,L^{*}\outerproduct{i}{i}L\big]
for readability so that

(43)

\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}U^{*}\outerproduct{i}{i}U=R^{*}K_{i}R.

This splitting forces us to consider the distributions of the
L
and
R
separately. Therefore, just as in the paper
[
23
]
by McClean et alii, we write the probability distribution generated by the entire circuit as

(44)

\rho(U)dU=\delta(U-LR)\rho_{1}(L)\rho_{2}(R)dLdR

where
dU,dL,
and
dR
are the Haar measure on
\mathcal{U}(d)
,
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
are densities on
\mathcal{U}(d)
, and
\delta
is the dirac measure centered at the
0-
matrix. This distribution forces any unitary
U
to be split as the product of two other unitaries
L
and
R
with their own distributions
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
respectively. To quantify these distribution we’ll need to use the notion of
unitary k-designs
. A distribution
P
is defined to be a k-design if
P
matches the Haar measure on the unitary group up to and including the k-th moment
[
40
,
41
,
35
,
36
]
. In other words,

(45)

\int_{\mathcal{U}(d)}U^{\otimes l}\otimes(U^{*})^{\otimes l}P(U)d\mu(U)=\int_{\mathcal{U}(d)}U^{\otimes l}\otimes(U^{*})^{\otimes l}d\mu(U)

for all
0\leq l\leq k
. In practice, exact
k
-designs are costly to produce. And so we’ll also need the notion of an
\varepsilon-
approximate
k
-design
[
41
]
. We say that a a measure
\nu
is an
\varepsilon
-approximate
k
-design if and only if

(46)

(1-\varepsilon)\int_{\mathcal{U}(d)}U^{\otimes k}X(U^{*})^{\otimes k}dU\leq\int_{\mathcal{U}(d)}U^{\otimes k}X(U^{*})^{\otimes k}d\nu(U)\leq(1+\varepsilon)\int_{\mathcal{U}(d)}U^{\otimes k}X(U^{*})^{\otimes k}dU

for any
X\in\text{L}\,\big(\mathbb{C}^{d\otimes k}\big)
, where
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
dU
is the Haar measure on
\mathcal{U}(d)
. Such measures
\nu
are attainable in practice using only polynomially many gates in the number of qubits
[
40
,
41
]
, thus the result is relevant for NISQ devices.

With the distribution
\rho
from Equation (
44
) in mind, we will split the computation of the mean and variance of
\partial_{\theta}T_{f}(\rho;\theta)
into two cases; One in which we assume that
\rho_{1}
is a 4-design, and the other in which
\rho_{2}
is a 4-design. Note that this assumption is a practical one since it has been proven
[
40
,
41
]
that polynomial depth quantum circuits such as those given in Equation (
14
) are
\epsilon-
approximate k-designs, if they are of sufficient depth. Therefore, since the gates used the ansatz of
Equation (
14
) form a universal gate set, if the depth of that
ansatz is sufficiently large, then at least one of
L
and
R
that appear in Equation (
41
) has sufficient depth in order to yield that
\rho_{1}
or
\rho_{2}
is a 4-design.

Next let’s expand the various components of
\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}T_{f}(\rho;\theta)
using the definitions of
q_{i}
and
\Phi_{i}
, given in Equations (
12
) and (
18
) respectively, for each
i\in\{1,\dots,d\}
.

(47)

\begin{split}q_{i}&=\bra{\psi}\Big(I_{\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B}}\otimes U^{*}\outerproduct{i}{i}U\Big)\ket{\psi}\\
&=\Big(\sum_{j_{1}^{\prime}}\sqrt{p_{j_{1}^{\prime}}}\bra{\psi_{j_1'}}\otimes\bra{j_1'}\Big)\Big(I_{\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B}}\otimes U^{*}\outerproduct{i}{i}U\Big)\Big(\sum_{j_{1}}\sqrt{p_{j_{1}}}\ket{\psi_{j_1}}\otimes\ket{j_1}\Big)\\
&=\sum_{j_{1},j_{1}^{\prime}}\sqrt{p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{1}^{\prime}}}\bra{\psi_{j_1'}}\ket{\psi_{j_1}}\bra{j_1'}U^{*}\outerproduct{i}{i}U\ket{j_1}\\
&=\sum_{j_{1},j_{1}^{\prime}}\sqrt{p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{1}^{\prime}}}\bra{\psi_{j_1'}}\ket{\psi_{j_1}}\bra{i}U\ket{j_1}\overline{\bra{i}U\ket{j_1'}}\\
&=\sum_{j_{1},j_{1}^{\prime}}\sqrt{p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{1}^{\prime}}}\bra{\psi_{j_1'}}\ket{\psi_{j_1}}\bra{i}LR\ket{j_1}\overline{\bra{i}LR\ket{j_1'}}.\\
\end{split}

It can be shown in a similar fashion that

(48)

\text{Tr}\,_{\mathcal{A}}\Phi_{i}=\sum_{j_{1},j_{1}^{\prime}}\sqrt{p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{1}^{\prime}}}\text{ }\text{Tr}\,_{\mathcal{A}}\outerproduct{\psi_{j_1}}{\psi_{j_1'}}\bra{i}LR\ket{j_1}\overline{\bra{i}LR\ket{j_1'}}.\\

Using the commutator expression given in Equation (
42
) for the derivative of
q_{i}
as follows:

(49)

\begin{split}\frac{\partial q_{i}}{\partial\theta}&=\bra{\psi}\Big(I_{\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B}}\otimes\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}U^{*}\outerproduct{i}{i}U\Big)\ket{\psi}\\
&=\bra{\psi}\Big(I_{\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B}}\otimes R^{*}K_{i}R\Big)\ket{\psi}\\
&=\sum_{j_{1},j_{1}^{\prime}}\sqrt{p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{1}^{\prime}}}\bra{\psi_{j_1'}}\ket{\psi_{j_1}}\bra{j_1'}R^{*}K_{i}R\ket{j_1}.\\
\end{split}

In the same way, a similar expression is found for

:

(50)

\frac{\partial\text{Tr}\,_{\mathcal{A}}\Phi_{i}}{\partial\theta}=\sum_{j_{1},j_{1}^{\prime}}\sqrt{p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{1}^{\prime}}}\text{ }\text{Tr}\,_{\mathcal{A}}\outerproduct{\psi_{j_1}}{\psi_{j_1'}}\bra{j_1'}R^{*}K_{i}R\ket{j_1}.

Putting all of this together we see that

(51)

\begin{split}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}q_{i}^{2}&=2q_{i}\frac{\partial q_{i}}{\partial\theta}\\
&=\sum_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{1}^{\prime},j_{2}^{\prime}}\sqrt{p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{1}^{\prime}}p_{j_{2}}p_{j_{2}^{\prime}}}\bra{\psi_{j_1'}}\ket{\psi_{j_1}}\bra{\psi_{j_2'}}\ket{\psi_{j_2}}\bra{i}LR\ket{j_1}\overline{\bra{i}LR\ket{j_1'}}\bra{j_2'}R^{*}K_{i}R\ket{j_2}.\\
\end{split}

Also,
\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\text{Tr}\,\Big(\text{Tr}\,_{\mathcal{A}}\Phi_{i}^{2}\Big)
has a similar expression:

(52)

\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\text{Tr}\,\Big(\text{Tr}\,_{\mathcal{A}}\Phi_{i}^{2}\Big)=2\text{Tr}\,\Big(\text{Tr}\,_{\mathcal{A}}\Phi_{i}\frac{\partial\text{Tr}\,_{\mathcal{A}}\Phi_{i}}{\partial\theta}\Big)\\

which can be seen to be equal to

(53)

2\sum_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{1}^{\prime},j_{2}^{\prime}}\sqrt{p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{1}^{\prime}}p_{j_{2}}p_{j_{2}^{\prime}}}\text{ }\text{Tr}\,\Big(\text{Tr}\,_{\mathcal{A}}\outerproduct{\psi_{j_1}}{\psi_{j_1'}}\text{Tr}\,_{\mathcal{A}}\outerproduct{\psi_{j_2}}{\psi_{j_2'}}\Big)\bra{i}LR\ket{j_1}\overline{\bra{i}LR\ket{j_1'}}\bra{j_2'}R^{*}K_{i}R\ket{j_2}.

Putting all of this together, we can expand
\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}T_{f}(\rho;\theta)
as

(54)

\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}T_{f}(\rho;\theta)=2\sum_{i}\Bigg(q_{i}\frac{\partial q_{i}}{\partial\theta}-\text{Tr}\,\Big(\text{Tr}\,_{\mathcal{A}}\Phi_{i}\frac{\partial\text{Tr}\,_{\mathcal{A}}\Phi_{i}}{\partial\theta}\Big)\Bigg),

and then integrate this expression term by term using the expressions above. In what follows, we’ll fix an
i
and integrate the terms
q_{i}\frac{\partial q_{i}}{\partial\theta}
and
\text{Tr}\,\Big(\text{Tr}\,_{\mathcal{A}}\Phi_{i}\frac{\partial\text{Tr}\,_{\mathcal{A}}\Phi_{i}}{\partial\theta}\Big)
separately for clarity. After the computation of the integrals, we’ll then sum the results to arrive at the appropriate expected value.

Now notice that the expressions given in equations (
51
) and (
53
) are of the form

(55)

2\sum_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{1}^{\prime},j_{2}^{\prime}}\sqrt{p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{1}^{\prime}}p_{j_{2}}p_{j_{2}^{\prime}}}c_{j_{1},j_{1}^{\prime},j_{2},j_{2}^{\prime}}\bra{i}LR\ket{j_1}\overline{\bra{i}LR\ket{j_1'}}\bra{j_2'}R^{*}K_{i}R\ket{j_2}

where
|c_{j_{1},j_{1}^{\prime},j_{2},j_{2}^{\prime}}|\leq 1
for any choice of indices. Because we only need an upper estimate for our variance, we’ll soon see that we don’t need the exact values of the
c_{j_{1},j_{1}^{\prime},j_{2},j_{2}^{\prime}}
and that we’ll only use the fact that they are bounded in absolute value by 1. Next we need to integrate the above expression with respect to the distribution
\rho_{1}(L)\rho_{2}(R)dLdR
. Since only the expression
\bra{i}LR\ket{j_1}\overline{\bra{i}LR\ket{j_1'}}\bra{j_2'}R^{*}K_{i}R\ket{j_2}
is dependent on
L
and
R
, we can integrate this expression and substitute its value back into Equation (
55
).

In the integration formula given in Lemma
A.1
, the expressions that were integrated were given in terms of the coordinates of the unitaries, but the expression we currently have is not in this form. We must therefore force the expression into this form and expand the commutator
R^{*}K_{i}R
. It’s easy to see that
\bra{i}LR\ket{j_1}\overline{\bra{i}LR\ket{j_1'}}\bra{j_2'}R^{*}K_{i}R\ket{j_2}
is, up to a multiple of
\sqrt{-1}
, equal to

(56)

\bra{i}LR\ket{j_1}\overline{\bra{i}LR\ket{j_1'}}\cdot\Big(\bra{j_2'}R^{*}VL^{*}\outerproduct{i}{i}LR\ket{j_2}-\bra{j_2'}R^{*}L^{*}\outerproduct{i}{i}LVR\ket{j_2}\Big).

For our computations, we’ll just use the first term,
\bra{j_2'}R^{*}VL^{*}\outerproduct{i}{i}LR\ket{j_2}
, of the commutator since the computations with the second term are nearly identical. Suppose for now that
\rho_{1}(L)dL
is at least a 2-design, then
\rho_{1}(L)dL
is equal to the Haar-distribution
dL
up to the second moment. Thus

(57)

\begin{split}&\int\int\bra{i}LR\ket{j_1}\overline{\bra{i}LR\ket{j_1'}}\bra{j_2'}R^{*}VL^{*}\outerproduct{i}{i}LR\ket{j_2}\rho_{1}(L)\rho_{2}(R)dLdR\\
&=\int\int\bra{i}LR\ket{j_1}\bra{i}LR\ket{j_2}\overline{\bra{i}LR\ket{j_1'}}\text{ }\overline{\bra{i}LVR\ket{j_2'}}\rho_{2}(R)dLdR.\end{split}

Using the translation invariance of the Haar-integral on the Unitary group, we define the change of variables
W=LR
with
dW=dL
. The integral then becomes

(58)

\begin{split}&\int\int\bra{i}LR\ket{j_1}\bra{i}LR\ket{j_2}\overline{\bra{i}LR\ket{j_1'}}\text{ }\overline{\bra{i}LVR\ket{j_2'}}\rho_{2}(R)dLdR\\
&=\int\int\bra{i}W\ket{j_1}\bra{i}W\ket{j_2}\overline{\bra{i}W\ket{j_1'}}\text{ }\overline{\bra{i}WR^{*}VR\ket{j_2'}}dW\rho_{2}(R)dR\\
&=\int\int W_{i,j_{1}}W_{i,j_{2}}\overline{W_{i,j_{1}^{\prime}}}\text{ }\overline{\bra{i}WR^{*}VR\ket{j_2'}}dW\rho_{2}(R)dR.\\
\end{split}

The last thing that we need to do before using the integration formula is to express
\overline{\bra{i}WR^{*}VR\ket{j_2'}}
in terms of the coordinates of
W
. To do this, first define
Q=R^{*}VR
so that

(59)

\begin{split}R^{*}VR\ket{j_2'}&=Q\ket{j_2'}\\
&=\sum_{\alpha}Q_{\alpha,j_{2}^{\prime}}\ket{\alpha},\\
\end{split}

and thus

(60)

\begin{split}\overline{\bra{i}WR^{*}VR\ket{j_2'}}&=\overline{\bra{i}WQ\ket{j_2'}}\\
&=\sum_{\alpha}\overline{Q_{\alpha,j_{2}^{\prime}}}\text{ }\overline{\bra{i}W\ket{\alpha}}.\\
\end{split}

Substituting this expression back into the integral we see that

(61)

\begin{split}&\int\int W_{i,j_{1}}W_{i,j_{2}}\overline{W_{i,j_{1}^{\prime}}}\text{ }\overline{\bra{i}WR^{*}VR\ket{j_2'}}dW\rho_{2}(R)dR\\
&=\sum_{\alpha}\int\overline{Q_{\alpha,j_{2}^{\prime}}}\int W_{i,j_{1}}W_{i,j_{2}}\overline{W_{i,j_{1}^{\prime}}}\text{ }\overline{\bra{i}W\ket{\alpha}}dW\rho_{2}(R)dR\\
&=\sum_{\alpha}\int\overline{Q_{\alpha,j_{2}^{\prime}}}\int W_{i,j_{1}}W_{i,j_{2}}\overline{W_{i,j_{1}^{\prime}}}\text{ }\overline{W_{i,\alpha}}dW\rho_{2}(R)dR.\\
\end{split}

Notice that all of the row coordinates of the
W
’s in the integral are equal to
i
, so we can suppress the
\delta_{i_{k},i_{\sigma(k)}^{\prime}}
terms in the integration formula from Lemma (
A.1
) since they are always equal to 1. The index
\alpha
also took the place of

in the column coordinate of one of the
W
s in the last expression of the above equation.Therefore the

must be treated as a

when using the integration formula from Lemma
A.1
. For clarity, we isolate the inner integral and compute it as follows:

(62)

\begin{split}&\int W_{i,j_{1}}W_{i,j_{2}}\overline{W_{i,j_{1}^{\prime}}}\text{ }\overline{W_{i,\alpha}}dW\\
&=\text{Wg}\Big((1)_{2}\Big)\delta_{j_{1},j_{1}^{\prime}}\delta_{j_{2},\alpha}\hskip 36.135pt(\sigma=\tau=(1)_{2})\\
&+\text{Wg}\Big((1,2)_{2}\Big)\delta_{j_{1},j_{1}^{\prime}}\delta_{j_{2},\alpha}\hskip 36.135pt(\sigma=(1,2)_{2},\tau=(1)_{2})\\
&+\text{Wg}\Big((1,2)_{2}\Big)\delta_{j_{1},\alpha}\delta_{j_{2},j_{1}^{\prime}}\hskip 36.135pt(\sigma=(1)_{2},\tau=(1,2)_{2})\\
&+\text{Wg}\Big((1)_{2}\Big)\delta_{j_{1},\alpha}\delta_{j_{2},j_{1}^{\prime}}\hskip 36.135pt(\sigma=\tau=(1,2)_{2})\\
&=\Big(\text{Wg}(1,1)+\text{Wg}(2)\Big)\Big(\delta_{j_{1},j_{1}^{\prime}}\delta_{j_{2},\alpha}+\delta_{j_{1},\alpha}\delta_{j_{2},j_{1}^{\prime}}\Big).\\
\end{split}

Defining

and substituting the last expression of the above equation back into Equation (

), we see that the value of the integral is equal to

(63)

\begin{split}&C\sum_{\alpha}\int\overline{Q_{\alpha,j_{2}^{\prime}}}\Big(\delta_{j_{1},j_{1}^{\prime}}\delta_{j_{2},\alpha}+\delta_{j_{1},\alpha}\delta_{j_{2},j_{1}^{\prime}}\Big)\rho_{2}(R)dR\\
&=C\int\overline{Q_{j_{2}j_{2}^{\prime}}}\delta_{j_{1},j_{1}^{\prime}}\rho_{2}(R)dR+C\int\overline{Q_{j_{1},j_{2}^{\prime}}}\delta_{j_{2},j_{1}^{\prime}}\rho_{2}(R)dR.\end{split}

Ignoring the integral and outside constants for the moment, let’s plug the expressions
\overline{Q_{j_{2}j_{2}^{\prime}}}\delta_{j_{1},j_{1}^{\prime}}
and
\overline{Q_{j_{1},j_{2}^{\prime}}}\delta_{j_{2},j_{1}^{\prime}}
into Equation (

, we see that

\begin{split}\sum_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{1}^{\prime},j_{2}^{\prime}}\sqrt{p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{1}^{\prime}}p_{j_{2}}p_{j_{2}^{\prime}}}c_{j_{1},j_{1}^{\prime},j_{2},j_{2}^{\prime}}\overline{Q_{j_{2},j_{2}^{\prime}}}\delta_{j_{1},j_{1}^{\prime}}&=\sum_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{2}^{\prime}}p_{j_{1}}\sqrt{p_{j_{2}}p_{j_{2}^{\prime}}}c_{j_{1},j_{1}^{\prime},j_{2},j_{2}^{\prime}}\overline{Q_{j_{2},j_{2}^{\prime}}}.\\
\end{split}

Now taking absolute values,

(65)

\begin{split}\Big|\sum_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{2}^{\prime}}p_{j_{1}}\sqrt{p_{j_{2}}p_{j_{2}^{\prime}}}c_{j_{1},j_{1}^{\prime},j_{2},j_{2}^{\prime}}\overline{Q_{j_{2},j_{2}^{\prime}}}\Big|&\leq\sum_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{2}^{\prime}}p_{j_{1}}\sqrt{p_{j_{2}}p_{j_{2}^{\prime}}}\big|c_{j_{1},j_{1}^{\prime},j_{2},j_{2}^{\prime}}\big|\cdot\big|Q_{j_{2},j_{2}^{\prime}}\big|\\
&\leq\sum_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{2}^{\prime}}p_{j_{1}}\sqrt{p_{j_{2}}p_{j_{2}^{\prime}}}\big|Q_{j_{2},j_{2}^{\prime}}\big|\\
&=\Bigg(\sum_{j_{1}}p_{j_{1}}\Bigg)\cdot\Bigg(\sum_{j_{2},j_{2}^{\prime}}\sqrt{p_{j_{2}}p_{j_{2}^{\prime}}}\big|Q_{j_{2},j_{2}^{\prime}}\big|\Bigg)\\
&=\sum_{j_{2},j_{2}^{\prime}}\sqrt{p_{j_{2}}p_{j_{2}^{\prime}}}\big|Q_{j_{2},j_{2}^{\prime}}\big|.\\
\end{split}

Lastly using the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality and the fact that the Hilbert-Schmidt norm of a

\sqrt{d}
, we can see that

(66)

\begin{split}\sum_{j_{2},j_{2}^{\prime}}\sqrt{p_{j_{2}}p_{j_{2}^{\prime}}}\big|Q_{j_{2},j_{2}^{\prime}}\big|&\leq\Bigg(\sum_{j_{2},j_{2}^{\prime}}p_{j_{2}}p_{j_{2}^{\prime}}\Bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Bigg(\sum_{j_{2},j_{2}^{\prime}}\big|Q_{j_{2},j_{2}^{\prime}}\big|^{2}\Bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
&=\|Q\|_{HS}\\
&=\sqrt{d}.\\
\end{split}

The computation when substituting the term
\overline{Q_{j_{1},j_{2}^{\prime}}}\delta_{j_{2},j_{1}^{\prime}}
is almost exactly the same so we omit it for brevity. We have thus showed that when
\rho_{1}(L)dL
is at least a 2 design and
\rho_{2}(R)dR
is an arbitrary distribution, the integral of Equation (

) is bounded in absolute value as

(67)

\begin{split}&\Bigg|\int\int 2\sum_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{1}^{\prime},j_{2}^{\prime}}\sqrt{p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{1}^{\prime}}p_{j_{2}}p_{j_{2}^{\prime}}}c_{j_{1},j_{1}^{\prime},j_{2},j_{2}^{\prime}}\bra{i}LR\ket{j_1}\overline{\bra{i}LR\ket{j_1'}}\bra{j_2'}R^{*}K_{i}R\ket{j_2}\rho_{1}(L)dL\rho_{2}(R)dR\Bigg|\\
&\leq\int 2C\sqrt{d}\rho_{2}(R)dR\\
&=\frac{2\sqrt{d}}{d(d+1)}.\end{split}

And since both
\mathbb{E}\Bigg[2q_{i}\frac{\partial q_{i}}{\partial\theta}\Bigg]
and
\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\text{Tr}\,\Big(\text{Tr}\,_{\mathcal{A}}\Phi_{i}^{2}\Big)\Bigg]
are both bounded by the above integral, it follows that

Therefore

(69)

\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}T_{f}(\rho;\theta)\Bigg]=\sum_{i}\Bigg(\mathbb{E}\Bigg[2q_{i}\frac{\partial q_{i}}{\partial\theta}\Bigg]-\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\text{Tr}\,\Big(\text{Tr}\,_{\mathcal{A}}\Phi_{i}^{2}\Big)\Bigg]\Bigg)\sim\mathscr{O}\Bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\Bigg).\\

This shows that the expected gradients converge exponentially to 0 in the number of qubits when

is at least a 2-design and the distribution of
R
is arbitrary.
