1.
Introduction

Let
k
be a field, which we assume is imperfect of characteristic
p
. In this case there exist pseudo-reductive
k
-groups which are not reductive. Recall that a smooth, connected, affine algebraic
k
-group
G
is
pseudo-reductive
if the largest smooth connected unipotent normal
k
-subgroup
\mathscr{R}_{u,k}(G)
is trivial.
†
†

* Funded by Leverhulme Trust Research Project Grant number RPG-2021-080

We are interested in the structure of the geometric unipotent radical
R:=\mathscr{R}_{u}(G_{\bar{k}})=\mathscr{R}_{u,\bar{k}}(G_{\bar{k}})
of
G
. Since
R
is a
p
-group, it makes sense to study its
exponent
: the minimal integer
s
such that the
p^{s}
-power map on the geometric unipotent radical factors through the trivial group. We denote the
exponent
of
R
by
e(R)
.

M. Bate, B. Martin, G. Röhrle and the third author previously gave some bounds for
e(R)
in
[
1
]
. For example,
[
1
, Lem. 4.1]
implies that if
k^{\prime}/k
is a simple purely inseparable field extension with
(k^{\prime})^{p^{e}}\subseteq k
, then
e(R)\leq e
.

The monographs
[
4
]
and
[
5
]
contain a classification of pseudo-reductive groups. They are all related in some way or other to the Weil restrictions of reductive groups, which are themselves pseudo-reductive. We focus on groups which are Weil restrictions, since general pseudo-reductive groups contain central pseudo-reductive subgroups whose classification is thought to be intractable. Thus we assume unless stated otherwise that
G=\mathrm{R}_{k^{\prime}/k}(G^{\prime})
for some reductive
k^{\prime}
-group
G^{\prime}
where
k^{\prime}
is a finite non-zero reduced
k
-algebra. Since we are interested in
e(R)
, by the remarks in
[
1
, §4]
we may as well consider the case that
k
is separably closed and
k^{\prime}
is a purely inseparable field extension of
k
. In this case
G^{\prime}
is a split reductive group and as such is classified by its root datum.

Recall that the exponent of the extension
k^{\prime}/k
is the smallest integer
e
such that
(k^{\prime})^{p^{e}}\subseteq k
. To describe our results, we need more sensitive data about the structure of
k^{\prime}/k
. Since
k^{\prime}/k
is purely inseparable, we can appeal to the results in
[
10
]
and
[
11
]
, which guarantee a
normal generating sequence

\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{l}
of
k^{\prime}
over
k
with certain properties—see Definition
2.6
below for more details.
In particular, these elements come with certain exponents which are invariants of
k^{\prime}/k
. These are the integers
e_{1}\geq\ldots\geq e_{l}
such that
e_{i}
is minimal subject to

\alpha_{i}^{p^{e_{i}}}\in k(\alpha_{1}^{p^{e_{i}}},\ldots,\alpha_{i-1}^{p^{e_{i}}}).

It is immediate that
e_{1}=e
and it follows from the Tower Law that
[k^{\prime}:k]=\prod p^{e_{i}}
.
Let
r\in\mathbb{N}
. We define integers

\displaystyle\m(k^{\prime}/k)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{l}(p^{e_{i}}-1)+1=\sum_{i=1}^{l}p^{e_{i}}-l+1,

\displaystyle\Em(k^{\prime}/k)

\displaystyle:=\lceil\log_{p}(\m(k^{\prime}/k))\rceil,

\displaystyle\mr(k^{\prime}/k,r)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{r}p^{e_{i}}\text{,\quad where we take $e_{i}=0$ for $i>l$},

\displaystyle\emr(k^{\prime}/k,r)

\displaystyle:=\lceil\log_{p}(\mr(k^{\prime}/k,r))\rceil,

\displaystyle\text{and finally }\E(k^{\prime}/k,r)

\displaystyle:=\min\{\Em(k^{\prime}/k),\emr(k^{\prime}/k,r)\}.

In other words
\Em(k^{\prime}/k)
is the minimal
s
such that
p^{s}\geq\m(k^{\prime}/k)
, and similarly for
\emr
.

In answering a question of
[
1
]
on the exponent of the intersection of
R
with a pseudo-Borel subgroup, we discovered that these numbers give lower bounds on
e(R)
and for large enough rank determine the exponent of
R
exactly. We first state our results in the case that
G^{\prime}
is a general linear group.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
k^{\prime}/k
be a finite purely inseparable field extension of a separably closed field
k
of characteristic
p
. Suppose
k^{\prime}/k
has exponent
e
and a normal generating sequence
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{l}
with sequence of exponents
e_{1},\ldots,e_{l}
. For
r\in\mathbb{N}
, let
G^{\prime}:=\GL_{r}
,
G:=\mathrm{R}_{k^{\prime}/k}(G^{\prime})
and
R:=\mathscr{R}_{u}(G_{\bar{k}})
. Furthermore let
B^{\prime}
denote a Borel subgroup of
G^{\prime}
and
B:=\mathrm{R}_{k^{\prime}/k}(B^{\prime})\subseteq G
the corresponding pseudo-Borel subgroup of
G
. The following hold:

(i)

if
s
satisfies
p^{s}\geq p^{e}r
then
e(R)
is at most
s
; i.e.
e(R)\leq\lceil e+\log_{p}(r)\rceil
;

(ii)

we have
e(R\cap B_{\bar{k}})=\E(k^{\prime}/k,r)
;

(iii)

in particular,
e(R)\geq\E(k^{\prime}/k,r)
;

(iv)

if
\sum_{i=r+1}^{l}(p^{e_{i}}-1)<r-1
then
e(R)=\E(k^{\prime}/k,r)=\Em(k^{\prime}/k)
.

Remarks 1.2
.

(i) The bounds in Theorem
1.1
are both arithmetic and Lie-theoretic in character: the integers
\m(k^{\prime}/k)
and
\Em(k^{\prime}/k)
depend only on the arithmetic structure of
k^{\prime}/k
but
\mr(k^{\prime}/k,r)
,
\emr(k^{\prime}/k,r)
and
\E(k^{\prime}/k,r)
depend on both
k^{\prime}/k
and the rank
r
of
G^{\prime}
.

(ii). Part (i) of Theorem
1.1
improves
[
1
, Lem. 4.4]
, which required
p^{s}\geq r^{2}(p^{e}-1)
. As a corollary of (i) and (iv), if
G=\mathrm{R}_{k^{\prime}/k}(\GL_{r})
for
p\geq r\geq 2
then the exponent of
\mathscr{R}_{u}(G_{\bar{k}})
is
e
if
k^{\prime}/k
is a simple extension and is
e+1
otherwise. If
r=1
, then
e(R)=e
as
\mathrm{R}_{k^{\prime}/k}(\GL_{1})
is sent into the canonical
\GL_{1}

k
-subgroup by the
p^{e}
-power map, since it also sends
k^{\prime}
into
k
.

(iii). In
[
1
, Rem. 4.5(iv)]
, it was asked if the exponent
e(R)
always coincides with the exponent
e(R\cap B_{\bar{k}})
.
Clearly Theorem
1.1
(ii)-(iv) answers this question in the affirmative when
G^{\prime}=\GL_{r}
of large enough rank.
We give an example to show that in general the answer is ‘no’ (see Proposition
4.3
), coming from
\SL_{2}
in characteristic
2
. In fact, in Section
4
we give a complete description of
e(R)
when
G^{\prime}
is
\SL_{2}
,
\PGL_{2}
or
\GL_{2}
.

Together with the rank
1
results in Section
4
, the following (proved in Section
5
) confirms the large rank behaviour in all simple groups, with mild conditions on the characteristic.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
k^{\prime}/k
be a finite, purely inseparable field extension of a separably closed field
k
of characteristic
p
. Suppose
k^{\prime}/k
has exponent
e
and a normal generating sequence
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{l}
with sequence of exponents
e_{1},\ldots,e_{l}
. Let
G^{\prime}
be a (split) simple algebraic
k
-group of rank
r\geq 2
,
G:=R_{k^{\prime}/k}(G^{\prime})
and let
R:=\mathscr{R}_{u}(G_{\bar{k}})
. If
G^{\prime}
is type
B,C,D,F
then assume
p\neq 2
or
G^{\prime}
is
\SO_{n}
or
\Sp_{2n}
, and if
G
is of type
E_{6}
then assume

. Then the exponent
e(R)\geq\E(k^{\prime}/k,r)
; if
\sum_{i=r+1}^{l}(p^{e_{i}}-1)<r-1
then in fact,
e(R)=\E(k^{\prime}/k,r)=\Em(k^{\prime}/k)
.

2.
Preliminaries and Notation

Let
k
be a field of characteristic
p
and
G
be a
k
-group, by which we mean an affine algebraic group scheme of finite type over
k
.
The
k
-unipotent radical
is the maximal smooth connected normal unipotent
k
-subgroup of
G
and we denote this by
\mathscr{R}_{u,k}(G)
.
The
geometric unipotent radical
is the
\bar{k}
-unipotent radical of the base change
G_{\bar{k}}
of
G
to
\bar{k}
. This is denoted by
\mathscr{R}_{u}(G_{\bar{k}})
.
We call a smooth connected
k
-group
G

reductive
if
\mathscr{R}_{u}(G_{\bar{k}})=1
and
pseudo-reductive
if
\mathscr{R}_{u,k}(G)=1
. From now on
G
will always be pseudo-reductive. We recall the definition and key properties of Weil restriction from
[
4
, A.5]
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
B\to B^{\prime}
be a finite flat map of noetherian rings, and
X^{\prime}
a quasi-projective
B^{\prime}
-scheme. The
Weil restriction
is a finite type
B
-scheme satisfying the universal property

\mathrm{R}_{B^{\prime}/B}(X^{\prime})(A)=X^{\prime}(B^{\prime}\otimes_{B}A)

for all
B
-algebras
A
.

The following proposition makes use of the natural map

(2.2)

\displaystyle q_{G^{\prime}}:\mathrm{R}_{k^{\prime}/k}(G^{\prime})_{k^{\prime}}\to G^{\prime},

which is induced by
k^{\prime}\otimes_{k}A\to A;a\otimes b\mapsto ab
for any
k^{\prime}
-algebra
A
.

Proposition 2.3

(
[
4
, Prop. A.5.11, Thm. 1.6.2]
,
[
1
, §2]
,
[
1
, Lem. 3.4]
)
.

Let
k^{\prime}/k
be a finite and purely inseparable field extension,
G^{\prime}
a non-trivial smooth connected
k^{\prime}
-group and
G=\mathrm{R}_{k^{\prime}/k}(G^{\prime})
.

(i)

The kernel of
q_{G^{\prime}}
is a smooth connected unipotent
k^{\prime}
-subgroup and thus it is contained in
\mathscr{R}_{u,k^{\prime}}(G_{k^{\prime}})
.

(ii)

If
G^{\prime}
is reductive over
k^{\prime}
, then the kernel of
q_{G^{\prime}}
has field of definition over k equal to
k^{\prime}\subseteq\bar{k}
. Thus the kernel
\ker(q_{G^{\prime}})
coincides with
\mathscr{R}_{u,k^{\prime}}(G_{k^{\prime}})
, which is a
k^{\prime}
-descent of
\mathscr{R}_{u}(G_{\bar{k}})
(i.e.
\mathscr{R}_{u,k^{\prime}}(G_{k^{\prime}})_{\bar{k}}\cong\mathscr{R}_{u}(G_{\bar{k}})
).

(iii)

If
H^{\prime}
is a reductive subgroup of
G^{\prime}
then the geometric unipotent radical of
\mathrm{R}_{k^{\prime}/k}(H^{\prime})
is a subgroup of the geometric unipotent radical of
\mathrm{R}_{k^{\prime}/k}(G^{\prime})
.

(iv)

If
f:G_{1}\to G_{2}
is an étale isogeny of reductive
k^{\prime}
-groups then
\mathrm{R}_{k^{\prime}/k}(f)_{\bar{k}}
induces an isomorphism of geometric unipotent radicals of the respective Weil restrictions.

We are interested in the following invariant of a unipotent
k
-group:

Definition 2.4
.

Let
k
be a field of characteristic
p
and
U
a unipotent
k
-group.
The exponent
e(U)
of
U
is the minimal
s
such that the
p^{s}
-power map on
U
factors through the trivial group.

Clearly, this definition is insensitive to base change. As in
[
1
, §4]
we may therefore assume
k
is separably closed for the remainder of the article.

When calculating with matrices, we make use of the fact that if
U(k)\subset U
is dense (e.g. if
k
is perfect
[
6
, Thm. 34.4]
), then a map factors through the trivial group if and only if it maps to the identity on
U(k)
.
Since
R=\mathscr{R}_{u}(G_{\bar{k}})
descends to
R^{\prime}:=\mathscr{R}_{u,k^{\prime}}(G_{k^{\prime}})
whose
k^{\prime}
-points are dense (by the fact that
k^{\prime}
is separably closed) we have
e(R)
is the smallest
s
such that
p^{s}
kills
R^{\prime}(k^{\prime})
.

Following
[
9
]
, let
\mathfrak{B}:=k^{\prime}\otimes_{k}k^{\prime}
. Then
\mathfrak{B}
is a local ring with maximal ideal

\mathfrak{m}=\langle 1\otimes x-x\otimes 1\mid x\in k^{\prime}\rangle=\ker(\phi),

where
\phi:k^{\prime}\otimes_{k}k^{\prime}\to k^{\prime};a\otimes b\mapsto ab
. Moreover,
\mathrm{R}_{k^{\prime}/k}(G^{\prime})_{k^{\prime}}
can be identified with
\mathrm{R}_{\mathfrak{B}/k^{\prime}}(G^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{B}})
and the
k^{\prime}
-points of the
k^{\prime}
-unipotent radical of the former is the kernel
G^{\prime}(\mathfrak{m})
of the map
G^{\prime}(\mathfrak{B})\to G^{\prime}(k)
induced by
\phi
. Suppose now
G^{\prime}=\GL_{r}
and
G=\mathrm{R}_{k^{\prime}/k}(G^{\prime})
. As the entries of any
M\in\Mat(r,\mathfrak{m})
are nilpotent elements of
\mathfrak{B}
, it follows that
I_{r}+M
has invertible determinant, hence is an element of
G^{\prime}(\mathfrak{m})
. Therefore

\mathscr{R}_{u,k}(\mathrm{R}_{k^{\prime}/k}(\GL_{r})_{k^{\prime}})(k^{\prime})=\{I_{r}+M\mid M\in\Mat(r,\mathfrak{m})\}.

As
I_{r}
and
M
commute, we have
A^{p^{s}}=I_{r}+M^{p^{s}}=I_{r}
is equivalent to
M^{p^{s}}=0
. Hence:

Lemma 2.5
.

The exponent of the geometric unipotent radical of
\mathrm{R}_{k^{\prime}/k}(\GL_{r})
is the smallest
s
such that
M^{p^{s}}=0
for all
M\in\Mat(r,\mathfrak{m})
.

It was observed in
[
1
, Lem. 4.1]
that as
\mathfrak{m}^{n}=0
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, one knows that
s
in the lemma above is bounded above by the minimal
t
such that
p^{t}\geq n
: the entries of
M^{p^{t}}
are homogeneous polynomials of degree
p^{t}
in elements of
\mathfrak{m}
and so must vanish.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
k^{\prime}/k^{\prime\prime}/k
be a tower of finite purely inseparable field extensions.

(i)

If
x\in k^{\prime}
then the minimal
s\in\mathbb{N}
such
x^{p^{s}}\in k^{\prime\prime}
is called
the exponent of
x
with respect to
k^{\prime\prime}
and denoted by
e_{k^{\prime\prime}}(x)
.

(ii)

We say that
x\in k^{\prime}
is
normal in
k^{\prime}/k^{\prime\prime}
if
e_{k^{\prime\prime}}(x)\geq e_{k^{\prime\prime}}(y)
for all
y\in k^{\prime}
.

(iii)

A sequence
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{l}
in
k^{\prime}
is called a
normal sequence
if for every
1\leq i\leq l
and
k_{i}=k[\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{i}]
we have that
\alpha_{i}
is normal in
k^{\prime}/k_{i-1}
, and
\alpha_{i}\notin k_{i-1}
.

(iv)

A normal sequence
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{l}
is called a
normal generating sequence of
k^{\prime}/k
if
k[\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{l}]=k^{\prime}
.

(v)

For a normal generating sequence
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{l}
of
k^{\prime}/k
we set
e_{i}=e_{k_{i-1}}(\alpha_{i})
. We call
e_{1},\ldots,e_{l}
a
sequence of exponents for
k^{\prime}/k

Note that if
e_{1},\ldots,e_{l}
is a sequence of exponents for
k^{\prime}/k
then we have
e_{1}\geq e_{2}\geq\ldots\geq e_{l}
due to
[
11
, Prop. 5]
. Crucially, by
loc. cit.
we also have

(2.7)

\alpha_{i}^{p^{e_{i}}}\in k(\alpha_{1}^{p^{e_{i}}},\ldots,\alpha_{i-1}^{p^{e_{i}}})\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq l.

In particular, as the
e_{i}
are decreasing,

(2.8)

(k^{\prime})^{p^{e_{i}}}\subseteq k(\alpha_{1}^{p^{e_{i}}},\ldots,\alpha_{i-1}^{p^{e_{i}}}).

Combining this with the main result of
[
10
]
, we get that the sequence of exponents is an invariant of the field extension
k^{\prime}/k
. More precisely:

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
k^{\prime}/k
be a finite purely inseparable field extension.
Suppose that
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{l}
and
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{l^{\prime}}
are normal generating sequences of
k^{\prime}/k
with sequences of exponents
e_{1},\ldots,e_{l}
and
e^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,e^{\prime}_{l^{\prime}}
.
Then
l=l^{\prime}
and
e_{i}=e^{\prime}_{i}
for
1\leq i<l
.

By the lemma, the numbers
\m(k^{\prime}/k)
,
\mr(k^{\prime}/k,r)
defined in the introduction are both invariants of
k^{\prime}/k
and
(k^{\prime}/k,r)
respectively. To relate these with
\mathfrak{m}
, we first prove:

Lemma 2.10
.

For a normal generating sequence
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{l}
of
k^{\prime}/k
, define
a_{i}:=1\otimes\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{i}\otimes 1\in\mathfrak{m}
. Then:

(i)

the ideal
\mathfrak{m}
is generated by the
a_{i}
over
k^{\prime}
;

(ii)

the
p^{e_{i}}
-power map takes
\mathfrak{m}
into the
k^{\prime}
-subspace
k^{\prime}[a_{1}^{p^{e_{i}}},\ldots,a_{i-1}^{p^{e_{i}}}]\cap\mathfrak{m}
.

Proof.

(i) Since
\mathfrak{m}
is a codimension
1
(left)
k^{\prime}
-subspace of
\mathfrak{B}
, it is a codimension
[k^{\prime}:k]

k
-subspace of
\mathfrak{B}
. Define
J=\{(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{l})\in\mathbb{Z}^{l}\mid 0\leq b_{i}\leq p^{e_{i}}-1\}
.
For
b=(b_{1},...,b_{l})\in J
we set
x^{b}=\prod_{i=1}^{l}\alpha_{i}^{b_{i}}
.
Then
\{x^{b}\}_{b\in J}
is a
k
-basis of
k^{\prime}
and
\{1\otimes x^{b}\}
is a
k^{\prime}
-basis of
\mathfrak{B}
. For
b\neq(0,\ldots,0)
, the elements
a_{b}:=1\otimes x^{b}-x^{b}\otimes 1=1\otimes x^{b}-x^{b}(1\otimes 1)
are therefore linearly independent, generating a codimension
1
subspace of
\mathfrak{B}
, which is contained in the codimension
1
kernel
\mathfrak{m}
of the multiplication map
x\otimes y\mapsto xy
; hence they form a
k^{\prime}
-basis of
\mathfrak{m}
.
We wish to show
a_{b}\in\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}:=\mathfrak{m}\cap k^{\prime}[a_{1},\ldots,a_{l}]
by induction on
\sum b_{i}
, the case
\sum b_{i}=1
being trivial.
So let
\sum b_{i}>1
. Without loss of generality, suppose
b_{1}>0
and let
b^{\prime}=b-(1,0,\ldots,0)
. Then inductively
a_{b^{\prime}}=1\otimes x^{b^{\prime}}-x^{b^{\prime}}\otimes 1\in\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}
. Now one checks directly that
a_{b}=x^{b^{\prime}}a_{1}+\alpha_{1}x^{b^{\prime}}-a_{b^{\prime}}\cdot a_{1}\in\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}
as required. (And it follows that
\mathfrak{m}=\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}
.)

(ii) Define
\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}:=k^{\prime}[a_{1}^{p^{e_{i}}},\ldots,a_{i-1}^{p^{e_{i}}}]\cap\mathfrak{m}
. From (i), we can express any element of
\mathfrak{m}
as a polynomial in
a_{1},\ldots,a_{l}
over
k^{\prime}
. Since
k^{\prime}
has characteristic

, the
p^{e_{i}}
-power map distributes over addition and so it suffices to check that
(a_{1}^{r_{1}}\cdots a_{l}^{r_{l}})^{p^{e_{i}}}\in\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}
for

. For this it suffices to check that
a_{j}^{p^{e_{i}}}\in\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}
for

. Now
a_{j}^{p^{e_{i}}}=(1\otimes\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{j}\otimes 1)^{p^{e_{i}}}=1\otimes\alpha_{j}^{p^{e_{i}}}-\alpha_{j}^{p^{e_{i}}}\otimes 1
and by (
2.8
) we can write
\alpha_{j}^{p^{e_{i}}}
as a

-polynomial

in
k[\alpha_{1}^{p^{e_{i}}},\ldots,\alpha_{i-1}^{p^{e_{i}}}]
with no constant term. But applying part (i) with

,
a_{j}^{p^{e_{i}}}
in place of

for

and

in place of

, we deduce that

is indeed a polynomial in the

with

as required.∎

We may now identify the minimal integer

such that
\mathfrak{m}^{n}=0
with the integer
\m(k^{\prime}/k)
. In fact we also prove something stronger:

Lemma 2.11
.

With notation as above, let
d\leq l
and suppose
m_{1},\ldots,m_{d}\in\mathfrak{m}
and
f_{1},\ldots,f_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.

If
\sum f_{i}\geq\mr(k^{\prime}/k,d)-d+1=\sum p^{e^{i}}-d+1
then
m_{1}^{f_{1}}\cdots m_{d}^{f_{d}}=0
.

In particular, the minimum
n
such that
\mathfrak{m}^{n}=0
is
\m(k^{\prime}/k)
.

Proof.

Assume for a contradiction that
m:=m_{1}^{f_{1}}\cdots m_{d}^{f_{d}}\neq 0
. We proceed by induction on
d
, observing that the product of any factors of
m
is also non-zero. It does no harm to assume that
f_{1}\geq\ldots\geq f_{d}
. If
d=1
, then
\mr(k^{\prime}/k,1)=p^{e_{1}}=p^{e}
and indeed
m=0
, a contradiction. Otherwise, let
c
be maximal such that
f_{c}\geq p^{e_{c}}
. This implies
\sum_{i=1}^{c}f_{i}\geq\mr(k^{\prime}/k,c)-c+1
so if
c<d
, then
m_{1}^{f_{1}}\cdots m_{c}^{f_{c}}
is zero by induction, a contradiction. Therefore
c=d
. Let
(q_{i},r_{i})
be the quotient and remainder when
p^{e_{d}}
is divided into
f_{i}
. As
f_{i}\geq f_{d}\geq p^{e_{d}}
we have each
q_{i}\geq 1
. Now

f_{1}+\ldots+f_{d}=p^{e_{d}}(q_{1}+\ldots+q_{d})+(r_{1}+\ldots+r_{d})\geq p^{e_{1}}+\ldots+p^{e_{d}}-d+1

and so

\displaystyle p^{e_{d}}(q_{1}+\ldots+q_{d})

\displaystyle\geq p^{e_{d}}(q_{1}+\ldots+q_{d-1}+1)

\displaystyle\geq p^{e_{1}}+\ldots+p^{e_{d}}-d+1-d(p^{e_{d}}-1)

\displaystyle=p^{e_{d}}(p^{e_{1}-e_{d}}+\ldots+p^{e_{d-1}-e_{d}}-d+1)+1.

Since the second expression above is a multiple of
p^{e_{d}}
, we have

q_{1}+\ldots+q_{d}\geq p^{e_{1}-e_{d}}+\ldots+p^{e_{d-1}-e_{d}}-(d-2).

Now as
q_{i}p^{e_{d}}\leq f_{i}
for each
i
, we have
m^{\prime}=m_{1}^{q_{1}p^{e_{d}}}\cdots m_{d}^{q_{d}p^{e_{d}}}\neq 0
. By Lemma
2.10
(ii), we may write each
m_{i}^{p^{e_{d}}}
as a polynomial in
k^{\prime}[a_{1}^{p^{e_{d}}},\ldots,a_{d-1}^{p^{e_{d}}}]
. After expanding
m^{\prime}
, any constituent monomial will be of the form
a_{1}^{\nu_{1}}\cdots a_{d-1}^{\nu_{d-1}}
where
\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}\nu_{i}=p^{e_{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}q_{i}\geq p^{e_{1}}+\ldots+p^{e_{d-1}}-p^{e_{d}}(d-2)
and each
\nu_{i}
is divisible by
p^{e_{d}}
. Furthermore, as
a_{d-1}^{p^{e_{d-1}}}
can be re-expressed in terms of the
a_{i}
with
i<d-1
, we may assume
\nu_{d-1}\leq p^{e_{d-1}}-p^{e_{d}}
. It follows that

\sum_{i=1}^{d-2}\nu_{i}\geq p^{e_{1}}+\ldots+p^{e_{d-2}}-p^{e_{d}}(d-3).

Continuing inductively,
\nu_{1}\geq p^{e_{1}}-p^{e_{d}}+1
. Thus
\nu_{1}\geq p^{e_{1}}
and hence all monomials in
m^{\prime}
are zero, a contradiction.

We now tackle the second statement. To see that
n\geq\m(k^{\prime}/k)
, observe that
a:=\prod_{i=1}^{l}a_{i}^{p^{e_{i}}-1}
is a product of
\m(k^{\prime}/k)-1
elements from
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{l}\}
. Expanding this product in terms of the
k
-basis
\{x^{b}\otimes x^{c}\mid b,c\in J\}
of
\mathfrak{B}
, the coefficient of
1\otimes x^{b}
where
b=(p^{e_{1}}-1,\ldots,p^{e_{l}}-1)
is easily seen to be
1
. It follows that
a\neq 0
.

For the upper bound, take
d=l
in the first part of the lemma, noting that
\m(k^{\prime}/k)=\mr(k^{\prime}/k,l)-l+1
.
∎

3.
Bounds in the case of the general linear group

In this section,
G^{\prime}=\GL_{r}
and
G=\mathrm{R}_{k^{\prime}/k}(G^{\prime})
, where
k^{\prime}/k
is purely inseparable of exponent
e
.
In
[
1
, Lem. 4.4]
it was shown that the exponent

of

is at most

, where

is chosen such that

.
Using the Cayley–Hamilton theorem in its full generality (see

, III.8.11]
) yields an improvement.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
(i).

Let

, as in Lemma
2.5
.
By the Cayley–Hamilton theorem, we have
\chi_{M}(M)=0
, where
\chi_{M}(\lambda)=\lambda^{r}+\sum_{t=0}^{r-1}f_{t}\lambda^{t}
is the characteristic polynomial of
M
with degree

.
Observe that the coefficient
f_{t}
is either zero or a homogeneous polynomial in the entries of
\mathfrak{m}
with degree

.
Since the matrices
M^{r-1},\ldots,M,I_{r}
all commute and

has characteristic

, we get

M^{rp^{e}}=-(f_{r-1}^{p^{e}}M^{(r-1)p^{e}}+\ldots+f_{1}^{p^{e}}M^{p^{e}}+f_{0}^{p^{e}}I_{r})=0,

since
f_{t}^{p^{e}}=0
for all
f_{t}\in\mathfrak{m}
.
Choosing

such that

we get

.
∎

Let

be the upper Borel subgroup, whose points are upper triangular matrices, and

its Weil restriction—a pseudo-Borel subgroup in the terminology of
[
5
, C.2]
. We now specify certain elements

with

to construct lower bounds on

which depend on

and the exponents

of a normal generating sequence.
Moreover, we calculate exactly the exponent of the intersection

.

Before we give the proof, we make some elementary remarks about powers of matrices.
If
A
is a commutative ring and

is the
r\times r
elementry matrix with
e_{i,j}=1
and zeros everywhere else, then we have
E_{i,j}E_{l,s}=E_{i,s}
if and only if
j=l
and
0
else. Write

for a general element of
\Mat(r,A)
.
Then after expansion, any non-zero term in the power

is of the form
a_{w}\cdot E_{i_{1},i_{n+1}}
where
a_{w}=a_{i_{1},i_{2}}\cdot a_{i_{2},i_{3}}\cdots a_{i_{l},i_{n+1}}
, and

.
Furthermore, the
(i,j)
-th entry of

is the sum over all

such that

and

. For
w\in\{1,\ldots,r\}^{n+1}
, say
n
is the
length
of

.
Now, if

is an upper triangular matrix, then

will be zero unless

for each

.
Hence the only

yielding non-zero

are non-decreasing sequences of integers from

, whose length is bounded above by

(Lemma

).

Proof of Theorem
1.1
(ii)-(iv).

We work inside

. There are two cases. Suppose we have
\sum_{i=r+1}^{l}(p^{e_{i}}-1)<r-1
. Then there is an integer

with

maximal such that
(p^{e_{q+1}}-1)+\ldots+(p^{e_{l}}-1)\geq q-1
. Define the matrix

as follows.

(i) Start with

;

(ii) From the top left corner, fill the first
q
spaces on the diagonal with
a_{1},\ldots,a_{q}
respectively;

(iii) Fill up the first

elements of the superdiagonal with
p^{e_{l}}-1
entries of
a_{l}
, then
p^{e_{l-1}}-1
entries of
a_{l-1}
and so on;

(iv) If one has now written down
p^{e_{q+1}}-1
entries

, then stop. Otherwise put
a_{q}
in the
q
th position of the superdiagonal. If, after this, there are only
p^{e_{q+1}}-1-\tau
entries

with
\tau\geq 1
on the superdiagonal, we set also the
(q+1)
st diagonal element equal to
a_{q}
.

We claim
M^{\m(k^{\prime}/k)-1}\neq 0
.
Take the sequence

w=(\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{p^{e_{1}}\text{ times}},\underbrace{2,\ldots,2}_{p^{e_{2}}\text{ times}},\ldots,\underbrace{q,\ldots,q}_{p^{e_{q}}\text{ times}},\underbrace{q+1,\ldots,q+1}_{\tau+1\text{ times}})

and observe that

.
A little thought shows that this is the unique sequence

of this length with
a_{w}\neq 0
starting at

and ending at

.
This proves the claim. Hence we conclude
e(R\cap B_{\bar{k}})\geq\Em(k^{\prime}/k)=\E(k^{\prime}/k,r)
. Since

by Lemmas
2.11
and
2.5
, we have

and so this case is done.

Now suppose
\sum_{i=r+1}^{l}(p^{e_{i}}-1)\geq r-1
. One constructs a similar

. Start as before with

. One fills the diagonal with

and the superdiagonal with any

elements from the list

.
This gives a matrix

such that

where

. Hence

.

We need to demonstrate the upper bound for this case. Take a matrix
M\in B^{\prime}(\mathfrak{m})
and any sequence

of length
\mr(k^{\prime}/k)
such that

in a product of

. Suppose the entries on the diagonal of

are

. Then

for non-negative integers

and

a product of

For part (iii) of the theorem, we note that any lower bound for

is automatically one for

. For part (iv) we just observe that

by Lemmas

.∎

4.
Extended example of
\SL_{2}
and
\PGL_{2}

In this section we give a complete description of
e(R)
in case
G^{\prime}=\SL_{2},\PGL_{2}
or
\GL_{2}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
k^{\prime}/k
be purely inseparable of characteristic
p
and exponent
e
.
Let
G
be
\mathrm{R}_{k^{\prime}/k}(\GL_{2})
,
\mathrm{R}_{k^{\prime}/k}(\PGL_{2})
or
\mathrm{R}_{k^{\prime}/k}(\SL_{2})
, the last of these only if
p\neq 2
.
Then
e(R)
is
e
if
k^{\prime}/k
is simple and
e+1
otherwise.

Proof.

If
G^{\prime}
is
\GL_{2}
, then Theorem
1.1
(iv) gives the exponent
e(R)=e
for
k^{\prime}/k
simple of exponent
e
, and at least
e+1
when
k^{\prime}/k
is non-simple. However, by Theorem
1.1
(i) we have
e(R)
at most
e+1
.
As the map
\GL_{2}\to\PGL_{2}
is smooth, and
\SL_{2}\subset\GL_{2}
, we immediately obtain
e
or
e+1
respectively as upper bounds for both the cases
G^{\prime}=\SL_{2}
and
G^{\prime}=\PGL_{2}
.
In fact, the matrices constructed in the proof of Theorem
1.1
(ii)-(iv) and their relevant powers are clearly non-zero in their image in
\PGL_{2}(\mathfrak{B})
and so the exponents of the radicals in case
G^{\prime}=\GL_{2}
and
G^{\prime}=\PGL_{2}
coincide.
Now by Proposition
2.3
(iv) these also coincide for
\SL_{2}
outside of characteristic
2
, since the isogeny
f:\SL_{2}\to\PGL_{2}
is smooth.
∎

We see some different behaviour for
\SL_{2}
in characteristic
2
; in particular, the exponents of
R_{u}(G_{\bar{k}})\cap R_{u}(B_{\bar{k}})
and
R_{u}(G_{\bar{k}})
do not coincide.

Recall that a unital ring
R
has characteristic
2
if the equation
1+1=0
holds in
R
.
We prove a general formula for
2^{s}
-th powers of matrices over rings of characteristic
2
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring of characteristic
2
and
M=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}
with
a,b,c,d\in R
. Then for all
s\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we have

\displaystyle M^{2^{s}}=\begin{pmatrix}a^{2^{s}}+\sum_{i=0}^{s-1}b^{2^{i}}c^{2^{i}}t^{2^{s}-2^{i+1}}&bt^{2^{s}-1}\\
ct^{2^{s}-1}&d^{2^{s}}+\sum_{i=0}^{s-1}b^{2^{i}}c^{2^{i}}t^{2^{s}-2^{i+1}}\end{pmatrix},

where
t=a+d
.

Proof.

We argue by induction on
s
. For
s=0
, we just get
M
on both sides.
So assume the result for
s
and set
l_{s}=\sum_{i=0}^{s-1}b^{2^{i}}c^{2^{i}}t^{2^{s}-2^{i+1}}
.
We note that

\displaystyle l_{s}^{2}+bct^{2^{s+1}-2}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}b^{2^{i}}c^{2^{i}}t^{2^{s+1}-2^{i+1}}+bct^{2^{s+1}-2}=l_{s+1}

holds. And so we get

\displaystyle M^{2^{s+1}}=(M^{2^{s}})^{2}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}a^{2^{s+1}}+l_{s}^{2}+bct^{2^{s+1}-2}&bt^{2^{s}-1}(a^{2^{s}}+d^{2^{s}})\\
ct^{2^{s}-1}(a^{2^{s}}+d^{2^{s}})&d^{2^{s+1}}+l_{s}^{2}+bct^{2^{s+1}-2}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}a^{2^{s+1}}+l_{s+1}&bt^{2^{s+1}-1}\\
ct^{2^{s+1}-1}&d^{2^{s+1}}+l_{s+1}\end{pmatrix}.\qed

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
G^{\prime}=\SL_{2}
,
p=2
and suppose
k^{\prime}/k
has normal generating sequence
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{l}
with exponents
e_{1}\geq e_{2}\geq\ldots\geq e_{l}
. Let
B^{\prime}
be an upper Borel subgroup of
G^{\prime}
and
B=\mathrm{R}_{k^{\prime}/k}(B^{\prime})
a pseudo-Borel.

(i) We have
e(R\cap B_{\bar{k}})=e
;

(ii) We have
e(R)=\begin{cases}e+1\text{ if }e_{1}=e_{2}\\
e\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proof.

We first calculate the exponent of
B^{\prime}(\mathfrak{m})=\ker B^{\prime}(\mathfrak{B})\to B^{\prime}(k^{\prime})
. A general element is
I+M
with
M=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
0&d\\
\end{pmatrix}
, where
a,b,d\in\mathfrak{m}=\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{l}\rangle
, satisfying
a+d+ad=0
from the condition
\det(I+M)=1
. As
a\in\mathfrak{m}
,
1+a
is invertible in
\mathfrak{B}
, and so it follows that
d=a(1+a)^{-1}
and
a+d=ad=a^{2}(1+a)^{-1}
. By Lemma
4.2
, the
2^{s}
-th power of this matrix is

\begin{pmatrix}a^{2^{s}}&b(a^{2}(1+a)^{-1})^{2^{s}-1}\\
0&d^{2^{s}}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Choosing for example
a=a_{1}=1\otimes\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{1}\otimes 1
, one sees the minimal
s
for which
M^{2^{s}}=0
is at least
e_{1}=e
and also that if
s\geq e
then the diagonal entries vanish. For
s=e
, the off-diagonal entry is a multiple of
(a^{2})^{2^{e}-1}
, which has at least
2^{e}
factors of
a
, hence is also. So
e(R\cap B_{\bar{k}})=e
, proving (i).

For (ii), a general element of
G^{\prime}(\mathfrak{m})
is a matrix
I+M
where
M=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\\
\end{pmatrix}
, satisfying
a+d+ad+bc=0
, since
\det(I+M)=1
.
If
l=1
, (i.e.
k^{\prime}/k
is simple of exponent
e=e_{1}
), then it is clear that
e(R)=e
. Suppose
e_{2}\leq e_{1}-1=e-1
. We may write
d=(1+a)^{-1}(a+bc)
, hence
t=a+d=(1+a)^{-1}(a^{2}+bc)
. Therefore
t^{2^{e-1}}=(1+a)^{-2^{e-1}}(a^{2^{e}}+b^{2^{e-1}}c^{2^{e-1}})=(1+a)^{-2^{e-1}}(b^{2^{e-1}}c^{2^{e-1}})
. Now by Lemma
2.10
we have
m^{2^{e-1}}\in\langle a_{1}^{2^{e-1}}\rangle\subseteq\mathfrak{m}
for any
m\in\mathfrak{m}
. It follows that
b^{2^{e-1}}c^{2^{e-1}}
has a factor of
a_{1}^{2^{e-1}}\cdot a_{1}^{2^{e-1}}=a_{1}^{2^{e}}=0
. By the same token,
t^{2^{e-1}}=0
and hence, using the lemma,
M^{2^{e}}=0
. Therefore
e(R)=e
as required.

Now suppose
e_{1}=e_{2}
. Since
G\subseteq\mathrm{R}_{k^{\prime}/k}(\GL_{2})
we have
e(R)\leq e+1
. Set
b=a_{1}
,
c=a_{2}
,
d=0
and
a=a_{1}a_{2}
. Then

M^{2^{e}}=\begin{pmatrix}(a_{1}a_{2})^{2^{e}}+\sum_{i=1}^{e-1}(a_{1}a_{2})^{2^{i}+2^{e}-2^{i+1}}&a_{1}(a_{1}a_{2})^{2^{e}-1}\\
a_{2}(a_{1}a_{2})^{2^{e}-1}&\sum_{i=1}^{e-1}(a_{1}a_{2})^{2^{i}+2^{e}-2^{i+1}}\\
\end{pmatrix}

The off-diagonal entries are zero, but
\sum_{i=1}^{e-1}(a_{1}a_{2})^{2^{i}+2^{e}-2^{i+1}}=\sum_{i=1}^{e}(a_{1}a_{2})^{2^{e}-2^{i}}
and the
i=e-1
entry is
(a_{1}a_{2})^{2^{e-1}}
. Provided
e_{2}=e_{1}
, this element is non-zero by the same argument as in the proof of Lemma
2.11
—in a

-basis of
\mathfrak{B}
, the coefficient of
1\otimes\alpha_{1}^{2^{e-1}}\alpha_{2}^{2^{e-1}}
in
(a_{1}a_{2})^{2^{e-1}}
is evidently
1
. It follows that
M^{2^{e}}\neq 0
and
e(R)=e+1
.
∎

A_{8}
maximal rank subgroups, evident from the Borel–de-Siebenthal algorithm. Hence each contains a skewed
\GL_{7}
and
\GL_{8}
subgroup
L
respectively, satisfying (i) as required. By

8

, any Levi subgroup
L
of
G_{2}
is isomorphic to
\GL_{2}
(rather than
\SL_{2}\times\Gm
, for example), hence satisfies (i). If
G^{\prime}
is of type
F_{4}
, then we take
L
to be an
A_{3}
Levi subgroup of the
\Spin_{9}
subgroup of
G^{\prime}
. Now
L
admits an étale isogeny with the
\GL_{4}
Levi subgroup of
\SO_{9}\cong\Spin_{9}/Z(\Spin_{9})
if
p\neq 2
; so
L
satisfies (ii) in that case, and if
p=2
then
L
is listed in (iii). It remains to deal with the case
G^{\prime}=E_{6}
. Using Bourbaki notation
[
3
]
, we take
L
to be the

-Levi subgroup corresponding to the nodes
\{1,3,4,5,6\}
of the Dynkin diagram explicitly. Assume first
G^{\prime}
is simply connected. Then
\mathscr{D}(L)
is simply connected. Since
L
centralises the
\SL_{2}

root groups, where

is the longest root, the central torus

is the image of the cocharacter

h_{\tilde{\alpha}}(t)=h_{\alpha_{1}}(t)h_{\alpha_{2}}(t^{2})h_{\alpha_{3}}(t^{2})h_{\alpha_{4}}(t^{3})h_{\alpha_{5}}(t^{2})h_{\alpha_{6}}(t)

G/Z(G^{\prime})

We prove the last statement. Suppose (ii) holds. Then we note that if
\pi:A\to B
is an étale central isogeny of reductive groups, then
\mathrm{R}_{k^{\prime}/k}(A)\to\mathrm{R}_{k^{\prime}/k}(B)
is an isogeny whose kernel has trivial intersection with the unipotent radical. As in Proposition
2.3
(iv), it follows that the induced map
\mathscr{R}_{u}(\mathrm{R}_{k^{\prime}/k}(A)_{\bar{k}})\to\mathscr{R}_{u}(\mathrm{R}_{k^{\prime}/k}(B)_{\bar{k}})
is an isomorphism as required. If (i) holds, then
L

\GL_{r}

\mathscr{R}_{u}(\mathrm{R}_{k^{\prime}/k}(L\times\Gm)_{\bar{k}})\cong\mathscr{R}_{u}(\mathrm{R}_{k^{\prime}/k}(L)_{\bar{k}})\times\mathscr{R}_{u}(\mathrm{R}_{k^{\prime}/k}(\Gm)_{\bar{k}})
; since
L

, the exponent of the product is equal to that of the first factor,
e(R)
. The same argument with
\GL_{r}\times\Gm

implies the claim.
∎

Proof of Corollary
1.3
.

With the hypotheses on
p
, by Lemma
5.1
we get a reductive subgroup

of

, with

By Proposition
2.3
(iii) it follows that

\sum_{i=r+1}^{l}(p^{e_{i}}-1)<r-1
