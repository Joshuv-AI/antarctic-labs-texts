Abstract
The squeezed cross-bispectrum B κ , Ly ​ α B_{\kappa,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha} between the gravitational lensing in the Cosmic Microwave Background and the 1D Ly α \alpha forest power spectrum can constrain bias parameters and break degeneracies between σ 8 \sigma_{8} and other cosmological parameters. We detect B κ , Ly ​ α B_{\kappa,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha} with 4.8 ​ σ 4.8\sigma significance at an effective redshift z eff = 2.4 z_{\mathrm{eff}}=2.4 using Planck PR3 lensing map and over 280,000 quasar spectra from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument’s first-year data. We test our measurement against metal contamination and foregrounds such as Galactic extinction and clusters of galaxies by deprojecting the thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect. We compare our results to a tree-level perturbation theory calculation and find reasonable agreement between the model and measurement.



I
Introduction

The observed large-scale structure of the universe was largely produced by the force of gravity acting on
initial Gaussian density fluctuations. This non-linear evolution couples the originally linearly independent modes of the density field over time, and gives rise to a non-zero bispectrum and higher-order correlations.
In the position-dependent power spectrum picture, the gravitational collapse will be faster in overdense regions due to the presence of more matter, which will then make the matter field clumpier and enhance the local small-scale power spectrum
(
Li et al. 2014
;
Chiang et al. 2014
)
.

The Ly
\alpha
forest and Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) lensing maps provide a rare opportunity to observe this effect as one needs a power spectrum and an accompanying large-scale density mode estimate at multiple locations in the Universe. The Ly
\alpha
forest technique maps out the matter field using the absorption lines in the quasar spectrum. The 1D power spectrum (
P_{\mathrm{1D}}
) of the Ly
\alpha
forest is most sensitive to small-scale physics and has been precisely measured in the redshift range of
2\lesssim z\lesssim 4

(
Palanque-Delabrouille et al. 2013
;
Walther et al. 2017
;
Karaçaylı et al. 2022
;
Ravoux et al. 2023
;
Karaçaylı et al. 2024
)
. The paths of the CMB photons are distorted by the intervening matter due to gravitational lensing, which was first detected in cross-correlations
(
Smith et al. 2007
;
Hirata et al. 2008
)
. The lensing convergence
\kappa
constructed from CMB temperature and polarization anisotropies corresponds to an integrated density field along the line of sight weighted by the lensing kernel
W_{\kappa}(\chi)

(
Bartelmann and Maturi 2017
)
. This kernel is broad, but peaks at
z\approx 2
, which overlaps with the redshift range of Ly
\alpha

P_{\mathrm{1D}}
measurements.

The enhancement of
P_{\mathrm{1D}}
due to large-scale density fluctuations was proposed by
Zaldarriaga et al. 2001
. The first measurement of the CMB lensing and
P_{\mathrm{1D}}
cross-bispectrum
B_{\kappa,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
was reported by
Doux et al. 2016
using about 87,000 SDSS-III/BOSS spectra
(
Dawson et al. 2013
)
at
5\sigma
. In this work, we measure
B_{\kappa,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
using over 280,000 quasar spectra from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument’s
(
Levi et al. 2013
;
DESI Collaboration et al. 2016a
, DESI,)
first-year quasar sample. These quasar spectra are part of the future Data Release 1
(
DESI Collaboration et al. 2025
, DR1,)
. Key DESI science papers using DR1 already include the highest precision measurements of baryon acoustic oscillations (BAO) from galaxies and quasars
(
DESI Collaboration et al. 2024a
)
, and from the Ly
\alpha
forest
(
DESI Collaboration et al. 2024b
)
, and the cosmological results from these BAO measurements
(
DESI Collaboration et al. 2024c
)
. To measure
P_{\mathrm{1D}}
, we apply the optimal estimator method
(
McDonald et al. 2006
;
Karaçaylı et al. 2024
)
, which is robust against strong sky emission lines, defective CCD pixels, and low-signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) spectra. This estimator naturally provides a covariance matrix based on the large-scale correlations and the wavelength-dependent pipeline noise for each quasar and enables the optimal weighting for the measurement of
B_{\kappa,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
.

Notable similar analyses include cross-correlations between the amplitude of Ly
\alpha
forest flux decrements and
\kappa

(
Vallinotto et al. 2009
;
Vallinotto et al. 2011
)
, the galaxy-galaxy-
\kappa
bispectrum with a
>20\sigma
detection
(
Farren et al. 2023
)
, and Ly
\alpha
forest and CMB temperature cross-correlations to study the Sunyaev-Zel’dovich effect
(
Croft et al. 2006
)
.

The outline of the paper is as follows. We overview DESI quasar spectra and Planck lensing data in Section
II
. We present the quasar continuum fitting algorithm,
B_{\kappa,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
estimation, and measurement results in Section
III
.
We develop a tree-level perturbation theory for
B_{\kappa,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
, and present the best-fit results in Section
IV
. We discuss measurement and modeling challenges in Section
V
, and finally summarize in Section
VI
.

II
Data

II.1
DESI quasar spectra

The DESI collaboration began a five-year survey in May 2021 to advance the understanding of the nature of dark energy through the most precise clustering measurements of galaxies, quasars, and the Ly
\alpha
forest ever obtained. DESI is mounted on the 4 m Mayall telescope and can obtain 5000 spectra in each observation
(
DESI Collaboration et al. 2016b
;
Silber et al. 2023
)
. It has ten, identical spectrographs that are in a climate-controlled enclosure to minimize instrumental systematic errors
(
DESI Collaboration et al. 2022
;
Guy et al. 2023
)
. DESI target selection was based on the photometry from the Legacy Imaging Surveys
(
Dey et al. 2019
)
and the Wide-field Infrared Explorer
(
Wright et al. 2010
)
, and is described in detail in
Myers et al. 2023
. The collaboration refined the target selection algorithms during the Survey Validation
(
DESI Collaboration et al. 2024d
)
period in early 2021 with a significant visual inspection effort
(
Alexander et al. 2023
)
. The preliminary quasar target sample is presented in
Yèche et al. 2020
and the final quasar target selection in
Chaussidon et al. 2023
.

We use the DR1 quasar observations in this analysis. This sample has over 1.5 million quasars observed between December 2020 and June 2022
(
Ross et al. 2024
)
. Nearly 450,000 of these quasars are at
z>2.1
, and therefore the DESI spectra include the Ly
\alpha
forest region. At the end of its five-year mission, DESI is expected to collect approximately 800,000 Ly
\alpha
quasars (
z>2.1
) and have twice as much exposure time as DR1 per quasar spectrum on average, which could improve the
B_{\kappa,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
covariance matrix by a factor of 3.5.

Broad absorption line (BAL) features are identified using an algorithm similar to the one presented by
Guo and Martini 2019
, except that it does not use the Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) classifier. A detailed study of these features will be presented by
Martini et al. 2024
. 18.5% of our DR1 quasars have BAL features. We mask the wavelength ranges of S
iv
, P
v
, C
iii
, Ly
\alpha
, N
v
, and Si
iv
ions based on the velocity ranges of the absorption troughs observed for the C
iv
BAL features outside of the forest region.

Damped Ly
\alpha
systems (DLA) are identified using a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)
(
Ho et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2022
)
. We mask all DLAs (
\log N_{\mathrm{HI}}>20.3
) except the ones with confidence level less than 0.3 in quasars with
\overline{\mathrm{SNR}}<3
, where the average signal-to-noise ratio
\overline{\mathrm{SNR}}
is calculated between 1420–1480 Å in quasar’s rest frame
(
Karaçaylı et al. 2024
)
. There are 88,858 DLAs observed in 73,513 quasars in our catalog. We mask the regions where the model DLA transmission profile is below 80% and correct the damping wings at larger transmission values based on the same model profile
(
Karaçaylı et al. 2024
)
. These systems and also neutral hydrogen systems with
\log N_{H\textsc{i}}\gtrsim 19
are expected to be correlated with CMB lensing, which we further address in Section
III
.

III
Measurement

III.1
Continuum fitting

We use the standardized continuum fitting algorithm that was developed over the years and has been applied to both
\xi_{\mathrm{3D}}
and
P_{\mathrm{1D}}
measurements
(
du Mas des Bourboux et al. 2020
;
Karaçaylı et al. 2024
)
. We summarize the algorithm below and refer the reader to
du Mas des Bourboux et al. 2020
,
Ramírez-Pérez et al. 2024
and
Karaçaylı et al. 2024
for a detailed description.

In this continuum fitting framework, the definition of the quasar continuum absorbs the mean transmission of the IGM
\overline{F}(z)
, such that the quasar “continuum”
\overline{F}C_{q}(\lambda_{\mathrm{RF}})
is given by

\displaystyle\overline{F}C_{q}(\lambda_{\mathrm{RF}})

\displaystyle=\overline{C}(\lambda_{\mathrm{RF}})\left(a_{q}+b_{q}\Lambda\right)

(1)

\displaystyle\Lambda

\displaystyle=\frac{\log\lambda_{\mathrm{RF}}-\log\lambda_{\mathrm{RF}}^{(1)}}{\log\lambda_{\mathrm{RF}}^{(2)}-\log\lambda_{\mathrm{RF}}^{(1)}},

(2)

where
\lambda_{\mathrm{RF}}
is the wavelength in the quasar’s rest frame,
\lambda_{\mathrm{RF}}^{(1,2)}
are the minimum and maximum wavelengths considered in the calculation,
\overline{C}(\lambda_{\mathrm{RF}})
is the global mean continuum, and finally
a_{q}
and
b_{q}
are two quasar diversity parameters. Note that these parameters do not only fit for intrinsic quasar diversity such as brightness, but also for the IGM mean transmission. Given these definitions, transmitted flux fluctuations are given by

\delta_{F}^{(q)}(\lambda)=\frac{f_{q}(\lambda)}{\overline{F}C_{q}(\lambda_{\mathrm{RF}})}-1,

(3)

where
\lambda=(1+z_{q})\lambda_{\mathrm{RF}}
is the observed wavelength and
f_{q}(\lambda)
is the observed flux.

This continuum fitting algorithm assigns each pixel a variance
\sigma_{q}^{2}(\lambda)
:

\sigma_{q}^{2}(\lambda)=\eta(\lambda)\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{pipe}}(\lambda)+\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{LSS}}(\lambda)(\overline{F}C_{q})^{2}(\lambda),

(4)

based on the observed variance statistics of
\delta_{F}
, which includes a pipeline noise correction
\eta(\lambda)
term as a scaling of the pipeline variance estimates and an additive large-scale structure variance term
\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{LSS}}(\lambda)
. In the Ly
\alpha
forest region of the quasar spectrum, the
\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{LSS}}
term is dominated by actual large-scale fluctuations. However, outside that region, it mostly reflects the additive errors made by the pipeline in the noise estimation since large-scale fluctuations due to metal absorption contribute little to this term.

In
Karaçaylı et al. 2024
, we found that the noise calibration errors originate at the CCD level, and we proposed data splits based on spectrograph and CCD amplifier location to mitigate these errors. Even though the cross-correlation signal is not biased due to noise calibration errors, a noise recalibration step moderately improves the significance of our cross-correlation detection. Regular analyses use the spectra in
healpix
grouping in which the multiple, different exposures of the same object are co-added. To calculate and propagate this CCD-amplifier-dependent correction, we use the
tile
grouping of DESI observations, where a tile is a fixed pointing of the telescope with fixed fiber assignments to specific targets
(
DESI Collaboration et al. 2023
)
. This grouping guarantees that the same quasar will be observed by the same spectrograph at the same (approximate) location on the CCD if it is observed in the same tile. We split the data into 20 subsets based on spectrograph and CCD amplifier region, and calculate the
\eta
and
\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{LSS}}
values between 1600–1800 Å in the quasar’s rest frame for each subset. This range excludes the dominant Si
iv
and C
iv
systems, but is still affected by weak Mg
ii
absorption.

We then perform the continuum fitting for each subset of quasar spectra using
qsonic
2
2

2

https://qsonic.readthedocs.io/en/stable/

(
Karaçaylı 2024
)
while correcting each CCD amplifier region’s noise estimates using the
\eta
and
\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{LSS}}
values obtained above. We define the Ly
\alpha
forest region to be between 1045 Å
<\lambda_{\mathrm{RF}}<
1185 Å, and a sideband region to be 1268 Å
<\lambda_{\mathrm{RF}}<
1380 Å. We use this sideband region to quantify the possible metal contributions to the cross-correlation. We limit the observed wavelength coverage to 3600 Å
<\lambda<
5350 Å, which only resides in the blue channel. The wavelengths contaminated by sky lines are naturally down-weighted by the pipeline, but we mask certain particularly strong lines due to difficulties in modeling
3
3

3

https://github.com/corentinravoux/p1desi/blob/main/etc/skylines/list_mask_p1d_DESI_EDR.txt
. For the Ly
\alpha
forest region only, we use the fiducial mean flux of
Becker et al. 2013
in
qsonic
to alleviate the coupling between
\overline{F}
and quasar diversity parameters.

We select quasars with an average SNR greater than one at wavelengths greater than the Ly
\alpha
emission line of the quasar, and with an average SNR greater than 0.3 in the forest region. After these steps, we have 299,781 quasars that satisfy the SNR cuts and that produce valid continuum fits. We then use the 278,098 quasars that overlap with the Planck lensing map.

Since the quadratic estimator is essentially a weighted average, low-SNR spectra are naturally down-weighted, so these cuts aim to remove possibly non-quasar objects from our input. For example, an SNR cut of 2 in the forest region keeps the top 34% of spectra, but increases the covariance matrix by about only 20%.

III.2
Power spectrum and cross bispectrum estimation

The quadratic maximum likelihood estimator (QMLE) gives a power spectrum
\bm{p}_{q}
and its covariance matrix
\mathbf{C}_{q}
for each quasar
q

Karaçaylı et al. 2020
;
Karaçaylı et al. 2024
. This covariance matrix
\mathbf{C}_{q}
includes contributions from the pipeline noise estimates and the signal contribution based on a fiducial power spectrum (see Appendix
A
for a summary). Using these matrices as inverse weights, we build the following bispectrum estimator:

\hat{B}_{a}=N_{ab}\sum_{q}W^{q}_{bc}\left(p^{q}_{c}-\hat{\overline{{p}}}_{c}\right)\left(\kappa^{q}-\hat{\overline{{\kappa}}}_{b}\right),

(5)

where indices
a,b,c
correspond to
(z,k)
bins;
b,c
are summed over per Einstein notation; and weights are given by
\mathbf{W}_{q}=\mathbf{C}_{\mathrm{tot}}\mathbf{C}^{-1}_{q}
and
\mathbf{C}_{\mathrm{tot}}\equiv\sum_{q}\mathbf{C}_{q}
such that
\sum_{q}\mathbf{W}_{q}=\mathbf{I}
. The mean power spectrum and
\kappa
are estimated using these weights:

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{\overline{{p}}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{q}\textbf{W}_{q}\bm{p}_{q},

(6)

\displaystyle\hat{\overline{{\kappa}}}_{a}

\displaystyle=\sum_{q}\kappa_{q}\sum_{b}W^{q}_{ab}.

(7)

The sample bias correction term
\mathbf{N}
is given by

N^{-1}_{ab}=\delta^{K}_{ab}-\sum_{q}\left(\mathbf{W}^{2}_{q}\right)_{ab}.

(8)

We provide a derivation of the sample bias correction in Appendix
B

We measure
P_{\mathrm{1D}}
in 35 linearly-spaced
k
bins with
\Delta k=6\times 10^{-4}~
s km
-1
and in 4 redshift bins with
\Delta z=0.4
starting at
z=2.0
. Unfortunately, the detection significance in each
z
bin is low, so we average all
z
bins using the total Fisher matrix (
\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{tot}}=\mathbf{C}^{-1}_{\mathrm{tot}}
) to improve our measurement. The redshift-averaged estimates are given by a linear operation
\hat{\bar{B}}_{k}=T_{ka}\hat{B}_{a}
, where

T_{ka}\propto\sum_{z}F^{\mathrm{tot}}_{(z,k),a},

(9)

such that each row of
T
is normalized,
\sum_{a}T_{ka}=1
. Note that this choice correctly takes the correlations between redshift bins into account in averaging.

The estimator in Eq.
5
can be straightforwardly extended to
\theta
separations between
P_{\mathrm{1D}}
and
\kappa
by calculating
\kappa_{q}
as a mean inside the ring around the quasar for an angular width
\Delta\theta
. The resulting 2D bispectrum
\zeta(k,\theta)
is in Fourier space in the radial direction and in real space in the angular direction. Since the Wiener filter suppresses angular fluctuations below
\theta<0.45^{\circ}

(L>400)
, it means that
B_{\kappa,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}\approx\zeta(k,\theta\lesssim 0.45^{\circ})
and also
\zeta(k,\theta)
will be highly correlated between angular bins separated by the same angle. Furthermore,
\theta=0.45^{\circ}
corresponds to
r_{\perp}=31~
Mpc
h^{-1}
separation, and
\zeta(k,\theta)
decreases to non-detectable levels by
r_{\perp}=70~
Mpc
h^{-1}
. To observe the angular dependence more finely, we use slightly narrower
\theta
bins that are linearly-spaced and centered at
0.2^{\circ},0.4^{\circ},0.6^{\circ}
, and
0.8^{\circ}
with
\Delta\theta=0.2^{\circ}
.

We estimate the covariance matrix using the non-parametric bootstrap method
(
Efron 1979
)
. To account for the angular correlations, we group
B_{\kappa,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
measurements into continuous regions on the sky using HEALPix pixelization scheme
(
Górski et al. 2005
)
. DESI organizes spectra using
N_{\mathrm{side}}=64
, which corresponds to an angular resolution of
0.92^{\circ}
. This is larger than the Wiener filter scale, and captures the large angular scale correlations in bootstrap realizations. There are 12,803 healpixels. We randomly assign an observation frequency for each healpix based on a Poisson distribution with a mean of one
(
Hanley and MacGibbon 2006
;
Chamandy et al. 2012
)
. Using the healpix number as the seed in random number generation generates correlated bootstrap realization between angular bins. To speed up these repeated runs, we do not recalculate the sample bias correction term, and use the original sample’s value for each realization. We calculate the covariance matrix with 50,000 realizations.

could be attributed to several possible absorption sources besides the diffuse intergalactic medium gas. These sources are high-column density (HCD) systems and metals. To isolate the Ly
\alpha
forest contribution, we further refine our bispectrum measurement using
P_{\mathrm{1D}}
measurement techniques. We note that
Doux et al. 2016
has no such refinements, therefore
4.8\sigma
remains the comparable quantity between the two studies.

First, the continuum marginalization affects the largest scales in the bispectrum, similar to
P_{\mathrm{1D}}
. These modes are further contaminated by HCDs, which include unidentified DLAs and any neutral hydrogen system with
\log N_{H\textsc{i}}\gtrsim 19
. These effects add large power to small
k
bins and strongly correlate them, as seen in Figures
1
and
2
. We remove the first two
k
bins to be conservative for these reasons. This has a relatively minor effect of reducing the detection significance to
4.5\sigma
.

Second, we estimate the metal contamination in the cross-correlation by estimating the bispectrum in the sideband (SB) region. We find that all the
k
modes are highly correlated due to the weak nature of the signal, correlated fluctuations sourced by systematics in this region, and signal originating from line profiles
(
Karaçaylı et al. 2023
)
. Hence, we detect a metal-
\kappa
cross-correlation at a weaker significance (around
1.6\sigma
). We subtract this
\kappa-
SB bispectrum from the
\kappa-
Ly
\alpha
bispectrum to statistically remove the metal contamination (orange line in Figure
1
), and add the bootstrap covariance matrices such that
\mathbf{C}_{\mathrm{new}}=\mathbf{C}_{\mathrm{Lya}}+\mathbf{C}_{\mathrm{SB}}
. This step individually reduces the detection significance to
3.0\sigma
. Further removing the first two
k
bins reduces our detection significance to
2.7\sigma
.

We emphasize that that detection of absoption
-\kappa
correlation remains
4.8\sigma
, but allowing for metal and HCD contributions means detection of Ly
\alpha
alone is
2.7\sigma
.

IV
Theory

In this section, we present a standard perturbation theory framework for
B_{\kappa,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
, evaluate it at the tree level, and provide best-fit values for the Ly
\alpha
forest parameters. We also revisit and correctly incorporate the Wiener filter of the CMB lensing map into the position-dependent formalism.

An integral of the density field in the line of sight gives the lensing convergence
\kappa
:

\displaystyle\kappa=

\displaystyle\int W_{\kappa}(\chi)\delta_{m}(\chi)\mathrm{d}\chi,

(10)

\displaystyle W_{\kappa}(\chi)=

\displaystyle\frac{3H_{0}^{2}\Omega_{m,0}}{2c^{2}}\frac{(\chi_{\mathrm{CMB}}-\chi)\chi}{a\chi_{\mathrm{CMB}}},

(11)

where
\chi
is the comoving distance and
\chi_{\mathrm{CMB}}
is the comoving distance to the CMB source plane at
z_{\mathrm{CMB}}=1100

Bartelmann and Maturi 2017
. To obtain the cross-correlations between
P_{\mathrm{1D}}
and
\kappa
at redshift
z_{*}
, we employ the Limber approximation and limit the density field contributions to
\kappa
to the redshift range such that the lensing kernel is approximately constant
W_{\kappa}(z)\approx W_{\kappa}(z_{*})
(see Appendix
C
for the expressions without the Limber approximation).

\kappa(\bm{x})=W_{\kappa}(z_{*})\int\mathrm{d}\chi\,\delta_{m}(\chi,\bm{x}),

(12)

where
\bm{x}
is the 2D vector perpendicular to the line of sight. Wiener filtering smoothes the field on this surface:

\delta^{\mathrm{WF}}_{m}(\chi,\bm{x})=\int\frac{d^{2}\bm{p}_{\bot}}{(2\pi)^{2}}e^{i\bm{p}_{\bot}\cdot\bm{x}}W_{\mathrm{WF}}(p_{\bot}\chi_{*})\tilde{\delta}_{m}(\chi,\bm{p}_{\bot}),

(13)

where we used the flat-sky approximation
l=p_{\bot}\chi_{*}
and defined
\chi_{*}\equiv\chi(z_{*})
.

We now begin the 1D Ly
\alpha
-\kappa
bispectrum calculation, which we define as follows:

\displaystyle\langle\delta_{F}(k,\bm{x})\delta_{F}(k^{\prime},\bm{x})\kappa(\bm{x})\rangle\equiv 2\pi\delta_{D}(k+k^{\prime})B^{\mathrm{1D}}_{FF\kappa}(k)

(14)

\displaystyle=W_{\kappa}(z_{*})\int\mathrm{d}\chi\,\langle\delta_{F}(k,\bm{x})\delta_{F}(k^{\prime},\bm{x})\delta^{\mathrm{WF}}_{m}(\chi,\bm{x})\rangle.

(15)

The density fields can be written in terms of 3D Fourier transforms:

\displaystyle\delta_{F}(k,\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{2}\bm{q}_{\bot}}{(2\pi)^{2}}e^{i\bm{q}_{\bot}\cdot\bm{x}}\tilde{\delta}_{F}(\bm{q}_{\bot},k),

(16)

\displaystyle\delta^{\mathrm{WF}}_{m}(\chi,\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{3}\bm{p}}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{i\bm{p}\cdot(\bm{x},\chi)}W_{\mathrm{WF}}(p_{\bot}\chi_{*})\tilde{\delta}_{m}(\bm{p}),

(17)

and then the terms in brackets will yield the 3D bispectrum between Ly
\alpha
forest and matter fields.

\langle\tilde{\delta}_{F}(\bm{q})\tilde{\delta}_{F}(\bm{q}^{\prime})\tilde{\delta}_{m}(\bm{p})\rangle=(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{D}_{\bm{q}\bm{q}^{\prime}\bm{p}}B^{\mathrm{3D}}_{FFm}(\bm{q},\bm{p})

(18)

Note that the integration over
d\chi dp_{z}
sets
p_{z}=0
and yields
2\pi\delta_{D}(k+k^{\prime})
. The ensuing calculus yields the following expression for the 1D
\kappa-
Ly
\alpha
bispectrum:

\displaystyle B^{\mathrm{1D}}_{FF\kappa}(k,z)

\displaystyle=W_{\kappa}(z)\int\frac{d^{2}\bm{q}_{\bot}d^{2}\bm{p}_{\bot}}{(2\pi)^{4}}

\displaystyle\times B^{\mathrm{3D}}_{FFm}(\bm{q},\bm{q}^{\prime},\bm{p})W_{\mathrm{WF}}(p_{\bot}\chi_{*}),

(19)

where
\bm{q}=(\bm{q}_{\bot},k)
,
\bm{p}=(\bm{p}_{\bot},0)
and
\bm{q}^{\prime}=(-\bm{q}_{\bot}-\bm{p}_{\bot},-k)
. We found the integration over
\bm{p}_{\bot}
to be more robust against the divergences in the tree-level bispectrum expansion. We present a cross-bispectrum expression including angular modes in Appendix
C
.

These expressions are in units of Mpc
h^{-1}
, whereas Ly
\alpha

P_{\mathrm{1D}}
is conventionally calculated in velocity units. The conversion factor between these two units is dependent on redshift and cosmology:

1~\mathrm{Mpc}~h^{-1}=100\frac{E(z)}{1+z}~\mathrm{km~s}^{-1}.

(20)

Accurate analytical modeling of
B^{\mathrm{3D}}_{FFm}
is a difficult task not only because of the non-linear gravitational evolution but also because of nuisance parameters needed to describe the non-linearities in the Ly
\alpha
forest. The effective field theory for the Ly
\alpha
forest is a promising avenue to incorporate all the nuisance parameters
(
Ivanov 2024
)
, but is outside the scope of this work. Instead, we are going to limit our analysis to a simple large-scale structure bias model for
\delta_{F}
with some pressure smoothing and thermal broadening such that

\tilde{\delta}_{F}(\bm{q})=b_{F}(1+\beta_{F}\mu^{2})\tilde{\delta}_{m}(\bm{q})e^{-q^{2}/k_{p}^{2}-q_{z}^{2}\sigma_{th}^{2}/2},

(21)

where
k_{p}
is the pressure smoothing/filtering scale
(
Gnedin and Hui 1998
)
, and
\sigma_{th}=\sqrt{k_{B}T/m_{p}}
is the thermal broadening scale where we reparameterize temperature into a power law such that
T\rightarrow T_{0}10^{\log T-4}
at
T_{0}=10^{4}~
K. Since the bispectrum needs a non-linear treatment even to first order and our measurement is at a modest
3.0\sigma
detection significance, we ignore non-linear enhancement terms and additional angular dependencies proposed in the fitting functions in the literature
(
McDonald 2003
;
Arinyo-i-Prats et al. 2015
)
. We note that
\sigma_{th}
is in velocity units and needs to be converted to Mpc
h^{-1}
units using Eq.
20
. We then calculate the matter bispectrum using tree-level perturbation theory up to second-order

B^{\mathrm{3D}}_{mmm}=2F_{2}(\bm{q},\bm{p})P_{L}(q)P_{L}(p)+\mathrm{two\ cyc.\ terms},

(22)

where
P_{L}
is the linear matter power spectrum and the
F_{2}
kernel is
Jain and Bertschinger 1994

F_{2}(\bm{q},\bm{p})=\frac{5}{7}+\frac{\bm{q}\cdot\bm{p}}{2}\left[\frac{1}{q^{2}}+\frac{1}{p^{2}}\right]+\frac{2}{7}\frac{(\bm{q}\cdot\bm{p})^{2}}{q^{2}p^{2}}.

(23)

Symmetry allows us to write the integral in Eq.
IV
as follows:

\int\frac{d^{2}\bm{q}_{\bot}d^{2}\bm{p}_{\bot}}{(2\pi)^{4}}\rightarrow\int\frac{p^{2}_{\bot}d\ln p_{\bot}}{2\pi}\int\frac{q^{2}_{\bot}d\ln q_{\bot}d\phi}{(2\pi)^{2}}.

(24)

Furthermore, since the integrand only depends on
w\equiv\cos\phi
, we integrate the angular part by using the Chebyshev–Gauss quadrature method:

\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\rightarrow 2\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{dw}{\sqrt{1-w^{2}}}.

(25)

There are two major shortcomings of our model. First, the second-order bias terms such as
b_{2}
of the Ly
\alpha
forest will contribute to tree-level expressions at the leading order. Second, additional non-linear enhancements and angular-dependent effects are expected to contribute to the model similar to
P_{\mathrm{1D}}
. As noted above in the text, the effective field theory for the Ly
\alpha
forest can incorporate all these terms.

Fit to measurement

A full cosmological analysis is unlikely to be successful given the current error bars on
B_{\kappa,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
and is therefore outside the scope of our paper. Instead, we fix the cosmological parameters to Planck 2018 values
(
Planck Collaboration et al. 2020b
)
:
\Omega_{b}h^{2}=0.02242,\Omega_{c}h^{2}=0.11934,h=0.6766,n_{s}=0.9665,\ln(10^{10}A_{s})=3.044
. Since
B_{\kappa,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
integrates over the angular modes, it is less sensitive to the redshift space distortion parameter
\beta_{F}
. So, we also fix
\beta_{F}=1.67
from the Ly
\alpha
3D correlation function analysis
(
du Mas des Bourboux et al. 2020
)
. We add Gaussian priors of
k_{p}=7\pm 30~
h
Mpc
-1
and
\log T=4\pm 2

(
Walther et al. 2019
;
Gaikwad et al. 2021
;
Villasenor et al. 2023
)
. We then fit this model to our
B_{\kappa,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
measurement that has had the side band metal contamination and the first two
k
bins removed. We calculate the linear matter power spectrum using
CosmoPower

(
Spurio Mancini et al. 2022
)
and minimize
\chi^{2}
using
iminuit

(
James and Roos 1975
;
Dembinski et al. 2022
)
.

IV.2
Position-dependent framework

Doux et al. 2016
provides an intuitive theoretical model for
B_{\kappa,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
based on the location-dependent power spectrum and the response of the power spectrum to a large-scale density mode. Their model has a quadratic dependence on the Wiener filter, whereas our model has a linear dependence.
Doux et al. 2016
erroneously define the large-scale density mode based on the Wiener filter. However, this filter is a post-processing step based on the instrumental noise properties of the CMB experiment, so the power spectrum cannot respond to it (unless the quasar spectra are angularly smoothed with the same Wiener filter). Following
Chiang and Slosar 2018
, the correct formulation for the location-dependent modeling is as follows:

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{1D}}(k,\textbf{x})

\displaystyle=P^{\mathrm{lin}}_{\mathrm{1D}}(k)+\overline{\delta}(\textbf{x})\frac{\partial P_{\mathrm{1D}}}{\partial\overline{\delta}}

(26)

\displaystyle\langle P_{\mathrm{1D}}(k,\textbf{x})\kappa(\textbf{x})\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\overline{\delta}(\textbf{x})\kappa(\textbf{x})\rangle\frac{\partial P_{\mathrm{1D}}}{\partial\overline{\delta}},

(27)

where the “survey window function”
W_{L}
for each quasar is a line in space, so the large-scale density mode is a simple average in the radial direction without any angular smoothing component:
\overline{\delta}(\textbf{x})=\frac{1}{\Delta\chi}\int d\chi\ \delta_{m}(\chi,\bm{x})W_{L}(\chi-\chi_{*})
, where
W_{L}
is one within the Ly
\alpha
forest region and zero otherwise
(
Chiang et al. 2014
)
. Then, the variance term is given by

\displaystyle\langle\overline{\delta}(\textbf{x})\kappa(\textbf{x})\rangle

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d\chi}{\Delta\chi}\frac{d^{3}\bm{p}}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{-ip_{z}(\chi-\chi_{*})}

\displaystyle\qquad\times W_{\kappa}(\chi)W_{\mathrm{WF}}(p_{\bot}\chi)\tilde{W}_{L}(p_{z})P(p)

(28)

\displaystyle\langle\overline{\delta}(\textbf{x})\kappa(\textbf{x})\rangle

\displaystyle\approx W_{\kappa}(\chi_{*})\int\frac{d^{2}\bm{p}_{\bot}}{(2\pi)^{2}}W_{\mathrm{WF}}(p_{\bot}\chi_{*})P(p_{\bot}),

(29)

where we apply the Limber approximation in the last line and recover the linear dependence on the Wiener filter. The response term
\partial P_{\mathrm{1D}}/\partial\overline{\delta}
can be calculated with the standard perturbation theory approach as described in
Doux et al. 2016
.

\frac{\partial P_{\mathrm{1D}}}{\partial\overline{\delta}}(k_{z})=\int\frac{d^{2}\bm{k}_{\bot}}{(2\pi)^{2}}b_{F}^{2}\left(1+\beta_{F}\mu^{2}\right)^{2}D(k,\mu)f(k)P_{L}(k),

(30)

where
k=\sqrt{k_{\bot}^{2}+k_{z}^{2}}
,
\mu=k_{z}/k
,
D(k,\mu)
is the non-linear fitting function for the Ly
\alpha
forest
(
McDonald 2003
;
Arinyo-i-Prats et al. 2015
)
and
f(k)
is the response of the linear matter power spectrum
(
Chiang et al. 2014
)
:

f(k)=\frac{68}{21}-\frac{1}{3}\frac{d\ln k^{3}P_{L}(k)}{d\ln k}.

(31)

As noted in
Doux et al. 2016
, this formulation of
\partial P_{\mathrm{1D}}/\partial\overline{\delta}
ignores the response of bias and other parameters to the large-scale density mode. Alternatively, the response can be numerically derived from separate universe simulations
(
Chiang et al. 2017
;
Chiang and Slosar 2018
)
. Furthermore, as noted by
Chiang et al. 2017
, Eq.
26
will have additional terms related to velocity bias and tidal field.

V
Discussion

V.1
Measurement concerns

B_{\kappa,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
faces the same challenges as
P_{\mathrm{1D}}
, originating with the quasar continuum fitting algorithm, HCDs, metals, and pipeline noise and resolution estimates. We partially addressed the errors in the quasar continuum fitting, HCDs, and metal contamination in Section
III.4
. Fortunately,
B_{\kappa,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
is unbiased against noise systematics as instrumental noise and
\kappa
are uncorrelated. However, scatter in noise errors between quasars will inflate the covariance of
B_{\kappa,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
. We applied a CCD amplifier-dependent correction to mitigate this effect as described in Section
III.1
. The spectrograph resolution errors will introduce a scale-dependent bias to
B_{\kappa,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
since the resolution correction is multiplicative. This effect is relevant at higher
k
values than we measure, so we ignore it in this work.

Additionally, our continuum fitting method biases the quasar continuum estimates towards the average transmission in the forest, which introduces a small bias that affects all scales. For
P_{\mathrm{1D}}
measurements, this bias is at most 1% at low redshifts (
z<2.6
). Since our measurements are not as precise, we ignore the scaling of
P_{\mathrm{1D}}
in
B_{\kappa,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
. However, the correlations between these biased continuum errors and lensing convergence could be more important for
B_{\kappa,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
.
Chiang and Slosar 2018
calculated that these correlations could constitute 30% of the total signal at
z=2.2
and become comparable to the true signal at
z=2.6
. Due to spectral noise, the fitted continuum amplitude is not as correlated with the underlying large-scale density field as assumed in
Chiang and Slosar 2018
, so these should be considered upper bounds.

Another possible correlated contamination between DESI quasar spectra and the Planck gravitational lensing map is the Galactic extinction. This is because Galactic extinction corrections are applied to both the data used for quasar target selection and the CMB measurements. We calculate the
B_{E,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
bispectrum between
P_{\mathrm{1D}}
and
E_{B-V}
by replacing
\kappa
with
E_{B-V}
values in the DESI DR1 quasar catalog
(
Schlegel et al. 1998
, SFD, )
. We then quantify the correlation between
\kappa
and
E_{B-V}
by binning quasar
\kappa
values with respect to
E_{B-V}
. Figure
7
shows our measured
\kappa-E_{B-V}
relation.

Figure 7:
\kappa-E_{B-V}
relation. The best linear fit is
\alpha=0.01
. As an additional test, we also compare DESI
E_{B-V}
values to the corrected SFD with CIB removed dust map
E_{B-V}
values from
Chiang 2023
, which yields a smaller bias value of
\alpha=0.005
. These weak biases do not affect our measurement.

For a linear bias relation of
\kappa=\alpha E_{B-V}+\mathrm{signal}
,
B_{\kappa,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
will have a dust contamination term proportional to
\alpha
such that
\Delta
B_{\kappa,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
=\alpha B_{E,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
. We do not detect
B_{E,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
at any significance, but find that it is numerically comparable to
B_{\kappa,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
. However, we also find that
\alpha=0.01
and this weak correlation between
\kappa
and the Galactic extinction does not change our bispectrum measurement and does not increase our error budget. As an additional test, we compare DESI
E_{B-V}
values to the corrected SFD (cSFD) with Cosmic Infrared Background (CIB) removed dust map values from
Chiang 2023
and find
\delta E_{B-V}\sim 0.001
. cSFD dust map has a better correction for the imprint of the large-scale structure and yields a smaller bias value between the gravitational lensing and Galactic extinction (
\alpha=0.005
). For stronger correlations or more precise future bispectrum measurements, the
\kappa-E_{B-V}
bias model can be generalized to any polynomial such that:

\displaystyle\Delta\kappa

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}\alpha_{n}E_{B-V}^{n}

(32)

\displaystyle\Delta B_{\kappa,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}\alpha_{n}B^{(n)}_{E,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha},

(33)

where
B^{(n)}_{E,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
is the bispectrum between
P_{\mathrm{1D}}
and
E^{n}_{B-V}
. Alternatively, this
\kappa-E_{B-V}
relation can be used to de-bias
\kappa
values. The angular correlations between Galactic extinction and gravitational lensing can produce spurious correlations. They may require a more careful treatment when measuring the angular
\delta_{F}-\kappa
cross-power spectrum and cross-bispectrum.

To test for contamination from clusters in the foreground, we also measure
B_{\kappa,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
using the thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich (tSZ) effect-deprojected lensing maps. These are temperature-only (TT) estimates. We find both
B_{\kappa,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
completely agree within the error bars with slight downward trend at low
k
, but the detection significance becomes
4.1\sigma
due to lost signal.

We also test the Planck PR4 lensing map to gauge the improvements in the new pipeline
(
Carron et al. 2022
)
. We find a small increase (
\approx 0.2\sigma
) in detection significance, which is not significant enough to repeat the analysis with this new reduction. The Atacama Cosmology Telescope (ACT) map has an even lower reconstruction noise power but covers a smaller area than Planck
(
Madhavacheril et al. 2024
)
, so the net gain will be similarly incremental.

V.2
Modeling concerns

Modeling Ly
\alpha

P_{\mathrm{1D}}
is a challenging task since it requires sufficient accuracy at small scales to model the nonlinearities involved in gravitational collapse and hydrodynamics, yet also substantial volumes to capture the full extent of modern surveys. The generation of hydrodynamical simulations that incorporate all these effects is computationally expensive. One promising approach is to use emulators that are trained on existing simulations to capture the essential mapping between cosmological parameters and the corresponding
P_{\mathrm{1D}}
(
Pedersen et al. 2021
;
Fernandez et al. 2022
;
Cabayol-Garcia et al. 2023
)
. This approach essentially trades the free bias parameters
b_{F},\beta_{F}
with external observations of the IGM mean flux
\overline{F}
, and gains additional constraining power. Similar emulators for
B_{\kappa,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
will be powerful in advancing cosmological interpretation.

With increased precision in
B_{\kappa,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
, metal contributions such as Si
iii
and Si
ii
oscillations may need to be included in the model.

A further modeling complication is the He
ii
reionization at
z\approx 3

(
Gaikwad et al. 2021
)
, and the residual ionization bubbles. The beginning, strength, and duration of the reionization will be related to the overall density in a given region. The net effect of this inhomogeneous process will likely add power to
k>0.01
s km
-1
modes
(
Upton Sanderbeck and Bird 2020
)
.

VI
Summary

We presented a 1D CMB lensing–Ly
\alpha
forest bispectrum measurement using DESI DR1 quasar spectra and the Planck PR3 lensing map. This cross-bispectrum measurement is robust against some instrumental systematics and has a
\sigma_{8}^{4}
dependence, compared to the
\sigma_{8}^{2}
dependence of the power spectrum, which could help break the
b_{F}-\sigma_{8}
degeneracy. This is the second measurement of its kind and our
4.8\sigma
detection is comparable to the first detection presented by
Doux et al. 2016
at
5\sigma
. We found that the calibration of the pipeline noise estimates based on CCD amplifier regions reduced the errors in
B_{\kappa,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
by reducing the scatter in noise systematics from quasar to quasar. This calibration produced an improvement in our detection significance of about
0.7\sigma
.

We performed a shuffle test to confirm the signal was real and then isolated the signal to the neutral hydrogen in the intergalactic medium by subtracting the metal contamination. We measured
\kappa-
metals bispectrum using the sideband technique and found that the metal contamination was not insignificant. With this additional uncertainty, our detection significance decreased to
2.7\sigma
.

We investigated how Galactic extinction and clusters of galaxies could be foreground contaminants that correlate with the Ly
\alpha
forest from DESI and
\kappa
from Planck. We found that these foregrounds are not strong enough to affect
B_{\kappa,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
at our current precision. The angular correlations within these foregrounds could complicate future angular
\kappa-
Ly
\alpha
cross-power spectrum and cross-bispectrum measurements.

We developed a theoretical model within the Limber and flat-sky approximations and calculated it using tree-level perturbation theory. Our model provided a reasonable fit, but it lacks the second-order bias terms such as
b_{2}
and nuisance parameters for additional non-linear enhancements and angular dependent effects. The effective field theory of the Ly
\alpha
forest is an encouraging avenue for future study
(
Ivanov 2024
)
.

In the future,
B_{\kappa,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
can be used to constrain bias parameters and break the degeneracy between
\sigma_{8}
and other cosmological parameters. This requires sizable improvements in the data quality of both the Ly
\alpha
forest and the reconstructed CMB lensing maps.

Appendix A
Quadratic estimator

The QMLE works in real space (instead of Fourier space) to estimate the power spectrum, and can weight pixels by the pipeline noise and the intrinsic Ly
\alpha
large-scale structure correlations. In this section, we provide a summary of our previous work
Karaçaylı et al. 2020
;
Karaçaylı et al. 2024
.

Our QMLE implementation adopts a fiducial power spectrum
P_{\mathrm{fid}}(k,z)
to calculate the signal contribution of 1D Ly
\alpha
forest correlations of the covariance matrix.

\frac{kP_{\mathrm{fid}}}{\pi}=A\frac{(k/k_{0})^{3+n+\alpha\ln k/k_{0}}}{1+(k/k_{1})^{2}}\left(\frac{1+z}{1+z_{0}}\right)^{B+\beta\ln k/k_{0}},

(34)

where
k_{0}=0.009~
s km
-1
and
z_{0}=3.0
. We stress that this functional form is sufficient to weight pixels, but does not capture all of the scientific information in
P_{\mathrm{1D}}
. The parameters we used in this analysis are the best-fit values to the DESI early data
P_{\mathrm{1D}}
measurement
Karaçaylı et al. 2024
and are listed in Table

Given a collection of pixels representing normalized flux fluctuations
\bm{\delta}_{F}
, the quadratic estimator is formulated as follows:

\hat{\theta}_{a}=\sum_{b}\frac{1}{2}F^{-1}_{ab}(d_{b}-n_{b}-t_{b}),

(35)

where

\displaystyle d_{a}

\displaystyle=\bm{\delta}_{F}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{Q}_{a}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\bm{\delta}_{F},

(36)

\displaystyle n_{a}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{Q}_{a}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{N}),

(37)

\displaystyle t_{a}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{Q}_{a}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{fid}}).

(38)

The covariance matrix
\mathbf{C}\equiv\langle\bm{\delta}_{F}\bm{\delta}_{F}^{T}\rangle
is the sum of signal and noise,
\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{fid}}+\mathbf{N}
. Note that this is the covariance matrix between forest pixels at different wavelengths. The quantity
\mathbf{Q}_{a}\equiv\partial\mathbf{C}/\partial\theta_{a}
and the estimate of the Fisher matrix is

F_{ab}=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{Q}_{a}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{Q}_{b}).

(39)

Assuming different quasar spectra are uncorrelated, the Fisher matrix
F_{ab}
and the expression in parentheses in Eq.
35
can be computed for each quasar, then accumulated, i.e.
\mathbf{F}=\sum_{q}\mathbf{F}_{q}
, etc. The power spectrum covariance matrix noted in the text is the inverse of this Fisher matrix, i.e.
\mathbf{C}_{q}=\mathbf{F}^{-1}_{q}
.

Appendix B
Derivation of sample bias correction

The bispectrum estimator in Eq.
5
is biased without the sample correction term
\mathbf{N}
. We derive this bias by calculating the expected value of the biased estimator
\hat{B}^{*}_{a}
.

\displaystyle\hat{B}^{*}_{a}=

\displaystyle\sum_{q}W^{q}_{ab}\left(p^{q}_{b}-\hat{\overline{{p}}}_{b}\right)\left(\kappa^{q}-\hat{\overline{{\kappa}}}_{a}\right)

(40)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{q}W^{q}_{ab}p^{q}_{b}\kappa^{q}-\sum_{q}\kappa_{q}W^{q}_{ab}\hat{\overline{{p}}}_{b}

\displaystyle-\sum_{q}W^{q}_{ab}p^{q}_{b}\hat{\overline{{\kappa}}}_{a}+\sum_{q}W^{q}_{ab}\hat{\overline{{p}}}_{b}\hat{\overline{{\kappa}}}_{a}

(41)

We use the Einstein summation convention such that all repeated indices imply summation
except
for the index
a
.
The third and fourth terms cancel regardless of the choice for the mean
\kappa
estimation method.
Then:

\displaystyle\hat{B}^{*}_{a}=

\displaystyle\sum_{q}W^{q}_{ab}p^{q}_{b}\kappa^{q}-\sum_{q}W^{q}_{ab}\hat{\overline{{p}}}_{b}\kappa^{q}

(42)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{q}W^{q}_{ab}p^{q}_{b}\kappa_{q}-\sum_{q,q^{\prime}}W^{q}_{ab}W^{q^{\prime}}_{bc}p^{q^{\prime}}_{c}\kappa^{q}

(43)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{q}W^{q}_{ab}p^{q}_{b}\kappa^{q}-\sum_{q}W^{q}_{ab}W^{q}_{bc}p^{q}_{c}\kappa^{q}

\displaystyle-\sum_{q,q^{\prime}\neq q}W^{q}_{ab}W^{q^{\prime}}_{bc}p^{q^{\prime}}_{c}\kappa^{q}

(44)

\displaystyle\langle\hat{B}^{*}_{a}\rangle=

\displaystyle\sum_{q}W^{q}_{ab}\langle p\kappa\rangle_{b}-\sum_{q}W^{q}_{ab}W^{q}_{bc}\langle p\kappa\rangle_{c}

\displaystyle-\sum_{q}W^{q}_{ab}\sum_{q^{\prime}\neq q}W^{q^{\prime}}_{bc}\langle{p}\rangle_{c}\langle{\kappa}\rangle,

(45)

Using the relation
\sum_{q^{\prime}\neq q}\mathbf{W}_{q^{\prime}}=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}_{q}
, we can collect all the terms into a simple expression

\langle\hat{B}^{*}_{a}\rangle=\left(\mathbf{I}-\sum_{q}\mathbf{W}^{2}_{q}\right)_{ab}\left(\langle p\kappa\rangle_{b}-\langle{p}\rangle_{b}\langle{\kappa}\rangle\right),

(46)

and read
\mathbf{N}^{-1}=\mathbf{I}-\sum_{q}\mathbf{W}^{2}_{q}
. This sampling bias correction term is small as expected from a large sample, which is shown in Figure
8
.
Note that this matrix is not symmetric.

. This correction is small as expected from a large sample.

Appendix C
Derivation of bispectrum

We can derive the bispectrum expression without assuming a constant lensing kernel in a redshift bin:

\displaystyle\kappa^{\mathrm{WF}}(\chi,\bm{x})=

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}\chi\ W_{\kappa}(\chi)\delta^{\mathrm{WF}}_{m}(\chi,\bm{x}),

(47)

\displaystyle\delta^{\mathrm{WF}}_{m}(\chi,\bm{x})=

\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{2}\bm{p}_{\bot}}{(2\pi)^{2}}e^{i\bm{p}_{\bot}\cdot\bm{x}}W_{\mathrm{WF}}(p_{\bot}\chi)\tilde{\delta}_{m}(\chi,\bm{p}_{\bot}),

(48)

where we still applied the flat-sky approximation. The 3D Fourier transform of
\delta^{\mathrm{WF}}_{m}
is modified accordingly:

\delta^{\mathrm{WF}}_{m}(\chi,\bm{x})=\int\frac{d^{2}\bm{p}_{\bot}dp_{z}}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{i(\bm{p}_{\bot}\cdot\bm{x}+p_{z}\chi)}W_{\mathrm{WF}}(p_{\bot}\chi)\tilde{\delta}_{m}(\bm{p}).

(49)

The bispectrum calculation then proceeds as follows:

\displaystyle\langle\delta_{F}(k,\bm{x})\delta_{F}(k^{\prime},\bm{x})\kappa(\bm{x})\rangle

\displaystyle\quad=\int\mathrm{d}\chi\ W_{\kappa}(\chi)\langle\delta_{F}(k,\bm{x})\delta_{F}(k^{\prime},\bm{x})\delta^{\mathrm{WF}}_{m}(\chi,\bm{x})\rangle

(50)

\displaystyle\quad=\int\mathrm{d}\chi\int\frac{d^{2}\bm{q}_{\bot}d^{2}\bm{q}^{\prime}_{\bot}d^{2}\bm{p}_{\bot}}{(2\pi)^{6}}e^{i(\bm{q}_{\bot}+\bm{q}^{\prime}_{\bot}+\bm{p}^{\prime}_{\bot})\cdot\bm{x}}\int\frac{dp_{z}}{2\pi}e^{ip_{z}\chi}

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\times W_{\kappa}(\chi)W_{\mathrm{WF}}(p_{\bot}\chi)B^{\mathrm{3D}}_{FFm}(\bm{q},\bm{q}^{\prime},\bm{p})

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\times(2\pi)^{2}\delta_{D}(\bm{q}_{\bot}+\bm{q}^{\prime}_{\bot}+\bm{p}_{\bot})

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\times(2\pi)\delta_{D}(k+k^{\prime}+p_{z}).

(51)

We can integrate out
\bm{q}^{\prime}_{\bot}
as before. The integration over
p_{z}
now yields
p_{z}=-k-k^{\prime}
due to the Dirac delta function. Then:

\displaystyle\langle\delta_{F}(k,\bm{x})\delta_{F}(k^{\prime},\bm{x})\kappa(\bm{x})\rangle

\displaystyle\quad=\int\frac{d^{2}\bm{q}_{\bot}d^{2}\bm{p}_{\bot}}{(2\pi)^{4}}B^{\mathrm{3D}}_{FFm}(\bm{q},\bm{q}^{\prime},\bm{p})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times\int\mathrm{d}\chi\ e^{-i(k+k^{\prime})\chi}W_{\kappa}(\chi)W_{\mathrm{WF}}(p_{\bot}\chi)

(52)

So far we only used the flat-sky approximation. Now we apply the Limber approximation by assuming the integrand slowly varies in
\chi
:

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}\chi\ e^{-i(k+k^{\prime})\chi}W_{\kappa}(\chi)W_{\mathrm{WF}}(p_{\bot}\chi)

\displaystyle\qquad\approx(2\pi)\delta_{D}(k+k^{\prime})\left[W_{\kappa}(\chi_{*})W_{\mathrm{WF}}(p_{\bot}\chi_{*})+O_{1}\right]

(53)

and recover the expression in the main text. A first-order correction to the Limber approximation is given by

\displaystyle O_{1}=\frac{(\Delta z)^{2}}{8}\chi^{\prime\prime}_{*}[

\displaystyle W^{\prime}_{\kappa}(\chi_{*})W_{\mathrm{WF}}(p_{\bot}\chi_{*})

\displaystyle+p_{\bot}W_{\kappa}(\chi_{*})W^{\prime}_{\mathrm{WF}}(p_{\bot}\chi_{*})],

(54)

which yields 0.1% corrections on
B_{\kappa,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha}
for
\Delta z=0.4
at
z_{*}=2.4
.

To include the angular modes in our model, we start from the following definition:

\langle\delta_{F}(k,\bm{x})\delta_{F}(k^{\prime},\bm{x})\kappa(\bm{x}^{\prime})\rangle=2\pi\delta_{D}(k+k^{\prime})\zeta(k,r),

(55)

where
r=|\bm{x}^{\prime}-\bm{x}|=\theta\chi_{*}
and the bispectrum is given by the Fourier transform:

B^{\mathrm{2D}}_{FFm}(k,p_{\bot})=\int d^{2}\bm{r}e^{i\bm{p}_{\bot}\cdot\bm{r}}\zeta(k,r).

(56)

Under the same flat-sky and Limber approximations, we can then obtain the following:

\displaystyle B^{\mathrm{2D}}_{FFm}(k,p_{\bot})=W_{\kappa}(\chi_{*})W_{\mathrm{WF}}(p_{\bot}\chi_{*})

\displaystyle\qquad\times\int\frac{q_{\bot}dq_{\bot}d\phi}{(2\pi)^{2}}B^{\mathrm{3D}}_{FFm}(\bm{q},\bm{q}^{\prime},\bm{p}),

(57)

where
\bm{q}_{\bot}\cdot\bm{p}_{\bot}=q_{\bot}p_{\bot}\cos\phi
in
B^{\mathrm{3D}}_{FFm}
calculation. Note that
B^{\mathrm{1D}}_{FFm}(k)
is equivalent to
\zeta(k,r=0)
. By applying the inverse Fourier transform, we can recover the previous
B^{\mathrm{1D}}_{FFm}
expressions.

\displaystyle\zeta(k,r)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{2}\bm{p}_{\bot}}{(2\pi)^{2}}e^{-i\bm{p}_{\bot}\cdot\bm{r}}B^{\mathrm{2D}}_{FFm}(k,p_{\bot})

(58)

\displaystyle B^{\mathrm{1D}}_{FFm}(k)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{2}\bm{p}_{\bot}}{(2\pi)^{2}}B^{\mathrm{2D}}_{FFm}(k,p_{\bot})

(59)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{p_{\bot}dp_{\bot}}{2\pi}B^{\mathrm{2D}}_{FFm}(k,p_{\bot})

(60)
