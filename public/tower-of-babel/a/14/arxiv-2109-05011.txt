Signal-to-noise-ratio and SNR-max detection statistics in template bank searches for exotic physics transients with networks of quantum sensors
T. Daykin, C. Ellis, A. Derevianko

Abstract
Quantum sensor networks such as the existing networks of atomic clocks and magnetometers offer intriguing capabilities in searches for transient signals such as dark matter or fields sourced by powerful astrophysical events. The common matched-filter technique relies on signal-to-noise (SNR) detection statistic to probe such transients. For macroscopic dark matter objects, the network would register a sweeping signal as the object propagates through at galactic velocities. A potential event is registered when the SNR from a specific template exceeds a threshold set by a desired false positive rate. Generically, to span the continuous parameter space for the network-exotic-physics encounter, one has to deal with multiple templates. In such template bank searches, the natural generalization of the SNR statistic is the SNR-max statistic, defined as the maximum of the absolute values of SNRs determined from individual template matching. While individual SNR realizations are Gaussian distributed, SNR-max probability distribution is non-Gaussian. Moreover, as the individual template-bank SNRs are computed using the same network data streams, SNRs become correlated between the templates. Cross-template correlations have a sizable effect on the SNR-max probability and cumulative distributions, and on the threshold SNR-max values. Computing threshold SNR-max values for large template banks is computationally prohibitive and we develop analytic approaches to computing properties of SNR-max statistic. This is done for cases when the template bank is nearly orthogonal (small cross-template correlations) and for banks with cross-template correlation coefficients distribution “squeezed” about the most probable cross-template correlation value. Since the cross-template correlation coefficients quantify the similarity of templates, increasing correlations tend to decrease SNR-max thresholds for specific values of false positive rates. Increasing the number of templates in the bank increases the SNR-max thresholds. Our derivations are carried out for networks that may exhibit colored noise and cross-node correlations. Specific applications are illustrated with a dark matter search with atomic clocks onboard satellites of Global Positioning System (GPS) and with a “toy” planar network with cyclic rotational symmetry.

The MFT is an ubiquitous technique, utilized for example by the Laser Interferometer Gravitational Wave Observatory (LIGO) in their gravitational wave detection

;

Further maximization of the template specific SNR variables (ignoring dependence on sign), was performed over the template bank. In this process, a Gaussian distribution was considered instead of the distribution for the maximum of the absolute valued SNRs (SNR-max). As we discuss in this paper, the SNR-max distribution is no longer a Gaussian distribution, but a skewed (non-Gaussian) distribution. Moreover, there exist correlations in the SNR statistics computed for different templates in the bank, which alter the uncorrelated SNR-max distribution. Thresholds for specific false positive rates determined in Ref.
Panelli et al. 2020
overestimate the thresholds due to the effect of correlated templates in a template bank. One of the goals of this paper is to determine the thresholds correctly.

II
SNR and SNR-max statistics

In the previous work
Panelli et al. 2020
by our group, we explored the properties of the SNR statistic in the context of networks of quantum sensors. In this section we set up the problem, review relevant results from Ref.
Panelli et al. 2020
, and then introduce the SNR-max detection statistic.

Throughout this paper, we assume that the network data streams are stationary and Gaussian-distributed. The sensor noise can be colored and the noise can be correlated across the network. For an atomic clock network, the cross-network correlation comes from referencing every clock phase to that of “reference” clock common to all clocks
Roberts et al. 2017
. Notice our assumption of the noise stationarity; for clocks this implies working with frequency data instead of phase (time or time bias as recorded by the clock) data.

Network data streams
\mathbf{d}
are comprised of sensor measurements
d_{i}^{a}
, where the subscript
i
refers to the time stamp (time
t_{i}
) and superscript
a
identifies a specific sensor with the total number of sensors
N_{\rm S}
. The time grid is assumed to be uniform with a step size
\Delta_{t}
. We will refer to a time interval
[t_{i},t_{i+1})
as an epoch
i
.

In the most basic search for transient signals, network data streams
\mathbf{d}
are partitioned into data windows of fixed length
J
and the data inside each window are matched to a bank of
M
templates. We enumerate windows with an index
w
and refer to the portion of the data stream within a specific data window as
\mathbf{d}^{w}
. The template bank contains multiple signal templates,
\mathbf{s}_{k}
,
k=\overline{1,M}
. The templates must reflect underlying physics of the sought signals. The length of each template in time domain is equal to that of the data window
J
. For a network, each template is represented by an
N_{\rm S}\times J
matrix.

As a concrete example of physics-motivated generation of a signal template bank, in Sec.
III
we discuss the bank used in the GPS.DM search. Briefly, in the GPS.DM search we probe data streams for sweeps by walls of DM bubbles, with the assumption that the radius of the DM bubble is much larger than the network spatial extent. Then the network is swept by planar walls. These hypothesized walls are to sweep through the network with velocities within a range prescribed by the SHM
Bovy and Tremaine 2012
. Each template is distinguished by the specific values of a DM wall incoming velocity and orientation. We typically use
M=1024
templates in the bank to ensure a sufficiently dense coverage of the sweep parameters. There are nominally
N_{\rm S}=32
atomic clocks on-board GPS satellites populating orbits on a spherical shell of diameter
D\sim 5\times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{km}
. The atomic clock time readings (biases) are sampled every
\Delta_{t}=30\,\mathrm{s}
and the length of data window
J=61
is determined by the ratio of the maximum network sweep time to the sampling time,

(D/v_{\mathrm{min}})/\Delta_{t}
, where the minimum speed
v_{\mathrm{min}}\approx 25\,\mathrm{km/s}
. The minimum value of the speed is determined by the requirement that the spatial network configuration does not change substantially over the duration of the sweep, since the orbital velocity of the satellites is
\sim 5\,\mathrm{km/s}
.

The sought physics signal can be parameterized as
h\mathbf{s}_{k}
, with
h
being the amplitude or the strength of the signal.
h
is to be estimated from the match to the template.
Typically,
h
encodes the coupling of the sensor to the exotic physics.
Formally, the signal strength estimator
for a specific window
w
and a template
k
reads
Panelli et al. 2020

\hat{h}^{w}_{k}=\frac{\left(\mathbf{d}^{w}\right)^{T}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{s}_{k}}{\mathbf{s}_{k}^{T}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{s}_{k}}\,,

(1)

where
\mathbf{E}^{-1}

is the inverse of the network noise covariance matrix
\mathbf{E}
, discussed in Sec.
II.0.1
.

Further, the standard deviation of the estimated signal amplitude, Eq. (
1
), is
Panelli et al. 2020

\sigma_{h,k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathbf{s}_{k}^{T}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{s}_{k}}}\,.

(2)

This uncertainty does not depend on the measurement data but it does depend on a specific template.
Finally, the SNR,
\rho^{w}_{k}
, in window
w
for a template
k
, is simply the signal strength estimator (
1
) divided by the standard deviation of the signal strength (
2
),

\rho^{w}_{k}\equiv\frac{\hat{h}^{w}_{k}}{\sigma_{h,k}}=\frac{\mathbf{s}_{k}^{T}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{d}^{w}}{\sqrt{\mathbf{s}_{k}^{T}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{s}_{k}}}.

(3)

As shown in Ref.
Panelli et al. 2020
, in the absence of a signal (i.e. when
\bm{d}^{w}\equiv\bm{n}^{w}
, noise of sensors), the SNR statistic is Gaussian-distributed with zero mean and variance of 1,

\rho^{w}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1).

(4)

Here
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma)
is the normal distribution with mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma^{2}
.
This can be seen by examining the SNR definition (
3
). The denominator,
\sqrt{\mathbf{s}_{k}^{T}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{s}_{k}}
, is a constant as the template and the network noise covariance matrix are fixed, while the numerator
\mathbf{s}_{k}^{T}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{d}^{w}
is a linear combination of Gaussian random variables.
Thereby, the SNR is also a Gaussian random variable with mean
\left\langle\rho^{w}\right\rangle\propto\langle\mathbf{n}^{w}\rangle=0
. The SNR variance is
\left\langle(\rho^{w}_{k})^{2}\right\rangle=\mathrm{Var}((\mathbf{n}^{w})^{T}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{s_{k}})/\mathbf{s}_{k}^{T}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{s}_{k}
. Ref.
Panelli et al. 2020
(Appendix B) shows that
\mathrm{Var}((\mathbf{n}^{w})^{T}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{s}_{k})=(\mathbf{s}_{k})^{T}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{s}_{k}
, which results in an SNR variance
\left\langle(\rho^{w}_{k})^{2}\right\rangle=1
.

Eq. (
4
) means that given multiple realizations of the signal-free data streams, the values of SNR
\rho_{k}^{w}
for a fixed template
\mathbf{s}_{k}
are distributed as

p_{\mathrm{SNR}}(\rho_{k}^{w})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(\rho_{k}^{w})^{2}/2}\,.

(5)

The above definitions and properties of the SNR statistic hold for a general case of colored noise and multi-sensor networks.
If the subsequent windows overlap, then the SNR values for the two windows are correlated as they are based on partially overlapping data. Even then the SNR probability distribution in individual windows still follows Eq. (
5
). This can be proven by marginalizing joint probability distribution (multi-variate normal distribution) over SNRs in all other overlapping windows.

So far we reviewed the properties of the SNR statistic for a single template.
Practical applications involve banks containing multiple templates.
For a given window
w
, all
M
individual template SNRs are compared and the maximum of the
absolute
values of the individual template SNRs is used as a detection statistic,

z^{w}\equiv\max(|\rho_{1}^{w}|,..,|\rho_{M}^{w}|)\,.

(6)

Following the literature
Owen 1996b
;
Babak 2008
;
Apostolatos 1995
, we refer to this quantity as the
SNR-max statistic
. To simplify notation, from this point forward we drop the window superscript
w
, so that the above definition reads
z\equiv\max(|\rho_{1}|,..,|\rho_{M}|)
.

Matching the same data stream to multiple templates
induces cross-template correlations in the SNR statistics,
\langle\rho_{i}\rho_{j}\rangle\neq 0
. In addition, comparing SNRs and taking their maximum per Eq. (
6
) suppresses smaller values of SNR.
This suppression and cross-template correlations substantially modify the single template SNR distribution (
5
). It is the focus of our paper to quantitatively characterize the SNR-max probability and cumulative distributions. Specifically, we are interested in a practically important threshold value for false positives. We remind the reader that the detection can be claimed when the value of chosen statistic exceeds the detection threshold
Allen 2021
. The threshold values for SNR-max are required to ensure (at a given confidence level) that the observed SNR-max values exceeding this threshold are due to probed physics and not due to randomness. Our previous analysis
Panelli et al. 2020
did not take into account that the detection statistics may be correlated by templates or that our statistic is no longer Gaussian-distributed.
Here we present a more sophisticated analysis to improve upon the treatment of Ref.
Panelli et al. 2020
. Before proceeding to our analysis, below we review relevant properties of the network noise covariance matrix
\mathbf{E}
, entering the SNR definition (
3
).

II.0.1
Network noise covariance matrix
\mathbf{E}

Network noise covariance matrix
\mathbf{E}
is defined by matrix elements

E_{(al)(bm)}=\left\langle n_{l}^{a}n_{m}^{b}\right\rangle\,.

(7)

This definition involves averaging over realizations of signal-free data
n_{l}^{a}
, i.e. intrinsic noise of the sensors. Without loss of generality, we assume that the noise has zero mean,
\langle n_{l}^{a}\rangle=0
.
In our index convention, the letters at the beginning of alphabet
a,b,\ldots
enumerate the sensors (range
\overline{1,N_{\rm S}}
) , while letters in the middle of alphabet
l,m,\ldots
— the epochs (index range
\overline{1,J}
in a data window). We also introduced a compound index notation
(al)
, which organizes sensor-epoch data points into a vector, i.e. we unroll a
N_{\rm S}\times J
matrix into a 1D array. For example,
for the first window,
\mathbf{d}^{w=1}=(d_{1}^{1},d_{2}^{1},\ldots d_{J}^{1},d_{1}^{2},d_{2}^{2},\ldots d_{J}^{2},\ldots d_{1}^{N_{\rm S}},d_{2}^{N_{\rm S}},\ldots d_{J}^{N_{\rm S}})^{T}
. We will use Greek letters for such compound indexes, e.g. Eq. (
7
) can be rewritten as
E_{\alpha\beta}=\left\langle n_{\alpha}n_{\beta}\right\rangle
.
We remind the reader that the covariance matrix is symmetric and positive semi-definite.

As an illustration, for a network of white-noise sensors without a common reference sensor, the noise covariance matrix is diagonal,

E_{(al)(bm)}=\sigma^{2}_{a}\delta_{ab}\delta_{lm}\,,

(8)

with
\sigma_{a}^{2}
being the noise variance for sensor
a
. The matrix elements of its inverse are

\left(E^{-1}\right)_{(al)(bm)}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{a}^{2}}\delta_{lm}\delta_{ab}\,,

(9)

i.e.,
\mathbf{E}^{-1}
is a diagonal matrix.

The same network but referenced to a common sensor (variance
\sigma_{R}^{2}
) has the noise covariance matrix

E_{(al)(bm)}=(\sigma^{2}_{a}\delta_{ab}+\sigma_{R}^{2})\delta_{lm}\,.

(10)

Compared to the no-reference sensor case, Eq. (
8
), the reference sensor introduces off-diagonal matrix elements.
When all the sensors, except for the reference sensor, are assumed to have the same variance
\sigma^{2}
,
the inverse of the covariance matrix
\mathbf{E}^{-1}
can be found analytically
Panelli et al. 2020

\left(E^{-1}\right)_{(al)(bm)}=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\delta_{lm}\left(\delta_{ab}-\frac{1}{N_{\rm S}}\frac{\xi}{1+\xi}\right)\,,

(11)

where the parameter

\xi=N_{\rm S}\sigma_{R}^{2}/\sigma^{2}

(12)

has the meaning of the square of the ratio of network-averaged sensitivity (
\propto\sigma/\sqrt{N_{\rm S}}
) to the reference sensor sensitivity (
\propto\sigma_{R}
). We will refer to
\xi
as the relative reference-sensor sensitivity parameter.

It is instructive to consider a limiting case of a single sensor (
N_{\rm S}=1
) data stream with white noise of zero mean and variance
\sigma^{2}
. In this case, the inverse of the covariance matrix is simply

\mathbf{E}^{-1}=\sigma^{-2}\mathbf{I},

where
\mathbf{I}
is the
J\times J
identity matrix. Further, Eqs. (
1
), (
2
), and (
3
)
reduce to

\hat{h}^{w}_{k}=\left(\mathbf{d}^{w}\cdot\mathbf{s}_{k}\right)/{\left(\mathbf{s}_{k}\cdot\mathbf{s}_{k}\right)}
,

\sigma_{h}=\sigma/{\sqrt{\left(\mathbf{s}_{k}\cdot\mathbf{s}_{k}\right)}}
, and

\rho^{w}_{k}=\left(\mathbf{d}^{w}\cdot\mathbf{s}_{k}\right)/{(\sigma\sqrt{\left(\mathbf{s}_{k}\cdot\mathbf{s}_{k}\right)})},

where we used the conventional definition of the dot product of two vectors.

III
Generation of template banks in the searches for transients

Having defined the SNR and SNR-max statistics, now we formalize the procedure for template bank generation.
Template banks must reflect signals prescribed by the underlying physics to be probed. While the signal templates are application-specific, some generic requirements and challenges can be illustrated with specific examples.
First, in Sec.
III.1
we consider a “toy” 2D problem of a network search for transient signals induced by sweeps of randomly oriented lines through a circular network of high-degree symmetry. This toy problem introduces a number of relevant issues specific for networks, such as the angular and speed resolutions.
After understanding the simplified problem, in Sec.
III.2
, we discuss a more practical case of the GPS.DM search
Roberts et al. 2017
;
Panelli et al. 2020
;
Derevianko and Pospelov 2014b
;
Derevianko 2018b
.

III.1
Toy problem: Circular topology network with
C_{N_{\rm S}}
rotational symmetry

As an illustration of generating template banks and their properties, we consider a planar network with a circular topology where identical point-like sensors are distributed evenly on a circle of radius
R
, see Fig.
1
.

Sensors’ polar angles are given by

\phi_{a}=\frac{2\pi}{N_{\rm S}}(a-1),

(13)

so that the sensor coordinates are

\bm{r}_{a}=R(\cos\phi_{a},\sin\phi_{a})^{T}=R\hat{n}_{a},

(14)

where we used the coordinate system with the origin at the circle center and

This arrangement of sensors has the

We note that this 2D network topology is of relevance to the configuration of satellites in the GPS constellation. The satellites in the GPS network are arranged into six equally-spaced orbital planes with an inclination of
55^{\circ}
, and each nearly-circular orbit is populated with 4-5 satellites.
Because the satellites are not evenly spaced apart within each orbit however, the
C_{N_{\rm S}}
symmetry is broken. Still, understanding our toy network provides a number of useful insights applicable to general network topology.

With the toy network topology specified, now we generate a template bank for sweeps of this 2D network by “lines”. These lines can be thought as 2D cross-sections of thin DM walls. The lines sweep through the network, leaving a geographically distributed imprint on the sensor signals. The sensor is considered to be affected in a given epoch
l
, when the line crosses the sensor in the time interval
[t_{l},t_{l}+\Delta_{t})
.

In our 2D example, the velocities
\bm{v}
of the lines lie in the network plane. However, the component of the velocity parallel to the line leads to an unobservable translation of the line along itself. Different templates are then uniquely specified by the direction and value of velocity component
\bm{v}_{\perp}=v_{\perp}\hat{u}
, where
\hat{u}
is the unit vector perpendicular to the line and pointing in the direction of the sweep.
We will use polar angle
\theta
in the network reference frame to characterize incidence direction
\hat{u}
. For example, template bank in Fig.
1

samples
M=5
directions with
\theta_{k}=(2\pi/M)\,(k-1)
,
k=\overline{1,M}
.
For simplicity, we assume that all the directions
\hat{u}
are equally probable. The bank should provide an adequate sampling of both incidence directions
\hat{u}
and speeds

.

Templates can be generated based on the following argument.
Suppose
t_{O}
and

are the moments of time when the line reaches the network center and the sensor

, respectively. The difference between these two times is

t_{a}-t_{O}=(\hat{u}\cdot\hat{n}_{a})\frac{R}{v_{\perp}}.

(15)

Since our time sampling is finite, the sensor signal time series is simply
s_{l}^{a}=\delta_{l,l_{a}}
, where the epoch

is given by

l_{a}=\left\lfloor(\hat{u}\cdot\hat{n}_{a})\frac{R}{v_{\perp}\Delta_{t}}\right\rfloor+l_{O}.

(16)

Here

is the floor function and
l_{O}=(J+1)/2
is the center of the data window.

v_{\perp}

If
v_{\perp}>(v_{\perp})_{\mathrm{max}}=2R/\Delta_{t}

\Delta l=1

(v_{\perp})_{\mathrm{max}}

\Delta_{t}

As
v_{\perp}
decreases, the template width
\Delta l
increases and different sensors begin to be perturbed at different epochs. On average, we expect
{N_{\rm S}}/{\Delta l}

\bar{g}=N_{\rm S}\frac{v_{\perp}\Delta_{t}}{2R}.

(18)

This degeneracy factor is a rule of thumb.
For example, for the bank and network parameters in Fig.
2

being aligned with one of the network axes of symmetry.
Generically, in the limit of small degeneracy factors,
\bar{g}\leq 1

To generate the template bank we need to sample continuous parameter space of directions
\hat{u}
and normal speeds
v_{\perp}
. This leads to the question of angular and velocity resolutions of our network.

We start with the angular resolution. Consider two sweeping lines which have
the same
v_{\perp}
but differ by their angles of incidence:
\theta
and
\theta^{\prime}=\theta+\Delta_{\theta}
. Because both the number of sensors and time sampling are finite, for sufficiently small relative tilt angles
\Delta_{\theta}
, sweeps by both lines
produce the same template. As we increase the tilt, there is some critical value
of
\Delta_{\theta}
when the two sweeps start mapping onto two distinct templates.
Then the two incident directions can be resolved in the network search.
The critical value of the tilt determines the network angular resolution.

As an illustration, consider the template construction in Fig.
2
for a line sweeping from the left,
\theta=0
.
Templates for tilted sweeps can be generated by rotating the network sensors counter-clockwise by the angle
\Delta_{\theta}
about the circle center. Since the sensors 5 and 7 are near the edge of epoch 3 slice (and sensors 2 and 10 are near the edge of epoch 10), even small tilts
\Delta_{\theta}>0
move sensor 7 into epoch 4 and sensor 2 into epoch 11. However, sensors being at the slice edge is an exceptional situation. Generically, the sensors are more likely to be found in the middle of epoch slices. Then the angular resolution
is determined by the maximally separated sensors within the same slice, i.e. sensors swept within the central epoch. This leads to the worst-case-scenario angular resolution estimate,

\Delta_{\theta}\approx\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{v_{\perp}\Delta_{t}}{2R}\right)\,.

(19)

Recalling the relation (
17
) for the template width
\Delta l
, we observe that

\sin\Delta_{\theta}=1/\Delta l
. When the template collapses into a single column due to high values of normal velocity,
\Delta l=1
, and the angular resolution is lost (
\Delta_{\theta}\rightarrow\pi/2
) in agreement with our previous qualitative statements. In the opposite limit of
\Delta l\gg 1
,
\Delta_{\theta}\approx 1/\Delta l
. For the template of Fig.
2
,
\Delta_{\theta}\approx 1/12\,\mathrm{radians}
. Full, resolution-limited, coverage of the incidence angles by the bank then requires
M=2\pi/\Delta_{\theta}\approx 75
templates. Having degenerate (affected in the same epoch, but spatially separated) sensors improves the angular resolution. It is worth emphasizing that the derived angular resolution is a pure consequence of the line-network encounter geometry and it does not account for the intrinsic sensor noise, see Refs.
Roberts et al. 2018
;
Panelli et al. 2020
, which will worsen the angular resolution (
19
).

Angular resolution of a geographically-distributed network has been considered in several papers. For example,
in Ref.
Dailey et al. 2021
, there is a brief discussion on the angular resolution for bursts of exotic fields propagating through a network of quantum sensors. Their discussion suggests that the angular resolution is roughly the ratio of the temporal resolution
\Delta_{t}
to the field burst propagation time through the sensor network (
2R/v_{\perp}
), in agreement with
our formula (
19
) in the limit of high sampling rates,
\Delta_{t}\ll 2R/v_{\perp}
. We also refer the reader to papers
Monnier 2003

Romano and Cornish 2017
discussing “Rayleigh criterion” angular resolution for a network of interferometers.

Now we find the resolution in normal velocities
v_{\perp}
. We apply the same logic of finding the minimum increment
\Delta_{v}
so that the sweeps with two velocities
v_{\perp}
and
v_{\perp}+\Delta_{v}
map into two distinct templates.
Since the number of sensors and time sampling is finite, vanishingly small variations in the normal velocity would produce identical templates. A distinct new template is generated when the normal velocity is increased by a critical value
\Delta v
,

\Delta_{v}\approx\frac{v_{\perp}^{2}\Delta_{t}}{2R}.

(20)

This velocity resolution improves for slower sweeps, as the template spreads over more epochs, see Eq. (
17
).

We will return to this toy problem in the context of computing template bank covariance matrices in Sec.
IV.1

III.2
GPS.DM search

Signal templates in the GPS.DM search are determined by the particular DM model used (monopoles, strings, domain walls, etc…) and the parameters associated with the event. For concreteness, we focus on DM wall events, which are observed as a sweep through the geographically-distributed network, with a given sensor perturbed when the wall overlaps with the sensor. The relevant parameters for a particular event are the speed, incident direction, time of the event, and the thickness of the wall. As in the toy problem, only the normal component of the sweep velocity matters, however the velocity vector now spans 3D. This parameter space is continuous and it is necessary to discretize it to keep the number of templates finite. We discuss the discretization strategies in Sec.
III.3
.

The standard halo model (SHM) dictates the necessary parameter prior distributions. For DM objects the velocity distribution in the halo frame is quasi-Maxwellian and isotropic with dispersion
v\approx 300\,\mathrm{km/s}

Bovy and Tremaine 2012
and with a cutoff set by the escape velocity of
v_{\mathrm{esc}}\approx 550\,\mathrm{km/s}
.
Additionally, the solar system travels through the halo at galactic velocities of
v_{g}\approx 200\,\mathrm{km/s}
toward the Cygnus constellation,
implying that over
90\%
of DM sweeps come from the forward facing hemisphere
Roberts et al. 2017
. In the GPS.DM search, we are interested in the normal velocity of the DM wall in the Earth Centered Inertial (ECI) frame. In Fig.
3
we reproduce the normal velocity distribution for DM walls bound by the galaxy in the ECI frame
Roberts et al. 2017
. The maximum normal velocity,
v_{\perp}
, in this frame is the addition of the DM escape velocity and the velocity of the solar system,
v_{\perp}<v_{\mathrm{esc}}+v_{g}\approx 770\,\mathrm{km/s}
. As previously stated, the minimum normal speed that can be resolved must be larger than the velocities of the GPS satellites
v_{a}\approx 5\,\mathrm{km/s}
. This comes from the requirement that we can fix the positions of orbiting satellites for a given time window. Similar to the toy problem of Sec.
III.1
, the event parameters determine in which order the sensors are swept and the overall sweep time. These characteristics distinguish the templates within the signal template bank.

The GPS data-streams from satellite clocks are given as time measurement biases with with respect to a fixed terrestrial reference sensor (clock)
R
. DM-induced sensor bias (phase) of a given sensor
a
is proportional to an integral of the DM-induced frequency shift
Derevianko and Pospelov 2014a
. Since the clock biases are a subject to a random-walk noise, we whiten the data by differencing the data streams.

Both satellite and reference clocks are affected by the DM sweeps. In general, sensitivity of the clock to the variation of fundamental constants depends on the clock atom. The GPS
constellation employs mostly Rb and a few Cs clocks. A precision reference clock can be either an H-maser or a Rb clock (see Ref.
Roberts et al. 2017
for details) and one can choose among several terrestrial clocks to serve as the reference clock. To simplify our consideration, we assume that we are only dealing with a network of Rb clocks (including the reference clock). Then the DM object affects each clock with the same signal amplitude
h
. This is the limiting case of a homogeneous network. To bring out
the essentials, we further focus on “thin” walls characterized by an interaction time with each sensor (clock) being much shorter than the sampling time interval
\Delta_{t}
. This reduces the perturbation of atomic frequencies of each clock to Kronecker deltas.

In general, the order in which the sensors are swept by the DM object depends on the geometry of the network. However, for a terrestrial network, or when the reference clock role is assigned to one of the satellite clocks, one can not ascertain which sensors (reference or network) would be affected first in the window. III.3
Strategies in choosing template banks

Choosing templates in the template bank is a trade-off between computational cost and the false-negative probability of missing the sought signal. Indeed, one would like to provide dense coverage of the transient parameter space to minimize false negatives, however increasing the number of templates
M
raises computational cost of the search. We address this question in this section and refine some of the statements further in the text.

There is a natural network resolution-limited choice of templates.
For example, in the GPS.DM search for thin walls, Sec.
III.2
, one may visualize the template parameter space (normal speed, polar and azimuthal angles of incidence for DM wall) as points in 3D space. Network resolutions (
\Delta_{v}
and
\Delta_{\theta}
, Eqs. (
24
,
23
)) determine a small “resolution volume” enclosing each point in this continuous space. Points within the “resolution volume” lead to identical templates.
Thereby, the resolutions
\Delta_{\theta}
and
\Delta_{v}
effectively determine the grid
in this 3D parameter space. As an illustration, consider a thin wall sweeping the GPS constellation at the most probable normal speed
v_{\perp}=209\,\mathrm{km/s}
(from Fig.
3
). Then for
\Delta_{t}=30\,\mathrm{s}
and
2R\approx 5\times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{km}
, the normal speed resolution is
\Delta_{v}\approx 26\,\mathrm{km/s}
and an angular resolution in both polar and azimuthal angles of incidence is
\Delta_{\theta}\approx 7.2^{\circ}
. Complete resolution-limited coverage in normal speed coordinate requires 68 points (with
25\,\mathrm{km/s}\leq v_{\perp}<760\,\mathrm{km/s}
; the grid is non-uniform because
\Delta_{v}
depends on
v_{\perp}
). For a fixed value of speed
v_{\perp}=209\,\mathrm{km/s}
, 25 points for the polar angle and 50 points for the azimuthal angle are required for resolution-limited angular coverage. Covering the entire 3D cube in the template parameter space requires
\sim 2\times 10^{4}
points. This is still computationally prohibitive in our experience.

Since the most important criteria in choosing the bank is reducing the probability of false negatives, the discussed resolution-limited bank construction can be further augmented by the importance sampling technique
Rubinstein 1981
, where the parameter priors determine the density of sampling the template parameter space. For example, in the GPS.DM search, the normal velocity distribution, shown in Fig.
3
,
is a peaked distribution. Thus we need more points on the
v_{\perp}
grid near the maximum of the distribution
and less points in the tails to maintain the probability of false negatives uniform throughout the bank.
This can be accomplished by using a non-linear grid or using Monte-Carlo sampling. To optimize the resulting grid, we would further limit the spacing between the velocity grid points to be greater than the resolution
\Delta_{v}
.
The angular grids can be optimized with symmetry-adapted choice of template bank that takes advantage of the network symmetry, see Sec.
IV.1
.

Ref.
Cannon et al. 2010
addressed a similar question of bank optimization in the context of gravitational wave searches.
They proposed to optimize the bank using the singular value decomposition technique. However, these authors dealt with a single sensor (not a network) and it remains to be seen if their technique can be generalized to the network search.

An issue related to the network resolution, is the degree of similarity between the templates. This degree of similarity will be quantified in the following Sec.
IV
with cross-template correlation coefficients. The generated bank can be optimized further based on this consideration. We also remind the reader that the SNR-max statistic is the quantitative measure governing the results of the search. The choice of template bank affects the SNR-max PDF, CDF, and threshold values for false positives. These characteristics of the SNR-max detection statistic is the focus of the remainder of this paper.

IV
Template bank covariance matrix

Consider a template bank comprised of
M
templates.
Since the same data,
\mathbf{d}^{w}
, is used to compute the SNR (
3
)
\rho_{k}
for each template,
\mathbf{s}_{k}
, then in general, different template SNRs are correlated, i.e. cross-template correlation coefficients
\Sigma_{ij}\equiv\langle\rho_{i}\rho_{j}\rangle\neq 0
.
Because each SNR is a Gaussian random variable with zero mean, see Sec.
II
,
their joint PDF
f\left(\bm{\rho}|\mathbf{\Sigma}\right)
is given by the multivariate normal distribution uniquely specified by the
M
-dimensional template bank covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}
,

f\left(\bm{\rho}|\mathbf{\Sigma}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det(2\pi\bm{\Sigma}})}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\rho}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{\rho}\right)\,.

(26)

Here
\bm{\rho}=\left(\rho_{1},...,\rho_{M}\right)^{T}
and
\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}
is the inverse of the covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
.

We compute matrix elements of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
in Appendix
A
. The result reads

\Sigma_{ij}=\frac{\mathbf{s}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{s}_{j}}{\sqrt{\left(\mathbf{s}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{s}_{i}\right)\left(\mathbf{s}_{j}^{T}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{s}_{j}\right)}}\,,

(27)

where the subscripts
i
and
j
identify templates. This expression depends only on the templates and the noise covariance matrix
\mathbf{E}
. The diagonal matrix elements of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
are equal to 1, in agreement with Ref.
Panelli et al. 2020
. The off-diagonal matrix element vanishes if the templates are orthogonal in the generalized sense,
\mathbf{s}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{s}_{j}=0
. Eq. (
27
) is simply
(\mathbf{s}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{s}_{j})
divided by the generalized norms of
\mathbf{s}_{i}
and
\mathbf{s}_{j}
, where the generalized dot product is understood as
(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b})\equiv\mathbf{a}^{T}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{b}
.

Qualitatively, the cross-template correlations
\Sigma_{ij}
’s quantify the degree of similarity between two templates. If the two templates are completely dis-similar (fully orthogonal),
\Sigma_{ij}=0
. If the two templates are identical (up to an overall sign),
\Sigma_{ij}=\pm 1
. To refine statements of Sec.
III.3
, it is desired to generate banks with the smallest possible values of cross-template correlations to avoid testing data streams against nearly-identical templates. In this sense, the most optimal choice of the bank templates would be a fully-orthogonal bank. However, as we show in Secs.
IV.1
and
IV.2
, constructing a fully-orthogonal bank is not possible for at least some of the applications.

The result (
26
) holds
for any network with stationary white or colored Gaussian noise and with and without cross-node correlations. It also holds for any template bank choice. Namely the properties of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
determine the SNR-max statistic PDF, CDF, and threshold values. In the rest of this section, we illustrate computation and properties of the template bank covariance matrix with our toy problem of Sec.
III.1
and the GPS.DM templates of Sec.
III.2
.

IV.1
Toy problem and symmetry-adapted bank construction

Now we return to the toy problem of searching for line sweeps through a circular
topology network of Sec.
III.1
. In this problem, a template
\mathbf{s}_{i}
is uniquely specified by
(\bm{v}_{\perp})_{i}
, i.e. by the incident direction
\hat{u}_{i}
and the normal speed
(v_{\perp})_{i}>0
of the sweeping line. In component form,

(s_{i})_{l}^{a}=\delta_{l,l_{a}^{i}},

(28)

where
l_{a}^{i}
is the epoch when sensor
a
is crossed by the line moving with
(\bm{v}_{\perp})_{i}
, see Eq. (
16
).

We are interested in properties of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
as these determine SNR-max CDF, PDF and threshold values. The bank covariance matrix elements, Eq. (
27
), simplify to

\Sigma_{ij}=\frac{1}{N_{\rm S}}\sum_{a=1}^{N_{\rm S}}\delta_{l_{a}^{i},l_{a}^{j}}.

(29)

The sum counts the number of sensors that are affected in the same epoch in both templates. Apparently,
\Sigma_{ij}\geq 0
, as a result of the signals being always positive. In addition,
\Sigma_{ij}\leq 1
, with
\Sigma_{ij}
reaching the value of 1 only when both templates are identical.

We fix
v_{\perp}
and discuss selections of incident directions
\hat{u}
of sweeping lines. The
C_{N_{\rm S}}
symmetry of our network suggests a symmetric choice of
\hat{u}_{i}:\hat{u}_{i}||\hat{n}_{a}
, i.e. sweeps towards sensors.
An example for an
M=5
template bank is shown in Fig.
1
(right panel). Because of the rotational symmetry, all templates in the bank can be easily generated by cyclic permutation of sensor labels. For the Fig.
1

2
:
1\rightarrow 3,2\rightarrow 4,...,9\rightarrow 1,10\rightarrow 2
and then swapping the rows of the resulting template, so that the sensors are normally ordered.

We remind the reader that
\mathbf{\Sigma}
is a symmetric matrix, with diagonal elements
\Sigma_{ii}=1
. Based on the rotational symmetry arguments for the constructed bank, we expect all matrix elements
\Sigma_{i,i\pm 1}
to be equal; the same applies to groups of matrix elements
\Sigma_{i,i\pm 2}
,
\Sigma_{i,i\pm 3}
, etc. Here
i\pm n
is understood as mod(
i\pm n,M
). In general,
\Sigma_{i,i\pm 1}\neq\Sigma_{i,i\pm 2}\neq\Sigma_{i,i\pm 3}\neq\ldots
.
Such matrices belong to a class of symmetric Toeplitz matrices that offer several computational advantages. This property is a reflection of the underlying symmetry and would not hold for randomly-selected
{\hat{u}_{i}}
.

Our explicit computations for the
M=5
bank of Fig.
1
with templates of Fig.
2
show that all off-diagonal matrix elements
\Sigma_{i,j\neq i}=1/5
, i.e. all pairwise template overlaps have two simultaneously perturbed sensors. The CDF and PDF of such template bank covariance matrices with
\Sigma_{i,j\neq i}=r
can be treated analytically, see Sec.
V.4
. We refer to such banks as
perfectly-squeezed
. The CDF
C_{M=5}(Z|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r=1/5})
for such a bank is given by Eq. (
65
).

Now we expand our bank of Fig.
1
to include all
N_{\rm S}=10
possible incident directions towards sensors. The discussed
M=5
bank in Fig.
1
is just a subset of this
M=10
bank. We find that all off-diagonal matrix elements of the bank covariance matrix between the Fig.
1
bank and newly added templates vanish.
The bank breaks into two mutually orthogonal sub-banks.
Thus
\mathbf{\Sigma}
assumes a block diagonal form with
\Sigma_{i,j\neq i}=1/5
for each block. Thereby, the joint probability distribution (
26
) factorizes and
the resulting SNR-max CDF is the product

C_{M=10}(Z|\mathbf{\Sigma})=\\
C_{M=5}(Z|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r=1/5})C_{M=5}(Z|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r=1/5}).

We may continue the process of building the symmetry-adapted bank by augmenting the

The GPS constellation, discussed in the following section, unfortunately, lacks point symmetries and a different approach is required.

V
SNR-max distribution for a template bank

To reiterate, at this point we have discussed the properties of templates and the template bank covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}
. Now we return to the discussion of the SNR-max statistic and
derive its distributions. The starting point is the joint probability distribution (
26
) for SNRs in the template bank.
The joint PDF is fully characterized by the template bank covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}
,
which is a symmetric and positive semi-definite matrix with diagonal matrix elements
\Sigma_{ii}=1
. Off-diagonal matrix elements of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
are given by Eq. (
27
).
In this section, we determine an analytic form for the CDF and PDF of the SNR-max statistic (
6
),

z=\max(|\rho_{1}|,..,|\rho_{M}|)
.
Apparently,
z\geq 0
.

We start the derivation by computing the CDF of the SNR-max statistic.
CDF
C_{M}\left(Z|\bm{\Sigma}\right)

is the probability that the SNR-max statistic
z
takes a value less than or equal to
Z
. The condition
z\leq Z
implies that all the individual template SNRs lie in the interval
-Z\leq\rho_{k}\leq Z
.
Thereby, we can express the CDF as an integral of the joint probability distribution (
26
),

\displaystyle C_{M}\left(Z|\bm{\Sigma}\right)=

(35)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det(2\pi\bm{\Sigma})}}\int_{-Z}^{+Z}d\rho_{1}...\int_{-Z}^{+Z}d\rho_{M}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\rho}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{\rho}\right)\,.

This
M
-dimensional integral can be visualized as an integral of a multivariate normal distribution over an
M
-dimensional hyper-cube centered at
0
and having a side of
2Z
.

To date there is no closed form for the CDF of a multivariate normal distribution, so the
M
-dimensional integral requires numerical evaluation (typically using Monte-Carlo techniques
Peter Lepage 1978
;
Press and Farrar 1990
).
For dynamically evolving networks, such as
the GPS constellation where satellites are moving, this approach is
computationally prohibitive, as templates change with the network geometry, and have to be generated anew for every window.
Every newly generated template bank would have a different template bank covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
, new CDF, and a new threshold for false-positives. Partially, this problem can be mitigated by observing that there is a 12-hour periodicity in the satellite orbits, i.e. network geometry repeats every 12 hours. Then the computations can be reduced by storing template banks and threshold values for the 12 hour period.
Even with this shortcut, one still has to deal with a collection of 1440 template banks for individual
\Delta_{t}=30\,\mathrm{s}
epochs.

The cumulative distribution can be expressed using the underlying probability density
p_{M}\left(\rho|\bm{\Sigma}\right)
,

C_{M}\left(Z|\bm{\Sigma}\right)=\int_{0}^{Z}p_{M}\left(z|\mathbf{\Sigma}\right)dz
,
or

p_{M}\left(z|\mathbf{\Sigma}\right)=\left(\frac{d}{dZ}C_{M}\left(Z|\mathbf{\Sigma}\right)\right)_{Z\rightarrow z}.

(36)

We carry out the differentiation of the SNR-max CDF (
37
) in Appendix
C
. The resulting SNR-max PDF reads

\displaystyle p_{M}\left(z|\bm{\Sigma}\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\sqrt{\det\left(2\pi\bm{\Sigma}\right)}}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\left({\displaystyle\prod\limits_{n=1,n\neq k}^{M}}\int_{-z}^{+z}d\rho_{n}\right)\times

(37)

\displaystyle\times\left[\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\rho}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{\rho}\right)\right]_{\rho_{k}=z}.

In general, this SNR-max PDF is sufficient, yet it still involves
M
evaluations of
(M-1)
-dimensional integrals. Thus, for large banks this PDF
still requires computationally-expensive numerical evaluation.

These considerations force us to explore approximate techniques for evaluating SNR-max CDF and PDF. In Sec.
V.1
we review the SNR-max CDF and PDF for uncorrelated templates or fully-orthogonal bank (
\bm{\Sigma}=\bm{I}
).
In Sec.
V.2
, we consider a simple case of two correlated templates. In Sec.
V.3
we derive an approximate SNR-max PDF and CDF for a nearly-orthogonal template bank, i.e. when off-diagonal
|\Sigma_{ij}|\ll 1
. Lastly, in Sec.
V.4
we consider the SNR-max PDF and CDF for a “squeezed” template bank, when off-diagonal matrix elements
\Sigma_{ij}\approx r
for all templates.

Finally, for completeness, we mention an apparent simplification when
\bm{\Sigma}
has a block-diagonal form, a case encountered in the toy problem, Sec.
IV.1
. This happens when the template bank can be decomposed into mutually orthogonal sub-banks of dimensions
M^{\prime}
and
M^{\prime\prime}
,
M^{\prime}+M^{\prime\prime}=M
.
If
\bm{\Sigma}
contains two blocks
\bm{\Sigma}^{\prime}
and
\bm{\Sigma}^{\prime\prime}
, spanning SNR sub-spaces
\bm{\rho}^{\prime}
and
\bm{\rho}^{\prime\prime}
, the joint PDF (
26
) factorizes
f\left(\bm{\rho}|\mathbf{\Sigma}\right)=f\left(\bm{\rho}^{\prime}|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\prime}\right)f\left(\bm{\rho}^{\prime\prime}|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\prime\prime}\right)
. Then the CDF factorizes as well,

C_{M}\left(Z|\bm{\Sigma}\right)=C_{M^{\prime}}\left(Z|\bm{\Sigma}^{\prime}\right)C_{M^{\prime\prime}}\left(Z|\bm{\Sigma}^{\prime\prime}\right)\,.

V.1
Fully-orthogonal bank (independent templates)

For a fully-orthogonal bank, all the off-diagonal matrix elements of the template bank covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
vanish and
\bm{\Sigma}
reduces to an identity matrix
\bm{I}
. Then the general SNR-max CDF (
35
) is simply

\displaystyle C_{M}\left(Z|\bm{I}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left(2\pi\right)^{M/2}}\left(\int_{-Z}^{+Z}d\rho\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\rho^{2}\right)\right)^{M}

(38)

\displaystyle=\left[\text{erf}\left(\frac{Z}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right]^{M}\,,

where Gaussian error function
\mathrm{erf}\left(x\right)=(2/\sqrt{\pi})\int_{0}^{x}du\,e^{-u^{2}}
.
Further, differentiating CDF (
38
) with respect to
Z
, we arrive at the SNR-max PDF for a bank of
M
independent templates

p_{M}\left(z|\bm{I}\right)=M\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}e^{-\frac{z^{2}}{2}}\left(\text{erf}\left(\frac{z}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right)^{M-1}.

(39)

These results subsume the limiting case of the
M=1
bank. In this case, we are dealing with a single template, so that the SNR-max statistic (
6
),
z=|\rho|
, is an absolute value of the SNR for that one template. From the SNR distribution (
5
) we conclude that
z
is a half-normally distributed variable with probability distribution

p_{1}(z)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}e^{-\frac{z^{2}}{2}}\,.

(40)

Integrating it, we obtain CDF
C_{1}(Z)=\mathrm{erf}\left(Z/\sqrt{2}\right)
. These results are in agreement with Eqs. (
38
,
39
) for
M=1
.

V.2
Bank of
M=2

With the results for a fully-orthogonal bank established, we start quantifying the role of cross-template correlations with an analytically tractable case of a bank consisting of two templates.
For the

\Sigma_{12}=\Sigma_{21}\equiv r

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}

\displaystyle\bm{=}\left(\begin{array}[c]{cc}1&r\\

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-r^{2}}\left(\begin{array}[c]{cc}1&-r\\

26

Applying Eq. (
37

\displaystyle p_{2}\left(z|\,r\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}e^{-\frac{z^{2}}{2}}\times

(45)

\displaystyle\left(\text{erf}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\frac{1+r}{1-r}}z\right)+\text{erf}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\frac{1-r}{1+r}}z\right)\right).

V.3
SNR-max distribution for a nearly-orthogonal bank

Suppose that the correlations between various templates are small, i.e.
|\Sigma_{ij}|\ll 1
for all
i\neq j
, such that the template covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}
can be represented as a sum of the identity matrix
\bm{I}
(fully-orthogonal template bank) and a perturbation matrix
\bm{A}
.
In this case, the bulk of the SNR-max CDF and PDF is determined by the
distributions for fully-orthogonal bank,
Eqs. (
38
,
39
), with corrections that depend on
\bm{A}
:

\displaystyle C_{M}\left(Z|\bm{\Sigma}\right)\approx

\displaystyle C_{M}\left(Z|\bm{\Sigma}=\bm{I}\right)+\delta_{I}C_{M}\left(Z|\bm{\Sigma}\right)\,,

(50)

\displaystyle p_{M}\left(z|\bm{\Sigma}\right)\approx

\displaystyle p_{M}\left(z|\bm{\Sigma=I}\right)+\delta_{I}p_{M}\left(z|\bm{\Sigma}\right)\,.

(51)

Here we use the symbol
\delta_{I}
to emphasize that the expansion is about
\bm{\Sigma}=\bm{I}
.
In Appendix
D
, we demonstrate that these corrections are proportional to the trace of
{\bm{A}^{2}}
. Below we summarize the derivation and results from Appendix
D
.

The general expression for the SNR-max CDF (
35
) depends on the determinant and the inverse of the template bank covariance matrix.
The determinant can be approximated as

\det\left(\bm{I}+\bm{A}\right)\approx 1-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}{\left(\bm{A}^{2}\right)}.

(52)

Here we omitted terms containing
\mathrm{Tr}{\left(\bm{A}\right)}=0
as, by construction, the diagonal matrix elements of the perturbation matrix
\bm{A}
vanish. Notice that
\mathrm{Tr}{\left(\bm{A}^{2}\right)}=\mathrm{Tr}(\bm{\Sigma}^{2})-M
.
Similarly, the inverse of
\bm{\Sigma}
can be approximated as

\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}=\left(\bm{I}+\bm{A}\right)^{-1}\approx\bm{I}-\bm{A}+\bm{A}^{2}.

(53)

Based on these approximations and Taylor expansion of the exponential in the SNR-max CDF (
35
), in Appendix
D
we derived the correction to the SNR-max CDF,

\displaystyle\delta_{I}C_{M}\left(Z|\bm{\Sigma}\right)

\displaystyle=

(54)

\displaystyle\left(\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\Sigma}^{2})-M\right)\frac{Z^{2}e^{-Z^{2}}}{2\pi}\left[\text{erf}\left(\frac{Z}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right]^{M-2}\,.

Similarly, the correction to the SNR-max PDF is found by taking the derivative of the above correction to the CDF, leading to

\displaystyle\delta_{I}p_{M}\left(z|\bm{\Sigma}\right)=\left(\mathrm{Tr}(\bm{\Sigma}^{2})-M\right)\frac{ze^{-z^{2}}}{\pi}\left(\operatorname{erf}\left(\frac{z}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right)^{M-3}

\displaystyle\times\quad\left[\frac{(M-2)ze^{-z^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}+\left(1-z^{2}\right)\operatorname{erf}\left(\frac{z}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right]\,.

(55)

The advantage of these approximate formulas is that the multi-dimensional integrations in
the general SNR-max CDF and PDF expressions
(
35
,
37
) have been carried out explicitly. If the bank changes due to time evolution of network geometry, e.g., for orbiting satellites, only
\mathrm{Tr}(\bm{\Sigma}^{2})
needs to be reevaluated.
There is, however, a region of validity of these approximations (see Appendix
D
),

z\ll 1/\sqrt{\max\left|A_{ij}\right|}\,,

(56)

which limits practical applicability of these approximations.

As a test of these approximate formulae, we consider a limiting case of two-template bank, see Sec.
V.2
, with the closed-form SNR-max PDF (
45
). In this case,
\mathrm{Tr}(\bm{\Sigma}^{2})-M=2r^{2}
and Eq. (
V.3
) becomes

\delta_{I}p_{2}\left(z|r\right)=2r^{2}\left(1-z^{2}\right)\frac{ze^{-z^{2}}}{\pi}\,.

(57)

This result is in agreement with the small-
r
expansion of Eq. (
45
).

V.4
SNR-max distribution for a squeezed template bank

In Sec.
IV
we encountered a practical search scenario when matrix elements of the bank covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}
are centered about a non-zero value
r
(see Fig.
6
).
Motivated by this scenario, in this section we consider an approximation for the SNR-max CDF when the off-diagonal matrix elements of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
exhibit small deviations from some value
r
. (As follows from the derivation of this section, the optimal choice of
r
corresponds to the average of off-diagonal matrix elements of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
.) This is the case of squeezed banks discussed in Sec.
IV
. Then
\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r}+\delta\mathbf{\Sigma}
,
where the perturbation matrix
\delta\mathbf{\Sigma}
is “small” compared to the highly-structured reference covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r}
. To the leading order in
\delta\mathbf{\Sigma}
, the SNR-max CDF
C_{M}(Z|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r}+\delta\mathbf{\Sigma})
is determined by
C_{M}(Z|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r})
.
Similar arguments apply to PDF
p_{M}(Z|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r})
.
Below, we evaluate these leading order
contributions, leaving the cumbersome derivation of the

\delta\mathbf{\Sigma}
-correction for future work.

Explicitly, we assume that matrix elements of the reference matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r}
are given by

\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r}\right)_{ij}=\delta_{ij}+(1-\delta_{ij})r\,,

(58)

i.e., all off-diagonal matrix elements of
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r}
are identical and equal to
r
. For example,
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r}
for
M=3
reads

\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r}=\left(\begin{array}[c]{ccc}1&r&r\\
r&1&r\\
r&r&1\end{array}\right)\,.

(59)

In Sec.
V.2
we considered a special case of such matrices for
M=2
.
It is the highly-structured nature of
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r}
that allows us to derive a closed-form expression for the SNR-max CDF
C_{M}(Z|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r})
below.

We derived the inverse of
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r}
entering the multivariate normal distribution.
The inverse retains the general structure of
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r}
with matrix elements

\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r}^{-1}\right)_{ij}=X\delta_{ij}+Y\,,

(60)

where

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=1/(1-r)\,,

(61)

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=-\frac{r}{1+(M-1)r}X\,.

(62)

The derived inverse
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r}^{-1}
reproduces that for the
M=2
case, Eq. (
V.2
).
With the matrix inverse (
60
), the joint PDF (
26
) reads

\displaystyle f\left(\bm{\rho}|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det(2\pi\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r})}}\times

(63)

\displaystyle\exp{\left\{-\frac{1}{2(1-r)}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{M}\rho^{2}_{i}-\frac{r}{1+(M-1)r}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{M}\rho_{i}\right)^{2}\right)\right\}}\,,

with
\det(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r})=\left(1-r\right)^{M-1}\left(1+(M-1)r\right)
.
This joint PDF is equivalent to

\displaystyle f\left(\bm{\rho}|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r}\right)=\frac{1}{(2\pi(1-r))^{M/2}}\times

(64)

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}du\quad\frac{e^{-u^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\prod_{i=1}^{M}\exp\left(-\frac{\left(\rho_{i}-\sqrt{r}u\right)^{2}}{2(1-r)}\right)\,,

which can be verified by direct integration. Remarkably, this integral representation has the advantage of decoupling the SNRs
\rho_{i}
.

The properties of multi-variate normal distribution for the class of covariance matrices represented by Eq. (
58
) have been considered in the literature (see Ref.
Tong 1990
and references therein). We warn the reader, however, that the literature contains misprints (for example, Eq. (
63
) has misprints in Ref.
Tong 1990
), uses different notation, and proceeds through the less transparent (at least to our taste) “exchangeable-normal-variables” proofs.

The CDF
C_{M}(Z|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r})
is still an
M
-dimensional integral (
35
) of the multivariate normal distribution (
63
). With the alternative representation (
64
) of the joint PDF, this integral can be reduced to a one-dimensional integral,

\displaystyle C_{M}(Z|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r})=\frac{1}{2^{M}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}du\,\frac{e^{-u^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\times

(65)

\displaystyle\left[\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{Z+\sqrt{r}u}{\sqrt{2(1-r)}}\right)-\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{-Z+\sqrt{r}u}{\sqrt{2(1-r)}}\right)\right]^{M}.

This CDF is equivalent to the result of Ref.
Tong 1990
expressed in a different notation. For
r=0
, we recover the CDF for independent templates, Eq. (
38
).

Differentiating this CDF with respect to
Z
, we determine the SNR-max PDF

\displaystyle p_{M}(z|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r})=\frac{1}{2^{M}}\frac{M}{\pi}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-r^{2}}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}du\,e^{-u^{2}/2}\times

(66)

\displaystyle\left[\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{z+\sqrt{r}u}{\sqrt{2(1-r)}}\right)-\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{-z+\sqrt{r}u}{\sqrt{2(1-r)}}\right)\right]^{M-1}

\displaystyle\times\left[\exp\left(-\frac{(z+\sqrt{r}u)^{2}}{2(1-r)}\right)+\exp\left(-\frac{(z-\sqrt{r}u)^{2}}{2(1-r)}\right)\right]\,.

Given the bank covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
, what is the optimal choice of
r
? The answer to this question is not obvious - there are many possibilities like taking
r
as the most probable value, or the median, or the average of the
\Sigma_{i\neq j}
matrix elements distribution. Below we show that
the optimal choice of
r
is the mean value of off-diagonal matrix elements of
the bank covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
. Indeed, consider the small-
\delta\mathbf{\Sigma}
expansion of the determinant normalizing the joint probability distribution,

\displaystyle\det(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r}+\delta\mathbf{\Sigma})\approx

\displaystyle\left(1+\mathrm{Tr}{(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r}^{-1}\delta\mathbf{\Sigma})}\right)\det\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(1+Y\sum_{ij}(\delta\mathbf{\Sigma})_{ij}\right)\det\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r}\,,

where
Y
is the off-diagonal matrix element of
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}_{r}
given in Eq. (
62
). To suppress the
\delta\mathbf{\Sigma}
correction, we have to require that
r
is chosen in such a way that
\sum_{ij}(\delta\mathbf{\Sigma})_{ij}=0
. This condition leads to the optimal choice

r=\frac{1}{M(M-1)}\sum_{i>j}\Sigma_{ij}\,,

(67)

corresponding to the mean value of off-diagonal matrix elements of the bank covariance matrix.

Threshold values for specific values of false positive rates for squeezed template banks are discussed in Sec.
VI
.

VI
Threshold values for the SNR-max statistic

Practical applications require certain level of confidence in the detection statistic. Typically, confidence levels such as
90\%,95\%
, and
99\%
are suitable for many applications requiring less sensitivity far into the tails of a distribution. Threshold values are determined by false positive rates,
q
, and for the enumerated confidence levels,
q
values are
0.1,0.05
, and
0.01
, respectively. The meaning of a false positive rate is the probability of falsely accepting the hypothesis in question, when the data should have supported the null hypothesis. In the context of searches for transients, a false positive happens when the SNR-max statistic
z
exceeds a certain threshold
Z^{\ast}
, but this in fact, is due to statistical randomness mimicking the transient and not due to the sought exotic physics.
Suppose for a desired false positive rate,
q
, we want to determine the threshold value
Z^{\ast}
associated with that false positive rate. We may simply relate the false positive rate to the threshold value by using the CDF

1-q=C_{M}\left(Z^{\ast}|\bm{\Sigma}\right).

(68)

As
C_{M}\left(Z|\bm{\Sigma}\right)
is a monotonically increasing
function of
Z
,
Z^{\ast}
increases for decreasing rate
q
.

Even without explicit computations, we qualitatively expect that for a fixed false-positive rate
q
,

(i)

Threshold value
Z^{\ast}
increases with the increasing size
M
of template bank.

(ii)

Threshold value
Z^{\ast}
decreases with the growing cross-template correlations.

Indeed, the random data have more chance to match one of the templates in a larger template bank leading to a higher threshold value, supporting statement (i). As to the statement (ii), suppose we keep the bank size
M
the same, but increase one of the cross-template correlation coefficients
\Sigma_{ij}
all the way to 1. Then the two templates
i
and
j
become identical,
effectively reducing the bank to
M-1
templates. Then we can apply the statement (i) resulting in
Z^{\ast}
being lowered. In particular, this means that for a fixed rate of false positives, a bank of correlated templates
has a lower SNR-max threshold than a fully-orthogonal bank (uncorrelated or independent templates.)

Evaluation of threshold values for a desired false positive rates for a large bank with arbitrary bank covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
requires Monte-Carlo integration of an
M
-dimensional multivariate distribution, see Sec.
V
. While Monte-Carlo integration can yield the desired threshold value for any
\bm{\Sigma}
and dimensionality
M
, the computational cost can be prohibitive when
M
is large and when sampling far into the tail of the distribution, as needed for small false positive rates. Therefore, in this section we present several analytical results that avoid or greatly reduce numerical costs.

For example, if the
M
templates in the bank are fully orthogonal, i.e.
\bm{\Sigma}=\bm{I}
, the analytic CDF is given by Eq. (
38
) and thresholds are

Z^{*}=\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{erf}^{-1}\left((1-q)^{1/M}\right)\,.

(69)

This formula illustrates our qualitative statement (i). For
q\ll 1
,
Z^{*}

is given by the conventional threshold formula for a Gaussian distribution,

Z^{*}=\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{erf}^{-1}\left(1-q_{\mathrm{eff}}\right)
with

q_{\mathrm{eff}}=q/M
. As
M
is increased,
q_{\mathrm{eff}}
decreases, resulting in larger values of
Z^{*}
.

The discussion for the case of the two correlated template SNR-max CDF in Sec.
V.2
suggests that for a fixed value of
q
, the threshold value
Z^{\ast}
moves to lower
values the more correlated the templates are,

Z^{\ast}\left(r=1\right)<Z^{\ast}\left(r\right)<Z^{\ast}\left(r=0\right).

(70)

, which is in agreement with the qualitative statement (ii).

VI.2
Threshold for a nearly-orthogonal template bank

Suppose we can represent the bank covariance matrix as a sum of the reference matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}
and a small correction
\delta\mathbf{\Sigma}
. This is the case for a nearly-orthogonal template bank of Sec.
V.3
, where
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}=\mathbf{I}
, and for a “squeezed” bank of Sec.
V.3
,
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r}
. Then
the thresholds for specific false positive rates can be calculated in a perturbative fashion. The dominant term in calculating the threshold value is determined by the CDF
C_{M}(Z^{*}|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0})
with a corrective term
\delta C_{M}(Z^{*}|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0})
,

C_{M}(Z|\mathbf{\Sigma})=C_{M}(Z|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0})+\delta C_{M}(Z|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0})\,.

(71)

The threshold is determined from Eq. (
68
),
1-q=C_{M}(Z^{*}|\mathbf{\Sigma})
, with
Z^{*}=Z_{0}^{*}+\delta Z^{*}
. Here
Z_{0}^{*}
is the reference threshold and
\delta Z^{*}
is the correction to the threshold. The reference threshold satisfies the equation
1-q=C_{M}(Z^{*}_{0}|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0})
.
We would like to find the correction to the threshold
\delta Z^{*}
, so that

1-q=C_{M}(Z^{*}_{0}+\delta Z^{*}|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0})+\delta C_{M}(Z^{*}_{0}|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0})\,.

(72)

Explicitly,

\displaystyle C_{M}(Z^{*}_{0}+\delta Z^{*}|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0})

\displaystyle\approx C_{M}(Z^{*}_{0}|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0})+\delta Z^{*}\times\frac{\partial C_{M}}{\partial Z}

\displaystyle=C_{M}(Z^{*}_{0}|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0})+\delta Z^{*}\times p_{M}(Z^{*}_{0}|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}).

From here, the correction to the threshold is

\delta Z^{*}\approx-\frac{\delta C_{M}(Z^{*}_{0}|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0})}{p_{M}(Z^{*}_{0}|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0})}\,.

(73)

Now we apply this formalism to the determination of the threshold for a nearly-orthogonal template bank, i.e.
\bm{\Sigma}_{0}=\bm{I}
. Then
Z^{*}_{0}=\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{erf}^{-1}\left((1-q)^{1/M}\right)
, Eq. (
69
). The corrective CDF term and the SNR-max PDF in Eq. (
73
) are per Sec.
V.3
,

\displaystyle\delta C_{M}(Z_{0}^{*}|\bm{I})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}{\left(\bm{A}^{2}\right)}\frac{Z_{0}^{\ast 2}e^{-Z_{0}^{\ast 2}}}{2\pi}\left[\text{erf}\left(\frac{Z^{\ast}_{0}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right]^{M-2},

\displaystyle p_{M}(Z^{*}_{0}|\bm{I})

\displaystyle=M\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}e^{-\frac{Z_{0}^{\ast 2}}{2}}\text{erf}\left(\frac{Z^{\ast}_{0}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{M-1}\,.

Plugging these into Eq. (
73
), we arrive at the correction to the threshold value,

\delta Z^{*}=-\frac{\mathrm{Tr}{\left(\bm{A}^{2}\right)}}{M}\left[\text{erf}\left(\frac{Z^{\ast}_{0}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right]^{-1}Z_{0}^{\ast 2}e^{-Z_{0}^{\ast 2}}\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2\pi}}.

(74)

Since
\mathrm{Tr}{\left(\bm{A}^{2}\right)}>0
, we see that
\delta Z^{\ast}<0
, so that
Z^{\ast}<Z_{0}^{\ast}
.
This is consistent with our qualitative expectation (ii) that cross-template correlations reduce the threshold values for a given rate of false positives.

We remind the reader that for a nearly-orthogonal bank,
\mathrm{Tr}{\left(\bm{A}^{2}\right)}=\mathrm{Tr}(\bm{\Sigma}^{2})-M
.
The advantage of our approximation (
74
) is that if the bank changes due to time evolution of network geometry, e.g., for orbiting satellites, only
\mathrm{Tr}(\bm{\Sigma}^{2})
needs to be reevaluated to readjust the threshold for the new bank.

VII
Conclusions and the utility of entanglement

The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) statistic and template bank searches have seen a variety of applications in modern physics and other fields. Here we focused on the max-SNR detection statistic in the context of searches for transient signals with networks of precision quantum sensors. For large template banks, especially for dynamically evolving networks,
computation of threshold rates is computationally prohibitive. We developed several approximate methods for computing properties of the
SNR-max statistic that forgo much of the computational costs.
We developed the probability and cumulative distribution for the SNR-max statistic correlated from
M
templates in a template bank. Generically the cross-template correlations give rise to diminished thresholds for desired false positive rates.
While our paper was motivated by dark matter and other exotic physics searches, the results of Secs.
III
—
VI
have a wide range of applicability. We also anticipate that our detailed exposè on template bank construction and optimization in the cases of the pedagogical “toy” network and the GPS network (Sec.
III
) will be useful in the nascent applications of precision quantum sensors and their networks in fundamental physics.

What is the utility of entanglement in the searches for transients? For individual sensors, the entanglement or spin-squeezing is a useful resource, as it improves the accuracy of a single-shot measurement typical of a search for short transients. Cross-node or geographically-distributed entanglement
Kómár et al. 2016
is not useful and is, in fact, detrimental to the network searches for transients. Indeed, projective measurement on a single node collapses the distributed wave-function, effectively rendering all other nodes deaf to the transient. Then the network loses both velocity and angular resolution.

Appendix A
Derivation of matrix elements of template bank covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
, Eq. (
27
)

Matrix elements of the template bank covariance matrix are defined as

\Sigma_{ij}=\langle\rho_{i}^{w}\rho_{j}^{w}\rangle\,,

(76)

where SNR statistics
\rho_{i}^{w}
and
\rho_{j}^{w}
are given by Eq. (
3
) and the averaging is over signal-free data. From here on we drop the window superscript
w
. Explicitly,

\langle\rho_{i}\rho_{j}\rangle=\frac{\langle(\mathbf{d}^{T}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{s}_{i})(\mathbf{d}^{T}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{s}_{j})\rangle}{\sqrt{(\mathbf{s}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{s}_{i})(\mathbf{s}_{j}^{T}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{s}_{j})}}\,.

(77)

To carry out the averaging, we expand the products in component form

\mathbf{d}^{T}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{s}_{i}=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}d_{\alpha}E_{\alpha\beta}^{-1}(s_{\beta})_{i},

(78)

where we used Greek letters for compound indices that encode both the sensor and the epoch indexes, see Sec.
II
.
Then the numerator of Eq. (
77
) can be rearranged into

\displaystyle\left\langle\sum_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta}d_{\alpha}E_{\alpha\beta}^{-1}(s_{\beta})_{i}d_{\gamma}E_{\gamma\delta}^{-1}(s_{\delta})_{j}\right\rangle=

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta}\left\langle d_{\alpha}d_{\gamma}\right\rangle E_{\alpha\beta}^{-1}E_{\gamma\delta}^{-1}(s_{\beta})_{i}(s_{\delta})_{j}\,.

(79)

For signal-free data, the combination
\left\langle d_{\alpha}d_{\gamma}\right\rangle
is the matrix element of the noise covariance matrix,

\left\langle d_{\alpha}d_{\gamma}\right\rangle=E_{\alpha\gamma}\,.

This simplifies Eq. (
79
) to

\displaystyle\sum_{\beta,\delta}(s_{\beta})_{i}(s_{\delta})_{j}\sum_{\alpha,\gamma}E_{\alpha\gamma}\left(E^{-1}\right)_{\gamma\delta}\left(E^{-1}\right)_{\alpha\beta}=

(80)

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha,\beta}(s_{\alpha})_{j}\left(E^{-1}\right)_{\alpha\beta}(s_{\beta})_{i}=\mathbf{s}_{j}^{T}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{s}_{i}.

Here we used the identity,

\sum_{\gamma}E_{\alpha\gamma}(E^{-1})_{\gamma\delta}=\bm{I}_{\alpha\delta}=\delta_{\alpha\delta}.

With this simplification, the template bank covariance matrix element (
77
) becomes

\Sigma_{ij}=\frac{\mathbf{s}_{j}^{T}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{s}_{i}}{\sqrt{\left(\mathbf{s}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{s}_{i}\right)\left(\mathbf{s}_{j}^{T}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{s}_{j}\right)}}\,.

(81)

From here, the variances of individual SNR statistics are
\sigma^{2}_{\rho_{i}}=\langle(\rho_{i})^{2}\rangle=\Sigma_{ii}=1
, in agreement with Ref.
Panelli et al. 2020
. This concludes our proof of Eq. (
27
).

Appendix B
Template bank covariance matrix element construction

We are interested in the behavior of the template covariance matrix elements
\Sigma_{ij}
for our generalized signal template construction (Sec.
III
).
To streamline notation, we rename
\mathbf{s}_{i}
and
\mathbf{s}_{j}
to
\mathbf{s}
and
\mathbf{s}^{\prime}
respectively.
The template covariance matrix element (
27

\Sigma_{ss^{\prime}}=\frac{\mathbf{s}^{T}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{s^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{\left(\mathbf{s}^{T}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{s}\right)\left(\mathbf{s^{\prime}}^{T}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{s^{\prime}}\right)}}.

(82)

For concreteness, we focus on a white noise network of identical sensors
a
referenced to a common reference sensor
R
. In this case, the inverse covariance matrix is given by Eq. (
11
), which we reproduce below

\left(E^{-1}\right)_{lm}^{ab}=A\,\delta_{lm}\delta^{ab}+B\,(1-\delta^{ab})\delta_{lm},

(83)

where coefficients

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\frac{1+(1-1/N_{\rm S})\xi}{1+\xi}\,,

(84)

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\frac{1}{N_{\rm S}}\frac{\xi}{1+\xi}

(85)

are expressed in terms of
\xi=N_{\rm S}\sigma_{R}^{2}/\sigma^{2}
.
In our index convention, the letters at the beginning of alphabet
a,b,\ldots
enumerate the sensors (range
\overline{1,N_{\rm S}}
) , while letters in the middle of alphabet
l,m,\ldots
— the epochs (index range
\overline{1,J}
in a data window).

Since we consider the signal amplitude
h
to be the same for the network and reference sensors as described in Sec.
III
, we may simply take out
h
from the templates. Then the GPS.DM templates (thin DM walls) can be represented using Kronecker symbols,

s_{l}^{a}=\delta_{l,l_{a}}+(-1)\delta_{l,l_{R}}\,.

(86)

The signal for each sensor
a
consists of a positive spike at epoch
l_{a}
, and a negative spike at the reference epoch
l_{R}
which is identical for all the templates,
l_{R}=(J+1)/2
. Then, the individual templates are characterized by the set of network sensor perturbation epochs

\{l\}={l_{1},l_{2},...,l_{N_{\rm S}}}\,.

(87)

for the template
\bm{s}
and a similar (but not identical) set
\{l^{\prime}\}
for the template
\bm{s}^{\prime}
.

In general,
l_{a}
can be equal to
l_{R}
, meaning that the sensor
a
and the reference sensor are affected by the transient in the same epoch; in this case the signal reduces to zero,
s_{l}^{a}=0
. In addition, several sensors can be affected by the transient signal in the same epoch. In this case, we refer to this subset of sensors as “degenerate”. When sensor
a
is degenerate with sensor
b
for template
\bm{s}
,
l_{a}=l_{b}
in the set
\{l\}
.

In our derivations below, we will use the fact that
l_{a}
and
l_{R}

\sum_{l}\delta_{l,l_{a}}=\sum_{l}\delta_{l,l_{R}}=1.

(88)

First, consider the numerator of Eq. (
82
),
\mathbf{s}^{T}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{s}^{\prime}
, which can be expanded into

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}^{T}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{s}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

(89)

\displaystyle A\sum_{a,b}^{N_{\rm S}}\sum_{l,m}^{J}s^{a}_{l}\delta^{ab}\delta_{lm}\,s^{\prime b}_{m}+B\sum_{a}^{N_{\rm S}}\sum_{b\neq a}^{N_{\rm S}}\sum_{l,m}^{J}s^{a}_{l}\delta_{lm}s^{\prime b}_{m}.

The first sum can be represented as

\displaystyle\sum_{l,a}s^{a}_{l}s^{\prime a}_{l}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l,a}\left(\delta_{l,l_{a}}-\delta_{l,l_{R}}\right)\left(\delta_{l,l_{a}^{{}^{\prime}}}-\delta_{l,l_{R}}\right)=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{l,a}\delta_{l,l_{a}}\delta_{l,l_{a}^{{}^{\prime}}}\right)+

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{l,a}\delta_{l,l_{R}}-\sum_{l,a}\delta_{l,l_{R}}\delta_{l,l_{a}}-\sum_{l,a}\delta_{l,l_{R}}\delta_{l,l_{a}^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle\equiv K_{1}^{s,s^{\prime}}+R_{1}^{s,s^{\prime}}.

Here we introduced

K_{1}^{s,s^{\prime}}\equiv\sum_{a}\delta_{l_{a},l_{a}^{{}^{\prime}}},

(90)

which counts the number of sensors with a perturbation epoch identical to both templates. If the two templates are identical,
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{s}^{\prime}

K_{1}^{s,s}=N_{\rm S}

Similarly,

R_{1}^{s,s^{\prime}}\equiv N_{\rm S}-\sum_{a}\left(\delta_{l_{R},l_{a}}+\delta_{l_{R},l_{a}^{{}^{\prime}}}\right)

(91)

characterizes the network-reference sensor degeneracy.
If both templates have no degeneracies between network and the reference sensors, then
R_{1}^{s,s^{\prime}}=N_{\rm S}
.

The second sum in Eq. (
89
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\sum_{a}^{N_{\rm S}}\sum_{b\neq a}^{N_{\rm S}}\sum_{l}^{J}s^{a}_{l}s^{\prime b}_{l}=

(92)

\displaystyle\sum_{a}^{N_{\rm S}}\sum_{b\neq a}^{N_{\rm S}}\sum_{l}^{J}\left(\delta_{l,l_{a}}-\delta_{l,l_{R}}\right)\left(\delta_{l,l_{b}^{\prime}}-\delta_{l,l_{R}}\right)=

\displaystyle\sum_{a}^{N_{\rm S}}\sum_{b\neq a}^{N_{\rm S}}\delta_{l_{a},l_{b}^{\prime}}+\sum_{a}^{N_{\rm S}}\sum_{b\neq a}^{N_{\rm S}}(1)-\sum_{a}^{N_{\rm S}}\sum_{b\neq a}^{N_{\rm S}}\delta_{l_{R},l_{a}}-\sum_{a}^{N_{\rm S}}\sum_{b\neq a}^{N_{\rm S}}\delta_{l_{R},l_{b}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\equiv K_{2}^{s,s^{\prime}}+R_{2}^{s,s^{\prime}}\,,

with

\displaystyle K_{2}^{s,s^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{a}^{N_{\rm S}}\sum_{b\neq a}^{N_{\rm S}}\delta_{l_{a},l_{b}^{{}^{\prime}}}\,,

(93)

\displaystyle R_{2}^{s,s^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\equiv N_{\rm S}(N_{\rm S}-1)-\sum_{a}^{N_{\rm S}}\sum_{b\neq a}^{N_{\rm S}}\left(\delta_{l_{R},l_{a}}+\delta_{l_{R},l_{b}^{{}^{\prime}}}\right)

(94)

\displaystyle=(N_{\rm S}-1)R_{1}^{ss^{\prime}}\,.

K_{2}^{s,s^{\prime}}
defines the cross-template network degeneracy between network sensors
a,b\neq a
.
R_{2}^{s,s^{\prime}}
quantifies the cross-template degeneracy between the reference and the network sensors. If there are no degeneracies between the network sensors
a,b\neq a
and the reference sensor
R

R_{2}^{s,s^{\prime}}=N_{\rm S}(N_{\rm S}-1)
.
For two identical templates,
K_{2}^{s,s}\equiv\sum_{a}^{N_{\rm S}}\sum_{b\neq a}^{N_{\rm S}}\delta_{l_{a},l_{b}}
. This definition of
K_{2}^{s,s}
depends on the degeneracy of the network with respect to the template. If, for instance, all the sensors are affected by the transient at different epochs,
l_{a}\neq l_{b}
and
a\neq b
,
K_{2}^{s,s}=0
. Thus
K_{2}^{s,s}
quantifies the degeneracy of the network with respect to template
\mathbf{s}
. Further, if all of the sensors are degenerate (
l_{a}=l_{b}
for all
a,b
) then
K_{2}^{s,s}=N_{\rm S}(N_{\rm S}-1)
.

Combining these results, we arrive at the expression for the
\mathbf{s}^{T}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{s^{\prime}}
product in the numerator
of Eq. (
82
)

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}^{T}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{s^{\prime}}=AK_{1}^{s,s^{\prime}}+CR_{1}^{s,s^{\prime}}+BK_{2}^{s,s^{\prime}}\,,

(95)

where
C=A+(N_{\rm S}-1)B=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{1+\xi}\right)
. The products in the denominator of Eq. (
82
), such as
\mathbf{s}^{T}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{s}
, can be obtained from Eq. (
95
) with substitution
s^{{}^{\prime}}\rightarrow s
.

With Eq. (
95

82
) as

\displaystyle\Sigma_{ss^{\prime}}=

(96)

\displaystyle\frac{AK_{1}^{s,s^{\prime}}+CR_{1}^{s,s^{\prime}}+BK_{2}^{s,s^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{\left(AK_{1}^{s,s}+CR_{1}^{s,s}+BK_{2}^{s,s}\right)(AK_{1}^{s^{\prime},s^{\prime}}+CR_{1}^{s^{\prime},s^{\prime}}+BK_{2}^{s^{\prime},s^{\prime}})}}.

This expression can be simplified further,

\displaystyle\Sigma_{ss^{\prime}}=

(97)

\displaystyle\frac{aK_{1}^{s,s^{\prime}}+R_{1}^{s,s^{\prime}}+bK_{2}^{s,s^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{\left(aK_{1}^{s,s}+R_{1}^{s,s}+bK_{2}^{s,s}\right)\left(aK_{1}^{s^{\prime},s^{\prime}}+R_{1}^{s^{\prime},s^{\prime}}+bK_{2}^{s^{\prime},s^{\prime}}\right)}},

with

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=1+(1-1/N_{\rm S})\xi\,,

(98)

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=-\xi/N_{\rm S}\,.

(99)

Eq. (
B
) is an exact expression for the thin planar transients, such as thin DM walls.

Now we introduce a concept of bank-averaged cross-template correlation coefficient
\overline{\Sigma}_{ss^{\prime}}
. This quantity is useful in quantifying
properties of the SNR-max statistic for squeezed banks of Sec.
V.4
.
We assume that the number of templates is large so that the bank covers the accessible parameter space for the sweep parameters, discussed in Sec.
III
. In addition, we assume that we can average over spatial positions of the nodes, see the uniform-occupancy approximation for a spherically-distributed network of Sec.
III.2
.

With these approximations, we determine the typical values
\bar{K}_{1},\bar{K}_{2}
, and
\bar{R}_{1}
of the degeneracy coefficients. The key to computing these quantities is

25
)

\overline{\Delta l}=2R/(v_{\perp}^{p}\Delta_{t})\,,

(100)

where
v^{p}_{\perp}=209\,\mathrm{km/s}
the most probable speed of the encounter. For GPS.DM data
\overline{\Delta l}=8

III.2

\overline{\Delta l}

Leading to

\displaystyle\bar{K}_{1}^{s,s}

\displaystyle=\bar{K}_{1}^{s^{\prime},s^{\prime}}=N_{\rm S},

\displaystyle\bar{K}_{1}^{s,s^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=N_{\rm S}/\overline{\Delta l},

(101)

\displaystyle\bar{K}_{2}^{s,s}

\displaystyle=\bar{K}_{2}^{s^{\prime},s^{\prime}}=\bar{K}_{2}^{s,s^{\prime}}={N_{\rm S}(N_{\rm S}-1)}/{\overline{\Delta l}},

\displaystyle\bar{R}_{1}^{s,s}

\displaystyle=\bar{R}_{1}^{s^{\prime},s^{\prime}}=\bar{R}_{1}^{s,s^{\prime}}=\left(1-{2}/{\overline{\Delta l}}\right)N_{\rm S}\,.

\displaystyle\overline{\Sigma}_{ss^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2+\xi(1-1/N_{\rm S})}\,.

(102)

For a network of white noise sensors without a reference sensor, such as the GNOME network of magnetometers

a=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}
,
b=0

\displaystyle\overline{\Sigma}_{ss^{\prime}}={1}/{\overline{\Delta l}}\,.

(103)

Appendix C
Derivation of SNR-max PDF, Eq. (
35
)

SNR-max CDF is given by an
M
-dimensional integral over a hyper-cube,

C_{M}\left(Z|\bm{\Sigma}\right)=\int_{-Z}^{+Z}d\rho_{1}...\int_{-Z}^{+Z}d\rho_{M}f\left(\bm{\rho}|\mathbf{\Sigma}\right)\,,

(104)

with joint probability distribution (
26
),

f\left(\bm{\rho}|\mathbf{\Sigma}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det(2\pi\bm{\Sigma}})}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\rho}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{\rho}\right)\,.

(105)

SNR-max PDF, the focus of our derivation, is a derivative of CDF,

p_{M}\left(Z|\mathbf{\Sigma}\right)=\frac{d}{dZ}C_{M}\left(Z|\mathbf{\Sigma}\right)\,.

(106)

To compute this derivative, we apply the Leibniz rule,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dZ}\int_{a(Z)}^{b(Z)}g(u,Z)du=

(107)

\displaystyle=\int_{a(Z)}^{b(Z)}\frac{\partial g(u,Z)}{\partial Z}du+g(b(Z),Z)\frac{db}{dZ}-g(a(Z),Z)\frac{da}{dZ}.

As a preliminary step, we introduce partial integrals of the joint PDF

I_{n}\left(\rho_{1},\ldots\rho_{n};Z\right)\equiv\left(\prod_{k=n+1}^{M}\int_{-Z}^{Z}d\rho_{k}\right)f\left(\bm{\rho}|\mathbf{\Sigma}\right)

(108)

with boundary values
I_{0}(;Z)=C_{M}\left(Z|\mathbf{\Sigma}\right)
and

I_{M}(\bm{\rho};)=f\left(\bm{\rho}|\mathbf{\Sigma}\right)
.
Apparently,

I_{n}\left(\rho_{1},\ldots\rho_{n};Z\right)=\int_{-Z}^{Z}d\rho_{n+1}I_{n+1}\left(\rho_{1},\ldots\rho_{n+1};Z\right)\,.

(109)

As we repeatedly apply the Leibniz rule to the differentiating the CDF (
104
), we encounter the general structure

\displaystyle\left({\prod\limits_{m=1}^{n}{\int\limits_{-Z}^{Z}{d{\rho_{m}}}}}\right)\frac{d}{{dZ}}\left({\prod\limits_{l=n+1}^{M}{\int\limits_{-Z}^{Z}{d{\rho_{l}}}}}\right)f\left(\bm{\rho}|\mathbf{\Sigma}\right)=

\displaystyle=\left({\prod\limits_{m=1}^{k}{\int\limits_{-Z}^{Z}{d{\rho_{m}}}}}\right)\frac{d}{{dZ}}{I_{n}\left(\rho_{1},\ldots\rho_{n};Z\right)},

which can be evaluated as

\begin{array}[]{l}\frac{d}{{dZ}}{I_{n}}\left({{\rho_{1}},\cdots,{\rho_{n}};Z}\right)=\\
\frac{d}{{dZ}}\int_{-Z}^{Z}d{\rho_{n+1}}{I_{n+1}}\left({{\rho_{1}},\cdots,{\rho_{n+1}};Z}\right)\\
=\int_{-Z}^{Z}d{\rho_{n+1}}\frac{d}{{dZ}}{I_{n+1}}\left({{\rho_{1}},\cdots,{\rho_{n+1}};Z}\right)+\\
+{\left.{{I_{n+1}}\left({{\rho_{1}},\cdots,{\rho_{n+1}};Z}\right)}\right|_{{\rho_{n+1}}=Z}}+\\
+{\left.{{I_{n+1}}\left({{\rho_{1}},\cdots,{\rho_{n+1}};Z}\right)}\right|_{{\rho_{n+1}}=-Z}}.\end{array}

Applying this recursion relation to
I_{0}(;Z)=C_{M}\left(Z|\mathbf{\Sigma}\right)
we eventually
reach
I_{M}(\bm{\rho};)=f\left(\bm{\rho}|\mathbf{\Sigma}\right)
for which the derivative with respect to
Z
vanishes. The result reads

\displaystyle p_{M}\left(Z|\bm{\Sigma}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det\left(2\pi\bm{\Sigma}\right)}}\times

(110)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{M}[\left(\int_{-Z}^{+Z}{\prod\limits_{n=1,n\neq k}^{M}}d\rho_{n}\right)\left[\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\rho}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{\rho}\right)\right]_{\rho_{k}=+Z}

\displaystyle+\left(\rho_{k}=-Z\right)].

The quadratic form
\bm{\rho}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{\rho}
is invariant under
\bm{\rho}\rightarrow-\bm{\rho}
. Thus by changing the variables
\bm{\rho}\rightarrow-\bm{\rho}
in the second term we can show that it is equal to the first
term. Thereby, the SNR-max PDF reduces to

\displaystyle p_{M}\left(z|\bm{\Sigma}\right)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\det\left(2\pi\bm{\Sigma}\right)}}\times

(111)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{M}{\prod\limits_{n=1,n\neq k}^{M}}\left(\int_{-z}^{+z}d\rho_{n}\right)\left[\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\rho}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{\rho}\right)\right]_{\rho_{k}=+z}.

This concludes our proof of Eq. (
35
) of the main text.

Appendix D
Derivation of SNR-max CDF and PDF for a nearly-orthogonal bank, Eqs. (
54
,
V.3
)

Suppose that the correlations between the various templates were small, i.e.
|\Sigma_{ij}|\ll 1
for
i\neq j
, such that the template covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}
can be thought of as the identity matrix
\bm{I}
plus some perturbation matrix
\bm{A}
:
\mathbf{\Sigma}=\bm{I}+\bm{A}
.
In this case, the bulk of the SNR-max CDF and PDF is determined by the
distributions for fully-orthogonal bank
with corrections that depend on
\bm{A}
:

\displaystyle C_{M}\left(Z|\bm{\Sigma}\right)\approx

\displaystyle C_{M}\left(Z|\bm{I}\right)+\delta_{I}C_{M}\left(Z|\bm{\Sigma}\right)\,,

(112)

\displaystyle p_{M}\left(z|\bm{\Sigma}\right)\approx

\displaystyle p_{M}\left(z|\bm{I}\right)+\delta_{I}p_{M}\left(z|\bm{\Sigma}\right)\,.

(113)

Here we use the symbol
\delta_{I}
to emphasize that the expansion is about
\bm{\Sigma}=\bm{I}
. The leading terms are given by Eqs. (
38
,
39
), reproduced below,

\displaystyle C_{M}\left(Z|\bm{I}\right)

\displaystyle=\left[\text{erf}\left(\frac{Z}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right]^{M}\,,

(114)

\displaystyle p_{M}\left(z|\bm{I}\right)

\displaystyle=M\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}e^{-\frac{z^{2}}{2}}\left(\text{erf}\left(\frac{z}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right)^{M-1}.

(115)

The goal of this Appendix is to derive the corrections
\delta_{I}C_{M}\left(Z|\bm{\Sigma}\right)
and
\delta_{I}p_{M}\left(z|\bm{\Sigma}\right)
.

The determinant of the template covariance matrix can be approximated as

\det\left(\bm{I}+\bm{A}\right)\approx 1+\mathrm{Tr}{\left(\bm{A}\right)}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathrm{Tr}{\left(\bm{A}\right)}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}{\left(\bm{A}^{2}\right)}+...

(116)

Here the elements of the perturbative matrix
\bm{A}
are

A_{ij}=\left(1-\delta_{ij}\right)\Sigma_{ij}.

(117)

Since the perturbation matrix
\bm{A}
has zeros along the diagonal,
\mathrm{Tr}{\left(\bm{A}\right)}=0
, and the determinant simplifies to

\det\left(\bm{\Sigma}\right)=\det\left(\bm{I}+\bm{A}\right)\approx 1-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}{\left(\bm{A}^{2}\right)}.

(118)

Notice that
\mathrm{Tr}{\left(\bm{A}^{2}\right)}
can be related to
\mathrm{Tr}{\left(\bm{\Sigma}^{2}\right)}
. Indeed,
\bm{\Sigma}^{2}=\bm{I}+2\bm{A}+\bm{A}^{2}
,
so that

\mathrm{Tr}{\left(\bm{A}^{2}\right)}=\mathrm{Tr}{\left(\bm{\Sigma}^{2}\right)}-M\,.

(119)

Also since, by construction,
\bm{A}
is a symmetric matrix,
\mathrm{Tr}{\left(\bm{A}^{2}\right)}=\sum_{ij}\left(A_{ij}\right)^{2}>0
.

As for the inverse of the template covariance matrix, this can also be approximated as

\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}=\left(\bm{I}+\bm{A}\right)^{-1}\approx\bm{I}-\bm{A+A}^{2}.

(120)

Now that we have our approximate inverse template covariance matrix and determinant, we can simplify the SNR-max CDF (
35
),

\displaystyle C_{M}\left(Z|\bm{\Sigma}\right)=\frac{1}{\left(2\pi\right)^{M/2}\sqrt{\det\bm{\Sigma}}}

(121)

\displaystyle\int_{-Z}^{+Z}d\rho_{1}...\int_{-Z}^{+Z}d\rho_{M}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\rho}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{\rho}\right).

Using expansion (
120
), we represent the exponential in
C_{M}
as

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\rho}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{\rho}\right)

(122)

\displaystyle\approx\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\rho}^{T}\bm{\rho}\right)\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\bm{\rho}^{T}\bm{A\rho}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\rho}^{T}\bm{A}^{2}\bm{\rho}\right).

Expanding out the second and the third exponentials up to
O(\bm{A}^{2})
we obtain

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\rho}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{\rho}\right)

(123)

\displaystyle\approx\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\rho}^{T}\bm{\rho}\right)\times

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\rho}^{T}\bm{A\rho+}\frac{1}{8}\left(\bm{\rho}^{T}\bm{A\rho}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\rho}^{T}\bm{A}^{2}\bm{\rho}\right).

This expansion converges as long as

|\rho|\ll 1/\sqrt{\max\left|A_{ij}\right|}
.

Using these expansions, the correction to the SNR-max CDF reads

\displaystyle\delta_{I}C_{M}\left(Z|\bm{\Sigma}\right)\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\mathrm{Tr}{\left(\bm{A}^{2}\right)}C_{M}\left(Z|\bm{I}\right)

(124)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\neq j}A_{ij}\langle\rho_{i}\rho_{j}\rangle_{Z}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{8}\sum_{i\neq j}\sum_{k\neq l}A_{ij}A_{kl}\langle\left(\rho_{i}\rho_{j}\right)\left(\rho_{k}\rho_{l}\right)\rangle_{Z}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}\left(\bm{A}^{2}\right)_{ij}\langle\rho_{i}\rho_{j}\rangle_{Z}\,.

Here we kept all the terms up to
O(\bm{A}^{2})
and
introduced “averaging” notation

\langle g(\bm{\rho})\rangle_{Z}\equiv\int_{-Z}^{+Z}d\rho_{1}...\int_{-Z}^{+Z}d\rho_{M}g(\bm{\rho})f\left(\bm{\rho}|\bm{I}\right)\,,

(125)

where subscript
Z
emphasizes that the
M
-dimensional integral is evaluated
over hyper-cube centered at
\bm{\rho}=0
and of side
2Z
.
f\left(\bm{\rho}|\bm{I}\right)
is a joint PDF for fully orthogonal bank,

f\left(\bm{\rho}|\bm{I}\right)=\frac{1}{\left(2\pi\right)^{M/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\rho}^{T}\bm{\rho}\right)\,.

(126)

This joint PDF factorizes into the product of exponentials, simplifying evaluation of averages. The required averages in Eq. (
124
) are

\displaystyle\langle\rho_{i}\rho_{j}\rangle_{Z}=\delta_{ij}\,\langle\rho^{2}\rangle_{Z}C_{M-1}\left(Z|\bm{I}\right)\,,

(127)

\displaystyle\left\langle\left(\rho_{i}\rho_{j}\right)\left(\rho_{k}\rho_{l}\right)\right\rangle_{Z}^{i\neq j,k\neq l}=
