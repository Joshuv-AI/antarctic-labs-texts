Linear quadratic Chabauty
Stevan Gajović, J. Steffen Müller

Abstract
. We introduce a new quadratic Chabauty method to compute the integral points on certain even degree hyperelliptic curves. Our approach relies on a nontrivial degree zero divisor supported at the two points at infinity to restrict the p p -adic height to a linear function; we can then express this restriction in terms of holomorphic Coleman integrals under the standard quadratic Chabauty assumption. Then we use this linear relation to extract the integral points on the curve. We also generalize our method to integral points over number fields. Our method is significantly simpler and faster than all other existing versions of the quadratic Chabauty method. We give examples over ℚ \mathbb{Q} and ℚ ⁡ ( 7 ) \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{7}) .



1.
Introduction

Suppose that
X/\mathbb{Q}
is a hyperelliptic curve, given by an affine equation
y^{2}=f(x)
with

f\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
squarefree.
We set

\mathcal{U}=\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{Z}[x,y]/(y^{2}-f(x))\,.

Balakrishnan, Besser and the
second author developed in
[
BBM16
]
a
p
-adic method, called

quadratic Chabauty
, to
compute the set
\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{Z})
of integral points in
X(\mathbb{Q})
in the following
situation: Suppose that
f
is monic and of odd degree
2g+1>2
,
let
p
be a prime of good reduction for

X
, suppose that the Mordell–Weil rank
r
of
J(\mathbb{Q})
is equal to the genus

g
of
X
, and that the closure of
J(\mathbb{Q})
has finite index in
J(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
.
In
[
BBM16
]
, the authors construct a nontrivial locally
analytic function
\rho\colon\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{Z}_{p})\to\mathbb{Q}_{p}
such that
\rho(\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{Z}))\subseteq T
, for a finite and computable set
T\subset\mathbb{Q}_{p}
.
The construction depends on properties of the

p
-adic height pairing
h
introduced by Coleman and
Gross
[
CG89
]
.
This result was later reproved in
[
BD18
]
by
Balakrishnan–Dogra via Chabauty–Kim theory; using similar ideas, they
also developed a quadratic Chabauty method for rational points on certain
curves (since this assumes that the Picard number is at least 2, it does
not supersede the quadratic Chabauty method for integral points, which has
no such assumption).
It was later extended in
[
BBBM21
]
to the case of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-integral points on certain hyperelliptic curves
X
of odd degree over number fields
K
.
This quadratic Chabauty method can be used to compute
\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{Z})
explicitly,
see
[
BBM16
,
BBM17
]
. For this, one can use algorithms for
h
due to Balakrishnan and
Besser
[
BB12
]
and the second author
[
Mül14
]
.
The construction of
\rho
involves the following tools:

(i)

p
-adic Arakelov theory as developed by Besser
[
Bes05
]
;

(ii)

an extension of local Coleman–Gross heights to
divisors with common support;

(iii)

double Coleman integrals;

(iv)

Coleman integrals from tangential base points.

Therefore this construction is quite technical. In the present paper, we
find a similar but much simpler method to construct a function
\rho
that
we use to compute integral points on
even
degree hyperelliptic curves
(under certain conditions). Our construction does not require any of the
tools (i)–(iv).

1.1.
Linear quadratic Chabauty for
\mathbb{Z}
-integral points

In Section
4

we introduce a new quadratic Chabauty method for integral points on certain
even degree hyperelliptic curves
X/\mathbb{Q}
satisfying
r=g
.
Our approach differs from that of
[
BBM16
,
BBM17
]
in that it relies on the
existence of a nontrivial divisor of degree 0 on
X
supported at infinity. It is also notably faster.

Our method is based on the following result, which we prove
in
section

4.1
:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
X\colon y^{2}=f(x)
be a hyperelliptic curve over
\mathbb{Q}
such that
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
is a squarefree polynomial of degree
2g+2
whose leading
coefficient is a square in
\mathbb{Z}
. Suppose
r=g
. Let
p>g
be a prime of
good reduction for
X
.
Then there is a nontrivial computable locally
analytic function
\rho\colon\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{Z}_{p})\to\mathbb{Q}_{p}
and a finite and computable set
T
, such that if
P
is an integral point on
X
, then
\rho(\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{Z}))\subseteq T
.

We briefly describe the construction of
\rho
and
T
.
It relies on the global
p
-adic height pairings
h
due
to Coleman and Gross
[
CG89
]
.
This is a sum of local heights

h=\sum_{v\in\mathbb{Z}\;\text{prime}}h_{v}=h_{p}+\sum_{q\neq p\;\text{finite prime}}h_{q}\,.

More precisely, we use
Coleman integrals and the local height
h_{p}
(see

section

2.3
)
to construct
\rho
, and we analyze local heights away from

p
to obtain the set
T
(see
section

2.2
and

section

3.2
).

We may assume that
f
is monic and that the functions

f_{0},\ldots,f_{g-1}\colon J(\mathbb{Q})\to\mathbb{Q}_{p}

defined by

f_{i}(a)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\int_{0}^{a}\frac{x^{i}dx}{y}
induce a basis of
the space of linear maps
J(\mathbb{Q})\otimes\mathbb{Q}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, since otherwise
the method of Chabauty–Coleman may be applied.
Hence the linear function

\lambda\colon J(\mathbb{Q})\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}_{p}
given by

\lambda(a)=h(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},a)
can be represented as a
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
-linear combination

\lambda=\sum_{i=0}^{g-1}\alpha_{i}f_{i}
.
For simplicity, we assume that
we know an integral point
Q\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{Z})
. Then

\rho(P)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sum_{i=0}^{g-1}\alpha_{i}\int_{Q}^{P}\frac{x^{i}dx}{y}\omega_{i}-h_{p}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},P-Q)

defines a computable nontrivial locally analytic function

\rho\colon\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{Z}_{p})\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}_{p}
. For
P\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{Z})
, we have that

\rho(P)=\sum_{q\neq p}h_{q}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},P-Q).

For
q\neq p
and
P\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{Z}_{q})
, Lemma
3.1

below shows that
h_{q}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},P-Q)
vanishes for good primes
q

and takes values in a computable finite set for bad primes
q
, and we
obtain
T
from these finite sets.

We discuss an explicit method for the
computation of
\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{Z})
stemming from Theorem
1.1

in
section

4.2
and we give a precision analysis
in
section

4.3
.
In
section

4.4
, we illustrate our method by
computing the set of integral points on

X\colon y^{2}=x^{6}+2x^{5}-7x^{4}-18x^{3}+2x^{2}+20x+9\,.

In this example, the finite subset of
X(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
resulting from our method equals the
set of integral points on
X
. In general, this equality might not hold,
but in such cases we can combine our approach with the Mordell–Weil sieve
(see
[
BS10
]
) to still find the integral points.

Since we assume that the leading coefficient
f_{2g+2}
of
f
is a square,
there is also an elementary method to compute the integral points on curves satisfying the
conditions of Theorem
1.1
; namely, a variant of
Runge’s method can be applied, see Appendix
A
.
A similar approach works for a negative leading coefficient.
To deal with non-square positive leading coefficients, one would have to
extend the method from
[
BBM16
]
using
p
-adic Arakelov theory and
double Coleman integrals to this case.

1.2.
Linear quadratic Chabauty for integral points over
number fields

Similar to the extension of the techniques of
[
BBM16
]
in
[
BBBM21
]
,
we can extend our approach to compute the
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-integral points on certain
even degree hyperelliptic curves
X
over number fields
K
of degree

d>1
.
This method is based on Theorem
5.1
,
which generalizes Theorem
1.1
.
For this result, we assume that
\infty_{\pm}\in X(K)
and that
p
is a
prime that splits completely in
K
such that
X
has good reduction at all primes
above
p
.
As in
[
BBBM21
]
, a necessary condition is that

\operatorname{rk}(J/K)\leq dg-r_{K}
, where
r_{K}=\operatorname{rk}\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\ast}
.
For our extension, we use that the
p
-adic height depends on a choice of
a continuous idèle class character

\chi\colon\mathbb{A}_{K}^{\ast}/K^{\ast}\to\mathbb{Q}_{p}
.
While there is a canonical choice for
\chi
when
K=\mathbb{Q}
, this is false in
general,
and in fact it helps to use the maximal number of independent
\chi
. These give rise to
d-r_{K}
equations satisfied by the image
of the
\mathcal{O}_{K}
points on
X
in
X(\mathbb{Q}_{p})^{d}
. In addition,
we use
r_{K}
equations coming from restriction of scalars and Coleman
integrals, as in the work of Siksek for Chabauty over number fields
[
Sik13
]
.

When the conditions of Theorem
5.1
are satisfied,
then we may use it to compute a subset of

X(\mathbb{Q}_{p})^{d}
which contains the images of all
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-integral points under
the natural embedding (see

section

5.2
).
In
section

5.3

we illustrate our technique by determining the set of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
integral
points on

y^{2}=x^{6}+x^{5}-wx^{4}+(1-w)x^{3}+x^{2}+wx+w\,,

where
K=\mathbb{Q}(w)
and
w^{2}=7
.
This computation required a combination of linear quadratic Chabauty with the
Mordell–Weil sieve. We are not aware of any other method that would be able to handle this example.

In contrast
to the case
X/\mathbb{Q}
, the set computed by our method is not necessarily finite.
For instance,
when
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
has even degree and its leading coefficient
f_{2g+2}
is a positive
non-square in
\mathbb{Z}
, then our approach does not allow us to compute the
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-integral points on
X\colon y^{2}=f(x)

for
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{f_{2g+2}})
, so this approach cannot be used to compute the

\mathbb{Z}
-integral points either.

2.
Coleman–Gross
p
-adic heights

We fix a prime number
p\in\mathbb{Z}
. Let
K
be a number
field and let
X/K
be a smooth projective and geometrically integral curve of genus
g>0
, with good reduction at all primes above
p
.
The
p
-adic height on the Jacobian
J/K
of
X
depends on the choice of a continuous idèle class character

(2.1)

\chi\colon\mathbb{A}_{K}^{\ast}/K^{\ast}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Q}_{p}

and of a subspace
W_{\mathfrak{p}}\subset\operatorname{H^{1}_{dR}}(X_{\mathfrak{p}}/K_{\mathfrak{p}})

complementary to the space of holomorphic forms for each
\mathfrak{p}\mid p
.
Coleman-Gross construct the height pairing on
J
by defining local height
pairings
h_{v}
for all finite primes
v

of
K
, and then summing them up. We make use of the following
notation: for a finite place
v
of
K
we denote by
K_{v}
the completion
of
K
at
v
, by
\mathcal{O}_{v}
its ring of integers and by
\pi_{v}
a
uniformizer. We write
X_{v}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}X\otimes K_{v}
.

Definition 2.1
.

A
local height pairing
(with respect to
\chi
) at a finite prime
v
of

K
is a continuous, symmetric and bi-additive function
h_{v}
that assigns to a pair of
divisors
D_{1},D_{2}\in\operatorname{Div}^{0}(X_{v})
with disjoint support an element of

\mathbb{Q}_{p}
with the following property:
for all
f\in K_{v}(X_{v})^{\ast}
and
D_{2}\in\operatorname{Div}^{0}(X_{v})
such that
\operatorname{supp}(\operatorname{div}(f))\cap\operatorname{supp}(D_{2})=\emptyset
, we have

h_{v}(\operatorname{div}(f),D_{2})=\chi_{v}(f(D_{2})).

The construction of
h_{v}
depends on whether
\chi_{v}
is unramified
(see
section

2.2
) or is ramified
(see
section

2.3
).
When
D_{1},D_{2}\in\operatorname{Div}^{0}(X)
, the local heights satisfy
h_{v}(D_{1},D_{2})\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}h_{v}(D_{1}\otimes K_{v},D_{2}\otimes K_{v})=0
for all but finitely many
v
. Thus, their sum for
D_{1},D_{2}\in\operatorname{Div}^{0}(X)
is well-defined:

h(D_{1},D_{2})\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sum_{v}h_{v}(D_{1},D_{2})\colon\operatorname{Div}^{0}(X)\times\operatorname{Div}^{0}(X)\longrightarrow\mathbb{Q}_{p}\,.

By
[
CG89
, Sections 1, 5]
,
h
descends to a bilinear
pairing on
J(K)
, called the (global Coleman–Gross)
p
-adic height
pairing
:

h\colon J(K)\times J(K)\to\mathbb{Q}_{p}\,.

2.1.
Continuous
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
-valued idèle class
characters

In this section, we briefly recall
properties of continuous idèle class characters with codomain
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
,
following
[
BBBM21
, §2.1]
. See also
[
Gaj22
, §6.5.4]
.

We say that a continuous idèle class character (
2.1
)
is
ramified
at a place
\mathfrak{q}
if
\chi_{\mathfrak{q}}(\mathcal{O}_{K_{\mathfrak{q}}}^{\ast})\neq 0
.
If
\chi
is unramified at
\mathfrak{q}
, then
\chi_{\mathfrak{q}}
is completely determined by

\chi_{\mathfrak{q}}(\pi_{\mathfrak{q}})
.
For continuity reasons,
\chi
is unramified at
any place
\mathfrak{q}\nmid p
.
On the other hand, for

\mathfrak{p}\mid p
, there is a
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
-linear map
\operatorname{tr}_{\mathfrak{p}}\colon K_{\mathfrak{p}}\to\mathbb{Q}_{p}

such that

(2.2)

\chi_{\mathfrak{p}}(x)=\operatorname{tr}_{\mathfrak{p}}\circ\log_{\mathfrak{p}}(x)\quad\text{for all}\;x\in\mathcal{O}_{K_{\mathfrak{p}}}^{\ast}\,.

If
\chi
is ramified at
\mathfrak{p}
(see
[
BBBM21
, Remark 2.1]
), there is
a branch of the logarithm
\log_{\mathfrak{p}}\colon K_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\ast}\to K_{\mathfrak{p}}
such that
(
2.2
) remains valid on
K_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\ast}
.
From now on, we assume that
\chi
is ramified at least one
\mathfrak{p}\mid p
, and
we fix the branch of the logarithm at
\mathfrak{p}\mid p
to be this
\log_{\mathfrak{p}}
.

By
[
BBBM21
, Lemma 2.2]
,
\chi
is uniquely determined by
\operatorname{tr}_{\mathfrak{p}}
for
all
\mathfrak{p}\mid p
such that
\chi_{\mathfrak{p}}
is ramified.
The choice
\operatorname{tr}_{\mathfrak{p}}=\operatorname{tr}_{K_{\mathfrak{p}}/\mathbb{Q}_{p}}
in
(
2.2
) for all
\mathfrak{p}
such that
\chi_{\mathfrak{p}}
is ramified,
we get the
cyclotomic idèle class
character
, see
[
BBBM21
, Lemma 2.3, Example 2.7]
. In particular,
when
K
is totally real, then this is, up to scaling, the unique continuous idèle class character (
[
BBBM21
, Corollary 2.4, Example 2.7]
). The ground fields in our examples in
section

4.4
and
section

5.3
are indeed totally real.
For instance, when
K=\mathbb{Q}
, the cyclotomic character is given by
\operatorname{tr}_{\mathfrak{p}}=\operatorname{id}
. Then

\chi_{\mathfrak{p}}=\log_{p}
is defined by

\log_{p}(p)=0
, since
\chi_{q}(p)=0
for all
q\neq p
by continuity.
Similarly, we also have
\chi_{q}(q)=-\log_{p}(q)
.
When
K
is a real quadratic number field and
p
splits in

K
, then, identifying
K_{\mathfrak{p}}
with
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
for all
\mathfrak{p}\mid p
, we can again
take
\operatorname{tr}_{\mathfrak{p}}=\operatorname{id}
, and we find
\chi_{\mathfrak{p}}=\log_{p}
with
\log_{p}(p)=0
; for

\mathfrak{q}\nmid p
, we have
\chi_{\mathfrak{q}}(\pi_{q})=-\log_{p}(N_{K_{\mathfrak{q}}/\mathbb{Q}_{q}}(\pi_{q}))
.

2.2.
Local heights when
\chi_{v}
is
unramified

We briefly review the construction of
h_{v}
for a prime
v=\mathfrak{q}
of
K
such that

\chi_{\mathfrak{q}}
is unramified, for instance a prime
\mathfrak{q}\nmid p
.
Given two divisors
D_{1},D_{2}\in\operatorname{Div}^{0}(X_{\mathfrak{q}})
with disjoint support, we
denote by
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
extensions to
a regular model of
X_{\mathfrak{q}}
such that the
intersection multiplicity
(\mathcal{D}_{i}\cdot V)\in\mathbb{Q}
vanishes
for all vertical divisors
V
.

Proposition 2.2
.

[
CG89
, Proposition 1.2]

For a prime
\mathfrak{q}
such that
\chi_{\mathfrak{q}}
is unramified, there is a unique local height pairing
h_{\mathfrak{q}}
in
the sense of Definition
2.1
. It is
defined by

(2.3)

h_{\mathfrak{q}}(D_{1},D_{2})=\chi_{\mathfrak{q}}(\pi_{\mathfrak{q}})\cdot(\mathcal{D}_{1}\cdot\mathcal{D}_{2})\,.

We describe the possible values
h_{\mathfrak{q}}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},D_{2})
on even
degree hyperelliptic curves in §
3.2
. These are crucial in our linear quadratic Chabauty method in Sections
4
and
5
.

2.3.
Local heights when
\chi_{v}
is
ramified

When
\chi_{v}
is ramified, then
v=\mathfrak{p}\mid p
and one can define a local height pairing
h_{\mathfrak{p}}
by the Coleman integral

(2.4)

h_{\mathfrak{p}}(D_{1},D_{2})=\operatorname{tr}_{\mathfrak{p}}\left(\int_{D_{2}}\omega_{D_{1}}\right)\,,

were
\omega_{D_{1}}
is a differential of the third kind whose residue
divisor is
D_{1}
(see
[
CG89
]
or
[
BB12
, Section
2]
). There are many such differentials, and one uses the choice
of a subspace
W_{\mathfrak{p}}\subset\operatorname{H^{1}_{dR}}(X_{\mathfrak{p}}/K_{\mathfrak{p}})
, complementary to the
holomorphic differentials, to normalize
\omega_{D_{1}}
as
in
[
BB12
, Definition 2.7]
.
Furthermore,
h_{\mathfrak{p}}
is symmetric if and only if
W_{\mathfrak{p}}
is isotropic with
respect to the cup product pairing (see
[
CG89
, Proposition
5.2]
), which we assume from now on.

Proposition 2.3
.

[
CG89
, Proposition
5.2]

Let
\mathfrak{p}\mid p
be a prime such that
\chi_{\mathfrak{p}}
is ramified and let
W_{\mathfrak{p}}\subset\operatorname{H^{1}_{dR}}(X_{\mathfrak{p}}/K_{\mathfrak{p}})
be a complementary subspace that is
isotropic with respect to the cup product.
Then (
2.4
) defines
a local height pairing in the sense of Definition
2.1
.

3.
Local heights for linear quadratic Chabauty on hyperelliptic
curves

In this section, we discuss the local heights that we use later on
for our linear quadratic Chabauty method.
We consider an even degree hyperelliptic curve
X\colon y^{2}=f(x)
over
K
with
f\in\mathcal{O}_{K}[x]
squarefree and monic.
Let
\mathcal{U}=\operatorname{Spec}\mathcal{O}_{K}[x,y]/(y^{2}-f(x))
and let

\chi
be a continuous idèle
class character (
2.1
) such that
\chi_{\mathfrak{p}}
is ramified for at least
one prime
\mathfrak{p}\mid p
of
K
. We assume that
p
is totally split in
K
.

The key idea of any variant of the quadratic Chabauty method is to use
bilinearity of the global height pairing and to analyze certain local
heights. Different variants of the method use
different heights.
The local heights of interest in the present article are of the form

h_{v}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},P-Q)
, with
P,Q\in X_{v}(K_{v})
,
where
X_{v}=X\otimes K_{v}
.

3.1.
Local heights for linear Quadratic Chabauty: the ramified
case

Suppose that
\chi_{\mathfrak{p}}
is ramified.
Extending and simplifying an earlier algorithm for odd degree hyperelliptic
curves due to Balakrishnan and Besser
[
BB12
]
, the authors show
in
[
GM23
]
how to compute
h_{\mathfrak{p}}
on
X_{\mathfrak{p}}
, which we view as
a curve over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
.
We briefly describe how to compute

h_{\mathfrak{p}}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},R-S)
for affine points
R,S\in X_{\mathfrak{p}}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
.
Since the residue divisor of
\frac{x^{g}dx}{y}
is precisely

\infty_{-}-\infty_{+}
, we obtain

(3.1)

h_{\mathfrak{p}}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},R-S)=\operatorname{tr}_{\mathfrak{p}}\left(\int_{S}^{R}\frac{x^{g}dx}{y}-\sum_{i=0}^{g-1}u_{i}\int_{S}^{R}\frac{x^{i}dx}{y}\right)

for certain
u_{0},\ldots,u_{g-1}\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}
that are used to normalize

\omega_{\infty_{-}-\infty_{+}}
with
respect to a chosen complementary subspace
W_{\mathfrak{p}}
; here
\operatorname{tr}_{\mathfrak{p}}
is as
in (
2.2
).
The numbers
u_{0},\ldots,u_{g-1}
depend only on
X_{\mathfrak{p}}
,
W_{\mathfrak{p}}
and the action of Frobenius on

\operatorname{H^{1}_{dR}}(X_{\mathfrak{p}})
,
but
not on
R
or
S
. We can compute
u_{0},\ldots,u_{g-1}

efficiently using
[
GM23
, §3.2, Steps (vi), (vii)]
. The necessary Coleman
integrals can be computed efficiently using Balakrishnan’s algorithm
[
Bal15
]
.

3.2.
Local heights for linear Quadratic Chabauty: the unramified
case

Suppose that
\chi_{\mathfrak{q}}
is unramified.
One can compute

h_{\mathfrak{q}}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},P-Q)
using algorithms from
[
Hol12
,
Mül14
,
vBHM20
]
.
We now find the possible values that

h_{\mathfrak{q}}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},P-Q)
can take for
P,Q\in\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{q}})
. In
particular, there are only finitely many such values.
Recall from Equation (
2.3
) that the local height on
X_{\mathfrak{q}}
can be
expressed in terms of intersection theory on a regular model. As
in
[
BBM16
]
, we use a specific regular model, constructed as follows:
Let
F(X,Z)
be the degree
2g+2
homogenization of
f
. Then the equation

Y^{2}=F(X,Z)
defines a model of
X_{\mathfrak{q}}
in the weighted projective plane over

\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{q}}
, with weight 1 attached to
X
and
Z
and weight
g+1
attached to
Y
.
Let
\mathcal{X}
be a strong desingularization of this model (meaning
that this desingularization is an isomorphism outside the singular locus,
see
[
Liu02
, Definition 8.3.39]
).
We denote the set of irreducible components of multiplicity 1 of its
special fiber
\mathcal{X}_{\mathfrak{q}}
by
V_{1}(\mathcal{X})
. Since all points in

X(K_{\mathfrak{q}})
reduce to a smooth point of
\mathcal{X}_{\mathfrak{q}}
, there is a
natural specialization map

\operatorname{sp}\colon X(K_{\mathfrak{q}})\to V_{1}(\mathcal{X}).

Let
D(\mathcal{X})
denote the free abelian group on
V_{1}(\mathcal{X})

and let
M
be the intersection matrix of
\mathcal{X}_{\mathfrak{q}}
.
The Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse of
-M
induces a bilinear pairing
L^{+}

on
D(\mathcal{X})
.

Lemma 3.1
.

(a)

Let
R,S\in\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{q}})
. Then we have

h_{\mathfrak{q}}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},R-S)=L^{+}(\operatorname{sp}(\infty_{-})-\operatorname{sp}(\infty_{+}),\operatorname{sp}(R)-\operatorname{sp}(S))\chi_{\mathfrak{q}}(\pi_{\mathfrak{q}})\,.

(b)

The set

T_{\mathfrak{q}}=\{L^{+}(\operatorname{sp}(\infty_{-})-\operatorname{sp}(\infty_{+}),\operatorname{sp}(R)-\operatorname{sp}(S))\,\colon\,R,S\in\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{q}})\}

is finite.

(c)

If
\mathcal{X}_{\mathfrak{q}}
has only one component, then

T_{\mathfrak{q}}=\{0\}
. In particular, this holds when
X_{\mathfrak{q}}
has good reduction.

(d)

If
f
is monic, and if the reduction of
f
is not a square, then

T_{\mathfrak{q}}=\{0\}
.

Proof.

It follows easily from Equation (
2.3
) that for points

P,Q,R,S\in X(K_{\mathfrak{q}})
such that
\{P,Q\}\cap\{R,S\}=\varnothing
, we have

h_{\mathfrak{q}}(P-Q,R-S)=\left((P_{\mathcal{X}}-Q_{\mathcal{X}}\cdot R_{\mathcal{X}}-S_{\mathcal{X}})+L^{+}(\operatorname{sp}(P)-\operatorname{sp}(Q),\operatorname{sp}(R)-\operatorname{sp}(S))\right)\chi_{\mathfrak{q}}(\pi_{q})\,,

where the subscript
\mathcal{X}
denotes the Zariski closure in

\mathcal{X}
. See, for instance,
[
HdJ15
, Corollary 7.5]
. If

P=\infty_{-},Q=\infty_{+}
and if
R,S\in\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{q}})
, then the first term vanishes, proving (
a
).
To prove (
b
), it suffices to note that
V_{1}(\mathcal{X})
is finite.
When
\infty_{-}
and
\infty_{+}
specialize to the same component in

V_{1}(\mathcal{X})
, then

L^{+}(\operatorname{sp}(\infty_{-})-\operatorname{sp}(\infty_{+}),\operatorname{sp}(R)-\operatorname{sp}(S))
is trivial. This proves (
c
). It
also proves (
d
), since if
f
does not reduce to a square,
then there is a unique component of
\mathcal{X}_{\mathfrak{q}}
that contains the
images of points of good reduction modulo
\pi_{q}
, and if
f
is monic, then both points at infinity have good reduction.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

In practice, it is easy to determine the set
T_{\mathfrak{q}}
using
Magma
’s command
RegularModel
, but the computation of the regular model does not always succeed. If it does, then the command

PointOnRegularModel
can be used to find the components

\operatorname{sp}(\infty_{-}),\;\operatorname{sp}(\infty_{+}),\;\operatorname{sp}(R),\;\operatorname{sp}(S),

so that we only need to compute all
values
L^{+}(\operatorname{sp}(\infty_{-})-\operatorname{sp}(\infty_{+}),\Gamma-\Gamma^{\prime})
, where
\Gamma
and
\Gamma^{\prime}
run through
V_{1}(\mathcal{X})
.

Remark 3.3
.

The set
T_{\mathfrak{q}}
plays a crucial part in our description of the linear quadratic Chabauty method for integral points below. We stress that it is much simpler to describe and to compute than the corresponding sets
[
BBM16
, Equation (5.2)]
in the odd degree case and that for many examples it is trivial.

4.
Linear quadratic Chabauty for
\mathbb{Z}
-integral points

In this section, we introduce the linear quadratic Chabauty method to
compute the integral points on certain even degree hyperelliptic curves
over
\mathbb{Q}
. Let

X\colon y^{2}=f(x)=f_{2g+2}x^{2g+2}+\cdots+f_{0}

be a hyperelliptic curve over
\mathbb{Q}
such that
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
is a squarefree polynomial
of degree
2g+2
and
f_{2g+2}
is a square in
\mathbb{Z}
. Using the obvious
change of variables defined over
\mathbb{Q}
, we may and will assume that
f
is
monic; integral points are preserved.
We assume that the rank
r
of the Mordell-Weil group
J(\mathbb{Q})
of the Jacobian
J/\mathbb{Q}
of
X
equals
g
and that
p
is a prime of good reduction such that the closure of
J(\mathbb{Q})
in
J(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
has finite index,
so that we cannot use the classical method of Chabauty and Coleman.
We set

\mathcal{U}=\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{Z}[x,y]/(y^{2}-f(x));

our goal is to compute
\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{Z})
, the set of integral points in
X(\mathbb{Q})
.

4.1.
Linear quadratic Chabauty for
\mathbb{Z}
-integral points: theory

We now state and prove our result on linear quadratic Chabauty for
\mathbb{Z}
-integral points, and we show how it implies Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
X/\mathbb{Q}\colon y^{2}=f(x)
be a hyperelliptic curve such that
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x]

is a squarefree polynomial of degree
2g+2>3
whose leading coefficient
is a square in
\mathbb{Z}
. Let
\omega_{i}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\frac{x^{i}dx}{y}
and let

J
be the Jacobian of
X
. Suppose that
r\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\operatorname{rk}(J/\mathbb{Q})=g
and that the closure of
J(\mathbb{Q})
has finite index in
J(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
. Let
p
be a prime of good reduction for
X
and let
h=\sum_{v}h_{v}
be the Coleman-Gross
p
-adic height pairing with respect to the cyclotomic idèle class character and a complementary subspace
W
of
\operatorname{H^{1}_{dR}}(X/\mathbb{Q}_{p})
, which we assume to be given explicitly. Assume that we know an integral point
Q\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{Z})
.

(a)

There are constants

\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{g-1}\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}
such that for all
a\in J(\mathbb{Q})
we have

h(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},a)=\alpha_{0}\int_{a}\omega_{0}+\cdots+\alpha_{g-1}\int_{a}\omega_{g-1}\,.

(b)

The function
\rho\colon\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{Z}_{p})\to\mathbb{Q}_{p}
defined by

\rho(P)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\alpha_{0}\int_{Q}^{P}\omega_{0}+\cdots+\alpha_{g-1}\int_{Q}^{P}\omega_{g-1}-h_{p}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},P-Q)

is locally analytic, computable and not identically 0.

(c)

Let
T=\{\sum_{q\neq p}-l_{q}\log_{p}(q)\,\colon\,l_{q}\in T_{q}\}
, where
T_{q}
is defined in Lemma
3.1
(
b
). Then
T
is finite and computable, and we have

\rho(P)\in T
for all
P\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{Z})
.

Proof.

Since the closure of
J(\mathbb{Q})
has finite index in
J(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
,
the map
\log\colon J(\mathbb{Q})\otimes\mathbb{Q}_{p}\to\mathrm{H}^{0}(X_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}},\Omega^{1})^{\vee}
is an isomorphism, so it follows that the linear maps

f_{i}\colon J(\mathbb{Q})\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}_{p}
given by
f_{i}(a)=\int_{0}^{a}\omega_{i}
induce a basis of the space of linear maps
J(\mathbb{Q})\otimes\mathbb{Q}_{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}_{p}
. The map

\lambda(a)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}h(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},a)

belongs to this space, proving (
a
).

Part (
b
) follows from (
3.1
).
For part (
c
), we recall that
T_{q}=\{0\}
for almost all prime
numbers
q\neq p
, by Lemma
3.1
(
c
). Therefore, all
sums in the definition of the set
T
are finite. Further, all
T_{q}
are finite by Lemma
3.1
(
b
), and computable, using, for example, algorithms from
[
vBHM20
]
, so
T
is finite and computable. For
P\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{Z})
, we have

\rho(P)=h(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},P-Q)-h_{p}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},P-Q)=\sum_{q\neq p}h_{q}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},P-Q).

Hence the final assertion of part (
c
) follows from
Lemma
3.1
(
a
).
∎

Remark 4.2
.

We can replace
\rho
by the function

\rho(P)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\alpha_{0}\int_{\iota(P)}^{P}\omega_{0}+\cdots+\alpha_{g-1}\int_{\iota(P)}^{P}\omega_{g-1}-h_{p}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},P-\iota(P))

if no integral point is known; Theorem
4.1
remains valid in this case.

Remark 4.3
.

If
p
is a prime of good ordinary reduction, then there is a canonical
choice for the subspace
W_{p}
, namely the unit root subspace with respect
to Frobenius. However, our method works for any complementary isotropic

W_{p}
; see also the discussion in
[
GM23
, §3.1, Step (iv)]
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Suppose that
X/\mathbb{Q}
satisfies the conditions of Theorem
1.1
.
Theorem
1.1
follows at once from
Theorem
4.1
and Remark
4.2
if the
closure of
J(\mathbb{Q})
has finite index in
J(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
.
By construction, the function
\rho
is not identically 0.
If this condition does not hold, we can use Chabauty–Coleman to compute

X(\mathbb{Q})
and thus
\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{Z})
.
∎

4.2.
Computing
\mathbb{Z}
-integral points using linear quadratic Chabauty

We keep the notation of §
4.1
and we assume
that the conditions of Theorem
4.1
are satisfied. Then we
get the
following algorithm for the
computation of
\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{Z})
.
Recall that a well-behaved uniformizer at a point
P\in X(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
is a
uniformizer
t
at
P
reduces to a uniformizer at the image

\bar{P}\in X(\mathbb{F}_{p})
of
P
under reduction modulo
p
. Choosing a
well-behaved uniformizer at any point of the residue disc

D(P)=D(\bar{P})\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathrm{red}^{-1}(\bar{P})

induces a bijection of
D(P)
with
p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
(see for instance
[
LT02
]
).

Algorithm 4.4
.

(Linear quadratic Chabauty for integral points)

(i)

Find all integral points
P\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{Z})
up to some height bound.

(ii)

For each bad prime
q
, compute the set
T_{q}
using Remark
3.2
.

(iii)

Find
g
independent points
a_{1},\ldots,a_{g}\in J(\mathbb{Q})
and compute

h(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},a_{i})

for
1\leq i\leq g
using the
algorithms from

[
GM23
, §3.1,§3.2]
for
h_{p}
, and using one of the algorithms from
[
Hol12
,
Mül14
,
vBHM20
]

for
h_{q}
for primes
q\neq p
.

(iv)

Using Step (
iii
), compute

\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{g-1}\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}
such that for all
a\in J(\mathbb{Q})
we have

h(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},a)=\alpha_{0}\int_{a}\omega_{0}+\cdots+\alpha_{g-1}\int_{a}\omega_{g-1}.

Define the function
\rho\colon\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{Z}_{p})\to\mathbb{Q}_{p}

by

\rho(P)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\alpha_{0}\int_{Q}^{P}\omega_{0}+\cdots+\alpha_{g-1}\int_{Q}^{P}\omega_{g-1}-h_{p}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},P-Q).

(v)

In each affine residue disc, fix a point
P_{0}\in X(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
. Use a well-behaved uniformizer
t_{P_{0}}=t_{P_{0}}(z)
at
P_{0}
to
parametrize the points
P
inside this residue disc and express
\rho(P)
as a power series in
z
.

(vi)

For each
t\in T
, find the set
A_{t}\subset\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{Z}_{p})
of all points corresponding to a zero of
\rho(z)=t
.

(vii)

If
A\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\bigcup_{t\in T}A_{t}
does not equal the set computed in Step (
i
), use the Mordell-Weil sieve
to separate which points in
A
are rational and which are not as
in
[
BBM17
]
. If this does not succeed, go to (i). If it does,
return the integral points among the remaining ones.

Remark 4.5
.

(a)

In Step (
iii
), we preferably choose points in
J(\mathbb{Q})

represented by divisors whose support consists only of points in

\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{Z})
. However, it is still possible to compute
\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{g-1}
if we need to use other representatives.

(b)

We compute the power series in Step (
v
) as follows. We write

(4.1)

\rho(P)=\alpha_{0}\int_{Q}^{P_{0}}\omega_{0}+\cdots+\alpha_{g-1}\int_{Q}^{P_{0}}\omega_{g-1}-h_{p}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},P_{0}-Q)

\qquad\qquad+\alpha_{0}\int_{P_{0}}^{P}\omega_{0}+\cdots+\alpha_{g-1}\int_{P_{0}}^{P}\omega_{g-1}-h_{p}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},P-P_{0}).

All the quantities in the first line of Equation
(
4.1
) are independent of
P
, and we have
already explained how to compute them. Recalling that we have

h_{p}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},P-P_{0})=\int_{P_{0}}^{P}\omega_{g}-\left(\beta_{0}\int_{P_{0}}^{P}\omega_{0}+\cdots+\beta_{g-1}\int_{P_{0}}^{P}\omega_{g-1}\right)

for some coefficients
\beta_{0},\ldots,\beta_{g-1}\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, all the integrals that appear in the second line of Equation (
4.1
) are tiny integrals. As tiny integrals are computed integrating term by term, if a point
P
is described using a parameter
z
, the function
\rho
is indeed expressed as a power series in
z
.

(c)

We can get a smaller set
A
of zeros by noting that
the sets
T_{q}
are actually larger than necessary. In fact, it suffices to compute the set of all values

L^{+}(\operatorname{sp}(\infty_{-})-\operatorname{sp}(\infty_{+}),\Gamma-\operatorname{sp}(Q))\,,

where
\Gamma
runs through
V_{1}(\mathcal{X})
.

(d)

Recall from Remark
4.2
that we do not need
to have an integral point
Q

available, since we can use the function

\rho(P)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\alpha_{0}\int_{\iota(P)}^{P}\omega_{0}+\cdots+\alpha_{g-1}\int_{\iota(P)}^{P}\omega_{g-1}-h_{p}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},P-\iota(P))\,,

where
\iota
is the hyperelliptic involution.
In this case, we could get smaller sets
T_{q}
by computing how
\iota
acts
on the special fiber
\mathcal{X}_{q}
, but we have not implemented this.

We have implemented Algorithm
4.4
, and our code is available
from
https://github.com/steffenmueller/LinQC
. Most of our implementation is
in
SageMath

[
The23
]
,
but Step (
ii
) and the
computation of local heights at
q\neq p
in Step (
iii
) are implemented in
Magma

[
BCP97
]
, since they require the computation of a regular model.

4.3.
Precision estimates

We now analyze the loss of precision in Algorithm
4.4
. Steps
(
i
) and (
ii
) are exact. When computing heights away from

p
, we get
\mathbb{Q}
-valued intersection numbers, multiplied by
\log_{p}(q)
(or, more
generally,
\chi_{p}(q)
), which we can compute to any desired precision. Thus, the
precision analysis for Step (
iii
) depends only on the precision analysis for the local height at
p
,
which is discussed in
[
GM23
, §3.4]
; there we also
analyze precision for the Coleman integrals in Step
(
iv
). The precision in Step (
iv
)
also depends on
k\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\operatorname{ord}_{p}(\det N)
, where
N
is the matrix

N=\left(\int_{a_{j}}\omega_{i}\right)_{i,j}

because we use
the inverse of
N
to compute the constants
\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{g-1}
. Thus, if we
want to compute
\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{g-1}
up to precision
p^{m}
, we have to compute
all previous quantities up to precision
p^{m+k}
. Note that
k
depends only on the set

\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{g}\}
, which we can choose. Therefore, we can precompute the value
k
and
deduce the required precision for the other parts of the algorithm.
In Step (
v
) we consider tiny integrals of differentials which are
holomorphic in these residue discs, where one endpoint is given by a parameter. Hence the
possible loss of precision can be bounded as in
[
BBK10
, Proposition 18]
.
Since Step
(
vii
) uses exact information at primes
v\neq p
, it does not require a precision analysis.

In Step (
vi
), we locate the zeros in the unit disc of a
p
-adic power series

F
, truncated at
finite level
M
and with coefficients correct to
m
digits of
p
-adic precision.
The extent to which the resulting zeros of this polynomial coincide with the zeros of the
power series itself depends on
m
,
M
, and on the
valuations of the coefficients of
F
. If we have a lower bound for these valuations, then
we can use
[
BBCF+19
, Proposition 3.11]
to determine the minimum values of
m

and
M
so that the zeros are provably correct to the desired
precision.
In general, it can be difficult to obtain bounds on these valuations (see for
instance
[
BDM+23
, Section 4]
).
Fortunately, in our case
F
is of a rather simple nature: up to constants, it is a linear combination of
tiny integrals of differentials
\omega_{i}
for
i\in\{0,\ldots,g\}
in
residue discs
where these do not have poles. Hence the only negative valuations come from integrating
terms of the form
a_{j}t^{j}
, where
p\mid(j+1)
, as
in
[
BBCF+19
, Lemma 3.10]
.

4.4.
Example: A curve of genus 2

We illustrate the method from
section

4.2
with the following example. Consider the curve
X/\mathbb{Q}
defined by

X\colon y^{2}=f(x)=x^{6}+2x^{5}-7x^{4}-18x^{3}+2x^{2}+20x+9

with
LMFDB
-label
5295.a.79425.1
. By

https://www.lmfdb.org/Genus2Curve/Q/5295/a/79425/1
, the rank of its
Jacobian
J
over
\mathbb{Q}
is 2. Hence we may apply linear quadratic Chabauty, and we will use this to prove that the set of integral points on
X
is

(4.2)

\{(0,\pm 3),(1,\pm 3),(-1,\pm 1),(-2,\pm 3),(-4,\pm 37)\}.

Fix
Q=(-1,1)\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{Z})
. Let
p
be a prime of good ordinary reduction
for
X
. Using Lemma
3.1
, we compute that for any prime
q\neq p

and any
P\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{Z})
, we have

h_{q}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},P-Q)=0.

Namely, for
q\neq 2
,
f
is not a square modulo
q
. At 2, the given model is not minimal at 2; the minimal model

X^{\min}\colon y^{2}+(x^{3}+x^{2}+1)y=-2x^{4}-5x^{3}+5x+2

is smooth at 2 and any isomorphism
X\to X^{\min}
maps 2-integral points to 2-integral points. Therefore the local height at 2 is trivial as well.

The next step is to express the Coleman-Gross height in terms of Coleman
integrals, i.e., to compute
\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1}
from Algorithm

4.4
, Step (
iv
). We first need to
compute
h(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},a_{1}),~h(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},a_{2}),
for two
linearly independent
a_{1},a_{2}\in J(\mathbb{Q})
. As explained
in Remark
4.5
(a), we prefer to use two
\mathbb{Z}
-linearly independent elements of

J(\mathbb{Q})
that have a representative of the shape
P-R
for
P,R\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{Z})
because then it is easier to compute the heights
h(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},P-R)
. We can indeed find such representatives for this curve. Namely, if we denote
Q_{0}=(0,3)
and
Q_{1}=(1,3)
, then
[Q_{0}-Q]
and
[Q_{1}-Q]
are
\mathbb{Z}
-linearly independent in
J(\mathbb{Q})
.

By the above, we have
h(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},P-R)=h_{p}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},P-R)
for
P,R\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{Z})
. Taking
P=Q_{0},R=Q
, and
P=Q_{1},R=Q
, respectively, we get two linear equations:

h(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},Q_{i}-Q)=h_{p}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},Q_{i}-Q)=\alpha_{0}\int_{Q}^{Q_{i}}\omega_{0}+\alpha_{1}\int_{Q}^{Q_{i}}\omega_{1}\,,\quad i=0,1\,.

We use
p=7
. Solving the system above, we compute

\alpha_{0}=5+4\cdot 7+6\cdot 7^{2}+7^{3}+6\cdot 7^{4}+O(7^{5}),\;\alpha_{1}=6+3\cdot 7+5\cdot 7^{2}+3\cdot 7^{3}+4\cdot 7^{4}+O(7^{5})\,.

Now we define the function
\rho\colon\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{Z}_{p})\to\mathbb{Q}_{p}
by

\rho(P)=\alpha_{0}\int_{Q}^{P}\omega_{0}+\alpha_{1}\int_{Q}^{P}\omega_{1}-h_{p}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},P-Q)\,.

As explained in Step (
v
) of Algorithm
4.4
, in each residue disc,
we analyze the zeros of
\rho
by fixing a point
P_{0}
and then choosing a
well-behaved uniformizer at
P_{0}
to establish a bijection between the
residue disc
D(P_{0})
and
7\mathbb{Z}_{7}
.
All affine residue discs modulo 7 contain exactly one known integral point
from (
4.2
). Hence, for each residue disc, we choose that
integral point as a representative. To prove that this integral point is
the only one in its residue disc, it suffices to show that
z=0
is the only zero
of the power series expansion of
\rho
in the disc.
This indeed happens for all known integral points on
X
.

For example, let us consider the point
Q_{0}=(0,3)
. All points in
D(Q_{0})
have
x
-coordinate equal to
7z
for some
z\in\mathbb{Z}_{7}
. We compute that

\rho(z)=(6\cdot 7+O(7^{2}))z+(6\cdot 7^{3}+O(7^{4}))z^{2}+(6\cdot 7^{3}+O(7^{4}))z^{3}+(7^{4}+O(7^{5}))z^{4}+O((7z)^{5})\,.

By Strassmann’s theorem (see, for example,
[
Gou93
, Theorem 4.2.4]
), this power series has at most one zero. Since we already know that
z=0
is a zero, we conclude that
Q_{0}
is the only integral point on
X
inside its residue disc. The same argument applies for all integral points and shows that the set of known integral points (
4.2
) is the complete set of integral points on
X
.
The code for this example can be found
at
https://github.com/steffenmueller/LinQC
.

5.
Linear quadratic Chabauty for integral points over number fields

Let
K
be a number field of degree
d
with signature
(r_{1},r_{2})
; we
denote the rank of its unit group by
r_{K}=r_{1}+r_{2}-1
. In this section we
adapt the strategy from Section
4
to compute the

\mathcal{O}_{K}
-integral points on certain even degree hyperelliptic curves over
K
. Let

X/K\colon y^{2}=f(x)
be a hyperelliptic curve such that
f\in\mathcal{O}_{K}[x]

has no repeated roots.
Let
J
be the Jacobian of

X
and let
g
be its genus.
As in Section
4
, denote

\mathcal{U}=\operatorname{Spec}\mathcal{O}_{K}[x,y]/(y^{2}-f(x));

then
\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
is the set of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-integral points on
X
.
The restriction of scalars
\operatorname{Res}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(X)
is a

so we need at least
d
linear conditions to cut out
\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
.

Siksek
[

]
has extended Chabauty–Coleman to curves over number fields by using
all primes of
K
above a fixed prime
p
and
the
gd
-dimensional variety
\operatorname{Res}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(J)
, which has
gd
linear
functionals to
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
,
given in terms of Coleman integrals.
Hence, if

\operatorname{rk}(J/K)\leq gd-d\,,

we can construct
d
linear equations
satisfied by
X(K)
in terms of Coleman integrals. While these equations
often cut out a finite set in practice, this is not always the case. For
instance, if
X
is defined over
\mathbb{Q}
and does not satisfy the Chabauty
rank condition over
\mathbb{Q}
, this method cannot succeed over any number field
K
, even if
X
satisfies the rank condition over
K
. In addition, the method sometimes does not work for more subtle reasons, as explained in
[
Dog23
]
.
For more work on Chabauty methods based on restriction of scalars,
see
[
Tri21
]
.

In
[
BBBM21
]
, Balakrishnan et al. extend the quadratic Chabauty
method from
[

]
and combine it with Siksek’s approach when

is monic and of odd degree.
One can show that there are at least
r_{2}+1
linearly independent

-adic
height pairings (see

, Section 2.2]
), corresponding to independent continuous

-adic idèle class characters of

, and each of these leads to an additional equation satisfied by the integral points. Hence the quadratic Chabauty method has a chance to work when

\operatorname{rk}(J/K)\leq dg-d+r_{2}+1=dg-r_{K}.

As in Siksek’s work,
a necessary condition for the method to cut out a finite set is that (

) is satisfied for all subfields of

over which

is defined.

5.1.
Linear quadratic Chabauty for integral points over number fields:
theory

In the remainder of this section, we extend the linear quadratic Chabauty method introduced in
the previous section to the case where

f\in\mathcal{O}_{K}[x]
is monic and of even degree
2g+2
.
We assume (
5.1
) throughout.
For practical reasons, we consider a prime
p\in\mathbb{Z}
of good reduction for

X
that splits completely in
K
, say
p\mathcal{O}_{K}=\mathfrak{p}_{1}\cdots\mathfrak{p}_{d}
. Otherwise, we
would have to compute Coleman integrals over proper extensions of
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
,
which is currently not implemented (this is work in progress due to
Best, Kaya and Keller).
However, as in
[
BBBM21
]
, our results
remain true for primes that do not split completely.

Denote by
\sigma_{i}
the embedding
\sigma_{i}\colon K\xhookrightarrow{}K_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}\simeq\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, for
1\leq i\leq d
. We obtain an
embedding
\sigma\colon K\xhookrightarrow{}K\otimes\mathbb{Q}_{p}\simeq\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}

defined by
\sigma(u)=(\sigma_{1}(u),\ldots,\sigma_{d}(u))
for
u\in K
. Via
this embedding, we identify
X(K\otimes\mathbb{Q}_{p})\simeq X(\mathbb{Q}_{p})^{d}
; there are
induced embeddings
\sigma\colon X(K)\xhookrightarrow{}X(K\otimes\mathbb{Q}_{p})

and
\sigma\colon\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{O}_{K})\xhookrightarrow{}\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{O}_{K}\otimes\mathbb{Z}_{p})
.
We also obtain embeddings
\sigma_{i}\colon J(K)\xhookrightarrow{}J(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
,
which lead to
dg
linear maps
J(K)\to\mathbb{Q}_{p}
given by

\ell_{j,i}(a)=\int_{\sigma_{i}(a)}\omega_{j},

for
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
and
j\in\{0,\ldots,g-1\}
. We also denote the
base-changed linear maps
\ell_{j,i}\colon J(K)\otimes\mathbb{Q}_{p}\to\mathbb{Q}_{p}
. Because of our assumption (
5.1
) there are at least
r_{K}
linear relations between the
\ell_{j,i}
’s in the space
(J(K)\otimes\mathbb{Q}_{p})^{\vee}
.

As in
[
BBBM21
, Condition 4.1]
, from now on, we assume the following condition.

Condition 1
.

We have
\langle\ell_{j,i}\rangle_{1\leq i\leq d,0\leq j\leq g-1}=(J(K)\otimes\mathbb{Q}_{p})^{\vee}
.

We also recall that for each continuous idèle class character
\chi\colon\mathbb{A}_{K}^{\ast}/K^{\ast}\to\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, there is a corresponding
p
-adic
height
h^{\chi}
.
From now on, we assume that
\chi
is ramified
over at least one prime
\mathfrak{p}
of
K
above
p
(see

section

2.3
). Since we assume
Condition
1
, we can express the linear map

\lambda^{\chi}\colon J(K)\to\mathbb{Q}_{p},\hskip 5.69054pt\lambda^{\chi}(a)=h^{\chi}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},a),

extended linearly to
J(K)\otimes\mathbb{Q}_{p}\to\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, as

(5.2)

\lambda^{\chi}=\sum_{j=0}^{g-1}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{j,i}^{\chi}\ell_{j,i},\text{ for some $\alpha_{j,i}^{\chi}\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}$.}

We decompose
h^{\chi}\colon J(K)\to\mathbb{Q}_{p}
into two parts

h^{\chi}(a)=\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\mid p}h^{\chi}_{\mathfrak{p}}(D)+\sum_{\mathfrak{q}\nmid p}h^{\chi}_{\mathfrak{q}}(D)\,,

where
D\in\operatorname{Div}^{0}(X)
represents
a\in J(K)
.

For
D\in\operatorname{Div}^{0}(X)
such that
\infty_{\pm}\notin\operatorname{supp}(D)
, we can rewrite Equation (
5.2
) as

\sum_{\mathfrak{q}\nmid p}h^{\chi}_{\mathfrak{q}}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},D)=\sum_{j=0}^{g-1}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{j,i}^{\chi}\int_{\sigma_{i}(D)}\omega_{j}-\sum_{i=1}^{d}h^{\chi}_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},\sigma_{i}(D)).

Suppose that there is an
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-integral point
Q\in\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
.
Then, as in Section
4
, for
\mathfrak{p}\mid p
, the
function
P\mapsto h^{\chi}_{\mathfrak{p}}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},P-Q)
is
locally analytic on
\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{O}_{K_{\mathfrak{p}}})
, since it is given by
Coleman integrals. Moreover, by Lemma
3.1
for
\mathfrak{q}\nmid p
, the function

P\mapsto h^{\chi}_{\mathfrak{q}}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},P-Q)

takes values in a computable finite set
T_{\mathfrak{q}}
when
P\in\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{O}_{K_{\mathfrak{q}}})
, and
T_{\mathfrak{q}}=\{0\}
for primes
\mathfrak{q}
of good reduction.
Hence we get
r_{2}+1
relations

\sum_{j=0}^{g-1}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{j,i}^{\chi}\int^{\sigma_{i}(P)}_{\sigma_{i}(Q)}\omega_{j}-\sum_{i=1}^{d}h^{\chi}_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},\sigma_{i}(P)-\sigma_{i}(Q))\in T_{\chi}

for
P\in\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
,
where
T_{\chi}
is a computable finite set.
Together with
r_{K}
linear relations of the shape

(5.3)

\sum_{j=0}^{g-1}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\beta_{j,i}^{(k)}\int_{\sigma_{i}(a)}\omega_{j}=0,

for
1\leq k\leq r_{K}
, we form a number of systems of
d
linear equations
which we try to use to determine
\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
.

Using the above, we obtain the following generalization of Theorem
4.1
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
X/K\colon y^{2}=f(x)
be a hyperelliptic curve such that
f\in\mathcal{O}_{K}[x]

is monic, squarefree, and has degree
2g+2
. Suppose
\operatorname{rk}(J/K)\leq dg-r_{K}
. Let
p\in\mathbb{Z}
be a prime that splits in
K
such that all primes
above
p
in
K
are primes of good reduction for
X
. Assume
Condition
1
and that there is point
Q\in\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
.
Fix independent continuous idèle
class characters
\chi_{r_{K}+1},\ldots,\chi_{d}
.

Then there are
d
computable locally analytic functions

\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{d}\colon\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{O}_{K}\otimes\mathbb{Z}_{p})\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}_{p}
with the following properties.

(i)

For
1\leq k\leq r_{K}
and
\mathcal{P}=(P_{1},\ldots,P_{d})\in\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{O}_{K}\otimes\mathbb{Z}_{p})
, we have

\rho_{k}(\mathcal{P})=\sum_{j=0}^{g-1}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\beta_{j,i}^{(k)}\int_{\sigma_{i}(Q)}^{P_{i}}\omega_{j}

and
\rho_{k}(\mathcal{P})=0
for
\mathcal{P}\in\sigma(\mathcal{U}(K))
.

(ii)

For
r_{K}+1\leq k\leq d
and
\mathcal{P}=(P_{1},\ldots,P_{d})\in\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{O}_{K}\otimes\mathbb{Z}_{p})
,
we have

\rho_{k}(\mathcal{P})=\sum_{j=0}^{g-1}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{j,i}^{\chi_{k}}\int_{\sigma_{i}(Q)}^{P_{i}}\omega_{j}-\sum_{i=1}^{d}h^{\chi_{k}}_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},P_{i}-\sigma_{i}(Q))\,;

moreover,
there is a finite and computable set
T_{k}
such that

\rho_{k}(\mathcal{P})\in T_{k}
for all
\mathcal{P}\in\sigma(\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{O}_{K}))
.

Proof.

The proof follows from the discussion above and by a straightforward modification of the proof of Theorem
4.1
.
∎

By the same reasoning as in Remark
4.2
and Remark
4.5
(
d
), it is not necessary to assume that we have found an
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-integral point on

.

5.2.
Computing
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-integral points

We now discuss how Theorem
5.1
may be used to compute the
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-integral points in practice. We imitate the steps from Algorithm
4.4
, except for a few minor changes that we now explain.

Steps (
i
), (
ii
) and (
iii
), or
(
vii
) are exactly as in Algorithm
4.4
. Step
(
iv
) now has two parts. First, we find
dg-r_{K}

linearly independent elements
a_{1},\ldots,a_{dg-r_{K}}\in J(K)
. Using basic
linear algebra, we find
r_{K}
linear relations amongst

\int_{\sigma_{i}(a_{s})}\omega_{j}
for
1\leq i\leq d
,
0\leq j\leq g-1
,

1\leq s\leq dg-r_{K}
. In this way, we get relations of the form
(
5.3
).
Next, for each chosen idèle class character
\chi
, we express

h^{\chi}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},a)
in terms of
\int_{\sigma_{i}(a)}\omega_{j}
,
again by computing all quantities involved for

a\in\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{dg-r_{K}}\}
. It is again a basic linear algebra task to
compute coefficients
\alpha_{j,i}^{\chi}
as in
(
5.2
).
From now on, we assume that the functions
\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{d}
have been computed.

Step (
v
) differs from the
\mathbb{Z}
-integral case because we
now consider
d
residue discs at once, coming from the
d
primes

\mathfrak{p}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{p}_{d}
. For each
\overline{\mathcal{P}}=(\overline{P_{1}},\ldots,\overline{P_{d}})\in\overline{X}(\mathbb{F}_{p})^{d}
, we introduce the residue polydisc notation

\mathcal{D}(\overline{\mathcal{P}})\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{(P_{1},\ldots,P_{d})\in X(K\otimes\mathbb{Q}_{p})\colon P_{k}\equiv\overline{P_{k}}\pmod{\mathfrak{p}_{k}},\text{ for each $1\leq k\leq d$}\}.

For each
\mathcal{P}\in\mathcal{D}(\overline{\mathcal{P}})
, we also use the
notation

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P})\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathcal{D}(\overline{\mathcal{P}})

for the residue polydisc that contains
\mathcal{P}
. We use any point

\mathcal{P}=(P_{1},\ldots,P_{d})\in\mathcal{D}(\overline{\mathcal{P}})
and well-behaved uniformizers

t_{\mathfrak{p}_{1}}=t_{\mathfrak{p}_{1}}(z_{1}),\ldots,

t_{\mathfrak{p}_{d}}=t_{\mathfrak{p}_{d}}(z_{d})

with respect to
\mathfrak{p}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{p}_{d}
, respectively, to parametrize all points
in
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P})
by tuples
(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})\in(p\mathbb{Z}_{p})^{d}
.

Next, we express the functions
\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{d}
in the parameters

(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})
by splitting Coleman integrals and local heights above

p
using the point
\mathcal{P}
. Namely, we imitate Step
(
v
) of Algorithm
4.4
, as explained in Remark
4.5
(b), considering residue discs with respect to all
\mathfrak{p}_{i}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
.

In Step (
vi
), we need to find solutions to systems of multivariable power series with separated variables. Namely, we get
d
equations of the shape

F_{k}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})=0
, where

•

F_{k}\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}[[z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}]]
;

•

F_{k}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})=a_{k}+F_{k,1}(z_{1})+\cdots+F_{k,d}(z_{d})
, with
a_{k}\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}
,
F_{k,j}\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}[[z_{j}]]
,
F_{k,j}(0)=0
, for all
1\leq j\leq d
;

•

for each
1\leq k\leq r_{K}
,
a_{k}
is a constant that depends only on
k
and
\mathcal{P}
;

•

for each
r_{K}+1\leq k\leq d
, there are
\#T_{k}
values of
a_{k}
, and
a_{k}
can be written as
a_{k}=b_{k}+u
, where
b_{k}
depends only on
k
and
\mathcal{P}
and
u\in T_{k}
; here
T_{k}
is the set defined in Theorem
5.1
.

In total, we get
\#T_{r_{K}+1}\cdot\#T_{r_{K}+2}\cdots\#T_{d}
systems to
solve. In general, we can solve such systems using a multivariable version
of Hensel’s Lemma proved in
[
Con
]
. For a practical algorithm for systems in two variables,
see
[
BBBM21
, Appendix A]
. We use a
SageMath
-implementation of this algorithm due to Francesca Bianchi.

We mention a specific situation that allows us to conclude that there is a unique solution to the system. This is a special case of
[
BBBM21
, Theorem A.1]
. Recall that
p\geq 3
. Let us write

F_{k,j}(z_{j})=c_{k,j,1}z_{j}+c_{k,j,2}z_{j}^{2}+c_{k,j,3}z_{j}^{3}+O(z_{j}^{4}).

Denote
\mathcal{M}=(c_{k,j,1})_{1\leq k,j\leq d}\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d\times d}
. If
\det(\mathcal{M})\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}
, then the system has a unique solution
(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})\in(p\mathbb{Z}_{p})^{d}
. This is particularly useful in the special case when
\mathcal{P}\in\sigma(\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{O}_{K}))
, and we can prove that
z_{1}=\cdots=z_{d}=0
is the unique solution to the system above. This implies that
\mathcal{P}
is the unique point in the intersection
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P})\cap\sigma(\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{O}_{K}))
.

We have implemented the method described in this section,
see
https://github.com/steffenmueller/LinQC
.

5.2.1.
Precision analysis

We only give a brief sketch of the precision analysis in a special case, which
includes our example in §
5.3
. In this
case, the precision analysis is quite
similar to
section

4.3
. As the variables in the
functions

\rho_{k}
are separated, the precision analysis for the computation of the

\rho_{k}
is the same as over
\mathbb{Q}
. For the same reason, solving for the roots of the

\mathbb{Q}

In this case, we use naive Hensel lifting, as explained in
[

. In particular, we never reach

[
BBBM21
, Algorithm 1]
, which would require a more

5.3.
A real quadratic example

Let
K=\mathbb{Q}(w)
, where
w^{2}=7
.
Using an implementation of the method discussed
in §
5.2
, we now show that the set of

\mathcal{O}_{K}
-integral points
\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
on the hyperelliptic
curve
X/K
defined by

y^{2}=x^{6}+x^{5}-wx^{4}+(1-w)x^{3}+x^{2}+wx+w

is exactly

(5.4)

\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{O}_{K})=\{(-1,0),(1,\pm 2)\}\,.

We are not aware of any other method that solves this problem.

It is easy to see that
\#J(K)_{\mathrm{tors}}=1
.
The
Magma
-implementation of the two-descent algorithm
from
[
Sto01
]
shows that
\operatorname{rk}(J/K)=3
. The points
a_{1}=[(1,-2)-\infty_{-}]
and
a_{2}=[(1,2)-(-1,0)]
are
independent and all differences of small
K
-rational points on

lie in
the subgroup
\langle a_{1},a_{2}\rangle\subset J(K)\otimes\mathbb{Q}
(note
that
X(K)
contains the small non-integral points
\infty_{\pm},(\frac{4-w}{3},\frac{196-94w}{27})
).
Let
a_{3}\in J(K)
be given by the Mumford representation

a_{3}=\left(x^{2}+\frac{125+43w}{81}x+\frac{28+197w}{567},\;\frac{121513+40337w}{45927}x+\frac{85001+23797w}{45927}\right)\,.

Then
J(K)\otimes\mathbb{Q}=\langle a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\rangle
.
We checked that in fact the subgroup of
J(K)
generated by
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}

is saturated at all primes below 100.

The prime
3
splits as
3\mathcal{O}_{K}=\mathfrak{p}_{1}\mathfrak{p}_{2}
and
X
has good reduction at
\mathfrak{p}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{p}_{2}
.
For all
1\leq j\leq 3
, the points
2a_{j}
have the property that

has a representative

such that for both

,

is of the form
Q_{1}+Q_{2}-R_{1}-R_{2}
, where

reduce to affine points and
R_{1},R_{2}\in X(K)

are the points with
x
-coordinate 1.
Working with these representatives, we easily check that
Condition
1
holds. Hence
Theorem
5.1
is applicable.

We now go through the steps of the number field version of
Algorithm
4.4
, discussed in §
5.2
.
We choose
Q=(-1,0)
to be our integral base point and we use
the cyclotomic idèle class character on
K
, which we denote by
\chi
and
which we drop from the notation.
Recall that for a real quadratic field,
\chi
is, up to scalar, the only
continuous idéle class character.
Lemma
3.1
shows that
T\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}T_{1}=\{0\}
.
Using the above-mentioned representatives, we compute the local heights

h_{q}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},D_{j})
for

via the
Magma
command

LocalIntersectionData
, which implements the algorithm
from
[
Mül14
]
. We compute the local heights

using the
SageMath
-implementation of the algorithm from

[
GM23
, §3.2]
.
This allows us to solve for coefficients
\alpha_{j,i}
(note that these
are not unique). The coefficients
\beta_{j,i}
are simpler to compute, requiring only

In this way, we find power series expansion of the two functions
\rho_{1}

and
\rho_{2}
in each residue disc. We solve for their common
zeros
(z_{1},z_{2})\in X(K\otimes\mathbb{Q}_{p})
using a

-implementation
of
[

, Algorithm A.1]
, due to Francesca Bianchi.

In addition to the known integral points

, there are 8

To show that they are not in the image of

, we apply the Mordell–Weil sieve. See
[
BBM17
]

and
[
BD18
, Appendix]
for more details. For each extra

\underline{z}
we compute

\underline{\tilde{\alpha}}=(\tilde{\alpha}_{1},\tilde{\alpha}_{2},\tilde{\alpha}_{3}),\in(\mathbb{Z}/3^{5}\mathbb{Z})^{3}

We use the prime ideals of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
generated by
(29,w+6),(37,w+28)
and

, respectively. The residue fields

of

as above, the corresponding coset of

.

).

5.4.
Obstructions to linear quadratic Chabauty

Let us now consider hyperelliptic
curves
X/\mathbb{Q}\colon y^{2}=f(x)=f_{2g+2}x^{2g+2}+\cdots+f_{0}
such that
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
is squarefree.
Assume first that
f_{2g+2}>1
is squarefree, so that the elementary
approach described in Appendix
A
is not applicable.
If we denote
K\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{f_{2g+2}})
, then
\infty_{\pm}\in X(K)\backslash X(\mathbb{Q})
. Therefore, we cannot use the method from Section

to determine the
\mathbb{Z}
-integral points on
X
.
It is tempting to try to compute

\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
using the approach of Section

, as

find
\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{Z})
. Unfortunately, this does not work, as we now explain.

Let
p
be a prime of good reduction that splits in
K
; write
p\mathcal{O}_{K}=\mathfrak{p}_{1}\mathfrak{p}_{2}
. Since
K
is a real quadratic field, there is only one
p
-adic height
h(\cdot,\cdot)
on
J(K)
(up to scaling), induced by the cyclotomic idèle class character.
However, it turns out that the restriction of

\lambda\colon J(K)\longrightarrow\mathbb{Q}_{p}\,;\quad a\mapsto h(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},a)\,.

to
J(\mathbb{Q})
is identically zero, due to Galois equivariance
of the Coleman-Gross height. See
[
Gaj22
, §6.5.5]
for more
details, and an alternative proof gives more precise
information on the local heights in
[
Gaj22
, Lemma 6.5.6, Remark
6.5.7]
. It follows that the resulting locally analytic
function is identically zero and hence cannot be used for quadratic
Chabauty, see
[
Gaj22
, §6.6.1]
.
In fact, a necessary condition to compute the
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-integral points over a
real quadratic field
K
using our method, is that the curve is not a
quadratic twist of a curve defined over
\mathbb{Q}
.
The problem persists for higher degree number fields containing
K
, and
similar problems arise if we use inert primes rather than split primes.

The issue described above generalizes to arbitrary quadratic fields if we
use the cyclotomic
p
-adic height.
If
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{f_{2g+2}})
is an imaginary quadratic field, the same
problem occurs if we instead use the
anticyclotomic idèle class character (see
[
Gaj22
, §6.5.4,§6.6.1]
).
Instead, one could use the sum of the cyclotomic and anticyclotomic height
as in
[
BD18
]
. In this
case, we have to require the stronger rank condition
\operatorname{rk}(J/K)\leq 2g-1
.

Remark 5.2
.

We expect that a modified version of the approach
from
[
BBM16
]
using
p
-adic Arakelov theory and double Coleman integrals can be used to compute the
\mathbb{Z}
-integral points on even degree hyperelliptic curves
y^{2}=f(x)
when
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
, assuming that the usual rank condition is satisfied.
We also expect that such an approach can be generalized to number fields as in
[
BBBM21
]
.

Appendix A
A special case of Runge’s method

For expository purposes, we recall how Runge’s method
[
Run87
]
(see
[
Sch08
, Chapter 5]
for a nice survey) can sometimes be used to determine the integral points on monic hyperelliptic curves of even degree in an elementary way. For a more general approach and a proof that uses Runge’s machinery, see
[
Wal95
, Theorem 3]
. Throughout this appendix, we assume that
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
is squarefree and of degree
2n>0
whose leading coefficient
a_{2n}
is a non-zero square in
\mathbb{Z}
, say
a_{2n}=b^{2}
, where
b\in\mathbb{N}
.

Theorem A.1
.

Let
X/\mathbb{Q}
be the hyperelliptic curve defined by
y^{2}=f(x)
.
There is an explicit constant
N>0
depending only on the coefficients of
f
, such that if
(x_{0},y_{0})\in X(\mathbb{Q})
with
x_{0},y_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
, then
|x_{0}|\leq N
.

We prove Theorem
A.1
using Lemma
A.2
and Lemma
A.3
.

Lemma A.2
.

There exists a
polynomial

(A.1)

g(x)=bx^{n}+c_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+c_{1}x+c_{0}\in\mathbb{Q}[x]

such that
\deg(f-g^{2})\leq n-1
. Furthermore, we have

c_{i}\in\dfrac{1}{(2b)^{2(n-i)-1}}\mathbb{Z}\,\quad\text{ for }\;0\leq i\leq n-1\,.

Proof.

We explicitly construct a polynomial
g
satisfying (
A.1
) by equating coefficients
of
x^{k}
for
n\leq k\leq 2n-1
in
g^{2}
and
f
.
From these equalities, we see inductively that each
c_{i}
for
n-1\geq i\geq 0
is uniquely
determined, and that
c_{i}\in\dfrac{1}{(2b)^{2(n-i)-1}}\mathbb{Z}
.
Thus,
g\in\dfrac{1}{(2b)^{2n-1}}\mathbb{Z}[x]\subset\mathbb{Q}[x]
.
∎

Let us denote
h\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}f-g^{2}
, where
g
is defined by Lemma
A.2
. Multiply
h
and
f
by the minimal power
(2b)^{2k}
of
2b
such that
(2b)^{2k}h,(2b)^{2k}f,(2b)^{k}g
have integral coefficients.
By Lemma
A.2
,
we know that
k\leq 2n-1
. Let
F\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(2b)^{2k}f
,
G\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(2b)^{k}g

and
H\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(2b)^{2k}h
; then
F=G^{2}+H
. We can write

G(x)=p_{n}x^{n}+\cdots+p_{1}x+p_{0},\hskip 8.53581ptH(x)=q_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+q_{1}x+q_{0},

where
p_{0},\ldots,p_{n},q_{0},\ldots,q_{n-1}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
p_{n}\neq 0
, and not all
q_{i}
’s
are zero.

The integral points on
X
inject into the integral points on the curve

X^{\prime}\colon y^{2}=F(x)
via

(x_{0},y_{0})\in X(\mathbb{Q})\mapsto(x_{0},(2b)^{k}y_{0})\in X^{\prime}(\mathbb{Q})
.
We can bound the integral points on

X^{\prime}
in the following way. Denote

M\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\dfrac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(2|p_{i}|+|q_{i}|)+1}{2|p_{n}|}.

Lemma A.3
.

If
(x_{0},y_{0})\in X^{\prime}(\mathbb{Q})
is an
integral point, then
H(x_{0})=0
or

|x_{0}|\leq M
.

Proof.

We prove that
F(x_{0})
lies
between
(G(x_{0})-1)^{2}
and
(G(x_{0})+1)^{2}
for almost all
x_{0}
. On the one
hand, we have

\displaystyle|(G(x)\pm 1)^{2}-G(x)^{2}|

\displaystyle=|2G(x)\pm 1|\geq 2|G(x)|-1

\displaystyle\geq 2|p_{n}||x|^{n}-2|p_{n-1}||x|^{n-1}-\cdots-2|p_{1}||x|-2|p_{0}|-1\mathrel{{=}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}}P_{U}(|x|)\,.

On the other hand,

|H(x)|\leq|q_{n-1}||x|^{n-1}+\cdots+|q_{1}||x|+|q_{0}|.

Therefore we deduce

P_{U}(|x|)-|H(x)|\geq 2|p_{n}||x|^{n}-(2|p_{n-1}|+|q_{n-1}|)|x|^{n-1}-\cdots-(2|p_{1}|+|q_{1}|)|x|-(2|p_{0}|+|q_{0}|)-1.

For
x\in\mathbb{Z}
,
x\neq 0
, we have the inequalities
|x|^{k}\leq|x|^{n-1}
for all

0\leq k\leq n-1
, so

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(2|p_{i}|+|q_{i}|)|x|^{i}+1\leq\left(\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(2|p_{i}|+|q_{i}|)+1\right)|x|^{n-1}.

For
|x|>M
it follows that

\left(\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(2|p_{i}|+|q_{i}|)+1\right)|x|^{n-1}<2|p_{n}||x|^{n}.

Thus, for
|x_{0}|>M
, we have

|(G(x_{0})\pm 1)^{2}-G(x_{0})^{2}|\geq P_{U}(|x_{0}|)>|H(x_{0})|,

implying that
G(x_{0})^{2}+H(x_{0})
lies between
(G(x_{0})-1)^{2}
and
(G(x_{0})+1)^{2}
. Hence, if it is a square of an integer, it must be exactly
G(x_{0})^{2}
, implying
H(x_{0})=0
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
A.1
.

Let
(x_{0},y_{0})\in X(\mathbb{Q})
be an integral point. Using the injection of
integral points of
X
into the integral points of
X^{\prime}
, Lemma

A.3
, implies that
|x_{0}|\leq M
or
H(x_{0})=0
. If
H(x_{0})=0
, we
can easily bound
|x_{0}|
.
Furthermore, the proof is constructive, and the required bounds are computed only in terms of coefficients of
f
. Hence we have described an algorithm that in a finite number of steps can determine all integral points on
X
, finishing the proof of Theorem
A.1
.
∎

Remark A.4
.

(1)

We do not state the bound
N
explicitly in terms of the coefficients of
f
. However, it is clear from the construction that the bound is a rational function in terms of the coefficients of
f
. In practice, we found that the bound is sufficiently small to easily compute the integral points.

(2)

The bounds from the algorithm can be improved. Namely, using standard real analysis, we can locate
all zeros of
H
and find all real intervals on which the inequality
P_{U}(|x|)\leq|H(x)|
is satisfied.

(3)

We can determine
\overline{X}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
, for some set of small primes
p
, imposing conditions on
x
-coordinates modulo
p
. In this way, we can speed up the search for integral points on
X
.

Remark A.5
.

If we assume that
a_{2n}<0
, then there is also an elementary method to determine the integral points on
X
. Since
\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty_{-}}f(x)=\lim_{x\to\infty_{+}}f(x)=-\infty
, it follows that
f(x)\geq 0
can only happen for finitely many
x\in\mathbb{Z}
. Using basic calculus, it is easy to determine an explicit constant
M^{\prime}
, depending only on the coefficients of
f
, such that if
|x|>M^{\prime}
, then
f(x)\leq-1
.
