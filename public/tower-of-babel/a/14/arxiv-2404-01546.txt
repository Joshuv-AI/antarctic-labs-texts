Time-Varying Matrix Factor Models
Bin Chen, Elynn Y. Chen, Stevenson Bolivar, Rong Chen

Abstract
Matrix-variate data of high dimensions are frequently observed in finance and economics, spanning extended time periods, such as the long-term data on international trade flows among numerous countries. To address potential structural shifts and explore the matrix structure’s informational context, we propose a time-varying matrix factor model. This model accommodates changing factor loadings over time, revealing the underlying dynamic structure through nonparametric principal component analysis and facilitating dimension reduction. We establish the consistency and asymptotic normality of our estimators under general conditions that allow for weak correlations across time, rows, or columns of the noise. A novel approach is introduced to overcome rotational ambiguity in the estimators, enhancing the clarity and interpretability of the estimated loading matrices. Our simulation study highlights the merits of the proposed estimators and the effective of the smoothing operation. In an application to international trade flow, we investigate the trading hubs, centrality, patterns, and trends in the trading network. JEL Classifications : C13, C14, C32, C55 Keywords: High-dimensionality, Kernel estimation, Matrix factor models, Matrix time series, Time-varying loadings.

1
Introduction

Matrix-variate data are increasingly prevalent across various fields, such as economics, finance, and social networks. Two significant types of such data are particularly noteworthy: Dynamic transportation networks are represented by matrices of monthly import-export volumes between countries, highlighting the complex, weighted, and directional relationships. Dynamic panel data, vital for investor portfolio decisions, are matrices capturing various firm attributes (e.g., stock prices, book-to-market ratio, dividend-to-price ratio) at different times, capturing firm performance and market position.

The field of statistical analysis for matrix-variate data is still emerging, with matrix factor models being pivotal for dimension reduction and uncovering inherent structures.
Wang et al. (2019)
introduced a matrix factor model for time series, featuring constant matrix factor loadings and time-varying latent factors, estimated through eigenanalysis of auto-cross-covariance matrices. This model has been expanded into a constrained version by
Chen et al. (2020)
and a threshold variant by
Liu & Chen (2019)
. Contrasting these approaches,
Chen & Fan (2023)
developed a novel estimation method by analyzing an aggregate of the sample mean matrix and contemporary covariance matrices, proving to be advantageous in several contexts.

While matrix factor models have been proven effective, a common assumption in the literature is that factor loadings remain constant over time – an assumption not always realistic in practice. Changes in policies, preferences, technology, and environmental factors can influence economic variables’ relationships, challenging the stability of factor loadings. Empirical evidence, such as that presented by
Chen et al. (2022)
, indicates that ignoring these potential shifts in factor loadings can result in inconsistent estimates and unreliable conclusions.

Recently, the concept of smooth structural changes in economic variables has garnered attention, leading to the development of time-varying time series and panel data models. These models, particularly nonparametric time-varying parameter models introduced by
Robinson (1989)
and further explored by
Cai (2007)
,
Chen & Hong (2012)
,
Zhang & Wu (2012)
,
Inoue et al. (2017)
, and
Chen & Maung (2023)
, offer a flexible approach to modeling economic variables. These models stand out for their minimal assumptions on the time-varying parameters’ functional forms, requiring only that they change smoothly over time. Inspired by the versatility and effectiveness of this approach, we employ this framework to propose time-varying matrix factor models for analyzing matrix time series.

In this paper, we investigate a matrix factor model with loading matrices defined as unknown, smooth, time-varying functions. We aim to estimate these functions and the latent factors using local Principal Component Analysis (PCA), developing a theory of inference that includes consistency, convergence rates, and limiting distributions. This approach accommodates time-varying loadings and weak correlations across time, rows, or columns of the noise matrices. We generalize the eigenvalue ratio-based estimator for latent dimensions
(
Ahn & Horenstein, 2013
)
to our time-varying context and establish the consistency of the new method.

Traditional methods reduce matrix observations into vectors and apply local PCA to high-dimensional factor models
(
Su & Wang, 2017
)
, losing essential matrix structure and leading to less effective analyses. Our matrix-based estimator is compared against vectorized approaches through simulations to demonstrate its superior ability to preserve and leverage the matrix structure. Additionally, we tackle the rotation ambiguity inherent in matrix factor models by introducing a smoothing process for the time-varying loading spaces, facilitating clearer interpretation.

This paper is organized as follows: Section
2
introduces matrix factor models with time-varying loadings. Section
3
describes our local PCA estimation approach and a generalized eigenvalue ratio-based estimator for latent dimensions. Section
4
discusses the theoretical foundations, including consistency and asymptotic normality. Section
5
details the smoothing process for time-varying functions. Section
6
assesses our methods through simulation, while Section
7
applies them to international trade data. All mathematical proofs and additional results are provided in the Appendix.

2
Matrix Factor Models with Time-Varying Loadings

Let
\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}
,
t\in[T]
, be a matrix-valued time series, where each
\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}=[Y_{t,ij}]
is a matrix of size
p\times q
.
We consider the following matrix factor models with time-varying factor loadings,

\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}=\boldsymbol{R}_{t}\boldsymbol{F}_{t}\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}_{t}+\boldsymbol{E}_{t},\quad t\in[T],

(2.1)

where
\boldsymbol{F}_{t}
is the
k\times r
common factor matrix,
\boldsymbol{R}_{t}
(
\boldsymbol{C}_{t}
) is a
p\times k
(
q\times r
) time-varying row (column) loading matrix and the sequence of matrices
\boldsymbol{E}_{t}
is the noise matrix.
The noise term
\boldsymbol{E}_{t}
is assumed to be uncorrelated with
\boldsymbol{F}_{t}
, yet is allowed to be weakly correlated across rows, columns, and times.
It generalizes the matrix factor model:

\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}=\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{F}_{t}\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{E}_{t},

(2.2)

by allowing for structural changes in factor loadings
\boldsymbol{R}
and
\boldsymbol{C}
. To cover a wide range of potential smooth temporal variation, we follow the literature on smooth time-varying parameter models
(
Robinson, 1989
;
Cai, 2007
;
Su & Wang, 2017
)
) and model
\boldsymbol{R}_{t,i.}
(
\boldsymbol{C}_{t,j.}
) as a non-stochastic function of
t/T
, that is,

\boldsymbol{R}_{t,i.}=\boldsymbol{R}_{i\cdot}(t/T)\mbox{ \ \ and \ \ }\boldsymbol{C}_{t,j.}=\boldsymbol{C}_{j\cdot}(t/T),

where
\boldsymbol{R}_{t,i.}(\cdot)
(
\boldsymbol{C}_{t,j.}(\cdot)
) is an unknown smooth function of
t/T
on
[0,1]
for each
i
(
j
). The specification that loading matrices
\boldsymbol{R}_{t,i.}(\cdot)
and
\boldsymbol{C}_{t,j.}(\cdot)
are some functions of ratio
t/T
rather than time
t
only is a common scaling scheme in the literature for the ease of theoretical analysis
(
Phillips & Hansen, 1990
;
Robinson, 1991
;
Cai, 2007
)
.
We note that model (
2.1
) is subject to scale and rotational ambiguity in its individual components
\boldsymbol{R}_{t}
,
\boldsymbol{F}_{t}
and
\boldsymbol{C}_{t}
. Our objective is to estimate sensible representations of
\boldsymbol{R}_{t}
and
\boldsymbol{C}_{t}
among their equivalent classes and the corresponding
\boldsymbol{F}_{t}
, as the signal
\boldsymbol{S}_{t}=\boldsymbol{R}_{t}\boldsymbol{F}_{t}\boldsymbol{C}_{t}^{\top}
is uniquely defined.

As a motivating example, we introduce the international trade flow time series, with a detailed analysis in Section
7
. The data,
\mathbf{Y}_{t}
, is represented as a square matrix for each time
t
, where each element,
y_{t,ij}
, indicates the trade volume from country
i
(exporter) to country
j
(importer). The time series, spanning from January 1982 to December 2018, depicted in Figure 1, showcases the non-stationary nature of the data, highlighting a globalization trend.
Chen & Chen (2023)
interpreted the matrix factor model for transport data, viewing the factor process
\boldsymbol{F}_{t}
as representing trade volumes among
(latent) trading hubs
, analogous to trade volumes among groups of countries. This conceptualization suggests that a country’s exports pass through these trading hubs before reaching the importing country, with
\boldsymbol{F}_{t}
detailing hub-to-hub trade activities. The loading matrices
\boldsymbol{R}_{t}
and
\boldsymbol{C}_{t}
indicate each country’s involvement with the hubs, with elements like
\boldsymbol{R}_{t,11}
showing country 1’s interaction with export hub 1. The cumulative export volume of a country, such as country 1, is calculated as
\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{r}\boldsymbol{R}_{t,1k}\boldsymbol{F}_{t,k1}
. A visual representation for June 2016 (left figure in Figure
2
) demonstrates this model, where hub sizes reflect trade volume
\boldsymbol{F}_{t}
and line widths show participation levels
\boldsymbol{R}_{t}
and
\boldsymbol{C}_{t}
, with minor values (less than 0.15) excluded for clarity. This visualization reveals, for example, significant trade between China’s primary export hub and the main import hubs (Hub 1 and 2) used by the US, Canada, Mexico, Japan, Korea, and Taiwan in that month. The right figure in Figure
2
illustrates changes over time in China’s export loading vectors, highlighting structural shifts in trading patterns based on a five-year moving analysis.

3
Estimation

The time-varying nature of (
2.1
) presents a significant challenge in estimating
\boldsymbol{R}_{t}
,
\boldsymbol{C}_{t}
, and
\boldsymbol{F}_{t}
, as there is effectively only one sample point available at each time
t
. To see this challenge, we first analyze the problem on the population level. Under the time-varying matrix factor model (
2.1
), the second
uncentered
moment writes

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{R}_{t}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\boldsymbol{F}_{t}\boldsymbol{C}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{t}\boldsymbol{F}_{t}^{\top}\right]\boldsymbol{R}_{t}^{\top}+{\mathbb{E}}\left[\boldsymbol{E}_{t}\boldsymbol{E}_{t}^{\top}\right],

(3.1)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{C}_{t}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\boldsymbol{F}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{R}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{R}_{t}\boldsymbol{F}_{t}\right]\boldsymbol{C}_{t}^{\top}+{\mathbb{E}}\left[\boldsymbol{E}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{E}_{t}\right],

(3.2)

which aggregate the information of unknown parameters
\boldsymbol{R}_{t}
and
\boldsymbol{C}_{t}
from both the first moment
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}\right]=\boldsymbol{R}_{t}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\boldsymbol{F}_{t}\right]\boldsymbol{C}_{t}^{\top}
and the second
centered
moment
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}-{\mathbb{E}}\left[\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}\right]\right)\left(\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}-{\mathbb{E}}\left[\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}\right]\right)^{\top}\right]
.

Noticing that the first term on the right-hand-side of (
3.1
) is of rank
k
under some mild conditions and ignoring the second term (as justified by the pervasive assumption below), it is easy to see
\boldsymbol{R}_{t}
is the same as the top
k
eigenvectors of the second moment, up to an affine transformation. Similar observation holds for
\boldsymbol{C}_{t}
with (
3.2
).

This motivates the employment of the spectral method based on the sample estimators of
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}^{\top}\right]
and
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}\right]
to estimate
\boldsymbol{R}_{t}
and
\boldsymbol{C}_{t}
.
As model (
2.1
) is time varying, we use nonparametric kernel smoothing to estimate
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}^{\top}\right]
and
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}\right]
, based on the smoothness assumption of the loading functions
\boldsymbol{R}_{i\cdot}(t/T)
and

\boldsymbol{C}_{i\cdot}(t/T)
and the stationary assumptions of the factor
process
\boldsymbol{F}_{t}
, which leads to smoothness of
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}^{\top}\right]
and
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}\right]
in
t/T
.
Specifically, we define sample scatter matrix
\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{R,t}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{C,t}
as

\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{R,t}=\frac{1}{pqT}\sumop\displaylimits_{s=1}^{T}K_{h,ts}\boldsymbol{Y}_{s}\boldsymbol{Y}_{s}^{\top}\\
\mbox{ \ \ and \ \ }\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{C,t}=\frac{1}{pqT}\sumop\displaylimits_{s=1}^{T}K_{h,ts}\boldsymbol{Y}_{s}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Y}_{s}

(3.3)

where
K_{h,st}
is a boundary-modified kernel function:

K_{h,st}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}h^{-1}k(\frac{s-t}{Th})/\intop\nolimits_{-t/\left(Th\right)}^{1}k\left(u\right)\,du&ift\in\left[1,\lfloor Th\rfloor\right],\\
h^{-1}k(\frac{s-t}{Th})&ift\in\left[\lfloor Th\rfloor,T-\lfloor Th\rfloor\right],\\
h^{-1}k(\frac{s-t}{Th})/\intop\nolimits_{-1}^{\left(1-t/T\right)/h}k\left(u\right)\,du&otherwise.\end{array}\right.

(3.4)

The kernel
k(\cdot):[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is a predetermined symmetric probability density function, with
h=h(p,q,T)
as the bandwidth parameter for the row (column) loading matrix
\boldsymbol{R}_{t}
(
\boldsymbol{C}_{t}
), and
\lfloor Th\rfloor
representing the integer part of
Th
. Common examples of
k(\cdot)
are the uniform, Epanechnikov, and quartic kernels
(
Hastie et al., 2009
)
.

To estimate
\boldsymbol{R}_{t}
, and
\boldsymbol{C}_{t}
, we use
\sqrt{p}
(
\sqrt{q}
) multiplied by the eigenvectors corresponding to the
k
(
r
) largest eigenvalues of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{R,t}
(
\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{C,t}
).
Due to rotational ambiguity, the solution
\widehat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{t}
(
\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}_{t}
) is one of the representatives of the column space of
\boldsymbol{R}_{t}
(
\boldsymbol{C}_{t}
). They do not necessarily constitute a smooth function
\boldsymbol{R}_{t}=\boldsymbol{R}(t/T)
as
t
changes. In Section
5
we present a
smoothing procedure to obtain a smooth
representation. Furthermore, the realized latent factor
\boldsymbol{F}_{t}
and
the signal part
\boldsymbol{S}_{t}
can be obtained by

\widehat{}\boldsymbol{F}_{t}=\frac{1}{pq}\widehat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}_{t}\mbox{\ \ and \ \ }\widehat{}\boldsymbol{S}_{t}=\frac{1}{pq}\widehat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{t}\widehat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}_{t}\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}_{t}^{\top}.

The above local-PCA-based method assumes that the latent dimensions
k\times r
are known. However, in practice, we need to estimate
k
and
r
as well. We extend the eigenvalue ratio-based estimator, proposed by
Ahn & Horenstein (2013)
. Let
\widehat{\lambda}_{1,t}\geq\widehat{\lambda}_{2,t}\geq\cdots\geq\widehat{\lambda}_{k,t}\geq 0
be the ordered eigenvalues of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{R,t}
. The generalized eigenvalue ratio-based estimator for
k
can be defined as

\widehat{k}=\underset{1\leq j\leq k_{max}}{\text{argmax}}\frac{1}{T}\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\widehat{\lambda}_{j,t}}{\widehat{\lambda}_{j+1,t}},

(3.5)

and
k_{max}
is a selected upper bound. We could choose
k_{max}=\lfloor p/2\rfloor
or
k_{max}=\lfloor p/3\rfloor
. The estimator
\widehat{r}
can be defined analogously with respect to
\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{C,t}
.

4
Theory

Let
\boldsymbol{R}_{t}^{\left(1\right)}

\left(\boldsymbol{C}_{t}^{\left(1\right)}\right)
and
\boldsymbol{R}_{t}^{\left(2\right)}

\left(\boldsymbol{C}_{t}^{\left(2\right)}\right)
denote the first and second order derivative of
\boldsymbol{R}_{t}

\left(\boldsymbol{C}_{t}\right)
respectively, and
\boldsymbol{R}_{t}^{\left(0\right)}=\boldsymbol{R}_{t}
and
\boldsymbol{C}_{t}^{\left(0\right)}=\boldsymbol{C}_{t}
. To derive the asymptotic properties of our estimators, we impose the following regularity conditions.

Assumption 1

(
\alpha
-mixing
)
.

The vector-variate process

\{{\textsc{vec}}\left(\boldsymbol{F}_{t}\right)^{\top},{\textsc{vec}}\left(\boldsymbol{E}_{t}\right)^{\top}\}^{\top}

is a
\alpha
-mixing process with mixing coefficients

\alpha(h)=\underset{\tau}{\sup}\underset{A\in\mathcal{F}_{-\infty}^{\tau},B\in\mathcal{F}_{\tau+h}^{\infty}}{\sup}\left|P(A\cap B)-P(A)P(B)\right|
, where
\mathcal{F}_{\tau}^{s}
is the
\sigma
-field generated by
\{{\textsc{vec}}\left(\boldsymbol{F}_{t}\right)^{\top},{\textsc{vec}}\left(\boldsymbol{E}_{t}\right)^{\top}\}^{\top}:{\tau}\leq t\leq s\}
, and the mixing coefficients satisfy the condition that

\sumop\displaylimits_{h=1}^{\infty}\alpha(h)^{1-2/\gamma}<\infty
,
for some
\gamma>2
.

Assumption 2

(
Factor and noise matrices
)
.

There is a positive constant
C<\infty
such that

(a)

Factor matrix
\boldsymbol{F}_{t}
is of fixed dimension
k\times r
, and
\max_{t}\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{F}_{t}\right\rVert^{4}\leq C
.

(b)

For all
i\in[p]
,
j\in[q]
and
t\in[T]
,
{\mathbb{E}}\left[e_{t,ij}\right]=0
and
\mathbb{E}\lvert e_{t,ij}\rvert^{8}\leq C
.

(c)

Factor and noise are uncorrelated, that is,
{\mathbb{E}}\left[e_{t,ij}f_{s,lh}\right]=0
for any
t,s\in[T]
,
i\in[p]
,
j\in[q]
,
l\in[k]
, and
h\in[r]
.

Assumption 3

(
Loading matrix
)
.

There exists a positive constant
C<\infty
such that

(a)

For each row of
\boldsymbol{R}_{t}
,
\max_{t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{R}_{t,i\cdot}}\right\|\leq C
, and, as
p,q\rightarrow\infty
, we have
\max_{t}\left\|{p^{-1}{\boldsymbol{R}_{t}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{R}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{R,t}}\right\|\longrightarrow 0
for some
k\times k
positive definite matrix
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{R,t}
.

(b)

For each row of
\boldsymbol{C}_{t}
,
\max_{t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{C}_{t,i\cdot}}\right\|\leq C
, and, as
p,q\rightarrow\infty
,
\max_{t}\left\|{q^{-1}{\boldsymbol{C}_{t}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{C,t}}\right\|\longrightarrow 0
for some
r\times r
positive definite matrix
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{C,t}
.

Assumption 4

(
Cross row (column) correlation of noise
\boldsymbol{E}_{t}
)
.

There exists some positive constant
C<\infty
such that,

(a)

Let
\boldsymbol{U}_{E}={\mathbb{E}}\left[\boldsymbol{E}_{t}\boldsymbol{E}_{t}^{T}/q\right]
and
\boldsymbol{V}_{E}={\mathbb{E}}\left[\boldsymbol{E}_{t}^{T}\boldsymbol{E}_{t}/p\right]
, we assume
\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}_{E}}\right\|_{1}\leq C
and
\left\|{\boldsymbol{V}_{E}}\right\|_{1}\leq C
.

(b)

For all
i\in[p]
and
j\in[q]
and
t\in[T]
, we assume
\sumop\displaylimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\in[p]\\
l\neq i\end{subarray}}\sumop\displaylimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in[q]\\
h\neq j\end{subarray}}\lvert{\mathbb{E}}\left[e_{t,ij}e_{t,lh}\right]\rvert\leq C
.

(c)

For any row
i,l\in[p]
, any time
t,s\in[T]
, and any column
j\in[q]
,

\sumop\displaylimits_{m\in[p]}\sumop\displaylimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in[q]\\
h\neq j\end{subarray}}\lvert{\rm Cov}\left[\left(e_{t,ij}e_{t,lj},\;e_{s,ih}e_{s,mh}\right)\right]\rvert\leq C

Similar, for any column
j,h\in[q]
, any time
t\in[T]
, and any row
i\in[p]
,

\sumop\displaylimits_{m\in[q]}\sumop\displaylimits_{s\in[T]}\sumop\displaylimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\in[p]\\
l\neq i\end{subarray}}\lvert{\rm Cov}\left[\left(e_{t,ij}e_{t,ih},\;e_{s,lj}e_{s,lm}\right)\right]\rvert\leq C.

Assumption 5
.

\boldsymbol{R}_{i\cdot}(t/T)
and
\boldsymbol{C}_{j\cdot}(t/T)
have continuous derivatives up to the second order. Moreover, there exists
m>2
,
1<a,b<\infty
,
1/a+1/b=1
,
c=0,1,2
such that, for some positive
C<\infty
, and any
t\in[T]
,

(a)

For any
l\in[k]
, and
i\in[p]
,
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\lvert\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{q}e_{t,ij}\rvert^{mb}\right]={{\cal O}}\left(1\right)
,
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\lVert\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{q}\boldsymbol{C}_{t,j\cdot}^{\left(c\right)}e_{t,ij}\right\rVert^{mb}\right]={{\cal O}}\left(1\right)
, and
\max_{t}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\lVert\boldsymbol{f}_{t,l\cdot}\right\rVert^{ma}\right]\leq C
.

(b)

For any
h\in[r]
, and
j\in[q]
,

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\lvert\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{p}e_{t,ij}\rvert^{mb}\right]={{\cal O}}\left(1\right)
,

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\lVert\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{p}\boldsymbol{R}_{t,i\cdot}^{\left(c\right)}e_{t,ij}\right\rVert^{mb}\right]={{\cal O}}\left(1\right)
,
and
\max_{t}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\lVert\boldsymbol{f}_{t,\cdot h}\right\rVert^{ma}\right]\leq C
.

(c)

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\lvert\frac{1}{\sqrt{pq}}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{p}\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{q}e_{t,ij}\rvert^{mb}\right]={{\cal O}}\left(1\right)
and
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\lVert\frac{1}{\sqrt{pq}}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{p}\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{q}\boldsymbol{R}_{t,i\cdot}^{\left(c\right)}\boldsymbol{C}_{t,j\cdot}^{\left(c\right)\top}e_{t,ij}\right\rVert^{mb}\right]={{\cal O}}\left(1\right)
.

Assumption 6
.

k:[-1,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
is a symmetric and Lipschitz
continuous probability density function such that
\intop\nolimits_{-1}^{1}k(u)du=1
,
\intop\nolimits_{-1}^{1}uk(u)du=0
, and
\intop\nolimits_{-1}^{1}u^{2}k(u)du<\infty
.

The
\alpha
-mixing condition in Assumption
1
allows weak temporal correlations for both the factors and noises. Assumption
2
mainly imposes moment conditions on the errors, factors, and their interactions. Assumption
3
is an extension of the pervasive assumption
(
Stock & Watson, 2002
)
to the matrix variate data.
It ensures that each row and column of the factor matrix
\boldsymbol{F}_{t}
has a nontrivial contribution to the variance of rows and columns of
\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}
. Assumptions
1
-
3
are standard in the literature on factor models and similar assumptions have been imposed in
Chen & Fan (2023)
and
Yu et al. (2022)
.

Assumption
1
addresses temporal dependence, while the dimensions
p
and
q
influence our estimator’s convergence rates through cross-row and cross-column dependencies. To leverage information across rows and columns, we introduce Assumptions
4
and
5
, which are met if errors
\boldsymbol{E}_{t}
are i.i.d. across rows and columns at any time
t
, independent from factor
\boldsymbol{F}_{t}
, and meet specific moment conditions. These assumptions accommodate mild cross-row, cross-column, and temporal dependencies. Assumption
6
is standard for kernel regression and is fulfilled by typical second-order kernels like Epanechnikov, uniform, and quartic kernels.

Let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{V}}_{R,t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{V}}_{C,t}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
be the time-varying diagonal matrices consisting of the first
k
and
r
largest eigenvalues of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{R,t}
(
\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{C,t}
) in a descending order respectively. By the definition of our estimators
\widehat{\boldsymbol{R}_{t}}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}_{t}}
, we have

\widehat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{t}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{R,t}\widehat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{t}\widehat{\boldsymbol{V}}_{R,t}^{-1}\mbox{\ \ and \ \ }\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}_{t}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{C,t}\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}_{t}\widehat{\boldsymbol{V}}_{C,t}^{-1}.

Similar to traditional factor models, the latent factor matrix
\boldsymbol{F}_{t}
and loading matrices
\boldsymbol{R}_{t}

5
Smooth Loading Functions

In this section, we present an approach to obtaining a ‘smooth’ estimation of the loading functions
\boldsymbol{R}_{t}=\boldsymbol{R}(t/T)
(
\boldsymbol{C}_{t}=\boldsymbol{C}(t/T)
), which is important for interpretation and visualization.
We present the method for
\boldsymbol{R}_{t}
, and that for
\boldsymbol{C}_{t}
is similar.

First we note that the estimator

\widehat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{t}
obtained in Section
3
are not necessarily smooth over
t/T
, which is incurred by the fact that
\widehat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{t}
are estimated separately for each time
t
, the inherit rotational ambiguity associated with PCA, and the possible eigenvalue coalescing at time
t-1
and
t
.
Specifically, the columns of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{t}
are
\sqrt{p}
multiplied by the top
k
eigenvectors of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{R,t}
corresponding to the
k\times 1
eigenvalue vector
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t}=[\widehat{\alpha}_{t,1},\cdots,\widehat{\alpha}_{t,r}]^{\top}
in descending order.
The matrix
\boldsymbol{M}_{R,t}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{R,t}
are both smooth functions of
t
, under the assumption that
\boldsymbol{R}_{t}
and
\boldsymbol{C}_{t}
are smooth.
When
\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{R,t}

and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{R,t-1}
are close, the eigenvectors
\widehat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{t}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{t-1}
and the eigenvalues
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t-1}
would be close most of the time, except in two situations: (1) a
sign change
in the eigenvalue/eigenvector when
\widehat{\alpha}_{t-1,i}
and
\widehat{\alpha}_{t,i}
changes signs; and (2) an
order switching
in the eigenvalues when the eigenvector
\widehat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{\cdot j}((t-1)/T)
is closer to
\widehat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{\cdot j+1}(t/T)
than to
\widehat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{\cdot j}(t/T)
. This happens when
the eigenvalues at
t-1
and
t
corresponding to the smooth eigenvectors switch
order from
j
to
j+1
and vice versa. We assume the switching only involves two adjacent eigenvalues.

Motta et al. (2023)
introduce the
\sqrt{2}
-signed and
\sqrt{2}
-bridged methods for tracking smoothly evolving covariance matrices in independent normal observations. The
\sqrt{2}
-signed technique assigns eigenvector signs at time
t
based on closeness to those at
t-1
, starting with an order determined by singular values at
t=1
. The
\sqrt{2}
-bridged approach identifies coalescing areas where two eigenvalues nearly interchange in magnitude.

Our setting is different from that considered in

Motta et al. (2023)
as we consider cross-moment

\boldsymbol{M}_{R,t}
with a potentially underlying
time varying mean, and there is a strong serial
dependence induced by the smoothing operation in
the construction of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{R,t}
. Empirical
study shows that the approach by
Motta et al. (2023)

indeed encounters difficulty in our situation.
We propose an alternative method for identifying coalescing regions, a crucial input for the second stage of the procedure introduced by
Motta et al. (2023)
. We treat the problem as a
jump point detection
problem under nonparametric smoothing framework. Specifically we use left and right kernels to estimate the underlying smooth functions on the left and right of the potential jump points and use them to construct
a test statistics that is sensitive to jumps.

Define the one sided kernel
K^{*}_{h^{*},st}=2h^{*-1}k(\frac{s-t}{Th^{*}})
,
and correspondingly

\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{R,t}^{+}=\sumop\displaylimits_{s=t}^{t+\lfloor h^{*}T\rfloor}K^{*}_{h^{*},st}\mathbf{Y}_{s}\mathbf{Y}_{s}^{\top},\mbox{\ \ and \ \ }\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{R,t}^{-}=\sumop\displaylimits_{s=t-\lfloor h^{*}T\rfloor}^{s=t-1}K^{*}_{h^{*},st}\mathbf{Y}_{s}\mathbf{Y}_{s}^{\top}.

Let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{i,t}^{+}/\sqrt{p}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{i,t}^{-}/\sqrt{p}
be the
i
-th eigenvectors of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{R,t}^{+}

and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{R,t}^{-}
, respectively.
Our objective is to detect if there is a switching in the eigenvalues/eigenvectors between
t-1
and
t
.
Assuming switching only occurs between two adjacent eigenvalues,
we take a sequential approach and define the
test statistics, for
i=1,\ldots r-1
:

T_{i,t}=\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{i,t}^{-}\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{i,t}^{-\top}-\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{i,t}^{+}\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{i,t}^{+\top}\right\lVert^{2}+\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{i+1,t}^{-}\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{i+1,t}^{-\top}-\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{i+1,t}^{+}\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{i+1,t}^{+\top}\right\lVert^{2}-\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{i:i+1,t}^{-}\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{i:i+1,t}^{-\top}-\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{i:i+1,t}^{+}\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{i:i+1,t}^{+\top}\right\lVert^{2},

(5.1)

where
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{j:j+1,t}\in\mathrm{R}^{p\times 2}
is a submatrix of
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{t}
composed of its
i
-th and
i+1
-th columns only.
The use of
\mathbf{R}_{j,t}^{\pm}{\mathbf{R}_{j,t}^{\pm}}^{\top}
instead of
\mathbf{R}_{j,t}^{\pm}
serves the purpose of circumventing the sign switching and also enhancing the differences.
The intuition of the last term in
T_{j,t}
is

\left\lVert\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-}{\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-}}^{\top}-\mathbf{R}_{t}^{+}{\mathbf{R}_{t}^{+}}^{\top}\right\lVert^{2}=\left\lVert\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{r}\left(\mathbf{R}_{j,t}^{-}{\mathbf{R}_{j,t}^{-}}^{\top}-\mathbf{R}_{j,t}^{+}{\mathbf{R}_{j,t}^{+}}^{\top}\right)\right\lVert^{2}\leq\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{r}\left\lVert\mathbf{R}_{j,t}^{-}{\mathbf{R}_{j,t}^{-}}^{\top}-\mathbf{R}_{j,t}^{+}{\mathbf{R}_{j,t}^{+}}^{\top}\right\lVert^{2},

with the equality reached when there is no switching.
The last term in (
5.1
) actually serves two purposes. First, the last term is not sensitive on whether
there is indeed a switch between
i
-th and
i+1
-th columns, but it tends to capture the time varying differences of the linear space spanned the two columns
between the left and right side of
t
, hence partially compensating the difference in the first two terms due to the same effect. It makes (
5.1
) more sensitive to switching but less sensitive to the time
varying nature of the functions around
t
. Second,
it makes the test statistic less sensitive to the situation when the switching is actually between
the
i-1
-th and
i
-th eigenvalues/eigenvectors.
In this case,
\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{i,t}^{-}\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{i,t}^{-\top}-\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{i,t}^{+}\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{i,t}^{+\top}\right\lVert^{2}
will be large, but it may be largely cancelled by the
last term in (
5.1
).
It is interesting to note that,
using the orthonormality property of the matrices,

T_{j,t}
can be rewritten as
T_{i,t}=2\left[\left(\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{i,t}^{-\top}\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{i+1,t}^{+}\right)^{2}+\left(\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{i+1,t}^{-\top}\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{i,t}^{+}\right)^{2}\right]
, showing that
T_{i,t}
actually measures the distance (angle) after swapping the
i
-th and
(i+1)
-th columns.

When there is a switching between
\boldsymbol{R}_{i,t}

and
\boldsymbol{R}_{i+1,t}
, it has impact on
their sample versions in a much wider time range, due to the smoothing operation. In addition, when two eigenvalues are the same, their corresponding eigenvectors are not uniquely identified. Hence the estimators
\widehat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{i,t}
would be very inaccurate when
t
is close to the
actual switching point. Hence corrections and re-estimation are needed in a region around the switching point.
We determine the significant regions (coalescing regions) that are
impacted by the switching
using the continuous subsets of
T_{i,t}
that
exceeds a certain
thresholds
thr_{i}
for
i\in[r-1]
, similar to an outlier detection procedure. We define these coalescing regions as
P_{m}=(a_{m},b_{m}]\subset[T]
and its associated switching column
\kappa_{m}
such that for all
t\in P_{m}
,
T_{\kappa_{m},t}>thr_{k_{m}}
and
T_{\kappa_{m},a_{m}-1}\leq thr_{\kappa_{m}}
and
T_{\kappa_{m},b_{m}+1}\leq thr_{\kappa_{m}}
.
Here,
m=1,2,\cdots,M
where
M
is the total number of regions. We use the thresholds
thr_{i}=\max\{1.5IQR_{i},1.5IQR\}
, where
IQR_{j}
is the interquartile range of
T_{j,t}
and
IQR
is the global interquartile range employing the data set
\{T_{j,t}:j\in[r-1];\ t=[T]\}
. It is possible to develop a bootstrap
type of procedures similar to that of
Motta et al. (2023)
though it is much more involved due to our complex setting. Empirically we found the
ad hoc
procedure using IQR is quite reasonable.

Next, using the coalescing regions
P_{m}
and its associated switching order
\kappa_{m}
, we follow the approach of
Motta et al. (2023)
to perform a switching and smoothing operation. Specifically, starting from
i=1
, we identify all regions
P_{m}
’s such that
\kappa_{m}=i
, perform the switching of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{i,t}
outside these intervals, and apply smoothing splines to interpolate the functions inside the
P_{m}
regions. The interpolated function are further adjusted using Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization to obtain the final estimates
(
Motta et al., 2023
)
.

Note that the smoothed loading functions are still subject to
global
rotation ambiguity. To obtained more interpretable representation, we perform a global
varimax
(
Kaiser, 1958
)
rotation on the
(dT)\times r
matrix by stacking the smoothed
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{R}}_{t}
,
t\in[T]
matrices.

7
Empirical Application

Understanding international trade flow patterns and their evolution is crucial for policy-making, economic forecasting, and firm optimization. International trade data, forming a dynamic sequence of matrix variates, closely mirrors network structures, offering insights into interaction patterns, evolutionary significance, and natural actor groupings within the network. Theoretical and empirical research on international trade networks has advanced significantly
(
Chaney, 2016
)
, with the Global Vector Autoregressive (GVAR) approach being particularly notable for analyzing trade network interactions
(
Chudik & Pesaran, 2016
)
. For instance,
Bussiére et al. (2012)
and
Greenwood-Nimmo et al. (2012)
utilized GVAR models to study demand shocks’ effects on global imbalances and forecast trade imbalances across 33 countries, respectively. However, GVAR’s reliance on a pre-determined weighting matrix to address dimensionality issues is a limitation. This study introduces a time-varying matrix factor model for international trade data, estimating time-varying loading matrices and latent factors concurrently.

7.1
Data and sample

We use monthly multilateral import and export volumes of commodity goods among 24 countries and regions from January 1982 to December 2018. The
data come from the International Monetary Fund Direction of Trade Statistics (IMF-DOTS). The countries and regions included in the study are Australia(AU), Canada(CA), China Mainland(CN), Denmark(DK), Finland(FI), France(FR), Germany(DE), Hong Kong(HK), Indonesia(ID), Ireland(IE), Italy(IT), Japan(JP), Korea(KR), Malaysia(MY), Mexico(MX), Netherlands(NL), New Zealand(NZ), Singapore(SG), Spain(ES), Sweden(SE), Taiwan(TW), Thailand(TH), United Kingdom(GB), and the United States(US).

In our study, we utilize the time-varying matrix factor model (
2.1
) to examine international trade flow data, where
Y_{t}
is depicted as a
24\times 24
matrix, with rows and columns representing exporting and importing countries, respectively. We rely on import data valued in U.S. dollars for their presumed accuracy over export figures
(
Linnemann, 1967
)
. It’s noteworthy that Taiwan’s trade data, absent in the IMF-DOTS, is inferred from its trading partners’ export information. To mitigate the effects of significant one-off transactions and trade anomalies, we calculate trade flows using three-month averages instead of monthly snapshots.

2
, the

latent factors in model (

) can be interpreted as the transport volumes of a condensed trading network among

export hubs and
r
import hubs in our application.
We use the generalized ratio-based method in (
3.5
) as well as the screen plot to select
\left(4,4\right)
as the number of dimensions of the latent hubs. For better visualization and interpretation, all estimated
\boldsymbol{R}_{t}
and
\boldsymbol{C}_{t}
are normalized so the sum of absolute values of each column is 1.

Figure

shows the heatmap of the initial estimates of
\boldsymbol{R}_{t}

T_{i,t}
in (

by our model selection procedure. It appears that the significance of the 5th eigenvalue of

7.3
Interpretation of results

Here we adopt the interpretation of the model described in Section
2
. Figures
10
and
11
provide insights into the dynamics of the globalization.
The latent Export Hub 1 (top figure in Figure
10
) can be viewed as a China-dominated export hub, as China has the maximum loading overall. Before 2001, Japan had a higher loading on this hub than China, indicating a shift in exporting centrality within the Asian economy. China’s loading on this hub experiences significant growth, particularly following its accession to the WTO in 2001. Despite this, Japan, Canada, Hong Kong and Taiwan also remain significant contributors to this export hub.

The latent Export Hub 2 can be viewed as a USA-dominated export hub as evidenced by the substantially larger loading of the USA compared to other countries. While China also participates in this hub, the United States maintains dominance throughout the sample period.
The latent Export Hub 3 can be interpreted as a hub dominated by European countries such as Germany, France, Italy, Netherlands, and Spain. Last, the latent Export Hub 4 was mainly used by APEC countries, in which Japan dominated throughout the period, while Korea, Taiwan and Hong Kong emerge as active participants post-2000. United States also participated in this hub, albeit to a lesser extent.

On the import side, United States dominated Import Hub 1 (top figure in Figure
11
). Canada, Japan and Mexico used Import Hub 2. The European countries used Import Hub 3, and China dominated in Import Hub 4.

8
Conclusion

Modeling high-dimensional matrix-valued time series has attracted increasing attention recently. We contribute to this literature by proposing a new time-varying matrix factor model that may be more applicable in real applications, establishing the asymptotic properties of a local PCA estimator for the model, and proposing a generalized eigenvalue-ratio estimator for latent dimensions. Our proposed estimators are intuitively appealing and straightforward to compute. They fully preserve the matrix structure and explore the local variation of the loading matrices. Moreover, our results are obtained under very general conditions that allow for weak correlations across time, rows, and columns. A smoothing operation is used to obtain a smooth representation of the time-varying loading matrices in order to facilitate easy interpretation of the model. Simulations have shown that the nonparametric estimator for factor loadings and the generalized eigenvalue ratio-based estimator perform well in finite samples. The empirical application concerning the international trade flow reveals some interesting time-varying patterns.
