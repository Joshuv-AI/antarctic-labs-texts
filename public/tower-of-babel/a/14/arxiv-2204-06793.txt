Abstract
. Within the framework of Hilbert spaces, we solve nonlocal problems in bounded domains with prescribed conditions on the complement of the domain. Our main focus is on the inhomogeneous Neumann problem in a rather general setting. We also study the transition from complement value problems to local boundary value problems. Several results are new even for the fractional Laplace operator. The setting also covers relevant models in the framework of peridynamics.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Main Results

Over the last years, there have been several studies of nonlocal Neumann problems of the following type: Given a bounded open set
\Omega\subset\R^{d}
, one is interested in well-posedness for

(
N
)

\displaystyle Lu=f\text{ in }\Omega,\qquad\mathcal{N}u=g\text{ on }\R^{d}\setminus\Omega\,,

where
L
is an integral or integro-differential operator and
\cN
is a related integral operator, which plays the role of some kind of normal derivative on
\R^{d}\setminus\Omega
. The main goal of this article is to prove well-posedness results for (

N

) in a general setting. We assume:

\displaystyle Lu(x)

\displaystyle=\pv\int_{\R^{d}}\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)k(x,y)\mathrm{d}y\qquad

\displaystyle(x\in\R^{d})\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}u(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(u(y)-u(x))k(x,y)\mathrm{d}x\qquad

\displaystyle(y\in\Omega^{c})\,.

Here,
k:\R^{d}\times\R^{d}\setminus\operatorname{diag}\to[0,\infty)
is measurable and satisfies

(E)

\displaystyle\Lambda^{-1}\nu(y-x)\leq k(x,y)\leq\Lambda\nu(y-x)\quad(x,y\in\R^{d})\,,

where
\nu:\R^{d}\setminus\{0\}\to[0,\infty)
is the density of a symmetric Lévy measure, i.e.,
\nu
satisfies

(L)

\displaystyle\nu(h)=\nu(-h)\text{ for all }h\neq 0\,\,\,\text{ and }\,\,\,\int_{\R^{d}}\big(1\land|h|^{2}\big)\nu(h)\mathrm{d}h<\infty\,.

The main new contributions of the present article include the following:

(1)

Extending previous results, e.g. from
[
DROV17
]
,
[
MPL19
]
,
[
DTZ22
]
, we treat the inhomogeneous problem for natural choices of data
g
. The corresponding results are new for the fractional Laplace operator.

(2)

We provide a general framework that includes integrable and singular kernels at the same time.

(3)

We show that the trace spaces introduced in
[
DK19
]
and
[
BGPR20
]
coincide.

(4)

We introduce a new Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator based on the operator
\cN
.

(5)

Our well-posedness results are aligned with classical results for second order partial differential operators. We show convergence of nonlocal to local problems, where we treat singular and bounded kernels together.

Let us explain condition (
E
). We denote
a\land b=\min(a,b)
for
a,b\in\R
. In the case
k(x,y)=\nu(y-x)
with
\nu
as above, the operator
L
is translation invariant and generates a symmetric Lévy process. The density
\nu
defines the “order” of the operator
L
, which becomes apparent in the case of
\nu(h)=C_{d,\alpha}|h|^{-d-\alpha}
for
h\neq 0
where
\alpha\in(0,2)
is fixed and
C_{d,\alpha}
is an appropriate constant. The resulting operator is the so-called fractional Laplace operator
(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}
.
The choice of
C_{d,\alpha}
ensures the relation
\widehat{(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}u}(\xi)=|\xi|^{\alpha}\widehat{u}(\xi)
for all functions
u
in
C^{\infty}_{c}(\R^{d})
. Let us mention that asymptotically one has
C_{d,\alpha}\asymp\alpha(2-\alpha)
. This will play an important role for our analysis. Further details about the fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}
and the constant
C_{d,\alpha}
can be found in
[
AAS67
,
NPV12
,
FG20
]
. Finally, let us mention that, the assumptions (
E
) and (
L
) are not sufficient in order to guarantee the existence of the pointwise expression
Lu(x)
in the general case, even if
u
is smooth. The Hilbert space approach used in
Section 4
avoids this issue because we only deal with the corresponding quadratic forms.
It is worth to mention that the nonlocal operator
\mathcal{N}
was initially introduced by
[
DROV17
]
and is called the
nonlocal normal derivative operator
across the boundary of
\Omega
with respect to
\nu
. Another type of such an operator appeared earlier in the literature see for instance
[
DGLZ12
]
.

Let us quickly review the classical Neumann problem, for the reader’s convenience. Let
\Omega\subset\R^{d}
be a bounded open subset whose boundary
\partial\Omega
is sufficiently regular. Given
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
and
g:\partial\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
measurable, the classical inhomogeneous Neumann problem associated to the data
f
and
g
consists in finding a function
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying

(1.1)

\displaystyle-\Delta u=f\quad\text{in}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \Omega\quad\quad\text{ and }\quad\quad\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}=g\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{on}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \partial\Omega.

Here
\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}
denotes the outward normal derivative of
u
on
\partial\Omega
. From a weak formulation point of view,
u
is said to be a weak solution of (
1.1
) if
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla u(x)\cdot\nabla v(x)\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}f(x)v(x)\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\partial\Omega}g(y)v(y)\mathrm{d}\sigma(y),\quad\mbox{for all}\penalty\ \penalty\ v\in H^{1}(\Omega)\,.

The Neumann boundary problem has received considerably less attention in the literature compared to the Dirichlet boundary problem. Classical textbooks like
[
Mik78
]
treat the basic aspects. A rigorous treatment including regularity up to the boundary, Schauder estimates,
L^{p}
estimates and the variational formulation can be found in the lecture notes
[
Leo13
]
. A recent article covering classical results for elliptic equations in divergence form is
[
DV09
]
.

Following
[
FKV15
,
SV14
,
SV13
]
we introduce a bilinear form
\cE
by

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u,v)=\frac{1}{2}\!\!\iint\limits_{(\Omega^{c}\times\Omega^{c})^{c}}\!\!\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)\big(v(x)-v(y)\big)\,\nu(x\!-\!y)\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y

for all smooth functions with compact support. As in the local case, a main tool in the study of Neumann problems, is a Gauss-Green type formula for
u,v\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\R^{d})
, see
A.5
:

(1.3)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}Lu(x)v(x)\mathrm{d}x=\mathcal{E}(u,v)-\int_{\Omega^{c}}\mathcal{N}u(y)v(y)\mathrm{d}y.

Relation (
1.3
) motivates us to introduce an energy space
V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})
as the vector space of all measurable functions
u:\R^{d}\to\R
such that the restriction
u|_{\Omega}
belongs to
L^{2}(\Omega)
and
\cE(u,u)
is finite. See
Section 2.2
for more details. The energy space
V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})
can be seen as a nonlocal analog of
H^{1}(\Omega)
. Let us make an interesting observation. Let
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
u\in V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})
be a minimizer of the functional
v\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\cE(v,v)-\int_{\Omega}fv
in the space
V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})
. Then
\cE(u,v)=0
for all smooth functions with compact support in
\R^{d}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
. Since
u,v\in V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})
, the Fubini theorem implies

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{c}}\cN u(y)v(y)\mathrm{d}y=\cE(u,v)=0\,,

which implies
\cN u=0
in
\Omega^{c}
, see Corollary
4.8
. On the one hand, this observation is aligned with the classical theory where the normal derivative appears naturally when minimizing the energy. On the other hand, and this is interesting, here we do not need to assume any regularity of the kernel
k(x,y)
and the boundary
\partial\Omega
.

Let us summarize the main results of this work.

(1)

The first step is to define a base space
L^{2}(\R^{d},\widetilde{\nu})
, in which we can define the complement value problems. We define
\widetilde{\nu}
and two alternative options
\overline{\nu},\nu^{*}
in
2.15
. In
Section 2.2
we study embedding results of corresponding function spaces.

(2)

The next step is to introduce
T_{\nu}(\Omega^{c})
as the trace space of
V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})
in
Section 2.6
. In this section, we study equivalent norms of the trace space and a density result.

(3)

An important tool in the proof of well-posedness results is the compact embedding
V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
, which is a core result in
Section 3
, see
Theorem 3.10
.

(4)

Section 4
is dedicated to well-posedness results. We focus on the Neumann problem in
Section 4.1
. An existence result for problem (

N

) is given in
Theorem 4.11
. We also discuss a more general Robin-type complement value problem.

(5)

The setup of this work allows to define a fully nonlocal Dirichlet-to-Neumann map with the help of the nonlocal Neumann-type derivative
\cN
. For
\Omega\subset\R^{d}
, the Dirichlet data are given on
\Omega^{c}
and mapped to
\cN u
on
\Omega^{c}
, where
u
satisfies the nonlocal equation in
\Omega
. Thus, this map can be viewed as a nonlocal analog of the well-known Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator given in
[
CS07
]
. Basic properties are formulated in
Theorem 4.20
together with spectral properties in
Theorem 4.23
.

(6)

The analogy between the classical Neumann problem and problem (

N

) leads to a convergence result when considering a sequence of complement value problems for the fractional Laplace operator
(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}
where
\alpha\to 2
.
Theorem 5.4
establishes the convergence of the corresponding sequence of solutions
u_{\alpha}
as
\alpha\to 2
.

1.2.
Related literature

Nonlocal complements value problems have been studied in several works. In particular, the Dirichlet problem is studied in many articles. For translation invariant problems, see the survey
[
RO16
,
AFR20
]
for fine regularity results and
[
FKV15
,
BV16
]
for the Hilbert space approach in a similar setting as in this work.

An early contribution to nonlocal Neumann problems is
[
DROV17
]
, where also the Gauss-Green formula appears for a special case. There is a difference between our approach and the one in
[
DROV17
]
, which explains why we are able to study the inhomogeneous Neumann problem. Let us explain our approach for the simplest setup of the fractional Laplace operator
(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}
, i.e.,
\nu(h)=C_{d,\alpha}|h|^{-d-\alpha}
. Given
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
g\in L^{2}(\Omega^{c},(1+|x|)^{d+\alpha}\mathrm{d}x)
, motivated by the Gauss-Green formula (
1.3
) we say that
u\in V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})
is a weak solution or a variational solution of the inhomogeneous Neumann problem (

N

) if

(
V
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u,v)=\int_{\Omega}f(x)v(x)\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega^{c}}g(y)v(y)\mathrm{d}y\quad\text{ for all }v\in V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})\,.

By testing (

V

) with
v=1
gives the following necessary compatibility condition

(
C
)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}f(x)\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega^{c}}g(y)\mathrm{d}y=0\,.

Equality (

V

) should be contrasted with the variational formulation of (
1.1
) in the classical case: Given
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
g\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
, find
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla u(x)\cdot\nabla v(x)\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}f(x)v(x)\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\partial\Omega}g(y)v(y)\mathrm{d}\sigma(y)\quad\text{ for all }v\in H^{1}(\Omega)\,.

Note that
[
DROV17
, Def. 3.6]
and subsequent definitions like
[
MPL19
, Definition 2.7]
look very similar to (

V

) at first glance. However, the norm of the test space defined in
[
DROV17
, Eq. (3.1)]
,
[
MPL19
, Section 2]
depends on the Neumann data
g
, which is not natural. Our test space
V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})
in the weak formulation (

V

) does not depend on the Neumann data
g
. For the general case, we refer the reader to
4.3
. It is worth mentioning that the weighted space
L^{2}(\Omega^{c},(1+|x|)^{d+\alpha}\mathrm{d}x)
is the natural function space for the Neumann data
g
. In fact in Theorem
4.13
we are able to find some
g
not belonging to
L^{2}(\Omega^{c},(1+|x|)^{d+\alpha}\mathrm{d}x)
,
\Omega=B_{1}(0)
for which the variational Neumann problem (

V

) with
f=0
does not have any weak solution in
V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})
.

The aforementioned issue does not show up for
homogeneous
nonlocal Neumann problems. For such problems, several results have been proved, e.g., regularity up to a boundary of a domain for the fractional Laplace operator in
[
AFR20
]
. A particular observation linking the homogeneous Neumann problem to the regional fractional Laplace operator is provided in
[
Aba20
]
. Eigenvalues of nonlocal mixed problems are studied in
[
LMP+18
]
. Various nonlinear Neumann problems are studied in
[
Che18
,
MPL19
,
CC20
,
ATL20
,
MPL21
,
BS21
]
. Some higher order nonlocal Neumann problems are treated in
[
BMPS20
]
. The classical Neumann problem is closely linked to reflected diffusions. It turns out to be a challenging problem to establish a similar link between the nonlocal Neumann problem and a Markov jump process together with its reflection. An attempt is made in
[
Von21
]
, which we comment on in detail in Remark
2.38
.

1.3.
Peridynamics and volume constraints on bounded sets

In the literature, several nonlocal problems are studied in the area of peridynamics. Most of these models require complement conditions (a.k.a. volume constraints) not necessarily on the whole complement of the domain
\Omega
but only often on a part of the complement. Here, we would like to point out that our setting can be adapted to fit such requirements. Let us exemplify this with a simple model. Consider the symmetric kernel of the form
k(x,y)=\nu(x-y)
with
\nu
supported around the origin, say
\supp\,\nu\subset B_{\delta}(0)
for some
\delta>0
. A popular example of a kernel in peridynamic models is given by
\nu(h)=\mathds{1}_{B_{\delta}}(h)
. In this case it is natural to assume the complement condition not on the whole complement
\R^{d}\setminus\Omega
but only on
\Omega(\delta)=\{x\in\R^{d}\setminus\Omega:\dist(x,\partial\Omega)<\delta\}
. A nonlocal problem of the form
Lu=f
in
\Omega
is then supplemented with a complement condition prescribed on the volume constraint
\Omega(\delta)
, e.g.,
u=g
on
\Omega(\delta)
for a Dirichlet problem or
\mathcal{N}u=g
on
\Omega(\delta)
for a Neumann problem. Here,
f:\Omega\to\R
and
g:\Omega(\delta)\to\R
are given data. Our approach can easily be adopted to cover this case. In comparison to the weak formulation (

V

), one would need to replace

V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})
by the space
V_{\nu}(\Omega|E)
defined as in (
2.3
), with
E=\Omega\cup\Omega(\delta)

and recall that
\nu(h)=\mathds{1}_{B_{\delta}}(h)
. Indeed, assuming for simplicity that
\Omega
is bounded Lipschitz and connected, the well-posedness of the Neumann and the Dirichlet problem can be formulated as follows.

Corollary 1.1
.

Let
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
g\in L^{2}(\Omega(\delta))
. Then there is a unique variational solution
u\in V_{\nu}(\Omega|E)^{\perp}=V_{\nu}(\Omega|E)\cap\{\int_{\Omega}u\mathrm{d}x=0\}
to the Neumann problem
Lu=f
in
\Omega
and
\mathcal{N}u=g
on
\Omega(\delta)
, i.e.,

(1.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u,v)=\int_{\Omega}f(x)v(x)\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega(\delta)}g(y)v(y)\mathrm{d}y\quad\text{ for all }v\in V_{\nu}(\Omega|E)^{\perp}\,.

Moreover, there is a constant
C>0
independent of
f
and
g
such that

\displaystyle\|u\|_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|E)}\leq C\big(\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|g\|_{L^{2}(\Omega(\delta))}\big).

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
g\in V_{\nu}(\Omega|E)
. Then there is a unique variational solution
u\in V_{\nu}(\Omega|E)
to the Dirichlet problem

Lu=f
in
\Omega
and
u=g
on
\Omega(\delta)
, i.e.,

(1.5)

\displaystyle u-g\in V_{\nu,0}(\Omega|E)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{E}(u,v)=\int_{\Omega}f(x)v(x)\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{ for all }v\in V_{\nu,0}(\Omega|\R^{d})\,.

Here we denote
V_{\nu,0}(\Omega|E)=V_{\nu}(\Omega|E)\cap\{u|_{\Omega(\delta)}=0\}
. Moreover, there is a constant
C>0
independent of
f
and
g
such that

\displaystyle\|u\|_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|E)}\leq C\big(\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|g\|_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|E)}\big).

The proofs of
1.1
and
1.2
are analogous to the ones of
Theorem 4.11
and
Theorem 4.18
; we also refer to
[
Fog23a
]
for a more general setting. Both results are well known for special cases of
\nu
in the area of peridynamics, see
[
DTZ22
, Section 3.2]
and
[
KMS19
]
. We refer the reader to the exposition and the references in
[
Du19
]
. Let us mention some related results. An early work is
[
BT10
]
where several nonlocal complement value problems are studied for integrable kernels with fixed support (horizon). Problems for nonlocal nonlinear problems involving nonlocal operators of regional type are studied in
[
BM14
,
BMCP15
]
. Nonlocal Dirichlet problems driven by nonsymmetric singular kernels are considered in
[
FKV15
]
for scalar functions and in
[
KMS19
]
for vector-valued functions. The vanishing-horizon limit has been considered in several works, see
Section 5
. For references related to numerical results see
[
DY21
]
. We provide
\Gamma
-convergence results for vanishing horizons in
5.2
and mention related results from peridynamics. Our systematic approach in terms of functional analysis allows to treat general cases of
\nu
resp. general data
g
in comparison with
[
DTZ22
]
.

The paper is organized as follows. In

2.
Lévy measures and nonlocal function spaces

In this section we introduce generalized Sobolev-Slobodeckij-like function spaces with respect to a Lévy measure
\nu
and an open subset
\Omega\subset\R^{d}
, in particular
V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})
and nonlocal trace spaces
T_{\nu}(\Omega^{c})
. The function spaces are tailor-made for nonlocal elliptic complement value problems including the Neumann problem. We prove the existence of an embedding of
V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})
into weighted spaces
L^{2}(\R^{d},\nu^{\prime})
for different measures
\nu^{\prime}
and into the nonlocal trace space
T_{\nu}(\Omega^{c})
. We are able to compare
T_{\nu}(\Omega^{c})
with known trace spaces, see
2.32
. A main result of this section is
Theorem 2.26
, which proves that the bilinear form
(\cE,V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d}))
is a regular Dirichlet form on
L^{2}(\R^{d},\nu^{\prime})
. This result allows to construct jump processes with some sort of reflection.

Throughout this work, let
\nu
be a Lévy measure whose density is a measurable symmetric function
\nu:\R^{d}\setminus\{0\}\to[0,\infty)
satisfying (
L
). We will impose further conditions on
\nu
where needed. For simplicity, we assume in our main results that
\nu
has full support. See
Section 1.3
for a discussion on how this can be relaxed.

2.1.
Lévy condition and energy forms

Before we begin, let us make an observation that nicely links (
L
) with nonlocal energies.

Theorem 2.1
.

Assume
\nu:\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}\to[0,\infty)
is measurable and radial

. Then the energy

\displaystyle\iint_{\R^{d}\R^{d}}\big(u(y)\!-\!u(x)\big)^{2}\nu(x\!-\!y)\;\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x

Proof.

By (
2.7

L

u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\R^{d})
. For the converse, let
u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\R^{d})
be nontrivial and
\varepsilon>0
, then there is
\delta>0
such that

\displaystyle\|\nabla u(\cdot+h)-\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\R^{d})}<\varepsilon\quad\text{ if }|h|\leq\delta\,.

Using the fundamental theorem of calculus,
\frac{1}{2}b^{2}\leq a^{2}+(b-a)^{2}
and polar coordinates yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}\iint_{\R^{d}\R^{d}}&\big(u(y)\!-\!u(x)\big)^{2}\nu(x\!-\!y)\;\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x\geq\int_{\R^{d}}\int_{B_{\delta}(0)}\Big|\int_{0}^{1}\nabla u(x+th)\cdot h\mathrm{d}t\Big|^{2}\nu(h)\mathrm{d}h\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\R^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}|\nabla u(x)\cdot w|^{2}\mathrm{d}\sigma_{d-1}(w)\Big(\int_{0}^{\delta}r^{d+1}\nu(r)\mathrm{d}r\Big)\mathrm{d}x-\varepsilon\int_{B_{\delta}(0)}|h|^{2}\nu(h)\mathrm{d}h\\
&\geq\Big(\frac{1}{2}K_{d,2}\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\R^{d})}-\varepsilon\Big)\int_{B_{\delta}(0)}|h|^{2}\nu(h)\mathrm{d}h.\end{split}

Recall that, invariance of the Lebesgue measure under rotations implies for all
z\in\R^{d}

\displaystyle\fint_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}|w\cdot z|^{2}\mathrm{d}\sigma_{d-1}(w)=K_{d,2}|z|^{2}\quad\text{with}\quad K_{d,2}=\fint_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}|w\cdot e|^{2}\mathrm{d}\sigma_{d-1}(w)=\frac{1}{d}.

Therefore, choosing
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{4}K_{d,2}\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\R^{d})},
we obtain

\displaystyle\iint_{\R^{d}\R^{d}}\big(u(y)\!-\!u(x)\big)^{2}\nu(x\!-\!y)\;\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}K_{d,2}\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\R^{d})}\int_{B_{\delta}(0)}|h|^{2}\nu(h)\mathrm{d}h.

It remains to show that
\nu
is integrable away from the origin.
Consider another
u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\R^{d})
with
\supp u\subset B_{\tau}(0)
and
0<\tau<\delta/2
. For all
x\in B_{\tau}
we have
B_{\tau}(0)\subset B_{\delta}(x)
and hence

\displaystyle\iint_{\R^{d}\R^{d}}\big(u(y)\!-\!u(x)\big)^{2}\nu(x\!-\!y)\;\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq 2\int_{B_{\tau}(0)}|u(x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\int_{\R^{d}\setminus B_{\tau}(0)}\nu(x-y)\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\geq 2\int_{B_{\tau}(0)}|u(x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\int_{\R^{d}\setminus B_{\delta}(0)}\nu(h)\mathrm{d}h.

This together with the previous estimate implies that
\nu\in L^{1}(\R^{d},1\land|h|^{2}\mathrm{d}h)
, i.e.,
\nu
satisfies condition
(
L
)
.
∎

2.2.
Sobolev-Slobodeckij-like spaces

Let
\Omega\subset\R^{d}
be open. Define the space
H_{\nu}(\Omega)
by

\displaystyle H_{\nu}(\Omega)=\Big\{u\in L^{2}(\Omega)\,:\,\cE_{\Omega}(u,u)<\infty\Big\}\,,

equipped with the norm
\|u\|^{2}_{H_{\nu}(\Omega)}=\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\cE_{\Omega}(u,u)
, where

(2.1)

\displaystyle\cE_{\Omega}(u,v)=\iint\limits_{\Omega\Omega}\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)\big(v(x)-v(y)\big)\,\nu(x\!-\!y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\,.

When
\nu\in L^{1}(\R^{d})
, e.g., in the case
\nu(h)=\mathbbm{1}_{B_{1}}(h)
, the space
H_{\nu}(\Omega)
equals
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

Following
[
FKV15
]
we introduce the vector space
V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})
as follows:

\displaystyle V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})=\Big\{u:\R^{d}\to\R\text{ meas.}:\,u|_{\Omega}\in L^{2}(\Omega),|u|^{2}_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}<\infty\Big\},

where the seminorm is defined by

\displaystyle|u|^{2}_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}=\iint\limits_{\Omega\R^{d}}\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)^{2}\,\nu(x\!-\!y)\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y<\infty\,.

We endow the vector space
V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})
with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}
given by

\displaystyle\|u\|^{2}_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}=\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+|u|^{2}_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}\,.

Next, given functions
u,v\in V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})
, we define a bilinear form
\cE
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u,v)=\frac{1}{2}\!\!\iint\limits_{(\Omega^{c}\times\Omega^{c})^{c}}\!\!\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)\big(v(x)-v(y)\big)\,\nu(x\!-\!y)\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\,.

Lemma 2.2
.

We have
|u|^{2}_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}\leq\mathcal{E}(u,u)\leq 2|u|^{2}_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}
for any measurable function
u
.

Proof.

On the one hand, the inequality

\displaystyle\iint\limits_{\Omega\R^{d}}\!\!\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)^{2}\,\nu(x\!-\!y)\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x\ \leq\iint\limits_{(\Omega^{c}\times\Omega^{c})^{c}}\hskip-8.61108pt\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)^{2}\nu(x\!-\!y)\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x=2\cE(u,u)

holds trivially true. On the other hand,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u,u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\!\!\iint\limits_{\R^{d}\R^{d}}\!\!\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)^{2}\,\big[\mathds{1}_{\Omega}(x)\lor\mathds{1}_{\Omega}(y)\big]\nu(x\!-\!y)\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\iint\limits_{\R^{d}\R^{d}}\!\!\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)^{2}\,\big[\mathds{1}_{\Omega}(x)+\mathds{1}_{\Omega}(y)\big]\nu(x\!-\!y)\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\iint\limits_{\Omega\R^{d}}\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)^{2}\,\nu(x\!-\!y)\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x\,,

which completes the proof.
∎

Some authors find it convenient to work with the smaller space
V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})\cap L^{2}(\R^{d})
equipped with its corresponding norm. For the study of nonlocal Dirichlet problems this restriction is not necessary, though. On the other hand, the requirement
u|_{\Omega}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
for
u\in V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})
is natural as shown by the following observation.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
\nu
be a unimodal Lévy measure and
\Omega\subset\R^{d}
be a bounded open set. Assume
\Omega\subset B_{R/2}(0)
for some
R\geq 1
with
\nu(R)\neq 0
. Then
\cE(u,u)<\infty
implies
u|_{\Omega}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
.

The condition that
\nu
is unimodal is not restrictive at all and we recall its definition for the readers’ convenience.

Definition 2.4
.

A Lévy density
\nu
is called
unimodal
if it is radial with an almost decreasing profile, i.e., there is a constant
c\geq 1
such that
\nu(r)\leq c\,\nu(s)
for all
r,s>0
with
s\leq r
.

Remark 2.5
.

There are radial Lévy measures, which are not almost decreasing such as

\nu(x)=|x|^{-d-1}\big(\tfrac{2+\cos(|x|)}{3}\big)^{|x|^{4}}\,.

Proof of
2.3
.

First, since
\Omega\subset B_{R/2}(0)
, then for all
x,y\in\Omega
we have
\nu(x\!-\!y)\geq c^{\prime}
with
c^{\prime}=c\nu(R)>0
. By Jensen’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\iint\limits_{(\Omega^{c}\times\Omega^{c})^{c}}\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)^{2}\nu(x\!-\!y)\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\geq c^{\prime}\iint\limits_{\Omega\Omega}(|u(x)|-|u(y)|)^{2}\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\geq c^{\prime}|\Omega|\int_{\Omega}\Big(|u(x)|-\hbox{$\fint_{\Omega}|u|$}\Big)^{2}\mathrm{d}x.

This shows that the mean value
\fint_{\Omega}|u|
is finite. We conclude
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
because of

\int_{\Omega}|u(x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\leq 2\int_{\Omega}\Big(|u(x)|-\hbox{$\fint_{\Omega}$}|u|\Big)^{2}\mathrm{d}x+2|\Omega|\Big(\hbox{$\fint_{\Omega}|u|$}\Big)^{2}\,.

∎

\displaystyle\begin{split}V_{\nu,0}(\Omega|\R^{d})&=\{u\in V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})\penalty\ |\penalty\ u=0\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{a.e. on }\R^{d}\setminus\Omega\}\\
&=\{u\in H_{\nu}(\R^{d})\penalty\ |\penalty\ u=0\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{a.e. on }\R^{d}\setminus\Omega\}\,.\end{split}

2.3.
Weighted
L^{2}
-spaces

In order to set up the Dirichlet problem in
L^{2}
-spaces over
\R^{d}
, we define a Borel measure on
\R^{d}
that captures the behavior of
\nu
at infinity. There are several possibilities.

Definition 2.15
.

Let
\nu
satisfies (
L
) with full support and
B\subset\R^{d}
be non-empty and open. Define the weights
\overline{\nu},\widetilde{\nu}:\R^{d}\to[0,\infty]
by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nu}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{B}\left(1\wedge\nu(x-y)\right)\mathrm{d}y,

\displaystyle\overline{\nu}(x)

\displaystyle=\essinf\limits_{y\in B}\nu(x-y)\,.

If
\nu
is a unimodal Lévy measure, then we define the Borel measure
\nu^{*}:\R^{d}\to[0,\infty]
by

\displaystyle\nu^{*}(x)

\displaystyle=\nu(R(1+|x|))\,,

where
R>1
is an arbitrary fixed number.

Example 2.16
.

Let
0<\alpha<2
and
\nu(h)=|h|^{-d-\alpha}
for
h\neq 0
. Let
B\subset\R^{d}
be open and bounded, and
R>1
. Then

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nu}(x)\asymp\overline{\nu}(x)\asymp\nu^{*}(x)\asymp(1+|x|)^{-d-\alpha}\,,

where the constants behind the relation
\asymp
depend on the choice of
B
and
R
. See
Theorem 2.22
for the general case.

Let us discuss important properties of the three measures
\widetilde{\nu}
,
\overline{\nu}
, and
\nu^{*}
.
The following lemmas show that it is possible to define certain norms on
V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})
(with nice properties), which are equivalent to the norm
\|\cdot\|_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}
.

Lemma 2.17

(Properties of
\widetilde{\nu}
)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\R^{d}
be open and
\nu
satisfies (
L
) with full support. Assume
B\subset\Omega
.

(1)

We have
\widetilde{\nu}\in L^{\infty}(\R^{d})
. Moreover, if
|B|<\infty
, then
\widetilde{\nu}\in L^{1}(\R^{d})
.

(2)

The embedding
V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\R^{d},\widetilde{\nu})
is continuous. If
|B|<\infty
, then
L^{2}(\R^{d},\widetilde{\nu})\hookrightarrow L^{1}(\R^{d},\widetilde{\nu})
is continuous.

(3)

If
\nu
is unimodal and
B
is bounded, then on
V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})
, the norms
\|\cdot\|^{\#}_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}
and
\|\cdot\|^{*}_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}
are equivalent, where

\displaystyle\|u\|^{*2}_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}

\displaystyle=\int_{\R^{d}}|u(x)|^{2}\widetilde{\nu}(x)\mathrm{d}x+\iint\limits\limits_{(\Omega^{c}\times\Omega^{c})^{c}}(u(x)-u(y))^{2}\nu(x\!-\!y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\,,

\displaystyle\|u\|^{\#2}_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}|u(x)|^{2}\widetilde{\nu}(x)\mathrm{d}x+\iint\limits\limits_{(\Omega^{c}\times\Omega^{c})^{c}}(u(x)-u(y))^{2}\nu(x\!-\!y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\,.

Furthermore, if
\Omega
is bounded then the norms
\|\cdot\|_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}
and
\|\cdot\|^{*}_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}
are also equivalent.

Proof.

Firstly, we observe
1\land\nu\in L^{1}(\R^{d})
. It follows
\widetilde{\nu}(x)\leq\|1\land\nu\|_{L^{1}(\R^{d})}
for almost every
x\in\R^{d}
. If
|B|<\infty
, then Fubini’s theorem implies

\displaystyle\int_{\R^{d}}\widetilde{\nu}(x)\mathrm{d}x\leq|B|\|1\land\nu\|_{L^{1}(\R^{d})}<\infty\,.

The continuous embedding
L^{2}(\R^{d},\widetilde{\nu})\hookrightarrow L^{1}(\R^{d},\widetilde{\nu})
follows directly. The continuity of the embedding

V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\R^{d},\widetilde{\nu})
is obtained as follows

\displaystyle\int_{\R^{d}}|u(x)|^{2}\widetilde{\nu}(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{B}|u(y)|^{2}\left(\int_{\R^{d}}1\land\nu(x-y)\mathrm{d}x\right)\mathrm{d}y+2\iint\limits_{B\R^{d}}(u(x)-u(y))^{2}1\land\nu(x-y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\int_{\Omega}|u(y)|^{2}\mathrm{d}y+C_{1}\iint\limits_{\Omega\R^{d}}(u(x)-u(y))^{2}\nu(x-y)\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y=C_{1}\|u\|^{2}_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}.

Here
C_{1}=2\|1\land\nu\|_{L^{1}(\R^{d})}+2
. The following inequalities obviously hold

\displaystyle\sqrt{C_{1}+1}\,\|u\|_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}\geq\|u\|^{*}_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}\geq\|u\|^{\#}_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}.

Next if
\nu
is unimodal and
B
is bounded, then there is a constant
c^{\prime}>0
such that
\widetilde{\nu}(x)\geq c^{\prime}
for all
x\in B
.
The following estimates hold

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|u(x)|^{2}

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nu}(x)\mathrm{d}x+\iint\limits_{\Omega\Omega^{c}}(u(x)-u(y))^{2}\nu(x-y)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq c^{\prime}\int_{B}|u(x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x+\iint\limits_{\Omega\Omega^{c}}(u(x)-u(y))^{2}\nu(x-y)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq c^{\prime}\|1\land\nu\|_{L^{1}(\R^{d})}^{-1}\iint\limits_{B\Omega^{c}}|u(x)|^{2}1\land\nu(x-y)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x+\iint\limits_{B\Omega^{c}}(u(x)-u(y))^{2}\nu(x-y)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq(1\land c^{\prime}\|1\land\nu\|_{L^{1}(\R^{d})}^{-1})\iint\limits_{\Omega^{c}B}\Big[|u(x)|^{2}+(u(x)-u(y))^{2}\Big]1\land\nu(x-y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}(1\land c^{\prime}\|1\land\nu\|_{L^{1}(\R^{d})}^{-1})\int_{\Omega^{c}}u^{2}(y)\widetilde{\nu}(y)\mathrm{d}y\,.

The first and the last line imply that
\|u\|^{*}_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}\leq C\|u\|^{\#}_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}
for some constant
C>0
. Thus the norms
\|\cdot\|^{*}_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}
and
\|\cdot\|^{\#}_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}
are equivalent. If in addition
\Omega
is bounded, then
\|1\land\nu\|_{L^{1}(\R^{d})}\geq\widetilde{\nu}(x)\geq c^{\prime}
for all
x\in\Omega
for some
c^{\prime}>0
. The equivalence of the norms
\|\cdot\|_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}
and
\|\cdot\|^{\#}_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}
is thus proved.
∎

Lemma 2.18

(Properties of
\overline{\nu}
)
.

Assume
\Omega\subset\R^{d}
is open. Let
\nu
satisfies (
L
) with full support. Assume
B\subset\Omega
is open and nonempty.

(1)

\overline{\nu}\in L^{1}(\R^{d})
and, if
\nu
is unimodal, then
\overline{\nu}\in L^{\infty}(\R^{d})
.

(2)

The embeddings
V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\R^{d},\overline{\nu})\hookrightarrow L^{1}(\R^{d},\overline{\nu})
are continuous.

(3)

If
\nu
is unimodal and
B
is bounded then the norms
\|\cdot\|^{\#}_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}
and
\|\cdot\|^{*}_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}
are equivalent, where
\|u\|^{*}_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}
and
\|u\|^{\#}_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}
are defined as in
2.17
(iii) with
\widetilde{\nu}
replaced by
\overline{\nu}
.

Furthermore, if
\Omega
is bounded then the norms
\|\cdot\|_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}
and
\|\cdot\|^{*}_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}
are equivalent.

Proof.

To prove
(i)
, select
x_{0}\in B
and
r>0
such that
B_{8r}(x_{0})\subset B
. Note that, for
x\in B_{2r}(x_{0})
and
y\in B^{c}_{4r}(x_{0})\cap B
or for
x\in B^{c}_{2r}(x_{0})
and
y\in B_{r}(x_{0})
we have
|h|\geq r
where
h=x-y
. Therefore, for almost all
\xi\in B^{c}_{4r}(x_{0})
and
z\in B_{r}(x_{0})
we find that

\displaystyle\int_{B_{2r}(x_{0})}\underset{y\in B^{c}_{4r}(x_{0})\cap B}{\essinf}\nu(x-y)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\int_{|x-y|\geq r}\nu(x-\xi)\mathrm{d}x=\int_{|h|\geq r}\nu(h)\mathrm{d}h,

\displaystyle\int_{B^{c}_{2r}(x_{0})}\underset{y\in B_{r}(x_{0})}{\essinf}\hskip 5.69054pt\nu(x-y)\mathrm{d}x

(4.1)

\displaystyle Lu(x)=\pv\int_{\R^{d}}\big(u(y)-u(x)\big)k(x,y)\mathrm{d}y\,.

Definition 4.1

We define a nonlocal operator

4.1.
Neumann boundary condition

In light of the Gauss-Green formula (

Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u,v)=\int_{\Omega}f(x)v(x)\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega^{c}}g(y)v(y)\mathrm{d}y,\quad\mbox{for all}\penalty\ \penalty\ v\in C^{1}_{b}(\R^{d}).

with respect to the variable

hence the relation in (

in

that is (

Conversely, assume

solves (

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}Lu(x)v(x)\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}f(x)v(x)\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega^{c}}g(y)v(y)\mathrm{d}y-\int_{\Omega^{c}}\mathcal{N}u(y)v(y)\mathrm{d}y,\hskip 9.24994pt\mbox{for all}\penalty\ \penalty\ v\in C^{1}_{b}(\R^{d}).

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}Lu(x)v(x)\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}f(x)v(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{c}}g(y)v(y)\mathrm{d}y-\int_{\Omega^{c}}\mathcal{N}u(y)v(y)\mathrm{d}y

(

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\langle f,v\rangle+\langle g,v\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{E}(v,v)-\int_{\Omega}fv\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega^{c}}gv\mathrm{d}x

Let

⟂

(

⟂

⟂

g\mapsto\mathscr{D}_{\lambda}g=\mathcal{E}_{-\lambda}(u_{g},\widetilde{\cdot})
such that
\langle\mathscr{D}_{\lambda}g,v\rangle=\mathcal{E}_{-\lambda}(u_{g},\widetilde{v})
, the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map with respect to the operator
L-\lambda
.

Theorem 4.20
.

The Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator
\mathscr{D}_{\lambda}:T_{\nu}(\Omega^{c})\to T_{\nu}(\Omega^{c})^{\prime}
with
g\mapsto\mathscr{D}_{\lambda}g=\mathcal{E}_{-\lambda}(u_{g},\widetilde{\cdot})
is well defined, linearly bounded and self-adjoint. Moreover, if we take
c=\min(1,-\lambda)
then for all
g\in T_{\nu}(\Omega^{c})
we have

\displaystyle\langle\mathscr{D}_{\lambda}g,g\rangle\geq c\|u_{g}\|^{2}_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}.

In particular if
c>0
it follows that

\displaystyle\langle\mathscr{D}_{\lambda}g,g\rangle\geq c\|g\|^{2}_{T_{\nu}(\Omega^{c})}.

Proof.

Consider
v^{\prime}\in V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})
another extension of
v
then
v^{\prime}-\widetilde{v}\in V_{\nu,0}(\Omega|\R^{d})
and by definition of
u_{g}
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{-\lambda}(u_{g},v^{\prime}-\widetilde{v})=0\quad\text{that is}\quad\mathcal{E}_{-\lambda}(u_{g},\widetilde{v})=\mathcal{E}_{-\lambda}(u_{g},v^{\prime}).

Therefore the mapping
v\mapsto\mathcal{E}_{-\lambda}(u_{g},\widetilde{v})
is well defined, linear and bounded on
T_{\nu}(\Omega^{c})
. Indeed,

\displaystyle|\mathcal{E}_{-\lambda}(u_{g},\widetilde{v})|\leq(|\lambda|+1)\|u_{g}\|_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}\|\widetilde{v}\|_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}.

Since the extension
\widetilde{v}
of
v
is arbitrarily chosen, upon the estimate (
4.21
) we obtain

\displaystyle|\mathcal{E}_{-\lambda}(u_{g},\widetilde{v})|\leq C\|g\|_{T_{\nu}(\Omega^{c})}\|v\|_{T_{\nu}(\Omega^{c})}.

This shows that,
\mathcal{E}_{-\lambda}(u_{g},\widetilde{\cdot})
belongs
T_{\nu}(\Omega^{c})^{\prime}
. Subsequently it also
follows from this estimate that the mapping
\mathscr{D}_{\lambda}:T_{\nu}(\Omega^{c})\to T_{\nu}(\Omega^{c})^{\prime}
with
g\mapsto\mathscr{D}_{\lambda}g=\mathcal{E}_{-\lambda}(u_{g},\widetilde{\cdot})
is linear and bounded. Now let
g,h\in T_{\nu}(\Omega^{c})
specializing the definition of
\mathscr{D}_{\lambda}
with
\widetilde{g}=u_{g}
and
\widetilde{h}=u_{h}
the self-adjointness is obtained as follows

\displaystyle\langle\mathscr{D}_{\lambda}g,h\rangle=\mathcal{E}_{-\lambda}(u_{g},u_{h})=\mathcal{E}_{-\lambda}(u_{h},u_{g})=\langle\mathscr{D}_{\lambda}h,g\rangle.

The choice
c=\min(1,-\lambda)
leads to
\langle\mathscr{D}_{\lambda}g,g\rangle=\mathcal{E}_{-\lambda}(u_{g},u_{g})\geq c\|u_{g}\|^{2}_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}
.
∎

Remark 4.21
.

The above definition is motivated by the following observation. Assume
u_{g}
is as before and
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\R^{d})
. The Gauss-Green formula (
A.5
) gives

(4.22)

\displaystyle\langle\mathscr{D}_{\lambda}g,\varphi\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}_{-\lambda}(u_{g},\varphi)=\int_{\Omega^{c}}\mathcal{N}u_{g}(y)\varphi(y)\mathrm{d}y.

From the second equality we can identify
\mathscr{D}_{\lambda}g=\mathcal{N}u_{g}\in L^{2}(\Omega^{c},\widetilde{\nu}^{-1})\subset T_{\nu}(\Omega^{c})^{\prime}
. Hence
\mathscr{D}_{\lambda}:g\mapsto\mathcal{N}u_{g}
, which agrees with conceptual idea behind the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map in the classical case.

Theorem 4.22
.

Let the assumptions of

be in force. Denote by
L_{\beta}
the operator

. Then the point spectrum
\sigma_{p}(L_{\beta})=(\gamma_{n}(\beta))_{n}
of

is infinitely countable say
0<\gamma_{1}(\beta)\leq\gamma_{2}(\beta)\leq\cdots\leq\gamma_{n}(\beta)\leq\cdots
, with
\gamma_{n}(\beta)\to\infty
as

, and the corresponding eigenfunctions belong to

and form an orthonormal basis of

.

Proof.

It suffices to proceed as in the proof of

.
∎

Next, we see the relation between the spectrum of the operator
L
subject to Robin boundary condition and that of Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator.

Theorem 4.23
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{\lambda_{n}:n\geq 1\}
and
\beta:\Omega^{c}\to\R
be measurable. Consider the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map
\mathscr{D}_{\lambda}:T_{\nu}(\Omega^{c})\to T_{\nu}(\Omega^{c})^{\prime},\,\,\mathscr{D}_{\lambda}g=\mathcal{N}u_{g}
.
Then,
0\in\sigma_{p}(\mathscr{D}_{\lambda}+\beta)
if and only if
\lambda\in\sigma_{p}(L_{\beta})
. In addition,
\dim\ker(L_{\beta}-\lambda)=\dim\ker(\mathscr{D}_{\lambda}+\beta)
.

Proof.

Let
u\in\ker(L_{\beta}-\lambda)
then for all
v\in V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})
,

\displaystyle Q_{\beta}(u,v)=\lambda\int_{\Omega}u(x)v(x)\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{equivalently}\quad\mathcal{E}_{-\lambda}(u,v)=-\int_{\Omega^{c}}u(y)v(y)\beta(y)\mathrm{d}y.

Set
g=\operatorname{Tr}(u)=u|_{\Omega^{c}}
, with the aid of (
4.22
) the above relation reduces to

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{c}}\mathcal{N}u_{g}(y)v(y)\mathrm{d}y=-\int_{\Omega^{c}}g(y)v(y)\beta(y)\mathrm{d}y.

Thus
g\in\ker(\mathscr{D}_{\lambda}+\beta)
. We have shown that the mapping
T:\ker(L_{\beta}-\lambda)\to\ker(\mathscr{D}_{\lambda}+\beta)
with
u\mapsto\operatorname{Tr}(u)
is well defined and onto. Both assertions will follow once we show that
T
defines a bijection, in other words we only have to show that
T
is one-to-one. For
u\in\ker(L_{\beta}-\lambda)
if
\operatorname{Tr}(u)=0
then from the first relation above, we have
\mathcal{E}(u,v)=\lambda\int_{\Omega}u(x)v(x)\mathrm{d}x
for all
v\in V_{\nu,0}(\Omega|\R^{d})
. Necessarily,
u=0
otherwise
\lambda
is a Dirichlet eigenvalue which is not the case by assumption.
∎

5.
Transition from nonlocal to local

The main purpose of this section is to prove the convergence of a sequence of nonlocal Neumann problems to a local Neumann problem, i.e., the corresponding solutions converge. The main result of this section is
Theorem 5.4
. We consider the following set-up: Let
(\nu_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in(0,2)}
be a family of Lévy radial functions approximating the Dirac measure at the origin, i.e., for every
\alpha,\delta>0

(5.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\nu_{\alpha}\geq 0\,\,\text{ is radial},\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1\land|h|^{2})\nu_{\alpha}(h)\mathrm{d}h=d,\quad\lim_{\alpha\to 2}\int_{|h|>\delta}\nu_{\alpha}(h)\mathrm{d}h=0\,.\end{split}

Note that there is no restriction on the support of
\nu_{\alpha}
. The above definition of
(\nu_{\alpha})_{0<\alpha<2}
generalizes the spectrum of possible approximation sequences in
[
FKV20
,
DTZ22
]
. Note that, convergence of nonlocal variational structures including finite dimensional Galerkin methods have already been considered in
[
MD15
]
and
[
BMCP15
]
for homogeneous nonlocal problems of vanishing horizon-type.

We denote
L_{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}
to be the nonlocal operators associated with
\nu_{\alpha}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle L_{\alpha}u(x)

\displaystyle=2\pv\int_{\R^{d}}(u(x)-u(y))\nu_{\alpha}(x-y)\,\mathrm{d}y,

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}u(x)

\displaystyle=2\int_{\Omega}(u(x)-u(y))\nu_{\alpha}(x-y)\,\mathrm{d}y.

The associated energy forms are defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}_{\Omega}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\iint\limits_{\Omega\Omega}\big(u(y)-u(x)\big)\big(v(y)-v(x)\big)\nu_{\alpha}(x-y)\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\iint\limits_{(\Omega^{c}\times\Omega^{c})^{c}}\big(u(y)-u(x)\big)\big(v(y)-v(x)\nu_{\alpha}(x-y)\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y.

Let us mention two prototypical examples of interest here. For more concrete examples we refer the reader to
[
FKV20
,
FG20
]
.

Example 5.1
.

Define
\nu_{\alpha}(h)=a_{d,\alpha}|h|^{-d-\alpha}
with
a_{d,\alpha}=\tfrac{d\alpha(2-\alpha)}{2|\mathbb{S}^{d-1}|}.
Indeed, passing through polar coordinates yields

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1\land|h|^{2})|h|^{-d-\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}h=|\mathbb{S}^{d-1}|\Big(\int_{0}^{1}r^{1-\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}r+\int_{1}^{\infty}r^{-1-\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}r\Big)=\frac{2d|\mathbb{S}^{d-1}|}{d\alpha(2-\alpha)}=da^{-1}_{d,\alpha}.

For
\delta>0,
a similar computation gives

\displaystyle a_{d,\alpha}\int\limits_{|h|\geq\delta}(1\land|h|^{2})|h|^{-d-\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}h\leq\frac{d\alpha(2-\alpha)}{2d}\int_{\delta}^{\infty}r^{-1-\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}r=\frac{d}{2}(2-\alpha)\delta^{-\alpha}\xrightarrow{\alpha\to 2}0.

The choice of
\nu_{\alpha}(h)=a_{d,\alpha}|h|^{-d-\alpha}
gives rise to a multiple of fractional Laplace operator, i.e.,
L_{\alpha}=\frac{a_{d,\alpha}}{C_{d,\alpha}}(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}
, where we recall that
C_{d,\alpha}
is the normalizing constant of
(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}
. Note however that
\frac{a_{d,\alpha}}{C_{d,\alpha}}\to 1
as
\alpha\to 2
see
[
AAS67
,
NPV12
,
FG20
]
.

Example 5.2
.

Let
\nu\in L^{1}(\R^{d},1\land|h|^{2})
be any radial Lévy density that is normalized, i.e.,

\int_{\R^{d}}1\land|h|^{2}\,\nu(h)\mathrm{d}h=d.

Let a family
(\nu_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in(0,2)}
be defined by
\nu_{\alpha}=\nu^{2-\alpha}
where
\nu^{\varepsilon}
is a rescaled version of
\nu
in the following sense:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\nu^{\varepsilon}(h)=\begin{cases}\varepsilon^{-d-2}\nu\big(h/\varepsilon\big)&\text{if}\penalty\ \penalty\ |h|\leq\varepsilon\\
\varepsilon^{-d}|h|^{-2}\nu\big(h/\varepsilon\big)&\text{if}\penalty\ \penalty\ \varepsilon<|h|\leq 1\\
\varepsilon^{-d}\nu\big(h/\varepsilon\big)&\text{if}\penalty\ \penalty\ |h|>1.\end{cases}\end{split}

Then, as shown in
[
Fog23b
, Proposition 2.2]
,
(\nu_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in(0,2)}
satisfies (
5.1
). Note that, as a possible simple example, one could consider
\nu(h)=c\mathds{1}_{B_{1}(0)}(h)
, so that
(\nu_{\alpha})
would correspond to what is known as vanishing horizon in peridynamics, see
[
DY21
,
DTZ22
]
.

The next result implies the convergence of the nonlocal normal derivative to the local one. We point out that a similar convergence has been recently established in
[
HK23
]
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Assume
\Omega\subset\R^{d}
is an open bounded set with Lipschitz boundary. Let
\varphi\in C^{2}_{b}(\R^{d})
and
v\in V_{\nu_{\alpha}}(\Omega|\R^{d})
. The following assertions hold true.

(1)

There is a constant
C>0
independent of
\alpha
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{\alpha\in(0,2)}\Big|\int_{\Omega^{c}}\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}\varphi(y)v(y)\mathrm{d}y\Big|\leq C\|\varphi\|_{C^{2}_{b}(\R^{d})}\|v\|_{V_{\nu_{\alpha}}(\Omega|\R^{d})}\,.

(2)

Assume
v\in H^{1}(\R^{d})
then

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\to 2}\int_{\Omega^{c}}\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}\varphi(y)v(y)\mathrm{d}y=\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial n}(x)v(x)\mathrm{d}\sigma(x)\,.

Proof.

In view of the estimates (
A.1
) and (
A.4
) respectively, we have

\displaystyle|L_{\alpha}\varphi|\leq 4d\|\varphi\|_{C^{2}_{b}(\R^{d})}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(\varphi,\varphi)\leq 4d|\Omega|\|\varphi\|^{2}_{C^{1}_{b}(\R^{d})}\quad\text{for all }\penalty\ \penalty\ \alpha\in(0,2)\,.

By the continuity of the linear mapping
v\mapsto\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(\varphi,v)-\int_{\Omega}L_{\alpha}\varphi(x)v(x)\mathrm{d}x
, the Gauss-Green formula (
A.5
) is applicable for
\varphi\in C_{b}^{2}(\R^{d})
and
v\in V_{\nu_{\alpha}}(\Omega|\R^{d})
. Therefore, with the help of the above estimates we get
(i)
as follows

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{\Omega^{c}}\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}\varphi(y)v(y)\mathrm{d}y\Big|

\displaystyle=\Big|\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(\varphi,v)-\int_{\Omega}L_{\alpha}\varphi(x)v(x)\mathrm{d}x\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(\varphi,\varphi)^{1/2}\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(v,v)^{1/2}+\|L_{\alpha}\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\varphi\|_{C^{2}_{b}(\R^{d})}\|v\|_{V_{\nu_{\alpha}}(\Omega|\R^{d})}\,.

Noting that
L_{\alpha}\varphi(x)\xrightarrow{\alpha\to 2}-\Delta\varphi(x)
for all
x\in\R^{d}
(see
[
Fog23b
, Proposition 2.4]
) and that
|L_{\alpha}\varphi|\leq\frac{4}{d}\|\varphi\|_{C^{2}_{b}(\R^{d})}
, the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem yields

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}L_{\alpha}\varphi(x)v(x)\mathrm{d}x\xrightarrow{\alpha\to 2}\int_{\Omega}-\Delta\varphi(x)v(x)\mathrm{d}x\,.

On the other hand, according to
[
Fog23b
]
and
[
FKV20
, Theorem 3.4]
, we have that

(5.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\iint\limits_{\Omega\Omega}(v(x)-v(y))^{2}\nu_{\alpha}(x-y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\xrightarrow{\alpha\to 2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v(x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\\
&\iint\limits_{\Omega\Omega^{c}}(v(x)-v(y))^{2}\nu_{\alpha}(x-y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\xrightarrow{\alpha\to 2}0.\end{split}

So that,
\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(v,v)\xrightarrow{\alpha\to 2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v(x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x
. Thus we also have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(\varphi,v)\xrightarrow{\alpha\to 2}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\varphi(x)\cdot\nabla v(x)\mathrm{d}x.

Finally from the foregoing and the local Gauss-Green formula we obtain
(ii)
as follows

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\to 2}\int_{\Omega^{c}}\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}\varphi(y)v(y)\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\lim_{\alpha\to 2}\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(\varphi,v)-\lim_{\alpha\to 2}\int_{\Omega}L_{\alpha}\varphi(x)v(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\nabla\varphi(x)\cdot\nabla v(x)\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}\Delta\varphi(x)v(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial n}(x)v(x)\mathrm{d}\sigma(x)\,.

∎

Theorem 5.4

(
Convergence of weak solution
)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\R^{d}
be an open bounded and connected domain with Lipschitz boundary. Let
(f_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
be functions converging in the weak sense to another function
f
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
and let
g_{\alpha}=\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}\varphi
and
g=\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial n}
for some
\varphi\in C^{2}_{b}(\R^{d})
. Assume
u_{\alpha}\in V_{\nu_{\alpha}}(\Omega|\R^{d})^{\perp}
is a weak solution to
L_{\alpha}u=f_{\alpha}
on
\Omega
and
\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}u=g_{\alpha}
on
\Omega^{c}
that is,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(u_{\alpha},v)=\int_{\Omega}f_{\alpha}(x)v(x)+\int_{\Omega^{c}}g_{\alpha}(x)v(x)\quad\text{for all }\penalty\ \penalty\ v\in V_{\nu_{\alpha}}(\Omega|\R^{d})^{\perp}\,.

Let
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)^{\perp}
be the unique weak solution in to the Neumann problem
-\Delta u=f
in
\Omega
and
\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}=g
on
\partial\Omega
i.e.

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla u(x)\cdot\nabla v(x)\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}f(x)v(x)\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\partial\Omega}g(x)v(x)\mathrm{d}\sigma(x)\qquad\text{for all $u\in H^{1}(\Omega)^{\perp}$}.

Then
(u_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
strongly converges to
u
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, i.e.,
\|u_{\alpha}-u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\xrightarrow{\alpha\to 2}0
. Moreover, the following weak convergence of the energy forms holds true

(5.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(u_{\alpha},v)\xrightarrow{\alpha\to 2}\int_{\Omega}\nabla u(x)\cdot\nabla v(x)\mathrm{d}x\qquad\text{for all $v\in H^{1}(\R^{d})$}.

Remark 5.5
.

(1)

In case of the homogeneous problem, i.e., for
\varphi=0
and
f_{\alpha}=f
, the corresponding result is a direct consequence of the Mosco-convergence of
(\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(\cdot,\cdot),V_{\nu_{\alpha}}(\Omega|\R^{d}))_{\alpha}
to the gradient form
\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u(x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x
with domain
H^{1}(\Omega)
, see
[
FKV20
]
.

(2)

The convergence in result of Theorem
5.4
remains true if one replaces the Neumann condition with the Dirichlet condition, see
[
FG20
]
.

(3)

Examples of the type of
5.2
have been considered in relation to models in peridynamics, see
[
DTZ22
, Section 4.2]
and
[
BMCP15
]
for a natural nonlinear setting.

(4)

The assertion of the theorem remains true under the weaker assumption that
(g_{\alpha},\psi)_{L^{2}(\Omega^{c})}
convergences to
(g,\psi)_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}
for all
\psi\in H^{1}(\R^{d})
.

(5)

It is desirable to study
Theorem 5.4
under more general assumptions, e.g., under a weaker assumption than
\varphi\in C^{2}_{b}(\R^{d})
. A sufficient condition to be expected is
g\in H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
.

Proof.

A compactness argument as in
[
Pon04
, Corollary 2.1]
, see
[
FG20
, Chapter 5]
, shows that for certain
\alpha_{0}\in(0,2)
there exists a constant positive
C>0
depending only on
\alpha_{0},\Omega
and
d
such that for all
v\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{\perp}
and all
\alpha\in(\alpha_{0},2)

(5.4)

\displaystyle\|v\|^{2}_{V_{\nu_{\alpha}}(\Omega|\R^{d})}\leq C\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(v,v).

In view of the weak convergence, we can assume without loss generality that
\sup_{\alpha\in(0,2)}\|f_{\alpha}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}<\infty
. This together with the definition of
u_{\alpha}
along with
5.3

(i)
yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(u_{\alpha},u_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}f_{\alpha}(x)u_{\alpha}(x)\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega^{c}}g_{\alpha}(y)u_{\alpha}(y)\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq\|u_{\alpha}\|_{V_{\nu_{\alpha}}(\Omega|\R^{d})}(\|f_{\alpha}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\varphi\|_{C_{b}^{2}(\R^{d})})

\displaystyle\leq C\|u_{\alpha}\|_{V_{\nu_{\alpha}}(\Omega|\R^{d})}.

Combining this with (
5.4
), then for a generic constant
C>0
independent of
\alpha
we have the following uniform boundedness

(5.5)

\displaystyle\|u_{\alpha}\|_{H_{\nu_{\alpha}}(\Omega)}\leq\|u_{\alpha}\|_{V_{\nu_{\alpha}}(\Omega|\R^{d})}\leq C\qquad\text{for all }\alpha\in(\alpha_{0},2)\,.

Recall that, see
[
Fog23b
,
FG20
]
,
\|u\|_{H_{\nu_{\alpha}}(\Omega)}\xrightarrow{\alpha\to 2}\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
for all
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
. Whence from
[
KS03
, Lemma 2.2]
there exists
u^{\prime}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
and a subsequence
\alpha_{n}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}2
such that,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\big(u_{\alpha_{n}},v\big)_{H_{\nu_{\alpha_{n}}}(\Omega)}=\big(u^{\prime},v\big)_{H^{1}(\Omega)},

where, we recall that

\displaystyle\big(w,\,v\big)_{H_{\nu_{\alpha}}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}w(x)v(x)\mathrm{d}x+\iint\limits_{\Omega\Omega}(w(x)-w(y))(v(x)-w(y))\nu_{\alpha}(x-y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\big(w,v\big)_{H^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}w(x)v(x)\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\nabla w(x)\cdot\nabla v(x)\mathrm{d}x\,.

By virtue of the asymptotic compactness, see
[
BBM01
,
FG20
,
Pon04
]
; see also
[
AAS67
, Section 4]
, there exists a further subsequence that we still denote by
(\alpha_{n})_{n}
and a function
u^{\prime\prime}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
such that
\|u_{\alpha_{n}}-u^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0
. It is not difficult to show that
u^{\prime}=u^{\prime\prime}
almost everywhere in
\Omega
,
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)^{\perp}
where we let
u=u^{\prime}
, and that for all
v\in H^{1}(\Omega)

(5.6)

\displaystyle\iint\limits_{\Omega\Omega}(u_{\alpha_{n}}(x)-u_{\alpha_{n}}(y))(v(x)-v(y))\nu_{\alpha_{n}}(x-y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}\nabla u(x)\cdot\nabla v(x)\mathrm{d}x\,.

It remains to show that
u
is the weak solution of the corresponding local Neumann problem. To this end, we fix
v\in H^{1}(\Omega)^{\perp}
, given that
\Omega
has a Lipschitz boundary we let
\overline{v}\in H^{1}(\R^{d})
be an extension of
v
. The uniform boundedness in (
5.5
) and the convergence in (
5.2
) yield

\displaystyle\iint\limits_{\Omega\Omega^{c}}\big|(u_{\alpha_{n}}(x)-u_{\alpha_{n}}(y))(\overline{v}(x)-\overline{v}(y))\big|

\displaystyle\nu_{\alpha_{n}}(x-y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq C\iint\limits_{\Omega\Omega^{c}}(\overline{v}(x)-\overline{v}(y))^{2}\nu_{\alpha_{n}}(x-y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0\,.

This combined with (
5.6
) gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\alpha_{n}}(u_{\alpha_{n}},\overline{v})\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}\nabla u(x)\cdot\nabla v(x)\mathrm{d}x\,.

In particular, since
\overline{v}\in H^{1}(\R^{d})
can be arbitrarily chosen, we have the weak convergence

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\alpha_{n}}(u_{\alpha_{n}},v)\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}\nabla u(x)\cdot\nabla v(x)\mathrm{d}x\,\qquad\text{for all $v\in H^{1}(\R^{d})$}.

We know that
\overline{v}\in V_{\nu_{\alpha}}(\Omega|\R^{d})^{\perp}
for all
\alpha\in(0,2)
, thus by definition of
u_{\alpha_{n}}
it follows that,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\alpha_{n}}(u_{\alpha_{n}},\overline{v})=\int_{\Omega}f_{\alpha_{n}}(x)v(x)\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega^{c}}g_{\alpha_{n}}(y)\overline{v}(y)\mathrm{d}y\,.

By
5.3

(ii)
and the fact that
f_{\alpha_{n}}\rightharpoonup f
weakly in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, letting
n\to\infty
we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla u(x)\cdot\nabla v(x)\mathrm{d}x\,=\int_{\Omega}f(x)v(x)\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\partial\Omega}g(x)v(x)\mathrm{d}\sigma(x)\,.

By virtue of the uniqueness of the limit
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)^{\perp}
, the same reasoning can be applied to any other subsequence
(\alpha_{n})_{n}
with
\alpha_{n}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}2
and hence the claimed convergences hold true for the whole sequence as desired.
∎

L
driven by the density of a Lévy measure
\nu:\R^{d}\setminus\{0\}\to[0,\infty)
satisfying condition (
L
). Throughout this section we assume
k(x,y)=\nu(x-y)
for all
x\neq y
. The main goals include a definition of
Lu
as a distribution in Proposition
A.2
and the Gauss-Green formula for nonlocal operators in
A.5
.

A.1.
Basics on the operator
L

Given
k\in\mathbb{N}
, denote
C_{b}^{k}(\R^{d})
as the space of bounded functions of class
C^{k}
whose derivatives up to order
k
are bounded. Recall that for a sufficiently smooth function
v:\R^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, the operator
L
is defined by

\displaystyle Lv(x)

\displaystyle=\pv\int\limits_{\R^{d}}(v(x)-v(y))\nu(x\!-\!y)\mathrm{d}y=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}L_{\varepsilon}v(x)

where

\displaystyle L_{\varepsilon}v(x)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\R^{d}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\!\!(v(x)-v(y))\nu(x\!-\!y)\mathrm{d}y\qquad\hbox{$(x\in\R^{d};\varepsilon>0)$}.

Here are some basic properties of the operator
L
.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
u\in C^{2}_{b}(\R^{d})
. Then the following properties are satisfied.

(1)

The map
x\mapsto Lu(x)
is bounded and uniformly continuous. Moreover,

\displaystyle Lu(x)=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\R^{d}}(u(x+h)+u(x-h)-2u(x))\nu(h)\,\mathrm{d}h.

(2)

For each
\varepsilon>0
, the map
x\mapsto L_{\varepsilon}u(x)
is uniformly continuous.

(3)

The family
(L_{\varepsilon}u(x))_{\varepsilon}
is uniformly bounded and uniformly converges to
Lu,
i.e.

\displaystyle\|L_{\varepsilon}u-Lu\|_{L^{\infty}(\R^{d})}\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0}0.

Proof.

Let
u\in C^{2}_{b}(\R^{d})
. A simple change of variables implies

\displaystyle L_{\varepsilon}u(x)=-\frac{1}{2}\int\limits_{\R^{d}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(0)}(u(x+h)+u(x-h)-2u(x))\nu(h)\,\mathrm{d}h.

An application of the fundamental theorem of calculus yields

\displaystyle(u(x+h)+u(x-h)-2u(x))

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\big[\nabla u(x+th)-\nabla u(x-th)\big]\cdot h\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}2t\big[D^{2}u(x-th+2sth)\cdot h\big]\cdot h\,\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t\,.

Since
u
and its Hessian
D^{2}u
are bounded functions, we deduce

(A.1)

\displaystyle\left|u(x+h)+u(x-h)-2u(x)\right|\leq 2\|u\|_{C^{2}_{b}(\R^{d})}(1\land|h|^{2}),\quad\quad x,h\in\R^{d}.

The integrability of the function
h\mapsto(1\land|h|^{2})\nu(h)
entails the boundedness of
x\mapsto Lu(x)

and the uniform boundedness of
x\mapsto L_{\varepsilon}u(x)
. It also allows us to get rid of the principal value formulation. Furthermore, we can prove the uniform convergence of
(L_{\varepsilon}u)_{\varepsilon}
to
Lu
by

\displaystyle\|L_{\varepsilon}u-Lu\|_{L^{\infty}(\R^{d})}\leq 2\|u\|_{C^{2}_{b}(\R^{d})}\int_{B_{\varepsilon}(0)}(1\land|h|^{2})\nu(h)\mathrm{d}h\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0}0.

In order to prove the uniform continuity, we fix
x,z\in\R^{d}
close enough, say
|x-z|\leq\delta
with
0<\delta<1
. Then for every h
\in\R^{d}
,
h\neq 0
,

\displaystyle 2|u(x)-u(z)|+|u(x+h)-u(z+h)|+|u(x-h)-u(z-h)|\leq 4\delta\|u\|_{C^{2}_{b}(\R^{d})}.

This combined with (
A.1
) yields the uniform continuity via the integrability of
h\mapsto(1\land|h|^{2})\nu(h)
as follows,

\displaystyle\|Lu(x)-Lu(z)\|_{L^{\infty}(\R^{d})}\leq 2\|u\|_{C^{2}_{b}(\R^{d})}\int_{\R^{d}}(\delta\land|h|^{2})\nu(h)\mathrm{d}h\xrightarrow{\delta\to 0}0.

The uniform continuity of
x\mapsto L_{\varepsilon}u(x)
follows analogously.
∎

In order for
Lu(x)
to be defined,
u
needs to possess two properties: some regularity in the neighborhood of the point
x
and some weighted integrability for
|x|\to\infty
. As shown above, being
C^{2}
in the neighborhood of
x
is more than sufficient as is boundedness for
|x|\to\infty
. Let us investigate some mild condition on
u
as
|x|\to\infty
that still allows a suitable definition of
Lu
. In order to do so, we additionally assume that
\nu
is unimodal.

Proposition A.2
.

Let
\nu
be a unimodal Lévy measure. Define a weight
\widehat{\nu}
on
\R^{d}
by
\widehat{\nu}(x)=\nu(\frac{1}{2}(1+|x|))
.

(1)

For
u\in C^{2}(\R^{d})\cap L^{1}(\R^{d},\widehat{\nu})
, the expression
Lu(x)
exists for every
x\in\R^{d}
.

(2)

Assume that
\nu
has full support. For
u\in L^{1}(\R^{d},\widehat{\nu})
the expression
Lu
is defined in the
distributional sense via the mapping
\varphi\mapsto\langle Lu,\varphi\rangle=(u,L\varphi)_{L^{2}(\R^{d})}
.

(3)

Assume that
\nu
satisfies the scaling condition (
2.5
). Let
\Omega\subset\R^{d}
be open and bounded and
u\in V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})
. Then
Lu
is defined in the
distributional sense.

Remark A.3
.

Note that
\widehat{\nu}\in L^{1}(\R^{d})\cap L^{\infty}(\R^{d})
and that
L^{1}(\R^{d},\widehat{\nu})
contains
L^{\infty}(\R^{d})
.

Example A.4
.

If
\nu(h)=|h|^{-d-\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in(0,2)
, then
\widehat{\nu}(h)\asymp(1+|h|)^{-d-\alpha}
.

Proof.

For the proof of (i) we decompose the integral in the definition of
Lu(x)
into the two domains
\{|y|\leq 2|x|+1\}
and
\{|y|>2|x|+1\}
. In the first domain we employ the Taylor formula as in the proof of
A.1
. For
y
in the second domain we observe

\displaystyle|x-y|\geq|y|-|x|\geq\frac{|y|}{2}+\frac{|y|}{2}-|x|\geq\frac{|y|+1}{2}\,.

Thus, for
y
from the second domain, by property
2.4
we conclude
\nu(x\!-\!y)\leq c\widehat{\nu}(y)
and thus

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\{|y|>2|x|+1\}}|u(x)|\nu(x\!-\!y)\mathrm{d}y+\int\limits_{\{|y|>2|x|+1\}}|u(y)|\nu(x\!-\!y)\mathrm{d}y\leq K_{\nu}|u(x)|+\|u\|_{L^{1}(\R^{d},\widehat{\nu})}.

For the proof of (ii), let
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\R^{d})
be supported in
B_{R}(0)
for some
R\geq 1
. We claim

(A.2)

\displaystyle|L\varphi(x)|\leq C\|\varphi\|_{C^{2}_{b}(\R^{d})}\widehat{\nu}(x)\qquad\text{for all}\penalty\ x\in\R^{d}.

with some constant
C=C(R,d,\nu)
depending only on
R,d
and
\nu
. Indeed, suppose
|x|\geq 4R
, so that
\varphi(x)=0
. Since
|x-y|\geq\frac{|x|}{2}+R\geq\frac{1}{2}(1+|x|)
for
y\in B_{R}(0)
, the property
2.4
implies
\nu(x\!-\!y)\leq c\widehat{\nu}(x)
. Accordingly,

\displaystyle|L\varphi(x)|\leq\int_{B_{R}(0)}|\varphi(y)|\nu(x\!-\!y)\mathrm{d}y\leq c|B_{R}(0)|\|\varphi\|_{C^{2}(\R^{d})}\widehat{\nu}(x).

Whereas, if
|x|\leq 4R
the proof of (
A.2
) is complete using (
A.1
) as follows. Since
\frac{1}{2}(1+|x|)\leq 4R
we have
\widehat{\nu}(x)\geq c_{1}
for an appropriate constant
c_{1}>0
depending on
R
and
\nu
. Thus we conclude

\displaystyle|L\varphi(x)|\leq 4\Theta\|\varphi\|_{C^{2}_{b}(\R^{d})}\leq c_{1}^{-1}4\Theta\|\varphi\|_{C^{2}_{b}(\R^{d})}\widehat{\nu}(x)

with
\Theta=\int_{\R^{d}}(1\land|h|^{2})\nu(h)\mathrm{d}h
. Note that in case of the fractional Laplace operator the estimate (
A.2
) is analogous to
[
FW12
, Lemma 2.1]
. Finally, (
A.2
) yields

\displaystyle|(u,L\varphi)_{L^{2}(\R^{d})}|\leq C\|\varphi\|_{C^{2}_{b}(\R^{d})}\int_{\R^{d}}|u(x)|\widehat{\nu}(x)\mathrm{d}x.

This shows that
Lu
is a distribution when
u\in L^{1}(\R^{d},\widehat{\nu})
. With regard to (iii) let
\Omega\subset\R^{d}
be open and bounded. We show that the embedding
V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})\hookrightarrow L^{1}(\R^{d},\widehat{\nu})
is continuous under the additional scaling assumption (
2.5
).
Indeed, for
u\in V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})
we assume
\Omega\subset B_{R}(0)
for some
R\geq 1
. Then
|x-y|\leq R(1+|x|)
for all
x\in\R^{d}
and all
y\in\Omega
so that by (
2.5
) and
2.4
we deduce
\widehat{\nu}(x)\leq C\nu(R(1+|x|))\leq cC\nu(x\!-\!y)
. Here
c,C>0
are constants independent of
x
and
y
. Proceeding as in
2.17
, one arrives at the estimate

\displaystyle\int_{\R^{d}}|u(x)|\widehat{\nu}(x)\mathrm{d}x\leq C\|u\|_{V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}.

Therefore, regarding the preceding arguments
Lu
is also a distribution whenever
u\in V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})
.
∎

A.2.
Gauss-Green type formula

Having at hand a nonlocal analog of the normal derivative as in
4.1
, it makes sense to study a formula that resembles the classical Gauss-Green formula. Such formulas have been established in several contexts. See
[
DGLZ13
]
for numerous identities of a nonlocal vector calculus in the case of bounded kernels and
[
DROV17
]
for the case of the fractional Laplace operator. Recall the classical Gauss-Green formula (see
[
Neč67
, Chap 3]
,
[
Tri92
, Appendix A.3]
or
[
BF13
, Theorem III.1.8]
) says for all
u\in H^{2}(\Omega)\penalty\ \hbox{and}\penalty\ v\in H^{1}(\Omega),

(A.3)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(-\Delta)u(x)v(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\nabla u(x)\cdot\nabla v(x)\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\partial\Omega}\gamma_{1}u(x)\gamma_{0}v(x)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(x).

A reasonable explanation to this terminology is given in the
5.3
.
For a function
u\in C_{b}^{1}(\R^{d})
we know

(A.4)

\displaystyle|u(x)-u(y)|\leq 2\|u\|_{C_{b}^{1}(\R^{d})}(1\land|x-y)|)\qquad\qquad(x,y\in\R^{d})\,,

which implies

\displaystyle\iint\limits_{(\Omega^{c}\times\Omega^{c})^{c}}\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)^{2}\,\nu(x\!-\!y)\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\leq 8\|u\|^{2}_{C_{b}^{1}(\R^{d})}\iint\limits_{\Omega\R^{d}}(1\land|x-y|^{2})\,\nu(x\!-\!y)\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y<\infty.

Proposition A.5

(
Gauss-Green type formula
)
.

Let
\Omega
be open and bounded. For
u\in C_{b}^{2}(\R^{d})
and
v\in C_{b}^{1}(\R^{d})

(A.5)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}[Lu(x)]v(x)\mathrm{d}x=\mathcal{E}(u,v)-\int_{\Omega^{c}}\mathcal{N}u(y)v(y)\mathrm{d}y.

In particular, by choosing
v=1
one deduces

(A.6)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}Lu(x)\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\Omega^{c}}\mathcal{N}u(y)\mathrm{d}y.

Proof.

Let
u\in C_{b}^{2}(\R^{d})
and
v\in C_{b}^{1}(\R^{d})
. With the aid of
A.1
we can write

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}[Lu(x)]v(x)\mathrm{d}x=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Omega}v(x)\mathrm{d}x\int\limits_{\R^{d}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x)}((u(x)-u(y))\nu(x\!-\!y)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int\limits_{\Omega}\int\limits_{\Omega\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x)}(u(x)-u(y))v(x)\nu(x\!-\!y)\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x+\int\limits_{\Omega}\int\limits_{\Omega^{c}}(u(x)-u(y))v(x)\nu(x\!-\!y)\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x.

On one side, by a symmetry argument we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\Omega}\int\limits_{\Omega\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x)}(u(x)-u(y))v(x)\nu(x\!-\!y)\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\hskip-8.61108pt\iint\limits_{\Omega\times\Omega\cap\{|x-y|>\varepsilon\}}\hskip-12.91663pt(u(x)-u(y))v(x)\nu(x\!-\!y)\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{2}\hskip-17.22217pt\iint\limits_{\Omega\times\Omega\cap\{|x-y|>\varepsilon\}}\hskip-17.22217pt(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))\nu(x\!-\!y)\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\iint\limits_{\Omega\Omega}(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))\nu(x\!-\!y)\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x

where one gets rid of the principal value using the estimate (
A.4
) applied to
u
and
v
.
On the other side, with the help of Fubini’s theorem we have

\displaystyle\iint\limits_{\Omega\Omega^{c}}(u(x)-u(y))v(x)\nu(x\!-\!y)\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\iint\limits_{\Omega\Omega^{c}}(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))\nu(x\!-\!y)\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x+\int\limits_{\Omega^{c}}v(y)\mathrm{d}y\int\limits_{\Omega}(u(x)-u(y))\nu(x\!-\!y)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\iint\limits_{\Omega\Omega^{c}}(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))\nu(x\!-\!y)\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\iint\limits_{\Omega^{c}\Omega}(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))\nu(x\!-\!y)\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\int\limits_{\Omega^{c}}\mathcal{N}u(y)v(y)\mathrm{d}y\,.

Altogether inserted in the initial relation provide the desired relation.
∎

As a direct consequence of
A.5
we have the following.

Corollary A.6

(Second Gauss-Green identity)
.

For all
u,v\in C_{b}^{2}(\R^{d})
we have

(A.7)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}v(x)Lu(x)-u(x)Lv(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega^{c}}u(y)\mathcal{N}v(y)-v(y)\mathcal{N}u(y)\,\mathrm{d}y.

We now look at certain aspect of the dual of the trace space
T_{\nu}(\Omega^{c})
in relation with the nonlocal normal derivative operator
\mathcal{N}
.

Theorem A.7
.

For any linear continuous form
\ell:T_{\nu}(\Omega^{c})\to\mathbb{R}
there exists a function
w\in V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})
such that for every
v\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\overline{\Omega}^{c})

\displaystyle\ell(v)=\int_{\Omega^{c}}\mathcal{N}w(y)v(y)\mathrm{d}y.

In particular, given a measurable function
g:\Omega^{c}\to\mathbb{R}
if the linear mapping
\ell_{g}:v\mapsto\int_{\Omega^{c}}g(y)v(y)\mathrm{d}y
is continuous on
T_{\nu}(\Omega^{c})
then, there exists
w\in V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})
such that
g=\mathcal{N}w
almost everywhere on
\Omega^{c}
.

Proof.

Let
\ell\in T_{\nu}(\Omega^{c})^{\prime}
then because of the continuity of the trace operator
\operatorname{Tr}
, the linear form
\ell\circ\operatorname{Tr}
is also continuous on
V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})
. By Riesz’s representation theorem there exists
w\in V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})
such that
\ell\circ\operatorname{Tr}(v)=\left(v,w\right)_{(V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})}
for each
v\in V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})
. In particular, for
v\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\overline{\Omega}^{c})
identified with its zero extension on
\Omega
so that
\operatorname{Tr}(v)=v
, we remain with

\displaystyle\ell(v)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}w(x)v(x)\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\iint\limits_{(\Omega^{c}\times\Omega^{c})^{c}}(w(x)-w(y))(v(x)-v(y))\nu(x\!-\!y)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega^{c}}v(y)\mathrm{d}y\int_{\Omega}(w(y)-w(x))\nu(x\!-\!y)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega^{c}}\mathcal{N}w(y)v(y)\mathrm{d}y.

Furthermore, if
g:\Omega^{c}\to\mathbb{R}
is such that
\ell_{g}
is continuous on
T_{\nu}(\Omega^{c})
then by the above computation, it follows that
g=\mathcal{N}w
almost everywhere on
\Omega^{c}
since

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{c}}g(y)v(y)\mathrm{d}y=\int_{\Omega^{c}}\mathcal{N}w(y)v(y)\mathrm{d}y\qquad\hbox{for all }\penalty\ \penalty\ v\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega}^{c}).

∎

Remark A.8
.

The second statement of
Theorem A.7
particularly suggests that the space of all measurable functions
g:\Omega^{c}\to\mathbb{R}
for which the linear form
v\mapsto\int_{\Omega^{c}}g(y)v(y)\mathrm{d}y
is continuous on
T_{\nu}(\Omega^{c})
is contained in
\mathcal{N}(V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d}))
(the range of
\mathcal{N}
).

Remark A.9
.

The nonlocal normal derivative
\mathcal{N}u
of a function measurable
u:\R^{d}\to\R
can be thought of as the restriction of the regional operator on
\Omega
associated with
k(x,y)=\nu(x-y)
on
\R^{d}\setminus\Omega
. It might be interesting to know some situations where the pointwise definition
\mathcal{N}u(x)
makes sense at least almost everywhere. It is straightforward to verify the following: (i) if
u\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
then
\mathcal{N}u(x)
exists for almost every
x\in\R^{d}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
, (ii) if
u\in V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})
then
\mathcal{N}u\in L^{2}_{\loc}(\R^{d}\setminus\overline{\Omega})
, (iii) more generally, if
u\in V_{\nu}(\Omega|\R^{d})
then
\mathcal{N}u\in L^{2}(\R^{d}\setminus\Omega,w^{-1}(x)\mathrm{d}x)
where
w(x)=\int_{\Omega}\nu(x-y)\mathrm{d}y
,
x\in\R^{d}\setminus\Omega
.
