Abstract
. The space of chains on a compact connected space encodes all the different ways of continuously growing out of a point until exhausting the space. A chain is generic if its orbit under the action of the underlying homeomorphism group is comeager. In this paper we show that a large family of topological spaces do not have a generic chain: in addition to all manifolds of dimension at least 3, for which the result was already known, our theorem covers all compact surfaces except for the sphere and the real projective plane – for which the question remains open – as well as all other homogeneous Peano continua, circle excluded. If the spaces are moreover strongly locally homogeneous, which is the case for any closed manifold and the Menger curve, we prove that chains cannot be classified up to homeomorphism by countable structures, and that the underlying homeomorphism groups have non-metrizable universal minimal flows, with all orbits meager, in contrast to the case of 1-dimensional manifolds. The proof of the main result is of combinatorial nature, and it relies on the creation of a dictionary between open sets of chains on one side, and walks on finite connected graphs on the other.

1.
Introduction

Given a metrizable compact connected space
X
, a
maximal chain of compact connected sets of

– just

which is linearly ordered by inclusion and maximal with respect to this property.
Equivalently, it is a homeomorphic image of the interval
[0,1]
in the hyperspace
C(X)
of compact connected subsets of
X
such that

is mapped to a singleton,
1
to the point
X\in C(X)
and for each

, the image of

is a subset of the image of
t

[
15
]
*Lemma 14.7.
The collection

of all such chains therefore represents all the different ways one can start, say at time

, and then continuously grow out of it, until, at time

A classification of such chains up to homeomorphisms of

is only feasible for very simple spaces, such as the interval or the circle (see

At the very least, we can ask for which spaces there is

only one chain, that is, whether there is
\mathcal{C}\in\Phi(X)
such that its orbit under the action
\Homeo(X)\curvearrowright\Phi(X)
is comeager in
\Phi(X)
, endowed with its natural compact hyperspace topology.
In such case, we say that
\mathcal{C}
is a

.

Focusing on compact manifolds, the chain on the circle
S^{1}
consisting of all intervals centered around some point

is generic in
\Phi(S^{1})
, and a similar statement holds for the closed interval (see

and

). On the other hand, Gutman, Tsankov and Zucker proved in
[

that no closed manifold of dimension at least

has generic chains, and nor does the Hilbert cube.
The case of surfaces was left open. A corollary of our main theorem gives the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

If
X
is a compact surface other than the sphere or the real projective plane, then

has no generic chain.

We obtain
Theorem

1.1
as consequence of a stronger statement which not only recovers the aforementioned result of
[
12
]
, but which moreover covers a vast class of Peano continua going well
beyond the context of manifolds.

A
Peano continuum
is a metrizable compact connected space which is locally connected.
Among notable examples which are not manifolds are the Sierpiński carpet and the Menger curve. These are universal one-dimensional continua obtained by iteratively carving out the square and the cube, respectively, similarly to how the Cantor space is obtained from the unit interval.

Before stating our main theorem, we introduce some basic terminology.
A subset
K\subseteq X
is
locally separating
if
U\setminus K
is disconnected for some connected open
U\subseteq X
.
A space is
planar
if it admits an embedding in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and it is
locally non-planar
if none of its nonempty open sets are planar.
Recall that a
simple closed curve
in
X
is the image of an embedding of the circle
S^{1}
into
X
.
Let us say that a Peano continuum
X

has a circular covering
if it admits a covering
O_{0},\dots,O_{\ell-1}
in
\ell\geq 4

connected
open subsets such that
\Cl(O_{i})\cap\Cl(O_{j})\neq\emptyset
if and only if
\lvert i-j\rvert\leq 1\text{ mod}\,\ell
, and
O_{i}\setminus\bigcup_{j\neq i}O_{j}
is connected for each

.

Our main result is the following.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
X
be a Peano continuum with no locally separating points.
If
X
either

(1)

has a locally non-planar open subset,

(2)

has a planar open set containing a simple closed curve which is not locally separating, or

(3)

has a circular covering,

then
X
has no generic chain.

Let us parse the above hypotheses for
X
a compact manifold (we refer to
[
27
]
for background and definitions).
A compact manifold has a locally separating point if and only if it is
1
-dimensional, and it has a locally non-planar open subset if and only if it is at least
3
-dimensional.
A boundary of a surface is a simple closed curve which is not locally separating and lies in a planar open set.
Finally, the sphere and the real projective plane are the only closed surfaces without a circular covering (see
Proposition

6.13
).

We prove
Theorem

1.2
by first establishing a correspondence between Peano continua and open sets of chains on one side, and finite connected graphs and walks on these graphs on the other.
We then find a combinatorial necessary condition for the existence of a generic chain on a Peano continuum (
Theorem

5.9
), and prove that it is does not hold under our assumptions.

The combinatorial condition is an
off-by-one
weak amalgamation principle, roughly stating that each walk can be refined by another walk in such a way that any two different walks refining it can be reconciled by a small perturbation.
We extract the combinatorial idea at the core of the main proof of
[
12
]
– that chains can be made irreconcilable by winding enough times in opposite directions – and discretize it, producing irreconcilable walks which wind around circular subgraphs.

The discretization relies on a handful of classical continuum-theoretical black-boxes, all of which are stated in
Section

4
.
In the following two sections, these black-boxes are used to derive fundamental combinatorial statements about graphs and walks on graphs.
The proof of
Theorem

1.2
is purely combinatorial.

This approach to the study of topological and dynamical properties of compact metrizable spaces by combinatorial means traces its roots to the field of
projective Fraïssé theory
, first introduced in
[
16
]
.
In rough terms, one constructs an infinite compact zero-dimensional graph, the
combinatorial model
of
X
, and finite approximations of such graph, and studies them to derive properties about
X
, leaning on the dictionary between the combinatorics of countable structures and the dynamics of Polish non-archimedean groups put forth in the seminal paper
[
18
]
.
The aforementioned Menger curve was studied under this lens in
[
32
]
, and
it follows from the results in
[
3
]
, again obtained in a projective Fraïssé theoretical framework, that the Lelek fan, a one-dimensional continuum which is not locally connected, has a generic
downward closed
chain.

Nonetheless, our strategy is innovative in that it is the first instance in which non-trivial
necessary
conditions for the existence of a comeager orbit for an action of a homeomorphism group of a compact connected space have been translated to a combinatorial statement.
Such statement is a weakening of the weak amalgamation principle which was isolated as a criterion for the existence of comeager orbits in certain actions of automorphism groups of countable structures by Ivanov
[
17
]
, and independently by Kechris and Rosendal
[
19
]
.
In this paper, we restrict ourselves to finite combinatorial objects, without explicit mention of the combinatorial models of the spaces involved, though our proofs could also be used to derive the analogous statements for such combinatorial models and their groups of automorphisms.

Theorem

1.2
has important dynamical consequences for the groups of homeomorphisms involved.
If
G
is a topological group, we call a continuous action
G\curvearrowright Y
on a compact space a
G
-flow

A

-flow is

if every orbit is dense.
Both classifying minimal flows of a group
G
and, vice versa, classifying groups based on the properties of their minimal flows are central goals in

.
Some groups of homeomorphisms have very simple minimal dynamics: the only minimal flow of the group
\Homeo_{+}([0,1])
of orientation preserving homeomorphisms of the interval is the trivial action and the only non-trivial minimal flow of
\Homeo_{+}(S^{1})
is the action on the circle itself (see
[
33
]
).

A number of dividing lines regarding the complexity of the minimal dynamics of a topological group have been studied, and are particularly well understood when
G
is Polish, i.e., separable and completely metrizable.
A Polish group is
extremely amenable
if all of its minimal flows are trivial, it is
CAP
if they are metrizable (see
[
5
]
), and it has the
generic point property
if every minimal flow has a comeager orbit (see
[
2
]
).
Every extremely amenable group is CAP, every Polish CAP group has the generic point property (see
[
6
]
), and no locally compact non-compact group has the generic point property.
Such properties can also be stated in terms of the
universal minimal flow
of
G
: CAP groups are also known as groups
with metrizable universal minimal flow.

Until recently (see
[
4
]
), there was essentially only one known example of Polish group with the generic point property which is not CAP (see
[
25
]
).
In
[
12
]
it is proved that the subgroup
\Homeo_{0}(X)
of homeomorphisms isotopic to the identity is not CAP, for any closed surface
X
, but it was left open whether such groups have the generic point property.
Since for a closed surface
X
the flow
\Homeo_{0}(X)\curvearrowright\Phi(X)
is minimal (
[
13
]
),
Theorem

1.1
allows us to cover all but two of these cases.

Theorem 1.3
.

If
X
is a closed surface which is not the sphere or the real projective plane, then
\Homeo_{0}(X)
does not have the generic point property.

The following fascinating questions remain open.

Question 1.4

(
[
12
]
*Question 1.3)
.

Is there a generic chain on the sphere
S^{2}
? Does
\Homeo(S^{2})
have the generic point property? What about the real projective plane?

Let us end this introduction with a series of corollaries of
Theorem

1.2
.
A space
X
is
homogeneous
if the action
\Homeo(X)\curvearrowright X
is transitive.
Homogeneous Peano continua are either the circle, or a closed surface, or are locally non-planar (see
[
31
]
*Remark 8).

Corollary 1.5
.

No homogeneous Peano continuum except for the circle and, possibly, the sphere and real projective plane, has a generic chain.

An example of a homogeneous Peano continuum is the Menger curve, which is the unique one-dimensional locally non-planar Peano continuum without locally separating points (see
[
1
]
).
Another is the Hilbert cube, for which the non-existence of generic chain was already proved in
[
12
]
.

On the other hand, the Sierpiński carpet, which is the unique one-dimensional planar Peano continuum without locally separating points (see
[
37
]
), is not homogeneous.
Nonetheless, it has plenty of non-locally-separating simple closed curves, since it can be constructed as the complement, in the sphere, of countably many pairwise disjoint open disks whose union is dense in the sphere itself.
In particular, any open set is planar and contains a non-locally-separating simple closed curve, so the Sierpiński carpet falls within the scope of
Theorem

1.2
.

A one-dimensional Peano continuum with no locally separating points is either the Menger curve or has a nonempty open connected subset homeomorphic to an open subset of the Sierpiński carpet (see
[
24
]
*Lemma 1.1). Therefore

Corollary 1.6
.

One-dimensional Peano continua without locally separating points do not have a generic chain.

We prove in
Theorem

7.7
that a sufficient condition for minimality of the action
\Homeo(X)\curvearrowright\Phi(X)
, when
X
is a Peano continuum without locally separating points, is for
X
to be
strongly locally homogeneous
, see
Section

7
for the definition. For example, all closed manifolds of dimension at least
2
are strongly locally homogeneous.
Since these spaces are in particular homogeneous, they have no generic chain, by
Corollary

1.5
, except possibly if they are the sphere or the real projective plane. Therefore:

Corollary 1.7
.

If
X
is a strongly locally homogeneous Peano continuum which is not the circle, the sphere, or the real projective plane, then
\Homeo(X)
does not have the generic point property.
In particular, its universal minimal flow is not metrizable and has no comeager orbit.

Among other examples of spaces which fit the hypotheses of
Corollary

1.7
are the Menger curve, the universal
k
-dimensional compactum
\mu^{k}
, and any compact connected
\mu^{k}
-manifold, for
k\geq 1
(see
[
7
]
*Theorem 3.2.2).

For the spaces in
Corollary

1.7
, we are also able to prove that the action
\Homeo(X)\curvearrowright\Phi(X)
is
generically turbulent
, which implies a strong non-classifiability result for the orbit equivalence relation of the action.
We say that chains on
X
are
classifiable by countable structures
if there is a Borel function
\mathcal{M}\colon\Phi(X)\to\mathrm{Mod}(\mathcal{L})
to the space of structures with domain
\mathbb{N}
in some countable relational language
\mathcal{L}
such that

\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\in\Homeo(X)\cdot\mathcal{C}\Longleftrightarrow\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C})\cong\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime}),

that is, if we can reduce the orbit equivalence relation among chains to the isomorphism relation between the associated structures.
By Hjorth’s theorem (see
[
14
]
) we obtain:

Corollary 1.8
.

If
X
is a strongly locally homogeneous Peano continuum which is not the circle, the sphere, or the real projective plane, then chains on
X
are not classifiable by countable structures.

This is in contrast with the case of
1
-dimensional manifolds, whose chains are classifiable by countable structures and have a generic element, as we prove in
Appendix

A
.
The techniques in this appendix are distinct from those in the other sections.

We remark that the first part of
1.4
could be answered in the positive by proving that chains on the sphere are classifiable by countable structures, but we believe that this is not the case.

In
Appendix

B
, we prove that
\Homeo(S)\curvearrowright\Phi(S)
is minimal, for the Sierpiński carpet

, and thus that
\Homeo(S)
does not have the generic point property, and its universal minimal flow is not metrizable and has no comeager orbit.
The proof is rather lengthy and technical; it could foreseeably be simplified by finer results on the degree of transitivity of the diagonal actions
\Homeo(S)\curvearrowright S^{n}
, which we were not able to find in the literature.
We leave the question of classifiability by countable structures of chains on the Sierpiński carpet open.

2.
Peano Continua and Chains

In this preliminary section we briefly introduce Peano continua and their spaces of maximal connected chains. We refer to
[
30
,
15
]
for a more thorough presentation of these topics.

A
continuum
is a compact connected metrizable space. A
Peano continuum
is a locally connected continuum. An
arc
is a homeomorphic image of the interval
[0,1]
; a
continuum theoretical graph
is a space composed of finitely many arcs that intersect each other at most in their endpoints; a
continuum theoretical tree
is a connected continuum theoretical graph that contains no simple closed curve.

We refer the reader to
[
30
]
*Chapter VIII for the proofs of the following basic properties of Peano continua which will be implicitly used throughout the rest of the paper.

(1)

Every Peano continuum is arcwise connected.

(2)

Every open connected subset of a Peano continuum is arcwise connected.

(3)

Whenever
U
is an open connected subset of a Peano continuum and
F\subseteq U
is a finite set of points, there exists a continuum theoretical tree
T\subseteq U
with
F\subseteq T
.

Given a topological space
X
and
Y\subseteq X
we denote by
\Cl(Y)
,
\Int(Y)
and
\Bd(Y)
respectively the closure, interior and boundary of
Y
.

Given a family
\mathcal{W}
of open subsets of a space
X
and
C\subseteq X
, we define

\Star(C,\mathcal{W})\coloneqq\{W\in\mathcal{W}:\Cl(W)\cap C\not=\emptyset\}.

It is generally more common to define
\Star(C,\mathcal{W})
as the set of those
W\in\mathcal{W}
that meet
C
, without taking their closures first. Our definition is slightly different, but it will simplify the notation throughout the paper.

If
V
is an open set, let

\mathcal{W}(V)\coloneqq\{W\in\mathcal{W}:W\subseteq V\},

and

\Core(V,\mathcal{W})\coloneqq\{W\in\mathcal{W}:\Cl(W)\subseteq V\}.

Note that
\Core(V,\mathcal{W})=\mathcal{W}(V)\setminus\Star(\Bd(V),\mathcal{W})
.

An open set
U
is
regular
if
U=\Int(\Cl(U))
.
If
U,V\subseteq X
are regular open sets, let
U\vee V
be the regular open set
\Int(\Cl(U)\cup\Cl(V))
and, similarly, let
\bigvee\mathcal{U}\coloneqq\Int(\Cl(\bigcup_{U\in\mathcal{U}}U))
, for any family
\mathcal{U}
of regular open sets.

Let
\Homeo(X)
denote the set of all homeomorphisms from
X
onto itself. The
compact-open topology
on
\Homeo(X)
is the topology generated by sets of the following form, with
K
being a compact subset of
X
and
U
an open one:

\{f\in\Homeo(X):f[K]\subseteq U\}.

If
X
is a compact metrizable space, the compact-open topology on
\Homeo(X)
agrees with the topology of uniform convergence and turns
\Homeo(X)
into a Polish topological group.

2.1.
The Space of Maximal Connected Chains

Given a topological space
X
, let
K(X)
denote the set of compact subspaces of
X
. The
Vietoris topology
on
K(X)
is generated by sets of form
\{K\in K(X):K\subseteq U\}
and
\{K\in K(X):K\cap U\neq\emptyset\}
, for
U
open in
X
.
If
X
is compact, the Vietoris topology on
K(X)
is also compact and the subspace
C(X)\subseteq K(X)
of
subcontinua
, i.e. compact connected sets, is closed (hence compact itself).
If
X
is moreover metrizable, say by a metric
d
, then the associated
Hausdorff metric
on
K(X)
, defined by

d_{H}(K,K^{\prime})\coloneqq\max\left\{\sup_{x\in K}d(x,K^{\prime}),\sup_{x^{\prime}\in K^{\prime}}d(K,x^{\prime})\right\},

is compatible with the Vietoris topology.
Given
U_{0},\dots,U_{n-1}

regular
open sets, we define the open set:

O(U_{0},\dots,U_{n-1})\coloneqq\left\{K\in K(X):K\cap U_{i}\not=\emptyset\text{ for every }i<n\text{ and }K\subseteq\bigvee_{i<n}U_{i}\right\}.

A collection
\mathcal{C}
of compact subsets of
X
is called a
chain
(a
nest
in
[
15
]
) if it is linearly ordered by inclusion, that is, if for every
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathcal{C}
, either
C_{1}\subseteq C_{2}
or
C_{2}\subseteq C_{1}
. A chain
\mathcal{C}\subseteq K(X)
is
maximal
if it is not properly contained in any other chain, and it is
connected
if it consists of connected sets.

If
X
is compact and
\mathcal{C}\subseteq K(X)
is a maximal chain, then
\mathcal{C}
is compact (as a subspace of
K(X)
), hence it can be thought of as a point in
K(K(X))
.
If moreover
X
is a continuum, every
maximal connected chain is the image of a topological embedding
[0,1]\to C(X)
, so it is in particular an element of
C(C(X))
(see
[
15
]
*Lemma 14.7).
In this case, we define the space
\Phi(X)
of
maximal connected chains on
X
as

\Phi(X)\coloneqq\{\mathcal{C}\in C(C(X)):\mathcal{C}\text{ is a maximal connected chain}\},

which is a compact subset of
C(C(X))
(see
[
34
]
*§2).
The map
\Phi(X)\to X
that to each chain
\mathcal{C}
associates the unique element of the singleton
\bigcap\mathcal{C}
– the so-called
root
of
\mathcal{C}
– is continuous.
Given a compatible metric
d
on
X
, we denote by
d_{\Phi}
the Hausdorff metric on
\Phi(X)
relative to
d_{H}
.

From now on, all chains we consider are maximal and connected.

The action
\Homeo(X)\curvearrowright X
naturally induces a continuous action
\Homeo(X)\curvearrowright\Phi(X)
defined as
h\cdot\mathcal{C}\coloneqq\{h[C]:C\in\mathcal{C}\}
, for
h\in\Homeo(X)
and
\mathcal{C}\in\Phi(X)
.
We say that a chain
\mathcal{C}\in\Phi(X)
is
generic
if
\Homeo(X)\cdot\mathcal{C}
is comeager in
\Phi(X)
.

3.
Walks on Graphs

Throughout this paper, the word graph refers to finite connected reflexive undirected graphs.

In this section we present definitions and terminology on graphs and walks, as well as some preliminary results on the notion of winding number, which is introduced in
Definition

3.2
.

Let
(G,E)
be a graph.
We denote by
d_{G}
, or just
d
if there is no risk of confusion, the
path metric
on
G
.
If
x\in G
and
A\subseteq G
, we write
x\mathbin{E}A
if there is
y\in A
with
x\mathbin{E}y
, and we denote by
[A]_{n}\coloneqq\{x\in G:d_{G}(x,A)\leq n\}
the closed ball of
d_{G}
-radius
n
around
A
.
If
A=\{x\}
, we write
[x]_{n}
.

If
f,g\colon(G,E)\to(H,E^{\prime})
are functions between the same two graphs, we denote by
d_{\sup}(f,g)\coloneqq\max\{d_{H}(f(x),g(x)):x\in G\}
their distance according to the supremum metric.

A function
f\colon(G,E)\to(H,E^{\prime})
is a
monotone epimorphism
(also called

in
[
32
]
) if it is a homomorphism of graphs (that is,
f(x)\mathbin{E}^{\prime}f(y)
whenever
x\mathbin{E}y
) such that the preimage of each nonempty connected subset of
H
is nonempty and connected in
G
.
Equivalently, it is a homomorphism of graphs which is surjective on edges and such that the preimage of each vertex of
H
is nonempty and connected (see
[
32
]
*Lemma 1.1).
In particular, monotone epimorphisms are onto.

A
walk
on
(G,E)
is a sequence of vertices
w=\langle w(0),\dots,w(\ell-1)\rangle
such that
w(i)\mathbin{E}w(i+1)
for every
i<\ell-1
.
We say that
\ell
is the
length
of
w
and denote it by
\len(w)
.
We admit the empty walk, which has length
0
.
We denote by
\Imm(w)\coloneqq\{w(i):i<\len(w)\}
.
Adopting a common programming language convention, let us write
w(-1)
in lieu of
w(\len(w)-1)
for the last element of
w
.
With a slight abuse of notation, we shall write
[w]_{n}

for the set
[\Imm(w)]_{n}
.

If
f\colon(G,E)\to(H,E^{\prime})
is a graph homomorphism and
w
is a walk on
(G,E)
, let
f\cdot w
be the induced walk
\langle f(w(0)),\dots,f(w(-1))\rangle
on
(H,E^{\prime})
.

A walk
w
is

(1)

reduced
if
w(i)\neq w(i+1)
for every
i<\len(w)-1
;

(2)

uncrossed
if whenever
i,j<\len(w)-1
are such that
w(i)=w(j)
, then
w(i+1)=w(j+1)
;

(3)

a
path
if
w(i)=w(j)
if and only if
i=j
;

(4)

a
spaced path
if
w(i)\mathbin{E}w(j)
if and only if
|i-j|\leq 1
for every
i,j<\len(w)
.

If
A,B\subseteq C\subseteq G
are nonempty subsets, a
path from
A
to
B
in
C
is a path

on

such that

and

Such a path always exists if

If

is a path, we refer to

A walk is a

if it has length at least

,
w(i)\neq w(j)
for each

, and

.
We modify the definition of spaced for circular paths accordingly.
We also define the

of a circular path

as

Given two walks

on
(G,E)

is

,
and that

is
confined
in some

.
We say that

refines

, if:

for every

, there is

such that
\{w(i):i<t\}=\{v(j):j<s\}
.

In words:

starts on the same vertex as

, it then visits each

vertex that

visits, in the same order, possibly backtracking from time to time, until it covers all that has been visited by
v
, after which it is allowed to roam free.

We say that

monotonically

if there is a strictly increasing function

with
f(0)=0
and such that

w(i)=v(j)
for all

when

,

and
w(i)=v(-1)
for

.
If

, then it refines

, and

are confined to one another.

Given two walks

on
(G,E)
such that

, let

be the walk obtained by the

of

and

.
Note that each reduced uncrossed walk

admits a

, where

is a path,

is a circular path, and

is an initial segment of

(
v,c
or

could be empty).

Finally, we will say that a reduced uncrossed walk is a

if its decomposition is

, for a path

and a nonempty circular path

.

Monotone maps allow lifting walks, as we see in the following lemma.

3.1.
The Winding Number of a Walk

Definition 3.2
.

Let

be a graph
and

be a circular path on

For each ordered pair of vertices

\weight_{C}(x,y)\coloneqq\begin{cases}1&\text{if there is }i<\ell\text{ such that }x=C(i),\,y=C(i+1\text{ mod}\,\ell)\\
-1&\text{if there is }i<\ell\text{ such that }y=C(i),\,x=C(i+1\text{ mod}\,\ell)\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Notice that
\weight_{C}
is well-defined since

is a path.
For any walk

on

, we define its

as

\wind_{C}(w)\coloneqq\sum_{i<\len(w)-1}\weight_{C}(w(i),w(i+1)).

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a graph
and
C
be a circular path on
\mathcal{S}
.
Let

and

be walks on

such that

.
Then
\wind_{C}(w)=\wind_{C}(v)
.

Proof.

By assumption there is a strictly increasing function

with

f(0)=0\text{ and such that }w(i)=v(j)\text{ for all }f(j)\leq i<f(j+1).

In particular

\weight_{C}(w(i),w(i+1))=0

\displaystyle\wind_{C}(w)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j<\len(v)-1}\weight_{C}(w(f(j)),w(f(j+1)))

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a graph and
C
be a circular path on
\mathcal{S}
.
Let
\mathcal{W}
be a graph and
\alpha\colon\mathcal{W}\to\mathcal{S}
be a graph homomorphism.
Let

be a spaced path on
\mathcal{W}
and

be a walk on
\mathcal{W}
which refines and is confined to an initial segment of

.
Then

\wind_{C}(\alpha\cdot z)=\wind_{C}(\alpha\cdot\langle w(0),\dots,w(j)\rangle),

where

is the unique index such that

.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on the length

of

If

, then

\wind_{C}(\alpha\cdot z)=0=\wind_{C}(\alpha\cdot\langle w(0)\rangle).

For

.
Suppose

, for some

.
Since

, which is a spaced path, it must be that

.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a graph and
C
be a spaced circular path on
\mathcal{S}
of circular length
\ell\geq 4
.
Let
z,z^{\prime}
be walks of the same length
k
on
\mathcal{S}
, which are confined to
C
and such that, for each
i<k
,
d_{\mathcal{S}}(z(i),z^{\prime}(i))\leq 1
, and also, for
i<k-1
,
d_{\mathcal{S}}(z^{\prime}(i),z(i+1))\leq 1
.
Then
\lvert\wind_{C}(z)-\wind_{C}(z^{\prime})\rvert\leq 2
.

Proof.

We prove that under the hypotheses:

\wind_{C}(z^{\prime})=\weight_{C}(z^{\prime}(0),z(0))+\wind_{C}(z)+\weight_{C}(z(-1),z^{\prime}(-1)).

The conclusion then follows immediately, since the values
\weight_{C}(z^{\prime}(0),z(0))
and
\weight_{C}(z(-1),z^{\prime}(-1))
have absolute values at most
1
.

We show by induction that for each
i<k
:

(3.2)

\wind_{C}(\langle z^{\prime}(0),\dots,z^{\prime}(i)\rangle)=\weight_{C}(z^{\prime}(0),z(0))+\\
+\wind_{C}(\langle z(0),\dots,z(i)\rangle)+\weight_{C}(z(i),z^{\prime}(i)).

If
i=0
, then
\weight_{C}(z^{\prime}(0),z(0))=-\weight_{C}(z(0),z^{\prime}(0))
, so (
3.2
) holds.

Suppose (
3.2
) is true for
i<k-1
and let us prove it for
i+1
.
By definition of
\wind_{C}
it suffices to verify that

\weight_{C}(z^{\prime}(i),z^{\prime}(i+1))=\weight_{C}(z(i),z(i+1))+\\
+\weight_{C}(z(i+1),z^{\prime}(i+1))-\weight_{C}(z(i),z^{\prime}(i)).

But
-\weight_{C}(z(i),z^{\prime}(i))=\weight_{C}(z^{\prime}(i),z(i))
and, using that
z^{\prime}(i),z(i+1)
lie in
C
at distance at most
1
we get

\weight_{C}(z^{\prime}(i),z(i))+\weight_{C}(z(i),z(i+1))=\weight_{C}(z^{\prime}(i),z(i+1)).

Also
z^{\prime}(i),z^{\prime}(i+1)
are at distance at most
1
, so

\weight_{C}(z^{\prime}(i),z(i+1))+\weight_{C}(z(i+1),z^{\prime}(i+1))=\weight_{C}(z^{\prime}(i),z^{\prime}(i+1)),

and we are done.
∎

4.
Brick Partitions

In this section we summarize some well-known definitions and facts about brick partitions of Peano continua which will be of use in the next sections.

A
quasi-partition
of a space
X
is a finite family
\mathcal{W}
of pairwise disjoint open subsets whose union is dense in
X
.
If
d
is a metric on
X
, the
mesh
of a quasi-partition
\mathcal{W}
, denoted
\mathrm{mesh}_{d}(\mathcal{W})
, is the maximum of the diameters of its elements.
Each quasi-partition
\mathcal{W}
gives rise to a finite graph
(\mathcal{W},E)
– its
nerve graph
–, namely the graph whose vertex set is
\mathcal{W}

and such that there is an edge
W\mathbin{E}W^{\prime}
if and only if
\Cl(W)\cap\Cl(W^{\prime})\not=\emptyset
.
To avoid confusion, we say that
A\subseteq\mathcal{W}
is
\mathbin{E}
-connected
when
A
is connected as a subgraph of
(\mathcal{W},E)
. With a slight abuse of notation, we shall denote the edge relation on nerve graphs of different quasi-partitions always with the letter
E
(it will always be clear from the context to which quasi-partition we are referring to).

A metric space
X
is
uniformly locally connected
if for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that for all
x,y\in X
,
d(x,y)<\delta
implies that there is a connected set
C\subseteq X
with
x,y\in C
and
\diam(C)<\varepsilon
. Peano continua are uniformly locally connected, and in a Peano continuum the connected set
C
can be always assumed to be an arc.

A quasi-partition
\mathcal{W}=\{W_{0},\ldots,W_{n-1}\}
of a Peano continuum
X
is called a
brick partition
if
W_{i}
is a connected and uniformly locally connected regular open set for every
i<n
, and
W_{i}\vee W_{j}
is uniformly locally connected for all
i,j<n
, with respect to some/each compatible metric on
X
.
In particular, if
W_{i}\mathbin{E}W_{j}
then
W_{i}\vee W_{j}
is connected.

Given a quasi-partition
\mathcal{U}
of a Peano continuum
X
and a partition
\mathcal{P}=\{\mathcal{U}_{0},\dots,\mathcal{U}_{n-1}\}
of
\mathcal{U}
into
\mathbin{E}
-connected subsets, the
amalgam
of
\mathcal{U}
with respect to
\mathcal{P}
is the quasi-partition
\{\bigvee\mathcal{U}_{0},\dots,\bigvee\mathcal{U}_{n-1}\}
of
X
.
The amalgam of a brick partition is again a brick partition.

A family of open sets
\mathcal{W}
is said to
refine
another one
\mathcal{V}
, in symbols
\mathcal{W}\preceq\mathcal{V}
, if every element of
\mathcal{W}
is contained in an element of
\mathcal{V}
.
In the case of brick partitions, a refinement
\mathcal{W}\preceq\mathcal{V}
gives rise to a monotone epimorphism from the nerve graph of
\mathcal{W}
to that of
\mathcal{V}
, given by mapping
W\mapsto V
whenever
W\subseteq V
.
We denote such map by
\rho^{\mathcal{W}}_{\mathcal{V}}\colon\mathcal{W}\to\mathcal{V}
.
Given two brick partitions
\mathcal{W},\mathcal{V}
and two walks
w,v
on
\mathcal{W}
and
\mathcal{V}
respectively,
we write
(\mathcal{W},w)\preceq(\mathcal{V},v)
whenever
\mathcal{W}\preceq\mathcal{V}
and
\rho^{\mathcal{W}}_{\mathcal{V}}\cdot w\sqsubseteq v
.

We will make use of the following fundamental result of Bing from
[

,

]

Theorem 4.1

(

6.2.
Robust cycles

The next step is to find a sufficient condition on

Broadly speaking, our goal is to

Once this is ensured, if

is negative and very large in absolute value. If

would permit to amalgamate up to distance one

were chosen to have very distant winding numbers. The idea of using the winding number to show the failure of such amalgamation goes back to the proof of
[

]
*Theorem 1.2.

Lemma 6.8

of

Claim 6.8.1

6.5.
Circular covering

We recall from the introduction that a Peano continuum

has a circular covering if it admits a covering
O_{0},\dots,O_{\ell-1}
in
\ell\geq 4
connected open subsets such that
\Cl(O_{i})\cap\Cl(O_{j})\neq\emptyset

is connected for each

.
Examples of Peano continua with no locally separating points admitting a circular covering are the torus, the Menger curve or the Sierpiński carpet.

Proposition 6.11
.

A Peano continuum has a circular covering if and only if it admits a brick partition whose nerve graph is a cycle of length at least

Proof.

Suppose that

has a brick partition

whose nerve graph is a cycle of length

Say the elements of

are

Let

be given by

We can moreover pick

fine enough so that no element of
\Star(\Cl(S_{i}),\mathcal{W})

Note furthermore that every

Moreover,

Conversely, suppose

Define

These sets are closed and pairwise disjoint, since

Let
X
be a Peano continuum with no locally separating points.
Let
G\leq\Homeo(X)
be such that
G\curvearrowright X
is locally transitive.
Then the action
G\curvearrowright\Phi(X)
is minimal and comeagerly many points are turbulent.

Proof.

By
Proposition

7.5
,
G\curvearrowright X
is minimal, so to prove minimality of
G\curvearrowright\Phi(X)
it is enough to check that the conditions of
Proposition

7.1
are satisfied.
To this end, let
\mathcal{C}\in\Phi(X)
with root
x_{0}
be given, together with nonempty open connected sets
U_{0},U_{1},V
such that
U_{0},U_{1}\subseteq V
.

By minimality of
G\curvearrowright X
, up to a translation, we can assume that
x_{0}\in U_{0}
.
Let
C\in\mathcal{C}
be non-trivial and such that
C\subseteq V
, and pick
x_{1}\in C\setminus\{x_{0}\}
.
Fix moreover
y_{1}\in U_{1}
distinct from
x_{0}
.
By
Proposition

7.5
, there is
g\in G_{V}
such that
g(x_{0})=x_{0}\in U_{0}
and
g(x_{1})=y_{1}\in U_{1}\cap g[C]
.
As
g
is the identity outside
V
, we get
g[C]\subseteq V
, hence the action
G\curvearrowright\Phi(X)
is minimal by
Proposition

7.1
.

Towards proving that comeagerly many points of
\Phi(X)
are turbulent, fix
a sequence
\mathcal{U}_{n}
of brick partitions of
X
such that
\mathcal{U}_{n+1}\preceq\mathcal{U}_{n}
and the mesh of
\mathcal{U}_{n}
tends to zero.
By
Proposition

5.2
, the set
Z\coloneqq\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\bigcup\Phi(\mathcal{U}_{n})
is comeager in
\Phi(X)
.
Let
\mathcal{C}\in Z
with root
x_{0}
, an open neighborhood
\mathbf{U}
of
\mathcal{C}
, and an open neighborhood
G_{0}
of the identity in
G
be given.
Since
\mathcal{C}\in Z
, there are
n\in\mathbb{N}
and a walk
u
on
\mathcal{U}_{n}
such that
\Imm(u)=\mathcal{U}_{n}
and
\mathcal{C}\in\mathbf{O}_{\mathcal{U}_{n},u}\subseteq\mathbf{U}
.
To lighten notation, let us rename
\mathcal{U}_{n}
to
\mathcal{U}
.

We prove that the closure of the
(\mathbf{U},G_{0})
-local orbit of
\mathcal{C}
contains
\mathbf{O}_{\mathcal{U},u}
.
To this end, let
\mathbf{V}\subseteq\mathbf{O}_{\mathcal{U},u}
be open and nonempty (notice that
\mathcal{C}
need not belong to
\mathbf{V}
).
By
Corollary

5.4
, there are a brick partition and a walk

such that
\mathbf{O}_{\mathcal{V},v}\subseteq\mathbf{V}
.
Up to a refinement, by
Theorem 6.2

(Upgrading Walks to Paths)
.
, we can suppose that

is a spaced path, and that
\mathcal{V}
is fine enough that

G_{\mathcal{V},1}\subseteq G_{0},

where
G_{\mathcal{V},1}
is defined in (
5.8
).
Crucially, for any
V\mathbin{E}V^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{V}
, the rigid stabilizer

(see (

)) is a subset of
G_{\mathcal{V},1}
, and hence of
G_{0}
.
We use this fact repeatedly from here onwards.

By picking
\mathcal{V}
fine enough we can moreover assume there is
V_{0}\in\mathcal{V}(u(0))
such that
x_{0}\in\Cl(V_{0})
and such that
[V_{0}]_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{V}(u(0))
. Fix a path

P
from
V_{0}
to
v(0)
in
\mathcal{V}(u(0))
.
We will move
x_{0}
along
P
, step by step.
As a preliminary move, let
\mathcal{C}_{-1}\coloneqq\mathcal{C}
and, using
Proposition

7.5
, find
g_{-1}\in G_{[V_{0}]_{1}}\subseteq G_{\mathcal{V},1}
mapping
x_{0}
to some
y_{0}\in V_{0}=P(0)

and set
\mathcal{C}_{0}\coloneqq g_{-1}\cdot\mathcal{C}
. Note that
\mathcal{C}_{0}\in\mathbf{O}_{\mathcal{U},u}
since
\mathcal{C}\in\mathbf{O}_{\mathcal{U},u}
and

fixes every element of

setwise.
Suppose next we are given
\mathcal{C}_{i}\in\mathbf{O}_{\mathcal{U},u}
with root
y_{i}\in P(i)
, for some
i<\len(P)-1
.
By
Proposition

7.5
, there is
g_{i}\in G_{P(i)\vee P(i+1)}
which maps
y_{i}
to some point
y_{i+1}\in P(i+1)
.
Again, since
g_{i}
fixes every element of
u

\mathcal{C}_{i+1}\coloneqq g_{i}\cdot\mathcal{C}_{i}
belongs to
\mathbf{O}_{\mathcal{U},u}
.

To ease the notation, let
\mathcal{D}_{0}\coloneqq\mathcal{C}_{\len(P)-1}
and
z_{0}\coloneqq x_{\len(P)-1}
.
Then
z_{0}
is the root of
\mathcal{D}_{0}
and it belongs to
v(0)
.
We now proceed by induction.

Suppose that
\mathcal{D}_{j}\in\mathbf{O}_{\mathcal{U},u}
with root

is given,
and
\mathcal{D}_{j}\in\mathbf{O}_{\mathcal{V},\langle v(0),\dots,v(j)\rangle}

Let

.
By

is pointwise fixed by

.
Moreover,

\mathcal{D}_{j+1}\in\mathbf{O}_{\mathcal{V},\langle v(0),\dots,v(j+1)\rangle}.

It is left to prove that

.
If
\rho^{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathcal{U}}\cdot\langle v(0),\dots,v(j+1)\rangle\sqsubseteq u
, then

\mathbf{O}_{\mathcal{V},\langle v(0),\dots,v(j+1)\rangle}\subseteq\mathbf{O}_{\mathcal{U},u}
, in which case we conclude by (

).
Otherwise, it holds that
v(j)\vee v(j+1)\subseteq\bigvee_{k<\len(u)-1}u(k)
.
By inductive assumption

, so there is

with

.
Therefore

, and since
h_{j}
fixes
u(-1)
, we can infer

.
Moreover,
h_{j}[A]\subseteq\bigvee_{k<\len(u)}u(k)
since the set on the right is setwise fixed by
h_{j}
.
We conclude by
Lemma

7.2
.

Since
g_{i},h_{j}\in G_{\mathcal{V},1}\subseteq G_{0}
and
\mathcal{C}_{i},\mathcal{D}_{j}\in\mathbf{O}_{\mathcal{U},u}\subseteq\mathbf{U}
for each

, it follows that
\mathcal{D}_{\len(v)-1}
is in the
(\mathbf{U},G_{0})
-local orbit of
\mathcal{C}
.
But
\mathcal{D}_{\len(v)-1}\in\mathbf{O}_{\mathcal{V},v}\subseteq\mathbf{V}
by (

), so we are done.
∎

We finally obtain:

Corollary 7.8
.

Let
X
be a strongly locally homogeneous Peano continuum which is not the circle, the sphere, or the real projective plane.
Then
\Homeo(X)
does not have the generic point property.
In particular, its universal minimal flow is not metrizable and has no comeager orbit.

The same is true for any closed
G\leq\Homeo(X)
which acts locally transitively on
X
.

Proof.

The action
G\curvearrowright\Phi(X)
is minimal, by
Theorem

7.7
.
But
\Homeo(X)\curvearrowright\Phi(X)
has no comeager orbit by
Corollary

1.5
, so neither does
G\curvearrowright\Phi(X)
.
∎

Corollary 7.9
.

Let
X
be a strongly locally homogeneous Peano continuum which is not the circle, the sphere, or the real projective plane.
Then chains on
X
are not classifiable by countable structures.

Proof.

This follows by combining
Theorem

7.4
,
Theorem

7.7
, and
Corollary

1.5
.
∎

Remark 7.11
.

Corollary

7.8
was proved in the case of closed manifolds of dimension at least 3, and the Hilbert cube, in
[
12
]
.
Moreover, one can more easily show that
\Homeo(Y)
and the subgroup

of orientation preserving homeomorphism, for an orientable closed surface of positive genus

A.1.

We begin by giving a more concrete description of

Recall from

The elements of

\varphi\colon C([0,1])\to\mathbb{R}^{2}

(0,0)
,
(0,1)
and
(1,1)

]
*Example 5.1.

For pairs of points in

iff

and

Recall that every chain in
\Phi([0,1])
is the image of an injective continuous order-preserving function

Under the identification of

\mathcal{C}\in\Phi([0,1])
gives rise to an arc
\varphi\cdot\mathcal{C}\subseteq T
containing the vertex

(x_{0},x_{0})

.

with the subspace of

of such arcs, dropping any mention of

.

\Homeo_{+}([0,1])
acts on

The induced action on

Definition A.1
.

Given a chain
\mathcal{C}\subseteq T
, a closed subset
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
is called a
subchain (of
\mathcal{C}
)
if it is connected (in
T
).
A non-trivial subchain
\mathcal{I}
is

(1)

horizontal
if for all
(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}
, we have
y=y^{\prime}
,

(2)

vertical
if for all
(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}
, we have
x=x^{\prime}
,

(3)

strictly monotone
in
\mathcal{C}
if for all
(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}
, we have
x=x^{\prime}
iff
y=y^{\prime}
.

A subchain
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
is
maximal horizontal
if it is horizontal and it is not properly contained in any horizontal subchain of
\mathcal{C}
.
Analogously, we define
maximal vertical subchains
and
maximal strictly monotone subchains
.
By Zorn’s Lemma, each horizontal/vertical/strictly monotone subchain, is contained in a maximal subchain of the same type.
A subchain is
maximal
if it is either maximal horizontal, maximal vertical, or maximal strictly monotone.

Each subchain
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
has a maximum and a minimum element with respect to
\sqsubseteq
.
If
(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})
are the maximum and minimum of
\mathcal{I}
, we call the intervals
[x_{1},x_{2}]
and
[y_{2},y_{1}]
in

the

and

of
\mathcal{I}
, respectively, and denote them by
\mathrm{dom}(\mathcal{I})
and
\mathrm{codom}(\mathcal{I})
, see
Figure

2
.
Notice that if
\mathcal{I}
is maximal, then at least one among the domain and codomain is non-trivial.

\includeinkscape

[scale=1]img/domain

\mathcal{I}
.

Note that if
\mathcal{C}\subseteq T
is a chain,
and
\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
are distinct maximal subchains, then their domains meet in at most one point, and the same is true for their codomains.
Since the domains of maximal horizontal and maximal strictly monotone subchains are non-trivial, it follows that there are only countably many such subchains.
By reasoning about codomains, we also see that there are countably many maximal vertical subchains, so that
\mathcal{C}
contains countably many maximal subchains.

We claim that if
(x_{0},x_{0})
is the root of
\mathcal{C}
, then the union of the domains of maximal subchains of
\mathcal{C}
is dense in
[0,x_{0}]
, while the union of their codomains is dense in
[x_{0},1]
.
The projection of
\mathcal{C}
on the
x

[0,x_{0}]
and on the
y
-axis it is
[x_{0},1]
.
Suppose
x_{0}\neq 0,1
, as otherwise the statement is trivial.
Let us show the result for the domains, the result for the codomains follows analogously.
Fix
0\leq x_{1}\leq x_{2}\leq x_{0}
, and let
(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})
be points in
\mathcal{C}
.
If the subchain
\mathcal{I}
with maximum
(x_{1},y_{1})
and minimum
(x_{2},y_{2})
is not strictly monotone, there is a subchain included in it which is either vertical or horizontal.
In all three cases,
\mathcal{I}
intersects a maximal subchain whose domain contains a point between
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
, and we are done.

Before proceeding with the proof of the classifiability result we are after, we need to check that a few sets that will arise in its proof are Borel, which we do in the following two lemmas.

Lemma A.2
.

The following subsets of
\Phi([0,1])\times C([0,1])
(identified with a subspace of
C(T)\times T
as above) are Borel:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{E}^{H}\coloneqq\{(\mathcal{C},(x,y)):&(x,y)\text{ is the maximum of a maximal}\\
&\text{horizontal subchain in }\mathcal{C}\},\end{split}

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{E}^{V}\coloneqq\{(\mathcal{C},(x,y)):&(x,y)\text{ is the maximum of a maximal}\\
&\text{vertical subchain in }\mathcal{C}\},\end{split}

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{E}^{S}\coloneqq\{(\mathcal{C},(x,y)):&(x,y)\text{ is the maximum of a maximal}\\
&\text{strictly monotone subchain in }\mathcal{C}\}.\end{split}

Proof.

(x,y)\in\mathcal{C}
is a closed condition.
Then
(x,y)
is the maximum of a horizontal subchain in
\mathcal{C}
if and only if
(x+1/n,y)\in\mathcal{C}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, which is a
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{0}_{2}
condition.
Finally, it is the maximum of a
maximal
horizontal subchain in
\mathcal{C}
if and only if moreover
(x-1/n,y)\not\in\mathcal{C}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, which is a
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{2}
condition, showing that
\mathcal{E}^{H}
is Borel.
The argument for
\mathcal{E}^{V}
is exactly the same, so it remains to show that
\mathcal{E}^{S}
is Borel.
Indeed,
(x,y)
is the maximum of a maximal strictly monotone subchain in
\mathcal{C}
if and only if the following conditions are all satisfied:

(a)

(x,y)\in\mathcal{C}
, which is a closed condition,

(b)

there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
there are no
(x_{1},y_{1})
and
(x_{2},y_{2})\in\mathcal{C}
such that for
i\in\{1,2\}
,
x\leq x_{i}\leq x+1/n
,
y\geq y_{i}\geq y-1/n
and either
x_{1}=x_{2}
and
y_{1}=y_{2}+1/m
or
y_{1}=y_{2}
and
x_{1}=x_{2}+1/m
, which is a
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{0}_{3}
condition,

(c)

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
m\in\mathbb{N}
and there exist
(x_{1},y_{1})
and
(x_{2},y_{2})\in\mathcal{C}
such that for
i\in\{1,2\}
,
x\geq x_{i}\geq x-1/n
,
y\leq y_{i}\leq y+1/n
and either
x_{1}=x_{2}
and
y_{1}=y_{2}+1/m
or
y_{1}=y_{2}
and
x_{1}=x_{2}+1/m
, which is a
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{3}
condition,

showing that

is Borel.

∎

Lemma A.3
.

The subspace
\Phi^{\infty}([0,1])\subseteq\Phi([0,1])
consisting of all chains containing infinitely many maximal subchains is Borel.
The subspace
\Phi^{0}([0,1])
of chains with no horizontal or vertical subchains is
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{2}
.

Proof.

Note that a chain contains finitely many maximal subchains if and only if it contains finitely many horizontal subchains and finitely many vertical subchains,
since between any two distinct maximal strictly monotone subchains there must be a maximal horizontal or vertical subchain.
Therefore, to prove that
\Phi^{\infty}([0,1])
is Borel, it suffices to show that, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the sets
\Phi^{\geq n}_{H}([0,1])
and
\Phi^{\geq n}_{V}([0,1])
consisting respectively of chains containing at least
n
horizontal subchains and chains containing at least
n
vertical subchains are Borel.
Note that, for all
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
the set
\Phi^{\geq n,m}_{H}([0,1])
consisting of all chains that contain at least
n
horizontal subchains, which are each of length at least
1/m
and are pairwise at distance at least
1/m
is closed, and that every element of
\Phi^{\geq n}_{H}([0,1])
is contained in
\Phi^{\geq n,m}_{H}([0,1])
for some
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
.
This shows that

\Phi^{\geq n}_{H}([0,1])=\bigcup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\Phi^{\geq n,m}_{H}([0,1])

is
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{0}_{2}
.
By an analogous argument
\Phi^{\geq n}_{V}([0,1])
is also
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{0}_{2}
, showing that
\Phi^{\infty}([0,1])
is
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{3}
.

Moreover,
\Phi^{0}([0,1])
is the complement of the union of
\Phi^{\geq 1}_{H}([0,1])
and
\Phi^{\geq 1}_{H}([0,1])
, and thus is
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{2}
.
∎

Proposition A.4

The chain
\mathcal{C}_{0}\coloneqq\{[1/2-\alpha,1/2+\alpha]:0\leq\alpha\leq 1/2\}
is a generic chain on
[0,1]
.

Proof.

We prove that
\Homeo([0,1])\cdot\mathcal{C}_{0}=\Phi^{0}([0,1])
and that
\Phi^{0}([0,1])
is dense in
\Phi([0,1])
, and conclude by
Lemma

A.3
.

The chain
\mathcal{C}_{0}
is the graph of the function
f_{0}(x)\coloneqq 1-x
from
[0,1/2]\to[1/2,1]
.
Clearly
\Phi^{0}([0,1])
is invariant under the action of
\Homeo([0,1])
, so fix
\mathcal{C}\in\Phi^{0}([0,1])
with the objective of finding
h\in\Homeo([0,1])
such that
h\cdot\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{0}
.
Up to precomposing with a
g\in\Homeo_{+}([0,1])
which maps the minimum of
\mathcal{C}
to
(1/2,1/2)
, we can suppose that also
\mathcal{C}
has minimum
(1/2,1/2)
.
Then
\mathcal{C}
is the graph of a continuous strictly decreasing function
f\colon[0,1/2]\to[1/2,1]
such that
f(0)=1,f(1/2)=1/2
.
Let

be the identity on
[0,1/2]
and

for

.
Then

that is,

.

To check for density, notice that given any finite sequence
(x_{0},y_{0})\sqsubseteq\cdots\sqsubseteq(x_{n-1},y_{n-1})
of points in the interior of
T
, and any
\varepsilon>0
, there is a continuous strictly decreasing function

such that
f(0)=1
and
\lvert f(x_{i})-y_{i}\rvert<\varepsilon
, for all

, and that the intersection of the graph of

with

is a chain in
\Phi^{0}([0,1])
.
We conclude by noticing that the above condition determines a basic open set in
\Phi([0,1])
.
∎

Theorem A.5
.

Chains on the interval
[0,1]
are classifiable by countable structures.

Proof.

Since
\Homeo_{+}([0,1])
is a clopen subgroup of

Theorem A.6

Appendix B
The Sierpiński carpet

This appendix is devoted to proving that the action
\Homeo(S)\curvearrowright\Phi(S)
is minimal, for the Sierpiński carpet
S
, and thus concluding that
\Homeo(S)
does not have the generic point property and its universal minimal flow is not metrizable.
Since, as we will see, the Sierpiński carpet is not homogeneous, and thus not strongly locally homogeneous, the minimality of
\Homeo(S)\curvearrowright\Phi(S)
does not follow from
Theorem

7.7
.
We do not settle whether this action is generically turbulent.

An
S
-curve
is a one-dimensional planar Peano continuum such that its complement in the plane consists of countably many connected open sets whose boundaries are mutually disjoint simple closed curves.
[
37
]
*Theorem 4 states there exists a unique one-dimensional planar Peano continuum
S
with no locally separating points, and that all
S
-curves are homeomorphic to such
S
. The space
S
is called the
Sierpiński carpet
.

It is immediate that
S
satisfies condition (
2
) of
Theorem

1.2
, and it is not hard to see that it also satisfies condition (
3
).

Corollary B.1
.

There is no generic chain on the Sierpiński carpet.

The Sierpiński carpet has two types of points:
rational
and
irrational
.
The collection of rational points is given by the union of all the boundaries of the maximal connected components of the complements of
S
in the plane or, equivalently and more abstractly,
it is the union of all non-trivial non-locally-separating proper subcontinua of
S
(see
[
10
]
*§8).
There are countably many maximal such subcontinua, called
rational boundary curves
, they are all simple closed curves, and their union is dense and co-dense in the space.
Since it is the union of countably many non-locally-separating subcontinua, the set of rational points is a non-locally-separating
F_{\sigma}
set, by
[
28
]
*Theorem 2.2.
The set of irrational points is the complement.
A subcontinuum of
S
is said to be rational/irrational if all of its points are rational/irrational.

The action
\Homeo(S)\curvearrowright S
has two orbits, the rational and irrational points
[
22
]
, thus it is minimal, but not locally transitive.
