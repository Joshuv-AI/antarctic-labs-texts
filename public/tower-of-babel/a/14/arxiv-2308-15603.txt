Abstract
This paper considers multiple domination on Kneser graphs. We focus on k k -tuple dominating sets, 2 2 -packings and the associated graph parameters k k -tuple domination number and 2 2 -packing number. In particular, we compute the 2 2 -packing number of Kneser graphs K ⁡ ( 3 ​ r − 2 , r ) K({3r-2,r}) and in odd graphs we obtain minimum k k -tuple dominating sets of K ⁡ ( 7 , 3 ) K(7,3) and K ⁡ ( 11 , 5 ) K(11,5) for every k k . Besides, we determine the Kneser graphs K ⁡ ( n , r ) K({n,r}) with k k -tuple domination number exactly k + r k+r and find all the minimum k k -tuple dominating sets for these graphs, which generalize results for domination on Kneser graphs. Finally, we give a characterization of the k k -tuple dominating sets of K ⁡ ( n , 2 ) K({n,2}) in terms of the occurrences of the elements in [ n ] [n] , which allows us to obtain minimum sized k k -tuple dominating sets of K ⁡ ( n , 2 ) K({n,2}) for n ≥ Ω ⁡ ( k ) n\geq\Omega(\sqrt{k}) . Keywords : Kneser graphs, multiple domination, k k -tuple domination, 2 2 -packings.

2

Packings and dominating sets have been extensively studied in graphs. In this regard,
2
-packings have been received particular attention and determining the
2
-packing number of classes of graphs is a challenging and interesting topic, see e.g.
[
28
,
4
,
10
,
8
]
. Given a graph
G
, a subset
S\subseteq V(G)
is
called a
2
-packing
of
G
if for every pair of vertices
u,v\in S
, their closed neighborhoods satisfy
N\left[{u}\right]\cap N\left[{v}\right]=\emptyset
. In other words, no two vertices in the
2
-packing have any common neighbor. The
2
-packing number
of a graph
G
, denoted by
\rho\left(G\right)
, is the maximum cardinality of a
2
-packing in
G
. In
[
7
]
, the author establishes a relationship between the
2
-packing number and the
k
-tuple domination number.

Theorem 2
.

[
7
, Theorem 2.3]

Let
k\geq 2
be an integer. For any graph
G
of order
n
and
\delta\left({G}\right)\geq k
,

k\rho\left(G\right)\leq\gamma_{\times k}\left(G\right)\leq n-\rho\left(G\right).

It is known that when
n\geq 3r-1
with
r\geq 2
, the diameter of the Kneser graph
K({n,r})
is equal to
2

[
38
]
.
Therefore, in these cases the
2
-packing number of the Kneser graph
K({n,r})
is equal to
1
.
Thus, for the remaining of this section, we consider
2r+1\leq n\leq 3r-2
.
Similarly to other works on domination on Kneser graphs, we use
\rho\left(n,r\right)
to denote the
2
-packing number of the Kneser graph
K({n,r})
, and
\gamma_{\times k}\left(n,r\right)
to denote the
k
-tuple domination number of the Kneser graph
K({n,r})
. For positive integers

, we denote by

and

Let

be a
2
-packing in

. If

and

are two vertices in

then

. Besides, notice that if

, then

|[n]\setminus(u\cup v)|=|[n]|-|u|-|v|+|u\cap v|\geq r,

This implies that there exists a vertex

Remark 3
.

Let

. A set

of

2

if and only if for every pair

Some results on extremal combinatorics and intersecting families can be used to give upper bounds for the

\rho\left(n,r\right)\leq\binom{n}{(3r-1)-n}

In the case

We introduce the following notation, which will be used throughout the paper. Given a set of vertices

Let

-set, then it holds

Proof.

Let

-set of

\gamma_{\times k}\left(G\right)=|D|\geq\sum_{u\in S}\underbrace{|N[u]\cap D|}_{\geq k}\geq k|S|=k\rho\left(G\right)=\gamma_{\times k}\left(G\right).

(2)

In consequence,

3
General results on
k
-tuple domination on Kneser graphs

We note that the bounds for the
k
-tuple domination number given in Theorem
2
are not tight in general. For example, as we have mentioned,
\rho(K({n,r}))=1
if
n\geq 3r-1
, but
\gamma_{\times 1}\left(n,r\right)=\gamma\left(K({n,r})\right)\geq r+1
for
n\geq r^{2}+r
. Also, the known general bounds for
k
-tuple domination number of graphs
[
7
,
9
,
15
,
37
]
are not efficient to determine the
\gamma_{\times k}\left(n,r\right)
for
n\geq 2r+1
. Moreover, some of these upper bounds applied to
\gamma_{\times k}\left(n,r\right)
are increasing with respect to
n
whereas, as we will prove in Theorem
12
, the parameter
\gamma_{\times k}\left(n,r\right)
is decreasing with respect to
n
. Therefore, in this section we will apply properties of Kneser graphs to study
k
-tuple dominating sets on
K({n,r})
for
n
large enough.

Since Kneser graphs are regular graphs and each vertex in
K({n,r})
has degree
\binom{n-r}{r}
, then the Kneser graph
K({n,r})
can only have a
k
-tuple dominating set if
k\leq\binom{n-r}{r}+1
. Furthermore, if
k=\binom{n-r}{r}+1
, the entire set of vertices in
K({n,r})
is the only
k
-tuple dominating set.
The Kneser graph
K({n,1})
is isomorphic to the complete graph
K_{n}
. In this case,
\gamma_{\times k}\left(n,1\right)=k
for
k\in[n]
.
On the other hand, if
n=2r
, then the Kneser graph
K({2r,r})
is isomorphic to
\frac{1}{2}\binom{2r}{r}
copies of
K_{2}
. It is noteworthy that
\gamma_{\times 1}\left(2r,r\right)=\frac{1}{2}\binom{2r}{r}
,
\gamma_{\times 2}\left(2r,r\right)=\binom{2r}{r}
, and no
k
-tuple dominating set exists for these graphs if
k\geq 3
.
Thus, for the subsequent discussion, we consider
n\geq 2r+1
and
r\geq 2
.

In Theorem
1
it is shown that if
n\geq r^{2}+r
, then
\gamma_{\times 1}\left(n,r\right)=\gamma\left(K({n,r})\right){}=r+1
. Moreover, for every
k
we state the following result.

Lemma 9
.

If
n\geq r(k+r)
, then
\gamma_{\times k}\left(n,r\right)=k+r
.

Proof.

Let
D=\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{k+r}\}
be a set of vertices of
K({n,r})
such that
u_{i}\cap u_{j}=\emptyset
for all
i\neq j
. It is possible since
n\geq r(k+r)
. Consider a vertex
u
of
K({n,r})
. If
u\in D
, then there exists
i\in\{1,\ldots,k+r\}
such that
u=u_{i}
. Since
u\cap u_{j}=\emptyset
for all
j\neq i
, we have
D\setminus\{u_{i}\}\subseteq N(u)
. Thus,

|D\cap N[u]|=|D|=k+r\geq k.

If
u\notin D
, we have
|\{i:u_{i}\cap u\neq\emptyset\}|\leq r
. So

|D\cap N[u]|\geq|D|-r=k.

Therefore
D
is a
k
-tuple dominating set of
K({n,r})
with cardinality
k+r
, and
\gamma_{\times k}\left(n,r\right)\leq k+r
.

Now, assume that there exists a
k
-tuple dominating set
D
with cardinality
|D|=k+r-1
. Let us consider
r
distinct vertices
u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}
of
D
. Let
a_{1}\in u_{1}
and for each
2\leq i\leq r
we choose
a_{i}\in u_{i}
such that
a_{i}\notin\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{i-1}\}
. Let
w=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{r}\}
. If
w\neq u_{i}
for every
i\in[r]
, then we have
w\cap u_{i}\neq\emptyset
, and so
u_{i}\notin N[w]
for all
i\in[r]
. It turns out that

|D\cap N[w]|\leq|D|-r\leq k-1,

contradicting the fact that
D
is
k
-tuple dominating.

If
w=u_{j}
for some
j
, note that we can choose
b\in[n]\setminus\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}u_{i}\right)
. This choice is possible since
|\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}u_{i}|\leq n-1
. In fact,
u_{j}\subseteq\left(\bigcup_{i\neq j}u_{i}\right)\cup\{a_{j}\}
and then

\left|\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}u_{i}\right|\leq\left|\bigcup_{i\neq j}u_{i}\right|+1\leq r(r-1)+1<r(k+r)\leq n.

Let
w^{\prime}=\left[w\setminus\{a_{j}\}\right]\cup\{b\}
. We have
w^{\prime}\neq u_{i}
for every
i\in[r]
. Besides,
a_{i}\in w^{\prime}\cap u_{i}
for
i\neq j
, and
w^{\prime}\cap u_{j}=w\setminus\{a_{j}\}
. Therefore
w^{\prime}\cap u_{j}\neq\emptyset
. Thus,
u_{i}\notin N[w^{\prime}]
for all
i\in[r]
. Then

|D\cap N[w^{\prime}]|\leq|D|-r\leq k-1,

contradicting the fact that
D
is
k
-tuple dominating.

Therefore, it cannot exist a
k
-tuple dominating set of
K({n,r})
with cardinality less than
k+r
. We conclude that
\gamma_{\times k}\left(n,r\right)=k+r
.
∎

Note that the condition

in Lemma

guarantees the existence of a set of

pairwise disjoint vertices of

.
In the next result, we state that unless

and

, this kind of set families characterizes the

-sets in

for these values of

Let

if

, otherwise. If

Let

This result does not hold when

and

. In fact, in

As a by product of lemmas

and

Theorem 11
.

For
k\geq 2
,
\gamma_{\times k}\left(n,r\right)=k+r
if and only if
n\geq r(k+r)
.

For
n<r(k+r)
we will see that
\gamma_{\times k}\left(n,r\right)
is greater than
k+r
. To this end we observe monotonicity properties of the parameter
\gamma_{\times k}\left(n,r\right)
with respect to
k
and
n
.

Note that if
D
is a
(k+1)
-tuple dominating set of a graph
G
and
v\in D
, then
D\setminus\{v\}
is a
k
-tuple dominating set (
k\geq 1
).
Therefore, for any graph
G
, we have
\gamma_{\times k}\left(G\right)<\gamma_{\times(k+1)}\left(G\right)
whenever
\delta\left({G}\right)\geq k-1
. Then it turns out that for fixed

and

, and

, we have
\gamma_{\times k}\left(n,r\right)<\gamma_{\times(k+1)}\left(n,r\right)
, i.e.
\gamma_{\times k}\left(n,r\right)
is strictly increasing with respect to

.

In addition, notice that there is not monotonicity for the
k
-tuple domination number with respect to induced subgraphs. However, in the case of Kneser graphs, if

,

is an induced subgraph of

and we will show that

for every

such that

. This is, for each fixed

,
\gamma_{\times k}\left(n,r\right)
is decreasing with respect to

.

Theorem 12
.

If
D
is a

is a

Let

be a

-tuple dominating set of

. In order to see that

is a

-tuple dominating set of

of

containing the element

satisfies

|N_{n+1}[u]\cap D|\geq k,

where

denotes the neighbourhood of the vertex

in the graph
K({t,r})
.

Let
\tilde{u}
be a
(r-1)
-subset of

and

. Let us define, for

,

. Note that if

, then

|N_{n+1}[u]\cap D|=|\{w\in D:|w\cap\tilde{u}|=0\}|\geq|N_{n}(u_{b})\cap D|\geq\begin{cases}k-1,&\text{if }u_{b}\in D\\

Therefore, if there exists

Otherwise, consider

for some

and in consequence,

. Consider

-tuple dominating set of

∎

,
\gamma_{\times k}\left(n,r\right)\geq k+r

Lemma 18

. The amount of vertices in

that contain either the element

or

On the one hand, if

\displaystyle|D\cap N\left[{u}\right]|

\displaystyle=|D|-|\{v\in D:v\neq u\wedge v\cap u\neq\emptyset\}|=

(5)

\displaystyle=|D|-\left(|\{v\in D:a\in v\vee b\in v\}|-1\right)=

\displaystyle=|D|-(i_{a}+i_{b}-1)+1=|D|+2-(i_{a}+i_{b}).

On the other hand, whether

(6)

From (
5
) and (
6
) it turns out that

-tuple dominating set if and only if for every pair of elements

\begin{cases}|D|+2-(i_{a}+i_{b})\geq k,&\text{if }\{a,b\}\in D,\\
|D|-(i_{a}+i_{b})\geq k,&\text{if }\{a,b\}\notin D.\end{cases}

Or, equivalently

i_{a}+i_{b}\leq\begin{cases}|D|-k+2,&\text{if }\{a,b\}\in D,\\
|D|-k,&\text{if }\{a,b\}\notin D.\end{cases}

Proposition 21

(7)

. The first inequality arises from the fact that
k>\frac{n-3}{2}
. In consequence
\alpha=\left\lceil\frac{2k}{n-3}\right\rceil\geq 2
. On the other hand, we have
k\leq a\,\frac{n-3}{4}
, which leads us to
\frac{2k}{n-3}\leq\frac{a}{2}
. Whether
n
is even or odd,
a
turns out to be even. Thus,
\frac{a}{2}\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha=\left\lceil\frac{2k}{n-3}\right\rceil\leq\frac{a}{2}
, or equivalently
2\alpha\leq a
.
If
n
is even,
a=n-4
, and it holds
n\geq 2\alpha+4
.
If
n
is odd, then
a=n-6+(n\mod 4)
, and it holds
n\geq 2\alpha+5
if
n\equiv 1\mod 4
, and
n\geq 2\alpha+3
if
n\equiv 3\mod 4
. Moreover, if
\alpha
is odd and
n\equiv 3\mod 4
, then
\frac{a}{2}=\frac{n-3}{2}
is even. Thus,
\alpha\leq\frac{a}{2}-1
, and we have
n\geq 2\alpha+5
.
In any case,
n\geq 2\alpha+3+(\alpha\mod 2)
as claimed.
