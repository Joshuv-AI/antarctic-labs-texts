1.
Introduction

As a classic topic, Sidon’s problems attracts much interest in combinatorial number theory, where a set
A=\{a_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
of positive integers is called
Sidon
if the differences

a_{i}-a_{j}(i\neq j)
are all distinct. For a positive real number
x
, denote
A(x)
by the number of elements
a\in A
with
a\leqslant x
. In
[
2
]
, Erdős and Graham posed an interesting problem as follows:

Erdős-Graham Problem.
Let
A=\{a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdots\}
and
B=\{b_{1}<b_{2}<\cdots\}
be sequences of integers satisfying
A(x)>\varepsilon\sqrt{x}
and
B(x)>\varepsilon\sqrt{x}
for some
\varepsilon>0
. Is it true that
a_{i}-a_{j}=b_{k}-b_{t}
has infinitely many solutions?

In 1984, Erdős and Freud
[
1
]
provided a negative answer by choosing
A
as nonnegative integers consisting only even powers of two and
B
as nonnegative integers consisting only odd powers of two, since every integer can be uniquely written as the sum of different powers of two and

\displaystyle\liminf_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\min\{A(x)B(x)\}}{\sqrt{x}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}.

In
[
1
]
, Erdős and Freud also introduced the notations
SP
and
IN
as follows:

\displaystyle SP=\limsup_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{A(x)B(x)}{x},\hskip 11.38109ptIN=\liminf_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\min\{A(x),B(x)\}}{\sqrt{x}}.

Two sets of nonnegative integers
A=\{a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdots\}
and
B=\{b_{1}<b_{2}<\cdots\}
are defined as
disjoint
, if
\{A-A\}\bigcap\{B-B\}=\{0\}
, namely, the equation
a_{i}+b_{t}=a_{j}+b_{k}
has only trivial solution. Erdős and Freud
[
1
]
proved the following nice results for disjoint sets
A
and
B
:

Theorem A.
(
[
2
, Theorem 1]
)
The largest possible value of
SP
is 2, moreover the following more precise estimations hold:

1.1. To any function
H(x)
with
\limsup_{x\rightarrow\infty}H(x)=\infty
, we can construct sets
A,B
such that

\displaystyle A(x)B(x)\geqslant 2x-H(x)

is valid for infinitely many (integer) values of
x
.

1.2. The previous result is best possible: for any
A
and
B
,
A(x)B(x)-2x\rightarrow-\infty
(
x\rightarrow\infty
).

Theorem B.
(
[
2
, Theorem 3.3]
)
If
SP=2
, then
IN=0
.

In this paper, we further consider the properties of disjoint sets and prove that:

Theorem 1.1
.

For disjoint sets
A
and
B
, assume that
\{x_{1}<x_{2}<\cdots\}
is a set of positive integers such that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{A(x_{n})B(x_{n})}{x_{n}}=2,\hskip 8.53581pt\mbox{namely,}\hskip 8.53581ptSP=2.

(i) For any
0<c_{1}<c_{2}<1,

c_{1}x_{n}\leqslant y\leqslant c_{2}x_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{A(y)B(y)}{y}\rightarrow 1\hskip 8.53581pt\mbox{as}\hskip 8.53581ptn\rightarrow\infty.

(ii) For any
1<c_{3}<c_{4}<2,

c_{3}x_{n}\leqslant y\leqslant c_{4}x_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle A(y)B(y)=(2+o(1))x_{n}\hskip 8.53581pt\mbox{as}\hskip 8.53581ptn\rightarrow\infty.

(iii)

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{A(2x_{n})B(2x_{n})}{2x_{n}}\leqslant\frac{3}{2}.

Furthermore, the constant
\frac{3}{2}
is best possible.

We characterize the set
\{x_{1}<x_{2}<\cdots\}
such that
\frac{A(x_{n})B(x_{n})}{x_{n}}\rightarrow 2
for disjoint sets
A
and
B
.

Theorem 1.2
.

(i) For disjoint sets
A
and
B

\{x_{1}<x_{2}<\cdots\}
is a set of positive integers with

for all large

(1.1)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{A(x_{n})B(x_{n})}{x_{n}}=2,

then

(1.2)

\displaystyle\frac{x_{n+1}}{x_{n}}\rightarrow\infty\hskip 11.38109pt\mbox{as}\hskip 11.38109ptn\rightarrow\infty.

be a set of positive integers satisfying (

and

such that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{A(x_{n})B(x_{n})}{x_{n}}=2.

Furthermore, we generalize the above Theorem B, that is:

Theorem 1.3

For disjoint sets
A
and
B

, then

(1.3)

\displaystyle\frac{IN^{6}}{IN^{2}+8}\leqslant 64(2-SP).

For disjoint sets

For a given positive integer
