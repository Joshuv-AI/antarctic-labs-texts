An inverse boundary value problem for the inhomogeneous porous medium equation
Cătălin I. Cârstea, Tuhin Ghosh, Gen Nakamura

Abstract
In this paper we establish uniqueness in the inverse boundary value problem for determining the two coefficients in the inhomogeneous porous medium equation ϵ ∂ t u − ∇ ⋅ ( γ ∇ u m ) = 0 \epsilon\partial_{t}u-\nabla\cdot(\gamma\nabla u^{m})=0 , with m > 1 m>1 , in dimension 3 or higher, which is a degenerate parabolic type quasilinear PDE. Our approach relies on using a Laplace transform to turn the original equation into a coupled family of nonlinear elliptic equations, indexed by the frequency parameter ( 1 / h 1/h in our definition) of the transform. A careful analysis of the asymptotic expansion in powers of h h , as h → ∞ h\to\infty , of the solutions to the transformed equation, with special boundary data, allows us to obtain sufficient information to deduce the uniqueness result.



1
Introduction

We would like to begin by recalling the classical inverse boundary value problem of Calderón, introduced in
[
5
]
. Suppose
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with
n\geq 3
, is a a bounded, smooth domain and
\gamma\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
is a bounded positive function with a positive lower bound. If we consider the (forward) boundary value problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla\cdot(\gamma(x)\nabla u(x))=0,\quad x\in\Omega\\[5.0pt]
u|_{\partial\Omega}=g\in C^{\infty}(\partial\Omega),\end{array}\right.

(1)

then we can introduce the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map (DN map)

\Lambda_{\gamma}(g)=\left.\gamma\partial_{\nu}u\right|_{\partial\Omega}=\left.\gamma\nu\cdot\nabla u\right|_{\partial\Omega},

(2)

where
u
is the solution of (
1
), and
\nu
is the outer pointing unit normal on
\partial\Omega
. Calderón’s problem refers to the question of determining
\gamma
from a knowledge of
\Lambda_{\gamma}
. The important subproblem of uniqueness is the question of showing that the correspondence
\gamma\to\Lambda_{\gamma}
is, in fact, injective. This question has been answered affirmatively in
[
39
]
. Note that, by the linearity of the DN map, if
\Lambda_{\gamma}(g)
is given for only non-negative Dirichlet data
g
, then the whole
\Lambda_{\gamma}
operator, defined on complex valued functions, can be recovered. We will use this observation later in the paper.

The subject of this paper is the following boundary value problem for the (inhomogeneous)
porous medium equation
(PME)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\epsilon(x)\partial_{t}u(t,x)-\nabla\cdot(\gamma(x)\nabla u^{m}(t,x))=0,\quad(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
u(0,x)=0,\quad u|_{[0,\infty)\times\partial\Omega}=\phi,\quad u\geq 0.\end{array}\right.

(3)

Here
m>1
, and the coefficients
\epsilon
and
\gamma
are bounded positive functions, which also have strictly positive lower bounds. For convenience we will assume
\epsilon,\gamma\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
.
Note that consistency requires that
\phi(0,x)=0
,
\phi\geq 0
.

The Dirichlet-to-Neumann map associated to (
3
) (informally) should be

\Lambda_{\epsilon,\gamma}^{PM}(\phi)=\gamma\partial_{\nu}u^{m}|_{[0,\infty)\times\partial\Omega}.

(4)

In order to rigorously define this map, we must first discuss the existence of solutions to (
3
). We will first introduce a notion of weak solutions on arbitrary, finite time intervals. Then we will see how these lead to a notion of solutions to (
3
) for infinite time.

As its name suggests, the PME can be seen as a model for the flow of a gas through a porous medium. The function
u(t,x)
being the
density
of the gas at time
t
and position
x
. If
p
is the
pressure
, then
Darcy’s law
states that the velocity of the gas is
-\frac{k}{\mu}\nabla p
, where the parameter
k
is the
permeability
of the medium and
\mu
is the
dynamic viscosity
of the gas. The pressure is related to the density via a
state equation
of the form
p=p_{0}u^{\sigma}
, where
p_{0}
is a constant
reference pressure
and
\sigma\geq 1
is the
polytropic exponent
. The value of this exponent depends on the gas but also on other factors, such as if the process being described is isothermal (then
\sigma=1
), adiabatic (then
\sigma>1
), etc. Conservation of mass requires that the following
continuity equation
holds

\epsilon\partial_{t}u+\nabla\cdot\left(\left(-\frac{k}{\mu}\right)u\nabla p\right)=0,

(5)

where
0<\epsilon<1
is the porosity of the medium (i.e. the fraction of the volume of the material that the gas can fill). Setting
\gamma=-\frac{\sigma kp_{0}}{(1+\sigma)\mu}
,
m=\sigma+1
, and noting the state equation, we see that we obtain exactly equation (
3
). The parameters
\epsilon
and
\gamma
then depend on the particular gas considered, and also on the properties of the medium.

From the purely mathematical point of view, the PME is interesting as a particularly simple nonlinear modification of the classical heat equation, which nevertheless can exhibit very different properties from its linear counterpart. It can be classified as a degenerate parabolic type quasilinear equation. Though it retains some of the features of the heat equation, such as having a maximum principle and a comparison principle, solutions to the PME are not typically real analytic in time, and the unique continuation property does not hold. To illustrate this, consider the following solutions to the PME with constant coefficients, known as Barenblatt solutions:

u(t,x)=t^{-\alpha}\left(C-k|x|^{2}t^{-2\beta}\right)_{+}^{\frac{1}{m-1}}

(6)

(
\alpha
,
\beta
,
k
depend on
m
,
n
;
C
is arbitrary.) These solutions vanish on a set with non-empty interior and the interface between the vanishing and non-vanishing regions can be said to move at a finite speed.

From the point of view of the study of inverse problems, the degenerate parabolic nature of the PME makes it a particularly interesting case to study, because known results of any kind for degenerate parabolic or elliptic PDE are still very few. We feel it is particularly interesting to see in what way the kinds of results that can be obtained may be constrained by finite speed of propagation behavior of solutions.

A good reference for the mathematical theory of the PME is the monograph
[
40
]
. Though the particular case of the inhomogeneous equation is not treated there, most of what follows regarding the existence of solutions to the boundary value problem is closely based on the treatment of the homogeneous case in
[
40
]
.

Before giving the specific definition of weak solutions which we will be working with, we must introduce a few notations. For
0<T\leq\infty
, let
Q_{T}:=(0,T)\times\Omega
and
S_{T}:=(0,T)\times\partial\Omega
. Let

C_{t}(Q_{T})=\left\{\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\overline{Q_{T}}):\mbox{supp\;}\varphi\cap\left[\{T\}\times\Omega\right]=\emptyset\right\}

(7)

and let
H^{1}_{t}(Q_{T})
be the completion of this space in
H^{1}(Q_{T})
. We will also denote by
H^{\frac{1}{2}}_{t}(S_{T})
the subspace of
H^{\frac{1}{2}}(S_{T})
that consists of traces of
H^{1}_{t}(Q_{T})
functions.
Similarly, we also introduce

C_{\diamond}(Q_{T})=\left\{\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\overline{Q_{T}}):\mbox{supp\;}\varphi\cap\left[S_{T}\cup\{T\}\times\Omega\right]=\emptyset\right\},

(8)

and its completion
H^{1}_{\diamond}(Q_{T})
in
H^{1}(Q_{T})
.

Since we will be working with weak solutions, it is useful to recall a few things about boundary traces. We will denote by
\tau_{\Omega}
the boundary trace operator for the domain
\Omega
. It is bounded between
H^{1}(\Omega)
and
H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial\Omega)
.

We will denote by
\tau_{Q_{T}}
the boundary trace operator for
Q_{T}
to the side boundary
S_{T}
. It is not hard to check that
\tau_{Q_{T}}
is bounded from
L^{2}((0,T);H^{1}(\Omega))
to
L^{2}((0,T);H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial\Omega))
.

For our convenience later in the paper, we would like to consider the equation (
3
) with an added source term

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\epsilon(x)\partial_{t}u(t,x)-\nabla\cdot(\gamma(x)\nabla u^{m}(t,x))=f(t,x),\quad(t,x)\in Q_{\infty},\\[5.0pt]
u(0,x)=0,\quad u|_{[0,\infty)\times\partial\Omega}=\phi(t,x),\quad u\geq 0.\end{array}\right.

(9)

Definition 1
.

Suppose
0<T<\infty
. We say that
u\in L^{\infty}(Q_{T})
,
u\geq 0
, is a weak solution of (
9
) in
Q_{T}
if it
satisfies the following conditions:

1.

\nabla(u^{m})
exists in the sense of distributions and
\nabla(u^{m})\in L^{2}(Q_{T})
;

2.

for any test function
\varphi\in C_{\diamond}(Q_{T})
(or, equivalently, any
\varphi\in H^{1}_{\diamond}(Q_{T})
)

\int_{Q_{T}}\gamma\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla(u^{m})\,\text{d}t\,\text{d}x-\int_{Q_{T}}\epsilon\partial_{t}\varphi\,u\,\text{d}t\,\text{d}x=\int_{Q_{T}}\varphi f\,\text{d}t\,\text{d}x;

(10)

3.

\tau_{Q_{T}}(u^{m})=\phi^{m}
.

Let the Hölder spaces
\mathcal{H}^{\frac{l}{2},l}(Q_{T})
,
l\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\setminus\mathbb{N}
be as defined in
[
28
, Chapter I, page 7]
. In section
2
we will prove the following result.

Theorem 1
.

For any
0<T<\infty
, if
\phi\geq 0
,
\phi\in C(\overline{S_{T}})\cap\mathcal{H}^{1+\frac{\beta}{2},2+\beta}(S_{T})
,
\beta\in(0,1)
,
\phi|_{t=0}=0
, and if
f\in L^{\infty}(Q_{T})
,
f\geq 0
, then there exists a unique weak solution
u
of (
9
) in
Q_{T}
. This solution satisfies the energy estimate

||u^{m}||_{L^{2}((0,T);H^{1}(\Omega))}\leq C(1+T)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(||\phi^{m}||_{W^{1,\infty}(S_{T})}+||\phi||_{L^{\infty}(S_{T})}+||f||_{L^{\infty}(Q_{T})}\right),

(11)

with a constant
C>0
that depends on
\Omega
,
m
, and the upper and lower bounds of
\epsilon
and
\gamma
.
The solution
u
satisfies the maximum principle

0\leq\sup_{Q_{T}}u\leq\sup_{S_{T}}\phi.

(12)

If
\phi_{1}\leq\phi_{2}
,
f_{1}\leq f_{2}
are as above and give rise to weak solutions
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
, then

u_{1}\leq u_{2}.

(13)

Since the length of the time interval in the above theorem is arbitrary, it follows that we also have infinite time weak solutions, in the sense specified below.

Corollary 1
.

If
\phi\geq 0
,
\phi\in C(\overline{S_{\infty}})\cap\mathcal{H}^{1+\frac{\beta}{2},2+\beta}_{loc}(S_{\infty})
,
\beta\in(0,1)
,
\phi|_{t=0}=0
, then there exists
u:Q_{\infty}\to[0,\infty)
such that, for any
0<T<\infty
,
u
is a weak solution of (
3
) in
Q_{T}
and

0\leq\sup_{Q_{T}}u\leq\sup_{S_{T}}\phi.

(14)

This allows us to rigorously define the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map for all
\phi
satisfying the conditions in Corollary
1
. If
u
is the corresponding weak solution and
\psi\in H^{1}_{t}(Q_{T})
, then we can define the Neumann data
\gamma\partial_{\nu}u^{m}|_{S_{T}}
for each
0<T\leq\infty
by

\langle\gamma\partial_{\nu}u^{m}|_{S_{T}},\psi|_{S_{T}}\rangle=\int_{Q_{T}}\left(\gamma\nabla\psi\cdot\nabla(u^{m})-\epsilon\partial_{t}\psi u\right)\,\text{d}t\,\text{d}x.

(15)

We see then that
\Lambda_{\epsilon,\gamma}^{PM}(\phi)\in\left(H^{\frac{1}{2}}_{t}(S_{T})\right)^{\prime}
.

Suppose now that we have two sets of coefficients,
\epsilon^{(i)}
,
\gamma^{(i)}
and
\epsilon^{(ii)}
,
\gamma^{(ii)}
that are as above. Let
M>0
. The main result of our paper is the following.

Theorem 2
.

If
\Lambda_{\epsilon^{(i)},\gamma^{(i)}}^{PM}(\phi)=\Lambda_{\epsilon^{(ii)},\gamma^{(ii)}}^{PM}(\phi)
, for all
0\leq\phi\leq M
,
\phi\in C(\overline{S_{\infty}})\cap\mathcal{H}^{1+\frac{\beta}{2},2+\beta}(S_{\infty})
,
\beta\in(0,1)
,
\phi|_{t=0}=0
, then
\gamma^{(i)}=\gamma^{(ii)}
and
\epsilon^{(i)}=\epsilon^{(ii)}
.

Note that if
u(t,x)
is a solution to the porous medium equation, and
\lambda>0
, then
u_{\lambda}(t,x)=\lambda^{\frac{1}{m-1}}u(\lambda t,x)
is also a solution to the same equation. It follows that it is sufficient to prove Theorem
2
for one value of
M
, such as
M=2
.

We will prove Theorem
2
in section
3
. Our method involves first going from an equation for the function
u
to an equivalent problem for the function
v=u^{m}
. We then use a Laplace transform in time to introduce functions

V(h,x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{t}{h}}v(t,x)\,\text{d}t.

(16)

These satisfy a family of equations, indexed by
h
, of the form

\nabla\cdot(\gamma(x)\nabla V(h,x))=\mathcal{N}(h,x),

(17)

with the right hand side also being constructed from the solution
u
. In principle this is an infinite, coupled system of equations. One key technical part of the argument is to show that
V(h,x)
satisfies decoupled elliptic inequalities of the form

0\leq\nabla\cdot(\gamma(x)\nabla V(h,x))\leq h^{-\frac{1}{m}}\epsilon(x)\left(V(h,x)\right)^{\frac{1}{m}}.

(18)

If we choose boundary data of the form
\phi^{m}(t,x)=\chi(t)^{m}g(x)
, we are then able to derive the first two terms in the asymptotic expansion of
V(h,x)
in powers of
h
, as
h\to\infty
. The corresponding expansion of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map leads to separate integral identities that the differences
\gamma^{(i)}-\gamma^{(ii)}
and
\epsilon^{(i)}-\epsilon^{(ii)}
must satisfy. These quickly lead to the claimed uniqueness result.

Finally, we would like to comment briefly on the related inverse problem for the

Indeed, we can give here a sketch of how the inverse boundary value problem for the fast diffusion equation may be reduced to a known result. As for the PME, one starts with the change of function
v(t,x)=u(t,x)^{m}

\epsilon\partial_{t}v^{\frac{1}{m}}-\nabla\cdot(\gamma\nabla v)=0.

(19)

, a simple computation gives that we must have
\alpha=\frac{m}{1-m}>0
and

-\nabla\cdot(\gamma\nabla w)+\frac{\epsilon}{1-m}w^{\frac{1}{m}}=0,

(20)

so the question is reduced to the invese boundary value problem for this last equation. This uniquness for the coefficients in this problem has already (almost) been established in
[

2
The forward problem

In this section we give a proof to Theorem
1
, which gives the existence of finite time weak solutions. The method used is very similar to what can be found in
[
40
]
, the difference being that in that reference the coefficients are taken to be constant.

We begin by introducing a sequence
u_{k}
,
k=1,2,\ldots
, consisting of solutions to the boundary value problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\epsilon\partial_{t}u_{k}-\nabla\cdot(\gamma\nabla u^{m}_{k})=f_{k},\\[5.0pt]
u_{k}(0,x)=\frac{1}{k},\quad u_{k}|_{[0,T)\times\partial\Omega}=\phi+\frac{1}{k},\end{array}\right.

(21)

where we choose functions
f_{k}\in C^{\infty}(Q_{T})
such that

f(t,x)\leq f_{k+1}(t,x)\leq f_{k}(t,x)\leq f(t,x)+\frac{1}{k}.

(22)

Intuitively, we expect the solutions
u_{k}
(if they exist) to satisfy the maximum principle

\frac{1}{k}\leq u_{k}(t,x)\leq\frac{1}{k}+\sup_{S_{T}}\phi+\mu t\sup_{Q_{T}}f_{k},

(23)

where
\mu=(\inf_{\Omega}\epsilon)^{-1}
.
With this in mind, we redefine the
u_{k}
to be the solutions of

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\epsilon\partial_{t}u_{k}-\nabla\cdot\left(a_{k}(x,u_{k})\nabla u_{k})\right)=f_{k},\\[5.0pt]
u_{k}(0,x)=\frac{1}{k},\quad u_{k}|_{[0,T)\times\partial\Omega}=\phi+\frac{1}{k},\end{array}\right.

(24)

where the function
a_{k}(x,\lambda)
is chosen so as to make (
24
) a uniform quasilinear parabolic problem (i.e. it avoids the degeneracy at
\lambda=0,\infty
), but such that if

\frac{1}{k}\leq\lambda\leq\sup_{S_{T}}\phi+\mu T\sup_{Q_{T}}f+\frac{1+\mu T}{k},

(25)

we have

a_{k}(x,\lambda)=m\gamma(x)\lambda^{m-1}.

(26)

Outside this domain
a_{k}
is extended smoothly in such a way that (
24
) is non-degenerate.
In this case (see
[
28
, Chapter V, page 452, Theorem 6.1]
), the problem (
24
) has a solution
u_{k}\in C^{1,2}(Q_{T})\cap C(\overline{Q_{T}})
which furthermore satisfies (see
[
31
, Theorem 9.7]
) the maximum principle (
23
). Since (
23
) is satisfied, it follows that
u_{k}
is in fact also a solution to (
21
).

Note that
u_{k}
is also a solution to
\epsilon\partial_{t}u_{k}-\nabla\cdot\left(a_{k+1}(x,u_{k},\nabla u_{k})\right)=f_{k}
. Therefore, by the comparison principle (see
[
31
, Theorem 9.7]
), we have that

0\leq u_{k+1}\leq u_{k},\quad\forall k=1,2\ldots.

(27)

We can then define the pointwise limit

u(t,x)=\lim_{k\to\infty}u_{k}(t,x).

(28)

This function will turn out to be the weak solution to (
3
) that we are searching for.

Since clearly
u\leq u_{k}
for all
k
, by (
23
) we have that

0\leq u(t,x)\leq\sup_{S_{T}}\phi+\mu t\sup_{Q_{T}}f.

(29)

By the monotone convergence theorem we have that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{Q_{T}}(u_{k}-u)^{2}\,\text{d}t\,\text{d}x=0,

(30)

i.e. that
u_{k}\to u
in
L^{2}(Q_{T})
. Similarly, it also holds that
u_{k}^{m}\to u^{m}
in
L^{2}(Q_{T})
.

We will denote by
\tilde{\phi}:Q_{T}\to[0,\infty)
a smooth extension of the Dirichlet data, such that we still have
\tilde{\phi}(0,x)=0
for all
x\in\Omega
. We can choose this extension in such a way that

||\tilde{\phi}||_{W^{1,\infty}(Q_{T})}\leq C||\phi||_{W^{1,\infty}(S_{T})},

(31)

with a constant
C>0
.

Let

\eta_{k}=u_{k}^{m}-\left(\tilde{\phi}+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{m},

(32)

which is zero on
S_{T}
. We can multiply (
21
) by
\eta_{k}
and integrate over
Q_{T}
. Note that

-\int_{Q_{T}}\eta_{k}\nabla(\gamma\nabla u_{k}^{m})\,\text{d}t\,\text{d}x\\[5.0pt]
=\int_{Q_{T}}\gamma|\nabla u_{k}^{m}|^{2}\,\text{d}t\,\text{d}x-\int_{Q_{T}}\gamma\nabla\left(\tilde{\phi}+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{m}\cdot\nabla u_{k}^{m}\,\text{d}t\,\text{d}x,

(33)

and that

\int_{Q_{T}}\epsilon\partial_{t}u_{k}\eta_{k}\,\text{d}t\,\text{d}x=\int_{Q_{T}}\epsilon\partial_{t}u_{k}u_{k}^{m}\,\text{d}t\,\text{d}x-\int_{Q_{T}}\epsilon\partial_{t}u_{k}\left(\tilde{\phi}+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{m}\,\text{d}t\,\text{d}x\\[5.0pt]
=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\epsilon}{m+1}u_{k}^{m+1}(T)\,\text{d}x-\int_{\Omega}\epsilon u_{k}(T)\left(\tilde{\phi}(T)+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{m}\,\text{d}x\\[5.0pt]
+\int_{Q_{T}}\epsilon u_{k}\partial_{t}\left(\tilde{\phi}+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{m}\,\text{d}t\,\text{d}x.

(34)

Putting these two together we have

\int_{Q_{T}}\gamma|\nabla u_{k}^{m}|^{2}\,\text{d}t\,\text{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\epsilon}{m+1}u_{k}^{m+1}(T)\,\text{d}x\\[5.0pt]
=\int_{Q_{T}}\gamma\nabla\left(\tilde{\phi}+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{m}\cdot\nabla u_{k}^{m}\,\text{d}t\,\text{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\epsilon u_{k}(T)\left(\tilde{\phi}(T)+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{m}\,\text{d}x\\[5.0pt]
-\int_{Q_{T}}\epsilon u_{k}\partial_{t}\left(\tilde{\phi}+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{m}\,\text{d}t\,\text{d}x+\int_{Q_{T}}f_{k}\left[u_{k}^{m}-\left(\tilde{\phi}+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{m}\right].

(35)

By straightforward estimates we can conclude that there exists a constant
C>0
, depending on
m
and the upper and lower bounds of
\epsilon
and
\gamma
, such that

\int_{Q_{T}}|\nabla u_{k}^{m}|^{2}\,\text{d}t\,\text{d}x+\int_{\Omega}u_{k}^{m+1}(T)\,\text{d}x\leq C\Bigg[||u_{k}||_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}^{2}+||u_{k}^{m}||_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}^{2}+||f_{k}||_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}^{2}\\[5.0pt]
+\left\|\left(\tilde{\phi}+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{m}\right\|_{H^{1}(Q_{T})}^{2}+\left\|\left(\tilde{\phi}+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{m}\right\|_{L^{\frac{m+1}{m}}(\Omega)}\Bigg].

(36)

There is then a constant
C^{\prime}>0
, independent of
k
and
T
, such that

\int_{Q_{T}}|\nabla u_{k}^{m}|^{2}\,\text{d}t\,\text{d}x<C^{\prime}(1+T)\Bigg(\left\|\left(\phi+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{m}\right\|_{C^{0,1}(S_{T})}^{2}+\left\|\left(\phi+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{m}\right\|_{C(S_{T})}\\[5.0pt]
+\left\|\phi+\frac{1}{k}\right\|_{C(S_{T})}^{2}+\left\|f+\frac{1}{k}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{T})}^{2}\Bigg).

(37)

It follows that (a subsequence of)
\nabla u_{k}^{m}
converges weakly in
L^{2}(Q_{T})
to a limit
U
. It is easy to see that
U=\nabla u^{m}
in the sense of distributions.

Since we have that
\tau_{Q_{T}}(u_{k}^{m})(t)\to\phi^{m}
in
L^{2}((0,T);H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial\Omega))
by construction, and also that
\tau_{Q_{T}}(u_{k}^{m})\rightharpoonup\tau_{Q_{T}}(u^{m})
, it follows that

\tau_{Q_{T}}(u^{m})=\phi^{m}.

(38)

This shows that
u
is a weak solution of (
3
) in
Q_{T}
.

In order to prove uniqueness of weak solutions, we start by assuming
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
are both weak solutions of (
3
) in
Q_{T}
. We have that for all test functions
\varphi

\int_{Q_{T}}\gamma(\nabla u_{1}^{m}-\nabla u_{2}^{m})\nabla\varphi\,\text{d}t\,\text{d}x-\int_{Q_{T}}\epsilon(u_{1}-u_{2})\partial_{t}\varphi\,\text{d}t\,\text{d}x=0.

(39)

Let

\varphi(t,x)=\int_{t}^{T}(u_{1}^{m}(s,x)-u_{2}^{m}(s,x))\,\text{d}s.

(40)

It is not hard to check that
\varphi\in H^{1}_{\diamond}(Q_{T})
, so we can use it in the above identity. We obtain

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\gamma\left(\int_{0}^{T}(\nabla u_{1}^{m}-\nabla u_{2}^{m})\,\text{d}t\right)^{2}\,\text{d}x+\int_{Q_{T}}\epsilon(u_{1}-u_{2})(u_{1}^{m}-u_{2}^{m})\,\text{d}t\,\text{d}x=0.

(41)

The first term is clearly positive and, by the monotonicity of the
z\to z^{m}
function in
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, so is the second. It follows that
u_{1}=u_{2}
.

Regarding the energy inequality claimed in the statement of the Theorem, it is enough to note that

||\nabla u^{m}||_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}||\nabla u_{k}^{m}||_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}\\[5.0pt]
\leq C(1+T)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(||\phi^{m}||_{W^{1,\infty}(S_{T})}+||\phi||_{L^{\infty}(S_{T})}+||f||_{L^{\infty}(Q_{T})}\right).

(42)

Finally, suppose we have boundary data and sources
\phi_{1}\leq\phi_{2}
,
f_{1}\leq f_{2}
, with corresponding solutions
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
. It is enough to observe that the approximating sequences defined above must satisfy

u_{1,k}\leq u_{2,k},

(43)

and this property is preserved in the limit.
This concludes the proof.

3
The inverse problem

In this section we give a proof of Theorem
2
. We do this by transforming the equation so that the principal term becomes linear. Then we use a Laplace transform in time to transform the equation into a (coupled) family of nonlinear elliptic equations. Below, when we write
\epsilon
and
\gamma
, we mean any one of the pairs
\epsilon^{(i)}
,
\gamma^{(i)}
or
\epsilon^{(ii)}
,
\gamma^{(ii)}
.

3.1
The first reformulation of the problem

We first perform the change of function
v=u^{m}
. The new function satisfies the equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\epsilon(x)\partial_{t}v(t,x)^{\frac{1}{m}}-\nabla\cdot(\gamma(x)\nabla v(t,x))=0,\\[5.0pt]
v(0,x)=0,\quad v|_{[0,\infty)\times\partial\Omega}=f(t,x),\quad v\geq 0,\end{array}\right.

(44)

where
f=\phi^{m}
. It is clear that the notions of weak solutions we have introduced for equation (
3
) give corresponding notions of weak solutions to (
44
), whose existence is then guaranteed by Theorem
1
. The associated Dirichlet-to-Neumann map is

\Lambda^{v}_{\epsilon,\gamma}(f)=\gamma\partial_{\nu}v|_{[0,\infty)\times\partial\Omega},

(45)

and it contains the same information as
\Lambda_{\epsilon,\gamma}^{PM}
, when considering
f\in C(\overline{S_{\infty}})\cap C^{\infty}(S_{\infty})
,
f\geq 0
, and such that
f(0,x)=0
for all
x\in\Omega
.

3.2
Reformulation of the problem using the Laplace transform

The next reformulation of the problem uses the Laplace transform to turn our equation into an elliptic one. From now on we will only consider boundary data of a particular form, namely
f(t,x)=\chi(t)^{m}g(x)
, with
g\in C(\partial\Omega)
, and
\chi\in C^{\infty}([0,\infty))
an increasing function such that
\chi(t)=0
if
t\leq 1/2
and
\chi(t)=1
if
t\geq 1
. For
h>0
define

V(h,x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{t}{h}}v(t,x)\,\text{d}t.

(46)

Note that since (in the sense of distributions) we have

\nabla V(h,x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{t}{h}}\nabla v(t,x)\,\text{d}t,

(47)

it then follows that

||\nabla V(h,\cdot)||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{t}{h}}||\nabla v(t,\cdot)||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,\text{d}t\\[5.0pt]
\leq\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{t}{h}}\,\text{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\;\int_{Q_{\infty}}e^{-\frac{t}{h}}|\nabla v(t,x)|^{2}\,\text{d}t\,\text{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}<\infty,

(48)

where the last inequality holds since, by Theorem
1
,

\int_{Q_{T}}|\nabla v(t,x)|^{2}\,\text{d}t\,\text{d}x

(49)

has an at most polynomial growth as
T\to\infty
, so the integral over
Q_{\infty}
converges.
Therefore we have that
V(h,\cdot)\in H^{1}(\Omega)
.

If
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, and
\zeta\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
is such that
\zeta(t)=1
when
|t|\leq 1
and
\zeta(t)=0
when
|t|\geq 2
, then

\left\langle\nabla(\gamma\nabla V(h,\cdot)),\varphi\right\rangle=-\int_{\Omega}\gamma(x)\nabla V(h,x)\cdot\nabla\varphi(x)\,\text{d}x\\[5.0pt]
=-\lim_{T\to\infty}\int_{Q_{\infty}}\zeta(\frac{t}{T})e^{-\frac{t}{h}}\gamma(x)\nabla v(t,x)\cdot\nabla\varphi(x)\,\text{d}t\,\text{d}x\\[5.0pt]
=-\lim_{T\to\infty}\int_{Q_{\infty}}\epsilon(x)\left[\frac{1}{T}\zeta^{\prime}(\frac{t}{T})e^{-\frac{t}{h}}-\frac{1}{h}\zeta(\frac{t}{T})e^{-\frac{t}{h}}\right]v^{\frac{1}{m}}(t,x)\varphi(x)\,\text{d}t\,\text{d}x\\[5.0pt]
=\int_{\Omega}\varphi(x)\left[\frac{1}{h}\epsilon(x)\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{t}{h}}v^{\frac{1}{m}}(t,x)\,\text{d}t\right]\,\text{d}x.

(50)

It follows that

\nabla\cdot(\gamma(x)\nabla V(h,x))=\mathcal{N}(h,x),

(51)

where

\mathcal{N}(h,x)=\frac{1}{h}\epsilon(x)\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{t}{h}}v^{\frac{1}{m}}(t,x)\,\text{d}t.

(52)

The value
V(h,x)
takes on the boundary (in the sense of traces) is

X(h)g(x)=\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{t}{h}}\chi(t)^{m}\,\text{d}t\right)g(x).

(53)

Here we note that

he^{-\frac{1}{h}}=\int_{1}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{t}{h}}\,\text{d}t\leq X(h)\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{t}{h}}\,\text{d}t=h,

(54)

so
X(h)=\mathscr{O}(h)
,
X(h)^{-1}=\mathscr{O}(h^{-1})
as
h\to\infty
.

We then conclude that
V
satisfies the family of boundary value problems

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla\cdot(\gamma(x)\nabla V(h,x))=\mathcal{N}(h,x),\\[5.0pt]
V|_{\partial\Omega}=X(h)g,\quad V\geq 0.\end{array}\right.

(55)

We define the associated DN map as essentially (up to a multiplicative correction) the Laplace transform of
\Lambda^{v}_{\epsilon,\gamma}
, namely

\Lambda^{h}_{\epsilon,\gamma}(g)=X(h)^{-1}\gamma\partial_{\nu}V(h,\cdot)|_{\partial\Omega}.

(56)

We have added the
X(h)^{-1}
factor for the sake of convenience in the computations that follow below.

Let
m^{\prime}
be such that
m^{\prime-1}+m^{-1}=1
. Then by Hölder’s inequality

0\leq\mathcal{N}(h,x)=\frac{1}{h}\epsilon(x)\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{t}{m^{\prime}h}}\left(e^{-\frac{t}{h}}v(t,x)\right)^{\frac{1}{m}}\,\text{d}t\\[5.0pt]
\leq h^{-\frac{1}{m}}\epsilon(x)\left(V(h,x)\right)^{\frac{1}{m}},

(57)

so

||\mathcal{N}(h,\cdot)||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq h^{-\frac{1}{m}}||\epsilon||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}||V(h,\cdot)||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{\frac{1}{m}}\\[5.0pt]
\leq Ch^{-\frac{1}{m}}||V(h,\cdot)||_{W^{1,p}(\Omega)}^{\frac{1}{m}},

(58)

for a
p>n
.

By eliptic regularity estimates for (
55
) we have

||V(h,\cdot)||_{W^{2,p}(\Omega)}\leq C\left(h||g||_{W^{2-\frac{1}{p},p}(\partial\Omega)}+||\mathcal{N}(h,\cdot)||_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\right)\\[5.0pt]
\leq C\left(h||g||_{W^{2-\frac{1}{p},p}(\partial\Omega)}+h^{-\frac{1}{m}}||V(h,\cdot)||_{W^{1,p}(\Omega)}^{\frac{1}{m}}\right).

(59)

Then

\max\left(||V(h,\cdot)||_{W^{2,p}(\Omega)},1\right)\\[5.0pt]
\leq C\left(h||g||_{W^{2-\frac{1}{p},p}(\partial\Omega)}+1+h^{-\frac{1}{m}}\left[\max\left(||V(h,\cdot)||_{W^{1,p}(\Omega)},1\right)\right]^{\frac{1}{m}}\right)\\[5.0pt]
\leq C\left(h||g||_{W^{2-\frac{1}{p},p}(\partial\Omega)}+1+h^{-\frac{1}{m}}\max\left(||V(h,\cdot)||_{W^{1,p}(\Omega)},1\right)\right).

(60)

There clearly exists an
h_{0}>0
such that if
h>h_{0}
we have

||V(h,\cdot)||_{W^{2,p}(\Omega)}\leq hC\left(||g||_{W^{2-\frac{1}{p},p}(\partial\Omega)}+h^{-1}\right).

(61)

3.3
First order asymptotic expansions and the determination of
\gamma

We make the following Ansatz

V(h,x)=X(h)V_{0}(x)+R_{1}(h,x),

(62)

where
V_{0}\in W^{2,p}(\Omega)
solves the equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla\cdot(\gamma(x)\nabla V_{0}(x))=0,\\[5.0pt]
V_{0}|_{\partial\Omega}=g,\quad g\geq 0.\end{array}\right.

(63)

It follows that
R_{1}
must satisfy

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla\cdot(\gamma(x)\nabla R_{1}(h,x))=\mathcal{N}(h,x),\\[5.0pt]
R_{1}|_{\partial\Omega}=0.\end{array}\right.

(64)

We observe that
R_{1}
is a subsolution for the elliptic operator
\nabla\cdot\gamma\nabla
, so we must have that
R_{1}\leq 0
.

By elliptic estimates we have that
R_{1}
is bounded uniformly in
h>h_{0}

||R_{1}(h,\cdot)||_{W^{2,p}(\Omega)}\leq C||\mathcal{N}(h,\cdot)||_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\\[5.0pt]
\leq Ch^{-\frac{1}{m}}||V(h,\cdot)||_{W^{1,p}(\Omega)}^{\frac{1}{m}}\leq C\left(||g||_{W^{2-\frac{1}{p},p}(\partial\Omega)}^{\frac{1}{m}}+h^{-\frac{1}{m}}\right).

(65)

The asymptotic behavior of
\Lambda^{h}_{\epsilon,\gamma}(g)
as
h\to\infty
is then

\Lambda^{h}_{\epsilon,\gamma}(g)=\gamma\partial_{\nu}V_{0}(\cdot)|_{\partial\Omega}+\mathscr{O}(h^{-1}).

(66)

Now, since we must have
\Lambda^{h}_{\epsilon^{(i)},\gamma^{(ii)}}(g)=\Lambda^{h}_{\epsilon^{(i)},\gamma^{(ii)}}(g)
for all
g\in C(\overline{\Omega})\cap C^{\infty}(\Omega)
, with
g\geq 0
, it follows that corresponding orders in
h
of their asymptotic expansions must also be equal. From the
h^{0}
order term we then get that the DN maps for the original Calderón problem, with conductivities
\gamma^{(i)}
and
\gamma^{(ii)}
, are equal. That is, we have
\Lambda_{\gamma^{(i)}}=\Lambda_{\gamma^{(ii)}}
. It follows then form
[
39
]
that
\gamma^{(i)}=\gamma^{(ii)}
.

3.4
Asymptotic expansions up to the second order and the determination of
\epsilon

In order to also prove the equality of the
\epsilon^{(i)}
,
\epsilon^{(ii)}
coefficients, we need to look deeper into the asymptotic expansion of the DN map. Noting that

X(h)V_{0}(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{t}{h}}\chi(t)^{m}V_{0}(x)\,\text{d}t,

(67)

and also that
V_{0}\geq 0
, let

\mathcal{N}_{0}(h,x)=\frac{1}{h}\epsilon(x)\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{t}{h}}\chi(t)V_{0}^{\frac{1}{m}}(x)\,\text{d}t=\epsilon(x)\hat{\chi}(h)V_{0}^{\frac{1}{m}}(x),

(68)

where

\hat{\chi}(h)=\frac{1}{h}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{t}{h}}\chi(t)\,\text{d}t=\mathscr{O}(1).

(69)

We refine the Ansatz (
62
) to

V(h,x)=X(h)V_{0}(x)+V_{1}^{h}(x)+R_{2}(h,x),

(70)

where
V_{1}^{h}\in W^{2,p}(\Omega)
is the solution to

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla\cdot(\gamma(x)\nabla V_{1}^{h}(x))=\mathcal{N}_{0}(h,x),\\[5.0pt]
V_{1}^{h}|_{\partial\Omega}=0.\end{array}\right.

(71)

As
V_{1}^{h}
is a subsolution for the elliptic operator
\nabla\cdot\gamma\nabla
, it follows that
V_{1}\leq 0
.

Returning to (
70
), we see that
R_{2}
must satisfy the equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla\cdot(\gamma(x)\nabla R_{2}(h,x))=\left(\mathcal{N}(h,x)-\mathcal{N}_{0}(h,x)\right),\\[5.0pt]
R_{2}|_{\partial\Omega}=0.\end{array}\right.

(72)

Let

w_{0}(t,x)=\chi(t)^{m}V_{0}(x)

(73)

and note that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\epsilon\partial_{t}w_{0}^{\frac{1}{m}}-\nabla\cdot(\gamma\nabla w_{0})=\epsilon\chi^{\prime}(t)V_{0}^{\frac{1}{m}}\geq 0,\\[5.0pt]
w_{0}|_{t=0}=0,w_{0}|_{(0,\infty)\times\partial\Omega}=v|_{(0,\infty)\times\partial\Omega}.\end{array}\right.

(74)

By the last part of Theorem
1
, it follows that
w_{0}\geq v
. This implies that

\mathcal{N}(h,x)\leq\mathcal{N}_{0}(h,x).

(75)

It follows that
R_{2}
is a supersolution for the elliptic operator
\nabla\cdot\gamma\nabla
, so we have that
R_{2}\geq 0
.

We would like to also obtain an estimate for the difference
{\mathcal{N}}_{0}-{\mathcal{N}}
. To this end, using the same Hölder inequality trick employed above in equation (
57
), we estimate

0\leq{\mathcal{N}}_{0}(h,x)-{\mathcal{N}}(h,x)=\frac{\epsilon(x)}{h}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{t}{h}}(w_{0}^{\frac{1}{m}}(t,x)-v^{\frac{1}{m}}(t,x))\,\text{d}t\\[5.0pt]
\leq\frac{\epsilon(x)}{h}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{t}{h}}(w_{0}(t,x)-v(t,x))^{\frac{1}{m}}\,\text{d}t\\[5.0pt]
\leq\epsilon(x)h^{-\frac{1}{m}}\left(X(h)V_{0}(x)-V(h,x)\right)^{\frac{1}{m}}\\[5.0pt]
=\epsilon(x)h^{-\frac{1}{m}}\left(-R_{1}(h,x)\right)^{\frac{1}{m}}.

(76)

It follows from (
65
) that

||{\mathcal{N}}-{\mathcal{N}}_{0}||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}=\mathscr{O}(h^{-\frac{1}{m}}),

(77)

as
h\to\infty
.
By elliptic estimates

||R_{2}(h,\cdot)||_{W^{2,p}(\Omega)}=\mathscr{O}(h^{-\frac{1}{m}}).

(78)

This gives the following asymptotic expansion for
\Lambda^{h}_{\epsilon,\gamma}
as
h\to\infty

\Lambda^{h}_{\epsilon,\gamma}(g)=\gamma\partial_{\nu}V_{0}(\cdot)|_{\partial\Omega}+h^{-1}\gamma\partial_{\nu}V_{1}(\cdot)|_{\partial\Omega}+\mathscr{O}(h^{-1-\frac{1}{m}}).

(79)

As before, we will use the
h^{-1}
order in the expansion in order to obtain the uniqueness result for
\epsilon
.

Let
W
be any smooth solution to
\nabla\cdot(\gamma\nabla W)=0
. We have that

\langle\gamma\partial_{\nu}V_{1}|_{\partial\Omega},W|_{\partial\Omega}\rangle=\int_{\Omega}\gamma\nabla V_{1}\cdot\nabla W\,\text{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\epsilon V_{0}^{\frac{1}{m}}W\,\text{d}x\\[5.0pt]
=\int_{\Omega}\epsilon V_{0}^{\frac{1}{m}}W\,\text{d}x.

(80)

If
\Lambda^{h}_{\epsilon,\gamma}
is known, then so are all the integral quantities of the form that appears on the right hand side of the above equation.

We can take
V_{0}
to be of the form

V_{0}(x)=1+sH(x),\quad\nabla\cdot(\gamma\nabla H)=0,\;H>0.

(81)

Then

m\left.\frac{\,\text{d}}{\,\text{d}s}\right|_{s=0}\int_{\Omega}\epsilon\left(1+sH(x)\right)^{\frac{1}{m}}W\,\text{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\epsilon HW\,\text{d}x

(82)

is determined by
\Lambda^{h}_{\epsilon,\gamma}
. From here it is easy to see that we must have

\int_{\Omega}\left(\epsilon^{(i)}-\epsilon^{(ii)}\right)UW\,\text{d}x=0

(83)

for all
U,W
such that
\nabla\cdot(\gamma\nabla U)=\nabla\cdot(\gamma\nabla W)=0
. As shown in
[
39
]
, the span of such products
UV
is dense in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, so this implies that
\epsilon^{(i)}=\epsilon^{(ii)}
.
