Multiple and weak Markov properties in Hilbert spaces with applications to fractional stochastic evolution equations
Kristin Kirchner and Joshua Willems

Abstract
. We furthermore investigate the relations between them. In particular, for solutions to the stochastic evolution equation ℒ ​ X = W ˙ Q \mathcal{L}X=\dot{W}^{Q}\! , where ℒ \mathcal{L} is a linear operator acting on functions mapping from 𝕋 \mathbb{T} to U U and ( W ˙ Q ​ ( t ) ) t ∈ 𝕋 (\dot{W}^{Q}(t))_{t\in\mathbb{T}} is the formal derivative of a U U -valued (cylindrical) Q Q -Wiener process, we prove necessary and sufficient conditions for the weakest Markov property via locality of the precision operator ℒ ∗ ​ ℒ \mathcal{L}^{*}\!\mathcal{L} . 1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background and motivation

Gaussian Markov random fields play an important role
for various applications, such as
the analysis of time series or longitudinal data, image processing and spatial statistics,
see e.g.
[
42
, Section 1.3]
.
The latter
focuses on the statistical modeling
of spatial or spatiotemporal
dependence in data
collected from phenomena encountered in disciplines
such as
climatology
[
1
]
,
epidemiology
[
26
]
and
neuroimaging
[
34
]
.
The popularity of Gaussian Markov random fields among
the larger class of Gaussian random fields is a consequence
of their additional conditional independence properties,
which entail a sparse precision structure and
facilitate efficient computational methods for statistical inference.
In particular, hierarchical models based on Gaussian Markov random fields allow for efficient
Bayesian inference using Markov chain Monte Carlo methods, see e.g.
[
42
, Section 4.1]
.

Since a Gaussian process is fully characterized
by its second-order structure, i.e.,
the mean and covariance function,
a natural way to specify its distribution is
to choose a suitable second-order structure.
Alternatively, the dynamics of Gaussian random fields defined on
a Euclidean domain
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be specified
by means of stochastic partial differential equations
(SPDEs), such as the white noise
(\mathcal{W}(x))_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
driven equation

(1.1)

LX(x)=\mathcal{W}(x),\quad x\in\mathcal{D}.

Here,
L
is
a linear operator
acting on real-valued functions defined on
\mathcal{D}
.
A spatial
Gaussian random field
(X(x))_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
is said to have the Markov property
if the subcollections
(X(x))_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{1}}

and
(X(x))_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{2}}

corresponding to
pairs of disjoint subdomains
\mathcal{D}_{1},\mathcal{D}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{D}

are independent
conditional on
(X(x))_{x\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}
for
some non-trivial ‘splitting’ set
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{D}
separating the two.
The precise specification of these sets,
which respectively carry
the intuitive interpretations
of
past
,
future
and
present
, leads to
various definitions of the Markov property.
According to the theory of Rozanov
[
41
]
,
a real-valued Gaussian random field
satisfying (
1.1
)
has such a Markov property
if and only if its
precision operator
L^{*}\!L
is local, where
L^{*}
denotes the adjoint of
L
.

An important example
in spatial statistics is the choice of a
fractional-order differential operator

L:=\tau(\kappa^{2}-\Delta)^{\beta}
in (
1.1
),
where
\Delta
is the Laplacian,
\mathcal{W}
is Gaussian white noise
and
\tau,\kappa,\beta\in(0,\infty)
.
Whittle
[
47
]
observed that the covariance function of the
stationary solution
(X(x))_{x\in\mathcal{D}}
to (
1.1
)
with
\mathcal{D}=\mathbb{R}^{d}

then belongs to the widely used

Matérn
covariance class

[
33
]
.
This observation motivated the
SPDE approach

for spatial statistical modeling proposed by
Lindgren, Rue and Lindström
[
29
]
. Here, one considers (
1.1
)
on a bounded Euclidean
domain
\mathcal{D}\subsetneq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, augmented with boundary conditions,
and approximates the resulting
Whittle–Matérn fields
by means of
efficient numerical methods available for (S)PDEs.
Owing to its ease of generalization and
its computational efficiency as
compared to covariance-based techniques,
this approach
has gained widespread popularity,
see e.g.
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
17
,
28
,
43
]
.
Since in this case
the precision operator is given by

L^{*}\!L=\tau^{2}(\kappa^{2}-\Delta)^{2\beta}\!
,
we find that Whittle–Matérn fields are Gaussian
Markov
random fields
in the sense of Rozanov
[
41
]

precisely when
2\beta\in\mathbb{N}
.

Recently, extensions of the SPDE approach
incorporating time dependence
have been discussed.
A class of space–time equations
which has been proposed in this context is

(1.2)

(\partial_{t}+L)^{\gamma}X(t,x)=\dot{\mathcal{W}}^{Q}(t,x),\quad(t,x)\in\mathbb{T}\times\mathcal{D},

where
\mathbb{T}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
represents a time interval and

\dot{\mathcal{W}}^{Q}
is spatiotemporal Gaussian noise,
which is
spatially colored by an operator
Q
, see
[
24
,
27
]
.
In particular,
it has been shown in
[
24
]
that
equation (
1.2
) extends the Matérn model in terms of spatial marginal covariance,
and that the interplay of its parameters governs
smoothness in space and time as well as
the degree of separability.

Spatiotemporal random fields
can be viewed as

U
-valued stochastic processes by
letting a Hilbert space
U
encode the spatial variable,
so that (
1.2
) corresponds to
a stochastic fractional evolution equation
of the form

(1.3)

(\partial_{t}+A)^{\gamma}X(t)=\dot{W}^{Q}(t),\quad t\in\mathbb{T}.

The (temporal) Markov property of solutions to (
1.2
) is then
equivalent to that of the
U
-valued solution process
(X(t))_{t\in\mathbb{T}}
,
where the Markov behavior is considered
with respect to the index set
\mathbb{T}
.
Moreover, viewing (
1.3
) as a special case
of

(1.4)

\mathcal{L}X(t)=\dot{W}^{Q}(t),\quad t\in\mathbb{T},

where
\mathcal{L}
is now a linear operator acting on functions from
\mathbb{T}
to
U
,
the theory of Rozanov
[
41
]
suggests that
locality of the precision operator
\mathcal{L}^{*}\!\mathcal{L}
,
also acting on functions
f\colon\mathbb{T}\to U
, can be used to
characterize
temporal Markov behavior of the solution
X
.

1.2.
Contributions

In this work we define

simple
,
multiple
(
N
-ple
for
N\in\mathbb{N}
) and
weak
Markov properties
for stochastic processes which take values in a Hilbert space
U
.
These definitions generalize
those appearing for instance in
[
18
,
40
,
41
]

for real-valued processes to infinite dimensions,
see Definitions
3.1
,
3.3
and
3.5
,
respectively.
Besides gathering them in once place, we establish their interrelations,
see Proposition
3.6
and Remark
3.7
.
The main results are Theorems
3.8
and
3.10
,
which give necessary and sufficient conditions,
in terms of the precision operator
\mathcal{L}^{*}\!\mathcal{L}
, for the weakest notion of Markovianity for a
U
-valued
Gaussian process defined via (
1.4
).
These results are proven by a non-trivial extension of the theory by Rozanov
[
41
, Chapters 2 and 3]

from the real-valued to the
U
-valued setting.

In order to consider the more concrete class of processes defined via (
1.3
),
we construct
a stochastic integral for deterministic operator-valued integrands defined on the
whole of
\mathbb{R}
with respect to a two-sided (cylindrical)

Q
-Wiener process
(W^{Q}(t))_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
, see Subsection
2.2
.
We employ this stochastic integral to
define the mild solution process
Z_{\gamma}=(Z_{\gamma}(t))_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
to (
1.3
)
on
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}
, see Definition
4.5
.
Our rigorous definition of the
fractional space–time operator
(\partial_{t}+A)^{\gamma}
for
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
,
see Definition
4.3
,
extends the Weyl fractional calculus in the sense that one recovers the Weyl fractional derivatives and integrals
defined in
[
22
, Section 2.3]
upon specializing to
U=\mathbb{R}
and
A=0
.

We show that the mild solution
Z_{\gamma}
to (
1.3
)
satisfies the
N
-ple Markov property if
\gamma=N\in\mathbb{N}
, see Theorem
4.9
.
Conversely, we use Theorem
3.8
to show that

Z_{\gamma}
is in general not weakly Markov for
\gamma\not\in\mathbb{N}
.
This complements
[
14
, Theorem 2.7]
, where the authors show that any
time-homogeneous
U
-valued Gaussian simple Markov process is the solution to a first-order stochastic evolution equation.

Finally, we discuss another interesting aspect of
the SPDE (
1.3
): A fractional
Q
-Wiener process
(W_{H}^{Q}(t))_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
with Hurst parameter
H\in(0,1)
,
as defined for instance in
[
12
]
, can be obtained
as a limiting case of (
1.3
)
with
\gamma=H+\frac{1}{2}
and
A=\varepsilon\operatorname{Id}_{U}
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, see Proposition
5.3
.
The proof is based on a Mandelbrot–Van Ness
[
31
]
type
integral representation of
W^{Q}_{H}
, again using the two-sided stochastic integral from Subsection
2.2
,
see Proposition
5.2
.
The case
H=\frac{1}{2}
corresponds to a (non-fractional)

Q
-Wiener process and is thus Markov. Conversely, although
the results of Theorems
3.8
and
3.10
do not
apply directly, the above observation
provides evidence that
W^{Q}_{H}
does not satisfy a weak Markov property
for
H\in(0,1)\setminus\{\frac{1}{2}\}
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Notation

The sets
\mathbb{N}:=\{1,2,3,\dots\}
and
\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}

denote the positive and non-negative integers,
respectively.
We write
s\wedge t
(or
s\vee t
) for the
minimum (or maximum)
of two real numbers
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
.
Empty sums and products are assigned the values zero and one, respectively.
Given two arbitrary sets
D
and
E
,
the preimage of
E_{0}\subseteq E
under the function
f\colon D\to E
is defined by

{f^{-1}[E_{0}]=\{f\in E_{0}\}=\{x\in D:f(x)\in E_{0}\}}
.
The identity on
D
and the indicator function of
D_{0}\subseteq D

are respectively denoted by
\operatorname{Id}_{D}\colon D\to D
and

\mathbf{1}_{D_{0}}\colon D\to\{0,1\}
.

Let

(U,\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle_{U})
and

(\widetilde{U},\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle_{\widetilde{U}})
denote Hilbert spaces
with induced norms
{\|\cdot\|_{U}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\widetilde{U}}
.
Banach spaces are usually denoted by

(E,\|\cdot\|_{E})
and
(F,\|\cdot\|_{F})
. These
spaces are all assumed to be real and separable.

We write
{\mathscr{L}(E;F)}

for the Banach space of bounded linear operators equipped with the usual operator norm.
The adjoint operator of
T\in\mathscr{L}(E;F)

is denoted by
{T^{*}\in\mathscr{L}(F^{*};E^{*})}
, where
E^{*},F^{*}
denote the dual spaces;
if
T\in\mathscr{L}({U};\widetilde{U})
, then
we can identify
U^{*}=U

and
\widetilde{U}^{*}=\widetilde{U}
via the Riesz
maps, so that

T^{*}\in\mathscr{L}(\widetilde{U};{U})
.
We
abbreviate

\mathscr{L}(E):=\mathscr{L}(E;E)
,
and similarly for the other spaces of linear operators.

We write
T\in\mathscr{L}^{+}(U)

to indicate that
T\in\mathscr{L}(U)
is self-adjoint
positive definite, meaning that
T^{*}=T
and
there exists
\theta\in(0,\infty)
such that

\langle Tx,x\rangle_{U}\geq\theta\|x\|_{U}^{2}

for all
x\in U
.
Let

\mathscr{L}_{2}(U;\widetilde{U})\subseteq\mathscr{L}(U;\widetilde{U})

denote the Hilbert space of all Hilbert–Schmidt operators
equipped with
the inner product

\langle T,S\rangle_{\mathscr{L}_{2}(U;\widetilde{U})}:=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\langle Te_{j},Se_{j}\rangle_{\widetilde{U}}
,
where

(e_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is any orthonormal basis for
U
.
The trace of
T\in\mathscr{L}^{+}(U)
is defined by

\operatorname{tr}T:=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\langle Te_{j},e_{j}\rangle_{U}.

We say that
T\in\mathscr{L}(U)

belongs to the Banach space
\mathscr{L}_{1}(U)
of trace class operators
if
\|T\|_{\mathscr{L}_{1}(U)}:=\operatorname{tr}|T|<\infty
for
|T|:=(T^{*}T)^{\frac{1}{2}}
,
using the operator square root
(see e.g.
[
45
, Proposition 8.27]
)
and set
\mathscr{L}_{1}^{+}(U):=\mathscr{L}^{+}(U)\cap\mathscr{L}_{1}(U)
.

A linear operator
A
on
E
with domain
\mathsf{D}(A)

is denoted by
{A\colon\mathsf{D}(A)\subseteq E\to E}
;
for its range we write
\mathsf{R}(A)
.
We call
A
closed if its graph

\mathsf{G}(A):={\{(x,Ax):x\in\mathsf{D}(A)\}}

is closed with respect to the norm

\|(x,Ax)\|_{\mathsf{G}(A)}=\|x\|_{\mathsf{D}(A)}=\|x\|_{E}+\|Ax\|_{E}
,
and densely defined if
\mathsf{D}(A)
is dense in
E
.
If the closure
\overline{\mathsf{G}(A)}
is the graph of a linear operator
\overline{A}
,
then we call
\overline{A}
the closure of
A
.
We write
A\subseteq\widetilde{A}
if
\mathsf{G}(A)\subseteq\mathsf{G}(\widetilde{A})
.

Throughout this work, we assume that a complete probability space

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
is given,
meaning that
\mathcal{F}
contains the collection
\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{P}}
of
\mathbb{P}
-null sets.
We abbreviate the phrase “
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely” by “
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.”
In what follows, we call
a function
Z\colon\Omega\to E

an
E
-valued random variable if it is strongly
\mathbb{P}
-measurable, see
Subsection
A.1
in Appendix
A
.
We write
Z\sim N(m,Q)
if
Z
is a
U
-valued Gaussian random variable
with mean
m\in U
and covariance operator
Q\in\mathscr{L}^{+}_{1}(U)
;
its existence is guaranteed by
[
4
, Theorem 2.3.1]
.
Two stochastic processes
(X(t))_{t\in\mathbb{T}}
and

(\widetilde{X}(t))_{t\in\mathbb{T}}
are said to be modifications
of each other if
\mathbb{P}(X(t)=\widetilde{X}(t))=1
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
,
where
\mathbb{T}=[0,\infty)
,
\mathbb{T}=[0,T]
for some
T\in(0,\infty)
or
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{H},\mathcal{G}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
be sub-
\sigma
-algebras of
\mathcal{F}
.
The join of two
\sigma
-algebras is denoted by
\mathcal{G}_{1}\vee\mathcal{G}_{2}:=\sigma(\mathcal{G}_{1}\cup\mathcal{G}_{2})
.
We write
\mathcal{G}_{1}\perp\!\!\!\perp\mathcal{G}_{2}
to indicate that
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}
are independent.
The expression

denotes the conditional expectation of a random variable
Z
given
\mathcal{H}
,
and the conditional probability of
A\in\mathcal{F}
given
\mathcal{H}
is defined by
\mathbb{P}(A\mid\mathcal{H}):=\mathbb{E}[1_{A}\mid\mathcal{H}]
,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.
The notation
\mathcal{G}_{1}\perp\!\!\!\perp_{\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{G}_{2}
indicates that

\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}
are conditionally independent given
\mathcal{H}
, i.e.,

(2.1)

\forall G_{1}\in\mathcal{G}_{1},\,G_{2}\in\mathcal{G}_{2}:\quad\mathbb{P}(G_{1}\cap G_{2}\mid\mathcal{H})=\mathbb{P}(G_{1}\mid\mathcal{H})\mathbb{P}(G_{2}\mid\mathcal{H}),\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.},

When conditioning on
the natural
\sigma
-algebra
\sigma(Y)=\{Y^{-1}[B]:B\in\mathcal{B}(E)\}
generated
by an
E
-valued random variable
Y
,
we may simply write
Y
instead of
\sigma(Y)
; e.g.,
\mathbb{E}[Z\mid Y]
or
\mathcal{G}_{1}\perp\!\!\!\perp_{Y}\mathcal{G}_{2}
.

2.2.
Stochastic integration with respect to a two-sided Wiener process

Let
(W_{1}^{Q}(t))_{t\geq 0}
and
(W_{2}^{Q}(t))_{t\geq 0}

be independent
U
-valued standard
Q
-Wiener processes for a given
Q\in\mathscr{L}_{1}^{+}(U)
,
see for instance
[
30
, Section 2.1]
, and define

W^{Q}(t):=\begin{cases}W_{1}^{Q}(t),&\quad t\in[0,\infty);\\
W_{2}^{Q}(-t),&\quad t\in(-\infty,0).\end{cases}

Then the
two-sided
Q
-Wiener process

W^{Q}:=(W^{Q}(t))_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
satisfies the following:

(WP1)

W^{Q}(t)
has mean zero and

W^{Q}(t)-W^{Q}(s)\sim N(0,(t-s)Q)
for
t\geq s
;

(WP2)

W^{Q}
has continuous sample paths;

(WP3)

W^{Q}(t_{4})-W^{Q}(t_{3})\perp\!\!\!\perp W^{Q}(t_{2})-W^{Q}(t_{1})
for

t_{1}<t_{2}\leq t_{3}<t_{4}
.

One can
define a stochastic integral with respect to such a process
using a construction analogous to the one-sided case, as presented for instance in
[
30
, Section 2.3]
.
Restricting ourselves to deterministic integrands
\Phi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathscr{L}(U;\widetilde{U})
,
this procedure yields a square-integrable stochastic integral

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Phi(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(t)\in L^{2}(\Omega;\widetilde{U})

which exists if and only if

\Phi(\,\cdot\,)Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R};\mathscr{L}_{2}(U;\widetilde{U}))
;
see Subsection
A.2
in Appendix
A

for the definitions of these (Bochner) spaces.
In this case, it satisfies the following Itô isometry:

(2.2)

\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Phi(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;\widetilde{U})}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|\Phi(t)Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\|_{\mathscr{L}_{2}(U;\widetilde{U})}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

As in the one-sided case, we can extend the definition of the stochastic integral
to allow for an operator
Q\in\mathscr{L}^{+}(U)\setminus\mathscr{L}_{1}^{+}(U)

which is not trace-class.
To this end, one defines a
cylindrical
Q
-Wiener process

(W^{Q}(t))_{t\in\mathbb{R}}

on
U
, which
can be identified with a
\widetilde{Q}
-Wiener process on
a larger Hilbert space
\mathscr{U}\supseteq U

with Hilbert–Schmidt embedding
{\iota\in\mathscr{L}_{2}(U;\mathscr{U})}
,
where
\widetilde{Q}=\iota Q\iota^{*}\in\mathscr{L}_{1}^{+}(\mathscr{U})
.
We can then define the stochastic integral with respect to the associated
cylindrical
Q
-Wiener process, which still satisfies (
2.2
).
Such an embedding
\iota\in\mathscr{L}_{2}(U;\mathscr{U})

always exists, and the resulting integral does not depend
on
\iota
, see
[
30
, Section 2.5]
.

Now we turn to the matter of
\mathbb{R}
-indexed filtrations
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
associated
to
(W^{Q}(t))_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
.
First we recall the situation in
the one-sided case. Here, the
integral process

\bigl(\int_{0}^{t}\Phi(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{1}^{Q}(r)\bigr)_{t\geq 0}:=\bigl(\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}_{[0,t]}(r)\Phi(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{1}^{Q}(r)\bigr)_{t\geq 0}

is adapted to the natural filtration

\mathcal{F}_{t}^{W_{1}^{Q}}:=\sigma\bigl(W_{1}^{Q}(s):0\leq s\leq t\bigr)\vee\sigma(\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{P}}),\quad t\in[0,\infty),

whenever
\Phi(\,\cdot\,)Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\in L^{2}(0,t;\mathscr{L}_{2}(U;\widetilde{U}))

for all
t\in[0,\infty)
,
which is immediate from the definition of the stochastic integral.
Since property
(WP3)
for
(W_{1}^{Q}(t))_{t\geq 0}

implies that
W_{1}^{Q}(t^{\prime})-W_{1}^{Q}(s^{\prime})\perp\!\!\!\perp\mathcal{F}_{s}^{W_{1}^{Q}}

for all
0\leq s\leq s^{\prime}<t^{\prime}\leq t
,
the stochastic integral
\int_{s}^{t}\Phi(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{1}^{Q}(r)

is also independent of
\mathcal{F}_{s}^{W_{1}^{Q}}
​.
The combination of the previous two facts implies that

\bigl(\int_{0}^{t}\Phi(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{1}^{Q}(r)\bigr)_{t\geq 0}

is a martingale with respect to
\bigl(\mathcal{F}^{W_{1}^{Q}}_{t}\bigr)_{t\geq 0}
.

In the two-sided case, we instead work with the

(completed) filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\delta W^{Q}})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
generated by the increments of
W^{Q}
,
defined by

(2.3)

\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\delta W^{Q}}:=\sigma(W^{Q}(u)-W^{Q}(s):s<u\leq t)\vee\sigma(\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{P}}),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Note that we have
\mathcal{F}^{\delta W^{Q}}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{F}^{W^{Q}}_{t}

for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
\mathcal{F}^{W^{Q}}_{t}=\mathcal{F}^{\delta W^{Q}}_{t}

for
t\in[0,\infty)
, where

\mathcal{F}^{W^{Q}}_{t}
is generated
by
(W^{Q}(s))_{s\in(-\infty,t]}
for each
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
We point out that
(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\delta W^{Q}})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
is normal:
It is complete by definition and
right-continuous by Proposition
B.6

in Appendix
B
.
By property
(WP3)
,
our two-sided Wiener process
(W^{Q}(t))_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
now satisfies

(WP3
′
)

\forall s\leq s^{\prime}<t:\quad W^{Q}(t)-W^{Q}(s^{\prime})\perp\!\!\!\perp\mathcal{F}_{s}^{\delta W^{Q}}\!,

so that by an argument,
analogous to the one-sided case,

\bigl(\int_{-\infty}^{t}\Phi(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(r)\bigr)_{t\in\mathbb{R}}

is a martingale with respect to
(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\delta W^{Q}})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}

for every
\Phi(\,\cdot\,)Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R};\mathscr{L}_{2}(U,\widetilde{U}))
.
Unlike
(W_{1}^{Q}(t))_{t\geq 0}
, however,
the process
(W^{Q}(t))_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
itself will not be a martingale
with respect to any filtration, see Proposition
B.7
in Appendix
B
.
We refer the reader to
[
2
,
3
]
for more details on the subject of real-valued
martingale type processes indexed
by
\mathbb{R}
and stochastic integration
with respect to such processes.

We finally record a sufficient condition for
changing the order of integration between a deterministic Lebesgue integral
over a
\sigma
-finite measure space and a stochastic integral over
\mathbb{R}
with respect to
(W^{Q}(t))_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
.
See
[
46
]
for a version of this result in the one-sided case with stochastic integrands.
For deterministic integrands, the two-sided generalization admits the following short proof.

Theorem 2.1

(Stochastic Fubini theorem)
.

Let
(S,\mathscr{A},\mu)
be a
\sigma
-finite measure space
and suppose
\Psi\colon S\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathscr{L}(U,\widetilde{U})

satisfies
\Psi Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\in L^{1,2}(S\times\mathbb{R};\mathscr{L}_{2}(U;\widetilde{U}))

(see Subsection
A.2
of Appendix
A
).
Then

\Psi Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\in L^{2,1}(\mathbb{R}\times S;\mathscr{L}_{2}(U;\widetilde{U}))

and

(2.4)

\int_{S}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Psi(s,r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(s)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{S}\Psi(s,r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(r),\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

Proof.

Let
(\Psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of functions

\Psi_{n}\colon S\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathscr{L}(U;\widetilde{U})

which are linear combinations of
functions of the form

(\mathbf{1}_{B}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{(a,b]})\otimes T

for
a<b
,
B\in\mathscr{A}
with
\mu(B)<\infty

and
T\in\mathscr{L}(U;\widetilde{U})
such that

(2.5)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{S}\biggl[\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|[\Psi(s,r)-\Psi_{n}(s,r)]Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\|^{2}_{\mathscr{L}_{2}(U;\widetilde{U})}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\biggr]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(s)=0.

An application of the Minkowski inequality for integrals
[
44
, Section A.1]

to the function
g_{n}(s,r):=\|[\Psi(s,r)-\Psi_{n}(s,r)]Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\|_{\mathscr{L}_{2}(U;\widetilde{U})}

for each
n\in\mathbb{N}

then yields

\biggl[\int_{\mathbb{R}}\biggl|\int_{S}g_{n}(s,r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(s)\biggr|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\biggr]^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\int_{S}\biggl[\int_{\mathbb{R}}|g_{n}(s,r)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\biggr]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(s),

which together with (
2.5
) shows that

(2.6)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\biggl|\int_{S}\|[\Psi(s,r)-\Psi_{n}(s,r)]Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\|_{\mathscr{L}_{2}(U;\widetilde{U})}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(s)\biggr|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r=0.

By the respective definitions
of the (deterministic and stochastic) integrals
involved,
(
2.5
)
and (
2.6
)
imply the first and last steps of

\displaystyle\int_{S}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Psi(s,r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(s)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{S}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Psi_{n}(s,r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(s)

\displaystyle\>=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{S}\Psi_{n}(s,r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(r)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{S}\Psi(s,r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(r)\text{ in }L^{2}(\Omega;\widetilde{U}),

the second identity can be verified by direct computation
for simple functions.
∎

3.
Markov properties for Hilbert space valued stochastic processes

Let
X=(X(t))_{t\in\mathbb{T}}
be a

U
-valued stochastic process
indexed by

\mathbb{T}
, see Subsection
2.1
.
Intuitively,
X
is said to be a Markov process if,
at any instant,
its past and future states
are independent conditional on the present.
Varying the amount of information from the present gives rise to different
Markov properties, which we will list in decreasing order of strength.

3.1.
Simple Markov property

The following definition
is often just referred to as the Markov property, see also
[
9
, p. 77]

or
[
11
, Equation (6.2), p. 81]
.
We use the adjective
simple
to differentiate it from
the weaker notions of Markov behavior which will be given below.

Definition 3.1
.

An
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{T}}
-adapted
U
-valued
stochastic process
(X(t))_{t\in\mathbb{T}}
is said to have
the
simple Markov property
if for all

s\leq t
and

B\in\mathcal{B}(U)
, we have

(3.1)

\mathbb{P}(X(t)\in B\mid\mathcal{F}_{s})=\mathbb{P}(X(t)\in B\mid X(s)),\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

Note that (
3.1
) in the above definition can equivalently be replaced by

(3.2)

\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X(t))\mid\mathcal{F}_{s}]=\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X(t))\mid X(s)],\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.},

for all
s\leq t
and
\varphi
belonging to the Banach space
B_{b}(U)

of bounded measurable functions from

U
to
\mathbb{R}
equipped with the norm
\|\varphi\|_{B_{b}(U)}:=\sup_{x\in U}|f(x)|
;
this can be
shown using approximation by simple functions.

In particular, this viewpoint suggests the following characterization of the simple Markov property
by means of
transition operators
, which is sometimes taken as the definition,
for instance in
[
40
, Chapter III, Definition 1.3]
.

Proposition 3.2
.

An
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{T}}
-adapted
U
-valued
stochastic process
(X(t))_{t\in\mathbb{T}}

is simple Markov if and only if there
exists a family
(T_{s,t})_{s\leq t\in\mathbb{T}}

of linear operators on
B_{b}(U)
satisfying,
for all
s\leq t
in
\mathbb{T}
and
\varphi\in B_{b}(U)
,

(3.3)

\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X(t))\mid\mathcal{F}_{s}]=T_{s,t}\varphi(X(s)),\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

In this case, the
transition operators

(T_{s,t})_{s\leq t\in\mathbb{T}}
have the following properties:

(TO1)

T_{s,t}\varphi(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in U
if
\varphi\in B_{b}(U)
is non-negative,

(TO2)

T_{s,t}\mathbf{1}_{U}=\mathbf{1}_{U}
,

(TO3)

T_{s,u}\varphi(X(s))=T_{s,t}T_{t,u}\varphi(X(s))
,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.,
for all
\varphi\in B_{b}(U)
and
s\leq t\leq u
.

Proof.

The sufficiency part of the assertion can be verified by applying

\mathbb{E}[\;\cdot\mid X(s)]

on both sides of (
3.3
) and using
elementary properties of conditional expectations to conclude that

\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X(t))\mid X(s)]=T_{s,t}\varphi(X(s))
holds

\mathbb{P}
-a.s. for any
\varphi\in B_{b}(U)
.
This implies (
3.2
), hence
X
has the simple Markov property.

Conversely, suppose that
X
is simple Markov.
Since
U
is separable
and thus a Polish space, there exists a regular conditional
distribution of
X(t)
given
X(s)

for each
s\leq t

[
21
, Theorem 8.5]
,
i.e., a mapping

\pi_{s,t}\colon U\times\mathcal{B}(U)\to[0,1]
such that
for any
x\in U
and
B\in\mathcal{B}(U)
,

\pi_{s,t}(x,\,\cdot\,)
is a probability measure on
(U,\mathcal{B}(U))
,

\pi_{s,t}(\,\cdot\,,B)\colon U\to[0,1]
is Borel measurable
and

\pi_{s,t}(X(s),B)=\mathbb{P}(X(t)\in B\mid X(s))=\mathbb{P}(X(t)\in B\mid\mathcal{F}_{s}),\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

We use this to define the transition operator

T_{s,t}\varphi(x):=\int_{U}\varphi(y)\,\pi_{s,t}(x,\mathrm{d}y),\quad\varphi\in B_{b}(U),\;x\in U.

Properties
(TO1)
and
(TO2)
are consequences of the fact that

\pi_{s,t}(x,\,\cdot\,)
is a probability measure on
\mathcal{B}(U)
for each
x\in U
.
Equation (
3.3
)
can be verified using approximation by simple functions as before;
in conjunction with the tower property
of conditional expectations, we find
(TO3)
.
∎

Property
(TO3)
is also known as the
Chapman–Kolmogorov relation
.
If
T_{s,t}
only depends on the difference
t-s
, then
X
is said to be

time-homogeneous
and we write
T_{t}:=T_{0,t}
,
so that
(TO3)
reduces to the semigroup law
T_{t+s}=T_{t}T_{s}
.

Lastly, we can also characterize the simple Markov property in terms of conditional independence:
By Theorem
B.1
in Appendix
B
,
the simple Markov property is equivalent to

\forall s\leq t:\quad\mathcal{F}_{s}\perp\!\!\!\perp_{X(s)}\sigma(X(t)).

In fact, according to
[
21
, Lemma 11.1]
, this
is in turn equivalent to

(3.4)

\forall s\in\mathbb{T}:\quad\mathcal{F}_{s}\perp\!\!\!\perp_{X(s)}\sigma(X(t):t\geq s).

3.2.
Multiple Markov property

The following weaker notion of Markov behavior
dates back to Doob, who introduced it
in the context of stationary real-valued Gaussian processes
[
10
, pp. 271–272]
.
We generalize it to square-integrable
U
-valued processes
with some mean-square differentiability, i.e.,

(X(t))_{t\in\mathbb{T}}\subseteq L^{2}(\Omega;U)

such that
t\mapsto X(t)
is classically differentiable
from
\mathbb{T}
to
L^{2}(\Omega;U)
.
In works such as
[
18
,
41
]
,
which treat the real-valued setting,
this is called the

multiple Markov property (in the restricted sense)
,
where “restricted” refers to the required differentiability; we will omit this descriptor.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
X=(X(t))_{t\in\mathbb{T}}\subseteq L^{2}(\Omega;U)

be an
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{T}}
-adapted
U
-valued
stochastic process and suppose that
N\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then
X
has the

N
-ple Markov property
if it
has
N-1
mean square derivatives
and, for all

s\leq t
in
\mathbb{T}
and

B\in\mathcal{B}(U)
,

(3.5)

\mathbb{P}(X(t)\in B\mid\mathcal{F}_{s})=\mathbb{P}(X(t)\in B\mid X(s),X^{\prime}(s),\dots,X^{(N-1)}(s)),\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

Setting
\mathbf{X}(t):=(X^{(k)}(t))_{k=0}^{N-1}
, one defines
a process taking values in the direct product Hilbert space
(U^{N},\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle_{U^{N}}\!)
, where
the inner product

(3.6)

\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle_{U^{N}}:=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\langle x_{j},y_{j}\rangle_{U},\qquad\mathbf{x}=(x_{j})_{j=1}^{N},\,\mathbf{y}=(y_{j})_{j=1}^{N}\in U^{N}\!,

induces the product topology on the set
U^{N}
​.
In particular, the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of
U^{N}

satisfies
\mathcal{B}(U^{N})=\otimes^{N}\mathcal{B}(U)
by
[
21
, Lemma 1.2]
.
Theorem
B.1

in Appendix
B
again yields an equivalent formulation of the
N
-ple Markov property
in terms of conditional independence:

(3.7)

\forall s\in\mathbb{T}:\quad\mathcal{F}_{s}\perp\!\!\!\perp_{\mathbf{X}(s)}\sigma(X(t):t\geq s).

Note that
\sigma(\mathbf{X}(s))\vee\mathcal{F}_{s}=\mathcal{F}_{s}
since the mean-square derivatives of
X
can be replaced
by left derivatives, see the proof of Proposition
3.6
below.
Arguing as in
[
21
, Lemma 11.1]
, one can show that this
is in turn equivalent to

(3.8)

\forall s\in\mathbb{T}:\quad\mathcal{F}_{s}\perp\!\!\!\perp_{\mathbf{X}(s)}\sigma(\mathbf{X}(t):t\geq s),

which is nothing more than the simple Markov property
for
\mathbf{X}
. Thus,
we can apply Proposition
3.2
to derive the following characterization.

Corollary 3.4
.

An
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{T}}
-adapted and square-integrable

U
-valued stochastic process
X=(X(t))_{t\in\mathbb{T}}

with
N-1
mean-square derivatives
is
N
-ple Markov
if and only if there
exists a family
(T_{s,t})_{s\leq t\in\mathbb{T}}

of linear operators on
B_{b}(U^{N})
satisfying,
for all
s\leq t\leq u
in
\mathbb{T}
and
\varphi\in B_{b}(U^{N})
,

(3.9)

\mathbb{E}[\varphi(\mathbf{X}(t))\mid\mathcal{F}_{s}]=T_{s,t}\varphi(\mathbf{X}(s)),\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

In this case,
(T_{s,t})_{s\leq t\in\mathbb{T}}
satisfies properties
(TO1)
–
(TO3)
.

3.3.
Weak Markov properties; relations between concepts

We now define two Markov properties for which
the “present” at time
s\in\mathbb{T}
is represented by
information from neighborhoords around
s
.
As we will prove in Proposition
3.6
below, these two notions are equivalent.
They appear in, e.g.,
[
41
, p. 62]
and
[
18
, Equation (5.87), p. 115]
.

Definition 3.5
.

An
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{T}}
-adapted
U
-valued
stochastic process
(X(t))_{t\in\mathbb{T}}
has

(i)

the
weak Markov property
if, for every
s\in\mathbb{T}
,

(3.10)

\exists\delta>0:\forall\varepsilon\in(0,\delta):\quad\mathcal{F}_{s}\perp\!\!\!\perp_{\mathscr{A}_{\varepsilon}(s)}\sigma(X(t):t\geq s),

where
\mathscr{A}_{\varepsilon}(s):=\sigma(X(u):u\in(s-\varepsilon,s+\varepsilon)\cap\mathbb{T})
;

(ii)

the
\sigma
-Markov property
if, for every
s\in\mathbb{T}
,

(3.11)

\mathcal{F}_{s}\perp\!\!\!\perp_{\partial\mathscr{A}(s)}\sigma(X(t):t\geq s),

where
\partial\mathscr{A}(s):=\bigcap_{\varepsilon>0}\mathscr{A}_{\varepsilon}(s)
.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
X=(X(t))_{t\in\mathbb{T}}
be an
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{T}}
-adapted
U
-valued stochastic process.
We have the following
relations between Markov properties:

\text{simple Markov}\implies\text{$\sigma$-Markov}\iff\text{weak Markov}.

If
N,M\in\mathbb{N}
are such that
N\geq M
and

X
has
N-1
mean-square derivatives, then we moreover have

\text{$M$-ple Markov}\implies\text{$N$-ple Markov}\implies\text{weak Markov}.

Proof.

If
X
has the weak Markov property, then by definition
we have the following identity for fixed
s\in\mathbb{T}
,
B_{-}\in\mathcal{F}_{s}
and
B_{+}\in\sigma(X(t):t\geq s)
:

(3.12)

\mathbb{P}(B_{-}\mid\mathscr{A}_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{n}}}(s))\mathbb{P}(B_{+}\mid\mathscr{A}_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{n}}}(s))=\mathbb{P}(B_{-}\cap B_{+}\mid\mathscr{A}_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{n}}}(s)),\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.},

whenever
n\in\mathbb{N}
is large enough.
Note that
(\mathscr{G}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}:=(\mathscr{A}_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{n}}}(s))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is
a non-increasing sequence of
sub-
\sigma
-algebras of
\mathcal{F}
, i.e., a backward filtration on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
.
Therefore,
(\mathbb{P}(B\mid\mathscr{G}_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a backward martingale with respect to

(\mathscr{G}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
for any
B\in\mathcal{F}
.
Combined with the fact that
\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathscr{G}_{n}=\partial\mathscr{A}(s)
,
the backward martingale convergence theorem
[
15
, Section 12.7, Theorem 4]
implies that we may take
the
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. limit as
n\to\infty
in (
3.12
) to find that
X
is
\sigma
-Markov.

Now let
N,M\in\mathbb{N}
with
N\geq M

be such that
X
has the
M
-ple Markov
property and
N-1
mean-square derivatives.
When considering
\sigma(X^{\prime}(s))
at
s\in\mathbb{T}
,
we can restrict ourselves to mean-square left derivatives, i.e.,
we consider the sequence

(3.13)

(\Delta_{n}(s))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}:=(n[X(s)-X(s-n^{-1})])_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

converging to
X^{\prime}(s)
in the
L^{2}(\Omega;U)
-norm as
n\to\infty
.
Consequently, there exists a subsequence
(\Delta_{n_{k}}(s))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

\Delta_{n_{k}}(s)\to X^{\prime}(s)
,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s., as
k\to\infty
.
Since
\Delta_{n_{k}}(s)
is
\mathcal{F}_{s}
-measurable for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we conclude that

X^{\prime}(s)
is
\mathcal{F}_{s}
-measurable and thus

\sigma(X^{\prime}(s))\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{s}
. By induction, this extends to

\sigma(X(s),X^{\prime}(s),\dots,X^{(M-1)}(s))\subseteq\sigma(X(s),X^{\prime}(s),\dots,X^{(N-1)}(s))\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{s},

so that Lemma
B.2
(ii)
yields the
N
-ple
Markov property as formulated in (
3.7
).

It remains
to show that the
N
-ple Markov property for
N\in\mathbb{N}

and the
\sigma
-Markov property imply the weak Markovianity of
X
.
Fixing
s\in\mathbb{T}
and
\varepsilon>0
, we can set

(3.14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\prime}:=\sigma(X(u):u\in(s-\varepsilon,s]\cap\mathbb{T})\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{s},

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{2}^{\prime}:=\sigma(X(u):u\in[s,s+\varepsilon)\cap\mathbb{T})\subseteq\sigma(X(t):t\in[s,\infty)\cap\mathbb{T}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\prime}:=\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\prime}\vee\mathcal{H}_{2}^{\prime}=\mathscr{A}_{\varepsilon}(s).

Since
\sigma(X(s))\subseteq\partial\mathscr{A}(s)\subseteq\mathscr{A}_{\varepsilon}(s)
,
by Lemma
B.2
(iii)
the simple (i.e., 1-ple) Markov or
\sigma
-Markov property
of
X
would imply

(3.15)

\mathcal{F}_{s}\perp\!\!\!\perp_{\mathscr{A}_{\varepsilon}(s)}\sigma(X(t):t\in[s,\infty)\cap\mathbb{T}),

and thus the weak Markov property since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary.
It remains to show that (
3.15
) also holds if
X
is
N
-ple Markov.
Choosing
K\in\mathbb{N}
so large that
n_{k}>\varepsilon^{-1}

for all
k\geq K
,
we find that
(\Delta_{n_{k}}(s))_{k\geq K}
(see (
3.13
))
is a sequence of

\mathscr{A}_{\varepsilon}(s)
-measurable random variables
converging
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. to
X^{\prime}(s)
. As before, this procedure
can be repeated inductively to yield

\mathcal{H}:=\sigma(X(s),X^{\prime}(s),\dots,X^{(N-1)}(s))\subseteq\mathscr{A}_{\varepsilon}(s).

This justifies the use of Lemma
B.2
(iii)

to establish (
3.15
)
for the remaining case, and the desired conclusion follows.
∎

Remark 3.7
.

An analog to Definition
3.5
for
generalized

U
-valued stochastic processes

(X(\phi))_{\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}

is obtained by replacing
\sigma(X(u):u\in J)

with the natural
\sigma
-algebra

\sigma(X(\phi):\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}),\,\operatorname{supp}\phi\subseteq J)

generated by
X
on an open set
J\subseteq\mathbb{T}
.
Since pointwise evaluation is not meaningful for such processes,
there is no analog to the simple Markov property.
Furthermore,
although the proof of the implication

\text{weak Markov}\implies\text{$\sigma$-Markov}

carries over, its converse now fails:
The distributional derivative of white noise is
a generalized process which is
\sigma
-Markov but not weak Markov, see
[
41
, p. 62]
.

3.4.
Characterization of weakly Markov Gaussian processes

A
U
-valued stochastic process
X=(X(t))_{t\in\mathbb{T}}
is said to be Gaussian if,
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\{t_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subseteq\mathbb{T}
,
the
U^{n}
-valued random variable

(X(t_{1}),X(t_{2}),\dots,X(t_{n}))
is Gaussian.
For such processes, we shall characterize the weak Markov property of Definition
3.5

by extending the theory of Rozanov
[
41
]

from real-valued to
U
-valued processes.

We consider the case of a mean-square continuous Gaussian process
X

which
is the solution of a stochastic evolution equation of the form

\mathcal{L}X=\dot{W}
for some linear operator
\mathcal{L}\colon\mathsf{D}(\mathcal{L})\subseteq L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U)\to L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U)
;
here,
\dot{W}
denotes spatiotemporal Gaussian white noise,
cf. (
1.1
) and (
1.3
).
More precisely, we assume that
\mathcal{L}
has a bounded inverse
\mathcal{L}^{-1}

which
colors

X
, meaning

(3.16)

\langle X,\phi\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U)}\overset{d}{=}\mathscr{W}(\mathcal{L}^{-*}\phi),\quad\forall\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T};U),

where
\mathcal{L}^{-*}\coloneqq(\mathcal{L}^{-1})^{*}=(\mathcal{L}^{*})^{-1}
and
\overset{d}{=}
indicates equality in distribution.
Here,
(\mathscr{W}(f))_{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U)}\subseteq L^{2}(\Omega)
is an
L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U)
-isonormal Gaussian process,
i.e., a family of mean-zero and real-valued Gaussian random variables satisfying

(3.17)

\langle\mathscr{W}(f),\mathscr{W}(g)\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\langle f,g\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U)},\quad\forall f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U).

The following theorem then states that the locality of the

precision operator

\mathcal{L}^{*}\mathcal{L}
is necessary for

X
to possess the weak Markov property.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
\mathcal{L}\colon\mathsf{D}(\mathcal{L})\subseteq L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U)\to L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U)
be a
boundedly invertible linear
operator,
and suppose that
X=(X(t))_{t\in\mathbb{T}}
is a mean-square continuous Gaussian
U
-valued process
colored by
\mathcal{L}^{-1}
.
Let
F
be a dense subset of
U
for which

C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T};F)\subseteq\mathsf{D}(\mathcal{L})
.
Furthermore, suppose that

C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T};F)
and its image under
\mathcal{L}
are dense
subsets of

L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U)
.

If
X
has the weak Markov property from Definition
3.5
with respect
to its natural filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{X})_{t\in\mathbb{T}}
, then

(3.18)

\forall J\in\mathscr{I}:\quad\langle\mathcal{L}\phi,\mathcal{L}\psi\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U)}=0,\quad\forall\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(J;F),\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}\setminus\overline{J};F),

where
\mathscr{I}
denotes the set of
all open intervals
J\subseteq\mathbb{T}
.

Proof.

For all
J\in\mathscr{I}
we
define a closed subspace
\mathfrak{H}(J)
of
L^{2}(\Omega)
by

(3.19)

\mathfrak{H}(J):=\overline{\{\langle X,\phi\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U)}:\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(J;F)\}}^{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Then the family
(\mathfrak{H}(J))_{J\in\mathscr{I}}
is
a
Gaussian random field
in the sense of
[
41
, Chapter 2, Section 3.1]
,
and we can connect it to the present setting by showing that

(3.20)

\sigma(X(t):t\in J)=\sigma(\mathfrak{H}(J)).

Indeed, we have
\sigma(\mathfrak{H}(J))\subseteq\sigma(X(t):t\in J)

since
\langle X,\phi\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U)}
is
measurable with respect to the latter
\sigma
-algebra for all
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(J;F)
with
\operatorname{supp}\phi\subseteq J
.
In order to establish the converse inclusion, it suffices to verify the claim that
X(t)
is
\sigma(\mathfrak{H}(J))
-measurable
for each
t\in J
.
Let
(e_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
be an orthonormal basis of
U

and write

(3.21)

X(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\langle X(t),e_{j}\rangle_{U}\,e_{j}\quad\text{in }L^{2}(\Omega;U).

Now we will show that
\langle X(t),e_{j}\rangle_{U}
is
\sigma(\mathfrak{H}(J))
-measurable for every
j\in\mathbb{N}
.
In fact, by the density of
F\subseteq U
it suffices to consider
\langle X(t),x\rangle_{U}
for
x\in F
.
Let
(\phi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq C_{c}^{\infty}(J)
be a sequence of
bump functions concentrating around
t
,
i.e., we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}}f(s)\phi_{n}(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=f(t)
in
E

for any
f\in C(\mathbb{T};E)
, where
E
is an arbitrary Banach space.
It follows from the mean-square continuity of
X
that

f:=\langle X(\,\cdot\,),x\rangle_{U}\in C(\mathbb{T};L^{2}(\Omega))
, thus with
E:=L^{2}(\Omega)
we obtain

\langle X(t),x\rangle_{U}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\langle X(s),x\rangle_{U}\phi_{n}(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\langle X(s),\phi_{n}(s)x\rangle_{U}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\quad\text{in }L^{2}(\Omega).

Passing to a
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.-convergent subsequence in the rightmost expression,
we find that
\langle X(t),x\rangle_{U}
is a limit of
\sigma(\mathfrak{H}(J))
-measurable
random variables. Thus, each summand in (
3.21
) is

\sigma(\mathfrak{H}(J))
-measurable, and passing to a
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.-convergent subsequence of

(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\langle X(t),e_{j}\rangle_{U}\,e_{j})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
proves the claim.

The theory of
[
41
, Chapter 2, Section 3.1]
,
now implies that
X
has the weak Markov property from Definition
3.5
if and only
if
(\mathfrak{H}(J))_{J\in\mathscr{I}}
is Markov in the sense of
[
41
, p. 97]
.
For a general
B\subseteq\mathbb{T}
, we define

(3.22)

\mathfrak{H}_{+}(B):=\bigcap\nolimits_{\varepsilon>0}\mathfrak{H}(B^{\varepsilon}),

where

B^{\varepsilon}:=\{t\in\mathbb{T}:\operatorname{dist}(t,B)<\varepsilon\}

denotes an open
\varepsilon
-neighborhood of
B
.
Using the definition (
3.22
) for
B\in\{\partial J,J,\mathbb{T}\setminus J\}
,
the Markov property for
(\mathfrak{H}(J))_{J\in\mathscr{I}}

implies that
[
41
, Equations (3.14), p. 97]

are satisfied for every
J\in\mathscr{I}
:

(3.23)

\mathfrak{H}_{+}(\partial J)=\mathfrak{H}_{+}(J)\cap\mathfrak{H}_{+}(\mathbb{T}\setminus J)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{H}_{+}(J)^{\perp}\perp\mathfrak{H}_{+}(\mathbb{T}\setminus J)^{\perp},

where we take
L^{2}(\Omega)
-orthogonal complements in
\mathfrak{H}(\mathbb{T})
.

Next we define

\langle X^{*},\,\cdot\,\rangle\colon C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T};F)\to L^{2}(\Omega)

by

(3.24)

\langle X^{*},\phi\rangle:=\mathscr{W}(\mathcal{L}\phi),\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.},\quad\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T};F),

to which we associate the spaces

(3.25)

\mathfrak{H}^{*}(J):=\overline{\{\langle X^{*},\phi\rangle:\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(J;F)\}}^{L^{2}(\Omega)},\quad J\in\mathscr{I}.

Then
(\langle X^{*},\phi\rangle)_{\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T};F)}
is dual to

(\langle X,\psi\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U)})_{\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T};F)}

in the sense that

(3.26)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\langle X,\phi\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U)}\langle X^{*},\psi\rangle]=\mathbb{E}[\mathscr{W}(\mathcal{L}^{-*}\phi)\mathscr{W}(\mathcal{L}\psi)]=\langle\phi,\psi\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U)}

for
\phi,\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T};F)
.
Next we will prove

(3.27)

\mathfrak{H}(\mathbb{T})=\mathfrak{H}^{*}(\mathbb{T}).

by showing that both of these sets are equal to

\mathscr{Z}:=\overline{\{\mathscr{W}(f):f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U)\}}^{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

First, we note that
\mathfrak{H}(\mathbb{T})
and
\mathfrak{H}^{*}(\mathbb{T})

are clearly contained in
\mathscr{Z}
.
Now let
Z\in\mathscr{Z}
and
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary, and choose

f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U)
such that
\|\mathscr{W}(f)-Z\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}<\frac{1}{3}\varepsilon
.
Since the image of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T};F)
under
\mathcal{L}
is assumed to be dense in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U)
,
we may furthermore choose
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T};F)
such that

\|\mathcal{L}\phi-f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;U)}<\frac{2}{3}\varepsilon
.
It follows that

\displaystyle\|Z-\langle X^{*},\phi\rangle\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\|Z-\mathscr{W}(f)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\mathscr{W}(\mathcal{L}\phi-f)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\|Z-\mathscr{W}(f)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\mathcal{L}\phi-f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U)}<\varepsilon,

which shows
Z\in\mathfrak{H}^{*}(\mathbb{T})
since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary.
On the other hand,
since
\mathcal{L}
is densely defined and has a bounded inverse, it is in particular closed,
hence
\mathcal{L}^{*}
exists and is also densely defined by
[
45
, Proposition 10.22]
.
It thus follows that

\mathsf{R}(\mathcal{L}^{-*})=\mathsf{R}((\mathcal{L}^{*})^{-1})=\mathsf{D}(\mathcal{L}^{*})
is dense in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U)
,
so that there exists
g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U)
satisfying

\|f-\mathcal{L}^{-*}g\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U)}<\frac{1}{3}\varepsilon
. Finally,
we choose
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T};F)
such that

\|\psi-g\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U)}<\|\mathcal{L}^{-*}\|_{\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U))}^{-1}\frac{1}{3}\varepsilon
so that

\displaystyle\|Z-\langle X,\psi\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U)}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle<\tfrac{1}{3}\varepsilon+\|\mathscr{W}(f-\mathcal{L}^{-*}g)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\mathscr{W}(\mathcal{L}^{-*}g-\mathcal{L}^{-*}\psi)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{3}\varepsilon+\|f-\mathcal{L}^{-*}g\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U)}+\|\mathcal{L}^{-*}(g-\psi)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U)}<\varepsilon,

hence also
Z\in\mathfrak{H}(\mathbb{T})
.
We conclude that (
3.27
) holds.

The necessity of (
3.18
) for the weak Markov property
of
X
will follow from

(3.28)

\mathfrak{H}^{*}(J)\subseteq\mathfrak{H}_{+}(\mathbb{T}\setminus J)^{\perp},\quad\forall J\in\mathscr{I}.

Indeed, if
X
is weakly Markov, then (
3.28
)
would imply that the random variables

\displaystyle\xi:=\langle X^{*},\phi\rangle

\displaystyle\in\mathfrak{H}^{*}(J)\subseteq\mathfrak{H}_{+}(\mathbb{T}\setminus J)^{\perp},

\displaystyle\eta:=\langle X^{*},\psi\rangle

\displaystyle\in\mathfrak{H}^{*}(\mathbb{T}\setminus J)\subseteq\mathfrak{H}_{+}(J)^{\perp},

where
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(J;F)
and
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}\setminus J;F)
,
are orthogonal by (
3.23
).
Therefore,

0=\langle\xi,\eta\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\mathbb{E}[\langle X^{*},\phi\rangle\langle X^{*},\psi\rangle]=\langle\mathcal{L}\phi,\mathcal{L}\psi\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U)},

which shows (
3.18
).
Note that by definition (
3.25
) and density, the orthogonality extends to all
\xi\in\mathfrak{H}^{*}(J)

and
\eta\in\mathfrak{H}^{*}(\mathbb{T}\setminus J)
.

In order to verify (
3.28
), we again take
\xi=\langle X^{*},\phi\rangle

with
\phi
as above.
By the compact support of the latter, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

\langle\phi,\psi\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U)}=0
for all
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}((\mathbb{T}\setminus J)^{\varepsilon};F)
.
Hence, for
\eta=\langle X,\psi\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U)}\in\mathfrak{H}((\mathbb{T}\setminus J)^{\varepsilon})
we have

\xi\perp\eta

by (
3.26
), from which we can deduce
\xi\perp\mathfrak{H}_{+}(\mathbb{T}\setminus J)
,
and thus (
3.28
).
∎

In order to state and prove sufficient conditions for weak Markovianity of
X

in terms of the locality of its precision operator,
we first need to collect some definitions which are based on objects encountered
in the proof of Theorem
3.8
.
Namely, we will define spaces
(\mathcal{H}(J))_{J\in\mathscr{I}}

such that
\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{T})
is
unitarily isomorphic to
\mathfrak{H}(\mathbb{T})

and there exists a dense injection
\iota\colon C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T};F)\to\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{T})
.

Associating,
to each
\eta\in\mathfrak{H}(\mathbb{T})
,
a mapping
I^{-1}\eta\colon C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T};F)\to\mathbb{R}
given by

(3.29)

I^{-1}\eta(\phi):=\mathbb{E}[\langle X,\phi\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U)}\eta],\quad\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T};F),

sets up a linear map
I^{-1}\colon\mathfrak{H}(\mathbb{T})\to\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{T})
,
where
\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{T})
is defined as the range of
I^{-1}
.
It is also
injective since
I^{-1}\eta(\phi)=0
for all
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T};F)

means
\eta\perp C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T};F)
in
\mathfrak{H}(\mathbb{T})
,
and thus
\eta=0
by (
3.19
).
Equipping
\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{T})
with the inner product

\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{T})}:=\langle Iv_{1},Iv_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\quad v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathfrak{H}(\mathbb{T}),

renders

I\colon\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{T})\to\mathfrak{H}(\mathbb{T})

a unitary isomorphism.
For
J\in\mathscr{I}
we can then define

(3.30)

\mathcal{H}(J):=\bigvee\nolimits_{\!\!\varepsilon>0}\,\{v\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{T}):v(\phi)=0\text{ for all }\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}((\mathbb{T}\setminus J)^{\varepsilon};F)\},

where
\bigvee
denotes the closed linear span.

A dense injection
\iota\colon C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T};F)\to\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{T})
is obtained
by defining
{\iota v\colon C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T};F)\to\mathbb{R}}
in the following way,
for any
v\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T};F)
:

\iota v(\phi):=\langle v,\phi\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U)},\quad\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T};F).

Indeed, we find
\iota v\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{T})

since the duality relations (
3.26
) and (
3.27
)
between
X
and
X^{*}

imply that

\langle X^{*},v\rangle\in\mathfrak{H}^{*}(\mathbb{T})=\mathfrak{H}(\mathbb{T})

satisfies

\iota v(\phi)=\mathbb{E}[\langle X,\phi\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U)}\langle X^{*},v\rangle]=I^{-1}\langle X^{*},v\rangle(\phi),\quad\forall\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T};F).

Moreover, the injectivity follows in the same way as for
I^{-1}
.
To show density of the range, fix an arbitrary
v\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{T})
.
Then
{Iv\in\mathfrak{H}(\mathbb{T})=\mathfrak{H}^{*}(\mathbb{T})}
and thus
there exists a sequence

(\psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T};F)

such that
\langle X^{*},\psi_{n}\rangle\to Iv
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
Consequently,
we have
\iota\psi_{n}=I^{-1}\langle X^{*},\psi_{n}\rangle\to v
in
\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{T})
.

Remark 3.9
.

For centered, real-valued Gaussian random fields
(Z(x))_{x\in\mathcal{X}}

which are indexed by a compact metric space
(\mathcal{X},d_{\mathcal{X}})
and moreover mean-square continuous,
a unitary isomorphism can be established between the
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
-closure
of all linear combinations of point evaluations
and the dual of the Cameron–Martin space for its associated Gaussian measure on
the space
L^{2}(\mathcal{X};\mathbb{R})
, see
[
23
, Lemma 4.1(iii)]
.
We point out its analogy to
the unitary isomorphism

I\colon\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{T})\to\mathfrak{H}(\mathbb{T})
defined above.

Theorem 3.10
.

Let the linear operator
\mathcal{L}\colon\mathsf{D}(\mathcal{L})\subseteq L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U)\to L^{2}(\mathbb{T};U)
,
the
U
-valued process
X=(X(t))_{t\in\mathbb{T}}
and the subset
F\subseteq U

be as in Theorem
3.8
.
Recall that
\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{T})
is the range of the
linear mapping
I^{-1}
defined by (
3.29
)
and
\mathcal{H}(J)
is given by (
3.30
) for all
J\in\mathscr{I}
.
If

(3.31)

\mathcal{H}(J)=\overline{\iota C_{c}^{\infty}(J;F)}^{\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{T})},\quad\forall J\in\mathscr{I},

then (
3.18
) implies that
X
has the weak Markov property
from Definition
3.5
.

Proof.

We will show that, under the additional assumption (
3.31
),

(3.32)

\mathfrak{H}^{*}(J)=\mathfrak{H}_{+}(\mathbb{T}\setminus J)^{\perp},\quad\forall J\in\mathscr{I}.

Note that inclusion (
3.28
) also holds without this assumption,
see the proof of Theorem
3.8
.
Identities (
3.27
) and (
3.32
) express that

(\mathfrak{H}(J))_{J\in\mathscr{I}}
and
(\mathfrak{H}^{*}(J))_{J\in\mathscr{I}}
are
dual

in the sense of
[
41
, Chapter 2, Section 3.5]
.
In this situation, the theorem on
[
41
, p. 100]
yields that

is weakly Markov if and only if
(\mathfrak{H}^{*}(J))_{J\in\mathscr{I}}

is
orthogonal
, meaning that

(3.33)

\mathfrak{H}^{*}(J)\perp\mathfrak{H}^{*}(\mathbb{T}\setminus\overline{J}),\quad\forall J\in\mathscr{I},

)
by the respective definitions of

and

.

To verify (

), note that for each

,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{H}((\mathbb{T}\setminus J)^{\varepsilon})^{\perp}

\displaystyle=\{\eta\in\mathfrak{H}(\mathbb{T}):\langle\eta,\xi\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=0\text{ for all }\xi\in\mathfrak{H}((\mathbb{T}\setminus J)^{\varepsilon})\}

\displaystyle=\{\eta\in\mathfrak{H}(\mathbb{T}):U^{-1}\eta(\phi)=0\text{ for all }\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}((\mathbb{T}\setminus J)^{\varepsilon};F)\}

On the other hand, definition (

).
∎

Remark 3.11
.

In order for
locality of the precision operator

to imply
the weak Markovianity of
X
,
one needs to verify

3.31
).
In the real-valued case,

\mathcal{L}
for (

)
to hold are
[

,

[

, Chapter 3, Section 3.2]

Although it is expected that analogous results can be derived in the

-valued setting,
this subject is out of scope for the processes considered in the remainder of this article,

3.10
,
see Subsection
4.4
.
However, we do use Theorem
3.8

to show when the process lacks Markov behavior in Subsection

.

4.
Fractional stochastic abstract Cauchy problem on
\mathbb{R}

The aim of this section is to define a Hilbert space valued stochastic process
(Z_{\gamma}(t))_{t\in\mathbb{R}}

which can be interpreted as a
mild solution
to an equation of the form

(4.1)

(\partial_{t}+A)^{\gamma}X(t)=\dot{W}^{Q}(t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

In Section
4.1
we specify the setting in which equation (
4.1
) will be considered.
The fractional parabolic differential operator

(\partial_{t}+A)^{\gamma}
is defined in Section
4.2
,
whereas
the noise term
\dot{W}^{Q}
in (

) is the formal

-Wiener process defined in Section

.
In Section

we combine these two notions to

,
and we indicate its relation to the fractional

-Wiener process defined in Section
5
.
Lastly, in Section

we prove that

,

4.1.
Setting

The standing assumption throughout this section on the Hilbert space
U

and the linear operator

is as follows.

Assumption 4.1
.

The linear operator

-A\colon\mathsf{D}(A)\subseteq U\to U

on the separable real Hilbert space

generates an

exponentially stable

-semigroup
(S(t))_{t\geq 0}
of bounded

, i.e.,

\exists M_{0}\in[1,\infty),w\in(0,\infty):\forall t\in[0,\infty):\quad\|S(t)\|_{\mathscr{L}(U)}\leq M_{0}e^{-wt}.

In addition, we may assume one or both of the following conditions
on the fractional power
\gamma
and the linear operator
Q
:

Assumption 4.2
.

(i)

There exist
\gamma_{0}\in(\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},\infty)
and

such that

\int_{0}^{\infty}\|t^{\gamma_{0}-1}S(t)Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\|_{\mathscr{L}_{2}(U)}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t<\infty.

(ii)

The
C_{0}
-semigroup

For a general account of the theory of

-semigroups, the reader
is referred to
[
13
,
37
]
.
Note that the results in these works, while stated for
complex Hilbert spaces, can be applied to the real setting by employing

of (linear operators on)
U
, see e.g. Subsection B.2.1 of
[
24
, Appendix B]
.
In particular, by Assumption
4.2
(ii)
we mean that the complexification of

[

, Section 2.5]
.
We will not emphasize this matter in what follows.

Note that

(\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},\infty)
is the maximal range on which Assumption

(i)
can hold.

4.2
(i)
holds for some

then
the same is true for all

.
These facts are proven in Subsection
B.2
of Appendix
B
.

Under Assumption

(ii)
,
we have the classical derivative

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}A^{j}S(t)=-A^{j+1}S(t)

from
(0,\infty)
to

for all

;
moreover, by
[

, Chapter 2, Theorem 6.13(c)]
,

(4.3)

\exists M_{j}\in[1,\infty)\colon\>\forall t\in(0,\infty):\quad\|A^{j}S(t)\|_{\mathscr{L}(U)}\leq M_{j}t^{-j}e^{-wt}.

In what follows, we will simply write
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}:=(\mathcal{F}^{\delta W^{Q}}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}

for the increment filtration of
W^{Q}
defined by (
2.3
).

4.2.
Fractional parabolic calculus and the deterministic problem

In this section we first consider the following deterministic counterpart to equation (
4.1
):

(4.4)

(\partial_{t}+A)^{\gamma}u(t)=f(t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

where
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R};U)
.
In order to define its mild solution,
we introduce the operations of fractional parabolic integration and differentiation,
generalizing the scalar-valued setting with
A=0
which is treated in
[
22
, Chapter 2]
.

Definition 4.3
.

Let Assumption
4.1
hold.
Given a function
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to U
, we define its
Weyl type fractional parabolic integral

\mathfrak{I}^{\gamma}f\colon\mathbb{R}\to U
of order
\gamma\in(0,\infty)

by

(4.5)

\mathfrak{I}^{\gamma}f(t):=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\int_{-\infty}^{t}(t-s)^{\gamma-1}S(t-s)f(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{\gamma-1}S(r)f(t-r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r

if these Bochner integrals,
which are equivalent by the change of variables
{r:=t-s}
,
exist for almost all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

Viewing
\mathfrak{I}^{\gamma}
as a linear operator, it turns out that

\forall p\in(1,\infty):\quad\mathfrak{I}^{\gamma}\in\mathscr{L}(L^{p}(\mathbb{R};U))\quad\text{with}\quad\|\mathfrak{I}^{\gamma}\|_{\mathscr{L}(L^{p}(\mathbb{R};U))}\leq\frac{M_{0}}{w^{\gamma}},

which follows by combining estimate (
4.2
) with Minkowski’s integral inequality
[
44
, Section A.1]
.
Setting
\mathfrak{I}^{0}:=\operatorname{Id}_{U}
,
the family
(\mathfrak{I}^{\gamma})_{\gamma\geq 0}
is a semigroup of bounded operators
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R};U)
. Indeed,
the semigroup law

(4.6)

\forall\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in[0,\infty)\colon\quad\mathfrak{I}^{\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}}=\mathfrak{I}^{\gamma_{1}}\mathfrak{I}^{\gamma_{2}},

follows from an argument involving Fubini’s theorem and
[
36
, Equation (5.12.1)]
.
The adjoint operator
\mathfrak{I}^{\gamma*}
of
\mathfrak{I}^{\gamma}
satisfies
the following formula for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
:

(4.7)

\mathfrak{I}^{\gamma*}f(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\int_{t}^{\infty}(s-t)^{\gamma-1}[S(s-t)]^{*}f(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

Given a linear operator
T\colon\mathsf{D}(T)\subseteq U\to U

and a measure space
(S,\mathscr{A},\mu)
, let us define

\mathcal{T}_{S}\colon\mathsf{D}(\mathcal{T}_{S})\subseteq L^{2}(S;U)\to L^{2}(S;U)
by

(4.8)

\mathsf{D}(\mathcal{T}_{S})=L^{2}(S;\mathsf{D}(T))\quad\text{and}\quad[\mathcal{T}_{S}f](s):=Tf(s),\;\text{a.a. }s\in S,\;f\in\mathsf{D}(\mathcal{T}_{S}).

Using this notion, we have

(4.9)

(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{R}})f=\partial_{t}f+\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{R}}f,\quad f\in\mathsf{D}(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{R}})=H^{1}(\mathbb{R};U)\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R};\mathsf{D}(A)),

where

\partial_{t}
denotes the Bochner–Sobolev weak derivative
from Subsection
A.2
in Appendix
A
with

\mathsf{D}(\partial_{t})=H^{1}(\mathbb{R};U)\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R};U)
.

Now define the

Weyl type fractional parabolic derivative
of order
\gamma\in[0,\infty)

as the linear operator

\mathfrak{D}^{\gamma}:=(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{R}})^{\lceil\gamma\rceil}\mathfrak{I}^{\lceil\gamma\rceil-\gamma}

on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R};U)
on its natural domain

\mathsf{D}(\mathfrak{D}^{\gamma})=\Bigl\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R};U):\mathfrak{I}^{\lceil\gamma\rceil-\gamma}f\in\mathsf{D}\bigl({(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{R}})^{\lceil\gamma\rceil}}\bigr)\Bigr\}.

The operator
\mathfrak{D}^{\gamma}

is to be interpreted as
(\partial_{t}+A)^{\gamma}
in (
4.4
).
This raises the question of how this definition relates
to the concept of fractional powers,
such as the one defined for
C_{0}
-semigroup generators in Appendix
C
.
In general, we cannot define fractional powers
of the operator
\partial_{t}+\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{R}}
itself since it may fail to be closed,
which would exclude the applicability of both Definition
C.1

(since generators of
C_{0}
-semigroups are closed
[
37
, Chapter 1, Corollary 2.5]
)
and more general definitions
(see the introduction of
[
32
]
).
In fact, by the operator sum approach to maximal
L^{p}
-regularity, see
[
25
, Discussion 1.18]
,

\partial_{t}+\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{R}}
being closed is equivalent
A
having maximal
L^{p}
-regularity.
In the Hilbert space setting, this is equivalent to
-A
generating a bounded analytic semigroup
(i.e., Assumptions
4.1
and
4.2
(ii)
), see
[
25
, Corollary 1.7]
.

However, Proposition
C.4
shows that
the closure
\mathcal{B}:=\overline{\partial_{t}+\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{R}}}
exists and does admit
fractional powers
\mathcal{B}^{\gamma}
in the sense of Definition
C.1

for all
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
. In particular,
\mathcal{B}
is boundedly invertible and in fact
we have
\mathcal{B}^{-\gamma}=\mathfrak{I}^{\gamma}
for
\gamma\in[0,\infty)
.
By
[
16
, Proposition 3.2.1(b)]
,
it holds that
\mathcal{B}^{\gamma_{1}}\mathcal{B}^{\gamma_{2}}\subseteq\mathcal{B}^{\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}}

for all
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\mathsf{D}(\mathcal{B}^{\gamma_{1}}\mathcal{B}^{\gamma_{2}})=\mathsf{D}(\mathcal{B}^{\gamma_{2}})\cap\mathsf{D}(\mathcal{B}^{\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}})
.
We thus recover (
4.6
) and find

\forall\gamma\in[0,\infty):\quad\mathfrak{D}^{\gamma}=(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{R}})^{\lceil\gamma\rceil}\mathfrak{I}^{\lceil\gamma\rceil-\gamma}\subseteq\mathcal{B}^{\lceil\gamma\rceil}\mathfrak{I}^{\lceil\gamma\rceil-\gamma}=\mathcal{B}^{\lceil\gamma\rceil}\mathcal{B}^{\gamma-\lceil\gamma\rceil}=\mathcal{B}^{\gamma}.

It follows that
\mathfrak{D}^{\gamma}
and

\mathfrak{I}^{\gamma}
are inverses of each other
in the sense that
\mathfrak{D}^{\gamma}\mathfrak{I}^{\gamma}f=f

and
\mathfrak{I}^{\gamma}\mathfrak{D}^{\gamma}f=f

for all
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R};U)
such that the respective left-hand sides exist.
Therefore,
u:=\mathfrak{I}^{\gamma}f

is the unique solution to the equation
\mathfrak{D}^{\gamma}u=f

whenever it belongs to
\mathsf{D}(\mathfrak{D}^{\gamma})
(which may fail if
\partial_{t}+\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{R}}
is not closed).
Relaxing this assumption, we shall call
u\in C(\mathbb{R};U)

a
mild solution
to (
4.4
) if it is given by the formula

(4.10)

\forall t\in\mathbb{R}:\quad u(t)=\mathfrak{I}^{\gamma}f(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\int_{-\infty}^{t}(t-s)^{\gamma-1}S(t-s)f(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

4.3.
Mild solution process

Combining the spatiotemporal fractional integration theory from Subsection
4.2

with the stochastic integral defined in Subsection
2.2
,
we can give a rigorous definition for the mild solution to (
4.1
).
We first introduce the notion of predictability for a stochastic process indexed by
\mathbb{R}
.

Definition 4.4
.

An
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
-adapted
U
-valued process
(X(t))_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
is
predictable

with respect to
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}

if the mapping
(t,\omega)\mapsto X(t,\omega)

is strongly measurable with respect to the
predictable
\sigma
-algebra

\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{R}\times\Omega}:=\sigma((s,t]\times F_{s}:s<t,\,F_{s}\in\mathcal{F}_{s})\vee\sigma(\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{P}}).

Definition 4.5
.

Let Assumption
4.1
be satisfied and
let
\gamma\in(\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},\infty)
be such that
Assumption
4.2
(i)
holds with
\gamma_{0}=\gamma
.
Define the process

Z_{\gamma}=(Z_{\gamma}(t))_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
by

(4.11)

Z_{\gamma}(t):=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\int_{-\infty}^{t}(t-s)^{\gamma-1}S(t-s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(s),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

A
(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\delta W^{Q}})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
-predictable modification of
Z_{\gamma}
is said to be a
mild solution

to (
4.1
).

The first part of the following result
asserts that
Z_{\gamma}
is mean-square continuous.
Since
Z_{\gamma}
is adapted to
\bigl(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\delta W^{Q}}\bigr)_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
,
there does indeed exist a modification of
Z_{\gamma}
which is predictable with respect
to this filtration, cf.
[
9
, Proposition 3.7(ii)]
, the proof of which
can be generalized to unbounded index sets.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let Assumption
4.1
be satisfied.
Suppose that
t_{0}\in[-\infty,\infty)
,
\gamma\in(\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},\infty)

are given.
Define
\mathbb{T}:=[t_{0},\infty)
if
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{T}:=\mathbb{R}
if
t_{0}=-\infty

and let
Assumption
4.2
(i)
hold
for
\gamma_{0}=\gamma
. Then the process
(Z_{\gamma}(t\mid t_{0}))_{t\in\mathbb{T}}
defined by

(4.12)

Z_{\gamma}(t\mid t_{0}):=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}(t-s)^{\gamma-1}S(t-s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(s),\quad t\in\mathbb{T},

where
Z_{\gamma}(\,\cdot\,\mid-\infty):=Z_{\gamma}
, is mean-square continuous on
\mathbb{T}
.

If in addition Assumption
4.2
(ii)
is satisfied and there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}

such that Assumptions
4.2
(i)
holds
for
\gamma_{0}=\gamma-N
,
then
(Z_{\gamma}(t\mid t_{0}))_{t\in\mathbb{T}}
has
N
mean square derivatives and

(4.13)

\forall t\in[t_{0},\infty),\,n\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}\colon\>\left(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}+A\right)\tfrac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}t^{n}}Z_{\gamma}(t\mid t_{0})=\tfrac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}t^{n}}Z_{\gamma-1}(t\mid t_{0}).

Proof.

The mean-square continuity follows directly
by Lemma
B.8
in Appendix
B
,
so we turn to the mean-square differentiability.
We introduce the
auxiliary processes

Z_{\beta,j}(t):=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\beta)}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}(t-s)^{\beta-1}A^{j}S(t-s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(s),\quad t\in[t_{0},\infty),

for
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\beta\in(\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},\infty)

such the right-hand side exists.

We claim
that, under Assumptions
4.2
(i)
–
(ii)

with

\gamma_{0}=\gamma-N
,
the function

{t\mapsto t^{\beta-1}A^{j}S(t)Q^{\frac{1}{2}}}

belongs to

H^{1}_{0,\{0\}}(0,\infty;\mathscr{L}_{2}(U))

if

\beta-j-\gamma+N\in[1,\infty)
.
To this end, we first note that the product rule for the (classical) derivative yields

\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}t^{\beta-1}A^{j}S(t)Q^{\frac{1}{2}}=(\beta-1)t^{\beta-2}A^{j}S(t)Q^{\frac{1}{2}}-t^{\beta-1}A^{j+1}S(t)Q^{\frac{1}{2}}

with values in
\mathscr{L}(U)
for all
t\in(0,\infty)
.
Combining (
4.3
) with an argument involving a change of variables and the semigroup property
(cf. the proof of Lemma
B.4
in Appendix
B
),
one can show that the
L^{2}(0,\infty;\mathscr{L}_{2}(U))
-norms of
the two functions on the right-hand side
can be estimated by that of
t\mapsto t^{\beta-j-2}S(t)Q^{\frac{1}{2}}
,
which is finite since
\beta-j-1\geq\gamma_{0}
.
Again by (
4.3
), we have

\|t^{\beta-1}A^{j}S(t)Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\|_{\mathscr{L}(U)}\leq M_{j}t^{\beta-j-1}\|Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\|_{\mathscr{L}(U)}\to 0\quad\text{as }t\downarrow 0

since
\beta-j-1\geq\gamma_{0}>0
. Noting that
\mathscr{L}_{2}(U)\hookrightarrow\mathscr{L}(U)

and using
[
24
, Lemma A.9]
then proves the claim.

Thus, we may apply Lemma
B.8
from Appendix
B
,
write the result as two separate integrals,
and pull the
closed operator
A
out of the stochastic integral defining

Z_{\beta,j+1}
(cf.
[
9
, Proposition 4.30]
) to find

(4.14)

\displaystyle Z_{\beta,j}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=Z_{\beta-1,j}(t)-Z_{\beta,j+1}(t).

(4.15)

\displaystyle=Z_{\beta-1,j}(t)-AZ_{\beta,j}(t).

Rearranging equation (
4.15
) for
\beta=\gamma
and
j=0

implies (
4.13
) in the case
n=0
.
Applying (
4.14
) iteratively, we find
that
Z_{\beta,j}
has the
n
th mean-square derivative

(4.16)

Z_{\beta,j}^{(n)}(t)=\sum_{m=0}^{n}(-1)^{m}{n\choose m}Z_{\beta-n+m,j+m}(t),

provided that
\beta-j-\gamma+N\in[n,\infty)
.
Now we again let
\beta=\gamma
and
j=0

and apply (
4.15
)
with

\beta^{\prime}=\gamma-n+m
and

j^{\prime}=m
to each term on the right-hand side to derive (
4.13
) for the remaining values of
n
.
∎

The statistical relevance of the process
Z_{\gamma}
is motivated by the following fact,
which demonstrates a situation in which it has a marginal
temporal covariance
structure of Matérn type. Let
(Q_{Z_{\gamma}}(s,t))_{s,t\in\mathbb{R}}\subseteq\mathscr{L}_{1}^{+}(U)
denote
the covariance operators of
Z_{\gamma}
,
defined in general via the relation

(4.17)

\langle Q_{Z_{\gamma}}(s,t)x,y\rangle_{U}=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle Z_{\gamma}(s)-\mathbb{E}\bigl[Z_{\gamma}(s)\bigr],x\right\rangle_{U}\left\langle Z_{\gamma}(t)-\mathbb{E}[Z_{\gamma}(t)],y\right\rangle_{U}\right]

for all
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
and
x,y\in U
.
Note that
\mathbb{E}\bigl[Z_{\gamma}(\,\cdot\,)\bigr]\equiv 0
in this case.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
\gamma\in(\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},\infty)
,
A:=\kappa\operatorname{Id}_{U}
with
{\kappa\in(0,\infty)}
and
suppose that
Assumption
4.2
(i)
is satisfied for
\gamma_{0}=\gamma
.
Then the covariance of
Z_{\gamma}
is
separable and its temporal part
is of Matérn type,
i.e.,

(4.18)

\forall s,t\in\mathbb{R},\,s\neq t:\quad Q_{Z_{\gamma}}(s,t)=\frac{2^{\frac{1}{2}-\gamma}\kappa^{1-2\gamma}}{\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma(\gamma)}(\kappa|t-s|)^{\gamma-\frac{1}{2}}K_{\gamma-\frac{1}{2}}(\kappa|t-s|)\,Q.

Proof.

For
A=\kappa\operatorname{Id}_{U}
, Assumption
4.1
is trivially satisfied and
the definition of
Z_{\gamma}
takes on the following form for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
:

(4.19)

Z_{\gamma}(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\int_{-\infty}^{t}(t-r)^{\gamma-1}e^{-\kappa(t-r)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(r)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}k_{\gamma,\kappa}(t-r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(r),

with real-valued convolution kernel

k_{\gamma,\kappa}(t):=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\gamma)}t^{\gamma-1}_{+}e^{-\kappa t}
,
where
t_{+}^{\gamma-1}:=t^{\gamma-1}
if
t\in[0,\infty)
and
t_{+}^{\gamma-1}:=0
otherwise.
Define the linear operator
\widetilde{k}(s,r;x)\in\mathscr{L}(U;\mathbb{R})

for
s,r\in\mathbb{R}
and
x\in U

by

\widetilde{k}(s,r;x)h:=k_{\gamma,\kappa}(s-r)\langle x,h\rangle_{U},\quad h\in U.

Then combining the Itô isometry (
2.2
) and the polarization identity yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\langle Z_{\gamma}(s),x\rangle_{U}\,\langle Z_{\gamma}(t),y\rangle_{U}]=\mathbb{E}\biggl[\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widetilde{k}(s,r;x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(r)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widetilde{k}(t,r;y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(r)\biggr]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle\widetilde{k}(s,r;x)Q^{\frac{1}{2}},\widetilde{k}(t,r;y)Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\rangle_{\mathscr{L}_{2}(U;\mathbb{R})}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r=\biggl\langle\int_{\mathbb{R}}k_{\gamma,\kappa}(s-r)k_{\gamma,\kappa}(t-r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\,Qx,y\biggr\rangle_{U}.

Since
x,y\in U
were arbitrary,
we find
for all
h\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
the covariance operators

(4.20)

Q_{Z_{\gamma}}(t+h,t)=Q_{Z_{\gamma}}(t,t+h)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}k_{\gamma,\kappa}(t-r)k_{\gamma,\kappa}(t+h-r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\,Q.

Using the change of variables
u(r):=h+2(t-r)
in the integral, we obtain

(4.21)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle k_{\gamma,\kappa}(t-r)k_{\gamma,\kappa}(t+h-r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}k_{\gamma,\kappa}(\tfrac{u-h}{2})k_{\gamma,\kappa}(\tfrac{u+h}{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{1-2\gamma}}{[\Gamma(\gamma)]^{2}}\int_{|h|}^{\infty}(u^{2}-h^{2})^{\gamma-1}e^{-\kappa u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u=\frac{2^{\frac{1}{2}-\gamma}\kappa^{1-2\gamma}}{\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma(\gamma)}(\kappa|h|)^{\gamma-\frac{1}{2}}K_{\gamma-\frac{1}{2}}(\kappa|h|),

where the last identity follows by
[
35
, Part I, Equation (3.13)]
.
∎

Proposition 4.8

By (

Step 1: Well-definedness of

For a monotone class argument,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle[\varphi(\mathbf{Z}_{N}(t\mid s,\bm{\xi}))\mid\mathcal{F}_{s}]=\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\varphi(\mathbf{Z}_{N}(t\mid s,\mathbf{x}_{k}))\mathbf{1}_{A_{k}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{s}]

\displaystyle=\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\varphi(\mathbf{Z}_{N}(t\mid s,\mathbf{x}_{k}))]\mathbf{1}_{A_{k}}=\mathbb{E}[\varphi(\mathbf{Z}_{N}(t\mid s,\bm{\xi}))]=[T_{s,t}\varphi](\bm{\xi}),\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

This shows the desired property for simple
\bm{\xi}
.
For a general
\bm{\xi}\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{s},\mathbb{P};U^{N})
,
we can find a sequence
(\bm{\xi}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of

\mathcal{F}_{s}
-measurable,
U^{N}
-valued simple
random variables such that
\bm{\xi}_{n}\to\bm{\xi}
in
L^{2}(\Omega;U^{N})
.
By the continuity of the mapping

\bm{\xi}\mapsto\mathbf{Z}_{N}(t\mid s,\bm{\xi})
on
L^{2}(\Omega;U^{N})

and of
\varphi
,
we have
{\varphi(\mathbf{Z}_{N}(t\mid s,\bm{\xi}_{n}))\to\varphi(\mathbf{Z}_{N}(t\mid s,\bm{\xi}))}

in
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
. From this, one can derive that

\mathbb{E}[\varphi(\mathbf{Z}_{N}(t\mid s,\bm{\xi}_{n}))\mid\mathcal{F}_{s}](\omega)\to\mathbb{E}[\varphi(\mathbf{Z}_{N}(t\mid s,\bm{\xi}))\mid\mathcal{F}_{s}](\omega),

\mathbb{P}
-a.s., passing to a subsequence if needed.
On the other hand, we can pass to a further subsequence
in order to assume that
\bm{\xi}_{n}(\omega)\to\bm{\xi}(\omega)
in
U^{N}
,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.,
and deduce that
[T_{s,t}\varphi](\bm{\xi}_{n}(\omega))\to[T_{s,t}\varphi](\bm{\xi}(\omega))
in
\mathbb{R}
,
finishing the density argument.

Finally, the statement regarding
(Z_{N}(t))_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
follows from Proposition
4.8
.
∎

4.4.3.
Non-Markovianity in the fractional case

We conclude this section by showing how
Theorem
3.8
can be used
to deduce that
Z_{\gamma}
is not weakly Markov (see Definition
3.5
)
if
\gamma\not\in\mathbb{N}
. To this end, we determine
the coloring operator of
Z_{\gamma}
.

Proposition 4.14
.

Let
Assumption
4.1
be
satisfied and suppose that
\gamma\in(\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},\infty)

is such that Assumption
4.2
(i)

holds for
\gamma_{0}=\gamma
.
Then
the coloring operator of

Z_{\gamma}
, see (
3.16
), is given by

\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{\gamma}=\mathfrak{I}^{\gamma}\mathcal{Q}_{\mathbb{R}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\in\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R};U))
,
where
\mathfrak{I}^{\gamma}
is as in (
4.5
).

Proof.

Note that for each
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};U)
, we have

(4.48)

\displaystyle\langle Z_{\gamma},f\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};U)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{-\infty}^{t}\langle(t-s)^{\gamma-1}S(t-s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(s),f(t)\rangle_{U}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,

(4.49)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widetilde{\Psi}_{f}(t,s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.},

where
\widetilde{\Psi}_{f}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathscr{L}(U;\mathbb{R})
is given by

(4.50)

\widetilde{\Psi}_{f}(t,s)u:=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\langle(t-s)^{\gamma-1}S(t-s)u,f(t)\rangle_{U}\,\mathbf{1}_{(-\infty,t)}(s),\quad t,s\in\mathbb{R},\,u\in U.

Indeed, the latter integral is well-defined since

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle\biggl[\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\|\widetilde{\Psi}_{f}(t,s)Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{\mathscr{L}_{2}(U;\mathbb{R})}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\biggr]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\biggl[\int_{-\infty}^{t}\|(t-s)^{\gamma-1}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}[S(t-s)]^{*}f(t)\|_{U}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\biggr]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\biggl[\int_{0}^{\infty}\|s^{\gamma-1}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}[S(s)]^{*}\|_{\mathscr{L}_{2}(U)}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\biggr]^{\frac{1}{2}}\biggl[\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|f(t)\|_{U}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\biggr]<\infty;

note that the first integral on the last line is finite by Assumption
4.2
(i)

and the second by the compact support of
f
.
This also shows that Theorem
2.1
is applicable, hence

(4.51)

\displaystyle\langle Z_{\gamma},f\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};U)}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widetilde{\Psi}_{f}(t,s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(s)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widetilde{\Phi}_{f}(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(s),\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.},

where
\widetilde{\Phi}_{f}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathscr{L}(U;\mathbb{R})
is given by

(4.52)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Phi}_{f}(s)u:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widetilde{\Psi}_{f}(t,s)u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\int_{s}^{\infty}\langle(t-s)^{\gamma-1}S(t-s)u,f(t)\rangle_{U}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

(4.53)

\displaystyle=\langle u,\mathfrak{I}^{\gamma*}f(s)\rangle_{U},\quad\forall s\in\mathbb{R},\,u\in U,

where we recall equation (
4.7
) for the adjoint of
\mathfrak{I}^{\gamma}
.
Consequently, the Itô isometry (
2.2
) yields

(4.54)

\|\langle Z_{\gamma},f\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};U)}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|\widetilde{\Phi}_{f}(t)Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\|_{\mathscr{L}_{2}(U;\mathbb{R})}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\|\mathcal{Q}_{\mathbb{R}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathfrak{I}^{\gamma*}f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};U)}^{2}.

An application of the polarization identity then shows
the coloring property (
3.16
)
with
\mathcal{L}^{-*}_{\gamma}=\mathcal{Q}_{\mathbb{R}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathfrak{I}^{\gamma*}
.
∎

Example 4.15
.

Let
Assumptions
4.1
and

5.
Fractional
Q
-Wiener process

In this section we consider the
fractional
Q
-Wiener process
,
which is a
U
-valued analog to
fractional Brownian motion;
we define it as in
[
12
, Definition 2.1]
.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
Q\in\mathscr{L}_{1}^{+}(U)
.
A
U
-valued Gaussian process
(W^{Q}_{H}(t))_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
is called a
fractional
Q
-Wiener process
with Hurst parameter
H\in(0,1)
if

(f-WP1)

\mathbb{E}[W^{Q}_{H}(t)]=0
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
;

(f-WP2)

Q_{H}(s,t)=\frac{1}{2}(|t|^{2H}+|s|^{2H}-|t-s|^{2H})Q
for all
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
;

(f-WP3)

W^{Q}_{H}
has continuous sample paths.

Here,
(Q_{H}(s,t))_{s,t\in\mathbb{R}}\subseteq\mathscr{L}_{1}^{+}(U)

are the covariance operators of
W^{Q}_{H}
,
cf. (
4.17
).

Note that for
H=\frac{1}{2}
, the above definition reduces to a characterization of a standard
(non-fractional)
Q
-Wiener process when restricted to
[0,\infty)
.

In the real-valued setting, the fractional Brownian motion
can also be represented as a stochastic integral over
\mathbb{R}

[
31
, Definition 2.1]
.
Proposition
5.2
below
shows that such a representation is also available in the
U
-valued setting
using the stochastic integral over
\mathbb{R}
from Section
2.2
.
This representation can be leveraged to prove that a fractional
Q
-Wiener process
can be viewed as a limiting case of the class of processes discussed in Section
4
,
see Proposition
5.3
.
We conclude this section by making some remarks about the Markov behavior of
W^{Q}_{H}
, see
Subsection
5.2
.

5.1.
Integral representation and relation to
Z_{\gamma}

Let
Q\in\mathscr{L}_{1}^{+}(U)
and let
(W^{Q}(t))_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
be a two-sided
Q
-Wiener process, see
Subsection
2.2
.
We define the process
(\widehat{W}^{Q}_{H}(t))_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
via
the following Mandelbrot–Van Ness
[
31
]
type integral:

(5.1)

\widehat{W}_{H}^{Q}(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}K_{H}(t,r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(r),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

where the
integration kernel
K_{H}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

(5.2)

K_{H}(t,r):=\frac{(t-r)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}_{+}-(-r)_{+}^{H-\frac{1}{2}}}{C_{H}},\quad(t,r)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

The normalizing constant
C_{H}
is chosen as

C_{H}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Bigl|(1-r)_{+}^{H-\frac{1}{2}}-(-r)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}_{+}\Bigr|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r=\frac{3-2H}{4H}\mathrm{B}(2-2H,H+\tfrac{1}{2}),

where
\mathrm{B}
denotes the beta function
[
39
, Theorem B.1]
,
so that
\widehat{Q}_{H}(1,1)=Q
, where
(\widehat{Q}_{H}(t,s))_{t,s\in\mathbb{R}}

denotes the family of covariance operators associated to
\widehat{W}^{Q}_{H}
.
From this definition it follows that

(5.3)

\widehat{W}^{Q}_{H}(t)=\frac{1}{C_{H}}\int_{-\infty}^{0}[(t-r)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}-(-r)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(r)+\frac{1}{C_{H}}\int_{0}^{t}(t-r)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(r)

for
t\in[0,\infty)
, whereas for
t\in(-\infty,0)
we have

(5.4)

\widehat{W}^{Q}_{H}(t)=\frac{1}{C_{H}}\int_{-\infty}^{t}[(t-r)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}-(-r)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(r)+\frac{1}{C_{H}}\int_{t}^{0}(-r)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(r).

In particular, for
H=\frac{1}{2}
and
t\in[0,\infty)

we find
\widehat{W}^{Q}_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(t)=W^{Q}(t)
,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.

We then have the following relation to the fractional
Q
-Wiener process:

Proposition 5.2
.

Formula (
5.1
) yields a well-defined square-integrable
random variable

\widehat{W}^{Q}_{H}(t)\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\delta W^{Q}},\mathbb{P};U)
for each
t\in\mathbb{R}
, and there exists a modification of
the process
(\widehat{W}^{Q}_{H}(t))_{t\in\mathbb{R}}

which is a fractional
Q
-Wiener process in the sense of Definition
5.1
.

Proof.

By the Itô isometry (
2.2
) it suffices to note that

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\bigl\|\widehat{W}^{Q}_{H}(t)\bigr\|_{U}^{2}\Bigr]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|K_{H}(t,r)Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\|_{\mathscr{L}_{2}(U)}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r=\operatorname{tr}Q\int_{\mathbb{R}}|K_{H}(t,r)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r<\infty

to see that
\widehat{W}^{Q}_{H}(t)\in L^{2}(\Omega;U)
is well-defined.
Since
K_{H}
is a deterministic kernel integrated with respect to a mean-zero Gaussian
process
W^{Q}
, it readily follows that
\widehat{W}^{Q}_{H}
is also mean-zero Gaussian.
For the covariance operators
(\widehat{Q}_{H}(t,s))_{t,s\in\mathbb{R}}

of
\widehat{W}^{Q}_{H}
, we can argue as in the proof of Proposition
4.7
to find

(5.5)

\forall t,s\in\mathbb{R}:\quad\widehat{Q}_{H}(t,s)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}K_{H}(s,r)K_{H}(t,r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\,Q=\mathbb{E}[B_{H}(t)B_{H}(s)]\,Q,

where
B_{H}=(B_{H}(t))_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
denotes (real-valued) fractional Brownian motion.
Hence
(f-WP2)
holds by the properties of
B_{H}
.

Lastly, we shall establish the existence of a continuous modification of (
5.1
).
To this end, we first remark that

\widehat{W}^{Q}_{H}
is self-similar with exponent
H
and that its increments are stationary;
this means for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
we have, respectively,

(5.6)

\displaystyle\forall\alpha\in(0,\infty)

\displaystyle:\quad\widehat{W}^{Q}_{H}(\alpha t)\overset{d}{=}\alpha^{H}\widehat{W}^{Q}_{H}(t),

(5.7)

\displaystyle\forall h\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle:\quad\widehat{W}^{Q}_{H}(t+h)-\widehat{W}^{Q}_{H}(t)\overset{d}{=}\widehat{W}^{Q}_{H}(h).

The proofs of these statements are analogous to the real-valued case;
they involve changing variables in the integral (
5.1
) and using the facts that

\alpha^{-\frac{1}{2}}W^{Q}(\alpha\,\cdot\,)
and

W^{Q}(\,\cdot\,+h)
, respectively, are also two-sided
Q
-Wiener processes.
Thus,

(5.8)

\displaystyle\bigl\|\widehat{W}^{Q}_{H}(t+h)-\widehat{W}^{Q}_{H}(t)\bigr\|_{L^{p}(\Omega;U)}=\bigl\|\widehat{W}^{Q}_{H}(|h|)\bigr\|_{L^{p}(\Omega;U)}=|h|^{H}\bigl\|\widehat{W}^{Q}_{H}(1)\bigr\|_{L^{p}(\Omega;U)}

for any
p\in(1,\infty)
.
By the Kahane–Khintchine inequalities (see, e.g.,
[
20
, Theorem 6.2.6]
),
there exists a constant
C_{p}\in(0,\infty)
such that

\bigl\|\widehat{W}^{Q}_{H}(1)\bigr\|_{L^{p}(\Omega;U)}\leq C_{p}\bigl\|\widehat{W}^{Q}_{H}(1)\bigr\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;U)}=C_{p}\operatorname{tr}Q.

We have thus shown that
t\mapsto\widehat{W}^{Q}_{H}(t)

is
H
-Hölder continuous from
\mathbb{R}
to
L^{p}(\Omega;U)
.
Since
p\in(1,\infty)
was arbitrary, we can apply
the Kolmogorov–Chentsov theorem
[
8
, Corollary 3.10]

with
p\in(\nicefrac{{1}}{{H}},\infty)
to conclude
that
\widehat{W}^{Q}_{H}
has a modification with continuous sample paths.
∎

Now we consider the relation between fractional
Q
-Wiener process
and the process
Z_{\gamma}
considered in Section
4
.
For
\varepsilon\in(0,\infty)
, let
Z_{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}
denote the mild solution
to (
4.1
) with
A=\varepsilon\operatorname{Id}_{U}

and define the process

\bigl(\overline{Z}_{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(t)\bigr)_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
by

(5.9)

\overline{Z}_{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(t):=\frac{\Gamma(\gamma)}{C_{\gamma-\frac{1}{2}}}(Z_{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(t)-Z_{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(0)),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Note that

\widehat{W}^{Q}_{H}(t)
can formally be written as a
“convergent difference of divergent integrals,” cf.
[
31
, Footnote 3]
:

\frac{1}{C_{H}}\biggl[\int_{-\infty}^{t}(t-s)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(s)-\int_{-\infty}^{0}(-s)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(s)\biggr].

The above expression would correspond to taking
\varepsilon=0
in (
5.9
),
which is ill-defined
since Assumption
4.2
(i)
cannot be satisfied.
However, the next result shows that a fractional
Q
-Wiener process
can be viewed as a limiting case of
\overline{Z}_{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
Q\in\mathscr{L}_{1}^{+}(U)
and
\gamma\in(\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}})
.
The family of stochastic processes
(\overline{Z}_{\gamma}^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,\infty)}

defined by (
5.9
) converges uniformly
on compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}
in mean-square sense to the fractional

Q
-Wiener process
\widehat{W}^{Q}_{H}
in (
5.1
)
with Hurst parameter

H=\gamma-\frac{1}{2}
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
:

(5.10)

\forall\,T\in(0,\infty):\quad\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\sup_{t\in[-T,T]}\left\|\widehat{W}^{Q}_{\gamma-\frac{1}{2}}(t)-\overline{Z}_{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;U)}=0.

Proof.

For
t\in[0,\infty)
, we can write

\displaystyle\widehat{W}^{Q}_{\gamma-\frac{1}{2}}(t)

\displaystyle-\overline{Z}_{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(t)=\frac{1}{C_{\gamma-\frac{1}{2}}}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{\gamma-1}\bigl(1-e^{-\varepsilon(t-s)}\bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(s)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{C_{\gamma-\frac{1}{2}}}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\left[(t-s)^{\gamma-1}\bigl(1-e^{-\varepsilon(t-s)}\bigr)-(-s)^{\gamma-1}\bigl(1-e^{\varepsilon s}\bigr)\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(s).

After applying the Itô isometry to each of these integrals
and using the respective changes of variables
s^{\prime}:=-s
and
s^{\prime}:=t-s
, we obtain

(5.11)

\displaystyle\biggl\|\widehat{W}^{Q}_{\gamma-\frac{1}{2}}(t)

\displaystyle-\overline{Z}_{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(t)\biggr\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;U)}^{2}=\frac{I_{1}(t)+I_{2}(t)}{C_{\gamma-\frac{1}{2}}}(\operatorname{tr}Q)^{2},

(5.12)

\displaystyle I_{1}(t)

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{|t|}s^{2\gamma-2}\bigl(1-e^{-\varepsilon s}\bigr)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s,

(5.13)

\displaystyle I_{2}(t)

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|(|t|+s)^{\gamma-1}\bigl(1-e^{-\varepsilon(|t|+s)}\bigr)-s^{\gamma-1}\bigl(1-e^{-\varepsilon s}\bigr)\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

For
t\in(-\infty,0)
we find (
5.11
)
by instead splitting into integrals over

(-\infty,t)
and
(t,0)
and changing variables

s^{\prime}:=t-s
and
s^{\prime}:=-s
, respectively.

For
t\in[-T,T]
, the estimate
1-e^{-x}\leq x
for
x\in[0,\infty)
yields for the first term:

\displaystyle I_{1}(t)\leq\varepsilon^{2}\int_{0}^{\lvert t\rvert}s^{2\gamma}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\lvert t\rvert^{2\gamma+1}}{2\gamma+1}\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}T^{2\gamma+1}}{2\gamma+1}.

For
I_{2}
, we apply the fundamental theorem of calculus to the function

u\mapsto(u+s)^{\gamma-1}(1-e^{-\varepsilon(u+s)}),

followed by
Minkowski’s integral inequality
[
44
, Section A.1]
to find

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\biggl|\int_{0}^{\lvert t\rvert}[(\gamma-1)(u+s)^{\gamma-2}(1-e^{-\varepsilon(u+s)})+\varepsilon(u+s)^{\gamma-1}e^{-\varepsilon(u+s)}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\biggr|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\Biggl(\int_{0}^{T}\biggl[\int_{0}^{\infty}\Bigl|(\gamma-1)(u+s)^{\gamma-2}(1-e^{-\varepsilon(u+s)})+\varepsilon(u+s)^{\gamma-1}e^{-\varepsilon(u+s)}\Bigr|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\biggr]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\Biggr)^{2}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{3-2\gamma}\Biggl(\int_{0}^{T}\biggl[\int_{\varepsilon u}^{\infty}\Bigl|(\gamma-1)v^{\gamma-2}(1-e^{-v})+v^{\gamma-1}e^{-v}\Bigr|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v\biggr]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\Biggr)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{3-2\gamma}\,T^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Bigl|(\gamma-1)v^{\gamma-2}(1-e^{-v})+v^{\gamma-1}e^{-v}\Bigr|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v,

where we performed the change of variables
v(s):=\varepsilon(u+s)
on the third line.

The improper integral on the last line converges:
As
v\downarrow 0
, the squares of both terms are of order
\mathcal{O}(v^{2\gamma-2})
, where
we again use
1-e^{-v}\leq v
for the first term,
and we have
2\gamma-2\in(-1,1)
;
the square of the first term is of order
\mathcal{O}(v^{2\gamma-4})
as
v\to\infty
,
with
2\gamma-4\in(-3,-1)
, whereas the second term decays exponentially.

The convergence thus follows by letting

\varepsilon\downarrow 0
in the previous two displays.
∎

5.2.
Remarks on Markov behavior

Now we consider the Markov behavior of fractional
Q
-Wiener processes with Hurst parameter
H\in(0,1)
.
Since the case
H=\frac{1}{2}
corresponds to a standard
Q
-Wiener process, we find that
W^{Q}_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}

is simple Markov, whereas we can expect that
W^{Q}_{H}
is not weakly Markov
for
H\neq\frac{1}{2}
.

In the real-valued case, the first published proof of non-Markovianity
appears to be
[
19
]
, which shows that
B_{H}
is not simple Markov for
H\neq\frac{1}{2}

using a characterization
in terms of its covariance function which is valid for Gaussian processes.
This result can be improved by applying the theory of
[
18
, Chapter V]
for Gaussian
N
-ple Markov processes
to the Mandelbrot–Van Ness representation of
B_{H}
. Namely,
according to
[
18
, Theorem 5.1]
,
any real-valued Gaussian process of the form

\biggl(\int_{-\infty}^{t}K(t,s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}B(s)\biggr)_{t\in\mathbb{R}}

can only be
N
-ple Markov for
N\in\mathbb{N}
if its integration kernel
F
can
be written as

K(s,t)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}f_{j}(s)g_{j}(t)

for some functions
(f_{j})_{j=1}^{N},(g_{j})_{j=1}^{N}
. This condition is not
satisfied for
K_{H}
from (
5.2
).

In order to establish that
W^{Q}_{H}
(or
B_{H}
)
does not have the weak Markov property for
H\neq\frac{1}{2}
,
one could attempt to associate a
nonlocal precision operator to the process and apply the necessary condition
from Theorem
3.8
.
Formally, the coloring operator
\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{H}

of
W^{Q}_{H}
acts on functions
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to U
as

\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{H}f(t)=\frac{1}{C_{H}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}K_{H}(t,s)f(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s,\quad\forall t\in\mathbb{R}.

For certain ranges of
H
, see for instance
[
39
, Equation (31)]
,
an explicit formula of
its inverse
\mathcal{L}_{H}
can also be determined.
The operator

\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{H}
is bounded on some
weighted Hölder space by
[
39
, Theorem 6]
,
but there is no reason to expect that it is bounded on
a Hilbert space such as
L^{2}(\mathbb{R};U)
. Therefore,
Theorem
3.8
is not directly applicable, as
it
would need to be extended to the Banach space setting,
which is beyond the scope of this work.

Appendix A
Spaces of vector-valued functions

A.1.
Spaces of measurable, continuous and differentiable functions

Let
(S,\mathscr{A},\mu)
be a measure space.
We abbreviate the phrases
“almost everywhere” and
“almost all”
by
“a.e.” and “a.a.”​,
respectively.

Let
\mathcal{B}(E)
denote the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of
E
.
Given
x\in E
and
f\colon S\to\mathbb{R}
,
we define
f\otimes x\colon S\to E
by
[f\otimes x](\,\cdot\,):=f(\,\cdot\,)x
.
A function
f\colon(S,\mathscr{A},\mu)\to(E,\mathcal{B}(E))

is said to be
\mu
-strongly measurable if it is the
\mu
-a.e. limit
of linear combinations of
\mathbf{1}_{A}\otimes x
, where

x\in E
and
A\in\mathscr{A}
with
\mu(A)<\infty
.
We denote by
B_{b}(S,\mathscr{A})
(resp.
C_{b}(S)
) the Banach space of

\mathscr{A}
-measurable (resp. continuous) and bounded functions
f\colon S\to\mathbb{R}
endowed with the
norm
\|f\|_{B_{b}(S,\mathscr{A})}=\|f\|_{C_{b}(S)}=\sup_{s\in S}|f(s)|
.
If the
\sigma
-algebra is clear from context, we may simply write
B_{b}(S)
.
The set
C_{c}^{\infty}(J;E)
consists of infinitely differentiable functions
from
J
to
E
with compact support.

A.2.
Bochner and Sobolev spaces

For
p\in[1,\infty)
, let
L^{p}(S,\mathscr{A}\!,\mu;E)
denote the Bochner space of
(equivalence classes of)
strongly measurable,

p
-integrable functions
equipped with the norm

\|f\|_{L^{p}(S,\mathscr{A}\!,\mu;E)}:=\left(\int_{S}\|f(s)\|_{E}^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(s)\right)^{\!\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}}\!\!.

If the
\sigma
-algebra and measure are clear from context,
we may suppress them from our notation and simply write
L^{p}(S;E)
.
The space
L^{2}(S,\mathscr{A}\!,\mu;U)
is a Hilbert space
when equipped with the inner product

\langle f,g\rangle_{L^{2}(S,\mathscr{A}\!,\mu;U)}:=\int_{S}\langle f(s),g(s)\rangle_{U}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(s)
.

If
(S,\mathscr{A}\!,\mu)
and
(T,\mathscr{B},\nu)
are
\sigma
-finite measure spaces,
then the measurable space
(S\times T,\mathscr{A}\otimes\mathscr{B})
,
equipped with the product
\sigma
-algebra

\mathscr{A}\otimes\mathscr{B}:=\sigma(A\times B:A\in\mathscr{A},B\in\mathscr{B}),

admits a unique
\sigma
-finite
product measure
\mu\times\nu

satisfying

(\mu\times\nu)(A\times B)=\mu(A)\nu(B)\quad\forall A\in\mathscr{A},\,B\in\mathscr{B}.

For
p,q\in[1,\infty)
, we define the
mixed-exponent Bochner space

L^{p,q}(S\times T;E)
as the Banach space of (equivalence classes of)
strongly
(\mu\times\nu)
-measurable functions
f\colon S\times T\to E

such that

(A.1)

\|f\|_{L^{p,q}(S\times T;E)}:=\biggl[\int_{S}\biggl[\int_{T}\|f(s,t)\|_{E}^{q}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(t)\biggr]^{\frac{p}{q}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(s)\biggr]^{\frac{1}{p}}<\infty.

Intervals
J\subseteq\mathbb{R}
will be equipped with the Lebesgue measure
\lambda

and Lebesgue
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}(J)\vee\sigma(\mathcal{N}_{\lambda})
.
A function
f\in L^{p}(J;E)
is said to be weakly differentiable,
with weak derivative
\partial_{t}f
,
if

\int_{J}\phi^{\prime}(t)f(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=-\int_{J}\phi(t)[\partial_{t}f](t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\quad\forall\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(J;\mathbb{R}).

More generally, we denote its
n
th weak derivative by
\partial_{t}^{n}f
.
If
\partial_{t}^{\,j}f\in L^{p}(J;E)

for all
j\in\{0,\dots,n\}
, then
f
belongs to
the Bochner–Sobolev space
W^{n,p}(J;E)
, which is
a Banach space using the norm

\|f\|_{W^{n,p}(J;E)}:=(\sum_{j=0}^{n}\|\partial_{t}^{j}f\|_{L^{p}(J;E)}^{p})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}}
.
If
p=2
and
E=U
, then
H^{n}(J;U):=W^{n,2}(J;U)
is a Hilbert space
whose norm is induced by the
inner product

\langle f,g\rangle_{H^{n}(J;U)}:=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\langle\partial_{t}^{\,j}f,\partial_{t}^{\,j}g\rangle_{L^{2}(J;U)}
.

B.1.

Let

Theorem B.1

(Doob’s conditional independence property)
.

We have

if and only if

(B.1)

\mathbb{P}(G_{2}\mid\mathcal{G}_{1}\vee\mathcal{H})=\mathbb{P}(G_{2}\mid\mathcal{H}),\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.},\quad\forall G_{2}\in\mathcal{G}_{2}.

Lemma B.2
.

If

, then

(i)

\mathcal{G}_{1}\vee\mathcal{H}\perp\!\!\!\perp_{\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{G}_{2}

(ii)

\mathcal{G}_{1}\perp\!\!\!\perp_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}}\mathcal{G}_{2}
for any

-algebra

such that
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{1}

\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{1},\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{2}

-algebras satisfying

and
\mathcal{H}_{2}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{2}\vee\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

Part
(i)
is
[

, Corollary 8.11(i)]
;
combining it with

\mathcal{G}_{1}=\mathcal{G}_{1}\vee\mathcal{H}^{\prime}

and
{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}=\mathcal{H}\vee\mathcal{H}^{\prime}}

.
To prove

\mathcal{G}_{1}\vee\mathcal{H}\perp\!\!\!\perp_{\mathcal{H}\vee\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\prime}}\mathcal{G}_{2}\vee\mathcal{H}

by parts

and

Proposition B.6
.

Let

\mathbb{E}[Z\mid\mathcal{F}^{\delta W^{Q}}_{t+}]=\mathbb{E}[Z\mid\mathcal{F}^{\delta W^{Q}}_{t}],\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.},

for which (

)

\mathcal{F}_{\infty}^{\delta W^{Q}}
is equal to

\sigma\biggl(\prod\nolimits_{(s,u)\in\mathcal{J}}Y_{(s,u)}:\mathcal{J}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\textup{ finite},\,Y_{(s,u)}\in B_{b}(\Omega,\sigma(W_{s,u}))\,\,\forall(s,u)\in\mathcal{J}\!\biggr)\!\vee\sigma(\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{P}}).

Consider

Z:=\prod_{(s,u)\in\mathcal{J}}Y_{(s,u)}

for some finite
\mathcal{J}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
.
Define

Z_{1}:=\prod_{(s,u)\in\mathcal{J}\cap\mathcal{I}_{1}}Y_{(s,u)}

and
Z_{2}:=\prod_{(s,u)\in\mathcal{J}\cap\mathcal{I}_{2}}Y_{(s,u)}
.
Then we have
Z=Z_{1}Z_{2}
, with
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}

as in the definition of
\mathscr{C}
, finishing the proof of the identity

\mathcal{F}_{\infty}^{\delta W^{Q}}=\sigma(\mathscr{C})
.
∎

Proposition B.7
.

A process

(W^{Q}(t))_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
satisfying

(WP1)
cannot be a martingale with respect to any filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
(W^{Q}(t))_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
is a martingale with respect to some filtration
(\mathcal{F})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
.
Then the same holds for the real-valued process
W_{h}^{Q}(t):=\langle W^{Q}(t),h\rangle_{U}
,
where we choose
h\in U
such that
\langle Qh,h\rangle_{U}^{2}>0

to ensure that
(W_{h}^{Q}(t))_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
has nontrivial increments.
In particular,
(W^{Q}_{h}(-n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a backward martingale
with respect to
(\mathcal{F}_{-n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, implying that
it
converges
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. and in
L^{1}(\Omega)
as
n\to\infty

by the backward martingale convergence theorem
[
15
, Section 12.7, Theorem 4]
.
But this contradicts
(WP1)
, since
(W^{Q}_{h}(-n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

cannot be a Cauchy sequence in
L^{1}(\Omega)
as the process
has (non-trivial) stationary increments.
∎

B.4.
Mean-square differentiability of stochastic convolutions

The following lemma regarding
mean-square continuity and differentiation under
the integral sign generalizes
[
24
, Propositions 3.18 and 3.21]
to stochastic convolutions
with respect to a two-sided Wiener process.

Lemma B.8
.

Let
t_{0}\in[-\infty,\infty)
be such that

\Psi(\,\cdot\,)Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\in L^{2}(0,\infty;\mathscr{L}_{2}(U))
and
set
\mathbb{T}:=[t_{0},\infty)
if
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{T}:=\mathbb{R}
if
t_{0}=-\infty
.
Then the stochastic convolution

(\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\Psi(t-s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(s))_{t\in\mathbb{T}}
is mean-square continuous.

If
\Psi(\,\cdot\,)Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\in H^{1}_{0,\{0\}}(0,\infty;\mathscr{L}_{2}(U))
,
then

(\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\Psi(t-s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(s))_{t\in\mathbb{T}}
is mean-square differentiable on
\mathbb{T}

with

(B.6)

\displaystyle\forall t\in\mathbb{T}:\quad\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\Psi(t-s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(s)=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\partial_{t}\Psi(t-s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(s),\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

Proof.

Adapting the proof of
[
24
, Proposition 3.18]

amounts to establishing that

\lim_{h\to 0}\int_{0\vee(-h)}^{\infty}\|[\Psi(u+h)-\Psi(u)]Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\|^{2}_{\mathscr{L}_{2}(U)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u=0,

which holds by the continuity of translations in
L^{2}(0,\infty;\mathscr{L}_{2}(U))
,
see for instance
[
24
, Lemma A.4]
for this specific form.
Similarly, for the mean-square differentiability
we note that compared to the proof of
[
24
, Proposition 3.21]
, only the terms

I_{1}^{h^{\pm}}
change when passing from
t_{0}=0
to a general
t_{0}
.
For
t\in\mathbb{T}
and
h\in(0,\infty)
, we have

(B.7)

I^{h^{+}}_{1}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\biggl[\frac{\Psi(t+h-s)-\Psi(t-s)}{h}-\Psi^{\prime}(t-s)\biggr]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(s),

whereas for
t\in(t_{0},\infty)
and
h\in[t_{0}-t,0)
(or
h\in(-\infty,0)
if
t_{0}=-\infty
),

(B.8)

I^{h^{-}}_{1}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t+h}\biggl[\frac{\Psi(t+h-s)-\Psi(t-s)}{h}-\Psi^{\prime}(t-s)\biggr]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(s).

Using the Itô isometry (
2.2
) and applying the change of variables
u:=t-s
:

(B.9)

\displaystyle\bigl\|I^{h^{\pm}}_{1}\bigr\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega;U)}=\int_{0\vee(-h)}^{t-t_{0}}\,\biggl\|\biggl[\frac{\Psi(u+h)-\Psi(u)}{h}-\Psi^{\prime}(u)\biggr]Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\biggr\|^{2}_{\mathscr{L}_{2}(U)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u.

The proof is finished upon noting that replacing
t
by
t-t_{0}
does not affect the remaining step of the argument.
∎

Appendix C
Fractional powers of the parabolic operator

Let
A\colon\mathsf{D}(A)\subseteq U\to U
be a linear operator
on a real Hilbert space
U
.

Definition C.1
.

Under Assumption
4.1
, we define
the negative fractional power operator
A^{-\alpha}
of order
\alpha\in(0,\infty)

as the
\mathscr{L}(U)
-valued Bochner integral

(C.1)

A^{-\alpha}:=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\alpha-1}S(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

Then
A^{-\alpha}
is injective and we define

A^{\alpha}x:=(A^{-\alpha})^{-1}x
for

x\in\mathsf{D}(A^{\alpha}):=\mathsf{R}(A^{-\alpha})
.
For
\alpha=0
we set
A^{0}:=\operatorname{Id}_{U}
.

See
[
37
, Section 2.6]
for more details regarding
this definition of fractional powers.

Lemma C.2
.

Let
(S,\mathscr{A},\mu)
be a measure space
such that

is nontrivial and consider the linear
operator
\mathcal{A}_{S}
on

).
Then the following statements hold:

(a)

\mathcal{A}_{S}\in\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(S;U))

A\in\mathscr{L}(U)

(b)

\mathcal{A}_{S}
is closed if and only if
A
is;

(c)

If
A
generates a
C_{0}
-semigroup
(T(t))_{t\geq 0}
on
U
,
then

\mathcal{A}_{S}
generates the
C_{0}
-semigroup
(\mathcal{T}_{S}(t))_{t\geq 0}

.

Proof.

For
(a)
–
(b)

one can argue as in the proof of
[
24
, Lemma A.1]
,
replacing
T^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{1}_{(0,T)}

by
another function with unit

-norm.
The proof of

is entirely analogous to
that of
[
24
, Proposition A.3]
.
∎

The next two statements and their proofs are analogous
to Propositions A.5 and 3.2 of
[

]
, respectively.

Proposition C.3
.

For
t\in\mathbb{R}
, let the operator
\mathcal{T}(t)\in\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R};U))

\mathcal{T}(t)f:=f(\,\cdot\,-t),\quad f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R};U).

.

Suppose that Assumption
4.1

\mathcal{B}:=\overline{\partial_{t}+\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{R}}}
