Right Nilpotency of Braces of Cardinality p 4 p^{4}
Dora Puljić

Abstract
We determine right nilpotency of braces of cardinality p 4 p^{4} . If a brace of cardinality p 4 p^{4} has an abelian multiplicative group, then it is left and right nilpotent, so we only consider braces with non-abelian multiplicative groups. We show right nilpotency in all cases using the sufficient condition of A ∗ c = 0 A\ast c=0 for some central element c c of a brace A A .

In 2016, skew braces were introduced by L. Guarnieri and L. Vendramin in

Advancements in the classification of braces have been made: cyclic braces were classified in

2
Right Nilpotent Braces

A
brace
is a triple
(A,+,\circ)
where
(A,+)
is an abelian group,
(A,\circ)
is a group and

a\circ(b+c)+a=a\circ b+a\circ c

for all
a,b,c\in A
. The star operation
\ast
is defined as

a\circ b=a\ast b+a+b.

Then, equivalently, a brace is a triple
(A,+,\ast)
where
(A,+)
is an abelian group,
(A,\ast)
is a group and

a\ast(b+c)=a\ast b+a\ast c

for all
a,b,c\in A
. We will refer to
(A,\circ)
as the multiplicative group of the brace.

A brace is
left nilpotent
if there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
A^{n}=0
, where
A^{i+1}=A\ast A^{i}
and
A^{1}=A
. A brace is
right nilpotent
if there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
A^{(n)}=0
, where
A^{(i+1)}=A^{(i)}\ast A
and
A^{(1)}=A
. A brace is
strongly nilpotent
if there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
A^{[n]}=0
, where
A^{[i+1]}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}A^{[j]}*A^{[i+1-j]}
and
A^{[1]}=A
.

For a brace
A
we will denote the set of elements
c\in A
such that
c\ast a=a\ast c
for all
a\in A
by
Z(A)
, and we will call such elements central.
We will use the following throughout:
By
[
18
]
if a central element
c
of a brace
A
is such that
A\ast c=0
, then
c\ast A=0
. Therefore
c
generates an ideal
I
in
A
such that
I\ast A=A\ast I=0
. By a result of Bachiller’s
[
2
]
, this implies that
A/I
is right nilpotent as it is a brace of cardinality
p^{3}
or less. Hence
A
is right nilpotent. Therefore, to show the brace
A
is right nilpotent it suffices to show that
A\ast c=0
for some
c\in Z(A)
.

We begin with a theorem.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
A
be a brace of cardinality
p^{4}
with
(A,+)
either
C_{p}\times C_{p^{3}}
or
C_{p^{2}}\times C_{p^{2}}
. If
(A,+)\cong C_{p}\times C_{p^{3}}
, then we let
m=2
, and if
(A,+)\cong C_{p^{2}}\times C_{p^{2}}
, then we let
m=1
. Suppose
(A,\circ)
is generated by
\{P,Q_{1},Q_{2},\dots,Q_{i}\}
for some
i
. We assume the following:

1.

P^{p^{m}}
is central,

2.

P^{p^{m}}\in A^{2}
,

3.

ord(Q_{i})\leq p^{m}
,

4.

we can write any
a\in A
as
P^{k}\circ\prod_{\circ}Q_{j}^{a_{j}}
, where
k,a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
and the
Q_{j}
in the product appear in any order.

Then
P\ast p^{m}P^{k}=0
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.

We will apply this theorem in the following sections to specific cases of braces to show that we can find a central element, namely
P^{p^{m}}
, for which
A\ast P^{p^{m}}=0
. The goal of this section is to prove this theorem. We now assume the assumptions of the theorem.

Proposition 2.2
.

P\ast P^{n}=\frac{1}{6}(n-2)(n-1)nP\ast(P\ast(P\ast P))+\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)P\ast(P\ast P)+n(P\ast P).

Proof.

We prove the proposition by induction. The base case clearly holds. Now, we have

\displaystyle P\ast P^{n+1}

\displaystyle=P\ast(P\circ P^{n})=P\ast(P\ast P^{n})+P\ast P+P\ast P^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)P\ast(P\ast(P\ast P))+nP\ast(P\ast P)+P\ast P

\displaystyle\;\;\;+\frac{1}{6}(n-2)(n-1)nP\ast(P\ast(P\ast P))+\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)P\ast(P\ast P)

\displaystyle\;\;\;+n(P\ast P)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}(n-1)n(n+1)P\ast(P\ast(P\ast P))+\frac{1}{2}n(n+1)P\ast(P\ast P)

\displaystyle\;\;\;+(n+1)P\ast P.

∎

Proposition 2.3
.

p^{m}(P\ast P)\in A^{3}
and
P\ast P^{p^{m}}=\frac{1}{2}p^{m}(p^{m}-1)P\ast(P\ast P)+p^{m}(P\ast P).

Proof.

By Proposition
2.2
we have

\displaystyle P\ast P^{p^{m}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}(p^{m}-2)(p^{m}-1)p^{m}P\ast(P\ast(P\ast P))+\frac{1}{2}p^{m}(p^{m}-1)P\ast(P\ast P)

\displaystyle\;\;\;+p^{m}(P\ast P),

which implies that
p^{m}(P\ast P)\in A^{3}.
This in turn implies that

P\ast P^{p^{m}}=\frac{1}{2}p^{m}(p^{m}-1)P\ast(P\ast P)+p^{m}(P\ast P).

∎

Corollary 2.4
.

p^{m}P\ast(P\ast P)=P\ast(P\ast P^{p^{m}}).

Proposition 2.5
.

p^{m}P\ast(P\ast P^{n})=np^{m}P\ast(P\ast P)=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
P^{p^{m}}=p^{m}P
.

Proof.

As before we have

\displaystyle P\ast P^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}(n-2)(n-1)nP\ast(P\ast(P\ast P))+\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)P\ast(P\ast P)

\displaystyle\;\;\;+n(P\ast P),

which implies

p^{m}P\ast(P\ast P^{n})=np^{m}P\ast(P\ast P).

Note that
P^{p^{m}}\in p^{m}A.
Let
a\in A
so that
P^{p^{m}}=p^{m}a
. Then we can write
a=P^{k}\circ\prod_{\circ}Q_{i}^{a_{i}}
, where
k,a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
and the
Q_{i}
in the product appear in some order. Then we have

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=P^{k}\ast(\prod_{\circ}Q_{i}^{a_{i}})+P^{k}+\prod_{\circ}Q_{i}^{a_{i}}

\displaystyle=P^{k}\ast(\sum\prod_{\ast}Q_{i}^{a_{i}})+P^{k}+\sum\prod_{\ast}Q_{i}^{a_{i}},

where
\sum\prod_{\ast}Q_{i}^{a_{i}}
signifies some sum of
\ast
-products of
Q_{i}
. Hence

P^{p^{m}}=p^{m}a=p^{m}P^{k}.

We have

\displaystyle p^{m}P\ast(P\ast P)=p^{m}P\ast(P\ast P^{k})=p^{m}kP\ast(P\ast P).

This implies
k=1
and
P^{p^{m}}=p^{m}P
.We have

P\ast P^{p^{m}}=\frac{1}{2}p^{m}(p^{m}-1)P\ast(P\ast P)+p^{m}(P\ast P),

so

\frac{1}{2}p^{m}(p^{m}-1)P\ast(P\ast P)=0.

As
\frac{1}{2}(p^{m}-1)
is an integer which is not divisible by
p
, it follows that
p^{m}P\ast(P\ast P)=0.

∎

Corollary 2.6
.

p^{m}(P\ast P^{n})=np^{m}(P\ast P).

Proposition 2.7
.

p^{m}P^{-1}=-p^{m}P.

Proof.

Recall that
P^{p^{m}},P^{-p^{m}}\in p^{m}A
, so we can write
P^{p^{m}}=p^{m}b
,
P^{-p^{m}}=p^{m}c
for some
b,c\in A
. Let
b=P^{b_{1}}\circ\prod_{\circ}Q_{i}^{b_{i}}
,
c=P^{c_{1}}\circ\prod_{\circ}Q_{i}^{c_{i}}
for some
b_{i},c_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
, so

P^{p^{m}}=p^{m}P^{b_{1}},\quad P^{-p^{m}}=p^{m}P^{c_{1}}.

Now consider
P^{-p^{m}}\ast P^{p^{m}}
. We have

\displaystyle P^{-p^{m}}\ast P^{p^{m}}

\displaystyle=-P^{-p^{m}}-P^{p^{m}}

\displaystyle=-p^{m}P^{c_{1}}-p^{m}P^{b_{1}}.

Also, as
P^{kp^{m}}\in Z(A)

\displaystyle P^{-p^{m}}\ast P^{p^{m}}

\displaystyle=p^{m}(P^{-p^{m}}\ast P^{b_{1}})

\displaystyle=p^{m}(P^{b_{1}}\ast p^{m}P^{c_{1}})

\displaystyle=0,

so
p^{m}P^{b_{1}}=-p^{m}P^{c_{1}}
. Therefore

\displaystyle P^{-p^{m}}\ast P^{p^{m}}

\displaystyle=-(p^{m}P^{b_{1}}\ast p^{m}P^{b_{1}})

\displaystyle=-(p^{m}P^{b_{1}})^{2}+2p^{m}P^{b_{1}}

\displaystyle=0,

so
(p^{m}P^{b_{1}})^{2}=2p^{m}P^{b_{1}}
, which implies
(p^{m}P^{b_{1}})^{n}=np^{m}P^{b_{1}}
. Hence
(P^{p^{m}})^{n}=nP^{p^{m}}
and similarly
(P^{-p^{m}})^{n}=nP^{-p^{m}}
. It follows that
P\ast P^{-p^{m}}=P\ast-P^{p^{m}}=-p^{m}(P\ast P).
Now we have

\displaystyle P\ast P^{-p^{m}}

\displaystyle=P\ast(-p^{m}P^{b_{1}})

\displaystyle=-p^{m}(P\ast P^{b_{1}})

\displaystyle=-p^{m}b_{1}(P\ast P)

by Corollary
2.6
. Hence
b_{1}=1.
Now we have

\displaystyle P\ast P^{-p^{m}}

\displaystyle=P\ast(p^{m}P^{c_{1}})

\displaystyle=p^{m}(P\ast P^{c_{1}})

\displaystyle=p^{m}c_{1}(P\ast P),

so
c_{1}=-1.

∎

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
a\in A
. Then
P\ast p^{m}a=0.

Proof.

Let
a\in A
. Then we can write
a=P^{n}\circ\prod_{\circ}Q_{i}^{a_{i}}
, where
n,a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
and the
Q_{i}
in the product appear in some order. Then, as before,

p^{m}a=p^{m}P^{n}.

We have

P\ast p^{m}P^{n}=np^{m}P\ast P.

Consider

We have

\displaystyle p^{m}(P\ast P^{p^{m}-1})

\displaystyle=-p^{m}(P\ast P)

\displaystyle=p^{m}(P\ast(P^{p^{m}}\circ P^{-1}))

\displaystyle=p^{m}(P\ast(P^{-1}\ast P^{p^{m}})+P\ast P^{-1}+P\ast P^{p^{m}})

\displaystyle=-p^{m}P-p^{m}P^{-1}

\displaystyle=0.

∎

This lemma finishes the proof of Theorem

A\ast pA\cong C_{p}
. Similarly, note that
A\ast(A\ast pA)\neq A\ast pA
, so
\lvert A\ast(A\ast pA)\rvert=1
. Hence for any element
c\in A\ast pA
, we have
A\ast c=0
. Hence
A
is right nilpotent.

∎

3.5
X

Suppose
(A,\circ)
is the group X given by the relations

P^{p^{2}}=Q^{p}=R^{p}=1,\;\;P\circ Q=Q\circ P,\;\;Q\circ R=R\circ Q,\;\;R^{-1}\circ P\circ R=P\circ Q.

Then
Q\in Z(A)
. We have

R^{-1}\circ P^{p}\circ R=(Q\circ P)^{p}=Q^{p}\circ P^{p}=P^{p},

so
P^{p}\in Z(A)
.

Proposition 3.9
.

A
is right nilpotent.

Proof.

We let
S:=\langle Q,P^{p}\rangle
, the group generated by
Q
and
P^{p}
under the
\circ
operation. Hence
S\subseteq Z(A)
is an abelian group of order
p^{2}
.

Now, as
Q,P^{p}\in pA
, we have
S\subseteq pA
.
As
(A,+)\cong C_{p^{2}}\times C_{p^{2}}
,
\lvert pA\rvert=p^{2}
and
S=pA\subseteq Z(A)
.

We now proceed as in Proposition
3.8
.

∎

4
C_{p}\times C_{p^{3}}

In this section we characterise the right nilpotency of braces of cardinality
p^{4}
with additive group
C_{p}\times C_{p^{3}}
.

Suppose the brace
(A,+,\circ)
has
(A,+)\cong C_{p}\times C_{p^{3}}
. This implies that
(A,\circ)
has an element of order
p^{3}.
Now, by
[
6
]
the only non-abelian group of order
p^{4}
with an element of order
p^{3}
is defined by the relations

P^{p^{3}}=1,\;\;Q^{p}=1,\;\;Q^{-1}\circ P\circ Q=P^{1+p^{2}}.

Notice that
P^{p^{2}}\in Z(A)
. Now, as
P^{p^{2}}
is a commutator we have
P^{p^{2}}\in A^{2}
.

Notice now that
P^{p^{2}}\in p^{2}A
, so to show
A
is right nilpotent it suffices to prove that
A\ast p^{2}A=0
. As any element of
A
is a product of
P
s and
Q
s, it suffices to prove
Q\ast p^{2}A=0
, and
P\ast p^{2}A=0.

Proposition 4.1
.

Q\ast p^{2}A=P\ast p^{2}A=0.

Proof.

Let
a\in A
. Then we can write
a=P^{a_{1}}\circ Q^{a_{2}}
for some
a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
. We have

\displaystyle p^{2}a

\displaystyle=p^{2}(P^{a_{1}}\ast Q^{a_{2}}+P^{a_{1}}+Q^{a_{2}})

\displaystyle=p^{2}P^{a_{1}}.

By Theorem
2.1
we have
P\ast p^{2}P^{a_{1}}=0
. We have

\displaystyle Q\ast p^{2}a

\displaystyle=Q\ast p^{2}P^{a_{1}}

\displaystyle=p^{2}(P^{a_{1}-a_{1}p^{2}}\ast Q-P^{a_{1}}+P^{a_{1}-a_{1}p^{2}})

\displaystyle=0.

∎
