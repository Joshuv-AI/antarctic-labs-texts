The curse of random quantum data
Kaining Zhang, Junyu Liu, Liu Liu, Liang Jiang, Min-Hsiu Hsieh, Dacheng Tao

Abstract
Quantum machine learning, which involves running machine learning algorithms on quantum devices, may be one of the most significant flagship applications for these devices. Unlike its classical counterparts, the role of data in quantum machine learning has not been fully understood. In this work, we quantify the performances of quantum machine learning in the landscape of quantum data. Provided that the encoding of quantum data is sufficiently random, the performance, we find that the training efficiency and generalization capabilities in quantum machine learning will be exponentially suppressed with the increase in the number of qubits, which we call “the curse of random quantum data”. Our findings apply to both the quantum kernel method and the large-width limit of quantum neural networks. Conversely, we highlight that through meticulous design of quantum datasets, it is possible to avoid these curses, thereby achieving efficient convergence and robust generalization. Our conclusions are corroborated by extensive numerical simulations.

In this paper, we uncover a novel phenomenon related to the interplay between quantum machine learning performance and the distribution of quantum data, which we term

We discover that, similar to the barren plateau phenomenon in the gradient efficiency for training dynamics, across a typical class of quantum machine learning algorithms, when quantum data is encoded randomly, the algorithm’s learning performance markedly declines. Specifically, the spectrum of the quantum neural tangent kernel diminishes in line with the dimension of the Hilbert space, and similarly, the reduction in the generalization error during training is also constrained by the Hilbert space’s dimension. This phenomenon may not be restricted to any particular training strategy; it is observable in both the quantum kernel method and the variational quantum algorithm at or beyond the frozen kernel limit. II.1
Background of quantum machine learning

We begin by setting up notations and methods for quantum machine learning. The training set is denoted by
\mathcal{A}=\{a\}=\{(\rho_{a},y_{a})\}
, where each sample
a
consists of the input state in the density matrix form
\rho_{a}
and the label
y_{a}
. The test set is denoted by
\mathcal{B}=\{\hat{b}\}
. In the task of QML, we aim to approximate the label
y
with the parameterized prediction function
z(\bm{\theta})
. Here a common approach is to minimize the mean squared error on the training set as the loss function:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{2|\mathcal{A}|}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\left(z_{a}(\bm{\theta})-y_{a}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{2|\mathcal{A}|}\left\|\bm{r}_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{\theta})\right\|_{2}^{2},

(1)

where

\bm{r}_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{\theta}):=\{r_{a}(\bm{\theta})\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}:=\{z_{a}(\bm{\theta})-y_{a}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}

(2)

is the training residual vector. The predication function is given by

z_{a}(\bm{\theta})={\rm Tr}[O(\bm{\theta})\rho_{a}].

(3)

In this paper, we focus on two approaches for generating the predication function. The first is the quantum kernel method:

O_{\rm qkm}(\bm{\theta})=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\theta_{a}\rho_{a}.

(4)

The second approach is the quantum neural network using the observable
O
and the variational quantum circuit
V(\bm{\theta})
:

O_{\rm qnn}(\bm{\theta})={V(\bm{\theta})}^{\dagger}OV(\bm{\theta}).

(5)

For the QNN case, we train the parameter using gradient descent with some learning rate
\eta
, i.e.

\bm{\theta}(t+1)=\bm{\theta}(t)-\eta\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{\theta}(t)).

(6)

Suppose the parameter
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
. Let
\eta\rightarrow 0
, the gradient descent dynamics approximates to the continuous regime known as the gradient flow:

\dot{\bm{\theta}}=-\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{\theta})=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}J(\bm{\theta})\bm{r}_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{\theta}),

(7)

where
J(\bm{\theta})\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times|\mathcal{A}|}
is the Jacobian matrix with entries
J_{da}(\bm{\theta})=\frac{\partial z_{a}}{\partial\theta_{d}}(\bm{\theta})
.
We are interested in the convergence rate of the loss function, i.e.

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{\theta})={}

\displaystyle\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{\theta})^{T}\dot{\bm{\theta}}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle-\frac{D}{|\mathcal{A}|^{2}}\bm{r}_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{\theta})^{T}K(\bm{\theta})\bm{r}_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle-\frac{2D}{|\mathcal{A}|}\lambda_{\min}\left[K(\bm{\theta})\right]{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{\theta}),

(8)

where

K(\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{D}J(\bm{\theta})^{T}J(\bm{\theta})

(9)

is the QNTK at the point
\bm{\theta}
.
Eq. (
8
) shows that the loss decays linearly when the least eigenvalue of QNTK is lower bounded by some positive constant. In the rest of this paper, we will prove rigorous convergence guarantees based on further analyses to the least eigenvalue of QNTK.

II.2
Generalization error with quantum data

Since input states are engaged in the training of QML via quantum kernel or QNTK, their distributions could naturally affect training and generalization performances. Here, we provide some negative examples for datasets sampled from state 2-designs. Specifically, we consider the quantum kernel method and the quantum neural network approach separately in Theorems
1
and
2
. Related proofs can be found in Appendix
C
.
The generalization error has up to exponentially small improvement from the extremely overfitting situation, when the size of datasets is relatively small compared with the Hilbert dimension.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose all
N
-qubit quantum states in the training and test datasets
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are independently sampled from state 2-designs and the size of datasets is smaller than
2^{N/2}
. Let
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}}(t)
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}(t)
be the training and the test loss function of the quantum kernel method (Eqs. (
3
) and (
4
)), respectively. Then, with high probability,

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}(\infty)\gtrsim{}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}(0)-\frac{|\mathcal{A}|}{2^{N-1}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\sqrt{\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}}(0)\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}(0)},

(10)

where the expectation is taken under state
2
-designs for states in
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose all
N
-qubit quantum states in the training and test datasets
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are independently sampled from state 2-designs. Let
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}}(t)
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}(t)
be the training and the test loss function of the quantum neural network (Eqs. (
3
) and (
5
)), where the label is given as
y:={\rm Tr}[OU\rho U^{\dagger}]
for a target unitary
U
with the zero trace observable
O
. Then, under the frozen QNTK regime,

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}(\infty)\geq{}\left(1-\frac{|\mathcal{A}|}{2^{2N}-1}\right)\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}(0),

(11)

where the expectation is taken under
2
-designs distributions for states in
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
and the target unitary
U
.

We remark that Theorem
2
provides a novel result about learning quantum dynamics from the no-free-lunch framework perspective.
Compared with existing results
[
52
]
that mainly focus on the lower bound with an absolute formulation, we provide a bound with a relative formulation for the test loss with trained parameters. As shown in Eq. (
11
), the bound is in terms of the initial test loss times a constant, which is exponentially close to
1
with polynomially scaled training datasets. Therefore, the loss on the test set with
2
-design states only has exponentially small improvements from its initial value, unless the size of the training set exceeds an exponentially large threshold. However, training QNN with exponentially large datasets requires tremendous computational resources. Such kind of datasets are also unavailable in practice for large-scale problems.

Theorems
1
and
2
reveal one critical issue of quantum machine learning from the data perspective, which we name as
the curse of random quantum data
. For input states distributed uniformly in the Hilbert space, the corresponding QML task exhibits poor generalization performance. This phenomenon is universal and is independent of certain QML method employed. One intuitive explanation of the curse of random quantum data is that uniformly distributed quantum states have inherently bad learnability, unless the dataset has exponentially large size. Specifically, quantum representation learning is hard for datasets composed of nearly orthogonal states without prior knowledge. However, practical QML datasets do not grow exponentially with increasing qubits in general. Therefore the distribution of input states given as quantum data or quantum encoding would play an essential role, which may rule out the potential of benign generalization.

II.3
Spectrum of QNTK with quantum data

We have shown the
learnability
issue from uniformly random quantum data, i.e., datasets with
2
-design states have bad generalization performances. Here we present another
trainability
issue induced by uniformly random quantum data from the optimization perspective in QNN.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\{(\rho_{a},y_{a})\}
be the training datasets with independent
N
-qubit
2
-design states
\rho_{a}
. Denote by
z_{a}={\rm Tr}[OV(\bm{\theta})\rho_{a}V(\bm{\theta})^{\dagger}]
the QNN with the observable
O
and the variational circuit
V(\bm{\theta})=\prod_{d=D}^{1}\exp[-iH_{d}\theta_{d}/2]W_{d}
, where the hamiltonian
H_{d}
is Hermitian unitary and
W_{d}
is the fixed unitary. Then there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\lambda_{\max}\left[K(\bm{\theta})\right]\leq{}\frac{\|O\|_{F}^{2}}{4^{N}}\left(1+\frac{C|\mathcal{A}|}{2^{N}\delta}\sqrt{\ln|\mathcal{A}|}\right)

(12)

with probability at least
1-\delta
.

As shown in Theorem
3
, the QNTK has exponentially small spectrum with increased qubits
N
. Since the convergence rate is controlled by the spectrum of QNTK, we should avoid using uniformly distributed input states in the training of QNN. One appropriate substitute is a biased state distribution. For convenience, we first show the state
2
-design distribution in the form of Pauli decomposition. Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{4^{N}\times|\mathcal{A}|}
be the matrix that stores the Pauli decomposition coefficients of states in
\mathcal{A}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\rho_{a}=\sum_{\bm{i}\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{\otimes N}}\frac{1}{2^{N}}A_{\bm{i}a}\sigma_{\bm{i}},

(13)

where the basis
\sigma_{\bm{i}}:=\sigma_{i_{0}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma_{i_{N}}
is the tensor product of Pauli matrices. When each
\rho_{a}
is sampled independently from a
2
-design distribution,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\text{2-design}}A_{\bm{i}a}={}

\displaystyle 0,

(14)

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\text{2-design}}A_{\bm{i}a}A_{\bm{j}b}={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{N}+1}\delta_{\bm{i}\bm{j}}\delta_{ab}.

(15)

for all
\bm{i},\bm{j}\neq\bm{0}
.
We recap that the Pauli coefficient coefficients of
2
-designs states has the following two properties: 1) zero mean and covariance; 2) unbiased variance with the value
(2^{N}+1)^{-1}
. Therefore, each element of
A
except
A_{\bm{0}a}
has a small size around
\mathcal{O}(2^{-N/2})
, which technically contributes to exponentially small generalization improvement and QNTK. To overcome this issue, we introduce the state distribution in Proposition
1
. The proposed distribution is generalized from Eqs. (
14
) and (
15
), such that coefficients have biased variances on different Pauli bases.

Proposition 1
.

States
\rho_{a}
in the dataset
\mathcal{A}
are generated independently from the same distribution, such that for all
\bm{i},\bm{j}\neq\bm{0}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}A_{\bm{i}a}={}

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}A_{\bm{i}a}A_{\bm{j}b}={}

\displaystyle\alpha_{\bm{i}}\delta_{\bm{i}\bm{j}}\delta_{ab}.

Here we present an example that lead to the coefficient distribution in Proposition
1
. We consider states from finite local-depth circuits (FLDC)
[
53
]
, where the parameter in circuits are independently sampled from the uniform distribution on
[0,2\pi]
. Numerical evidence is provided in Figure
1
.
Denote by
\mu_{\bm{i}}
the expectation of each Pauli decomposition coefficient
A_{\bm{i}a}
over the distribution of
a
. Firstly, we demonstrate that the expectation
\mu_{\bm{i}}
is zero.
Due to the central limit theorem, the statistical term

\displaystyle\left|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}A^{\prime}_{\bm{i}a}\right|:={}

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}A_{\bm{i}a}}{\mathop{\rm SD}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}A_{\bm{i}a}}\right|\lesssim|\mu_{\bm{i}}|+\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{A}|}}|g|,

(16)

where

is a standard normal distributed variable. As illustrated in Figure
1a
, the average of the statistical term in Eq. (
16
) decays inversely with the square root of
|\mathcal{A}|
when it grows. Therefore each
\mu_{\bm{i}}=0
for
S\in\{1,2,3,4\}
.
Next, we reveal the scaling of the variance
\alpha_{\bm{i}}
in terms of the qubit number and the block layer. As shown in Figure
1b
, when the qubit number increases,
the variance remains large when the number of circuit layer is small. With growing circuit layers, the variance converges to the Haar limit
1/(2^{N}+1)
for
S\in\{1,2,3,4\}

Convergence analysis of training QNNs

Here we provide two examples of QNNs with rigorous convergence analysis, where the input state in the training dataset satisfies Proposition

Assumption 1

(Locally smooth condition)
.

Let

\left\|J(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})-J(\bm{\theta})\right\|_{2}\leq{}\sqrt{C_{0}D}\|\bm{\theta}^{\prime}-\bm{\theta}\|_{2}.

Theorem 4

(Linear convergence of QNN training w.r.t. Proposition

IV
Discussion

In this manuscript, we uncover the training and generalization issues of quantum machine learning with uniformly random quantum data. We demonstrate the separation performances of quantum datasets with different distributions in terms of the convergence rate and the generalization capability. Specifically, we prove fast linear convergence when optimizing the
\ell_{2}
-norm loss function under certain input state conditions.
By studying the lower bound of QNTK, we derive reasonable convergence rate when the number of parameters and the number of qubits in local Hamiltonians exceed some moderate thresholds. All proposed theorems are verified by numerical experiments.

Despite these advancements, further research is imperative for a more profound understanding of QNN training. Notably, our analysis relies on the random matrix theory and assumes certain stochastic properties of datasets. Although numerical experiments indicate favorable convergence with real-world datasets, elucidating training dynamics on datasets with explicit real-world or quantum characteristics remains an area for future exploration.
Furthermore, this research primarily analyzes QNNs from the perspective of dataset properties, and VQCs are initialized with zero parameters. We expect that theories with zero initialization could be engaged as technical tools in the study with other initializations by merging the initial VQC into input states.
Another intriguing direction for future work is to refine the parameter threshold that guarantees the linear convergence, as experiments indicate rapid convergence for circuits with the number of parameters
D\sim 10^{2}
, which is significantly lower than our theoretical thresholds outlined in Section
II.4
. Additionally, we hope that this manuscript could inspire further research into the convergence behavior of variational models with analogous structures, such as the training of tensor networks
[
60
]
.

). After the training, the linear model in Eq. (
38
) can be optimized to achieve zero training loss with the parameter

\bm{\theta}(\infty)={k}^{-1}\bm{y}_{\mathcal{A}}.

(40)

At the optimal parameter point, the prediction of a sample
\hat{b}=(\rho_{\hat{b}},y_{\hat{b}})
from the test dataset is given by

\displaystyle z_{\hat{b}}(\infty)={}

\displaystyle\sum_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}{k}_{\hat{b}a^{\prime}}\theta_{a^{\prime}}(\infty)

(from Eq. (
38
))

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}{k}_{\hat{b}a^{\prime}}[{k}^{-1}]_{a^{\prime}a}{y}_{a}

(from Eq. (
40
))

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}{k}_{\hat{b}a^{\prime}}[{k}^{-1}]_{a^{\prime}a}\left({z}_{a}(0)-{r}_{a}(0)\right)

(from
r=z-y
)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}{k}_{\hat{b}a^{\prime}}\theta_{a^{\prime}}(0)-\sum_{a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}{k}_{\hat{b}a^{\prime}}[{k}^{-1}]_{a^{\prime}a}r_{a}(0)

(from Eq. (
39
))

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle z_{\hat{b}}(0)-\sum_{a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}{k}_{\hat{b}a^{\prime}}[{k}^{-1}]_{a^{\prime}a}r_{a}(0),

(41)

where
\bm{r}_{\mathcal{A}}(t):=\bm{z}_{\mathcal{A}}(t)-\bm{y}_{\mathcal{A}}
is the training residual vector at the optimization step
t
.
So, the residual of the sample
\hat{b}
after training is given by,

r_{\hat{b}}(\infty)=z_{\hat{b}}(\infty)-y_{\hat{b}}=r_{\hat{b}}(0)-\sum_{a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}{k}_{\hat{b}a^{\prime}}[{k}^{-1}]_{a^{\prime}a}r_{a}(0).

(42)

We denote
\mathcal{L}(t):=\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}(t))
for convenience. Therefore, we obtain the generalization error,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{B}|\mathcal{A}}={}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}(\infty)-\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}}(\infty)=\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}(\infty)=\frac{1}{2|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{\hat{b}\in\mathcal{B}}r_{\hat{b}}^{2}(\infty)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{\hat{b}\in\mathcal{B}}\left(r_{\hat{b}}(0)-\sum_{a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}{k}_{\hat{b}a^{\prime}}[{k}^{-1}]_{a^{\prime}a}r_{a}(0)\right)^{2}

(43)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{\hat{b}\in\mathcal{B}}r_{\hat{b}}^{2}(0)+\frac{1}{2|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{\hat{b}\in\mathcal{B}}\left(\sum_{a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}{k}_{\hat{b}a^{\prime}}[{k}^{-1}]_{a^{\prime}a}r_{a}(0)\right)^{2}

(44)

\displaystyle-{}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{\hat{b}\in\mathcal{B}}\sum_{a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}{k}_{\hat{b}a^{\prime}}[{k}^{-1}]_{a^{\prime}a}r_{\hat{b}}(0)r_{a}(0).

(45)

We remark that the first term is the loss on the set
\mathcal{B}
before the training. To show the improvement of the prediction on the test dataset, we define the following quantity as the relative generalization error,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{B}|\mathcal{A}}:={}

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{B}|\mathcal{A}}-\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}(0)

(46)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{\hat{b}\in\mathcal{B}}\left(\sum_{a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}{k}_{\hat{b}a^{\prime}}[{k}^{-1}]_{a^{\prime}a}r_{a}(0)\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{\hat{b}\in\mathcal{B}}\sum_{a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}{k}_{\hat{b}a^{\prime}}[{k}^{-1}]_{a^{\prime}a}r_{\hat{b}}(0)r_{a}(0)

(47)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{\hat{b}\in\mathcal{B},a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathcal{A}}g_{\hat{b}a_{1}}g_{\hat{b}a_{2}}r_{a_{1}}(0)r_{a_{2}}(0)-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{\hat{b}\in\mathcal{B},a\in\mathcal{A}}g_{\hat{b}a}r_{\hat{b}}(0)r_{a}(0),

(48)

where we use the metric

g_{\hat{b}a}=\sum_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}{k}_{\hat{b}a^{\prime}}[{k}^{-1}]_{a^{\prime}a}.

(49)

When
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{B}|\mathcal{A}}\geq 0
, the element in set
\mathcal{B}
has equal or worse prediction averagely after the training, i.e., we face the overfitting issue. Therefore we hope
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{B}|\mathcal{A}}\leq-C
, where the constant
C>0
could be as large as possible.

Next, we analyze the scaling of generalization error when input datasets are sampled from state
2
-designs. Specifically, suppose states
\rho_{a}=|\psi_{a}\rangle\langle\psi_{a}|
in training and test datasets are independent
2
-design states, i.e.,
|\psi_{a}\rangle=U_{a}|0\rangle
and
U_{a}
s are randomly chosen from
\mathcal{U}(2^{N})
according to the unitary
2
-design. The quantum kernel method gives

\displaystyle k_{ab}={}

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}[\rho_{a}\rho_{b}]=|\langle\psi_{a}|\psi_{b}\rangle|^{2}.

Since input states are engaged in the quantum kernel, different state distributions could naturally induce different training and generalization performances.
Here, we repeat the generalization error result in the main text in Theorem
S1
for convenience, where the notation
\mathcal{L}(t):=\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}(t))
is employed. The generalization error only has exponentially small improvement from the extremely overfitting situation.

Theorem S1
.

Suppose all
N
-qubit quantum states in the training and test datasets
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are independently sampled from state 2-designs and the size of datasets is smaller than
2^{N/2}
. Let
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}}(t)
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}(t)
be the training and the test loss function at step
t
of the quantum kernel method, respectively. Then, with high probability,

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}(\infty)\gtrsim{}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}(0)-\frac{|\mathcal{A}|}{2^{N-1}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\sqrt{\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}}(0)\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}(0)},

(50)

where the expectation is taken under state
2
-designs for states in
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
.

Proof.

The main idea is to find that the metric
g_{\hat{b}a}
in Eq. (
49
) is exponentially small on average. To achieve this, we first prove that for Haar-randomly distributed input states, the quantum kernel is close to the identity matrix with an additional exponentially small term. We refer to the Pauli decomposition form of quantum states with random matrix theory. Specifically, let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{(4^{N}-1)\times|\mathcal{A}|}
be the coefficient matrix that stores coefficients for input states
\{|\psi_{a}\rangle\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
,

A_{\bm{i},a}=\frac{1}{2^{N}}{\rm Tr}\left[|\psi_{a}\rangle\langle\psi_{a}|\sigma_{\bm{i}}\right],

(51)

where
\bm{i}\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{\otimes N}/\bm{0}
denotes the index of Pauli matrices except the identity matrix:
\bm{i}=(i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{N})
. We remark that the coefficient for the identity matrix is always
1/2^{N}
since
{\rm Tr}[|\psi_{a}\rangle\langle\psi_{a}|]=1
, and the density matrix could be recovered as

|\psi_{a}\rangle\langle\psi_{a}|=\frac{1}{2^{N}}\sigma_{\bm{0}}+\sum_{\bm{i}\neq\bm{0}}A_{\bm{i},a}\sigma_{\bm{i}}.

(52)

Thus, the quantum kernel can be written as

\displaystyle{k}_{a^{\prime}a}={}

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}\left[|\psi_{a^{\prime}}\rangle\langle\psi_{a^{\prime}}|\psi_{a}\rangle\langle\psi_{a}|\right]

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{N}}+\sum_{\bm{i},\bm{j}}A_{\bm{i},a^{\prime}}A_{\bm{j},a}{\rm Tr}[\sigma_{\bm{i}}\sigma_{\bm{j}}]

(using Eq. (
52
))

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{N}}+2^{N}\left[A^{T}A\right]_{a^{\prime}a}.

(53)

The matrix formulation of Eq. (
53
) is

\displaystyle{k}={}

\displaystyle 2^{N}A^{T}A+\frac{1}{2^{N}}\bm{e}\bm{e}^{T},

(54)

where
\bm{e}=(1,1,\cdots,1)^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{A}|}
.

For random input states sampled independently from state
2
-designs, the matrix
A
is a random matrix with independent columns, so we could apply Lemma
S7
for analyzing the kernel property. To match the isotropic column condition in Lemma
S7
, we introduce the matrix

B=2^{N}\sqrt{2^{N}+1}A.

(55)

Matrix
B
has independent isotropic columns since

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\rm 2-design}\left[B_{a^{\prime}}B_{a}^{T}\right]_{\bm{i}\bm{j}}={}

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\rm 2-design}2^{2N}(2^{N}+1)A_{\bm{i},a^{\prime}}A_{\bm{j},a}

(using Eq. (
55
))

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\rm 2-design}(2^{N}+1){\rm Tr}\left[|\psi_{a^{\prime}}\rangle\langle\psi_{a^{\prime}}|\sigma_{\bm{i}}\right]{\rm Tr}\left[|\psi_{a}\rangle\langle\psi_{a}|\sigma_{\bm{j}}\right]

(using Eq. (
51
))

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\delta_{a^{\prime}a}\delta_{\bm{i}\bm{j}}.

(using Lemmas
S1
and
S2
)

The
\ell_{2}
-norm of
B_{a}
is
\sqrt{4^{N}-1}
since

\displaystyle\|B_{a}\|_{2}^{2}={}

\displaystyle 4^{N}(2^{N}+1)\sum_{\bm{i}}A_{\bm{i},a}^{2}

(using Eq. (
55
))

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle 4^{N}(2^{N}+1)\frac{1}{2^{N}}\left({\rm Tr}\left[|\psi_{a}\rangle\langle\psi_{a}|^{2}\right]-\frac{1}{2^{N}}\right)

(using Eq. (
52
))

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle 4^{N}-1.

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4^{N}-1}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\rm 2-design}\max_{a}\sum_{a^{\prime}\neq a}\left(B_{a}^{T}B_{a^{\prime}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4^{N}-1}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\rm 2-design}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{a^{\prime}\neq a}\left(B_{a}^{T}B_{a^{\prime}}\right)^{2}

(56)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{16^{N}(2^{N}+1)}{2^{N}-1}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\rm 2-design}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{a^{\prime}\neq a}\left(\sum_{\bm{i}}A_{\bm{i},a}A_{\bm{i},a^{\prime}}\right)^{2}

(using Eq. (
55
))

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{16^{N}(2^{N}+1)}{2^{N}-1}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\rm 2-design}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{a^{\prime}\neq a}\frac{1}{4^{N}}\left({\rm Tr}\left[|\psi_{a}\rangle\langle\psi_{a}|\psi_{a^{\prime}}\rangle\langle\psi_{a^{\prime}}|\right]-\frac{1}{2^{N}}\right)^{2}

(using Eq. (
52
))

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{4^{N}(2^{N}+1)}{2^{N}-1}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{a^{\prime}\neq a}\left[\frac{2}{2^{N}(2^{N}+1)}-\frac{2}{2^{2N}}+\frac{1}{2^{2N}}\right]

(using Lemmas
S1
and
S2
)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle|\mathcal{A}|^{2}-|\mathcal{A}|

(57)

By using Lemma
S7
with Eq. (

(58)

where

is an absolute constant. Let the matrix

(59)

where
\mathbb{E}\|X\|_{F}\leq C_{0}\sqrt{\frac{(|\mathcal{A}|^{2}-|\mathcal{A}|)\ln|\mathcal{A}|}{4^{N}-1}}
by using Eqs. (
54
) and (
55

, we use the norm bound of

\|X\|_{F}\leq\mathcal{O}(2^{-N})
with high probability. Therefore the inverse

\left[{k}^{-1}\right]_{a^{\prime}a}=\delta_{a^{\prime}a}-\frac{1}{2^{N}}\left(1-\delta_{a^{\prime}a}\right)-X_{a^{\prime}a}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{4^{N}}\right)=2\delta_{a^{\prime}a}-{k}_{a^{\prime}a}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{4^{N}}\right)

(60)

with high probability.
By employing Eq. (
60

g

\displaystyle g_{\hat{b}a}={}

\displaystyle\sum_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}{k}_{\hat{b}a^{\prime}}\left(2\delta_{a^{\prime}a}-{k}_{a^{\prime}a}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{4^{N}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{|\mathcal{A}|}{4^{N}}\right)+\sum_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}{k}_{\hat{b}a^{\prime}}\left(2\delta_{a^{\prime}a}-{k}_{a^{\prime}a}\right)

(61)

which holds with high probability for all
\hat{b}
and
a
.

Then, we proceed to analyze the relative generalization error. By using Eq. (
48
), we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{B}|\mathcal{A}}-\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}(0)\right]=\frac{1}{2|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{\hat{b}\in\mathcal{B},a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathcal{A}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{b}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a_{1}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a_{2}}\left[g_{\hat{b}a_{1}}g_{\hat{b}a_{2}}r_{a_{1}}(0)r_{a_{2}}(0)\right]-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{\hat{b}\in\mathcal{B},a\in\mathcal{A}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{b}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a}\left[g_{\hat{b}a}r_{\hat{b}}(0)r_{a}(0)\right],

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2^{N}|\mathcal{B}|}\left(\bm{e}^{T}\bm{y}_{\mathcal{B}}\right)\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{A}}\left[\bm{e}^{T}\left(2I-{k}\right)\bm{r}_{\mathcal{A}}(0)\right]+\frac{1}{4^{N}|\mathcal{B}|}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{A}}\left[\bm{\theta}(0)^{T}\left(\bm{e}\bm{e}^{T}+{k}\right)\left(2I-{k}\right)\bm{r}_{\mathcal{A}}(0)\right]

C.2
Generalization error of quantum neural network

Next, we consider the quantum machine learning model utilizing variational quantum circuit to generate predications for input samples. Specifically, we denote by

Appendix D
Spectral analysis of the quantum neural tangent kernel

We begin with a warm-up example that shows the exponentially small spectrum of QNTK with
2
-designs states as datasets.

Lemma S8
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\{(\rho_{a},y_{a})\}
be the training datasets with independent
N
-qubit
2
-design states
\rho_{a}
. Denote by
z_{a}={\rm Tr}[OV(\bm{\theta})\rho_{a}V(\bm{\theta})^{\dagger}]
the QNN with the observable
O
and the variational circuit
V(\bm{\theta})=\prod_{d=D}^{1}\exp[-iH_{d}\theta_{d}/2]W_{d}
, where the hamiltonian
H_{d}
is Hermitian unitary and
W_{d}
is the fixed unitary. Then the QNTK has exponentially small spectrum, i.e. there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\lambda_{\max}\left[K(\bm{\theta})\right]\leq{}\frac{\|O\|_{F}^{2}}{4^{N}}\left(1+\frac{C|\mathcal{A}|}{2^{N}\delta}\sqrt{\ln|\mathcal{A}|}\right)

(105)

with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Proof.

We follow notations in Lemma
S3
. For convenience, we assume the value of each parameter
\theta_{d}
is absorbed into the fixed unitary
W_{d}
, i.e.,
\theta_{d}\equiv 0
. Denote by

\displaystyle O_{d}:={}

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{2}{W_{d:1}}^{\dagger}\big[{W_{D:d+1}}^{\dagger}OW_{D:d+1},H_{d}\big]W_{d:1}.

(106)

We can write the partial derivative of
z_{a}
in the formulation with Pauli decomposition coefficients:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial z_{a}}{\partial\theta_{d}}={}

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}\left[O_{d}\rho_{a}\right]

(using Lemma
S3
)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{N}}\left[B^{\otimes}\left(\bm{c}_{O_{d}}\otimes\bm{c}_{\rho_{a}}\right)\right]_{\bm{0}}

(using notation in Lemma
S5
)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{N}}\bm{c}_{O_{d}}^{T}\bm{c}_{\rho_{a}}.

(calculating
B^{\otimes}
in Lemma
S5
)

Therefore, the Jacobian matrix is

\displaystyle J=\frac{1}{2^{N}}C_{O}^{T}A,

(107)

where
C_{O}\in\mathbb{R}^{(4^{N}-1)\times D}
stores the Pauli decomposition of matrices
\{O_{d}\}_{d=1}^{D}
for
d\in\{1,2,\cdots,D\}
. We remark that the each matrix
O_{d}
only corresponds to
4^{N}-1
coefficients since the coefficient
c_{O_{d},\bm{0}}=\frac{1}{2^{N}}{\rm Tr}[O_{d}I]=0
. Similarly the matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{(4^{N}-1)\times|\mathcal{A}|}
stores the Pauli decomposition of states
\{\rho_{a}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
, which is the same with the notation in Eq. (
51
).
By using Eq. (
9
), the QNTK is

\displaystyle K(\bm{\theta})={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{D}J^{T}J

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4^{N}D}A^{T}C_{O}C_{O}^{T}A.

Thus, the largest eigenvalue is upper bounded as

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}\left[K(\bm{\theta})\right]\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4^{N}D}\lambda_{\max}\left[A^{T}A\right]\lambda_{\max}\left[C_{O}C_{O}^{T}\right].

(108)

We remark that by using Eqs. (
55
-
58
), there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}\left[A^{T}A\right]\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{N}}+\frac{C|\mathcal{A}|}{4^{N}\delta}\sqrt{\ln|\mathcal{A}|}

(109)

with probability at least
1-\delta
.
Besides, we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}\left[C_{O}C_{O}^{T}\right]

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\left\|C_{O}\right\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{d=1}^{D}\sum_{\bm{i}\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{N}/\bm{0}}\left(c_{O_{d},\bm{i}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{d=1}^{D}2^{N}{\rm Tr}\left[O_{d}^{2}\right]
