Mathematical Foundations of Distributionally Robust Multistage Optimization Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Alois Pichler, Alexander Shapiro

Abstract
Distributionally robust optimization involves various probability measures in its problem formulation. They can be bundled to constitute a risk functional. For this equivalence, risk functionals constitute a fundamental building block in distributionally robust stochastic programming. Multistage programming requires conditional versions of risk functionals to re-assess future risk after partial realizations and after preceding decisions. This paper discusses a construction of the conditional counterpart of a risk functional by passing its genuine characteristics to its conditional counterparts. The conditional risk functionals turn out to be different from the nested analogues of the original (law invariant) risk measure. It is demonstrated that the initial measure and its nested decomposition can be used in a distributionally robust multistage setting.

Multistage stochastic programming, distributional robustness, conditional risk measures, dynamic equations, stochastic games, rectangularity

Introduction

This paper addresses
Distributionally Robust Optimization
(DRO) when applied to multistage stochastic programming problems. This robust approach involves various probability measures and discloses an optimal solution which is robust with respect to varying distributions.
DRO involves the functional

{\cal R}(Z):=\sup_{Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}}\E_{Q}[Z]

(1)

for a chosen so-called
ambiguity
set
{\mathfrak{M}}
of probability measures.
Various constructions of the ambiguity set were suggested in the applications.
It is beyond the scope of this paper to give a survey of this literature, we can mention for example
[
6
,
23
,
24
]
and references therein.
By duality, DRO is closely related to the
risk averse
approach where
{\cal R}
is viewed as a risk measure
(cf.
[
2
,
4
,
19
]
).

In case the ambiguity set
{\mathfrak{M}}=\{P\}
is a singleton this reduces to the risk neutral approach. The essential difference between the DRO and the risk averse approaches is how the corresponding functional
{\cal R}
is defined.

In the static setting the equation (
1
) of the objective functional
{\cal R}
is reasonably straightforward. Multistage problems with decisions made dynamically, on the other hand, are considerably more involved.

I
n the risk averse approach the corresponding risk measure, associated with the sequential decision process, usually is formulated in the nested (composite) form (cf.
[
5
,
12
,
15
]
). Such nested formulations are amendable to Bellman’s principle of optimality and suitable for writing the respective dynamic programming equations. This in turn is directly related to the concept of time consistency meaning that a decision maker should not reconsider optimality of the decisions at the later stages of the process. In that respect nested formulations provide a natural framework for the optimality criteria at every stage of the process.

In the DRO setting the situation is more delicate.
It turns out that seemingly natural extensions of the DRO and the risk averse approaches could lead to somewhat different frameworks. The basic question of formulating a conditional counterpart of the DRO functional (
1
) turns out to be not straightforward. The main goal of this paper is to elaborate on the involved fundamental questions in a mathematical context.

Outline

The following section elaborates the mathematical setting for distributionally robust functionals and risk measures. Section
3
addresses their conditional counterparts. Nested formulations are important in multistage stochastic optimization, they are considered in Section
4
. The corresponding dynamic programming equations are elaborated in Section
5
before we conclude in Section
6
.

2
Preliminaries and the static setting

Let
(\Omega,{\cal F})
be a measurable space and
{\mathfrak{M}}
be a nonempty set of probability measures (distributions) on
(\Omega,{\cal F})
. We denote by
{\mathfrak{P}}
the set of all probability distributions on
(\Omega,{\cal F})
, thus
{\mathfrak{M}}\subset{\mathfrak{P}}
.
The corresponding functional

{\cal R}(Z):=\sup_{Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}}\E_{Q}[Z],\quad Z\in{\cal Z},

(2)

is defined on a linear space
{\cal Z}
of measurable functions
Z\colon\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
, where we use the notation
\E_{Q}[Z]
to emphasize that the expectation
is with respect to the probability measure
Q\in{\mathfrak{P}}
.
We refer to
{\mathfrak{M}}
as the
ambiguity set
of probability measures and to
{\cal R}
as the
distributionally robust
functional.

We assume that
{\cal Z}
is a linear (vector) space of measurable functions such that
{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{\Omega}\in{\cal Z}
, where
{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{A}
is the indicator function of the set
A
, i.e.,
{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{A}(x)=1
for
x\in A
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{A}(x)=0
otherwise.
This implies that
Z+a\in{\cal Z}
, if
Z\in{\cal Z}
and
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
We associate with
{\cal Z}
a linear space
{\cal Z}^{*}
of finite signed measures
on
(\Omega,{\cal F})
such that
{\cal Z}
and
{\cal Z}^{*}
are paired
vector spaces with respect to the bilinear form

\langle Z,Q\rangle:=\int_{\Omega}Z(\omega)\,Q(d\omega),\quad Z\in{\cal Z},\ Q\in{\cal Z}^{*}.

(3)

The integral in (
3
) is supposed to be well-defined and finite valued for all
Z\in{\cal Z}
and
Q\in{\cal Z}^{*}
.

We assume that
{\mathfrak{M}}\subset{\cal Z}^{*}
and hence, for any
Z\in{\cal Z}
and
Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}
, the expectation
\E_{Q}[Z]=\langle Z,Q\rangle
is well-defined and finite valued.

Stochastic optimization problems are often formulated by involving continuous probability measures, but computations need to be implemented with discrete measures. It is thus essential to elaborate on both, discrete and continuous probability measures. For this reason we consider and address the following settings separately before combining them in the general framework.

2.1
Random variables with finite moments

Consider the
space
{\cal Z}:=L_{p}(\Omega,{\cal F},P)
,
p\in[1,\infty)
, of random variables
Z:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
with finite
p
-th order moments.
We refer to
P
as the
reference
probability measure.
The space
{\cal Z}
, equipped with the corresponding norm
\|\cdot\|_{p}
, is a Banach space. Its dual space (of continuous linear functionals) is
L_{q}(\Omega,{\cal F},P)
such that
1/p+1/q=1
,
q\in(1,\infty]
. Suppose that the set
{\mathfrak{M}}
consists of probability measures
Q
absolutely continuous with respect to
P
such that their density
\zeta=dQ/dP
belongs to the space
L_{q}(\Omega,{\cal F},P)
.

Specifically, we pair
{\cal Z}=L_{p}(\Omega,{\cal F},P)
with its dual
{\cal Z}^{*}=L_{q}(\Omega,{\cal F},P)
with the respective bilinear form

\langle Z,\zeta\rangle:=\int_{\Omega}Z(\omega)\zeta(\omega)\,P(d\omega),\quad Z\in{\cal Z},\ \zeta\in{\cal Z}^{*}.

(4)

Note that here
Z\in{\cal Z}
is actually a class of measurable variables which can be different from each other on sets of
P
-measure zero. We have that the respective functional
{\cal R}(\cdot)
is finite valued on
{\cal Z}
iff the set

{\mathfrak{A}}:=\{\zeta=dQ/dP\colon Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}\}

(5)

is bounded in the corresponding norm
\|\cdot\|_{q}
. The set
{\mathfrak{A}}
can be assumed to be convex and closed in the weak
∗
topology of the dual space
{\cal Z}^{*}
. We refer to
{\mathfrak{A}}
as the dual set of the risk functional
{\cal R}
.

2.2
Continuous random variables

Suppose that the set
\Omega
is a compact metric space and
{\cal F}
is its Borel
\sigma
-algebra. Let
{\cal Z}:=C(\Omega)
be the space of continuous functions
Z\colon\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
equipped with the supremum norm
\|Z\|_{\infty}=\sup_{\omega\in\Omega}|Z(\omega)|
. The dual space of
C(\Omega)
is formed by finite signed measures on
(\Omega,{\cal F})
with respect to the bilinear form (
3
) (Riesz representation).
It could be noted that the dual norm
\|\cdot\|^{*}
of
{\cal Z}^{*}=C(\Omega)^{*}
is the total variation norm and
\|Q\|^{*}=1
for any probability measure
Q\in{\mathfrak{P}}
. Also, for any
Q\in{\mathfrak{P}}
and
Z
,
Z^{\prime}\in{\cal Z}
we have that

\E_{Q}[Z^{\prime}]=\E_{Q}[Z+Z^{\prime}-Z]\leq\E_{Q}[Z]+a,

where
a:=\sup_{\omega\in\Omega}|Z^{\prime}(\omega)-Z(\omega)|=\|Z^{\prime}-Z\|_{\infty}
.
It follows that
{\cal R}(\cdot)
, as defined in (
2
), is finite valued and

|{\cal R}(Z^{\prime})-{\cal R}(Z)|\leq\|Z^{\prime}-Z\|_{\infty},

(6)

i.e.,
{\cal R}
is Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant
1
.

2.3
Bounded functions

Consider the space
{\cal Z}:={\mathbb{B}}(\Omega)
consisting of measurable and bounded functions
Z\colon\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
. Equipped with the supremum norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
this becomes a Banach space. Its dual (of continuous linear functionals) is quite complicated.
Nevertheless, we can pair it with the space
{\cal Z}^{*}
of finite signed measures on
(\Omega,{\cal F})
employing the bilinear form (
3
).
As in the previous example, it can be shown that the inequality (
6
) holds for any
Z
,
Z^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{B}}(\Omega)
, and hence the corresponding distributionally robust functional (
2
) is finite valued and continuous in the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
topology. However, we will have to deal here with the paired topologies of the spaces
{\cal Z}
and
{\cal Z}^{*}
.

There is a natural order relation making the space
{\cal Z}
from any of the three subsections above a partially ordered set. For
Z
,
Z^{\prime}\in{\cal Z}
we write
Z^{\prime}\succeq Z
(or
Z\preceq Z^{\prime}
) if
Z^{\prime}\geq Z
almost surely with respect to the reference measure
P
in the
L_{p}
setting, and
Z^{\prime}(\omega)\geq Z(\omega)
for all
\omega\in\Omega
in the
C(\Omega)
and
{\mathbb{B}}(\Omega)
settings.

2.4
Correspondence of robust functionals with risk functionals

It is straightforward to verify that the distributionally robust functional (
2
) satisfies the following conditions for any
Z
,
Z^{\prime}\in{\cal Z}
:

1.

subadditivity:

{\cal R}(Z+Z^{\prime})\leq{\cal R}(Z)+{\cal R}(Z^{\prime})
,

2.

monotonicity:
if
Z^{\prime}\succeq Z
,
then
{\cal R}(Z^{\prime})\geq{\cal R}(Z)
,

3.

translation equivariance:
if
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
, then
{\cal R}(Z+a)={\cal R}(Z)+a
,

4.

positive homogeneity:
if
\lambda>0
, then

{\cal R}(\lambda\,Z)=\lambda\,{\cal R}(Z)
.

Conversely, in the
L_{p}
and
C(\Omega)
frameworks,
any
real valued
functional
{\cal R}\colon{\cal Z}\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfying the axioms
1
–
4
is continuous and can be represented in the dual form (
1
)
for an appropriate set
{\mathfrak{M}}
of probability measures (cf.
[
16
, Proposition 3.1]
).
For the
{\mathbb{B}}(\Omega)
setting this is more involved, in order to guarantee the dual representation it should be also verified that
{\cal R}
is lower semi-continuous with respect to the considered paired topologies.

In the risk averse perspective the most important example of the functional
{\cal R}
is the Average Value-at-Risk

\avr_{\alpha}(Z)=\inf_{\tau\in{\mathbb{R}}}\left\{\tau+(1-\alpha)^{-1}\E_{P}[Z-\tau]_{+}\right\},\;\alpha\in[0,1),

(7)

with
{\cal Z}=L_{1}(\Omega,{\cal F},P)
.
In some publications it is also called Conditional Value-at-Risk, Expected Shortfall, Expected Tail Loss; variational representation (
7
) is due to Rockafellar and Uryasev

[
13
]
.

In the dual form, it has the representation (
2
) with the corresponding dual set

{\mathfrak{A}}=\left\{\zeta\colon 0\leq\zeta\leq 1/(1-\alpha),\text{ and }\int_{\Omega}\zeta\,dP=1\right\},

(8)

which constitute densities with respect to the reference measure
P
.
For
\alpha=1
,
\AVaR_{1}(Z)=\esssup(Z)
is the essential supremum.

3
Conditional counterparts of distributionally robust functionals

To set up the distributionally robust optimization in a multistage framework we need to define a conditional counterpart of the robust functional
{\cal R}

defined in (
2
). To this end let
{\cal G}
be a
\sigma
-subalgebra of
{\cal F}
. Then for any
Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}
we can consider the corresponding conditional expectation
\E_{Q|{\cal G}}[Z]
of a random variable
Z\in{\cal Z}

(we can refer to
[
8
]
, e.g., for a rigorous discussion of the precise meaning of conditional expectations).

3.1
Conditional discrepancy of risk functionals

A general caveat arises with a rigorous definition of conditional functionals, i.e., with functionals conditioned on
\sigma
-subalgebras.
To elaborate the disparity we consider a conditional counterpart of the risk functional (
2
) first and a variant next, which is most common in the literature. Both approaches seem natural in
convenient
contexts, but they differ essentially.

3.1.1
The conditional analogue

Consider the conditional counterpart

{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}(Z)=\esssup_{Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}}\E_{Q|{\cal G}}[Z]

(9)

of (
2
). A rigorous meaning of (
9
) is given in Section
3.4
below; however, the inconsistency already occurs in a finite setting in which the essential supremum in (
9
) is a usual maximum.

Definition 1
.

We say that a family
\{A_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of nonempty sets
A_{i}\in{\cal F}
is a
partition
of a set
A\in{\cal F}
if
\cup_{i\in I}A_{i}=A
and
A_{i}\cap A_{j}=\emptyset
for
i\neq j
. It is said that the partition is countable (finite) if the index set
I
is countable (finite).

Example 2
.

Suppose the set
\Omega=\{\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n}\}
is finite, equipped with the
\sigma
-algebra
{\cal F}
of all its subsets and the reference probability measure
P
assigns
probabilities
p_{i}>0
to each
\omega_{i}\in\Omega
,
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Then any probability measure
Q
on
(\Omega,{\cal F})
is absolutely continuous with respect to
P
, and with a subalgebra
{\cal G}
of
{\cal F}
is associated a finite partition
\{\Upsilon_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of
\Omega
such that a variable
Z\colon\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
is
{\cal G}
-measurable iff it is constant on every
\Upsilon_{i}
,
i\in I
.

Now suppose that the ambiguity set
{\mathfrak{M}}
has the following property:

\begin{array}[]{lll}&&\text{For every $i\in I$ and every $\bar{\omega}\in\Upsilon_{i}$ there is a measure $Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}$ such that:}\\
&&\text{ $Q(\{\bar{\omega}\})>0$ and
$Q(\{\omega\})=0$ for all $\omega\in\Upsilon_{i}\setminus\{\bar{\omega}\}$.}\end{array}

(10)

For such a measure
Q
the conditional expectation
\E_{Q|{\cal G}}[Z](\cdot)
is constant on the set
\Upsilon_{i}
with the corresponding value

\E_{Q}[Z\,{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{\Upsilon_{i}}]/Q(\Upsilon_{i})=Z(\bar{\omega}).

(11)

It follows with (
9
) that the corresponding conditional risk functional is

{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}(Z)(\omega)=\max_{\omega^{\prime}\in\Upsilon_{i}}Z(\omega^{\prime}),\quad\omega\in\Upsilon_{i}.

(12)

That is,
{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}(Z)
is the conditional supremum of
Z
, conditioned on the atoms
\Upsilon_{i}
of the
\sigma
-algebra
{\cal G}
. Note that the conditional risk functional (
12
) is independent of the reference measure
P
, provided that
P(\{\omega\})>0
for all
\omega\in\Omega
.

The property (
10
) above is not uncommon.
Consider, for example, the functional
{\cal R}:=\avr_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Its dual representation (
8
), of the ambiguity (dual) set
{\mathfrak{A}}
,
has the property (
10
) if
P(\Upsilon_{i})\leq\alpha
for all
i\in I
, i.e., if the atoms of
{\cal G}
are small relative to
\alpha
.

Indeed, the set
{\mathfrak{A}}
consists of densities
\zeta
such that
0\leq\zeta(\omega)\leq(1-\alpha)^{-1}
for all
\omega\in\Omega
, and
\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\zeta(\omega)P(\{\omega\})=1
. For
i\in I
consider
\Upsilon_{i}
and a point
\bar{\omega}\in\Upsilon_{i}
. Choose
\zeta\in{\mathfrak{A}}
such that

\zeta(\bar{\omega})=1
, and
\zeta(\omega)=0
for
\omega\in\Upsilon_{i}\setminus\{\bar{\omega})
and
\zeta(\omega):=\kappa

for
\omega\in\Omega\setminus\Upsilon_{i}
with
\kappa:=\frac{1-{P(\{\bar{\omega}}\})}{1-P(\Upsilon_{i})}
. Note that
0\leq\kappa\leq(1-\alpha)^{-1}
since
P(\Upsilon_{i})\leq\alpha
.
The probability measure corresponding to this density is the required measure
Q
.

3.1.2
The law invariant analogue

The definition (
7
) of the
\avr_{\alpha}
leads to the following definition of its conditional counterpart (e.g.,
[
15
]
)

\avr_{\alpha|{\cal G}}(Z)=\essinf_{Y\in L_{1}(\Omega,{\cal G},P)}\left\{Y+(1-\alpha)^{-1}\E_{P|{\cal G}}[Z-Y]_{+}\right\}.

(13)

The above Example
2
demonstrates the difference between the nested
\avr
, defined in (
13
), and the distributionally robust counterpart of
\avr
obtained from the dual representation (
8
).

In contrast to formula (
12
), the nested
\avr_{\alpha|{\cal G}}(Z)
takes the value given by the respective Average Value-at-Risk on every set
\Upsilon_{i}
.
The approach (
9
) seems to be natural from the distributional robustness point of view while it is different from the nested approach to (law invariant) risk measures. We are going to discuss this in the next section.

3.2
The “right” conditional analogue

In the risk averse approach it is natural to consider law invariant risk measures such that the value
{\cal R}(Z)
depends only on the distribution (with respect to the reference probability measure) of
Z
. Then intuitively the nested conditional counterpart is defined in the same way as a function of the conditional distribution of
P
. The definition (
13
) of the nested Average Value-at-Risk is of that form.

On the other hand, suppose for example that the ambiguity set
{\mathfrak{M}}
is given by convex combinations of a finite set
\{Q_{1},\dots,Q_{m}\}
of probability measures. Then the definition via (
9
) makes sense. Also in some cases the functional
{\cal R}
is not law invariant, and consequently its law invariant conditional counterpart cannot be defined.
So the question of what is a
right
definition of the conditional counterpart of the distributionally robust functional is open for discussion.
The nested approach was discussed extensively in the literature on risk averse stochastic optimization
(e.g.,
[
12
,
15
]
).
On the other hand the conditional counterpart (
9
) appears naturally as an extension of the definition (
2
) of the distributionally robust functional
(e.g.,
[
17
]
).
In Section
4.2
we will discuss the
rectangular
setting where both approaches are equivalent.
Unless stated otherwise we deal in the remainder with the conditional distributed robust functional
{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}
stated in (
9
) above.

To address the mathematical foundation we need to give a rigorous meaning to the setting (
9
) for uncountable measurable spaces.
It is tempting to define the conditional counterpart of
{\cal R}(Z)
as the pointwise supremum,

{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}(Z)(\omega):=\sup_{Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}}\E_{Q|{\cal G}}[Z](\omega),\quad\omega\in\Omega.

(14)

However, there are several technical problems with a rigorous meaning of the right-hand side of (
14
). Actually
\E_{Q|{\cal G}}[Z]
is a class of
{\cal G}
-measurable functions such that its versions can be different from each other on a set of
Q
-measure zero. So it is not clear what the supremum of such functions over
Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}
really means; even if properly defined, its measurability is not obvious when the set
{\mathfrak{M}}
is uncountable.
We will need the concepts of essential supremum and infimum, discussed in the next section.

3.3
The essential supremum

Unless stated differently we assume in this section that there is a reference probability measure
P
on
(\Omega,{\cal F})
and that every
Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
P
. We use the notation
Q\ll P
to denote that
Q
is absolutely continuous with respect to
P
. We employ the concept of essential supremum.

Let us start with some basic definitions of partially ordered sets. A binary relation
\preceq
on a set
{\mathfrak{S}}
is said to be a
partial order
on
{\mathfrak{S}}

if for any
x
,
x^{\prime}
,
x^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathfrak{S}}
the following properties hold:
(i)
x\preceq x
,
(ii) if
x\preceq x^{\prime}
and
x^{\prime}\preceq x
, then
x=x^{\prime}
,
(iii) if
x\preceq x^{\prime}
and
x^{\prime}\preceq x^{\prime\prime}
, then
x\preceq x^{\prime\prime}
.
It said that an element
y\in{\mathfrak{S}}
is an
upper bound
for a (nonempty) set
{\mathfrak{V}}\subset{\mathfrak{S}}
if
x\preceq y
for any
x\in{\mathfrak{V}}
. If an upper bound
y
of
{\mathfrak{V}}
belongs to
{\mathfrak{V}}
, then
y
is said to be the
largest element
of
{\mathfrak{V}}
. The lower bound and the smallest element of
{\mathfrak{V}}
are defined in the analogous way.
Note that by the property (ii) of the partial order, it follows that if the largest (smallest) element exists, then it is unique. The least upper bound of
{\mathfrak{V}}
, which is the smallest element of the set of the upper bounds of
{\mathfrak{V}}
, is
called the supremum of
{\mathfrak{V}}
and denoted
\sup{\mathfrak{V}}
. That is,
\sup{\mathfrak{V}}
is an element of
{\mathfrak{S}}
such that
x\preceq\sup{\mathfrak{V}}
for any
x\in{\mathfrak{V}}
, and if
y
is an upper bound of
{\mathfrak{V}}
, then
\sup{\mathfrak{V}}\preceq y
. If the supremum
\sup{\mathfrak{V}}
exists, then it is unique.

Clearly if the set
{\mathfrak{V}}
has the largest element
\bar{x}
, then
\bar{x}=\sup{\mathfrak{V}}
.
With the set
{\mathfrak{V}}
we associate its order complement

{\mathfrak{V}}^{c}:=\{y\in{\mathfrak{S}}\colon x\preceq y\text{ for all }x\in{\mathfrak{V}}\}.

(15)

It is straightforward to verify the following well known result.

Lemma 3
.

The supremum
\sup{\mathfrak{V}}
exists iff its order complement set
{\mathfrak{V}}^{c}
has the smallest element
\bar{y}
, in which case
\bar{y}=\sup{\mathfrak{V}}
.

For random variables
X
,
Y
we can define the partial order
X\preceq Y

meaning that
X\leq Y
almost surely with respect to the reference measure
P
.
In the considered setting the supremum of a set
{\mathfrak{X}}
of measurable functions is called the essential supremum of
{\mathfrak{X}}
. Denote by
\overline{\mathbb{R}}:=\mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
the extended real line.

Definition 4
.

The
essential supremum
of a (nonempty) set
{\mathfrak{X}}
of measurable functions
X\colon\Omega\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
, denoted
\esssup{\mathfrak{X}}
, is a measurable function

X^{*}
satisfying the following properties:
(i)

for any
X\in{\mathfrak{X}}
it follows that
X\preceq X^{*}
,

(ii)
if
Y
is a measurable function such that
X\preceq Y
for all
X\in{\mathfrak{X}}
, then
X^{*}\preceq Y
.

The
essential infimum
is defined in the analogous way and is denoted
\essinf{\mathfrak{X}}
, that is
\essinf{\mathfrak{X}}:=-\esssup(-{\mathfrak{X}}).
Let us emphasize that these concepts of the essential supremum and infimum are defined with respect to the reference measure
P
.
In the setting of Definition
4
the essential supremum always exists and,
as it was pointed above, is unique (e.g.,
[
4
, Appendix A.5]
).

Proposition 5
.

Let
{\mathfrak{X}}=\{X_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a nonempty set of measurable functions
X_{i}\colon\Omega\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
. Then
the essential supremum
X^{*}=\esssup{\mathfrak{X}}
exists and, moreover, there is a countable set

J\subset I
such that
P
-almost surely
X^{*}=\sup_{i\in J}X_{i}
.

Remark 6
.

In some settings there is no natural reference probability measure.
In such cases we can consider the settings of
{\cal Z}=C(\Omega)
or
{\cal Z}={\mathbb{B}}(\Omega)
. Let
{\mathfrak{X}}\subset{\cal Z}
be a nonempty set of functions and
{\mathfrak{X}}^{c}
be its order complement.
Consider the pointwise partial order
X\preceq Y
,
meaning that
X(\omega)\leq Y(\omega)
for all
\omega\in\Omega
, and the
respective pointwise supremum and pointwise infimum functions

X^{*}(\omega):=\sup_{X\in{\mathfrak{X}}}X(\omega)\text{ and }\bar{X}(\omega):=\inf_{X\in{\mathfrak{X}}^{c}}X(\omega),\;\omega\in\Omega.

It is not difficult to see that the set
{\mathfrak{X}}
has the largest element iff
X^{*}\in{\cal Z}
(i.e.,
X^{*}
is continuous in the
C(\Omega)
setting and measurable in the
{\mathbb{B}}(\Omega)
setting), in which case
X^{*}=\esssup{\mathfrak{X}}
. Also, by Lemma
3
we have that the
\esssup{\mathfrak{X}}
exists iff
\bar{X}
belongs to
{\cal Z}
, in which case
\bar{X}(\omega)=\esssup{\mathfrak{X}}
.

For example,
consider the
C(\Omega)
-setting with
\Omega:=[0,1]
. Let
[a,b]
be an interval such that
0<a\leq b<1
, and
{\mathfrak{X}}
be the set of functions
X\colon[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that
0\leq X(\omega)\leq 1
,
\omega\in[0,1]
,
X(\omega)=0
,
\omega\in[a,b]
. Here the pointwise supremum function
X^{*}
has the form
X^{*}(\omega)=0
for
\omega\in[a,b]
, and
X^{*}(\omega)=1
otherwise. Since
X^{*}
is not a continuous function, the set
{\mathfrak{X}}
does not have the largest element.
Consider now the order complement
{\mathfrak{X}}^{c}
and the corresponding pointwise infimum function
\bar{X}
.
If
a<b
, then
\bar{X}(\omega)=0
for
\omega\in(a,b)
, and
\bar{X}(\omega)=1
otherwise. In that case the set
{\mathfrak{X}}^{c}
does not have the smallest element and the essential supremum of the set
{\mathfrak{X}}
does not exist. On the other hand if
a=b
, then the constant function
\bar{X}(\cdot)\equiv 1
is the smallest element of
{\mathfrak{X}}^{c}
and is the essential supremum of
{\mathfrak{X}}
.

3.4
Conditioning risk functionals

This section resumes the
L_{p}
-setting described in Section
2.1
above; that is,
{\cal Z}=L_{p}(\Omega,{\cal F},P)
.
It could be noted that conditional expectations with respect to different probability measures could have arbitrary values on sets having positive reference probability measure.
The following remark elaborates the consequences for the conditional risk functional.

Remark 7
.

Let
P
be a reference probability measure on
(\Omega,{\cal F})
.
Suppose that
{\cal G}={\cal F}
.
For any
Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}
and
{\cal F}
-measurable variable
Z\colon\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
, we have that
Q
-almost surely
\E_{Q|{\cal F}}[Z]=Z
.
If
{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}
is viewed as a mapping from
{L}_{p}(\Omega,{\cal F},P)
to
{L}_{p}(\Omega,{\cal G},P)
, then the respective translation equivariance axiom (cf.
3
) requires that
P
-almost surely
{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}(Y)=Y
for any
{\cal G}
-measurable
Y
. When
{\cal G}={\cal F}
this means that
P
-almost surely
{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}(Z)=Z
for any
Z\in{\cal Z}
.
On the other hand, it could happen for some
Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}
and
A\in{\cal F}
that
Q(A)=0
while
P(A)>0
, i.e.,
P
is not absolutely continuous with respect to
Q
.
In that case a version of
\E_{Q|{\cal F}}[Z]
can have arbitrary values on the set
A
,
and hence is not equal
Z
almost surely with respect to the reference measure
P
.

In order to provide a definition of conditional distributionally robust functionals we use the concept of essential supremum applied to the
family
\left\{\E_{Q|{\cal G}}[Z](\cdot)\colon Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}\right\}
of
{\cal G}
-measurable functions.
For that we need to choose a specific version of
\E_{Q|{\cal G}}[Z]
for a given
Z\in{\cal Z}
. If
P
is absolutely continuous with respect to
every

Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}
, then we can use any version of
\E_{Q|{\cal G}}[Z]
. However, there are natural examples where this does not hold for some
Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}
. In order to deal with this we consider the essential infimum (taken with respect to
P
) of the set of versions of
\E_{Q|{\cal G}}[Z]
, denoted

\essinf\E_{Q|{\cal G}}[Z]
.

Definition 8
.

For a probability measure
Q
and a random variable
Z
we define

\essinf\E_{Q|\mathcal{G}}[Z]

(taken with respect to
P
) as the essential infimum of the set of
versions of
\E_{Q|{\cal G}}[Z]
.

The
\essinf\E_{Q|{\cal G}}[Z]
takes the value
-\infty
on any set
A\in{\cal G}
such that
Q(A)=0
while
P(A)>0
.
If
P
is absolutely continuous with respect to
Q
, then

\essinf\E_{Q|{\cal G}}[Z]=\E_{Q|{\cal G}}[Z]
.
The essential infimum
\essinf\E_{Q|{\cal G}}[Z]
exists for every
Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}
. However, for
Z\in L_{p}(\Omega,{\cal F},P)
, the corresponding
\essinf\E_{Q|{\cal G}}[Z]
belongs to
L_{p}(\Omega,{\cal F},P)
only if it takes the value
-\infty
on a set of
P
-measure zero, that is only if
P\ll Q
.

The essential supremum of the family
\left\{\essinf\E_{Q|{\cal G}}[Z],\;Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}\right\}
leads to the following definition of the conditional functional
{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}
. As before,
X\preceq Y

means that
X\leq Y
almost surely with respect to the reference measure
P
.

Definition 9
(Conditional distributionally robust functional).

Let
{\cal G}
be a
\sigma
-subalgebra of
{\cal F}
. A version of the conditional distributionally robust functional is defined as a
{\cal G}
-measurable variable
{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}(Z)\colon\Omega\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}

satisfying the following properties:

(i)

for every
Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}
the inequality
\essinf\E_{Q|{\cal G}}[Z]\preceq{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}(Z)
holds,

(ii)

if
Y\colon\Omega\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is a
{\cal G}
-measurable variable such that
\essinf\E_{Q|{\cal G}}[Z]\preceq Y
holds for every
Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}
, then
{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}(Z)\preceq Y
.

We use the notation

{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}(Z)=\ess_{Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}}\E_{Q|{\cal G}}[Z]

(16)

for the
conditional distributionally robust
functional.

Note that if
{\cal G}=\{\emptyset,\Omega\}
is trivial, then
{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}
coincides with the initial distributionally robust functional
{\cal R}
in (
2
).

Proposition 10
.

For all
Z\in{\cal Z}
it holds that

{\cal R}(Z)\leq{\cal R}\big({\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}(Z)\big).

Proof.

For every
Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}
we have from the tower property of the expectation that

\E_{Q}{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}(Z)\geq\E_{Q}\E_{Q|{\cal G}}[Z]=\E_{Q}[Z].

The assertion follows by passing to the supremum with respect to
Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}
.

It is not difficult to verify that
{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}
satisfies the respective counterparts of the axioms
1
,
2
and
4
specified in Section
2
.
The conditional counterpart of the translation equivariance axiom
3
is:
{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}(Z+Y)={\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}(Z)+Y
for any
Z\in{\cal Z}
and
{\cal G}
-measurable
Y\in{\cal Z}
. If there is a set
A\in{\cal F}
such that
P(A)>0
and
Q(A)=0
for
every

Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}
, then
{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}
would not satisfy the translation equivariance axiom (see the discussion of Remark
7
).
If
P\in{\mathfrak{M}}
then this cannot happen and the translation equivariance axiom follows.

3.5
Conditioning on strictly monotone risk functionals

It was already mentioned that the conditional distributionally robust
functional
{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}
is monotone, i.e., if
Z^{\prime}\succeq Z
, then
{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}(Z^{\prime})\succeq{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}(Z)
.
For verification of certain interchangeability properties of minimization operators and risk measures, which are used for deriving the dynamic equations, there is a need for a stronger property of strict monotonicity
(cf.
[
18
]
).

When
Z^{\prime}\succeq Z
and
Z^{\prime}\neq Z
we write this as
Z^{\prime}\succ Z
, which means that
P
-almost surely
Z^{\prime}\geq Z
and
P\{Z^{\prime}>Z\}>0
.

Definition 11
(Strict monotonicity).

It is said that
{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}
is
strictly monotone
if for any random variables
Z^{\prime}
,
Z
such that
{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}(Z^{\prime})
and
{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}(Z)
are well-defined the following implication holds:

Z^{\prime}\succ Z
implies
{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}(Z^{\prime})\succ{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}(Z)
.

If
{\cal G}=\{\emptyset,\Omega\}
is trivial, then
{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}={\cal R}
and Definition
11
reduces to the definition of strict monotonicity for the distributionally robust functional
{\cal R}
.
The natural question is:
“What is a relation between the strict monotonicity of
{\cal R}
and its conditional counterpart?”

Proposition 12
.

Suppose that
{\cal R}
is strictly monotone. Then for any
A\in{\cal F}
such that
P(A)>0
there is
\varepsilon>0
such that
Q(A)\geq\varepsilon
for every
Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}
.

Proof.

Let

{\mathfrak{A}}:=\{\zeta\in{\cal Z}^{*}\colon\zeta=dQ/dP,\;Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}\}

be the corresponding (convex, bounded, weakly
∗
closed) dual set of density functions.
We have that
{\cal R}
is strictly monotone iff (cf.
[
18
]
)

\int_{A}\zeta(\omega)P(d\omega)>0\;\text{for every}\;\zeta\in{\mathfrak{A}}\;\text{ and any}\;A\in{\cal F}\;\text{such that}\;P(A)>0.

(17)

Note that since the set
{\mathfrak{A}}
is weakly
∗
compact, the minimum of
\int_{A}\zeta\,dP
over
\zeta\in{\mathfrak{A}}
is attained. Therefore, for the respective set
{\mathfrak{M}}
condition (
17
) can be restated as announced.

The property (
17
) implies that
P
is absolutely continuous with respect to every
Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}
. Thus, if
{\cal R}
is strictly monotone, then

\essinf\E_{Q|{\cal G}}[Z]
coincides with

\E_{Q|{\cal G}}[Z]
for every
Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}
.

Remark 13
.

Suppose that
{\cal G}={\cal F}
, and
P
-almost surely
{\cal R}_{|{\cal F}}(Z)=Z
for every
Z\in{\cal Z}
. Then
{\cal R}_{|{\cal F}}(Z^{\prime})\neq{\cal R}_{|{\cal F}}(Z)
whenever
Z^{\prime}\neq Z
, and hence
{\cal R}_{|{\cal F}}
is strictly monotone by the definition. On the other hand,
{\cal R}
can be not strictly monotone, i.e., it can happen that there is a measurable set
A
such that
Q(A)=0
for some
Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}
while
P(A)>0
.
This indicates that the conditional functional
{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}
can be strictly monotone even if
{\cal R}
is not.

Proposition 14
.

If the distributionally robust functional
{\cal R}
is strictly monotone, then the respective conditional distributionally robust functional
{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}
is strictly monotone.

Proof.

Suppose that
{\cal R}
is strictly monotone. Let

Z^{\prime}
,
Z\in{\cal Z}
be such that
Z^{\prime}\succ Z
.
It follows that
Z^{\prime}\succeq Z+Y
, where
Y=\gamma\,{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{A}

for some
\gamma>0
and
A\in{\cal F}
such that
P(A)>0
. We need to show that
{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}(Z^{\prime})\neq{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}(Z)
. We argue by a contradiction, so suppose that
{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}(Z^{\prime})={\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}(Z)
.
By Proposition
5
there is a sequence
\{Q_{n}\}\subset{\mathfrak{M}}
such that
{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}[Z]=\sup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\E_{Q_{n}|{\cal G}}[Z]
.
We have that

{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}(Z^{\prime})\succeq\sup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\E_{Q_{n}|{\cal G}}[Z^{\prime}]=\sup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left(\E_{Q_{n}|{\cal G}}[Z]+\E_{Q_{n}|{\cal G}}[Y]\right)\succeq\sup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\E_{Q_{n}|{\cal G}}[Z].

It follows that
\E_{Q_{n}|{\cal G}}[Y]
converges a.s. to
0
as
n\to\infty
.
On the other hand,

\E\left\{\E_{Q_{n}|{\cal G}}[Y]\right\}=\E[Y]=\gamma\,Q_{n}(A).

Moreover, by (
17
) we have that
Q_{n}(A)\geq\varepsilon
for some
\varepsilon>0
and all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. It follows that
\E_{Q_{n}|{\cal G}}[Y]
cannot converge a.s. to
0
, which gives the required contradiction. This completes the proof that
{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}
is strictly monotone.

As it was pointed in Remark
13
, the converse implication: “if
{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}
is strictly monotone, then
{\cal R}
is strictly monotone” does not hold.

3.6
The reference measure

The preceding subsections require that there exists a reference probability measure. However, there are natural examples where there is no a priori defined reference probability measure. That is, the measures in
{\mathfrak{M}}
are not necessarily defined with respect to a reference measure and indeed, there are situations where a priori reference probability measure does not exist.
This section describes situations where it is possible to construct a reference probability measure
P
on
(\Omega,{\cal F})
.
We say that a probability measure
P
is a
reference measure
for
{\mathfrak{M}}
if every
Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
P
.
The following construction reveals the smallest of all reference measures dominating all measures in
{\mathfrak{M}}
.

Consider

\mu(A):=\sup\limits_{Q_{i}\in{\mathfrak{M}}}\left\{\sum_{i\in I}Q_{i}(A_{i})\colon A=\bigcup_{i\in I}A_{i},\;|I|<\infty\right\},\quad A\in{\cal F}.

(18)

The supremum in (
18
) is taken among all finite partitions

\{A_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of the set
A
and
\{Q_{i}\colon i\in I\}\subset{\mathfrak{M}}
.
In particular, if the set
\Omega=\{\omega_{1},\dots\}
is countable equipped with the
\sigma
-algebra
{\cal F}
of all its subsets, then

\mu(\{\omega_{i}\})=\sup_{Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}}Q(\{\omega_{i}\}).

(19)

Proposition 15
(Reference measure).

The set valued function
\mu(\cdot)
, defined in (
18
),
is the smallest measure such that

Q(A)\leq\mu(A),\quad\forall Q\in{\mathfrak{M}},A\in{\cal F}.

(20)

Proof.

It is evident that
\mu(A)\geq 0
and
\mu(\emptyset)=0
. To demonstrate that (
18
) is a measure it remains to demonstrate its
\sigma
-additivity.
To this end consider a set
B\in{\cal F}
and let
\{B_{j}\}_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be its countable partition. By the definition of
\mu
for any
\varepsilon>0
there is a finite partition
\{A_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of
B
such that

\mu(B)<\sum_{i\in I}\mu(A_{i})+\varepsilon.

(21)

Then

\mu(B)-\varepsilon<\sum_{i\in I}\mu(A_{i})=\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\sum_{i\in I}Q_{i}(A_{i}\cap B_{j})\leq\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mu(B_{j}).

For the converse inequality, let
\{A_{ji}\}_{i\in I_{j}}
be a partition of
B_{j}
,
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
, such that

\mu(B_{j})<\sum_{i\in I_{j}}Q_{i}(A_{ji})+\varepsilon\,2^{-j}.

(22)

By summing (
22
) for
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we obtain that
\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mu(B_{j})<\mu(B)+\varepsilon
. This demonstrates that
\mu(B)=\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mu(B_{j})
, and hence
\mu
is a measure.

Finally, let
\nu
be a measure such that
\nu(B)\geq Q(B)
for all
Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}
. Let the partition be chosen as in (
21
).
Then

\nu(B)=\sum_{i\in I}\nu(A_{i})\geq\sum_{i\in I}Q_{i}(A_{i})>\mu(B)-\varepsilon.

It follows that
\nu\geq\mu
and
\mu
thus is the smallest of all measures dominating the measures in
{\mathfrak{M}}
.

Remark 16
.

We have by (
20
) that
\mu
dominates every measure
Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}
, i.e.,
Q\ll\mu
for all
Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}
.
The measure
\mu
is not necessarily a probability measure. If
\mu(\Omega)<\infty
, then it can be normalized by considering the probability measure

\tilde{P}(A):=\mu(A)/\mu(\Omega)
,
A\in{\cal F}
.

3.7
Conditioning risk functionals on countable partitions

As it was pointed above, in some settings there is no a priori defined reference probability measure on
(\Omega,{\cal F})
. This motivates to consider the following construction for conditioned risk functionals on countably generated sigma fields.

Let
\{\Upsilon_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a
countable
partition of the set
\Omega
(cf. Definition
1
). This partition generates a subalgebra
{\cal G}:=\sigma(\Upsilon_{i}\colon i\in I)
of
{\cal F}
, consisting of the empty set and the sets
A=\cup_{j\in{\cal J}}\Upsilon_{j}
taken over all index sets
{\cal J}\subset I
.
Then
X\colon\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
is
{\cal G}
-measurable iff
X(\omega)
is constant on every
\Upsilon_{i}
,
i\in I
.
The conditional expectation
\E_{Q|{\cal G}}[X]
is a

{\cal G}
-measurable function
\E_{Q|{\cal G}}[X]\colon\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}

taking the following value at
\omega\in\Upsilon_{i}
,
i\in I
, such that
Q(\Upsilon_{i})\neq 0
,

\displaystyle\E_{Q|{\cal G}}[X](\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Q(\Upsilon_{i})}\int_{\Upsilon_{i}}X(\omega^{\prime})Q(d\omega^{\prime})

(23)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Q(\Upsilon_{i})}\sum_{\omega^{\prime}\in\Upsilon_{i}}Q(\{\omega^{\prime}\})\,X(\omega^{\prime}).

(24)

When the
\sigma
-subalgebra
{\cal G}
is generated by the countable partition, formula (
23
) defines a version of the corresponding conditional expectation. Again any
two versions of
\E_{Q|{\cal G}}[X]
can have different values on such
\Upsilon_{i}
that
Q(\Upsilon_{i})=0
.

As above, we can now define a version of
{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}(Z)
as a
{\cal G}
-measurable variable taking value

\displaystyle{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}(Z)(\omega)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sup_{Q\in{\mathfrak{M}},\;Q(\Upsilon_{i})\neq 0}\frac{1}{Q(\Upsilon_{i})}\int_{\Upsilon_{i}}Z(\omega^{\prime})Q(d\omega^{\prime})

(25)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{Q\in{\mathfrak{M}},\;Q(\Upsilon_{i})\neq 0}\E_{Q|{\cal G}}[Z](\omega)

at
\omega\in\Upsilon_{i}
such that
Q(\Upsilon_{i})\neq 0
for at least one
Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}
.

We have here that
\essinf\E_{Q|{\cal G}}[Z](\omega)=-\infty
for such
\omega\in\Upsilon_{i}
that
Q(\Upsilon_{i})=0
. Therefore, in the case of countable partition, formula (
25
) gives a closed form expression for the
\esssup_{Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}}\E_{Q|{\cal G}}[Z]
described in Definition
9
.
Let
\bar{\Upsilon}
be the union of the sets
\Upsilon_{i}
such that
Q(\Upsilon_{i})\neq 0
for at least one
Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}
.
Then a version of the conditional functional
{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}(Z)(\omega)
is uniquely defined for
\omega\in\bar{\Upsilon}
, and can be arbitrary on
\Omega\setminus\bar{\Upsilon}
. If the set
\bar{\Upsilon}
is strictly smaller than
\Omega
, i.e., there is
i\in I
such that
Q(\Upsilon_{i})=0
for all
Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}
, then the corresponding translation equivariance property does not hold. In fact, it is not difficult to verify that this condition is also sufficient for the translation equivariance. That is, the following lemma holds.

Lemma 17
.

In the considered framework of countable partition, the conditional translation equivariance property holds iff for every
i\in I
there is
Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}
such that
Q(\Upsilon_{i})>0
.

Framework of
C(\Omega)
-setting

Let
\Omega
be a compact metric space and
{\cal R}
be defined in the form (
2
) with
{\mathfrak{M}}\subset C(\Omega)^{*}
being a nonempty convex set of probability measures. We can assume that
{\mathfrak{M}}
is closed in the weak
∗
topology of
C(\Omega)^{*}
.
Let
{\cal G}
be a
\sigma
-subalgebra of
{\cal F}
generated by a countable partition
\{\Upsilon_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of the set
\Omega
. Then
{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}
can be written in the form (
25
). In particular, this framework is relevant for the moment constraints setting (we will discuss this below). Here the functional
{\cal R}
is strictly monotone iff the following condition holds (cf.
[
18
]
):

Q(A)>0\;\text{for every}\;Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}\;\text{ and any open set }\;A\subset\Omega.

(26)

Consequently, if
{\cal R}
is strictly monotone, then
\essinf\E_{Q|{\cal G}}[Z]
coincides with

\E_{Q|{\cal G}}[Z]
for every
Z\in{\cal Z}
.

Let
Z
,
Z^{\prime}\in C(\Omega)
be such that
Z^{\prime}\succ Z
. Since
Z
,
Z^{\prime}\colon\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
are continuous functions, this implies that there is an open set
A\subset\Omega
such that
Z^{\prime}(\omega)>Z(\omega)
for all
\omega\in A
.
By using (
26
) it is possible to show in a way similar to Proposition
14
, that in this framework strict monotonicity of
{\cal R}
implies strict monotonicity of the corresponding conditional functional
{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}
.

Example 18
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a convex compact set with a nonempty interior, equipped with its Borel
\sigma
-algebra
{\cal F}
. Suppose that the set
{\mathfrak{M}}
consists of a family of probability measures
Q
on
(\Omega,{\cal F})
such that
|{\rm supp}(Q)|\leq n
, i.e., the support of each
Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}
has no more than
n
points. This setting is relevant when the ambiguity set is defined by a finite number
m
of moment constraints.
By the Richter–Rogosinski Theorem in that case it suffices to consider probability measures supported on no more than
m+1
points. Let
{\cal G}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by a
countable partition
\{\Upsilon_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of
\Omega
.
Then formula (
25
) can be applied using the respective conditional expectation in the form (
24
). By (
26
) the corresponding distributionally robust functional
{\cal R}
cannot be strictly monotone in this setting. On the other hand, its conditional counterpart
{\cal R}_{|G}
is strictly monotone if
Q(\Upsilon_{i})>0
for every
Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}
and
i\in I
. Of course this could happen only if
|I|\leq n
.

4
Composite distributionally robust functionals

The results presented in this section are motivated by
[
17
]
.
Unless stated otherwise we assume here the
L_{p}
-setting of Section
2.1
. We also assume that the translation equivariance
axiom holds for the conditional distributionally robust functional
{\cal R}_{|{\cal G}}
for every subalgebra
{\cal G}
, in particular

{\cal R}_{|{\cal F}}(Z)=Z
for every
Z\in{\cal Z}
.
Let
{\cal F}_{1}\subset\cdots\subset{\cal F}_{T}
be a filtration (sequence of
\sigma
-algebras) on
(\Omega,{\cal F})
, with
{\cal F}_{T}={\cal F}
and
{\cal F}_{1}=\{\emptyset,\Omega\}
being trivial. By the tower property of the conditional expectation we have for
Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}
that

\E_{Q}[Z]=\E_{Q|{\cal F}_{1}}\Big[\E_{Q|{\cal F}_{2}}\big[\cdots\E_{Q|{\cal F}_{T-1}}[\E_{Q|{\cal F}_{T}}[Z]]\big]\Big].

(27)

Note that since
{\cal F}_{1}
is trivial,
\E_{Q|{\cal F}_{1}}
is just the unconditional expectation
\E_{Q}
, we write it in that form for uniformity of the notation.

Suppose that
\ess_{Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}}\E_{Q|{\cal F}_{t}}(Z)
is well-defined for every
Z\in{\cal Z}
and
t=1,\dots,T
. It follows that

\displaystyle{\cal R}(Z)

\displaystyle=\sup_{Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}}\E_{Q|{\cal F}_{1}}\Big[\E_{Q|{\cal F}_{2}}\big[\cdots\E_{Q|{\cal F}_{T-1}}[\E_{Q|{\cal F}_{T}}[Z]]\big]\Big]

(28)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{{\cal Q}_{1}\in{\mathfrak{M}}}\E_{{\cal Q}_{1}|{\cal F}_{1}}\Big[\cdots\ess_{{\cal Q}_{T-1}\in{\mathfrak{M}}}\E_{{\cal Q}_{T-1}|{\cal F}_{T-1}}\big[\ess_{{\cal Q}_{T}\in{\mathfrak{M}}}\E_{{\cal Q}_{T}|{\cal F}_{T}}[Z]\big]\Big].

(29)

That is (cf. Proposition
10
)

{\cal R}(Z)\leq{\mathfrak{R}}(Z),

(30)

where

{\mathfrak{R}}(Z):=\sup_{{\cal Q}_{1}\in{\mathfrak{M}}}\E_{{\cal Q}_{1}|{\cal F}_{1}}\Big[\cdots\ess_{{\cal Q}_{T-1}\in{\mathfrak{M}}}\E_{{\cal Q}_{T-1}|{\cal F}_{T-1}}\big[\ess_{{\cal Q}_{T}\in{\mathfrak{M}}}\E_{{\cal Q}_{T}|{\cal F}_{T}}[Z]\big]\Big].

(31)

This can be also written as

{\mathfrak{R}}(Z)={\cal R}_{|{\cal F}_{1}}\Big({\cal R}_{|{\cal F}_{2}}\big(\cdots{\cal R}_{|{\cal F}_{T-1}}({\cal R}_{|{\cal F}_{T}}(Z))\big)\Big),

(32)

with
{\cal R}_{|{\cal F}_{t}}
being the respective conditional distributionally robust functionals. Note that
{\cal R}_{|{\cal F}_{1}}={\cal R}
since
{\cal F}_{1}
is trivial.

Remark 19
.

Suppose that the conditional functionals
{\cal R}_{|{\cal F}_{t}}
in the right-hand side of (
32
) are defined as the
nested
counterparts of the corresponding law invariant coherent risk measures. Formula (
32
) still defines a (possibly not law invariant) coherent risk measure
{\mathfrak{R}}
. For example
{\cal R}_{|{\cal F}_{t}}
can be the nested
\avr_{\alpha|{\cal F}_{t}}
. In that case it can happen that the inequality (
30
) does not hold (see, e.g.,
[
10
,
11
]
).

Note that since
{\cal F}_{T}={\cal F}
it follows that
Q
-almost surely
\E_{Q|{\cal F}_{T}}[Z]=Z
. However, as it was discussed in the previous section, this does not imply that
\E_{Q|{\cal F}_{T}}[Z]=Z
in the
P
-a.s. sense unless
P
is absolutely continuous with respect to
Q
. Anyway, because of the assumption
{\cal R}_{|{\cal F}}(Z)=Z
, we have that
{\cal R}_{|{\cal F}_{T}}(Z)=Z
for every
Z\in{\cal Z}
.
We refer to
{\mathfrak{R}}
as the
composite
(distributionally robust) functional associated with the filtration
{\mathfrak{F}}=\{{\cal F}_{1},\dots,{\cal F}_{T}\}
.

Suppose that
{\mathfrak{R}}(Z)
is finite valued for every
Z\in{\cal Z}
. Since the conditional functionals inherit the respective properties of subadditivity, monotonicity and positive homogeneity, it follows that
{\mathfrak{R}}\colon{\cal Z}\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfies the respective axioms
1
,
2
and
4
.
The translation equivariance axiom is also assumed to hold. Since
{\mathfrak{R}}
is finite valued, it follows that it is continuous and has the following dual representation (cf.
[
19
, Theorem 6.7]
).

Proposition 20
.

Suppose that the composite functional
{\mathfrak{R}}\colon{\cal Z}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is finite valued and the translation equivariance axiom holds for every subalgebra of
{\cal F}
.
Then there exists a convex, bounded, weakly
∗
closed set
\widehat{{\mathfrak{A}}}\subset{\cal Z}^{*}
of density functions such that

{\mathfrak{R}}(Z)=\sup_{\zeta\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{A}}}}\int_{\Omega}Z(\omega)\zeta(\omega)P(d\omega),\quad Z\in{\cal Z}.

(33)

We can also write (
33
) in the form

{\mathfrak{R}}(Z)=\sup_{Q\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{M}}}}\E_{Q}[Z],\;\;Z\in{\cal Z},

(34)

where
\widehat{{\mathfrak{M}}}
is the set of probability measures
Q
absolutely continuous with respect to the reference probability measure
P
such that
dQ/dP\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{A}}}
, that is

\widehat{{\mathfrak{M}}}=\big\{Q\in{\mathfrak{P}}\colon dQ/dP\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{A}}}\big\}.

(35)

Compared with the representation (
1
) of
{\cal R}
, we have by (
30
) that
{\mathfrak{M}}\subset\widehat{{\mathfrak{M}}}
.
Moreover, the inclusion is strict, i.e.
\widehat{{\mathfrak{M}}}\neq{\mathfrak{M}}
, if the inequality (
30
) is strict for some
Z\in{\cal Z}
.

The set
\widehat{{\mathfrak{M}}}
, in the representation (
34
), is derived in an abstract way; its constructive description could be complicated even in the rectangular case discussed below (see Example
25
).

Remark 21
.

A similar analysis can be applied to the
C(\Omega)
-setting. Since the space
C(\Omega)
is a Banach lattice, we have that if a functional defined on
C(\Omega)
is real (finite) valued convex and monotone, then it is continuous in the norm topology of
C(\Omega)
(cf.
[
19
, Theorem 7.91]
). It follows that if the composite functional
{\mathfrak{R}}
, of the form (
32
), is well-defined and finite valued, then it has the dual representation (
34
) for some set
\widehat{{\mathfrak{M}}}\subset C(\Omega)^{*}
of probability measures.

In the
{\mathbb{B}}(\Omega)
-setting the situation is more delicate. Even if the corresponding composite functional is well-defined and finite valued, in order to ensure its dual representation (
33
) it should be verified that it is lower semi-continuous with respect to the considered paired topologies.

4.1
The nested functional

As it was discussed in Section
3.4
the nested and the ‘
\ess
’ approaches could lead to different definitions of conditional functionals. In order to reconcile these two approaches we can proceed as follows.
Let
\Xi_{t}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{t}}
be a nonempty, closed set equipped with its Borel
\sigma
-algebra
{\cal B}_{t}
,
t=1,\dots,T
, and
{\cal B}={\cal B}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\cal B}_{T}

be the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on the set
\Xi:=\Xi_{1}\times\cdots\times\Xi_{T}
. We deal in this section with the measurable space
(\Xi,{\cal B})
and the corresponding
space
{\cal Z}
of random variables
\Xi\to{\mathbb{R}}
. For
t=2,\dots,T
,
let
{\cal M}_{t}^{\xi_{[t-1]}}
be a nonempty set of (marginal) probability distributions on
(\Xi_{t},{\cal B}_{t})
, depending on the history
\xi_{[t-1]}\in\Xi_{1}\times\cdots\times\Xi_{t-1}
, and
{\cal M}_{1}
be a set of probability distributions on
(\Xi_{1},{\cal B}_{1})
.

Consider a variable
Z\in{\cal Z}
,
and going backward in time for
t=T,\dots,2
, starting with
Z_{T}=Z
,
define

Z_{t-1}(\xi_{[t-1]}):=\ess_{Q_{t}\in{\cal M}_{t}^{\xi_{[t-1]}}}\left\{\E_{Q_{t}|\xi_{[t-1]}}[Z_{t}]=\int_{\Xi_{t}}Z_{t}(\xi_{[t-1]},\xi_{t})\,Q_{t}(d\xi_{t})\right\}.

(36)

The corresponding
nested
functional is defined as

{\mathfrak{R}}(Z):=\sup_{Q_{1}\in{\cal M}_{1}}\E_{Q_{1}}\left[Z_{1}(\xi_{1})\right].

(37)

In the construction (
36
)–(
37
)
there is no naturally defined ambiguity set
{\mathfrak{M}}
and the associated distributionally robust functional
{\cal R}
is obtained by the construction (
29
).
On the other hand, if the above functional
{\mathfrak{R}}
is finite valued on the space
{\cal Z}
then it can be represented in the form (
34
). The corresponding set
\widehat{{\mathfrak{M}}}
of probability distributions could be quite complicated.

In a finite dimensional setting we can think about data process as the corresponding scenario tree, with a node at stage
t
representing the history
\xi_{[t]}:=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{t})
of the process. Then at every node
\xi_{[t]}
is defined the corresponding set
{\cal M}_{t}^{\xi_{[t-1]}}
of conditional probability distributions of the child nodes of
\xi_{[t]}
. With the set
{\cal M}_{t}^{\xi_{[t-1]}}
is associated the respective coherent risk measure defined on the space of child nodes.
The composition of these conditional risk measures defines the corresponding nested risk measure. For such detail construction we can refer to
[
19
, Section 6.8.1]
.
In the general setting of continuous distributions a rigorous formulation of the right-hand side of (
36
) could be quite delicate and is beyond the scope of this paper.
We can refer to
[
8
, Section 5]
for an introduction of disintegration with mathematical rigor and for an appropriate discussion.

Next we discuss the rectangular setting where the conditional distributionally robust approach, associated with an ambiguity set, is equivalent to the nested construction.

4.2
Rectangular setting

The nested construction (
36
) is simplified considerably if the sets of marginal distributions do not depend on the history of the data process.
That is, for
t=1,\dots,T
,
let
{\cal M}_{t}
be a nonempty set of probability measures on
(\Xi_{t},{\cal B}_{t})
and consider the corresponding set

{\mathfrak{M}}:=\left\{Q=Q_{1}\times\cdots\times Q_{T}\colon Q_{t}\in{\cal M}_{t},\;t=1,\dots,T\right\}

(38)

of probability measures on
(\Xi,{\cal B})
.
We refer to this setting as
rectangular
.
The vector
\xi_{t}\in\Xi_{t}
can be viewed as an element of the measurable space
(\Xi_{t},{\cal B}_{t})
or as a random vector having a considered probability distribution (measure) on
(\Xi_{t},{\cal B}_{t})
.
The sets
{\cal M}_{t}
represent respective marginal distributions and the rectangular setting can be considered as a distributionally robust counterpart of the stagewise independence condition.
If we view
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{T}
as a random process having distribution
Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}
, then (
38
) means that the random
vectors
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{T}
, are mutually independent with respective marginal distributions
Q_{t}\in{\cal M}_{t}
.

The corresponding variables, defined in (
36
), can be written here as

Z_{t-1}(\xi_{[t-1]}):=\sup_{Q_{t}\in{\cal M}_{t}}\left\{\E_{Q_{t}|\xi_{[t-1]}}[Z_{t}]=\int_{\Xi_{t}}Z_{t}(\xi_{[t-1]},\xi_{t})\,Q_{t}(d\xi_{t})\right\}.

(39)

The supremum in (
39
) is taken with respect to the respective marginal distributions.

The rectangular setting constitutes a notable, special case.

Remark 22
(Equivalence of the nested and the conditional approach).

In the considered rectangular setting the composite functional
{\mathfrak{R}}
does
not
depend on whether the nested or conditional distributionally robust approach is used.

Example 23
(Average Value-at-Risk).

Suppose that for every
t=1,\dots,T
, the set
{\cal M}_{t}
of marginal distributions consists of probability measures absolutely continuous with respect to a reference distribution
P_{t}
on
(\Xi_{t},{\cal B}_{t})
and the corresponding densities correspond to the
\avr_{\alpha_{t}}
risk measure taken with respect to
P_{t}
. Then for
t=T,\dots,2
and
Z_{T}=Z
we have

Z_{t-1}(\xi_{[t-1]})=\avr_{\alpha_{t}}\big(Z_{t}(\xi_{[t-1]},\xi_{t})\big),

(40)

where for given
\xi_{[t-1]}

the Average Value-at-Risk of
Z_{t}(\xi_{[t-1]},\cdot)
is
computed with respect to the distribution
P_{t}
of
\xi_{t}
. Finally,

{\mathfrak{R}}(Z)=\avr_{\alpha_{1}}(Z_{1})
.

When
\xi_{[T]}
is viewed as random, the supremum should be replaced by the essential supremum. Consequently, the composite functional
{\mathfrak{R}}
can be written as

{\mathfrak{R}}(Z)=\sup_{Q_{1}\in{\cal M}_{1}}\E_{Q_{1}}\left[\ess_{Q_{2}\in{\cal M}_{2}}\E_{Q_{2}|\xi_{[1]}}\Big[\,\cdots\,\ess_{Q_{T}\in{\cal M}_{T}}\E_{Q_{T}|\xi_{[T-1]}}[Z]\Big]\right].

(41)

On the other hand the functional
{\cal R}(Z)
is

{\cal R}(Z)=\sup_{Q_{1}\in{\cal M}_{1},\dots,Q_{T}\in{\cal M}_{T}}\int_{\Xi}Z(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{T})\,Q_{1}(d\xi_{1})\dots Q_{T}(d\xi_{T}),

(42)

\Xi=\Xi_{1}\times\cdots\times\Xi_{T}
.
As before the inequality (
30
) and the representation (
34
) hold. The set
\widehat{{\mathfrak{M}}}
is different from the set
{\mathfrak{M}}
and does not have the rectangular structure of the form (
38
).
The equality
{\cal R}(Z)={\mathfrak{R}}(Z)
, for some
Z\in{\cal Z}
, can happen only in rather exceptional cases even in this rectangular setting.

Remark 24
.

The integral in the right-hand side of (
42
) does not depend on the order of integration, i.e., for any permutation
\{i_{1},\dots,i_{T}\}
of the set
\{1,\dots,T\}
, we can write

{\cal R}(Z)=\sup_{Q_{i_{1}}\in{\cal M}_{i_{1}},\dots,Q_{i_{T}}\in{\cal M}_{i_{T}}}\int Z(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{T})\,Q_{i_{1}}(d\xi_{i_{1}})\dots Q_{i_{T}}(d\xi_{i_{T}}).

(43)

On the other hand in the definition (
41
) of the composite functional
{\mathfrak{R}}
the order is important.

4.3
Difference of the distributionally robust and the composite functional

Remark
22
above addresses a special risk functional which can be employed in both, the nested and the conditional settings. In order to see how the composite functional
{\mathfrak{R}}
differs from
{\cal R}
let us consider (in the rectangular framework) the following example of two stage case.

Example 25
.

For
T=2

and the rectangular setting
consider the corresponding composite functional

{\mathfrak{R}}(Z)=\sup_{Q_{1}\in{\cal M}_{1}}\E_{Q_{1}}\left[\sup_{Q_{2}\in{\cal M}_{2}}\E_{Q_{2}|\xi_{1}}[Z]\right].

(44)

Under suitable regularity conditions
(e.g.,
[
14
, Theorem 14.60]
)
, we can interchange the supremum and integral operators to write

\E_{Q_{1}}\left[\sup_{Q_{2}\in{\cal M}_{2}}\E_{Q_{2}|\xi_{1}}[Z]\right]=\sup_{Q^{(\cdot)}_{2}\in{\mathfrak{V}}}\E_{Q_{1}}\left[\E_{Q^{\xi_{1}}_{2}|\xi_{1}}[Z]\right].

(45)

The maximum in the right-hand side of (
45
) is with respect to family
{\mathfrak{V}}
of mappings
\xi_{1}\mapsto Q^{\xi_{1}}_{2}
, from
\Xi_{1}
to
{\cal M}_{2}
, such that the integral

\E_{Q_{1}}\left[\E_{Q^{\xi_{1}}_{2}|\xi_{1}}[Z]\right]=\int_{\Xi_{1}}\left(\int_{\Xi_{2}}Z(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\,Q^{\xi_{1}}_{2}(d\xi_{2})\right)Q_{1}(d\xi_{1})

(46)

is well-defined. The notation
Q_{2}^{(\cdot)}
emphasizes that the probability measure
Q^{\xi_{1}}_{2}
in (
45
) is a function of
\xi_{1}
. If we take the mapping

\xi^{1}\mapsto Q^{\xi_{1}}_{2}
in (
45
) to be constant, i.e.,
Q^{\xi_{1}}_{2}=Q_{2}
for all
\xi_{1}
, then the corresponding measure is
Q=Q_{1}\times Q_{2}
.

The right-hand side of (
46
) defines a
functional
\phi\colon{\cal Z}\to{\mathbb{R}}
. This functional is
linear, monotone and
\phi(Z+a)=\phi(Z)+a
for
Z\in{\cal Z}
and
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Hence, there exists a probability measure
Q
on
\Xi_{1}\times\Xi_{2}
, which depends on the mapping
\xi^{1}\mapsto Q^{\xi_{1}}_{2}
(and also on
Q_{1}
), such that

\E_{Q_{1}}\left[\E_{Q^{\xi_{1}}_{2}|\xi_{1}}[Z]\right]=\E_{Q}[Z].

(47)

For measurable sets
A_{1}\subset\Xi_{1}
and
A_{2}\subset\Xi_{2}
, and

Z(\xi_{1},\xi_{2}):={\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{A_{1}}(\xi_{1}){\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{A_{2}}(\xi_{2})
, we have

\int_{A_{1}}\left(\int_{{\cal A}_{2}}Z(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\,Q^{\xi_{1}}_{2}(d\xi_{2})\right)Q_{1}(d\xi_{1})=\int_{A_{1}}Q^{\xi_{1}}_{2}(A_{2})\,Q_{1}(d\xi_{1})

(48)

and hence, for

A:=A_{1}\times A_{2}
,

Q(A)=\int_{A_{1}}Q^{\xi_{1}}_{2}(A_{2})\,Q_{1}(d\xi_{1}).

(49)

The set
\widehat{{\mathfrak{M}}}
is obtained by taking the union of such measures
Q
over all mappings in
{\mathfrak{V}}
and
Q_{1}\in{\cal M}_{1}
,
and then taking the topological closure of the convex hull of that family.
Consequently,

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{R}}(Z)

\displaystyle=\sup_{Q_{1}\in{\cal M}_{1},Q_{2}^{(\cdot)}\in{\mathfrak{V}}}\int_{\Xi_{1}}\int_{\Xi_{2}}Z(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\,Q^{\xi_{1}}_{2}(d\xi_{2})Q_{1}(d\xi_{1})

(50)

\displaystyle=\sup_{Q\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{M}}}}\E_{Q}[Z].

(51)

For example suppose that the set
\Xi_{1}
is finite,
|\Xi_{1}|=n
, and the set
{\cal M}_{1}
is given by the convex hull of a finite family
\{Q_{1}^{1},\dots,Q_{1}^{m_{1}}\}
of probability measures on
(\Xi_{1},{\cal B}_{1})
, and
{\cal M}_{2}
is given by the convex hull of a finite family
\{Q_{2}^{1},\dots,Q_{2}^{m_{2}}\}
of probability measures on
(\Xi_{2},{\cal B}_{2})
. Then the set
{\mathfrak{M}}
is given by the convex hull of the family

\{Q_{1}^{i}\times Q_{2}^{j}\},\;i=1,\dots,m_{1},\;j=1,\dots,m_{2}.

(52)

The set
\widehat{{\mathfrak{M}}}
, on the other hand, is obtained by taking the convex hull of the family

\{Q_{1}^{i}\times Q_{2}^{j_{k}}\},\;i=1,\dots,m_{1},\;j_{k}\in\{1,\dots,m_{2}\},\;k=1,\dots,n.

(53)

The number of elements (probability measures) in the family (
52
) could be as large as
m_{1}\times m_{2}
, while in the family (
53
) it could be as large as
m_{1}\times(m_{2})^{n}
(some of these probability measures could be equal to each other).
Family (
52
) is a subset of family (
53
) obtained by taking indexes
j_{k}\equiv j
constant (independent of
k
).
It could be noted that
{\mathfrak{R}}(Z)={\cal R}(Z)
for some
Z\in{\cal Z}
if the supremum in (
50
) with respect to
Q_{2}^{\xi_{1}}
does not depend on
\xi_{1}
.

In the considered rectangular framework it is possible to consider cases without assuming existence of a reference probability measure.

Moment constraints

Suppose that for
t=1,\dots,T
, the set
{\cal M}_{t}
consists of probability distributions
Q_{t}
on
(\Xi_{t},{\cal B}_{t})
such that

\E_{Q_{t}}[\Psi_{ti}(\xi_{t})]=b_{ti},\;i=1,\dots,m_{t},

(54)

for some measurable functions
\Psi_{ti}:\Xi_{t}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and
b_{ti}\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
By duality, under mild regularity conditions, the corresponding variable
Z_{t-1}(\xi_{[t-1]})
, defined in (
39
), is give by the optimal value of the following problem (e.g.,
[
19
, Section 6.7]
)

\begin{array}[]{cll}\min\limits_{\lambda_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{t}}}&\lambda_{t0}+\sum_{i=1}^{m_{t}}b_{ti}\lambda_{ti}\\
{\rm s.t.}&\lambda_{t0}+\sum_{i=1}^{m_{t}}\lambda_{ti}\Psi_{ti}(\xi_{t})\geq Z_{t}(\xi_{[t-1]},\xi_{t}),\;\xi_{t}\in\Xi_{t}.\end{array}

(55)

If every set
\Xi_{t}
is compact (and hence the set
\Xi
is compact) and the function
Z_{T}=Z
is continuous on the set
\Xi
, then every
Z_{t}
is continuous on the set
\Xi_{1}\times\cdots\times\Xi_{t}
(this can be shown by induction going backward in time and using, e.g.,
[
19
, Theorem 7.23]
). It is natural here to use the framework of the
C(\Xi)
setting (discussed in Section
2.2
). Consequently, there is a set
\widehat{{\mathfrak{M}}}
of probability measures on
(\Xi,{\cal B})
such that the corresponding composite functional
{\mathfrak{R}}
is representable in the form (
34
). The set
\widehat{{\mathfrak{M}}}
can be different from the corresponding set
{\mathfrak{M}}
(defined in (
38
)).

Example 26
.

Suppose that
\Xi_{2}:=[\alpha,\beta]
, for some
\alpha<\beta
, and the set
{\cal M}_{2}
consists of probability measures supported on the interval
[\alpha,\beta]
and having mean
\mu\in[\alpha,\beta]
. Suppose further that
Z(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
is convex continuous in
\xi_{2}\in[\alpha,\beta]
. Then the respective maximum over
Q_{2}\in{\cal M}_{2}
is attained at the probability measure

Q^{*}_{2}=p\,\delta(\alpha)+(1-p)\delta(\beta)

supported on the end points of the interval
[\alpha,\beta]
, with
p:=(\beta-\mu)/(\beta-\alpha)
. For such
Z
the respective maximum over
Q_{2}\in{\cal M}_{2}
does not depend on
\xi_{1}
, and hence
{\mathfrak{R}}(Z)={\cal R}(Z)
. Note that the assumption that

Z(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
is convex in
\xi_{2}
is essential here, and that
{\mathfrak{R}}(Z)
can be different from
{\cal R}(Z)
for general
Z
.

4.4
Consequences for multistage stochastic optimization

A typical setting in stochastic optimization considers robustifications at every new decision, i.e., based on the realized history of the entire process. In a scenario tree, this corresponds to a robustification at every node.
A convenient and widely used setting in this respect involves the Wasserstein metric and the nested distance. In what follows we elaborate this setting in the two-stage case first and then proceed to the more general multistage situation.

4.4.1
Two-stage regularity

The Wasserstein distance is a distance for probability measures. Given this distance
d
of measures, a typical ambiguity set is (called Wasserstein ambiguity set in
[
3
,
6
]
)

{\mathfrak{M}}:=\{Q\colon d(P,Q)\leq r\},

(56)

where
P
is some reference measure, often an empirical measure
P=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}
for independent observations
X_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n
. The radius
r
of the Wasserstein ball (
56
) is given the interpretation of a confidence interval to collect all empirical measures with a probability of
95\,\%
, say.

Definition 27
(Wasserstein distance).

Let
P
and
Q
be probability measures on a Polish space
(\Xi,d)
. The Wasserstein distance of order
r\geq 1
is

d_{r}(P,Q)^{r}=\inf\iint_{\Xi\times\Xi}d(\xi,\tilde{\xi})^{r}\,\pi(d\xi,d\tilde{\xi}),

(57)

where the infimum is among all bivariate measures
\pi
with marginals

\displaystyle\pi(A\times\Xi)

\displaystyle=P(A)\text{ and}

(58)

\displaystyle\pi(\Xi\times B)

\displaystyle=Q(B)

(59)

for all Borel sets
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\Xi)
.

The Wasserstein distance is of importance because of its duality relation, which is the content of the Kantorovich–Rubinstein theorem, cf.
[
22
]
or
[
1
]
. Indeed, it holds that

\left|\E_{Q}[Z]-\E_{P}[Z]\right|\leq L_{Z}\cdot d_{1}(Q,P),

(60)

where
L_{Z}
is the Lipschitz constant of the random variable
Z
, i.e.,
|Z(\xi)-Z(\tilde{\xi})|\leq L_{Z}\,d(\xi,\tilde{\xi})
.
For Lipschitz continuous functions we find ourselves in the
C(\Omega)
setting addressed in the introduction. Note that this setting allows addressing atomic and non-atomic probability measures simultaneously.

Based on (
60
) we have the following result in a two-stage setting.

Theorem 28
.

Let
P
be a reference measure and the ambiguity set

{\mathfrak{M}}=\{Q\colon d_{1}(P,Q)\leq\epsilon\}

(61)

collect all measures, which do note deviate by more than
\epsilon\geq 0
in Wasserstein distance from the reference measure
P
. Then the bound

\left|{\cal R}(Z)-\E_{P}[Z]\right|\leq L_{Z}\cdot\epsilon

(62)

holds for the risk functional
{\cal R}(Z)=\sup_{Q\in{\mathfrak{M}}}\E_{Q}[Z]
.

T
he ambiguity set (
61
) is popular in applications (cf.
[
3
]
). For this reason we emphasize that the
C(\Omega)
setting is important here, as the measures in (
61
) are non-dominated in the sense of Section
3.6
.
Further, the bound (
62
) allows comparing the risk functional
{\cal R}
with the much simpler risk neutral expectation.

4.4.2

Multistage regularity and the nested distance
The nested distance builds on the Wasserstein distance, but respects the evolution of the process in addition.

For the corresponding robustification in the multistage situation let
P\in{\mathfrak{P}}
be the law of a reference process with disintegration

\E_{P}[Z]=\int_{A_{1}}\int_{A_{2}}\dots\int_{A_{T}}Z(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{T})\,P_{T}^{\xi_{[T-1]}}(d\xi_{T})\dots P_{2}^{\xi_{1}}(d\xi_{2})P_{1}(d\xi_{1}).

{}

definition[Nested distance, cf. Definition
27
(Wasserstein distance)]
For
P
and
Q
probability laws on a Polish space
(\Xi,d)
with
\Xi:=\Xi_{1}\times\dots\times\Xi_{T}
, the
nested distance
of order
r\geq 1
is

{\sf nd}_{r}(P,Q)^{r}:=\inf\iint_{\Xi\times\Xi}d(\xi,\tilde{\xi})^{r}\,\pi(d\xi,d\tilde{\xi}),

where the infimum is among all bivariate measures
\pi
on
\Xi\times\Xi
with conditional marginals

\displaystyle\pi^{\xi_{[t]},\eta_{[t]}}(A\times\Xi)

\displaystyle=P^{\xi_{[t]}}(A)\text{ a.s.\ and}

\displaystyle\pi^{\xi_{[t]},\eta_{[t]}}(\Xi\times B)

\displaystyle=Q^{\eta_{[t]}}(B)\text{ a.s.}

for all Borel sets
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\Xi)
and times
t=1,\dots,T
.

Given some history
\xi_{[t-1]}
consider now the specific ambiguity set
{\cal M}_{t}^{\xi_{[t-1]}}
with

\displaystyle Q_{t}^{\xi_{[t-1]}}

\displaystyle\in{\cal M}_{t}^{\xi_{[t-1]}}

(63)

iff

\displaystyle d_{1}\big(Q_{t}^{\xi_{[t-1]}},P_{t}^{\tilde{\xi}_{[t-1]}}\big)\leq\epsilon_{t}+\kappa_{t}\,d(\xi_{[t-1]},\tilde{\xi}_{[t-1]})\text{ for all }\tilde{\xi}_{[t-1]}.

The ambiguity set (
63
) is constructed in the same way as (
61
) and contains all transitions which are close, in the sense specified, to the corresponding transitions of
P
.

We consider the risk functional

{\mathfrak{R}}(Z)=\sup_{Q_{1}\in{\cal M}_{1}}\E_{Q_{1}}\left[Z_{1}(\xi_{1})\right],

(64)

with conditional measures

Z_{t-1}(\xi_{[t-1]})=\sup_{Q_{t}\in{\cal M}_{t}^{\xi_{[t-1]}}}\left\{\E_{Q_{t}|\xi_{[t-1]}}[Z_{t}]=\int_{\Xi_{t}}Z_{t}(\xi_{[t-1]},\xi_{t})\,Q_{t}(d\xi_{t})\right\}

(65)

on the nodes
\xi_{[t-1]}
. The risk measure (
64
) is the risk measure defined in (
36
)–(
37
) above with transitions specified in (
63
).

Based on
[
9
, Proposition 4.26]
we have the following result which allows comparing the risk measure with the simple expectation with respect to the reference probability measure.

Proposition 29
.

It holds that

|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathfrak{R}}(Z)-\E_{P}[Z]|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\leq{\sf nd}_{1}(P,Q)\leq}L_{Z}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\epsilon_{t}w_{t}\prod_{s=t+1}^{T}(1+w_{s}\kappa_{s})

for every
Q
with conditional marginals in
\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\xi_{[t-1]}}
,
t=1,\dots,T
and provided that the objective
Z
is Lipschitz with

|Z(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{T})-Z(\tilde{\xi}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\xi}_{T})|\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}w_{t}\,d_{t}(\xi_{t},\tilde{\xi}_{t}).

A notable situation arises for the stagewise independent measure
P=P_{1}\times\dots\times P_{T}
in the rectangular case.

Corollary 30
.

Suppose that

Q_{t}\in{\cal M}_{t}\text{ iff }d_{1}(P_{t},Q_{t})\leq\epsilon_{t}.

(66)

Then

\left|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathfrak{R}}(Z)-\E_{P}[Z]\right|\leq L_{Z}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\epsilon_{t}\,w_{t}.

Similarly to (
56
), the interpretation of (
63
) and (

) in the multistage setting is based on ambiguity sets of conditional transitions. The results in Proposition

and the corollary compare the ambiguous approach with the risk neutral approach and measure the impact of considering risk.

5
Distributionally robust multistage optimization

Following the general paradigm of this paper we consider in this section different approaches to formulation of distributionally robust multistage stochastic optimization problems. Again, we observe conceptual differences which we highlight here.

Consider the
L_{p}
setting and a composite functional
{\mathfrak{R}}
of the form (
32
). The involved
{\cal R}_{|{\cal F}_{t}}
can be the respective conditional distributionally robust functionals or the nested risk measures associated with a law invariant coherent risk measure. In both cases there is a set
\widehat{{\mathfrak{M}}}
of probability measures such that
{\mathfrak{R}}
can be represented in the form (
34
) provided that
{\mathfrak{R}}\colon{\cal Z}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is finite valued.

Consider the multistage stochastic program

\min\limits_{\pi\in\Pi}{\mathfrak{R}}(Z^{\pi}),

(67)

where
\Pi
denotes the set of policies
\pi=\left(x_{1},x_{2}(\xi_{[2]}),\dots,x_{T}(\xi_{[T]})\right)
satisfying the feasibility constraints

x_{1}\in{\cal X}_{1},\;x_{t}\in{\cal X}_{t}(x_{t-1},\xi_{t}),\;t=2,\dots,T-1,

(68)

and such that the total cost

\begin{array}[]{ll}Z^{\pi}:=\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}(x_{t}^{\pi},\xi_{t})\end{array}

(69)

belongs to the space
{\cal Z}
on which the functional
{\mathfrak{R}}
is defined.
Here
x_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{t}}
,
t=1,{\dots},T
,
f_{t}\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{t}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{t}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
are continuous functions and

{\cal X}_{t}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{t-1}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{t}}\rightrightarrows{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{t}}
,
t=2,{\dots},T
, are measurable multifunctions. The first stage
data, i.e., the vector
\xi_{1}
, the function

f_{1}\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{1}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, and the
set
{\cal X}_{1}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{1}}
are deterministic. The feasibility constraints in (
68
) should be satisfied almost surely with respect to the reference measure
P
.

Consider the nested
risk
functional
{\mathfrak{R}}
discussed in section
4.1
.
We can view the corresponding risk averse stochastic program (
67
) as a stochastic game. In the framework of Markov decision processes (MDP), stochastic games were introduced in Shapley
[
20
]
and were studied extensively (see, e.g., the survey
[
7
]
). For a policy
\pi\in\Pi
, we can think about the opponent who chooses at
every stage
t
a distribution
Q^{\pi}_{t}\in{\cal M}_{t}^{\xi_{[t-1]}}

for given realization
\xi_{[t-1]}
of the data process and decision
x_{t}=x_{t}^{\pi}
.
A choice of such probability distributions defines the corresponding policy of the opponent. For
\pi\in\Pi
, denote by
\Gamma^{\pi}
the set of such policies of the opponent. Then the risk averse program (
67
) can be written in the following minimax form

\min_{\pi\in\Pi}\sup_{\gamma\in\Gamma^{\pi}}\E^{\pi,\gamma}[Z^{\pi}],

(70)

where
Z^{\pi}
is the total cost (
69
),
\gamma\in\Gamma^{\pi}
is policy of the opponent with the respective distributions
Q^{\pi}_{t}\in{\cal M}_{t}^{\xi_{[t-1]}}
, and

\E^{\pi,\gamma}\left[\,\cdot\,\right]:=\E_{Q^{\pi}_{1}}\left[\E_{Q^{\pi}_{2}|\xi_{[1]}}\big[\cdots\E_{Q^{\pi}_{T}|\xi_{[T-1]}}[\,\cdot\,]\big]\right],

(71)

is the corresponding expectation written in the nested (composite) form. Therefore for
\pi\in\Pi
we have that

\sup_{\gamma\in\Gamma^{\pi}}\E^{\pi,\gamma}[Z^{\pi}]={\mathfrak{R}}(Z^{\pi}).

(72)

Consider the
rectangular
case, i.e., assume that the set
{\mathfrak{M}}
is given in the form (
38
). Then the nested and the conditional distributionally robust approaches are equivalent, and
the respective dynamic programming equations for value (cost-to-go) functions can be written as

\displaystyle V_{t}\left(x_{t-1},\xi_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\inf\limits_{x_{t}\in{\cal X}_{t}(x_{t-1},\xi_{t})}\left\{f_{t}(x_{t},\xi_{t})+{\cal V}_{t+1}\left(x_{t}\right)\right\},

(73)

\displaystyle{\cal V}_{t+1}\left(x_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\sup\limits_{Q_{t+1}\in{\cal M}_{t+1}}\E_{Q_{t+1}}\left[V_{t+1}\left(x_{t},\xi_{t+1}\right)\right],

(74)

t=1,\dots,T
, with
{\cal V}_{T+1}(\cdot)\equiv 0
and
{\cal X}_{1}(x_{0},\xi_{1})\equiv{\cal X}_{1}
.
A sufficient condition for

\bar{x}_{t}=\pi_{t}(\bar{x}_{t-1},\xi_{t})
to be an optimal policy is

\bar{x}_{t}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}\limits_{x_{t}\in{\cal X}_{t}(\bar{x}_{t-1},\xi_{t})}\left\{f_{t}(x_{t},\xi_{t})+{\cal V}_{t+1}\left(x_{t}\right)\right\}.

(75)

This condition is also necessary if the corresponding functional is
strictly
monotone. Here policy of the opponent is defined by a choice of distribution
Q^{\pi}_{t}\in{\cal M}_{t}
, at every stage of the process, which depends on decision
x_{t}=x_{t}^{\pi}
but not on
\xi_{[t-1]}
. Again the corresponding distributionally robust/risk averse problem (
67
) can be written in the minimax form (
70
).

In order to derive a dual of the risk averse multistage problem (
67
) the ‘min’ and ‘max’ operators in the representation (
70
) cannot be interchanged, even in the rectangular case, since the set
\Gamma^{\pi}
depends on
\pi\in\Pi
.

On the other hand by (
34
) we can write problem (
67
) in the form

\min_{\pi\in\Pi}\sup_{Q\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{M}}}}\E_{Q}[Z^{\pi}].

(76)

The dual of the minimax problem (
76
) is obtained by interchanging the ‘min’ and ‘max’ operators, that is

\max_{Q\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{M}}}}\inf_{\pi\in\Pi}\E_{Q}[Z^{\pi}].

(77)

The optimal value of dual problem (
77
) is always less than or equal to the optimal value of the primal problem.

If the problem (
67
) is convex, e.g., the objective functions
f_{t}(\cdot,\xi_{t})
are convex and the constraints are linear, then under certain regularity conditions the optimal values of problems (
76
) and (
77
) are equal to each other.

Consider the

Consequently, it is possible to apply Sion’s minimax theorem

As it was discussed before, even in the rectangular case the set

Suppose that the maximum in the right-hand side of (

6
Summary

The literature suggests various approaches towards constructing multistage counterparts of distributionally robust functionals.
One approach is to use the “static” functional
{\cal R}
which is not adjusted to the dynamics of the decision process. The other approach, now routinely used in the risk averse stochastic optimization, is to employ nested formulations of the corresponding risk measures. In such an approach the risk is controlled at every stage of the decision process while the total value of the corresponding objective function is irrelevant.

Although natural in some sense, this paper exposes that the respective nested
distributionally robust
approach could be very different. It is demonstrated that in the case of the rectangular setting, which is important from an application perspective, both approaches are equivalent.

We investigate the foundations by addressing the individual features, the major differences and particularly the relevance of conditioned risk functionals for multistage stochastic optimization.
