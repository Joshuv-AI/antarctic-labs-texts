Operational Umbral Calculus
Kei Beauduin

Abstract
In this paper, we explore the effectiveness of almost purely operational methods in the study of umbral calculus. To accomplish this goal, we systematically reconstruct the theory operationally , offering new proofs and results throughout. Our approach is applied to the study of invertible power series, where we notably offer a concise two-line proof of Lagrange’s inversion theorem, derive formulas for both fractional and regular compositional iterates and generalize Jabotinsky matrices. In addition, we will develop several new insights into umbral operators, including their fractional exponents and novel expressions, in connection with the previously discussed applications. Finally, we will elaborate on both established and new concepts and examples, such as pseudoinverses of delta operators and a natural generalization of the Laguerre polynomials.



1
Introduction

At its inception, umbral calculus comprised a set of informal but powerful techniques devised by Blissard in the late 19th century. These techniques mainly involved manipulating the indices and exponents of certain number sequences, particularly
Bernoulli numbers
, while exploiting the properties of exponents. Despite yielding new identities, these techniques initially faced skepticism from the scientific community due to their lack of rigor.

In the early 1970s, Rota et al.
[
25
,
30
]
laid the first rigorous foundation for umbral calculus through its integration with Pincherle and Amaldi’s ideas on linear operator
[
28
]
and the theory of Sheffer sequences. The latter was a theory about certain polynomial sequences, independently developed in different directions by Sheffer
[
33
]
and Steffensen
[
35
]
, generalizing that of Appell
[
2
]
. Consequently, numerous novel frameworks for the study of umbral calculus emerged. Notably, in collaboration with Roman, Rota further developed the notion in
[
29
]
that the informal methods of the nineteenth century could be elucidated by linear forms over the set of polynomials, referred to as
linear functionals
. As the theory evolved, umbral calculus increasingly diverged from its origins, prompting Rota and Taylor to construct what they designated as the
Classical umbral calculus

[
31
]
, representing the first successful axiomatic foundation for the umbral calculus of the 19th century.

The aim of this manuscript is to extend the foundational work established by Rota in his seminal papers with a closer study of the coefficients of basic polynomial sequences and an emphasis on the use of linear operators. This new methodology, coupled with new convenient notations, will enable the derivation of unexpectedly new results.

In
Sections

2
and
3
, we will follow Rota’s initial approach to develop the fundamental tools from
[
30
]
, starting with the definition of
delta operators
, while incorporating a more systematic use of linear operators. For example, we will introduce
bivariate characterizations
of the shift-invariant, umbral, and Sheffer operators, which will simplify some of the proofs. In
Section

4
, we will conduct an in-depth study of the coefficients of basic sets and, based on some of Labelle’s insights
[
20
]
, apply them to the iterative theory
[
38
]
to uncover new proofs of Jabotinsky’s formulas
[
16
,
17
]
for the
iterative logarithm
and fractional iterates of the power series. This will be facilitated by observing that the coefficients of umbral operators and Jabotinsky matrices are very similar, though the former is algebraically more convenient.

In
Section

5
, these formulas, together with operational identities, will be later applied to derive closed forms for umbral operators and initiate a discussion on their fractional exponents. Following that, we identify a particular class of umbral operators that fulfill a natural operator identity. We will then construct pseudoinverses for delta operators, we coined
sigma operators
, which have application in a surprising new definition for the generalization of summation with fractional bounds. Finally, numerous classic and new examples will be discussed in
Section

6
.

2
Definitions and first results

This section introduces the main objects of study:
delta
,
umbral
and
Sheffer
operators. Their construction will be followed by proofs of their basic properties, which will enhance our understanding of them. These insights will pave the way to demonstrate the equivalence between basicness and binomial type for polynomial sequences.

2.1
Preliminaries

Let
\mathbb{K}
(typically
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
) be a field of characteristic zero. In this paper, our focus lies on the
\mathbb{K}
-algebra of univariate polynomials, denoted by
P
, or on the
\mathbb{K}
-algebra of formal univariate power series, denoted by
\tilde{\textnormal{P}}
. Within
P
, we define our linear operators. The definitions for the initial operators are provided in Figure
1
.

It is customary to represent the composition and the application linear function as an implicit product, such that
(\text{x}\circ D)(f)(x)
is more succinctly expressed as
\text{x}Df(x)
. This notation is particularly intuitive when viewing linear operators as a generalization of scalar multiplication. By slight abuse of notation, we denote the identity as
1
rather than
I
, as it can be interpreted as a special case of the scalar operator with
c=1
.

Readers should note that, depending on the context, we will utilize the evaluation operator
\mathcal{E}^{\left(a\right)}
either as an endomorphism (mapping a function to a constant function) or as a linear form (a linear operator mapping to the field
\mathbb{K}
). Additionally, in multivariate settings, we will distinguish linear operators by indexing the variable to which they apply. By default, operators are applied to the variable
x
. In other words, if
U
is a linear operator,
U_{x}=U
. Moreover, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the
n
-th coefficient in the Taylor series of
f(x)
using the common notation
[x^{n}]
. In our linear operators, this can be expressed as

[x^{n}]f(x)=\frac{1}{n!}\mathcal{E}D^{n}f(x).

(1)

For a formal power series
f(x)=\sum a_{n}x^{n}
, we define its
order
and
degree
as the index of the first and last nonzero coefficients, respectively. We denote them by “
\ord
” and “
\deg
”, and adhere to the standard convention
\deg 0=-\infty=-\ord 0
.

Although we will not discuss the topology and convergence of formal power series, we should mention that it can be successfully described by the ultrametric space induced by the norm
\left\lVert f\right\rVert=2^{-\ord(f)}
.

For
p\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, where
\mathbb{N}^{*}
denotes the set of natural numbers excluding zero, we differentiate the notation for iteration and exponentiation,

f^{p}(x)=f(f^{p-1}(x))\quad\mathbin{\textnormal{and }}\quad f(x)^{p}=f(x)\cdot f(x)^{p-1},

with
f^{0}(x)=x
and
f(x)^{0}=1
.
We will employ the
generalized exponentiation
operation for linear operators, introduced by the author in
[
4
]
and defined as

U^{V}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(U-1)^{n}\binom{V}{n},

(2)

where
\binom{x}{n}=\frac{(x)_{n}}{n!}
represents the
generalized binomial coefficient
, and
(x)_{n}=x(x-1)\cdots(x-n+1)
denotes the
falling factorial
. It’s important to note that in general

U^{V}\neq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\binom{V}{n}(U-1)^{n},

(3)

as linear operators do not always commute.

2.2
Polynomial sequences and invertible operators

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\textnormal{P}_{n}
be the set of polynomials with a single variable
x
of degree less than
n
. Define
\hat{\textnormal{P}}_{n}\coloneqq\textnormal{P}_{n}\setminus\textnormal{P}_{n-1}
to represent the subset of polynomials of degree exactly
n
(with the understanding that
\textnormal{P}_{-1}=\varnothing
). We denote by
\mathcal{L}(S)
the set of endomorphisms over a set
S
.

A sequence of polynomials
(p_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is said to be a
polynomial sequence

\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
if, for all positive integers
n
,
p_{n}(x)\in\hat{\textnormal{P}}_{n}
. It is self-evident that
(p_{k})_{0\leq k\leq n}
is a basis for
\textnormal{P}_{n}
, leading to the following proposition.

Proposition 2.1
.

If
U,V\in\mathcal{L}(\textnormal{P})
then
U=V
if and only if for some polynomial sequence
\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,
Up_{n}(x)=Vp_{n}(x)
for all positive integers
n
.

Now we define

\hat{\mathcal{L}}\coloneqq\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{L}(\hat{\textnormal{P}}_{n})\subset\mathcal{L}(\textnormal{P})

(4)

the set of linear operators that preserve the polynomial degree upon application.

Definition 2.1
.

A polynomial sequence
\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is said to be unitary when
[x^{n}]p_{n}(x)=1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

For every operator in
\varphi\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}
, we define its associated polynomial sequence
\{\varphi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
by
\varphi_{n}(x)\coloneqq\varphi x^{n}
. As
\varphi
preserves the degree of polynomials, it induces a transformation between bases of
P
. Consequently, the ensuing proposition is valid.

Proposition 2.2
.

If
\varphi\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}
, then
\varphi
has an inverse
\varphi^{-1}
.

This proposition indicates the presence of a division ring structure in
\hat{\mathcal{L}}
. Furthermore, the choice of mapping monomials by
\varphi
to their corresponding polynomial sequences entails no loss of generality. Indeed, selecting an alternative sequence, such as
\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, would yield an identical analysis, but with umbral operators of the form
\varphi p^{-1}
.

A special type of polynomial sequence that can be subjected to study via umbral calculus is polynomial sequences of
binomial type
.

Definition 2.2
.

We say
\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is of binomial type if for all
x,y
and
n\in\mathbb{N}

p_{n}(x+y)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}p_{k}(x)p_{n-k}(y).

(5)

It is worth noting that there are two ways to view this functional equation. By setting
q_{n}(x)\coloneqq p_{n}(x)/n!
, we obtain a convolution identity

q_{n}(x+y)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}q_{k}(x)q_{n-k}(y).

(6)

Both conventions are used in the literature
[
30
,
12
]
, but we will demonstrate that the original convention is operationally more convenient.

Theorem 2.1
.

For
\varphi\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}
, the application
\varphi\mapsto\{\varphi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bijective.

Proof
.

For the surjectivity, suppose that we have a polynomial sequence
\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. We define
\varphi
by
\varphi(x^{n})=p_{n}(x)
extended by linearity to power series. By definition,
\varphi
does not affect the degree, so
\varphi\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}
.

For injectivity, suppose
\varphi,\psi\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}
such that
\varphi_{n}(x)=\psi_{n}(x)
for all
n
and
x
. Equivalently,
\varphi x^{n}=\psi x^{n}
for all
n
, and hence
\varphi=\psi
by
Proposition

2.1
. Thus, the map is injective.

We designate
p\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}
as the inverse of the map in
Theorem

2.1
for
\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, and extend the notion of unitarity to operators in
\hat{\mathcal{L}}
by saying that
p
is unitary if and only if
\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is unitary.

We aim to present bivariate characterizations of some known classes of operators. It is observed that many of the operators studied in umbral calculus are characterized in bivariate settings. A notable instance of such an operator is the linear operator that maps monomials to sequences of binomial-type polynomials.

Proposition 2.3
.

A polynomial sequence
\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is of binomial type if and only if, for two variables
x,y
,
p_{x+y}=p_{x}p_{y}
.

Proof
.

On the one hand,
p_{x+y}(x+y)^{n}=p_{n}(x+y)
. On the other hand

p_{x}p_{y}(x+y)^{n}=p_{x}p_{y}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}x^{k}y^{n-k}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}p_{k}(x)p_{n-k}(y)=p_{n}(x+y).

2.3
Delta operators

The construction of the original umbral calculus
[
25
,
30
]
was performed using delta operators. While we believe this is not the most natural way to construct the theory, it is arguably the simplest and the easiest to motivate.

Definition 2.3

(shift-invariance)
.

An operator
U\in\mathcal{L}(\textnormal{P})
is said to be shift-invariant if it commutes with
E^{a}
for all
a
.

Example 2.1
.

The simplest example of shift-invariant operators is, of course,
E^{a}
. Another example is the derivative operator
D
. However, the multiplication operator
x
is not shift-invariant.

The following proposition provides a bivariate characterization of shift-invariant operators.

Proposition 2.4
.

A linear operator
U
is shift-invariant if and only if, for two variables
x
and
y
,
U_{x}=U_{x+y}
.

Proof
.

The equivalence is explained by the general identity for linear operators:
E_{x}^{y}U_{x}=U_{x+y}E_{x}^{y}
.

Definition 2.4

(delta operator)
.

An operator
Q
is a delta operator if
Q
is shift-invariant and
Qx=c
for some constant
c\neq 0
. If
c=1
, we say that
Q
is unitary.

Example 2.2
.

After the derivative operator
D
, the second most important (unitary) delta operator is the forward difference operator
\Delta
, defined by

\Delta f(x)=f(x+1)-f(x).

Writing
\Delta=E-1
, it can be easily checked that
\Delta
satisfies the definition conditions.

While the definition of delta operators may appear too weak to support a robust mathematical theory, this perception is mistaken, it underscores why this approach to constructing umbral calculus is, in fact, remarkably elegant.

Proposition 2.5

(
[
30
, Propositions 2.1 and 2.2]
)
.

If
Q
is a delta operator, then

(a)

for
a\in\mathbb{K}
,
Qa=0
.

(b)

for a nonconstant polynomial
p(x)
,
\deg Qp(x)=\deg p(x)-1
.

Proof
.

(a)

We make a single use of all the given properties

Qa+c=Qa+Qx=Q(a+x)=QE^{a}x=E^{a}Qx=E^{a}c=c,

conequently
Qa=0
.

(b)

For
n>0
, let
q(x)=Qx^{n}
. For any
a

\displaystyle q(x+a)

\displaystyle=E^{a}q(x)=E^{a}Qx^{n}=QE^{a}x^{n}=Q(x+a)^{n}

\displaystyle=Q\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}a^{k}x^{n-k}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}a^{k}Qx^{n-k}.

Therefore, by applying
\mathcal{E}
, we recover the polynomial
q(a)
, for any
a

\mathcal{E}q(x+a)=q(a)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}a^{k}\mathcal{E}Qx^{n-k}.

The coefficient in front of
a^{n}
is
\mathcal{E}Qx^{0}=0
by (a). The one in front of
a^{n-1}
is
n\mathcal{E}Qx=nc\neq 0
. Therefore,
q(x)
is indeed of degree
n-1
.

Corollary 2.1

(
[
12
, Corollary 2.1.9b]
)
.

If
p(x)
is a polynomial such that
Qp(x)=0
then
p(x)
is constant.

Proof
.

Let us prove the contrapositive and suppose that
p(x)
is nonconstant, that is, of degree
\geq 1
. Then, by
Proposition

2.5
,
\deg Qp(x)\geq 0
. In other words,
Qp(x)\neq 0
.

2.4
Sheffer and umbral operators

Definition 2.5
.

An invertible operator
s
is called a Sheffer operator for
Q
if

Qs_{n}(x)=ns_{n-1}(x),

We then refer to
\{s_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
as a Sheffer sequence or set for
Q
. If
Q=D
, then
s
is an Appell operator and
\{s_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
an Appell sequence or set.

An invertible operator
\varphi
is said to be an umbral operator or basic for
Q
if it satisfies

•

\{\varphi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Sheffer set for
Q

•

\varphi_{0}(x)=1

•

for
n>0
,
\varphi_{n}(0)=0
.

We denote the relation by
\varphi\leadsto Q
. We then call
\{\varphi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
the basic sequence or set of
Q
. Finally,
\varphi
is said to be a Sheffer/Appell/umbral operator if it is for some delta operator.

The additional conditions imposed on basic sets naturally constrain Sheffer sets, facilitating a bijection with their delta operator.

Proposition 2.6

(
[
30
, Proposition 2.3]
)
.

(a)

For every Sheffer operator
s
, there exists a unique delta operator
Q
such that
s
is a Sheffer operator for
Q
.

(b)

There is a one-to-one correspondence between the set of delta operators and the set of umbral operators.

Proof
.

(a)

If
R
also serves as a delta operator for
s
, then, in particular, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
Qs_{n}(x)=Rs_{n}(x)
. Since
\{s_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
forms a polynomial sequence, by
Proposition

2.1
,
R=Q
.

(b)

We construct by induction the basic polynomial sequence
\{\varphi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
for
Q
. The initial term is uniquely imposed by its definition. Assume, for
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, that
\{\varphi_{k}\}_{0\leq k<n}
is uniquely determined and satisfies the basic set conditions. Given that it constitutes a basis for
\textnormal{P}_{n-1}
, the expression
Qx^{n}\in\textnormal{P}_{n-1}
(by
Proposition

2.5
) can be uniquely decomposed with respect to the basis

Qx^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}c_{k}\varphi_{k}(x),\quad c_{n-1}\neq 0.

Should
\varphi_{n}(x)
meet the criteria of the Sheffer condition, it can be reformulated as follows

Q\left(x^{n}-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{c_{k}}{k+1}\varphi_{k+1}(x)\right)=0,

wherein, by
Corollary

2.1
, the operand of
Q
must be constant. It is chosen to be 0, ensuring that
\varphi_{n}(0)=0
and thereby uniquely defining
\varphi_{n}(x)
as

\varphi_{n}(x)=\frac{n}{c_{n-1}}\left(x^{n}-\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{c_{k-1}}{k}\varphi_{k}(x)\right).

Consequently, we have constructed the unique basic sequence for
Q
. In particular, it is a Sheffer sequence. Therefore, by (a),
Q
is established as its unique delta operator, thus confirming the bijection.

Example 2.3
.

The basic set for
D
is evidently the monomials
\{x^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. This generalizes straightforwardly for the delta operator
D/\lambda
, where
\lambda\neq 0
. Its basic set is verified to be
\{(\lambda x)^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Since this transformation constitutes a scaling action, we refer to the umbral operator
\mathcal{S}^{\left(\lambda\right)}\leadsto D/\lambda
as the
stretch operator
.

We define
\phi
via the relation
\phi\leadsto\Delta
. It can be verified that
\phi_{n}(x)=(x)_{n}
, the falling factorials. Later, we shall develop tools to derive expressions for basic sets, eliminating the need to guess their expression.

Proposition 2.7
.

If
\varphi\leadsto Q
, then these statements are equivalent

(a)

Qx=c
.

(b)

[x^{n}]\varphi_{n}(x)=c^{-n}
.

(c)

\varphi_{1}(x)=x/c
.

In particular, we have the equivalence between
Q
being unitary,
\varphi
being unitary, and
\varphi_{1}(x)=x
.

Proof
.

(c
\Rightarrow
a)

Follows from
Qx=Qc\varphi_{1}(x)=c\varphi_{0}(x)=c
.

(b
\Rightarrow
c)

Is evident because
\varphi_{1}(0)
is always
0
.

(a
\Rightarrow
b)

This follows from the construction of
\varphi_{n}(x)
we made in the proof of
Proposition

2.6
combined with the fact that
[x^{n-1}]Qx^{n}=cn
(as shown in the proof of
Proposition

2.5
(b)).

A primary focus of this paper is to characterize Sheffer and basic sets using operator identities. This is straightforwardly achieved for Sheffer sets through a general commutation identity.

Proposition 2.8
.

An invertible operator
s
is a Sheffer operator for
Q
if and only if
Qs=sD
.

Proof
.

This set of equalities essentially proves the equivalence

\begin{array}[]{ccc}Qs_{n}(x)&=&ns_{n-1}(x)\\
\rotatebox{90.0}{$\,=\mkern 7.0mu$}&&\rotatebox{90.0}{$\,=\mkern 7.0mu$}\\[-3.0pt]
Qsx^{n}&=&sDx^{n}.\end{array}

The fact that the monomials form a basic set for
D
and are also of binomial type is no coincidence, as we will show that all basic sets are of binomial type. Additionally, we will demonstrate that the converse holds true as well.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
30
, Theorem 1]
)
.

\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a basic set for some delta operator
Q
if and only if
\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a binomial type polynomial sequence.

Proof
.

(
\Rightarrow
)

We proceed by induction on
n
. The binomial property (
Definition

2.2
) trivially holds for
n=0
.

Let
n>0
.
Qp_{n}(x+y)=QE^{y}p_{n}(x)=E^{y}np_{n-1}(x)=np_{n-1}(x+y)
. Assuming the induction hypothesis for
n-1
, that is,
p_{n-1}(x+y)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{k}p_{k}(x)p_{n-1-k}(y)
, where
x
is the variable and
y
serves as a parameter. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle Qp_{n}(x+y)

\displaystyle=n\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{k}p_{k}(x)p_{n-1-k}(y)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}n\binom{n-1}{j-1}p_{j-1}(x)p_{n-j}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}j\binom{n}{j}p_{j-1}(x)p_{n-j}(y)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{n}{k}Qp_{k}(x)p_{n-k}(y).

Therefore, by linearity

Q\left(p_{n}(x+y)-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{n}{k}p_{k}(x)p_{n-k}(y)\right)=0

In accordance with
Corollary

2.1
, the argument of
Q
must be constant. However, upon evaluation at
x=0
, this constant is identified as
p_{n}(y)
since
p_{k}(0)=0
for
k>0
. Additionally,
p_{0}(x)=1
. Consequently

p_{n}(x+y)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}p_{k}(x)p_{n-k}(y),

thus finishing the induction.

(
\Leftarrow
)

Analogous to the proof of
Theorem

2.1
, we define
Q
by
Q(p_{n}(x))=np_{n-1}(x)
and
Q(p_{0}(x))=0
extended by linearity to all power series. In particular, because
p_{1}(x)=cx
for some nonzero constant
c
,
Qx=Qp_{1}(x)/c=p_{0}(x)/c=1/c\neq 0
. For the shift-invariance, we invoke the binomial property

\displaystyle QE^{y}p_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=Qp_{n}(x+y)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}Qp_{k}(x)p_{n-k}(y)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}kp_{k-1}(x)p_{n-k}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}n\binom{n-1}{j}p_{j}(x)p_{n-j-1}(y)=np_{n-1}(x+y)=E^{y}Qp_{n}(x),

and by linearity,
QE^{y}=E^{y}Q
, confirming that
Q
is a delta operator for
\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

Example 2.4

(Chu-Vandermonde identity)
.

The binomial property for
\{(x)_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
gives

(x+y)_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}(x)_{k}(y)_{n-k},

which, in the fashion of
equation

6
, can be rewritten into Chu’s generalization of the Vandermonde identity

\binom{x+y}{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{x}{k}\binom{y}{n-k}.

3
Classic theorems

With the foundational layer of umbral calculus established, we shall now present the first set of computational tools, referred to as the “classic theorems”. These tools, which were central to the groundbreaking work of Rota
[
30
]

3.1
Shift-invariant operator expansion

Undoubtedly, one of the most important early results of umbral calculus is the Expansion Theorem. It expresses every shift-invariant operator as a power series in a delta operator. Such formulas are free from convergence issues in
P
, as their application to polynomials invariably yields a finite sum, ranging from 0 to the polynomial’s degree. Remarkably, this property also applies to any power series that would typically be divergent.

Theorem 3.1

(Expansion theorem.
[
30
, Theorem 2]
)
.

Q
is a delta operator with basic set
\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
if and only if for all shift-invariant operators
U

U=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\frac{Q^{k}}{k!},\quad a_{k}=\mathcal{E}Up_{k}(x).

Proof
.

(
\Rightarrow
)

We apply
\mathcal{E}U
to the binomial identity

\mathcal{E}Up_{n}(x+y)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\mathcal{E}Up_{k}(x)p_{n-k}(y)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!}\mathcal{E}Up_{k}(x)Q_{y}^{k}p_{n}(y),

where the summation index is extended extended to infinity, as the additional terms are zero. By
Proposition

2.4
and the inherent commutativity of linear operators applied to different variables, we establish

\mathcal{E}_{x}U_{x}p_{n}(x+y)=\mathcal{E}_{x}U_{y}p_{n}(y+x)=U_{y}\mathcal{E}_{x}p_{n}(y+x)=U_{y}p_{n}(y).

Setting
a_{k}=\mathcal{E}Up_{k}(x)
, we obtain the desired result by linearity.

(
\Leftarrow
)

Conversely, we initially show that
Q
is a delta operator. Using the given hypothesis with
E^{a}
for any
a
, gives

E^{a}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p_{k}(a)\frac{Q^{k}}{k!}.

This demonstrates the shift-invariance of
Q
. Apply
(*)
to
x

x+a=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p_{k}(a)\frac{Q^{k}}{k!}x.

Since
\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
forms a polynomial sequence and thus a basis, the identification of the coefficient before
p_{1}(a)
leads to the conclusion that
Qx
must be non-zero. Therefore,
Q
is a delta operator associated with a unique basic set
\{q_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Applying
(*)
to
q_{n}(x)
yields

q_{n}(x+a)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}p_{k}(a)q_{n}(x),

as
\{q_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is basic. Letting
x=0
gives
q_{n}(a)=p_{n}(a)
. Therefore,
\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is the basic set for
Q
.

The ramifications of this theorem can be distilled to the established connection between shift-invariant operators and power series. Many analogues of operations on power series can be defined for operators, a notion that will be elaborated upon in the subsequent sections.

Example 3.1
.

Specifying the formula for
E^{a}
in the context of the proof with
Q=D
, we derive the following expression

E^{a}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a^{k}\frac{D^{k}}{k!}=e^{aD}.

(7)

This identity, which dates back to Lagrange (1772)
[
10
, p. 6]
, was written as an operator translation of Taylor’s theorem. This makes the following proposition clear.

Proposition 3.1
.

An operator is shift-invariant if and only if it commutes with the derivative
D
.

Proof
.

(
\Rightarrow
)

For an operator
U
, expanding
e^{aD}U=Ue^{aD}
allows us to identify coefficients in front of
a
, revealing that
DU=UD
.

(
\Leftarrow
)

We generalize
DU=UD
as
D^{k}U=UD^{k}
for all positive integers
k
. Summing this equality with a factor of
a^{k}/k!
recovers the shift-invariance.

Corollary 3.1
.

An operator is an Appell operator if and only if it is shift-invariant and invertible.

Proof
.

By
Proposition

2.8
, an Appell operator is characterized by
DA=AD
. The proof follows with
Proposition

3.1
.

More generally than in
Example

3.1
, the previous theorem ensures that every shift-invariant operator can be written as a power series in
D
. This enables us to apply a wide range of established results from power series theory to our operators.

Definition 3.1

(Indicator)
.

If
T
is shift-invariant, we associate a power series
\tilde{T}
, called the indicator, defined by
\tilde{T}(D)\coloneqq T
. It is then natural to use the composition of indicators to define a new operator

•

T\diamond U\coloneqq(\tilde{T}\circ\tilde{U})(D)=\tilde{T}(U)
.

•

For

,
T^{[n]}\coloneqq\tilde{T}^{n}(D)
. If
\tilde{T}
has a compositional inverse,

Naturally, we have the following properties.

Proposition 3.2
.

•

\diamond
is associative and right-distributive over

.

•

D
is the neutral element with respect to
\diamond
.

•

T^{[n]}\diamond T^{[m]}=T^{[n+m]}
.

•

(T^{[n]})^{[m]}=T^{[nm]}
.

Example 3.2
.

If we look back at
Examples

2.2
and
3.1
, then we realize that
\tilde{\Delta}(t)=e^{t}-1
.

Translating the fact that two power series with the same argument commute to shift-invariant operators, the subsequent corollary is concluded.

Corollary 3.2

(
[
30
, Corollary 3.4]
)
.

Two shift-invariant operators commute.

The expansion theorem enables us to define operator division for non-invertible shift-invariant operators. Division of operators has been extensively utilized by Steffensen
[
35
]
and others, albeit without a proper definition. We will later demonstrate that such a definition is crucial in order to avoid arriving at contradictory equations.

Definition 3.2

(Operator division)
.

For
k\geq 0
, let
U=D^{k}P
and
V=D^{k}R
be two shift-invariant operators with

being invertible. Then we define

U/V=\frac{U}{V}\coloneqq PR^{-1}.

One natural property that this division still holds is the following:

\frac{U}{V}V=U.

(8)

3.2
The isomorphism theorem

The isomorphism theorem is a fundamental result that is crucial to state explicitly, although it often becomes implicitly used in practice. It was first properly formulated in the work of Rota and Mullin
[
25
]
, despite being extensively used by earlier authors such as Pincherle
[
28
]
and Steffensen
[
35
]
. It serves as a crucial tool for applying the known results of power series.

Theorem 3.2

(Isomorphism theorem
[
30
, Theorem 3]
)
.

If
Q
is a delta operator and

a formal power series, the application
f\mapsto f(Q)
is a ring isomorphism that maps the ring of formal power series
\tilde{\textnormal{P}}
to the ring of shift-invariant operators.

The proof is straightforward.

Now, we can freely apply the known properties of multiplicative and compositional inverses in the ring of formal power series to their operational analogues.

Proposition 3.3

(
[
30
, Corollaries 3.1, 3.2 and Proposition 4.4]
)
.

(a)

A shift-invariant operator

is invertible if and only if
T1\neq 0
.

(b)

is a delta operator if and only if

, for some Appell operator

.

(c)

A shift-invariant operator

(see

3.1
) has a power series compositional inverse

. In this case,

is a well-defined delta operator.

Proof
.

(a)

The power series

has a multiplicative inverse if and only if

\tilde{T}(0)=T1
, so by the isomorphism theorem (

), the result is proved.

(c)

If

is a delta operator, then

and

2.4

is of order

having a power series compositional inverse

.

Q^{[-1]}x=(\tilde{Q}^{-1})^{\prime}(0)=\frac{1}{\tilde{Q}^{\prime}(\tilde{Q}^{-1}(0))}=\frac{1}{\tilde{Q}^{\prime}(0)}=\frac{1}{c}\neq 0,

thus

is a delta operator.

(b,

\Rightarrow
)

Consequently, we also factor out an

is an Appell operator.

(
\Leftarrow
)

If

By the definition of operator division (
Definition

3.2
) and point (b) of the above proposition, we can infer the following corollary (utilizing the more concise characterization of

3.1

Corollary 3.3
.

If

and

is an Appell operator, and

Example 3.3
.

The

defined by

\mathcal{B}\coloneqq\frac{D}{\Delta},

is an Appell operator. Furthermore, it maps monomials to the

\mathcal{B}x^{n}=\mathcal{B}_{n}(x).

One must be cautious with operator division, as it does not behave the same as the usual division. For example, we can write

, but we do not have the equality

\mathcal{B}=-D\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}E^{k},

With all the tools developed and the notation established, we are now prepared to state the following theorem. It is significant because it demonstrates the closure of Sheffer and umbral operators under composition while providing explicit expressions for the associated delta operators in terms of power series composition. This aspect will play a central role in connecting the umbral calculus to the iterative theory in

.

Theorem 3.3

(
[
30
, Theorem 7]
)
.

If
\varphi
is a Sheffer operator for
Q
and
\psi
is a Sheffer operator for
R
, then

(a)

\psi\varphi
is a Sheffer operator for

.

(b)

If
\varphi\leadsto Q
and
\psi\leadsto R

\psi\varphi\leadsto Q\diamond R

and for all

,

(c)

\varphi^{n}
is a Sheffer operator for

.

(d)

If
\varphi\leadsto Q
, then
\varphi^{n}\leadsto Q^{[n]}
.

Proof
.

(a)

According to

2.8
,
\varphi D=\tilde{Q}(D)\varphi
and
\psi D=R\psi
. If we express
\tilde{Q}(t)=\sum a_{k}t^{k}

\psi\varphi D=\psi\tilde{Q}(D)\varphi=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\psi D^{k}\varphi=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}R^{k}\psi\varphi=\tilde{Q}(R)\psi\varphi.

Using

(b)

Suppose that
\varphi

(\psi\varphi)_{n}(0)=a_{1}\psi_{1}(0)+\ldots+a_{n}\psi_{n}(0)=\varphi_{n}(0)=0.

Furthermore

So, by definition,
\psi\varphi

(c, d)

For

n

If
\varphi

3.3
The Pincherle derivative and the closed forms

In this section, we will develop the Pincherle derivative to facilitate the expression and proof of closed forms for basic sets (
Theorem

3.4
). Subsequently, we will discuss the immediate corollaries of this theorem.

Definition 3.3
.

We call
T^{\prime}
the Pincherle derivative of an operator
T
, defined by

T^{\prime}\coloneqq T\text{x}-\text{x}T.

Proposition 3.4

(
[
30
, Propositions 4.1 and 4.2]
)
.

(a)

The Pincherle derivative is linear over polynomials of
x
.

Let
T
be a shift-invariant operator,

(b)

T^{\prime}
is shift-invariant.

(c)

The indicator and the derivative commute, that is,
\widetilde{T^{\prime}}=(\tilde{T})^{\prime}

Proposition

3.4
essentially implies that the Pincherle derivative behaves similarly to the usual derivative for shift-invariant operators, with
D
acting as a “variable”. In particular, we inherit the operational analogues of the product rule and the chain rule.

Proof
.

(a) is obvious. The product rule implies that
D^{\prime}=D\text{x}-\text{x}D=1
. With this in mind, we can easily show by induction that
(D^{k})^{\prime}=kD^{k-1}
, from which (b) and (c) follow.

Theorem 3.4

(Closed forms
[
30
, Theorem 4]
)
.

If
\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is the basic set for
Q
then for
n\geq 0

Transfer formula:

p_{n}(x)=Q^{\prime}(D/Q)^{n+1}x^{n}
.

Steffensen formula :

p_{n}(x)=\text{x}(D/Q)^{n}x^{n-1}
.

Recurrence formula :

p_{n+1}(x)=\text{x}(Q^{\prime})^{-1}p_{n}(x)
.

The first pair of equations is traditionally referred to as the “transfer formulas”. To avoid ambiguity, given that both will be employed in the following, the latter shall be designated after Steffensen, in recognition of his contributions to the theorem as a whole
[
35
]
.

Proof
.

Let us define
P\coloneqq D/Q
, which is well defined by
Proposition

3.3
(b), and
q_{n}(x)=Q^{\prime}P^{n+1}x^{n}
.
First, we establish the equivalence between the transfer formula and the Steffensen formula using the properties of the Pincherle derivative (see
Proposition

3.4
). For
n\geq 0
:

Q^{\prime}P^{n+1}=(DP^{-1})^{\prime}P^{n+1}=(D^{\prime}P^{-1}-DP^{\prime}P^{-2})P^{n+1}=P^{n}-P^{\prime}P^{n-1}D,

which we apply to the monomials

\displaystyle q_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=Q^{\prime}P^{n+1}x^{n}=P^{n}x^{n}-P^{\prime}P^{n-1}nx^{n-1}

\displaystyle=P^{n}x^{n}-(P^{n})^{\prime}x^{n-1}=P^{n}x^{n}-(P^{n}\text{x}-\text{x}P^{n})x^{n-1}

\displaystyle=\text{x}P^{n}x^{n-1}.

With this formula, we have
q_{0}(x)=1
and for
n>0
,
q_{n}(0)=0
. We want to show that
\{q_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Sheffer sequence for
Q=DP^{-1}
to show its basicity. By the uniqueness of the basic sequence, we would consequently have
\{q_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}=\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. To this end, we reuse the transfer formula

Qq_{n}(x)=DP^{-1}Q^{\prime}P^{n+1}x^{n}=Q^{\prime}P^{n}Dx^{n}=nQ^{\prime}P^{n}x^{n-1}=nq_{n-1}(x).

Therefore, the first two formulas hold.

For the recurrence formula, we first invert the first transfer formula.
Q^{\prime}
is invertible because
P^{-1}
is

x^{n}=P^{-n-1}(Q^{\prime})^{-1}p_{n}(x).

Now we apply
\text{x}P^{n+1}
on both sides to get the Steffensen formula back at index
n+1
on the left hand-side

p_{n+1}(x)=\text{x}P^{n+1}P^{-n-1}(Q^{\prime})^{-1}p_{n}(x)=\text{x}(Q^{\prime})^{-1}p_{n}(x).

Example 3.4
.

To derive the basic set
\{\phi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
for
\Delta
, we use the recurrence relation. Given
\left(\Delta^{\prime}\right)^{-1}=e^{-D}=E^{-1}
, we compute

\displaystyle\phi_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\text{x}E^{-1}\phi_{n-1}(x)=x\phi_{n-1}(x-1)

\displaystyle=x(x-1)\phi_{n-2}(x-2)=\ldots=x(x-1)\cdots 1\cdot\phi_{0}(x),

\{\phi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is basic, so
\phi_{0}(x)=1
, hence
\phi_{n}(x)=(x)_{n}
the falling factorials, as expected. Similarly, for
Q=D/\lambda
, using either of the formulas, we obtain
\mathcal{S}^{\left(\lambda\right)}x^{n}=\lambda^{n}x^{n}
.

The two previous theorems exemplify the significant roles played by both polynomial sequences and their associated umbral operators. It is essential to consider both aspects in our analysis, as relying solely on one is insufficient.

The following commutation identities emerge directly from the recurrence formula.

(
[
30
, Proposition 7.3]
)
.

\varphi

\varphi\text{x}=\text{x}(Q^{\prime})^{-1}\varphi=\text{x}\varphi(Q^{[-1]})^{\prime}.

The first equality is immediate thanks to the recurrence relation of

\varphi\text{x}x^{n}=\varphi_{n+1}(x)=\text{x}(Q^{\prime})^{-1}\varphi_{n}(x)=\text{x}(Q^{\prime})^{-1}\varphi x^{n}.

,
\varphi^{-1}\leadsto Q^{[-1]}

.

As easily as in

4.4
Application to iteration theory

Umbral calculus, as we have constructed it, can be interpreted as an investigation into the structure of invertible power series through the following series of bijections :

5
The operator point of view

This section presents a collection of various results related to operators. 5.1
A sidestep into operational calculus

The expansion theorem provides a formula for expanding shift-invariant operators in terms of the derivative

Theorem 5.1

If

By construction

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}f_{k}(\text{x})g_{k}(Q)\G_{\varphi}(x,t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}f_{k}(x)g_{k}(t)\G_{\varphi}(x,t)=\G_{U\varphi}(x,t).

After identifying the coefficients of

Lemma 5.1

Let

\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}f(x)&u(x)&v(x)\\
\hline\cr\hline\cr\rule{0.0pt}{9.90276pt}ax&g(x)&g(x)^{-1}\\

5.3
Explicit formulas for umbral operators

The topic of closed forms for umbral operators is challenging. Except for the identity, they are not shift-invariant, making them inapplicable to the Expansion theorem. Rota expressed the thought of solving “Pincherle differential equations”
[
30
, p. 711]
umbral operators were satisfying, to find their expressions. However, due to all of the noncommutative issues, this idea was never developed and probably never will. The first formula was published by Garsia
[
13
]
, although it can be directly derived from Corollary 7 of
[
30
]
.

Proposition 5.3
.

If
\varphi\leadsto Q
,

\varphi^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\text{x}^{k}}{k!}\mathcal{E}Q^{k}.

This result is in fact its own extension of the Taylor expansion in a different direction from the expansion theorem.

Proof
.

Expanding
E^{y}
into powers of
Q
via the expansion theorem (
Theorem

3.1
), and applying the result to a polynomial
p(x)
yields

p(x+y)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\varphi_{n}(y)}{n!}Q^{k}p(x),

which we use in this calculation

\varphi^{-1}p(x)=\varphi^{-1}_{x}\mathcal{E}_{y}p(y+x)=\varphi^{-1}_{x}\mathcal{E}_{y}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\varphi_{n}(x)}{n!}Q_{y}^{k}p(y)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}\mathcal{E}_{y}Q_{y}^{k}p(y)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\text{x}^{n}}{n!}\mathcal{E}Q^{k}p(x),

to then notice the equality of the operators.

The proof suggests an obvious generalization, which we do not state, since the special case above is the simplest, and umbral operators are better manipulated in their simplest forms. This formula is, in fact, the definition of the inverse of a basic sequence used in
[
29
, p. 111]
(referred to as conjugates).

This formula, while simple to use, has the drawback of including the evaluation operator
\mathcal{E}
which is impractical due to its limited set of identities. Di Bucchianico and Loeb, using the Kurbanov-Maksimov expansion (
Theorem

5.1
), derived a more elegant formula expressed solely in terms of
x
and
D
. Furthermore, it can be reformulated in a more compact and aesthetically pleasing manner using our notation.

Theorem 5.4

(Kurbanov-Maksimov closed form
[
6
, Proposition 21]
)
.

If
\varphi\leadsto Q
,

\varphi=(e^{\text{x}})^{Q^{[-1]}-D}.

Proof
.

After using
Theorem

4.2

\frac{\G_{\varphi}(x,t)}{\G_{1}(x,t)}=e^{x(\tilde{Q}^{-1}(t)-t)}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!}x^{k}(\tilde{Q}^{-1}(t)-t)^{k},

hence by Kurbanov-Maksimov expansion (
Theorem

5.1
).

\varphi=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!}\text{x}^{k}(\tilde{Q}^{-1}(D)-D)^{k}=(e^{\text{x}})^{Q^{[-1]}-D}.

Example 5.1
.

Most applications of this theorem will not lead to useful closed forms. For example,
\phi=(e^{\text{x}})^{\log(1+D)-D}

The closed forms for basic sequences (

\displaystyle Q^{\prime}(D/Q)^{\text{x}D}x^{n}=Q^{\prime}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(D/Q-1)^{k}\binom{\text{x}D+1}{k}x^{n}

Deriving the equality

Now we want to investigate the formula for umbral operators in the simplest form possible, i.e.

5.4
Sigma operators

Although it is a simple consequence of
Proposition

3.3
(a) and
Proposition

2.5
(a) that delta operators are not invertible, it seems entirely natural to define some kind of
antiderivative
analog to the derivative but for other delta operators. This idea dates back to Steffensen
[
35
]
where he defined the antiderivative
\bar{D}
such that
D\bar{D}=1
and
(\bar{D}f)(0)=0
for any polynomial
f
, and extended the definition to delta operators via
\bar{Q}=\bar{D}(D/Q)
. Steffensen realized that
D
and
\bar{D}
did not commute since
\bar{D}Df(x)=f(x)-f(0)
. It is upon this fact that we will define pseudoinverses of delta operators. We shall call them
sigma operators
.

Proposition 5.4
.

For any delta operator
Q
, there exists a unique linear operator
Q^{-1}
such that

QQ^{-1}=1\quad\mathbin{\textnormal{and }}\quad Q^{-1}Q=1-\mathcal{E}.

(40)

Moreover, if
\{s_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Sheffer set for
Q
,

Q^{-1}s_{n}(x)=\frac{s_{n+1}(x)-s_{n}(0)}{n+1}.

(41)

We call
Q^{-1}
the sigma operator associated with
Q
.

Proof
.

Let
\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be the basic set for
Q
. Define
Q^{-1}
by
Q^{-1}p_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{n+1}p_{n+1}(x)
and extended by linearity on all power series. So by the binomial theorem (
Theorem

3.5
)

\displaystyle Q^{-1}s_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=Q^{-1}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}s_{n-k}(0)p_{k}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}\binom{n}{j-1}s_{n-j+1}(0)\frac{p_{j}(x)}{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}\binom{n+1}{j}s_{n+1-j}(0)p_{j}(x)=\frac{s_{n+1}(x)-s_{n+1}(0)}{n+1},

because
p_{0}(x)=1
. Now we verify that it follows the said properties

\displaystyle QQ^{-1}f(x)=Q\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{a_{k}}{k+1}p_{k+1}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}p_{k}(x)=f(x)

\displaystyle Q^{-1}Qf(x)=Q^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}kp_{k-1}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}p_{k}(x)=f(x)-a_{0}=(1-\mathcal{E})f(x)

Thus,
Q^{-1}
exists, and to show the unicity, suppose
I_{Q}
is another sigma operator, then, in particular,
Q^{-1}Q=I_{Q}Q
. Because
Q
is surjective,
Q^{-1}=I_{Q}
.

Proposition 5.5
.

If
\varphi\leadsto Q
, for positive integers
n
and
p

(n+1)\cdots(n+p)Q^{-p}\varphi_{n}(x)=\varphi_{n+p}(x),

where
Q^{-p}\coloneqq(Q^{-1})^{p}
. In particular,
\varphi_{n}(x)=n!Q^{-n}1
.

Sigma operators can be easily extended by defining
Q^{-1}_{(a)}\coloneqq E^{-a}Q^{-1}E^{a}
. They are characterized by

QQ^{-1}_{(a)}=1,\quad\quad Q^{-1}_{(a)}Q=1-\mathcal{E}^{\left(a\right)}.

(42)

This extension allows for a broader application of sigma operators.

Example 5.3
.

An unsurprising exact formula for the
integral operator

\mathcal{I}\coloneqq D^{-1}
applied to functions is

\mathcal{I}_{\left(a\right)}f:x\longmapsto\int_{a}^{x}f(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,

which holds by the unicity.
\mathcal{I}_{\left(a\right)}D=1-\mathcal{E}^{\left(a\right)}
is the operational translation of the fundamental theorem of calculus.

For the
sum operator

\Sigma\coloneqq\Delta^{-1}
, we have the natural formula to compute it, but it only works when
a
and
x
are integers

\Sigma_{\left(a\right)}f:x\longmapsto\sum_{k=a}^{x-1}f(k),

and
\Sigma_{\left(a\right)}\Delta=1-\mathcal{E}^{\left(a\right)}
is simply a rewording of the telescopic sum. We will discuss later how
\Sigma_{\left(a\right)}
actually constitutes a generalization of the summation operation to fractional indices.

Since sigma operators act as pseudo-inverses, it is logical to assume they are connected to general inverses and operator division. This assumption is indeed correct.

Proposition 5.6
.

For
Q,R
two delta operators, these identities hold

(a)

\mathcal{E}^{\left(a\right)}Q^{-1}_{(a)}=0
.

(b)

For an Appell operator
A
,
(AQ)^{-1}_{(a)}=Q^{-1}_{(a)}A^{-1}
.

(c)

Q^{-1}_{(a)}=R^{-1}_{(a)}\dfrac{R}{Q}
.

(d)

Q/R=QR^{-1}_{(a)}=QR^{-1}
.

Proof
.

First, by definition

Q^{-1}_{(a)}=Q^{-1}_{(a)}(QQ^{-1}_{(a)})=(1-\mathcal{E}^{\left(a\right)})Q^{-1}_{(a)}=Q^{-1}_{(a)}-\mathcal{E}^{\left(a\right)}Q^{-1}_{(a)},

from which we deduce (a). Furthermore, (a) implies (b) when we use that
AQ(AQ)^{-1}_{(a)}=1
and apply
Q^{-1}_{(a)}A^{-1}
on the left

Q^{-1}_{(a)}A^{-1}=Q^{-1}_{(a)}Q(AQ)^{-1}_{(a)}=(1-\mathcal{E}^{\left(a\right)})(AQ)^{-1}_{(a)}=(AQ)^{-1}_{(a)}.

Setting
A=R/Q
(which is an Appell operator by
Corollary

3.3
) we obtain (c) with
equation

8
. (d) follows by applying
R
to the left.

Example 5.4
.

With
Proposition

5.6
(d), we can obtain an integral formula for the inverse of the Bernoulli operator
\mathcal{B}^{-1}
since by

3.3

\mathcal{B}^{-1}=\Delta/D=\Delta\mathcal{I}_{\left(a\right)}.

Thus

\mathcal{B}^{-1}f(x)=\int_{a}^{x+1}f(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t-\int_{a}^{x}f(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\int_{x}^{x+1}f(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

Example 5.5

(Faulhaber’s formula)
.

Bernoulli numbers, defined in
Example

4.5
, notably find utility in expressing the sum of the first
n
-th powers. To find the formula, we apply identity (c) of
Proposition

5.6
, that is,
\Sigma=\mathcal{I}\mathcal{B}
, to the monomials

\sum_{k=0}^{x-1}k^{n}=\int_{0}^{x}\mathcal{B}_{n}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\frac{\mathcal{B}_{n+1}(x)-\mathcal{B}_{n+1}(0)}{n+1}=\frac{\mathcal{B}_{n+1}(x)-\mathcal{B}_{n+1}}{n+1},

(43)

because of
equation

41
. By
Theorem

3.5
we also have the following

\sum_{k=0}^{x-1}k^{n}=\int_{0}^{x}\mathcal{B}_{n}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{x}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\mathcal{B}_{k}(0)t^{n-k}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\mathcal{B}_{k}\frac{x^{n+1-k}}{n+1-k}.

(44)

Both formulas can be transformed into a better closed form, also known as
Faulhaber’s formula

\sum_{k=0}^{x-1}k^{n}=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n+1}{k}\mathcal{B}_{k}x^{n+1-k}.

(45)

Proposition 5.7

(Euler-Maclaurin identity)
.

For two delta operators
Q
and
R
, if you can write
Q/R=\sum a_{k}Q^{k}
, then

R^{-1}_{(a)}-a_{0}Q^{-1}_{(a)}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}(1-\mathcal{E}^{\left(a\right)})Q^{k-1}.

Proof
.

We simply apply
Proposition

5.6
(c) and expand

R^{-1}_{(a)}=Q^{-1}_{(a)}\frac{Q}{R}=Q^{-1}_{(a)}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}Q^{k}=a_{0}Q^{-1}_{(a)}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}(1-\mathcal{E}^{\left(a\right)})Q^{k-1}.

Example 5.6
.

The Bernoulli operator can be expanded with Bernoulli numbers :

\mathcal{B}=D/\Delta=\frac{D}{e^{D}-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathcal{B}_{k}}{k!}D^{k},

(46)

applying
Proposition

5.7
, we obtain the operational form of the classical Euler-Maclaurin formula

\Sigma_{\left(a\right)}-\mathcal{I}_{\left(a\right)}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathcal{B}_{k}}{k!}(1-\mathcal{E}^{\left(a\right)})D^{k-1}.

(47)

Proposition 5.8
.

If
\varphi\leadsto Q
. Let
s=\dfrac{Q}{R}\varphi
with
R
being another delta operator. then

R^{-1}_{(a)}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{s_{n}(0)-s_{n}(a-\text{x})}{n!}Q^{n-1}.

The formula containing the operator
x
supports the fact that sigma operators are not shift-invariant.

Proof
.

By
Proposition

5.6
(c)

\displaystyle R^{-1}_{(a)}G_{\varphi}(x,t)

\displaystyle=Q^{-1}_{(a)}\frac{Q}{R}e^{x\tilde{Q}^{-1}(t)}=Q^{-1}_{(a)}\G_{s}(x,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{Q^{-1}_{(a)}s_{n}(x)}{n!}t^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{s_{n+1}(x)-s_{n+1}(a)}{(n+1)!}t^{n}=\frac{\G_{s}(x,t)-\G_{s}(a,t)}{t},

because
s
is a Sheffer set for
Q
. Now to apply the Kurbanov-Maksimov expansion (
Theorem

5.1
) we perform this calculation, keeping in mind
Theorem

4.2

\frac{R^{-1}_{(a)}G_{\varphi}(x,t)}{G_{\varphi}(x,t)}=\frac{\G_{s}(0,t)-\G_{s}(a-x,t)}{t}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{s_{n}(0)-s_{n}(a-x)}{n!}t^{n-1},

whence the result.

It is more convenient to write
Q^{-1}_{(a)}
in terms of
Q
as
\varphi_{n}(0)=0
for
n>0
.

Corollary 5.3
.

If
\varphi\leadsto Q
,

Q^{-1}_{(a)}=-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\varphi_{n}(a-\text{x})}{n!}Q^{n-1}.

Example 5.7
.

For the integration operator, we obtain

\mathcal{I}_{\left(a\right)}=-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(a-\text{x})^{k}}{k!}D^{k-1},

(48)

which was featured in
[
6
]
(in the case
a=0
), contrary to the very remarkable formula for the discrete analogue

\Sigma_{\left(a\right)}=-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\binom{a-\text{x}}{k}\Delta^{k-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom{\text{x}-a+k}{k+1}(-\Delta)^{k}.

(49)

The surprising aspect is that recalling the definition of
\Sigma_{\left(a\right)}
in
Example

5.3
, we understand it sums the values of its argument between two integer bounds. However, this formula allows sums to be extended to any bounds, including complex ones. It does not carry the same intuition that we have with integer bounds and should not be confused with summation over some set
\sum_{k\in S}
. Instead, it represents a different kind of operation. For example, using
Proposition

5.6
(c) and
Theorem

4.2

\Sigma e^{xt}=\mathcal{I}\mathcal{B}e^{xt}=\frac{t}{e^{t}-1}\mathcal{I}e^{xt}=\frac{t}{e^{t}-1}\frac{e^{xt}-1}{t}=\frac{e^{xt}-1}{e^{t}-1},

(50)

which is the fractional generalization of the geometric sum formula

\sum_{k=0}^{x-1}e^{kt}=\sum_{k=0}^{x-1}(e^{t})^{k}=\frac{e^{xt}-1}{e^{t}-1},

where
x
is a positive integer and
t
is nonzero. Müller and Schleicher have previously established a successful theory of fractional sums in
[
26
]
. Their theory bears similarities with the fractional sums that arise as a consequence of the Ramanujan summation technique
[
7
, Section 1.4.2]
. Therefore, it would be intriguing to explore the connections, and possibly establish equivalence, between
\Sigma
and the previously developed fractional sums.

6
Examples

To thoroughly demonstrate the effectiveness of the previously developed tools, we will explore some of the most important basic sets, including
Abel
,
Touchard
, and
Laguerre
polynomials, after revisiting the more recent example
[
11
, Example 3.2.7 c]
. Specifically, through quick computations, we will derive both old and new identities, some of which previously lacked umbral proofs. Furthermore, a new generalization of Laguerre polynomials will be introduced, which naturally arises from
Theorem

5.6
and is referred to as
degenerate

6.1
A summarizing example featuring Catalan numbers

The
logistic derivative
defined by
\Lambda\coloneqq D(1-D)
can be easily checked to be a delta operator. We define the
Catalan operator
by
\mathcal{C}\leadsto\Lambda
. The coefficients of Catalan polynomials can be quickly computed with the Steffensen formula (
Theorem

3.4
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\text{x}\left(\frac{D}{\Lambda}\right)^{n}x^{n-1}=\text{x}(1-D)^{-n}x^{n-1}=\text{x}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom{-n}{k}(-D)^{k}x^{n-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n+k-1}{n-1}\frac{(n-1)!}{(n-1-k)!}x^{n-k}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{2n-k-1}{n-1}\frac{(n-1)!}{(k-1)!}x^{k}.

(51)

Because we can write

\Lambda=\frac{1-(1-2D)^{2}}{4},

(52)

the Catalan operator is part of the special class of umbral operators, thus, by
Theorem

5.3
, it is immediate that.

\mathcal{C}\text{x}^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{2n-k-1}{n-1}\frac{(n-1)!}{(k-1)!}\text{x}^{k}(1-2D)^{k-2n}\mathcal{C}.

(53)

Inversion of
equation

52
yields

\Lambda^{[-1]}=\dfrac{1-\sqrt{1-4D}}{2}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}C_{n-1}D^{n},

(54)

where
C_{n}
is the
n
-th the
Catalan number
. Without
Corollary

4.2
, it would be much more difficult to find the formula for the following power series

\left(\dfrac{1-\sqrt{1-4D}}{2}\right)^{k}=\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}\frac{k}{n}\binom{2n-k-1}{n-1}D^{n},

(55)

and constitutes yet another way to recover the famous formula for the Catalan numbers (with
k=1
)

C_{n}=\frac{1}{n+1}\binom{2n}{n}.

(56)

Moreover, we can also infer the evaluation of Bell polynomials at Catalan numbers

B_{n,k}(C_{0},\ldots,C_{n-k})=\frac{(n-1)!}{(k-1)!}\binom{2n-k-1}{n-1}.

(57)

Finding the inverse
\mathcal{C}^{-1}\leadsto\Lambda^{[-1]}
is made easier by
Proposition

4.7
. Indeed

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\Lambda}{D}\right)^{k}x^{n}=(1-D)^{k}x^{n}=\sum_{p=0}^{k}\binom{k}{p}(-D)^{p}x^{n}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{p=0}^{k}\binom{k}{p}(-1)^{p}\frac{n!}{(n-p)!}x^{n-p}=\sum_{p=n-k}^{n}\binom{k}{n-p}\frac{n!}{p!}(-1)^{n-p}x^{p},

from which we recover the coefficient at index
p=k

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\mathcal{C}^{-1}}=\begin{cases}0,\text{ if }k<\left\lceil\dfrac{n}{2}\right\rceil\\[9.0pt]
\displaystyle\binom{k}{n-k}\frac{n!}{k!}(-1)^{n-k},\text{ if }k\geq\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil,\end{cases}

(58)

keeping in mind that the binomial coefficient vanishes when
k\geq n-k
, or equivalently when
k\geq\left\lceil n/2\right\rceil
. The equality can be rewritten as

\mathcal{C}^{-1}_{n}(x)=\sum_{k=\left\lceil n/2\right\rceil}^{n}\binom{k}{n-k}\frac{n!}{k!}(-1)^{n-k}x^{k}.

(59)

\mathcal{C}^{-1}
is also part of the special class, hence, looking at
equation

54
, we obtain more generally

\mathcal{C}^{-1}\text{x}^{n}=\sum_{k=\left\lceil n/2\right\rceil}^{n}\binom{k}{n-k}\frac{n!}{k!}(-1)^{n-k}\text{x}^{k}(1-4D)^{k-n/2}\mathcal{C}^{-1}.

(60)

The sigma operator for
\Lambda
can be expressed with integration thanks to
Proposition

5.6
(c)

\Lambda^{-1}=D^{-1}\frac{D}{\Lambda}=\mathcal{I}\frac{1}{1-D},

(61)

and when applied to polynomials, this has a double integral representation

\Lambda^{-1}f(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}f(z+t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z,

(62)

because of the known formula

\frac{1}{1-D}=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}E^{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

(63)

6.2
Abel polynomials and idempotent numbers

The Abel operator is defined for some fixed constant
a
by
A\leadsto\mathcal{D}\coloneqq DE^{a}
. Once again, the formula of the shifted derivative suggests the use of the Steffensen formula

\displaystyle A_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\text{x}(D/\mathcal{D})^{n}x^{n-1}=\text{x}E^{-an}x^{n-1}

\displaystyle=x(x-an)^{n-1}.

(64)

The Abel polynomials are basic; thus, they are also of binomial type (
Theorem

3.5
) and
Abel’s identity
is immediate

(x+y)(x+y-an)^{n-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}x(x-ak)^{k-1}y(y-a(n-k))^{n-k-1}.

(65)

By
Theorem

5.5
, we can recover the closed form for the Abel umbral operator

A=\mathcal{D}^{\prime}E^{-a}(E^{-a})^{\text{x}D}=(1+aD)(E^{-a})^{\text{x}D}.

(66)

From this equation, we define the
smooth Abel
operator

\mathcal{A}\coloneqq(E^{-a})^{\text{x}D}=(1+aD)^{-1}A.

(67)

Proposition

3.5
assures us that
\mathcal{A}
is a Sheffer operator for
\mathcal{D}
. It is given by

\mathcal{A}_{n}(x)=(E^{-a})^{\text{x}D}x^{n}=(x-an)^{n}.

(68)

Thanks to
Theorem

3.5
, we can express the “smoother” Abel identity

(x+y-an)^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}x(x-ak)^{k-1}(y-a(n-k))^{n-k}.

(69)

The sigma operator can also be expressed with integration after using
Proposition

5.6
(c)

\mathcal{D}^{-1}=\mathcal{I}E^{-a}.

(70)

The Niederhausen transform (
Theorem

4.4
) allows a very quick computation of the inverse of
A
. Indeed,
\binom{n}{k}\mathcal{S}^{\left(a\right)}_{n-k}(k)=\binom{n}{k}(ak)^{n-k}
is the coefficients of a basic set, whose inverse is basic for
De^{aD}=\mathcal{D}
. So we precisely obtain

A^{-1}_{n}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}(ak)^{n-k}x^{k},

(71)

and

\mathcal{D}^{[-1]}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-an)^{n-1}}{n!}D^{n}.

(72)

For
a=1
, the coefficients
\binom{n}{k}k^{n-k}
that appear are known as
idempotent numbers
, and the power series for
\mathcal{D}^{[-1]}
corresponds to the main branch of the
Lambert W function
.

6.3
Divided difference, rising factorials and Touchard polynomials

The
divided difference
operator is
\Delta_{h}=(E^{h}-1)/h
. It generalizes
\Delta=\Delta_{1}
. We can extend the definition to
h=0
by
\Delta_{0}\coloneqq\lim_{h\to 0}\Delta_{h}
, which, by the definition of the derivative, equals
D
. We can reduce the study of its basic sets by noticing that
\Delta_{h}=(D/h)\diamond\Delta\diamond(hD)
. Thus, by
Theorem

3.3
, we deduce this formula for
\phi_{h}\leadsto\Delta_{h}

\phi_{h}=\mathcal{S}^{\left(1/h\right)}\phi\mathcal{S}^{\left(h\right)}.

(73)

Therefore

\displaystyle\phi_{h,n}(x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq(\phi_{h})_{n}(x)=\mathcal{S}^{\left(1/h\right)}\phi\mathcal{S}^{\left(h\right)}x^{n}

\displaystyle=h^{n}(x/h)_{n}=x(x-h)\cdots(x-(n-1)h).

(74)

\phi_{h}
is part of the special class of umbral operators of
Theorem

5.3
, and we can work out the identity

\phi_{h}\text{x}^{n}=h^{n}(\text{x}/h)_{n}E^{-hn}\phi_{h}.

(75)

This can also be proved by the repeated application of
Corollary

3.4
.

The other important instance of
\phi_{h}
is the case
h=-1
. Indeed, the
backward difference

\nabla\coloneqq\Delta_{-1}=1-E^{-1}
is the delta operator for the
rising factorials

\{\left\langle x\right\rangle_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. If we denote the umbral operator with
\rho\coloneqq\phi_{-1}
, then
\rho_{n}(x)=\left\langle x\right\rangle_{n}\coloneqq x(x+1)\cdots(x+n-1)
, or more generally

\rho\text{x}^{n}=\left\langle\text{x}\right\rangle_{n}E^{n}\rho.

(76)

Just as we have the formula for the unsigned Stirling number of the second kind,
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi^{-1}}
, there is a corresponding formula for the first kind,
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\rho}
.

6.3.1
Bernoulli polynomials of the second kind

A known Sheffer set for
\Delta
is the
Bernoulli polynomials of the second kind
. They can be defined with

\psi\coloneqq\mathcal{B}^{-1}\phi.

(77)

They are a Sheffer set for
\Delta
based on
Proposition

2.8
. We can use
Example

5.4
to obtain a closed form formula

\psi_{n}(x)=\int_{x}^{x+1}(t)_{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

(78)

Second kind Bernoulli polynomials satisfy this interesting commutation property

\phi D=D\psi.

(79)

6.3.2
Touchard polynomials

The Touchard polynomials (or
exponential polynomials
)
\{T_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are defined as

T_{n}(x)\coloneqq\phi^{-1}x^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}x^{k},

(80)

Therefore,
T=\phi^{-1}
. Because
\Delta=e^{D}-1
, from
Theorem

3.3
, we deduce that the Touchard polynomials are a basic set of polynomials for the delta operator
\mathcal{L}\coloneqq\Delta^{[-1]}=\log(1+D)
.

Defining
Bell numbers
as follows

B_{n}\coloneqq T_{n}(1)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k},

(81)

we are able to deduce many of its properties. One notable result related to the higher-order exponentiation of
\phi
, valid for
n>0
, is

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{1}_{\phi^{-2}}=B_{n}.

(82)

It follows from
Corollary

4.1

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{1}_{\phi^{-2}}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{j}_{\phi^{-1}}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{j}{1}_{\phi^{-1}}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{j}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{j}{1},

and the equations
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{j}{1}=1
and
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{0}=\delta_{n}
. Nevertheless, the subsequent

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi^{-2}}=B_{n,k}(B_{1},\ldots,B_{n-k+1}),

(83)

following from
equation

25
, do not serve as a bivariate generalization of Bell numbers, since
equation

82
does not include the trivial case
B_{0}=1
. The formula is merely an incidental result rooted in the fact that exponentiation is a key function in umbral calculus.

The recurrence relation falls off
Theorem

3.4
and
Corollary

3.4
. Indeed,
(\mathcal{L}^{[-1]})^{\prime}=E
, so

T_{n+1}(x)=\text{x}\phi^{-1}Ex^{n}=x\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}T_{k}(x).

(84)

This operationally proves the equivalent umbral identity of
[
30
, p. 60]
. By evaluating at
x=1
, we obtain the well-known recurrence

B_{n+1}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}B_{k}.

(85)

The formula may be extended further by initially generalizing the operator formula, in a manner comparable to
Example

3.5
.

Proposition 6.1

(Spivey identity)
.

\phi^{-1}\text{x}^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}\text{x}^{k}\phi^{-1}E^{k}.

Proof
.

Use
Example

3.5
in

\sum_{k=0}^{n}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi}\phi^{-1}\text{x}^{k}=\phi^{-1}(\text{x})_{n}=\text{x}^{n}\phi^{-1}E^{n},

to recover the desired result after applying the first inversion formula (
Theorem

4.1
).

Thus,
\phi^{-1}
is certainly part of the special class, so the identity is alternatively proved with
Theorem

5.3
and the identity
(1+D)\phi^{-1}=\phi^{-1}E
.

This proposition straightforwardly implies Spivey’s identity
[
34
]
and its generalization
[
14
]
.

Corollary 6.1

(Generalized Spivey identity)
.

T_{n+m}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}x^{k}\sum_{j=0}^{m}\binom{m}{j}k^{m-j}T_{j}(x).

Proof
.

Applying
Proposition

6.1
to
x^{m}

T_{n+m}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}\text{x}^{k}\phi^{-1}(x+k)^{m}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}x^{k}\sum_{j=0}^{m}\binom{m}{j}k^{m-j}T_{j}(x).

At
x=1
, we recover Spivey’s original identity on Bell numbers

B_{n+m}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}\sum_{j=0}^{m}\binom{m}{j}k^{m-j}B_{j}.

(86)

6.3.3
Dobiński’s formula

Recall the identity of
equation

35

\text{x}^{n}D^{n}=(\text{x}D)_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi}(\text{x}D)^{k}
. Therefore, by the first inversion formula (
Theorem

4.1
)

(\text{x}D)^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}\text{x}^{k}D^{k}.

(87)

Proposition 6.2

(Dobiński’s formula)
.

T_{n}(x)=e^{-x}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{k^{n}x^{k}}{k!}.

Proof
.

On the on hand

(\text{x}D)^{n}e^{x}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(\text{x}D)^{n}\frac{x^{k}}{k!}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{k^{n}x^{k}}{k!},

and on the other hand, we get by
equation

87

(\text{x}D)^{n}e^{x}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}\text{x}^{k}D^{k}e^{x}=T_{n}(x)e^{x},

whence the result.

Looking at Dobiński’s formula, it is clear that if we define the
generating function
linear operator by

\mathcal{G}f(x)\coloneqq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{f(k)}{k!}x^{k},

(88)

then this identity follows.

Corollary 6.2

(Dobiński identity)
.

\phi^{-1}=e^{-\text{x}}\mathcal{G}.

A first consequence of this is a surprising identity for the integer evaluation of
\phi
.

Corollary 6.3
.

If
f
is a polynomial

\phi(f)(k)=\mathcal{E}D^{k}e^{x}f(x).

Proof
.

The formula is obtained by rewriting the previous corollary (
Corollary

6.2
) as
\mathcal{G}\phi=e^{\text{x}}
and applying it to
f

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\phi(f)(k)}{k!}x^{k}=e^{x}f(x),

from which we identify the coefficients.

Furthermore, the Dobiński identity (
Corollary

6.2
) allows us to find an elegant integral formula for
\rho
.

Proposition 6.3
.

for a polynomial
f
and for
x>-\deg f

\rho f(x)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(x)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{x-1}e^{-t}f(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

Proof
.

Using
equation

73
with
h=-1
and
Corollary

6.2
, we see that

\mathcal{N}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{N}\rho=\mathcal{N}\mathcal{G}\phi\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{N}e^{\text{x}}\mathcal{N}=e^{-\text{x}}

and applying this to
f
yields

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\rho(f)(-k)}{k!}(-x)^{k}=e^{-x}f(x).

Now we can apply Ramanujan’s Master theorem
[
1
]
and obtain

\rho(f)(x)\Gamma(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{x-1}e^{-t}f(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

The condition of convergence on
x
is based on the convergence domain of the integral representation of the
\Gamma
function (which is for
x>0
).

6.4
Laguerre polynomials and its new generalization

6.4.1
Degenerate associated Laguerre polynomials

If we consider the simplest families of umbral operators in the Écalle form, that is,
L_{p}\coloneqq e^{-\text{x}D^{p+1}}
for
p
a positive integer, then by
Corollary

5.1
, we can show that they are basic for
\Psi_{p}\coloneqq D/\sqrt[p]{1-pD^{p}}
, when
p>0
and
\Psi_{0}\coloneqq eD
(where
e
is Euler’s number) when
p=0
. We denote the basic set by
\{L_{p,n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
(with
L_{p,n}(x)\coloneqq(L_{p})_{n}(x)
) and introduce the cross-sequence
\{L^{\left(\alpha\right)}_{p,n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
defined by
L^{\left(\alpha\right)}_{p}\coloneqq(1-pD^{p})^{\alpha}L_{p}
and call them
degenerate associated Laguerre polynomials
as they generalize
associated
(or
generalized
)
Laguerre polynomials
, which we can retrieve with the case
p=1
(see next section). Immediately from
Proposition

3.6
, we recover

L^{\left(\alpha+\beta\right)}_{p,n}(x+y)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}L^{\left(\alpha\right)}_{p,k}(x)L^{\left(\beta\right)}_{p,n-k}(y).

(89)

In the following, we first compute
L^{\left(\alpha\right)}_{p,n}(x)
for
p\geq 1
(as the case
p=0
is a completely known special case) and then deduce
L_{p}=L^{\left(0\right)}_{p}
.
\Psi_{p}^{\prime}=1/(1-pD^{p})^{1/p+1}
, thus, with the transfer formula (
Theorem

3.4
) we have
L_{p,n}(x)=\Psi_{p}^{\prime}(\sqrt[p]{1-pD^{p}})^{n+1}x^{n}=(1-pD^{p})^{n/p-1}x^{n}
. Therefore

\displaystyle L^{\left(\alpha\right)}_{p,n}(x)

\displaystyle=(1-pD^{p})^{n/p+\alpha-1}x^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom{\frac{n}{p}+\alpha-1}{k}(-pD^{p})^{k}x^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor n/p\right\rfloor}\binom{\frac{n}{p}+\alpha-1}{k}\frac{n!}{(n-pk)!}(-p)^{k}x^{n-pk}.

(90)

At indices multiples of
p
, we get the nicer formula

L^{\left(\alpha\right)}_{p,pn}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n+\alpha-1}{n-k}\frac{(pn)!}{(pk)!}(-p)^{n-k}x^{pk}.

(91)

It is also possible to find another closed form for
L^{\left(\alpha\right)}_{p}
.

Proposition 6.4
.

For
p\in\mathbb{N}

L_{p}^{(\alpha)}=e^{-\text{x}D^{p+1}-\alpha pD^{p}}.

Proof
.

Because
[D^{p},\text{x}D^{p+1}]=p(D^{p})^{2}
, by the Berry identity
[
4
, Corollary 3.4]
and
[
4
, Proposition 2.4]
,

e^{-\text{x}D^{p+1}-\alpha pD^{p}}=((1-pD^{p})^{1/p})^{p\alpha}e^{-\text{x}D^{p+1}}=L^{\left(\alpha\right)}_{p}.

The differential equation characterizing the degenerate associated Laguerre polynomials can be found with
Corollary

3.5
and the Pincherle derivative

\displaystyle(1-pD^{p})^{\alpha}\text{x}

\displaystyle=\text{x}(1-pD^{p})^{\alpha}+((1-pD^{p})^{\alpha})^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\text{x}(1-pD^{p})^{\alpha}-\alpha p^{2}D^{p-1}(1-pD^{p})^{\alpha-1}

\displaystyle=(\text{x}(1-pD^{p})-\alpha p^{2}D^{p-1})(1-pD^{p})^{\alpha-1}.

therefore

\displaystyle nL^{\left(\alpha\right)}_{p,n}(x)

\displaystyle=(1-pD^{p})^{\alpha}\text{x}\frac{\Psi_{p}}{\Psi_{p}^{\prime}}L_{p,n}(x)

\displaystyle=(\text{x}(1-pD^{p})-\alpha p^{2}D^{p-1})(1-pD^{p})^{\alpha-1}D(1-pD^{p})L_{p,n}(x)

\displaystyle=(\text{x}(1-pD^{p})-\alpha p^{2}D^{p-1})DL^{\left(\alpha\right)}_{p,n}(x),

in other words,
\{L^{\left(\alpha\right)}_{p,n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are solutions to the differential equation

xpf^{(p+1)}+\alpha p^{2}f^{(p)}-xf^{\prime}+nf=0.

(92)

This generalizes to the associated version what we would have obtained with
corollary

3.5
.

6.4.2

The associated Laguerre polynomials are retrieved through

and with

where

Proposition 6.5

L\text{x}^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}(-1)^{n-k}\text{x}^{k}L^{\left(k+n\right)}.

Proposition 6.6

(Erdelyi’s duplication formula)
.

L_{n}(\lambda x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}\lambda^{k}(\lambda-1)^{n-k}L_{k}(x).

This equality can be proven by two methods, as described in

Proof
.

We compute the coefficient of

\displaystyle\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{X^{(\lambda)}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=k}^{n}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{j}{k}_{L^{-1}}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{j}_{\mathcal{S}^{\left(\lambda\right)}L}=\sum_{j=k}^{n}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{j}{k}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{j}(-1)^{n-j}\lambda^{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{n!}{k!}\sum_{j=k}^{n}\binom{j-1}{k-1}\binom{n-1}{j-1}(-1)^{n-j}\lambda^{j}=\frac{n!}{k!}\binom{n-1}{k-1}\sum_{j=k}^{n}\binom{n-k}{j-k}(-1)^{n-j}\lambda^{j}

\displaystyle=\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}\sum_{j=0}^{n-k}\binom{n-k}{j}(-1)^{n-j-k}\lambda^{j+k}=\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}\lambda^{k}(\lambda-1)^{n-k}.
