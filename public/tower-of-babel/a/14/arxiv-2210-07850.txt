Early stopping for L 2 L^{2} -boosting in high-dimensional linear models
Bernhard Stankewitz

Abstract
Increasingly high-dimensional data sets require that estimation methods do not only satisfy statistical guarantees but also remain computationally feasible. In this context, we consider L 2 L^{2} -boosting via orthogonal matching pursuit in a high-dimensional linear model and analyze a data-driven early stopping time τ \tau of the algorithm, which is sequential in the sense that its computation is based on the first τ \tau iterations only. This approach is much less costly than established model selection criteria, that require the computation of the full boosting path. We prove that sequential early stopping preserves statistical optimality in this setting in terms of a fully general oracle inequality for the empirical risk and recently established optimal convergence rates for the population risk. Finally, an extensive simulation study shows that at an immensely reduced computational cost, the performance of these type of methods is on par with other state of the art algorithms such as the cross-validated Lasso or model selection via a high dimensional Akaike criterion based on the full boosting path.

Iterative estimation procedures typically have to be combined with a data-driven

1.1
A general oracle inequality for the empirical risk

In order to state results for sequential early stopping of OMP in model
(
1.1
), as minimal assumptions, we require that the rows

(X_{i})_{i\leq n}
of the design matrix
\mathbf{X}=(X_{i}^{(j)})_{i\leq n,j\leq p}
are independently and identically distributed such that
\mathbf{X}

has full rank
n
almost surely.
We also require that the noise terms
(\varepsilon_{i})_{i\leq n}
are
independently and identically distributed and assume that, conditional on the
design, a joint subgaussian parameter for the noise terms exists.

(A1)

(SubGE)
:

Conditional on the design, the noise terms are centered subgaussians with a
joint parameter
\overline{\sigma}^{2}>0
, i.e., for all

i\leq n
and
u\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(e^{u\varepsilon_{i}}|X_{i})

\displaystyle\leq e^{\frac{u^{2}\overline{\sigma}^{2}}{2}}\qquad\text{almost surely.}

Complementary to
\overline{\sigma}^{2}
, we set
\underline{\sigma}^{2}:=\text{Var}(\varepsilon_{1})
.

By conditioning, we have
\underline{\sigma}^{2}=\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{E}(\varepsilon_{1}^{2}|X_{1}))\leq\overline{\sigma}^{2}
.
Assumption

(SubGE)

permits heteroscedastic error terms
(\varepsilon_{i})_{i\leq n}
,
allowing us to treat both regression and classification.

Example 1.1
.

(a)

(Gaussian Regression):

For
\varepsilon_{1},\dots\varepsilon_{n}\sim N(0,\sigma^{2})
i.i.d., we have
\underline{\sigma}^{2}=\sigma^{2}=\overline{\sigma}^{2}
.

(b)

(Classification):

For classification, we consider i.i.d. observations

\displaystyle Y_{i}\sim\text{Ber}(f^{*}(X_{i})),\qquad i=1,\dots,n.

(1.3)

Then, the noise terms are given by
\varepsilon_{i}=Y_{i}-f^{*}(X_{i})
with

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\varepsilon_{i}|X_{i})

\displaystyle=f^{*}(X_{i})(1-f^{*}(X_{i}))+(1-f^{*}(X_{i}))(-f^{*}(X_{i}))=0.

(1.4)

Conditional on the design, the noise is bounded by one.
This implies that
\overline{\sigma}^{2}\leq 1
.

For the asymptotic analysis, we assume that the observations stem from
a sequence of models of the form (
1.1
), where

p=p^{(n)}\to\infty
and
\log(p^{(n)})/n\to 0
for

n\to\infty
.
We allow the quantities
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{X}^{(n)},\beta^{*}=(\beta^{*})^{(n)}\text{ and }\varepsilon=\varepsilon^{(n)}
to vary in
n
.
For notational convenience, we keep this dependence implicit.

In this setting,
L^{2}
-boosting based on OMP is used to estimate

f^{*}
and perform variable selection at the same time.
Empirical correlations between data vectors are measured via the
empirical
inner product

\langle a,b\rangle_{n}:=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}b_{i}
with norm
\|a\|_{n}:=\langle a,a\rangle_{n}^{1/2},
for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
By
\widehat{\Pi}_{J}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n},
we denote the orthogonal projection with respect to

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{n}
onto the span of the columns

\{X^{(j)}:j\in J\}
of the design matrix.
OMP is initialized at
\widehat{F}^{(0)}:=0
and then iteratively
selects the covariates
X^{(j)},j\leq p
, which maximize the empirical
correlation with the residuals
Y-\widehat{F}^{(m)}
at the current
iteration
m
.
The estimator is updated by projecting onto the subspace spanned by the selected
covariates.
Explicitly, the procedure is given by the following algorithm:

Algorithm 1

Orthogonal matching pursuit (OMP)

1:

\widehat{F}^{(0)}\leftarrow 0,\widehat{J}_{0}\leftarrow\emptyset

2:

for

m=0,1,2,\dots

do

3:

\widehat{j}_{m+1}\leftarrow\argmax_{j\leq p}\Big|\Big\langle Y-\widehat{F}^{(m)},\frac{X^{(j)}}{\|X^{(j)}\|_{n}}\Big\rangle_{n}\Big|

4:

\widehat{J}_{m+1}\leftarrow\widehat{J}_{m}\cup\big\{\widehat{j}_{m+1}\big\}

5:

\widehat{F}^{(m+1)}\leftarrow\widehat{\Pi}_{\widehat{J}_{m+1}}Y

6:

end

for

Maximizing the empirical correlation between the residuals

Y-\widehat{F}^{(m)}
and
X^{(\widehat{j}_{m+1})}
at
iteration
m
is equivalent to minimizing
\|Y-\widehat{F}^{(m+1)}\|_{n}^{2}
, i.e., OMP performs
greedy optimization
for the residual norm.
It is therefore natural to stop this procedure at
\tau
from Equation
(
1.2
) when the residual norm reaches a
critical value.

From a statistical perspective, we are interested in the risk of the estimators

\widehat{F}^{(m)},m\geq 0
.
Initially, we consider the
empirical risk

\displaystyle\|\widehat{F}^{(m)}-f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|(I-\widehat{\Pi}_{m})f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}+\|\widehat{\Pi}_{m}\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}=b_{m}^{2}+s_{m},

(1.5)

where we introduce the notation
\widehat{\Pi}_{m}:=\widehat{\Pi}_{\widehat{J}_{m}},b_{m}^{2}:=\|(I-\widehat{\Pi}_{m})f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}
for the squared
empirical bias
and
s_{m}:=\|\widehat{\Pi}_{m}\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}
for the
empirical stochastic error
.
1
1

1

In the term
b_{m}^{2}
, we use the standard overloading of notation,
letting
f^{*}
denote
(f^{*}(X_{i}))_{i\leq n}
,
see Section
1.3
.

Note that at this point, we cannot simply take expectations, due to the
non-linear, stochastic choice of
\widehat{J}_{m}
.
The definition of the orthogonal projections

(\widehat{\Pi}_{m})_{m\geq 0}
with respect to

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{n}
guarantees that the mappings

m\mapsto b_{m}^{2}
and
m\mapsto s_{m}
are monotonously decreasing
and increasing, respectively.

This reveals the fundamental problem of selecting an iteration of the procedure
in Algorithm
1
.
We need to iterate far enough to sufficiently reduce the bias, yet not too far
as to blow up the stochastic error.
For
m\geq n
, we have
b_{m}^{2}=0\text{ but also }s_{m}=\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}
, which converges to
\underline{\sigma}^{2}
by the law of large
numbers.
In particular, this means iterating Algorithm
1
indefinitely will
not produce a consistent estimator of the unknown signal
f^{*}
.
Since the decay of the bias depends on
f^{*}
, no a priori, i.e., data
independent, choice of the iteration will perform well in terms of the risk
uniformly over different realizations of
f^{*}
.
Therefore, Algorithm
1
needs to be combined with a data-driven
choice
\widehat{m}
of the effectively selected iteration, which is

adaptive
.
This means either, without prior knowledge of
f^{*}
, the choice

\widehat{m}
satisfies an
oracle inequality
relating its performance
to that of the ideal oracle iteration

\displaystyle m^{\mathfrak{o}}=m^{\mathfrak{o}}(f^{*}):=\argmin_{m\geq 0}\|\widehat{F}^{(m)}-f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}

(1.6)

or, in terms of convergence rates for the risk,
\widehat{m}
performs
optimally for multiple classes of signals without prior knowledge of the class
to which the true signal
f^{*}
belongs.

Our analysis in Section
2
shows that in order to derive
such an adaptation result for the sequential early stopping time
\tau
in
Equation (
1.2
), ideally, the critical value

\kappa
should be chosen depending on the iteration as

\displaystyle\kappa=\kappa_{m}=\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}+\frac{C_{\tau}m\log p}{n},\qquad m\geq 0,

(1.7)

where
C_{\tau}\geq 0
is a non-negative constant.
Since the
empirical noise level

\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}
is
unknown, it has to be replaced by an estimator
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}
and
we redefine

\displaystyle\tau:=\inf\{m\geq 0:r_{m}^{2}\leq\kappa_{m}\}\qquad\text{ with }\qquad\kappa_{m}:=\widehat{\sigma}^{2}+\frac{C_{\tau}m\log p}{n},\qquad m\geq 0.

(1.8)

Our first main result is an oracle inequality for the stopped empirical risk at

\tau
.

Theorem 1.2

(Oracle inequality for the empirical risk)
.

Under Assumption

(SubGE)
,
the empirical risk at the stopping time
\tau
in Equation
(
1.8
) with

C_{\tau}\geq 8\overline{\sigma}^{2}

satisfies

\displaystyle\|\widehat{F}^{(\tau)}-f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\min_{m\geq 0}\Big(7\|\widehat{F}^{(m)}-f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}+\frac{(8\overline{\sigma}^{2}+C_{\tau})m\log p}{n}\Big)+|\widehat{\sigma}^{2}-\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}|

\displaystyle\leq 7\|\widehat{F}^{(m^{\mathfrak{o}})}-f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}+\frac{(8\overline{\sigma}^{2}+C_{\tau})m^{\mathfrak{o}}\log p}{n}+|\widehat{\sigma}^{2}-\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}|

with probability converging to one.

The oracle inequality is completely general in the sense that no
assumption on
f^{*}
is required.
In particular, the result also holds for non-sparse
f^{*}
.
The first term on the right-hand side involving the iteration
m^{\mathfrak{o}}
from Equation (
1.6
) is of optimal
order and the second term matches the upper bound for the empirical stochastic
error at iteration
m^{\mathfrak{o}}
we derive in Lemma

2.5
.
The last term is the absolute estimation error of
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}

for the empirical noise level.
The result is closely related to Theorem 3.3 in Blanchard et al.

[
3
]
.
Whereas they state their oracle inequality in expectation, ours is formulated
\omega
-pointwise on the underlying probability space, which is slightly
stronger.
In particular, this leads to the term
|\widehat{\sigma}^{2}-\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}|

in the inequality, which will be essential for the noise estimation problem,
see Section
4
.

1.2
Optimal adaptation for the population risk

The
population
counterpart of the empirical inner product is
\langle f,g\rangle_{L^{2}}:=\mathbb{E}(f(X_{1})g(X_{1}))
with norm
\|f\|_{L^{2}}:=\langle f,f\rangle_{L^{2}}^{1/2}
for functions
f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{P}^{X_{1}}),
where
\mathbb{P}^{X_{1}}
denotes the distribution of one observation
of the covariates.
Identifying
\widehat{F}^{(m)}
with its corresponding function in the
covariates, the
population risk
of the estimators is given by
\|\widehat{F}^{(m)}-f^{*}\|_{L^{2}}^{2},m\geq 0
.
Assuming that all of the covariates are square-integrable, for
J\subset\{1,\dots,p\}
, let
\Pi_{J}:L^{2}(\mathbb{P}^{X_{1}})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{P}^{X_{1}})
denote the orthogonal projection with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{L^{2}}
onto the span of the covariates
\{X_{1}^{(j)}:j\in J\}
.
Setting
\Pi_{m}:=\Pi_{\widehat{J}_{m}}
, the population risk decomposes into

\displaystyle\|\widehat{F}^{(m)}-f^{*}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|(I-\Pi_{m})f^{*}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\widehat{F}^{(m)}-\Pi_{m}f^{*}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=B_{m}^{2}+S_{m},

(1.9)

where
B_{m}^{2}:a=\|(I-\Pi_{m})f^{*}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
is the squared
population bias
and
S_{m}:=\|\widehat{F}^{(m)}-\Pi_{m}f^{*}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
is the
population stochastic error
.

Note that
B_{m}^{2}
and
S_{m}
are not the exact population
counterparts of the empirical quantities
b_{m}^{2}
and
s_{m}
, since
we have to account for the difference between
\widehat{\Pi}_{m}
and
\Pi_{m}
.
The challenge of selecting the iteration in Algorithm
1
discussed in
the previous section is the same for the population risk.
The mapping
m\mapsto B_{m}^{2}
is monotonously decreasing,
S_{0}=0
and
S_{m}
approaches
\text{Var}(\varepsilon_{1})
for
m\to\infty
assuming that the difference between
\widehat{\Pi}_{m}
and
\Pi_{m}
becomes negligible.
Due to this difference, however, the mapping
m\mapsto S_{m}
is no longer
guaranteed to be monotonous.
Both
B_{m}
and
S_{m}
are still random quantities due to the
randomness of
\widehat{J}_{m}
.

In order to derive guarantees for the population risk, additional assumptions
are required.
We quantify the sparsity of the coefficients
\beta^{*}
of
f^{*}
:

(A2)

(Sparse)
:

We assume one of the two following assumptions holds.

(i)

\beta^{*}
is
s
-sparse for some
s\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
i.e.,
\|\beta^{*}\|_{0}\leq s
, where
\|\beta^{*}\|_{0}
is the cardinality of the support
S:=\{j\leq p:|\beta^{*}_{j}|\neq 0\}
.
Additionally, we require that

\displaystyle s\|\beta^{*}\|_{1}^{2}=s\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{p}|\beta^{*}_{j}|\Big)^{2}=o\Big(\frac{n}{\log p}\Big),\quad\|f^{*}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq C_{f^{*}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\min_{j\in S}|\beta^{*}_{j}|\geq\underline{\beta},

where
C_{f^{*}},\underline{\beta}>0
are numerical
constants.

(ii)

\beta^{*}
is
\gamma
-sparse for some
\gamma\in[1,\infty)
, i.e.,
\|\beta^{*}\|_{2}\leq C_{\ell^{2}}
and

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in J}|\beta_{j}^{*}|

\displaystyle\leq C_{\gamma}\Big(\sum_{j\in J}|\beta_{j}^{*}|^{2}\Big)^{\frac{\gamma-1}{2\gamma-1}}\qquad\text{ for all }J\subset\{1,\dots,p\},

where
C_{\ell^{2}},C_{\gamma}>0
are numerical constants.

Assumptions like
(Sparse)
(i) are standard in the literature on high
dimensional models, see e.g., Bühlmann and van de Geer

[
7
]
.
Note that the conditions in (i) imply that
s=o((n/\log p)^{1/3})
.

(Sparse)
(ii) encodes
a decay of the coefficients
\beta^{*}
.
It includes several well known settings as special cases.

Example 1.3
.

(a)

(
\ell^{1/\gamma}
-boundedness):

For
\gamma\in[1,\infty)
and some
C_{\ell^{1/\gamma}}>0
, let the coefficients satisfy
\sum_{j=1}^{p}|\beta^{*}_{j}|^{1/\gamma}\leq C_{\ell^{1/\gamma}}
.
Then, Hoelder’s inequality yields

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in J}|\beta^{*}_{j}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in J}|\beta^{*}_{j}|^{\frac{1}{2\gamma-1}}|\beta^{*}_{j}|^{\frac{2\gamma-2}{2\gamma-1}}\leq\Big(\sum_{j\in J}|\beta^{*}_{j}|^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}\Big)^{\frac{\gamma}{2\gamma-1}}\Big(\sum_{j\in J}|\beta^{*}_{j}|^{2}\Big)^{\frac{\gamma-1}{2\gamma-1}}

(1.10)

\displaystyle\leq(C_{\ell^{1/\gamma}})^{\frac{\gamma}{2\gamma-1}}\Big(\sum_{j\in J}|\beta^{*}_{j}|^{2}\Big)^{\frac{\gamma-1}{2\gamma-1}}\qquad\text{ for all }J\subset\{1,\dots,p\},

i.e., Assumption

(Sparse)
(ii)
is satisfied with
C_{\gamma}=(C_{\ell^{1/\gamma}})^{\frac{\gamma}{2\gamma-1}}
.
For
\gamma\to\infty
, this approaches the setting in (i), in which
the support
S
is finite.

(b)

(Polynomial decay):

For
\gamma\in(1,\infty)
and
C_{\gamma}^{\prime}\geq c_{\gamma}^{\prime}>0
, let the coefficients satisfy

\displaystyle c_{\gamma}^{\prime}j^{-\gamma}\leq|\beta^{*}_{(j)}|\leq C_{\gamma}^{\prime}j^{-\gamma}\qquad\qquad\text{ for all }j\leq p,

(1.11)

where
(\beta^{*}_{(j)})_{j\leq p}
is a reordering of the
(\beta^{*}_{j})_{j\leq p}
with decreasing absolute values.
Then, Assumption

(Sparse)
(ii)
is satisfied with
C_{\gamma}
proportional to
C_{\gamma}^{\prime}(c_{\gamma}^{\prime})^{-(2\gamma-2)/(2\gamma-1)},
see Lemma A1.2 of Ing
[
12
]
.

For the covariance structure of the design, we assume subgaussianity and some
additional boundedness conditions.

(A3)

(SubGD)
:

The design variables are centered subgaussians in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
with
unit variance, i.e., there exists some
\rho>0
such that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\text{ with }\|x\|=1,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}e^{u\langle x,X_{1}\rangle}

\displaystyle\leq e^{\frac{u^{2}\rho^{2}}{2}},\quad u\in\mathbb{R}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\text{Var}(X_{1}^{(j)})=1\quad\text{ for all }j\leq p.

Remark 1.4

(Inclusion of an Intercept)
.

Assumption

(SubGD)

still allows to include an intercept additional to the design variables.
If the intercept is selected, we have just applied Algorithm

to
the data

centered at their empirical mean for which

(SubGD)

is satisfied up to a negligible term.
If it is not selected, the result is identical to applying the Algorithm

(A4)

(CovB)

The complete covariance matrix

1.3
Further notation

We overload both the notation of the empirical and the population inner products
with functions and vectors respectively, i.e., for
f,g:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R},
we set

\displaystyle\|f\|_{n}^{2}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(X_{i})^{2}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\langle f,g\rangle_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(X_{i})g(X_{i})

(1.22)

and also, e.g.,

\displaystyle\langle\varepsilon,f\rangle_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}f(X_{i})\qquad\text{ or }\qquad\|Y\|_{L^{2}}^{2}:=\mathbb{E}Y_{1}^{2}.

(1.23)

Further, as in Bühlmann
[
6
]
, for

j\leq p
, we denote the
j
-th coordinate projection as
g_{j}(x):=x^{(j)},x\in\mathbb{R}^{p},
and vectors of dot products via

\displaystyle\langle\cdot,g_{J}\rangle_{n}:=(\langle\cdot,g_{j}\rangle_{n})_{j\in J}\in\mathbb{R}^{|J|}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\langle\cdot,g_{J}\rangle_{L^{2}}:=(\langle\cdot,g_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}})_{j\in J}\in\mathbb{R}^{|J|}

(1.24)

for
J\subset\{1,\dots,p\}
.
This way, Equation (
1.13
) in Assumption

(CovB)

can be restated as

\displaystyle\sup_{|J|\leq M_{n},k\not\in J}\|\Gamma_{J}^{-1}\langle g_{k},g_{J}\rangle_{L^{2}}\|_{1}\leq C_{\text{Cov}}.

(1.25)

Analogously to the population covariance matrix
\Gamma=(\langle g_{j},g_{k}\rangle_{L^{2}})_{j,k\leq p}
, we define the empirical covariance matrix
\widehat{\Gamma}:=(\langle g_{j},g_{k}\rangle_{n})_{j,k\leq p}
.
Using the same notation for partial matrices as in Assumption

(CovB)
and
X^{(J)}=(X_{i}^{(j)})_{i\leq n,j\in J}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times|J|}
, the projections
\widehat{\Pi}_{J}
and
\Pi_{J}
can be written
as

\displaystyle\widehat{\Pi}_{J}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n},\qquad\qquad\qquad\ \ \ \widehat{\Pi}_{J}y:

\displaystyle=(X^{(J)})^{\top}\widehat{\Gamma}_{J}^{-1}\langle y,g_{J}\rangle_{n},

(1.26)

\displaystyle\Pi_{J}:L^{2}(\mathbb{P}^{X_{1}})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{P}^{X_{1}}),\qquad\Pi_{J}h:

\displaystyle=g_{J}^{\top}\Gamma_{J}^{-1}\langle h,g_{J}\rangle_{L^{2}}

for
J\subset\{1,\dots,p\}
.

At points where we switch between a linear combination
f
of the columns
of the design and its coefficients, we introduce the notation
\beta(f)
.
We use this, e.g., for the coefficients of the population residual function
\beta((I-\Pi_{m})f^{*})
as in Equation (
1.16
).
For coefficients
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we also use the general set
notation
\beta_{J}:=(\beta_{j})_{j\in J}\in\mathbb{R}^{|J|}
for
J\subset\{1,\dots,p\}
.

Throughout the paper, variables
c>0
and
C>0
denote small and
large constants respectively.
They may change from line to line and can depend on constants defined in our
assumptions.
They are, however, independent of
n,\overline{\sigma}^{2}
and

\rho^{2}
.

Many statements in our results are formulated with a postpositioned “with
probability converging to one”.
This always refers to the whole statement including quantifiers.
E.g. in Lemma
2.5
, the result is to be
read as:
There exists an event with probability converging to one on which for all
iterations
m\geq 0
, the inequality
s_{m}\leq C\overline{\sigma}^{2}m\log(p)/n
is satisfied.

2
Empirical risk analysis

Since the stopping time
\tau
in Equation
(
1.2
) is defined in terms of the squared
empirical residual norm
r_{m}^{2}m\geq 0
, its functioning principles are initially best explained by analyzing the
stopped empirical risk
\|\widehat{F}^{(\tau)}-f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}
.
We begin by formulating an intuition for why
\tau
is adaptive.

2.1
An intuition for sequential early stopping

Ideally, an adaptive choice
\widehat{m}
of the iteration in Algorithm

1
would approximate the classical oracle iteration

m^{\mathfrak{o}}
from Equation (
1.6
), which
minimizes the empirical risk.
The sequential stopping time
\tau
, however, does not have a direct
connection to
m^{\mathfrak{o}}
.
In fact, its sequential definition guarantees that
\tau
does
not incorporate information about the squared bias
b_{m}^{2}

for iterations
m>\tau
.
Instead,
\tau
mimics the
balanced oracle iteration

\displaystyle m^{\mathfrak{b}}=m^{\mathfrak{b}}(f^{*}):=\inf\{m\geq 0:b_{m}^{2}\leq s_{m}\}.

(2.1)

Fortunately, the empirical risk at
m^{\mathfrak{b}}
is essentially
optimal up to a small discretization error, which opens up the possibility of
sequential adaptation in the first place.

Lemma 2.1

(Optimality of the balanced oracle)
.

The empirical risk at the balanced oracle iteration
m^{\mathfrak{b}}

satisfies

\displaystyle\|\widehat{F}^{(m^{\mathfrak{b}})}-f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\widehat{F}^{(m^{\mathfrak{o}})}-f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}+\Delta(s_{m^{\mathfrak{b}}})=2\min_{m\geq 0}\|\widehat{F}^{(m)}-f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}+\Delta(s_{m^{\mathfrak{b}}}),

where
\Delta(s_{m}):=s_{m}-s_{m-1}
is the discretization error of the empirical stochastic error at
m
.

Proof.

If
m^{\mathfrak{b}}>m^{\mathfrak{o}}
, then the definition of
m^{\mathfrak{b}}
and the monotonicity of

m\mapsto b_{m}^{2}
yield

\displaystyle\|\widehat{F}^{(m^{\mathfrak{b}})}-f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle=b_{m^{\mathfrak{b}}}^{2}+s_{m^{\mathfrak{b}}}\leq 2b_{m^{\mathfrak{b}}}^{2}+\Delta(s_{m^{\mathfrak{b}}})\leq 2b_{m^{\mathfrak{o}}}^{2}+\Delta(s_{m^{\mathfrak{b}}})

(2.2)

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\widehat{F}^{(m^{\mathfrak{o}})}-f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}+\Delta(s_{m^{\mathfrak{b}}}).

Otherwise, if
m^{\mathfrak{b}}\leq m^{\mathfrak{o}}
, then analogously, the monotonicity of
m\mapsto s_{m}
yields
\|\widehat{F}^{(m^{\mathfrak{b}})}-f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}\leq 2s_{m^{\mathfrak{b}}}\leq 2\|\widehat{F}^{(m^{\mathfrak{o}})}-f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}
.
∎

The connection between
\tau
and
m^{\mathfrak{b}}
can be seen by
decomposing the squared residual norm
r_{m}^{2}
into

\displaystyle r_{m}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|(I-\widehat{\Pi}_{m})f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}+2\langle(I-\widehat{\Pi}_{m})f^{*},\varepsilon\rangle_{n}+\|(I-\widehat{\Pi}_{m})\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}

(2.3)

\displaystyle=b_{m}^{2}+2c_{m}+\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}-s_{m}

with the
cross term

\displaystyle c_{m}:=\langle(I-\widehat{\Pi}_{m})f^{*},\varepsilon\rangle_{n},\qquad m\geq 0.

(2.4)

Indeed, Equation (
2.3
) yields that the
stopping condition
r_{m}^{2}\leq\kappa
is equivalent to

\displaystyle b_{m}^{2}+2c_{m}

\displaystyle\leq s_{m}+\kappa-\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}.

(2.5)

Assuming that
c_{m}
can be treated as a lower order term, this implies
that, up to the difference
\kappa-\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}
,

\tau
behaves like
m^{\mathfrak{b}}
.

The connection between a discrepancy-type stopping rule and a balanced oracle
was initially drawn in Blanchard et al.

[
3
,
4
]
.
Whereas their oracle quantities were defined in terms of non-random population
versions of bias and variance, ours have to be defined
\omega
-pointwise on
the underlying probability space.
This is owed to the fact that, even conditional on the design
\mathbf{X}
,
the squared bias
b_{m}^{2}
is still a random quantity due to the random
selection of
\widehat{J}_{m}
in Algorithm
1
.
This is a subtle but important distinction, which leads to a substantially
different analysis.

2.2
A general oracle inequality

In this section, we derive the first main result in Theorem

1.2
.
As in Blanchard et al.
[
3
]
, the key ingredient is that
via the squared residual norm
r_{m}^{2},m\geq 0
, the stopped estimator

\widehat{F}^{(\tau)}
can be compared with any other estimator

\widehat{F}^{(m)}
in empirical norm.
Note that the statement in Lemma
2.2
is
completely deterministic.

Lemma 2.2

(Empirical norm comparison)
.

For any
m\geq 0
,
the stopped estimator
\widehat{F}^{(\tau)}

with
\tau
from Equation (
1.2
)
satisfies

\displaystyle\|\widehat{F}^{(\tau)}-\widehat{F}^{(m)}\|_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\widehat{F}^{(m)}-f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}+2|c_{m}|

\displaystyle+(\kappa-\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2})\mathbf{1}\{\tau<m\}+(\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}+\Delta(r_{\tau}^{2})-\kappa)\mathbf{1}\{\tau>m\},

where

c_{m}
is the cross term from Equation
(
2.4
)
and

\Delta(r_{m}^{2}):=r_{m}^{2}-r_{m-1}^{2}

is the discretization error of the squared residual norm at
m
.

Proof.

Fix
m\geq 0
.
We have

\displaystyle\|\widehat{F}^{(\tau)}-\widehat{F}^{(m)}\|_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|Y-\widehat{F}^{(\tau)}+\widehat{F}^{(m)}-Y\|_{n}^{2}=r_{\tau}^{2}-2\langle(I-\widehat{\Pi}_{\tau})Y,(I-\widehat{\Pi}_{m})Y\rangle_{n}+r_{m}^{2}

\displaystyle=(r_{m}^{2}-r_{\tau}^{2})\mathbf{1}\{\tau>m\}+(r_{\tau}^{2}-r_{m}^{2})\mathbf{1}\{\tau<m\}.

(2.6)

On
\{\tau>m\}
, we use the definition of
\tau
in
Equation (
1.2
)
to estimate

\displaystyle r_{m}^{2}-r_{\tau}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq r_{m}^{2}-\kappa+\Delta(r_{\tau}^{2})=b_{m}^{2}+2c_{m}+\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}-s_{m}-\kappa+\Delta(r_{\tau}^{2})

(2.7)

\displaystyle\leq\|\widehat{F}^{(m)}-f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}+2c_{m}+\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}-\kappa+\Delta(r_{\tau}^{2}).

On
\{\tau\leq m\}
, analogously, we obtain
r_{\tau}^{2}-r_{m}^{2}\leq\|\widehat{F}^{(m)}-f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}-2c_{m}+\kappa-\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2},
which finishes the proof.
∎

In order to translate this norm comparison to an oracle inequality, it suffices
to control the cross term and the discretization error of the residual norm.
This is already possible under Assumption

(SubGE)
.
The proof of Lemma
2.3
is deferred to Appendix
B
.

Lemma 2.3

(Bounds for the cross term and the discretization error)
.

Under Assumption

(SubGE)
,
the following statements hold:

(i)

With probability converging to one, the cross term satisfies

\displaystyle|c_{m}|

\displaystyle\leq b_{m}\sqrt{\frac{4\overline{\sigma}^{2}(m+1)\log p}{n}}\qquad\text{ for all }m\geq 0.

(ii)

With probability converging to one, the discretization error
of the squared residual norm satisfies

\displaystyle\Delta(r_{m}^{2})

\displaystyle\leq 2b_{m-1}^{2}+\frac{8\overline{\sigma}^{2}m\log p}{n}\qquad\text{ for all }m\geq 1.

Together, Lemmas

and

in Equation

accounts for the discretization error of
the residuals norm.
With this choice of

2.2

yields for any fixed

\displaystyle\|\widehat{F}^{(\tau)}-f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2(\|\widehat{F}^{(\tau)}-\widehat{F}^{(m)}\|_{n}^{2}+\|\widehat{F}^{(m)}-f^{*}\|_{n}^{2})

(2.8)

\displaystyle\leq 2\big(2\|\widehat{F}^{(m)}-f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}+2|c_{m}|+(\kappa_{\tau}-\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2})\mathbf{1}\{\tau<m\}.

\displaystyle\qquad\ \ \quad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\ \ +(\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}+\Delta(r_{\tau}^{2})-\kappa_{\tau})\mathbf{1}\{\tau>m\}\big).

Under Assumption

(SubGE)
,
with probability converging to one, the estimates from Lemma

2.3
then imply that
on
\{\tau<m\}

\displaystyle\|\widehat{F}^{(\tau)}-f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 6\|\widehat{F}^{(m)}-f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}+\frac{(8\overline{\sigma}^{2}+C_{\tau})m\log p}{n}+\widehat{\sigma}^{2}-\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2},

(2.9)

using that
4(m+1)\leq 8m
.
Analogously, on
\{\tau>m\}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\widehat{F}^{(\tau)}-f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 7\|\widehat{F}^{(m)}-f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}+\frac{8\overline{\sigma}^{2}m\log p}{n}+\frac{(8\overline{\sigma}^{2}-C_{\tau})\tau\log p}{n}+\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}-\widehat{\sigma}^{2},

(2.10)

where we have used that
b_{\tau-1}^{2}\leq b_{m}^{2}
.
Combining the events and taking the infimum over
m\geq 0

yields the result in Theorem
1.2
.
We reiterate that here, it is the
\omega
-pointwise analysis that preserves the term
|\widehat{\sigma}^{2}-\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}|
in the result.

2.3
Explicit bounds for empirical quantities

In order to derive a convergence rate from Theorem

These will also be essential for the analysis of the stopped population risk.
We begin by establishing control over the most basic quantities.

Lemma 2.4

(Uniform bounds in high probability)
.

Under Assumptions

(SubGE)

and

(SubGD)
,
the following statements hold:

(i)

There exists some

\displaystyle\sup_{j,k\leq p}|\langle g_{j},g_{k}\rangle_{n}-\langle g_{j},g_{k}\rangle_{L^{2}}|\leq C_{g}\sqrt{\frac{\rho^{4}\log p}{n}}.

(ii)

There exists some

such that with probability
converging to one,

\displaystyle\sup_{j\leq p}|\langle\varepsilon,g_{j}\rangle_{n}|\leq C_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{\frac{\overline{\sigma}^{2}\log p}{n}}.

(iii)

There exists some
C_{\Gamma}>0
such that
for any fixed

\displaystyle\sup_{|J|\leq c_{\text{Cov}}n/\log p}\frac{\|\widehat{\Gamma}_{J}-\Gamma_{J}\|_{\text{op}}}{\rho^{2}}\leq c_{\text{Cov}}C_{\Gamma}

with probability converging to one.

(iv)

There exist

such that with
probability converging to one,

\displaystyle\sup_{|J|\leq c_{\text{Cov}}n/\log p}\|\widehat{\Gamma}_{J}^{-1}\|_{\text{op}}\leq C_{\Gamma^{-1}}.

Some version of this is needed in all results for

-boosting
in high-dimensional models, see Lemma 1 in Bühlmann

[

]
, Lemma A.2 in Ing and Lai

[

]
or Assumptions (A1) and (A2) in Ing

[

]
.
A proof for our setting is detailed in Appendix

.
Note that Lemma

with cardinality of order up to

from Lemma A.2 in Ing and Lai
[

]
, where

For our results, we only need that

\displaystyle s_{m}

Proof.

For

\displaystyle s_{m}

Lemma 2.7

(Bound for the empirical bias)

,

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\text{ for all }m\geq 0

Remark 2.8

(Optimality of the rates)
.

For simplicity, we consider
p=n
, a fixed, orthogonal
(with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{n}
)

\mathbf{X}
and
\varepsilon\sim N(0,\sigma^{2}I_{n})
.
Conceptually,
\rho^{2}=0
in this setting.
When
\beta^{*}
is
s
-sparse, the squared empirical bias satisfies

\displaystyle b_{m}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|(I-\widehat{\Pi}_{m})f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}\geq\|\beta(\widehat{\Pi}_{m}f^{*})-\beta^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\underline{\beta}^{2}

(2.14)

for any
m\leq s
.
Similarly, when
\beta^{*}
is
\gamma
-sparse,

\displaystyle b_{m}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|(I-\widehat{\Pi}_{m})f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}\geq\|\beta^{*}_{m\text{-term}}-\beta^{*}\|_{2}^{2},

(2.15)

where
\beta^{*}_{m\text{-term}}
is the best

\beta^{*}
with respect to the euclidean norm.
For

with polynomial decay as in Equation

, see e.g., Lemma A.3 in Ing

.

Conversely, for

guarantees that

\displaystyle s_{m}

\displaystyle=\|\widehat{\Pi}_{m}\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}Z_{(n-j+1)}^{2}\geq\frac{mZ_{(p-m+1)}^{2}}{n},

(2.16)

where

denotes the
j

,

, which are again independent, identically distributed

Noting that

.

\displaystyle\inf_{m\geq 0}\sup_{f^{*}}\|\widehat{F}^{(m)}-f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}\geq c\begin{cases}\frac{\sigma^{2}s\log p}{n},&\beta^{*}\ s\text{-sparse},\\
\Big(\frac{\sigma^{2}\log p}{n}\Big)^{1-\frac{1}{2\gamma}},&\beta^{*}\ \gamma\text{-sparse}\end{cases}

(2.17)

with probability converging to one, where the infimum is taken over
either all
f^{*}
satisfying

(Sparse)
(i)
or over all
f^{*}
satisfying

(Sparse)
(ii).

3
Population risk analysis

In this section, we analyze the stopped population risk
\|\widehat{F}^{(\tau)}-f^{*}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
with
\tau
from Equation (
1.8
).
Unlike the empirical risk, the population risk cannot be expressed in terms of
the residuals straight away.
Instead, we examine the stopping condition
r_{m}^{2}\leq\kappa_{m}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle b_{m}^{2}+2c_{m}

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{\sigma}^{2}-\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}+\frac{C_{\tau}m\log p}{n}+s_{m}.

(3.1)

We show separately that for a suitable choice of
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}
,
condition (
3.1
) guarantees that
\tau

stops neither too early nor too late.
In combination, this yields Theorem
1.8
.
For the analysis, it becomes essential that we have access to the fast norm
change for the population residual term
(I-\Pi_{m})f^{*}
from Proposition
2.6
, which guarantees that
empirical and population norm remain of the same size until the squared
population bias reaches the optimal rate
\mathcal{R}(s,\gamma)
.
This control is not already readily available by standard tools, e.g.,
Wainwright
[
26
]
.

3.1
No stopping too early

The sequential procedure stops too early if the squared population bias

B_{m}^{2}=\|(I-\Pi_{m})f^{*}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

has not reached the optimal rate of convergence yet, i.e.,

\tau<\tilde{m}_{s,\gamma,G}
, where

\displaystyle\tilde{m}_{s,\gamma,G}:=\begin{cases}\inf\{m\geq 0:S\subset\widehat{J}_{m}\},&\beta^{*}\ s\text{-sparse},\\
\inf\Big\{m\geq 0:\|(I-\Pi_{m})f^{*}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq G\Big(\frac{(\overline{\sigma}^{2}+\rho^{4})\log p}{n}\Big)^{1-\frac{1}{2\gamma}}\Big\},&\beta^{*}\ \gamma\text{-sparse}\end{cases}

(3.2)

for any constant
G>0
.
Note that under

(CovB)
,
for
s
-sparse
\beta^{*}
,

\displaystyle B_{m}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|(I-\Pi_{m})f^{*}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\begin{cases}\geq c_{\lambda}\|\tilde{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}\geq c_{\lambda}\underline{\beta}^{2},&m<\tilde{m}_{s,\gamma,G},\\
=0,&m\geq\tilde{m}_{s,\gamma,G}.\end{cases}

(3.3)

Therefore, a condition for stopping too early is given by

\displaystyle\exists m<\tilde{m}_{s,\gamma,G}:b_{m}^{2}+2c_{m}

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{\sigma}^{2}-\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}+\frac{C_{\tau}m\log p}{n}+s_{m},

(3.4)

where we may vary
G>0
.

Using the norm change inequality for the bias in Proposition

2.6
, we can derive that the left-hand side of
condition (
3.4
) is of the same order as
B_{m}^{2}
,
i.e.,

\displaystyle b_{m}^{2}+2c_{m}

\displaystyle\geq\begin{cases}\frac{c_{\lambda}}{8}\underline{\beta}^{2},&\beta^{*}\ s\text{-sparse},\\
\frac{G}{8}\Big(\frac{(\overline{\sigma}^{2}+\rho^{4})\log p}{n}\Big)^{1-\frac{1}{2\gamma}},&\beta^{*}\ \gamma\text{-sparse}\end{cases}

(3.5)

with probability converging to one.
At the same time, Proposition
1.7

guarantees that

\tilde{m}_{s,\gamma,G}\leq m^{*}_{s,\gamma}

from Equation (
1.18
) with probability converging
to one for
G
large enough.
Therefore, if
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}
does not substantially overestimate
the empirical noise level and
C_{\tau}
is chosen proportional to

(\overline{\sigma}^{2}+\rho^{4})
,
Lemma
2.5

implies that the right-hand side of condition (
3.4
)
satisfies

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}^{2}-\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}+\frac{C_{\tau}m\log p}{n}+s_{m}\leq C\mathcal{R}(s,y)

(3.6)

with probability converging to one
for a constant
C>0
independent of
G
.
For
G
large enough, condition (
3.4
) can therefore
only be satisfied on an event with probability converging to zero.
Together, this yields the following result:

Proposition 3.1

(No stopping too early)
.

Under Assumptions

(SubGE)
,

(Sparse)
,

(SubGD)

and

(CovB)
,
choose
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}
in Equation
(
1.8
) such that

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}^{2}\leq\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}+C\mathcal{R}(s,\gamma)

with probability converging to one.
Then, for
G>0
large enough and any choice

C_{\tau}=c(\overline{\sigma}^{2}+\rho^{4})

in (
1.8
)
with
c\geq 0
, the sequential stopping time satisfies

\tilde{m}_{s,\gamma,G}\leq\tau<\infty
, with probability converging to one.
On the corresponding event, it holds that

\displaystyle B_{\tau}^{2}=\|(I-\Pi_{\tau})f^{*}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\begin{cases}0,&\beta^{*}\ s\text{-sparse},\\
G\Big(\frac{(\overline{\sigma}^{2}+\rho^{4})\log p}{n}\Big)^{1-\frac{1}{2\gamma}},&\beta^{*}\ \gamma\text{-sparse}.\end{cases}

The technical details of the proof are provided in Appendix

A
.
Proposition
3.1
guarantees that
\tau

controls the population bias on an event with probability converging to one.
It is noteworthy that in order to do so, it is only required that

\widehat{\sigma}^{2}
is smaller than the empirical noise level up to
a lower order term and also the choice
C_{\tau}=0
in
Equation (
1.8
) is allowed.
We will further discuss this in Section

5
.

3.2
No stopping too late

The sequential procedure potentially stops too late when the bound in Lemma

1.6
no longer guarantees that the
population stochastic error
S_{\tau}
is of optimal order, i.e., when
there is no constant
H>0
such that
\tau
can be bounded by

Hm^{*}_{s,\gamma}
on a large event for
m^{*}_{s,\gamma}
from
Equation (
1.18
).
For stopping too late, we therefore consider the condition

r_{m}^{2}>\kappa_{m}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle b_{m}^{2}+2c_{m}>\widehat{\sigma}^{2}-\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}+\frac{C_{\tau}m\log p}{n}+s_{m}\qquad\text{ for }\qquad m=Hm^{*}_{s,\gamma}.

(3.7)

For
s
-sparse
\beta^{*}
and

, the left-hand side vanishes

.
For
\gamma
-sparse
\beta^{*}
, the results in Lemma

and Lemma

(i) yield that the

\sqrt{H}((\overline{\sigma}^{2}+\rho^{4})\log(p)/n)^{1-1/(2\gamma)}

At the same time, for
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}
large enough
and a choice
C_{\tau}=c(\overline{\sigma}^{2}+\rho^{4})
with

, the right-hand

only provides an upper bound
for

.

sufficiently large, this yields that condition

(No stopping too late)
.

Under Assumptions

,

,

(SubGD)

and

(CovB)
,

\widehat{\sigma}^{2}
in Equation

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}^{2}\geq\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}-C\mathcal{R}(s,\gamma)

\displaystyle=\|\widehat{F}^{(\tau)}-\Pi_{\tau}f^{*}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq CH\mathcal{R}(s,\gamma).

.

3.2
complements Proposition

in that it guarantees that

.

4
Estimation of the empirical noise level

For any real application, the results in Theorem

1.2
and Theorem

1.8
require access to a suitable
estimator
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}
of the empirical noise level

\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}
.
In this section, we demonstrate that under reasonable assumptions, such
estimators do in fact exist.
In particular, we analyze the
Scaled Lasso
noise estimate

\widehat{\sigma}^{2}
from Sun and Zhang
[
21
]

in our setting.
It is noteworthy that our estimation target is the empirical noise level

\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}
rather than its almost sure limit

\text{Var}(\varepsilon_{1})
.

Remark 4.1

(Estimating
\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}
vs.
\text{Var}(\varepsilon_{1})
)
.

In general, it is easier to estimate
\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}
than

\text{Var}(\varepsilon_{1})
.
We illustrate this fact in the simple location-scale model

\displaystyle Y_{i}=\mu+\varepsilon_{i},\qquad i=1,\dots,n,

(4.1)

where
\varepsilon_{i}\sim N(0,\sigma^{2})
i.i.d.
Simple calculations yield that the standard noise estimator
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}:=\|Y-\widehat{\mu}(1,\dots,1)^{\top}\|_{n}^{2}
with
\widehat{\mu}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\sigma^{2}}|\widehat{\sigma}^{2}-\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}|\leq C\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\qquad\text{ for all }\sigma^{2}>0,

(4.2)

where the subscript
\sigma^{2}
denotes the expectation with respect to

N(0,\sigma^{2})
.
Conversely, for
\text{Var}(\varepsilon_{1})=\sigma^{2}
, a
convergence rate of
n^{-1}
can only be reached for the squared risk.
Indeed, it follows from an application of van-Trees’s inequality, see Gill and
Levit
[
11
]
, that for any
\delta^{2}>0
,

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{\sigma}^{2}}\sup_{\sigma^{2}>\delta^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\sigma^{2}}(\widehat{\sigma}^{2}-\sigma^{2})^{2}

\displaystyle\geq c\frac{\delta^{4}}{n}

(4.3)

for
n\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large, where the infimum is taken over
all measurable functions
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}
.
This indicates that for the absolute risk we cannot expect a rate faster than

n^{-1/2}
.

The fact that in general,
\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}
can be
estimated with a faster rate than
\text{Var}(\varepsilon_{1})
together
with the
\omega
-pointwise analysis is essential in circumventing a lower
bound restriction as in Corollary 2.5 of Blanchard et al.

[
3
]
.

We briefly recall the approach in Sun and Zhang
[
21
]
.
The authors consider the joint minimizer
(\widehat{\beta},\widehat{\sigma})
of the
Scaled Lasso

objective

\displaystyle L_{\lambda_{0}}(\beta,\sigma):=\frac{\|Y-X\beta\|_{2}^{2}}{2n\sigma}+\frac{\sigma}{2}+\lambda_{0}\|\beta\|_{1},\qquad\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\sigma>0,

(4.4)

where
\lambda_{0}
is a penalty parameter chosen by the user.
Since
L_{\lambda_{0}}
is jointly convex in
(\beta,\sigma)
, the minimizer can be implemented efficiently.
For
\lambda>0
and
\xi>1
, they set

\displaystyle\mu(\lambda,\xi):=(\xi+1)\min_{J\subset\{1,\dots,p\}}\inf_{\nu\in(0,1)}\max\Big(\frac{\|\beta^{*}_{J^{c}}\|_{1}}{\nu},\frac{\lambda|J|/(2(1-\nu))}{\kappa^{2}((\xi+\nu)/(1-\nu),J)}\Big),

(4.5)

with the
compatibility factor

\displaystyle\kappa^{2}(\xi,J):=\min\Big\{\frac{\|X\Delta\|_{2}^{2}|J|}{n\|\Delta_{J}\|_{1}^{2}}:\|\Delta_{J^{c}}\|_{1}\leq\xi\|\Delta_{J}\|_{1}\Big\},

(4.6)

see Bühlmann and van de Geer
[
7
]
.
In Theorem 2 of
[
21
]
, Sun and Zhang then show that

\displaystyle\max\Big(1-\frac{\widehat{\sigma}}{\|\varepsilon\|_{n}},1-\frac{\|\varepsilon\|_{n}}{\widehat{\sigma}}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha^{*}:=\frac{\lambda_{0}\mu(\|\varepsilon\|_{n}\lambda_{0},\xi)}{\|\varepsilon\|_{n}}

(4.7)

on the event

\displaystyle\Omega_{\text{Noise}}:=\Big\{\sup_{j\leq p}\langle g_{j},\varepsilon\rangle_{n}\leq(1-\alpha^{*})\frac{\xi-1}{\xi+1}\|\varepsilon\|_{n}\lambda_{0}\Big\}.

\displaystyle\mu(\|\varepsilon\|_{n}\lambda_{0},\xi)

\displaystyle\leq C\begin{cases}s\sqrt{\frac{\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}\log p}{n}},&\beta^{*}\ s\text{-sparse},\\
\Big(\frac{\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}\log p}{n}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2\gamma}},&\beta^{*}\ \gamma\text{-sparse},\\
\end{cases}

(4.9)

as long as the compatibility factor from Equation

Usually this can be guaranteed on an event with high probability:
When the rows of design matrix

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\lambda_{\min}(\Gamma)}{16}\qquad\text{whenever }|J|\leq\frac{(1+\xi)^{-2}}{800}\frac{\lambda_{\min}(\Gamma)}{\max_{j\leq p}\Gamma_{jj}}\frac{n}{\log p}

(4.10)

with probability larger than

1-e^{-n/32}/(1-e^{-n/32})

The combination of these results allow to formulate Proposition

and Theorem

Proposition 4.2

(Fast noise estimation)
.

Under Assumptions

(SubGE)
,

(Sparse)

and

(CovB)
with
Gaussian design

(X_{i})_{i\leq n}\sim N(0,\Gamma)

set

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}\alpha^{*}}{(1-\alpha^{*})^{2}}\qquad\text{ with }\qquad\alpha^{*}=\frac{\lambda_{0}\mu(\|\varepsilon\|_{n}\lambda_{0},\xi)}{\|\varepsilon\|_{n}}.

\displaystyle|\widehat{\sigma}^{2}-\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}|

\displaystyle\leq C\begin{cases}\frac{\overline{\sigma}^{2}s\log p}{n},&\beta^{*}\ s\text{-sparse},\\
\Big(\frac{\overline{\sigma}^{2}\log p}{n}\Big)^{1-1/(2\gamma)},&\beta^{*}\ s\text{-sparse}.\end{cases}

and Theorem

.

from Equation (

estimator
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}
therefore provides a fully data-driven

Proof of Proposition
4.2
(Fast noise estimation).

Theorem 2 in Sun and Zhang
[
21
]
states that on the
event

\displaystyle\Omega_{\text{Lasso}}=\Big\{\sup_{j\leq p}|\langle g_{j},\varepsilon\rangle_{n}|\leq(1-\alpha^{*})\frac{\xi-1}{\xi+1}\|\varepsilon\|_{n}\lambda_{0}\Big\},

(A.16)

the Scaled Lasso noise estimator
\widehat{\sigma}
satisfies

\displaystyle\max\Big(1-\frac{\widehat{\sigma}}{\|\varepsilon\|_{n}},1-\frac{\|\varepsilon\|_{n}}{\widehat{\sigma}}\Big)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\min(\widehat{\sigma},\|\varepsilon\|_{n})}{\max(\widehat{\sigma},\|\varepsilon\|_{n})}\leq\alpha^{*}=\frac{\lambda_{0}\mu(\|\varepsilon\|_{n}\lambda_{0},\xi)}{\|\varepsilon\|_{n}}.

This implies that on

\displaystyle|\widehat{\sigma}^{2}-\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}|

\displaystyle=(\widehat{\sigma}+\|\varepsilon\|_{n})(\max(\widehat{\sigma},\|\varepsilon\|_{n})-\min(\widehat{\sigma},\|\varepsilon\|_{n}))

\displaystyle\leq\Big(1+\frac{\max(\widehat{\sigma},\|\varepsilon\|_{n})}{\min(\widehat{\sigma},\|\varepsilon\|_{n})}\Big)\max(\widehat{\sigma},\|\varepsilon\|_{n})\lambda_{0}\mu(\|\varepsilon\|_{n}\lambda_{0},\xi)

\displaystyle\leq\Big(1+\frac{1}{1-\alpha^{*}}\Big)\max(\widehat{\sigma},\|\varepsilon\|_{n})\lambda_{0}\mu(\|\varepsilon\|_{n}\lambda_{0},\xi)

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\frac{1}{1-\alpha^{*}}+\frac{1}{(1-\alpha^{*})^{2}}\Big)\|\varepsilon\|_{n}\lambda_{0}\mu(\|\varepsilon\|_{n}\lambda_{0},\xi)\leq\frac{2\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}\alpha^{*}}{(1-\alpha^{*})^{2}},

\alpha^{*}<1

since for
\alpha^{*}>1

is
empty.
It remains to be shown that Equation
(
A.18
) provides a meaningful
bound on an event with probability converging to one.

-sparse
\beta^{*}

For

\beta^{*}

Without loss of generality, we can assume that the

\displaystyle\leq|\beta^{*}_{m}|\sum_{j>m}|\beta^{*}_{j}|\leq|\beta^{*}_{m}|C_{\gamma}\Big(\sum_{j>m}|\beta^{*}_{j}|^{2}\Big)^{\frac{\gamma-1}{2\gamma-1}}.

(A.21)

Rearranging yields

,
which implies

\displaystyle\sum_{j>m+1}|\beta^{*}_{j}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j>m}|\beta^{*}_{j}|^{2}-|\beta^{*}_{m}|^{2}\leq\sum_{j>m}|\beta^{*}_{j}|^{2}\Big(1-C_{\gamma}^{-2}\Big(\sum_{j>m}|\beta^{*}_{j}|^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2\gamma-1}}\Big).

As in the proof of Proposition

[

]
by setting

.

(A.23)

\displaystyle\leq m\lambda+Cm^{1-\gamma}.

\displaystyle\leq C(\xi+1)\max(2,\kappa^{-2}(2\xi+1,J_{\lambda}))\Big(\frac{\|\varepsilon\|_{n}^{2}\log p}{n}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2\gamma}}.

(A.24)

on an event

(Two-step procedure).

Combining the reasoning from the proof of Proposition

3.1
with the bound from Lemma

2.5
, the left-hand side of

A.36
) is larger than

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{c_{\lambda}}{16}\underline{\beta}^{2},&\beta^{*}\ s\text{-sparse},\\

with probability converging to one and

sufficiently large.

A.36
), we

\tau<m^{*}_{s,\gamma}
from Equation
(
1.18
).

\tau
with
m^{*}_{s,\gamma}
.

5.2

2.3

whereas the right-hand side is smaller than

Proof of Lemma

[

[

(iii)

For

, a union bound yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big\{\sup_{|J|\leq c_{\text{iter}}n/\log p}\frac{\|\widehat{\Gamma}_{J}-\Gamma_{J}\|_{\text{op}}}{\rho^{2}}\geq t\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{|J|\leq c_{\text{iter}}n/\log p}\mathbb{P}\Big\{\frac{\|\widehat{\Gamma}_{J}-\Gamma_{J}\|_{\text{op}}}{\rho^{2}}\geq t\Big\}.

(B.10)

For any fixed
J

we can choose a

-net

, see Corollary 4.2.13

By an approximation argument,

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\Gamma}_{J}-\Gamma_{J}\|_{\text{op}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\max_{v\in\mathcal{N}}|\langle(\widehat{\Gamma}_{J}-\Gamma_{J})v,v\rangle|

(B.11)

\displaystyle=2\max_{v\in\mathcal{N}}|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle X_{i}^{(J)},v\rangle^{2}-\mathbb{E}\langle X_{i}^{(J)},v\rangle^{2}|,

with
X_{i}^{(J)}=(X_{i}^{(j)})_{j\in J}\in\mathbb{R}^{|J|}.

As in (i), the

,
i=1,\dots,n
, are independent subexponential random variables
with
parameters
(C\rho^{4},C\rho^{2})
, i.e., by a union bound and
Bernstein’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big\{\frac{\|\widehat{\Gamma}_{J}-\Gamma_{J}\|_{\text{op}}}{\rho^{2}}\geq t\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v\in\mathcal{N}}\mathbb{P}\Big\{|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle X_{i}^{(J)},v\rangle-\mathbb{E}\langle X_{i}^{(J)},v\rangle|\geq\frac{t^{2}\rho^{2}}{2}\Big\}

(B.12)

\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot 9^{m}\exp\big(-cn\min(t^{2},t)\big).

Together, this yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big\{\sup_{|J|\leq c_{\text{iter}}n/\log p}\frac{\|\widehat{\Gamma}_{J}-\Gamma_{J}\|_{\text{op}}}{\rho^{2}}\geq t\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=1}^{\lfloor c_{\text{iter}}n/\log p\rfloor}\sum_{J:|J|=m}\mathbb{P}\Big\{\frac{\|\widehat{\Gamma}_{J}-\Gamma_{J}\|_{\text{op}}}{\rho^{2}}\geq t\Big\}

(B.13)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=1}^{\lfloor c_{\text{iter}}n/\log p\rfloor}\binom{p}{m}2\cdot 9^{m}\exp\big(-cn\min(t^{2},t)\big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2c_{\text{iter}}n}{\log p}\exp\Big(\frac{c_{\text{iter}}n}{\log p}\log(9p)-cn\min(t^{2},t)\Big).

Setting
t=c_{\text{iter}}C_{\Gamma}
with
C_{\Gamma}>0
large enough yields the result.

(iv)

Set
c_{\text{iter}}<c_{\lambda}/(C_{\Gamma}\rho^{2})
, with

from Assumption

(CovB)
,
and consider

For
n
large enough, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Q_{n})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\Big\{\inf_{|J|\leq c_{\text{iter}}n/\log p}\lambda_{\min}(\widehat{\Gamma}_{J})>0\Big\}

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\Big\{\inf_{|J|\leq c_{\text{iter}}n/\log p}\Big(\lambda_{\min}(\Gamma_{J})-\|\widehat{\Gamma}_{J}-\Gamma_{J}\|_{\text{op}}\Big)>0\Big\}

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\Big\{\inf_{|J|\leq c_{\text{iter}}n/\log p}\Big(c_{\text{iter}}C_{\Gamma}\rho^{2}-\|\widehat{\Gamma}_{J}-\Gamma_{J}\|_{\text{op}}\Big)>0\Big\}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}1,

where we have used (iii) and

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}(\widehat{\Gamma}_{J})

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{\min}(\Gamma_{J})-\|\widehat{\Gamma}_{J}-\Gamma_{J}\|_{\text{op}}

(B.15)

by Weyl’s inequality. Now, let

F_{n}:=\{\sup_{|J|\leq c_{\text{iter}}n/\log p}\|\widehat{\Gamma}_{J}-\Gamma_{J}\|_{\text{op}}\leq c_{\text{iter}}C_{\Gamma}\rho^{2}\}

be the event from (iii).
For
n
large enough and

we then have

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \mathbb{P}\Big(\Big\{\sup_{|J|\leq c_{\text{iter}}n/\log p}\|\widehat{\Gamma}_{J}^{-1}\|\leq C_{\Gamma^{-1}}\Big\}\cap F_{n}\cap Q_{n}\Big)

(B.16)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\Big(\Big\{\Big(1-\frac{c_{\text{iter}}C_{\Gamma}\rho^{2}}{c_{\lambda}}\Big)\sup_{|J|\leq c_{\text{iter}}n/\log p}\|\widehat{\Gamma}_{J}^{-1}\|_{\text{op}}\leq\frac{1}{c_{\lambda}}\Big\}\cap F_{n}\cap Q_{n}\Big)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\Big(\Big\{\forall|J|\leq c_{\text{iter}}n/\log p:\big(1-\|\widehat{\Gamma}_{J}-\Gamma_{J}\|_{\text{op}}\|\Gamma_{J}^{-1}\|_{\text{op}}\big)\|\widehat{\Gamma}_{J}^{-1}\|_{\text{op}}\leq\|\Gamma_{J}^{-1}\|_{\text{op}}\Big\}\cap F_{n}\cap Q_{n}\Big)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}(F_{n}\cap Q_{n})\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}1,

where we have used Banach’s Lemma for the inverse in the last inequality,
which yields that for fixed
J
,

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\Gamma}_{J}^{-1}\|_{\text{op}}

\displaystyle=\|\widehat{\Gamma}_{J}^{-1}-\Gamma_{J}^{-1}+\Gamma_{J}^{-1}\|_{\text{op}}\leq\frac{\|\Gamma_{J}^{-1}\|_{\text{op}}}{1-\|\Gamma_{J}^{-1}(\widehat{\Gamma}_{J}-\Gamma_{J})\|_{\text{op}}}

(B.17)

as long as

Otherwise, the inequality

\displaystyle\big(1-\|\widehat{\Gamma}_{J}-\Gamma_{J}\|_{\text{op}}\|\Gamma_{J}^{-1}\|_{\text{op}}\big)\|\widehat{\Gamma}_{J}^{-1}\|_{\text{op}}\leq\|\Gamma_{J}^{-1}\|_{\text{op}}

(B.18)

is trivially true, since the left-hand side is negative.

Lemma B.2

(Lower bound for order statistics)
.

Let
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{p}\sim N(0,\sigma^{2})
i.i.d.
Then, the order statistic
Z_{(p-m+1)}
satisfies
Z_{(p-m+1)}\geq c\sqrt{\sigma^{2}\log p}
with probability converging to one for
p\to\infty
, as long as

m\leq cp^{\varrho}
for some
\varrho\in(0,1)
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, let
p/m\in\mathbb{N}
.
Split the sample into
m
groups of size
p/m
and for a natural number
k\leq m
, let
Z^{(k)}
denote the maximal value of the
Z_{j},j\leq p
which belong to the
k
-th group.
Then, by a union bound,

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \mathbb{P}\Big\{Z_{(p-m+1)}\geq\sqrt{\sigma^{2}\log\Big(\frac{p}{m}\Big)}\Big\}\geq\mathbb{P}\Big\{\min_{k\leq m}Z^{(k)}\geq\sqrt{\sigma^{2}\log\Big(\frac{p}{m}\Big)}\Big\}

(B.23)

\displaystyle\geq 1-m\mathbb{P}\Big\{Z^{(1)}<\sqrt{\sigma^{2}\log\Big(\frac{p}{m}\Big)}\Big\}=1-m\Big(1-\mathbb{P}\Big\{Z^{(1)}\geq\sqrt{\sigma^{2}\log\Big(\frac{p}{m}\Big)}\Big\}\Big).

By independence, we can further estimate

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \mathbb{P}\Big\{Z^{(1)}\geq\sqrt{\sigma^{2}\log\Big(\frac{p}{m}\Big)}\Big\}=1-\Big(1-\mathbb{P}\Big\{Z_{1}\geq\sqrt{\sigma^{2}\log\Big(\frac{p}{m}\Big)}\Big\}\Big)^{\frac{p}{m}}

(B.24)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\Big(1-\frac{c}{\sqrt{(p/m)\log(p/m)}}\Big)^{\frac{p}{m}}\geq 1-e^{-c\sqrt{\frac{p}{m}/\log(\frac{p}{m})}},

using the lower Gaussian tail bound from, e.g., Proposition 2.1.2 in
Vershynin
[
25
]
.
Together, this yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big\{Z_{(p-m+1)}\geq\sqrt{\sigma^{2}\log\Big(\frac{p}{m}\Big)}\Big\}

\displaystyle\geq 1-me^{-c\sqrt{\frac{p}{m}/\log(\frac{p}{m})}}\xrightarrow{p\to\infty}1.

(B.25)

On the event on the left-hand side, the order statistic satisfies
Z_{(p-m+1)}\geq c\sqrt{(1-\varrho)\sigma^{2}\log p}
.
∎

\displaystyle=\Big\langle\varepsilon,\frac{(I-\widehat{\Pi}_{J})g_{k}}{\|g_{k}\|_{n}}\Big\rangle_{n}

(C.22)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j\not\in J}\beta_{j}\Big[\frac{\langle g_{j},(I-\widehat{\Pi}_{J})g_{k}\rangle_{n}}{\|g_{k}\|_{n}}-\langle g_{j},(I-\Pi_{J})g_{k}\rangle_{L^{2}}\Big].

From Lemma
2.4
(i) and Corollary

B.1
(ii), we obtain an event with probability
converging to one on which

\displaystyle\Big\langle\varepsilon,\frac{(I-\widehat{\Pi}_{J})g_{k}}{\|g_{k}\|}\Big\rangle_{n}

\displaystyle\leq 2\langle\varepsilon,(I-\widehat{\Pi}_{J})g_{k}\rangle_{n}\leq C\sqrt{\frac{(\sigma^{2}+\rho^{4})\log p}{n}}.

(C.23)

Further, for
j,k\not\in J
, we can estimate

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \frac{\langle g_{j},(I-\widehat{\Pi}_{J})g_{k}\rangle_{n}}{\|g_{k}\|_{n}}-\langle g_{j},(I-\Pi_{J})g_{k}\rangle_{L^{2}}

(C.24)

\displaystyle\leq\Big|\frac{1}{\|g_{k}\|_{n}}-1\Big|\|g_{j}\|_{n}\|g_{k}\|_{n}+|\langle g_{j},(I-\widehat{\Pi}_{J})g_{k}\rangle_{n}-\langle g_{j},(I-\Pi_{J})g_{k}\rangle_{L^{2}}|.

From Lemma
2.4
(i), we obtain an event with probability converging to one on which

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \Big|\frac{1}{\|g_{k}\|_{n}}-1\Big|\|g_{j}\|_{n}\|g_{k}\|_{n}\leq|\|g_{k}\|_{n}-1|\|g_{j}\|_{n}

(C.25)

\displaystyle\leq|\|g_{k}\|_{n}^{2}-1|\|g_{j}\|_{n}\leq C\sqrt{\frac{\rho^{4}\log p}{n}}.

Additionally,

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ |\langle g_{j},(I-\widehat{\Pi}_{J})g_{k}\rangle_{n}-\langle g_{j},(I-\Pi_{J})g_{k}\rangle_{L^{2}}|

(C.26)

\displaystyle\leq|\langle g_{j},g_{k}\rangle_{n}-\langle g_{j},g_{k}\rangle_{L^{2}}|+|\langle g_{j},(\widehat{\Pi}_{J}-\Pi_{J})g_{k}\rangle_{n}|+|\langle g_{j},\Pi_{J}g_{k}\rangle_{n}-\langle g_{j},\Pi_{J}g_{k}\rangle_{L^{2}}|

\displaystyle\leq(C_{\text{Cov}}+1)\sup_{j,k\leq p}|\langle g_{j},g_{k}\rangle_{n}-\langle g_{j},g_{k}\rangle|+|\langle g_{j},\widehat{\Pi}_{J}(I-\Pi_{J})g_{k}\rangle_{n}|

by Assumption
(CovB)
.
The first term can be treated by Lemma

2.4
(i) again.
Using the representation of
\widehat{\Pi}_{J}
in Equation
(
1.26
), the second term can be estimated against

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ |\langle(I-\Pi_{J})g_{j},\widehat{\Pi}_{J}(I-\Pi_{J})g_{k}\rangle_{n}|+|\langle\Pi_{J}g_{j},\widehat{\Pi}_{J}(I-\Pi_{J})g_{k}\rangle_{n}|

(C.27)

\displaystyle=|\langle(I-\Pi_{J})g_{j},g_{J}\rangle_{n}^{\top}\widehat{\Gamma}_{J}^{-1}\langle(I-\Pi_{J})g_{k},g_{J}\rangle_{n}|+|\langle\Pi_{J}g_{j},(I-\Pi_{J})g_{k}\rangle_{n}|

\displaystyle\leq\|\widehat{\Gamma}_{J}^{-1}\|_{\text{op}}\|\langle(I-\Pi_{J})g_{j},g_{J}\rangle_{n}^{\top}\|_{2}^{2}+|\langle g_{J}^{\top}\Gamma_{J}^{-1}\langle g_{j},g_{J}\rangle,(I-\Pi_{J})g_{k}\rangle_{n}|

\displaystyle\leq\|\widehat{\Gamma}_{J}^{-1}\|_{\text{op}}M_{n}\sup_{j\in J,k\not\in J}|\langle(I-\Pi_{J})g_{k},g_{j}\rangle_{n}|^{2}+C_{\text{Cov}}\sup_{j\in J,k\not\in J}|\langle(I-\Pi_{J})g_{k},g_{j}\rangle_{n}|.

Analogously to before, the remaining terms can now be treated by Lemma

2.4
(iv) and Corollary

B.1
(i). The result now follows by intersecting all
the events and taking the supremum in Equation
(
C.22
).

Step 5:

Under Assumption

(Sparse)
(i), the general argument from Step 1 is the same.
However, we need to consider the events

\displaystyle A_{n}(m):

\displaystyle=\Big\{\sup_{|J|\leq m,k\leq p}|\widehat{\mu}_{J,k}-\mu_{J,k}|\leq C\sqrt{\frac{(\overline{\sigma}^{2}+\|\beta^{*}\|_{1}\rho^{4})s\log p}{n}}\Big\},

(C.28)

\displaystyle B_{n}(m):

\displaystyle=\Big\{\inf_{l\leq m}\sup_{l\leq p}|\mu_{\widehat{J}_{l},j}|>\frac{2C}{1-\xi}\sqrt{\frac{(\overline{\sigma}^{2}+\|\beta^{*}\|_{1}\rho^{4})s\log p}{n}}\Big\}.

On
A_{n}(m)\cap B_{n}(m)^{c}
, the analysis is exactly the same as before.
In Step 4, we can also argue the same.
We merely have to account for the coefficients of
f^{*}
by a factor

\|\beta^{*}\|_{1}
, instead of shifting them into the constant.

On
A_{n}(m)\cap B_{n}(m)
, we obtain that for any
l\leq m
,

\displaystyle\|(I-\Pi_{l})f^{*}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{|J|\leq l,j\leq p}|\mu_{J,j}|\sum_{j\not\in\widehat{J}_{l}}|\beta^{*}_{j}|\leq\sup_{|J|\leq l,j\leq p}|\mu_{J,j}|\Big(s\sum_{j\not\in\widehat{J}_{l}}|\beta^{*}_{j}|^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(C.29)

Since additionally,
\sum_{j\not\in\widehat{J}_{l}}|\beta^{*}_{j}|^{2}\leq c\|(I-\Pi_{l})f^{*}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{|J|\leq l,j\leq p}\mu_{J,j}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{c}{s}\|(I-\Pi_{l})f^{*}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(C.30)

Recursively, this yields

\displaystyle\|(I-\Pi_{m})f^{*}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|(I-\Pi_{m-1})f^{*}-\mu_{\widehat{J}_{m-1},\widehat{j}_{m}}g_{\widehat{j}_{m}}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\|(I-\Pi_{m-1})f^{*}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\mu_{\widehat{J}_{m-1},\widehat{j}_{m}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|(I-\Pi_{m-1})f^{*}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\xi^{2}\sup_{j\leq p}\mu_{\widehat{J}_{m-1},j}^{2}\leq\|(I-\Pi_{m-1})f^{*}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\Big(1-\frac{c}{s}\Big)

(C.31)

\displaystyle\leq\|f^{*}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\exp\Big(\frac{-cm}{s}\Big).

Finally, as long as
S\not\subset\widehat{J}_{m}
,

\displaystyle\|(I-\Pi_{m})f^{*}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\beta((I-\Pi_{m})f^{*})^{\top}\Gamma\beta((I-\Pi_{m})f^{*})\geq c_{\lambda}\underline{\beta}^{2}.

(C.32)

However, for
m=Cs
with some

and
n\in\mathbb{N}

large enough,

\displaystyle\|(I-\Pi_{m})f^{*}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c_{\lambda}}{4}\underline{\beta}^{2}+\sqrt{\frac{(\overline{\sigma}^{2}+\|\beta^{*}\|_{1}\rho^{4})s\log p}{n}}\leq\frac{c_{\lambda}}{2}\underline{\beta}^{2}

(C.33)

with probability converging to one.
This yields the last claim of Proposition
1.7
.
