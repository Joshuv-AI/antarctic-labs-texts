On the vanishing viscosity limit for the full viscous MHD system with critical axisymmetric initial data
Youssouf Maafa, Mohamed Zerguine

Abstract
. The current paper establishes the global well-posedness issue for the full viscous MHD equations in the axisymmetric setting. Global solutions are obtained in critical Besov spaces uniformly to the viscosity when the resistivity is fixed in the spirit of [ 4 , 22 , 28 ] . Furthermore, strong convergence in the resolution spaces with a rate of convergence is also studied.

1.
Introduction

Magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) is the branch of continuum mechanics which deals with the interaction of electromagnetic
fields and conducting fluids. The modeling consists of a coupling between the equations of continuum fluid mechanics and the Maxwell equations of electromagnetism. This coupled phenomenon governs by the following set equations:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}v_{\mu}+v_{\mu}\cdot\nabla v_{\mu}-\mu\Delta v_{\mu}+\nabla p_{\mu}=B_{\mu}\cdot\nabla B_{\mu}&\textrm{if $(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}$,}\\
\partial_{t}B_{\mu}+v_{\mu}\cdot\nabla B_{\mu}-\kappa\Delta B_{\mu}=B_{\mu}\cdot\nabla v_{\mu}&\textrm{if $(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}$,}\\
\textnormal{div}v_{\mu}=0,\quad\textnormal{div}B_{\mu}=0,&\\
({v}_{\mu},{B}_{\mu})_{|t=0}=({v}^{\mu}_{0},{B}^{\mu}_{0}).\end{array}\right.

(MHD
μ,κ
)

Here,
v(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
refers to the velocity vector field of the fluid localized in
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
at time
t>0
and
B(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
designate the intensity of the magnetic field, which both are assumed to be solenoidal. The scalar quantity
p(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}
is the force of the internal pressure, and it may be determined in terms of
v
and
B
using the Calderón-Zygmund transform.

p\equiv-\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}\mathfrak{R}_{i}\mathfrak{R}_{j}(v^{i}v^{j})+\sum_{i=1}^{2}\mathfrak{R}_{i}\mathfrak{R}_{j}(B^{i}B^{j})\triangleq p_{v}+p_{B},

where
\mathfrak{R}_{i}=\frac{\partial_{i}}{\sqrt{-\Delta}}
stands to Riesz’s operator. The positive parameters
\mu
and
\kappa
represent the viscosity and resistivity of the fluid, respectively.

Mathematical modeling of the Cauchy problem associated with the magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) has been a subject of great interest during the last few decades since the seminal work of Alfvén
[
1
]
, due to the many practical applications as a scientific endeavor of great importance in various contexts: geomagnetism and planetary magnetism, astrophysics, nuclear fusion (plasma) physics, and liquid metal technology. For extensive literature about this subject, we refer to
[
2
]
.

To derive MHD’s governing equations, consider an electrical conductivity fluid; and assume that magnetic fields are prevalent. The electrical conductivity and the prevalence of magnetic fields provide two effects. First, electric currents are generated by the motion of the electrically conducting fluid across the magnetic lines of force. As a result, the associated magnetic fields contribute to changes in the existing fields. Second, the fluid elements carrying current traverse magnetic lines of force contribute to additional forces acting on the fluid particle. This twofold interaction between the motions and the fields is responsible for patterns of behavior that are often unexpected and striking. This interaction gives us the well-known Maxwell’s equations,

(Gauss)

\displaystyle\textnormal{div}E=\frac{\rho_{c}}{\epsilon_{0}},\quad\textnormal{div}B=0,

(Maxwell-Faraday)

\displaystyle\textnormal{curl}E=-\mu\partial_{t}B,

(Maxwell-Ampère)

\displaystyle\textnormal{curl}B=\mu_{0}J+\varepsilon_{0}\mu_{0}\partial_{t}E.

In electromagnetic units,
\rho_{c}
is the charge density,
J
is the electric current density,
\varepsilon_{0}
the permittivity of free space, and
\mu_{0}
the magnetic permeability of free space. We point out that Bellac and Lévy-Leblond was shown in
[
8
]
that Maxwell’s equations possess two distinct, still utterly coherent, non-relativistic limits. The two limits correspond to situations where either
|E|\gg c|B|
(electric limit) or
|E|\ll c|B|
(magnetic limit). Each of the two limits is Galilei covariant, albeit the transformations of the fields and the density, and current are not the same in the two cases. The electric limit amounts to disregard the time-derivative of the magnetic field in Maxwell-Faraday’s law of induction. In contrast, the magnetic limit obtains by neglecting the displacement current in Maxwell-Ampère’s equation. Physically, the magnetic limit means that the electromagnetic field is instantaneously at equilibrium, and we can ignore the propagation of information by electromagnetic waves. As a result, Maxwell-Faraday’s formula in the magnetic limit leads to
\textnormal{div}J=0
, so the electric current lines are closed (like the magnetic field lines
\textnormal{div}B=0
).

Visibly the above system is not yet closed. For this purpose, we need to know the expression for the electric current field
J
. On account of the nature of the fluid provided by Ohm’s law

J=\sigma(E+\mu v\times B).

In the framework of the magnetic limit, by combining the previous equations, we shall obtain after an elementary calculus the second equation in the system (
MHDμ,κ
). When a motion of a conducting fluid induces currents through a magnetic field, a Lorentz force will act on the fluid and modify its motion. This force is given by

\mathcal{L}=\kappa J\times B=\kappa\textnormal{curl}(B\times B).

With some vectorial identity, the force
\mathcal{L}
can be decomposed in the following way

\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{2}\nabla|B|^{2}+(B\cdot\nabla)B.

The last formula says that the magnetic force density has two parts: the first one occurs in the first equation of (
MHDμ,κ
) and is known as the magnetic pressure
\frac{1}{2}|B|^{2}
orthogonal to
B
, and second, an orthogonal curvature force
(B\cdot\nabla)B
. This curvature force acts toward the line’s center of curvature and is the magnetic-field-line analog of the force that operates on a curved wire or string under tension. For further information about the derivation of the MHD equations and some dynamical aspects, see
[
12
]
.

In the absence of the magnetic field (
B\equiv 0
), the system (
MHDμ,κ
) boils down to the classical incompressible Navier-Stokes equations denoted by (
NSμ
) and reads as follows.

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}v+v\cdot\nabla v+\nabla p-\mu\Delta v=0&\textrm{if $(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}$,}\\
\textnormal{div}v=0,&\\
{v}_{|t=0}={v}_{0}.\end{array}\right.

(NS
μ
)

When the viscous forces vanish (
\mu=0
), we still are finding the incompressible Euler equations denoted by (
E
) and governed by the system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}v+v\cdot\nabla v+\nabla p=0&\textrm{if $(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}$,}\\
\textnormal{div}v=0,&\\
{v}_{|t=0}={v}_{0}.\end{array}\right.

(E)

The investigation of the local/global well-posedness issue began early with the celebrated work of Lichtenstein
[
34
]
and Gunther
[
21

with

and

. The globalization of this solution for 2D Euler in Hölder spaces remounts to Wolibner
[
44
]
. In the same direction, Ebin and Marsden
[
17
]
explored the manifold structure of certain groups of diffeomorphisms with Sobolev spaces
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N}),s>\frac{N}{2}+1
to assert that Euler’s equations are well-posed. This result was later extend by Bourguignon and Brézis
[
11
]
for
W^{s,p}
spaces with
s>\frac{N}{p}+1
. In the
\mathbb{R}^{N}
framework, Kato established that Euler’s equations admits a unique local solution, for initial data
v_{0}\in H^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
with
m
is an integer be such that
m>\frac{N}{2}+1
and
v\in C\big([0,T^{\star});H^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\big)
, where
T^{\star}
is the maximal lifespan of the solution which satisfies:

\limsup_{t\uparrow T^{\star}}\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla v(\tau)\|_{L^{\infty}}d\tau=\infty,

Next, Kato and Ponce
[
30
]
enhanced this result for
W^{s,p}
with
s
is a real number,
s>1+\frac{N}{p}
, taking into account the following commutator estimate:

\|\Lambda^{s}(fg)-f\Lambda^{s}g\|_{L^{p}}\leq C\|\nabla f\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\Lambda^{s-1}g\|_{L^{p}}+\|\Lambda^{s}f\|_{L^{p}}\|g\|_{L^{\infty}},

(1.1)

where,
\Lambda^{s}
refers to the nonlocal operator
(\mathbb{I}-\Delta)^{s/2}
with
s>0
and
C=C(N,s,p)
. We mention that,
p
belongs only to
(1,\infty)
. For
p=\infty
, the problem contributes some hardness, so, it has conjectured subsequently in
[
20
]
. Some new Kato-Ponce type inequality developed latterly in a series of papers, e.g
[
10
]
. Also, the regularity of the solutions of Euler equations has a close link with the vorticity dynamics. The vorticity variable is denoted by
\omega=\textnormal{curl }v
and defined as a skew-matrix with entries:

\omega_{i,j}=\partial_{j}v^{i}-\partial_{i}v^{j},\;1\leq i,j\leq N.

A blow-up vorticity criterion for Kato’s solutions following Beale-Kato-Majda
[
7
]
(short BKM) reads as follows: if
T^{\star}
is the maximal lifespan time, then we have:

\limsup_{t\uparrow T^{\star}}\int_{0}^{t}\|\omega(\tau)\|_{L^{\infty}}d\tau=\infty.

(1.2)

In particular, for
N=2
, the vorticity can be identified as a scalar function of the type
\omega=\partial_{2}v^{1}-\partial_{1}v^{1}
which evolves the following nonlinear transport equation:

\partial_{t}\omega+v\cdot\nabla\omega=0,

(1.3)

which permits us to recover the velocity via Biot-Savart law in the following way:

v(t,x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{(x-y)^{\perp}}{|x-y|^{2}}\omega(t,x)dx,\quad x^{\perp}=(-x_{2},x_{1}).

This shows that Euler equations have a Hamiltonian structure and, in turn, provides an infinity of conservation laws as
\|\omega(t)\|_{L^{p}}=\|\omega_{0}\|_{L^{p}}
for all
p\in[1,\infty]
. Ergo, in light of (
1.2
) the Kato’s solutions are globally well-posed in time.

The aforementioned functional spaces like Sobolev spaces
W^{s,p}
with
s\in\mathbb{R},s>1+\frac{N}{p}
are sometimes called sub-critical space for D-dimensional Euler and the space
W^{1+\frac{N}{p},p}
is called critical. The criticality index
s_{N,p}=1+\frac{N}{p}
is the minimal prerequisite in some sense to achieve the energy estimate. We underline that for these types of spaces, we do not know whether the BKM criterion works or not. For this purpose, Vishisk
[
43
]
developed a new result about global well-posedness for 2D Euler equations in the critical Besov spaces
B^{1+1/p}_{p,1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

\|f\circ\Psi\|_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}\leq C\big(1+\log\big(\|\Psi\|_{\textnormal{Lip}}\|\Psi^{-1}\|_{\textnormal{Lip}}\big)\big)\|f\|_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}},

(1.4)

where,
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a diffeomorphism which preserves Lebesgue’s measure. This result was extended later by T. Hmidi and S. Keraani
[
25
]
for 2D Navier-Stokes equations, where they used the Lagrangian coordinates to establish that the velocity is a Lipschitz function globally in time and bounded uniformly in viscosity. Moreover, they investigated an inviscid limit result between the Navier-Stokes equations and the Euler one. For 3D Euler’s equations, the vorticity is a vectorial function defined by
\omega=\nabla\wedge v
and satisfies the following system:

\partial_{t}\omega+(v\cdot\nabla)\omega-(\omega\cdot\nabla)v=0.

The stretching term
(\omega\cdot\nabla)v
is the main difficulty that affects the motion of the fluid and, so we don’t reach the global regularity of Euler’s equations. In contrast, some partial results exist in the framework of so-called axisymmetric flows without swirl. An axisymmetric solution without swirl of Euler’s equations, meaning that the solution can be split in the cylindrical coordinates
(r,\theta,z)
as follows:

v(t,x)=v^{r}(t,r,z)\vec{e}_{r}+v^{z}(t,r,z)\vec{e}_{z},

x_{1}=r\cos\theta,\quad x_{2}=r\sin\theta,\quad r\geq 0,\quad 0\leq\theta<2\pi.

\vec{e}_{r}=\Big(\frac{x_{1}}{r},\frac{x_{2}}{r},0\Big),\quad\vec{e}_{\theta}=\Big(-\frac{x_{2}}{r},\frac{x_{1}}{r},0\Big),\quad\vec{e}_{z}=(0,0,1).

(1.5)

\omega_{\theta}=\partial_{z}v^{r}-\partial_{r}v^{z}.

\Omega_{|t=0}=\Omega_{0}.\end{array}\right.

(1.7)

1.1.
Aims and the main results

The main concerns of this paper are twofold. The first part interests to conduct the same result as in
[
22
]

MHDμ

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}v+v\cdot\nabla v+\nabla p={B}\cdot\nabla B&\textrm{if $(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}$,}\\
\partial_{t}B+v\cdot\nabla B-\Delta B=B\cdot\nabla v&\textrm{if $(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}$,}\\
\textnormal{div}v=0,\quad\textnormal{div}B=0,&\\
({v},{B})_{|t=0}=({v}_{0},{B}_{0})\end{array}\right.

(MHD
0
)

when the viscosity is small enough and we quantify the rate of convergence between velocities and magnetic fields.

The first main result treats essentially the global and uniqueness topic for the system (
MHDμ
) with initial data lying to some critical Besosov spaces. More precisely, we will prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1

(Uniform boundedness of the velocity and magnetic fields)
.

Let

and
(v^{\mu}_{0},B^{\mu}_{0})\in\big(L^{2}\cap\mathscr{B}_{p,1}^{1+\frac{3}{p}}\big)\times L^{2}

and
B^{r}_{0}=B^{z}_{0}=0
. Assume that

\Big(\frac{\omega^{\mu}_{\theta}(0)}{r},\frac{B^{\mu}_{\theta}(0)}{r}\Big)\in L^{3,1}\times\big(L^{2}\cap L^{\infty}\big),

(1.9)

where,
\omega^{\mu}_{\theta}(0)
is the angular component of the vorticity
v^{\mu}_{0}
. We distinguish two cases:

\bullet

Case

p=\infty
. If
B^{\mu}_{0}\in\mathscr{B}_{\sigma,1}^{-1+\frac{3}{\sigma}},\;\sigma\in[2,\infty)
. Then the system (
MHDμ
) admits a unique global solution in time
(v,B)
, so have

(v,B)\in\mathscr{C}\big(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathscr{B}_{\infty,1}^{1}\big)\times\Big(\mathscr{C}\big(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathscr{B}_{\sigma,1}^{-1+\frac{3}{\sigma}}\big)\cap L^{1}_{\textnormal{loc}}\big(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathscr{B}_{\infty,1}^{1}\big)\Big).

\bullet

Case

If

. Then the system (
MHDμ
) admits a unique global solution in time
(v,B)

(v,B)\in\mathscr{C}\Big(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathscr{B}_{p,1}^{1+\frac{3}{p}}\Big)\times\Big(\mathscr{C}\big(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathscr{B}_{p,1}^{-1+\frac{3}{p}}\big)\cap L^{1}_{\textnormal{loc}}\big(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathscr{B}_{p,1}^{1+\frac{3}{p}}\big)\Big).

The proof of Theorem

requires two main steps. The first one is the boundedness of the vorticity through time by exploiting axisymmetric Biot-Savart law and Lorentz spaces. Unhappily, this is not enough to propagate the Lipschitz norm of the velocity since the BKM criterion is not known to be valid for critical regularities. Therefore, we stand driven to derive a new estimate for the vorticity in the Besov space
\mathscr{B}^{0}_{\infty,1}
as stated in (

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}\omega_{\theta}+v\cdot\nabla\omega_{\theta}-\mu\big(\Delta-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\big)\omega_{\theta}=\frac{v_{r}}{r}\omega_{\theta}-\partial_{z}\frac{(B_{\theta})^{2}}{r},&\\
\partial_{t}B_{\theta}+v\cdot\nabla B_{\theta}-\big(\Delta-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\big)B_{\theta}=\frac{v_{r}}{r}B_{\theta}.\end{array}\right.

(1.10)

By putting
\Omega=\frac{\omega_{\theta}}{r}
and
\Sigma=\frac{B_{\theta}}{r}

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}\Omega+v\cdot\nabla\Omega-\mu\big(\Delta+\frac{2}{r}\partial_{r}\big)\Omega=-\partial_{z}\Sigma^{2},&\\
\partial_{t}\Sigma+v\cdot\nabla\Sigma-\big(\Delta+\frac{2}{r}\partial_{r}\big)\Sigma=0.\end{array}\right.

(1.11)

Compared to the resistive MHD (
\mu=0
) recently treated in
[

we have an additional term in
\Omega-
equation which contributes a hardness at the level to estimate
\Omega
in the Lorentz space

).

Our second main result motivates by establishing the inviscid limit of the system (

) towards (
MHD0

Theorem 1.6

and

\|v_{\mu}-v\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}\mathscr{B}^{0}_{p,1}}+\|B_{\mu}-B\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}\mathscr{B}^{-1}_{p,1}\cap L^{1}_{t}\mathscr{B}^{1}_{p,1}}\leq\Big((\mu t)^{\frac{1}{5}+\frac{3}{5p}}+(\mu t)^{\frac{3}{5p}}+(\mu t)^{\frac{3}{\max(p,6)}}\Big)\Phi_{6}(t).

(1.12)

the same as in

The direct demonstration of the previous theorem in the Besov spaces seems very difficult since the system stems from the difference between systems (

Structure of the paper
. The layout of the present paper is as follows. Section 2 gives a few results about the Littlewood-Paley theory like dyadic decomposition of the unity, Besov spaces, their properties, and paradifferential calculus. We also state a technical lemma about the persistence regularity in Besov spaces for transport-diffusion equation governs the density and magnetic evolution. Section 3 motivates by treating two parts. The first one concerns the energy estimates for different quantities in Lebesgue space

(resp. Besov and Lorentz space

2.1.
Vocabulary of Littlewood-Paley theory

This subsection starts with the definition of the Lorentz spaces and a brief concise about Littlewood-Paley theory.

Definition 2.1
.

For a measurable function
f^{\star}
we define its increasing rearrangement by

f^{\star}(t)=\inf\big\{s\in\mathbb{R}_{+}:\mu(\{x,|f(x)|>s\})\leq t\big\},

where
\mu
refers to the usual Lebesgue measure. For
(p,q)\in[1,\infty]^{2}
, the Lorentz space
L^{p,q}
is the set of functions
f
such that
\|f\|_{L^{p,q}}<\infty
, with

\|f\|_{L^{p,q}}\triangleq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\bigg(\int_{0}^{\infty}\big(t^{\frac{1}{p}}f^{\star}(t)\big)^{q}\frac{dt}{t}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{q}}&\textrm{for $q\in[1,\infty)$,}\\
\sup_{t>0}t^{\frac{1}{p}}f^{\star}(t)&\textrm{for $q=\infty$.}\end{array}\right.

We can also define the Lorentz space
L^{p,q}
by the real interpolation process from the Lebesgue spaces

L^{p,q}=[L^{1},L^{\infty}]_{1-\frac{1}{p},q}.

where
(p,q)\in]1,\infty[\times[1,\infty]
. These spaces are characterized by the following properties:

(i)

L^{p,p}=L^{p}
,

(ii)

L^{p,q_{1}}\hookrightarrow L^{p,q_{2}}
for every
1\leq q_{1}\leq q_{2}\leq\infty
,

(iii)

\|uv\|_{L^{p,q}}\leq\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}\|v\|_{L^{p,q}}.

Next, we state a few phrases about the so-called Littlewood-Paley and some of its properties.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\chi\in\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
be a reference cut-off function, monotonically decaying along rays and so that:
\chi\equiv 1
on
B(0,\frac{1}{2})
and
0\leq\chi\leq 1
on
B(\frac{1}{2},1)
. Define
\varphi(\xi)\triangleq\chi(\frac{\xi}{2})-\chi(\xi)
. We obviously check that
\varphi\geq 0
and

\textnormal{supp }\varphi\subset\mathscr{C}\triangleq\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\frac{1}{2}\leq\|\xi\|\leq 1\}.

We have the following elementary properties, see for example
[
6
,
14
]
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
\chi
and
\varphi
be as above. Then the following assertions are hold.

(i)

Decomposition of the unity:

\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{3},\quad\chi(\xi)+\sum_{q\geq 0}\varphi(2^{-q}\xi)=1.

(ii)

Almost orthogonality in the sense of
\ell^{2}
:

\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{3},\quad\frac{1}{2}\leq\chi^{2}(\xi)+\sum_{q\geq 0}\varphi^{2}(2^{-q}\xi)\leq 1.

The Littlewood-Paley or cut-off operators or dyadic blocks are defined as follows.

Definition 2.4
.

For every

\Delta_{-1}u\triangleq\chi(\textnormal{D})u,\quad\Delta_{q}u\triangleq\varphi(2^{-q}\textnormal{D})u\quad\mbox{if}\;q\in\mathbb{N},\quad S_{q}u\triangleq\sum_{j\leq q-1}\Delta_{j}u\quad\mbox{for}\;q\geq 0.

Some properties of
\Delta_{q}
and

are listed in the following proposition.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let

(i)

|p-q|\geq 2\Longrightarrow\Delta_{p}\Delta_{q}u\equiv 0
,

(ii)

|p-q|\geq 4\Longrightarrow\Delta_{q}(S_{p-1}u\Delta_{p}v)\equiv 0
,

(iii)

\Delta_{q},S_{q}:L^{p}\rightarrow L^{p}
uniformly with respect to

and

.

(iv)

u=\sum_{q\geq-1}\Delta_{q}u.

Likewise, the homogeneous operators

and

are defined by

\forall{q}\in\mathbb{Z}\quad\dot{\Delta}_{q}=\varphi(2^{q}D)u,\quad\dot{S}_{q}=\sum_{j\leq q-1}\dot{\Delta}_{j}v.

(2.1)

Now, we define the Besov spaces in the following way.

3.
Axisymmetric flows for MHD

In this section we develop the axisymmetric geometry for the MHD equations (

derive their cylindrical form. For this purpose, let
x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
define
r=(x^{2}_{1}+x^{2}_{2})^{1/2}

(v,B,p)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}v(t,x)=v^{r}(t,r,z)\vec{e}_{r}+v^{\theta}(t,r,z)\vec{e}_{\theta}+v^{z}(t,r,z)\vec{e}_{z}\\

\left\{\begin{array}[]{llllll}\partial_{t}v^{r}+v^{r}\partial_{r}v^{r}+v^{z}\partial_{z}v^{r}-\frac{(v^{\theta})^{2}}{r}-\mu(\Delta-\frac{1}{r^{2}})v^{r}+\partial_{r}p=B^{r}\partial_{r}B^{r}+B^{z}\partial_{z}B^{r}-\frac{(B^{\theta})^{2}}{r},&\\

with, the incompressibility requirement

\partial_{r}v^{r}+\frac{v^{r}}{r}+\partial_{z}v^{z}=0,\quad\partial_{r}B^{r}+\frac{B^{r}}{r}+\partial_{z}B^{z}=0.

If
(v^{\theta}_{0},B^{r}_{0},B^{z}_{0})

Proposition 4.1

\zeta
in cylindrical basis
(\vec{e}_{r},\vec{e}_{\theta},\vec{e}_{z})
. We perform the result in two steps: we claim first that the components of
\zeta
does not rely upon on the angular variale
\theta
. Next, we claim that the components
\zeta_{r}
and
\zeta_{z}
vanish.

We intend to check that (
4.1
) is invariant under rotation transform. Doing so, let us consider the rotation with angle
\alpha
and axis
(oz)
,
\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}
and defined by

\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\cos\alpha&-\sin\alpha&0\\
\sin\alpha&\cos\alpha&0\\
0&0&1\end{array}\right),\quad\alpha\in\mathbb{R}.

Setting
{\zeta}_{\alpha}(t,x)=\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}^{-1}\zeta{(t,\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}x)}
. We show that
{\zeta}_{\alpha}
satisfies also (
4.1
). The fact that the operator
\Delta
commutes with rotations, then it follows

\Delta(\zeta(t,\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}x))=(\Delta\zeta)(t,\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}x),

one deduce that

\Delta\zeta_{\alpha}(t,x)=\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}^{-1}(\Delta\zeta)(t,\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}x).

(4.3)

About the advection term
v\cdot\nabla\zeta
, we check that

(\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}^{-1}(v\cdot\nabla\zeta))(t,\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}x)=(v\cdot\nabla\,\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}^{-1}\zeta)(t,\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}x).

Consequently,

v(t,x)\cdot\nabla\,(\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}^{-1}\zeta(t,\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}x))=\big(\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}v(t,\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}^{-1}x)\cdot\nabla\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}^{-1}\zeta\big)(t,\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}(x)).

Since the velocity is axisymmetric vector field, thus we obtain

v(t,x)\cdot\nabla\,\zeta_{\alpha}(t,x)=\big(v\cdot\nabla\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}^{-1}\zeta\big)(t,\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}(x))).

Join together the last two estimates, it hlods

(\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}^{-1}(v\cdot\nabla\zeta))(t,\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}x)=v(t,x)\cdot\nabla\zeta_{\alpha}(t,x).

(4.4)

For the stretching term
\zeta\cdot\nabla v
step by step we find that

\displaystyle(\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}^{-1}(\zeta\cdot\nabla v))(t,\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\zeta\cdot\nabla\,\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}^{-1}v)(t,\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}x)

(4.5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(\zeta\cdot\nabla\,v(t,\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}^{-1}x\big)(t,\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\zeta_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\,v(t,x).

Collecting (
4.3
), (
4.4
) and (
4.5
) and insert them in (
4.1
) to infer that

\partial_{t}\zeta_{\alpha}+v\cdot\nabla\zeta_{\alpha}-\mu\Delta\zeta_{\alpha}=\zeta_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla v+\textnormal{curl}(B\cdot\nabla B).

The fact that
\zeta_{\alpha}^{0}(x)=\zeta^{0}(x)
and the uniqueness issue confirm that
\zeta_{\alpha}(t,x)=\zeta(t,x)
. Consequently, the components of
\zeta
don’t depend on the angle
\theta
, so have the first step is reached.

Now, our task is to prove that the components
\zeta_{r}
and
\zeta_{z}
vanish. To do this, we write their equations and taking the inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
for
\zeta
’s equation with
\vec{e}_{r}
. Exploring the fact
v
is axisymmetric, so a straightforward computations allow us to write

\displaystyle(v\cdot\nabla\zeta)\cdot\vec{e}_{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v_{r}\partial_{r}\zeta_{r}+v_{z}\partial_{z}\zeta_{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v\cdot\nabla\zeta_{r},

and

\displaystyle(\zeta\cdot\nabla v)\cdot\vec{e}_{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\zeta_{r}\partial_{r}v_{r}+\frac{1}{r}\zeta_{\theta}\partial_{\theta}v_{r}+\zeta_{z}\partial_{z}v_{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\zeta_{r}\partial_{r}v_{r}+\zeta_{z}\partial_{z}v_{r}.

For the dissipative term we have by definition and by the first step,

\displaystyle\mu\Delta\zeta\cdot\vec{e}_{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu\Big(\partial_{r}^{2}\zeta\cdot\vec{e}_{r}+\frac{1}{r}\partial_{r}\zeta_{r}\cdot\vec{e}_{r}+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\partial_{\theta}^{2}\zeta\cdot\vec{e}_{r}+\partial_{z}^{2}\zeta\cdot\vec{e}_{r}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu\Big(\partial_{r}^{2}(\zeta\cdot\vec{e}_{r})+\frac{1}{r}\partial_{r}(\zeta\cdot\vec{e}_{r})+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\Big(\partial_{\theta}^{2}(\zeta\cdot\vec{e}_{r})+(\zeta\cdot\vec{e}_{r})

\displaystyle-2\partial_{\theta}\zeta\cdot\vec{e}_{\theta}\Big)+\partial_{z}^{2}(\zeta\cdot\vec{e}_{r})\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu\Big(\partial_{r}^{2}\zeta_{r}+\frac{1}{r}\partial_{r}\zeta_{r}+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\partial_{\theta}^{2}\zeta_{r}-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\zeta_{r}-\frac{2}{r^{2}}\partial_{\theta}\zeta_{\theta}+\partial_{z}^{2}\zeta_{r}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu(\Delta-\frac{1}{r^{2}})\zeta_{r}.

For the last term
\textnormal{curl}(B\cdot\nabla B)
, we combine (
3.3
) and
\textnormal{curl}(B\cdot\nabla B)=-\partial_{z}\big(\frac{B^{\theta}}{r}B\big)
to obtain

\textnormal{curl}(B\cdot\nabla B)\cdot\vec{e}_{r}=0.

Finally, we discover that
\zeta_{r}
obeys the following Cauchy problem:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}\zeta_{r}+v\cdot\nabla\zeta_{r}-\mu(\Delta-\frac{1}{r^{2}})\zeta_{r}=\zeta_{r}\partial_{r}v_{r}+\zeta_{z}\partial_{z}v_{r},\\
{\zeta_{r}}_{|t=0}=\zeta_{r}^{0}.\end{array}\right.

(4.6)

Similarly, we can find that the component
\zeta_{z}
satisfies the following equation:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}\zeta_{z}+v\cdot\nabla\zeta_{z}-\mu\Delta\zeta_{z}=\zeta_{r}\partial_{r}v_{z}+\zeta_{z}\partial_{z}v_{z},\\
{\zeta_{z}}_{|t=0}=0.\end{array}\right.

(4.7)

At this stage, we develop and
L^{p}
estimate for
\zeta_{r}
(resp.
\zeta_{z}
) by multiplying (
4.6
) (resp. (
4.7
)) by
|\zeta_{r}|^{p-2}\zeta_{r}
(resp.
|\zeta_{r}|^{p-2}\zeta_{r}
). Thereafter, integrating by parts over
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, account
\textnormal{div}v=0
and Hölder’s inequality give

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\frac{d}{dt}\|\zeta_{r}(t)\|_{L^{p}}^{p}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\mu(p-1)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\nabla\zeta_{r}|^{2}|\zeta_{r}|^{p-2}dx+\mu\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{|\zeta_{r}|^{p}}{r^{2}}dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\zeta_{r}|^{p}\partial_{r}v_{r}dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\Sigma_{z}|\zeta_{r}|^{p-2}\zeta_{r}\partial_{z}v_{r}dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Big(\|\zeta_{r}\|_{L^{p}}^{p}+\|\zeta_{z}\|_{L^{p}}\|\zeta_{r}\|_{L^{p}}^{p-1}\Big)\|\nabla v\|_{L^{\infty}},

Therefore

\|\zeta_{r}(t)\|_{L^{p}}\leq\|\zeta_{r}^{0}\|_{L^{p}}+\int_{0}^{t}\big(\|\zeta_{r}(\tau)\|_{L^{p}}+\|\zeta_{z}(\tau)\|_{L^{p}}\big)\|\nabla v(\tau)\|_{L^{\infty}}d\tau,

respectively

\|\zeta_{z}(t)\|_{L^{p}}\leq\|\zeta_{z}^{0}\|_{L^{p}}+\int_{0}^{t}\big(\|\zeta_{r}(\tau)\|_{L^{p}}+\|\zeta_{z}(\tau)\|_{L^{p}}\big)\|\nabla v(\tau)\|_{L^{\infty}}d\tau.

Putting together the last two estimates, Gronwall’s inequality asserts for every
p\in[2,\infty]
that

\|\zeta_{r}(t)\|_{L^{p}}+\|\zeta_{z}(t)\|_{L^{p}}\leq\Big(\|\zeta_{r}^{0}\|_{L^{p}}+\|\zeta_{z}^{0}\|_{L^{p}}\Big)e^{2\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla v(\tau)\|_{L^{\infty}}d\tau}.

Since
\zeta_{r}^{0}=\zeta_{z}^{0}=0
then we deduce that
\zeta_{r}(t)=\zeta_{z}(t)=0
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, so
(ii)
is then achieved.

(iii)
We emphasize that the first statement is a direct consequence of
\zeta\wedge\vec{e}_{\theta}=\vec{0}.
Next, we will claim the exact equation that governs the angular component
\zeta_{\theta}
. For this aim, we develop an analog calculus as above to clear that

\displaystyle\Delta\zeta\cdot\vec{e}_{\theta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta(\zeta_{\theta}\vec{e}_{\theta})\cdot\vec{e}_{\theta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta\zeta_{\theta}-\frac{\zeta_{\theta}}{r^{2}},

and

\displaystyle(v\cdot\nabla\zeta)\cdot\vec{e}_{\theta}=v\cdot\nabla\zeta_{\theta},\quad(\zeta\cdot\nabla v)\cdot\vec{e}_{\theta}=\frac{v_{r}}{r}\zeta_{\theta}.

Thanks to (
4.1
), the angular components
\zeta_{\theta}
evolves

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}\zeta_{\theta}+v\cdot\nabla\zeta_{\theta}-\mu(\Delta-\frac{1}{r^{2}})\zeta_{\theta}=\frac{v_{r}}{r}\zeta_{\theta}-\partial_{z}\frac{(B_{\theta})^{2}}{r},\\
{\zeta_{\theta}}_{|t=0}=\zeta_{\theta}^{0},\end{array}\right.

(4.8)

which accomplish the proof of the proposition.
∎

5.
A priori estimate

This section comprises three important parts. The first one addresses to the energy estimates for different quantities like
v,B
and
\frac{B_{\theta}}{r}
,…, in various functional spaces. The second part, cares with an axisymmetric estimates for the same quantities, in particular, the vorticity

in

In the sequel we will agree the following notation: we denote by
