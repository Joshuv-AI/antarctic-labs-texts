On the category of structure species
Yasuhiro Wakabayashi

Abstract
. The purpose of the present paper is to make a mathematical study of the differences and relations among possible structures inherent in an object, as well as of the whole structure constituted by them (i.e., the structure of structures), against the background of the structuralism by Claude Lévi-Strauss and others. Our discussion focuses on Blanchard’s categorical reformulation of the notion of structure species introduced originally by Bourbaki. The main result of the present paper asserts that a category can be reconstructed, up to a certain slight indeterminacy, from the category of structure species on it. This result is partially motivated by various reconstruction theorems that have been shown in the context of anabelian geometry.

1.
Introduction: Lévi-Strauss’ structuralism

1.1.

Structuralism
is a general theory of culture and methodology
that
focuses on relationships rather than individual objects, or alternatively, where objects are defined by the set of relationships of which they are part and not by the qualities possessed by them taken in isolation.
According to this mode of knowledge,
phenomena of human life are not intelligible except through their interrelations.
These relations constitute a
structure
, and behind local variations in the surface phenomena there are constant laws of abstract structure (cf.
[
Cal
]
,
[
Rot
]
,
[
SBla
]
).

Structuralism in Europe developed in the early 20th century
from insights in the field of linguistics of, mainly,
Ferdinand de Saussure and the subsequent Prague, Moscow,
and Copenhagen schools of linguistics.
After World War II, an array of scholars in the humanities borrowed Saussure’s concepts for use in their respective fields.
By the early 1960s, structuralism as a movement was coming into its own and some believed that it offered a single unified approach to human life that would embrace all disciplines.
Claude Lévi-Strauss, a French anthropologist, was the first such scholar, sparking a widespread interest in structuralism.

Lévi-Strauss defined “structure” as
a whole consisting of elements and relations between elements, which retain their invariant properties through a series of transformations.
To be precise, a model with structural value satisfies several requirements described as follows (cf.
[
Lev2
]
, Part 5, Chap. XV):

(i)

The structure exhibits the characteristics of a system. It is made up of several elements, none of which can undergo a change without effecting changes in all the other elements.

(ii)

For any given model there should be a possibility of ordering a series of transformations resulting in a group of models of the same type.

(iii)

The above properties make it possible to predict how the model will react if one or more of its elements are submitted to certain modifications.

(iv)

The model should be constituted so as to make immediately intelligible all the observed facts.

The structures listed, at least, as illustrations are kinships, political ideologies, mythologies, ritual, art, code of etiquette, and even cooking (cf.
[
Rot
]
). The discernment of these structures and their comparative analysis, which takes into account their distribution both historically and geographically, is indeed the subject matter of structural anthropology.
In “
The Elementary Structures of Kinship
” (cf.
[
Lev3
]
), Claude Lévi-Strauss examined kinship systems from a structural point of view and demonstrated how apparently different social organizations were different permutations of a few basic kinship structures.
The kinship system provides a way to order individuals according to certain rules; social organization is another way of ordering individuals and groups; social stratifications, whether economic or political, provide us with a third type; and all these orders can themselves be ordered by showing the kinds of relationship which exist among them, i.e., how they interact with one another on both the synchronic and the diachronic levels.
One may construct models valid not only for one type of order (kinship, social organization, economic relations, etc.) but where numerous models for all types of order are themselves ordered inside a total model.
In
[
Rot
]
, Chap. II, N. Rotenstreich explained that

We do not start out with scattered concepts: we start with structures and move to further structures. We start with order
and move from another order or to an order of orders.
There is a structure to the relation between these orders or structures. This “
structure of structures
”

*
*

*

Lévi-Strauss developed a similar idea with the term “order of orders” (cf.
[
Lev2
]
).

is not just a static relation of coexistence, i.e. language beside kinship, etc., or even
not one of subordination whereby a narrow structure such as rites is comprised in or is secondary to a wider structure such as society.

1.2.

In the present paper, we make a mathematical study of the differences and relations among possible structures inherent in an object, as well as the whole structure constituted by them (i.e., the structure of structures), against the background of the structuralism by Lévi-Strauss and others.
Our discussion focuses on
some objects treated in structural theory, starting with Bourbaki.
As is well known, Bourbaki,
the collective pseudonym of a group of predominantly French mathematicians, undertook, in the mid-20th century,
the task of making a unified development of central parts of
modern mathematics in largely formalized language.
†
†

†

In 1943, André Weil, one of the collaborators in Bourbaki, met Claude Lévi-Strauss in New York,
which led to a small collaboration.
By using a mathematical model based on group theory, Weil
described marriage rules for four classes of people within Australian aboriginal society.
This contribution appeared in an appendix of Lévi-Strauss’s book (cf.
[
Lev3
]
).

This resulted in a long series of books “
Éléments de Mathématique
” that became very influential.
In a manifesto written by Bourbaki in 1950, some main principles of their structuralist view of mathematics were presented.

In one of these books (cf.
[
Bou
]
),
Bourbaki developed their theory of
structures in a set-theoretic manner; the building blocks are called
structure species
.
Roughly speaking, a structure species is a set, or a collection of sets, endowed with relations and operations not only among their members
but also among collections of elements of these sets, relations among them, etc.
A basic example is the structure species of ordered sets, where, from a set
S
, we obtain (by a suitable echelon construction scheme) the power set
P(S\times S)
of the product
S\times S
and a binary relation
s\in P(S\times S)
(called the typical characterization) equipped with a relation
describing the axiom of an ordered set.

In
[
ABla
]
, Blanchard introduced the concept of structure species on a category very close to the concept of structure species in the sense of Bourbaki and proved that
it is equivalent to the concept of structure species in the sense of Sonner (cf.
[
Son
]
).
The purpose of the present paper is to consider “structure of structures” formulated in terms of Blanchard’s structure species and investigate how this concept can capture the essence of things.
That is, we discuss the issue of how much information concerning a given category is contained in the knowledge about the
structure of structure species
on that category.

The following is the main result of the present paper, which
asserts that a category can be reconstructed, up to a certain slight indeterminacy,
from the category of structure species on it (the proof will be given in §
3
):

Theorem A
.

Let us fix a universe
\mathscr{U}
.
Let
X
and
X^{\prime}
be connected
\mathscr{U}
-small categories.
Denote by
\mathcal{S}p_{X}
and
\mathcal{S}p_{X^{\prime}}
the categories of structure species on
X
and
X^{\prime}
respectively.
Also, denote by
X^{\mathrm{op}}
the opposite category of
X
.
Then, the following two conditions are equivalent to each other:

(a)

X\cong X^{\prime}
or
X^{\mathrm{op}}\cong X^{\prime}
.

(b)

\mathcal{S}p_{X}\cong\mathcal{S}p_{X^{\prime}}
.

Here, given two categories
\mathcal{C}
,
\mathcal{D}
, we write
\mathcal{C}\cong\mathcal{D}
if
\mathcal{C}
is equivalent to
\mathcal{D}
.

1.3.

Finally, we remark that
Theorem
A
can be regarded as a variant of various reconstruction theorems that have been shown in the context of anabelian geometry (cf. Remark
3.4.2
).
In 1983, A. Grothendieck wrote a letter to G. Faltings (cf.
[
Gro2
]
), outlining what is today known as the anabelian conjectures.
(Many of the claims based on these conjectures have now been proved by mathematicians.)
These conjectures concern the possibility of reconstructing certain arithmetic varieties (e.g., hyperbolic curve over a number or a
p
-adic local field) from their étale fundamental groups.
Our study
is partially motivated by the anabelian philosophy of Grothendieck;
this is because a structure species, or equivalently a constructive functor (cf. Definition
2.3.1
, (i)), on a category
X
may be regarded, in some sense, as categorical realization of coverings over
X
(cf.
[
Son
]
, § 3).
Moreover, if
X
is a groupoid, then one can interpret Theorem
A
as a reconstruction assertion for
X
by means of the fundamental group associated with the Galois category of constructive functors over
X
(cf. Remark
2.5.3
).
Similar category-theoretic reconstructions can be found in
[
Moc1
]
,
[
Moc2
]
, and
[
Wak
]
.

2.
Structures species and constructive functors

In this section, we recall structure species on a category defined by Blanchard and the equivalence of structure species and constructive functors.
After that, we examine constructive functors on a groupoid.

2.1.
Preliminaries on categories

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a category.
We denote by
\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{C})
(resp.,
\overline{\mathrm{Ob}}(\mathcal{C})
; resp.,
\mathrm{Mor}(\mathcal{C})
) the set of objects (resp., the set of isomorphism classes of objects; resp., the set of morphisms) in
\mathcal{C}
.
For two objects
a,b\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{C})
, we denote by
\mathrm{Mor}_{\mathcal{C}}(a,b)
, or
\mathrm{Mor}(a,b)
, the set of morphisms
a\rightarrow b
in
\mathcal{C}
.
Also, we write

(1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Mor}\{a,b\}:=\mathrm{Mor}(a,b)\cup\mathrm{Mor}(b,a)

(hence
\mathrm{Mor}\{a,b\}=\mathrm{Mor}\{b,a\}
and
\mathrm{Mor}\{a,a\}=\mathrm{Mor}(a,a)
).
Also, write

(2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Mor}(a,b)^{\ncong}\ \left(\text{resp.},\ \mathrm{Mor}\{a,b\}^{\ncong}\right)

for the subset of
\mathrm{Mor}(a,b)
(resp.,
\mathrm{Mor}\{a,b\}
) consisting of non-invertible morphisms.
An object
a
in
\mathcal{C}
is said to be
minimal
if it is not an initial object and any monomorphism
b\hookrightarrow a
in
\mathcal{C}
, where
b
is not an initial object, is necessarily an isomorphism.

Next, we denote by
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}
the opposite category of
\mathcal{C}
.
Also, given each functor
F:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
, we shall write
F^{\mathrm{op}}:\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{op}}
for the functor between the respective opposite categories naturally induced by
F
.
We denote by
\mathcal{C}^{\cong}
the category whose objects are the elements of
\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{C})
and whose morphisms are the isomorphisms in
\mathrm{Mor}(\mathcal{C})
.
Given two categories
\mathcal{C}
,
\mathcal{D}
, we shall write
\mathcal{C}\cong\mathcal{D}
(resp.,
\mathcal{C}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{isom}}}{{\cong}}\mathcal{D}
) if
\mathcal{C}
is equivalent (resp., isomorphic) to
\mathcal{D}
.

Recall that a category
\mathcal{C}
is said to be a groupoid
if the morphisms in
\mathcal{C}
are all invertible.
By a
groupoid in
\mathcal{C}
, we mean a subcategory
\mathcal{D}
of
\mathcal{C}
forming a groupoid such that,
if
a,a^{\prime}
are objects in
\mathcal{D}
and
u:a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\rightarrow}}a
is an isomorphism in
\mathcal{C}
, then
u
lies in
\mathrm{Mor}(\mathcal{D})
.

A category
\mathcal{C}
is said to be a
preorder
if
\mathrm{Mor}(a,b)
has at most one element for all objects
a,b\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{C})
.
We write
a<b
if
\mathrm{Mor}(a,b)
is nonnempty;
the binary relation “
<
” in
\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{C})
is reflexive and transitive.
If in addition “
<
”
is symmetric,
\mathcal{C}
is said to be an
order
.
Note that “
<
” is symmetric precisely when the only isomorphisms of the preorder
\mathcal{C}
are the identity morphisms.
If
(T,<)
is a partial order, then we write
T^{\perp}
for the order defined in such a way that
\mathrm{Ob}(T^{\perp})=T
and
\mathrm{Mor}(a,b)\neq\emptyset
precisely when
a<b
.

Throughout the present paper, we shall fix a universe
\mathscr{U}
.
Denote by
\mathcal{S}et
the category consisting of
\mathscr{U}
-small sets and maps between them.
Denote by
\mathcal{C}at
the category consisting of
\mathscr{U}
-small categories and functors between them.
Moreover, denote by
\mathcal{O}rd
the full subcategory of
\mathcal{C}at
consisting of
\mathscr{U}
-small orders.
To each
\mathscr{U}
-small set
T
, we associate the category
\mathcal{D}is(T)
, whose objects are the elements in
T
and whose only morphisms are identity morphisms.
The assignment
T\mapsto\mathcal{D}is(T)
defines a functor
\mathcal{D}is:\mathcal{S}et\rightarrow\mathcal{O}rd
.

2.2.
Structure species

Let us recall the categorical reformulation of Bourbaki’s structure species discussed in
[
ABla
]
.
Fix a
\mathscr{U}
-small category
X
.

Definition 2.2.1

(cf.
[
ABla
]
, § 2, Definition 2.1)
.

Denote by
J:X^{\cong}\hookrightarrow X
the natural inclusion.

(i)

Let us consider a pair

(3)

\displaystyle\Sigma:=(E,S)

consisting of two functors
E:X\rightarrow\mathcal{O}rd
,
S:X^{\cong}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}et
.
We say that
\Sigma
is
a
(covariant) structure species
on
X

if the composite
\mathcal{D}is\circ S
is a subfunctor of
E\circ J
, meaning that,
for every
a\in\mathrm{Ob}(X^{\cong})
, the category
(\mathcal{D}is\circ S)(a)
is a subcategory of
(E\circ J)(a)
and the inclusion
(\mathcal{D}is\circ S)(a)\hookrightarrow(E\circ J)(a)
is functorial with respect to
a
.

(ii)

Let
\Sigma:=(E,S)
and
\Sigma^{\prime}:=(E^{\prime},S^{\prime})
be structure species on
X
.
A
morphism of structure species
from
\Sigma
to
\Sigma^{\prime}
is defines as a natural transformation
\phi:S\rightarrow S^{\prime}
such that, for every morphism
u:a\rightarrow b
in
X
and for every
U\in\mathrm{Ob}(S(a))
and
V\in\mathrm{Ob}(S(b))
, we have
E^{\prime}(u)(\phi_{a}(U))<\phi_{b}(V)
whenever
E(u)(U)<V
.

Remark 2.2.2
.

Let
\Sigma:=(E,S)
be a structure species on
X
.
As mentioned in the Remark following
[
ABla
]
, § 2, Definition 2.1,

E
and
S
respectively correspond to the
echelon
and
structure scheme
of
\Sigma
in the traditional terminology of Bourbaki (cf.
[
Bou
]
, Chap. IV, § 1).

Example 2.2.3

(Continuous maps on topological spaces)
.

For each
\mathscr{U}
-small set
T
, we shall write
P(T)
for the power set (i.e., the set of subsets) of
T
.
Let
P^{+}
(resp.,
P^{-}
) be the functor
\mathcal{S}et\rightarrow\mathcal{S}et
(resp.,
\mathcal{S}et^{\mathrm{op}}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}et
)
defined as follows:

•

For each object
T
in
\mathcal{S}et
, we set
P^{+}(T):=P(T)
(resp.,
P^{-}(T):=P(T)
).

•

For each morphism
f:T\rightarrow T^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{S}et
, we set
P^{+}(f)
(resp.,
P^{-}(f)
) to be the map
P^{+}(T)\rightarrow P^{+}(T^{\prime})
given by
U\mapsto f(U)
for every
U\in P^{+}(T)
(resp., the map
P^{-}(T^{\prime})\rightarrow P^{-}(T)
given by
U^{\prime}\mapsto f^{-1}(U^{\prime})
for every
U^{\prime}\in P(T^{\prime})
).

The maps
P^{+}(f)
and
P^{-}(f)
defined for
any morphism
f:T\rightarrow T^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{S}et

are non-decreasing when
both
P(T)\left(=P^{+}(T)=P^{-}(T)\right)
and
P(T^{\prime})\left(=P^{+}(T^{\prime})=P^{-}(T^{\prime})\right)
are equipped with the order structures determined by “
\subseteq
”.
Hence, the assignments
T\mapsto(P^{+}(T),\subseteq)^{\perp}
and
T\mapsto(P^{-}(T),\subseteq)^{\perp}
respectively induce functors
\mathcal{P}^{+}:\mathcal{S}et\rightarrow\mathcal{O}rd
and
\mathcal{P}^{-}:\mathcal{S}et^{\mathrm{op}}\rightarrow\mathcal{O}rd
.

Now, let
Z
be a
\mathscr{U}
-small subcategory of
\mathcal{S}et^{\mathrm{op}}
.
We write
E:=\mathcal{P}^{+}\circ P^{-}|_{Z}:Z\rightarrow\mathcal{S}et\rightarrow\mathcal{O}rd
.
For each
T\in\mathrm{Ob}(Z)
, denote the set of topologies on
T
by
\mathcal{T}op(T)
.
If
f:T\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\rightarrow}}T^{\prime}
is a bijection of
\mathscr{U}
-small sets, then
P^{+}(P^{-}(f))
induces a bijection
\mathcal{T}op(f):\mathcal{T}op(T^{\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathcal{T}op(T)
.
The assignments
T\mapsto\mathcal{T}op(T)
and
f\mapsto\mathcal{T}op(f)
together yield a functor

\mathcal{T}op:Z^{\cong}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}et
.
The resulting pair

(4)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\mathrm{top}}:=(E,\mathcal{T}op)

forms a structure species on
Z
.

We shall denote by

(5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}p_{X}

the category consisting of structure species on
X
and morphisms between them.
If there exists an equivalence of categories
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\rightarrow}}X^{\prime}
(where
X^{\prime}
is another
\mathscr{U}
-small category), then we can construct an equivalence of categories
\mathcal{S}p_{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathcal{S}p_{X^{\prime}}
in an evident manner.

2.3.
Constructive functors

Next, let us recall an equivalent realization of a structure species, which is a constructive functor in the sense of
[
ABla
]
.
In
[
Son
]
, § 2, Definition 3, this notion was introduced under the name “structure species”.

Definition 2.3.1

(cf.
[
ABla
]
, § 3, Definitions 3.1 and 3.2)
.

(i)

Let
F:Y\rightarrow X
be a functor between
\mathscr{U}
-small categories.
We say that
F
is a
constructive functor
on
X

(or simply,
F
is
constructive
)
if it satisfies the following conditions:

–

F
is faithful.

–

For every
a\in\mathrm{Ob}(Y)
and for every isomorphism
u
in
X
with domain
F(a)
,
there exists uniquely an isomorphism
u_{Y}
in
Y
with domain
a
satisfying the equality
F(u_{Y})=u
.

We often refer to the second condition as the
lifting property
on
F
.

(ii)

Let
F:Y\rightarrow X
and
F^{\prime}:Y^{\prime}\rightarrow X
be constructive functors on
X
.
A
morphism of constructive functors
from
F
to
F^{\prime}

is defined as a functor
\Phi:Y\rightarrow Y^{\prime}
satisfying the equality
F^{\prime}\circ\Phi=F
.

We shall denote by

(6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}on_{X}

the category consisting of constructive functors on
X
and morphisms between them.
One may verify that
\mathcal{C}on_{X}
admits finite coproducts and fiber products.
If
f:X^{\prime}\rightarrow X
is a functor between
\mathscr{U}
-small categories, then the assignment
Y\mapsto f^{*}(Y):=Y\times_{X}X^{\prime}
defines a functor

(7)

\displaystyle f^{*}:\mathcal{C}on_{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}on_{X^{\prime}}.

Here, we shall construct a constructive functor associated to a structure species.
Let
\Sigma:=(E,S)
be a structure species on
X
.
Denote by
Y_{\Sigma}
the category defined as follows:

•

The objects of
Y_{\Sigma}
are pairs
(a,T)
such that
a\in\mathrm{Ob}(X)
and
T\in S(a)
.

•

The morphisms from
(a,T)
to
(a^{\prime},T^{\prime})
are morphisms
u:a\rightarrow a^{\prime}
in
X
such that
E(u)(T)<T^{\prime}
, where “
<
” is the relation defined on the order
E(a^{\prime})
.
The composite law for morphisms in
Y_{\Sigma}
is defined in a natural manner.

Moreover, we set

(8)

\displaystyle F_{\Sigma}:Y_{\Sigma}\rightarrow X

to be the functor given by
(a,T)\mapsto a
and
u\mapsto u
.
This functor forms a constructive functor on
X
(cf.
[
ABla
]
, § 2, Propositions 2.1 and 2.2).
To a morphism of structure species
\phi:\Sigma\rightarrow\Sigma^{\prime}
, we can associate a morphism of constructive functors
F_{\phi}:F_{\Sigma}\rightarrow F_{\Sigma^{\prime}}
(but we will omit the details of this construction).
According to
[
ABla
]
, § 4, Theorem 2, the assignments
\Sigma\mapsto F_{\Sigma}
and
\phi\mapsto F_{\phi}
together define an equivalence of categories

(9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}p_{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathcal{C}on_{X}.

2.4.
Property of a constructive functor

In this section, we observe one property of a constructive functor (cf. Proposition
2.4.3
).
Before doing so, let us recall the connectedness of a category.

Definition 2.4.1
.

A category
\mathcal{C}
is said to be
connected
if it is nonempty and
for any pair of objects
a,b\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{C})
,
there exists a finite sequence
(c_{1},\cdots,c_{n})
of objects in
\mathcal{C}

such that
c_{1}=a
,
c_{n}=b
, and
\mathrm{Mor}\{c_{j},c_{j+1}\}\neq\emptyset
for any
j=1,\cdots,n-1
.

Example 2.4.2
.

A category
\mathcal{C}
is connected if it has either an initial object or a terminal object.
In particular, a category consisting of
\mathscr{U}
-small sets containing either the empty set or a singleton is connected.

Next, we prove the following assertion, which will be used in the subsequent discussion.

Proposition 2.4.3
.

Let
F:Y\rightarrow X
and
F^{\prime}:Y^{\prime}\rightarrow X
be constructive functors on
X
such that
\mathrm{Ob}(Y)\neq\emptyset
and
Y^{\prime}
is a connected groupoid.
Also, let
\Phi:F\rightarrow F^{\prime}
be a morphism of constructive functors.
Then, the maps
\mathrm{Ob}(\Phi):\mathrm{Ob}(Y)\rightarrow\mathrm{Ob}(Y^{\prime})
and
\mathrm{Mor}(\Phi):\mathrm{Mor}(Y)\rightarrow\mathrm{Mor}(Y^{\prime})
induced by
\Phi
are surjective.

Proof.

By assumption
\mathrm{Ob}(Y)\neq\emptyset
, there exists an object
a_{Y}
in
Y
.
Write
a_{Y^{\prime}}:=\Phi(a_{Y})
.
Now, let us take an arbitrary object
b_{Y^{\prime}}
in
Y^{\prime}
.
Since
Y^{\prime}
is a connected groupoid, we can find an isomorphism
u_{Y^{\prime}}:a_{Y^{\prime}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\rightarrow}}b_{Y^{\prime}}
in
Y^{\prime}
.
If we write
u:=F^{\prime}(u_{Y^{\prime}})
, then the lifting property on
F
implies that
there exists an isomorphism
u_{Y}:a_{Y}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\rightarrow}}b_{Y}
in
Y
(for some
b_{Y}\in\mathrm{Ob}(Y)
) with
F(u_{Y})=u
.
Since both
u_{Y^{\prime}}
and
\Phi(u_{Y})
are isomorphisms with domain
a_{Y^{\prime}}
lifting
u
,
the lifting property on
F
again implies that
u_{Y^{\prime}}=\Phi(u_{Y})
and hence
b_{Y^{\prime}}=\Phi(b_{Y})
.
This shows the surjectivity of
\mathrm{Ob}(\Phi)
.
The surjectivity of
\mathrm{Mor}(\Phi)
can be proved by using a similar argument, so we will finish the proof here.
∎

2.5.
Constructive functor on a groupoid

This subsection deals with
a specific constructive functor on a groupoid.
From Proposition
2.5.2
described later,
this constructive functor
can be thought of as the universal covering of a topological space or a graph (cf. Example
2.5.1
, Remark
2.5.3
).

Let
G
be a
\mathscr{U}
-small groupoid and
e
an object in
G
.
Suppose further that
G
is connected, which implies
\mathrm{Mor}(a,a^{\prime})\neq\emptyset
for any
a,a^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Ob}(G)
.
We shall set

Remark 2.5.4

The cultural model, as well as the paradigm for knowledge and practice, in the modern Western world envisioned by the structuralism of Lévi-Strauss and others is characterized by an organizational structure of an
arborescence
system that looks for the origin of ‘‘things” and for the culmination or conclusion of those ‘‘things”.
In this model, a small idea, like a seed,
takes root and grows into a tree with a sturdy trunk supporting numerous branches, all linked to and traceable back to the original idea.

‡
‡

‡

Due to criticism of how an object can be explained only with such a picture,
a perspective that focuses (not only on the origin and conclusion but) on the process of its creation and the possibility of its change arose later. It led to the establishment of the position known today as

.
In this context,

[

The results of Proposition

This section is devoted to proving Theorem

Remark 3.4.2
.

The reconstruction carried out in Theorem
A
is reminiscent of “Grothendieck conjecture”-type theorems in anabelian geometry.
Anabelian geometry is, roughly speaking, an area of arithmetic geometry that
discusses the issue of how much information concerning the geometry of certain arithmetic varieties (e.g., hyperbolic curves over a number field or a
p
-adic local field) is contained in the knowledge of the étale fundamental groups, or equivalently, the categories of finite étale coverings.
The classical point of view of anabelian geometry
centers around a comparison between two arithmetic varieties (or more generally, two geometric objects of the same kind) via their étale fundamental groups and it is referred to as
bi-anabelian geometry
.
On the other hand,

mono-anabelian geometry
, being an alternative and relatively new formulation, centers around the task of establishing a
group-theoretic algorithm whose input data consists of a single
abstract topological group isomorphic to the étale fundamental group of a single
geometric object.
In particular, it requires us to reconstruct, unlike the bi-anabelian formulation, the desired data without any mention of some “fixed reference model” copy of initial objects.
For basic references, we refer the reader to
[
Hos
]
,
[
Moc3
]
.

As explicitly verbalized in
[
Son
]
, § 3, constructive functors on a category
X
may be regarded, in some sense, as categorical realizations of coverings over
X
.
In fact, if
X
is a groupoid
G
, then the results of Proposition
2.5.2
enable us to consider the constructive functor
F_{G,e}:Y_{G,e}\rightarrow G
as if it were the universal covering of
G
.
Accordingly, we might say that
\mathcal{S}p_{X}
(
\cong\mathcal{C}on_{X}
by (
9
))
is like the Galois category consisting of finite étale coverings.
Notwithstanding the fact that Theorem
A
is stated in a completely bi-anabelian way,
the proof of the theorem actually furnishes a mono-anabelian algorithm that reconstructs
X
from
\mathcal{S}p_{X}
.
Here, however, we will omit the details of the formulation, as well as the proof, in that way.

Remark 3.4.3

C. Lévi-Strauss developed

Because he believed that there was no one authentic version of a myth, rather that they were all manifestations of the same language, he sought to find the fundamental units of myth, namely, the

The canonical formula of mythical transformation is an expression proposed in 1955 by Lévi-Strauss in order to account for the abstract relations occurring between characters and their attributes in a myth understood as the collection of its variants.
According to the canonical formula, a myth is reducible to an expression:

This formula describes a structural relationship between a set of narrative terms and their transmutative relationships.

]
for a reference on this formula.

Note that J. Morava tried to develop a truly mathematical argument for the canonical formula (cf.
[
Mor1
]
,
[
Mor2
]
).
He proposed to interpret it
as the existence of an anti-isomorphism of the quaternion group.
On the other hand, as a mathematical study of myths in another direction, we might expect that some kind of symmetry or structure on the whole category of structure species satisfying the condition expressed by (

) serves as a metaphorical explanation of some truth that Lévi-Strauss expected from myths.
However, at the time of writing the present paper, the author does not have any effective ideas for the development of this argument.

4.
Appendix: Category of categories over a category

In this Appendix, we establish, as an analogy of Theorem
A
,
the reconstruction of a category
X
from
the category of categories over
X
, i.e., the category of functors
F:Y\rightarrow X
(that are
not necessarily a structure species).
The conclusion differs from the case of Theorem
A
in that we can reconstruct (up to a certain indeterminacy) not only the equivalence class of
X
but also its isomorphism class.
The following Proposition
4.0.1
(resp.,
4.0.2
; resp.,
4.0.3
) will be proved by using a much simpler argument than was used in the corresponding previous assertion, i.e., Proposition
3.1.3
(resp.,
3.2.1
; resp.,
3.3.2
).
So we will leave their proofs to the reader.

Let
X
be a
\mathscr{U}
-small category.
Denote by

(47)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}at_{X}

the category defined as follows:

•

The objects are functors
of the form
F:Y\rightarrow X
, where
Y
is a
\mathscr{U}
-small category.

•

The morphisms from
F:Y\rightarrow X
to
F^{\prime}:Y^{\prime}\rightarrow X
are functors
\Psi:Y\rightarrow Y^{\prime}
satisfying
F=F^{\prime}\circ\Psi
.

The identity functor
\mathrm{id}_{X}:X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\rightarrow}}X
is a terminal object in this category.

For each object
e
in
X
, we shall write
Y_{e}:=\mathcal{D}is(\{e\})
and write

(48)

\displaystyle F_{e}:Y_{e}\hookrightarrow X

for the natural functor; this functor species an object in
\mathcal{C}at_{X}
.
We denote by

(49)

\displaystyle{{}^{\ddagger}}\mathrm{Ob}(X)\left(\subseteq\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{C}at_{X})\right)

the set of objects in
\mathcal{C}at_{X}
of the form
F_{e}
for some
e\in\mathrm{Ob}(X)
.
The assignment
e\mapsto F_{e}

gives a bijection of sets

(50)

\displaystyle\zeta_{X}:\mathrm{Ob}(X)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\rightarrow}}{{}^{\ddagger}}\mathrm{Ob}(X).

Proposition 4.0.1
.

Let
F:Y\rightarrow X
be an object in
\mathcal{C}at_{X}
.
Then,
F
is
minimal in
\mathcal{C}at_{X}

if and only if
F
is isomorphic to
F_{e}
for some
e\in\mathrm{Ob}(X)
.
In particular, the subset
{{}^{\ddagger}}\mathrm{Ob}(X)
of
\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{C}at_{X})
can be reconstructed category-theoretically from the data
\mathcal{C}at_{X}
(i.e., of a category).

Next, let
e
and
e^{\prime}
be (possibly the same) objects in
X
.
For each morphism
v:e\rightarrow e^{\prime}
in
X
, we shall set

Y_{v}

to be the subcategory of
X

satisfying
\mathrm{Ob}(Y_{v})=\{e,e^{\prime}\}
and
\mathrm{Mor}(Y_{v})=\{\mathrm{id}_{e},\mathrm{id}_{e^{\prime}},v\}
.
Denote by the natural inclusion by

(51)

\displaystyle F_{v}:Y_{v}\hookrightarrow X.

The inclusions
Y_{e}\hookrightarrow Y_{v}
and
Y_{e^{\prime}}\hookrightarrow Y_{v}
induce the morphism

(52)

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{v}:F_{e}\sqcup F_{e^{\prime}}\rightarrow F_{v}

in
\mathcal{C}at_{X}
.

We shall denote by

(53)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{e,e^{\prime}}

the category defined as follows:

•

The objects in
\mathcal{D}_{e,e^{\prime}}
are pair
(F,\Upsilon)
consisting of an object
F:Y\rightarrow X
in
\mathcal{C}at_{X}
and a non-invertible monomorphism
\Upsilon:F_{e}\sqcup F_{e^{\prime}}\hookrightarrow F
.

•

The morphisms from
(F,\Upsilon)
to
(F^{\prime},\Upsilon^{\prime})
are morphisms
\Psi:F\rightarrow F^{\prime}
satisfying
\Upsilon^{\prime}=\Psi\circ\Upsilon
.

For each element
v\in\mathrm{Mor}(e,e^{\prime})
,
the pair
(F_{v},\Upsilon_{v})
introduced above specifies an object in
\mathcal{D}_{e,e^{\prime}}
.
The assignment
(F,\Upsilon)\mapsto\Upsilon
defines a functor

(54)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{e,e^{\prime}}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}at_{X}.

It follows from Proposition
4.0.1
that the category
\mathcal{D}_{e,e^{\prime}}
together with the functor (
54
) can be reconstructed category-theoretically from the data
(\mathcal{C}at_{X},F_{e},F_{e^{\prime}})
(i.e., of a category and two minimal objects in this category).
Also, as in the case of “
\mathcal{C}_{e,e^{\prime}}
” introduced in §
3.2
, there exists a natural identification
\mathcal{D}_{e,e^{\prime}}=\mathcal{D}_{e^{\prime},e}
, by which we will not distinguish between
\mathcal{D}_{e,e^{\prime}}
and
\mathcal{D}_{e^{\prime},e}
.
In particular, this identification allows us to consider
(F_{w},\Upsilon_{w})
’s for various
w\in\mathrm{Mor}(e^{\prime},e)
as objects in
\mathcal{D}_{e,e^{\prime}}
.
We denote by

(55)

\displaystyle{{}^{\ddagger}}\mathrm{Mor}\{e,e^{\prime}\}

the set of morphisms in
\mathcal{C}at_{X}
of the form
\Upsilon_{v}
for some
v\in\mathrm{Mor}\{e,e^{\prime}\}
.
The assignment
v\mapsto\Upsilon_{v}
defines a bijection of sets

(56)

\displaystyle\zeta_{e,e^{\prime}}:\mathrm{Mor}\{e,e^{\prime}\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\rightarrow}}{{}^{\ddagger}}\mathrm{Mor}\{e,e^{\prime}\}.

Proposition 4.0.2
.

Let
(F,\Upsilon)
be an object in
\mathcal{C}at_{X}
.
The following two conditions are equivalent to each other:

•

(F,\Upsilon)
is minimal in
\mathcal{D}_{e,e^{\prime}}
and there is no triple of morphisms

(57)

\displaystyle(\Upsilon_{0}:F_{e}\rightarrow F_{0},\Upsilon^{\prime}_{0}:F_{e^{\prime}}\rightarrow F^{\prime}_{0},\Psi:F_{0}\sqcup F^{\prime}_{0}\rightarrow F)

in
\mathcal{C}at_{X}
such that
\Psi
is an isomorphism and satisfies the equality
\Upsilon=\Psi\circ(\Upsilon_{0}\sqcup\Upsilon^{\prime}_{0})
.

•

(F,\Upsilon)
is isomorphic to
(F_{v},\Upsilon_{v})
for some
v\in\mathrm{Mor}\{e,e^{\prime}\}
.

In particular, the subset
{{}^{\ddagger}}\mathrm{Mor}\{e,e^{\prime}\}
of
\mathrm{Mor}(\mathcal{C}at_{X})
can be reconstructed category-theoretically from the data
(\mathcal{C}at_{X},F_{e},F_{e^{\prime}})
(i.e., of a category and two minimal objects in this category).

Given three (possibly the same) objects
e,e^{\prime},e^{\prime\prime}
in
X
,
we shall write

(58)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Com}(e,e^{\prime},e^{\prime\prime})^{\mathcal{C}at}

\displaystyle:=\left\{(u,u^{\prime},u^{\prime\prime})\in\mathrm{Mor}(e,e^{\prime})\times\mathrm{Mor}(e^{\prime},e^{\prime\prime})\times\mathrm{Mor}(e,e^{\prime\prime})\,\Big|\,u^{\prime\prime}=u^{\prime}\circ u\right\},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Com}(e,e^{\prime},e^{\prime\prime})_{\mathrm{op}}^{\mathcal{C}at}

\displaystyle:=\left\{(u,u^{\prime},u^{\prime\prime})\in\mathrm{Mor}(e^{\prime},e)\times\mathrm{Mor}(e^{\prime\prime},e^{\prime})\times\mathrm{Mor}(e^{\prime\prime},e)\,\Big|\,u^{\prime\prime}=u\circ u^{\prime}\right\}.

Then, we have the following assertion.

Proposition 4.0.3
.

Let us choose
u\in\mathrm{Mor}\{e,e^{\prime}\}
,
u^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Mor}\{e^{\prime},e^{\prime\prime}\}
, and
u^{\prime\prime}\in\mathrm{Mor}\{e,e^{\prime\prime}\}
.
Then, the following two conditions are equivalent to each other:

•

Either
u\circ u^{\prime}
or
u^{\prime}\circ u
can be defined and one of the equalities
u^{\prime\prime}=u\circ u^{\prime}
,
u^{\prime\prime}=u^{\prime}\circ u
holds.

•

The colimit
F_{\mathrm{limit}}^{\mathcal{C}at}
of the diagram

\textstyle{F_{e}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathrm{inclusion}}
\scriptstyle{\mathrm{inclusion}}
\textstyle{F_{u^{\prime}}}
\textstyle{F_{u}}

exists in
\mathcal{C}at_{X}
, and there exists a monomorphism
F_{u^{\prime\prime}}\hookrightarrow F_{\mathrm{limit}}^{\mathcal{C}at}
in
\mathcal{C}at_{X}
via which the morphism
\Upsilon_{u^{\prime\prime}}:F_{e}\sqcup F_{e^{\prime\prime}}\hookrightarrow F_{u^{\prime\prime}}
is compatible with the morphism
F_{e}\sqcup F_{e^{\prime\prime}}\hookrightarrow F_{\mathrm{limit}}^{\mathcal{C}at}
arising naturally from
\Upsilon_{u}
and
\Upsilon_{u^{\prime}}
.

In particular, the image of
\mathrm{Com}(e,e^{\prime},e^{\prime\prime})^{\mathcal{C}at}\sqcup\mathrm{Com}(e,e^{\prime},e^{\prime\prime})^{\mathcal{C}at}_{\mathrm{op}}
via the bijection

(63)

\displaystyle\zeta_{e,e^{\prime},e^{\prime\prime}}:=\zeta_{e,e^{\prime}}\times\zeta_{e^{\prime},e^{\prime\prime}}\times\zeta_{e,e^{\prime\prime}}:

\displaystyle\ \mathrm{Mor}\{e,e^{\prime}\}\times\mathrm{Mor}\{e^{\prime},e^{\prime\prime}\}\times\mathrm{Mor}\{e,e^{\prime\prime}\}

\displaystyle\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\rightarrow}}{{}^{\ddagger}}\mathrm{Mor}\{e,e^{\prime}\}\times{{}^{\ddagger}}\mathrm{Mor}\{e^{\prime},e^{\prime\prime}\}\times{{}^{\ddagger}}\mathrm{Mor}\{e,e^{\prime\prime}\}

can be reconstructed category-theoretically from the data
(\mathcal{C}at_{X},F_{e},F_{e^{\prime}},F_{e^{\prime\prime}})
(i.e., of a category and three minimal objects in this category).

The previous three propositions enable us to prove the following assertion.
(The proof is entirely similar to the proof of Theorem
A
.)

Theorem 4.0.4
.

Let
X
and
X
be connected
\mathscr{U}
-small categories.
Then, the following conditions are equivalent to each other:

(a)

X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{isom}}}{{\cong}}X^{\prime}
or
X^{\mathrm{op}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{isom}}}{{\cong}}X^{\prime}
.

(b)

\mathcal{C}at_{X}\cong\mathcal{C}at_{X^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

The implication (a)
\Rightarrow
(b) follows immediately from
the existence of an equivalence of categories
\mathcal{C}at_{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathcal{C}at_{X^{\mathrm{op}}}
obtained by assigning

F\mapsto F^{\mathrm{op}}
for each
F\in\mathcal{C}at_{X}
.

Next, we shall consider the inverse direction (b)
\Rightarrow
(a).
Suppose that there exists an equivalence of categories
\beta:\mathcal{C}at_{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathcal{C}at_{X^{\prime}}
.
By Proposition
4.0.1
,
\beta
induces, via
\zeta_{X}
and
\zeta_{X^{\prime}}
, a bijection

(64)

\displaystyle\overline{\beta}:\mathrm{Ob}(X)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathrm{Ob}(X^{\prime}).

It follows from Proposition
4.0.2
that, for each
e,e^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Ob}(X)
, the equivalence
\beta
induces, via
\zeta_{e,e^{\prime}}
and
\zeta_{\overline{\beta}(e),\overline{\beta}(e^{\prime})}
,
a bijection of sets

(65)

\displaystyle\overline{\beta}_{e,e^{\prime}}:\mathrm{Mor}\{e,e^{\prime}\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathrm{Mor}\{\overline{\beta}(e),\overline{\beta}(e^{\prime})\}.

Moreover, by applying Proposition
4.0.3
and using the bijections
\zeta_{e,e^{\prime},e^{\prime\prime}}
and
\zeta_{\overline{\beta}(e),\overline{\beta}(e^{\prime}),\overline{\beta}(e^{\prime\prime})}
,
we obtain a bijection

(66)

\displaystyle\overline{\beta}_{e,e^{\prime},e^{\prime\prime}}:

\displaystyle\ \mathrm{Com}(e,e^{\prime},e^{\prime\prime})^{\mathcal{C}at}\sqcup\mathrm{Com}(e,e^{\prime},e^{\prime\prime})^{\mathcal{C}at}_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathrm{Com}(\overline{\beta}(e),\overline{\beta}(e^{\prime}),\overline{\beta}(e^{\prime\prime}))^{\mathcal{C}at}\sqcup\mathrm{Com}(\overline{\beta}(e),\overline{\beta}(e^{\prime}),\overline{\beta}(e^{\prime\prime}))^{\mathcal{C}at}_{\mathrm{op}}

for any
e,e^{\prime},e^{\prime\prime}\in\mathrm{Ob}(X)
.
If
X
has only one object
e
, then the bijections
\overline{\beta}
,
\overline{\beta}_{e,e}
, and
\overline{\beta}_{e,e,e}
show that

X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{isom}}}{{\cong}}X^{\prime}
.
Hence, it suffices to consider the case where
\sharp\mathrm{Ob}(X)\geq 2
.
Since
X
is assumed to be connected,
there exists a morphism
u_{0}:e_{0}\rightarrow e^{\prime}_{0}
in
X
with
e_{0}\neq e^{\prime}_{0}
.

Here, suppose that
\overline{\beta}_{e_{0},e^{\prime}_{0}}(u_{0})\in\mathrm{Mor}(\overline{\beta}(e_{0}),\overline{\beta}(e^{\prime}_{0}))
.
From the connectedness of
X
again,
one may verify (for the same reason as stated in the comment on Remark
3.3.3
) that

\overline{\beta}_{e,e^{\prime}}
is restricted to a bijection

(67)

\displaystyle\overline{\beta}^{\circledcirc}_{e,e^{\prime}}:\mathrm{Mor}(e,e^{\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathrm{Mor}(\overline{\beta}(e),\overline{\beta}(e^{\prime}))

for any
e,e^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Ob}(X)
.
Moreover,
\overline{\beta}_{e,e^{\prime},e^{\prime\prime}}
is restricted to a bijection

(68)

\displaystyle\overline{\beta}_{e,e^{\prime},e^{\prime\prime}}:\mathrm{Com}(e,e^{\prime},e^{\prime\prime})^{\mathcal{C}at}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathrm{Com}(\overline{\beta}(e),\overline{\beta}(e^{\prime}),\overline{\beta}(e^{\prime\prime}))^{\mathcal{C}at}

for any
e,e^{\prime},e^{\prime\prime}\in\mathrm{Ob}(X)
.
The bijections
\overline{\beta}
,
\overline{\beta}^{\circledcirc}_{e,e^{\prime}}
, and
\overline{\beta}^{\circledcirc}_{e,e^{\prime},e^{\prime\prime}}
for various
e,e^{\prime},e^{\prime\prime}\in\mathrm{Ob}(X)
yield an isomorphism of categories
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\rightarrow}}X^{\prime}
.

On the other hand, if
\overline{\beta}_{e_{0},e^{\prime}_{0}}(u_{0})\in\mathrm{Mor}(\overline{\beta}(e^{\prime}_{0}),\overline{\beta}(e_{0}))
,
then the problem, as in the proof of Theorem
A
, reduces to the previous case by using the equivalence of categories
\mathcal{C}at_{X^{\prime}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathcal{C}at_{X^{\prime\mathrm{op}}}
given by
F\mapsto F^{\mathrm{op}}
.
At any rate, we conclude that
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{isom}}}{{\cong}}X^{\prime}
or
X^{\mathrm{op}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{isom}}}{{\cong}}X^{\prime}
, as desired.
This completes the implication (b)
\Rightarrow
(a).
