Optimal control of SPDEs driven by time-space Brownian motion
Nacira Agram , Bernt Øksendal, Frank Proske, Olena Tymoshenko

Abstract
In this paper we study a Pontryagin type stochastic maximum principle for the optimal control of a system, where the state dynamics satisfy a stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE) driven by a two-parameter (time-space) Brownian motion (also called Brownian sheet). We first discuss some properties of a Brownian sheet driven linear SPDE which models the growth of an ecosystem. Further, applying time-space white noise calculus we derive sufficient conditions and necessary conditions of optimality of the control. Finally, we illustrate our results by solving a linear quadratic control problem and an optimal harvesting problem in the plane. We also study possible applications to machine learning.

Introduction

The purpose of this paper is to study optimal control of systems driven by the Brownian sheet.

Throughout this work, we denote by
\{B(t,x):t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}\}
a Brownian sheet and
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
a complete probability space on which we define the (completed)
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{F}_{t,x}
generated by
B(s,a),s\leq t,a\leq x
.

Wong & Zakai
[
WZ
]
generalised the notion of stochastic integrals with respect to 1-parameter Brownian motion to stochastic integrals driven by the two-parameter Brownian sheet.

Let us denote by
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
the positive quadrant of the plane and let
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
,
We define a first type stochastic integral with respect to the two-parameter Brownian motion in Cairoli
[
C72
]
denoted by:

\displaystyle\int_{R_{z}}\phi(\zeta)B(d\zeta)

and a second type
[
WZ74
]
stochastic integral denoted by

\displaystyle\int_{R_{z}}\int_{R_{z}}\psi(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime})B(d\zeta)B(d\zeta^{\prime}),

where
R_{z}=[0,t]\times[0,x]
if
z=(t,x)
.

In Wong & Zakai
[
WZ
]
, an Itô formula for stochastic integrals in the plane is given.

Example 1.1

(An optimal harvesting problem)

A classical model for the growth of an ecosystem (e.g. a population or a forest) with value
Y(t)
at time
t
in a random environment is the geometric Brownian motion, defined by the Itô stochastic differential equation (SDE)

dY(t)=\kappa Y(t)dt+\gamma Y(t)dB(t),\hskip 10.00002ptt\geq 0,\hskip 10.00002ptY(0)>0,

where
\kappa
and
\gamma
are given constants.
Equivalently, in terms of white noise
\overset{\bullet}{B}
and Wick product
\diamond
, the equation can be written

\frac{d}{dt}Y(t)=\kappa Y(t)+\gamma Y(t)\diamond\overset{\bullet}{B}(t),\hskip 10.00002ptY(0)>0,

where

\overset{\bullet}{B}(t)=\frac{d}{dt}B(t)\text{ is (time) white noise.}

A natural extension of this model to the case where the noise of the environment depends on both time
t
and position
x
, is the following SPDE in the value
Y(t,x)
of the ecosystem at time
t
and position
x
:

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t\partial x}Y(t,x)=\alpha_{0}(t,x)Y(t,x)+\beta_{0}(t,x)Y(t,x)\diamond\overset{\bullet}{B}(t,x),\hskip 10.00002ptY(0,0)>0,

(1.1)

where
\alpha_{0}(t,x)
and
\beta_{0}(t,x)
are given bounded deterministic functions, and

\displaystyle\overset{\bullet}{B}(t,x)=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t\partial x}B(t,x)\text{ is
time-space white noise.}

(1.2)

Alternatively, if we use the following result:

\int_{R_{z}}\varphi(s,a)B(ds,da)=\int_{R_{z}}\varphi(s,a)\diamond\overset{\bullet}{B}(s,a)dsda,\text{ }\hskip 9.24994pt\forall\varphi,t,x.

(1.3)

(see e.g. Holden et al
[
HØUZ
]
),
we can rewrite the equation (
1.1
) as:

\displaystyle Y(t,x)=Y(0,0)+\int_{R_{z}}\alpha_{0}(\zeta)Y(\zeta)d\zeta+\int_{R_{z}}\beta_{0}(\zeta)Y(\zeta)B(d\zeta).

(1.4)

Assume for simplicity that
\alpha_{0}
and
\beta_{0}
are constants.

If we at
(t,x)
harvest from
Y(t,x)
at the rate
u(t,x)
,
the dynamics (
1.1
) becomes

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t\partial x}Y_{u}(t,x)=\alpha_{0}Y_{u}(t,x)-u(t,x)+\beta_{0}Y_{u}(t,x)\diamond\overset{\bullet}{B}(t,x),

or, in integral form,

\displaystyle Y_{u}(t,x)

\displaystyle=Y(0,0)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}\{\alpha_{0}Y_{u}(s,a)-u(s,a)\}dsda

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}\beta_{0}Y_{u}(s,a)B(ds,da).

For given utility functions
U_{1},U_{2}
and given constants
T>0,X>0

define the combined utility of the harvesting and the terminal population by

J(u)=E\left[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{X}U_{1}(u(s,a))dsda+U_{2}(Y_{u}(T,X))\right].

We want to find the harvesting strategy
u^{\ast}(s,x)
which maximizes the
utility of the harvest, i.e.

J(u^{\ast})=\sup_{u\in\mathcal{A}}J(u).

Here the set of admissible controls is denoted by
\mathcal{A}.

Example 1.2

(A linear-quadratic (LQ) problem)

Consider the following linear-quadratic (LQ) control problem for time-space random fields:

Suppose the state
Y(t,x)
is given by

\displaystyle Y(t,x)=Y(0,0)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}u(s,a)dsda+\beta B(t,x),\hskip 9.24994ptt\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}.

We want to drive the state
Y
to 0 at time-space
(T,X)
with minimal use of energy. Hence
we put

J(u)=-\tfrac{1}{2}E\Big[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{X}u^{2}(s,a)dsda+\theta Y^{2}(T,X)\Big],

where
\theta>0
is a given constant.

The problem is to find
u^{\ast}\in\mathcal{A}
such that

\displaystyle J(u^{\ast})=\sup_{u\in\mathcal{A}}J(u).

(1.5)

Example 1.3

(A machine learning problem)

Consider the following hyperbolic SPDE:

Y(t,x)=y-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}u(s,a)\nabla f(Y(s,a))dsda+\sigma B(t,x),y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},t,x\geq 0\text{,}

(1.6)

where
B
is a Brownian sheet in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
\nabla f
is the gradient of a function
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R})
. Further,
u:\Omega\times\left[0,\infty\right)^{2}\longrightarrow\left[0,\infty\right)
is a stochastic learning rate
in time
and
space, which is assumed to be an adapted random field. By
formally setting
u=\eta\delta_{x}
in (
1.6
) for the Dirac delta
function
\delta_{x}
in a fixed point
x
and
\eta\geq 0
we get
(as a special case of (
1.6
) the SDE

dY_{t}=-\eta\nabla f(Y_{t})dt+\sigma dB_{t},Y_{0}=x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},t\geq 0\text{.}

(1.7)

The latter type of SDE (
1.7
) is used in
machine learning in connection with the stochastic gradient descent method
(SGD) to minimize or maximize the objective or loss function
f
. Since the
dynamics (
1.6
) is more general than that of (
1.7
), one may
replace for the sake of a deeper understanding of the classical SGD
approach (at the possible expense of numerical tractability) the equation (
1.7
) by (
1.6
) and study an optimal control problem with respect
to the (time-space) stochastic learning rate
u
.

In order to illustrate this application
in a simplified framework, we approximate
\nabla f
(for smooth
f
) by
Taylor‘s expansion in the case of
d=1
and consider in this paper the
controlled process

\displaystyle Y_{u}(t,x)

\displaystyle=Y(0,0)-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}u(s,a)Y_{u}(s,a)dsda

\displaystyle+\beta_{0}B(t,x),Y(0,0),\beta_{0}\in\mathbb{R},t,x\geq 0

with respect to (in this context) natural performance functional

J(u)=-E\left[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{X}u^{2}(s,a)dsda+\theta Y^{2}(T,X)\right]

for
\theta>0
.

2
A general formulation

The examples mentioned above are special cases of the following general optimal stochastic control problem:

We study optimal control of solutions
Y(t,x),t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}
of SPDEs of the form

Y_{u}(t,x)=Y(t_{0},x_{0})+\int_{R(t,x)}\alpha_{u}(Y_{u}(s,a))dsda+\int_{R(t,x)}\beta_{u}(Y_{u}(s,a))B(ds,da),

(2.1)

where

R(t,x)=R^{(t_{0},x_{0})}(t,x)=[t_{0},t]\times[x_{0},x],t\geq t_{0},x\geq x_{0},

and

B(t,x)=\text{Brownian sheet.}

The differential form of (
2.1
) is

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t\partial x}Y_{u}(t,x)=\alpha_{u}(Y_{u}(t,x))+\beta_{u}(Y_{u}(t,x))\diamond\overset{\bullet}{B}(t,x).

(2.2)

The identity of (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) comes from the fact that

\int_{R(t,x)}\varphi(s,a)B(ds,da)=\int_{R(t,x)}\varphi(s,a)\diamond\overset{\bullet}{B}(s,a)dsda,\text{ }\hskip 9.24994pt\forall\varphi,t,x.

(2.3)

See e.g. Holden et al
[
HØUZ
]
.

Remark 2.1

Hyperbolic SPDEs of the type (
1.1
) have been studied
over the years by several authors. See e.g. Cairoli
[
C72
]
and Yeh

[
Y
]
, who established strong existence and pathwise uniqueness of
solutions
Y
of the equation

\displaystyle Y(t,x)

\displaystyle=y_{0}+\int_{R(t,x)}b(s,a,Y(s,a))dsda

\displaystyle+\int_{R(t,x)}\sigma(s,a,Y(s,a))B(ds,da),y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(2.4)

when
b
and
\sigma
are Lipschitz continuous vector fields of linear
growth.

Further, smoothness of solutions to (
2.4
) in the sense of
Malliavin differentiability for sufficiently regular
b
and
\sigma
was
analysed in Nualart & Sanz
[
NS
]
. See also Bogso et al
[
BDMPP
]
, where the authors
construct Malliavin differentiable unique solutions to (
2.4
),
when the drift vector field
b
is merely bounded and measurable and
\sigma
is given by the unit matrix. As for other works in this direction (in the
case of both weak and strong solutions), we also refer to Yeh
[
Y87
]
and
[
Y85
]
.

In addition we refer to the book of Nualart
[
N
]
.

Finally, we want to point out
the interesting link between hyperbolic SPDEs and non-linear (random) wave
equations, when
d=1
and
b
,
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
:

By applying the orthogonal transformation
u=x+t,v=x-t
to the SPDE (
2.2
) we see that the corresponding differential
version of equation (
2.4
) can be transformed into the following
non-linear stochastic wave equation:

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}Y(t,x)-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}Y(t,x)=\sigma(Y(t,x))\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t\partial x}\widetilde{B}(t,x)+b(Y(t,x)),

where
\widetilde{B}
is another Brownian sheet, obtained by applying the inverse orthogonal transformation to
B
.
See e.g. Walsh
[
W
]

for
further details.

In the sequel we assume that the performance functional
J_{u}(t_{0},x_{0})
has the form

\small{J_{u}(t_{0},x_{0})=E^{(t_{0},x_{0})}\left[\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\int_{x_{0}}^{X}f_{u}(Y_{u}(s,a))dsda+g(Y_{u}(T,X))\right],}

(2.5)

and consider the following problem:

Problem 2.2

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a given family of admissible controls. Find an optimal control
u^{\ast}\in\mathcal{A}
and the value function
\Phi
such that

\Phi(t_{0},x_{0})=J_{u^{\ast}}(t_{0},x_{0})=\sup_{u\in\mathcal{A}}J_{u}(t_{0},x_{0}).

(2.6)

The control
u^{\ast}
is called an optimal control and the function
\Phi
is referred to as the value function of this problem.

We handle those problems by using a maximum principle approach. Therefore we need to study the adjoint equation given by a BSPDE in the plane. The existence and the uniqueness of solutions to such a BSPDE were proven in Zaidi & Nualart
[
ZN
]
for a particular Lipschitz constant.

3
A discussion of the solution of the SPDE

It is natural to ask what are the properties the solution
Y(t,x)
of the equation

\displaystyle Y(t,x)=Y(0,0)+\int_{R_{z}}\alpha_{0}(\zeta)Y(\zeta)d\zeta+\int_{R_{z}}\beta_{0}(\zeta)Y(\zeta)B(d\zeta).

(3.1)

For example, is
Y(t,x)
always positive?

To study this question, let us suppose that the coefficients
\alpha_{0},\beta_{0}
are bounded
deterministic functions. Then it follows from (
1.1
) that

\widetilde{Y(t,x)}(z)=Y(0,0)+\int_{R_{t,x}}\widetilde{K(s,a)}(z)\widetilde{Y(s,v)}(z)dsdv\text{,}

where
\widetilde{Y(t,x)}(z)
denotes the Hermite transform of
Y(t,x)
for
z\in\left(\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}}\right)_{c}
(set of all finite
sequences in
\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}}
) and where
R_{(t,x)}=\left[0,t\right]\times\left[0,x\right]
. Further,

K(s,a)=\alpha_{0}(s,a)+\beta_{0}(s,a)\overset{\bullet}{B}(s,a),

where

\overset{\bullet}{B}(s,a)=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial s\partial a}B(s,a)\text{ is }\text{white noise.}

(3.2)

See
[
HØUZ
]
for the properties of the Hermite transform.

Then, using Picard iteration, we find with
y_{0}=Y(0,0)>0
, the
semi-explicit solution

\displaystyle\widetilde{Y(t,x)}(z)

\displaystyle=y_{0}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\int_{R_{(t,x)}}\int_{R_{(s_{1},a_{1})}}...\int_{R_{(s_{n-1},a_{n-1})}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\widetilde{K(s_{j},a_{j})}(z)ds_{n}da_{n}...ds_{1}da_{1},

(3.3)

for
z\in\left(\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}}\right)_{c}
, where
s_{1}>...>s_{n}
,
a_{1}>...>a_{n}
.

By Theorem 2.11.4 in Holden et al
[
HØUZ
]

Y(t,x)
is positive if and only if for all
m
the function

g(y):=\widetilde{Y(t,x)}(iy)e^{-\tfrac{1}{2}|y|^{2}};\hskip 9.24994pti=\sqrt{-1},y=(y_{1},y_{2},...,y_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

(3.4)

is positive definite.

In this context, note that

\displaystyle\widetilde{K(s,a)}(iy)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{0}(s,a)+\beta_{0}(s,a)\widetilde{\overset{\bullet}{B}(s,a)}(iy)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{0}(s,a)+\beta_{0}(s,a)\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mu_{k}(s,a)iy_{k}.

(3.5)

Here
\mu_{k}(s,a)
is the
k
’th element in an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
consisting of tensor products of Hermite functions. See
Holden et al
[
HØUZ
]
, Section 2.2.1 and 2.2.2.

Combining the above we get

\displaystyle\widetilde{Y(t,x)}(iy)

(3.6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y_{0}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\int_{R(t,x)}\int_{R(s_{1},a_{1})}...\int_{R(s_{n-1},a_{n-1})}

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(\alpha_{0}(s_{j},a_{j})+i\beta_{0}(s_{j},a_{j})\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mu_{k}(s_{j},a_{j})y_{k}\right)ds_{n}da_{n}...ds_{1}da_{1}\text{.}

Therefore the positivity question is equivalent to the following:

Is for all
m=1,2.,...
the function
g:\mathbb{R}^{m}\mapsto\mathbb{C}
given by

\displaystyle g(y):=y_{0}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\int_{R(t,x)}\int_{R(s_{1},a_{1})}...\int_{R(s_{n-1},a_{n-1})}

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(\alpha_{0}(s_{j},a_{j})+i\beta_{0}(s_{j},a_{j})\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mu_{k}(s_{j},a_{j})y_{k}\right)ds_{n}da_{n}...ds_{1}da_{1}e^{-\tfrac{1}{2}|y|^{2}}

positive definite
?

It turns out that the latter is not true, in general. In what follows, we
want to give an explanation for this in the case of
\alpha_{0}=0
and
\beta_{0}
given by

\beta_{0}(s,a)=\beta_{1}(s)\beta_{2}(a)\text{,}

where
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
are bounded measurable functions. Assume
that
m=1
. We also note that we can write

\mu_{1}(s,a)=\xi_{1}(s)\xi_{2}(a)

with elements
\xi_{1}
and
\xi_{2}
of an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

In this case, we obtain the representation

\displaystyle\widetilde{Y(t,x)}(iy_{1})

(3.7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y_{0}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\int_{R(t,x)}\int_{R(s_{1},a_{1})}...\int_{R(s_{n-1},a_{n-1})}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(i\beta_{0}(s_{j},a_{j})\mu_{1}(s_{j},a_{j})y_{1}\right)ds_{n}da_{n}...ds_{1}da_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y_{0}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(n!)^{2}}\left(i\eta(t,x)y_{1}\right)^{n}\text{,}

where
\eta(t,x):=\int_{R(t,x)}\beta_{0}(s,a)\mu_{1}(s,a)dsda
. Let us now have a look at the expression

i^{k}\left(\eta(t,x)y_{1}\right)^{k}\exp(-\frac{1}{2}\left|y_{1}\right|^{2}).

Then for a standard normally distributed random variables
Z
, the inverse
Fourier transform of the latter is given by

\displaystyle i^{k}\frac{1}{(\sqrt{2\pi})^{1/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\eta(t,x)y_{1}\right)^{k}\exp(-\frac{1}{2}\left|y_{1}\right|^{2})\exp(iu_{1}y_{1})dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i^{k}E\left[\left(\eta(t,x)Z\right)^{k}\exp(iu_{1}Z)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\eta(t,x)\right)^{k}\frac{\partial^{k}}{\partial u_{1}^{k}}\varphi_{Z}(u_{1})=\left(\eta(t,x)\right)^{k}\frac{\partial^{k}}{\partial u_{1}^{k}}(\exp(-\frac{1}{2}u_{1}^{2}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\eta(t,x)\right)^{k}(-1)^{k}h_{k}(u_{1})\exp(-\frac{1}{2}u_{1}^{2})\text{,}

where
\varphi_{Z}
denotes the characteristic function of
Z
and where
h_{k}
is the
k
’th Hermite polynomial.

So, using dominated convergence, the inverse Fourier transform of the
function
g
(for
m=1
) is

b(u_{1})\exp(-\frac{1}{2}u_{1}^{2})\text{,}

(3.8)

where

b(u_{1}):=y_{0}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(n!)^{2}}\left(\eta(t,x)\right)^{n}(-1)^{n}h_{n}(u_{1})\text{.}

Let us now show that the function
b
cannot be non-negative. For this
purpose let us recall the following properties of Hermite polynomials:

h_{n}(x+y)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{n!}{(n-k)!k!}x^{n-k}h_{k}(y)

and

h_{k}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\phi(s,a)B(ds,da)\right)=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\phi(s,a)B(ds,da)\right)^{\diamond k}\text{,}

where
\diamond
denotes the Wick product and where
\phi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
with
\left\|\phi\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=1
(see

[
HØUZ
]
).

So we obtain that

\displaystyle h_{n}\left(c+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\phi(s,a)B(ds,da)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{n!}{(n-k)!k!}c^{n-k}h_{k}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\phi(s,a)B(ds,da)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(c+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\phi(s,a)B(ds,da)\right)^{\diamond n}

for all
c\in\mathbb{R}
. Assume that the above function
b
is
non-negative. Then the random variable

\displaystyle b\left(c+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\phi(s,a)B(ds,da)\right)

\displaystyle=y_{0}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{(n!)^{2}}\left(\eta(t,x)\right)^{n}h_{n}\left(c+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\phi(s,a)B(ds,da)\right)

\displaystyle=y_{0}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(n!)^{2}}\left(-\eta(t,x)(c+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\phi(s,a)B(ds,da))\right)^{\diamond n}\text{.}

must be non-negative, too, for all
c\in\mathbb{R}
.

Let
\phi
be the
first basis element of an orthornormal basis of (tensored) Hermite functions
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Then the Hermite transform of
b(c+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\phi(s,a)B(ds,da))
evaluated at
z=iy
,
y\in\mathbb{R}
is given
by

y_{0}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(n!)^{2}}\left(-\eta(t,x)(c+iy)\right)^{n}\text{.}

So using once again the positivity criterion in (
3.4
), the
expression

y_{0}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(n!)^{2}}\left(-\eta(t,x)(c+iy)\right)^{n}e^{-\tfrac{1}{2}|y|^{2}}

must be
in particular
for
y=0
non-negative (according to the
definition of a positive-definite function).

So for

f_{0}(r):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(n!)^{2}}r^{n}

the expression

y_{0}f_{0}(-\eta(t,x)c)

must be non-negative for all
c
. On the other hand, it is known that the
function
f_{0}
, which is related to the Bessel function of order zero, has the
following asymptotic property:

f_{0}(y)\sim(\pi\sqrt{\left|y\right|})^{-1/2}\cos(2\sqrt{\left|y\right|}-\frac{\pi}{4})\text{ for }y\rightarrow-\infty\text{.}

See e.g. Nualart
[
N87
]
. Hence, if
\eta(t,x)\neq 0
, the above
expression can become negative, which leads to a contradiction. So we
conclude that the function
b
and therefore also the function in (
3.8
) can become negative. So it follows from Bochner’s theorem that the
function
g
is not positive definite.

We get the following conclusion:

Theorem 3.1

The solution
Y(t,x)
of the equation (
3
) is not always positive

Remark 3.2

Using a different method, we mention that the latter result was obtained in
Nualart
[
N87
]
in the special case of
\alpha_{0}=0
and
\beta_{0}
a constant. There Nualart proves that
Y(t,x)<0
uniformly in
(t,x)
on an open set, with positive probability.

4
Background

4.1
The Itô formula

To study such optimal control problems, we will use a version of the Itô
formula for such systems. First we introduce some notation
from Wang & Zakai
[
WZ
]
.

•

We put
\zeta=(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})=(s,a)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
and

d\zeta=d\zeta_{1}d\zeta_{2}=dsda
,

•

B(t,x)
is a Brownian sheet;
t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}
,

•

z=(z_{1},z_{2})=(t,x),R_{z}=[0,z_{1}]\times[0,z_{2}]
,

•

\int_{R_{z}}\varphi(\zeta)B(d\zeta)

denotes the Itô
integral with respect to

B(\cdot)

over

R_{z}
,

•

\int_{R_{z}}\psi(\zeta)d\zeta
is 2-dimensional Lebesgue integral of
\psi
,

•

if
a=(a_{1},a_{2}),b=(b_{1},b_{2})
, then

a\vee b=(\max(a_{1},b_{1}),\max(a_{2},b_{2})).

•

I((a_{1},a_{2})\bar{\wedge}(b_{1},b_{2}))=1\newline
\text{ if }a_{1}\leq b_{1}\hskip 9.24994pt\&\hskip 9.24994pta_{2}\geq b_{2},\hskip 9.24994pt0

otherwise
.

Theorem 4.1
(Itô formula, Wang & Zakai
[
WZ
]
)

Suppose

Y(z)=Y_{0}+\int_{R_{z}}\alpha(\zeta)d\zeta+\int_{R_{z}}\beta(\zeta)B(d\zeta)+\iint\limits_{R_{z}\times R_{z}}\psi(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime})B(d\zeta)B(d\zeta^{\prime}).

(4.1)

Then, if
f:\mathbb{R\rightarrow R}
is smooth, we have

\displaystyle f(Y(z))=f(Y_{0})+\int_{R_{z}}f^{\prime}(Y(\zeta))[\alpha(\zeta)d\zeta+\beta(\zeta)B(d\zeta)]

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{R_{z}}f^{\prime\prime}(Y(\zeta))\beta^{2}(\zeta)d\zeta

\displaystyle+\iint\limits_{R_{z}\times R_{z}}\Big\{f^{\prime\prime}(Y(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime}))u_{k}\widetilde{u}_{l}+f^{\prime}(Y(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime}))\psi(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime})\Big\}B(d\zeta)B(d\zeta^{\prime})

\displaystyle+\iint\limits_{R_{z}\times R_{z}}\Big\{f^{\prime\prime}(Y(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime}))\Big(u_{k}\alpha(\zeta)+\psi(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime})\widetilde{u}_{l}\Big)

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{2}f^{(3)}(Y(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime}))u^{2}_{k}\widetilde{u}_{l}\Big\}d\zeta B(d\zeta^{\prime})

\displaystyle+\iint\limits_{R_{z}\times R_{z}}\Big\{f^{\prime\prime}(Y(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime}))\Big(\widetilde{u}_{l}\alpha(\zeta^{\prime})+\psi(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime})u_{k}\Big)

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{2}f^{(3)}(Y(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime}))u^{2}_{k}\widetilde{u}_{l}\Big\}d\zeta B(d\zeta^{\prime})

\displaystyle+\iint\limits_{R_{z}\times R_{z}}I(\zeta\bar{\wedge}\zeta^{\prime})\Big\{f^{\prime\prime}(Y(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime}))\Big(\alpha(\zeta^{\prime})\alpha(\zeta)+\tfrac{1}{2}\psi^{2}(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime})\Big)

\displaystyle+f^{(3)}(Y(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime}))u\widetilde{u}\psi(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime})+\tfrac{1}{2}f^{(3)}(Y(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime}))\left[\alpha(\zeta^{\prime})\widetilde{u}^{2}+\alpha(\zeta)u^{2}\right]

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{4}f^{(4)}(Y(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime}))u^{2}\widetilde{u}^{2}\Big\}d\zeta d\zeta^{\prime},

where

u=\beta(\zeta^{\prime})+\int_{R_{z}}I(\zeta\bar{\wedge}\zeta^{\prime})\psi(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime})B(d\zeta),

\widetilde{u}=\beta(\zeta)+\int_{R_{z}}I(\zeta\bar{\wedge}\zeta^{\prime})\psi(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime})B(d\zeta^{\prime}).

It is proved in
[
WZ
]
that the double
B(d\zeta)B(d\zeta^{\prime})
-integrals, and the mixed
d\zeta B(d\zeta^{\prime})
and
B(d\zeta)d\zeta^{\prime}
-integrals are all weak martingales and hence have expectation
0
. Therefore, by the Itô formula above we get the following:

Theorem 4.2

(Dynkin formula)

\displaystyle E[f(Y(z))]

\displaystyle=f(Y_{0})+E\Big[\int_{R_{z}}\Big\{\alpha(\zeta)f^{\prime}(Y(\zeta))+\frac{1}{2}\beta^{2}(\zeta)f^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(Y(\zeta))\Big\}d\zeta

\displaystyle+\iint\limits_{R_{z}\times R_{z}}I(\zeta\bar{\wedge}\zeta^{\prime})\Big\{f^{\prime\prime}(Y(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime}))\Big(\alpha(\zeta^{\prime})\alpha(\zeta)+\tfrac{1}{2}\psi^{2}(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime})\Big)

\displaystyle+f^{(3)}(Y(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime}))u_{k}\widetilde{u}_{l}\psi(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime})

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{2}f^{(3)}(Y(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime}))\left[\alpha(\zeta^{\prime})\widetilde{u}^{2}_{l}+\alpha(\zeta)u^{2}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{4}f^{(4)}(Y(\zeta\vee\zeta^{\prime}))u^{2}_{k}\widetilde{u}^{2}_{l}\Big\}d\zeta d\zeta^{\prime}\Big].

Lemma 4.3
(Integration by parts)

Suppose that

\displaystyle Y_{k}(z)=Y_{k}(0)+\int_{R_{z}}\alpha_{k}(\zeta)d\zeta+\int_{R_{z}}\beta_{k}(\zeta)B(d\zeta)+\iint\limits_{R_{z}\times R_{z}}\psi_{k}(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime})B(d\zeta)B(d\zeta^{\prime}).\hskip 9.24994ptk=1,2.

Then

\displaystyle E[Y_{1}(z)Y_{2}(z)]=Y_{1}(0)Y_{2}(0)

\displaystyle+E\Big[\int_{R_{z}}\Big\{Y_{1}(\zeta)\alpha_{2}(\zeta)+Y_{2}(\zeta)\alpha_{1}(\zeta)+\beta_{1}(\zeta)\beta_{2}(\zeta)\Big\}d\zeta

\displaystyle+\iint\limits_{R_{z}\times R_{z}}I(\zeta\bar{\wedge}\zeta^{\prime})\Big\{\alpha_{1}(\zeta^{\prime})\alpha_{2}(\zeta)+\alpha_{1}(\zeta)\alpha_{2}(\zeta^{\prime})+\psi_{1}(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime})\psi_{2}(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime})\Big\}d\zeta d\zeta^{\prime}\Big].

Proof.  The proof follows from the Itô formula, Proposition 5.1 in Wang & Zakai
[
WZ
]
.

\square

5
BSPDEs in the plane

To simplify the notation we sometimes put
z=(t,x),\zeta=(s,a)
in the following:

Let
\mathcal{P}
be the predictable
\sigma
-algebra of subsets of
\Omega\times R_{z_{0}}
generated by the sets
(z,z^{\prime}]\times A
where
A\in\mathcal{F}_{z}
and we denote by
\mathcal{D}
the
\sigma
-algebra of
\Omega\times R_{z_{0}}\times R_{z_{0}}
generated by the sets
(z_{1},z_{1}^{\prime}]\times(z_{2},z_{2}^{\prime}]\times A
where
(z_{1},z_{1}^{\prime}]\bar{\wedge}(z_{2},z_{2}^{\prime}]
and
A\in\mathcal{F}_{z_{1}\vee z_{2}}
.

The solution of the BSDE will live in the following spaces:

•

L^{2}_{a,1}
is the space of predictable processes {
\phi(z),z\in R_{z_{0}}
}, such that

E[\int_{R_{z}}\phi(z)^{2}dz<\infty]
,

•

L^{2}_{a,2}
is the space of processes {
\psi(z,z^{\prime}),(z,z^{\prime})\in R_{z_{0}}\times R_{z_{0}}
}, such that

(a)

\psi(z,z^{\prime})=0
unless
z\bar{\wedge}z^{\prime}
,

(b)

\psi
is
\mathcal{D}
-measurable,

(c)

E[\int_{R_{z}}\int_{R_{z}}\psi(z,z^{\prime})^{2}dzdz^{\prime}<\infty]
.

Let us recall now the representation of square integrable martingales.

Theorem 5.1
(Wong & Zakai
[
WZ
]
)

If
M=\{M(z),\mathcal{F}_{z},z\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}\}
is square integrable martingale, then for each
z\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}

\displaystyle M(z)=M(0)+\int_{R_{z}}\phi(\zeta)B(d\zeta)+\iint\limits_{R_{z}\times R_{z}}\psi(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime})B(d\zeta)B(d\zeta^{\prime}),

(5.1)

where
\phi,\psi
are adapted processes.

Let
Z=(T,X)
and if we fix a rectangle
R_{Z}=[0,T]\times[0,X]
, and let
\xi
be an
\mathcal{F}_{Z}
-measurable random variable and
h(\omega,\zeta,p,q)
is a
\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{R}}\times\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{R}}
-measurable function such that
\int_{R_{z}}|h(\zeta,p(\zeta),q(\zeta))|d\zeta<\infty
. Then we can define a triple of processes
(p,q,r)\in L^{2}_{a,1}\times L^{2}_{a,1}\times L^{2}_{a,2}
solution of the BSPDE in the plane

\displaystyle p(z)

\displaystyle=\xi-\int_{R_{Z}\setminus R_{z}}h(\zeta,p(\zeta),q(\zeta))d\zeta-\int_{R_{Z}\setminus R_{z}}q(\zeta)B(d\zeta)

\displaystyle-\int_{R_{Z}\setminus R_{z}}\int_{R_{Z}\setminus R_{z}}r(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime})B(d\zeta)B(d\zeta^{\prime}).

(5.2)

Alternatively, let us introduce the notation

\displaystyle M_{r}(z)=\iint\limits_{R_{z}\times R_{z}}r(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime})B(d\zeta)B(d\zeta^{\prime}),\hskip 9.24994ptr\in L_{a,2}.

(5.3)

Then
M_{r}(z)
is a martingale, and we can write the equation for
(p,q,r)
above in differential form as follows

\displaystyle p(dz)

\displaystyle=h(z,p(z),q(z))dz+q(z)B(dz)+M_{r}(dz),\hskip 9.24994ptz\leq Z,

(5.4)

\displaystyle p(Z)

\displaystyle=\xi.

Assumptions
We impose the following set of assumptions:

(i)

\xi\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{z_{0}},P)
,

(ii)

h(\cdot,p,q)\in L^{2}_{a,1}
for all
p,q\in\mathbb{R}
,

(ii)

|h(\zeta,p,q)-h(\zeta^{\prime},p^{\prime},q^{\prime})|^{2}\leq K_{1}|p-p^{\prime}|^{2}+K_{2}|q-q^{\prime}|^{2}
, for all
p,q,p^{\prime},q^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\zeta\in R_{Z}.

Let
f_{0}
be the Bessel function of order zero and
r_{0}\approx 1.4458
be the first nonnegative zero of
J_{0}
:

r_{0}=inf\left\{t>0:f_{0}(2\sqrt{t})=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{{j!}^{2}}t^{j}=0\right\}.

Theorem 5.2
(Existence and Uniqueness (Zaidi & Nualart)
[
ZN
]
)

Under the above assumptions (i)-(iii) and if the Lipschitz constant satisfies
K_{1}|z_{0}|<\sqrt{r_{0}}
and
K_{2}|z_{0}|<\sqrt{r_{0}}
,
there exists a unique solution of the BSPDE (
5
).

6
Maximum principle approaches

We introduce the following notation:

Definition 6.1

For general functions
h,k:[0,T]\times[0,X]\mapsto\mathbb{R}
, we define

\displaystyle(h\star k)(t,x)=\int_{0}^{x}\int_{-t}^{T}\{h(t,x)k(s,a)+h(s,a)k(t,x)\}dsda

(6.1)

Note that with this notation we have

\displaystyle\iint\limits_{R_{z}\times R_{z}}I(\zeta\bar{\wedge}\zeta^{\prime})\Big\{\alpha_{1}(\zeta^{\prime})\alpha_{2}(\zeta)+\alpha_{1}(\zeta)\alpha_{2}(\zeta^{\prime})\Big\}d\zeta d\zeta^{\prime}=\int_{R_{z}}(\alpha_{1}\star\alpha_{2})(\zeta)d\zeta,

(6.2)

and the integration by parts formula can be written

Lemma 6.2
(Integration by parts (2))

Suppose that

\displaystyle Y_{k}(z)=Y_{k}(0)+\int_{R_{z}}\alpha_{k}(\zeta)d\zeta+\int_{R_{z}}\beta_{k}(\zeta)B(d\zeta),\hskip 9.24994ptk=1,2.

Then

\displaystyle E[Y_{1}(z)Y_{2}(z)]=Y_{1}(0)Y_{2}(0)

\displaystyle+E\Big[\int_{R_{z}}\Big\{Y_{1}(\zeta)\alpha_{2}(\zeta)+Y_{2}(\zeta)\alpha_{1}(\zeta)+\beta_{1}(\zeta)\beta_{2}(\zeta)+(\alpha_{1}\star\alpha_{2})(\zeta)\Big\}d\zeta\Big].

(6.3)

Given a subset
U
of
\mathbb{R}
we denote by
\mathcal{U}
the set of all
\mathcal{F}_{t,x}
-adapted control processes
u=\{u(t,x),t<T,x<X\}
valued in
U
. We then define the set of admissible control processes
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{U}
to be the collection of all
\mathcal{F}_{t,x}
-adapted processes with values in
U
.

Let
f
and
g
be given functions and consider
the performance functional

\displaystyle J(u)=E\Big[\int_{R_{Z}}f(\zeta,Y(\zeta),u(\zeta))d\zeta+g(Y(Z))\Big],

where

R_{Z}=[0,T]\times[0,X],

with
Z=(T,X)
for some given
T>0,X>0
, and the state
Y
of the system is described by the equation

\displaystyle Y(z)=Y(t,x)=Y(0)+\int_{R_{z}}\alpha(\zeta,Y(\zeta),u(\zeta))d\zeta+\int_{R_{z}}\beta(\zeta,Y(\zeta),u(\zeta))B(d\zeta),\hskip 9.24994ptz\leq Z,

(6.4)

where
R_{z}=[0,t]\times[0,x]
when
z=(t,x)
, and
u
denotes a control process.

Problem 6.3

We want to find
\widehat{u}\in\mathcal{A}
such that

J(\widehat{u})=\sup_{u\in\mathcal{A}}J(u).

(6.5)

The maximum principle approach to this problem, adapted to the time-space situation, is to introduce the following associated Hamiltonian

H:R_{Z}\times R_{Z}\times\mathbb{R}\times U\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}

given by

\displaystyle H(z,Y,u,p,q,L)=f(z,y,u)+p\alpha(z,y,u)+q\beta(z,y,u)+(L\star\alpha)(z),

(6.6)

where we recall that

(L\star\alpha)(z)=\int_{0}^{x}\int_{t}^{T}\Big\{L(z)\alpha(\zeta^{\prime},y,u)+L(\zeta^{\prime})\alpha(z,y,u)\Big\}d\zeta^{\prime};\hskip 9.24994ptz=(t,x)

and the adjoint processes
(p,q,r,L)=(p(t,x),q(t,x),r(t,x,\cdot),L(t,x))
are given by the following equations:

•

L:R_{Z}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is defined implicitly by the integral equation

\displaystyle L(z)=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial y}(z,Y(z),u(z),p(z),q(z),r(z,\cdot),L(z));\hskip 9.24994ptz=(t,x)

(6.7)

•

and
(p,q,r)
is the solution of the BSPDE

\displaystyle\begin{cases}p(dz)&=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial y}(z,Y(z),p(z),q(z),r(z,\cdot),L(z))dz\\
&+q(z)B(dz)+M_{r}(dz),\hskip 9.24994pt0\leq(t,x)\leq(T,X),\\
p(Z)&=\frac{\partial g}{\partial y}(Y(Z)),\end{cases}

(6.8)

or, in integrated form,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}p(z)&=p(0)-\int_{R_{z}}\frac{\partial H}{\partial y}(\zeta,Y(\zeta),p(\zeta),q(\zeta),r(\zeta,\cdot),L(\zeta))d\zeta\\
&+\int_{R_{z}}q(\zeta)B(d\zeta)+\iint\limits_{R_{z}\times R_{z}}r(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime})B(d\zeta)B(d\zeta^{\prime}),\hskip 9.24994ptz\leq Z.\\
p(Z)&=\frac{\partial g}{\partial y}(Y(Z))\end{cases}

(6.9)

There are two versions of the maximum principle for this problem, namely the so-called
sufficient maximum principle
and the
necessary maximum principle
.
We present them both below.

6.1
The sufficient maximum principle

Theorem 6.4
(Sufficient maximum principle)

Suppose
\widehat{u}\in\mathcal{A}
with corresponding solutions
\widehat{Y},(\widehat{p},\widehat{q},\widehat{r},\widehat{L})
of the equations above. Moreover, suppose that

y\mapsto g(y)
is concave and
Y,u\mapsto H(z,Y,u,p,q,r,L)
is concave for all
z,p,q,r,L
and that

\displaystyle\small\sup_{v\in A}

\displaystyle H(z,\widehat{Y}(\cdot),v,\widehat{p}(z),\widehat{q}(z),\widehat{r}(z,\cdot),\widehat{L}(z))

(6.10)

\displaystyle=H(z,\widehat{Y}(\cdot),\widehat{u}(z),\widehat{p}.(z),\widehat{q}(z),\widehat{r}(z,\cdot),\widehat{L}(z)),

(6.11)

Then
\widehat{u}
is an optimal control for problem (
6.5
).

Proof.  Suppose
\widehat{u}\in\mathcal{A}
satisfies (
6.10
) with corresponding
\widehat{Y}
. Choose another
u\in\mathcal{A}
. Then

\displaystyle J(u)-J(\widehat{u})=I_{1}+I_{2},

(6.12)

where

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=E\Big[\int_{R_{Z}}\Big\{f(\zeta,Y(\zeta),u(\zeta))-f(\zeta,\widehat{Y}(\zeta),\widehat{u}(\zeta))\Big\}d\zeta\Big]=E\Big[\int_{R_{Z}}\tilde{f}(\zeta)d\zeta\Big],

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=E[g(Y(Z))-g(\widehat{Y}(Z))].

Using the definition of
H
we can write

\displaystyle I_{1}=E\Big[\int_{R_{Z}}\Big\{H(\zeta)-\widehat{H}(\zeta)-\widehat{p}(\zeta)\widetilde{\alpha}(\zeta)-\widehat{q}(\zeta)\widetilde{\beta}(\zeta)-(\widehat{L}\star\tilde{\alpha})(\zeta)\Big\}d\zeta\Big],

(6.13)

where
\tilde{\alpha}=\alpha-\widehat{\alpha},\widehat{\alpha}=\alpha(\zeta,\widehat{Y}(\zeta),\widehat{u}(\zeta))
etc.

Using the concavity of
g
and Lemma
6.2
, and the fact that the
B(dz)
-integrals and the
B(dz)B(dz^{\prime})
-integrals are orthogonal (see
[
CW
]
, Theorem 2.5), we get

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\leq E\Big[\frac{\partial g}{\partial y}(\widehat{Y}(Z))\widetilde{Y}(Z)\Big]=E\Big[\widehat{p}(Z)\tilde{Y}(Z)\Big]

\displaystyle=E\Big[\int_{R_{Z}}\Big\{\widehat{p}(\zeta)\widetilde{\alpha}(\zeta)-\frac{\partial\widehat{H}}{\partial y}\tilde{Y}(\zeta)+\widehat{q}(\zeta)\tilde{\beta}(\zeta)-(\frac{\partial\widehat{H}}{\partial y}\star\widetilde{\alpha})(\zeta)\Big\}d\zeta\Big].

(6.14)

Adding (
6.13
) and (
6.14
) we get, using that

\widehat{L}=-\frac{\partial\widehat{H}}{\partial y}
and the concavity of
H(y,u)
,

\displaystyle J(u)-J(\widehat{u})

\displaystyle=I_{1}+I_{2}\leq E\Big[\int_{R_{Z}}\Big\{H(\zeta)-\widehat{H}(\zeta)-\frac{\partial\widehat{H}}{\partial y}(\zeta)\tilde{Y}(\zeta)\Big\}d\zeta\Big]

(6.15)

\displaystyle\leq E\Big[\int_{R_{Z}}\frac{\partial\widehat{H}}{\partial u}(\zeta)\tilde{u}(\zeta)\rangle d\zeta\Big]\leq 0\text{ by condition \eqref{max.cond}}.

(6.16)

This proves that

\displaystyle J(u)-J(\widehat{u})\leq 0\text{ for all }u\in\mathcal{A},

(6.17)

and therefore
\widehat{u}
is optimal.

\square

6.2
The necessary maximum principle

It is a drawback of the sufficient maximum principle that we have to assume that
y\longmapsto g(y)
and
(y,u)\longmapsto H(z,y,u,p,q,L)
are concave. The following result does not need concavity, but we have to add conditions of the set
\mathcal{A}
of admissible controls instead, as follows:

(A1)

\mathcal{A}
is a convex set

(A2)

For all
z_{0}=(t_{0},x_{0})<Z=(T,X)

and all bounded
\mathcal{F}_{z_{0}}
-measurable random variables
\theta_{z_{0}}
, the control

u_{z_{0}}(\zeta)=\theta_{z_{0}}\mathbf{1}_{R_{z_{0}}}(\zeta)

is admissible, where

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{R_{z_{0}}}(\zeta)=\begin{cases}1\text{ if }\zeta\in R_{z_{0}}\\
0\text{ if }\zeta\notin R_{z_{0}}\end{cases}

(6.18)

is the indicator function of the rectangle
R_{z_{0}}=[t_{0},T]\times[x_{0},X]
.

Lemma 6.5

For all
u,v\in\mathcal{A}
the derivative process

G(\zeta):=\underset{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}{\lim}\frac{Y^{u+\epsilon v}(\zeta)-Y^{u}(\zeta)}{\epsilon}

satisfies the equation

\displaystyle G(z)

\displaystyle=G(0)+\int_{R_{z_{0}}}\Big\{\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial y}(\zeta)G(\zeta)+\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial u}(\zeta)v(\zeta)\Big\}d\zeta

\displaystyle+\int_{R_{z_{0}}}\Big\{\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial y}(\zeta)G(\zeta)+\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial u}(\zeta)v(\zeta)\Big\}B(d\zeta),

where
\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial y}(\zeta)=\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial y}(\zeta,Y^{u}(\zeta),u(\zeta))
etc.

Proof.  This follows by the chain rule.

\square

Lemma 6.6

For all
u,v\in\mathcal{A}
, we have

\frac{d}{d\epsilon}J(u+\epsilon v)_{\epsilon=0}=E\Big[\int_{R_{Z}}\frac{\partial H}{\partial u}(\zeta)v(\zeta)d\zeta\Big].

Proof.

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\epsilon}

\displaystyle J(u+\epsilon v)_{\epsilon=0}

\displaystyle=\underset{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}{lim}\frac{1}{\epsilon}E\Big[\int_{R_{Z}}\Big\{f(\zeta,Y^{u+\epsilon v}(\zeta),u+\epsilon v)(\zeta))-f(\zeta,Y^{u}(\zeta),u)(\zeta))\Big\}d\zeta

\displaystyle+g(Y^{u+\epsilon v}(Z))-g(Y^{u}(Z))\Big]

\displaystyle=E\Big[\int_{R_{Z}}\Big\{\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(\zeta,Y^{u}(\zeta),u(\zeta))G(\zeta)+\frac{\partial f}{\partial u}(\zeta,Y^{u}(\zeta),u(\zeta))v(\zeta)\Big\}d\zeta

\displaystyle+\frac{\partial g}{\partial y}(Y^{u}(Z))G(Z)\Big]=I_{1}+I_{2},

where

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=E\Big[\int_{R_{Z}}\Big(\frac{\partial H}{\partial y}(\zeta)-\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial y}(\zeta)p(\zeta)-\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial y}(\zeta)q(\zeta)-(L\star\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial y})(\zeta)\Big)G(\zeta)d\zeta

\displaystyle+\int_{R_{Z}}\Big(\frac{\partial H}{\partial u}(\zeta)-\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial u}(\zeta)p(\zeta)-\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial u}(\zeta)q(\zeta)-(L\star\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial u}(\zeta)\Big)v(\zeta)d\zeta\Big],

and

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=E\Big[\frac{\partial g}{\partial y}(Y^{u}(Z))G(Z)\Big]=E[p(Z)G(Z)]

\displaystyle=E\Big[\int_{R_{Z}}\Big(p(\zeta)\Big\{\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial y}(\zeta)G(\zeta)+\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial u}(\zeta)v(\zeta)\Big\}-\frac{\partial H}{\partial y}(\zeta)G(\zeta)

\displaystyle+q(\zeta)\Big\{\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial y}(\zeta)G(\zeta)+\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial u}(\zeta)v(\zeta)\Big\}-\big\{(\frac{\partial H}{\partial y}\star(\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial y}G+\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial u}v)\big\}(\zeta)\Big\}d\zeta\Big].

Adding
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
, we get, if we choose

L=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial y}
,

\frac{d}{d\epsilon}J(u+\epsilon v)_{\epsilon=0}=E\Big[\int_{R_{Z}}\frac{\partial H}{\partial u}(\zeta)v(\zeta)d\zeta\Big].

\square

From Lemma 6.4, we deduce the following:

Theorem 6.7
(Necessary maximum principle)

Suppose
\widehat{u}\in\mathcal{A}
is optimal for Problem 2.5. Then

\displaystyle\frac{\partial H}{\partial u}(\zeta,\widehat{Y}(\zeta),\widehat{u}(\zeta),\widehat{p}(\zeta),\widehat{q}(\zeta),\widehat{L}(\zeta))=0\text{ for a.a. }\zeta.

Proof.  Since
J(\widehat{u}+\epsilon v)_{\epsilon=0}\leq J(\widehat{u})
for all
\epsilon,v,
we get by Lemma 6.4 that

E\Big[\int_{R_{Z}}\frac{\partial H}{\partial u}(\zeta)v(\zeta)d\zeta\Big]\leq 0,\text{ for all }v\in\mathcal{A}.

In particular, applying this to

v(\zeta)=\theta_{z_{0}}\mathbf{1}_{R_{z_{0}}}(\zeta)

as in A2, this gives

E\Big[\int_{R_{z_{0}}}\frac{\partial H}{\partial u}(\zeta)\theta_{z_{0}}d\zeta\Big]\leq 0.

Since this holds for all
z_{0}
we deduce that

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t_{0}\partial x_{0}}\left(E\left[\int_{R_{z_{0}}}\frac{\partial H}{\partial u}\theta_{z_{0}}d\zeta\right]\right)=\frac{\partial H}{\partial u}(z_{0})\theta_{z_{0}}\leq 0.

Since this holds for all
\theta_{z_{0}}\in\mathbb{R}
, we conclude that

\frac{\partial H}{\partial u}(z_{0})=0.

\square

7.1
Return to the optimal harvesting problem in the plane

Suppose that the growth of a population at time
t
and position
x
with density
Y(t,x)
satisfies

\displaystyle Y_{u}(t,x)

\displaystyle=Y(0,0)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}\{\alpha_{0}Y_{u}(s,a)-u(s,a)\}dsda

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}\beta_{0}Y_{u}(s,a)B(ds,da),

where
\alpha_{0},\beta_{0}
are given constants and
Y(0,0)>0.

For given constants
T>0,X>0
such that
T>t,X>x
, define the combined utility of the harvesting and the terminal population by

J(u)=E\left[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{X}ln(u^{2}(s,a))dsda+\theta Y_{u}(T,X)\right],

where
\theta
is a given bounded,
\mathcal{F}_{Z}
-measurable random variable.

Problem 7.1

We want to find the harvesting strategy
u^{\ast}(s,x)
which maximizes the
utility of the harvest, i.e. we want to find
u^{\ast}\in\mathcal{A}
such that

\displaystyle J(u^{\ast})=\sup_{u\in\mathcal{A}}J(u).

The associated Hamiltonian for this case is

\displaystyle H(t,x,y,u,p,q,L)=ln(u^{2})+(\alpha_{0}y-u)p+\beta_{0}yq+(L\star(\alpha_{0}y-u)).

The Hamiltonian has a maximum at
u
given by the equation

\frac{\partial H}{\partial u}=\frac{2}{u(z)}-p-(L\star 1)(z)=0.

Hence we must have

u=\frac{2}{p+(L\star 1)},

where

\displaystyle(L\star 1)(z)=x(T-t)L(z)+\int_{0}^{x}\int_{t}^{T}L(\zeta^{\prime})d\zeta^{\prime}.

(7.1)

Since

\displaystyle\frac{\partial H}{\partial y}=\alpha_{0}p+\beta_{0}q+(L\star\alpha_{0})

(7.2)

the corresponding adjoint equation is

\displaystyle\begin{cases}p(dz)&=-(\alpha_{0}p+\beta_{0}q+L\star\alpha_{0})(z)dz\\
&+q(z)B(dz)+M_{r}(dz),\hskip 9.24994pt0\leq(t,x)\leq(T,X),\\
p(Z)&=\frac{\partial g}{\partial y}(Y(Z)).\end{cases}

(7.3)

The adjoint variable
L
is defined by

\displaystyle L(z)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial y}(z)=-[\alpha_{0}p(z)+\beta_{0}q(z)+(L\star 1)(z)]

\displaystyle=-[\alpha_{0}p(z)+\beta_{0}q(z)+x(T-t)L(z)+\int_{0}^{x}\int_{t}^{T}L(\zeta^{\prime})d\zeta^{\prime},

(7.4)

which is an integral equation for
L(z)
.

We summarise this as follows:

Theorem 7.2

Let
z>0
and assume that
p(z)+(L\star 1)(z)\neq 0
. Then the optimal harvesting rate
u^{*}
for Problem
7.1
is given by

\displaystyle u^{*}(t,x)=u^{*}(z)=\frac{2}{p(z)+(L\star 1)(z)},

with
p
and
L
given by (
7.3
) and (
7.1
), respectively.

7.2
Return to the linear-quadratic (LQ) problem in the plane

We now return to the linear-quadratic (LQ) control problem for time-space random fields discussed in the introduction:

Suppose the state
Y(t,x)
is given by

\displaystyle Y(t,x)=Y(0,0)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}u(s,a)dsda+\beta B(t,x);\hskip 9.24994ptt\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}.

(7.5)

We want to drive the state
Y(t,x)
to 0 at time
T
and point
X
with minimal use of energy. Hence
we put

J(u)=-\tfrac{1}{2}E\left[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{X}u^{2}(s,a)dsda+\theta Y^{2}(T,X)\right],

(7.6)

where
\theta>0
is a given constant.

Problem 7.3

We want to find
u^{\ast}\in\mathcal{A}
such that

\displaystyle J(u^{\ast})=\sup_{u\in\mathcal{A}}J(u).

(7.7)

In this case we have
\nabla_{y}H=0
, so we can simplify the Hamiltonian to

\displaystyle H_{0}(t,x,y,u,p,q)=-\tfrac{1}{2}u^{2}+up+\beta q

(7.8)

The maximum of
u\mapsto H_{0}(u)
is obtained when
\frac{\partial H_{0}}{\partial u}=-u+p=0
, i.e. when

u=p.

(7.9)

The adjoint equation is

\displaystyle p(dz)

\displaystyle=q(z)B(dz);\hskip 9.24994ptz<Z=(T,X)

\displaystyle p(T,X)

\displaystyle=\theta Y(T,X)

(7.10)

Let us try to put

\displaystyle p(t,x)=\lambda(t,x)Y(t,x)

(7.11)

for some deterministic function
\lambda
.
Then by the Itô formula

\displaystyle\lambda(t,x)Y(t,x)

\displaystyle=\lambda(0,0)Y(0,0)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}\Big\{Y(s,a)\frac{\partial^{2}\lambda}{\partial s\partial x}(s,a)+\lambda(s,a)u(s,a)\Big\}dsda

(7.12)

\displaystyle+\text{ terms containing }B(ds,da).

Using the concept of quadratic variation of
2-
parameter martingales (see e.g. Imkeller
[
I2
]
), one finds that the decomposition of a (continuous)
2-
parameter ”semimartingale”, which is given by a sum of a
2-
parameter process of bounded variation and a
2-
parameter martingale, is unique. So, comparing the latter equation with the adjoint equation, we see that we must have

\displaystyle Y(t,x)\frac{\partial^{2}\lambda}{\partial t\partial x}(t,x)+\lambda(t,x)u(t,x)=0\text{ for all }t,x.

(7.13)

Combining this with (
7.11
) we get

\displaystyle Y(t,x)\Big[\frac{\partial^{2}\lambda}{\partial t\partial x}(t,x)+\lambda^{2}(t,x)\Big]=0,

(7.14)

with terminal condition

\displaystyle\lambda(T,X)=\theta.

(7.15)

In addition we get from (
7.11
) the other boundary condition

\displaystyle\lambda(0,0)=\frac{E[\theta Y(T,X)]}{Y(0,0)}.

(7.16)

With this choice of
u,p,\lambda
we see that all the conditions of the sufficient maximum principle are satisfied, and we have proved the following:

Theorem 7.4

The optimal control
\widehat{u}
for the LQ problem (
7.3
) is given in feedback form by

\displaystyle\widehat{u}(t,x)=\lambda(t,x)Y(t,x);\hskip 9.24994ptt\leq T,x\leq X,

(7.17)

where
\lambda(t,x)
solves the time-space Riccati equation

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{\partial^{2}\lambda}{\partial t\partial x}(t,x)+\lambda^{2}(t,x)=0;\hskip 9.24994pt0\leq t\leq T,0\leq x\leq X,\\
\lambda(T,X)=\theta,\\
\lambda(0,0)=\frac{E[\theta Y(T,X)]}{Y(0,0)}.\end{cases}

(7.18)

Remark 7.5

Let
\varphi_{1}
be a solution to the Riccati equation

\dot{\varphi}_{1}(t)=(\varphi_{1}(t))^{2},\varphi_{1}(0)=1,0\leq t\leq T

and
\varphi_{2}
be a solution to

\dot{\varphi}_{2}(x)=-(\varphi_{2}(x))^{2},\varphi_{2}(0)=\theta,0\leq x\leq X.

Define
\lambda(t,x)=

\alpha_{1}(t)\alpha_{2}(x)
, where
\alpha_{1}(t):=\varphi_{1}(T-t)
and
\alpha_{2}(x):=\varphi_{2}(X-x)
. Then

\frac{\partial^{2}\lambda(t,x)}{\partial t\partial x}=\dot{\varphi}_{1}(T-t)\dot{\varphi}_{2}(X-x)=(\alpha_{1}(t))^{2}(-(\alpha_{2}(t))^{2})=-(\lambda(t,x))^{2}

with
\lambda(T,X)=\varphi_{1}(0)\varphi_{2}(0)=\theta
.

By
solving the Riccati equations, we find that
\lambda
given by

\lambda(t,x)=\frac{1}{(1-T+t)(\theta^{-1}+X-x)}

is an explicit solution to the above hyperbolic PDE with boundary condition
\lambda(T,X)=\theta
for
0<T<1
.

Let us now have a look at the other
condition

\lambda(0,0)=\frac{\theta E\left[Y(T,X)\right]}{Y(0,0)}

We observe
that

E\left[Y(t,x)\right]=Y(0,0)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}\lambda(s,a)E\left[Y(s,a)\right]dsda\text{.}

So, if we use Picard iteration combined with the fact that
\lambda
can be written as a product of a function in
t
and another function in
x
, we see that the solution of the latter equation has the
representation

E\left[Y(t,x)\right]=Y(0,0)f\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}\lambda(s,a)dsda\right),

where the function
f:\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is defined by

f(y)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{y^{n}}{(n!)^{2}}\text{.}

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{X}\lambda(s,a)dsda

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{X}\frac{1}{(1-T+s)(\theta^{-1}+X-a)}dsda

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\log(1-T)\log(1+X\theta).

So the condition

\lambda(0,0)=\frac{\theta E\left[Y(T,X)\right]}{Y(0,0)}

is
equivalent to

\frac{1}{(1-T)(\theta^{-1}+X)}=\theta f(-\log(1-T)\log(1+X\theta))

or

1=(1-T)(1+X\theta)f(-\log(1-T)\log(1+X\theta)).

(7.19)

For given
T<1
and
\theta>0
the expression on the right hand side of the
latter equation converges to
(1-T
) for
X\rightarrow 0
. For
X\rightarrow\infty
, this expression converges to
\infty
. Because of
continuity, we then see that there exists a
X=X(T,\theta)>0
such that the
equation (
7.19
) is satisfied. Using such a time horizon
X
,
gives the other boundary condition.

7.3
Example related to machine learning

In machine learning the (continuous-time) stochastic gradient descent method
(see e.g.
[
MS
]
and the references therein) is used to minimize an
objective function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Compared to the classical gradient descent method without noise, this approach is
especially computationally efficient, when the dimension
d
in practical optimization problems is high. If the objective
function is sufficiently smooth the critical points of
f
with respect to
local or global minima may be found by means of solutions to SDEs of the
type

dY_{t}=-\eta\nabla f(Y_{t})dt+\beta_{0}dB_{t},Y_{0}=x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},t\geq 0\text{,}

(7.20)

where
\eta\geq 0
is the learning rate (or step size),
\beta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
B_{t},t\geq 0
a Brownian motion and where
\nabla

denotes the gradient of a function. In general, the selection of an
”optimal” learning rate
\eta
, which determines the optimal step size
towards a minimum in the sense of speed, is in general difficult. If
\eta

is chosen too small, the solution may converge too slowly to a critical point.
On the other hand, a too large
\eta
could result in overshoot or
divergence. In order to gain a deeper understanding of the latter problem,
one may consider instead of the SDE (
7.20
) a more general framework
(at the possible expense of computational cost) in connection with the
following type of hyperbolic SPDE:

Y(t,x)=y-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}u(s,a)\nabla f(Y(s,a))dsda+\beta_{0}B(t,x),y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},t,x\geq 0\text{,}

(7.21)

where
u:\Omega\times\left[0,\infty\right)^{2}\longrightarrow\left[0,\infty\right)
is a stochastic learning rate in time and space given by
an adapted random field and where
B
is a Brownian sheet in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Formally, by choosing in (
7.21
)
u=\eta\delta_{x}
for the
Dirac delta function
\delta_{x}
in a fixed point
x
and
\eta\geq 0
we obtain an SDE of the type (
7.20
). So the random field dynamics (
7.21
) provides a more general framework than that in the one-parameter case (
7.20
) for finding the critical points of
f
.

On the other hand, we may
view the integral term

\int_{0}^{x}u(s,a)\nabla f(Y(s,a))da

in (
7.21
) for a fixed
x
and a certain class of stochastic
2-
parameter learning rate processes as an (weighted) average of
\nabla f(Y(s,a))
,
0\leq a\leq x
in
(
7.20
). Here
Y(s,a),0\leq a\leq x
can be interpreted as a group of mountain hikers in the optimisation landscape who communicate with each other with respect to (average) gradient information in order to find the descent to the valley (i.e. minimum).

The latter, combined with the ”exploration ability”
of the Brownian sheet with respect to the spatial parameter direction in the
optimisation landscape, suggests a solution that converges to rather flat
minima, while escaping from sharp minima. The convergence to flat minima, however, is in many applications a
favorable feature from a machine learning point of view (see
[
HS
]
).

In order to construct optimal stochastic
2-
parameter learning rate
processes one may e.g study stochastic control problems based on the
stochastic maximum principle for SPDEs driven by a Brownian sheet with
respect to certain performance functionals as e.g.

J(u)=-E\left[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{X}u^{2}(s,a)dsda+f(Y(T,X))\right]\text{,}

(7.22)

where one mimimises the expected value of
f(Y(T,X))
, while the ”energy invested” in
u
is kept minimal.

Using the first order Taylor expansion, we can also approximate
\nabla f

in (
7.21
) by an affine function
g
given by
g(x)=a+Ax
for
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and obtain a more simplified framework for our
stochastic control problem with respect to
u
. In this setting, let us now
consider the case
d=1
and the following controlled process:

\displaystyle Y_{u}(t,x)

\displaystyle=Y(0,0)-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}u(s,a)Y_{u}(s,a)dsda+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}\beta_{0}B(ds,da).

Problem 7.6

We want to maximise the performance functional

J(u)=-E\Big[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{X}u^{2}(s,a)dsda+\theta Y^{2}(T,X)\Big].

In this case the associated Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle H(t,x,y,u,p,q,L)=-u^{2}-yup+\beta_{0}q-(L\star(uy)),

which gives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial H}{\partial y}=-up-(L\star u)

(7.23)

and hence the adjoint variable
L
is given by

\displaystyle L=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial y}=up+(L\star u)

(7.24)

which is an integral equation for
L(z)
.

The adjoint BSDE is

\displaystyle p(z)

\displaystyle=p(0)+\int_{R_{z}}(u(\zeta)p(\zeta)+(L\star u))(\zeta)d\zeta

\displaystyle-\int_{R_{z}}q(\zeta)B(d\zeta)-\iint\limits_{R_{z}\times R_{z}}r(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime})B(d\zeta)B(d\zeta^{\prime}),\hskip 9.24994ptz\leq(T,X)

(7.25)

\displaystyle p(T,X)

\displaystyle=-2\theta Y(T,X).

(7.26)

In order to maximize
H
with respect to
u
, we first compute

\frac{\partial H}{\partial u}=-2u-yp-(L\star y)

which gives the optimal control

u=u^{*}=\tfrac{1}{2}(yp+(L\star y))

We have proved:

Theorem 7.7

The optimal control
u(z)=u^{*}(x)
for Problem
7.6
is
given as the 4th component of the solution
(Y(z),L(z),p(z),u(z))
of the coupled system of equations, consisting of

\displaystyle u(z)

\displaystyle=u^{*}(z)=\tfrac{1}{2}[Y(z)p(z)+(L\star Y)(z)],

(7.27)

\displaystyle Y(t,x)

\displaystyle=Y(0,0)-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}u(s,a)Y_{u}(s,a)dsda+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}\beta_{0}B(ds,da),

(7.28)

\displaystyle L(z)

\displaystyle=u(z)p(z)+(L\star u)(z)

(7.29)

and

\displaystyle\begin{cases}p(z)&=p(0)+\int_{R_{z}}(u(\zeta)p(\zeta)+(L\star u))(\zeta)d\zeta\\
&-\int_{R_{z}}q(\zeta)B(d\zeta)-\iint\limits_{R_{z}\times R_{z}}r(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime})B(d\zeta)B(d\zeta^{\prime}),\hskip 9.24994ptz\leq(T,X)\\
p(T,X)&=-2\theta Y(T,X).\end{cases}

(7.30)

Remark 7.8

In the more general case, when
\triangledown f(x)=Ax
for
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, one can show a similar result about the optimal control
u^{\ast}
with
respect to the controlled process (
7.21
) and performance functional
(
7.22
