Scattering of surface waves by ocean currents: the U2H map
Han Wang 1, Ana B. Villas Bôas 2, Jacques Vanneste 1, William R. Young 3

Abstract
Ocean turbulence at meso- and submesocales affects the propagation of surface waves through refraction and scattering, inducing spatial modulations in significant wave height (SWH). We develop a theoretical framework that relates these modulations to the current that induces them. We exploit the asymptotic smallness of the ratio of typical current speed to wave group speed to derive a linear map – the U2H map – between surface current velocity and SWH anomaly. The U2H map is a convolution, non-local in space, expressible as a product in Fourier space by a factor independent of the magnitude of the wavenumber vector. Analytic expressions of the U2H map show how the SWH responds differently to the vortical and divergent parts of the current, and how the anisotropy of the wave spectrum is key to large current-induced SWH anomalies. We implement the U2H map numerically and test its predictions against WAVEWATCH III numerical simulations for both idealised and realistic current configurations.

Introduction

Surface gravity waves (SGWs) propagate through currents resulting from ocean meso- and submesoscale turbulence and from the surface expression of tides and internal gravity waves. Much work, both historical and recent, has focussed on the effect of internal waves and tides on SGWs
(
Barber 1949
;
Perry & Schimke 1965
;
Phillips 1977
;
Osborne & Burch 1980
;
Tolman 1990
;
Hao & Shen 2020
;
Ho et al. 2023
, e.g.)
. Recognition of the role of meso- and submesoscale turbulence in shaping the open-ocean surface wave field is comparatively recent and relies on ocean observations and modeling
(
Holthuijsen & Tolman 1991
;
Romero et al. 2017
;
Ardhuin et al. 2017
;
Romero et al. 2020
;
Villas Boâs et al. 2020
)
.
In this work we develop a theoretical framework that can be used to understand the effect of meso- and submesoscale turbulence on SGWs.

Refraction and scattering of open-ocean deepwater SGWs by eddies, fronts, filaments and vortices results in fluctuations in significant wave height (SWH) with length scales reflecting those of the underlying turbulent field – see figure
1
. Fluctuations in SWH modulate SGW breaking and thus affect all aspects of air-sea exchange
(
Cavaleri et al. 2012
;
Villas Bôas et al. 2019
)
. There are also implications for the formation of extreme waves and remote sensing.

Scale separation between SGW wavelengths and the larger spatial scale of the currents makes it possible to adopt a phase-averaged description, focusing on the density of wave action
\mathcal{A}({\bm{x}},{\bm{k}},t)

in the position–wavevector
({\bm{x}},{\bm{k}})
-space. Action density satisfies a transport equation which, because of its high dimensionality, poses analytic and numerical challenges, even when linearised by neglecting wave–wave interactions. As a result, numerous open questions remain about the relation between the currents and the fluctuations in SWH they induce.
Some of these questions can be addressed by numerical solution of the transport equation
(
Villas Boâs et al. 2020
)
. These computations are costly and the results can be difficult to interpret.

In §
2
we develop an alternative approach that directly links SGW amplitude to current, reducing computational costs and providing new insights. This approach relies on the smallness of the ratio
\varepsilon
between the typical current speed
U
and the SGW group speed
c_{g}
:

\varepsilon=U/c_{g}\ll 1.

(1)

SGWs with wavelengths greater than 10 m have group speed in excess of 2 m s
-1
. For these relatively long waves
\varepsilon\ll 1
holds in all but the most extreme ocean conditions.
In a steady-state scenario and in the absence of current, that is, for
\varepsilon=0
, the action density
\mathcal{A}({\bm{x}},{\bm{k}})
can be taken as spatially uniform,
\bar{\mathcal{A}}({\bm{k}})
say. For
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
and neglecting wind forcing, dissipation and wave-wave interactions, the current induces a small,
O(\varepsilon)

{\bm{x}}
-dependent modulation so that the current-perturbed action spectrum is
\mathcal{A}({\bm{x}},{\bm{k}})=\bar{\mathcal{A}}({\bm{k}})+a({\bm{x}},{\bm{k}})
.
At leading order, the anomaly
a({\bm{x}},{\bm{k}})
is linearly related to both the surface current
\bm{U}({\bm{x}})
and the background wave action
\bar{\mathcal{A}}
.
Similarly the anomaly of any measure of wave amplitude deduced from
\mathcal{A}({\bm{x}},{\bm{k}})
is linearly related to
\bm{U}({\bm{x}})
and
\bar{\mathcal{A}}({\bm{k}})
.

:

(
Villas Boâs et al. 2020
)
, with the arrow indicating the primary direction of wave propagation. SWH anomaly

, is narrow banded in frequency (with periods around 10.3 s and wavelength 165.5 m) and angle (with peak angle
\theta_{p}=0
and width parameter

).

https://shorturl.at/bef14
.

We focus on SWH and obtain an explicit form for the linear map that relates the SWH anomaly
h_{s}({\bm{x}})
to the current velocity
\bm{U}({\bm{x}})
. This map, which we term U2H map, turns out to be a convolution, best expressed as

\frac{\hat{h}_{s}}{\bar{H}_{s}}=\hat{\bm{L}}\bcdot\hat{\bm{U}}

(2)

in terms of the Fourier transforms
\hat{h}_{s}
of
h_{s}
and
\hat{\bm{U}}
of
\bm{U}
, and of the mean SWH
\bar{H}_{s}
.

In section
3
we obtain several alternative and approximate expressions for the transfer function
\hat{\bm{L}}
that embodies the U2H map. Depending on details of the currents
\bm{U}
and the base-state action spectrum
\bar{\mathcal{A}}({\bm{k}})
one of these different expression of
\hat{\bm{L}}
may be most effective.

We show a complicated example in figure
1
. Panel (a) shows the surface current speed in a simulation of the California Current system. Panel (b) shows the SWH anomaly
h_{s}
computed using WAVEWATCH III (Wave Height, Water Depth, and Current Hindcasting third generation wave model, hereafter WW3) which solves the four-dimensional transport equation satisfied by the action
\mathcal{A}({\bm{x}},{\bm{k}},t)

(
Tolman et al. 2009
)
. Panel (c) shows the result of applying the U2H map to the current in panel (a). The computational details for panels (b) and (c) are described in §
4.1
. The match between the results of the (computationally expensive) WW3 computation in (b) and of the (much cheaper) application of U2H in (c) is excellent. Throughout the paper we assess the accuracy of the U2H map (
2
) by comparing its predictions to numerical simulations using WW3.

In §
4
we examine the SWH anomaly
h_{s}
induced by realistic flows and by simple flows such as vortices. In §
5
we consider
h_{s}
produced by special wave spectra. We show, in particular, that
h_{s}
vanishes (to leading order in
\varepsilon
) for isotropic wave spectra. The complementary limit is highly directional wave spectra, characteristic of ocean swell. Swell produces strongly anisotropic SGW anomalies aligned with the dominant direction of wave propagation, i.e. streaks in SWH. The limit of highly directional wave spectra is delicate in that it is non-uniform in the small parameter,
\delta
say, characterising the directional width. The results of the present paper require that
\delta\gg\varepsilon
. For
\delta=O(\varepsilon)
, the SWH response is nonlinear in the current and the assumptions leading to the U2H map break down. An asymptotic form for the SWH in this case is derived in
Wang et al. 2023
under the additional assumption of a localised current.

2
Formulation

We start with the conservation equation

\partial_{t}\mathcal{A}+\bnabla_{\bm{k}}\omega\bcdot\bnabla_{\bm{x}}\mathcal{A}-\bnabla_{\bm{x}}\omega\bcdot\bnabla_{\bm{k}}\mathcal{A}=0

(3)

for the wave action density per unit mass
\mathcal{A}({\bm{x}},{\bm{k}},t)
in position–wavevector space
(
Komen et al. 1996
;
Janssen 2004
, e.g.)
. Here
\omega
is the absolute frequency of deep-water SGWs, related to the intrinsic frequency

\sigma(k)=\sqrt{gk}

(4)

with
k=|{\bm{k}}|
, by

\omega({\bm{x}},{\bm{k}})=\sigma(k)+\bm{k}\bcdot\bm{U}({\bm{x}}),

(5)

where
\bm{U}({\bm{x}})
is the surface velocity of the ocean current, taken to be horizontal and time independent.
The wave energy per unit mass spectrum is related to the action density according to

\mathcal{E}({\bm{x}},{\bm{k}},t)=\sigma(k)\mathcal{A}({\bm{x}},{\bm{k}},t).

(6)

With equipartition between kinetic and potential energy of deep-water SGW the root mean square sea-surface displacement is related to the wave energy spectrum by

g\eta^{2}_{\mathit{rms}}=\int\mathcal{E}({\bm{x}},{\bm{k}},t)\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{k}}.

(7)

The polar representation of the wavenumber vector
{\bm{k}}
is

{\bm{k}}=k\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta\\
\sin\theta\end{pmatrix},

(8)

and integrations in the form of
\int(\cdot)\mathrm{d}{\bm{k}}
(as in (
7
)) can be expressed as
\iint(\cdot)k\mathrm{d}k\mathrm{d}\theta
.
Compared to conventions adopted in wave modelling communities, our definitions of the wave action density per unit mass
\mathcal{A}({\bm{x}},{\bm{k}},t)
and wave energy per unit mass spectrum
\mathcal{E}({\bm{x}},{\bm{k}},t)
are related to the definitions of wave action density
N(k,\theta;{\bm{x}},t)
and surface elevation spectrum
F(k,\theta;{\bm{x}},t)
as appear in WW3
(
The WAVEWATCH III Development Group 2016
)
via
\mathcal{A}({\bm{x}},{\bm{k}},t)=gN(k,\theta;{\bm{x}},t)
and
\mathcal{E}({\bm{x}},{\bm{k}},t)=gF(k,\theta;{\bm{x}},t)
.

Our focus is on the spatial distribution of wave energy, obtained by integrating (
6
) in
{\bm{k}}
and conventionally reported in terms of the SWH defined as
H_{s}({\bm{x}},t)=4\eta_{rms}({\bm{x}},t)
. SWH can be obtained from the action spectrum with

H_{s}({\bm{x}},t)=4\left(g^{-1}\int\sigma(k)\mathcal{A}({\bm{x}},{\bm{k}},t)\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{k}}\right)^{1/2}\,.

(9)

The action equation (
3
) relies on an assumption of spatial scale separation between surface gravity waves and currents. It also neglects forcing, dissipation and wave–wave interactions. We make three further assumptions:

1.

in the absence of currents, the wave action takes a background value
\bar{\mathcal{A}}({\bm{k}})
that is independent of space and time;

2.

the current is steady and we restrict our attention to the steady-state wave-action response;

3.

the typical current velocity is small compared with the typical group speed of SGWs, so that the Doppler shift
\bm{k}\bcdot\bm{U}({\bm{x}})
is a small correction to the intrinsic frequency
\sigma(k)
.

As preparation for a perturbation expansion we make assumption
(iii)
explicit by writing

\omega({\bm{x}},{\bm{k}})=\sigma(k)+\varepsilon\bm{k}\bcdot\bm{U}({\bm{x}}).

(10)

Here we avoid a formal scaling analysis and retain dimensional variables. Thus
\varepsilon
should from now on be regarded as a bookkeeping parameter that identifies terms that are
O(U/c_{g})
and is ultimately set to
1
. We

expand the action as

\mathcal{A}({\bm{x}},{\bm{k}},t)=\bar{\mathcal{A}}({\bm{k}})+\varepsilon a({\bm{x}},{\bm{k}},t)+O(\varepsilon^{2}),

(11)

assuming that the leading-order action
\bar{\mathcal{A}}({\bm{k}})
is independent of space and time.
The presence of the currents leads to anomalies at order
\varepsilon
captured by
a({\bm{x}},{\bm{k}},t)
. We relate
a({\bm{x}},{\bm{k}},t)
to
\bm{U}({\bm{x}})
by introducing (
11
) into (
3
) to obtain

\partial_{t}a+\bm{c_{g}}\bcdot\bnabla_{{\bm{x}}}a=(\bnabla_{{\bm{k}}}\bar{\mathcal{A}}\bcdot\bnabla_{\bm{x}})\bm{U}\bcdot{\bm{k}},

(12)

where
\bm{c_{g}}(k)=\sqrt{g/4k^{3}}{\bm{k}}
is the group velocity. We focus on the steady-state response
a({\bm{x}},{\bm{k}})
, independent of
t
. This satisfies
(
12
) where the time derivative term is omitted. Causality is enforced by adding a linear dissipation term to find

(\bm{c_{g}}\bcdot\bnabla_{{\bm{x}}}+\mu)a=(\bnabla_{{\bm{k}}}\bar{\mathcal{A}}\bcdot\bnabla_{\bm{x}})\bm{U}\bcdot{\bm{k}}.

(13)

The non-dissipative, causal solution is then obtained in the limit
\mu\to 0^{+}
.

We solve (
12
) in terms of the Fourier transforms

\hat{a}(\bm{q},{\bm{k}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\int a({\bm{x}},{\bm{k}})\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{q}\cdot{\bm{x}}}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\hat{\bm{U}}(\bm{q})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\int\bm{U}({\bm{x}})\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{q}\cdot{\bm{x}}}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}.

(14)

We emphasise the distinction between the newly introduced wavevector
\bm{q}
, which captures spatial variations at the current scale, and the original wavevector
{\bm{k}}
, which represents spatial variations of the wave phase. Introducing (
14
) into (
13
) leads to

\hat{a}(\bm{q},{\bm{k}})=\lim_{\mu\to 0^{+}}\frac{({\bm{k}}\bcdot\hat{\bm{U}})(\bm{q}\bcdot\bnabla_{{\bm{k}}}\bar{\mathcal{A}})}{\bm{c_{g}}\bcdot\bm{q}-\mathrm{i}\mu}.

(15)

The limit
\mu\to 0^{+}
above is taken in all expressions involving
\mu
and we proceed with lighter notation in which the limit is understood.

Our focus is on the SWH, which we expand as

H_{s}({\bm{x}})=\bar{H}_{s}+\varepsilon h_{s}({\bm{x}})+O(\varepsilon^{2}).

(16)

The leading order term
\bar{H}_{s}
is a constant. The anomaly
h_{s}({\bm{x}})
is deduced from
a({\bm{x}},{\bm{k}})
by
Taylor expanding (
9
):

h_{s}({\bm{x}})=\frac{8}{g\bar{H}_{s}}\int\sigma(k)a({\bm{x}},{\bm{k}})\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{k}}\,.

(17)

An analogous formula relates the Fourier transform
\hat{h}_{s}(\bm{q})
of
h_{s}({\bm{x}})
to
\hat{a}(\bm{q},{\bm{k}})
.
Substituting (
15
) into (
17
) gives

\frac{\hat{h}_{s}(\bm{q})}{\bar{H}_{s}}=\hat{\bm{L}}(\bm{q})\bcdot\hat{\bm{U}}(\bm{q})\,,

(18)

where

\hat{\bm{L}}(\bm{q})=\frac{8}{g\bar{H}_{s}^{2}}\int\frac{\bm{q}\bcdot\bnabla_{{\bm{k}}}\bar{\mathcal{A}}}{\bm{c_{g}}\bcdot\bm{q}-\mathrm{i}\mu}\sigma{\bm{k}}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{k}}.

(19)

Eq. (
18
) shows that
h_{s}
is obtained from
\bm{U}
via a linear map – the U2H map. This map is naturally expressed in terms of Fourier transforms, with the complex vector
\hat{\bm{L}}(\bm{q})
acting as a transfer function. It is clear from (
19
) that
\hat{\bm{L}}
depends on the wavevector
\bm{q}
only through its orientation: introducing the polar representation

\bm{q}=q\,\bm{e}_{\bm{q}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}q\begin{pmatrix}\cos\varphi\\
\sin\varphi\end{pmatrix}\,,

(20)

with
\bm{e}_{\bm{q}}
the unit vector in the direction of
\bm{q}
and
-\pi<\varphi\leq\pi
, we can rewrite (
19
) as

\hat{\bm{L}}(\bm{q})=\hat{\bm{L}}(\varphi)=\frac{8}{g\bar{H}_{s}^{2}}\int\frac{\bm{e}_{\bm{q}}\bcdot\bnabla_{{\bm{k}}}\bar{\mathcal{A}}}{\bm{c_{g}}\bcdot\bm{e}_{\bm{q}}-\mathrm{i}\mu}\sigma{\bm{k}}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{k}}.

(21)

It follows from the reality of
h_{s}({\bm{x}})
that
\hat{h}_{s}(-\bm{q})=\hat{h}_{s}^{*}(\bm{q})
and hence

\hat{\bm{L}}(-\varphi)=\hat{\bm{L}}^{*}(\varphi)\,.

(22)

Eq. (
21
) implies that, in physical space,
h_{s}({\bm{x}})
is expressed as a convolution of
\bm{U}({\bm{x}})
with a kernel
\bm{L}({\bm{x}})
– the inverse Fourier transform of
\hat{\bm{L}}(\bm{q})
– that is homogeneous of degree
-2
, that is,
\bm{L}(\lambda{\bm{x}})=\lambda^{-2}\bm{L}({\bm{x}})
. Linear maps of this type are known as (two-dimensional) Calderón–Zygmund transforms
(e.g.
Stein 1970
, Ch. 2)
. While the right-hand side of (
21
) appears ambiguous for
\bm{q}=0
(since
\varphi
is then not defined), we simply take
\hat{h}_{s}(0)=0
, corresponding to the vanishing of the spatial mean of
h_{s}({\bm{x}})
, consistent with the definition of
h_{s}({\bm{x}})
as an anomaly.

From (
18
) and (
21
) we draw the important conclusion that patterns in
h_{s}
have scales set by the scales of
\bm{U}
(not vorticity). But the angular dependence of
h_{s}
depends (linearly) on the wave spectrum. In the next sections we refine this conclusion by obtaining alternative and approximate expressions for
\hat{\bm{L}}(\bm{q})
.

3
The U2H map

3.1
Alternative forms of
\hat{\bm{L}}(\varphi)

A useful expression for
\hat{\bm{L}}(\varphi)
is obtained by substituting the identity
(\bm{q}\bcdot\bm{p}){\bm{k}}=({\bm{k}}\bcdot\bm{q})\bm{p}-({\bm{k}}^{\perp}\bcdot\bm{p})\bm{q}^{\perp}
with
\bm{p}=\bnabla_{{\bm{k}}}\bar{\mathcal{A}}
in (
19
) to obtain

\hat{\bm{L}}(\varphi)=\frac{8}{g\bar{H}_{s}^{2}}\left(\int\frac{\sigma{\bm{k}}\bcdot\bm{q}}{\bm{c_{g}}\bcdot\bm{q}-\mathrm{i}\mu}\bnabla_{\bm{k}}\bar{\mathcal{A}}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{k}}-\int\frac{\sigma{\bm{k}}^{\perp}\bcdot\bnabla_{\bm{k}}\bar{\mathcal{A}}}{\bm{c_{g}}\bcdot\bm{q}-\mathrm{i}\mu}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{k}}\,\bm{q}^{\perp}\right).

(23)

Noting that
\bm{c_{g}}=\sigma{\bm{k}}/(2k^{2})
, and that the multiplication of
\mu
by a positive factor is irrelevant, we rewrite (
23
) as

\hat{\bm{L}}(\varphi)=\frac{16}{g\bar{H}_{s}^{2}}\left(\int\frac{k^{2}{\bm{k}}\bcdot\bm{q}}{{\bm{k}}\bcdot\bm{q}-\mathrm{i}\mu}\bnabla_{\bm{k}}\bar{\mathcal{A}}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{k}}-\int\frac{k^{2}{\bm{k}}^{\perp}\bcdot\bnabla_{\bm{k}}\bar{\mathcal{A}}}{{\bm{k}}\bcdot\bm{q}-\mathrm{i}\mu}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{k}}\,\bm{q}^{\perp}\right).

(24)

Now,
\mu
can be safely set to
0
in the first integral which, on integrating by parts, reduces to

\int k^{2}\bnabla_{\bm{k}}\bar{\mathcal{A}}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{k}}=-2\bm{P},

(25)

where

\bm{P}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\int\!\bar{\mathcal{A}}({\bm{k}}){\bm{k}}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{k}}

(26)

is the wave momentum.
For the second integral we use the polar representation (
8
)
of the SGW wavevector.
Noting that
\bnabla_{\bm{k}}\bar{\mathcal{A}}=\partial_{k}\bar{\mathcal{A}}\,{\bm{k}}/k+\partial_{\theta}\bar{\mathcal{A}}\,{\bm{k}}^{\perp}/k^{2}
and that
{\bm{k}}\bcdot\bm{q}=kq\cos(\theta-\varphi)
we obtain

\hat{\bm{L}}(\varphi)=-\frac{16}{g\bar{H}_{s}^{2}}\left(2\bm{P}+\int\frac{k\partial_{\theta}\bar{\mathcal{A}}}{\cos(\theta-\varphi)-\mathrm{i}\mu}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{k}}\,\bm{e}_{\bm{q}}^{\perp}\right)\,,

(27)

where

\bm{e}_{\bm{q}}^{\perp}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\begin{pmatrix}-\sin\varphi\\
\ \ \cos\varphi\end{pmatrix}

(28)

is the unit vector perpendicular to the wavevector
\bm{q}
.

Starting from (
27
) we can derive an explicit expression for the transfer function
\hat{\bm{L}}(\varphi)
as a Fourier series in
\varphi
. We rewrite (
27
) as

\hat{\bm{L}}(\varphi)=-\frac{16}{g\bar{H}_{s}^{2}}\left(2\bm{P}+\,\bm{e}_{\bm{q}}^{\perp}\,\partial_{\varphi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathcal{P}(\theta)}{\cos(\theta-\varphi)-\mathrm{i}\mu}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\right)\,,

(29)

where

\mathcal{P}(\theta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!\bar{\mathcal{A}}(k,\theta)\,k^{2}\mathrm{d}k\,.

(30)

The wave momentum in (
26
) is

\bm{P}=\oint\mathcal{P}(\theta)\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta\\
\sin\theta\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{d}\theta\,.

(31)

With the results above
\hat{\bm{L}}(\varphi)
depends on the leading-order action spectrum
\bar{\mathcal{A}}({\bm{k}})
only through the function
\mathcal{P}(\theta)
. This function can be expanded in Fourier series as

\mathcal{P}(\theta)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}p_{n}\,\mathrm{e}^{n\mathrm{i}\theta},\quad\textrm{with}\quad 2\pi p_{n}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\!\!\mathcal{P}(\theta)\,\mathrm{e}^{-n\mathrm{i}\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,.

(32)

Computations detailed in Appendix
A
express the integral in (
29
) (as
\mu\to 0^{+}
) in terms of the
p_{n}
. This puts the transfer function into the form

\hat{\bm{L}}(\varphi)=\frac{16}{g\bar{H}_{s}^{2}}\left(\bm{e}_{\bm{q}}^{\perp}\,\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}n(-\mathrm{i})^{|n|}\,2\pi p_{n}\,\mathrm{e}^{n\mathrm{i}\varphi}-2\bm{P}\right)\,,

(33)

where the wave momentum is

\bm{P}=\begin{pmatrix}+\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}2\pi p_{1}\\
-\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}2\pi p_{1}\end{pmatrix}.

(34)

Equation (
33
) provides the transfer function
\hat{\bm{L}}(\varphi)
in a
form readily computable from any given background wave spectrum
\bar{\mathcal{A}}(k,\theta)
.

3.2
Contributions of the divergent and vortical parts of current

The 2D Helmholtz decomposition of
\bm{U}
into divergent and vortical parts is

\bm{U}=\underbrace{\bnabla\phi}_{\bm{U}_{\phi}}+\underbrace{\bnabla^{\perp}\psi}_{\bm{U}_{\psi}}\,,

(35)

where
\phi
and
\psi
are the potential and streamfunction, and
\bnabla^{\perp}=(-\p_{y},\p_{x})
. The corresponding decomposition of the Fourier transform
\hat{\bm{U}}
is

\hat{\bm{U}}(\bm{q})=\mathrm{i}q\hat{\phi}(\bm{q})\bm{e}_{\bm{q}}+\mathrm{i}q\hat{\psi}(\bm{q})\bm{e}_{\bm{q}}^{\perp}.

(36)

In view of (
18
), we can separate the contributions of the divergent and vortical parts of the currents by expressing the transfer function
\hat{\bm{L}}(\bm{q})
in terms of its components along
\bm{e}_{\bm{q}}
and
\bm{e}_{\bm{q}}^{\perp}
. Projecting (
33
) on
\bm{e}_{\bm{q}}
and
\bm{e}_{\bm{q}}^{\perp}
gives

\hat{\bm{L}}(\varphi)=\hat{{L}}_{\parallel}\,\bm{e}_{\bm{q}}+\hat{{L}}_{\perp}\,\bm{e}_{\bm{q}}^{\perp},

(37)

where

\hat{{L}}_{\parallel}(\varphi)=-\frac{32}{g\bar{H}_{s}^{2}}\bm{P}\bcdot\bm{e}_{\bm{q}}

(38)

and

\hat{{L}}_{\perp}(\varphi)=\frac{16}{g\bar{H}_{s}^{2}}\left(\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}n(-\mathrm{i})^{|n|}2\pi p_{n}\,\mathrm{e}^{n\mathrm{i}\varphi}-2\bm{P}\bcdot\bm{e}_{\bm{q}}^{\perp}\right).

(39)

Because
\bm{e}_{-\bm{q}}=-\bm{e}_{\bm{q}}
, the symmetry property (
22
) implies that
\hat{{L}}_{\parallel}(-\varphi)=-\hat{{L}}_{\parallel}^{*}(\varphi)
and
\hat{{L}}_{\perp}(-\varphi)=-\hat{{L}}_{\perp}^{*}(\varphi)
.

Combining the contributions proportional to
p_{\pm 1}
(stemming from
n=\pm 1
in the series and from
2\bm{P}\bcdot\bm{e}_{\bm{q}}^{\perp}
), we can rewrite (
39
) as

\hat{{L}}_{\perp}(\varphi)=\frac{16}{g\bar{H}_{s}^{2}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}n(-\mathrm{i})^{|n|}2\pi\tilde{p}_{n}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\varphi},

(40)

where

\tilde{p}_{n}=\begin{cases}2p_{\pm 1}\,,\qquad&\text{if $n=\pm 1$;}\\
p_{n}\,,\qquad&\text{if $n\not=\pm 1$.}\end{cases}

(41)

With the form (
37
) for
\hat{\bm{L}}(\varphi)
and the Helmholtz decomposition (
36
), the linear map (
18
) becomes

\frac{\hat{h}_{s}(\bm{q})}{\bar{H}_{s}}=\mathrm{i}q\hat{{L}}_{\parallel}(\varphi)\hat{\phi}(\bm{q})+\mathrm{i}q\hat{{L}}_{\perp}(\varphi)\hat{\psi}(\bm{q})\,.

(42)

\hat{{L}}_{\parallel}
and
\hat{{L}}_{\perp}
control the dependence of
h_{s}
on, respectively, the divergent and vortical parts of the current.
In general,
\hat{{L}}_{\parallel},\,\hat{{L}}_{\perp}\not=0
, and both the divergent and vortical parts of the current induce modulations in SWH.
However, we show in §
5.3
that for highly directional SGW spectra
\hat{{L}}_{\perp}\gg\hat{{L}}_{\parallel}
, i.e. the vortical part of the current is dominant.

4
Application to specific currents

Given the wave spectrum
\bar{\mathcal{A}}({\bm{k}})
and the current
\bm{U}({\bm{x}})
, the U2H map
\hat{h}_{s}/\bar{H}_{s}=\hat{\bm{L}}\bcdot\hat{\bm{U}}
, with
\hat{\bm{L}}
in (
33
) or (
37
)–(
39
), enables the computation of
h_{s}
. In this section, we carry out this computation. We first use a numerical procedure suitable for arbitrary currents which we apply to two realistic configurations.
We then consider the idealised cases of purely divergent and purely vortical currents for which we obtain analytic results. In all cases we compare the U2H predictions with the results of WW3 simulations.

4.1
Numerical implementation for arbitrary current

The velocity field
\bm{U}({\bm{x}})
is discretised on a regular grid and its Fourier transform
\hat{\bm{U}}(\bm{q})
is obtained on the dual Fourier grid by Fast Fourier transform (FFT).
To prevent numerical artefacts due to the non-periodicity of the currents, we use a large computational domain, zero-padding
\bm{U}
in the periphery.

The inverse FFT of the product
\hat{h}_{s}(\bm{q})/\bar{H}_{s}=\hat{\bm{L}}(\varphi)\bcdot\hat{\bm{U}}(\bm{q})
yields
h_{s}({\bm{x}})
on the spatial grid.
A Jupyter Notebook of this implementation is available on
https://shorturl.at/bef14
, where users can customize the input currents and background wave spectrum. We refer the reader to this Notebook for complete implementation details.

For the examples of this paper, we take the background wave action spectrum
\bar{\mathcal{A}}(k,\theta)
of the separable (in
k
and
\theta
) form detailed in appendix
B
. The wavenumber dependence is defined by a truncated Gaussian in
\sigma(k)
and the angular dependence
D(\theta)
follows the model of
Longuet-Higgins et al. 1963

D(\theta)\propto\cos^{2s}\left((\theta-\theta_{p})/2\right),

(43)

where
\theta_{p}
is the primary angle of wave propagation, measured from the
x
-axis and in the direction of
{\bm{k}}
, and the parameter
s
controls the directional spread. Large values, say
s\gtrsim 10
, correspond to swell-like sea states. For integer
s
, the coefficients
p_{n}
in (
32
) required for
\hat{\bm{L}}(\varphi)
have a simple closed form and vanish for
|n|>s
(see appendix
B
).

For comparison with U2H, we carry out WW3 simulations that approximate a steady solution of the linear action equation (
3
). The set up is as described in
Wang et al. 2023
except for two aspects of the wave forcing. First, the forcing imposes the spectrum
\bar{\mathcal{A}}({\bm{k}})
on the entire boundary i.e. waves enter the rectangular domain from all four sides. This improved formulation ensures that the wave spectrum in the absence of currents is uniform even for spectra with broad directional spread. (In
Wang et al. 2023
waves entered the computational domain only from the western boundary. Even in the complete absence of currents the resulting steady-state solution decreased with
x
as ‘wave shadows’ from the northern and southern boundaries encroach into the centre of the domain.) Second, to ensure
consistency with U2H, we zero-pad the domain of the currents in strips with widths of 4 grid spacings so that waves are forced at current-free boundaries. For both U2H and WW3 we report SWH anomalies obtained by subtracting the spatial average over the (unpadded) domain shown in the figures.

4.2
Divergent current

For a purely divergent current, with
\bm{U}_{\psi}=0
, (
42
) reduces to

\hat{h}_{s}(\bm{q})=-\frac{32}{g\bar{H}_{s}}\mathrm{i}q\hat{\phi}(\bm{q})\,\bm{P}\bcdot\bm{e}_{\bm{q}}\,.

(44)

Since
\mathrm{i}q\bm{e}_{\bm{q}}=\mathrm{i}\bm{q}
, the inverse Fourier transform of (
44
) is

h_{s}=-\frac{32}{g\bar{H}_{s}}\bm{U}_{\phi}\bcdot\bm{P}\,.

(45)

Thus the SWH anomaly that arises in response to a divergent current is proportional to the component of current velocity along the wave momentum. In particular, the response is local and vanishes where the current vanishes.

4.3
Vortical current

For a purely vortical currents,
\bm{U}_{\phi}=0
in (
35
) and the U2H map in (
42
) is determined by the scalar transfer function
\hat{{L}}_{\perp}(\varphi)
, which is explicitly computed from the series in (
40
).
As a demonstration, we consider a Gaussian vortex, with zero divergence and vorticity in physical and Fourier space given by

\zeta({\bm{x}})=\frac{\kappa}{2\pi r_{\mathit{v}}^{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-r^{2}/(2r_{\mathit{v}}^{2})}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\hat{\zeta}(\bm{q})=\kappa\mathrm{e}^{-r_{\mathit{v}}^{2}q^{2}/2},

(47)

where
\kappa
is the circulation.
We take advantage of the axisymmetry of this flow to carry out the Fourier inversion leading to
h_{s}({\bm{x}})
analytically. Calculations detailed in Appendix
D
yield the explicit expression

h_{s}({\bm{x}})=-\frac{16\mathrm{i}}{g\bar{H}_{s}}\frac{\kappa}{r_{\mathit{v}}}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-r^{2}/4r_{v}^{2}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}n\,\tilde{p}_{n}\,I_{|n|/2}(r^{2}/4r_{v}^{2})\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\nu},

(48)

where
{\bm{x}}=r(\cos\nu,\sin\nu)
and the
I_{|n|}
are modified Bessel functions. The coefficients
\tilde{p}_{n}
depend only on the wave spectrum and are related to the already obtained
p_{n}
according to (
41
). Formula (
48
) has the advantage over the general implementation of the U2H map described in §
4.1
in that it gives
h_{s}({\bm{x}})
at any location without the need for entire computational domains in both physical and Fourier domains.

Isotropic wave spectrum

5.2
Mildly anisotropic wave spectrum

The U2H map is particularly simple for the spectrum

\bar{\mathcal{A}}({\bm{k}})=\mathcal{A}_{0}(k)+\mathcal{A}_{c}(k)\cos\theta,

(49)

e.g., as in the LHCS spectrum with
s=1
used for the first row of figures
4
and
5
.
For the action spectrum in (
49
) the wave momentum (
26
) can be written as

\bm{P}=|\bm{P}|\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}=|\bm{P}|\cos\varphi\,\bm{e}_{\bm{q}}-|\bm{P}|\sin\varphi\,\bm{e}_{\bm{q}}^{\perp},

(50)

where

|\bm{P}|=\pi\int\!\!\mathcal{A}_{c}(k)k^{2}\mathrm{d}k.

(51)

The function
\mathcal{P}(\theta)
defined in (
30
) is then

\mathcal{P}(\theta)=\int\!\!\mathcal{A}_{0}(k)k^{2}\mathrm{d}k+\frac{|\bm{P}|}{\pi}\cos\theta.

(52)

and
2\pi p_{1}=2\pi p_{-1}=|\bm{P}|
.
The transfer function in (
33
) reduces to

\hat{\bm{L}}(\varphi)=\frac{32}{g\bar{H}_{s}^{2}}|\bm{P}|\left(2\sin\varphi\,\bm{e}_{\bm{q}}^{\perp}-\cos\varphi\,\bm{e}_{\bm{q}}\right)\,.

(53)

With the Helmholtz decomposition (
36
), the U2H map is

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{h}_{s}(\bm{q})}{\bar{H}_{s}}

\displaystyle=\hat{\bm{L}}(\bm{q})\bcdot\hat{\bm{U}}(\bm{q})\,,

(54)

\displaystyle=\frac{32}{g\bar{H}_{s}^{2}}\left(2|\bm{P}|\,\mathrm{i}q\sin\varphi\,\hat{\psi}-|\bm{P}|\,\mathrm{i}q\cos\varphi\,\hat{\phi}\right)\,.

(55)

The inverse Fourier transform can be taken by inspection and the result written as

h_{s}=-\frac{32}{g\bar{H}_{s}}\left(2\bm{U}_{\psi}\bcdot\bm{P}+\bm{U}_{\phi}\bcdot\bm{P}\right)\,.

(56)

In this simple case, only the component of current along
\bm{P}
produces a SWH anomaly which turns out to be local, vanishing where the current vanishes. In (
56
) the vortical part of the current,
\bm{U}_{\psi}
, is twice as effective as the divergent part,
\bm{U}_{\phi}
. The divergent contribution in (
56
) is identical to that in (
45
) which applies to arbitrary wave spectra. This example, which corresponds to the first rows of figures
4
and
5
, shows that the response to divergent currents is not always negligible relative to the vortical response. This is in contrast with
Villas Boâs et al. 2020
’s suggestion that only the vortical part of the current affects
h_{s}
. The next section however shows that the imprint of the vortical part of the current is much larger than that of the divergent part for highly directional spectra.

5.3
Highly directional wave spectrum

We conclude above that
h_{s}
is small for an isotropic wave spectrum. It is of interest to examine the opposite limit of a highly directional wave spectrum. This limit corresponds to an action spectrum of the form

\bar{\mathcal{A}}(k,\theta)=\delta^{-1}\bar{\mathcal{A}}(k,\Theta),\quad\textrm{where}\quad\Theta=\theta/\delta,

(57)

with
\delta\ll 1
the relevant small parameter, and we assume that
\theta=0
is the primary propagation direction. The prefactor
\delta^{-1}
ensures that the action spectrum integrated over
\theta
is
O(1)
. Correspondingly,

\mathcal{P}(\theta)=\delta^{-1}\mathcal{P}(\Theta).

(58)

For simplicity, we abuse notation by using the same symbols
\bar{\mathcal{A}}
and
\mathcal{P}
on both sides of (
57
)–(
58
), distinguishing them by their arguments. Similar to the treatment of
\varepsilon
in (
10
), we use
\delta
as a bookkeeping parameter that is set to
1
in the end.

Taking (
29
) as a starting point, we obtain an asymptotic approximation to
\hat{\bm{L}}(\varphi)
in appendix
C
. There we show that the dominant contribution to
\hat{\bm{L}}(\varphi)
comes from the integral term and is large in small regions around
\varphi=\pm\pi/2
. In terms of the rescaled variable

\Phi_{\pm}=(\varphi\mp\pi/2)/\delta=O(1),

(59)

the leading-order approximation to the transfer function in these regions is

\hat{\bm{L}}(\varphi)\sim\hat{{L}}_{\perp}(\varphi)\begin{pmatrix}\mp 1\\
0\end{pmatrix}\sim\frac{16}{g\bar{H}_{s}^{2}\delta^{2}}\,\partial_{\Phi_{\pm}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}{\Theta-\Phi_{\pm}\mp\mathrm{i}\mu}\,\mathrm{d}\Theta\,\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

(60)

Thus
\hat{\bm{L}}(\varphi)
is dominant and
O(\delta^{-2})
in narrow,
O(\delta)
, sectors around
\varphi=\pm\pi/2
. We conclude that:

1.

For typical
\hat{\bm{U}}(\bm{q})
, patterns of
h_{s}
take the form of structures elongated in the direction of propagation of the waves, i.e. streaks, with an
O(\delta)
aspect ratio.

2.

\hat{{L}}_{\perp}=O(\delta^{-2})\hat{{L}}_{\parallel}
, since
\hat{{L}}_{\parallel}=O(1)
(see (
77
)). According to (
42
), this implies that the divergent-free, vortical part of the velocity field
\bm{U}
has an asymptotically larger impact on
h_{s}
than the potential part.

3.

Highly directional waves produce SWH anomalies larger by a factor
\delta^{-1}
than those induced for spectra with
O(1)
directional spread. (This estimate accounts for both the factor
\delta^{-2}
in (
77
) and the
O(\delta)
width of the support of
\hat{{L}}_{\perp}(\varphi)
implied by (
59
)).

4.

As a result of (iii), the linear approximation that underpins U2H requires that
\varepsilon\ll\delta
in addition to
\varepsilon\ll 1
. We discuss this further at the end of the section.

We illustrate the asymptotic result (
77
) by considering the limit
s\to\infty
of the LHCS spectrum. Taking this limit in (
71
) gives

\mathcal{P}(\Theta)=\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\mathrm{e}^{-\Theta^{2}/2},\quad\textrm{where}\quad\delta=\sqrt{2/s}

(61)

and
\alpha
a constant determined by the dependence of the spectrum on
k
.
Using (
61
) and the Sokhotski–Plemelj theorem, we rewrite the integral term in (
77
) as

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}{\Theta-\Phi_{\pm}\mp\mathrm{i}\mu}\,\mathrm{d}\Theta

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left(\pm\mathrm{i}\pi\,\mathrm{e}^{-\Phi_{\pm}^{2}/2}+\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\Theta^{2}/2}}{\Theta-\Phi_{\pm}}\,\mathrm{d}\Theta\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha\left(\pm\mathrm{i}\sqrt{\pi/2}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\Phi_{\pm}^{2}/2}-\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{daw}(\Phi_{\pm}/\sqrt{2})\right),

(62)

where
\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int
denotes the Cauchy principal value and
\mathrm{daw}(\cdot)
denotes the Dawson function
(
DLMF 2023
)
. Using that
\mathrm{daw}^{\prime}(x)=1-2x\,\mathrm{daw}(x)

(
DLMF 2023
)
we can evaluate the right-hand side of (

\hat{{L}}_{\perp}(\varphi)=\frac{16\alpha}{g\bar{H}_{s}^{2}\delta^{2}}\begin{cases}\mathrm{i}\sqrt{\pi/2}\,\Phi_{+}\mathrm{e}^{-\Phi_{+}^{2}/2}+1-\sqrt{2}\Phi_{+}\,\mathrm{daw}(\Phi_{+}/\sqrt{2}),&\textrm{for}\ \ 0\leq\varphi<\pi;\\
\mathrm{i}\sqrt{\pi/2}\,\Phi_{-}\mathrm{e}^{-\Phi_{-}^{2}/2}-1+\sqrt{2}\Phi_{-}\,\mathrm{daw}(\Phi_{-}/\sqrt{2}),&\textrm{for}\ \ -\pi<\varphi<0\,.\end{cases}

(63)

We conclude by connecting the results of this section with those of
Wang et al. 2023
. They focus on the regime
\delta\ll 1
and on localised currents. Using matched asymptotics, they obtain an asymptotic expression for the total SWH
H_{s}=\bar{H}_{s}+h_{s}
in the presence of currents. This expression holds without the linearity assumption

that underpins the U2H map. Specifically, they consider the distinguished limit

which leads to

.
Wang et al. 2023

\varepsilon\ll\delta\ll 1
. In appendix

6
Discussion and conclusion

In the oceanographic regime with
U/c_{g}\ll 1
the effect of currents on an underlying spatially uniform action spectrum
\bar{\mathcal{A}}({\bm{k}})
can be determined by solving the linear problem in (
12
) and (
13
) for the anomaly in action density
a({\bm{x}},{\bm{k}})
. We have focussed on extraction of the anomaly in SWH
h_{s}({\bm{x}})
via the weighted
{\bm{k}}
-integral of
a({\bm{x}},{\bm{k}})
in (
17
). The results are in good agreement with numerical solutions of WW3. There is a significant generalization of this procedure: given
a({\bm{x}},{\bm{k}})
, other important SGW properties, such as the current-induced anomaly in the Stokes drift, are only a
{\bm{k}}
-integral away.

Assumptions involved in the U2H map, listed in §
2
, are violated in several ocean-relevant scenarios: interactions of surface waves with tidal and near-inertial currents violate the assumption of steady current
(
Tolman 1988
;
Tolman 1990
;
Ho et al. 2023
;
Halsne et al. 2024
;
Gemmrich & Garrett 2012
, e.g.,)
; short surface waves in the saturation range of the wave spectrum violate the assumptions of scale separation and large group speed
(
Rascle et al. 2017
;
Lenain & Pizzo 2021
;
Vrećica et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
; and conditions of active wave generation and dissipation make the source terms non-negligible
(
Holthuijsen & Tolman 1991
;
Chen et al. 2007
;
Romero et al. 2017
, e.g.,)
. These scenarios are not explored in this work. Nevertheless, for relatively long surface waves interacting with open-ocean currents, our assumptions are often satisfied.

Linearity of the partial differential equation (
12
) implies that there is a linear map from
\bm{U}
to
h_{s}
. Can this U2H map be inverted to produce an H2U map?
Not in general: U2H maps a vector field to a scalar field, so cannot be expected to have an inverse.
The non-invertibility of U2H is illustrated by considering the mildly anisotropic spectrum of §
5.2
:
(
55
) implies that
h_{s}=0
for a velocity field with potential and streamfunction satisfying
2\psi_{y}-\phi_{x}=0
, demonstrating the non-uniqueness of
\bm{U}
for a given
h_{s}
. Observations of
h_{s}
, however, provide partial information about
\bm{U}
. For swell-like waves, in particular, §
5.3
shows that
h_{s}
can be approximated by a linear operator acting on the vortical component of the current; in this case, we can infer vorticity from
h_{s}
.

An important qualitative result emerging quickly from the analysis is that the transfer function,
\hat{\bm{L}}
in (
21
), does not depend on the magnitude
q
of the current wavenumber
\bm{q}
, but only on its direction
\varphi
. This implies that the spatial scale of variations in
h_{s}
is set by those of the current
\bm{U}
, e.g. power-law
\bm{U}
-spectra result in power-law
h_{s}
-spectra with the same slope
(
Ardhuin et al. 2017
;
Romero et al. 2020
;
Villas Boâs et al. 2020
)
. Using (
42
) we are now exploring the ramifications of this result.

Appendix A
Computable form of the transfer function
\hat{\bm{L}}

Introducing (
32
) into the integral in (
29
) and changing integration variable from
\theta
to
\theta+\varphi
gives

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathcal{P}(\theta)}{\cos(\theta-\varphi)-\mathrm{i}\mu}\,\mathrm{d}\theta=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{n\mathrm{i}\theta}}{\cos\theta-\mathrm{i}\mu}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\right)\,p_{n}\,\mathrm{e}^{n\mathrm{i}\varphi}.

(64)

The integrals on the right-hand side can be evaluated explicitly using contour integration in the complex plane. Consider first
n\geq 0
and let
z=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}
so that

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{n\mathrm{i}\theta}}{\cos\theta-\mathrm{i}\mu}\,\mathrm{d}\theta=-2\mathrm{i}\oint\frac{z^{n}}{z^{2}-2\mathrm{i}\mu z+1}\,\mathrm{d}z.

(65)

We can now use the residue theorem, noting that the integrand has to the two poles
z_{\pm}=\pm\mathrm{i}+\mathrm{i}\mu+O(\mu^{2})
and that only
z_{-}
is enclosed by the integration contour since
\mu\to 0^{+}
. This leads to

\lim_{\mu\to 0^{+}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{n\mathrm{i}\theta}}{\cos\theta-\mathrm{i}\mu}\,\mathrm{d}\theta=2\pi(-\mathrm{i})^{n-1}\quad\textrm{for}\ \ n\geq 0.

(66)

For
n<0
, we can let
\theta\mapsto-\theta
on the left-hand side of (
65
) to conclude that (
66
) holds with
n\mapsto-n
. Hence, for any
n
,

\lim_{\mu\to 0^{+}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{n\mathrm{i}\theta}}{\cos\theta-\mathrm{i}\mu}\,\mathrm{d}\theta=2\pi(-\mathrm{i})^{|n|-1},

(67)

and (
64
) reduces in the limit
\mu\to 0^{+}
to

\lim_{\mu\to 0^{+}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathcal{P}(\theta)}{\cos(\theta-\varphi)-\mathrm{i}\mu}\,\mathrm{d}\theta=2\pi\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}(-\mathrm{i})^{|n|-1}p_{n}\,\mathrm{e}^{n\mathrm{i}\varphi}.

(68)

Taking the derivative in
\varphi
lead to the coefficient in front of
\bm{e}_{\bm{q}}^{\perp}
in (
33
).

The computation of the wave momentum
\bm{P}
in terms of the Fourier coefficients of the action spectrum is straightforward: using (
26
), (
8
), (
30
) and (
32
) we find

\displaystyle\bm{P}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathcal{A}(k,\theta)k^{2}\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta\\
\sin\theta\end{pmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}k\mathrm{d}\theta=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathcal{P}(\theta)\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta\\
\sin\theta\end{pmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta\\
\sin\theta\end{pmatrix}\,\mathrm{e}^{n\mathrm{i}\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,p_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}+\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}2\pi p_{1}\\
-\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}2\pi p_{1}\end{pmatrix}.

(69)

Substituting (
68
)–(
69
) into (
27
) yields (
33
).

Appendix B
The LHCS spectrum

We use the spectrum proposed by
Longuet-Higgins et al. 1963
. This takes the separable form

\bar{\mathcal{A}}(k,\theta)=f(k)\times\underbrace{\frac{\Gamma(s+1)}{2\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(s+1/2)}\cos^{2s}\left((\theta-\theta_{p})/2\right)}_{D(\theta)}.

(70)

For convenience, we assume that the peak angle
\theta_{p}=0
in this appendix.
(For
\theta_{p}\neq 0
, the final expression for
p_{n}
should be multiplied by
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}n\theta_{p}}
.)

In (
70
) the parameter
s\geq 0
controls the directional spread around the primary direction of wave propagation conventionally taken along the positive
x
-axis. The wavenumber function
f(k)
is chosen so that the frequency spectrum is a truncated Gaussian
with standard deviation of
0.040
rad s
-1
and peak angular frequencies at
\sigma=0.44
rad s
-1
(corresponding to peak period at
14.3
s) for the Gulf Stream example in figure
2
), or at
\sigma=0.61
rad s
-1
(corresponding to peak period at
10.3
s) for all the other examples.

We take advantage of linearity to report all numerical results in terms of the relative SWH anomaly
h_{s}/\bar{H}_{s}
so the amplitude of the Gaussian in (
70
), proportional to
\bar{H}_{s}
, is unimportant in this paper.

With (
70
), the function
\mathcal{P}(\theta)
in (
30
) becomes

\mathcal{P}(\theta)=\alpha D(\theta),\quad\textrm{where}\quad\alpha\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!f(k)k^{2}\mathrm{d}k.

(71)

The Fourier coefficients of
D(\theta)
, denoted
p_{n}
in (
32
), are real and satisfy
p_{-n}=p_{n}
. When
s
is an integer
\Gamma(s+1/2)
can be expressed in terms of factorials and then these coefficients are

p_{n}=\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\frac{(s!)^{2}}{(s+n)!(s-n)!}}&\textrm{for}\ \ |n|\leq s\\
0&\textrm{for}\ \ |n|>s.\end{cases},

(72)

The wave momentum is

\bm{P}=\frac{\alpha s}{s+1}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

(73)

The spectrum (
49
) is a particular case of (
70
) with
s=1
.

Appendix C
U2H map for highly directional spectra

We derive an asymptotic approximation for
\hat{\bm{L}}(\varphi)
in (
29
) for a spectrum (
57
) in the limit
\delta\to 0
. We first note that

\bm{P}=\int_{-\pi/\delta}^{\pi/\delta}\mathcal{P}(\Theta)\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\delta\Theta)\\
\sin(\delta\Theta)\end{pmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}\Theta=2\pi p_{0}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}+O(\delta).

(74)

Thus the first term in (
29
) makes an
O(1)
contribution to
\hat{\bm{L}}(\varphi)
. To evaluate the second term, we approximate the integral involved as

\displaystyle\int\frac{\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}{\cos(\delta\Theta-\varphi)-\mathrm{i}\mu}\,\mathrm{d}\Theta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\cos\varphi}\int\mathcal{P}(\Theta)\,\mathrm{d}\Theta+O(\delta)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi p_{0}}{\cos\varphi}+O(\delta).

(75)

This is also
O(1)
except near
\varphi=\pm\pi/2
. There we use the rescaled variables
\Phi_{\pm}=(\varphi\mp\pi/2)/\delta
to write

\displaystyle\int\frac{\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}{\cos(\delta\Theta-\varphi)-\mathrm{i}\mu}\,\mathrm{d}\Theta

\displaystyle=\pm\int\frac{\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}{\sin(\delta\Theta-\delta\Phi_{\pm})\mp\mathrm{i}\mu}\,\mathrm{d}\Theta

\displaystyle=\pm\delta^{-1}\int\frac{\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}{\Theta-\Phi_{\pm}\mp\mathrm{i}\mu}\,\mathrm{d}\Theta+O(\delta).

(76)

Introducing (
74
), (
75
) and (
76
) into (
29
), we find

\hat{\bm{L}}(\varphi)\sim\frac{16}{g\bar{H}_{s}^{2}}\times\begin{cases}-\displaystyle{\delta^{-2}\partial_{\Phi_{+}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}{\Theta-\Phi_{+}-\mathrm{i}\mu}\,\mathrm{d}\Theta\,\,\bm{e}_{\bm{q}}^{\perp}+O(1)}&\textrm{for}\ \ |\varphi-\pi/2|=O(\delta)\\
\displaystyle{\delta^{-2}\partial_{\Phi_{-}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}{\Theta-\Phi_{-}+\mathrm{i}\mu}\,\mathrm{d}\Theta\,\,\bm{e}_{\bm{q}}^{\perp}+O(1)}&\textrm{for}\ \ |\varphi+\pi/2|=O(\delta)\\
\displaystyle{-2\pi p_{0}\left(\frac{\sin\varphi}{\cos^{2}\varphi}\,\bm{e}_{\bm{q}}^{\perp}+\begin{pmatrix}2\\
0\end{pmatrix}\right)}&\textrm{for}\ \ |\varphi\mp\pi/2|=O(1)\end{cases}.

(77)

Under the assumption of a localised current,
we now derive an approximation for
h_{s}({\bm{x}})
by introducing (
77
) in the inverse Fourier transform

h_{s}({\bm{x}})/\bar{H}_{s}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\hat{\bm{L}}(\varphi)\bcdot\hat{\bm{U}}(\bm{q})\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\bm{q}\bcdot{\bm{x}}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{q}

(78)

of (
18
). We compute explicitly the contribution of the boundary layer at
\varphi=\pi/2
; the contribution of the other boundary layer is its complex conjugate, denoted by
\mathrm{c.c.}
, and is added. Using the polar representation of
\bm{q}
and the approximation

\bm{q}=q(-\delta\Phi,1)+O(\delta^{2}),

(79)

where we denote
\Phi_{+}
by
\Phi
for simplicity, we obtain

\displaystyle h_{s}({\bm{x}})\sim

\displaystyle\frac{16\delta^{-1}}{(2\pi)^{2}g\bar{H}_{s}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\hat{U}(0,q)q\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}qy}\,\mathrm{d}q\int\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\Phi}\int\frac{\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}{\Theta-\Phi-\mathrm{i}\mu}\,\mathrm{d}\Theta\right)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\delta qx\Phi}\,\mathrm{d}\Phi

\displaystyle+\mathrm{c.c.},

(80)

Here we implicitly assume that
x=O(\delta^{-1})
since this turns out to be the range of
x
for which
h_{s}
is the largest.
Integrating by parts in
\Phi
and swapping the order of the
\Theta
and
\Phi
integrations produces

h_{s}({\bm{x}})\sim\frac{16\mathrm{i}x}{(2\pi)^{2}g\bar{H}_{s}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\hat{U}(0,q)q^{2}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}qy}\,\mathrm{d}q\int\mathcal{P}(\Theta)\left(\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi}{\Theta-\Phi-\mathrm{i}\mu}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}q\delta x\Phi}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\Theta+\mathrm{c.c.}

(81)

The
\Phi
-integral can be evaluated as a contour integral. For
x\geq 0
, we close the contour in the lower half plane where the simple pole
\Phi=\Theta-\mathrm{i}\mu
is located. In the limit
\mu\to 0^{+}
the integral becomes
2\pi\mathrm{i}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}q\delta x\Theta}
, leading to

h_{s}({\bm{x}})\sim-\frac{8x}{\pi g\bar{H}_{s}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\hat{U}(0,q)q^{2}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}qy}\,\mathrm{d}q\int\mathcal{P}(\Theta)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}q\delta x\Theta}\,\mathrm{d}\Theta+\mathrm{c.c.}.

(82)

Recognising the integral with respect to
\Theta
as the Fourier transform
\hat{\mathcal{P}}(q\delta x)
of
\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
, we obtain the compact expression

h_{s}({\bm{x}})\sim-\frac{8x}{\pi g\bar{H}_{s}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\hat{U}(0,q)\hat{\mathcal{P}}(q\delta x)q^{2}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}qy}\,\mathrm{d}q+\mathrm{c.c.}\quad\textrm{for}\ \ x\geq 0.

(83)

For
x<0
, the integration contour can be closed in the upper half
\Phi
-plane, where there are no poles, hence
h_{s}({\bm{x}})=0
for
x<0
. The reality conditions
\hat{U}^{*}(0,q)=\hat{U}(0,-q)
and
\hat{\mathcal{P}}^{*}(q\delta x)=\hat{\mathcal{P}}(-q\delta x)
can then be used to reduce the result to:

h_{s}({\bm{x}})\sim-\frac{8x}{\pi g\bar{H}_{s}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{U}(0,q)\hat{\mathcal{P}}(q\delta x)q^{2}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}qy}\,\mathrm{d}q\quad\textrm{for}\ \ x\geq 0,

(84)

and
h_{s}({\bm{x}})=0
for
x<0
.

We now show that (
84
) is equivalent to the result obtained by
Wang et al. 2023
in the linear regime
\varepsilon\ll\delta\ll 1
. In the notation of the present paper, their result reads

h_{s}({\bm{x}})=-\frac{\bar{H}_{s}}{2}\int D^{\prime}(\Theta)\Delta(y-x\Theta)\mathrm{d}\Theta\quad\textrm{for}\ \ x\geq 0,

(85)

where
D(\Theta)
is the (normalised) angular distribution of the spectrum and

\Delta(y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{c_{g\star}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\!\zeta(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}x,

(86)

with
c_{g\star}
the peak group speed. The function
\Delta(y)
can be written in terms of the Fourier transform of the current as

\Delta(y)=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\pi c_{g\star}}\int q_{2}\hat{U}(0,q_{2})\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}q_{2}y}\,\mathrm{d}q_{2}.

(87)

Introducing (
87
) into (
85
)

h_{s}=-\frac{\bar{H}_{s}x}{4\pi c_{g\star}}\int\hat{U}(0,q)\hat{D}(qx)q^{2}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}qy}\mathrm{d}q\quad\textrm{for}\ \ x\geq 0.

(88)

The result in (
88
) is shown to be identical to (
84
) by noting that

\displaystyle\bar{H}_{s}^{2}D(\theta)

\displaystyle=16g^{-1}\iint\sigma(k)\mathcal{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}k\mathrm{d}k\mathrm{d}\theta\,D(\theta)=32g^{-1}\iint c_{g}\mathcal{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}k^{2}\mathrm{d}k\mathrm{d}\theta

(89)

\displaystyle\approx 32g^{-1}c_{g\star}\mathcal{P}(\theta).

(90)

Here we use the definition (
30
) of
\mathcal{P}(\theta)
in the case of a separable spectrum and we approximate
c_{g}(k)\approx c_{g\star}
as appropriate for a highly directional spectrum.

Appendix D
Axisymmetric vortex

For a purely vortical, axisymmetric flow, with
\hat{\phi}(q)=0
and
\hat{\psi}(q)=-q^{-2}\hat{\zeta}(q)
,
combining (
40
) into (
42
) yields

h_{s}(\bm{q})=-\frac{16\mathrm{i}}{g\bar{H}_{s}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}n(-\mathrm{i})^{|n|}\,2\pi\tilde{p}_{n}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\varphi}\,q^{-1}\hat{\zeta}(q)\,.

(91)

The inverse Fourier transform is

h_{s}({\bm{x}})=-\frac{16\mathrm{i}}{g\bar{H}_{s}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}n(-\mathrm{i})^{|n|}\,\frac{2\pi\tilde{p}_{n}}{2\pi}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!\hat{\zeta}(q)\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}qr\cos(\varphi-\nu)+\mathrm{i}n\varphi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi}{2\pi}\,\mathrm{d}q\,\,.

(92)

In the
\varphi
-integral above,
\nu
is the polar angle in physical space. The
\varphi
-integral can be reduced to Bessel functions

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}qr\cos(\varphi-\nu)+\mathrm{i}n\varphi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi}{2\pi}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}qr\cos\alpha}\cos(n\alpha)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\alpha}{2\pi}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\nu}

(93)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}^{|n|}J_{|n|}(qr)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\nu}\,.

(94)

Substituting (
94
) into (
92
) we obtain

h_{s}({\bm{x}})=-\frac{16\mathrm{i}}{g\bar{H}_{s}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}n\tilde{p}_{n}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!\hat{\zeta}(q)J_{|n|}(qr)\,\mathrm{d}q\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\nu},

(95)

valid for any vortical axisymmetric flow.
For the Gaussian vortex in (
47
) the
q
-integral reduces to

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!J_{|n|}(qr)\hat{\zeta}(q)\,\mathrm{d}q

\displaystyle=\kappa\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!J_{|n|}(qr)\mathrm{e}^{-r_{\mathit{v}}^{2}q^{2}/2}\,\mathrm{d}q

(96)

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa}{r_{v}}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-r^{2}/4r_{v}^{2}}I_{|n|/2}(r^{2}/4r_{v}^{2}),

(97)

where
I_{|n|/2}
is the modified Bessel function. This simplifies
h_{s}({\bm{x}})
in (
95
) to (
48
).
