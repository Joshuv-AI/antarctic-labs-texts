Algebras of length one Journal: Journal of Pure and Applied Algebra
O.V. Markova, C. Martínez, R.L. Rodrigues

Abstract
Let 𝒜 {\cal A} be an algebra over a field 𝔽 {\mathbb{F}} and let 𝒮 \mathcal{S} be a generating set of 𝒜 {\cal A} . The length of 𝒮 \mathcal{S} indicates the maximal length needed to express an arbitrary element of 𝒜 {\cal A} as a linear combination of words in the elements of 𝒮 \mathcal{S} . The length of an algebra 𝒜 {\cal A} is defined as the maximum of lengths of its generating sets. In this paper (not necessarily associative) algebras, over fields of arbitrary characteristic, having length equal to 1 are determined.

Introduction

In the present paper, we will denote by
{\cal A}
a unital (with identity element
1_{{\cal A}}
) not necessarily associative algebra over a field
{\mathbb{F}}
.
Information about non-associative algebras can be found, for instance, in
[
16
,
19
,
21
]
.

Any product of a finite number of elements of a finite subset
\mathcal{S}\subset{\cal A}
is a word in the letters from
\mathcal{S}
. The length of a word is equal to the number of letters different of
1_{{\cal A}}
in the corresponding product. By convention,
1_{{\cal A}}
is a word in any subset
{\mathcal{S}}

of length 0.

If
\mathcal{S}
is a generating system of the algebra
{\cal A}
, that is,
{\cal A}
is the smallest subalgebra of
{\cal A}
containing
\mathcal{S}
, then any element of
{\cal A}
can be expressed as a linear combination of words in elements of
\mathcal{S}
.
If we can express all elements of
{\cal A}
using words of length at most
k
, but we can not use only words of length at most
k-1
, we say that the length of the generating system is
k
. The length of a finitely generated algebra
{\cal A}
,
l({\cal A})
, is the maximal length of its finite generating systems.

Notice that the unital algebra
{\cal A}
has length equal to 0 if and only if
{\cal A}={\mathbb{F}}1_{{\cal A}}
. Otherwise length is a positive integer or infinite.

If
{\cal A}
is not unital, we can consider the unital hull of
{\cal A}
,
\tilde{{\cal A}}={\mathbb{F}}1_{{\cal A}}+{\cal A}
, and clearly
l({\cal A})=l(\tilde{{\cal A}})
.

A finite-dimensional associative algebra has always finite length and a finitely generated associative algebra with finite length has finite dimension. Algebras considered in this paper will be always finite-dimensional.

Guterman and Kudryavtsev in
[
4
,
5
]
started the study of length of non-associative algebras. In particular, they showed that any finite-dimensional non-associative algebra
{\cal A}
has finite length, and if
\dim{\cal A}=n>2
, then the length is bounded by
l({\cal A})\leq 2^{\dim{\cal A}-2}
.

The length of an algebra is an important invariant for the study of finite-dimensional algebras. In some sense it measures the multiplicative complexity of the algebra. The knowledge of a universal upper bound for the length gives the maximal size of products in generators that we need to consider in order to verify some property.
First of all, the length of a subset in a given algebra determines the complexity of the procedures verifying whether this set generates the algebra and computing a basis of the algebra from the generating set. For matrix algebras, properties under consideration include simultaneous reduction (by similarity) of a set of matrices to a canonical, for example, (block-)triangular form
[
3
]
, or unitary similarity
[
2
]
. That is, the length function has applications in computational methods of matrix theory.

Therefore, the study of algebras whose length is close to the minimal value is of interest. Clearly the minimal non-trivial value of the length is
1
. Matrix algebras of length
1
were described in
[
13
]
. The complete classification of associative algebras of length
1
is also known (see
[
14
]
).

A similar question for non-associative algebras had not been addressed until now, may be because the problem, in a totally general context, seems somehow wild.
It was unclear what kind of tools should be used. In this paper we address this question and give a complete description of algebras of length
1
over an arbitrary field
{\mathbb{F}}
, mainly using methods of linear algebra and linearization techniques.

Our main result gives a characterization of algebras of length 1 in terms of a basis whose multiplication table satisfies some precise conditions.

1
Preliminaries

In order to place the problem in the proper context and to make it easier to readers we will start with some general definitions and results.

Notation 1.1
.

Given
\mathcal{S}\subseteq{\cal A}
,
{\mathbb{F}}\mathcal{S}
(or
{\mathbb{F}}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
, if
\mathcal{S}=\left\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\right\}
) denotes the vector space linearly spanned by the elements of
\mathcal{S}
and
{\rm alg}(\mathcal{S})
(or
{\rm alg}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
, if
\mathcal{S}=\left\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\right\}
) will represent the subalgebra of
{\cal A}
generated by
\mathcal{S}
, that is,
{\rm alg}(\mathcal{S})=\cap\ {\cal B}
, where
{\cal B}
runs over all subalgebras of
{\cal A}
with
\mathcal{S}\subseteq{\cal B}
.

The set of all words in
\mathcal{S}
with length
not greater than
i
is denoted by
\mathcal{S}^{i}
, for
i\geq 0
and the set
{\cal L}_{i}(\mathcal{S})
stands for
{\mathbb{F}}\mathcal{S}^{i}
. Clearly
\displaystyle\bigcup_{i\geq 0}\mathcal{L}_{i}(\mathcal{S})={\rm alg}(\mathcal{S})
.

Remark 1.2
.

A subset
\mathcal{S}\subset{\cal A}
is a generating set of
{\cal A}
if and only if
{\cal A}={\rm alg}(\mathcal{S})
.

With these definitions and notations in mind we may now introduce formally the concept of length, both for
\mathcal{S}
and for
{\cal A}
, as follows.

Definition 1.3
.

The length of a finite subset
\mathcal{S}
of
{\cal A}
is

l(\mathcal{S})={\rm min}\left\{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}:{\rm alg}(\mathcal{S})={\cal L}_{k}(\mathcal{S})\right\}.

Definition 1.4
.

The length of a finitely generated algebra
{\cal A}
is

l({\cal A})={\rm max}\left\{l(\mathcal{S}):{\cal A}={\rm alg}(\mathcal{S})\hskip 14.22636pt\mathcal{S}\ {\rm finite}\right\}.

Given a set
\mathcal{S}
its length evaluation is a standard linear algebraic problem: the construction of a basis of
{\rm alg}(\mathcal{S})
. But its computational complexity comes from the fact that the number of words in each
\mathcal{S}^{i}
grows exponentially. We would like to emphasize that the different behaviour in associative and non-associative cases is linked to the different properties of the sequence
\{\dim{\cal L}_{i}(\mathcal{S})\},\ i=0,1,\ldots
. In the associative case this sequence is strictly increasing, that is,
\dim{\cal L}_{i}(\mathcal{S})<\dim{\cal L}_{i+1}(\mathcal{S})
, until it stabilizes at step
l(\mathcal{S})
, that is,
{\cal L}_{l(\mathcal{S})}(\mathcal{S})={\rm alg}(\mathcal{S})
and
\dim{\cal L}_{n}(\mathcal{S})=\dim{\rm alg}(\mathcal{S})
for every
n\geq l(\mathcal{S})
. In the non-associative case the sequence
\{\dim{\cal L}_{i}(\mathcal{S})\},\ i=0,1,\ldots
is non-decreasing, that is,
\dim{\cal L}_{i}(\mathcal{S})\leq\dim{\cal L}_{i+1}(\mathcal{S})
until it stabilizes. But in the non-associative case the equality
{\cal L}_{i}(\mathcal{S})={\cal L}_{i+1}(\mathcal{S})
does not imply that
{\cal L}_{i}(\mathcal{S})={\rm alg}(\mathcal{S})
. However, for a given
n\geq 1
, it is sufficient to have

\dim{\cal L}_{n}(\mathcal{S})=\dim{\cal L}_{n+1}(\mathcal{S})=\ldots=\dim{\cal L}_{2n}(\mathcal{S})

to guarantee that the identity
\dim{\cal L}_{n}(\mathcal{S})=\dim{\cal L}_{n+t}(\mathcal{S})=\dim{\rm alg}(\mathcal{S})
holds for any
t\geq 1
, as it was established in
[
5
, Proposition 2.3]
. Consequently, the equality
{\cal L}_{1}(\mathcal{S})={\cal L}_{2}(\mathcal{S})
implies that
{\cal L}_{1}(\mathcal{S})={\cal L}_{t}(\mathcal{S})={\rm alg}(\mathcal{S})
for all
t\geq 2
.

Applications of the length function appear, for example, in computational methods of matrix theory, since the length determines the complexity of some rational procedures. Al’pin and Ikramov extended the classical Specht criterion for unitary similarity between two complex
n\times n
matrices to unitary similarity between two normal matrix sets of cardinality
m
. In the same way, the well-known result of Pearcy extending Specht’s theorem can be used as a finiteness criterion. The complexity of this criterion depends both, on
n
and on the length of algebras under their analysis
[
2
,
3
]
.

The problem of computing lengths of the matrix algebra
M_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
, as a function of the matrix size
n
, was first addressed by Paz
[
18
]
in 1984, and still remains open. The known bounds are non-linear functions of
n
, see
[
18
,
17
,
20
]
. Linear bounds for specific generating sets has been found in
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
. See also references therein.

In the case of arbitrary associative algebras Pappacena
[
17
]
provided an upper bound for the length of any finite-dimensional associative algebra
{\cal A}
as a function of two invariants of
{\cal A}
: its dimension and
m({\cal A})
, the maximal degree of minimal polynomials of elements in the algebra. In
[
12
]
a stronger bound, depending of the same two parameters, was obtained for commutative algebras. A study of algebraic properties of the length function, namely the length of the direct sum, can be found in
[
6
]
.

Let us remember the classification of associative algebras of length 1.

Theorem 1.5

(
[
14
, Theorem 3]
)
.

Let
{\mathbb{F}}
be an arbitrary field and let
{\cal A}
be a finite-dimensional associative unital
{\mathbb{F}}
-algebra. Then
l({\cal A})=1
if and only if it is one of the algebras in the following list:

(1)

{\cal A}\cong{\mathbb{F}}\oplus{\mathbb{F}}
;

(2)

{\cal A}
is a field,
\dim{\cal A}=2
;

(3)

{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{F}}_{2}
,
{\cal A}\cong{\mathbb{F}}\oplus{\mathbb{F}}\oplus{\mathbb{F}}
;

(4)

dim

{\cal A}/J({\cal A})=1
,
J({\cal A})\neq 0
,
J({\cal A})^{2}=0
, where
J({\cal A})
denotes the Jacobson radical of
{\cal A}
;

(5)

There exist elements
e,f\in{\cal A}
such that

e^{2}=e,f^{2}=f,ef=fe=0,e+f=1;{\rm dim}\ e{\cal A}e={\rm dim}\ f{\cal A}f=1;f{\cal A}e=0,e{\cal A}f\neq 0.

Algebras of different types are not isomorphic. Two algebras
{\cal B},\mathcal{C}
of type
(4)
(respectively of type
(5)
) are isomorphic if and only if
{\rm dim}\ {\cal B}={\rm dim}\ \mathcal{C}
.

From now on we will not assume associativity of the considered algebras. Our aim is to determine algebras of length 1, without any additional assumption.

2
General results

In this section we give some general results about algebras of length 1. The main result of the section gives a characterization of them that is essential for the rest of results in this paper. Some consequences of this result are given explicitly. In particular, algebras of length 1 are proved to be power-associative.

To start, let us remark that
l({\cal A})=1
means that if
\mathcal{S}
is an arbitrary generating set of
{{\cal A}}
and
\bar{\mathcal{S}}=\mathcal{S}\cup\{1_{{\cal A}}\}
, then
\bar{\mathcal{S}}
linearly spans
{\cal A}
, that is,
{\cal A}=\rm{alg}(\mathcal{S})
implies
{\cal A}={\mathbb{F}}\bar{\mathcal{S}}
. If
{\cal A}
is a finitely generated algebra over a field
{\mathbb{F}}
and
l({\cal A})=1
, then
{\cal A}
is finite-dimensional.

Next proposition will play the main role to get our description of algebras of length 1 in terms of the existence of a basis with a known multiplication table.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
{\cal A}
be an
{\mathbb{F}}
-algebra of dimension
n
with identity element
1_{{\cal A}}
.
{\cal A}
has length
1
if and only if
ab\in{\mathbb{F}}(1_{{\cal A}},a,b)
, for all
a,b\in{\cal A}
.

Proof.

Let
{\cal A}
be an
{\mathbb{F}}
-algebra of length 1. We will prove first that
a^{2}\in{\mathbb{F}}(1_{A},a)
. If
a\in{\mathbb{F}}(1_{{\cal A}})
, the result is obvious. Therefore we can assume w.l.g. that
\left\{1_{{\cal A}},a\right\}
is a linearly independent set, and extend it to a basis
\left\{s_{1}=a,s_{2}=1_{{\cal A}},s_{3},\ldots,s_{n}\right\}
of
{\cal A}
. So
a^{2}=\alpha_{1}a+\alpha_{2}1_{{\cal A}}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}s_{n}
for some
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\in{\mathbb{F}}
. If
a^{2}\notin{\mathbb{F}}(1_{{\cal A}},a)
, then there is some
\alpha_{i}\neq 0
,
i>2
. We can assume (reordering the basis if needed) that
\alpha_{3}\neq 0
. This implies that
s_{3}\in{\rm alg}(s_{1}=a,s_{2}=1_{{\cal A}},s_{4},\ldots,s_{n})
. Consequently
{\cal A}={\rm alg}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime})\neq{\mathbb{F}}\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
, where
\overline{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}=\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=\mathcal{S}\setminus\left\{s_{3}\right\}
, a contradiction.

If
a,b\in{\cal A}
, let us consider
\left\{1_{{\cal A}},a,b\right\}
. For
\left\{1_{{\cal A}},a,b\right\}
linearly dependent the result directly follows from what has been proved above. So we can assume that
\left\{1_{{\cal A}},a,b\right\}
are linearly independent and extend this set (as we did before) to a basis
\left\{1_{{\cal A}},a,b,s_{4},\ldots,s_{n}\right\}
. Then
ab=\lambda_{1}1_{{\cal A}}+\lambda_{2}a+\lambda_{3}b+\lambda_{4}s_{4}+\cdots+\lambda_{n}s_{n}
for some
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\in{\mathbb{F}}
. If there is some
\lambda_{i}\neq 0
,
i\geq 4
(we can assume
\lambda_{4}\neq 0)
, then considering
\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}=\mathcal{S}\setminus\left\{s_{4}\right\}
we have, as before,
{\cal A}={\rm alg}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime})\neq{\mathbb{F}}\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}
, a contradiction.

The converse is clear.
∎

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
{\cal A}
be a finite dimensional algebra with identity element
1_{{\cal A}}
over a field
{\mathbb{F}}
. If
l({\cal A})=1
, then
a^{2}\in{\mathbb{F}}(1_{{\cal A}},a)
, for all
a\in{\cal A}
.

Next results immediately follow from Proposition
2.1
.

Corollary 2.3
.

Every unital algebra of dimension
2
over an arbitrary field has length
1
.

Let us notice that any 2-dimensional unital algebra is associative.

A power-associative algebra over a field
{\mathbb{F}}
is an algebra in which every subalgebra generated by a single element is associative. Examples include associative, Lie, Jordan and alternative algebras.

Since the unital subalgebra generated by an element of an algebra
{\cal A}
with
l({\cal A})=1
has dimension at most 2, it is associative. Now next corollary immediately follows.

Corollary 2.4
.

An algebra
{\cal A}
of length
1
over a field
{\mathbb{F}}
is power-associative.

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a subalgebra of a unital finite-dimensional
{\mathbb{F}}
-algebra
{\cal A}
. If
l({\cal A})=1
, then
l(\mathcal{B})=1
.

Notice that the above Corollaries
2.2
and
2.5
extend results of Corollary 2.2 and Lemma 2.1 of
[
13
]
from associative case.

In general, it is not true that
l({\cal B})\leq l({\cal A})
for any
{\cal B}
subalgebra of
{\cal A}
, even in the associative case (see
[
8
]
).

3
Characteristic different from 2

In this section we will address the aim of the paper in the case of characteristic different from 2. So in this section
{\mathbb{F}}
will denote a field with
{\rm char}({\mathbb{F}})\neq 2
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
{\cal A}
be an
{\mathbb{F}}
-algebra of dimension
n
and length
1
with identity element
1_{{\cal A}}
. Then
there is a basis
{\cal B}
of
{\cal A}
such that
b^{2}\in{\mathbb{F}}1_{{\cal A}}
, for every
b\in{\cal B}
.

Proof.

Let
\left\{a_{1}=1_{{\cal A}},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}\right\}
be a basis of
{\cal A}
. According to Corollary
2.2
, we can write
a_{i}^{2}=\alpha_{i}1_{{\cal A}}+\gamma_{i}a_{i}
, for some
\alpha_{i},\gamma_{i}\in{\mathbb{F}}
, for all
2\leq i\leq n
. It is easy to check that the basis
{\cal B}=\left\{a_{1}=1_{{\cal A}},a_{2}-\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{2}1_{{\cal A}},\ldots,a_{n}-\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{n}1_{{\cal A}}\right\}
satisfies the required conditions.
∎

Definition 3.2
.

A basis
{\cal B}=\left\{1_{{\cal A}},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}\right\}
of a unital algebra
{\cal A}
satisfying that
a_{i}^{2}=\mu_{i}1_{{\cal A}}
for some
\mu_{i}\in{\mathbb{F}}
,
2\leq i\leq n
will be called canonical basis.

Notice that Lemma
3.1
says that an algebra of length 1 has some canonical basis.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
{\cal A}
be an
{\mathbb{F}}
-algebra of length 1 with identity element
1_{{\cal A}}
and let
{\cal B}=\left\{a_{1}=1_{{\cal A}},a_{2},a_{3},\ldots,a_{n}\right\}
be a canonical basis of
{\cal A}
. Then
a_{i}a_{j}+a_{j}a_{i}\in{\mathbb{F}}1_{{\cal A}}
, for every
2\leq i\neq j\leq n
.

Proof.

Since
{\cal B}
is a canonical basis,
a_{i}^{2}=\mu_{i}1_{{\cal A}}
, for some
\mu_{i}\in{\mathbb{F}}
,
2\leq i\leq n
.

By Proposition
2.1
,
a_{i}a_{j}\in{\mathbb{F}}(1_{{\cal A}},a_{i},a_{j})
for every pair of indices
i\neq j\in\{2,\ldots,n\}
. So, there are elements
\alpha_{ij},\beta_{ij},\beta_{ji}^{*},\alpha_{ji},\beta_{ji},\beta_{ij}^{*}\in{\mathbb{F}}
,
2\leq i<j\leq n
, such that
a_{i}a_{j}=\alpha_{ij}1_{{\cal A}}+\beta_{ij}a_{i}+\beta_{ji}^{*}a_{j}
and
a_{j}a_{i}=\alpha_{ji}1_{{\cal A}}+\beta_{ji}a_{j}+\beta_{ij}^{*}a_{i}
.

By Corollary
2.2
we know that for an arbitrary
\lambda\in{\mathbb{F}}
,
(a_{i}+\lambda a_{j})^{2}\in{\mathbb{F}}(1_{{\cal A}},a_{i}+\lambda a_{j}).
Since
(a_{i}+\lambda a_{j})^{2}=a_{i}^{2}+\lambda a_{i}a_{j}+\lambda a_{j}a_{i}+\lambda^{2}a_{j}^{2}=

\displaystyle=(\mu_{i}+\lambda^{2}\mu_{j})1_{{\cal A}}+\lambda(\alpha_{ij}1_{{\cal A}}+\beta_{ij}a_{i}+\beta_{ji}^{*}a_{j})+\lambda(\alpha_{ji}1_{{\cal A}}+\beta_{ji}a_{j}+\beta_{ij}^{*}a_{i}),

we conclude that the coefficient of
a_{j}
is
\lambda
times the coefficient of
a_{i}
, that is,
\lambda(\beta_{ji}^{*}+\beta_{ji})=\lambda^{2}(\beta_{ij}+\beta_{ij}^{*}).

Since
|{\mathbb{F}}|\geq 3
and every
\lambda\in{\mathbb{F}}
is a root of
x(\beta_{ji}^{*}+\beta_{ji})=x^{2}(\beta_{ij}+\beta_{ij}^{*}),
we conclude that
\beta_{ji}^{*}+\beta_{ji}=0
for every distinct
i,j\in\{2,\ldots,n\}
. That is,
a_{i}a_{j}=\alpha_{ij}1_{{\cal A}}+\beta_{ij}a_{i}-\beta_{ji}a_{j}
,
a_{j}a_{i}=\alpha_{ji}1_{{\cal A}}+\beta_{ji}a_{j}-\beta_{ij}a_{i}
.

This implies that
a_{i}a_{j}+a_{j}a_{i}=(\alpha_{ij}+\alpha_{ji})1_{{\cal A}}
, and completes the proof.
∎

We will consider first the case
{\rm dim}\ {\cal A}=3
, since it behaves in a different way.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
{\cal A}
be a unital
{\mathbb{F}}
-algebra of dimension
3
.
If there is a canonical basis
\left\{a_{1}=1_{{\cal A}},a_{2},a_{3}\right\}
such that
a_{2}a_{3}+a_{3}a_{2}\in{\mathbb{F}}1_{{\cal A}}
then
l({\cal A})=1
.

Proof.

Let
a
and
b
be arbitrary elements of
{\cal A}
. If
\mathcal{S}=\left\{1_{{\cal A}},a,b\right\}
is a linearly independent set, then it is a basis of
{\cal A}
and so
ab\in{\mathbb{F}}\mathcal{S}={\mathbb{F}}(1_{{\cal A}},a,b)
.

Otherwise, there are scalars
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in{\mathbb{F}}
, not all of them equal to zero, such that
\alpha 1_{{\cal A}}+\beta a+\gamma b=0
.

If
\gamma=0
, then
\alpha 1_{{\cal A}}+\beta a=0
, that is,
a\in{\mathbb{F}}1_{{\cal A}}
. This implies that

ab\in{\mathbb{F}}b\subseteq{\mathbb{F}}(1_{{\cal A}},b)
.

If
\gamma\neq 0
,
b\in{\mathbb{F}}(1_{{\cal A}},a)
, so
ab\in{\mathbb{F}}(a,a^{2})
. So, in order to prove that
l({\cal A})=1
, we only need to show that
a^{2}\in{\mathbb{F}}(1_{{\cal A}},a)
, for every
a\in{\cal A}
.

Given
a=\lambda_{1}1_{{\cal A}}+\lambda_{2}a_{2}+\lambda_{3}a_{3}\in{\cal A}
, using that
{\cal B}
is a canonical basis it follows that
a^{2}=

=\lambda_{1}(\lambda_{1}1_{{\cal A}}+\lambda_{2}a_{2}+\lambda_{3}a_{3})+\lambda_{2}a_{2}(\lambda_{1}1_{{\cal A}}+\lambda_{2}a_{2}+\lambda_{3}a_{3})+\lambda_{3}a_{3}(\lambda_{1}1_{{\cal A}}+\lambda_{2}a_{2}+\lambda_{3}a_{3})

=2\lambda_{1}(\lambda_{1}1_{{\cal A}}+\lambda_{2}a_{2}+\lambda_{3}a_{3})-\lambda_{1}^{2}1_{{\cal A}}+\lambda_{2}^{2}a_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{3}^{2}a_{3}^{2}+\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}(a_{2}a_{3}+a_{3}a_{2})

=2\lambda_{1}a-\lambda_{1}^{2}1_{{\cal A}}+\lambda_{2}^{2}a_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{3}^{2}a_{3}^{2}+\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}(a_{2}a_{3}+a_{3}a_{2})\in{\mathbb{F}}(1_{{\cal A}},a)
,

since
a_{2}a_{3}+a_{3}a_{2},a_{2}^{2},a_{3}^{2}\in{\mathbb{F}}1_{{\cal A}}
.
∎

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
{\cal A}
be a
3
-dimensional
{\mathbb{F}}
-algebra with identity element
1_{{\cal A}}
. Then
l({\cal A})=1
if and only if there is a canonical basis
\left\{a_{1}=1_{{\cal A}},a_{2},a_{3}\right\}
of
{\cal A}
such that
a_{2}a_{3}+a_{3}a_{2}\in{\mathbb{F}}1_{{\cal A}}
.

Definition 3.6
.

Let
{\cal A}
be a unital
{\mathbb{F}}
-algebra. A canonical basis of
{\cal A}
,
{\cal B}=\left\{1_{{\cal A}},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}\right\}

(a_{i}^{2}=\mu_{i}1_{{\cal A}},\mu_{i}\in{\mathbb{F}})
will be called special if there are elements
\alpha_{ij},\beta_{i}\in{\mathbb{F}}
, for every
2\leq i\neq j\leq n
satisfying
a_{i}a_{j}=\alpha_{ij}1_{{\cal A}}+\beta_{j}a_{i}-\beta_{i}a_{j}
.

We have proved in Lemma
3.1
that a unital algebra
{\cal A}
of length 1 has always a canonical basis. Furthermore, Lemma
3.3
gives a way to get a canonical basis from any basis of
{\cal A}
that contains
1_{{\cal A}}
. Next proposition will show that an arbitrary canonical basis of
{\cal A}
is special.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
{\cal A}
be a unital algebra of length
1
. Then an arbitrary canonical basis
{\cal B}=\left\{1_{{\cal A}},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}\right\}
is special.

Proof.

Assume
l({\cal A})=1
. The case
{\rm dim}(A)\leq 3
is obvious, by Lemma
3.3
. So we will assume
{\rm dim}({\cal A})\geq 4
. Using Lemma
3.3
we know the existence of scalars
\mu_{i}
,
i\in\left\{2,\ldots,n\right\}
and
\alpha_{ij},\beta_{ij}
for arbitrary distinct indices
i,j\in\left\{2,\ldots,n\right\}
such that
a_{i}^{2}=\mu_{i}1_{{\cal A}}
,

Definition 3.9

A Jordan algebra is an algebra

over a field

,
{\rm char}\ {\mathbb{F}}\neq 2

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
{\mathbb{F}}
be a proper extension of
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
and
{\cal A}
a unital
{\mathbb{F}}
-algebra of dimension
3
. Then
l({\cal A})=1
if and only if there is a basis
{\cal B}=\left\{a_{1}=1_{{\cal A}},a_{2},a_{3}\right\}
of
{\cal A}
whose multiplication table satisfies the following conditions:

(i)

a_{2}^{2}\equiv\delta_{2}a_{2}
,
a_{3}^{2}\equiv\delta_{3}a_{3}
,
\delta_{2},\delta_{3}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
;

(ii)

a_{2}a_{3}\equiv\beta_{2}a_{2}+\beta_{3}^{\ast}a_{3}
,
a_{3}a_{2}\equiv\beta_{3}a_{3}+\beta_{2}^{\ast}a_{2}
,
\beta_{2}
,
\beta_{2}^{\ast}
,
\beta_{3}
,
\beta_{3}^{\ast}\in{\mathbb{F}}
and
\beta_{2}+\beta_{2}^{\ast}+\delta_{3}=0=\beta_{3}+\beta_{3}^{\ast}+\delta_{2}
.

Proof.

Assume that
l({\cal A})=1
. Take
\left\{b_{1}=1_{{\cal A}},b_{2},b_{3}\right\}
a basis of
{\cal A}
. By Corollary
2.2
we know that
b_{i}^{2}\equiv\gamma_{i}b_{i}
,
i=2,3
, for some
\gamma_{i}\in{\mathbb{F}}
. If
\gamma_{i}\neq 0
, taking
a_{i}=\gamma_{i}^{-1}b_{i}
, we get a basis
{\cal B}=\left\{1_{{\cal A}},a_{2},a_{3}\right\}
of
{\cal A}
satisfying that
a_{2}^{2}\equiv\delta_{2}a_{2}
and
a_{3}^{2}\equiv\delta_{3}a_{3}
,
\delta_{i}=0
or
1
, that is,
\delta_{2},\delta_{3}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
.

Furthermore,
a_{2}a_{3}\equiv\beta_{2}a_{2}+\beta_{3}^{*}a_{3}
, and
a_{3}a_{2}\equiv\beta_{3}a_{3}+\beta_{2}^{*}a_{2}
for some
\beta_{2},\beta_{3},\beta_{2}^{*},\beta_{3}^{*}\in{\mathbb{F}}
, by Proposition
2.1
.

Corollary
2.2
implies that, for all
\lambda\in{\mathbb{F}}
,

(a_{2}+\lambda a_{3})^{2}\equiv\delta_{2}a_{2}+\lambda(\beta_{2}a_{2}+\beta_{3}^{*}a_{3}+\beta_{3}a_{3}+\beta_{2}^{*}a_{2})+\lambda^{2}\delta_{3}a_{3}\in{\mathbb{F}}(1_{{\cal A}},a_{2}+\lambda a_{3}).

This implies that
\lambda(\delta_{2}+\lambda\beta_{2}+\lambda\beta_{2}^{*})=\lambda\beta_{3}^{*}+\lambda\beta_{3}+\lambda^{2}\delta_{3}
, that is,
\lambda(\beta_{3}^{*}+\beta_{3}+\delta_{2})=\lambda^{2}(\beta_{2}+\beta_{2}^{*}+\delta_{3}).

Since
|{\mathbb{F}}|\geq 4
, we can conclude that
\beta_{3}^{*}+\beta_{3}+\delta_{2}=0=\beta_{2}+\beta_{2}^{*}+\delta_{3}
.

Conversely, let us assume the existence of a basis whose multiplication table satisfies the conditions (i) and (ii), as described in the theorem. By Proposition
2.1
we only need to prove that for arbitrary elements
a,b
of
{\cal A}
,
ab\in{\mathbb{F}}(1_{{\cal A}},a,b)
. Since this claim is obviously true when
\{1_{{\cal A}},a,b\}
is a basis, we only have to consider the case in which the 3 elements are linearly dependent, what reduces to prove that, for an arbitrary element
a\in{\cal A}
the element
a^{2}\in{\mathbb{F}}(1_{{\cal A}},a)
. Since every element
a\in{\cal A}
can be expressed as
a=\mu 1_{{\cal A}}+a^{\prime}
, where
a^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{F}}(a_{2},a_{3})
, and
a^{2}=\mu^{2}1_{{\cal A}}+a^{\prime 2}
, the claim is true for any
a\in{\mathbb{F}}(1_{{\cal A}},a_{2},a_{3})
if and only if it is true for any
a^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{F}}(a_{2},a_{3})
. So, let us assume that
a=\lambda_{2}a_{2}+\lambda_{3}a_{3}
. First of all notice that
a_{2}a_{3}+a_{3}a_{2}\equiv\delta_{3}a_{2}+\delta_{2}a_{3}
. Then

a^{2}=\lambda_{2}^{2}a_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}(a_{2}a_{3}+a_{3}a_{2})+\lambda_{3}^{2}a_{3}^{2}\equiv\lambda_{2}^{2}\delta_{2}a_{2}+\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}(\delta_{2}a_{3}+\delta_{3}a_{2})+\lambda_{3}^{2}\delta_{3}a_{3}.

If
\delta_{2}=\delta_{3}=\delta
, we get
a^{2}\equiv(\lambda_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3})\delta a_{2}+(\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}+\lambda_{3}^{2})\delta a_{3},
that is,

a^{2}\equiv(\lambda_{2}+\lambda_{3})\delta(\lambda_{2}a_{2}+\lambda_{3}a_{3}),

what proves the result in this case.

When
\delta_{2}\neq\delta_{3}
, we can assume, without loss of generality, that
\delta_{2}=1
and
\delta_{3}=0
. So

a^{2}\equiv\lambda_{2}^{2}a_{2}+\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}a_{3}=\lambda_{2}a,

what finishes the proof of the theorem.
∎

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
{\mathbb{F}}
be a proper extension of
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
and
{\cal A}
a unital
{\mathbb{F}}
-algebra of dimension
3
. Then
l({\cal A})=1
if and only if there is a basis
{\cal B}^{\ast}=\left\{a_{1}^{\ast}=1_{{\cal A}},a_{2}^{\ast},a_{3}^{\ast}\right\}
whose multiplication table satisfies one of the following 3 conditions:

1.

{a_{2}^{\ast}}^{2}\equiv 0,{a_{3}^{\ast}}^{2}\equiv 0,a_{2}^{\ast}a_{3}^{\ast}\equiv 0
,
a_{3}^{\ast}a_{2}^{\ast}\equiv 0
;

2.

{a_{2}^{\ast}}^{2}\equiv a_{2}^{\ast}
,
{a_{3}^{\ast}}^{2}\equiv a_{3}^{\ast}
,
a_{2}^{\ast}a_{3}^{\ast}\equiv a_{3}^{\ast},a_{3}^{\ast}a_{2}^{\ast}\equiv a_{2}^{\ast}
;

3.

{a_{2}^{\ast}}^{2}\equiv 0,{a_{3}^{\ast}}^{2}\equiv a_{3}^{\ast}
,
a_{2}^{\ast}a_{3}^{\ast}\equiv 0
,
a_{3}^{\ast}a_{2}^{\ast}\equiv a_{3}^{\ast}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.4
we know that if
{\cal A}
is an algebra of length 1 then there is a basis
{\cal B}=\left\{a_{1}=1_{{\cal A}},a_{2},a_{3}\right\}
with a multiplication table given by

\displaystyle a_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\delta_{2}a_{2},

\displaystyle a_{3}^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\delta_{3}a_{3}

\displaystyle a_{2}a_{3}

\displaystyle\equiv\beta_{2}a_{2}+\beta_{3}^{\ast}a_{3},

\displaystyle a_{3}a_{2}\equiv

\displaystyle\beta_{3}a_{3}+\beta_{2}^{\ast}a_{2},

where
\delta_{2},\beta_{2},\beta_{2}^{\ast},\delta_{3},\beta_{3},\beta_{3}^{\ast}\in{\mathbb{F}}
, and
\beta_{2}+\beta_{2}^{\ast}+\delta_{3}=0=\beta_{3}+\beta_{3}^{\ast}+\delta_{2}.

We will distinguish 3 cases, when both
\delta_{2}
and
\delta_{3}
are either 0 or 1 and when one of them is 0 and the other is 1.

Case 1.
If
\delta_{2}=\delta_{3}=0
,
b_{2}^{2}\equiv 0
,
b_{3}^{2}\equiv 0
,
\beta_{2}+\beta_{2}^{\ast}=0=\beta_{3}+\beta_{3}^{\ast}
, then
\beta_{2}=\beta_{2}^{\ast}
,
\beta_{3}=\beta_{3}^{\ast}
, then
a_{2}a_{3}\equiv\beta_{2}a_{2}+\beta_{3}a_{3}\equiv a_{3}a_{2}
, then
a_{2}a_{3}+a_{3}a_{2}\equiv 0
.

Taking
a_{2}^{\ast}=\beta_{3}1_{{\cal A}}+a_{2}
,
a_{3}^{\ast}=\beta_{2}1_{{\cal A}}+a_{3}
, the basis
{\cal B}_{1}^{\ast}=\left\{a_{1}^{\ast}=1_{{\cal A}},a_{2}^{\ast},a_{3}^{\ast}\right\}
satisfies condition 1.

Case 2.
If
\delta_{2}=\delta_{3}=1
,
a_{2}^{2}\equiv a_{2}
,
a_{3}^{2}\equiv a_{3}
,
\beta_{2}^{\ast}=\beta_{2}+1
,
\beta_{3}^{\ast}=\beta_{3}+1
, then
a_{2}a_{3}\equiv\beta_{2}a_{2}+(\beta_{3}+1)a_{3}
,
a_{3}a_{2}\equiv(\beta_{2}+1)a_{2}+\beta_{3}a_{3}
.

Consider the basis
{\cal B}_{2}^{\ast}=\left\{a_{1}^{\ast}=1_{{\cal A}},a_{2}^{\ast}=a_{2}+\beta_{3}1_{{\cal A}},a_{3}^{\ast}=a_{3}+\beta_{2}1_{{\cal A}}\right\}
. It satisfies condition 2.

Case 3.
If
\delta_{2}=0,\delta_{3}=1
, then
\beta_{2}^{\ast}=\beta_{2}+1,

\beta_{3}^{\ast}=\beta_{3}
. So
a_{2}^{2}\equiv 0
,
a_{3}^{2}\equiv 1
,
a_{2}a_{3}=\beta_{2}a_{2}+\beta_{3}a_{3}
,
a_{3}a_{2}=\beta_{2}a_{2}+(\beta_{3}+1)a_{3}
. Take
a_{2}^{\ast}=a_{2}+\beta_{3}1_{{\cal A}}
and
a_{3}^{\ast}=a_{3}+\beta_{2}1_{{\cal A}}
. Then the basis
{\cal B}_{3}^{\ast}=\left\{a_{1}^{\ast}=1_{{\cal A}},a_{2}^{\ast},a_{3}^{\ast}\right\}
satisfies condition 3.
∎

Finally we will address the case of dimension greater than 3.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
{\mathbb{F}}
be a field of characteristic
2
and
{\cal A}
an
{\mathbb{F}}
-algebra with
{\rm dim}\ {\cal A}\geq 4
. Then
l({\cal A})=1
if and only if there is a basis
{\cal B}^{\ast}=\left\{a_{1}^{\ast}=1_{{\cal A}},a_{2}^{\ast},\ldots,a_{n}^{\ast}\right\}
of
{\cal A}
whose multiplication table satisfies one of the following two conditions:

(I)

{a_{i}^{\ast}}^{2}\equiv 0
, for
i=2,\ldots,n
,
a_{i}^{\ast}a_{j}^{\ast}\equiv a_{j}^{\ast}a_{i}^{\ast}\equiv\beta_{ij}a_{i}^{\ast}+\beta_{ji}a_{j}^{\ast}
,

(II)

{a_{i}^{\ast}}^{2}\equiv a_{i}^{\ast}
, for
i=2,\ldots,n
,
a_{i}^{\ast}a_{j}^{\ast}\equiv\beta_{ij}a_{i}^{\ast}+(1+\beta_{ji})a_{j}^{\ast}
.

Notice that in the last case
a_{i}^{\ast}a_{j}^{\ast}+a_{j}^{\ast}a_{i}^{\ast}\equiv a_{i}^{\ast}+a_{j}^{\ast}
.

Proof.

Assume
l({\cal A})=1
and consider
\left\{1_{{\cal A}},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n}\right\}
a basis of
{\cal A}
. Then
b_{i}^{2}\equiv\delta_{i}b_{i}
, for some
\delta_{i}\in{\mathbb{F}}
,
2\leq i\leq n
, by Corollary
2.2
.

Arguing as in Theorem
4.4
, we can assume that
\delta_{i}\in\left\{0,1\right\}
, for all
2\leq i\leq n
.

Furthermore, by Proposition
2.1
,
b_{i}b_{j}\in{\mathbb{F}}(1_{{\cal A}},b_{i},b_{j})
, for all
2\leq i\neq j\leq n
. So
b_{i}b_{j}\equiv\beta_{ij}b_{i}+\beta_{ji}^{*}b_{j}
, for some
\beta_{ij},\beta_{ji}^{*}\in{\mathbb{F}}
. Using again Proposition
2.1
, we know that for all distinct
i,j,k\in\left\{2,\ldots,n\right\}
, the following conditions hold:

(i)

b_{i}(b_{j}+b_{k})\equiv\beta_{ij}b_{i}+\beta_{ji}^{*}b_{j}+\beta_{ik}b_{i}+\beta_{ki}^{*}b_{k}\in{\mathbb{F}}(1_{{\cal A}},b_{i},b_{j}+b_{k})
, which implies
\beta_{ji}^{*}=\beta_{ki}^{*}
. Let us define
\beta_{i}^{*}=\beta_{ji}^{*}
for any
j\neq i\in\left\{2,\ldots,n\right\}
;

(ii)

(b_{j}+b_{k})b_{i}\equiv\beta_{ji}b_{j}+\beta_{ij}^{*}b_{i}+\beta_{ki}b_{k}+\beta_{ik}^{*}b_{i}\in{\mathbb{F}}(1_{{\cal A}},b_{j}+b_{k},b_{i})
, which implies
\beta_{ji}=\beta_{ki}
. Let us define
\beta_{i}=\beta_{ji}
for any
j\neq i\in\left\{2,\ldots,n\right\}
;

(iii)

(b_{i}+b_{j})(b_{i}+b_{k})\equiv\delta_{i}b_{i}+\beta_{k}b_{i}+\beta_{i}^{*}b_{k}+\beta_{i}b_{j}+\beta_{j}^{*}b_{i}+\beta_{k}b_{j}+\beta_{j}^{*}b_{k}\in{\mathbb{F}}(1_{{\cal A}},b_{i}+b_{j},b_{i}+b_{k})
which implies
\delta_{i}+\beta_{k}+\beta_{j}^{*}=\beta_{i}+\beta_{k}+\beta_{i}^{*}+\beta_{j}^{*}
. Then, by (i) and (ii), we can conclude that
\beta_{i}+\beta_{i}^{*}=\delta_{i}
.

Therefore,
b_{i}b_{j}\equiv\beta_{j}b_{i}+(\beta_{i}+\delta_{i})b_{j}
and
b_{j}b_{i}\equiv\beta_{i}b_{j}+(\beta_{j}+\delta_{j})b_{i}
, for all
i,j\in\left\{2,\ldots,n\right\}
such that
i\neq j
.

Claim.
We can assume that
\delta_{2}=\cdots=\delta_{n}
.

Indeed, if either all scalars are 0 or all scalars are 1, there is nothing to prove. Suppose that
r-1
scalars are 0 and
n-r
are 1. Without loss of generality (reordering elements), we can assume that
\delta_{2}=\cdots=\delta_{r}=0
and
\delta_{r+1}=\cdots=\delta_{n}=1
.

Observe that

(b_{s}+b_{r+1})^{2}\equiv\delta_{s}b_{s}+\beta_{r+1}b_{s}+\beta_{s}^{*}b_{r+1}+\beta_{s}b_{r+1}+\beta_{r+1}^{*}b_{s}+\delta_{r+1}b_{r+1}

=(\beta_{r+1}+\beta_{r+1}^{*})b_{s}+(\beta_{s}+\beta_{s}^{*}+1)b_{r+1}=\delta_{r+1}b_{s}+(\delta_{s}+1)b_{r+1}=b_{s}+b_{r+1},

for all
2\leq s\leq r
.

Thus,
{\cal B}=\left\{1_{{\cal A}},a_{2}=b_{2}+b_{r+1},\ldots,a_{r}=b_{r}+b_{r+1},a_{r+1}=b_{r+1},\ldots,a_{n}=b_{n}\right\}
is a basis of
{\cal A}
such that
a_{i}^{2}\equiv a_{i}
.

In any case, we can consider a basis
{\cal B}^{\ast}=\left\{a_{1}^{\ast}=1_{{\cal A}},a_{2}^{\ast},\ldots,a_{n}^{\ast}\right\}
of
{\cal A}
such that
{a_{i}^{\ast}}^{2}\equiv\delta a_{i}^{\ast}
, for all
2\leq i\leq n
, where
\delta\in\left\{0,1\right\}
. Clearly,
\delta=0
gives statement (1), and
\delta=1
gives statement (2).

Conversely, suppose that there is a basis
{\cal B}^{\ast}=\left\{a_{1}^{\ast}=1_{{\cal A}},a_{2}^{\ast},\ldots,a_{n}^{\ast}\right\}
that satisfies either (I) or (II). Let
a=\lambda_{1}1_{{\cal A}}+\cdots+\lambda_{n}{a_{n}}^{\ast}
and
b=\gamma_{1}1_{{\cal A}}+\cdots+\gamma_{n}a_{n}^{\ast}
be arbitrary elements of
{\cal A}
. Then

Case 1:
If
{a_{i}^{\ast}}^{2}\equiv 0
, for
i=2,\ldots,n
,
a_{i}^{\ast}a_{j}^{\ast}\equiv a_{j}^{\ast}a_{i}^{\ast}\equiv\beta_{j}a_{i}^{\ast}+\beta_{i}a_{j}^{\ast}
,
for all
i\neq j=2,\ldots,n
, then

ab\equiv(\gamma_{2}\beta_{2}+\cdots+\gamma_{n}\beta_{n})a+(\lambda_{2}\beta_{2}+\cdots+\lambda_{n}\beta_{n})b\in{\mathbb{F}}(1_{{\cal A}},a,b).

Case 2:
If
{a_{i}^{\ast}}^{2}\equiv a_{i}^{\ast}
, for
i=2,\ldots,n
,
a_{i}^{\ast}a_{j}^{\ast}\equiv\beta_{j}a_{i}+(1+\beta_{i})a_{j}
, for all
i\neq j=2,\ldots,n
, then

ab\equiv(\gamma_{2}\beta_{2}+\cdots+\gamma_{n}\beta_{n})a+(\lambda_{2}+\cdots+\lambda_{n}+\lambda_{2}\beta_{2}+\cdots+\lambda_{n}\beta_{n})b\in{\mathbb{F}}(1_{{\cal A}},a,b).

In both cases, we can conclude that
l({\cal A})=1
, by Proposition
2.1
, which completes our proof.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

If
a=\lambda_{1}1_{{\cal A}}+\lambda_{2}a_{2}+\cdots+\lambda_{n}a_{n}
is an arbitrary element of an algebra
{\cal A}
satisfying Theorem
4.6
(I) then
a^{2}\equiv 0
. Similarly, if
{\cal A}
satisfies Theorem
4.6
(II) then
a^{2}\equiv(\lambda_{2}+\cdots+\lambda_{n})a.
So Theorem
4.6
is a kind of classification theorem in the line of Theorem
1.5
. The same applies to Theorem
4.2
and Corollary
4.5
.

Conclusion

We have given a complete characterization of algebras of length 1, without additional conditions neither on the algebra nor on the characteristic of the field.

In case of characteristic 2 we have obtained a kind of classification similar to the one obtained in the associative case (see Theorem
1.5
).

In case of
{\rm char}({\mathbb{F}})\neq 2
, our result characterizes algebras of length one through the existence of a particular basis (an
special basis
). Of course there are classification problems that remain opened and that probably only could be addressed under additional conditions on the algebra.

However our result allows the construction of an easy algorithm to decide if a given algebra, with some known basis and the corresponding multiplication table, has length one.
