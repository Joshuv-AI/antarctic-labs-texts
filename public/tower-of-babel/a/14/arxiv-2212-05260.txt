Examining marginal properness in the external validation of survival models with squared and logarithmic losses
Raphael Sonabend*, John Zobolas*, Riccardo De Bin, Philipp Kopper, Lukas Burk, Andreas Bender

Abstract
Scoring rules promote rational and honest decision-making, which is important for model evaluation and becoming increasingly important for automated procedures such as ‘AutoML’. In this paper we survey common squared and logarithmic scoring rules for survival analysis, with a focus on their theoretical and empirical properness. We introduce a marginal definition of properness and show that both the Integrated Survival Brier Score (ISBS) and the Right-Censored Log-Likelihood (RCLL) are theoretically improper under this definition. We also investigate a new class of losses that may inform future survival scoring rules. Simulation experiments reveal that both the ISBS and RCLL behave as proper scoring rules in practice. The RCLL showed no violations across all settings, while ISBS exhibited only minor, negligible violations at extremely small sample sizes, suggesting one can trust results from historical experiments. As such we advocate for both the RCLL and ISBS in external validation of models, including in automated procedures. However, we note practical challenges in estimating these losses including estimation of censoring distributions and densities; as such further research is required to advance development of robust and honest evaluation in survival analysis.

Introduction

In survival analysis, model evaluation has traditionally focused on concordance indices, which assess the ranking ability of predictions
[
24
,
18
,
50
,
4
]
.
While widely used, these measures solely evaluate discrimination, i.e., a model’s ability to distinguish between high- and low-risk individuals.
However, survival models can also be used to make predictions about the distribution of an individual’s risk over time, necessitating evaluation metrics beyond concordance indices.
Scoring rules provide an alternative by assessing the overall predictive ability of a model, incorporating both calibration and discrimination
[
37
,
16
]
.
Over the past decades, these measures have gained popularity as probabilistic forecasts are recognized to be superior to deterministic predictions in quantifying uncertainty
[
11
,
12
]
.
Formalisation and development of scoring rules have primarily been due to Dawid
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
and Gneiting and Raftery
[
16
]
; although the earliest measures promoting “rational” and “honest” decision-making date back to the 1950s
[
6
,
19
]
.
In classification and (probabilistic) regression
[
21
]
settings there are established definitions for scoring rules and specific losses have been defined, most popular of which are the Brier score (or ‘squared loss’)
[
6
]
and Log loss (or ‘logarithmic loss’)
[
19
]
.
Scoring rules have also been extensively studied in Bayesian decision theory
[
3
]
, where they serve as tools for evaluating and comparing probabilistic predictions.
In the context of survival analysis, early discussions on loss functions for censored data can be found in Korn
et al.

[
31
]
, while Graf
et al.

[
20
]
introduced the use of inverse probability of censoring weighting (IPCW) to account for censoring.

In this paper, we examine the properness of a subset of scoring rules proposed in survival analysis.
We consider properness both theoretically and in practice, with the latter assessing if losses exhibit properness (even when theoretically improper) in simulation experiments.
Following the popularity seen in classification and regression settings, we focus on squared and logarithmic losses, which are two of the most widely used measure classes for evaluating survival models as evidenced in recent benchmark studies
[
28
,
54
,
35
,
5
,
25
]
.
For comprehensive reviews incorporating absolute losses and other variations of scoring rules, including
R^{2}
forms, we recommend Hielscher
et al.

[
26
]
, Choodari-Oskooei
et al.

[
8
,
7
]
, and Rahman
et al.

[
39
]
.
We limit our scope to measures utilized for external validation of survival models and thus exclude losses that require additional hyperparameter estimation, as is often the case in deep learning model optimization
[
34
,
40
,
48
,
23
,
55
]
.
We place particular emphasis on established scoring rules to ensure a fair and neutral evaluation, while briefly exploring a novel class of losses to stimulate further research.

In

2
Notation

Throughout this paper we assume only right-censoring is present in the data and focus on continuous-time models and measures.
We use the following notation throughout.
Let
Y,C
be random variables taking values in
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R},\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
respectively.
In addition, define
T:=\min(Y,C)
, and
\Delta:=\mathbb{I}(T=Y)
, with
\mathbb{I}
denoting the indicator function where
\mathbb{I}(A)=1
if the logical statement
A
is true, otherwise
\mathbb{I}(A)=0
.
We interpret these as follows:
Y
is the theoretical (partially unobservable) survival time (or time of event of interest),
C
is the theoretical censoring time,
T
is the observable outcome time, and
\Delta
is the event indicator (1 if the outcome is the event of interest and
T=Y
, 0 otherwise and
T=C
).
Lower-case letters refer to observations of random variables, for example
(t,\delta)
are observed outcomes of
(T,\Delta)
.

In survival analysis all time variables are non-negative and therefore we define random variables below with this in mind.
Let
Z
be some random variable, then
f_{Z}(z)
is the probability density function,
F_{Z}(z)=\int^{z}_{0}f_{Z}(u)\ du
is the cumulative distribution function and
S_{Z}(z)=1-F_{Z}(z)
is the survival function.
To simplify notation, we use
\hat{S}
to refer to an estimation of the survival function (usually from some survival model), which may be an unconditional
\hat{S}(t)
or covariate-dependent
\hat{S}(t|X)
estimate where
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
is a matrix of co-variates.
Further,
\hat{F}
and
\hat{f}
are the estimated cumulative distribution and density functions respectively.
When presented within the same equation then it is assumed
\hat{S}(t)=1-\hat{F}(t)=1-\int^{t}_{0}\hat{f}(u)\ du
.
Subscripts are used to indicate the above functions defined by some random variable, in particular
S_{Y}
, and
S_{C}
refer to the survival functions of the theoretical survival time and theoretical censoring time, analogously for the probability density functions and cumulative distribution functions.

Finally, as a shorthand we write
\bar{\mathbb{R}}
to denote the extended Reals:
\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty,+\infty\}
.

3
Properness definitions for survival losses

Before moving into the survival setting, we first revisit the definition of properness in the probabilistic classification setting (probabilistic regression is analogous) and justify the requirement for strict properness
[
16
,
13
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a family of distributions over
[0,1]
, let
\mathcal{Y}=\{0,1\}
, let
p_{Y}
be the true distribution of interest. Then, for any
p_{Y},p
in
\mathcal{P}
and for any
Y\sim p_{Y}
; a classification loss
L_{C}:\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\bar{\mathbb{R}}
is called

1.

proper
if
\underset{Y\sim p_{Y}}{\mathbb{E}}[L_{C}(p_{Y},Y)]\leq\underset{Y\sim p_{Y}}{\mathbb{E}}[L_{C}(p,Y)]
;

2.

strictly proper

\underset{Y\sim p_{Y}}{\mathbb{E}}[L_{C}(p_{Y},Y)]\leq\underset{Y\sim p_{Y}}{\mathbb{E}}[L_{C}(p,Y)]
with equality if and only if
p=p_{Y}
.

A loss is proper if the optimal prediction (the one that matches the truth) minimizes the loss; but this theoretical definition is not always practical.
For example,
L_{42}(p,Y):=42
is
technically
proper as it is minimized by the optimal prediction, but it clearly fails to distinguish between models.
Whilst being technically proper,
L_{42}
fails to meet the actual objective of a proper scoring rule, which is to encourage truthful predictions by comparing the ground truth to the prediction
[
19
]
.
However,
L_{42}
is not strictly proper as the truth does not
uniquely
minimize the loss; by definition, strictly proper losses must encourage truthful predictions.

Before moving to the survival setting we state (with proof in
Appendix

A
) the following proposition, which serves as a recurring reference throughout the paper (see also Gneiting
et al.

[
16
]
):

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
L_{A}
and
L_{B}
be two loss functions over a target space
\mathcal{Y}
and let
\alpha\in[0,1]
be independent of
L_{A}
,
L_{B}
, the prediction, and the outcome.
Define
L_{\alpha}
as the convex sum of two loss functions:

L_{\alpha}:=\alpha L_{A}+(1-\alpha)L_{B}

Then,

1.

If both
L_{A}
and
L_{B}
are proper,
L_{\alpha}
is
proper
;

2.

If at least one of
L_{A}
and
L_{B}
are strictly proper, and the other is proper, then
L_{\alpha}
is
strictly proper
.

Early discussions on survival loss functions can be found in Korn
et al.

[
31
]
and Graf
et al.

[
20
]
, however neither paper formally defined the concept of a scoring rule or of properness in survival analysis.
Later papers have also derived scoring rules without defining these concepts, instead depending on the definitions found in probabilistic regression (e.g. see Avati
et al.

[
1
]
).

To our knowledge, the first formal definitions of properness for survival scoring rules appear in Sonabend
et al.

[
45
]
, Rindt
et al.

[
41
]
and Yanagisawa
[
55
]
.
The latter two both define properness with expectations taken over the data-generating process
(Y,C)
, whereas earlier works (including those that discuss properness but without formal definitions) take expectations over the observed outcomes
(T,\Delta)
[
13
,
27
,
45
]
.
As
(T,\Delta)
are deterministic functions of
(Y,C)
these differences are technically equivalent but conceptually distinct.
We argue that defining properness in terms of
(T,\Delta)
better reflects the purpose of properness put forward in Gneiting and Raftery
[
16
]
, which states that the objective is to ‘reward’ a model for making predictions close to the observed outcome, here
(t,\delta)
.
The random variables
(T,\Delta)
have samples that can observed, which is not the case for
(Y,C)
.
This choice also highlights the core challenge of survival analysis: the observable variables,
(T,\Delta)
, are used to evaluate a prediction of an unobservable quantity,
Y
.

Perhaps a more important distinction is whether properness should be defined marginally, with respect to the fully observed data distribution – or locally – conditioned on censoring,
Y|C=c
, and/or covariates,
Y,C|X=x
[
41
]
.
Conditional properness, especially when taking expectations over
C
, involves conditioning on an unobservable quantity, which may limit the loss in practice.
In this paper, we advocate for marginal properness over
(T,\Delta)
which avoids this limitation.
As will be seen in
Section

5
, making use of IPCW can remove the need to implicitly condition on unobservable quantities.

With this grounding we can now define marginal properness for survival scoring rules:

Definition 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a family of distributions over
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, let
\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, and let
S_{Y}
be the true survival distribution of interest. Then, for any
S_{Y},S
in
\mathcal{P}
and for any
Y\sim S_{Y}
and
C
taking values in
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
with
T:=\min\{Y,C\}
and
\Delta:=\mathbb{I}(T=Y)
; a survival loss
L:\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{T}\times\{0,1\}\mapsto\bar{\mathbb{R}}
is called

1.

proper
if
\underset{(T,\Delta)}{\mathbb{E}}[L(S_{Y},T,\Delta)]\leq\underset{(T,\Delta)}{\mathbb{E}}[L(S,T,\Delta)]
;

2.

strictly proper
if
\underset{(T,\Delta)}{\mathbb{E}}[L(S_{Y},T,\Delta)]\leq\underset{(T,\Delta)}{\mathbb{E}}[L(S,T,\Delta)]
with equality if and only if
S=S_{Y}
.

To elucidate the above notation, in this setting
\mathcal{P}
is the class of all time-to-event distributions, then
S_{Y}
is a specific distribution with
Y\sim S_{Y}
, which could be a probability distribution such as Weibull(
\alpha
,
k
).
In practice,
S
will often be an approximation of
S_{Y}
from a (covariate dependent) survival model fit to covariates
X
and outcomes
(t,\delta)
, such as an accelerated failure time model, Cox proportional hazards model, or some machine learning method.
Below we adopt the notation
\hat{S}
in loss definitions to emphasize that these scores are evaluated on predicted survival distributions, distinguishing them from the true generating distribution
S_{Y}
.

For the remainder of this paper we assume
Y\perp\!\!\!\perp C
.
We take this to reflect either genuine independence in the single-event setting, or effective independence when modeling cause-specific outcomes in the competing risks setting.
Whilst researchers often assume conditional independence
[
15
,
41
,
33
,
38
]
,
Y\perp\!\!\!\perp C\lvert X
, this distinction is not relevant here as we focus on losses and not their estimators.

Before continuing, we state the following proposition (proof in Appendix
A
), which will prove valuable when considering properness of losses in the rest of this paper:

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\phi
be a real-valued measurable and integrable, such as an evaluation loss, and let
Y,C,T
be as defined above with
Y\perp\!\!\!\perp C
. Then,

\underset{(T,\Delta)}{\mathbb{E}}[\phi(\hat{S},T,\Delta)]=\int^{\infty}_{0}f_{Y}(t)S_{C}(t)\phi(\hat{S},t|\delta=1)+f_{C}(t)S_{Y}(t)\phi(\hat{S},t|\delta=0)\ dt\\

(1)

Note that if there is no censoring in the data generating process then
S_{C}(t)=1,f_{C}(t)=0\ \forall t
and the above reduces to
\int^{\infty}_{0}f_{Y}(t)\phi(\hat{S},t)\ dt=\mathbb{E}_{Y}[\phi(\hat{S},t)]
, recovering the regression setting
[
27
]
.

4
Squared and logarithmic losses

This paper focuses on squared and logarithmic scoring rules.
We investigate common losses in each group; in particular, we discuss the claimed properness properties of these losses in the literature and make propositions to prove or disprove these claims.
We separate discussion between
exact
(
Section

4.1
) and
IPCW
losses (
Section

4.2
), where the latter explicitly incorporate the censoring distribution in the loss calculation.
For each, we present losses observation-wise, for which the sample mean is taken over observations to yield a single score with properness following via
3.2
.
Further, we let
(t,\delta)
be an observed survival outcome (time and status), and let
\hat{S}
(and by extension
\hat{F}=1-\hat{S}
) be some survival function, which in practice is usually an estimate of
S_{Y}
.

4.1
Exact survival losses

We define the following survival losses:

•

Survival Continuous Ranked Probability Score (SCRPS)
[
1
]

L_{SCRPS}(\hat{S},t,\delta)=\int^{t}_{0}\hat{F}^{2}(\tau)\ d\tau+\delta\int^{\infty}_{t}\hat{S}^{2}(\tau)\ d\tau

(2)

•

Negative Log-Likelihood (NLL)

L_{NLL}(\hat{S},t)=-\log\left(\hat{f}(t)\right)

(3)

•

Right-Censored Log-Likelihood (RCLL)
[
1
]

L_{RCLL}(\hat{S},t,\delta)=-\log\left(\delta\hat{f}(t)+(1-\delta)\hat{S}(t)\right)

(4)

Avati
et al.

[
1
]
defined the SCRPS and assumed the properness of the loss (with no assumptions about censoring) by stating that it is the weighted CRPS, which is proper, however, no proof is given.
Rindt
et al.

[
41
]
claim improperness of this loss by first demonstrating that Avati’s assumption of properness due to the weighting is incorrect, and secondly by simulation.

The NLL is a very well-established loss in the regression and classification literature, but its first application to survival data appears to be in Goldstein
et al.

[
17
]
, who imply properness of the loss in the context of neural network optimization, however they do not provide a definition of properness for survival nor any properness proof.
This loss does not take into account the censoring status of an observation, treating all outcomes as events.

Finally, Avati
et al.

[
1
]
defined the RCLL, which is also a special case of the survival scoring rule proposed by Dawid
et al.

[
13
]
.
Rindt
et al.
prove the RCLL to be strictly proper under their own definition of properness when
Y\perp\!\!\!\perp C

[
41
,
55
]
.

For a loss function to be proper, the expected loss should be minimized by
\hat{S}=S_{Y}
.
However,
3.4
shows that the expectation of any ‘exact’ survival loss depends on the censoring distribution through
S_{C}
and
f_{C}
.
This dependence compromises the properness as the minimization is influenced by the censoring process and not solely by the target distribution.
Therefore, under our definition of properness (
Section

3
), SCRPS (
Equation

2
), RCLL (
Equation

4
), and NLL (
Equation

3
) are improper scoring rules.
Proofs of the first two claims are given in
Appendix

B
(NLL is clearly a special case of RCLL and thus proof follows analogously).

Note, as stated at the end of the last section, if there is no censoring, then the three exact losses above reduce to the CRPS and log-loss, respectively, both of which are strictly proper in the uncensored world
[
16
]
.

4.2
IPCW survival losses

The most common survival loss appears to be an adaptation of the Brier (or squared) score derived in Graf
et al.

[
20
]
.
This loss employs an inverse probability of censoring weighting (IPCW) approach to weight the loss by the probability of being censored.
In practice, the censoring distribution is estimated with a function
\hat{G}
which is either an unconditional estimator, such as the Kaplan-Meier estimator, or a conditional prediction, such as from a Cox proportional hazards model
[
9
]
.
Given a large enough sample for training, and assuming independence of
Y
and
C
, the Kaplan-Meier estimator converges to the true censoring distribution
[
30
]
.
However, the Kaplan-Meier estimator is biased when
Y
and
C
are not independent, even if
Y
and
C
are conditionally independent given covariates,
Y\perp\!\!\!\perp C\ |\ X

[
15
]
.
Choosing a conditional estimator is non-trivial as it introduces a recursive dependence: estimators must be evaluated to inform other estimators.
Furthermore, in high-dimensional settings (where covariates far exceed the number of samples,
p\gg n
), identifying relevant covariates to estimate
\hat{G}(X)
becomes particularly challenging, especially when using traditional models such as Cox regression
[
33
]
.
In practice, a slight bias is typically accepted, and the Kaplan-Meier estimator is more commonly used to approximate
S_{C}
, as demonstrated in several recent benchmark studies
[
28
,
54
,
33
,
35
,
46
,
5
,
25
,
29
,
22
]
.
Finally, we note that research into this area is nascent.
For example, only in the past year have researchers tested whether training, test, or complete datasets should be used to estimate the censoring distribution to avoid biased IPCW estimators
[
38
]
.

IPCW losses are frequently misrepresented as estimators, with the censoring distribution (usually the survival function of the censoring distribution,
S_{C}
), being represented by an estimate (usually denoted
\hat{G}
) in equations.
Properness is a property of
losses
and not
estimators
hence, we restrict our manuscript to the former and present these below (a poor estimator may violate many properties of a loss, not just properness).

All the IPCW survival losses listed below appear to be attributed to Graf
et al.

[
20
]
or are derivatives of this work.
Let
\tau^{*}\in\mathcal{T}
be the time to evaluate the score at or up to (depending whether it’s an integrated score or not),
(t,\delta)
the observed survival outcome (time and event status), and let the other variables be as defined in Section
2
.
Then we define,

•

Survival Brier Score
[
20
]

L_{SBS}(\hat{S},t,\delta,\tau^{*})=\frac{\hat{S}^{2}(\tau^{*})\mathbb{I}(t\leq\tau^{*},\delta=1)}{S_{C}(t)}+\frac{\hat{F}^{2}(\tau^{*})\mathbb{I}(t>\tau^{*})}{S_{C}(\tau^{*})}

(5)

•

Integrated Survival Brier Score
[
20
]

L_{ISBS}(\hat{S},t,\delta,\tau^{*})=\int^{\tau^{*}}_{0}L_{SBS}(\hat{S},t,\delta,\tau)\ d\tau=\int^{\tau^{*}}_{0}\frac{\hat{S}^{2}(\tau)\mathbb{I}(t\leq\tau,\delta=1)}{S_{C}(t)}+\frac{\hat{F}^{2}(\tau)\mathbb{I}(t>\tau)}{S_{C}(\tau)}\ d\tau

(6)

•

Integrated Survival Log-Likelihood
[
20
]

L_{ISLL}(\hat{S},t,\delta,\tau^{*})=-\int^{\tau^{*}}_{0}\frac{\log(\hat{F}(\tau))\mathbb{I}(t\leq\tau,\delta=1)}{S_{C}(t)}+\frac{\log(\hat{S}(\tau))\mathbb{I}(t>\tau)}{S_{C}(\tau)}\ d\tau

(7)

•

Integrated Binomial Log-Likelihood
[
41
]

L_{IBLL}(\hat{S},t,\delta,\tau^{*})=\int^{\tau^{*}}_{0}\frac{\log(\hat{F}(\tau))\mathbb{I}(t\leq\tau,\delta=1)}{S_{C}(t)}+\frac{\hat{S}(\tau)\mathbb{I}(t>\tau)}{S_{C}(\tau)}\ d\tau

(8)

The ISLL was first introduced by Graf
et al.

[
20
]
, but they did not claim properness. However, Han
et al.

[
23
]
later asserted that ISLL is proper.
Despite this claim, ISLL has seen limited use in both the literature and software, with only one implementation known to the authors
[
44
]
.
Additionally, Rindt
et al.

[
41
]
introduced the IBLL, which is closely related to ISLL, and proved it to be improper.

Graf
et al.

[
20
]
claimed properness for the uncensored version of the Integrated Brier Score (see IBS formula on page 2537, which does not use the IPCW), though without explicitly defining properness or providing a formal proof.
This is a known result in probabilistic regression
[
21
,
16
]
.
Rindt
et al.

[
41
]
argue that the ISBS (with IPCW) is generally improper but proper if
Y\perp\!\!\!\perp C
.
We also note the work of Kvamme
et al.

[
33
]
, which extends the ISBS to Type I censoring, though no claims of properness are made.

As covered in
Section

4.1
, exact losses are not proper under our definition of properness due to the dependence on censoring seen in
3.4
.
The IPC weighting introduced in Graf
et al.

[
20
]
tries to compensate for this dependency, but is only partially successful.
To see this, we first show that for a single evaluation time
\tau^{*}
and assuming
Y\perp\!\!\!\perp C
, then the SBS (
5
) is strictly proper.
We then discuss why extending to multiple time points invalidates properness.

To begin, the expectation of SBS is (full calculations in Appendix
B.3
):

\underset{(T,\Delta)}{\mathbb{E}}[L_{SBS}(\hat{S},T,\Delta)]=\hat{S}^{2}(\tau^{*})F_{Y}(\tau^{*})+\hat{F}^{2}(\tau^{*})S_{Y}(\tau^{*})

(9)

which is uniquely minimized by
\hat{S}(\tau^{*})=S_{Y}(\tau^{*})
, establishing strict properness for a given time-point
\tau^{*}
.
This final qualification is a highly important caveat.
If the goal is to assess a model’s performance specifically at
\tau^{*}
(and no other time-point), then the SBS is an appropriate validation measure.
Practitioners often compute the SBS across many time points to produce a
prediction error curve

[
36
]
; however, this does not yield a single summary score to compare predictions across different models.

Technically, one could minimize the SBS for each individual time-point
S(\tau)=\hat{S}(\tau)
, resulting in a score that looks like the ISBS and one might be tempted to invoke
3.2
to argue that the ISBS inherits properness as a weighted average of SBS scores.
However,
3.2
requires the aggregation weights
\alpha_{i}
to be independent of the individual scores, which fails for the ISBS (
6
) since its weights depend on the very time‐points used inside the loss.
As such, the conditions required for the preservation of properness in a sum or integral of scores do not hold.

Furthermore, the ISBS is not proper in its own right, with analytical proof by counter-example given in Appendix
B.4
.
Not only is the ISBS not analytically proper, but it can also be deliberately ‘gamed’.
To see this, consider the degenerate model which predicts a survival probability of exactly
1
up until some hyperparameter
\tau^{\dagger}
and thereafter predicts a probability of
0
:
\hat{S}(\tau)=\mathbb{I}(\tau<\tau^{\dagger})
.
Evaluating such a model at a single time-point
\tau
yields:

L_{SBS}(\mathbb{I}(\tau<\tau^{\dagger}),t,\delta,\tau)=\begin{cases}1/S_{C}(t),&\text{ if }\tau<\tau^{\dagger}\wedge t\leq\tau\wedge\delta=1\\
1/S_{C}(\tau),&\text{ if }\tau\geq\tau^{\dagger}\wedge t>\tau\\
0,&\text{ otherwise }\\
\end{cases}

The ISBS under this model is:

L_{ISBS}(\mathbb{I}(\tau<\tau^{\dagger}),t,\delta,\tau^{*})=\int^{\tau^{\dagger}}_{0}\frac{\mathbb{I}(t\leq\tau,\delta=1)}{S_{C}(t)}\ d\tau+\int_{\tau^{\dagger}}^{\tau^{*}}\frac{\mathbb{I}(t>\tau)}{S_{C}(\tau)}\ d\tau

(10)

When censoring mostly occurs late in time, the left-hand side of
Equation

10
can be very large as
S_{C}(t)\rightarrow 0
when the proportion of censoring quickly drops around
t
, in contrast
S_{C}(\tau)
remains close to
1
for earlier time-points.
This creates a strong incentive to reduce the contribution from the left-hand side, which can be done by decreasing
\tau^{\dagger}
.
In contrast, if most censoring occurs earlier (and either events also occur early or the proportion of censoring is high), then the left-hand side of
Equation

10
is
0
for the majority of time-points whereas the right-hand side can be very large as
S^{-1}_{C}(\tau)\rightarrow\infty
, hence the model is incentivized to reduce the right-hand side by increasing
\tau^{\dagger}
.
The model and loss are therefore both heavily influenced by the censoring distribution (even when
Y\perp\!\!\!\perp C
), allowing the loss to be ‘gamed’.
In our experiments we found that this degenerate model had a 14% improvement over Kaplan-Meier, outperformed a random survival forest by 3% and Cox model by 8% when tuning
\tau^{\dagger}
and evaluating on ISBS (see
Supporting information
).
This brings into question whether the ISBS is measuring meaningful predictive accuracy, or merely tracking the distribution of censoring over time.
Furthermore, our experiments suggest that the ISBS does not decompose cleanly into calibration and discrimination as previously stated
[
20
]
.
This is shown in our experiments in which the degenerate model performs best with respect to the ISBS but worse with respect to calibration (D-calibration
[
22
]
=
10^{10}
) and discrimination (Harrell’s C
[
24
]
=
0.5
) measures.

The practical implications of improperness are discussed in
Section

6
.

5
Investigating new classes of IPCW losses

Thus far, we have shown that exact losses are improper due to the dependence of censoring resulting from
3.4
, moreover, we have shown that existing IPCW losses are also improper because the chosen weights still leave an expression depending on
C
.
In this section, we consider how to use
3.4
to construct losses that remove the dependence on
C
.
Concretely, this results in three measure forms, for each let
\phi
be some real-valued measurable and integrable function and let
(t,\delta)
be the observed survival outcome (time and status), then:

L_{\phi 1}(\hat{S},t,\delta)=\frac{\delta}{S_{C}(t)}\phi(\hat{S},t,\delta=1)

(11)

L_{\phi 2}(\hat{S},t,\delta)=\frac{1-\delta}{f_{C}(t)}\phi(\hat{S},t,\delta=0)

(12)

L_{\phi 3}(\hat{S},t,\delta)=\delta\frac{\phi(\hat{S},t,\delta=1)}{S_{C}(t)}+(1-\delta)\frac{\phi(\hat{S},t,\delta=0)}{f_{C}(t)}

(13)

We can investigate these further by examining the expectations of each (calculations for
L_{\phi 3}
are in Appendix
C
, others are a special case).
Let
p_{\delta}:=P(\Delta=1)
, then:

\underset{(T,\Delta)}{\mathbb{E}}[L_{\phi 1}(\hat{S},T,\Delta)]=\int^{\infty}_{0}f_{Y}(t)\phi(\hat{S},t,\delta=1)\ dt=p_{\delta}\underset{Y}{\mathbb{E}}[\phi(\hat{S},T,\Delta=1)]

(14)

\underset{(T,\Delta)}{\mathbb{E}}[L_{\phi 2}(\hat{S},T,\Delta)]=\int^{\infty}_{0}S_{Y}(t)\phi(\hat{S},t,\delta=0)\ dt=(1-p_{\delta})\underset{S_{Y}}{\mathbb{E}}[\phi(\hat{S},T,\Delta=0)]

(15)

where
\underset{S_{Y}}{\mathbb{E}}
is the expectation over
Y
weighted by
S_{Y}(t)
.

\begin{split}\underset{(T,\Delta)}{\mathbb{E}}[L_{\phi 3}(\hat{S},T,\Delta)]&=\int^{\infty}_{0}f_{Y}(t)\phi(\hat{S},t,\delta=1)+S_{Y}(t)\phi(\hat{S},t,\delta=0)\ dt\\
&=p_{\delta}\underset{Y}{\mathbb{E}}[\phi(\hat{S},T,\Delta=1)]+(1-p_{\delta})\underset{S_{Y}}{\mathbb{E}}[\phi(\hat{S},T,\Delta=0)]\end{split}

(16)

The expectation of
L_{\phi 1}
can be interpreted as the conditional expectation of
\phi
over uncensored survival times.
Whereas
L_{\phi 2}
is the conditional expectation of
\phi
over censored observations weighted by the probability of survival after
t
, i.e., the contribution from the loss diminishes with the probability of survival.

L_{\phi 3}
is a combination of the two.

L_{\phi 1}
losses follow
[
27
,
45
,
51
]
.
With such losses, one has the option to compute the expectation using the sample mean over all
n
observations or uncensored observations only, i.e.,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\delta_{i}}{S_{C}(t_{i})}\phi(\hat{S}_{i},t_{i},\delta_{i}=1)\quad\text{ or }\quad\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\delta_{i}}{S_{C}(t_{i})}\phi(\hat{S}_{i},t_{i},\delta_{i}=1)

The formulations only differ by a constant so the ranking of observations remains the same.
In the left-hand case, censored observations are assigned the optimal score of zero.
As censoring increases, the aggregate loss collapses toward zero, regardless of model quality, and the estimator is effectively discouraged from learning the true data-generating distribution.
In scenarios with severe censoring, all models attain nearly zero loss regardless of their predictions and become indistinguishable.
In the right-hand case, as the proportion of censoring increases, the effective sample size decreases, leading to increased variance and reduced reliability for model comparison
[
42
]
.
As these losses are not practically useful, we do not explore them further.

L_{\phi 2}
losses are even less practical than
L_{\phi 1}
losses as they suffer from the same problems and only evaluate censored observations, for which even less information is known.

Combining the above losses results in the
L_{\phi 3}
class.
This class solves the problems of the other two by evaluating both censored and uncensored observations, and also does not depend on
C
.
However, one theoretical drawback of this loss form is that the weight on the right-hand side of
Equation

13
depends on
f_{C}(t)
.
Usually, IPCW losses use
S_{C}
in the denominator, which is bounded in
[0,1]
, but
f_{C}
is not upper-bounded and therefore could reduce the entire right-hand of the expression to zero.
In practice, this is often mitigated by estimating

f_{C}(t)
through interpolation of
S_{C}(t)
; this results in a density approximation that
can
still potentially greatly exceed
1
or be unstable when the sample size is small, so careful estimation remains essential.

We can investigate these losses further with a specific choice for
\phi
.
For example, substituting the RCLL (
4
) in
Equation

13
yields:

L_{RCLL*}(\hat{S},t,\delta)=\delta\frac{-\log\left(\hat{f}(t)\right)}{S_{C}(t)}+(1-\delta)\frac{-\log\left(\hat{S}(t)\right)}{f_{C}(t)}

(17)

With expectation (full calculations in Appendix
C
):

\mathbb{E}[L_{RCLL*}(\hat{S},t,\delta)]=\begin{cases}-\int^{\infty}_{0}f_{Y}(t)\log\left(\hat{f}(t)\right)\ dt,&\delta=1\\
-\int^{\infty}_{0}S_{Y}(t)\log\left(\hat{S}(t)\right)\ dt,&\delta=0\end{cases}

(18)

The two halves of the equation are respectively uniquely minimized by
\hat{f}=f_{Y}
and
\hat{S}=S_{Y}
(Jensen’s inequality), hence the total expression is minimized when the predicted distribution aligns with the true survival distribution and this is a strictly proper scoring rule.
It is interesting to note that the expectation of RCLL* (
Equation

18
) using our definition of properness gives the same final result as the expectation of the RCLL under Rindt
et al.
’s
[
41
]
alternative properness definition.
This convergence in results is due to the handling of censoring and which variables are considered when taking the expectation to calculate properness (
Section

4.2
).
In our definition, we rely on explicitly incorporating the censoring distribution within the loss, whereas Rindt
et al.
avoid this by conditioning in the expectation on an unobservable quantity.
As we directly estimate the distribution of
C
, this suggests that replacing
S_{C}(t)
and
f_{C}(t)
with
S_{C}(t|X)
and
f_{C}(t|X)

could
yield losses that perform well when
Y\perp\!\!\!\perp C|X
, though as discussed in
Section

4.2
this is a non-trivial problem and beyond the scope of this paper.

One could construct a similar loss for the SBS, however, as we have seen in the previous section this is already proper.
Extending this to the ISBS would also not solve the problems discussed in the previous section.

We believe there is significant potential for further development of
L_{\phi 3}
losses; however we defer this exploration for future work to ensure focus on examining existing losses.
A small pilot experiment to motivate future work is included in
Section

6
.

6
Experimental Results

In this section, we investigate whether survival scoring rules are proper
in practice
, according to the definition of properness given in
3.3
.
In particular, we test whether the scoring rules SBS (
5
), ISBS (
6
), RCLL (
4
) and RCLL* (
17
) satisfy the properness inequality across a wide range of survival distributions and sample sizes.
To this end, we structure our investigation into four parts:

1.

In
Section

6.2
we investigate properness using the true underlying survival, prediction, and censoring distributions, and very large sample sizes (
n
) to approximate the expectation for
3.3
via the law of large numbers.

2.

In
Section

6.3
we repeat the analysis but replace the true censoring distribution
S_{C}(t)
with an empirical Kaplan-Meier estimate
\hat{G}(t)
, reflecting how scores are typically computed in practice.

3.

In
Section

6.4
we investigate the reliability of scoring rules in real-world settings where evaluation is often performed on small or moderately sized test datasets.

4.

In
Section

6.5
we present initial pilot experiments investigating the behavior of the newly introduced RCLL* scoring rule.

These experiments offer a comprehensive evaluation of practical properness and help clarify whether prior uses of potentially improper scores (like ISBS) may have led to misleading model comparisons.

6.1
Simulation Design

To evaluate properness, we generate synthetic data using Weibull distributions, which define a flexible family
\mathcal{P}
of survival distributions.
Each dataset includes true event times (
y
), censoring times (
c
), and predicted event times (
\hat{y}
), from which observed times
t
and censoring indicators
\delta
are computed:

\displaystyle y_{i}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\text{Weibull}(\alpha_{Y},\sigma_{Y}),\quad\text{true survival times}

\displaystyle c_{i}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\text{Weibull}(\alpha_{C},\sigma_{C}),\quad\text{censoring times}

\displaystyle\hat{y}_{i}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\text{Weibull}(\alpha_{\hat{Y}},\sigma_{\hat{Y}}),\quad\text{`predicted' survival times}

\displaystyle t_{i}=\min\{y_{i},c_{i}\},\quad\delta_{i}=\mathbb{I}(t_{i}=y_{i}),\quad\text{observed outcome}

\displaystyle i=1,...,n,\quad\text{generated samples}

The index
i
refers to a particular sample within a distribution.
Shape and scale parameters (
\alpha,\sigma
) are drawn independently from:

\alpha_{Y},\sigma_{y},\alpha_{C},\sigma_{C},\alpha_{\hat{Y}},\sigma_{\hat{Y}}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\text{Uniform}(0.5,5)

(19)

This wide parameter range induces high variability across survival and censoring distributions (
Figure

1
), thereby increasing the likelihood of detecting violations of properness.
Since
Y\perp\!\!\!\perp C
by construction, the setup satisfies the random censoring assumption.

Figure 1:
Weibull density (top) and survival (bottom) curves for randomly generated shape,
\alpha
, and scale,
\sigma
parameters.
Each curve corresponds to a different parameter pair, illustrating the broad variability across simulated distributions.

To test properness across a wide range of settings and distributions, we conduct:

•

K=10{,}000
independent simulations

•

m=1{,}000
randomly sampled distributions per simulation

•

n\in\{10,50,100,250,500,750,1000\}
samples per distribution

Specifically, for each simulation
k\in{1,\dots,K}
(where each

k
denotes a complete, independent experiment with its own randomly sampled distribution parameters as in (
19
)), we generate a fixed set of
m=1000
independent Weibull distribution triplets indexed by
j\in{1,\dots,m}
.
Each triplet consists of a true survival distribution
S_{Y}^{j}
, a censoring distribution
S_{C}^{j}
, and a mismatched predicted distribution
\hat{S}^{j}
, all sampled as described above.
From each triplet we generate
n
samples by drawing event times, censoring times, and computing observed outcomes
(t_{i}^{j},\delta_{i}^{j})
.
The sample size
n
can be thought of as the number of test set observations for which predictions are evaluated.

For each distribution
j\in{1,\dots,m}
and observation-wise scoring rule
L_{\text{score}}
, we compute the mean score difference, D, across samples:

D_{\text{score}}^{k,j}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[L_{\text{score}}(S_{Y}^{j},t_{i}^{j},\delta_{i}^{j})-L_{\text{score}}(\hat{S}^{j},t_{i}^{j},\delta_{i}^{j})\right]

(20)

We then average over all
m
distribution draws to compute the simulation-level expected score difference:

\bar{D}_{\text{score}}^{k}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}D_{\text{score}}^{k,j}

(21)

We also record the empirical standard deviation across distribution-level differences,
s^{k}_{D}
, to quantify variability across the
m
draws:

s^{k}_{D}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(D^{k,j}_{\text{score}}-\bar{D}^{k}_{\text{score}}\right)^{2}}

(22)

To assess whether the observed average difference
\bar{D}_{\text{score}}^{k}
indicates a statistically significant violation, we construct a 95% confidence interval using a
t
-distribution, as the population variance of
D^{k,j}_{\text{score}}
is unknown:

\bar{D}_{\text{score}}^{k}\pm t_{0.975,m-1}\cdot\frac{s^{k}_{D}}{\sqrt{m}}

(23)

Keeping in mind our motivation to assess whether scoring rules like ISBS may have led to misleading model comparisons, we aim to evaluate whether a scoring rule can reliably distinguish models with genuinely different predictive performance.
Minor differences in scores between models with similar performance are often unavoidable but unlikely to impact practical decision-making, such as model selection or tuning.
In practice, such small inconsistencies are resolved through standard resampling procedures like K-fold cross-validation
Therefore we record a statistically significant violation in simulation
k
if
\bar{D}^{k}_{\text{score}}>\epsilon
for a small threshold
\epsilon>0
, and the confidence interval lower bound exceeds zero.
We adopt a conservative threshold of
\epsilon=10^{-3}
to focus on practically meaningful violations of properness.

This design allows us to systematically evaluate whether each scoring rule satisfies the properness inequality (
3.3
) across a large, diverse range of survival distributions and sample sizes.
Importantly, a violation means that the mismatched prediction
\hat{S}
receives a lower expected score than the true distribution
S_{Y}
, contradicting the definition of properness.

6.2
Properness Using Known Distributions

We evaluated empirical properness for the RCLL, SBS and ISBS scoring rules using the Weibull simulation design described in Section
6.1
.
RCLL is time-independent and relies on the survival
S(t)
and density
f(t)
functions (
Equation

4
).
SBS is evaluated at fixed time points
\tau^{*}
, chosen here as the 10th, 50th (median), and 90th percentiles of the observed times
t
(
Equation

5
).
ISBS integrates the Brier Score across time (
Equation

6
); we approximate it using 50 equidistant points from the 5th to the 80th percentile of
t
.
Both SBS and ISBS also depend on the censoring distribution
S_{C}(t)
.
All required quantities (
S
,
f
,
S_{C}
) are computed directly from the generated known Weibull distributions.

We performed
K=10{,}000
independent simulation experiments, each involving
m=1{,}000
draws of distribution triplets and use
n=1{,}000
samples per draw to approximate population expectations under the law of large numbers.
As shown in
Table

(see
Supporting Information
for complete table of results), none of the three scoring rules exhibited statistically significant violations of properness under this setup.
This includes ISBS and RCLL, which are theoretically improper, yet demonstrate empirical properness in the large-sample regime.

6.3
Estimating the Censoring Distribution

In this second experiment, we examine how empirical properness is affected when the true censoring distribution
S_{C}(t)
is unknown and must instead be estimated from data.
This is particularly relevant for scoring rules such as SBS and ISBS, which explicitly depend on
S_{C}(t)
through the inverse probability of censoring weighting (IPCW).
In contrast, RCLL does not depend on
S_{C}(t)
and therefore we exclude it in this experiment.
As discussed in
Section

4.2
, the Kaplan-Meier estimator is the standard nonparametric method for estimating
S_{C}(t)
under random censoring.
We estimate
\hat{G}(t)
using the Kaplan-Meier estimator applied to observed outcomes
(t_{i},1-\delta_{i})
, with
t_{i}=\min\{y_{i},c_{i}\}
and
\delta_{i}=\mathbb{I}(t_{i}=y_{i})
, across all
n
samples per distribution (see Section
6.1
).
To evaluate scores at arbitrary time points, we use constant interpolation of
\hat{G}(t)
, as is common in practice
[
47
]
.

We repeated the simulation design from the previous experiment with the IPCW-based scores estimated with
\hat{G}(t)
, and evaluated properness violations.
As shown in
Table

2
, neither SBS nor ISBS exhibited statistically significant violations in this setup.
This indicates that Kaplan-Meier estimation of the censoring distribution does not compromise the properness behavior of the SBS and ISBS scores when sample size is sufficiently large.

6.4
Robustness at Small Sample Sizes

In this experiment, we assess whether empirical properness holds at smaller sample sizes, reflecting real-world evaluation scenarios where sampling variability may obscure the theoretical guarantees of properness.
We simulate
K=10{,}000
runs across varying
n\in\{10,50,100,250,500,750\}
and evaluate violations for SBS, ISBS, and RCLL.
As before, the results using the Kaplan-Meier estimate
\hat{G}(t)
for censoring matched closely those using the true censoring distribution
S_{C}(t)
.
We found that (
Table

2
):

•

RCLL
showed no violations for any
n
, highlighting its strong empirical robustness - even though it is theoretically improper (
Section

4.1
).

•

SBS
exhibited violations that were both time-dependent and sample-size-dependent, with most violations occurring at early evaluation times and small
n
.
All violations disappeared for
n\geq 250
.
Importantly, the magnitude of differences remained small across all
n
, with mean
D_{\text{score}}<0.01
, indicating limited practical impact.

•

ISBS
showed minor violations only at
n=10
, with no violations for
n>50
, suggesting strong practical reliability in realistic settings.

These findings support the trustworthiness of prior evaluations using ISBS and RCLL.
Notably, ISBS proved more robust than SBS in low sample size regimes, despite SBS being theoretically strictly proper.
We attribute this to instability in inverse probability censoring weights when
\hat{G}(t)\to 0
, which often occurs at later time points.
Even with safeguards (e.g., capping
\hat{G}(t)
at
10^{-5}
), this instability disproportionately impacts SBS, whereas ISBS smooths over such fluctuations by integrating across many time points.
Based on these results, we recommend integrating ISBS only up to the 80th percentile of observed times to reduce censoring instability at the tail; a recommendation supported in prior work
[
2
,
54
,
46
]
.
Finally, RCLL emerges as a highly robust alternative for evaluation.

6.5
Investigating RCLL*

In this section, we present preliminary experiments with the RCLL* scoring rule to motivate future work on the development and evaluation of new scoring rules.
First, we assessed empirical properness of RCLL* using the Weibull-based simulation design introduced in
Section

6.1
.
Next, we repeated the experiment using an estimated censoring distribution, similar to the procedure from
Section

6.3
, to evaluate the impact of censoring estimation on practical properness.
Finally, we conducted a small-scale benchmark experiment using real survival datasets to explore whether RCLL* behaves differently from established survival metrics in practice.

To evaluate the RCLL* (
17
) we first estimated the censoring survival function
S_{C}(t)
using the Kaplan–Meier estimator, followed by linear interpolation of the resulting
\hat{G}(t)
over the ordered grid of observed time points
t_{0}\leq\cdots\leq t_{n}
.
The corresponding density
f_{C}(t)
was then derived by differentiating the interpolated
\hat{G}(t)
, after removing duplicate
\hat{G}
values to maintain strict monotonicity and ensure non-negative density values.
For extrapolation beyond the observed support, we extended the linear slope at the boundaries, preserving monotonicity and maintaining non-negative density.
In the small benchmark experiment using real-world datasets, model-predicted survival curves
\hat{S}(t)
were linearly interpolated using the same procedure as for the censoring distribution
\hat{G}
, and their densities
\hat{f}(t)
were obtained similarly by differentiation.
The full results are available in
Supporting Information
, in summary:

1.

The RCLL* appears
empirically
proper, with no violations to properness even when considering small sample sizes.

2.

When estimating the censoring distribution in the RCLL* with the Kaplan-Meier estimator, we still found the loss to be
practically
proper, with no violations.

3.

Comparing the RCLL* to the RCLL, ISBS, Harrell’s C
[
24
]
(higher C-index is better), and D-calibration
[
22
]
(lower D-calibration is better), we found that the RCLL* was better than the other scoring rules at identifying deviations from discrimination, calibration, and proportional hazards. For example:

(a)

On the
lung
dataset we found that the Cox model has the best RCLL and ISBS but the random survival forest had the best RCLL*, whilst the discrimination of the Cox model was slightly better (0.62 vs. 0.59), it’s calibration was significantly worse (23.6 vs 9.15).

(b)

7
Discussion and Conclusion

Survival analysis is widely applied in healthcare, finance, and engineering, where reliable models are essential for decision-making that directly affects public well-being.
Machine learning survival analysis
[
52
,
43
]
is also increasingly prevalent, especially the use of deep learning models
[
53
]
, which are hard to interpret and require robust methods of external validation to ensure they are appropriate for a given task.
As these models frequently make probabilistic predictions, scoring rules are essential to fully capture the quality of these predictions.
Proper scoring rules ensure that the loss accurately separates ‘good’ and ‘bad’ predictions, i.e., that are closer to the truth.
Strictly proper scoring rules are uniquely minimized by the ‘correct’ prediction, i.e., those that exactly equal the truth.
The most commonly used scoring rules in survival analysis are squared and logarithmic losses, which are also found in regression and classification settings.

In this paper, we introduced definitions of scoring rules and properness in a survival context, with specific marginal definitions for
Y\perp\!\!\!\perp C
settings.
We listed existing claims in the literature about commonly used squared and logarithmic losses for external validation, and supplemented these with our own analytical proofs of properness and improperness, as well as empirical experiments to examine the effect of these claims.
Under our definition of properness, we could only verify properness of the un-integrated, IPCW-based Brier Score proposed by Graf
et al.

[
20
]
.
We showed that the Right-Censored Log-Likelihood (RCLL) introduced in Avati
et al.

[
1
]
is not proper under our definition, which avoids conditioning expectations over unobservable quantities, namely the censoring distribution.
We also disproved the common claim that the integrated survival Brier score (ISBS)
[
20
]
is proper, moreover we showed this loss can be easily ‘gamed’.

Despite these analytical proofs, SBS, ISBS, and RCLL behaved as proper scoring rules in empirical simulations: that is, when simulating data generating processes over a large number of samples and a diverse set of distributions, the true distribution consistently achieved the lowest average expected loss.
Small sample experiments revealed that RCLL never violated properness at any sample size, showing exceptional robustness even if theoretically improper.
ISBS exhibited only minor violations at (
n=10
), but none for (
n>50
), indicating strong practical reliability; we recommend evaluating ISBS up to a data-dependent percentile cut-off (such as the 80th percentile) to avoid tail-instability from heavy censoring.
SBS was the least robust on small sample sizes, which is intuitive as it evaluates predictions at a single time-point, making it more sensitive to censoring and sample variability.
Our findings indicate that prior studies based primarily on the ISBS for model evaluation continue to be trustworthy.

We believe these losses exhibit properness in practice, despite being theoretically improper, as exploiting their weaknesses requires non-trivial manipulation.
For the ISBS one is actively constructing a model that is known to make bad predictions, whereas the RCLL requires a complex optimization procedure.
Both cases are cumbersome and would produce models that perform poorly on all other losses.
In general, when these losses are used by well-intentioned practitioners they are valid and useful metrics.

Finally, we briefly explored a new class of losses that utilizes a different variation of IPCW to weight existing losses.
Applying this new weighting scheme to the RCLL, yields a loss (RCLL*) that is both proper according to our theoretical framework and in addition demonstrated strong empirical properness.
Compared to the RCLL and ISBS, the RCLL* appears to more effectively capture deviations from calibration, discrimination, and proportional hazards assumptions.
Future research is required to further investigate this class and to explore the development of novel scoring rule families tailored to the unique challenges of survival data.

A core limitation of this paper comes from assuming
Y
and
C
to be independent.
This is a common restriction in survival analysis evaluation papers given the non-identifiability problem
[
49
]
, wherein accurately estimating true survival times becomes exceedingly challenging, if not impossible, when censoring and survival times are not independent.
Recent work has demonstrate one can employ the copula framework for evaluation, see Czado and Van Keilegom
[
10
]
and Deresa and Van Keilegom
[
14
]
; but research in this area remains nascent.

Despite the widespread assumption that
Y\perp\!\!\!\perp C
in survival analysis, this condition is often implicit and not routinely verified in experimental studies, particularly in healthcare applications where such independence may not hold.
In practice, researchers typically assume conditional independence (
Y\perp\!\!\!\perp C\mid X
), where survival and censoring are assumed independent given covariates.
However, the extent to which this assumption holds in real-world settings remains an open question.
To our knowledge, few studies have explicitly assessed this assumption or its implications for model evaluation.
Moreover, the robustness of scoring rules under various degrees of conditional independence is largely unexplored.
If the independence assumption (marginal or conditional) is violated, scoring rule evaluations may become difficult to interpret, as no loss is proper in this case.

We believe future work should more clearly justify independence assumptions underlying evaluation pipelines and, where this is not possible, turn to alternative frameworks for dependent censoring
[
14
]
or use competing risks
[
32
]
, where appropriate.
We note that in the competing risks setting, the losses discussed in this paper have been used to evaluate cause-specific outcomes
[
34
,
2
]
and thus our findings are relevant to such settings.
Building on these insights, future research should further investigate proper losses tailored for competing risks and multi-state settings, particularly for evaluating all-cause outcomes.
Additionally, the development of proper scoring rules for settings involving left- and interval-censoring represents an important and largely unexplored direction.

Our findings offer reassurance: commonly used metrics such as ISBS and RCLL are empirically reliable, even if theoretically improper, and thus remain appropriate for model validation in future research.
We recommend that studies continue to use these metrics while also reporting concordance indices and calibration measures to ensure robust and well-interpreted results
[
56
]
.

A.1
Proof of
3.2

Let
L_{A}
and
L_{B}
be two loss functions over a target space
\mathcal{Y}
and let
\alpha\in[0,1]
be independent of
L_{A}
,
L_{B}
, the prediction, and the outcome.
Define
L_{\alpha}
as the convex sum of two loss functions:

L_{\alpha}:=\alpha L_{A}+(1-\alpha)L_{B}

Then,

1.

If both
L_{A}
and
L_{B}
are proper,
L_{\alpha}
is
proper
;

2.

If at least one of
L_{A}
and
L_{B}
are strictly proper, and the other is proper, then
L_{\alpha}
is
strictly proper
.

Proof.

If
\alpha
is independent of
L_{A}
and
L_{B}
then

\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim p_{Y}}[L_{\alpha}(p_{Y},Y)]=\alpha\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim p_{Y}}[L_{A}(p,Y)]+(1-\alpha)\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim p_{Y}}[L_{B}(p,Y)]

which follows as expectations distribute over sums and
\alpha
is a constant that can be pulled out of both expectations.

If
L_{A}
and
L_{B}
are both proper then each component is minimized at
p=p_{Y}
.
As each component is minimized by the same value, so too is the weighted sum
L_{\alpha}
, and hence
L_{\alpha}
is proper.

Now say
L_{B}
is proper and
L_{A}
is strictly proper, then take some
p\neq p_{Y}
, therefore by definition

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim p_{Y}}[L_{A}(p,Y)]>\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim p_{Y}}[L_{A}(p_{Y},Y)]\\
\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim p_{Y}}[L_{B}(p,Y)]\geq\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim p_{Y}}[L_{B}(p_{Y},Y)]\end{split}

Combining these inequalities we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim p_{Y}}[L_{\alpha}(p,Y)]

\displaystyle=\alpha\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim p_{Y}}[L_{A}(p,Y)]+(1-\alpha)\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim p_{Y}}[L_{B}(p,Y)]

\displaystyle>\alpha\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim p_{Y}}[L_{A}(p_{Y},Y)]+(1-\alpha)\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim p_{Y}}[L_{B}(p_{Y},Y)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim p_{Y}}[L_{\alpha}(p_{Y},Y)]

Therefore,
L_{\alpha}
is
strictly proper
.
∎

Definitions and Lemmas

Definition A.1
.

Let
X
be an absolutely continuous random variable and let
\Delta
be a discrete random variable. Then,

1.

The
mixed joint density
of
(X,\Delta)
is defined by

f_{X,\Delta}(x,\delta)=f_{X|\Delta}(x|\delta)P(\Delta=\delta)

(24)

where
f_{X|\Delta}(x|\delta)
is the conditional probability density function of
X
given
\Delta=\delta
.

2.

The
mixed joint cumulative distribution function
of
(X,\Delta)
is given by

F_{X,\Delta}(x,\delta)=\sum_{\delta^{\prime}\leq\delta}\int_{-\infty}^{x}f_{X,\Delta}(u,\delta^{\prime})\ du

(25)

Lemma A.2
.

Let
Y,C
be jointly absolutely continuous random variables supported on the Reals with joint density function
f_{Y,C}(y,c)
and let
\Delta=\mathbb{I}(Y\leq C)
, then the mixed joint density of
(Y,\Delta)
is given by

f_{Y,\Delta}(y,\delta)=\begin{cases}\int^{\infty}_{y}\ f_{Y,C}(y,c)\ dc,&\delta=1\\
\int^{y}_{-\infty}f_{Y,C}(y,c)\ dc,&\delta=0\end{cases}\\

(26)

Proof.

Proof follows by transformation of random variables via the joint cdf.

The joint cdf of
(Y,C)
is defined by,

F_{Y,C}(y,c)=\int^{y}_{-\infty}\int^{c}_{-\infty}f_{Y,C}(s,t)\ dt\ ds

(27)

By definition of indicator variables,
\Delta=1
if and only if
C\geq Y
and
0
otherwise and so:

\displaystyle F_{Y,\Delta}(y,\delta)

\displaystyle=P(Y\leq y,\Delta\leq\delta)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}P(Y\leq y),&\delta=1\\
P(Y\leq y,C<Y),&\delta=0\\
\end{cases}

where the first case follows as
\Delta\in\{0,1\}
and hence
P(Y\leq y,\Delta\leq 1)=P(Y\leq y)
as
\Delta
is marginalised out. The second case follows as
\Delta\in\{0,1\}
and so
P(\Delta\leq 0)=P(\Delta=0)
, and by definition of indicator variables
\Delta=0
if and only if
C<Y
. Now focusing on the second case,

\displaystyle P(Y\leq y,C<Y)

\displaystyle=\iint_{\{t\leq y,c<t\}}f_{Y,C}(t,c)\ dt\ dc

\displaystyle=\int^{y}_{-\infty}\int^{t}_{-\infty}f_{Y,C}(t,c)\ dc\ dt

The first line follows by definition of joint probabilities and the second by change of notation. Now,

F_{Y,\Delta}(y,\delta)=\begin{cases}F_{Y}(y),&\delta=1\\
\int^{y}_{-\infty}\int^{t}_{-\infty}f_{Y,C}(t,c)\ dc\ dt,&\delta=0\\
\end{cases}

(28)

The joint density follows by differentiating the CDF:

\displaystyle f_{Y,\Delta}(y,\delta)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{\partial\left(F_{Y,\Delta}(y,1)-F_{Y,\Delta}(y,0)\right)}{\partial y},&\delta=1\\
\frac{\partial F_{Y,\Delta}(y,0)}{\partial y},&\delta=0\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\int^{y}_{-\infty}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}f_{Y,C}(t,c)\ dc\ dt-\int^{y}_{-\infty}\int^{t}_{-\infty}f_{Y,C}(t,c)\ dc\ dt\right),&\delta=1\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\int^{y}_{-\infty}\int^{t}_{-\infty}f_{Y,C}(t,c)\ dc\ dt\right),&\delta=0\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\int^{y}_{-\infty}\left(\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}f_{Y,C}(t,c)\ dc-\int^{t}_{-\infty}f_{Y,C}(t,c)\ dc\right)\ dt\right),&\delta=1\\
\int^{y}_{-\infty}f_{Y,C}(y,c)\ dc,&\delta=0\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\int^{y}_{-\infty}\int^{\infty}_{t}f_{Y,C}(t,c)\ dc\ dt\right),&\delta=1\\
\int^{y}_{-\infty}f_{Y,C}(y,c)\ dc,&\delta=0\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\int^{\infty}_{y}f_{Y,C}(y,c)\ dc,&\delta=1\\
\int^{y}_{-\infty}f_{Y,C}(y,c)\ dc,&\delta=0\end{cases}

where the first equality is the definition of the joint mixed pdf in terms of the cdf, the second equality follows by substituting
Equation

28
, the third by taking the partial derivative of the second case over
y
and by linearity of integration in the first case, the fourth by subtracting the inner integrals, and the fifth by taking the partial derivative of the first case over
y
.
∎

Corollary A.3
.

Let
Y,C
be jointly absolutely continuous random variables supported on the Reals with joint density function
f_{Y,C}(y,c)
and let
\Delta=\mathbb{I}(Y\leq C)
. As a direct corollary to
A.2
, if
Y
and

are independent then the mixed joint density of

is given by

f_{Y,\Delta}(y,\delta)=\begin{cases}f_{Y}(y)S_{C}(y),&\delta=1\\
f_{Y}(y)F_{C}(y),&\delta=0\end{cases}

(29)

Proof.

From
A.2
, for any jointly absolutely continuous random variables,
Y,C
supported on the Reals and

it holds that,

f_{Y,\Delta}(y,\delta)=\begin{cases}\int^{\infty}_{y}\ f_{Y,C}(y,c)\ dc,&\delta=1\\

If

are independent then

by definition of independence. Substituting this result into the above equation,

\displaystyle f_{Y,\Delta}(y,\delta)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\int^{\infty}_{y}\ f_{Y}(y)f_{C}(c)\ dc,&\delta=1\\
\int^{y}_{-\infty}f_{Y}(y)f_{C}(c)\ dc,&\delta=0\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}f_{Y}(y)\int^{\infty}_{y}\ f_{C}(c)\ dc,&\delta=1\\
f_{Y}(y)\int^{y}_{-\infty}f_{C}(c)\ dc,&\delta=0\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}f_{Y}(y)S_{C}(y),&\delta=1\\
f_{Y}(y)F_{C}(y),&\delta=0\end{cases}

where the first equality holds as
Y,C
independent, the second by properties of integration, and the third by definition of the cumulative distribution and survival functions.
∎

B.1
L_{RCLL}
is not proper

Proof.

We prove that
L_{RCLL}
is not proper by showing that the optimal prediction,
\hat{f}=f_{Y}
, does not minimize the loss and
L_{RCLL}
is therefore not proper by definition.

First, substituting the definition of the RCLL (
4
) into
3.4
:

\mathbb{E}[L_{RCLL}(\hat{S},t,\delta)]=-\int^{\infty}_{0}f_{Y}(t)S_{C}(t)\log(\hat{f}(t))+f_{C}(t)S_{Y}(t)\log(\hat{S}(t))\ dt

We define

I_{u}:=\int f_{Y}(t)S_{C}(t)\ dt,\quad I_{c}:=\int f_{C}(t)S_{Y}(t)\ dt

And multiply and divide these within each half of the integral

\mathbb{E}[L_{RCLL}(\hat{S},t,\delta)]=-\int^{\infty}_{0}\frac{I_{u}f_{Y}(t)S_{C}(t)}{I_{u}}\log(\hat{f}(t))\ dt-\int^{\infty}_{0}\frac{I_{c}f_{C}(t)S_{Y}(t)}{I_{c}}\log(\hat{S}(t))\ dt

As
I_{c}
and
I_{u}
are constants we can pull them out of the integral resulting in

\mathcal{S}:=\mathbb{E}[L_{RCLL}(\hat{S},t,\delta)]=-I_{u}\int^{\infty}_{0}p_{u}\log(\hat{f}(t))\ dt-I_{c}\int^{\infty}_{0}p_{c}\log(\hat{S}(t))\ dt

(34)

where

p_{u}=\frac{f_{Y}(t)S_{C}(t)}{I_{u}},\quad p_{c}=\frac{f_{C}(t)S_{Y}(t)}{I_{c}}

one can think of
p_{u}
as the conditional probability of the event occurring at
t
, given censoring has not occurred by that time, and similarly
p_{c}
as the conditional probability of censoring at
t
, given the event has not occurred by that time.

Starting with the first term in
Equation

34
, by Gibbs’ inequality,

-\int p_{u}(t)\log p_{u}(t)\ dt\leq-\int p_{u}(t)\log\hat{f}(t)\ dt

with equality, and thus uniquely minimized, only when
\hat{f}=p_{u}
.

For the second term in
Equation

34
we observe that
p_{c}
is strictly positive,
-\log(x)
is strictly convex for
x>0
, and note integration preserves convexity.
Hence,
\hat{S}\mapsto-\int p_{c}(t)\log\hat{S}(t)\ dt
is strictly convex over the set of strictly positive survival functions.
We note this slight restriction in the proof but this is a reasonable in practice, especially as administrative censoring (which always occurs in practice) guarantees non-zero survival probabilities at the final observed time-point.
By convexity, the second term has a unique minimum that must depend on
p_{c}
; we do not require a closed-form expression for this minimizer to complete this proof.

Hence, the total expected loss
\mathcal{S}
in
Equation

34
is the sum of two strictly convex functions and strictly convex overall.
It is uniquely minimized when both components are minimized individually, i.e.,
\hat{f}=p_{u}
and
\hat{S}
is the unique minimizer of the second term which depends on
p_{c}
.
Since
p_{u}\neq f_{Y}
and the minimizer of the second term depends on
p_{c}
and therefore does not equal
S_{Y}
in general, it follows that the loss is not minimized by the true distributions.
Therefore,
L_{RCLL}
is not strictly proper by definition.

To extend the argument to full improperness, we show there exists some coherent pair (
\hat{f},\hat{S})
such that the expected RCLL is lower than for the true model.
Define the feasible set of coherent models:

\mathcal{F}:=\left\{(\hat{f},\hat{S})\Big|\hat{S}(t)=\int^{\infty}_{t}\hat{f}(u)\ du,\ \hat{f}\geq 0,\ \int\hat{f}=1\right\}

The expected loss
\mathcal{S}
defined in
Equation

34
is strictly convex with unique minimizer

(f^{*},S^{*})=\underset{(\hat{f},\hat{S})\in\mathcal{F}}{\arg\min}\ \mathcal{S}(\hat{f},\hat{S})

The global minimum of
\mathcal{S}
occurs at the pair
p_{u}
and the global minimizer of the second term in
Equation

34
, which depends on
p_{c}
, however this pair would not be coherent and does not belong to
\mathcal{F}
.
The true model
(f_{Y},S_{Y})
is feasible, but in general does not minimize
\mathcal{S}
over
\mathcal{F}
, since
(p_{u},p_{c})
depends on both the event and censoring distributions, while
(f_{Y},S_{Y})
does not.
Thus, there exists a feasible model,
(f^{*},S^{*})
, such that
\mathbb{E}[L_{RCLL}(S^{*},t,\delta)]<\mathbb{E}[L_{RCLL}(S_{Y},t,\delta)]
and the RCLL is not proper by definition.
∎

B.2
L_{SCRPS}
is not proper

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and let
\mathcal{P}
be a family of absolutely continuous distributions over
\mathcal{T}
containing at least two elements. Let
\upsilon,\xi
be some distributions in
\mathcal{P}
. Let
Y\sim\xi
and let
C\perp\!\!\!\perp Y
be a random variable taking values in
\mathcal{T}
. Let
T:=\min\{Y,C\}
and
\Delta:=\mathbb{I}(T=Y)
. Finally let
\hat{Y}\sim\upsilon
be a random variable representing a prediction.

A loss
L
is proper if
\forall C,\upsilon,\xi:L(\upsilon,\xi)\geq L(\xi,\xi)
(
3.3
).
Hence, we will use proof by counter-example to demonstrate
L
is improper by showing
\exists C,\upsilon,\xi:L(\upsilon,\xi)<L(\xi,\xi)
.
For this counter-example we take:
C\sim\xi,\xi=\operatorname{Exp}(1),\upsilon=\operatorname{Exp}(\mu)
.
First calculating the expectation of
L_{SCRPS}(\xi,\upsilon)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{L_{SCRPS}}](\xi,\upsilon)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\int^{T}_{0}F^{2}_{Y}(\tau)\ d\tau+\Delta\int^{\infty}_{T}S^{2}_{Y}(\tau)\ d\tau\right]

\displaystyle=\int^{\infty}_{0}f_{Y}(y)S_{C}(y)\left(\int^{y}_{0}F^{2}_{Y}(\tau)\ d\tau+\int^{\infty}_{y}S^{2}_{Y}(\tau)\ d\tau\right)\ dy+

\displaystyle\int^{\infty}_{0}f_{C}(c)S_{Y}(c)\left(\int^{c}_{0}F^{2}_{Y}(\tau)\ d\tau\right)dc

\displaystyle\text{\lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{prop:exp} as }Y\perp\!\!\!\perp C

\displaystyle=\int^{\infty}_{0}f_{Y}(y)S_{Y}(y)\left(2\int^{y}_{0}F^{2}_{Y}(\tau)\ d\tau+\int^{\infty}_{y}S^{2}_{Y}(\tau)\ d\tau\right)\ dy

\displaystyle\text{let }C\sim\xi

\displaystyle=\int^{\infty}_{0}e^{-2y}\left(2\int^{y}_{0}1-2e^{-\mu\tau}+e^{-2\mu\tau}\ d\tau+\int^{\infty}_{y}e^{-2\mu\tau}\ d\tau\right)\ dy

\displaystyle\text{let }\xi=\operatorname{Exp}(1),\upsilon=\operatorname{Exp}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\int^{\infty}_{0}e^{-2y}\left(\frac{-6-e^{-2y\mu}+8e^{-y\mu}+4y\mu}{2\mu}\right)\ dy

\displaystyle\text{integration}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\mu^{3}+\mu+2}{4(\mu+2)\mu(\mu+1)}

\displaystyle\text{integration}

To finish the counter-example we let
\mu=1.5
so
\upsilon=\operatorname{Exp}(1.5)
and observe:

\mathbb{E}[{L_{SCRPS}}(\xi,\upsilon)]=0.1952<\mathbb{E}[S_{L_{SCRPS}}(\xi,\xi)]=0.208333

(35)

Hence we have found a counter-example where some

has a lower SCRPS in expectation and hence

is not proper by definition.
∎

B.3
L_{SBS}
is proper

Proof.

Taking expectations of
L_{SBS}
we have:

\displaystyle\underset{(T,\Delta)}{\mathbb{E}}[L_{SBS}(\hat{S},T,\Delta)]

\displaystyle=\int^{\infty}_{0}f_{Y}(t)S_{C}(t)L_{SBS}(\hat{S},t,\delta=1)+f_{C}(t)S_{Y}(t)L_{SBS}(\hat{S},t,\delta=0)\ dt

\displaystyle\lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{prop:exp}

\displaystyle=\int^{\infty}_{0}f_{Y}(t)S_{C}(t)\left(\frac{\hat{S}^{2}(\tau^{*})\mathbb{I}(t\leq\tau^{*})}{S_{C}(t)}+\frac{\hat{F}^{2}(\tau^{*})\mathbb{I}(t>\tau^{*})}{S_{C}(\tau^{*})}\right)+f_{C}(t)S_{Y}(t)\left(\frac{\hat{F}^{2}(\tau^{*})\mathbb{I}(t>\tau^{*})}{S_{C}(\tau^{*})}\right)\ dt

\displaystyle\text{def. SBS}

\displaystyle=\hat{S}^{2}(\tau^{*})\int^{\tau^{*}}_{0}f_{Y}(t)\ dt+\frac{\hat{F}^{2}(\tau^{*})}{S_{C}(\tau^{*})}\int^{\infty}_{\tau^{*}}f_{Y}(t)S_{C}(t)\ dt+\frac{\hat{F}^{2}(\tau^{*})}{S_{C}(\tau^{*})}\int^{\infty}_{\tau^{*}}f_{C}(t)S_{Y}(t)\ dt

\displaystyle=\hat{S}^{2}(\tau^{*})F_{Y}(\tau^{*})+\frac{\hat{F}^{2}(\tau^{*})}{S_{C}(\tau^{*})}\int^{\infty}_{\tau^{*}}f_{Y}(t)S_{C}(t)+f_{C}(t)S_{Y}(t)\ dt

Note the following relationship:

\frac{d}{dt}S_{Y}(t)S_{C}(t)=S_{Y}^{\prime}(t)S_{C}(t)+S_{Y}(t)S_{C}^{\prime}(t)=-\left(f_{Y}(t)S_{C}(t)+S_{Y}(t)f_{C}(t)\right)

Substituting this expression into the above yields

\displaystyle\underset{(T,\Delta)}{\mathbb{E}}[L_{SBS}(\hat{S},T,\Delta)]

\displaystyle=\hat{S}^{2}(\tau^{*})F_{Y}(\tau^{*})-\frac{\hat{F}^{2}(\tau^{*})}{S_{C}(\tau^{*})}\int^{\infty}_{\tau^{*}}\frac{d}{dt}S_{Y}(t)S_{C}(t)\ dt

\displaystyle=\hat{S}^{2}(\tau^{*})F_{Y}(\tau^{*})-\frac{\hat{F}^{2}(\tau^{*})}{S_{C}(\tau^{*})}\left[S_{Y}(t)S_{C}(t)\right]^{\infty}_{t=\tau^{*}}

\displaystyle=\hat{S}^{2}(\tau^{*})F_{Y}(\tau^{*})-\frac{\hat{F}^{2}(\tau^{*})}{S_{C}(\tau^{*})}[0-S_{Y}(\tau^{*})S_{C}(\tau^{*})]

\displaystyle=\hat{S}^{2}(\tau^{*})F_{Y}(\tau^{*})+\hat{F}^{2}(\tau^{*})S_{Y}(\tau^{*})

Taking the partial derivative with respect to
\hat{S}(\tau^{*})
:

\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{S}(\tau^{*})}=2\hat{S}(\tau^{*})F_{Y}(\tau^{*})-2\left(1-\hat{S}(\tau^{*})\right)S_{Y}(\tau^{*})

and setting to zero we find the minimum at:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=2\hat{S}(\tau^{*})(1-S_{Y}(\tau^{*}))-2\left(1-\hat{S}(\tau^{*})\right)S_{Y}(\tau^{*})

\displaystyle\hat{S}(\tau^{*})(1-S_{Y}(\tau^{*}))

\displaystyle=\left(1-\hat{S}(\tau^{*})\right)S_{Y}(\tau^{*})

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{S}(\tau^{*})}{1-\hat{S}(\tau^{*})}

\displaystyle=\frac{S_{Y}(\tau^{*})}{1-S_{Y}(\tau^{*})}

\displaystyle\hat{S}(\tau^{*})

\displaystyle=S_{Y}(\tau^{*})

Taking the second derivative:

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\hat{S}(\tau^{*})^{2}}=2F_{Y}(\tau^{*})+2S_{Y}(\tau^{*})=2

Which is positive and constant, hence
L_{SBS}
is strictly convex with global (unique) minimum at
\hat{S}(\tau^{*})=S_{Y}(\tau^{*})
and is therefore strictly proper by definition.

∎

also depends on the variable
\tau^{*}
and therefore the inequality must also hold for any potential value of
\tau^{*}
– otherwise the loss could be proper for some values of
\tau^{*}
but not all, which is not useful as
\tau^{*}
is often determined dynamically.
We will use proof by counter-example to demonstrate
L
is improper by showing
\exists C,\chi,\omega,\tau^{*}:L(\chi,\omega,\tau^{*})<L(\omega,\omega,\tau^{*})
.
For this counter-example we take:
C\sim\omega,\omega=\operatorname{Exp}(2),\chi=\operatorname{Exp}(\mu),\tau^{*}=1
.

First calculating the expectation of
L_{ISBS}(\omega,\chi)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[L_{ISBS}](\omega,\chi)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\int^{\tau^{*}}_{0}\frac{S_{\hat{Y}}^{2}(\tau)\mathbb{I}(T\leq\tau,\Delta=1)}{S_{C}(T)}+\frac{F_{\hat{Y}}^{2}(\tau)\mathbb{I}(T>\tau)}{S_{C}(\tau)}\ \ d\tau\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\int^{\tau^{*}}_{T}\frac{S_{\hat{Y}}^{2}(\tau)\Delta}{S_{C}(T)}\ d\tau+\int^{T}_{0}\frac{F_{\hat{Y}}^{2}(\tau)}{S_{C}(\tau)}\ d\tau\right]

\displaystyle\text{change limits}

\displaystyle=\begin{split}&\int f_{Y}(y)S_{C}(y)\int^{\tau^{*}}_{y}\frac{S_{\hat{Y}}^{2}(\tau)}{S_{C}(y)}\ d\tau dy+\int f_{Y}(y)S_{C}(y)\int^{y}_{0}\frac{F_{\hat{Y}}^{2}(\tau)}{S_{C}(\tau)}\ d\tau dy\ +\\
&\quad\quad\quad\int f_{C}(c)S_{Y}(c)\int^{c}_{0}\frac{F_{\hat{Y}}^{2}(\tau)}{S_{C}(\tau)}\ d\tau dc\end{split}

\displaystyle\text{\lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{prop:exp} as }Y\perp\!\!\!\perp C

\displaystyle=\begin{split}&\int f_{Y}(y)\int^{\tau^{*}}_{y}S_{\hat{Y}}^{2}(\tau)\ d\tau dy+\int f_{Y}(y)S_{C}(y)\int^{y}_{0}\frac{F_{\hat{Y}}^{2}(\tau)}{S_{C}(\tau)}\ d\tau dy\ +\\
&\quad\quad\quad\int f_{C}(c)S_{Y}(c)\int^{c}_{0}\frac{F_{\hat{Y}}^{2}(\tau)}{S_{C}(\tau)}\ d\tau dc\\
\end{split}

\displaystyle\text{canceling }S_{C}

\displaystyle=\int f_{Y}(y)\int^{1}_{y}S_{\hat{Y}}^{2}(\tau)\ d\tau dy+2\int f_{Y}(y)S_{Y}(y)\int^{y}_{0}\frac{F_{\hat{Y}}^{2}(\tau)}{S_{Y}(\tau)}\ d\tau dy

\displaystyle\text{let }C\sim\omega,\tau^{*}=1

\displaystyle=\int 2e^{-2y}\int^{1}_{y}e^{-2\mu\tau}\ d\tau dy+4\int e^{-4y}\int^{y}_{0}\frac{1-2e^{-\mu\tau}+e^{-2\mu\tau}}{e^{-2\tau}}\ d\tau dy

\displaystyle\text{let }\omega=\operatorname{Exp}(2),\chi=\operatorname{Exp}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\begin{split}&\int e^{-2y}\frac{e^{-2y\mu}-e^{-2\mu}}{2\mu}\ dy\ +\\
&\quad\quad\quad 4\int e^{-4y}\left(\frac{e^{y(2-2\mu)}}{2-2\mu}-\frac{2e^{y(2-\mu)}}{2-\mu}+\frac{2}{2-\mu}+\frac{1}{2\mu-2}+\frac{e^{2y}-1}{2}\right)\ dy\end{split}

\displaystyle\text{integration}

\displaystyle=-\frac{e^{-2\mu}(\mu-e^{2\mu}+1)}{2\mu(2+2\mu)}-4\left(\frac{(\frac{\mu^{2}}{4-6\mu+2\mu^{2}}-1)}{4}+\frac{2}{4-\mu^{2}}-\frac{1}{4-4\mu^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\text{integration}

\displaystyle=-\frac{e^{-2\mu}(\mu-e^{2\mu}+1)}{2\mu(2+2\mu)}-\frac{\mu^{2}}{(2+\mu)(2+2\mu)}

To finish the counter-example we let
\mu=3
so
\chi=\operatorname{Exp}(3)
and observe:

\mathbb{E}[{L_{ISBS}}(\omega,\chi)]=-0.2044<\mathbb{E}[S_{L_{ISBS}}(\omega,\omega)]=-0.1273

(36)

Hence we have found a counter-example where some
\upsilon\neq\xi
has a lower ISBS in expectation and hence
L_{ISBS}
is not proper by definition.

∎
