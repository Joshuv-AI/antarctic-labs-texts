Using Random Walks to Establish Wavelike Behavior in an FPUT System with Random Coefficients
Joshua A. McGinnis, J. Douglas Wright

Abstract
We consider a linear Fermi-Pasta-Ulam-Tsingou lattice with random spatially varying material coefficients. Using the methods of stochastic homogenization we show that solutions with long wave initial data converge in an appropriate sense to solutions of a wave equation. The convergence is strong and both almost sure and in expectation, but the rate is quite slow. The technique combines energy estimates with powerful classical results about random walks, specifically the law of the iterated logarithm.

Introduction

We prove an almost sure convergence result for solutions of the following one-dimensional random polymer linear Fermi-Pasta-Ulam-Tsingou (FPUT) lattice in the long wave limit:

\displaystyle m(j)\ddot{u}(j)=k(j)\left[u(j+1)-u(j)\right]-k(j-1)\left[u(j)-u(j-1)\right].

(1.1)

Here

,

and

.
We choose the coefficients

with standard deviation

. We similarly take the coefficients
1/k(j)
(“the springs”)
to be i.i.d. with support in
[a_{k},b_{k}]\subset{\bf{R}}^{+}
and deviation
\sigma_{k}
.
This system is well-understood when these coefficients are either constant or periodic with respect to

[
8
]
, but for the random problem most of what is known is formal or numerical
[
6
,
9
]
.

For initial conditions whose wavelength is

\epsilon\in(0,1)
, we prove that the
\ell^{2}
norm of the difference between true solutions and appropriately scaled solutions to the wave equation is at most
O\left(\sqrt{\log\log(1/\epsilon)}\right)

for times of
O(1/\epsilon)
for almost every realization. While such an absolute error diverges
as
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
, it happens that this is
enough to establish an almost sure convergence result within the “coarse-graining” setting
used in
[
8
]
to study the (multi-dimensional) periodic problem.
In addition to the almost sure convergence, we are able to provide estimates on the mean of the error in terms of
\sigma_{m}
and
\sigma_{k}
and prove convergence in mean.

The articles
[
2
,
5
]
study the nonlinear FPUT lattice with periodic coefficients.
These show that soliton-like solutions exist for very large time scales using Korteweg-de Vries (KdV) approximations.
The authors of
[
5
]
used the so called multiscale method of homogenization, a
by-now classical tool with a long history in PDE for deriving effective equations, see
[
1
]
. In this paper, we carry out a very similar approach in deriving and proving the results; however, our expansions only result in an effective wave equation, not the KdV equation. In our setting, since the coefficients are random, it is necessary to average over the entire lattice. The law of large numbers implies this average is equal to the expectation, so the speed of the approximate solution depends on the expectation of the random variables. The probability theory hinges upon classical but extremely powerful asymptotic analysis of random walks, namely the law of iterated logarithms, as well as basic martingale theory.

3
Probabilistic estimates

In this section we provide tools which will allow us to compute the

norms of the residuals for all
|t|\leq T_{0}/\epsilon

3.1
Almost Sure Estimates

One can find the statement of the LIL in
[
3
]
and more details can be found in
[
4
]
. Here we present the theorem in a form convenient to us.

Theorem 3.1
.

(The Law of Iterated Logarithms)

Suppose
y(j)
(
j\in{\bf{Z}}
) are i.i.d random variables with mean zero and
{\mathbb{E}}[y^{2}]=\sigma^{2}
. Define the (two-sided) random walk
\chi

via

\chi(j)\coloneqq\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}y(i)\quad\text{and}\quad\chi(-j)\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{j}y(-i)

(3.1)

for
j>0
and
\chi(0)=0
.

Then

\limsup_{|j|\to\pm\infty}\frac{\pm\chi(j)}{\sqrt{2|j|\log\log(|j|)}}\overset{a.s.}{=}\sigma.

The LIL is an extremely sharp
description of a random walk. It says that, with a probability of one, the magnitude of

\chi(j)
exceeds the curve
\sigma\sqrt{2j\log\log(j)}
(by any fixed amount) only a finite number of times
but comes arbitrarily near it an infinite number of times. Here is how we use the LIL:

Corollary 3.2
.

For almost every realization of
\{k(j)\}
and
\{m(j)\}
there is a finite positive constant

C_{\omega}=C_{\omega}(k,m)

for which

|\chi_{k}(j)|+|\chi_{m}(j)|\leq C_{\omega}\sqrt{|j|\log\log(|j|+e)}

for all
j\in{\bf{Z}}
.

Remark 1
.

The constant
C_{\omega}
is almost surely finite by the LIL, but it may be extremely large.
There is no way to determine its magnitude except in very special circumstances.
Note, however, it does not depend on
\epsilon
.

Remark 2
.

In this paper, we use a small modification of the usual “big
C
” notation.
If a constant in an estimate depends on the particular realization of the coefficients
we mark it as “
C_{\omega}
.”
If it does not, we omit the subscript
\omega
.
All such constants
C_{\omega}

are always almost surely finite.
No such constants will ever depend on
\epsilon
.

Proof.

The LIL implies that for almost every realization of
\{k(j)\}
there is a natural number
N_{k}
such that

|\chi_{k}(j)|\leq 2\widetilde{k}\sigma_{k}\sqrt{2|j|\log\log(|j|)}

when
|j|\geq N_{k}
. Then put

C_{k}:=\max\left\{{2\widetilde{k}\sigma_{k}\sqrt{2}},\max_{0<|j|\leq N_{k}}{\chi_{k}(j)\over\sqrt{|j|\log\log(|j|)}}\right\}.

It follows that
|\chi_{k}(j)|\leq C_{k}\sqrt{|j|\log\log(|j|)}\leq C_{k}\sqrt{|j|\log\log(|j|+e)}

for all
j
. The same argument can be used to estimate
\chi_{m}
.
∎

Given the growth rate in the LIL, we introduce a new norm fashioned to absorb it:

\|F\|_{H^{s}_{LIL}}:=\sum_{i=0}^{s}\|(1+|\cdot|\log\log(|\cdot|+e))^{1/2}F^{(i)}\|_{L^{2}}.

The space
H^{s}_{LIL}
will be the completion of
L^{2}
with respect to this norm.
Similarly, we also introduce

\|F\|_{H^{s}_{sr}}:=\sum_{i=0}^{s}\|(1+|\cdot|)^{1/2}F^{(i)}\|_{L^{2}}

and the space
H^{s}_{sr}
. Note that
\|F\|_{H^{s}}\leq\|F\|_{H^{s}_{sr}}\leq\|F\|_{H^{s}_{LIL}}

where
H^{s}
is the usual
L^{2}
-based Sobolev space of functions
{\bf{R}}\to{\bf{R}}
which are weakly
s
-times
differentiable.

Now we unveil the two main estimates we need to provide almost sure control of the residuals.

and

In the above
\chi

\chi_{k}
or
\chi_{m}

To prove these we need some calculus estimates.

Lemma 3.4
.

For all
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, and
a,b\in{\bf{R}}

|a+b|\log\log(|a+b|+e)\leq|a|\log\log(2|a|+e)+|b|\log\log(2|b|+e)

and

\log\log(|x|+e)\leq\log(2\log(\epsilon|x|+e))+\log\log(\epsilon^{-1}+e).

Proof.

The first inequality follows from the fact that
|x|\log\log(|x|+e)
is a convex function.

We will show the second inequality in two steps. First we show that

\log\log(|x|+e)\leq\log(2\log(\epsilon|x|+\epsilon^{-1}+e)).

Since
\log
is monotonic, this inequality follows from

|x|+e\leq(\epsilon|x|+\epsilon^{-1}+e)^{2},

which is trivial.

Now we show that

\log(2\log(\epsilon|x|+\epsilon^{-1}+e))\leq\log(2\log(\epsilon|x|+e))+\log\log(\epsilon^{-1}+e).

Note that at
x=0
, equality holds. For
x\geq 0
we have that

\frac{d}{dx}\log(\epsilon x+\epsilon^{-1}+e)=\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon x+\epsilon^{-1}+e}

and

\frac{d}{dx}\log(\epsilon|x|+e)\log(\epsilon^{-1}+e)=\frac{\epsilon\log(\epsilon^{-1}+e)}{\epsilon x+e}.

Since

\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon x+\epsilon^{-1}+e}\leq{\epsilon\over\epsilon x+e}\leq\frac{\epsilon\log(\epsilon^{-1}+e)}{\epsilon x+e},

we see that
\log(\epsilon x+\epsilon^{-1}+e)
grows more slowly than
\log(\epsilon x+e)\log(\epsilon^{-1}+e)
. Since both functions are even, we get by symmetry that

2\log(\epsilon|x|+\epsilon^{-1}+e)\leq 2\log(\epsilon|x|+e)\log(\epsilon^{-1}+e).

Taking
\log
of both sides, we get the desired result.
∎

\sup_{|t|\leq T_{0}/\epsilon}\left(\|\Res_{1}(\widetilde{r}_{\epsilon},\widetilde{p}_{\epsilon})\|_{\ell^{2}}+\|\Res_{1}(\widetilde{r}_{\epsilon},\widetilde{p}_{\epsilon})\|_{\ell^{2}}\right)\leq C_{\omega}\epsilon\sqrt{\log\log(1/\epsilon)}.

(3.5)

Proof.

We prove the estimate for the piece involving
\Res_{1}
as
the other part is all but identical. A tedious calculation shows that

\displaystyle\text{Res}_{1}(\widetilde{r}_{\epsilon},\widetilde{p}_{\epsilon})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\widetilde{k}\bar{m}}}\left(-\delta^{+}[A(\epsilon(j-ct))]+\epsilon A^{\prime}(\epsilon(j-ct))\right)

(3.6)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\widetilde{k}\bar{m}}}\left(-\delta^{+}[B(\epsilon(j+ct))]+\epsilon B^{\prime}(\epsilon(j+ct))\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{c\epsilon^{2}\chi_{m}(j)}{k(j)}A^{\prime}(\epsilon(j-ct))+\frac{c\epsilon^{2}\chi_{m}(j)}{k(j)}B^{\prime}(\epsilon(j+ct))

\displaystyle-\frac{\epsilon\chi_{k}(j+1)}{\sqrt{\widetilde{k}\bar{m}}}\delta^{+}[A^{\prime}(\epsilon(j-ct))]-\frac{\epsilon\chi_{k}(j+1)}{\sqrt{\widetilde{k}\bar{m}}}\delta^{+}[B^{\prime}(\epsilon(j+ct))].

The terms in the first two lines are fully deterministic and estimable using Lemma 4.3 of
[
5
]
.
Specifically the
\ell^{2}
norm of
each is controlled by

C\epsilon^{3/2}\left(\|A\|_{H^{2}}+\|B\|_{H^{2}}\right)

for
|t|\leq T_{0}/\epsilon
.
This is dominated by the right hand side of (
3.5
).
Using (
3.2
) we see that the
\ell^{2}
norm in the third line is controlled by

\epsilon^{2}\left(C_{\omega}\epsilon^{-1}\sqrt{\log\log(1/\epsilon)}\left(\|A^{\prime}\|_{H^{1}_{LIL}}+\|B^{\prime}\|_{H^{1}_{LIL}}\right)\right)

for
|t|\leq T_{0}/\epsilon
.
Again this is dominated by the right hand side of (
3.5
).
Similarly we use (
3.3
) to handle the terms in the last line, which are controlled by

\epsilon\left(C_{\omega}\sqrt{\log\log(1/\epsilon)}\left(\|A^{\prime}\|_{H^{2}_{LIL}}+\|B^{\prime}\|_{H^{2}_{LIL}}\right)\right).

It is here we see why
H^{3}_{LIL}
is needed in (
3.5
).
∎

Remark 3
.

We quickly note that if the
k(j)
and
m(j)
are constant with respect to
j
, one can easily
chase through this proof and see that the estimate size of the residuals decreases to
C\epsilon^{3/2}
. Likewise
if the springs and masses vary periodically, one finds that
\chi_{m}(j)
and
\chi_{k}(j)
are in
\ell^{\infty}

and then this proof would demonstrate the size of the residuals is bounded by
C\epsilon^{1/2}
.

3.2
Boundedness in Mean

The almost sure boundedness does not provide us with any kind of description for the
\omega
dependent constant
C_{\omega}
. In this section we estimate the error in mean, finding estimates in terms of
\sigma_{m}
and
\sigma_{k}
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
y(j)
and
\chi(j)
be as in Theorem
3.1
and
n\in{\bf{Z}}^{+}\cup\{0\}
. Consider the process

W_{j}(n):=\chi(j+n)-\chi(j).

Then, for every
j
,
W_{j}(n)
is a martingale
in the variable
n
and, for any
N>0
,

{\mathbb{E}}[\max_{0\leq n\leq N}(W_{j}(n))^{2}]\leq 4N\sigma^{2}.

(3.7)

Proof.

From the definition of
\chi
, we have

W_{j}(n)=y(j)+\cdots+y(j+n-1).

(3.8)

Then

{\mathbb{E}}[|W_{j}(n)|]\leq C|n|.

Conditioning upon
W_{j}(n)
gives

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[W_{j}(n+1)|W_{j}(n)]={\mathbb{E}}[y(j+n)+W_{j}(n)|W_{j}(n)]=W_{j}(n).

This proves that
W_{j}
is a martingale. From the basic theory of martingales this tells us
|W_{j}|^{2}
is a submartingale. It follows from the
L^{p}
maximum inequality, see
[
3
]
, and a direct computation using (
3.8
)

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\max_{0\leq n\leq N}(W_{j}(n))^{2}\right]\leq 4{\mathbb{E}}[|W_{j}(N)|^{2}]=4N\sigma^{2}.

∎

Remark 4
.

We can define a similar process
W_{j}(n):=\chi(j-n)-\chi(j)
which would have exactly the same properties but with a different version of (
3.8
) i.e.

W_{j}(n)=y(j-n)+\cdots+y(j-1).

This symmetry allows us to handle positive and negative times with the same argument.

We use the following corollary in the results that follow.

Corollary 3.7
.

\chi_{k}(j+n)-\chi_{k}(j)\quad\text{and}\quad\chi_{m}(j+n)-\chi_{m}(j)

are martingales in
n
with

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\max_{0\leq n\leq N}\left(\chi_{k}(j+n)-\chi_{k}(j)\right)^{2}\right]\leq 4N\widetilde{k}\sigma_{k}^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\ {\mathbb{E}}\left[\max_{0\leq n\leq N}\left(\chi_{k}(j+n)-\chi_{k}(j)\right)^{2}\right]\leq 4N\dfrac{\sigma^{2}_{m}}{\bar{m}}.

We have now gotten the necessary probability out of the way to prove the following lemma, analogous to Lemma
3.3
, but in expectation.

Lemma 3.8
.

For any
T_{0}>0
and
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
the following inequalities hold

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{|t|\leq T_{0}/\epsilon}\|\chi(\cdot)F(\epsilon(\cdot-ct))\|_{\ell^{2}}\right]\leq 2\sqrt{2}\epsilon^{-1}\sigma\max\{2\sqrt{|c|T_{0}},1\}\|F\|_{H^{2}_{sr}}

(3.9)

and

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{|t|\leq T_{0}/\epsilon}\|\chi(\cdot)\delta^{\pm}F(\epsilon(\cdot-ct))\|_{\ell^{2}}\right]\leq 3\sqrt{2}\sigma\max\{2\sqrt{|c|T_{0}},1\}\|F\|_{H^{3}_{sr}}.

(3.10)

In the above
\chi
is either
\chi_{k}
or
\chi_{m}
with
\sigma
either
\sigma_{k}\sqrt{\widetilde{k}}
or
\dfrac{\sigma_{m}}{\sqrt{\bar{m}}}
respectively.

Proof.

Without loss of generality (see Remark
4
) let
t\in{\bf{R}}^{+}\cup\{0\}
. Write
ct=\lfloor ct\rfloor+\alpha
where
\alpha\in[0,1).
Let
n\in{\bf{Z}}
in the following. We start with the inequality

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T_{0}/\epsilon}\|\chi(\cdot)F(\epsilon\cdot-\epsilon ct)\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}}\leq\sup_{0\leq n\leq cT_{0}/\epsilon,\alpha\in[0,1)}\sum_{j\in{\bf{Z}}}\chi(j)^{2}F(\epsilon j-\epsilon n-\epsilon\alpha)^{2}.

The inequality is due to the fact that for any
t\in[0,\lfloor T_{0}/\epsilon\rfloor+1)
there exists an
n\in[0,cT_{0}/\epsilon]
and
\alpha\in[0,1)
s.t.
n+\alpha=ct
, which is a slightly greater range for
t
than we initially cared about. Using the Mean Value Theorem, we have that

F(\epsilon j-\epsilon n-\epsilon\alpha)=F(\epsilon j-\epsilon n)-\epsilon\alpha F^{\prime}(x_{j})

where
x_{j}\in(\epsilon j-\epsilon n-\epsilon\alpha,\epsilon j-\epsilon n).
Substituting this in and using the basic inequality
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2(a^{2}+b^{2})
, we get

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq t\leq T_{0}/\epsilon}\|\chi(\cdot)F(\epsilon\cdot-\epsilon ct)\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{0\leq n\leq cT_{0}/\epsilon,\alpha\in[0,1)}\sum_{j\in{\bf{Z}}}\chi(j)^{2}(F(\epsilon j-\epsilon n)-\epsilon\alpha F^{\prime}(x_{j}))^{2}.

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{0\leq n\leq cT_{0}/\epsilon,\alpha\in[0,1)}2\sum_{j\in{\bf{Z}}}\chi(j)^{2}F(\epsilon j-\epsilon n)^{2}+\chi(j)^{2}(\epsilon\alpha F^{\prime}(x_{j}))^{2}.

Call the expectation of the first term
I
and the second’s expectation
II
. Note that
I
does not depend upon
\alpha
. We find from a change of indices, that

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{0\leq n\leq cT_{0}/\epsilon}2\sum_{j\in{\bf{Z}}}\chi(j+n)^{2}F(\epsilon j)^{2}\right]

(3.11)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{0\leq n\leq cT_{0}/\epsilon}2\sum_{j\in{\bf{Z}}}(\chi(j+n)-\chi(j)+\chi(j))^{2}F(\epsilon j)^{2}\right]

Using the same basic inequality as above we get

I\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{0\leq n\leq cT_{0}/\epsilon}4\sum_{j\in{\bf{Z}}}\left(\chi(j)^{2}+(\chi(j+n)-\chi(j))^{2}\right)F(\epsilon j)^{2}\right].

(3.12)

The supremum sees only the term with
n
, and Fubini’s theorem allows the expected value to pass through the sum. And so

I\leq 4\sum_{j\in{\bf{Z}}}\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[\chi(j)^{2}\right]+{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{0\leq n\leq cT_{0}/\epsilon}(\chi(j+n)-\chi(j))^{2}\right]\right)F(\epsilon j)^{2}.

A direct computation on the first term using the definition of
\chi
and using Corollary
3.7
on the second term we find

I\leq 4\sum_{j\in{\bf{Z}}}\left(\sigma^{2}|j|+4\sigma^{2}cT_{0}\epsilon^{-1}\right)F(\epsilon j)^{2}.

(3.13)

According to Lemma 4.3 and 4.4 from
[
5
]
,
I
is dominated by

8\epsilon^{-2}\sigma^{2}\max\{4cT_{0},1\}\|F\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{sr}}.

(3.14)

Now we turn our attention to
II
. We can eliminate the
\alpha
dependence by taking
\alpha=1
i.e. choose
\widetilde{x}_{j}
s.t.

F^{\prime}(\widetilde{x}_{j})=\max_{x\in[\epsilon j-\epsilon n-\epsilon,\epsilon j-\epsilon n]}F^{\prime}(x).

Then

(\epsilon\alpha F^{\prime}(x_{j}))^{2}\leq(\epsilon F^{\prime}(\widetilde{x}_{j}))^{2}.

Shifting the index by
n
we get

II={\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{0\leq n\leq cT_{0}/\epsilon}\sum_{j\in{\bf{Z}}}\chi(j+n)^{2}F^{\prime}(\widetilde{x}_{j+n})^{2}\right]

where
\widetilde{x}_{j+n}\in[\epsilon j-\epsilon,\epsilon j]
does not depend on
n
. We therefore may relabel
\widetilde{x}_{j}=\widetilde{x}_{j+n}.
We use the same steps here as we used from to (
3.12
) to (
3.13
).

II\leq 4\sum_{j\in{\bf{Z}}}(\sigma^{2}|j|+4\sigma^{2}cT_{0}\epsilon^{-1})\epsilon^{2}F^{\prime}(\epsilon\widetilde{x}_{j})^{2}.

Again, by Lemma 4.3 from
[
5
]
,
II
is dominated by

8\sigma^{2}\max\{4cT_{0},1\}\|F\|^{2}_{H^{2}_{sr}}.

(3.15)

By (
3.14
) and (
3.15
) we have

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T_{0}/\epsilon}\|\chi(\cdot)F(\epsilon\cdot-\epsilon ct\|^{2}\right]\leq 8\epsilon^{-2}\sigma^{2}\max\{4cT_{0},1\}\|F\|^{2}_{H^{2}_{sr}}.

An standard application of Jensen’s inequality yields

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T_{0}/\epsilon}\|\chi(\cdot)F(\epsilon\cdot-\epsilon ct\|\right]\leq 2\sqrt{2}\epsilon^{-1}\sigma\max\{2\sqrt{cT_{0}},1\}\|F\|_{H^{2}_{sr}}.

This proves (
3.9
).

The exact same trickery that was used in Lemma
3.3

works to prove (
3.10
). Using (
3.9
) and then (
3.4
)

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T_{0}/\epsilon}\|\chi(\cdot)\delta^{+}F(\epsilon(\cdot-ct))\|_{\ell^{2}}\right]\leq 3\sqrt{2}\sigma\max\{2\sqrt{cT_{0}},1\}\|F\|_{H^{3}_{sr}}.

This shows (
3.10
).
∎

Remark 5
.

The functions in this subsection are required to be once more differentiable than the functions in the previous subsection, due to the use of the Mean Value Theorem in the beginning of the proof of the previous lemma.

Now we can prove:

Proposition 3.9
.

Fix
A,B\in H^{4}_{sr}
and take
\widetilde{r}_{\epsilon}
and
\widetilde{p}_{\epsilon}
as in (
2.18
). Fix
T_{0}>0
. For there exists a positive constant
C(\widetilde{k},\bar{m},a_{k},b_{k},a_{m},b_{m},T_{0},\|A\|_{H_{sr}^{4}},\|B\|_{H_{sr}^{4}})

for which
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
implies

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{|t|\leq T_{0}/\epsilon}\left(\|\Res_{1}(\widetilde{r}_{\epsilon},\widetilde{p}_{\epsilon})\|_{\ell^{2}}+\|\Res_{2}(\widetilde{r}_{\epsilon},\widetilde{p}_{\epsilon})\|_{\ell^{2}}\right)\right]\leq C\epsilon\left(\epsilon^{1/2}+\max\{\sigma_{m},\sigma_{k}\}\right).

(3.16)

Proof.

The proof begins the same way as the proof for Proposition
3.5
except now we take expectation of (
3.6
).
Since the first two lines of (

) are deterministic, using Lemma 4.3 in

, they are controlled by

\epsilon^{3/2}\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\widetilde{k}\bar{m}}}\left(\|A\|_{H^{2}}+\|B\|_{H^{2}}\right).

Next use (
3.9
) to control the third line with

\dfrac{2\sqrt{2}\epsilon\sigma_{m}c\max\{2\sqrt{|c|T_{0}},1\}}{a_{k}\sqrt{\bar{m}}}\left(\|A^{\prime}\|_{H_{sr}^{2}}+\|B^{\prime}\|_{H_{sr}^{2}}\right),

which is dominated by (

). We use (

)
to estimate the fourth line:

\dfrac{3\sqrt{2}\epsilon\sigma_{k}\max\{2\sqrt{|c|T_{0},}1\}}{\sqrt{\bar{m}}}\left(\|A^{\prime}\|_{H_{sr}^{3}}+\|B^{\prime}\|_{H_{sr}^{3}}\right).

As before, the estimate for
\Res_{2}
follows a parallel argument and is omitted.
∎

4
Error estimates

In this section we prove rigorous estimates
using “energy” arguments, similar to
[
5
,
2
,
10
]
.

4.1
The energy argument

Let
r
and
p
be a true solution to (
1.2
) and take
\widetilde{r}_{\epsilon}

and
\widetilde{p}_{\epsilon}
as in (
2.18
). Define
error functions

\eta
and
\xi
implicitly by

r=\widetilde{r}_{\epsilon}+\frac{\eta}{k}\quad\text{and}\quad p=\widetilde{p}_{\epsilon}+\xi.

(4.1)

It is our goal to determine the size in
\ell^{2}
of
\eta
and
\xi

during the period
|t|\leq T_{0}/\epsilon
.
To that end, insert (
4.1
) into (
1.2
) to find that

\displaystyle\frac{\dot{\eta}}{k}

\displaystyle=\delta^{+}\xi+\Res_{1}

(4.2)

\displaystyle m\dot{\xi}

\displaystyle=\delta^{-}\eta+\Res_{2}

where

\Res_{1}=\Res_{1}(\widetilde{r}_{\epsilon},\widetilde{p}_{\epsilon})

and

\Res_{2}=\Res_{2}(\widetilde{r}_{\epsilon},\widetilde{p}_{\epsilon})

as in (
2.1
).

Next
define the
energy
to be

H(t)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in{\bf{Z}}}\left[k(j)^{-1}\eta^{2}(j,t)+m(j)\xi^{2}(j,t)\right].

Since we have assumed that the
k(j)
and
m(j)
are drawn from distributions with support in

[a_{k},b_{k}]\subset{\bf{R}}^{+}
and
[a_{m},b_{m}]\subset{\bf{R}}^{+}
, respectively, a short calculation shows that

\sqrt{H}
is equivalent to
\|\eta,\xi\|_{\ell^{2}\times\ell^{2}}
and the constants of equivalence depend only
on
a_{k},a_{m},b_{k}
and
b_{m}
. That is to say, the equivalence is realization independent.

Time differentiation of
H
gives

\dot{H}=\sum_{j\in{\bf{Z}}}\left[k^{-1}\eta\dot{\eta}+m\xi\dot{\xi}\right].

Using (
4.2
)

\dot{H}=\sum_{j\in{\bf{Z}}}\left[\eta(\delta^{+}\xi+\text{Res}_{1})+\xi(\delta^{-}\eta+\text{Res}_{2})\right].

Summing by parts:

\dot{H}=\sum_{j\in{\bf{Z}}}\left[\eta\text{Res}_{1}+\xi\text{Res}_{2}\right].

Cauchy-Schwarz implies that

\dot{H}\leq\|\text{Res}_{1},\text{Res}_{2}\|_{\ell^{2}\times\ell^{2}}\|\eta,\xi\|_{\ell^{2}\times\ell^{2}}.

Then we use the equivalence of
\sqrt{H}
and
\|\eta,\xi\|_{\ell^{2}\times\ell^{2}}
to get:

\dot{H}\leq C\|\text{Res}_{1},\text{Res}_{2}\|_{\ell^{2}\times\ell^{2}}\sqrt{H}.

Set

\Gamma_{\epsilon}\coloneqq\sup_{|t|\leq T_{0}/\epsilon}\|\text{Res}_{1},\text{Res}_{2}\|_{\ell^{2}\times\ell^{2}},

so
\dot{H}/\sqrt{H}\leq C\Gamma_{\epsilon}
. We integrate from
0
to
t

2\sqrt{H(t)}\leq 2\sqrt{H(0)}+C\Gamma_{\epsilon}t.

And so, for
t\leq T_{0}/\epsilon
, we have

\sqrt{H(t)}\leq\sqrt{H(0)}+C\Gamma_{\epsilon}T_{0}\epsilon^{-1}.

If we use the equivalence of the
\sqrt{H}
and
\|\eta,\xi\|_{\ell^{2}\times\ell^{2}}
once again, we find that we have proven

\sup_{|t|\leq T_{0}/\epsilon}\|\eta(t),\xi(t)\|_{\ell^{2}\times\ell^{2}}\leq C\|\eta(0),\xi(0)\|_{\ell^{2}\times\ell^{2}}+C\Gamma_{\epsilon}\epsilon^{-1}.

(4.3)

A key feature of the above inequality is that the only place where the specific realization of the springs and masses enters is through
\Gamma_{\epsilon}
.

4.2
Almost sure error estimates

We can now prove our first main theorem, which is about almost sure estimation of the absolute error:

Theorem 4.1
.

Fix
\Phi,\Psi\in H^{3}_{LIL}
and
T_{0}>0
. Let
r
and
p
be the solution of (
1.2
)
with initial data

r(j,0)=\Phi(\epsilon j)/k(j)\quad\text{and}\quad p(j,0)=\Psi(\epsilon j).

For almost every realization of
\{k(j)\}
and
\{m(j)\}
there is a finite positive constant

C_{\omega}=C_{\omega}(k,m,a_{k},b_{k},a_{m},b_{m},\|\Phi\|_{H^{3}_{LIL}},\|\Psi\|_{H^{3}_{LIL}})

for which
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
implies

\sup_{|t|\leq T_{0}/\epsilon}\left\|r(\cdot,t)-{1\over k(\cdot)}\left(A(\epsilon(\cdot-ct)+B(\epsilon(\cdot+ct)\right)\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq C_{\omega}\sqrt{\log\log(1/\epsilon)}

and

\sup_{|t|\leq T_{0}/\epsilon}\left\|p(\cdot,t)-{1\over\sqrt{\widetilde{k}\bar{m}}}\left(-A(\epsilon(\cdot-ct)+B(\epsilon(\cdot+ct)\right)\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq C_{\omega}\sqrt{\log\log(1/\epsilon)}.

In the above

A(X):={1\over 2}\Phi(X)-{\sqrt{\widetilde{k}\bar{m}}\over 2}\Psi(X)\quad\text{and}\quad B(X):={1\over 2}\Phi(X)+{\sqrt{\widetilde{k}\bar{m}}\over 2}\Psi(X).

Remark 6
.

In the case where the masses and springs vary periodically instead of randomly, the size of the error decreases to
C\epsilon^{1/2}
; in fact the proof we supply in a moment together with Remark
3
suffices to demonstrate this. Likewise, if the masses and springs are constant a slightly modified version of
the proof can be used to decrease the error to
C\epsilon^{3/2}
. It is this extra wiggle room in the error in these cases which opens the door to longer time scales and KdV-like approximations.

Proof.

Form
\widetilde{r}_{\epsilon}
and
\widetilde{p}_{\epsilon}
from the functions
A
and
B
as specified in (
2.18
) and

\eta
and
\xi
as in (
4.1
). A bit of algebra shows that

\eta(j,0)=\epsilon{\chi_{m}(j)}\left(A^{\prime}(\epsilon j)+B^{\prime}(\epsilon j)\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\xi(j,0)=\epsilon{\chi_{k}(j)\over\sqrt{\widetilde{k}\bar{m}}}\left(-A^{\prime}(\epsilon j)+B^{\prime}(\epsilon j)\right).

(4.4)

Using (
3.2
) in a very crude way, we see that almost surely

\|\eta(0),\xi(0)\|_{\ell^{2}\times\ell^{2}}\leq C_{\omega}\sqrt{\log\log(1/\epsilon)}

with the constant depending on
\|A\|_{H^{2}_{LIL}}
and
\|B\|_{H^{2}_{LIL}}
.
We estimated
\Gamma_{\epsilon}
in Proposition
3.5
and found that

\Gamma_{\epsilon}\leq C_{\omega}\epsilon\sqrt{\log\log(1/\epsilon)}
when
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
almost surely.
Therefore (
4.3
) gives

\sup_{|t|\leq T_{0}/\epsilon}\|\eta(t),\xi(t)\|_{\ell^{2}\times\ell^{2}}\leq C_{\omega}\sqrt{\log\log(1/\epsilon)}.

To finish the proof we note that the triangle inequality tells us

\begin{split}&\left\|r(\cdot,t)-{1\over k(\cdot)}\left(A(\epsilon(\cdot-ct))+B(\epsilon(\cdot+ct))\right)\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\\
\leq&\left\|r(t)-\widetilde{r}_{\epsilon}(t)\right\|_{\ell^{2}}+\left\|\widetilde{r}_{\epsilon}(\cdot,t)-{1\over k(\cdot)}\left(A(\epsilon(\cdot-ct))+B(\epsilon(\cdot+ct))\right)\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\\
\leq&C\|\eta(t)\|_{\ell^{2}}+C\epsilon\|{\chi_{m}(\cdot)}A^{\prime}(\epsilon(\cdot-ct))\|+C\epsilon\|{\chi_{m}(\cdot)}B^{\prime}(\epsilon(\cdot-ct))\|_{\ell^{2}}.\end{split}

The terms involve
A
and
B
can be estimated using (
3.2
) by
C_{\omega}\sqrt{\log\log(1/\epsilon)}

so we find

\sup_{|t|\leq T_{0}/\epsilon}\left\|r(\cdot,t)-{1\over k(\cdot)}\left(A(\epsilon(\cdot-ct))+B(\epsilon(\cdot+ct))\right)\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq C_{\omega}\sqrt{\log\log(1/\epsilon)}.

The remaining estimate in the Theorem
4.1
is shown by a parallel argument and is omitted.
∎

It may seem like the estimates in Theorem
4.1
are utterly useless since the size of the error diverges
as
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
. But the error in that theorem is the
absolute
error; the
relative
error does in fact
vanish in the limit.

Corollary 4.2
.

Under the same conditions as in Theorem
4.1
we almost surely have

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\sup_{|t|\leq T_{0}/\epsilon}{\left\|r(\cdot,t)-{1\over k(\cdot)}\left(A(\epsilon(\cdot-ct))+B(\epsilon(\cdot+ct))\right)\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\over\|r(t)\|_{\ell^{2}}}=0

and

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\sup_{|t|\leq T_{0}/\epsilon}{\left\|p(\cdot,t)-{1\over\sqrt{\widetilde{k}\bar{m}}}\left(-A(\epsilon(\cdot-ct))+B(\epsilon(\cdot+ct))\right)\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\over\|p(t)\|_{\ell^{2}}}=0.

Proof.

The reverse triangle inequality gives

\|r(t)\|_{\ell^{2}}\geq\left\|{1\over k(\cdot)}\left(A(\epsilon(\cdot-ct))+B(\epsilon(\cdot+ct))\right)\right\|_{\ell^{2}}-\left\|r(\cdot,t)-{1\over k(\cdot)}\left(A(\epsilon(\cdot-ct)+B(\epsilon(\cdot+ct)\right)\right\|_{\ell^{2}}.

Using Lemma 4.3 from
[
5
]
for the first term and Theorem
4.1
for the second
we obtain

\|r(t)\|_{\ell^{2}}\geq C\epsilon^{-1/2}-C_{\omega}\sqrt{\log\log(1/\epsilon)}

for all
|t|\leq T_{0}/\epsilon
.
This is positive for
\epsilon
small enough and so we get the first limit in the corollary by dividing
the absolute error for
r
in Theorem
4.1
by this estimate and taking the limit. The second limit is analogous.
∎

4.3
Error estimate in mean

We can now prove our second main theorem, which is an estimate of the mean of the error.

Theorem 4.3
.

Fix
\Phi,\Psi\in H^{4}_{sr}
and
T_{0}>0.
Let
r
and
p
be the solution of (
1.2
) with initial data

r(j,0)=\Phi(\epsilon j)/k(j)\quad\text{and}\quad p(j,0)=\phi(\epsilon j).

There exists a positive constant
C(\widetilde{k},\bar{m},a_{k},b_{k},a_{m},b_{m},T_{0},\|A\|_{H^{4}_{sr}},\|B\|_{H^{4}_{sr}})
for which
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
implies

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{|t|\leq T_{0}/\epsilon}\left\|r(\cdot,t)-{1\over k(\cdot)}\left(A(\epsilon(\cdot-ct)+B(\epsilon(\cdot+ct)\right)\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\right]\leq C\left(\epsilon^{1/2}+\max\{\sigma_{m},\sigma_{k}\}\right)

and

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{|t|\leq T_{0}/\epsilon}\left\|p(\cdot,t)-{1\over\sqrt{\widetilde{k}\bar{m}}}\left(-A(\epsilon(\cdot-ct)+B(\epsilon(\cdot+ct)\right)\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\right]\leq C\left(\epsilon^{1/2}+\max\{\sigma_{m},\sigma_{k}\}\right).

In the above

A(X):={1\over 2}\Phi(X)-{\sqrt{\widetilde{k}\bar{m}}\over 2}\Psi(X)\quad\text{and}\quad B(X):={1\over 2}\Phi(X)+{\sqrt{\widetilde{k}\bar{m}}\over 2}\Psi(X).

Proof.

Begin as in the proof of Theorem
4.1
.
Using (
3.9
) on (
4.4
)

\|\eta(0),\xi(0)\|_{\ell^{2}\times\ell^{2}}\leq C\max\{\sigma_{m},\sigma_{k}\}

with constant
C
depending on
\|A\|_{H^{3}_{sr}}
and
\|B\|_{H^{3}_{sr}}.

Proposition
3.9
gives us

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Gamma_{\epsilon}\right]\leq C\epsilon\left(\epsilon^{1/2}+\max\{\sigma_{m},\sigma_{k}\}\right)

when
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
.
Therefore (
4.3
) gives

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{|t|\leq T_{0}/\epsilon}\|\eta(t),\xi(t)\|_{\ell^{2}\times\ell^{2}}\right]\leq C\left(\epsilon^{1/2}+\max\{\sigma_{m},\sigma_{k}\}\right).

To finish the proof we note that the triangle inequality tells us

\begin{split}&{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{|t|\leq T_{0}/\epsilon}\left\|r(\cdot,t)-{1\over k(\cdot)}\left(A(\epsilon(\cdot-ct))+B(\epsilon(\cdot+ct))\right)\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\right]\\
\leq&{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{|t|\leq T_{0}}\left\|r(t)-\widetilde{r}_{\epsilon}(t)\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\right]+{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{|t|\leq T_{0}}\left\|\widetilde{r}_{\epsilon}(\cdot,t)-{1\over k(\cdot)}\left(A(\epsilon(\cdot-ct))+B(\epsilon(\cdot+ct))\right)\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\right]\\
\leq&{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{|t|\leq T_{0}}C\|\eta(t)\|_{\ell^{2}}\right]+{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{|t|\leq T_{0}}C\epsilon\|{\chi_{m}(\cdot)}A^{\prime}(\epsilon(\cdot-ct))\|\right]+{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{|t|\leq T_{0}}C\epsilon\|{\chi_{m}(\cdot)}B^{\prime}(\epsilon(\cdot-ct))\|_{\ell^{2}}\right].\end{split}

The
C
depends on
a_{k},b_{k},a_{m}
, and
b_{m}
, which are fixed, so we may pull it out of the expected value. The terms that involve
A
and
B
can be estimated using (
3.9
) by
C\max\{\sigma_{k},\sigma_{m}\}

so we find

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{|t|\leq T_{0}/\epsilon}\left\|r(\cdot,t)-{1\over k(\cdot)}\left(A(\epsilon(\cdot-ct))+B(\epsilon(\cdot+ct))\right)\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\right]\leq C\left(\epsilon^{1/2}+\max\{\sigma_{k},\sigma_{m}\}\right).

The remaining estimate in the Theorem
4.3
is shown by a parallel argument and is omitted.

∎

5
Coarse-graining

We now prove strong convergence results using the
ideas of coarse-graining from
[
8
]
.
We need quite a few tools.
Letting
f:{\bf{Z}}\to{\bf{R}}
and
g,u,v:{\bf{R}}\to{\bf{R}}
define

\displaystyle F[f](\kappa)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{j\in{\bf{Z}}}e^{-ij\kappa}f(j)

\displaystyle F^{-1}[g](j)

\displaystyle:=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}g(\kappa)e^{i\kappa j}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[u](\xi)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{{\bf{R}}}u(x)e^{-i\xi x}dx

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{-1}[v](x)

\displaystyle:=\int_{{\bf{R}}}v(\xi)e^{i\xi x}d\xi

\displaystyle\theta_{\phi}(\kappa)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}1&\kappa\in(-\phi,\phi)\\
0&\text{else}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[f](x)

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{F}^{-1}[\theta_{\pi}(\cdot)F[f](\cdot)](x)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}[u](j)

\displaystyle:=u(j).

These are, in order, the Fourier Transform for sequences, its inverse, the Fourier transform of functions
{\bf{R}}\to{\bf{R}}
, its inverse, the indicator function of
(-\phi,\phi)
, a “low pass” interpolation operator, and a sampling operator.
To be clear, in the above
x,\xi,\kappa\in{\bf{R}}
and
j\in{\bf{Z}}
, always.

The operator
\mathcal{L}
converts a sequence
f
defined on
{\bf{Z}}
to a new function defined on
{\bf{R}}.
The sampling function
\mathcal{S}
returns a sequence from a function defined on
{\bf{R}}
. It is an easy exercise to show that
\mathcal{SL}[f](j)=f(j)
so it is clear
\mathcal{L}
is an interpolation operator. Another essential property is the following.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
f
be a sequence in
\ell^{2}
. Then

\|f\|_{\ell^{2}}=2\pi\|\mathcal{L}[f]\|_{L^{2}({\bf{R}})}.

Proof.

By Plancherel’s theorem:

\|F[f]\|_{L^{2}(-\pi,\pi)}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\|f\|_{\ell^{2}}.

Then by a slightly different Plancherel’s theorem:

\|\mathcal{F}^{-1}[\theta_{\pi}F[f]]\|_{L^{2}({\bf{R}})}=\|\theta_{\pi}F[f]\|_{L^{2}({\bf{R}})}=\|F[f]\|_{L^{2}(-\pi,\pi)}

completing the proof. ∎

We need one more lemma before we can state the strong convergence results.
It states that for a smooth enough function, the more frequently it is sampled,
the more is interpolation looks like the original function.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
f:{\bf{R}}\to{\bf{R}}
be in
H^{s}
with
s>1/2
and put
f_{\epsilon}(x)=f(\epsilon x)
.
Then

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\|\mathcal{L}\mathcal{S}[f_{\epsilon}](\cdot/\epsilon)-f\|_{L^{2}}=0.

Proof.

From their definitions we have

\mathcal{L}{{\mathcal{S}}}[f_{\epsilon}](x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}(\sum_{j\in{\bf{Z}}}e^{-i\kappa j}f(\epsilon j))e^{i\kappa x}d\kappa=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}(\sum_{j\in{\bf{Z}}}e^{-i\frac{\kappa}{\epsilon}\epsilon j}f(\epsilon j))e^{i\kappa x}d\kappa.

Changing variables with
u=\kappa/\epsilon

we get

\mathcal{L}{{\mathcal{S}}}[f_{\epsilon}](x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi/\epsilon}^{\pi/\epsilon}(\sum_{j\in{\bf{Z}}}\epsilon e^{-iu\epsilon j}f(\epsilon j))e^{iu\epsilon x}du.

Exchanging the sum and integral and then computing the integral.

\mathcal{L}{{\mathcal{S}}}[f_{\epsilon}](x)=\sum_{j\in{\bf{Z}}}f(\epsilon j)\sinc(x-j).

This
\sinc
is the normalized
\sinc
function.

Now put
\widetilde{f}_{\epsilon}(X):=\mathcal{F}^{-1}[\theta_{\pi/\epsilon}\mathcal{F}[f]](X).

\widetilde{f}_{\epsilon}
is a band limited approximation of
f
. Using Plancherel’s theorem

\|f-\widetilde{f}_{\epsilon}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}=\|\mathcal{F}[f]-\theta_{\pi/\epsilon}\mathcal{F}[f]\|^{2}_{L^{2}}=\int_{\kappa>|\pi/\epsilon|}\left|\mathcal{F}[f](\kappa)\right|^{2}d\kappa.

Since
f\in H^{s}
we have by Cauchy-Schwarz, when
\epsilon\in(0,1)
:

\begin{split}\|f-\widetilde{f}_{\epsilon}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}=&\int_{\kappa>|\pi/\epsilon|}{1\over|\kappa|^{2s}}|\kappa|^{2s}\left|\mathcal{F}[f](\kappa)\right|^{2}d\kappa\\
\leq&\left(\int_{|\kappa|\geq\pi/\epsilon}|\kappa|^{-2s}d\kappa\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{|\kappa|\geq\pi/\epsilon}|\kappa|^{2s}\left|\mathcal{F}[f](\kappa)\right|^{2}d\kappa\right)^{1/2}\\
\leq&C\epsilon^{{s-1/2}}\|f\|_{H^{s}}.\end{split}

Since
s>1/2
we see that
\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\|f-\widetilde{f}_{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}}=0
.

Since
\widetilde{f}_{\epsilon}
is band limited, it is exactly equal to its cardinal series, see
[
7
]
,

\widetilde{f}_{\epsilon}(X)=\sum_{j\in{\bf{Z}}}f(\epsilon j)\sinc(X/\epsilon-j).

But this is exactly equal to
{\mathcal{L}}{\mathcal{S}}[f_{\epsilon}](X/\epsilon)
.
Therefore we have shown that

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\|\mathcal{L}\mathcal{S}(f_{\epsilon})(\cdot/\epsilon)-f(\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}=0.

∎

Here is our first course-graining result:

Theorem 5.3
.

Fix
\Phi,\Psi\in H^{3}_{LIL}
and
T_{0}>0
. Let
r
and
p
be the solution of (
1.2
)
with initial data

r(j,0)=\Phi(\epsilon j)/k(j)\quad\text{and}\quad p(j,0)=\Psi(\epsilon j).

Put

Q_{\epsilon}(X,\tau)={\mathcal{L}}[kr(\cdot,\tau/\epsilon)](X/\epsilon)\quad\text{and}\quad P_{\epsilon}(X,\tau)={\mathcal{L}}[p(\cdot,\tau/\epsilon)](X/\epsilon).

Suppose that
Q_{0}(X,\tau)
and
P_{0}(X,\tau)
solve (
2.14
) with initial
data
Q_{0}(X,0)=\Phi(X)
and
P_{0}(X,0)=\Psi(X)
.
Then, almost surely,

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\sup_{|\tau|\leq T_{0}}\left(\|Q_{\epsilon}(X,\tau)-Q_{0}(X,\tau)\|_{L^{2}}+\|P_{\epsilon}(X,\tau)-P_{0}(X,\tau)\|_{L^{2}}\right)=0.

Proof.

We show the limit for
\|P_{\epsilon}(X,\tau)-P_{0}(X,\tau)\|_{L^{2}}
as the other is all but identical.
By the triangle inequality we have

\|P_{\epsilon}(\cdot,\tau)-P_{0}(\cdot,\tau)\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|P_{\epsilon}(\cdot,\tau)-{\mathcal{L}}{\mathcal{S}}[P_{0}(\epsilon\cdot,\tau)](\cdot/\epsilon)\|_{L^{2}}+\|{\mathcal{L}}{\mathcal{S}}[P_{0}(\epsilon\cdot,\tau)](\cdot/\epsilon)-P_{0}(\cdot,\tau)\|_{L^{2}}.

The second term vanishes as
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
by virtue of Lemma
5.2
.
(In fact, given (
2.15
) one sees that this convergence happens uniformly for all
\tau\in{\bf{R}}
.)

For the first term we do a change of variables
X=\epsilon x
and
\tau=\epsilon t
to get

\|P_{\epsilon}(\cdot,\tau)-{\mathcal{L}}{\mathcal{S}}[P_{0}(\epsilon\cdot,\tau)](\cdot/\epsilon)\|_{L^{2}}=\sqrt{\epsilon}\|P_{\epsilon}(\epsilon\cdot,\epsilon t)-{\mathcal{L}}{\mathcal{S}}[P_{0}(\epsilon\cdot,\epsilon t)](\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}.

Then we use the definition of
P_{\epsilon}
and Lemma
5.1
to get

\|P_{\epsilon}(\cdot,\tau)-{\mathcal{L}}{\mathcal{S}}[P_{0}(\epsilon\cdot,\tau)](\cdot/\epsilon)\|_{L^{2}}={1\over 2\pi}\sqrt{\epsilon}\|p(\cdot,t)-{\mathcal{S}}[P_{0}(\epsilon\cdot,\epsilon t)]\|_{\ell^{2}}.

Using (
2.15
) and the formulas relating
\Phi
and
\Psi
to
A
and
B

in Theorem
4.1
we see

{\mathcal{S}}[P_{0}(\epsilon\cdot,\epsilon t)](j)={1\over\sqrt{\widetilde{k}\bar{m}}}\left(-A(\epsilon(j-ct)+B(\epsilon(j+ct)\right).

Thus we can use the final estimate in Theorem
4.1
to get

\sup_{|\tau|\leq T_{0}}\|P_{\epsilon}(\cdot,\tau)-{\mathcal{L}}{\mathcal{S}}[P_{0}(\epsilon\cdot,\tau)](\cdot/\epsilon)\|_{L^{2}}\leq\sup_{|t|\leq T_{0}/\epsilon}{1\over 2\pi}\sqrt{\epsilon}\|p(\cdot,t)-{\mathcal{S}}[P_{0}(\epsilon\cdot,\epsilon t)]\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq C_{\omega}\sqrt{\epsilon\log\log(1/\epsilon)}.

(5.1)

The right hand side goes to zero as
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
and we are done.

∎

6.2
Conclusion

Our results are significant in several important ways regarding the description of approximate waves in the random polymer linear FPUT system. We have proven from first principles that that solutions to the wave equation are good approximate solutions to the system studied here. We showed that the absolute error only grows at most like
O(\log\log(1/\epsilon)
almost surely and is constant in mean, but also small in mean if the masses and springs have small deviation. Using an interpolation operator with strong analytic properties we were able to show that the interpolated approximate solutions converged to interpolated true solutions in a relative sense a.s. and in expectation. Such results provide a rigorous justification for claiming that the relative error is made arbitrarily small by taking
\epsilon
to be small.

The advantage of our method comes from the use of the random walk in capturing the build up of error. Since random walks of independent variables are well studied and sharp asymptotic estimates are known, we were able to use the random walk to its full extent. Although it remains unproven if the error we achieved is sharp, the numerical results suggest it is close, and it seems nothing more about the asymptotics of the random walk, at least in the almost sure sense, could be used to prove sharper bounds. It also remains unclear if the random walk is an intrinsic part of the mechanics of the problem or if it is only a useful fiction for modeling the error. To what extent could it be further exploited here and in other models that have similar dynamics

With this work we have laid the foundation for a couple of questions. First, can the error term be modeled by a random variable independent of
\epsilon
with a nice probability distribution such as a Gaussian. There is also the question as to whether the results can be extended to higher dimensions. Probably most interesting, is what happens on larger time scales? In the periodic setting, solitons are known to last up to times proportional to
1/\epsilon^{3}
; however, it is not clear how to continue with the current methodology as was done to derive the KdV equations in the periodic case. This is mainly because one needs to make sense of
\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{|j|\leq n}\chi(j)/n
, which, even if one optimistically replaces
\chi(j)
with
\sqrt{|j|}
, will diverge. This raises the question: is it is possible to find an effective equation describing the the dynamics for longer times and will these descriptions be statistical or is there room to achieve anything more definite, like the high probability and almost sure results constructed here?
