Abstract
. A general system of several ordinary differential equations coupled with a reaction-diffusion equation in a bounded domain with zero-flux boundary condition is studied in the context of pattern formation. These initial-boundary value problems may have regular ( i.e. sufficiently smooth) stationary solutions. This class of close-to-equilibrium patterns includes stationary solutions that emerge due to the Turing instability of a spatially constant stationary solution. The main result of this work is instability of all regular patterns. It suggests that stable stationary solutions arising in models with non-diffusive components must be far-from-equilibrium exhibiting singularities. Such discontinuous stationary solutions have been considered in our parallel work [ Stable discontinuous stationary solutions to reaction-diffusion-ODE systems , preprint (2021)].

Main results

The goal of this work is to construct certain non-constant stationary solutions of problem (

(\boldsymbol{U},V)=\big(\boldsymbol{U}(x),V(x)\big)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{U},V)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\Delta_{\nu}V+g(\boldsymbol{U},V)

\displaystyle=0,

Definition 2.1
.

A pair
(\boldsymbol{U},V)=\big(\boldsymbol{U}(x),V(x)\big)

is
a weak solution of problem (

)
if

•

\boldsymbol{U}

•

•

•

•

the equality

(2.2)

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\nabla V(x)\cdot\nabla\varphi(x)\,\text{d}x+\int_{\Omega}g\big(\boldsymbol{U}(x),V(x)\big)\varphi(x)\,\text{d}x=0

holds true for all test functions

Definition 2.2
.

A weak solution in the sense of Definition
2.1
to problem (
2.1

\boldsymbol{U},V\in{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
and, moreover,
there exists a

Remark 2.3
.

Notice that every regular solution of problem (
2.1
) satisfies the equation

(2.3)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}\big(\boldsymbol{U}(x),V(x)\big)=\boldsymbol{f}\big(\boldsymbol{k}(V(x)),V(x)\big)=0\quad\text{for all}\quad x\in\Omega,

where
V=V(x)
is a solution of the elliptic Neumann problem

(2.4)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\nu}V+h(V)=0\qquad\text{for}\quad x\in\Omega

with
h(V)=g\big(\boldsymbol{k}(V),V\big)
.
Since we require
V\in{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
and since

is

, by a standard elliptic regularity, we have
V\in W^{2,p}(\Omega)
for each

and consequently
\boldsymbol{U}\in W^{2,p}(\Omega)^{n}
for every
p\in(1,\infty)
. In particular, every regular stationary solution satisfies
(\boldsymbol{U},V)\in C(\overline{\Omega})^{n+1}
.

Remark 2.4
.

Assume that
\big(\boldsymbol{U}(x),V(x)\big)
is a non-constant regular solution to problem (

). Then, there exists

and

Remark 2.9
.

Let us illustrate both instability Theorems

and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}_{t}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{A}_{0}\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{B}_{0}v,

\displaystyle=\Delta_{\nu}v+\boldsymbol{C}_{0}\boldsymbol{u}+d_{0}v,

Remark 2.10
.

Condition (

3.
Existence of regular stationary solutions

First, we prove a general result on the existence of non-constant solutions to Neumann boundary value problem (
2.4
). This is rather standard result obtained for example in
[
33
, Sec. 3]
or
[
32
, Sec. 2]
in the case of simple eigenvalues. We recall one possible construction of such solutions using variational methods in the following lemma which is a generalization of
[
30
, Theorem 11.32]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
N\leq 6
. Assume that
r\in C_{b}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
satisfies
r(0)=r^{\prime}(0)=0
.
There exists a sequence of numbers
d_{\ell}\to 1
and a sequence of non-constant functions

v_{\ell}\in W^{1,2}(\Omega)
such that
\|v_{\ell}\|_{W^{1,2}}\to 0
and which satisfy the boundary value problem

(3.1)

\displaystyle d_{\ell}\Delta_{\nu}v_{\ell}+(\mu_{k}+1-d_{\ell})v_{\ell}+r(v_{\ell})

\displaystyle=0\qquad\text{for}\quad x\in\Omega.

Proof.

We prove this lemma by using the Rabinowitz Bifurcation Theorem
[
29
, Thm. 0.2]
for equations with a variational formulation.
It is assumed in that approach that

•

E
is a real Hilbert space,

•

I\in C^{2}(E,\mathbb{R})
with
I^{\prime}(u)=Lu+H(u)
,

•

L
is linear and
H(u)=o(\|u\|)
at
u=0
,

•

\mu
is an isolated eigenvalue of
L
of a finite multiplicity.

Under these assumptions, by
[
29
, Thm. 0.2]
, the couple
(\mu,0)\in\mathbb{R}\times E
is a bifurcation point of the equation

(3.2)

\displaystyle G(\lambda,v)\equiv Lv+H(v)-\lambda v=0

which means that each neighborhood of
(\mu,0)
contains a solution
(\lambda,v)
with
\|v\|\neq 0
of equation (
3.2
). In our case, we use the the usual Sobolev space
E=W^{1,2}(\Omega)
with the equivalent scalar product

(3.3)

\displaystyle\langle u,v\rangle_{W^{1,2}(\Omega)}=\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,\text{d}x+\int_{\Omega}uv\,\text{d}x

as well as the functional

(3.4)

\displaystyle I(v)=\frac{\mu_{k}+1}{2}\int_{\Omega}v^{2}\,\text{d}x+\int_{\Omega}R(v)\,\text{d}x

with
R(v)=\int_{0}^{v}r(s)\,ds
. It is an elementary calculation to show that

•

I\in C\big(W^{1,2}(\Omega),\mathbb{R}\big)
,

•

it is differentiable in the Fréchet sense and for each
v\in W^{1,2}(\Omega)

(3.5)

\displaystyle DI(v)\varphi=(\mu_{k}+1)\int_{\Omega}v\varphi\,\text{d}x+\int_{\Omega}r(v)\varphi\,\text{d}x,

•

DI\in C\Big(W^{1,2}(\Omega),\;
Lin
\big(W^{1,2}(\Omega),\mathbb{R}\big)\Big)
.

The second Fréchet derivative at the point
v\in W^{1,2}(\Omega)
is represented by the bilinear form

(3.6)

\displaystyle\big\langle D^{2}I(v)\varphi,\psi\big\rangle=(\mu_{k}+1)\int_{\Omega}\varphi\psi\,\text{d}x+\int_{\Omega}r^{\prime}(v)\varphi\psi\,\text{d}x.

Let us show that
D^{2}I\in C\Big(W^{1,2}(\Omega),
Lin
\big(W^{1,2}(\Omega),
Lin
(W^{1,2}(\Omega),\mathbb{R})\big)\Big)
. For
v_{n}\to v
in
W^{1,2}(\Omega)
and
\psi,\varphi\in W^{1,2}(\Omega)
we estimate

(3.7)

\displaystyle\left|\big\langle\big(D^{2}I(v_{n})-D^{2}I(v)\big)\varphi,\psi\big\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\Omega}|r^{\prime}(v_{n})-r^{\prime}(v)||\varphi||\psi|\,\text{d}x

\displaystyle\leqslant\|r^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}\int_{\Omega}|v_{n}-v||\varphi||\psi|\,\text{d}x

\displaystyle\leqslant\|r^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}\|v_{n}-v\|_{3}\|\varphi\|_{3}\|\psi||_{3}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|r^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}\|v_{n}-v\|_{W^{1,2}}\|\varphi\|_{W^{1,2}}||\psi||_{W^{1,2}}.

The last inequality follows from the Sobolev embeddings with the assumption
N\leqslant 6
.

In particular, for each test function
\varphi\in W^{1,2}(\Omega)
, we obtain

(3.8)

\displaystyle I^{\prime}(v)(\varphi)=(\mu_{k}+1)\int_{\Omega}v\varphi\,\text{d}x+\int_{\Omega}r(v)\varphi\,\text{d}x\equiv Lv(\varphi)+H(v)(\varphi),

where, by the assumption on
r=r(v)
, we obtain immediately that

H(v)=o\big(\|v\|_{W^{1,2}}\big)
as
\|v\|_{W^{1,2}}\to 0
.

Notice now that
\lambda=1
is an isolated eigenvalue of finite multiplicity of the operator
L
. Indeed, this claim is equivalent to the equality

(3.9)

\displaystyle Lv(\varphi)=\langle v,\varphi\rangle_{W^{1,2}(\Omega)}\qquad\text{for all}\quad\varphi\in W^{1,2}(\Omega),

that is, to the equation

(3.10)

\displaystyle(\mu_{k}+1)\int_{\Omega}v\varphi\,dx=\int_{\Omega}\nabla v\cdot\nabla\varphi\,\text{d}x+\int_{\Omega}v\varphi\,\text{d}x\qquad\text{for all}\quad\varphi\in W^{1,2}(\Omega),

which obviously reduces to the eigenvalue problem for
\Delta_{\nu}
. Now, we apply the fact that
\mu_{k}
is an isolated eigenvalue of finite multiplicity.

Thus, by the Rabinowitz Theorem
[
29
, Thm. 0.2]
, the couple
(1,0)
is a bifurcation point of equation (
3.2
) which means that there exists a sequence of numbers
d_{\ell}\to 1
and nonzero
\{v_{\ell}\}\subset W^{1,2}(\Omega)
such that
\|v_{\ell}\|_{W^{1,2}}\to 0
, satisfying the following equation

(3.11)

\displaystyle Lv_{\ell}(\varphi)+H(v_{\ell})(\varphi)-d_{\ell}\langle v,\varphi\rangle_{1,2}=0\qquad\text{for all}\quad\varphi\in W^{1,2}(\Omega)

which, in our setting, is equivalent to the equation satisfied by the weak solutions
v_{\ell}\in W^{1,2}(\Omega)
to problem (
3.1
):

(3.12)

\displaystyle-d_{\ell}\int_{\Omega}\nabla v_{\ell}\cdot\nabla\varphi\,\text{d}x+(\mu_{k}+1-d_{\ell})\int_{\Omega}v_{\ell}\varphi\,\text{d}x+\int_{\Omega}r(v_{\ell})\varphi\,\text{d}x=0

for all
\varphi\in W^{1,2}(\Omega)
.
∎

4.1.
Linearised operator

We analyse the stability of stationary solution to problem (
1.1
)-(
1.5
) via the usual linearisation procedure. In order to apply that approach in this section, we discuss stability properties of the zero solution to the following linear reaction diffusion-ODE system

(4.1)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{t}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\boldsymbol{B}\psi,

\displaystyle x\in\overline{\Omega},\quad t>0,

\displaystyle\psi_{t}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{\nu}\psi+\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{\varphi}+d\psi,

\displaystyle x\in\Omega,\quad t>0,

with matrices

(4.2)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{A}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}a_{11}(x)&\dots&a_{1n}(x)\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{n1}(x)&\dots&a_{nn}(x)\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{B}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{B}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}(x)\\
\vdots\\
b_{n}(x)\\
\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{C}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{C}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}(x)&\dots&c_{n}(x)\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle=d(x),

where

(4.3)

\displaystyle a_{ij},\;b_{i},\;c_{i},\;d\in L^{\infty}(\Omega),\quad\text{for}\;i,j\in\{1,\cdots,n\}.

In the following, we study properties of the operator
\big(\mathcal{L}_{p},D(\mathcal{L}_{p})\big)
defined by the formula

(4.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{p}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\varphi}\\
\psi\end{pmatrix}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\boldsymbol{B}\psi\\
\Delta_{\nu}\psi+\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{\varphi}+d\psi\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{with}\quad D(\mathcal{L}_{p})=L^{p}(\Omega)^{n}\times W^{2,p}_{\nu}(\Omega)

for each
p\in(1,\,\infty)
, where

(4.5)

\displaystyle W^{2,p}_{\nu}(\Omega)=\{v\in W^{2,p}(\Omega):\,\partial_{\nu}v=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega\}

is the Sobolev space
W^{2,p}(\Omega)
supplemented with the Neumann boundary condition (see
e.g.

[
40
, Ch. 2, sec 2.4]
).

Proposition 4.1
.

For each
p\in(1,\infty)
the operator
\big(\mathcal{L}_{p},D(\mathcal{L}_{p})\big)
generates an analytic semigroup of linear operators on
L^{p}(\Omega)^{n+1}
.

Proof.

This fact is well-known in the case of the Laplace operator with the Neumann boundary condition, see
e.g.

[
5
, Ch. II, Sec. 5]
. Here, we consider bounded perturbations of this operator.
∎

In order to study a nonlinear stability, we need a counterpart of the semigroup from Proposition
4.1
acting on a space of bounded continuous functions. First, we recall that the operator
\big(\Delta_{\nu},D(\Delta_{\nu})\big)
with the domain

(4.6)

\displaystyle D(\Delta_{\nu})=\left\{u\in\bigcap_{p\geqslant 1}W^{2,p}_{loc}(\Omega):\quad u,\,\Delta u\in C(\overline{\Omega}),\quad\partial_{\nu}u=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega\right\}

is a sectorial operator on the space

, see
[

Proposition 4.2
.

If

for

then the operator
given by the formula

(4.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\infty}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\varphi}\\
\psi\end{pmatrix}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\boldsymbol{B}\psi\\
\Delta_{\nu}\psi+\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{\varphi}+d\psi\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{with}\quad D(\mathcal{L}_{\infty})=C(\overline{\Omega})^{n}\times D(\Delta_{\nu})

generates an analytic semigroup of linear operators on

.

One proves this proposition in the same way as Proposition

.

4.2.
Spectra of multiplication operators

As usual, we denote by
\sigma(L)
the spectrum of a linear operator
\big(L,D(L)\big)
and
s(L)=\sup\{{\rm Re\,}\lambda:\lambda\in\sigma(L)\}
its spectral bound. In particular, for a square matrix
\boldsymbol{M}
with constant elements

(4.8)

\displaystyle s(\boldsymbol{M})=\max\{{\rm Re\,}\lambda:\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\text{ is an eigenvalue of }\boldsymbol{M}\}.

Now, we discuss spectra of matrix multiplication operators induced by matrices with
x
-dependent and bounded coefficients. The following results can be found either in the work
[
4
]
or in the PhD dissertation
[
14
]
and we recall them together with the proofs in slightly less general versions.

First we show that the spectrum of the multiplication operator consist only of approximative eigenvalues.

Lemma 4.3
.

For
\boldsymbol{M}(x)=\big(m_{i,j}(x)\big)_{i,j=1}^{n}
with
m_{i,j}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, we define the multiplication operator on
L^{p}(\Omega)^{n}
with
p\in[1,\infty)
by the formula
\big(\boldsymbol{M}(\cdot)\boldsymbol{\xi}\big)(x)=\boldsymbol{M}(x)\boldsymbol{\xi}(x)
. Then its spectrum has the form

(4.9)

\displaystyle\sigma\big(\boldsymbol{M}(\cdot)\big)=\left\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:\begin{array}[]{l}\forall{\varepsilon>0}\quad\exists{\Omega_{\varepsilon}\subset\Omega}\ open\ set\quad\exists{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\\
such\ that\ \|\boldsymbol{M}(\cdot)\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\varepsilon}-\lambda\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})}\leqslant\varepsilon|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\varepsilon}|\end{array}\right\}.

Proof.

Denote by
\boldsymbol{M}_{ess}
the set on the right-hand side of equality (
4.9
) and let
\lambda\in\boldsymbol{M}_{ess}
. Suppose that
\lambda\notin\sigma(\boldsymbol{M}(\cdot))
. Hence, there a exists a constant
C>0
such that

(4.10)

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\|_{p}\leqslant C\|(\boldsymbol{M}(\cdot)-\lambda\boldsymbol{I})\boldsymbol{\xi}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\quad\text{for all}\quad\boldsymbol{\xi}\in L^{p}(\Omega)^{n}.

Now by the definition of
\boldsymbol{M}_{ess}
, for each
\varepsilon>0
there exist open set
\Omega_{\varepsilon}\subset\Omega
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\varepsilon}
such that
\|\boldsymbol{M}(\cdot)\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\varepsilon}-\lambda\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})}\leqslant\varepsilon|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\varepsilon}|
. Let
\boldsymbol{\xi}=\frac{1}{|\Omega_{\varepsilon}|}\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\varepsilon}
in inequality (
4.10
). Then,

(4.11)

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\|\big(\boldsymbol{M}(\cdot)-\lambda\boldsymbol{I}\big)\boldsymbol{\xi}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\|\big(\boldsymbol{M}(\cdot)-\lambda\boldsymbol{I}\big)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})}\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})}\leqslant C\varepsilon\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}.

For sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
, we have
\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}=0
which is contradiction.

Conversely, let
\lambda\notin\boldsymbol{M}_{ess}
. By the definition of
\boldsymbol{M}_{ess}
, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that, for all
\Omega_{\varepsilon}\subset\Omega
and for all vectors
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\varepsilon}|=1
we have
\|\boldsymbol{M}(\cdot)\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\varepsilon}-\lambda\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})}\geqslant\varepsilon
. It implies that the inequality
|\boldsymbol{M}(x)\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\varepsilon}-\lambda\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\varepsilon}|\geqslant\varepsilon|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\varepsilon}|
holds true for almost all
x\in\Omega
. Thus,

(4.12)

\displaystyle\|\big(\boldsymbol{M}(\cdot)-\lambda\boldsymbol{I}\big)\boldsymbol{\xi}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}=\left(\int_{\Omega}\big|\big(\boldsymbol{M}(x)-\lambda\boldsymbol{I}\big)\boldsymbol{\xi}(x)\big|^{p}\,\text{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\geqslant\varepsilon\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

and the operator
\boldsymbol{M}(\cdot)-\lambda\boldsymbol{I}
is invertible, hence
\lambda\notin\sigma\big(\boldsymbol{M}(\cdot)\big)
.
∎

The following lemma explains a relation between
\sigma\big(\boldsymbol{M}(\cdot)\big)
and the eigenvalues of the matrices
\boldsymbol{M}(x)
for each
x\in\Omega
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Assume that
\boldsymbol{M}(x)=\big(m_{i,j}(x)\big)_{i,j=1}^{n}
with
m_{i,j}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Assume that there exists a closed set
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\Omega
such that
|\Omega^{\prime}|=0
and
m_{i,j}\big|_{\Omega\setminus\Omega^{\prime}}
is continuous. Then

(4.13)

\displaystyle\sigma\big(\boldsymbol{M}(\cdot)\big)=\overline{\bigcup_{x\in\Omega\setminus\Omega^{\prime}}\sigma\big(\boldsymbol{M}(x)\big)}.

Proof.

Let
\lambda\in\overline{\bigcup_{x\in\Omega\setminus\Omega^{\prime}}\sigma\big(\boldsymbol{M}(x)\big)}
. Hence, there exists a sequence
\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset\Omega\setminus\Omega^{\prime}
such that the matrices
\boldsymbol{M}(x_{n})
have eigenvalues satisfying
\lambda_{n}\to\lambda
. Denote by
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\lambda_{n}}
the corresponding eigenvector,
i.e.

(\boldsymbol{M}(x)-\lambda_{n})\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\lambda_{n}}=0
. By the assumption, matrix elements
m_{i,j}(x)
are continuous functions on
\Omega\setminus\Omega^{\prime}
. Since eigenvalues of the matrix
\boldsymbol{M}(x)
are also continues functions of
x

(see Remark
4.5
, below), for each
\varepsilon>0
there exists a ball
B_{\delta}(x_{n})
such that for all
y\in B_{\delta}(x_{n})

(4.14)

\displaystyle|(\boldsymbol{M}(y)-\lambda_{n})\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\lambda_{n}}|\leqslant\varepsilon|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\lambda_{n}}|.

We take
n
sufficiently large and apply the triangle inequality to obtain

(4.15)

\displaystyle|(\boldsymbol{M}(y)-\lambda)\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\lambda_{n}}|\leqslant|(\boldsymbol{M}(y)-\lambda_{n})\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\lambda_{n}}|+|(\lambda_{n}-\lambda)\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\lambda_{n}}|\leqslant 2\varepsilon|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\lambda_{n}}|.

Thus, by Lemma
4.3
, we have
\lambda\in\sigma(\boldsymbol{M}(\cdot))
.

Conversely, assume that
\lambda\notin\overline{\bigcup_{x\in\Omega\setminus\Omega^{\prime}}\sigma\big(\boldsymbol{M}(x)\big)}
. Thus, for each
x\in\Omega\setminus\Omega^{\prime}
, the matrix
\big(\boldsymbol{M}(x)-\lambda\boldsymbol{I}\big)
is invertible and there exists
\varepsilon_{x}>0
such that for all vectors
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have

(4.16)

\displaystyle\big|(\boldsymbol{M}(x)-\lambda\boldsymbol{I})\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\varepsilon}\big|\geqslant\varepsilon_{x}|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\varepsilon}|.

We show that there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
\varepsilon_{x}\geqslant\varepsilon>0
uniformly for all
x\in\Omega\setminus\Omega^{\prime}
. Indeed, suppose
a contrario
that there exists a sequence
x_{n}\in\Omega\setminus\Omega^{\prime}
satisfying
\varepsilon_{x_{n}}\to 0
. We take the sequence of vectors
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\varepsilon_{x_{n}}}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with
|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\varepsilon_{x_{n}}}|=1
satisfying

(4.17)

\displaystyle\big|(\boldsymbol{M}(x_{n})-\lambda\boldsymbol{I})\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\varepsilon_{x_{n}}}\big|=\varepsilon_{x_{n}}\to 0.

Letting
n\to\infty
we have that
\lambda
is arbitrary close to an eigenvalue of matrix
\boldsymbol{M}(x_{n})
, hence
\lambda\in\overline{\bigcup_{x\in\Omega\setminus\Omega^{\prime}}\sigma\big(\boldsymbol{M}(x)\big)}
. Therefore, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that for all
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have

(4.18)

\displaystyle\big|(\boldsymbol{M}(x)-\lambda\boldsymbol{I})\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\varepsilon}\big|\geqslant\varepsilon|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\varepsilon}|,\quad\text{for all}\quad x\in\Omega\setminus\Omega^{\prime}.

Lemma
4.3
implies again that
\lambda\notin\sigma(\boldsymbol{M}(\cdot))
.
∎

4.3.
Spectrum of the reaction-diffusion-ODE operator

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
\big(\mathcal{L}_{p},D(\mathcal{L}_{p})\big)
, with
p\in(1,\infty)
, be the operator given by formula (
4.4
) with matrices (
4.2
)-(
4.3
).
Denote by
\sigma\big(\boldsymbol{A}(\cdot)\big)
be the spectrum of multiplication operator
\boldsymbol{A}(\cdot)
on
L^{p}(\Omega)^{n}
. Then, the spectrum of
\big(\mathcal{L}_{p},D(\mathcal{L}_{p})\big)
can be characterized as

(4.19)

\displaystyle\sigma(\mathcal{L}_{p})=\sigma\big(\boldsymbol{A}(\cdot)\big)\cup\{\text{eigenvalues of }\;\mathcal{L}_{p}\}.

In particular, the spectrum
\sigma(\mathcal{L}_{p})
is independent of
p
.

Proof.

We improve the reasoning form
[
21
, Thm 4.5]
, where one ODE coupled with one PDE were considered.
First, we prove that
\sigma\big(\boldsymbol{A}(\cdot)\big)\subset\sigma(\mathcal{L}_{p})
by showing that for each
\lambda\in\sigma\big(\boldsymbol{A}(\cdot)\big)
the operator

(4.20)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{p}-\lambda\boldsymbol{I}:L^{p}(\Omega)^{n}\times{W^{2,p}_{\nu}(\Omega)}\rightarrow L^{p}(\Omega)^{n}\times L^{p}(\Omega)

defined by formula

(4.21)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}_{p}-\lambda\boldsymbol{I})\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\varphi}\\
\psi\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}(\boldsymbol{A}-\lambda)\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\boldsymbol{B}\psi\\
\Delta_{\nu}\psi+\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{\varphi}+(d-\lambda)\psi\end{pmatrix}

cannot have a bounded inverse. Indeed, suppose
a contrario
that
(\mathcal{L}_{p}-\lambda\boldsymbol{I})^{-1}
exists and is a bounded operator from
(L^{p}(\Omega))^{n+1}
into
D(\mathcal{L}_{p})
equipped with the graph norm. The graph norm of
\mathcal{L}_{p}
is equivalent to the norm on the product space
(L^{p}(\Omega))^{n}\times({W^{2,p}_{\nu}(\Omega)})
by
[
40
, Section 2.4]
. Thus, there exists a number
K>0
such that

(4.22)

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\varphi}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}+\|\psi\|_{W^{2,p}(\Omega)}\leqslant K\big(

\displaystyle\|(\boldsymbol{A}-\lambda\boldsymbol{I})\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\boldsymbol{B}\psi\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{\nu}\psi+\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{\varphi}+(d-\lambda)\psi\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\big)

for all
(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\psi)\in L^{p}(\Omega)^{n}\times{W^{2,p}_{\nu}(\Omega)}
. Let
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary (to be chosen later on). By Lemma
4.3
there exists
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
|\boldsymbol{\xi}|=1
and a ball
B_{\varepsilon}
such that

(4.23)

\displaystyle\|(\boldsymbol{A}(\cdot)-\lambda\boldsymbol{I})\boldsymbol{\xi}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{\varepsilon})}\leqslant\varepsilon.

Let us show that, for arbitrary
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
with
\text{supp}\;\psi\subset B_{\varepsilon}
, we may find
\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in L^{p}(\Omega)^{n}
with the following properties

•

\boldsymbol{\varphi}=\rho\boldsymbol{\xi}
for some
\rho\in{L^{p}(\Omega)}
with
\text{supp}\,\rho\subset B_{\varepsilon}
,

•

\zeta=\Delta_{\nu}\psi+\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{\varphi}+(d-\lambda)\psi
satisfies
\|\zeta\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leqslant\varepsilon\|\boldsymbol{\varphi}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}
.

Indeed, we cut the function
\boldsymbol{C}
at the level
\varepsilon
in the following way

(4.24)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{C}^{\varepsilon}=\boldsymbol{C}^{\varepsilon}(x)\equiv\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{C}(x)&\text{if}\quad\big|\boldsymbol{C}(x)\boldsymbol{\xi}\big|>\varepsilon,\\
(\varepsilon,\cdots,\varepsilon)&\text{if}\quad\big|\boldsymbol{C}(x)\boldsymbol{\xi}\big|\leqslant\varepsilon.\end{cases}

Thus we obtain

(4.25)

\displaystyle\zeta=\Delta_{\nu}\psi+\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{\varphi}+(d-\lambda)\psi=\Delta_{\nu}\psi+\boldsymbol{C}^{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{\varphi}+(d-\lambda)\psi+(\boldsymbol{C}-\boldsymbol{C}^{\varepsilon})\boldsymbol{\varphi}

for

(4.26)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varphi}=\frac{-(\Delta_{\nu}\psi+(d-\lambda)\psi)}{\boldsymbol{C}^{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{\xi}}\boldsymbol{\xi}=\rho\boldsymbol{\xi}\in L^{p}(\Omega)^{n}\quad\text{and}\quad\zeta=(\boldsymbol{C}-\boldsymbol{C}^{\varepsilon})\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in L^{p}(\Omega)

with
\|(\boldsymbol{C}-\boldsymbol{C}^{\varepsilon})\boldsymbol{\xi}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leqslant n\varepsilon
.

Now, noting that supp
\boldsymbol{\varphi}\subset B_{\varepsilon}
and
\boldsymbol{\varphi}(x)=\rho(x)\boldsymbol{\xi}
, we obtain the inequality

(4.27)

\displaystyle\|(\boldsymbol{A}-\lambda\boldsymbol{I})\boldsymbol{\varphi}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leqslant\|(\boldsymbol{A}-\lambda\boldsymbol{I})\boldsymbol{\xi}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{\varepsilon})}\|\rho\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leqslant\varepsilon\|\boldsymbol{\varphi}\|_{{L^{p}(\Omega)}}.

Thus, substituting the functions
\boldsymbol{\varphi},\psi,\boldsymbol{\xi}
into inequality (
4.22
), we obtain the estimate

(4.28)

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\varphi}\|_{{L^{p}(\Omega)}}

\displaystyle+\|\psi\|_{W^{2,p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leqslant K\big(\|(\boldsymbol{A}-\lambda\boldsymbol{I})\boldsymbol{\varphi}\|_{{L^{p}(\Omega)}}+\|\boldsymbol{B}\psi\|_{{L^{p}(\Omega)}}+\|\zeta\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant K\big((n+1)\varepsilon\|\boldsymbol{\varphi}\|_{{L^{p}(\Omega)}}+\|\boldsymbol{B}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|\psi\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\big).

Hence, choosing
\varepsilon>0
small enough we compensate the term
K\varepsilon(n+1)\|\boldsymbol{\varphi}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}
by its counterpart on the left-hand side of inequality (
4.28
) and obtain estimates

(4.29)

\displaystyle\|\psi\|_{W^{2,p}(\Omega)}\leqslant\big(1-K(n+1)\varepsilon\big)\|\boldsymbol{\varphi}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}+\|\psi\|_{W^{2,p}_{\nu}(\Omega)}\leqslant K\|\boldsymbol{B}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|\psi\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

which obviously can not hold for all
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that supp
\psi\subset B_{\varepsilon}
. This completes the proof that
\sigma\big(\boldsymbol{A}(\cdot)\big)\subset\sigma(\mathcal{L}_{p})
. Moreover, by Lemma
4.4
, the spectrum
\sigma\big(\boldsymbol{A}(\cdot)\big)
is independent of
p
.

Next, we prove that the remainder of the spectrum
\big(\mathcal{L}_{p},D(\mathcal{L}_{p})\big)
consists of a discrete set of eigenvalues by analysing the corresponding resolvent equations

(4.30)

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{A}-\lambda\boldsymbol{I})\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\boldsymbol{B}\psi

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{F},\quad x\in\overline{\Omega},

\displaystyle\Delta_{\nu}\psi+\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{\varphi}+(d-\lambda)\psi

\displaystyle=G,\quad x\in\Omega

with arbitrary
(\boldsymbol{F},G)\in L^{p}(\Omega)^{n}\times L^{p}(\Omega)
. Since,
\lambda\notin\sigma(\boldsymbol{A}(\cdot))
we can solve the first equation with respect to
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
and substitute into the second equation to obtain the boundary value problem

(4.31)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\nu}\psi+q(\lambda)\psi=r(\lambda)

where

(4.32)

\displaystyle q(\lambda)

\displaystyle=q(x,\lambda)=-\boldsymbol{C}(x)\big(\boldsymbol{A}(x)-\lambda\boldsymbol{I}\big)^{-1}\boldsymbol{B}(x)+d(x)-\lambda,

\displaystyle r(\lambda)

\displaystyle=r(x,\lambda)=-\boldsymbol{C}(x)\big(\boldsymbol{A}(x)-\lambda\boldsymbol{I}\big)^{-1}\boldsymbol{F}(x)+G(x).

By the Analytic Fredholm Theorem for Banach spaces from
[
35
]
, used in the same way as in
[
21
, proof of Thm 4.5]
we conclude that
the boundary value problem (
4.31
)
has no unique solution only for a countable set of
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
.
Eigenfunctions corresponding to those eigenvalues satisfy elliptic equation (
4.31
), hence, by standard elliptic regularity, they belong to
{W^{2,p}_{\nu}(\Omega)}
for each
p\in(1,\infty)
. This means that the set of eigenvalues of
\mathcal{L}_{p}
is independent of
p
.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

By Theorem
4.6
, the spectrum of the operator
\big(\mathcal{L}_{\infty},D(\mathcal{L}_{\infty})\big)
given by formula (
4.7
) with the matrix coefficients

(4.33)

\displaystyle a_{ij},\;b_{i},\;c_{i},\;d\in C(\overline{\Omega}),\quad\text{for}\;i,j\in\{1,\cdots,n\}

satisfies

(4.34)

\displaystyle\sigma\left(\mathcal{L}_{p}\right)=\sigma\big(\boldsymbol{A}(\cdot)\big)\cup\{\text{eigenvalues of }\;\mathcal{L}_{p}\}\subset\sigma(\mathcal{L}_{\infty}).

Indeed, by the elliptic regularity, eigenfunctions of
-\Delta_{\nu}
belong to
{W^{2,p}_{\nu}(\Omega)}
for each
p\in(1,\infty)
, thus they are continuous up to the boundary.
Using analogous estimates as those in the proof of Theorem
4.6
with
L^{p}
-norm replaced by the
L^{\infty}
-norm on
C(\overline{\Omega})
, we obtain that

\sigma\big(\boldsymbol{A}(\cdot)\big)\subset\sigma(\mathcal{L}_{\infty}).

5.
Instability of regular steady states

We are in a position to prove the main results of this work.

Proof of Theorem
2.7
.

Let
(\boldsymbol{U},V)
be a regular stationary solution (either constant or non-constant). We consider the operator
\big(\mathcal{L}_{p},D(\mathcal{L}_{p})\big)
given by formula (
4.4
) and the matrices

(5.1)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}(x)

\displaystyle=D_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{f}({\boldsymbol{U}(x)},{V(x)}),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{B}(x)

\displaystyle=D_{v}\boldsymbol{f}({\boldsymbol{U}(x)},{V(x)}),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{C}(x)

\displaystyle=D_{\boldsymbol{u}}g({\boldsymbol{U}(x)},{V(x)}),

\displaystyle d(x)

\displaystyle=D_{v}g({\boldsymbol{U}(x)},{V(x)}).

Under the assumption
s\big(D_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{f}({\boldsymbol{U}(x)},{V(x)})\big)>0
we obtain that
s(\mathcal{L}_{p})>0
by Theorem
4.6
. Since
(\boldsymbol{U},V)
is a regular solution, the matrix coefficients belong to the space
C(\overline{\Omega})
(see Remark
2.3
). Consequently, Remark
4.7
provides
s(\mathcal{L}_{\infty})>0
which implies that
(\boldsymbol{U},V)
is linearly unstable in
C(\overline{\Omega})
. The nonlinear instability in the Lyapunov sense of this solution is an immediate consequence of Theorem
A.2
from Appendix.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.8
.

Now, we consider non-constant regular solution
\big(\boldsymbol{U}(x),V(x)\big)
such that
s\big(f_{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{U}(\cdot),\,V(\cdot))\big)\leqslant 0
and
\det\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\big(\boldsymbol{U}(x),\,V(x)\big)\neq 0
for all
x\in\overline{\Omega}
. Moreover, we assume that
\Omega
is convex. Here, we are inspired by methods from the papers
[
1
,
23
]
where instability of non-constant solutions to one reaction-diffusion equation was shown. We consider the operator
\big(\mathcal{L}_{2},D(\mathcal{L}_{2})\big)
given by formula (
4.4
) with the matrices (
5.1
) and we study the eigenvalue problem

(5.2)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\boldsymbol{B}\psi

\displaystyle=\lambda\boldsymbol{\varphi},

\displaystyle\Delta_{\nu}\psi+\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{\varphi}+d\psi

\displaystyle=\lambda\psi.

It follows from assumptions
(
2.19
) and (
2.20
)
combined with Lemma
4.4
that

\det\big(\boldsymbol{A}(x)-\lambda\boldsymbol{I}\big)\neq 0

for all
\lambda\geqslant 0
and all
x\in\overline{\Omega}
,
hence,
the matrix
(\boldsymbol{A}(x)-\lambda\boldsymbol{I})
is invertible. Thus, we substitute
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
computed from the first equation in system (
5.2
) into the second equation which takes the following form

(5.3)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\nu}\psi+\Big(-\boldsymbol{C}(\boldsymbol{A}-\lambda\boldsymbol{I})^{-1}\boldsymbol{B}+d\Big)\psi=\lambda\psi.

For all
\lambda\geqslant 0
we define the family of the operators

(5.4)

\displaystyle G(\lambda)

\displaystyle:\psi\mapsto\Delta_{\nu}\psi+\Big(-\boldsymbol{C}(\boldsymbol{A}-\lambda\boldsymbol{I})^{-1}\boldsymbol{B}+d\Big)\psi

with the domain
D\big(G(\lambda)\big)={W^{2,2}_{\nu}(\Omega)}
. We study their eigenvalues, namely, the numbers
\eta\in\mathbb{C}
for which the equation

(5.5)

\displaystyle G(\lambda)\psi=\eta\psi

has a nonzero solution and we are going to show that
\eta=\lambda
for some
\lambda>0
. This will give a positive eigenvalue of system (
5.2
) because, under our assumption, system (
5.2
) is equivalent to the equation
G(\lambda)\psi=\lambda\psi
.

First we show that

(5.6)

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\overline{\Omega}\\
\lambda\geqslant 0\end{subarray}}\left|-\boldsymbol{C}(x)(\boldsymbol{A}(x)-\lambda\boldsymbol{I})^{-1}\boldsymbol{B}(x)+d(x)\right|<\infty.

Let
\lambda\geqslant 0
. By assumption (
2.20
), the continuity of the determinant, and regularity of solutions, there exists
\overline{\varepsilon}>0
such that every
\rho\in\sigma\big(\boldsymbol{A}(\cdot)-\lambda\boldsymbol{I}\big)
satisfies
|\rho|\geqslant\overline{\varepsilon}
. Consequently,
|\det(\boldsymbol{A}(x)-\lambda\boldsymbol{I})|\geqslant\left(\max\{\overline{\varepsilon},\lambda\}\right)^{n}
for every
x\in\overline{\Omega}
and hence

(5.7)

\displaystyle\|(\boldsymbol{A}(x)-\lambda\boldsymbol{I})^{-1}\|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\det(\boldsymbol{A}(x)-\lambda\boldsymbol{I})|}\left\|{\text{adj}(\boldsymbol{A}(x)-\lambda\boldsymbol{I})}\right\|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{{\|\text{adj}(\boldsymbol{A}(\cdot)-\lambda\boldsymbol{I})\|_{\infty}}}{\left(\max\{\overline{\varepsilon},\lambda\}\right)^{n}}\leqslant\frac{C(\lambda^{n}+1)}{\left(\max\{\overline{\varepsilon},\lambda\}\right)^{n}}\leqslant C.

Now, denote by
\eta_{0}(\lambda)
the largest eigenvalue of problem
G(\lambda)\psi=\eta_{0}(\lambda)\psi.

The operator
\big(G,D(G)\big)
is self-adjoint, hence, the eigenvalue
\eta_{0}(\lambda)
is given by Rayleigh quotient

(5.8)

\displaystyle\eta_{0}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\psi\in{W^{1,2}(\Omega)}\\
\|\psi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1\end{subarray}}\big\langle G(\lambda)\psi,\psi\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\psi\in{W^{1,2}(\Omega)}\\
\|\psi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1\end{subarray}}\left(-\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\psi|^{2}\,\text{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\Big(-\boldsymbol{C}(\boldsymbol{A}-\lambda\boldsymbol{I})^{-1}\boldsymbol{B}+d\Big)\psi^{2}\,\text{d}x\right).

By inequality (
5.6
), we immediately obtain that
\sup_{\lambda\geqslant 0}\eta_{0}(\lambda)<\infty
. In the next step of this proof, we show that
\eta_{0}(0)>0
.

In the one dimensional case
\Omega=(a,b)\subset\mathbb{R}
, differentiating equations (
2.1
) we obtain

(5.9)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{U}_{x}+\boldsymbol{B}V_{x}=0\quad\text{and}\quad(V_{x})_{xx}+\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{U}_{x}+dV_{x}=0

which yields

(5.10)

\displaystyle(V_{x})_{xx}+(-\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{B}+d)V_{x}=0.

Choosing
\psi=V_{x}/\|V_{x}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
in the functional in the right hand side of equation (
5.8
), integrating by parts, and using relations (
5.10
) we obtain the inequality

(5.11)

\displaystyle\eta_{0}(0)\geqslant\frac{1}{\|V_{x}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}\int_{\Omega}-V_{xx}V_{xx}+(-\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{B}+d)V_{x}^{2}\,\text{d}x=0.

The hypothesis
\eta_{0}(0)=0
implies that
V_{x}
is a maximizer of the functional in the right hand side of equation (
5.8
) with
\lambda=0
, hence,
V_{x}
satisfies equation (
5.10
) with Neumann boundary conditions, namely,
V_{xx}(x)=0
for
x\in\{a,b\}=\partial\Omega
. This ordinary differential equation for
V_{x}
supplemented with the boundary conditions

(5.12)

\displaystyle V_{x}(x)=0\quad\text{and}\quad V_{xx}(x)=0\quad\text{for}\quad x\in\{a,b\}=\partial\Omega

has the unique solution
V_{x}\equiv 0
. Hence,
V
(and so
U
) is a constant function which is a contradiction. Thus,
\eta_{0}(0)>0
.

The proof in higher dimensions is analogous. Differentiating equations (
2.1
) with respect to
x_{j}
for
j\in\{1,...,n\}
, we obtain

(5.13)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{U}_{x_{j}}+\boldsymbol{B}V_{x_{j}}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\Delta V_{x_{j}}+\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{U}_{x_{j}}+dV_{x_{j}}=0.

Thus, by direct calculations, analogous to those in (
5.11
) and by the divergence theorem, we obtain

(5.14)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\langle G(0)V_{x_{j}},V_{x_{j}}\rangle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{\nu}|\nabla V|^{2}\,d\sigma.

Now, we use the use the inequality
\partial_{\nu}|\nabla V|^{2}\leqslant 0
on
\partial\Omega
which is valid for every
V\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
and for every convex
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Detailed calculations leading to this inequality can be found either in
[
1
, p. 269]
or in
[
23
, Lemma 5.3]
. Thus, at least one term on the left hand side of equality (
5.14
), is nonnegative, which implies
that
\eta_{0}(0)\geqslant 0
in the variational formula (
5.8
). If
\eta_{0}(0)=0
, by a variational argument analogously as in the one dimensional case, equations
(
5.13
) have to be satisfied by the function
V_{x_{j_{0}}}
together with the Neumann boundary conditions for
V_{x_{j_{0}}}
. Applying the Hopf maximum principle (see e.g.
[
28
, Theorems 5 and 7 in Sec. 3]
), we obtain immediately that
V_{x_{j_{0}}}\equiv 0
which is a contradiction because we deal with the non-constant solution
V
. Hence, we have got
\eta_{0}(0)>0
.

Therefore, since
\eta_{0}(\lambda)
is a continuous and bounded function on
[0,\infty)
satisfying
\eta_{0}(0)>0
, there exists
\overline{\lambda}>0
such that
\eta_{0}(\overline{\lambda})=\overline{\lambda}
. The number
\overline{\lambda}
is a positive eigenvalue of system (
5.2
) and hence
s(\mathcal{L}_{2})>0
. Since
(\boldsymbol{U},V)
is a regular solution, we conclude by Remark
4.7
that the number
\overline{\lambda}
is also an eigenvalue of
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
. Since
s(\mathcal{L}_{\infty})>0
, the nonlinear instability follows immediately from Theorem
A.2
in Appendix.
∎

Appendix A
Linearisation principle

In order to show that a linear instability of regular stationary solutions of problem (
1.1
)-(
1.5
) implies their nonlinear instability in the Lyapunov sense, we recall the reasoning from
[
21
, Thm 2.1]
. That approach concerns general evolution equation

(A.1)

w_{t}=\mathcal{L}w+\mathcal{N}(w),\qquad w(0)=w_{0},

where
\mathcal{L}
is the generator of a
C_{0}
-semigroup of
linear operators
\{e^{t\mathcal{L}}\}_{t\geq 0}
on a Banach space
X

and
\mathcal{N}
is a nonlinear operator such that
{\mathcal{N}(w)}=o\left(\|{w}\|\right)
as
w\to 0
. Here, we recall one of the possible instability results, where the existence of a positive part of the spectrum of the linear operator
\mathcal{L}
is sufficient to show that the zero solution of equation (
A.1
)
is unstable.

Theorem A.1

(
[
34
, Thm 1]
)
.

Consider an abstract problem (
A.1
), where

(1)

the linear operator
\mathcal{L}
generates a strongly continuous semigroup of linear operators on a Banach space
X
,

(2)

the intersection of the spectrum of
\mathcal{L}
with the right half-plane
\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:\;{\rm Re\,}\lambda>0\}
is nonempty.

(3)

\mathcal{N}:X\to X
is continuous and there exist constants
\rho>0
,
\eta>0
, and
C>0

such that
\|\mathcal{N}(w)\|_{X}\leq C\|w\|_{X}^{1+\eta}
for all
\|w\|_{X}<\rho
.

Then the zero solution of this equation is (nonlinearly) unstable.

Theorem A.2
.

Let
(\boldsymbol{U},V)
be a regular stationary solution of problem (
1.1
)-(
1.5
). Assume that the corresponding linearisation operator
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
, defined in Proposition
4.2
and with matrices (
5.1
), satisfies
s(\mathcal{L}_{\infty})>0
. Then
(\boldsymbol{U},V)
is nonlinearly unstable in the space
C(\overline{\Omega})^{n+1}
.

Proof.

Applying the usual linearisation procedure we obtain that the perturbation of a stationary solution
(\boldsymbol{U},V)
satisfies the semi-linear problem

(A.2)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\varphi}\\
\psi\end{pmatrix}=\mathcal{L}_{\infty}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\varphi}\\
\psi\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{R}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\psi)\\
R_{2}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\psi)\end{pmatrix}

with the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
given by formula (
4.4
) with matrices (
5.1
) and where
the remainders
\boldsymbol{R}_{1}
and
R_{2}
are obtained from nonlinearities
\boldsymbol{f},\,g
via the Taylor expansion. We apply Theorem
A.1
with the Banach space
X=C(\overline{\Omega})^{n+1}
. The remainders
\boldsymbol{R}_{1},\,R_{2}
in system (
A.2
) satisfy the condition (
3
) of Theorem
A.1
with
\eta=1
.
∎
