Budget Constrained Interactive Search for Multiple Targets
Xuliang Zhu 1 , Xin Huang 1 , Byron Choi 1 , Jiaxin Jiang 1 , Zhaonian Zou 2 , Jianliang Xu 1

Abstract
. Interactive graph search leverages human intelligence to categorize target labels in a hierarchy, which is useful for image classification, product categorization, and database search. However, many existing interactive graph search studies aim at identifying a single target optimally, and suffer from the limitations of asking too many questions and not being able to handle multiple targets. To address these two limitations, in this paper, we study a new problem of b udget constrained i nteractive g raph s earch for m ultiple targets called 𝗄𝖡𝖬 \mathsf{kBM} - 𝖨𝖦𝖲 \mathsf{IGS} 𝗉𝗋𝗈𝖻𝗅𝖾𝗆 \mathsf{problem} . Specifically, given a set of multiple targets 𝒯 \mathcal{T} in a hierarchy and two parameters k k and b b , the goal is to identify a k k -sized set of selections 𝒮 \mathcal{S} , such that the closeness between selections 𝒮 \mathcal{S} and targets 𝒯 \mathcal{T} is as small as possible, by asking at most a budget of b b questions. We theoretically analyze the updating rules and design a penalty function to capture the closeness between selections and targets. To tackle the 𝗄𝖡𝖬 \mathsf{kBM} - 𝖨𝖦𝖲 \mathsf{IGS} 𝗉𝗋𝗈𝖻𝗅𝖾𝗆 \mathsf{problem} , we develop a novel framework to ask questions using the best vertex with the largest expected gain, which provides a balanced trade-off between target probability and benefit gain. Based on the 𝗄𝖡𝖬 \mathsf{kBM} - 𝖨𝖦𝖲 \mathsf{IGS} framework, we first propose an efficient algorithm 𝖲𝖳𝖡𝖨𝖲 \mathsf{STBIS} to handle the 𝖲𝗂𝗇𝗀𝗅𝖾 \mathsf{Single} 𝖳𝖺𝗋𝗀𝖾𝗍 \mathsf{Target} problem, which is a special case of 𝗄𝖡𝖬 \mathsf{kBM} - 𝖨𝖦𝖲 \mathsf{IGS} . Then, we propose a dynamic programming based method 𝗄𝖡𝖬 \mathsf{kBM} - 𝖣𝖯 \mathsf{DP} to tackle the 𝖬𝗎𝗅𝗍𝗂𝗉𝗅𝖾 \mathsf{Multiple} 𝖳𝖺𝗋𝗀𝖾𝗍𝗌 \mathsf{Targets} problem. To further improve efficiency, we propose two heuristic but efficient algorithms, 𝗄𝖡𝖬 \mathsf{kBM} - 𝖳𝗈𝗉𝗄 \mathsf{Topk} and 𝗄𝖡𝖬 \mathsf{kBM} - 𝖣𝖯 \mathsf{DP} +. 𝗄𝖡𝖬 \mathsf{kBM} - 𝖳𝗈𝗉𝗄 \mathsf{Topk} develops a variant gain function and selects the top- k k vertices independently. 𝗄𝖡𝖬 \mathsf{kBM} - 𝖣𝖯 \mathsf{DP} + uses an upper bound of gains and prunes disqualified vertices to save computations. Experiments on large real-world datasets with ground-truth targets verify both the effectiveness and efficiency of our proposed algorithms.

Recently, Tao et al.

\mathsf{IGS}
) to locate

in a hierarchy

, with as few questions as possible. For example, Figure

A directed edge from one vertex to another represents the concept-instance relationship, e.g., “pet” is a general concept of four instances “cat”, “dog”, “rabbit”, and “turtle”. Note that the target is unknown in advance. To identify the target, interaction is allowed to iteratively ask questions using the vertices in the hierarchy, e.g., “Is this a wild?”, “Is this a pet?”. Assuming that the target is “pet” in Figure

Effective algorithms with theoretical guarantee are proposed for finding the exact target using at most
\lceil\log_{2}{h}\rceil(1+\lfloor\log_{2}{n}\rfloor)+(d-1)\cdot\lceil\log_{d}{n}\rceil
questions, where
n
,
d
,
h
are respectively the number of vertices, the maximum out-degree, and the hierarchy height in
\mathcal{H}
. However, two issues remain open:

\bullet

Finding nearly-optimal targets using a constrained budget
.
IGS
(
tao2019interactive
)
may incur a high cost to identify the exact target.
It does not limit the number of questions that can be asked.
In the worst case, the proposed algorithm asks
(d-1)\cdot\lfloor\log_{d}{n}\rfloor
questions to optimally identify the target. In real hierarchy datasets, the out-degree
d
could be large, e.g., ImageNet
(
deng2009imagenet
)
has
d=391
and
n=74,401
. Thus, users may need to answer
782
questions, which is not very practical. Even worse, asking questions is potentially costly
(
parameswaran2011human
)
, which motivates the problem of budget constrained
\mathsf{IGS}
to bound the total cost.

\bullet

Identifying multiple targets
. Existing studies on the
\mathsf{IGS}
problem
(
tao2019interactive
;
li2020efficient
)
only consider a single target, where the answer has one and only one target. However, in real applications of object categorization, an object may have multiple labels. Even worse, it is difficult to determine in advance how many labels the object may have. For example, Figure
1(b)
shows an uncategorized image object. Both “cat” and “fish” are suitable to label the object, but either one alone is not good enough.

To address the above issues, in this paper, we propose a new
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{IGS}
problem of interactive graph search for identifying multiple targets
\mathcal{T}
using a constrained budget to ask at most
b
questions. Specifically, in each round, our
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{IGS}
scheme asks a question in the form “Given a query vertex
q
in tree
\mathcal{H}
, can vertex
q
reach one of targets in
\mathcal{T}
?” and receives the answer from human-assisted interactions. On the basis of the previous answers, the
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{IGS}
scheme determines the next question to ask.
Finally, it selects a set of vertices to represent the targets
after
b
questions are answered.

However, it is significantly challenging to identify the most suitable selections in the
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{IGS}
problem, for the following reasons. First, the number of targets is unknown in advance. Given an uncategorized object, it may have one or more ground-truth labels. Second, in the worst case, a total of
O(n)
questions is needed to find the exact answers regarding targets, which makes the selection of
b
questions difficult. Third, since the targets are unknown, another challenge is how to measure the goodness of a solution, i.e., the closeness between selections and targets.

In light of the above, the objective of our problem is formulated as finding a
k
-sized selection set of vertices to approach the targets as close as possible, w.r.t. an input number of
k
and a budget of
b
questions.
For example, consider the hierarchy and the uncategorized image object in Figure
1
. Assume that
k=2
and
b=2
. We ask two questions of “Is this
x
?’’

To tackle the
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{IGS}
problem, we propose a novel
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{IGS}
framework, which uses a greedy strategy to ask the best question with the largest expected gain at each round. Specifically, vertices have different probabilities to be targets and may get
\mathsf{Yes}
/
\mathsf{No}
answers for questions asked. In general, a vertex at the top level of the hierarchy has a high probability of getting a
{\mathsf{Yes}}
answer. However, the benefit of getting a
{\mathsf{Yes}}
answer can be less than a
\mathsf{No}
answer, which implies that none of descendants are targets. Therefore, we propose an expected gain to trade-off the target probability and benefit gain.
Thus, the
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{IGS}
framework can find the vertex with the largest expected gain to ask the next question.
On the basis of the
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{IGS}
framework, we first propose an efficient algorithm STIGS to solve the
\mathsf{SingleTarget}

\mathsf{problem}
, which is a special case of
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{IGS}
with
|\mathcal{T}|=1
.
It can update the gains of all vertices in
O(n)
for each question. Different from the
\mathsf{SingleTarget}

\mathsf{problem}
, it is difficult to calculate the gains in the
\mathsf{Multiple}

\mathsf{Targets}

\mathsf{problem}
.
We then develop a
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{DP}
method to calculate the optimal penalty between the
k
-sized selections and potential targets. The algorithm takes
O(bnh^{2}dk^{2})
time. To further improve efficiency, we propose two heuristic but efficient algorithms,
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{Topk}
and
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{DP}
+. The first method,
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{Topk}
, uses an independent penalty function, which avoids costly enumerations to find the optimal result. The second method,
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{DP}
+, improves
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{DP}
by invoking an upper bound to quickly identify the best vertex to ask the next question before all expected gains are updated.
To summarize, we make the following contributions:

\bullet

We propose a new
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{IGS}

\mathsf{problem}
of budget constrained interactive graph search for identifying multiple targets in a hierarchical tree. We raise the problem of finding the
k
-sized selections close to multiple targets using a fixed number of
b
questions, and formally design a penalty function to measure the closeness between selections and targets (Section
3
).

\bullet

We give theoretical analysis of potential targets and
\mathsf{Yes}
-
\mathsf{candidate}
, which offers useful updating rules to prune disqualified candidates. Moreover, for a specific question, we discuss the probabilities of
\mathsf{Yes}
and
\mathsf{No}
answers, and analyze the expected gain of asking this question. On the basis of the updating rules and expected gains, we propose a novel
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{IGS}
framework to tackle the
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{IGS}

\mathsf{problem}
by asking
b
good questions (Section
4
).

\bullet

We investigate and tackle one instance of
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{IGS}

\mathsf{problem}
, the
\mathsf{SingleTarget}

\mathsf{problem}
, where the target involves a single answer. On the basis of the
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{IGS}
framework, we derive new updating rules and propose a greedy algorithm
\mathsf{STBIS}
. We further develop a DFS technique to accelerate
\mathsf{STBIS}
(Section
5
).

\bullet

We propose three efficient algorithms for identifying multiple targets based on the
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{IGS}
framework. First, we propose a dynamic programming based technique
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{DP}
, which computes the gain scores for all vertices optimally. To further improve efficiency, we propose two fast algorithms,
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{Topk}
and
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{DP}
+, based on an alternative function of independent gain score and a pruning upper bound for lazy gain computation, respectively (Section
6
).

\bullet

We conduct extensive experiments on real-world datasets with
ground-truth multiple targets
to validate the efficiency and effectiveness of our proposed framework and algorithms (Section
7

n=|V|
and the height of
\mathcal{H}
be
h
.
For a vertex
v\in V
, we denote its children of
v
by
{\mathsf{child}}(v)=\{u:\langle v,u\rangle\in E\}
and its unique parent by
{\mathsf{par}}(v)
where
\langle{\mathsf{par}}(v),v\rangle\in E
.
A vertex
v
with no children is called leaf, i.e.,
{\mathsf{child}}(v)=\emptyset
.

Given two vertices
u
and
v
, we say that
u
can reach
v
(denoted as
u\rightarrow v
), if and only if there exists a directed path from
u
to
v
in
\mathcal{H}
. If
u
cannot reach
v
, we use
u\nrightarrow v
to represent it. Note that
v\rightarrow v
and
r\rightarrow v
for any vertex
v\in V
.
Moreover, the distance from
u
to
v
is denoted by
{\mathsf{dist}}\langle u,v\rangle
, as the length of the shortest path from
u
to
v
in
\mathcal{H}
. If
u\rightarrow v
, the distance
{\mathsf{dist}}\langle u,v\rangle
is the height difference between two vertices
u
and
v
in
\mathcal{H}
. Note that
{\mathsf{dist}}\langle v,v\rangle=0
and
{\mathsf{dist}}\langle u,v\rangle

=+\infty
if
u\nrightarrow v
.
In addition, the ancestors of a vertex
v
, denoted by
{\mathsf{anc}}(v)
, are defined to be the set of vertices that can reach
v
in
\mathcal{H}
, i.e.,
{\mathsf{anc}}(v)=

\{u\in V:u\rightarrow v\}
.

v
, denoted by
{\mathsf{des}}(v)
, are defined to be the set of vertices that are reachable from
v
in
G
, i.e.,

3.2.
kBM-IGS Interactive Scheme

In the following, we introduce the scheme of budget-based interactive graph search for identifying multiple targets. In contrast to IGS
(
tao2019interactive
)
and BinG
(
li2020efficient
)
, our
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{IGS}
has new rules and features for asking a limited number of questions.
The interactive scheme
has four components:
targets
,
questions
,
\mathsf{Yes}
-
\mathsf{candidates}

, and
selections
.

Targets.
The targets are a set of vertices in tree
\mathcal{H}
, denoted as
\mathcal{T}\subseteq V
. The goal of our
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{IGS}
is finding the target set
\mathcal{T}
, which needs to be identified through a few rounds of question asking. The targets
\mathcal{T}
can be chosen arbitrarily from
V
. In other words,
\mathcal{T}
may be any hidden vertices distributed over the whole tree
\mathcal{H}
.

The target set
\mathcal{T}
has two characteristics:
variant cardinality
and
target independence
. First, in terms of target cardinality,
we categorize
\mathcal{T}
into two types,
\mathsf{SingleTarget}
and
\mathsf{MultipleTargets}
, following
(
parameswaran2011human
)
.
If the size of
\mathcal{T}
is known and
|\mathcal{T}|=1
, we call it
\mathsf{SingleTarget}
.
If the size of
\mathcal{T}
is unknown and variant with
|\mathcal{T}|\geq 1
, we call it
\mathsf{MultipleTargets}
, which does not constrain the size of
\mathcal{T}
.
On the other hand,
the target set
\mathcal{T}
must satisfy the property of target independence, that is, any two vertices in
\mathcal{T}
are not related
(
parameswaran2011human
)
:

\forall v,u\in\mathcal{T},\text{ if }v\neq u\text{, then }v\nrightarrow u.

Consider the example shown in Figure
1(a)
, where we assume the target set is
\mathcal{T}=
{“cat”, “fish”}. Although “pet” is also a correct terminology to represent the object in Figure
1(b)
, it is not suitable to be added into
\mathcal{T}
, as it would violate the property of target independence as “pet” reaches “cat” in Figure
1(a)
. Actually, “cat” is a more precise label to describe the object than “pet” in this example.

Questions.
To identify targets, one can ask a search question in the form “Given a query vertex
q
in tree
\mathcal{H}
, can vertex
q
reach one of targets in

3.3.
Penalty between Selections and Targets

Given a budget of questions that can be asked and an unknown number of targets, it is challenging to determine the locations of targets in a large tree
\mathcal{H}
. Instead of giving a simple boolean result, we develop an evaluation metric to quantify the goodness of our selections. In the following, we introduce another important feature of
penalty
in
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{IGS}
. The penalty is an evaluation metric defined on the basis of distance, which measures the closeness between
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
.

Pair-wise Penalty
. Assume that we use a vertex
v\in V
to cover a given target
t\in\mathcal{T}
. If
v=t
, the choice
v
exactly identifies the target
t
. If
v\neq t
, it needs to give a penalty score for using
v
to cover the target
t
. On the basis of the distance, we give the definition of pair-wise penalty score as follows.

(1)

{\mathsf{f}}\langle v,t\rangle=\left\{\begin{aligned} {\mathsf{dist}}\langle v,t\rangle,\ \text{if}~v\in{\mathsf{anc}}(t)\\
{\mathsf{dist}}\langle r,t\rangle,\ \text{if}~v\notin{\mathsf{anc}}(t)\\
\end{aligned}\right.\vskip-2.84544pt

By the above definitions, we consider two cases: 1)
v\in{\mathsf{anc}}(t)
and 2)
v\notin{\mathsf{anc}}(t)
. First, for
v\in{\mathsf{anc}}(t)
, indicating
v\rightarrow t
, the best selection
v
should have
{\mathsf{dist}}\langle v,t\rangle=0
. The further the distance
{\mathsf{dist}}\langle v,t\rangle
, the larger the penalty. Second, for
v\notin{\mathsf{anc}}(t)
, indicating
v\nrightarrow t
, we give a full penalty of the largest distance between
r
and
t
, for using
v
to cover
t
, i.e.,
{\mathsf{f}}\langle v,t\rangle={\mathsf{dist}}\langle r,t\rangle
. The deeper the location of target
t
, the larger the penalty. As a result, if a target can be reached by our selections, we use the shortest distance to indicate its closeness. Otherwise, if a target is not reachable from our selections, we give a distance-based penalty.

Set-wise Penalty
. On the basis of the pair-wise penalty, we give the definitions of set-wise penalty distance below.

Definition 0 (Penalty).

Given a set of targets
\mathcal{T}
and a set of selections
\mathcal{S}
, the penalty of
\mathcal{S}
covering a target
t\in\mathcal{T}
is defined as the minimum penalty of using a vertex
v\in\mathcal{S}
to cover
t
, denoted as

(2)

{\mathsf{f}}(\mathcal{S},t)=\min_{v\in\mathcal{S}}{\mathsf{f}}\langle v,t\rangle=\min_{v\in\mathcal{S}\cup\{r\}}{\mathsf{dist}}\langle v,t\rangle.

Moreover, the penalty of
\mathcal{S}
covering targets
\mathcal{T}
is defined as the total penalty sum of
\mathcal{S}
covering all targets
t\in\mathcal{T}
, denoted by

(3)

{\mathsf{f}}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T})=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}{\mathsf{f}}(\mathcal{S},t)=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}{\min_{v\in\mathcal{S}\cup\{r\}}{\mathsf{dist}}\langle v,t\rangle}.

Obviously, if
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{T}
, the penalty is
{\mathsf{f}}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T})=0
. The smaller the penalty, the better the selections
\mathcal{S}
. In Figure
3
, assume that
\mathcal{S}=\{v_{2},v_{3}\}
and
\mathcal{T}=\{v_{2},v_{5},v_{8}\}
, thus
{\mathsf{f}}\langle v_{2},v_{8}\rangle=3
and
{\mathsf{f}}\langle v_{3},v_{8}\rangle=1
. The set-wise penalty
{\mathsf{f}}(\mathcal{S},v_{8})=1
,
{\mathsf{f}}(S,v_{5})=2
and
{\mathsf{f}}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T})=3
.

3.5.
Applications

We motivate the
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{IGS}
problem with three applications.

Image categorization.

New images (e.g., biomedical images, surveillance photos, and user-uploaded images in online social networks) are continuously being generated and need to be classified by humans to identify objects and labels
(
parameswaran2011human
;
tao2019interactive
)
. Our
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{IGS}
scheme can leverage the crowd-aided intelligence to identify multiple objects in an image using a budget constrained interactive graph search.
First, an image may have
multiple labels
, e.g., the image shown in Figure
1(b)
has two labels “cat” and “fish”.
Second, answering a question involves certain communication, latency, and monetary costs. Given
a limited budget for rewards
, it is necessary to constrain the total number of questions to be asked and select the most suitable labels to categorize the image.

Manual curation.

Manual curation extends an existing taxonomy (e.g., Yago, Wikipedia, and web of concepts) by adding new entities to the taxonomy
(
tao2019interactive
)
. Given a new entity, interactive graph search can be used to find the nodes to
parent
the new entity via crowdsourcing.
Different from the existing study
(
tao2019interactive
)
,
our
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{IGS}
problem allows an entity to be a child of
multiple parents
, which is common in real-world applications, e.g., “whale” is a subclass of both “mammal” and “aquatic animal”. Moreover, on the basis of economic considerations, we can use
a limited budget
to constrain the total number of questions that can be asked.

Cold-start recommendation.

Due to the lack of users’ preferences in cold-start recommendations, online platforms (e.g., Twitter, TikTok, and YouTube) can ask a few questions to identify users’ interests and then offer personalized recommendations in a more effective way. Users’ preferences may be diverse, which are usually not limited to a single interest, e.g., one may like “traveling”, “financial news”, “movies”, and so on. To avoid users becoming bored due to being asked too many questions, our
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{IGS}
scheme can ask
only a small number of questions
using an interest hierarchy and adjusts its asking strategy
dynamically based on the previous answers
.

Remark
.

In practical crowdsourcing applications, while human mistakes are inevitable, they can be minimized or eliminated by adopting effective quality control measures such as expert review, majority voting, group consensus, and so on
(
daniel2018quality
)
. As validated in
(
tao2019interactive
)
, the influence of such mistakes on the outcome of the graph search algorithms is negligible. Thus, as with the previous works
(
parameswaran2011human
;
tao2019interactive
;
li2020efficient
)
, we assume in our algorithm design that the workers always give correct answers. For those cases where human mistakes are not eliminated and the workers give wrong answers, we will assess the quality of our methods in Section
7
.

Potential targets.
We first define the potential targets, denoted by

Lemma 0.

Given a vertex

, if the question

Proof.

First, for

q
cannot reach any target

. For each vertex

,

,

. Thus,
{\mathsf{des}}(q)\cap\mathcal{T}=\emptyset
, and all vertices

can be pruned from potential targets, denoted as
{\mathsf{des}}(q)\cap\mathcal{P}=\emptyset
.
∎

Lemma 0.

Given a vertex

, if the question
{\mathsf{reach}}(q)={\mathsf{Yes}}
, all vertices

are not targets, which should be pruned from potential targets, i.e.,
{\mathsf{anc}}(q)\cap\mathcal{P}=\{q\}
.

Proof.

For
{\mathsf{reach}}(q)={\mathsf{Yes}}
,
\exists t\in\mathcal{T}
satisfies
q\rightarrow t
. On the basis of the independence property of targets, for other target

, we can get
t^{\prime}\nrightarrow t
. Thus, for the vertex
u\in{\mathsf{anc}}(q)\setminus\{q\}
,
u\rightarrow t
, so
u\notin\mathcal{T}
and all vertices
u
can be pruned from potential targets.
∎

4.2.
kBM-IGS Framework

In this section, we introduce a novel
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{IGS}
framework for identifying multiple targets via a series of
b
interactive questions. The key idea is asking
good questions
to reduce potential targets
\mathcal{P}
and refine
\mathcal{Y}
to be specified by Theorem
5
.

Motivations.
We use a toy example
\mathcal{H}
in Figure
3
to show the general ideas of our framework.
Assume that
\mathcal{P}=V
and
\mathcal{Y}=\{r\}
.
First, we consider a vertex
v_{1}
and ask the question
{\mathsf{reach}}(v_{1})
.
If
{\mathsf{reach}}(v_{1})={\mathsf{No}}
, all the descendants of
v_{1}
can be pruned from
\mathcal{P}
, which achieves a considerable gain by reducing
|\mathcal{P}|
from 10 to 2.
But, if
{\mathsf{reach}}(v_{1})={\mathsf{Yes}}
,
\mathcal{P}
will only reduce the vertex
v_{0}
, which achieves a limited gain.
Unfortunately, assuming that the targets are randomly distributed in
V
,
v_{1}
has a low probability of getting
{\mathsf{reach}}(v_{1})={\mathsf{No}}
. This is because there exist 8 descendants of
v_{1}
, and if any vertex
u\in{\mathsf{des}}(v_{1})
is the target,
{\mathsf{reach}}(v_{1})={\mathsf{Yes}}
holds.
Thus,
v_{1}
may not be a good choice for questioning.
Second, we consider a leaf vertex
v_{9}
. If
{\mathsf{reach}}(v_{9})={\mathsf{Yes}}
, we surely know that
v_{9}
is one desired target, i.e.,
v_{9}\in\mathcal{T}
, which achieves lots of gains. But, if
{\mathsf{reach}}(v_{9})={\mathsf{No}}
,
\mathcal{P}
will only reduce
v_{9}
, which achieves a limited gain. However, it has a high probability of getting
{\mathsf{reach}}(v_{9})={\mathsf{No}}
and a low probability to
{\mathsf{reach}}(v_{9})={\mathsf{Yes}}
. We need to select good vertices by making a balanced trade-off between the probability and gains.
To do so, the
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{IGS}
framework develops a ranking evaluation function for vertices, which is based on
target probability
and
gain score
.

Target probability
. Naturally, the vertices at the top levels of
\mathcal{H}
(e.g., root
r
in Figure
3
) have high probabilities of getting a
{\mathsf{Yes}}
answer and low probabilities to get a
{\mathsf{No}}
answer.

On the contrary, the vertices at the bottom levels of
\mathcal{H}
(e.g., leaf
v_{9}
in Figure

) have high probabilities of getting a
{\mathsf{No}}
answer and low probabilities to get a
{\mathsf{Yes}}
answer.

Therefore, for a vertex
v
, we denote the probabilities of
{\mathsf{reach}}(v)={\mathsf{Yes}}
and
{\mathsf{reach}}(v)={\mathsf{No}}
respectively as
{\mathsf{p_{Yes}}}(v)
and
{\mathsf{p_{No}}}(v)
, which satisfy
{\mathsf{p_{Yes}}}(v)+{\mathsf{p_{No}}}(v)=1
. The specific calculations of
{\mathsf{p_{Yes}}}(v)
and
{\mathsf{p_{No}}}(v)
are based on the descendants
{\mathsf{des}}(v)
, which will be introduced in Sections
5
and
6
.

. First, we define the potential penalty. Instead of using the targets
\mathcal{T}
as in Problem
1

\mathcal{S}
and potential targets
\mathcal{P}
as we do not know the exact
\mathcal{T}
, as follows.

Definition 0 (Potential Penalty).

Given a set of potential targets
\mathcal{P}
, a set of
\mathsf{Yes}
-
\mathsf{candidates}

\mathcal{Y}
, and a number

{\mathsf{g}}(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{P},k)=\min_{\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{Y},|\mathcal{S}|\leq k}{{\mathsf{f}}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{P})}
.

The potential penalty
{\mathsf{g}}(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{P},k)
is to select the best
k
vertices from the
\mathsf{Yes}
-
\mathsf{candidates}

\mathcal{Y}
in order to identify the potential targets in
\mathcal{P}
. The less
|\mathcal{P}|
and the closer
\mathcal{S}
to
\mathcal{P}
is, the lower score
{\mathsf{g}}(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{P},k)
is, which is better. On the basis of potential penalties, we present the definition of gain score. For a given vertex
v\in V
with existing
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Y}
, we ask a new question

The gain of
{\mathsf{reach}}(v)

={\mathsf{Yes}}
is denoted as
{\mathsf{g_{Yes}}}(v)

={\mathsf{g}}(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{P},k)-

{\mathsf{g}}(\hat{\mathcal{Y}}_{v},\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{v},k)
where

,
\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{v}
are the updated potential targets and
\mathsf{Yes}
-
\mathsf{candidates}
after asking the question
{\mathsf{reach}}(v)={\mathsf{Yes}}
; Similarly, the gain of
{\mathsf{reach}}(v)={\mathsf{No}}
is denoted as
{\mathsf{g_{No}}}(v)={\mathsf{g}}(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{P},k)-

{\mathsf{g}}(\bar{\mathcal{Y}}_{v},\bar{\mathcal{P}}_{v},k)
where
\bar{\mathcal{Y}}_{v}
,

are the updated potential targets and
\mathsf{Yes}
-
\mathsf{candidates}
after asking the question
{\mathsf{reach}}(v)={\mathsf{No}}
.

On the basis of the target probabilities and gain scores, we define an integrated function of expected gain as follows.

Definition 0 (Expected Gain).

Given a vertex
v

∎

Second, the

5.2.
The STBIS Algorithm

In this section, we propose a greedy algorithm
\mathsf{STBIS}
to find a single target. We begin with probability calculation.

Probability calculation.
Before asking any questions, each vertex has an equal probability of being the target. Thus, we let each vertex
u
have a probability of
{\mathsf{pr}}(u)=\frac{1}{n}
where
n=|V|
. Our proposed algorithm can be easily extended to other vertex probability distribution based on historical query logs as
(
li2020efficient
)
.
Therefore, for a vertex
v

v

and the no probability follows
{\mathsf{p_{No}}}(v)=1-{\mathsf{p_{Yes}}}(v)

The updated probability after each question is calculated as:

(4)

{\mathsf{pr}}(u)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &{\mathsf{pr}}(u)\cdot\frac{|\mathcal{P}|}{|\mathcal{P}_{new}|},\ &u\in\mathcal{P}_{new}\\
&0,\ &u\notin\mathcal{P}_{new}\\
\end{aligned}\right.

where

is the potential targets after asking a question
{\mathsf{reach}}(q_{i})
where
1\leq i\leq b
. The general idea is to assign impossible targets with a probability value of zero and keep the sum probability equal to
1
.

Quality and complexity analysis.

We analyze the quality of our approximate penalty function
{\mathsf{g}}^{\prime}(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{P},k)
as follows.

Theorem 2.

It holds that
{\mathsf{LB}}_{k}\leq{\mathsf{g}}^{\prime}(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{P},k)\leq{\mathsf{UB}}_{k}
, where
{\mathsf{LB}}_{k}={\mathsf{g}}(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{P},k)-(k-1)\cdot({\mathsf{f}}(\{r\},\mathcal{P})-{\mathsf{g}}(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{P},k))
and
{\mathsf{UB}}_{k}={\mathsf{g}}(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{P},k)
.
If
k=1
,
{\mathsf{g}}^{\prime}(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{P},1)={\mathsf{g}}(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{P},1)
.

Proof.

First, we prove
{\mathsf{g}}^{\prime}(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{P},k)\leq{\mathsf{UB}}_{k}
. Assume that
\mathcal{S}^{*}
is the best selection in terms of potential penalty, i.e.,
{\mathsf{g}}(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{P},k)={\mathsf{f}}(\mathcal{S}^{*},\mathcal{P})
. Then
{\mathsf{g}}(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{P},k)={\mathsf{f}}(\mathcal{S}^{*},\mathcal{P})\geq{\mathsf{f}}(\{r\},\mathcal{P})-\sum_{x\in\mathcal{S}^{*}}{{\mathsf{IG}}(x)}\geq{\mathsf{g}}^{\prime}(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{P},k).
Thus,
{\mathsf{g}}^{\prime}(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{P},k)\leq{\mathsf{g}}(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{P},k)={\mathsf{UB}}_{k}
.

Second, we prove
{\mathsf{g}}^{\prime}(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{P},k)\geq{\mathsf{LB}}_{k}
. We have
{\mathsf{g}}^{\prime}(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{P},k)\geq{\mathsf{f}}(\{r\},\mathcal{P})-k\cdot\max_{x\in\mathcal{Y}}{{\mathsf{IG}}(x)}=k\cdot({\mathsf{f}}(\{r\},\mathcal{P})-\max_{x\in\mathcal{Y}}{{\mathsf{IG}}(x)})-(k-1)\cdot{\mathsf{f}}(\{r\},\mathcal{P})\geq k\cdot{\mathsf{g}}(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{P},k)-(k-1)\cdot{\mathsf{f}}(\{r\},\mathcal{P})={\mathsf{g}}(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{P},k)-(k-1)\cdot({\mathsf{f}}(\{r\},\mathcal{P})-{\mathsf{g}}(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{P},k))
={\mathsf{LB}}_{k}
.

Overall,
{\mathsf{LB}}_{k}\leq{\mathsf{g}}^{\prime}(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{P},k)\leq{\mathsf{UB}}_{k}
. For
k=1
, we have
{\mathsf{UB}}_{k}={\mathsf{g}}(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{P},k)={\mathsf{LB}}_{k}
. Thus,
{\mathsf{g}}^{\prime}(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{P},1)={\mathsf{g}}(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{P},1)
holds.

Conclusion and Future Work

In this paper, we study the problem of
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{IGS}
to identify multiple targets in a hierarchy via a constrained budget of interactive questions. To effectively tackle the problem, we propose a novel
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{IGS}
framework to select the vertex with the maximum expected gain to ask question. On the basis of the
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{IGS}
framework, we develop
\mathsf{STBIS}
algorithm to identify a single target and a dynamic programming based method
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{DP}
to identify multiple targets. To accelerate the efficiency, we propose two heuristic algorithms
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{Topk}
and
\mathsf{kBM}
-
\mathsf{DP}

opens up interesting questions. One challenging direction is how to control the quality of target selections, given that crowd workers may give wrong answers.
It takes non-trivial efforts to identify wrong answers in various possible forms and make a balanced budget cost between
trusting the given answers by asking further questions
and

suspecting the given answers by repeating questions to double-check
in a principled way.
