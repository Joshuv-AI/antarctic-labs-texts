Randomized least-squares with minimal oversampling and interpolation in general spaces
Matthieu Dolbeault, Moulay Abdellah Chkifa

Abstract
In approximation of functions based on point values, least-squares methods provide more stability than interpolation, at the expense of increasing the sampling budget. We show that near-optimal approximation error can nevertheless be achieved, in an expected L 2 L^{2} sense, as soon as the sample size m m is larger than the dimension n n of the approximation space by a constant ratio. On the other hand, for m = n m=n , we obtain an interpolation strategy with a stability factor of order n n . The proposed sampling algorithms are greedy procedures based on [ BSS09 ] and [ LS18 ] , with polynomial computational complexity.



Keywords.

Least squares, Interpolation, Christoffel function

Introduction and main results

Let
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
be a probability space.
We consider the problem of estimating an unknown function
f:X\to\mathbb{C}

from evaluations of
f
at chosen points
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\in X
.
We assess the error between
f
and its estimator
\tilde{f}
either in the
L^{2}(X,\mu)
norm

\|g\|_{L^{2}}:=\left(\int_{X}|g(x)|^{2}d\mu(x)\right)^{1/2},

or in the uniform norm
\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(X,\mu)}
.
Given a subspace
V_{n}\subset L^{2}(X,\mu)
of dimension
n
, we
would like the estimator
\tilde{f}
to belong to
V_{n}
and to perform almost as well as
the best approximation of
f
in
V_{n}
, that is, its orthogonal projection

P_{n}f=\argmin_{v\in V_{n}}\|f-v\|_{L^{2}}.

As we only have access to point-wise observations,
we cannot explicitly compute
P_{n}f
in general.
In this context, a classical
approach consists in considering
a solution to the weighted least-squares problem

P_{n}^{m}f\in\argmin_{v\in V_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}s_{i}|f(x_{i})-v(x_{i})|^{2},

where we may use some weights
s_{1},\dots,s_{m}>0
.
We would like this problem to admit a unique solution, and therefore require
m\geqslant n
.
Similar to
P_{n}
for the
L^{2}
norm,
the operator
P_{n}^{m}
is the orthogonal projector
onto
V_{n}
with respect to the empirical norm

\|g\|_{m}:=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}s_{i}|g(x_{i})|^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

The approximation accuracy is inherently related to the points

and
the weights
s_{i}
. For the sake of illustration, consider the
setting where

n

is the Lagrange interpolation operator

For equally spaced points

, even when

is infinitely smooth.
This is the so-called

[
MM08
]
.
The phenomenon persists with equally spaced points even for

[
BX09
]
and theoretically explained in
[
PTK11
]
. In fact,
the Runge phenomenon occurs for any points that do not cluster quadratically like Chebyshev points, see
[
APS19
]
.
It is
however defeated by interpolation on Chebyshev type points.

As far as interpolation in spaces

A generic choice is that of Fekete points, which
ensure that

\|g\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}=\sup_{x\in X}|g(x)|

2
Least-squares

Let
\varphi=(\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{n})
be an orthonormal basis of
V_{n}
in
L^{2}(X,\mu)
. For any
x\in X
, we consider
\varphi(x)
as a vector in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, denote
\varphi(x)^{*}
its conjugate transpose, and
|\varphi(x)|^{2}=\varphi(x)^{*}\varphi(x)
its
squared euclidian norm.
Observe that, given any
n\times n
matrix
{\bm{M}}
,

\int_{X}\varphi(x)^{*}{\bm{M}}\varphi(x)d\mu(x)=\Tr\left({\bm{M}}\int_{X}\varphi(x)\varphi(x)^{*}d\mu(x)\right)=\Tr({\bm{M}}).

(4)

Adopting the formalism from
[
KUV21
,
MU21
,
NSU22
]
), we let
{\bf f}=(\sqrt{s_{i}}f(x_{i}))_{1\leqslant i\leqslant m}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
be the measurement vector, and

{\bm{L}}=(\sqrt{s_{i}}\varphi_{j}(x_{i}))_{1\leqslant i\leqslant m,1\leqslant j\leqslant n}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}

the collocation matrix. Then the weighted least-square estimator writes

P_{n}^{m}f=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\varphi_{j},\quad a={\bm{L}}^{+}{\bf f},

where
{\bm{L}}^{+}=({\bm{L}}^{*}{\bm{L}})^{-1}{\bm{L}}^{*}
stands for the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse of
{\bm{L}}
. In the sequel, we will make sure that
{\bm{L}}
has full column
rank
n
, so that the Gram matrix

{\bm{A}}_{m}:={\bm{L}}^{*}{\bm{L}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}s_{i}\varphi(x_{i})\varphi(x_{i})^{*}

is indeed invertible.
Denoting
g=f-P_{n}f
the optimal residual error and
{\bf g}=(\sqrt{s_{i}}g(x_{i}))_{1\leqslant i\leqslant m}
the associated vector, the least-squares error decomposes as

\|f-P_{n}^{m}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\|f-P_{n}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|P_{n}f-P_{n}^{m}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\|g\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|P_{n}^{m}g\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(5)

There are two possible strategies for bounding
\|P_{n}^{m}g\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
:
either we use

\|P_{n}^{m}g\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=|{\bm{A}}_{m}^{-1}{\bm{L}}^{*}{\bf g}|^{2}\leqslant\|{\bm{A}}_{m}^{-1}\|_{2\to 2}^{2}|{\bm{L}}^{*}{\bf g}|^{2}=\lambda_{\min}({\bm{A}}_{m})^{-2}|\langle\varphi,g\rangle_{m}|^{2},

(6)

where
\langle\varphi,g\rangle_{m}:=(\langle\varphi_{j},g\rangle_{m})_{1\leqslant j\leqslant n}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, or we bound it by

\|P_{n}^{m}g\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=|{\bm{L}}^{+}{\bf g}|^{2}\leqslant\|{\bm{L}}^{+}\|_{2\to 2}^{2}|{\bf g}|^{2}=\|{\bm{A}}_{m}^{-1}\|_{2\to 2}\|g\|_{m}^{2}=\lambda_{\min}({\bm{A}}_{m})^{-1}\|g\|_{m}^{2}.

(7)

The first approach is expected to give better estimates when
m
is much larger than
n
, since in that case the discrete inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{m}
weakly converges to the continuous inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{L^{2}}
, and as
g
is orthogonal to
V_{n}
, the vector
\langle\varphi,g\rangle_{m}
should be small. On the other hand, when
m
is close to
n
,
{\bm{L}}
may be ill-conditioned, leading to small values of
\lambda_{\min}({\bm{A}}_{m})
, thus favouring the second approach.

In both situations, one should choose the points
x_{i}
and weights
s_{i}
in order to control the smallest eigenvalue of
{\bm{A}}_{m}
from below.
This can be ensured through a greedy selection of points, based on the
effective resistance

[
SS11
]

\varphi(x)^{*}({\bm{A}}-\ell{\bm{I}})^{-1}\varphi(x)

of a point
x\in X
with respect to a hermitian matrix
{\bm{A}}
, with
\ell
a lower bound on the eigenvalues of
{\bm{A}}
, and
{\bm{I}}
the identity matrix.
Intuitively, if the points
x_{1},\dots,x_{i-1}
are already fixed, the effective resistance of
x_{i}
with respect to the partial Gram matrix

{\bm{A}}_{i-1}=\sum_{\iota=1}^{i-1}s_{\iota}\varphi(x_{\iota})\varphi(x_{\iota})^{*}

quantifies how close
\varphi(x_{i})
is to the eigenvectors of
{\bm{A}}_{i-1}
with small eigenvalues, helping the
lower potential

\Tr(({\bm{A}}-\ell{\bm{I}})^{-1})
to decrease when going from
{\bm{A}}_{i-1}
to
{\bm{A}}_{i}
. The key idea in
[
BSS09
]
is to increase the lower barrier
\ell
at each step, without increasing the potential more than it had decreased when adding sample
x_{i}
. Keeping the potential bounded ensures that all updates of
\ell
are of the same size
\delta
, and its trace form is chosen to allow rank-one matrices
s_{i}\varphi(x_{i})\varphi(x_{i})^{*}
to decrease the potential, independently of the distribution of eigenvalues of
{\bm{A}}_{i-1}
. In this way, we obtain the desired bound
\lambda_{\min}({\bm{A}}_{m})>\ell_{m}
at the end of the algorithm.

As in
[
BSS09
]
, it is also possible to ensure an upper bound
\lambda_{\max}({\bm{A}}_{m})<u_{m}
on the eigenvalues of
{\bm{A}}_{m}
, by considering the
upper potential

{\Tr((u{\bm{I}}-{\bm{A}})^{-1})}
. Although
\lambda_{\max}({\bm{A}}_{m})
does not explicitly appear in the least-squares formulation, it is worth noticing that the ratio
u_{m}/\ell_{m}
controls the condition number
\lambda_{\max}({\bm{A}}_{m})/\lambda_{\min}({\bm{A}}_{m})
of
{\bm{A}}_{m}
, which determines the computational cost of solving the least-squares problem via an iterative solver, as well as its robustness to numerical error. This is discussed in, e.g., Section 5.3.4 of
[
ABW22
]
.

Nevertheless, we chose not to include this upper potential, because it would have prevented us from achieving the critical sampling budget
m\approx n
. Heuristically, when we use both upper and lower potentials, the vector
\varphi(x_{i})
should increase the smallest eigenvalues of
{\bm{A}}_{i-1}
, while staying far from the eigenvectors with largest eigenvalues. Due to the second condition, the lower barrier can only increase half as much as it could without it, resulting in the constraint
m\geqslant 2n
. In addition, we still achieve an a posteriori upper bound, see (
21
), which is not particularly sharp, but sufficient for our purposes. Lastly, a full inversion of matrix
{\bm{A}}_{m}
, with complexity
{\cal O}(n^{3})
, remains reasonable in view of the moderate values of
n
imposed by the sampling costs, see Section
5
.

3
Random sampling by effective resistance

We first consider an approach combining (
5
) and (
6
) to obtain the first estimate (
1
) in Theorem
1.1
. To observe the appropriate decay as
r\to\infty
, we impose that the vector

\langle\varphi,g\rangle_{m}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}s_{i}\varphi(x_{i})\overline{g(x_{i})}

is an unbiased estimator of
\langle\varphi,g\rangle_{L^{2}}=0
, by taking weights
s_{i}
inversely proportional to the sampling density of
x_{i}
. If the points
x_{i}
are independent, the euclidian norm
|\langle\varphi,g\rangle_{m}|^{2}
is therefore bounded in expectation by the sum of the variances of each term, of the form

\mathbb{E}(s_{i}^{2}|\varphi(x_{i})|^{2}|g(x_{i})|^{2})\propto\int_{X}s_{i}|\varphi(x_{i})|^{2}|g(x_{i})|^{2}d\mu(x_{i})\leqslant\big\|s_{i}|\varphi(x_{i})|^{2}\big\|_{L^{\infty}}\|g\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

As a result, to control
\ell_{m}
from below and these variances from above, the appropriate sampling density for point
x_{i}
is a combination of the effective resistance mentioned earlier
and the so-called
Christoffel function

|\varphi(x)|^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\varphi_{j}(x)|^{2}
.

Algorithm
1

is inspired by
the deterministic procedure from
[
BSS09
]
and
its randomization presented in
[

]
, which has already been applied to least-squares recovery in
[
CP19
]
.
It
takes as inputs the parameters

\varepsilon\in(0,1),\qquad\eta=\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\in(0,\infty),\qquad\text{and}\qquad\gamma\geqslant 0,

which respectively influence the size of the barrier increments
\delta_{i}=\ell_{i}-\ell_{i-1}
, the weights
s_{i}
,
and the balance between effective resistance and Christoffel function in the sampling density.

Input:
probability measure
\mu
, parameter
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, and
\gamma\geqslant 0
.

{\bm{A}}_{0}={\bf 0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
\ell_{0}=-n

for

i=1,\dots,m

do

Let
{\bm{Y}}_{i}=({\bm{A}}_{i-1}-\ell_{i-1}{\bm{I}})^{-1}

Update
\ell_{i}=\ell_{i-1}+\delta_{i}
, where
\delta_{i}:={\varepsilon}/{\Psi_{i}}
with
\Psi_{i}=\Tr({\bm{Y}}_{i})+\gamma

Let
{\bm{Z}}_{i}=({\bm{A}}_{i-1}-\ell_{i}{\bm{I}})^{-1}

Let

Algorithm 1

Random sampling by effective resistance

For the sake of the analysis, we also define

Lemma 3.1
.

The algorithm is
almost surely
well defined and

{\bm{A}}_{i}\succ\ell_{i+1}{\bm{I}}
for all
0\leqslant i\leqslant m

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
i
. At initialization
{\bm{A}}_{0}\succ-n{\bm{I}}=\ell_{0}{\bm{I}}
.
For
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
fixed, assume that
{\bm{A}}_{i-1}\succ\ell_{i-1}{\bm{I}}
. This
implies that
{\bm{Y}}_{i}
is well-defined, positive definite, and

\delta_{i}{\bm{I}}=\frac{\varepsilon{\bm{I}}}{\Tr({\bm{Y}}_{i})+\gamma}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\preceq}\frac{\varepsilon{\bm{I}}}{\lambda_{\max}({\bm{Y}}_{i})}={\lambda_{\min}({\bm{A}}_{i-1}-\ell_{i-1}{\bm{I}})}\,\varepsilon{\bm{I}}\prec{\bm{A}}_{i-1}-\ell_{i-1}{\bm{I}}.

(8)

Hence
{\bm{A}}_{i-1}\succ(\ell_{i-1}+\delta_{i}){\bm{I}}=\ell_{i}{\bm{I}}
, so

is well-defined,
positive definite, and by identity (

proving that
\rho_{i}d\mu/\Xi_{i}
is indeed a probability density.

Finally
s_{i}>0
almost surely, so
{\bm{A}}_{i}\succcurlyeq{\bm{A}}_{i-1}\succ\ell_{i}{\bm{I}}

Contrarily to most variations on the algorithm from

from above. Instead, parameter

To bound the eigenvalues of

from below, we use the lower barrier

controlled by the lower potential
\Tr({\bm{Y}}_{i})
. As the densities

are positive, the sample

could be in any part of

,
so there cannot be any positive deterministic lower bound on

. However,
the following lemma proves a monotonicity property in expectation, similar to Lemmas 4.4 in
[

and
[

Lemma 3.2
.

The sequence

(\mathbb{E}(\Tr({\bm{Y}}_{i}))_{1\leqslant i\leqslant m+1}
is non-increasing.

Proof.

Let
1\leqslant i\leqslant m
,
and denote
u_{i}=\sqrt{s_{i}}\varphi(x_{i})
.
Applying the Sherman-Morrison formula yields

{\bm{Y}}_{i+1}=({\bm{Z}}_{i}^{-1}+u_{i}u_{i}^{*})^{-1}={\bm{Z}}_{i}-\frac{{\bm{Z}}_{i}u_{i}u_{i}^{*}{\bm{Z}}_{i}}{1+u_{i}^{*}{\bm{Z}}_{i}u_{i}}.

(9)

By design of the sampling density and weights,

u_{i}^{*}{\bm{Z}}_{i}u_{i}=s_{i}\varphi(x_{i})^{*}{\bm{Z}}_{i}\varphi(x_{i})\leqslant s_{i}\rho_{i}(x_{i})=\eta,

(10)

so by definition of
\eta=\varepsilon/(1-\varepsilon)
, which implies

, we obtain

{\bm{Y}}_{i+1}-{\bm{Z}}_{i}\preccurlyeq-\frac{{\bm{Z}}_{i}u_{i}u_{i}^{*}{\bm{Z}}_{i}}{1+\eta}=-\frac{\varepsilon}{\eta}s_{i}{\bm{Z}}_{i}\varphi(x_{i})\varphi(x_{i})^{*}{\bm{Z}}_{i}.

(11)

On the other hand,
{\bm{Y}}_{i}
and
{\bm{Z}}_{i}
commute because their inverses do, and we can write

{\bm{Z}}_{i}-{\bm{Y}}_{i}=({\bm{Y}}_{i}^{-1}-{\bm{Z}}_{i}^{-1}){\bm{Y}}_{i}{\bm{Z}}_{i}=\delta_{i}{\bm{Y}}_{i}{\bm{Z}}_{i}.

Combining this identity to the previous estimate and taking traces gives

\Tr({\bm{Y}}_{i+1})-\Tr({\bm{Y}}_{i})=\delta_{i}\Tr({\bm{Y}}_{i}{\bm{Z}}_{i})-\frac{\varepsilon}{\eta}s_{i}\varphi(x_{i})^{*}{\bm{Z}}_{i}^{2}\varphi(x_{i}).

Observe that
{\bm{Y}}_{i}
and
{\bm{Z}}_{i}
only depend on the
samples
x_{1},\dots,x_{i-1}
. As a result, if these samples are fixed,
taking the expectation
\mathbb{E}_{x_{i}}=\mathbb{E}(\,\cdot\,|x_{1},\dots,x_{i-1})
with respect to point
x_{i}
, we arrive at

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x_{i}}\big(\Tr({\bm{Y}}_{i+1})\big)-\Tr({\bm{Y}}_{i})

\displaystyle=\delta_{i}\Tr({\bm{Y}}_{i}{\bm{Z}}_{i})-\frac{\varepsilon}{\eta}\int_{X}\frac{\eta}{\rho_{i}(x_{i})}\varphi(x_{i})^{*}{\bm{Z}}_{i}^{2}\varphi(x_{i})\frac{\rho_{i}(x_{i})}{\Xi_{i}}d\mu(x_{i})

(12)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\left(\frac{\Tr({\bm{Y}}_{i}{\bm{Z}}_{i})}{\Tr({\bm{Y}}_{i})+\gamma}-\frac{\Tr({\bm{Z}}_{i}^{2})}{\Tr({\bm{Z}}_{i})+\gamma}\right),

where we have used identity (
4
)
. Since

{\bm{Z}}_{i}={\bm{Y}}_{i}+\delta_{i}{\bm{Y}}_{i}{\bm{Z}}_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad{\bm{Z}}_{i}^{2}={\bm{Y}}_{i}{\bm{Z}}_{i}+\delta_{i}{\bm{Y}}_{i}{\bm{Z}}_{i}^{2},

(13)

we have by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

\Tr({\bm{Y}}_{i}{\bm{Z}}_{i})\Tr({\bm{Z}}_{i})-\Tr({\bm{Y}}_{i})\Tr({\bm{Z}}_{i}^{2})=\delta_{i}\left(\Tr({\bm{Y}}_{i}{\bm{Z}}_{i})^{2}-\Tr({\bm{Y}}_{i})\Tr({\bm{Y}}_{i}{\bm{Z}}_{i}^{2})\right)\leqslant 0.

(14)

Moreover,

\Tr({\bm{Y}}_{i}{\bm{Z}}_{i})-\Tr({\bm{Z}}_{i}^{2})=-\delta_{i}\Tr({\bm{Y}}_{i}{\bm{Z}}_{i}^{2})=-\delta_{i}\Tr({\bm{Z}}_{i}{\bm{Y}}_{i}{\bm{Z}}_{i})<0
. Hence

the right-hand side in (
12

Taking an expectation over the previous samples

in turn implies that

\mathbb{E}\big(\Tr({\bm{Y}}_{i+1})\big)
is smaller than
\mathbb{E}\big(\Tr({\bm{Y}}_{i})\big)
, which concludes

.
∎

1
verifies

{\bm{A}}_{m}\succcurlyeq\alpha{\bm{I}}\quad a.s.,\qquad\text{with}\quad\alpha=(n-1)(\delta r-1).

Proof.

After the last iteration of the algorithm, we have

\lambda_{\min}({\bm{A}}_{m})=\ell_{m}+(\lambda_{\max}({\bm{Y}}_{m+1}))^{-1}\geqslant m\delta-n+(\Tr({\bm{Y}}_{m+1}))^{-1}=(\delta r-1)(n-1).

∎

We now have all the tools for the proof of the main theorem.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
, equation (
2
).

Introducing the set

E_{i}=\left\{x\in{X},\;w_{i}(x)\geqslant\kappa\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}\right\},

observe that

\begin{array}[]{ll}\Gamma_{i}&=\displaystyle\int_{E_{i}}R_{i}d\mu=\displaystyle\int_{X}w_{i}(x)d\mu(x)-\int_{E_{i}^{c}}w_{i}(x)d\mu(x)\\
&\geqslant\displaystyle\Tr({\bm{W}}_{i})-\int_{X}\kappa\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}d\mu\geqslant(1-\kappa)\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}.\end{array}

(18)

Taking the expectation
\mathbb{E}_{x_{i}}
over point
x_{i}
, with
x_{1},\dots,x_{i-1}
fixed, we compute

\mathbb{E}_{x_{i}}(s_{i}|g(x_{i})|^{2})=\int_{E_{i}}\frac{1}{R_{i}(x)}|g(x)|^{2}\frac{R_{i}(x)}{\Gamma_{i}}d\mu(x)\leqslant\frac{\|g\|_{L^{2}}^{2}}{\Gamma_{i}}\leqslant\frac{1}{1-\kappa}\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}\|g\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

where
g=f-P_{n}f\in L^{2}(X,\mu)
.
The same holds for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant m
, and consequently

\mathbb{E}\left(\|g\|_{m}^{2}\right)\leqslant\frac{m}{1-\kappa}\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}\|g\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

which together with (
7
) and Proposition
4.3
gives

\mathbb{E}(\|P_{n}^{m}g\|^{2})\leqslant\alpha^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left(\|P_{n}^{m}g\|_{m}^{2}\right){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\leqslant\alpha^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left(\|g\|_{m}^{2}\right)}\leqslant\displaystyle\frac{r}{m(\delta r-1)}\frac{m}{1-\kappa}\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}\|g\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Taking
\delta={1}/{\sqrt{r}}
and adding (
5
),
we arrive at (
2
).
∎

Remark 4.4
.

For
\kappa=0
, the density of point
x_{i}
is proportional to
w_{i}(x)_{+}=\max(w_{i}(x),0)
. A sampling density proportional to the positive part of a function also occurs in
[
LS17
]
, equation (6). There, it is used to select points which increase the lower potential without increasing too much the upper potential.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
, equation (
3
).

As
V_{n}\subset L^{\infty}
, we can assume that
f
is also in
L^{\infty}
, otherwise the right-hand side would be infinite.
We introduce the notation

P_{n}^{\infty}f{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\in}\argmin_{v\in V_{n}}\|f-v\|_{L^{\infty}}

for a Chebyshev projection of
f
onto
V_{n}
with respect to the Banach norm
\|\cdot\|_{L^{\infty}}
, and take
g=f-P_{n}^{\infty}f
the associated residual. As
1/{s_{i}}=w_{i}(x_{i})\geqslant\kappa{(1-\delta)}/{\delta}
,
it holds

\|g\|_{m}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}s_{i}|g(x_{i})|^{2}\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{m}s_{i}\|g\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\leqslant\frac{m\delta}{\kappa(1-\delta)}\|g\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\quad a.s.

By a triangular inequality

\|f-P_{n}^{m}f\|_{L^{2}}\leqslant\|f-P_{n}^{\infty}f\|_{L^{2}}+\|P_{n}^{m}f-P_{n}^{\infty}f\|_{L^{2}}=\|g\|_{L^{2}}+\|P_{n}^{m}g\|_{L^{2}}.

We conclude by bounding the operator norm
\|P_{n}^{m}\|_{L^{\infty}\to L^{2}}
through

\|P_{n}^{m}g\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leqslant\alpha^{-1}\|P_{n}^{m}g\|_{m}^{2}\leqslant \alpha^{-1}\|g\|_{m}^{2}\leqslant\frac{r}{\delta r-1}\frac{\delta}{\kappa(1-\delta)}\|g\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}.

(19)

As a result, Algorithm

with

implies inequality (

).
∎

Remark 4.5
.

In the randomized setting,
\mu
could be any positive measure on
X
.
On the contrary, in the uniform setting, we need
\mu
to be a probability measure to bound
L^{2}
norms by
L^{\infty}
norms.
Similar results would hold if
\mu
was a positive measure of finite mass, with all
L^{\infty}
norms multiplied by a factor
\mu(X)
, see for instance
[
LT22
,
Tem21
,
KPUU23
]
.

We turn to the proof of the last result of the introduction, which is stated in
strong uniform norm to avoid technicalities.

Proof of Corollary
1.4
.

Denote
\mathcal{L}^{\infty}
the set of bounded functions, and
\|f\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}:=\sup_{x\in X}|f(x)|
the associated norm. As
V_{n}\subset\mathcal{L}^{\infty}
, we can assume that
f
is also in
\mathcal{L}^{\infty}
, otherwise the right-hand side would be infinite. Taking
g=f-P_{n}^{\infty}f
the
\mathcal{L}^{\infty}
-residual, we write as above

\|f-P_{n}^{m}f\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}\leqslant\|f-P_{n}^{\infty}f\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}+\|P_{n}^{m}f-P_{n}^{\infty}f\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}=\|g\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}+\|P_{n}^{m}g\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}.

For any probability measure
\mu
on
X
,
consider
(\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{n})
an orthonormal basis of
V_{n}
in
L^{2}=L^{2}(X,\mu)
. Then the supremum of the Christoffel function is, by a Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

K_{n}:=\sup_{x\in X}|\varphi(x)|^{2}=\sup_{x\in X}\max_{c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{|\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}\varphi_{j}(x)|^{2}}{|c|^{2}}=\max_{v\in V_{n}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\|v\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}^{2}}{\|v\|_{L^{2}}^{2}}.

By simply considering (
19
) in the case
m=n
,
\kappa=1
, and
\delta=1/\sqrt{r}\leqslant 1-1/2n
, we derive the inequality

\|P_{n}^{n}g\|_{L^{2}}\leqslant(1-\delta)^{-1}\|g\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}\leqslant 2n\|g\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}
. As
P_{n}^{n}g\in V_{n}
, we obtain

\|P_{n}^{n}g\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}\leqslant\sqrt{K_{n}}\|P_{n}^{n}g\|_{L^{2}}\leqslant 2n\sqrt{K_{n}}\|g\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}.

We now rely on the main corollary of the paper
[
KW60
]
.
Since
X
is compact, there exists a measure
\mu^{*}
for which

K_{n}=n
.
Using such a measure in our framing,
we obtain by application of the above

\|P_{n}^{n}g\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}\leqslant 2n\sqrt{n}\|g\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}
,
which concludes the proof.
∎

Let us briefly explain the result in
[
KW60
]
to our setting. Given

\phi=(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{n})
a basis of
V_{n}
and
\mu

a probability measure over
X
, we consider the
n\times n

symmetric positive semi-definite matrix
M(\mu):=\int_{X}\phi\phi^{*}d\mu
. Assuming
this matrix is non-singular,
\varphi:={M(\mu)}^{-1/2}\phi
is also basis
of
V_{n}
and is orthonormal with respect to
\mu
. In particular

\varphi(x)^{*}\varphi(x)=\phi^{*}{M(\mu)}^{-1}\phi
is the
associated Christoffel function. The optimal probability measures from

[
KW60
]
are characterized by

\mu^{*}\in\argmin_{\mu\in\mathrm{Prob}(X)}\,\sup_{x\in X}\;\phi(x)^{*}M(\mu)^{-1}\phi(x)=\argmax_{\mu\in\mathrm{Prob}(X)}\,\det\left(M(\mu)\right).

These two equivalent extremum problems are difficult
to solve for general compacts
X
and spaces
V_{n}
, thus making
the approach from Corollary
1.4
unpractical. Nevertheless, it gives a theoretical bound on the approximation error achievable by this method.
We refer to
[
Bos90
]
for an alternative proof of the result in
[
KW60
]
, with
applications to interpolation, in particular to the so-called Fejer problem. We also note that
[
KW60
]
answers Remark 5.5 in
[
PU22
]
.

By inspection of the proof of Corollary
1.4
, we observe that
any measure
\mu
used in Algorithm
1
yields a bound of the form

\|f-P_{n}^{n}f\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}\leqslant(1+2n\sqrt{K_{n}})\|f-P_{n}^{\infty}f\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}.

(20)

Corollary
1.4
can also be stated for
m\geqslant n
, as done in Remark
1.5
.
For instance, Algorithm
1
for
m=2n
, hence
with parameters
\delta=1/\sqrt{r}\leqslant 1/\sqrt{2}
,

\kappa=1
, and any measure
\mu
, yields

\|f-P_{n}^{m}f\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}\leqslant(1+4\sqrt{K_{n}})\|f-P_{n}^{\infty}f\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}.

The above remarks has to be combined with upper bounds on
K_{n}
, for which
we give a few examples in polynomial approximation.

We first consider the space
V_{n}=\mathbb{P}_{n-1}
of algebraic polynomials of degree less
than
n
, restricted to
X=[-1,1]
. By considering the
uniform
measure

d\mu(x)=dx/2
, we have
K_{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(2j+1)=n^{2}
,
while with the
arcsine
measure
d\mu(x)=\frac{dx}{\pi\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}
, we obtain

K_{n}=1+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}2=2n-1
,
where we used uniform bounds on the Legendre and Chebyshev polynomials, respectively.

Similar results are also available for spaces
of multivariate polynomials indexed in lower set, see
[
CCM+15
, Lemma 3.1]
.
We recall that a lower set, or downward closed set, is a set of multi-indices

\Lambda\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
such that
\nu\in\Lambda

implies
\nu^{\prime}\in\Lambda
for all
\nu^{\prime}\leqslant \nu
, the last inequality being understood component-wise.
For
X=[-1,1]^{d}
, we consider polynomial approximation spaces of the form

V_{n}=\Span\{x_{1}^{\nu_{1}}\dots x_{d}^{\nu_{d}},\;\nu\in\Lambda\},

with
\Lambda
a lower set of cardinality
n
. Among the associated spaces

V_{n}
, one can find polynomials of bounded degree in each variable, of bounded
total degree, as well as hyperbolic cross spaces, which are of important use in
high-dimensional approximation
[
DTU18
]
. The following estimates hold:

•

for
\mu
the
uniform
measure,

K_{n}=\sum_{\nu\in\Lambda}\prod_{j=1}^{d}(1+2\nu_{j})

is
bounded by
n^{2}
,

•

for
\mu
the tensorized
arcsine
measure,

K_{n}=\sum_{\nu\in\Lambda}2^{|\nu|_{0}}

is
bounded by
n^{\frac{\log 3}{\log 2}}
.

The estimates
n^{2}
and
n^{\frac{\log 3}{\log 2}}
are established in
[
CCM+15
]
.
We note also the plain linear estimates
C_{\Lambda}n
where

C_{\Lambda}=\max_{\nu\in\Lambda}\prod_{j=1}^{d}(1+2\nu_{j})

and
C_{\Lambda}=\max_{\nu\in\Lambda}2^{|\nu|_{0}}\leqslant 2^{d}
, respectively. Such estimates
might be much smaller, for instance in the second setting when
d
is small
.
Combining these bounds with (
20
)
implies the results given at the end of the introduction.

In the particular case of polynomials of fixed total degree, invariance by affine transformations
allows to treat more general cases.

Theorem 4.6

(
[
CD21
]
, Theorems 5.4 and 5.6)
.

For
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
a compact domain,
d\mu(x)={dx}/{|X|}
the
uniform

measure, and
V_{n}
a set of polynomials of fixed total degree, it holds
K_{n}\leqslant C_{X}n^{2}
if

X
has a Lipschitz boundary, and
K_{n}\leqslant C_{X}n^{1+1/d}
if
X
has a smooth boundary.

It is worth noting that the bound
K_{n}\leqslant C_{X}n^{2}
also holds for arbitrary lower sets when the domain is a union of rectangles of fixed volume, see Theorem 6.5 of
[
AH20
]
.

All these results show that, in many instances, we have
K_{n}=O(n^{2})
with the uniform measure. Therefore Algorithm
1
provides a feasible way to sample points for which the Lebesgue constant is
\mathbb{L}_{n}=O(n^{2})
.

5
Numerical aspects

Our two algorithms have a polynomial complexity in
n
, since one has to solve linear systems of size
n
at each iteration. In fact,

\Tr({\bm{Y}}_{i+1})
can be computed without the knowledge of
{\bm{Y}}_{i+1}
,
by taking a trace in the Shermann-Morrison formula (
9
),
i.e.

\Tr({\bm{Y}}_{i+1})=\Tr({\bm{Z}}_{i})-\frac{\varphi(x_{i})^{*}{\bm{Z}}_{i}^{2}\varphi(x_{i})}{s_{i}+\varphi(x_{i})^{*}{\bm{Z}}_{i}\varphi(x_{i})},

so we only need a matrix inversion for
{\bm{Z}}_{i}
. Moreover, this can be done efficiently by updating the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
{\bm{A}}_{i}
from one iteration to the next, see
[
LS18
]
for details.

In
[
LS18
]
and
[
LS17
]
, polynomial and exponential modifications of the lower potential are also proposed, allowing to draw the samples by batches, and therefore reducing the complexity to
a linear expression in
n
up to logarithmic factors, in the particular case of subsampling a graph laplacian
. The proofs are more involved, produce larger constants and require a large value of
r
, so we did not adapt them to our setting.

The essential remaining difficulty is to draw each point
x_{i}
according to its prescribed density

\rho_{i}
(or
R_{i}
). We start with the following observation.

Remark 5.1
.

An important application is the case where
X=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{M}\}
is a finite set,
and
\mu
is the uniform measure on
X
.
The orthonormality of the basis
\varphi=(\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{n})
is encoded
by the following decomposition of the identity into a sum of rank-one matrices:

\sum_{i=1}^{M}v_{i}v_{i}^{*}={\bm{I}},\qquad v_{i}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}(\varphi_{1}(x_{i}),\dots,\varphi_{n}(x_{i}))^{\top}.

In this situation, it suffices to evaluate
\rho_{i}
at all points of
X
, and to select
x_{i}=x
with probability
\rho_{i}(x)/\sum_{x^{\prime}\in X}\rho_{i}(x^{\prime})
.
Our algorithms
1
and
1
can also work as a sample reduction technique, see for example
[
CD22
]
, where the evaluation points are extracted from a large initial sample
x_{1},\dots,x_{M}
. Indeed, it suffices to apply our algorithms to the set
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{M}\}
, equipped with the empirical measure
\mu=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\delta_{x_{i}}
.

When drawing points directly from an infinite set
X
, one may use an acceptance-rejection
strategy. Notice that
\rho_{i}
in Algorithm
1

and
w_{i}
in Algorithm
1
are of the form
\varphi(x)^{*}{\bm{M}}\varphi(x)
,
with either
{\bm{M}}={\bm{Z}}_{i}+\gamma{\bm{I}}/n
or
{\bm{M}}={\bm{W}}_{i}
. Moreover, some routines
have been developed
[
CM17
,
AC20
,
ACD23
,
Mig21
,
Mig19
,
CD21
]

for drawing points from the Christoffel measure
\frac{1}{n}|\varphi(x)|^{2}d\mu(x)
in relevant
multivariate settings.

In the case of Algorithm
1
, as
{\bm{M}}={\bm{Z}}_{i}+\gamma{\bm{I}}/n
is positive semi-definite,
it suffices to draw candidate
points
x
from the Christoffel measure,
and to accept them with probability

\frac{\varphi(x)^{*}{\bm{M}}\varphi(x)}{\lambda_{\max}({\bm{M}})|\varphi(x)|^{2}}\leqslant 1.

The probability of accepting a point is

\int_{x\in X}\frac{\varphi(x)^{*}{\bm{M}}\varphi(x)}{\lambda_{\max}({\bm{M}})|\varphi(x)|^{2}}\frac{|\varphi(x)|^{2}}{n}d\mu(x)=\frac{\Tr({\bm{M}})}{n\lambda_{\max}({\bm{M}})}\geqslant\frac{1}{n},

therefore we need in average
n\lambda_{\max}({\bm{M}})/\Tr({\bm{M}})\leqslant n
draws from the Christoffel measure to find
x_{i}
.

In the case of Algorithm
1
,
{\bm{M}}={\bm{W}}_{i}
may have negative eigenvalues, so the bound
\lambda_{\max}({\bm{M}})\leqslant\Tr({\bm{M}})
may not hold.
In order to draw a sample from the probability density

{R_{i}(x)}/{\Gamma_{i}}
, we can still rely on acceptance-rejection from
the Christoffel measure, and accept points with probability

\frac{R_{i}(x)}{\lambda_{\max}({\bm{W}}_{i})|\varphi(x)|^{2}}\in[0,1].

The acceptance probability is then

\int_{x\in X}\frac{R_{i}(x)}{\lambda_{\max}({\bm{W}}_{i})|\varphi(x)|^{2}}\frac{|\varphi(x)|^{2}}{n}d\mu(x)=\frac{\Gamma_{i}}{n\lambda_{\max}({\bm{W}}_{i})}\geqslant\frac{(1-\kappa)(1-\delta)^{2}}{n}

in view of Remark
4.2
, and we need to at most
n(1-\kappa)^{-1}(1-\delta)^{-2}
candidate points in average.
When
\kappa=1
, inspection of (
18
) and (
17
)
respectively shows that

\Gamma_{i}\geqslant\Tr({\bm{W}}_{i})-\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}\quad\text{and}\quad\Tr({\bm{W}}_{i})\geqslant\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}\Tr({\bm{Z}}_{i}),

so that

\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}\Gamma_{i}\geqslant\Tr({\bm{Z}}_{i})-1=\delta\Tr({\bm{Y}}_{i}{\bm{Z}}_{i})\geqslant\delta\Tr({\bm{Y}}_{i}^{2})\geqslant\frac{\delta}{n}\Tr({\bm{Y}}_{i})^{2}=\frac{\delta}{n},

hence we can replace the factor
1-\kappa
in the acceptance probability by a factor
\delta/n
.

Remark 5.2
.

In the first iterations
1\leqslant i<n
, the maximal eigenvalue of
{\bm{M}}
has multiplicity at least
n-i
, so the bound on the acceptance probability is
n-i
times higher. This implies for instance that there are in average less than 2 (respectively, less than
2(1-\kappa)^{-1}(1-\delta)^{-2}
) rejections for
i=1,\dots,n/2
in Algorithm
1
(respectively, Algorithm
1
), which we observe in practice.

Even with such methods, the computational complexity remains dominated by the sampling step: Algorithm
1
runs in
{\cal O}(mn^{3}t)
time, where the factor
m
comes
from the number of iterations,
a factor
n^{2}
from the matrix-vector multiplication in the evaluation of
\rho_{i}
, the last factor
n

from the average number of rejections,
and
t
is the time needed to generate a point with the Christoffel density. The linear algebra takes
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
time per iteration, hence
\mathcal{O}(mn^{3})
time in total. Similarly, Algorithm
1
runs in
{\cal O}\left({mn^{3}t}(1-\kappa)^{-1}(1-\delta)^{-2}\right)
time, the only
difference coming from the bound on the acceptance ratio. Therefore, applying our sampling
strategies may prove quite challenging for large values of
n
.

Remark 5.3
.

In the deterministic setting, it is not necessary to draw each point
x_{i}
with a density proportional to
\rho_{i}(x)
: the only requirement is that

w_{i}(x_{i})\geqslant\kappa\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}

for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant m
.
The idea of using the algorithm from
[
BSS09
]
, still in a deterministic context, but with an infinite set
X
, seems to originate in
[
DPS+21
]
, which investigates Marcinkiewicz-type discretization theorems. However, it seems that searching for
x_{i}
by rejection sampling works better in practice than sorting
X
and looking for the first point that achieves the above condition
[
BSU23
]
.

We
next comment
on the relatively limited importance of the weights. In Algorithm
1
, the sampling density decomposes as

\frac{\rho_{i}(x)}{\Xi_{i}}=(1-p)\,\frac{\varphi(x)^{*}{\bm{Z}}_{i}\varphi(x)}{\Tr({\bm{Z}}_{i})}+p\,\frac{|\varphi(x)|^{2}}{n},

with
p={\gamma}/\Xi_{i}
.
In others words, we are sampling
from a mixture of effective resistance and Christoffel density. Here we only know that
s_{i}|\varphi(x_{i})|^{2}\leqslant{n\eta}/{\gamma}
,
which by the way yields

\lambda_{\max}({\bm{A}}_{m})\leqslant\Tr({\bm{A}}_{m})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}s_{i}|\varphi(x_{i})|^{2}\leqslant\frac{mn\eta}{\gamma}.

(21)

But
there is no
a priori uniform bound on the weights. If we desire such a bound, it suffices to add a third, constant term in the sampling density:

\frac{\rho_{i}(x)}{\Xi_{i}}=\frac{\Tr({\bm{Z}}_{i})}{\Xi_{i}}\frac{\varphi(x)^{*}{\bm{Z}}_{i}\varphi(x)}{\Tr({\bm{Z}}_{i})}+\frac{\gamma}{\Xi_{i}}\frac{|\varphi(x)|^{2}}{n}+\frac{\gamma_{\infty}}{\Xi_{i}}

for a new parameter
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma_{\infty}>0}
, where we redefine
\Xi_{i}
as
\Tr({\bm{Z}}_{i})+\gamma+\gamma_{\infty}
. To choose a point according to this density, one can simply draw it from

d\mu(x)
with probability
\gamma_{\infty}/\Xi_{i}
, from the Christoffel measure with probability

\gamma/\Xi_{i}
, and from the effective resistance measure otherwise. This choice immediately
yields
s_{i}=\eta/\rho_{i}(x_{i})\leqslant\eta/\gamma_{\infty}
, however taking
\gamma_{\infty}>0
deteriorates a
bit the other estimates, which is why we did not include it earlier.

Having a uniform bound on the weights
s_{i}
is particularly important in case of noisy samples, where one can only observe
f(x_{i})+\varepsilon_{i}
for some random
\varepsilon_{i}
. Then the estimates of Theorem
1.1
hold with an additional noise term of the form

\|{\bm{L}}^{+}(\sqrt{s_{i}}\varepsilon_{i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant m}\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant\lambda_{\min}({\bm{A}}_{m})^{-1}\frac{\eta}{\gamma_{\infty}}\|(\varepsilon_{i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant m}\|_{2}^{2},

due the definition of the weighted least-squares estimator in Section
2
. We refer to Theorems 5.3 and 5.19 of
[
ABW22
]
for details.

Concerning Algorithm
1
, in the deterministic setting, one can replace each weight
s_{i}
by its upper bound
\frac{\delta}{\kappa(1-\delta)}
, resulting in an unweighted discrete norm

\|g\|_{m}^{2}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}|g(x_{i})|^{2},

without changing our estimates. We refer to
[
BSU23
]
for earlier results on subsampling of frames with unweighted discrete norms.
In any case, in numerical experiments, the weights seem to have lesser importance than the position of the points.

6
Numerical experiments

We conclude with a few illustrations in the space of multivariate polynomials, focusing on a function whose best approximation is explicitly known.
Our code is available at the following address:

github.com/Belloliva/minimal-oversampling-for-multivariate-polynomial-least-squares
.

In the univariate setting, the Legendre polynomials
(L_{k})_{k\geqslant 0}
are orthonormal in
L^{2}([-1,1],dx/2)
,
and satisfy

\|L_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}}=L_{k}(1)=\sqrt{2k+1}.

We recall that their generating
function is given by

g_{y}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{L_{k}(x)}{L_{k}(1)}\,y^{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-2xy+y^{2}}},\quad x\in[-1,1],\quad y\in(-1,1).

Now, on the domain
X=[-1,1]^{d}
with
d\in\mathbb{N}
, one can consider tensorized Legendre polynomials

L_{{\bm{k}}}({\bm{x}}):=\prod_{j=1}^{d}L_{k_{j}}(x_{j}),\qquad{\bm{x}}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in X,\quad{\bm{k}}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{d})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d},

which are orthonormal for the uniform measure
d\mu({\bm{x}})=d{\bm{x}}/2^{d}
over
X
.

Fixing
{\bm{y}}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})\in(0,1)^{d}
, we would like to approximate
the multivariate function

{\bm{x}}\mapsto g_{\bm{y}}({\bm{x}})=\prod_{j=1}^{d}g_{y_{j}}(x_{j})=\sum_{{\bm{k}}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}c_{\bm{k}}L_{{\bm{k}}}({\bm{x}}),\qquad c_{\bm{k}}=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\frac{y_{j}^{k_{j}}}{\sqrt{2k_{j}+1}}.

This is a typical representer of holomorphic functions on
X
with
anisotropic dependance on
{\bm{x}}
parameterized by
{\bm{y}}
.
Its
L^{2}(X,\mu)
norm is analytically given by

\|g_{\bm{y}}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\int_{-1}^{1}|g_{y_{j}}(x_{j})|^{2}\frac{dx_{j}}{2}=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{2y_{j}}\ln\left(\frac{1+y_{j}}{1-y_{j}}\right).

Moreover, the best approximation space of dimension
n
for
g_{\bm{y}}
w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}}
is

V_{n}=\Span\{L_{\bm{k}},\;{\bm{k}}\in\Lambda_{n}\},

where
\Lambda_{n}
is the set of
n
multi-indices
{\bm{k}}
with largest values of
c_{\bm{k}}
.
Note that
\Lambda_{n}
is lower and is easily computed, since the coefficients
c_{\bm{k}}

are decreasing for the component-wise order,
and that the approximation error is

\min_{v\in V_{n}}\|g_{\bm{y}}-v\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\Big\|g_{\bm{y}}-\sum_{{\bm{k}}\in\Lambda_{n}}c_{\bm{k}}L_{\bm{k}}\Big\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\|g_{\bm{y}}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\sum_{{\bm{k}}\in\Lambda_{n}}c_{\bm{k}}^{2}.

(22)

Remark 6.2
.

In view of the discussion from Section
5
, the last three schemes necessitate sampling from
Christoffel measure, which can be efficiently implemented by
first drawing
{\bm{k}}
uniformly in
\Lambda_{n}
, and then drawing
{\bm{x}}
from the measure

|L_{\bm{k}}({\bm{x}})|^{2}d\mu({\bm{y}})=\prod_{j=1}^{d}|L_{k_{j}}(x_{j})|^{2}\frac{dx_{j}}{2}.

For the second step, exploiting the product structure of
L_{\bm{k}}
, it suffices to draw each component
x_{j}
independently from

|L_{k_{j}}(x)|^{2}\frac{dx}{2}
.
When
k_{j}=0
, this is just the uniform measure over
[-1,1]
.
Otherwise, we rely once more on acceptance/rejection from the arcsine
measure, that is from the cosine of uniform points. Thanks to the so-called Berstein inequality for Legendre
polynomials

\frac{|L_{k}(x)|^{2}}{2}\leqslant\frac{2k+1}{k}\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{1-x^{2}}},

the acceptance probability is at least
k_{j}/(2k_{j}+1)\geqslant 1/3
for
k_{j}\geqslant 1
, so there are at most
2\supp({\bm{k}})\leqslant 2\min(n,d)
rejections in average. As a conclusion, the time needed to generate a point from the Christoffel probability measure is
t=\mathcal{O}(d\min(n,d))
.
We refer to Section 5 of
[
CM17
]
, as well as
[
CD21
]
and
[
Mig21
]
, for an overview of efficient sampling strategies on more general domains.
