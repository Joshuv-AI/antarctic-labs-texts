On the Weisfeiler algorithm of depth- 1 1 stabilization
Gang Chen, Qing Ren, Ilia Ponomarenko

Abstract
. An origin of the multidimensional Weisfeiler-Leman algorithm goes back to a refinement procedure of deep stabilization, introduced by B. Weisfeiler in a paper included in the collective monograph “On construction and identification of graphs” (1976). This procedure is recursive and the recursion starts from an algorithm of depth- 1 1 stabilization, which has never been discussed in the literature. A goal of the present paper is to show that a simplified algorithm of the depth- 1 1 stabilization has the same power as the 3 3 -dimensional Weisfeiler-Leman algorithm. It is proved that the class of coherent configurations obtained at the output of this simplified algorithm coincides with the class introduced earlier by the third author. As an application we also prove that if there exist at least two nonisomorphic projective planes of order q q , then the Weisfeiler-Leman dimension of the incidence graph of any projective plane of order q q is at least 4 4 .



1.
Introduction

In 1968, B. Weisfeiler and A. Leman proposed an algorithm
\WL
for testing graph isomorphism
[
19
]
. The idea of the
\WL
is as follows: analyzing the triples of vertices of a graph
{\cal G}
with vertex set
\Omega
, construct a canonical partition
\WL({\cal G})
of the Cartesian square
\Omega^{2}
, such that pairs from different classes lie in different orbits of the induced action of the group
\aut({\cal G})
on
\Omega^{2}
. It should be noted that the goal (as Weisfeiler later wrote) was to construct an automorphic partition of
{\cal G}
, i.e., a partition of
\Omega
into the orbits of the group
\aut({\cal G})
. A year later, it became clear that the constructed algorithm did not achieve this goal
[
1
]
.

It can be seen from the 1968 paper that the authors understood the need to include individualization of vertices in the “right” algorithm. Probably, this was fully realized in the paper by Weisfeiler, included by him in the collective monograph
[
18
, Section O]
, where he introduced the concept of deep stabilization. Again, the goal was to find the automorphic partition of
{\cal G}
, but here the individualization of the vertices of
{\cal G}
has already explicitly been used. The number of individualized vertices is just the depth of the algorithm
\Ws_{m}
of
depth-
m
stabilization
proposed by Weisfeiler. The algorithm starts with
\WL
and calls the algorithm
W_{m-1}
(
m\geq 2
) recursively, followed by a depth-
1
stabilization (the exact definitions can also be found in Section
4
). It was conjectured that if
m
is at least
O(\log n)
, then the depth-
m
stabilization determines the automorphic partition of
any graph of order
n
. Furthermore, it was proved that depth-
1
stabilization determines this partition in some nontrivial special cases
[
18
, Section P]
.

The algorithm now known as the multidimensional Weisfeiler-Leman algorithm was used by many
researchers in the late 1970s and early 1980s, see
[
2
]
. The
m
-dimensional version
\WL_{m}
of this algorithm (the
m
-dim WL
) analyzes
(m+1)
-tuples of vertices, but the algorithm itself is non-recursive and does not explicitly use individualization of the vertices of input graph (note that
\WL_{2}=\WL
). This algorithm has been extensively studied in the context of the graph isomorphism problem and descriptive complexity theory (see
[
12
]
) and culminated in its use in the Babai quasipolynomial algorithm
[
2
]
. At the same time, it became clear that the
\WL_{m}
cannot determine the automorphic partition of some graphs
{\cal G}
even if
m=cn
for an absolute constant
c

[
4
]
(see also
[
8
]
).

One of the main goals of the present paper is to compare the power of the depth-
1
stabilization
\Ws=\Ws_{1}
and the
m
-dim WL. It should be noted that these algorithms being applied to a graph
{\cal G}
have different outputs (for
m\geq 3
). The algorithm

constructs a partition
\Ws({\cal G})
of the set
\Omega^{2}
, that is a

in the sense of D. Higman
[
14
]
(or stationary graph in terms of B. Weisfeiler and A. Leman), whereas the second one constructs the partition
\WL_{m}({\cal G})
of the Cartesian power

, which is an
m
-ary coherent configuration

For
m=2
, this is an ordinary coherent configuration.
in terms of L. Babai
[

]
(see also
[

). A natural way to compare the outputs is to consider the projection
\pr_{2}\WL_{m}({\cal G})
of the
m
-ary coherent configuration
\WL_{m}({\cal G})
to the first two coordinates, which is an ordinary coherent configuration. Our first result states that in this sense the power of

\pr_{2}\WL_{3}({\cal G})\leq\Ws({\cal G})\leq\pr_{2}\WL_{4}({\cal G})

The exact definition of the depth-
1
stabilization is quite cumbersome, see Section
4

1
stabilization, which was actually studied by B. Weisfeiler in more detail, and the output of which is also a coherent configuration (in general, smaller than that in the full version of the depth-
1
stabilization), denoted below by

can be thought as the output of the Weisfeiler-Leman algorithm applied to the graph

in which any pair of vertices is colored by a multiset of colors of this pair in each coherent configuration

,
\alpha\in\Omega
, where

in

Theorem 1.1
.

For every colored graph
{\cal G}
, we have

(2)

\WL_{3}({\cal G})=\WL_{3}(\WLD({\cal G}))\quad\text{and}\quad\pr_{2}\WL_{3}({\cal G})=\WLD({\cal G}).

Although our informal definition of
\WLD({\cal G})
is given for colored graphs, the concept of coloring is not necessary. Indeed, the coherent configurations which are stable with respect to
\WLD
are
sesquiclosed
in the sense of
[
17

, and, moreover,
\WLD({\cal G})
is just the intersection of all coherent configurations on

express some relations between
3
-ary and
2
-ary coherent configurations.

On the other hand, the concept of coloring is essential if one is interested in applying the depth-
m
stabilization and the
m
-dim
\WL
to test isomorphism directly, without computing an automorphic partition. In this case, it is said that (a) graphs
{\cal G}
and
{\cal G}^{\prime}
are
\WL_{m}
-equivalent
(or not distinguished by the algorithm

) if the colorings of the partitions
\WL_{m}({\cal G})

\WL_{m}

identifies

\WL_{m}
-equivalent to
{\cal G}
is isomorphic to
{\cal G}
. In a similar way, one can define the
\WLD
-equivalence and
\WLD
-identification.

Theorem 1.2
.

Two graphs are
\WL_{3}
-equivalent if and only if they are
\WLD
-equivalent. Moreover, the
\WL_{3}
and
\WLD
identify the same graphs.

The structure of the colorings used in the above definitions is quite complicated, for example, the coloring constructed by the
\WL_{m}
contains full information about the intersection numbers of the corresponding
m
-ary coherent configuration. From algebraic point of view, the colorings of two
m
-ary coherent configurations are equal if and only if the color preserving bijection between their color classes is an algebraic isomorphism between these coherent configurations (for
m=2
, an algebraic isomorphism is what was called a weak equivalence in
[
18
]
). The proof of Theorem
1.2
is given just in terms of the algebraic isomorphisms. In this language, the algorithm
\WL
identifies a graph
{\cal G}
if and only if the coherent configuration
\WL({\cal G})
is separable, i.e., every algebraic isomorphism from it is induced by a (combinatorial) isomorphism
[
11

. In particular, it gives a method to prove that a given graph has the
Weisfeiler-Leman dimension
at most
2
, where the Weisfeiler-Leman dimension of a graph
{\cal G}
is the smallest
m
for which the
\WL_{m}
identifies
{\cal G}
.

In general, not every algebraic isomorphism of a coherent configuration respects the property of being sesquiclosed. In this connection, it is quite natural to consider sesquiclosed algebraic isomorphisms, i.e., those that can be extended to algebraic isomorphisms of one-point extensions. The graphs
{\cal G}
of the Weisfeiler-Leman dimension at most
3
can be characterized as those for which the coherent configuration
\WLD({\cal G})
is
sesquiseparable
in the sense that every sesquiclosed algebraic isomorphism from it is induced by an isomorphism. As an immediate consequence of Theorem
1.2
we obtain the following statement.

Corollary 1.3
.

The Weisfeiler-Leman dimension of a graph
{\cal G}
is at most
3
if and only if the coherent configuration
\WLD({\cal G})
is sesquiseparable.

To illustrate the above concepts, we consider the incidence graphs of finite projective planes. On the first glance, the results obtained in
[
10
]
make impression that the
\WL_{3}
cannot distinguish these graphs. Actually, this is true but a direct deduction does not give a nontrivial lower bound on the Weisfeiler-Leman dimension. On the other hand, the technique developed in the present paper enables us to do this.

Theorem 1.4
.

The incidence graphs of finite projective planes of the same order are
\WLD
-equivalent.

Theorems
1.4
and
1.1
show that any two incidence graphs of finite projective planes of the same order
q
are
\WL_{3}
-equivalent. Thus the Weisfeiler-Leman dimension of any such graph is at least
4
if there are at least two nonisomorphic projective planes of order
q
(which is the case for infinitely many prime powers
q
).

Corollary 1.5
.

Let
q
be a positive integer such that there are at least two nonisomorphic projective planes of order
q
. Then the Weisfeiler-Leman dimension of the incidence graph of every finite projective plane of order
q
is at least
4
.

2.1.
Notation

Throughout the paper,
\Omega
denotes a finite set. The
i
th coordinate of an
m
-tuple
x\in\Omega^{m}
is denoted by
x_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,m
. The concatenation of
x
and

is defined to be the
(m+k)
-tuple
x\cdot y=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m},y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})
.

For
\Delta\subseteq\Omega
, the Cartesian product
\Delta\times\Delta
and its diagonal are denoted by
{\bf 1}_{\Delta}
and
1_{\Delta}
, respectively; for
\delta\in\Delta
, we abbreviate
1_{\delta}=1_{\{\delta\}}
. For a relation

, we set
s^{*}=\{(\alpha,\beta):(\beta,\alpha)\in s\}
,
\alpha s=\{\beta\in\Omega:\ (\alpha,\beta)\in s\}
for all
\alpha\in\Omega
. For relations

, we put
r\cdot s=\{(\alpha,\beta)\!:\ (\alpha,\gamma)\in r,\ (\gamma,\beta)\in s
for some

.
For any set
S
of relations, we denote by

the set of all unions of elements of
S
. For any mapping
f:\Omega\to\Omega^{\prime}
, we set
s^{f}=\{(\alpha^{f},\beta^{f}):\ (\alpha,\beta)\in s\}
.

The partitions of the same set are partially ordered in a standard way:

if each class of

is a union of some classes of

, or equivalently,
S^{\cup}\subseteq T^{\cup}
. The join of

and

is defined as a uniquely determined partition

. For any set

of partitions of the same set, that is closed with respect to the join, one can define a mapping

from the set of all partitions of this set to

, that takes

to the join of all
T\in{\cal S}
such that

. This mapping is a closure operator in the sense that for each partition
S
, we have
{\mathfrak{c}}(S)\geq S
,
{\mathfrak{c}}({\mathfrak{c}}(S))={\mathfrak{c}}(S)
, and
{\mathfrak{c}}(S)\geq{\mathfrak{c}}(T)
if

3
]
.

2.2.
Rainbows

Let
S
be a partition of
\Omega^{2}
. A pair
{\cal X}=(\Omega,S)
is called a

on
\Omega
if

(C1)

1_{\Omega}\in S^{\cup}
,

(C2)

s^{*}\in S
for all
s\in S
.

The numbers
|\Omega|
and
\rk({\cal X})=|S|
are called the
degree
and
rank
of
{\cal X}
, respectively. The elements of
S
and of
S^{\cup}
are called
basis relations
and
relations
of
{\cal X}
. A unique basis relation containing the pair
(\alpha,\beta)
is denoted by
r(\alpha,\beta)=r_{\cal X}(\alpha,\beta)
. The set of all relations of
{\cal X}
is closed with respect to intersections and unions. Any
\Delta\subseteq\Omega
for which
1_{\Delta}\in S
is called a
fiber
of
{\cal X}
; the set of all of them is denoted by

. In view of condition (C1), the set

\Omega

of
{\cal X}
.

Let

be a rainbow. A

{\cal X}
to

is defined to be a bijection

such that

for all

. In this case, the rainbows
{\cal X}
and
{\cal X}^{\prime}
are said to be
isomorphic
. The group of all isomorphisms of
{\cal X}
to itself contains a normal subgroup

\aut({\cal X})=\{f\in\sym(\Omega):\ s^{f}=s\text{ for all }s\in S\}

called the

of
{\cal X}
.

The partial order of the partitions of
\Omega^{2}
induces a partial order of the rainbows on
\Omega
. Namely, given two such rainbows
{\cal X}
and
{\cal Y}
, we set

{\cal X}\leq{\cal Y}\ \Leftrightarrow\ S({\cal X})^{\cup}\subseteq S({\cal Y})^{\cup}

and say that
{\cal X}
is an
fusion
of
{\cal Y}
and
{\cal Y}
is an
extension
of
{\cal X}
. The minimal and maximal elements with respect to this order are the
trivial
and
discrete
rainbows, respectively; in the former case,
S
consists of
1_{\Omega}
and its complement (unless
\Omega
is a singleton), and in the last case,

consists of singletons. The

of rainbows

and
{\cal Y}
is defined as a uniquely determined rainbow

such that
S({\cal X}\cap{\cal Y})=S({\cal X})\vee S({\cal Y})
, or equivalently,

S({\cal X}\cap{\cal Y})^{\cup}=S({\cal X})^{\cup}\,\cap\,S({\cal Y})^{\cup}.

A

from
{\cal X}
to
{\cal X}^{\prime}
is defined to be a bijection
\varphi:S\rightarrow S^{\prime}
such that for all

we have
\varphi(s)\subseteq 1_{\Omega^{\prime}}\ \Leftrightarrow\ s\subseteq 1_{\Omega}
, and
\varphi(s^{*})=\varphi(s)^{*}
. In this case,
{\cal X}
and
{\cal X}^{\prime}
are said to be
similar
. Extending

in a natural way to bijections
S^{\cup}\to{S^{\prime}}{\phantom{x}\hskip-5.69054pt{}^{\cup}}
and
F^{\cup}\to{F^{\prime}}{\phantom{x}\hskip-5.69054pt{}^{\cup}}
, denoted also by

\varphi(1_{\Omega})=1_{\Omega^{\prime}}\quad\text{and}\quad\Omega^{\varphi}=\Omega^{\prime}.

Every isomorphism
f:{\cal X}\to{\cal X}^{\prime}

induces
the similarity
\varphi_{f}:{\cal X}\to{\cal X}^{\prime},

s\mapsto s^{f}
. A similarity
\varphi
is induced by an isomorphism
f
if it holds that
\varphi=\varphi_{f}
. The identity similarity of
{\cal X}
is denoted by
\id_{\cal X}
. A similarity
\psi
from a rainbow
{\cal Y}\geq{\cal X}

extends

.

2.3.
Coherent configurations

A rainbow
{\cal X}
is called a
coherent configuration
if it satisfies the additional condition

(C3)

given
r,s,t\in S
, the
intersection number

c_{r,s}^{t}=|\alpha r\cap\beta s^{*}|
does not depend on the choice of
(\alpha,\beta)\in t
.

For any basis relation
t\in S
such that
t\subseteq 1_{\Omega}
, the intersection number
c_{s,s^{*}}^{t}
is either zero or is equal to
|\alpha s|
for all
\alpha
such that
\alpha s\neq\varnothing
; in the latter case, the number
n_{s}=|\alpha s|
is called the
valency
of

. Every basis relation

is contained in the Cartesian product of two uniquely determined fibers which are called the
left and right supports
of
s
, respectively.

For homogeneity sets
\Delta
and
\Gamma
, we denote by
S_{\Delta,\Gamma}
the set of all basis relations contained in
\Delta\times\Gamma
, and abbreviate
S_{\Delta}=S_{\Delta,\Delta}
. The rainbow
{\cal X}_{\Delta}=(\Delta,S_{\Delta})
is a coherent configuration called the
restriction
of
{\cal X}
to
\Delta
. The coherent configuration
{\cal X}
is said to be
homogeneous
or a
scheme
if
|F|=1
, or equivalently,
1_{\Omega}\in S
.

Let
G\leq\sym(\Omega)
be a permutation group. Denote by
S
the set of all orbits
(\alpha,\beta)^{G}
in the componentwise action of
G
on
\Omega\times\Omega
, where
\alpha,\beta\in\Omega
. Then the pair

\inv(G)=\inv(G,\Omega)=(\Omega,S)

is a coherent configuration. Any coherent configuration
{\cal X}
associated with some permutation group

in this way is said to be

. In this case every fiber
\Delta
of
{\cal X}
is an orbit of

, and the points of
\Delta
can be identified with the right cosets of a point stabilizer

with

, so that the action of

is

if and only if
{\cal X}=\inv(\aut({\cal X}))
.

Let
{\cal X}
and
{\cal X}^{\prime}
be coherent configurations. A bijection
\varphi:S\rightarrow S^{\prime}
,
r\mapsto r^{\prime}
is called an
algebraic isomorphism
from
{\cal X}
to
{\cal X}^{\prime}
if

c_{r^{\prime},s^{\prime}}^{t^{\prime}}=c_{r,s}^{t}\quad\text{for all}\ r,s,t\in S.

Every algebraic isomorphism of coherent configurations is also a similarity of the corresponding rainbows. Furthermore,
\varphi
preserves the degree, rank, and supports of basis relations, i.e., if
\Delta,\Gamma
are fibers of
{\cal X}
, then
r\in S_{\Delta,\Gamma}
if and only if
r^{\prime}\in S_{\Delta^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime}}
.

Not every algebraic isomorphism is induced by an isomorphism. The coherent configuration
{\cal X}
is said to be
separable
if every algebraic isomorphism from
{\cal X}
is induced by an isomorphism. Any trivial or discrete coherent configuration is separable.

2.4.
Coherent closure

The set of all coherent configurations on
\Omega
is closed with respect to intersection. The

of a set

is defined to be the intersection
\WL(T)
of all coherent configurations

on
\Omega
, for which

. The notation reflects the fact that

can be computed by the algorithm

When

for some rainbow

on
\Omega

and abbreviate

Let
{\cal X}
and
{\cal X}^{\prime}
be rainbows and

Let

,

. An algebraic isomorphism

The

-extension of

If

. Note that it exists only if

, where

(respectively,
\Delta^{\prime}
) is the fiber of

(respectively,

).

When

and

Let

\text{{\bf while }}{\cal X}<\sigma_{i}({\cal X})\text{ for some }i\in I\text{ {\bf do} }{\cal X}:=\sigma_{i}({\cal X})\text{ {\bf od}}

It should be noted that our description differs somewhat from Weisfeiler’s, since he represented coherent configurations as matrices whose elements are (pairwise non-commuting) variables; in particular, the relation
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{{}_{i}}}}{{\sim}}
was not calculated explicitly, but was presented implicitly, in the form of equalities of the coefficients of some polynomials in these variables.

Some properties of the coherent configurations closed with respect to the depth-

Summarizing the above, we note a difference between the Weisfeiler deep stabilization algorithm and the modern multidimensional Weisfeiler-Leman algorithm. Namely, the first one does not construct an explicit coloring of the Cartesian power of the underlying set (although such a coloring can easily be defined, see Section
6

In accordance with

(S2)

The conditions (S1) and (S2) are independent. Indeed, if

and

are rank
3
graphs, and it does not satisfy condition (S2), because any one-point extension of

is separable (see the proof of
[
16

) and hence the identity algebraic isomorphism
\id_{\cal X}
has no the
\alpha\alpha^{\prime}
-extension, where
\alpha
is a vertex of
{\cal G}
and
\alpha^{\prime}
is a vertex of
{\cal G}^{\prime}

Now, let

be the scheme of the Shrikhande graph, see e.g.

is a scheme of rank

, and a straightforward computation shows that any one-point extension of

has exactly

cannot satisfy condition (S1).
