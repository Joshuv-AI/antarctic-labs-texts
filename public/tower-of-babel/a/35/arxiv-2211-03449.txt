How to Coordinate Edge Devices for Over-the-Air Federated Learning?
Mohammad Ali Sedaghat 1 , Ali Bereyhi 2 , Saba Asaad 2 , and Ralf R. Müller 2

Abstract
This work studies the task of device coordination in wireless networks for over-the-air federated learning (OTA-FL). For conventional metrics of aggregation error, the task is shown to describe the zero-forcing (ZF) and minimum mean squared error (MMSE) schemes and reduces to the NP-hard problem of subset selection. We tackle this problem by studying properties of the optimal scheme. Our analytical results reveal that this scheme is found by searching among the leaves of a tree with favorable monotonic features. Invoking these features, we develop a low-complexity algorithm that approximates the optimal scheme by tracking a dominant path of the tree sequentially. Our numerical investigations show that the proposed algorithm closely tracks the optimal scheme.

Introduction

Federated learning (FL) is a privacy-preserving framework for distributed learning that enables edge devices to address a learning task, e.g., training a global model, jointly without need for sharing their local data
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. This ensures device privacy and provides a joint scheme for learning that performs considerably better than individual distributed learning while keeping the communication load of the system tractable
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
.

The promising learning performance in FL comes along with several challenges which are roughly divided into the three categories of
privacy
,
statistical properties of local datasets
and
communication-efficiency
. With respect to privacy, FL has been shown to be vulnerable against model inversion attack
[
7
,
8
]
. This follows from the fact that edge devices in FL share their local models that are often strongly correlated with their local data
[
9
,
10
]
. Statistical properties of local datasets further refers to the sensitivity of FL against data distribution. Initial studies on FL have shown promising performance and fast convergence of this framework for homogeneous local datasets. Nevertheless, obtaining these favorable behaviors have been shown to face some analytical challenges when we deviate from simple statistical models for local datasets
[
11
]
. This is in particular crucial, as the exact statistical model of data is rather unknown in practice.

This study focuses on the latter class of challenges in FL, i.e.,
communication-efficiency
, that refers to communication-related challenges, when the FL framework is to be implemented in a wireless network. In fact, FL schemes require several rounds of communication between a central node, often called the parameter server (PS), and the edge devices. This is in particular challenging when the scheme runs over remote wireless devices introducing various sources of imperfection to the setting, e.g., rate-limited communication links and noisy assessment of local model parameters
[
12
,
13
,

]
.

I-A

Over-the-Air Federated Learning

FL performs distributed learning in three main phases:

1.

Starting with an initial global model, edge devices learn a model locally from their datasets. They then share the local models with the PS.

2.

The PS collects the local models and
aggregates
them into a global model.

3.

The global model is sent back to the devices, and the devices repeat local learning via the new global model.

The PS and edge devices keep iterating these three phases for several communication rounds until they converge.

Classical approaches for implementation of FL in a wireless network suggest a separate decoding and aggregation at the PS
[
15
,
16
]
. This means that the PS decodes first the received local models and then combines the decoded models into a global model via a predefined
aggregation policy
. An alternative approach is proposed in
[
17
]
which invokes the idea of
analog function computation

[
18
]
to perform model aggregation directly over the air. We refer to this latter approach as over-the-air FL (OTA-FL) in this paper. OTA-FL invokes the signal combination in the multiple access channel to realize the predefined aggregation policy directly over-the-air. In other words, the edge devices in OTA-FL transmit their local models with proper scaling, such the desired model aggregation is calculated from the a linearly-transformed version of the received signal at the PS. In various use-cases, OTA-FL is more efficient than separate decoding and aggregation
[
19
]
. In fact, OTA-FL does not need to orthogonalize communication resources, i.e., allocate individual bandwidth and time slot to every device, and hence it enjoys both advantages of lower computation and resource efficiency
[
20
,
21
,
22
]
.

I-B

Contributions and Organization

As OTA-FL relies on the uplink channel for model aggregation,
coordination
plays a key role in its performance. In fact, the PS and devices need to agree on a communication protocol, such that the over-the-air aggregated model is as close as possible to the desired form. Prior studies often consider a basic approach for coordination that is known to be sub-optimal from the information theoretic point of view. In this work, we study the concept of coordination in OTA-FL in great details. In this respect, we first formulate an
optimal
coordination scheme for a given metric of aggregation error mathematically. We then show that using the conventional metric of mean squared error (MSE) for aggregation error, the
optimal
coordination scheme reduces to an MSE minimization problem, while the commonly-used strategy for coordination is simply zero-forcing. Our analyses show that both strategies reduce to the NP-hard problem of subset selection and hence deal with the same level of hardness. We tackle the problem of subset selection in both minimum MSE and zero-forcing schemes by studying the properties of the solution. Our investigations reveal that the solution in both cases can be found by searching over the leaves of a tree with some monotonic properties. In the light of these properties, we propose low-complexity algorithms which sequentially approximate the solution by tracking a dominant path towards leaves. Our simulation results show that the proposed tree-based algorithm can track the optimal performance very closely while enjoying a drastically lower computational complexity.

The remaining of this manuscript is organized as follows: We first formulate the problem concretely in Section
II
. Minimum MSE and zero-forcing coordination schemes are then illustrated in Sections
III
and
IV
, respectively. Sections
V
and
VI
investigates the analytical properties of the zero-forcing and minimum MSE schemes, respectively, and propose a tree-based algorithm for their approximation with low complexity. The validity of the derivations are confirmed through numerical investigation in Section
VII
. Finally, Section
VIII
concludes the paper.

II
Problem Formulation

A wireless network with
L
single-antenna edge devices and a PS is considered. The devices employ an FL scheme, e.g., federated averaging, to address a common learning task, e.g., training a given model, jointly over their distributed local datasets. The PS is equipped with an
N
-element array-antenna. Throughout the analyses, we assume that
N\geq L
. This assumption is however taken for the sake of brevity. The case of
N<L
is then discussed separately as a remark; see Remark
1
.

At the beginning of each coherence interval, the devices send predefined pilots in their uplink channels. The PS then estimates the channel state information (CSI) based on the received signals. For simplicity, we assume that the pilots are mutually orthogonal and that the estimation error is negligible. Hence, the PS knows the CSI perfectly. Throughout the analyses, we assume that the channels experience frequency-flat fading processes. This models transmission over a channel with a narrow frequency band. The derivations are in principle extendable to wide-band transmission in multi-carrier systems with frequency selective fading processes.

II-A

OTA-FL Setting

Following the FL scheme, each device determines its local model parameters from its local dataset. It then shares this local model with the PS through its uplink channel. Let
s_{\ell}
be the model parameter of device
\ell
that is to be shared with the PS in a given time-frequency slot. Device
\ell
transmits this parameter by applying a channel-dependent scaling coefficient
b_{\ell}
, i.e., it transmits

\displaystyle x_{\ell}=b_{\ell}s_{\ell}.

(1)

The local models
\left\{s_{\ell}\right\}
are assumed to be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) with mean zero and unit variance
1
1

1

Note that in practice
\left\{s_{\ell}\right\}
are unbiased versions of the model parameters, e.g., quantized gradient values, which for weakly-correlated local datasets fit into this model
[
23
]
.

satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}\left\{\left|x_{\ell}\right|^{2}\right\}=\left|b_{\ell}\right|^{2}\leq P.

(2)

The devices communicate over a fading Gaussian MAC. Hence, the PS receives
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
which is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathbf{h}_{\ell}x_{\ell}+\mathbf{n}.

(3)

Here,
\mathbf{h}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
denotes the complex uplink channel vector of device
\ell
, and
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
is additive white Gaussian noise with mean zero and variance
\sigma^{2}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{n}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\boldsymbol{0},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{N}\right)
.

The ultimate goal of the PS is to combine the local models according to a predefined strategy specified by the FL scheme. More precisely, the PS aims to determine

\displaystyle\theta=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\phi_{\ell}s_{\ell}

(4)

for some predefined coefficients
\left\{\phi_{\ell}\right\}
, and then share it with the devices through its downlink channels. To this end, the PS invokes the idea of OTA-FL and combines the local models directly over-the-air: it estimates the desired global model, i.e.,
\theta
, by combining the received signal elements. In other words, it employs a linear receiver
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
and determines an estimate of
\theta
as

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}=\mathbf{m}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathbf{m}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{h}_{\ell}x_{\ell}+\mathbf{m}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{n},

(5)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathbf{m}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{h}_{\ell}b_{\ell}s_{\ell}+\mathbf{m}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{n}.

(6)

It is readily to shown that by restricting the estimation at the PS to be linear, the OTA-FL scheme is optimal: there exists a receiver
\mathbf{m}
and scaling coefficients
b_{\ell}
whose corresponding estimate of global model, i.e.,
\hat{\theta}
in (
6
), recovers the estimate achieved by combining the optimal linear estimation of local models according to the predefined strategy in (
4
)
[
17
]
.

II-B

Aggregation Error of the OTA-FL Setting

As communication is carried out through a noisy network, the estimated global model
\hat{\theta}
contains some error as compared with the desired global model
\theta
. This error is often called
aggregation error
and can be evaluated in various respect. The classical approach in OTA-FL is to quantify the aggregation error in terms of the mean squared error (MSE).

Using the MSE as the metric, the aggregation error in this setting is given by

\displaystyle\epsilon\left(\mathbf{m},\left\{b_{\ell}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}\left\{\left|\hat{\theta}-\theta\right|^{2}\right\},

(7)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}\left\{\left|\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\left(\mathbf{m}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{h}_{\ell}b_{\ell}-\phi_{\ell}\right)s_{\ell}+\mathbf{m}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{n}\right|^{2}\right\},

(8)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\left|\mathbf{m}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{h}_{\ell}b_{\ell}-\phi_{\ell}\right|^{2}+\sigma^{2}\left\|\mathbf{m}\right\|^{2}.

(9)

The error expression can be compactly represented as

\displaystyle\epsilon\left(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{B}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\|\mathbf{B}\mathbf{H}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{m}-\boldsymbol{\phi}\right\|^{2}+\sigma^{2}\left\|\mathbf{m}\right\|^{2}

(10)

where
\mathbf{B}=\mathrm{Diag}\left\{b_{\ell}\right\}
,
\phi=\left[\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{L}\right]^{\mathsf{T}}
and
\mathbf{H}
is the uplink channel matrix, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}=\left[\mathbf{h}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{h}_{L}\right].

(11)

II-C

Coordination Schemes for the OTA-FL Setting

To establish FL in the network, the PS needs to coordinate the devices periodically: after the uplink training phase at the beginning of a coherence interval, the PS specifies the uplink scaling coefficients of the devices, i.e.,
\left\{b_{\ell}\right\}
, and the linear receiver
\mathbf{m}
. The devices are then informed about their scaling coefficients by a downlink transmission. Afterwards, throughout the coherence interval, the PS and devices carry out their communication according to the OTA-FL scheme described in Section
II-A
using the coefficients and receiver specified in the training phase. It is worth mentioning that the PS only shares the scaling coefficients with the devices and not the complete CSI.

In general, the optimality of a coordination strategy depends on the choice of metric that determines the quality of the FL scheme. A good approach is to consider the aggregation error as the metric and design the coordination strategy, such that the error is minimized
2
2

2

This is in particular effective, since the MSE of estimated global model directly affects the learning quality of the FL scheme
[
17
]
.
. In this case, the optimal coordination strategy is formulated as

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{m}^{\star},\mathbf{B}^{\star}\right)=\argmin_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{C}^{N},\left|b_{\ell}\right|^{2}\leq P}\epsilon\left(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{B}\right).

(12)

This is in fact a minimum MSE (MMSE) problem. We hence refer to this strategy as MMSE coordination. Due to its non-convexity, MMSE coordination is computationally intractable in a generic network.

An alternative approach for coordination is to use the idea of zero-forcing (ZF)
[
17
,
24
]
. In this approach, the PS ignores noise in the channel and coordinates the devices, such that the noise-free version of the estimated global model constructs the desired global model. From (
6
), this means that the PS finds
\mathbf{m}
and
\left\{b_{\ell}\right\}
, such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{h}_{\ell}b_{\ell}=\phi_{\ell}.

(13)

In this case, the aggregation error reduces to

\displaystyle\epsilon\left(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{B}\right)=\sigma^{2}\left\|\mathbf{m}\right\|^{2}.

(14)

The PS hence solves the following problem

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}^{\rm zf}=

\displaystyle\argmin_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}}\left\|\mathbf{m}\right\|^{2}

(15)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}\;\left|\frac{\phi_{\ell}}{\mathbf{m}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{h}_{\ell}}\right|^{2}\leq P\;\text{ for }\;\ell\in\left[L\right],

to find the receiver. The scaling coefficients
\left\{b_{\ell}\right\}
are then determined from (
13
) by replacing
\mathbf{m}
with
\mathbf{m}^{\rm zf}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle b_{\ell}^{\rm zf}=\frac{\phi_{\ell}}{\mathbf{h}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{m}^{\rm zf}}

(16)

In the sequel, we refer to this scheme as ZF coordination.

III
MMSE Coordination for OTA-FL

The design problem for MMSE coordination is non-convex and generally challenging to solve. Nevertheless, for some particular cases, the solution can be derived tractably. An instance is given in
[
25
]
, where the solution of a mathematically similar problem is derived for an uplink scenario with a single-antenna PS in closed form, despite its non-convexity. Motivated by this result, we present in the sequel some preliminary analyses. We show that the problem in the general case with a multi-antenna PS is more complicated and unlike the single-antenna case, the optimal MMSE scheme cannot be derived analytically. The analyses however give some important insights on MMSE coordination that pave the way for the proposed algorithms in next sections. We start the analyses by the following lemma:

\displaystyle\max_{\ell\in\left[L\right]}\left|\hat{b}_{\ell}\right|^{2}=P.

A new receiver is further constructed from

IV
ZF Coordination for OTA-FL

Subset Selection for ZF Coordination

The computational complexity of the subset selection problem grows exponentially with the number of devices: there are in total
2^{L}-1
possible choices

5

and

for the device subset. Thus, the complete search algorithm poses exponential complexity to the system. We hence look for a low-complexity approach to find a good choice for the device subset.

We start the derivations by defining the concept of a
feasible
setting for ZF coordination:

Definition 1
.

The receiver
\mathbf{m}
and coefficients
\left\{b_{\ell}\right\}
describe a feasible ZF setting, if
\left|b_{\ell}\right|^{2}\leq P
and
\mathbf{m}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{h}_{\ell}b_{\ell}=\phi_{\ell}
for
\ell\in\left[L\right]
.

A feasible ZF setting only applies ZF on the received signal, i.e., it sets the first term in the right hand side of (
9
) zero, and ignores the optimization in (
15
) that minimizes the aggregation error. In this respect, we can see the ZF coordination scheme as a feasible ZF setting with minimal aggregation error. It is further easy to show that a feasible ZF setting is uniquely specified by its set of devices transmitting with power
P
:

Lemma 6
.

Let
\mathbb{S}
denote the set of devices which transmit with power
P
in a feasible ZF setting with receiver
\mathbf{m}
and scaling coefficients
\left\{b_{\ell}\right\}
. Then,
\mathbf{m}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{P}}\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{S}}^{*}\left(\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{S}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{S}}^{*}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{\mathbb{S}},

(29)

and
b_{\ell}
is determined from (
13
) for
\ell\in\left[L\right]
.

Proof.

The proof takes exactly the same steps as in Lemma
5
. We hence skip the proof.
∎

Considering the definition, we now focus on the number of feasible ZF setting for coordination
C^{\mathrm{zf}}
. Following Lemmas
1
and
4
, it is readily concluded that

\displaystyle C^{\mathrm{zf}}\leq 2^{L}-1.

(30)

It is further easy to show that
C^{\mathrm{zf}}
can be considerably smaller than
2^{L}-1
: for a non-empty subset
\mathbb{S}\subseteq\left[L\right]
, determine
{\mathbf{m}}
and
b_{\ell}
from Lemma
6
. The scaling coefficients are however not guaranteed to satisfy the transmit power constraint. Thus, the described setting is not necessarily feasible for ZF coordination. This observation implies that not all the
2^{L}-1
settings determined by applying ZF over the choices of
\mathbb{S}
describe a feasible ZF setting, and hence
C^{\mathrm{zf}}
can be in general considerably smaller than
2^{L}-1
.

Considering the above behavior, an algorithmic approach for approximating the ZF scheme is to track down the feasible ZF settings (or at least a subset of them). If there are only a few of them, i.e.,
C^{\mathrm{zf}}
is small; then, the ZF coordination scheme is readily found by a finite-dimensional search. The following theorem describes the conditions under which the feasible ZF settings and the ZF scheme are explicitly found.

Theorem 1
.

Let
s
be index of the device whose channel norm is smallest, i.e.,

\displaystyle s=\argmin_{\ell\in\left[L\right]}\left\|\mathbf{h}_{\ell}\right\|,

(31)

and define
{\mathrm{g}}_{\ell}=\mathbf{h}_{s}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{h}_{\ell}/\phi_{\ell}
for
\ell\in\left[L\right]
. Assume that
\left|{\mathrm{g}}_{s}\right|\leq\left|{\mathrm{g}}_{\ell}\right|
for all
\ell\in\left[L\right]
with equality holding for devices in set
\mathbb{S}\subseteq\left[L\right]
. Then,
{\mathbb{D}^{{\mathrm{zf}}}}=\mathbb{S}
, i.e., only the device in
\mathbb{S}
transmit with the maximum power
P
. The ZF coordination scheme in this case is described with

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}^{\mathrm{zf}}=\frac{\phi_{s}\mathbf{h}_{s}^{*}}{\sqrt{P}\left\|\mathbf{h}_{s}\right\|^{2}}.

(32)

Proof.

We start the proof by showing that the given setting is feasible for ZF coordination. To this end, let
\mathbf{m}=\mathbf{m}^{\mathrm{zf}}
as given in the theorem and
b_{s}=\sqrt{P}
. We first note that

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{h}_{s}b_{s}=\phi_{s},

(33)

which satisfies the ZF constraint (
13
) for device
s
. For other devices, we apply ZF by setting

\displaystyle b_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{\phi_{\ell}}{\mathbf{m}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{h}_{\ell}}=\frac{\phi_{\ell}\left\|\mathbf{h}_{s}\right\|^{2}}{\phi_{s}\mathbf{h}_{s}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{h}_{\ell}}\sqrt{P}=\frac{{\mathrm{g}}_{s}}{{\mathrm{g}}_{\ell}}\sqrt{P}.

(34)

This is hence concluded that under the constraint
\left|{\mathrm{g}}_{s}\right|\leq\left|{\mathrm{g}}_{\ell}\right|
, this ZF setting is feasible. Moreover, it is concluded that in this case, all devices with
\left|{\mathrm{g}}_{\ell}\right|=\left|{\mathrm{g}}_{s}\right|
transmit with power
P
.

We now show that the described feasible ZF setting is in fact the ZF scheme with minimal aggregation error. To this end, let
\mathbf{m}^{\mathrm{zf}}
and
\left\{b_{\ell}^{\mathrm{zf}}\right\}
denote the receiver and scaling coefficients of the ZF scheme, respectively. We decompose
\mathbf{m}^{\mathrm{zf}}
in terms of its projection on
\mathbf{h}_{s}^{*}
and its component in the null space of
\mathbf{h}_{s}^{*}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}^{\mathrm{zf}}=c\;\mathbf{h}_{s}^{*}+c_{\perp}\mathbf{h}_{s}^{\perp*}

(35)

for some constants
c
and
c_{\perp}
and
\mathbf{h}_{s}^{\perp}
being a vector orthogonal to
\mathbf{h}_{s}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{h}_{s}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{h}_{s}^{\perp}=0
. To fulfill the ZF condition for device
s
, we need to have
\mathbf{m}^{{\mathrm{zf}}\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{h}_{s}=0
. This concludes that

\displaystyle c=\frac{\phi_{s}}{\left\|\mathbf{h}_{s}\right\|^{2}b_{s}},

(36)

and noting that
\left|b_{s}\right|^{2}\leq P
, we should set

\displaystyle\left|c\right|\geq\frac{\phi_{s}}{\left\|\mathbf{h}_{s}\right\|^{2}\sqrt{P}}.

(37)

Considering (
14
), we conclude that the aggregation error is

\displaystyle\epsilon\left(\mathbf{m}^{\mathrm{zf}},\left\{b_{\ell}^{\mathrm{zf}}\right\}\right)=\sigma^{2}\left(\left|c\right|^{2}\;\left\|\mathbf{h}_{s}\right\|^{2}+\left|c_{\perp}\right|^{2}\left\|\mathbf{h}_{s}^{\perp}\right\|^{2}\right)

(38)

that is minimized by setting
\left|c\right|
to its lower bound in (
37
) and
c_{\perp}=0
. This describes the feasible ZF setting with minimal aggregation error. Hence, the proof is concluded.
∎

Theorem
1
describes a sufficient condition under which the ZF scheme is determined closed-form. This condition is very likely to hold in practical scenarios in which few devices have significantly larger path-losses as compared with the others edge devices in the network. We consider an example of such a scenario throughout the numerical investigations. It is worth mentioning that in a homogeneous setting, i.e.,
\phi_{\ell}=\phi
for
\ell\in\left[L\right]
, with a single-antenna PS, the sufficient condition in Theorem
1
always hold. This is obvious, since in this case only the device with weakest channel should transmit with the maximum power for ZF coordination

V-A

ZF Coordination without Closed-Form Solution

If the sufficient condition in Theorem
1
does not hold, ZF coordination is found by searching over all feasible settings. This search procedure is exponentially hard. We hence develop a tractable algorithm which approximates the solution by polynomial complexity. In this respect, the following lemma, can be further useful to limit our search

Lemma 7
.

If the condition in Theorem
1
does not hold; then, there are at least two devices which transmit with the maximum power
P
in ZF coordination.

Proof.

The proof is readily given by contradiction. We hence skip it here.
∎

Considering Lemma
7
and the earlier results on ZF coordination, we can have the following statements about the optimal ZF scheme, when it is not given by Theorem
1
:

•

It is a feasible ZF setting.

•

There are at least two devices with transmit power
P
.

We further note that when all the devices transmit with power
P
, a feasible ZF setting is always guaranteed to be determined: let
b_{\ell}=\sqrt{P}
for
\ell\in\left[L\right]
and
\mathbf{m}
to be the one given in Lemma
5
for
{\mathbb{D}^{{\mathrm{zf}}}}=\left[L\right]
. These findings leads to this heuristic conclusion that a good approximate for the optimal ZF scheme is given by finding a feasible ZF setting with minimal number of devices transmitting with power
P
. This heuristic conclusion leads us to a tree-based algorithm which is presented in the sequel.

Remark 1
.

As mentioned in Section
II
, throughout the paper we assume
N\geq L
. This however does not restrict the scope of analyses. In fact, the derivations are further valid for settings with
N<L

V-B

A Tree-Based Search Scheme

We now present a tree-based algorithm to find the optimal ZF scheme. Although its core idea comes from our heuristic conclusions, it is shown that with full complexity this algorithm is exact. Before we present the algorithm, we state the following lemma which serves as the analytical foundation of the tree-based algorithm:

Lemma 8
.

Let
\left(\mathbf{m}_{1},\left\{b_{1\ell}\right\}\right)
and
\left(\mathbf{m}_{2},\left\{b_{2\ell}\right\}\right)
describe two feasible ZF setting for coordination. Denote the set of devices transmitting with power
P
in these two settings with
\mathbb{S}_{1}
and
\mathbb{S}_{2}
, respectively. If
\mathbb{S}_{1}\subseteq\mathbb{S}_{2}
; then,

\displaystyle\epsilon\left(\mathbf{m}_{1},\left\{b_{1\ell}\right\}\right)\leq\epsilon\left(\mathbf{m}_{2},\left\{b_{2\ell}\right\}\right).

(39)

Proof.

The proof is readily concluded by the fact that the feasible region of the error minimization problem for subset
\mathbb{S}_{1}
contains the feasible region of the optimization problem defined with subset
\mathbb{S}_{2}
. This implies that the error obtained by the former minimization is smaller than the one in the latter. This concludes the proof.
∎

The above result implies that while searching for the optimal ZF coordination, those feasible ZF settings whose corresponding subsets include other feasible subsets can be further ignored
7
7

7

From Lemma
7
, we further conclude that this search is restricted to
\left|S\right|\geq 2
.
. We hence can graphically visualize the search for the optimal feasible setting via a
tree
: consider a graph whose nodes denote those subsets of
\left[L\right]
that describe feasible ZF settings. The node corresponding to
\mathbb{S}\subseteq\left[L\right]
is connected to its
children
which are the subsets of
\mathbb{S}
describing feasible ZF settings. Any set
\mathbb{S}\neq\left[L\right]
is further connected to its
parent
which is the feasible ZF set
\tilde{\mathbb{S}}
containing
\mathbb{S}
, i.e.,
\mathbb{S}\subset\tilde{\mathbb{S}}
. If there are multiple choices for the parent of
\mathbb{S}
; then, the set with largest cardinality is selected
8
8

8

If there are multiple nodes with largest cardinality, the we choose the parent among them at random.
. This way we built a tree whose root is
\left[L\right]
and whose cardinality of the sets corresponding to the nodes decreases as we move towards the leaves.

Consider a network with

\phi_{\ell}=0.25

Lemma
8
implies that the optimal ZF scheme with minimal aggregation error is among the leaves of the tree representing ZF feasible settings. Therefore, the problem of finding the optimal ZF scheme reduces to a search for the leaf with minimal error, i.e., the leaf with minimal norm of the receiver. It is worth mentioning that this search is still exponentially complex, since in general construction of the tree requires
2^{L}-1
feasibility checks. Its visual representation however describes a tractable approximation approach: starting from the root, we move towards the leaves in a step-wise fashion. In each step, we go from a parent to the child whose aggregation error is minimum among all the children. The search is finally over as we arrive at a leaf. This leaf is taken as the approximate of the solution.

V-C

Approximate ZF Coordination

The proposed tree-based search lead to an approximation of ZF coordination which we refer to as approximate ZF (AZF). The details on this algorithm are as follows:

1.

We initiate the search at the root of the tree by setting
\mathbb{S}^{(0)}=\left[L\right]
. The initial receiver
\mathbf{m}^{(0)}
and scaling coefficients are further found via Lemma
6
.

2.

At step
i
, we consider all subsets of
\mathbb{S}^{(i)}
that differ with
\mathbb{S}^{(i)}
in only one element. For each subset, we determine the receiver from
\mathbf{m}^{(i)}
and the corresponding channel vectors via rank-one update of matrix inverse; see
[
26
]
. We then calculate the scaling coefficients and check if they satisfy the transmit power constraint. If the power constraint is satisfied, we collect the subset as a feasible ZF subset of
\mathbb{S}^{(i)}
.

3.

Among the feasible ZF subsets of
\mathbb{S}^{(i)}
, we set
\mathbb{S}^{(i+1)}
to be the one whose receiver has minimal norm.

The algorithm stops at step
i_{\rm T}
, where there is no feasible ZF subsets for the set
\mathbb{S}^{(i_{\rm T})}
.

The AZF scheme needs to search among
L\left(L+1\right)/2
subsets in the worst-case scenario, and hence is computationally tractable. It can be further extended by considering multiple dominant paths of the tree. Such an extension can potentially lead to a more accurate approximation of the ZF scheme at the expense of higher computational complexity.

Subset Selection for MMSE Coordination

We now get back to the MMSE scheme and utilize the framework developed for ZF coordination to design a tractable algorithm for approximating the MMSE scheme. We start the derivations by defining the less-intuitive concept of a
feasible MMSE setting
. Before stating the definition, let us look back to the key properties of the MMSE scheme: from Lemmas
1
and
2
, we know that the scaling coefficients in this scheme are either
\sqrt{P}
or satisfy the ZF constraint. We further note that given the set of devices transmitting with power
P
, the receiver can be found via (
26
). These features are dual to those satisfied by the ZF scheme. We hence invoke Definition
1
and define a feasible setting for MMSE coordination as follows:

Definition 2
.

Consider the subset of devices
\mathbb{S}\subseteq\left[L\right]
whose cardinality is
\left|\mathbb{S}\right|=S
. Let
\mathbf{m}
be

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{P}}\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{S}}^{*}\left(\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{S}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{S}}^{*}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{P}\mathbf{I}_{S}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{\mathbb{S}}.

(41)

Let
\left|b_{\ell}\right|=\sqrt{P}
for
\ell\in\mathbb{S}
and determine
b_{\ell}
for
\ell\notin\mathbb{S}
from the equation
\mathbf{m}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{h}_{\ell}b_{\ell}=\phi_{\ell}
. The set
\mathbb{S}
is said to describe a feasible MMSE setting for coordination, if

\left|b_{\ell}\right|^{2}<P
for
\ell\notin\mathbb{S}
.

Lemmas
1
and
2
along with (
26
) imply that the subset of devices transmitting with power
P
in the MMSE scheme, i.e.,
\mathbb{D}
, describes a feasible MMSE setting. In other words, similar to ZF coordination, this definition relaxes the MMSE scheme by dropping the optimality constraint. We next follow the same steps as for ZF coordination to show that the MMSE scheme is given by searching over the tree of feasible settings for the smallest subset.

Properties of Feasible MMSE Settings

Similar to Theorem
1

\mathbb{D}

Theorem 2
.

Let
s
be index of the device whose channel norm is smallest, i.e.,

\displaystyle s=\argmin_{\ell\in\left[L\right]}\left\|\mathbf{h}_{\ell}\right\|.

Define the vector
\mathbf{g}

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{H}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{h}_{s}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{P}\mathbf{o}_{s}\right),

\left|{\mathrm{g}}_{s}\right|\leq\left|{\mathrm{g}}_{\ell}\right|

\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{S}
, i.e., only the device in

transmit with the maximum power

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}^{\star}=\frac{\sqrt{P}\phi_{s}\mathbf{h}_{s}^{*}}{P\left\|\mathbf{h}_{s}\right\|^{2}+\sigma^{2}}.

(44)

Proof.

The proof follows similar steps as those taken for the proof of Theorem
1
. We hence skip the details.
∎

Theorem
1
describes the sufficient condition for tractable calculation of the MMSE scheme. For networks which do not satisfy this condition, one can show that similar findings, as those given in Sections
V-A
and

for ZF coordination, are reported as well in this case. In particular, we can show that feasible MMSE settings reduce in aggregation error as their corresponding subset of devices with maximum transmit power shrinks.

Lemma 9
.

Let
\left(\mathbf{m}_{1},\left\{b_{1\ell}\right\}\right)

VIII
Conclusions

Low-complexity algorithms have been proposed for MMSE and ZF coordination in OTA-FL. The algorithms find efficient approximation of the optimal MMSE and ZF schemes using a tree-based search algorithm which solves the equivalent subset selection problem. Our numerical investigations depict that the proposed algorithms track closely the optimal schemes.

The analytical results of this study indicate that both the MMSE and ZF coordination schemes deal with the same level of hardness, while the former leads to a lower aggregation error. The numerical investigations further reveal that the tree-based approximation of MMSE coordination results in less computational complexity and more robustness as compared to the approximated ZF scheme. Considering the favorable behavior of the MMSE scheme with respect to performance and complexity, one concludes that MMSE is a better approach for device coordination in OTA-FL.
