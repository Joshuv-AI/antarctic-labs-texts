Asymptotics for Pull on the Complete Graph
Konstantinos Panagiotou, Simon Reisser

Abstract
We study the randomized rumor spreading algorithm pull on complete graphs with n n vertices. Starting with one informed vertex and proceeding in rounds, each vertex yet uninformed connects to a neighbor chosen uniformly at random and receives the information, if the vertex it connected to is informed. The goal is to study the number of rounds needed to spread the information to everybody, also known as the runtime . In our main result we provide a description, as n n gets large, for the distribution of the runtime that involves a martingale limit. This allows us to establish that in general there is no limiting distribution and that convergence occurs only on suitably chosen subsequences ( n i ) i ∈ ℕ (n_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}} of ℕ \mathbb{N} , namely when the fractional part of ( log 2 ⁡ n i + log 2 ⁡ ln ⁡ n i ) i ∈ ℕ (\log_{2}n_{i}+\log_{2}\ln n_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}} converges.

Introduction

Randomized rumour spreading has applications in replicated databases

We start by selecting a vertex and equipping it with some piece of information.
Then we proceed in rounds, in which every vertex yet uninformed connects to a neighbor chosen uniformly at random and receives the information, if the vertex it connected to is informed.

The most basic case, and the one studied here, is to set

In that case specifying the initial vertex is not necessary; we therefore just write

for the runtime of

Results

Our main result describes the distribution of the runtime of
pull
on complete graphs.

Theorem 1.1
.

There is a continuous random variable
X
such that, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\big|P(X_{n}\geq k)-P(\lceil\log_{2}n+\log_{2}\ln n+X\rceil\geq k)\big|=o(1).

Actually, we can provide some information about
X
. To this end, let us write
I_{t}
for the set of informed vertices at the start of round
t
. In particular,
|I_{0}|=1
. By definition of the protocol, every uninformed vertex becomes informed in round
t
independently with probability
|I_{t}|/n
. That is, in distribution

\displaystyle|I_{t+1}|=|I_{t}|+\text{Bin}\big(n-|I_{t}|,|I_{t}|/n\big).

If
|I_{t}|=o(n)
, then the binomial distribution is very close to being Poisson, and we thus may approximate
|I_{t}|
by the sequence of random variables given by

\displaystyle J_{0}=1,\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{and}\penalty\ \penalty\ J_{t+1}=J_{t}+\text{Po}(J_{t}),\penalty\ \penalty\ t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

This sequence doubles every round in expectation,
\mathbb{E}[J_{t}]=2^{t}
. Moreover, it is fairly easy to establish that
(H_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
with
H_{t}=2^{-t}J_{t}
is a martingale and uniformly integrable. Thus, the Martingale Convergence Theorem guarantees the existence of a random variable
H
such that
H_{t}
converges almost surely to
H
.
In essence,
2^{-t}|I_{t}|
is ’close’ to
H
for large
t
(and
n
); we formalize this statement in Lemma
2.1
below. The random variable
X
is then given as
X=-\log_{2}H
.

From this construction of
H
we may obtain further information about its distribution. For example, the characteristic function
\varphi
of
H
has the property
\varphi=\lim_{t\to\infty}\varphi_{t}
, where
\varphi_{t}
is the characteristic function of
H_{t}
, by Levy’s Continuity Theorem.
Using this we establish in Section
2.1
that
H
is continuous and almost surely positive, two main ingredients in the proof of Theorem
1.1
. However, finding more properties of
H
, like a handy expression for its density or expressions for its moments, turned out to be a tough challenge that we leave as an open problem.

Let us denote with
(X+x)\!\!\mid_{\mathbb{Z}}
the distribution of
X
translated by
x
and restricted to integers only, that is,
(X+x)\!\!\mid_{\mathbb{Z}}
is the random variable with domain
\mathbb{Z}
and distribution

\displaystyle P\big((X+x)\!\!\mid_{\mathbb{Z}}\ \leq k\big):=P(X\leq k-x),\quad\ k\in\mathbb{Z}.

With this definition at hand and by choosing a suitable subsequence we can derive a limiting distribution from Theorem
1.1
.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
x\in[0,1)
and let
n_{i}
be a strictly increasing sequence of natural numbers such that
\log_{2}n_{i}+\log_{2}\ln n_{i}-\lfloor\log_{2}n_{i}+\log_{2}\ln n_{i}\rfloor\to x

\displaystyle X_{n_{i}}-\lfloor\log_{2}n_{i}+\log_{2}\ln n_{i}\rfloor\to(X+x)\!\!\mid_{\mathbb{Z}}.

This corollary warrants some further remarks.
First of all, it is not immediately clear that a sequence with the required properties exists, at least it was not to us.
Luckily it requires only moderate effort to find a suitable one. For example, we may choose

n_{i}=\big\lfloor\exp(W(2^{i+x}))\big\rfloor,\quad i\in\mathbb{N},

where

is the principal branch of the Lambert
W

for all

As

Thus, as

Outline

The paper is structured as follows. In the next section we give the proof of the main results, which is based on three key lemmas characterising the different phases of
pull
. At first, as long as less than
n^{1/3}
vertices are informed,
pull
is best described by a branching process, which, suitably normalized, has limiting distribution
H
, see Lemma
2.1
. After that, the protocol follows essentially a deterministic recurrence relation as described in Lemma
2.2
. Once there are only
o\big(\sqrt{n}\big)
uninformed vertices remaining the behaviour changes once more, in that all these vertices will be informed in one additional round, see Lemma
2.3
.
The proof of Theorem
1.1
, based on these lemmas as outlined, is given in Subsection
2.1
. After that, we give the short proof for Corollary
1.2
in Subsection
2.2
. The proofs of Lemmas
2.1
-
2.3
can be found in Subsections
2.3
-
2.5
.

Proofs

2.1
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Our first auxiliary lemma establishes that initially – as long as there are not too many informed vertices – the number of informed vertices essentially doubles in each round and the deviation from perfect doubling can be described in terms of a non-trivial random variable.

Lemma 2.1
.

There is a continuous and almost surely positive random variable
H
such that for all
\varepsilon>0
there are constants
n_{0},t_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
and
t_{0}\leq t\leq\log_{2}(n^{1/3})

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\big|P\big({2^{-t}|I_{t}|\geq x}\big)-P(H\geq x)\big|\leq\varepsilon.

The proof is in Section
2.3
. From now on we fix some
\varepsilon>0
and set for the remainder

t_{1}:=\lfloor\log_{2}(n^{1/3})\rfloor,

where
n\geq n_{0}
is given by the previous lemma.
For rounds
t\geq t_{1}
it turns out that the behaviour of
pull
can be best described by a (deterministic) recurrence relation.
We have roughly
2^{t_{1}}\approx n^{1/3}
informed vertices, enough so that it is reasonable to assume that the number of newly informed vertices in the following rounds is strongly concentrated around its expectation.

Denote by
U_{t}
the set of uniformed vertices at the start of round
t
.
Then it is easy to see that
\mathbb{E}[|U_{t+1}|\bigm|I_{t}]=(|U_{t}|/n)^{2}n
, see also Lem.
2.14
below, and thus we expect
|U_{t_{1}+t}|
, given
|U_{t_{1}}|
, to be close to
(|U_{t_{1}}|/n)^{2^{t}}n
.
The only thing that we have to take care of is that (small) deviations from the expectation are not blown out of proportions when considering multiple rounds.
The next lemma, that we prove in Subsection
2.4
, does exactly that.
With high probability, or abbreviated as whp, means with probability tending to 1 as
n
tends to infinity.

Lemma 2.2
.

With high probability

\bigcap_{\penalty\ t\geq 0}\left\{\Big||U_{t_{1}+t}|-\big(|U_{t_{1}}|/n\big)^{2^{t}}n\Big|\leq|U_{t_{1}+t}|\cdot n^{-1/50}+n^{1/4}\right\}.

This lemma will enable us to track the process all the way until there are fewer than
\sqrt{n}
uniformed vertices remaining.
Indeed, as we will argue shortly,
\sqrt{n}
is an important threshold in the following sense. On the one hand, if there are substantially more than
\sqrt{n}
uninformed vertices, very likely the process will not terminate in the next round.
In contrast, if there are much less than
\sqrt{n}
uninformed vertices, the process will likely terminate in the next round.
Furthermore, we will see that it is very unlikely that
|U_{t}|=\Theta\big(\sqrt{n}\big)
for some
t
, where the process terminates only with constant probability.
The next lemma summarizes our findings.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
T=\min\big\{t\in\mathbb{N}:|U_{t}|<\sqrt{n}\big\}
, then with high probability

|U_{T-1}|=\omega\big(\sqrt{n}\big),\quad|U_{T}|=o\big(\sqrt{n}\big),\quad|U_{T}|>0\quad\text{and}\quad|U_{T+1}|=0.

The last statement implies immediately that whp
X_{n}=T+1
and it thus provides a handy way to compute
X_{n}
. To that end we utilize Lemma
2.2
that guarantees whp for all
t\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle|U_{t_{1}+t}|=\big(1+o(1)\big)\big(|U_{t_{1}}|/n\big)^{2^{t}}n+O\big(n^{1/4}\big).

Together with Lemma
2.3
this implies whp

\big(|U_{t_{1}}|/n\big)^{2^{T-t_{1}-1}}n=\omega\big(\sqrt{n}\big)\quad\text{and}\quad\big(|U_{t_{1}}|/n\big)^{2^{T-t_{1}}}n=o\big(\sqrt{n}\big)

and therefore also whp

T=\min\Big\{t\in\mathbb{N}:\big(|U_{t_{1}}|/n\big)^{2^{t-t_{1}}}n<\sqrt{n}\Big\}=\min\Big\{t\in\mathbb{N}:(1-|I_{t_{1}}|/n)^{2^{t-t_{1}}}n<\sqrt{n}\Big\}.

Let
T^{\prime}
be the real number such that
\big(1-|I_{t_{1}}|/n\big)^{2^{T^{\prime}}}n=\sqrt{n}
. Then
T=\big\lfloor T^{\prime}+t_{1}+1\big\rfloor

and it is straightforward to verify that

\displaystyle T^{\prime}=\log_{2}n-\log_{2}|I_{t_{1}}|+\log_{2}\ln n-1+o(1).

Therefore, as Lemma
2.3
yields whp
X_{n}=T+1=\lfloor T^{\prime}+t_{1}+2\big\rfloor
,

\displaystyle\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\left|P(X_{n}\geq k)-P\Big(\big\lfloor\log_{2}n+\log_{2}\ln n-\log_{2}\big(2^{-t_{1}}|I_{t_{1}}|\big)+1+o(1)\big\rfloor\geq k\Big)\right|=o(1).

In Lemma
2.1
we showed that
2^{-t_{1}}|I_{t_{1}}|
converges in distribution to a random variable
H
; since
H
is continuous so is
X=-\log_{2}H
and the claim in Theorem
1.1
follows readily.

Proof of Corollary
1.2

Theorem
1.1
states that there is a continuous random variable
X
such that for all
x\in[0,1)
and strictly increasing sequences
n_{i}
such that
\log_{2}n_{i}+\log_{2}\ln n_{i}-\lfloor\log_{2}n_{i}+\log_{2}\ln n_{i}\rfloor\to x

\displaystyle\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\Big|P\big(X_{n_{i}}\geq k\big)-P\big(\log_{2}n_{i}+\log_{2}\ln n_{i}+X+1\geq k\big)\Big|=o(1).

Setting
\{y\}=y-\lfloor y\rfloor
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}
and substituting
k=\lfloor\log_{2}n_{i}+\log_{2}\ln n_{i}\rfloor+t+1
we obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}\Big|P\big(X_{n_{i}}\geq\lfloor\log_{2}n_{i}+\log_{2}\ln n_{i}\rfloor+t+1\big)-P\big(\{\log_{2}n_{i}+\log_{2}\ln n_{i}\}+X\geq t\big)\Big|=o(1).

Thus
X
being a continuous random variable

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}\big|P\big(X_{n_{i}}\geq\lfloor\log_{2}n_{i}+\log_{2}\ln n_{i}\rfloor+1+t\big)-P(x+X\geq t)\big|=o(1).

Thus

P(X_{n_{i}}-\lfloor\log_{2}n_{i}+\log_{2}\ln n_{i}\rfloor\leq t)\overset{i\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}P(X\leq t-x)
,
as claimed.

Proof of Lemma
2.1

To prove Lemma
2.1
first recall that

\displaystyle J_{0}=1\quad\text{ and }\quad J_{t+1}=J_{t}+\text{Po}(J_{t}),\penalty\ H_{t}=2^{-t}J_{t},\ t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

We show three claims in order to prove Lemma
2.1
, namely that
|I_{t}|
is close to
J_{t}
, then that
(H_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is a martingale that converges (to
H
) and finally that the limit is absolutely continuous.

|I_{t}|
and
J_{t}
are close.

We begin with a simple lemma that determines the first and second moment of
J_{t}
.

Lemma 2.4
.

For all
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
J_{t}
as defined above

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[J_{t}]=2^{t}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}[J_{t}^{2}]=2^{t-1}(3\cdot 2^{t}-1).

Proof.

We compute both moments inductively, starting with the base case

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[J_{0}]=1\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}[J_{0}^{2}]=1.

Moreover, using the tower property of the expectation and that
\mathbb{E}[\text{Po}(\lambda)]=\lambda
for any
\lambda>0
we obtain by induction

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[J_{t+1}]=\mathbb{E}[J_{t}+\text{Po}(J_{t})]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbb{E}\big[J_{t}+\text{Po}(J_{t})\bigm|J_{t}\big]\Big]=2\cdot\mathbb{E}[J_{t}]=2^{t+1}.

We compute the second moment similarly. Since
\mathbb{E}[\text{Po}(\lambda)^{2}]=\lambda+\lambda^{2}
we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[J_{t+1}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\big(J_{t}+\text{Po}(J_{t})\big)^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[J_{t}^{2}+2\cdot J_{t}\text{Po}(J_{t})+\text{Po}(J_{t})^{2}\right]=4\cdot\mathbb{E}[J_{t}^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[J_{t}]

\displaystyle=4\cdot 2^{t-1}(3\cdot 2^{t}-1)+2^{t}=2^{t}(3\cdot 2^{t+1}-1).

∎

The next (well-known) statement bounds the distance between two Poisson distributed random variables and furthermore quantifies the distance in the Poisson limit theorem.
Recall that the
total variation distance
for two integer valued random variables
X,Y
can be defined as

\displaystyle\textnormal{d}(X,Y):=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\big|P(X=k)-P(Y=k)\big|.

(2.1)

Lemma 2.5

(
[
23
]
, Eq. 3.6 and Thm. 4.1)
.

a)

Let
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
and
X\sim\textnormal{Po}(\lambda_{1})
and
Y\sim\textnormal{Po}(\lambda_{2})
be independent Poisson-distributed random variables. Then

\textnormal{d}(X,Y)\leq|\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}|
.

b)

Let
X\sim\textnormal{Bin}(n,p)
and
Y\sim\textnormal{Po}(np).
Then
\textnormal{d}(X,Y)\leq np^{2}
.

With these ingredients at hand we can give a bound on the distance of
|I_{t}|
and
J_{t}
that is quite strong as long as
t
is not too large.

Lemma 2.6
.

For all
t\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\textnormal{d}_{t}:=\textnormal{d}\big(|I_{t}|,J_{t}\big)\leq 2\cdot 4^{t}/n.

Proof.

Note that it suffices to consider only the case
t\leq\log_{4}n
, as otherwise the claimed bound is greater than one and consequently trivially true.
There are
|I_{t}|
informed vertices in round
t
. Then the probability of any vertex
v\in U_{t}
to be informed in that round is
|I_{t}|/n
and furthermore it is independent of all other uninformed vertices, that is, the number of newly informed vertices is binomially distributed with
|U_{t}|
tries and success probability
|I_{t}|/n
.
Thus, in distribution,

\displaystyle|I_{t+1}|=|I_{t}|+\text{Bin}\big(|U_{t}|,|I_{t}|/n\big),

(2.2)

an equation that we have already encountered in the introduction.
We prove the statement of the lemma by induction over
t
. The base case is obvious as
|I_{0}|=1=J_{0}
. For the induction step, we use (
2.1
) together with
|I_{t}|
and
J_{t}
only taking values on the positive integers, to get

\displaystyle 2\cdot\textnormal{d}_{t+1}=\sum_{k\geq 1}\big|P\big(|I_{t+1}|=k\big)-P(J_{t+1}=k)\big|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}P\big(|I_{t+1}|=k\bigm||I_{t}|=\ell\big)P\big(|I_{t}|=\ell\big)-P\big(J_{t+1}=k\bigm|J_{t}=\ell\big)P(J_{t}=\ell)\right|+P(J_{t+1}>n).

To simplify this expression we consider the auxiliary calculation

\displaystyle\penalty\ \sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big|\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}P\big(|I_{t+1}|=k\bigm||I_{t}|=\ell\big)\big(P\big(|I_{t}|=\ell\big)-P(J_{t}=\ell)\big)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\penalty\ \sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\Big|P\big(|I_{t}|=\ell\big)-P(J_{t}=\ell)\Big|\sum_{k=\ell}^{n}P\big(|I_{t+1}|=k\bigm||I_{t}|=\ell\big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\penalty\ \sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\Big|P\big(|I_{t}|=\ell\big)-P(J_{t}=\ell)\Big|\leq 2\cdot\textnormal{d}_{t}.

In order to obtain a bound for the tail probability of
J_{t+1}
we use Lemma
2.4
as well as the assumption
t\leq\log_{4}n
so that by Chebyshev’s inequality and plenty of room to spare

\displaystyle P(J_{t+1}>n)\leq P\big(|J_{t+1}-\mathbb{E}[J_{t+1}]|>n-\mathbb{E}[J_{t+1}]\big)\leq\frac{\text{Var}[J_{t+1}]}{(n-\mathbb{E}[J_{t+1}])^{2}}\leq 4^{t}/n.

Applying these bounds to
\textnormal{d}_{t+1}
we get

\displaystyle 2\cdot\textnormal{d}_{t+1}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}P(J_{t}=\ell)\Big|P\big(|I_{t+1}|=k\bigm||I_{t}|=\ell\big)-P\big(J_{t+1}=k\bigm|J_{t}=\ell\big)\Big|+2\cdot\textnormal{d}_{t}+4^{t}/n.

Next we plug in the distributions for
|I_{t+1}|-|I_{t}|
(binomial) and
J_{t+1}
(Poisson) to get

\displaystyle 2\cdot\textnormal{d}_{t+1}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}P(J_{t}=\ell)\Big|P\big(\text{Bin}(n-\ell,\ell/n)=k-\ell\big)-P\big(\text{Po}(\ell)=k-\ell\big)\Big|+2\cdot\textnormal{d}_{t}+4^{t}/n

and shifting indices yields

\displaystyle 2\cdot\textnormal{d}_{t+1}\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}P(J_{t}=\ell)\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big|\big(P(\text{Bin}(n-\ell,\ell/n)=k\big)-P\big(\text{Po}(\ell)=k\big)\Big|+2\cdot\textnormal{d}_{t}+4^{t}/n

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}P(J_{t}=\ell)\cdot 2\cdot\textnormal{d}\big(\text{Bin}(n-\ell,\ell/n),\text{Po}(\ell)\big)+2\cdot\textnormal{d}_{t}+4^{t}/n.

As
d
is a metric we can use the triangle inequality and with Lemma
2.5
we get for all
0\leq\ell\leq n

\displaystyle\textnormal{d}\big(\text{Bin}(n-\ell,\ell/n),\text{Po}(\ell)\big)

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{d}\big(\text{Bin}(n-\ell,\ell/n),\text{Po}((n-\ell)\ell/n)\big)+\textnormal{d}\big(\text{Po}((n-\ell)\ell/n),\text{Po}(\ell)\big)

\displaystyle\leq(n-\ell)(\ell/n)^{2}+\big|(n-\ell)\ell/n-\ell\big|,

which is at most
{2\ell^{2}}/{n}
.
By plugging this into the previous inequality we get

\displaystyle\textnormal{d}_{t+1}\leq\penalty\ \sum_{\ell=1}^{n}P(J_{t}=\ell)\frac{2\ell^{2}}{n}+\textnormal{d}_{t}+4^{t}/n\leq 2\cdot\frac{\mathbb{E}[J_{t}^{2}]}{n}+\textnormal{d}_{t}+4^{t}/n.

Lemma
2.4
determines the second moment of
J_{t}
. By using the induction hypothesis we conclude

\displaystyle\textnormal{d}_{t+1}\leq 3\cdot 4^{t}/n+2\cdot 4^{t}/n+4^{t}/n\leq 2\cdot 4^{t+1}/n.

∎

(H_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is a martingale that converges to
H
.

Next we show that the sequence
(H_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is a martingale and converges almost surely and in
\mathcal{L}^{2}
to the random variable
H
.

Lemma 2.7
.

There is a random variable
H
such that
H_{t}\to H
almost surely and in
\mathcal{L}^{2}
.
Furthermore
H
has mean 1 and variance
1/2.

Proof.

First we show that
H_{t}
is a martingale. Let
\mathcal{F}_{t}
be the filtration induced by the random variables
J_{t}
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[H_{t+1}\bigm|\mathcal{F}_{t}\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[2^{-t-1}\big(J_{t}+\text{Po}(J_{t})\big)\bigm|\mathcal{F}_{t}\big]=2^{-t}J_{t}=H_{t}.

Next, we show that
H_{t}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
. Therefore we compute

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[|H_{t}|^{2}\big]=2^{-2t}\mathbb{E}\big[J_{t}^{2}\big]=2^{-2t}\cdot 2^{t}(3\cdot 2^{t+1}-1)\leq 6

using Lemma
2.4
and consequently

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\mathbb{E}\big[|H_{t}|^{2}\big]<\infty.

(2.3)

This yields the integrability of
H_{t}
and as
H_{t}
is obviously measurable with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{t}
we conclude that it is indeed a martingale. Thus (
2.3
) and
\mathcal{L}^{p}
convergence of martingales implies the first claim, see e.g.
[
10
, Thm. 4.4.6]
. The values for the expectation and the variance follow immediately from Lemma
2.4
by scaling with
2^{-t}
and
2^{-2t}
respectively and then taking the limit.
∎

An even stronger version of this lemma could be shown, i.e., the martingale converges in
\mathcal{L}^{p}
for all
p>1
. In any case, the version stated here suffices for our purposes.

In order to show the properties of
H
claimed in Lemma
2.1
we need to describe its characteristic function. The next lemma does exactly that, but we need a definition first. Let

\displaystyle h(x)=h^{(1)}(x)=xe^{x-1},\quad h^{(t+1)}=h^{(t)}\circ h,\ t\in\mathbb{N}.

(2.4)

This function is not new in the context of rumor spreading, it plays an important role in the closely related context of
[
6
]
, where it describes the evolution of the number of uninformed vertices of
push
on complete graphs.

Lemma 2.8
.

The characteristic functions
\varphi_{t}
of
H_{t}
and
\varphi
of
H
satisfy

\displaystyle\varphi_{t}(x)=h^{(t)}\Big(e^{ix2^{-t}}\Big)\quad\text{and}\quad\varphi(x)=\lim_{t\to\infty}\varphi_{t}(x).

Proof.

To prove the claim we first compute the probability generating function
\tilde{J}_{t}(x)
of
J_{t}
.
Note that
\tilde{J_{0}}(x)=x
, as
P(J_{0}=1)=1
. For
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
|x|\leq 1
we get

\displaystyle\tilde{J}_{t+1}(x)=\sum_{k\geq 0}P(J_{t+1}=k)x^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}P\big(J_{t}+\text{Po}(J_{t})=k\big)x^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}\sum_{0\leq\ell\leq k}P\big(\text{Po}(\ell)=k-\ell\big)P(J_{t}=\ell)x^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}P(J_{t}=\ell)\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{\ell^{k}}{k!}e^{-\ell}x^{k+\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}P(J_{t}=\ell)e^{-\ell+x\ell}x^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}P(J_{t}=\ell)\big(xe^{x-1}\big)^{\ell}=\tilde{J_{t}}\big(xe^{x-1}\big)=\big(\tilde{J_{t}}\circ h\big)(x).

Thus
J_{t}
has characteristic function
x\mapsto h^{(t)}\big(e^{ix}\big)
and as
H_{t}=2^{-t}J_{t}
we immediately obtain that
H_{t}
has characteristic function
x\mapsto h^{(t)}\big(e^{ix2^{-t}}\big)
.
With Levy’s continuity theorem we infer that the characteristic function of
H_{t}
converges to the characteristic function of
H
, as Lemma
2.7
guarantees that
H_{t}
converges to
H
almost surely and therefore also in distribution.
∎

Properties of
H
.

In the last part of this section we show that
H
is absolutely continuous and almost surely positive. To show absolute continuity, we will argue that the characteristic function of
H
is integrable. To achieve this we first find a recurrence relation for real and imaginary parts of
\varphi_{t}
, the characteristic function of
H_{t}
. We then use this description to find a second order approximation of
\varphi_{t}
that eventually allows us to uniformly bound the absolute value of
\varphi_{t}
by an integrable function.

The mapping that we will use to describe the real and imaginary parts of
\varphi_{t}
is given by

\displaystyle F=F^{(1)}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2},F\left(\binom{R}{I}\right)=e^{-1+R}\begin{pmatrix}\cos I&-\sin I\\
\sin I&\penalty\ \cos I\end{pmatrix}\binom{R}{I},\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0ptF^{(t+1)}=F^{(t)}\circ F,\kern 5.0ptt\in\mathbb{N}.

Moreover, we set
F^{(0)}
to be the identity on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
\varphi_{t}
be the characteristic function of
H_{t}
. Set
I_{t}(x)=\textnormal{Im}\big(\varphi_{t}(x)\big)
(the imaginary part),
R_{t}(x)=\textnormal{Re}\big(\varphi_{t}(x)\big)
(the real part) and
a_{t}(x)=|\varphi_{t}(x)|
. Then for all
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

\displaystyle\binom{R_{t}(x)}{I_{t}(x)}=F^{(t)}\left(\binom{\cos(x2^{-t})}{\sin(x2^{-t})}\right).

and

\displaystyle a_{t+1}(x)=a_{t}(x/2)\exp\big(-1+R_{t}(x/2)\big).

Proof.

Using Lemma
2.8
we obtain for
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

\displaystyle\varphi_{t+1}(x)=h^{(t+1)}\left(e^{ix2^{-t-1}}\right)=h\left(h^{(t)}\left(e^{i(x/2)2^{-t}}\right)\right)=h\big(\varphi_{t}(x/2)\big).

(2.5)

We continue with a simple observation.
For two complex numbers
z,w
the imaginary and real parts of their product satisfy

\displaystyle\text{Re}(z\cdot w)=\text{Re}(z)\text{Re}(w)-\text{Im}(z)\text{Im}(w)\quad\text{and}\quad\text{Im}(z\cdot w)=\text{Re}(z)\text{Im}(w)+\text{Im}(z)\text{Re}(w).

Using this observation and (
2.5
) we obtain

\displaystyle I_{t+1}(x)

\displaystyle=\text{Im}\big(\varphi_{t+1}(x)\big)=\text{Im}\Big(\varphi_{t}(x/2)\exp\big(-1+\varphi_{t}(x/2)\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=R_{t}(x/2)\text{Im}\Big(\exp\big(-1+\varphi_{t}(x/2)\big)\Big)+I_{t}(x/2)\text{Re}\Big(\exp\big(-1+\varphi_{t}(x/2)\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=\Big(R_{t}(x/2)\sin\big(I_{t}(x/2)\big)+I_{t}(x/2)\cos\big(I_{t}(x/2)\big)\Big)\exp\big(\!-1+R_{t}(x/2)\big)

and similarly for the real part

\displaystyle R_{t+1}(x)

\displaystyle=\text{Re}\big(\varphi_{t+1}(x)\big)=\text{Re}\Big(\varphi_{t}(x/2)\exp\big(-1+\varphi_{t}(x/2)\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=R_{t}(x/2)\text{Re}\Big(\exp\big(-1+\varphi_{t}(x/2)\big)\Big)-I_{t}(x/2)\text{Im}\Big(\exp\big(-1+\varphi_{t}(x/2)\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=\Big(R_{t}(x/2)\cos\big(I_{t}(x/2)\big)-I_{t}(x/2)\sin\big(I_{t}(x/2)\big)\Big)\exp\big(\!-1+R_{t}(x/2)\big).

Applying these two equations repeatedly and remembering that
\varphi_{0}(x)=e^{ix}
and therefore
R_{0}(x)=\cos(x)
as well as
I_{0}(x)=\sin(x)
implies the first claim.
To show the second claim in the lemma (about
a_{t+1}
) we use again (
2.5
) and
|e^{z}|=e^{\text{Re}(z)}
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}

\displaystyle a_{t+1}(x)=\big|\varphi_{t}(x/2)\cdot\exp\big(\varphi(x/2)-1\big)\big|=a_{t}(x/2)\cdot\exp\big(-1+R_{t}(x/2)\big).

∎

With that recursive description at hand we can derive a (first) handy approximation for
\varphi_{t}
.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
. For all
0\leq j\leq\max\{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:|x2^{-t+j}|\leq 1/16\}

\displaystyle\left|F^{(j)}\left(\binom{\cos(x2^{-t})}{\sin(x2^{-t})}\right)-\binom{1-x^{2}2^{-2t+j-2}(3\cdot 2^{j}+1)}{x2^{-t+j}}\right|\leq\binom{|x^{3}|2^{3(-t+j)}}{|x^{2}|2^{2(-t+j)}}.

Proof.

If
\big\{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:|x2^{-t+j}|\leq 1/16\big\}=\emptyset
we have nothing to show, thus we assume that
j_{\max}:=\max\big\{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:|x2^{-t+j}|\leq 1/16\big\}\geq 0
. We will show the claim by induction over all
0\leq j\leq j_{\max}
. Very important ingredients in the forthcoming arguments are the following estimates for smallish
x
that are rather easy to show:

\displaystyle\left|\cos(x)-\left(1-\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right)\right|\leq\frac{x^{4}}{24}\quad\text{for all}\quad|x|\leq 7

(2.6)

and

\displaystyle\big|\sin(x)-x\big|\leq\frac{x^{3}}{5}\quad\text{for all}\quad|x|\leq 2.

(2.7)

These estimates yield (with quite some room to spare) immediately the induction start
(j=0)
, as by convention
F^{(0)}
is the identity on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
We proceed with the induction step. For the following computations abbreviate

\displaystyle\alpha_{j}=2^{-2t+j-2}(3\cdot 2^{j}+1),\quad\beta_{j}=2^{-t+j}\quad\text{and}\quad\Delta_{j}=\binom{\Delta_{j,1}}{\Delta_{j,2}}=\binom{|x^{3}|2^{-3t+3j}}{|x^{2}|2^{-2t+2j}}.

In the remainder of this proof we use the following notation. For real numbers
a,b
we write
a\pm b
to denote
some
real number
c
that satisfies
|a-c|\leq b
. In particular, if we apply a function, e.g.,
F
, to
a\pm b
we understand that as
F
applied to some number
c
in the designated interval.
This notation is useful as we are only interested in upper and lower bounds on
F(a\pm b)
that we can deduce from
a
and
b
only.

Let
0\leq j\leq j_{\max}-1
. By applying the induction hypothesis we get

\displaystyle F^{(j+1)}\left(\binom{\cos(x2^{-t}}{\sin(x2^{-t}}\right)=F\left(\binom{1-x^{2}\alpha_{j}}{x\beta_{j}}\pm\Delta_{j}\right)=:\binom{F_{1}}{F_{2}}.

(2.8)

Using the definition of
F
we obtain for the first component

\displaystyle F_{1}=(F_{11}-F_{12})F_{13},

where we abbreviated

\displaystyle F_{11}

\displaystyle=\big(1-x^{2}\alpha_{j}\pm\Delta_{j,1}\big)\cos\big(x\beta_{j}\pm\Delta_{j,2}\big)

\displaystyle F_{12}

\displaystyle=\big(x\beta_{j}\pm\Delta_{j,2}\big)\sin\Big(x\beta_{j}\pm\Delta_{j,2}\big)

\displaystyle F_{13}

\displaystyle=\exp\big(-x^{2}\alpha_{j}\pm\Delta_{j,1}\big).

To study these expressions we look at three recurring components first. Note that, as
(\Delta_{j,2})^{2}\leq\Delta_{j,1}/16
by our assumption on
j\leq j_{\max}
,

\displaystyle\big(x\beta_{j}\pm\Delta_{j,2}\big)^{2}=(x\beta_{j})^{2}\pm 2|x|\beta_{j}\Delta_{j,2}\pm(\Delta_{j,2})^{2}=(x\beta_{j})^{2}\pm\frac{33}{16}\Delta_{j,1}.

(2.9)

Furthermore, using again
|x|2^{-t+j}\leq 1/16
guaranteed by
j\leq j_{\max}
,

\displaystyle\big|\big(x\beta_{j}\pm\Delta_{j,2}\big)^{3}\big|\leq\sum_{i=0}^{3}\binom{3}{i}(|x|\beta_{j})^{i}(\Delta_{j,2})^{3-i}\leq\Delta_{j,1}\left(1+\frac{3}{16}+\frac{3}{16^{2}}+\frac{1}{16^{3}}\right)\leq\frac{6}{5}\Delta_{j,1}

(2.10)

and similarly

\displaystyle\big|\big(x\beta_{j}\pm\Delta_{j,2}\big)^{4}\big|\leq\sum_{i=0}^{4}\binom{4}{i}(|x|\beta_{j})^{i}(\Delta_{j,2})^{4-i}\leq\frac{4}{50}\Delta_{j,1}.

(2.11)

Lastly, we use again
|x|2^{-t+j}\leq 1/16
and
x^{4}a_{j}^{2}\leq\Delta_{j,1}/16
to bound

\displaystyle\big|\big(x^{2}\alpha_{j}\pm\Delta_{j,1}\big)^{2}\big|\leq|x|^{4}\alpha_{j}^{2}+|x|^{2}\alpha_{j}\Delta_{j,1}+\Delta_{j,1}^{2}\leq\Delta_{j,1}\left(\frac{1}{16}+\frac{1}{16^{2}}+\frac{1}{16^{3}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{8}\Delta_{j,1}.

(2.12)

Combining these bounds with (
2.6
) and (
2.7
) we will obtain estimates for the
\sin
and
\cos
terms in
F_{11}
and
F_{12}
. By (
2.6
)

\displaystyle\cos\big(x\beta_{j}\pm\Delta_{j,2}\big)=1-\frac{(x\beta_{j}\pm\Delta_{j,2})^{2}}{2}\pm\frac{(x\beta_{j}\pm\Delta_{j,2})^{4}}{24}

and by combining this with (
2.9
) and (
2.11
) we obtain that

\displaystyle\cos\big(x\beta_{j}\pm\Delta_{j,2}\big)=1-\frac{(x\beta_{j})^{2}}{2}\pm\frac{17}{16}\Delta_{j,1}.

(2.13)

In the same way, using (
2.7
) and (
2.10
),

\displaystyle\sin\big(x\beta_{j}\pm\Delta_{j,2}\big)=(x\beta_{j}\pm\Delta_{j,2})\pm\frac{\big(x\beta_{j}\pm\Delta_{j,2}\big)^{3}}{5}=x\beta_{j}\pm\Delta_{j,2}\pm\frac{\Delta_{j,1}}{4}.

(2.14)

Having done these preparations we proceed with deriving bounds for
F_{11},F_{12},F_{13}
.
We begin with
F_{11}
and using (
2.13
) as well as
|x|2^{-x+j}\leq 1/16
we obtain that

\displaystyle F_{11}=\left(1-x^{2}\alpha_{j}\pm\Delta_{j,1}\right)\left(1-\frac{(x\beta_{j})^{2}}{2}\pm\frac{17}{16}\Delta_{j,1}\right)=1-x^{2}\left(\alpha_{j}+\frac{\beta_{j}^{2}}{2}\right)\pm\frac{17\Delta_{j,1}}{8}.

Furthermore, by making use of (
2.14
) and
|x|2^{-x+j}\leq 1/16
we obtain

\displaystyle F_{12}=\left(x\beta_{j}\pm\Delta_{j,2}\right)\left(x\beta_{j}\pm\Delta_{j,2}\pm\frac{\Delta_{j,1}}{4}\right)=(x\beta_{j})^{2}\pm\frac{17\Delta_{j,1}}{8}

and using the estimate
|\exp(x)-(1+x)|\leq x^{2}
, valid for all
|x|\leq 1
, as well as (
2.12
), we get

\displaystyle F_{13}=1-x^{2}\alpha_{j}\pm\Delta_{j,1}\pm\big(x^{2}\alpha_{j}\pm\Delta_{j,1}\big)^{2}=1-x^{2}\alpha_{j}\pm\frac{9\Delta_{j,1}}{8}.

Thus, putting
F_{11},F_{12}
and
F_{13}
together and using oce more
|x|2^{-t+j}\leq 1/16
, we get that

\displaystyle F_{1}

\displaystyle=\left(1-x^{2}\left(\alpha_{j}+\frac{3\beta_{j}^{2}}{2}\right)\pm\frac{34\Delta_{j,1}}{8}\right)\left(1-x^{2}\alpha_{j}\pm\frac{9\Delta_{j,1}}{8}\right)

\displaystyle=1-x^{2}\left(2\alpha_{j}+\frac{3\beta_{j}^{2}}{2}\right)\pm 6\Delta_{j,1}=1-x^{2}\alpha_{j+1}\pm\Delta_{j+1,1}

confirming the induction step on the first component in (
2.8
). Going forward we switch our attention to the second component, which we again split into three parts

\displaystyle F_{2}=(F_{21}-F_{22})F_{13},

where

\displaystyle F_{21}

\displaystyle=\big(1-x^{2}\alpha_{j}\pm\Delta_{j,1}\big)\sin\big(x\beta_{j}\pm\Delta_{j,2}\big)

\displaystyle F_{22}

\displaystyle=\big(x\beta_{j}\pm\Delta_{j,2}\big)\cos\big(x\beta_{j}\pm\Delta_{j,2}\big).

Similarly, as above using (
2.14
), (
2.13
) and
|x|2^{-t+j}\leq 1/16
we extend these expressions. We start with
F_{21}

\displaystyle F_{21}

\displaystyle=\left(1-x^{2}\alpha_{j}\pm\Delta_{j,1}\right)\left(x\beta_{j}\pm\Delta_{j,2}\pm\frac{\Delta_{j,1}}{4}\right)=x\beta_{j}\pm\frac{9\Delta_{j,2}}{8}.

Continuing with
F_{22}
, again applying (
2.13
) and
|x|2^{-t+j}\leq 1/16
,

\displaystyle F_{22}=\left(x\beta_{j}\pm\Delta_{j,2}\right)\left(1-\frac{(x\beta_{j})^{2}}{2}\pm\frac{17\Delta_{j,1}}{16}\right)=x\beta_{j}\pm\frac{17\Delta_{j,2}}{16}.

Finally, we combine
F_{21},F_{22}
and
F_{13}
and with
|x|2^{-t+j}\leq 1/16
we get

\displaystyle F_{2}

\displaystyle=\left(2x\beta_{j}\pm\frac{33\Delta_{j,2}}{16}\right)\left(1-x^{2}\alpha_{j}\pm\frac{9\Delta_{j,1}}{8}\right)=2x\beta_{j}\pm 3\Delta_{j,2}=x\beta_{j+1}\pm\Delta_{j+1,2}.

Thus we confirmed the induction step and conclude the proof.
∎

The next lemma bounds the absolute value of
\varphi_{t}(x)
for large values of
x
and
t
implying that
\varphi
is integrable, a sufficient condition for the absolute continuity of
H
. We did not make any effort to optimize the involved constants.

Lemma 2.11
.

For all
x\in\mathbb{R},\ |x|\geq 2^{2^{17}}
and
t\in\mathbb{N},\ t\geq\log_{2}(16|x|)
,

\displaystyle|\varphi_{t}(x)|=a_{t}(x)\leq|x|^{-1.2}.

Proof.

Let
\delta=1/32
. As
|x|\geq 1
there is some
t_{0}\geq 0
such that
x2^{-t_{0}}\in[\delta,2\delta]
. To be completely explicit,

t_{0}:=\lceil\log_{2}(16|x|)\rceil.

Thus
t\geq t_{0}
and set
j^{\star}=t-t_{0}\geq 0
. Then with Lemma
2.10
and
\sigma(x)
denoting the sign of
x

\displaystyle\binom{F_{1}}{F_{2}}:=F^{(j^{\star})}\left(\binom{\cos(x2^{-t})}{\sin(x2^{-t})}\right)\leq\binom{1-5\delta^{2}/8}{\sigma(x)\cdot 2\delta}+\binom{(2\delta)^{3}}{(2\delta)^{2}}\leq\binom{1-\delta^{2}/2}{\sigma(x)\cdot 2\delta+(2\delta)^{2}}.

(2.15)

Furthermore observe that by the definition of
F
and the facts that
0\leq\cos i\leq 1
and
0\leq i\sin i
for all
i\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]

\displaystyle F\left(\binom{r}{i}\right)=e^{-1+r}\binom{r\cos i-i\sin i}{r\sin i+i\cos i}\leq\binom{re^{-1+r}}{\pi/2}\quad\text{for all }r\in[0,1],\ i\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2].

(2.16)

Moreover, recall from Lemma
2.9
that

\binom{R_{i}(x2^{-t+i})}{I_{i}(x2^{-t+i})}=F^{(i)}\left(\binom{\cos(x2^{-t})}{\sin(x2^{-t})}\right)\quad\text{for}\quad R_{t}(x)=\text{Re}(\varphi_{t}(x))\text{ and }I_{t}(x)=\text{Im}(\varphi_{t}(x)).

Thus we can bound
R_{i}(x2^{-t+i}),\ i\geq j^{\star}

by using (
2.16
) for the first
i-j^{\star}
applications of
F
and (
2.15
) for the remaining
j^{\star}
to infer that

\displaystyle\binom{R_{i}(x2^{-t+i})}{I_{i}(x2^{-t+i})}\leq\binom{F_{1}\cdot e^{(i-j^{\star})\cdot(-1+F_{1})}}{\pi/2}\leq\binom{(1-\delta^{2}/2)\cdot e^{-(i-j^{\star})\cdot\delta^{2}/2}}{\pi/2},\quad i\geq j^{\star}.

In particular

\displaystyle R_{i}(x2^{-t+i})\leq 1\quad\text{and}\quad R_{i^{\star}}\big(x2^{-t+i^{\star}}\big)\leq e^{-2}\quad\text{for all}\quad i\geq 0\text{ and }i^{\star}\geq j^{\star}+4/\delta^{2}.

(2.17)

Now we switch our focus to
a_{t}(x)
. By applying Lemma
2.9
and (
2.17
)

\displaystyle a_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=a_{t-1}(x/2)\cdot e^{-1+R_{t-1}(x/2)}\leq\exp\left({\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\big(-1+R_{i}(x2^{-t+i})\big)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left({\sum_{i=j^{\star}+4/\delta^{2}}^{t-1}\big(-1+R_{i}(x2^{-t+i})\big)}\right)\leq\exp\left({\sum_{i=j^{\star}+4/\delta^{2}}^{t-1}\big(-1+e^{-2}\big)}\right).

Note that the sum in the exponential is non-empty, as the definition of
t_{0}
implies that
j^{\star}\leq t-\log_{2}\big(|x|/(2\delta)\big)
and as
|x|>2^{2^{17}}
we have
\log_{2}\big(|x|/(2\delta)\big)>4/\delta^{2}+1
.
Thus

\displaystyle a_{t+1}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Big(\big(\log_{2}(|x|/(2\delta))-4/\delta^{2}\big)\big(-1+e^{-2}\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Big(-\big(4/\delta^{2}-\log_{2}(2\delta)\big)\big(-1+e^{-2}\big)\Big)\cdot|x|^{(-1+e^{-2})/\ln 2}.

This implies that
a_{t}(x)\leq|x|^{-1.2}
, since numerically
(-1+e^{-2})/\ln 2\leq-1.24
and for all
x
with
|x|>2^{2^{17}}
additionally
\exp(-(4/\delta^{2}-\log_{2}(2\delta))(-1+e^{-2}))\cdot|x|^{-0.04}\leq 1
.
∎

A close inspection of the previous proof suggests that actually
a_{t}(x)\sim|x|^{-c}
with
c=1/\ln 2\approx 1.44
. This would be interesting (and it is harder) to prove and it may have important consequences, but we will not need that; for our purpose it is enough to know that
|\varphi|
is integrable.
Next we prove the last remaining claim in Lemma
2.1
.

Lemma 2.12
.

H
is absolute continuous and almost surely positive.

Proof.

\varphi
is a characteristic function and therefore bounded by 1. Thus by Lemma
2.11
it is integrable and this implies the absolute continuity of
H
.
Next we argue that
H
is almost surely positive. We have shown that
H_{t}\geq 0
for all
t
and as
H_{t}\overset{t\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}H
it follows that
H\geq 0
. Furthermore we have just shown that
H
is indeed a continuous random variable and therefore
P(H=0)=0
and consequently
H>0
almost surely.

∎

Conclusion.

We have shown that
2^{-t}|I_{t}|
is close to
H_{t}
, which is a martingale that converges to
H
, a continuous random variable. Concluding this subsection we infer Lemma
2.1
from these statements.

Proof of Lemma
2.1
.

In Lemma
2.6
we have shown that for all
t\in\mathbb{N}

\textnormal{d}(|I_{t}|,J_{t})=\textnormal{d}(2^{-t}|I_{t}|,2^{-t}J_{t})\leq 2\cdot 4^{t}/n.

Moreover in Lemma
2.12
we have shown convergence of
2^{-t}J_{t}
to
H
in
\mathcal{L}^{2}
and therefore also in distribution. Thus for all
\varepsilon>0

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\big|P\big({2^{-t}|I_{t}|\geq x}\big)-P\big({2^{-t}J_{t}\geq x}\big)\big|\leq\varepsilon/2

as well as

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\big|P\big({2^{-t}J_{t}\geq x}\big)-P(H\geq x)\big|\leq\varepsilon/2.

and therefore the claimed convergence follows by the triangle inequality.
Lemma
2.12
shows the final claim:
H
is continuous and almost surely positive.
∎

Now that we have proven Lemma
2.1
, we state and prove a simple corollary for later reference.

Corollary 2.13
.

Let
t_{1}=\lfloor(1/3)\log_{2}n\rfloor
. Then with high probability

|I_{t_{1}}|=\Theta\big(n^{1/3}\big).

Proof.

Lemma
2.1
yields that

\displaystyle P\big(|I_{t_{1}}|=o(n^{1/3})\big)\leq P\big(H=2^{-t_{1}}o(n^{1/3})\big)+o(1)=P\big(H=o(1)\big)+o(1).

Since
H
has a density this is
o(1)
. Moreover, by applying again Lemma
2.1

\displaystyle P\big(|I_{t_{1}}|=\omega(n^{1/3})\big)\leq P\big(H=2^{-t_{1}}\omega(n^{1/3})\big)+o(1)=P\big(H=\omega(1)\big)+o(1).

However, since
P(H\geq h)\to 0
when
h\to\infty
the proof is completed.
∎

2.4
Proof of Lemma
2.2

We begin with a simple lemma that determines the expected number of informed and uninformed vertices after a given round.

Lemma 2.14
.

For any
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[|U_{t+1}|\bigm|I_{t}\big]=\big(|U_{t}|/n\big)^{2}\,n\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}\big[|I_{t+1}|\bigm|I_{t}\big]=2|I_{t}|-|I_{t}|^{2}/n.

Proof.

From the definition of
pull
we know
|I_{t+1}|=|I_{t}|+\text{Bin}\big(n-|I_{t}|,|I_{t}|/n\big)
, see also (
2.2
), thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[|I_{t+1}|\bigm|I_{t}\big]=|I_{t}|+\big(n-|I_{t}|\big)\cdot\frac{|I_{t}|}{n}=2|I_{t}|-\frac{|I_{t}|^{2}}{n}.

Using the relation
|I_{t}|=n-|U_{t}|
yields directly the second claim.
∎

A key property that simplifies greatly the computations in this section is the following observation, in a similar form introduced in
[
5
]
and also applied in
[
6
]
.

Lemma 2.15
.

For any
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

\displaystyle\textnormal{Var}\big[|I_{t+1}|\bigm|I_{t}\big]\leq\min\big\{\mathbb{E}\big[|I_{t+1}|\bigm|I_{t}\big],\mathbb{E}\big[|U_{t+1}|\bigm|I_{t}\big]\big\}.

Proof.

As
|I_{t+1}|=|I_{t}|+\text{Bin}\big(|U_{t}|,|I_{t}|/n\big)
and
|U_{t}|=n-|I_{t}|
,

\displaystyle\textnormal{Var}\big[|I_{t+1}|\bigm|I_{t}\big]=\text{Var}\big[|I_{t}|+\text{Bin}(|U_{t}|,|I_{t}|/n)\bigm|I_{t}\big]=\text{Var}\big[\text{Bin}(|U_{t}|,|I_{t}|/n)\bigm|I_{t}\big]=\frac{|U_{t}|^{2}\cdot|I_{t}|}{n^{2}}.

This is obviously bounded from above by
\mathbb{E}\big[|U_{t+1}|\bigm|I_{t}\big]=|U_{t}|^{2}/n
as well as by
\mathbb{E}\big[|I_{t+1}|\bigm|I_{t}\big]=|I_{t}|+|U_{t}|\cdot|I_{t}|/n
.
∎

Lemma
2.15
and Chebychev’s inequality ensure that the number of informed vertices is highly concentrated around its expectation as soon as enough vertices are informed. Compare the next lemma to
[
6
, Lem. 3.4]
for a similar statement for
push
.

Lemma 2.16
.

Let
t_{1}=\lfloor\log_{2}(n^{1/3})\rfloor
. For
t\in\mathbb{N},\ 0<\varepsilon<1/4
let
C_{t}
denote the event

\Big||I_{t+1}|-\mathbb{E}\big[|I_{t+1}|\bigm|I_{t}\big]\Big|\leq M(I_{t})^{1/2+\varepsilon}+n^{\varepsilon},\penalty\ \text{where}\penalty\ M(I_{t})=\min\big\{\mathbb{E}\big[|I_{t+1}|\bigm|I_{t}\big],\mathbb{E}\big[|U_{t+1}|\bigm|I_{t}\big]\big\}.

Then

\displaystyle P\left(\bigcap\limits_{t\geq t_{1}}C_{t}\bigm|I_{t_{1}}\right)=1-o(1).

Proof.

Observe that Corollary
2.13
implies whp

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[|I_{t+1}|\mid I_{t}\big]\geq|I_{t}|\geq|I_{t_{1}}|\geq n^{1/4}\quad\text{for all}\quad t\geq t_{1}.

(2.18)

Set
n^{\prime}:=n-n^{1/2+\varepsilon/3}
. Then

P\left(\overline{C_{t}}\bigm|I_{t}\right)=P\left(\overline{C_{t}}\mathbf{1}_{|I_{t}|>n^{\prime}}\bigm|I_{t}\right)+P\left(\overline{C_{t}}\mathbf{1}_{|I_{t}|\leq n^{\prime}}\bigm|I_{t}\right).

If
|I_{t}|>n^{\prime}
, then
|U_{t}|<n^{1/2+\varepsilon/3}
and so
M(I_{t})\leq\mathbb{E}[|U_{t+1}|\penalty\ |\penalty\ I_{t}]=|U_{t}|^{2}/n\leq n^{2\varepsilon/3}
. In that case
\overline{C_{t}}
thus implies that
|U_{t+1}|\geq n^{\varepsilon}\geq n^{\varepsilon/3}\mathbb{E}[|U_{t+1}|\penalty\ |\penalty\ I_{t}]
. By Markov’s inequality

P\left(\overline{C_{t}}\mathbf{1}_{|I_{t}|>n^{\prime}}\bigm|I_{t}\right)\leq P\big(|U_{t+1}\geq n^{\varepsilon/3}\mathbb{E}[|U_{t+1}|\penalty\ |\penalty\ I_{t}]\bigm|I_{t}\big)\leq n^{-\varepsilon/3}.

If
|I_{t}|\leq n^{\prime}
, then
|U_{t}|\geq n^{1/2+\varepsilon/3}
and using (
2.18
) also
M(I_{t})\geq\min\{n^{1/4},|U_{t}|^{2}/n\}\geq n^{2\varepsilon/3}
.
In this case, using Lemma
2.15
,
\overline{C_{t}}
implies that

\Big||I_{t+1}|-\mathbb{E}\big[|I_{t+1}|\bigm|I_{t}\big]\Big|>M(I_{t})^{1/2+\varepsilon}\geq n^{2\varepsilon^{2}/3}\text{Var}\big[|I_{t+1}|\bigm|I_{t}\big]^{1/2}.

By Chebychev’s inequality

\displaystyle P\left(\overline{C_{t}}\mathbf{1}_{|I_{t}|\leq n^{\prime}}\bigm|I_{t}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\penalty\ P\left(\Big||I_{t+1}|-\mathbb{E}\big[|I_{t+1}|\bigm|I_{t}\big]\Big|>n^{2\varepsilon^{2}/3}\text{Var}\big[|I_{t+1}|\bigm|I_{t}\big]^{1/2}\Bigm|I_{t}\right)\leq n^{-\varepsilon^{2}}.

By combining both cases we get the very crude bound

\displaystyle P\big(\overline{C_{t}}\bigm|I_{t}\big)\leq n^{-\varepsilon^{2}}\quad\text{for all }t\geq t_{1}.

(2.19)

The large deviation bounds (
1.1
) give us that
X_{n}\geq 2\log_{2}n
has exponentially small probability. Thus

P\left(\bigcup_{t\geq 2\log_{2}n}\overline{C_{t}}\mid I_{t_{1}}\right)=o(1).

A union bound and (
2.19
), applied to
O(\log n)
many
t_{1}\leq t\leq t_{2}
, then yield the claim.
∎

Lemma
2.16
shows that
|I_{t}|
is closely concentrated around its (conditional) expectation in all rounds.
This translates directly to concentration of
|U_{t+1}|
around
(|U_{t}|/n)^{2}n=\mathbb{E}[|U_{t+1}|\bigm|U_{t}]
for all
t\geq t_{1}
.
Using this, we are now ready to prove Lemma
2.2
, that is,
|U_{{t_{1}}+t}|
is close to
(|U_{t_{1}}|/n)^{2^{t}}n
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
with high probability.

Proof of Lemma
2.2
.

We assume that
|U_{t_{1}}|=\Theta\big(n^{1/3}\big)
, which we know from Corollary
2.13
has high probability.
Consequently we can apply Lemma
2.16
with
\varepsilon=1/10
and thus we get with high probability for all
t\geq t_{1}

\displaystyle\big||I_{t+1}|-\mathbb{E}\big[|I_{t+1}|\bigm|I_{t}\big]\big|\leq\Big(\min\big\{\mathbb{E}\big[|I_{t+1}|\bigm|I_{t}\big],\mathbb{E}\big[|U_{t+1}|\bigm|I_{t}\big]\big\}\Big)^{3/5}+n^{1/10}.

(2.20)

For the rest of this proof we assume in addition (
2.20
), that is, we assume that
(|I_{t}|)_{t\geq t_{1}}
(and thus also
(|U_{t}|)_{t\geq t_{1}}
and
(\mathbb{E}[|I_{t+1}|\bigm|I_{t}])_{t\geq t_{1}}
) are sequences of numbers with the aforementioned properties.
In particular, (
2.20
) implies for all
\delta>0
that
\big|2|I_{t}|-|I_{t+1}|\big|\leq\delta|I_{t}|
for all
t\geq\ t_{1}
and
n>\delta^{-15}
, where
t_{1}=\lfloor\log_{2}(n^{1/3})\rfloor
. Therefore

\displaystyle|I_{t_{1}+s}|\leq(2+\delta)^{s}|I_{t_{1}}|\quad\text{for all}\ s\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\ \delta>0\text{ and }n>\delta^{-15}.

(2.21)

Set

\beta_{t_{1}+s}:=\big(|U_{t_{1}}|/n\big)^{2^{s}},\quad s\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Note that
t_{1}+s
is just a different way to parameterize
t\geq t_{1}
, which simplifies the notation when
t_{1}
is involved. In particular
t_{1}
is always fixed to the aforementioned value.

We will next argue that for all
t\geq t_{1}
, abbreviating
\Delta_{t}:=\big||U_{t}|-\beta_{t}n\big|
,

\Delta_{t+1}\leq\Big(\min\big\{2|I_{t}|,|U_{t}|^{2}/n\big\}\Big)^{3/5}+n^{1/10}+\Big(2|U_{t}|/n+\Delta_{t}/n\Big)\Delta_{t}.

(2.22)

To see this, note first that by using (
2.20
) and Lemma
2.14
,

\displaystyle\big||U_{t+1}|-(|U_{t}|/n)^{2}n\big|=\big||I_{t+1}|-\mathbb{E}\big[|I_{t+1}|\bigm|I_{t}\big]\big|\leq\Big(\min\big\{2|I_{t}|,|U_{t}|^{2}/n\big\}\Big)^{3/5}+n^{1/10}.

Secondly, applying the triangle inequality, i.e.,
|x+y|\leq 2|x|+|x-y|
for all

, yields

\displaystyle\big|(|U_{t}|/n)^{2}n-\beta_{t+1}n\big|

\displaystyle=\big||U_{t}|^{2}/n-\beta_{t}^{2}n\big|=\big||U_{t}|/n+\beta_{t}\big|\cdot\big||U_{t}|-\beta_{t}n\big|

\displaystyle\leq\Big(2|U_{t}|/n+\big||U_{t}|/n-\beta_{t}\big|\Big)\big||U_{t}|-\beta_{t}n\big|.

The triangle inequality then implies (
2.22
).

In the remainder of this proof we will look at the bound of

in (

) in three different ways to distinguish in each case a different behaviour. Just to wit, at first
\Delta_{t}
doubles as long as the number of informed vertices doubles. However, as soon as the doubly exponential shrinking of the uninformed vertices takes over, also the error

shrinks rapidly, so that

always remains

. In end we just make sure that

stays small and does not increase any more.

We will make this outline more precise by formulating matching claims, which we then use to infer the statement of this lemma. We prove the claims afterwards. Our first claim is that for
\delta=1/100
and
d=400

\Delta_{t_{1}+s}\leq d(2+\delta)^{s}|I_{t_{1}}|^{3/5}\quad\text{for all}\ 0\leq s\leq(14/15)\log_{2}n-t_{1}\text{ and }n>\delta^{-15}.

(
Claim 1
)

By using that
|I_{t_{1}}|=\Theta(n^{1/3}
and that
14/15-1/3+1/5=4/5<0.85
,
(
Claim 1
) implies for sufficiently large

that for

\displaystyle\Delta_{t_{1}+s}\leq d\left(2+\delta\right)^{s+1}|I_{t_{1}}|^{3/5}\leq n^{0.85}\quad\text{for all }0\leq s\leq t_{2}-t_{1}.

(2.23)

Moreover, (
2.21
) yields

\displaystyle|I_{t_{1}+s}|

\displaystyle\leq\left(2+\delta\right)^{s+1}|I_{t_{1}}|=o(n)\quad\text{for all }0\leq s\leq t_{2}-t_{1},

and therefore, as
|U_{t_{1}+s}|=n-|I_{t_{1}+s}|=\Theta(n)
,

\displaystyle\Delta_{t_{1}+s}\leq|U_{t_{1}+s}|\cdot n^{-1/10}\quad\text{for all }0\leq s\leq t_{2}-t_{1}.

(2.24)

From (
1.1
) we know that whp
X_{n}\leq\log_{2}n+2\log_{2}\ln n
, thus we need to bound
\Delta_{t}
for at most an additional
(1/15)\log_{2}n+2\log_{2}\ln n

For these steps we will need a different bound, as the bound in (
Claim 1
) is only useful as long as
t_{1}+s<\log_{2}n
; otherwise the term
(2+\delta)^{s}
blows up.
Our next claim is

\Delta_{t_{2}+s}\leq|U_{t_{2}+s}|\cdot(2+\delta)^{s}\cdot n^{-1/11}\quad\text{for all }s\in\mathbb{N}\ \text{with}\ |U_{t_{2}+s}|\geq n^{1/4}\text{ and }n\geq\delta^{-111}.

(
Claim 2
)

We saw that it suffices to apply (
Claim 2
) for at most
s\leq(1/15)\log_{2}n+2\log_{2}\ln n
additional steps. For such
s
we obtain that
(2+\delta)^{s+1}n^{-1/11}\leq n^{-1/50}
for sufficiently large
n
and therefore we conclude from (
2.24
) for all
t\leq t_{2}
and (
Claim 2
) for all
t>t_{2}

\Delta_{t}\leq|U_{t}|\cdot n^{-1/50}\quad\text{for all}\quad t\geq t_{1}\quad\text{as long as}\quad|U_{t}|\geq n^{1/4}.

For
t\geq t_{1}
such that
|U_{t}|\leq n^{1/4}
the claim of the Lemma follows trivially.

Now we show (
Claim 1
) and (
Claim 2
), starting with (
Claim 1
), which we are going to do by induction.
The base case follows directly from (
2.22
). Let
t\geq t_{1}
, from (
2.22
) and using that
|I_{t}|=\Omega\big(n^{1/3}\big)
we get that

\displaystyle\Delta_{t+1}

\displaystyle\leq 2|I_{t}|^{3/5}+\big(2+\Delta_{t}/n\big)\Delta_{t}.

Observe that by using the induction hypothesis, we obtain that

\displaystyle\Delta_{t_{1}+s}\leq|U_{t_{1}+s}|\cdot n^{-1/10}

and thus using (
2.21
), the induction hypothesis, and

gives us

\displaystyle\Delta_{t_{1}+s+1}

\displaystyle\leq 2\big((2+\delta)^{s}|I_{t_{1}}|\big)^{3/5}+(2+\delta/2)\left(d(2+\delta)^{s}|I_{t_{1}}|^{3/5}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{2}{d}(2+\delta)^{-2s/5}+2+\frac{\delta}{2}\right)d(2+\delta)^{s}|I_{t_{1}}|^{3/5}.

Our choice of

and

guarantees that
{(2/d)}(2+\delta)^{(-2/5)s}\leq{(2/d)}(2+\delta)^{(-2/5)}\leq\delta/2
for all

and (

) follows.

We continue with (

) that we we will prove by induction, too.
To that end, we observe that (

) also implies that as long as

and

\displaystyle\big||U_{t}|^{2}/n-|U_{t+1}|\big|\leq\delta|U_{t+1}|/4.

(2.25)

The base case of (

) follows directly from (

). For the induction step we apply (

\displaystyle\Delta_{t_{2}+s+1}

Using the assumption that

we get, as

).
∎

2.5
Proof of Lemma
2.3

We will prove this lemma in two steps, first we start with a simple lemma showing that, if there are much more than
\sqrt{n}
uninformed vertices remaining,
pull
will not end in the next round. Moreover if there are substantially less than
\sqrt{n}
uninformed the protocol will end in the next round.

Lemma 2.17
.

Let
t,t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
|U_{t}|\leq\sqrt{n}/\ln n
and
|U_{t^{\prime}}|\geq\sqrt{n}\ln n
. Then

P(|U_{t+1}|=0\mid I_{t})=o(1)\quad\text{and}\quad P(|U_{t^{\prime}+1}|>0\mid I_{t^{\prime}})=o(1).

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[|U_{t+1}|\bigm|I_{t}\big]=\frac{|U_{t}|^{2}}{n}\leq\ln^{-2}n.

This yields with Markov’s inequality the claim for
t
.
To see the claim for
t^{\prime}
we observe first that the probability of one uninformed vertex
v\in U_{t^{\prime}}
being informed in the next round is

\displaystyle P\big(u\in I_{t^{\prime}+1}\bigm|u\in U_{t^{\prime}}\big)=\frac{|I_{t^{\prime}}|}{n}=\left(1-\frac{|U_{t^{\prime}}|}{n}\right).

If
|U_{t^{\prime}+1}|=0
, then all
|U_{t^{\prime}}|
uninformed vertices need to be informed in the next round, and as that happens independently, we get

\displaystyle P\big(|U_{t^{\prime}+1}|=0\bigm|I_{t^{\prime}}\big)=\left(1-\frac{|U_{t^{\prime}}|}{n}\right)^{|U_{t^{\prime}}|}\leq e^{-|U_{t^{\prime}}|^{2}/n}\leq e^{-\ln^{2}n}.

∎

Besides the two cases that we considered in the previous lemma a third case is also possible. Indeed, if there are about
\sqrt{n}
uninformed vertices, the process ending in the next round may happen with some non-trivial probability. The next lemma shows that, however, that this is very unlikely to happen. This means that once the process crosses the threshold of
\sqrt{n}
it will terminate in the next round with high probability.

Lemma 2.18
.

With high probability for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
,

|U_{t}|\notin\Big[\sqrt{n}/\ln n,\sqrt{n}\ln n\Big].

Proof.

Let
t_{1}=\lfloor\log_{2}(n^{1/3})\rfloor
and consider the events

\displaystyle\left\{|I_{t_{1}}|=\Theta\big(n^{1/3}\big)\right\},

(Event 1)

and with
\eta=\eta(n)=\log_{2}n+\log_{2}\ln n-\lfloor\log_{2}n+\log_{2}\ln n\rfloor
,

\displaystyle\left\{\log_{2}\big(2^{-t_{1}}|I_{t_{1}}|\big)\notin\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left[k+\eta-\frac{3\log_{2}\ln(n^{2})}{\ln n},k+\eta+\frac{3\log_{2}\ln(n^{2})}{\ln n}\right]\right\},

(Event 2)

as well as

\displaystyle\bigcap_{\penalty\ t\geq 0}\left\{\Big||U_{t_{1}+t}|-\big(|U_{t_{1}}|/n\big)^{2^{t}}n\Big|\leq|U_{t_{1}+t}|\cdot n^{-1/50}+n^{1/4}\right\}.

(Event 3)

All these events occur with high probability.
For (
Event 1
) this was already established in Corollary
2.13
.
To see the claim for (
Event 2
) we observe that
\log_{2}\big(2^{-t_{1}}|I_{t_{1}}|\big)
converges to the continuous random variable
\log_{2}H
, see Lemma
2.1
, and that the right side in (
Event 2
) converges to a
\log_{2}H
null-set. (
Event 3
) was handled in Lemma
2.2
.

In the remainder of this proof we condition on these events, that is, we assume that
(|I_{t}|)_{t\geq t_{1}}
(and thus also
(|U_{t}|)_{t\geq t_{1}}
) are sequences of numbers with the aforementioned properties.

Observe that for
n^{1/3}<a<b<n-n^{1/3}
, (
Event 1
) and (
Event 3
) imply for
t\geq t_{1}
and
n
large enough

(|U_{t_{1}}|/n)^{2^{t-t_{1}}}n\notin[a/2,2b]\quad\Longrightarrow\quad|U_{t}|\notin[a,b];

to see this, note that by assumption
|U_{t}|=(1+o(1))(|U_{t_{1}}|/n)^{2^{t-t_{1}}}n
and so for large enough
n
if
|U_{t}|\in[a,b]
, then with room to spare
(|U_{t_{1}}|/n)^{2^{t-t_{1}}}n\in[a/2,2b]
.

Set
u_{t}=(1-|I_{t_{1}}|/n)^{2^{t-t_{1}}}n
and define
T_{u}=\min\{t\in\mathbb{N}:u_{t}<2\sqrt{n}\ln n\}
as well as
T_{\ell}=\min\{t\in\mathbb{N}:u_{t}<\sqrt{n}/(2\ln n)\}
.
With these definitions we can apply the implication we just derived to the event in the statement of the lemma and obtain that

\displaystyle T_{u}=T_{\ell}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad|U_{t}|\notin\big[\sqrt{n}/\ln n,\sqrt{n}\ln n\big].

(2.26)

Observe that (
Event 1
) implies that

u_{\lfloor(4/3)\log_{2}n\rfloor}n=\big(|U_{t_{1}}|/n\big)^{2^{\lfloor(4/3)\log_{2}n\rfloor-t_{1}}}n=o(1),

thus
T_{u},T_{\ell}<(4/3)\log_{2}n
and consequently we need to study
u_{t}
for that range of
t
only. Therefore, using
1-x=e^{-x+O(x^{2})}
for small
x
and
0\leq t<(4/3)\log_{2}n

\displaystyle u_{t}

\displaystyle=n\big(1-|I_{t_{1}}|/n\big)^{2^{t-t_{1}}}=n\cdot\exp\big(-2^{t-t_{1}}|I_{t_{1}}|/n+O(2^{t-t_{1}}|I_{t_{1}}|^{2}/n^{2})\big)

\displaystyle=\big(1+O(n^{-2/3})\big)\cdot n\cdot\exp\left(-2^{t-t_{1}}|I_{t_{1}}|/n\right).

To determine
T_{u}
and
T_{\ell}
it suffices to solve the equations

\displaystyle\exp\left(-2^{t-t_{1}}|I_{t_{1}}|/n\right)=\big(1+O(n^{-2/3})\big)c_{n}n^{-1/2},\quad\text{for }c_{n}\in\big\{2\ln n,\ 1/2\ln n\big\}.

Applying logarithms twice and using the first order expansion
\ln(1+x)=x+O(x^{2}),\ |x|<1/2
, we readily obtain that

\displaystyle T_{u}=\Big\lfloor\log_{2}n-\log_{2}(2^{-t_{1}}|I_{t_{1}}|)+\log_{2}\ln n-1-\frac{2\log_{2}\ln(n^{2})}{\ln n}+O(1/\ln n)\Big\rfloor

and

\displaystyle T_{\ell}=\Big\lfloor\log_{2}n-\log_{2}(2^{-t_{1}}|I_{t_{1}}|)+\log_{2}\ln n-1+\frac{2\log_{2}\ln(n^{2})}{\ln n}+O(1/\ln n)\Big\rfloor.

Observe that for any
x,y,z\in[0,1]
with
x\leq y
it holds that
\lfloor x+z\rfloor\neq\lfloor y+z\rfloor
if and only if
z\in[1-y,1-x)
; to see this just note that if
z<1-y
, then both terms are equal to 0, if
z\geq 1-x
then both terms are equal to 1 and otherwise just one of them is 0.
Thus for
\eta=\log_{2}n+\log_{2}\ln n-\lfloor\log_{2}n+\log_{2}\ln n\rfloor
and
n
large enough

\displaystyle\log_{2}\big(2^{-t_{1}}|I_{t_{1}}|\big)\notin\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left[k+\eta-\frac{3\log_{2}\ln(n^{2})}{\ln n},k+\eta+\frac{3\log_{2}\ln(n^{2})}{\ln n}\right]\quad\Longrightarrow\quad T_{u}=T_{\ell}.

Since we have assumed (
Event 2
)
we have just established that
T_{u}=T_{\ell}
, and together with (
2.26
) the proof is completed.
