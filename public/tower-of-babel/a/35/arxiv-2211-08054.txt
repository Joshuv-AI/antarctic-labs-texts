Quantitative Estimates for Operator-Valued and Infinitesimal Boolean and Monotone Limit Theorems Thanks: …
Octavio Arizmendi, Marwa Banna, Pei-Lun Tseng

Abstract
. We provide Berry-Esseen bounds for sums of operator-valued Boolean and monotone independent variables, in terms of the first moments of the summands. Our bounds are on the level of Cauchy transforms as well as the Lévy distance. As applications, we obtain quantitative bounds for the corresponding CLTs, provide a quantitative ”fourth moment theorem” for monotone independent random variables including the operator-valued case, and generalize the results by Hao and Popa on matrices with Boolean entries. Our approach relies on a Lindeberg method that we develop for sums of Boolean/monotone independent random variables. Furthermore, we push this approach to the infinitesimal setting to obtain the first quantitative estimates for the operator-valued infinitesimal free, Boolean and monotone CLT.

1.
Introduction

Noncommutative probability theory deals with operators from a probabilistic viewpoint. Like any probabilistic theory, one of its main notions is that of independence. A special feature of noncommutative probability is however the diversity of such notions, where five of them are considered to be the most fundamental following the classification of natural products of Muraki
[
24
]
from the categorical axiomatization viewpoint of Ben Ghorbal and Schürmann
[
13
]
. Other than the well studied tensor independence and free independence, the are three other notions satisfying such axioms: Boolean independence, monotone independence and its mirror image antimonotone independence.
Operator-valued (OV) and operator-valued infinitesimal (OVI) extensions of these different notions of independence also exist, where independence is defined with respect to some conditional expectation and some completely positive linear
\mathcal{B}
-bimodule map respectively, see Sections
2.1
and
2.2
.

In this paper, we are mainly concerned with operator-valued Boolean and monotone independence. We are interested in studying distributions of sums of independent variables
x_{1}+\dots+x_{n}
, for which we prove Berry-Esseen results that are further extended to the operator-valued infinitesimal setting. One should note that Boolean and monotone independences have the special feature that constants are not independent from any non-trivial algebra
\mathcal{A}
. Thus adapting strategies for free independence, as for example in
[
6
]
, is not direct. For instance, when working with Cauchy transforms one has the problem that (Boolean or monotone) independence of
X
from
Y
, does not imply the independence of
X
from
(z-Y)^{-1}
, for a complex number
z
. Thus in order to make precise estimates of joint moments one needs a more detailed analysis.

Our main results, stated in Section
1.1
, have various applications including quantitative bounds for the operator-valued Boolean and monotone central limit theorems. These bounds are on the level of the operator-valued Cauchy transforms as well as the Lévy distance and are merely in terms of the moments of fourth and second order, see Sections
4.1
and
4.2
. As further applications, we provide in Section
4.3
a quantitative ”fourth moment theorem” for the class of infinitely divisible measures with respect to the monotone convolution including the operator-valued case. Our approach also generalizes the results by Hao and Popa
[
30
]
on matrices with Boolean entries to include general variance profiles, see Section
5
.
Finally, as our results extend to the infinitesimal setting, we provide in Section
4.4
the first quantitative estimates for the operator-valued infinitesimal free, Boolean and monotone central limit theorems. Moreover, we provide in Appendix
A
an algebraic construction of the operator-valued infinitesimal product in the free, Boolean and monotone settings.

Our approach relies on the Lindeberg method
[
19
]
which is a powerful method that allows comparing distributions of sums of independent variables. The idea is to compare the distribution of
x_{1}+\dots+x_{n}
with that of
y_{1}+\dots+y_{n}
, by keeping track of the effect of changing
x_{i}
by
y_{i}
, one at a time. This method has been used in noncommutative probability previously, in the context of random matrices, free and exchangeable random variables; see the papers
[
5
,
6
,
10
]
.

1.1.
Statement of results

Let
(\mathcal{A},E,\mathcal{B})

C^{\ast}
-probability space and let

Assumption 1
.

The two families

and
\{y_{1},\dots,y_{N}\}
consist of self-adjoint elements in

•

E[x_{j}]=E[y_{j}]=0

•

E\big[x_{j}b\,x_{j}\big]=E\big[y_{j}\,b\,y_{j}\big],
for all

To state our main estimate we need to introduce first some notation on operator-valued moments

\alpha_{2}(x)=\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\|E[x_{i}^{2}]\|,\quad\alpha_{4}(x):=\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\sup\|E[x_{i}b^{*}x_{i}^{2}bx_{i}]\|\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{\alpha}_{4}(x)=\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\|E[x_{i}^{4}]\|,

where the supremum is taken over
b\in\mathcal{B}
such that

(Boolean Case)

(1.1)

If, in addition,
(\mathcal{A},\varphi)

(1.2)

Let

(Monotone Case)

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
OV Probability Theory

Operator-valued probability theory allows considering conditional expectations and gives a wider range of applicability. To define such spaces, we start by introducing some notions and recalling some definitions. Let
\mathcal{A}
be a unital algebra over
\comp
and
\mathcal{B}
a unital subalgebra of
\mathcal{A}
. We say that
\mathcal{C}
is a subalgebra of
\mathcal{A}
over
\mathcal{B}
if
\mathcal{C}
is a subalgebra of
\mathcal{A}
and
bc,cb\in\mathcal{C}
, for all
b\in\mathcal{B}
and
c\in\mathcal{C}
. A subalgebra of
\mathcal{A}
over
\mathcal{B}
may not contain the unit of
\mathcal{A}
.

For
x_{1},\dots,x_{r}\in\mathcal{A}
, let
\mathcal{B}\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{r}\rangle_{0}
denote the subalgebra of
\mathcal{A}
over
\mathcal{B}
consisting of finite sums of elements of
\{b_{1}x_{i_{1}}b_{2}\dots x_{i_{n}}b_{n+1}:b_{i}\in\mathcal{B},n\geq 1,i_{1},\dots,i_{n}\in\{1,\dots,r\}\}
. Note that, in general,
\mathcal{B}
is not contained in
\mathcal{B}\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{r}\rangle_{0}
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be another unital algebra containing
\mathcal{B}
as a subalgebra.
A map
f
from
\mathcal{A}
to
\mathcal{D}
is called
\mathcal{B}
-linear if
f(b_{1}xb_{2}+y)=b_{1}f(x)b_{2}+f(y),
for all
b_{1},b_{2}\in\mathcal{B}
and
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
. A
\mathcal{B}
-linear map
E
with values in
\mathcal{B}
is called a
conditional expectation
if
E(b)=b,
for all
b\in\mathcal{B}
. From now on we assume that
E
is a conditional expectation in the above sense. A
random variable
is an element of
\mathcal{A}
and a
random vector
is an element of
\mathcal{A}^{r}
for any
r\geq 1
.

A
W^{\ast}
-probability space is a pair
(\mathcal{A},\varphi)
where
\mathcal{A}
is a von Neumann algebra and
\varphi
is a faithful normal state on
\mathcal{A}
.

(\mathcal{A},E,\mathcal{B})
is called an
operator-valued
C^{*}
-probability space
if
\mathcal{A}
is a unital
C^{*}
-algebra,
\mathcal{B}
is a unital subalgebra, and
E:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{B}
is a conditional expectation that is completely positive. Working in such spaces allows conditioning with respect to
E
which carries many similar properties as the scalar-valued expectation
\varphi
. In addition, if
(\mathcal{A},\varphi)
is a tracial
W^{\ast}
-probability space and
\mathcal{B}
is a von Neumann subalgebra of
\mathcal{A}
, then there exists a unique conditional expectation
E:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{B}
that preserves the trace, i.e.
\varphi\circ E=\varphi
.
Note that in the tracial setting, the existence of a trace-preserving conditional expectation
E
was proved in
[
39
]
. As in the Boolean and monotone cases, the state
\varphi
is non-tracial, we will only consider the settings where there exists a conditional expectation
E
with
\varphi\circ E=\varphi
.

A main difference, however, is that notions of positivity for operator-valued distributions need to hold for all matrix amplifications.
It is also needed to extend the different notions of independence to the operator-valued setting, where independence is defined with respect to the conditional expectation
E
. This was done over time, where the notion of OV free independence was first introduced in
[
40
]
. On the other hand, Boolean independence, introduced by Speicher and Woroudi
[
35
]
, and monotone independence, introduced by Muraki
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
, were extended later on to the operator-valued setting (see
[
29
,
28
,
16
,
33
]
).
We recall these notions of independence in the following definition.

Definition 2.1
.

Given an OV
C^{*}
-probability space
(\mathcal{A},E,\mathcal{B})
.

•

Sub-algebras
(\mathcal{A}_{i})_{i\in I}
of
\mathcal{A}
that contain
\mathcal{B}
are called

freely independent (or just free for short) over
\mathcal{B}
if for
i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{n}\in I
,
i_{1}\neq i_{2}\neq i_{3}\dots\neq i_{n}
, and
x_{j}\in\mathcal{A}_{i_{j}}
with
E[x_{j}]=0
for all
j=1,2,\dots,n
, then

\displaystyle E[x_{1}\cdots x_{n}]=0.

•

(The possibly non-unital)
\mathcal{B}
-bimodule subalgebras
(\mathcal{A}_{i})_{i\in I}
of
\mathcal{A}
are called
Boolean independent over
\mathcal{B}
if for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathcal{A}
such that
x_{j}\in\mathcal{A}_{i_{j}}
where
i_{1}\neq i_{2}\neq\dots\neq i_{n}\in I
, we have

\displaystyle E[x_{1}\cdots x_{n}]=E[x_{1}]\cdots E[x_{n}].

•

Assume that
\Lambda
is equipped with a linear order
<
. (The possibly non-unital)
\mathcal{B}
-bimodule subalgebras
(\mathcal{A}_{i})_{i\in\Lambda}
of
\mathcal{A}
are called
monotone independent over
\mathcal{B}
if

\displaystyle E[x_{1}\cdots x_{j}\cdots x_{n}]=E[x_{1}\cdots x_{j-1}E[x_{j}]x_{j+1}\cdots x_{n}]

whenever
x_{j}\in\mathcal{A}_{i_{j}},i_{j}\in\Lambda
for all
j
and
i_{j-1}<i_{j}>i_{j+1}
, where one of the inequalities is eliminated if
j=1
or
j=n
.

A crucial difference between monotone independence from the free and Boolean independence is the lack of symmetry, so when talking about monotone tuples of variables we need to specify an order. Therefore, we say subalgebras
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\beta}
are monotone independent in the sense that they are monotone independent with the order
\alpha<\beta
and we denote it by
A_{\alpha}\prec A_{\beta}.

Given an OV probability space
(\mathcal{A},E,\mathcal{B})
, elements
(x_{i})_{i\in I}
of
\mathcal{A}
are said to be freely independent over
\mathcal{B}
if the unital algebras generated by elements
x_{i}
over
\mathcal{B}
are freely independent. Similarly,
(x_{i})_{i\in I}
are said to be Boolean (respectively monotone) independent if the non-unital
\mathcal{B}
-bimodule subalgebras generated by
x_{i},(i\in I)
over
\mathcal{B}
form a Boolean (respectively monotone) independent family. Since the notion of monotone independence depends on the order, we write
x\prec y
or
\mathcal{A}_{x}\prec\mathcal{A}_{y}
whenever
x,y
are monotone independent.

As in the scalar, noncommutative joint distributions are defined in the operator-valued realm as the collection of all possible
\mathcal{B}
-valued mixed moments.

Definition 2.2

(
\mathcal{B}
-valued joint distributions)
.

Let
X=(x_{1},\dots,x_{r})
be a random vector in an OV
C^{*}
-probability space
(\mathcal{A},E,\mathcal{B})
and let
i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\in\{1,\dots,r\}
for
n\geq 1
. The multilinear map
\mu^{X}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}
defined by

\mu^{X}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}(b_{1},\dots,b_{k-1})=E(x_{i_{1}}b_{1}\cdots b_{k-1}x_{i_{k}})

is called the
(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
-moment of
X
. Then the

\mathcal{B}
-distribution
of
X
is defined as the collection of all possible
(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
-moments of
X
:

\mu^{X}=\big\{\mu^{X}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}(b_{1},\dots,b_{k-1})\ |\ i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\in\{1,\dots,r\},b_{1},\dots,b_{k-1}\in\mathcal{B}\big\}.

Moreover in the OV setting, convergence in distribution means convergence in norm of all joint
\mathcal{B}
-valued moments. More precisely, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
X_{n}=(x_{n}^{(i)})_{i\in I}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
and
X=(x_{i})_{i\in I}\in\mathcal{A}
, we say
(X_{n})_{n}
converges to
X
in distribution over
\mathcal{B}
if for any
k\geq 0
,
i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\in\{1,\dots,r\}
and
b_{0},b_{1},\dots,b_{k}\in\mathcal{B}
,

\|\mu^{X_{n}}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}(b_{1},\dots,b_{k-1})-\mu^{X}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}(b_{1},\dots,b_{k-1})\|\longrightarrow 0\quad\text{ as }\quad n\to\infty.

Let us now introduce the notion of Cauchy transforms, which is a crucial tool to study distributions. Suppose that
(\mathcal{A},E,\mathcal{B})
is an OV
C^{*}
-probability space. The resolvent of an element
x\in\mathcal{A}
, is given by
G_{x}(b)=(b-x)^{-1}
for any
b\in\mathcal{B}
such that
b-x
is invertible. If
x=x^{*}\in\mathcal{A}
, then the
OV Cauchy transform of
x
is given by

\mathcal{G}_{x}^{\mathcal{B}}(b):=E[G_{x}(b)]\quad\text{ for all }b\in\mathbb{H}^{+}(\mathcal{B}),

where
\mathbb{H}^{+}(\mathcal{B})
denotes the OV upper half plane of
\mathcal{B}
defined by

\mathbb{H}^{+}(\mathcal{B}):=\big\{b\in\mathcal{B}\mid{\Im}m(\mathfrak{b})=\frac{1}{2i}(b-b^{*})>0\big\}.

Note that for each
x=x^{*}\in\mathcal{A}
,
b-x
is invertible for all
b\in\mathbb{H}^{+}(\mathcal{B})
over which
\mathcal{G}_{x}^{\mathcal{B}}
is hence well-defined. If
\mathcal{B}=\mathbb{C}
, then we have the (scalar-version)
Cauchy transform of
x

\mathcal{G}_{x}(z):=\varphi[G_{x}(z)]\quad\text{ for all }z\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

where
\mathbb{C}^{+}
denotes the upper half complex plane. Note that

{\mathcal{G}_{x}}
encodes all the moments of
x
; indeed, for any
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
such that
|z|<\|x\|
, we have

\mathcal{G}_{x}(z)=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\varphi(x^{n})}{z^{n+1}}.

For later use, we note that for a given
x=x^{*}\in\mathcal{A}
, we obtain by the resolvent identity

\|G_{x}(z)\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{A},\varphi)}^{2}=\varphi\left[(\bar{z}-x)^{-1}(z-x)^{-1}\right]=-\frac{{\Im}m(\mathcal{G}_{x}(z))}{{\Im}m(z)}\qquad\text{for all }z\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

which together with the fact that
-\frac{1}{\pi}\Im(\mathcal{G}_{x}(t+i\varepsilon))\,\mathrm{d}t
is a probability measure for every
\varepsilon>0
yields that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|G_{x}(t+i\varepsilon)\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{A},\varphi)}^{2}dt=\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon}\qquad\text{for all }\varepsilon>0.

However in the OV setting, the OV Cauchy transform
\mathcal{G}_{x}^{\mathcal{B}}
alone does not encode all possible moments of
x
and one would need for this purpose all the matrix amplifications
\{\mathcal{G}_{1_{k}\otimes x}^{M_{k}(\mathcal{B})}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\mathcal{G}_{x}^{\mathcal{B}}
defined for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
by

\mathcal{G}_{1_{k}\otimes x}^{M_{k}(\mathcal{B})}(b):=id_{k}\otimes E[G_{1_{k}\otimes x}(b)]\quad\text{ for all }b\in M_{k}(\mathcal{B})\text{ such that }b-1_{k}\otimes x\text{ is invertible. }

For more details on this matter, see
[
40
]
.

The combinatorial approach for OV free probability theory was first introduced by Speicher
[
34
]
. The OV Boolean case was later developed by Popa
[
29
]
while the OV monotone case was investigated in
[
16
,
28
]

We denote by
NC(n)
the set of non-crossing partitions of
[n]:=\{1,\dots,n\}

\pi=\{V_{1},\dots,V_{r}\}
such that
V_{i}
and

do not cross for any
1\leq i,j\leq r
. The blocks of
\pi

V_{1},\dots,V_{r}
and the size (in this case

) of the partition is denoted by
|\pi|
. We denote furthermore by
NC_{2}(n)
the set of non-crossing pair partitions of
[n]
. In order to describe cumulants for various notions of independence, we give further notation:

Notation 2.3
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{A}

\mathcal{B}

•

For

A crucial property of OV free and Boolean cumulants is the fact that they capture independence over
\mathcal{B}
. Indeed, the notions of free and Boolean independence can be characterized by the property of vanishing mixed cumulants as described below:

Proposition 2.5
.

Suppose that
(\mathcal{A},E,\mathcal{B})
is an OV
C^{*}
-probability space.

•

that contain
\mathcal{B}

\mathcal{B}

•

\mathcal{B}
-bimodule subalgebras

\mathcal{B}
if and only if for

and
i_{1},\dots,i_{s}\in[n]

\beta_{s}^{\mathcal{B}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{s})=0.

In the free and Boolean case, it is easy to see - using the above property of vanishing of mixed cumulants - that independence is preserved when lifted to matrices. In the following proposition, we prove directly and without relying on cumulants that the statement remains valid in the case of monotone independence.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},E,\mathcal{B})
be an OV
C^{*}
-probability space and
\mathcal{A}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{n}
be subalgebras over
\mathcal{B}

\mathcal{A}_{1}\prec A_{2}\prec\cdots\prec\mathcal{A}_{n}
over
\mathcal{B}

over

in the OV
C^{*}
-probability space
(M_{N}(\mathcal{A}),id_{N}\otimes E,M_{N}(\mathcal{B}))
.

Proof.

Let
A_{m}=[a^{(m)}_{r,s}]_{r,s=1}^{N}\in M_{N}(\mathcal{A}_{i_{m}})
for each
m
where
i_{1},\cdots,i_{k}\in[n]
with
i_{1}\neq i_{2}\neq\cdots\neq i_{n}

i_{k_{0}-1}<i_{k_{o}}>i_{k_{0}+1}

\displaystyle\Big[(id_{N}\otimes E)[A_{1}A_{2}\cdots A_{k_{0}-1}A_{k_{0}}A_{k_{0}+1}\cdots A_{n}]\Big]_{i,j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{n-1}\in[N]}E\big[a_{i,i_{1}}^{(1)}a_{i_{1},i_{2}}^{(2)}\cdots a^{(k_{0}-1)}_{i_{k_{0}-2},i_{k_{0}-1}}a^{(k_{0})}_{i_{k_{0}-1},i_{k_{0}}}a^{(k_{0}+1)}_{i_{k_{0}},i_{k_{0}+1}}\cdots a^{(n)}_{i_{n-1},j}\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{n-1}\in[N]}E\big[a_{i,i_{1}}^{(1)}a_{i_{1},i_{2}}^{(2)}\cdots a^{(k_{0}-1)}_{i_{k_{0}-2},i_{k_{0}-1}}E\big[a^{(k_{0})}_{i_{k_{0}-1},i_{k_{0}}}\big]a^{(k_{0}+1)}_{i_{k_{0}},i_{k_{0}+1}}\cdots a^{(n)}_{i_{n-1},j}\big]

\displaystyle E\Big[\sum_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{n-1}\in[N]}a_{i,i_{1}}^{(1)}a_{i_{1},i_{2}}^{(2)}\cdots a^{(k_{0}-1)}_{i_{k_{0}-2},i_{k_{0}-1}}E\big[a^{(k_{0})}_{i_{k_{0}-1},i_{k_{0}}}\big]a^{(k_{0}+1)}_{i_{k_{0}},i_{k_{0}+1}}\cdots a^{(n)}_{i_{n-1},j}\Big]

2.3.
Positivity of conditional expectations

Since the conditional expectation
E
is positive, it induces a
\mathcal{B}
-valued pre-inner product

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:\ \mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{B},\ (x,y)\mapsto E[x^{\ast}y]

with respect to which
\mathcal{A}
becomes a right pre-Hilbert
\mathcal{B}
-module. In particular, we have the following analogue of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality:

\|E[x^{\ast}y]\|^{2}\leq\|E[x^{\ast}x]\|\|E[y^{\ast}y]\|.

(2.2)

The positivity of
E
also implies the following important inequality

\|E[x^{\ast}wx]\|\leq\|w\|\|E[x^{\ast}x]\|,

(2.3)

which holds for all
x\in\mathcal{A}^{n}
and
w\in M_{n}(\mathcal{A})
satisfying
w\geq 0
.

If
\mu
and
\nu
are two Borel probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
, then

•

the
Lévy distance
is defined by

L(\mu,\nu):=\inf\{\varepsilon>0\mid\forall t\in\mathbb{R}:\ \mathcal{F}_{\mu}(t-\varepsilon)-\varepsilon\leq\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(t)\leq\mathcal{F}_{\mu}(t+\varepsilon)+\varepsilon\};

•

the
Kolmogorov distance
is defined by

\Delta(\mu,\nu):=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|\mathcal{F}_{\mu}(t)-\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(t)|.

The Lévy distance provides a metrization of convergence in distribution and may be bounded in terms of the associated Cauchy transforms of
\mu
and

as follows:

L(\mu,\nu)\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{\pi}}+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|{\Im}m(\mathcal{G}_{\mu}(t+i\varepsilon))-{\Im}m(\mathcal{G}_{\nu}(t+i\varepsilon))|\,\mathrm{d}t.

(2.4)

For a proof of (

), we refer to Appendix B in

]
.

3.
Proof of the main results

In order to prove our main results, Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
, we rely on an operator-valued Lindeberg method, which allows replacing the

’s by the

’s, one at a time, and controlling the error of such a replacement. We follow the first lines of the proof of Theorem 3.1 in
[
6
]
for which we recall the following basic algebraic identity:

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
x
and
y
be invertible in some unital complex algebra
\mathcal{A}
, then for each
m\in\mathbb{N}
, the following identity holds:

\displaystyle x^{-1}-y^{-1}=\sum^{m}_{k=1}y^{-1}\big[\big(y-x\big)y^{-1}\big]^{k}+x^{-1}\big[\big(y-x\big)y^{-1}\big]^{m+1}.

Proof.

The proof follows by induction on
m
and the algebraic identity

x^{-1}\big[\big(y-x\big)y^{-1}\big]^{m+1}-y^{-1}\big[\big(y-x\big)y^{-1}\big]^{m+1}=x^{-1}\big[\big(y-x\big)y^{-1}\big]^{m+2}.

∎

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},E,\mathcal{B})
be an operator-valued
C^{\ast}
-probability space. Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
and consider the families
x=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\}
and
y=\{y_{1},\dots,y_{N}\}
of self-adjoint elements in
\mathcal{A}
. For any

, set

\mathbf{z}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}x_{j}+\sum_{j=i+1}^{N}y_{j}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}x_{j}+\sum_{j=i+1}^{N}y_{j}.

Then for any
\mathfrak{b}\in\mathbb{H}^{+}(\mathcal{B})
,

\displaystyle G_{\mathbf{z}_{N}}(\mathfrak{b})-G_{\mathbf{z}_{0}}(\mathfrak{b})

\displaystyle=\sum^{N}_{i=1}\big(A_{i}(\mathfrak{b})+B_{i}(\mathfrak{b})+C_{i}(\mathfrak{b})\big),

(3.1)

where for each
i=1,\dots,N
,

\displaystyle A_{i}(\mathfrak{b})

\displaystyle=G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\ x_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})-G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\ y_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b}),

\displaystyle B_{i}(\mathfrak{b})

\displaystyle=G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big(x_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)^{2}-G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big(y_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)^{2},

\displaystyle C_{i}(\mathfrak{b})

\displaystyle=G_{\mathbf{z}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big(x_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)^{3}-G_{\mathbf{z}_{i-1}}(\mathfrak{b})\big(y_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)^{3}.

Proof.

We start by writing, for any
\mathfrak{b}\in\mathbb{H}^{+}(\mathcal{B})
, the difference as a telescoping sum:

\displaystyle G_{\mathbf{z}_{N}}(\mathfrak{b})-G_{\mathbf{z}_{0}}(\mathfrak{b})

\displaystyle=\sum^{N}_{i=1}\big(G_{\mathbf{z}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})-G_{\mathbf{z}_{i-1}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)=\sum^{N}_{i=1}\Big(\!\big(G_{\mathbf{z}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})-G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)-\big(G_{\mathbf{z}_{i-1}}(\mathfrak{b})-G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)\!\Big).

Noting that
\mathbf{z}_{i}-\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}=x_{i}
and
\mathbf{z}_{i-1}-\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}=y_{i}
, then by applying the algebraic identity in Lemma
3.1
up to order
3
, we get

\displaystyle G_{\mathbf{z}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})-G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})

\displaystyle=G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\ x_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})+G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big(x_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)^{2}+G_{\mathbf{z}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big(x_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)^{3}\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle G_{\mathbf{z}_{i-1}}(\mathfrak{b})-G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})

\displaystyle=G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\ y_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})+G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big(y_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)^{2}+G_{\mathbf{z}_{i-1}}(\mathfrak{b})\big(y_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)^{3}.

Putting the above terms together and summing over

∎

, and hence we infer that

\displaystyle\|E[C_{i}(\mathfrak{b})]\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathfrak{b}^{-1}\|\|{\Im}m(\mathfrak{b})^{-1}\|^{3}\sqrt{\alpha_{2}(x)}\Big(\sqrt{\alpha_{4}(x)}+\sqrt{\alpha_{4}(y)}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\|{\Im}m(\mathfrak{b})^{-1}\|^{4}\sqrt{\alpha_{2}(x)}\Big(\sqrt{\alpha_{4}(x)}+\sqrt{\alpha_{4}(y)}\Big).

Finally, summing over
i=1,\ldots,N
we get the bound in (
1.1
).

Remark 3.3
.

Note that the last inequality we use the fact that
\|\mathfrak{b}^{-1}\|\leq\|{\Im}m(\mathfrak{b})^{-1}\|
for all
\mathfrak{b}\in\mathbb{H}^{+}(\mathcal{B})
. The proof of this inequality can be found in
[
14
]
, or it also can be easily verified as follows: we write

\mathfrak{b}=x+iy\text{ where }x=x^{*}\text{ and }y=y^{*}>0\in\mathcal{B}.

Then we observe that

\mathfrak{b}^{-1}=y^{-1/2}[w+i]^{-1}y^{-1/2}\text{ where }w=y^{-1/2}xy^{-1/2},

which implies that

\|\mathfrak{b}^{-1}\|\leq\|y^{-1/2}\|^{2}\|(w+i)^{-1}\|=\|y^{-1}\|\|(w+i)^{-1}\|.

Finally, note that
w
is self-adjoint, and hence

\|(w+i)^{-1}\|=\frac{1}{d(i,\sigma(w))}\leq 1,

where
\sigma(w)
is the spectrum of
w
and
d(i,\sigma(w))=\inf\ \{\ |i-s|\mid s\in\sigma(w)\}
.

Proof of (
1.2
).
As the conditional expectation
E
preserves
\varphi
,
\varphi=\varphi\circ E
, the first steps of the proof follow in the same way as for (
1.1
). For each
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
, we have that

\displaystyle\varphi[G_{\mathbf{x}_{N}}(z)]-\varphi[G_{\mathbf{y}_{N}}(z)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varphi\big[C_{i}(z)\big]=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varphi\big[G_{\mathbf{z}_{i}}(z)\big(x_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)\big)^{3}-G_{\mathbf{z}_{i-1}}(z)\big(y_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)\big)^{3}\big].

We start by controlling the first term of
C_{i}(z)
. By similar computations as in (
3.1
), we get

\displaystyle\big|\varphi\big[

\displaystyle G_{\mathbf{z}_{i}}(z)x_{i}G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)x_{i}G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)x_{i}G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)\big]\big|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|G_{\mathbf{z}_{i}}(z)\|^{2}\cdot\varphi\big[G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)x_{i}^{2}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)^{\ast}\big]\cdot\varphi\big[G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)^{\ast}x_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)^{\ast}x_{i}^{2}G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)x_{i}G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)\big].

First, we note that
\|G_{\mathbf{z}_{i}}(z)\|\leq\|{\Im}m(z)^{-1}\|
. As
\varphi=\varphi\circ E
, then by similar computations as in (
3.3
) and the positivity of
E
, we have

\displaystyle\varphi\big[G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)\ x_{i}^{2}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)^{\ast}\big]

\displaystyle=\varphi\big[G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)\ E[x_{i}^{2}]\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)^{\ast}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\big\|E[x_{i}^{2}]\big\|\cdot\varphi\big[G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)^{\ast}\big]\leq\alpha_{2}(x)\big\|G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)\big\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{A},\varphi)}^{2}.

Again, by similar computations as in (
3.1
) and the positivity of
E
, we get

\displaystyle\varphi\big[G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)^{\ast}x_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)^{\ast}x_{i}^{2}G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)x_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)\big]

\displaystyle=\varphi\big[G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)^{\ast}\cdot E\big[x_{i}\bar{z}^{-1}x_{i}^{2}z^{-1}x_{i}\big]\cdot G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)\big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|z|^{2}}\big\|E[x_{i}^{4}]\big\|\cdot\varphi\big[G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)^{\ast}G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)\big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{{\Im}m(z)^{2}}\widetilde{\alpha}_{4}(x)\big\|G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)\big\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{A},\varphi)}^{2},

where
\widetilde{\alpha}_{4}(x):=\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\|E[x_{i}^{4}]\|
. Putting the above terms together we get for any
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
,

\big|\varphi\big[G_{\mathbf{z}_{i}}(z)x_{i}G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)x_{i}G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)x_{i}G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)\big]\big|\leq\frac{1}{{\Im}m(z)^{2}}\sqrt{\alpha_{2}(x)\widetilde{\alpha}_{4}(x)}\big\|G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)\big\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{A},\varphi)}^{2}.

Similarly, we control the second term in
\varphi[C_{i}(z)]
and get

\big|\varphi\big[G_{\mathbf{z}_{i-1}}(z)y_{i}G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)y_{i}G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)y_{i}G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)\big]\big|\leq\frac{1}{{\Im}m(z)^{2}}\sqrt{\alpha_{2}(y)\widetilde{\alpha}_{4}(y)}\big\|G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)\big\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{A},\varphi)}^{2}.

As the second order moments match,
\alpha_{2}(x)=\alpha_{2}(y)
and hence

\big|\varphi[G_{\mathbf{x}_{N}}(z)]-\varphi[G_{\mathbf{y}_{N}}(z)]\big|\leq\frac{1}{{\Im}m(z)^{2}}\sqrt{\alpha_{2}(x)}\left(\sqrt{\widetilde{\alpha}_{4}(x)}+\sqrt{\widetilde{\alpha}_{4}(y)}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big\|G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)\big\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{A},\varphi)}^{2}.

Now taking
z=t+i\varepsilon
and using the fact that for each
x=x^{\ast}\in\mathcal{A}
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|G_{x}(t+i\varepsilon)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon},

(3.6)

we prove that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big|{\Im}m\big(\varphi[G_{\mathbf{x}_{N}}(t+i\varepsilon)]\big)-{\Im}m\big(\varphi[G_{\mathbf{y}_{N}}(t+i\varepsilon)]\big)\big|\text{d}t\leq\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon^{3}}\sqrt{\alpha_{2}(x)}\left(\sqrt{\widetilde{\alpha}_{4}(x)}+\sqrt{\widetilde{\alpha}_{4}(y)}\right)N.

∎

3.2.
Monotone Case

We prove now Theorem
1.2
relative to monotone independence. The first lines of the proof are the same as in the Boolean case and rely on the operator-valued Lindeberg method in Proposition
3.2
. However, as the order of the index set in the monotone case matters, this will require that we further expand the terms
\mathbf{z}_{i}^{0}
in the power series expansions of the relevant resolvents before factorizing with respect to monotone independence. For this aim, we would need the following lemma:

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}
and
w
be self-adjoint elements in
\mathcal{A}
that are such that
\{x_{1},x_{2}\}\prec w\prec\{y_{1},y_{2}\}
over
\mathcal{B}
. Then, for any
n,m\geq 0
,

\displaystyle E\big[(x_{1}+y_{1})^{n}w(x_{2}+y_{2})^{m})\big]=\sum^{n}_{k=0}

\displaystyle\sum^{m}_{\ell=0}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q_{0},...,q_{k}\geq 0\\
q_{0}+\dots+q_{k}=n-k\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{0},...,p_{\ell}\geq 0\\
p_{0}+\dots+p_{\ell}=m-\ell\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad E\Big[E[y_{1}^{q_{0}}]x_{1}E[y_{1}^{q_{1}}]\dots E[y_{1}^{q_{k-1}}]x_{1}E[y_{1}^{q_{k}}]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\cdot E[w]\cdot E[y_{2}^{p_{0}}]x_{2}E[y_{2}^{p_{1}}]\dots E[y_{2}^{p_{\ell-1}}]x_{2}E[y_{2}^{p_{\ell}}]\Big].

Proof.

For any
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
and
r\geq 0
, we have by the noncommutative binomial expansion

\displaystyle(x+y)^{r}

\displaystyle=\sum^{r}_{k=0}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q_{0},...,q_{k}\geq 0\\
q_{0}+\dots+q_{k}=r-k\end{subarray}}y^{q_{0}}xy^{q_{1}}\dots y^{q_{k-1}}xy^{q_{k}}\,.

Hence, we write

\displaystyle E\big[(x_{1}+y_{1})^{n}w(x_{2}+y_{2})^{m})\big]=

\displaystyle\sum^{n}_{k=0}\sum^{m}_{\ell=0}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q_{0},...,q_{k}\geq 0\\
q_{0}+\dots+q_{k}=n-k\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{0},...,p_{\ell}\geq 0\\
p_{0}+\dots+p_{\ell}=m-\ell\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\quad E\Big[y_{1}^{q_{0}}x_{1}y_{1}^{q_{1}}\dots y_{1}^{q_{k-1}}x_{1}y_{1}^{q_{k}}\cdot w\cdot y_{2}^{p_{0}}x_{2}y_{2}^{p_{1}}\dots y_{2}^{p_{\ell-1}}x_{2}y_{2}^{p_{\ell}}\Big].

Now as
\{x_{1},x_{2}\}\prec w\prec\{y_{1},y_{2}\}
over
\mathcal{B}
, from the definition of monotone independence with amalgamation over
\mathcal{B}
, we get

\displaystyle E\Big[

\displaystyle y_{1}^{q_{0}}x_{1}y_{1}^{q_{1}}\dots y_{1}^{q_{k-1}}x_{1}y_{1}^{q_{k}}\cdot w\cdot y_{2}^{p_{0}}x_{2}y_{2}^{p_{1}}\dots y_{2}^{p_{\ell-1}}x_{2}y_{2}^{p_{\ell}}\Big]

\displaystyle=E\Big[E[y_{1}^{q_{0}}]x_{1}E[y_{1}^{q_{1}}]\dots E[y_{1}^{q_{k-1}}]x_{1}E[y_{1}^{q_{k}}]\cdot w\cdot E[y_{2}^{p_{0}}]x_{2}E[y_{2}^{p_{1}}]\dots E[y_{2}^{p_{\ell-1}}]x_{2}E[y_{2}^{p_{\ell}}]\Big]

\displaystyle=E\Big[E[y_{1}^{q_{0}}]x_{1}E[y_{1}^{q_{1}}]\dots E[y_{1}^{q_{k-1}}]x_{1}E[y_{1}^{q_{k}}]\cdot E[w]\cdot E[y_{2}^{p_{0}}]x_{2}E[y_{2}^{p_{1}}]\dots E[y_{2}^{p_{\ell-1}}]x_{2}E[y_{2}^{p_{\ell}}]\Big],

which ends the proof.
∎

Having the above factorization lemma in hand, we prove the following bounds that will be used at several occasions in the proof later on.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
x,y
and
w
be self-adjoint elements in
\mathcal{A}
such that
x\prec w\prec y
over
\mathcal{B}
, then for any
b_{1},b_{2}\in\mathbb{H}^{\pm}(\mathcal{B})
,

(i)

E\big[G_{x+y}(b_{1})\ w\ G_{x+y}(b_{2})\big]=E\big[G_{x}\big(E[G_{y}(b_{1})]^{-1}\big)\cdot E[w]\cdot G_{x}\big(E[G_{y}(b_{2})]^{-1}\big)\big].

Moreover, if
W\geq 0
, then for any
b_{1},b_{2}\in\mathbb{H}^{\pm}(\mathcal{B})
,

(ii)

\big\|E[G_{x+y}(b_{1})\ w\ G_{x+y}(b_{2})]\big\|\leq\big\|E[w]\big\|\cdot\big\|{\Im}m(E[G_{y}(b_{1})]^{-1})^{-1}\big\|\cdot\big\|{\Im}m(E[G_{y}(b_{2})]^{-1})^{-1}\big\|
,

and for any
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
,

(iii)

\varphi[G_{x+y}(z)\ w\ G_{x+y}(z)^{\ast}]\leq\big\|E[w]\big\|\cdot\|G_{x}\big(E[G_{y}(z)]^{-1}\big)\|_{L_{2}(\mathcal{A},\varphi)}^{2}.

Proof.

(i)

Writing the resolvent as a power series as in (
3.2
), we have for
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
that are such that
\max\{\|b_{1}^{-1}\|,\|b_{2}^{-1}\|\}<1/\|x+y\|
,

E\big[G_{x+y}(b_{1})\ w\ G_{x+y}(b_{2})\big]=\sum_{n,m\geq 0}b_{1}^{-1}E\big[(xb_{1}^{-1}+yb_{1}^{-1})^{n}wb_{2}^{-1}(xb_{2}^{-1}+yb_{2}^{-1})^{m}\big].

Now, we get by Lemma
3.4
after summing over
n,m\geq 0
,

\displaystyle E\big[G_{x+y}(b_{1})\ w\ G_{x+y}(b_{2})\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,\ell\geq 0}\sum_{q_{0},...,q_{k}\geq 0}\sum_{p_{0},...,p_{\ell}\geq 0}\mathbb{E}\Big[b_{1}^{-1}E\big[(yb_{1}^{-1})^{q_{0}}\big]xb_{1}^{-1}E\big[(yb_{1}^{-1})^{q_{1}}\big]\cdots xb_{1}^{-1}E\big[(yb_{1}^{-1})^{q_{k}}\big]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\cdot E[w]\cdot b_{2}^{-1}E\big[(yb_{2}^{-1})^{p_{0}}\big]xb_{2}^{-1}E\big[(yb_{2}^{-1})^{p_{1}}\big]\cdots xb_{2}^{-1}E\big[(yb_{2}^{-1})^{p_{\ell}}\big]\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,\ell\geq 0}E\Bigg[E\Big[\sum_{q_{0}\geq 0}b_{1}^{-1}(yb_{1}^{-1})^{q_{0}}\Big]\cdot x\cdot E\Big[\sum_{q_{1}\geq 0}b_{1}^{-1}(yb_{1}^{-1})^{q_{1}}\Big]\cdots x\cdot E\Big[\sum_{q_{k}\geq 0}b_{1}^{-1}(yb_{1}^{-1})^{q_{k}}\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\cdot E[w]\cdot E\Big[\sum_{p_{0}\geq 0}b_{2}^{-1}(yb_{2}^{-1})^{p_{0}}\Big]\cdot x\cdot E\Big[\sum_{p_{1}\geq 0}b_{2}^{-1}(yb_{2}^{-1})^{p_{1}}\Big]\cdots x\cdot E\Big[\sum_{p_{\ell}\geq 0}b_{2}^{-1}(yb_{2}^{-1})^{p_{\ell}}\Big]\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,\ell\geq 0}E\Big[E\big[G_{y}(b_{1})\big]\big(x\cdot E\big[G_{y}(b_{1})\big]\big)^{k}\cdot E[w]\cdot E\big[G_{y}(b_{2})\big]\big(x\cdot E\big[G_{y}(b_{2})\big]\big)^{\ell}\Big].

For
i\in\{1,2\}
, we set
\widetilde{b}_{i}=E[G_{y}(b_{i})]^{-1}
and write

\displaystyle E\big[G_{x+y}(b_{1})\ w\ G_{x+y}(b_{2})\big]

\displaystyle=E\Big[\sum_{k\geq 0}\widetilde{b}_{1}^{-1}(x\widetilde{b}_{1}^{-1})^{k}\cdot E[w]\cdot\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\widetilde{b}_{2}^{-1}(x\widetilde{b}_{2}^{-1})^{\ell}\Big]

\displaystyle=E\Big[G_{x}\big(E[G_{y}(b_{1})]^{-1}\big)\cdot E[w]\cdot G_{x}\big(E[G_{y}(b_{2})]^{-1}\big)\Big],

which proves the desired equality for
b_{1},b_{2}\in\mathbb{H}^{\pm}(\mathcal{B})
such that
\max(\|b_{1}^{-1}\|,\|b_{2}^{-1}\|)<1/||x+y||
. The equality can be extended to
\mathbb{H}^{\pm}(\mathcal{B})
since both of its sides are analytical.

(ii)
The second part of the lemma follows directly from the first:

\displaystyle\big\|E[G_{x+y}(b)\ w\ G_{x+y}(b)^{\ast}]\big\|

\displaystyle=\big\|E[G_{x+y}(b)\ w\ G_{x+y}(b^{\ast})]\big\|

\displaystyle=\big\|E\big[G_{x}\big(E[G_{y}(b)]^{-1}\big)\cdot E[w]\cdot G_{x}\big(E[G_{y}(b^{\ast})]^{-1}\big)\big]\big\|

\displaystyle\leq\big\|G_{x}\big(E[G_{y}(b)]^{-1}\big)\big\|\cdot\big\|G_{x}\big(E[G_{y}(b^{\ast})]^{-1}\big)\big]\big\|\cdot\big\|E[w]\big\|

\displaystyle\leq\big\|{\Im}m\big(E[G_{y}(b)]^{-1}\big)^{-1}\big\|\cdot\big\|{\Im}m\big(E[G_{y}(b^{\ast})]^{-1}\big)^{-1}\big\|\cdot\big\|E[w]\big\|

\displaystyle\leq\big\|{\Im}m(b)^{-1}\big\|^{2}\cdot\big\|E[w]\big\|\,,

where the last inequality follows from the fact that
{\Im}m\big(E[G_{y}(b)]^{-1}\big)\geq{\Im}m(b)
for any
b\in\mathbb{H}^{+}(\mathcal{B})
, see Remark 2.5 in
[
7
]
.

(iii)
The last estimate also follows from
(i)
: for any
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
,

\displaystyle\varphi[G_{x+y}(z)\ w\ G_{x+y}(z)^{\ast}]

\displaystyle=\varphi\big[G_{x}\big(E[G_{y}(z)]^{-1}\big)\cdot E[w]\cdot G_{x}\big(E[G_{y}(z)]^{-1}\big)^{\ast}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\big\|E[w]\big\|\cdot\varphi\big[G_{x}\big(E[G_{y}(z)]^{-1}\big)G_{x}\big(E[G_{y}(z)]^{-1}\big)^{\ast}\big]

\displaystyle=\big\|E[w]\big\|\cdot\|G_{x}\big(E[G_{y}(z)]^{-1}\big)\|_{L_{2}(\mathcal{A},\varphi)}^{2},

where the inequality follows from the positivity of
\varphi
.
∎

Having the above lemmas in hand, we are now ready to prove Theorem
1.2
relative to the monotone case.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
\mathcal{A}_{x_{1},y_{1}}\prec\mathcal{A}_{x_{2},y_{2}}\prec\dots\prec\mathcal{A}_{x_{N},y_{N}}
over
\mathcal{B}
. Indeed, one can simply take copies of the families
\{x_{1},\dots x_{N}\}
and
\{y_{1},\dots y_{N}\}
where this holds, for instance, we could choose
\mathcal{A}_{x_{1}}\prec\mathcal{A}_{x_{2}}\prec\cdots\prec\mathcal{A}_{x_{N}}\prec\mathcal{A}_{y_{1}}\prec\mathcal{A}_{y_{2}}\cdots\prec\mathcal{A}_{y_{N}}
. The starting point is the operator-valued Lindeberg method in Proposition
3.2
where we note that
\mathbf{x}_{N}=\mathbf{z}_{N}
and
\mathbf{y}_{N}=\mathbf{z}_{0}
.

Proof of (
1.3
)
. Again, we will prove that
E\big[A_{i}(\mathfrak{b})\big]=0
,
E\big[B_{i}(\mathfrak{b})\big]=0
and that

\displaystyle\|E[C_{i}(\mathfrak{b})]\|\leq\|{\Im}m(\mathfrak{b})^{-1}\|^{4}\sqrt{\alpha_{2}(x)}\left(\sqrt{\alpha_{4}(x)}+\sqrt{\alpha_{4}(y)}\right)\ .

As above, all the identities here will be proven for
\mathfrak{b}\in\mathbb{H}^{+}(\mathcal{B})
such that
\|\mathfrak{b}^{-1}\|
is small enough and then get extended analytically to
\mathbb{H}^{+}(\mathcal{B})
. Considering the telescoping sum in (
3.1
), we fix
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
and start by controlling the first order term

E\big[A_{i}(\mathfrak{b})\big]=E\big[G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\ x_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big]-E\big[G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\ y_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big].

We set
\mathbf{u}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}x_{j}
and
\mathbf{v}_{i}=~\sum_{j=i+1}^{N}y_{j}
, and note that
\mathbf{u}_{i}\prec x_{i}\prec\mathbf{v}_{i}
over
\mathcal{B}
. Then taking into account that
E[x_{i}]=0
, we get by Lemma
3.5
,

\displaystyle E\big[G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\ x_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big]

\displaystyle=E\big[G_{\mathbf{u}_{i}+\mathbf{v}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\ x_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{u}_{i}+\mathbf{v}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big]

\displaystyle=E\big[G_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}\big(E[G_{\mathbf{v}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})]^{-1}\big)\cdot E[x_{i}]\cdot G_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}\big(E[G_{\mathbf{v}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})]^{-1}\big)\big]=0.

Similarly, we prove that
E\big[G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\ y_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big]=0
and infer that
E[A_{i}(\mathfrak{b})]=0
. For the second order term in (
3.1
):

E\big[B_{i}(\mathfrak{b})\big]=E\big[G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big(x_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)^{2}\big]-E\big[G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big(y_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)^{2}\big],

we develop the first term using (
3.2
):

\displaystyle E\Big[G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big(x_{i}G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)^{2}\Big]\!

\displaystyle=E\Big[G_{\mathbf{u}_{i}+\mathbf{v}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})x_{i}G_{\mathbf{u}_{i}+\mathbf{v}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})x_{i}G_{\mathbf{u}_{i}+\mathbf{v}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\Big]

\displaystyle=\!\!\sum_{n,m,r\geq 0}\!\!E\Big[\mathfrak{b}^{-1}\big((\mathbf{u}_{i}+\mathbf{v}_{i})\mathfrak{b}^{-1}\big)^{n}x_{i}\mathfrak{b}^{-1}\big((\mathbf{u}_{i}+\mathbf{v}_{i})\mathfrak{b}^{-1}\big)^{m}x_{i}\mathfrak{b}^{-1}\big((\mathbf{u}_{i}+\mathbf{v}_{i})\mathfrak{b}^{-1}\big)^{r}\Big]

\displaystyle=\!\!\sum_{n,m,r\geq 0}\!\!\mathfrak{b}^{-1}E\Big[\big(\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}+\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i}\big)^{n}\widetilde{x}_{i}\big(\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}+\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i}\big)^{m}\widetilde{x}_{i}\big(\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}+\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i}\big)^{r}\Big],

where
\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}=\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathfrak{b}^{-1}
,
\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i}=\mathbf{v}_{i}\mathfrak{b}^{-1}
and
\widetilde{x}_{i}=x_{i}\mathfrak{b}^{-1}
. Now using the noncommutative binomial expansion, we write

E\Big[\big(\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}+\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i}\big)^{n}\widetilde{x}_{i}\big(\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}+\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i}\big)^{m}\widetilde{x}_{i}\big(\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}+\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i}\big)^{r}\Big]\\
=\sum^{n}_{k=0}\sum^{m}_{\ell=0}\sum^{r}_{s=0}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q_{0},...,q_{k}\geq 0\\
q_{0}+\dots+q_{k}=n-k\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{0},...,p_{\ell}\geq 0\\
p_{0}+\dots+p_{\ell}=m-\ell\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{0},...,t_{s}\geq 0\\
t_{0}+\dots+t_{s}=r-s\end{subarray}}\\
\qquad\qquad E\Big[\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i}^{q_{0}}\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i}^{q_{1}}\dots\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i}^{q_{k}}\cdot\widetilde{x}_{i}\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i}^{p_{0}}\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i}^{p_{1}}\dots\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i}^{p_{\ell}}\cdot\widetilde{x}_{i}\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i}^{t_{0}}\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i}^{t_{1}}\dots\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i}^{t_{s}}\Big].

Noting that
\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}\prec\widetilde{x}_{i}\prec\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i}
over
\mathcal{B}
and taking into account that
E[\widetilde{x}_{i}]=E[x_{i}]\mathfrak{b}^{-1}=0
, we get whenever
m\neq 0

\displaystyle E\Big[\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i}^{q_{0}}\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i}^{q_{1}}

\widetilde{x}=\{\widetilde{x}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{x}_{N}\}
and
\widetilde{y}=\{\widetilde{y}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{y}_{N}\}
where

\widetilde{x_{j}}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{j}&0\\
0&x_{j}\end{bmatrix}\text{ and }\widetilde{y_{j}}=\begin{bmatrix}y_{j}&0\\
0&y_{j}\end{bmatrix}

for each
j=1,\dots,N
. Finally, for each
i=1,\dots,N
, let

\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}_{i}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{i}\widetilde{x}_{j}+\sum\limits_{j=i+1}^{N}\widetilde{y}_{j}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}^{0}_{i}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{i-1}\widetilde{x}_{j}+\sum\limits_{j=i+1}^{N}\widetilde{y}_{j};

and note that

\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}_{N}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}_{N}&0\\
0&\mathbf{x}_{N}\end{bmatrix}:=\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}_{N}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{y}_{N}&0\\
0&\mathbf{y}_{N}\end{bmatrix}:=\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{N}.

Also note that if
x
and
y
are infinitesimally free, then by Proposition
2.12
, it follows that
\mathcal{A}_{\widetilde{x}_{1}},\mathcal{A}_{\widetilde{x}_{2}},

\dots,\mathcal{A}_{\widetilde{x}_{N}},\mathcal{A}_{\widetilde{y}_{1}},\mathcal{A}_{\widetilde{y}_{2}},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{\widetilde{y}_{N}}
are free over
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}
. In addition, we observe that under Assumption
2
, for each
j=1,\dots,N
,

\widetilde{E}[\widetilde{x}_{j}]=\widetilde{E}\begin{bmatrix}x_{j}&0\\
0&x_{j}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}E[x_{j}]&E^{\prime}[x_{j}]\\
0&E[x_{j}]\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}.

Moreover, for any
B=\begin{bmatrix}b&b^{\prime}\\
0&b\end{bmatrix}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}
and for each
j=1,\dots,N
, we have

\widetilde{E}[\widetilde{x}_{j}B\widetilde{x}_{j}]=\begin{bmatrix}E[x_{j}bx_{j}]&E^{\prime}[x_{j}bx_{j}]+E[x_{j}b^{\prime}x_{j}]\\
0&E[x_{j}bx_{j}]\end{bmatrix}\!=\!\begin{bmatrix}E[y_{j}by_{j}]&E^{\prime}[y_{j}by_{j}]+E[y_{j}b^{\prime}y_{j}]\\
0&E[y_{j}by_{j}]\end{bmatrix}=\widetilde{E}[\widetilde{y}_{j}B\widetilde{y}_{j}].

Therefore, we conclude that
\widetilde{x}
and
\widetilde{y}
satisfy the assumption in
[
6
, Theorem 3.1]
, which implies that for all
B=\begin{bmatrix}\mathfrak{b}&b^{\prime}\\
0&\mathfrak{b}\end{bmatrix}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}
with
\mathfrak{b}\in\mathbb{H}^{+}(\mathcal{B})
and
b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}
,

\widetilde{E}\big[G_{\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}_{N}}(B)\big]-\widetilde{E}\big[G_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{N}}(B)\big]=\sum^{N}_{i=1}\Big(\widetilde{E}\big[G_{\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}_{i}}(B)\big(\widetilde{x_{i}}\ G_{\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}^{0}_{i}}(B)\big)^{3}\big]-\widetilde{E}\big[G_{\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}_{i-1}}(B)\big(\widetilde{y_{i}}\ G_{\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}^{0}_{i}}(B)\big)^{3}\big]\Big).

(3.12)

Here we note that all the resolvents above are well-defined; indeed,

G_{\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}_{N}}(B)=\begin{bmatrix}(\mathfrak{b}-\mathbf{x}_{N})^{-1}&-(\mathfrak{b}-\mathbf{x}_{N})^{-1}b^{\prime}(\mathfrak{b}-\mathbf{x}_{N})^{-1}\\
0&(\mathfrak{b}-\mathbf{x}_{N})\end{bmatrix}.

In particular, for a given
\mathfrak{b}\in\mathbb{H}^{+}(\mathcal{B})
, if we let
B=\begin{bmatrix}\mathfrak{b}&0\\
0&\mathfrak{b}\end{bmatrix}
, then
the left hand side of (
3.12
) is

\begin{bmatrix}E[G_{\mathbf{x}_{N}}(\mathfrak{b})]-E[G_{\mathbf{y}_{N}}(\mathfrak{b})]&E^{\prime}[G_{\mathbf{x}_{N}}(\mathfrak{b})]-E^{\prime}[G_{\mathbf{y}_{N}}(\mathfrak{b})]\\
0&E[G_{\mathbf{x}_{N}}(\mathfrak{b})]-E[G_{\mathbf{y}_{N}}(\mathfrak{b})]\end{bmatrix}.

On the other hand, we observe that

\widetilde{E}\big[G_{\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}_{i}}(B)(\widetilde{x_{i}}\ G_{\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}^{0}_{i}}(B))^{3}\big]=\begin{bmatrix}E\big[G_{z_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big(x_{i}G_{z_{i}^{0}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)^{3}\big]&E^{\prime}\big[G_{z_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big(x_{i}G_{z_{i}^{0}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)^{3}\big]\\
0&E\big[G_{z_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big(x_{i}G_{z_{i}^{0}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)^{3}\big]\end{bmatrix},

and

\widetilde{E}\big[G_{\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}_{i-1}}(B)\big(\widetilde{y_{i}}\ G_{\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}^{0}_{i}}(B)\big)^{3}\big]=\begin{bmatrix}E\big[G_{{z}_{i-1}}(\mathfrak{b})\big({y_{i}}\ G_{{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)^{3}\big]&E^{\prime}\big[G_{{z}_{i-1}}(\mathfrak{b})\big({y_{i}}\ G_{{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)^{3}\big]\\
0&E\big[G_{{z}_{i-1}}(\mathfrak{b})\big({y_{i}}\ G_{{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)^{3}\big]\end{bmatrix}

for each
i=1,\dots,N
. Therefore, the
(1,2)
-entry of the right hand side of (
3.12
) is nothing but

\sum^{N}_{i=1}\Big(E^{\prime}\big[G_{\mathbf{z}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big(x_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)^{3}\big]-E^{\prime}\big[G_{\mathbf{z}_{i-1}}(\mathfrak{b})\big(y_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)^{3}\big]\Big).

By comparing both sides of (
3.12
), we conclude that (
3.11
) holds.

Similarly, if
x
and
y
are infinitesimally Boolean (respectively monotone) independent that satisfy Assumption
2
, then
\widetilde{x}
and
\widetilde{y}
are Boolean (respectively monotone) independent that satisfy Assumption
1
with respect to
\widetilde{E}
. Therefore, combining the estimates in Section 3.1 and Section 3.2, (
3.11
) also holds whenever
x
and
y
are either infinitesimally Boolean or monotone independent.
∎

Remark 3.7
.

Note that the fact that
E^{\prime}
is completely bounded and self-adjoint implies that
E^{\prime}=E_{1}-E_{2}
for some completely positive maps
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
. Therefore, for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
, we have

\|E^{\prime}[a]\|=\|E_{1}[a]-E_{2}[a]\|\leq\|E_{1}[a]\|+\|E_{2}[a]\|\leq 2\|a\|.

Suppose
(\widetilde{A},\widetilde{E},\widetilde{B})
is an upper triangular probability space that is induced by
(\mathcal{A},E,E^{\prime},\mathcal{B})
, then for a given
A=\begin{bmatrix}a&a^{\prime}\\
0&a\end{bmatrix}\in\widetilde{A}
,

\big\|\widetilde{E}\left[A\right]\big\|=\|E[a]\|+\|E^{\prime}[a]+E[a^{\prime}]\|\leq 3(\|a\|+\|a^{\prime}\|)=3\|A\|.

Proof of (
1.5
)

Following Proposition
3.6
and Remark
3.7
,

\displaystyle\|E^{\prime}\big[G_{\mathbf{x}_{N}}(\mathfrak{b})\big]-E^{\prime}\big[G_{\mathbf{y}_{N}}(\mathfrak{b})\big]\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum^{N}_{i=1}\Big(\|E^{\prime}\big[G_{\mathbf{z}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big(x_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)^{3}\big]\|+\|E^{\prime}\big[G_{\mathbf{z}_{i-1}}(\mathfrak{b})\big(y_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)^{3}\big]\|\Big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\sum^{N}_{i=1}\left(\|G_{\mathbf{z}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big(x_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)^{3}\|+\|G_{\mathbf{z}_{i-1}}(\mathfrak{b})\big(y_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)^{3}\|\right).

Note that for each
1\leq i\leq N
,

\|G_{\mathbf{z}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big(x_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)^{3}\|\leq\|G_{\mathbf{z}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\|\cdot\|x_{i}\|^{3}\cdot\|G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\|^{3}\leq\|{\Im}m(\mathfrak{b})^{-1}\|^{4}\|x_{i}\|^{3}.

Similarly, we prove that

\|G_{\mathbf{z}_{i-1}}(\mathfrak{b})\big(y_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)^{3}\|\leq\|{\Im}m(\mathfrak{b})^{-1}\|^{4}\|y_{i}\|^{3}.

Thus, we obtain the desired result.
∎

4.
Operator-valued Central Limit Theorems

The aim of this section is to provide an application of our main results in Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
to the operator-valued central theorems for Boolean and monotone independence respectively. We will show how with our quantitative bounds in terms of the fourth and second operator-valued moments, we can furthermore obtain results on the fourth moment theorem for infinitely divisible measures in Section
4.3
. Finally, we provide in Section
4.4
the first quantitative estimates in the infinitesimal setting.

Consider an operator-valued
C^{\ast}
-probability space
(\mathcal{A},E,\mathcal{B})
. All along this section, we let
x:=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
be a family of self-adjoint elements in
\mathcal{A}
that are centered with respect to
E
and set

X_{n}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}.

4.1.
Boolean CLT

The Boolean Central Limit Theorem was first proved in the scalar-valued setting in the original paper by Speicher and Woroudi
[
35
]
. Quantitative versions were provided in
[
3
]
and
[
32
]
in terms of Lévy distance. In the operator-valued setting, the Boolean CLT was then proved in
[
8
]
, where its quantitative extension may be found in
[
18
]
or in the notes
[
17
]
.

We improve on the above-mentioned quantitative results by providing quantitative estimates in terms of the moments instead of the operator norm that can be pushed to obtaining quantitative bounds on the Lévy distance. We start by letting
B_{n}
be a centered
\mathcal{B}
-valued Bernoulli element whose variance is given by the completely positive map

\eta_{n}:\mathcal{B}\rightarrow\mathcal{B},\qquad b\mapsto\eta_{n}(b)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}E[x_{j}bx_{j}].

We provide in the following theorem quantitative results on the level of the operator-valued Cauchy transforms as well as the Lévy distance.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
x:=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
be a family of self-adjoint elements in
\mathcal{A}
that are Boolean independent with amalgamation over
\mathcal{B}
and that are such that
E[x_{j}]=0
. Then, for any
\mathfrak{b}\in\mathbb{H}^{+}(\mathcal{B})
,

\displaystyle\big\|E[G_{X_{n}}(\mathfrak{b})]-E[G_{B_{n}}(\mathfrak{b})]\big\|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\|{\Im}m(\mathfrak{b})^{-1}\|^{4}\sqrt{\alpha_{2}(x)}\left(\sqrt{\alpha_{4}(x)}+\sqrt{\alpha_{2}(x)^{2}}\right).

Furthermore, there is a universal positive constant
c
such that

L(\mu_{X_{n}},\mu_{B_{n}})\leq c\big(\alpha_{2}(x)(\widetilde{\alpha}_{4}(x)+\alpha_{2}(x)^{2})\big)^{1/14}n^{-1/14}.

Proof.

The proof follows by a direct application of Theorem
1.1
where we choose
y=\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\}
to be a family of
\mathcal{B}
-valued Bernoulli elements that are Boolean independent over
\mathcal{B}
and are such that
E[y_{j}]=0
and
E[y_{j}by_{j}]=E[x_{j}bx_{j}]
for any
j\in[n]
and
b\in\mathcal{B}
. Moreover, we note that in this case, we have that

E[y_{j}b^{*}y_{j}^{2}by_{j}]=E[y_{j}b^{*}y_{j}]E[y_{j}by_{j}].

As
E
is a completely positive map, we note that
\sup_{b\in\mathcal{B},\|b\|=1}\|E[y_{j}b^{*}y_{j}]\|=\|E[y_{j}^{2}]\|
and hence we get that
\alpha_{4}(y)=\alpha_{2}(x)^{2}
. To end the proof of the first part of the theorem, it remains to notice that
\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}y_{j}
is a centered
\mathcal{B}
-valued Bernoulli element with variance
\eta_{n}
, (See
[
17
, Lemma 6.2.5]
).

To prove the bound on the Lévy distance, we apply similarly (
1.2
) which yields for any
\varepsilon>0

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big|{\Im}m\big(\varphi[G_{X_{n}}(t+i\varepsilon)]\big)-{\Im}m\big(\varphi[G_{B_{n}}(t+i\varepsilon)]\big)\big|\text{d}t\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon^{3}}\sqrt{\alpha_{2}(x)}\left(\sqrt{\widetilde{\alpha}_{4}(x)}+\sqrt{\alpha_{2}(x)^{2}}\right).

Then using the bound in (
2.4
), we get for any
\varepsilon>0

L(\mu_{X_{n}},\mu_{B_{n}})\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{\pi}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{3}}\sqrt{\alpha_{2}(x)}\left(\sqrt{\widetilde{\alpha}_{4}(x)}+\sqrt{\alpha_{2}(x)^{2}}\right).

Finally, optimizing over
\varepsilon>0
, we get the desired bounds on the Lévy distance.
∎

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},E,\mathcal{B})
be an operator-valued
C^{\ast}
-probability space. Consider two operator-valued Bernoulli elements
B_{0},B_{1}
with respective covariance maps
\eta_{0},\eta_{1}:\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{B}
. Then, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
and each
\mathfrak{b}\in\mathbb{H}^{+}(M_{k}(\mathcal{B}))
, we have

\|\mathcal{G}^{M_{k}(\mathcal{B})}_{\mathbf{1}_{k}\otimes B_{1}}(\mathfrak{b})-\mathcal{G}^{M_{k}(\mathcal{B})}_{\mathbf{1}_{k}\otimes B_{0}}(\mathfrak{b})\|\leq k\|{\Im}m(\mathfrak{b})^{-1}\|^{3}\|\eta_{1}-\eta_{0}\|.

(4.1)

Moreover, if
(\mathcal{A},\varphi)
is a
W^{*}
-probability space with
\varphi=\varphi\circ E
, then the scalar-valued Cauchy transforms of
B_{1}
and
B_{0}
satisfy

\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\mathcal{G}_{B_{1}}(t+i\varepsilon)-\mathcal{G}_{B_{0}}(t+i\varepsilon)|\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\|\eta_{1}-\eta_{0}\|

(4.2)

for each
\varepsilon>0
and, with the universal positive constant

c=5(\frac{1}{4\pi})^{2/5}<1.817
, we have that

L(\mu_{B_{1}},\mu_{B_{0}})\leq c\|\eta_{1}-\eta_{0}\|^{1/5}.

(4.3)

While the proof is similar to that of the free case in
[
6
, Theorem 3.5]
, we write it again for the convenience of the reader as its arguments will be used repeatedly in the coming sections.

Proof.

Fix
m\in\mathbb{N}
and let
x=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\}
and
y=\{y_{1},\dots,y_{m}\}
be two Boolean independent families consisting of Boolean independent copies of the given Bernoulli elements
\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}B_{0}
and
\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}B_{1}
respectively. Adopting the notation in Proposition
3.2
, we note that
\mathbf{z}_{m}
and
\mathbf{z}_{0}
have the same distributions as
B_{0}
and
B_{1}
respectively. Hence following (
3.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle E[G_{B_{0}}(\mathfrak{b})]-E[G_{B_{1}}(\mathfrak{b})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\left(E\big[G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big(x_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)^{2}\big]-E\big[G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big(y_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)^{2}\big]\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m\sqrt{m}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\left(E\big[G_{\mathbf{z}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big(x_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)^{3}\big]-E\big[G_{\mathbf{z}_{i-1}}(\mathfrak{b})\big(y_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)^{3}\big]\right).

Then for each
i\in[m]
, we follow the steps of (
3.1
) to obtain

\displaystyle E\big[G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})x_{i}G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})x_{i}G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big]=E[G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})]E[x_{i}b^{-1}x_{i}]E[G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})]

Hence,

\displaystyle\big\|E\big[G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big(x_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)^{2}\big]-E\big[G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big(y_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)^{2}\big]\big\|

\displaystyle\leq\big\|E[G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})]\big\|\big\|(\eta_{0}-\eta_{1})(b^{-1})\big\|E[G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})]\big\|

\displaystyle\leq\|b^{-1}\|\|{\Im}m(b)^{-1}\|^{2}\|\eta_{0}-\eta_{1}\|.

As for the third order term, we get for each
i\in[m]
,

\big\|E\big[G_{\mathbf{z}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big(x_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)^{3}\big]\big\|+\big\|E\big[G_{\mathbf{z}_{i-1}}(\mathfrak{b})\big(y_{i}\ G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(\mathfrak{b})\big)^{3}\big]\big\|\leq 2\|{\Im}m(\mathfrak{b})\|^{4}\left(\|x_{i}\|^{3}+\|y_{i}\|^{3}\right).

Therefore, we obtain

\|E[G_{B_{0}}(\mathfrak{b})]-E[G_{B_{1}}(\mathfrak{b})]\|\\
\leq\|\mathfrak{b}^{-1}\|\|{\Im}m(\mathfrak{b})^{-1}\|^{2}\|\eta_{0}-\eta_{1}\|+\frac{2}{\sqrt{m}}\|{\Im}m(\mathfrak{b})^{-1}\|^{4}(\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\|x_{i}\|^{3}+\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\|y_{i}\|^{3}).

(4.4)

Finally, noting that (
4.4
) holds for any
m
, we let
m\to\infty
to obtain the desired bounded for
k=1
. The proof of the assertion for general
k\in\mathbb{N}
can be easily proved by noting that Boolean independence is preserved under matrix amplification and then applying (
4.4
) to
1_{k}\otimes B_{1}
and
1_{k}\otimes B_{0}
in the operator-valued
C^{*}
-probability space
(M_{k}(\mathcal{A}),id_{k}\otimes E,M_{k}(\mathcal{B}))
. Hence, we directly obtain for all
\mathfrak{b}\in\mathbb{H}^{+}(M_{k}(\mathcal{B}))
,

\|\mathcal{G}^{M_{k}(\mathcal{B})}_{\mathbf{1}_{k}\otimes B_{0}}(\mathfrak{b})-\mathcal{G}^{M_{k}(\mathcal{B})}_{\mathbf{1}_{k}\otimes B_{1}}(\mathfrak{b})\|\leq\|{\Im}m(\mathfrak{b})^{-1}\|^{3}\|\operatorname{id}_{k}\otimes\eta_{0}-\operatorname{id}_{k}\otimes\eta_{1}\|.

Using the fact that
\|\operatorname{id}_{k}\otimes\eta_{0}-\operatorname{id}_{k}\otimes\eta_{1}\|\leq k\|\eta_{0}-\eta_{1}\|
, which follows from
[
26
, Exercise 3.10]
, we arrive at the desired bound for general
k
.
Finally, to prove (
4.2
) and (
4.3
), we note that for each
i\in[m]
and
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
,

\displaystyle\big|\varphi\big[G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)\big](\eta_{0}-\eta_{1})(z)\varphi\big[G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)\big]\big|\leq\frac{1}{|z|}\|\eta_{0}-\eta_{1}\|\big|\varphi\big[G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)\big]\big|^{2}\leq\frac{\|\eta_{0}-\eta_{1}\|}{{\Im}m(z)}\big\|G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}}(z)\big\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Then, the remaining of the proof follows the analogous argument of proof in
[
6
, Theorem 3.5]
.
∎

4.2.
Monotone CLT

This section is devoted to studying the operator-valued monotone CLT. The scalar-valued case was first proved by
[
23
]
before getting extended to the operator-valued setting, see
[
31
]
and
[
16
]
. Moreover, quantitative versions can be also found in
[
4
]
in the scalar case and in
[
18
]
and
[
17
]
in the operator-valued case.

It is similar to Boolean case in the previous subsection that our main improvement is providing quantitative estimates in terms of moments instead of the operator norm, and it allows us to get the quantitative bounds on Lévy distance.

For any
1\leq j\leq n
, we denote by
\nu_{\eta_{j}}
the
\mathcal{B}
-valued arcsine distribution with variance given by the completely positive map

\eta_{j}:\mathcal{B}\rightarrow\mathcal{B},\qquad b\mapsto\eta_{j}(b)=E[x_{j}bx_{j}].

We denote by
A_{n}
the
\mathcal{B}
-valued generalized arcsine element of
\mathcal{A}
whose distribution is given by
\mathrm{dil}_{n^{-1/2}}(\nu_{\eta_{1}}\rhd\dots\rhd\nu_{\eta_{n}})
. For more details on generalized arcsine distributions, see
[
17
, Chapter 7]
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
x:=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
be a family of self-adjoint elements in
\mathcal{A}
that are monotone independent with amalgamation over
\mathcal{B}
, i.e.
\mathcal{A}_{x_{1}}\prec\mathcal{A}_{x_{2}}\prec\dots\prec\mathcal{A}_{x_{N}}
over
\mathcal{B}
, and that are such that
E[x_{j}]=0
. Then, for any
\mathfrak{b}\in\mathbb{H}^{+}(\mathcal{B})
,

\displaystyle\big\|E[G_{X_{n}}(\mathfrak{b})]-E[G_{A_{n}}(\mathfrak{b})]\big\|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\|{\Im}m(\mathfrak{b})^{-1}\|^{4}\sqrt{\alpha_{2}(x)}\left(\sqrt{\alpha_{4}(x)}+\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}\alpha_{2}(x)^{2}}\right).

Furthermore, in the scalar case where
\mathcal{B}=\mathbb{C}
, there is a universal positive constant
c
such that

L(\mu_{X_{n}},\mu_{A_{n}})\leq c\Big(\alpha_{2}(x)\big(\widetilde{\alpha}_{4}(x)+\frac{3}{2}\alpha_{2}(x)^{2}\big)\Big)^{1/14}n^{-1/14}.

Proof.

The proof follows by a direct application of Theorem
1.2
where we choose
y=\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\}
to be a family of
\mathcal{B}
-valued arcsine elements that are such that
\mathcal{A}_{x_{1}}\prec\mathcal{A}_{x_{2}}\prec\dots\prec\mathcal{A}_{x_{N}}\prec\mathcal{A}_{y_{1}}\prec\mathcal{A}_{y_{2}}\prec\dots\prec\mathcal{A}_{y_{N}}
over
\mathcal{B}
,
E[y_{j}]=0
and
E[y_{j}by_{j}]=E[x_{j}bx_{j}]
for any
j\in[n]
and
b\in\mathcal{B}
. Now note that since
y_{j}
is a
\mathcal{B}
-valued arcsine element, then

E[y_{j}b^{*}y_{j}^{2}by_{j}]=E[y_{j}b^{*}y_{j}]E[y_{j}by_{j}]+\frac{1}{2}E\big[y_{j}b^{*}E[y_{j}^{2}]by_{j}\big]=E[x_{j}b^{*}x_{j}]E[x_{j}bx_{j}]+\frac{1}{2}E\big[x_{j}b^{*}E[x_{j}^{2}]bx_{j}\big]

where we have used the fact that the moments of the second order of
y_{j}
and
x_{j}
match. Using the fact that
E
is positive and that
\sup\|E[x_{j}bx_{j}]\|=\|E[x_{j}^{2}]\|
where the supremum is taken over
b\in\mathcal{B}
with
\|b\|=1
, we conclude that

\alpha_{4}(y)\leq\frac{3}{2}\alpha_{2}(x)^{2}.

Setting
A_{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}y_{j}
, it remains to notice that
A_{n}
is a
\mathcal{B}
-valued generalized arcsine element whose distribution is given by
\mathrm{dil}_{n^{-1/2}}(\nu_{\eta_{1}}\rhd\dots\rhd\nu_{\eta_{n}})
where
\nu_{\eta_{j}}
is the arcsine distribution of variance
\eta_{j}
.

To prove the bound on the Lévy distance, we apply similarly (
1.4
) which yields for any
\varepsilon>0

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big|{\Im}m\big(\varphi[G_{X_{n}}(t+i\varepsilon)]\big)-{\Im}m\big(\varphi[G_{A_{n}}(t+i\varepsilon)]\big)\big|\text{d}t\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon^{3}}\sqrt{\alpha_{2}(x)}\left(\sqrt{\widetilde{\alpha}_{4}(x)}+\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}\alpha_{2}(x)^{2}}\right).

Then using the bound in (
2.4
), we get for any
\varepsilon>0
,

∎

4.3.
Fourth moment theorem for monotone infinitely divisible measures

Fourth moment theorems refer to a simplification of the moment method to prove the weak convergence of a sequence of random variables
y_{n}
to a given random variable
y
. Such theorems state that if the fourth moment of
y_{n}
,
\varphi[y_{n}^{4}]
, approaches the fourth moment of a random variable
y
,
\varphi[y^{4}]
, then
y_{n}\to y
weakly. In certain cases, one can even quantify such convergence in terms of the difference
|\varphi[y_{n}^{4}]-\varphi[y^{4}]|
.

Here we are concerned with the class of infinitely divisible measures with respect to the monotone convolution, which we denote by
ID(\rhd)
. This completes the main theorems from
[
2
]
where the authors give a quantitative version of the results in
[
1
]
, in the cases of free and tensor independence. A fourth moment theorem was given in
[
3
]
for the Boolean case keeping the monotone case open. Our results complete the picture by including the monotone case and proving quantitative bounds for the fourth moment theorem in this setting.

The main observation is that a Berry-Esseen estimate may be used to prove such a theorem. Indeed, using the fact that
\varphi[x^{4}]\geq\varphi[x^{2}]^{2}
, we deduce by Theorem
4.3
that if the
x_{i}
’s are monotonically independent and identically distributed variables with mean
0
, variance
1
and finite fourth moment
\varphi[x_{i}^{4}]
, then

L(\mu_{X_{n}},\mu_{A_{n}})\leq K\Big(\frac{\varphi[x_{i}^{4}]}{n}\Big)^{1/14},

(4.9)

for some constant
K>0
. In order to connect the above bound to a fourth moment theorem we will use the monotone cumulants. It was shown by Hasebe and Saigo
[
15
]
that the sequence of monotone cumulants
\{h_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
satisfies the property that for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
:
h_{n}(\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i},\dots,\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i})=mh_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{1}),
whenever
\{x_{i}\}^{m}_{i=1}
is a collection of identically distributed monotone independent variables. The first four cumulants are written in terms of the first four moments as follows:

\begin{split}h_{1}(x)&=\varphi[x],\\
h_{2}(x,x)&=\varphi[x^{2}]-\varphi[x]^{2},\\
h_{3}(x,x,x)&=\varphi[x^{3}]-\frac{5}{2}\varphi[x^{2}]\varphi[x]+\frac{3}{2}\varphi[x]^{3},\\
h_{4}(x,x,x,x)&=\varphi[x^{4}]-\frac{3}{2}\varphi[x^{2}]^{2}-3\varphi[x^{3}]\varphi[x]+\frac{37}{6}\varphi[x^{2}]\varphi[x]^{2}-\frac{8}{3}\varphi[x]^{4}.\end{split}

(4.10)

Note that if we normalize so that
\varphi[x]=0
and
\varphi[x^{2}]=1
, then
h_{2}(x,x)=\varphi[x^{2}]=1
and
h_{4}(x,x,x,x)=\varphi[x^{4}]-\frac{3}{2}\varphi[x^{2}]^{2}=\varphi[x^{4}]-1.5
. Having this in hand, we now state our fourth moment theorem for the scalar-valued monotone case, which is completely new. The key point is that now we have a bound in terms of the second and fourth moment, which was not the case in previous works, see
[
4
, Theorem 1.1]
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
Y
be a monotone infinitely divisible random variable with mean
0
and variance
1
and
A
be a standard arcsine element. Then
L(\mu_{Y},\mu_{A})\leq K(\varphi[Y^{4}]-1.5)^{1/14}
,
for some
K>0
.

Proof.

Since
Y
is infinitely divisible, then for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we may write
Y
as a sum of
n
identically monotone independent random variables

Y=y_{1}+\dots+y_{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(\sqrt{n}y_{1}+\dots+\sqrt{n}y_{n}).

Noting that
\varphi[(\sqrt{n}y_{i})^{2}]=h_{2}(\sqrt{n}y_{i},\sqrt{n}y_{i})=nh_{2}(y_{i},y_{i})=h_{2}(Y,Y)=1
and
h_{4}(\sqrt{n}y_{i})=n^{2}h_{4}(y_{i},y_{i},y_{i},y_{i})=nh_{4}(Y,Y,Y,Y),
then

\varphi[(\sqrt{n}y_{i})^{4}]=h_{4}(\sqrt{n}y_{i},\sqrt{n}y_{i},\sqrt{n}y_{i},\sqrt{n}y_{i})+1.5=nh_{4}(Y,Y,Y,Y)+1.5\,.

For a standard arcsine element
A
, we may assume, without loss of generality, that
A=A_{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(x_{1}+\dots+x_{n})
where the
x_{i}
’s are also standard arcsine variables. With this observation, Theorem
4.3
holds and hence the bound (
4.9
) yields that

\displaystyle L(\mu_{Y},\mu_{A})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L(\mu_{Y},\mu_{A_{n}})\leq K\left(\frac{\varphi[(\sqrt{n}y_{i})^{4}]}{n}\right)^{1/14}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle K\left(\frac{h_{4}(\sqrt{n}y_{i},\sqrt{n}y_{i},\sqrt{n}y_{i},\sqrt{n}y_{i})+1.5}{n}\right)^{1/14}=K\left(h_{4}(Y,Y,Y,Y)+\frac{1.5}{n}\right)^{1/14}.

Since
n
is arbitrary, we let it go to infinity to obtain as desired

L(\mu_{A},\mu_{Y})\leq K(h_{4}(Y,Y,Y,Y))^{1/14}=K(\varphi[Y^{4}]-1.5)^{1/14}.

∎

In the case of operator-valued monotone independent variables, we can give a bound for the difference of the Cauchy transforms in terms of the fourth
\mathcal{B}
-valued cumulants.

For this aim, we need analogue operator-valued formulas to those in (
4.10
). As before, when we restrict to the centered case, i.e.
E[x]=0
, the formulas simplify and we get that

h_{1}^{\mathcal{B}}(x)=E(x)=0
,
h_{2}^{\mathcal{B}}(xb,x)=E[xbx]=\eta(b)
,
h_{3}^{\mathcal{B}}(xb_{1},xb_{2},x)=E[xb_{1}xb_{2}x]
and

h_{4}^{\mathcal{B}}(xb_{1},xb_{2},xb_{3},x)=E[xb_{1}xb_{2}xb_{3}x]-E[xb_{1}x]b_{2}E[xb_{3}x]-\frac{1}{2}E\left[xb_{1}E[xb_{2}x]b_{3}x\right].

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},E,\mathcal{B})
be a
C^{\ast}
-probability space and
Y
be monotone
n
-divisible element over
\mathcal{B}
with

and

and

\big\|E[G^{M_{k}(\mathcal{B})}_{1_{k}\otimes Y}(\mathfrak{b})]-E[G^{M_{k}(\mathcal{B})}_{1_{k}\otimes A}(\mathfrak{b})]\big\|\leq k\|{\Im}m(\mathfrak{b})^{-1}\|^{4}\sqrt{\|\eta(1)\|}\sqrt{\sup\|h_{4}^{\mathcal{B}}(Yb,Y,Yb^{\prime},Y)\|},

where

\mathcal{B}

.

As the above bound holds on the level of the fully matricial extensions of Cauchy transforms, it is sufficient to capture convergence in distribution over

which yields the following operator-valued monotone fourth moment theorem:

Corollary 4.7

Let
(\mathcal{A},E,\mathcal{B})
be a

where the supremum is taken over all

to a
\mathcal{B}

If, in addition,

with respect to

.

Proof of Theorem
4.6
.

Let
Y
be monotone
n
-divisible over
\mathcal{B}
. We write
Y
as a sum of
n
identically distributed monotone independent random variables

Y=y_{1}+\dots+y_{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(\sqrt{n}y_{1}+\dots+\sqrt{n}y_{n}).

For a
\mathcal{B}
-valued arcsine element

are themselves
\mathcal{B}

.

holds and yields following bound: for any

and any

\displaystyle\big\|E[G_{Y}(\mathfrak{b})]-E[G_{A}(\mathfrak{b})]\big\|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\|{\Im}m(\mathfrak{b})^{-1}\|^{4}B_{n}(y),

where

To control the above estimate, we note that

\alpha_{2}(\sqrt{n}y_{1})=\|E[\sqrt{n}y_{1}1\sqrt{n}y_{1}]\|=\|h_{2}^{\mathcal{B}}(\sqrt{n}y_{1}1,\sqrt{n}y_{1})\|=\|h_{2}^{\mathcal{B}}(Y1,Y)\|=\|\eta(1)\|.

\displaystyle E[\sqrt{n}y_{1}b^{*}(\sqrt{n}y_{1})^{2}b\sqrt{n}y_{1}]

\displaystyle=h_{4}^{\mathcal{B}}(\sqrt{n}y_{1}b^{*},\sqrt{n}y_{1},\sqrt{n}y_{1}b,\sqrt{n}y_{1})+\eta(b^{*})\eta(b)+\frac{1}{2}\eta(b^{*}\eta(1)b)

\displaystyle=nh_{4}^{\mathcal{B}}(Yb^{*},Y,Yb,Y)+\eta(b^{*})\eta(b)+\frac{1}{2}\eta(b^{*}\eta(1)b),

which yields that

\displaystyle\alpha_{4}(\sqrt{n}y_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{b\in\mathcal{B},\ \|b\|=1}\|E[\sqrt{n}y_{1}b^{*}(\sqrt{n}y_{1})^{2}b\sqrt{n}y_{1}]\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{b\in\mathcal{B},\ \|b\|=1}n\|h_{4}^{\mathcal{B}}(Yb^{*},Y,Yb,Y)\|+\|\eta(1)\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\eta(1)\|.

Hence, we for any

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}B_{n}(y)\!\leq\!\sqrt{\|\eta(1)\|}\!\left(\!\sqrt{\sup\limits_{b\in\mathcal{B},\ \|b\|=1}\!\|h_{4}^{\mathcal{B}}(Yb^{*},Y,Yb,Y)\|+\frac{\|\eta(1)\|^{2}}{n}+\frac{\|\eta(1)\|}{2n}}+\!\sqrt{\frac{3\|\eta(1)\|^{2}}{2n}}\right).

Finally letting

\big\|E[G_{Y}(\mathfrak{b})]-E[G_{A}(\mathfrak{b})]\big\|\leq\|{\Im}m(\mathfrak{b})^{-1}\|^{4}\sqrt{\|\eta(1)\|}\sqrt{\sup\limits_{b\in\mathcal{B},\ \|b\|=1}\|h_{4}^{\mathcal{B}}(Yb^{*},Y,Yb,Y)\|}.

(4.11)

k=1

2.6

and

4.4.
Infinitesimal CLTs

This section is devoted to studying operator-valued infinitesimal central limit theorems, that were introduced in
[
27
]
, see Theorem
2.17
. We provide in this paper the first quantitative results in this setting that are on the level of operator-valued infinitesimal Cauchy transforms.

Suppose that
(\mathcal{A},E,E^{\prime},\mathcal{B})
is an OV
C^{*}
-infinitesimal probability space. Let
x:=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
be a family of self-adjoint elements in
\mathcal{A}
such that
E[x_{j}]=E^{\prime}[x_{j}]=0
for all
j
and set

X_{n}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}.

Given the setting we consider, we will denote by
S_{n}
a
\mathcal{B}
-valued infinitesimal semicircle, Bernoulli or generalized arcsine element (see
[
37
, Section 3.3]
) with the infinitesimal variance
(\eta_{n},\eta_{n}^{\prime})
given by

\eta_{n}(b)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}E[x_{j}bx_{j}]\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\eta^{\prime}_{n}(b)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}E^{\prime}[x_{j}bx_{j}].

We assume that our OVI probability space is rich enough to contain a family
y:=\{y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\}
of independent
\mathcal{B}
-valued infinitesimal semicircular/Bernoulli/arcsine elements, that is itself independent of
x
and is such that
E[y_{i}by_{i}]=E[x_{i}bx_{i}]
and
E^{\prime}[y_{i}by_{i}]=E^{\prime}[x_{i}bx_{i}]
for any
i=1,\dots,n
. In the monotone case, the independence of
y
from
x
is assumed to hold in the sense that
\mathcal{A}_{x_{1}}\mathrel{\scalebox{.9}[1]{$\prec$}\mkern-5.0mu\scalebox{.4}[1]{$\prec$}\mkern-5.5mu\scalebox{.4}[1]{$\prec$}}\dots\mathrel{\scalebox{.9}[1]{$\prec$}\mkern-5.0mu\scalebox{.4}[1]{$\prec$}\mkern-5.5mu\scalebox{.4}[1]{$\prec$}}\mathcal{A}_{x_{n}}\mathrel{\scalebox{.9}[1]{$\prec$}\mkern-5.0mu\scalebox{.4}[1]{$\prec$}\mkern-5.5mu\scalebox{.4}[1]{$\prec$}}\mathcal{A}_{y_{1}}\mathrel{\scalebox{.9}[1]{$\prec$}\mkern-5.0mu\scalebox{.4}[1]{$\prec$}\mkern-5.5mu\scalebox{.4}[1]{$\prec$}}\dots\mathrel{\scalebox{.9}[1]{$\prec$}\mkern-5.0mu\scalebox{.4}[1]{$\prec$}\mkern-5.5mu\scalebox{.4}[1]{$\prec$}}\mathcal{A}_{y_{n}}
over
\mathcal{B}
.

We state now the first quantitative estimates in the infinitesimal setting that follow directly from Theorem
1.3
and that provide analogue estimates to those in Section 4.

Theorem 4.8
.

Let
x:=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
be a family of self-adjoint elements in
\mathcal{A}
that are infinitesimally free/Boolean/monotone independent with amalgamation over
\mathcal{B}
and that are such that
E[x_{j}]=E^{\prime}[x_{j}]=0
. Then, for any
\mathfrak{b}\in\mathbb{H}^{+}(\mathcal{B})
,

\big\|E^{\prime}\big[G_{X_{n}}(\mathfrak{b})\big]-E^{\prime}\big[G_{S_{n}}(\mathfrak{b})\big]\big\|\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{n}}\|{\Im}m(\mathfrak{b})^{-1}\|^{4}\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\|x_{i}\|^{3}+8\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\|E[x_{i}^{2}]\|^{\frac{3}{2}}\right),

where
S_{n}
is a
\mathcal{B}
-valued infinitesimal semicircular/Bernoulli/generalized arcsine element with the infinitesimal variance

Let

5.1.
Matrix Valued Bernoulli

Let
(\mathcal{A},E,\mathcal{B})
be an OV
C^{*}
-probability space, and
(\mathcal{A},\varphi)
be a
C^{*}
-probability space such that
\varphi=\varphi\circ E
. Let
B
be a
\mathcal{B}
-valued Bernoulli element with variance map
\eta(b)=E[BbB]
. We illustrate in the following lemma what the scalar-valued distribution of
B
is. For a measure
\mu
, let us denote by
\mu^{(2)}
the push forward of the measure
\mu
along the map
t\to t^{2}.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
B
be a
\mathcal{B}
-valued Bernoulli element with
\eta(b)=E[BbB]
as variance map, then for any
n\in\mathbb{N}

\varphi[B^{2n}]=\varphi[\eta(1)^{n}]\quad\text{ and }\quad\varphi[B^{2n+1}]=0.

(5.1)

In particular, if
\mu_{B}
and
\mu_{\eta(1)}
are the distributions of
B
and
\eta(1)
with respect to
\varphi
, respectively, then
\mu_{B}
is the unique symmetric measure such that
\mu^{(2)}_{B}=\mu_{\eta(1)}
.

Proof.

To compute the moments of a
\mathcal{B}
-valued Bernoulli variable
B
, we apply the Boolean moment-cumulant formula and use the fact that the only non-vanishing cumulant of
B
is
\beta_{2}(Bb,B)=\eta(b)
. Since there is only one pair interval partition on
[2n]
, namely
\pi=\{\{1,2\},\dots,\{2n-1,2n\}\}
, we get

E[B^{2n}]=\eta(1)^{n}\text{ and }E[B^{2n+1}]=0.

Now applying
\varphi
on both sides, we obtain (
5.1
) and end the proof.
∎

Having Lemma
5.1
in hand, we obtain immediately the following result. We give a proof for the convenience of the reader.

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
B
and
\{B_{n}\}_{n}\geq 0
be
\mathcal{B}
-valued Bernoulli elements with variance maps
\eta
and
\{\eta_{n}\}_{n}\geq 0
, respectively. Then,
\mu_{B_{n}}\to\mu_{B}
weakly, if and only if
\mu_{\eta_{n}(1)}\to\mu_{\eta(1)}
, weakly.

Proof.

To relate
\mu_{\eta_{n}(1)}
and
\mu_{\eta(1)}
, we first note that
\mu_{B_{n}}([-t,t])=\mu_{\eta_{n}(1)}([0,t^{2}])
, and similarly,
\mu_{B}([-t,t])=\mu_{\eta(1)}([0,t^{2}])
. If
\mu_{B_{n}}\to\mu_{B}
,
then

\mu_{B_{n}}([-t,t])\to\mu_{B}([-t,t])

and then
\mu_{\eta_{n}(1)}([0,t^{2}])

\to\mu_{\eta(1)}([0,t^{2}])
, which implies that
\eta_{n}(1)\to\eta(1)
, since by definition
\eta_{n}(1)\to\eta(1)
are measure supported on
\mathbb{R^{+}}
. The converse follows the same lines by the observations that symmetric measures are determined by the measure on the intervals of the form
[-t,t]
.

∎

Remark 5.3

(Norm of

We note that one can construct

Lemma 5.4

Let
(\mathcal{A},\varphi)
be a non-commutative probability space. We denote by

\big[\beta^{M_{n}(\mathbb{C})}_{m}\big(A_{n}^{(r_{1})},A_{n}^{(r_{2})},\dots,A_{n}^{(r_{m})}\big)\big]_{ij}=\sum^{n}_{i_{2},\ldots,i_{m}=1}\beta_{m}\big(a_{ii_{2}}^{(r_{1})},a_{i_{2}i_{3}}^{(r_{2})},\ldots,a_{i_{m}j}^{(r_{m})}\big).

With Lemma

Proposition 5.5
.

In the quadruple
(M_{n}(\mathcal{A}),\operatorname{tr}_{n}\otimes\varphi,id_{n}\otimes\varphi,M_{n}(\mathbb{C}))

is an operator valued Bernoulli element over

\eta_{n}:\mathcal{D}_{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}_{n},\qquad D\mapsto\eta_{n}(D)

\big(\eta_{n}(D)\big)_{i,j}=\delta_{ij}\Big(\sum^{i-1}_{k=1}d_{kk}\alpha_{ik}+d_{ii}\sigma_{i}+\sum^{n}_{k=i+1}d_{kk}\tilde{\alpha}_{ik}\Big).

In particular, the distribution of
B_{n}
with respect to

\mu_{B_{n}}=\frac{1}{2n}\sum^{n}_{i=1}(\delta_{\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}}+\delta_{-\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}}).

(5.3)

Proof.

The fact that
B_{n}

\eta
-circular elements that are all Boolean independent up to symmetry, it follows that for any

\beta^{M_{n}(\mathbb{C})}_{2}(B_{n}C,B_{n}):=\eta_{n}(C)
is given for any

by

\displaystyle\big[\beta^{M_{n}(\mathbb{C})}_{2}(B_{n}C,B_{n})\big]_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum^{n}_{k,\ell=1}\beta_{2}(b_{ik}c_{k\ell},b_{\ell j})=\delta_{ij}\sum^{n}_{k=1}c_{kk}\beta_{2}(b_{ik},b_{ki})

\displaystyle=\delta_{i,j}\big(\sum^{i-1}_{k=1}c_{kk}\alpha_{ik}+c_{ii}\sigma_{i}+\sum^{n}_{k=i+1}c_{kk}\tilde{\alpha}_{ik}\big).

As any

-valued joint Boolean cumulants of order larger than

is equal to

B_{n}
is an operator-valued Bernoulli element over
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})

\eta_{n}
sends the subalgebra of diagonal matrices
\mathcal{D}_{n}\subset M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
into itself, to see that
B_{n}
is in fact an operator-valued Bernoulli element over
\mathcal{D}_{n}
. We omit the proof of this statement in the Boolean case, which can be proven in the same way as its free analogue in
[
25
, Theorem 3.1]
.

To prove the last part of the proposition, we note that by Lemma
5.1
, we have

\operatorname{tr}_{n}\otimes\varphi[B_{n}^{2k}]=\operatorname{tr}_{n}\otimes\varphi[\eta(1)^{k}]=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{k}\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{tr}_{n}\otimes\varphi[B_{n}^{2k+1}]=0.

On the other hand, denoting by
\delta_{\lambda}
the Dirac measure at
\lambda
, we can easily check that the
m
-th moment of the probability measure
\mu=\frac{1}{2n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}(\delta_{\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}}+\delta_{-\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}})
is equal to
\operatorname{tr}_{n}\otimes\varphi[B_{n}^{m}]
, which proves (
5.3
).

∎

Example 5.6
.

We show in the following examples how we can explicitly compute the spectral distribution of
B_{n}
using Proposition
5.5
and find their limiting distribution, as
n
tends to infinity. We consider the cases when the entries are identically distributed and when they have a variance profile.

(i)

Identically distributed entries: For any
1\leq j\leq i\leq n
, let
\sigma_{i}=\sigma
,
\alpha_{ij}=\alpha/n
and
\tilde{\alpha}_{ij}=\tilde{\alpha}/n
, and assume without loss of generality that
0<\tilde{\alpha}\leq\alpha
.
In this case,

\lambda_{i}=\sigma+\frac{i-1}{n}\alpha+\frac{n-i}{n}\tilde{\alpha}=\sigma+\tilde{\alpha}-\frac{\alpha}{n}+\frac{i}{n}(\alpha-\tilde{\alpha}).

Now as
\lambda_{i+1}-\lambda_{i}=(\alpha-\tilde{\alpha})/{n}
, we notice that the
\lambda_{i}
’s are equally spaced with
\lambda_{1}=\sigma+\frac{n-1}{n}\tilde{\alpha}
and
\lambda_{n}=\sigma+\frac{n-1}{n}\alpha
. Thus, in the limit as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\mu_{X_{n}}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\delta_{\lambda_{i}}\to\text{Uniform}\big(\sigma+\tilde{\alpha},\ \sigma+\alpha\big).

One can also see this by computing the cumulative distribution function of
X_{n}
for any
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
,

P(X_{n}\leq w)=\frac{w-(\sigma+\tilde{\alpha})+\alpha/n}{\alpha-\tilde{\alpha}}\longrightarrow\frac{w-(\sigma+\tilde{\alpha})}{\alpha-\tilde{\alpha}}\text{ as }n\to\infty.

From this, one can easily show that
\mu_{B_{n}}:=\frac{1}{2n}\sum^{n}_{i=1}(\delta_{\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}}+\delta_{-\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}})\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow\infty]{}\mu
with density

\mu(dt)=\frac{|t|}{(\alpha-\tilde{\alpha})}dt,\quad|t|\in(\sqrt{\sigma+\tilde{\alpha}},\sqrt{\sigma+\alpha}).

Moreover, in the case where
\tilde{\alpha}=\alpha
,
B_{n}
is a scalar Bernoulli element with distribution

\mu_{B_{n}}=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{\sqrt{\sigma+{\frac{n-1}{n}}\alpha}}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{-\sqrt{\sigma+{\frac{n-1}{n}}\alpha}},

which converges to
\frac{1}{2}\delta_{\sqrt{\sigma+\alpha}}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{-\sqrt{\sigma+\alpha}}.

(ii)

Variance profile: Assume for any
1\leq j\leq i\leq n
,
\alpha_{ij}=\tilde{\alpha}_{ij}=|i-j|/n^{2}
and
\sigma_{i}=0
. Then in this case, it is easy to see that

\lambda_{i}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum^{n}_{k=1}|k-i|=\frac{(n-i+1)(n-i)}{2n^{2}}+\frac{i(i-1)}{2n^{2}}.

This means that for
t\in[0,1]
,
\lambda_{[tn]}\to\frac{1}{2}-t(1-t)
. Thus if
\rho
is the limiting distribution of
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\delta_{\lambda_{i}}
, it satisfies that
\rho((1/4,(t^{2}+1)/4))=t
, for
t\in(0,1)
. From where the the density of
\rho
is given by
\rho(dt)=\frac{4}{\sqrt{4t-1}}dt
for
t\in(1/4,1/2)
and thus

\mu(dt)=\frac{2|t|}{\sqrt{4t^{2}-1}}\quad|t|\in(1/2,\sqrt{1/2}).

5.2.
Wigner Matrices with Boolean entries and variance profile

Consider the
n\times n
operator-valued matrix
A_{n}
defined by

A_{n}=\sum_{1\leq j\leq i\leq n}\big(e_{ij}\otimes a_{ij}+e_{ij}^{*}\otimes a_{ij}^{*}\big)

(5.4)

where we recall that
e_{ii}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{n}}E_{ii}
and
e_{ij}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}E_{ij}
for
j<i
with
(E_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
denoting the standard matrix units in
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
.

Let
a=\{a_{ij}\mid 1\leq j\leq i\leq n\}
be a family of elements of
\mathcal{A}
that are Boolean independent, centered with respect to
\varphi
and are such that
a_{ii}=a_{ii}^{*}
,
\varphi[a_{ii}^{2}]:=\sigma_{i}
for all
i
and
\varphi[a_{ij}^{2}]=0
,
\varphi[a_{ij}a_{ij}^{*}]:=\alpha_{ij}
and
\varphi[a_{ij}^{\ast}a_{ij}]:=\tilde{\alpha}_{ij}
for all
j<i
. Note that the entries of
A_{n}
are assumed to be, up to symmetry, Boolean independent but do not need to be identically distributed. We will give quantitative estimates on the distribution of such matrices in terms of Cauchy transforms. Our results generalize the ones in Popa and Hao
[
30
]
in the case of C*-algebras, since we do not impose a condition on the convergence of moments of order larger than
4
for the entries and more important we allow having a variance profile in the matrix.

With this aim, we apply Theorem
1.1
in the quadruple
(M_{n}(\mathcal{A}),

\operatorname{tr}_{n}\otimes\varphi,id_{n}\otimes\varphi,M_{n}(\mathbb{C}))
associated with
(\mathcal{A},\varphi)
.

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
A_{n}
be an operator-valued Wigner matrix described above and
B_{n}
an operator-valued Bernoulli
B_{n}
as in (
5.2
). Then for any
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
,

\big|(\operatorname{tr}_{n}\otimes\varphi)[G_{A_{n}}(z)]-(\operatorname{tr}_{n}\otimes\varphi)[G_{B_{n}}(z)]\big|\leq\frac{16}{{\Im}m(z)^{4}}\Big(\max_{j\leq i}\|a_{ij}\|^{3}\Big)\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}.

(5.5)

Proof.

Let
b=\{b_{ij}\mid 1\leq j\leq i\leq n\}
be a family of
Boolean independent elements
such that

•

for any
1\leq i\leq n
,
b_{ii}
is a Bernoulli element with
\varphi[b_{ii}]=0
and
\varphi[b_{ii}^{2}]=\varphi[a_{ii}^{2}]
,

•

for any
1\leq j<i\leq N
,
b_{ij}
is a
\eta
-diagonal circular element with
\varphi[b_{ij}]=0
and
\varphi[b_{ij}b_{ij}^{*}]=\varphi[a_{ij}a_{ij}^{*}]:=\alpha_{ij}
and
\varphi[b_{ij}^{\ast}b_{ij}]=\varphi[a_{ij}^{\ast}a_{ij}]:=\tilde{\alpha}_{ij}
.

Without loss of generality,
b
can be assumed to be Boolean independent from
a
. Now set
S_{N}
to be the
N\times N
operator-valued Wigner matrix whose entries are given by the family
b
, i.e.

B_{n}=\sum_{1\leq j\leq i\leq n}\big(e_{ij}\otimes b_{ij}+e_{ij}^{*}\otimes b_{ij}^{*}\big).

Finally, set
x_{ij}=e_{ij}\otimes a_{ij}+e_{ij}^{*}\otimes a_{ij}^{*}
and
y_{ij}=e_{ij}\otimes b_{ij}+e_{ij}^{*}\otimes b_{ij}^{*}
for all
1\leq j\leq i\leq n
. By an application of Lemma
5.4
, one sees that, when lifted to matrices, Boolean independence is preserved, in the sense that independence of the entries
\{a_{ij},b_{ij}\mid 1\leq j\leq i\leq n\}
with respect to
\varphi
implies independence with amalgamation over
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
of
\{x_{ij},y_{ij}\mid 1\leq j\leq i\leq n\}
with respect to
\operatorname{id}_{n}\otimes\varphi
. Moreover, the variables are centered and the moments of second order match: for any
b\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
,

(\operatorname{id}_{n}\otimes\varphi)[x_{ij}]=(\operatorname{id}_{n}\otimes\varphi)[y_{ij}]=0\quad\text{and}\quad(\operatorname{id}_{n}\otimes\varphi)\big[x_{ij}bx_{ij}\big]=(\operatorname{id}_{n}\otimes\varphi)\big[y_{ij}\,b\,y_{ij}\big].

Having this in hand and setting
N=n(n+1)/2
, we follow the same steps as Theorem
1.1
. By adopting its notation, we get for any
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
,

\big|(\operatorname{tr}_{n}\otimes\varphi)[G_{A_{n}}(z)]-(\operatorname{tr}_{n}\otimes\varphi)[G_{B_{n}}(z)]\big|\leq\sum_{1\leq j\leq i\leq n}\Big(\big|(tr_{n}\otimes\varphi)[W_{ij}(z)]\big|+\big|(tr_{n}\otimes\varphi)[\widetilde{W}_{ij}(z)]\big|\Big),

where

\displaystyle W_{ij}(z)

\displaystyle=G_{\mathbf{z}_{ij}}(z)x_{ij}G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{ij}}(z)x_{ij}G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{ij}}(z)x_{ij}G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{ij}}(z),

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}_{ij}(z)

\displaystyle=G_{\mathbf{z}_{i-1,j}(z)}y_{ij}G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{ij}}(z)y_{ij}G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{ij}}(z)y_{ij}G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{ij}}(z).

We start by controlling the term:

\displaystyle\big|(tr_{n}\otimes

\displaystyle\varphi)\big[G_{\mathbf{z}_{ij}}(z)(e_{ij}\otimes a_{ij})G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{ij}}(z)(e_{ij}\otimes a_{ij})G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{ij}}(z)(e_{ij}\otimes a_{ij})G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{ij}}(z)\big]\big|

\displaystyle\leq\|G_{\mathbf{z}_{ij}}(z)\|\|G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{ij}}(z)\|(tr_{n}\otimes\varphi)\big[\big|(e_{ij}\otimes a_{ij})G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{ij}}(z)(e_{ij}\otimes a_{ij})G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{ij}}(z)(e_{ij}\otimes a_{ij})\big|\big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n\sqrt{n}}\|G_{\mathbf{z}_{ij}}(z)\|\|G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{ij}}(z)\|(tr_{n}\otimes\varphi)\big[\big|E_{ij}\otimes a_{ij}[G_{\mathbf{z}_{ij}^{0}}(z)]_{ji}a_{ij}[G_{\mathbf{z}_{ij}^{0}}(z)]_{ji}a_{ij}\big|\big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}\sqrt{n}}\|G_{\mathbf{z}_{ij}}(z)\|\|G_{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{ij}}(z)\|^{3}\|a_{ij}\|^{3}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}\sqrt{n}}\frac{1}{{\Im}m(z)^{4}}\|a_{ij}\|^{3}.

As the other terms are treated in the same way, we get that

\displaystyle\big|(tr_{n}\otimes\varphi)[W_{ij}(z)]\big|

\displaystyle\leq 8\frac{1}{n^{2}\sqrt{n}}\frac{1}{{\Im}m(z)^{4}}\|a_{ij}\|^{3},

\displaystyle\big|(tr_{n}\otimes\varphi)[\widetilde{W}_{ij}(z)]\big|

\displaystyle\leq 8\frac{1}{n^{2}\sqrt{n}}\frac{1}{{\Im}m(z)^{4}}\|b_{ij}\|^{3}.

Summing over
1\leq j\leq i\leq n
, we finally get for any
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
,

\big|(\operatorname{tr}_{n}\otimes\varphi)[G_{A_{n}}(z)]-(\operatorname{tr}_{n}\otimes\varphi)[G_{B_{n}}(z)]\big|\leq\frac{8}{{\Im}m(z)^{4}}\max_{j\leq i}\Big(\|a_{ij}\|^{3}+\|b_{ij}\|^{3}\Big)\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}.

As the
b_{ij}
’s are
\eta
-diagonal elements with the same variance as the
a_{ij}
’s, then by Remark
5.3
we have that
\|b_{ij}\|=\max\{\sqrt{\alpha_{ij}},\sqrt{\widetilde{\alpha}_{ij}}\}
.
To obtain
5.5
it remains to notice that
||a_{ij}||^{2}=||a_{ij}a_{ij}^{*}||\geq\varphi[a_{ij}a_{ij}^{*}]=\alpha_{ij}
and
||a_{ij}||^{2}=||a_{ij}^{*}a_{ij}||\geq\varphi[a_{ij}^{*}a_{ij}]=\widetilde{\alpha}_{ij}
.

∎

Combining Theorem
5.7
with Theorem
4.2
we can get limit theorem for Wigner Matrices with Boolean entries.

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
A_{n}
be as in (
5.7
) and suppose that
||a_{ij}||\leq C
, for some constant
C
independent of
N
.
Furthermore let
\eta_{n}(b)=(id_{n}\otimes\varphi)[A_{n}bA_{n}]
and suppose that
\eta_{n}(1)
has distribution
\rho_{n}
with respect
tr\otimes\varphi
. If,
\rho_{n}
to converges to
\rho
in distribution, then, the distribution of
A_{n}
with respect to
tr\otimes\varphi
converges to the unique symmetric measure
\mu
such that
\mu^{(2)}=\rho
.

Proof.

Recall that convergence in distribution is equivalent to uniform convergence of the Cauchy transform on compact sets. Let
\mu_{A_{n}}
be the distribution of
A_{n}
with respect to
tr\otimes\varphi
. We will prove that for any
\varepsilon>0
, and any
K
a compact sets of
\mathbb{C}^{+}
there is an
N
such that, for any
n>N
,

\big|\mathcal{G}_{\mu_{A_{n}}}(z)-\mathcal{G}_{\mu}(z)\big|\leq\varepsilon,\quad\text{for all }z\in K.

To do this, let
B_{n}
be an operator-valued Bernoulli as in Theorem
5.7
with distribution
\rho_{n}
and let
B
be an operator-valued Bernoulli random variable with variance map
\eta
such that
\eta(1)
has distribution
\rho
. Since
\rho_{n}
converges weakly to
\rho
, then by Corollary
5.2
,
\mu_{B_{n}}
converges to

, weakly. Hence, there exists an integer
N_{1}>1
such that, for all

,

\big|\mathcal{G}_{\mu_{B_{n}}}(z)-\mathcal{G}_{\mu_{B}}(z)\big|\leq\varepsilon/2,\quad\text{for all }z\in K.

On the other hand, equation (
5.5
) yields that there exists
N_{2}>1
such that for all

,

|\mathcal{G}_{\mu_{A_{n}}}(z)-\mathcal{G}_{\mu_{B_{n}}}(z)\big|\leq\varepsilon/2,\quad\text{for all }z\in K.

(5.6)

The result follows by taking

.
∎

Appendix A
Operator-valued Infinitesimal Products

This appendix is dedicated to the study of operator-valued infinitesimal products of operator-valued infinitesimal probability spaces. Note that the OV free product was introduced in
[
34
]
, while the OV Boolean and monotone cases have been studied in
[
31
]
and
[
28
]
, respectively. We also refer the reader to
[
17
, Chapter 7]
for a nice summary on the subject. However, this is less studied in the infinitesimal setting where only the (scalar-valued) infinitesimal free product was constructed in
[
12
]
. In this appendix, we give an algebraic construction of the operator-valued infinitesimal product algebraically in the free, Boolean, and monotone settings.

First of all, let us recall the following result (see
[
17
, Theorem 4.3.1]
):
suppose that
H_{1},\dots,H_{n}
are Hilbert-
\mathcal{B}
-bimodules and
\xi_{j}
is a unit vector in
H_{j}
for each
j=1,\dots,n
. In addition, for each
j
, we define the map
E_{j}[b]=\langle\xi_{j},b\xi_{j}\rangle
for all
b\in\mathcal{B}(H_{j})
. Then there exists a Hilbert
\mathcal{B}
-bimodule
H
and a unit vector
\xi
in
H
, and an injective
*
-homomorphism
\rho_{j}:\mathcal{B}(H_{j})\to\mathcal{B}(H)
such that

E[\rho_{j}(b)]=E_{j}[b]\text{ for each }b\in\mathcal{B}(H_{j}).

Moreover,
\rho_{1}(\mathcal{B}(H_{1})),\rho_{2}(\mathcal{B}(H_{2})),\dots,\rho_{n}(\mathcal{B}(H_{n}))
are freely/Boolean/monotone independent with respect to
E
.

Suppose that for each
j
, there exists a self-adjoint
\mathcal{B}
-bimodule linear map
E^{\prime}_{j}:\mathcal{B}(H_{j})\to\mathcal{B}
that is completely bounded with
E_{j}^{\prime}[1]=0
.
Here we let
\mathcal{A}
be be the unital
C^{*}
-algebra generated by
\rho_{1}(\mathcal{B}(H_{1})),\dots,\rho_{n}(\mathcal{B}(H_{n}))
.
For each notion of independence, our aim is to construct a self-adjoint
\mathcal{B}
-bimodule linear map
E^{\prime}
on
\mathcal{A}
that vanishes on
\mathcal{B}
and for which
\rho_{1}(\mathcal{B}(H_{1})),\rho_{2}(\mathcal{B}(H_{2})),

\dots,\rho_{n}(\mathcal{B}(H_{n}))
are infinitesimally independent in the OVI probability space
(\mathcal{A},E,E^{\prime},\mathcal{B})
. Note that for each
j
, we shall define
E^{\prime}
on
\rho_{j}(\mathcal{B}(H_{j}))
as follows:

E^{\prime}[\rho_{j}(b)]:=E^{\prime}_{j}[b]\text{ for all }b\in\mathcal{B}(H_{j}).

To describe the construction of
E^{\prime}
on
\mathcal{A}
, it is sufficient to show how
E^{\prime}
is defined on terms of the following form

\rho_{j_{1}}(\mathcal{B}(H_{j_{1}}))\rho_{j_{2}}(\mathcal{B}(H_{j_{2}}))\dots\rho_{j_{k}}(\mathcal{B}(H_{j_{k}}))

where
j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\in[n]
and
j_{1}\neq j_{2}\neq\dots\neq j_{k}
.

The Infinitesimally Free Case:

E^{\prime}[\rho_{j_{1}}(b_{1})\dots\rho_{j_{k}}(b_{k})]:=\sum\limits_{s=1}^{k}E[\rho_{j_{1}}(b_{1})\dots\rho_{j_{s-1}}(b_{s-1})E^{\prime}[\rho_{j_{s}}(b_{s})]\rho_{j_{s+1}}(b_{s+1})\dots\rho_{j_{k}}(b_{k})]

where
b_{1}\in H_{j_{1}},\dots,b_{k}\in H_{j_{k}}
for some
j_{1}\neq j_{2}\neq\dots\neq j_{k}
that
E_{j_{1}}(b_{1})=\dots=E_{j_{k}}(b_{k})=0.

The Infinitesimally Boolean Case:

E^{\prime}[\rho_{j_{1}}(b_{1})\dots\rho_{j_{k}}(b_{k})]:=\sum\limits_{s=1}^{k}E[\rho_{j_{1}}(b_{1})]\dots E[\rho_{j_{s-1}}(b_{s-1})]E^{\prime}[\rho_{j_{s}}(b_{s})]E[\rho_{j_{s+1}}(b_{s+1})]\dots E[\rho_{j_{k}}(b_{k})]

where
b_{1}\in H_{j_{1}},\dots,b_{k}\in H_{j_{k}}
for some
j_{1}\neq j_{2}\neq\dots\neq j_{k}
.

The Infinitesimally Monotone Case:

\displaystyle E^{\prime}[\rho_{j_{1}}(b_{1})\dots\rho_{j_{k}}(b_{k})]

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle E^{\prime}[\rho_{j_{1}}(b_{1})\dots\rho_{j_{s-1}}(b_{s-1})E[\rho_{j_{s}}(b_{s})]\rho_{j_{s+1}}(b_{s+1})\dots\rho_{j_{k}}(b_{k})]

\displaystyle+E[\rho_{j_{1}}(b_{1})\dots\rho_{j_{s-1}}(b_{s-1})E^{\prime}[\rho_{j_{s}}(b_{s})]\rho_{j_{s+1}}(b_{s+1})\dots\rho_{j_{k}}(b_{k})]

whenever
b_{1}\in H_{j_{1}},\dots,b_{k}\in H_{j_{k}}
and
j_{s-1}<j_{s}>j_{s+1}
.

Note that the fact that
E^{\prime}
is well-defined on
\mathcal{A}
in the Boolean case is obvious. For the free case, we observe that
\mathcal{A}
can be written as follows:

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}1\oplus\bigoplus\limits_{n\geq 0}\bigoplus\limits_{i_{1}\neq\cdots\neq i_{n}}\rho_{i_{1}}(\mathcal{B}(H_{i_{1}}))^{\circ}\rho_{i_{2}}(\mathcal{B}(H_{i_{2}}))^{\circ}\dots\rho_{i_{n}}(\mathcal{B}(H_{i_{n}}))^{\circ}

where
\rho_{i_{j}}(\mathcal{B}(H_{i_{j}}))^{\circ}
is the set of centered elements of
\rho_{i_{j}}(\mathcal{B}(H_{i_{j}}))^{\circ}
. Due to the direct sum decomposition, we only need to define it on
\mathcal{B}1
and each of
\rho_{i_{1}}(\mathcal{B}(H_{i_{1}}))^{\circ}\rho_{i_{2}}(\mathcal{B}(H_{i_{2}}))^{\circ}\dots\rho_{i_{n}}(\mathcal{B}(H_{i_{n}}))^{\circ}
.
Therefore, for the free case,
E^{\prime}
is well-defined on
\mathcal{A}
. In addition,
E^{\prime}
is also well-defined on
\mathcal{A}
for the monotone case by recursively computing the mixed moments.

Since
E_{j}
and
E_{j}^{\prime}
are both self-adjoint for each
j
, we can easily see that
E^{\prime}
is self-adjoint for each case. Moreover, following the definition of
E^{\prime}
, it is clear that
\rho_{1}(\mathcal{B}(H_{1})),\rho_{2}(\mathcal{B}(H_{2})),\dots,

\rho_{n}(\mathcal{B}(H_{n}))
are infinitesimally freely/ Boolean/monotone independent with respect to
(E,E^{\prime})
. However, it is not clear whether we have any continuity property for
E^{\prime}
. Hence, we only have the operator-valued infinitesimal products in the algebraic sense.

*
