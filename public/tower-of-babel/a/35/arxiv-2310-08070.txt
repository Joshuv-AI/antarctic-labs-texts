Tight Time-Space Lower Bounds for Constant-Pass Learning
Xin Lyu, Avishay Tal, Hongxun Wu, Junzhao Yang

Abstract
In his breakthrough paper, Raz showed that any parity learning algorithm requires either quadratic memory or an exponential number of samples [FOCS’16, JACM’19]. A line of work that followed extended this result to a large class of learning problems. Until recently, all these results considered learning in the streaming model, where each sample is drawn independently, and the learner is allowed a single pass over the stream of samples. Garg, Raz, and Tal [CCC’19] considered a stronger model, allowing multiple passes over the stream. In the 2 2 -pass model, they showed that learning parities of size n n requires either a memory of size n 1.5 n^{1.5} or at least 2 n 2^{\sqrt{n}} samples. (Their result also generalizes to other learning problems.) In this work, for any constant q q , we prove tight memory-sample lower bounds for any parity learning algorithm that makes q q passes over the stream of samples. We show that such a learner requires either Ω ⁡ ( n 2 ) \Omega(n^{2}) memory size or at least 2 Ω ⁡ ( n ) 2^{\Omega(n)} samples. Beyond establishing a tight lower bound, this is the first non-trivial lower bound for q q -pass learning for any q ≥ 3 q\geq 3 . Similar to prior work, our results extend to any learning problem with many nearly-orthogonal concepts. We complement the lower bound with an upper bound, showing that parity learning with q q passes can be done efficiently with O ⁡ ( n 2 / log ⁡ q ) O(n^{2}/\log q) memory.

We study time-space lower bounds for multi-pass learning problems. We provide a nearly tight lower bound for two-pass learning parity algorithms:

Extractor-based framework.

Our results apply more generally to any learning problem with many nearly pairwise orthogonal concepts (i.e. concepts that agree on roughly half of the inputs). Alternatively,
to any learning problem whose associated matrix (as defined in

Our main theorems,

Technical Overview

In this section, we will present the road map for our paper, including the difficulties and a sketch of our main ideas for bypassing them.

2.1
Recap of the One-Pass Lower Bound

Both our work and the previous work on the two-pass learning bound
[
GRT19
]
are based on the proof techniques for one-pass lower bound
[
Raz17
,
GRT18
]
. Let us sketch its main idea.

Computational Model.

The proof models the computation as a read-once branching program: The input to the branching program is a sequence of pairs
(a_{1},b_{1}),(a_{2},b_{2}),\dots,(a_{T},b_{T})\in A\times\{-1,1\}
. Each of them represents an equation
M(a_{i},x)=b_{i}
. These
a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{T}\in A
are sampled uniformly at random, while
b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{T}
are all generated according to the hidden vector
x\in X
. (In our paper, for simplicity, we will identify
X
with
\{0,1\}^{n}
.) We label the layers of the branching program by
0,1,2,\dots,T
. Let
v
be the current vertex. Initially, it is equal to the starting vertex of the branching program at layer
0
. At each layer
i
, we read
(a_{i+1},b_{i+1})
and move
v
along one corresponding edge to layer
i+1
. At the end of the computation,
v
will reach the last layer
T
. Then, it outputs a vector
x_{v}
. We say it is successful if and only if
x_{v}=x
.

The length of this branching program is the number of samples
T
. The width is
2^{S}
where
S\leq n^{2}/16
is the memory bound. We want to prove that when
S\leq n^{2}/16
and
T\leq 2^{n/16}
, the program cannot succeed with constant probability.

Main Idea of the One-Pass Lower Bound.

When outputting
x_{v}
, the optimal strategy is to output the
x^{\prime}
with the highest posterior probability
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v}(x^{\prime})
. Intuitively, if the distribution
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v}
is very spread, measured by its
\ell_{2}
norm, the vertex
v
will have a small chance of answering
x
correctly. We will define its
\ell_{2}
norm as

\|\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v}\|_{2}\coloneqq\mathop{\mathbf{E}}_{x^{\prime}\sim X}\left[\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v}^{2}(x^{\prime})\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Initially, as
X=\{0,1\}^{n}
, the uniform prior
\mathbb{P}_{x}
has
\|\mathbb{P}_{x}\|_{2}=2^{-n}
.
As
v
moves along the computational path, the posterior distribution
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v}
evolves. In the end, one can show that, for some
\epsilon>0
, if
\|\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v}\|_{2}\leq 2^{\epsilon n}\cdot 2^{-n}
, the probability that
v
answers
x
correctly will be less than
2^{-\Theta(n)}
. (Think of
\epsilon
as a small constant, say
\epsilon=0.1
.)

Hence, to upper bound the success probability, it is sufficient to upper bound the probability that we ever reach a vertex
v
with
\|\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v}\|_{2}>2^{\epsilon n}\cdot 2^{-n}
on our computational path. To show this, we will enumerate all target vertices
t
with
\|\mathbb{P}_{x\mid t}\|_{2}>2^{\epsilon n}\cdot 2^{-n}
and prove that the probability of reaching a fixed
t
is less than
2^{-\Theta(n^{2})}
. Then, our desired upper bound follows from a union bound over all
2^{n^{2}/16+O(n)}
possibilities of vertex
t
.

Progress Measure.

To study the probability of reaching
t
, we need to look at the similarity between our current posterior
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v}
and the target
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid t}
, captured by their inner product
\langle\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v},\mathbb{P}_{x\mid t}\rangle
, defined as

\left\langle\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v},\mathbb{P}_{x\mid t}\right\rangle\coloneqq\mathop{\mathbf{E}}_{x^{\prime}\sim X}\left[\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v}(x^{\prime})\cdot\mathbb{P}_{x\mid t}(x^{\prime})\right]=\frac{1}{|X|}\sum_{x^{\prime}\in X}\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v}(x^{\prime})\cdot\mathbb{P}_{x\mid t}(x^{\prime}).

This measures our progress towards
t
. To show that we reach
t
with a very small probability, we will show that for a uniform random
a\in A
, reading equation
M(a,x)=b
will, w.h.p., makes little progress.

Let the posterior distribution after reading this equation be
\mathbb{P}^{(a,b)}_{x\mid v}
. Then after normalization, the similarity becomes

\left\langle\mathbb{P}^{(a,b)}_{x\mid v},\mathbb{P}_{x\mid t}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{|X|}\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x^{\prime}\in X\\
M(a,x^{\prime})=b\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v}(x^{\prime})\cdot\mathbb{P}_{x\mid t}(x^{\prime})\Bigg/\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x^{\prime}\in X\\
M(a,x^{\prime})=b\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v}(x^{\prime}).

We say
a\in A
cuts
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v}
evenly if

\sum_{x^{\prime}\in X:M(a,x^{\prime})=0}\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v}(x^{\prime})\approx\sum_{x^{\prime}\in X:M(a,x^{\prime})=1}\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v}(x^{\prime}).

Similarly, we say
a\in A
cuts the point-wise product
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{x\mid t}
evenly if this holds for
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{x\mid t}
instead of
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v}
.

\left(2^{-\Theta(n)}\right)^{2\epsilon n}=2^{-\Theta(n^{2})}

Stopping Rules.

Now we turn to handle the first case. Although it happens with probability at most
2^{-\Theta(n)}
, it only needs to happen once to make a huge progress. Thus, the
2^{-\Theta(n)}
probability is not enough to afford the union bound over all
2^{\Theta(n^{2})}
many targets
t
.

Luckily, we do not have to do union-bound. Observe that whether
a\in A
cuts
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v}
evenly is independent of the target
t
. Whenever we see an equation
a\in A
that cuts
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v}
unevenly in our computational path, we can stop right away. Since there are
T\approx 2^{n/16}
layers in our branching program, a simple union bound over them shows that the overall probability of stopping is still
2^{-\Theta(n)}
. Moreover, if we did not stop, the previous argument shows that we reach any target vertex
t
with probability
2^{-\Theta(n^{2})}
. Overall, our algorithm succeeds with a very small probability.

But this is not the only stopping rule. Recall that a uniformly random
a\in A
cuts both
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v}
and
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{x\mid t}
evenly w.h.p. (by extractor property) only when they are spread enough. We also need stopping rules to guarantee this. Formally, we have the following stopping rules.

•

(Bad Edge) If
a
does not cut
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v}
evenly, we stop.

•

(Significant State) If
\|\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v}\|_{2}\geq 2^{\epsilon n}\cdot 2^{-n}
, we stop.

This guarantees that the distribution of
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v}
will be spread enough for the extractor property.

•

(Significant Value) If
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v}(x)>2^{\epsilon n}\cdot 2^{-n}
, we stop.

After applying this rule, we know
\|\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v}\|_{\infty}\leq 2^{\epsilon n}
. Since
\|\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{x\mid t}\|_{2}\leq\|\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v}\|_{\infty}\cdot\|\mathbb{P}_{x\mid t}\|_{2}
, this guarantees that
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{x\mid t}
will be spread enough for the extractor property.

2.2
The Proof Framework: Two Passes

Our work builds on the approach taken by the previous two-pass lower bound
[
GRT19
]
. We will now sketch their proof framework.

Computational Model.

A two pass branching program reads its input
(a_{1},b_{1}),(a_{2},b_{2}),\dots,(a_{T},b_{T})
twice in the
exact same order
. At the first pass, the starting vertex is
v_{0}
, and after reading its input, the computational path reaches a vertex
v_{1}
at the end of the first pass (which is also the first layer of the second pass). In the second pass, the computational path starts from
v_{1}
and reaches
v_{2}
at the last layer after reading the input again. Then it will output a vector
x_{v_{2}}\in X
.

For any two vertices
u
and
v
in the program, we use
u\ \widetilde{\to}\ v
to denote the following event (over
x,a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{T}
): Imagine that we set the starting vertex of the branching program at
u
, the path from
u
determined by
x,a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{T}
reaches
v
without stopping.

•

For a vertex
v_{1}
in the last layer of the first pass,
v_{0}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v_{1}
means that the first pass ends at
v_{1}
.

•

For any vertex
v_{1}
in the last layer of the first pass, and vertex
v_{2}
in the last layer of the second pass,
v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v_{2}
means that the second pass will end at
v_{2}
if it were starting at
v_{1}
.

First Attempt.

Moving from one pass to two passes, one might consider the following natural approach: First, apply the above argument to the first pass and conclude that, at the end of the first pass, the similarity
\langle\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v},\mathbb{P}_{x\mid t}\rangle
is small. Second, apply it to the second pass and argue that such similarity grows slowly in the second pass too.

However, such a direct approach would not work. Consider a program that (1) magically learns
x
, (2) remembers
x\oplus a_{1}
(thinking of
x
and
a_{1}
as bit strings), and (3) forgets
x
and
a_{1}
at the end of the first pass. Conditioning on what
v
remembers,
x\oplus a_{1}
, the distribution
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v}
is uniformly random, just like the prior
\mathbb{P}_{x}
. This is because
x
is encrypted by the one-time pad using
a_{1}
. So in the eyes of our analysis, this magical first pass is no different from a trivial first pass. If we do not rule out the possibility of such a magical first pass, what could happen in the second pass is that, after seeing
a_{1}
again, the program combines
a_{1}
with its knowledge of
x\oplus a_{1}
and immediately decodes
x
.

v_{1}
is unique since
v
remembers it.

When
v=v_{2}
for some vertex
v_{2}
in the last layer (of the second pass), this simplifies to

\displaystyle(v_{0}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v_{2})

\displaystyle=(v_{0}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v_{1})\land(v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v_{2})

\displaystyle=(v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v_{2}).

Progress Measure.

When the program reaches
v_{2}
, the optimal strategy for it is to output the
x^{\prime}\in X
with the highest
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{0}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v_{2}}(x^{\prime})
. Note that this equals
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v_{2}}(x^{\prime})
(by the equation above).

Hence, similar to the one-pass case, when the distribution
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v_{2}}
is spread enough (
\|\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v_{2}}\|_{2}\geq 2^{\epsilon n}\cdot 2^{-n}
), vertex
v_{2}
cannot answer
x
correctly. To upper bound the probability of answering
x
correctly, we only need to upper bound the probability of reaching any target state
t
with
\|\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ t}\|_{2}\geq 2^{\epsilon n}\cdot 2^{-n}
.

Initially,
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v_{1}}=\mathbb{P}_{x}
. As the current vertex
v
moves along the computational path from
v_{1}
, the posterior
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v}
evolves similarly to the one-pass case. Let the similarity
\langle\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v},\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ t}\rangle
be the progress measure, and let
\mathbb{P}^{(a,b)}_{x\mid v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v}
be the posterior after reading a new equation
(a,b)
. We have,

\left\langle\mathbb{P}^{(a,b)}_{x\mid v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v},\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ t}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{|X|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x^{\prime}\in X\\
M(a,x^{\prime})=b\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v}(x^{\prime})\cdot\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ t}(x^{\prime})\Bigg/\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x^{\prime}\in X\\
M(a,x^{\prime})=b\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v}(x^{\prime}).

Until now, this seems like a natural generalization of the one-pass case. However, for one pass, we heavily rely on the fact that
a\in A
is uniformly random. In the second pass, we no longer have this property. For example, the program could simply remember
a_{1}\in A
from the first pass, then in the second pass,
a_{1}
is completely deterministic, with no randomness at all.

High-Probability Edges.

The previous work
[
GRT19
]
calls such
a_{i}\in A
that is remembered by the program a
high-probability edge
. Formally, for a vertex
v
in the
i
-th layer of the second pass, we say that
a
is a high-probability edge at
v
(denoted by
a\in\mathrm{High}(v)
) if and only if

\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[a_{i+1}=a\mid v_{0}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v]\geq 2^{\epsilon\cdot n}\cdot 2^{-n}.

Since these edges occur with too large probability (much higher than the uniform case,
2^{-n}
), we cannot simply stop when they cut distributions unevenly (like we did for one-pass).

1.

If this
a\in A
cuts
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v}
unevenly: When the denominator
\sum_{x^{\prime}\in X,M(a,x^{\prime})=b}\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v}(x^{\prime})<2^{-c}
, the similarity might be larger by a factor of
2^{c}
, causing huge progress. For now, we ignore this issue and assume that it never arises. We will explain how we handle it in
Section

2.3
.

2.

If this
a\in A
cuts
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ t}
unevenly, but still cuts
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v}
evenly: In this case, the worst case is the same as in one-pass. Namely, the numerator does not decrease, while the denominator is halved. Then the similarity at most doubles.

Their key observation is the following. Intuitively, to remember a single
a\in A
, we need at least
\Omega(n)
memory. Since the memory bound
S\leq n^{2}/16
, the program can only remember
O(n)
many such
a
’s. So there can be at most
O(n)
high probability edges.

Hence, to handle the case in
Item

2
, we observe that these
O(n)
many high-probability edges only blow up the similarity by
2^{O(n)}
. Since initially similarity
\langle\mathbb{P}_{x},\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ t}\rangle=2^{-2n}
, and we want to prove that it would increase to
\|\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ t}\|_{2}^{2}\geq 2^{2\epsilon n}\cdot 2^{-2n}
with very small probability. As long as the constant hidden by big
O
is much smaller than
\epsilon
, this blow-up is negligible. We will pick the correct constants to ensure that this is indeed the case.

2.3
New Ingredient: Bias Counters

As mentioned in the first case, if a high probability edge
a
cuts
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v}
unevenly, the similarity might grow a lot. First, we explain how the previous work
[
GRT19
]
gets around this issue. Then, we will introduce our new idea. This is the key idea for proving the tight memory lower bound for two passes.

Very-bad edge.

To get around this issue, they defined “very-bad edges”, which is the high probability edges
a
that cut
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v}
in a very biased way. Formally,
a\in\mathrm{High}(v)
is “very-bad” if

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x^{\prime}\in X\\
M(a,x^{\prime})=b\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v}(x^{\prime})\leq 2^{-\sqrt{n}}.

•

On the one hand, when a high probability edge is not very-bad, it only blows up the similarity at most by a factor of
2^{\sqrt{n}}
.

If we set the memory bound
S
to be
O(n^{3/2})
, there can only be
c\cdot\sqrt{n}
many high probability edges for some constant
0<c<1
(since remembering one needs
\Omega(n)
memory). Then these
c\cdot\sqrt{n}
many high probability not-very-bad edges can blow up the potential by at most
2^{cn}
. As long as
c\ll\epsilon
, this will be acceptable for our purpose.

•

On the other hand, when an edge
(a,b)
is very-bad, conditioning on we reached this vertex,
x
is distributed as
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v}
. Over the randomness of
x
, the probability that we traverse this edge
(a,b)
instead of
(a,-b)
is at most
2^{-\sqrt{n}}
.

First of all, note this is completely independent of the target
t
. So we do not have to union bound over
t
. Then if we set the sample bound
T
to be
2^{O(\sqrt{n})}
, we can stop immediately whenever we meet a very bad edge. For each step, we stop with probability
2^{-\sqrt{n}}
. By union-bound over all
T
steps, we conclude that the overall stopping probability is still
2^{-\Omega(\sqrt{n})}
.

Therefore, they can prove that for some constant
\epsilon>0
, any two-pass algorithm with
S\leq\epsilon n^{3/2}
memory and
T\leq 2^{\epsilon\sqrt{n}}
samples cannot succeed with constant probability.

New Idea: Bias Counter.

Instead of stopping immediately at biased “very-bad edges”, we introduce a counter
\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}}
to keep track of the accumulated biases: In the second pass, initially, when we were at the starting vertex
v_{1}
, we let
\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}}\leftarrow 0
. For any high probability edge
(a,b)
from current vertex
v
(satisfying
a\in\mathrm{High}(v)
), we say it is
\Delta
-biased if

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x^{\prime}\in X\\
M(a,x^{\prime})=b\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v}(x^{\prime})\in[2^{-\Delta-1},2^{-\Delta}).

Whenever we traverse a
\Delta
-biased edge,
we will update our counter (roughly) by

\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}}\leftarrow\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}}+\Delta.

Note a
\Delta
-biased edge has to be a high probability edge. There can be at most
O(n)
high probability edges when
S\leq n^{2}/16
. Hence we will make at most
O(n)
such updates.

•

On one hand, when the counter
\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}}\leq\epsilon\cdot n
, the high probability edges we have traversed can blow up the similarity by at most
2^{\epsilon n}
.

This follows almost directly from the definition of
\Delta
: We increase the counter by
\Delta
if and only if we traversed a
\Delta
-biased edge. As we have discussed, such an edge would blow up the similarity by at most
2^{\Delta}
. Hence in total, they can blow up the similarity by at most
2^{\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}}}
.

•

On the other hand, the overall probability that
\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}}>\epsilon\cdot n
is small. If we stop whenever the counter exceeds the threshold
\epsilon\cdot n
, we can show the overall stopping probability will be small.

This is because we traverse a
\Delta
-biased edge
(a,b)
instead of the other edge
(a,-b)
with probability at most
2^{-\Delta}
over the randomness of
x
. To gain some intuition, let us think about two extreme cases:

–

Case 1: Each time, the counter increases a little, e.g.,
\Delta\approx 100
. In this case, w.p.
1-(2^{-100})^{(\epsilon/100)\cdot n}=1-2^{-\epsilon n}
, the counter
\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}}
is going to increase like this for less than
(\epsilon/100)\cdot n
times.

(For
\Delta\ll 100
, since there are at most
O(n)
updates, as long as the constant hiding by this big-
O
is much smaller than
\epsilon
, we can ignore these updates, as the total increase due to them is negligible compared to
\epsilon\cdot n
.)

–

Case 2: Each time, the counter increases a lot, i.e.,
\Delta=\delta n
for
0<\delta<1
. In this case, w.p.
1-(2^{-\delta n})^{(\epsilon/\delta)}=1-2^{-\epsilon n}
, the counter
\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}}
will increase like this for less than
\frac{\epsilon}{\delta}
times.

In both cases, the counter
\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}}
will not overflow with high probability. To make
\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}}
larger than
\epsilon\cdot n
, it is necessary to have a sufficiently large number of (correspondingly) sufficiently large
\Delta
, and this is very unlikely. This resembles a famous quote:

“You can fool some of the people all of the time, and all of the people some of the time, but you can not fool all of the people all of the time.”— Abraham Lincoln

Formally, we can show that for each layer
i\in[T]
, the counter overflows at layer
i
with probability at most
2^{-\epsilon n}
. Together with a union bound over
T\ll 2^{\epsilon n}
layers, we prove that the overall stopping probability for counter overflow is small.

This allows us to prove that for some constant
\epsilon^{\prime}>0
, any two-pass algorithm with
S\leq\epsilon^{\prime}n^{2}
memory and
T\leq 2^{\epsilon^{\prime}n}
samples cannot success with constant probability. This new idea is the key to proving a tight lower bound for two-pass learning.

In our actual proof, we will modify the program so that each vertex
v
will “remember” a unique counter value
\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}}(v)
. More details about this modification, the bias counter, and related stopping rules will be presented in
Section

4
.

Potential Argument.

To implement the idea, we will introduce a new stopping rule: We stop whenever the counter
\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}}
exceeds
\epsilon\cdot n
. We have to prove that overall, we stop due to this new rule with a small probability.

Unlike previous stopping rules, this new stopping rule is “soft”, in the sense that when a rare event of probability
2^{-\Delta}
happens, it does not stop right away. Instead, it accumulates such rare events and only stops when enough rare events have occurred. Previously, we only need to analyze stopping probability based on the randomness at the current step. But now, we have to look at the computation history and exploit the fact that we stop only when a lot of rare events have happened in history.

This makes it harder to analyze the stopping probability. Our main technical contribution to two-pass is to come up with a potential analysis resolving the issue. Roughly speaking, our potential function is defined as
\Phi\approx 2^{\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}}}.
Initially, the expectation of
\Phi
is
1
(as we have
\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}}=0
at the starting vertex). Since in each step,
\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}}
increases by
\Delta
with probability roughly
2^{-\Delta}
, the expectation of
\Phi
(of the current state
v
) will be (almost) non-increasing. At the end of the computation, we can use Markov’s inequality to bound the probability of
\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}}>\epsilon\cdot n]
by
\frac{1}{2^{\epsilon n}}
.

We will give a more detailed overview and more intuitions at
Section

5.1
.

2.4
Transfer Lemma

However, the proof still has the last important missing piece. In
Section

2.3
, we argued intuitively that for a
\Delta
-biased edge
(a,b)
, with only
2^{-\Delta}
probability over the randomness of
x
, we traverse
(a,b)
instead of
(a,-b)
. But there is one important subtlety.

Informal discussion.

Note we defined the
\Delta
-biased edges w.r.t. the posterior distribution
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v}
. So we actually showed is the following:

For all starting vertex
v_{1}
of the second pass, after
v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v
, the computational path will then traverse a
\Delta
-biased edge from
v
w.p. at most
2^{-\Delta}
.

But ideally, we want to argue about the two-pass branching program, starting from
v_{0}
. The ideal statement will be:

For the starting vertex
v_{0}
of the first pass, after
v_{0}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v
, the computational path will then traverse a
\Delta
-biased edge from
v
w.p. at most
2^{-\Delta}
.

If
v=v_{2}
is a vertex in the last layer of the second pass, as explained in
Section

2.2
, we know that
v_{0}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v_{2}
is equivalent to
v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v_{2}
. This is because the second pass remembers the first pass, so
v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v_{2}
can “certify” that the first pass indeed reaches
v_{1}
.

But for stopping rules, it is crucial that we stop or update counters in the middle of the program. So
v
is not always going to be in the last layer. Suppose
v
is in the middle of the second layer and
v^{\prime}
is its corresponding vertex in the first layer. As explained in
Section

2.2
, we have

(v_{0}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v)=(v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v)\land(v^{\prime}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v_{1}).

Hence the statement we showed differs from the ideal one. Intuitively, this is because
v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v
can only certify that
v_{0}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v^{\prime}
. We need extra arguments for controlling
v^{\prime}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v_{1}
. This is why we need the transfer lemma, to transfer the statement we showed into the ideal statement we want.

This is the most technical part of
[
GRT19
]
. In
Section

6
, we will frame this subtlety as an adaptive issue and give a slightly simplified proof together with a clean interpretation. This perspective enables us to generalize this lemma to multiple passes.

Distribution Mismatch.

To explain this subtlety in full clarity, let us carefully examine the definition of being
\Delta
-biased. We say that a high probability edge
(a,b)
is
\Delta
-biased if,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x^{\prime}\in X\\
M(a,x^{\prime})=b\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v}(x^{\prime})\leq[2^{-\Delta-1},2^{-\Delta}).

Note that the vector
x
is sampled conditioning on the event
v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v
. Hence it corresponds to the following random process:

•

First, sample a uniformly random
x\in X
and let
v=v_{1}
.

•

At the
i
-th step uniformly sample
a_{i}\in A
and move
v
along the edge
(a_{i},M(a_{i},x))
.

The definition of
\Delta
-biased edges is saying that conditioning on this process reaches
v
, the probability that
M(a,x)=b
is at most
2^{-\Delta}
. However, when we talk about our overall stopping probability, we are referring to a different random process:

•

First, sample a random
x\sim\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{0}\to v_{1}}
and let
v=v_{1}
.

•

At the
i
-th step uniformly sample
a_{i}\sim\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[a_{i}\mid v_{0}\to v]
and move
v
along edge
(a_{i},M(a_{i},x))
.

We can see there is a distribution mismatch between these two. Conditioning on we reached
v
, in the first process, the posterior distribution of
x
is
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v}
, while in the second process, the posterior distribution of
x
is
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{0}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v}
. As explained in
Section

2.2
, in general for a vertex
v
,
(v_{0}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v)=(v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v)\land(v^{\prime}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v_{1})
. The posterior distribution at the end of the second process

\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{0}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v}=\mathbb{P}_{x\mid(v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v)\land(v^{\prime}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v_{1})}

which is not only conditioning on (1) the event that the path from
v_{1}
reaches
v
but also on (2) the event that in the first pass, the path from
v^{\prime}
picks
v_{1}
as the end vertex.

The issue is that we ultimately want to prove that the stopping probability is small in the second process
v_{0}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v
. But as
\Delta
-bias has been defined w.r.t. the first process, our previous argument only shows that the stopping probability is small in the first process
v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v
.

Transfer Lemma.

To resolve this,
[
GRT19
]
introduced the following lemma, “transferring” an upper bound on the stopping probability of the first process
v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v
to an upper bound on the stopping probability of the second process
v_{0}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v
.

Informal Statement:

For any two-pass algorithm, suppose for all fixed starting vertex
v_{1}
, the computational path from
v_{1}
stops with probability less than
2^{-cn}
for some
0<c<1
. The computational path from
v_{0}
will stop in the second pass with probability less than
2^{-(c-\epsilon)n}
for some
0<\epsilon<1
.

Our Perspective.

Note the assumption of this lemma says that
for all fixed
v_{1}
, over the randomness of
x
and
a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{T}
, the probability of stopping is small. The reason there is this distribution mismatch is that the vertex
v_{1}
is not fixed beforehand but picked by the first pass adaptively (depending on
x,a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{T}
as well). Hence this is really an issue caused by adaptivity.

We will then interpret the proof as exploiting the fact that the first pass (a memory-bounded one-pass algorithm) has a limited ability to adaptively choose the worst
v_{1}
. This perspective, which seems missing from the previous works, is crucial for us to generalize this lemma to multiple passes. A more detailed overview will be given in
Section

6
.

2.5
Extending to Multiple Passes

To extend this to multiple passes, we first adapt our modification on the program and stop rules beyond two passes. This turns out to be simple, and the details will be presented in
Section

8
. Our new counter and its potential analysis extend to multi-pass smoothly as well. This will be included in
Section

10.1
.

Main Challenge: Transfer Lemma.

However, for the transfer lemma, the original proof crucially relies on the fact that the algorithm has only two passes. Subtle technical difficulties arise when generalizing it to multiple passes. We will give detailed intuitions and discussions in
Section

9
.

Current Bottleneck: Transfer Lemma.

Currently, our lower bound stops at
O(\log\log n)
many passes. This is because of the transfer lemma. To prove a lower bound for
q
-passes, we will use our result for
(q-1)
-passes in a black-box way. This implicitly applies the transfer lemma for
q-1
times. It turns out that each application of the transfer lemma is quite costly: In the informal statement in
Section

2.4
, the original
2^{-cn}
bound on stopping probability is demoted to a
2^{-(c-\epsilon)n}
bound after applying transfer lemma. Roughly speaking, if
\epsilon>0
is a constant, we can at most apply this lemma a constant number of times. Then we can only prove a lower bound for a fixed constant number of passes.

By carefully choosing parameters, we can prove that for any algorithm with
q
passes, to succeed with constant probability, it necessarily requires either
\Omega\!\left(\frac{n^{2}}{c_{q}}\right)
memory or
2^{\Omega\left(n/c_{q}\right)}
samples where
c_{q}=100^{3^{q}}
. In particular, this implies a
n^{2-o(1)}
memory lower bound for
o(\log\log n)
-pass learning.
The bottleneck in improving these results beyond
O(\log\log n)
passes is the successive application of the transfer lemma. It remains an interesting open problem whether a better tradeoff such as
c_{q}=\exp(q)
or even
c_{q}={\mathsf{poly}}(q)
can be proved. For example: can a
n^{o(1)}
-pass algorithm learn the hidden vector
x
using
n^{2-\Omega(1)}
memory and
2^{n^{1-\Omega(1)}}
samples?

3.1
Notation

Let
(x,y,z)
be a joint random variable. We use
x\perp y
to denote that
x
and
y
are
independent
.
x\perp y\mid z=z^{\prime}
denotes that
x
and
y
are independent,
conditioned
on
z=z^{\prime}
.

\mathbb{R}^{+}
denotes the set of non-negative real numbers. All the logarithms will have base
2
.

For a list of items
a=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{T})
, we use
a_{\leq i}
(resp.
a_{>i}
) to define the length-
i
prefix (resp. length-
(n-i)
suffix) of
a
.

3.2
Norms and Inner Products

In this paper, we will deal with functions of the form
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
. Given
p\geq 1
, define the
\ell_{p}
-norm of
f
as

\|f\|_{p}=\left(\mathop{\mathbf{E}}_{x\sim X}[f(x)^{p}]\right)^{1/p}.

Let
f,g
be two functions. We define
\langle f,g\rangle:=\mathop{\mathbf{E}}_{x\sim X}[f(x)\cdot g(x)]
as the inner product between
f
and
g
. We collect some basic facts about norms in the following.

•

Monotonicity:
\|f\|_{p}\leq\|f\|_{q}
for every
p\leq q
.

•

Cauchy-Schwarz inequality:
\langle f,g\rangle\leq\|f\|_{2}\cdot\|g\|_{2}

•

(Special case of) Hölder’s inequality:
\|fg\|_{p}\leq\|f\|_{p}\cdot\|g\|_{\infty}
.

•

Truncation trick: Suppose
\|f\|_{2}\leq A
. Let
f^{>B}(x)=\mathbbm{1}_{f(x)>B}\cdot f(x)
. Then

\|f^{>B}\|_{1}:=\mathop{\mathbf{E}}_{x\sim X}[f(x)\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{f(x)>B}]\leq\mathop{\mathbf{E}}_{x\sim X}\left[\frac{f(x)^{2}}{B}\right]\leq\frac{A^{2}}{B}.

3.3
Learning Tasks and
L2
-Extractors

Following
[
GRT18
]
, we use a matrix
M\in\{-1,1\}^{A\times X}
to describe a learning task. Here,
X
is the domain of concepts, and
A
is the domain of possible inputs.
M
corresponds to the following learning problem: An unknown element
x\in X
was chosen uniformly at random. The learner tries to learn
x
from a stream of samples,
(a_{1},b_{1}),(a_{2},b_{2}),\dots
, where for each
i
,
a_{i}\in A
is uniformly at random chosen and
b_{i}=M(a_{i},x)
. In particular, we consider the setting where the learner is allowed to see the same stream of samples for
q\geq 2
passes.

Definition 1
.

We say a learning matrix
M:A\times X\to\{-1,1\}
a
({k_{\mathrm{ext}}},{\ell_{\mathrm{ext}}},{r_{\mathrm{ext}}})
-
L2
-Extractor, if for every non-negative
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
with
\frac{\|f\|_{2}}{\|f\|_{1}}\leq 2^{{\ell_{\mathrm{ext}}}}
, there are at most
2^{-{k_{\mathrm{ext}}}}\cdot|A|
rows
a\in A
with

|\langle M_{a},f\rangle|\geq 2^{-{r_{\mathrm{ext}}}}\cdot\|f\|_{1},

where
M_{a}\in\{-1,1\}^{X}
denotes the restriction of
M
to the
a
-th row.

Below, we collect a few examples of learning tasks from
[
GRT18
]
, whose matrices are good
L2
-extractors. For a more comprehensive list, see
[
GRT18
]
.

•

Parities.
In parity learning, one identifies
X=\{0,1\}^{n}
and
A=\{0,1\}^{n}
. Then,
M(a,x)=(-1)^{\langle a,x\rangle}
for every
a,x
. It can be shown that
M
is an
L2
-extractor with

{k_{\mathrm{ext}}}=\Omega(n),\quad{\ell_{\mathrm{ext}}}=\Omega(n),\quad{r_{\mathrm{ext}}}=\Omega(n).

•

Sparse Parities.
In sparse parity learning,
the hidden vector
x
is known to have Hamming weight exactly
\ell
.
We still identify
A=\{0,1\}^{n}
. But now
X=\{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}\mid\sum_{i}x_{i}=\ell\}
. The learning matrix
M(a,x)=(-1)^{\langle a,x\rangle}
is an
L2
-extractor with

{k_{\mathrm{ext}}}=\Omega(n),\quad{\ell_{\mathrm{ext}}},{r_{\mathrm{ext}}}=\Omega(\ell)\qquad\text{Assuming $\ell\leq n/2$.}

{k_{\mathrm{ext}}}=\Omega(n/\ell^{0.1}),\quad{\ell_{\mathrm{ext}}},{r_{\mathrm{ext}}}=\Omega(\ell\log(\ell))\qquad\text{Assuming $\ell\leq n^{0.9}$.}

Indeed, the problem requires either superlinear samples or superpolynomial memory for any
\ell=\omega(\log(n)/\log\log(n))

[
KRT17
]
.

•

Random matrix.
Consider a random learning matrix
M\in\{\pm 1\}^{A\times X}
with
|A|=|X|=2^{n}
, where every entry of
M
is chosen from
\{-1,1\}
uniformly at random. With high probability, the resulting matrix
M
is an
L2
-extractor with

{k_{\mathrm{ext}}}=\Omega(n),\quad{\ell_{\mathrm{ext}}}=\Omega(n),\quad{r_{\mathrm{ext}}}=\Omega(n).

That means, a “random” learning task will be subject to our lower bound with high probability.

3.4
Computational Model

In this paper, we will model a
q
-pass learning algorithm as a
q
-pass ordered branching program, which can see exactly the same input stream
q
times in the same order.

Definition 2

(
q
-pass branching program for learning)
.

A
q
-pass (ordered) branching program for learning, with length
T
and width
d
, is a directed (muti) graph with vertices arranged in
nT+1
layers, and each layer contains at most
d
vertices. In the first layer which we think of as layer
0
, we fix the vertex
0
to be the starting vertex, denoted by
v_{0}
. The
j
-th pass vertices are those vertices from layer
(j-1)T
to layer
jT+1
. We will denote these
j
-th pass layers using vertex sets
V^{(j)}_{0},V^{(j)}_{1},\dots,V^{(j)}_{T}
. Note for all
j>1
,
V^{(j)}_{0}=V^{(j-1)}_{T}
.

Every vertex except the last layer has
2^{n+1}
many outgoing edges. Each edge is labeled by a unique pair
(a,b)\in A\times\{-1,1\}
. Every last-layer vertex is labeled by one element
\widetilde{x}(v)\in X
, which is the output of the program when reaching that vertex.

Definition 3

(Computational Path)
.

Initially, the current vertex is the start vertex ,
v\leftarrow v_{0}
. Given the input
(a_{1},b_{1}),(a_{2},b_{2}),\dots,(a_{T},b_{T})\in A\times\{-1,1\}
, in the
i
-th step (
i=1,2,\dots,T
),
the program reads
(a_{i},b_{i})
, move from its current vertex
v
in
V^{(j)}_{i-1}
along the edge with that label, and arrive at a vertex in
V^{(j)}_{i}
. Once
v\in V_{T}^{(q)}
, the program will stop and output
\widetilde{x}(v)
. This defines a computational path.

Definition 4

(Success Probability)
.

The success probability of a program is defined as

\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{T}\sim A,\ x\sim X\\
\forall i\in[T]\ b_{i}=M(a_{i},x)\end{subarray}}\left[\text{The output of the program matches $x$}\right]

where
x
and
a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{T}
are sampled uniformly at random.

Setup for Two Passes

4.1
Modifying the Program

To ease our analysis, we would like to have the following modifications to the program. The modification will be a two-stage process. We will use
B_{0}
to denote the original program, use
B_{1}
to denote the program after the first stage, and use
B_{2}
to denote the final program.

Stage 1: Remember the First Pass.

We keep the first pass of the program unchanged. For the second pass of the program, we will now force it to remember the last state of the first pass, which we denote as
v_{1}\in V^{(1)}_{T}
. (This is also the starting vertex of the second pass because
V^{(1)}_{T}=V^{(2)}_{0}
.) Besides this, the second pass program also runs a copy of the first pass in its memory. Namely, we modify the second pass so that now each vertex in layer
i
is a triple
(v^{\prime},v_{1},v_{B_{0}})\in V^{(1)}_{i}\times V^{(1)}_{T}\times V^{(2)}_{i}
, which means:

•

The program remembers that the first pass leads to the vertex
v_{1}
.

•

When traversing for the first pass, it visited vertex
v^{\prime}\in V^{(1)}_{i}
in the
i
-th layer.

•

The (original) second pass program visits
v_{B_{0}}
in the
i
-th layer.

Having this definition in mind, it is easy to modify the topology of the program. Namely, if after reading the sample
(a_{i+1},b_{i+1})
,
v_{B_{0}}
reaches
w_{B_{0}}
and
v^{\prime}
reaches
w^{\prime}
, we add an edge from
(v^{\prime},v_{1},v_{B_{0}})
to
(w^{\prime},v_{1},w_{B_{0}})
with label
(a_{i+1},b_{i+1})
.

Note such modification only blows up the memory by a constant factor (from
\log d
to
3\log d
if the branching program has width
d
). We can without loss of generality only work with modified programs. In the following, we will use
v_{B_{1}}
to denote a (modified) vertex in the second pass. That is,
v_{B_{1}}=(v^{\prime},v_{1},v_{B_{0}})
.
Note that
v_{B_{1}}
uniquely determines both
v^{\prime}
and
v_{1}
. We use
B_{1}
to denote the program after this modification.

Stage 2: Biasness and High-probability edge Counters.

In this stage, we also keep the first pass of the program unchanged. For the second pass, we will further attach two “counters” to each state, denoted by
\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}}
and
\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{high}}
. Initially, they are both zero. When we traverse with an edge
(a,b)
from vertex
v_{B_{1}}
, we will increase these two counters by some amount that is uniquely determined by
v_{B_{1}}
and
(a,b)
. The update rules will be specified later in
Section

4.3
.

Similar to how we added
v^{\prime}
and
v_{1}
to the memory of the second pass in Stage 1, we will also add
\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}}
and
\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{high}}
to the memory. Namely, for each vertex
v_{B_{1}}
, we will create many duplicates of it and label them by
(v_{B_{1}},\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{high}},\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}})
. In this way, a vertex in the second pass remembers not only
v_{B_{1}}=(v^{\prime},v_{1},v_{B_{0}})
but also
\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{high}}
and
\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}}
. We will apply the modification layer-by-layer as we describe in the next.

Now we will describe how we modify the edges. In layer-
0
of the second pass, the counters are initialized to
0
. In general, suppose we have modified the first
i-1
layers. Every vertex in layer
i
should be of the form
(v_{B_{1}},\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{high}},\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}})
. Fix one such vertex
v_{B_{1}}
. For each edge labeled
(a,b)
in
B_{1}
that goes from
v_{B_{1}}
to
w_{B_{1}}
, we calculate the increased value of the counters,
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
, based on the available information:
v_{B_{1}},\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{high}},\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}}
and
(a,b)
. Then we add an edge from
(v_{B_{1}},\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{high}},\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}})
to
(w_{B_{1}},c_{1},c_{2})
with label
(a,b)
.
We apply the modification for each edge between the
i
-th layer and the
(i+1)
-th layer in
B_{1}
.

We will restrict the range of the counters to be integers in
[0,\log|X|]
. Hence, they require only
O(\log\log|X|)
bits to store.
Since the desired output
x\in X
already requires at least
\log|X|
memory to store, we can without loss of generality ignore the memory blowup of this modification. Hence we will only work with the modified program
B_{2}
. In the paper, we simply use
v
to denote a vertex in the program
B_{2}
. Note that in the second pass,
v
uniquely determines
v^{\prime},v_{1},v_{B_{0}},\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}},\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{high}}
. As the counters
\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}}
and
\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{high}}
are now uniquely determined by vertex
v
, we will denote them by
\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}}(v)
and
\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{high}}(v)
.

Finally, note that our modification does not change the functionality of
B
. Therefore, if
B_{2}
succeeds in learning with very low probability, so does
B
.

Comparison with
[
GRT19
]
.

The idea of “running a copy of the previous pass” is inherited from their work. However, our implementation is slightly simpler. For one, we observed that we don’t need to remember the set of indices on which the second pass traverses a high-probability edge. Only knowing the number of high-probability edges one has traversed so far, i.e.
\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{high}}
, is sufficient.

The introduction of the new counter
\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}}
is novel in our work. Although
\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}}
and
\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{high}}
require us to modify the program in the same way, these two counters are for completely different purposes. This new counter
\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}}
is crucial for achieving the tight quadratic lower bound on the memory of any efficient parity learner (and more generally the tight lower bound on the memory for any learning problem whose corresponding matrix is an
L_{2}
-Extractor).

Comparison with
[
LRZ23
]
.

This recent work by Liu, Raz and Zhan introduced a notion of “badness level”, which is a technical alternative to the previous potential function in
[
Raz17
,
GRT18
]
, namely, the function
\langle\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v},\mathbb{P}_{x\mid s}\rangle^{k}
. Although such “badness level” has also a counter-like definition,
\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}}
and “badness level” are counting completely different things. In our work, the potential function is nice enough to work with and gives shorter proofs. So we will use the original potential function of
[
Raz17
,
GRT18
]
.

4.2
The First Pass

In this section, we will set up the notation and stopping rules for the first pass.

Computational Path.

The computational path starting from
v_{0}
is uniquely determined by
x\in X,a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{T}\in A
. We use
v_{0}\ \widetilde{\to}\ \widetilde{v}
to denote the event that the (untruncated) computational path reaches
\widetilde{v}
(on
x,a_{1},\dots,a_{T}
).

For a vertex
v^{\prime}\in V^{(1)}_{i}
, we can consider the subprogram which consists of only layers
i,i+1,\dots,T
. In this subprogram, we set
v^{\prime}
as the starting vertex. The computational path starting from
v^{\prime}
is uniquely determined by
x\in X,a_{i+1},a_{i+2},\dots,a_{T}
. We use
v^{\prime}\ \widetilde{\to}\ \widetilde{v}
to denote the event that the (untruncated) computational path from
v^{\prime}
reaches
\widetilde{v}
(on
x,a_{i+1},a_{i+2},\dots,a_{T}
).

To define the stopping rules, we need to first define truncated paths.

Truncated Path from
v_{0}
(Informal).

For any
x\in X,a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{T}\in A
, we have a uniquely determined computational path. The truncated path will be a prefix of it. The stopping rules specify when the truncated path stops. We will let
v_{0}\to\widetilde{v}
denote the event that from
v_{0}
, we reached
\widetilde{v}
without stopping. Even if we arrived at
\widetilde{v}
and then stopped, this still counts as
v_{0}\to\widetilde{v}
since we have reached
\widetilde{v}
.

We will give the formal definition of the truncated path after we have introduced the stopping rules. For any vertex
v^{\prime}\in V^{(1)}_{i}
, we will define the truncated path starting from
v^{\prime}
. But this definition is much more subtle. We will defer it to after the formal definition.

Bad Events and Stopping Rules.

In the definition of stopping rules, we will need
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{0}\to v^{\prime}}
, defined as the distribution of
x
conditioning on events
v_{0}\to v^{\prime}
. (The prior distribution of
x,a_{1},\dots,a_{T}
is uniform.) Although
v_{0}\to v^{\prime}
is defined using the stopping rules, this is not a circular definition because we will define stopping rules and truncated paths layer by layer: For
v^{\prime}\in V^{(1)}_{i}
, the event
v_{0}\to v^{\prime}
will only depend on the stopping rules of layers
1,2,\dots,i-1
. Also the stopping rules of layer
i
will only depend on events
v_{0}\to v^{\prime}
for
v^{\prime}\in V^{(1)}_{i}
.

Suppose at layer
i
, the truncated path currently reaches vertex
v^{\prime}\in V^{(1)}_{i}
. Recall that
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{0}\to v^{\prime}}
is the distribution of
x
conditioning on event
v_{0}\to v^{\prime}
. Based on it, we define the following bad events. They will appear in the layer
i
stopping rules.

•

Bad edges.

The set of bad edges at state
v^{\prime}
is defined as

\mathrm{Bad}(v^{\prime})=\left\{(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\in A\times\{-1,1\}:\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x^{\prime}\sim\mathbb{P}_{x|v_{0}\to v^{\prime}}}[M(a^{\prime},x^{\prime})=1]\not\in\left(\frac{1}{2}-2^{-{r_{\mathrm{ext}}}},\frac{1}{2}+2^{-{r_{\mathrm{ext}}}}\right)\right\}.

Note that an edge
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
being bad or not only depends on
a^{\prime}
. For brevity, we sometimes abuse notation by using
\mathrm{Bad}(v^{\prime})
to refer to a subset of
A
, which consists of all “bad”
a^{\prime}\in A
.

•

Significant States.
If
v^{\prime}
is a state that satisfies
\|\mathbb{P}_{x|v_{0}\to v^{\prime}}\|_{2}\geq 2^{-n}\cdot 2^{\ell_{\mathrm{sigs}}^{(1)}}
, we will call
v^{\prime}
a significant state.

•

Significant Values.
Define the set of significant values at
v^{\prime}
as

\mathrm{SigV}(v^{\prime})=\{x^{\prime}:\mathbb{P}_{x|v_{0}\to v^{\prime}}(x^{\prime})\geq 2^{-n+{\ell_{\mathrm{sigv}}}}\}.

In the first pass, we will immediately stop whenever we encounter these bad events. Formally, these are the stopping rules that are applied
before
traversing from
V^{(1)}_{i}
to
V^{(1)}_{i+1}
:

1.

If we are currently at
v^{\prime}\in V^{(1)}_{i}
, which is itself a significant state, then we stop.

2.

If
v^{\prime}
is not significant, but the next edge
(a,b)
satisfies that
a\in\mathrm{Bad}(v^{\prime})
, then we stop.

3.

If
v^{\prime}
is not significant but
x\in\mathrm{SigV}(v^{\prime})
, then we stop.

If none of these rules apply, we take a step forward from
v^{\prime}
to some
\widetilde{v}\in V^{(1)}_{i+1}
following the next edge. Then we say the truncated path reaches
\widetilde{v}
.

Truncated Path from
v_{0}
(Formal).

Initially for layer
0
,
v_{0}\to v_{0}
is always true. Suppose we have finished the definition for layers
0,1,\dots,i
. Then, for each
\widetilde{v}\in V^{(1)}_{i+1}
, we can define
v_{0}\to\widetilde{v}
recursively.

v_{0}\to\widetilde{v}\equiv\bigvee_{\widetilde{u}\in V^{(1)}_{i}}(v_{0}\to\widetilde{u})\land\left[\begin{aligned} &\text{From $\widetilde{u}$, we traverse an edge and reach $\widetilde{v}$}\\
&\text{without triggering any stopping rules at $\widetilde{u}$}\end{aligned}\right].

Truncated Path from
v^{\prime}
.

For a vertex
v^{\prime}\in V_{i}^{(1)}
, we now define the truncated path starting from
v^{\prime}
. First, from
x,a_{i+1},a_{i+2},\dots,a_{T}
, we can uniquely determine the computational path from
v^{\prime}
. Then
v^{\prime}\to\widetilde{v}
is defined as the event that the computational path reaches
\widetilde{v}
without triggering the stopping rules.

The subtlety here is that the conditional distributions
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{0}\to\widetilde{v}}
in the stopping rules are still defined using truncated paths from
v_{0}
instead of that from
v^{\prime}
. Formally, for each
\widetilde{v}\in V_{i+1}
,

v^{\prime}\to\widetilde{v}\equiv\bigvee_{\widetilde{u}\in V^{(1)}_{i}}(v^{\prime}\rightarrow\widetilde{u})\land\left[\begin{aligned} &\text{From $\widetilde{u}$, we traverse an edge and reach $\widetilde{v}$}\\
&\text{without triggering any stopping rules at $\widetilde{u}$ where}\\
&\text{the distributions $\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{0}\to\widetilde{u}}$ in the stopping rules}\\
&\text{are still defined w.r.t. $v_{0}\to\widetilde{u}$.}\end{aligned}\right]

We will list several facts to fully explain the subtlety.
They are heavily used in both the previous work
[
GRT19
]
and ours:

•

For any vertex
v_{1}
in the first pass, the event
v_{0}\to v_{1}
is implied by
(v_{0}\to v^{\prime})\land(v^{\prime}\to v_{1})
.

This fact emphasizes that when we define
v^{\prime}\to v_{1}
, we are still defining the stopping rules w.r.t. the truncated paths from
v_{0}
, not
v_{1}
. Otherwise, this fact would not hold.

•

The probability
\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[v^{\prime}\to\widetilde{v}]
can be interpreted as follows: First, we sample
x\in X
and
a_{i+1},a_{i+2},\dots,a_{T}

uniformly at random
. Then,
\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[v^{\prime}\to\widetilde{v}]
is the probability the truncated path from
v^{\prime}
defined by
x,a_{i+1},a_{i+2},\dots,a_{T}
reaches
\widetilde{v}
. We emphasize its comparison to the next item.

•

The probability
\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[v^{\prime}\to\widetilde{v}\mid v_{0}\to v^{\prime}]
can be interpreted as follows: First we sample
x\in X

according to
\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{0}\to v^{\prime}}
. (Note the event
v_{0}\to v^{\prime}
only depends on
x,a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{i}
.) Then we sample
a_{i+1},a_{i+2},\dots,a_{T}

uniformly at random
, because
v_{0}\to v^{\prime}
is independent of them.

Then,
\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[v^{\prime}\to\widetilde{v}\mid v_{0}\to v^{\prime}]
is the probability that the truncated path from
v^{\prime}
determined by these
x,a_{i+1},a_{i+2},\dots,a_{T}
reaches
\widetilde{v}
.

The first pass stops with small probability.

The following theorem is proved in
[
GRT18
]
.

Theorem 4

(Main Theorem of
[
GRT18
]
)
.

Suppose
M
is a
({k_{\mathrm{ext}}},{\ell_{\mathrm{ext}}},{r_{\mathrm{ext}}})
-
L_{2}
-extractor. Let
\ell,r
be two parameters satisfying:

•

4\ell\leq\min\{{k_{\mathrm{ext}}},{\ell_{\mathrm{ext}}}\}-4
,

•

r\leq\frac{1}{3}\min\{{r_{\mathrm{ext}}},{k_{\mathrm{ext}}},\ell\}
.

Let
B
be a one-pass branching program for the learning task of
M
. Suppose
B
has:

•

Width:
2^{\frac{1}{8}{k_{\mathrm{ext}}}\ell}
.

•

Length:
T=2^{r}
.

Then, over uniformly random
x\sim X
,
(a_{1},\dots,a_{T})\sim A^{T}
, the probability that
B
stops is at most
2^{-2r}
. Put in other words, there exists an event
G\subseteq X\times A^{T}
such that

\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x,a_{1},\dots,a_{T}}[(x,a_{1},\dots,a_{T})\not\in G]\leq 2^{-\ell}.

Furthermore, denote by
v_{0}
the starting vertex of
B
. For any final vertex
v
of
B
, it holds that

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{P}_{x|(v_{0}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v)\land G}\|_{2}\leq 2^{\ell+1}2^{-n},\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{P}_{x|(v_{0}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v)\land G}\|_{\infty}\leq 2^{3\ell+4}2^{-n}.

4.3
The Second Pass

Now we will set up the second pass. Below we will fix a vertex
v_{1}\in V^{(2)}_{0}
as the starting vertex. Let
\ell
be a “meta” parameter. Most of the relevant parameters in our proof are stated as multiples of
\ell
for brevity.

Computational Path from
v_{1}
.

For the subprogram containing layers
V^{(2)}_{0},V^{(2)}_{1},\dots,V^{(2)}_{T}
, we set the vertex
v_{1}\in V^{(2)}_{0}
as the starting vertex. For any
x\in X,a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{T}\in A
, the (untruncated) computational path from
v_{1}
is defined as the computational path in that subprogram. Recall that we use
v_{1}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v
to denote the event that the computational path from
v_{1}
reaches
v
.

Truncated Path from
v_{1}
.

We use
v_{1}\to v
to denote the event that from
v_{1}
, the computational path reached
v
without stopping. Here we are only considering the second pass subprogram.

To avoid circular definitions, we will define truncated paths and stopping rules layer by layer. In layer
i
, as the stopping rules for all previous layers are well-defined, the event
v_{1}\to v
will be well-defined for all
v\in V^{(2)}_{i}
. Then we can define the layer
i
bad events and stopping rules.

Since truncated paths with an arbitrary starting vertex in the second pass are not used in our two-pass result, we will not define them here.

Truncated Path from
v_{0}
.

For a vertex
v
in the second pass, we use
v_{0}\to v
to denote the event that (1) the computational path starting from
v_{0}
reaches
v
and (2) before reaching
v
it did not trigger any first pass or second pass stopping rule. Note
v_{0}\to v
is equivalent to
v_{0}\to v_{1}\land v_{1}\to v
.

Bad Events

Let
v\in V^{(2)}_{i}
be a vertex in layer
i
of the second pass. We will define the bad events of layer-
i
. Unlike the first pass, we do
not
always stop immediately when we encounter them.

•

High-probability edges.
An input

Proof Strategy

Having defined the probability space, we will show that:

1.

For any fixed

\mathop{\mathbf{E}}_{v\mid v_{0}\to v^{\prime}}[\Phi(v)]

2.

By our stopping rule,

is always at most
\ell

Together these show that

\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}}(v)>\ell_{\mathrm{bias}}^{(2)}\mid v_{0}\to v^{\prime}]\leq\frac{\mathop{\mathbf{E}}_{v\mid v_{0}\to v^{\prime}}[2^{\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}}(v)}]}{2^{\ell_{\mathrm{bias}}^{(2)}}}\leq\frac{\mathop{\mathbf{E}}_{v\mid v_{0}\to v^{\prime}}[\Phi(v)]\cdot 2^{\ell}}{2^{\ell_{\mathrm{bias}}^{(2)}}}\leq 2^{\ell-\ell_{\mathrm{bias}}^{(2)}}.

Intuition.

We give some intuition for this potential function: After traversing an edge with input

, the counter

increases when some small-probability bad event happens. More specifically, when traversing an edge

with label

where

In other words, with probability

. With probability

2^{-\Delta}\cdot 2^{\lfloor{\Delta\rfloor}}+(1-2^{-\Delta})\leq 2.

So the expectation of

at most doubles after traversing a high-probability edge. Thus, by introducing an extra

term to account for the high-probability edges traversed so far, we can show the expectation of
\Phi(v)=2^{\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}}(v)-\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{high}}(v)}
is almost

3

Evolution of Potential

Let us first analyze how the potential function changes after traversing an edge. Below we will always fix a vertex
v_{1}\in V_{0}^{(2)}
and a layer
i\in[T]
. For the second second-pass-only truncated path
\mathcal{T}^{(2)}_{v_{1}}
and any vertex
u\in V^{(2)}_{i-1}
, we will let
(v_{1}\to u)
denote the event
\mathcal{T}^{(2)}_{v_{1}}(i-1)=u

Lemma 1
.

Fixing
v_{1}\in V_{0}^{(2)}
, for any vertex
u
in the second pass and an edge labeled
(a,b)

\Phi^{a,b}(u)\leq\Phi(u)\cdot\frac{1+2\cdot 2^{-{r_{\mathrm{ext}}}}}{2\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\mid v_{1}\to u}[M(a,x)=b]}.

Proof.

We need to discuss the four cases: Let
v
be the vertex we reach after traversing this edge.

•

We stopped on
u
due to stopping rules. Then
\Phi^{a,b}(u)=\Phi(\mathbf{halt})=0
since we force
u
to traverse to
\mathbf{halt}
after reading
(a,b)
.

•

a\not\in\mathrm{Bad}(u)
: In this case, both
\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}}
and
\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{high}}
are unchanged. We know
\Phi^{a,b}(u)=\Phi(u)
. By the definition of bad edges, we know that

\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\mid v_{1}\to u}[M(a,x)=b]\in\left(\frac{1}{2}-2^{-{r_{\mathrm{ext}}}},\frac{1}{2}+2^{-{r_{\mathrm{ext}}}}\right).

As a result

\frac{\Phi^{a,b}(u)}{\Phi(u)}=1\leq\frac{1+2\cdot 2^{-{r_{\mathrm{ext}}}}}{2\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\mid v_{1}\to u}[M(a,x)=b]}.

•

a\in\mathrm{Bad}(u)\setminus\mathrm{High}(u)
: By our stopping rule, we will stop on this edge. Hence
\Phi^{a,b}(u)=\Phi(\mathbf{halt})=0
. The inequality trivially holds.

•

a\in\mathrm{High}(u)
: In this case, the counter
\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{high}}(v)=\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{high}}(u)+1
and
\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}}(v)=\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathrm{bias}}(u)+\Delta
with
\Delta=\left\lfloor-\log\left(\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\mid v_{1}\to v}[M(a,x)=b]\right)\right\rfloor
. Hence,

\frac{\Phi^{a,b}(u)}{\Phi(u)}=2^{\Delta-1}\leq\frac{1}{2\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\mid v_{1}\to u}[M(a,x)=b]}.

Having verified all possible cases, we conclude the validity of the lemma.
∎

As mentioned in

6.2.1

First, we can assume

This definition ensures that

Claim 1

For all

Proof.

As

\mathbb{P}_{x\mid v_{0}\to v^{\prime}}(x^{\prime})\leq 2^{\ell_{\mathrm{flat}}^{(1)}}\cdot 2^{-n}

7.1.1

Applying

7.1.2
Too Many High-Probability Edges

For each vertex

at the end of the first pass, let

be the event the program starts from

and traverses more than

We observe that

\displaystyle\sum_{v_{1}}\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[v_{0}\to v_{1}]\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[E_{v_{1}}|v_{0}\to v_{1}]\leq\sum_{v_{1}}\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[E_{v_{1}}].

Fix one vertex

. It suffices to show that

many

We first observe a simple fact.

Fact 1
.

For any vertex

in the second pass, it holds that

.

This follows because, under some distribution over the next sample

(namely, the distribution of

conditioned on

), each high-probability edge occurs with probability at least
2^{-n+\frac{{k_{\mathrm{ext}}}}{2}}
. As such, there can be at most

such edges.

Now we are ready to upper bound
\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[E_{v_{1}}]
. The key point is that when we analyze

without conditioning anything about

, we have that
all of

by Fact

, over uniformly random samples, the next edge

belongs to
\mathrm{High}(v)
with probability at most

. We can union-bound over the
T

Finally, summing up
\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[E_{v_{1}}]
over

completes the proof.

Comparison with
[
GRT19
]
.

We note that our proof for high-probability edge overflow is significantly simpler than the one presented in
[
GRT19
]
, which required the use of information theory and a quite delicate calculation. Furthermore, our proof can upper bound the stopping probability by an exponentially small quantity, whereas
[
GRT19
]
can only get a constant (e.g.,

) upper bound. Having an exponentially small stopping probability is crucial for extending our result to the multi-pass case.

7.1.3

By the extractor property, for each non-significant

in the second pass, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\text{stop due to bad edge in the second pass}]

7.1.6
Wrap-up: the Success Probability is Small

The previous sections show that the probability of stopping is small.

Denote by

the union of all stopping events. Let
G
be the complement of

. We have shown that

\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x,a_{1},\dots,a_{T}}[(x,a_{1},\dots,a_{T})\in\overline{G}]\leq 2^{-\frac{2}{3}\ell+O(1)}.

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{P}_{x|(v_{0}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v)\land G}\|_{2}\leq 2^{\ell_{\mathrm{sigs}}^{(2)}+1}\cdot 2^{-n},\quad\text{and,}

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{P}_{x|(v_{0}\ \widetilde{\to}\ v)\land G}\|_{\infty}\leq 2^{{\ell_{\mathrm{sigv}}}+1}\cdot 2^{-n}.

Therefore, conditioning on

, we conclude the two-pass learning algorithm succeeds in learning

with an exponentially small probability. This proves

.
