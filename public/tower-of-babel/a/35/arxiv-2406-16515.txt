Abstract
Non-deterministic read-once branching programs, also known as non-deterministic free binary decision diagrams (nFBDD), are a fundamental data structure in computer science for representing Boolean functions. In this paper, we focus on #nFBDD, the problem of model counting for non-deterministic read-once branching programs. The #nFBDD problem is #P-hard, and it is known that there exists a quasi-polynomial randomized approximation scheme for #nFBDD. In this paper, we provide the first FPRAS for #nFBDD. Our result relies on the introduction of new analysis techniques that focus on bounding the dependence of samples.

Introduction

Read-once branching programs or binary decision diagrams are fundamental data structures in computer science used to represent Boolean functions. Their variants have been discovered multiple times across various sub-fields of computer science, and consequently, they are referred to by many acronyms
[
Jr.78
,
Bry86
,
Weg00
]
. In this paper, we focus on non-deterministic read-once branching programs, also known as non-deterministic free binary decision diagrams (nFBDD).

We study the following computational problem:

#nFBDD
: Given a non-deterministic read-once branching program
B
over a Boolean set of variables
X
, compute the number of models of
B
, i.e., the number of assignments over
X
that
B
maps to
1
.

From a database perspective, #nFBDD is an important problem owing to the recent connections between query evaluation and knowledge compilation
[
JS12
,
JS13
,
MO18
,
Mon20
,
AC24a
,
AAC+24
]
. The field of knowledge compilation has its origins in the artificial intelligence community, where functions represented in input languages are compiled into target languages that can support queries tractably (often viewed as polynomial time)
[
DM02
]
. The typical queries of interest are satisfiability, entailment, enumeration, and counting.

The target languages in the context of databases have been variants of binary decision diagrams, also referred to as branching programs, and circuits in decomposable negation normal form (DNNF)
[
AC24a
,
AAC+24
]
. A binary decision diagram is a representation of a Boolean function as a directed acyclic graphs where the nodes correspond to variables and the sinks correspond to values, i.e.,
0
or
1
. One of the most well-studied forms is the ordered binary decision diagram (OBDD), where the nodes correspond to variables and, along every path from root to leaf, the variables appear in the same order
[
Bry86
]
. A generalization of OBDD is nOBDD, where internal nodes can also represent disjunction (
\vee
) gates.

nFBDD are a natural generalization of nOBDDs, as they do not impose restrictions on the ordering of Boolean variables. Since nFBDD do not impose such restrictions, they are known to be exponentially more succinct than nOBDD; that is, there exist functions for which the smallest nOBDD is exponentially larger than the smallest nFBDD
[
ACMS20
]
. From this viewpoint, nFBDDs occupy a space between nOBDD and DNNF circuits, as they are exponentially more succinct than nOBDDs, while DNNFs are quasi-polynomially more succinct than nFBDD
[
BL15
,
ACMS20
]
.

In the context of databases, the connection between knowledge compilation and query evaluation has been fruitful, leading to the discovery of both tractable algorithms and lower bounds. Of particular note is the application of the knowledge compilation paradigm in query evaluation on probabilistic databases
[
JS13
]
, Shapley value computation
[
DFKM22
]
, the enumeration of query answers, probabilistic graph homomorphism
[
AvBM24
]
, counting answers to queries
[
ACJR21
]
. The knowledge compilation-based approach involves first representing the database task as a query over a Boolean function and then demonstrating that the corresponding Boolean function has a tractable representation in a given target language, which also supports the corresponding query in polynomial time
[
AC24b
]
.
For example, in the context of query evaluation over probabilistic databases, one can show that the problem of probabilistic query evaluation can be represented as a weighted model counting problem over nOBDD when the underlying query is a path query
[
AvBM24
]
. Since there is a fully polynomial-time randomized approximation scheme (FPRAS) for the problem of model counting over nOBDD
[
ACJR19
]
, it follows that probabilistic query evaluation for regular path queries over tuple-independent databases admits an FPRAS
[
AvBM24
]
. In the context of aggregation tasks, the underlying query is often model counting and its variants
[
Men21
]
.

The aforementioned strategy makes it desirable to have target languages that are as expressive as possible while still supporting queries such as counting in polynomial time. In this context, the recent flurry of results has been enabled by the breakthrough of Arenas, Croquevielle, Jayaram, and Riveros, who showed that the problem of #nOBDD admits an FPRAS
[
ACJR19
]
. As mentioned earlier, nOBDD imposes a severe restriction on variable ordering, i.e., along every path from root to leaf, the variable ordering remains the same. nFBDD generalizes nOBDD by alleviating this restriction, thereby enabling succinct representations for several functions that require exponentially large nOBDD. Since nFBDD generalize nOBDD, the #P-hardness of #nFBDD, the problem of model counting over nFBDD, immediately follows. Accordingly, in light of the recent discovery of FPRAS for #nOBDD, an important open question is whether there exists an FPRAS for #nFBDD. The best known prior result provides a quasi-polynomial time algorithm owing to reduction of nFBDD to DNNF (and accordingly
(+,\times)
-programs)
[
GJK+97
]
. As noted in Section
2.1
, the techniques developed in the context of design of FPRAS for #nOBDD do not extend to handle the case for #nFBDD and therefore, design of FPRAS for #nFBDD would require development of new techniques.

The primary contribution of our work is to answer the aforementioned question affirmatively, which is formalized in the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Let
B
be an nFBDD over
n
variables,
\varepsilon>0
and
\delta>0
.
Algorithm
\textup{{approxMCnFBDD}}(B,\varepsilon,\delta)
runs in time
O(n^{11}|B|^{10}\varepsilon^{-4}\log(\delta^{-1}))
and returns
\mathsf{est}
with the guarantee that

\Pr\left[\mathsf{est}\in(1\pm\varepsilon)|B^{-1}(1)|\right]\geq 1-\delta.

2.1
Related Work

As noted in Section
1
, the literature on binary decision diagrams is extensive; therefore, we will focus solely on related results in the context of the model counting problem. The problem of #nFBDD is #P-complete: membership in #P is immediate as every assignment can be evaluated in PTIME, and the #P-hardness follows from the observation that the problem of #DNF,
i.e., counting the number of satisfying assignments of Boolean formulas in Disjunctive Normal Form, is #P-hard
[
Val79
]
. Moreover, every DNF can be represented as an nFBDD such that the size of the resulting nFBDD is polynomial in the size of the DNF. Furthermore, it is also known that the problem of #nOBDD is SpanL-complete
[
ACJR19
]
.

Given the #P-hardness, a natural direction of research is to understand the complexity of approximation. The discovery of polynomial-time randomized approximation schemes for #P-hard problems has been of long-standing interest and has led to several theoretically deep and elegant results. One such result was that of Karp and Luby
[
KL83
]
in the context of #DNF, relying on Monte Carlo sampling. Building on Monte Carlo sampling, Kannan, Sampath, and Mahaney
[
KSM95
]
proposed a quasi-polynomial running time approximation scheme for #nOBDD.
1
1

1

The result of
[
KSM95
]
was stated for regular languages, but the underlying techniques can handle #nOBDD.
In a follow-up work
[
GJK+97
]
, this result was extended to handle context-free grammars by reducing the problem of counting words of a context-free grammar to estimating the support size of multilinear
(+,\times)
-programs. It is straightforward to see that the same reduction can be applied in the context of #DNNF, implying a quasi-polynomial runtime approximation for #nFBDD. Since then, the question of the existence of a fully polynomial-time randomized approximation scheme for #nFBDD and its generalizations has remained open.

In a major breakthrough, Arenas et al.
[
ACJR19
]
provided an FPRAS for #nOBDD. Their technique relied on union of sets estimation à la Karp-Luby and the generation of independent samples via the self-reducibility union property.
2
2

2

The term
self-reducibility union property
was coined by Meel, Chakraborty, and Mathur
[
MCM24
]
to explain the high-level idea of
[
ACJR19
]
.
The self-reducibility union property can be informally stated as follows: The set of models conditioned on a given partial assignment (according to the variable ordering of the given nOBDD) can be expressed as the union of models of the states of the given nOBDD. In a follow-up work
[
ACJR21
]
, Arenas et al. observed that the problem of model counting over structured DNNF (st-DNNF) circuits also admits FPRAS.
In this context, it is worth highlighting that the self-reducibility union property does not hold for nFBDD and there exists exponential separation between nFBDD and st-DNNF, i.e., there is a family of functions for which the smallest FBDD is exponentially smaller than the smallest st-DNNF, and therefore, the problem of whether there exists an FPRAS for #nFBDD remains open.

3
Technical Overview

Our algorithm proceeds in a bottom-up manner and for every node
q
of given nFBDD
B
, we keep: (1) a number
p(q)\in(0,1]
which seeks to approximate
\frac{1}{|\mathsf{mod}(q)|}
, and therefore,
\frac{1}{p(q)}
can be used to estimate
|\mathsf{mod}(q)|
, and (2) sets of samples
\{S^{r}(q)\}_{r=1}^{n_{s}n_{t}}
of
\mathsf{mod}(q)
where
n_{s}
and
n_{t}
are polynomial in
n=|var(B)|
,
\varepsilon^{-1}
and
\log|B|
respectively. Few comments are in order: we keep many (
n_{s}\cdot n_{t}
) independent sets of samples so as to rely on the median of means estimator. As mentioned earlier, our algorithm works in bottom-up manner, in particular, for a node
q
, we will compute
(p(q),\{S^{r}(q)\}_{r})
using the values of
p(\cdot)
and
\{S^{r}(\cdot)\}_{r}
of its children.

Ideally, we want every model of
q
to be in
S^{r}(q)
identically and independently with probability
p(q)
. However, obtaining iid samples is computationally expensive, which resulted in quasi-polynomial runtimes in earlier studies
[
GJK+97
]
. Recent works on FPRAS for nOBDD achieved independence by leveraging self-reducibility union property
[
ACJR19
,
ACJR21
]
, but, as remarked in Section
2.1
, the self-reducibility union property does not hold for nFBDD and therefore, it is not known how to accomplish independence among samples without giving up on the desiderata of polynomial time.

The key insight in our approach is to give up the desire for independence altogether, in particular, we do not even ensure pairwise independence, i.e., even for
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathsf{mod}(q)
, it may not be the case that
\Pr[\alpha\in S^{r}(q)|\alpha^{\prime}\in S^{r}(q)]=\Pr[\alpha\in S^{r}(q)]
. Of course, we do need to quantify the dependence. In order to discuss the dependence, we first discuss how we update
p(q)
and
S^{r}(q)
for decision nodes and
\lor
-nodes.

•

Let
q=ite(x,q_{1},q_{0})
. Then we compute
p(q)
and
S^{r}(q)
as
p(q)=\left(\frac{1}{p(q_{0})}+\frac{1}{p(q_{1})}\right)^{-1}
and
S^{r}(q)=\left(\textup{{reduce}}\left(S^{r}(q_{0}),\frac{p(q)}{p(q_{0})}\right)\otimes\{x\mapsto 0\}\right)\cup\left(\textup{{reduce}}\left(S^{r}(q_{1}),\frac{p(q)}{p(q_{1})}\right)\otimes\{x\mapsto 1\}\right)
, where
\textup{{reduce}}(S,p)
is the operation that keeps each element of a set
S
with probability
p
.

•

Let
q
be a
\lor
-node such that
q=q_{1}\lor q_{2}
(assuming two children for simplicity). Furthermore, for simplicity of exposition and for the sake of high-level intuition, assume
p(q_{1})=p(q_{2})
. The technical difficulty for
\lor
-nodes arises from the fact that it is possible that for a given
\alpha\in\mathsf{mod}(q)
, we have
\alpha\in\mathsf{mod}(q_{1})\cap\mathsf{mod}(q_{2})
. Therefore, in order to ensure no
\alpha
has undue advantage even if it lies in the models set of multiple children, we update
p(q)
and
S^{r}(q)
as follows: we first compute
\rho=\min(p(q_{1}),p(q_{2}))
and
\hat{S}^{r}(q)=S^{r}(q_{1})\cup(S^{r}(q_{2})\setminus\mathsf{mod}(q_{1}))

and then
p(q)=\underset{0\leq j<n_{t}}{\mathsf{median}}\left(\frac{1}{\rho\cdot n_{s}}\sum_{r=j\cdot n_{s}+1}^{(j+1)n_{s}}|\hat{S}^{r}(q)|\right)^{-1}
followed by
S^{r}(q)=\textup{{reduce}}\left(\hat{S}^{r}(q),\frac{p(q)}{\rho}\right)
.

Observe that the usage of
S^{r}(q_{2})\setminus\mathsf{mod}(q_{1})
ensures that for every
\alpha\in\mathsf{mod}(q)
, there is exactly one child of
q^{\prime}\in\mathsf{children}(q)
such that if
\alpha\in\hat{S}^{r}(q)
then
\alpha\in S^{r}(q^{\prime})
, and therefore, no
\alpha
has
undue advantage
.

It is worth re-emphasizing the crucial departure in our work from earlier efforts is embrace of dependence. For instance, consider
q=q_{1}\lor q_{2}
and
\hat{q}=q_{1}\lor q_{3}
, then
S^{r}(q)
and
S^{r}(\hat{q})
will of course be reusing samples
S^{r}(q_{1})
, and therefore, do not even have pairwise independence. Now, of course, we need to bound dependence so as to retain any hope of computing
p(q)
from
S^{r}(q)
. To this end, we focus on the following quantity of interest:
\Pr[\alpha\in S^{r}(q)\mid\alpha^{\prime}\in S^{r}(q)]
for
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathsf{mod}(q)
, which determines the variance for the estimator. In this regard, for every
(\alpha,q)
, we can define a derivation path, denoted as
\mathsf{path}(\alpha,q)
, where for every
\lor
-node,
\mathsf{path}(\alpha,q)
is
\mathsf{path}(\alpha,q^{\prime})
appended with
q
, where
q^{\prime}
is the first child of q such that
\alpha\in\mathsf{mod}(q^{\prime})
. The key observation is that our computations ensure
\Pr[\alpha\in S^{r}(q)\mid\alpha^{\prime}\in S^{r}(q)]
depends on the first node (starting from the
1
-sink)
q^{*}
where the derivation paths
\mathsf{path}(\alpha,q)
and
\mathsf{path}(\alpha,q^{\prime})
diverge. In particular, it turns out:

\Pr[\alpha\in S^{r}(q)|\alpha^{\prime}\in S^{r}(q)]\leq\frac{p(q)}{p(q^{*})}.

One might wonder whether the above expression suffices: it turns out it does, because the number of pairs
(\alpha,\alpha^{\prime})
whose derivation paths diverge for the first time at
q^{*}
can be shown to be bounded by
\frac{|\mathsf{mod}(q)|^{2}}{|\mathsf{mod}(q^{*})|}
, which suffices to show that the variance of the estimator can be bounded by constant factor of square of its mean, thus allowing us to use median of means estimator.

We close off by remarking that the situation is more nuanced than previously described, as
p(q)
is itself a random variable. Although the high-level intuition remains consistent, the technical analysis requires coupling, based on a carefully defined random process, detailed in

Derivation paths

Models can have several accepting paths in an nFBDD. For
q
a node of
B
and
\alpha\in\mathsf{mod}(q)
, we map
(\alpha,q)
to a canonical accepting path, called the
derivation path
of
\alpha
for
q
, denoted by
\mathsf{path}(\alpha,q)
. A path
\mathcal{P}
is formally represented with a tuple
(V(\mathcal{P}),E(\mathcal{P}))
, with
V(\mathcal{P})
a sequence of vertices and
E(\mathcal{P})
a sequence of edges.

Definition 1
.

Let
q\in B
and
\alpha\in\mathsf{mod}(q)
. The derivation path
\mathsf{path}(\alpha,q)
is defined as follows:

•

If
q
is the
1
-sink then
\alpha=\alpha_{\emptyset}
and the only derivation path is
\mathsf{path}(\alpha_{\emptyset},q)=(\{q\},\emptyset)
.

•

If
q=ite(x,q_{1},q_{0})
, let
\alpha^{\prime}
be the restriction of
\alpha
to
var(\alpha)\setminus\{x\}
, then
V(\mathsf{path}(\alpha,q))=V(\mathsf{path}(\alpha^{\prime},q_{\alpha(x)}))\cdot q
and
E(\mathsf{path}(\alpha,q))
is
E(\mathsf{path}(\alpha^{\prime},q_{\alpha(x)}))
plus the
\alpha(x)
-edge of
q
.

•

If
q=q_{1}\lor\dots\lor q_{k}
with the children ordering
\mathsf{children}(q)=(q_{1},\dots,q_{k})
, let
i
be the smallest integer between
1
and
k
such that
\alpha\in\mathsf{mod}(q_{i})
then
V(\mathsf{path}(\alpha,q))=V(\mathsf{path}(\alpha,q_{i}))\cdot q
and
E(\mathsf{path}(\alpha,q))
is
E(\mathsf{path}(\alpha,q_{i}))
plus the edge between
q_{i}
and
q
.

Our algorithm constructs sample sets in a way that respect derivation paths. That is, an assignment
\alpha\in\mathsf{mod}(q)
may end up in
S^{r}(q)
only if it is derived through
\mathsf{path}(\alpha,q)
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
q\in B
and
\alpha\in\mathsf{mod}(q)
, let
V(\mathsf{path}(\alpha,q))=(q_{0},q_{1},\dots,q_{i-1},q_{i})
with
q_{0}
the
1
-sink and
q_{i}=q
. For every
j\in[0,i-1]
, let
\alpha_{j}
be the restriction of
\alpha
to
var(q_{j})
. In a run of
approxMCnFBDD_core
,
\alpha\in S^{r}(q)
holds only if
\alpha_{j}\in S^{r}(q_{j})
holds for every
j\in[0,i-1]
.

Proof.

\alpha_{0}=\alpha_{\emptyset}\in S^{r}(1\text{-sink})=S^{r}(q_{0})
holds by construction. Now consider
j>0
, it is sufficient to show that
\alpha_{j}\in S^{r}(q_{j})
only if
\alpha_{j-1}\in S^{r}(q_{j-1})
.

If
q_{j}=ite(x,q_{j,1},q_{j,0})
then
\alpha_{j}=\alpha_{j-1}\cup\{x\mapsto\alpha(x)\}
and
q_{j-1}=q_{j,\alpha(x)}
. Looking at
estimateAndSample
for decision nodes, one sees that
\alpha_{j}\in S^{r}(q_{j})
only if
\alpha_{j-1}\in\textup{{reduce}}(S^{r}(q_{j,\alpha(x)}),\frac{p(q_{j})}{p(q_{j-1})})
, so only if
\alpha_{j-1}\in S^{r}(q_{j,\alpha(x)})=S^{r}(q_{j-1})
.

If
q_{j}
is a
\lor
-node with
\mathsf{children}(q_{j})=(q^{0}_{j},\dots,q^{k}_{j})
then
\alpha_{j}=\alpha_{j-1}
and there is an

such that
q_{j-1}=q^{i}_{j}
. Looking at
estimateAndSample
for decision node, one sees that
\alpha_{j}\in S^{r}(q_{j})
only if
\alpha_{j}\in\hat{S}^{r}(q_{j})
, only if
\alpha_{j}
is
\textup{{reduce}}(S^{r}(q^{\ell}_{j}),\rho/p(q^{\ell}_{j}))
for the smallest

such that
\alpha_{j}\in\mathsf{mod}(q^{\ell}_{j})

\alpha_{j}=\alpha_{j-1}\in S^{r}(q^{\ell}_{j})

\mathsf{path}(\alpha,q)
implies that

∎

Given two derivation paths
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
. We call their

denoted by
\mathsf{lcpn}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{P}^{\prime})
, the deepest node where the two paths diverge, that is, the first node contained in both paths from which the they follow different edges. Note that if
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
are consistent up to node
q^{\prime}
, and
q=ite(x,q^{\prime},q^{\prime})
, and
\mathcal{P}
follows the

\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
follows the

even though they both contain

.

Definition 2
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=((q_{0},\dots,q_{k}),(e_{1},\dots,e_{k}))
and
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}=((q^{\prime}_{0},\dots,q^{\prime}_{\ell}),(e^{\prime}_{1},\dots,e^{\prime}_{\ell}))
be two derivation paths. The last common prefix node, denoted by
\mathsf{lcpn}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{P}^{\prime})
, is the node
q_{i}
for the biggest
i
such that
(q_{0},\dots,q_{i})=(q^{\prime}_{0},\dots,q^{\prime}_{i})
and
(e_{1},\dots,e_{i})=(e^{\prime}_{1},\dots,e^{\prime}_{i})
.

Note that every derivation path contains the
1
-sink for first node, so the last common prefix node is well-defined. Let
V(\mathsf{path}(\alpha,q))=(q_{0},\dots,q_{i})
with
q_{0}
the
1
-sink and
q_{i}=q
. For every
0\leq\ell\leq i
, we define

I(\alpha,q,\ell):=\{\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathsf{mod}(q)\mid\mathsf{lcpn}(\mathsf{path}(\alpha,q),\mathsf{path}(\alpha^{\prime},q))=q_{\ell}\}.

The following result will play a key role in bounding the variance of estimators in the analysis.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathsf{mod}(q)
and
V(\mathsf{path}(\alpha,q))=(q_{0},\dots,q_{i})
with
q_{0}
the
1
-sink and
q_{i}=q
. For every
0\leq\ell\leq i
,
|I(\alpha,q,\ell)|\leq\frac{|\mathsf{mod}(q)|}{|\mathsf{mod}(q_{\ell})|}
.

Proof.

Let
I(\alpha,q,\ell)=\{\alpha^{1},\alpha^{2},\dots\}
. Let
\alpha_{\ell}
be the restriction of
\alpha
to
var(q_{\ell})
. By definition, every
\alpha^{i}
is of the form
\alpha_{\ell}\cup\beta^{i}
for some assignment
\beta^{i}
to
var(q)\setminus var(q_{\ell})
. Consider
\alpha^{\prime}_{\ell}\in\mathsf{mod}(q_{\ell})
, then every
\alpha^{\prime}_{\ell}\cup\beta^{i}
is in
\mathsf{mod}(q)
. Since the assignments
\alpha^{i}
s differ on
var(q)\setminus var(q_{\ell})
, the
\beta^{i}
s are pairwise distinct, and therefore
\{\alpha^{\prime}_{\ell}\cup\beta^{i}\}_{i}
is a set of
|I(\alpha,q,\ell)|
distinct assignments in
\mathsf{mod}(q)
. Considering all
|\mathsf{mod}(q_{\ell})|
possible choices of assignment for
\alpha^{\prime}_{\ell}
, we find that
\{\alpha^{\prime}_{\ell}\cup\beta^{i}\}_{\alpha^{\prime}_{\ell},i}
is a set of
|\mathsf{mod}(q_{\ell})|\cdot|I(\alpha,q,\ell)|

\mathsf{mod}(q)
. Hence
|\mathsf{mod}(q_{\ell})|\cdot|I(\alpha,q,\ell)|\leq|\mathsf{mod}(q)|
.
∎

6
The Framework for the Analysis

We introduce a random process that simulates
approxMCnFBDD_core
. Our intuition is that, for every
\alpha\in\mathsf{mod}(q)
, a statement in the veins of “
\Pr[\alpha\in S^{r}(q)]=p(q)
” should hold. The problem is that this equality makes no sense because
\Pr[\alpha\in S^{r}(q)]
is a fixed real value whereas
p(q)
is a random variable. The variables
S^{r}(q)
and
p(q)
for different
q
are too dependent of each other so we use a random process to work with new variables that behave more nicely. The global picture is that the random process simulates all possible runs of the algorithm for all possible values of
(p(q))_{q}
at once. In the random process,
S^{r}(q)
is simulated by a different variable for each possible run. We can then “replace”
S^{r}(q)
by one of these variables assuming enough knowledge on the algorithm run up to
q
. This knowledge is recorded in what we call a
history
for
q
.

6.1
History

A
history

h
for a set of nodes
Q
is a mapping

h:Q\rightarrow\mathbbm{Q}\cup\{\infty\}.

h
is
realizable
when there exists a run of
\textup{{approxMCnFBDD\_core}}^{*}
that gives the value
h(q)
to
p(q)
for every
q\in Q
. Such a run is said
compatible
with
h
. Two histories
h
for
Q
and
h^{\prime}
for
Q^{\prime}
are
compatible
when
h(q)=h^{\prime}(q)
for all
q\in Q\cap Q^{\prime}
. Compatible histories can be merged into an history
h\cup h^{\prime}
for
Q\cup Q^{\prime}
. For
q\in Q
and
t\in\mathbbm{Q}
, we write
h\cup(q\mapsto t)
to refer to the history
h
augmented with
h(q)=t
. For
q\in B
, we define the set
\mathsf{desc}(q)
of its descendants by
\mathsf{desc}(1\text{-sink})=\emptyset
and
\mathsf{desc}(q)=\mathsf{children}(q)\cup\bigcup_{q^{\prime}\in\mathsf{children}(q)}\mathsf{desc}(q^{\prime})
. Note that
q\not\in\mathsf{desc}(q)
. We only study histories realizable for sets
Q
that are closed for
\mathsf{desc}
, that is, if
q\in Q
and
q^{\prime}
is a descendant of
q
, then
q^{\prime}\in Q
. Thus we abuse terminology and refer to a history for
\mathsf{desc}(q)
as a history for
q
. The only history for the sinks is the vacuous history
h_{\emptyset}
for
Q=\emptyset
(because no descendants).

6.2

The random process comprises
n_{s}n_{t}
independent copies identified by the superscript

q

•

If

•

If

•

If
q=ite(x,q_{1},q_{0})

\mathfrak{S}^{r}_{h,t}(q)=\textup{{reduce}}\left(\mathfrak{S}^{r}_{h_{0},t_{0}}(q_{0})\otimes\{x\mapsto 0\},\frac{t}{t_{0}}\right)\cup\textup{{reduce}}\left(\mathfrak{S}^{r}_{h_{1},t_{1}}(q_{1})\otimes\{x\mapsto 1\},\frac{t}{t_{1}}\right).

•

If
q=q_{1}\lor\dots\lor q_{k}
then for every
\mathfrak{S}^{r}_{h_{1},t_{1}}(q_{1})
,
\dots
,
\mathfrak{S}^{r}_{h_{k},t_{k}}(h_{k})
with realizable and pairwise compatible histories, we define

and the variable

Now for all

Let

\textup{{approxMCnFBDD\_core}}^{*}
for

Median of means

We have that

n_{s}\geq\frac{4n}{\kappa^{2}}
)

By taking the median, we decrease the
\frac{1}{4}
upper bound to a much smaller value. Let
E_{j}
be the indicator variable taking value
1
if and only if
\mathfrak{M}_{j,h}(q)\not\in\nabla(q)
and let
\bar{E}=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{t}}E_{j}
. We have
\textup{E}[\bar{E}]\leq\frac{n_{t}}{4}
so Hoeffding bound gives

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\mathsf{median}(\mathfrak{M}_{1,h}(q),\dots,\mathfrak{M}_{n_{t},h}(q))\not\in\nabla(q)\right]=\Pr\left[\bar{E}>\frac{n_{t}}{2}\right]\leq\Pr\left[\bar{E}-\textup{E}(\bar{E})\geq\frac{n_{t}}{4}\right]\leq e^{-n_{t}/8}\leq\frac{2^{-n|B|}}{16|B|}

where the last inequality comes from
n_{t}=16n|B|\geq 8n|B|+8\ln(16|B|)
. Using Lemma
12
we et

\displaystyle P(q)\leq\sum_{h\in\mathcal{H}^{*}_{q}}\Pr\left[\mathsf{median}(\mathfrak{M}_{1,h}(q),\dots,\mathfrak{M}_{n_{t},h}(q))\not\in\nabla(q)\right]\leq\sum_{h\in\mathcal{H}^{*}_{q}}\frac{2^{-n|B|}}{16|B|}\leq\frac{1}{16|B|}.

Finally we obtain the lower bound of Lemma
8
:

\Pr\left[\bigcup_{q\in B}p(q)\not\in\Delta(q)\right]\leq\sum_{q\in B}P(q)\leq\frac{1}{16}

7.2
Proof of Lemma
9

We first bound
\Pr\left[\bigcup_{r,q}|S^{r}(q)|\geq\theta\right]
from above by

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\bigcup_{r,q}|S^{r}(q)|\geq\theta\right]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\left[\bigcup_{r,q}|S^{r}(q)|\geq\theta\text{ and }\bigcap_{q^{\prime}\in B}p(q^{\prime})\in\frac{1\pm\kappa}{|\mathsf{mod}(q^{\prime})|}\right]+\Pr\left[\bigcup_{q^{\prime}\in B}p(q^{\prime})\not\in\frac{1\pm\kappa}{|\mathsf{mod}(q^{\prime})|}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\left[\bigcup_{r,q}|S^{r}(q)|\geq\theta\text{ and }\bigcap_{q^{\prime}\in B}p(q^{\prime})\in\Delta(q^{\prime})\right]+\frac{1}{16}

(Lemma
8
)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{r,q}\Pr\left[|S^{r}(q)|\geq\theta\text{ and }\bigcap_{q^{\prime}\in B}p(q^{\prime})\in\Delta(q^{\prime})\right]+\frac{1}{16}

(union bound)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{r,q}\Pr\left[|S^{r}(q)|\geq\theta\text{ and }p(q)\in\Delta(q)\right]+\frac{1}{16}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{r,q}\Pr\left[|S^{r}(q)|\mathbbm{1}(p(q)\in\Delta(q))\geq\theta\right]+\frac{1}{16}

(
\theta>0
)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{r,q}\textup{E}\left[|S^{r}(q)|\mathbbm{1}(p(q)\in\Delta(q))\right]+\frac{1}{16}

(Markov’s inequality)

We use the following result to finish the proof of Lemma
9
.

Lemma 14
.

Let
q\in B
with
\mathsf{mod}(q)\neq\emptyset
. For every
r\in[n_{s}n_{t}]
and
\alpha\in\mathsf{mod}(q)
,
\textup{E}\left[\frac{\mathbbm{1}(\alpha\in S^{r}(q))}{p(q)}\right]=1
.

One can see
\textup{E}\left[\frac{\mathbbm{1}(\alpha\in S^{r}(q))}{p(q)}\right]=1
as a correct variant of “
\Pr[\alpha\in S^{r}(q)]=p(q)
” (which is fallacious as it states that a fixed probability equals a random variable). Let us assume Lemma
14
for now. If
q
is the
0
-sink then
|S^{r}(0\text{-sink})|=0
so
\textup{E}\left[|S^{r}(0\text{-sink})|\mathbbm{1}(p(0\text{-sink})\in\Delta(0\text{-sink}))\right]=0
. Now suppose
\mathsf{mod}(q)\neq\emptyset
.

\displaystyle\textup{E}\left[|S^{r}(q)|\mathbbm{1}(p(q)\in\Delta(q))\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathsf{mod}(q)}\textup{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}(\alpha\in S^{r}(q))\mathbbm{1}(p(q)\in\Delta(q))\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathsf{mod}(q)}\textup{E}\left[\frac{\mathbbm{1}(\alpha\in S^{r}(q))}{p(q)}\cdot p(q)\mathbbm{1}(p(q)\in\Delta(q))\right]

The random variable
p(q)\mathbbm{1}(p(q)\in\Delta(q))
is always less than the upper limit of
\Delta(q)
, i.e.,
\frac{1+\kappa}{|\mathsf{mod}(q)|}
. Hence

\displaystyle\textup{E}\left[|S^{r}(q)|\mathbbm{1}(p(q)\in\Delta(q))\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1+\kappa}{|\mathsf{mod}(q)|}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathsf{mod}(q)}\textup{E}\left[\frac{\mathbbm{1}(\alpha\in S^{r}(q))}{q}\right]=1+\kappa

(Lemma
14
)

Going back to the bound on
\Pr\left[\bigcup_{r,q}|S^{r}(q)|\geq\theta\right]
we get

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\bigcup_{r,q}|S^{r}(q)|\geq\theta\right]\leq\frac{1}{\theta}\sum_{r,q}(1+\kappa)+\frac{1}{16}=\frac{(1+\kappa)n_{s}n_{t}|B|}{\theta}+\frac{1}{16}\leq\frac{1}{8}

(
\theta=\lceil 16(1+\kappa)n_{s}n_{t}|B|\rceil
)

Thus it only remains to show the correctness of Lemma
14
to finish the proof of Lemma
9
.

Proof of Lemma
14
.

We proceed by induction on the height of
q
. The base case is that of the
1
-sink. We have that
p(1\text{-sink})=1
and
S^{r}(1\text{-sink})=\{\alpha_{\emptyset}\}=\mathsf{mod}(1\text{-sink})
so it is indeed verified that

\textup{E}\left[\frac{\mathbbm{1}(\alpha_{\emptyset}\in S^{r}(1\text{-sink}))}{p(1\text{-sink})}\right]=1

For the inductive case, we distinguish the situation where
q
is a decision from that where
q
is a
\lor
node.

Suppose
q
is a decision node
ite(x,q_{1},q_{0})
. Let
b=\alpha(x)
then and
\alpha_{b}
be the restriction of
\alpha
to
\mathit{var}(q_{b})
.

\frac{\mathbbm{1}(\alpha\in S^{r}(q))}{p(q)}=\frac{\mathbbm{1}(\alpha\in\textup{{reduce}}(S^{r}(q_{b}),\frac{p(q)}{p(q_{b})}))}{p(q)}

Let
A
be the finite set of all possible values for
p(q)
and
B
be the finite set of all possible values for
p(q_{b}
). Let
A(\alpha_{b})\subseteq A
be the set of values
t
such that
\Pr[p(q)=t,\,\alpha_{b}\in S^{r}(q_{b})]\neq 0
. Let
B(\alpha_{b},t)
be the set of values
t^{\prime}
such that
\Pr[p(q_{b})=t^{\prime},\,p(q)=t,\,\alpha_{b}\in S^{r}(q_{b})]\neq 0
.

E

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\mathbbm{1}(\alpha\in S^{r}(q))}{p(q)}\right]=\sum_{t\in A}\frac{1}{t}\Pr\left[\alpha\in S^{r}(q),\,p(q)=t\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\in A(\alpha_{b})}\sum_{t^{\prime}\in B(\alpha_{b},t)}\frac{1}{t}\Pr\left[\alpha_{b}\in\textup{{reduce}}\left(S^{r}(q_{b}),\frac{t}{t^{\prime}}\right)\bigg|\,\,\begin{matrix}p(q_{b})=t^{\prime},\,p(q)=t,\\
\alpha_{b}\in S^{r}(q_{b})\end{matrix}\right]\Pr\left[\begin{matrix}p(q_{b})=t^{\prime},\,p(q)=t,\\
\alpha_{b}\in S^{r}(q_{b})\end{matrix}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\in A(\alpha_{b})}\sum_{t^{\prime}\in B(\alpha_{b},t)}\frac{1}{t^{\prime}}\cdot\Pr\left[p(q_{b})=t^{\prime},\,p(q)=t,\,\alpha_{b}\in S^{r}(q_{b})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\in A}\sum_{t^{\prime}\in B}\frac{1}{t^{\prime}}\Pr\left[p(q_{b})=t^{\prime},\,p(q)=t,\,\alpha_{b}\in S^{r}(q_{b})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t^{\prime}\in B}\frac{1}{t^{\prime}}\sum_{t\in A}\Pr\left[p(q_{b})=t^{\prime},\,p(q)=t,\,\alpha_{b}\in S^{r}(q_{b})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t^{\prime}\in B}\frac{1}{t^{\prime}}\Pr\left[p(q_{b})=t^{\prime},\,\alpha_{b}\in S^{r}(q_{b})\right]=\textup{E}\left[\frac{\mathbbm{1}(\alpha_{b}\in S^{r}(q_{b}))}{p(q_{b})}\right]

By induction
\textup{E}\left[\frac{\mathbbm{1}(\alpha_{b}\in S^{r}(q_{b}))}{p(q_{b})}\right]=1
so we are done.

We move to the case where
q
is a
\lor
node. Then

\frac{\mathbbm{1}(\alpha\in S^{r}(q))}{p(q)}=\frac{\mathbbm{1}(\alpha\in\textup{{reduce}}(\hat{S}^{r}(q_{b}),\frac{p(q)}{\rho(q)}))}{p(q)}

Let
C
be the finite set of all possible values for
\rho(q)
and
C(\alpha,t)
the set of values
t^{\prime}
such that
\Pr[\rho(q)=t^{\prime},\,p(q)=t,\,\alpha\in\hat{S}^{r}(q)]\neq 0
. The same analysis as before with
p(q_{b})=t^{\prime}
replaced by
\rho(q)=t^{\prime}
,
\alpha_{b}\in S^{r}(q_{b})
replaced by
\alpha\in S^{r}(q)
,
B
replaced by
C
and
B(\alpha_{b},t)
replaced by
C(\alpha,t)
gives

\textup{E}\left[\frac{\mathbbm{1}(\alpha\in S^{r}(q))}{p(q)}\right]=\textup{E}\left[\frac{\mathbbm{1}(\alpha\in\hat{S}^{r}(q))}{\rho(q)}\right]

There is a unique child
q^{\prime}
of
q
such that
\alpha\in\hat{S}^{r}(q)
if and only if
\alpha\in\textup{{reduce}}(S^{r}(q^{\prime}),\frac{\rho(q)}{p(q^{\prime})})
. Let
D
be the finite set of all possible values for
p(q^{\prime})
and, for
t\in C
,
D(\alpha,t)
be the set of values such that
\Pr[p(q^{\prime})=t^{\prime},\,\rho(q)=t,\,\alpha\in\hat{S}^{r}(q)]\neq 0
. Again, the same analysis as before shows that

\textup{E}\left[\frac{\mathbbm{1}(\alpha\in\hat{S}^{r}(q))}{\rho(q)}\right]=\textup{E}\left[\frac{\mathbbm{1}(\alpha\in S^{r}(q^{\prime}))}{p(q^{\prime})}\right]

The induction hypothesis gives us
\textup{E}\left[\frac{\mathbbm{1}(\alpha\in S^{r}(q^{\prime}))}{p(q^{\prime})}\right]=1
so we are done.
∎

8
Conclusion

In this paper, we resolved the open problem of designing an FPRAS for #nFBDD. Our work also introduces a new technique to quantify dependence, and it would be interesting to extend this technique to other languages that generalize nFBDD. Another promising direction for future work would be to improve the complexity of the proposed FPRAS to enable practical adoption.
