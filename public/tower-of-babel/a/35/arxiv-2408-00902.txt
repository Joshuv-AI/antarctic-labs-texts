Atomistic simulations of out-of-equilibrium quantum nuclear dynamics
Francesco Libbi, Anders Johansson, Lorenzo Monacelli, Boris Kozinsky

Abstract
The rapid advancements in ultrafast laser technology have paved the way for pumping and probing the out-of-equilibrium dynamics of nuclei in crystals. However, interpreting these experiments is extremely challenging due to the complex nonlinear responses in systems where lattice excitations interact, particularly in crystals composed of light atoms or at low temperatures where the quantum nature of ions becomes significant. In this work, we address the nonequilibrium quantum ionic dynamics from first principles. Our approach is general and can be applied to simulate any crystal, in combination with a first-principles treatment of electrons or external machine-learning potentials. It is implemented by leveraging the nonequilibrium time-dependent self-consistent harmonic approximation (TD-SCHA), with a stable, energy-conserving, correlated stochastic integration scheme that achieves an accuracy of 𝒪 ⁡ ( d ​ t 3 ) \mathcal{O}(dt^{3}) . We benchmark the method with both a simple one-dimensional model to test its accuracy and a realistic 40-atom cell of SrTiO 3 \text{SrTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}} under THz laser pump, paving the way for simulations of ultrafast THz-Xray pump-probe spectroscopy like those performed in synchrotron facilities.



I
Introduction

Nuclear quantum effects often play a crucial role in determining properties of materials
[
1
]
, affecting their thermodynamic stability
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
, electronic structure
[
5
,
6
]
, and transport phenomena
[
7
]
. Understanding and accurately accounting for quantum contributions in tunneling and vibrational statistics requires simulations that go beyond classical nuclei approximation. Path-integral molecular dynamics (PIMD) is the most common approach to simulate nuclear quantum effects in complex anharmonic crystals
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
, especially with the ring-polymer formulation
[
12
,
13
]
, which is an exact framework for sampling the equilibrium nuclear density matrix.
However, PIMD is rigorously formulated with an assumption of thermodynamic equilibrium as it evolves the trajectories in imaginary time. Simulating out-of-equilibrium quantum nuclear dynamics requires a theory for the real-time evolution, such as the path-integral quantum Monte Carlo (PIMC),
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
, which is extremely challenging due to the appearance of the so-called sign problem, in which different trajectories contribute with different signs, introducing noise in determining dynamical observables
[
18
]
. Therefore, most applications of real-time PIMC remain limited to systems composed of only a few degrees of freedom
[
19
]
.

Several techniques have been proposed to overcome the limits of real-time PIMC to study nuclear quantum effects in systems of realistic interest.
Among these, the time-dependent self-consistent harmonic approximation (TD-SCHA
[
20
,
21
]
) holds significant promise.
The theory extends the stochastic self-consistent harmonic approximation (SSCHA)
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
, a well-established technique to simulate equilibrium thermodynamics of solids accounting for quantum nuclear fluctuations. The success of SSCHA lies in the adoption of approximations that are particularly effective for crystals, achieving a computational cost that is orders of magnitude lower than PIMD, while still producing predictions in very good agreement with experiments
[
2
,
3
,
7
,
25
,
26
]
.

TD-SCHA has already been employed in the linear response regime, where it enabled the prediction of Raman and IR spectra of metallic hydrogen with unprecedented accuracy
[
27
]
. However, up to now, no application of TD-SCHA beyond equilibrium has been attempted. The difficulty in obtaining accurate and stable dynamic solutions of TD-SCHA equations is challenging due to the need for the evaluation of ensemble averages of the nuclear potential energy landscape.

In this work we address this challenge by developing an algorithm to solve the dynamical TD-SCHA equations and simulate the out-of-equilibrium dynamics of nuclei in complex realistic systems. In Sec.
II
, we revise the TD-SCHA equations of motion for the nuclear density matrix. Sec.
III
introduces three different numerical algorithms to integrate the TD-SCHA equations, and their stability is discussed in Sec.
IV
.
Sec.
V
presents the correlated sampling technique to perform the evaluation of the stochastic quantum averages. This formulation ensures both the efficient evaluation of ensemble averages and the numerical stability of the equations. Crucially, we show that such a correlated approach conserves energy in one dimensional problems independently of the number of stochastic configurations adopted. We benchmark the method in Sec.
VI
, where we compare the different numerical schemes on a one dimensional model system. Finally, in Sec.
VII
, we provide an example of the application of TD-SCHA to realistic systems by studying the quantum dynamics in
\mathrm{SrTiO_{3}}
(STO) when driven out of equilibrium by a strong laser pulse of THz frequency.

II
Time-dependent self-consistent harmonic approximation

The TD-SCHA formulation leverages the Wigner formalism
[
28
,
29
]
.
The Wigner transform of the nuclear quantum density matrix
\hat{\rho}(t)
describing the quantum state is defined as

\rho(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{P},t)=\int\frac{e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\mathbf{P}\cdot\mathbf{R}^{\prime}}}{(2\pi\hbar)^{3N}}\Bigl\langle\mathbf{R}+\frac{\mathbf{R}^{\prime}}{2}\Bigl|\hat{\rho}(t)\Bigr|\mathbf{R}-\frac{\mathbf{R}^{\prime}}{2}\Bigr\rangle d\mathbf{R}^{\prime}\ ,

(1)

and it maps the quantum operator
\hat{\rho}(t)
into a function of positions and momenta
\rho(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{P},t)
that is analogous to the classical nuclear density. All atomic quantities are rescaled by mass to simplify the notation:
R_{i}=\tilde{R}_{i}\sqrt{m_{i}}
and
P_{i}=\tilde{P}_{i}/\sqrt{m_{i}}
, where the index
i
goes from 1 to
3N
, containing both the Cartesian and atomic index, and the tilde indicates the standard (not mass-rescaled) quantities. Dynamical averages of any quantum observable are obtained by tracing
\mathbf{R}
and
\mathbf{P}
on the density:

\left<O\right>(t)=\int d\mathbf{R}d\mathbf{P}\rho(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{P},t)O(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{P}).

(2)

The TD-SCHA method is based on expressing the Wigner density matrix as a general Gaussian form in terms of ionic positions
\mathbf{R}
and momenta
\mathbf{P}
:

\rho(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{P},t)=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}({\delta\mathbf{R}}\cdot\bm{\alpha}\cdot{\delta\mathbf{R}}+{\delta\mathbf{P}}\cdot\bm{\beta}\cdot{\delta\mathbf{P}}+{\delta\mathbf{R}}\cdot\bm{\gamma}\cdot{\delta\mathbf{P}})}\ .

(3)

Here
{\delta\mathbf{R}}(t)=\mathbf{R}-\mathbfcal{R}(t)
and
{\delta\mathbf{P}}(t)=\mathbf{P}-\mathbfcal{P}(t)
and
\mathcal{N}
is the normalization factor, where
\mathbfcal{R}(t)=\braket{\mathbf{R}}(t)
represent average positions and
\mathbfcal{P}(t)=\braket{\mathbf{P}}(t)
the average momenta.
The
\bm{\alpha}(t)
,
\bm{\beta}(t)
and
\bm{\gamma}(t)
matrices are related to, respectively, position-position, momentum-momentum, and position-momentum covariances by the following relations:

\mathbf{A}^{-1}=\braket{\delta R_{i}\delta R_{j}}^{-1}=\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\gamma}\cdot\bm{\beta}^{-1}\cdot\bm{\gamma}^{T}\ ,

(4)

\mathbf{B}^{-1}=\braket{\delta P_{i}\delta P_{j}}^{-1}=-\bm{\gamma}^{T}+\bm{\beta}\cdot\bm{\gamma}^{-1}\cdot\bm{\alpha}\ ,

(5)

\bm{\Gamma}^{-1}=\braket{\delta R_{i}\delta P_{j}}^{-1}=\bm{\beta}-\bm{\gamma}^{T}\cdot\bm{\alpha}^{-1}\cdot\bm{\gamma}^{T}\ .

(6)

The evolution of the density Wigner-space density matrix
\rho(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{P},t)
is determined by the propagation in time of the parameters
\mathbfcal{R}(t)
,
\mathbfcal{P}(t)
,
\mathbf{A}(t)
,
\mathbf{B}(t)
and
\bm{\Gamma}(t)
.
Analogously to the time-dependent Hartree-Fock or time-dependent density functional theory for electrons, the time evolution is obtained by imposing the least action principle
[
21
]
, leading to the time-dependent self-consistent Liouville-von Neumann equation for the density matrix:

i\hbar\frac{\partial\hat{\rho}}{\partial t}=\left[\mathcal{H}[\hat{\rho}],\hat{\rho}\right],

(7)

where
\mathcal{H}[\hat{\rho}]
is a self-consistent harmonic Hamiltonian whose parameters depend on the anharmonic potential and the density matrix
\hat{\rho}
and the square brackets indicate the quantum commutator (more details in Ref.
[
20
]
). Notably, in TD-SCHA,
\mathcal{H}[\hat{\rho}]
is local in time, so the time evolution depends only on the current quantum state.
Expressing Eq.
7
in the Wigner formalism and substituting the Gaussian form for the density matrix leads to the set of differential equations

\begin{cases}\dot{\mathbfcal{R}}=\mathbfcal{P}\\
\dot{\mathbfcal{P}}=\braket{\mathbf{f}}\\
\dot{\mathbf{A}}=\bm{\Gamma}+\bm{\Gamma}^{{\dagger}}\\
\dot{\mathbf{B}}=-\langle\bm{\partial}^{2}V\rangle\bm{\Gamma}-\bm{\Gamma}^{{\dagger}}\langle\bm{\partial}^{2}V\rangle\\
\dot{\bm{\Gamma}}=\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{A}\langle\bm{\partial}^{2}V\rangle\end{cases}\ ,

(8)

where the dot over a tensor
\dot{\circ}
indicates the time-derivative, the product between tensors is the standard rows-by-columns contraction among all indices, and the dagger symbol indicates the matrix transposition operation
O^{\dagger}_{ij}=O_{ji}
.
Here, the atomic potential energy landscape (PES),
V(\mathbf{R},t)
, enters the quantum averages of forces
\langle\mathbf{f}\rangle
and the average curvature tensor
\langle\bm{\partial}^{2}V\rangle
, defined as

\langle f_{a}\rangle(t)=-\int d\mathbf{R}d\mathbf{P}\frac{\partial V}{\partial R_{a}}(t)\rho(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{P},t),

(9)

\langle\partial_{ab}^{2}V\rangle(t)=\int d\mathbf{R}d\mathbf{P}\frac{\partial^{2}V}{\partial R_{a}\partial R_{b}}(t)\rho(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{P},t).

(10)

The solution of Eq. (
8
) provides the quantum state
\rho(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{P},t)
, enabling the direct computation of the time envelope of any quantum observable.

The stationary solution of Eq. (
8
) coincides with the equilibrium fixed-volume state that minimizes the Helmholtz free energy
[]
and can be obtained with the standard SSCHA algorithm
[
21
,
22
]
.

When simulating a pump-probe experiment, the system is prepared at equilibrium and perturbed with a radiation pulse modeled as a time-dependent external potential
V_{\text{ext}}(\mathbf{R},t)
. The overall potential that enters in Eq. (
9
) and (
10
) is

V(\mathbf{R},t)=V_{\text{BO}}(\mathbf{R})+V_{\text{ext}}(\mathbf{R},t),

(11)

where
V_{\text{BO}}(\mathbf{R})
is the instantaneous interaction potential of nuclei within the Born-Oppenheimer approximation that depends only on the nuclear positions.

III
Numerical integration of TD-SCHA equations

Numerical solutions of the TD-SCHA equations of motion (
8
) have so far been limited to simple one-dimensional models
[
21
]
and linear-response calculations, enabled by an efficient Lanczos algorithm
[
20
]
. The major challenge to applying TD-SCHA in the out-of-equilibrium regime is associated with the cost of calculating the averages
\langle\mathbf{f}\rangle
and particularly
\langle\bm{\partial}^{2}V\rangle
, Eq. (
10
), as sampling the second derivatives of the potential is computationally expensive.
This section introduces a finite-difference scheme to integrate the TD-SCHA equations with an error scaling as
\mathcal{O}(dt^{3})
that requires the computation of Eq. (
10
) only once per time step.
Expanding the time evolution of TD-SCHA quantities in the Taylor series to second order in the time step increment
dt
, we derive the following expressions:

\begin{cases}\mathbfcal{R}_{t+dt}=\mathbfcal{R}_{t}+\mathbfcal{P}_{t}dt+\frac{1}{2}\braket{\mathbf{f}_{t}}dt^{2}+\mathcal{O}(dt^{3})\\
\mathbfcal{P}_{t+dt}=\mathbfcal{P}_{t}+\braket{\mathbf{f}_{t}}dt+\frac{1}{2}\mathbfcal{P}^{\prime\prime}_{t}dt^{2}+\mathcal{O}(dt^{3})\\
\mathbf{A}_{t+dt}=\mathbf{A}_{t}+\Bigl(\bm{\Gamma}+\bm{\Gamma}^{{\dagger}}\Bigr)_{t}dt+\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{A}\langle\bm{\partial}^{2}V\rangle\Bigr)_{t}dt^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\mathbf{B}-\langle\bm{\partial}^{2}V\rangle\mathbf{A}\Bigr)_{t}dt^{2}+\mathcal{O}(dt^{3})\\
\mathbf{B}_{t+dt}=\mathbf{B}_{t}-\Bigl(\langle\bm{\partial}^{2}V\rangle\bm{\Gamma}+\bm{\Gamma}^{{\dagger}}\langle\bm{\partial}^{2}V\rangle\Bigr)_{t}dt+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{B}^{\prime\prime}_{t}dt^{2}+\mathcal{O}(dt^{3})\\
\bm{\Gamma}_{t+dt}=\bm{\Gamma}_{t}+\Bigl(\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{A}\langle\bm{\partial}^{2}V\rangle\Bigr)_{t}dt+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime\prime}_{t}dt^{2}+\mathcal{O}(dt^{3})\end{cases}

(12)

Notably, while the equations for the evolution of
\mathbfcal{R}
and
\mathbf{A}
are explicit up to
\mathcal{O}(dt^{3})
, we need the values of the second derivatives of
\mathbf{B}_{t}
,
\bm{\Gamma}_{t}
and
\mathbfcal{P}_{t}
.

To preserve the
\mathcal{O}(dt^{3})
error of the time propagation we use the central difference formula to approximate those second derivatives (see Appendix
B
):

F_{t+dt}=F_{t}+\frac{1}{2}(F^{\prime}_{t}+F^{\prime}_{t+dt})dt+\mathcal{O}(dt^{3})\ ,

(13)

where
F
represents a generic variable. This expression only requires the knowledge of its first derivatives at times
t
and
t+dt
, but not of the second derivative.
We can rely on two observations:
(i) The calculation of
\langle\mathbf{f}\rangle
and
\langle\bm{\partial}^{2}V\rangle
depends only on
\mathbfcal{R}
and
\mathbf{A}
as the potential is a function of only the positions (see Eqs.
9
-
10
and Appendix
A
);
(ii) both
\mathbfcal{R}
and
\mathbf{A}
can be integrated explicitly with accuracy
\mathcal{O}(dt^{3})
.
The GV algorithm we devise comprises the following steps:
(I) calculate
\mathbfcal{R}_{t+dt}
and
\mathbf{A}_{t+dt}
with accuracy
\mathcal{O}(dt^{3})
using the first and the third of Eqs.
12
respectively;
(II) use
\mathbfcal{R}_{t+dt}
and
\mathbf{A}_{t+dt}
to determine
\braket{\mathbf{f}}_{t+dt}
and
{{\langle\bm{\partial}^{2}V\rangle}_{t+dt}}
;
(III) determine
\mathbfcal{P}
,
\mathbf{B}
and
\bm{\Gamma}
using Eq.
13

\begin{cases}\mathbfcal{P}_{t+dt}=\mathbfcal{P}_{t}+\langle\mathbf{f}\rangle_{\bar{t}}\,dt+\mathcal{O}(t^{3})\\
\mathbf{B}_{t+dt}=\mathbf{B}_{t}-\Bigl(\langle\bm{\partial}^{2}V\rangle\bm{\Gamma}+\bm{\Gamma}^{{\dagger}}\langle\bm{\partial}^{2}V\rangle\Bigr)_{\bar{t}}dt+\mathcal{O}(dt^{3})\\
\bm{\Gamma}_{t+dt}=\bm{\Gamma}_{t}+\Bigl(\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{A}\langle\bm{\partial}^{2}V\rangle\Bigr)_{\bar{t}}dt+\mathcal{O}(dt^{3})\ .\end{cases}

(14)

Here we use the shorthand
F_{\bar{t}}
to indicate

\frac{1}{2}(F_{t}+F_{t+dt})
.
Even though Eq.
14
is implicit in the variables
\mathbf{B}
and
\bm{\Gamma}
, it requires the calculation of
\langle\mathbf{f}\rangle
and
\langle\bm{\partial}^{2}V\rangle
just once per time step (due to observation (i)), and it evolves the parameters with accuracy
\mathcal{O}(dt^{3})
. Furthermore, the equations for integrating
\mathbfcal{R}
and
\mathbfcal{P}
coincide with the familiar
velocity Verlet
scheme
[
30
]
.

We now compare the above GV integration scheme with alternatives, namely explicit Euler (EE) and semi-implicit Euler (SIE) algorithms, showing that the GV is more accurate than both EE and SIE and stable for larger values of the time-step
dt
.
Alternative integration strategies for Eqs. (
12
) are founded on the semi-implicit Euler method. Since
\bm{\Gamma}
is related to the derivatives of
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
(see Eqs.
8
), the semi-implicit Euler (SIE) scheme consists in updating
\bm{\Gamma}
first, then computing
\mathbf{A}_{t+dt}
and
\mathbf{B}_{t+dt}
by using
\bm{\Gamma}_{t+dt}
instead of
\bm{\Gamma}_{t}
:

\begin{cases}\mathbf{A}_{t+dt}=\mathbf{A}_{t}+\Bigl(\bm{\Gamma}+\bm{\Gamma}^{{\dagger}}\Bigr)_{t+dt}dt+\mathcal{O}(dt^{2})\\
\mathbf{B}_{t+dt}=\mathbf{B}_{t}-\Bigl(\langle\bm{\partial}^{2}V\rangle_{t}\bm{\Gamma}_{t+dt}+\bm{\Gamma}^{{\dagger}}_{t+dt}\langle\bm{\partial}^{2}V\rangle_{t}\Bigr)dt+\mathcal{O}(dt^{2})\\
\bm{\Gamma}_{t+dt}=\bm{\Gamma}_{t}+\Bigl(\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{A}\langle\bm{\partial}^{2}V\rangle\Bigr)_{t}dt+\mathcal{O}(dt^{2})\\
\end{cases}\ .

(15)

Instead,
\mathbfcal{R}
and
\mathbfcal{P}
are evolved according to Verlet.
These integration schemes can be compared to the simplest approach, the explicit Euler (EE) scheme, where all the parameters are evolved simultaneously:

\begin{cases}\mathbf{A}_{t+dt}=\mathbf{A}_{t}+\Bigl(\bm{\Gamma}+\bm{\Gamma}^{{\dagger}}\Bigr)_{t}dt+\mathcal{O}(dt^{2})\\
\mathbf{B}_{t+dt}=\mathbf{B}_{t}-\Bigl(\langle\bm{\partial}^{2}V\rangle_{t}\bm{\Gamma}_{t}+\bm{\Gamma}^{{\dagger}}_{t}\langle\bm{\partial}^{2}V\rangle_{t}\Bigr)dt+\mathcal{O}(dt^{2})\\
\bm{\Gamma}_{t+dt}=\bm{\Gamma}_{t}+\Bigl(\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{A}\langle\bm{\partial}^{2}V\rangle\Bigr)_{t}dt+\mathcal{O}(dt^{2})\\
\end{cases}\ .

(16)

IV
Stability of the integration schemes

Here, we investigate the stability of the integration schemes introduced in the previous section. Particularly, we are interested in the dynamics of the variables
\mathbf{A}
,
\mathbf{B}
, and
\bm{\Gamma}
, which are not present in classical Newtonian nuclear dynamics equations.

Let us consider a 1D wave packet evolving in a Harmonic potential.
Thanks to the constant curvature
\bm{\kappa}
of the PES,
\mathbf{A}
,
\mathbf{B}
and
\bm{\Gamma}
do not depend on the average coordinates (centroids)
\mathbfcal{R}
and
\mathbfcal{P}
since

\langle\bm{\partial}^{2}V\rangle=\bm{\kappa}.

(17)

The TD-SCHA equations for these variables reduce to

\begin{pmatrix}\dot{A}\\
\dot{B}\\
\dot{\Gamma}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&2\\
0&0&-2\kappa\\
-\kappa&1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A\\
B\\
\Gamma\end{pmatrix}\ .

(18)

By rescaling the parameters as

\begin{cases}A^{\prime}=\sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{2}}A\\
B^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\kappa}}B\\
t^{\prime}=\sqrt{2\kappa}t\\
\end{cases}

(19)

we get a generalized equation that does not depend on the PES curvature
\kappa

\begin{pmatrix}\dot{A^{\prime}}\\
\dot{B^{\prime}}\\
\dot{\Gamma^{\prime}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\
0&0&-1\\
-1&1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A^{\prime}\\
B^{\prime}\\
\Gamma^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}\ .

(20)

The above equation can be written in a compact notation as

\dot{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{x}

(21)

where the propagation matrix
M
is skew-symmetric. This symmetry imposes that the norm of
\mathbf{x}
is conserved

A^{\prime 2}+B^{\prime 2}+\Gamma^{\prime 2}=\mathrm{const}\ .

(22)

In the following, we omit the prime symbol to maintain a cleaner notation and introduce the integer step
n
as
n=t/dt
. The stability of the methods is investigated by calculating the step transformation matrix
\mathbf{S}(dt)
, which connects the degrees of freedom at time step
n+1
with those at step
n
:

\mathbf{x}_{n+1}=\mathbf{S}(dt)\mathbf{x}_{n}\ .

(23)

Iterating Eq. (
23
), we obtain

\mathbf{x}_{n+1}=\mathbf{S}(dt)^{n}\mathbf{x}_{0}\ .

(24)

The stability condition is achieved if the propagator
\mathbf{S}(dt)^{n}
remains finite for arbitrary large powers
n
. This is equivalent to requiring that all its eigenvalues
\lambda
are such that
|\lambda|\leq 1
.
We calculate the step transformation matrices for EE, SIE, and GV schemes. The details of the derivation are reported in Appendix
B
.
For the EE method we find that

\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}=1+2dt^{2}>1\qquad\forall dt\ ,

(25)

meaning that the EE method is
unconditionally unstable
.
The stability condition obtained for the SIE method is instead

dt_{SIE}\leq\frac{1}{\omega}\ ,

(26)

where
\omega=\sqrt{\kappa}
is the frequency of the harmonic oscillator, while for the GV method we obtain

dt_{GV}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\omega}\ .

(27)

Thus, both the SIE and GV are stable for sufficiently small
dt
, with the GV method having a larger stability range. The integration step of the GV algorithm must thus be shorter than approximately 1/5th of the shortest period of vibrational motion of an atomic system.

V
Stochastic formulation

The ensemble averages of the potential energy and its derivatives are multidimensional integrals that are challenging to calculate. One strategy for addressing this issue involves expanding the potential energy in a Taylor series centered at a high-symmetry point of the structure , which allows analytically computing the thermodynamic averages. This approach relies on the analytic knowledge of Gaussian integrals, and it is at the basis of the self-consistent phonon (SCP) approach
[
31
]
.
The alternative consists in evaluating the integrals through a stochastic Monte Carlo algorithm, as exploited by the SSCHA approach
[
22
]
.
Here, we introduce a stochastic formulation for the TD-SCHA. The ensemble average of the potential energy on the nuclear density in Eq. (
3
) can be calculated as:

\braket{V}_{\mathcal{D}}=\frac{1}{N_{c}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{c}}V(\mathbfcal{R}(t)+\mathbf{J}(t)\cdot\mathbf{y}_{i}(t))\ ,

(28)

where
\mathbf{y}^{i}(t)
are i.i.d. normal random variables ,
N_{c}
is the number of stochastic configurations, and the subscript
\mathcal{D}
stands for the discrete evaluation of the ensemble average (a more detailed introduction is in Appendix
C
).
\mathbf{J}(t)
is the principal square root (one of many possibilities) of the position-position covariance
\mathbf{A}(t)
:

J_{ab}=\sum_{\mu}\sqrt{\lambda_{\mu}}e_{\mu a}e_{\mu b}\ ,

(29)

where
\lambda_{\mu}
and
e_{\mu}
are respectively eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
\mathbf{A}
.
The TD-SCHA equations only require the averages of the first and second derivatives of the potential. In our formulation, both averages require only the calculation of forces, which can be obtained either from first principles or from surrogate machine-learning force field models.
The average of the first derivative of the potential simplifies to

\Bigl\langle\frac{\partial V}{\partial R_{a}}\Bigr\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}=-\frac{1}{N_{c}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{c}}f_{a}(\mathbfcal{R}+\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{y}_{i})\ ,

(30)

where the Cartesian force component is

f_{a}=-\frac{\partial V}{\partial R_{a}}\ .

(31)

(Here, we have omitted the time dependence for clarity in notation).
The calculation of the ensemble average of the second derivatives leverages integration by parts to solely utilize the forces
[
23
]

\Bigl\langle\frac{\partial^{2}V}{\partial R_{a}\partial R_{b}}\Bigr\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}=-\sum_{cd}A^{-1}_{ac}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{c}}J_{cd}\ y_{di}f_{b}(\mathbfcal{R}+\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{y}_{i})\ .

(32)

Eq.
32
is symmetric in the Cartesian indexes
a
and
b
only in the limit
N_{c}\to\infty
. For a finite number of configurations, it must be symmetrized:

\Bigl\langle\frac{\partial^{2}V}{\partial R_{a}\partial R_{b}}\Bigr\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}^{sym}=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl\langle\frac{\partial^{2}V}{\partial R_{a}\partial R_{b}}\Bigr\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}+\frac{1}{2}\Bigl\langle\frac{\partial^{2}V}{\partial R_{a}\partial R_{b}}\Bigr\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}^{T}

(33)

The stochastic evaluation of these integrals is characterized by Gaussian noise, which decreases as
1/N_{c}
. If the random displacements
\mathbf{y}_{i}
are sampled in uncorrelated way at each time step, this implies the presence of stochastic noise as input in the TD-SCHA differential equations, which can significantly affect their accuracy and stability.
As demonstrated in Ref.
[
21
]
, the TD-SCHA equations conserve the total energy in the absence of external potentials acting on the system:

\frac{d}{dt}\sum_{a}\Bigl[\frac{B_{aa}+\mathcal{P}_{a}^{2}}{2}\Bigr]+\frac{d}{dt}\braket{V}=0

(34)

However, the total energy is conserved only in the limit for
N_{c}\to\infty
.
We can show that these issues can be fixed by using the same random configurations
\mathbf{\bar{y}}_{i}
in different time step evaluations, which we refer to as correlated sampling. This choice introduces a systematic bias but eliminates the stochastic noise of the ensemble averages of forces and curvatures, making the time evolution smooth. Moreover, we can demonstrate that for one-dimensional problems, energy conservation holds true for any finite number of configurations when a constant
\mathbf{\bar{y}}_{i}
is employed as
dt\to 0
(see Appendix
D
).
For higher-dimensional problems, energy conservation remains dependent on the number of configurations due to arbitrariness in the definition of
\mathbf{J}
. Nevertheless, the correlated sampling approach drastically improves the energy conservation for a given number of configurations
\mathrm{N}_{c}
, as shown numerically in the following sections. The possibility of choosing a gauge for
\mathbf{J}
that allows for energy conservation independent of the number of configurations in higher-dimensional problems is discussed in the Appendix
D
and will be the subject of future research.

VI
Tests

We test the integration schemes on a one-dimensional model with potential energy

V(u)=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(-au^{2}-bu^{3}+cu^{4}\Bigr)\ ,

(35)

where

VIII
Conclusions

In this work, we introduced the first approach to simulate nonequilibrium quantum nuclear dynamics using the TD-SCHA. We derived an integration scheme, the Generalized Verlet, which allows for the evolution of the equations with an error of
\mathcal{O}(dt^{3})
, demonstrating that its conditional stability is consistent and of the same order as the Nyquist sampling rate. Additionally, we introduced a stochastic formulation of the TD-SCHA, which enables efficient calculation of ensemble averages while ensuring the stability of the evolution. Finally, we showcased the method’s potential, proving it is well-suited for simulating quantum nonequilibrium processes in pump-probe setups on the scale of hundreds of atoms.

Appendix B
Numerical Stability of Integation Schemes

First of all, we prove an identity for the central finite difference approximation of a derivative. We first write the Taylor expansion of a function
F
up to the third order centered at
t
:

F_{t+dt}=F_{t}+F^{\prime}_{t}dt+\frac{1}{2}F^{\prime\prime}_{t}dt^{2}+\mathcal{O}(dt^{3})

(43)

we can write the same expression by centering it in
t+dt
,

F_{t}=F_{t+dt}-F^{\prime}_{t+dt}dt+\frac{1}{2}F^{\prime\prime}_{t+dt}dt^{2}+\mathcal{O}(dt^{3})

(44)

Adding up these equations we get

F_{t+dt}=F_{t}+\frac{1}{2}(F^{\prime}_{t}+F^{\prime}_{t+dt})dt+\frac{1}{2}(F^{\prime\prime}_{t}-F^{\prime\prime}_{t+dt})dt^{2}+\mathcal{O}(dt^{3})\ .

(45)

However, from the following equation

F^{\prime\prime}_{t+dt}=F^{\prime\prime}_{t}+F^{\prime\prime\prime}_{t}dt+\mathcal{O}(dt^{2})

(46)

we get

(F^{\prime\prime}_{t+dt}-F^{\prime\prime}_{t})dt^{2}=F^{\prime\prime\prime}_{t}dt^{3}+\mathcal{O}(dt^{4})

(47)

leading to the result

F_{t+dt}=F_{t}+\frac{1}{2}(F^{\prime}_{t}+F^{\prime}_{t+dt})dt+\mathcal{O}(dt^{3})\ ,

(48)

which corresponds to Eq.
13
of the main text. This equation indicates that we can evolve a function
F
with accuracy up to third-order by utilizing the average of the derivative at time
t
and that at time
t+dt
.
Eq.
13
can be employed to derive the Verlet algorithm:

\begin{cases}\mathbfcal{P}_{t+dt}=\mathbfcal{P}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\langle\mathbf{f}\rangle_{t}+\langle\mathbf{f}\rangle_{t+dt}\Bigr)dt+\mathcal{O}(dt^{3})\\
\mathbfcal{R}_{t+dt}=\mathbfcal{R}_{t}+\mathbfcal{P}_{t}dt+\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathbf{f}\rangle_{t}dt^{2}+\mathcal{O}(dt^{3})\end{cases}\ .

(49)

In the following, we study the stability of the explicit Euler, semi-implicit Euler and generalized Verlet methods.

1. Explicit Euler method

At each step of the explicit Euler method, the variables are updated according to the following rule:

\mathbf{x}_{n+1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&dt\\
0&1&-dt\\
-dt&dt&1\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{x}_{n}=\mathbf{S}(dt)\mathbf{x}_{n}

(50)

leading to

\mathbf{x}_{n+1}=\mathbf{S}(dt)^{n}\mathbf{x}_{0}

(51)

The exponentiation of such a matrix requires the calculation of its eigenvalue, which are

\begin{cases}\lambda_{1}=1\\
\lambda_{2}=1-i\sqrt{2}dt\\
\lambda_{3}=1+i\sqrt{2}dt\\
\end{cases}

(52)

The stability condition for preventing divergence of the power sequence in Eq.
51
is

|1\pm i\sqrt{2}dt|\leq 1

(53)

which is never satisfied. The explicit Euler method is thus
unconditionally unstable
.

2. Semi-implicit Euler method

At each step, first
\Gamma
is updated:

\Gamma_{n+1}=\Gamma_{n}+(B_{n}-A_{n})dt

(54)

and then
A
and
B

\begin{cases}A_{n+1}=A_{n}+\Gamma_{n+1}dt\\
B_{n+1}=B_{n}-\Gamma_{n+1}dt\end{cases}

(55)

Substituting Eq.
54
into
55
, we obtain

\begin{cases}A_{n+1}=A_{n}+\Gamma_{n}dt+(B_{n}-A_{n})dt^{2}\\
B_{n+1}=B_{n}-\Gamma_{n}dt-(B_{n}-A_{n})dt^{2}\end{cases}

(56)

or equivalently

\begin{pmatrix}A_{n+1}\\
B_{n+1}\\
\Gamma_{n+1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1-dt^{2}&dt^{2}&dt\\
dt^{2}&1-dt^{2}&-dt\\
-dt&dt&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A_{n}\\
B_{n}\\
\Gamma_{n}\end{pmatrix}\ .

(57)

The eigenvalues of the step matrix are

\begin{cases}\lambda_{1}=1\\
\lambda_{2}=1-dt^{2}-dt\sqrt{dt^{2}-2}\\
\lambda_{3}=1-dt^{2}+dt\sqrt{dt^{2}-2}\\
\end{cases}

(58)

The eigenvalues
\lambda_{2}
,
\lambda_{3}
are real for
dt\geq\sqrt{2}
, or complex conjugated otherwise. We easily note that

\lambda_{2}<-1\qquad\forall dt\geq\sqrt{2}

(59)

meaning that this method is
unstable
in such range.
For
dt<\sqrt{2}
,
\lambda_{2,3}
are complex, with modulus

|\lambda_{2,3}|^{2}=(1-dt^{2})^{2}+2dt^{2}-dt^{4}=1

(60)

therefore the method is
stable
.
Remembering the definition
19
, the stability condition for this method is

t\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}}=\frac{1}{\omega}

(61)

where
\omega
is the frequency of the harmonic oscillator (we remind that
\kappa=\frac{1}{m}\frac{\partial^{2}V}{\partial R^{2}}
due to the mass rescaling convention adopted).

3. Generalized Verlet

The same analylisis can be performed on the GV algorithm.
In this case, the variables are updated according to

\begin{cases}A_{n+1}=A_{n}+\Gamma_{n}dt+\frac{1}{2}(B_{n}-A_{n})dt^{2}\\
B_{n+1}=B_{n}-\frac{1}{2}(\Gamma_{n}+\Gamma_{n+1})dt\\
\Gamma_{n+1}=\Gamma_{n}+\frac{1}{2}(B_{n}-A_{n}+B_{n+1}-A_{n+1})dt\end{cases}

(62)

After some tedious algebra, it is possible to write explicitly the transformation in matrix form as

\begin{pmatrix}A_{n+1}\\
B_{n+1}\\
\Gamma_{n+1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1-\frac{dt^{2}}{2}&\frac{dt^{2}}{2}&dt\\
\frac{-dt^{4}+4dt^{2}}{2dt^{2}+8}&\frac{dt^{4}-2dt^{2}+8}{2dt^{2}+8}&\frac{dt^{3}-4dt}{dt^{2}+4}\\
\frac{dt^{3}-4dt}{dt^{2}+4}&\frac{-dt^{3}+4dt}{dt^{2}+4}&\frac{-3dt^{2}+4}{dt^{2}+4}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A_{n}\\
B_{n}\\
\Gamma_{n}\end{pmatrix}\ ,

(63)

with eigenvalues

\begin{cases}\lambda_{1}=1\\
\lambda_{2}=\frac{4-3dt^{2}-2\sqrt{2}dt\sqrt{dt^{2}-4}}{dt^{2}+4}\\
\lambda_{3}=\frac{4-3dt^{2}+2\sqrt{2}dt\sqrt{dt^{2}-4}}{dt^{2}+4}\\
\end{cases}\ .

(64)

The eigenvalues are complex for
dt<2
, with modulus

|\lambda_{2,3}|^{2}=1\ ,

(65)

and the method is stable, whereas for
dt>2

\lambda_{2}<-1\ ,

(66)

thus the method is unstable.
To conclude, the stability condition for such an algorithm is

dt<\sqrt{\frac{2}{\kappa}}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\omega}\ .

(67)

The stability range is thus larger than that of the SIE.

In the following section we define some properties which will be useful for the development of the theory reported in the main text.
The ensemble average of the potential energy at time t is given by

\braket{V}=\int V(\mathbfcal{R}(t)+\delta\mathbf{R})\ \sqrt{\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3N}\mathrm{det}\ A}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\delta\mathbf{R\cdot}\mathbf{A}^{-1}(t)\cdot\delta\mathbf{R}}\ d(\delta\mathbf{R})

(68)

where the integration is carried over the variables
\delta R_{a}
whose number is
3N
.
We now perform a change of variables which is meant to turn the Gaussian in Eq.
68
into a normal distribution. We start from the modal decomposition of the matrix
A

A_{ab}=\sum_{\mu}\lambda_{\mu}e_{\mu a}e_{\mu b}\ ,

(69)

and express the displacement
\delta R_{a}
in normal coordinates:

\delta R_{a}=\sum_{b}J_{ab}y_{b}\ .

(70)

The determinant of the Jacobian matrix is

\mathrm{det}\ J=\prod_{\mu}\sqrt{\lambda_{\mu}}=\sqrt{\mathrm{det}\ A}\ .

(71)

The change of variables of Eq.
70
turns the integral
68
into

\braket{V}=\int V(\mathcal{R}_{a}(t)+\sum_{b}J_{ab}(t)y_{b})\ \prod_{b}\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}y_{b}^{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}dy_{b}\ .

(72)

Eq.
72
provides the starting point for the stochastic calculation of the ensemble averages, expressed in Eq.
28
of the main text.

Appendix D
Energy conservation

Here, we outline the main steps introduced in Ref.
[
21
]
to derive energy conservation for infinitely many configurations. We then show the conditions under which such conservation holds regardless of the number of configurations, which represents the result of the current work. The time derivative of the quantum kinetic energy reads

\frac{d}{dt}\sum_{i}\Bigl\langle\frac{P_{i}^{2}}{2}\Bigl\rangle=\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\sum_{i}\mathcal{P}_{i}^{2}+\mathrm{Tr}\ \mathbf{B}}{2}=\sum_{i}\Bigl(\mathcal{P}_{i}\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{i}+\frac{\dot{B}_{ii}}{2}\Bigr)

(73)

Combining the first and second of Eqs.
8
, we obtain

\sum_{i}\mathcal{P}_{i}\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{i}=-\sum_{i}\Bigl\langle\frac{\partial V}{\partial R_{i}}\Bigr\rangle\dot{\mathcal{R}}_{i}\ ,

(74)

while the third and fourth of Eqs.
8
give

\sum_{i}\frac{\dot{B}_{ii}}{2}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}\Bigl\langle\frac{\partial^{2}V}{\partial R_{i}\partial R_{j}}\Bigr\rangle\dot{A}_{ij}\ .

(75)

Importantly, these equations also hold when the discrete expressions for forces (Eq.
30
) and curvatures (Eq.
33
) are used to drive the dynamics

\sum_{i}\mathcal{P}_{i}\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{i}=-\sum_{i}\Bigl\langle\frac{\partial V}{\partial R_{i}}\Bigr\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}\dot{\mathcal{R}}_{i}\ ,

(76)

\sum_{i}\frac{\dot{B}_{ii}}{2}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}\Bigl\langle\frac{\partial^{2}V}{\partial R_{i}\partial R_{j}}\Bigr\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}\dot{A}_{ij}\ .

(77)

Following Ref.
[
21
]
, it is possible to show that

\Bigl\langle\frac{\partial V}{\partial R_{i}}\Bigr\rangle=\frac{\partial\braket{V}}{\partial\mathcal{R}_{i}}

(78)

and

\frac{1}{2}\Bigl\langle\frac{\partial^{2}V}{\partial R_{i}R_{j}}\Bigr\rangle=\frac{\partial\braket{V}}{\partial\mathcal{A}_{ij}}\ .

(79)

Inserting Eqs.
78
and
79
into Eq.
73
we obtain

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\Bigl({\sum_{i}\mathcal{P}_{i}^{2}+\mathrm{Tr}\ \mathbf{B}}\Bigr)=-\frac{\partial\braket{V}}{\partial\mathcal{R}_{i}}\dot{\mathcal{R}}_{i}-\frac{\partial\braket{V}}{\partial\mathcal{A}_{ij}}\dot{A}_{ij}=-\frac{d}{dt}\braket{V}\ ,

(80)

This proves the conservation of energy in absence of external forces. However, for this energy conservation to hold for any configuration, we need to demonstrate that Eqs.
78
and
79
also hold for the discrete expression of forces and curvatures, namely:

\Bigl\langle\frac{\partial V}{\partial R_{i}}\Bigr\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}=\frac{\partial\braket{V}_{\mathcal{D}}}{\partial\mathcal{R}_{i}}

(81)

and

\frac{1}{2}\Bigl\langle\frac{\partial^{2}V}{\partial R_{i}R_{j}}\Bigr\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}=\frac{\partial\braket{V}_{\mathcal{D}}}{\partial\mathcal{A}_{ij}}\ .

(82)

Equation
81
can be easily derived by applying the chain rule to differentiate Equation
28
:

\Bigl\langle\frac{\partial V}{\partial R_{a}}\Bigr\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}=-\frac{1}{N_{c}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{c}}f_{a}(\mathbfcal{R}+\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{y}_{i})=\frac{1}{N_{c}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{c}}\frac{\partial V}{\partial\mathcal{R}_{a}}(\mathbfcal{R}+\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{y}_{i})=\frac{\partial\braket{V}_{\mathcal{D}}}{\partial\mathcal{R}_{a}}\ ,

(83)

The derivation of Eq.
82
is more complex, and does not hold in the most general case. From Eqs.
32
and
33
, we know that

\Bigl\langle\frac{\partial^{2}V}{\partial R_{a}\partial R_{b}}\Bigr\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}^{sym}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{cd}A^{-1}_{ac}\sum_{i=1}^{N}J_{cd}y_{di}f_{b}(\mathbfcal{R}+\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{y}_{i})\ -\frac{1}{2}\sum_{cd}A^{-1}_{bc}\sum_{i=1}^{N}J_{cd}y_{di}f_{a}(\mathbfcal{R}+\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{y}_{i})\ .

(84)

At the same time, the derivative of
\braket{V}_{\mathcal{D}}
with the respect to the element
A_{ab}
is

\frac{\partial}{\partial A_{ab}}\braket{V}_{\mathcal{D}}=-\frac{1}{N_{c}}\sum_{cd}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{c}}f_{c}(\mathbfcal{R}+\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{y}_{i})\frac{\partial J_{cd}}{\partial A_{ab}}y_{d}

(85)

In order to prove Eq.
82
, it would be enough to show that

\frac{\partial J_{cd}}{\partial A_{ab}}=\frac{1}{4}\delta_{ac}J^{-1}_{bd}+\frac{1}{4}\delta_{bc}J^{-1}_{ad}\ .

(86)

In one dimensional problems, identity
86
reduces to

\frac{\partial J}{\partial A}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{A}}\ ,

(87)

which is satisfied since
J=\sqrt{A}
. Therefore, in one dimensional problems, the conservation of energy holds independently on the number of configurations, provided that the time step is reasonably small.

For higher dimensional problems, the validity of the identity
86
depends on the definition of
\mathrm{\mathbf{J}}
. In fact, there are infinite many ways to obtain the square root of a symmetric matrix, defined as the matrix
\mathrm{\mathbf{J}}
such that

\mathrm{\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{J}\mathbf{J}^{T}}\ .

(88)

This is because multiplying the matrix
\mathrm{\mathbf{J}}
by any orthogonal matrix
\mathbf{O}
results in a matrix that still satisfies Eq.
88
.
The definition of
\mathrm{\mathbf{J}}
in Eq.
29
does not satisfy the identity
86
. In fact, using the rules derived in Appendix 5 of Ref.
[
23
]
, we easily obtain

\sum_{ab}\frac{\partial J_{cd}}{\partial A_{ab}}\dot{A}_{ab}=\sum_{ab}\sum_{\mu\nu}\frac{e_{a\mu}e_{b\nu}e_{c\mu}e_{d\nu}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\mu}}+\sqrt{\lambda_{\nu}}}\dot{A}_{ab}\ .

(89)

The problem of finding an expression for
\mathrm{\mathbf{J}}
that satisfies identity
86
is very important and will be the subject of future research efforts.
