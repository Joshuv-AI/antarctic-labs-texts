Abstract




Abstract:

We show that the assumption of a weak form of the Hardy-Littlewood conjecture
on the Goldbach problem
suffices to disprove the possible existence of exceptional zeros of
Dirichlet
L
-functions. This strengthens a result of the authors
named in the title.

1.
Introduction

A fundamental problem in analytic number theory is that of establishing
zero-free regions for Dirichlet
L
-functions. In case the corresponding
character
\chi\mathop{\rm{mod}}q
is complex, or alternatively, in the case of complex
zeros
\rho=\beta+i\gamma
with
\gamma\neq 0
, one has long known
how to produce zero-free regions of the type

(1.1)

\sigma\geqslant 1-c/\log q(|t|+2)

where
s=\sigma+it
. In the case both
\chi
and
s
are
real much less is known, nothing more recent than a famous “ineffective”
estimate of
Siegel which replaces
\log q
by
q^{\varepsilon}
. This exponentially
weaker result has been a serious impediment to progress in many basic
questions.

In the absence of a solution to the problem of these exceptional zeros there
are some useful results showing their rarity. There have also been
attempts to relate the question to other very difficult problems.
One famous example, by now folklore, shows that the non-existence of such zeros
would follow from improvements seductively small in the
Brun-Titchmarsh theorem which gives uniform upper bounds for the number of
primes in an arithmetic progression.

In more recent years some results have been obtained by showing how relatively
good bounds for exceptional zeros would follow from assumptions about the
seemingly less directly related Goldbach conjecture.
The latter famous statement predicts that every even integer exceeding two
can be written as the sum of two primes. Hardy and Littlewood [HL] put
forth a conjectured asymptotic formula for the number of representations of
n

as the sum of two primes. Following the normal practice in the subject, we
find it simpler to consider a weighted sum over the repsentations involving
the von Mangoldt function, one which leads to an entirely equivalent
conjecture. Let

(1.2)

G(n)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1}+m_{2}=n\\
2\nmid m_{1}m_{2}\end{subarray}}\Lambda(m_{1})\Lambda(m_{2}).

The Hardy-Littlewood conjecture predicts that, for
n
even, we have

G(n)\sim{\mathfrak{S}}(n)n

where
{\mathfrak{S}}(n)
is a certain positive product over the primes, defined
in (
6.2
) to (
6.4
) and easily large enough to imply Goldbach
for all large
n
.

A rather weakened, but still formidable, form of the Hardy-Littlewood
conjecture which features in this (and in earlier) work is as follows.

Weak Hardy-Littlewood-Goldbach Conjecture
:
For all
sufficiently large even
n
we have

(1.3)

\delta{\mathfrak{S}}(n)n<G(n)<(2-\delta){\mathfrak{S}}(n)n,

for some fixed

0<\delta<1
.

Despite having a slightly different name, this is the same conjecture
employed in [GS].
We are going to prove the following result.

THEOREM 1
.

Assume that the Weak Hardy-Littlewood-Goldbach Conjecture (
1.3
)
holds for all sufficiently large even
n
. Then there are no zeros of
any Dirichlet
L
-function
in the region (
1.1
) with a positive constant
c
which is now
allowed to depend on
\delta
.

Of course, as remarked above, we are allowed to restrict the proof to the
case of real zeros of
L
-functions with real characters. We can also assume
that the modulus
q
is large since if there were only a finite number of
exceptional zeros we could reach a region of type (
1.1
) simply by
adjusting the constant
c
(since
L(1,\chi)>0
).

Precursors of this work
include [Fe], [BHMS], [BH], [Ji] and [GS]. A discussion of their results
can be found in [GS].
In those works the results led to an interval of width
c/(\log q)^{2}
rather
than
c/\log q
.
Aside from an only slightly different
approach, our main innovation is the use of
a theorem of Bombieri, described in Section 4, which allows us to incorporate
the zero repulsion principle used, for example, in connection with Linnik’s
theorem on the least prime in an arithmetic progression.

2.
A Goldbach number generating series

We let
N
and
q
be given positive integers. We consider the sum

(2.1)

S(q)=\sum_{n\equiv 0(\mathop{\rm{mod}}q)}G(n)e^{-n/N}

where
G(n)
is given by (
1.2
).
We are going to estimate
S(q)
in two different ways and
then compare the results.

3.
Zeros of
L
-functions and an explicit formula

By the orthogonality property for the characters we write

\displaystyle S(q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1}+m_{2}\equiv 0(\mathop{\rm{mod}}q)\\
2\nmid m_{1}m_{2}\end{subarray}}\Lambda(m_{1})\Lambda(m_{2})e^{-(m_{1}+m_{2})/N}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{\chi(\mathop{\rm{mod}}q)}\chi(-1)\sum_{m}\Bigl(\chi(m)\Lambda(m)e^{-m/N}\Bigr)^{2}+O\Bigl(N(\log q)^{2}\Bigr)\

where the error term accounts for a trivial estimation of the contribution
to the sum from terms
m_{1}
,
m_{2}
with
(m_{1}m_{2},2q)\neq 1
.

Let
N\geqslant q
.
The principal character contributes to
S(q)
an amount

(3.1)

S_{0}(q)=\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\Bigl(\sum_{(m,q)=1}\Lambda(m)e^{-m/N}\Bigr)^{2}=\frac{N^{2}}{\varphi(q)}\Bigl(1+O\bigl(\frac{1}{\log N}\bigr)\Bigr).

The exceptional character
\chi_{1}(\mathop{\rm{mod}}\,q)
contributes

(3.2)

S_{1}(q)=\frac{\chi_{1}(-1)}{\varphi(q)}P(\chi_{1})^{2}

where for any
\chi
we have

(3.3)

P(\chi)=\sum_{m}\chi(m)\Lambda(m)e^{-m/N}.

We have the trivial bound
P(\chi)\ll N
. Hence, all the other characters

\chi\neq\chi_{0},\chi_{1}
, contribute at most

(3.4)

S_{\infty}(q)\ll\frac{N}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{\chi\neq\chi_{0},\chi_{1}}|P(\chi)|.

We evaluate
P(\chi)
for each
\chi\neq\chi_{0}
as follows:

(3.5)

\displaystyle P(\chi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(2)}\Gamma(s)N^{s}\frac{-L^{\prime}}{L}(s)ds

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\rho}\Gamma(\rho)N^{\rho}+O\Bigl(N^{\frac{1}{2}}(\log N)^{2}\Bigr),

on moving the line of integration to
{\rm Re\,s}=\frac{1}{2}
.
Here,
\rho=\beta+i\gamma
runs over the zeros of
L(s,\chi)

with
\frac{1}{2}\leqslant\beta<1
,
giving the explicit formula for the sum
P(\chi)
in (
3.3
).

4.
Linnik’s zero repulsion à la Bombieri

We have the following result.

PROPOSITION 4.1
.

Suppose there is a real character
\chi_{1}(\mathop{\rm{mod}}q)
with a real
zero
\beta_{1}
of
L(s,\chi_{1})
in the segment

(4.1)

1-\frac{c}{\log q}\leqslant\beta_{1}<1

where
c
is a small positive constant. Let
N(\alpha,T)
denote
the number of zeros,
\rho=\beta+i\gamma
,
\rho\neq\beta_{1}
of

L(s,\chi)
for all
\chi(\mathop{\rm{mod}}q)
, counted with multiplicity, in the region

(4.2)

\alpha\leqslant\beta<1,\,\,|\gamma|\leqslant T.

Then, for
\frac{1}{2}\leqslant\alpha\leqslant 1,T\geqslant 2

(4.3)

N(\alpha,T)\ll(1-\beta_{1})(\log q)q^{b(1-\alpha)}T^{3}

where
b
and the implied constant are absolute.

This proposition follows from Théorème 14 of Bombieri [B]
which states the following.

PROPOSITION 4.2
.

For
\frac{1}{2}\leqslant\alpha\leqslant 1
and
T\geqslant 2
, we have

\sum_{q\leqslant T}\,\sideset{}{{}^{*}}{\sum}_{\chi(\mathop{\rm{mod}}q)}N(\alpha,T,\chi)\ll(1-\beta_{1})(\log T)T^{b(1-\alpha)}

where
\chi
runs over primitive
characters and
N(\alpha,T,\chi)
is the number of zeros

\rho=\beta+i\gamma
, of
L(s,\chi)
counted with multiplicity,
in the region (
4.2
), apart from one real and simple zero

\beta_{1}\geqslant 1-c/\log T
.

To deduce Proposition 4.1 we first consider the case
T\leqslant q
.
Now, our sum
N(\alpha,T)
satisfies

\displaystyle N(\alpha,T)

\displaystyle\leqslant N(\alpha,q)\leqslant\sum_{\chi(\mathop{\rm{mod}}q)}N(\alpha,q,\chi)=\sum_{k|q}\sideset{}{{}^{*}}{\sum}_{\chi(\mathop{\rm{mod}}k)}N(\alpha,q,\chi)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{k\leqslant q}\,\sideset{}{{}^{*}}{\sum}_{\chi(\mathop{\rm{mod}}k)}N(\alpha,q,\chi)\ll(1-\beta_{1})(\log q)q^{b(1-\alpha)},

giving the result in this case.

On the other hand, if
T>q
the result follows from the classical
bound
N(T,\chi)\ll T\log qT
.

We remark that the factor
T^{3}
in (
4.3
) is wasteful but
simplifies the proof and is unimportant for our application.

5.
First estimation of
S(q)

We are now ready to complete the estimation of
S(q)
by means of
Dirichlet characters.
We input the result of Proposition 4.1. We derive by partial summation that

(5.1)

\sum_{\chi(\mathop{\rm{mod}}q)}\,\sum_{\rho\neq\beta_{1}}|\Gamma(\rho)N^{\rho}|\ll N(1-\beta_{1})\log q,

provided
N\geqslant q^{b+1}
. Hence, by (
3.4
) and (
3.5
), we have

(5.2)

S_{\infty}(q)\ll\frac{N^{2}}{\varphi(q)}(1-\beta_{1})\log q.

On the other hand, taking only the exceptional character rather than
the others in (
5.1
), we get

(5.3)

P(\chi_{1})=-\Gamma(\beta_{1})N^{\beta_{1}}+O\bigl(N(1-\beta_{1})\log q\bigr)

and so

(5.4)

P(\chi_{1})^{2}=\Gamma(\beta_{1})^{2}N^{2\beta_{1}}+O\bigl(N^{2}(1-\beta_{1})\log q\bigr).

Finally, adding the above estimates we obtain

(5.5)

\displaystyle S(q)

\displaystyle=S_{0}(q)+\frac{\chi_{1}(-1)}{\varphi(q)}N^{2\beta_{1}}+O\Bigl(\frac{N^{2}}{\varphi(q)}(1-\beta_{1})\log q\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\bigl(1+\chi_{1}(-1)+\varepsilon(q,N)\bigr)\frac{N^{2}}{\varphi(q)},

where

(5.6)

\varepsilon(q,N)\ll(1-\beta_{1})\log N+(\log N)^{-1}

if
N\geqslant q^{b+1}
. The implied constant is absolute.

6.
A model of
S(q)

In this section we consider the a model for
S(q)
to be
given by (
2.1
).
Although our computations in this section, as for those earlier, are
unconditional, the closeness of the model to the sum
S(q)
is not.
That will rest on the assumption in the next section.

The Hardy-Littlewood conjecture for
the Goldbach problem asserts that, for
n
even,

(6.1)

G(n)\sim{\mathfrak{S}}(n)n,

where

(6.2)

{\mathfrak{S}}(n)=2CH(n),

and, in turn,
C
is the positive absolute constant

(6.3)

C=\prod_{p>2}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{(p-1)^{2}}\Bigr),

and
H(n)
is the multiplicative function

(6.4)

H(n)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p|n\\
p>2\end{subarray}}\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{(p-2)}\Bigr).

LEMMA 6.1
.

We have

(6.5)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\equiv 0(\mathop{\rm{mod}}q)\\
n\,\,{\rm even}\end{subarray}}{\mathfrak{S}}(n)ne^{-n/N}=\frac{N^{2}}{\varphi(q)}\Bigl(1+O\Bigl(\frac{q}{N}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Bigr).

Proof.

Pulling out the constant factor
2C
we evaluate the sum

(6.6)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\equiv 0(\mathop{\rm{mod}}q)\\
n\,\,{\rm even}\end{subarray}}H(n)ne^{-n/N}=\frac{N}{2\pi i}\int_{(2)}\Gamma(s+1)N^{s}Z_{q}(s)ds

where

\ Z_{q}(s)=\sum_{n\equiv 0(\mathop{\rm{mod}}k)}H(n)n^{-s}

and
k=[2,q]=2q/(2,q)
. We have

\displaystyle Z_{q}(s)

\displaystyle=H(k)k^{-s}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p|\ell\\
p\nmid k\end{subarray}}\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{p-2}\Bigr)\ell^{-s}

\displaystyle=H(k)k^{-s}\prod_{p|k}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{p^{s}}\Bigr)\prod_{p\nmid k}\Bigl(1+\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{p-2}\Bigr)\frac{1}{p^{s}}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{p^{s}}\Bigr)^{-1}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=H(k)k^{-s}\zeta(s)\prod_{p\nmid k}\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{p^{s}(p-2)}\Bigr).

This has a simple pole at
s=1
with residue

(6.7)

{\rm res}_{s=1}Z_{q}(s)=\frac{H(k)}{k}\prod_{p\nmid k}\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{p(p-2)}\Bigr)=\frac{1}{2C\varphi(q)}.

Moving the line of integration in (
6.6
) to

{\rm Re\,s}=\frac{1}{2}
, we obtain (
6.5
).

∎

7.
Completion

In this section we begin by completing our second estimation for
S(q)
by
comparing its model. In this we need to draw on an unproved
assumption. We assume the following.

Weak Hardy-Littlewood-Goldbach Conjecture
:
For all
sufficiently large even
n
we have

(7.1)

\delta{\mathfrak{S}}(n)n<G(n)<(2-\delta){\mathfrak{S}}(n)n,

where

0<\delta<1

is fixed.

From (
7.1
) and Lemma 6.1 we see that, with
N\geqslant q^{2}
,

(7.2)

\delta\frac{N^{2}}{\varphi(q)}\Bigl(1+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log N}\Bigr)\Bigr)<S(q)<(2-\delta)\frac{N^{2}}{\varphi(q)}\Bigl(1+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log N}\Bigr)\Bigr).

We now combine our two estimations for
S(q)
. By (
7.2
)
and (
5.5
) we have

(7.3)

\delta<1+\chi_{1}(-1)+\varepsilon(q,N)<2-\delta

if
N\geqslant q^{b+1}
, where (a now slightly different)
\varepsilon(q,N)

still satisfies (
5.6
). Taking the constant
c
in (
4.1
)
to be sufficiently small, we see from (
5.6
) that

(7.4)

|\varepsilon(q,N)|\leqslant\tfrac{\delta}{2}.

Since
1+\chi_{1}(-1)=0
or
=2
, this is not possible.

Conclusion
: The assumption (
7.1
) with some positive
\delta

implies that there is a real zero-free interval
s\geqslant 1-c(\delta)/\log q
,
(and hence a zero-free region in the plane

\sigma\geqslant 1-c(\delta)/\log q(|t|+2)

for all
L(s,\chi)
to all moduli
q
). In the case that
\chi_{1}(-1)=1
only
the upper bound in (
7.1
) is required and in the other case,
that
\chi_{1}(-1)=-1
, only the lower bound is required.
