A characterization of maximal homogeneous-quadratic-free sets An extended abstract of this article appeared at IPCO 2023 MPS2023 . This full version contains many new results, including a resolution of a conjecture posed in the extended abstract.
Gonzalo Muñoz, Joseph Paat, Felipe Serrano

Abstract
The intersection cut framework was introduced by Balas in 1971 as a method for generating cutting planes in integer optimization. In this framework, one uses a full-dimensional convex S S -free set, where S S is the feasible region of the integer program, to derive a cut separating S S from a non-integral vertex of a linear relaxation of S S . Among all S S -free sets, it is the inclusion-wise maximal ones that yield the strongest cuts. Recently, this framework has been extended beyond the integer case in order to obtain cutting planes in non-linear settings. In this work, we consider the specific setting when S S is defined by a homogeneous quadratic inequality. In this ‘quadratic-free’ setting, every function Γ : D m → D n \Gamma:D^{m}\to D^{n} , where D k D^{k} is the unit disk in ℝ k \mathbb{R}^{k} , generates a representation of a quadratic-free set. While not every Γ \Gamma generates a maximal quadratic free set, it is the case that every full-dimensional maximal quadratic free set is generated by some Γ \Gamma . Our main result shows that the corresponding quadratic-free set is full-dimensional and maximal if and only if Γ \Gamma is non-expansive and satisfies a technical condition. This result yields a broader class of maximal S S -free sets than previously known. Our result stems from a new characterization of maximal S S -free sets (for general S S beyond the quadratic setting) based on sequences that ‘expose’ inequalities defining the S S -free set. Keywords. Quadratic programming, cutting planes, intersection cuts, S S -free sets

Introduction

Given a closed set

1.1
Contributions

In order to study full-dimensional
Q
-free sets, we consider a special family of sets parameterized by functions of the form
\Gamma:D^{m}\to D^{n}
.
Given such a
\Gamma
, define the set

C_{\Gamma}:=\{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}:\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta})^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{y}\geq 0\penalty\ \penalty\ \forall\penalty\ \boldsymbol{\beta}\in D^{m}\}.

(2)

Sets of this form play an important role for us because for every
\Gamma
, the set
C_{\Gamma}
is
Q
-free.
Indeed, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality any
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
in the interior of
C_{\Gamma}
satisfies
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{y}<\|\boldsymbol{x}\|
for all
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in D^{m}
which implies that
\|\boldsymbol{y}\|<\|\boldsymbol{x}\|
.
We have observed the following:

Remark 1
(Every
C_{\Gamma}
is
Q
-free)

Let
\Gamma:D^{m}\to D^{n}
and
C_{\Gamma}
be defined as in (
2
).
The set
C_{\Gamma}
is closed, convex and
Q
-free.

Moreover, not only is
C_{\Gamma}

Q
-free for every
\Gamma
, but interestingly every full-dimensional maximal
Q
-free set can be written in the ‘standard form’ (
2
).
This constitutes our first main result.

Theorem 1.1
(Standard form is necessary for maximality)

Let
C
be a full-dimensional closed convex maximal
Q
-free set.
There exists a function
\Gamma:D^{m}\to D^{n}
such that
C=C_{\Gamma}
.

Remark 2

(Lower-dimensional maximal
Q
-free sets are always hyperplanes)

As with many of our results, Theorem
1.1
focuses on sets that are full-dimensional.
One reason why we emphasize full-dimensionality is because lower-dimensional maximal
Q
-free sets are necessarily hyperplanes.
Indeed, a lower-dimensional maximal
Q
-free sets is contained in a hyperplane
H
, which has empty interior and is therefore trivially
Q
-free.

In light of Theorem
1.1
, the question of characterizing full-dimensional maximal
Q
-free sets can be reduced to characterizing the functions
\Gamma
corresponding to maximal
C_{\Gamma}
.
We demonstrate that a meaningful property in this direction is the ‘expansivity’ of
\Gamma
.

Definition 1

(Expansive, Non-expansive, isometric, strictly non-expansive)

Let
\Gamma:D^{m}\to D^{n}
.
Two points
\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}\in D^{m}
are

•

expansive
if
\|\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta})-\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})\|>\|\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}\|
.

•

isometric
if
\|\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta})-\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})\|=\|\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}\|
.

•

non-expansive
if
\|\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta})-\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})\|\leq\|\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}\|
.

•

strictly non-expansive

\|\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta})-\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})\|<\|\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}\|
.

The function
\Gamma
is
non-expansive
(respectively,
strictly non-expansive
) if every pair of distinct points
\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}\in D^{m}
are non-expansive (respectively, strictly non-expansive).

Observe that for a function
\Gamma:D^{m}\to D^{n}
, two points
\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}\in D^{m}
are non-expansive (respectively, expansive, isometric, or strictly non-expansive) if and only if
\boldsymbol{\beta}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}\leq\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta})^{\mathsf{T}}\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})
(respectively,
\boldsymbol{\beta}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}>\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta})^{\mathsf{T}}\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})
,
\boldsymbol{\beta}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}=\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta})^{\mathsf{T}}\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})
, or
\boldsymbol{\beta}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}<\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta})^{\mathsf{T}}\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})
) because
\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}\in D^{m}
and
\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}),\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})\in D^{n}
.

Remark 3

The reader may be familiar with the notion of a
contracting
function.
The function
\Gamma
is contracting if there exists
L<1
such that

\|\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{1})-\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{2})\|\leq L\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{2}\|\quad\forall\ \boldsymbol{\beta}^{1},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{2}\in D^{m}.

If
\Gamma
is contracting, then it is strictly non-expansive.
However, the converse is not true.
Consider
n=m=2
and define
\Gamma
using polar coordinates: for
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
, we define
\gamma(\theta)=-\theta(\theta-2\pi)/(4\pi)
and define
\Gamma
such that

\beta(\theta):=(\cos(\theta),\sin(\theta))\mapsto\Gamma(\beta(\theta)):=(\cos(\gamma(\theta)),\sin(\gamma(\theta))).

\Gamma
is strictly non-expansive; we omit a full technical proof of this, but we illustrate this fact in Figure
1
.
Let us now argue that
\Gamma
is not contracting.
Take
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{1}=(1,0)
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{2}(\theta)=(\cos(\theta),\sin(\theta))
.
Note that
\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{1})=(1,0)
and thus

\lim_{\theta\to 0}\frac{\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{1})^{\mathsf{T}}\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{2})}{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{1}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{2}}=\lim_{\theta\to 0}\frac{\cos(-\theta(\theta-2\pi)/(4\pi))}{\cos(\theta)}=1.

This implies
\lim_{\theta\to 0}\|\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{1})-\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{2})\|/\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{2}\|=1
, and thus
\Gamma
cannot be contracting.

Figure 1
:
Plot of
\beta(\theta_{1})^{\mathsf{T}}\beta(\theta_{2})-\Gamma(\beta(\theta_{1}))^{\mathsf{T}}\Gamma(\beta(\theta_{2}))
for
\Gamma
defined in Remark
3
. This quantity is non-positive and takes the value 0 only when
\theta_{1}=\theta_{2}
(mod
2\pi
), which shows
\Gamma
is strictly non-expansive.

Our second main result characterizes functions
\Gamma
that yield full-dimensional
Q
-free sets.

Theorem 1.2
(Characterizing maximality of
C_{\Gamma}
)

Let
\Gamma:D^{m}\to D^{n}
and define
C_{\Gamma}
as in (
2
).
The set
C_{\Gamma}
is a full-dimensional maximal
Q
-free set if and only if
\Gamma
is non-expansive and
(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{0})\not\in\conv(\{(\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}),-\boldsymbol{\beta})\,:\ \boldsymbol{\beta}\in D^{m}\})
.

The backwards direction in Theorem
1.2
generalizes the work in
(
MPS2023
, Theorem 3)
, where the additional assumption that
C_{\Gamma}
is polyhedral is needed.
Furthermore, the result settles the conjecture stated prior to Theorem 3 in their work.
The forward direction in Theorem
1.2
is new to this work, and it allows for a complete characterization of maximality.
Note that Theorem
1.2
implies that a discontinuous
\Gamma
cannot yield a maximal
Q
-free set.

For the case when
\Gamma
is strictly non-expansive, we can prove that
C_{\Gamma}
is a full-dimensional maximal
Q
-free set without
any extra condition.
Indeed, if
\Gamma
is strictly non-expansive, then for each
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}
, the point
(\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}),\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})
strictly satisfies every defining inequality for
C_{\Gamma}
except
\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}){}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{y}\geq 0
; the set
\{(\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}),\boldsymbol{\beta}):\ \boldsymbol{\beta}\in D^{m}\}
is compact because
\Gamma
is continuous; thus, there is some
\delta>0
such that
\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}){}^{\top}\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})-\boldsymbol{\beta}{}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}\geq\delta
; hence, the midpoint of distinct points
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime\prime}\in D^{m}
satisfies all inequalities strictly and with some slack; thus,
C_{\Gamma}
is full-dimensional.
In Lemma
2
we show that if
C_{\Gamma}
is full-dimensional and
\Gamma
is continuous, then
(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{0})\not\in\conv(\{(\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}),-\boldsymbol{\beta})\,:\ \boldsymbol{\beta}\in D^{m}\})
.
Together with Theorem
1.2
, we have arrived at the following corollary.

Corollary 1
(Strictly non-expansive implies full-dimensionality)

Let
\Gamma:D^{m}\to D^{n}
and define
C_{\Gamma}
as in (
2
).
If
\Gamma
is strictly non-expansive, then
C_{\Gamma}
is a full-dimensional maximal
Q
-free set.

Corollary
1
generalizes
(
MS2022
, Theorem 2.1)
, where
\Gamma
is a constant function.
Example
1
in Section
2
illustrates the construction of a maximal
Q
-free set using a non-constant
\Gamma
function that is strictly non-expansive.

Theorem 1.3
(A characterization of polyhedrality)

Let

Moreover,
C_{\Gamma}=\{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}:\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta})^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{y}\geq 0\penalty\ \penalty\ \forall\penalty\ \boldsymbol{\beta}\in I\}
.

Examples

Underlying the proof of Theorem
1.2
is our final main result, which is a general characterization of maximality of
S
-free sets.
We turn once again to the case when
S
is a lattice to motivate this result.
Lovász proves that if
C
is maximal
S
-free, then
C
is a polyhedron and every facet
F
of

contains a lattice point
\boldsymbol{z}^{F}
in its relative interior.
The point
\boldsymbol{z}^{F}
is similar to a ‘blocking point’ used to generate maximal
S
-free sets when

is a mixed-integer set through lifting

;
CCZ2011
;

In order for

to be

must be separated from

by a facet defining inequality.
In fact,

is the unique facet separating
\boldsymbol{z}^{F}
from

; thus, in a way
\boldsymbol{z}^{F}
‘exposes’

.
The notion of exposing points is considered by Muñoz and Serrano

-free set

has an exposing point, then

is maximal.
However, there are maximal
Q
-free sets defined by inequalities that do not have exposing points; see Example
3
in Section
2
.
A generalization of this is the notion of an exposing sequence.

Definition 2
(Exposing sequence)

Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a convex set and let
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{x}\leq\alpha_{0}
, with
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
, be a valid inequality for
C
.
A sequence
(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an
exposing sequence
for
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{x}\leq\alpha_{0}
if
\lim_{t\to\infty}(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{t},{\delta_{0}^{t}})=(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\alpha_{0})
for every sequence
((\boldsymbol{\delta}^{t},{\delta^{t}_{0}}))_{t=1}^{\infty}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
such that
\|\boldsymbol{\delta}^{t}\|=\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|
,
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{t}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{x}\leq\delta^{t}_{0}
is a valid inequality for
C
, and
{\boldsymbol{\delta}^{t}}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{x}^{t}\geq{\delta^{t}_{0}}
for each
t
.

Remark 4

Muñoz and Serrano
MS2022
define a notion of ‘exposing sequence at infinity’.
This is more restrictive than Definition
2
.
Moreover, it can be shown that an exposing sequence at infinity reduces to an exposing point for

is a cone.

Theorem 1.4

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be closed, and let
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a closed convex full-dimensional

-free set.

C
is a maximal
S
-free set if and only if there exists a set

such that

C=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{x}\leq\alpha_{0}\penalty\ \penalty\ \forall\ (\boldsymbol{\alpha},\alpha_{0})\in I\}

and each
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\alpha_{0})\in I
has an exposing sequence
(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}
in
S
.

Lemma 5

Let
\Gamma:D^{m}\to D^{n}
be non-expansive.
Let
I\subseteq D^{m}
be a finite set of pairwise isometric points.
The following properties hold true:

1.

If
\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in I}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\boldsymbol{\beta}\in D^{m}
, where
\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\geq 0
for each
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in I
, then
\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in I}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta})\in D^{n}
and
\Gamma(\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in I}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\boldsymbol{\beta})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in I}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta})
.

2.

If
\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in I}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta})\in D^{n}
, where
\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\geq 0
for each
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in I
, then
\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in I}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\boldsymbol{\beta}\in D^{m}
and
\Gamma(\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in I}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\boldsymbol{\beta})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in I}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta})
.

Proof

Set
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}:=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in I}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\boldsymbol{\beta}
.
Using the isometry of points in
I
, we have

\|\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in I}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta})\|^{2}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}\in I}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}}\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}){}^{\mathsf{T}}\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})\\
=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}\in I}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}=\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|^{2}.

In the case of Property

1
, we assume
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in D^{m}
, so the previous equation proves that
\|\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in I}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta})\|=1
.
In the case of Property

2
, we assume
\|\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in I}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta})\|^{2}=1
, so the previous equation proves that
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in D^{m}
.
Therefore, it remains to show that

\Gamma(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in I}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta})
in both cases; we
prove these simultaneously.

Using the non-expansive property of
\Gamma
and the nonnegativity of
\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, we have

1=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in I}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{\beta}\leq\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in I}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\Gamma(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})^{\mathsf{T}}\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\Gamma(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})^{\mathsf{T}}\big(\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in I}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta})\big).

The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality implies that
1=\Gamma(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})^{\mathsf{T}}(\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in I}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}))
.
Since both vectors have unit norm, we conclude that
\Gamma(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in I}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta})
.

Our final lemma states that if an inequality
\Gamma(\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}})^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{x}-\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{y}\geq 0
is implied by other inequalities of the same form indexed by
I\subseteq D^{m}
, then
\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
must be isometric with
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in I
.
This will be used in the proof of Theorem
1.3
to help establish that we have a covering of
D^{m}
by isometric points.

Lemma 6

Let
\Gamma:D^{m}\to D^{n}
be non-expansive.
Let
\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in D^{m}
, let
I\subseteq D^{m}
be a finite set, and let

are isometric for each
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in I

Proof

Notice that

\begin{array}[]{rclclcl}0&=&\displaystyle\Gamma(\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}})^{\mathsf{T}}\Gamma(\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}})-\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}}&=&\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in I}\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta})\right){}^{\mathsf{T}}\Gamma(\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}})-\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in I}\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\boldsymbol{\beta}\right){}^{\mathsf{T}}\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\\[21.33955pt]

0\geq\sqrt{2t+1}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n+m}\lambda_{[i,t]}\left((\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{[i,t]}){}{}^{\top}\overline{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{[i,t]}{}^{\top}\overline{\boldsymbol{y}}\right)\right]-\tau\cdot 4\Delta.

(11)

Now, suppose there exists some
i\in\{1,\dotsc,n+m\}
for which
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{i}
is not equal to
\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
and
\lambda_{i}>0
.
According to (
6
), we then have
\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{i}){}{}^{\top}\overline{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{i}{}{}^{\top}\overline{\boldsymbol{y}}>0
.
Under this assumption, the right-hand side of (
11
) is lower bounded by
\sqrt{2t+1}\cdot\lambda_{[i,t]}\cdot(\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{[i,t]}){}{}^{\top}\overline{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{[i,t]}{}{}^{\top}\overline{\boldsymbol{y}})-\tau\cdot 4\Delta
, which tends to
\infty
as
t\to\infty
.
However, this contradicts the inequality in (
11
).
So, if
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{i}
is not equal to
\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, then
\lambda_{i}=0
.

In conclusion, if
\lambda_{i}>0
, then
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{i}=\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
.
Hence,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n+m}\lambda_{[i,t]}\left(\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{[i,t]}),\boldsymbol{\beta}^{[i,t]}\right)=\left(\Gamma(\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}}),\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right),

which demonstrates that
((\boldsymbol{x}^{t},\boldsymbol{y}^{t}))_{t=\overline{t}}^{\infty}
is an exposing sequence for
\Gamma(\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}})^{\top}{\boldsymbol{x}}-\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}}{}{}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{y}}\geq 0
.

6.2
A necessary condition for maximality

In this section, we prove the following result.

Lemma 8

Let
\Gamma:D^{m}\to D^{n}
and define
C_{\Gamma}
as in (
2
).
If
C_{\Gamma}
is a full-dimensional maximal
Q
-free set, then
\Gamma
is non-expansive and
(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{0})\not\in\conv(\{(\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}),-\boldsymbol{\beta})\ :\boldsymbol{\beta}\in D^{m}\})
.

Proof

Let us first prove that
\Gamma
is non-expansive.
By contradiction, assume
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{1},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{2}\in D^{m}
are such that

\|\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{1})-\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{2})\|>\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{2}\|\Leftrightarrow\boldsymbol{\beta}^{1}{}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{2}>\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{1})^{\top}\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{2})

This implies that
(\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{1}),\boldsymbol{\beta}^{1})\not\in C_{\Gamma}
.
Define the convex cone

C^{\prime}:=C_{\Gamma}+\cone((\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{1}),\boldsymbol{\beta}^{1})),

where the addition is the Minkowski sum.
We claim
C^{\prime}
is
Q
-free and strictly contains
C_{\Gamma}
.

The fact that
C_{\Gamma}\subsetneq C^{\prime}
follows because
(\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{1}),\boldsymbol{\beta}^{1})\in C^{\prime}\setminus C_{\Gamma}
.
To prove that
C^{\prime}
is
Q
-free, we show that if
(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}})\in\intr(C^{\prime})
, then
(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}})\not\in Q
.
Given
(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}})\in\intr(C^{\prime})
, we can write

(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}})=(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})+\lambda(\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{1}),\boldsymbol{\beta}^{1})

for some
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\in\intr(C_{\Gamma})
(as
C_{\Gamma}
is full-dimensional) and
\lambda>0
(see
(
R1970
, Corollary 6.6.2)
).
From this,

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}\|^{2}=\|\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2}+\lambda^{2}+2\lambda\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{1})^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}\geq

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2}+\lambda^{2}+2\lambda\boldsymbol{\beta}^{1}{}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{y}\|^{2}+\lambda^{2}+2\lambda\boldsymbol{\beta}^{1}{}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}}\|^{2},

where the first inequality holds because
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\in C_{\Gamma}
and the second inequality holds because
C_{\Gamma}
is
Q
-free.
Therefore,
(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}})\not\in Q
and
C^{\prime}
is
Q
-free.
However, this contradicts the maximality of
C_{\Gamma}
.

Now that we have established that
\Gamma
is non-expansive, we see that it is continuous.
Thus, we can use Lemma
2
to conclude that
(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{0})\not\in\conv(\{(\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}),-\boldsymbol{\beta})\ :\boldsymbol{\beta}\in D^{m}\})
.

7
A proof of Theorem
1.3

(\Leftarrow)
We show that if
\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in D^{m}\setminus I
, then
\Gamma(\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}}){}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{x}-\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{y}\geq 0
is implied by the inequalities indexed by
I
.
Let
\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in D^{m}\setminus I
.
By assumption, there exists a set
J\subseteq I
of pairwise isometric points satisfying
\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in\cone(J)
.
Hence, there exist
\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\geq 0
for each
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in J
such that
\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in J}\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\boldsymbol{\beta}
.
We have
\Gamma(\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in J}\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta})
by Lemma
5
.
This shows that
(\Gamma(\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}}),-\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\in\cone(\{(\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}),-\boldsymbol{\beta}):\boldsymbol{\beta}\in J\})
.
Hence,
\Gamma(\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}})^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{x}-\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{y}\geq 0
is implied by the inequalities indexed by
I
.

(\Rightarrow)

C_{\Gamma}
is a polyhedron, so by Lemma
3
there is a finite representation

C_{\Gamma}=\left\{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ \Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta})^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{x}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{y}\geq 0\penalty\ \penalty\ \forall\ \boldsymbol{\beta}\in I\right\}.

Let
\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in D^{m}\setminus I
.
The inequality
\Gamma(\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}})^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{x}-\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{y}\geq 0
is valid for
C_{\Gamma}
, so there exists a set
J\subseteq I
and positive coefficients
\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
for each
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in J
such that

(\Gamma(\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}}),\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in J}\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}),\boldsymbol{\beta}).

Lemma
6
states that

\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{\beta}=\Gamma(\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}})^{\mathsf{T}}\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta})

for all
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in J
.
For each
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}\in J
, we have

\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})^{\mathsf{T}}\Gamma(\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}})-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in J}\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})^{\mathsf{T}}\Gamma({\boldsymbol{\beta}})-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}{}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}).

The left-hand side is 0 because
\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}
are isometric, and every summand on the right-hand side is nonnegative because
\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}>0
and
\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})^{\mathsf{T}}\Gamma({\boldsymbol{\beta}})-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}{}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\geq 0
by the non-expansive property of
\Gamma
.
Hence,
\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})^{\mathsf{T}}\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}{}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
for all
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in J
.
As
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\prime}
was arbitrarily chosen in
J
, we see that all elements of
J
are pairwise isometric and
\overline{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in\cone(J)
.

8
Future Work

Even though we have fully characterized full-dimensional maximal
Q
-free sets, there are multiple lines of future work on maximal quadratic-free sets.

One future line of work we consider interesting is related to maximal polyhedral
Q
-free sets.
Theorem
1.3
characterizes when
C_{\Gamma}
is polyhedral;
loosely speaking, it reduces polyhedrality to finding a set of isometric points that cover
D^{m}
.
With this in mind, one may consider whether an arbitrary finite set of points in
D^{m}
can be extended to an ‘isometric cover’ for some function
\Gamma
.
The following example illustrates that this is not always possible.

Example 5

Set
I:=\{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{1},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{2},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{3},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{4}\}\subseteq D^{2}
, where

\boldsymbol{\beta}^{1}:=\big(0,1\big),\penalty\ \boldsymbol{\beta}^{2}:=\big(1,0\big),\penalty\ \boldsymbol{\beta}^{3}:=\big(0,-1\big),\penalty\ \text{and}\penalty\ \boldsymbol{\beta}^{4}:=\big(-\sqrt{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}},-\sqrt{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}\big).

The points are drawn in the next figure.

\boldsymbol{\beta}^{1}
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{2}
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{3}
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{4}

We claim that the set
I
cannot be used in Theorem
1.3
to define a non-expansive map
\Gamma
that corresponds to a full-dimensional
Q
-free polyhedron
C_{\Gamma}
.
Indeed, if
I
could be used to construct such a
\Gamma
, then the
I
must cover
D^{2}
with isometric pairs of points, that is, each consecutive pair of
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{i}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{i+1}
in
I
must be isometric.
Thus, the image of the arc
(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{1},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{2})
under
\Gamma
must be a rotation of the red arc; similarly the image of the purple arc under
\Gamma
must be a rotation of the purple arc.
Given that
C_{\Gamma}
is full-dimensional, we then conclude that
\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{1})=\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{3})
otherwise we would have
(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{0})\in\conv(\{(\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}),-\boldsymbol{\beta})\,:\ \boldsymbol{\beta}\in D^{2}\})
and contradict Theorem
1.2
.
However, the blue (respectively, the green) arc must be mapped by
\Gamma
to an arc of the same length.
The equation
\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{1})=\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{3})
then shows that the blue and green arcs must have the same length, which is not true.

We believe that finding a characterization of when a set of points in
D^{m}
can be used to define a maximal
Q
-free polyhedron is an important follow-up question in the understanding of maximal
Q
-free sets.

Beyond the homogeneous setting, it is interesting and potentially impactful to consider the non-homogeneous case.
For instance, which maximal sets in the homogeneous setting are maximal for the non-homogeneous setting, or how can one lift maximal
Q
-free sets to handle the non-homogeneous setting.
