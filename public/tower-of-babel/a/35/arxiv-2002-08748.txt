On the Uniqueness of Simultaneous Rational Function Reconstruction Conference: International Symposium on Symbolic and Algebraic Computation; 20-23 July 2020; Kalamata, Messinia, Greece.
Eleonora Guerrini, Romain Lebreton, Ilaria Zappatore

Abstract
. This paper focuses on the problem of reconstructing a vector of rational functions given some evaluations, or more generally given their remainders modulo different polynomials. The special case of rational functions sharing the same denominator, a.k.a. Simultaneous Rational Function Reconstruction (SRFR), has many applications from linear system solving to coding theory, provided that SRFR has a unique solution. The number of unknowns in SRFR is smaller than for a general vector of rational function. This allows to reduce the number of evaluation points needed to guarantee the existence of a solution, but we may lose its uniqueness. In this work, we prove that uniqueness is guaranteed for a generic instance.



1.
Introduction

The Vector rational function reconstruction (VRFR) is the problem of finding all rational functions
\boldsymbol{v}/\boldsymbol{d}=(v_{1}/d_{1},\dots,v_{n}/d_{n})
which satisfy some degree constraints, given a certain number of their evaluations
(\boldsymbol{v}/\boldsymbol{d})(\alpha_{j})=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}
. We consider a generalized version of this problem, where we suppose to know the images modulo different polynomials
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
,
i.e.

u_{i}=v_{i}/d\bmod a_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq n
.
The
Simultaneous Rational Function Reconstruction
(SRFR) problem is a
particular case of the vector rational function reconstruction where the rational functions
\boldsymbol{v}/d=(v_{1}/d,\dots,v_{n}/d)
share the same denominator (see Section
2.1
).
We can apply the SRFR in different problems: from the decoding of classic and interleaved Reed-Solomon codes to the polynomial linear system solving.
As in the classic rational function reconstruction we focus on the homogeneous linear system related to our equations in its weaker form,
i.e.

\boldsymbol{v}-d\boldsymbol{u}\equiv 0\bmod\boldsymbol{a}
.
If the number of equations is equal to the number of unknowns minus one then there always exists a non-trivial solution.
From now on, we will assume to be in this case.
Note that the common denominator constraint of SRFR implies less unknowns than general VRFR, so less equations.
This has a direct impact on the complexity of its applications.
However, the uniqueness in not anymore guarantied as shown in Counterexample
2.2
.
Having a unique solution is fundamental for decoding algorithms or Evaluation-Interpolation methods (like for instance in linear system solving). This paper focuses on the conditions that guarantee the uniqueness of solutions of the SRFR.

Previous works show that in the application of SRFR for polynomial linear system solving, the uniqueness is ensured under some specific degree conditions
(
OS 07
)
.
We have reasons to believe that we can generalize this result: we
conjecture that for almost all
(\boldsymbol{v},d)
the SRFR problem admits a unique solution (see Conjecture
2.5
).

We can learn more about the conditions of uniqueness from the results coming from error correcting codes. Interleaved Reed Solomon Codes (IRS) can be seen as the evaluation of a vector of polynomials
\boldsymbol{v}
. The problem of decoding IRS codes consists in the reconstruction of the vector of polynomials
\boldsymbol{v}
by its evaluations, some possibly erroneous.
A classic approach to decode IRS codes is the application of the SRFR for instances
\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{e}
where
\boldsymbol{e}
are the errors. Results from coding theory show that for all
\boldsymbol{v}
and almost all errors
\boldsymbol{e}
, we get the uniqueness of SRFR for the corresponding instance
\boldsymbol{u}
(provided that there are not too many errors)
(
BKY 03
;
BMS 04
;
SSB 09
)
. There is a natural generalization of SRFR when errors occur (SRFRwE, see Section
2.2
), which can be seen as fractional generalization of IRS
(
GLZ 19
;
GLZ 20
)
. We conjecture that we can decode almost all codeword
(\boldsymbol{v}/d)
and almost all errors
\boldsymbol{e}
of this fractional code (Conjecture
2.9
). In this paper we present a result which is a step towards this conjecture. We prove that uniqueness is guaranteed for a generic instance
\boldsymbol{u}
of SRFR, (Theorem
5.2
). Our result is valid not only given evaluations, but also in the general context of any moduli
a
.

Our approach to prove Theorem
5.2
is to study the degrees of a relation module. Solutions of SRFR are related to generators of a row reduced basis of this
\mathbb{K}[x]
-module which have a
negative shifted-row degree. Shifts are necessary to integrate degree constraints. We show that for generic instances, there is only one generator with negative row degree, hence uniqueness of the SRFR solution.

Previous works studied generic degrees of different but related modules:
e.g.

for the module of generating polynomials of a scalar matrix sequence
(
Vil 97
)
,
for the kernel module of a polynomial matrix and specific matrix dimensions
(
JV 05
)
. Both cases does not consider any shift. The generic degrees also appear in dimensions of blocks in a shifted Hessenberg form. However, the link with the degree of a module is unclear and no
shift is discussed (shifted Hessenberg is not related to our shift)
(
PS 07
)
.
We prove our result for any shift and any matrix dimension by adapting some of their techniques, and by proving that they apply to the specific relation modules related to SRFR.

In Section
2
we introduce the motivations of our work, started from the classic SRFR to the extended version with errors. We also show their respective applications in polynomial linear system solving and in error correcting algorithms.
In Section
3
, we
define the algebraic tools that we will use to prove our technical results of the Section
4
.
In Section
5
we explain how these results are linked to the uniqueness of the solution of the SRFR and we finally prove the Theorem
5.2
about the generic uniqueness.

2.
Motivations

2.1.
Rational Function Reconstruction

In this section we recall standard definitions and we state our problem, starting from rational function reconstruction and its application to linear algebra.
Let
\mathbb{K}
be a field,
a,u\in\mathbb{K}[x]
with
\deg(u)<\deg(a)
.
The
Rational Function Reconstruction
(shortly RFR) is the problem of reconstructing a rational function
v/d\in\mathbb{K}(x)
such that

(1)

{}\gcd(d,a)=1,\frac{v}{d}\equiv u\bmod a,\deg(v)<N,\deg(d)<D.

We focus on the weaker equation:

(2)

{}v\equiv du\bmod a,\deg(v)<N,\deg(d)<D.

The RFR problem generalizes many problems including the
Padé approximation
if
a=x^{f}
and the
Cauchy interpolation
if
a=\prod_{i=1}^{f}(x-\alpha_{i})
, where the
\alpha_{i}
are pairwise distinct elements of the field
\mathbb{K}
.
The homogeneous linear system related to the Equation (
2
) has

\deg(a)
equations and
N+D
unknowns.
If
\deg(a)=N+D-1
, the dimension of the solution space of Eq. (
2
) is
at least
1
and it always admits a non-trivial solution. Moreover, such a
solution is unique in the sense that all solutions are polynomial multiples of a
unique one,
(v_{\min},d_{\min})
(see
e.g.
(
GG 13
, Theorem
5.16)
). On the other hand, Equation (
1
) does
not always have a solution, but when a solution exists, it is unique. Indeed, it
is
v_{\min}/d_{\min}
and we can reconstruct it by the
Extended
Euclidean Algorithm
(EEA). Throughout this paper, we will focus on
Equation (
2
).

The RFR can be naturally extended to the vector case as follows.
Let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{K}[x]
with degrees
f_{i}=\deg(a_{i})
and
\boldsymbol{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})\in\mathbb{K}[x]^{n}
where
\deg(u_{i})<f_{i}
. Let
0<N_{i},D_{i}<f_{i}
. The
Vector Rational Function Reconstruction
(VRFR) is the problem of reconstructing
(v_{i},d_{i})
for
1\leq i\leq n
such that

v_{i}\equiv d_{i}u_{i}\bmod a_{i},\deg(v_{i})<N_{i},\deg(d_{i})<D_{i}.

We can apply the RFR componentwise and so, if
f_{i}=N_{i}+D_{i}-1
, we can uniquely reconstruct the solution.

Definition 2.1.

(SRFR)
Given
\boldsymbol{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})\in\mathbb{K}[x]^{n}
where
\deg(u_{i})<f_{i}
, and degree bounds
0<N_{i}<f_{i}
and
0<D<\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\{f_{i}\}
, we want to reconstruct the tuple
(\boldsymbol{v},d)=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n},d)
such that

(3)

{}v_{i}\equiv du_{i}\bmod a_{i},\deg(v_{i})<N_{i},\deg(d)<D.

We denote
\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{u}}
the set of solutions.

The SRFR is then the problem of reconstructing a vector of rational functions with the same denominator.
Therefore, if
f_{i}=N_{i}+D-1
for
1\leq i\leq n
, we can uniquely reconstruct the solution. In this case, the common denominator property allows to reduce the number of unknowns, with an impact on the degree of the
a_{i}
’s. In detail, the number of equations of (
3
) is
\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}
, while the number of the unknowns,
i.e.
the coefficients of
\boldsymbol{v}
and
d
, is
\sum_{i=1}^{n}N_{i}+D
.
If

(4)

{}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}N_{i}+D-1

then Equation (
3
) always admits a non-trivial solution. However, the uniqueness is not anymore guarantied.

Counterexample 2.2.

Let
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{F}_{11}
,
n=2
,
N_{1}=N_{2}=2
,
D=3
and

a_{1}=a_{2}=\prod_{i=1}^{3}(x-2^{i})=x^{3}+8x^{2}+x+2
. Let
\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{v}/d
with

\boldsymbol{v}=(2x+6,8x+2)
and
d=2x^{2}+2x+2
invertible modulo
a_{i}
. Then the
SRFR with instance
\boldsymbol{u}
has two
\mathbb{K}[x]
-linearly independent solutions

(d,\boldsymbol{v})=(4x^{2}+9x+10,0,0)
and
(d^{\prime},\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})=(8x+3,9x+5,3x+9)
.

Uniqueness is a central property for the applications of SRFR: unique decoding algorithms are essential in error correcting codes, and it is also a necessary condition to use evaluation interpolation techniques in computer algebra.
The study of the bound on the number of equations which guaranties the uniqueness of SRFR has also repercussion on the complexity. Indeed, the complexity of decoding algorithms or evaluation interpolation techniques depends on this number of equations. So decreasing this number has a direct impact on the complexity.

We denote by
s
the rank of the
\mathbb{K}[x]
-module spanned by the solutions
\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{u}}
. Therefore, all solutions can be written as a linear combination
\sum_{i=1}^{s}c_{i}p_{i}
of
s
polynomials
p_{i}
with polynomial coefficients
c_{i}
.
The case
s=1
corresponds to what we call uniqueness of the solution.
In
(
OS 07
)
, the authors studied the particular case where
a_{1}=\ldots=a_{n}=a
and
N_{1}=\ldots=N_{n}=N
.
They proved the following,

Theorem 2.3.

(
OS 07
, Theorem 4.2)

Let
k
be minimal such that
\deg(a)\geq N+(D-1)/k
, then the rank
s
of the solution space
\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{u}}
satisfies

s\leq k
.

Note that if
k=1
, the solution is always unique (
s=1
). This matches the uniqueness condition on the
\deg(a)
of VRFR. On the other hand, if
k=n
and
\deg(a)\geq N+(D-1)/n
then
s\leq n
which is always true. Hence in this case the theorem does not provide any new information about the solution space.
This theorem represents a connection between the classic bound on the
\deg(a)=N+D-1
which guaranties the uniqueness and the
ideal
one,
i.e.
\deg(a)=N+(D-1)/n
(see Equation (
4
)), which exploits the common denominator property.
They also proposed an algorithm that computes a complete basis of the solution space using
\mathcal{O}(nk^{\omega-1}B(\deg(a)))
operations in
\mathbb{K}

where

2\leq\omega\leq 3
is the exponent of the matrix multiplication and

B(t):=M(t)\log t
where
M
is the classic polynomial multiplication arithmetic complexity (see
(
GG 13
)
for instance).
In
(
RS 16
)
the complexity was improved. In particular, they introduced an algorithm that computes the solution space (in the general case of different moduli,
i.e.
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
) with complexity
\mathcal{O}(n^{\omega-1}B(f)\log(f/n)^{2})
where
f=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\{\deg(a_{i})\}
.

We now came back to general case of the SRFR. The main result of this work is to prove that when the degree constraints guarantee the existence of the solution, then for almost all
\boldsymbol{u}
we also get the uniqueness (see Theorem
5.2
).

Theorem 2.4.

If Equation (
4
) is satisfied, then for almost all
instances
\boldsymbol{u}
the SRFR admits a unique solution,
i.e.
it has rank
s=1
.

We will both use the expressions “almost all” or “generic”, meaning that there exists a polynomial
R
such that a certain property is true for all instances that do not cancel
R
. In our case, we state that there exists a polynomial
R
such that the SRFR admits a unique solution for all instances
\boldsymbol{u}
such that
R(\boldsymbol{u})\neq 0
.

The SRFR problem has a natural application in a linear algebra context.

Application to polynomial linear system solving

Suppose that we want to compute the solution of a full rank polynomial linear system,
\boldsymbol{y}(x)=A^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{K}(x)
where
A\in\mathbb{K}[x]^{n\times n}
and
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{K}[x]^{n\times 1}
, from its image modulo a polynomial
a(x)
. We will refer to this problem as
polynomial linear system solving
(shortly PLS).
We remark that, by the Cramer’s rule,
\boldsymbol{y}
is vector of rational functions with the same denominator:
PLS is then a special case of SRFR.
In
(
OS 07
)
, the authors proved that
the solution space is uniquely generated (
s=1
)
when
\deg(a)\geq N+(D-1)/n
in the special case of
D=N=n\deg(A)
and
\deg(A)=\deg(b)
. They exploited another bound on the degree of
a
based on
(
Cab 71
)
.

In view of Theorem
2.4
and as our experiments suggest, we
could hope for the following,

Conjecture 2.5.

If Equation (
4
) is satisfied then for almost all
(\boldsymbol{v},d)
with
\gcd(d,a_{i})=1
, the SRFR with
\boldsymbol{u}=\frac{\boldsymbol{v}}{d}
as input admits a unique solution.

Since we have proved the uniqueness for generic instances
\boldsymbol{u}
, it would be sufficient to show the existence of an instance
\boldsymbol{u}
of the form
\boldsymbol{v}/d
to prove the conjecture.

2.2.
Reconstruction with Errors

In this section we introduce the problem of the Simultaneous Rational Function with Errors (
(
BK 14
;
KPSW 17
;
GLZ 19
;
Per 14
;
GLZ 20
)
),
i.e.
the SRFR in a scenario where errors may occur in some evaluations.
Throughout this section we suppose that
\mathbb{K}
is a finite field of cardinality
q
, we fix
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{f}\}
pairwise distinct evaluation points in
\mathbb{K}
and we consider the polynomial
a=\prod_{i=1}^{f}(x-\alpha_{i})
.

Definition 2.6.

(SRFR with Errors)
Fix
0<N,D,\varepsilon<f\leq q
. An instance of the SRFR with errors
(SRFRwE) is a matrix
\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathbb{K}^{n\times f}
whose columns are

\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}=\boldsymbol{v}(\alpha_{j})/d(\alpha_{j})+\boldsymbol{e}_{j}
for some reduced

\boldsymbol{v}/d\in\mathbb{K}(x)^{n\times 1}
and some error matrix
\boldsymbol{e}
. The reduced vector must
satisfy
\deg(\boldsymbol{v})<N
,
\deg(d)<D
and
d(\alpha_{i})\neq 0
. The error matrix
must have its
error support

E:=\{1\leq j\leq f\mid\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\neq\boldsymbol{0}\}
which satisfies

|E|\leq\varepsilon
.

The solution of the SRFRwE instance
\boldsymbol{\omega}
is
(\boldsymbol{v},d)
.

SRFRwE as Reed-Solomon code decoding

We observe that if
n=1
and
D=1
,
\boldsymbol{v}/d
is a polynomial. Then the
SRFRwE is the problem of recovering a polynomial
v
given evaluations, some of
which possibly erroneous. So in this case, SRFRwE is the problem of decoding an
instance of a
Reed-Solomon code
.

Its vector generalization, that is
n>1
and
D=1
, coincides with the decoding
of an
homogeneous Interleaved Reed-Solomon (IRS) code
. Indeed, an IRS
codeword can be seen as the evaluation of a vector of polynomials
\boldsymbol{v}
on

\boldsymbol{\alpha}
. Thus decoding IRS codes is the problem of recovering
\boldsymbol{v}
from

\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}=\boldsymbol{v}(\alpha_{j})+\boldsymbol{e}_{j}
.

Let us now detail how we can solve SRFRwE using SRFR. We use the same technique
of decoding RS and IRS codes

(
BW 86
;
BKY 03
;
PR 17
)
. We
introduce the
Error Locator Polynomial

\Lambda=\prod_{j\in E}(x-\alpha_{j})
. Its roots are the erroneous evaluations so

\deg(\Lambda)=|E|\leq\varepsilon
. We consider the
Lagrangian polynomials

u_{i}\in\mathbb{K}[x]
such that
u_{i}(\alpha_{j})=\omega_{ij}
for any
1\leq i\leq n
. The classic approach is to remark that
(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\psi)=(\Lambda(x)\boldsymbol{v}(x),\Lambda(x)d(x))
is a solution of

(5)

{}\boldsymbol{\varphi}=\psi\boldsymbol{u}\bmod\prod_{i=1}^{f}(x-\alpha_{i}).

In order to reconstruct

(\boldsymbol{v},d)
it suffices to study the set of

(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\psi)
which verify Equation (
5
) and
such that
\deg(\boldsymbol{\varphi})<N+\varepsilon
and
\deg(\psi)<D+\varepsilon
. In this way we reduce SRFRwE to SRFR (see Eq.
3
).
Hence, if

f=(N+\varepsilon)+(D+\varepsilon)-1=N+D+2\varepsilon-1
we can uniquely
reconstruct every component of the vector
(
cf.

(
BK 14
;
KPSW 17
)
).

It is possible to reduce the number of evaluations w.r.t. the maximal number of
errors
\varepsilon
in the setting of IRS decoding (
D=1
).

Theorem 2.7 (
(
BKY 03
;
BMS 04
;
SSB 09
)
).

Fix
0<N,\varepsilon<f\leq q
and
E
such that
|E|\leq\varepsilon
. If

f=N-1+\varepsilon+\varepsilon/n
, then for all
(\boldsymbol{v},1)
and almost
all error matrices
\boldsymbol{e}
of support
E
, the SRFRwE admits a unique solution on
the instance
\boldsymbol{\omega}
where
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}=\boldsymbol{v}(\alpha_{j})/d(\alpha_{j})+\boldsymbol{e}_{j}
.

We prove a similar result in the rational function case,

Theorem 2.8 (
(
GLZ 19
;
GLZ 20
)
).

Fix
0<N,D,\varepsilon<f\leq q
and
E
such that
|E|\leq\varepsilon
. If

f=N+D-1+\varepsilon+\varepsilon/n
, then for all
(\boldsymbol{v},d)
and almost
all error matrices
\boldsymbol{e}
of support
E
, the SRFRwE admits a unique solution on
the instance
\boldsymbol{\omega}
where
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}=\boldsymbol{v}(\alpha_{j})/d(\alpha_{j})+\boldsymbol{e}_{j}
.

Since the problem of SRFRwE reduces to a simultaneous rational function
reconstruction, the Equation (
5
) always admits a nontrivial
solution whenever
f=N+\varepsilon+(D+\varepsilon-1)/n
. Our ideal result would
be to prove a uniqueness result also in this case. Our experiments suggest the
following,

Conjecture 2.9.

Fix
0<N,D,\varepsilon<f\leq q
and
E
such that
|E|\leq\varepsilon
. If

f=N+\varepsilon+(D+\varepsilon-1)/n
, then for almost all
(\boldsymbol{v},d)
and almost
all error matrices
\boldsymbol{e}
of support
E
, the SRFRwE admits a unique solution on
the instance
\boldsymbol{\omega}
where
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}=\boldsymbol{v}(\alpha_{j})/d(\alpha_{j})+\boldsymbol{e}_{j}
.

Note that Conjecture
2.5
is for almost all fractions
(\boldsymbol{v},d)

whereas Theorems
2.7
and
2.8
are for all fractions.
This difference is due to Counterexample
2.2
, which states that we
can not have uniqueness for all
(\boldsymbol{v},d)
when
f=N+(D-1)/n
. This latter number
of evaluations matches the one of Conjecture
2.5
in the situation
without errors
\varepsilon=0
. Remark that this obstruction does not affect
Theorems
2.7
and
2.8
because their number of
evaluations
f
becomes
N+D-1
when
\varepsilon=0
.

Our result Theorem
2.4
is a first step towards
Conjecture
2.5
: Since uniqueness of the SRFR is true generic
instance
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}
, it remains to prove the existence of an instance of the
form
\boldsymbol{v}(\alpha_{j})/d(\alpha_{j})+\boldsymbol{e}_{j}
for any
E
such that

|E|\leq\varepsilon
to prove the conjecture.

The SRFRwE was first introduced by
(
BK 14
)
in a special case of its application,
i.e.
the Polynomial Linear System Solving with Errors, that we will introduce in the following paragraph.

Polynomial linear system solving with errors

We now suppose that we want to compute the unique solution of a PLS

\boldsymbol{y}(x)=\boldsymbol{v}(x)/d(x)=A^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{K}[x]^{n\times n}
in a scenario where some errors
occur

(
BK 14
;
KPSW 17
;
GLZ 19
)
. In
detail, we fix
f
distinct evaluation points

\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{f}\}
such that
d(\alpha_{i})\neq 0
. In our
model, we suppose that there is a black box which for any evaluation point

\alpha_{i}
, gives a solution of the evaluated systems of linear equations,

i.e.
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}=A(\alpha_{i})^{-1}b(\alpha_{i})
. However, this black box could do some errors in the computations. In
particular, an evaluation
\alpha_{i}
is
erroneous
if

\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\neq\boldsymbol{v}(\alpha_{i})/d(\alpha_{i})
and we denote by

E:=\{i\mid\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\neq\boldsymbol{v}(\alpha_{i})/d(\alpha_{i})\}
the set of erroneous
positions. We refer to the problem of reconstructing the solution of a PLS in
this model of errors as
Polynomial Linear System Solving with Errors

(shortly PLSwE). We observe that if
i\in E
, then there exists a nonzero

\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\in\mathbb{K}^{n\times f}
such that

\boldsymbol{y}_{i}=\boldsymbol{v}(\alpha_{i})/d(\alpha_{i})+\boldsymbol{e}_{i}
. Hence, this problem is a special case of
SRFRwE. Here we want to reconstruct a vector of rational functions which is a
solution of a polynomial linear system. Therefore, all the results about uniqueness
of the previous sections hold. Furthermore, in
(
KPSW 17
)

authors introduced another bound which guaranties the uniqueness based on the
bounds on the degree of the polynomial matrix
A
and the vector
\boldsymbol{b}
.

3.
Preliminaries

In this section we will give some definitions and set out the notation that we
will use throughout this paper. We refer
to
(
Nei 16
)
for the definitions and lemmas of this section, and
for historical references.

3.1.
Row degrees of a
\mathbb{K}[x]
-module

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a field and
\mathbb{K}[x]
the ring of polynomials over
\mathbb{K}
. We start by
defining the row degree of a vector, then of a matrix. Let

\boldsymbol{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{\nu})\in\mathbb{K}[x]^{\nu}=\mathbb{K}[x]^{1\times\nu}
and

\boldsymbol{s}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{\nu})\in\mathbb{Z}^{\nu}
a shift.

Definition 3.1 (Shifted row degree).

Let
r_{i}=\deg(p_{i})+s_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq\nu
.
The
\boldsymbol{s}
-row degree
of
\boldsymbol{p}
is
\text{rdeg}_{\boldsymbol{s}}(p)=\max_{1\leq i\leq\nu}(r_{i})
.

We also denote
\boldsymbol{p}=([r_{1}]_{s_{1}},\ldots,[r_{\nu}]_{s_{\nu}})
a vector of polynomials where
r_{i}=\deg(p_{i})+s_{i}
.

We can extend this definition to polynomial matrices. In fact, let
P\in\mathbb{K}[x]^{\rho\times\nu}
be a polynomial matrix, with
\rho\leq\nu
.
Let
P_{j,*}
be the
j
-th row of
P
for
1\leq j\leq\rho
.
We can define the
\boldsymbol{s}
-row degrees
of the matrix
P
as
\text{rdeg}_{\boldsymbol{s}}(P):=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{\rho})
where
r_{j}:=\text{rdeg}_{\boldsymbol{s}}(P_{j,*})
.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be a
\mathbb{K}[x]
-submodule of
\mathbb{K}[x]^{\nu}=\mathbb{K}[x]^{1\times\nu}
. Since
\mathbb{K}[x]
is a
principal ideal domain,
\mathcal{N}
is
free
of
rank

\rho:=\text{rank}(\mathcal{N})
less than
\nu

(
DF 03
, Section 12.1, Theorem
4)
. Hence, we can consider a
basis

P\in\mathbb{K}[x]^{\rho\times\nu}
,
i.e.
a full rank polynomial matrix, such
that

\mathcal{N}=\mathbb{K}[x]^{1\times\rho}P=\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}P\mid\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\mathbb{K}[x]^{1\times\rho}\}
.

Our goal is to define a notion of row degrees of
\mathcal{N}
in order to study later
the
\mathbb{K}
-vector space

\mathcal{N}_{<r}:=\left\{\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathcal{N}\ \middle|\ \text{rdeg}_{\boldsymbol{s}}(\boldsymbol{p})<r\right\}

for some
r\in\mathbb{N}
. Different bases
P
of
\mathcal{N}
have different
\boldsymbol{s}
-row
degrees so we need more definitions. We start with row reduced bases.

Let
\boldsymbol{t}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{\nu})\in\mathbb{Z}^{\nu}
. We denote by
X^{\boldsymbol{t}}
a diagonal matrix whose entries are
x^{t_{1}},\ldots,x^{t_{\nu}}
.

Definition 3.2 (Shifted Leading Matrix).

The
\boldsymbol{s}
-leading matrix of
P
is a matrix in
\mathbb{K}^{\rho\times\nu}
, whose entries are the coefficient of degree zero of
X^{-\text{rdeg}_{\boldsymbol{s}}(P)}PX^{\boldsymbol{s}}
.

Definition 3.3.

(Row reduced basis)
A basis
P\in\mathbb{K}[x]^{\rho\times\nu}
of
\mathcal{N}
is
\boldsymbol{s}
-row reduced
(shortly
\boldsymbol{s}
-reduced) if its leading matrix
LM_{\boldsymbol{s}}(P)
has full rank.

This definition is equivalent to
(
Nei 16
, Definition 1.10)
,
which implies that all
\boldsymbol{s}
-reduced basis of
\mathcal{N}
have the same row degree, up
to permutation. We now focus on the following crucial property.

Proposition 3.4.

(Predictable degree property)

P
is
\boldsymbol{s}
-reduced if and only if for all
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{\rho})\in\mathbb{K}[x]^{1\times\rho}
,

\text{rdeg}_{\boldsymbol{s}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}P)=\max_{1\leq i\leq\rho}(\deg(\lambda_{i})+\text{rdeg}_{\boldsymbol{s}}(P_{i,*}))=\text{rdeg}_{\boldsymbol{d}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})

where
\boldsymbol{d}=\text{rdeg}_{\boldsymbol{s}}(P)
.

The proof of this classic proposition can be found for instance in

(
Nei 16
, Theorem 1.11)
. This latter proposition is useful because it implies that

\dim_{\mathbb{K}}\mathcal{N}_{<r}=\sum_{\{i|r_{i}<r\}}(r-r_{i})
where
(r_{1},\dots,r_{\rho})

is the
\boldsymbol{s}
-row degree of any
\boldsymbol{s}
-reduced basis of
\mathcal{N}
.

Since we will need to define the
\boldsymbol{s}
-row degrees of
\mathcal{N}
uniquely, not just
up to permutation, we need to introduce ordered weak Popov form, which relies on the notion of pivot.

Definition 3.5 (Pivot).

Let
\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathbb{K}[x]^{1\times\nu}
. The
\boldsymbol{s}
-pivot index
of
\boldsymbol{p}
is
\max\{j\ |\ \text{rdeg}_{\boldsymbol{s}}(\boldsymbol{p})=\deg(p_{j})+s_{j}\}
. Moreover the corresponding
p_{j}
is the
\boldsymbol{s}
-pivot entry
and
\deg(p_{j})
is the
\boldsymbol{s}
-pivot degree
of
\boldsymbol{p}
.

We can naturally extend the notion of pivot to polynomial matrices.

Definition 3.6.

((Ordered) weak Popov form)

The basis
P
of
\mathcal{N}
in
\boldsymbol{s}
-weak Popov form
if the
\boldsymbol{s}
-pivot indices of its rows are pairwise distinct. On the other hand, it is in
\boldsymbol{s}
-ordered weak Popov form
if the sequence of the
\boldsymbol{s}
-pivot indices of its rows is strictly increasing.

A basis in
\boldsymbol{s}
-weak Popov form is
\boldsymbol{s}
-reduced. Indeed,
LM_{\boldsymbol{s}}(P)
becomes,
up to row permutation, a lower triangular matrix with non-zero entries on the
diagonal. Hence it is full-rank.

Assume from now on that
\mathcal{N}
is a submodule of
\mathbb{K}[x]^{\nu}
of rank
\nu
and that

P
is a basis of
\mathcal{N}
in
\boldsymbol{s}
-ordered weak Popov form. Then its pivot indices
must be
\{1,\dots,\nu\}
.

Weak Popov bases have a strong degree
minimality property, stated in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.7 (
(
Nei 16
, Lemma 1.17)
).

Let
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\nu}
,
P
be a basis of
\mathcal{N}
in
\boldsymbol{s}
-weak Popov form with

\boldsymbol{s}
-pivot degrees
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{\nu})
. Let
\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathcal{N}
whose pivot
index is
1\leq i\leq\nu
. Then the
\boldsymbol{s}
-pivot degree of
\boldsymbol{p}
is

\geq d_{i}
or equivalently
\text{rdeg}_{\boldsymbol{s}}(\boldsymbol{p})\geq\text{rdeg}_{\boldsymbol{s}}(P_{i,*})
.

As it turns out, ordered weak Popov basis are reduced basis for which the
\boldsymbol{s}
-row degree is unique.
The following lemma is a
consequence of Lemma
3.7
.

Lemma 3.8 (
(
Nei 16
, Lemma 1.25)
).

Let
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\nu}
and assume
\mathcal{N}
is a submodule of
\mathbb{K}[x]^{\nu}
of rank

\nu
. Let
P
and
Q
be two bases of
\mathcal{N}
in
\boldsymbol{s}
-ordered weak Popov
form. Then
P
and
Q
have the same
\boldsymbol{s}
-row degrees and
\boldsymbol{s}
-pivot degrees.

3.2.
Link between pivot and leading term

In this section, we will focus on the relation between pivots of weak Popov
bases and leading terms w.r.t. a specific monomial order, as in Gröbner basis theory (see for instance

(
CLO 98
)
).

Let
\mathbb{K}[\boldsymbol{x}]:=\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
be the ring of multivariate polynomials.
Recall that a
monomial in

\mathbb{K}[\boldsymbol{x}]
is a product of powers of the
indeterminates
\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{i}}:=x^{i_{1}}_{1}\cdots x^{i_{n}}_{n}
for some

\boldsymbol{i}:=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
. On the other hand, a
monomial
in

\mathbb{K}[\boldsymbol{x}]^{n}
is
\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{i}}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{j}
, where
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}
is the
canonical basis of the
\mathbb{K}[\boldsymbol{x}]
-module
\mathbb{K}[\boldsymbol{x}]^{n}
.

A
monomial order
on
\mathbb{K}[\boldsymbol{x}]^{n}
is a
total order

\prec
on
the monomials of
\mathbb{K}[\boldsymbol{x}]^{n}
such that, for any monomials

\varphi\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\boldsymbol{i}},\psi\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathbb{K}[\boldsymbol{x}]^{n}
and any monomial
\tau\neq 1
,

\tau\in\mathbb{K}[\boldsymbol{x}]
,

\varphi\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\boldsymbol{i}}\prec\psi\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\Longrightarrow\varphi\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\boldsymbol{i}}\prec\tau\varphi\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\boldsymbol{i}}\prec\tau\psi\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\boldsymbol{j}}.

Given a
monomial order
\prec
on
\mathbb{K}[\boldsymbol{x}]^{n}
and
f\in\mathbb{K}[\boldsymbol{x}]^{n}
, the

\prec
-initial term

in_{\prec}(f)
of
f
is the term of
f
whose
monomial is the greatest with respect to the order
\prec
.
We remark that in the case of
\mathbb{K}[x]
, the only monomial order must be the natural degree order
x^{a}<x^{b}\Longleftrightarrow a<b
.

Definition 3.9.

(shifted-TOP order)
Let
\prec
be a monomial order on
\mathbb{K}[\boldsymbol{x}]
. We consider the
\mathbb{K}[\boldsymbol{x}]
-module
\mathbb{K}[\boldsymbol{x}]^{n}
with its canonical basis
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}
and let
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n}
be monomials in
\mathbb{K}[\boldsymbol{x}]
.
Then
\prec
induces the following monomial order on
\mathbb{K}[\boldsymbol{x}]^{n}
called
\boldsymbol{s}
-TOP (Term Over Position):

\varphi\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{i}\prec_{\boldsymbol{s}-TOP}\psi\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{j}\iff(\varphi\gamma_{i}\prec\psi\gamma_{j})\text{ or }(\varphi\gamma_{i}=\psi\gamma_{j}\text{ and }i<j)

for any pairs of monomials
\varphi\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{i}
and
\psi\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{j}
of
\mathbb{K}[\boldsymbol{x}]^{n}
.

As for the univariate module
\mathbb{K}[x]^{n}
, the only monomial order
\prec
on
\mathbb{K}[x]
is the
natural
one. The
shifting monomials
are
x^{s_{i}}
, defined by the shift
\boldsymbol{s}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
. Hence, the
\boldsymbol{s}
-TOP order on
\mathbb{K}[x]^{n}
is

(6)

{}x^{a}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{i}<_{\boldsymbol{s}\text{-TOP}}x^{b}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{j}\Longleftrightarrow(a+s_{i},i)\prec_{lex}(b+s_{j},j)

where
\prec_{lex}
is the
lexicographic order
on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

We can now state the link between this monomial order and the pivot’s definition: let
\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathbb{K}[x]^{1\times n}
and
in_{\prec_{\boldsymbol{s}\text{-TOP}}}(\boldsymbol{p})=\alpha x^{d}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{i}
be the
\prec_{\boldsymbol{s}-TOP}
-initial term of
\boldsymbol{p}
, then the
\boldsymbol{s}
-pivot index, entry, and degree are respectively
i
,
p_{i}
and
d
. This will be useful later on, in
e.g.
Proposition
4.3
.

4.
Row Degree of the Relation Module

Fix
m\geq n\geq 0
, and
M\in\mathbb{K}[x]^{m\times n}
.
We consider a
\mathbb{K}[x]
-submodule
\mathcal{M}
of
\mathbb{K}[x]^{n}
. We define the
\mathbb{K}[x]-
module homomorphism

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\hat{\varphi_{M}}:&\mathbb{K}[x]^{m}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{K}[x]^{n}/\mathcal{M}\\
&\boldsymbol{p}&\longmapsto&\boldsymbol{p}M\end{array}{}.

Set
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{M},M}:=\ker(\hat{\varphi_{M})}
to get the injection

\varphi_{M}:\mathbb{K}[x]^{m}/\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{M},M}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{K}[x]^{n}/\mathcal{M}.

We call
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{M},M}
the
relation module
because
p\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{M},M}\Leftrightarrow\varphi_{M}(\boldsymbol{p})=\boldsymbol{p}M=0\bmod\mathcal{M}
,
i.e.
\boldsymbol{p}
is a relation between rows of
M
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{m}
be the
canonical basis
of
\mathbb{K}[x]^{m}
,
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\prime}_{n}
the
canonical basis
of
\mathbb{K}[x]^{n}
and
\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\equiv\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{i}\bmod\mathbb{K}[x]^{m}/A_{\mathcal{M},M}
for
1\leq i\leq m
.

Remark 4.1
.

We observe that by the
Invariant Factor Form of modules over Principal Ideal Domains
(
cf.

(
DF 03
, Theorem 4, Chapter 12)
),

\mathcal{K}:=\mathbb{K}[x]^{n}/\mathcal{M}\simeq\mathbb{K}[x]^{n}/\left\langle a_{i}(x)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\prime}_{i}\right\rangle_{1\leq i\leq n}

for nonzeros
a_{i}(x)\in\mathbb{K}[x]
such that

a_{n}(x)|a_{n-1}(x)|\ldots|a_{1}(x)
.
The polynomials
a_{i}(x)
are the
invariants
of the module
\mathcal{M}
.
We also denote
f_{i}:=\deg(a_{i}(x))
and we observe that

f_{1}\geq f_{2}\geq\ldots\geq f_{n}
.

From now on we will assume that

\mathcal{M}=\left\langle a_{i}(x)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\prime}_{i}\right\rangle_{1\leq i\leq n}
. It means that
any
\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathcal{K}
can be seen as
(q_{1}\bmod a_{1},\dots,q_{n}\bmod a_{n})
.
Using the result of Lemma
3.8
, we can define the row and pivot
degrees of the relation module
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{M},M}
.

Definition 4.2 (Row and pivot degrees of the relation module).

Let
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
be a shift and
P
be any basis of
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{M},M}
in ordered
weak Popov form. The
\boldsymbol{s}
-row degrees of the relation module
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{M},M}
are

\boldsymbol{\rho}:=\text{rdeg}_{\boldsymbol{s}}(P)=(\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{m})
and the
\boldsymbol{s}
-pivot degrees are

\boldsymbol{\delta}:=(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{m})
where
\delta_{i}=\rho_{i}-s_{i}
.

Throughout this paper we will also denote
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{M}
and
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{M}
when we want
to stress out the matrix dependency.

4.1.
Row degree as row rank profile

In this section, we will see that the row degrees of the relation module can be
deduced from the row rank profile of a matrix associated to
\hat{\varphi}_{M}
. We
start by associating the pivot degree of
\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{M},M}
to linear dependency relation.

Proposition 4.3.

There exists
\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{M},M}
with
\boldsymbol{s}
-pivot index
i
and
\boldsymbol{s}
-pivot
degree
d
if and only if
x^{d}\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\in B_{M}^{\prec x^{d}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{i}}
where

B_{M}^{\prec x^{d}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{i}}:=\langle x^{n}\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\mid x^{n}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{j}\prec_{\boldsymbol{s}-TOP}x^{d}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{i}\rangle
.

Proof.

Fix
i,d\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathbb{K}[x]^{n}
with
\boldsymbol{s}
-pivot index
i
and
\boldsymbol{s}
-pivot
degree
d
, so
r:=\text{rdeg}_{\boldsymbol{s}}(\boldsymbol{p})=d+s_{i}
. Then

\boldsymbol{p}=([\leq r]_{s_{1}},\ldots,[\leq r]_{s_{i-1}},[r]_{s_{i}},[<r]_{s_{i+1}},\ldots,[<r]_{s_{m}})
(see Definition
3.1
) and we can write
\boldsymbol{p}=cx^{d}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{i}+\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}
where
c\in\mathbb{K}^{*}
and

\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}=([\leq r]_{s_{1}},\ldots,[\leq r]_{s_{i-1}},[<r]_{s_{i}},[<r]_{s_{i+1}},\ldots,[<r]_{s_{m}})
. So
\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{M},M}
has

s
-pivot index
i
and degree
d

\Leftrightarrow

x^{d}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{i}=-1/c\ \boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}\bmod\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{M},M}

\Leftrightarrow

x^{d}\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\in\left\langle x^{n}\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\ \middle|\begin{array}[]{ll}n+s_{j}\leq d+s_{i},&\text{for }1\leq j\leq i-1\\
n+s_{j}<d+s_{i},&\text{for }i\leq j\leq m\end{array}\right\rangle=B_{M}^{\prec x^{d}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{i}}.\ \square

Theorem 4.4.

Let
\boldsymbol{\delta}
be the
\boldsymbol{s}
-pivot degrees of the relation module
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{M},M}
.
Then
\delta_{j}=min\{d\mid x^{d}\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\in B_{M}^{\prec x^{d}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{j}}\}
for any

1\leq j\leq m
.

Proof.

Fix
1\leq j\leq m
. During this proof we denote

\overline{\delta}_{j}:=min\{d\mid x^{d}\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\in B_{M}^{\prec x^{d}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{j}}\}
. We want to prove that

\delta_{j}=\overline{\delta}_{j}
. Recall that by Proposition
4.3
,

x^{\delta_{j}}\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\in B_{M}^{\prec x^{\delta_{j}}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{j}}
. Hence, by the minimality
of
\overline{\delta}_{j}
,
\delta_{j}\geq\overline{\delta}_{j}
. On the other
hand,

x^{\overline{\delta}_{j}}\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\in B_{M}^{\prec x^{\overline{\delta}_{j}}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{j}}
so by Proposition
4.3

there exists
\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{M},M}
of
\boldsymbol{s}
-pivot index
j
and degree

\overline{\delta}_{j}
. Finally, by Lemma
3.7
we can conclude that

\overline{\delta}_{j}\geq\delta_{j}
.
∎

We now define the
ordered matrix

Mo_{M}
as the matrix of

\hat{\varphi}_{M}
w.r.t. particular
\mathbb{K}
-vector space bases: the rows of
Mo_{M}

from top to bottom are the monomials
of
\mathbb{K}[x]^{m}
sorted
increasingly for the
\prec_{\boldsymbol{s}-TOP}
order (see Eq. (
6
)). The
columns of
Mo_{M}
are written
w.r.t.
the basis

\{\boldsymbol{x}^{i}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\prime}_{j}\}_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq n\\
0\leq i<f_{j}\end{subarray}}
of

\mathbb{K}[x]^{n}/\mathcal{M}
. Therefore,
Mo_{M}
has finite rank

\text{rank}(Mo_{M})=\text{rank}(\hat{\varphi}_{M})=\text{rank}(\varphi_{M})
, infinite number of
rows and
(\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i})=\dim_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathbb{K}[x]^{n}/\mathcal{M})
columns.

Monomial row rank profile

Our goal is to relate the row rank profile of
Mo_{M}
to the row degree of the
relation module. The classic definition of row rank profile of a rank

r
polynomial matrix is the lexicographically smallest sequence of
r
indices
of linearly independent rows (
cf.

(
DPS 15
)
for instance). Since the
rows of our ordered matrix
Mo_{M}
correspond to monomials, we will transpose the
previous definition to monomials instead of indices.

Let
\mathsf{Mon}_{r}
be the sets of
r
monomials of
\mathbb{K}[x]^{m}
. We define the
lexicographical ordering on
\mathsf{Mon}_{r}
by comparing lexicographically the sorted
monomials for
\prec_{\boldsymbol{s}-TOP}
. In detail,
\mathcal{F}<_{lex}\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
iff
there exists
1\leq t\leq r
s.t.
x^{i_{l}}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{j_{l}}=x^{u_{l}}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{v_{l}}
for
l<t
and
x^{i_{t}}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{j_{t}}\prec_{\boldsymbol{s}-TOP}x^{u_{t}}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{v_{t}}

where
\mathcal{F}=\{x^{i_{l}}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{j_{l}}\}_{1\leq l\leq r}
and

\mathcal{F}^{\prime}=\{x^{u_{l}}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{v_{l}}\}_{1\leq l\leq r}
and both

\{x^{i_{l}}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{j_{l}}\}
and
\{x^{u_{l}}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{v_{l}}\}
are increasing for the

\prec_{\boldsymbol{s}-TOP}
order.

We will use this lexicographic order on monomials to define the row rank profile
of
Mo_{M}
.
Let
r=\text{rank}(Mo_{M})
.

Definition 4.5 (Row rank profile).

For any matrix
M\in\mathbb{K}[x]^{m\times n}
, we define the
row rank profile

of
Mo_{M}
(shortly
RRP_{M}
) as the family of monomials of
\mathbb{K}[x]^{m}
defined by

RRP_{M}:=min_{<_{lex}}\mathcal{P}_{M}
where

\mathcal{P}_{M}:=\left\{\mathcal{F}\in\mathsf{Mon}_{r}\ \middle|\ \{mM\}_{m\in\mathcal{F}}\text{ are linearly independent in }\mathcal{K}\right\}.

We now introduce a particular family of monomials, that we will frequently use:
we will denote

\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{d}}:=\{x^{i}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{j}\}_{\begin{subarray}{c}i<d_{j}\\
1\leq j\leq m\end{subarray}}

for any
\boldsymbol{d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{m})\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
.

This family allows us to finally relate the row rank profile of
Mo_{M}
to the row
degree of the relation module.

Proposition 4.6.

The row rank profile of the ordered matrix
Mo_{M}
is given by the pivot degrees
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{M}
of
the relation module
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{M},M}
,
i.e.

RRP_{M}=\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}_{M}}
.

Proof.

We fix the matrix
M
in order to simplify notations. We define

\delta^{\prime}_{j}=min\left\{\delta\ |\ x^{\delta}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{j}\notin RRP\right\}

and
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\prime}=(\delta^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\delta^{\prime}_{m})
. By properties of row rank
profile, we have that
x^{\delta_{j}}\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\in B^{\prec x^{\delta_{j}}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{j}}

(otherwise we could create a smaller family of linearly independent monomial
with
x^{\delta_{j}}\boldsymbol{e}_{j}
). Using Theorem
4.4
, we deduce that

\delta^{\prime}_{j}\geq\delta_{j}
. Therefore

\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\subset\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\prime}}\subset RRP
. Since the families of
monomials
\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
and
RRP
have the same cardinality
r=\text{rank}(Mo)
,
they are equal so
\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}=RRP
.
∎

4.2.
Constraints on relation’s row degree

We will now focus on integer tuples
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{M}
which can be achieved. For this
matter, in the light of Proposition
4.6
, we need to understand
which families
\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{d}}
of monomials can be linearly independent in the
ordered matrix,
i.e.
belong to
\mathcal{P}_{M}
(see Definition
4.5
).

Recall that

\mathcal{K}=\mathbb{K}[x]^{n}/\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{K}[x]^{n}/\left\langle a_{i}(x)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\prime}_{i}\right\rangle_{1\leq i\leq n}

and
f_{i}=\deg(a_{i}(x))
are non-increasing as in Remark
4.1
. Recall also from Definition
4.5
that
\mathcal{P}_{M}
is the set of families
\mathcal{F}
of
r
monomials in
\mathbb{K}[x]^{m}
such that
\{mM\}_{m\in\mathcal{F}}
are linearly independent in
\mathbb{K}[x]^{n}/\mathcal{M}
.

Theorem 4.7.

Let
\boldsymbol{d}\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
be non-increasing. We can extend
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
by
f_{n+1}=\ldots=f_{m}=0
. Then
\exists M\in\mathbb{K}[x]^{m\times n}
such that

\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{d}}\in\mathcal{P}_{M}

if and only if
\sum_{i=1}^{l}d_{i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{l}f_{i}
for all
1\leq l\leq m
.

The non-increasing property of
\boldsymbol{d}
can be lifted: let
\boldsymbol{d}
be non-increasing and
\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime}
be any permutation of
\boldsymbol{d}
. Then
\exists M\in\mathbb{K}[x]^{m\times n}
such that
\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{d}}\in\mathcal{P}_{M}
if and only if
\exists M^{\prime}\in\mathbb{K}[x]^{m\times n}
such that
\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{P}_{M^{\prime}}
. Indeed, permuting
\boldsymbol{d}
amounts to permuting the components of
\boldsymbol{p}
,
i.e.
permuting the rows of
M
. This does not affect the existence property.

The latter proposition is an adaptation of
(
Vil 97
, Proposition 6.1)
and
its derivation
(
PS 07
, Theorem 3)
. Even if the statements of these two
papers are in a different but related context, their proof can be applied almost
straightforwardly.
We will still provide the main steps of the proof, for the sake of clarity and also because we will have to adapt the proof later in Theorem
5.2
.
Note also that we complete the ’if’ part of the proof because it was not detailed in earlier references. For this matter, we introduce the following

Lemma 4.8.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be a
\mathbb{K}[x]
-submodule of
\mathcal{K}
of rank
l
.
Then the dimension of
\mathcal{N}
as
\mathbb{K}
-vector space is at most
f_{1}+\ldots+f_{l}
.

Proof.

First, remark that if
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{q}}\in\mathcal{N}
has its first non-zero element
at index
p
then
a_{p}(x)\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{q}}=0
. Now since
\mathcal{N}
has rank
l
,
we can consider the matrix
B
whose rows are the
l
elements of a basis of

\mathcal{N}
. We operate on the rows of
B
to obtain the

and finally we have our result for generic
\boldsymbol{u}
.

In order to do so, we show that the construction of the proof of the
Theorem
4.7
provides a matrix of the form
R_{\boldsymbol{u}}
in our case.
In our case
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n+1})=(N_{1},\ldots,N_{n},D-1)
and
m=n+1
, where

f_{n+1}=0
. In particular, by SRFR assumptions, for any
1\leq i\leq n
,

d_{i}\leq f_{i}
and so the matrices
\overline{K}_{i}=[K(\boldsymbol{u}_{i},d_{i})]
are already in the
Krylov form. On the other hand, the last matrix is in the form

\overline{K}_{n+1}=[K(x^{d_{j}}\boldsymbol{u}_{j},t_{j})]_{1\leq j\leq n}
where

d_{j}+t_{j}=f_{j}
. Then
\widetilde{K}_{n+1}=[K(\sum_{j=1}^{n}x^{s^{\prime}_{j}}\boldsymbol{u}_{j},d_{j})]
and
we need to prove that
s^{\prime}_{j}\geq 0
differently because we don’t have the
assumption about the non-increasing
\boldsymbol{d}
. Recall that
s^{\prime}_{j}
is
s_{j}
minus the number
of columns added to extend the matrix to the left. This number of columns is
at most
d_{n+1}
minus the size
t_{l}
of the current block. So

s^{\prime}_{l}\geq d_{l}-(d_{n+1}-t_{l})=d_{l}-(d_{n+1}-(f_{l}-d_{l}))=f_{l}-d_{n+1}\geq 0
because
d_{n+1}=D-1\leq D\leq\min(f_{i})
and so the construction
works.
