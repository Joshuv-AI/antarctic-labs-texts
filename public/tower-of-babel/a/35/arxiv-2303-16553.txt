Dynamics of Binary System around Supermassive Black Hole
Kei-ichi Maeda, Priti Gupta, Hirotada Okawa

Abstract
We discuss motion of a binary system around a supermassive black hole. Using Fermi-Walker transport, we construct a local inertial reference frame and set up a Newtonian binary system. Assuming a circular geodesic observer around a Schwarzschild black hole, we write down the equations of motion of a binary. Introducing a small acceleration of the observer, we remove the interaction terms between the center of mass (CM) of a binary and its relative coordinates. The CM follows the observer’s orbit, but its motion deviates from an exact circular geodesic. We first solve the relative motion of a binary system, and then find the motion of the CM by the perturbation equations with the small acceleration. We show that there appears the Kozai-Lidov (KL) oscillations when a binary is compact and the initial inclination is larger than a critical angle. In a hard binary system, KL oscillations are regular, whereas in a soft binary system, oscillations are irregular both in period and in amplitude, although stable. We find an orbital flip when the initial inclination is large. As for the motion of the CM, the radial deviations from a circular orbit become stable oscillations with very small amplitude.

In this paper, when we discuss on a binary system near SMBH, we follow another approach, i.e., a binary system is treated as perturbations of SMBH spacetime. In order to analyze such a hierarchical system, we first prepare a local inertial frame and set up a binary in this frame. \displaystyle{1\over 2}\mu\dot{\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$r$}}^{2}+{Gm_{1}m_{2}\over r}+{\cal L}_{{\rm rel}\mathchar 45\omega}(\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$r$},\dot{\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$r$}})+{\cal L}_{{\rm rel}\mathchar 45\bar{\cal R}}(\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$r$},\dot{\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$r$}}),

with

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{{\rm rel}\mathchar 45\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mu\left[\epsilon_{\hat{j}\hat{k}\hat{\ell}}\omega^{\hat{\ell}}r^{\hat{k}}\dot{r}^{\hat{j}}-{1\over 2}\left(\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$\omega$}^{2}\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$r$}^{2}-\left(\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$\omega$}\cdot\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$r$}\right)^{2}\right)\right]\,,

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{{\rm rel}\mathchar 45\bar{\cal R}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{1\over 2}\mu\bar{\cal R}_{\hat{0}\hat{k}\hat{0}\hat{\ell}}r^{\hat{k}}r^{\hat{\ell}}\,.

Here,
\mu=m_{1}m_{2}/(m_{1}+m_{2})
is the reduced mass. When we consider only
{\cal L}_{\rm N}
,
we can separate the variables

R
and

r
.
In particular, when the observer follows the geodesic (
\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$a$}=0
and
\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$\omega$}=0
),
the orbit of
\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$R$}=0
is a solution of the equation for

R
.
It means that the center of mass (CM) follows the observer’s geodesic.
We have only the equation for the relative coordinate

r
. However, when we include the 0.5 PN term, it is not the case.
The 0.5PN Lagrangian
{\cal L}_{1/2}
is rewritten by use of

R
and

r
as

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{1/2}={\cal L}_{1/2\mathchar 45{\rm CM}}(\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$R$},\dot{\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$R$}})+{\cal L}_{1/2\mathchar 45{\rm rel}}(\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$r$},\dot{\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$r$}})+{\cal L}_{1/2\mathchar 45{\rm int}}(\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$R$},\dot{\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$R$},}\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$r$},\dot{\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$r$}}),

(12)

where

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{1/2\mathchar 45{\rm CM}}(\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$R$},\dot{\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$R$}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{2\over 3}(m_{1}+m_{2})R_{\hat{0}\hat{k}\hat{j}\hat{\ell}}R^{\hat{k}}R^{\hat{\ell}}\dot{R}^{\hat{j}},

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{1/2\mathchar 45{\rm rel}}(\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$r$},\dot{\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$r$}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{2\over 3}\mu{(m_{1}-m_{2})\over(m_{1}+m_{2})}R_{\hat{0}\hat{k}\hat{j}\hat{\ell}}r^{\hat{k}}r^{\hat{\ell}}\dot{r}^{\hat{j}},

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{1/2\mathchar 45{\rm int}}(\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$R$},\dot{\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$R$},}\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$r$},\dot{\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$r$}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{2\over 3}\mu R_{\hat{0}\hat{k}\hat{j}\hat{\ell}}\left[r^{\hat{k}}r^{\hat{\ell}}\dot{R}^{\hat{j}}+\left(R^{\hat{k}}r^{\hat{\ell}}+r^{\hat{k}}R^{\hat{\ell}}\right)\dot{r}^{\hat{j}}\right].

(13)

Due of the interaction term
{\cal L}_{1/2\mathchar 45{\rm int}}
, the orbit of
\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$R$}=0
is no longer a solution even if the acceleration vanishes. The motion of the CM (
\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$R$}(\tau
)) couples with the relative motion
(\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$r$}(\tau))
. As a result, not only the orbit of a binary but also the motion of the CM will become complicated even if the observer’s orbit is a geodesic.

However, if we introduce an appropriate acceleration

a

in 0.5PN order
to cancel the interaction terms,
\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$R$}=0
will become a solution, i.e.,
the CM can follow the observer’s motion as follows:
Integrating by parts the interaction term, we find

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{1/2\mathchar 45{\rm int}}(\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$R$},\dot{\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$R$},}\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$r$},\dot{\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$r$}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{2\over 3}\mu\bar{\cal R}_{\hat{0}\hat{k}\hat{j}\hat{\ell}}\left[\dot{R}^{\hat{j}}r^{\hat{k}}r^{\hat{\ell}}+\dot{r}^{\hat{j}}\left(R^{\hat{k}}r^{\hat{\ell}}+r^{\hat{k}}R^{\hat{\ell}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle 2\mu\left[{1\over 3}{d\bar{\cal R}_{\hat{0}\hat{k}\hat{j}\hat{\ell}}\over d\tau}r^{\hat{k}}r^{\hat{\ell}}+\bar{\cal R}_{\hat{0}\hat{k}\hat{j}\hat{\ell}}r^{\hat{k}}\dot{r}^{\hat{\ell}}\right]R^{\hat{j}}~~{\rm(integration~by~part)}\,,

where the time derivative of the curvature is evaluated along the observer’s orbit.

If we define the acceleration by

\displaystyle a_{\hat{j}}={2\mu\over m_{1}+m_{2}}\left[{1\over 3}{d\bar{\cal R}_{\hat{0}\hat{k}\hat{j}\hat{\ell}}\over d\tau}r^{\hat{k}}r^{\hat{\ell}}+\bar{\cal R}_{\hat{0}\hat{k}\hat{j}\hat{\ell}}r^{\hat{k}}\dot{r}^{\hat{\ell}}\right]\,,

two terms
{\cal L}_{1/2\mathchar 45{\rm int}}
and
{\cal L}_{{\rm CM}\mathchar 45a}
cancel each other.
As a result, the Lagrangians for

R
and

r
are decoupled,
and
\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$R$}=0
becomes an exact solution of the equation for

R
, which is
derived from the Lagrangian
(
{\cal L}_{\rm CM}+{\cal L}_{1/2\mathchar 45{\rm CM}}
).
The CM follows the observer’s orbit and therefore, we obtain the decoupled equation for the relative coordinate

r
.

In order to obtain the proper observer’s orbit,
which is not a geodesic but may be close to the geodesic,
we have to solve the equation of motion including small acceleration such that

\displaystyle{Du_{\rm CM}^{\mu}\over d\tau}=a^{\mu}=e^{\mu\hat{j}}a_{\hat{j}}={2\mu\over m_{1}+m_{2}}e^{\mu\hat{j}}\left[{1\over 3}{d\bar{\cal R}_{\hat{0}\hat{k}\hat{j}\hat{\ell}}\over d\tau}r^{\hat{k}}r^{\hat{\ell}}+\bar{\cal R}_{\hat{0}\hat{k}\hat{j}\hat{\ell}}r^{\hat{k}}\dot{r}^{\hat{\ell}}\right]\,.

(14)

As a result, we first solve the equation for the relative coordinate

r
, which is
obtained only by the Lagrangian
{\cal L}_{\rm rel}(\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$r$})+{\cal L}_{1/2\mathchar 45{\rm rel}}(\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$r$})
.
Note that when
m_{1}=m_{2}
, we have only Newtonian
Lagrangian
{\cal L}_{\rm rel}
because
{\cal L}_{1/2\mathchar 45{\rm rel}}=0
vanishes.
After obtaining the solution of
\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$r$}(\tau)
,
we find the motion for the CM
(or the observer) in the background spacetime
by solving Eq. (
14
). Using the relative motion
\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$r$}(\tau)
with the solution of the CM motion
(
x_{\rm CM}^{\mu}(\tau)
),
we will obtain a binary motion in a given curved background spacetime (
x_{1}^{\mu}(\tau)\,,x_{2}^{\mu}(\tau)
).

IV
A binary system in a Schwarzschild spacetime

We consider a spherically symmetric supermassive black hole.
The background spacetime is given by Schwarzschild solution as

\displaystyle d\bar{s}^{2}=-fd\mathfrak{t}^{2}+{1\over f}d\mathfrak{r}^{2}+\mathfrak{r}^{2}d\Omega^{2}\,,

(15)

where

\displaystyle f=1-{\mathfrak{r}_{g}\over\mathfrak{r}}.

(16)

The gravitational radius
\mathfrak{r}_{g}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{r}_{g}\equiv{2GM\over c^{2}},

where
M
is a gravitational mass of the supermassive black hole. In what follows, we set
G=1
and
c=1
for brevity unless specified otherwise.

IV.1
Proper reference frame

We first discuss Newtonian dynamics for which Lagrangian
{\cal L}_{\rm N}
is given by Eq. (
9
).
In this case, the CM of a binary system follows a geodesic observer.
We consider an observer, which moves along a
circular geodesic with the radius
\mathfrak{r}=\mathfrak{r}_{0}
.
The orbit is assumed to be
on the equatorial plane without loss of generality.
We introduce a rotating tetrad system along this geodesic such that

\displaystyle e_{\hat{0}}^{~\mu}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle u^{\mu}=\left({\sqrt{1+\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{w}_{0}^{2}}\over\sqrt{f(\mathfrak{r}_{0})}}\,,0\,,0\,,\mathfrak{w}_{0}\right),

\displaystyle e_{\hat{1}}^{~\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(0\,,\sqrt{f(\mathfrak{r}_{0})}\,,0\,,0\right),

\displaystyle e_{\hat{2}}^{~\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left({\mathfrak{r}_{0}\mathfrak{w}_{0}\over\sqrt{f(\mathfrak{r}_{0})}}\,,0\,,0\,,{\sqrt{1+\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{w}_{0}^{2}}\over\mathfrak{r}_{0}}\right),

\displaystyle e_{\hat{3}}^{~\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(0\,,0\,,-{1\over\mathfrak{r}_{0}}\,,0\right)\,,

where
the angular frequency
\mathfrak{w}_{0}
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{w}_{0}=u^{3}={d\varphi\over d\tau}={1\over\mathfrak{r}_{0}(\mathfrak{r}_{0}/M-3)^{1/2}}\,.

(17)

We then calculate the angular velocity
\omega^{\hat{j}}
of this rotating tetrad system as

\displaystyle\omega^{\hat{j}}={1\over 2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(3)}}{{\epsilon}}^{\hat{j}\hat{k}\hat{\ell}}e_{\hat{\ell}\mu}{De_{\hat{k}}^{~\mu}\over d\tau}=\mathfrak{w}_{\rm R}\delta^{\hat{j}}_{~\hat{3}}\,,

where

\displaystyle\mathfrak{w}_{\rm R}={1\over\mathfrak{r}_{0}(\mathfrak{r}_{0}/M)^{1/2}},

(18)

is the angular frequency of the rotating tetrad frame
(See Fig.
1
).
Note that this angular frequency is different from
the observer’s angular frequency
\mathfrak{w}_{0}
.
The difference between two angular frequencies
\mathfrak{w}_{0}
and
\mathfrak{w}_{R}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{w}_{\rm dS}\equiv\mathfrak{w}_{0}-\mathfrak{w}_{\rm R}

(19)

describes the rotation of non-rotating inertial frame moving along a circular orbit, which may cause de Sitter precession
de Sitter 1916
.

A local inertial tetrad system
\{\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$e$}_{\hat{x}}\,,\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$e$}_{\hat{y}}\,,\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$e$}_{\hat{z}}\}
rotating with angular frequency

\mathfrak{w}_{\rm R}

along a circular orbit.

Next, we calculate the Riemann curvature in the above tetrad system.
In the static tetrad system

\{e^{\bar{\alpha}}_{~\mu}\}
with

\displaystyle e^{\bar{0}}_{~0}=\sqrt{f(\mathfrak{r}_{0})}\,,~e^{\bar{r}}_{~r}={1\over\sqrt{f(\mathfrak{r}_{0})}}\,,~e^{\bar{\theta}}_{~\theta}=\mathfrak{r}_{0}\,,~e^{\bar{\phi}}_{~\phi}=\mathfrak{r}_{0}\,,

the non-trivial components of the Riemann curvature of
the Schwarzschild spacetime are given by

\displaystyle\bar{\cal R}_{\bar{0}\bar{r}\bar{0}\bar{r}}=-{\mathfrak{r}_{g}\over\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{3}}\,,~\bar{\cal R}_{\bar{0}\bar{\theta}\bar{0}\bar{\theta}}=\bar{\cal R}_{\bar{0}\bar{\phi}\bar{0}\bar{\phi}}={\mathfrak{r}_{g}\over 2\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{3}},

\displaystyle\bar{\cal R}_{\bar{\theta}\bar{\phi}\bar{\theta}\bar{\phi}}={\mathfrak{r}_{g}\over\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{3}}\,,~\bar{\cal R}_{\bar{r}\bar{\theta}\bar{r}\bar{\theta}}=R_{\bar{r}\bar{\phi}\bar{r}\bar{\phi}}=-{\mathfrak{r}_{g}\over 2\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{3}}\,.

We then introduce the Descartes coordinates
(\bar{x},\bar{y},\bar{z})

such that

\bar{x}
-direction is the same as the
r
-direction, but the
\bar{y}
and
\bar{z}
-directions are

\phi
and
-\theta
-directions, respectively, as shown in Fig.
1
.
Since the tetrad system in this coordinates is given by

\displaystyle e^{\bar{0}}_{~0}=\sqrt{f(\mathfrak{r}_{0})}\,,~e^{\bar{x}}_{~r}={1\over\sqrt{f(\mathfrak{r}_{0})}}\,,~e^{\bar{y}}_{~\phi}=\mathfrak{r}_{0}\,,~e^{\bar{z}}_{~\theta}=-\mathfrak{r}_{0}\,,

we find the curvature components in this tetrad system as

\displaystyle\bar{\cal R}_{\bar{0}\bar{x}\bar{0}\bar{x}}=-{\mathfrak{r}_{g}\over\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{3}}\,,~\bar{\cal R}_{\bar{0}\bar{y}\bar{0}\bar{y}}=\bar{\cal R}_{\bar{0}\bar{z}\bar{0}\bar{z}}={\mathfrak{r}_{g}\over 2\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{3}},

\displaystyle\bar{\cal R}_{\bar{y}\bar{z}\bar{y}\bar{z}}={\mathfrak{r}_{g}\over\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{3}}\,,~R_{\bar{x}\bar{y}\bar{x}\bar{y}}=\bar{\cal R}_{\bar{z}\bar{x}\bar{z}\bar{x}}=-{\mathfrak{r}_{g}\over 2\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{3}}\,.

The transformation matrix between the observer’s tetrad and static tetrad (
\bar{x},\bar{y},\bar{z}
) is given by

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\hat{0}}^{~\bar{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sqrt{1+\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{w}_{0}^{2}}\,,0\,,\mathfrak{r}_{0}\mathfrak{w}_{0}\,,0\right),

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\hat{1}}^{~\bar{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(0\,,1\,,0\,,0\right),

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\hat{2}}^{~\bar{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\mathfrak{r}_{0}\mathfrak{w}_{0}\,,0\,,\sqrt{1+\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{w}_{0}^{2}}\,,0\right),

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\hat{3}}^{~\bar{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(0\,,0\,,0\,,1\right).

It is just the Lorentz boost with velocity

\displaystyle\mathfrak{v}={\mathfrak{r}_{0}\mathfrak{w}_{0}\over\sqrt{1+\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{w}_{0}^{2}}},

in
0\,\mathchar 45\,y
plane.

The components in the observer’s proper reference frame are given by

\displaystyle\bar{\cal R}_{\hat{0}\hat{x}\hat{0}\hat{x}}=-\bar{\cal R}_{\hat{y}\hat{z}\hat{y}\hat{z}}=-\left(1+{3\over 2}\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{w}_{0}^{2}\right){\mathfrak{r}_{g}\over\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{3}}\,,~

\displaystyle\bar{\cal R}_{\hat{0}\hat{y}\hat{0}\hat{y}}=-\bar{\cal R}_{\hat{z}\hat{x}\hat{z}\hat{x}}={1\over 2}{\mathfrak{r}_{g}\over\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{3}}\,,~

\displaystyle\bar{\cal R}_{\hat{0}\hat{z}\hat{0}\hat{z}}=-\bar{\cal R}_{\hat{x}\hat{y}\hat{x}\hat{y}}=\left({1+3\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{w}_{0}^{2}\over 2}\right){\mathfrak{r}_{g}\over\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{3}},

\displaystyle\bar{\cal R}_{\hat{0}\hat{x}\hat{y}\hat{x}}=-\bar{\cal R}_{\hat{0}\hat{z}\hat{y}\hat{z}}=-{3\over 2}\epsilon_{1}\mathfrak{r}_{0}\mathfrak{w}_{0}\sqrt{1+\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{w}_{0}^{2}}\,{\mathfrak{r}_{g}\over\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{3}}\,.

IV.2
Equations of motion of a binary

Since the CM of a binary follows the observer’s circular geodesic (
\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$R$}=0
), we have to solve only the equations of motion for the relative coordinate

r
.
Using
x=r^{\hat{1}}\,,y=r^{\hat{2}}\,,z=r^{\hat{3}}
,
the relative motion of a binary is
given by the Lagrangian

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{\rm rel}(\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$r$},\dot{\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$r$}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over 2}\mu\dot{\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$r$}}^{2}+{Gm_{1}m_{2}\over r}+{\cal L}_{{\rm rel}\mathchar 45\mathfrak{w}}(\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$r$},\dot{\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$r$}})+{\cal L}_{{\rm rel}\mathchar 45\bar{\cal R}}(\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$r$},\dot{\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$r$}}),

(20)

with

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{{\rm rel}\mathchar 45\mathfrak{w}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mu\left[\mathfrak{w}_{0}\left(x\dot{y}-y\dot{x}\right)-{\mathfrak{w}_{0}^{2}\over 2}\left(x^{2}+y^{2}\right)\right],

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{{\rm rel}\mathchar 45\bar{\cal R}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{\mu\over 2}\left(\bar{\cal R}_{\hat{0}\hat{x}\hat{0}\hat{x}}x^{2}+\bar{\cal R}_{\hat{0}\hat{y}\hat{0}\hat{y}}y^{2}+\bar{\cal R}_{\hat{0}\hat{z}\hat{0}\hat{z}}z^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{\mu\mathfrak{r}_{g}\over 4\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{3}}\left[y^{2}-2x^{2}+z^{2}+3\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{w}_{0}^{2}(-x^{2}+z^{2})\right]\,.

The first and second terms in
{\cal L}_{{\rm rel}\mathchar 45\mathfrak{w}}
describe the Coriolis force and
the centrifugal force, respectively.
The first half terms in
{\cal L}_{{\rm rel}\mathchar 45\bar{\cal R}}
are the same
as those in Newtonian hierarchical triple system under quadrupole approximation,
while the last half terms are relativistic corrections.
Note that in the present approach (approximation up to the second order of
x^{\hat{a}}
),
we cannot go beyond quadrupole approximation.

In order to analyze the relative motion of a binary,
it may be better to go to a non-rotating initial reference frame.
Since the angular frequency of a rotating tetrad frame is
\mathfrak{w}_{\rm R}
,
the position
(x,y,z)
in the rotating frame can be replaced by the position
(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y},\mathsf{z})
in a non-rotating Descartes’ coordinate system by use of the following transformation;

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathsf{x}\cos\mathfrak{w}_{\rm R}\tau-\mathsf{y}\sin\mathfrak{w}_{\rm R}\tau,

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathsf{x}\sin\mathfrak{w}_{\rm R}\tau+\mathsf{y}\cos\mathfrak{w}_{\rm R}\tau,

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathsf{z}\,.

The Lagrangian
{\cal L}_{\rm rel}
in a non-rotating proper reference frame
is given by

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{\rm rel}={1\over 2}\mu\left({d{\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$\mathsf{r}$}}\over d\tau}\right)^{2}+{Gm_{1}m_{2}\over\mathsf{r}}+{\cal L}_{\rm rel\mathchar 45dS}(\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$\mathsf{r}$},\dot{\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$\mathsf{r}$}})+{\cal L}_{{\rm rel}\mathchar 45\bar{\cal R}}(\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$\mathsf{r}$},\tau)\,,

(21)

where

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{\rm rel\mathchar 45dS}(\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$\mathsf{r}$},\dot{\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$\mathsf{r}$}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu\mathfrak{w}_{\rm dS}\left(\dot{\mathsf{x}}\mathsf{y}-\dot{\mathsf{y}}\mathsf{x}\right)+{\mu\over 2}\mathfrak{w}_{\rm dS}^{2}\left(\mathsf{x}^{2}+\mathsf{y}^{2}\right),

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{{\rm rel}\mathchar 45\bar{\cal R}}(\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$\mathsf{r}$},\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{\mu\mathfrak{r}_{g}\over 4\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{3}}\left[\mathsf{x}^{2}+\mathsf{y}^{2}+\mathsf{z}^{2}-3\left(1+\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{w}_{0}^{2}\right)\left(\mathsf{x}\cos\mathfrak{w}_{\rm R}\tau-\mathsf{y}\sin\mathfrak{w}_{\rm R}\tau\right)^{2}+3\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{w}_{0}^{2}\mathsf{z}^{2}\right].

Since the momentum is defined by

\displaystyle\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{x}}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle{\partial{\cal L}\over\partial\dot{\mathsf{x}}}=\mu\dot{\mathsf{x}}+\mu\mathfrak{w}_{\rm dS}\mathsf{y},

\displaystyle\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{y}}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle{\partial{\cal L}\over\partial\dot{\mathsf{y}}}=\mu\dot{\mathsf{y}}-\mu\mathfrak{w}_{\rm dS}\mathsf{x},

\displaystyle\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{z}}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle{\partial{\cal L}\over\partial\dot{\mathsf{z}}}=\mu\dot{\mathsf{z}}\,,

we obtain the Hamiltonian as

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{\rm rel}={\cal H}_{0}+{\cal H}_{1}\,,

(22)

where

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over 2\mu}{\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$\mathsf{p}$}}^{2}-{Gm_{1}m_{2}\over\mathsf{r}},

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{1\mathchar 45{\rm dS}}+{\cal H}_{1\mathchar 45\bar{\cal R}},

with

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{1\mathchar 45{\rm dS}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathfrak{w}_{\rm dS}\left(\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{y}}\mathsf{x}-\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{x}}\mathsf{y}\right),

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{1\mathchar 45\bar{\cal R}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mu\mathfrak{r}_{g}\over 4\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{3}}\left[\mathsf{x}^{2}+\mathsf{y}^{2}+\mathsf{z}^{2}-3\left(1+\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{w}_{0}^{2}\right)\left(\mathsf{x}\cos\mathfrak{w}_{\rm R}\tau-\mathsf{y}\sin\mathfrak{w}_{\rm R}\tau\right)^{2}+3\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{w}_{0}^{2}\mathsf{z}^{2}\right].

The term
{\cal H}_{1\mathchar 45{\rm dS}}
gives the so-called
de Sitter precession as follows:
Let us consider the model with
{\cal H}^{\prime}={\cal H}_{0}+{\cal H}_{1\mathchar 45{\rm dS}}
.
The Hamilton equations are given as

\displaystyle\dot{\mathsf{x}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\partial{\cal H}^{\prime}\over\partial\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{x}}}={\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{x}}\over\mu}-\mathfrak{w}_{\rm dS}\mathsf{y},

\displaystyle\dot{\mathsf{y}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\partial{\cal H}^{\prime}\over\partial\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{y}}}={\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{y}}\over\mu}+\mathfrak{w}_{\rm dS}\mathsf{x},

\displaystyle\dot{\mathsf{z}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\partial{\cal H}^{\prime}\over\partial\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{z}}}={\mathsf{p}_{z}\over\mu},

and

\displaystyle\dot{\mathsf{p}}_{\mathsf{x}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{\partial{\cal H}^{\prime}\over\partial\mathsf{x}}=-{Gm_{1}m_{2}\mathsf{x}\over\mathsf{r}^{3}}-\mathfrak{w}_{\rm dS}\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{y}},

\displaystyle\dot{\mathsf{p}}_{\mathsf{y}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{\partial{\cal H}^{\prime}\over\partial\mathsf{y}}=-{Gm_{1}m_{2}\mathsf{y}\over\mathsf{r}^{3}}+\mathfrak{w}_{\rm dS}\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{x}},

\displaystyle\dot{\mathsf{p}}_{\mathsf{z}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{\partial{\cal H}^{\prime}\over\partial\mathsf{z}}=-{Gm_{1}m_{2}\mathsf{z}\over\mathsf{r}^{3}}.

We then calculate the time evolution of the angular momentum
\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$L$}=(L_{\mathsf{x}},L_{\mathsf{y}},L_{\mathsf{z}})
.
Using the equations of motion, we find

\displaystyle\dot{L}_{\mathsf{x}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{d\over d\tau}(\mathsf{y}\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{z}}-\mathsf{z}\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{y}})=\dot{\mathsf{y}}\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{z}}+\mathsf{y}\dot{\mathsf{p}}_{\mathsf{z}}-\dot{\mathsf{z}}\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{y}}-\mathsf{z}\dot{\mathsf{p}}_{\mathsf{y}}=-\mathfrak{w}_{\rm dS}L_{\mathsf{y}},

\displaystyle\dot{L}_{\mathsf{y}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{d\over d\tau}(\mathsf{z}\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{x}}-\mathsf{x}\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{z}})=\dot{\mathsf{z}}\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{x}}+\mathsf{z}\dot{\mathsf{p}}_{\mathsf{x}}-\dot{\mathsf{x}}\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{z}}-\mathsf{x}\dot{\mathsf{p}}_{\mathsf{z}}=\mathfrak{w}_{\rm dS}L_{\mathsf{x}},

\displaystyle\dot{L}_{\mathsf{z}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{d\over d\tau}(\mathsf{x}\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{y}}-\mathsf{y}\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{x}})=\dot{\mathsf{x}}\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{y}}+\mathsf{x}\dot{\mathsf{p}}_{\mathsf{y}}-\dot{\mathsf{y}}\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{x}}-\mathsf{y}\dot{\mathsf{p}}_{\mathsf{x}}=0.

From these equations, we find that the
\mathsf{z}
-component of the angular momentum is conserved, and
(L_{\mathsf{x}},L_{\mathsf{y}})
rotates around the
\mathsf{z}
-axis with the constant angular frequency

\mathfrak{w}_{\rm dS}
which is just the de Sitter precession.
The full equations are given as

\displaystyle\dot{\mathsf{x}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\partial{\cal H}\over\partial\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{x}}}={\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{x}}\over\mu}-\mathfrak{w}_{\rm dS}\mathsf{y},

(23)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathsf{y}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\partial{\cal H}\over\partial\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{y}}}={\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{y}}\over\mu}+\mathfrak{w}_{\rm dS}\mathsf{x},

(24)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathsf{z}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\partial{\cal H}\over\partial\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{z}}}={\mathsf{p}_{\mathsf{z}}\over\mu},

(25)

and

\displaystyle\dot{\mathsf{p}}_{\mathsf{x}}

\displaystyle=

The minimum curvature radius at the radius

\displaystyle d\mathfrak{l}=(1-e^{2})^{{3\over 2}}\left(1+e\cos f\right)^{-2}df.

(48)

In Newtonian dynamics,
\mathfrak{l}=n(\tau-\tau_{0})
, where
n
is the mean motion.
Hence, in this approximation, the time derivative (
d/d\tau
) is given by the derivative with respect to
the true anomaly (
d/df
).

Assuming
f=0
at
\tau=0
, we find

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathsf{x}}(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-e_{0})\cos\Omega_{0}\cos\omega_{0}-\sin\Omega_{0}\sin\omega_{0}\cos I_{0},

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathsf{y}}(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-e_{0})\sin\Omega_{0}\cos\omega_{0}+\cos\Omega_{0}\sin\omega_{0}\cos I_{0},

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathsf{z}}(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-e_{0})\sin\omega_{0}\sin I_{0},

and

\displaystyle{d\tilde{\mathsf{x}}\over d\tilde{\tau}}(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sqrt{1+e_{0}\over 1-e_{0}}\,\left[\cos\Omega_{0}\sin\omega_{0}+\sin\Omega_{0}\cos\omega_{0}\cos I_{0}\right],

\displaystyle{d\tilde{\mathsf{y}}\over d\tilde{\tau}}(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{1+e_{0}\over 1-e_{0}}\,\left[-\sin\Omega_{0}\sin\omega_{0}+\cos\Omega_{0}\cos\omega_{0}\cos I_{0}\right],

\displaystyle{d\tilde{\mathsf{z}}\over d\tilde{\tau}}(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{1+e_{0}\over 1-e_{0}}\,\cos\omega_{0}\sin I_{0}.

Hence, when we prepare the initial orbital parameters (
e_{0}\,,I_{0}\,,\omega_{0}\,,\Omega_{0}
),
we can provide the initial data for the normalized evolution equations (
23
) -(
28
).

V.3
Binary motion near ISCO radius

In a hierarchical triple system, there are several
important features. One is the so-called Kozai-Lidov (KL) oscillations.
If the system is inclined more than some critical angle, there appears an oscillation between the eccentricity and inclination angle.
The second interesting feature is an orbital flip, which may appear when
the inclination angle evolves into near
90^{\circ}
.
The last one which we show is a chaotic feature in the long-time evolution.

Here, we show our numerical results. In order to discuss the properties of
a binary orbit, it is more convenient to use the orbital parameters assuming that the binary motion is close to an elliptic orbit.

In order to extract the orbital parameters
from the orbit given by the Cartesian
coordinates, one can use the osculating orbit when the orbit is close to an ellipse. However, one must be careful
with the definitions of orbital elements when using the osculating method.
For instance, the magnitude of the
normalized Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector,
which is defined by

\displaystyle\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$e$}\equiv\tilde{\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$\mathsf{p}$}}\times(\tilde{\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$\mathsf{r}$}}\times\tilde{\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$\mathsf{p}$}})-{\tilde{\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$\mathsf{r}$}}\over\tilde{\mathsf{r}}}\,,

(49)

is commonly used for a measure of orbital eccentricity, but
it is
not always appropriate.
It may show an “apparent” rise in eccentricity or unphysical rapid oscillations especially when the eccentricity is very small
Will 2019
.
We take caution and extract the elements’ information from physical orbit. In that case, it may be better to
define the eccentricity by the averaged one over one cycle
as

\displaystyle\langle e\rangle\equiv{\mathsf{r}_{\rm max}-\mathsf{r}_{\rm min}\over\mathsf{r}_{\rm max}+\mathsf{r}_{\rm min}}.

where
\mathsf{r}_{\rm max}
and
\mathsf{r}_{\rm min}
correspond to orbital separation at adjacent turning points of an eccentric orbit.

The inclination angle
I
is defined as mutual inclination between angular momenta of the inner and outer binary. In the present case, since the outer binary is just a circular motion on the equatorial plane, the inclination is given by

\displaystyle I=\cos^{-1}\left({L_{\mathsf{z}}\over|\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$L$}|}\right)\,,

where
\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$L$}\equiv\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$\mathsf{r}$}\times\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$\mathsf{p}$}
is the angular momentum of a binary.

The other two essential angles
\Omega
and
\omega
governing the orientation of the orbital plane and the orbit are also computed in the post-process. The line that marks the intersection of the orbital plane with the reference plane (the equatorial plane in the present case) is called the node line, and the point on the node line where the orbit passes above the reference plane from below is called the ascending node. The angle between the reference axis (say
\mathsf{x}
-axis) and node line vector

N
is the longitude of ascending node
\Omega
. First, node line is defined as,

\displaystyle\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$N$}=\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$e$}_{\mathsf{z}}\times\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$h$}\,.

where
\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$e$}_{\mathsf{z}}
is normal to the reference plane (the unit vector in the
\mathsf{z}
direction) and
\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$h$}=\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$L$}/\mu
is specific angular momentum vector of a binary. Thus,
\Omega
is computed as,

\displaystyle\Omega=\cos^{-1}(N_{\mathsf{x}}/N)\,.

The argument of periapsis
\omega
is the angle between node line and periapsis measured in the direction of motion. Therefore,

\displaystyle\omega=\cos^{-1}\bigg(\frac{\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$N$}\cdot\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$e$}}{N\,e}\bigg)\,.

When the orbit can be approximated well by the osculating one,

e
is given by the normalized Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector (
49
).
Otherwise, we define the averaged eccentricity vector by

\displaystyle\langle\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$e$}\rangle\equiv-{\left(\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$\mathsf{r}$}_{\rm min}+\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$\mathsf{r}$}_{\rm max}\right)\over\left(\mathsf{r}_{\rm min}+\mathsf{r}_{\rm max}\right)}

pointing towards the periapsis,
where
\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$\mathsf{r}$}_{\rm max}
and
\!\!\mbox{ \boldmath$\mathsf{r}$}_{\rm min}

are numerical data of position vector.
We have used both definitions and found that most results agree well.

V.3.1
Firmness of a binary and stability

As we discuss in §.
V.1
, a binary near SMBH may be broken when the tidal force by SMBH is stronger than
the mutual gravitational attractive force of a binary. We introduce a firmness parameter of a binary
\mathfrak{f}

defined by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle{{\rm gravitational~force}\over{\rm tidal~force~by~SMBH}}={{Gm_{1}m_{2}/\ell_{\rm binary}^{2}}\over{\mu\mathfrak{r}_{g}\,\ell_{\rm binary}/\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{3}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left({m_{1}+m_{2}\over 2M}\right)\left({\mathfrak{r}_{0}\over\ell_{\rm binary}}\right)^{3}\approx{1\over\epsilon^{2}}\left({\mathfrak{r}_{0}\over\mathfrak{r}_{g}}\right)\,.

If the firmness parameter
\mathfrak{f}
is smaller than
O(1)
, we expect that the tidal force will break a binary.
Hence,
\mathfrak{f}>1
is a necessary condition for stability of a binary. When we are interested in a binary motion near SMBH,
this stability condition gives
\epsilon\leq O(1)
. We can confirm numerically that our present system is really unstable
when
\epsilon\geq 0.5
. In what follows, we numerically analyze a binary system under the conditions of
\epsilon\leq 0.4
.

V.3.2
Kozai-Lidov oscillation

Here, we show some numerical examples, which shows
the KL oscillations in the long-time evolution.

We expect the KL oscillations to occur when the inclination angle is larger than the critical value.
Under the quadrupole approximation in a Newtonian hierarchical system, the critical inclination angle is given by

I_{\rm crit}^{\rm(N)}=\sin^{-1}\sqrt{2/5}\approx 39.23^{\circ}
.
In the present model, it can be obtained by the double-averaging
analysis of the Lagrange planetary equations, which shows
that the critical value is slightly larger
as the radius
\mathfrak{r}_{0}
gets smaller, and
it increases to
41.6^{\circ}
near the ISCO radius (See Appendix
B.2
).
It seems hard to obtain the exact critical value
by numerical simulations, although we
have found consistent results.

The typical time scale of the KL oscillations is given by
Suzuki et al. 2019
;
Antognini 2015
;
Shevchenko Springer, 2017

\displaystyle T_{\rm KL}\sim{P_{\rm out}^{2}\over P_{\rm in}}\,,

(50)

where
P_{\rm in}
and
P_{\rm out}
are the periods of an inner binary and of an outer binary, respectively.
For a hierarchical triple system, since
P_{\rm in}\ll P_{\rm out}
,

T_{\rm KL}\gg P_{\rm out}
, which means that the KL oscillation is a secular effect.

In the first model (Model Ic), we choose

\mathfrak{r}_{0}=3.5\mathfrak{r}_{g}
and
\epsilon=0.1
.
It corresponds to

a_{0}=0.0023\mathfrak{r}_{g}\approx 0.0045{\rm AU}

for
m_{1}=m_{2}=10M_{\odot}
and
M=10^{8}M_{\odot}
.
Since
G(m_{1}+m_{2})/c^{2}\approx 30{\rm km}\ll a_{0}
,
the binary motion can be described by a Newtonian orbit in a local inertial frame. We then adopt the initial conditions as
the eccentricity
e_{0}=0.01
and inclination
I_{0}=60^{\circ}
.
The numerical results are given in Figs.

and
3
.

VI
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we discuss motion of a binary system near SMBH.
Using Fermi-normal coordinates, we set up Newtonian self-gravitating system in the local
proper reference frame.
Assuming a circular geodesic observer around a Schwarzschild SMBH,
we write down the equations of motion of a binary.
To remove the interaction terms between the CM of a binary and its relative coordinates,
we introduce a small acceleration of the observer.
As a result, the CM follows the observer’s orbit, but its motion deviates from an exact
geodesic.
Since the relative motion is decoupled from the system, we first solve it, and then
find the motion of the CM by the perturbation equations with the small acceleration, which is given by the relative motion.

We show that there appears the KL oscillations when a binary is compact enough and
the inclination angle is larger than the critical value.
If the firmness parameter
\mathfrak{f}
is larger than a few hundreds,
the oscillations are regular.
However when
\mathfrak{f}
is around a few tens,
although we find the stable KL oscillations, but the oscillations
become irregular both in the oscillation period and in the amplitude.
Especially, if the initial inclination is large, we find an orbital flip.

One of the most interesting and important subjects of a binary system near SMBH is
the gravitational waves. When the KL oscillations is found in a binary motion,
we expect a large amount of the GW emission because the eccentricity becomes large.
The large eccentricity also provides much higher frequencies than that from a circular binary
Randall and Xianyu 2019a
;
Hoang et al. 2019
;
Gupta et al. 2020
.
Another interesting point on the GWs from the KL oscillations is
that the large amount of the GW emission repeats periodically
with the KL oscillation time scale.
It is a good advantage in the observations because we have
a certain preparation time for next observations.

There are two GW sources in a hierarchical triple system: One is the GWs from an inner binary and the other is those from the outer binary.
The time scale of the emission of gravitational waves from a circular binary is evaluated as
Maggiore 2007

\displaystyle\tau_{\rm GW}={5\over 256}{c^{5}R_{0}^{4}\over G^{3}m^{2}\mu}\,,

where
m=m_{1}+m_{2}
and
\mu=m_{1}m_{2}/(m_{1}+m_{2})
and
R_{0}
is the initial distance.
Hence, the ratio of the time scale of an outer binary to that of an inner binary
is

\displaystyle{\tau_{\rm outer}\over\tau_{\rm inner}}={m_{1}m_{2}\over M^{2}}\left({\mathfrak{r}_{0}\over\ell_{\rm binary}}\right)^{4}.

In the present model, as we discuss in §.
V.1
,
we have some constraints. From stability condition of a binary (
39
), we have

\displaystyle{\ell_{\rm binary}\over\mathfrak{r}_{0}}\,\mbox{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textstyle<}}{{\textstyle\sim}}$}}\,\left({m_{1}+m_{2}\over 2M}\right)^{1/3}\,,

while from the validity of Newtonian dynamics, we find

\displaystyle{\ell_{\rm binary}\over\mathfrak{r}_{0}}\,\mbox{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textstyle>}}{{\textstyle\sim}}$}}\,\left({m_{1}+m_{2}\over 2M}\right){\mathfrak{r}_{g}\over\mathfrak{r}_{0}}\,.

If a binary
exists near the ISCO radius (
\mathfrak{r}_{0}\sim 3\mathfrak{r}_{g}
),
we find

\displaystyle 2\times 10^{-5}\,\mbox{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textstyle<}}{{\textstyle\sim}}$}}\,{\tau_{\rm outer}\over\tau_{\rm inner}}\,\mbox{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textstyle<}}{{\textstyle\sim}}$}}\,8\times 10^{16}\,.

Hence, in most cases, gravitational waves from the outer orbit are less effective compared with those from the inner binary.
However when the binary is close to instability range, it is not the case.
In fact, in our examples discussed in this paper,
if we assume a circular binary, we find

\displaystyle{\tau_{\rm outer}\over\tau_{\rm inner}}\sim\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}3\times 10^{-3}&{\rm(Model~I)}\\
8\times 10^{-5}&{\rm(Model~II)}\\
8\times 10^{-3}&{\rm(Model~III)}\\
\end{array}\right.

The GWs from the outer binary become much larger than those from the inner binary. However if there exists the Kozai-Lidov oscillation,
the emission time scale is reduced by the factor
Peters and Mathews 1963

\displaystyle F(e_{\rm in})\approx{768\over 429}\left(1-e_{\rm in}^{2}\right)^{7/2},

when
e_{\rm in}\approx 1
.
As a result, the GWs from the inner binary may become larger that those from the outer binary.

In recent years, three-body systems and the emission of GWs from them have received significant attention
Amaro-Seoane
et al. 2010
;
Antonini and Perets 2012
;
Hoang and Naoz 2018
;
Antonini
et al. 2016b
;
Meiron et al. 2017
;
Robson et al. 2018
;
Randall and Xianyu 2019b
;
Randall and Xianyu 2019a
;
Hoang et al. 2019
;
Emami and Loeb 2019
;
Gupta et al. 2020
. Our future work will involve evaluating the GWs from the present hierarchical triple setting using the black hole perturbation approach, since near the ISCO radius the quadrupole formula may not be valid
Cardoso et al. 2021
.

In this paper, we assume that
the CM of a binary moves along a circular orbit, but
an eccentric orbit is interesting to be studied since the Kozai-Lidov oscillation may be modulated on a longer timescale
Lithwick and Naoz 2011
;
Katz et al. 2011
;
Naoz et al. 2013
;
Li et al. 2014
;
Liu et al. 2015
.
However, for such a highly eccentric orbit,
the present proper reference frame expanded
up to the second order of the spatial coordinates
x^{\hat{a}}

may not be sufficient.
We may need higher-order terms in the metric,
where the derivatives of the Riemann curvature appear
Nesterov 1999
;
Delva and Angonin 2012
.
Although the basic equations are very complicated,
such an extension is straightforward.

Another natural direction would be an extension to a rotating SMBH that may allow us to study the precession of the binary orbit around the Kerr black hole. Such systems can reveal the impact of spin on GWs emitted from a nearby binary. Recent research
Camilloni et al. 2023
considers the secular dynamics of the binary system distorted by a much larger Kerr black hole’s tidal forces. This is done by deriving the magnetic and electric tidal moment at quadrupole orders.

Hence, once we find the perturbation Hamiltoninan
\bar{\cal H}_{1}
in terms of the orbital parameters, we obtain the planetary equations.

The proper Hamiltonian is described by the orbital parameters by inserting the relation given in Eq.(
47
) with Eq. (
62
).
We then find the perturbed Hamiltonian as

\displaystyle\bar{\cal H}_{1}=\bar{{\cal H}}_{1\mathchar 45{\rm dS}}+\bar{{\cal H}}_{1\mathchar 45\bar{\cal R}}\,,

where

\displaystyle\bar{{\cal H}}_{1\mathchar 45{\rm dS}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathfrak{w}_{\rm dS}\mathsf{r}^{2}(a,e,f)\left\{n\cos I(1-e^{2})^{-3/2}(1+e\cos f)^{2}-\mathfrak{w}_{\rm dS}\left(\cos^{2}(\omega+f)+\sin^{2}(\omega+f)\cos^{2}I\right)\right\}~~~~~~~

(70)

\displaystyle\bar{{\cal H}}_{1\mathchar 45\bar{\cal R}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{r}_{g}\over 4\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{3}}\mathsf{r}^{2}(a,e,f)\Big\{1-3(1+\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{w}_{0}^{2})\left[\cos(\Omega+\mathfrak{w}_{\rm R}\tau)\cos(\omega+f)-\sin(\Omega+\mathfrak{w}_{\rm R}\tau)\sin(\omega+f)\cos I\right]^{2}

(71)

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~+3\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{w}_{0}^{2}\sin^{2}(\omega+f)\sin^{2}I\Big\}\,.

We then obtain the planetary equations for the present hierarchical triple system from Eqs. (
64
) - (
69
).

B.1
Double-averaging approach

Here, instead of solving the Lagrange planetary equations themselves,
which is equivalent to our numerical methods in the text,
we take average of the perturbed Hamiltonian over two periods,
the inner and outer orbital periods,
and then analyze the simplified equations, because we are interested in the long-time behaviour of the present system
such as the Kozai-Lidov mechanism.

The double-averaged Hamiltonian is defined by

\displaystyle\langle\langle\bar{\cal H}_{1}\rangle\rangle\equiv{1\over 2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\mathfrak{l}_{\rm out}\left({1\over 2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\mathfrak{l}\,\bar{\cal H}_{1}\right)

Since the outer orbit is circular, we find that
\mathfrak{l}_{\rm out}=f_{\rm out}=\mathfrak{w}_{0}\tau
.
We also have

\displaystyle d\mathfrak{l}={1\over\sqrt{1-e^{2}}}\left({\mathsf{r}\over a}\right)^{2}df\,.

Inserting Eqs. (
70
) and (
71
) into the above integrals,
we find the doubly-averaged Hamiltonian as

\displaystyle\langle\langle\bar{\cal H}_{1}\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathfrak{w}_{\rm dS}na^{2}\sqrt{1-e^{2}}\cos I-{a^{2}\over 8}\Big\{(2+3e^{2})\Big[\mathfrak{w}_{\rm dS}^{2}(3+\cos 2I)+{\mathfrak{r}_{g}\over 8\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{3}}(1+3\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{w}_{0}^{2})(1+3\cos 2I)\Big]

(72)

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~+10e^{2}\sin^{2}I\cos 2\omega\left[\mathfrak{w}_{\rm dS}^{2}+{3\mathfrak{r}_{g}\over 8\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{3}}(1+3\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{w}_{0}^{2})\right]\Big\}.

Using the doubly-averaged Hamiltonian Eq.(
72
), we obtain the Lagrange planetary equations as

\displaystyle\dot{e}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{5\over 4}\left(\mathfrak{w}_{\rm dS}^{2}+{3\mathfrak{r}_{g}\over 8\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{3}}(1+3\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{w}_{0}^{2})\right){e\sqrt{1-e^{2}}\over n}\left(1-\cos 2I\right)\sin(2\omega),

(73)

\displaystyle\dot{I}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{5\over 4}\left(\mathfrak{w}_{\rm dS}^{2}+{3\mathfrak{r}_{g}\over 8\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{3}}(1+3\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{w}_{0}^{2})\right){e^{2}\over n\sqrt{1-e^{2}}}\sin 2I\sin(2\omega),

(74)

\displaystyle\dot{\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over 4n}\left(\mathfrak{w}_{\rm dS}^{2}+{3\mathfrak{r}_{g}\over 8\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{3}}(1+3\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{w}_{0}^{2})\right)\Big[\sqrt{1-e^{2}}[3+5\cos 2I+5\left(1-\cos 2I\right)\cos 2\omega]

(75)

\displaystyle+{5e^{2}\over\sqrt{1-e^{2}}}(1+\cos 2I)(1-\cos 2\omega)\Big]+{2\sqrt{1-e^{2}}\over n}\mathfrak{w}_{\rm dS}^{2},

\displaystyle\dot{\Omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathfrak{w}_{\rm dS}+{\cos I\over 2n\sqrt{1-e^{2}}}\left(\mathfrak{w}_{\rm dS}^{2}+{3\over 8}{\mathfrak{r}_{g}\over\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{3}}(1+3\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{w}_{0}^{2})\right)\left[-(2+3e^{2})+5e^{2}\cos(2\omega)\right].

(76)

The semi-major axis
a
is constant in the present approximation.
Also, we can easily check from Eqs. (
73
) and (
74
) that

\displaystyle{d\over d\tau}\left(\sqrt{1-e^{2}}\cos I\right)=0\,,

which corresponds to conservation of the
z
-component of the angular momentum.

B.2
KL oscillations

Introducing a “potential” by

V_{S}\equiv-\langle\langle\bar{\cal H}_{1}\rangle\rangle
,
we rewrite the above planetary equations as

\displaystyle\dot{e}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{\sqrt{1-e^{2}}\over na^{2}e}{\partial{V_{S}}\over\partial\omega},

(77)

\displaystyle\dot{I}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cos I\over na^{2}\sin I\sqrt{1-e^{2}}}{\partial{V_{S}}\over\partial\omega},

(78)

\displaystyle\dot{\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\sqrt{1-e^{2}}\over na^{2}e}{\partial{V_{S}}\over\partial e}-{\cos I\over na^{2}\sin I\sqrt{1-e^{2}}}{\partial{V_{S}}\over\partial I},

(79)

\displaystyle\dot{\Omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over na^{2}\sin I\sqrt{1-e^{2}}}{\partial{V_{S}}\over\partial I}\,.

(80)

We obtain the closed form of a set of the differential equations
for
e\,,I\,,
and
\omega
by Eqs. (
77
), (
78
)
and (
79
).
It gives several properties of KL oscillations such as
the oscillation amplitude of the eccentricity
and the oscillation time scale as analyzed in Newtonian and 1PN hierarchical triple system
Suzuki et al. 2020
.

The potential is written by use of

\eta\equiv\sqrt{1-e^{2}}
and
\mu_{I}\equiv\cos I

as

\displaystyle V_{S}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle-\langle\langle\bar{\cal H}_{1}\rangle\rangle={a^{2}\mathfrak{r}_{g}(1+3\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{w}_{0}^{2})\over 32\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{3}}v_{S}(\eta,\mu_{I}),

where

\displaystyle v_{S}(\eta,\mu_{I})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle 2(-1+3\mu_{I}^{2}\eta^{2})(1+\alpha_{\rm dS})+12C_{KL}+4\alpha_{\rm dS}\left(2-{3n\over\mathfrak{w}_{\rm dS}}\mu_{I}\eta\right),

with

\displaystyle\alpha_{\rm dS}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle{8\mathfrak{w}_{\rm dS}^{2}\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{3}\over 3\mathfrak{r}_{g}(1+3\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{w}_{0}^{2})},

\displaystyle C_{\rm KL}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle(1-\eta^{2})\left[(1+2\alpha_{\rm dS})-{5\over 2}(1+\alpha_{\rm dS})(1-\mu_{I}^{2})\sin^{2}\omega\right].

Note that when
\alpha_{\rm dS}=0
, we find the same equations for Newtoninan hierarchical triple system with quadrupole approximation.
The terms with
\alpha_{\rm dS}
give relativistic corrections.

Introducing the normalized time
\tilde{\tau}
, which is defined by

\displaystyle\tilde{\tau}\equiv{\tau\over\tau_{\rm KL}},

with the typical KL time scale

\displaystyle\tau_{\rm KL}\equiv{32n\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{3}\over\mathfrak{r}_{g}(1+3\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{w}_{0}^{2})}\left(\sim{P_{\rm out}^{2}\over P_{\rm in}}\right)\,,

the above planetary equation is rewritten as

\displaystyle{d\eta\over d\tilde{\tau}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\partial{v_{S}}\over\partial\omega},

\displaystyle{1\over\mu_{I}}{d\mu_{I}\over d\tilde{\tau}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{1\over\eta}{\partial{v_{S}}\over\partial\omega},

\displaystyle{d\omega\over d\tilde{\tau}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{\partial{v_{S}}\over\partial\eta}+{\mu_{I}\over\eta}{\partial{v_{S}}\over\partial\mu_{I}}.

From these equations, we can easily show that

\displaystyle{d(\mu_{I}\eta)\over d\tilde{\tau}}=0~\,,~~~{dv_{S}\over d\tilde{\tau}}=0\,,

which means there exist two conserved quantities
\vartheta\equiv\mu_{I}\eta
and
C_{\rm KL}
just as the Newtoninan and 1PN hierarchical triple system under dipole approximation.
Using these two conserved quantities, we obtain a single equation for
\eta
as

\displaystyle{d\eta^{2}\over d\tilde{\tau}}=-24\sqrt{2}\sqrt{f(\eta^{2})g(\eta^{2})},

with

\displaystyle f(\eta^{2})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle(1+2\alpha_{\rm dS})(1-\eta^{2})-C_{\rm KL},

\displaystyle g(\eta^{2})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle-5(1+\alpha_{\rm dS})\vartheta^{2}+\left[5(1+\alpha_{\rm dS})\vartheta^{2}+3+\alpha_{\rm dS}+2C_{\rm KL}\right]\eta^{2}-(3+\alpha_{\rm dS})\eta^{4}.

Setting
\xi=\eta^{2}
, we find

\displaystyle{d\xi\over d\tilde{\tau}}=-24\sqrt{2(1+2\alpha_{\rm dS})(3+\alpha_{\rm dS})}\sqrt{(\xi-\xi_{0})(\xi-\xi_{+})(\xi-\xi_{-})},

where

\displaystyle\xi_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-{C_{\rm KL}\over 1+2\alpha_{\rm dS}},

\displaystyle\xi_{\pm}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over 2}\Big[\left(1+{5(1+\alpha_{\rm dS})\over 3+\alpha_{\rm dS}}\vartheta^{2}+{2\over 3+\alpha_{\rm dS}}C_{\rm KL}\right)\pm\sqrt{\left(1+{5(1+\alpha_{\rm dS})\over 3+\alpha_{\rm dS}}\vartheta^{2}+{2\over 3+\alpha_{\rm dS}}C_{\rm KL}\right)^{2}-{20(1+\alpha_{\rm dS})\over 3+\alpha_{\rm dS}}\vartheta^{2}}\Big],

are the solutions of
f(\xi)=0
and
g(\xi)=0
, respectively.

We can find the relativistic
corrections with
\alpha_{\rm dS}
, which is evaluated as

\displaystyle\alpha_{\rm dS}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{3\mathfrak{r}_{g}^{2}\over\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{2}\left(1+\sqrt{1-{3\mathfrak{r}_{g}\over 2\mathfrak{r}_{0}}}\right)^{2}}\leq{1\over 3\left(1+{1\over\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2}}\approx 0.114382.

The equality is found in the case of the ISCO radius (
\mathfrak{r}_{0}=3\mathfrak{r}_{g})
.

Analyzing the above equation, we find that there exists KL oscillation in this system just the same as in Newtonian hierarchical triple system, and we can classify the KL solutions by the sign of
C_{\rm KL}
into two cases:
(1)
C_{\rm KL}>0
(rotation) and (2)
C_{\rm KL}<0
(libration).

B.2.1
C_{\rm KL}>0
(rotation)

In this case,
0<\xi_{-}<1<\xi_{+}
and
0<\xi_{0}<1
.
This is possible if

\displaystyle 0<C_{\rm KL}<1+2\alpha_{\rm dS}\,.

Hence we find the maximum and minimum values of the eccentricity as

\displaystyle e_{\rm max}=\sqrt{1-\xi_{-}}~~,~~e_{\rm min}=\sqrt{1-\xi_{0}}.

The KL time scale is given by

\displaystyle T_{\rm KL}=\tau_{\rm KL}\,\mathfrak{T}_{\rm KL}^{\rm(rot)},

(81)

where

\displaystyle\mathfrak{T}_{\rm KL}^{\rm(rot)}\equiv{1\over 12\sqrt{2(1+2\alpha_{\rm dS})(3+\alpha_{\rm dS})}}\times\int_{\xi_{-}}^{\xi_{0}}d\xi{1\over\sqrt{(\xi-\xi_{0})(\xi-\xi_{+})(\xi-\xi_{-})}}\,.~~~~~~~

(82)

B.2.2
C_{\rm KL}<0
(libration)

Since
0<\xi_{-}<\xi_{+}<1
and
\xi_{0}<0
in this case,
we find

\displaystyle e_{\rm max}=\sqrt{1-\xi_{-}}\,,~~~e_{\rm min}=\sqrt{1-\xi_{+}}.

It occurs when

\displaystyle-{3+\alpha_{\rm dS}\over 2}<C_{\rm KL}<0\,,~~~{\rm and}~~~\vartheta<{(\sqrt{3+\alpha_{\rm dS}}-\sqrt{-2C_{\rm KL}})\over\sqrt{5(1+\alpha_{\rm dS})}}\,.

The KL time scale is given by

\displaystyle T_{\rm KL}=\tau_{\rm KL}\,\mathfrak{T}_{\rm KL}^{\rm(lib)},

(83)

where

\displaystyle\mathfrak{T}_{\rm KL}^{\rm(lib)}\equiv{1\over 12\sqrt{2(1+2\alpha_{\rm dS})(3+\alpha_{\rm dS})}}\times\int_{\xi_{-}}^{\xi_{+}}d\xi{1\over\sqrt{(\xi-\xi_{0})(\xi-\xi_{+})(\xi-\xi_{-})}}\,.~~~~~~~

(84)

The maximum and minimum values of the eccentricity in the KL oscillations

\vartheta
and
C_{\rm KL}
.
