Abstract
. It is well known that the area U U of the triangle formed by three tangents to a parabola X X is half of the area T T of the triangle formed by joining their points of contact. In this article, we study some properties of U U and T T for strictly convex plane curves. As a result, we establish a characterization for parabolas.



Dong-Soo Kim and Kyu-Chul Shim

1.
Introduction

Let
X=X(s)
be a unit speed smooth curve in the plane

{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
with nonvanishing curvature,
and let
A=X(s)
,
A_{i}=X(s+h_{i}),i=1,2,
be three distinct neighboring points on
X
.
Denote by
\ell
,
\ell_{1}
,
\ell_{2}
the tangent
lines passing through the points
A,A_{1},A_{2}
and by
B,B_{1},B_{2}

the intersection points
\ell_{1}\cap\ell_{2}
,
\ell\cap\ell_{1}
,

\ell\cap\ell_{2}
, respectively.
It is well known that the area
U(s,h_{1},h_{2})=|\bigtriangleup BB_{1}B_{2}|
of the triangle
formed by three tangents to a parabola is half of the area

T(s,h_{1},h_{2})=|\bigtriangleup AA_{1}A_{2}|
of the
triangle formed by joining their points of contact ([1]).

The present article studies whether this property exhaustively characterizes parabolas.

Usually, a regular plane curve
X:I\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
defined on an open interval
is called
convex
if, for all
t\in I
, the trace
X(I)
lies
entirely on one side of the closed half-plane determined by the tangent line at
X(t)
([2]).

Hereafter, we will say that a simple convex curve
X
in the plane

{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
is
strictly convex
if the curve is smooth (that is, of class
C^{(3)}
) and is of positive curvature
\kappa

with respect to the unit normal
N
pointing to the convex side.
Hence, in this case we have
\kappa(s)=\left<X^{\prime\prime}(s),N(X(s))\right>>0
, where
X(s)
is an arclength parametrization of
X
.

For a smooth function
f:I\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
defined on an open interval, we will also say that
f
is

strictly convex
if the graph of
f
has positive curvature
\kappa
with respect to the upward unit normal
N
. This condition is equivalent to the positivity of
f^{\prime\prime}(x)
on
I
.

Suppose that
X
is a strictly convex curve in the plane

{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
with the unit normal
N
pointing to the convex side.
For a fixed point
P=A\in X
, and for a sufficiently small
h>0
, consider the line
m
passing through

P+hN(P)
which is parallel to
the tangent
\ell
of
X
at
P
.
Let us denote by
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
the points where the line
m
intersects the curve
X
.
We denote by
L_{P}(h)
the length
|A_{1}A_{2}|
of the chord
A_{1}A_{2}
.

Let us denote by
\ell_{1}
,
\ell_{2}
the tangent
lines passing through the points
A_{1},A_{2}
and by
B,B_{1},B_{2}

the intersection points
\ell_{1}\cap\ell_{2}
,
\ell\cap\ell_{1}
,

\ell\cap\ell_{2}
, respectively. We denote by
T_{P}(h),U_{P}(h)
the area
|\bigtriangleup AA_{1}A_{2}|
,

|\bigtriangleup BB_{1}B_{2}|
, of triangles, respectively.
Then, obviously we have
T_{P}(h)=\frac{h}{2}L_{P}(h)
.

In this paper, first of all, in Section 2 we prove the following:

Theorem 1.

Let
X
denote a strictly convex
C^{(3)}
curve in the plane

{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{T_{P}(h)}{h\sqrt{h}}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\kappa(P)}}

(1.1)

and

\displaystyle\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{U_{P}(h)}{h\sqrt{h}}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2\sqrt{\kappa(P)}}.

(1.2)

Next in Section 3, using Theorem 1 we characterize parabolas as follows.

Theorem 2.

Let
X=X(s)
denote a strictly convex
C^{(3)}
curve in the plane

{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. Suppose that for all
s
and sufficiently small
h_{i},i=1,2
,
the curve
X
satisfies

\displaystyle U(s,h_{1},h_{2})=\lambda(s)T(s,h_{1},h_{2}).

(1.3)

Then, we have
\lambda(s)=\frac{1}{2}
and
X
is an open part of a parabola.

In [6], Krawczyk showed that for a strictly convex
C^{(4)}
curve
X=X(s)
in the plane

{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, the following holds:

\displaystyle\lim_{h_{1},h_{2}\rightarrow 0}\frac{T(s,h_{1},h_{2})}{U(s,h_{1},h_{2})}=2.

(1.4)

His application of (1.4) states that if a strictly convex
C^{(4)}
curve
X=X(s)
in the plane

{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
satisfies (1.3), then
\lambda(s)=\frac{1}{2}
and
X
is an open part of
the graph of a quadratic polynomial.

But, for example, consider a function
f(x)
given by

\displaystyle y=\frac{2\sqrt{a}cx+1-\sqrt{4\sqrt{a}cx+1}}{2c^{2}},

(3.14)

where
a,c>0
. Then, the function
f
is defined on
I=(-\frac{1}{4\sqrt{a}c},\infty)
.
Its graph
X
is strictly convex and satisfies (1.3) with
\lambda=\frac{1}{2}
.
Note that
X
is not
the graph of a quadratic polynomial, but an open part of the parabola given in (3.16) in Section 3.

In [5], the first
author and Y. H. Kim established five characterizations of parabolas, which are the converses of
well-known properties of parabolas originally due to Archimedes
([8]). In [3] and [4], they also proved the higher dimensional analogues of some results in [5].

Among the graphs of functions, B. Richmond and T. Richmond established a dozen characterizations of parabolas
using elementary techniques ([7]). In their paper, parabola means the graph of a quadratic polynomial in one
variable.

Finally, we pose a question as follows.

Question 3.

Let X be a strictly convex
C^{(3)}
plane curve.
Suppose that for each
P\in X
there exists a positive number
\epsilon=\epsilon(P)>0

such that
U_{P}(h)=T_{P}(h)/2
for all
h
with
0<h<\epsilon(P)
.
Then, is it an open part of a parabola?

Throughout this article, all curves are of class
C^{(3)}
and connected, unless otherwise mentioned.

2.
Preliminaries and Theorem 1

In order to prove Theorem 1, we need the following lemma ([5]).

Lemma 4.
Suppose that
X
is a strictly convex
C^{(3)}
curve in the plane

{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
with the unit normal
N
pointing to the convex side. Then
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{\sqrt{h}}L_{P}(h)=\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\kappa(P)}},

(2.1)

where
\kappa(P)
is the curvature of
X
at
P
with respect to the unit normal
N
.

First of all, we give a proof of (1.1) in Theorem 1. Since
T_{P}(h)=\frac{h}{2}L_{P}(h)
, it
follows from Lemma 4 that the following holds:

\displaystyle\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{h\sqrt{h}}T_{P}(h)=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\kappa(P)}}.

(1.1)

In order to prove (1.2) in Theorem 1, we fix an arbitrary point
P
on
X
.
Then, we may take a coordinate system
(x,y)

of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
:
P
is taken to be the origin
(0,0)
and
x
-axis is the tangent line
\ell
of
X
at
P
.
Furthermore, we may regard
X
to be locally the graph of a non-negative strictly convex function
f:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}

with
f(0)=f^{\prime}(0)=0
. Then
N
is the upward unit normal.

Since the curve
X
is of class
C^{(3)}
, the Taylor’s formula of
f(x)
is given by

f(x)=ax^{2}+g(x),

(2.2)

where
2a=f^{\prime\prime}(0)
and
g(x)
is an
O(|x|^{3})
function.
From
\kappa(P)=f^{\prime\prime}(0)>0
, we see that
a
is positive.

For a sufficiently small
h>0
, we denote by
A_{1}(s,f(s))
and
A_{2}(t,f(t))

the points where the line
m:y=h
meets the curve
X
with
s<0<t
.
Then
f(s)=f(t)=h
and we get
B_{1}(s-h/f^{\prime}(s),0)
,
B_{2}(t-h/f^{\prime}(t),0)
and
B(x_{0},y_{0})
with

x_{0}=\frac{tf^{\prime}(t)-sf^{\prime}(s)}{f^{\prime}(t)-f^{\prime}(s)}

(2.3)

and

y_{0}=\frac{(t-s)f^{\prime}(t)f^{\prime}(s)+h(f^{\prime}(t)-f^{\prime}(s))}{f^{\prime}(t)-f^{\prime}(s)}.

(2.4)

Noting that
L_{P}(h)=t-s
, one obtains

\displaystyle 2U_{P}(h)

\displaystyle=\{t-s-\frac{h}{f^{\prime}(t)}+\frac{h}{f^{\prime}(s)}\}(-y_{0})

(2.5)

\displaystyle=h^{2}\frac{(f^{\prime}(t)-f^{\prime}(s))}{-f^{\prime}(s)f^{\prime}(t)}-2hL_{P}(h)+\frac{-f^{\prime}(s)f^{\prime}(t)}{f^{\prime}(t)-f^{\prime}(s)}L_{P}(h)^{2}.

It follows from (2.5) that

\displaystyle 2\frac{U_{P}(h)}{h\sqrt{h}}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{P}(h)-2\frac{L_{P}(h)}{\sqrt{h}}+\frac{1}{\alpha_{P}(h)}(\frac{L_{P}(h)}{\sqrt{h}})^{2},

(2.6)

where we denote

\displaystyle\alpha_{P}(h)=\sqrt{h}\frac{(f^{\prime}(t)-f^{\prime}(s))}{-f^{\prime}(s)f^{\prime}(t)}.

(2.7)

Finally, we prove a lemma, which together with (2.6) and Lemma 4, completes the proof of (2) in Theorem 1.

Lemma 5.
We have the following.

\displaystyle\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\alpha_{P}(h)=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\kappa(P)}}.

(2.8)

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle\alpha_{P}(h)=\frac{\beta_{P}(h)}{\gamma_{P}(h)},

(2.9)

where we denote

\displaystyle\beta_{P}(h)=\frac{f^{\prime}(t)-f^{\prime}(s)}{t-s}

(2.10)

and

\displaystyle\gamma_{P}(h)=\frac{-f^{\prime}(s)f^{\prime}(t)}{\sqrt{h}(t-s)}.

(2.11)

Applying mean value theorem to the derivative
f^{\prime}(x)
of
f(x)
shows
that as
h
tends to
0
,
\beta_{P}(h)
goes to
f^{\prime\prime}(0)=\kappa(P)
.
To get the limit of
\gamma_{P}(h)
,
we put

\displaystyle\delta_{P}(h)=\frac{f^{\prime}(s)f^{\prime}(t)}{st}

(2.12)

and

\displaystyle\eta_{P}(h)=\frac{-st}{\sqrt{h}(t-s)}.

(2.13)

Then, we have

\displaystyle\gamma_{P}(h)=\delta_{P}(h)\eta_{P}(h).

(2.14)

Note that

\displaystyle\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\delta_{P}(h)=\kappa(P)^{2}.

(2.15)

If we use
L_{P}(h)=t-s
,
\eta_{P}(h)
can be written as

\displaystyle\eta_{P}(h)=(-\frac{st}{h})/(\frac{L_{P}(h)}{\sqrt{h}})

(2.16)

and the numerator of (2.16) can be decomposed as

\displaystyle-\frac{st}{h}=(\frac{L_{P}(h)}{\sqrt{h}}-\frac{t}{\sqrt{h}})\frac{t}{\sqrt{h}}.

(2.17)

Now, to obtain the limit of
\frac{t}{\sqrt{h}}
, we use (2.2).
Recalling that
\kappa(P)=f^{\prime\prime}(0)=2a
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{t}{\sqrt{h}}=\frac{t}{\sqrt{at^{2}+g(t)}}.

(2.18)

Since
g(x)
is an
O(|x|^{3})
function, (2.18) implies that

\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}t/\sqrt{h}=1/\sqrt{a}
.
Hence, together with (2.17), Lemma 4 shows that
\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}(-st)/h=1/a
,
and hence from (2.16) we get

\displaystyle\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\eta_{P}(h)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{a}}.

(2.19)

Thus, it follows from (2.14) and (2.15) that

\displaystyle\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\gamma_{P}(h)=2a\sqrt{a}.

(2.20)

Using
\kappa(P)=2a
, together with (2.9) and (2.10), (2.20) completes the proof of Lemma 5.
\quad\square

3.
Proof of Theorem 2

In this section, we prove Theorem 2.

Suppose that
X=X(s)
denote a strictly convex
C^{(3)}
curve in the plane

{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
which satisfies for all
s
and sufficiently small
h_{i},i=1,2
,

\displaystyle U(s,h_{1},h_{2})=\lambda(s)T(s,h_{1},h_{2}).

(1.3)

Then, in particular, for all
P=X(s)
and sufficiently small
h>0
the curve
X
satisfies

\displaystyle U_{P}(h)=\lambda(P)T_{P}(h).

(3.1)

Hence, Theorem 1 implies that
\lambda(P)=\frac{1}{2}
.

In order to prove the remaining part of Theorem 2,
first, we fix an arbitrary point
A
on
X
.
As in Section 1, we take a coordinate system
(x,y)

of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
:
A
is taken to be the origin
(0,0)

and
x
-axis is the tangent line
\ell
of
X
at
A
.
Furthermore, we may regard
X
to be locally the graph of a non-negative strictly convex
function
f:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}

with
f(0)=f^{\prime}(0)=0
and
2a=f^{\prime\prime}(0)>0
.

For sufficiently small
|s|
and
|t|
with
0<s<t
or
t<s<0
,
we let
A_{1}=(s,f(s)),A_{2}=(t,f(t))
be two neighboring points of
A
on
X
.
Then, the area
T(s,t)
of the triangle
\bigtriangleup AA_{1}A_{2}
is given by

\displaystyle 2\epsilon T(s,t)

\displaystyle=(sf(t)-tf(s)),

(3.2)

where
\epsilon=1
if
0<s<t
and
\epsilon=-1
if
t<s<0
.

Denote by
\ell
,
\ell_{1}
,
\ell_{2}
the tangent
lines passing through the points
A,A_{1},A_{2}
and by
B,B_{1},B_{2}

the intersection points
\ell_{1}\cap\ell_{2}
,
\ell\cap\ell_{1}
,

\ell\cap\ell_{2}
, respectively.
Then we have

B_{1}(s-f(s)/f^{\prime}(s),0)
,
B_{2}(t-f(t)/f^{\prime}(t),0)
and
B(x_{0},y_{0})
with

y_{0}=\frac{(t-s)f^{\prime}(t)f^{\prime}(s)+f(s)f^{\prime}(t)-f^{\prime}(s)f(t)}{f^{\prime}(t)-f^{\prime}(s)}.

(3.3)

Hence the area
U(s,t)
of the triangle
\bigtriangleup BB_{1}B_{2}
is given by

\displaystyle 2\epsilon U(s,t)

\displaystyle=\{t-s-\frac{f(t)}{f^{\prime}(t)}+\frac{f(s)}{f^{\prime}(s)}\}(-y_{0})

(3.4)

\displaystyle=\frac{\{(t-s)f^{\prime}(t)f^{\prime}(s)+f(s)f^{\prime}(t)-f^{\prime}(s)f(t)\}^{2}}{f^{\prime}(s)f^{\prime}(t)(f^{\prime}(t)-f^{\prime}(s))}.

Second, we prove

Lemma 6.
The function
f
satisfies the following:

\displaystyle f(t)f^{\prime}(t)^{2}=4a(tf^{\prime}(t)-f(t))^{2},

(3.5)

where
a
is given by
f^{\prime\prime}(0)=2a
.

Proof.
Since the curve
X
satisfies (1.3) with
\lambda=1/2
, we get

2U(s,t)=T(s,t)
. By letting
s\rightarrow 0
, from (3.2) we get

\displaystyle\epsilon\lim_{s\rightarrow 0}\frac{T(s,t)}{s}=\frac{f(t)}{2},

(3.6)

where we use
f^{\prime}(0)=0
. From (3.4) we also get

\displaystyle 2\epsilon\lim_{s\rightarrow 0}\frac{U(s,t)}{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{\{f^{\prime\prime}(0)tf^{\prime}(t)-f^{\prime\prime}(0)f(t)\}^{2}}{f^{\prime\prime}(0)f^{\prime}(t)^{2}}

(3.7)

\displaystyle=2a\frac{\{tf^{\prime}(t)-f(t)\}^{2}}{f^{\prime}(t)^{2}},

where we use
f^{\prime}(0)=0
and
f^{\prime\prime}(0)=2a>0
. Together with (3.6), (3.7) completes the proof.
\quad\square

Third, we prove

Lemma 7.
The function
f
satisfies the following:

\displaystyle 2f(t)^{2}f^{\prime\prime}(t)=f^{\prime}(t)^{2}\{tf^{\prime}(t)-f(t)\}.

(3.8)

Proof.
By letting
s\rightarrow t
, we get from (3.2)

\displaystyle\epsilon\lim_{s\rightarrow t}\frac{T(s,t)}{s-t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\lim_{s\rightarrow t}\frac{sf(t)-tf(s)}{s-t}

(3.9)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(f(t)-tf^{\prime}(t)).

On the other hand, from (3.4) we get

\displaystyle 2\epsilon\lim_{s\rightarrow t}\frac{U(s,t)}{s-t}

\displaystyle=\lim_{s\rightarrow t}\frac{\{(t-s)f^{\prime}(t)f^{\prime}(s)+f(s)f^{\prime}(t)-f^{\prime}(s)f(t)\}^{2}}{(s-t)f^{\prime}(s)f^{\prime}(t)(f^{\prime}(t)-f^{\prime}(s))}

(3.10)

\displaystyle=-\frac{f(t)^{2}f^{\prime\prime}(t)}{f^{\prime}(t)^{2}}.

Since
T=2U
, together with (3.9), (3.10) completes the proof.
\quad\square

By eliminating
tf^{\prime}(t)-f(t)
from (3.5) and (3.8), we get

\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{4\sqrt{a}}\frac{f^{\prime}(t)^{3}}{f(t)^{3/2}}.

(3.11)

Letting
y=f(t)
, a standard method of ordinary differential equations
using the substitution
w=dy/dt
and
y^{\prime\prime}(t)=w(dw/dy)
leads to

\displaystyle dt=(\frac{1}{2\sqrt{ay}}+c)dy,

(3.12)

where
c
is a constant. Since
f(0)=0
, we obtain from (3.12)

\displaystyle t=\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}(\sqrt{y}+cy).

(3.13)

After replacing
t
by
x
, we have for
y=f(x)

\displaystyle y=\begin{cases}\frac{2\sqrt{a}cx+1-\sqrt{4\sqrt{a}cx+1}}{2c^{2}},&\text{if $c\neq 0,$}\\
ax^{2},&\text{if $c=0.$}\end{cases}

(3.14)

Note that

\displaystyle f(0)=f^{\prime}(0)=0,f^{\prime\prime}(0)=2a\quad\text{and}\quad f^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)=-12\sqrt{a}ac\quad\text{or}\quad 0.

(3.15)

It follows from (3.14) that the curve
X
around an arbitrary point
A

is an open part of the parabola defined by

\displaystyle ax^{2}-2\sqrt{a}cxy+c^{2}y^{2}-y=0.

(3.16)

Finally using (3.15), in the same manner as in [5], we can show that the curve
X
is globally an open
part of a parabola. This completes the proof of Theorem 2.

4.
Corollaries and examples

In this section, we give some corollaries and examples.

Suppose that
X=X(s)
is a strictly convex
C^{(3)}
curve in the plane

{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
which satisfies for all
s
and sufficiently small
h_{i},i=1,2
,

\displaystyle U(s,h_{1},h_{2})=\lambda(s)T(s,h_{1},h_{2})^{\mu(s)},

(4.1)

where
\lambda(s)
and
\mu(s)
are some functions.
Then, in particular, for all
P=X(s)
and sufficiently small
h>0
the curve
X
satisfies

\displaystyle U_{P}(h)=\lambda(P)T_{P}(h)^{\mu(P)}.

(4.2)

Using Theorem 1, by letting
h\rightarrow 0
we see that
\mu(P)=1
.
Hence, Theorem 1 again implies that
\lambda(P)=\frac{1}{2}
.

Thus, from Theorem 2 we get

Corollary 8.
Let
X
denote a strictly convex curve in the plane
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.
Then, the following are equivalent.

1)
X
satisfies (4.1) for some functions
\lambda(s)
and
\mu(s)
.

2)
X
satisfies (4.1) with
\lambda=\frac{1}{2}
and
\mu=1
.

3)
X
is an open part of a parabola.

Finally, we give an example of a convex curve which satisfies

\displaystyle U_{P}(h)=\frac{1}{2}T_{P}(h).

(1.5)

for sufficiently small
h>0
at every point
P\in X
, but it is not a parabola.
Note that the example is not of class
C^{(2)}
, and hence it is not strictly convex either.

Example 9.

Consider the graph
X
of a function
f:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
which is given by
for some positive distinct constants
a
and
b

\displaystyle f(x)=\begin{cases}ax^{2},&\text{if $x<0,$}\\
bx^{2},&\text{if $x\geq 0.$}\end{cases}

(4.3)

It is straightforward to show that if
P
is the origin, then for all
h
we have

\displaystyle U_{P}(h)=\frac{1}{2}T_{P}(h).

(4.4)

Hence
X
satisfies
U_{P}(h)=T_{P}(h)/2
at the origin for all
h>0
. If
P\in X
is not the origin, then
there exists a positive number
\varepsilon(P)
such that
for every positive number
h
with
h<\varepsilon(P)
,

X
satisfies
U_{P}(h)=T_{P}(h)/2
.

Thus,
X
satisfies
U_{P}(h)=T_{P}(h)/2
for sufficiently small
h>0
at every point
P\in X
. But it is not a parabola.
