A thousand fermions in a 3D harmonic trap via Monte Carlo simulations
Siu A. Chin

Abstract
By use of a special wave function derived from similarly transformed propagators, this work shows that the energy of a thousand spin-balanced fermions in a three-dimensional harmonic potential can be accurately computed using the Monte Carlo method.



I
Introduction

It is well known that the sign problem has plagued the Monte Carlo simulations of many-fermion systems
in Path Integral
cep96
, Diffusion
ass07
and Ground State Path Integral
sar00
;
yan17
;
chin20

Monte Carlo methods. Even when there is no sign problem, as in the Variational Monte Carlo (VMC) method
with a trial wave function of the form

\Psi={\det}_{\uparrow}|\phi_{k}({\bf r}_{i})|{\det}_{\downarrow}|\phi_{k}({\bf r}_{i})|\prod_{i<j}f({\bf r}_{ij}),

(1)

where
k=\{n\ell m\}
specifies a set of single particle states and
f({\bf r}_{ij})
is the Jastrow correlation function,
it remains technically burdensome to sample such a wave function for large number of fermions.
For more than, say, 40 fermions, it is very tedious to specify the set of lowest energy single particle states,
or to evaluate them in the Slater determinant
{\det}|\phi_{k}({\bf r}_{i})|
.
Except for atoms, most finite fermion systems use
the harmonic oscillator basis states. In this work, we show that there is a much simpler wave
function for describing any number of non-interacting fermions in a harmonic
oscillator, with
no need of knowing the analytical form of its single particle wave functions
.
As shown in Sect.
IV
, the energy of up to a thousand spin-balanced fermions can be
computed using this special wave function (
22
), suggesting the feasibility of
doing very large scale VMC, or even Ground State Path Integral Monte Carlo
sar00
;
yan17
;
chin20

calculations on 3D fermion systems.

The discovery of this wave function (
22
) is rather circuitous.
In order to account for the charge-density-wave (CDW) or Wigner crystal (WC)
density distributions observed in earlier calculations
yos79
;
yos83

on the fractional quantum Hall effect,
Maki and Zotos
mak83
postulated an ansatz wave function of the form

\Psi({\bf x}_{1},{\bf x}_{2}\dots{\bf x}_{N})\propto\det\Bigl|\exp[-\frac{1}{2}({\bf x}_{i}-{\bf s}_{j})^{2}]\Bigr|,

(2)

where each lowest Landau state’s position
{\bf x}_{i}
is localized at a variational position
{\bf s}_{i}
.
Unfortunately, this wave function’s ground state energy at filling factor
\nu=1/3

remains higher than that of Laughlin’s wave function
lau83
, even when
{
{\bf s}_{i}
} is the optimal two-dimensional triangular lattice.
However, this wave function remained useful for describing WC states in quantum dots
with and without a magnetic field
kai02
;
har02

when {
{\bf s}_{i}
} can be determined by minimizing the classical electron potential energy.

For a given set of {
{\bf s}_{i}
}, the ansatz wave function
(
2
) breaks translational and rotational invariance and remains
ad hoc
despite
the fact that symmetry-breaking density distributions similar to those produced by (
2
)
can be seen in unrestricted Hartree-Fock calculations
yan99
. It was not until
recently that there is a natural way of deriving such symmetry-breaking wave functions
from similarly transformed propagators
chin20
.

In this work we will first review how wave function such as (
2
) can be derived in
Sect.
II
. In Sect.
III
, we verify analytically that the special wave function (
22
)
correctly gives wave functions for up to four free fermions in a
D
-dimension harmonic oscillator.
in Appendix A, we show that the special wave function (
22
)
gives the correct wave function for any number of fermions in a 1D harmonic oscillator.
In Sect.
IV
, the Monte Carlo method is used to evaluate the ground state energy of up
to a thousand spin-balanced fermions in a
3D harmonic oscillator. Conclusions are stated in Sect.
V
.

II
similarly transformed propagators

For completeness, we give here an expanded review below on similarly transformed propagators
chin20
.
The key insight here is that the diagonal element of the imaginary time propagator

G({\bf x},{\bf x};\tau)=\langle{\bf x}|{\rm e}^{-\tau H}|{\bf x}\rangle,

(3)

needed for extracting the ground state energy and wave function
of a
D
-dimension Hamiltonian

H=-\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}+V({\bf x})

(4)

at the large
\tau
limit

\lim_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}G({\bf x},{\bf x},\tau)\longrightarrow\psi_{0}^{2}({\bf x}){\rm e}^{-\tau E_{0}}+\cdots,

(5)

is invariant under the similarity transformation

G({\bf x},{\bf x};\tau)=\phi({\bf x})\langle{\bf x}|{\rm e}^{-\tau H}|{\bf x}\rangle\phi^{-1}({\bf x})=\langle{\bf x}|\phi({\bf x}){\rm e}^{-\tau H}\phi^{-1}({\bf x})|{\bf x}\rangle=\langle{\bf x}|{\rm e}^{-\tau\tilde{H}}|{\bf x}\rangle

(6)

and can be computed equally from the transformed Hamiltonian
chin90

\widetilde{H}\rho=\phi({\bf x})H\phi^{-1}({\bf x})\rho=-\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}\rho+\nabla\cdot\Bigl[{\bf v}({\bf x})\rho\Bigr]+E_{L}({\bf x})\rho,

(7)

where the drift velocity
{\bf v}({\bf x})
and local energy
E_{L}({\bf x})
are defined by

{\bf v}({\bf x})=\frac{\nabla\phi({\bf x})}{\phi({\bf x})}\qquad{\rm and}\qquad E_{L}({\bf x})=\frac{H\phi({\bf x})}{\phi({\bf x})},

(8)

provided that
\phi({\bf x})\neq 0
at all
{\bf x}
.

As an example of this invariance, consider the case of the harmonic oscillator
with
V({\bf x})={\bf x}^{2}/2
.
The exact propagator for
H
is known to be
fey72

G({\bf x},{\bf x}_{0};\tau)=\frac{1}{[2\pi\sinh(\tau)]^{D/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sinh(\tau)}[\cosh(\tau)({\bf x}^{2}+{\bf x}_{0}^{2})-2{\bf x}\cdot{\bf x}_{0}]\right),

(9)

while that of
\widetilde{H}
, with
\phi({\bf x})=\psi_{0}({\bf x})=\exp(-{\bf x}^{2}/2)
, is the
Ornstein-Uhlenbeck
uhl
propagator

\widetilde{G}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{0};\tau)=\frac{1}{[2\pi T(\tau)]^{D/2}}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2T(\tau)}({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{0}{\rm e}^{-\tau})^{2}\right]{\rm e}^{-\tau E_{0}},

(10)

where
T(\tau)=(1-{\rm e}^{-2\tau})/2
and
E_{0}=D/2
. Despite their distinct appearances, they are
indeed the same when
{\bf x}_{0}={\bf x}
:

\displaystyle G({\bf x},{\bf x};\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{[2\pi T(\tau)]^{D/2}}{\rm e}^{-\tau E_{0}}\exp\left(-\frac{{\bf x}^{2}}{\sinh(\tau)}[\cosh(\tau)-1]\right),

(11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{[2\pi T(\tau)]^{D/2}}{\rm e}^{-\tau E_{0}}\exp\left(-\frac{{\bf x}^{2}}{2T(\tau)}[1+{\rm e}^{-2\tau}-2{\rm e}^{-\tau}]\right).

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}({\bf x},{\bf x};\tau).

The reason why one should consider the transformed propagator is that
while the exact propagator (
6
) can be computed using
H
or
\widetilde{H}

as above, their low order (in
\tau
) approximate propagators can be very different.
The first-order propagator computed from
H
and
\widetilde{H}
are respectively

\displaystyle G_{1}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{0};\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{[2\pi\tau]^{D/2}}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2\tau}({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{0})^{2}\right]{\rm e}^{-\tau V({\bf x}_{0})},

(12)

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}_{1}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{0};\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{[2\pi\tau]^{D/2}}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2\tau}({\bf x}-{\bf x}(\tau))^{2}\right]{\rm e}^{-\tau E_{L}({\bf x}_{0})},

(13)

where
{\bf x}(\tau)
is the trajectory satisfying the drift equation

\frac{d{\bf x}(\tau)}{d\tau}={\bf v}({\bf x}(\tau))=\frac{\nabla\phi({\bf x}(\tau))}{\phi({\bf x}(\tau))}

(14)

having the same initial position
{\bf x}(0)={\bf x}_{0}
.

In the case of the harmonic oscillator, (
12
) bears no resemblance to (
9
).
However, for
\widetilde{H}
with
\phi({\bf x})=\psi_{0}({\bf x})=\exp(-{\bf x}^{2}/2)
, the
drift equation

\frac{d{\bf x}(\tau)}{d\tau}=-{\bf x}(\tau)

(15)

yields the solution

{\bf x}(\tau)={\bf x}_{0}{\rm e}^{-\tau},

(16)

resulting in the transformed first-order propagator

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}_{1}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{0};\tau)=\frac{1}{[2\pi\tau]^{D/2}}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2\tau}({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{0}{\rm e}^{-\tau})^{2}\right]{\rm e}^{-\tau E_{0}}

(17)

which is already close to the exact propagator (
10
), differs only in
having the variance
\tau
rather than
T(\tau)
.
As a matter of fact, its trace

\int d{\bf x}\,\widetilde{G}_{1}({\bf x},{\bf x};\tau)=[2\sinh(\tau/2)]^{-D}=\int d{\bf x}\,\widetilde{G}({\bf x},{\bf x};\tau)=\int d{\bf x}\,G({\bf x},{\bf x};\tau)

(18)

gives same partition function as the exact propagator (
9
).
If one regards the
variance

\tau
in (
17
) as a variational parameter
fixed at
\tau=1
, then the (
17
) will yield the correct wave function
(not its square) as
\tau\rightarrow\infty

in the trajectory equation

{\bf x}(\tau)={\bf x}_{0}{\rm e}^{-\tau}
.

This is of course an ideal case where one can take
\phi({\bf x})
to be the
exact ground state
\psi_{0}({\bf x})
, but this illustrates the
possibility of improving the propagator by exploiting the invariance (
6
) under

\phi({\bf x})
. Historically, the transformed propagator (
13
) with an approximate ground
state
\phi({\bf x})
has been used to accelerate the convergence of the Feynman-Kac path integral

and is the formal basis for doing Diffusion Monte Carlo with importance-sampling
chin90
;
mos82
;
rey82
.

Generalizing to the case of
N

interacting
particles in a harmonic oscillator,
if one knows the exact bosonic ground state
\psi_{0}({\bf x}_{1},{\bf x}_{2}\cdots{\bf x}_{N})
,
then an excellent approximation to the (unnormalized) single particle state would be

\displaystyle\psi_{i}({\bf x}_{i})=\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}({\bf x}_{i}-{\bf s}_{i})^{2}\right],

(19)

where
{\bf s}_{i}={\bf x}_{i}(\tau\rightarrow\infty)
. If
{\bf s}_{i}\neq 0
,
then it must be a
stationary
point of (
14
), implying that

\nabla_{{\bf x}_{i}}\psi_{0}({\bf x}_{1},{\bf x}_{2}\cdots{\bf x}_{N})|_{{\bf s}_{i}}=0.

(20)

This means that {
{\bf s}_{i}
}
is a set of particle positions that
maximizes the bosonic ground state wave function, rather than just minimizes the classical
potential energy
. Such a discrete set of none zero {
{\bf s}_{i}
} would automatically breaks translational
and rotational symmetry even if
\psi_{0}({\bf x}_{1},{\bf x}_{2}\cdots{\bf x}_{N})
does not. This is
because one must necessarily start with a discreet set of initial positions
{
{\bf x}_{i0}
}. The drift equation then evolves them toward
the set of discrete stationary points {
{\bf s}_{i}
}. Anti-symmetrizing the set of
single particle states (
19
) then yields Maki-Zotos type wave function (
2
).

A fundamental test of this transformed propagator approach of deriving
the many-fermion wave function is whether for

N

non-interacting bosons
in a harmonic oscillator, the resulting wave function (
2
)
correctly describe
N

non-interacting fermions
in the same potential.
We first verify analytically that this is indeed the case for up to four fermions in the next Section.

III
Analytical few-fermion wave functions

For
N
-particles in a harmonic oscillator, the bosonic ground state wave function (ignoring normalization)
is

\psi_{B}({\bf x}_{1},{\bf x}_{2}\cdots{\bf x}_{N})=\prod_{i=1}^{N}{\rm e}^{-{\bf x}_{i}^{2}/2},

(21)

thereby yielding the same drift equation (
15
) and the same solution (
16
) for each
particle:
{\bf x}_{i}(\tau)={\bf x}_{i0}{\rm e}^{-\tau}
. Anti-symmetrizing the single particle state (
19
) then
yields the
N
-fermion wave function as

\Psi({\bf x}_{1},{\bf x}_{2}\dots{\bf x}_{N})=\lim_{{\bf s}_{j}\rightarrow 0}\det\Bigl(\exp[-\frac{1}{2}({\bf x}_{i}-{\bf s}_{j})^{2}]\Bigr).

(22)

It is crucial to note here that the formalism requires anti-symmetrizing the single particle states
first, before taking the
{\bf s}_{j}\rightarrow 0
limit. Note also that
at a large finite
\tau
,
{\bf s}_{j}={\bf x}_{j0}{\rm e}^{-\tau}
is very close to zero,
but never actually zero. Therefore analytically, the limit
{\bf s}_{j}\rightarrow 0
means that one
should expand and keep only the leading non-vanishing powers of
{\bf s}_{j}
.

For
N=2
, the unnormalized antisymmetrized wave function is

\displaystyle\Psi({\bf x}_{1},{\bf x}_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\det\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\rm e}^{-({\bf x}_{1}-{\bf s}_{1})^{2}/2}&{\rm e}^{-({\bf x}_{1}-{\bf s}_{2})^{2}/2}\\
{\rm e}^{-({\bf x}_{2}-{\bf s}_{1})^{2}/2}&{\rm e}^{-({\bf x}_{2}-{\bf s}_{2})^{2}/2}.\end{array}\right)

Factoring out
{\rm e}^{-{\bf x}^{2}_{1}/2}
and
{\rm e}^{-{\bf x}^{2}_{2}/2}
from rows one and two
respectively and
{\rm e}^{-{\bf s}^{2}_{1}/2}
and
{\rm e}^{-{\bf s}^{2}_{2}/2}
from columns one and two
respectively gives

\displaystyle\Psi({\bf x}_{1},{\bf x}_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{{\bf s}_{k}\rightarrow 0}{\rm e}^{-({\bf x}^{2}_{1}+{\bf x}^{2}_{2}+{\bf s}^{2}_{1}+{\bf s}^{2}_{2})/2}\det\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{1}\cdot{\bf s}_{1}}&{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{1}\cdot{\bf s}_{2}}\\
{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{2}\cdot{\bf s}_{1}}&{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{2}\cdot{\bf s}_{2}}\end{array}\right),

(29)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{-({\bf x}_{1}^{2}+{\bf x}_{2}^{2})/2}{\bf x}_{21}\cdot{\bf s}_{21},

where we have expanded to first order in
{\bf s}_{j}
and defined
{\bf x}_{ij}={\bf x}_{i}-{\bf x}_{j}
and
{\bf s}_{ij}={\bf s}_{i}-{\bf s}_{j}
.
This is the correct two-fermion wave function in a harmonic oscillator
of any dimension. For example, in three dimension,
the two-fermion wave functions are three-fold degenerate given by

\displaystyle\Psi({\bf x}_{1},{\bf x}_{2}){\rm e}^{({\bf x}_{1}^{2}+{\bf x}_{2}^{2})/2}

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle\det\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&x_{1}\\
1&x_{2}\end{array}\right)\quad{\rm or}\quad\det\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&y_{1}\\
1&y_{2}\end{array}\right)\quad{\rm or}\quad\det\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&z_{1}\\
1&z_{2}\end{array}\right).

One sees that (
29
) is correctly a linear superposition of these three degenerate states
with arbitrary coefficients
({\bf s}_{2}-{\bf s}_{1})_{k}
. Note that one must have
{\bf s}_{1}\neq{\bf s}_{2}
, otherwise the wave function vanishes.

Generalizing (
III
) to
N=3
fermions gives

\displaystyle\Psi({\bf x}_{1},{\bf x}_{2},{\bf x}_{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{{\bf s}_{k}\rightarrow 0}{\rm e}^{-({\bf x}^{2}_{1}+{\bf x}^{2}_{2}+{\bf x}^{2}_{3})/2}\det\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{1}\cdot{\bf s}_{1}}&{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{1}\cdot{\bf s}_{2}}&{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{1}\cdot{\bf s}_{3}}\\
{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{2}\cdot{\bf s}_{1}}&{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{2}\cdot{\bf s}_{2}}&{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{2}\cdot{\bf s}_{3}}\\
{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{3}\cdot{\bf s}_{1}}&{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{3}\cdot{\bf s}_{2}}&{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{3}\cdot{\bf s}_{3}},\end{array}\right).

Multiply the first row by
{\rm e}^{({\bf x}_{2}-{\bf x}_{1})\cdot{\bf s}_{1}}={\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{21}\cdot{\bf s}_{1}}
and
{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{31}\cdot{\bf s}_{1}}

and subtract that from the second and third row respectively gives

\displaystyle\det\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{1}\cdot{\bf s}_{1}}&{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{1}\cdot{\bf s}_{2}}&{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{1}\cdot{\bf s}_{3}}\\
{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{2}\cdot{\bf s}_{1}}&{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{2}\cdot{\bf s}_{2}}&{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{2}\cdot{\bf s}_{3}}\\
{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{3}\cdot{\bf s}_{1}}&{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{3}\cdot{\bf s}_{2}}&{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{3}\cdot{\bf s}_{3}}\end{array}\right)=\det\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{1}\cdot{\bf s}_{1}}&{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{1}\cdot{\bf s}_{2}}&{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{1}\cdot{\bf s}_{3}}\\
0&{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{2}\cdot{\bf s}_{2}}-{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{1}\cdot{\bf s}_{2}+{\bf x}_{21}\cdot{\bf s}_{1}}&{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{2}\cdot{\bf s}_{3}}-{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{1}\cdot{\bf s}_{3}+{\bf x}_{21}\cdot{\bf s}_{1}}\\
0&{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{3}\cdot{\bf s}_{2}}-{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{1}\cdot{\bf s}_{2}+{\bf x}_{31}\cdot{\bf s}_{1}}&{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{3}\cdot{\bf s}_{3}}-{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{1}\cdot{\bf s}_{3}+{\bf x}_{31}\cdot{\bf s}_{1}}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad={\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{1}\cdot{\bf s}_{1}}\det\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{2}\cdot{\bf s}_{2}}(1-{\rm e}^{-{\bf x}_{21}\cdot{\bf s}_{21}})&{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{2}\cdot{\bf s}_{3}}(1-{\rm e}^{-{\bf x}_{21}\cdot{\bf s}_{31}})\\
{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{3}\cdot{\bf s}_{2}}(1-{\rm e}^{-{\bf x}_{31}\cdot{\bf s}_{21}})&{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{3}\cdot{\bf s}_{3}}(1-{\rm e}^{-{\bf x}_{31}\cdot{\bf s}_{31}}).\end{array}\right)

In the
{\bf s}_{j}\rightarrow 0
limit, to second-order in
{\bf s}_{j}
, the above determinant becomes

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\det\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}({\bf x}_{21}\cdot{\bf s}_{21})&({\bf x}_{21}\cdot{\bf s}_{31})\\
({\bf x}_{31}\cdot{\bf s}_{21})&({\bf x}_{31}\cdot{\bf s}_{31})\end{array}\right)

(54)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle({\bf x}_{21}\cdot{\bf s}_{21})({\bf x}_{31}\cdot{\bf s}_{31})-({\bf x}_{31}\cdot{\bf s}_{21})({\bf x}_{21}\cdot{\bf s}_{31})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle({\bf x}_{21}\times{\bf x}_{31})\cdot({\bf s}_{21}\times{\bf s}_{31})

(55)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle({\bf x}_{1}\times{\bf x}_{2}+{\bf x}_{2}\times{\bf x}_{3}+{\bf x}_{3}\times{\bf x}_{1})\cdot({\bf s}_{1}\times{\bf s}_{2}+{\bf s}_{2}\times{\bf s}_{3}+{\bf s}_{3}\times{\bf s}_{1})

The final form (

{\bf x}_{i}\leftrightarrow{\bf x}_{j}

In 3D, there are also three degenerate states for three fermions:

\displaystyle\det\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&y_{1}&z_{1}\\
0&y_{21}&z_{21}\\
0&y_{31}&z_{31}\\
\end{array}\right)=y_{21}z_{31}-y_{31}z_{21}=({\bf x}_{21}\times{\bf x}_{31})_{x},

\displaystyle\det\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&z_{1}&x_{1}\\

\end{array}\right)=z_{21}x_{31}-x_{31}z_{21}=({\bf x}_{21}\times{\bf x}_{31})_{y},

\displaystyle\det\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&x_{1}&y_{1}\\
0&x_{21}&y_{21}\\
0&x_{31}&y_{31}\\
\end{array}\right)=x_{21}y_{31}-x_{31}y_{21}=({\bf x}_{21}\times{\bf x}_{31})_{z}.

The most general three-fermion wave function is therefore a linear combination of these
three state as given by (
55

({\bf s}_{21}\times{\bf s}_{31})_{k}
.

In 2D, only
({\bf s}_{21}\times{\bf s}_{31})_{z}

54

to obtain the 3-fermion wave function. In Appendix A,

For

\displaystyle\det\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{1}\cdot{\bf s}_{1}}&{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{1}\cdot{\bf s}_{2}}&{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{1}\cdot{\bf s}_{3}}&{\rm e}^{{\bf x}_{1}\cdot{\bf s}_{4}}\\

four-fermions wave function in 2D. We will not bother with this task here.
However, for any dimension and any number of fermions,
one can always evaluate the wave function (
22
) numerically.
In the next Section, we show that the wave function (
22
) can be used in Monte Carlo calculations
to obtain the ground state energy of up to 1000 spin-balanced fermions in a 3D harmonic oscillators.

IV
Numerical evaluation of fermion energies

In this work, we will only focus on the fermion part of the wave function (
1
) and
leave the Jastrow correlation for later more specific applications.
The wave function can be rewritten as

\Psi({\bf x}_{1},{\bf x}_{2}\dots{\bf x}_{2N})=\det{\bf M}_{\uparrow}({\bf x}_{1},\dots{\bf x}_{N})\det{\bf M}_{\downarrow}({\bf x}_{N+1},\dots{\bf x}_{N+L})=\exp(S_{\uparrow}+S_{\downarrow}),

(79)

with

S_{\uparrow}({\bf x}_{1},{\bf x}_{2}\dots{\bf x}_{N})=\ln(\det{\bf M}_{\uparrow})

(80)

where
{\bf M}_{\uparrow}
is the
N\times N
matrix for particles
i=1
to
N

M_{\uparrow ij}=\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}({\bf x}_{i}-{\bf s}_{j})^{2}\right].

(81)

Similarly one can define

S_{\downarrow}=\ln(\det{\bf M}_{\downarrow})
where
{\bf M}_{\downarrow}
is the
L\times L
matrix (
81
)
for particles
i=N+1
to
N+L
. The computer code evaluates the determinant of both
N\times N
and
L\times L

matrices and can study various unequal spin cases for
N\neq L
.
Here, only results for the spin-balance case of
L=N
is presented. The resulting energy is directly
computed from two determinants and not from doubling the energy of a single determinant.

The local energy function for the spin-up fermions is then given by (See Appendix B)

\displaystyle E_{L}^{\uparrow}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{H\Psi}{\Psi}=\frac{\sum_{i}^{N}[-\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{i}+V({\bf x}_{i})]\Psi}{\Psi}

(82)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[-\frac{1}{2}[\nabla^{2}_{i}S_{\uparrow}+(\nabla_{i}S_{\uparrow})^{2}]+\frac{1}{2}{\bf x}_{i}^{2}\right],

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N\frac{D}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}({\bf s}_{i}^{2}-\tilde{\bf s}_{i}^{2})-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}({\bf x}_{i}-\tilde{\bf s}_{i})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\bf x}_{i}^{2},

where

\tilde{\bf s}_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}{\bf s}_{k}M_{\uparrow ik}M^{-1}_{\uparrow ki}.

(83)

The spin-down local energy
E_{L}^{\downarrow}
is similarly defined for particles
i=N+1
to
2N
.
One then computes the energy expectation value

E_{2N}=\frac{\int d{\bf x}_{1}\cdots d{\bf x}_{2N}(E_{L}^{\uparrow}+E_{L}^{\downarrow})\Psi^{2}({\bf x}_{1},{\bf x}_{2}\dots{\bf x}_{2N})}{\int d{\bf x}_{1}\cdots d{\bf x}_{2N}\Psi^{2}({\bf x}_{1},{\bf x}_{2}\dots{\bf x}_{2N})}

(84)

using the Metropolis
et al.
algorithm
met
. Since one is sampling
\Psi^{2}
,
there is no sign problem in this calculation.
The fermion character of the problem is encapsulated in
\tilde{\bf s}_{i}
, which requires
computing the inverse matrix
{\bf M}^{-1}
.

V
Conclusions

The thermodynamics of a system of harmonic confined fermions in 3D, including its ground state
energies, has been extensively studies by Brosens
et al.
bro98
using the exact
harmonic propagator in the low temperature limit. In that study, the ground state energy is
its final goal. Here, we have proposed a remarkably simple wave function (
22
) that
can yield the ground state energy directly, for up to a thousand harmonic fermions,
without evaluating any single particle wave function.
Moreover, because it is a wave function, and not a propagator, it can be part of an initial
trial function for doing very large scale VMC, or even Ground State Path Integral Monte Carlo
sar00
;
yan17
;
chin20

calculations on
interacting
3D fermions.

It is known that non-interacting fermions, due to Pauli’s exclusion principle, can probe the excited states
of any potential. Lyubartsev
lyu05
has even suggested that by anti-symmetrization, one can also probe
the excited states of an interacting, many-particle system. Mathematically, the wave function (
22
)
can yield the spectrum of the harmonic oscillator in
any
dimension. It seems miraculous that
such a simple wave function can automatically account for the degeneracy of each harmonic state in
D
-dimension,
which is the partition of a non-negative integer
N
by a sum of
D
non-negative integers.

The generalization of (
22
) to the Coulomb potential case of
V(r)=-Z/r
would seem to be

\Psi({\bf x}_{1},{\bf x}_{2}\dots{\bf x}_{N})=\lim_{{\bf s}_{j}\rightarrow 0}\det\Bigl(\exp[-Z|{\bf x}_{i}-{\bf s}_{j}|]\Bigr).

(85)

The wave function (
22
) works for the harmonic oscillator because all of its excited state have the same
Gaussian factor
{\rm e}^{-r^{2}/2}
. For the Coulomb potential, excited states have different Slater
orbitals
{\rm e}^{-Zr/n}
depending on the principle quantum number
n
. Therefore, (
85
)
cannot possibly be the exact wave function for
N
non-interacting fermions in a Coulomb potential.
Thus the simple wave function (
22
) is unique to the harmonic oscillator.

Appendix A
The N-fermion wave function in one dimension

The
N=2
determinant in 1D can be computed alternatively as

\displaystyle\det\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\rm e}^{x_{1}s_{1}}&{\rm e}^{x_{1}s_{2}}\\
{\rm e}^{x_{2}s_{1}}&{\rm e}^{x_{2}s_{2}}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{x_{1}s_{1}}{\rm e}^{x_{2}s_{2}}-{\rm e}^{x_{2}s_{1}}{\rm e}^{x_{1}s_{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{1}=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n_{1}!}x_{1}^{n_{1}}s_{1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{n_{2}=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n_{2}!}x_{2}^{n_{2}}s_{2}^{n_{2}}-\sum_{n_{1}=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n_{1}!}x_{2}^{n_{1}}s_{1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{n_{2}=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n_{2}!}x_{1}^{n_{2}}s_{2}^{n_{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{1}=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n_{2}=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n_{1}!n_{2}!}s_{1}^{n_{1}}s_{2}^{n_{2}}(x_{1}^{n_{1}}x_{2}^{n_{2}}-x_{2}^{n_{1}}x_{1}^{n_{2}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{1}=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n_{2}=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n_{1}!n_{2}!}s_{1}^{n_{1}}s_{2}^{n_{2}}\det\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}x_{1}^{n_{1}}&x_{1}^{n_{2}}\\
x_{2}^{n_{1}}&x_{2}^{n_{2}}\end{array}\right).

Since
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
are column indices and the determinant vanishes for

n_{1}=n_{2}
, the sum is only over

n_{1}<n_{2}
and
n_{2}<n_{1}
. The latter case can be view as the first case with
n_{1}
interchanged with
n_{2}
.
This interchange corresponds to the old determinant with a negative sign, hence

\displaystyle\det\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\rm e}^{x_{1}s_{1}}&{\rm e}^{x_{1}s_{2}}\\
{\rm e}^{x_{2}s_{1}}&{\rm e}^{x_{2}s_{2}}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{1}<n_{2}}\frac{1}{n_{1}!n_{2}!}\left[s_{1}^{n_{1}}s_{2}^{n_{2}}\det\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}x_{1}^{n_{1}}&x_{1}^{n_{2}}\\
x_{2}^{n_{1}}&x_{2}^{n_{2}}\end{array}\right)-s_{1}^{n_{2}}s_{2}^{n_{1}}\det\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}x_{1}^{n_{1}}&x_{1}^{n_{2}}\\
x_{2}^{n_{1}}&x_{2}^{n_{2}}\end{array}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{1}<n_{2}}\frac{1}{n_{1}!n_{2}!}\det\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}s_{1}^{n_{1}}&s_{1}^{n_{2}}\\
s_{2}^{n_{1}}&s_{2}^{n_{2}}\end{array}\right)\det\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}x_{1}^{n_{1}}&x_{1}^{n_{2}}\\
x_{2}^{n_{1}}&x_{2}^{n_{2}}\end{array}\right).

This expansion was used in the study of the quantum Hall effect by Mikhailov
mik01
.

The lowest order terms in
s_{i}
are therefore given by
n_{1}=0
and
n_{2}=1
, resulting in

\displaystyle\det\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\rm e}^{x_{1}s_{1}}&{\rm e}^{x_{1}s_{2}}\\
{\rm e}^{x_{2}s_{1}}&{\rm e}^{x_{2}s_{2}}\end{array}\right)=\det\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&s_{1}\\
1&s_{2}\end{array}\right)\det\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&x_{1}\\
1&x_{2}\end{array}\right)=s_{21}x_{21},

reproducing (
29
).

Similar manipulations gives the
N=3
determinant,

\displaystyle\det\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\rm e}^{x_{1}s_{1}}&{\rm e}^{x_{1}s_{2}}&{\rm e}^{x_{1}s_{3}}\\
{\rm e}^{x_{2}s_{1}}&{\rm e}^{x_{2}s_{2}}&{\rm e}^{x_{2}s_{3}}\\
{\rm e}^{x_{3}s_{1}}&{\rm e}^{x_{3}s_{2}}&{\rm e}^{x_{3}s_{3}}\end{array}\right)=\sum_{n_{1}<n_{2}<n_{3}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n_{1}!n_{2}!n_{3}!}\det\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}s_{1}^{n_{1}}&s_{1}^{n_{2}}&s_{1}^{n_{3}}\\
s_{2}^{n_{1}}&s_{2}^{n_{2}}&s_{2}^{n_{3}}\\
s_{3}^{n_{1}}&s_{3}^{n_{2}}&s_{3}^{n_{3}}\end{array}\right)\det\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}x_{1}^{n_{1}}&x_{1}^{n_{2}}&x_{1}^{n_{3}}\\
x_{2}^{n_{1}}&x_{2}^{n_{2}}&x_{2}^{n_{3}}\\
x_{3}^{n_{1}}&x_{3}^{n_{2}}&x_{3}^{n_{3}}\end{array}\right).

(121)

The first non-vanishing term in the above sum, is the third order term in
s_{i}
given by
n_{1}=0
,
n_{2}=1
,
n_{3}=2
,

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\det\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&s_{1}&s_{1}^{2}\\
1&s_{2}&s_{2}^{2}\\
1&s_{3}&s_{3}^{2}\end{array}\right)\det\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&x_{1}&x_{1}^{2}\\
1&x_{2}&x_{2}^{2}\\
1&x_{3}&x_{3}^{2}\end{array}\right)=\frac{1}{2}s_{21}s_{31}s_{32}x_{21}x_{31}x_{32},

which correctly changes sign whenever
x_{i}\leftrightarrow x_{j}
.

For
N
-fermion, the above two cases (
A
) and (
A
) generalize to the
the
N\times N
Vandermonde determinant:

\det\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}1&x_{1}&x_{1}^{2}&\cdots&x_{1}^{N-1}\\
1&x_{2}&x_{2}^{2}&\cdots&x_{2}^{N-1}\\
1&x_{2}&x_{2}^{2}&\cdots&x_{2}^{N-1}\\
1&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots\\
1&x_{N}&x_{n}^{2}&\cdots&x_{N}^{N-1}\end{array}\right)=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq N}(x_{j}-x_{i}),

(129)

yielding the
N
-fermion wave function:

\Psi(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots x_{n})\propto\prod_{i<j}(s_{j}-s_{i})\prod_{i<j}(x_{j}-x_{i})\exp(-\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{2}/2).

(130)

This is an example in which the determinant wave function can be explicit given without evaluating
any specific single particle wave function,
i.e.
, Hermit polynomials.

Appendix B
The Hamiltonian energy estimator

Since the below applies equally to
{\bf M}_{\uparrow}
and
{\bf M}_{\downarrow}
,
for clarity, we will suppress the
\uparrow
and
\downarrow
labels. Given that

{\bf M}=M_{lk}=\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}({\bf x}_{l}-{\bf s}_{k})^{2}\right],

(131)

one has

\displaystyle\nabla_{i}S

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla_{i}\ln({\det}{\bf M})={\rm Tr}[{\bf M}^{-1}\nabla_{i}{\bf M}]=\sum_{kl}M^{-1}_{kl}\nabla_{i}M_{lk},

(132)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{kl}M^{-1}_{kl}({\bf x}_{l}-{\bf s}_{k})\delta_{il}M_{lk}=-\sum_{k}M^{-1}_{ki}({\bf x}_{i}-{\bf s}_{k})M_{ik},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-({\bf x}_{i}-\sum_{k}{\bf s}_{k}M_{ik}M^{-1}_{ki})=-({\bf x}_{i}-\tilde{\bf s}_{i}),

and therefore

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}_{i}S

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\nabla_{i}\cdot({\bf x}_{i}-\sum_{k}{\bf s}_{k}M_{ik}M^{-1}_{ki}),

(133)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-D+\sum_{k}{\bf s}_{k}\cdot\nabla_{i}(M_{ik}M^{-1}_{ki}).

In the following, repeated indices
l
and
n
are summed over, but not for
i
or
k
,

\displaystyle\nabla_{i}(M_{ik}M^{-1}_{ki})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-({\bf x}_{i}-{\bf s}_{k})M_{ik}M^{-1}_{ki}-M_{ik}M^{-1}_{kl}(\nabla_{i}M_{ln})M^{-1}_{ni}

(134)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Bigl[({\bf x}_{i}-{\bf s}_{k})M_{ik}M^{-1}_{ki}-M_{ik}M^{-1}_{ki}({\bf x}_{i}-{\bf s}_{n})M_{in}M^{-1}_{ni}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Bigl[{\bf x}_{i}M_{ik}M^{-1}_{ki}-{\bf s}_{k}M_{ik}M^{-1}_{ki}-M_{ik}M^{-1}_{ki}{\bf x}_{i}+M_{ik}M^{-1}_{ki}{\bf s}_{n}M_{in}M^{-1}_{ni}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Bigl[-{\bf s}_{k}M_{ik}M^{-1}_{ki}+M_{ik}M^{-1}_{ki}\tilde{\bf s}_{i}\Bigr].

Now the sum over
k
in (
133
) yields

\displaystyle\sum_{k}{\bf s}_{k}\cdot\nabla_{i}(M_{ik}M^{-1}_{ki})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{k}{\bf s}_{k}\cdot\Bigl[-{\bf s}_{k}M_{ik}M^{-1}_{ki}+M_{ik}M^{-1}_{ki}\tilde{\bf s}_{i}\Bigr]

(135)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k}{\bf s}^{2}_{k}M_{ik}M^{-1}_{ki}-\tilde{\bf s}_{i}^{2},

and the final sum over
i
gives

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nabla^{2}_{i}S=-ND+\sum_{i=1}^{N}({\bf s}_{i}^{2}-\tilde{\bf s}_{i}^{2}).

(136)

The local energy is therefore

\displaystyle E_{L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[-\frac{1}{2}[\nabla^{2}_{i}S_{\uparrow}+(\nabla_{i}S_{\uparrow})^{2}]+\frac{1}{2}{\bf x}_{i}^{2}\right],

(137)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N\frac{D}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}({\bf s}_{i}^{2}-\tilde{\bf s}_{i}^{2})-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}({\bf x}_{i}-\tilde{\bf s}_{i})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\bf x}_{i}^{2}.

If
{\bf M}
were diagonal, as in the boson case, then
\tilde{\bf s}_{i}={\bf s}_{i}
, and the above is just

\displaystyle E_{L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N\frac{D}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}({\bf x}_{i}-{\bf s}_{i})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\bf x}_{i}^{2},

(138)

which correctly reproduces the
N
-boson energy of
ND/2
when
{\bf s}_{i}\rightarrow 0
.

Appendix C
Fermion energies in a 3D harmonic oscillator

The spectrum of the 3D harmonic oscillator is given by

E_{m}=(m-1)+\frac{3}{2}=\frac{2m+1}{2},

(139)

where

m-1=n_{x}+n_{y}+n_{z},

(140)

with the ground state corresponding to
m=1
.

Given
m
,
n_{x}
can take on
m
values from 0 to
(m-1)
.
For
n_{x}=(m-1)
, one must have just
(n_{y},n_{z})=(0,0)
. For
n_{x}=(m-1)-1
,
one can have
(n_{y},n_{z})=
(1,0) or (0,1). For
n_{x}=(m-1)-2
,
(n_{y},n_{z})=(2,0),(1,1),(0,2)
, etc..
The total degeneracy for level
E_{m}
is therefore
1+2+3+\cdots+m
, given by

g=\frac{1}{2}m(m+1).

(141)

Therefore, for closed-shell occupation up to and including the
n^{th}
level,
we have total particle number and energy

N(n)=\sum_{m=1}^{n}\frac{1}{2}m(m+1),

E(n)=\sum_{m=1}^{n}\frac{1}{2}m(m+1)\frac{2m+1}{2}.

For equal spin-up and spin-down fermions, multiply by 2 gives

N(n)=\sum_{m=1}^{n}m(m+1),

E(n)=\sum_{m=1}^{n}\frac{1}{2}m(m+1)(2m+1).

Since we have the power sums

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{n}m

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}n(n+1),

(142)

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{n}m^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{6}n(n+1)(2n+1),

(143)

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{n}m^{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}n^{2}(n+1)^{2},

(144)

we have the following
closed shell
results

\displaystyle N(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}n(n+1)+\frac{1}{6}n(n+1)(2n+1)=\frac{1}{3}n(n+1)(n+2),

(145)

\displaystyle E(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}n(n+1)[n^{2}+3n+2]=\frac{1}{4}n(n+1)^{2}(n+2),

(146)

which agree with a previous, different derivation of Brosens
et al.
bro98
where
their
L=n-1
.

For any
M
between two closed shells
n
and
n+1
,
N(n)\leq M\leq N(n+1)
, the energy is

E(n)
plus the number of particle greater then
N(n)
, which is
[M-N(n)]
, times
the energy level at
m=n+1
, which is

E_{M}=E(n)+[M-N(n)]\left(\frac{2(n+1)+1}{2}\right).

(147)
