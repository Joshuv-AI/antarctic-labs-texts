The anatomy of Boris type solvers and the Lie operator formalism for deriving large time-step magnetic field integrators
Siu A. Chin ∗ and Durward Cator

Abstract
This work gives a Lie operator derivation of various Boris solvers via a detailed study of trajectory errors in a constant magnetic field. These errors in the gyrocenter location and the gyroradius are the foundational basis for why Boris solvers existed, independent of any finite-difference schemes. This work shows that there are two distinct ways of eliminating these errors so that the trajectory of a charged particle in a constant magnetic field is exactly on the cyclotron orbit. One way reproduces the known second-order symmetric Boris solver. The other yields a previously unknown, but also on-orbit solver, not derivable from finite-difference schemes. By revisiting some historical calculations, it is found that many publications do not distinguish the poorly behaved leap-frog Boris solver from the symmetric second-order Boris algorithm. This symmetric second-order Boris solver’s trajectory is much more accurate and remains close to the exact orbit in a combined nonuniform electric and magnetic field at time-steps greater than the cyclotron period. Finally, this operator formalism showed that Buneman’s cycloid fitting scheme is mathematically identical to Boris’ on-orbit solver and that Boris’ E-B splitting is unnecessary. Key words : Plasmas simulation, Boris solvers, magnetic field integrators, large time-step methods ∗ Corresponding author, chin@physics.tamu.edu

II
The Lie operator method

The equations of motion for a charged particle in a static electric
{\bf E}({\bf r})
and
magnetic field
{\bf B}({\bf r})=B({\bf r})\hat{\bf B}({\bf r})
can be written as

\frac{d{\bf r}}{dt}={\bf v}\quad{\rm and}\quad\frac{d{\bf v}}{dt}=\omega({\bf r})\hat{\bf B}({\bf r})\times{\bf v}+{\bf a}({\bf r})

(1)

where
{\bf v}\equiv{\bf p}/m
,
\omega({\bf r})=(-q)B({\bf r})/m
and
{\bf a}({\bf r})={\bf F}({\bf r})/m=q{\bf E}({\bf r})/m
.
(Note that
\omega>0

counter-clockwise when the magnetic field is out of the page.)
The vectors
{\bf r}
and
{\bf v}
are fundamental and independent dynamical variables.

For any other dynamical variable
W({\bf r},{\bf v})

1

by

\displaystyle\frac{dW}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial W}{\partial{\bf r}}\cdot\frac{d{\bf r}}{dt}+\frac{\partial W}{\partial{\bf v}}\cdot\frac{d{\bf v}}{dt}

(2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Bigl({\bf v}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial{\bf r}}+(\omega\hat{\bf B}\times{\bf v}+{\bf a})\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial{\bf v}}\Bigr)W,

which can be directly integrated to yield the operator solution

W(t)={\rm e}^{t(T+V_{BF})}W(0)

(3)

where one has defined Lie operators
dep69
;
dra76
;
yos93

T={\bf v}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial{\bf r}}

(4)

and

\displaystyle V_{BF}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega(\hat{\bf B}\times{\bf v})\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial{\bf v}}+{\bf a}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial{\bf v}}\equiv V_{B}+V_{F}.

(5)

To solve (
3

so that (
3
) can be reduce to

n

\exp[{\Delta t}(T+V_{BF})]
via
Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff type approximations, of which the simplest examples are

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}(T+V_{BF})}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}T}{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}V_{BF}}

(6)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}T}{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}V_{B}}{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}V_{F}}.

The action of each individual operator can easily be computed via series expansion:

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}T}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\bf r}\\
{\bf v}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1+{\Delta t}{\bf v}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial{\bf r}}+\frac{{\Delta t}^{2}}{2}({\bf v}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial{\bf r}})^{2}+\cdots)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\bf r}\\
{\bf v}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\bf r}+{\Delta t}{\bf v}\\
{\bf v}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}V_{F}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\bf r}\\
{\bf v}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1+{\Delta t}{\bf a}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial{\bf v}}+\frac{{\Delta t}^{2}}{2}({\bf a}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial{\bf v}})^{2}+\cdots)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\bf r}\\
{\bf v}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\bf r}\\
{\bf v}+{\Delta t}{\bf a}\end{array}\right).

More generally, the product approximation

{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}(T+V_{F})}=\prod_{i=1}^{N}{\rm e}^{a_{i}{\Delta t}T}{\rm e}^{b_{i}{\Delta t}V_{F}}

(23)

with suitable coefficients
a_{i}
and

, then generates sequential updates
(
II
) and (
II
), which is a
symplectic integrator

;
ser16
;
chin20

of arbitrary order for
solving (
1
) without a magnetic field. Since (
II
) and (
II
) are
sequential translations, it is obvious that any algorithm of the form (

)
is phase-volume preserving.
Likewise, the approximation

{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}(T+V_{B})}=\prod_{i=1}^{N}{\rm e}^{a_{i}{\Delta t}T}{\rm e}^{b_{i}{\Delta t}V_{B}}

(24)

will generate sequential updates which are
exact energy conserving

for solving
(
1
) with only a magnetic field. This Lie operator method is powerful in that even
without knowing the explicit form of
\exp(b_{i}{\Delta t}V_{B})
, one can prove that (
24
) is
exact energy conserving because by (
II
),

{\rm e}^{a_{i}{\Delta t}T}{\bf v}^{2}={\bf v}^{2}

(25)

and

{\rm e}^{b_{i}{\Delta t}V_{B}}{\bf v}^{2}=(1+b_{i}{\Delta t}\omega(\hat{\bf B}\times{\bf v})\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial{\bf v}}+\cdots){\bf v}^{2}={\bf v}^{2},

(26)

since after differentiating

, the resulting triple product vanishes.
Moreover (

) implies that the effect of
{\rm e}^{b_{i}{\Delta t}V_{B}}
on

must only be a rotation. Consequently

24

Finally, the approximation

{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}(T+V_{BF})}=\prod_{i=1}^{N}{\rm e}^{a_{i}{\Delta t}T}{\rm e}^{b_{i}{\Delta t}V_{BF}}

will generate sequential updates of a

;

However, the explicit form of

{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}V_{B}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\bf r}\\

{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}V_{BF}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\bf r}\\
{\bf v}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\bf r}\\

where

\displaystyle{\bf v}_{B}({\bf r},{\bf v},{\Delta t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf v}+\sin\theta(\hat{\bf B}\times{\bf v})+(1-\cos\theta)\hat{\bf B}\times(\hat{\bf B}\times{\bf v})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf v}_{\|}+\cos\theta{\bf v}_{\perp}+\sin\theta(\hat{\bf B}\times{\bf v}_{\perp})

\displaystyle{\bf v}_{F}({\bf r},{\bf v},{\Delta t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\Delta t}{\bf a}+\frac{1}{\omega}(1-\cos\theta)\hat{\bf B}\times{\bf a}+{\Delta t}(1-\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta})\hat{\bf B}\times(\hat{\bf B}\times{\bf a})

(32)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\Delta t}{\bf a}_{\|}+\frac{1}{\omega}\Bigl[(1-\cos\theta)(\hat{\bf B}\times{\bf a}_{\perp})+\sin\theta{\bf a}_{\perp}\Bigr],

(33)

with
{\theta}=\omega({\bf r}){\Delta t}
and where
{\bf v}_{||}+{\bf v}_{\perp}={\bf v}
,
{\bf a}_{||}+{\bf a}_{\perp}={\bf a}

are components parallel and perpendicular to the local magnetic field direction
\hat{\bf B}({\bf r})
.

Eq.(

acting on
{\bf v}
, which is to rotate only

thereby preserving
|{\bf v}|
and the kinetic energy. This is the same as exactly solving

\frac{d{\bf v}}{dt}=\omega({\bf r})\hat{\bf B}({\bf r})\times{\bf v}

holding

{\cal C}=(\hat{\bf B}({\bf r})\times\ )
.
Since

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}\omega{\cal C}}{\bf v}={\rm e}^{{\theta}{\cal C}}({\bf v}_{||}+{\bf v}_{\perp}),

III
First-order magnetic field integrators

Boris

originally derived his solver by modifying the implicit midpoint method.
Here, we will show how Boris type solvers can be derived systematically from the
Lie operator method without referencing any finite-difference schemes.
By Boris type solver, we shall mean any algorithm in which the
argument of the trigonometric functions in the velocity update (
31
) is not directly defined
as
{\theta}=\omega({\bf r}){\Delta t}
, but as some other functions of
{\theta}
.

The two basic first-order approximations are

\displaystyle{\bf v}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf v}_{B}({\bf r}_{0},{\bf v}_{0},{\Delta t})

\displaystyle{\bf r}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf r}_{0}+{\Delta t}{\bf v}_{1}

and M1B,

\displaystyle{\bf r}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf r}_{0}+{\Delta t}{\bf v}_{0}

\displaystyle{\bf v}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf v}_{B}({\bf r}_{1},{\bf v}_{0},{\Delta t}).

(40)

Eq.(

by
{\theta}=\omega({\bf r}){\Delta t}
, leaving its parallel component and magnitude unchanged.
M1A first rotates
{\bf v}_{0}
by

then moves to the new position along

then rotates it after arrival.
In our naming scheme, the
suffix A or B denotes the algorithm whose first step is updating the velocity or the position,
respectively.

The working of these two algorithms can be easily analyzed for a negatively charged particle
in a constant magnetic field in the
\hat{\bf z}

1

When the particle is at
{\bf r}_{0}=(r_{0},0)

. At
{\bf r}_{1}
, it would rotate the velocity
from the vertical

and move it to

.
Since both
{\bf r}_{0}
and

must be on the algorithm’s gyro-circle of radius

. This means that

must lie on the perpendicular bisector of

y_{c}=r_{g}{\theta}/2.

(41)

At
{\bf r}_{1}

is the rotation angle from the vertical and the supplementary angle

Since
{\theta}+2\alpha=\pi\rightarrow{\theta}/2+\alpha=\pi/2

This also means that the length of the bisector is

and hence

\displaystyle x_{c}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r_{g}-R_{g}\cos({\theta}/2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf r}_{n-1/2}+{\Delta t}{\bf v}_{n}

\displaystyle=

We will refer to this algorithm as MLF (magnetic leap frog).

and consequently,

\displaystyle\sin{\theta}_{C}={\theta}\sqrt{1-{\theta}^{2}/4},\quad\cos{\theta}_{C}=(1-{\theta}^{2}/2).

(53)

In this case,

(54)

which limits the algorithm to
|{\theta}|\leq 2
.
The resulting three fundamental algorithms with rotation angle
{\theta}_{C}

are previously unknown Boris type solvers and will be referred to as
C1A, C1B and CLF. They are characterized by having errors in the
gyrocenter
(x_{c},y_{c})
for C1A and C1B, but only the
x_{c}
error for CLF.

IV
Second-order magnetic field integrators

The leap frog construction can eliminate first-order errors
y_{c}=\pm r_{g}{\theta}/2

by use of staggered time steps (
46
), as shown in Fig.
4
.
However, as also shown in Fig.
4
, even the adoption of either Boris angle
cannot completely get rid of both errors
R_{g}
and
x_{c}
.
Here, we show how this can be done by symmetric second-order methods
which are superior to the use of staggered time steps.

Figure 8:
(color online)
For the same configuration as Fig.
3
, the
two second-order algorithms M2A and M2B produce two square orbits
having the correct rotation angles. C2A and B2B are the two second-order Boris solvers with
trajectories exactly on the gyro-circle but with out-of-phase rotation angles.

In sequential symplectic integrators
yos93
;
chin20
, it is well known that
first-order errors can be automatically removed by a time-symmetric
concatenation of the two first-order methods,

{\cal T}_{2A}={\rm e}^{({\Delta t}/2)V_{B}}{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}T}{\rm e}^{({\Delta t}/2)V_{B}}\quad{\rm and}\quad{\cal T}_{2B}={\rm e}^{({\Delta t}/2)T}{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}V_{B}}{\rm e}^{({\Delta t}/2)T}

(59)

yielding the following second-order integrators M2A,

\displaystyle{\bf v}_{1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf v}_{B}({\bf r}_{0},{\bf v}_{0},{\Delta t}/2)

\displaystyle{\bf r}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf r}_{0}+{\Delta t}{\bf v}_{1/2}

\displaystyle{\bf v}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf v}_{B}({\bf r}_{1},{\bf v}_{1/2},{\Delta t}/2)

(60)

and M2B,

\displaystyle{\bf r}_{1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf r}_{0}+\frac{1}{2}{\Delta t}{\bf v}_{0}

\displaystyle{\bf v}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf v}_{B}({\bf r}_{1/2},{\bf v}_{0},{\Delta t})

\displaystyle{\bf r}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf r}_{1/2}+\frac{1}{2}{\Delta t}{\bf v}_{1}.

(61)

In order to facilitate comparison with staggered time-step algorithms,
sequentially updated variables in the above two algorithms have been subscripted by
the accumulated time step of that variable.
For the same test problem as in Fig.
3
, they now produce the two
upright square orbits as labeled in Fig.
8
.
The glaring off-set errors
y_{c}=\pm r_{g}{\theta}/2
in Fig.
3
are now absent.

Figure 9:
(color online)
The anatomy of second-order Poisson solvers M2A and M2B.

The anatomy of these two algorithms are shown in Fig.
9
.
For M2A, the particle starts at
{\bf r}_{0}
, rotates by
{\theta}/2
, then travels the
full distance
r_{g}{\theta}
to
{\bf r}_{1}
. This is just
{\bf r}_{1}-{\bf r}_{0}
of M1B rotated by
{\theta}/2

and therefore
y_{c}=0
. One then has the bottom right triangle with

\sin({\theta}/2)=\frac{r_{g}{\theta}/2}{R_{g}}\quad\rightarrow\quad R_{g}=r_{g}\frac{{\theta}/2}{\sin({\theta}/2)}

(62)

and again

x_{c}=r_{g}-R_{g}=r_{g}(1-\frac{{\theta}/2}{\sin({\theta}/2)}).

(63)

At first sight, M2A is no better than MLF, since it still has errors
R_{g}
and
x_{c}
.
However, since the
y_{c}
error in this case is eliminated by a rotation so that
R_{g}
is
along the
x
-axis, the
x_{c}
error above has the same error dependence as
R_{g}

(in contrast to (
43
)),
and
both
can be simultaneously set to zero by
the alternative Boris angle
\sin({\theta}_{C}/2)={\theta}/2
!
This means that if
{\bf r}_{0}
is initially at the gyrocircle, then

{\bf r}_{1}
, and all subsequent positions must also be on the gyrocircle, as long as

|{\theta}/2|\leq 1
. We will refer to this algorithm as C2A. Note that for C2A,
only its defining angle (
52
) is needed in (
60
), not its double angle (
53
).

Similarly, for M2B, from Fig.
9
, the particle starts at
{\bf r}_{0}
,
moves along
{\bf v}_{0}
a distance
r_{g}{\theta}/2
to
{\bf r}_{1/2}
,
rotates
{\bf v}_{0}
by
{\theta}
, then travels
r_{g}{\theta}/2
again to
{\bf r}_{1}
.
The points
{\bf r}_{0}
and
{\bf r}_{1}
here are just midpoints of M1B with downward shifted
y\rightarrow y-r_{g}{\theta}/2
,
resulting in
y_{c}=0
.
From the base triangle, one now has

\tan({\theta}/2)=r_{g}({\theta}/2)/R_{g}\quad\rightarrow\quad R_{g}=r_{g}\frac{{\theta}/2}{\tan({\theta}/2)}

(64)

and

x_{c}=r_{g}-R_{g}=r_{g}(1-\frac{{\theta}/2}{\tan({\theta}/2)}).

(65)

Again, because
R_{g}
is now along the
x
-axis,
x_{c}
above has the same error dependence as
R_{g}
.
The original Boris angle
\tan({\theta}_{B}/2)={\theta}/2
then also simultaneously eliminate both, but in this
case trajectories will be
exactly on the gyrocircle for
all

{\Delta t}
. We will refer to this algorithm as B2B,
or the
symmetric
second-order Boris solver.
The trajectories of C2A and B2B are as shown in Fig.
8
. The rotation angles
of M2A and M2B are again exactly correct, while those of C2A and B2B are ahead and behind
by approximately the same amount.

Algorithms B2A and C2B, corresponding to choosing the wrong Boris angle for M2A and M2B
will not yield trajectories on the gyro-circle.
They are just phase-shifted versions of M2A and M2B and therefore
not shown in Fig.
8
. All six algorithms will converge as second-order integrators
at small
{\Delta t}
. However, one perennial problem of
plasma physics simulations is that one would like to use time steps not limited by the
rapid local cyclotron motion and short gyro-period. The Boris solver B2B is unique
in that in the limit of
{\theta}\rightarrow\infty
,

{\theta}_{B}\rightarrow\pi
, B2B’s trajectory will just bounce back and forth nearly
as straight lines across the diameter of the gyro-circle. Thus in contrast to
all other algorithms, only B2B’s trajectory remains bounded
to the exact orbit even as
{\Delta t}\rightarrow\infty
.
As shown in Figs.
5

and
6
, its gyro-radius remains nearly identical to the exact result
even for a non-uniform magnetic field and is orders of magnitude smaller
than those of B1A, B1B or BLF.

In a recent work, one of us has given an alternative derivation
chin20b
of C2A and B2B
by requiring M2A’s and M2B’s trajectory to be exactly on the gyro-circle.
That then automatically forces the gyro-center to the origin and
R_{g}=r_{g}
.
That derivation did not explain why one has to start with M2A and M2B.
The present derivation shows that
first-order algorithms have off-center errors
x_{c}
and
y_{c}
. The error

y_{c}
must first be eliminated by symmetric second-order solvers M2A and M2B.
The errors
x_{c}
and
R_{g}
can then be eliminated simultaneously
by a suitable choice of Boris angles, resulting in on-orbit trajectories.
The two derivations are therefore complementary.
A third derivation of C2A and B2B has been implicitly given in
Ref.
chin08
sometime ago. For completeness, that derivation will now be
summarized in Appendix A.

Algorithm B2B is cited as the Boris solver in Refs.
sto02
;
he15
;
kna15
;
rip18
.
Most think that B2B is just a reformulation
kna15
of, or is “essentially the same”
rip18

as, BLF. As shown in this work, this is not the case. BLF has the error gyroradius
R_{g}
(
48
)
while B2B does not. The difference between the two will be explained in the next section.

The fact that B2B trajectory in a constant magnetic field is exactly on the gyrocircle
seemed not to be widely known after Boris superseded Buneman’s derivation
bir85
,
otherwise, it would not have been necessary for Stoltz, Cary, Penn and Wurtele
sto02

to explicitly verify that again in 2002. This on-orbit property is also
not
noted in some
recent publications
zen18
;
rip18
;
ric20
. This may also be due to the fact that many authors
were not aware of the difference between BLF and B2B.

V
Leap frog Boris and symmetric Boris are different algorithms

As shown in Sect.
III
, the leap frog Boris solver BLF, as originally formulated by Boris
bor70
,
and widely disseminated by Birdsall and Langdon
bir85
, has the large
R_{g}
error and is not on-orbit.
Yet, many publications
sto02
;
he15
;
kna15
;
rip18
that use the on-orbit solver B2B, do not
distinguish the latter as being different from the original “Boris solver”. In this section, we make it
absolutely clear that the two are different algorithms having different gyroradii.

Consider iterating B2B in its operator form in a constant magnetic field

{\cal T}_{2B}^{n}=\cdots|{\rm e}^{({\Delta t}/2)T}{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}V_{B}}{\rm e}^{({\Delta t}/2)T}|{\rm e}^{({\Delta t}/2)T}{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}V_{B}}{\rm e}^{({\Delta t}/2)T}|{\rm e}^{({\Delta t}/2)T}{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}V_{B}}{\rm e}^{({\Delta t}/2)T},

(66)

where each vertical bar
|
indicates the end point of each iteration where
{\bf r}_{n}
and
{\bf v}_{n}

are outputted at integer time steps beginning with
n=1
. The rotating angle in
{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}V_{B}}

is
{\theta}_{B}
.

Iterating the leap frog solver BLF corresponds to iterating B1B with an initial
half time-step backward position:

\displaystyle{\cal T}_{LF}^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cdots|{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}V_{B}}{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}T}|{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}V_{B}}{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}T}|{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}V_{B}}{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}T}{\rm e}^{-({\Delta t}/2)T},

(67)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cdots|{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}V_{B}}{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}T}|{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}V_{B}}{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}T}|{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}V_{B}}{\rm e}^{({\Delta t}/2)T}.

At every end point, because of the the initial
{\rm e}^{-({\Delta t}/2)T}
, the position variable is always at
half integer time steps
{\bf r}_{n-1/2}
, while
{\bf v}_{n}
remains at integer time steps.
Because of this, positions at integer time step

{\bf r}_{n}

do not exist
for BLF.
Any attempt to define an integer time-step position
{\bf r}_{n}
for BLF, is an
ad hoc

alteration of the algorithm, making it no longer a leap frog algorithm.
For example, one can define the non-existent
{\bf r}_{n}
in BLF as

{\bf r}_{n}={\bf r}_{n-1/2}+\frac{1}{2}{\Delta t}{\bf v}_{n}\quad{\rm and}\quad{\bf r}_{n+1/2}={\bf r}_{n}+\frac{1}{2}{\Delta t}{\bf v}_{n},

(68)

so that (
45
) is satisfied. Introducing
{\bf r}_{n}
this way is tantamount to splitting
{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}T}

in (
67
) into two halves,

\displaystyle{\cal T}_{LF}^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cdots|{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}V_{B}}{\rm e}^{\frac{1}{2}{\Delta t}T}{\rm e}^{\frac{1}{2}{\Delta t}T}|{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}V_{B}}{\rm e}^{\frac{1}{2}{\Delta t}T}{\rm e}^{\frac{1}{2}{\Delta t}T}|{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}V_{B}}{\rm e}^{({\Delta t}/2)T}

(69)

and moving the end points to

\displaystyle{\cal T}_{LF}^{n}=\cdots{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}V_{B}}{\rm e}^{\frac{1}{2}{\Delta t}T}|{\rm e}^{({\Delta t}/2)T}{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}V_{B}}{\rm e}^{({\Delta t}/2)T}|{\rm e}^{({\Delta t}/2)T}{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}V_{B}}{\rm e}^{({\Delta t}/2)T}

(70)

so that it now resembles (
66
).
This last step, of moving the end points to the middle of
{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}T}
, where no such end point existed
in the original leap frog algorithm, fundamentally changed BLF to that of B2B. This is
not
a proof that
BLF is “equivalent” to B2B, but is an
ad hoc
derivation of B2B from BLF in the absence of
a systematic formalism.

Figure 10:
(color online)
The position P of BLF goes to Q along the chord of the
R_{g}
circle after one
{\Delta t}
-step update.
Each of B2B’s position is only a half
{\Delta t}
-step to the mid-chord, resulting in the correct gyroradius
r_{g}
.

The reason why the two solvers have different gyroradii is extremely simple.
For B2B, if one outputs the position only at
the end of
N
iterations, then (
66
) is effectively

{\cal T}_{2B}^{n}=|{\rm e}^{({\Delta t}/2)T}{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}V_{B}}{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}T}\cdots{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}V_{B}}{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}T}{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}V_{B}}{\rm e}^{({\Delta t}/2)T},

(71)

which is exactly like BLF of (
67
),
except

for the last position update
{\rm e}^{({\Delta t}/2)T}
. Before this update, all positions and velocities of
B2B (
71
) are
identical
to those of BLF (
67
). Both are on the same
gyrocircle of radius
R_{g}
(
48
).
For BLF, the next position, due to the next update
{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}T}
, will be the position Q,
at a distance
v{\Delta t}=(v/\omega)(\omega{\Delta t})=r_{g}{\theta}
from the original position P,
along a chord of the circle, as shown in Fig.
10
.
However, the position output by B2B, due to the final
{\rm e}^{({\Delta t}/2)T}
, is only
a distance
v{\Delta t}/2=r_{g}{\theta}/2
to the middle of the chord, giving its distance from the center as

\sqrt{R_{g}^{2}-r_{g}^{2}{\theta}^{2}/4}=r_{g}
! Every iterated position of BLF is always on the larger

R_{g}
circle. Every iterated position of B2B is that of BLF
plus
a half-time step position
to the mid-chord of the
R_{g}
circle. Therefore, each position of B2B is
always
at the
smaller
r_{g}
circle.

This is also illustrated in Fig.
7
. After 600 iterations of BLF,
applying the final half-time step position update immediately drops the trajectory back to the correct
gyrocircle of B2B, as indicated by the black connecting line.

VI
Second-order electric and magnetic field integrators

For a combined electric and magnetic field,
second order algorithms from (
27
) are

{\cal T}_{2A}={\rm e}^{({\Delta t}/2)V_{BF}}{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}T}{\rm e}^{({\Delta t}/2)V_{BF}}\quad{\rm and}\quad{\cal T}_{2B}={\rm e}^{({\Delta t}/2)T}{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}V_{BF}}{\rm e}^{({\Delta t}/2)T},

(72)

which will be named as EM2A and EM2B.
Since the action of
{\rm e}^{{\Delta t}V_{BF}}
on
{\bf r}
and
{\bf v}
are known via (
30
), the
algorithms are straightforwardly defined.
However, we will give here a more intuitive derivation
of (
33
) to make clear its connection with
the original works of Buneman
bun67
and Boris
bor70
.

To minimize distractions, we will ignore the trivial motions parallel to the magnetic field
and assume that both
{\bf v}
and
{\bf a}
are perpendicular to
{\bf B}
.
To solve (
1
), one can set

{\bf E}\times{\bf B}

where we have denoted the action of the rotation operator by a square bracket and that

\tilde{\bf v}_{1}=\tilde{\bf v}({\Delta t})

{\bf v}_{1}-{\bf u}=R({\bf r},{\theta})[{\bf v}_{0}-{\bf u}].

(76)

Here, we go beyond Buneman by letting the rotation operator acts on each velocity,

\displaystyle{\bf v}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R({\bf r},{\theta})[{\bf v}_{0}-{\bf u}]+{\bf u}

(77)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R({\bf r},{\theta})[{\bf v}_{0}]+{\bf u}-\cos{\theta}{\bf u}-\sin{\theta}(\hat{\bf B}\times{\bf u})

(78)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R({\bf r},{\theta})[{\bf v}_{0}]+\frac{1}{\omega}\left[(1-\cos{\theta})\hat{\bf B}\times{\bf a}+\sin{\theta}{\bf a}\right],

(79)

which is then just (
30
) without the parallel motion.

Buneman’s velocity update (
76
) was considered undesirable because
|{\bf u}|\propto 1/\omega\propto 1/B({\bf r})

and is singular where
B({\bf r})\approx 0
. However, there is
no
such singularity in
(
79
),
after

{\bf u}
has been rotated and combined. In the usual case of
{\Delta t}<1

\omega\propto B({\bf r})\rightarrow 0

\frac{1}{\omega}\left[(1-\cos{\theta})\hat{\bf B}\times{\bf a}+\sin{\theta}{\bf a}\right]\rightarrow{\Delta t}\left[{\theta}(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{{\theta}^{2}}{4!}+\cdots)\hat{\bf B}\times{\bf a}+(1-\frac{{\theta}^{2}}{3!}+\cdots){\bf a}\right]

(80)

with no singular terms. The only problem is when
{\Delta t}>>1
such that when

\omega\rightarrow 0

{\theta}
remains finite.

, when the rotating angle is
{\theta}_{B}

The splitting is completely unnecessary.
Replacing the rotating angle in the drift term of (

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega}\left(\frac{{\theta}^{2}/2}{1+{\theta}^{2}/4}\hat{\bf B}\times{\bf a}+\frac{{\theta}}{1+{\theta}^{2}/4}{\bf a}\right)

(81)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\Delta t}\left(\frac{{\theta}/2}{1+{\theta}^{2}/4}\hat{\bf B}\times{\bf a}+\frac{1}{1+{\theta}^{2}/4}{\bf a}\right).

{\bf B}

81

Eq.(22), corresponding to the velocity update

\displaystyle(1+\frac{1}{2}{\theta}{\cal C}){\bf v}_{0}+{\Delta t}{\bf a},

\displaystyle=

VII
Conclusions and future directions

In this work, we have derived various Boris solvers on the basis of the Lie operator method,
the same formalism used to derive symplectic integrators. The advantage of this approach
is that it can uncover trajectory errors, which are the foundational basis for Boris solvers,
not obvious from finite-difference schemes. The glaring off-set error
of the gyro-center, as well as that of the gyro-radius, can be used to easily identify
first-order or leap-frog Boris solvers in historical calculations and current discussions.

Our formalism provides a global view of the structure of algorithms, as illustrated in
Sect.
V
, which unambiguously differentiate the intrinsic symmetric second-order Boris solver
from the conventional leap frog Boris solver.
Such a global view naturally suggests a simple explanation of why their gyroradii
are different, as shown in Fig.
10
. This observation is not obvious from just
examining the analytical form of their respective algorithm.

By using the cross-product operator
{\cal C}
, we were able to show easily the equivalence of the
velocity update in Buneman’s drift-subtracting scheme, Boris’s original inversion algorithm,
and Boris’ E-B splitting method. Most surprisingly,
we found that Boris’ E-B splitting was
unnecessary in that the there was no problem for the splitting to solve,
when Buneman’s drift-subtracting scheme is properly implemented as (
81
).
The realization that one has effectively
{\cal C}=``i
” immediately make many results obvious.
Representing the cross-product as a
3\times 3
matrix
sto02
;
he15
;
kna15
completely obscures
this crucial insight.

By repeating some historical calculations, this work showed that the second-order Boris solver EB2B,
can be used for large
{\Delta t}
calculations with far greater accuracy than previously thought. It is
the only algorithm currently known to be stable at
{\Delta t}
greater than the local gyro-period
for nonuniform fields. The obvious future direction is to devise beyond second-order, more accurate
Boris-like integrators which are simultaneously stable at large time steps.
