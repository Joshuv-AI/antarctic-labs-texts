Relativistic variational methods and the Virial Theorem
Charlotte Froese Fischer, Michel Godefroid

Abstract
In the case of the one-electron Dirac equation with a point nucleus the Virial Theorem (VT) states that the ratio of the kinetic energy to potential energy is exactly − 1 -1 , a ratio that can be an independent test of the accuracy of a computed solution. This paper studies the virial theorem for subshells of equivalent electrons and their interactions in many-electron atoms. It shows that some Slater integrals impose conditions on a single subshell but others impose conditions between subshells. The latter slow the rate of convergence of the self-consistent field process in which radial functions are updated one at a time. Several cases are considered.

2
General theory

In classical mechanics, the virial theorem (VT) is well known
[
7
]
. It relates the total kinetic energy of a stable system of discrete particles, bound by potential forces, with that of the total potential energy of the system. The validity of this theorem for atoms was proved by Finkelstein
[
8
]
for the Schrödinger equation in 1928. Fock
[
9
,
10
]
, who used the variational method to derive the Hartree-Fock method for atoms, was the first to derive the theorem for the Dirac equations using a "stretching method", also referred to as a “scaling”
[
11
]
or “dilation” procedure
[
2
]
. In this analysis we will consider scaling as a perturbation of the many-electron wave function, analyze the effect of such a perturbation on each of the orbital equations, and relate the results to the virial theorem for the total energy.

In order to analyse the effect on the energy of a single electron in a potential from the scaling of the radial function, it is convenient to write Eq. (
1
) in operator form, namely

\Big(T(r)+V(r)+M+\epsilon_{a}\;S\Big)\;u_{a}(r)=0,

(6)

where

\displaystyle T(r)=

\displaystyle c\left[\begin{array}[]{c c}0&-\left(\frac{d}{dr}-\frac{\kappa}{r}\right)\\
\frac{d}{dr}+\frac{\kappa}{r}&0\end{array}\right],\;\;V(r)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c c}V(r)&0\\
0&V(r)\end{array}\right],

\displaystyle M=-2c^{2}\left[\begin{array}[]{c c}0&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right],\;\;S=\left[\begin{array}[]{c c}1&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right].

Here
T(r)
is the kinetic energy operator,
V(r)
the potential energy operator,
M
is a constant matrix referred to as the mass operator, whereas
S
is the identity matrix.

Consider the scaling perturbation,
r\rightarrow\lambda r

of the radial function, so that Eq. (
6
) becomes

\Big(T(r)+V(r)+M+\epsilon_{a}(\lambda)\;S\Big)\,u_{a}(\lambda r)=0,

(18)

where
\epsilon_{a}
is now a function of
\lambda
. Before deriving an expression for the energy parameter, let us "de-scale" the entire radial equation by replacing
r
by
r/\lambda
so that the equation becomes

\Big(T(r/\lambda)+V(r/\lambda)+M+\epsilon_{a}(\lambda)\;S\Big)\,u_{a}(r)=0.

(19)

Notice that, whereas the scaled radial function went back to its original function, the operators in the radial equation changed.

In Eq. (
19
), the function
u_{a}(r)
is normalized and so, multiplying on the left by
u^{t}_{a}(r)
and integrating, it follows that

-\epsilon_{a}(\lambda)=\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{t}_{a}(r)\;\Big(\,T(r/\lambda)+V(r/\lambda)+M\Big)\,u_{a}(r)dr

(20)

For a solution
u_{a}(r)
, stationary with respect to the perturbation parameter
\lambda
, the energy will satisfy the variational condition,

\Big(\frac{d\,\epsilon(\lambda)}{d\lambda}\Big)_{\lambda=1}=0.

(21)

In order to continue the analysis, let us consider a continuous operator
{O}(r)
and define the expectation value of the operator to be

\langle O\rangle_{a}(\lambda)=\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{t}_{a}(r)\,O(r/\lambda)\,u_{a}(r)dr

(22)

and
\langle O\rangle_{a}\equiv\langle O\rangle_{a}(\lambda=1)
.
Then, according to
Eqs. (
18
,
20
),

\langle T\rangle_{a}+\langle V\rangle_{a}+\langle M\rangle_{a}+\epsilon_{a}=0,

(23)

and the variational condition of Eq. (
21
)
leads to

\Big(\frac{d\langle T\rangle_{a}(\lambda)}{d\lambda}+\frac{d\langle V\rangle_{a}(\lambda)}{d\lambda}\Big)_{\lambda=1}=0.

(24)

Thus we have shown that the scaling of the energy depends only on the scaling of
T(r)+V(r)
.

The scaling of
T(r)
is simple.
Clearly
\kappa/r\rightarrow\lambda\,\kappa/r
as
r\rightarrow r/\lambda
. The operator
d/dr
scales in the same manner, so that

T(r/\lambda)=\lambda\,T(r),\;\;\forall\;r.

(25)

Hence

\Big(\frac{d\langle T\rangle_{a}(\lambda)}{d\lambda}\Big)_{\lambda=1}=\langle T\rangle_{a}.

(26)

But in general, since differentiation with respect to
\lambda
and integration with respect to
r
commute,

\Big(\frac{d\langle O\rangle_{a}(\lambda)}{d\lambda}\Big)_{\lambda=1}=\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{t}_{a}(r)\;\Big(\frac{\partial O(r/\lambda)}{\partial\lambda}\Big)_{\lambda=1}\;u_{a}(r)dr\;.

(27)

Let
\widetilde{r}=r/\lambda
. Then

\Big(\frac{\partial O(r/\lambda)}{\partial\lambda}\Big)_{\lambda=1}=\Big(\frac{dO(\widetilde{r})}{d\widetilde{r}}\times\frac{d\widetilde{r}}{d\lambda}\Big)_{\lambda=1}=-r\frac{d}{dr}O(r).

(28)

The
-rd/dr
operator was first introduced by Fock
[
9
,
10
]
and we will refer to it as the Fock rule.

The discussion so far has assumed a single electron in a potential. We will extend this to a single orbital in a Hartree potential for equivalent electrons in a given subshell, and finally, an orbital in a Hartree-Fock potential for multiple subshells of equivalent electrons, all of which are scaled. We will show that, in general, the scaling condition of Eq. (
24
) resulting from the stationary condition of the orbital energy Eq. (
21
) with respect to the variation of
\lambda
(at
\lambda=1)
), has the form

\langle T\rangle_{a}+\langle V\rangle_{a}+\langle W\rangle_{a}=\langle R\rangle_{a}\;,

(29)

where
\langle R\rangle_{a}
is referred to as a residual for the orbital equation, a quantity that is zero for exact solutions in the case of a single orbital but may not be zero in the case where the variation includes multiple orbitals. At the same time, it is referred to as a residual because, in numerical work, results will not be exactly zero, in which case
\langle R\rangle_{a}
reflects the accuracy of the numerical procedures.
Expressions for
\langle W\rangle_{a}
appearing in Eq. (
29
) will be determined, as a correction arising from the non-hydrogenic potential. We refer to this expression as the orbital virial theorem (OVT) to distinguish it from the VT for the total energy for systems with multiple orbitals and
will show that
\sum_{a}q_{a}\langle R\rangle_{a}=0
for a solution that satisfies the virial theorem. Here
q_{a}
is the occupation number of shell
a
.

3
Point nucleus:
V(r)=-Z/r

In the special case of a single electron in a potential for an atom or ion with a point nucleus,
V(r)=-Z/r
, where
Z
is a constant representing the nuclear charge. Hence, like the kinetic energy,

\langle{V}\rangle_{a}(\lambda)=\lambda\langle V\rangle_{a}
.
Taking the partial derivative with respect to
\lambda
, and then setting
\lambda=1
and
R(r)=0

[
9

10

Essentially, the kinetic energy and the potential energy are equal in magnitude but opposite in sign, with the kinetic energy being positive, and with the sum being a test of accuracy of the computed solution.
In this case, the potential function is said to be homogeneous of degree
-1
(ie. scales as

)
[
12

and
\langle W\rangle_{a}=0.

4
General Case:
V(r)=-Z(r)/r

In the case of multiple equivalent electrons, the effective nuclear charge varies as a function of
r
as in the Hartree-potential for a subshell, or as in the case of a finite nucleus, where the potential near the nucleus is modified.
For such a situation, where the potential can be represented as
V(r)=-Z(r)/r
, the Fock rule yields

\left(\frac{d\langle V\rangle_{a}(\lambda)}{{d}\lambda}\right)_{\lambda=1}=\left\langle-r\frac{dV(r)}{dr}\right\rangle_{a}=\left\langle V(r)\right\rangle_{a}+\left\langle\frac{dZ(r)}{dr}\right\rangle_{a}\;.

(31)

Thus scaling of this potential has introduced a new type of term, namely
\langle W\rangle_{a}=\langle dZ(r)/dr\rangle_{a}=\langle Z^{\prime}(r)\rangle_{a}
. Notice that it depends on a derivative. Therefore, it
is not affected by adding or subtracting a constant. If
Z(r)=Z-s(r)
,
then
\langle W\rangle_{a}=-\langle s^{\prime}(r)\rangle_{a}
, where
s(r)/r
represents the deviation
of
V(r)
from the Coulomb potential
-Z/r
.

Given these quantities, the Virial Theorem of an electron in a potential remains that of Eq. (
29
) but now
\langle W\rangle_{a}
will not be zero.
Adding and subtracting
\langle W\rangle_{a}
to the orbital energy equation (
23
),
we get :

\langle T\rangle_{a}+\langle V\rangle_{a}+\langle W\rangle_{a}+\langle M\rangle_{a}-\langle W\rangle_{a}+\epsilon_{a}=0\;.

(32)

Noting that by Eq. (
29
), the sum of the first three terms is
\langle R\rangle_{a}
,
we obtain the second orbital virial relationship, namely

\langle M\rangle_{a}+\langle R\rangle_{a}-\langle W\rangle_{a}+\epsilon_{a}=0.

(33)

5
DHF equations for multiple orbitals

The theory presented so far has been discussed strictly as an electron (
a
) in a potential but it can readily be extended to a subshell (say
\alpha
) of
q_{a}
equivalent electrons by multiplying the orbital properties by the occupation number. For example, for the
1s^{2}
subshell containing two
1s
electrons, we have

\langle T\rangle_{1s^{2}}=2\langle T\rangle_{1s},\ \ \langle V\rangle_{1s^{2}}=2\langle V\rangle_{1s},\ \ \langle W\rangle_{1s^{2}}=2\langle W\rangle_{1s},\ \ \langle R\rangle_{1s^{2}}=2\langle R\rangle_{1s}.

(35)

Multiplying also Eq. (
29
) by
q_{1s}=2
, we get the OVT for the
1s^{2}
subshell, namely

\langle T\rangle_{1s^{2}}+\langle V\rangle_{1s^{2}}+\langle W\rangle_{1s^{2}}=\langle R\rangle_{1s^{2}}.

(36)

In this form, the radial equations for the orbitals are derived from the variation of a specific orbital without dividing by the occupation number for the subshell, as often is done for the derivation of the Hartree-Fock equations for multi-subshell atoms.

In order to derive expressions for the various kinetic, potential, and other properties of subshells, we will outline an algorithm for determining contributions from radial integrals and their coefficients as they appear in an energy expression and then derive the scaling equation for the total energy by summing over the subshells. This approach will include both direct and exchange contributions to the potential. For an exact (self-consistent) solution, for which
\sum_{a}q_{a}\langle R\rangle_{a}=0
, we now have

\sum_{\alpha}\langle R\rangle_{\alpha}=0
by Eq. (
35
).

In our analysis here we will consider Dirac-Hartree-Fock energy expressions that include only one-electron integrals,

I(a,a)=\langle T\rangle_{a}+\langle V_{1}\rangle_{a}+\langle M\rangle_{a}

and Slater integrals,

F^{k}(a,b)
or
G^{k}(a,b)
that are 2-electron contributions to
\langle V_{2}\rangle_{\alpha}
where

\langle V\rangle_{\alpha}=\langle V_{1}\rangle_{\alpha}+\langle V_{2}\rangle_{\alpha}.

Given an orbital basis and an energy expression, the kinetic and potential energies for subshells can readily be computed directly without considering orbital potentials.

The one-electron integrals
I(a,a)
, multiplied by their coefficients, contribute directly to
\langle T\rangle_{\alpha}
,
\langle V\rangle_{\alpha}
, and
\langle M\rangle_{\alpha}
, but contributions from Slater integrals that may depend on two different orbitals (subshells) and then contribute to both subshells, need further investigation.

5.1
Slater integrals

The relativistic Slater integral, usually denoted as
R^{k}(ab,cd)
, is a two-dimensional integral with two co-ordinates, say
(r,s)
. Let us denote radial factors in terms of vector products of large and small components

\rho(ac;r)=u_{a}^{t}(r)u_{c}(r),\mbox{ and }\rho(bd;s)=u_{b}^{t}(s)u_{d}(s).

Slater integrals have many symmetries, arising from the symmetry of a product, and the symmetry of the co-ordinate system. In the canonical form, orbitals are in a designated order such that
a<=c,b<=d
,
a<=b
and, if
a=b
, then also
c<=d
. Consequently, a typical canonical integral is

R^{k}(ab,cd)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\rho(ac;r)\frac{Y^{k}(bd;r)}{r}\right)dr\ \ \equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{Y^{k}(ac;r)}{r}\rho(bd;r)\right)dr\;,

(37)

where

\displaystyle Y^{k}(bd;r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{r}\left(\frac{s}{r}\right)^{k}\rho(bd;s)ds+\int_{r}^{\infty}\left(\frac{r}{s}\right)^{(k+1)}\rho(bd;s)ds

(38)

Notice that
Y^{k}(bd;r)
has two contributions, and could be defined as

\displaystyle Y^{k}(bd;r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r^{-k}\;A(bd;r)+r^{k+1}\;B(bd;r)\;,

(39)

with
A(bd;r)
from charge density
s<r
, and
B(bd;r)
, from charge density
s>r
, namely

A(bd;r)=\int_{0}^{r}s^{k}\rho(bd;s)ds\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;B(bd;r)=\int_{r}^{\infty}s^{-(k+1)}\rho(bd;s)ds\;.

(40)

Because the variable
r
simply defines the range of integration, the derivatives of
A
and
B
obey a useful relation

r^{-k}A^{\prime}(bd;r)+r^{k+1}B^{\prime}(bd;r)=0\;.

(41)

The calculation of Slater integrals (and related
Y^{k}
functions) is the most time-consuming aspect of atomic structure calculations. Hartree
[
17
]
, who did calculations with a desk calculator, found that the most efficient method was to solve two first-order differential equations that do not depend on powers of
r^{k}
. The solution of the first differential equation for outward integration, was the function

Z^{k}(bd;r)=r^{-k}\int_{0}^{r}s^{k}\rho(bd;s)ds,

(42)

whereas the second was inward integration of an equation for the function
Y^{k}(bd;r)
, with the initial starting condition,
Y^{k}(bd;r_{max})=Z^{k}(bd;r_{max})
. Procedures for
solving these equations have recently been evaluated
[
4
]
.

For the variation of the potential with respect to scaling we need to consider the contribution to a potential, say
V^{k}(bd;r)\equiv Y^{k}(bd;r)/r
or

V^{k}(bd;r)=r^{-(k+1)}A(bd;r)+r^{k}B(bd;r)\;.

(43)

5.2
Scaling of the
V^{k}(bd;r)
operator

Applying the Fock rule (
28
) on the potential term
V^{k}(bd;r)
and using the property (
41
), it readily follows that

\Big(\frac{\partial V^{k}(bd;r/\lambda)}{\partial\lambda}\Big)_{\lambda=1}=-r\frac{d}{dr}V^{k}(bd;r).

(44)

can be written as

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{\partial V^{k}(bd;r/\lambda)}{\partial\lambda}\Big)_{\lambda=1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V^{k}(bd;r)+W^{k}(bd;r)=Y^{k}(bd;r)/r+W^{k}(bd;r)

(45)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(k+1)r^{-(k+1)}A(bd;r)-kr^{k}B(bd;r)\;.

By definition (
42
),
r^{-(k+1)}A(bd;r)=Z^{k}(bd;r)/r
. Using Eq. (
39
) it follows that
r^{k}B(bd;r)=Y^{k}(bd;r)/r-Z^{k}(bd;r)/r
.
Consequently,
W^{k}(bd;r)
can be extracted from Eq. (
45
) as

\displaystyle W^{k}(bd;r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Z^{k}(bd;r)/r-Y^{k}(bd;r)/r+k\left(2Z^{k}(bd;r)/r-Y^{k}(bd;r)/r\right)

(46)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(2k+1)\;Z^{k}(bd;r)/r-(k+1)Y^{k}(bd;r)/r

5.3
Symmetry of Slater integrals in DHF energy expressions

The above derivation was a general derivation for any combination of orbitals. In DHF calculations only
R^{k}(ab,ab)
or
R^{k}(ab,ba)
symmetries occur. Then, if
d=b
and
c=a

the contribution to the scaling

\langle W\rangle_{\alpha}
would be

\langle W^{k}(bb;r)\rangle_{aa}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho(aa;r)W^{k}(bb;r)dr.

(47)

Here we have introduced the subscript
aa
to denote the
\rho(r)
function required for the calculation of the expectation value.
Scaling in the other co-ordinate, yields a contribution to
\langle W\rangle_{\beta}
as

\langle W^{k}(aa;r)\rangle_{bb}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho(bb;r)W^{k}(aa;r)dr.

(48)

In the case of a
G^{k}(ab)
Slater integral there is only one possibility, namely
Y^{k}(ab;r)
and
\rho(ab;r)
,
and the integral

\langle W^{k}(ab;r)\rangle_{ab}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho(ab;r)W^{k}(ab;r)dr,

(49)

would contribute to both

\langle W\rangle_{\alpha}
and
\langle W\rangle_{\beta}
, multiplied by the coefficient of the Slater integral.

5.4
Total energy virial equation

Computationally, the list of one-electron integrals and Slater integrals, along with their coefficients, defines the total energy of a state and also
determines the subshell quantities
\langle T\rangle_{\alpha}
,
\langle V\rangle_{\alpha}
,
\langle M\rangle_{\alpha}
, and
\langle W\rangle_{\alpha}
. Then

\langle T\rangle=\sum_{\alpha}\langle T\rangle_{\alpha},\quad\langle M\rangle=\sum_{\alpha}\langle M\rangle_{\alpha},\quad\langle W\rangle=\sum_{\alpha}\langle W\rangle_{\alpha},\quad\langle R\rangle=\sum_{\alpha}\langle R\rangle_{\alpha}

(50)

are values that contribute directly to the total energy and its dilation equation. However, the sum of potential energies of subshells is different. Because the Slater integrals with orbitals
a,b
from subshells
\alpha,\beta

make contributions to both
\langle V\rangle_{\alpha}
and
\langle V\rangle_{\beta}
(or twice to
\langle V\rangle_{\alpha}
when
b=a
), we have

\sum_{\alpha}\langle V\rangle_{\alpha}=\langle V\rangle+\langle V_{2}\rangle

(51)

where
\langle V\rangle
is the potential energy for the total energy and
\langle V_{2}\rangle
represents the contribution from the 2-body Slater integrals. Then we have the two equations,

\displaystyle\langle T\rangle+\langle V\rangle+\langle M\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\;,

\displaystyle\langle T\rangle+\langle V\rangle+\langle U\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle R\rangle\quad\mbox{where}\quad\langle U\rangle=\langle V_{2}\rangle+\langle W\rangle.

(52)

Consequently,
\langle T\rangle+\langle V\rangle=\langle R\rangle-\langle U\rangle
. Substituting into the energy equation, we get the mass dilation condition

\langle M\rangle+\langle R\rangle-\langle U\rangle=E.

(53)

Thus an exact solution requires that
\langle U\rangle=0
and also
\langle R\rangle=0
in which case it satisfies two conditions, namely
\langle T\rangle+\langle V\rangle=0
and
\langle M\rangle=E.

1s

\lambda>1

In MCDHF calculations, the contribution to
\langle U\rangle
will be zero if the contribution from each Slater integral is zero, namely that for each Slater integral its contribution to
\langle U\rangle=\langle W\rangle+\langle V_{2}\rangle

is zero.

Three cases need to be considered all with
k=0
, in our present examples.

1.

F^{k}(a,a)
:
By Eq. (
46
) the scaling is the same in both co-ordinates so

2\langle W^{0}(aa;r)\rangle_{aa}+F^{0}(a,a)=2\langle Z^{0}(aa;r)\rangle_{aa}-F^{0}(a,a)=0.

(54)

2.

G^{0}(a,b)
:
In this case the contribution is the same for each co-ordinate and the sum (involving both subshells) is

2\langle W^{0}(ab;r)\rangle_{ab}+G^{0}(a,b)=2\langle Z^{0}(ab;r)\rangle_{ab}-G^{0}(a,b)=0.

(55)

3.

F^{0}(a,b)
:
In this case, the scaling depends on the co-ordinate and the effect of scaling for orbital
a
differs from that for orbital
b
. However, the combined contribution to the dilation equation for the total energy should still be zero, namely

\langle W^{0}(bb;r)\rangle_{aa}+\langle W^{0}(aa;r)\rangle_{bb}+F^{0}(a,b)=\langle Z^{0}(bb;r)\rangle_{aa}+\langle Z^{0}(aa;r)\rangle_{bb}-F^{0}(a,b)=0.

(56)

Similar equations hold for
k>0
since, as seen in Eq. (
46
),
k
multiplies a factor

\left(2Z^{k}(bd;r)/r-Y^{k}(bd;r)/r\right)

(57)

for which an expectation value should be zero.
For example, the list of integrals
F^{k}(3d,3d),k=0,2,4
in the energy expression for a
3d^{10}
subshell would have the conditions,
2\langle Z^{k}(3d3d;r)\rangle_{3d3d}-F^{k}(3d,3d)=0,
associated with them for each
k
, a condition that might be difficult to satisfy exactly.
By Eqs. (
39
,
42
), this condition, in general, reduces to

\left(2Z^{k}(bd;r)/r-Y^{k}(bd;r)/r\right)=r^{-k}A(bd;r)-r^{k+1}B(bd;r).

(58)

Thus the virial theorem requires that certain expectation values of this difference be zero. In other words,
the inner and outer contributions to the potential from the
\rho(bd;r)
charge densities must be equal in magnitude, at least in the case of
F^{k}(a,a)
and
G^{k}(a,b)
. In the case of
F^{k}(a,b)
, two potentials and charge densities are involved, which probably explains why the
\langle R\rangle_{\alpha}
values in Table
1
tend to increase.

Let us confirm some results for the simple case of
1s^{2}
. The formula for the energy is

E=2\,\Big(\langle T\rangle_{1s}+\langle V_{1}\rangle_{1s}+\langle M\rangle_{1s}\Big)+F^{0}(1s,1s).

Then the subshell equations for the energy and dilation are, respectively,

\displaystyle 2\langle T\rangle_{1s}+2\langle V_{1}\rangle_{1s}+2F^{0}(1s,1s)+2\langle M\rangle_{1s}+2\epsilon(1s)=0,

\displaystyle 2\langle T\rangle_{1s}+2\langle V_{1}\rangle_{1s}+2F^{0}(1s,1s)+2\langle W\rangle_{1s}=2\langle R\rangle_{1s}.

(59)

The latter may be written as

\langle T\rangle+\langle V\rangle+\langle U\rangle=\langle R\rangle,

(60)

where

\langle V\rangle=2\langle V_{1}\rangle+F^{0}(1s,1s)

and

\langle U\rangle=F^{0}(1s,1s)+\langle W\rangle_{1s^{2}}=F^{0}(1s,1s)+2\langle W\rangle_{1s}

with

\langle W\rangle_{1s^{2}}=2\langle W^{0}(1s,1s;r)\rangle_{1s1s}=2\langle Z^{0}(1s,1s;r)/r-Y^{0}(1s,1s;r)/r\rangle_{1s1s}

by Eq. (
46
).
As a result, for an exact solution of
1s^{2}
, where
\langle U\rangle=0
and
\langle R\rangle=0
, we would have the condition

\langle T\rangle/\langle V\rangle=-1\quad{\mbox{a}nd}\quad\langle Z^{0}(1s,1s;r)/r\rangle_{1s1s}=\frac{1}{2}\langle Y^{0}(1s,1s;r)\rangle_{1s1s}\equiv\frac{1}{2}F^{0}(1s,1s).

(61)

Here
\rho(1s,1s;r)
is both a weighting factor for the expectation value and the charge density function that determines a contribution to the potential as a function of
r
.

7
Concluding remarks

Our study of the virial theorem has shown how extensively the variational method relies on cancellation in the computation. For the orbital equation the positive kinetic energy and the negative potential energy representing the interaction between an electron and the nucleus are the largest contributors to the total energy, yet they nearly cancel, particularly as the nuclear charge increases. All calculations for this study were done in double precision of about fifteen (15) significant digits. What is important is the the accuracy of the sum of these two integrals. Possibly these two quantities should be computed in quadruple precision. It also showed how the residual for the pairs of orbitals needed to be balanced.
Thus simultaneous updates of orbitals may be better able to balance the virial theorem conditions that accurate solutions of the variational problem need to accomplish. The performance of these methods as implemented in an
\mathtt{SPHF}

[
19
]
program was better for the NR method when orbital rotation in not used. But most tests focused on light atoms – what is needed are stable and accurate multiconfiguration methods for systems with several open shells such as the lanthanides and actinides. The
virial theorem could be a useful tool.
