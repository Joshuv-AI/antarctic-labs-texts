An Optimal Transportation Principle for Interacting Paths and Congestion
Rene Cabrera

Abstract
. In this work we study a modification of the Monge-Kantorovich problem taking into account path dependence and interaction effects between particles. We prove existence of solutions under mild conditions on the data, and after imposing stronger conditions, we characterize the minimizers by relating them to an auxiliary Monge-Kantorovich problem of the more standard kind. With this notion of how particles interact and travel along paths, we produce a dual problem. The main novelty here is to incorporate an interaction effect to the optimal path transport problem. This covers for instance, N N -body dynamics when the underlying measures are discrete. Lastly, our results include an extension of Brenier’s theorem on optimal transport maps.

[

[
4

gave a new impetus to the field which has considerably expanded and matured in the last three decades. The interested reader can find more about the history of the field, for example in the books by Villani
[
21
]
,
[

]
and Santambrogio
[
17

. As a result, the optimal transportation in its modern formulation (see below) is known as the Monge-Kantorovich problem (MKP). Monge set the precedent to study the optimal path transport problem to incorporate particle trajectories
[

[
2

The optimal transport problem may also be viewed as a distance problem between two probability measures, and the time-dependent minimization problem may be viewed as a minimal path problem

1.2.
MKP

Let us briefly review the modern description of Monge’s problem for the quadratic cost function,
c(x,y)=|x-y|^{2}
. One considers two density functions
f(x)
and
g(y)
. Then if we have two probability measures
\mu
on
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\nu
on
Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
\mu(x)=f(x)\;dx
and
\nu(y)=g(y)\;dy
. Suppose
T
is any (Borel) measurable function of
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
to
Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
T

pushes

\mu
forward to
\nu
. This is denoted by
T_{\sharp}\mu=\nu
and it means that for any measurable (Borel) subset

B\subset Y,\;\nu(B):=T_{\sharp}\mu(B)=\mu(T^{-1}(B))\;\text{and where}\;T^{-1}(B):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:T(x)\in B\}

Equivalently,
\int_{T^{-1}(B)}f(x)dx=\int_{B}g(y)dy
for all Borel sets
B
. When
T
is measure preserving (
T_{\sharp}\mu=\nu
), using the change of variables formula for any continuous function
h\in C^{0}(\bar{Y})
,

(2)

\displaystyle\int_{Y}h(y)\;d\nu(y)=\int_{X}h(T(x))\;d\mu(x)

is another characterization.

Monge’s problem
is: Minimize the total
transportation cost

(3)

\displaystyle\int_{X}c(x,T(x))d\mu(x)=\int_{X}|x-T(x)|^{2}\;d\mu(x)

among all
T
pushing forward
\mu
to
\nu

[
1
]
.

In 1940 Leonid Kantorovich
[
12
]
in some sense “relaxed” Monge’s problem
[
1
]
and
[
21
]
by introducing a linear program formulation for the problem. Concretely, again for the quadratic cost function, Kantorovich considered probability measures
\pi
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
with left and right marginals
\mu,\nu

\mu[A]=\pi[A\times\mathbb{R}^{n}]
and
\pi[\mathbb{R}^{n}\times B]=\nu[B]
for any (Borel) measurable subsets
A,B
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. An equivalent criterion for
\pi
to have left and right marginals
\mu,\nu
is the following linearity of
\pi

[
21
]

\forall(\varphi,\psi)\in L^{1}(d\mu)\times L^{1}(d\nu),\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}[\varphi(x)+\psi(y)]\;d\pi(x,y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(x)d\mu(x)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\psi(y)d\nu(y).

Kantorovich’s problem
is: Minimize

(4)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}c(x,y)d\pi(x,y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}|x-y|^{2}\;d\pi(x,y)

among all
\pi
having marginals
\mu,\nu
. The set of such measures is denoted by
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
, and it is never empty, as it contains the product measure
\mu\otimes\nu
; also
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is convex. So this problem is actually a linear minimization problem with convex constraints. Whenever
\pi
satisfies the marginal condition we say
\pi
is admissible. A basic result in functional analysis applying continuity and compactness arguments is the existence of minimizers of functionals
[
1
]
,
[
17
]
, and
[
21
]
.

Remark 1.1
.

As mentioned earlier, Kantorovich’s problem is a relaxation of Monge’s problem
[
1
]
. To illustrate this, if
(Id,T):X\to X\times Y
is defined by
(Id,T)(x):=(x,T(x))
and
T_{\sharp}\mu=\nu
, then
(Id,T)_{\sharp}\mu\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
, i.e., in a sense Kantorovich’s problem contains Monge’s.

An important property we require of costs on paths is that of
coercivity
, to prove existence of minimizers for (
5
) for energy
c(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{1}L(\dot{\gamma},\gamma,t)\;dt
with Lagrangian
L(\dot{\gamma},\gamma,t)=\frac{1}{2}|\dot{\gamma}|^{2}-V(\gamma(t),t)
; this is to, geometrically speaking, avoid paths that oscillate a lot. In this case the interpretation is that the cost
c(\gamma)
would be rather large and thus would be too costly, and we wish to eschew this in our theory (see Condition
1
for further details).

The optimal path (Kantorovich) problem is to minimize

(5)

\displaystyle\inf_{\pi\in\Pi_{\text{path}}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})}\int_{\Omega}c(\gamma)\;d\pi(\gamma),

this problem admits a solution under general conditions (Theorem
1.4
). A similar result to Theorem
1.4
applies to a new formulation in which we incorporate interacting paths (Theorem
1.8
).
The former is analogues to a result that extends Brenier
[
4
]
and Gangbo and McCann
[
8
]
. Indeed it turns out that these minimizers are given by maps (Theorem
1.5
)

\Gamma:X\times[0,1]\longrightarrow\Omega,

with the properties

\Gamma(x,0)=x\quad\text{for all}\quad x\in X;\quad\Gamma(x,1)=T(x),\quad T_{\sharp}\mu_{0}=\mu_{1}.

For a map
T
that solves an auxiliary transport problem, the optimal measure will be given by
\pi_{\Gamma}:=(\Gamma)_{\sharp}\mu_{0}
and solves the Kantorovich’s problem (
5
) uniquely.
Here,
\mu_{0}
is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue; and, in turn

solves the Monge’s path dependent problem for the auxilary cost,
c_{\textbf{e}}(x,y)
, (
9
). For all

in the support of
\mu_{0}
, the minimal path is of the form
t\mapsto\gamma_{x,y}(t)
, just like in the content of Theorem
1.5
. And thus for
T(x)
the optimal map pushing
\mu_{0}
forward to
\mu_{1}
with respect to
c_{e}
, then the optimal map of paths is given by the composition
\Gamma(x,t):=\gamma_{x,T(x)}(t)
.

We also establish the dual problem in view of these settings to characterize the minimizers of the optimal path problem.

2.
Minimal paths

In
[
22
]
, Villani explains a construction in optimal transport that an
action
,
\mathcal{A}
, which measures the cost of displacing along a continuous path
\gamma
, defined on a time interval, is used to consider a cost,
c
, by minimizing the action among paths that go from the initial point of the path,
x
, to the final point of the path,
y
,

\displaystyle c(x,y)=\inf\{\mathcal{A}(\gamma):\gamma(0)=x,\;\gamma(1)=y,\;\gamma\in C([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{n})\}.

Similarly, we use this classical construction to define the endpoint cost function (
9
). A typical and classical example of such an action is the kinetic energy,
\mathcal{A}(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{1}|\dot{\gamma}(t)|^{2}/2dt
. More generally, an action is given by Lagrangians as Villani details in
[
22
]
. And following this line of thinking heavily, we begin our study of our cost action functional.
We cover some results pertaining a Lagrangian
L(\dot{\gamma},\gamma,t)
and its action functional, which is given by the time integral of
L(\dot{\gamma},\gamma,t)
along the path, which defines a cost function
c(\gamma):=\int_{0}^{1}L(\dot{\gamma},\gamma,t)\;dt
, just like in Villani’s general example of
\mathcal{A}(\gamma)
. The action functional will be interpreted as the cost of that path. We next consider a Lagrangian with a given potential
V(x,t)
. All these preliminary propositions will be used in Section
3
and Section
4
.

The following elementary proposition will be useful in what follows. It indicates how close a path is to a linear path.

Proposition 2.1
.

Suppose
\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
C^{2}
and
\delta>0
is such that

\displaystyle|\ddot{\gamma}(t)|\leq\delta\quad\forall t\in[0,1].

Then for every
t\in[0,1]
we have

\displaystyle|\gamma(t)-(\gamma(0)+t[\gamma(1)-\gamma(0)])|\leq\sqrt{n}\delta\quad\text{and}\quad|\dot{\gamma}(t)-(\gamma(1)-\gamma(0))|\leq\sqrt{n}\delta.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{e}(t):=\gamma(t)-(\gamma(0)+t(\gamma(1)-\gamma(0)))
. Then
\mathbf{e}(0)=\mathbf{e}(1)=\mathbf{0}
, and
\mathbf{e}(t)
is continuously differentiable on
[0,1]
. Furthermore,
\ddot{\mathbf{e}}(t)=\ddot{\gamma}(t)
and
|\ddot{\mathbf{e}}(t)|\leq\delta
.
Therefore for any
0\leq t_{1}<t_{2}\leq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{e}}(t_{2})-\dot{\mathbf{e}}(t_{1})=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\ddot{\mathbf{e}}(t)dt.

So
|\dot{\mathbf{e}}(t_{2})-\dot{\mathbf{e}}(t_{1})|\leq\delta
for all
0\leq t_{1}<t_{2}\leq 1
.

In coordinate components of
\mathbf{e}(t)
, we will show that for each
i=1,\ldots,n
there is some
t_{i}\in[0,1]
such that
\dot{\mathbf{e}_{i}}(t_{i})=0
. Thus, in this case,
|\dot{\mathbf{e}_{i}}(t)|\leq\delta
for all

Then

\displaystyle|\dot{\mathbf{e}}(t)|\leq\sqrt{\delta^{2}+\cdots+\delta^{2}}=\sqrt{n}\delta.

According to the mean value theorem, applied to
\mathbf{e}_{i}(t_{i})
, there exists

such that

\displaystyle 0=\mathbf{e}_{i}(1)-\mathbf{e}_{i}(0)=\dot{\mathbf{e}}_{i}(\overline{t}_{i}).

Therefore for all

\mathbf{e}(t)=\mathbf{e}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\dot{\mathbf{e}}(s)ds=\int_{0}^{t}\dot{\mathbf{e}}(s)ds
. Then
|\mathbf{e}(t)|\leq\sqrt{n}\delta
and the proposition follows at once.

∎

We will focus on Lagrangians of the form

\displaystyle L(\dot{\gamma}(t),\gamma(t),t):=\frac{1}{2}|\dot{\gamma}(t)|^{2}-V(\gamma(t),t),

where

is a continuously differentiable potential which is bounded from below. Then we consider the cost function

\displaystyle c(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{1}L(\dot{\gamma}(t),\gamma(t),t)\;dt.

We see that this corresponds to the classical Lagrangian

, where

\gamma
at

, with endpoints

and

of

. It is well known that if

, the minimizers of

with endpoints fixed satisfy Newton’s dynamical equation

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}x}{dt^{2}}=-\nabla_{x}V(x,t).

Everything that follows can be done for more general Lagrangians, but we will focus on the Lagrangian above for the sake of concreteness.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}

. Then the minimizers of

and

Proof.

This is just the Euler-Lagrange equation applied to the cost functional, see Evans’

∎

Notice that when

Corollary 2.3

Suppose the Lagrangian is given by

\displaystyle|\gamma(t)-(\gamma(0)+t[\gamma(1)-\gamma(0)])|\leq\sqrt{n}\delta\quad\text{and}\quad|\dot{\gamma}(t)-(\gamma(1)-\gamma(0))|\leq\sqrt{n}\delta.

Proof.

Choose a minimizer

of

∎

Let us show now that for any pair of points

Proposition 2.4
.

Let

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle\ddot{\gamma}(t)

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{x}V(\gamma(t),t),

\displaystyle\gamma(0)

\displaystyle=x,

\displaystyle\gamma(1)

\displaystyle=y.

Then

\displaystyle\omega_{x,y}(t)=(1-t)x+ty+t\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)\nabla V(\omega(s),s)\;ds-\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)\nabla V(\omega(s),s)\;ds.

.
Then we have

\displaystyle|\gamma_{x_{1},y_{1}}(t)-\gamma_{x_{2},y_{2}}(t)|\leq|(1-t)(x_{1}-x_{2})

\displaystyle+t(y_{1}-y_{2})|

\displaystyle+t\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)|\nabla V(\gamma_{x_{1},y_{1}}(s),s)-\nabla V(\gamma_{x_{2},y_{2}}(s),s)|\;ds

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)|\nabla V(\gamma_{x_{1},y_{1}}(s),s)-\nabla V(\gamma_{x_{2},y_{2}}(s),s)|\;ds

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\leq|x_{1}-x_{2}|+|y_{1}-y_{2}|

\displaystyle+L\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)|\gamma_{x_{1},y_{1}}(s)-\gamma_{x_{2},y_{2}}(s)|\;ds

\displaystyle+L\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)|\gamma_{x_{1},y_{1}}(s)-\gamma_{x_{2},y_{2}}(s)|\;ds

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\leq|x_{1}-x_{2}|+|y_{1}-y_{2}|

\displaystyle+L\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)\;ds\sup_{[0,t]}|\gamma_{x_{1},y_{1}}(s)-\gamma_{x_{2},y_{2}}(s)|

\displaystyle+L\sup_{[0,t]}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)\;ds\;\sup_{[0,t]}|\gamma_{x_{1},y_{1}}(s)-\gamma_{x_{2},y_{2}}(s)|.

Noticing that the term with the common factor equals

and after rearranging, the above estimate equals the estimate, since

\displaystyle d(\gamma_{x_{1},y_{1}},\gamma_{x_{2},y_{2}})

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\;d(\gamma_{x_{1},y_{1}},\gamma_{x_{2},y_{2}})\leq\frac{1}{1-L}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|+|y_{1}-y_{2}|),

we are done with the proof of the proposition.

3.
Minimizers and their properties

3.1.
Existence of minimizers

Before showing that minimizers of a linear functional on paths
do
exist, a few helpful results are in order. An important tool in measure theory is compactness of measures. For instance in a compact metric space
X
, if
\{\mu_{k}\}
is a sequence of probability measures bounded above by a constant
C>0
,

\mu_{k}(X)\leq C\quad\text{for all }\quad k,

then one can find a subsequence of measures
\{\mu_{k_{j}}\}
and another nonnegative measure
\mu
such that
\mu_{k_{j}}\rightharpoonup\mu
as
j\to\infty
, i.e.
\int\phi\;d\mu_{k_{j}}\to\int\phi\;d\mu
as
j\to\infty
for all
\phi\in C(X).
A more general result due to Prokhorov, Theorem
3.2
, includes this line of reasoning. First, however, we need the following definition.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
X
be a metric space and let
\{\mu_{k}\}
be a sequence of nonnegative Borel measures in
X
. The sequence is said to be
tight
if for each
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a compact set
X_{\varepsilon}\subset X
such that

\mu_{k}(X\setminus X_{\varepsilon})\leq\varepsilon\quad\text{for all }\quad k.

Prokhorov’s theorem, which can be found in either
[
21
]
or
[
17
]
, is the following.

Theorem 3.2

(Prokhorov’s Theorem)
.

Let
X
be a separable metric space, and let
\{\mu_{k}\}
be a sequence of nonnegative Borel measures. If the sequence
\{\mu_{k}\}
is tight, then there exists a subsequence
\{\mu_{k_{j}}\}
and another nonnegative Borel measure
\mu
such that

\mu_{k_{j}}\rightharpoonup\mu\quad\text{as}\quad j\to\infty.

We need two additional propositions before we prove existence of minimizers. And Condition
1
will help us with the proof of Theorem
1.4
as we will momentarily see.

Proposition 3.3
.

Condition
2
implies Condition
1
.

Proof.

We have
c(\gamma):=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{2}|\dot{\gamma}(t)|^{2}-V(\gamma(t),t)\;dt
is bounded from below and lower semi-continuous. To be more concrete, Proposition
3.4
establishes the lower semi-continuity part. That
c(\gamma)
is bounded from below follows from

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{2}|\dot{\gamma}(t)|^{2}-V(\gamma(t),t)\;dt\geq-K,

as
V
is bounded by
K>0
.
Recall the set
\Omega_{M,N}
from Condition
1
. We will show this set is compact for all
N>0
. To this end, we will show
\Omega_{M,N}
is uniformly bounded and uniformly continuous, and then invoke Arzela-Ascoli. We first show the latter. Given
\varepsilon>0
, fix
0\leq t_{2}<t_{1}\leq 1
. We have to show
|\gamma(t_{1})-\gamma(t_{2})|\leq\varepsilon
. Indeed, up to a factor of
\frac{1}{2}
, we have

\displaystyle|\gamma(t_{1})-\gamma(t_{2})|\leq\int_{t_{2}}^{t_{1}}|\dot{\gamma}(t)|\;dt

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{0}^{1}|\dot{\gamma}(t)|^{2}\;dt\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{t_{2}}^{t_{1}}1\;dt\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{0}^{1}|\dot{\gamma}(t)|^{2}-V(\gamma(t),t)+V(\gamma(t),t)dt\right)^{1/2}\;\left|t_{1}-t_{2}\right|^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{0}^{1}|\dot{\gamma}(t)|^{2}-V(\gamma(t),t)dt+K\right)^{1/2}\;\left|t_{1}-t_{2}\right|^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{N+K}\;|t_{1}-t_{2}|^{1/2}.

This shows
\gamma
has a bounded Hölder seminorm. Now we check
\Omega_{M,N}
is uniformly bounded. This follows from the fact that
|\gamma(0)|\leq M
for every
\gamma\in\Omega_{M,N}
and the modulus of continuity given above.
∎

Proposition 3.4
.

The cost function
c(\gamma)
given by Condition
2
is lower semi-continuous.

Proof.

Assume
\gamma_{n}\to\gamma
in the sup-norm in
[0,1]
, we are going to show
\liminf_{n\to\infty}c(\gamma_{n})\geq c(\gamma).

The cost function

has two terms. The first term satisfies

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{1}|\dot{\gamma_{n}}(t)|^{2}\;dt\geq\int_{0}^{1}|\dot{\gamma}(t)|^{2}\;dt

from
[
6
, Ch8.2.2, Theorem 1]
, as
\gamma
is Lipschitz continuous and
|\dot{\gamma}|^{2}
smooth, convex and bounded below. While the second term has the potential

which is uniformly continuous; this means that given
\varepsilon>0
, there is

such that

\displaystyle\|\gamma_{n}-\gamma\|\leq\delta\implies|V(\gamma_{n},t)-V(\gamma,t)|\leq\varepsilon

in

. In turn this then means that the second term satisfies

∎

.

.

Since

which implies

\displaystyle\pi_{k}(\Omega_{M,N}^{c})\leq\frac{1}{N}\mathcal{E}_{0}(\pi_{k})\leq\frac{C}{N}\quad\text{for all }\quad k

and so

attains its infimum at

∎

c_{\textbf{e}}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}|x-y|^{2}
.

2.
If
c(\gamma):=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{p}|\dot{\gamma}(t)|^{p}\;dt,\;p\geq 1
, in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
c_{\textbf{e}}(x,y)=\frac{1}{p}|x-y|^{p}
.

It is not too difficult to show this. In fact we can find in Villani’s books
[
21
]
,
[
22
]
:

Claim. If
h
is a convex function defined on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
\inf\{\int_{0}^{1}h(\dot{\gamma}(t))\;dt\;:\;\gamma(0)=x,\gamma(1)=y\}=h(y-x)
.

We have
\int_{0}^{1}h(\dot{\gamma}(t))\;dt\geq h\Big(\int_{0}^{1}\dot{\gamma}(t)dt\Big)=h(y-x)
, by Jensen’s inequality
[

]
,
[

.

The Kantorovich problem associated to paths is the problem of minimizing the linear functional
\pi\mapsto\int c(\gamma)\;d\pi
subject to the linear constraints
(e_{0})_{\sharp}\pi=\mu_{0}
,
(e_{1})_{\sharp}\pi=\mu_{1}
, and
\pi\geq 0
. Linear minimization problems with convex constraints of this type admit a natural dual problem, see Solomon’s book
[
19
, Ch 10]
. We now define this dual problem, as is usually done, using Lagrange multipliers.

For any pair
(\phi,\psi)\in L^{1}(d\mu_{0})\times L^{1}(d\mu_{1})
and
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, we define

\displaystyle L(\pi;\phi,\psi):=\int_{\Omega}c(\gamma)\;d\pi(\gamma)

\displaystyle+\int_{X}\phi(x)d\mu_{0}(x)-\int_{\Omega}\phi(\gamma(0))d\pi(\gamma)

\displaystyle+\int_{X}\psi(x)d\mu_{1}(y)-\int_{\Omega}\psi(\gamma(1))d\pi(\gamma)

Then after some rearrangement

\displaystyle L(\pi;\phi,\psi)=\int_{X}\phi(x)d\mu_{0}(x)

\displaystyle+\int_{X}\psi(y)d\mu_{1}(y)

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}(c(\gamma)-(\phi(\gamma(0))+\psi(\gamma(1))))\;d\pi(\gamma).

Minimizing over all
\pi\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega)
, we get:

Proposition 3.5
.

Given any pair of functions
\phi\in L^{1}(\mu_{0})
,
\psi\in L^{1}(\mu_{1})
, we define

\displaystyle D(\phi,\psi):=\inf_{\pi\geq 0}L(\pi;\phi,\psi).

Then we have

\displaystyle D(\phi,\psi)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\int_{X}\phi(x)d\mu_{0}(x)+\int_{X}\psi(y)d\mu_{1}(y)&\textnormal{ if }\phi(x)+\psi(y)\leq c_{\textbf{e}}(x,y)\quad\forall x,y\\
-\infty&\textnormal{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

Proof.

Since the first two terms in
L(\pi;\phi,\psi)
are independent of
\pi
,

\displaystyle D(\phi,\psi)

\displaystyle=\int_{X}\phi(x)d\mu_{0}(x)+\int_{X}\psi(y)d\mu_{1}(y)+\inf_{\pi}\int_{\Omega}(c(\gamma)-(\phi(\gamma(0))+\psi(\gamma(1))))\;d\pi(\gamma).

If there exists some
\gamma_{0}
for which
c(\gamma_{0})-\phi(\gamma_{0}(0))-\psi(\gamma_{0}(1))<0
, then we can make the minimum
-\infty
. Just take a Dirac mass at
\gamma_{0}
with very large mass

\displaystyle\pi=\lambda\delta_{\gamma_{0}}\quad\text{for}\quad\lambda>0;

letting
\lambda\to+\infty
,
D(\phi,\psi)=-\infty
. Else, for all
\gamma\in\Omega
,
c(\gamma)-\phi(\gamma(0))-\psi(\gamma(1))\geq 0
, therefore the third term is
\geq 0
for all
\pi
, and taking
\pi=0
we get the inf equal to zero,
D(\phi,\psi)=\int_{X}\phi\;d\mu_{1}+\int_{X}\psi\;d\mu_{1}
.
∎

Thus we arrive at the dual problem:

Definition 3.6
.

(Dual problem.) Let
(\phi,\psi)\in L^{1}(\mu_{0})\times L^{1}(\mu_{1})
. Let

\displaystyle\Pi^{*}:=\{\phi,\psi:\phi(x)+\psi(y)\leq c_{\textbf{e}}(x,y)\}

The dual problem consists of finding the following supremum

\displaystyle\sup\left\{\int_{X}\phi(x)d\mu_{0}(x)+\int_{X}\psi(y)d\mu_{1}(y):(\phi,\psi)\in\Pi^{*}\right\}.

The latter is reminiscent of a dual problem coming from the standard theory of optimal transportation
[
1
, Section 1.3]
. The following is the main fact about the dual problem, we recall
X
is the closure of bounded, open, and connected set and we recall the function
c_{\textbf{e}}(x,y)
defined by (
9
).

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
c:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be lower semi-continuous. Let
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(X)
and
\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(X)
. Assume that
c_{\textbf{e}}(x,y)\leq f(x)+g(y)
for some
f\in L^{1}(d\mu_{0}),g\in L^{1}(d\mu_{1})
, then

(13)

\displaystyle\inf_{\pi\in\Pi_{\text{path}}}\left\{\int_{\Omega}c(\gamma)\;d\pi(\gamma)\right\}=\sup_{(\phi,\psi)\in\Pi^{*}}\left\{\int_{X}\phi(x)\;d\mu_{0}(x)+\int_{X}\psi(y)d\mu_{1}(y)\right\}

The proof of this theorem will be given in the end of Section
3.3
. For now we go over some important consequences of Theorem
3.7
.

Corollary 3.8
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3.7
, if
\pi_{0}\in\Pi_{\text{path}}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
achieves the infimum, then for
\pi_{0}
-a.e.
\gamma\in\Omega
we have
c(\gamma)=c_{\textbf{e}}(\gamma(0),\gamma(1))
.

Proof.

Let
(\phi_{0},\psi_{0})\in\Pi^{*}
be optimal. Then by Theorem
3.7
we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}c(\gamma)\;d\pi_{0}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\int_{X}\phi_{0}(x)\;d\mu_{0}(x)+\int_{X}\psi_{0}(y)\;d\mu_{1}(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(\phi_{0}(\gamma(0))+\psi_{0}(\gamma(1)))\;d\pi_{0}(\gamma).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(c(\gamma)-(\phi_{0}(\gamma(0))-\psi_{0}(\gamma(1))))\;d\pi_{0}(\gamma)=0.

Since the integrand is
\geq 0
, not just
\pi_{0}
-a.e. it follows that
c(\gamma)=\phi_{0}(\gamma(0))+\psi_{0}(\gamma(1))

\pi_{0}
-a.e.
\gamma
. Since
\phi_{0}(x)+\psi_{0}(y)\leq c_{\textbf{e}}(x,y)
,
c(\gamma)=\inf\{c(\gamma):\;\gamma(0)=x,\gamma(1)=y\}
for
\pi_{0}
-a.e.
\gamma
.
∎

Corollary

indicates that whenever

Lemma 3.11

Let

\gamma_{0}\in\text{spt}(\pi)

Proof.

Suppose
\gamma_{0}\in\text{spt}\;(\pi)

\pi\in\Pi_{\textit{path}}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})

\gamma^{\prime}
be the minimal path from
x_{0}:=\gamma^{\prime}(0)
to
y_{0}:=\gamma^{\prime}(1)
, arguing by contradiction, if
\gamma_{0}
is not minimal, there is
\varepsilon>0
such that

\displaystyle c(\gamma^{\prime})<c(\gamma_{0})-\varepsilon/2.

Let
W:=B_{r}(x_{0})\times B_{r}(y_{0})

(to be specified later),
(e_{0},e_{1}):\Omega\to X\times X

\displaystyle T:=(e_{0},e_{1})^{-1}\left(W\right).

This is an open set in
\Omega
containing the path
\gamma_{0}
.
Consider the measure,
\bar{\pi}:=\pi\llcorner T/\pi\left[T\right]
;
\pi\left[T\right]
will be positive as
\gamma_{0}\in\text{spt}\;(\pi).
Take
0<\epsilon_{0}<\pi\left[T\right]
. Define the measures

\displaystyle\nu_{x}:=(e_{0})_{\sharp}\bar{\pi}\quad\text{and}\quad\nu_{y}:=(e_{1})_{\sharp}\bar{\pi}.

Now build a measure
\widetilde{\pi}\in\Pi(\nu_{x},\nu_{y})
as follows. Let
g:X\times X\to\Omega
be defined by

\displaystyle g_{t}(x,y)=\gamma_{x,y}(t)

such that
\gamma_{x,y}

is the minimal path from
x
to
y
. Then set

and define

\displaystyle\pi^{\prime}:=\pi-\epsilon_{0}\bar{\pi}+\epsilon_{0}\widetilde{\pi}.

That

being positive, which is thanks to

. The marginals of

share the same marginals of
\pi
. Concretely,

\displaystyle(e_{0})_{\sharp}\pi^{\prime}=\mu_{0}-\epsilon_{0}\nu_{x}+\epsilon_{0}(e_{0})_{\sharp}\widetilde{\pi},

and for every Borel subset

Finally, we will show

, thereby contradicting the optimality of
\pi
. Let

be defined as the minimal
\gamma
between

. That the map

between
x
and

\displaystyle c\circ m(\gamma)=c(\gamma_{\text{min}})\leq c(\gamma).

Fix
\delta>0
such that if
\gamma\in T
(

\displaystyle\|\gamma_{\text{min}}-\gamma^{\prime}\|_{\infty}<\delta\;\;(\mbox{if }r<r(\delta):=\delta/2).

The lower semi-continuity of

. On the other hand, for all

, the continuity of

\displaystyle c(\gamma)\leq c(\gamma^{\prime})+\varepsilon/4.

Putting this all together yields,

\displaystyle\int c(\gamma)d\pi(\gamma)-\int c(\gamma)d\pi^{\prime}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{0}\int c(\gamma)\;d\bar{\pi}(\gamma)-\epsilon_{0}\int c(\gamma)\;d\widetilde{\pi}(\gamma)

\displaystyle\geq\epsilon_{0}\int c(\gamma_{\text{min}})\;d\overline{\pi}(\gamma)-\epsilon_{0}\int c(\gamma)\;d\widetilde{\pi}(\gamma)

\displaystyle\geq\epsilon_{0}\int\left(c(\gamma^{\prime})+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)\;d\overline{\pi}(\gamma)-\epsilon_{0}\int\left(c(\gamma^{\prime})+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)\;d\widetilde{\pi}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{0}\;\frac{\varepsilon}{4}>0.

By definition

. Furthermore, we used the fact that both

and

are supported on

Lemma 3.12
.

Suppose
c:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuous cost function and
\pi\in\Pi_{\text{path}}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
optimal with respect to

. Let
\check{\pi}:=(e_{0},e_{1})_{\sharp}\pi\in\Pi(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})

, spt
\;(\check{\pi})
, is

-cyclically monotone. Moreover,

Proof.

We follow a classical proof from standard optimal transportation, in particular see the presentation in Santambrogio’s book

17

Let
\check{\pi}:=(e_{0},e_{1})_{\sharp}\pi

-cyclically monotone. Then there exist

,

from

to

, and
\widetilde{\gamma}_{i}

\displaystyle\varepsilon<\frac{1}{2n}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{e}(x_{i},y_{i})-c_{e}(x_{i},y_{\tau(i)})\right).

\displaystyle T_{i}:=(e_{0},e_{1})^{-1}\left(B_{r}(x_{i})\times B_{r}(y_{i})\right)\cap\Omega_{\text{min}}

.

Now define the measures

\displaystyle\pi_{i}:=\pi\llcorner T_{i}/\pi\left[T_{i}\right],\quad\nu_{x,i}:=(e_{0})_{\sharp}\pi_{i},\quad\text{and}\quad\nu_{y,i}:=(e_{1})_{\sharp}\pi_{i},

and note that
\pi\left[T_{i}\right]
will be positive for each

\gamma_{i}
is contained in the support of
\pi
. Equivalently
(x_{i},y_{i})\in\text{spt}\;(\check{\pi})
. Take

.

Construct a measure
\widetilde{\pi}_{i}\in\Pi\left(\nu_{x,i},\nu_{y,\tau(i)}\right)
, for every

, in the following way. Let
g
be a map

\displaystyle g:X\times X\to\Omega\quad\text{defined by}\quad(x,y)\mapsto\gamma_{x,y},

that is,

\gamma_{x,y}(0)=x
and
\gamma_{x,y}(1)=y
. Then the estimates

for all

and

\widetilde{\pi}_{i}:=(g)_{\sharp}\left(\nu_{x,i}\otimes\nu_{y,\tau(i)}\right)

That

\displaystyle(e_{0})_{\sharp}\widetilde{\pi}=\mu_{0}-\epsilon_{0}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nu_{x,i}+\epsilon_{0}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(e_{0})_{\sharp}(g)_{\sharp}\left(\nu_{x,i}\otimes\nu_{y,\tau(i)}\right),

containing the points over the entire second copy of

.

is positive, thereby contradicting the optimality of

|\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi_{m})-\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi_{k})|<\varepsilon/2\quad\text{for all }\quad m,k\geq K.

The pointwise convergence of
\{\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi_{k})\}
gives that
for any
\varepsilon>0
, we can find an integer
K^{\prime}>0
such that

|\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi_{k})-\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi)|<\varepsilon/2\quad\text{for all }\quad k\geq K^{\prime}.

Then taking
K^{\prime\prime}:=\max(K,K^{\prime})
,

|\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi_{k_{j}})-\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi)|\leq|\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi_{k_{j}})-\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi_{m})|+|\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi_{m})-\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi)|<\varepsilon\quad\text{for all}\;j,m\geq K^{\prime\prime},

the desired contradiction. Therefore
\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi_{k_{j}})

converges uniformly to the continuous
\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi)
.
Relabeling
\{\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi_{k_{j}})\}
to
\{\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi_{k})\}
, subsequently
\{\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi_{k})\}
converges
uniformly to
\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi)
for all sufficiently large
k.

This means that for any given
\varepsilon>0
, we can find an integer

N>0
, not depending on
\gamma
, with
N>3/\varepsilon
such that

\sup_{\gamma\in X_{n}}|\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi_{k})-\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi)|\leq\frac{1}{k}\leq\frac{1}{N}<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}\quad\text{for all}\quad k\geq N\quad\text{sufficiently large}.

So that then for each fixed
n
,

\displaystyle I_{0,k}\leq\sup_{\gamma\in X_{n}}|\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi_{k})-\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{3}\quad\text{for all}\quad k\quad\text{sufficiently large.}

Subsequently, the uniform convergence—hence strong convergence—allows us to conclude
I_{0,k}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
.

Let us now turn to
I_{1,k}
. From each compact
\Omega_{M,N}
arising from the
Condition
1
with
C/N>0
, let
\varepsilon>0
be given
such that
C/N=:\varepsilon/3
. Then quite simply for each fixed

N>0
,

\displaystyle I_{1,k}\leq\pi(\Omega\setminus\Omega_{M,N})\sup_{\gamma\in\Omega\setminus\Omega_{M,N}}|\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi_{k})-\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{N}\Big(\sup_{\gamma\in\Omega\setminus\Omega_{M,N}}|\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi_{k})|+\sup_{\gamma\in\Omega\setminus\Omega_{M,N}}|\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi)|\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 2\frac{\varepsilon}{3}\quad\text{for all }\quad k.

Putting all this together in (
25
) and hence in (
23
) yields

I_{k}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{3}+2\frac{\varepsilon}{3}=\varepsilon,\quad\text{for all }\quad k\quad\text{sufficiently large}.

The lemma is now proved.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

Note that the above claim held true for general
\mathcal{K}
having a
b
modulus of continuity. But for the more specific example, if we define
\mathcal{K}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(\gamma,\sigma):=\theta\int_{0}^{1}e^{-\beta|\gamma(t)-\sigma(t)|^{2}}\;dt,

where
\beta>0
and
\theta>0
, the claim also holds true. In fact, since
e^{-|x|^{2}}
is Lipschitz,

\displaystyle\Big|e^{-|x|^{2}}-e^{-|y|^{2}}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq C|x-y|.

Interchanging the values of
x
and
y
we achieve the Lipschitz estimate. Thus,
\exp\{-\beta|\sigma(t)-\gamma(t)|^{2}\}
is Lipschitz. Then
\mathcal{K}(\gamma,\sigma):=\int_{0}^{1}e^{-\beta|\gamma(t)-\sigma(t)|^{2}}dt
is uniformly Lipschitz in each coordinate,
|\mathcal{K}(\gamma_{1},\sigma)-\mathcal{K}(\gamma_{2},\sigma)|\leq C\|\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}\|
. Then

\displaystyle|\mathcal{U}(\gamma_{1},\pi)-\mathcal{U}(\gamma_{2},\pi)|

\displaystyle=\Big|\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{K}(\gamma_{1},\sigma)d\pi(\sigma)-\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{K}(\gamma_{2},\sigma)d\pi(\sigma)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\Big|\mathcal{K}(\gamma_{1},\sigma)-\mathcal{K}(\gamma_{2},\sigma)\Big|d\pi(\sigma)

\displaystyle\leq C\|\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}\|.

4.2.
The dual problem

For the rest of the paper we only consider the interactions given by
\mathcal{K}(\gamma,\sigma)=\theta\int_{0}^{1}\exp\{-\beta|\gamma(t)-\sigma(t)|^{2}\}\;dt
, for some
\theta,\beta>0
. In order for the results here to be as “smooth” as possible, we will make a small notational change to the functional
\mathcal{E}(\pi)
, (
7
). Namely, without losing generality,

(26)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\pi):=\int_{\Omega}c(\gamma)d\pi(\gamma)+\int_{\Omega}2\;\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi)d\pi(\gamma).

The difference is the factor of
2
in front of
\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi)
.

We start with a heuristic discussion of the dual problem and Lagrange multipliers (we make this more rigorous in the next section). Just like in Section
3.2
we wish to minimize a functional subject to linear constraints. The novelty here is we have a nonlinear functional. Let us elaborate on this, the interaction term
\int_{\Omega}\Big(2\;\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{K}(\gamma,\sigma)\;d\pi(\sigma)\Big)\;d\pi(\gamma)
is actually
quadratic
with respect to
\pi
. We venture into what we did in the beginning of Section
3.2
to produce the required constraint of the optimal path Kantorovich duality with interaction. For the moment let us abandon rigor and see where this takes us—it will take us to the correct dual problem when we look at interaction terms.

For
(\phi,\psi)\in C_{c}^{0}(X)\times C_{c}^{0}(X)
and
\pi\in\mathcal{M}^{+}(\Omega)
, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\pi;\phi,\psi):=\int_{\Omega}c(\gamma)d\pi(\gamma)+\int_{\Omega}2\;\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi)d\pi(\gamma)

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\phi(\gamma(0))d\pi(\gamma)+\int_{X}\phi(x)d\mu_{0}(x)

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\psi(\gamma(1))d\pi(\gamma)+\int_{X}\psi(y)d\mu_{1}(y).

After rearranging

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\pi;\phi,\psi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\textbf{\Big(}c(\gamma)+2\;\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi)-(\phi(\gamma(0))+\psi(\gamma(1)))\textbf{\Big)}\;d\pi(\gamma)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+\int_{X}\phi(x)d\mu_{0}(x)+\int_{X}\psi(y)d\mu_{1}(y)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Q}(\pi;\phi,\psi)+\int_{X}\phi(x)d\mu_{0}(x)+\int_{X}\psi(y)d\mu_{1}(y),

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(\pi;\phi,\psi):=\int_{\Omega}c(\gamma)+2\;\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi)-\left(\phi(\gamma(0))+\psi(\gamma(1))\right)\;d\pi(\gamma).

Notice that
\mathcal{Q}(\pi;\phi,\psi)
contains the quadratic term that we talked about in the prequel. Then the dual function is

(27)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\phi,\psi):=\inf_{\pi\geq 0}\left\{\mathcal{Q}(\pi;\phi,\psi)+\int_{X}\phi(x)\;d\mu_{0}(x)+\int_{X}\psi(y)\;d\mu_{1}(y)\right\}.

The business at hand is to minimize the quadratic term
\mathcal{Q}(\pi;\phi,\psi)
with respect to
\pi
. But how can one minimize such quadratic term in
\pi
? This can be answered if we recognize this as minimizing a quadratic functional in infinite dimensions and compare to the more tangible problem in finite dimension.

The minimization of
\mathcal{Q}(\pi;\phi,\psi)
has the form:
\min_{p\geq 0}q(p)
for

(28)

\displaystyle q(p):=\langle b,p\rangle+\langle Ap,p\rangle,

where
\langle\;,\;\rangle
is the Euclidean inner product,
b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is positive, and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
a positive semi-definite matrix. The term
\langle Ap,p\rangle
can be interpreted as the quadratic term in (
27
), that is, the term
\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi)d\pi(\gamma)
. Futher,
p
represents
\pi
, while
Ap
represents
2\;\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi)
, and
b
represents
c(\gamma)-\phi(\gamma(0))-\psi(\gamma(1))
. Then

\langle b,p\rangle:=\Big\langle c(\gamma)-(\phi(\gamma(0))+\psi(\gamma(1))),\pi\Big\rangle=\int_{\Omega}\textbf{(}c(\gamma)-(\;\phi(\gamma(0))+\psi(\gamma(1))\;)\textbf{)}\;d\pi(\gamma);

\langle Ap,p\rangle:=\Big\langle 2\;\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi),\pi\Big\rangle=\int_{\Omega}2\textbf{\Big(}\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi)\textbf{\Big)}\;d\pi(\gamma).

Thanks to Fubini and Tonelli the inner products above make (kind of) sense and their sum equal
\mathcal{Q}(\pi;\phi,\psi)
. Then to minimize
\mathcal{Q}(\pi;\phi,\psi)
with respect to
\pi\geq 0
in
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, it suffices to minimize (
28
) with respect to
p\geq 0
. Take a derivative of
q(p)
with respect to
p
, set it equal to zero, and solve for minimum. To be cautious we will use vector notation. Take a gradient with respect to
p

\nabla_{p}q(p)=b+2Ap.

We have the constraint
p\geq 0
, so at the minimizer
p_{\text{min}}
we have, by the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions
[
19
, Ch 10.2.2]
,

\displaystyle\nabla_{p}q(p)

\displaystyle\geq 0\textnormal{ (coordinate wise)},

\displaystyle\nabla_{p_{i}}q(p)

\displaystyle=0\textnormal{ if }p_{i}>0.

Let us interpret this in our infinite dimensional problem, here
\pi_{\text{min}}
corresponds to the
p_{\text{min}}
, and we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi_{\text{min}})

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{1}{2}(c(\gamma)-(\phi(\gamma(0))+\psi(\gamma(1))),\;\forall\;\gamma\in\Omega

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi_{\text{min}})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}(c(\gamma)-(\phi(\gamma(0))+\psi(\gamma(1))),\;\forall\;\gamma\in\text{spt}(\pi_{\textnormal{min}}).

Rearranging, this can be written as

(29)

\displaystyle\phi(\gamma(0))+\psi(\gamma(1))

\displaystyle\leq c(\gamma)+2\;\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi_{\text{min}}),\;\forall\;\gamma\in\Omega,

(30)

\displaystyle\phi(\gamma(0))+\psi(\gamma(1))

\displaystyle=c(\gamma)+2\;\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi_{\text{min}}),\;\forall\;\gamma\in\text{spt}(\pi_{\text{min}}).

Thus, we have a good guess about the condition that characterizes the optimal
\pi
in (
27
). More concretely, and mimicking what we have already done in Section
3.2
, if there exists a path
\gamma
for which
c(\gamma)+2\;\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi)-(\phi(\gamma(0))+\psi(\gamma(1)))<0
, then we can make
\min_{\pi\geq 0}\mathcal{Q}(\pi;\phi,\psi)=-\infty
and in turn make
\mathcal{D}(\phi,\psi)=-\infty
. Otherwise, for all
\sigma\in\Omega
, and keeping
\gamma
fixed (remembering the proof of Proposition
3.5
),
\phi(\gamma(0))+\psi(\gamma(1))\leq c(\gamma)+2\;\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi)

\pi
-a.e.

Note that for any
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, we may consider the auxiliary dual problem,

(31)

\displaystyle D_{\pi}(\phi,\psi)=\sup\left\{\int_{X}\phi(x)\;d\mu_{0}(x)+\int_{X}\psi(y)\;d\mu_{1}(y):\phi(\gamma(0))+\psi(\gamma(1))\leq c(\gamma)+2\;\mathcal{U}(\gamma;\pi)\right\}

For the moment let us assume that Theorem
3.7
’s equality holds for (
26
) having constraint (
29
). Suppose the following holds:

(32)

\displaystyle\inf\left\{\mathcal{E}(\pi):\;\pi\in\Pi_{\text{path}}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})\right\}=\sup\left\{\mathcal{D}(\phi,\psi)\right\}

Suppose
(\phi_{0},\psi_{0})
maximizes (
31
) and
\pi_{0}
is optimal for (
26
). Then, thanks to (
29
-30)

c(\gamma)+2\;\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi_{0})=\phi_{0}(\gamma(0))+\psi_{0}(\gamma(1))
. Furthermore, strong duality (
32
) ensures

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left[c(\gamma)+2\;\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi_{0})\right]d\pi_{0}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\int_{X}\phi_{0}(x)d\mu_{0}(x)+\int_{X}\psi_{0}(y)\;d\mu_{1}(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\textbf{[}\phi_{0}(\gamma(0))+\psi_{0}(\gamma(1))\textbf{]}d\pi_{0}(\gamma).

Equivalently

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\textbf{[}c(\gamma)+2\;\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi_{0})-(\phi_{0}(\gamma(0))+\psi_{0}(\gamma(1)))\textbf{]}d\pi_{0}(\gamma)=0.

The integrand is nonnegative by (
29
), so it has to vanish
\pi_{0}
-a.e.
Moreover, if we further stipulate something very similar like the endpoint cost function (
9
) to
c(\gamma)+2\;\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi_{0})
, say (
35
) for which the constraint
\phi_{0}(x)+\psi_{0}(y)\leq\inf_{\gamma(0)=x,\gamma(1)=y}\;c(\gamma)+2\;\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi_{0})
holds
\pi_{0}
-a.e., then we would have

\displaystyle c(\gamma)+2\;\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi_{0})

\displaystyle=\phi_{0}(\gamma(0))+\psi_{0}(\gamma(1))

\displaystyle=\phi_{0}(x)+\psi_{0}(y)

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{\gamma(0)=x,\;\gamma(1)=y}\textbf{[}c(\gamma)+2\;\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi_{0})\textbf{]},

which in turn would imply
c(\gamma)+2\;\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi_{0})=\inf_{\gamma(0)=x,\;\gamma(1)=x}\textbf{[}c(\gamma)+2\;\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi_{0})\textbf{]}
.

With this in mind, we state the interaction analogue of Theorem
3.7
, i.e., we establish (
32
), but prove it in the end of Section
4.3
after some preliminary discussion and a bit of mathematical machinery coming from Definition
4.5
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Strong duality holds. Namely,

\displaystyle\inf_{\pi\in\Pi_{\text{path}}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})}\int_{\Omega}\Big(c(\gamma)+2\;\mathcal{U}(\gamma;\pi)\Big)d\pi(\gamma)=\sup_{\phi,\psi}\mathcal{D}(\phi,\psi).

4.3.
Supergradient of a concave function

We give a rigorous account to our discussion in Section
4.2
. The background material needed in this section comes from Rockafellar
[
16
, §23]
. Namely, we use the fact that
x
is in the maximization set of
f
—a concave function—if and only if
0
is in the supergradient of
f
.

From (
27
), let
q(\phi,\psi):=\inf_{\pi\geq 0}\mathcal{Q}(\pi;\phi,\psi)
. So that for all
\phi,\psi
there is a certain
\pi_{*}\geq 0
such that,

(33)

\displaystyle q(\phi,\psi)=\mathcal{Q}(\pi_{*};\phi,\psi)\leq\mathcal{Q}(\pi;\phi,\psi),\quad\forall\pi\geq 0.

In this case, from equation (
27
),

\mathcal{D}(\phi,\psi):=\inf_{\pi\geq 0}\mathcal{L}(\pi;\phi,\psi)=\inf_{\pi\geq 0}\mathcal{Q}(\pi;\phi,\psi)+\mathcal{J}(\phi,\psi)=q(\phi,\psi)+\mathcal{J}(\phi,\psi),

where
\mathcal{J}(\phi,\psi)=\int\phi\;d\mu_{0}+\int\psi\;d\mu_{1}
.
It is immediate to see that
\mathcal{J}(\phi,\psi)
is linear in
\phi,\psi
. For each fixed
\pi\geq 0
,
\mathcal{Q}(\pi;\phi,\psi)
is linear in
\phi,\psi
and so
q(\phi,\psi)
is a minimum of a family of linear functionals. In other words, for all
(\phi_{0},\psi_{0})
we can find
\pi_{0}
such that
q(\phi_{0},\psi_{0})=\mathcal{Q}(\pi_{0};\phi_{0},\psi_{0})
and
q(\phi,\psi)\leq\mathcal{Q}(\pi;\phi,\psi)
. Thus,
q
is concave in
\phi,\psi
. The superdifferentiability analogue of
\mathcal{D}(\phi,\psi)
is given—just as in
[
16
, §23]
.

Remark 4.5
.

The
superdifferential
of
\mathcal{D}
is made of functions of the form

\displaystyle\mathfrak{l}_{\pi}(\phi,\psi):=-\int_{\Omega}[\phi(\gamma(0))+\psi(\gamma(1))]\;d\pi(\gamma)+\int_{X}\phi(x)d\mu_{0}(x)+\int_{X}\psi(y)d\mu_{1}(y),

for some
\pi\geq 0
. In particular,
\mathfrak{l}_{\pi}
is in
\partial\mathcal{D}(\phi_{0},\psi_{0})

if

\mathcal{D}(\phi,\psi)\leq\mathcal{D}(\phi_{0},\psi_{0})+\mathfrak{l}_{\pi}(\phi-\phi_{0},\psi-\psi_{0})\quad\forall(\phi,\psi).

Notice the pair
(\phi_{0},\psi_{0})
is a maximum of
\mathcal{D}
if and only if the
0
functional is in
\partial\mathcal{D}(\phi_{0},\psi_{0})
, i.e., if there is a
\pi
such that
\mathfrak{l}_{\pi}(\phi,\psi)=0
for all
(\phi,\psi).

This aligns perfectly well with Rockafellar’s statement
[
16
]
:
\pi
—having the form
\mathfrak{l}_{\pi}
—is in the maximization set of
\mathcal{D}(\phi,\psi)
for all
(\phi,\psi)
if and only if
0
is in the supergradient of
\mathcal{D}
. That is, if and only if
\mathfrak{l}_{\pi}
is identically zero.

A consequence of this indicates that if there exists
\pi\geq 0
such that for all
\phi,\psi

\displaystyle 0=\mathfrak{l}_{\pi}=-\int_{\Omega}[\phi(\gamma(0))+\psi(\gamma(1))]d\pi(\gamma)+\int_{X}\phi(x)d\mu_{0}(x)+\int_{X}\psi(y)d\mu_{1}(y),

then,
(e_{0})_{\sharp}\pi=\mu_{0}
and
(e_{1})_{\sharp}\pi=\mu_{1}
and thusly
\pi
is admissible.

Armed with this discussion, we are ready to prove Theorem
4.4
.

Proof of Theorem
4.4
.

Let
(\phi_{*},\psi_{*})
be the maximum of
\mathcal{D}(\phi,\psi)
. In view of the above discussion (and Remark
4.5
), there exists
\pi_{*}\geq 0
such that
\mathfrak{l}_{\pi_{*}}\in\partial\mathcal{D}(\phi_{*},\psi_{*})
and
\mathfrak{l}_{\pi_{*}}=0
. Equivalently,
q(\phi_{*},\psi_{*})=\mathcal{Q}(\pi_{*};\phi_{*},\psi_{*})
. In particular,
\pi_{*}\in\Pi_{\text{path}}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
. Then, for all
(\phi,\psi)
and all admissible
\pi
, and applying the inequality (
33
) we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}c(\gamma)+2\;\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi_{*})\;d\pi_{*}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}(\pi_{*},\phi_{*},\psi_{*})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Q}(\pi_{*};\phi_{*},\psi_{*})+\int_{X}\phi_{*}(x)d\mu_{0}(x)+\int_{X}\psi_{*}(y)d\mu_{1}(y)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{Q}(\pi;\phi_{*},\psi_{*})+\int_{X}\phi_{*}(x)d\mu_{0}(x)+\int_{X}\psi_{*}(y)d\mu_{1}(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}c(\gamma)+2\;\mathcal{U}(\gamma,\pi)\;d\pi(\gamma)\quad\forall\;\pi\quad\text{admissible}

The latter inequality indicates
\pi_{*}
minimizes
\int(c(\gamma)+2\;\mathcal{U}(\gamma;\pi))d\pi
. So since
\pi_{*}
is optimal, then

\displaystyle\phi_{*}(\gamma(0))+\psi_{*}(\gamma(1))

\displaystyle=c(\gamma)+2\;\mathcal{U}(\gamma;\pi_{*}),\quad\text{in the support of}\;\pi_{*}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\int_{X}\phi_{*}(x)\;d\mu_{0}(x)+\int_{X}\psi_{*}(y)\;d\mu_{1}(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(\phi_{*}(\gamma(0))+\psi_{*}(\gamma(1)))\;d\pi_{*}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(c(\gamma)+2\;\mathcal{U}(\gamma;\pi_{*}))\;d\pi_{*}(\gamma)

\pi_{*}
-a.e. But
(\phi_{*},\psi_{*})
solves the maximization (
27
); thus

\displaystyle\sup\mathcal{D}(\phi,\psi)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi_{\text{path}}}\mathcal{E}(\pi).

∎

Remark 4.6
.

The above result says that any solution
(\phi_{*}.\psi_{*})
to the dual problem produces a solution
\pi_{*}
to the primal problem.

A consequence of strong duality is a characterization of optimizers using the auxiliary dual problem. This is the content of the next result.

Theorem 4.7
.

If
\pi_{0}
solves the primal problem, then
(\phi_{0},\psi_{0})
solves the auxiliary dual problem.

Proof.

First of all using the auxiliary dual problem (
31
),
D_{\pi}(\phi,\psi)\leq\mathcal{E}(\pi)
for all
(\phi,\psi)
; for all admissible
\pi
. For the reverse inequality, we proceed as follows. Let
\pi_{0}
be some minimizer for the primal problem. Define
(\phi_{0},\psi_{0})
maximizing (
31
). From the previous Section
4.2
, any pair
(\phi_{0},\psi_{0})
that achieves the supremum are such that

(34)

\displaystyle\phi_{0}(\gamma(0))+\psi_{0}(\gamma(1))=c(\gamma)+2\;\mathcal{U}(\gamma;\pi_{0})\quad\forall\gamma\in\text{spt}(\pi_{0}).

This implies, as
\pi_{0}\in\Pi_{\text{path}}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
,

\displaystyle D_{\pi}(\phi_{0},\psi_{0})

\displaystyle=\int_{X}\phi_{0}(x)\;d\mu_{0}(x)+\int_{X}\psi_{0}(y)\;d\mu_{1}(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(\phi_{0}(\gamma(0))+\psi_{0}(\gamma(1)))\;d\pi_{0}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(c(\gamma)+2\;\mathcal{U}(\gamma;\pi_{0}))\;d\pi_{0}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}(\pi_{0}).

The second to last equation follows from (
34
).

Remembering the Lagrangian multipliers functional from Section
4.2
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\pi_{0};\phi_{0},\psi_{0})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(c(\gamma)+2\;\mathcal{U}(\gamma;\pi_{0})-(\phi_{0}(\gamma(0))+\psi_{0}(\gamma(1)))\;d\pi_{0}(\gamma)+\int_{X}\phi_{0}\;d\mu_{0}+\int_{X}\psi_{0}\;d\mu_{1},

and thanks to (
34
) the first integral vanishes (and so
\mathcal{Q}(\pi_{0};\phi_{0},\psi_{0})
vanishes as well), and thus

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\pi_{0};\phi_{0},\psi_{0})

\displaystyle=\int_{X}\phi_{0}(x)\;d\mu_{0}(x)+\int_{X}\psi_{0}(y)\;d\mu_{1}(y)

\displaystyle=D_{\pi}(\phi_{0},\psi_{0}).

Hence, from (
27
), we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\phi_{0},\psi_{0})\leq\mathcal{L}(\pi_{0};\phi_{0},\psi_{0})=\mathcal{E}(\pi_{0}).

But by the KKT conditions, the inequality becomes an equality, and hence
\mathcal{D}(\phi_{0},\psi_{0})=\mathcal{E}(\pi_{0})
.
∎

4.4.
The effective cost

Toward the end of Section
2
we introduced the
endpoint function
(
9
) which represented the minimum cost function of a path going from
x
to
y
. Now for the problem with interaction we will introduce what we call an “effective” endpoint cost function that includes the interaction term.

Therefore given
\pi
, define
c_{0}(\gamma):=c(\gamma)+2\;\mathcal{U}(\gamma;\pi)
as the
effective cost
. The analogue of the endpoint cost function (
9
) is

(35)

\displaystyle c_{\text{eff}}(x,y):=\inf_{\gamma(0)=x,\gamma(1)=y}\;c_{0}(\gamma).

In what follows we deal explicitly with

\displaystyle c(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{2}|\dot{\gamma}(t)|^{2}dt,\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{U}(\gamma;\pi):=\theta\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{1}e^{-|\gamma(t)-\sigma(t)|^{2}}dt\;d\pi(\sigma),

for some
\theta<<1
to be determined later. We start by showing that the optimal path of
c_{0}(\gamma)
is sufficiently close to the optimal path of
c(\gamma)
, if
\theta
is small.

Proposition 4.8
.

Fix
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
and
\theta>0
. Then for any path
\gamma:[0,1]\to X
which is Lipschitz in
[0,1]
and twice differentiable in
(0,1)
define,

\displaystyle c_{0}(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{2}|\dot{\gamma}(t)|^{2}dt+2\theta\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\Omega}e^{-|\gamma(t)-\sigma(t)|^{2}}d\pi(\sigma)\;dt

Given
x,y
, let
\gamma_{0}
be optimal for
c_{0}(\gamma)
. Then
\gamma_{0}
is of the form
\gamma_{0}(t)=\gamma_{euc}(t)+\theta E(\theta,t)
; where
\gamma_{euc}
is optimal for
c(\gamma)
and where
E(\theta,t)
satisfies the estimate

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}|E(\theta,t)|\leq\sqrt{n}.

Proof.

Notice that
d\pi_{t}(z):=(e_{t})_{\sharp}\pi,

We now provide a Lipschitz bound on

Proposition 4.10
.

Given
\mathbf{w},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

\displaystyle|T_{\mathbf{z}}(\mathbf{w})-T_{\mathbf{z}}(\mathbf{u})|\leq n^{2}|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{u}|.

Proof.

We firstly bound a partial derivative of
T_{\mathbf{z}}(\mathbf{w})
. We have

\partial_{w_{j}}T_{\mathbf{z}}^{i}(\mathbf{w})=e^{-|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{z}|^{2}}(\delta_{ij}-2(w_{i}-z_{i})(w_{j}-z_{j})).

For radial
r=|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{z}|
, we get an upper bound,

\Big|\partial_{w_{j}}T_{\mathbf{z}}^{i}(\mathbf{w})\Big|\leq e^{-|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{z}|^{2}}(1+|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{z}|^{2})\leq e^{-r^{2}}(1+r^{2})\leq 1.

Secondly, borrowing notation and calculations from Spivak’s
Calculus on Manifolds

[
20
]
, we observe that

T_{\mathbf{z}}^{i}(\mathbf{w})-T_{\mathbf{z}}^{i}(\mathbf{u})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left[T_{\mathbf{z}}^{i}(w_{1},\ldots,w_{j};u_{j+1},\ldots,u_{n})-T_{\mathbf{z}}^{i}(w_{1},\ldots,w_{j-1};u_{j},\ldots,u_{n})\right].

As
T_{\mathbf{z}}(\mathbf{w})
is continuously differentiable, the mean value theorem gives us for every
i
and
j

\displaystyle T_{\mathbf{z}}^{i}(w_{1},\ldots,w_{j};u_{j+1},\ldots,u_{n})-T_{\mathbf{z}}^{i}(w_{1},\ldots,w_{j-1};u_{j},\ldots,u_{n})=(w_{j}-u_{j})\partial_{w_{j}}T_{\mathbf{z}}^{i}(v_{ij}),

for some
v_{ij}
. The absolute value of the right-hand side of this is
|w_{j}-u_{j}|\;|\partial_{w_{j}}T_{\mathbf{z}}^{i}(v_{ij})|\leq|w_{j}-u_{j}|
. Then
|T_{\mathbf{z}}^{i}(\mathbf{w})-T_{\mathbf{z}}^{i}(\mathbf{u})|\leq n|w_{j}-u_{j}|\leq n|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{u}|
, since each
|w_{j}-u_{j}|\leq|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{u}|
. Hence,
|T_{\mathbf{z}}(\mathbf{w})-T_{\mathbf{z}}(\mathbf{u})|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}|T_{\mathbf{z}}^{i}(\mathbf{w})-T_{\mathbf{z}}^{i}(\mathbf{u})|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}n|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{u}|=n^{2}|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{u}|,
and the proposition follows.
