Parsimonious Learning-Augmented Approximations for Dense Instances of 𝒩 ​ 𝒫 \mathcal{NP} -hard Problems
Evripidis Bampis, Bruno Escoffier, Michalis Xefteris

Abstract
The classical work of [ 6 ] provides a scheme that gives, for any ϵ > 0 \epsilon>0 , a polynomial time 1 − ϵ 1-\epsilon approximation algorithm for dense instances of a family of 𝒩 ​ 𝒫 \mathcal{NP} -hard problems, such as Max-CUT and Max - k k - SAT . In this paper we extend and speed up this scheme using a logarithmic number of one-bit predictions. We propose a learning augmented framework which aims at finding fast algorithms which guarantees approximation consistency, smoothness and robustness with respect to the prediction error. We provide such algorithms, which moreover use predictions parsimoniously, for dense instances of various optimization problems.

Introduction

In an era marked by the widespread adoption of Machine Learning technology, ML predictors, capable of learning to predict the unknown based on (past) data, are employed to solve numerous problems daily. Due to an effort to exploit this development, there has been a trend in recent years that tries to use ML predictions in order to overcome known worst-case computational limitations. The goal is to provide algorithms that use a possibly erroneous predictor to enhance their performance when the prediction is accurate, while still providing worst case performance guarantees.

The formal framework for these learning-augmented algorithms (or algorithms with predictions) has been presented by Lykouris and Vassilvitskii in their seminal paper
[
36

consistent

smooth
, meaning that the quality of the solution produced degrades smoothly with the error made in the prediction, and
robust
. The robustness requires that the performance of the algorithm remains close to the one of the best worst-case algorithm even when the prediction is bad (see Section
2
for formal definitions).

This vein of work has produced various results for online algorithms, i.e., algorithms that are not aware of the whole (future) input of the problem, including scheduling

Notation and Preliminaries

We start by giving a definition of density for each problem studied in this work.

Definition 2
.

An undirected graph
G(V,E)

In the paper, we assume that

Definition 3
.

A Polynomial Integer Program (PIP) is of the form

\displaystyle\text{max }p(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})

\displaystyle s.t.\qquad

\displaystyle l_{i}\leq p_{i}(\textbf{x})\leq u_{i}

\displaystyle i=1,\dots,m

\displaystyle x_{i}\in\{0,1\}

\displaystyle\forall i\leq n,

where

are polynomials. The PIP could have minimization instead of maximization. If all

have degree at most

Let us now define a class of PIPs that are easier to approximate. Note that solving PIPs is

-hard in general.

Definition 4
.

A degree-

c\cdot n^{d-i}
.

A
c
-smooth degree-
d

c
-smooth polynomials with degree at most
d
.

We assume that

Prediction Model

Let
\mathcal{I}
be an instance of an optimization problem and
S

Definition 5

(Prediction error)
Let

be a multiset and fix an optimal solution
\textbf{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in\{0,1\}^{n}

\forall j\in S

(

|S|

)

\textit{error}=\sum_{j\in S}|\hat{a_{j}}-a_{j}|=\sum_{j\in S}\textit{error}_{j},

where
\textit{error}_{j}=|\hat{a_{j}}-a_{j}|,\forall j\in S

The

Learning-augmented approximation framework.

Learning-augmented algorithms have three main properties that we adjust in the context of this work.
In the Learning Augmented Approximation (LAA) framework, we say that a (randomized) algorithm is:

•

\alpha
-
consistent

\alpha
-approximation with high probability when
\textit{error}=0

•

\beta

\beta

with high probability regardless of the value of

, and

•

\gamma
-
smooth
for a continuous function

, if it is a
\gamma(\textit{error})
-approximation with high probability.

Note that the smoothness of a polynomial has no connection with the smoothness of a learning-augmented algorithm. In the paper, the distinction between the two notions will be made clear due to the context of each sentence.

Overview of Algorithm

First, let us write

\displaystyle\text{max }p(\textbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\cdot\sum_{j\in N(i)}(1-x_{j})

\displaystyle\text{max }p(\textbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\cdot\big(|N(i)|-r_{i}(\textbf{x})\big)

(1)
