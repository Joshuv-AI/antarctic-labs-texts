Autonomous feedback stabilization of a cavity-coupled spin oscillator
Julian Wolf, Olive H. Eilbott, Joshua A. Isaacs, Kevin P. Mours, Jonathan Kohler, Dan M. Stamper-Kurn

Abstract
We report out-of-equilibrium stabilization of the collective spin of an atomic ensemble through autonomous feedback by a driven optical cavity. For a magnetic field applied at an angle to the cavity axis, dispersive coupling to the cavity provides sensitivity to a combination of the longitudinal and transverse spin. Coherent backaction by cavity light onto the atoms, conditioned by the optical cavity susceptibility, stabilizes the collective spin state at an arbitrary energy. The set point tracking and closed-loop gain spectrum of the feedback system are characterized and found to agree closely with analytic predictions.



As in the case of classical systems, the state and evolution of quantum systems can be tailored by feedback control
Lloyd2000
;
Mabuchi2005
;
Zhang2017
.
At a scientific level, the development of a quantum control theory, one that integrates entanglement and non-classical effects of dissipation and measurement, opens a new line of inquiry into non-equilibrium and open quantum systems.
At an applied level, feedback control allows quantum devices to operate robustly, mitigating errors in system preparation and calibration as well as decoherence.
Feedback control underpins important tasks such as error correction in quantum computation
Shor1995
;
Steane1996
;
Geerlings2013
and sensing
Kessler2014
;
Dur2014
;
Zhou2020
, entanglement purification
Pan2001
, and adaptive measurement
Armen2002
.

Quantum feedback control can be divided broadly into the two categories of measurement-based and autonomous feedback.
In the measurement-based approach, properties of a quantum system are read out on a classical sensor, the measurement record of which is used by an extrinsic classical control device to alter the ensuing coherence, dissipation, and measurement operations on the system
Sayrin2011
;
Vijay2012
;
Riste2012
;
CampagneIbarcq2013
;
Doherty2000
.
The feedback system’s design must account for noise and backaction that are intrinsic to quantum measurement.

By comparison, in autonomous (or coherent) quantum feedback the corrective response that steers a quantum subsystem is built into the quantum system itself.
Control is achieved by structuring the drive and dissipation of an open quantum system so that entropy is reliably extracted as the quantum system is steered to the desired final state. Examples of such schemes include autonomous error correction in bosonic code spaces
Gertler2021
;
Puri2020
;
Ma2021
, quantum state preparation
Murch2012
;
Riste2013
;
Shankar2013
;
Lin2013
;
Leghtas2013
;
Andersen2016
, optical noise cancellation
Mabuchi2008
, and generating spin squeezing of atoms in a driven optical resonator
SchleierSmith2010
.

In this work, we develop a coherent feedback scheme to stabilize the energy of an ensemble of quantum spins.
Our scheme employs optical backaction in a driven cavity to realize closed-loop autonomous feedback.
Under negative-feedback conditions, we observe that cavity spin optodynamics
Brahms2010
;
Kohler2017
deterministically steer the collective spin toward a steady-state energy that is set by the frequency of the driving optical field.
By examining both the light that drives the system and the atomic spins that respond to this drive, we quantify the tuning sensitivity as well as the closed-loop gain spectrum of the autonomous feedback system and find close agreement with a theoretical model.

The feedback system comprises the collective spin of an ultracold atomic gas interacting with an optical cavity mode.
In particular, an ensemble of
N_{\text{a}}\approx 1400
non-degenerate
87
Rb
atoms, cooled to around
3\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro K}
, is trapped predominantly in a single antinode of a standing-wave optical dipole trap (ODT, wavelength
842\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
) resonant with a TEM
00
mode of an optical Fabry–Pérot cavity
Purdy2010
;
Kohler2017
;
Kohler2018
;
Zeiher2021
.
The atoms are initially prepared in the
|{f=2},{m_{f}=2}\rangle
hyperfine level of the electronic ground state.

The atomic ensemble interacts strongly with a second TEM
00
cavity mode (the “pump” mode) whose frequency
\omega_{\text{c}}
is detuned from the atomic D
2
transition (frequency
\omega_{\text{a}}
, wavelength
780\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
) by
\varDelta_{\text{ca}}\equiv\omega_{\text{c}}-\omega_{\text{a}}=2\pi\times$-35\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}$
.
The half-linewidth of the cavity at the D
2
wavelength is
\kappa/2\pi=$1.82\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}$
.
We minimize the effects of the spatial dependence of the atom–cavity coupling by trapping atoms at a location where the trapping field and pump field antinodes coincide (Fig.
1
a)
Purdy2010
.
The symmetric coupling of the atoms to the cavity field allows the ensemble to be addressed in terms of a total (dimensionless) spin
F=2N_{\text{a}}\approx 2800
and a mean dispersive cavity–atom coupling
g_{\text{c}}=g_{0}^{2}/\varDelta_{\text{ca}}
, where
g_{0}=2\pi\times$13\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}$
is the vacuum Rabi coupling of a single atom to the pump mode, averaged over the atom’s motion in the ODT
Purdy2010
.

For a cavity pumped with circularly polarized (
\sigma_{\text{R}}
, or
\sigma_{-}
for an external field pointing along the cavity axis) light, the dynamics of the system are governed by the Hamiltonian
Brahms2010
(Appendix A)

\hat{H}=-\hbar\varDelta_{\text{pc}}\hat{c}^{\dagger}\hat{c}+\hbar\omega_{\text{s}}\hat{F}_{z}+\hbar g_{\text{c}}\big[\alpha_{0}N_{\text{a}}-\alpha_{1}\hat{F}_{k}\big]\,\hat{c}^{\dagger}\hat{c},

(1)

written in a frame rotating at the pump frequency
\omega_{\text{p}}
.
Here,
\varDelta_{\text{pc}}\equiv\omega_{\text{p}}-\omega_{\text{c}}
is the pump–cavity detuning,
\hat{c}
is the cavity pump mode annihilation operator, and
\omega_{\text{s}}=g_{F}\mu_{\text{B}}B/\hbar=$300\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}$
is the Larmor frequency (where
g_{F}
is the Landé
g
-factor,
B
the applied magnetic field strength, and
\mu_{\text{B}}
is the Bohr magneton).
The spin projections
\hat{F}_{z}
and
\hat{F}_{k}
in Eq.
1
are defined by the applied magnetic field
\vec{B}=B\hat{z}
and the cavity axis
\hat{k}
(Fig.
1
a).
The constants
\alpha_{0}=\frac{2}{3}
and
\alpha_{1}=\frac{1}{6}
describe the scalar and vector interactions, respectively, between the atoms and the cavity pump field (Appendix A).
We have treated atom–cavity interactions as purely dispersive, accounting for
|\varDelta_{\text{ca}}|
being large compared to the atomic half-linewidth (
\gamma=2\pi\times$3\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}$
) and to the collective atom–cavity coupling strength
\sqrt{N}g_{0}
.

The collective atom–cavity interaction is the sum of two terms.
A scalar (spin-independent) dispersive atom–light interaction shifts the cavity resonance frequency proportional to atom number
N_{\text{a}}
.
With
N_{\text{a}}
being constant during the few-
\mathrm{ms}
duration of the spin feedback experiments, it is useful to absorb this static frequency shift into an effective constant pump–cavity detuning
\varDelta_{\text{set}}\equiv\varDelta_{\text{pc}}-g_{\text{c}}\alpha_{0}N_{\text{a}}
.
In addition, a vector (spin-dependent) atom–cavity interaction shifts the resonance frequency of the
\sigma_{-}
cavity mode by an amount proportional to the spin component
\hat{F}_{k}
(Appendix A).

We now outline how this quantum system autonomously includes the essential elements of a feedback control system.
In such a control system, a control variable, which represents the state of the plant (the subsystem to be controlled), is measured by a sensor.
A comparator generates an error signal as the difference of the sensor output and an externally determined set point.
A controller conditions the error signal and acts on the plant.
Under proper negative-feedback conditions, the control variable is stabilized unconditionally.

Identifying
\hat{F}_{k}=\hat{F}_{x}\sin\theta_{B}+\hat{F}_{z}\cos\theta_{B}
, with
\theta_{B}
being the angle between the applied magnetic field and
\hat{k}
(Fig.
1
a), we observe that the cavity is sensitive both to the longitudinal spin
\hat{F}_{z}
(equivalently, the bare spin energy) and the transverse spin
\hat{F}_{x}
.
The longitudinal spin
\hat{F}_{z}
plays the role of the control variable, and the cavity shift proportional to
\hat{F}_{z}
acts as a coherent sensor.
Accounting for this shift, the net detuning of the pump light from the cavity, averaged over the fast effects of Larmor precession, is given by
\hat{\varDelta}\equiv\varDelta_{\text{set}}+g_{\text{s}}\hat{F}_{z}\cos\theta_{B}
, with
g_{\text{s}}\equiv\alpha_{1}g_{\text{c}}
.
The system Hamiltonian Eq.
1
can now be rewritten as

\hat{H}=-\hbar\hat{\varDelta}\hat{c}^{\dagger}\hat{c}+\hbar\omega_{\text{s}}\hat{F}_{z}-\hbar g_{\text{s}}\hat{c}^{\dagger}\hat{c}\,\hat{F}_{x}\sin\theta_{B}.

(2)

The net detuning
\hat{\varDelta}
represents the control system error signal, proportional to the difference between the instantaneous value of
\hat{F}_{z}
and the externally determined longitudinal spin set point

F_{z}^{\text{set}}=-\frac{\varDelta_{\text{set}}}{g_{\text{s}}\cos\theta_{B}}.

(3)

The final term in Eq.
2
completes the autonomous control system, serving as the feedback controller.
As described in Refs.
Kippenberg2008
;
Brahms2010
;
Kohler2018
, the Larmor precessing transverse spin modulates the cavity field intensity through the spin-dependent dispersive interaction. In turn, this modulation, conditioned by cavity dynamics, acts resonantly on the precessing spin and alters its energy.

As in the case of cavity optomechanics
Marquardt2007
, the resulting energy dynamics of the spin ensemble can be described in terms of cavity-induced sideband asymmetry
Vuletic2000
.
Modulation of the cavity resonance by the precessing spin shifts optical power from the pump light into first-order frequency sidebands with optical frequencies
\omega_{\text{p}}\pm\omega_{\text{s}}
.
While a free-space modulator would generate sidebands with equal power, here, the cavity spectrum induces a sideband asymmetry (Fig.
1
b).
For a pump blue-detuned from cavity resonance (
\varDelta>0
), the cavity induces stronger emission on the Stokes (red) sideband, reducing the net energy of the optical pump and, in turn, increasing the energy of the spin ensemble.
Similarly, a pump red-detuned from cavity resonance (
\varDelta<0
) reduces the energy of the spin ensemble.
With the correct sign of
g_{\text{s}}\cos\theta_{B}
in Eq.
3
, the response of the spin ensemble in either case brings
\varDelta
closer to zero.
The system arrives at a stable steady state, with
\langle\hat{F}_{z}\rangle=F_{z}^{\text{set}}
, that is determined by the pump frequency
\omega_{\text{p}}
(through
\varDelta_{\text{set}}
) and that, notably, is independent of the initial state of the collective spin and insensitive to many perturbations.

We first confirm experimentally that the spin ensemble is autonomously stabilized to a state determined by the external set point.
To this end, the collective spin is initiated to
\langle\hat{F}_{z}(t=0)\rangle=0
using a coherent rf
\pi/2
-pulse at drive frequency
\omega_{\text{s}}
, such that
\varDelta(t=0)=\varDelta_{\text{set}}
.
The cavity is then pumped with light at a constant
\varDelta_{\text{set}}
and allowed to evolve.
The light emitted by the cavity is measured on a balanced heterodyne detector
Brooks2012
;
Brahms2012
;
Zeiher2021
(total detection efficiency
\epsilon_{\text{det}}=$2.2\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}$
), allowing the power in the Stokes and anti-Stokes sidebands to be detected as independent time traces (Fig.
2
a).
The difference in the power of the two sidebands directly measures the instantaneous energy transfer from the pump light to the collective spin.
The cumulative sum of this difference measures the total energy
\delta E(t)
added to the collective spin, leading up to time
t
.
As shown in Fig.
2
a and b, an initial
\varDelta_{\text{set}}<0
leads to an enhancement of the anti-Stokes sideband and a net energy transfer
\delta E<0
, driving the spin to a low energy state, while
\varDelta_{\text{set}}>0
has the opposite effect.

The spin state achieved after long evolution times under autonomous feedback, at a given
\varDelta_{\text{set}}
, is shown in Fig.

Here, we measure the longitudinal spin by terminating the feedback, reorienting the magnetic field along
\hat{k}
, and measuring the spin-dependent cavity shift (Appendix B).
For each
\theta_{B}
, the measured response shows a tuning range, centered about
\varDelta_{\text{set}}=0
, within which the steady-state spin energy varies linearly with
\varDelta_{\text{set}}
.
The sideband-based energy transfer measurements show the same trend as the spin measurements, through the relation
\hbar\omega_{\text{s}}\langle\hat{F}_{z}\rangle(t)=\delta E(t)
, but are found to be less precise.
By fitting the response curves to an analytical model (discussed below and in Appendix C), we determine the linear sensitivity of the steady-state spin to
\varDelta_{\text{set}}
near
\varDelta_{\text{set}}=0
.
This linear sensitivity (Fig.
2

3
) for a range of field angles.

Outside the linear tuning range
|\varDelta_{\text{set}}|>|2N_{\text{a}}g_{\text{s}}\cos\theta_{B}|
, one would expect the feedback system to rail, driving the spin ensemble to one of its extremal energy states.
Such railing is observed for
\theta_{B}\gtrsim$55\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}$
.
Here, the cavity field modulation amplitude, proportional to
\sin\theta_{B}
, is large, driving the spin quickly to its steady state.
In contrast, for shallower angles (
\theta_{B}\lesssim$55\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}$
), the collective spin undergoes dephasing during feedback, reducing its total magnitude to
||\vec{F}||<2N_{\text{a}}
before the system can reach its steady state.

Next, we investigate the dynamical response of the autonomous feedback system.
Considering the Hamiltonian of Eq.
2
and adding terms accounting for pumping into and leakage out of the cavity mode, the cavity field evolves according to

\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\hat{c}=\mathrm{i}\big(\varDelta_{\text{set}}+g_{\text{s}}\hat{F}_{k}\big)\hat{c}-\kappa\hat{c}+\kappa\eta,

(4)

where
\eta
is the coherent-state amplitude of the field pumping the cavity.
The energy of the collective spin, meanwhile, evolves according to

\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\hat{F}_{z}=g_{\text{s}}\hat{c}^{\dagger}\hat{c}\,\hat{F}_{y}\,\sin\theta_{B}.

(5)

For
\omega_{\text{s}}\gg g_{\text{s}}
, as in our experiment, the optodynamical Larmor frequency shift (analogue of the optomechanical spring shift)
Brahms2010
is small and the transverse spin can be approximated as
\hat{F}_{y}=F_{\perp}\sin\omega_{\text{s}}t
; in practice, this approximation relies on terms with nontrivial commutation relations only entering in at a higher order than is being considered, and amounts to treating
\hat{F}_{y}
as equal to its expectation value.

We expect a cavity field comprising a carrier at frequency
\omega_{\text{p}}
and sidebands at frequencies
\omega_{\text{p}}\pm\omega_{\text{s}}
:
\hat{c}=\hat{c}_{0}+\hat{c}_{+}e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{\text{s}}t}+\hat{c}_{-}e^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{\text{s}}t}
.
In the limit of small modulation depth
(g_{\text{s}}/2\kappa)F_{\perp}|\sin\theta_{B}|\ll 1
, the amplitudes and phases of the sidebands
\hat{c}_{\pm}
can be calculated directly from Eq.
4
.
Inserting this solution for the cavity field into Eq.
5
, we find that the cavity resonance frequency is pulled toward its set point at a damping rate given as

\beta\equiv\frac{1}{\varDelta}\frac{\dd\varDelta}{\dd t}=-2F_{\perp}^{2}\sin^{2}\theta_{B}\cos\theta_{B}\frac{g_{\text{s}}^{3}}{\kappa^{3}}\omega_{\text{s}}\bar{n}.

(6)

Here,
\bar{n}\equiv\bar{n}_{\text{max}}\,\kappa^{2}/(\kappa^{2}+\varDelta^{2})
with
\bar{n}_{\text{max}}=|\eta|^{2}
.
This simple model allows for straightforward simulation of how the system will act under a variety of conditions.

We probe the dynamics of our feedback system in two experiments.
First, we characterize the system’s closed-loop transfer function by pumping the cavity with a time-varying tone
\varDelta_{\text{set}}(t)=\varDelta_{0}\sin\omega_{\text{mod}}t
and measuring the response
\delta E(t)
.
We predict the shift to the cavity resonance due to the spin response to be
\varDelta_{\text{exp}}(t)=-g_{\text{s}}\cos\theta_{B}\delta E(t)/\hbar\omega_{\text{s}}
; this can be compared to the setpoint detuning
\varDelta_{\text{set}}(t)
(Fig.
3
a).
At each modulation frequency
\omega_{\text{mod}}
, the closed-loop gain
G_{\text{cl}}
is calculated as the ratio between the response and the perturbation (Fig.
3
b, black circles):

G_{\text{cl}}[\omega_{\text{mod}}]=\frac{2\mathrm{i}}{\varDelta_{0}T}\int_{0}^{T}\dd t\,\varDelta_{\text{exp}}(t)\,\exp(-\mathrm{i}\omega_{\text{mod}}t),

(7)

where
T=2\pi s/\omega_{\text{mod}}
, for integer
s
.
For a pure integrator system such as ours with damping rate
\beta
, we expect a closed-loop gain of

G_{\text{cl}}[\omega]=\frac{\mathrm{i}(\beta/\omega)}{1+\mathrm{i}(\beta/\omega)},

(8)

which should describe the system well for
\varDelta_{0}\ll\kappa,|2N_{\text{a}}g_{\text{s}}\cos\theta_{B}|
(Fig.
3
b, gray line).
Our measurements qualitatively match the amplitude response predicted by Eq.
8
, but the data quality is limited by a signal-to-noise ratio of approximately
0.1
–
0.3
(dominated by shot noise on the detection of the sideband fields, which is exacerbated by the low
\epsilon_{\text{det}}

SNR
) as well as by the large
\varDelta_{0}
used for this measurement (which leads to the collective spin being pulled away from the linear feedback regime
F_{\perp}\approx 2N_{\text{a}}
; see below).
These limitations prevent us from measuring the phase response of
G_{\text{cl}}
, and from making rigorous comparisons to theory.

Second, and more quantitatively, we characterize the impulse response function of the feedback system.
Here, we initialize the collective spin near
\langle\hat{f}_{z}\rangle=+1
, and then suddenly turn on the pump light in order to impose feedback with a set point of
F_{z}^{\text{set}}/N_{\text{a}}=-1
(Fig.
3
c).
Time-resolved direct spin measurements track the system evolution toward the set point (Appendix B).
For regions over which
\beta
is approximately constant (namely,
|\langle\hat{f}_{z}\rangle|\leq 1
), Eq.
6
states that
\langle\hat{F}_{z}\rangle
should approach
F_{z}^{\text{set}}
exponentially.
Fitting the observed spin response to an exponential relaxation yields an empirical value of
\beta=$450(\pm 60)\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}$
(Fig.
3
c).
For the same experimental parameters (
\theta_{B}=$60\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}$
,
\omega_{\text{s}}=2\pi\times$300\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}$
,
\bar{n}_{\text{max}}=2.4
,
N_{\text{a}}=1100
), Eq.
6
predicts
\beta=$1600\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}$
.
The disagreement between our measurement and this simple theory prediction is due, in part, to the large modulation depth imposed by the precessing spins in our experimental setting: here,
(g_{\text{s}}/2\kappa)F_{\perp}\sin\theta_{B}=0.4
, which warrants the inclusion of terms corresponding to higher-order scattering events (measured as sidebands at
\pm 2\omega_{\text{s}}
).
Accounting for these corrections reduces the expected gain to
\beta=$790\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}$
(Appendix C).
The analytical model also does not account for the dephasing of the spin ensemble.
Constructing an accurate model for dephasing in our system is not straightforward, but any form of dephasing will have the effect of decreasing
F_{\perp}
, and thus
\beta
, which may explain the remaining discrepancy.

In this work, we have shown that autonomous feedback generated by optical backaction of a driven cavity onto a spin ensemble stabilizes the ensemble energy at an energy determined by the cavity pump frequency.
The optical cavity emission provides a real-time record of the feedback dynamics.
In future work, information from this real-time optical signal may also be used to enhance the feedback stabilization through additional measurement-based feedback
Hofman1998
;
Wang2001
.

This system can equivalently be described as autonomous feedback stabilization of the optical cavity’s resonance frequency.
From this viewpoint, the control variable is
\varDelta
.
The spin ensemble now plays the part of the controller by which the cavity is autonomously tuned to be in resonance with the light with which it is driven.

Our feedback setup stabilizes the spin ensemble to a specific value of the longitudinal spin, but does not control the phase at which this spin undergoes Larmor precession because of the time translation symmetry of our scheme.
Future work may investigate methods for more complete control of the quantum spin state,
e.g.
, applying phase coherent modulation at the Larmor frequency, either to the optical pump field or to an applied magnetic field, so as to stabilize the Larmor precession phase.

Another target for future investigation is the fluctuation of the spin ensemble under steady-state feedback.
In steady state, the ensemble should respond to the quantum noise of the cavity field, generating fluctuations in the longitudinal spin as well as the Larmor precession phase.
At the same time, coherent feedback suppresses longitudinal spin fluctuations.
The balance between quantum-optical fluctuations and coherent dissipation, achieved in the steady state and away from thermal equilibrium, may be revealed in the spectrum of the cavity output.
However, in our current setup, technical noise on
\omega_{\text{c}}
, the pump light spectrum, and optical detectors obscures this quantum noise signature.

Appendix A
A. Derivation of the autonomous spin stabilization Hamiltonian

The Hamiltonian for the autonomous spin stabilization system can be written, generically, as a sum of cavity, spin, and interaction terms:
\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{\text{c}}+\hat{H}_{\text{s}}+\hat{H}_{\text{int}}
.

In the lab frame, the cavity Hamiltonian is simply given by
\hat{H}_{\text{c}}^{\text{lab}}=\hbar\omega_{\text{c}}\hat{c}^{\dagger}\hat{c}
.
We find it helpful to move to a frame rotating at the frequency
\omega_{\text{p}}
of the cavity pump laser, such that

\hat{H}_{\text{c}}=-\hbar\varDelta_{\text{pc}}\hat{c}^{\dagger}\hat{c}=-\hbar\varDelta_{\text{pc}}\hat{n},

(9)

where
\varDelta_{\text{pc}}\equiv\omega_{\text{p}}-\omega_{\text{c}}
is the pump–cavity detuning.
Here, the cavity annihilation operator
\hat{c}
and the photon occupation operator
\hat{n}
include light of both right- and left-handed circular polarizations.
Although the left- and right-handed cavity modes interact differently with the atomic ensemble, their bare energies are approximately degenerate, and here they can be considered together as
\hat{n}=\hat{n}_{\text{R}}+\hat{n}_{\text{L}}
.

For an ensemble of noninteracting atoms indexed
i
at positions
\vec{r}_{i}
and spin projection
f_{z}^{(i)}
along the direction of the magnetic field, the spin Hamiltonian is given by
\hat{H}_{\text{s}}=\sum_{i}\hbar\omega_{\text{s}}(\vec{r}_{i})\,\hat{f}_{z}^{(i)}
.
Here, the local spin precession frequency is given by
\hbar\omega_{\text{s}}(\vec{r})=g_{F}\mu_{\text{B}}|\vec{B}(\vec{r})|
, where
g_{F}
is the Landé
g
-factor and
\mu_{\text{B}}
is the Bohr magneton.
For a localized ensemble of atoms, the magnetic field is approximately constant, such that this can be rewritten in terms of an average spin precession frequency
\omega_{\text{s}}
and a total spin projection
\hat{F}_{z}=\sum_{i}\hat{f}_{z}^{(i)}
:

\hat{H}_{\text{s}}=\hbar\omega_{\text{s}}\hat{F}_{z}.

(10)

Generically, the interaction between the cavity and atom
i
is described by

\hat{H}_{\text{int}}^{(i)}=\hbar\sum_{g,e}g_{g;e}^{+}(\vec{r}_{i})\,\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{+}\,\hat{\sigma}_{e;g}^{(i)}\,\delta_{m+1,m^{\prime}}\\
{}+g_{g;e}^{-}(\vec{r}_{i})\,\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{-}\,\hat{\sigma}_{e;g}^{(i)}\,\delta_{m-1,m^{\prime}}+\text{h.c.}

(11)

Here, the summation runs over all possible transitions from the ground-state
manifold
g\equiv|f=2,m\rangle
to the excited states
e\equiv|f^{\prime}=3,m^{\prime}\rangle
, with polarization-dependent coupling strengths
g_{g;e}^{\pm}
.
When the cavity–atom detuning
\varDelta_{\text{ca}}
is large compared to the hyperfine splittings
\varDelta_{f^{\prime}}
in the excited (
f^{\prime}=3
) manifold that is being addressed, the excited states can be eliminated.
This approximation results in a spin-dependent dispersive interaction Hamiltonian, describing dynamics within the ground-state manifold:

\hat{H}_{\text{int}}^{(i)}=\hbar g_{\text{c}}|U(\vec{r}_{i})|^{2}\bigg\{\alpha_{0}\big(\hat{n}_{+}+\hat{n}_{-}\big)+\alpha_{1}\big(\hat{n}_{+}-\hat{n}_{-}\big)\hat{f}_{k}^{(i)}\\
{}+\alpha_{2}\bigg[\big(\hat{n}_{+}+\hat{n}_{-}\big)\Big(\hat{f}_{k}^{(i)}\Big)^{2}-\hat{c}_{-}\hat{c}_{+}\Big(\hat{f}_{+}^{(i)}\Big)^{2}-\hat{c}_{+}\hat{c}_{-}\Big(\hat{f}_{-}^{(i)}\Big)^{2}\bigg]\bigg\},

(12)

where
|U(\vec{r}_{i})|^{2}
is the local relative intensity of the cavity pump mode, where
\hat{c}_{\pm}
are the annihilation operators for left- and right-handed cavity modes, which are approximately degenerate in our system, and where
\hat{f}_{k}
and
\hat{f}_{\pm}
are the spin operators relative to a quantization axis along the cavity axis
\hat{k}
.
Here, the scalar, vector, and tensor interactions between the spin and the cavity field are described by coupling coefficients
(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\to(2/3,1/6,0)
in the limit of large
\varDelta_{\text{ca}}
(Fig.
4
).
In our system, the atomic ensemble is primarily localized within a single antinode of the cavity pump field, which allows the local cavity field
U(\vec{r}_{i})
to be treated as approximately constant.
This leaves

\hat{H}_{\text{int}}=\hbar g_{\text{c}}\Big\{\alpha_{0}N_{\text{a}}\hat{n}+\alpha_{1}\big(\hat{n}_{+}-\hat{n}_{-}\big)\hat{F}_{k}\Big\},

(13)

such that the total system Hamiltonian, in the limit
|\varDelta_{\text{ca}}|\gg|\varDelta_{f^{\prime}}|
, is given by

\hat{H}=-\hbar\varDelta_{\text{pc}}\hat{n}+\hbar\omega_{\text{s}}\hat{F}_{z}\\
{}+\hbar g_{\text{c}}\Big\{\alpha_{0}N_{\text{a}}\hat{n}+\alpha_{1}\big(\hat{n}_{+}-\hat{n}_{-}\big)\hat{F}_{k}\Big\}.

(14)

When the cavity is pumped with only right-handed (
\sigma_{-}
) light, this reduces to Eq.
1
of the main text.
When the cavity is pumped with only left-handed light, the sign of the cavity–spin interaction flips, and with it the sign of the gain of the feedback system.

.
At

(experimental value, gray dashed line), the tensor coupling

is negligible, and the scalar and vector coupling coefficients have reached their asymptotic values.

Appendix B
B. Nondestructive measurement of the collective atomic spin state

When the externally applied magnetic field is parallel to the cavity axis (
\theta_{B}=$0\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}$
), the system Hamiltonian (Eq.
1
of the main text), corresponding to pumping the cavity with right-handed (
\sigma_{-}
) light commutes with the total spin energy
\hat{F}_{z}
since this becomes equivalent to the projection
\hat{F}_{k}
of the spins along the cavity axis:

\hat{H}^{-}=-\hbar\bigg(\varDelta_{\text{pc}}-\frac{2}{3}g_{\text{c}}N_{\text{a}}+\frac{1}{6}g_{\text{c}}\hat{F}_{z}\bigg)\,\hat{c}^{\dagger}\hat{c}+\hbar\omega_{\text{s}}\hat{F}_{z},

(15)

where the superscript on
\hat{H}^{-}
indicates that this Hamiltonian only considers the right-handed cavity mode.
If the dispersive shift
\varDelta_{N}
to the cavity resonance condition is measured by comparing the resonance frequencies with and without the presence of atoms, it will be given by

\varDelta_{N}^{-}=-\frac{2}{3}g_{\text{c}}N_{\text{a}}+\frac{1}{6}g_{\text{c}}\langle\hat{F}_{z}\rangle,

(16)

where the superscript on
\varDelta_{N}^{-}
indicates that this is the dispersive shift to the right-handed cavity mode.
If the atom number
N_{\text{a}}
were known exactly, this measurement would be sufficient to determine the collective spin energy
\hat{F}_{z}
of the ensemble; however, variable atom loss between state preparation and readout make this impractical.

By pumping the cavity with left-handed light, different information can be acquired.
Considering the case of an external field parallel to the cavity axis, the Hamiltonian can be derived which describes the left-handed (
\sigma_{+}
) cavity mode:

\hat{H}^{+}=-\hbar\bigg(\varDelta_{\text{pc}}-\frac{2}{3}g_{\text{c}}N_{\text{a}}-\frac{1}{6}g_{\text{c}}\hat{F}_{z}\bigg)\,\hat{c}^{\dagger}\hat{c}+\hbar\omega_{\text{s}}\hat{F}_{z},

(17)

which corresponds to a dispersive shift

\varDelta_{N}^{+}=-\frac{2}{3}g_{\text{c}}N_{\text{a}}-\frac{1}{6}g_{\text{c}}\langle\hat{F}_{z}\rangle.

(18)

Figure 5:

An offset acousto-optic modulator (AOM) is used to sweep the frequency of the pump light back and forth across cavity resonance.
This is repeated four times: twice with the atomic ensemble present in the cavity, and twice after the atoms have been expelled.
For the first of each pair of sweeps, right-handed (
\sigma_{-}
) pump light is used; between sweeps, a pair of LCVRs is used to switch the polarization of the pump light to left-handed (
\sigma_{+}
) (grey dashed line).
Comparing the resonance conditions with and without atoms present in the cavity offers a direct measure of
\varDelta_{N}^{\pm}
, which can be used to calculate the final collective spin
\hat{F}_{z}
and atom number
N_{\text{a}}
of the ensemble.

Using a pair of liquid crystal variable retarders (LCVRs) at the input and the output of the cavity, the polarization of the light pumping the cavity can be switched rapidly between left- and right-handed without otherwise affecting the detection chain.
By measuring the resonance frequencies of each of the polarizations with the atomic ensemble present in the cavity, and then repeating both measurements with the atoms absent, the total atom number and collective spin can be recovered (Fig.
5
):

\displaystyle N_{\text{a}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{3}{4}\frac{\varDelta_{N}^{+}+\varDelta_{N}^{-}}{g_{\text{c}}};

(19)

\displaystyle\langle\hat{F}_{z}\rangle

\displaystyle=-3\frac{\varDelta_{N}^{+}-\varDelta_{N}^{-}}{g_{\text{c}}}.

(20)

The same effect can be achieved by changing the orientation of the magnetic field to
\theta_{B}=$180\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}$
between the first and second measurements of
\varDelta_{N}
, such that
\hat{F}_{k}=-\hat{F}_{z}
, but this takes too long to be practical due to the self-inductance of the coils used to generate the field.
The effect can also be achieved by using an external rf field to drive a
\pi
-pulse on the collective spin, taking
\hat{F}_{z}\to-\hat{F}_{z}
between measurements; this approach has been successfully used in the past, but its dependence on the calibration of the rf drive makes it less appealing than switching the polarization of the pump light.

Appendix C
C. Quantum model of a collective spin coupled to an optical cavity

The damping rate
\beta
of the autonomous feedback system can be derived by considering how the system evolves in time.
Considering the Hamiltonian Eq.
2
of the main text, and including terms accounting for pumping into and (non-Hermitian) leakage out of the cavity mode, the cavity field evolves according to

\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\hat{c}=\mathrm{i}\big(\varDelta_{\text{set}}+g_{\text{s}}\hat{F}_{k}\big)\hat{c}-\kappa\hat{c}+\kappa\eta,

(21)

where
\eta
is the coherent-state amplitude of the field pumping the cavity.
Here, the field operator
\hat{c}
corresponds to the cavity field at frequency
\omega_{\text{p}}
.

Without any coupling to the cavity,
g_{\text{s}}=0
, and Eq.
21
can be solved directly, giving

\hat{c}_{0}=\eta\frac{\kappa}{\kappa-\mathrm{i}\varDelta_{\text{set}}}.

(22)

If the effects of the coupling between the cavity and the collective spin are small, the perturbation to the field can be approximated by

\hat{c}(t)=\hat{c}_{0}+\hat{c}^{\prime}(t).

(23)

The collective spin, meanwhile, evolves according to

\displaystyle\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\hat{F}_{x}

\displaystyle=-\omega_{\text{s}}\hat{F}_{y}+g_{\text{s}}\hat{c}^{\dagger}\hat{c}\hat{F}_{y}\cos\theta_{B};

(24)

\displaystyle\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\hat{F}_{y}

\displaystyle=\omega_{\text{s}}\hat{F}_{x}+g_{\text{s}}\hat{c}^{\dagger}\hat{c}\big(\hat{F}_{z}\sin\theta_{B}-\hat{F}_{x}\cos\theta_{B}\big);

\displaystyle\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\hat{F}_{z}

\displaystyle=-g_{\text{s}}\hat{c}^{\dagger}\hat{c}\hat{F}_{y}\sin\theta_{B}.

For
\omega_{\text{s}}\gg|g_{\text{s}}|
, as in our experiment, the transverse components admit solutions
\hat{F}_{y}\propto F_{\perp}\sin\omega_{\text{s}}t
.
For spins precessing at frequency
\omega_{\text{s}}
at polar angle
\chi
and azimuthal angle
\phi=\omega_{\text{s}}t
, the projection of the spin along the cavity axis looks like
\hat{F}_{k}=F_{\perp}\sin\theta_{B}\cos\phi+\hat{F}_{z}\cos\theta_{B}
, where
F_{\perp}\equiv F\sin\chi
and
F_{z}\equiv F\cos\chi
.
Substituting this into Eq.
21
gives

\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\hat{c}^{\prime}=\mathrm{i}\big(\varDelta_{\text{set}}+g_{\text{s}}F_{\perp}\sin\theta_{B}\cos\omega_{\text{s}}t+g_{\text{s}}\hat{F}_{z}\cos\theta_{B}\big)\big(\hat{c}_{0}+\hat{c}^{\prime}\big)\\
-\kappa\big(\hat{c}_{0}+\hat{c}^{\prime}\big)+\kappa\eta.

(25)

To lowest order, it seems reasonable to expect a solution that looks like effective cavity drives at frequencies
\pm\omega_{\text{s}}
due to the modulation of the bare pump field
\hat{c}_{0}
by the precessing spins.
This leads to the ansatz

\hat{c}^{\prime}(t)=\hat{c}_{+}e^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{\text{s}}t}+\hat{c}_{-}e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{\text{s}}t}.

(26)

Plugging this into Eq.
25
and ignoring quickly rotating terms
\sim e^{\pm 2\mathrm{i}\omega_{\text{s}}t}
as well as terms of order
[(g_{\text{s}}/2\kappa)F_{\perp}\sin\theta_{B}]^{2}
gives

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{0}

\displaystyle=\eta\mathcal{L}(\varDelta_{\text{set}}+g_{\text{s}}F_{z}\cos\theta_{B});

(27)

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{+}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\frac{g_{\text{s}}}{\kappa}F_{\perp}\sin\theta_{B}\,\mathcal{L}(\varDelta_{\text{set}}+g_{\text{s}}F_{z}\cos\theta_{B}+\omega_{\text{s}})\,\hat{c}_{0};

(28)

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{-}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\frac{g_{\text{s}}}{\kappa}F_{\perp}\sin\theta_{B}\,\mathcal{L}(\varDelta_{\text{set}}+g_{\text{s}}F_{z}\cos\theta_{B}-\omega_{\text{s}})\,\hat{c}_{0}.

(29)

Here,
\mathcal{L}(\varDelta)\equiv\kappa/(\kappa-\mathrm{i}\varDelta)
refers to a Lorentzian line at center frequency
\varDelta
with width
\kappa
.

It is desirable to find the effect of the cavity field on the spin energy
F_{z}
.
This is given by Eq.
24
, and depends on the instantaneous occupation number
\hat{n}\equiv\hat{c}^{\dagger}\hat{c}
of the cavity mode:

\displaystyle\hat{n}

\displaystyle=\hat{n}_{0}+\big(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{+}\,\hat{c}_{0}+\hat{c}_{0}^{\dagger}\,\hat{c}_{-}\big)\,e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{\text{s}}t}+\big(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{-}\,\hat{c}_{0}+\hat{c}_{0}^{\dagger}\,\hat{c}_{+}\big)\,e^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{\text{s}}t}

(30)

\displaystyle=\hat{n}_{0}-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\frac{g_{\text{s}}}{\kappa}F_{\perp}\sin\theta_{B}\hat{n}_{0}

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle\times\Big[\mathcal{L}(-\varDelta_{\text{set}}-g_{\text{s}}F_{z}\cos\theta_{B}+\omega_{\text{s}})\,e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{\text{s}}t}

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle-\mathcal{L}(\varDelta_{\text{set}}+g_{\text{s}}F_{z}\cos\theta_{B}+\omega_{\text{s}})\,e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{\text{s}}t}

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle+\mathcal{L}(-\varDelta_{\text{set}}-g_{\text{s}}F_{z}\cos\theta_{B}-\omega_{\text{s}})\,e^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{\text{s}}t}

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle-\mathcal{L}(\varDelta_{\text{set}}+g_{\text{s}}F_{z}\cos\theta_{B}-\omega_{\text{s}})\,e^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{\text{s}}t}\Big]

Again, terms of order
(\omega_{\text{s}}/\kappa)^{2}
, corresponding to second-order sidebands, have been ignored.
Using the cycle-averages
\overline{e^{\pm\mathrm{i}\phi}\sin\phi}=\pm\mathrm{i}/2
, this gives the mean change in energy of the collective spin to be

\displaystyle\overline{\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\langle\hat{F}_{z}\rangle}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}F_{\perp}^{2}\sin^{2}\theta_{B}\frac{g_{\text{s}}^{2}}{\kappa}\big\langle\hat{c}_{0}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{0}\big\rangle

(31)

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle\times\Big[\Real\mathcal{L}(\varDelta_{\text{set}}+g_{\text{s}}F_{z}\cos\theta_{B}+\omega_{\text{s}})

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle-\Real\mathcal{L}(\varDelta_{\text{set}}+g_{\text{s}}F_{z}\cos\theta_{B}-\omega_{\text{s}})\Big],

where the overline indicates time averaging over a Larmor precession cycle.
As expected, for
\varDelta_{\text{set}}<-g_{\text{s}}F_{z}\cos\theta_{B}
, the first Lorentzian term is larger, and
F_{z}
decreases; conversely,
F_{z}
increases for
\varDelta_{\text{set}}>-g_{\text{s}}F_{z}\cos\theta_{B}
.

Figure 6:

The coupled differential equations Eq.
35
are propagated forward in time, and the results are used as a model function to fit to the measured values of
F_{z}
(black triangles) for varying setpoints
\varDelta_{\text{set}}
.
The output of the fit (gray line) can be used to calculate the sensitivity of the autonomous feedback system to input noise.

The damping rate of the feedback system can be calculated as the exponential rate at which it approaches resonance in the limit
g_{\text{s}}F_{z}\cos\theta_{B}\to-\varDelta_{\text{set}}
.
In the unresolved sideband regime
\omega_{\text{s}}\ll\kappa
, the asymmetry between the sidebands reduces to

\Real\mathcal{L}(\varDelta_{\text{set}}+g_{\text{s}}F_{z}\cos\theta_{B}+\omega_{\text{s}})\\
{}-\Real\mathcal{L}(\varDelta_{\text{set}}+g_{\text{s}}F_{z}\cos\theta_{B}-\omega_{\text{s}})\\
{}\approx-\frac{4\omega_{\text{s}}(\varDelta_{\text{set}}+g_{\text{s}}F_{z}\cos\theta_{B})}{\kappa^{2}}.

(32)

Using this approximation along with Eq.
31
and Eq.
27
, and noting that
\eta^{2}=\bar{n}_{\text{max}}
corresponds to the mean on-resonance photon occupation of the cavity, the damping rate of the system looks like

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=-\frac{g_{\text{s}}\cos\theta_{B}}{\varDelta_{\text{set}}+g_{\text{s}}F_{z}\cos\theta_{B}}\overline{\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\langle\hat{F}_{z}\rangle}

(33)

\displaystyle=-2F_{\perp}^{2}\sin^{2}\theta_{B}\cos\theta_{B}\frac{g_{\text{s}}^{3}}{\kappa^{3}}\omega_{\text{s}}\bar{n},

where
\bar{n}=\bar{n}_{\text{max}}\,\kappa^{2}/(\kappa^{2}+[\varDelta_{\text{set}}+g_{\text{s}}F_{z}\cos\theta_{B}]^{2})
is the true cavity-filtered photon occupation of the cavity.
For the parameters used in our experiment, this amounts to a damping rate of
\beta=$1600\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}$
.
Notably, however, these parameters do not fall well within the low-modulation regime used to approximate Eq.
27
.

The inclusion of higher-order terms
[(g_{\text{s}}/2\kappa)F_{\perp}\sin\theta_{B}]^{2}
has the effect of reducing the carrier amplitude found in Eq.
27
.
In particular, the full expression for the amplitude looks like

\hat{c}_{0}=\eta\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(0)\\
{}\times\Big[1+\Big(\frac{g_{\text{s}}F_{\perp}\sin\theta_{B}}{2\kappa}\Big)^{2}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(0)\big[\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\omega_{\text{s}})+\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(-\omega_{\text{s}})\big]\Big]^{-1},

(34)

where
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\nu)\equiv\mathcal{L}(\varDelta_{\text{set}}+g_{\text{s}}F_{z}\cos\theta_{B}+\nu)
has been written for brevity.
For the parameters used in our experiment, this amounts to a correction factor of
0.7
, resulting in a correction factor of
0.5
to
\bar{n}
and to the final damping rate:
\beta=$790\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}$
.
The solutions given by Eq.
28
and Eq.
29
still ignore quickly rotating terms corresponding to higher-order sidebands; however, these effects are confirmed experimentally to be small.

In order to simulate the dynamics of the system,
F_{z}\equiv\langle\hat{F}_{z}\rangle
can be treated as a c-number and Eq.
33
can be used to propagate
F_{z}
forward in time.
In the absence of any dephasing, this treatment can be made complete by requiring that the total spin is conserved,
F_{z}^{2}+F_{\perp}^{2}=4N_{\text{a}}^{2}
.
Dephasing can be included heuristically by decreasing
F_{\perp}
over time.
In practice, this decrease can take many functional forms, but a simple exponential decay captures much of the system dynamics.
Simulating the feedback process, then, amounts to propagating two coupled differential equations:

\displaystyle\frac{\dd}{\dd t}F_{z}

\displaystyle=\beta(F_{\perp})\,F_{z};

(35)

\displaystyle\frac{\dd}{\dd t}F_{\perp}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\beta(F_{\perp})\,F_{z}^{2}}{F_{\perp}}-\Gamma F_{\perp}.

The resulting values of
F_{z}
can be used as a model function for least-squares fitting, where
\Gamma
, as well as an overall offset to
F_{z}
which accounts for systematic measurement errors, are allowed to vary (Fig.
6
).
These fits are used to extract the sensitivities reported in Fig.
2
d.
