A modified proof for Higman’s embedding theorem
V. H. Mikaelian

Abstract
. We suggest a briefer version for the proof of Higman’s embedding theorem stating that a finitely generated group can be embedded into a finitely presented group if and only if it is recursively presented. In particular, we shorten the main part of original proof establishing characterization of recursive relations in terms of benign subgroups in free groups. Also, the construction we suggest is adaptable for constructive embeddings of recursive groups into explicitly given finitely presented groups.



1991 Mathematics Subject Classification
20F05, 20E06, 20E07.

Introduction

This note is a part of our research on Higman embeddings, also including recent articles

Our first purpose here is to suggest a shorter and we hope considerably simpler version of the proof for Higman’s remarkable embedding theorem:

Theorem 1.1

(Theorem 1 in
[
10
]
)
.

A finitely generated group can be embedded in a finitely presented group if and only if it is recursively presented.

Recursive presentation for a group
G
presumes that there is a presentation
G=\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\mathrel{|}r_{1},r_{2},\ldots\,\rangle
such that the set of defining relations
\{r_{1},r_{2},\ldots\}
is recursive enumerable, i.e., it is the range of some recursive function.
This result establishes deep connections between the logical notion of recursion and theory of group.
For its applications we refer to
Ch. 12 of
[
28
]
,
Ch. IV of
[
12
]
,
Ch. I, IV, VI in
[
3
]
, and to the work of Ol’shanskii and Sapir
[
25
]
.

And the second purpose of the construction we suggest is that it is adaptable for
explicit
embeddings of recursive groups into finitely presented groups. In particular, using this embedding we in

[
22
]
construct explicit embeddings of the group
{\mdmathbb Q}
into finitely presented groups.
The question of the existence of such group embeddings is posed in Problem 14.10 (a) in Kourovka Notebook in 1999
[
11
]
, see points 1.1, 1.2 in
[
22
]
for details.

1.1.
The main steps of Higman’s proof

[
10
]
starts by Kleene’s formal characterization of partial recursive functions on the set of non-negative integers as the class of
functions that can be obtained from the zero, successor and
identity functions
using the operations of composition, primitive recursion, and minimization (see
[
8
,
27
]
or the newer text
[
5
]
).
Let
{\mdmathbb Z}
be the set of all integers, and
\mathcal{E}
be the set of all functions
f:{\mdmathbb Z}\to{\mdmathbb Z}
with finite support in the sense that
f(i)=0
for all but finitely many integers
i\in{\mdmathbb Z}
. To each such function
f\in{\mathcal{E}}
one can constructively assign a unique non-negative Gödel number.
This makes
\mathcal{E}
an effectively enumerable set, and one may put into correspondence to each subset
{\mathcal{B}}
of
{\mathcal{E}}
the respective set of non-negative integers corresponding to the functions
f\in{\mathcal{B}}
.
Then
{\mathcal{B}}
is
recursively enumerable
, if that set is the
range
of some recursive function.
Next, Section 2 in
[
10
]
gives a different characterization for recursively enumerable subsets of
{\mathcal{E}}
: two initial subsets
{\mathcal{Z}}
and
{\mathcal{S}}
of
{\mathcal{E}}
, and a series of operations on subsets of
{\mathcal{E}}
are introduced (see (
H
) in
2.2
below), and Theorem 3 states that
{\mathcal{B}}
is recursively enumerable if and only if it can be obtained from
{\mathcal{Z}}
and
{\mathcal{S}}
by operations (
H
).
Only after these preparations the group-theoretical argument starts in
[
10
]
. The key concept of
benign subgroup
is introduced (see
3.1
), and to each
{\mathcal{B}}
a specific subgroup
A_{\mathcal{B}}
of the free group
F=\langle a,b,c\rangle
of rank
3
is put into correspondence. The main result of Section 3 and of Section 4 in
[
10
]
is:

Theorem 1.2

(Theorem 4 in
[
10
]
)
.

The subset
\mathcal{B}
of
\mathcal{E}
is recursively enumerable if and only if
A_{\mathcal{B}}
is a benign subgroup in
F=\langle a,b,c\rangle
.

Then in final brief Section 5 the above theorem is generalized:
a subgroup of
any
finitely generated free group is recursively enumerable if and only if it is benign. The “Higman Rope Trick”, as it is sometimes
called
[
31
]
, closes the proof by showing how a recursively presented group can be embedded in a finitely presented group.
Here we suggest a shorter proof for Theorem
1.2
, and our sections
3
,
4
roughly correspond to sections 3, 4 in
[
10
]
.

We argument some passages that are just stated in

1.3.
Other proofs for Higman’s embedding theorem

Higman’s original proof is very complicated, and this motivated others to suggest alternative proofs for Theorem
1.1
.
Lindon and Schupp present in Section IV ​. ​7 of
[
12
]
a proof related to Valiev’s approach
[
30
]
.
Solving Hilbert’s Tenth Problem
[
14
]
establishes that a subset of
{\mdmathbb Z}^{n}
is recursively enumerable if and only if it is Diophantine.
The proof in
[
12
]
uses this Diophantine characterization, i.e., it relies on a “third party” result (which itself demands a complicated proof).

A proof reflecting Aanderaa’s work
[
1
]
is given by Rotman in Ch. 12 of
[
28
]
. It applies the auxiliary construction interpreting Turing machines via semigroups, and also uses the Boone-Britton group
[
6
,
7
]
. Group diagrams allow
[
28
]
to shorten the proof of
[
1
]
.

Besides the mentioned two well known textbooks
[
12
,
28
]
, other proofs can be found in the Appendix of Shoenfield’s textbook on logic
[
29
]
, in the article of Adyan and Durnev
[
2
]
, etc. Typically, they relay on auxiliary constructions, such as those built to show undecidability of the word problem in semigroups
[
26
,
13
]
and in groups

[
24
,
6
,
7
]
.

This comparison stresses importance of Higman’s straightforward approach of
mimicking recursion
by means of some elegant group-theoretical constructions only, without resorting to “third party” concepts.
A motivation of our note is to emphasize that Higman’s original idea still is one of the clearest approaches to handle this subject.

We would like to also announce the recent article
[
20
]
in which we use the current embedding as a basis to suggest an algorithms, called
H
-Machine, which explicitly builds the subset
{\mathcal{B}}
of
{\mathcal{E}}

for certain classes of groups,
such as the free abelian, metabelian, soluble, nilpotent groups, the groups

{\mdmathbb Q}
and
\mdmathbb C_{p^{\infty}}
, divisible abelian groups, etc.

.
Denote by

2.2.
The Higman operations on subsets of
{\mathcal{E}}

Define some specific subsets of
{\mathcal{E}}
:

{\mathcal{Z}}\!=\!\big\{(0)\big\},\;\;{\mathcal{S}}\!=\!\big\{(a,a+\!1)\mathrel{|}a\!\in\!{\mdmathbb Z}\big\},\;\;{\mathcal{E}}_{m}\!\!=\!\big\{(a_{0},\ldots,a_{m-1})\mathrel{|}a_{i}\!\in\!{\mdmathbb Z},\;i\!=\!0,\ldots,\!m-1\big\},\,m\!=\!0,\!1,\ldots

(
{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
consists of zero function only).
We will use the following operations from
[
10
]
:

(H)

\iota,\;\upsilon;\quad\quad\rho,\;\sigma,\;\tau,\;\theta,\;\zeta,\;\pi,\;\omega_{m}\;\;(\text{for each $m=1,2,\ldots$})

which we call
Higman operations
.
For any subsets
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
of
{\mathcal{E}}
define

\iota(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})=\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{B}
and

\upsilon(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})=\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B}
.
The rest of Higman operations are unary functions on the subsets of
\mathcal{E}
:

f\in\rho(\mathcal{A})
iff there is a
g\in\mathcal{A}
such that
f(i)=g(-i)
,

f\!\in\!\sigma(\mathcal{A})
iff there​ is​ a​
g\in\mathcal{A}
such​ that​
f(i)=g(i-1)
,

f\!\in\tau(\mathcal{A})
iff there is a
g\!\in\!\mathcal{A}
such that
f(0)\!=\!g(1)
,
f(1)\!=\!g(0)
and
f(i)\!=\!g(i)
for
i\!\neq\!0,1
,

f\in\theta(\mathcal{A})
iff there is a
g\in\mathcal{A}
such that
f(i)=g(2i)
,

f\in\zeta(\mathcal{A})
iff there is a
g\in\mathcal{A}
such that
f(i)=g(i)
for
i\neq 0
,

f\in\pi(\mathcal{A})
iff there is a
g\in\mathcal{A}
such that
f(i)=g(i)
for
i\leq 0
,

f\in\omega_{m}(\mathcal{A})
iff for every
i\in{\mdmathbb Z}
there is a
g=\big(f(mi),\;f(mi+1),\ldots,f(mi+m-1)\big)\in\mathcal{A}
. Since the support of any
f\in{\mathcal{E}}
is finite, either
\mathcal{A}
contains the zero function, or
\omega_{m}(\mathcal{A})=\emptyset
.

The more general operations
\alpha
and
\lambda
mentioned
[
10
]
are not used here.

Example 2.1
.

Some simple applications of Higman operations are easy to check:

\rho({\mathcal{S}})=\big\{f\in{\mathcal{E}}\mathrel{|}f(-1)=f(0)+1,f(i)=0\text{ for }i\neq-1,0\big\}
.

Thus,
\sigma\rho({\mathcal{S}})=\big\{(a+\!1,a)\mathrel{|}a\!\in\!{\mdmathbb Z}\big\}=\tau({\mathcal{S}})
.

Since
\zeta({\mathcal{Z}})=\big\{(a)\mathrel{|}a\in{\mdmathbb Z}\big\}=\big\{(a,0)\mathrel{|}n\in{\mdmathbb Z}\big\}

\iota\big(\!\tau({\mathcal{S}}),\zeta({\mathcal{Z}})\big)=\big\{(1,0)\big\}=\big\{(1)\big\}
.

As

\zeta(\sigma\zeta)^{2}({\mathcal{Z}})=\big\{(a_{0},a_{1},a_{2})\mathrel{|}a_{0},a_{1},a_{2}\in{\mdmathbb Z}\big\}={\mathcal{E}}_{3}

Since

\sigma^{3}\rho\pi({\mathcal{Z}})=\{f\in{\mathcal{E}}\mathrel{|}\ f(i)=0\text{ for }i\geq 3\}

Clearly,

.

\omega_{2}\big(\!\upsilon({\mathcal{S}},{\mathcal{Z}})\big)=\{f\in{\mathcal{E}}\mathrel{|}\ f(2i+1)=f(2i)+1\text{ \,or\,}f(2i+1)=f(2i)=0\text{ for any }i\in{\mdmathbb Z}\}
. Say,
(7,8,\;0,0,\;2,3)\in\omega_{2}\big(\!\upsilon({\mathcal{S}},{\mathcal{Z}})\big)

(7,8),(2,3)\in{\mathcal{S}}
and
(0,0)\in{\mathcal{Z}}
.
Also,
\omega_{2}({\mathcal{S}})=\emptyset

2.5.
Subgroups in free constructions

Lemma

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\Gamma=G*_{\varphi}\!H
be the free product of the groups
G
and
H
with amalgamated subgroups

A\leq G
and
B\leq H

with respect to the isomorphism

\varphi:A\to B
.
If
G^{\prime},H^{\prime}
respectively are subgroups of
G,H
, such that
for
A^{\prime}=G^{\prime}\cap A
and
B^{\prime}=H^{\prime}\cap B

we have

\varphi(A^{\prime})=B^{\prime}
, then for the subgroup
\Gamma^{\prime}=\langle G^{\prime},H^{\prime}\rangle
of
\Gamma
and for the restriction
\varphi^{\prime}
of
\varphi
on
A^{\prime}
we have:

(1)

\Gamma^{\prime}=G^{\prime}*_{\varphi^{\prime}}H^{\prime}
,

(2)

\Gamma^{\prime}\cap A=A^{\prime}
and
\Gamma^{\prime}\cap B=B^{\prime}
,

(3)

\Gamma^{\prime}\cap G=G^{\prime}
and
\Gamma^{\prime}\cap H=H^{\prime}
.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
\Gamma=G*_{A}H
,
and let
G^{\prime}\leq G
,
H^{\prime}\leq H
be subgroups for which
G^{\prime}\cap\,A=H^{\prime}\cap\,A
. Then for
\Gamma^{\prime}=\langle G^{\prime},H^{\prime}\rangle
and
A^{\prime}=G^{\prime}\cap\,A
we have:

(1)

\Gamma^{\prime}=G^{\prime}\!*_{A^{\prime}}H^{\prime}
, in particular, if
A\leq G^{\prime}\!,\,H^{\prime}
, then
\Gamma^{\prime}=G^{\prime}*_{A}H^{\prime}

(2)

\Gamma^{\prime}\cap\,A=A^{\prime}
, in particular, if

, then
\Gamma^{\prime}\cap\,A=A
;

(3)

\Gamma^{\prime}\cap\,G=G^{\prime}
and

\Gamma^{\prime}\cap\,H=H^{\prime}
.

2.6.
The “conjugates collecting” process

Let
{\mathfrak{X}}
and
{\mathfrak{Y}}

be any disjoint subsets in a group
G
. It is easy to see that any element
w\in\langle{\mathfrak{X}},{\mathfrak{Y}}\rangle
can be written as:

(2.1)

w=u\cdot v=x_{1}^{\pm v_{1}}x_{2}^{\pm v_{2}}\cdots x_{k}^{\pm v_{k}}\cdot v

with some
v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{k},\,v\in\langle{\mathfrak{Y}}\rangle
, and

x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k}\in{\mathfrak{X}}
.
Indeed, since
w
is in
\langle{\mathfrak{X}},{\mathfrak{Y}}\rangle
, present
w
as a product of elements from
{\mathfrak{X}}
, from
{\mathfrak{Y}}
and of their inverses. Then by grouping where necessary some elements from
{\mathfrak{Y}}
, and by adding some trivial elements we can rewrite it as:

(2.2)

w=z_{1}^{\vphantom{8}}x_{1}^{\pm 1}z_{2}^{\vphantom{8}}\,x_{2}^{\pm 1}z_{3}^{\vphantom{8}}\cdots z_{k}^{\vphantom{8}}\,x_{k}^{\pm 1}z_{k+1}^{\vphantom{8}}

where
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\in{\mathfrak{X}}
and

z_{1},\ldots,z_{k+1}\in\langle{\mathfrak{Y}}\rangle
.
For instance, if
{\mathfrak{X}}=\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\}
and

{\mathfrak{Y}}=\{y_{1},y_{2}\}
, then

w=x_{2}^{-1}y_{1}^{3}y_{2}^{\vphantom{8}}x_{1}^{2}x_{3}^{\vphantom{8}}
can be rewritten as

w=z_{1}\,x_{2}^{-1}z_{2}^{\vphantom{8}}x_{1}z_{3}^{\vphantom{8}}x_{1}z_{4}^{\vphantom{8}}x_{3}^{\vphantom{8}}z_{5}^{\vphantom{8}}
,
where

3.
The main properties and basic examples of benign subgroups

3.1.
Definition and main properties of benign subgroups

The central group-theoretical notion used in
[
10
]
to construct embeddings into finitely presented groups is the concept of benign subgroups. Higman gives three equivalent definitions of which we use the first one:

Definition 3.1
.

A subgroup
H
of a finitely generated group
G
is called a
benign subgroup
in
G
, if
G
can be embedded in a finitely presented group
K
which has a finitely generated subgroup
L
such that
G\cap L=H
.

As a first evident example of a benign subgroup one may take any finitely generated subgroup
H
in any finitely presented group
G
. Then we just have to choose
L\!=\!H
.
It is interesting to compare Definition
3.1
with Theorem V (a variation of the well-known theorem on embeddings of countable groups into 2-generator groups) in earlier article
[
9
]
.

The following properties have simple proofs covered in
[
10
]
by Lemma 3.6 and Lemma 3.7:

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated group with two benign subgroups
H,\,H^{\prime}
. Then:

(1)

H\cap H^{\prime}
is benign in
G
,

(2)

\langle H,H^{\prime}\rangle
is benign in
G
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
G,\,N
be finitely generated groups both possessing embeddings into some finitely presented groups, and let
\varphi:G\to N
be any homomorphism. Then:

(1)

if a subgroup
H
is benign in
G
, then its image
\varphi(H)
is benign in
N
,

(2)

if a subgroup
H
is benign in
N
, then its (complete) preimage
\varphi^{-1}(H)
is benign in
G
.

Remark 3.4
.

It is trivial to verify by Definition
3.1
that the subgroup
H
of a finitely generated group
G
is benign in
G
, provided that
H
is benign in a finitely generated group
G^{\prime}
containing
G
.
On the other hand, if we additionally require that
G^{\prime}
(and, therefore, also
G
) can be embedded into a finitely presented group, then

H
is benign in
G^{\prime}
, provided that
H
is benign in
G
. To see this just apply
(
1
) in
Lemma
3.3
to the identical embedding
G\to G^{\prime}
.
Combining these facts, let us following
[
10
]
agree to call
H

benign
(without specifying in
which
group
G
), whenever there is a finitely presented overgroup containing the groups discussed.

twice
, i.e., conjugate the above by
r^{2}
to get
the element
a^{b_{l_{4}}}
with

l_{4}=(0,0,0,\;0,0,0,\;2,5,3,\;7,2,4)
.
Next apply step 3 and step 1 again to conjugate

a^{b_{l_{4}}}
by

g_{f}
for
f=(7,2,4)
. We get the element

a^{b_{l_{5}}}
with

l_{5}=(7,2,4,\;0,0,0,\;2,5,3,\;7,2,4)
.
Finally, we again apply step 2, i.e., conjugate
a^{b_{l_{5}}}
by
r
to discover in
W_{{\mathcal{B}}}
the element
a^{b_{l}}=a_{l}
.
Since this procedure can easily be performed for an arbitrary
l\in\omega_{m}{\mathcal{B}}
, we get that
A_{\omega_{m}{\mathcal{B}}}\leq W_{{\mathcal{B}}}
.

Let us single out an auxiliary “slimmer” subgroup
′
in
\Omega
.
Since
\langle b,c\rangle\cap P=B_{0,m}
,
the subgroup
\big\langle\langle b,c\rangle,g,h,k\big\rangle
is equal to
\Sigma^{\prime}=\langle b,c\rangle*_{B_{0,m}}(g,h,k)
.
Since
\Sigma^{\prime}
contains each of
b,c,g,h,k
it also contains
L
. Thus,
\Sigma^{\prime}\cap R=L
, and using
(
1
) in Corollary
2.5

we see that
\langle\Sigma^{\prime},a\rangle
is equal to
\Pi^{\prime}=\Sigma^{\prime}*_{L}a
.
Finally, since
a,b,c\in\Pi^{\prime}
, the subgroup
\langle\Pi^{\prime},r\rangle
is equal to
{}^{\prime}=\Pi^{\prime}*_{\rho}r
.
The advantage of
′
is that it is an extension of
\langle b,c\rangle
by means of three “nested” HNN-extensions, and we, thus, possess the
full
list of defining relations of
′
.

Since any
w\in W_{{\mathcal{B}}}
also is in
′
, it can be brought to its normal form involving
r
and some elements from
\Pi^{\prime}
.
The latters can in turn be brought to normal forms involving
a
and some elements from
\Sigma^{\prime}
.
Then the latters can further be brought to normal forms involving
g,h,k
and some elements from
\langle b,c\rangle
.
I.e.,
w
can be brought to a
unique “nested” normal form
.
Detect the cases when it involves
a,b,c
only.
The only relations of
′
involving
g,h,k
are
a^{g_{f}}\!=a^{b_{f}}
. Thus, the only way by which
g,h,k
may be eliminated in the normal form is to have in
w
subwords of type
g_{f}^{\!-1}a\,g_{f}^{\vphantom{8}}=a^{g_{f}}
​,  to replace them by respective
a^{b_{f}}\in\langle a,b,c\rangle
.
If after that some subwords
g_{f}
still remain, then three scenario cases are possible:

Case 1
. We may have a subword of type
w^{\prime}=g_{f}^{-1}a^{b_{l}}g_{f}^{\vphantom{8}}
for
such an
l
that
l(i)=0
for
i=0,\ldots,m-1
.
Check example of step 1, when this is achieved for
l=l_{2}=(0,0,0,\;7,2,4)
and
f=(2,5,3)
.
Then just replace
w^{\prime}
by
a^{b_{l_{3}}}
for an
l_{3}\in\omega_{m}{\mathcal{B}}
(e.g.
l_{3}=(2,5,3,\;7,2,4)
).

Case 2
. If
w^{\prime}=g_{f}^{-1}a^{b_{l}}g_{f}
,  but the condition
l(i)=0
fails for an
i=0,\ldots,m-1
, then
g_{f}
does
not
commute with
b_{l}
, so we cannot apply the relation
a^{g_{f}}\!=a^{b_{f}}
, and so
w\notin\langle a,b,c\rangle
.
Turning to example in steps 1–3, notice that for, say,
f=(2,5,3)\in{\mathcal{B}}
we may
never
get something like

a^{(g_{f})^{\,2}}\!\!=(a^{b_{0}^{2}\,b_{1}^{5}\,b_{2}^{3}})^{g_{f}}\!\!=a^{(b_{0}^{2}\,b_{1}^{5}\,b_{2}^{3})^{2}}
because
g_{f}
does not commute with
b_{0},b_{1},b_{2}
. That is, all the
new
functions
l
we get are from
\omega_{m}{\mathcal{B}}

only
.

Case 3
. If
g_{f}
is in
w
, but not in a subword
g_{f}^{-1}a^{b_{l}}g_{f}
, we again have
w\notin\langle a,b,c\rangle
.

This means, if
w\in\langle a,b,c\rangle
, then elimination of
g,h,k
turns
w
to a product of elements from
\langle r\rangle
and of some
a^{b_{l}}
for some
l\in\omega_{m}{\mathcal{B}}
(
a
also is of that type, as
(0)\in{\mathcal{B}}
).
Now apply
2.6
for

{\mathfrak{X}}=\{a^{b_{l}}\mathrel{|}l\in\omega_{m}{\mathcal{B}}\}

and
{\mathfrak{Y}}=\{r\}
to state that

w
is a product of some power
r^{k}
and of some elements each of which is an
a^{b_{l}}
conjugated by a power
r^{n_{i}}
of
r
.
These conjugates are in
\omega_{m}{\mathcal{B}}
, and so
w\in\langle a,b,c\rangle
if and only if
k=0
, i.e., if

w\in A_{\omega_{m}{\mathcal{B}}}
.

It remains to notice that since
F=\langle a,b,c\rangle
is a free group,
F\cap W_{{\mathcal{B}}}=A_{\omega_{m}{\mathcal{B}}}
.
Since
A_{{\mathcal{B}}}
is benign, its image

\langle g_{f}\mathrel{|}f\in{\mathcal{B}}\rangle
also is benign. Then

W_{{\mathcal{B}}}=\langle g_{f}\!,\,a,\,r\mathrel{|}f\in{\mathcal{B}}\rangle
is benign by
(
2
) in Lemma
3.2
, and
A_{\omega_{m}{\mathcal{B}}}
is benign by (
1
) in Lemma
3.2
.
