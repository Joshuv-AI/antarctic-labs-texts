𝒳 \mathcal{X} -arability of mixed quantum states
Harm Derksen, Nathaniel Johnston, Benjamin Lovitz

Abstract
The problem of determining when entanglement is present in a quantum system is one of the most active areas of research in quantum physics. Depending on the setting at hand, different notions of entanglement (or lack thereof) become relevant. Examples include separability (of bosons, fermions, and distinguishable particles), Schmidt number, biseparability, entanglement depth, and bond dimension. In this work, we propose and study a unified notion of separability, which we call 𝒳 \mathcal{X} -arability , that captures a wide range of applications including these. For a subset (more specifically, an algebraic variety) of pure states 𝒳 \mathcal{X} , we say that a mixed quantum state is 𝒳 \mathcal{X} -arable if it lies in the convex hull of 𝒳 \mathcal{X} . We develop unified tools and provable guarantees for 𝒳 \mathcal{X} -arability, which already give new results for the standard separability problem. Our results include: • An 𝒳 \mathcal{X} -tensions hierarchy of semidefinite programs for 𝒳 \mathcal{X} -arability (generalizing the symmetric extensions hierarchy for separability), and a new de Finetti theorem for fermionic separability. • A hierarchy of eigencomputations for optimizing a Hermitian operator over 𝒳 \mathcal{X} , with applications to 𝒳 \mathcal{X} -tanglement witnesses and polynomial optimization. • A hierarchy of linear systems for the 𝒳 \mathcal{X} -tangled subspace problem, with improved polynomial time guarantees even for the standard entangled subspace problem, in both the generic and worst case settings.

Introduction

Let
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathcal{H}_{m}
be a (finite dimensional, complex) Hilbert space over
m

mixed state
)
\rho\in\mathrm{D}\left(\mathcal{H}\right)

\rho=\sum_{i}p_{i}\;\psi_{i,1}\psi_{i,1}^{*}\otimes\dots\otimes\psi_{i,m}\psi_{i,m}^{*}
. Conversely,

,
HHHH09
]
.

More generally, it is natural to ask if a state
\rho
can be prepared as a probabilistic mixture of pure states lying in some other set
\mathcal{X}

\rho

\mathcal{X}
-arable

\rho
is
\mathcal{X}
-tangled

\mathcal{X}
-arable. We work in the general setting when

is a

f_{1},\dots,f_{p}

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}=\{\psi\psi^{*}:f_{1}(\psi)=\cdots=f_{p}(\psi)=0\quad\text{and}\quad\lVert\mspace{1mu}\psi\mspace{1mu}\rVert=1\}.

(1)

We say that

cut out

.

-separable states,

-producible states, states of bounded Schmidt rank, matrix product states, and tree tensor network states.

\mathcal{X}
-arability thus captures a wealth of applications that are central to quantum entanglement theory, including separability (of both distinguishable and indistinguishable particles)

,

.

1.1
\mathcal{X}
-tensions hierarchy for
\mathcal{X}
-arability

We obtain the following semidefinite programming hierarchy for deciding if a state is
\mathcal{X}
-arable (see Section
3
):

Theorem 1.3

(
\mathcal{X}
-tensions hierarchy)
.

Let
\rho\in\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{H})
be a state. Then
\rho
is
\mathcal{X}
-arable if and only if for all
k
there exists
\sigma_{k}\in\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k})
for which
\operatorname{Tr}_{k-1}(\sigma_{k})=\rho
and
\operatorname{Im}(\sigma_{k})\subseteq\mathcal{X}^{k}
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{k}:=\spn\{\psi^{\otimes k}:\psi\psi^{*}\in\mathcal{X}\}.

(2)

We call
\sigma_{k}
an
\mathcal{X}
-tension
of
\rho
. When
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{Sep}}
is the set of pure product states, this specializes to the symmetric extensions hierarchy of
[
DPS04
]
with all
m
subsystems extended simultaneously. More generally, one can replace
\mathcal{X}^{k}
with
I_{k}^{\perp}
, where
I
is the ideal generated by any
f_{1},\dots,f_{p}
cutting out
\mathcal{X}
(see Section
1.3
below). While it is easy to describe
\mathcal{X}^{k}
(or
I_{k}^{\perp}
) abstractly as in (
2
), it can be difficult to write down an explicit basis. We give explicit descriptions of
\mathcal{X}^{k}
(or
I_{k}^{\perp}
) for all of the varieties in Example
1.1
.

In the case of fermionic separability we prove a
quantitative
version of this result, which we call a
de Finetti theorem for fermionic separability
(see Section
3.2
). The key ingredient is the observation that
\mathcal{X}^{k}
forms an irreducible representation of the local unitary group in this case, allowing us to apply the results of
[
KM09
]
. Related results are proven in
[
CF12
,
KLC13
,
KZE17
]
, although these use a mode-partitioned notion of fermionic separability that is closer to the distinguishable-particle setting. By contrast, our result applies to the particle-level notion of fermionic separability introduced in
[
GKM11
]
(see Example

). These and other notions of entanglement in fermionic systems have found widespread applications
[
BFFM20
]
.

We illustrate the example of Schmidt number. Let
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2}
and let
\mathcal{X}_{r}\subseteq\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{H})
be the set of pure states of Schmidt rank at most
r

-arable is called the

of

. In the following, let
\Pi_{i,r+1}^{{\wedge}}
be the projection onto the antisymmetric subspace of
\mathcal{H}_{i}^{\otimes r+1}
, and let

be the symmetric subspace.

Corollary 1.4
.

\rho\in\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{H})
is
\mathcal{X}_{r}
-arable if and only if for all
k\geq r+1
there exists
\sigma_{k}\in\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k})
for which
\operatorname{Tr}_{k-1}(\sigma_{k})=\rho
and
\operatorname{Im}(\sigma_{k})\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{r}^{k}
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{r}^{k}=\operatorname{Ker}(\Pi_{1,r+1}^{{\wedge}}\otimes\Pi_{2,r+1}^{{\wedge}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes k-r-1})\cap S^{k}(\mathcal{H}).

(3)

We note that the hierarchy in Theorem
1.3
(and in this corollary) can be strengthened at each level by additionally imposing that
\sigma_{k}
is has positive partial transpose (PPT) with respect to some bipartitions of
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k}
(see Remark
3.3
). Similar statements apply to the other hierarchies below.

1.2
Hermitian optimization over
\mathcal{X}

Using Theorem
1.3
, we can obtain hierarchies for constrained Hermitian optimization problems of the form

\displaystyle H_{\mathcal{X}}^{\downarrow}:=\min_{\psi\psi^{*}\in\mathcal{X}}\langle\psi,H\psi\rangle\quad\quad\text{or}\quad\quad H_{\mathcal{X}}^{\uparrow}:=\max_{\psi\psi^{*}\in\mathcal{X}}\langle\psi,H\psi\rangle,

(4)

where
H\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{H})
is any Hermitian operator. Even in the special case when
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{Sep}}
, this problem has found many applications including computing the geometric measure of entanglement, determining the performance of QMA(2) protocols, and determining the ground-state energy of mean-field Hamiltonians. Moreover, Ref.
[
HM10
]
contains a list of 21 equivalent or closely related problems in quantum information and theoretical computer science. As further motivation,
H
forms a separating hyperplane for the set of
\mathcal{X}
-arable states (which we call an
\mathcal{X}
-tanglement witness
) if and only if
H_{\mathcal{X}}^{\downarrow}\geq 0
and
H
has at least one negative eigenvalue.

We use Theorem
1.3
to obtain a hierarchy of eigencomputations for the optimization problem (
4
). In the following, let
\lambda_{\min}(\cdot)
be the minimum eigenvalue.

Corollary 1.5
.

Let
\Pi_{\mathcal{X}^{k}}
be the orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{X}^{k}
, and let
\nu_{k}=\lambda_{\min}(\Pi_{\mathcal{X}^{k}}(H\otimes\mathds{1}^{\otimes k-1})\Pi_{\mathcal{X}^{k}})
. Then
\nu_{1}\leq\nu_{2}\leq\dots
, and
\lim_{k}\nu_{k}=H_{\mathcal{X}}^{\downarrow}
.

In particular, this gives a hierarchy of eigencomputations for determining if
H
is an
\mathcal{X}
-tanglement witness. We make this hierarchy explicit for all of the varieties mentioned in Example
1.1
.

Corollary
1.5
can also be proven as a consequence of
[
CD99
, Theorem 1]
if one is familiar with vector bundles. In Section
7
we apply Corollary
1.5
to constrained Hermitian polynomial optimization. In particular, we prove that the Hermitian sum of squares hierarchy of
[
DP09
]
, which is based on semidefinite programming, is equivalent to a hierarchy of
eigencomputations
in some settings, which can lead to computational savings.

1.3
\mathcal{X}
-tangled subspaces

Entangled subspaces
are subspaces of
\mathcal{H}
that exhibit some preset notion of entanglement
[
GW07
]
. We define an
\mathcal{X}
-tangled subspace
to be a subspace
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
that does not intersect
\mathcal{X}
. This captures many well-studied notions of entangled subspaces, including completely entangled subspaces, genuinely entangled subspaces, subspaces of high entanglement depth,
r
-entangled subspaces, and their bosonic/fermionic variants
[
Par04
,
CMW08
,
LJ22
,
DVA24
]
. An immediate application of
\mathcal{X}
-tangled subspaces is that any mixed quantum state supported on an
\mathcal{X}
-tangled subspace is
\mathcal{X}
-tangled.
\mathcal{X}
-tangled subspaces can also be used to construct
\mathcal{X}
-tanglement witnesses
[
Hor97
,
BDM+99
]
. Numerous other applications have appeared in recent years, including quantum error correction
[
GW07
,
HG20
]
and quantum cryptography
[
SS19
]
.

More generally, different
measures
of entanglement of subspaces have found applications in entanglement theory and quantum communication theory, most notably Hastings’ disproof of additivity of the Holevo capacity
[
HLW06
,
Has09
,
ASW11
,
DA19b
,
ZZZ24
]
. We introduce and study the
geometric measure of
\mathcal{X}
-tanglement
(GM
\mathcal{X}
) of
\mathcal{U}
, given by

\displaystyle E_{\mathcal{X}}(\mathcal{U}):=1-(\Pi_{\mathcal{U}})_{\mathcal{X}}^{\uparrow}\;,

(5)

where
\Pi_{\mathcal{U}}
denotes the orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{U}
and
(\Pi_{\mathcal{U}})_{\mathcal{X}}^{\uparrow}
is defined in (
4
). Note that (
E_{\mathcal{X}}(\mathcal{U})>0\iff\mathcal{U}
is
\mathcal{X}
-tangled). This generalizes the well-studied geometric measure of entanglement for pure states
[
WG03
]
as well as notions for subspaces studied in
[
DA19b
,
ZZZ24
]
. An immediate consequence of Corollary
1.5
is a hierarchy of eigencomputations for computing
E_{\mathcal{X}}(\mathcal{U})
.

Corollary 1.6
.

For each positive integer
k
, let
\nu_{k}
be the maximum eigenvalue of

\displaystyle\Pi_{\mathcal{X}^{k}}(\Pi_{\mathcal{U}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes k-1})\Pi_{\mathcal{X}^{k}}.

(6)

Then
\nu_{1}\geq\nu_{2}\geq\dots
and
E_{\mathcal{X}}(\mathcal{U})=1-\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{k}
.

We make this hierarchy explicit for all of the examples mentioned in Example
1.1
. Let us focus on the special case of this hierarchy that simply checks if
\mathcal{U}
is
\mathcal{X}
-tangled or not (see Section
6.5
):

Corollary 1.7

(Nullstellensatz hierarchy)
.

\mathcal{U}
is
\mathcal{X}
-tangled if and only if
\mathcal{X}^{k}\cap\mathcal{U}^{\otimes k}=\{0\}
for some
k
.

We call this the
Nullstellensatz hierarchy
because it can also be derived from Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz. For special cases of varieties
\mathcal{X}
, this specializes to the hierarchies studied in
[
JLV22
]
. We generalize and improve upon the polynomial time guarantees given in
[
JLV22
]
for this hierarchy, both in the generic and worst case settings.

1.3.1
Generic degree bounds for the Nullstellensatz hierarchy

While the
\mathcal{X}
-tangled subspace problem is NP Hard in the worst case, we prove polynomial time guarantees when
\mathcal{U}
is generically chosen (or “typical”). The following theorem shows that the Nullstellensatz hierarchy certifies
\mathcal{X}
-tanglement of generically chosen subspaces in polynomial time, up to an arbitrarily small multiplicative loss in dimension.

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be any of the varieties listed in Example
1.1
, let
0<\varepsilon<1
be arbitrary, and let
N=\dim(\mathcal{H})
be sufficiently large. Then a generically chosen subspace
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
of dimension
\dim(\mathcal{U})=(1-\varepsilon)N
is
\mathcal{X}
-tangled, and this is certified by the Nullstellensatz hierarchy in polynomial time.

This improves the genericity guarantees of
[
JLV22
]
, which hold only for particular choices of
\varepsilon
. For example, if
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}_{1}
is the set of pure bipartite product states, then the cited work only applies when
\varepsilon\geq 3/4
. Our improvement comes as a result of using more sophisticated algebraic techniques. We note that the work
[
JLV23
]
proposes a related algorithm to
recover
elements of
\mathcal{U}
contained in
\mathcal{X}
(see Remark
6.10
).

We prove Theorem
1.8
by showing that a generically chosen subspace
\mathcal{U}
can be certified
\mathcal{X}
-tangled at a constant level (or
degree
)
k
of the Nullstellensatz hierarchy. Theorem
1.8
is derived from the following general-purpose bound proven in Section
6
:

Theorem 1.9

(Generic degree bound)
.

Let
N=\dim(\mathcal{H})
, and let
s
and
k
be positive integers. If

\displaystyle\dim(\mathcal{X}^{k})<\binom{N-s+k}{k},

(7)

then a generically chosen
s
-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
is
\mathcal{X}
-tangled, and satisfies
\mathcal{X}^{k}\cap\mathcal{U}^{\otimes k}=\{0\}
.

1.3.2
Worst case degree bounds for the Nullstellensatz hierarchy

We also analyze the worst-case performance of the Nullstellensatz hierarchy. Remarkably, the Nullstellensatz hierarchy is guaranteed to terminate (i.e. detect every
\mathcal{X}
-tangled subspace) at a finite degree
k
; something that is known not to be possible for separability hierarchies such as symmetric extensions
[
Faw21
]
. Moreover, we use algebraic-geometric techniques to give explicit upper bounds on (or even determine exactly) the worst-case degree
k
for all of the varieties in Example
1.1
(see Table
1
).

Let us start with the example
\mathcal{X}_{r}\subseteq\mathrm{P}(\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n_{2}})
; the set of pure states of Schmidt rank at most
r
. Surprisingly, our results show that the
\mathcal{X}_{r}
-tangled subspace problem has a worst case polynomial time algorithm when
n_{1}
(or
n_{2}
) are fixed:

Theorem 1.10
.

The worst case degree required by the Nullstellensatz hierarchy to certify
\mathcal{X}_{r}
-tanglement is precisely
k=r(\min\{n_{1},n_{2}\}-r)+1
. In particular, the Nullstellensatz hierarchy gives a worst case polynomial time algorithm for the
\mathcal{X}_{r}
-tangled subspace problem when
n_{1}
(or
n_{2}
) are fixed.

For example, consider the specific case of checking entanglement of a qubit–qudit subspace, i.e.
r=1,n_{1}=2
. Then this theorem shows that the
k=2
level of the hierarchy is all that is needed, so entanglement of a subspace in this case can be determined by solving a
\binom{n_{2}}{2}\times\binom{\dim\mathcal{U}+1}{2}
linear system (see Section
6.5
). Furthermore, code that does this is provided in
[
JLV22
]
(although it was not known at that time that the code gave an exact answer when
k=2
in this case).

To describe our worst-case bounds more generally, it will be convenient to describe the Nullstellensatz hierarchy in greater generality. Let
I=\{\sum_{i}f_{i}g_{i}:g_{i}\text{ is a polynomial}\}
be the ideal generated by
f_{1},\dots,f_{p}
, let
R_{k}
be the set of homogeneous polynomials on
\mathcal{H}
of degree
k
, and let
I_{k}=I\cap R_{k}
. Similarly, let
I(\mathcal{U})
be the ideal generated by the linear equations defining
\mathcal{U}
. The
k
-th degree of the
Nullstellensatz hierarchy
checks if
I_{k}+I(\mathcal{U})_{k}=R_{k}
. If this equality holds, then
\mathcal{U}
is
\mathcal{X}
-tangled. If
I=I(\mathcal{X})
is the set of
all
polynomials vanishing on
\mathcal{X}
(which is perhaps more than enough to simply
cut out

\mathcal{X}
), then this equality is equivalent to
\mathcal{X}^{k}\cap\mathcal{U}^{\otimes k}=\{0\}
, reproducing the simplified hierarchy described above (see Section
6.5
).

To state our worst-case degree bounds, we require the technical notions of
Cohen-Macaulayness
of
R/I
and the
(Castelnuovo-Mumford) regularity

\mathrm{reg}(R/I)
, which are defined in Section
6
.

Theorem 1.11

(Worst case degree bound)
.

Let
N=\dim(\mathcal{H})
, and let
\mathcal{X}
be a variety cut out by homogeneous polynomials
f_{1},\dots,f_{p}
of degree at most
d
. Then degree
k=N(d-1)+1
of the Nullstellensatz hierarchy suffices to certify any
\mathcal{X}
-tangled subspace. If
R/I
is Cohen-Macaulay, then the worst-case degree is precisely
\mathrm{reg}(R/I)+1
.

This theorem applies to all of the varieties mentioned in Example
1.1
; see Table
1
. Furthermore, many of these varieties satisfy the Cohen-Macaulay condition, allowing us to precisely determine the worst-case degree required. Since the
\mathcal{X}
-tangled subspace problem is NP hard, we can expect the worst-case degree
k
to be at least linear in
N=\dim(\mathcal{H})
in general
[
BKS17
]
. Conversely, the degree bound
N(d-1)+1
appearing in Theorem
1.11
shows that linear scaling is often sufficient, since
d
is constant in
N
for many varieties of interest.

Background and notation

In this section we review some background and notation for this work. See e.g.
[
NC00
,
Wil13
,
Wat18
]
for more details on quantum information theory, and
[
Sha13
,

,
Har13
]

,

Let
\mathcal{H}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{H}_{m}
be (finite dimensional, complex) Hilbert spaces, and let
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathcal{H}_{m}
be the Hilbert space with Hermitian form
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
induced from the Hermitian forms on
\mathcal{H}_{i}
, which we take to be antilinear in the first argument. Let
\mathcal{H}^{*}
be the dual space. The Hermitian inner product defines an antilinear isomorphism
\mathcal{H}\cong\mathcal{H}^{*}
given by
\psi^{*}=\langle\psi,-\rangle
. Let
(\cdot,\cdot):\mathcal{H}^{*}\times\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be the bilinear form
(f,\psi)=f(\psi)
. Let
N=\dim(\mathcal{H})
, and let
e_{1},\dots,e_{N}\in\mathcal{H}
be an orthonormal basis with dual basis
x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\in\mathcal{H}^{*}
.

For a Hilbert space
\mathcal{J}
, let
\mathrm{Hom}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{H})
be the set of linear maps (homomorphisms) from
\mathcal{J}
to
\mathcal{H}
, Let
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{H})=\mathrm{Hom}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{H})
be the set of endomorphisms of
H
, let
\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H})
be the set of unitary operators, let
\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{H})\subseteq\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{H})
be the set of Hermitian operators, let
\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{H})\subseteq\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{H})
be the set of positive semidefinite operators, and let
\mathrm{D}\left(\mathcal{H}\right)\subseteq\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{H})
be the set of
density operators
(or
mixed states
, or simply
states
): positive semidefinite operators of trace one. Let
\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{H})\subseteq\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{H})
be the set of
pure states
: rank-one states, i.e. states that can be written as
\psi\psi^{*}
for a unit vector
\psi\in\mathcal{H}
.

For a subset
S\subseteq[m]
, let
\operatorname{Tr}_{S}:\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{H})\rightarrow\mathrm{End}(\bigotimes_{i\notin S}\mathcal{H}_{i})
be the partial trace
\operatorname{Tr}_{S}=\mathds{1}_{\otimes_{i\notin S}\mathcal{H}_{i}}\otimes\operatorname{Tr}_{\otimes_{i\in S}\mathcal{H}_{i}}
. For an integer
i\in[d]
let
\operatorname{Tr}_{i}:\mathrm{End}(S^{d}(\mathcal{H}))\rightarrow\mathrm{End}(S^{d-i}(\mathcal{H}))
be the partial trace over any
i
copies of
\mathcal{H}
(it does not matter which).

Let
\lambda_{\min}(\cdot)
and
\lambda_{\max}(\cdot)
be the minimum and maximum eigenvalues, respectively. For an integer tuple
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d}),
let
|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|=\alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{d}
.

2.1
Symmetric algebra, ideals, and varieties

For a permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{d}
, let
U_{\sigma}\in\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes d})
be the corresponding permutation of tensor factors
U_{\sigma}(\psi_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes\psi_{d})=\psi_{\sigma^{-1}(1)}\otimes\dots\otimes\psi_{\sigma^{-1}(d)}
, extended linearly. The
symmetric subspace

S^{d}(\mathcal{H})\subseteq\mathcal{H}^{\otimes d}
is the subspace of vectors
v
for which
U_{\sigma}v=v
for all
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{d}
. Let
\Pi_{d}=\frac{1}{d!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{d}}U_{\sigma}
be the orthogonal projection onto the symmetric subspace. The
antisymmetric subspace

\Lambda^{d}(\mathcal{H})\subseteq\mathcal{H}^{\otimes d}
is the set of vectors
v
for which
U_{\sigma}v=\mathrm{sign}(\sigma)v
for all
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{d}
. The antisymmetric subspace is spanned by vectors of the form
v_{1}{\wedge}\cdots{\wedge}v_{d}:=\frac{1}{d!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{d}}\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)v_{\sigma(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{\sigma(d)}
.

For
f\in S^{d}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
and
\psi\in\mathcal{H}
we use the shorthand
f(\psi):=(f,\psi^{\otimes d})
. For
g\in S^{c}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
we define
f\cdot g:=\Pi_{c+d}(f\otimes g)\in S^{c+d}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
, where
\Pi_{c+d}
is the projection onto
S^{c+d}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
. This makes
S^{\bullet}(\mathcal{H}^{*}):=\bigoplus_{d=0}^{\infty}S^{d}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
into an algebra, called the
symmetric algebra
. Let
S^{\underline{d}}(\mathcal{H}^{*})=\bigoplus_{c=0}^{d}S^{c}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
.

Note that
S^{d}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
is isomorphic to the space
\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{H}]_{d}
of homogeneous degree
d
polynomials on
\mathcal{H}
, by the map which sends
\ell^{\otimes d}
to
\ell^{d}
for
\ell\in\mathcal{H}^{*}
, extended linearly. Under this isomorphism,
f\cdot g
corresponds to the product of polynomials. For this reason, we often refer to elements of
S^{\underline{d}}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
as
polynomials
and elements of
S^{d}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
as
homogeneous polynomials
(or
forms
) of degree
d
.

An
ideal

I\subseteq S^{\bullet}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
is a linear subspace for which
S^{\bullet}(\mathcal{H}^{*})\cdot I\subseteq I
. For a subset
J\subseteq S^{\bullet}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
, let
\langle J\rangle:=\{\sum_{i}f_{i}g_{i}:f_{i}\in S^{\bullet}(\mathcal{H}^{*}),g_{i}\in J\}
be the ideal generated by
J
. By Hilbert’s basis theorem, every ideal is generated by finitely many polynomials
I=\langle f_{1},\dots,f_{p}\rangle
. An ideal
I
is
homogeneous
if it can be generated by (finitely many) homogeneous polynomials
f_{i}\in S^{d_{i}}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
. We say that
I
is
generated in degree (at most)
d
if one can take
d_{i}=d
(or
d_{i}\leq d
). It is a standard fact that if
I
is homogeneous then it can be written as
I=\bigoplus_{d=0}^{\infty}I_{d}
, with each
I_{d}\subseteq S^{d}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
a linear subspace called the
degree-
d
component
of
I
. For a positive integer
c
let
I_{\underline{c}}=\bigoplus_{d=0}^{c}I_{d}
.

For a subspace
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
, let
\mathcal{U}^{\perp}\subseteq\mathcal{H}^{*}
be the orthogonal complement of
\mathcal{U}
with respect to the bilinear form
(\cdot,\cdot)
, and let
\Pi_{\mathcal{U}}\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{H})
be the orthogonal projection (with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
) onto
\mathcal{U}
. For a homogeneous ideal
I\subseteq S^{\bullet}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
with degree-
d
component
I_{d}\subseteq S^{d}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
, we will often consider
I_{d}^{\perp}\subseteq S^{d}(\mathcal{H})
. Let
\Pi_{I,d}:=\Pi_{I_{d}^{\perp}}\in\mathrm{Pos}(S^{d}(\mathcal{H}))
, and let
\Pi_{d}:=\Pi_{S^{d}(\mathcal{H})}\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes d})
. We will often view
\Pi_{I,d}
as an element of
\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes d})
by setting it to zero on the orthogonal complement to
S^{d}(\mathcal{H})
in
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes d}
.

For a homogeneous ideal
I
, let
V(I)\subseteq P(\mathcal{H})
be the set of pure states
\psi\psi^{*}
for which
f(\psi)=0
for all
f\in I
(homogeneity ensures that this condition does not depend on phase). A
(projective) variety

\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{H})
is a subset of the form
\mathcal{X}=V(I)
for a homogeneous ideal
I
. One then says that
I

cuts out

\mathcal{X}
. The
ideal of
\mathcal{X}
, denoted
I(\mathcal{X})
, is the set of all polynomials vanishing on
\mathcal{X}
.
A special case of Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz is the following:

Theorem 2.1

(Hilbert’s weak nullstellensatz)
.

Let
I\subseteq S^{\bullet}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
be a homogeneous ideal. Then
{V(I)=\varnothing}
if and only if
I_{k}=S^{k}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
for
k\gg 0
.

3
\mathcal{X}
-arability and
\mathcal{X}
-tendability

Let
I\subseteq S^{\bullet}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
be a homogeneous ideal and let
\mathcal{X}=V(I)\subseteq\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{H})
the associated (projective) variety. We say that a state
\rho\in\mathrm{D}\left(\mathcal{H}\right)
is
\mathcal{X}
-arable
if
\rho\in\mathrm{conv}(\mathcal{X})
, and otherwise we that
\rho
is
\mathcal{X}
-tangled
. Our main result in this section is an
\mathcal{X}
-tensions
hierarchy for
\mathcal{X}
-arability, which generalizes the well-known symmetric extensions hierarchy for separability
[
DPS04
]
. We use this result to obtain explicit hierarchies for all of the varieties in Example
1.1
. In the case of fermionic separability, we also prove a
quantum de Finetti theorem
, giving quantitative convergence guarantees for this hierarchy.

Recall that
I_{k}^{\perp}\subseteq S^{k}(\mathcal{H})
is the orthogonal complement to
I_{k}\subseteq S^{k}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
with respect to the bilinear pairing (see Section
2
for more details).

Definition 3.1
.

For a state
\rho\in\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{H})
, a
(k,\mathcal{X})
-tension
of
\rho
is a state
\sigma_{k}\in\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k})
for which the following two properties hold:

1.

\mathrm{Im}(\sigma_{k})\subseteq I_{k}^{\perp}
, and

2.

\operatorname{Tr}_{k-1}(\sigma_{k})=\rho
.

We say
\rho
is
(k,\mathcal{X})
-tendable
if there exists a
(k,\mathcal{X})
-tension of
\rho
.

This is a slight abuse of notation, as a
(k,\mathcal{X})
-tension depends on the ideal
I
that one chooses to cut out
\mathcal{X}
.

Theorem 3.2

(
\mathcal{X}
-tensions hierarchy for
\mathcal{X}
-arability)
.

Let
\rho\in\mathrm{D}\left(\mathcal{H}\right)
be a state. Then
\rho
is
\mathcal{X}
-arable if and only if
\rho
is
(k,\mathcal{X})
-tendable for all
k
.

Proof.

If
\rho
is
\mathcal{X}
-arable, then
\rho=\sum_{i}p_{i}\psi_{i}\psi_{i}^{*}
for some
\psi_{i}\psi_{i}^{*}\in\mathcal{X}
and probability vector
p
. Then
\rho
is
(k,\mathcal{X})
-tendable to
\sigma_{k}:=\sum_{i}p_{i}(\psi_{i}\psi_{i}^{*})^{\otimes k}
.

Conversely, let
I
be generated in degree at most
d
, and for each
k\geq d
let
\tau_{k}=\operatorname{Tr}_{k-d}(\sigma_{k})
. By compactness of the set of density matrices, we can find a subsequence
\{\tau_{k_{j}}\}
so that
\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\tau_{k_{j}}
exists (call it
\tau
). Since
\operatorname{Im}(\sigma_{k})\subseteq S^{k}(\mathcal{H})
,
\tau
has a symmetric extension to
k_{j}
copies for arbitrarily large values of
k_{j}
, so
\tau\in\mathrm{conv}\{(\psi\psi^{*})^{\otimes d}:\psi\in\mathcal{H}\}
by
[
DPS04
]
(or the bosonic quantum de Finetti theorem reproduced in Theorem
3.14
below). Let
\tau=\sum_{i}p_{i}(\psi_{i}\psi_{i}^{*})^{\otimes d}
with each
p_{i}>0
. Since
\operatorname{Im}(\tau)\subseteq I_{d}^{\perp}
, it follows that
\psi_{i}^{\otimes d}\in I_{d}^{\perp}
for all
i
, so
\psi_{i}\psi_{i}^{*}\in\mathcal{X}
for all
i
. Hence,
\rho=\operatorname{Tr}_{d-1}(\tau)=\sum_{i}p_{i}\psi_{i}\psi_{i}^{*}
is
\mathcal{X}
-arable.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

Theorem
3.2
can be strengthened at each level by requiring that the
(k,\mathcal{X})
-tension
\sigma_{k}
has positive partial transpose (PPT) with respect to some bipartitions of
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k}
(see
[
Per96
,
HHH01
]
or
[
Wat18
]
), which also results in a complete hierarchy for
\mathcal{X}
-arability. This remark applies to many of the other results in this work.

3.1
Computing
I_{k}^{\perp}

In order to run the
\mathcal{X}
-tensions hierarchy described in Theorem
3.2
, one needs to find a homogeneous ideal
I
that cuts out out
\mathcal{X}
, and to describe
I_{k}^{\perp}
. Abstractly, one can take
I=I(\mathcal{X})
, in which case

\displaystyle I_{k}^{\perp}=\spn\{\psi^{\otimes k}:\psi\psi^{*}\in\mathcal{X}\}

(8)

(we referred to this space as
\mathcal{X}^{k}
in the introduction). However, it can be difficult to write down a basis for this space. The conceptually cleanest way to do this is to decompose
I_{k}^{\perp}
into irreducible representations of the local unitary group (see Section
3.1.1
). More concretely, it is often easier to write down a finite set of homogeneous polynomials
f_{1},\dots,f_{p}
that cut out
\mathcal{X}
, and let
I=\langle f_{1},\dots,f_{p}\rangle.

In this section we review some background on representation theory, and then give explicit descriptions of
I_{k}^{\perp}
for all of the varieties in Example
1.1
.

3.1.1
Local unitary symmetry

Many varieties
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{H})
that are of interest in quantum information are invariant under local unitaries
{U_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes U_{m}}
. In this case, it is clear from the expression (
8
) that
I(\mathcal{X})_{k}^{\perp}\subseteq S^{k}(\mathcal{H})
is invariant under powers of local unitaries
(U_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes U_{m})^{\otimes k}
, and the same is often true of other ideals
I
that we may choose to cut out
\mathcal{X}
. When this is the case, we can describe
I_{k}^{\perp}
by decomposing it into irreducible representations (irreps) of the local unitary group. For a finite dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{J}
, by
Schur-Weyl duality
the irreps of the unitary group
\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{J})
that appear in
\mathcal{J}^{\otimes d}
are indexed by
integer partitions

\lambda\vdash d
, which are tuples of non-increasing, positive integers summing to
d
. See e.g.
[
Lan12
,
FH13
]
for more details. The irrep indexed by
\lambda
is denoted
S^{\lambda}(\mathcal{J})\subseteq\mathcal{J}^{\otimes d}
. It follows that every irrep of the local unitary group on
S^{d}(\mathcal{H})
is of the form
S^{\lambda_{1}}(\mathcal{H}_{1})\otimes\dots\otimes S^{\lambda_{m}}(\mathcal{H}_{m})
for some integer partitions
\lambda_{i}\vdash d
.

3.1.2
Examples

Here we give explicit expressions for
I_{k}^{\perp}
for all of the varieties in Example
1.1
. These are computed using (
8
) along with standard results that can be found e.g. in
[
Lan12
]
.

Example 3.4

(Separability)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathcal{H}_{m}
. The set of pure product states

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{Sep}}=\{\psi_{1}\psi_{1}^{*}\otimes\dots\otimes\psi_{m}\psi_{m}^{*}:\psi_{i}\psi_{i}^{*}\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{H}_{i})\}\subseteq\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{H})

(9)

satisfies

\displaystyle I(\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{Sep}})_{k}^{\perp}=S^{k}(\mathcal{H}_{1})\otimes\dots\otimes S^{k}(\mathcal{H}_{m}).

(10)

Example 3.5

(Bosonic separability)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=S^{m}(\mathcal{J})
. The set of bosonic pure product states

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{Sep}}^{\vee}=\{(\psi\psi^{*})^{\otimes m}:\psi\psi^{*}\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{J})\}\subseteq\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{H})

(11)

\displaystyle I(\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{Sep}}^{\vee})_{k}^{\perp}=S^{km}(\mathcal{J}).

(12)

Example 3.6

(Schmidt rank)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2}
. The set
\mathcal{X}_{r}\subseteq\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{H})
of pure states of Schmidt rank at most

\displaystyle I(\mathcal{X}_{r})_{k}^{\perp}=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\vdash k\\
\ell(\lambda)\leq r\end{subarray}}S^{\lambda}(\mathcal{H}_{1})\otimes S^{\lambda}(\mathcal{H}_{2}).

(13)

Alternatively,
I(\mathcal{X}_{r})
is generated in degree
r+1
by the span of the
(r+1)\times(r+1)
minors, given by

\displaystyle I(\mathcal{X}_{r})_{r+1}=\Lambda^{r+1}(\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*})\otimes\Lambda^{r+1}(\mathcal{H}_{2}^{*})\in S^{r+1}(\mathcal{H}^{*}),

(14)

from which one can compute
I_{k}
directly. In concrete terms, if we let
\Pi_{\mathcal{J},k}^{{\wedge}}
be the orthogonal projection onto the antisymmetric space
\Lambda^{k}(\mathcal{J})
, then

\displaystyle I(\mathcal{X}_{r})_{k}^{\perp}=\operatorname{Ker}(\Pi_{\mathcal{H}_{1},k}^{{\wedge}}\otimes\Pi_{\mathcal{H}_{2},k}^{{\wedge}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes k-r-1})\cap S^{k}(\mathcal{H}).

(15)

We note that
\Pi_{\mathcal{H}_{1},k}^{{\wedge}}\otimes\Pi_{\mathcal{H}_{2},k}^{{\wedge}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes k-r-1}
was given the name
\Phi_{r}^{k}
in the prior work
[
JLV22
]
(see also Section
6.5
).

Example 3.7

(Matrix product states)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathcal{H}_{m}
. Let
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{MPS},r}\subseteq\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{H})
be the set of matrix product states of bond dimension at most
r

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{MPS},r}=\bigcap_{j=1}^{m-1}\{\psi\psi^{*}\text{ of Schmidt rank $\leq r$ in the bipartition }({\textstyle\bigotimes}_{i=1}^{j}\mathcal{H}_{j})\otimes({\textstyle\bigotimes}_{i=j+1}^{m}\mathcal{H}_{j})\}.

(16)

It follows that
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{MPS},r}
is cut out in degree

(r+1)\times(r+1)

\psi

m-1

\displaystyle I_{k}^{\perp}=\bigcap_{j=1}^{m-1}\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\vdash k\\

\displaystyle I_{k}^{\perp}=\bigcap_{j=1}^{m-1}\operatorname{Ker}(\Pi_{\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathcal{H}_{j},k}^{{\wedge}}\otimes\Pi_{\mathcal{H}_{j+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathcal{H}_{m},k}^{{\wedge}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes k-r-1})\cap S^{k}(\mathcal{H}).

3.2
A de Finetti theorem for fermionic separability

In some cases, a quantitative version of Theorem
3.2
, which upper bounds the distance between a
(k,\mathcal{X})
-tendable and the set of
\mathcal{X}
-arable states, can be obtained. This is closely related to various results which have been dubbed
quantum de Finetti theorems
. Thus, we call such a quantitative result a
de Finetti theorem for
\mathcal{X}
-arability
. In this section, we prove a de Finetti theorem for fermionic separability (i.e.
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{Sep}}^{{\wedge}}
-arability), and for completeness record similar results for separability (
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{Sep}}
-arability) and bosonic separability (
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{Sep}}^{\vee}
-arability), which are well-known. These examples are distinguished among the others in that
I_{k}^{\perp}
is an irreducible representation of the underlying local unitary group, allowing us to apply
[
KM09
, Theorem III.3, Remark III.4]
.

We note that the following result is quite different from the “fermionic de Finetti theorem” of
[
KZE17
]
, which considers a multi-mode notion of fermionic separability.

Theorem 3.12

(De Finetti theorem for fermionic separability)
.

Let
\mathcal{J}
be a Hilbert space of dimension
n
, and let
\mathcal{H}=\Lambda^{m}(\mathcal{J})
for
m\leq n
. If
\rho\in\mathrm{D}\left(\mathcal{H}\right)
is
(k,\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{Sep}}^{{\wedge}})
-tendable, then there exists an
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{Sep}}^{{\wedge}}
-arable state
\tau\in\mathrm{D}\left(\mathcal{H}\right)
for which

\displaystyle\lVert\mspace{1mu}\rho-\tau\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{1}\leq\frac{4m(n-m)}{n+k}.

(27)

Here,
\lVert\mspace{1mu}\cdot\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{1}
denotes the trace norm. The theorem assumes
m\leq n
because otherwise
\mathcal{H}=0
.

Proof.

We prove the theorem as a corollary to
[
KM09
, Theorem III.3, Remark III.4]
. By convexity, it suffices to assume that
\rho
is
(k,\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{Sep}}^{{\wedge}})
-tendable to a pure state. Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}

(28)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}

\displaystyle=S^{(k-1)^{(m)}}(\mathcal{J})

(29)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle=S^{k^{(m)}}(\mathcal{J})

(30)

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=\spn\{e_{1}{\wedge}\cdots{\wedge}e_{m}\}\subseteq\mathcal{A}.

(31)

Then
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{B}
is an irreducible subrepresentation (with multiplicity one) of unitary group
\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{J})
. Applying the standard dimension formula, we obtain
[
IN66
]

\displaystyle\dim(S^{k^{(m)}}(\mathcal{J}))=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[m]\\
j\in[{k}]\end{subarray}}\frac{n+j-i}{m-i+{k}-j+1}.

(32)

Let
\Pi_{X}
be the orthogonal projection onto
X
and let
\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}
be the orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{C}
. Note that
\Pi_{X}=\psi\psi^{*}
is a pure state,
\psi^{\otimes k-1}\in\mathcal{B}
, and
\operatorname{Tr}(\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}(\Pi_{X}\otimes(\psi\psi^{*})^{\otimes k-1}))=1
because
\psi^{\otimes k}\in\mathcal{C}
. Hence, the quantity
\delta(X)
given in
[
KM09
, Definition III.2]
is equal to

\displaystyle\frac{\dim(\mathcal{B})}{\dim(\mathcal{C})}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\in[m]}\frac{m+k-i}{n+k-i}

(33)

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{m+k}{n+k}\right)^{m}

(34)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{n-m}{n+k}\right)^{m}

(35)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{m(n-m)}{n+k}.

(36)

The desired bound then follows directly from the bound given in
[
KM09
, Theorem III.3, Remark III.4]
in terms of
\delta(X)
.
∎

For completeness, we state the quantum de Finetti theorems for separability and bosonic separability that one can obtain from
[
KM09
, Theorem III.3, Remark III.4]
. These are well known.

Theorem 3.13

(De Finetti theorem for separability)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathcal{H}_{m}
with
\dim(\mathcal{H}_{i})=n_{i}
. If
\rho\in\mathrm{D}\left(\mathcal{H}\right)
is
(k,\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{Sep}})
-tendable, then there exists a separable (i.e.
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{Sep}}
-arable) state
\tau\in\mathrm{D}\left(\mathcal{H}\right)
for which

\displaystyle\lVert\mspace{1mu}\rho-\tau\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{1}\leq\frac{4m(\max_{j}n_{j}-1)}{k+1}.

(37)

Theorem 3.14

(De Finetti theorem for bosonic separability)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=S^{m}(\mathcal{J})
with
\dim(\mathcal{J})=n
. If
\rho\in\mathrm{D}\left(\mathcal{H}\right)
is
(k,\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{Sep}}^{\vee})
-tendable, then there exists an
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{Sep}}^{\vee}
-arable state
\tau\in\mathrm{D}\left(\mathcal{H}\right)
for which

\displaystyle\lVert\mspace{1mu}\rho-\tau\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{1}\leq\frac{4m(n-1)}{k+1}.

(38)

4
Optimizing a Hermitian operator over
\mathcal{X}

Let
H\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{H})
be a Hermitian operator, let
I\subseteq S^{\bullet}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
be a homogeneous ideal and let
\mathcal{X}=V(I)\subseteq\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{H})
be the variety cut out by
I
. In this section we consider optimization problems of the form

\displaystyle H_{\mathcal{X}}^{\downarrow}:=\min_{\psi\psi^{*}\in\mathcal{X}}\langle\psi,H\psi\rangle\quad\quad\text{or}\quad\quad H_{\mathcal{X}}^{\uparrow}:=\max_{\psi\psi^{*}\in\mathcal{X}}\langle\psi,H\psi\rangle.

(39)

We obtain the following hierarchy of eigencomputations for computing
H_{\mathcal{X}}^{\uparrow}
. Recall that we define
\Pi_{I,k}\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k})
to be the orthogonal projection onto
I_{k}^{\perp}
. Recall also that in Section
3.1
we have computed
I_{k}^{\perp}
explicitly for all of the varieties in Example
1.1
.

Theorem 4.1
.

For each positive integer
k
, let

\displaystyle H_{k}:=\Pi_{I,k}(H\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes k-1})\Pi_{I,k}

(40)

and
\nu_{k}=\lambda_{\max}(H_{k})
. Then
\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\nu_{3},\dots
is a non-increasing sequence with

\displaystyle H_{\mathcal{X}}^{\uparrow}=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{k}.

(41)

In Section
7
we relate this to the
Hermitian sum of squares
(HSOS) hierarchy developed in
[
DP09
]
, proving the (non-trivial) fact that the HSOS hierarchy is equivalent to a spectral hierarchy in some settings. This result can also be proven using
[
CD99
, Theorem 1]
if one is familiar with vector bundles. We prove it as a consequence of Theorem
3.2
.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Observe that
\nu_{k}
is the optimal value of the following semidefinite program:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textup{maximize:}&\ \operatorname{Tr}\big((H\otimes I_{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes(k-1)})\sigma_{k}\big)\\
\textup{subject to:}&\ \operatorname{Im}(\sigma_{k})\subseteq I_{k}^{\perp}\\
&\ \sigma_{k}\in\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k}),\end{split}

(42)

which can be reformulated as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textup{maximize:}&\ \operatorname{Tr}(H\rho)\\
\textup{subject to:}&\ \rho\text{ is }(k,\mathcal{X})\text{-tendable}.\end{split}

(43)

This immediately shows that
H_{\mathcal{X}}^{\uparrow}
is bounded above by
\nu_{k}
, because every
\mathcal{X}
-arable state is
(k,\mathcal{X})
-tendable. It furthermore shows that the
\nu_{k}
are non-increasing, because the partial trace of a
(k,\mathcal{X})
-tension is a
(k-1,\mathcal{X})
-tension. Since the
\nu_{k}
are also bounded below (by the minimum eigenvalue of
H
, for example),
\lim_{k}\nu_{k}
exists. Let
\rho_{k}\in\mathrm{D}\left(\mathcal{H}\right)
be a state for which the optimum value of the SDP (
43
) is attained. By compactness of
\mathrm{D}\left(\mathcal{H}\right)
, this sequence contains a subsequence converging to some
\rho\in\mathrm{D}\left(\mathcal{H}\right)
, so
\lim_{k}\nu_{k}=\operatorname{Tr}(H\rho)
. Clearly
H_{\mathcal{X}}^{\uparrow}\leq\operatorname{Tr}(H\rho)
, since this inequality holds for each
\rho_{k}
. Conversely, since
\rho
is
(k,\mathcal{X})
-tendable for arbitrary
k
,
\rho
is
\mathcal{X}
-arable by Theorem
3.2
, so
H_{\mathcal{X}}^{\uparrow}\geq\operatorname{Tr}(H\rho)
. This completes the proof.
∎

4.1
\mathcal{X}
-tanglement witnesses

We say that

is
\mathcal{X}
-positive
if

. We say that

\mathcal{X}

if it is

-positive and has at least one negative eigenvalue. The set of

-arable states:

Proposition 4.2
.

A state

is

for every
\mathcal{X}
-tanglement witness

Theorem
4.1
implies the following hierarchy for certifying that a given operator

\mathcal{X}
-tanglement witness.

.

H\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{H})
is an
\mathcal{X}
-tanglement witness if and only if

and

5
\mathcal{X}
-tangled subspaces

Let

be the corresponding projective variety. For a subspace

, we say that

\displaystyle\{\psi\psi^{*}:\psi\in\mathcal{U}\}\cap\mathcal{X}=\varnothing.

(44)

We define the

\mathcal{X}
-tanglement
(GM
\mathcal{X}
) of

\displaystyle E_{\mathcal{X}}(\mathcal{U})=1-(\Pi_{\mathcal{U}})_{\mathcal{X}}^{\uparrow},

(45)

where
\Pi_{\mathcal{U}}
denotes the orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{U}
. Note that

if and only if
\mathcal{U}
is
\mathcal{X}
-tangled. When
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{Sep}}
, this specializes to the
geometric measure of entanglement
(GME) of subspaces studied in
[
DA19b
,
ZZZ24
]
, and when
\mathcal{U}=\spn\{\psi\}
is one-dimensional this becomes the well-studied GME of
\psi

[
WG03
]
.

Theorem
4.1
gives a hierarchy of maximum eigenvalue computations to determine

.

Corollary 5.1
.

For each positive integer
k
, let
\nu_{k}=\lambda_{\max}(\Pi_{I,k}(\Pi_{\mathcal{U}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes k-1})\Pi_{I,k}).
Then
\nu_{1}\geq\nu_{2}\geq\dots
and
E_{\mathcal{X}}(\mathcal{U})=1-\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}{\nu_{k}}
.

5.1
\mathcal{X}
-tangled states from
\mathcal{X}
-tangled subspaces

The
range criterion
is a well-known method of certifying entanglement of a mixed state. This generalizes straightforwardly to
\mathcal{X}
-tanglement, as follows:

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
\rho\in\mathrm{D}\left(\mathcal{H}\right)
be a state. If
\operatorname{Im}(\rho)\subseteq\mathcal{H}
is an
\mathcal{X}
-tangled subspace, then
\rho
is
\mathcal{X}
-tangled.

Proof.

We prove the contrapositive. Suppose
\rho
is
\mathcal{X}
-arable with decomposition
\rho=\sum_{i}p_{i}\psi_{i}\psi_{i}^{*}
. Then
\psi_{i}\in\operatorname{Im}(\rho)
and
\psi_{i}\psi_{i}^{*}\in\mathcal{X}
, so
\operatorname{Im}(\rho)
is not an
\mathcal{X}
-tangled subspace.
∎

More generally,
highly

\mathcal{X}
-tangled subspaces can be used to certify
highly
\mathcal{X}
-tangled
mixed states: States that remain
\mathcal{X}
-tangled after unitary perturbations. The following is a straightforward generalization of
[
ZZZ24
, Theorem 2]
.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\rho\in\mathrm{D}\left(\mathcal{H}\right)
be a state, and let
\mathcal{U}=\operatorname{Im}(\rho)\subseteq\mathcal{H}
. Then for any
H\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{H})
with
\lVert\mspace{1mu}H\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{1}<E_{\mathcal{X}}(\mathcal{U})^{1/2}
, the state
\rho^{\prime}=e^{iH}\rho e^{-iH}
is
\mathcal{X}
-tangled.

5.2
\mathcal{X}
-tanglement witnesses from
\mathcal{X}
-tangled subspaces

\mathcal{X}
-tangled subspaces can be used to construct
\mathcal{X}
-tanglement witnesses, as follows (see also
[
LJ22
, Section 5]
):

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
be an
\mathcal{X}
-tangled subspace, and let
\mu=1/{(\Pi_{\mathcal{U}})_{\mathcal{X}}^{\uparrow}}
. Then
\mu>1
and
H:=\mathds{1}-\mu\Pi_{\mathcal{U}}
is an
\mathcal{X}
-tanglement witness with
\dim(\mathcal{U})
negative eigenvalues of magnitude
\mu-1
.

The number and magnitude of negative eigenvalues roughly measures “how good” an
\mathcal{X}
-tanglement witness

is. This proposition shows that good witnesses can be constructed using subspaces of large dimension and high geometric measure.

6
Deciding subspace
\mathcal{X}
-tanglement using Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz

In this section we consider the special case of simply deciding if
\mathcal{U}
is
\mathcal{X}
-tangled, without determining the geometric measure
E_{\mathcal{X}}(\mathcal{U})
. Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz gives a hierarchy of linear systems for this problem, extending the hierarchy introduced in
[
JLV22
]
to general
\mathcal{X}
-tanglement:

Theorem 6.1

(Nullstellensatz hierarchy for subspace
\mathcal{X}
-tanglement)
.

A subspace
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
is
\mathcal{X}
-tangled if and only if
I_{k}+I(\mathcal{U})_{k}=S^{k}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
for
k\gg 0
.

Proof.

\mathcal{U}
is
\mathcal{X}
-tangled if and only if
V(I+I(\mathcal{U}))=\varnothing
. By Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz (Theorem
2.1
), this is equivalent to
I_{k}+I(\mathcal{U})_{k}=S^{k}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
for
k\gg 0
.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

One can check that if
I_{k}+I(\mathcal{U})_{k}=S^{k}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
, then
I_{k+1}+I(\mathcal{U})_{k+1}=S^{k+1}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
, justifying our use of the word “hierarchy.”

The
k
-th level of this hierarchy checks if
I_{k}+I(\mathcal{U})_{k}=S^{k}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
, which amounts to solving a linear system in the
\binom{N+k-1}{k}
-dimensional space
S^{k}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
, where
N=\dim(\mathcal{H})
. Because this is a computation in the degree-
k
part of the polynomial ring, we also call
k
the
degree
of the hierarchy. The computational complexity of using this hierarchy to certify
\mathcal{X}
-tanglement of
\mathcal{U}
depends on how large
k
needs to be for the equality
I_{k}+I(\mathcal{U})_{k}=S^{k}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
to hold. We refer to bounds on
k
as
degree bounds
for the hierarchy.

Since determining
\mathcal{X}
-tanglement of a subspace is NP-hard, one should expect the worst-case degree to grow at least linearly in
N
in general.
We say that a property holds for a
generically chosen
element if it holds on a Zariski open dense (full measure) subset of the underlying Hilbert space. We prove both worst case and generic degree bounds. Our starting point is the following classical fact (see
[
Har13
]
).

Fact 6.3
.

Let
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{H})
be a variety, and let
\dim(\mathcal{X})
be the (affine) dimension of
\mathcal{X}
(see Section
6.1
). Then:

1.

Every subspace
\mathcal{U}
of dimension
\dim(\mathcal{U})>N-\dim(\mathcal{X})
intersects
\mathcal{X}
.

2.

A generically chosen subspace
\mathcal{U}
of dimension
\dim(\mathcal{U})\leq N-\dim(\mathcal{X})
is
\mathcal{X}
-tangled.

So the relevant regime to consider is
\dim(\mathcal{U})\leq N-\dim(\mathcal{X})
, otherwise the problem is trivially solvable in polynomial time. While a generically chosen subspace satisfying this bound will be
\mathcal{X}
-tangled, it is not clear at what level the Nullstellensatz hierarchy will certify it to be so.

In Sections
6.2
and
6.3
we prove general purpose worst-case and generic degree bounds for the Nullstellensatz hierarchy, respectively.
In Section
6.4
we apply these degree bounds to the varieties in Example
1.1
. A collection of these examples is presented in Table
1
. In all cases, we find that
\dim(\mathcal{X})=o(N)
, and a generically chosen subspace of dimension
(1-\varepsilon)N
is certified
\mathcal{X}
-tangled at a constant degree
k
. Furthermore, in all cases we prove that
k=O(N)
is sufficient in the worst case. We improve this bound in some cases (see Theorem
1.10
).

An alternative hierarchy to decide if
\mathcal{U}
is
\mathcal{X}
-tangled can be obtained from Corollary
5.1
, which implies that
\mathcal{U}
is
\mathcal{X}
-tangled if and only if
\lambda_{\max}(\Pi_{I,k}(\Pi_{\mathcal{U}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes k-1})\Pi_{I,k})<1
for
k\gg 0
. Satisfyingly, in Section
6.5
we prove that this is equivalent to the Nullstellensatz hierarchy. We also clarify the relationship between the Nullstellensatz hierarchy of Theorem
6.1
and the hierarchy introduced in
[
JLV22
]
.

6.1
Commutative algebra background

Our degree bounds for the Nullstellensatz hierarchy require more background in commutative algebra, which we now briefly review. See
[
Eis05
,
Eis13
,
CLO13
]
for more details.

Let
R=S^{\bullet}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
, let
I\subseteq R
be a homogeneous ideal, and let
A
be a (commutative)
\mathbb{C}
-algebra (which we often take to be
R/I
). We say that
f_{1},\dots,f_{\ell}\in R
are
algebraically independent
if they do not satisfy any non-trivial polynomial equation, i.e.
g(f_{1},\dots,f_{\ell})=0\Rightarrow g=0
. An
A
-module

M
is a “vector space over
A
”: An additive abelian group with scalar multiplication
{A\times M\rightarrow M}
that distributes over addition in
A
and in
M
, and satisfies
(ab)m=a(bm)
for all
a,b\in A,m\in M
. A
homomorphism
of modules is a map
\phi
for which
\phi(a_{1}m_{1}+a_{2}m_{2})=a_{1}\phi(m_{1})+a_{2}\phi(m_{2})
. We say that
M
is a
finite

A
-module if there exist
m_{1},\dots,m_{p}\in M
for which every element of
M
is an
A
-linear combination of the
m_{i}
.

We will make use of the following simplified version of Noether’s Normalization lemma:

Lemma 6.4

(Noether Normalization)
.

Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{\ell}\in R
be homogeneous of the same degree, and let
A=\mathbb{C}[f_{1},\dots,f_{\ell}]
be the algebra they generate. Then there exist algebraically independent elements
g_{1},\dots,g_{p}\in\spn\{f_{1},\dots,f_{\ell}\}
for which
A
is a finite
\mathbb{C}[g_{1},\dots,g_{p}]
-module.

A
grading
of
A
is a direct sum decomposition
A={\textstyle\bigoplus}_{d=0}^{\infty}A_{d}
into
\mathbb{C}
-vector spaces
A_{d}
for which
A_{c}\cdot A_{d}\subseteq A_{c+d}
. The vector space
A_{d}
is called the
d
-th graded part
(or
homogeneous part of degree
d
) of
A
. The symmetric algebra is graded by degree
R_{d}=S^{d}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
, as is the quotient
R/I={\textstyle\bigoplus}_{d}R_{d}/I_{d}
. For a graded algebra
A
, define the
Hilbert function

\mathrm{HF}_{A}(d)=\dim(A_{d})
and the
Hilbert series

\mathrm{HS}_{A}(t)=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{HF}_{A}(d)t^{d}
.

For a graded algebra
A
and integer
c
, let
A(-c)
be the graded algebra with
d
-th graded part
A_{d-c}
if
c\leq d
and
0
otherwise. If
A
is a graded algebra, then we say
M
is a
graded
A
-module
if there is a direct sum decomposition
M={\textstyle\bigoplus}_{d=0}^{\infty}M_{d}
of abelian groups for which
A_{c}M_{d}\subseteq M_{c+d}
. The shifted modules
M(-c)
are defined similarly. A
homomorphism of graded algebras/modules

\phi:M\rightarrow N
is an algebra/module homomorphism for which
\phi(M_{d})\subseteq N_{d}
. An
exact sequence
of graded algebras/modules is a sequence of graded algebra/module homomorphisms

\displaystyle M^{(k)}\overset{\phi_{k}}{\longrightarrow}M^{(k-1)}\overset{\phi_{k-1}}{\longrightarrow}M^{(k-2)}\overset{\phi_{t-2}}{\longrightarrow}\cdots

(46)

for which
\operatorname{Im}(\phi_{i})=\operatorname{Ker}(\phi_{i-1})
for all
i
. A
(graded) minimal free resolution
of
A=R/I
is an exact sequence of the form

\displaystyle 0\longrightarrow{\textstyle\bigoplus}_{j=1}^{\beta_{t}}R(-c_{t,j})\overset{\phi_{t}}{\longrightarrow}{\textstyle\bigoplus}_{j=1}^{\beta_{t-1}}R(-c_{t-1,j})\overset{\phi_{t-1}}{\longrightarrow}\dots\overset{\phi_{1}}{\longrightarrow}{\textstyle\bigoplus}_{j=1}^{\beta_{0}}R(-c_{0,j})\overset{\phi_{0}}{\longrightarrow}R/I\overset{\phi_{-1}}{\longrightarrow}0,

(47)

where each arrow is a homomorphism of graded
A
-modules, and each
\beta_{i}
is minimal (= the minimum number of generators of
\operatorname{Ker}(\phi_{i-1})
). The
Castelnuovo-Mumford regularity
of
A
is defined as
\mathrm{reg}(A):=\max_{i,j}\{c_{i,j}-j\}
. It is a non-trivial but well-known fact that a minimal free resolution exists for any homogeneous ideal
I
, and that the regularity is independent of the choice of minimal free resolution
[
Eis05
]
.

Let
A
be a graded algebra. A collection of homogeneous elements
a_{1},\dots,a_{t}\in A
is called
regular
if each
a_{i}
is not a zero divisor in
A/\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{i-1}\rangle
. The
depth

\mathrm{depth}(A)
is the maximum length of a regular sequence of homogeneous elements of positive degree. An ideal
P\subseteq A
is
prime
if
P\neq A
and
(p,q\in A,pq\in P\Rightarrow p\in P\text{ or }q\in P)
. The
(Krull) dimension

\dim(A)
is the largest
d
for which there exists a strictly ascending chain
0=P_{0}\subsetneq P_{1}\subsetneq P_{2}\subsetneq\dots\subsetneq P_{d}
of prime ideals contained in
A
. For a projective variety
\mathcal{X}=V(I)
, we let
\dim(\mathcal{X}):=\dim(R/I)
be the
affine (Krull) dimension
of
\mathcal{X}
. It is known that
\mathrm{depth}(A)\leq\dim(A)
, and we say that
A
is
Cohen-Macaulay
if equality holds. The
radical
of
I
is defined as
\sqrt{I}=\{f:f^{k}\in I\text{ for some k}\}
. By Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz,
I(\mathcal{X})=\sqrt{I}
for any ideal
I
cutting out
\mathcal{X}
.

6.2
Worst-case degree bounds

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a complex Hilbert space of dimension
N
, let
R=S^{\bullet}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
, let
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{H})
be a variety and let
I\subseteq R
be a homogeneous ideal cutting out
\mathcal{X}
and generated in degree at most
d
. In this section we prove worst-case degree bounds for the Nullstellensatz hierarchy. We remark that the results of this section hold more generally over any algebraically closed field.

In the case when
R/I
is Cohen-Macaulay, the following classical result shows that
\mathrm{reg}(R/I)
determines the worst-case degree needed for the Nullstellensatz hierarchy. See
[
Eis05
, Proposition 4.14]

Theorem 6.5
.

Suppose that
R/I
is Cohen-Macaulay, and let
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
be a subspace. Then the following statements are equivalent:

1.

\mathcal{U}
is
\mathcal{X}
-tangled.

2.

I_{k}+I(\mathcal{U})_{k}=R_{k}\;\;
for
k=\mathrm{reg}(R/I)+1
.

Furthermore, the bound
k=\mathrm{reg}(R/I)+1
is tight: There exists an
\mathcal{X}
-tangled subspace
\mathcal{U}
such that
I_{k}+I(\mathcal{U})_{k}\subsetneq R_{k}\;\;
for
k=\mathrm{reg}(R/I)
.

The next theorem gives a worst-case degree bound in the case when
R/I
may not be Cohen-Macaulay.

Theorem 6.6
.

For a linear subspace
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
the following statements are equivalent:

1.

\mathcal{U}
is
\mathcal{X}
-tangled.

2.

I_{k}+I(\mathcal{U})_{k}=R_{k}\;\;
for
k=N(d-1)+1
.

Proof.

2\Rightarrow 1
is clear, so it suffices to prove
1\Rightarrow 2
. Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{\ell}\in R_{d}
be a vector space basis for
I_{d}+I(\mathcal{U})_{d}
. Then
\langle f_{1},\dots,f_{\ell}\rangle_{D}=I_{D}+I(\mathcal{U})_{D}
for all
D\geq d
. Let
A=\mathbb{C}[f_{1},\dots,f_{\ell}]
and
M_{A}
be the ideal generated by
f_{1},\dots,f_{\ell}
in
A
. We need to prove that
\langle f_{1},\dots,f_{\ell}\rangle_{k}=R_{k}
. By Noether’s normalization lemma, there exist algebraically independent elements
g_{1},\dots,g_{\tilde{N}}\in\spn\{f_{1},\dots,f_{\ell}\}
such that
A
is a finite
B:=\mathbb{C}[g_{1},\dots,g_{\tilde{N}}]
-module. Since
g_{1},\dots,g_{\tilde{N}}
are algebraically independent,
\tilde{N}\leq N
. Let
M_{B}
be the ideal generated by
g_{1},\dots,g_{\tilde{N}}
in
B
.

Claim 6.7
.

V(g_{1},\dots,g_{\tilde{N}})=\varnothing
.

Before proving the claim, we use it to prove the theorem. Since at least
N
equations are needed to cut out the empty set, the claim implies
\tilde{N}\geq N
, and hence
\tilde{N}=N
. For each
j=\{0,1,\dots,N\}
, let
K_{j}=R/(R\langle g_{1},\dots,g_{j}\rangle)
. Then we have an exact sequence of graded algebras

\displaystyle 0\rightarrow K_{j}(-d)\xrightarrow{g_{j}}K_{j}\rightarrow K_{j+1}\rightarrow 0.

(48)

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathrm{HS}_{K_{j}}(t)=\mathrm{HS}_{K_{j}(-d)}(t)+\mathrm{HS}_{K_{j+1}}(t).

(49)

Substituting
\mathrm{HS}_{K_{j}(-d)}(t)=t^{d}\mathrm{HS}_{K_{j}}(t),
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{HS}_{K_{j+1}}(t)=(1-t^{d})\cdot\mathrm{HS}_{K_{j}}(t).

(50)

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathrm{HS}_{K_{N}}(t)

\displaystyle=(1-t^{d})^{N}\cdot\mathrm{HS}_{R}(t)

(51)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1-t^{d}}{1-t}\right)^{N}

(52)

\displaystyle=(t^{d-1}+t^{d-2}+\dots+t+1)^{N}.

(53)

So the Hilbert series of
K_{N}
is equal to zero in degree
k=N(d-1)+1
. Equivalently,
R\langle g_{1},\dots,g_{N}\rangle_{k}=R_{k}.
Since
R_{k}\supseteq\langle f_{1},\dots,f_{\ell}\rangle\supseteq\langle g_{1},\dots,g_{N}\rangle
, this implies
\langle f_{1},\dots,f_{\ell}\rangle_{k}=R_{k}
. This completes the proof modulo proving the claim.

To prove the claim, we first observe that
\sqrt{AM_{B}}=M_{A}
. First note that
AM_{B}\subseteq M_{A}
, so
\sqrt{AM_{B}}\subseteq\sqrt{M_{A}}=M_{A}
. For the reverse inclusion, it suffices to prove that any homogeneous
h\in M_{A}
is contained in
\sqrt{AM_{B}}
. By
[
AM94
, Proposition 7.1]
it holds that
h^{k}=p_{0}+p_{1}h+\dots+p_{k-1}h^{k-1}
for some
k
and
p_{i}\in B
. Since
h
is homogeneous of positive degree, we can assume that each
p_{i}
is homogeneous of positive degree, so
p_{i}\in M_{B}
. This shows that
h^{k}\in AM_{B}
, so
h\in\sqrt{AM_{B}}
.

Hence,

\displaystyle\sqrt{R\;M_{B}}=\sqrt{R\;A\;M_{B}}=\sqrt{R\sqrt{A\;M_{B}}}=\sqrt{R\;M_{A}}=\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\rangle,

(54)

where the first equality follows from
R=RA
, the second follows from
\sqrt{IJ}=\sqrt{\sqrt{I}\sqrt{J}}
, the third is by above, and the fourth follows from the assumption
V(f_{1},\dots,f_{\ell})=0
.

Thus,

\displaystyle V(g_{1},\dots,g_{\tilde{N}})=V(R\;M_{B})=V(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=0.

(55)

6.3
Generic degree bounds

As before, let
\mathcal{H}
be a complex Hilbert space of dimension
N
, let
R=S^{\bullet}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
, let
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{H})
be a variety and let
I
be a homogeneous ideal cutting out
\mathcal{X}
. In this section we prove generic degree bounds for the Nullstellensatz hierarchy.

A
Zariski open set
is the set complement to a projective variety. We say that a property holds for a
generically chosen

s
-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
if it holds on a Zariski open dense subset of the set of all
s
-dimensional subspaces (which forms a variety called the
Grassmannian
). In particular, if a basis for
\mathcal{U}
is chosen at random with respect to the Haar measure, then the property holds with probability one.

The following results hold over any infinite field. The first result shows that a generic version of Theorem
6.5
holds even if
R/I
is not Cohen-Macaulay.

Theorem 6.8
.

For a generically chosen subspace
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
of dimension
\dim(\mathcal{U})\leq N-\dim(\mathcal{X})
, it holds that
I_{k}+I(\mathcal{U})_{k}=R_{k}
for

\displaystyle k=\mathrm{reg}(R/I)+1.

(56)

Proof.

Let
\dim(\mathcal{X})\leq r\leq N
, let
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{r}
be generically chosen linear forms, and let
\mathcal{U}=V(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{r})
so that
\mathcal{U}
is a generically chosen subspace of dimension
N-r
. Let
A=R/I
. By
[
Eis05
, Lemma 4.9, Corollary 4.11]
, a generically chosen linear form
\ell_{1}\in R_{1}
satisfies
\mathrm{reg}(A)\geq\mathrm{reg}(R/(I+\langle\ell_{1}\rangle))
. Continuing in this way, we obtain
\mathrm{reg}(A)\geq\mathrm{reg}(R/(I+\langle\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{r}\rangle))
. Furthermore,
\mathcal{U}\cap\mathcal{X}=\{0\}
(see e.g.
[
Har13
, Definition 11.2]
). By
[
Eis05
, Corollary 4.4]
,
\mathrm{reg}(R/(I+\langle\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{r}\rangle))
is the largest integer
k
for which
I_{k}+I(\mathcal{U})_{k}\neq R_{k}
. Hence equality holds for
k=\mathrm{reg}(A)+1
.
∎

The following theorem gives a bound on the dimension of a generically chosen subspace that can be certified
\mathcal{X}
-tangled at level
k
of the Nullstellensatz hierarchy.

Theorem 6.9
.

Let
s,k
be positive integers. If

\displaystyle\dim I_{k}^{\perp}<{N-s+k\choose k},

(57)

then a generically chosen
s
-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
satisfies
I_{k}+I(\mathcal{U})_{k}=R_{k}
.

Remark 6.10
.

Weaker results were presented in
[
JLV22
]
and
[
JLV23
, Theorem 20]
, with similar guarantees for a related algorithm to
recover
elements of
\mathcal{X}
contained in
\mathcal{U}
. The above theorem improves
[
JLV23
, Theorem 20]
for the problem of certifying
\mathcal{X}
-tanglement of a subspace, but it does not apply to the recovery setting. It is an interesting open problem to adapt Theorem
6.9
to the recovery setting. This would amount to proving that for generically chosen
\psi_{1}\psi_{1}^{*},\dots,\psi_{s}\psi_{s}^{*}\in\mathcal{X}
with
s
satisfying (
57
) (or similar), it holds that
I_{k}+I(\spn\{\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{s}\})_{k}=I(\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{s})_{k}
.

Example 6.11
.

Theorem
6.9
is sharp. Let
I=\langle x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{s-1}\rangle
and let
\mathcal{X}=V(I)
.
Then we have

.
Any subspace
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{H}

To prove Theorem

we fix a monomial ordering and invoke Galligo’s theorem that the leading monomial ideal of

is Borel fixed after a change of basis. In more details, we fix the

|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|>|\boldsymbol{\beta}|

Note that for any positive integers

An monomial ideal

6.5
An equivalence of hierarchies

An alternative hierarchy for determining if
\mathcal{U}
is
\mathcal{X}
-tangled can be obtained from Corollary
5.1
, which in particular says that
\mathcal{U}
is
\mathcal{X}
-tangled if and only
\nu_{k}<1
for
k\gg 0
. Satisfyingly, this is equivalent to the Nullstellensatz hierarchy:

Proposition 6.23
.

It holds that
\nu_{k}<1\iff I_{k}+I(\mathcal{U})_{k}=S^{k}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\nu_{k}<1\iff

\displaystyle\operatorname{Im}(\Pi_{\mathcal{U}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes k-1})\cap\operatorname{Im}(\Pi_{I,k})=\{0\}

(89)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle(\mathcal{U}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k-1})\cap I_{k}^{\perp}=\{0\}

(90)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle(\mathcal{U}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k-1})\cap S^{k}(\mathcal{H})\cap I_{k}^{\perp}=\{0\}

(91)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle S^{k}(\mathcal{U})\cap I_{k}^{\perp}=\{0\}

(92)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle I(\mathcal{U})_{k}^{\perp}\cap I_{k}^{\perp}=\{0\}

(93)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle I(\mathcal{U})_{k}+I_{k}=S^{k}(\mathcal{H}^{*}),

(94)

where the first two lines are obvious, the third line follows from
I_{k}^{\perp}\subseteq S^{k}(\mathcal{H})
, the fourth line follows from
(\mathcal{U}\otimes(\mathcal{H})^{\otimes k-1})\cap S^{k}(\mathcal{H})=S^{k}(\mathcal{U})
, the fifth line follows from
S^{k}(\mathcal{U})=I(\mathcal{U})_{k}^{\perp}
, and the last line follows from taking the orthogonal complement with respect to the bilinear pairing.
∎

We can also relate the Nullstellensatz hierarchy to the hierarchy of linear systems introduced in
[
JLV22
]
. There, in the examples they considered, the authors defined a map
\Phi\in\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k})
for which
\operatorname{Ker}(\Phi)\cap S^{k}(\mathcal{H})=I_{k}^{\perp}
(see also Example
3.6
). They then took a basis
u_{1},\dots,u_{\ell}
for
S^{k}(\mathcal{U})
, and checked if
\{\Phi(u_{1}),\dots,\Phi(u_{\ell})\}
is linearly independent. This holds if and only if
I_{k}^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{U}^{\otimes k}=\{0\}
, which in turn holds if and only if
I_{k}+I(\mathcal{U})_{k}=R_{k}
. Note that this amounts to solving a linear system of size
\dim(I_{k})\times\binom{\dim(\mathcal{U})+k-1}{k}
.

7
Hermitian polynomial optimization

In this section we use Theorem
4.1
to obtain a hierarchy of eigencomputations for optimizing a Hermitian form over a variety. We then prove that this hierarchy is equivalent to the Hermitian sum-of squares (HSOS) hierarchy of
[
DP09
, Theorem 2.1]
(see also
[
WM23
, Section 2.1]
) applied to this setting. While HSOS is in general a hierarchy of semidefinite programs, our results show that it is equivalent to a hierarchy of eigencomputations in our setting. This can be advantageous, as eigencomputations can be computed much faster in practice than full semidefinite programs.

7.1
Background on Hermitian polynomials

We begin with some background on Hermitian polynomials. See e.g.
[
D’A19a
]
for more details. For a homomorphism
R\in\mathrm{Hom}(S^{c}(\mathcal{H}),S^{d}(\mathcal{H}))
, let
R(z,w^{*}):=\langle w^{\otimes d},Rz^{\otimes c}\rangle
. More generally, for
R\in\mathrm{Hom}(S^{\underline{c}}(\mathcal{H}),S^{\underline{d}}(\mathcal{H}))
with degree
(a,b)
block
R_{(a,b)}\in\mathrm{Hom}(S^{a}(\mathcal{H}),S^{b}(\mathcal{H}))
, let
R(z,w^{*})=\sum_{a,b}R_{(a,b)}(z,w^{*})
.
The following proposition is standard, and shows that
R\in\mathrm{Hom}(S^{\underline{d}}(\mathcal{H}),S^{\underline{c}}(\mathcal{H}))
is uniquely determined by
R(z,z^{*})
. See also
[
D’A11
,
D’A19a
]
.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
R\in\mathrm{Hom}(S^{\underline{c}}(\mathcal{H}),S^{\underline{d}}(\mathcal{H}))
. If
R(z,z^{*})=0
for all
z\in\mathcal{H}
, then
R=0
.

Proof.

Let
(C_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}})_{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|\leq c,|\boldsymbol{\beta}|\leq d}
be the matrix of
R
in the monomial basis. Then
R(z,w^{*})=\sum_{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|\leq c,|\boldsymbol{\beta}|\leq d}C_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}z^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}w^{*\boldsymbol{\beta}}
. For
z=(e^{i\theta_{1}}x_{1},\dots,e^{i\theta_{N}}x_{N})\in\mathcal{H}
with
x_{i}
real we have

\displaystyle R(z,z^{*})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}C_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}e^{i(\alpha_{1}-\beta_{1})\theta_{1}}\cdots e^{i(\alpha_{N}-\beta_{N})\theta_{N}}x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1}}\cdots x_{N}^{\alpha_{N}+\beta_{N}}=0.

(95)

So for any
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}
it holds that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{\theta_{N}=0}^{2\pi}\cdots\int_{\theta_{1}=0}^{2\pi}R(z,z^{*})e^{i\gamma_{1}\theta_{1}}\cdots e^{i\gamma_{N}\theta_{N}}d\theta_{1}\dots d\theta_{N}

(96)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}}C_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}x^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\boldsymbol{\beta}}.

(97)

Since
C_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}x^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\boldsymbol{\beta}}
is the only term of degree
\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\boldsymbol{\beta}
in the sum, it follows that
C_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}=0
, so
R=0
.
∎

A
Hermitian polynomial
is an element
H\in\mathrm{End}(S^{\underline{d}}(\mathcal{H}))
for which
H(z,w^{*})=\overline{H(w,z^{*})}
for all
z,w\in\mathcal{H}
. Let
\mathrm{Herm}(S^{\underline{d}}(\mathcal{H}))\subseteq\mathrm{End}(S^{\underline{d}}(\mathcal{H}))
be the set of Hermitian polynomials, and let
\mathrm{Herm}(S^{d}(\mathcal{H}))\subseteq\mathrm{End}(S^{d}(\mathcal{H}))
be the set of Hermitian forms of bi-degree
(d,d)
. An immediate consequence of Proposition
7.1
is that
H(z,z^{*})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}C_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}z^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}z^{*\boldsymbol{\beta}}
is Hermitian if and only if it is Hermitian as an endomorphism, i.e. the matrix
(C_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}})_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}
is Hermitian (see also
[
D’A11
, Proposition 1.1]
). Let
\mathrm{Pos}(S^{\underline{d}}(\mathcal{H}))
be the set of
Hermitian sums-of-squares
: Hermitian polynomials of the form
P(z,z^{*})=\sum_{i}|q_{i}(z)|^{2}
for some
q_{i}\in S^{\underline{d}}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
. Similarly, these are the polynomials that are positive semidefinite as endomorphisms, i.e. the matrix
(C_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}})_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}
is positive semidefinite.

Note that the Hermitian form
H_{k}=H(z,z^{*})\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2(k-d)}
as an operator is given by
H_{k}=\Pi_{k}(H\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes{(k-d)}})\Pi_{k}
, where
\Pi_{k}
is the orthogonal projection onto
S^{k}(\mathcal{H})
, since
\langle z^{\otimes k},H_{k}z^{\otimes k}\rangle=H(z,z^{*})\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2(k-d)}
under this choice. As before, for a homogeneous ideal
I\subseteq S^{\bullet}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
, let
I_{k}^{\perp}\subseteq S^{k}(\mathcal{H})
be the orthogonal complement to
I_{k}
with respect to the bilinear pairing
\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}^{*}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
and let
\Pi_{I,k}\in\mathrm{Pos}(S^{k}(\mathcal{H}))
be the orthogonal projection onto
I_{k}^{\perp}
. Recall that we define
V(I)=\{vv^{*}:f(v)=0\;\;\forall f\in I\}\subseteq\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{H})
.

7.2
A hierarchy of eigencomputations

Theorem
4.1
implies the following hierarchy for optimizing a Hermitian form subject to homogeneous equality constraints. In the following, let
\lambda_{\min}(\cdot)
be the minimum eigenvalue.

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
H\in\mathrm{Herm}(S^{d}(\mathcal{H}))
be a Hermitian form and let
I\subseteq S^{\bullet}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
be a homogeneous ideal. For each
k\geq d
let
H_{k}=H(z,z^{*})\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2(k-d)}\in\mathrm{Herm}(S^{k}(\mathcal{H}))
and
\nu_{k}=\lambda_{\min}(\Pi_{I,k}H_{k}\Pi_{I,k})
. Then
\nu_{d},\nu_{d+1},\dots
forms a non-decreasing sequence for which

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{k}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}zz^{*}\in V(I)\end{subarray}}H(z,z^{*}).

(98)

Proof.

The fact that the sequence
\nu_{d},\nu_{d+1},\dots
is non-decreasing can be easily shown using similar techniques as in the proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Let
J\subseteq S^{\bullet}(S^{d}(\mathcal{H}^{*}))
be the homogeneous ideal with
J_{\ell}=I_{\ell d}+S^{\ell d}(\mathcal{H})^{\perp}\subseteq S^{\ell}(S^{d}(\mathcal{H}^{*}))
for each
\ell
. Then
V(J)=\{z^{\otimes d}:zz^{*}\in V(I)\}
, and we have

\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}zz^{*}\in V(I)\end{subarray}}H(z,z^{*})

\displaystyle=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}zz^{*}\in V(I)\end{subarray}}\langle z^{\otimes d},Hz^{\otimes d}\rangle

(99)

\displaystyle=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\psi\psi^{*}\in V(J)\end{subarray}}\langle\psi,H\psi\rangle.

(100)

By Theorem
4.1
, this is equal to
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{dk}
, where we define
\mu_{dk}=\lambda_{\min}(\Pi_{J,k}H_{dk}\Pi_{J,k})
. Note that
J_{k}^{\perp}=I_{dk}^{\perp}\cap S^{dk}(\mathcal{H})
, so
\Pi_{J,k}
is the projection onto the image of
\Pi_{I,dk}
. It follows that
\mu_{dk}=\nu_{dk}=\lambda_{\min}(\Pi_{I,dk}H_{dk}\Pi_{I,dk})
. So the sequence
\nu_{d},\nu_{d+1},\dots
has the same limit as the sequence
\mu_{d},\mu_{2d},\dots
and the statement holds.
∎

7.3
Relationship to HSOS

Theorem
7.2
is closely related to the HSOS hierarchy for Hermitian polynomial optimization
[
DP09
, Theorem 2.1]
(see also
[
WM23
, Section 2.1]
). For a subset
S\subseteq S^{\underline{d}}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
, let
|S|^{2}\subseteq\mathrm{Pos}(S^{\leq 2d}(\mathcal{H}))
be the set of polynomials that can be written as sums of Hermitian squares
|f(z)|^{2}
of elements
f\in S
. Note that
f\in S^{\underline{d}}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
satisfies
f(z)=0
if and only if
|f(z)|^{2}\leq 0
. Using this to translate equality constraints into inequality constraints, and invoking
[
LS05
, Lemma 1]
, the result
[
DP09
, Theorem 2.1]
gives the following hierarchy for Hermitian optimization under equality constraints:

Theorem 7.3
.

Let
H\in\mathrm{Herm}(S^{\underline{d}}(\mathcal{H}))
and let
I\subseteq S^{\bullet}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
be a homogeneous ideal. If
H(z,z^{*})>0
for all
zz^{*}\in V(I)
, then there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
for which

\displaystyle H(z,z^{*})\in\mathrm{Pos}(S^{\underline{k}}(\mathcal{H}))-|I_{\underline{k}}|^{2}+(1-\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2})\mathrm{Herm}(S^{\underline{k}}(\mathcal{H})).

(101)

We now prove that the hierarchies described in Theorems
7.2
and
7.3
are equivalent when
H
is bihomogeneous, in the sense that
\nu_{k}>0
if and only if (
101
) holds. The proof is similar in spirit to
[
dKLP05
, Proposition 2]
.

Theorem 7.4
.

Let
I\subseteq S^{\bullet}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
be a homogeneous ideal, let
H\in\mathrm{Herm}(S^{d}(\mathcal{H})),
and let
H_{k}=H(z,z^{*})\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2(k-d)}\in\mathrm{Herm}(S^{k}(\mathcal{H}))
. The following statements are equivalent:

1.

H(z,z^{*})\in\mathrm{Pos}(S^{\underline{k}}(\mathcal{H}))-|I_{\underline{k}}|^{2}+(1-\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2})\mathrm{Herm}(S^{\underline{k}}(\mathcal{H})).

2.

H_{k}\in\mathrm{Pos}(S^{{k}}(\mathcal{H}))-|I_{{k}}|^{2}
.

3.

\Pi_{I,k}H_{k}\Pi_{I,k}\in\mathrm{Pos}(S^{k}(\mathcal{H}))
.

Proof.

The equivalence
(2\iff 3)
is straightforward: Statement 3 holds if and only if
H_{k}=P-Q
for some
P,Q\in\mathrm{Pos}(S^{k}(\mathcal{H}))
with
\operatorname{Im}(Q)\subseteq I_{k}^{\perp}
. But the set of such
Q
is precisely
|I_{k}|^{2}
.

(2\Rightarrow 1)
: Statement
2
implies that

\displaystyle H_{k}

\displaystyle=H\cdot(1-1+\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2})^{k-d}

(102)

\displaystyle=H+H\sum_{s=1}^{k-d}\binom{k-d}{s}(\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2}-1)^{s}\in\mathrm{Pos}(S^{{k}}(\mathcal{H}))-|I_{{k}}|^{2}.

(103)

Subtracting terms containing nonzero powers of
(\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2}-1)
from both sides, we obtain

\displaystyle H\in\mathrm{Pos}(S^{{k}}(\mathcal{H}))-|I_{{k}}|^{2}+(1-\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2})\mathrm{Herm}(S^{k}(\mathcal{H})).

(104)

(1\Rightarrow 2)
: Let
P\in\mathrm{Pos}(S^{\underline{k}}(\mathcal{H})),Q\in|I_{\underline{k}}|^{2},S\in\mathrm{Herm}(S^{\underline{k}}(\mathcal{H}))
be such that

\displaystyle H=P-Q+(1-\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2})S.

(105)

Setting
\hat{z}=z/\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert
, we have

\displaystyle H(\hat{z},\hat{z}^{*})=P(\hat{z},\hat{z}^{*})-Q(\hat{z},\hat{z}^{*}).

(106)

Thus,

\displaystyle H_{k}=H(\hat{z},\hat{z}^{*})\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2k}=P(\hat{z},\hat{z}^{*})\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2k}-Q(\hat{z},\hat{z}^{*})\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2k}.

(107)

Since
H_{k}
is bihomogeneous of degree
(k,k)
, every other component must cancel, so

\displaystyle H_{k}(z,z^{*})=P_{(d,d)}(z,z^{*})\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2(k-d)}-Q_{(d,d)}(z,z^{*})\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2(k-d)}

(108)

where
P_{(d,d)}
is the component of
P
of bi-degree
(d,d)
, and similarly for
Q
. Clearly
P_{(d,d)}\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2(k-d)}\in\mathrm{Pos}(S^{k}(\mathcal{H}))
, and since
I
is homogeneous,
Q_{(d,d)}\in|I_{d}|^{2}
, so
{Q}_{(d,d)}\lVert\mspace{1mu}z\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2(k-d)}\in|I_{k}|^{2}
. This completes the proof.
∎
