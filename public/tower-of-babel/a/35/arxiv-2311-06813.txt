Abstract
. We prove the method of power iteration for matrices with at most finite entries from the Levi-Civita field 𝒞 \mathcal{C} under the assumption that there exists an eigenvalue with the strictly largest in absolute value complex part. In this case the weak convergence of a start vector to the eigenvector, that corresponds to the largest eigenvalue, is proven. Further, we prove that the Rayleigh quotient of the largest eigenvector also converges weakly to the corresponding eigenvalue. As a corollary, the same holds for matrices and polynomials over the Puiseux series field. In addition to that, we deliver an implementation of our method in Python. MSC2020: 65F15, 13F25, 12J15. Keywords: eigenvalue, matrix with polynomial entries, power iteration, Levi-Civita field, Puiseux series, power series.

Introduction

Matrices with power series and polynomial entries become interesting in algebraic geometry, see e.g.
[
3
]
,
[
16
]

On one hand, the eigenvalues of a matrix are roots of its characteristic polynomial, on the other hand, roots of a polynomial can be found as eigenvalues of its companion matrix.

Until now it seems like there exists only very little research on the problem of solving matrices over the field of power series, as opposed to, for example, the research on solving polynomials.
In
[
11
]
the problem of finding eigenvalues is solved for matrices with polynomial entries, but very costly, via calculation of the roots of the characteristic polynomial. Applications of this solution can be found for example in
[
17
]
,
[
19
]
and
[
30
]
. Further, in
[
8
]
and
[
26
]
the algorithms for finding determinants of matrices with polynomial entries are presented and analyzed. The classical approach to the problem of finding roots of polynomials over the field of power series consists of finding parts of the solution iteratively, lifting the solutions, and applying Newton iteration. Advanced versions of this method are discussed, for example, in
[
23
,
25
]
.

We present a power method algorithm for matrices with power series entries and believe that, besides being interesting on its own, it also opens the door to the development of further numerical approaches to finding the roots of multivariate polyomials, that might yield better stability and efficiency than existing methods. Our power method is proven to converge over the Levi-Civita field
\mathcal{C}
with complex coefficients, which includes the Puiseux series field, and the polynomial ring, see Figure
1
.

The Levi-Civita field
\mathcal{R}
is the smallest non-Archimedean real closed ordered field, which is Cauchy complete in the order topology, while the Levi-Civita field
\mathcal{C}
is its algebraic closure. Moreover,
\mathcal{R}
is the smallest Cauchy complete real closed field, containing a field of rational functions on one variable. The Levi-Civita field
\mathcal{R}
is, in fact, a Cauchy completion of the field of Puiseux series, and the latest is widely studied in algebraic geometry.

In this paper, we show, that the power iteration algorithm for the largest in modulus eigenvalue converges weakly for diagonalizable matrix over the Levi-Civita field
\mathcal{C}
if the eigenvalue has the strictly larger in absolute value complex part, then other eigenvalues, Theorem
5.5
. This is an analogue of the classical power iteration method for complex numbers
[
9
]
. Moreover, as a simple corollary we show, that the power iteration algorithm for diagonalizable matrices over the Puiseux series converges coefficient-wise under the same conditions, Corollary
5.7
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we present the Levi-Civita fields and preliminary results. In Section 3 we prove auxiliary results on weak convergence over the Levi-Civita fields, which up to our knowledge are not described in the literature, although some proofs are quite straightforward. In Section 4 we show that the power iteration algorithm converges for at most finite matrices, constructing the theoretical base for our method. In Section 5 we discuss, how the power iteration method can be applied to a wider range of matrices, in particular to the matrices over Puiseux series. In Section 6 we present examples, implemented in Python, and experimental results.

Note that any element

(resp.

((q_{n}),(a[q_{n}]))

We use the following terminology (all from the papers by Shamseddine and Berz, e.g.
[

]

Definition 2.3
.

For every
a
in
\mathcal{R}
(resp.
\mathcal{C}
) we denote:

•

\supp(a)=\{q\in\mathbb{Q}\;\mid\;a[q]\neq 0\}
,

•

\lambda(a)=\min(\supp(a))
.

Note that

Further, comparing any

•

a\sim b

\lambda(a)=\lambda(b)
,

•

a\approx b

\lambda(a)=\lambda(b)

•

Lemma 2.4
.

[

The relations

,

Addition on
\mathcal{R}
(resp.
\mathcal{C}
) is defined component-wise, i.e. for any

(resp.
\mathcal{C}
) we define

(a+b)[q]=a[q]+b[q].

Multiplication is defined as follows:

(a\cdot b)[q]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q_{a},q_{b}\in\mathbb{Q},\\
q_{a}+q_{b}=q\end{subarray}}a[q_{a}]\cdot b[q_{b}].

From the definition of multiplication immediately follows that for any
a,b\in\mathcal{R}
(resp.
\mathcal{C}
) holds

\lambda(ab)=\lambda(a)+\lambda(b).

There exists a natural embedding of real numbers (resp. complex numbers) into
\mathcal{R}
(resp.
\mathcal{C}
) defined by

\Pi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{R}
(resp.
\Pi:\mathbb{C}\to\mathcal{C}

\Pi(s)[q]=\begin{cases}s,\mbox{ if }q=0,\\
0,\mbox{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

We immediately see that
\Pi
is injective and direct calculations show that
\Pi(s_{1}+s_{2})=\Pi(s_{1})+\Pi(s_{2})
and
\Pi(s_{1}s_{2})=\Pi(s_{1})\cdot\Pi(s_{2})
. By slight abuse of notations, we will refer to
\Pi(s)
as

. It is clear, that the embedding preserves the algebraic structure and is injective, but not surjective.

Theorem 2.5
.

[
1
, Theorem 2.3 and Theorem 2.4]

(\mathcal{R},+,\cdot,0,1)
is a real closed field and
(\mathcal{C},+,\cdot,0,1)
is an algebraically closed field.

Clearly,
\mathcal{R}
can be embedded in
\mathcal{C}
with the preservation of the algebraic structure.

The order on
\mathcal{R}
is defined as follows: for any

we write

(x-y)[\lambda(a-b)]>0
. Then we denote the set of positive elements by

\mathcal{R}^{+}=\{a\in\mathcal{R}\;\mid\;a\succ 0\}.

We use the notation

Theorem 2.6

(\mathcal{R},+,\cdot,0,1,\succ)
is an ordered field and
(\mathcal{C},+,\cdot,0,1)
is its algebraic closure.

Now we can define an analogue of absolute value on
\mathcal{R}
as
|\cdot|:\mathcal{R}\to\mathcal{\mathcal{R}}^{+}\cup\{0\}

we define

|a|=\begin{cases}a,\mbox{ if }a\succeq 0,\\
-a,\mbox{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Moreover, the corresponding absolute value is

It is straightforward to prove, that

The number

is defined as follows:

d[q]=\begin{cases}1,q=1,\\

It is easy to see, that

Therefore, for

•

•

is

\lambda(a)<0

Moreover, we say that

3.
Properties of weak convergence

In this section, we prove auxiliary results on weak convergence over the Levi-Civita fields. The most interesting results here are Theorem
3.4
and Theorem
3.6
, which show us the continuity of weak convergence with respect to taking inverses and roots in some cases. These theorems are crucial in the proof of power iteration algorithm.

Firstly, we formulate an equivalent definition of weak convergence.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
(a_{n})
in
\mathcal{R}
(resp.
\mathcal{C}
) be a sequence. Then
a=wk\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}
if and only if
there exists
a\in\mathcal{R}
(resp.
\mathcal{C}
) such that for all
r_{0},r_{1}>0
in
\mathbb{R}
, there exists
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\|a_{n}-a\|_{r_{0}}<r_{1}\mbox{ for all }n>N_{0}.

Proof.

The “if” direction is clear by taking
r_{0}=1/r,r_{1}=r.

For the “only if direction” let us consider two cases. If
r_{1}r_{0}<1
we can estimate

\|a_{n}-a\|_{r_{0}}=\sup_{q_{i}\leq r_{0}}|a_{n}[q_{i}]-a[q_{i}]|\leq\sup_{q_{i}\leq 1/r_{1}}|a_{n}[q_{i}]-a[q_{i}]|=\|a_{n}-a\|_{1/r_{1}}<r_{1}.

If
r_{1}r_{0}\geq 1
we can argue in the following way:

\|a_{n}-a\|_{r_{0}}<1/r_{0}\leq r_{1}.

∎

Lemma 3.2
.

For a weakly convergent series
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}
in
\mathcal{R}
(resp.
\mathcal{C}
) the sequence
(a_{k})
converges weakly to zero.

Proof.

Let
s_{n}:=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}
and
s=wk\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}
be the limit. By Proposition
3.1
, for any
r_{0},r_{1}>0
in
\mathbb{R}
there is a
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\left\lVert s_{n}-s\right\rVert_{r_{0}}<r_{1}/2
for all
n\geq N
. Therefore, for any
n\geq N
we have,

\displaystyle\left\lVert s_{n}-s_{n+1}\right\rVert_{r_{0}}=\left\lVert s_{n}-s+s-s_{n+1}\right\rVert_{r_{0}}\leq\left\lVert s_{n}-s\right\rVert_{r_{0}}+\left\lVert s_{n+1}-s\right\rVert_{r_{0}}\leq r_{1}.

∎

.

Let

(resp.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
(a_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{\mathcal{R}}
and
a_{k}\xrightarrow{wk}a
as
k\to\infty

a[0]\neq 0
and the following conditions holds:

•

all
(a_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
a
are at most finite,

•

(a_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

Then

\dfrac{1}{a_{k}}\xrightarrow{wk}\dfrac{1}{a}.

Proof.

Firstly we prove, that the sequence
(b_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, where
b_{k}=\dfrac{1}{a_{k}}
, is regular. Let
\bar{q}\in\mathbb{Q}
be fixed. To prove that
(b_{k})
is regular, we need to prove that the set

\{q\leq\bar{q}\;\mid\;q\in\supp(b_{k}),k\in\mathbb{N}\}

is finite. Let us write
a_{k}
as

a_{k}=P_{k}+\alpha_{k},

where
\supp P_{k}\subset(-\infty,\bar{q}]
and
\supp\alpha_{k}\subset(\bar{q},+\infty)
. Further,

b_{k}=\dfrac{1}{a_{k}}=\dfrac{1}{P_{k}+\alpha_{k}}

and, consequently,

(2)

b_{k}+\dfrac{b_{k}\alpha_{k}}{P_{k}}=\dfrac{1}{P_{k}}.

Since
P_{k}[0]=a_{k}[0]\to a[0]
by Theorem
2.19

P_{k}[0]\neq 0
. Moreover,
P_{k}
is at most finite. Therefore,
\dfrac{1}{P_{k}}
coincides with the Maclaurin series of
P_{k}
in terms of variable
d^{\frac{1}{p}}
,
where

p={\operatorname{GCD}\left(\{n\;\mid\;\frac{m}{n}\in\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\supp(P_{k})\mbox{ is irreducible}\}\right)},

exists, since
\cup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\supp(P_{k})
is finite, due to the regularity of sequence
(a_{k})
.
Thus,
\left(\dfrac{1}{P_{k}}\right)_{k}
is regular. Further, note that for each
k
we have

\displaystyle\min\left(\supp\dfrac{b_{k}\alpha_{k}}{P_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=\min(\supp{b_{k}})+\min(\supp{\alpha_{k}})+\min\left(\supp\frac{1}{P_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle>0+\bar{q}+0=\bar{q}.

Therefore, due to (

(\supp b_{k})\cap(-\infty,\bar{q}]=\left(\supp\frac{1}{P_{k}}\right)\cap(-\infty,\bar{q}]

and

\{q\leq\bar{q}\;\mid\;q\in\supp(b_{k}),k\in\mathbb{N}\}=\{q\leq\bar{q}\;\mid\;q\in\supp\left(\frac{1}{P_{k}}\right),k\in\mathbb{N}\}

is finite, since
\left(\dfrac{1}{P_{k}}\right)_{k}
is regular. This yields the regularity of
b_{k}=\dfrac{1}{a_{k}}
.

Further, in order to prove
b_{k}\xrightarrow{wk}\dfrac{1}{a}
, let us denote

c_{k}:=b_{k}-\frac{1}{a}=\frac{1}{a_{k}}-\frac{1}{a}.

Note that
c_{k}
is at most finite. Further,
(c_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is regular, since
{b_{k}}
is regular and

(3)

a_{k}c_{k}=1-a_{k}\dfrac{1}{a}\xrightarrow{wk}0,

since
a_{k}\xrightarrow{wk}a
and weak convergence is continuous with respect to multiplication for the regular sequences by Lemma
2.22
.

Since
c_{k}
is regular and at most finite,

is left-finite and
\min(Q)\geq 0
, hence we can write

Q=(q_{i})_{i=0}^{\infty}
, where

and
(q_{i})
is strictly increasing.

Further, let us denote

and for

C_{i+1,k}:=C_{i,k}-C_{i,k}[q_{i}]d^{q_{i}}=C_{i,k}-c_{k}[q_{i}]d^{q_{i}}=\sum_{j=i+1}^{\infty}c_{k}[q_{j}]d^{q_{j}}.

Firstly, we prove by induction in

, that

(4)

a_{k}C_{i,k}\xrightarrow{wk}0.

Indeed, for
i=0

) leads to

a_{k}C_{0,k}=a_{k}c_{k}\xrightarrow{wk}0.

Assume (
4
) holds for

. Note that
\lambda(C_{i,k})\geq q_{i}
, hence,

a_{k}[0]c_{k}[q_{i}]=a_{k}[0]C_{i,k}[q_{i}]=(a_{k}C_{i,k})[q_{i}]\to 0\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R},

and, therefore,

(5)

c_{k}[q_{i}]\to 0\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R},

since
a_{k}[0]\to b[0]\neq 0
in
\mathbb{R}
. Hence,
c_{k}[q_{i}]d^{q_{i}}\xrightarrow{wk}0
.
Further, we have

a_{k}C_{i+1,k}=a_{k}C_{i,k}-a_{k}c_{k}[q_{i}]d^{q_{i}}\xrightarrow{wk}0,

since both terms converge weakly to

Altogether, (
4

in
\mathbb{R}
. Finally, since

c_{k}
is regular and

in
\mathbb{R}
for all

, we obtain by convergence criterium, Theorem

hence

∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Let

Let us denote

, we can assume, that

for all

and

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
(a_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{\mathcal{R}}
and
a_{k}\xrightarrow{wk}a
as
k\to\infty
. Let
a[0]\neq 0
and the following conditions holds:

•

all
(a_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
a
are at most finite,

•

(a_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is regular,

Then

\sqrt{a_{k}}\xrightarrow{wk}\sqrt{a}.

Proof.

Firstly, let us prove that the sequence
b_{k}:=\sqrt{a_{k}}
is regular. We use the approach similar to the one in proof of Theorem
3.4
. Let
\bar{q}\in\mathbb{Q}
be fixed. We write

(7)

a_{k}=P_{k}+\alpha_{k},

where
\supp P_{k}\subset(-\infty,\bar{q}]
and
\supp\alpha_{k}\subset(\bar{q},+\infty)
. W.l.o.g we can assume that
P_{k}[0]\neq 0
, since
a[0]\neq 0
. Further, let

b_{k}=R_{k}+\beta_{k},

where
\supp R_{k}\subset(-\infty,\bar{q}]
and
\supp\beta_{k}\subset(\bar{q},+\infty)
.
Then from
b_{k}^{2}=a_{k}
we conclude that
b_{k}
is at most finite, i.e. so is
R_{k}
, and
b_{k}[0]\neq 0
, moreover we get:

R_{k}^{2}+2\beta_{k}R_{k}+\beta_{k}^{2}=P_{k}+\alpha_{k},

and, comparing the supports of the summands, we get

R_{k}^{2}=_{\bar{q}}P_{k}.

Then we can conclude, that
R_{k}=_{\bar{q}}\sqrt{P_{k}}
. Indeed, assume

(8)

R_{k}=\sqrt{P_{k}}+S_{k},

where
S_{k}
is at most finite due to at most finiteness of
R_{k}
and
\sqrt{P_{k}}
. Then

R_{k}^{2}=P_{k}+2S_{k}\sqrt{P}_{k}+S_{k}^{2}=_{\bar{q}}P_{k}.

Therefore,

2S_{k}\sqrt{P}_{k}+S_{k}^{2}=_{\bar{q}}0,

and, hence,

(9)

\lambda\left(S_{k}\right)+\lambda\left(2\sqrt{P}_{k}+S_{k}\right)=\lambda\left(S_{k}(2\sqrt{P}_{k}+S_{k})\right)>\bar{q}.

Note that both summands are at most finite and
\left(2\sqrt{P}_{k}\right)[0]\neq 0
. Hence, for
\lambda_{0}:=\lambda\left(2\sqrt{P}_{k}+S_{k}\right)
we have two options:

•

if
\left(2\sqrt{P}_{k}\right)[0]\neq-S_{k}[0]
, then
\lambda_{0}=0
. Therefore,
\lambda\left(S_{k}\right)>\bar{q}
, from where, due to (
8
), follows
R_{k}=_{\bar{q}}\sqrt{P_{k}}
.

•

if
\left(2\sqrt{P}_{k}\right)[0]=-S_{k}[0]
and, hence,
\lambda(S_{k})=\lambda(-2\sqrt{P}_{k})=0
, then let us put

\overline{p}:=\lambda\left(2\sqrt{P}_{k}+S_{k}\right),

and we get

0+\overline{p}=\lambda\left(S_{k}\right)+\lambda\left(2\sqrt{P}_{k}+S_{k}\right)>\bar{q},

where the last inequality follows from (
9
), i.e.
\overline{p}>\bar{q}
, hence,

\lambda\left(2\sqrt{P}_{k}+S_{k}\right)>\overline{q},

which leads to
S_{k}=_{\bar{q}}-2\sqrt{P}_{k}
, i.e.
R_{k}=_{\bar{q}}~-\sqrt{P}_{k}
, and that is not possible, since
R_{k}\succ 0
by definition of the square root in an ordered field.

Therefore,
R_{k}=_{\bar{q}}\sqrt{P}_{k}
. We know that
(a_{k})
is regular, hence,
\cup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\supp(P_{k})
is finite by definition of
P_{k}
. Therefore, the sequence of Maclaurin expansions of
(\sqrt{P}_{k})_{k}
of variable
d^{\frac{1}{p}}
,
where

p={\operatorname{GCD}\left(\{n\;\mid\;\frac{m}{n}\in\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\supp(P_{k})\mbox{ is irreducible}\}\right)},

exists and is regular.
Then from

b_{k}=_{\bar{q}}R_{k}=_{\bar{q}}\sqrt{P}_{k},

follows, that the set

\{q\in\cup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\supp b_{k}\;\mid\;q<\bar{q}\}

is finite, i.e.
(b_{k})_{k}
is regular.

Moreover, from the above, we have that for any
\bar{q}
the following holds:

b_{k}[\bar{q}]=R_{k}[\bar{q}]=(\sqrt{P}_{k})[\bar{q}]

and due to Lemma
3.5
we have
(\sqrt{P}_{k})[\bar{q}]\to\left(\sqrt{P}\right)[\bar{q}]
,
where
P=wk\lim_{k\to\infty}P_{k}
. Further, due to (
7
) and
a_{k}\xrightarrow{wk}a
we can write

a=P+\alpha,

where
\alpha=wk\lim_{k\to\infty}\alpha_{k}
, i.e.
\supp\alpha\in(\bar{q},+\infty)
and by the same argument as above for
a_{k}
, we get
\sqrt{a}=_{\bar{q}}\sqrt{P}
. Therefore,

b_{k}[\bar{q}]=(\sqrt{P}_{k})[\bar{q}]\to(\sqrt{a})[\bar{q}]

in
\mathbb{R}
. Now due to an arbitrary choice of
\bar{q}
and regularity of
b_{k}
we can conclude by convergence criterium for weak convergence, Theorem
2.19
, that

b_{k}\xrightarrow{wk}\sqrt{a}.

∎

4.
Power iteration algorithm

In this section, we will prove the convergence of the power iteration algorithm in
\ell^{2}
-norm (Theorem
4.3
) and in maximum norm (Theorem
4.4
) under the certain natural conditions. These are the main results of this note.

In order to formulate the results, we introduce the definitions of the corresponding vector norms, which are similar to the classical case.

Definition 4.1
.

For any
\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{x}^{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}^{n})^{\mathrm{T}}\in\mathcal{R}^{n}
(resp.
\mathcal{C}^{n}
) a
maximum norm
is defined as follows

\|\mathbf{x}\|_{\max}=\max|\mathbf{x}^{i}|

and an
\ell^{2}
-norm
is given by

\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=\sqrt{|\mathbf{x}^{1}|^{2}+\dots+|\mathbf{x}^{n}|^{2}}.

Note that for
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{R}^{n}
or
\mathcal{C}^{n}
we have
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{\max},\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}\in\mathcal{R}^{+}\cup\{0\}
. Moreover,
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{\max}=\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=0
if and only if
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}:=(0,\dots,0)
. It follows directly from the definitions, that the triangle inequality holds in both cases.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let a matrix
A\in\mathcal{C}^{n,n}
be diagonalizable and its eigenvalues
\nu_{1},\dots\nu_{n}
are at most finite and satisfy the following condition

(10)

1=\nu_{1}[0]>\left|\nu_{2}[0]\right|\geq\dots\geq|\nu_{n}[0]|.

Further, let
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{C}^{n}
be a vector, such that for the representation

(11)

\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{w}_{1}+\mathbf{w}_{2}+\dots+\mathbf{w}_{n},

where
\mathbf{w}_{j}
is an eigenvector of
\nu_{j}
with at most finite entries holds:

(i)

all
\mathbf{w}_{j}
are at most finite,

(ii)

\mathbf{w}_{1}[0]\neq\mathbf{0}
,

Then the following holds:

1.

The sequence
\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{k}:=\sum_{j=2}^{n}\left(\frac{\nu_{j}}{\nu_{1}}\right)^{k}\mathbf{w}_{j}
is regular and

\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{k}\xrightarrow{wk}\mathbf{0}.

2.

\dfrac{1}{\|\mathbf{w}_{1}+\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{k}\|_{2}}\xrightarrow{wk}\dfrac{1}{\|\mathbf{w}_{1}\|_{2}}\

Moreover, if there exists a unique coordinate
i_{0}
such that
|\mathbf{w}^{i_{0}}_{1}[0]|=\max_{i}|\mathbf{w}^{i}_{1}[0]|
,
then

2^{enumi}.

\dfrac{1}{\|\mathbf{w}_{1}+\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{k}\|_{\max}}\xrightarrow{wk}\dfrac{1}{\|\mathbf{w}_{1}\|_{\max}}

Proof.

1.

Since for any
j>1
the ratio
\left(\dfrac{\nu_{j}}{\nu_{1}}\right)[0]
is at most finite and either
\left\lvert\left(\dfrac{\nu_{j}}{\nu_{1}}\right)[0]\right\rvert<1
or
\left(\dfrac{\nu_{j}}{\nu_{1}}\right)[0]=0
due to (
10
), the following holds

\left(\frac{\nu_{j}}{\nu_{1}}\right)^{k}\xrightarrow{wk}\mathbf{0}

due to Lemma
3.3
.

2.

holds due to a Definition
4.1
of
\ell^{2}
-norm, Lemma
2.22
and Theorems
3.4
and
3.6
.

2^{enumi}.

Firstly, let us show, that for
k
large enough

(12)

\|\mathbf{w}_{1}+\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{k}\|_{\max}=|\mathbf{w}_{1}^{i_{0}}+\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{k}^{i_{0}}|,

for the unique
i_{0}
such that
|\mathbf{w}_{1}^{i_{0}}[0]|=\max_{i}|\mathbf{w}_{1}^{i}[0]|
.
Indeed, for all
i
we have

(13)

\left|\mathbf{w}_{1}^{i}[0]+\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{k}^{i}[0]\right|\leq\left|\mathbf{w}_{1}^{i}[0]\right|+\left|\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{k}^{i}[0]\right|<|\mathbf{w}_{1}^{i_{0}}[0]|-|\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{k}^{i_{0}}[0]|\leq|\mathbf{w}_{1}^{i_{0}}[0]+\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{k}^{i_{0}}[0]|,

for all
k
such that

(14)

|\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{k}^{j}[0]|<\dfrac{\min_{i}\left(|\mathbf{w}_{1}^{i_{0}}[0]|-\left|\mathbf{w}_{1}^{i}[0]\right|\right)}{2}.

for all
j
. Note that condition (
14
) holds for all
k
large enough, since
\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{k}\xrightarrow{wk}\mathbf{0}
by (1) of the Theorem and, hence,
\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{k}[0]\to 0
in
\mathbb{R}
due to Theorem
2.19
. Then (
12
) follows from (
13
) due to the fact that
\mathbf{w}_{1}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{k}
are at most finite.

Moreover, we have
\mathbf{w}_{1}^{i_{0}}+\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{k}^{i_{0}}\xrightarrow{wk}\mathbf{w}_{1}^{i_{0}}
due to
\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{k}\xrightarrow{wk}\mathbf{0}
, and, further,

|\mathbf{w}_{1}^{i_{0}}+\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{k}^{i_{0}}|\xrightarrow{wk}|\mathbf{w}_{1}^{i_{0}}|,

Theorem 4.3

(Power iteration with
\ell^{2}
-norm)
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{C}^{n,n}
be a diagonalizable matrix and its eigenvalues
\nu_{1},\dots\nu_{n}\in\mathcal{R}
are at most finite and satisfy the following condition

(15)

1=\nu_{1}[0]>\left|\nu_{2}[0]\right|\geq\dots\geq|\nu_{n}[0]|.

Further, let
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{C}^{n}
a vector, such that for the representation

(16)

\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{w}_{1}+\mathbf{w}_{2}+\dots+\mathbf{w}_{n},

where
\mathbf{w}_{j}
is an eigenvector of
\nu_{j}
, holds:

(i)

all
\mathbf{w}_{j}
are at most finite,

(ii)

\mathbf{w}_{1}[0]\neq\mathbf{0}
,

Then for
\mathbf{x}_{k}:={A^{k}\mathbf{x}}
holds

\dfrac{\mathbf{x}_{k}}{\|\mathbf{x}_{k}\|_{2}}\xrightarrow{wk}\dfrac{\mathbf{w}_{1}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{1}\|_{2}}.

Proof.

The representation (
16
) for
\mathbf{x}
is unique, since
\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{n}
is a basis of
\mathcal{C}^{n}
. Moreover, we have

\mathbf{x}_{k}=A^{k}\mathbf{x}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\nu_{j}^{k}\mathbf{w}_{j}=\nu_{1}^{k}\left(\mathbf{w}_{1}+\sum_{j=2}^{n}\left(\frac{\nu_{j}}{\nu_{1}}\right)^{k}\mathbf{w}_{j}\right).

Let us denote
\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{k}:=\sum_{j=2}^{n}\left(\frac{\nu_{j}}{\nu_{1}}\right)^{k}\mathbf{w}_{j}
, i.e.
\mathbf{x}_{k}=\nu_{1}^{k}(\mathbf{w}_{1}+\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{k})
. Then

\dfrac{\mathbf{x}_{k}}{\|\mathbf{x}_{k}\|_{2}}=\dfrac{\nu_{1}^{k}(\mathbf{w}_{1}+\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{k})}{\nu_{1}^{k}\|\mathbf{w}_{1}+\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{k}\|_{2}}=\dfrac{\mathbf{w}_{1}+\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{k}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{1}+\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{k}\|_{2}},

where the first equality follows from
\nu_{1}\succ 0
(since
\nu_{1}
is at most finite and
\nu_{1}[0]=1
).
Therefore,

\dfrac{\mathbf{x}_{k}}{\|\mathbf{x}_{k}\|_{2}}=\left(\mathbf{w}_{1}+\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{k}\right)\dfrac{1}{\|\mathbf{w}_{1}+\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{k}\|_{2}}\xrightarrow{wk}\mathbf{w}_{1}\dfrac{1}{\|\mathbf{w}_{1}\|_{2}}

due to Lemma
4.2
, and continuity of weak convergence with respect to the addition and multiplication, Lemma
2.22
.
∎

Theorem 4.4

(Power iteration with maximum norm)
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
4.3
if there
exists a unique coordinate
i_{0}
such that

|\mathbf{w}^{i_{0}}_{1}[0]|=\max_{i}|\mathbf{w}^{i}_{1}[0]|,

then for
\mathbf{x}_{k}:={A^{k}\mathbf{x}}
holds

\dfrac{\mathbf{x}_{k}}{\|\mathbf{x}_{k}\|_{\max}}\xrightarrow{wk}\dfrac{\mathbf{w}_{1}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{1}\|_{\max}}.

The proof is completely analogues to the proof of Theorem

due to 2* of Lemma
4.2
.

Remark 4.5
.

Observe that the condition (
16
) holds for all
\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{x}^{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}^{n})^{\mathrm{T}}\in~\mathcal{C}^{n}
with at most finite entries, since

\mathbf{w}_{i}=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbf{x}^{k}\overline{\mathbf{v}}_{i}^{k}\right)\mathbf{v}_{i},

where
\mathbf{v}_{i}

Proposition 4.6

(Power iteration for eigenvalues)
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{C}^{n\times n}
be a diagonalizable matrix with at most finite entries, let
\mathbf{v}
be its eigenvector with at most finite entries, corresponding to the eigenvalue
\nu
and
\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}[0]>0
. Let
(\mathbf{u}_{k})_{k}\subset\mathcal{C}^{n}
be such that each
(\mathbf{u}_{k})
has at most finite entries. If
\mathbf{u}_{k}\xrightarrow{wk}\mathbf{v}
pointwise and is regular pointwise, then

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbf{u}_{k}^{*}A\mathbf{u}_{k}}{\|\mathbf{u}_{k}\|^{2}_{2}}\xrightarrow{wk}\nu,

where
\mathbf{u}_{k}^{*}=(\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{1},\dots\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{2},\dots,\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{n})
.

Proof.

Firstly, the sequences
(\|\mathbf{u}_{k}\|_{2}^{2})_{k}\subset\mathcal{R}
and
(\mathbf{u}_{k}^{*}A\mathbf{u}_{k})_{k}\subset\mathcal{C}
are at most finite by direct calculations and regular by Lemma
2.20
. Further,
\|\mathbf{u}_{k}\|^{2}\to\|\mathbf{v}\|^{2}
and
{\mathbf{u}_{k}^{*}A\mathbf{u}_{k}}\xrightarrow{wk}\mathbf{v}^{*}A\mathbf{v}
by Lemma
2.22
. Then by Theorem
3.4
we get that

\dfrac{1}{\|\mathbf{u}_{k}\|^{2}}\to\dfrac{1}{\|\mathbf{v}\|^{2}},

from where, again by Lemma
2.22
, follows

\dfrac{\mathbf{u}_{k}^{*}A\mathbf{u}_{k}}{\|\mathbf{u}_{k}\|^{2}}\to\dfrac{\mathbf{v}^{*}A\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|^{2}}.

Finally,

\dfrac{\mathbf{v}^{*}A\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|^{2}}=\dfrac{\mathbf{v}^{*}\nu\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|^{2}}=\nu.

∎

5.
Applications of power iteration algorithm

In Theorem
4.3
and Theorem
4.4
we assumed that for the eigenvalues
\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{n}\in\mathcal{C}
of matrix
A\in\mathcal{C}^{n\times n}
the following conditions are fulfilled:

(\mathrm{C}1)

all
\nu_{i}
are at most finite,

(\mathrm{C}2)

\nu_{1}[0]>\left|\nu_{2}[0]\right|\geq\dots\geq|\nu_{n}[0]|,

(\mathrm{C}3)

\nu_{1}[0]=1
,

In Proposition
5.1
we show that the first condition is fulfilled for any matrix with at most finite entries. Further, in Proposition
5.3
we show, that for a matrix
A\in\mathcal{C}^{n\times n}
with at most finite entries complex parts of eigenvalues
(i.e.
\nu[0]
) can be obtained as eigenvalues of the matrix
A[0]=(a_{ij}[0])
for
A=(a_{ij})
, i.e. the condition (C2) can be checked without knowledge of eigenvalues of
A
. Moreover, then
the matrix
A/\nu_{1}[0]
can be calculated, and will satisfy all conditions (C1)–(C3), see Corollary
5.5
.

Further in this Section, we discuss, how the power iteration algorithm can be applied for some matrices with infinitely large entries or whose all entries are infinitely small, see Theorem
5.5
and we show, that the power iteration algorithm, applied to the matrices over the field of Puiseux series leads to the result be an element of the same field, Corollary
5.7
.

Proposition 5.1

(Gershgorin Circle Theorem)
.

Let
A=(a_{ij})\in\mathcal{C}^{n\times n}
. The unity of the circles

K:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\{z\in\mathcal{C}\>\mid\;|z-a_{ii}|\preceq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1,\\
k\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}|a_{ik}|\;\}

contains all eigenvalues of
A
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{v}
be an eigenvector of
A
,
\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\max}=1
, and
\nu
be the corresponding eigenvalue. Let
j
be such that
|\mathbf{v}^{j}|=\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\max}=1
.

From
A\mathbf{v}=\nu\mathbf{v}
follows:

(a_{jj}-\nu)\mathbf{v}^{j}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1,\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}^{n}a_{jk}\mathbf{v}^{k}=0.

Hence,

\left\lvert(a_{jj}-\nu)\right\rvert=\left\lvert(a_{jj}-\nu)\right\rvert|\mathbf{v}^{j}|=\left\lvert(a_{jj}-\nu)\mathbf{v}^{j}\right\rvert\preceq\big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1,\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}^{n}a_{jk}\mathbf{v}^{k}\big|\preceq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1,\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}^{n}\left\lvert a_{jk}\right\rvert,

where the last inequality is true due to the triangle inequality and since

|\mathbf{v}^{k}|\preceq\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\max}=1.

∎

Next, we show in Proposition
5.3
, that in the case of at most finite matrix, the set of complex parts of its eigenvalues coincides with the set of eigenvalues of the complex part of the matrix. In order to do this we need the following notation: for any
a\in\mathcal{R}
(resp.
\mathcal{C}
) we denote by
\pi(a)
the
real part
(resp.
complex part
) of
a
, i.e.

\pi:\mathcal{R}\mbox{ (resp. }\mathcal{C}\mbox{)}\to\mathbb{R}\mbox{ (resp. }\mathbb{C}\mbox{)}:a\to a[0].

The following immediately follows:

Lemma 5.2
.

For all at most finite
a,b\in\mathcal{R}
(resp.
\mathcal{C}
) the map
\pi
is linear, i.e.

•

\pi(a+b)=\pi(a)+\pi(b),

•

\pi(ab)=\pi(a)\cdot\pi(b).

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{C}^{n\times n}
be a matrix with at most finite entries. Then

\pi(\sigma(A))=\sigma(\pi(A)),

where
\sigma
denotes the spectrum of a matrix.

Proof.

Firstly, we introduce a mapping
\pi_{P}
, corresponding to
\pi
, on polynomials over
\mathcal{C}
with at most finite coefficients

\pi_{P}:\mathcal{C}[x]\to\mathbb{C}[x]:\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}x^{i}\to\sum_{i=0}^{n}\pi(a_{i})x^{i}.

Secondly, we note, that due to Gershgorin Circle Theorem Proposition
5.1
, every eigenvalue
\nu
of
A
is at most finite.

Let us denote by
\chi
the characteristic polynomial of
A
. Then, by the linearity of
\pi
, Lemma
5.2
,
\pi(A)
has the characteristic polynomial
\pi_{P}(\chi)
.

“
\pi(\sigma(A))\subseteq\sigma(\pi(A))
”

Let

\chi=\sum_{i=0}^{n}b_{i}x^{i}.

Let
\nu\in\sigma(A)
. Then
\chi(\nu)=0
and we get

(\pi_{P}(\chi))(\pi(\nu))=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi(b_{i})(\pi(\nu))^{i}=\pi\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}\nu^{i}\right)=\pi(\chi(\nu))=0,

where the second equality is true due to linearity of
\pi
, Lemma
5.2
.
Hence,
\pi(\nu)\in\sigma(\pi(A))
.

“
\pi(\sigma(A))\supseteq\sigma(\pi(A))
”. Since
\mathcal{C}
is algebraically closed, we can write

\chi=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(\nu_{i}-x),

where
\nu_{i}
are eigenvalues of
A
. Then again due to the linearity of
\pi
, Lemma
5.2
, we obtain

\pi_{P}(\chi)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(\pi(\nu_{i})-x),

is a linear factor representation of
\pi_{P}(\chi)
which is known to be unique. Therefore for any
\mu\in\sigma(\pi(A))
, i.e
(\pi_{P}(\chi))(\mu)=0
we conclude that there exists
\pi(\nu_{i})=\mu
, i.e.
\mu\in\pi(\sigma(A))
.

∎

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{C}^{n\times n}
be an at most finite diagonalizable matrix. If all the eigenvalues of
\pi(A)
have different absolute values, then all the eigenvalues of
A
can be found by the power iteration method.

Proof.

This can be done, for example via subspace iteration. Furthermore, it is possible to use the inverse vector iteration. All of these methods are based directly on the power iteration.
∎

Let
B\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
. We say that
B
has a
dominated eigenvalue

\mu_{1}
if

|\mu_{1}|>\left|\mu_{2}\right|\geq\dots\geq|\mu_{n}|,

where
\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\dots\mu_{n}
are eigenvalues of
B
.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{C}^{n\times n}
be diagonalizable. Let
A^{\prime}=d^{-q_{0}}A
, where
q_{0}=\min_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\lambda(a_{ij})
. If the matrix
\pi(A^{\prime})\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
has a dominated eigenvalue, then the largest in modulus eigenvalue of
A
and a corresponding eigenvector can be found by power iteration method.

Proof.

Due to the definition of
A^{\prime}
all its entries are at most finite. Assume the eigenvalues of the matrix
\pi(A^{\prime})
to be

|\mu_{1}|>\left|\mu_{2}\right|\geq\dots\geq|\mu_{n}|.

Due to Proposition
5.3
we conclude, that the eigenvalues of
A^{\prime}
satisfy

|\nu^{\prime}_{1}[0]|>\left|\nu^{\prime}_{2}[0]\right|\geq\dots\geq|\nu^{\prime}_{n}[0]|.

and
\mu_{1}=\nu^{\prime}_{1}[0]
.
Therefore, one can apply Theorem
4.3
or Theorem
4.4
and Proposition
4.6
afterward to the matrix
A^{\prime}/\mu_{1}
to obtain its largest eigenvalue
\nu
. Then the largest in modulus eigenvalue of
A
is

\nu_{1}=\nu\mu_{1}d^{-q_{0}}.

∎

Remark 5.6
.

We do not discuss in this note, which matrices over
\mathcal{R}
or
\mathcal{C}
are diagonalizable. For example, symmetric matrices and matrices of symmetric operators on
\mathcal{R}^{n}
are diagonalizable over
\mathcal{R}
. Further, the companion matrices of polynomials with pairwise different roots in
\mathcal{R}
or
\mathcal{C}
are diagonalizable, see Section 6.

Now we will formulate Corollary
5.7
, which allows us to apply the power iteration algorithm to the field of Puiseux series. Let us recollect, that for
\mathbb{K}
being the field of real or complex numbers, the field of
Puiseux series
is a set of formal power series:

\mathbb{K}\{\{t\}\}=\left\{\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{\infty}s_{k}t^{\frac{k}{m}}\,:\,s_{k}\in\mathbb{K}\text{ and }k_{0}\in\mathbb{Z},m\in\mathbb{N}\right\}

with sum and multiplication defined naturally as for formal series.

The field of Puiseux series
\mathbb{R}\{\{t\}\}
is a subfield of the Levi-Civita field
\mathcal{R}
due to Proposition
2.11
. Moreover,
\mathbb{R}\{\{t\}\}
is a real-closed field itself, while
\mathbb{C}\{\{t\}\}
is algebraically closed, see e.g.
[
10
]
. This field is much more used in algebraic geometry and computer science, since Cauchy completeness with respect to strong convergence, which the Levi-Civita field possesses, is not usually needed there. Note that
\mathbb{R}\{\{t\}\}\subset\mathcal{R}
and
\mathbb{C}\{\{t\}\}\subset\mathcal{C}
are subfields, therefore for the elements
a\in\mathbb{R}\{\{t\}\}
(resp.
\mathbb{C}\{\{t\}\}
) apply the same notations as for
a\in\mathcal{R}
(resp.
C
), in particular if
a=\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{\infty}s_{k}t^{\frac{k}{m}}
, then

\lambda(a):=\dfrac{k_{0}}{m},

and if in addition
k_{0}\geq 0
, then

\pi(a)=\begin{cases}0,k_{0}>0,\\
k_{0},\mbox{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Then the following holds for matrices over the fields of Puiseux series:

Corollary 5.7
.

Let a matrix
A\in\mathbb{K}\{\{t\}\}^{n\times n}
be diagonalizable over
\mathbb{K}\{\{t\}\}
,
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
. Let
A^{\prime}=t^{-q_{0}}A
, where
q_{0}=\min_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\lambda(a_{ij})
. If the matrix
\pi(A^{\prime})\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
has a dominated eigenvalue, then the largest in modulus eigenvalue of
A
and corresponding eigenvector can be found by power iteration method.

Proof.

Is an immediate consequence of Theorem
5.5
.
∎

6.
Complexity and experimental results

We now have a look at the time complexity of the power iteration algorithm and the cost of arithmetic operations of polynomials. We investigate the complexity analysis first for companion matrices of polynomials
P
over
\mathbb{K}\{\{t\}\}
, whose coefficients are polynomials in
t
.
Looking at our matrix-vector multiplication with
A\in\mathbb{K}\{\{t\}\}^{n\times n}
and
A
being a companion matrix, we have
n
multiplications. Using the FFT Polynomial Multiplication we can assume
\mathcal{O}(nd_{t}\log(d_{t}))
complexity, where
d_{t}
is the maximum degree regarding
t
over all matrix entries. In our normalization step, we have to add and square our polynomials, resulting in a complexity of
\mathcal{O}(d_{t}\log(d_{t})n)
. Root taking and inversion can be neglected with a cost of
\mathcal{O}(d_{t}\log(d_{t}))

[
31
]
. Concluding our analysis, we have a time complexity of
\mathcal{O}(d_{t}\log(d_{t})n)
. For arbitrary matrices, we have
n^{2}
matrix multiplications resulting in a complexity of
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}d_{t}\log(d_{t}))
.
