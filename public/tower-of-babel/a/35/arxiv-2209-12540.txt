The Generalized Cluster Complex: Refined Enumeration of Faces and Related Parking Spaces
Theo DOUVROPOULOS a and Matthieu JOSUAT-VERGÈS b

Abstract
The generalized cluster complex was introduced by Fomin and Reading, as a natural extension of the Fomin–Zelevinsky cluster complex coming from finite type cluster algebras. In this work, to each face of this complex we associate a parabolic conjugacy class of the underlying finite Coxeter group. We show that the refined enumeration of faces (respectively, positive faces) according to this data gives an explicit formula in terms of the corresponding characteristic polynomial (equivalently, in terms of Orlik–Solomon exponents). This characteristic polynomial originally comes from the theory of hyperplane arrangements, but it is conveniently defined via the parabolic Burnside ring. This makes a connection with the theory of parking spaces: our results eventually rely on some enumeration of chains of noncrossing partitions that were obtained in this context. The precise relations between the formulas counting faces and the one counting chains of noncrossing partitions are combinatorial reciprocities, generalizing the one between Narayana and Kirkman numbers.

cluster complex; parking functions; noncrossing partitions; Fuß–Catalan numbers; finite Coxeter groups

Introduction

The
cluster complex
of a finite type cluster algebra was introduced by Fomin and Zelevinsky
[
18
]
. It is a simplicial complex, which can be built using
almost positive roots
as vertices. It can be viewed as the dual of a corresponding
associahedron
. A natural extension is the
generalized cluster complex
, defined by Fomin and Reading
[
17
]
via
colored almost-positive roots
. Although there is no related cluster algebra nor associahedron in this case, the generalized cluster complex can be given a representation theoretic interpretation via quiver representations
[
39
,
43
]
. Most importantly, this is a simplicial complex with nice enumerative and topological properties
[
6
,
7
,
18
,
38
,
40
]
. In particular, its number of facets is the
Fuß–Catalan number

\Cat^{(m)}(W):=\frac{1}{|W|}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(mh+e_{i}+1),

and its number of positive facets is the
positive Fuß–Catalan number

\Cat^{(m)}_{+}(W):=\frac{1}{|W|}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(mh+e_{i}-1).

Here,
W
is a finite and irreducible real reflection group,
h
is its Coxeter number, and
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
is the sequence of exponents. Moreover, the
Fuß parameter

m
is the number of different colors that a positive root can have. See Section
6
for details.

Consider a
flat
, i.e., an element
X
in the intersection lattice
L(W)
generated by the reflecting hyperplanes of
W
. There is an associated hyperplane arrangement on
X
called the
restricted arrangement
(a general reference on this subject is Orlik and Terao
[
28
]
). It turns out to be a
free arrangement
, so that its
characteristic polynomial

p_{X}(t)
is factorized in the form
\prod_{i=1}^{\dim(X)}\big(t-b_{i}^{X}\big)
where the roots
b_{i}^{X}
are positive integers called the
Orlik–Solomon exponents
. (Two flats that are congruent under the action of
W
have the same characteristic polynomial and we consider that indices are orbits of flats.) It has been established that these characteristic polynomials can be used to refine the enumeration of some Catalan families. In particular, Sommers
[
35
]
considers certain ideals in Lie theory (that correspond to the combinatorial notion of
nonnesting partitions
). He showed in
[
35
, Theorem 5.7]
that the number of such ideals (respectively, positive ideals) is the Catalan number
\Cat(W):=\Cat^{(1)}(W)
(
respectively, the positive Catalan number
\Cat_{+}(W):=\Cat^{(1)}_{+}(W)
)
. Moreover, he showed in
[
35
, Proposition 6.6]
that the number of ideals (respectively, positive ideals) associated to the orbit of a flat
X
under a certain natural map is

\frac{p_{X}(h+1)}{[N(W_{X}):W_{X}]},\qquad\text{respectively,}\qquad\frac{p_{X}(h-1)}{[N(W_{X}):W_{X}]}.

(Here, the denominator is the index of the parabolic subgroup
W_{X}
in its normalizer, see Section
3
for details.) These are known as
Kreweras numbers
. They respectively add up to
\Cat(W)
and
\Cat_{+}(W)
when we sum over all orbits of flats (or, to the
Narayana numbers

\operatorname{Nar}(W,k)
and
\operatorname{Nar}_{+}(W,k)
if we sum over orbits of flats of dimension
k
). The Fuß generalization also exists: see Rhoades
[
31
, Section 9]
(it relies on Athanasiadis’ notion of co-filtered chain of ideals
[
4
]
).

Another Catalan family consists of
noncrossing partitions
(see
[
8
]
for a recent survey). They are particularly important here because of their close connection to the cluster complex. A refined enumeration of these objects akin to Sommers’ exists: indeed Athanasiadis and Reiner
[
5
, Theorem 6.3]
had previously shown that such refined enumerations of nonnesting and noncrossing partitions coincide (without giving the explicit formulas in terms of characteristic polynomials). However, this coincidence is proved via a case-by-case check using the finite type classification and finding a more conceptual explanation is still an open problem. The most promising attempt in this direction is
parking space theory
, introduced by Armstrong, Reiner and Rhoades
[
3
]
: among various other features, it gives a representation theoretic framework to prove this kind of refined enumeration. In particular, the characteristic polynomials
p_{X}(t)
naturally appear there via an identity in the parabolic Burnside ring (see Orlik and Solomon
[
27
]
, or Section
3
), rather than via hyperplane arrangements. This theory was also extended in the Fuß–Catalan setting by Rhoades
[
31
]
. This will be reviewed in Section
3
. Let us just mention here that
m
-element chains (respectively, full support
m
-element chains) of noncrossing partitions are counted by
\Cat^{(m)}(W)

(
respectively,
\Cat_{+}^{(m)}(W)
)
, and a natural refinement gives the numbers:

\displaystyle\frac{p_{X}(mh+1)}{[N(W_{X}):W_{X}]},\qquad\text{respectively,}\qquad\frac{p_{X}(mh-1)}{[N(W_{X}):W_{X}]}.

(1.1)

See Theorems
3.10
and
3.12
for the precise statements.

The entries of the
f
-vector of the cluster complex (i.e., the number of faces of a given dimension) are known as the
Kirkman numbers
. It is known that Kirkman numbers and Narayana numbers are related by a combinatorial reciprocity, and one of our goals is to refine this phenomenon: in Corollary
7.3
, we show that the number of faces (respectively, positive faces) associated to the orbit of a flat
X
under a certain natural map is

\displaystyle(-1)^{\dim(X)}\frac{p_{X}(-mh-1)}{[N(W_{X}):W_{X}]},\qquad\text{respectively,}\qquad(-1)^{\dim(X)}\frac{p_{X}(-mh+1)}{[N(W_{X}):W_{X}]}.

(1.2)

The meaning of “combinatorial reciprocity” is that these numbers are related to those in (
1.1
) by
m\leftrightarrow-m
. (The combinatorial reciprocity mentioned above between Narayana and Kirkman numbers is recovered by summing over orbit of flats of a given dimension
k
.) The formulas in (
1.2
) gives our refined enumeration of the faces of the generalized cluster complex. The derivation is case-free, but eventually relies on Theorem
3.10
mentioned above (for which the only known proof is via a case-by-case check).

There are a few preliminaries that are interesting on their own, as well as nice consequences. We outline both by giving the detailed organization of this article:

2
contains some background material.

Parking space theory
is reviewed in Section
3
, following
[
3
,
31
]
. The aim is to present Theorems
3.10
and
3.12
, on which our results rely. Sections
4
and
5
contain proofs of the latter theorem (more precisely, we show the equivalence between Theorems
3.10
and
3.12
).

The
generalized cluster complex
is reviewed in Section
6
, following
[
10

17
,
18

40
]
. This section also contains the definition of the natural map from faces to orbits of flats. The formulas in terms of characteristic polynomials are obtained in Section
7
, via combinatorial reciprocities which make a link with chains of noncrossing partitions. It is also proved in Section
8
via a bijection, again making a link with certain chains of noncrossing partitions.
In Section
9
, we give some consequences concerning the
f
-vectors and
h
-vectors of the generalized cluster complex. In particular, an identity can be seen as a refinement of the relations between
f
- and

-vectors. Finally, Section
10

1.2
), proved via the combinatorics of the generalized cluster complex.

Minimal factorizations
of the Coxeter element are ubiquitous in the context of noncrossing partitions and cluster complexes. In Section

, we get a formula for a
q
-enumeration of certain minimal factorizations (where one factor is in a given parabolic conjugacy class and the others are reflections). This is related to the enumeration of faces of the generalized cluster complex, via a recursion which is equivalent to that in Section

on noncrossing partitions, denoted
\sqsubset
and
\ll
, are used throughout. We introduced them in

, and in some sense they refine the absolute order (used to define the lattice structure on noncrossing partitions). They are useful to prove an identity on parking spaces (Section

2
Preliminary definitions

Through this work,
W
is a finite real reflection group of rank
n
. We don’t assume that it be irreducible, unless stated otherwise. Its geometric representation is an
n
-dimensional Euclidean space
V\simeq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
T\subset W
denote the set of reflections, and
S\subset T
a set of simple reflections that we write
S=\{s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\}
. The standard parabolic subgroup
W_{I}
for
I\subset S
is the subgroup of
W
generated by
I
. Any subgroup conjugate to some
W_{I}
for
I\subset S
is called a parabolic subgroup. The
support
of
w\in W
is

\supp(w):=\min\big\{I\subset S\colon w\in W_{I}\big\},

where the minimum is taken with respect to inclusion, and
w
is said to have
full support
if
\supp(w)=S
.

2.1
The intersection lattice

Definition 2.1
.

For each
w\in W
, we denote

\Fix(w):=\ker(w-I)=\big\{v\in V\colon w(v)=v\big\}.

The
intersection lattice
of
W
is defined as

L(W):=\big\{\Fix(w)\colon w\in W\big\}.

By convention, the order relation on
L(W)
is reverse inclusion.

In type
A_{n-1}
, this is the lattice of set partitions of
\{1,\dots,n\}
ordered by refinement.

The poset
L(W)
is a lattice, and the join operation is given by intersection of subspaces. It is order-isomorphic to the lattice of parabolic subgroups of
W
(where the order is inclusion) via

\displaystyle X\mapsto W_{X}:=\{w\in W\colon X\subset\Fix(w)\}.

(2.1)

There is a natural action of
W
on
L(W)
induced by the action of
W
on
V
, and the corresponding action on parabolic subgroups is by conjugation. Let
\sim
denote the equivalence class given by the orbit decomposition.

The apparent clash of notation between
W_{I}
and
W_{X}
is dealt with by identifying the powerset of
S
with a subposet of
L(W)
, via

I\mapsto\Fix\Big(\prod_{s\in I}s\Big).

(The order of the product is given by a fixed total order on
S
.) Implicitly, it is assumed that
I
,
J
,
K
are in this subposet, while
X
,
Y
,
Z
are general flats in
L(W)
. In particular,
I\sim J
means that the standard parabolic subgroups
W_{I}
and
W_{J}
are conjugate. This convention will be used when some objects are indexed sometimes by
I
,
J
,
K
and sometimes by
X
,
Y
,
Z
.

2.2
The noncrossing partition lattice

Noncrossing partitions are defined with respect to a
standard Coxeter element

c
, which is the product of all the simple reflections in
S
. By reindexing the set
S=\{s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\}
, we can assume
c=s_{1}\cdots s_{n}
.

Definition 2.2
.

The
reflection length
of
w\in W
is defined by

\ell(w):=n-\dim(\Fix(w)).

It is also the minimal integer
k
such that
w
is a product of
k
reflections, and any such factorization
w=t_{1}\cdots t_{k}
is called
minimal
.

Definition 2.3
.

The
absolute order
of
W
is defined by
w_{1}\leq w_{2}
if
\ell(w_{1})+\ell\big(w_{1}^{-1}w_{2}\big)=\ell(w_{2})
. Alternatively,
w_{1}\leq w_{2}
iff a minimal reflection factorization of
w_{1}
is a subword of a minimal reflection factorization of
w_{2}
. The
noncrossing partition lattice
of
W
(with respect to a standard Coxeter element
c
), denoted
\NC(W,c)
, is defined as the order ideal containing elements below
c
in the absolute order.

This is a widely studied object, and we refer to Baumeister et al.
[
8
]
for a recent survey. Note that we have a map
w\mapsto\Fix(w)
from
\NC(W,c)
to
L(W)
. This map is injective, increasing and rank-preserving. Moreover,
w_{1},w_{2}\in\NC(W,c)
are conjugate (in
W
) iff
\Fix(w_{1})
and
\Fix(w_{2})
are in the same orbit under the action of
W
.

For each integer
m\geq 1
, we can define the
Fuß–Catalan numbers
and their
positive
counterpart:

\displaystyle\Cat^{(m)}(W):=\#\big\{(\pi_{i})_{1\leq i\leq m}\in\NC(W,c)^{m}\colon\pi_{1}\leq\dots\leq\pi_{m}\big\},

\displaystyle\Cat_{+}^{(m)}(W):=\#\big\{(\pi_{i})_{1\leq i\leq m}\in\NC(W,c)^{m}\colon\pi_{1}\leq\dots\leq\pi_{m},\;\supp(w_{m})=S\big\}.

In the case where
W
is irreducible, the formulas given in the introduction in terms of exponents and the Coxeter number hold. Otherwise, we have

\displaystyle\Cat^{(m)}\Bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{j}W_{i}\Bigg)=\prod_{i=1}^{j}\Cat^{(m)}\big(W_{i}\big),

\displaystyle\Cat_{+}^{(m)}\Bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{j}W_{i}\Bigg)=\prod_{i=1}^{j}\Cat_{+}^{(m)}\big(W_{i}\big).

The rank-refined enumeration of noncrossing partitions gives
Narayana numbers
. One can also define Fuß–Narayana numbers by (see
[
1
]
):

\displaystyle\operatorname{Nar}^{(m)}(W,i):=\#\big\{\!(\pi_{i})_{1\leq i\leq m}\in\NC(W,c)^{m}\colon\pi_{1}\leq\!\cdots\!\leq\pi_{m},\,\ell(\pi_{1})=i\big\},

(2.2)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Nar}_{+}^{(m)}(W,i):=\#\big\{\!(\pi_{i})_{1\leq i\leq m}\in\NC(W,c)^{m}\colon\pi_{1}\leq\!\cdots\!\leq\pi_{m},\,\ell(\pi_{1})=i,\,\supp(w_{1})=S\big\}.\!\!\!\!\!\!

(2.3)

Lemma 2.4
.

The following two sets are in bijection:

•

parabolic conjugacy classes
, i.e., conjugacy classes
\mathcal{X}\subset W
such that
\mathcal{X}\cap\NC(W,c)\neq\varnothing
,

•

L(W)/W
, i.e., orbits of flats under the action of
W
.

Proof.

We refer to
[
27
]
(see Lemma (3.4), Lemma (3.5), and the lines thereafter). Let us briefly describe the explicit bijections.

To each parabolic conjugacy class
\mathcal{X}
, we associate the orbit of
\Fix(w)
for some arbitrary
w\in\mathcal{X}
. In the other direction, consider a flat
X
defined up to the action of
W
. There is
I\subset S
such that
W_{X}
is conjugate to the standard parabolic subgroup
W_{I}
, and to the orbit of
X
we associate the conjugacy class of
\prod_{s\in I}s
(the order of the product is irrelevant).
∎

For
X\in L(W)
, the parabolic conjugacy class corresponding to its orbit in
L(W)/W
(via the previous bijection) is denoted
\mathcal{X}
. We sometimes use the bijection implicitly and the notation makes clear what are the objects. For example, if
w\in\NC(W,c)
then the condition
w\in\mathcal{X}
is equivalent to
\Fix(w)\sim X
.

In type
A_{n-1}
, every conjugacy class contains a noncrossing partition. The sets in the previous lemma identify with the set of integer partitions of
n
.

Lemma 2.5
.

For each
w\in\NC(W,c)
, the parabolic subgroup
W_{\Fix(w)}
is the minimal parabolic subgroup of
W
containing
w
.

Proposition 2.6
.

A noncrossing partition
w\in\NC(W,c)
is a standard Coxeter element of
W_{\Fix(w)}
. This means there is a factorization
w=t_{1}\cdots t_{k}
(called the
canonical factorization
, unique up to commutations among the factors) where the elements
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}
are the simple generators of
W_{\Fix(w)}
.

This has been observed by several authors. We refer to
[
9
, Proposition 3.1]
for a discussion.

2.3
A tale of two orders

The lattice structure of
\NC(W,c)
(the absolute order) can be refined: we introduced in
[
9
]
two partial order relations
\sqsubset
and
\ll
with many combinatorial properties. In particular, they are useful to deal with the combinatorics of the cluster complex.

Definition 2.7

(
[
9
]
)
.

Let
w\in\NC(W,c)
, and write its canonical factorization
w=t_{1}\cdots t_{k}
. We define
\sqsubset
and
\ll
on
\NC(W,c)
by:

•

v\sqsubset w
if
v
can be written as a subword of
t_{1}\cdots t_{k}
(so that
v\leq w
, in particular),

•

v\ll w
if
v\leq w
and
v
has full support in
W_{\Fix(w)}
, i.e., each
t_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq k
appears at least once in any factorization
v=t_{i_{1}}t_{i_{2}}\cdots
.

Note that these partial orders are such that
v\sqsubset w\Rightarrow v\leq w
and
v\ll w\Rightarrow v\leq w
.

Another characterization of these partial orders makes a close connection with the
Bruhat order
, denoted
\leq_{B}
. This will be used in Section
11
. It states that the cover relations for
\sqsubset
and
\ll
are such that

u\sqsubset\hskip-9.6739pt\cdot\hskip 9.6739ptv\;\Leftrightarrow\;u\lessdot v\quad\text{and}\quad u\leq_{B}v,\qquad u\ll\hskip-7.11317pt\cdot\hskip 7.11317ptv\;\Leftrightarrow\;u\lessdot v\quad\text{and}\quad u\geq_{B}v.

Let us give some other properties, mostly taken from
[
9
]
.

Proposition 2.8
.

For each
u,w\in\NC(W,c)
such that
u\leq w
, there exists a unique
v\in\NC(W,c)
such that
u\ll v\sqsubset w
.

(See also Lemma
8.3
for a related result.)

Proof.

Let us first consider the case where
w
is maximal, i.e., it is the Coxeter element
c
. In this case, let
I\subset S
be the support of
u
and
v
be the unique
v\sqsubset c
which is the product of
s_{i}
for
i\in I
. It is easily checked that it satisfies
u\ll v\sqsubset w
, and it is unique. The general case follows by doing the same procedure in the parabolic subgroup
W_{\Fix(w)}
.
∎

Proposition 2.9

(
[
9
, Corollary 4.10]
)
.

Let
w\in\NC(W,c)
. We have

•

The two orders
\leq
and
\sqsubset
agree on the set
\{v\in\NC(W,c)\colon v\sqsubset w\}
. The resulting poset is a boolean lattice of order
2^{\ell(w)}
, containing all elements that can be written as subwords of the canonical factorization of
w
.

•

The two orders
\leq
and
\ll
agree on the set
\{v\in\NC(W,c)\colon v\gg w\}
. The resulting poset is a boolean lattice of order
2^{\#\supp(w)-\ell(w)}
, and its maximal element is the unique
w^{\prime}
such that
w\ll w^{\prime}\sqsubset c
(given by the previous proposition).

3
Parking spaces and their characters

The goal of this section is to introduce some background on parking space theory, as well as a “prime” analog. The main result about the prime parking space is Theorem
3.12
.

Remark 3.1
.

The terminology is not well-established, but there does exist a notion of “prime parking function”. See
[
16
]
for a proof that there are
(n-1)^{n-1}
prime parking functions of length
n
. Our prime parking space is the natural analog in the Coxeter setting. The adjective “positive” as in the positive Fuß–Catalan number
\Cat^{(m)}_{+}(W)
is natural in cluster theory (see Section
6
). The term
“Fuß–Dogolon”
has also been coined as a prime/positive analog of “Fuß–Catalan”.

3.1
The parabolic Burnside ring

We first need some preliminaries about characters of
W
, due to Orlik and Solomon
[
27
]
. See also Geck and Pfeifer
[
20
, Chapter 2.4]
.

Generically, we will use bold symbols to denote characters. In particular,
{\bf 1}
denotes the trivial character (of a group which is clear from the context).

Definition 3.2
.

For
I\subset S
, let
\bPhi_{I}:=\mathbf{ind}_{W_{I}}^{W}(\bf 1)
(the trivial character of
W_{I}
induced to
W
). The
parabolic Burnside ring

R(W)
of
W
is the ring linearly generated by
(\bPhi_{I})_{I\subset S}
(as a subring of the character ring of
W
).

It is not obvious that the linear span of
(\bPhi_{I})_{I\subset S}
is indeed a ring. We refer to
[
20
, Section 2.4.3]
. It also follows from
loc. cit.
that a basis of
R(W)
is
(\bPhi_{I})_{I\in\Theta}
, where
\Theta
is a set of representatives of subsets of
S
modulo the equivalence relation
\sim
as in Lemma
2.4
. Using implicitly one of the bijections from Lemma
2.4
, we identify
\Theta
with a set of representatives for the quotient
L(W)/W
. We thus write
\bPhi_{X}
in place of
\bPhi_{I}
for
X\in L(W)
such that
W_{X}
and
W_{I}
are conjugate.

Note that
\bPhi_{S}
is the trivial character of
W
, and the unit of
R(W)
. Also, it can be seen that
\bPhi_{I}
is the character of the representation
\mathbb{C}^{W/W_{I}}
(the linearization of the group action on
W/W_{I}
where
W
acts by left multiplication on the cosets).

In type
A_{n}
,
R(W)
is the space of degree
n+1
symmetric functions under the Kronecker product.

Remark 3.3
.

Let us mention that
R(W)
is a subring of the
Burnside ring
of
W
(which is linearly generated by characters of the representations
\mathbb{C}^{W/W^{\prime}}
where
W^{\prime}
is any subgroup of
W
). The terminology comes from the fact that here we only consider parabolic subgroups.

Remark 3.4
.

The algebra
\mathbb{Q}\otimes R(W)
is the space of functions
\chi\colon W\to\mathbb{Q}
such that the value
\chi(w)
only depends on the orbit of
\Fix(w)
in
L(W)/W
. This characterization is essentially due to Orlik and Solomon
[
27
]
.

The
sign character
of
W
, denoted
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
, is defined by

\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(w):=(-1)^{\ell(w)}=(-1)^{n-\dim\Fix(w)}.

This character is usually defined with the Coxeter length rather than the reflection length, but they have the same parity as each reflection has odd Coxeter length. The following lemma shows how it acts on
R(W)
by multiplication.

Lemma 3.5

(Solomon
[
33
]
)
.

For any
J\subset S
, we have

\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\otimes\bPhi_{J}=\sum_{I\subset J}(-1)^{\#I}\bPhi_{I}.

(3.1)

Note that by taking
\bPhi_{J}
to be the trivial character (i.e.,
J=S
), the previous equation gives
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
as an alternating sum of
\bPhi_{I}
, showing in particular that
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\in R(W)
.

Now, define a class function
\bPsi_{t}\colon W\to\mathbb{Z}
for each integer
t
by

\bPsi_{t}(w)=t^{\dim\Fix(w)}.

In particular,
\bPsi_{-1}=(-1)^{n}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
.
Note that
\bPsi_{t}\in\mathbb{Q}\otimes R(W)
for each
t\in\mathbb{Z}
. This is easily seen from the characterization of

Remark 3.6

In the more general context of complex reflection groups, Ito and Okada

Proposition 3.7

From the previous proposition, we see that there are at least two ways to compute the characteristic polynomials (or the Orlik–Solomon exponents, by taking their roots):

Let us make the second point more explicit. The values of the characters

Another way to compute these characteristic polynomials is given by Sommers in

The denominator

Finally, let us mention the following statement (see also

Proposition 3.8
.

If

is the character of a representation, the multiplicity of the trivial character in

is the

\frac{1}{|W|}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(t+e_{i}).

Proof.

The trivial character is orthogonal to the other irreducible characters, and it follows that this multiplicity is

\frac{1}{|W|}\sum_{w\in W}\bPsi_{t}(w).

The result then follows from the Shephard–Todd formula

\sum_{w\in W}t^{\dim\Fix(w)}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(t+e_{i}).

In the two cases which are relevant to this work, we get that the multiplicity of the trivial character in

and

3.2
Parking spaces

We review the theory of parking spaces, introduced by Armstrong, Reiner and Rhoades
[
3
]
. This is a very brief account, which mostly aims at some enumeration formulas for chains of noncrossing partitions. In particular, we simply consider characters of
W
whereas the general theory deals with characters of the product of
W
with a cyclic group. Also, we focus on the
noncrossing parking space
but other kinds of parking spaces exist.

Assume that
W
is irreducible, and let
h
be its Coxeter number. A
parking space
is a representation of
W
having
\bPsi_{h+1}
as its character. The name comes from the fact that in type
A_{n}
, the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}
acting on parking functions of length
n+1
is such a parking space. More generally, a
t
-parking space (for an integer
t\geq 1
) is a representation of
W
having
\bPsi_{t}
as its character. In the case where
W
is a Weyl group, there is a root lattice
Q
acted on by
W
. It follows from Sommers
[
34
, Proposition 3.9]
that the quotient
Q/tQ
is a
t
-parking space for the natural action of
W
, upon some conditions on
t
(being relatively prime to
h
is sufficient). See also Haiman
[
22
, Section 7.3]
. For the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, other
t
-parking spaces are given by the action on
rational parking functions
(see
[
2
]
for details). See also
[
19
]
for rational parking functions in general type.

Two
t
-parking spaces are isomorphic as representations of
W
, since by definition they have the same character. However, it might be difficult to find an explicit isomorphism and it is therefore interesting to consider various kinds of parking spaces. The
noncrossing parking space
from
[
3
]
is defined in terms of noncrossing partitions. Rhoades’ generalization
[
31
]
in the Fuß–Catalan setting is

\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}w_{1},\dots,w_{m}\in\NC(W,c)\\
w_{1}\leq\dots\leq w_{m}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{C}^{W/W_{\Fix(w_{1})}}.

(3.5)

It can be viewed as the linearization of the
W
-set of
m
-
parking functions

\displaystyle\PF(W,m):=\biguplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}w_{1},\dots,w_{m}\in\NC(W,c)\\
w_{1}\leq\dots\leq w_{m}\end{subarray}}\{(w_{1},\dots,w_{m})\}\times W/W_{\Fix(w_{1})}

(where
W
acts on the second factor). Rather than the representation itself, we mostly consider its character

\displaystyle\mathbf{park}_{W,m}:=\sum_{X\in\Theta}\kappa(W,X,m)\bPhi_{X}\in R(W),

(3.6)

where

\displaystyle\kappa(W,X,m):=\#\big\{(w_{1},\dots,w_{m})\in\NC(W,c)^{m}\colon w_{1}\leq\dots\leq w_{m}\text{ and }\Fix(w_{1})\sim X\big\}.

(3.7)

Remark 3.9
.

The integers
\kappa(W,X,m)
are known as
Kreweras numbers
. Note that they refine Narayana numbers, as we have (as a consequence of the combinatorial definition):

\displaystyle\operatorname{Nar}^{(m)}(W,k)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\in\Theta,\\
\dim(X)=n-k\end{subarray}}\kappa(W,X,m).

(3.8)

Theorem 3.10

(
[
31
]
, except for
E_{7}
,
E_{8}
)
.

Assume that
W
is irreducible, with Coxeter number
h
. Then, the representation in (
3.5
) is an
(mh+1)
-parking space, i.e.,

. Equivalently,

\forall X\in\Theta,\quad\kappa(W,X,m)=\frac{p_{X}(mh+1)}{[N(W_{X}):W_{X}]}.

(3.9)

Proof.

Note that the equivalence between the two statements follows from Proposition

. Since the infinite families of the finite type classification have been treated (see

), there remains only to deal with a finite number of exceptional groups. This can be done by computer.
∎

Let us give some comments about the computer verification mentioned in this proof. First note that the computation is possible because we have here a finite statement about polynomials in

(whereas Rhoades’ conjectures
[

involves a cyclic group of order

might be lengthy, but an efficient way to get all noncrossing partitions is to use the characterization in

Using the formula for Fuß–Catalan numbers in terms of exponents

Now, let

\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}w_{1},\dots,w_{m-1}\in\NC(W,c),\;w_{m}\in\NC^{\prime}(W,c)\\

Again, we mostly consider its character

(3.11)

where

\displaystyle\kappa^{+}(W,X,m):=\#\big\{(w_{1},\dots,w_{m})\in\NC(W,c)^{m-1}\times\NC^{\prime}(W,c)\colon

\displaystyle\hphantom{\kappa^+(W, X, m) := \#\big\lx@text@lbrace}{}\ w_{1}\leq\dots\leq w_{m}\ \text{and}\ \Fix(w_{1})\sim X\big\}.

Remark 3.11

In complete analogy with (

\displaystyle\operatorname{Nar}_{+}^{(m)}(W,k)=\sum_{X\in\Theta,\;\dim(X)=n-k}\kappa^{+}(W,X,m).

In complete analogy with Theorem

Assume that

4
Proof of Theorem
3.12
via the
\boldsymbol{W}
-Laplacian

We begin this section by stating a property of the characters
\mathbf{park}_{W,m}
and
\mathbf{park\mathrlap{{}^{\prime}}}_{W,m}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

We have the “inclusion-exclusion” formulas

\displaystyle\mathbf{park}_{W,m}=\sum_{I\subset S}\mathbf{ind}_{W_{I}}^{W}(\mathbf{park\mathrlap{{}^{\prime}}}_{W_{I},m}),

\displaystyle\mathbf{park\mathrlap{{}^{\prime}}}_{W,m}=\sum_{I\subset S}(-1)^{\#S-\#I}\mathbf{ind}_{W_{I}}^{W}(\mathbf{park}_{W_{I},m}).

Proof.

First note that if
I\subset J\subset S
, we have
\mathbf{ind}_{W_{J}}^{W}\big(\mathbf{ind}_{W_{I}}^{W_{J}}({\bf 1})\big)=\mathbf{ind}_{W_{I}}^{W}({\bf 1})
. This transitivity property follows from the general theory of group characters. With this at hand, the equivalence between these identities follow from the same lines as the classical inclusion-exclusion principle.

It remains only to prove the first identity. For
I\subset S
, let
c_{I}
denote
\prod_{i\in I}s_{i}
where the order is such that
c_{I}\sqsubset c
. We have

\displaystyle\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}w_{1},\dots,w_{m}\in\NC(W,c)\\
w_{1}\leq\dots\leq w_{m},\;\supp(w_{m})=I\end{subarray}}\mathbb{C}^{W/W_{\Fix(w_{1})}}

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}w_{1},\dots,w_{m-1}\in\NC(W_{I},c_{I}),w_{m}\in\NC^{\prime}(W_{I},c_{I})\\
w_{1}\leq\dots\leq w_{m}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{C}^{W/W_{\Fix(w_{1})}},

as we can identify the indexing sets using the natural inclusion
\NC(W_{I},c_{I})\subset\NC(W,c)
. It is straightforward to see that
\mathbb{C}^{W/W_{\Fix(w_{1})}}
is the induction of
\mathbb{C}^{W_{I}/W_{\Fix(w_{1})}}
from
W_{I}
to
W
, so that the sum in the right-hand side of the previous equation is the prime parking space of
W_{I}
induced from
W_{I}
to
W
. By summing the previous equation over
I
and taking the character of the representations, we get the first identity in the proposition.
∎

Our goal is to show that the characters
\bPsi_{mh+1}
and
\bPsi_{mh-1}
satisfy the same relations. First note that these two characters are only defined in the irreducible case, and we need to extend them in the natural multiplicative way. For these we refer to the
abstract parking spaces
, as opposed to other kinds of parking spaces which are defined as characters of some explicit representation of
W
.

Definition 4.2
.

If
W
is irreducible with Coxeter number
h
, we define
\mathbf{park}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W,m}:=\bPsi_{mh+1}
and
\mathbf{park^{\prime}}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W,m}:=\bPsi_{mh-1}
. Otherwise, consider the decomposition
W=\prod_{i=1}^{r}W_{i}
into irreducible factors. Note that there is an isomorphism

R(W)\simeq\bigotimes_{i=1}^{r}R(W_{i})

and define

\displaystyle\mathbf{park}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W,m}:=\mathbf{park}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W_{1},m}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{park}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W_{r},m},

\displaystyle\mathbf{park^{\prime}}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W,m}:=\mathbf{park^{\prime}}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W_{1},m}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{park^{\prime}}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W_{r},m}.

More explicitly, there is a natural identification
L(W)\simeq\prod_{i=1}^{r}L(W_{i})
. So
w=(w_{1},\dots,w_{r})\in W
determines flats
Z_{i}\in L(W_{i})
by

\Fix(w)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}Z_{i}.

We then have

\mathbf{park}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W,m}(w)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}(mh_{i}+1)^{\dim(Z_{i})},\qquad\mathbf{park^{\prime}}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W,m}(w)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}(mh_{i}-1)^{\dim(Z_{i})},

where
h_{i}
is the Coxeter number of
W_{i}
.

Since the values of these characters at
w\in W
only depend on
\Fix(w)
, we can describe their parabolic inductions in terms of the geometry of hyperplane arrangements.

Lemma 4.3
.

For
X\in L(W)
,
w\in W
and
Z=\Fix(w)
, we have

\big(\mathbf{ind}_{W_{X}}^{W}\mathbf{park^{\prime}}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W_{X},m}\big)(w)=[N(X):W_{X}]\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\in L(W),\\
Y\sim X\text{ and }Y\subset Z\end{subarray}}\mathbf{park^{\prime}}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W_{Y},m}(w).

Proof.

By the general formula for induction of characters, we have

\big(\mathbf{ind}_{W_{X}}^{W}\mathbf{park^{\prime}}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W_{X},m}\big)(w)=\sum_{y\in W/W_{X},\;y^{-1}wy\in W_{X}}\mathbf{park^{\prime}}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W_{X},m}\big(y^{-1}wy\big).

Note that we have

\mathbf{park^{\prime}}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W_{(y\cdot X)},m}(w)=\mathbf{park^{\prime}}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W_{X},m}\big(y^{-1}wy\big),

as can be seen by using the inner automorphism
w\mapsto y^{-1}wy
(and its extension to
L(W)
which the action of
y
).
Moreover, we have

y^{-1}wy\in W_{X}\;\Leftrightarrow\;X\subset\Fix(y^{-1}wy)\;\Leftrightarrow\;X\subset y^{-1}\cdot\Fix(w)\;\Leftrightarrow\;y\cdot X\subset Z.

We can thus rewrite the sum and get

\big(\mathbf{ind}_{W_{X}}^{W}\mathbf{park^{\prime}}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W_{X},m}\big)(w)=\sum_{y\in W/W_{X},\;y\cdot X\subset Z}\mathbf{park^{\prime}}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W_{(y\cdot X)},m}(w).

By letting
Y=y\cdot X
, this can be rewritten

\displaystyle\big(\mathbf{ind}_{W_{X}}^{W}\mathbf{park^{\prime}}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W_{X},m}\big)(w)=[N(W_{X}):W_{X}]\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\in L(W)\\
Y\sim X\text{ and }Y\subset Z\end{subarray}}\mathbf{park^{\prime}}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W_{Y},m}(w).

To get the equality, it suffices to check that
[N(W_{X}):W_{X}]
is the number of
y\in W/W_{X}
such that
y\cdot X=Y
, for each
Y\sim X
. This is the orbit-stabilizer theorem, as the subgroup

N(W_{X})
is

N(W_{X})=\{w\in W\colon w\cdot X=X\}.

Remark 4.4
.

The previous lemma holds with
\mathbf{park}^{\operatorname{abs}}
in place of
\mathbf{park^{\prime}}^{\operatorname{abs}}
, with a completely similar proof.

Every parabolic subgroup
W_{X}
of
W
is a possibly reducible reflection group and we would like a simpler notation of the values of the parking characters
\mathbf{park}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W_{X},m}
and
\mathbf{park^{\prime}}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W_{X},m}
. For this reason, we introduce the following notation.

Notation 4.5

(multisets of Coxeter numbers)
.

Let
W
be an irreducible reflection group, and
X,Z\in L(W)
such that
X\subset Z
. Assume that
W_{X}=W_{1}\times\cdots\times W_{r}
is the decomposition into irreducible factors. Write
Z=\prod_{i=1}^{r}Z_{i}
, using as above
L(W_{X})\simeq\prod_{i=1}^{r}L(W_{i})
. Note that this isomorphism is such that

\dim(Z)-\dim(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\dim(Z_{i}),

which can be seen by comparing the rank functions on each side. Then we write the
multiset of Coxeter numbers
associated to
X
and
Z
as

(h_{i}(X,Z))_{1\leq i\leq\dim(Z)-\dim(X)}:=(\underbrace{h_{1},\dots,h_{1}}_{\dim(Z_{1})\text{ times}},\dots,\underbrace{h_{r},\dots,h_{r}}_{\dim(Z_{r})\text{ times}}),

where
h_{i}
is the Coxeter number of
W_{i}
. With this notation, we can write the parking space characters for parabolic subgroups: for each
w\in W_{X}
with
\Fix(w)=Z
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{park}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W_{X},m}(w)=\prod_{i=1}^{\dim(Z)-\dim(X)}(mh_{i}(X,Z)+1),

\displaystyle\mathbf{park^{\prime}}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W_{X},m}(w)=\prod_{i=1}^{\dim(Z)-\dim(X)}(mh_{i}(X,Z)-1).

What we need is a relation involving the Coxeter numbers of
W
and those of its parabolic subgroups that generalizes the one which appeared in
[
12
, Theorem 8.8]
. Recall that this was given as follows: if
W
is irreducible with Coxeter number
h
,

(h+t)^{n}=\sum_{X\in L(W)}\Bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{n-\dim(X)}h_{i}(X)\Bigg)\cdot t^{\dim(X)},

where
h_{i}(X)
for
1\leq i\leq n
is the special case of
(h_{i}(X,Z))_{1\leq i\leq\dim(Z)-\dim(X)}
when
Z
is the minimal element of
L(W)
. Such a generalization (see
[
14
, Section 4]
for details) is the following:

Proposition 4.6

(Corollary of the restricted Laplacian recursion)
.

For any
X,Z\in L(W)
such that
X\subset Z
, we have the following relation between Coxeter numbers:

\prod_{i=1}^{\dim(Z)-\dim(X)}\big(h_{i}(X,Z)+t\big)=\sum_{X\subset Y\subset Z}\Bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{\dim(Z)-\dim(Y)}h_{i}(Y,Z)\Bigg)t^{\dim(Y)-\dim(X)}.

(4.1)

Proof.

For
X,Z\in L(W)
such that
X\subset Z
, consider the set

\mathcal{A}^{X,Z}:=\{Y/X\colon X\subset Y\subset Z,\;\dim(Y)=\dim(Z)-1\},

which is a hyperplane arrangement in the quotient
Z/X

(
the essentialization of the restricted localization
\mathcal{A}_{X}^{Z}
)
. When
X
is the
0
-dimensional subspace, we just denote
\mathcal{A}^{Z}=\mathcal{A}^{X,Z}
.

Generalizing
[
12
]
, to this hyperplane arrangement there is an associated Laplacian
\mathcal{L}_{X,Z}
for which each hyperplane
K\in\mathcal{A}^{X,Z}
is taken with multiplicity equal to
h_{1}(K,Z)
. Its characteristic polynomial is the left-hand side of (
4.1
). This follows analogously to
[
12
, Proposition 3.13]
.

Now, the Laplacian recursion
[
12
, Proposition 8.3]
relates the characteristic polynomial of
\mathcal{L}_{X,Z}
to the determinants of
\mathcal{L}_{Y,Z}
for
X\subset Y\subset Z
, and gives precisely the above identity.
∎

The hyperplane arrangement
\mathcal{A}^{Z}
introduced above is called a
restricted arrangement
, see
[
27
,
28
]
. The number
r\big(\mathcal{A}^{Z}\big)
of regions in the complement of this arrangement is given by

r\big(\mathcal{A}^{Z}\big)=(-1)^{\dim(Z)}p_{Z}(-1)=\nu(Z)\cdot[N(W_{Z}):W_{Z}].

What we need is the latter equality, see
[
27
, equations (4.1) and (4.2)]
. It can also be obtained by plugging
t=-1
in (
3.2
), using (
3.4
) and (
3.1
) in the case
J=S
.

Proposition 4.7
.

Assume that
W
is irreducible, with Coxeter number
h
. For any
Z\in L(W)
, we have the following relation between Coxeter numbers:

(mh+1)^{\dim(Z)}=\sum_{X\subset Z}r\big(\mathcal{A}^{X}\big)\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{\dim(Z)-\dim(X)}(mh_{i}(X,Z)-1).

Proof.

Consider equation (
4.1
) in the case where
X
is the
0
-dimensional subspace, so that
h_{i}(X,Z)=h
. By replacing
t
with
t/m
and multiplying both sides by
m^{\dim(Z)}
, we get

(mh+t)^{\dim(Z)}=\sum_{Y\subset Z}\Bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{\dim(Z)-\dim(Y)}mh_{i}(Y,Z)\Bigg)\cdot t^{\dim(Y)}.

By plugging
t^{\dim(Y)}=\sum_{X\subset Y}\chi\big(\mathcal{A}^{X},t\big)
(which is the inverse of (
3.3
)), we obtain

\displaystyle(mh+t)^{\dim(Z)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{X\subset Y\subset Z}\Bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{\dim(Z)-\dim(Y)}mh_{i}(Y,Z)\Bigg)\cdot p_{X}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{X\subset Z}\Bigg(\sum_{X\subset Y\subset Z}\prod_{i=1}^{\dim(Z)-\dim(Y)}mh_{i}(Y,Z)\Bigg)\cdot p_{X}(t).

Again using (
4.1
) (with
t=1
), this gives

(mh+t)^{\dim(Z)}=\sum_{X\subset Z}\Bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{\dim(Z)-\dim(X)}(mh_{i}(X,Z)+1)\Bigg)\cdot p_{X}(t).

Now, replacing
(m,t)
with
(-m,-1)
and getting rid of the signs, this becomes

(mh+1)^{\dim(Z)}=\sum_{X\subset Z}\Bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{\dim(Z)-\dim(X)}(mh_{i}(X,Z)-1)\Bigg)\cdot(-1)^{\dim(X)}p_{X}(-1).

This is precisely what we needed to prove.
∎

Theorem 4.8
.

We have

\mathbf{park}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W,m}=\sum_{I\subset S}\mathbf{ind}_{W_{I}}^{W}\big(\mathbf{park^{\prime}}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W_{I},m}\big).

(4.2)

Proof.

We assume that
W
is irreducible, and let
h
be its Coxeter number. We let the reader check that the reducible case follows.

Let
\boldsymbol{\chi}
denote the character in the right-hand side of (
4.2
). Using the notation
\nu(X)
(see equation (
3.4
)), we have

\boldsymbol{\chi}=\sum_{X\in\Theta}\nu(X)\cdot\mathbf{ind}_{W_{X}}^{W}\big(\mathbf{park^{\prime}}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W_{X},m}\big).

Let
w\in W
and
Z=\Fix(w)
. Lemma
4.3
tells us that the evaluation of the character is

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\chi}(w)=\sum_{X\in\Theta}\nu(X)\cdot[N(X):W_{X}]\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}X^{\prime}\in L(W),\\
X^{\prime}\sim X\text{ and }X^{\prime}\subset Z\end{subarray}}\mathbf{park^{\prime}}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W_{X^{\prime}},m}(w).

This can be rewritten as a single sum over
X^{\prime}
, and we get

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\chi}(w)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\in L(W),\\
X\subset Z\end{subarray}}\nu(X)\cdot[N(X):W_{X}]\cdot\mathbf{park^{\prime}}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W_{X},m}(w)

\displaystyle=\sum_{X\subset Z}r\big(\mathcal{A}^{X}\big)\cdot\mathbf{park^{\prime}}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W_{X},m}(w).

Indeed, recall
\nu(X)\cdot[N(X):W_{X}]=(-1)^{\dim(X)}p_{X}(-1)=r\big(\mathcal{A}^{X}\big)
. From Proposition

∎

Theorem 4.9
.

We have

\mathbf{park^{\prime}}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W,m}=\sum_{I\subset S}(-1)^{\#S-\#I}\mathbf{ind}_{W_{I}}^{W}\big(\mathbf{park}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W_{I},m}\big).

Proof.

It is straightforward to adapt the proof of the previous theorem. We can also see it as a consequence: substitute
m
to
-m
in (
4.2
) and tensor with
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
, this gives (where
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{W_{I}}
denotes the sign character of
W_{I}
to distinguish it from
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
)

\displaystyle\mathbf{park^{\prime}}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W,m}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}\sum_{I\subset S}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\otimes\mathbf{ind}_{W_{I}}^{W}\big(\mathbf{park^{\prime}}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W_{I},-m}\big)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}\sum_{I\subset S}\mathbf{ind}_{W_{I}}^{W}\big(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{W_{I}}\otimes\mathbf{park^{\prime}}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W_{I},-m}\big)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}\sum_{I\subset S}\mathbf{ind}_{W_{I}}^{W}\big((-1)^{\#I}\mathbf{park}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W_{I},m}\big).

We used the identity

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\otimes\mathbf{ind}_{W_{I}}^{W}(\boldsymbol{\chi})=\mathbf{ind}_{W_{I}}^{W}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{W_{I}}\otimes\boldsymbol{\chi}),

(4.3)

where
\boldsymbol{\chi}
is any character of
W_{I}
. It easily follows from the general formula for induction, and the fact that
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{W_{I}}
is the restriction of
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
to
W_{I}
. Indeed, we have for any

\displaystyle\mathbf{ind}_{W_{I}}^{W}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{W_{I}}\otimes\boldsymbol{\chi})(w)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|W_{I}|}\sum_{x\in W,\;x^{-1}wx\in W_{I}}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{W_{I}}\otimes\boldsymbol{\chi})\big(x^{-1}wx\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|W_{I}|}\sum_{x\in W,\;x^{-1}wx\in W_{I}}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{W_{I}}\big(x^{-1}wx\big)\cdot\boldsymbol{\chi}\big(x^{-1}wx\big)

We have
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{W_{I}}\big(x^{-1}wx\big)=\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\big(x^{-1}wx\big)=\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(w)
, since
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{W_{I}}
is the restriction of
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
is a class function. It follows

\displaystyle\mathbf{ind}_{W_{I}}^{W}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{W_{I}}\otimes\boldsymbol{\chi})(w)

\displaystyle=\frac{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(w)}{|W_{I}|}\sum_{x\in W,\;x^{-1}wx\in W_{I}}\boldsymbol{\chi}\big(x^{-1}wx\big)=\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(w)\cdot\mathbf{ind}_{W_{I}}^{W}(\boldsymbol{\chi})(w).\qed

∎

We have thus proved that

and
\mathbf{park\mathrlap{{}^{\prime}}}_{W,m}
in Proposition

.

Proof of Theorem
3.12
.

We have
\mathbf{park}_{W_{I},m}=\mathbf{park}^{\operatorname{abs}}_{W_{I},m}
for all

by Theorem

. From the second identity in Proposition

Remark 5.2
.

We have obtained Theorem
3.12
as a consequence of Theorem
3.10
. It is worth pointing out that Proposition
5.1
actually proves the equivalence of these two theorems.

Below, we identify elements in
R(W)
with column vectors indexed by
\Theta
. We also use matrices with rows and columns indexed by
\Theta
. In both cases, we use bold fonts as we do for elements of
R(W)
. Note that
\otimes
still denotes the product of
R(W)
, but we use no sign for the matrix products.

Definition 5.3
.

Let
w
be a fixed element of
\NC(W,c)
such that
\Fix(w)\sim Y
. We define three matrices
\bQ
,
\bR
, and
\bN
by

\displaystyle\bQ_{X,Y}=\#\big\{v\in\NC(W,c)\colon\Fix(v)\sim X\text{ and }v\leq w\big\},

\displaystyle\bR_{X,Y}=\#\big\{v\in\NC(W,c)\colon\Fix(v)\sim X\text{ and }v\ll w\big\},

\displaystyle\bN_{X,Y}=\#\big\{v\in\NC(W,c)\colon\Fix(v)\sim X\text{ and }v\sqsubset w\big\}.

Also we define
\bD
as the diagonal matrix such that, for all
X\in\Theta
,

\bD_{X,X}=(-1)^{n-\dim X}.

Moreover,
\bI
denotes the identity matrix.

These matrices don’t depend on the chosen element
w
. In the case of
\bQ_{X,Y}
, this is a consequence of the fact that the absolute order is invariant under conjugation. As for the other matrices, it’s worth writing some details about the proof.

Lemma 5.4
.

The numbers
\bR_{X,Y}
and
\bN_{X,Y}
don’t depend on the chosen element
w
.

Proof.

Consider the sign character
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{W_{\Fix(w)}}\in R\big(W_{\Fix(w)}\big)
. Via Lemma
3.5
applied to
W_{\Fix(w)}
, we have

\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{W_{\Fix(w)}}=\sum_{v\sqsubset w}(-1)^{\ell(v)}\bPhi_{\Fix(v)},

where it is understood that
\bPhi_{\Fix(v)}\in R(W_{\Fix(w)})
. This is because the elements
v\sqsubset w
naturally correspond to subsets of the factors in the canonical factorization of
w
, i.e., subsets of the simple system of
W_{\Fix(w)}
. By inducing on
W
and using (
4.3
), we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\otimes\Phi_{Y}=\sum_{X}(-1)^{n-\dim(X)}\bN_{X,Y}\Phi_{X}.

(5.2)

Since the left-hand side doesn’t depend on the chosen
w
, the same is true for the numbers
\bN_{X,Y}
.

The result for
\bR_{X,Y}
follows because this number can be computed in terms of the coefficients of
\bQ
and
\bN
:

\displaystyle\bR_{X,Z}=\sum_{Y}\bQ_{X,Y}\bN_{Y,Z}(-1)^{\dim(Z)-\dim(Y)}.

(5.3)

This equality follows from an inclusion-exclusion procedure to compute
\bR_{X,Z}
, using Proposition
2.8
. We omit the details.
∎

Note that the matrices
\bQ
,
\bR
, and
\bN
are lower unitriangular (upon ordering
\Theta
so that dimension is increasing: for example
\dim(Y)=0
and
w=c
gives nonzero values of
\bQ_{X,Y}
for any

).

Lemma 5.5
.

For any
\bX\in R(W)
, we have
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\otimes\bX=\bD\bN\bX
.

Proof.

This is a rewriting of Lemma
3.5
. More explicitly, this follows from (
5.2
).
∎

Lemma 5.6
.

We have
(\bD\bN)^{2}=\bI
.

Proof.

This follows from the previous proposition, since
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\otimes\boldsymbol{\epsilon}={\bf 1}
. Alternatively, one can see the relation

as a reformulation of the inclusion-exclusion principle on subsets of

.
∎

Lemma 5.7
.

.

A combinatorial expansion of the product

shows that the

such that

and

, where

. (Also, equation (

∎

Lemma 5.8
.

We have
\bQ^{-1}=(\bD\bN)\bQ(\bD\bN)
.

Proof.

A combinatorial expansion of the product

shows that the coefficient of

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\in\NC(W,c)\\

where

Proposition 5.10

We have

Proof of Proposition

6
The generalized cluster complex

We review Fomin and Reading’s generalized cluster complex, introduced in
[
17
]
, and Tzanaki’s characterization from
[
40
]
. Afterwards, we introduce in Definition
6.13
the natural way to associate a parabolic conjugacy class to each face of this complex. This will be the basis of the refined enumeration of faces.

Through this section, we assume that
c
is a
bipartite Coxeter element
: we can write
S=S_{+}\uplus S_{-}
where
S_{\pm}
contains pairwise commuting reflections, and
c=c_{+}c_{-}
where
c_{\pm}
is the product of elements in
S_{\pm}
.

Let
\Phi\subset V
be a
root system
for
W
(in the sense of Coxeter groups),
\Phi_{+}\subset\Phi
a set of positive roots, and
\Delta\subset\Phi_{+}
the corresponding set of simple roots. For
\alpha\in\Phi
, we denote by
t_{\alpha}\in T
the unique reflection such that
t(\alpha)=-\alpha
. Finally, let
\Delta_{+}
and
\Delta_{-}
be the sets of simple roots such that
t_{\alpha}\in S_{\pm}
iff
\alpha\in\Delta_{\pm}
.

6.1
Colored almost-positive roots

Let
[m]=\{1,\dots,m\}
. We think of it as a set of “colors” that we use to define
colored roots
.

Definition 6.1

(Fomin and Reading
[
17
]
)
.

An
m
-colored root
is a pair
(\alpha,i)
, denoted
\alpha^{i}
for short, where
\alpha\in\Phi
and
i\in[m]
. It is
positive
if
\alpha\in\Phi_{+}
. It is
almost-positive
if either
\alpha\in\Phi_{+}
, or
\alpha\in-\Delta
and
i=1
. We denote by
\Phi^{(m)}_{\geq-1}
the set of almost-positive
m
-colored roots, and
\Phi^{(m)}_{+}
the set of positive
m
-colored roots.

When
m=1
, we can ignore colors and
\Phi^{1}_{\geq-1}
is thus identified with
\Phi_{\geq-1}:=\Phi_{+}\cup(-\Delta)
. This is the set of
almost-positive roots
introduced by Fomin and Zelevinsky to define the cluster complex
[
18
]
. In general, we also identify
\rho\in-\Delta
with the colored root
\rho^{1}
(as no confusion can arise).

Fomin and Zelevinsky
[
18
]
defined a rotation
\mathcal{R}
on
\Phi_{\geq-1}
by
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}_{+}\circ\mathcal{R}_{-}
, where

\mathcal{R}_{+}(\rho)=\begin{cases}\rho&\text{if }\rho\in-\Delta_{-},\\
c_{+}(\rho)&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\qquad\mathcal{R}_{-}(\rho)=\begin{cases}\rho&\text{if }\rho\in-\Delta_{+},\\
c_{-}(\rho)&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Explicitly, we can check that

\mathcal{R}(\rho)=\begin{cases}-\rho&\text{if }\rho\in(-\Delta_{+})\cup\Delta_{-},\\
c(\rho)&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Following Fomin and Reading
[
17
]
,
the higher order rotation
\mathcal{R}_{m}
acting on
\Phi_{\geq-1}^{(m)}
is defined as follows:

\mathcal{R}_{m}\big(\alpha^{i}\big)=\begin{cases}\alpha^{i+1}&\text{if }\alpha\in\Phi_{+}\text{ and }i<m,\\
\mathcal{R}(\alpha)^{1}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

It will be useful to make the second case more explicit:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{m}(\alpha^{m})=\begin{cases}(-\alpha)^{1}&\text{if }\alpha\in\Delta_{-},\\
c(\alpha)^{1}&\text{if }\alpha\in\Phi_{+}\backslash\Delta_{-},\end{cases}\qquad\mathcal{R}_{m}\big(\alpha^{1}\big)=\begin{cases}(-\alpha)^{1}&\text{if }\alpha\in-\Delta_{+},\\
c(\alpha)^{1}&\text{if }\alpha\in-\Delta_{-}.\end{cases}

Proposition 6.2

(
[
17
]
)
.

Assume
W
is irreducible, with Coxeter number
h
. Then each orbit
\omega
for the action of
\mathcal{R}_{m}
on
\Phi^{(m)}_{\geq-1}
satisfies

•

either
\#\omega=\frac{mh+2}{2}
and
\#(-\Delta)\cap\omega=1
,

•

or
\#\omega=mh+2
and
\#(-\Delta)\cap\omega=2
.

Moreover, in the latter case the two elements
-\delta_{i}
and
-\delta_{j}
of
(-\Delta)\cap\omega
are related by
w_{\circ}(\delta_{i})=\delta_{j}
, where
w_{\circ}
is the longest element of
W
.

Note that the previous proposition says that negative simple roots have the same proportion in each orbit.
This is similar to a classical result by Steinberg
[
37
]
for the action of the bipartite Coxeter element on
T
by conjugation. It states that each orbit is

•

either an (
h/2
)-element set containing one simple reflection,

•

or an
h
-element set containing two simple reflections.

6.2
The compatibility relation

The generalized cluster complex can be defined as the flag simplicial complex associated to a binary relation on
\Phi^{(m)}_{\geq-1}
, called
compatibility
.

Definition 6.3

(
[
17
]
)
.

The
compatibility relation

\mathrel{\|}
is the unique symmetric and irreflexive binary relation on
\Phi^{(m)}_{\geq-1}
, characterized by the conditions:

•

for all
\alpha,\beta\in\Phi^{(m)}_{\geq-1}
, we have
\alpha\mathrel{\|}\beta
iff
\mathcal{R}_{m}(\alpha)\mathrel{\|}\mathcal{R}_{m}(\beta)
,

•

if
\alpha\in-\Delta
and
\beta\in\Phi^{(m)}_{\geq-1}
, we have
\alpha\mathrel{\|}\beta
iff
\alpha
does not appear in the expansion of
\beta
(forgetting its color) as a linear combination of simple roots.

In particular, the elements of
-\Delta
are mutually compatible by the second condition. Note that these two conditions suffice to decide if
\alpha\mathrel{\|}\beta
holds, for any
\alpha,\beta\in\Phi^{(m)}_{\geq-1}
. Indeed, we can proceed as follows:

•

by Proposition
6.2
, there exists
i
such that
\mathcal{R}_{m}^{i}(\alpha)
is in
-\Delta
,

•

by the first condition above,
\alpha\mathrel{\|}\beta
holds iff
\mathcal{R}_{m}^{i}(\alpha)\mathrel{\|}\mathcal{R}_{m}^{i}(\beta)
holds,

•

by the second condition above, we can decide if
\mathcal{R}_{m}^{i}(\alpha)\mathrel{\|}\mathcal{R}_{m}^{i}(\beta)
by expanding
\mathcal{R}_{m}^{i}(\beta)
in terms of simple roots.

In particular, it follows that there exists at most one such relation
\mathrel{\|}
. Fomin and Reading
[
17
]
proved that such a relation does exist.

Definition 6.4

(
[
17
]
)
.

The
generalized cluster complex

\Upsilon(W,m)
is the flag simplicial complex generated by the compatibility relation
\|
of Definition
6.3
: its vertex set is
\Phi^{(m)}_{\geq-1}
, and its faces are sets of pairwise compatible vertices. The
positive part
of
\Upsilon(W,m)
, denoted
\Upsilon^{+}(W,m)
, is its full subcomplex having
\Phi^{(m)}_{+}
as vertex set. In the case
m=1
, we write
\Upsilon(W):=\Upsilon(W,1)
and
\Upsilon^{+}(W):=\Upsilon^{+}(W,1)
.

Explicit combinatorial descriptions have been given in classical types
[
17
, Section 5]
. In type
A_{n-1}
, we can identify
\Phi^{(m)}_{\geq-1}
with the proper diagonals of a convex
(mn+2)
-gon, and facets of
\Upsilon(W,m)
with
(m+2)
-angulations of the same polygon. The other faces are
dissections
of the polygon where every inner polygon is a
(mk+2)
-gon for some
k
. The negative face (containing all negative simple roots) is given by a “snake”
(m+2)
-angulation, and the explicit description of
\Upsilon^{+}(W,m)
can be deduced. See Fomin and Reading
[
17
]
for details.

Before stating more properties of
\Upsilon(W,m)
and
\Upsilon^{+}(W,m)
, let us give some reformulations of the definition.

6.3
Reflection ordering

An alternative characterization of the complex
\Upsilon(W,m)
can be given via a reflection ordering. It is due to Brady and Watt
[
10
]
in the case

, and Tzanaki
[
40
]
in the general case.

Proposition 6.5

(
[
37
]
)
.

There exists an indexing
\Phi_{\geq-1}=(\alpha_{i})_{1\leq i\leq nh/2+n}
with the following properties:

•

6.4
Other properties

Fomin and Reading proved various properties about their generalized cluster complex, some of them will be useful in the present work. First,
\Upsilon(W,m)
and
\Upsilon^{+}(W,m)
are purely
(n-1)
-dimensional. Their number of facets are respectively the Fuß–Catalan number
\Cat^{(m)}(W)
and the positive Fuß–Catalan number
\Cat_{+}^{(m)}(W)
. Another property that naturally follows from the definition is:

Proposition 6.9
.

Let
\rho\in\Delta
,
s=t_{\rho}
, and denote the irreducible factors of
W_{(s)}
as
W_{1}
,
W_{2}
, etc. Then the link of the vertex
-\rho
in
\Upsilon(W,m)
is the join
\Upsilon(W_{1},m)\star\Upsilon(W_{2},m)\star\cdots
.

By the above result, it is natural to extend the definition of
\Upsilon(W,m)
in the reducible case, by declaring that it is the join of the simplicial complexes
\Upsilon(W^{\prime},m)
where
W^{\prime}
runs through the irreducible factors of
W
.
(
In particular, this agrees with the fact that
\Cat^{(m)}(W)
and
\Cat_{+}^{(m)}(W)
are multiplicative over the irreducible factors of
W
)
.

The results of Brady and Watt and their extension by Tzanaki make clear that the cluster complex is related with minimal factorizations of the Coxeter element. In particular, to each face we can associate a noncrossing partition by taking the product of the corresponding reflections in an appropriate order (which is unique). This map has been used a lot in the literature (see for example
[
7
]
), but let us describe it explicitly.

Definition 6.10
.

Let
f=\{\rho_{1}\succ\dots\succ\rho_{k}\}\in\Upsilon(W,m)
. We define

\prod f:=t_{\rho_{1}}\cdots t_{\rho_{k}}\in\NC(W,c).

To see that this is well-defined, first note that from Proposition
6.8
we have
t_{\rho_{1}}\cdots t_{\rho_{k}}\allowbreak=c
if
k=n
(i.e.,
f
is a facet). In general, since a face is a subset of a facet we get that
t_{\rho_{1}}\cdots t_{\rho_{k}}
is a subword of a minimal reflection factorization of
c
. It follows that
\prod f\in\NC(W,c)
.

Proposition 6.11
.

For
w\in\NC(W,c)
, we have

\#\Big\{f\in\Upsilon^{+}(W,m)\colon\prod f=w\Big\}=\Cat^{(m)}_{+}\big(W_{\Fix(w)}\big).

Proof.

Essentially, it is possible to identify those
f\in\Upsilon^{+}(W,m)
such that
\prod f=w
with facets of
\Upsilon^{+}\big(W_{\Fix(w)},m\big)
. However, some care is needed to do that. Although
w
is a Coxeter element of
W_{\Fix(w)}
by Proposition
2.6
, it might not be bipartite Coxeter element. We thus need to use the generalized cluster complex associated to any standard Coxeter element, following the definition of Stump, Thomas, Williams
[
38
]
.

An alternative path is to first get the result for
m=1
. Indeed,
[
9
, Proposition 6.3]
gives a characterization of the compatibility relation on (uncolored) positive roots which is clearly stable by restriction to parabolic subgroups, allowing the identification mentioned above. Now, we have

\#\Big\{f\in\Upsilon^{+}(W,m)\colon\prod f=w\Big\}=\sum_{w=w_{1}\cdots w_{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\#\Big\{f\in\Upsilon^{+}(W)\colon\prod f=w_{i}\Big\},

where we sum over length-additive factorizations with
m
factors. Indeed, this can be proved bijectively: to
f\in\Upsilon^{+}(W,m)
we associate
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\in\Upsilon^{+}(W)
where
f_{i}
contains roots in
f
of color
i
. Using the case
m=1
of the proposition, we get

\#\Big\{f\in\Upsilon^{+}(W,m)\colon\prod f=w\Big\}=\sum_{w=w_{1}\cdots w_{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\Cat_{+}\big(W_{\Fix(w_{i})}\big).

The result follows from the identity

\Cat^{(m)}_{+}\big(W_{\Fix(w)}\big)=\sum_{w=w_{1}\cdots w_{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\Cat_{+}\big(W_{\Fix(w_{i})}\big),

where we sum over length-additive factorizations with
m
factors. This identity can be proved as follows: since it is invariant under conjugating
w
, we can assume
w
is a bipartite Coxeter element of
W_{\Fix(w)}
, and the result comes from double counting of facets in
\Upsilon(W_{\Fix(w)},m)
as above.
∎

6.5
The map to orbits of flats

The
Kreweras complement
is an anti-automorphism of
\NC(W,c)
defined by

. We use here a variant adapted to the bipartite Coxeter element.

Lemma 6.12
.

The map

is an involutive anti-automorphism of
\NC(W,c)
.

See, for example,
[
9
, Proposition 3.5]
.

Definition 6.13
.

For

, we define

\underline{f}:=c_{+}\Big(\prod f\Big)c_{-},

which is in
\NC(W,c)
by the previous lemma. The natural map to orbits of flats is

\displaystyle\Upsilon(W,m)

\displaystyle\to L(W)/W,

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto\text{ the orbit of }\Fix(\underline{f}).

Accordingly, we define for any

\displaystyle\Upsilon(W,X,m):=\big\{f\in\Upsilon(W,m)\colon\Fix(\underline{f})\sim X\big\},

\displaystyle\Upsilon^{+}(W,X,m):=\big\{f\in\Upsilon^{+}(W,m)\colon\Fix(\underline{f})\sim X\big\},

\displaystyle\gamma(W,X,m):=\#\Upsilon(W,X,m),

\displaystyle\gamma^{+}(W,X,m):=\#\Upsilon^{+}(W,X,m).

Remark 6.14
.

We could actually take the usual Kreweras complement
K(w)
instead of its bipartite variant. Indeed,
cw^{-1}
and
c_{+}wc_{-}
are conjugate (it easily follows from the fact that each element is conjugate to its inverse, see
[

, Corollary 3.2.14]
). We found the bipartite Kreweras complement convenient to do some calculations, but of course everything could be done with the usual one.

Let
(f_{i})_{-1\leq i\leq n-1}
denote the

-vector of
\Upsilon(W,m)

is the number of

-dimensional faces in the complex. Similarly,
(f^{+}_{i})_{-1\leq i\leq n-1}
denote the
f
-vector of

. In terms of

, we thus have:

\displaystyle f_{k-1}=\sum_{X\in\Theta,\;\dim(X)=k}\gamma(W,X,m)\qquad\text{and}\qquad f^{+}_{k-1}=\sum_{X\in\Theta,\;\dim(X)=k}\gamma^{+}(W,X,m).

(6.1)

In the next sections, we give the explicit formulas for the quantities

and
\gamma^{+}
. Another important property is that the natural map

in the previous definition is invariant under the rotation

(see Proposition

).

Observe that

and

Remark 6.15
.

As a motivation for the previous definition, let us give the following statement. If

, its

For example, consider type

7
Combinatorial reciprocities

In this section, we prove combinatorial reciprocities between the quantities
\kappa
,
\kappa^{+}
on one side and
\gamma^{+}
,
\gamma
on the other side (Theorem
7.1
below). We deduce the formulas for
\gamma^{+}
,
\gamma
in terms of characteristic polynomials (or Orlik–Solomon exponents) in Corollary
7.3
, as a consequence of the combinatorial reciprocities together with the formulas for
\kappa
and
\kappa^{+}
in Theorems
3.10
and
3.12
.

Theorem 7.1
.

We have

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}\kappa(W,X,-m)=\gamma^{+}(W,X,m),

(7.1)

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}\kappa^{+}(W,X,-m)=\gamma(W,X,m).

(7.2)

Before proving this, we need to state inclusion-exclusion formulas that relate
\kappa(W,X,m)
to
\kappa^{+}(W,X,m)
on one side, and
\gamma(W,X,m)
to
\gamma^{+}(W,X,m)
on the other side. To do this, it is convenient to extend the definitions of these quantities. Using the bijection from Lemma
2.4
, we write
\kappa(W,\mathcal{X},m)
in place of
\kappa(W,X,m)
and similarly for
\kappa^{+}
,
\gamma
and
\gamma^{+}
. Finally, we extend this definition in an additive way: if
\mathcal{Y}\subset W
is a disjoint union of parabolic conjugacy classes, say
\mathcal{Y}=\biguplus_{i=1}^{j}\mathcal{X}_{i}
, we write

\kappa(W,\mathcal{Y},m)=\sum_{i=1}^{j}\kappa(W,\mathcal{X}_{i},m)

and similarly for
\kappa^{+}
,
\gamma
, and
\gamma^{+}
. In particular, for each standard parabolic subgroup
W_{I}\subset W
and
\mathcal{X}
a parabolic conjugacy class of
W
, the intersection
\mathcal{X}\cap W_{I}
is such a union.

Now, we can state:

Proposition 7.2
.

We have

\displaystyle\kappa(W,\mathcal{X},m)=\sum_{I\subset S}\kappa^{+}(W_{I},\mathcal{X}\cap W_{I},m),

\displaystyle\kappa^{+}(W,\mathcal{X},m)=\sum_{I\subset S}(-1)^{n-\#I}\kappa(W_{I},\mathcal{X}\cap W_{I},m),

(7.3)

and

\displaystyle\gamma(W,\mathcal{X},m)=\sum_{I\subset S}\gamma^{+}(W_{I},\mathcal{X}\cap W_{I},m),

(7.4)

\displaystyle\gamma^{+}(W,\mathcal{X},m)=\sum_{I\subset S}(-1)^{n-\#I}\gamma(W_{I},\mathcal{X}\cap W_{I},m).

Proof.

The number
\kappa^{+}(W_{I},\mathcal{X}\cap W_{I},m)
counts chains
w_{1}\leq\dots\leq w_{m}
in
\NC(W_{I},c_{I})
where
w_{1}\in\mathcal{X}\cap W_{I}
and
w_{m}
has full support in
W_{I}
. By summing over
I
, we get the first equation. The second can be deduced, essentially via the same proof as in the classical inclusion-exclusion principle.

Let’s fix
I\subset S
. Then the map

f\mapsto f^{+}:=f\cap\Phi^{(m)}_{+}

is a bijection from faces
f\in\Upsilon(W,m)
such that
f\cap(-\Delta)=\{-\delta_{i}\colon i\in S\backslash I\}
to
\Upsilon^{+}(W_{I},m)
. To check the relation between
\underline{f}
and
\underline{f^{+}}
, note that with
f
as above we have

\prod f=\bigg(\prod_{i\in S^{+}\backslash I}s_{i}\bigg)\Big(\prod f^{+}\Big)\bigg(\prod_{i\in S^{-}\backslash I}s_{i}\bigg),

so that

\underline{f}=\bigg(\prod_{i\in S^{+}\cap I}s_{i}\bigg)\Big(\prod f^{+}\Big)\bigg(\prod_{i\in S^{-}\cap I}s_{i}\bigg)=\underline{f^{+}}.

The last equality comes from the fact that
\underline{f^{+}}
is defined with respect to the bipartite Coxeter element of
W_{I}
. It follows that the condition
\underline{f}\in\mathcal{X}
is equivalent to
\underline{f^{+}}\in\mathcal{X}\cap W_{I}
. By summing over
I\subset S
, we get (
7.4
). The inverse relation follows, via inclusion-exclusion as before.
∎

Proof of Theorem
7.1
.

Recall that
\kappa(W,\mathcal{X},m)
counts chains
w_{1}\leq\dots\leq w_{m}
in
\NC(W,c)
where
w_{1}\in\mathcal{X}
. If
w_{1}
is fixed, the other elements
w_{2},\dots,w_{m}
can be mapped to

and are thus in bijection with
(m-1)

\NC(W,c)
where the top element is below

. The number of such tuples
(w_{2},\dots,w_{m})
is thus given by the Fuß–Catalan number

. We get

\kappa(W,\mathcal{X},m)=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{X}}\Cat^{(m-1)}\big(W_{\Fix(w^{-1}c)}\big).

(7.5)

Note that we have a combinatorial reciprocity for Fuß–Catalan numbers

\displaystyle\Cat^{(-m)}(W)=(-1)^{n}\Cat^{(m-1)}_{+}(W).

This relation follows from the two formulas in terms of the exponents given in the introduction, and the fact that the (increasingly sorted) exponents satisfy
e_{i}=h-e_{n+1-i}
. Using this reciprocity, equation (
7.5
) gives

(-1)^{k}\kappa(W,\mathcal{X},-m)=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{X}}(-1)^{k}\Cat^{(-m-1)}\big(W_{\Fix(w^{-1}c)}\big)=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{X}}\Cat_{+}^{(m)}\big(W_{\Fix(w^{-1}c)}\big).

Since
w^{-1}c
is conjugate to
c_{+}wc_{-}
, we also have

(-1)^{k}\kappa(W,\mathcal{X},-m)=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{X}}\Cat_{+}^{(m)}\big(W_{\Fix(c_{+}wc_{-})}\big).

By Proposition
6.11
, each term

is the number of positive faces in

such that

. This is also the number of positive faces
f\in\Upsilon^{+}(W,m)
such that
\underline{f}=w
. So the sum is
\gamma^{+}(W,\mathcal{X},m)
by definition, and we have proved (

).

Then, by substitution
m\leftarrow-m
in (

) we get

\displaystyle\kappa^{+}(W,\mathcal{X},-m)=\sum_{I\subset S}(-1)^{n-\#I}\kappa(W_{I},\mathcal{X}\cap W_{I},-m).

Using (

) that we have just proved, we get

\kappa(W_{I},\mathcal{X}\cap W_{I},-m)=(-1)^{\#I-(n-k)}\gamma^{+}(W_{I},\mathcal{X}\cap W_{I},m).

To check the sign, note that in Theorem
7.1
the integer

is

for some

. Here, the elements of

have reflection length

in

(it is easily seen to be the same as their reflection length in

, which has rank

, this sign is thus given by the difference

\kappa^{+}(W,\mathcal{X},-m)=(-1)^{k}\sum_{I\subset S}\gamma^{+}(W_{I},\mathcal{X}\cap W_{I},m).

From (

∎

•

•

f
contains the negative roots
-\delta_{i}
for
i\in S\backslash\supp(w^{\prime}_{m})
.

To check that this is indeed in
\Upsilon(W,m)
, first note that from (
8.3
) we get

\prod f^{+}=(w^{\prime}_{0})^{-1}w^{\prime}_{m}

(8.4)

with the notation
f^{+}=f\cap\Phi^{(m)}_{+}
as before. Indeed, the order is such that (reading this product from left to right) we first read roots with color
1
, then roots with color
2
, etc. By Proposition
6.8
, this shows that
f^{+}\in\Upsilon^{+}(W,m)
. Then, each element
-\delta_{i}\in f
is such that
s_{i}\notin\supp(w_{m})
. Consequently,
s_{i}\notin\supp(t)
for each
t\in T
such that
t\leq w_{m}
. Thus this
-\delta_{i}
is compatible with the roots in
f^{+}
, and it follows
f\in\Upsilon(W,m)
.

To compute
\underline{f}
, let’s keep the notation as in the proof of Proposition
7.2
so that
f\cap(-\Delta)=\{-\delta_{i}\colon i\in S\backslash I\}
for some
I\subset S
. From (
8.4
), we get

\prod f=\bigg(\prod_{i\in S^{+}\backslash I}\bigg)(w^{\prime}_{0})^{-1}w^{\prime}_{m}\bigg(\prod_{i\in S^{-}\backslash I}\bigg)

so that

\underline{f}=\bigg(\prod_{i\in S^{+}\cap I}\bigg)(w^{\prime}_{0})^{-1}w^{\prime}_{m}\bigg(\prod_{i\in S^{-}\cap I}\bigg).

By definition, we have

w^{\prime}_{m}=\bigg(\prod_{i\in S^{+}\cap I}\bigg)\bigg(\prod_{i\in S^{-}\cap I}\bigg).

Indeed, the condition
w^{\prime}_{m}\sqsubset c
implies that a reduced factorization of
w^{\prime}_{m}
can be extracted from the canonical one of
c=c_{+}c_{-}
, and the definition of
f
implies that the elements that appear are those indexed by
I
. From the previous two equations, we get

\underline{f}=\bigg(\prod_{i\in S^{+}\cap I}\bigg)(w^{\prime}_{0})^{-1}\bigg(\prod_{i\in S^{+}\cap I}\bigg).

We thus have
f\in\Upsilon(W,X,m)
, since
\Fix(\underline{f})\sim\Fix((w^{\prime}_{0})^{-1})\sim\Fix(w^{\prime}_{0})\sim X
.

Describing the inverse bijection is straightforward. Starting from
f\in\Upsilon(W,m)
, define

•

w^{\prime}_{m}\sqsubset c
is the product of the reflections
t_{\alpha}
for
\alpha\in(-\Delta)\backslash f
,

•

(w^{\prime}_{i-1})^{-1}w^{\prime}_{i}
is the product of
t_{\alpha^{i}}
over positive roots
\alpha^{i}
(of color
i
) in
f
.

From
w^{\prime}_{i-1}
,
w^{\prime}_{i}
, and the positive roots of color
i
in
f
, we use the inverse bijection from Lemma
8.3
to get
w_{i}
such that
w^{\prime}_{i-1}\sqsubset w_{i}\ll w^{\prime}_{i}
. We omit details about checking that the two maps are indeed inverse bijections.

We thus have proved (
8.1
). Finally, observe that when we restrict this bijection to chains such that
w_{m}\ll c
, we get
w^{\prime}_{m}=c
(keeping the same notation), and the corresponding faces
f\in\Upsilon(W,\mathcal{X},m)
contain no vertex in
-\Delta
. So the bijection proves (
8.2
) as well.
∎

In the case where
X
is the minimal flat, our bijection specializes into a bijection between facets of
\Upsilon(W,m)
and chains
w_{1}\leq\dots\leq w_{m}
. Such a bijection was first given via representation theory by Buan, Reiten, and Thomas
[

]
. A more combinatorial one and various related bijections were given by Stump, Thomas, and Williams
[

]
.

To end this section, we use the previous bijection to get the proof of Corollary

.

Proof of Corollary
7.3
.

From Lemma
5.5
and
\bPsi_{-1}=(-1)^{n}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
, we have

(-1)^{n}\bD\bPsi_{-t}=\bN\bPsi_{t}.

By taking the coefficients via Proposition
3.7
and evaluating at
t=mh+1
and
t=mh-1
, this respectively gives

\displaystyle(-1)^{\dim(X)}\frac{p_{X}(-mh-1)}{[N(W_{X}):W_{X}]}=\sum_{Y\in\Theta}\bN_{X,Y}\frac{p_{Y}(mh+1)}{[N(W_{Y}):W_{Y}]},

\displaystyle(-1)^{\dim(X)}\frac{p_{X}(-mh+1)}{[N(W_{X}):W_{X}]}=\sum_{Y\in\Theta}\bN_{X,Y}\frac{p_{Y}(mh-1)}{[N(W_{Y}):W_{Y}]}.

Using the formulas for
\kappa
and
\kappa^{+}
obtained in (

) and (

), we see that the right-hand sides of the previous two equations give, respectively, the right-hand sides in (

) and (

).

By identifying the left-hand sides of the previous two equations with those of (

) and (

in Corollary

∎

9
Numerology of
\boldsymbol{f}
- and
\boldsymbol{h}
-vectors

Recall that the integers
f_{k}
are the entries of the
f
-vector of
\Upsilon(W,m)
. First, let us explain how our formulas for
\gamma
completely explain some partial factorizations of
f_{k}
as a polynomial in
m
, obtained by Fomin and Reading
[
17
, Section 8]
. They introduced the notion of
level
of an exponent
e_{i}
, by the rule that
mh+1+e_{i}
is a factor of
f_{k}
(as a polynomial in
m
) iff
e_{i}
has level at most
k
.

By examining the tables in
[
28
, Appendix C]
, we note that the Orlik–Solomon exponents
b_{i}^{X}
of
X\in\Theta
(with
\dim(X)=k)
often look like
e_{1},\dots,e_{k}
, namely the
k
smallest exponents of
W
(in fact, this happens exactly for the so-called
coincidental types

[
42
, Section 3.1.5]
:
A_{n}
,
B_{n}
,
I_{2}(m)
, and
H_{3}
, see
[
26
, Section 3.3]
). Now, consider the formula for
f_{k-1}
in (
6.1
) as a sum of polynomials in
m
. If some integer
b
is an Orlik–Solomon exponent for all
X\in\Theta
with
\dim(X)=k
, each term contains a factor
(mh+1+b)
so that their sum
f_{k-1}
also has this factor. Also, we can observe that there is an equivalence between the two statements:

•

The integer
b
is an Orlik–Solomon exponent for all
X\in\Theta
with
\dim(X)=k
.

•

The integer
b
is an Orlik–Solomon exponent for all
X\in\Theta
with
\dim(X)\geq k
.

(It is easily checked on a case-by-case basis.) Note that it might
a priori
happen that
f_{k}
contains a factor
mh+1-b
even though some of the terms in the sum of (
6.1
) don’t contain it. This actually never happen. The upshot of this discussion is:

Proposition 9.1
.

An exponent
e_{i}
of
W
has level
j
in the sense of Fomin and Reading
[
17
]
if and only if
e_{i}
is an Orlik–Solomon exponent of all
X\in\Theta
with
\dim(X)\geq j
.

For example in type
F_{4}
, the exponents
1
,
5
,
7
,
11
have respective levels
1
,
3
,
3
and
4
(see
[
17
, Table 1]
). The parabolic conjugacy class of type
B_{2}
has Orlik–Solomon exponents
1
and
3
, which differ from the two smallest exponents
1
and
5
(and this is the sole discrepancy, see
[
28
, Table C.9]
). This Orlik–Solomon exponent
3
(rather than
5
) explains that the exponent
5
has level
3
(rather than
2
).

Now, consider the
h
-vector
(h_{i})_{0\leq i\leq n}
of
\Upsilon(W,m)
and
\big(h^{+}_{i}\big)_{0\leq i\leq n}
of
\Upsilon^{+}(W,m)
, defined in terms of the
f
-vector via the polynomial relation

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n}f_{i-1}z^{n-i}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}h_{i}(z+1)^{n-i},\qquad\sum_{i=0}^{n}f^{+}_{i-1}z^{n-i}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}h^{+}_{i}(z+1)^{n-i}.

(9.1)

These
h
-vectors have been obtained by Athanasiadis and Tzanaki
[
6
,
7
]
and coincide with the Fuß–Narayana numbers of Armstrong
[
1
, Chapter 5]
. These results can be stated as follows:

Proposition 9.2

(Athanasiadis and Tzanaki
[
6
,
7
]
)
.

Recalling the definition of Narayana numbers in (
2.2
) and (
2.3
), for
0\leq k\leq n
we have

h_{n-k}=\operatorname{Nar}^{(m)}(W,k),\qquad h^{+}_{n-k}=\operatorname{Nar}_{+}^{(m)}(W,k).

Let us explain how the formulas in equations (
8.1
) and (
8.2
) can be seen as a refinement of the
f
- to
h
-vector transformation. By summing (
8.1
) over
X\in\Theta
of a given dimension
k
, the left-hand side gives
f_{k-1}
(see equation (
6.1
)). The sum in the right-hand side is

\displaystyle\sum_{Y\in\Theta}\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\in\Theta,\\
\dim(X)=k\end{subarray}}\bN_{X,Y}\bigg)\kappa(W,Y,m)=\sum_{Y\in\Theta}\binom{n-\dim Y}{n-k}\kappa(W,Y,m)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{n-j}{n-k}h_{j}.

Indeed, the sum between parentheses gives the binomial coefficient (this is clear from the combinatorial definition), then we use equation (
3.8
) and the previous proposition. The relation we obtain is the first in (
9.1
) (the second one is obtained similarly).

Let us also briefly show how the bijection from Section
8
gives the formulas above for
h_{k}
and
h^{+}_{k}
.

Proof of Proposition
9.2
.

Via the bijection from Section
8
, faces of
\Upsilon(W,m)
of dimension
k-1
are in bijection with chains
w_{0}\sqsubset w_{1}\leq\dots\leq w_{m}
in
\NC(W,c)
such that
\ell(w_{0})=n-k
. So, we get

\sum_{k=0}^{n}f_{k-1}z^{n-k}=\sum_{w_{0}\sqsubset w_{1}\leq\dots\leq w_{m}}z^{\ell(w_{0})}.

Since the order ideal containing elements below
w_{1}
for
\sqsubset
is a boolean lattice, the binomial theorem gives:

\sum_{k=0}^{n}f_{k-1}z^{n-k}=\sum_{w_{1}\leq\dots\leq w_{m}}(1+z)^{\ell(w_{1})}.

By comparing with (
9.1
), we immediately get the combinatorial formula for
h_{k}
. The one for
h^{+}_{k}
is obtained similarly.
∎

It is well known that a shelling of a simplicial complex can be used to find its
h
-vector. We refer to
[
41
]
. Explicitly, suppose we have a shelling of
\Upsilon(W,m)
, i.e., an indexing
F_{1},F_{2},\dots
of its facets such that for each
j
the (geometric) intersection

\displaystyle\Bigg(\bigcup_{i=1}^{j-1}F_{i}\Bigg)\cap F_{j}

(9.2)

is purely
1
-codimensional in
F_{j}
. Then
h_{k}
is the number of indices
j
such that the intersection in (
9.2
) is the union of
k
facets of
F_{j}
. It would be very interesting to show that our equation (
8.1
) can also be obtained via such a shelling of
\Upsilon(W,m)
, and similarly equation (
8.2
) from a shelling of
\Upsilon^{+}(W,m)
. A natural candidate for this is the shelling given by Stump, Thomas and Williams
[
38
]
.

From a different perspective, Reiner, Shepler, and Sommers
[
29
]
have constructed invariant-theoretic
q
-analogs of the Kirkman and Narayana numbers for the coincidental types. They have proven a
q
-version of the transformation between
f
- and
h
-vectors for these
q
-Kirkman and
q
-Narayana numbers. However, that works mostly in the level of formula manipulations. It is an interesting open problem to extend this, perhaps by finding a more conceptual construction, to all real reflection groups.

10
A recursion for the refined enumeration of faces

We give a recursion satisfied by the numbers
\gamma(W,X,m)
, in the spirit of Fomin and Reading’s recursion for the
f
-vector of
\Upsilon(W,m)
. Our main tool is also the rotation
\mathcal{R}_{m}
, and the method relies on the following key result.

Proposition 10.1
.

For each face
f\in\Upsilon(W,m)
,
\underline{f}
and
\underline{\mathcal{R}_{m}(f)}
are conjugate in
W
. In other words,
\Upsilon(W,X,m)
is stable under the action of
\mathcal{R}_{m}
.

Proof.

Let
f=\{\rho_{1}\succ\dots\succ\rho_{k}\}
, so that
\prod f=t_{\rho_{1}}\cdots t_{\rho_{k}}\in\NC(W,c)
and
\underline{f}=c_{+}(\prod f)c_{-}
. There is a factorization
\prod f=w_{+}w_{1}\cdots w_{m}w_{-}
obtained as follows:
w_{+}
(respectively,
w_{i}
,
w_{-}
) is obtained from
t_{\rho_{1}}\cdots t_{\rho_{k}}
by keeping the factors
t_{\rho_{j}}
such that
\rho_{j}\in-\Delta_{+}
(respectively, such that
\rho_{j}
is a positive root with color
i
, such that
\rho_{j}\in-\Delta_{-}
). This is possible because the definition of these factors agrees with the total order
\prec
on
\Phi^{(m)}_{\geq-1}
: the elements in
-\Delta_{+}
come first in
\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\dots
, etc. Using similar notations, the noncrossing partition associated to the face
f^{\prime}=\mathcal{R}_{m}(f)
is denoted
\prod f^{\prime}=w^{\prime}_{+}w^{\prime}_{1}\cdots w^{\prime}_{m}w^{\prime}_{-}
.

To find what are the factors of
\prod f^{\prime}
, we need to refine the factorization of
\prod f
. Note that from Proposition
6.5
, we get

\{\alpha_{nh/2-n+s+1},\dots,\alpha_{nh/2}\}=c^{-1}(-\Delta_{+}).

We write

w_{m}=w_{m,3}w_{m,2}w_{m,1},

where
w_{m,3}
contains the factors
t_{\alpha^{m}}
where
\alpha\in\Delta_{-}
,
w_{m,2}
contains the factors
t_{\alpha^{m}}
where
\alpha\in c^{-1}(-\Delta_{+})
, and
w_{m,1}
contains the other factors
t_{\alpha^{m}}
. As above, this is possible because this definition agrees with the ordering on
\Phi^{(m)}_{\geq-1}
.

By examining the action of
\mathcal{R}_{m}
, we can check

\displaystyle w^{\prime}_{+}=cw_{m,2}c^{-1},

\displaystyle w^{\prime}_{1}=\big(cw_{m,1}c^{-1}\big)\big(cw_{-}c^{-1}\big)w_{+},

\displaystyle w^{\prime}_{i}=w_{i-1}\text{ for }2\leq i\leq m,

\displaystyle w^{\prime}_{-}=w_{m,3}.

For example,
w^{\prime}_{-}=w_{m,3}
comes from the case
\mathcal{R}_{m}(\alpha^{m})=-\alpha
if
\alpha\in\Delta_{-}
.
Gathering the factors gives

\displaystyle\prod f^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\big(cw_{m,2}c^{-1}\big)\big(cw_{m,1}c^{-1}\cdot cw_{-}c^{-1}\cdot w_{+}\big)w_{1}\cdots w_{m-1}(w_{m,3})

\displaystyle=\big(cw_{m,2}w_{m,1}w_{-}c^{-1}\cdot w_{+})w_{1}\cdots w_{m-1}w_{m,3}

\displaystyle=\big(cw_{m,2}w_{m,1}w_{-}c^{-1}\big)\Big(\prod f\Big)(w_{m,2}w_{m,1}w_{-})^{-1}.

Using
c=c_{+}c_{-}
and the definition of
\underline{f}
, it follows

\underline{f^{\prime}}=c_{-}(w_{m,2}w_{m,1}w_{-})c_{-}\underline{f}c_{-}(w_{m,2}w_{m,1}w_{-})^{-1}c_{-}.

So
\underline{f^{\prime}}
and
\underline{f}
are conjugate.
∎

Proposition 10.2
.

If
W=W_{1}\times W_{2}
and
X=X_{1}\times X_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\gamma(W,X,m)=\gamma(W_{1},X_{1},m)\cdot\gamma(W_{2},X_{2},m).

(10.1)

Proof.

This follows from
\Upsilon(W,X,m)
being the join of
\Upsilon(W_{1},X_{1},m)
and
\Upsilon(W_{2},X_{2},m)
.
∎

To state the next proposition, we extend the definition of
\gamma(W,X,m)
in the same way as we did for
\kappa
and
\kappa^{+}
in Section
7
. We use parabolic conjugacy classes rather than flats, and if
\mathcal{Y}=\biguplus_{i=1}^{j}\mathcal{X}_{i}
is a disjoint union of parabolic conjugacy classes, we write

\displaystyle\gamma(W,\mathcal{Y},m)=\sum_{i=1}^{j}\gamma(W,\mathcal{X}_{i},m).

(10.2)

Proposition 10.3
.

Assume that
W
is irreducible and let
h
be its Coxeter number. We have

\gamma(W,\mathcal{X},m)=\frac{mh+2}{2k}\sum_{s\in S}\gamma(W_{(s)},\mathcal{X}\cap W_{(s)},m).

(10.3)

Proof.

Following the idea used in
[
17
]
to count facets of
\Upsilon(W,m)
, we consider
pointed faces
and let

\Upsilon^{\bullet}(W,X,m):=\big\{(f,\rho)\colon f\in\Upsilon(W,X,m),\;\rho\in f\big\}.

The result comes from double counting. First, we clearly have

\#\Upsilon^{\bullet}(W,\mathcal{X},m)=k\cdot\#\Upsilon(W,\mathcal{X},m)=k\cdot\gamma(W,\mathcal{X},m),

as each face
f\in\Upsilon(W,\mathcal{X},m)
contains
k
vertices.

Second, we consider the action of
\mathcal{R}_{m}
on
\Upsilon^{\bullet}(W,\mathcal{X},m)
by
\mathcal{R}_{m}\big((f,\rho)\big)=\big(\mathcal{R}_{m}(f),\mathcal{R}_{m}(\rho)\big)
. Let
\omega\subset\Phi_{\geq-1}^{(m)}
be an orbit for the action of
\mathcal{R}_{m}
. We will show that

\displaystyle\#\big\{(f,\rho)\in\Upsilon^{\bullet}(W,\mathcal{X},m)\colon\rho\in\omega\big\}=\frac{mh+2}{2}\sum_{\alpha\in(-\Delta)\cap\omega}\gamma(W_{(t_{\alpha})},\mathcal{X}\cap W_{(t_{\alpha})},m).

(10.4)

By summing over the orbits
\omega
, it will follow that
\#\Upsilon^{\bullet}(W,\mathcal{X},m)
is the right hand side of (
10.3
) multiplied by
k
(since the union of the sets
(-\Delta)\cap\omega
is
-\Delta
). So it remains only to prove (
10.4
).

First observe that the cardinality in the left-hand side of (
10.4
) actually doesn’t depend on
\rho
. Indeed, the rotation
\mathcal{R}_{m}
immediately gives a bijection between the set associated to
\rho
and that associated to
\mathcal{R}_{m}(\rho)
.

Consider the first case of Proposition
6.2
. So,
\#\omega=\frac{mh+2}{2}
and
\omega
contains one element of
-\Delta
, that we denote by
-\delta_{i}
. By the previous observation, we get

\displaystyle\#\big\{(f,\rho)\in\Upsilon^{\bullet}(W,\mathcal{X},m)\colon\rho\in\omega\big\}=\frac{mh+2}{2}\#\big\{f\in\Upsilon(W,\mathcal{X},m)\colon{-}\delta_{i}\in f\big\}.

The cardinality in the right-hand side is also
\gamma\big(W_{(s_{i})},\mathcal{X}\cap W_{(s_{i})},m\big)
. We thus get the term indexed by
s_{i}
in (
10.3
) (up to the factor
k
).

Now, consider the second case of Proposition
6.2
. So,
\#\omega=mh+2
and
\omega
contains two elements of
-\Delta
, that we denote by
-\delta_{i}
and
-\delta_{j}
. In this case, we get

\displaystyle\#\big\{(f,\rho)\in\Upsilon^{\bullet}(W,\mathcal{X},m)\colon\rho\in\omega\big\}=(mh+2)\cdot\#\big\{f\in\Upsilon(W,\mathcal{X},m)\colon{-}\delta_{i}\in f\big\}.

Here, we get the two terms indexed by
s_{i}
and
s_{j}
in (
10.3
) (up to the factor
k
). Indeed these two terms are equal (since
s_{i}
and
s_{j}
are conjugate), and combine to give one of the two terms multiplied by
mh+2
.
∎

To finish this section, note that equations (

), (

), and (

) give an inductive procedure to compute

as follows:

•

if

is the conjugacy class of the Coxeter element, we have

(initial case),

•

if

is reducible, use (

),

•

if
W
is irreducible, use (
10.3
), then use (
10.2
) on each summand (in particular
\gamma\big(W_{(s)},\mathcal{X}\cap W_{(s)},m\big)=0
if

),

•

repeat the previous two steps, until each term can be treated by the initial case.

11
The recursion for counting factorizations

We consider a class of minimal factorizations of the Coxeter element and its refined enumeration via a
q
-statistic defined in terms of the orders
\sqsubset
and
\ll
. This kind of refined enumeration was first considered in
[
25
]
, and reinterpreted in
[
9
]
. In these references, we deal with reflection factorizations of the Coxeter element.

Here we consider more general factorizations where the first factor is in a given parabolic conjugacy class
\mathcal{X}
. They were considered by the first author in
[
13
]
. As for the refined enumeration, the obtained polynomial
\phi(W,\mathcal{X},q)
is shown to satisfy a recursion which is equivalent to the one obtained in Section
10
for
\gamma(W,X,m)
. Consequently, we get a formula for
\phi(W,\mathcal{X},q)
in terms of the Orlik–Solomon exponents from the formula for
\gamma
in Corollary
7.3
.

Definition 11.1
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a parabolic conjugacy class, and
k
such that the elements of
\mathcal{X}
have reflection length
n-k
. We define
\mathfrak{F}(W,\mathcal{X},c)
as the set of length-additive factorizations
c=wt_{1}\cdots t_{k}
where
w\in\mathcal{X}
and
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}\in T
. We define a statistic
\nil
on this set by

\nil(wt_{1}\cdots t_{k}):=\#\big\{i\in\{1,\dots,k\}\colon wt_{1}\cdots t_{i-1}\ll wt_{1}\cdots t_{i}\big\},

and the associated generating function:

\phi(W,\mathcal{X},q):=\sum_{wt_{1}\cdots t_{k}\in\mathfrak{F}(W,\mathcal{X},c)}q^{\nil(wt_{1}\cdots t_{k})}.

Let us comment on this definition. First, note that the factorizations above are
length-additive
, i.e.,
\ell(c)=\ell(w)+\ell(t_{1})+\dots+\ell(t_{k})
. Also, it is not
a priori
obvious from the definition that the generating function doesn’t depend on the Coxeter element. This property will be apparent from the recursion given below (in particular it is part of the induction hypothesis). We anticipate this, omitting dependence on
c
in the notation.

Since the factorizations are minimal, the two elements
wt_{1}\cdots t_{i-1}
and
wt_{1}\cdots t_{i}
in the definition of the
\nil
statistic form a cover relation in
\NC(W,c)
. As these two elements differ by multiplying with a reflection, they are comparable in the
Bruhat order
, denoted
\leq_{B}
. We have

\displaystyle wt_{1}\cdots t_{i-1}\ll wt_{1}\cdots t_{i}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad wt_{1}\cdots t_{i-1}\geq_{B}wt_{1}\cdots t_{i},

\displaystyle wt_{1}\cdots t_{i-1}\sqsubset wt_{1}\cdots t_{i}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad wt_{1}\cdots t_{i-1}\leq_{B}wt_{1}\cdots t_{i}.

Though it is interesting to make the connection with the order
\ll
used through this work, here it will be adequate to think in terms of the Bruhat order and write

\nil(wt_{1}\cdots t_{k})=\#\big\{i\in\{1,\dots,k\}\colon wt_{1}\cdots t_{i-1}\geq_{B}wt_{1}\cdots t_{i}\big\}.

Let’s recall some facts about
inversions
, which are closely connected to the Bruhat order. Recall from Section
6
that
\Phi_{+}
(respectively,
\Phi_{-}
) is the set of positive roots (respectively, negative roots). We call

•

\alpha\in\Phi_{+}
an
inversion
of
w\in W
if
w(\alpha)\in\Phi_{-}
,

•

t\in T
a
right inversion
of
w\in W
if
wt
has smaller Coxeter length than
w
.

These two notions are related:
\alpha\in\Phi_{+}
is an inversion of
w
if and only if
t_{\alpha}
is a right inversion of
w
.

Lemma 11.2
.

There is a bijection between right inversions of
c
and
S
. It sends
t
to the unique
s\in S
such that
ct\in W_{(s)}
.

Proof.

A reduced expression of
c
is
c=s_{1}\cdots s_{n}
. By a well-known fact, it follows that
c
has
n
right inversions
t_{1},\dots t_{n}
explicitly given by

ct_{i}=(s_{1}\cdots s_{i-1})\cdot(s_{i+1}\cdots s_{n}).

The result follows straightforwardly.
∎

Lemma 11.3
.

Assume that
W
is irreducible, and let
h
be its Coxeter number. Each orbit for the action of
c
on
\Phi
has cardinality
h
and contains exactly one inversion of
c
.

Proof.

The fact that the orbits have cardinality
h
follows from Steinberg’s indexing of the root system (that we mentioned in Section
6
, in relation with the reflection ordering). Though this concerns the bipartite Coxeter element, other standard Coxeter elements are conjugate to it and the result follows.

Next, we show that each orbit
\omega\subset\Phi
contains at least one inversion of
c
. Since
c
has
n
inversions by the previous lemma, it will follow that each of the
n
orbits contains exactly one inversion by the pigeonhole principle. So, let
\omega\subset\Phi
be an orbit. Since
h
is the order of
c
, we have

0=c^{h}-I=(c-I)\Bigg(\sum_{i=0}^{h-1}c^{i}\Bigg).

As
1
is not an eigenvalue of
c
(see
[
23
, Section 3.19]
for details),
c-I
is invertible. Therefore,

\sum_{i=0}^{h-1}c^{i}=0.

By evaluating the linear operators on both sides of this equation at some
\alpha\in\omega
, we get
{\sum_{\alpha\in\omega}\alpha=0}
. So
\omega
contains both positive roots and negative roots. We deduce that
\omega
contains at least a positive root whose image by
c
is negative, i.e., an inversion of
c
.
∎

The next proposition is similar to Proposition
6.2
(and Steinberg’s result described afterwards). But here, the statement holds for any standard Coxeter element and not just the bipartite Coxeter element.

Proposition 11.4
.

Assume that
W
is irreducible, and let
h
be its Coxeter number. Then each orbit
\omega\subset T
for the action of
c
by conjugation satisfies

•

either
\#\omega=h/2
and it contains one right inversion of
c
,

•

or
\#\omega=h
and it contains two right inversions of
c
.

Proof.

The action of
c
by conjugation on the set
T
of reflections is related to the action of
c
on roots by

t_{c(\alpha)}=c\cdot t_{\alpha}\cdot c^{-1}.

So, this essentially follows from Lemma
11.3
. Let
\omega\subset T
be an orbit. Note that
R^{-1}(\omega)\subset\Phi
is stable under the action of
c
and under the map
-I
.

First suppose that
R^{-1}(\omega)
is a single orbit under the action of
c
. Since the map
R
is 2 to 1,
\omega
has cardinality
h/2
. By Lemma
11.3
,
R^{-1}(\omega)
contains
1
inversion of
c
, and it follows that
\omega
contains one right inversion of
c
.

In the second case, suppose that
R^{-1}(\omega)
is the union of two orbits. Since the map
R
is 2 to 1,
\omega
has cardinality
h
. By Lemma
11.3
,
R^{-1}(\omega)
contains
2
inversion of
c
, and it follows that
\omega
contains two right inversion of
c
.
∎

To state the next result, we first need to slightly extend the definition of
\phi
. This is completely similar to what we did for
\gamma
and
\kappa
. So, if
\mathcal{Y}=\biguplus_{i=1}^{j}\mathcal{X}_{i}
is a disjoint union of parabolic conjugacy classes, we define

\phi(W,\mathcal{Y},q):=\sum_{i=1}^{j}\phi(W,\mathcal{X}_{i},q).

(11.1)

As before, this situation occurs when we consider the intersection of a parabolic conjugacy class with a parabolic subgroup.

Lemma 11.5
.

Let
t\in T
. There is an integer
i
such that
c^{i}tc^{-i}
is a right inversion of
c
, and this corresponds to an element
s\in S
via the bijection in Lemma
11.2
. With these notations, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}wt_{1}\cdots t_{k}\in\mathfrak{F}(W,\mathcal{X},c),\\[2.84526pt]
t_{k}=t\end{subarray}}\!\!q^{\nil(wt_{1}\cdots t_{k})}=\begin{cases}\phi\big(W_{(s)},\mathcal{X}\cap W_{(s)},q\big)&\text{if $t$ is a right inversion of $c$},\\
q\phi\big(W_{(s)},\mathcal{X}\cap W_{(s)},q\big)&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(11.2)

Proof.

Suppose first that
t
is a right inversion of
c
, so that
ct\in W_{(s)}
. It follows that
wt_{1}\cdots t_{k-1}\leq_{B}wt_{1}\cdots t_{k}
and this pair of elements doesn’t contribute to the
\nil
statistic. Since
ct
is
s_{1}\cdots s_{n}
with
s
omitted (see the proof of Lemma
11.2
), it is a standard Coxeter element of
W_{(s)}
. To each factorization
c=wt_{1}\cdots t_{k}
as in the left-hand side of (
11.2
), we associate
ct=wt_{1}\cdots t_{k-1}
which is again a minimal factorization. We thus immediately obtain the generating function
\phi(W_{(s)},\mathcal{X}\cap W_{(s)},q)
.

Consider the second case. We now have
wt_{1}\cdots t_{k-1}\geq_{B}wt_{1}\cdots t_{k}
, so that this pair of elements contribute by 1 to the
\nil
statistic, giving the factor
q
. As in the previous case, the sum can be viewed as a sum over factorizations of
ct
. We thus get
q\phi(W_{\Fix(ct)},\mathcal{X}\cap W_{\Fix(ct)},q)
, but we have

\phi\big(W_{\Fix(ct)},\mathcal{X}\cap W_{\Fix(ct)},q\big)=\phi\big(W_{(s)},\mathcal{X}\cap W_{(s)},q\big)

since
W_{\Fix(ct)}
is conjugate to
W_{(s)}
in
W
.
∎

Proposition 11.6
.

Assume that
W
is irreducible, and let
h
be its Coxeter number. We have

\phi(W,\mathcal{X},q)=\frac{2+q(h-2)}{2}\sum_{s\in S}\phi\big(W_{(s)},\mathcal{X}\cap W_{(s)},q\big).

(11.3)

Proof.

We partition the set
\mathfrak{F}(W,\mathcal{X},c)
into subsets
\mathfrak{F}_{\omega}
, where
\omega
are the orbits for the action of
c
on
T
,

\mathfrak{F}_{\omega}:=\{wt_{1}\cdots t_{k}\in\mathfrak{F}(W,c,\mathcal{X})\colon t_{k}\in\omega\},

so that
\phi(W,\mathcal{X},q)
is obtained by summing the generating functions of the sets
\mathfrak{F}_{\omega}
.

We first consider the first case of Proposition
11.4
, so that
\#\omega=\frac{h}{2}
and
\omega
contains a right inversion
t\in T
of
c
. Let
s\in S
such that
ct\in W_{(s)}
. Using Lemma
11.5
, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{wt_{1}\cdots t_{k}\in\mathfrak{F}_{\omega}}q^{\nil(wt_{1}\cdots t_{k})}=\big(1+q\big(\tfrac{h}{2}-1\big)\big)\phi\big(W_{(s)},\mathcal{X}\cap W_{(s)},q\big).

(11.4)

Now, we consider the other case and assume that
\#\omega=h
. By Proposition
11.4
, this orbit contains two right inversions of
c
, that we denote
t,t^{\prime}\in T
. Also, let
s,s^{\prime}\in S
such that
ct\in W_{(s)}
and
ct^{\prime}\in W_{(s^{\prime})}
. Using Lemma
11.5
, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{wt_{1}\cdots t_{k}\in\mathfrak{F}_{\omega}}q^{\nil(wt_{1}\cdots t_{k})}=(2+q(h-2))\phi\big(W_{(s)},\mathcal{X}\cap W_{(s)},q\big),

which can also be written

\displaystyle\sum_{wt_{1}\cdots t_{k}\in\omega}\!\!q^{\nil(wt_{1}\cdots t_{k})}\!=\!\big(1+q\big(\tfrac{h}{2}-1\big)\big)\big(\phi\big(W_{(s)},\mathcal{X}\cap W_{(s)},q\big)+\phi\big(W_{(s^{\prime})},\mathcal{X}\cap W_{(s^{\prime})},q\big)\big).

(11.5)

By summing (
11.4
) and (
11.5
), we get the result. The fact that each
s\in S
appears exactly once in the sum follows from Lemma
11.2
.
∎

As for the reducible case, we have the following statement.

Proposition 11.7
.

Suppose
W=W_{1}\times W_{2}
. For
i\in\{1,2\}
, let
\mathcal{X}_{i}
be a parabolic conjugacy class in
W_{i}
. Let
k_{i}
such that the elements of
\mathcal{X}_{i}
have reflection length
n_{i}-k_{i}
where
n_{i}
is the rank of
W_{i}
. Then

\phi(W_{1}\times W_{2},\mathcal{X}_{1}\times\mathcal{X}_{2},q)=\binom{k_{1}+k_{2}}{k_{1}}\phi(W_{1},\mathcal{X}_{1},q)\phi(W_{2},\mathcal{X}_{2},q).

(11.6)

Proof.

The Coxeter element is denoted
c=(c_{1},c_{2})
. For
i\in\{1,2\}
, the projection
W_{1}\times W_{2}\to W_{i}
on each factor gives a map
\mathfrak{F}(W_{1}\times W_{2},(c_{1},c_{2}),\mathcal{X}_{1}\times\mathcal{X}_{2})\to\mathfrak{F}(W_{i},c_{i},\mathcal{X}_{i})
(ignoring the factors
t_{j}
that are not reflections
W_{i}
, that give the unit of
W_{i}
). Together, these two maps give one map

\mathfrak{F}(W_{1}\times W_{2},\mathcal{X}_{1}\times\mathcal{X}_{2},(c_{1},c_{2}))\to\mathfrak{F}(W_{1},\mathcal{X}_{1},c_{1})\times\mathfrak{F}(W_{2},\mathcal{X}_{2},c_{2}).

It is easily seen that this map is
\binom{k_{1}+k_{2}}{k_{1}}
-to-
1
. Indeed, we recover the initial factorization
wt_{1}\cdots t_{k_{1}+k_{2}}
if we know which
t_{i}
correspond to a reflection of
W_{1}
(or
W_{2}
), and this gives a choice of
k_{1}
indices among
k_{1}+k_{2}
.

It is also easily seen that the
q
-statistic is preserved via the above map, as the Bruhat order on
W_{1}\times W_{2}
identifies with the product of the two Bruhat orders.
∎

Gathering the previous propositions, we have an inductive way to compute
\phi(W,\mathcal{X},q)
:

•

if
\mathcal{X}
is the conjugacy class of the Coxeter element, we have
\phi(W,\mathcal{X},q)=1
(initial case),

•

if
W
is reducible, use (
11.6
),

•

if
W
is irreducible, use (
11.3
), then use (
11.1
) on each term,

•

repeat the previous two steps until each term can be treated via the initial case.

Theorem 11.8
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a parabolic conjugacy class, and
k
such that the elements of
\mathcal{X}
have reflection length
n-k
. We have

\displaystyle\phi(W,\mathcal{X},q)=k!(1-q)^{k}\gamma\big(W,\mathcal{X},\tfrac{q}{1-q}\big).

(11.7)

Proof.

Up to rescaling of the polynomials, the induction to compute
\phi
is equivalent to the one that we show
\gamma
satisfies (see Section
10
). This shows the equality by induction.
∎

From the formula for
\gamma
in terms of a characteristic polynomial, we immediately obtain the following formula for
\phi
. It is conveniently stated in terms of the Orlik–Solomon exponents.

Corollary 11.9
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a parabolic conjugacy class, let
X=\Fix(w)
for some
w\in\mathcal{X}
,
k=\dim(X)
, and recall that
b_{1}^{X},\dots,b_{k}^{X}
are the Orlik–Solomon exponents of
X
. We have

\displaystyle\phi(W,\mathcal{X},q)=\frac{k!}{[N(W_{X}):W_{X}]}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\big(b_{i}^{X}+1+q\big(h-b_{i}^{X}-1\big)\big).

Proof.

This follows from (
11.7
), together with the formula for
\gamma
in (
7.7
).
∎

In particular, the case
q=1
gives

\displaystyle\phi(W,\mathcal{X},1)=\frac{k!h^{k}}{[N(W_{X}):W_{X}]}.

This was obtained by the first author
[
13
, Theorem 99]
by geometric methods (using the Lyashko–Looijenga morphism). On the other side, the case where
X
is the maximal element of
L(W)
corresponds to the
q
-enumeration of minimal reflection factorization of
c
. The second author in
[
25
]
(see also
[
9
, Section 4.6]
) showed that

\displaystyle\phi(W,\{0\},1)=\frac{n!}{|W|}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(d_{i}+q(h-d_{i}))

(where we use the
degree

d_{i}=e_{i}+1
rather the exponent
e_{i}
). This is a one-parameter refinement of the Deligne–Looijenga number

\frac{n!h^{n}}{|W|}.

Further comments and questions

Via Proposition
3.7
, the formulas we obtained in Corollary
7.3
imply

\displaystyle\sum_{X\in\Theta}(-1)^{\dim(X)}\gamma(W,X,m)\bPhi_{X}=\bPsi_{-mh-1}=(-1)^{n}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\otimes\bPsi_{mh+1},

\displaystyle\sum_{X\in\Theta}(-1)^{\dim(X)}\gamma^{+}(W,X,m)\bPhi_{X}=\bPsi_{-mh+1}=(-1)^{n}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\otimes\bPsi_{mh-1}.

This suggests a representation theoretic interpretation, which is the goal of our next paper
[
15
]
. There, we introduce
cluster parking functions
and their positive analog. They form a simplicial complex endowed with an action of
W
. The left-hand sides of the previous two equations correspond to their (equivariant) Euler characteristic. The main goal of the paper is to show that these complexes have the homotopy of (pure) wedge of spheres, so that the unique nonzero homology group have the right-hand sides above as characters (up to the sign
(-1)^{n}
).

In Corollary
7.3
, we give a refinement of the
f
-vector of the generalized cluster complex
\Upsilon(W,m)
, that looks
almost
like a flag
f
-vector for it (as in
[
36
, Chapter III, Section 4]
; almost because it is indexed by
classes
of subsets of
[n]
). This is surprising because
\Upsilon(W,m)
is not a balanced complex. On the other side, the Coxeter complex of
W
is balanced. It is natural to ask: is there a connection between these objects that can explain the appearance of this almost flag
f
-vector? Is this part of a more general theory for non-balanced complexes? We thank Vic Reiner for these questions.

In Proposition
10.1
, we showed that
\Upsilon(W,X,m)
is stable under the action of
\mathcal{R}_{m}
. It would be interesting to find a cyclic sieving phenomenon for this action (see
[
30
]
for similar and possibly related results).
