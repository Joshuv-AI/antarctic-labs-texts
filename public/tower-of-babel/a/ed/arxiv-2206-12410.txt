A monolithic Finite Element formulation for the hydroelastic analysis of Very Large Floating Structures
Oriol Colomés, Francesc Verdugo, Ido Akkerman

Abstract
In this work we present a novel monolithic Finite Element Method (FEM) for the hydroelastic analysis of Very Large Floating Structures (VLFS) with arbitrary shapes that is stable, energy conserving and overcomes the need of an iterative algorithm. The new formulation enables a fully monolithic solution of the linear free-surface flow, described by linear potential flow, coupled with floating thin structures, described by the Euler-Bernoulli beam or Poisson-Kirchhoff plate equations. The formulation presented in this work is general in the sense that solutions can be found in the frequency and time domains, it overcomes the need of using elements with C 1 C^{1} continuity by employing a continuous/discontinuous Galerkin (C/DG) approach, and it is suitable for Finite Elements of arbitrary order. We show that the proposed approach can accurately describe the hydroelastic phenomena of VLFS with a variety of tests, including structures with elastic joints, variable bathymetry and arbitrary structural shapes.

Introduction

Floating offshore structures are of great interest for many applications. A particular type of floating structures are the so called Very Large Floating Structures (VLFS). One can find several examples of VLFS
[
50
]
, such as floating airports
[
24
,
55
]
, floating breakwaters
[
37
,
11
]
, floating solar energy installations
[
46
,
42
]
, or even futuristic floating modular cities
[
45
,
14
]
. The study of the behavior of VLFS is, therefore, relevant for a wide variety of industries and scientific disciplines. One of the main characteristics of VLFS is that the overall structural stiffness is relatively low, behaving like elastic thin plates. In addition, due to their large dimensions, the incoming waves are typically relatively short compared with the structure length. Thus, due to the low stiffness combined with short incoming waves, the response of VLFS is governed by a strong coupling between inertial, hydrodynamic and elastic responses, what is known as hydroelastic response.

The study of hydroelastic phenomena entails several challenges, namely the strong coupling between the elastic deformation of the structure and its hydrodynamic response, the analysis of the structural response under the effect of nonlinear waves, the characterization of the behavior of finite structures or the nonlinear interaction between flow and structure, see
[
31
]
. During the last decades, several techniques have been developed to analyse hydroelastic phenomena for VLFS under the effect of waves. We refer the reader to
[
10
]
for an in depth review on different methods used for the hydroelastic analysis of VLFS. Some studies have been carried out based on experimental analysis of floating elastic platforms, see for instance
[
53
,
33
,
43
]
. However, experimental studies are limited in terms of structural size and wave conditions. Other studies are based on analytical or semi-analytical approaches, see
[
47
,
39
,
2
,
3
,
52
]
, where the fundamental behaviour of floating elastic structures is assessed, assuming infinite or finite floating platforms with regular shapes. Again, analytical approaches are limited to the study of VLFS with regular shapes,
e.g.
rectangular or circular platforms. Hence, the use of numerical techniques is essential for the analysis of the hydroelastic behavior of VLFS of finite size with irregular shapes and subject to a variety of wave input conditions.

One of the most popular numerical approaches for the hydroelastic analysis of VLFS relies on the mode expansion framework for linear potential flow theory, based on the Boundary Element Method. Assuming a negligible structure draft, this approach models the effect of the structure by a pressure distribution on the free surface. The dynamic pressure distribution is approximated by a set of panels with different accuracies,
e.g.
piece-wise constant, linear, quadratic or cubic, see
[
35
,
54
,
23
,
27
]
. Most of the works based on the panel method, or mode expansion, assume regular shaped structures. For the analysis of irregular shaped VLFS, the Finite Element Method (FEM) becomes predominant in the literature due to its suitability to model the structural behaviour of arbitrary shaped platforms. Some works have developed a coupling strategy between BEM and FEM frameworks, see for instance
[
22
,
23
,
49
,
44
,
40
]
.
The study of the hydroelastic phenomena in VLFS can be done in either the frequency domain or the time domain. The former assumes a linear response of the transient effects described as a time-harmonic motion, see for instance
[
26
,
34
,
16
]
. The time-domain analysis avoids the linear assumption, making it a suitable approach for the analysis of steep wave fronts and cases with highly nonlinear effects,
[
33
,
25
]
. In this work we develop a formulation that is suitable for both approaches, frequency and time domain.

VLFS hydroelasticity is inherently a Fluid-Structure Interaction (FSI) problem, that is a fluid flow problem coupled with a structural elasticity problem. There are two main frameworks that can be used to solve this type of problems: a monolithic approach, where a unified set of coupled governing equations are solved, and a partitioned approach, where the equations for the fluid and for the structure are solved separately and a coupling strategy is used to ensure compatibility between the two solutions.
One coupling strategy that can be used for a partitioned scheme is a weak coupling (also called a staggered scheme) strategy. Here, a new fluid solution is solved using the structural displacement of the previous time step. While the obtained fluid solution is used to determine the loading on the structure. One of the advantages of partitioned schemes is that, if the software is available separately for the flow and structural deformation, one can reuse them and just develop an interface that exchanges the coupling variables. However, stable behavior of this coupling method is not guaranteed due to the added-mass effect
[
9
]
. In case of incompressible fluids, selecting a smaller time step does not resolve this problem
[
8
]
.

Alternatively, one could use a strong coupling strategy, where the compatibility conditions are enforced simultaneously. This is done by iteratively solving fluid and structural updates until convergence is achieved.
For a strongly coupled the aforementioned instabilities due added-mass effect can be avoided by using sufficient relaxation in the iteration procedure. However, this may lead to excessive iterations even if the two separate problems are linear, see for instance
[
32
,
30
,
51
]
.
Otherwise, monolithic approaches are stable and do not require additional iterations to resolve the coupling. Mayor drawback is the fact that
ad hoc
software has to be developed in case it is not available for the coupled problem at hand. A particular feature of monolithic VLFS simulations is that they lead to a mixed-dimensional problem,
i.e.
a problem described by a system of Partial Differential Equations (PDEs) defined in domains of different topological dimension. The solution of mixed-dimensional PDEs in a FEM framework presents an additional challenge, related to the coupling of Finite Element (FE) spaces defined in different dimensions, see
[
15
]
.

In this work we present a novel monolithic FEM framework for the hydroelastic analysis of VLFS with arbitrary shapes that is stable, energy conserving and overcomes the need of an iterative algorithm. The formulation is based on the monolithic FEM approach proposed by Akkerman
et al.
in
[
1
]
for linear free-surface potential flow, which is here extended to floating structures modeled as Euler-Bernoulli beams or Poisson-Kirchhoff plates. Moreover, the formulation presented in this manuscript is general in the sense that solutions can be found in the frequency and time domains. In addition, the proposed formulation overcomes the need of using elements with
C^{1}
continuity,
i.e.
continuity of rotations on the element boundaries, by using a continuous/discontinuous Galerkin (C/DG) approach formulated in
[
18
]
. Note that we restrict this manuscript to the analysis of linear problems,
i.e.
linear potential flow coupled with linear Euler-Bernoulli/Poisson-Kirchhoff structural formulations. However, the framework would still hold for nonlinear potential flow theory and/or nonlinear structural models. For the sake of completeness, in this manuscript we focus on a detailed analysis of the formulation for linear problems, keeping its extension to nonlinear problems as a future work.

The manuscript is organized as follows: in Section
2
we describe the problem setting, with the definition of the governing equations for the fluid, structure in two and three-dimensional cases, as well as the coupling conditions. In Section
3
we develop the novel monolithic formulation for VLFS for the most general case,
i.e.
a floating Poisson-Kirchhoff plate in a three-dimensional domain, giving the expression of the fully discrete problem for both, frequency and time domains. Section
4
is dedicated to the numerical analysis of the method, where we prove consistency, energy conservation, stability and convergence statements. The numerical results are shown in Section
5
, where we analyse the behavior of the method for a variety of cases in two and three dimensions and for the frequency and time domains. The final conclusions are given in Section
6
.

2
Problem setting

Let us consider a thin structure floating in a fluid. We denote the fluid domain as
\Omega
, bounded by the bottom surface,
\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{b}}
, the inlet surface,
\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{in}}
, the outlet surface,
\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{out}}
, the free surface,
\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}}
, and the interface with the floating structure,
\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}}
. The floating structure is bounded by
\delta\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}}\equiv\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs,str}}
, a set of entities of dimension
d{-}2
with
d
the topological dimension of
\Omega
. We might also consider the case in which the structure has a set of internal joints with different structural properties, denoted as
\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{j}}
. The geometry of the idealized problem is given in Figure
1
.

Figure 1:
Sketch with the definition of the geometrical entities.

We use
\mathbf{n}
for the normal vector to the surface, while

\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}
to indicate the normal to the joint on the structure plane.
Note also that the normal
\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}
appears naturally in the formulation when integrating by parts on the structural domain, see Section
3.1
.

Let us also consider the following assumptions:

Assumption 1
.

The fluid in
\Omega
is inviscid, incompressible and irrotational and can be described well by (linear) potential flow.
We also assume that there is no cavitation, i.e. detachment of the structure with respect to the fluid.

Assumption 2
.

The incoming waves have a small steepness and can be well described well by (linear) Airy wave theory.

Assumption 3
.

The floating structures are thin and can be modeled by the linear Euler-Bernoulli equations in the case of a 2-dimensional domain and by the linear Poisson-Kirchhoff theory for the 3-dimensional domain.

2.1
Linear potential flow theory

Let us denote the fluid velocity as
\mathbf{u}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
. From the incompressibility condition assumed in Assumption
1
, we know that

\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0\quad\mbox{in }\Omega.

(1)

We also know that for an inviscid and irrotational flow, there exists a potential field,
\phi:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, that satisfies

\mathbf{u}=\nabla\phi.

(2)

Combining equations (
1
) and (
2
), we reach the governing equation for a potential flow:

\Delta\phi=0\quad\mbox{in }\Omega.

(3)

Equation (
3
) is supplemented with the appropriate boundary and interface conditions. Before detailing such conditions, we introduce some notation. Let us denote by
\eta
the surface elevation with respect to the surface of the fluid at rest, and by
\mathbf{n}
the normal vector to any surface pointing outwards, see Figure
1
. We also use the notation
(\cdot)_{t}
and
(\cdot)_{tt}
for the first and second order time derivative. Using this notation, the kinematic boundary conditions are given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{n}\cdot\nabla\phi

\displaystyle=0\quad\mbox{on }\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{b}},

(4a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{n}\cdot\nabla\phi

\displaystyle=\text{u}_{\scriptsize\mbox{in}}\quad\mbox{on }\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{in}},

(4b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{n}\cdot\nabla\phi

\displaystyle=\text{u}_{\scriptsize\mbox{out}}\quad\mbox{on }\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{out}},

(4c)

\displaystyle\mathbf{n}\cdot\nabla\phi

\displaystyle=\eta_{t}\quad\mbox{on }\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}}\cup\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}}.

(4d)

Equation (
4a
) is enforcing a
no penetration
boundary condition, where the velocity normal to the boundary is zero. In equations (
4b
) and (
4c
) we enforce a prescribed inlet and outlet normal velocities,
\text{u}_{\scriptsize\mbox{in}}
and
\text{u}_{\scriptsize\mbox{out}}
, respectively. At the free surface and at the beam interface, equation (
4d
), we enforce that the time derivative of the fluid surface is equal to the normal component of the velocity. Note that this last condition is only valid under Assumption
2
, where due to small wave steepness the normal component of the velocity is well approximated by the vertical velocity component. When this assumption is not valid, equation (
4d
) should be replaced by
\phi_{z}-\nabla\phi\nabla\eta=\eta_{t}
, where
\phi_{z}
is the directional derivative of
\phi
in the vertical direction.

In addition, the fluid satisfies the dynamic boundary conditions at the free surface and at the interface between the fluid and the structure. This condition is given by the Bernoulli’s equation for pressure in a potential flow, which at the free surface and interface boundary reads

p=-\rho_{w}\phi_{t}-g\rho_{w}\eta-\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla\phi\right)^{2}\quad\mbox{on }\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}}\cup\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}}.

(5)

Where
p
is the pressure,
\rho_{w}
the fluid density and
g=9.81\ \mbox{m/s${}^{2}$}
the gravity acceleration. Under Assumption
2
, the quadratic terms in equation (
5
) can be neglected, leading to the linearized condition

p=-\rho_{w}\phi_{t}-g\rho_{w}\eta\quad\mbox{on }\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}}\cup\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}}.

(6)

At the free surface, we enforce that the pressure is equal to the atmospheric pressure, which we assume to be zero,
i.e.

p=p_{a}=0
. At the fluid-structure interface, the pressure is in equilibrium with the structure dynamics. Using Assumption
3
,
i.e.
no cavitation and thin beam/plate theories, the structural motion is governed either by the Euler-Bernoulli equation form beams or the Poisson-Kirchoff plate theory. In what follows we describe the two cases.

2.2
Euler-Bernoulli beam theory

We start by defining the 2-dimensional case, where the floating structure is modeled as a 1-dimensional Euler-Bernoulli beam. In that case, we have that the surface elevation
\eta:\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, which from Assumption
3
is equivalent to the beam deflection, satisfies

\rho_{b}h_{b}\eta_{tt}+D\Delta^{2}\eta=p\quad\mbox{on }\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}}.

(7)

With
\rho_{b}
the structure density,
h_{b}
the structure thickness and
D
the structural rigidity, given in terms of the Young modulus
E
and the moment of inertia as
D\eqdef EI=Eh_{b}^{3}/12
. Here,
D
is assumed to be constant. Adding equations (
6
) and (
7
) together and using the fact that the atmospheric pressure is zero at the free surface, we have that the potential field and the fluid surface elevation satisfy the following conditions at the fluid free surface and fluid-structure interface

\displaystyle\phi_{t}+g\eta=0\quad\mbox{on }\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}},

(8a)

\displaystyle d_{0}\eta_{tt}+D_{\rho}\Delta^{2}\eta+\phi_{t}+g\eta=0\quad\mbox{on }\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}}.

(8b)

Where
d_{0}\eqdef\frac{\rho_{b}h_{b}}{\rho_{w}}
is the draft (submerged section) of the structure and
D_{\rho}\eqdef\frac{D}{\rho_{w}}
. Equation (
3
) together with the kinematic boundary conditions given in (
4
) and the dynamic boundary conditions given in (
8
), define the systems of equations that will be analysed in subsequent sections.

In addition, we consider that the structure is allowed to have free motion at the boundaries,
i.e.
zero moment,
M\eqdef D\Delta\eta=0
, and shear
V\eqdef\nabla(D\Delta\eta)\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}=0
. We also assume that in the case of having joints, these will act as linear rotational springs, meaning that the mean moment at the join will depend linearly on the relative rotation angle, with a spring constant of
k_{\varphi}
. Since there are not any external point moment or shear force applied to the joint, the shear and moment will be continuous at these points. These conditions can be summarized as

\displaystyle D\Delta{\eta}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\mbox{on }\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs,str}},

(9a)

\displaystyle\nabla(D\Delta\eta)\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\mbox{on }\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs,str}},

(9b)

\displaystyle\langle D\Delta{\eta}\rangle

\displaystyle=-k_{\varphi}[\![\nabla\eta\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!]

\displaystyle\mbox{on }\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{j}},

(9c)

\displaystyle[\![D\Delta{\eta}\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!]

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\mbox{on }\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{j}},

(9d)

\displaystyle[\![\nabla(D\Delta\eta)\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!]

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\mbox{on }\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{j}}.

(9e)

2.3
Poisson-Kirchhoff plate theory

For the 3-dimensional case, the floating structure is modeled as a 2-dimensional thin plate governed by the Poisson-Kirchhoff theory. In this case, the plate deflection,
i.e.
surface elevation, follows the relation

\rho_{b}h_{b}\eta_{tt}+\nabla^{2}\colon(\mathbf{C}\colon\nabla^{2}\eta)=p\quad\mbox{on }\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}}.

(10)

Where
\nabla^{2}
is the Hessian operator and
\mathbf{C}
is a fourth-order symmetric tensor containing the elastic coefficients. Note that the problem is defined in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, so the Hessian operator will be a
3\times 3
matrix, while the elastic tensor
\mathbf{C}
has dimension
2
on each component. Assuming that the structure remains contained in a plane aligned with the coordinate system,
e.g.
normal to the
z
-direction, one can extend the 2-dimensional definition of the tensor as follows

C_{ijkl}=\begin{cases}\frac{h_{b}^{3}}{12}[\mu(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk})+\lambda\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}]\qquad 1\leq i,j,k,l\leq 2,\\
0\qquad\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(11)

In equation (
11
),
\delta_{\alpha\beta}
is the Kronecker delta,
\mu\eqdef\frac{E}{2(1+\nu)}
and
\lambda\eqdef\frac{\nu E}{(1-\nu^{2})}
, with
E
the Young’s modulus and
\nu
the Poisson’s ratio.

Following the same procedure as in Section
2.2
, we introduce equation (
10
) into equation (
6
), resulting into the set of governing equations on the free and structure surfaces that read

\displaystyle\phi_{t}+g\eta=0\quad\mbox{on }\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}},

(12a)

\displaystyle d_{0}\eta_{tt}+\nabla^{2}\colon(\mathbf{C}_{\rho}\colon\nabla^{2}\eta)+\phi_{t}+g\eta=0\quad\mbox{on }\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}}.

(12b)

Where we have used the notation
C_{\rho,ijkl}\eqdef\frac{C_{ijkl}}{\rho_{w}}
. Again, we consider that the structure is free of reactions on its boundaries
\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs,str}}
,
i.e.
zero moment and zero shear. We also assume that we can have linear rotational springs on 1-dimensional joints,
\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{j}}
, where the normal is linearly dependent on the rotation to the joint, with a spring constant of
k_{\phi}
, assumed scalar for simplicity. These conditions are summarized in the following set of equations

\displaystyle(\mathbf{C}_{\rho}:\nabla^{2}\eta)\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\mbox{on }\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs,str}},

(13a)

\displaystyle(\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{C}_{\rho}:\nabla^{2}\eta))\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\mbox{on }\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs,str}},

(13b)

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{C}_{\rho}:\nabla^{2}\eta\rangle

\displaystyle=-k_{\varphi}[\![\nabla\eta\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!]

\displaystyle\mbox{on }\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{j}},

(13c)

\displaystyle[\![(\mathbf{C}_{\rho}:\nabla^{2}\eta)\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!]

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\mbox{on }\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{j}},

(13d)

\displaystyle[\![(\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{C}_{\rho}:\nabla^{2}\eta))\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!]

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\mbox{on }\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{j}}.

(13e)

Note that for 2-dimensional problems the Poisson-Kirchhoff formulation presented in equations (
12
)-(
13
) collapses to the Euler-Bernoulli formulation given in (
8
)-(
9
). Hereinafter we will restrict the derivations for the most general Poisson-Kirchhoff case. We refer the reader to Appendix
Appendix A. Formulation for the floating Euler-Bernoulli beam
for the equivalent formulation for the floating the Euler-Bernoulli beam case.

3
A monolithic Finite Element formulation

3.1
Weak form

In order to derive the weak form of the problem given by equations (
3
), (
4
) and (
8
), we first introduce some notation that will be used hereinafter. Let us denote by
L^{r}(\Omega)
,
1\leq r<\infty
, the spaces of functions such that their
r
-th power is absolutely integrable in
\Omega
. For the case in which
r=2
, we have a Hilbert space with inner product

(u,v)_{\Omega}\eqdef\int_{\Omega}u(\mathbf{x})\,v(\mathbf{x})d\Omega

(14)

and induced norm
\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\equiv\|u\|_{\Omega}\eqdef(u,u)_{\Omega}^{1/2}
. Abusing of the notation, the same symbol as in (
14
) will be used for the integral of the product of two functions, even if these are not in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, and both for scalar and vector fields. The space of functions whose distributional derivatives up to order
m
are in
L^{2}(\Omega)
are denoted by
H^{m}(\Omega)
. We will focus on the case of
m=1
, which is also a Hilbert space. Given a Banach space
X
,
L^{r}(0,T;X)
is the space of time dependent functions such that their
X
-norm is in
L^{r}(0,T)
, being
[0,T]
the time interval where such functions are defined. In addition, we will also use the inner product on a given boundary,
\Gamma\subset\Omega
, defined as

(u,v)_{\Gamma}\eqdef\int_{\Gamma}u(\mathbf{x})\,v(\mathbf{x})d\Gamma,

(15)

with the associated norm
\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\equiv\|u\|_{\Gamma}\eqdef(u,u)_{\Gamma}^{1/2}
.

Let
\mathcal{V}\eqdef L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))
be a functional space,
\mathcal{V}_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}}}
the trace space of
\mathcal{V}
on the free surface
\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}}
, i.e.
\mathcal{V}_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}}}\eqdef\{v|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}}}:v\in\mathcal{V}\}
, and
\mathcal{V}_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}}}
the trace space of
\mathcal{V}
on the structure
\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}}
. The weak form of the problem reads: find
[\phi,\kappa,\eta]\in\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}_{\Gamma}
such that

B([\phi,\kappa,\eta],[w,v,u])=L([w,v,u])\quad\forall[w,v,u]\in\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}}}\times\mathcal{V}_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}}}.

(16)

Where the bilinear form, assuming that the structure is continuous and satisfies the boundary conditions (
13a
)-(
13b
), is given by

\displaystyle B([\phi,\kappa,\eta],[w,v,u])\eqdef

\displaystyle(\nabla\phi,\nabla w)_{\Omega}-(\kappa_{t},w)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}}}+\beta\left(\phi_{t}+g\kappa,\alpha_{f}w+v\right)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}}}

(17)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle(\eta_{t},w)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}}}+\left(d_{0}\eta_{tt}+\phi_{t}+g\eta,u\right)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}}}+\left(\mathbf{C}_{\rho}:\nabla^{2}\eta,\nabla^{2}u\right)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}}},

with
\alpha_{f}
and
\beta
scaling parameters introduced for stability and dimensional consistency purposes, as discussed in Section
4
, defined in equations (
58
). Without loss of generality, we assume that there is no external loading acting on the structure, leading to

L([w,v])\eqdef(\text{u}_{\scriptsize\mbox{in}},w)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{in}}}+(\text{u}_{\scriptsize\mbox{out}},w)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{out}}}.

(18)

For the sake of completeness, we proceed with the description of the steps followed to reach the bilinear form (
17
). The first row is obtained by multiplying equation (
2
) against the test function,
w
, integrating over the domain
\Omega
, integrating by parts and replacing the normal velocity at the boundaries by the respective kinematic boundary condition as stated in (
4
).

Following the monolithic approach described in
[
1
]
, the last term in the first row of (
17
) incorporates the dynamic boundary condition on the free surface, equation (
8a
) and (
12a
). Here we multiply such condition against a modified test function,
\alpha_{f}w+v
, integrate over the free surface boundary
\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}}
and weight this contribution by a parameter,
\beta
. The term
\alpha_{f}w
is added to the test function
v
to guarantee coercivity of the system, see Section
4.2
. The main difference with respect to
[
1
]
is that in the cited work the authors select
\beta=\frac{1}{2}
as a fixed parameter, while here we analyse the relation between
\alpha_{f}
and
\beta
that results in a stable formulation.

Similarly, the second and third terms of the second row of (
17
) enforces the dynamic boundary condition on the beam surface, equation (
8b
) and (
12b
). In this case, however, the fourth order term is integrated by parts twice. In this process, we assume that the functions belong to
H^{2}(\Omega)
, so that the rotations are continuous across the structure. In addition, the lower-dimensional integrals that appear in the integration by parts cancel when we enforce the boundary conditions (
13a
)-(
13b
),
i.e.

\displaystyle\left((\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{C}_{\rho}:\nabla^{2}\eta))\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda},u\right)_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs,str}}}=0,

\displaystyle\left((\mathbf{C}_{\rho}:\nabla^{2}\eta)\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda},\nabla u\right)_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs,str}}}=0.

Remark 1

(Structure with joints)
.

The weak problem given in (
16
) assumes the variational space
\mathcal{V}_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}}}
belongs to the trace space of
H^{2}(\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}})
. However, when we have structural joints, the rotations (deflection gradients) are not continuous at the joint location. Thus, the space of functions
\eta
can be defined as the space of trace functions of
H^{2}(\Omega)
on
\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}}\setminus\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{j}}
, with continuous surface elevation, but discontinuous gradients on
\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{j}}
. With this definition of the space
\mathcal{V}_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}}}
, integrating by parts the fourth order term appearing in (
12b
), we have that

\displaystyle\left(\nabla^{2}:(\mathbf{C}_{\rho}:\nabla^{2}\eta),u\right)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}}}=

\displaystyle-\left(\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{C}_{\rho}:\nabla^{2}\eta),\nabla u\right)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}}}+\left((\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{C}_{\rho}:\nabla^{2}\eta))\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda},u\right)_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}}}

(19)

\displaystyle+\left([\![(\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{C}_{\rho}:\nabla^{2}\eta))\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!],\langle u\rangle\right)_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{j}}}+\left(\langle\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{C}_{\rho}:\nabla^{2}\eta)\rangle,[\![u\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!]\right)_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{j}}}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:zero_shear_plate},\eqref{eq:zero_shear_jump_plate},[\![u\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{j}}}]\!]=0}{=}

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{C}_{\rho}:\nabla^{2}\eta,\nabla^{2}u\right)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}}}-\left(\left(\mathbf{C}_{\rho}:\nabla^{2}\eta\right)\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda},\nabla u\right)_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}}}

\displaystyle-\left([\![\left(\mathbf{C}_{\rho}:\nabla^{2}\eta\right)\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!],\langle\nabla u\rangle\right)_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{j}}}-\left(\langle\mathbf{C}_{\rho}:\nabla^{2}\eta\rangle,[\![\nabla u\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!]\right)_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{j}}}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:zero_moment_plate},\eqref{eq:zero_moment_jump_plate},\eqref{eq:moment_mean_plate}}{=}

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{C}_{\rho}:\nabla^{2}\eta,\nabla^{2}u\right)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}}}+\left(k_{\rho}[\![\nabla\eta\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!],[\![\nabla u\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!]\right)_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{j}}}.

With
k_{\rho}\eqdef\frac{k_{\varphi}}{\rho_{w}}
. Then, the bilinear form equivalent to (
17
) will read

\displaystyle B_{\scriptsize\mbox{j}}([\phi,\kappa,\eta],[w,v,u])\eqdef B([\phi,\kappa,\eta],[w,v,u])+\left(k_{\rho}[\![\nabla\eta\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!],[\![\nabla u\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!]\right)_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{j}}}.

(20)

Since (
17
) is a particular case of (
20
) for
\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{j}}=\emptyset
, hereinafter we will use the later bilinear form.

3.2
Spatial discretization

Let us consider a FE partition
\Omega_{h}
of the domain
\Omega
from which we can construct conforming finite dimensional spaces for the potential
\mathcal{V}_{h}\subset\mathcal{V}
, for the surface elevation at the free surface
\mathcal{V}_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}},h}\subset\mathcal{V}_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}}}
and for the surface elevation at the structure
\mathcal{V}_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}},h}\subset\mathcal{V}_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}}}
. We denote by
\mathcal{E}_{h}
the set of facets (entities of one dimension lower than the dimension of
\Omega
) generated by the FE partition
\Omega_{h}
. We define as
\Gamma_{h}
the set of facets of
\mathcal{E}_{h}
that lie on the boundary of
\Omega
,
\partial\Omega
, i.e.
\Gamma_{h}\eqdef\mathcal{E}_{h}\cap\partial\Omega
. Following this notation, we also define the discrete boundary parts
\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{b},h}\eqdef\mathcal{E}_{h}\cap\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{b}}
,
\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{in},h}\eqdef\mathcal{E}_{h}\cap\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{in}}
,
\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{out},h}\eqdef\mathcal{E}_{h}\cap\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{out}}
,
\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs},h}\eqdef\mathcal{E}_{h}\cap\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}}
and
\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}\eqdef\mathcal{E}_{h}\cap\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}}
. In addition, we denote by
\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}
the set of edges or points between facets of
\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}
that are not joints and do not belong to the boundary of
\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}}
, see Figure
2
. We assume that in the case that the structure has one or multiple joints, these will lie in an edge or point between the FE partition facets
\mathcal{E}_{h}
.

Figure 2:
Sketch with the definition of the discrete geometrical entities.

Using this notation, the Galerkin FE formulation equivalent to (
16
) reads: find
[\phi_{h},\kappa_{h},\eta_{h}]\in\mathcal{V}_{h}\times\mathcal{V}_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}},h}\times\mathcal{V}_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}},h}
such that

B_{h}([\phi_{h},\kappa_{h},\eta_{h}],[w_{h},v_{h},u_{h}])=L_{h}([w_{h},v_{h},u_{h}])\quad\forall[w_{h},v_{h},u_{h}]\in\mathcal{V}_{h}\times\mathcal{V}_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}},h}\times\mathcal{V}_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}},h},

(21)

where the bilinear form is given by

\displaystyle B_{h}([\phi_{h},\kappa_{h},\eta_{h}],[w_{h},v_{h},u_{h}])\eqdef

\displaystyle(\nabla\phi_{h},\nabla w_{h})_{\Omega_{h}}-(\kappa_{h,t},w_{h})_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs},h}}+\beta\left(\phi_{h,t}+g\kappa_{h},\alpha_{f}w_{h}+v_{h}\right)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs},h}}

(22)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle(\eta_{h,t},w_{h})_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}+\left(d_{0}\eta_{h,tt}+\phi_{h,t}+g\eta_{h},u_{h}\right)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{C}_{\rho}:\nabla^{2}\eta_{h},\nabla^{2}u_{h}\right)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}+\left(k_{\rho}[\![\nabla\eta_{h}\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!],[\![\nabla u_{h}\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!]\right)_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{j}}},

and the linear right hand side is

L_{h}([w_{h},v_{h}])\eqdef(\text{u}_{\scriptsize\mbox{in}},w_{h})_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{in},h}}+(\text{u}_{\scriptsize\mbox{out}},w_{h})_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{out},h}}.

(23)

Note that the formulation defined by (
21
)-(
23
) assumes that the FE spaces are defined in
H^{2}(\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}}\setminus\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{j}})
, which requires
\mathcal{C}^{1}
continuity across elements in the structure, i.e continuous gradients between elements. This condition is satisfied by certain FE types such as the Morley
[
36
]
, Argyris
[
4
]
or NURBS-based FE
[
13
]
. Nonetheless, we propose an alternative formulation that can be generalized to
H^{1}(\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}})
FE defined, for instance, by Lagrange polynomials. This formulation is based on a Continuous/Discontinuous Galerkin (C/DG) approach for fourth order operators,
[
18
]
, where the discrete functions are continuous at the element nodes, but the gradient is discontinuous. The continuity of first order derivatives is weakly enforced via an interior penalty approach.

The resulting C/DG formulation reads: find
[\phi_{h},\kappa_{h},\eta_{h}]\in\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{h}\times\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}},h}\times\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}},h}
such that

\hat{B}_{h}([\phi_{h},\kappa_{h},\eta_{h}],[w_{h},v_{h}])=L_{h}([w_{h},v_{h},u_{h}])\quad\forall[w_{h},v_{h},u_{h}]\in\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{h}\times\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}},h}\times\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}},h},

(24)

where

\displaystyle\hat{B}_{h}([\phi_{h},\kappa_{h},\eta_{h}],[w_{h},v_{h},u_{h}])\eqdef

\displaystyle B_{h}([\phi_{h},\kappa_{h},\eta_{h}],[w_{h},v_{h},u_{h}])

(25)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\left(\langle\mathbf{C}_{\rho}:\nabla^{2}\eta_{h}\rangle,[\![\nabla u_{h}\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!]\right)_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}-\left([\![\nabla\eta_{h}\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!],\langle\mathbf{C}_{\rho}:\nabla^{2}u_{h}\rangle\right)_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma\hat{D}_{\rho}}{h}\left([\![\nabla\eta_{h}\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!],[\![\nabla u_{h}\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!]\right)_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}},

With
h
the characteristic element size,
\hat{D}_{\rho}\eqdef\frac{EI}{(1-\nu^{2})}
a constant that depends on the material properties and
\gamma
a constant that can be tuned to guaranty stability, see Section
4
. The first term of the second row in (
25
) is required for consistence. It originates from the integration by parts of the fourth order term in the plate equation after assuming continuity of test functions across boundary elements and weakly enforcing

\displaystyle[\![(\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{C}_{\rho}:\nabla^{2}\eta_{h}))\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!]=0

\displaystyle\qquad\mbox{on $\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}$},

(26a)

\displaystyle[\![(\mathbf{C}_{\rho}:\nabla^{2}\eta_{h})\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!]=0

\displaystyle\qquad\mbox{on $\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}$},

(26b)

i.e.
continuity of shear forces and moments across structure elements.
The second term in the second row enforces dual consistency, and improves overall accuracy of the method.
It penalizes the jump of surface elevation gradients across structural elements.
The term appearing in the last row of (
25
) is added for stability purposes, while also penalizing the jump of gradients across the structural elements.

Hereinafter we will work with the formulation defined by equations (
24
)-(
25
). The variational spaces
\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{h}
,
\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}},h}
and
\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}},h}
will be given by

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{h}

\displaystyle\eqdef\left\{w_{h}\in\mathcal{C}^{0}(\Omega):\ w_{h}|_{K}\in\mathbb{P}_{r}(K),\forall K\in\Omega_{h}\right\},

(27)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}},h}

\displaystyle\eqdef\left\{w_{h}|_{E}:\ w_{h}\in\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{h},\forall E\in\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}}\right\},

(28)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}},h}

\displaystyle\eqdef\left\{w_{h}|_{E}:\ w_{h}\in\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{h},\forall E\in\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}\right\},

(29)

where
\mathbb{P}_{r}(K)
is the space of Lagrange polynomials of degree
r\geq 2
in an element
K
.

3.3
Time discretization

The forms given in (
21
) or (
24
) describe a semi-discrete system of equations, i.e. discrete in space and continuous in time, resulting in a system of 2nd order ordinary differential equations (ODEs). In this work we consider two approaches to find the solution to the transient problem: a frequency domain and a time domain approach.

3.3.1
Frequency domain

The analysis of free surface flows in the Airy wave theory, described by linear potential flow, is suitable for a frequency domain formulation. This is also the case of dynamic analysis of linear structural response. When defining the formulation in the frequency domain, the response,
i.e
surface elevation and velocity potential, is assumed to be harmonic. That is, a space and time dependent variable,
\xi(\mathbf{x},t)
, can be given in terms of a prescribed frequency,
\omega
, and a time-independent variable,
\bar{\xi}(\mathbf{x})
as

\xi(\mathbf{x},t)=\bar{\xi}(\mathbf{x})\exp(-i\omega t).

(30)

Under this assumption, given an incoming wave frequency,
\omega
, the fully discrete problem reduces to find the set of time-independent and complex-valued fields, in our case
\bar{\phi}(\mathbf{x})
,
\bar{\kappa}(\mathbf{x})
and
\bar{\eta}(\mathbf{x})
. To simplify notation, in this subsection we will neglect the bar and hereinafter assume that
\eta\eqdef\bar{\eta}
,
\kappa\eqdef\bar{\kappa}
and
\phi\eqdef\bar{\phi}
. Hence, the final discrete problem in the frequency domain will read: find
[\phi_{h},\kappa_{h},\eta_{h}]\in\hat{\mathcal{V}}^{\omega}_{h}\times\hat{\mathcal{V}}^{\omega}_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}},h}\times\hat{\mathcal{V}}^{\omega}_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}},h}
such that

\hat{B}^{\omega}_{h}([\phi_{h},\kappa_{h},\eta_{h}],[w_{h},v_{h},u_{h}])=L^{\omega}_{h}([w_{h},v_{h},u_{h}])\quad\forall[w_{h},v_{h},u_{h}]\in\hat{\mathcal{V}}^{\omega}_{h}\times\hat{\mathcal{V}}^{\omega}_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}},h}\times\hat{\mathcal{V}}^{\omega}_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}},h},

(31)

with

\displaystyle\hat{B}^{\omega}_{h}([\phi_{h},\kappa_{h},\eta_{h}],[w_{h},v_{h},u_{h}])\eqdef

\displaystyle(\nabla\phi_{h},\nabla w_{h})_{\Omega_{h}}+(i\omega\kappa_{h},w_{h})_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs},h}}+\beta\left(g\kappa_{h}-i\omega\phi_{h},\alpha^{\omega}_{f}w_{h}+v_{h}\right)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs},h}}

(32)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle(i\omega\eta_{h},w_{h})_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}+\left((g-\omega^{2}d_{0})\eta_{h}-i\omega\phi_{h},u_{h}\right)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{C}_{\rho}:\nabla^{2}\eta_{h},\nabla^{2}u_{h}\right)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}+\left(k_{\rho}[\![\nabla\eta_{h}\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!],[\![\nabla u_{h}\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!]\right)_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{j}}}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\left(\langle\mathbf{C}_{\rho}:\nabla^{2}\eta_{h}\rangle,[\![\nabla u_{h}\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!]\right)_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}-\left([\![(\nabla\eta_{h}\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!],\langle\mathbf{C}_{\rho}:\nabla^{2}u_{h}\rangle\right)_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma\hat{D}_{\rho}}{h}\left([\![\nabla\eta_{h}\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!],[\![\nabla u_{h}\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!]\right)_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}},

and

L^{\omega}_{h}([w_{h},v_{h},u_{h}])\eqdef L_{h}([w_{h},v_{h},u_{h}]).

(33)

Here, the stabilization parameter
\alpha^{\omega}_{f}
appearing in (
32
) is defined as
\alpha^{\omega}_{f}\eqdef\frac{-i\omega}{g}\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}
, see Section
4
for further details on the justification of this definition.

It is important to highlight that the FE spaces
\hat{\mathcal{V}}^{\omega}_{h}
and
\hat{\mathcal{V}}^{\omega}_{\Gamma,h}
are finite-dimensional spaces of complex-valued functions, composed by real-valued shape functions and complex-valued degrees of freedom.

3.3.2
Time domain

As an alternative to the frequency domain approach, instead of assuming an harmonic response, one can discretize in time the semi-discrete system given by equation (
24
). Here, since we have a second-order ODE, we use the so called Newmark-beta time discretization scheme
[
38
]
. Let us consider a uniform discretization of the time domain, with a constant time step size
\Delta t
. At a given time step
n+1
, with
t^{n+1}=t^{n}+\Delta t
, an unknown,
x^{n+1}
, and its first time derivative, are defined by

\displaystyle x_{t}^{n+1}

\displaystyle\eqdef x_{t}^{n}+\Delta t\left[(1-\gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}})x_{tt}^{n}+\gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}x_{tt}^{n+1}\right],

(34)

\displaystyle x^{n+1}

\displaystyle\eqdef x^{n}+\Delta tx_{t}^{n}+\Delta t^{2}\left[\left(\frac{1}{2}-\beta_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}\right)x_{tt}^{n}+\beta_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}x_{tt}^{n+1}\right],

(35)

where
\gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}
and
\beta_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}
are two coefficients that determine the stability and accuracy of the scheme
[
21
]
. In this work we will use the pair
\gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}=0.5
and
\beta_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}=0.25
, which results in a second order accurate and unconditionally stable scheme.

Doing some manipulations, we can obtain an expression for the first and second time derivatives at
n+1
.
These derivatives depend on the unknown solution
x^{n+1}
, and the known solution and derivatives,
\left\{x^{n},x_{t}^{n},x_{tt}^{n}\right\}
,

\displaystyle x_{t}^{n+1}

\displaystyle=\delta_{t}\left(x^{n+1}-x^{n}\right)+\frac{1-\gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}}{\beta_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}}x_{t}^{n}+\Delta t\frac{1-\gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}}{2\beta_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}}x_{tt}^{n},

(36)

\displaystyle x_{tt}^{n+1}

\displaystyle=\delta_{tt}\left(x^{n+1}-x^{n}\right)-\frac{1}{\beta_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}\Delta t}x_{t}^{n}+\frac{1-2\beta_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}}{2\beta_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}}x_{tt}^{n}

(37)

where
\delta_{t}=\frac{\gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}}{\beta_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}\Delta t}
and
\delta_{tt}=\frac{1}{\beta_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}\Delta t^{2}}
.

Using the time discretization given by equations (
36
)-(
37
) for the potential and surface elevation time derivatives, we obtain the following fully discrete problem in the time domain: find
[\phi_{h}^{n+1},\kappa_{h}^{n+1},\eta_{h}^{n+1}]\in\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{h}\times\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}},h}\times\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}},h}
such that

\hat{B}_{h}^{n+1}([\phi_{h}^{n+1},\kappa_{h}^{n+1},\eta_{h}^{n+1}],[w_{h},v_{h},u_{h}])=L_{h}^{n+1}([w_{h},v_{h},u_{h}])\quad\forall[w_{h},v_{h},u_{h}]\in\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{h}\times\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}},h}\times\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}},h},

(38)

where

\displaystyle\hat{B}_{h}^{n+1}([\phi_{h}^{n+1},\kappa_{h}^{n+1}\eta_{h}^{n+1}],[w_{h},v_{h},u_{h}])\eqdef

\displaystyle(\nabla\phi_{h}^{n+1},\nabla w_{h})_{\Omega_{h}}-(\delta_{t}\kappa_{h}^{n+1},w_{h})_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs},h}}

(39)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\beta\left(\delta_{t}\phi_{h}^{n+1}+g\kappa_{h}^{n+1},\alpha_{f}w_{h}+v_{h}\right)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs},h}}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle(\delta_{t}\eta_{h}^{n+1},w_{h})_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}+\left(\delta_{tt}d_{0}\eta_{h}^{n+1}+\delta_{t}\phi_{h}^{n+1}+g\eta_{h}^{n+1},u_{h}\right)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{C}_{\rho}:\nabla^{2}\eta_{h}^{n+1},\nabla^{2}u_{h}\right)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}+\left(k_{\rho}[\![\nabla\eta_{h}^{n+1}\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!],[\![\nabla u_{h}\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!]\right)_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{j}}}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\left(\langle\mathbf{C}_{\rho}:\nabla^{2}\eta_{h}^{n+1}\rangle,[\![\nabla u_{h}\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!]\right)_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\left([\![\nabla\eta_{h}^{n+1}\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}]\!],\langle\mathbf{C}_{\rho}:\nabla^{2}u_{h}\rangle\right)_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma\hat{D}_{\rho}}{h}\left([\![\nabla\eta_{h}^{n+1}\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!],[\![\nabla u_{h}\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!]\right)_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}},

and

\displaystyle L_{h}^{n+1}([w_{h},v_{h},u_{h}])\eqdef

\displaystyle(\text{u}_{\scriptsize\mbox{in}},w_{h})_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{in},h}}+(\text{u}_{\scriptsize\mbox{out}},w_{h})_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{out},h}}

(40)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\left(\delta_{t}\kappa_{h}^{n}-\frac{1-\gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}}{\beta_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}}\kappa_{h,t}^{n}-\Delta t\frac{1-\gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}}{2\beta_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}}\kappa_{h,tt}^{n},w_{h}\right)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs},h}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\beta\left(\delta_{t}\phi_{h}^{n}-\frac{1-\gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}}{\beta_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}}\phi_{h,t}^{n}-\Delta t\frac{1-\gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}}{2\beta_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}}\phi_{h,tt}^{n},\alpha_{f}w_{h}+v_{h}\right)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs},h}}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\left(\delta_{t}\eta_{h}^{n}-\frac{1-\gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}}{\beta_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}}\eta_{h,t}^{n}-\Delta t\frac{1-\gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}}{2\beta_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}}\eta_{h,tt}^{n},w_{h}\right)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left(d_{0}\left(\delta_{tt}\eta_{h}^{n}+\frac{1}{\beta_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}\Delta t}\eta_{h,t}^{n}-\frac{1-2\beta_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}}{2\beta_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}}\eta_{h,tt}^{n}\right),u_{h}\right)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left(\delta_{t}\phi_{h}^{n}-\frac{1-\gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}}{\beta_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}}\phi_{h,t}^{n}-\Delta t\frac{1-\gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}}{2\beta_{\scriptsize\mbox{NB}}}\phi_{h,tt}^{n},u_{h}\right)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}.

In equation (
39
), the stabilization parameter is defined as
\alpha_{f}\eqdef\frac{\delta_{t}}{g}\frac{(1-\beta)}{\beta}
for the system to be stable, see Section
4
.

4
Numerical analysis

In this section we prove statements of consistency, coercivity, boundedness and energy conservation for the discrete formulation proposed in this work. In Section
4.2
we will first demonstrate these properties for the formulation without discontinuities, i.e. equation (
21
). After, in Section
4.3
, we will extend the analysis to include the C/DG formulation (
24
).

4.1
Preliminary definitions and theorems

Let us establish some definitions and theorems that will be later used in the numerical analysis of the formulation.

Definition 1
.

For any
w
we define the
H^{1}
-norm in
\Omega
as

\left\|w\right\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}=\left\|w\right\|^{2}_{\Omega}+\left\|\nabla w\right\|^{2}_{\Omega}.

(41)

Corollary 1
.

For
w\in H^{1}(\Omega)
we can bound the gradient. That is,

\|\nabla w\|_{\Omega_{h}}\leq\|w\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{h})},\qquad\forall w\in H^{1}(\Omega).

(42)

Proof.

The statement is a direct consequence of the definition of the
H_{1}{\Omega}
-norm.
∎

Definition 2
.

For any
w\in H^{1}(\Omega)
define by extension the trace operator
\gamma_{\partial\Omega}:H^{1}(\Omega)\rightarrow L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
such that

\gamma_{\partial\Omega}w=w|_{\partial\Omega},\forall w\in C^{\infty}(\Omega).

(43)

Theorem 1

(Trace theorem of Sobolev spaces)
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded simply connected Lipschitz domain. Then, the trace operator
\gamma_{\partial\Omega}
is a bounded linear operator from
H^{1}(\Omega)
to
L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
. That is,

\left\|\gamma_{\partial\Omega}w\right\|_{\partial\Omega}\leq C_{\partial\Omega}\left\|w\right\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}.

(44)

With
C_{\partial\Omega}
a constant that only depends on
\partial\Omega
.

See
[
17
]
for a proof of Theorem
1
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded connected Lipschitz domain and
f
be a linear form from
H^{1}(\Omega)

with a non-zero restriction on non-zero constant functions.
Then, there is a constant
C_{\Omega}>0
such that

C_{\Omega}\|w\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\|\nabla w\|_{\Omega}+|f(w)|,\qquad\forall w\in H^{1}(\Omega).

(45)

Let us define the function
f
appearing in Theorem
5
as

f(w)\eqdef\alpha_{f}\beta\delta_{t}\|\gamma_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}}}w\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}}},

with
\gamma_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}}}
the trace operator as defined in equation (
92
). Note that
f
is a linear form on
H^{1}(\Omega)
and its restriction on non-zero constant functions is non-zero, which holds for any
\alpha_{f},\beta,\delta_{t}>0
and any open boundary portion
\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}}\subseteq\partial\Omega
with non-zero measure,
i.e.

|\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}}|\eqdef\mbox{meas}(\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}})>0
. Then, we have that

|f(w)|=\alpha_{f}\beta\delta_{t}\|\gamma_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}}}w\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}}}=\alpha_{f}\beta\delta_{t}\|w\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}}},\qquad\forall w\in H^{1}(\Omega).

(46)

Corollary 2
.

For
w\in H^{1}(\Omega)
we can bound the
H^{1}(\Omega)
-norm as follows,

C_{\Omega}\|w\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\|\nabla w\|_{\Omega}+\alpha_{f}\beta\delta_{t}\|w\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}}},\qquad\forall w\in H^{1}(\Omega).

(47)

Proof.

Introducing (
46
) into Theorem
5
proofs the statement.
∎

Theorem 3
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded simply connected Lipschitz domain and
\Omega_{e}
an element of the FE triangulation of
\Omega
with characteristic element size

See

The consistency statement results from integrating by parts on each element the terms

\left(\mathbf{C}_{\rho}:\nabla^{2}\eta,\nabla^{2}u_{h}\right)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}

Proposition 2

(Energy conservation)

The semi-discrete problem (
21
) is energy conserving for any

With

(53)

Proof.

, the following statement holds

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle B_{h}([\phi_{h},\kappa_{h},\eta_{h}],[\phi_{h,t},\frac{1}{\beta}\kappa_{h,t}-\alpha_{f}\phi_{h,t},\eta_{h,t}])-L_{h}([\phi_{h,t},\frac{1}{\beta}\kappa_{h,t}-\alpha_{f}\phi_{h,t},\eta_{h,t}])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\nabla\phi_{h},\nabla\phi_{h,t})_{\Omega_{h}}-(\kappa_{h,t},\phi_{h,t})_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs},h}}+\beta\left(\phi_{h,t}+g\kappa_{h},\alpha_{f}\phi_{h,t}+\frac{1}{\beta}\kappa_{h,t}-\alpha_{f}\phi_{h,t}\right)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs},h}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left\|\nabla\phi_{h}\right\|^{2}_{\Omega_{h}}+\frac{g}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left\|\kappa_{h}\right\|^{2}_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs},h}}+\frac{d_{0}}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left\|\eta_{h,t}\right\|^{2}_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}+\frac{g}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left\|\eta_{h}\right\|^{2}_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}

∎

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle+

Proposition 3

(Coercivity)

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|[w_{h},v_{h},u_{h}]\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}\eqdef

\displaystyle B_{h}([w_{h},v_{h},u_{h}],[w_{h},v_{h},u_{h}])\eqdef

Proposition 4

(Boundedness)
.

If
\alpha_{f}
is given by (

. That is, there exists a constant

(63)

with
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\cdot\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}
defined in eq (
61

Proof.

Let us define the minimum draft

as the minimum value of the draft for any point in the structure, i.e.
d_{0}^{\min}\eqdef\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}}}d_{0}(\mathbf{x})
. Starting from the bilinear form defined in equation (
59
) and using Schwarz inequality, we have that

\displaystyle B_{h}([\phi_{h},\kappa_{h},\eta_{h}],[w_{h},v_{h},u_{h}])\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\phi_{h}\right\|_{\Omega_{h}}\left\|\nabla w_{h}\right\|_{\Omega_{h}}+\delta_{t}\left(\frac{\beta}{g}\right)^{1/2}\left\|(\beta g)^{1/2}\kappa_{h}\right\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs},h}}\left\|w_{h}\right\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs},h}}

(64)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle(1-\beta)\delta_{t}^{2}g^{-1}\left\|\phi_{h}\right\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs},h}}\left\|w_{h}\right\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs},h}}+\delta_{t}\left(\frac{\beta}{g}\right)^{1/2}\left\|\phi_{h}\right\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs},h}}\left\|(\beta g)^{1/2}v_{h}\right\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs},h}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left\|(\beta g)^{1/2}\kappa_{h}\right\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs},h}}\left\|(\beta g)^{1/2}v_{h}\right\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs},h}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\delta_{t}(\delta_{tt}d_{0}^{\min}+g)^{-1/2}\left\|(\delta_{tt}d_{0}+g)^{1/2}\eta_{h}\right\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}\left\|w_{h}\right\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left\|(\delta_{tt}d_{0}+g)^{1/2}\eta_{h}\right\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}\left\|(\delta_{tt}d_{0}+g)^{1/2}u_{h}\right\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\delta_{t}(\delta_{tt}d_{0}^{\min}+g)^{-1/2}\left\|\phi_{h}\right\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}\left\|(\delta_{tt}d_{0}+g)^{1/2}u_{h}\right\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{C}_{\rho}^{1/2}:\nabla^{2}\eta_{h}\right\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}\left\|\mathbf{C}_{\rho}^{1/2}:\nabla^{2}u_{h}\right\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left\|k_{\rho}^{1/2}[\![\nabla\eta_{h}\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!]\right\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}\left\|k_{\rho}^{1/2}[\![\nabla u_{h}\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!]\right\|_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{j}}}.

Using equations (
42
) and (
44
), and noting that
\|w\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}}}\leq\|w\|_{\partial\Omega}
and
\|w\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}}}\leq\|w\|_{\partial\Omega}
, we find that

\displaystyle B_{h}([\phi_{h},\kappa_{h},\eta_{h}],[w_{h},v_{h},u_{h}])\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\phi_{h}\right\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{h})}\left\|w_{h}\right\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{h})}+C_{\partial\Omega}\delta_{t}\left(\frac{\beta}{g}\right)^{1/2}\left\|(\beta g)^{1/2}\kappa_{h}\right\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs},h}}\left\|w_{h}\right\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{h})}

(65)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle C_{\partial\Omega}^{2}(1-\beta)\delta_{t}^{2}g^{-1}\left\|\phi_{h}\right\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{h})}\left\|w_{h}\right\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{h})}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle C_{\partial\Omega}\delta_{t}\left(\frac{\beta}{g}\right)^{1/2}\left\|\phi_{h}\right\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{h})}\left\|(\beta g)^{1/2}v_{h}\right\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs},h}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left\|(\beta g)^{1/2}\kappa_{h}\right\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs},h}}\left\|(\beta g)^{1/2}v_{h}\right\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs},h}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle C_{\partial\Omega}\delta_{t}(\delta_{tt}d_{0}^{\min}+g)^{-1/2}\left\|(\delta_{tt}d_{0}+g)^{1/2}\eta_{h}\right\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}\left\|w_{h}\right\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{h})}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left\|(\delta_{tt}d_{0}+g)^{1/2}\eta_{h}\right\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}\left\|(\delta_{tt}d_{0}+g)^{1/2}u_{h}\right\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle C_{\partial\Omega}\delta_{t}(\delta_{tt}d_{0}^{\min}+g)^{-1/2}\left\|\phi_{h}\right\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{h})}\left\|(\delta_{tt}d_{0}+g)^{1/2}u_{h}\right\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{C}_{\rho}^{1/2}:\nabla^{2}\eta_{h}\right\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}\left\|\mathbf{C}_{\rho}^{1/2}:\nabla^{2}u_{h}\right\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left\|k_{\rho}^{1/2}[\![\nabla\eta_{h}\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!]\right\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}\left\|k_{\rho}^{1/2}[\![\nabla u_{h}\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!]\right\|_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{j}}}.

Which can be written in matrix vector form as follows

\displaystyle B_{h}([\phi_{h},\kappa_{h},\eta_{h}],[w_{h},v_{h},u_{h}])\leq\left(\begin{matrix}\|\phi_{h}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{h})}\\
\left\|\left(\beta g\right)^{1/2}\kappa_{h}\right\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs},h}}\\
\left\|\left(\delta_{tt}d_{0}+g\right)^{1/2}\eta_{h}\right\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}\\

\left\|\left(\beta g\right)^{1/2}v_{h}\right\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs},h}}\\
\left\|\left(\delta_{tt}d_{0}+g\right)^{1/2}u_{h}\right\|_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}\\
\left\|\mathbf{C}^{1/2}_{\rho}:\nabla^{2}u_{h}\right\|_{\tilde{\Gamma}_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}\\

(66)

with

\displaystyle C_{b}=\max_{i}\left(\sum_{j}|\mathbf{A}_{ij}|\right),

(68)

we arrive at the bound (

).
∎

]

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|[w_{h},v_{h},u_{h}]\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}_{\scriptsize\mbox{CDG}}\eqdef

5
Numerical results

In this section we assess the behavior of the proposed formulation for a variety of two and three-dimensional tests, analysing the accuracy, convergence and conservation properties, as well as comparing with analytical and experimental solutions that can be found in existing literature.

We start with a problem with analytical solution for the 2-dimensional case, that is the evolution of a floating infinite beam subject to an initial harmonic condition. After, we assess the performance of the method for finite beams with elastic joints in the frequency and time domains, followed by the study of a finite floating beam over irregular sea bed. Finally, we assess the behavior of a floating plate in a 3-dimensional domain and we show that the proposed approach is suitable to solve problems with structures with arbitrary shape.

5.1
Implementation remarks

The monolithic FE formulation and other algorithms used in the experiments below have been implemented using the Julia programming language
[
7
]
version 1.7 and the Gridap finite element library
[
6
]
version 0.17. Gridap is a free and open-source finite element library fully implemented in Julia. One of its main distinctive features is its user interface, which has a high-level syntax that resembles the notation used to define weak forms mathematically. Internally, Gridap leverages the Julia JIT compiler to generate an efficient finite assembly loop from the user input automatically
[
48
]
, which results in efficient and easy to write user code. The formulations presented in this paper can be easily implemented using the high-level interface of Gridap in a convenient way. See, e.g., Figure
3
that contains the implementation of the numerical example in Section
5.2
. Note that, even though the proposed monolithic formulation is rather complex, its implementation in Gridap can be done in few lines of code. In particular, the definition of the weak form is very compact and has a clear connection with the corresponding mathematical notation. We have taken advantage of the Gridap support for multi-field PDEs and the possibility to combine interpolation spaces defined on geometries with different spatial dimensions. A crucial feature for the implementation of the monolithic formulation is the capacity of Gridap to integrate weak forms on domains different from the ones used to define the interpolation spaces. In particular, this makes possible to integrate the jump terms on
\Lambda
. This computation is particularly challenging from an implementation point of view since it involves geometries with three different spatial dimensions. E.g, in 2D,
\Lambda
is a 0-dimensional domain, the elevation
\eta
is defined on the 1-dimensional domain
\Gamma_{\mathrm{str}}
, and the code that implements the interpolation space for
\eta
is aware that
\Gamma_{\mathrm{str}}
is on the boundary of the 2-dimensional domain
\Omega
. This last implementation ingredient is used internally to compute the terms that involve operations between the elevation
\eta
and the potential
\phi
since the latter is defined on
\Omega
. To our best knowledge, other general-purpose finite element libraries are not able to handle this particular case, at least via such a compact high-level user interface. E.g., at the time of writing FEniCS is able to integrate weak forms on geometries with
d
and
d-1
space dimensions at most
[
15
]
and, thus, cannot be used to implement the formulation of this paper easily, which requires geometries with
d
,
d-1
, and also
d-2
space dimensions. The numerical results below have been computed on a laptop with an Intel(R) Core(TM) i7-8665U CPU at 1.90GHz with approximately 16GiB of RAM and should be reproducible on a machine with similar characteristics. The software used to generate the results presented in this section is available at the registered Julia package
MonolithicFEMVLFS.jl

6
Conclusions

In this manuscript we present a novel monolithic FE formulation for the hydroelastic analysis of thin floating structures that can be described by the Euler-Bernoulli beam theory, for the 2-dimensional case, or by the Poisson-Kirchhoff plate theory. We define the new formulation for both,
C^{1}
and
C^{0}
FE spaces. The later uses a continuous/discontinuous Galerkin approach for the displacements and rotations, respectively, resulting in consistent, stable and energy-conserving formulations. We show a practical implementation of the monolithic formulation in the pure Julia library Gridap.jl. We have shown that the statements proven in the numerical analysis section are supported by the numerical results. We also see that the method proposed in this manuscripts leads to results that are in good agreement with other experimental and numerical works in the literature. We test the method for a wide variety of cases, including 2 and 3-dimensional geometries, structures with elastic joints or domains with variable bathymetry.

Appendix A. Formulation for the floating Euler-Bernoulli beam

Weak form

Following the same steps as described in Section
3.1
, one can derive the weak form for the 2-dimensional case, where the floating structure is modeled as a 1-dimensional Euler-Bernoulli beam. In that case, the equivalent bilinear form to (
17
) would read

\displaystyle B_{\scriptsize\mbox{EB}}([\phi,\eta],[w,v])\eqdef

\displaystyle(\nabla\phi,\nabla w)_{\Omega}-(\eta_{t},w)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}}\cup\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}}}

(84)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\beta\left(\phi_{t}+g\eta,\alpha_{f}w+v\right)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs}}}+\left(d_{0}\eta_{tt}+\phi_{t}+g\eta,v\right)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}}}+\left(D_{\rho}\Delta\eta,\Delta v\right)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str}}}.

Equivalently, the bilinear form for a structure with joints is given by

\displaystyle B_{\scriptsize\mbox{j,EB}}([\phi,\eta],[w,v])\eqdef

\displaystyle B_{\scriptsize\mbox{EB}}([\phi,\eta],[w,v])+\left(k_{\rho}[\![\nabla\eta\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!],[\![\nabla v\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!]\right)_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{j}}}.

(85)

Spatial discretization

The equivalent semi-discrete problem using a C/DG formulation for the case of a floating Euler-Bernoulli beam reads: find
[\phi_{h},\eta_{h}]\in\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{h}\times\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{\Gamma,h}
such that

\hat{B}_{h,{\scriptsize\mbox{EB}}}([\phi_{h},\eta_{h}],[w_{h},v_{h}])=L_{h}([w_{h},v_{h}])\quad\forall[w_{h},v_{h}]\in\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{h}\times\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{\Gamma,h}.

(86)

Where

\displaystyle\hat{B}_{h,{\scriptsize\mbox{EB}}}([\phi_{h},\eta_{h}],[w_{h},v_{h}])\eqdef

\displaystyle(\nabla\phi_{h},\nabla w_{h})_{\Omega_{h}}-(\eta_{h,t},w_{h})_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs},h}\cup\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}

(87)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\beta\left(\phi_{h,t}+g\eta_{h},\alpha_{f}w_{h}+v_{h}\right)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs},h}}+\left(d_{0}\eta_{h,tt}+\phi_{h,t}+g\eta_{h},v_{h}\right)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left(D_{\rho}\Delta\eta_{h},\Delta v_{h}\right)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}+\left(k_{\rho}[\![\nabla\eta_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!],[\![\nabla v_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!]\right)_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{j}}}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\left(\langle D_{\rho}\Delta\eta_{h}\rangle,[\![\nabla v_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!]\right)_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}-\left([\![\nabla\eta_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!],\langle D_{\rho}\Delta v_{h}\rangle\right)_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma}{h}\left([\![D_{\rho}\nabla\eta_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!],[\![\nabla v_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!]\right)_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}.

Time discretization

The equivalent fully discrete problem in the frequency domain, as it has been defined in Section
3.3.1
, for the case of a 1-dimensional floating Euler-Bernoulli beam in a 2-dimensional domain would be given by: find
[\phi_{h},\eta_{h}]\in\hat{\mathcal{V}}^{\omega}_{h}\times\hat{\mathcal{V}}^{\omega}_{\Gamma,h}
such that

\hat{B}^{\omega}_{h,{\scriptsize\mbox{EB}}}([\phi_{h},\eta_{h}],[w_{h},v_{h}])=L^{\omega}_{h}([w_{h},v_{h}])\quad\forall[w_{h},v_{h}]\in\hat{\mathcal{V}}^{\omega}_{h}\times\hat{\mathcal{V}}^{\omega}_{\Gamma,h}.

(88)

With

\displaystyle\hat{B}^{\omega}_{h,{\scriptsize\mbox{EB}}}([\phi_{h},\eta_{h}],[w_{h},v_{h}])\eqdef

\displaystyle(\nabla\phi_{h},\nabla w_{h})_{\Omega_{h}}+(i\omega\eta_{h},w_{h})_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs},h}\cup\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}

(89)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\beta\left(g\eta_{h}-i\omega\phi_{h},\alpha^{\omega}_{f}w_{h}+v_{h}\right)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs},h}}+\left((g-\omega^{2}d_{0})\eta_{h}-i\omega\phi_{h},v_{h}\right)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left(D_{\rho}\Delta\eta_{h},\Delta v_{h}\right)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}+\left(k_{\rho}[\![\nabla\eta_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!],[\![\nabla v_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!]\right)_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{j}}}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\left(\langle D_{\rho}\Delta\eta_{h}\rangle,[\![\nabla v_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}]\!]\right)_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}-\left([\![D_{\rho}\nabla\eta_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}]\!],\langle\Delta v\rangle\right)_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma}{h}\left([\![D_{\rho}\nabla\eta_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}]\!],[\![\nabla v_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}]\!]\right)_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}.

Alternatively, the fully discrete problem in the time domain, as it has been defined in Section
3.3.2
, for the case of a 1-dimensional floating Euler-Bernoulli beam in a 2-dimensional domain would be given by: find
[\phi_{h}^{n+1},\eta_{h}^{n+1}]\in\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{h}\times\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{\Gamma,h}
such that

\hat{B}_{h,{\scriptsize\mbox{EB}}}^{n+1}([\phi_{h}^{n+1},\eta_{h}^{n+1}],[w_{h},v_{h}])=L_{h}^{n+1}([w_{h},v_{h}])\quad\forall[w_{h},v_{h}]\in\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{h}\times\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{\Gamma,h}.

(90)

Where

\displaystyle\hat{B}_{h,{\scriptsize\mbox{EB}}}^{n+1}([\phi_{h}^{n+1},\eta_{h}^{n+1}],[w_{h},v_{h}])\eqdef

\displaystyle(\nabla\phi_{h}^{n+1},\nabla w_{h})_{\Omega_{h}}-(\delta_{t}\eta_{h}^{n+1},w_{h})_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs},h}\cup\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}

(91)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\beta\left(\delta_{t}\phi_{h}^{n+1}+g\eta_{h}^{n+1},\alpha_{f}w_{h}+v_{h}\right)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{fs},h}}+\left(\delta_{tt}d_{0}\eta_{h}^{n+1}+\delta_{t}\phi_{h}^{n+1}+g\eta_{h}^{n+1},v_{h}\right)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left(D_{\rho}\Delta\eta_{h}^{n+1},\Delta v_{h}\right)_{\Gamma_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}+\left(k_{\rho}[\![\nabla\eta_{h}^{n+1}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!],[\![\nabla v_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!]\right)_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{j}}}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\left(\langle D_{\rho}\Delta\eta_{h}^{n+1}\rangle,[\![\nabla v_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!]\right)_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}-\left([\![D_{\rho}\nabla\eta_{h}^{n+1}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!],\langle\Delta v\rangle\right)_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma}{h}\left([\![D_{\rho}\nabla\eta_{h}^{n+1}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!],[\![\nabla v_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Lambda}]\!]\right)_{\Lambda_{\scriptsize\mbox{str},h}}.

Appendix B. Definitions, lemmas and theorems

Definition 3
.

For any
w\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
, define the trace operator
\gamma|_{\partial\Omega}
by

\gamma|_{\partial\Omega}w(x)=w(x),\qquad x\in\partial\Omega.

(92)

Theorem 4

(Trace theorem of Sobolev spaces)
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded simply connected Lipschitz domain. Then, the trace operator
\gamma|_{\partial\Omega}
is a bounded linear operator from
H^{1}(\Omega)
to
H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial\Omega)
. That is,

\left\|\gamma|_{\partial\Omega}w\right\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial\Omega)}\leq C_{\partial\Omega}\left\|w\right\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}.

With
C
a constant that only depends on
\partial\Omega
.

See
[
17
]
for a proof of Theorem
4
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded connected Lipschitz domain and
f
be a linear form from
H^{1}(\Omega)
with a non-zero restriction on constant functions.
Then, there is a constant
C_{\Omega}>0
such that

C_{\Omega}\|w\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\|\nabla w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+|f(w)|,\qquad\forall w\in H^{1}(\Omega).

(93)
