Interferometric measurement of the quadrature coherence scale using two replicas of a quantum optical state
Célia Griffet, Matthieu Arnhem, Stephan De Bièvre, Nicolas J. Cerf

Abstract
Assessing whether a quantum state ρ ^ \hat{\rho} is nonclassical ( i.e. , incompatible with a mixture of coherent states) is a ubiquitous question in quantum optics, yet a nontrivial experimental task because many nonclassicality witnesses are nonlinear in ρ ^ \hat{\rho} . In particular, if we want to witness or measure the nonclassicality of a state by evaluating its quadrature coherence scale, this a priori requires full state tomography. Here, we provide an experimental procedure for directly accessing this quantity with a simple linear interferometer involving two replicas (independent and identical copies) of the state ρ ^ \hat{\rho} supplemented with photon-number-resolving measurements. This finding, which we interpret as an extension of the Hong-Ou-Mandel effect, illustrates the wide applicability of the multicopy interferometric technique in order to circumvent state tomography in quantum optics.



I
Introduction

In quantum optics, a state
\hat{\rho}
is said to be optically classical provided its Glauber-Sudarshan
P
function is non-negative
Titulaer and Glauber 1965
, that is, if there exists a probability density function
P(z)
such that

\hat{\rho}=\int P(z)\,|z\rangle\langle z|\,\mathrm{d}^{2}z.

(1)

Here,
|z\rangle=\hat{D}(z)\,|0\rangle
are the coherent states with
z\in\mathbb{C}
,
|0\rangle
is the vacuum state, and
\hat{D}(z)=\exp(z\hat{a}^{\dagger}-z^{*}\hat{a})
is the displacement operator. In other words, an optically classical state is a statistical mixture of coherent states. Detecting the optical nonclassicality of a given state
\hat{\rho}
using this definition is, unfortunately, very difficult since the
P
function can be singular and hence hard to determine both theoretically and experimentally. Further, it is impractical to rule out the existence of a convex mixture of coherent states realizing
\hat{\rho}
by checking all realizations of
\hat{\rho}
. Instead, one is usually forced to resort to so-called witnesses, which only provide a necessary condition on the non-negativity of
P(z)
, and hence a sufficient condition on nonclassicality. Following this line, a large variety of witnesses and measures of optical nonclassicality have been proposed in recent decades in order to detect and quantify nonclassicality; see,
e.g.
, Refs.
Hillery 1985
;
Bach and Lüxmann-Ellinghaus 1986
;
Hillery 1987
;
Hillery 1989
;
Lee 1991
;
Agarwal and Tara 1992
;
Lee 1995
;
Lütkenhaus and Barnett 1995
;
Dodonov et al. 2000
;
Marian et al. 2002
;
Richter and Vogel 2002
;
Kenfack and Życzkowski 2004
;
Asbóth et al. 2005
;
Ryl et al. 2015
;
Sperling and Vogel 2015
;
Killoran et al. 2016
;
Alexanian 2018
;
Nair 2017
;
Ryl et al. 2017
;
Kwon et al. 2019
;
De Bièvre et al. 2019
;
Yadin and Vedral 2016
;
Yadin et al. 2018
;
Luo and Zhang 2019
;
Bohmann et al. 2020
.

In this paper, we focus on a witness of optical nonclassicality called the quadrature coherence scale (QCS), introduced in Ref.
De Bièvre et al. 2019
and further studied in Refs.
Horoshko et al. 2019
;
Hertz and De Bièvre 2020
;
Hertz et al. 2020
;
Hertz and De Bièvre 2023
. The QCS of the state
\hat{\rho}
of
n
bosonic modes is denoted as
\mathcal{C}(\hat{\rho})
and its square is defined as
De Bièvre et al. 2019
;
Hertz and De Bièvre 2020

\mathcal{C}^{2}(\hat{\rho})=\frac{1}{2n\,\mathcal{P}(\hat{\rho})}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{2n}{\mathrm{Tr}}\,[\hat{\rho},\hat{r}_{j}][\hat{r}_{j},\hat{\rho}]\right),

(2)

where
\mathbf{\hat{r}}=(\hat{x}_{1},\hat{p}_{1},\cdots,\hat{x}_{n},\hat{p}_{n})
is the vector of the position and momentum quadratures, and
\mathcal{P}(\hat{\rho})=\mathrm{Tr}(\hat{\rho}^{2})
is the purity of the state
\hat{\rho}
.
It was shown in Ref.
De Bièvre et al. 2019
that the QCS is a witness of optical nonclassicality: if
\mathcal{C}(\hat{\rho})>1
, then the state
\hat{\rho}
is nonclassical. Since the converse is not true, this witness is not faithful. It nevertheless provides both an upper and lower bound on some suitably defined distance from the set of optically classical states
\cal C_{\text{cl}}
and, as such, it defines an optical nonclassicality measure
De Bièvre et al. 2019
. In particular, the larger the QCS, the farther the state is from
\cal C_{\text{cl}}
. In addition,
\mathcal{C}^{2}(\hat{\rho})
has a direct physical interpretation as being inversely proportional to the decoherence time of
\hat{\rho}

Hertz and De Bièvre 2020
. The evaluation of the QCS on large families of benchmark states has confirmed its efficiency as an optical nonclassicality measure
De Bièvre et al. 2019
;
Horoshko et al. 2019
;
Hertz and De Bièvre 2020
;
Hertz et al. 2020
;
Hertz and De Bièvre 2023
.

The difficulty with measuring the QCS (or many other nonclassicality witnesses) of an arbitrary state
\hat{\rho}
arises from the fact that it is a nonlinear function of
\hat{\rho}
, as is obvious from Eq. (
2
). At first sight, it thus seems that the task of experimentally accessing
\mathcal{C}(\hat{\rho})
imposes one to carry out a full tomographic reconstruction of
\hat{\rho}
before computing Eq. (
2
). Here, we contradict this statement and exhibit an elegant solution that bypasses state tomography and only requires two replicas (independent and identical copies) of
\hat{\rho}
in order to measure
\mathcal{C}(\hat{\rho})
. The fact that nonlinear functions of a state may be estimated by quantum interferometry using several replicas of the state has long been known
Ekert et al. 2002
;
Alves et al. 2003
. Specifically, the evaluation of any polynomial in the matrix elements of
\hat{\rho}
can be reduced to the measurement of a joint observable over several replicas of
\hat{\rho}
(the order of the polynomial translates into the number of replicas)
Brun 2004
. This multicopy interferometric technique has, for example, been used to witness qubit entanglement by coupling two identical pairs of polarization-entangled photons with beam splitters
Bovino et al. 2005
. It was also shown to give direct access to the purity and entanglement of atomic qubits
in an optical lattice by coupling the atoms pairwise via beam-splitter operations
Moura Alves and Jaksch 2004
;
Palmer et al. 2005
.
Notably, this method has been exploited for experimentally accessing the entanglement in a many-body Bose-Hubbard system
Daley et al. 2012
;
Islam et al. 2015
. In particular, the purity of ultracold bosonic atoms in an optical lattice was accessed in Ref.
Islam et al. 2015
by probing the average parity of the particle number at the output of a 50:50 beam splitter (realized by controlled tunneling).

Nonetheless, this multicopy technique has not yet been much exploited in continuous-variable quantum information theory and quantum optics. An exception is the recent derivation of symplectic-invariant entropic uncertainty relations in phase-space by using a multicopy uncertainty observable
Hertz et al. 2019
. In a related work, it was also shown that nonclassicality witnesses based on matrices of moments of the optical field could be expressed as multicopy observables, resulting in possible experimental schemes to detect nonclassicality
Arnhem et al. 2022
. Here, we use similar ideas and show that the QCS of a state can be experimentally accessed by performing an interferometric measurement involving only two replicas of the state impinging on a 50:50 beam splitter. We thereby establish an interesting connection between the nonclassicality of a state and the resulting photon-number distribution at the output of the beam splitter, which can be viewed as an extension of the Hong-Ou-Mandel effect.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Sec.
II
, we describe the aforementioned two-copy interferometric circuit and show how it enables a measurement of the purity of a single-mode state, which constitutes the denominator of the QCS.
Then, in Sec.
III
, we
show how to use the same measurement to determine the numerator of the QCS, thereby establishing our central result.
In Sec.
IV
, we illustrate our
two-copy expression of the QCS [Eq. (
16
)]
by applying it to several families of benchmark states.
As a by-product of our analysis, we provide, in Sec.
V
, an alternative expression for the QCS that exploits the phase-space formulation of quantum optics and is of interest in its own right.
In the process, we show that the same interferometric circuit can be used to compute the overlap between two distinct single-mode states.
For notational convenience, we limit ourselves to the state of a single bosonic mode in Secs.
II
-
V
, but we then show in Sec.
VI
how to extend our results to multimode states. Finally, we conclude in Sec.
VII
.

II
Two-copy observable for measuring the purity

As is well known, directly accessing the purity
\mathcal{P}(\hat{\rho})=\mathrm{Tr}(\hat{\rho}^{2})
of a quantum state
\hat{\rho}
as the expectation value of some observable is impossible since it is nonlinear in
\hat{\rho}
. However, as is also well known and readily checked, the purity can be reexpressed as the expectation value of the so-called
swap
operator
\hat{S}
taken over two replicas of the state, that is
Ekert et al. 2002
;
Alves et al. 2003
,

\mathcal{P}(\hat{\rho})=\mathrm{Tr}\left((\hat{\rho}\otimes\hat{\rho})\,\hat{S}\right),

(3)

where
\hat{S}
is defined as

\hat{S}|\varphi\rangle|\psi\rangle=|\psi\rangle|\varphi\rangle
,
\forall\penalty\ |\varphi\rangle,|\psi\rangle
.
Note that
\hat{S}
is a unitary operator that is also Hermitian, so that it can be viewed as an observable. The fact that we need two replicas here is, of course, simply related to the fact that
\mathcal{P}(\hat{\rho})
is quadratic in
\hat{\rho}
.

To find the actual measurement scheme, we proceed as in Refs.
Daley et al. 2012
;
Islam et al. 2015
. We first write
\hat{S}
using the associated mode operators
\hat{a}
and
\hat{b}
, namely,

\hat{S}=e^{i\frac{\pi}{2}(\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\hat{b}^{\dagger})(\hat{a}-\hat{b})},

(4)

as proven in Appendix
A
.
In order to measure
\hat{S}
, we can simply use a 50:50 (balanced) beam splitter. The latter corresponds to the Gaussian unitary
Weedbrook et al. 2012

\hat{U}_{BS}=e^{\frac{\pi}{4}(\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{b}-\hat{a}\hat{b}^{\dagger})},

(5)

and, in the Heisenberg picture, it transforms the mode operators
\hat{a}
and
\hat{b}
as

\displaystyle\hat{c}

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{BS}^{\dagger}\,\hat{a}\,\hat{U}_{BS}=(\hat{a}+\hat{b})/\sqrt{2},

\displaystyle\hat{d}

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{BS}^{\dagger}\,\hat{b}\,\hat{U}_{BS}=(-\hat{a}+\hat{b})/\sqrt{2},

(6)

where
\hat{c}
and
\hat{d}
denote the corresponding output mode operators. Hence, Eq. (
4
) can be reexpressed as

\hat{S}=e^{i\pi\,\hat{d}^{\dagger}\hat{d}}=(-1)^{\hat{n}_{d}},

(7)

where
\hat{n}_{d}=\hat{d}^{\dagger}\hat{d}
is the photon number in mode
\hat{d}
.
Here,
\hat{d}
stands for the “difference” mode, that is, it exhibits destructive interference if we feed the beam splitter with two identical coherent states. From Eq. (
7
), it thus appears that the measurement of the purity of a state can be achieved by measuring the average parity of the photon number in the “difference” mode at the output of a 50:50 beam splitter after having sent two identical copies of the state at its input. Indeed, Eq. (
3
) reduces to

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\hat{\rho})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left((\hat{\rho}\otimes\hat{\rho})\,(-1)^{\hat{n}_{d}}\right),

(8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left(\hat{U}_{\mathrm{BS}}(\hat{\rho}\otimes\hat{\rho})\hat{U}_{\mathrm{BS}}^{\dagger}\,(-1)^{\hat{n}_{b}}\right),

where the first (second) line corresponds to the Heisenberg (Schrödinger) picture. An analogous expression can be found in Refs.
Daley et al. 2012
;
Islam et al. 2015
.

Figure 1:
Two-copy circuit implementing the measurement of the purity
\mathcal{P}(\hat{\rho})
as well as quadrature coherence scale
\mathcal{C}(\hat{\rho})
of a bosonic state
\hat{\rho}
. Two identical copies of the state are sent on a 50:50 beam splitter and the photon number statistics is measured in the output mode
d
(associated with destructive interference). The mean photon number parity yields the purity, while the numerator and denominator of Eq. (
16
) can be accessed separately in order to determine the QCS.

To sum up, the two-copy interferometric procedure in order to measure the purity of a state
\hat{\rho}
is represented in Fig.
1
. One must send two identical copies of
\hat{\rho}
on a 50:50 beam splitter, which results in the output state
\hat{U}_{\mathrm{BS}}\,(\hat{\rho}\otimes\hat{\rho})\,\hat{U}_{\mathrm{BS}}^{\dagger}
, and then measure the number of photons with a photon-number resolving detector in the output that is associated with destructive interference. The purity is simply equal to the expectation value of the parity of the photon number in the reduced state
\hat{\rho}_{d}=\mathrm{Tr}_{c}(\hat{\rho}\otimes\hat{\rho})
of mode
\hat{d}
, namely,

\mathcal{P}(\hat{\rho})=\mathrm{Tr}_{d}\left(\hat{\rho}_{d}\,(-1)^{\hat{n}_{d}}\right).

(9)

It makes sense that the output mode
\hat{d}
is solely involved here because we expect the purity to be invariant under displacements of
\hat{\rho}
in phase space. Indeed, mode
\hat{c}
must be disregarded since its state depends on the mean field of the input state
\hat{\rho}
(in contrast, the mean field of
\hat{\rho}_{d}
always vanishes, regardless of the mean field of
\hat{\rho}
). Furthermore, we easily understand that Eq. (
9
) may only depend on the photon number
\hat{n}_{d}
in mode
\hat{d}
since the purity is also invariant under rotations of
\hat{\rho}
in phase space (remember that a rotation of
\hat{\rho}
induces a rotation of
\hat{\rho}_{d}
since the beam splitter unitary
\hat{U}_{BS}
is covariant with respect to a pair of identical rotations).

III
Two-copy observable for measuring the QCS

The central result of this paper is a two-copy interferometric procedure for measuring the QCS of a state. In the case of a single-mode state
\hat{\rho}
, Eq. (
2
) reduces to

\mathcal{C}^{2}(\hat{\rho})=-\,\frac{1}{2\,\mathcal{P}(\hat{\rho})}\,\mathrm{Tr}\left([\hat{\rho},\hat{x}]^{2}+[\hat{\rho},\hat{p}]^{2}\right),

(10)

where the purity
\mathcal{P}(\hat{\rho})
appears in the denominator. The latter can be accessed by applying the multicopy technique as explained in Sec.
II
[see Eq. (
9
)], so we only need to focus on the numerator
\mathcal{N}(\hat{\rho})
. Writing

\mathcal{C}^{2}(\hat{\rho})=\frac{\mathcal{N}(\hat{\rho})}{\mathcal{P}(\hat{\rho})},

(11)

the numerator can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\hat{\rho})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{Tr}\left([\hat{\rho},\hat{x}]^{2}+[\hat{\rho},\hat{p}]^{2}\right),

(12)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\int(x-x^{\prime})^{2}\,|\langle x|\hat{\rho}|x^{\prime}\rangle|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\right.

\displaystyle\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ +\left.\int(p-p^{\prime})^{2}\,|\langle p|\hat{\rho}|p^{\prime}\rangle|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}p\,\mathrm{d}p^{\prime}\right),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\int(x-x^{\prime})^{2}\,\langle x,x^{\prime}|\hat{\rho}\otimes\hat{\rho}|x^{\prime},x\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\right.

\displaystyle\penalty\ \penalty\ +\left.\int(p-p^{\prime})^{2}\,\langle p,p^{\prime}|\hat{\rho}\otimes\hat{\rho}|p^{\prime},p\rangle\,\mathrm{d}p\,\mathrm{d}p^{\prime}\right),

where we have expanded the first and second terms of the right-hand side in the position and momentum basis, respectively.

In order to access
\mathcal{N}(\hat{\rho})
from two copies of
\hat{\rho}
, we proceed along the same line as for the purity
\mathcal{P}(\hat{\rho})
. It is easy to recognize the two-copy observable
\hat{N}
that appears in Eq. (
12
), that is,

\mathcal{N}(\hat{\rho})=\mathrm{Tr}\left((\hat{\rho}\otimes\hat{\rho})\,\hat{N}\right),

(13)

where

\hat{N}=\frac{1}{2}\left[(\hat{x}_{a}-\hat{x}_{b})^{2}+(\hat{p}_{a}-\hat{p}_{b})^{2}\right]\,\hat{S},

(14)

with
\hat{x}_{a}
(
\hat{p}_{a}
) and
\hat{x}_{b}
(
\hat{p}_{b}
) denoting the position (momentum) quadratures of modes
\hat{a}
and
\hat{b}
.
The terms involving these quadratures can be simplified by using the quadratures
(\hat{x}_{d},\hat{p}_{d})
of the difference mode
\hat{d}
, that is,

\hat{N}=(\hat{x}_{d}^{2}+\hat{p}_{d}^{2})\,\hat{S}=(1+2\,\hat{n}_{d})\,\hat{S}.

(15)

The observable
\hat{N}
plays the same role here as the swap operator
\hat{S}
in Eq. (
3
).
Note that the two factors in
\hat{N}
are commuting Hermitians, which implies that
\hat{N}
is itself Hermitian, as expected.
Remarkably, we see that
\hat{N}
is measurable with the same circuit as the one used for the purity in Fig.
1
. Indeed, putting together Eqs. (
7
) and (
15
), we obtain the two-copy expression for the QCS,

\mathcal{C}^{2}(\hat{\rho})=\frac{\mathrm{Tr}_{d}\left(\hat{\rho}_{d}\,(-1)^{\hat{n}_{d}}\,(1+2\,\hat{n}_{d})\right)}{\mathrm{Tr}_{d}\left(\hat{\rho}_{d}\,(-1)^{\hat{n}_{d}}\right)},

(16)

which is our main result. We again need to send two replicas of
\hat{\rho}
on a 50:50 beam splitter and measure the photon number
\hat{n}_{d}
of the state
\hat{\rho}_{d}
in the “difference” mode
\hat{d}
. From the measured statistics of
\hat{n}_{d}
, we can compute both the numerator and denominator of Eq. (
16
).

Similar to before, only the state of mode
\hat{d}
is involved in Eq. (
16
), which was expected since the QCS is invariant under displacements in phase space. Further, Eq. (
16
) only depends on the photon number
\hat{n}_{d}
in mode
\hat{d}
, which ensures that the QCS is, in addition, invariant under rotations in phase space.

IV
Applications

Let us discuss some applications of this expression of the QCS based on a two-copy observable, as obtained in Eq. (
16
). First, we emphasize that it gives a better grasp on the reason why it detects optical nonclassicality than the one-copy expression given by Eq. (
10
). As shown in Appendix
B
, Eq. (
16
) can indeed be used to quite easily prove that
\mathcal{C}^{2}\leq 1
for any classical state (mixture of coherent states). In contrast with the proof of Ref.
De Bièvre et al. 2019
, the physical interpretation is straightforward. Sending two identical coherent states
|\alpha\rangle\otimes|\alpha\rangle
in the 50:50 beam splitter of Fig.
1
results in a coherent state
|\sqrt{2}\,\alpha\rangle
in mode
\hat{c}
and the vacuum state
|0\rangle
in mode
\hat{d}
. Hence, the measured value of
\hat{n}_{d}
always vanishes and it is immediate that
\mathcal{C}^{2}(|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|)=1
for any coherent state
|\alpha\rangle
. Then, a simple calculation exploiting the Poisson distribution of the photon number in a coherent state is enough to prove that
\mathcal{C}^{2}
can only decrease when mixing coherent states.

Second, we note that the definition of the QCS as given by Eq. (
2
) [or Eq. (
10
) for a single mode] is not convenient for computing its value. A number of alternative expressions have been derived in Refs.
De Bièvre et al. 2019
;
Horoshko et al. 2019
;
Hertz and De Bièvre 2020
;
Hertz et al. 2020
that are more suitable for this purpose in various situations. For example, if the Wigner function of state
\hat{\rho}
is known, one can use Eq. (
44
), see below. Simple expressions also exist when the state
\hat{\rho}
is pure
De Bièvre et al. 2019
or Gaussian
Hertz and De Bièvre 2020
;
Hertz et al. 2020
. In what follows, we analyze the expression given by Eq. (
16
) and illustrate its merits. We start by rewriting the expression of the purity, given by Eq. (
8
), as

\mathcal{P}(\hat{\rho})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{n}\,(-1)^{n},

(17)

where

p_{n}=\langle n|\hat{\rho}_{\textrm{out},b}|n\rangle,

(18)

is the probability of finding
n
photons in the reduced state

\hat{\rho}_{\textrm{out},b}=\mathrm{Tr}_{a}\left(\hat{U}_{\mathrm{BS}}(\hat{\rho}\otimes\hat{\rho})\hat{U}_{\mathrm{BS}}^{\dagger}\right),

(19)

of the measured output mode of the beam splitter. Similarly,

\mathcal{N}(\hat{\rho})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{n}\,(-1)^{n}\,(1+2n)\,,

(20)

so that Eq. (
16
) can be rewritten as

{\mathcal{C}^{2}(\hat{\rho})=1+2\,\frac{\sum_{n}n\,(-1)^{n}p_{n}}{\sum_{n}(-1)^{n}p_{n}}.}

(21)

Whereas it looks simple, Eq. (
21
) yields a convenient method to compute the QCS theoretically only when the
p_{n}
’s can be easily calculated. Still, the main message of our paper is that, provided the
p_{n}
’s can be experimentally measured using the above interferometric scheme, we get a direct access to
\mathcal{C}^{2}(\hat{\rho})
from Eq. (
21
).

Interestingly, we may suggestively rewrite
\mathcal{C}^{2}(\hat{\rho})
in terms of some peculiar average. Let

\pi_{n}=\frac{(-1)^{n}\,p_{n}}{\sum_{n}(-1)^{n}\,p_{n}},

(22)

be the quasi-probability distribution associated with
p_{n}
(with
\sum_{n}\pi_{n}=1
but
\pi_{n}\ngeq 0
). Then, Eq. (
21
) becomes

\mathcal{C}^{2}(\hat{\rho})=1+2\,\langle n\,\rangle_{\pi},

(23)

where
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\pi}
denotes the average with respect to
\pi_{n}
. Hence,
\langle n\rangle_{\pi}>0
is equivalent to
\mathcal{C}^{2}(\hat{\rho})>1
as a sufficient condition for optical nonclassicality.

At this point, it is instructive to consider what happens when
\hat{\rho}=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
is a pure state, so that

\mathcal{P}(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|)=\sum_{n}(-1)^{n}p_{n}=1.

(24)

Since
\sum_{n}p_{n}=1
, this implies that only even values of
n
can be observed at the output of the 50:50 beam splitter (
p_{n}=0
if
n
is odd), which can be viewed as the manifestation of an extended Hong-Ou-Mandel effect. In this case
\pi_{n}=p_{n}
becomes a genuine probability distribution and the QCS is simply given by

\mathcal{C}^{2}(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|)=1+2\,\bar{n},

(25)

where
\bar{n}=\sum_{n}n\,p_{n}
is the average photon number in the measured output mode of the beam splitter. We see here that the QCS always exceeds one unless the pure state
|\psi\rangle
is a coherent state, in which case
p_{n}=\delta_{n,0}
and
\bar{n}=0
. This is consistent with the fact that the only classical pure states are coherent states. The value of the QCS for all other pure states is very easy to find since

\bar{n}=\langle\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\rangle_{\psi}-\langle\hat{a}\rangle_{\psi}\,\langle\hat{a}^{\dagger}\rangle_{\psi}
, where
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\psi}=\langle\psi|\cdot|\psi\rangle
denotes the expectation value in the input pure state
|\psi\rangle
. The number
\bar{n}
is sometimes also called the number of thermal (non-coherent) photons of
|\psi\rangle
. Furthermore, it is just equal to the average photon number
\bar{n}=\langle\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\rangle_{\psi}
of the input state if the latter state is centered on the origin (if the mean field vanishes). We then immediately recover the known expressions of the QCS for a vacuum squeezed state or a Fock state
De Bièvre et al. 2019
, namely,

\mathcal{C}^{2}(|\mathrm{Sq}_{r}\rangle\langle\ \mathrm{Sq}_{r}|)=\cosh(2r),\quad\mathcal{C}^{2}(|n\rangle\langle n|)=1+2n.

(26)

Let us turn to examples of mixed states
\hat{\rho}
and illustrate how the behavior of distribution of the
p_{n}
’s yields a qualitative idea on the value of the QCS. For this purpose, we consider the following two families of states

\hat{\rho}_{2M}=\frac{1}{2M}\sum_{n=1}^{2M}|n\rangle\langle n|,\quad\hat{\rho}_{\textrm{even},M}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{n=1}^{M}|2n\rangle\langle 2n|.

(27)

with
M\geq 1
. It was shown in Ref.
De Bièvre et al. 2019
that

\mathcal{P}(\hat{\rho}_{2M})=\frac{1}{2M},\quad\mathcal{P}(\hat{\rho}_{\textrm{even},M})=\frac{1}{M},

(28)

and

\mathcal{C}^{2}(\hat{\rho}_{2M})=1+\frac{1}{M},\quad\mathcal{C}^{2}(\hat{\rho}_{\textrm{even},M})=1+2(M+1).

(29)

In other words, as
M
increases, both states are increasingly mixed, but, whereas this goes with a lower nonclassicality for
\hat{\rho}_{2M}
, the nonclassicality of
\hat{\rho}_{\textrm{even},M}
gets higher. As shown in Ref.
De Bièvre et al. 2019
, this corresponds to increasingly fast oscillations in the Wigner function of
\hat{\rho}_{\textrm{even},M}
, which are absent for
\hat{\rho}_{2M}
. In Fig.
2
, we plot the numerically obtained values of
p_{n}
for both states for
M=5
. Some details of the underlying computations are provided in Appendix
C
. One observes on these graphs that the
p_{n}
’s evolve more smoothly as a function of
n
for
\hat{\rho}_{10}
than for
\hat{\rho}_{\textrm{even},5}
. Since for both states

\mathcal{P}(\hat{\rho})=\sum_{k=0}^{10}(p_{2k}-p_{2k+1})
,
this immediately explains the lower value of the purity for
\hat{\rho}_{10}
than for
\hat{\rho}_{\textrm{even},5}
. In general, it is clear that if the
p_{n}
’s evolve slowly with
n
, the value of the purity will tend to be smaller. If, on the other hand, they change sharply with successive
n
(with higher values for even
n
than for odd
n
), the purity will tend to be larger. This effect is accentuated in the numerator of the QCS because of the extra
n
factor. This qualitatively explains the large value of the QCS for
\hat{\rho}_{\textrm{even},5}
as a consequence of the sharp variations in the corresponding
p_{n}
’s as observed in Fig.

.

To further corroborate this picture, we compute the QCS for thermal states,

\hat{\rho}_{q}=(1-q)\sum_{n}q^{n}|n\rangle\langle n|,\qquad 0\leq q<1,

(30)

with a mean photon number
\langle\hat{n}\rangle_{q}=q/(1-q)
.
Since sending two identical thermal states at a beam splitter results in the same product of thermal states at the output
Weedbrook et al. 2012
, we simply have
p_{n}=(1-q)\,q^{n}
. The graph of
p_{n}
for
q=0.85
is shown in Fig.

and one sees that it is indeed very smooth as a function of
n
, without fast oscillations as expected since thermal states are well known to be classical.
Using Eqs. (
17
)
and (
20
), we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\hat{\rho}_{q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-q)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}q^{n}=\frac{1-q}{1+q},

(31)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\hat{\rho}_{q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-q)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}q^{n}(1+2n)=\frac{(1-q)^{2}}{(1+q)^{2}},\hskip 14.22636pt

(32)

so that Eq. (

) yields

\mathcal{C}^{2}(\hat{\rho}_{q})=\frac{1-q}{1+q}=\frac{1}{1+2\langle\hat{n}\rangle_{q}}<1.

(33)

In short, when the
p_{n}
’s can be determined, experimentally or otherwise, an inspection of their behavior as a function of
n
gives a good indication of whether the QCS is large or small. More precisely, one expects that when the
p_{n}
’s evolve slowly with
n
, the state has a small QCS, whereas sharp variations in the
p_{n}
’s indicate a large QCS, and hence a nonclassical state. Note that these variations can be viewed as a consequence of quantum interference, so that we see here again that large interference effects are associated to a large degree of nonclassicality. When the state is pure (except for coherent states), the full sequence
p_{n}
exhibits clear oscillations since all odd terms vanish (extended Hong-Ou-Mandel effect). When the state is mixed, the fluctuations may remain, but are less pronounced. Thus, overall, Eq. (
21
) suggests that the QCS is a measure of the intensity of these fluctuations.

In practice, it must be stressed that the above procedure for measuring the QCS requires optical phase stability since the two replicas of the state are interfered at a 50:50 beam splitter before being measured. Crucially, the accuracy of the obtained value of the QCS is also conditional on the ability of the detector in Fig.
1
to resolve the photon number; this is especially true given that Eq. (
21
) depends on the parity of the photon number. In case imperfect photon-number-resolving detectors are used (realized, for example, with arrays of on-off photodetectors) the limitations of the method should be carefully analyzed. Furthermore, the influence of mode mismatch, optical losses, and detector noise should be considered in order to effectively use our protocol (these imperfections could be overcome in an experimental demonstration of our protocol
Goldberg et al. 2023
, see note at the end of this paper).

VII
Conclusion

The quadrature coherence scale is an efficient nonclassicality measure, which can be expressed through several equivalent formulas, making it relatively easy to compute for a large variety of states
De Bièvre et al. 2019
;
Horoshko et al. 2019
;
Hertz and De Bièvre 2020
;
Hertz et al. 2020
;
Hertz and De Bièvre 2023
. It has a clear physical interpretation, notably because it is inversely proportional to the decoherence time of the state
Hertz and De Bièvre 2020
. However, since it is a nonlinear function of the density matrix, its measurement would
a priori
seem to require a complete quantum state tomography. We have shown that this problem can be avoided through the use of a simple two-copy interferometric measurement scheme using a 50:50 beam splitter associated with photon counting. The method can easily be adapted to multimode systems, in which case one needs to couple the modes pairwise using a stack of 50:50 beam splitters.
The challenge of this procedure is of course the need to ensure the interferometric stability of the joint measurement of two replicas together with reasonably low mode-mismatch, optical losses in the circuit, and extra noise, as well as the need for photon-number resolving detectors.

The underlying multicopy technique used here was put forward in the 2000s
Brun 2004
;
Ekert et al. 2002
;
Alves et al. 2003
and was more recently applied to bosonic atoms in optical lattices
Daley et al. 2012
;
Islam et al. 2015
as well as continuous-variable quantum optical systems
Hertz et al. 2019
;
Arnhem et al. 2022
.
The present work further extends the range of applicability of this technique in quantum optics.

Note:
Recently, our protocol for measuring the QCS has been successfully implemented in an experiment involving state-of-the-art (superconducting) photon-number-resolving detectors
Goldberg et al. 2023
.

Appendix A
Swap operator
\hat{S}
in Fock space

The swap operator
\hat{S}
naturally extends to the infinite-dimensional Fock space of a bosonic mode (or harmonic oscillator), in which case it is convenient to express it in terms of mode operators
\hat{a}
and
\hat{b}
. For this purpose, we introduce the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}=-(\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\hat{b}^{\dagger})(\hat{a}-\hat{b})
and write the Heisenberg evolution of
\hat{a}
and
\hat{b}
(setting
\hbar=1
), namely,

\displaystyle\hat{a}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp(it\hat{H})\,\hat{a}\,\exp(-it\hat{H}),

\displaystyle\hat{b}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp(it\hat{H})\,\hat{b}\,\exp(-it\hat{H}).

(50)

Hence,

\displaystyle i\,\frac{d\hat{a}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[\hat{a}(t),\hat{H}]=-\hat{a}(t)+\hat{b}(t),

\displaystyle i\,\frac{d\hat{b}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[\hat{b}(t),\hat{H}]=-\hat{b}(t)+\hat{a}(t),

(51)

resulting in the solution

\displaystyle\hat{a}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1+e^{2it}}{2}\,\hat{a}+\frac{1-e^{2it}}{2}\,\hat{b},

\displaystyle\hat{b}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1-e^{2it}}{2}\,\hat{a}+\frac{1+e^{2it}}{2}\,\hat{b}.

(52)

Setting
t=\pi/2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\hat{a}(\pi/2)=\hat{b},\qquad\hat{b}(\pi/2)=\hat{a},

(53)

effecting a swap of the two modes. This implies that
\hat{S}=e^{-i\frac{\pi}{2}\hat{H}}
, which proves Eq. (
4
).

\hfill\square

Using the unitary
\hat{U}_{BS}
corresponding to a 50:50 beam splitter as defined in Eq. (
5
), we can express the swap operator (
4
) in the Heisenberg picture as

\hat{S}=\hat{U}_{BS}^{\dagger}\,e^{i\pi\,\hat{b}^{\dagger}\hat{b}}\,\hat{U}_{BS}.

(54)

Hence, the swap operator can be implemented by processing the two modes (forwards) through a 50:50 beam splitter, acting with a
\pi
-phase shift in the second mode, and then processing the two modes (backwards) again through a 50:50 beam splitter. As we could expect,
\hat{S}
is simply the Gaussian unitary that corresponds to a Mach-Zehnder interferometer with a
\pi
-phase in one of the two arms, effecting a swap between the two modes.

As an illustration, let us check the effect of
\hat{S}
on two coherent states
|\alpha\rangle
and
|\beta\rangle
. We have

\displaystyle\hat{S}\,|\alpha\rangle\otimes|\beta\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{BS}^{\dagger}\,e^{i\pi\,\hat{b}^{\dagger}\hat{b}}\,\hat{U}_{BS}\penalty\ |\alpha\rangle\otimes|\beta\rangle,

(55)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{BS}^{\dagger}\,e^{i\pi\,\hat{b}^{\dagger}\hat{b}}\penalty\ \Big|\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\sqrt{2}}\Big\rangle\Big|\frac{-\alpha+\beta}{\sqrt{2}}\Big\rangle,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{BS}^{\dagger}\penalty\ \Big|\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\sqrt{2}}\Big\rangle\Big|\frac{\alpha-\beta}{\sqrt{2}}\Big\rangle,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{(\alpha+\beta)-(\alpha-\beta)}{2}\Big\rangle\Big|\frac{(\alpha+\beta)+(\alpha-\beta)}{2}\Big\rangle,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\beta\rangle\otimes|\alpha\rangle,

where we have used the fact that a product of coherent states
|\alpha\rangle\otimes|\beta\rangle
results under
\hat{U}_{BS}
into another product of coherent states

|(\alpha+\beta)/\sqrt{2}\rangle\otimes|(-\alpha+\beta)/\sqrt{2}\rangle
. Alternatively, we can simply check that
\hat{S}\,|\alpha\rangle\otimes|\beta\rangle
is a common eigenstate of
\hat{a}
and
\hat{b}
with respective eigenvalues
\beta
and
\alpha
(note the interchange). Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\hat{a}\,(\hat{S}\,|\alpha\rangle\otimes|\beta\rangle)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{S}\,(\hat{S}^{\dagger}\,\hat{a}\,\hat{S})\,|\alpha\rangle\otimes|\beta\rangle,

(56)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{S}\,\hat{b}\,|\alpha\rangle\otimes|\beta\rangle,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta\,(\hat{S}\,|\alpha\rangle\otimes|\beta\rangle),

and a similar equation holds for
\hat{b}\,(\hat{S}\,|\alpha\rangle\otimes|\beta\rangle)
.

Appendix B
Proof that QCS
\leq 1
for classical states

It was proven in Ref.
De Bièvre et al. 2019
that the QCS is smaller than or equal to
1
for all classical states, namely mixtures of coherent states
\hat{\rho}=\int P(\alpha)\,|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\alpha
.
Here, we provide an alternative (much simpler) proof of this result by taking advantage of the twocopy expression for the QCS given by Eq. (
16
).

To measure the QCS, we inject two identical copies of state
\hat{\rho}
in the circuit of Fig.
1
,
so the input state is

\hat{\rho}\otimes\hat{\rho}=\int P(\alpha)\,P(\beta)\,|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|\otimes|\beta\rangle\langle\beta|\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\alpha\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\beta

(57)

with
\int P(\alpha)\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\alpha=1
and
P(\alpha)\geq 0,\forall\alpha
. Each product term
|\alpha\rangle\otimes|\beta\rangle
of this mixture results, at the output of the 50:50 beam splitter, into another product of coherent states

|(\alpha+\beta)/\sqrt{2}\rangle\otimes|(-\alpha+\beta)/\sqrt{2}\rangle
. We only care here about the reduced output state in mode
\hat{d}
, that is

\hat{\rho}_{d}=\int P_{d}(\gamma)\,|\gamma\rangle\langle\gamma|\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\gamma,

(58)

where we have made the change of variables
\delta=(\alpha+\beta)/\sqrt{2}
and
\gamma=(-\alpha+\beta)/\sqrt{2}
, and then have integrated over variable
\delta
. Here,
P_{d}(\gamma)
of course depends on
P(\alpha)
but its explicit expression is irrelevant for the proof; we only note that
P_{d}(\gamma)\geq 0
,
\forall\gamma
, since it is a probability density, so that
\hat{\rho}_{d}
is a classical state too.

We are left with having to compute the mean values of
(-1)^{\hat{n}_{d}}
and
(-1)^{\hat{n}_{d}}(1+2\hat{n}_{d})
based on the distribution of the photon number
n_{d}
in state
\hat{\rho}_{d}
. Using the probability distribution of the photon number in a coherent state
|\gamma\rangle
,

p_{\gamma}(n)=e^{-|\gamma|^{2}}\frac{|\gamma|^{2n}}{n!},

(59)

we obtain the following expressions

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{d}=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n_{d}}\,p_{\gamma}(n_{d})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-|\gamma|^{2}},

(60)

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{d}=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n_{d}}\,(1+2n_{d})\,p_{\gamma}(n_{d})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-|\gamma|^{2}}(1-|\gamma|^{2}).

(61)

Hence, taking the average over
\gamma
, we obtain the simple expression for the QCS:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{2}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\frac{\int P_{d}(\gamma)\,e^{-|\gamma|^{2}}(1-|\gamma|^{2})\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\gamma}{\int P_{d}(\gamma)\,e^{-|\gamma|^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\gamma},

(62)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\int P_{d}(\gamma)\,e^{-|\gamma|^{2}}|\gamma|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\gamma}{\int P_{d}(\gamma)\,e^{-|\gamma|^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\gamma}.

(63)

Since the second term in this expression is always positive, the QCS can only be smaller than or equal to
1
for classical states.

\hfill\square

Appendix C
Computation of the
p_{n}
for phase-invariant states

In this appendix we briefly indicate how to compute the
p_{n}
in Sec.
IV
for phase-invariant states.

First, let us derive the photon number probability at the output if we put
N
photons in mode
\hat{a}
and
N^{\prime}
photons in mode
\hat{b}
. Then the initial state is given by:

|NN^{\prime}\rangle_{a,b}=\frac{\hat{a}^{\dagger N}}{\sqrt{N!}}\frac{\hat{b}^{\dagger N^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{N^{\prime}!}}|00\rangle_{a,b}.

(64)

Expanding this state in the number bases associated to the
c
and
d
modes yields:

\displaystyle|NN^{\prime}\rangle_{a,b}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-\hat{c}^{\dagger}+\hat{d}^{\dagger})^{N}}{\sqrt{2^{N}N!}}\frac{(\hat{c}^{\dagger}+\hat{d}^{\dagger})^{N^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{2^{N^{\prime}}N^{\prime}!}}|00\rangle_{a,b},

(65)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{N+N^{\prime}}\sum_{n^{\prime}=0}^{N^{\prime}}c(n,n^{\prime},N,N^{\prime})|N+N^{\prime}-n,n\rangle_{c,d},

(66)

where

c(n,n^{\prime},N,N^{\prime})=\frac{(-1)^{N-n+n^{\prime}}\sqrt{N!N^{\prime}!(N+N^{\prime}-n)!n!}}{\sqrt{2^{N+N^{\prime}}}(n-n^{\prime})!n^{\prime}!(N-n+n^{\prime})!(N^{\prime}-n^{\prime})!}.

(67)

Consequently, the photon probability distribution in mode
\hat{d}
is equal to:

p_{n}^{N,N^{\prime}}=\Big(\sum_{n^{\prime}=0}^{N^{\prime}}c(n,n^{\prime},N,N^{\prime})\Big)^{2}.

(68)

If the state is
\hat{\rho}=\sum_{m}\lambda_{m}|m\rangle\langle m|
, then, as the input state, we have:

\hat{\rho}\otimes\hat{\rho}=\sum_{m}\sum_{m^{\prime}}\lambda_{m}\lambda_{m^{\prime}}|m\rangle\langle m|\otimes|m^{\prime}\rangle\langle m^{\prime}|,

(69)

and the probability distribution is given by:

p_{n}=\sum_{m}\sum_{m^{\prime}}\lambda_{m}\lambda_{m^{\prime}}p_{n}^{m,m^{\prime}}.

(70)

These expressions are readily evaluated numerically and were used to produce the plots in Fig.
2
. It is also clear that these expressions are not a convenient starting point to analytically compute the QCS of the state
\hat{\rho}
.

Useful properties of Wigner functions

Setting
\hbar=1
, the Weyl transform
\tilde{A}(x,p)
of a linear operator
\hat{A}
is defined as

\tilde{A}(x,p)={1\over 2\pi}\int\langle x-{y\over 2}|\hat{A}|x+{y\over 2}\rangle\,e^{ipy}\,dy.

(71)

Applied to a density operator
\hat{\rho}
, the Weyl transform gives the Wigner function

W(x,p)={1\over 2\pi}\int\langle x-{y\over 2}|\hat{\rho}|x+{y\over 2}\rangle\,e^{ipy}\,dy,

(72)

while the Weyl transform of the identity operator
\hat{\openone}
is simply equal to the constant function
1/2\pi
. Note also that the Weyl transforms of operators
f(\hat{x})
and
g(\hat{p})
are
f(x)/2\pi
and
g(p)/2\pi
, respectively, where
f
and
g
are arbitrary functions. For any two linear operators
\hat{A}_{1}
and
\hat{A}_{2}
, the overlap formula reads

\mathrm{Tr}(\hat{A}_{1}\,\hat{A}_{2})=2\pi\int\tilde{A}_{1}(x,p)\,\tilde{A}_{2}(x,p)\,dx\,dp,

(73)

which implies, for example, that

\mathrm{Tr}(\hat{\rho})=\int W(x,p)\,dx\,dp.

(74)

In order to link the purity with the Wigner function of the reduced state
\hat{\rho}_{d}
in the “difference” mode (see Sec.
V
), we need to compute the Weyl transforms of the parity operator
(-1)^{\hat{n}}/\pi
, namely

\displaystyle{1\over 2\pi}\int\langle x-{y\over 2}|{(-1)^{\hat{n}}\over\pi}|x+{y\over 2}\rangle\,e^{ipy}\,dy

(75)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over 2\pi^{2}}\int\langle x-{y\over 2}|-x-{y\over 2}\rangle\,e^{ipy}\,dy,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\delta(2x)\over 2\pi^{2}}\int e^{ipy}\,dy,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\delta(x)\delta(p)\over 2\pi},

where the first equality is obtained by using the evolution equation and we have used the identity
\int e^{ipy}\,dy=2\pi\,\delta(p)
. Hence, using the overlap formula (
73
), we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\hat{\rho}\,(-1)^{\hat{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\pi\int W(x,p)\,{\delta(x)\delta(p)\over 2\pi}\,dx\,dp,

(76)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W(0,0),

which proves the well-known fact that the expectation value of the photon number parity is proportional to the value of the Wigner function at the origin. This fact has already been used to reconstruct a quantum state using unbalanced homodyne detection in Refs.
Wallentowitz and Vogel 1996
;
Banaszek and Wódkiewicz 1996
.

In order to express the QCS in terms of the Wigner function of state
\hat{\rho}_{d}
(see Sec.
V
), we also need to compute the Weyl transform of the operator
({\hat{x}}^{2}+{\hat{p}}^{2})\,(-1)^{\hat{n}}/\pi
and then apply the overlap formula. We first calculate the Weyl transform of
{\hat{x}}^{2}\,(-1)^{\hat{n}}/\pi
, namely

\displaystyle{1\over 2\pi}\int\langle x-{y\over 2}|{{\hat{x}}^{2}\,(-1)^{\hat{n}}\over\pi}|x+{y\over 2}\rangle\,e^{ipy}\,dy

(77)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over 2\pi^{2}}\int\left(x-{y\over 2}\right)^{2}\langle x-{y\over 2}|-x-{y\over 2}\rangle\,e^{ipy}\,dy,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\delta(2x)\over 8\pi^{2}}\int y^{2}\,e^{ipy}\,dy,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{\delta(x)\,\delta^{\prime\prime}(p)\over 8\pi},

where we have used the identity
\int y^{2}\,e^{ipy}\,dy=-2\pi\,\delta^{\prime\prime}(p)
.
Using the overlap formula, we then obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\hat{\rho}\,{\hat{x}}^{2}\,(-1)^{\hat{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\pi\int W(x,p)\,{\delta(x)\,\delta^{\prime\prime}(p)\over 8\pi}\,dx\,dp,

(78)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{1\over 4}{\partial^{2}W\over\partial p^{2}}\bigg|_{x=0,p=0},

where we have used the identity

\int f(x)\,\delta^{\prime\prime}(x)\ dx=f^{\prime\prime}(0)
. Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\hat{\rho}\,{\hat{p}}^{2}\,(-1)^{\hat{n}}\right)=-{1\over 4}{\partial^{2}W\over\partial x^{2}}\bigg|_{x=0,p=0},

(79)

so that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\hat{\rho}\,(\hat{x}^{2}+\hat{p}^{2})\,(-1)^{\hat{n}}\right)=-{1\over 4}\Delta W\bigg|_{x=0,p=0},

(80)

where
\Delta
stands for the Laplacian.

Appendix E
Gaussian purity, overlap, and QCS

Let us show how the known formulas of the purity, overlap, and QCS of Gaussian states can be painlessly rederived from the phase-space expressions for these quantities that we obtained in Sec
V
. To do this, we start from the expression of the Wigner function of a Gaussian state centered at origin, namely,

W(x,p)=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{\det\gamma}}\exp^{\left(-\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{r}^{T}\gamma^{-1}\,\mathbf{r}\right)},

(81)

with
\mathbf{r}=(x,p)^{T}
and where
\gamma
is the covariance matrix

\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{x}^{2}&\sigma_{xp}\\
\sigma_{xp}&\ \sigma_{p}^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(82)

Here,
\sigma_{x}^{2}
and
\sigma_{p}^{2}
are the variances of the
x
and
p
quadratures, respectively, and
\sigma_{xp}
stands for the covariance. It is sufficient to consider a centered state here since the purity and QCS are both invariant under displacements in phase-space (and displacements are easy to account for in the overlap between two states).

E.1
Purity

We can recover the expression of the purity by using Eq. (
37
).
If
\hat{\rho}
is a Gaussian state centered at origin, then
\hat{\rho}_{d}=\hat{\rho}
because the product of two identical Gaussian states impinging on a beam splitter remains unchanged
Weedbrook et al. 2012
. Thus,
W_{d}(0,0)=1/(2\pi\sqrt{\det\gamma})
according to Eq. (
81
), so that we have, from Eq. (
37
),

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left(\hat{\rho}^{2}\right)=\pi\,W_{d}(0,0)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\det\gamma}},

(83)

which is indeed the usual formula for the purity of a Gaussian state
Weedbrook et al. 2012
.

Interestingly enough, it then follows from Eq. (
9
) that, for a centered Gaussian state,

\mathrm{Tr}\left(\hat{\rho}^{2}\right)=\mathrm{Tr}\left(\hat{\rho}\,(-1)^{\hat{n}}\right).

E.2
Overlap

We now use Eq. (
37
) in the special case where
\hat{\rho}_{a}
and
\hat{\rho}_{b}
are two Gaussian states (both assumed to be centered for simplicity).
First note that, if
\hat{\rho}_{a}
(
\hat{\rho}_{b}
) is characterized by the covariance matrix
\gamma_{a}
(
\gamma_{b}
), then the output state
\hat{\rho}_{d}
of the 50:50 beam splitter is a centered Gaussian state with covariance matrix
\gamma_{d}=(\gamma_{a}+\gamma_{b})/2

Weedbrook et al. 2012
.
Its Wigner function as given by Eq. (
81
) admits the value at origin

W_{d}(0,0)=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{\det\gamma_{d}}}=\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{\det(\gamma_{a}+\gamma_{b})}}.

(84)

Using Eq. (
37
), we then find

\mathrm{Tr}(\hat{\rho}_{a}\,\hat{\rho}_{b})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det(\gamma_{a}+\gamma_{b})}},

(85)

which is the well-known formula for the overlap between two centered Gaussian states
Marian and Marian 2012
.

E.3
Quadrature coherence scale

If the input state is Gaussian, then
W_{d}
is simply equal to the Wigner function of the input state and has the form of Eq. (
81
). In that case, the Laplacian at origin is easily expressed as:

\displaystyle\Delta W_{d}\big|_{x=0,p=0}=-\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\gamma^{-1})}{2\pi\sqrt{\det\gamma}},

(86)

while
W_{d}(0,0)=1/(2\pi\sqrt{\det\gamma})
, so that we conclude that
the QCS of a Gaussian state of covariance matrix
\gamma
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{2}(\hat{\rho})=-{1\over 4}{\Delta W_{d}\over W_{d}}\bigg|_{x=0,p=0}={1\over 4}\,\mathrm{Tr}(\gamma^{-1}),

(87)

in agreement with the expression proven in Ref.
Hertz and De Bièvre 2020
.
