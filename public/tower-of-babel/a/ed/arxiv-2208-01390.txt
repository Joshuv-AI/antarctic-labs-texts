A Uniform Preconditioner for a Newton Algorithm for Total-Variation Minimization and Minimum-surface Problems
Xue-Cheng Tai, Ragnar Winther, Xiaodi Zhang, Weiying Zheng

Abstract
Solution methods for the nonlinear partial differential equation of the Rudin-Osher-Fatemi (ROF) and minimum-surface models are fundamental for many modern applications. Many efficient algorithms have been proposed. First order methods are common. They are popular due to their simplicity and easy implementation. Some second order Newton-type iterative methods have been proposed like Chan-Golub-Mulet method. In this paper, we propose a new Newton–Krylov solver for primal-dual finite element discretization of the ROF model. The method is so simple that we just need to use some diagonal preconditioners during the iterations. Theoretically, the proposed preconditioners are further proved to be robust and optimal with respect to the mesh size, the penalization parameter, the regularization parameter, and the iterative step, essentially it is a parameter independent preconditioner. We first discretize the primal-dual system by using mixed finite element methods, and then linearize the discrete system by Newton’s method. Exploiting the well-posedness of the linearized problem on appropriate Sobolev spaces equipped with proper norms, we propose block diagonal preconditioners for the corresponding system solved with the minimum residual method. Numerical results are presented to support the theoretical results.



keywords

ROF model; primal-dual; finite element method; Newton method; block preconditioners

1
Introduction

Image restoration is a fundamental and challenging task in image processing. A surge of research has been done in variational and PDE-based
approaches. The ROF model due to Rudin, Osher and Fatemi
[
26
]

is one of the most successfully and widely used mathematical models.
Given an image
f:\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R},\,d=1,2,3
,
the ROF model is trying to solve the following minimization problem:

\min_{v}\left\{E(v)=\int_{\Omega}\alpha|\nabla v|{\rm d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}(v-f)^{2}{\rm d}x\right\},

(1)

where
f
is the observed image,
\alpha>0

is the penalization parameter which controls the trade-off between
goodness-of-fit and visibility in its minimizer
u
. The
Euler-Lagrange equation for the minimization problem (
1
)
can be formally written as

-\alpha\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{\nabla u}{|\nabla u|}\right)+u-f=0\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega,\qquad\nabla u\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}=0\quad\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega.

(2)

Here and after,
{\boldsymbol{n}}
denotes the unit outer normal vector of
\partial\Omega
. This equation indeed characterizes the first-order optimality condition
of (
1
), which is also known as the curvature equation

[
25
]
. As a fundamental well studied model in the literature, the ROF model is important for many modern applications include scientific computing, image processing and data sciences.

To deal with the singularity caused by the
TV-norm minimization in (
1
), the following regularized minimization problem is often studied,

\min_{v}\left\{E_{\beta}(v)=\alpha\int_{\Omega}\sqrt{|\nabla v|^{2}+\beta}{\rm d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}(v-f)^{2}{\rm d}x\right\},

(3)

where the regularization parameter
\beta>0
is typically small. Since the regularized energy functional
E_{\beta}(v)
is strictly convex for
\beta>0
, the minimizer to (
3
) is unique. In
[
1
]
, it has been shown the solution of the regularized problem (
3
)
converges to the solution of (
1
) as
\beta\rightarrow 0
.
The convergence has been established rigorously in
[
35
]
.
It should be noted that this regularization technique is mostly used to compute the minimizer of the total variation energy and its
variants
[
7
,
13
]
.

It is well-known that first integral in (
3
) is the surface area of the graph of function
v
when
\beta=1
. Thus, model (
3
) is essentially the minimum surface problem when
\beta=1
. In this work, we will design a fast algorithm which has good convergence properties uniformly with respect to
\beta
which means that our algorithm works for the regularized total variation minimization model as well as for the minimum surface model.

The Euler-Lagrange equation corresponds to the minimization problem (
3
) reads

-\alpha\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{\nabla u}{\sqrt{|\nabla u|^{2}+\beta}}\right)+u-f=0\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega,\qquad\nabla u\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}=0\quad\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega.

(4)

We want to emphasis once more that our proposed method works uniformly with respect to
\beta\in(0,1]
.

Numerical solution to the minimization problem (
3
) poses a challenging problem due to
the presence of a highly nonlinear and non-differentiable term. To
get around this difficulty, a large effort has been devoted to construct
effective schemes for the minimization problem (
3
) and
the PDE problems (
2
) in the past two decades. For
instance, the artificial time marching scheme in
[
24
,
26
]
,
the lagged diffusivity fixed-point method in
[
15
,
30
]
,
Chan-Golub-Mulet method in
[
12
]
, the Bregman iteration
in
[
16
]
, the augmented Lagrangian technique in
[
32
]

and some others in
[
2
,
4
,
5
,
8
,
21
,
33
]
.
However, most of these numerical algorithms are gradient-decent type
and thus only first-order. Thus, the main motivation of this work
is to develop an effective second-order algorithm for the model (
4
).

It is well-known that a “good” algorithm for the nonlinear problem
should include not only fast iterative methods but also fast linear
solvers for the linear systems obtained after linearization. Solving
the linear systems is usually the most important, challenging and
time-consuming part in the overall simulation, which is due to the
large-scale and ill-conditions of the linear systems. For
the underlying nonlinear PDE problem (
4
), the
condition number of the linear system tends to infinity when the mesh
size is approaching zero. Moreover, the variability of the parameters,
such as the penalization parameter
\alpha
and the regularization parameter
\beta
, can additionally
influence the scale of the condition number of the system. However,
little work has been done to develop robust and efficient solvers
for the resulting linear systems. Here, the term “robust” posses
two underlying merits: (i) The convergence rate is independent of
the mesh size. (ii) The method is robust with respect to parameters.
In order to improve the efficiency of the numerical simulations, we
will also focus on designing robust preconditioners for the linearized
problem.

Based on the discussion carried out in the previous paragraphs, the
purpose of this paper is to propose a preconditioned Newton method
for primal-dual finite element discretization of the ROF model. We
shall adopt a primal-dual formulation of the model (
4
) which contains
the primal variable
u
, the dual variable
{\boldsymbol{p}}
and the multiplier
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
. We first discretize the primal-dual system
by using mixed finite element methods, and then linearize the discrete
nonlinear system by Newton’s method. Following the
operator preconditioning framework in
[
23
]
, we develop
block diagonal preconditioners for the linearized problem.
The derivation exploits its well-posedness on appropriate Sobolev
spaces equipped with proper norms. We also rigorously prove the condition
number of the preconditioned operator is uniformly bounded by a
constant independent of the mesh size, the penalization parameter
\alpha
and the regularization parameter
\beta
. Thus, the proposed preconditioners are robust with respect
to the mesh size, the smoothing parameter, the regularization parameter,
and the iterative step. As a product, we develop a second-order Newton
scheme with robust and optimal preconditioners for the model (
4
).
Finally, numerical experiments are supplied to test the accuracy
of our schemes and validate the uniform robustness of the proposed preconditioners.

We want to emphasis that the essential ideas presented in this work is rather general and can be used for other nonlinear problems. In
[
18
]
, the harmonic map problem was considered. A uniform preconditioner for the Newton iteration was also constructed using operator preconditioning framework published later in
[
23
]
. All these show that the methodology presented here can be used for a large class of problems.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
,
we introduce a primal-dual formulation of the ROF model and present
its Newton iterations. Section
3
is devoted to introducing
the finite element discretization and giving Newton’s
linearization for the discrete problem. In Section
4
,
we derive the well-posedness of the discrete problem on
chosen spaces equipped with subtle norms. We propose and analyze
the robust preconditioners in Section
5
. We carry
out several numerical experiments in Section
6
to
confirm the efficiency of our proposed algorithms. The paper ends
with a concluding remark in Section
7
.

2
Our proposed model and Newton algorithm

First we introduce some Sobolev spaces and norms used in this paper. Throughout the paper, we shall denote vector-valued quantities by boldface notations. Let
L^{2}(\Omega)
be the usual Hilbert space of square integrable functions which is equipped with the following inner product and norm,

\langle u,v\rangle:=\int_{\Omega}u(x)v(x)\mathrm{d}x,\qquad\left\|u\right\|\coloneqq\langle u,u\rangle^{1/2}.

Let
H^{1}(\Omega)
be its subspace with square integrable gradients, and its standard norm.
We also use the space
L^{\infty}
with its canonical norm,
\|v\|_{\infty}=\underset{x\in\Omega}{\operatorname{ess}\sup}|v(x)|
. For a vector
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
we shall use the notation

|\mathbf{x}|_{\beta}=\sqrt{|\mathbf{x}|^{2}+\beta}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}^{2}+\beta}.

For notation simplicity, we will also use
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
throughout this work to denote
L^{2}
-type of inner product for vectors, functions include vector functions and duality pairing. From the context where this notation is used, it is clear which inner product or duality this notation is referring to.
As usual,
\nabla
and
\nabla\cdot
will be used as (distributional) gradient and divergence operators.

Inspired by
[
32
]
, we introduce the auxiliary variable
\boldsymbol{p}=\nabla u
and reformulate (
3
) into an equivalent constrained minimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\boldsymbol{p}},u\\
{\boldsymbol{p}}=\nabla u\end{subarray}}\left\{\int_{\Omega}\alpha|{\boldsymbol{p}}|_{\beta}{\rm d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}(u-f)^{2}{\rm d}x\right\}.

(5)

The constraint condition can be enforced by use of a Lagrange multiplier
\boldsymbol{\mu}
, and we then seek stationary points to the Lagrangian functional

\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{q}},v,\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mu}})=\int_{\Omega}\left(\alpha|{\boldsymbol{q}}|_{\beta}+\frac{1}{2}(v-f)^{2}-\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\cdot({\boldsymbol{q}}-\nabla v)\right){\rm d}x.

Let
({\boldsymbol{p}},u,\boldsymbol{\lambda})
be one saddle point for this problem. As in
[
20
,
22
,
28
]
, the first-order optimality condition for (
5
) is:

\alpha{\boldsymbol{p}}/|{\boldsymbol{p}}|_{\beta}-\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=0,\quad u-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=f,\quad-{\boldsymbol{p}}+\nabla u=0\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega,

(6)

in conjunction with the following boundary
conditions

\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}=\nabla u\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}=0\qquad\text{ on }\partial\Omega,

(7)

Different from the original Euler-Lagrange equation (
4
)
for
u
, this system contains three variables, i.e. functions
u
,
{\boldsymbol{p}}

and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
. Following
[
12
]
, the method is dubbed as the primal-dual method.

To solve the nonlinear system (
6
)-(
7
),
we apply Newton’s method as the linearization technique. For convenience,
we write this system in the compact form
F({\boldsymbol{p}},u,\boldsymbol{\lambda})=0
,
where
F
is the non-linear map given by

F:({\boldsymbol{p}},u,\boldsymbol{\lambda})\mapsto\left(\alpha{\boldsymbol{p}}/|{\boldsymbol{p}}|_{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\lambda},u-f-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\lambda},-{\boldsymbol{p}}+\nabla u\right).

Therefore, Newton’s method to solve this problem is: Given
\left({\boldsymbol{p}}^{n},u^{n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{n}\right)
,
compute
\left({\boldsymbol{p}}^{n+1},u^{n+1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{n+1}\right)
by

\left({\boldsymbol{p}}^{n+1},u^{n+1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{n+1}\right)=\left({\boldsymbol{p}}^{n},u^{n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{n}\right)+\left(\delta{\boldsymbol{p}}^{n},\delta u^{n},\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{n}\right),

(8)

where the correction
\left(\delta{\boldsymbol{p}}^{n},\delta u^{n},\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{n}\right)

is defined by

DF\left({\boldsymbol{p}}^{n},u^{n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{n}\right)\left(\delta{\boldsymbol{p}}^{n},\delta u^{n},\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{n}\right)=-F\left({\boldsymbol{p}}^{n},u^{n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{n}\right).

(9)

As usual,
DF\left({\boldsymbol{p}}^{n},u^{n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{n}\right)
is the
Fréchet derivative of the operator
F
at
\left({\boldsymbol{p}}^{n},u^{n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{n}\right)
.
For a given vector field
{\boldsymbol{r}}
, for simplicity, assume that

{\boldsymbol{r}}(x)\neq 0
for all
x\in\Omega,
define the matrix valued
function
H({\boldsymbol{r}})=H({\boldsymbol{r}}(x))
by

H({\boldsymbol{r}})=\frac{1}{|{\boldsymbol{r}}|_{\beta}}\left(\mathrm{I}-\frac{{\boldsymbol{r}}{\boldsymbol{r}}^{t}}{|{\boldsymbol{r}}|_{\beta}^{2}}\right).

Here
{\boldsymbol{r}}^{t}
denotes the transpose of
{\boldsymbol{r}}
. It
is easy to see that the matrix
H({\boldsymbol{r}})
is symmetric and satisfies

\frac{\beta}{|{\boldsymbol{r}}|_{\beta}^{3}}\leq\frac{H({\boldsymbol{r}})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}}\leq\frac{1}{|{\boldsymbol{r}}|_{\beta}},\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\left\{\boldsymbol{0}\right\}.

(10)

In addition, the matrix
H({\boldsymbol{r}})
is uniformly elliptic provided
that
{\boldsymbol{r}}\in\boldsymbol{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)
.

As a consequence, for the
n
-th step of the Newton iteration the
residual system (
9
) can be written as:

\begin{cases}\alpha H({\boldsymbol{p}}^{n})\delta{\boldsymbol{p}}^{n}-\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{n}&={\boldsymbol{r}}^{n}_{{\boldsymbol{p}}},\\
\delta u^{n}-\nabla\cdot\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{n}&=r^{n}_{u},\\
-\delta{\boldsymbol{p}}^{n}+\nabla\delta u^{n}&={\boldsymbol{r}}^{n}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},\end{cases}

(11)

where the right-hand sides are given by

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{r}}^{n}_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq-\alpha{\boldsymbol{p}}^{n}/|{\boldsymbol{p}}^{n}|_{\beta}+\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{n},

\displaystyle r^{n}_{u}

\displaystyle\coloneqq f-u^{n}+\nabla\cdot\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{n},

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{r}}^{n}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq{\boldsymbol{p}}^{n}-\nabla u^{n}.

In subsequent sections, additional details on the iterations will
be provided.

3
Finite element discretization

In this subsection, we introduce the finite element discretization
of the system (
6
). Let
\mathcal{T}_{h}
be a quasi-uniform
and shape-regular simplex mesh of
\Omega
with mesh size
h
.
For any integer
k\geq 0,T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, let
P_{k}(T)
be the space of polynomials
of degree
k
, and define
\boldsymbol{P}_{k}(T)=(P_{k}(T))^{d}
.
We associate a triple of piecewise polynomial, finite-dimensional
spaces to approximate the solution
\left({\boldsymbol{p}},u,\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right)
,

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{{\boldsymbol{q}}\in\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\Omega):\left.{\boldsymbol{q}}\right|_{T}\in\boldsymbol{P}_{0}(T),\quad\forall T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\right\},

\displaystyle U_{h}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{v\in H^{1}(\Omega):\left.v\right|_{T}\in P_{1}(T),\quad\forall T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\right\},

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{W}}_{h}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\Omega):\left.\boldsymbol{\mu}\right|_{T}\in\boldsymbol{P}_{0}(T),\quad\forall T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\right\}.

Based on the above finite element spaces, the finite element approximation
of the system (
6
) is formulated as follows: Find

\left({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h},u_{h},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}\right)\in{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}\times U_{h}\times{\boldsymbol{W}}_{h}

such that for any
({\boldsymbol{q}}_{h},v_{h},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h})\in{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}\times U_{h}\times{\boldsymbol{W}}_{h}
,

\begin{cases}\langle\alpha{\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}/|{\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}|_{\beta},{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h}\rangle-\langle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{h},{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h}\rangle&=0,\\
\langle u_{h},v_{h}\rangle+\langle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{h},\nabla v_{h}\rangle&=\langle f,v_{h}\rangle,\\
-\langle{\boldsymbol{p}}_{h},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h}\rangle+\langle\nabla u_{h},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h}\rangle&=0.\end{cases}

(12)

Since we are interested in developing fast solvers for the discrete
problem, we do not elaborate on the well-posedness of (
12
)
and simply assume that it has a unique solution.

We shall apply the Newton algorithm (
9
) to the above finite element system as well.
Let
\left({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}^{n},u_{h}^{n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}^{n}\right)\in{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}\times U_{h}\times{\boldsymbol{W}}_{h}

be the approximate solutions of (
12
) at the
n
-th Newton
iteration. From the linearization in (
11
), for each
step of the Newton iteration, the residual equation reads: Find
\left(\delta{\boldsymbol{p}}^{n}_{h},\delta u^{n}_{h},\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{n}_{h}\right)\in{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}\times U_{h}\times{\boldsymbol{W}}_{h}

such that for any
({\boldsymbol{q}}_{h},v_{h},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h})\in{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}\times U_{h}\times{\boldsymbol{W}}_{h}
,

\begin{cases}\langle\alpha H({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}^{n})\delta{\boldsymbol{p}}^{n}_{h},{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h}\rangle-\langle\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{n}_{h},{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h}\rangle&=R^{n}_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}\left({\boldsymbol{q}}_{h}\right),\\
\langle\delta u^{n}_{h},v_{h}\rangle+\langle\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{n}_{h},\nabla v_{h}\rangle&=R^{n}_{u}\left(v_{h}\right),\\
-\langle\delta{\boldsymbol{p}}^{n}_{h},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h}\rangle+\langle\nabla\delta u^{n}_{h},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h}\rangle&=R^{n}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h}\right),\end{cases}

(13)

where the residual functionals are defined by

\displaystyle R^{n}_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}\left({\boldsymbol{q}}_{h}\right)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\langle-\alpha{\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}^{n}/|{\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}^{n}|_{\beta}+\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{h}^{n},{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h}\rangle,

\displaystyle R^{n}_{u}\left(v_{h}\right)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\langle f-u_{h}^{n},v_{h}\rangle-\langle\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}^{n},\nabla v_{h}\rangle,

\displaystyle R^{n}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h}\right)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\langle{\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}^{n}-\nabla u_{h}^{n},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h}\rangle.

Afterwards, the new solution
\left({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}^{n+1},u_{h}^{n+1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}^{n+1}\right)

is given by

\left({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}^{n+1},u_{h}^{n+1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}^{n+1}\right)=\left({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}^{n},u_{h}^{n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}^{n}\right)+\left(\delta{\boldsymbol{p}}^{n}_{h},\delta u^{n}_{h},\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{n}_{h}\right).

(14)

Given a
{\boldsymbol{r}}\in{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}
, we define the bilinear form
a_{\boldsymbol{r}}(\cdot,\cdot)
as:

\displaystyle a_{{\boldsymbol{r}}}\left(({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h},u_{h},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}),({\boldsymbol{q}}_{h},v_{h},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h})\right)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\langle\alpha H({\boldsymbol{r}}){\boldsymbol{p}}_{h},{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h}\rangle-\langle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{h},{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h}\rangle+\langle u_{h},v_{h}\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad+\langle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{h},\nabla v_{h}\rangle-\langle{\boldsymbol{p}}_{h},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h}\rangle+\langle\nabla u_{h},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h}\rangle,

and the residual functional
\ell_{h}(\cdot)
as:

\ell({\boldsymbol{q}}_{h},v_{h},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h})\coloneqq R^{n}_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}\left({\boldsymbol{q}}_{h}\right)+R^{n}_{u}\left(v_{h}\right)+R^{n}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h}\right).

Write
X_{h}\coloneqq{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}\times U_{h}\times{\boldsymbol{W}}_{h}
, then the problem (
13
) can be equivalently written as:
Given
\left({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}^{n},u_{h}^{n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}^{n}\right)\in X_{h}
,
find
\left(\delta{\boldsymbol{p}}^{n}_{h},\delta u^{n}_{h},\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{n}_{h}\right)\in X_{h}

such that

a_{{\boldsymbol{p}}^{n}_{h}}\left((\delta{\boldsymbol{p}}^{n}_{h},\delta u^{n}_{h},\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{n}_{h}),({\boldsymbol{q}}_{h},v_{h},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h})\right)=\ell({\boldsymbol{q}}_{h},v_{h},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h}),\quad\forall({\boldsymbol{q}}_{h},v_{h},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h})\in X_{h}.

(15)

For a given
{\boldsymbol{r}}\in{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}
, we introduce the operator
\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}}):X_{h}\rightarrow X_{h}^{*}
defined by

\langle\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h},u_{h},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}),({\boldsymbol{q}}_{h},v_{h},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h})\rangle\coloneqq a_{{\boldsymbol{r}}}\left(({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h},u_{h},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}),({\boldsymbol{q}}_{h},v_{h},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h})\right),\,\forall({\boldsymbol{p}}^{n}_{h},u^{n}_{h},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{n}_{h}),\,({\boldsymbol{q}}_{h},v_{h},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h})\in X_{h}.

(16)

We remind that
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
here refers to the duality pairing between
X_{h}^{*}
and
X_{h}
. Roughly speaking,
\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})
is the discrete version of the following operator,

\mathcal{A}({\boldsymbol{r}})\coloneqq\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\alpha H({\boldsymbol{r}})&0&-\mathbf{I}\\
0&\mathrm{I}&-\nabla\cdot\\
-\mathbf{I}&\nabla&0\end{array}\right).

(17)

We first note the form
\langle\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\cdot,\cdot\rangle

is symmetric, reflecting the symmetry of the saddle point operator

\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})
. Besides that, for the
n
-th step of
the Newton iteration, we need to solve a linear system with the operator

\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}^{n})
as the coefficient matrix. Since the operator
\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})
is indefinite and symmetric, we solve the linear system by the minimum residual method (MINRES)
[
23
]
. However, as one of the Krylov space methods, the convergence rate of MINRES depends on the condition number and the spectrum of
\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})
.
This motivates
the study of efficient preconditioners for the operator
\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})
.

Remark 3.1
.

Using the inverse estimate in the finite element space
{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}
,
we have

\left\|{\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}\right\|_{\infty}\leq C_{h}\left\|{\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}\right\|,

(18)

where the embedding constant
C_{h}
depends on the
mesh size but is finite. Thus,
H({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}^{n})\in{\boldsymbol{L}}^{\infty}(\Omega)
is uniformly elliptic, c.f (
10
). Accordingly, problems (
12
) and (
13
)
are well-defined.

4
Well-posedness of the linear system for the Newton algorithm

Now we discuss the well-posedness of scheme (
15
), which
is the foundation of the preconditioners we proposed. For this end,
we will use Babuška-Brezzi theory to analyze the mapping properties of the
operator
\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})
for any
{\boldsymbol{r}}\in{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}
.

As discussed in
[
23
]
, ensuring that the continuity
constants and the inf–sup constants are independent of
the physical parameters and the discretized parameters is crucial to design robust
block preconditioners for solving our problem. Note that the natural bound
on the saddle point operator
\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})
depends on
the vector field
{\boldsymbol{r}}
. Therefore, we equip the space
X_{h}

with the following
{\boldsymbol{r}}
-dependent inner-product

\left(({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h},u_{h},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}),({\boldsymbol{q}}_{h},v_{h},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h})\right)_{X_{r}}\coloneqq\alpha\langle H({\boldsymbol{r}}){\boldsymbol{p}}_{h},{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h}\rangle+\langle u_{h},v_{h}\rangle+\alpha\langle H({\boldsymbol{r}})\nabla u_{h},\nabla v_{h}\rangle+\alpha^{-1}\langle H({\boldsymbol{r}})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h}\rangle,

(19)

and norm

\left\|({\boldsymbol{q}}_{h},v_{h},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h})\right\|_{X_{r}}^{2}\coloneqq\alpha\langle H({\boldsymbol{r}}){\boldsymbol{q}}_{h},{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h}\rangle+\left\|v_{h}\right\|^{2}+\alpha\langle H({\boldsymbol{r}})\nabla v_{h},\nabla v_{h}\rangle+\alpha^{-1}\langle H({\boldsymbol{r}})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h}\rangle.

(20)

It is clear that the corresponding norm for the dual space
X_{h}^{*}

depends on
{\boldsymbol{r}}
and
\alpha
. Thanks to the estimate (
18
)
and (
10
), the matrix
H({\boldsymbol{r}})
is uniformly
elliptic and invertible. Hence, the weighted norm (
20
)
is well-defined in
X_{h}
.

After the introduction of these notations, we are able to show that
the operator
\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})
has the following properties.

Lemma 4.1

(Boundedness of
\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})
)
.

There is a positive constant
c_{0}
, independent
of
{\boldsymbol{r}},\alpha,\beta
and
h
, such that for all
({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h},u_{h},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h})
,
({\boldsymbol{q}}_{h},v_{h},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h})\in X_{h}
,
we have

\langle\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h},u_{h},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}),({\boldsymbol{q}}_{h},v_{h},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h})\rangle\leq c_{0}\left\|({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h},u_{h},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h})\right\|_{X_{r}}\left\|({\boldsymbol{q}}_{h},v_{h},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h})\right\|_{X_{r}}.

Proof.

This follows directly from the definition of the operator
\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})

and the definition of
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{X_{r}}
with

c_{0}=2
.
∎

We define the associated kernel space
Z_{h}\subset{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}\times U_{h}

by

Z_{h}=\left\{({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h},u_{h})\in{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}\times U_{h}\mid\langle\nabla u_{h}-{\boldsymbol{p}}_{h},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h}\rangle=0,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h}\in{\boldsymbol{W}}_{h}\right\}.

Note that
\nabla u_{h}\in{\boldsymbol{W}}_{h}
and
\boldsymbol{p}_{h}\in{\boldsymbol{W}}_{h}
,
thus taking
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h}=\nabla u_{h}-{\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}
gives
{\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}=\nabla u_{h}
.
Consequently, the discrete kernel space
Z_{h}
is further portrayed
as

Z_{h}=\left\{({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h},u_{h})\in{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}\times U_{h}\mid{\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}=\nabla u_{h}\right\}.

Lemma 4.2

(Coercivity on the kernel space)
.

There is a positive constant
c_{1}
, independent
of
{\boldsymbol{r}},\alpha,\beta
and
h
, such that for all
({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h},u_{h})\in Z_{h}
,
we have

\langle\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h},u_{h},0),({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h},u_{h},0)\rangle\geq c_{1}\left\|({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h},u_{h},0)\right\|_{X_{r}}^{2}.

Proof.

Just observe that on
Z_{h}

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h},u_{h},0),({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h},u_{h},0)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\alpha H({\boldsymbol{r}}){\boldsymbol{p}}_{h},{\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}\rangle+\left\|u_{h}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\langle\alpha H({\boldsymbol{r}}){\boldsymbol{p}}_{h},{\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}\rangle+\left\|u_{h}\right\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\langle\alpha H({\boldsymbol{r}})\nabla u_{h},\nabla u_{h}\rangle,

and that

\left\|({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h},u_{h},0)\right\|_{X_{r}}^{2}=\langle\alpha H({\boldsymbol{r}}){\boldsymbol{p}}_{h},{\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}\rangle+\left\|u_{h}\right\|^{2}+\alpha\langle H({\boldsymbol{r}})\nabla u_{h},\nabla u_{h}\rangle.

Then the desired inequality holds with
c_{1}=1/2
.
∎

Lemma 4.3

(Inf-sup condition)
.

There is a positive constant
c_{2}
, independent
of
{\boldsymbol{r}},\alpha,\beta
and
h
, such that

\sup_{({\boldsymbol{q}}_{h},v_{h})\in{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}\times U_{h}}\frac{\langle\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})(0,0,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}),({\boldsymbol{q}}_{h},v_{h},0)\rangle}{\left\|({\boldsymbol{q}}_{h},v_{h},0)\right\|_{X_{r}}}\geq c_{2}\left\|(0,0,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h})\right\|_{X_{r}},\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in{\boldsymbol{W}}_{h}.

Proof.

We have that

\displaystyle\sup_{({\boldsymbol{q}}_{h},v_{h})\in{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}\times U_{h}}\frac{\langle\mathcal{A}_{h}({{\boldsymbol{r}}})(0,0,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}),({\boldsymbol{q}}_{h},v_{h},0)\rangle}{\left\|({\boldsymbol{q}}_{h},v_{h},0)\right\|_{X_{r}}}

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{({\boldsymbol{q}}_{h},0)\in{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}\times U_{h}}\frac{-\langle\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h},{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h}\rangle}{\left(\alpha\langle H({{\boldsymbol{r}}}){\boldsymbol{q}}_{h},{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h}\rangle\right){}^{1/2}}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\alpha^{-1}\langle H({\boldsymbol{r}})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}\rangle\right)^{1/2},

where the last inequality follows by taking
{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h}=-\alpha^{-1}H({{\boldsymbol{r}}})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}
.
Since

\left\|(0,0,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h})\right\|_{X_{r}}=\left(\alpha^{-1}\langle H({{\boldsymbol{r}}})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}\rangle\right)^{1/2}.

This shows that the desired inequality holds with
c_{2}=1
.
∎

With the three lemmas above, we obtain the main result of this section.

Theorem 4.1
.

At each iteration for the Newton updating, the discretized
problem (
13
) is well-posed.

Proof.

By similar arguments of Theorem 5.2 in
[
23
]
, it is
straight-forward to reach the conclusion.
∎

Using adequately weighted spaces, we have that
\mathcal{A}_{h}({{\boldsymbol{r}}})

is an isomorphism from
X_{h}
to
X_{h}^{*}
such that
\left\|\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\right\|_{\mathcal{L}\left(X_{h},X_{h}^{*}\right)}

and
\left\|\left(\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\right)^{-1}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}\left(X_{h}^{*},X_{h}\right)}

are bounded independently of mesh sizes and the parameters. That is

\left\|\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\right\|_{\mathcal{L}\left(X_{h},X_{h}^{*}\right)}\leq C,\quad\left\|\left(\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\right)^{-1}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}\left(X_{h}^{*},X_{h}\right)}\leq c^{-1},

(21)

where the constants
C
and
c
only depend on
c_{0},c_{1},c_{2}
.

5
Robust preconditioners

In this section, we develop and analyze robust preconditioners. Let
\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})
be the "Riesz-operator"
mapping from
X_{h}^{*}
to
X_{h}
, which is induced by the weighted norm
\|\cdot\|_{X_{r}}
, for given
({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h},u_{h},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h})\in X_{h}^{*}
:

\left(\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h},u_{h},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}),({\boldsymbol{q}}_{h},v_{h},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h})\right)_{X_{r}}\coloneqq\langle({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h},u_{h},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}),({\boldsymbol{q}}_{h},v_{h},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h})\rangle,\quad({\boldsymbol{q}}_{h},v_{h},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h})\in X_{h}.

By using (
19
) and (
20
), we can see that it takes the following explicit form,

\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\alpha H({{\boldsymbol{r}}})&0&0\\
0&S_{{\boldsymbol{r}},h}&0\\
0&0&\alpha^{-1}H({{\boldsymbol{r}}})^{-1}\end{array}\right)^{-1},

(22)

where the operator
S_{{\boldsymbol{r}},h}\colon\,V_{h}\mapsto V_{h}^{*}
is defined by

\langle S_{{\boldsymbol{r}},h}u_{h},v_{h}\rangle\coloneqq\langle u_{h},v_{h}\rangle+\langle\alpha H({{\boldsymbol{r}}})\nabla u_{h},\nabla v_{h}\rangle,\qquad\forall u_{h},v_{h}\in V_{h}.

In fact,
S_{{\boldsymbol{r}},h}
is the finite element discretization for the operator
{\rm I}-\nabla\cdot\left(H\left({\boldsymbol{r}}\right)\nabla\right)
with Neumann boundary conditions.
Note that the matrix
H({\boldsymbol{r}})
is symmetric and uniformly elliptic, the second block,
S_{{\boldsymbol{r}},h}
,
is invertible. Following the operator preconditioning framework
[
23
]
,
the operator
\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})
is proposed as the preconditioner
for
\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})
.

In the following we estimate the condition number of the preconditioned operator
\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})
using the following formula:

\kappa\left(\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\right)=\left\|\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\right\|{}_{\mathcal{L}\left(X_{h},X_{h}\right)}\left\|\left(\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\right)^{-1}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}\left(X_{h},X_{h}\right)}.

(23)

Theorem 5.1
.

The condition number
\kappa\left(\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\right)

has a uniform bound, independent
of
{\boldsymbol{r}},\alpha,\beta
and
h
, in the sense that

1\leq\kappa\left(\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\right)\leq C/c,

where
C
and
c
are defined by (
21
).

Proof.

We note that the operator
\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})
has the property
that

\left\|\mathcal{B}_{h}({{\boldsymbol{r}}})\right\|{}_{\mathcal{L}\left(X_{h}^{*},X_{h}\right)}=\left\|\left(\mathcal{B}_{h}({{\boldsymbol{r}}})\right)^{-1}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}\left(X_{h},X_{h}^{*}\right)}=1.

Then we have

\left\|\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\right\|{}_{\mathcal{L}\left(X_{h},X_{h}\right)}=\left\|\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\right\|{}_{\mathcal{L}\left(X_{h},X_{h}^{*}\right)}\leq C,

and

\left\|\left(\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\right)^{-1}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}\left(X_{h},X_{h}\right)}\leq\left\|\left(\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\right)^{-1}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}\left(X_{h}^{*},X_{h}\right)}\left\|\left(\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\right)^{-1}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}\left(X_{h},X_{h}^{*}\right)}\leq c^{-1}.

We get the desired result using (
23
).
∎

This theorem suggests that
\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})
is a “good”
preconditioner for
\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})
in the sense that the
preconditioned operator has a condition number which is independent of
the mesh size (
h
), the penalization parameter (
\alpha
), the regularization
parameter (
\beta
) and the iterative step (
n
). We are using MINRES to solve the preconditioned linear system at each Newton iteration. We have the following convergence result for the MINRES.

Theorem 5.2
.

If
x^{0}
is the the initial value,

x^{m}
is the
m
-th iteration of MINRES method and
x
is the
exact solution, then there exists a constant
\delta\in(0,1)
, only
depending on the condition number
\kappa\left(\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\right)
,
such that

\left\langle\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\left(x-x^{m}\right),\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\left(x-x^{m}\right)\right\rangle^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq 2\delta^{m}\left\langle\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\left(x-x^{0}\right),\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\left(x-x^{0}\right)\right\rangle^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Moreover, an estimate leads to

\delta=\frac{\kappa\left(\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\right)-1}{\kappa\left(\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\right)+1}\leq\frac{C-c}{C+c}.

Proof.

Since the operator
\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\in\mathcal{L}(X^{*}_{h},X_{h})
is symmetric and positive definite,
\left\langle\left(\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\right)^{-1}\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
defines an inner product on
X_{h}
. Furthermore, it is easy to see that the preconditioned operator
\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\in\mathcal{L}(X_{h},X_{h})
is symmetric in this inner product.
So we shall use MINRES method which is defined with respect to the inner product
\left\langle\left(\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\right)^{-1}\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
. The estimate is obtained by applying Theorem 2.2 in
[
23
]
.
∎

The inverse of
S_{{\boldsymbol{r}},h}
needs to solve an elliptic linear problem. It could be costly to solve this elliptic problem, especially for three dimensional prolems:
Thus, we consider the following
inexact preconditioner

\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\alpha H({{\boldsymbol{r}}})&0&0\\
0&\widetilde{S}_{{\boldsymbol{r}},h}^{-1}&0\\
0&0&\alpha^{-1}H({{\boldsymbol{r}}})^{-1}\end{array}\right)^{-1}.

(24)

Here,
\widetilde{S}_{{\boldsymbol{r}},h}
is an operator that is spectrally equivalent to the action of the inverse
of the block
S_{{\boldsymbol{r}},h}
in the following sense:

c_{1,s}\langle S_{{\boldsymbol{r}},h}^{-1}v_{h},v_{h}\rangle\leq\langle\widetilde{S}_{{\boldsymbol{r}},h}v_{h},v_{h}\rangle\leq c_{2,s}\langle S_{{\boldsymbol{r}},h}^{-1}v_{h},v_{h}\rangle\quad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h},

(25)

where the constants
c_{1,s}
and
c_{2,s}
are independent of the
mesh size, the penalization parameter, the regularization parameter
and the iterative step. In the implementation,
\widetilde{S}_{{\boldsymbol{r}},h}
is obtained
by using standard multigrid method, algebraic multigrid method (AMG) or domain
decomposition method. Based on the results in
[
6
,
17
,
34
]
, we think the estimate (
25
) will hold if we use AMG to produce

\tilde{S}_{{\boldsymbol{r}},h}
, and our experiments seem to confirm this. Thus, the proposed preconditioner is very cheap and easy-to-implement.

When using
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})
as the preconditioner, we have the following results regarding the convergence estimate
of the corresponding preconditioned MINRES method.

Theorem 5.3
.

Assume that (
25
) is satisfied, then we have

1\leq\kappa\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}_{h}({{\boldsymbol{r}}})\mathcal{A}_{h}({{\boldsymbol{r}}})\right)\leq\frac{C\hat{c}_{2}}{c\hat{c}_{1}},

where
\hat{c}_{1}=\min\left(c_{1,s},1\right)
and
\hat{c}_{2}=\max\left(c_{2,s},1\right)
.
Moreover, if
x^{0}
is the the initial value,
x^{m}
is the
m
-th
iteration of MINRES method and
x
is the exact solution, then there
exists a constant
\delta\in(0,1)
, only depending on the condition
number
\kappa\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}_{h}({{\boldsymbol{r}}})\mathcal{A}_{h}({{\boldsymbol{r}}})\right)
,
such that

\left\langle\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}_{h}({{\boldsymbol{r}}})\mathcal{A}_{h}({{\boldsymbol{r}}})\left(x-x^{m}\right),\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\left(x-x^{m}\right)\right\rangle^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq 2\delta^{m}\left\langle\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}_{h}({{\boldsymbol{r}}})\mathcal{A}_{h}({{\boldsymbol{r}}})\left(x-x^{0}\right),\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\left(x-x^{0}\right)\right\rangle^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Furthermore, an estimate leads to

\delta=\frac{\kappa\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}_{h}({{\boldsymbol{r}}})\mathcal{A}_{h}({{\boldsymbol{r}}})\right)-1}{\kappa\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}_{h}({{\boldsymbol{r}}})\mathcal{A}_{h}({{\boldsymbol{r}}})\right)+1}\leq\frac{\hat{c}_{2}C-\hat{c}_{1}c}{\hat{c}_{2}C+\hat{c}_{1}c}.

Proof.

We only need to estimate the condition number of the preconditioned
operator
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}_{h}({{\boldsymbol{r}}})\mathcal{A}_{h}({{\boldsymbol{r}}})
, the
result of the convergence of preconditioned MINRES method is obvious. From
(
25
), we can see that
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}_{h}({{\boldsymbol{r}}})

satisfies

\hat{c}_{1}\left(x,x\right)_{\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})}\leq(x,x)_{\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})}\leq\hat{c}_{2}\left(x,x\right)_{\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})}.

(26)

This implies that

\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\left(\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\right)^{-1}\right\|{}_{\mathcal{L}\left(X_{h},X_{h}\right)}\leq\hat{c}_{2},\quad\left\|\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\left(\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\right)^{-1}\right)^{-1}\right\|{}_{\mathcal{L}\left(X_{h},X_{h}\right)}\leq\hat{c}_{1}^{-1}.

Similar to the proof of Theorem
5.1
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\right\|{}_{\mathcal{L}\left(X_{h},X_{h}\right)}=\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\left(\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\right)^{-1}\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\right\|{}_{\mathcal{L}\left(X_{h},X_{h}\right)}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\left(\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\right)^{-1}\right\|{}_{\mathcal{L}\left(X_{h},X_{h}\right)}\|\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\|_{\mathcal{L}\left(X_{h},X_{h}\right)}\leq C\hat{c}_{2},

and

\displaystyle\left\|\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\right)^{-1}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}\left(X_{h},X_{h}\right)}=\left\|\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\left(\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\right)^{-1}\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\right)^{-1}\right\|{}_{\mathcal{L}\left(X_{h},X_{h}\right)}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left\|\left(\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\right)^{-1}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}\left(X_{h},X_{h}\right)}\left\|\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\left(\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})\right)^{-1}\right)^{-1}\right\|{}_{\mathcal{L}\left(X_{h},X_{h}\right)}\leq c^{-1}\hat{c}_{1}^{-1}.

Therefore, we get the estimate of the condition number.
∎

As a consequence, the exact methods for the block solvers in the preconditioner
\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})

can be replaced by inexact methods and still maintain the desired
properties. So far, we have gotten a robust and effective solver when solving the linearized systems. This makes it very cheap
to solve the system for each iteration for Newton updating. Together,
it yields a second-order Newton scheme with robust and optimal preconditioners.

Numerical experiments

In this section, we present some numerical experiments to verify the

two-dimensional examples are reported below. All codes were
written in MATLAB based on the open-source finite element library

i
FEM

14

.

For comparison, we also implement the following fixed point method, which is
also known as Picard method. For this method, given the
n
-th iteration

\left({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}^{n},u_{h}^{n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}^{n}\right)\in{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}\times U_{h}\times{\boldsymbol{W}}_{h}
,

\left({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}^{n+1},u_{h}^{n+1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}^{n+1}\right)\in{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}\times U_{h}\times{\boldsymbol{W}}_{h}

is solved by

\begin{cases}\langle\alpha{\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}^{n+1}/\left|{\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}^{n}\right|_{\beta},{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h}\rangle-\langle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{h}^{n+1},{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h}\rangle&=0\quad\forall{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h}\in{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h},\\
\langle u_{h}^{n+1},v_{h}\rangle+\langle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{h}^{n+1},\nabla v_{h}\rangle-\langle f,v_{h}\rangle&=0\quad\forall v_{h}\in U_{h},\\
-\langle{\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}^{n+1},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h}\rangle+\langle\nabla u_{h}^{n+1},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h}\rangle&=0\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h}\in{\boldsymbol{W}}_{h}.\end{cases}

(27)

With the notation in Section
3
,
its residual form reads: Find
\left(\delta{\boldsymbol{p}}^{n}_{h},\delta u^{n}_{h},\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{n}_{h}\right)\in{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}\times U_{h}\times{\boldsymbol{W}}_{h}

such that for any
({\boldsymbol{q}}_{h},v_{h},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h})\in{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}\times U_{h}\times{\boldsymbol{W}}_{h}
,

\begin{cases}\langle\alpha\widehat{H}({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}^{n})\delta{\boldsymbol{p}}^{n}_{h},{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h}\rangle-\langle\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{n}_{h},{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h}\rangle&=R^{n}_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}\left({\boldsymbol{q}}_{h}\right),\\
\langle\delta u^{n}_{h},v_{h}\rangle+\langle\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{n}_{h},\nabla v_{h}\rangle&=R^{n}_{u}\left(v_{h}\right),\\
-\langle\delta{\boldsymbol{p}}^{n}_{h},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h}\rangle+\langle\nabla\delta u^{n}_{h},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h}\rangle&=R^{n}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h}\right),\end{cases}

(28)

where
\widehat{H}({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}^{n})\coloneqq 1/\left|{\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}^{n}\right|_{\beta}
.
Thus, the new solution
\left({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}^{n+1},u_{h}^{n+1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}^{n+1}\right)

is given by

\left({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}^{n+1},u_{h}^{n+1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}^{n+1}\right)=\left({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}^{n},u_{h}^{n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}^{n}\right)+\left(\delta{\boldsymbol{p}}^{n}_{h},\delta u^{n}_{h},\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{n}_{h}\right).

(29)

Clearly, the difference of Newton method and Picard method lies on
the matrix
H({\boldsymbol{r}})
and the scalar
\widehat{H}({\boldsymbol{r}})
. Note that
\widehat{H}({\boldsymbol{r}})
is bounded above by
1/\beta
and below by zero. Hence, the theory for Newton method in Sections
4
-
5
also applies to the Picard method as long as we replace
H({\boldsymbol{r}})
by
\widehat{H}({\boldsymbol{r}})
in
\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})
,
\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})
. For convenience,
we still use the notation
\mathcal{A}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})
,
\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})
for Picard method. From (
13
), (
28
), (
22
) and (
24
), we know that the costs of per iteration for Picard method and Newton method are almost the same. Thus, for convenience, only the iteration numbers needed by both methods are used for comparison in this section.

6.1
Implementation of block preconditioners

First of all, we discuss some implementation details of the proposed
block preconditioners. To solve the linear system obtained from the
finite element discretization, we use the MINRES method as an outer
iterative solver, with the tolerance for the relative residual in
the energy norm set to
\varepsilon=10^{-10}
. The block preconditioners designed
in Section
5
are used to accelerate the convergence
rate of MINRES. In the following, we implement both exact and inexact
inner solvers,
\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})
.
As we know, inverting the proposed block preconditioners ends up with
inverting diagonal blocks. Therefore, the main difference in implementation
is how to invert the second diagonal block. For the exact preconditioner

\mathcal{B}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})
, we call direct solvers implemented in
MATLAB. While for the inexact preconditioner
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}_{h}({\boldsymbol{r}})
,
we mainly call AMG preconditioned conjugate gradient (PCG) method to
define the operator
\widetilde{S}_{{\boldsymbol{r}},h}
. The tolerance of PCG in terms of
l_{2}
–norm
of the relative residual is
\varepsilon_{0}=10^{-3}
. The AMG we used is the classical algebraic multigrid method with Ruge-Stuben coarsening and standard interpolation
[
14
,
34
]
.

Without specifications, the initial guess
\left({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}^{0},u_{h}^{0},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}^{0}\right)

is taken to be the zero solution and the relative tolerances are set
by
10^{-6}
for the nonlinear iteration. Here the maximal iteration
number of the MINRES solver is set by
N=200
. For the sake of convenience,
we denote
N_{{\rm Newton}}
by the number of Newton’s
iterations,
N_{{\rm Picard}}
by the number of Picard’s
iterations and
N_{{\rm MINRES}}
by the average number of preconditioned
MINRES iterations for solving the linearized problem.

To ensure the
global convergence of the Newton algorithm, we introduce an additional
damping parameter
\theta
. Let
\boldsymbol{b}^{n}
be the vector
representation of the right hand of (
15
) at
n
-th iteration,
we use the classical backtracking line search method, which selects as step length
\theta^{n}
to be the first number in the sequence of
\left\{1/2^{k}\right\}_{k=0}^{\infty}

that satisfies the following criterion

\left\|\boldsymbol{b}^{k+1}\right\|_{l^{2}}\leq\left(1-\sigma\theta_{k}\right)\left\|\boldsymbol{b}^{n}\right\|_{l^{2}},

where
\sigma
is chosen as
10^{-4}
. Thus, the damped Newton-updating
is given by

\left({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}^{n+1},u_{h}^{n+1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}^{n+1}\right)=\left({\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}^{n},u_{h}^{n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}^{n}\right)+\theta^{n}\left(\delta{\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}^{n},\delta u_{h}^{n},\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}^{n}\right).

(30)

This subsection is to report on some numerical experiments.

Example 6.1
.

This example is to test the convergence rate of
finite element solutions and the robustness of the preconditioners
for some smooth problems. The computational domain
\Omega
is set as
(0,1)^{2}
.
The function
f
is chosen so that the exact solutions are given
by

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=\cos\left(\pi x\right)\cos\left(\pi y\right),

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{p}}

\displaystyle=-\pi\left(\sin\left(\pi x\right)\cos\left(\pi y\right),\cos\left(\pi x\right)\sin\left(\pi y\right)\right)^{\top},

Conclusions

In this paper, we propose a preconditioned Newton solver for primal-dual
finite element approximation of total variation minimization and minimum surface problems. We develop some block diagonal preconditioners
for the discrete problems at each Newton iteration, which are robust
with respect to the mesh size, the penalization parameter, the regularization
parameter, and the iterative step. We further prove that the resulting
preconditioned MINRES converges uniformly. The theoretical findings
are demonstrated by numerical experiments.
