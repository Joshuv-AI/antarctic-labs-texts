Dynamic Selection of p-Norm in Linear Adaptive Filtering via Online Kernel-Based Reinforcement Learning


Abstract
This study addresses the problem of selecting dynamically, at each time instance, the “optimal” p-norm to combat outliers in linear adaptive filtering without any knowledge on the potentially time-varying probability density function of the outliers. To this end, an online and data-driven framework is designed via kernel-based reinforcement learning (KBRL). Novel Bellman mappings on reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHSs) are introduced that need no knowledge on transition probabilities of Markov decision processes, and are nonexpansive with respect to the underlying Hilbertian norm. An approximate policy-iteration framework is finally offered via the introduction of a finite-dimensional affine superset of the fixed-point set of the proposed Bellman mappings. The well-known “curse of dimensionality” in RKHSs is addressed by building a basis of vectors via an approximate linear dependency criterion. Numerical tests on synthetic data demonstrate that the proposed framework selects always the “optimal” p-norm for the outlier scenario at hand, outperforming at the same time several non-RL and KBRL schemes.

Introduction

The least-squares (LS) error/loss (between an observed value and its predicted one) plays a
pivotal role in signal processing, e.g., adaptive filtering
[
1
]
, and
machine learning
[
2
]
. For example, the least-mean squares (LMS) and
recursive (R)LS
[
1
]
are two celebrated algorithms in adaptive filtering
and stochastic approximation which are based on the LS-error criterion. Notwithstanding, LS
methods are notoriously sensitive to the presence of outliers within data
[
3
]
,
where outliers are defined as (sparsely) contaminating data that do not adhere to a nominal
data generation model, and are often modeled as random variables (RVs) with non-Gaussian heavy
tailed distributions, e.g.,
\alpha
-stable ones
[
4
,
5
]
. To
combat the negative effects of outliers, several non-LS criteria, such as least mean
p
-power
(LMP)
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
and maximum correntropy (MC)
[
12
]
, have been
studied. This work focuses on the LMP criterion, owing to the well-documented robustness of LMP
against outliers
[
13
]
, while results on MC will be reported elsewhere.

This study builds around the classical data-generation model

y_{n}=\bm{\theta}_{*}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}_{n}+o_{n}
, where
n\in\mathbb{N}
denotes discrete
time (
\mathbb{N}
is the set of all non-negative integers),
\bm{\theta}_{*}
is the
L\times 1

vector whose entries are the system parameters that need to be identified,
o_{n}
is the RV
which models outliers/noise,
(\mathbf{x}_{n},y_{n})
stands for the input-output pair of
available data, where
\mathbf{x}_{n}
is an
L\times 1
vector and
y_{n}
is real-valued, and

\intercal
denotes vector/matrix transposition. For an arbitrarily fixed
\bm{\theta}_{0}
, the
LMP algorithm
[
6
]
generates estimates
(\bm{\theta}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of

\bm{\theta}_{*}
according to the following recursion:

\bm{\theta}_{n+1}\coloneqq\bm{\theta}_{n}+\rho p\lvert e_{n}\rvert^{p-2}e_{n}\mathbf{x}_{n}\,,

(1)

where
e_{n}\coloneqq y_{n}-\mathbf{x}_{n}^{\intercal}\bm{\theta}_{n}
,
\rho
is the learning rate
(step size), and
p
is a
fixed
user-defined real-valued number within the interval

[1,2]
to ensure that the
p
-norm loss

\lvert y_{n}-\mathbf{x}_{n}^{\intercal}\bm{\theta}\rvert^{p}
is a convex function of

\bm{\theta}

[
6
]
. Notice that if
p=1
and
2
, then (
1
) boils
down to the classical sign-LMS and LMS, respectively
[
1
]
.

Intuition suggests that the choice of
p
should be based on the distribution of
o_{n}
in the
data-generation model. For example, if
o_{n}
obeys a Gaussian distribution, then
p=2
should
be chosen (recall the maximum-likelihood criterion). To enhance robustness against outliers,
combination of adaptive filters with different forgetting factors, but with the same fixed

p
-norm, have been also introduced
[
9
]
. However, it seems that an

online
and
data-driven
solution to the problem of
dynamically

selecting
p
,
without
any prior knowledge on the distribution of
o_{n}
, which may

change with time,
is yet to be found.

This work offers a solution to the aforementioned open problem via reinforcement learning
(RL)
[
14
]
; a machine-learning paradigm where an “agent”
interacts with the surrounding environment to identify iteratively the policy which minimizes
the cost of its “actions.” More specifically, the well-known policy-iteration (PI)
framework
[
14
]
of RL is adopted, because of its well-documented
merits (e.g.,
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
) over the alternative
RL frameworks of temporal-difference (TD) and Q-learning
[
14
]
,
especially for continuous and high-dimensional state spaces such as the one considered here. PI
comprises two stages at every iteration
n
:
policy evaluation
and
policy
improvement
. At policy evaluation, the current policy is evaluated by a

Q
-function
[
14
]
, which represents, loosely speaking, the
long-term cost that the agent would suffer had the current policy been used to determine the
next state, whereas at the policy-improvement stage, the agent uses the
Q
-function values to
update the policy. The underlying state space is considered to be continuous and high
dimensional, due to the nature of the available data
(\mathbf{x}_{n},y_{n})
, while the action
space is considered to be discrete: an action is a value of
p
taken from a finite grid of the
interval
[1,2]
.

Deep neural networks offer approximating spaces for Q-functions, e.g.,
[
18
]
, but
they may require processing of batch data (even re-training) during online-mode operation,
since they may face test data generated by probability density functions (PDFs) different
from those of the training ones (dynamic environments). Such batch processing inflicts large
computational times and complexity, discouraging the application of deep neural networks to
online learning where a small complexity footprint is desired.

To meet the desired computational complexity requirements, this study builds an approximate
(A)PI framework for
online
RL along the lines of kernel-based
(KB)RL
[
15
,
16
,
17
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
]
. Central to the API design is the construction
of novel Bellman mappings
[
27
,
14
]
. The proposed
Bellman mappings are defined on a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)

\mathscr{H}

[
28
,
29
]
, which serves as the approximating
space for the
Q
-functions. In contrast to the prevailing route in
KBRL
[
15
,
16
,
17
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
]
, which views Bellman mappings as contractions
in
\mathscr{L}_{\infty}
-norm Banach spaces (by definition, no inner product available), this
study introduces nonexpansive
[
30
]
Bellman operators on
\mathscr{H}
to
capitalize on the reproducing property of the inner product of

\mathscr{H}

[
28
,
29
]
, and to open the door to powerful
Hilbertian tools
[
30
]
. This path offers also flexibility to the user to choose
any point from the potentially non-singleton fixed-point set of the nonexpansive Bellman
mapping, as opposed to the case of a contraction mapping which is known to have a unique fixed
point. A superset of the fixed-point set of one of the proposed Bellman mappings is designed
onto which the proposed API framework is based. To robustify the policy-improvement stage, the
well-known methodology of rollout
[
14
]
is employed. Moreover, to
address the issue of the “curse of dimensionality” that arises naturally in online learning
in RKHSs, the proposed framework is equipped with the approximate linear dependency (ALD)
criterion
[
31
]
. Note that
[
19
,
26
]
, being along
the lines of TD and Q-learning, do not include any discussion on Bellman mappings.

Unlike the classical Bellman operators, where information on transition probabilities of a
Markov decision process is needed
[
14
]
, the proposed Bellman
mappings need neither such information nor any training/offline data and past policies, but
sample and average the state space on-the-fly, at each
n
, to
explore
the
surrounding environment. This suits the current adaptive-filtering setting, where the presence
of outliers, with a possibly time-varying PDF, may render the information obtained offline or
from past policies outdated. As such, the proposed Bellman mappings fall closer
to
[
16
]
than to studies which use training data collected beforehand
(offline), e.g.,
[
32
,
17
,
33
]
.

It is worth stressing here that the proposed framework, together with its complementary
study
[
34
]
, appear to be the first attempts to apply RL arguments to robust
adaptive filtering. In contrast to
[
34
]
, where the state space is the
low-dimensional
\mathbb{R}^{4}
, this study considers the high-dimensional
\mathbb{R}^{2L+1}
(
\mathbb{R}
is
the set of all real numbers). This work constructs a finite-dimensional affine set as a
superset of the fixed-point set of one of the proposed Bellman mappings, as opposed to

[
34
]
where a potentially infinite-dimensional hyperplane is designed. To reduce
the size of the computational footprint of the proposed framework, ALD is used instead of the
random Fourier features (RFF)
[
35
]
in
[
34
]
. Finally,
rollout
[
14
]
is employed for robustification, whereas experience
replay
[
36
]
is applied in
[
34
]
.

Numerical tests on synthetic data demonstrate that the advocated framework
always

spots the value of
p
that leads to “optimal” performance,
without
any knowledge
on the PDF of the outliers, which is intentionally made to be time varying. Due to space
limitations, any proofs, results on convergence analysis, and further numerical tests will be
reported in the journal version of this paper.

Nonexpansive Bellman Mappings on RKHSs

2.1
State-Action Space

Following (
1
), the state space is defined as the following continuous and
high-dimensional

\mathfrak{S}\coloneqq\{\mathbf{s}\coloneqq(\mathbf{x},y,\bm{\theta}){\mathbin{}\mid\mathbin{}}\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{L},y\in\mathbb{R},\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}\}=\mathbb{R}^{2L+1}
, where
\mathbb{R}

stands for the set of all real numbers. The action space
\mathfrak{A}
is defined as any
finite grid of the interval
[1,2]
, so that an action
a\in\mathfrak{A}
becomes any value of

p
taken from that finite grid. The state-action space is defined as

\mathfrak{Z}\coloneqq\mathfrak{S}\times\mathfrak{A}
, and its element is denoted as

\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{s},a)
.

Along the lines of the general notation in
[
14
]
, consider now the
set of all mappings

\mathscr{M}\coloneqq\{\mu(\cdot)\nonscript\,|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}\mu(\cdot):\mathfrak{S}\to\mathfrak{A}:\mathbf{s}\mapsto\mu(\mathbf{s})\}
. In other words, given a
\mu\in\mathscr{M}
,

\mu(\mathbf{s})
denotes the action that the “system” may take at state
\mathbf{s}
to
“move to” the state
\mathbf{s}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{S}
. The set
\Pi
of policies is
defined as

\Pi\coloneqq\mathscr{M}^{\mathbb{N}}\coloneqq\{(\mu_{0},\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n},\ldots)\nonscript\,|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}\mu_{n}\in\mathscr{M},n\in\mathbb{N}\}
. A policy will be denoted by
\pi\in\Pi
. Given
\mu\in\mathscr{M}
, the stationary policy
\pi_{\mu}\in\Pi
is defined as

\pi_{\mu}\coloneqq(\mu,\mu,\ldots,\mu,\ldots)
. It is customary for
\mu
to denote
also the stationary policy
\pi_{\mu}
.

The one-step loss
g:\mathfrak{Z}\to\mathbb{R}:(\mathbf{s},a)\mapsto g(\mathbf{s},a)
, which
quantifies the cost of transition from the current state
\mathbf{s}
to the next one

\mathbf{s}^{\prime}
under action
a
, is defined for the current setting as:

\displaystyle g(\mathbf{s},a)\coloneqq|y-\bm{\theta}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}|^{a}+|y-\bm{\theta}^{\prime}{}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}|\,,

(2)

where

\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\coloneqq\bm{\theta}+\rho a\lvert y-\bm{\theta}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}\rvert^{a-2}(y-\bm{\theta}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x})\mathbf{x}
, according to
(
1
). Recall that action
a
is defined to take certain values of
p
from a
user-defined grid in
[1,2]
. The loss in (
2
) is motivated by classical
adaptive filtering
[
1
]
; namely, the first term in (
2
)
resembles the
prior loss,
while the second one mimics the
posterior loss.

Only the prior loss is chosen to be affected by action
a
in (
2
), because

a
applies to the current state
\mathbf{s}
and not
\mathbf{s}^{\prime}
. The long-term loss

Q:\mathfrak{Z}\to\mathbb{R}:(\mathbf{s},a)\mapsto Q(\mathbf{s},a)
quantifies the long-term
cost that the agent would suffer had the action
a
been used to determine the next state of

\mathbf{s}
.

2.2
Novel Bellman Mappings

Central to dynamic programming and RL
[
14
]
is the concept of
Bellman mappings which operate on
Q
-functions. Typical definitions are, e.g.,

[
37
]
,
\forall(\mathbf{s},a)\in\mathfrak{Z}
,

\displaystyle(T_{\mu}^{\diamond}Q)(\mathbf{s},a)

\displaystyle\coloneqq g(\mathbf{s},a)+\alpha\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{s}^{\prime}{\mathbin{}\mid\mathbin{}}(\mathbf{s},a)}\{Q(\mathbf{s}^{\prime},\mu(\mathbf{s}^{\prime}))\}\,,

(3a)

\displaystyle(T^{\diamond}Q)(\mathbf{s},a)

\displaystyle\coloneqq g(\mathbf{s},a)+\alpha\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{s}^{\prime}{\mathbin{}\mid\mathbin{}}(\mathbf{s},a)}\{\min_{a^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{A}}Q(\mathbf{s}^{\prime},a^{\prime})\}\,,

(3b)

where
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{s}^{\prime}{\mathbin{}\mid\mathbin{}}(\mathbf{s},a)}\{\cdot\}
stands for the conditional
expectation operator with respect to
\mathbf{s}^{\prime}
conditioned on
(\mathbf{s},a)
, and

\alpha\in[0,1)
is the discount factor. In the case where
Q
is considered an element of the
Banach space
\mathscr{L}_{\infty}
of all (essentially) bounded
functions
[
38
]
, equipped with the norm
\lVert\>\cdot\>\rVert_{\infty}
, then it can be
shown that the mappings in (
3
) are
contractions
[
14
]
, and according to the Banach-Picard
theorem
[
30
]
, they possess
unique
fixed points

Q^{\diamond}_{\mu},Q^{\diamond}
, i.e., points which solve the Bellman equations

T_{\mu}^{\diamond}Q^{\diamond}_{\mu}=Q^{\diamond}_{\mu}
and

T^{\diamond}Q^{\diamond}=Q^{\diamond}
, and which characterize “optimal” long-term
losses
[
14
]
.

Nevertheless, in most cases of practical interest, there is not sufficient information on the
conditional PDF to compute
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{s}^{\prime}{\mathbin{}\mid\mathbin{}}(\mathbf{s},a)}\{\cdot\}
. Motivated by this fact, this study proposes approximations of the Bellman
mappings in (
3
) by assuming that losses
g
and
Q
belong to an RKHS

\mathscr{H}
, i.e., a Hilbert space with inner product
\langle\cdot\mathop{}|\mathop{}\cdot\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}
,
norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathscr{H}}\coloneqq\langle\cdot\mathop{}|\mathop{}\cdot\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}^{1/2}
, and a
reproducing kernel
\kappa(\cdot,\cdot):\mathfrak{Z}\times\mathfrak{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
, such that

\kappa(\mathbf{z},\cdot)\in\mathscr{H}
,
\forall\mathbf{z}\in\mathfrak{Z}
, and the
reproducing property holds true:

Q(\mathbf{z})=\langle Q\mathop{}|\mathop{}\kappa(\mathbf{z},\cdot)\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}
,

\forall Q\in\mathscr{H}
,
\forall\mathbf{z}\in\mathfrak{Z}
. Space
\mathscr{H}
may be
infinite dimensional; e.g.,
\kappa(\cdot,\cdot)
is a Gaussian kernel
[
28
,
29
]
. For compact notations, let

\varphi(\mathbf{z})\coloneqq\kappa(\mathbf{z},\cdot)
, and

Q^{\intercal}Q^{\prime}\coloneqq\langle Q\mathop{}|\mathop{}Q^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}
.

Hereafter, losses
g,Q
are assumed to belong to
\mathscr{H}
. The proposed Bellman mappings

T_{\mu}:\mathscr{H}\to\mathscr{H}:Q\mapsto T_{\mu}Q
and

T:\mathscr{H}\to\mathscr{H}:Q\mapsto TQ
are defined as:

\displaystyle T_{\mu}Q

\displaystyle\coloneqq g+\alpha\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{N_{\textnormal{av}}}Q(\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{av}}_{j},\mu(\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{av}}_{j}))\cdot\psi_{j}\,,

(4a)

\displaystyle TQ

\displaystyle\coloneqq g+\alpha\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{N_{\textnormal{av}}}\inf\nolimits_{a_{j}\in\mathfrak{A}}Q(\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{av}}_{j},a_{j})\cdot\psi_{j}\,,

(4b)

where

in

Section

\bm{\Psi}\coloneqq[\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{N_{\textnormal{av}}}]

whose

Consider a subset

\mathfrak{B}\coloneqq\{\mathbf{z}^{\textnormal{b}}_{i}\}^{N_{\textnormal{b}}}_{i=1}\subset\mathfrak{Z}

. Define also

) is affine nonexpansive and

in

) is nonexpansive within the Euclidean space

(\mathbb{R}^{N_{\textnormal{b}}},\langle\cdot\mathop{}|\mathop{}\cdot\rangle_{\mathbf{K}_{\textnormal{b}}})
,
where

\langle\bm{\xi}\mathop{}|\mathop{}\bm{\xi^{\prime}}\rangle_{\mathbf{K}_{\textnormal{b}}}\coloneqq\bm{\xi}^{\intercal}\mathbf{K}_{\textnormal{b}}\bm{\xi^{\prime}}
.

Nonexpansivity for a mapping
T
in a (Euclidean) Hilbert space

(\mathscr{H},\langle\cdot\mathop{}|\mathop{}\cdot\rangle_{\mathscr{H}})
means

\lVert TQ-TQ^{\prime}\rVert_{\mathscr{H}}\leq\lVert Q-Q^{\prime}\rVert_{\mathscr{H}}
,

\forall Q,Q^{\prime}\in\mathscr{H}

[
30
]
. Moreover, mapping

T_{\mu}:\mathscr{H}\to\mathscr{H}
is affine iff

T_{\mu}(\lambda Q+(1-\lambda)Q^{\prime})=\lambda TQ+(1-\lambda)TQ^{\prime}
,

\forall Q,Q^{\prime}\in\mathscr{H}
,
\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.

Due to the approximate Bellman mappings offered in (

. The framework operates sequentially, with its

surrounding (

[n]

With

\mathfrak{B}[n-1]=\{\mathbf{z}^{\textnormal{b}}_{i}[n-1]\}^{N_{\textnormal{b}}[n-1]}_{i=1}

n
, and with

\mathscr{H}_{\textnormal{b}}[n-1]=\linspan(\{\varphi(\mathbf{z}^{\textnormal{b}}_{i}[n-1])\}^{N_{\textnormal{b}}[n-1]}_{i=1})

decide whether
\mathfrak{B}[n-1]

\mathfrak{B}[n]
by

\mathbf{s}_{n}=(\mathbf{x}_{n},y_{n},\bm{\theta}_{n})

of

, is tested against
\mathscr{H}_{\textnormal{b}}[n-1]
according to
ALD
[
31
]
. Note also that ALD ensures that vectors in

\mathscr{H}_{\textnormal{b}}[n]
are linearly independent, as needed in

1(ii)
. ALD is also utilized upon the arrival of

to check whether any of
these vectors enter the basis or not.

To utilize (
5a
), an
N_{\textnormal{b}}[n]\times 1
vector
\bm{\eta}_{n}

needs to be identified. According to
1(ii)
, such an
\bm{\eta}_{n}

identifies a vector
\tilde{g}_{n}\coloneqq\bm{\Phi}^{\textnormal{b}}_{n}\bm{\eta}_{n}
in

\mathscr{H}_{\textnormal{b}}[n]

\bm{\eta}_{n}
is chosen such that

[\tilde{g}_{n}(\mathbf{z}_{n})-g(\mathbf{z}_{n})]^{2}
is minimized, where

\mathbf{z}_{n}=(\mathbf{s}_{n},\mu_{n}(\mathbf{s}_{n}))
, and the value
g(\mathbf{z}_{n})
is
provided by (
2
). Here is where the reproducing property of the inner product
in
\mathscr{H}
becomes handy:

\tilde{g}_{n}(\mathbf{z}_{n})=\langle\tilde{g}_{n}\mathop{}|\mathop{}\varphi(\mathbf{z}_{n})\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}=\langle\bm{\Phi}^{\textnormal{b}}_{n}\bm{\eta}_{n}\mathop{}|\mathop{}\varphi(\mathbf{z}_{n})\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}=\mathbf{k}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{z}_{n})\bm{\eta}_{n}
, where

\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{z}_{n})\coloneqq\bm{\Phi}^{\textnormal{b}}_{n}{}^{\intercal}\varphi(\mathbf{z}_{n})=[\kappa(\mathbf{z}^{\textnormal{b}}_{1}[n],\mathbf{z}_{n}),\ldots,\kappa(\mathbf{z}^{\textnormal{b}}_{N_{\textnormal{b}}[n]}[n],\mathbf{z}_{n})]^{\intercal}
. Hence,

\bm{\eta}_{n}
can be chosen as:

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}_{n}\in{}

\displaystyle{}\arg\min\nolimits_{\bm{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\textnormal{b}}[n]}}[\mathbf{k}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{z}_{n})\bm{\eta}-g(\mathbf{z}_{n})]^{2}

\displaystyle{}=\{\bm{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\textnormal{b}}[n]}{\mathbin{}\mid\mathbin{}}\mathbf{k}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{z}_{n})\bm{\eta}=g(\mathbf{z}_{n})\}\,.

(7)

To this end, the following options are provided to choose from:

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}_{n}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\frac{g(\mathbf{z}_{n})}{\mathbf{k}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{z}_{n})\mathbf{K}_{\textnormal{b}}[n]\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{z}_{n})}\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{z}_{n})\,,

(8a)

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}_{n}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}_{n-1}+\frac{g(\mathbf{z}_{n})-\bm{\eta}_{n-1}^{\intercal}\mathbf{K}_{\textnormal{b}}[n]\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{z}_{n})}{\mathbf{k}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{z}_{n})\mathbf{K}_{\textnormal{b}}[n]\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{z}_{n})}\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{z}_{n})\,,

(8b)

where (
8a
) offers the minimum-norm solution of (
7
), while
(

) computes the (metric) projection

]
of

\bm{\eta}_{n-1}
onto the hyperplane in (

N_{\textnormal{b}}[n-1]\times 1

\bm{\eta}_{n-1}
to meet the length

, in case

. Following
1(ii)
, the inner product

\langle\cdot\mathop{}|\mathop{}\cdot\rangle_{\mathbf{K}_{\textnormal{b}}[n]}
was utilized in (
8b
).

To identify
\bm{\Upsilon}_{n}
, needed for the computations in

of

1
,

is followed where
\bm{\Upsilon}_{n}

,

Typically, the greedy rule

\mu_{n}\left(\mathbf{s}_{n}\right)=\arg\min_{a\in\mathfrak{A}}Q_{n}(\mathbf{s}_{n},a)
is
used for policy improvement
[
14
,
32
,
17
]
. However, it has been observed that this greedy rule may lead into instabilities
of the RL agent’s behavior and hinder its cognition about the surrounding environment

[
14
]
. The
\varepsilon
-greedy strategy

[
14
]
, a variant of the greedy one, has not performed well in the

. To address this potential drawback of greedy strategies,

[

]
is employed in

. With the user-defined

\displaystyle\mu_{n}(\mathbf{s}_{n})=\arg\min_{a\in\mathfrak{A}}\Big[

\displaystyle g(\mathbf{s}_{n},a)+\sum\nolimits_{m=1}^{M-1}\alpha^{m}g(\mathbf{s}_{n+m}^{\triangleright},\mu^{\triangleright}(\mathbf{s}_{n+m}^{\triangleright}))

\displaystyle+\alpha^{M}Q_{n}(\mathbf{s}_{n+M}^{\triangleright},\mu^{\triangleright}(\mathbf{s}_{n+M}^{\triangleright}))\Big]\,,

, and

\mathbf{s}_{n}
under action

\mu^{\triangleright}
. A large

(long
trajectory) may inflict long computational times and diminish the effect of

on policy
improvement through
\alpha^{M}
in (

\alpha\leq 1

[
14
]
. Note here that experience
replay
[

]
and not rollout was employed in
[
34
]
