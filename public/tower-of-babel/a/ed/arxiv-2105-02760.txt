Abstract
. Here is a simplified proof that every sharply transitive subset of PGL 2 ⁡ ( K ) \operatorname{PGL}_{2}(K) is a coset of a subgroup.



Let
G
be a group acting on a set
\Omega
on the left.
A subset
S\subseteq G
is called
sharply transitive
, or
regular
,
if for every
x,y\in\Omega
there is a unique
g\in G
such that
gx=y
.
For example, sharply transitive subsets of
S_{n}
can be identified with
n\times n
Latin squares,
while sharply 2-transitive subsets, i.e., sets which are sharply transitive for the action on ordered pairs,
can be identified with affine planes of order
n
.

In this note we consider
G=\operatorname{PGL}_{2}(K)
and its action on the projective line
\mathbf{P}^{1}=\mathbf{P}^{1}(K)
,
where
K
is a finite field of order
q
.
It follows from Dickson’s classification of the subgroups of
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(K)
that the only regular subgroups of
\operatorname{PGL}_{2}(K)
are

1.

cyclic groups
C_{q+1}
,

2.

dihedral groups
D_{(q+1)/2}
(
q
odd),

3.

A_{4}
(
q=11
),

4.

S_{4}
(
q=23
),

5.

A_{5}
(
q=59
)

(see
[
3
]
or
[
8
]
),
and there is a single conjugacy class of subgroups in each case.
Remarkably, other than these subgroups and their cosets, there are no further regular subsets of
G
.

Theorem 1

(
[
1
,
6
,
2
]
)
.

If
S\subseteq\operatorname{PGL}_{2}(K)
is sharply transitive on
\mathbf{P}^{1}
and
1\in S
then
S
is a subgroup.

This result was originally conjectured by Bonisoli in
[
3
]
.
The complete proof is spread across several papers:
in
[
1
,
6
]
, Bader, Lundardon, and Thas classified flocks of the hyperbolic quadric in
\mathbf{P}^{3}
,
and the equivalence with regular sets was noted in
[
2
]
.
The original proof is somewhat involved.
See
[
7
]
for a more recent summary.
A partly simplified proof is given by Durante and Siciliano
[
4
]
, but for the main technical step the reader is referred to
[
6
]
.
Here we give a short, self-contained, direct proof avoiding the detour through flocks.
Still, the interested reader will find close analogies with
[
4
,
6
]
.

The key step is the following lemma, which can be seen as a version of Segre’s “lemma of the tangents” (see
[
5
]
*(2)).

Lemma 2
.

Suppose
S
is a regular subset of
\operatorname{PGL}_{2}(K)
containing the elements

\displaystyle g_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&b_{1}\\
c_{1}&d_{1}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle g_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{2}&0\\
c_{2}&d_{2}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle g_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{3}&b_{3}\\
0&d_{3}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle g_{4}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{4}&b_{4}\\
c_{4}&0\end{pmatrix}.

Then

b_{1}d_{2}a_{3}c_{4}=c_{1}a_{2}d_{3}b_{4}.

Here and below elements of
\operatorname{PGL}_{2}(K)
are written as matrices,
understanding that the expression is determined only up to a scalar.
Elements of
\mathbf{P}^{1}
will be written as
(x:y)
.

Proof.

Assume first that
g_{1},g_{2},g_{3},g_{4}
are distinct. Label the elements of
S
as

\begin{pmatrix}a_{i}&b_{i}\\
c_{i}&d_{i}\end{pmatrix}\qquad(i=1,\dots,q+1).

By regularity, the columns
(a_{i}:c_{i})
and
(b_{i}:d_{i})
both trace out
\mathbf{P}^{1}
.
Since
S^{-1}
is also regular, the same is true of the rows
(a_{i}:b_{i})
and
(c_{i}:d_{i})
.
By excluding
0
and
\infty
in each case, it follows that the products

\prod_{i\neq 1,2}a_{i}/b_{i},\prod_{i\neq 2,4}b_{i}/d_{i},\prod_{i\neq 3,4}d_{i}/c_{i},\prod_{i\neq 1,3}c_{i}/a_{i}

are each equal to the product of all the nonzero elements of
K
, which is
-1
.
By multiplying these together we get

\frac{b_{1}d_{1}}{d_{1}c_{1}}\frac{d_{2}c_{2}}{c_{2}a_{2}}\frac{a_{3}b_{3}}{b_{3}d_{3}}\frac{c_{4}a_{4}}{a_{4}b_{4}}\prod_{i>4}\frac{a_{i}}{b_{i}}\frac{b_{i}}{d_{i}}\frac{d_{i}}{c_{i}}\frac{c_{i}}{a_{i}}=1.

Simplifying gives the claimed relation.

The only possible coincidences between
g_{1},g_{2},g_{3},g_{4}
are
g_{1}=g_{4}
and
g_{2}=g_{3}
.
In these cases the proof is similar.
∎

We can now prove
Theorem

1
.
Let
S\subseteq\operatorname{PGL}_{2}(K)
be a regular set containing
1
.
Let
g,h\in S\setminus\{1\}
.
It suffices to prove
gh\in S
.
By regularity, for each
x\in\mathbf{P}^{1}
there is a unique
k\in S
such that

kh^{-1}x=gx.

Moreover
k\neq h
, because
g
has no fixed points (likewise
k\neq g
).
Since
\mathbf{P}^{1}
has size
q+1
and there are at most
q
possibilities for
k
,
there is some
k\in S
such that
g^{-1}kh^{-1}
has at least two fixed points, say
x
and
y
.
Since
x
and
gx
are distinct, we may coordinatize
\mathbf{P}^{1}
in such a way that

x=(1:0),\quad gx=(0:1).

Since
h
has no fixed points,
h(1:0)\neq(1:0)
, so there is some
u\in\operatorname{PGL}_{2}(K)
of the form

u=\begin{pmatrix}1&*\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}

such that
hu(0:1)=(1:0)
.
For this
u
we have

ku(0:1)=kh^{-1}(1:0)=g(1:0)=(0:1).

Thus
Su
is a regular set containing the elements

\displaystyle gu=\begin{pmatrix}0&b\\
1&*\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle ku=\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\
*&1\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle u=\begin{pmatrix}1&*\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle hu=\begin{pmatrix}*&1\\
c&0\end{pmatrix},

for unknown nonzero values
a,b,c
(and further unknown values hidden by
*
).
By the lemma, we must have
a=bc
.
Hence

\displaystyle hk^{-1}g

\displaystyle=(hu)(ku)^{-1}(gu)u^{-1}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}*&1\\
c&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
*&a\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&b\\
1&*\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&*\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}a&*\\
0&bc\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&*\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

Since
hk^{-1}g
has at least two fixed points (
x
and
y
), we must have
hk^{-1}g=1
.
Hence
gh=k\in S
, as required.
