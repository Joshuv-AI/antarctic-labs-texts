Divisibility and primality in random walks
Michel J. G. WEBER

Abstract
. In this paper we study the divisibility and primality properties of the Bernoulli random walk. We improve or extend some of our divisibility results to wide classes of iid or independent non iid random walks. We also obtain new primality results for the Rademacher random walk. We study the value distribution of divisors of the random walk in the Cramér model, and obtain a general estimate of a similar kind to that of the Bernouilli model. Earlier results on divisors and quasi-prime numbers in the Bernoulli model are recorded, as well as some other recent in the Cramér random model, based on an estimate due to Selberg.

2.
Preamble on summation methods.

In background of the study of the divisibility/primality questions related to these models are often are summation methods, which may be of particular efficiency, as they are linked between them through Tauberian results. We first begin with some important facts. The uniform model (the uniform distribution on
\{0,1,2\ldots,n\}
, for each
n
), also called the Kubilius model, is related to Cesàro’s summation method, whereas the binomial model is to Euler
summation method, and there exist Tauberian theorems
comparing summation methods, in particular these two methods. Therefore fine arithmetical results can be directly used, for one summation method (one random model), depending on the rate of convergence in this one, to answer a question concerning another random model.

Definition 2.1
.

A set

A\subset{\mathbb{N}}
has Cesàro density
{\lambda}
of order 1 (
C_{1}
–density
{\lambda}
), if

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\#\{j\leq n:j\in A\}}{n}={\lambda}.

Often, the most readily available information about a set of integers is that it has
C_{1}
-Cesàro density with a given rate of convergence.

Definition 2.2
.

A subset
A
of
{\mathbb{N}}
is said to have

{\lambda}

with parameter

\varrho
(in short
{E}_{\varrho}
density
{\lambda}
) if

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j\in A}{{n}\choose{j}}\varrho^{j}(1-\varrho)^{n-j}={\lambda}.

Diaconis and Stein proved in
[
11
]

(Theorem 1) the following fundamental result:

Theorem 2.3
.

For any

, and
\varrho\in]0,1[
the following assertions are equivalent:

\displaystyle({\rm i})

\displaystyle{\qquad}\hbox{\it$A$ has ${E}_{\varrho}$ density ${\lambda}$},

\displaystyle({\rm ii})

\displaystyle{\qquad}\lim_{t\to\infty}e^{-t}\sum_{j\in A}\frac{t^{j}}{j!}={\lambda},

\displaystyle({\rm iii})

\displaystyle{\qquad}\hbox{\it for all ${\varepsilon}>0$},\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\#\{j\in A:n\leq j<n+{\varepsilon}\sqrt{n}\}}{{\varepsilon}\sqrt{n}}={\lambda}.

3.1.
Basic estimates.

Proposition 3.1
.

For any integers

,

\big|{\mathbb{P}}\{d|B_{n}\}-\frac{1}{d}\big|\leq{2\over d}\sum_{1\leq j<d/2}e^{-{2nj^{2}/d^{2}}}.

(3.1)

Proof.

From the formula

d\,\chi(d|B_{n})=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}e^{2i\pi{j\over d}B_{n}},

(3.2)

we get by integration,

{\mathbb{P}}\big\{d|B_{n}\big\}={1\over d}\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}e^{i\pi n{j\over d}}\big(\cos{\pi j\over d}\big)^{n}.

(3.3)

As

, we have in fact by distinguishing the case

{\mathbb{P}}\big\{d|B_{n}\big\}={1\over d}+{2\over d}\sum_{1\leq j<d/2}(\cos{\pi n{j\over d}})\big(\cos{\pi j\over d}\big)^{n}.

(3.4)

Thus

\big|{\mathbb{P}}\big\{d|B_{n}\big\}-\frac{1}{d}\big|\leq{2\over d}\sum_{1\leq j<d/2}e^{n\log(1-2\sin^{2}{\pi j\over 2d})}.

As

,

\log(1-2\sin^{2}{\pi j\over 2d})\leq-2\sin^{2}{\pi j\over 2d}\leq-2({j\over d})^{2}
. Thus

\displaystyle\big|{\mathbb{P}}\big\{d|B_{n}\big\}-\frac{1}{d}\big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{2\over d}\sum_{1\leq j<d/2}e^{-{2nj^{2}/d^{2}}}.

Proposition 3.3

\sup_{n\geq 1}\sum_{d<\sqrt{n}}\big|{\mathbb{P}}\big\{d|B_{n}\big\}-{1\over d}\big|<\infty.

Proof.

Let

By permuting the order of summation, we get

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\sum_{1\leq d<\nu}\Big\{\sum_{1\leq j\leq d}\int_{\nu j\over d+1}^{\nu j\over d}e^{-2t^{2}}{dt\over t}\Big\}=2\sum_{1\leq j<\nu}\sum_{j\leq d<\nu}\int_{\nu j\over d+1}^{\nu j\over d}e^{-2t^{2}}{dt\over t}

(3)
Estimates of

and

Recall that

Corollary 3.8
.

\Big|{\mathbb{E}\,}{\sigma}_{-1}(B_{n})-\zeta(2)\Big|\leq\ C\,{\log n\over\sqrt{n}}.

Proof.

Writing that
{\sigma}_{-1}(B_{n})=\sum_{d\leq n}\frac{1}{d}\chi\{d|B_{n}\}
since
B_{n}\leq n
, we have

{\mathbb{E}\,}{\sigma}_{-1}(B_{n})=\sum_{d\leq n}\frac{1}{d}\,{\mathbb{P}}\{d|B_{n}\}.

Now

{\mathbb{E}\,}{\sigma}_{-1}(B_{n})-\sum_{d\leq n}{1\over d^{2}}=\sum_{d\leq n}{1\over d}\big({\mathbb{P}}\big\{d|B_{n}\big\}-{1\over d}\big).

Whence,

\big|{\mathbb{E}\,}{\sigma}_{-1}(B_{n})-\sum_{d\leq n}{1\over d^{2}}\big|\leq{C\over\sqrt{n}}\ \sum_{d\leq n}{1\over d}\leq\ C\,{\log n\over\sqrt{n}}.

Consequently,

\Big|{\mathbb{E}\,}{\sigma}_{-1}(B_{n})-\zeta(2)\Big|\leq\ C\,{\log n\over\sqrt{n}}.

∎

Corollary 3.9
.

We have for
n>m\geq 2
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\big\{B_{n}\,{\it and}\,B_{m}\ {\it coprime}\big\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{d\leq m\wedge(n-m)}{{\mu}(d)\over d^{2}}

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}\Big({\log m\over\sqrt{m\wedge(n-m)}}+\Big({m\wedge(n-m)\over m\vee(n-m)}\Big)^{1/2}\Big).

Moreover,

\lim_{m\to\infty\atop{n}/{m}\to\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\big\{(B_{n},B_{m})=1\big\}=\frac{1}{\zeta(2)}.

Proof.

By using Möbius inversion formula,

{\mathbb{P}}\big\{(B_{n},B_{m})=1\big\}=\sum_{d\leq m\wedge(n-m)}{\mu}(d){\mathbb{P}}\{d|S_{m}\}\,{\mathbb{P}}\{d|S_{n-m}\}

=\sum_{d\leq m\wedge(n-m)}{\mu}(d)\Big({\mathbb{P}}\big\{d|S_{m}\big\}-{1\over d}+{1\over d}\Big)\,\Big({\mathbb{P}}\big\{d|S_{n-m}\big\}-{1\over d}+{1\over d}\Big)

=\sum_{d\leq m\wedge(n-m)}{\mu}(d)\Big({\mathbb{P}}\big\{d|S_{m}\big\}-{1\over d}+{1\over d}\Big)\,\Big({\mathbb{P}}\big\{d|S_{n-m}\big\}-{1\over d}+{1\over d}\Big)

=\sum_{d\leq m\wedge(n-m)}{\mu}(d)\Big({1\over d}+\mathcal{O}\big({1\over\sqrt{m}}\big)\Big)\,\Big({1\over d}+\mathcal{O}\big({1\over\sqrt{n-m}}\big)\Big)

=\sum_{d\leq m\wedge(n-m)}{{\mu}(d)\over d^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\Big(\sum_{d\leq m}{1\over d}\big({1\over\sqrt{m}}+{1\over\sqrt{n-m}}\big)+\Big({m\wedge(n-m)\over\sqrt{m(n-m)}}\Big)\Big)

=\sum_{d\leq m\wedge(n-m)}{{\mu}(d)\over d^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\Big({\log m(\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{n-m})\over\sqrt{m(n-m)}}+\Big({m\wedge(n-m)\over m\vee(n-m)}\Big)^{1/2}\Big).

For instance:

— If
(\log m)^{2}<n-m\leq m
,

\displaystyle\Big|{\mathbb{P}}\big\{B_{n}\,{\it and}\,B_{m}\ {\it coprime}\big\}-\sum_{d\leq n-m}{{\mu}(d)\over d^{2}}\Big|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\Big({\log m\over\sqrt{n-m}}+\Big({n-m\over m}\Big)^{1/2}\Big).

— If
n\geq 2m
,

\displaystyle\Big|{\mathbb{P}}\big\{B_{n}\,{\it and}\,B_{m}\ {\it coprime}\big\}-\sum_{d\leq m}{{\mu}(d)\over d^{2}}\Big|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\Big({\log m\over\sqrt{m}}+\Big({m\over n-m}\Big)^{1/2}\Big).

This implies the second claim.∎

This suggests to study the following problem: Let
n_{1}<n_{2}<\ldots<n_{k}
be positive integers such that

\min\{n_{j}/n_{i},1\leq i<j\leq k\}

is large. To estimate the probability that
B_{n_{1}},B_{n_{2}},\ldots,B_{n_{k}}
are mutually coprime, namely

{\mathbb{P}}\big\{(B_{n_{i}},B_{n_{j}})=1,\forall 1\leq i<j\leq k\big\}.

That problem is related to a nice notion: for a subset of an integer lattice, to be
visible from the origin
, in this case the set of random points
(x,y)
in
{\mathbb{N}}^{2}
,
x\neq y
, with coordinates in
\{B_{n_{1}},B_{n_{2}},\ldots,B_{n_{k}}\}
. Referring to section 3.8 in Apostol
[
1
]
, we say that two lattice points
P
and

are mutually visible if the segment which join them contains no lattice points other than the endpoints
P
and
Q
. We also recall that two lattice points
(a,b)
and
(m,n)
are mutually visible if and only if
(a-m,b-n)=1
, or equivalently if and only if
(a-m,b-n)
is visible from the origin. The standard case presented in
[
1
]
has clear link with Farey series, and Dirichlet’s easy estimate on the number of corresponding visible points is a particular case of a much more general result having a direct link with Riemann Hypothesis (RH). See
[
60
]
for more.

Remark 3.10

Let

A natural question arises from this nice result. It seems possible (although we have not investigated it) to estimate the probability

3.2.
The local limit theorem for binomial sums

The first local limit theorem was established nearly three centuries ago, in the binomial case. We refer to Szewczak and Weber
[

, Th. 1.1 for the statement under the form below.

Theorem 3.11

(De Moivre–Laplace (1733))
.

Let

0<\rho<1
. Let
X
be such that

{\mathbb{P}}\{X=1\}=p=1-{\mathbb{P}}\{X=0\}
. Let

X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
be independent copies of

X
and let
S_{n}=X_{1}+\ldots+X_{n}
,
n\geq 2
. Let also
0<{\gamma}<1
.
Then for all
k
such that
|k-np|\leq{\gamma}n\rho(1-\rho)
and
n\geq\max(\rho/(1-\rho),(1-\rho)/\rho)
, letting

x=\frac{k-n\rho}{\sqrt{n\rho(1-\rho)}}
,
we have

{\mathbb{P}}\{B_{n}(\rho)=k\}\ =\ \frac{e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi n\rho(1-\rho)}}\ e^{E},

with

|E|\leq\frac{3|x|+2|x|^{3}}{(1-{\gamma})\sqrt{n\rho(1-\rho)}}+\frac{1}{4n\min(\rho,1-\rho)(1-{\gamma})}
.

If

, then

\Big|{\mathbb{P}}\{B_{n}(\rho)=k\}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi n\rho(1-\rho)}}\,e^{-\frac{(k-n\rho)^{2}}{2n\rho(1-\rho)}}\Big|\ \leq\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi n\rho(1-\rho)}}\ e^{-\frac{(k-n\rho)^{2}}{2n\rho(1-\rho)}}\Big(\frac{c_{1}|x|^{3}}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{c_{2}|x|}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{c_{3}}{n}\Big).

The constants
c_{i}
,
i=1,2,3
are explicit and depend on

only.

A useful consequence is:
For any
b>0
,
n\geq 2
,

\sup_{k:|k-n\rho|\leq\sqrt{bn\log n}}\Big|{\mathbb{P}}\{B_{n}(\rho)=k\}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi n\rho(1-\rho)}}\,e^{-\frac{(k-n\rho)^{2}}{2n\rho(1-\rho)}}\Big|\,\leq\,C_{b}\Big(\frac{\log^{3/2}n}{\sqrt{n}}\Big).

(3.10)

3.4.
Sharper uniform estimate and Theta function.

One might believe in view of Proposition
3.3
that the central comparison term is
\frac{1}{d}
. But this is
not
so. The right comparison term -
with a sharper remainder term
- turns up to be more complicated, and is equal to
{\Theta(d,n)\over d}
where
\Theta(d,n)
is the Theta function,

\displaystyle{\Theta(d,n)=\sum_{\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}}e^{in\pi{\ell\over d}-{n\pi^{2}\ell^{2}\over 2d^{2}}},}

(3.11)

d\geq 1,\ n\geq 1
being integers.

The following Theorem proved in Weber
[
70
]
,
provides a uniform estimate on the whole range of divisors.

Theorem 3.13

(
[
70
]
, Theorem II)
.

(1)
We have the following uniform estimate:

\sup_{2\leq d\leq n}\Big|{\mathbb{P}}\big\{d|B_{n}\big\}-{\Theta(d,n)\over d}\Big|={\mathcal{O}}\Big(\frac{(\log n)^{5/2}}{n^{3/2}}\Big).

(3.12)

(2)
Further,
for any
{\alpha}>0
,

\sup_{d<\pi\sqrt{n\over 2{\alpha}\log n}}\big|{\mathbb{P}}\big\{d|B_{n}\big\}-{1\over d}\big|={\mathcal{O}}_{\varepsilon}\big(n^{-{\alpha}+{\varepsilon}}\big),{\qquad}\quad(\forall{\varepsilon}>0)

(3.13)

and for any
0<\rho<1
,

\sup_{d<(\pi/\sqrt{2})n^{(1-\rho)/2}}\big|{\mathbb{P}}\big\{d|B_{n}\big\}-{1\over d}\big|={\mathcal{O}}_{\varepsilon}\big(e^{-(1-{\varepsilon})n^{\rho}}\big),{\qquad}\quad(\forall 0<{\varepsilon}<1).

(3.14)

The uniform estimate (
3.12
) covers the critical region of divisors
d\sim\sqrt{n}
, and we don’t know any other estimate of this kind covering this case. This estimate also admits a version for binomial sums, which follows by simply copying the proof made in the Bernoulli case.

Remark 3.14
.

The proof we give is intrinsic, in particular we don’t see how to obtain this estimate from an approach using the uniform model.

{\mathbb{P}}\{B_{n}\,\hbox{ prime}\}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi n}}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}\cap[\frac{n}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{3n\log n},\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{3n\log n}]}e^{-{(2p-n)^{2}\over 2n}}+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\Big).

[

(

Obviously binomial random variables have a Bernoulli part. For binomial random variables, the above decomposition is direct.

If now

\displaystyle+L\big({1\over\ell}-{1\over LD}\big)\sum_{h=0}^{D-1}e^{2i\pi{j\over D}(h^{2}+kh)}.

\displaystyle{1\over\ell}\Big(\big|\sum_{h=0}^{D-1}e^{2i\pi{j\over D}(h^{2}+kh)}\big|+\big|\sum_{h=0}^{{\mu}}e^{2i\pi{j\over D}(h^{2}+kh)}\big|\Big).

We rewrite this for later use under the following form.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let

\ell\geq 1
,
\ell\equiv{\mu}\,{\rm mod}(D)

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{h=0}^{\ell-1}e^{2i\pi{j\over D}(h^{2}+kh)}-{\ell\over D}\sum_{h=0}^{D-1}e^{2i\pi{j\over D}(h^{2}+kh)}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\big|\sum_{h=0}^{D-1}e^{2i\pi{j\over D}(h^{2}+kh)}\big|+\big|\sum_{h=0}^{{\mu}}e^{2i\pi{j\over D}(h^{2}+kh)}\big|.

Thus the study of

reduces to the one of the difference

{\Delta}^{\prime}={1\over 2^{n}}\sum_{h=0}^{n}{{n}\choose{h}}e^{2i\pi{j\over D}(h^{2}+kh)}-{1\over D}\sum_{h=0}^{D-1}e^{2i\pi{j\over D}(h^{2}+kh)}.

By linearity of Euler summation, it suffices to have at disposal a bound for Euler sums, and we have a classical tool from the theory of divergent series.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let

\{a_{k},k\geq 0\}
be a sequence of reals. Put

,
\ell\geq 0

There exists an absolute constant

(6.7)

This is easily seen by using Abel summation and well-known property of the binomial distribution (Feller
[

Applying it with the choice

we get in view of Lemma

\displaystyle{\Delta}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{C\over\sqrt{n}}\max_{{\mu}=0}^{D-1}\big|\sum_{h=0}^{{\mu}}e^{2i\pi{j\over D}(h^{2}+kh)}\big|.

(6.8)

Whence the intermediate estimate.

.

For some absolute constant

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{h=0}^{n}2^{-n}{{n}\choose{h}}e^{2i\pi{j\over D}(h^{2}+kh)}-

The Lemma below is just a slight reformulation of Sarkösy’s estimate

\displaystyle\sum_{u=1-M}^{M-1}\Big|\sum_{y=\max(1-u,1)}^{\min(M,M-u)}e^{4i\pi{\alpha}uy}\Big|

(6.12)

Proof.

\displaystyle\sum_{y=1}^{M}\sum_{u=1-y}^{M-y}e^{2i\pi{\alpha}u(u+2y+k)}=\sum_{u=1-M}^{M-1}\sum_{y=\max(1-u,1)}^{\min(M,M-u)}e^{2i\pi{\alpha}u(u+2y+k)}

\displaystyle\sum_{u=1-M}^{M-1}\Big|\sum_{y=\max(1-u,1)}^{\min(M,M-u)}e^{4i\pi{\alpha}uy}\Big|.

So that,

Proof.

8.
Divisibility and primality in the Cramér random walk

Cramér’s probabilistic model basically consists with a sequence of independent random variables
\xi_{i}
, defined for

i\geq 3
by

{\mathbb{P}}\{\xi_{i}=1\}=\frac{1}{\log i},{\qquad}\quad{\mathbb{P}}\{\xi_{i}=0\}=1-\frac{1}{\log i}.

(8.1)

Let
m_{n}={\mathbb{E}\,}S_{n}=\sum_{j=3}^{n}\frac{1}{\log j}
,
B_{n}={\rm Var}S_{n}=\sum_{j=3}^{n}\frac{1}{\log j}(1-\frac{1}{\log j})
.
Cramér’s well-known twin prime conjecture, which seems largely accepted, does not however assert that there are any primes in the ‘primes’  of the model, which are the instants of jump
of the random walk
\{S_{n},n\geq 1\}
, recursively defined by

P_{1}=\inf\{n\geq 3:\xi_{n}=1\},{\qquad}P_{\nu+1}=\inf\{n>P_{\nu}:\xi_{n}=1\}{\qquad}\quad\nu\geq 1.

(8.2)

We refer to Granville
[
19
]
, Pintz
[
40
]
and Montgomery and Soundararajan
[
39
]
. Our recent paper Weber
[
64
]
offers a probabilistic complementary study, in particular of the ‘primes’
P_{\nu}
and of the primality properties of
\{S_{n},n\geq 1\}
.
Recall
that the standard limit theorems
are fulfilled,
and the invariance principle holds, derived from Sakhanenko’s. The law of the iterated logarithm implies that

\#\big\{\nu\in\mathcal{C}:\nu\leq x\big\}=\int_{2}^{x}\frac{{\rm d}t}{\log t}+\mathcal{O}\big(\sqrt{x\log\log x}\big),

(8.3)

with probability one. Cramér’s easier prediction on the order of magnitude of the remainder in the ‘prime number theorem’, cannot be separated from the fact that this one should also be sensitive to the subsequence on which it is observed, as shown in
[
64
]
. Such a property is hardly conceivable and was nowhere emphasized.
In a work in progress, that study is transposed to other random models for prime numbers.

Appendix B
Kubilius’ model

The Bernoulli model only involves one
probability space: the infinite product of probability spaces
(\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}},\mathcal{B}(\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}),{\mu}^{\mathbb{N}})
, where
{\mu}=\frac{1}{2}({\delta}_{0}+{\delta}_{1})
,
{\delta}_{x}
denoting the Dirac mass at point
x
. By construction, the Kubilius model relies upon all
probability spaces

(\{1,2,\ldots,n\},{\mu}_{n})

{\mu}_{n}
being the normalized counting measure.
This is an obstacle for the application of general criteria of almost sure convergence, typically Gál-Koksma, Stechkin or Móricz theorems.
However,
a bridge exists in Kubilius theory with some standard infinite product of probability spaces, namely with some inherent random walk built up from
independent non identically distributed random variables.

Let

\{Y_{p},p\geq 1\}
be a sequence of independent binomial random variables such that

{\mathbb{P}}\{Y_{p}=1\}=1/p
and

{\mathbb{P}}\{Y_{p}=0\}=1-1/p
. We can view
Y_{p}
as modelling whether an integer taken at random is divisible by
p
or not. Let

T_{n}=\sum_{p\leq n}Y_{p}.

By Mertens estimate

s_{n}^{2}={\mathbb{E}\,}(T_{n}-{\mathbb{E}\,}T_{n})^{2}=\sum_{p\leq n}{1\over p}-{1\over p^{2}}=\log\log n+\mathcal{O}(1)
.
The sequence
\{T_{n},n\geq 1\}
is known to asymptotically behave as the truncated prime divisor function

{\omega}(m,t)=\#\{p\leq t:p|m\},

at least when
t
is not too close to
m
. More precisely, let

{\omega}_{r}(m)=\big({\omega}(m,1),\ldots,{\omega}(m,r)\big),

where
r
is some integer with
2\leq r\leq x
, and put
u={\log x\over\log r}
. The bridge mentionned before is given by the following sharp estimate.

Theorem B.1
.

Given
c<1
arbitrary, we have uniformly in

x,r
and

Q\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{r}
,

\frac{\#\{m\leq x:{\omega}_{r}(m)\in Q\}}{x}={\mathbb{P}}\big\{(T_{1},\ldots,T_{r})\in Q\big\}+\mathcal{O}\big(x^{-c}+e^{-u\log u}\big).

We refer to Elliott
[
12
]
p.119-122 and Tenenbaum
[
54
]
where a thorough study is presented.

The error term
e^{-u\log u}

naturally brings restrictions in applications to asymptotic problems. To make it small,
it requires if

r=r(x)
that

r(x)=\mathcal{O}_{\varepsilon}(x^{\varepsilon})
for all
{\varepsilon}>0
. This amounts to truncate the prime divisor function
{\omega}(m)
at level

\mathcal{O}_{\varepsilon}(x^{\varepsilon})
, which is satisfactory as long as
m\ll x
. However, these integers have a negligible contribution on the size
of the left-term in Theorem
B.1
. The model is therefore mostly adapted to the analysis of the distribution of small prime divisors
of an integer (see notably
[
12
]
p.122).

1\leq\kappa<p^{r-2v_{p}(k)}
be such that
K\equiv\kappa\,{\rm mod}(p^{r-2v_{p}(k)})
.
The number of solutions is

\displaystyle\#\big\{Y\leq p^{r-v_{p}(k)}:Y

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle-{\kappa}\ {\rm mod}(p^{r-2v_{p}(k)})\big\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\#\big\{-\kappa+jp^{r-2v_{p}(k)}:1\leq j\leq p^{v_{p}(k)}\big\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p^{v_{p}(k)}.

Summarizing the case
r\geq 4
, if
r
is odd,
r=2r^{\prime}+1

\varrho_{k}(p^{r})=\begin{cases}2p^{v_{p}(k)}&\quad\hbox{\rm if $1\leq v_{p}(k)\leq r^{\prime}$},\cr p^{r^{\prime}}&\quad\hbox{\rm if $v_{p}(k)>r^{\prime}$}.\end{cases}

(C.5)

And if
r
is even,
r=2r^{\prime}

\varrho_{k}(p^{r})=\begin{cases}2p^{v_{p}(k)}&\quad\hbox{\rm if $1\leq v_{p}(k)<r^{\prime}$},\cr p^{r^{\prime}}&\quad\hbox{\rm if $v_{p}(k)\geq r^{\prime}$}.\end{cases}

(C.6)

This remains true if
r=1,2,3
. Observe that (
v_{p}(k)<r^{\prime}
,
r
even) or (
v_{p}(k)\leq r^{\prime}
,
r
odd) are equivalent to
v_{p}(k)<{r\over 2}
.

Therefore, for
all
r\geq 2

\varrho_{k}(p^{r})=\begin{cases}2&\quad{\rm if}\ p\not|k,\cr 2p^{v_{p}(k)}&\quad\hbox{\rm if}\ 1\leq v_{p}(k)<{r\over 2},\cr p^{\lfloor{r\over 2}\rfloor}\ &\quad\hbox{\rm if}\ v_{p}(k)\geq{r\over 2}.\end{cases}

(C.7)

Consequently

\varrho_{k}(D)=\prod_{p|D}\varrho_{k}(p^{v_{p}(D)})=\prod_{v_{p}(k)<{v_{p}(D)/2}}(2p^{v_{p}(k)})\cdot\prod_{v_{p}(k)\geq{v_{p}(D)/2}}p^{\lfloor{v_{p}(D)\over 2}\rfloor}.

(C.8)

Recall that

D_{1/2}\,=\,\prod_{p|D}p^{\lfloor{v_{p}(D)\over 2}\rfloor}.

We consider again several cases:

— If
p
is such that
v_{p}(D)=1
, then it produces in the writing of
\varrho_{k}(D)
only a factor 1, since
v_{p}(k)<1/2
, so
v_{p}(k)=0
and
\lfloor{v_{p}(D)\over 2}\rfloor=0
.

— Now let
p
be such that
v_{p}(D)\geq 2
, and compare
v_{p}(D)/2
with
\lfloor{v_{p}(D)\over 2}\rfloor
. If
v_{p}(D)
is even, both quantities are equal. If
v_{p}(D)
is odd,
v_{p}(D)=2z+1
, then

v_{p}(D)/2=z+1/2
,

\lfloor{v_{p}(D)\over 2}\rfloor=z
.

Thus in the latter case:

a) If
v_{p}(k)<{v_{p}(D)/2}
, namely
v_{p}(k)\leq z
, we get the factor
2p^{v_{p}(k)}=2p^{\min(v_{p}(k),z)}=2p^{v_{p}((k,D_{1/2}))}
. If
v_{p}(k)\geq{v_{p}(D)/2}
, namely
v_{p}(k)>z
, we have the factor
p^{z}=p^{\min(v_{p}(k),z)}=p^{v_{p}((k,D_{1/2}))}
.

b) In the case
v_{p}(D)
is even, if
v_{p}(k)<{v_{p}(D)/2}
, namely
v_{p}(k)<z
, we have the factor
2p^{v_{p}(k)}=2p^{\min(v_{p}(k),z)}=2p^{v_{p}((k,D_{1/2}))}
. If
v_{p}(k)\geq{v_{p}(D)/2}=z
, we have the factor
p^{z}=p^{\min(v_{p}(k),z)}=p^{v_{p}((k,D_{1/2}))}
.

Therefore the contribution of
a prime
p
such that
v_{p}(D)\geq 2
, is either
2p^{v_{p}((k,D_{1/2}))}
if
p
is part of the first product, or
p^{v_{p}((k,D_{1/2}))}
if
p
is part of the second.

A prime number
p
is part of the first product, if and only if the inequation
1\leq v_{p}(k)<v_{p}(D)/2
is fulfilled.
We
therefore get the condensed form,

\displaystyle\varrho_{k}(D)=\prod_{p|D}\varrho_{k}(p^{v_{p}(D)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{v_{p}(k)<{v_{p}(D)/2}}(2p^{v_{p}(k)})\cdot\prod_{v_{p}(k)\geq{v_{p}(D)/2}}p^{\lfloor{v_{p}(D)\over 2}\rfloor}

(C.9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{\#\{p\,:\,1\leq v_{p}(k)<v_{p}(D)/2\}}\,\prod_{p}p^{\min(v_{p}(k),v_{p}(D)/2)}

(C.10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{\#\{p\,:\,1\leq v_{p}(k)<v_{p}(D)/2\}}\,(k,D_{1/2}).

(C.11)

Finally, consider the case
k=0
, namely
\varrho_{0}(p^{r})=\#\big\{1\leq y\leq p^{r}:\ p^{r}|y^{2}\big\}
. Notice that

\varrho_{0}(p)=\#\big\{1\leq y\leq p:\ p|y\big\}=1
. Let

r>1
, and write
y=p^{s}Y
,
(Y,p)=1
and
1\leq s\leq r
. If
s=r
, there is the trivial unique solution
y=p^{r}
. If
1\leq s<r
,

p^{r}|y^{2}\Rightarrow 2s\geq r
, in which case, the number of solutions is

\#\big\{Y\leq p^{r-s}:(Y,p)=1\big\}=\phi(p^{r-s})=p^{r-s}(1-{1\over p}).

Consequently

\varrho_{0}(p^{r})=1+\sum_{r/2\leq s<r}p^{r-s}(1-{1\over p})
.
If
r
is even,
r=2r^{\prime}

\varrho_{0}(p^{r})=1+\sum_{{\sigma}=1}^{r^{\prime}}p^{{\sigma}}(1-{1\over p})=1+p\sum_{u=0}^{r^{\prime}-1}p^{u}(1-{1\over p})=1+p\Big({p^{r^{\prime}}-1\over p-1}\Big)({p-1\over p})=p^{r^{\prime}},

whereas if
r
is odd,
r=2r^{\prime}+1
,

\varrho_{0}(p^{r})=1+\sum_{{\sigma}=1}^{r^{\prime}}p^{{\sigma}}(1-{1\over p})=p^{r^{\prime}}
.
Thus

\varrho_{0}(p^{r})=p^{\lfloor{r\over 2}\rfloor}.

It follows that

\varrho_{0}(D)=\prod_{p|D}p^{\lfloor{v_{p}(D)\over 2}\rfloor}\,=\,D_{1/2}.

The proof is now complete.
