Two-photon absorption cross sections of pulsed entangled beams Preprint: AIP/123-QED
Frank Schlawin

Abstract
Entangled two-photon absorption (ETPA) could form the basis of nonlinear quantum spectroscopy at very low photon fluxes, since, at sufficiently low photon fluxes, ETPA scales linearly with the photon flux. When different pairs start to overlap temporally, accidental coincidences are thought to give rise to a ’classical’ quadratic scaling which dominates the signal at large photon fluxes and thus recovers a supposedly classical regime, where any quantum advantage is thought to be lost. Here we scrutinize this assumption and demonstrate that quantum-enhanced absorption cross sections can persist even to very large photon numbers. To this end, we use a minimal model for quantum light, which can interpolate continuously between the entangled pair and a high-photon-flux limit, to derive analytically ETPA cross sections and the intensity crossover regime. We investigate the interplay between spectral and spatial degrees of freedom, how linewidth broadening of the sample impacts the experimentally achievable enhancement.

Arguably the most active field involves the use of entangled photons as spectroscopic tools

The archetypical process, which is widely believed to encapsulate all the beneficial properties of entangled photons, is ETPA, where an entangled pair is absorbed by a quantum system via far off-resonant intermediate states.
For such a process to occur, it is necessary for both photons to be localised at the position of the quantum system at the same time. This implies that the efficiency of this process may depend on the spatial and the spectral correlations of the entangled pair - in addition to the bunched nature of the entangled light field.
In 1997 Fei et al. first condensed the interplay of these effects into the elegant formula for the ETPA cross section,
Fei et al. 1997

\displaystyle\sigma_{e}\simeq\frac{\delta_{r}}{A_{e}T_{e}}.

(1)

Here,
\delta_{r}
is the TPA cross section of randomly arriving photons. It is divided by the so-called "entanglement area"
A_{e}

This result has since been generalized to multiphoton cross sections

The above cross section gives rise to the detected ETPA rate,

\displaystyle R^{TPA}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{e}\phi+\delta_{r}\phi^{2}

Since entangled photons always arrive in pairs, their ETPA rate scales linearly in the photon flux density

;

When the flux increases, such that different pairs overlap in time, random coincidences between uncorrelated photons are expected to give rise to the classical quadratic scaling of the second term.

Light-matter interactions & quantum light model

II.1
The quantized electromagnetic field

We want to describe the situation sketched in Fig.
1

In an ideal optical system, the positive frequency component of the electric field operator in the image plane can then be written as

\displaystyle{\bf E}^{(+)}({\bf\rho},z=0,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}\sum_{\sigma}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!d\omega\;\int_{\mathds{R}^{2}}\!\!\!\!d^{2}q\left(\frac{\hbar\omega}{2\epsilon_{0}}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\times\left(\frac{\omega}{c^{2}k_{z}(\omega)}\right)^{1/2}\hat{a}_{\sigma}({\bf q},\omega){\bf e}_{{\bf q},\sigma}e^{i({\bf q}\cdot{\bf\rho}-\omega t)},

(3)

where
\hat{a}_{\sigma}({\bf q},\omega)
is the photon annihilation operator of a photon with transverse momentum
{\bf q}=(k_{x},k_{y})
,
frequency
\omega
, and polarization
\sigma
.
The operators satisfy

\displaystyle\left[\hat{a}_{\sigma}({\bf q},\omega),\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\sigma^{\prime}}({\bf q}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle=\delta_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}\delta({\bf q}-{\bf q}^{\prime})\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime}).

(4)

In addition,
{\bf e}_{{\bf q},\sigma}
is the polarization vector,
and the transverse position in the image plane is
{\bf\rho}=(x,y)
.
We employ the small bandwidth approximation, where we evaluate the frequency-dependent prefactor at the center frequency
\omega_{0}
of the traveling light pulse. We then have
k_{z}(\omega_{0})=n_{0}\omega_{0}/c
with
n_{0}
the refractive index of the medium, and we further approximate
{\bf e}_{{\bf q},\sigma}\simeq{\bf e}_{\sigma}
.
The electric field operator becomes simply

\displaystyle{\bf E}^{(+)}({\bf\rho},t)

\displaystyle=iE_{0}\sum_{\sigma}{\bf e}_{\sigma}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!d\omega\;\int\!\!d^{2}q\;\hat{a}_{\sigma}({\bf q},\omega)e^{i({\bf q}\cdot{\bf\rho}-\omega t)},

(5)

where

E_{0}=(2\pi)^{-3/2}((\hbar\omega_{0})/(2\epsilon_{0}n_{0}c))^{1/2}

II.2
Light-matter interaction

The dipolar light-matter interaction Hamiltonian (in the interaction picture with respect to molecular and field Hamiltonian) is given by

\displaystyle H_{l-m}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathds{R}^{3}}\!\!d^{3}r\;{\bf\hat{d}}(t)\cdot\left({\bf\hat{E}}^{(+)}({\bf r},t)+{\bf\hat{E}}^{(-)}({\bf r},t)\right),

(6)

where
{\bf\hat{d}}({\bf r},t)={\bf\hat{d}}^{(+)}({\bf r},t+{\bf\hat{d}}^{(-)}({\bf r},t
are the molecular dipole operator and its positive (negative) frequency components, respectively.
We consider the interaction of (quantum) light with a few-level sample system, as given by Fig.
1
(b).
It is described by the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{mol}

\displaystyle=\hbar\omega_{g}|g\rangle\langle g|+\hbar\omega_{e}|e\rangle\langle e|+\hbar\omega_{f}|f\rangle\langle f|,

(7)

where
|g\rangle
is the ground state. In the following, we set
\omega_{g}=0
without loss of generality.
The Hamiltonian then consists of an intermediate state
|e\rangle
and final state
|f\rangle
.
We can now expand the dipole operator in these states, such that it reads in the Schrödinger picture

\displaystyle\hat{{\bf d}}({\bf r})

\displaystyle=\delta({\bf r}-{\bf r}_{mol})\left({\bf d}_{eg}|e\rangle\langle g|+{\bf d}_{ef}|f\rangle\langle e|+H.c.\right).

(8)

Here
{\bf r}_{mol}=({\bf\rho}_{mol},z_{mol})
denotes the position of the molecule. We assumed it to be point-like, which is an excellent approximation for molecular light-matter interactions in the optical regime.
We further divide it into the positive and negative frequency components,

\displaystyle\hat{{\bf d}}({\bf r})

\displaystyle=\delta({\bf r}-{\bf r}_{mol})\left(\hat{{\bf d}}^{(+)}+\hat{{\bf d}}^{(-)}\right),

(9)

where

\displaystyle\hat{{\bf d}}^{(-)}

\displaystyle=\left({\bf d}_{eg}|e\rangle\langle g|+{\bf d}_{ef}|f\rangle\langle e|\right)

(10)

accounts for the excitation of the molecule (and
{\bf d}^{(+)}
for the de-excitation).

II.3
The PDC Model

In the sketch in Fig.
1
(a), a laser pulse with frequency
\omega_{p}
, bandwidth
\Omega_{p}
and transverse beam area
\sim(2\pi/Q_{p})^{2}
induces spontaneous downconversion in a nonlinear crystal, generating two quantum-correlated beams, traditionally called signal and idler.
Since the purpose of this paper is not to discuss the generation of PDC light in a multimode regime in great detail (many excellent reviews can be found on this topic, e.g. Ref.
Quesada et al. 2022
), here we only introduce the bigaussian model, which allows us to carry out many of the following calculations analytically.
Crucially, this model includes both spatial and spectral degrees of freedom, and is therefore capable of describing spatiotemporal correlations
Brambilla et al. 2004
;
Law and Eberly 2004
;
Gatti et al. 2012
;
Gatti et al. 2023
- sometimes dubbed ”X-entanglement"
Gatti et al. 2009
when the two degrees of freedom are not separable.

II.3.1
Effective action

The effective action, which governs the downconversion process in the setup of Fig.
1
(a), can be written as
La Volpe et al. 2021

\displaystyle\Sigma_{eff}=\hbar\Gamma\int d\omega_{s}\int d\omega_{i}

\displaystyle\;\int d^{2}q_{s}\int d^{2}q_{i}\;F_{PDC}({\bf q}_{s},\omega_{s};{\bf q}_{i},\omega_{i})

\displaystyle\times\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{s}({\bf q}_{s},\omega_{s})\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i}({\bf q}_{i},\omega_{i})+H.c,

(11)

Here, the polarization index
\sigma
of Eq. (
5
) is replaced by the signal and idler fields, labelled "
s
" and "
i
", respectively. These two fields are assumed to have a fixed polarization, which may be different (in type-II PDC) or identical (in type-I PDC). In the latter case, we assume that the two fields may still be distinguished, e.g. by their propagation direction.

\Gamma
is a dimensionless number, which quantifies the strength of the PDC process.
We have further introduced the joint spatiotemporal amplitude (JSA)
F_{PDC}
, which we choose to be normalized, i.e.

\displaystyle\int d\omega_{s}\int d\omega_{i}\;\int d^{2}q_{s}\int d^{2}q_{i}\;\left|F_{PDC}({\bf q}_{s},\omega_{s};{\bf q}_{i},\omega_{i})\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=1.

(12)

In general, the JSA has a complicated structure which cannot be factorized into spatial and spectral components
Brambilla et al. 2004
, and which gives rise to high-dimensional
X
-entanglement
Gatti et al. 2009
.
In this manuscript, we will use a particularly convenient model, where the spatial and the frequency phase matching functions are approximated by Gaussians. This is, e.g., appropriate for non-collinear PDC
La Volpe et al. 2021
. The JSA then reads

\displaystyle F_{PDC}({\bf q}_{s},\omega_{s};{\bf q}_{i},\omega_{i})

\displaystyle=F_{mom}({\bf q}_{s},{\bf q}_{i})F_{spec}(\omega_{s},\omega_{i})

(13)

with the momentum distribution

\displaystyle F_{mom}({\bf q}_{s},{\bf q}_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi Q_{p}Q_{m}}\exp\left[-\frac{({\bf Q}_{s}+{\bf Q}_{i})^{2}}{4Q_{p}^{2}}-\frac{({\bf Q}_{s}-{\bf Q}_{i})^{2}}{4Q_{m}^{2}}\right]

(14)

and the joint spectral amplitude

\displaystyle F_{spec}(\omega_{s},\omega_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi\Omega_{m}\Omega_{p}}}\exp\left[-\frac{(\Omega_{s}+\Omega_{i})^{2}}{4\Omega_{p}^{2}}-\frac{(\Omega_{s}-\Omega_{i})^{2}}{4\Omega_{m}^{2}}\right].

(15)

Here we have introduced variables that are shifted with respect to their mean, i.e.
{\bf Q}_{j}={\bf q}_{j}-{\bf q}_{j}^{(0)}
and
\Omega_{j}=\omega_{j}-\omega_{j}^{(0)}
.
Due to energy and momentum conservation, we have
\omega_{s}^{(0)}+\omega_{i}^{(0)}=\omega_{p}
and
{\bf q}_{s}^{(0)}+{\bf q}_{i}^{(0)}=0
. In the following, we will work with degenerate downconversion with
\omega_{s}^{(0)}=\omega_{i}^{(0)}=\omega_{p}/2
. This reduces some clutter in our notation, but has no implications for the TPA cross section with off-resonant intermediates states, which we will evaluate.
In Eq. (
14
), the first term in the exponential governs the distribution of the sum of the two momenta, described by the width
Q_{p}
.
This parameter is inversely proportional to the transverse beam width
w_{0}
of the laser pulse triggering the downconversion process.
In the following, we will characterize the transverse width of the entangled beam by
A_{p}=\lambda_{p}^{2}=(2\pi)^{2}/Q_{p}^{2}
.
The second term in Eq. (
14
) describing the distribution of the frequency differences is characterized by the quantity
Q_{m}
, which is determined by the phase matching in the nonlinear crystal
Brambilla et al. 2004
;
Beltran et al. 2017
and the emission angles of the PDC light which can be collected by the optical system
La Volpe et al. 2021
.
Eq. (
15
) is governed by similar physics: the distribution of the sum of the two photon frequencies is governed by
\Omega_{p}
, which is the bandwidth of the pump pulse generating the PDC light. The distribution of the frequency differences
\Omega_{m}
is again related to the phase matching conditions in the nonlinear crystal.

The bigaussian form of Eqs. (
14
) and (
15
) is very convenient, since it allows us to diagonalize the effective action (
II.3.1
) analytically. This can be done with the help of Mehler’s formula for Hermite polynomials, which yields

;
Horoshko et al. 2019

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{4}\frac{1+\zeta}{1-\zeta}(x+y)^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\frac{1-\zeta}{1+\zeta}(x-y)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-\zeta^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\zeta^{n}h_{n}(x)h_{n}(y),

(16)

where
0<\zeta<1
and
h_{n}(kx)=k^{1/2}(2^{n}n!\sqrt{\pi})^{-1/2}H_{n}(kx)e^{-(kx)^{2}/2}
1
1

1

Note that here the Hermite functions are normalized with respect to integrals over space or frequency, i.e.
\int d\omega\;h_{n}^{2}(\omega)=1
.
.
Note that the factor
(-1)^{n}
is included for the case of Eq. (
14
) and (
15

Q_{p}>Q_{m}
and
\Omega_{p}>\Omega_{m}

In the case where this condition is violated (e.g. when we evaluate the Fourier transform of these functions), we use Eq. (

.

We find for Eqs. (

\displaystyle\zeta_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Omega_{m}-\Omega_{p}}{\Omega_{m}+\Omega_{p}}

\tau=1/\sqrt{\Omega_{m}\Omega_{p}}

Likewise, for the momentum correlation function we obtain, with

Altogether, we can write the PDC action (

\displaystyle\Sigma_{eff}

\displaystyle=\hbar\Gamma(1-\zeta^{2}_{q})\sqrt{1-\zeta_{t}^{2}}\sum_{{\bf n}}\zeta_{t}^{n_{t}}\zeta_{q}^{n_{x}+n_{y}}\hat{A}^{\dagger}_{n_{t}n_{x}n_{y}}\hat{B}^{\dagger}_{n_{t}n_{x}n_{y}}+h.c.

where we defined the Schmidt mode operators

\displaystyle\hat{A}^{\dagger}_{{\bf n}}

\displaystyle=\int d\omega_{s}\int d^{2}q_{s}\;h_{{\bf n}}({\bf q}_{s},\omega_{s})\hat{a}_{s}^{\dagger}({\bf q}_{s},\omega_{s}),

and

\displaystyle\hat{B}^{\dagger}_{{\bf n}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{|{\bf n}|}\int d\omega_{i}\int d^{2}q_{i}\;h_{{\bf n}}({\bf q}_{i},\omega_{i})\hat{a}_{i}^{\dagger}({\bf q}_{i},\omega_{i}).

II.3.2
Output state

The quantum state of light generated by the PDC in a nonlinear crystal is given by

\displaystyle|\psi_{BSV}\rangle

\displaystyle=\exp\left[-\frac{i}{\hbar}\Sigma_{eff}\right]|0\rangle.

(22)

Correlation functions of this state are most conveniently calculated in the Heisenberg picture, where the input-output relations are readily derived as

\displaystyle\hat{A}^{(out)}_{{\bf n}}

\displaystyle=\cosh(r_{{\bf n}}\Gamma)\hat{A}_{{\bf n}}+\sinh(r_{{\bf n}}\Gamma)\hat{B}^{\dagger}_{{\bf n}},

(23)

\displaystyle\hat{B}^{(out)}_{{\bf n}}

\displaystyle=\cosh(r_{{\bf n}}\Gamma)\hat{B}_{{\bf n}}+\sinh(r_{{\bf n}}\Gamma)\hat{A}^{\dagger}_{{\bf n}},

(24)

where
r_{{\bf n}}=(1-\zeta^{2}_{q})\sqrt{1-\zeta_{t}^{2}}\zeta_{t}^{n_{t}}\zeta_{q}^{n_{x}+n_{y}}
.
Using Eq. (
5
), the electric field operators in the image plane are then given by

\displaystyle{\bf E}_{s}^{(+)}({\bf r},t)

\displaystyle=E_{0}{\bf e}_{s}\sum_{{\bf n}}h_{{\bf n},s}({\bf r},t)\hat{A}_{{\bf n}}^{(out)},

(25)

\displaystyle{\bf E}_{i}^{(+)}({\bf r},t)

\displaystyle=E_{0}{\bf e}_{i}\sum_{{\bf n}}h_{{\bf n},i}({\bf r},t)\hat{B}_{{\bf n}}^{(out)}.

(26)

Here,

E_{0}
is defined in Eq. (
5
) with
\omega_{0}=\omega_{p}/2
,
{\bf e}_{j}
the polarization vector, and
h_{{\bf n},s}({\bf r},t)
(

) are the Fourier transforms of the Hermite functions (
21
), i.e.

\displaystyle h_{{\bf n},s}({\bf r},t)

\displaystyle\equiv\int d\omega_{s}\int d^{2}q_{s}\;h_{{\bf n}}({\bf q}_{s},\omega_{s})e^{i({\bf q}_{s}\cdot{\bf\rho}-\omega_{s}t)},

(27)

and

\displaystyle h_{{\bf n},i}({\bf r},t)

\displaystyle\equiv(-1)^{|{\bf n}|}\int d\omega_{i}\int d^{2}q_{i}\;h_{{\bf n}}({\bf q}_{i},\omega_{i})e^{i({\bf q}_{i}\cdot{\bf\rho}-\omega_{i}t)}.

(28)

Finally, it is possible to calculate the Schmidt number explicitly in this model
La Volpe et al. 2021
. This quantity measures the effective dimensionality of the state of light. It is simply given by

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle=K_{t}K_{x}K_{y},

(29)

where we have
K_{t}=(\Omega_{m}/\Omega_{p}+\Omega_{p}/\Omega_{m})/2
, and

.
A useful approximation for the the Schmidt number, which we will use later, is obtained in the limit of strong two-photon entanglement, where e.g.

and

, such that the number of spectral modes is given by the ratio of the two numbers, respectively, i.e.

and

.

III
Two-photon absorption cross section

III.1
From the excitation probability to the cross section

As discussed in detail below, we will use time-dependent perturbation theory to calculate the two-photon excitation probability
P_{f}({\bf r}_{mol})
of a molecule at a position
{\bf r}_{mol}
interacting with the pulsed quantum light field, which we introduced in the previous section.
The full TPA signal (which could be measured e.g. by fluorescence) is then given by the sum over all the molecules’ contributions, which are contained in a sample. Since this will usually be a macroscopic number, we replace the summation over these contributions by an integral over the sample volume and the average molecular density
m_{0}
.
Up to an unimportant constant which depends on the e.g. the detection efficiency of the fluorescence photons, the measured signal is then given by
2
2

2

Note that the factor
1/(2\pi)^{2}
is introduced for consistency, such that the photon number density
\hat{a}^{\dagger}({\bf\rho},\omega)\hat{a}({\bf\rho},\omega)
integrated over space and frequency yields the mean photon number (
III.1
).

\displaystyle S_{TPA}

\displaystyle=\frac{m_{0}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{V_{sample}}\!\!\!\!d^{3}r_{mol}\;P_{f}({\bf r}_{mol}).

(30)

For the sample volume
V_{sample}
, we consider a thin slab of length
\Delta z
in the propagation direction of the light, such that we replace
\int dz\simeq\Delta z
.
The transverse extent
\Delta^{2}{\bf\rho}
in contrast is assumed much larger than the transverse width of the light field, such that we can extend the integration boundaries to infinity in the transverse directions.

The measured TPA rate is then given by the signal and the repetition rate

of the laser which generates the entangled pulses,

\displaystyle R^{TPA}

\displaystyle=S_{TPA}f_{rep}.

(31)

To bring this rate into the form of a two-photon absorption rate (
2
), we write it as

\displaystyle R^{TPA}

\displaystyle=N_{mol}\sigma_{e}\phi+N_{mol}\delta_{r}\phi^{2}

(32)

where the total number of molecules in the sample, which are irradiated by the light, is given by
N_{mol}=m_{0}\Delta zA_{p}
.
To establish the scaling with the photon flux density, we write the photon flux density as

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=\langle\hat{N}\rangle\times\frac{f_{rep}}{A_{p}},

(33)

where

\displaystyle\langle\hat{N}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=s,i}\int d\omega\int d^{2}q\;\langle\psi_{BSV}|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j}({\bf q},\omega)\hat{a}_{j}({\bf q},\omega)|\psi_{BSV}\rangle

III.2
Experimental setup and two-photon absorption probability

We next calculate the probability of entangled TPA by a point-like molecule at position
{\bf r}_{mol}=({\bf\rho}_{mol},0)
in the image plane of the optical setup (see Fig.

The two-photon absorption cross section can be calculated from the probability to excite the final molecular state

via the absorption of two photons. Thus, we need to calculate

\displaystyle P_{f}({\bf r}_{mol},t)

\displaystyle=\text{tr}\left\{|f(t)\rangle\langle f(t)|\mathcal{T}\exp\left[-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int^{t}_{t_{0}}\!\!d\tau H_{l-m,-}(\tau)\right]\varrho_{0}\right\},

(35)

where the initial state of the light-matter system at time

is given by the state of light emitted by the PDC source, Eq. (
22

\displaystyle\varrho_{0}

\displaystyle=\varrho_{sys}\otimes\varrho_{f},

(36)

with
\varrho_{sys}=|g(t_{0})\rangle\langle g(t_{0})|

\varrho_{f}=|\psi_{BSV}(t_{0})\rangle\langle\psi_{BSV}(t_{0})|

The time evolution is given by the Dyson series, and defined the time-ordering operator
\mathcal{T}

H_{l-m,-}

H_{l-m,-}\varrho=H_{l-m}\varrho-\varrho H_{l-m}

6

{\bf E}={\bf E}_{s}+{\bf E}_{i}

The final state population can be calculated from Eq. (
35

Using the rotating wave approximation,
H_{l-m}\simeq{\bf d}^{(+)}\cdot{\bf E}^{(-)}+{\bf d}^{(-)}\cdot{\bf E}^{(+)}

(c) (for details, see e.g. Ref.
Raymer et al. 2021b

\displaystyle P_{f}({\bf r}_{mol},t)

\displaystyle=\int^{t}_{t_{0}}\!\!d\tau_{4}\int^{\tau_{4}}_{t_{0}}\!\!d\tau_{3}\int^{\tau_{3}}_{t_{0}}\!\!d\tau_{2}\int^{\tau_{2}}_{t_{0}}\!\!d\tau_{1}\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4}=s,i}\!\!\!\!\!\!2\Re\bigg(C^{mat}_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4}}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\tau_{3}.\tau_{4})C^{field}_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4}}({\bf r}_{mol},\tau_{1},{\bf r}_{mol},\tau_{2};{\bf r}_{mol},\tau_{3},{\bf r}_{mol},\tau_{4})\bigg),

where

\displaystyle C^{mat}_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4}}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\tau_{3}.\tau_{4})

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\hbar^{4}}\text{tr}\left\{\mu^{(+)}_{j_{1}}(\tau_{1})\mu^{(+)}_{j_{2}}(\tau_{2})\mu^{(-)}_{j_{4}}(\tau_{4})\mu^{(-)}_{j_{3}}(\tau_{3})\varrho_{sys}\right\}

(38)

\displaystyle\mu_{j}(t)

\displaystyle={\bf e}_{j}\cdot{\bf d}(t).

(39)

Likewise, the correlation function of the field operators is given by

\displaystyle C^{field}_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4}}({\bf r}_{mol},\tau_{1};{\bf r}_{mol},\tau_{2};{\bf r}_{mol},\tau_{3};{\bf r}_{mol},\tau_{4})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{tr}\left\{E^{(-)}_{j_{1}}({\bf r}_{mol},\tau_{1})E^{(-)}_{j_{2}}({\bf r}_{mol},\tau_{2})E^{(+)}_{j_{4}}({\bf r}_{mol},\tau_{4})E^{(+)}_{j_{3}}({\bf r}_{mol},\tau_{3})\varrho_{f}\right\}.

The impact of the polarization degrees of freedom can be calculated with a rotational average. For ETPA, this was done in Ref.

III.2.1
The molecular correlation function

We will evaluate the molecular correlation function first.
Using the Feynman diagram in Fig.
1
(c), we evaluate the correlation function straightforwardly to find

\displaystyle C^{mat}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\tau_{3}.\tau_{4})

\displaystyle=\sum_{e,e^{\prime}}\mu^{\ast}_{ge}\mu^{\ast}_{ef}\mu_{ge^{\prime}}\mu_{e^{\prime}f}e^{i(\omega_{eg}+i\gamma_{eg})(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})}

\displaystyle\times e^{i(\omega_{fg}+i\gamma_{fg})(\tau_{3}-\tau_{2})}e^{i(\omega_{fe^{\prime}}+i\gamma_{e^{\prime}f})(\tau_{4}-\tau_{3})}.

(41)

This expression includes the summation over all the virtual states
e,e^{\prime}
, through which the ETPA process can take place.
We have further added phenomenological dephasing rates
\gamma_{ge}
and
\gamma_{ef}
, which broaden the linewidths of the resonances. The correlation function is most conveniently evaluated by changing to the time delay integration variables
t_{1}=\tau_{2}-\tau_{1}
,
t_{2}=\tau_{3}-\tau_{2}
, and
t_{3}=\tau_{4}-\tau_{3}
, and sending
t_{0}\rightarrow-\infty

We are further interested in the limit
t\rightarrow\infty
, i.e. in the final population after the entangled pulse has interacted with the molecule.
In this limit, the
\tau_{4}
-integration simply evaluates to
2\pi\delta(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}-\omega_{3}-\omega_{4})
.
With the definition of the electric field operator (
5
), we arrive at

\displaystyle P_{f}({\bf r}_{mol},t\rightarrow\infty)

\displaystyle=2(2\pi)^{5}\Re\frac{E_{0}^{4}}{\hbar^{4}}\sum_{e,e^{\prime}}\mu^{\ast}_{ge}\mu^{\ast}_{ef}\mu_{ge^{\prime}}\mu_{e^{\prime}f}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!d\omega_{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!d\omega_{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!d\omega_{3}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!dt_{3}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!dt_{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!dt_{1}

\displaystyle\times e^{i(\omega_{1}-\omega_{eg}+i\gamma_{eg})t_{1}+i(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}-\omega_{fg}+i\gamma_{fg})t_{2}+i(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}-\omega_{3}-\omega_{fe^{\prime}}+i\gamma_{fe^{\prime}})t_{3}}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4}}\text{tr}\left\{\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j_{3}}({\bf r}_{mol},\omega_{3})\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j_{4}}({\bf r}_{mol},\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}-\omega_{3})\hat{a}_{j_{2}}(\omega_{2})\hat{a}_{{\bf r}_{mol},j_{1}}({\bf r}_{mol},\omega_{1})\varrho_{f}\right\}.

(42)

Here, we use the mixed representation of the field operators,

\displaystyle\hat{a}({\bf r},\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathds{R}^{2}}d^{2}q\;\hat{a}({\bf q},\omega)\;e^{i({\bf q}\cdot{\bf\rho}+k_{z}(\omega)z)}.

(43)

Evaluating the time integrals for the case of off-resonant intermediate states, we replace

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!dt_{1}\;e^{i(\omega_{1}-\omega_{eg}+i\gamma_{eg})t_{1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{\omega_{1}-\omega_{eg}+i\gamma_{eg}}\simeq\frac{i}{\omega_{p}/2-\omega_{eg}},

(44)

and likewise for the
t_{3}
-integration, where we find

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!dt_{3}\;e^{i(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}-\omega_{3}-\omega_{fe^{\prime}}+i\gamma_{fe^{\prime}})t_{3}}\simeq\frac{i}{\omega_{p}/2-\omega_{fe^{\prime}}}.

(45)

This approximation assumes that the photon bandwidth
\sim\Omega_{m}
is much smaller than the detuning to the electronic resonances, as indicated in Fig.
1
(b).
We then obtain

\displaystyle P_{f}({\bf r}_{mol},t\rightarrow\infty)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{\omega_{p}/2}{\hbar\epsilon_{0}n_{0}c}\right)^{2}\sum_{e,e^{\prime}}\frac{\mu^{\ast}_{ge}\mu^{\ast}_{ef}\mu_{ge^{\prime}}\mu_{e^{\prime}f}}{(\omega_{eg}-\omega_{p}/2)(\omega_{p}/2-\omega_{fe^{\prime}})}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!d\omega_{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!d\omega_{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!d\omega_{3}\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{\gamma_{fg}}{\gamma_{fg}^{2}+(\omega_{fg}-\omega_{1}-\omega_{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4}}\text{tr}\left\{\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j_{3}}({\bf r}_{mol},\omega_{3})\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j_{4}}({\bf r}_{mol},\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}-\omega_{3})\hat{a}_{j_{2}}(\omega_{2})\hat{a}_{{\bf r}_{mol},j_{1}}({\bf r}_{mol},\omega_{1})\varrho_{f}\right\}.

(46)

Here we have taken the real part explicitly, using that the molecular response encapsulated in the term
\sum_{e,e^{\prime}}\ldots
is real.

III.2.2
The field correlation function

We next turn to the remaining field correlation function, where we can use the input output relations (
19
) and (
20
) to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4}}\text{tr}\left\{\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j_{3}}({\bf r}_{mol},\omega_{3})\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j_{4}}({\bf r}_{mol},\omega_{4})\hat{a}_{j_{2}}(\omega_{2})\hat{a}_{{\bf r}_{mol},j_{1}}({\bf r}_{mol},\omega_{1})\varrho_{f}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f^{\ast}({\bf r}_{mol},\omega_{1},\omega_{2})f({\bf r}_{mol},\omega_{3},\omega_{4})+g({\bf r}_{mol},\omega_{1},\omega_{3})g({\bf r}_{mol},\omega_{2},\omega_{4})+g({\bf r}_{mol},\omega_{1},\omega_{4})g({\bf r}_{mol},\omega_{2},\omega_{3}),

(47)

where
\omega_{4}=\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}-\omega_{3}
and we defined

\displaystyle f({\bf r},\omega_{1},\omega_{2})

\displaystyle=e^{-i\omega_{p}t}\sum_{{\bf n}}\sinh(r_{{\bf n}}\Gamma)\cosh(r_{{\bf n}}\Gamma)\left(h_{{\bf n},s}({\bf r},\omega_{1})h_{{\bf n},i}({\bf r},\omega_{2})+h_{{\bf n},i}({\bf r},\omega_{1})h_{{\bf n},s}({\bf r},\omega_{2})\right)

(48)

and

\displaystyle g({\bf r},\omega_{1},\omega_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bf n}}\sinh^{2}(r_{{\bf n}}\Gamma)\left(h_{{\bf n},s}({\bf r},\omega_{1})h_{{\bf n},s}({\bf r},\omega_{2})+h_{{\bf n},i}({\bf r},\omega_{1})h_{{\bf n},i}({\bf r},\omega_{2})\right).

(49)

The Hermite functions of the signal mode (and similarly the idler modes) are given by

\displaystyle h_{{\bf n},s}({\bf r},\omega)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathds{R}^{2}}d^{2}q_{s}\;h_{{\bf n}}({\bf q}_{s},\omega_{s})e^{i{\bf q}_{s}\cdot{\bf\rho}}

\displaystyle=i^{n_{x}+n_{y}}e^{i{\bf q}_{s}^{(0)}{\bf\rho}}\;h_{n_{t}}\left(\frac{\omega-\omega_{p}/2}{\sqrt{\Omega_{m}\Omega_{p}}}\right)h_{n_{x}}\left(\sqrt{Q_{m}Q_{p}}x\right)h_{n_{y}}\left(\sqrt{Q_{m}Q_{p}}y\right).

(50)

The function
f
describes the absorption of a correlated photon pair, as it contains only pairs of signal and idler functions,
h_{{\bf n},s}
and
h_{{\bf n},i}
, respectively, whereas the function
g
corresponds to the sum of the autocorrelation contributions of the two beams.
We note that spatial and spectral correlations, which are encoded in the Schmidt numbers
r_{{\bf n}}
, are connected non-trivially in Eq. (
III.2.2
). The degrees of freedom can be separated only in the weak downconversion limit
r_{{\bf n}}\Gamma\ll 1
, when the light field is composed predominantly of temporally separate entangled photon pairs.
In the following, we will therefore consider three limiting case: first, we consider the two-photon limit when the correlations are in fact separable. Second, when the light field is confined to a single spatial mode and only spectral correlations matter, and, third, when there is only a single spectral mode, and spatial correlations affect the signal.

IV
The two-photon limit

We first analyze the two-photon limit of the output state (
22

|\psi_{BSV}\rangle\simeq|0\rangle+\Gamma\ldots\hat{a}_{s}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i}^{\dagger}|0\rangle

\displaystyle f_{low-gain}({\bf r}_{mol},\omega_{1},\omega_{2})

\displaystyle\simeq 2\times\Gamma F_{mom}({\bf\rho}_{mol},\rho_{mol})F_{spec}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2}),

(52)

where the Fourier transform of the momentum correlation function evaluated at the position of the molecule
{\bf\rho}_{mol}
reads

\displaystyle F_{mom}({\bf\rho}_{mol},\rho_{mol})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{\mathds{R}^{2}}d^{2}q_{s}\int_{\mathds{R}^{2}}d^{2}q_{i}\;F_{mom}({\bf Q}_{s},{\bf Q}_{i})e^{i({\bf q}_{s}+{\bf q}_{i}){\bf\rho}_{mol}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}Q_{m}Q_{p}\exp\left[-Q_{p}^{2}{\bf\rho}_{mol}^{2}+i({\bf q}_{s}^{(0)}+{\bf q}_{i}^{(0)})\cdot{\bf\rho}_{mol}\right].

(53)

The factor 2 at the beginning of Eq. (
52

48
) are symmetric with respect to the exchange
\omega_{s}\leftrightarrow\omega_{i}
and
{\bf Q}_{s}\leftrightarrow{\bf Q}_{i}
(the signal photon or the idler photon can be absorbed first, respectively).
These two excitation pathways contribute the same result and yield the overall factor 2.
Hence, the TPA rate (
31
) separates into a spatial and frequency integrals, which can be treated separately,

\displaystyle R^{TPA}_{low-gain}

\displaystyle=r_{0}p_{spat}p_{freq}.

(54)

The prefactor
r_{0}
can be read off from Eqs. (
III.2.1
) and (
31
),

\displaystyle r_{0}

\displaystyle=m_{0}f_{rep}\Gamma^{2}\left(\frac{\omega_{p}/2}{\hbar\epsilon_{0}n_{0}c}\right)^{2}\sum_{e,e^{\prime}}\frac{\mu_{ge}^{\ast}\mu_{ef}^{\ast}\mu_{e^{\prime}f}\mu_{ge^{\prime}}}{(\omega_{eg}-\omega_{p}/2)(\omega_{p}/2-\omega_{fe^{\prime}})}.

(55)

The spatial contribution
p_{spat}
, given by the modulus square of Eq. (
IV
) integrated over the transverse position
\rho
, readily evaluates to

\displaystyle r_{spat}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta z}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{\mathds{R}^{2}}d^{2}\rho_{mol}\left(\frac{Q_{m}Q_{p}}{\pi}\right)^{2}e^{-2Q_{p}^{2}(x^{2}+y^{2})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta zQ_{m}^{2}}{2\pi},

(56)

and finally the frequency contribution is given by

\displaystyle p_{freq}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!d\omega_{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!d\omega_{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!d\omega_{3}\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{\gamma_{fg}}{\gamma_{fg}^{2}+(\omega_{fg}-\omega_{1}-\omega_{2})^{2}}F_{spec}(\omega_{3},\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}-\omega_{3})F_{spec}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2}).

(57)

This last dimensionless quantity encodes the enhancement of the ETPA cross section due to frequency entanglement. It was analyzed by Raymer et al. in great detail
Raymer et al. 2020
;
Landes et al. 2021a
;
Landes et al. 2021b
;
Raymer et al. 2021a
;
Raymer et al. 2021b
, and depends crucially on the broadening
\gamma_{fg}
of the final molecular state. If the broadening is very small, as proposed by Kang et al.
Kang et al. 2020
for ETPA in organic chromophores, one obtains a very large enhancement. If it is as broad as suggested by Raymer and coworkers for molecules in solution, the enhancement due to spectral entanglement is almost completely eroded.
With the bigaussian model for the JSA (
15
), we can solve the above integrations analytically, when we extend the integration boundaries to
-\infty
.
In case of resonant excitation, where
\omega_{p}=\omega_{fg}
, we obtain

\displaystyle p_{freq}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Omega_{m}}{\Omega_{p}}\;\text{erfcx}\left(\frac{\gamma_{fg}}{\sqrt{2}\Omega_{p}}\right),

(58)

where we find the scaled complementary error function erfcx.
It quantifies how efficiently the light field can activate the full two-photon response of the molecule.

The
\gamma_{fg}
-dependence of the ETPA cross section, Eq. (

), is shown as a function of

.

To obtain the photon flux scaling from Eq. (
54
), and hence extract the ETPA cross section, we evaluate the photon flux density in the low-gain regime

\phi_{low-gain}\simeq\frac{f_{rep}}{A_{p}}2\Gamma^{2}\sum_{{\bf n}}r^{2}_{{\bf n}}=2\frac{f_{rep}}{A_{p}}\Gamma^{2}
.
The second equality follows from the geometric series (or, equivalently, from the normalization (
12
) of the JSA).
With Eq. (
32
), we then obtain the ETPA absorption rate

\displaystyle\sigma_{e}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{(2)}}{A_{e}T_{e}}\text{eff}(\gamma_{fg}/\Omega_{p}).

(59)

In the first term, we encounter the sought-after result (
1
) with the classical TPA cross section
Landes et al. 2021a

\displaystyle\sigma^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\omega_{p}/2}{\hbar\epsilon_{0}nc}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{2\gamma_{fg}}\sum_{e,e^{\prime}}\frac{\mu_{ge}^{\ast}\mu_{ef}^{\ast}\mu_{e^{\prime}f}\mu_{ge^{\prime}}}{(\omega_{eg}-\omega_{p}/2)(\omega_{p}/2-\omega_{fe^{\prime}})},

(60)

as well as the entanglement area

\displaystyle A_{e}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{(2\pi)^{2}}{Q_{m}^{2}}

(61)

and the entanglement time

\displaystyle T_{e}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{2\pi}{\Omega_{m}}.

(62)

In addition, the ’classical’ formula (
1
) is multiplied by the efficiency function

\displaystyle\text{eff}(x)

\displaystyle\equiv x\;\text{erfcx}\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2}}\right).

(63)

At large

, when
\gamma_{fg}\gg\Omega_{p}
, the function saturates to eff
(x)\simeq 0.8
.
This situation may be encountered in molecular TPA with cw entangled photons, where you can simply use

\displaystyle\sigma^{mol}_{e}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{\sigma^{(2)}}{A_{e}T_{e}},

(64)

in accordance with Fei et al. (
1
).
At small
x
, in contrast, i.e. when
\gamma_{fg}\ll\Omega_{p}
, we find eff
(x)\simeq x
.
In this limit, the pump bandwidth
\Omega_{p}
is so large that it limits the efficient excitation of the molecule.
It is important to keep in mind, however, that the classical TPA cross section is inversely proportional to the resonance broadening,
\sigma^{(2)}\propto 1/\gamma_{fg}
. Therefore, the above discussion must be understood at fixed
\gamma_{fg}
, and for varying pump bandwidth
\Omega_{p}
. In particular, it does
not
imply that a large broadening that a large broadening, which pushes eff
(x)
towards saturation, would be beneficial for ETPA. Instead, if we include the full dependence of
\sigma_{e}
on the broadening, we find that

\displaystyle\sigma_{e}(\gamma_{fg})\propto\frac{1}{\gamma_{fg}}\text{eff}\left(\frac{\gamma_{fg}}{\sqrt{2}\Omega_{p}}\right).

(65)

As shown in Fig.
2
, this function only decreases with increasing broadening
\gamma_{fg}
.
When
\gamma_{fg}\ll\Omega_{p}
, we have eff
(x)\simeq x
, such that the broadening dependence cancels and we obtain an ETPA cross section with
\gamma_{fg}
in Eq. (
60
) replaced by
\Omega_{p}
.
When
\gamma_{fg}\gg\Omega_{p}
, Eq. (
63
) becomes constant, such that
\sigma_{e}\propto 1/\gamma_{fg}
.

\displaystyle\phi

Correlated contribution

We then find from Eq. (

\displaystyle P_{f}^{corr}({\bf r}_{mol})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\omega_{p}/2}{\hbar\epsilon_{0}n_{0}c}\right)^{2}\sum_{e,e^{\prime}}\frac{\mu^{\ast}_{ge}\mu^{\ast}_{ef}\mu_{ge^{\prime}}\mu_{e^{\prime}f}}{(\omega_{eg}-\omega_{0})(\omega_{0}-\omega_{fe^{\prime}})}\tilde{h}^{4}({\bf r}_{mol})\sum_{n_{t},n_{t}^{\prime}}(-1)^{n_{t}+n^{\prime}_{t}}\sinh(r_{n_{t}}\Gamma)\cosh(r_{n_{t}}\Gamma)\sinh(r_{n_{t}^{\prime}}\Gamma)\cosh(r_{n_{t}^{\prime}}\Gamma)

When we extend the lower integration boundaries to

where

,
and

. Eq. (
V.1
) evaluates to

\displaystyle P_{f}^{corr}({\bf r}_{mol})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\omega_{p}/2}{\hbar\epsilon_{0}n_{0}c}\right)^{2}\sum_{e,e^{\prime}}\frac{\mu^{\ast}_{ge}\mu^{\ast}_{ef}\mu_{ge^{\prime}}\mu_{e^{\prime}f}}{(\omega_{eg}-\omega_{0})(\omega_{0}-\omega_{fe^{\prime}})}\tilde{h}^{4}({\bf r}_{mol})\sum_{n_{t},n_{t}^{\prime}}\sinh(r_{n_{t}}\Gamma)\cosh(r_{n_{t}}\Gamma)\sinh(r_{n_{t}^{\prime}}\Gamma)\cosh(r_{n_{t}^{\prime}}\Gamma)

\displaystyle\times\int_{w_{0}}^{\infty}\!\!dw\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{w_{fg}}{w_{fg}^{2}+(\frac{\omega_{fg}-\omega_{p}}{\sqrt{2\Omega_{m}\Omega_{p}}}+w)^{2}}L_{n_{t}}\left(w^{2}\right)L_{n_{t}^{\prime}}\left(w^{2}\right)\exp\left[-w^{2}\right]

(74)

where
w_{fg}=\gamma_{fg}/\sqrt{2\Omega_{m}\Omega_{p}}
and
w_{0}=-\omega_{p}/\sqrt{2\Omega_{p}\Omega_{m}}
.
Sending
w_{0}\rightarrow-\infty
, the remaining
w
-integration can be carried out analytically or numerically by Mathematica with high efficiency (albeit the integration becomes unstable for extremely highly entangled states).
Still, it is instructive to consider one limiting case first.

Narrow resonance

In the limit of a very narrow resonance, when
w_{fg}\ll 1
, we can replace

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{w_{fg}}{w_{fg}^{2}+(\frac{\omega_{fg}-\omega_{p}}{\sqrt{2\Omega_{m}\Omega_{p}}}+w)^{2}}

\displaystyle\simeq\delta\left(w+\frac{\omega_{fg}-\omega_{p}}{\sqrt{2\Omega_{m}\Omega_{p}}}\right).

(75)

We then obtain on resonance (
\omega_{fg}=\omega_{p}
) from Eq. (
V.1
)

\displaystyle P_{f}^{corr}({\bf r}_{mol})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\omega_{p}/2}{\hbar\epsilon_{0}n_{0}c}\right)^{2}\sum_{e,e^{\prime}}\frac{\mu^{\ast}_{ge}\mu^{\ast}_{ef}\mu_{ge^{\prime}}\mu_{e^{\prime}f}}{(\omega_{eg}-\omega_{0})(\omega_{0}-\omega_{fe^{\prime}})}\tilde{h}^{4}({\bf r}_{mol})

\displaystyle\times\left[\sum_{n_{t}}\sinh(r_{n_{t}}\Gamma)\cosh(r_{n_{t}}\Gamma)\right]^{2}

(76)

The expression can be interpreted in two limiting cases: At a low photon flux, when
\Gamma\ll 1
, we can approximate, using the definition of the Schmidt eigenvalues in Eq. (
II.3.1
),

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{t}}\sinh(r_{n_{t}}\Gamma)\cosh(r_{n_{t}}\Gamma)\bigg|_{\Gamma\ll 1}

\displaystyle\simeq\sum_{n_{t}}r_{n_{t}}\Gamma=\Gamma\sqrt{\frac{\Omega_{m}}{\Omega_{p}}}\propto\langle\hat{n}\rangle^{1/2},

(77)

The excitation probability thus becomes proportional to the photon flux.
With Eq. (
71
),
we obtain the cross section

\displaystyle R^{TPA,corr}_{low-gain,single-mode}

\displaystyle=N_{mol}\frac{\sigma^{(2)}}{A_{p}T_{e}}\frac{\gamma_{fg}}{\Omega_{p}}\phi,

(78)

where
T_{e}=2\pi/\Omega_{m}
is the entanglement time (
62
), and
A_{p}
the transverse beam area.
This coincides with Eq. (
59
) of the previous section in the limit of a narrow resonance, with the entanglement area
A_{e}
(
61
) replaced by the transverse beam area
A_{p}
.
At high gain
\Gamma\gg 1
, we approximate

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{t}}\sinh(r_{n_{t}}\Gamma)\cosh(r_{n_{t}}\Gamma)\bigg|_{\Gamma\gg 1}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{4}\sum_{n_{t}}e^{2r_{n_{t}}\Gamma}\simeq\langle\hat{n}\rangle/2.

(79)

This means, the excitation probability is proportional to the squared photon flux, and we obtain the TPA rate at high-gain

\displaystyle R^{TPA,corr}_{high-gain,single-mode}

\displaystyle=N_{mol}\sigma^{(2)}\frac{\gamma_{fg}}{2\Omega_{p}}\frac{1}{T_{pulse}f_{rep}}\times\phi^{2}.

(80)

Here we encounter the product of the laser pulse duration
T_{pulse}=2\pi/\Omega_{p}
and the laser repetition rate
f_{rep}
. This factor is also encountered in TPA with ultrafast lasers, and enhances the pulsed TPA absorption cross section relative to the sample’s cw cross section when
T_{pulse}f_{rep}\ll 1
.

V.2
Uncorrelated contribution

Just like in the above calculation of the correlated contribution, we find

\displaystyle P_{f}^{unc}({\bf r}_{mol})

\displaystyle=2\left(\frac{\omega_{p}/2}{\hbar\epsilon_{0}n_{0}c}\right)^{2}\sum_{e,e^{\prime}}\frac{\mu^{\ast}_{ge}\mu^{\ast}_{ef}\mu_{ge^{\prime}}\mu_{e^{\prime}f}}{(\omega_{eg}-\omega_{0})(\omega_{0}-\omega_{fe^{\prime}})}\tilde{h}^{4}({\bf r}_{mol})\sum_{n_{t},n_{t}^{\prime}}\sinh^{2}(r_{n_{t}}\Gamma)\sinh^{2}(r_{n_{t}^{\prime}}\Gamma)

\displaystyle\times\int dw\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{w_{fg}}{w_{fg}^{2}+(\frac{\omega_{fg}-\omega_{p}}{\sqrt{2\Omega_{m}\Omega_{p}}}+w)^{2}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{n!}{m!}}w^{m-n}L^{(m-n)}_{n}\left(w^{2}\right)e^{-w^{2}/2}\right)^{2},

(81)

where
m=\text{max}(n_{t},n_{t}^{\prime})
, and
n=\text{min}(n_{t},n_{t}^{\prime})
.

Narrow resonance

We again replace the Lorentzian resonance by a delta-function (
75
).
On resonance, when
\omega_{fg}=\omega_{p}
, Eq. (
81
) then simplifies to

\displaystyle P_{f}^{unc}({\bf r}_{mol})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\omega_{p}/2}{\hbar\epsilon_{0}n_{0}c}\right)^{2}\sum_{e,e^{\prime}}\frac{\mu^{\ast}_{ge}\mu^{\ast}_{ef}\mu_{ge^{\prime}}\mu_{e^{\prime}f}}{(\omega_{eg}-\omega_{0})(\omega_{0}-\omega_{fe^{\prime}})}\tilde{h}^{4}({\bf r}_{mol})

\displaystyle\times\sum_{n_{t}}\sinh^{4}(r_{n_{t}}\Gamma).

(82)

To extract the TPA cross section from this expression, we wish to express the corresponding TPA signal as
\delta_{r}\phi^{2}
, where
\phi^{2}\propto(\sum_{n}\sinh^{2}(r_{n}\Gamma))^{2}
, see Eq. (
71
). We thus need to analyze the term
\sum_{n}\sinh^{4}(r_{n}\Gamma)
.
Given the Schmidt number
K
of the PDC light, we expect that in the limit of many Schmidt modes, we have
\sim K
terms which contribute to this expression. In contrast, the squared mean photon number should contain
\sim K^{2}
terms. Thus, in a highly entangled light field, when the parameter
\zeta_{t}
in Eq. (
17
) is close to one, we expect that

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{t}}\sinh^{4}(r_{n_{t}}\Gamma)\sim\frac{1}{K}\left(\sum_{n_{t}}\sinh^{2}(r_{n_{t}}\Gamma)\right)^{2}.

(83)

This should be true at least for sufficiently small photon numbers, when many Schmidt modes contribute to the signal.
In this regime, we obtain

\displaystyle R^{TPA,unc}_{high-gain,single-mode}

\displaystyle=N_{mol}\sigma^{(2)}\frac{\gamma_{fg}}{\Omega_{m}}\frac{1}{T_{pulse}f_{rep}}\times\phi^{2}.

(84)

Hence, in contrast to the correlated contribution (
80
), where the ratio
\gamma_{fg}/\Omega_{p}
appears, here it is the ratio
\gamma_{fg}/\Omega_{m}
, i.e. the ratio between the molecular resonance and the bandwidth
\Omega_{m}
of the individual photons. As
\Omega_{m}\gg\Omega_{p}
in a highly entangled beam, this reduces the uncorrelated contribution to the cross section considerably.

This behaviour again changes in a very high gain regime, when
e^{r_{0}\Gamma}\gg e^{r_{1}\Gamma}
. In this regime, the largest Schmidt mode becomes dominant, and the ETPA signal approaches that of a single-mode squeezed state in this mode. Then the spectral correlations lose their importance, and the uncorrelated TPA rate becomes exactly twice the correlated contribution (
80
),

\displaystyle R^{TPA,unc}_{veryhigh-gain,single-mode}

\displaystyle=2R^{TPA,corr}_{high-gain,single-mode}.

(85)

60

Intensity dependence of narrow molecular resonance–

The transition of a nonlinear optical signal from the separate photon pairs to a multi-photon state was first analyzed in Ref.
Schlawin and Mukamel 2013
.
In the following, we explore this behaviour in the limit (
75
), were
\gamma_{fg}\ll\Omega_{p}
.
The increase of the full ETPA signal on resonance is shown in Fig.
4
(a) for three different bandwidths
\Omega_{m}
ranging from
1.5\Omega_{p}
(very weak entanglement) to
50\Omega_{p}
(very strong entanglement). At photon numbers below
\langle\hat{N}\rangle\lesssim 100
, the strong quantum correlations in the latter case afford a large advantage over the former one. Moreover, whereas the linear signal scaling gives way to a quadratic scaling at
\langle\hat{N}\rangle\simeq 1
in the case of weak entanglement, it remains linear up to
\langle\hat{N}\rangle\simeq 100
in the strongly entangled case.
The crossover takes place when the mean photon number per Schmidt mode
n_{t}
becomes of order one, i.e. when
\langle\hat{A}^{\dagger}_{n_{t}}\hat{A}_{n_{t}}\rangle\sim 1
. Using the Schmidt number
K
as the effective number of modes, we expect this to be the case when
\langle\hat{N}\rangle/K\sim 1
. In the case of large bandwidths,
\Omega_{m}=50\Omega_{p}
, we have, using Eq. (
29
),
K\simeq\Omega_{m}/(2\Omega_{p})
and thus expect the crossover to take place when
\langle\hat{N}\rangle\simeq 25
. This estimation is consistent with our observations, given that the Schmidt number only gives an estimate for the effective dimensionality of the entangled state.

However, this extended linear regime is not necessarily an advantage in terms of absolute signal strength, as Fig.
4
(a) also reveals: when we increase the mean photon number even further,
\langle\hat{N}\rangle>100
, the weakly entangled signal with
\Omega_{m}=1.5\Omega_{p}
becomes
stronger
than the other two. This is entirely due to the larger strength of the uncorrelated contribution on resonance. Its bandwidth is narrower, and thus gives rise to a narrower resonance, too [compare Fig.
3
(a)]. On balance, this can be enough to overpower the much larger correlated contributions of highly entangled states.
It appears that at these very large photon numbers, a few-mode or single-mode entangled state is in fact preferable over a (highly entangled) multimode state - at least insofar as the total signal strength is concerned.
But in many proposed applications of entangled photons for spectroscopy, one is instead interested, e.g., in exploiting the quantum correlations of the entangled pulses to control the excited states in a multilevel system
Schlawin et al. 2013
;
Raymer et al. 2013
;
Oka 2011a
;
Oka 2011b
;
Svozilík et al. 2018
;
Svozilík et al. 2018
;
León-Montiel et al. 2019
;
Fujihashi et al. 2020
;
Ishizaki 2020
;
Fujihashi and Ishizaki 2021
, and this control is enabled by the absorption of pairs of correlated photons. The present results thus demonstrate that such a control is feasible not only in a separate pair limit, where the overall signal strength is very low. It can also be achieved with pulses containing much larger photon numbers. However, these states may not be optimal for inducing the largest possible ETPA signal, and one has to accept the uncorrelated background signal.

\displaystyle r_{rel}\equiv\frac{P_{f}^{corr}}{P_{f}^{unc}},

where the two terms are calculated with Eqs. (

Conversely, at fixed photon number, the ratio increases quadratically with the bandwidth

VI
Single spectral mode

We next turn to the analysis of the third limiting case, which we discuss in this manuscript, where only a single spectral mode must be considered. Such a state could be prepared, for instance, by spectral filtering of the PDC light (at the cost of reducing the photon flux).
This situation is easier to deal with than that the previous Section
V
. It basically amounts to analysing the probability of localising two photons simultaneously at the position of the absorber. A molecular response function is not involved (or rather, it only gives the same contribution to correlated and uncorrelated terms in the final signal).
In the following, we use the following single spectral mode function

\displaystyle\tilde{h}(\omega)

\displaystyle=(\pi\Omega_{p}^{2})^{-1/4}\exp\left[-\frac{(\omega-\omega_{p}/2)}{2\Omega_{p}^{2}}\right]

(87)

which we obtain from Eq. (
15
) by setting
\Omega_{m}=\Omega_{p}
.
Adapting Eq. (
III.2.1
) for this situation, we obtain

\displaystyle P_{f}^{corr}=\sigma^{(2)}2\gamma_{fg}\text{spec}(\omega_{p}-\omega_{fg},\Omega_{p},\gamma_{fg})

\displaystyle\times\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{n_{x},n_{y},n^{\prime}_{x},n^{\prime}_{y}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\sinh(r_{n_{x},n_{y}}\Gamma)\cosh(r_{n_{x},n_{y}}\Gamma)\sinh(r_{n^{\prime}_{x},n^{\prime}_{y}}\Gamma)\cosh(r_{n^{\prime}_{x},n^{\prime}_{y}}\Gamma)

\displaystyle\times h_{n_{x}}^{2}(\sqrt{Q_{m}Q_{p}}x)h_{n^{\prime}_{x}}^{2}(\sqrt{Q_{m}Q_{p}}x)h_{n_{y}}^{2}(\sqrt{Q_{m}Q_{p}}y)h_{n^{\prime}_{y}}^{2}(\sqrt{Q_{m}Q_{p}}y).

(88)

The uncorrelated contribution likewise simplifies to

\displaystyle P_{f}^{unc}=\sigma^{(2)}4\gamma_{fg}\text{spec}(\omega_{p}-\omega_{fg},\Omega_{p},\gamma_{fg})

\displaystyle\times\sum_{n_{x},n_{y}}\sum_{n^{\prime}_{x},n^{\prime}_{y}}(-1)^{n_{x}+n_{y}+n^{\prime}_{x}+n^{\prime}_{y}}\sinh^{2}(r_{n_{x},n_{y}}\Gamma)\sinh^{2}(r_{n^{\prime}_{x},n^{\prime}_{y}}\Gamma)

\displaystyle\times h_{n_{x}}^{2}(\sqrt{Q_{m}Q_{p}}x)h_{n^{\prime}_{x}}^{2}(\sqrt{Q_{m}Q_{p}}x)h_{n_{y}}^{2}(\sqrt{Q_{m}Q_{p}}y)h_{n^{\prime}_{y}}^{2}(\sqrt{Q_{m}Q_{p}}y).

(89)

In these expressions, we have combined the spectral integrals into the function

\displaystyle\text{spec}(\omega_{p}-\omega_{fg},\Omega_{p},\gamma_{fg})

\displaystyle=\int d\omega_{1}\int d\omega_{2}\int d\omega_{sum}\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{\gamma_{fg}}{\gamma_{fg}^{2}+(\omega_{fg}-\omega_{sum})^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\tilde{h}(\omega_{sum}-\omega_{1})\tilde{h}(\omega_{1})\tilde{h}(\omega_{sum}-\omega_{2})\tilde{h}(\omega_{2})

\displaystyle=\Re\tilde{w}(\omega_{p}-\omega_{fg}+i\gamma_{fg}),

(90)

which describes the overlap between the spectral mode function and the molecular response. In the present case, where we assumed Gaussian spectral modes, it evaluate to the real part of the Faddeeva function
\tilde{w}
, as the response is given by the convolution of a Gaussian and a Lorentzian, i.e. by a Voigt profile.
The only difference between the two terms in Eqs. (
VI
) and (
VI
) - apart from a factor 2 - is the scaling behaviour
\sim\sinh^{2}\cosh^{2}
vs
\sim\sinh^{4}
and the factor
(-1)^{n_{x}+n_{y}+n^{\prime}_{x}+n^{\prime}_{y}}
, which reduces the uncorrelated contribution, respectively. This contrasts with the previous section, where correlated and uncorrelated contributions were convoluted differently with the molecular response function, even in the limit of a a very narrow resonance (
75
).

VI
) and (
VI
) is shown in Fig.

for different mean photon numbers and a weakly entangled state with
Q_{m}=1.5Q_{p}

Q_{m}=10Q_{p}
. The uncorrelated contribution is shown as a dashed, gray line. In the strongly entangled case, the latter is much more narrowly peaked, signifying that ETPA is possible only in a very narrow area with the largest photon density.

VII
Conclusions

In this manuscript, we have investigated entangled two-photon absorption cross sections of pulsed entangled beams from the low- to the high-gain regime of parametric downconversion.
We have first presented a derivation of Fei’s seminal formula for the ETPA cross section (
1
) starting from a microscopic model of light.
Eq. (
1
) relies on a factorization of the entangled photon wavefunction into spatial and spectral components. When these degrees of freedom are quantum correlated
Brambilla et al. 2004
;
Gatti et al. 2009
, such that they cannot be factorized, the above formula does not necessarily apply and new effects relying on the joint spatio-temporal entanglement might be expected.

Our analysis shows that the formula (
1
) proposed by Fei et al. applies only in the limit of a very narrow (compared to the pump laser bandwidth) molecular resonance. A more realistic treatment, which accounts for a finite broadening, gives rise to the result (
59
). The finite resonance broadening leads to a reduction of the cross section when the experiment is conducted with continuous-wave entangled photon pairs.
This finding could explain some of the unsuccessful attempts at detecting ETPA with continuous-wave entangled photon pairs.
Moreover, we have clarified the microscopic origin of the entanglement time and the entanglement area, which appear in Eq. (
1
). These quantities are directly connected to the amount of entanglement in the photonic state only in certain limits (i.e. when
\Omega_{m}\gg\Omega_{p}
and
Q_{m}\gg Q_{p}
). Still, even in situations where this is not the case ( for instance, when both
\Omega_{m}
and
\Omega_{p}
are large), small entanglement times and the entanglement areas are always beneficial in enhancing the ETPA cross section.
In such a situation, we may still have
quantum
enhancement (due to the linear scaling brought about by the two-photon nature of the state), but it is not due to quantum entanglement.
As both the entanglement time and the entanglement area are controlled by properties of the nonlinear crystal where the entangled photons are generated, these findings should be reflected in the design of novel quantum light sources for spectroscopy
Moretti et al. 2023
.

We have further investigated the crossover from linear to quadratic regime of ETPA. We have seen that ETPA can benefit from the same enhancement as TPA with ultrafast lasers. We have further shown that anticipated crossover behaviour according to Eq. (
2
) is incorrect, as the probability for the absorption of correlated photon pairs also increases quadratically with the photon number at sufficiently large photon fluxes. Thus, the cross section in the quadratic regime is composed of two contributions, and quantum enhancement effects can persist to very large photon numbers.
They are lost only when the multimode nature of the light field becomes negligible. We point out, however, that in this very high-gain regime, corrections to the simple PDC model we employed in this manuscript may become substantial and will require a numerically more involved treatment of the light source
Christ et al. 2013
.
In this discussion, we have considered degenerate PDC. This is in fact the worst case scenario, where the uncorrelated contributions can als drive TPA resonantly. In non-degenerate PDC, this background would be suppressed.

In this manuscript, we have only treated the impact of spatial and spectral correlations separately. As one can see from the basic equations we derived here, their interplay may become highly nontrivial and give rise to interesting new effects in the high gain regime, which could be very appealing, e.g., for applications in nonlinear quantum imaging
Moreau et al. 2019
;
Ma and Doughty 2021
;
Bowen et al. 2023
or spatially resolved spectroscopy. This will be an interesting direction for future research.
Overall, our work highlights the interesting effects one can expect from nonlinear light-matter interactions of high-gain PDC light
Gilaberte Basset et al. 2019
;
Spasibko et al. 2017
;
Cutipa and Chekhova 2022
.
