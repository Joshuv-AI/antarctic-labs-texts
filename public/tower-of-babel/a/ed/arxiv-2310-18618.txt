Subspace projection regularization for large-scale Bayesian linear inverse problems
Haibo Li

Abstract
The Bayesian statistical framework provides a systematic approach to enhance the regularization model by incorporating prior information about the desired solution. For the Bayesian linear inverse problems with Gaussian noise and Gaussian prior, we propose an iterative regularization algorithm within the subspace projection regularization (SPR) framework. By treating the forward model matrix as a linear operator between two proper finite dimensional Hilbert spaces, we first introduce an iterative process that can generate a series of valid solution subspaces. The SPR method then projects the original problem onto these solution subspaces to get a series of low dimensional linear least squares problems, where an efficient procedure is developed to update the solutions of them to approximate the desired solution of the original problem. With the newly designed early stopping rules, this iterative algorithm can obtain a regularized solution with satisfied accuracy. We establish several theoretical results to reveal the regularization properties of the algorithm. Both small-scale and large-scale inverse problems are used to test the proposed algorithm and demonstrate its robustness and efficiency. The most computationally intensive operations in the proposed algorithm only involve matrix-vector products, making it highly efficient for large-scale problems.

Introduction

Inverse problems arise frequently in various scientific and engineering fields such as medical imaging, computed tomography, geoscience, data assimilation and so on
Kaip2006
;
Hansen2006
;
Buzug2008
;
Law2015
;
Richter2016
. In these problems, we aim to reconstruct unknown parameters or functions from indirect measurements that often include noise. In this paper, we consider linear inverse problems of the discrete form

\boldsymbol{b}=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\ \ \boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{M}),

(1.1)

where
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the underlying quantity we wish to reconstruct,
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is the discretized forward model matrix,
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the vector of observation, and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
is the noise that follows a zero mean Gaussian distribution with positive definite covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{M}
. In the sense of Hadamard
hadamard1923lectures
, this problem is usually ill-posed, which means that there may be multiple solutions that fit the observation equally well, or the solution is very sensitive respect to observation perturbation. The ill-posedness of inverse problems is a big challenge that prohibits us from computing an accurate solution by a direct inversion method.

Regularization is a very common technique to overcome ill-posedness, where the core idea is to use prior knowledge about the underlying solution to constrain the set of possible solutions and improve their stability and uniqueness properties. Here we deal with the inverse problem (
1.1
) and its regularization from a Bayesian perspective
Kaip2006
;
Stuart2010
, where the unknown and observation are modeled as random variables, and prior knowledge and assumptions about the unknown are combined with the observation to obtain a posterior probability distribution of
\boldsymbol{x}
. By (
1.1
), the observation vector
\boldsymbol{b}
has a conditional probability density function (pdf)

p(\boldsymbol{b}|\boldsymbol{x})\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}^{2}\right).

Maximizing
p(\boldsymbol{b}|\boldsymbol{x})
with respect to
\boldsymbol{x}
is known as the maximum likelihood estimate (MLE), where
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{B}}:=(\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{x})^{1/2}
is the
\boldsymbol{B}
-norm of
\boldsymbol{x}
for a positive definite matrix
\boldsymbol{B}
. To regularize MLE, we assume a Gaussian prior about the desired solution with the form
\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\lambda^{-1}\boldsymbol{N})
, where
\boldsymbol{N}
is a positive definite covariance matrix. Using the Bayes’ formula that estimates posterior pdf of
\boldsymbol{x}
after observation
\boldsymbol{b}
, we have

p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{b},\lambda)\propto p(\boldsymbol{x}|\lambda)p(\boldsymbol{b}|\boldsymbol{x})\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}^{2}-\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}^{2}\right).

The maximum a posterior estimate (MAP) of
\boldsymbol{x}
finds a regularized solution to (
1.1
) by maximizing the posterior pdf of
\boldsymbol{x}
, which leads to

\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}^{2}+\lambda\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}^{2}\}.

(1.2)

The form of (
1.2
) is the same as the Tikhonov regularization
Tikhonov1977
, where the regularization term
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}^{2}
enforces extra structure on the solution that comes from the prior distribution of
\boldsymbol{x}
. The regularization parameter
\lambda
controls the trade-off between the data-fit term and regularization term, which should be determined in advance.

The Bayesian statistical framework offers a systematic approach to enhance the regularization model by incorporating prior information about the solution. However, developing efficient algorithms for computing those informative solutions is very challenging. Firstly, the quality of the reconstructed solution heavily relies on the accuracy of the regularization parameter. Although various methods have been proposed, such as the L-curve (LC) method
Hansen1992
, generalized cross-validation (GCV)
Golub1979
and discrepancy principle (DP)
Morozov1966
, but each has its limitations and assumptions, and these methods require solving (
1.2
) for many different values of
\lambda
. Secondly, to solve (
1.2
), the Cholesky factorizations
\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}=\boldsymbol{L}_{M}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}_{M}
and
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}=\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}_{N}
are required to transform (
1.2
) to the standard 2-norm form

\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\{\|\boldsymbol{L}_{M}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b})\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|\boldsymbol{L}_{N}\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}^{2}\}

(1.3)

if an iterative solver for the above least squares problem is exploited; or
\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}
and
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}
must be computed if an iterative solver for the normal equation of (
1.2
) is exploited. For both the cases, it is not feasible to work with the matrix inversion and factorization if its dimension is very large or the explicit form of the matrix is not available.

Up to now, there are many numerical methods that deal with large-scale Bayesian linear inverse problems; here we review iterative methods that are based upon Krylov subspaces
liesen2013krylov
. The main idea behind these methods is to project the original linear system (
1.1
) onto a series of suitable low dimensional subspaces and solve these small projected problems step by step, and the iteration should be terminated early to avoid too many noisy components included in the solution
Kaip2006
;
Calvetti2018
. In this approach, a key point is that these subspaces must be elaborately constructed by an iterative process that can incorporate information about the prior distribution of
\boldsymbol{x}
. To this end, the priorconditioning technique is proposed, where the statistically inspired left and right priorconditioners are used to whiten the noise and unknown, respectively
calvetti2005priorconditioners
;
calvetti2017priorconditioned
;
Calvetti2018
. However, it usually requires that
\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}
and its Cholesky factorization can be computed easily, and
\boldsymbol{L}_{N}
instead of
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}
should be explicitly constructed as the right priorconditioner. In some scenarios of applications, the covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{N}
instead of
\boldsymbol{L}_{N}
is used to add prior information about
\boldsymbol{x}
, such as the Metérn class of covariance matrices
Genton2001classes
;
Roininen2011correlation
;
antil2023efficient
. This raises the need for new methods to construct suitable solution subspaces to incorporate prior information about
\boldsymbol{x}
while avoiding Cholesky factorizations and inversions of the prior covariance matrices.

Recently, a generalized hybrid iterative method is proposed for large-scale Bayesian linear inverse problems
Chung2017
, which is based on an iterative process called the generalized Golub-Kahan bidiagonalization (gen-GKB)
Arioli2013
. This method first transforms (
1.2
) to

\min_{\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{N}\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}^{2}+\lambda\|\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{\boldsymbol{N}}^{2}\}

(1.4)

by the substitution
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{N}\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
to avoid the inversion of
\boldsymbol{N}
. Then it uses gen-GKB to generate a series of
\boldsymbol{N}
-orthonormal bases of
k
-dimensional Krylov subspaces for
k=1,2,\dots
and projects (
1.4
) onto these subspaces to get a series of
k
-dimensional Tikhonov regularization problems. To avoid choosing a good regularization parameter in advance, this method adapts a hybrid regularization approach, which determines a regularization parameter
\lambda_{k}
for the projected
k
-dimensional problem at each
k
-th step using a parameter choosing criterion such as GCV
Kilmer2001
. By determining
\lambda_{k}
at each iteration, this method computes a series of iterative solutions which are used to approximate the best solution to (
1.4
) with the optimal parameter
\lambda_{opt}
.

The gen-GKB based hybrid regularization method is reasonable in the sense that the iterative solution lies within the same subspace as the MAP estimator of the original Bayesian inverse problem (
1.2
), thereby the prior information of
\boldsymbol{x}
encoded by the Gaussian prior can be well incorporated into the iterative solution. Furthermore, the computations of
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}
and factorizations of
\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}
and
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}
are not needed, which makes it very practical for solving large-scale inverse problems. Although this method is effective for many types of inverse problems, sometimes the regularization parameter
\lambda_{k}
can not be well determined, resulting in that
\lambda_{k}
does not converge to
\lambda_{opt}
or converges very slowly, and the corresponding iterative solution have a poor accuracy; this is a common potential flaw for hybrid methods
Chungnagy2008
;
Renaut2017
. Besides, this method needs to transform (
1.2
) to (
1.4
) first to apply gen-GKB, and there is not a direct motivation provided to explain why gen-GKB should be used to generate valid solution subspaces for (
1.2
).

In order to avoid choosing regularization parameter in advance for (
1.2
) or step by step for the generalized hybrid method, we consider the iterative subspace projection regularization (SPR) method
(
Engl2000
, §3.3)
. For the Bayesian linear inverse problem (
1.2
), the core idea is to seek a series of
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}
as the solution to

\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{k}}\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}},\ \ \mathcal{X}_{k}=\{\boldsymbol{x}:\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{S}_{k}}\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}\},

where
\mathcal{S}_{k}
is the subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of dimension
k=1,2,\dots
, and the iteration proceeds until an early stopping rule is satisfied to overcome too many noisy components are contained. We call
\mathcal{S}_{k}
the
solution subspace
, which should be constructed elaborately such that the information about the Gaussian noise
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
and desired property of
\boldsymbol{x}
encoded by the Gaussian prior can be incorporated into it. In this paper, we propose an iterative regularization algorithm within the SPR framework based on an iterative process that is equivalent to gen-GKB. This new algorithm exhibits good performance for indirectly solving the Bayesian linear inverse problem (
1.2
) and is very efficient for large-scale problems.

The main contributions of this paper are listed as follows:

\bullet

By treating the matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
as a bounded linear operator between the two Hilbert spaces
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}})
and
(\mathbb{R}^{m},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}})
, which are equipped with the
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}
- and
\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}
-inner products, we introduce an iterative process that can generate a series of
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}
-orthonormal bases of
k
-dimensional subspaces of
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}})
. This process is mathematically equivalent to gen-GKB, thereby we use the same name for it. The use of the new inner products instead of the standard ones provides a very natural view for developing iterative regularization methods for Bayesian linear inverse problems.

\bullet

With the solution subspace spanned by the
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}
-orthonormal basis generated by gen-GKB, at the
k
-th step the SPR method reduces the original problem to a
k
-dimensional standard linear least squares problem. We further design an efficient procedure that can quickly update the iterative solution
x_{k}
without solving the
k
-dimensional problem directly; the residual norm
\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}
and solution norm
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}
can also be updated quickly without computing
\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}
and
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}
. Using the residual norm or solution norm, we design three early stopping rules based on DP, LC and GCV, respectively. The most computationally intensive operations in the proposed algorithm only involve matrix-vector, thereby it is very efficient for large-scale problems.

\bullet

Several theoretical results related to the regularization properties of the proposed algorithm are established. Using the generalized singular value decomposition (GSVD) of
\{\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{L}_{N}\}
, we give an explicit expression of the solution subspace and build connections between it and the best solution subspace for the SPR method (i.e. the subspace spanned by the dominant right generalized singular vectors). We prove that the iterative solution has a filtered GSVD expansion form, where some dominant GSVD components are captured and the others are filtered out. All these results reveal that the solution subspace can incorporate the information about the Gaussian noise
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
and desired property of
\boldsymbol{x}
encoded by the Gaussian prior, thereby the proposed algorithm can get a good regularized solution with similar accuracy as the best Tikhonov solution to (
1.2
).

We use both small-scale and large-scale inverse problems to test the proposed algorithm and compare it with the generalized hybrid method in
Chung2017
. The experimental results show that our algorithm is more robust and efficient for large-scale Bayesian linear inverse problems.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we give the expression of the solution to (
1.2
) by using GSVD of
\{\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{L}_{N}\}
. In Section
3
, we derive the gen-GKB process by treating
\boldsymbol{A}
as a bounded linear operator between the two Hilbert spaces
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}})
and
(\mathbb{R}^{m},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}})
, and then propose the gen-GKB based SPR algorithm with proper early stopping rules. In Section
4
, we establish several theoretical results to reveal the regularization properties of the proposed algorithm. Numerical results are presented in Section
5
, and conclusions are provided in Section
6
.

2
Solving the Bayesian linear inverse problem by GSVD

For analyzing regularized inverse problems, GSVD is a useful tool
Hansen1989
. Since (
1.3
) and (
1.2
) are equivalent, we use the GSVD of the matrix pair
\{\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{L}_{N}\}
to discuss some properties of the MAP estimator that is the solution to (
1.3
). Since
\boldsymbol{L}_{N}
is invertible, the GSVD of
\{\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{L}_{N}\}
can be written as

\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{U}_{A}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{A}\boldsymbol{Z}^{-1},\ \ \boldsymbol{L}_{N}=\boldsymbol{U}_{L}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{L}\boldsymbol{Z}^{-1},

(2.1)

with

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{A}=\vtop{\hbox{$\left(\vbox{\vbox{\halign{ \hfil$#$\hfil\kern 2\p@\kern\@leftbwd& \thinspace\hfil$#$\hfil&& \quad\hfil$#$\hfil\cr\boldsymbol{D}_{A}$\hfil\kern 2.0pt\kern 6.66669pt&&r\crcr\kern 2.0pt\cr$\hfil\kern 2.0pt\kern 6.66669pt&\boldsymbol{0}&m-r\cr r$\hfil\kern 2.0pt\kern 6.66669pt&n-r\crcr}}}\kern 0.0pt\right)\kern-6.66669pt\kern 0.0pt$}\kern 44.86485pt},\ \ \ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{L}=\vtop{\hbox{$\left(\vbox{\vbox{\halign{ \hfil$#$\hfil\kern 2\p@\kern\@leftbwd& \thinspace\hfil$#$\hfil&& \quad\hfil$#$\hfil\cr\boldsymbol{D}_{L}$\hfil\kern 2.0pt\kern 6.66669pt&&r\crcr\kern 2.0pt\cr$\hfil\kern 2.0pt\kern 6.66669pt&\boldsymbol{I}&n-r\cr r$\hfil\kern 2.0pt\kern 6.66669pt&n-r\crcr}}}\kern 0.0pt\right)\kern-6.66669pt\kern 0.0pt$}\kern 44.86485pt},

(2.2)

where
\boldsymbol{D}_{A}=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{r})
,
1>\sigma_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{r}>0
and
\boldsymbol{D}_{L}=\mathrm{diag}(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{r})
,
0<\mu_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\mu_{r}<1
, and it holds that
\sigma_{i}^{2}+\mu_{i}^{2}=1
. Throughout the paper, we denote by
\boldsymbol{I}
and
\boldsymbol{0}
the identity matrix and zero matrix/vector, respectively, with orders clear from the context. The two matrices
\boldsymbol{U}_{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
and
\boldsymbol{U}_{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
are orthogonal, and
\boldsymbol{Z}
is invertible. Let
\boldsymbol{Z}=(\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{z}_{n})
. Define
\gamma_{i}=\sigma_{i}/\mu_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq r
and
\gamma_{i}=0
for
r+1\leq i\leq n
. We call
\gamma_{i}
the
i
-th largest generalized singular value and
\boldsymbol{z}_{i}
the
i
-th leading right generalized singular vector. Note that

\boldsymbol{Z}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A})\boldsymbol{Z}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{A},\ \ \ \boldsymbol{Z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}\boldsymbol{Z}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{L}^{2}.

(2.3)

Therefore,
\gamma_{i}^{2}
are generalized eigenvalues of the matrix pair
\{\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}\}
with
\boldsymbol{z}_{i}
the corresponding generalized eigenvectors
(
Golub2013
, §8.7)
. Since
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}
is positive definite, we can always normalize
\boldsymbol{z}_{i}
such that they are mutually
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}
-orthonormal, i.e.
\langle\boldsymbol{z}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{j}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}=\delta_{ij}
, where
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker symbol and
\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}:=\boldsymbol{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}
is the
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}
-inner product defined in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In the following part, we always assume that
\boldsymbol{z}_{i}
are mutually
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}
-orthonormal. Note that in this case,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{L}=\boldsymbol{I}
and
\gamma_{i}=\sigma_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq r
.

With the help of GSVD, the solution to (
1.3
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=[(\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{A})^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{A}+\lambda\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}_{N}]^{-1}(\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{A})^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{b}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{\gamma_{i}^{2}}{\gamma_{i}^{2}+\lambda}\frac{\boldsymbol{u}_{A,i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{b}}{\sigma_{i}}\boldsymbol{z}_{i}

(2.4)

where
\boldsymbol{u}_{A,i}
is the
i
-th column of
\boldsymbol{U}_{A}
. This is just the filtered GSVD expansion form solution for the MAP estimator
\boldsymbol{x}_{\lambda}
, where the filter factors
f_{i}:=\gamma_{i}^{2}/(\gamma_{i}^{2}+\lambda)
are applied to noisy coefficients
\boldsymbol{u}_{A,i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{b}
to dampen noisy components appeared in the regularized solution
Hansen1989
. The discrete Picard condition (DPC) plays a central role in the regularization of linear inverse problems, which says that the Fourier coefficients
|\boldsymbol{u}_{A,i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{b}_{\text{true}}|
on average decay to zero faster than the corresponding
\gamma_{i}
, where
\boldsymbol{b}_{\text{true}}=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{true}}
is the true observation
hansen1990discrete
. Based on DPC, the regularization parameter
\lambda
should be carefully chosen such that
f_{i}\approx 1
for small
i
and
f_{i}\approx 0
for large
i
.

To avoid determining
\lambda
in advance, the most natural alternative approach is to directly let
f_{i}=1
for small index and
f_{i}=0
for large index, which leads to the so called truncated GSVD (TGSVD) solution

x_{k}^{\text{tgsvd}}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\boldsymbol{u}_{A,i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{b}}{\sigma_{i}}\boldsymbol{z}_{i}.

(2.5)

By truncating the TGSVD solution at a proper
k
, the obtained solution can capture main information corresponding to dominant right generalized singular vectors while suppressing noise corresponding to others
Hansen1989
. These dominant
\boldsymbol{z}_{i}
play an important role in the regularized solution, since they contain the desirable information about
\boldsymbol{x}
encoded by the Gaussian prior.

For large-scale Bayesian inverse problems, the Cholesky factorizations of
\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}
and
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}
and the GSVD are very hard to compute. In this case, the generalized hybrid iterative method proposed in
Chung2017
is a good choice, which first transforms (
1.2
) to (
1.4
) and then uses gen-GKB to project (
1.2
) onto low dimensional subspaces to get small-scale problems. Although this method is effective for many types of inverse problems, sometimes the regularization parameter
\lambda_{k}
can not be well determined, resulting in that
\lambda_{k}
does not converge to
\lambda_{opt}
or converges very slowly, and the corresponding iterative solution can only have a poor accuracy.

In the following part, we approach the Bayesian linear inverse problem from the perspective of subspace projection regularization instead of Tikhonov regularization. This perspective provides a direct motivation to derive our new iterative process for generating solution subspaces. Based on this iterative process, we propose the new iterative regularization algorithm.

3
Subspace projection regularization based on generalized Golub-Kahan bidiagonalization

The
subspace projection regularization
(SPR) is a unified framework for developing iterative regularization methods
(
Engl2000
, §3.3)
. For the Bayesian linear inverse problem (
1.2
), the core idea is to compute a series of
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}
as the solution to

\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{k}}\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}},\ \ \mathcal{X}_{k}=\{\boldsymbol{x}:\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{S}_{k}}\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}\},

(3.1)

where
\mathcal{S}_{k}
is the subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of dimension
k=1,2,\dots
, and the iteration should be early stopped to overcome containing too many noisy components into the solution.

Remark 3.1

The TGSVD solution
x_{k}^{\text{tgsvd}}
is the
k
-th SPR solution if we choose
\mathcal{S}_{k}=\mathrm{span}\{\boldsymbol{Z}_{k}\}
for
k=1,2,\dots,r
, where
\boldsymbol{Z}_{k}=(\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{z}_{k})
. Throughout the paper, we denote by
\mathrm{span}\{\cdot\}
the subspace spanned by columns of a matrix or a group of vectors. To see it, let
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{Z}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}
to denote any vector in
\mathrm{span}\{\boldsymbol{Z}_{k}\}
, where
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{S}_{k}}\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}

\displaystyle=\min_{\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{Z}_{k}y}\|\boldsymbol{L}_{M}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b})\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\min_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\|\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{Z}_{k}y-\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{b}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\min_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\|\boldsymbol{U}_{A,k}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{A,k}y-\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{b}\|_{2},

where
\boldsymbol{U}_{A,k}
is the first
k
part of
\boldsymbol{U}_{A}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{A,k}
is the first
k
-by-
k
part of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
, and the last equality uses the GSVD relation (
2.1
). The above least squares problem has a unique solution
\boldsymbol{y}_{k}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{A,k}^{-1}\boldsymbol{U}_{A,k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{b}
. Thus, the solution to (
3.1
) is
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}=\boldsymbol{Z}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\boldsymbol{u}_{A,i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{b}}{\sigma_{i}}\boldsymbol{z}_{i}
, which is just
x_{k}^{\text{tgsvd}}
.

In fact, for any choice of
\mathcal{S}_{k}
, there exists a unique solution to (
3.1
). Using the same method as the above, let
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}
be any vector in
\mathcal{S}_{k}
, where
\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
whose columns are
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}
-orthonormal and span
\mathcal{S}_{k}
. Then the solution to (
3.1
) satisfies
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}=\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}_{k}
, where
\boldsymbol{y}_{k}
is the solution to

\min_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{Y}_{k}}\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2},\ \ \mathcal{Y}_{k}=\{\boldsymbol{x}:\min_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\|\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{b}\|_{2}\}.

This problem has a unique solution
\boldsymbol{y}_{k}=(\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{W}_{k})^{{\dagger}}\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{b}
, where
\boldsymbol{C}^{{\dagger}}
is the pseudoinverse of a matrix
\boldsymbol{C}
. Therefore, there exits a unique
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}
.

Comparing to computing the MAP estimator
\boldsymbol{x}_{\lambda}
in the Tikhonov regularization framework (
1.2
), the SPR framework utilizes those elaborately constructed subspaces
\mathcal{S}_{k}
to enforce additional structure on
\boldsymbol{x}
, which plays a similar role as the Tikhonov regularization term
\lambda\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}
arising from the Gaussian prior of
\boldsymbol{x}
. We call
\mathcal{S}_{k}
the
solution subspace
, which should be constructed such that the prior information about the desired solution is incorporated into it. Based on the expressions of
\boldsymbol{x}_{\lambda}
and
x_{k}^{\text{tgsvd}}
, the most ideal choice is
\mathcal{S}_{k}=\mathrm{span}\{\boldsymbol{Z}_{k}\}
, which just leads to the TGSVD solution.

3.1
Generalized Golub-Kahan bidiagonalization

For large-scale problems, computing GSVD is impractical. A reasonable SPR method should construct a series of solution subspaces that capture main information corresponding to some leading right generalized singular vectors
\{\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
as the iteration proceeds. Note that
\boldsymbol{z}_{i}
are mutually
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}
-orthonormal, and note that (
3.1
) has the form of weighted least squares. This motivates us to design an iterative process that generates a series of
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}
-orthonormal vectors and projects (
3.1
) onto subspaces spanned by these vectors.

Definition 1

Define two finite dimensional Hilbert spaces as
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}})
and
(\mathbb{R}^{m},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}})
that are equipped with the
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}
-inner product
\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}:=\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}
and
\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}
-inner product
\langle\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}:=\boldsymbol{y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}
, respectively.

Although the two topologies of
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}
- and standard inner product spaces are equivalent, the geometry structures of these two spaces are different. For dealing with (
3.1
), using
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}
- and
\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}
-inner product is more natural. In order to develop iterative process for
\boldsymbol{A}
between the two spaces
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}})
and
(\mathbb{R}^{m},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}})
, we first need the adjoint of the linear operator
\boldsymbol{A}
.
For the linear operator
\boldsymbol{A}:(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}})\to(\mathbb{R}^{m},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}})
defined as
\boldsymbol{A}:\boldsymbol{x}\mapsto\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}
for
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
under the canonical bases of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, define its adjoint
\boldsymbol{A}^{*}:(\mathbb{R}^{m},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}})\to(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}})
as
\boldsymbol{A}^{*}:\boldsymbol{y}\mapsto\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{y}
for
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, by the relation

\langle\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}=\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{y}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}.

Since the two Hilbert spaces are finite dimensional, it follows that
\boldsymbol{A}
is bounded, and the adjoint operator
\boldsymbol{A}^{*}
is a well-defined linear operator. Under the above two definitions, we can develop an iterative process that reduces
\boldsymbol{A}
to a bidiagonal form and generates two subspaces of
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}})
and
(\mathbb{R}^{m},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}})
, respectively. This can be achieved by applying the GKB process to
\boldsymbol{A}
with starting vector
\boldsymbol{b}
between the two Hilbert spaces
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}})
and
(\mathbb{R}^{m},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}})
; for the GKB process for compact linear operator, see
caruso2019convergence
. The basic recursive relations are as follows:

\displaystyle\beta_{1}\boldsymbol{u}_{1}=\boldsymbol{b},

(3.2)

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}=\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{u}_{i}-\beta_{i}\boldsymbol{v}_{i-1},

(3.3)

\displaystyle\beta_{i+1}\boldsymbol{u}_{i+1}=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}-\alpha_{i}\boldsymbol{u}_{i},

(3.4)

where
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{i}
are computed such that
\|\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}=\|\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\|_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}=1
, and we let
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}=\boldsymbol{0}
. In order to compute
\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{u}_{i}
, we need the matrix-form expression of
\boldsymbol{A}^{*}
.

Lemma 3.1

Under the canonical bases of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we have

\boldsymbol{A}^{*}=\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}.

(3.5)

Proof

Under the canonical bases, the linear mapping
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{y}
are the standard matrix-vector products. By the definition of
\boldsymbol{A}^{*}
, we have

(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x})^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{y}

for any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Therefore we have
\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}=\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}^{*}
, and the desired result is immediately obtained. ∎

In order to avoid explicitly computing
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}
-norm to obtain
\alpha_{i}
, let
\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}=\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{u}_{i}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}=\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}
. By (
3.3
) and (
3.5
), we have

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}=\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}-\beta_{i}\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i-1}.

(3.6)

Let
\bar{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}=A^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}-\beta_{i}\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i-1}
and
\boldsymbol{s}_{i}=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}-\alpha_{i}\boldsymbol{u}_{i}
. Then
\alpha_{i}=\|\boldsymbol{N}\bar{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}\|_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}=(\bar{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{N}\bar{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i})^{1/2}
and
\beta_{i+1}=\|\boldsymbol{s}_{i}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}=(\boldsymbol{s}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{s}_{i})^{1/2}
. We remark that computing with
\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}
can not be avoided to get
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}
. For the most commonly encountered cases that
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
is a Gaussian white noise or all of its components are uncorrelated,
\boldsymbol{M}
is diagonal and
\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}
can be directly obtained.
The whole iterative process is summarized in Algorithm
3.1
.

(\mathbb{R}^{m},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}})

\mathrm{span}\{\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}

Proposition 1

The group of vectors
\{\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
is an
\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}
-orthonormal basis of the Krylov subspace

\mathcal{K}_{k}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1},b)=\mathrm{span}\{(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1})^{i}b\}_{i=0}^{k-1},

and
\{\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
is an
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}
-orthonormal basis of the Krylov subspace

\mathcal{K}_{k}(\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{b})=\mathrm{span}\{(\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A})^{i}\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}\}_{i=0}^{k-1}.

Proof

We exploit the property of GKB for the linear operator
\boldsymbol{A}
with starting vector

between the two Hilbert spaces
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}})
and
(\mathbb{R}^{m},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}})

\{\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
and
\{\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
are the
\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}
-orthonormal basis and
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}
-orthonormal basis of the Krylov subspaces
\mathcal{K}_{k}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{A}^{*},\boldsymbol{b})
and
\mathcal{K}_{k}(\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{b})
, respectively. Using Lemma

, we have
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{A}^{*}=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}
,
\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}
and
\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{b}=\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}
. The proof is completed. ∎

\displaystyle\beta_{1}\boldsymbol{U}_{k+1}e_{1}=\boldsymbol{b},

(3.7)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}=\boldsymbol{U}_{k+1}\boldsymbol{B}_{k},

(3.8)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{U}_{k+1}=\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\boldsymbol{B}_{k}^{\top}+\alpha_{k+1}\boldsymbol{v}_{k+1}e_{k+1}^{\top},

(3.9)

\boldsymbol{e}_{1}

k+1

\beta_{2}&\alpha_{2}&&\\

&&\ddots&\alpha_{k}\\

\mathrm{span}\{\boldsymbol{U}_{k+1}\}
and

\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}\neq 0

3.2
Iterative regularization by subspace projection

At the
k
-th iteration of gen-GKB, we seek solution to (
3.1
) by setting
\mathcal{S}_{k}=\mathrm{span}\{\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\}
. Denote any vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
as
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}
with
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. By (
3.8
), we have

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathcal{S}_{k}}\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}

\displaystyle=\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}y-\boldsymbol{U}_{k+1}\beta_{1}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}

\displaystyle=\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\|\boldsymbol{U}_{k+1}(\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{U}_{k+1}\beta_{1}e_{1})\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}

\displaystyle=\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\|\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}-\beta_{1}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\|_{2},

where we use the property that
\boldsymbol{U}_{k+1}
is
\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}
-orthonormal. Under the assumption that gen-GKB does not terminate,
the above
k
-dimensional linear least squares problem has a unique solution. Therefore, the solution to (
3.1
) is

\boldsymbol{x}_{k}=\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}_{k},\ \ \ \boldsymbol{y}_{k}=\mathop{\text{argmin}}_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\|\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}-\beta_{1}e_{1}\|_{2}

(3.11)

for
k=1,2\dots
. Furthermore, we do not need to explicitly compute
\boldsymbol{y}_{k}
by solving (
3.11
) for each
k
. Instead, there is an efficient procedure to update
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}
from
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
step by step. The procedure is similar to the LSQR algorithm for standard linear least squares problems
Paige1982
.

First, perform the following QR factorization:

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{k}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{B}_{k}&\beta_{1}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{R}_{k}&\boldsymbol{g}_{k}\\
&\bar{\phi}_{k+1}\end{pmatrix}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc:c}\rho_{1}&\theta_{2}&&&\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@add@cssclass&\phi_{1}\\
&\rho_{2}&\theta_{3}&&\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@add@cssclass&\phi_{2}\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@add@cssclass&\vdots\\
&&&\rho_{k-1}&\theta_{k}\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@add@cssclass&\phi_{k-1}\\
&&&&\rho_{k}\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@add@cssclass&\phi_{k}\\
\hline\cr&&&&\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@add@cssclass&\bar{\phi}_{k+1}\end{array}\right),

where the orthogonal matrix
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{k}
is the product of a series of Givens rotation matrices that is used to zero out
\beta_{i+1}
:

\begin{pmatrix}c_{i}&s_{i}\\
s_{i}&-c_{i}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\bar{\rho}_{i}&0&\bar{\phi}_{i}\\
\beta_{i+1}&\alpha_{i+1}&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\rho_{i}&\theta_{i+1}&\phi_{i}\\
0&\bar{\rho}_{i+1}&\bar{\phi}_{i+1}\end{pmatrix},

where
i=1,\dots,k
. There exist efficient recursive formulae to compute these matrix elements, and here we adopt these formulae proposed in
Paige1982
(line 3–6 in Algorithm
3.2
). Using the orthogonality of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{k}
leads to

\|\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}-\beta_{1}e_{1}\|_{2}^{2}=\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{k}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{B}_{k}&\beta_{1}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{y}\\
-1\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\|\boldsymbol{R}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{g}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}+|\bar{\phi}_{k+1}|^{2}.

(3.12)

Thereby the solution to
\mathop{\text{argmin}}_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\|\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}-\beta_{1}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\|_{2}
is
\boldsymbol{y}_{k}=\boldsymbol{R}_{k}^{-1}\boldsymbol{g}_{k}
. Factorizing
\boldsymbol{R}_{k}
as

\boldsymbol{R}_{k}=\boldsymbol{D}_{k}\bar{\boldsymbol{R}}_{k},\ \ \boldsymbol{D}_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}\rho_{1}&&&\\
&\rho_{2}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&\rho_{k}\end{pmatrix},\ \ \bar{\boldsymbol{R}}_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\theta_{2}/\rho_{1}&&\\
&1&\theta_{3}/\rho_{2}&\\
&&\ddots&\theta_{k}/\rho_{k-1}\\
&&&1\end{pmatrix},

we obtain

\boldsymbol{x}_{k}=\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}_{k}=\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\boldsymbol{R}_{k}^{-1}\boldsymbol{g}_{k}=(\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\bar{\boldsymbol{R}}_{k}^{-1})(\boldsymbol{D}_{k}^{-1}\boldsymbol{g}_{k}).

(3.13)

Let
\boldsymbol{W}_{k}=\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\bar{\boldsymbol{R}}_{k}^{-1}=(\boldsymbol{w}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{w}_{k})
. Note that
\boldsymbol{D}_{k}^{-1}\boldsymbol{g}_{k}=(\phi_{1}/\rho_{1},\dots,\phi_{k}/\rho_{k})^{\top}
. Solving
\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\bar{\boldsymbol{R}}_{k}=\boldsymbol{V}_{k}
by back substitution and combining (
3.13
) leads to the recursive formula for
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{w}_{i}
:

\boldsymbol{x}_{i}=\boldsymbol{x}_{i-1}+(\phi_{i}/\rho_{i})\boldsymbol{w}_{i},\ \ \ \boldsymbol{w}_{i+1}=\boldsymbol{v}_{i+1}-(\theta_{i+1}/\rho_{i})\boldsymbol{w}_{i}.

(3.14)

From the above derivation, we have

\bar{\phi}_{k+1}=\|\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}_{k}-\beta_{1}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\|_{2}=\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}.

(3.15)

Thereby the residual norm can be updated very quickly without explicitly computing
\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}
. In order to compute
\|\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\|_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}
without explicitly using
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}
, let
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}=\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}_{k}=\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}
. By (
3.14

\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}=\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i-1}+(\phi_{i}/\rho_{i})\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}_{i},\ \ \ \bar{\boldsymbol{w}}_{i+1}=\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i+1}-(\theta_{i+1}/\rho_{i})\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}_{i},

(3.16)

and
\|\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\|_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}=(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})^{1/2}
. The above updating procedure is summarized in Algorithm

3.3
Early stopping rules

The SPR method usually exhibits semi-convergence behavior: as the iteration proceeds, the iterative solution first gradually approximate to
\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{true}}
, then the solution will deviate far from
\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{true}}
and eventually converges to
\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{naive}}=\boldsymbol{A}^{{\dagger}}\boldsymbol{b}

(
Engl2000
, §3.3)
. This is because the solution subspace will contain more and more noisy components as it gradually expands. The iteration at which the corresponding solution has the smallest error is called the semi-convergence point. In order to obtain a good regularized solution, we should stop the iteration early. Note that the iteration number
k
in SPR plays a similar role as the regularization parameter in Tikhonov regularization. Here we adapt several criteria for choosing regularization parameters to estimate the semi-convergence point.

Discrepancy principle.

For the case that
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
is a Gaussian white noise, if
\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\|_{2}
is known, one criterion for determining the early stopping iteration is the discrepancy principle (DP), which states that the discrepancy between the data and predicted output
\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{2}
, should be of the order of
\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\|_{2}

(
Hansen1998
, §7.2)
. If
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
is a non-white Gaussian noise, noticing that (
1.1
) leads to

\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{b}=\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{\epsilon},

(3.17)

and
\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{I})
, thereby this transformation whitens the noise, and the discrepancy is
\|\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{b}\|_{2}=\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}
. Since
\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}=\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
is a Gaussian white noise with zero mean, it follows that

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\|_{2}^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{trace}\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{trace}\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{\top}\right)\right]=\mathrm{trace}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{\top}\right]\right)=\mathrm{trace}\left(\boldsymbol{I}\right)=m.

Note from (
3.15
) that
\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}=\bar{\phi}_{k+1}
decreases monotonically since
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}
minimizes
\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}
in the gradually expanding subspace
\mathrm{span}\{\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\}
. Following DP, we should stop iteration at the first
k
satisfying

\bar{\phi}_{k+1}\leq\tau\sqrt{m}

(3.18)

with
\tau>1
slightly, such as
\tau=1.01
. Typically, the early stopping iteration determined by DP is slightly smaller than the semi-convergence point, thereby the corresponding solution is over-regularized.

L-curve.

Another early stopping rule is the L-curve criterion, which is a heuristic approach that does not need the noise norm
Hansen1992
. The idea is to plot in log-log scale the following curve

\left(\log\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}},\log\|\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\|_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}\right)=\left(\log\bar{\phi}_{k+1},\log(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})^{1/2}\right),

(3.19)

which frequently has a characteristic ‘L’ shape, and the corner corresponds to the point where further reduction in the residual comes only at the expense of a drastic increase in the regularization term. Thus, we should choose the early stopping iteration that corresponds to this corner, which is usually defined as the point of maximum curvature of the L-curve in a log-log plot.

Generalized cross-validation.

The generalized cross-validation (GCV) is a statistical approach for estimating the optimal regularization parameter, especially for the Gaussian white noise case
Golub1979
. For the SPR method where the iteration number
k
plays the role of the regularization parameter, this approach can be adapted to estimate the optimal iteration number
k_{0}
. Since the transformed system (
3.17
) equivalent to (
1.1
) has a white Gaussian noise
\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
, the GCV function with respect to
k
should be

\left((\theta_{i}^{(k)})^{2},\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\boldsymbol{p}_{i}^{(k)}\right)
converges to one of the generalized eigenvalue-vector pairs of
\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{z}=\xi\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}\boldsymbol{z}
as
k
increases.

Proof

By (
3.8
) and (
3.9
), we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}=(\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{U}_{k+1})\boldsymbol{B}_{k}=\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\boldsymbol{B}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}_{k}+\alpha_{k+1}\beta_{k+1}\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}\boldsymbol{v}_{k+1}\boldsymbol{e}_{k}^{\top},

where
\boldsymbol{e}_{k}
is the
k
-th column of the identity matrix of order
k
. By (
3.3
) and (
3.7
), we have

\alpha_{1}\beta_{1}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}=\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\beta_{1}\boldsymbol{u}_{1}=\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}.

Notice that
\boldsymbol{B}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}_{k}
is a symmetric tridiagonal matrix with diagonals
\alpha_{i}^{2}+\beta_{i+1}^{2}
and subdiagonals
\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,k
. The above two relations indicate that
\boldsymbol{T}_{k}
is the Ritz-Galerkin projection of
\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}
onto subspace
\mathrm{span}\{\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\}
generated by the Lanczos tridiagonalization of
\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}
with starting vector
\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}
under
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}
-inner product
(
Bai2000
, §5.5)
. Since the eigenvalue decomposition of
\boldsymbol{B}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}_{k}
is
\boldsymbol{B}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}_{k}=\boldsymbol{P}_{k}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{k}^{2}\boldsymbol{P}_{k}^{\top}
, by the convergence theory of Lanczos tridiagonalization for generalized eigenvalue problems, we obtain the desired result. ∎

By (
2.3
), the generalized eigenvalue-vector pairs for
\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{z}=\xi\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}\boldsymbol{z}
are
\{(\gamma_{i}^{2},\boldsymbol{z}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
. The convergence behavior of
\left((\theta_{i}^{(k)})^{2},\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\boldsymbol{p}_{i}^{(k)}\right)
as
k
increases is governed by the Kaniel-Paige-Saad theory that is similar to the eigenvalue problem of a single matrix
(
Golub2013
, §10.1.5)
, which states that it has a faster convergence to those generalized eigenvalues (and corresponding eigenvectors) lying in the two ends of the spectrum and well separated. Since
\gamma_{i}^{2}
decreases and clusters at zero, we can hope for a faster convergence of
\left((\theta_{i}^{(k)})^{2},\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\boldsymbol{p}_{i}^{(k)}\right)
to some largest generalized eigenvalue and corresponding vector. Therefore, the information in
\mathcal{S}_{k}
can be extracted to approximate dominant generalized eigenvectors
\boldsymbol{z}_{i}
. These vectors constitute basic features about
\boldsymbol{x}
encoded by the Gaussian prior. In fact, the regularized solution computed by genGKB_SPR contains those dominant
\boldsymbol{z}_{i}
while filtering out others. To reveal it, we need the following lemma, which generalizes
(
Van1986rate
, Property 2.8)
for the conjugate gradient (CG) method to a rank-deficient matrix.

Lemma 4.3

For a symmetric positive semi-definite linear system
\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{f}=\boldsymbol{d}
with
\boldsymbol{d}\in\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{C})
, where
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)
is the range space of a matrix, the
k
-th CG solution of it with
\boldsymbol{d}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
can be written as
\boldsymbol{f}_{k}=\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\mathcal{R}_{k}(\boldsymbol{C})\right)\boldsymbol{C}^{{\dagger}}\boldsymbol{d}
, where
\mathcal{R}_{k}
is the normalized characteristic polynomial of
\boldsymbol{T}_{k}
as (
4.11
) such that
\mathcal{R}_{k}(0)=1
.

Proof

Suppose the
k
-step standard Lanczos tridiagonalization of
\boldsymbol{C}
with starting vector
\boldsymbol{d}
is written as the following matrix-form:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{Q}_{k}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{Q}_{k}\boldsymbol{T}_{k}+\delta_{k+1}\boldsymbol{q}_{k+1}\boldsymbol{e}_{k}^{\top},

(4.9)

\displaystyle\delta_{1}\boldsymbol{q}_{1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{d},

(4.10)

where
\{\boldsymbol{q}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k+1}
are orthonormal and

\boldsymbol{T}_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}\eta_{1}&\delta_{2}&&\\
\delta_{2}&\eta_{2}&\ddots&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\delta_{k}\\
&&\delta_{k}&\eta_{k}\end{pmatrix}.

(4.11)

We note that this process must terminate, i.e.
\delta_{k+1}=0
for at most
k=l
steps, where
l=\mathrm{rank}(\boldsymbol{C})
, and the corresponding solution is just
\boldsymbol{C}^{{\dagger}}\boldsymbol{d}
; if the process does not terminate at the
k
-th step, then
\boldsymbol{T}_{k}
is invertible
(
bjorck2015numerical
, §4.2.3–§4.2.5)
. To prove this lemma, we use the fact that the
k
-th CG solution of
\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{f}=\boldsymbol{d}
with
\boldsymbol{d}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
is
\boldsymbol{f}_{k}=\boldsymbol{Q}_{k}\boldsymbol{T}_{k}^{-1}\delta_{1}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}
.

Let the compact SVD of
\boldsymbol{C}
be
\boldsymbol{C}=\boldsymbol{V}_{C}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{C}\boldsymbol{V}_{C}^{\top}
, where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{l\times l}
has positive diagonals and
\boldsymbol{V}_{C}
has
l
orthonormal columns. Since
\boldsymbol{d}\in\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{C})=\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{V}_{C})
, it follows that
\boldsymbol{q}_{i}\in\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{V}_{C})
for
i=1,\dots,k+1
. Let
\boldsymbol{q}_{i}=\boldsymbol{V}_{C}\bar{\boldsymbol{q}}_{i}
with
\bar{\boldsymbol{q}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{l}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{k}=(\bar{\boldsymbol{q}}_{1},\dots,\bar{\boldsymbol{q}}_{k})
. It follows that
\{\bar{\boldsymbol{q}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k+1}
are orthonormal. From (
4.9
) and (
4.10
) we get

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{C}\bar{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\bar{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{k}\boldsymbol{T}_{k}+\delta_{k+1}\bar{\boldsymbol{q}}_{k+1}\boldsymbol{e}_{k}^{\top},

\displaystyle\delta_{1}\bar{\boldsymbol{q}}_{1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{V}_{C}^{\top}\boldsymbol{d},

which is just the matrix-form of the Lanczos tridiagonalization of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{C}
with starting vector
\boldsymbol{V}_{C}^{\top}\boldsymbol{d}
. Let
\bar{\boldsymbol{f}}_{k}=\bar{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{k}\boldsymbol{T}_{k}^{-1}\delta_{1}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}
. Since
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{C}
is positive definite, by
(
Van1986rate
, Property 2.8)
we have
\bar{\boldsymbol{f}}_{k}=\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\mathcal{R}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{C})\right)\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{C}^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}_{C}^{\top}\boldsymbol{d}
. Using
\boldsymbol{Q}_{k}=\boldsymbol{V}_{C}\bar{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{k}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}_{k}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{V}_{C}\bar{\boldsymbol{f}}_{k}=\boldsymbol{V}_{C}\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\mathcal{R}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{C})\right)\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{C}^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}_{C}^{\top}\boldsymbol{d}

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\mathcal{R}_{k}(\boldsymbol{V}_{C}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{C}\boldsymbol{V}_{C}^{\top})\right)\boldsymbol{V}_{C}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{C}^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}_{C}^{\top}\boldsymbol{d}

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\mathcal{R}_{k}(\boldsymbol{C})\right)\boldsymbol{C}^{{\dagger}}\boldsymbol{d}.

The proof is completed. ∎

Theorem 4.3

The
k
-th regularized solution computed by genGKB_SPR has the expression

\boldsymbol{x}_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}f_{i}^{(k)}\frac{\boldsymbol{u}_{A,i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{b}}{\sigma_{i}}\boldsymbol{z}_{i}

(4.12)

with filter factors

f_{i}^{(k)}=1-\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{(\theta_{j}^{(k)})^{2}-\gamma_{i}^{2}}{(\theta_{j}^{(k)})^{2}},\ \ i=1,\dots,r.

(4.13)

Proof

Following the proof of Theorem
4.2
, we have

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-\top}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-1})\boldsymbol{L}_{N}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{L}_{N}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}(\boldsymbol{B}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}_{k})+\alpha_{k}\beta_{k}\boldsymbol{L}_{N}\boldsymbol{v}_{k+1}\boldsymbol{e}_{k},

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}\beta_{1}\boldsymbol{L}_{N}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-\top}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{b},

and
\{\boldsymbol{L}_{N}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k+1}
are 2-orthonormal. This implies that
\boldsymbol{L}_{N}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}
are the Lanczos vectors generated by the standard Lanczos tridiagonalization of
\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-\top}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-1}
with starting vector
\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-\top}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{b}
. Let
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}=\boldsymbol{L}_{N}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}
. Then by (
3.11
) we have
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}=\boldsymbol{L}_{N}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}(\boldsymbol{B}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}_{k})^{-1}\boldsymbol{B}_{k}^{\top}\beta_{1}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}=(\boldsymbol{L}_{N}\boldsymbol{V}_{k})\boldsymbol{T}_{k}^{-1}\alpha_{1}\beta_{1}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}
. Therefore,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}
is the
k
-th CG solution of the symmetric positive semi-definite linear system

\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-\top}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-\top}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{b}.

(4.14)

Note that

\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-\top}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{b}=(\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-1})^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}_{M}b,\ \ \ \boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-\top}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-1}=(\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-1})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-1}).

It follows that
\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-\top}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-\top}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-1})
and

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ (\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-\top}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-1})^{{\dagger}}\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-\top}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{b}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-1})^{{\dagger}}\boldsymbol{L}_{M}b=\left(\boldsymbol{U}_{A}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{A}(\boldsymbol{L}_{N}\boldsymbol{Z})^{-1}\right)^{{\dagger}}\boldsymbol{L}_{M}b

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{L}_{N}\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{A}^{{\dagger}}\boldsymbol{U}_{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}_{M}b,

where we use (
2.1
) and the property that
\boldsymbol{L}_{N}\boldsymbol{Z}
has orthonormal columns. Therefore, by Lemma
4.3
we have

\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}=\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\mathcal{R}_{k}(\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-\top}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-1})\right)\boldsymbol{L}_{N}\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{A}^{{\dagger}}\boldsymbol{U}_{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}_{M}b,

(4.15)

where
\mathcal{R}_{k}
is the normalized characteristic polynomial of
\boldsymbol{B}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}_{k}
such that
\mathcal{R}_{k}(0)=1
.

By (
2.3
), we have
\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-\top}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-1}=\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-\top}\boldsymbol{Z}^{-\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{A}\boldsymbol{Z}^{-1}\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-1}
. Since
\boldsymbol{Z}^{-1}\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-1}\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-\top}\boldsymbol{Z}^{-\top}=\left(\boldsymbol{Z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}\boldsymbol{Z}\right)^{-1}=\boldsymbol{I}
, we get
\boldsymbol{I}-\mathcal{R}_{k}(\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-\top}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-1})=\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-\top}\boldsymbol{Z}^{-\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{Z}^{-1}\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-1}
, where
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\mathrm{diag}\left(\{1-\mathcal{R}_{k}(\sigma_{i}^{2})\}_{i=1}^{n}\right)
, where
\sigma_{i}:=0
for
r+1\leq i\leq n
.
Therefore, (
4.15
) leads to
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}=\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-\top}\boldsymbol{Z}^{-\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{A}^{{\dagger}}\boldsymbol{U}_{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{b}
, and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{k}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-1}\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{-\top}\boldsymbol{Z}^{-\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{A}^{{\dagger}}\boldsymbol{U}_{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{b}=\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{A}^{{\dagger}}\boldsymbol{U}_{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{b}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{Z}_{r}&\boldsymbol{Z}_{r1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{r}&\\
&\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{r1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{D}_{A}^{-1}&\\
&\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{U}_{A,r}^{\top}\\
\boldsymbol{U}_{A,r1}^{\top}\end{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{b}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{Z}_{r}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{r}\boldsymbol{D}_{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{U}_{A,r}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{b},

where
\boldsymbol{Z}
,
\Lambda
and
\boldsymbol{U}_{A}
are partitioned such that the above matrix multiplication is reasonable. By Theorem
4.2
, the eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{B}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}_{k}
are
\{(\theta_{j}^{(k)})^{2}\}_{i=1}^{k}
. If follows that
\mathcal{R}_{k}((\theta_{j}^{(k)})^{2})=0
for
1\leq i\leq k
. Since
\mathcal{R}_{k}(0)=1
and the degree of
\mathcal{R}_{k}
is
k
, we have

\mathcal{R}_{k}(\theta)=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{(\theta_{j}^{(k)})^{2}-\theta}{(\theta_{j}^{(k)})^{2}}.

Therefore, we finally obtain
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}f_{i}^{(k)}\frac{\boldsymbol{u}_{A,i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{b}}{\sigma_{i}}\boldsymbol{z}_{i}
with
f_{i}^{(k)}
defined as (
4.13
). ∎

Theorem
4.3
shows that
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}
has a filtered GSVD expansion form. If the
k
Ritz values
\{(\theta_{j}^{(k)})^{2}\}_{i=1}^{k}
approximate the first
k
generalized singular values
\{\gamma_{i}^{2}\}_{i=1}^{k}
in natural order, i.e.
(\theta_{j}^{(k)})^{2}\approx\gamma_{i}^{2}
for
i=1,\dots,k
, from (
4.13
) we can justify that
f_{i}^{(k)}\approx 1
for
i=1,\dots,k
and
f_{i}^{(k)}
decreases monotonically to zero for
i=k+1,\dots,r
. Comparing (
4.12
) with (
2
) and (
2.5
), we find that
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}
contains the first
k
dominant GSVD components and filters the others. Therefore, as
k
increases, the iterative solution first approximates
\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{true}}
, but as
k
becomes too big,
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}
will diverge from
\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{true}}
since too many noisy components are contained. The transition point is the semi-convergence point, precisely what the early stopping rules aim to estimate.

5
Experimental Results

We conduct several numerical experiments to demonstrate the performance of our proposed algorithm. The generalized hybrid iterative method proposed in
Chung2017
is used as a comparison, which is also based on gen-GKB and equivalent to solving (
1.2
) by projecting it onto the solution space
\mathrm{span}\{\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\}
to get

\min_{\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}}\{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}^{2}+\lambda\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}^{2}\}=\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\|\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}-\beta_{1}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}^{2}.

(5.1)

In order to solve the above
k
dimensional projected problem, at each iteration, the weighted GCV (WGCV) method
Chungnagy2008
is adopted to determine a regularization parameter
\lambda_{k}
to form
\lambda_{k}\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}^{2}
. In the experiments, we name the generalized hybrid iterative method with WGCV as genHyb_WGCV. The convergence behaviors of the two methods are shown by plotting the variation of relative reconstruction error
\|\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{true}}\|_{2}/\|\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{true}}\|_{2}
with respect to
k
. The reconstructed solutions are also drawn to further compare the two methods. All the experiments are performed in MATLAB R2019b, where some codes in
Hansen2007
;
Gazzola2019
are exploited.

5.2
Two-dimensional inverse diffusion

In this experiment, we consider the two-dimensional (2D) inverse diffusion problem
PRdiffusion

Gazzola2019
. The forward problem is a 2D
diffusion equation in the spatial domain
\Omega=[0,1]^{2}
with homogenous Neumann boundary condition and an initial function
u_{0}
:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=\Delta u,\ \ (x,y)\in\Omega,\ t>0,\\
&\frac{\partial u}{\partial\mathbf{n}}=0,\ \ (x,y)\in\partial\Omega,\ t>0,\\
&u(0,x,y)=u_{0}(x,y),\ \ (x,y)\in\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.\qquad

where
\mathbf{n}
denotes the exterior normal to the boundary
\partial\Omega
. The inverse problem is to reconstruct
u_{0}
from the diffusion solution
u_{T}=u(T,x,y)
at time
t=T
. In the experiment, we set
T=0.005
and
u_{0}
be the following smooth function:

u_{0}(x,y)=0.7\exp\left(-\left(\frac{x-0.4}{0.12}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{y-0.5}{0.15}\right)^{2}\right)+\exp\left(-\left(\frac{x-0.7}{0.1}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{y-0.8}{0.08}\right)^{2}\right).

The above forward PDE is discretized on a uniform finite element mesh with
2(n_{1}-1)^{2}
triangular elements, thereby
u
is represented by the
n=n_{1}^{2}
values at the corners of the elements, and

is divided into 100 uniform time steps. The forward computation is the numerical solution of the above PDE implemented by the Crank-Nicolson-Galerkin finite element method, thereby
\boldsymbol{A}
is a functional handle. By choosing
n_{1}=128
, we have
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{128^{2}\times 128^{2}}
, which maps
\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{true}}
(the discretized

(the discretized
u_{T}
), and we add a Gaussian white noise
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we have proposed a new iterative regularization algorithm for solving Bayesian linear inverse problems. Within the subspace projection regularization framework, we first introduced an iterative process that can generate a series of valid solution subspaces by treating the forward model matrix as a linear operator between the two Hilbert spaces
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}})
and
(\mathbb{R}^{m},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}})
. The original problem is projected onto these subspaces becoming a series of low dimensional linear least squares problems. We have developed an efficient procedure to update the solutions of these projected problems as approximate iterative regularization solutions, as well as update the residual norm and solution norm of them. Early stopping rules based on DP, LC and GCV have been designed, with which the iterative algorithm can get a regularized solution to the original problem with a satisfied accuracy. Several theoretical results have been established, revealing the good regularization properties of the algorithm. Numerical experiments using both small-scale and large-scale inverse problems have been conducted to demonstrate the robustness and efficiency of the algorithm. Since the most computationally intensive operations in the proposed algorithm only involve matrix-vector products, it is highly efficient for large-scale Bayesian inverse problems.
