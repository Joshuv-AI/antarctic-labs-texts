On the Hartree-Fock Ground State Manifold in Magic Angle Twisted Graphene Systems
Kevin D. Stubbs, Simon Becker, Lin Lin

Abstract
. Recent experiments have shown that magic angle twisted bilayer graphene (MATBG) can exhibit correlated insulator behavior at half-filling. Seminal theoretical results towards understanding this phase in MATBG has shown that Hartree-Fock ground states (with a positive charge gap) can be exact many-body ground states of an idealized flat band interacting (FBI) Hamiltonian. We prove that in the absence of spin and valley degrees of freedom, the only Hartree-Fock ground states of the FBI Hamiltonian for MATBG are two ferromagnetic Slater determinants. Incorporating spin and valley degrees of freedom, we provide a complete characterization of the Hartree-Fock ground state manifold, which is generated by a U ⁡ ( 4 ) × U ⁡ ( 4 ) {\rm U}(4)\times{\rm U}(4) hidden symmetry group acting on five elements. We also introduce new tools for ruling out translation symmetry breaking in the Hartree-Fock ground state manifold, which may be of independent interest.

A key property of the FBI Hamiltonian for magic angle twisted graphene systems is that they can be

In fact, the Hamiltonians for these systems can be expressed as a sum of positive semidefinite terms so that the ground state energy is bounded below by zero.
Moreover, there exist ground states which minimize the energy of each individual term, also resulting in a value of zero.
As a consequence of the form of the FBI Hamiltonian, any many-body ground state of a frustration-free FBI Hamiltonian is necessarily half-filled (

1.2.
Discussion and open question

In this paper, we find conditions which allow us to characterize all Hartree-Fock ground states of frustration-free FBI Hamiltonians.
We then apply these conditions to the special case of magic angle twisted bilayer graphene with two flat bands and extend this to include valley and spin degrees of freedom.
It would be interesting to apply the methodology here to other FBI Hamiltonians corresponding to twisted multilayer graphene systems.

Considering the simplicity of the FBI model and the low amount of electron correlation of the many-body ground state observed in numerical studies at half-filling
[
22
,
13
]
, we propose the following conjecture:

Conjecture

(Many-Body Ground States of Frustration-Free FBI Hamiltonians)
.

Any many-body ground states of a frustration-free FBI Hamiltonian can be written as a linear combination of its Hartree-Fock ground states. In particular, for TBG-2 (with spin and valley), any many-body ground state can be written as

(1)

\ket{\Psi}=\int_{{\rm U}(4)\times{\rm U}(4)}\sum_{i=1}^{5}\alpha_{\mathfrak{g},i}\mathfrak{g}\ket{\Psi_{i}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{g},\quad\alpha_{\mathfrak{g},i}\in\mathbb{C}.

Here
\{\ket{\Psi_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{5}
are the ferromagnetic Slater determinant states which generate the Hartree-Fock ground state manifold.

2.
The Flat-Band Interacting Hamiltonian for Twisted Graphene

The Flat-Band Interacting (FBI) Hamiltonian for twisted bilayer graphene
is based on a periodic single particle Hamiltonian which has exactly flat bands.
We highlight the main features of this model and refer the reader to
[
4
, Section 2]
for more details.
Following the notation in
[
4
]
, we let
\Gamma
and
\Omega
denote the moiré lattice and real space unit cell in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
respectively. Similar, let
\Gamma^{*}
and
\Omega^{*}
denote the moiré reciprocal lattice and Brillouin zone in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
respectively.

Suppose that we are given a single particle Hamiltonian
H
with flat-bands which is periodic with respect to
\Gamma
.
Given such a Hamiltonian, let
\{\psi_{n\mathbf{k}}:\mathbf{k}\in\Omega^{*},n\in\mathcal{N}\}
be the Bloch eigenfunctions where
\mathcal{N}
is the set indexing the flat bands.
For a system with
N
-layers, each
\psi_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r}):=[\psi_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r};\sigma,j)]\in\mathbb{C}^{2N},
where
\sigma\in\{A,B\}
denotes the sublattice degree of freedom and
j\in\{1,\cdots,N\}
denotes the layer degree of freedom.
Furthermore, let
u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})=e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\psi_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})
denote the
\Gamma
-periodic Bloch functions normalized so that
\int_{\Omega}\|u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}=1
, where
\|u\|:=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}|u_{i}|^{2}}
is the
2
-norm of a vector
u\in\mathbb{C}^{n}.
Similarly, we denote by
\langle v,u\rangle:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\overline{v_{i}}u_{i}
the canonical inner-product.

The moiré Brillouin zone
\Omega
is discretized using a finite grid denoted by
\mathcal{K}

(2)

\mathcal{K}:=\left\{\frac{i}{n_{k_{x}}}\mathbf{g}_{1}+\frac{j}{n_{k_{y}}}\mathbf{g}_{2}:i\in\{0,1,\cdots,n_{k_{x}}-1\},j\in\{0,1,\cdots,n_{k_{y}}-1\}\right\}\subseteq\Omega^{*}.

Here
\mathbf{g}_{1}
,
\mathbf{g}_{2}
are a pair of generating vectors for the moiré reciprocal lattice
\Gamma^{*}
and
(n_{k_{x}},n_{k_{y}})
are the number of points in each of the two lattice directions.
We also define
N_{\mathbf{k}}:=\#|\mathcal{K}|=n_{k_{x}}n_{k_{y}}
.

While in this work
n_{k_{x}},n_{k_{y}}
are taken to be finite, we will be interested in the limit where both
n_{k_{x}},n_{k_{y}}
can become arbitrarily large towards the thermodynamic limit.
We make the following technical assumption on the grid
\mathcal{K}
in relation to the single particle Hamiltonian:

Assumption 1
.

For each
\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}
, let
\Pi(\mathbf{k})
be the orthogonal projector onto the flat-band eigenfunctions at
\mathbf{k}
.
We assume that the grid
\mathcal{K}
has been chosen so that for all pairs of momenta
\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}
there exists a sequence of momenta
\{\mathbf{k}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{L}\subseteq\mathcal{K}
so that
\mathbf{k}_{1}=\mathbf{k}
,
\mathbf{k}_{L}=\mathbf{k}^{\prime}
and
\|\Pi(\mathbf{k}_{i+1})-\Pi(\mathbf{k}_{i})\|<1
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,L\}
.

This assumption is satisfied for the chiral Bistritzer-MacDonald model
[
23
,
2
]
so long as
n_{k_{x}}
and
n_{k_{y}}
are both chosen sufficiently large. This is because the flat bands are gapped from the remaining bands and the Hamiltonian depends real-analytically on
\mathbf{k}.

Having fixed the flat-band eigenfunctions and the grid
\mathcal{K}
, we now define the FBI Hamiltonian.
At each
\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}
, we first define the band creation and annihilation operators,
\hat{f}_{n\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}
and
\hat{f}_{n\mathbf{k}}
, which create or annihilate a particle in state
\psi_{n\mathbf{k}}
respectively.
These many-body operators satisfy the canonical anti-commutation relation (CAR) as well as a periodicity condition:

(3)

\begin{split}\{\hat{f}^{{\dagger}}_{n\mathbf{k}},\hat{f}_{n^{\prime}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\}=\delta_{nn^{\prime}}\delta_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}},&\quad\{\hat{f}^{{\dagger}}_{n\mathbf{k}},\hat{f}^{{\dagger}}_{n^{\prime}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\}=\{\hat{f}_{n\mathbf{k}},\hat{f}_{n^{\prime}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\}=0\\
\hat{f}^{{\dagger}}_{n(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G})}=\hat{f}^{{\dagger}}_{n\mathbf{k}},&\quad\hat{f}_{n(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G})}=\hat{f}_{n\mathbf{k}},\quad\forall\mathbf{G}\in\Gamma^{*}.\end{split}

We also recall the definition of the number operator
\hat{N}:=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\sum_{m\in\mathcal{N}}\hat{f}_{m\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{f}_{m\mathbf{k}}
.

Next, we define the Fourier coefficients of the periodic Bloch functions
u_{n\mathbf{k}}
introduced at the beginning of this section

(4)

\hat{u}_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G};\sigma,j):=\int_{\Omega}e^{-i\mathbf{G}\cdot\mathbf{r}}u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r};\sigma,j)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}=\int_{\Omega}e^{-i(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G})\cdot\mathbf{r}}\psi_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r};\sigma,j)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}

and define the
form factor

(5)

\begin{split}[\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})]_{mn}&:=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{\mathbf{G}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}}\braket{\hat{u}_{m\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G}^{\prime}),\hat{u}_{n(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}(\mathbf{G}^{\prime})}\\
&=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{\mathbf{G}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}}\sum_{\sigma,j}\overline{\hat{u}_{m\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G}^{\prime};\sigma,j)}\hat{u}_{n(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}(\mathbf{G}^{\prime};\sigma,j)\end{split}

where
\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}
, and
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}+\Gamma^{*}
.

Finally, let
V
be radially symmetric function with Fourier transform

\hat{V}(\mathbf{q})>0
for all
\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
The FBI Hamiltonian for
H
with Coulomb potential
V
takes the form

(6)

\begin{split}\hat{H}_{\rm FBI}&=\frac{1}{N_{\mathbf{k}}|\Omega|}\sum_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}+\Gamma^{*}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\widehat{\rho}(-\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\\
\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})&=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\sum_{m,n\in\mathcal{N}}[\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})]_{mn}\left(\hat{f}_{m\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{f}_{n(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}-\frac{1}{2}\delta_{mn}\delta_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}}\right).\end{split}

The operator
\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})
satisfies
\widehat{\rho}(-\mathbf{q}^{\prime})=\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})^{\dagger}

[
4
, Lemma 5.1]
, and so
\hat{H}_{\rm FBI}
is positive semidefinite. Hence any state with zero energy must be a ground state.

We note that from the form of the FBI Hamiltonian, we can immediately conclude that any zero energy ground state must be half-filled.

Lemma 2.1
.

Any many-body ground state
\ket{\Phi}
of a frustration-free FBI Hamiltonian is half-filled. That is, it satisfies

(7)

\hat{N}\ket{\Phi}=\frac{\#|\mathcal{N}|N_{\mathbf{k}}}{2}\ket{\Phi}

where
\hat{N}
is the number operator
\hat{N}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\sum_{m\in\mathcal{N}}\hat{f}_{m\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{f}_{m\mathbf{k}}
.

Proof.

Let
\hat{H}_{\rm FBI}
be a frustration-free Hamiltonian, any many-body ground state
\ket{\Phi}
must satisfy
\braket{\Phi|\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\widehat{\rho}(-\mathbf{q}^{\prime})|\Phi}=0
.
From the definition of
\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})
, it can be verified that for all
\mathbf{k}\in\Omega^{*}
,
\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\bm{0})=I
.
In particular,

(8)

\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime}=\bm{0})=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\sum_{m\in\mathcal{N}}\left(\hat{f}_{m\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{f}_{m\mathbf{k}}-\frac{1}{2}\right)=\hat{N}-\frac{\#|\mathcal{N}|N_{\mathbf{k}}}{2}.

To be a ground state,
\ket{\Phi}
must satisfy

(9)

\braket{\Phi|\widehat{\rho}(\bm{0})\widehat{\rho}(\bm{0})|\Phi}=\left\|\left(\hat{N}-\frac{\#|\mathcal{N}|N_{\mathbf{k}}}{2}\right)\ket{\Phi}\right\|^{2}=0.

Hence,
\hat{N}\ket{\Phi}=\frac{\#|\mathcal{N}|N_{\mathbf{k}}}{2}\ket{\Phi}
as was claimed.
∎

3.
Hartree-Fock Theory for Flat-Band Interacting Hamiltonians

We begin this section by first reviewing the Hartree-Fock theory at half-filling without translation symmetry in
Section

3.1
.
We then derive a simple formula for the Hartree-Fock energy of the FBI Hamiltonian in
Section

3.2
.
As a consequence of this formula, we will obtain simple necessary and sufficient conditions for a Hartree-Fock state to be a ground state of a frustration-free FBI Hamiltonian.

3.1.
A Review of Hartree-Fock Theory without Translation Symmetry

For a system with an even number of bands, recall that a general Slater determinant at half-filling takes the form

(10)

\ket{\Psi_{S}}=\prod_{i=1}^{MN_{\mathbf{k}}}\hat{b}_{i}^{{\dagger}}\ket{\mathrm{vac}},

where
\ket{\mathrm{vac}}
is the vacuum state,
M
is half the number of available bands, and

(11)

\hat{b}_{i}^{{\dagger}}=\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\hat{f}_{n\mathbf{k}}^{{\dagger}}\Xi_{ni}(\mathbf{k}),\qquad\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\overline{\Xi_{ni}(\mathbf{k})}\Xi_{nj}(\mathbf{k})=\delta_{ij}

defines the creation operator for the Hartree-Fock orbitals.

The Hartree-Fock equations can be expressed in terms of the one-body reduced density matrix (1-RDM). The 1-RDM associated with a given Slater determinant
\ket{\Psi_{S}}
can be written as

(12)

[P(\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{k})]_{nm}=\braket{\Psi|\hat{f}_{m\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{f}_{n\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}|\Psi}=\sum_{i=1}^{MN_{\mathbf{k}}}\Xi_{ni}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\overline{\Xi_{mi}(\mathbf{k})}.

Note that due to the periodicity of the creation and annihilation operators in
Eq.

3
, the 1-RDM satisfies the property
P(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}+\mathbf{G}^{\prime})=P(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})
for all
\mathbf{G},\mathbf{G}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}
.

The 1-RDM
[P(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})]_{mn}
may be represented as a
(2MN_{\mathbf{k}})\times(2MN_{\mathbf{k}})
matrix as follows

(13)

P=\begin{bmatrix}P(\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{1})&P(\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2})&\cdots&P(\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{N_{\mathbf{k}}})\\
P(\mathbf{k}_{2},\mathbf{k}_{1})&P(\mathbf{k}_{2},\mathbf{k}_{2})&\cdots&P(\mathbf{k}_{2},\mathbf{k}_{N_{\mathbf{k}}})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
P(\mathbf{k}_{N_{\mathbf{k}}},\mathbf{k}_{1})&\cdots&\cdots&P(\mathbf{k}_{N_{\mathbf{k}}},\mathbf{k}_{N_{\mathbf{k}}})\\
\end{bmatrix}.

Due to the orthogonality relation on
\Xi(\mathbf{k})
given in
Eq.

11
, one may verify that
P
satisfies
P^{2}=P
,
P^{\dagger}=P
, and
\mathrm{Tr}{(P)}=MN_{\mathbf{k}}
(i.e.
P
is an orthogonal projection onto a
(MN_{\mathbf{k}})
-dimensional vector space).
The 1-RDM is
translation-invariant
if it takes the form

(14)

\begin{bmatrix}P(\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{1})&\bm{0}&\cdots&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&P(\mathbf{k}_{2},\mathbf{k}_{2})&\cdots&\bm{0}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\bm{0}&\cdots&\cdots&P(\mathbf{k}_{N_{\mathbf{k}}},\mathbf{k}_{N_{\mathbf{k}}})\end{bmatrix}.

3.2.
Hartree-Fock Energy of Flat-Band Interacting Hamiltonians

The Hartree-Fock energy for FBI Hamiltonians can also be written in terms of the shifted 1-RDM,
Q(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})
, defined as follows:

(15)

[Q(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})]_{mn}:=[P(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})]_{mn}-\frac{1}{2}\delta_{mn}\delta_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}}.

Since
P^{\dagger}=P
and
P^{2}=P
, one easily checks that
Q^{\dagger}=Q
and
Q^{2}=\frac{1}{4}I
.
Furthermore,
Q(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})
inherits the property
Q(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}+\mathbf{G}^{\prime})=Q(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})
for all
\mathbf{G},\mathbf{G}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}
from the 1-RDM
P
.

The Hartree-Fock energy can be written in terms of
Q(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})
as follows (
Appendix

A
) :

(16)

\begin{split}\langle&\Psi|\hat{H}_{\rm FBI}|\Psi\rangle=\frac{1}{N_{\mathbf{k}}|\Omega|}\sum_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}+\mathcal{K}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\left|\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\mathrm{Tr}{\Big(\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})Q(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\mathbf{k})\Big)}\right|^{2}\\
&+\frac{1}{4N_{\mathbf{k}}|\Omega|}\sum_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}+\mathcal{K}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\|\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\|_{F}^{2}\\
&-\frac{1}{N_{\mathbf{k}}|\Omega|}\sum_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}+\mathcal{K}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}}\mathrm{Tr}{\Big(\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})Q(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})^{\dagger}Q(\mathbf{k}^{\prime}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\mathbf{k})\Big)}.\end{split}

We can further simplify this expression by expanding the families of
(2M)\times(2M)
matrices
\{\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime}):\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}\}
and
\{Q(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}):\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}\}
into larger
(2MN_{\mathbf{k}})\times(2MN_{\mathbf{k}})
matrices similar to
Eq.

13
.
We start by defining notation for the standard basis on the space of 1-RDMs

(17)

\{\ket{n,\mathbf{k}}:n\in\mathcal{N},\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}\}

where we take the convention that if
\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}+\Gamma^{*}
then

(18)

\ket{n,\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}:=\ket{n,\widetilde{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}}

where
\widetilde{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{K}
is the unique representative of
\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{K}
modulo
\Gamma^{*}
lattice translations.
We additionally define the momentum shift matrix:

(19)

\pi_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}:=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\sum_{m\in\mathcal{N}}\ket{m,\mathbf{k}}\bra{m,\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}.

This shifts momentum
\mathbf{k}
to momentum
\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime}
modulo
\Gamma^{*}
in the basis
\ket{m,\mathbf{k}}
.

Using this basis, for any
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}
we define the matrix

(20)

\Lambda(\mathbf{q}^{\prime}):=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\sum_{m,n\in\mathcal{N}}[\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})]_{mn}\ket{m,\mathbf{k}}\bra{n,\mathbf{k}}

which can be expressed as a direct sum as follows

(21)

\Lambda(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})=\bigoplus_{n=1}^{N_{\mathbf{k}}}\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}_{n}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})=\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})&&&&\\
&\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})&&&\\
&&\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}_{3}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})&&\\
&&&\ddots&\\
&&&&\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}_{N_{\mathbf{k}}}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\par\end{bmatrix}.

Furthermore, the matrix
Q
can be written in this basis as

(22)

Q:=\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}}\sum_{m,n\in\mathcal{N}}[Q(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})]_{mn}\ket{m,\mathbf{k}}\bra{n,\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}.

Note that in this basis
Q=P-\frac{1}{2}I
. Now observe that

(23)

\begin{split}\pi_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}Q&=\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}}\sum_{m,n\in\mathcal{N}}[Q(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})]_{mn}\ket{m,\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}\bra{n,\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\\
&=\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}}\sum_{m,n\in\mathcal{N}}[Q(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})]_{mn}\ket{m,\mathbf{k}}\bra{n,\mathbf{k}^{\prime}},\end{split}

where the shift in
\ket{m,\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}
in the first line is due to the multiplication on the right by
\pi_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}
.
Performing the change of variables
\mathbf{k}\mapsto\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime}
is justified, since
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}+\Gamma^{*}
and
Q(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})
is invariant under shifts by
\Gamma^{*}
lattice vectors.

Similarly,

(24)

\begin{split}\pi_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\Lambda(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})^{\dagger}&=\sum_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}}\sum_{m,n\in\mathcal{N}}[\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})^{\dagger}]_{mn}\ket{m,\mathbf{k}^{\prime}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}\bra{n,\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\\
&=\sum_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}}\sum_{m,n\in\mathcal{N}}[\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})^{\dagger}]_{mn}\ket{m,\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\bra{n,\mathbf{k}^{\prime}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}\\
\end{split}

where we have used the fact that
\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})=\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})
for any
\mathbf{G}\in\Gamma^{*}
(see
Lemma

A.1
) to perform the change of variables
\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\mapsto\mathbf{k}^{\prime}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}
.

Therefore, the energy of a state
\ket{\Psi}
can be written

(25)

\begin{split}\braket{\Psi|\hat{H}_{\rm FBI}|\Psi}=&\frac{1}{N_{\mathbf{k}}|\Omega|}\sum_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}+\mathcal{K}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\left|\mathrm{Tr}{\Big(\Lambda(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\pi_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}Q\Big)}\right|^{2}\\
&+\frac{1}{4N_{\mathbf{k}}|\Omega|}\sum_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}+\mathcal{K}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\|\Lambda(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\|_{F}^{2}\\
&-\frac{1}{N_{\mathbf{k}}|\Omega|}\sum_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}+\mathcal{K}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\mathrm{Tr}{\bigg(\Lambda(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\pi_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}Q^{\dagger}\pi_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\Lambda(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})^{\dagger}Q\bigg)}.\end{split}

We now recall the following elementary identity, whose proof is in
Appendix

B
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
A,B
be arbitrary matrices then we have the following equality

(26)

\operatorname{Re}{\left(\mathrm{Tr}{\left(ABA^{\dagger}B^{\dagger}\right)}\right)}=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}{\Big(AA^{\dagger}B^{\dagger}B+A^{\dagger}ABB^{\dagger}\Big)}-\frac{1}{2}\|[A,B]\|_{F}^{2}.

Since the energy of a state is purely real, we can set
A=\Lambda(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\pi_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}
and
B=Q^{{\dagger}}
and use
Lemma

3.1
to rewrite
Eq.

25
as

(27)

\begin{split}\langle&\Psi|\hat{H}_{\rm FBI}|\Psi\rangle\\
&=\frac{1}{N_{\mathbf{k}}|\Omega|}\sum_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}+\Gamma^{*}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\left|\mathrm{Tr}{\Big(\Lambda(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\pi_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}Q\Big)}\right|^{2}\\
&+\frac{1}{4N_{\mathbf{k}}|\Omega|}\sum_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}+\Gamma^{*}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\|\Lambda(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\|_{F}^{2}\\
&-\frac{1}{2N_{\mathbf{k}}|\Omega|}\sum_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}+\Gamma^{*}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\mathrm{Tr}{\bigg(\pi_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\Lambda(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})^{\dagger}\Lambda(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\pi_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}QQ^{\dagger}+\Lambda(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\Lambda(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})^{\dagger}Q^{\dagger}Q\bigg)}\\
&+\frac{1}{2N_{\mathbf{k}}|\Omega|}\sum_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}+\Gamma^{*}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\|[Q,\Lambda(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\pi_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}]\|_{F}^{2}.\end{split}

But for Hartree-Fock states
QQ^{\dagger}=Q^{\dagger}Q=\frac{1}{4}I
, the middle two terms cancel, leaving us with

(28)

\begin{split}\langle&\Psi|\hat{H}_{\rm FBI}|\Psi\rangle\\
&=\frac{1}{N_{\mathbf{k}}|\Omega|}\sum_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}+\Gamma^{*}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\left(\left|\mathrm{Tr}{\Big(\Lambda(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\pi_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}Q\Big)}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|[Q,\Lambda(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\pi_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}]\|_{F}^{2}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{N_{\mathbf{k}}|\Omega|}\sum_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}+\Gamma^{*}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\left(\left|\mathrm{Tr}{\Big(\Lambda(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\pi_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}\Big(P-\frac{1}{2}I\Big)\Big)}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|[P,\Lambda(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\pi_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}]\|_{F}^{2}\right)\end{split}

where in the last line we have used
Q=P-\frac{1}{2}I
and that the identity commutes with all matrices.
Hence a Hartree-Fock state
\ket{\Psi}
is a ground state of
\hat{H}_{\rm FBI}
if and only if the following two conditions hold for all
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}+\Gamma^{*}

(29)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}{\Big(\Lambda(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\pi_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}\Big(P-\frac{1}{2}I\Big)\Big)}

\displaystyle=0,

(30)

\displaystyle[P,\Lambda(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\pi_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}]

\displaystyle=0.

While the trace condition
Eq.

29
can be used to eliminate certain states as ground states, the commutator condition
Eq.

30
is a powerful tool for characterizing possible Hartree-Fock ground states.

4.
Generalized Ferromagnetism in Flat-Band Interacting Hamiltonians

In a ferromagnet there are two ground states with equal energy:
\ket{\uparrow\uparrow\uparrow\uparrow\uparrow\cdots}
and
\ket{\downarrow\downarrow\downarrow\downarrow\downarrow\cdots}
.
For such ground states, by knowing the value of the spin at a single site, we can determine the value at all other sites.
Similar to a ferromagnet, if
\ket{\Psi}
is a Hartree-Fock ground state of
Eq.

6
, then by knowing the value of the 1-RDM at a single momenta there is an explicit method to compute 1-RDM at other momenta.
We term this property “generalized ferromagnetism” and formally have the following proposition:

Proposition 1

(Generalized Ferromagnetism)
.

Suppose that
P
is the 1-RDM of a Hartree-Fock ground state of a frustration-free FBI Hamiltonian
Eq.

6
and the grid
\mathcal{K}
satisfies
1
.
For any
\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2}\in\mathcal{K}
there exists a family of invertible matrices
\{B_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime}):\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K},\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\}
so that

(31)

P(\mathbf{k}_{1}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\mathbf{k}_{2}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})=B_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})^{-1}P(\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2})B_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime}).

Hence, if the value of
P(\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2})
is known, then
P(\mathbf{k}_{1}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\mathbf{k}_{2}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})
is determined for all
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}
.

An immediate implication of
Proposition

1
is that the Hartree-Fock ground states must be uniformly filled.

Corollary 1
.

If
P
is as in
Proposition

1
then for all
\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}
,
\mathrm{Tr}{(P(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}))}=\mathrm{Tr}{(P(\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}))}
.

Proof of
Corollary

1
.

Taking
\mathbf{k}_{1}=\mathbf{k}_{2}
in the statement of
Proposition

1
we see that

(32)

\begin{split}\mathrm{Tr}{(P(\mathbf{k}_{1}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\mathbf{k}_{1}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime}))}=\mathrm{Tr}{(B_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})^{-1}P(\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{1})B_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime}))}=\mathrm{Tr}{(P(\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{1}))}.\end{split}

Since the choice of
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}
was arbitrary, this proves the result.
∎

The proof of
Proposition

1
relies on the following lemma proven in our previous work:

Lemma 4.1

(Lemma 7.1
[
4
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}
and let
\Pi(\mathbf{k})
denote the flat-band projection at
\mathbf{k}
.
If
\|\Pi(\mathbf{k})-\Pi(\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\|<1
then there exists a
\mathbf{G}\in\Gamma^{*}
so that
\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}((\mathbf{k}^{\prime}-\mathbf{k})+\mathbf{G})
has full rank.

We now turn to prove
Proposition

1
.

Proof of
Proposition

1
.

Let us first fix some
\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}
and some
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}+\Gamma^{*}
.
We can then decompose
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}=\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G}
where
\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{K}
and
\mathbf{G}\in\Gamma^{*}
.
For this choice of
\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G}
,
Eq.

30
implies that for all
\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}

(33)

P(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})-\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})P(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}+\mathbf{q})=0.

If
\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})
is invertible then we have that

(34)

P(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}+\mathbf{q})=\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})^{-1}P(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G}).

While we cannot assume that
\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})
is invertible in general, if we choose
\mathbf{q}
sufficiently small, then by
Lemma

4.1
there exists a
\mathbf{G}
so that
\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})
must be invertible.

By
1
, we can construct a sequence
\{\mathbf{k}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{L}
which connects
\mathbf{k}
to
\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}
so that
\|\Pi(\mathbf{k}_{i+1})-\Pi(\mathbf{k}_{i})\|<1
for
i=1,\cdots,L-1
.
Along this path, there always exists a
\mathbf{G}_{i}
so that the form factor matrix
\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}_{i}}(\mathbf{k}_{i+1}-\mathbf{k}_{i}+\mathbf{G}_{i})
is invertible.
Therefore, we can prove the proposition by recursively applying
Eq.

34
.

To make the induction step more clear, we define the momentum difference
\Delta:=\mathbf{k}^{\prime}-\mathbf{k}
and the sequence of differences
\delta_{i}=\mathbf{k}_{i+1}-\mathbf{k}_{i}
.
By
Lemma

4.1
, there exists a
\mathbf{G}_{1}\in\Gamma^{*}
so that
\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}(\delta_{1}+\mathbf{G}_{1})
is invertible.
Therefore, by
Eq.

34

(35)

\begin{split}P(\mathbf{k}_{2},\mathbf{k}_{2}+\Delta)&=P(\mathbf{k}_{1}+\delta_{1},\mathbf{k}_{1}+\Delta+\delta_{1})\\
&=\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}(\delta_{1}+\mathbf{G}_{1})^{-1}P(\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{1}+\Delta)\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}_{1}+\Delta}(\delta_{1}+\mathbf{G}_{1}).\end{split}

We can then apply the same argument to the next momentum in the sequence
\mathbf{k}_{3}

(36)

\begin{split}P(\mathbf{k}_{3},\mathbf{k}_{3}+\Delta)&=P(\mathbf{k}_{2}+\delta_{2},\mathbf{k}_{2}+\Delta+\delta_{2})\\
&=\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}(\delta_{2}+\mathbf{G}_{2})^{-1}P(\mathbf{k}_{2},\mathbf{k}_{2}+\Delta)\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}_{2}+\Delta}(\delta_{2}+\mathbf{G}_{2}).\end{split}

Thus, by repeatedly applying
Eq.

34
along the path
\{\mathbf{k}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{L}
we conclude

(37)

P(\mathbf{k}_{L},\mathbf{k}_{L}+\Delta)=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{L-1}\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}_{i}}(\delta_{i}+\mathbf{G}_{i})\right)^{-1}P(\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{1}+\Delta)\left(\prod_{i=1}^{L-1}\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}_{i}+\Delta}(\delta_{i}+\mathbf{G}_{i})\right).

Therefore, since
\mathbf{k}_{1}=\mathbf{k}
,
\Delta=\mathbf{k}^{\prime}-\mathbf{k}
, and
\mathbf{k}_{L}=\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}
this proves the proposition.
∎

5.
The Sylvester Equation and Translation Breaking

While we considered
Eq.

33
with
\mathbf{q}\neq\bm{0}
in
Section

4
, let us now consider the case when
\mathbf{q}=\bm{0}
.
By choosing
\mathbf{q}=\bm{0}
, from
Eq.

33
we see that to be a ground state
P(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})
must satisfy

(38)

P(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{G})-\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G})P(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})=0\quad\forall\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K},\penalty\ \forall\mathbf{G}\in\Gamma^{*}.

The equation
XA-BX=C
for some fixed
A,B,C
is known as the Sylvester equation. A simple condition ensures that this equation has a unique solution (see
[
6
, Theorem VII.2.1]
with
C=0
).

Lemma 5.1
.

Consider the Sylvester-type equation with
A,B\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
,

(39)

XA-BX=0\text{ for }X\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}.

The value
X=0
is always a solution to this equation.
Furthermore,
X=0
is the unique solution if the eigenvalues of
A
and
B
are disjoint.

Combining
Eqs.

38
and
5.1
we have the following condition to rule out translation symmetry breaking.

Proposition 2

(No translation symmetry breaking)
.

Suppose that
P
is the 1-RDM of a Hartree-Fock ground state of a frustration-free FBI Hamiltonian
Eq.

6
.
For any
\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}
, if there exists a
\mathbf{G}\in\Gamma^{*}
so that the eigenvalues of
\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G})
and
\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{G})
are disjoint, then
P(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})=0
.

If the eigenvalues of
A
and
B
in
Eq.

39
are not disjoint then there can be many possible choices for
X
which solve
XA-BX=0
.
While characterizing all such possible solutions analytically is difficult in the general case, for the special case of TBG-2 there is additional structure which allow us to precisely determine the structure of the degeneracy in solutions to
Eq.

38
.

We begin by proving that no translation breaking occurs in spinless, valleyless TBG-2 in
Section

5.1
and then characterize the degeneracy in valleyful and spinful models in
Section

5.2
.
Finally in
Section

5.3
, we outline a numerical algorithm which can be used to determine a spanning set for the vector space of solutions to
Eq.

38
and prove its correctness.

5.1.
Application to Spinless, Valleyless TBG

We begin by converting
Eq.

38
from an equation depending on
\mathbf{G}\in\Gamma^{*}
to an equation depending on
\mathbf{r}\in\Omega
by taking the Fourier transform.

First, we define
\rho_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{r})\in\mathbb{C}^{(2M)\times(2M)}
as follows

(40)

[\rho_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{r})]_{m,n}:=\braket{u_{m\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r}),u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})}.

Since the periodic Bloch functions,
u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})
, are periodic with respect to
\Gamma
,
\rho_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{r})
is also periodic with respect to
\Gamma
; that is,
\rho_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{R})=\rho_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{r})
for all
\mathbf{R}\in\Gamma
.
A straightforward calculation shows that that the function
\mathbf{G}\mapsto\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})
coincides with the Fourier coefficients of
\rho_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}(\mathbf{r})
when considered as a periodic function on the unit cell
\Omega
.
More specifically,

(41)

\rho_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{\mathbf{G}\in\Gamma^{*}}e^{-i\mathbf{G}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G}).

Therefore, by multiplying by
e^{i\mathbf{G}\cdot\mathbf{r}}
and summing over
\mathbf{G}\in\Gamma^{*}
we can transform
Eq.

38
to real space as follows

(42)

P(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\rho_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{r})-\rho_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})P(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})=0,\qquad\forall\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K},\penalty\ \forall\mathbf{r}\in\Omega.

The Hamiltonian for TBG-2 in the chiral limit takes the form
[
23
,
2
]

(43)

H_{\mathbf{k}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&D_{\mathbf{k}}(\alpha)^{\dagger}\\
D_{\mathbf{k}}(\alpha)&0\end{bmatrix}.

Here,
D_{\mathbf{k}}(\alpha)=D(\alpha)+(k_{x}+ik_{y})I_{2\times 2}
where
D(\alpha)=\begin{bmatrix}2\bar{D}&\alpha V(\mathbf{r})\\
\alpha V(-\mathbf{r})&2\bar{D}\end{bmatrix}
with
\overline{D}=-i\partial_{\bar{z}}
and
V(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{n=0}^{2}\omega^{n}e^{-i\mathbf{q}_{n}\cdot\mathbf{r}}
with
\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}
.

The Hamiltonian
H_{\mathbf{k}}
satisfies two symmetries
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{L}
whose actions are defined as follows

(44)

\mathcal{Q}u(\mathbf{r}):=\begin{bmatrix}&&1&\\
&&&1\\
1&&&\\
&1&&\end{bmatrix}\overline{u(-\mathbf{r})}\qquad\mathcal{L}u(\mathbf{r}):=\begin{bmatrix}&1&&\\
-1&&&\\
&&&1\\
&&-1&\end{bmatrix}\overline{u(-\mathbf{r})}\\

Note that
\mathcal{Q}
maps
\mathbf{k}
to
\mathbf{k}
and
\mathcal{L}
maps
\mathbf{k}
to
-\mathbf{k}
and
[\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{L}]=0
.

Due to
\mathcal{Q}
symmetry, the zero eigenvectors of
H_{\mathbf{k}}
can be written as
\begin{bmatrix}0&v(\mathbf{r})\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
and
\begin{bmatrix}\,\overline{v(-\mathbf{r})}&0\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
.
Hence after a basis rotation,
\rho_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})
can be written as follows

(45)

\rho_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})=\begin{bmatrix}\|u_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})\|^{2}&\\
&\|u_{\mathbf{k}}(-\mathbf{r})\|^{2}\end{bmatrix}.

Due to
\mathcal{L}
symmetry and the fact that
[\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{L}]=0
, we may also choose an appropriate basis change so that a state
u_{\mathbf{0}}
satisfying
D(\alpha)u_{\mathbf{0}}=0
has the property

\left\|u_{\mathbf{0}}(\mathbf{r})\right\|=\left\|\mathcal{L}u_{\mathbf{0}}(\mathbf{r})\right\|=\left\|u_{\mathbf{0}}(-\mathbf{r})\right\|,

where, in this particular case,
\mathcal{L}
just denotes the upper
2\times 2
block of the symmetry defined in (
44
).
With these observations, we can now characterize all Hartree-Fock ground states of the FBI model for TBG-2.

Theorem 1
.

For TBG-2 at half-filling without the valley or spin degrees of freedom, the Hartree-Fock ground states take the form

(46)

P(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})=\begin{cases}P_{0}&\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\\
0&\mathbf{k}\neq\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\end{cases}

where
P_{0}
the same for all
\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}
and equal to either
\operatorname{diag}(1,0)
or
\operatorname{diag}(0,1)
.

Remark 1
.

The states found in
Theorem

1
are the same ferromagnetic Slater determinant states found in our previous work
[
4
]
.

Proof.

By
[
3
, Lemma
3.1
]
, we know that at a magic
\alpha
the one-particle Hamiltonian
H
exhibits precisely two flat bands at energy zero

(47)

\mathbf{u}_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})=F_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})\mathbf{u}_{\mathbf{0}}(\mathbf{r})\in\ker(D(\alpha)+(k_{1}+ik_{2})I_{2\times 2}),

where

F_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r}):=e^{\frac{(k_{1}+ik_{2})}{2}(-i(1+\omega)x+(\omega-1)y)}\frac{\theta(\frac{3(x_{1}+ix_{2})}{4\pi i\omega}+\frac{k_{1}+ik_{2}}{\sqrt{3}\omega})}{\theta\Big(\frac{3(x_{1}+ix_{2})}{4\pi i\omega}\Big)}.

Since
\theta
is an odd function,
F_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})
satisfies the reflection symmetry

F_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})=F_{-\mathbf{k}}(-\mathbf{r}).

The function
\mathbf{u}_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})
has a unique zero at
\mathbf{r}=\frac{4\pi}{3\sqrt{3}}(k_{2},-k_{1})^{\top}
per unit cell (note that both
\mathbf{u}_{\mathbf{0}}
and
\theta
have a simple zero at
\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{0}
).
This implies that

\|u_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})\|=\|F_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})u_{\mathbf{0}}(\mathbf{r})\|=\|F_{-\mathbf{k}}(-\mathbf{r})u_{\mathbf{0}}(-\mathbf{r})\|=\|u_{-\mathbf{k}}(-\mathbf{r})\|.

To show that
P(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})=0
for a disjoint pair
\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}
, it suffices to show by (
42
), that there are
\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\in\Omega
such that

{\|u_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})\|\neq\|u_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{r})\|}\text{ and }\|u_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\|\neq\|u_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\|.

To satisfy the first constraint, we may choose the unique point
\mathbf{r}=\frac{4\pi}{3\sqrt{3}}(k_{2},-k_{1})^{\top}
at which
u_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})=0,
then
u_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{r})\neq 0.

For this choice of
\mathbf{r},
we also have

u_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(-\mathbf{r})\neq 0\text{ as long as }\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\neq-\mathbf{k}.

This implies that

P(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})=0\text{ for }\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\neq-\mathbf{k}.

While we cannot directly conclude that
P(\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{k})=0
for
\mathbf{k}\notin\Gamma^{*}
, we may invoke
Eq.

31
, and use that we have previously shown that
P(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})=0
whenever
\mathbf{k}\notin\{\mathbf{k}^{\prime},-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\}
.
In particular,
Eq.

31
implies that for any
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Gamma^{*}
,

\operatorname{rank}(P(\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{k}))=\operatorname{rank}(P(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime},-\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime}))=0.

This implies that

P(\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{k})=0\text{ for all }\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}.

Finally, for
\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\neq\mathbf{0}
it follows from specializing in (
45
) to
\mathbf{r}=\frac{4\pi}{3\sqrt{3}}(k_{2},-k_{1})^{\top}
, in which case
\rho_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})=\operatorname{diag}(0,\|u_{\mathbf{k}}(-\mathbf{r})\|^{2})
with
u_{\mathbf{k}}(-\mathbf{r})\neq 0.

This implies that
P(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k})
is diagonal.
Since
P^{2}=P
and
P(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})=0
whenever
\mathbf{k}\neq\mathbf{k}^{\prime}
, we conclude that
P(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k})^{2}=P(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k})
and so its eigenvalues are
0
and
1
.
At half-filling, by
Corollary

1
, we know
\mathrm{Tr}{(P(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}))}=1
and so
P(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k})=\operatorname{diag}(1,0)
or
P(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k})=\operatorname{diag}(0,1)
for all
\mathbf{k}
.

Finally, for
\mathbf{k}^{\prime}=\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{0}
, the condition (
45
) does not imply any restrictions on
P(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0}),
since
\rho_{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0}}
is proportional to the identity matrix.
However, the proof of
Proposition

1
shows that one can choose diagonal
B_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})
. This implies by (
45
) that
\mathbf{k}\mapsto P(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k})
is either
\operatorname{diag}(1,0)
or
\operatorname{diag}(0,1)
for all
\mathbf{k}
.

To complete the proof, we must verify that
Eq.

29
also vanishes.
This follows from the sum rule

(48)

\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\mathrm{Tr}{\left(\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G})\begin{bmatrix}1&\\
&-1\end{bmatrix}\right)}=0\quad\forall\mathbf{G}\in\Gamma^{*}

proven in our previous work
[
4
, Lemma 4.3]
.

∎

5.2.
Extension to Valleyful and Spinful Models

In the previous section, we showed that in single valley TBG-2 the only possible Hartree-Fock ground states are the two ferromagnetic Slater determinants by using the specific properties of the TBG-2 eigenfunctions.
While the final conclusion depended on the specific form of these eigenfunctions all of the steps up to and including
Eq.

42
were done in a completely general setting (i.e. without reference to an underlying Hamiltonian).
Therefore, to translate these results to the more realistic valleyful and spinful settings we simply need to substitute in appropriate definition for
\rho_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})
.

Following the analysis in
[
8
]
, for the chiral model of TBG-2 with valley, the corresponding
\rho_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})
takes the form:

(49)

\rho_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}}^{\rm V}(\mathbf{r})=\begin{bmatrix}\|u_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})\|^{2}&&&\\
&\|u_{\mathbf{k}}(-\mathbf{r})\|^{2}&&\\
&&\|u_{\mathbf{k}}(-\mathbf{r})\|^{2}&\\
&&&\|u_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})\|^{2}\end{bmatrix}

and when including valley and spin it takes the form

(50)

\rho_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}}^{\rm V+S}(\mathbf{r})=\begin{bmatrix}\|u_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})\|^{2}&&&\\
&\|u_{\mathbf{k}}(-\mathbf{r})\|^{2}&&\\
&&\|u_{\mathbf{k}}(-\mathbf{r})\|^{2}&\\
&&&\|u_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})\|^{2}\end{bmatrix}\otimes I_{2\times 2}.

We will now show that when including the valley degree of freedom, the ground state exhibits a
{\rm U}(2)\times{\rm U}(2)
degeneracy.
A similar argument shows that in the presence of the valley
and spin
degrees of freedom, the ground state is unique up to a
{\rm U}(4)\times{\rm U}(4)
degree of freedom.

Theorem 2
.

At TBG-2 at half-filling with the valley degree of freedom but without spin, the Hartree-Fock ground states take the form

(51)

P(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})=\begin{cases}UP_{0}U^{\dagger}&\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\\
0&\mathbf{k}\neq\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\end{cases}

where
U
and
P_{0}
are the same for all
\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}
.
Furthermore,
U\in{\mathrm{U}}(2)\times{\mathrm{U}}(2)
and
P_{0}
is one of the following matrices
\operatorname{diag}(1,0,0,1)
,
\operatorname{diag}(1,1,0,0)
, and
\operatorname{diag}(0,1,1,0)
.

Proof.

Following the same reasoning used in the proof of
Theorem

1
, we know that
P(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})=0
for all
\mathbf{k}\neq\mathbf{k}^{\prime}
.
By
Proposition

1
, it suffices to choose a single
\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}
to fix the ground state.
Pick any
\mathbf{k}_{*}\in\mathcal{K}
so that
\|u_{\mathbf{k}_{*}}(\mathbf{r})\|\neq\|u_{\mathbf{k}_{*}}(-\mathbf{r})\|
for some
\mathbf{r}\in\Omega
.
To make the algebra simpler, we define the permutation matrix
\Pi
:

(52)

\Pi=\begin{bmatrix}1&&&\\
&&&1\\
&&1&\\
&1&&\end{bmatrix}

which satisfies
\Pi^{\dagger}=\Pi
and
\Pi^{2}=I
.
Applying
\Pi
to both sides of
Eq.

45
we find that for
P(\mathbf{k}_{*},\mathbf{k}_{*})
to be the 1-RDM of a ground state it must be that

(53)

\Big(\Pi P(\mathbf{k}_{*},\mathbf{k}_{*})\Pi\Big)\Big(\Pi\rho_{\mathbf{k}_{*},\mathbf{k}_{*}}(\mathbf{r})\Pi\Big)-\Big(\Pi\rho_{\mathbf{k}_{*},\mathbf{k}_{*}}(\mathbf{r})\Pi\Big)\Big(\Pi P(\mathbf{k}_{*},\mathbf{k}_{*})\Pi\Big)=0.

We will derive a characterization for all possible solutions
\Pi P(\mathbf{k}_{*},\mathbf{k}_{*})\Pi
of
Eq.

53
at half-filling.
Since
\Pi
is a unitary, this also characterizes all possible solutions
P(\mathbf{k}_{*},\mathbf{k}_{*})
satisfying
Eq.

42
after a change of basis.

Notice that

(54)

\begin{split}\Pi\rho_{\mathbf{k}_{*},\mathbf{k}_{*}}^{\rm V}(\mathbf{r})\Pi&=\begin{bmatrix}\|u_{\mathbf{k}_{*}}(\mathbf{r})\|^{2}&&&\\
&\|u_{\mathbf{k}_{*}}(\mathbf{r})\|^{2}&&\\
&&\|u_{\mathbf{k}_{*}}(-\mathbf{r})\|^{2}&\\
&&&\|u_{\mathbf{k}_{*}}(-\mathbf{r})\|^{2}\end{bmatrix}\\[4.30554pt]
&=\begin{bmatrix}\|u_{\mathbf{k}_{*}}(\mathbf{r})\|^{2}I_{2\times 2}&\\
&\|u_{\mathbf{k}_{*}}(-\mathbf{r})\|^{2}I_{2\times 2}\end{bmatrix}.\end{split}

So splitting
\Pi P(\mathbf{k}_{*},\mathbf{k}_{*})\Pi
into
2\times 2
blocks

(55)

\Pi P(\mathbf{k}_{*},\mathbf{k}_{*})\Pi=:\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{P}_{11}&\tilde{P}_{12}\\
\tilde{P}_{12}^{\dagger}&\tilde{P}_{22}\end{bmatrix},

we can explicitly compute the commutator in terms of
\tilde{P}_{11}
,
\tilde{P}_{22}
,
\tilde{P}_{12}
:

(56)

[\Pi P(\mathbf{k}_{*},\mathbf{k}_{*})\Pi,\Pi\rho_{\mathbf{k}_{*},\mathbf{k}_{*}}^{\rm V}(\mathbf{r})\Pi]=(\|u_{\mathbf{k}_{*}}(-\mathbf{r})\|^{2}-\|u_{\mathbf{k}_{*}}(\mathbf{r})\|^{2})\begin{bmatrix}&\tilde{P}_{12}\\
-\tilde{P}_{12}^{\dagger}&\end{bmatrix}.

Since
\|u_{\mathbf{k}_{*}}(-\mathbf{r})\|^{2}\neq\|u_{\mathbf{k}_{*}}(\mathbf{r})\|^{2}
for some
\mathbf{r}
, for
Eq.

45
to vanish it must be that
\tilde{P}_{12}\equiv 0
.
Hence any solution to
Eq.

53
must be block diagonal.

Since
P(\mathbf{k}_{*},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})=0
for all
\mathbf{k}_{*}\neq\mathbf{k}^{\prime}
,
\Pi P(\mathbf{k}_{*},\mathbf{k}_{*})\Pi
is an orthogonal projection.
The set of all transformations preserving this block structure while maintaining orthogonality is given by
U\in{\rm U}(2)\times{\rm U}(2)
where
U
takes the form

(57)

U=\begin{bmatrix}U_{1}&\\
&U_{2}\end{bmatrix}\quad U_{1},U_{2}\in{\rm U}(2).

Due to
Corollary

1
, at half-filling,
\Pi P(\mathbf{k}_{*},\mathbf{k}_{*})\Pi
has rank 2 and hence all possible solutions to
Eq.

53
must lie in one of three orbits:

(58)

U\begin{bmatrix}1&&&\\
&1&&\\
&&0&\\
&&&0\end{bmatrix}U^{\dagger}\qquad U\begin{bmatrix}1&&&\\
&0&&\\
&&1&\\
&&&0\end{bmatrix}U^{\dagger}\qquad U\begin{bmatrix}0&&&\\
&0&&\\
&&1&\\
&&&1\end{bmatrix}.U^{\dagger}

Suppose we choose
\Pi P(\mathbf{k}_{*},\mathbf{k}_{*})\Pi
to be one of the options in
Eq.

58
; we now will argue that
\Pi P(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k})\Pi=\Pi P(\mathbf{k}_{*},\mathbf{k}_{*})\Pi
for all
\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}
.
Due to
\mathcal{Q}
symmetry, the pair product
\Pi\rho_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}^{V}(\mathbf{r})\Pi
has the same form as in
Eq.

54
for all
\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}

[
8
]
. Therefore, the pair product commutes with
\Pi P(\mathbf{k}_{*},\mathbf{k}_{*})\Pi
and so following the proof of
Proposition

1
, we can conclude for a ground state
\Pi P(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k})\Pi=\Pi P(\mathbf{k}_{*},\mathbf{k}_{*})\Pi
for all
\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}
.

To complete the proof, we must also verify that that
Eq.

29
vanishes.
Since
[U,\Pi\rho_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}}^{V}(\mathbf{r})\Pi]=0
and
\Pi\rho_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}}^{V}(\mathbf{r})\Pi=I_{2\times 2}\otimes\rho_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})
, the sum rule
Eq.

48
implies
Eq.

29
vanishes.
The statement of the theorem follows reordering the basis by the permutation
\Pi
.
∎

Remark 2
.

We shall compare our result with statements in the physics literature.

(1)

In the physics literature, the structure
Eq.

53
is often expressed as
[\sigma_{z}\tau_{z},P(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k})]=0
where
\sigma_{z}
denotes the Pauli Z-matrix acting on the sublattice degree of freedom and
\tau_{z}
denotes the Pauli Z-matrix acting on the valley degree of freedom.
The operator
\sigma_{z}\tau_{z}
is called the Chern number operator.

(2)

In the physics literature, the
{\rm U}(2)\times{\rm U}(2)
(or
{\rm U}(4)\times{\rm U}(4)
) degree of freedom is often stated as a hidden symmetry of the FBI Hamiltonian.
The two perspectives are equivalent.
This is because if
\ket{\Psi}
is a ground state of
\hat{H}_{\rm FBI}
and
\mathcal{G}
is a symmetry satisfying
[\mathcal{G},\hat{H}_{FBI}]=0
, then
\mathcal{G}\ket{\Psi}
is also a ground state.

(3)

Since
U
is block diagonal, the orbits of
\operatorname{diag}(1,0,0,1)
and
\operatorname{diag}(0,1,1,0)
only contain one element.
These two states are referred to as the quantum hall (QH) states as they occupy the positive eigenspace and negative eigenspace of the Chern number operator
\sigma_{z}\tau_{z}
.
In contrast, the third orbit contains a manifold of states which include states which occupy both valleys; such states are referred to as the intervalley coherent (IVC) states.

Using the same proof strategy, we can easily extend
Theorem

2
to include spin:

Theorem 3
.

At TBG-2 at half-filling with the valley and spin degrees of freedom, the Hartree-Fock ground states take the form

(59)

P(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})=\begin{cases}UP_{0}U^{\dagger}&\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\\
0&\mathbf{k}\neq\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\end{cases}

where
U
and
P_{0}
are the same for all
\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}
.
Furthermore,
U\in{\mathrm{U}}(4)\times{\mathrm{U}}(4)
and
P_{0}
is equal to one of the following matrices
\operatorname{diag}(1,0,0,1,1,0,0,1)
,
\operatorname{diag}(1,1,0,1,1,0,0,0)
,
\operatorname{diag}(1,1,1,1,0,0,0,0)
,
\operatorname{diag}(0,0,1,0,0,1,1,1)
, or
\operatorname{diag}(0,1,1,0,0,1,1,0)
.

5.3.
An Algorithm for Determining Translation Symmetry Breaking

In cases when an analytical form of the form factor is not known,
Eq.

38
can be used to design an algorithm for identifying when translation symmetry breaking can occur.
From the vectorization identity, we can convert
Eq.

38
into a vector equation as follows

(60)

\left(\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{G})^{\top}\otimes I_{(2M)\times(2M)}-I_{(2M)\times(2M)}\otimes\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G})\right)\operatorname{vec}{(P(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}))}=0\quad\forall\mathbf{G}\in\Gamma^{*},

where
\operatorname{vec}{(\cdot)}
transforms a
(2M)\times(2M)
matrix into a vector with
(2M)^{2}
entries by stacking the columns.
Define the
(2M)^{2}\times(2M)^{2}
matrix
M_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{G})
:

(61)

M_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{G}):=\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{G})^{\top}\otimes I_{(2M)\times(2M)}-I_{(2M)\times(2M)}\otimes\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G}).

Then
P(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})
satisfies
Eq.

38
if and only if its vectorization lies in the kernel
M_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{G})
for all
\mathbf{G}\in\Gamma^{*}
.
We can further reduce our computational effort by squaring
M_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{G})
and summing over
\mathbf{G}
:

(62)

M_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}:=\sum_{\mathbf{G}\in\Gamma^{*}}M_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{G})^{\dagger}M_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{G}).

The following proposition is now immediate

Proposition 3
.

The matrix
M_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}
is positive semidefinite and its kernel is exactly the intersection of the kernels of all the
M_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{G})
.

Since the form factors are the Fourier coefficients of a smooth function (
Eq.

41
) we have that

(63)

\sum_{\{\mathbf{G}\in\Gamma^{*}:|\mathbf{G}|>L\}}\|\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{G})\|_{F}^{2}\rightarrow 0\quad\text{as}\quad L\rightarrow\infty

Hence, in practice we can truncate the sum over
\mathbf{G}
in
Eq.

62
and by the Weyl bound we can find a spanning set for all
P(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})
lying in the kernel of
M_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}
by taking a few of the smallest eigenmodes of the truncated matrix.

Remark 3
.

In a discretized setting,
Proposition

3
also gives us a computationally efficient method for verifying the “no common invariant subspace” condition in
[
4
, Theorem 4]
.

Appendix A
Calculation of the Hartree-Fock Energy

To perform this calculation, we will recall a few properties of the form factor which will play a role in the following calculations (see, e.g.
[
4
, Lemma 4.2]
for proofs):

Lemma A.1
.

The form factor matrix
\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})
satisfies the following identities for all
\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{K}
and all
\mathbf{G},\mathbf{G}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}

(64)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}(-\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{G}),

(65)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})

Throughout this calculation, we will suppress the summation indexes for
\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime},m,n,m^{\prime},n^{\prime}

Expanding the terms in
\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})
and performing normal ordering gives

(66)

\begin{split}N_{\mathbf{k}}|\Omega|\hat{H}_{\rm FBI}=&\sum_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\sum_{m,n,m^{\prime},n^{\prime}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})[\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})]_{mn}[\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(-\mathbf{q}^{\prime})]_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}\hat{f}_{m\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{f}_{m^{\prime}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\hat{f}_{n^{\prime}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}\hat{f}_{n(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}\\[4.30554pt]

\ket{\Psi}

64

(70)

\begin{split}N_{\mathbf{k}}|\Omega|\langle\Psi&|\hat{H}_{\rm FBI}|\Psi\rangle\\
=&\sum_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}}\mathrm{Tr}{\Big(\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})Q(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\mathbf{k})\Big)}\mathrm{Tr}{\Big(\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(-\mathbf{q}^{\prime})Q(\mathbf{k}^{\prime}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\Big)}\\
&-\sum_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}}\mathrm{Tr}{\Big(\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})Q(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(-\mathbf{q}^{\prime})Q(\mathbf{k}^{\prime}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\mathbf{k})\Big)}\\
&+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\mathrm{Tr}{\Big(\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})^{\dagger}\Big)}.\end{split}

Finally, using the identity
\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(-\mathbf{q}^{\prime})=\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})^{\dagger}
and performing the change of variables in the first sum
\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\mapsto\mathbf{k}^{\prime}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime}
, we can finally write the energy of
\ket{\Psi}
as

(71)

\begin{split}\langle\Psi&|\hat{H}_{\rm FBI}|\Psi\rangle\\
=&\frac{1}{N_{\mathbf{k}}|\Omega|}\sum_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}+\mathcal{K}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\left|\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\mathrm{Tr}{\Big(\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})Q(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\mathbf{k})\Big)}\right|^{2}\\
&+\frac{1}{4N_{\mathbf{k}}|\Omega|}\sum_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}+\mathcal{K}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\|\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\|_{F}^{2}\\
&-\frac{1}{N_{\mathbf{k}}|\Omega|}\sum_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}+\mathcal{K}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}}\mathrm{Tr}{\Big(\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})Q(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})^{\dagger}Q(\mathbf{k}^{\prime}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\mathbf{k})\Big)}\end{split}

which completes the calculation.

Appendix B
Proof of
Lemma

3.1

First, we calculate

(72)

\|[A,B]\|_{F}^{2}=\mathrm{Tr}{([A,B]([A,B])^{\dagger})}=-\mathrm{Tr}{([A,B]([A^{\dagger},B^{\dagger}]))}.

Now we expand the product of commutators

(73)

\begin{split}[A,B][A^{\dagger},B^{\dagger}]&=(AB-BA)(A^{\dagger}B^{\dagger}-B^{\dagger}A^{\dagger})\\
&=ABA^{\dagger}B^{\dagger}-ABB^{\dagger}A^{\dagger}-BAA^{\dagger}B^{\dagger}+BAB^{\dagger}A^{\dagger}\\
&=\left(ABA^{\dagger}B^{\dagger}+(ABA^{\dagger}B^{\dagger})^{\dagger}\right)-\left(ABB^{\dagger}A^{\dagger}+BAA^{\dagger}B^{\dagger}\right).\end{split}

Taking the trace of both sides let’s us conclude that

(74)

-\|[A,B]\|_{F}^{2}=2\operatorname{Re}{\left(\mathrm{Tr}{(ABA^{\dagger}B^{\dagger})}\right)}-\mathrm{Tr}{\left(A^{\dagger}ABB^{\dagger}+AA^{\dagger}B^{\dagger}B\right)}.

Rearranging we conclude that

(75)

\operatorname{Re}{\left(\mathrm{Tr}{(ABA^{\dagger}B^{\dagger})}\right)}=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}{\Big(AA^{\dagger}B^{\dagger}B+A^{\dagger}ABB^{\dagger}\Big)}-\frac{1}{2}\|[A,B]\|_{F}^{2}

which proves the lemma.
