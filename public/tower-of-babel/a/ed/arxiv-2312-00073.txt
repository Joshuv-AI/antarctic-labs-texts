Binary perceptrons capacity via fully lifted random duality theory
Mihailo Stojnic

Abstract




Abstract

We study the statistical capacity of the classical binary perceptrons with general thresholds
\kappa
. After recognizing the connection between the capacity and the bilinearly indexed (bli) random processes, we utilize a recent progress in studying such processes to characterize the capacity. In particular, we rely on
fully lifted
random duality theory (fl RDT) established in
[
45
]
to create a general framework for studying the perceptrons’ capacities. Successful underlying numerical evaluations are required for the framework (and ultimately the entire fl RDT machinery) to become fully practically operational. We present results obtained in that directions and uncover that the capacity characterizations are achieved on the second (first non-trivial) level of
stationarized
full lifting. The obtained results
exactly
match the replica symmetry breaking predictions obtained through statistical physics replica methods in
[
23
]
. Most notably, for the famous zero-threshold scenario,
\kappa=0
, we uncover the well known
\alpha\approx 0.8330786
scaled capacity.

Index Terms: Binary perceptrons; Fully lifted random duality theory
.

1
Introduction

After a prior, more than half of a century long, intensive studying, it is the last two decades in particular that brought an unprecedented interest to neural networks (NN) and machine learning (ML). Various research directions within these fields have been of interest, including, development of efficient algorithmic techniques, recognition of potential applications/connections to different scientific/engineering areas, creation of analytical tools for performance characterization and many others. Along similar lines, our primary interest here is the analytical progress and, in particular, theoretical studying of
perceptrons
– the moat basic NN units and almost unavoidable tools in any ML classifying concepts.

Due to their, basically irreplaceable, fundamental nature, perceptrons have been studied in many different fields and the amount of obtained results is rather large. It remains though completely fascinating that, even after quite a long period of studying and a plethora of great results achieved, many of the main concepts remain, from the analytical point, somewhat mysterious. Before we get to discuss some of them, we find it useful to briefly focus on the most relevant ones that are mathematically well understood.

As expected, we start with the most typical (and in some sense the simplest) perceptrons’ variants – the so-called
spherical
perceptrons. Perhaps surprisingly, even such simple NN units, are not easy to completely analytically characterize. For example, their classifying/storage
capacity
(as one of their most relevant features) is often difficult to compute and even harder to algorithmically achieve. Such a difficulty was recognized from the very early NN days. Consequently, the very early analytical treatments focused on various special cases and started with seminal works
[
52
,
26
,
10
]
where the zero-threshold capacity was precisely determined through combinatorial considerations closely related to high dimensional geometry and polytopal neighborliness (for relevant geometric followup extensions see, e.g.,
[
31
,
32
]
).

A generic analytical treatment started a couple of decades later though. Moreover, since the generic (non-zero thresholds) perceptrons turned out to be much harder than the zero-threshold ones, the early considerations relied on statistical physics insights. Unquestionably of greatest importance in that direction is the Gardner’s seminal work,
[
18
]
, which paved the way for many of the very best perceptrons’ analytical results that to this day stand as absolute state of the art. Namely,
[
18
]
and a follow-up
[
19
]
, recognized and reaffirmed the role of a
statistical approach
and consequently successfully adapted the, already well established statistical physics, replica approach so that it can treat almost any feature of various perceptrons models. These included accurate predictions/approximations for their storage capacities in several different scenarios: positive/negative thresholds, correlated/uncorrelated patterns, patterns stored incorrectly and many others. Almost all of the predictions obtained in
[
18
,
19
]
were later on (in identical or similar statistical contexts) established rigorously as mathematical facts (see, e.g.,
[
29
,
30
,
48
,
50
,
49
,
36
,
40
,
42
]
). These typically related to the above mentioned spherical perceptrons. In particular, in
[
29
,
30
]
, the authors confirmed the predictions made in
[
18
]
related to the storage capacity of the
positive
spherical perceptrons. Moreover, the prediction related to the volume of the bond strengths that satisfies the perceptron dynamics was also confirmed as fully correct. Later on, in
[
48
,
50
,
49
]
Talagrand reconfirmed these predictions through a related but somewhat different approach. In
[
36
]
, a completely different, random duality theory (RDT) based, framework was introduced and, again, used to confirm almost all of the predictions from
[
18
]
, including many previously not considered in
[
29
,
30
,
48
,
50
,
49
]
. An underlying
convexity
provided a substantial help in all of these, mathematically rigorous, treatments.

1.1
Hard cases — deviations from strong deterministic duality and convexity

As one starts facing scenarios where the strong deterministic duality does not hold, the above mathematically rigorous confirmations may become less effective. However, their power is still significant and, in almost all known instances, hard to beat. A prototypical such a scenario happens when the above positive spherical perceptrons transform into the
negative
counterparts. This happens as soon as the perceptrons’ thresholds move from
\kappa\geq 0
to
\kappa<0
. The underlying deterministic strong duality is lost and obtaining accurate capacity characterizations becomes notoriously hard. Still, quite a lot of progress has been made when facing scenarios like this. In particular,
[
40
]
ensured that the RDT power remains useful and proved the Talagrand’s conjecture from
[

,
50
,
49
]
that the
[
18
]
’s results related to the
negative
spherical perceptrons are also rigorous upper bounds. Utilizing a
lifted
RDT approach,
[
40
]
then continued even further and established that, in certain range of problem parameters, these rigorous bounds can even be lowered (for more on the ensuing, algorithmic and replica based negative spherical perceptrons results, see excellent references
[
14
,
12
,
13
,
15
,
16
,

[

[

,
19

Many other, so to say, “

” types of perceptrons have been intensively studied over the last several decades as well. Among the most challenging ones turned out to be the

perceptrons. An introductory replica based treatment of a subclass of such perceptrons, the so-called binary
\pm 1
perceptrons, was already given in foundational papers

,
19
]

As a result it was hinted that a more advanced version of the framework from

[
18
,
19

Many other great works are closely related as well and study various different relevant aspects. We mention some of them. In
[

[

). Also, various different classes of binary perceptrons are of interest as well. Particularly related are the so-called

We here first recognize the connection between the statistical perceptron problems and, in general, the so-called

2
Binary perceptrons as random feasibility problems (rfps)

We start with the following standard mathematical formulation of the
feasibility
problems with linear inequalities

Feasibility problem
\mathbf{\mathcal{F}}(G,{\bf b},{\mathcal{X}},\alpha)
:

find

\displaystyle{\bf x}

subject to

\displaystyle G{\bf x}\geq{\bf b}

(1)

\displaystyle{\bf x}\in{\mathcal{X}},

where
G\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
,
{\bf b}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times 1}
,
{\mathcal{X}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, and
\alpha=\frac{m}{n}
. As recognized in
[
36
,
42
,
40
,
37
]
, the above formulation is precisely the same one that describes the simplest perceptrons. The perceptron’s type is determined based on matrix
G
, vector
{\bf b}
, and set
{\mathcal{X}}
. In particular, for

{\mathcal{X}}=\{-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\}^{b}
(i.e., for
{\mathcal{X}}
being the corners of the
n
-dimensional unit norm hypercube), we obtain the so-called binary
\pm 1
perceptron with variable thresholds. For
{\bf b}=\kappa{\bf 1}
, where
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
{\bf 1}
(here and throughout the paper) is a vector of all ones of appropriate dimensions, one further obtains the binary
\pm 1
perceptron with
fixed
,
\kappa
, threshold. Moreover, for a generic
G
, we have a deterministic perceptron whereas for a random
G
a statistical one. Throughout the paper, we consider the so called Gaussian perceptrons where components of
G
are i.i.d. standard normal random variables. This makes the presentation much neater and easier to follow. We however, mention that all of our results can easily be adapted to fit, in place of the standard normal, pretty much any other distribution that can be pushed through the Lindenberg variant of the central limit theorem.

One can rewrite the feasibility problem from (
1
) as the following optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{{\bf x}}

\displaystyle f({\bf x})

subject to

\displaystyle G{\bf x}\geq{\bf b}

(2)

\displaystyle{\bf x}\in{\mathcal{X}},

where an artificial function
f({\bf x}):{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is introduced. Clearly, the above problem will be solvable only if it is actually feasible. Let us for a moment pretend that this is indeed the case. Then (
2
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\xi_{feas}^{(0)}(f,{\mathcal{X}})=\min_{{\bf x}\in{\mathcal{X}}}\max_{{\bf y}\in{\mathcal{Y}}_{+}}\left(f({\bf x})-{\bf y}^{T}G{\bf x}+{\bf y}^{T}{\bf b}\right),

(3)

where set
{\mathcal{Y}}_{+}
is a collection of
{\bf y}
such that
{\bf y}_{i}\geq 0,1\leq i\leq m
. Now, specializing back to
f({\bf x})=0
, one finds

\displaystyle\xi_{feas}^{(0)}(0,{\mathcal{X}})=\min_{{\bf x}\in{\mathcal{X}}}\max_{{\bf y}\in{\mathcal{Y}}_{+}}\left(-{\bf y}^{T}G{\bf x}+{\bf y}^{T}{\bf b}\right),

(4)

and observes that if there is an
{\bf x}
such that
G{\bf x}\geq{\bf b}
, i.e., such that (
1
) is feasible, then the best that the inner maximization can do is make
\xi_{feas}^{(0)}(0,{\mathcal{X}})=0
. On the other hand, if there is no such an
{\bf x}
, then at least one of the inequalities in
G{\bf x}\geq{\bf b}
is not satisfied and the inner maximization can trivially make
\xi_{feas}^{(0)}(0,{\mathcal{X}})=\infty
. It is not that difficult to see that from the feasibility point of view,
\xi_{feas}^{(0)}(0,{\mathcal{X}})=\infty
and
\xi_{feas}^{(0)}(0,{\mathcal{X}})>0
are equivalent (i.e., the problem is structurally insensitive with respect to
{\bf y}
scaling). One can therefore restrict to
\|{\bf y}\|_{2}=1
and ensure that
\xi_{feas}^{(0)}(0,{\mathcal{X}})
remains bounded. From (
4
), it then easily follows that determining

\displaystyle\xi_{feas}(0,{\mathcal{X}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{{\bf x}\in{\mathcal{X}}}\max_{{\bf y}\in{\mathcal{Y}}_{+},\|{\bf y}\|_{2}=1}\left(-{\bf y}^{T}G{\bf x}+{\bf y}^{T}{\bf b}\right)=\min_{{\bf x}\in\{-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\}}\max_{{\bf y}\in{\mathbb{S}}_{+}^{m}}\left(-{\bf y}^{T}G{\bf x}+\kappa{\bf y}^{T}{\bf 1}\right),

(5)

with
{\mathbb{S}}_{+}^{m}
being the positive orthant part of the
m
-dimensional unit sphere,
is of critical importance in characterizing the rfps from (
1
). Clearly, and as recognized in
[
36
,
42
,
40
,
37
]
, the positivity and non-positivity of the objective value in (
5
) (i.e., of
\xi_{feas}(f,{\mathcal{X}})
) ensure the infeasibility and feasibility of (
1
), respectively. As random
feasibility
problems (rfps) (for example, the one from (
1
)) are directly connected to perceptrons, handling their random
optimization
problems counterparts (for example, the one from (
5
)) is then of critical importance in determining various perceptrons’ features. Of our interest here is, of course, the
capacity
of the perceptrons when used as storage memories or classifiers. In a large dimensional statistical context, such a capacity is defined as follows

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{m}{n}

\displaystyle\alpha_{c}(\kappa)

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle\max\{\alpha|\hskip 5.78172pt\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{P}}_{G}\left(\xi_{perc}(0,{\mathcal{X}})\triangleq\xi_{feas}(0,{\mathcal{X}})>0\right)\longrightarrow 1\}

(6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max\{\alpha|\hskip 5.78172pt\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{P}}_{G}\left({\mathcal{F}}(G,{\bf b},{\mathcal{X}},\alpha)\hskip 5.05942pt\mbox{is feasible}\right)\longrightarrow 1\}.

For the completeness, we also mention that the corresponding deterministic capacity variant is defined exactly as above with
{\mathbb{P}}_{G}
removed. Throughout the paper, the subscript next to
{\mathbb{P}}
(and later on
{\mathbb{E}}
) denotes the randomness with respect to which the statistical evaluation is taken. On occasion, when this is clear from the contexts, the subscripts are left out.

2.1
Random feasibility problems as free energy instances

We start by introducing the so-called Hamiltonian

{\cal H}_{sq}(G)={\bf y}^{T}G{\bf x},

(7)

and the corresponding partition function

Z_{sq}(\beta,G)=\sum_{{\bf x}\in{\mathcal{X}}}\left(\sum_{{\bf y}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}e^{\beta{\cal H}_{sq}(G)}\right)^{-1},

(8)

where, for the overall generality, we take
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
as general sets (later on, we specialize to particular sets of our interest,
{\mathcal{X}}=\{-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\}^{n}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}={\mathbb{S}}_{+}^{m}
). The corresponding variant of the thermodynamic limit (average “

\displaystyle f_{sq}(\beta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{G}\log{(Z_{sq}(\beta,G)})}{\beta\sqrt{n}}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{G}\log\left(\sum_{{\bf x}\in{\mathcal{X}}}\left(\sum_{{\bf y}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}e^{\beta{\cal H}_{sq}(G)}\right)^{-1}\right)}{\beta\sqrt{n}}

(9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{G}\log\left(\sum_{{\bf x}\in{\mathcal{X}}}\left(\sum_{{\bf y}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}e^{\beta{\bf y}^{T}G{\bf x})}\right)^{-1}\right)}{\beta\sqrt{n}},

and its corresponding ground state special case

\displaystyle f_{sq}(\infty)\triangleq\lim_{\beta\rightarrow\infty}f_{sq}(\beta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\beta,n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{G}\log{(Z_{sq}(\beta,G)})}{\beta\sqrt{n}}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{G}\max_{{\bf x}\in{\mathcal{X}}}-\max_{{\bf y}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}{\bf y}^{T}G{\bf x}}{\sqrt{n}}

(10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{G}\min_{{\bf x}\in{\mathcal{X}}}\max_{{\bf y}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}{\bf y}^{T}G{\bf x}}{\sqrt{n}}.

Assuming that the components of
G
are i.i.d. standard normals and given their sign symmetry we also have

\displaystyle-f_{sq}(\infty)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{G}\min_{{\bf x}\in{\mathcal{X}}}\max_{{\bf y}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}{\bf y}^{T}G{\bf x}}{\sqrt{n}}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{G}\min_{{\bf x}\in{\mathcal{X}}}\max_{{\bf y}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}-{\bf y}^{T}G{\bf x}}{\sqrt{n}}.

(11)

One now observes that
f_{sq}(\infty)
is very tightly connected to
\xi_{feas}(0,{\mathcal{X}})
given in (
5
). Understanding
f_{sq}(\infty)
is therefore critically important. However, studying
f_{sq}(\infty)
directly is usually very hard. Instead, we rely on studying
f_{sq}(\beta)
, i.e., on studying the free energy defined for a general
\beta
. The results of such a generic studying are eventually specialized to the so-called ground state behavior,
\beta\rightarrow\infty
. Consequently, the results presented below easily account for any
\beta
. However, in the interest of easing the exposition, some terms of no significance in the ground state regime are, on occasion, neglected.
