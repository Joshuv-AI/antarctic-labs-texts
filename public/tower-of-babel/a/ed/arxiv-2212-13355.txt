Two Modes of Nonmonotonic Consequence † Thanks: † This is the text of my speech at the Logica Universalis webinar, which took place on May 11, 2022.
Alexei Muravitsky

Abstract
. We discuss two ways to implement a semantic approach to nonmonotonic consequence relations in an arbitrary propositional language. For one particular language, we also discuss the proof-theoretic framework that we connect with this semantic approach. This article is an addition to [ Muravitsky, 2021 ] .

What do we know and how do we know about nonmonotonic consequences?

2.
Logicality of (logical) consequences

First of all, we must explain the title of this section.

Even in the case of monotonic reasoning, if the answer to the

what

A logical inference is a movement from certain propositions (the premises) to a further proposition (the conclusion). We still need to know, however, which of these movements are to be called logical. (
[

The second point of view sounds from the corner of proof theory.

If we simply axiomatize or define the notion of logical consequence with the understanding that a logical consequence holds when this follows logically (!) from the axioms or the definitions, then one may rightly say whether anything really is achieved. (
[

[\ldots]

Definition 3.1

Lemma 3.1

(cf.
[

Given

\mathcal{V}_{0}:=\mathcal{V}(X)\cap\mathcal{V}(\alpha)

\mathbf{M}

X\models^{r}_{\mathbf{M}}\alpha~\Longleftrightarrow~(\text{for any $v_{\mathcal{V}_{0}}$ in $\mathbf{M}$},~v_{\mathcal{V}_{0}}\rightsquigarrow_{\mathbf{M}}X~\Longrightarrow~v_{\mathcal{V}_{0}}\rightsquigarrow_{\mathbf{M}}\alpha).

(16)

Proposition 3.2
.

Any operator
\mathbf{C}_{\mathcal{M}}^{r}
is logical relative to

\mathbf{Cn}_{\mathcal{M}}
.

Proof.

To prove the statement, we have to verify the conditions (log-1) and (log-2) for
\mathbf{Cn}_{\mathcal{M}}
and
\mathbf{C}_{\mathcal{M}}^{r}
. It must be clear that for this, it suffices to verify (log-1) and (log-2) for
\mathbf{Cn}_{\mathbf{M}}
and
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{M}}^{r}
, where
\mathbf{M}\in\mathcal{M}
.

Let us fix such
\mathbf{M}=\langle\mathbf{A},D\rangle
.

The condition (log-1), which is
\mathbf{Cn}_{\mathbf{M}}\leq\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{M}}^{r}
, obviously is true.

To prove (log-2), we use its form (
9
), that is,

X\models^{r}_{\mathbf{M}}\alpha~\text{and}~\alpha\models_{\mathbf{M}}\beta~\text{imply}~X\models^{r}_{\mathbf{M}}\beta.

Intending to apply (
16
), we denote
\mathcal{V}_{0}=\mathcal{V}(X)\cap\mathcal{V}(\beta)
and assume that
v_{\mathcal{V}_{0}}\rightsquigarrow_{\mathbf{M}}X
.

This implies that there is a restricted valuation
v\geq v_{\mathcal{V}_{0}}
such that
\mathbf{dom}(v)=\mathcal{V}(X)
and
v[X]\subseteq D
. In virtue of the first assumption, there is a valuation
v^{\prime}\geq v
such that
\mathbf{dom}(v^{\prime})=\mathcal{V}(X)\cup\mathcal{V}(\alpha)
and
v^{\prime}[X\cup\{\alpha\}]\subseteq D
. Now, given an arbitrary

, we define a valuation

as follows:

w[q]:=\begin{cases}\begin{array}[]{cl}v^{\prime}[p]&\text{if $p\in\mathcal{V}(X)\cup\mathcal{V}(\alpha)$}\\
a&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\end{cases}

It is obvious that

validates
\alpha
and hence, in virtue of the second assumption,
w
also validates
\beta

v_{\mathcal{V}_{0}}\leq w
, we conclude that
v_{\mathcal{V}_{0}}\rightsquigarrow_{\mathbf{M}}\beta
. It remains to apply Lemma
3.1
.
∎

The following properties of
\mathcal{M}
-r-consequence were established earlier.

Proposition 3.3

(
[

]

Any
\mathcal{M}
-r-consequence is weakly monotonic.

Proposition 3.4

(

If

In general, an operator
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{M}}^{r}
can fail (con-2), and the corresponding relation
\models^{r}_{\mathbf{M}}
fails the transitivity property:

X\models^{r}_{\mathbf{M}}\beta,~\text{for every $\beta\in Y$, and $Y\cup Z\vdash_{\mathbf{C}}\alpha$ imply $X\cup Z\models^{r}_{\mathbf{M}}\alpha$},

which is equivalent to the property:

Y\subseteq\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{M}}^{r}(X)~\text{implies}~\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{M}}^{r}(Y\cup Z)\subseteq\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{M}}^{r}(X\cup Z).

(17)

Example 3.1
.

Let
\mathbf{A}=\langle\textsf{A},\land,\lor,\neg,\bm{1}\rangle
be a nontrivial Boolean algebra with a unit
\bm{1}
, and let
\mathbf{M}=\langle\mathbf{A},\{\bm{1}\}\rangle
. Then, although
p\models^{r}_{\mathbf{M}}\neg q
, we observe that
p,q\not\models^{r}_{\mathbf{M}}\neg q
; and although
p\models^{r}_{\mathbf{M}}q
and
q\models^{r}_{\mathbf{M}}\neg p
, we observe that
p\not\models^{r}_{\mathbf{M}}\neg p
.

We note that (
17
) is a generalized form of (con-5). The property (con-6) also fails. Indeed, consider inclusion
\{p\}\subseteq\{p,q\}
. Although
\{p,q\}\subseteq\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{M}}^{r}(p)
,
\neg q\in\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{M}}^{r}(p)\setminus\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{M}}^{r}(\{p,q\})
.

In virtue of Proposition
2.1
-iv, (con-7) also fails.

Thus all cumulative properties can fail for some operators
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{M}}^{r}
, even when the matrix
\mathbf{M}
is finite, while for all
\mathbf{M}
,
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{M}}^{r}
is weakly monotonic, and for all finite
\mathbf{M}
,
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{M}}^{r}
is very strongly finitary.

Now if for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, for some
Z
with

Y\subseteq Z
and
\mathcal{V}^{\ast}_{\mathbf{\mathbf{M}_{i}}}(Z)\cap\mathcal{V}(\alpha)=\mathcal{V}^{\ast}_{\mathbf{\mathbf{M}_{i}}}(Y)\cap\mathcal{V}(\alpha)
, then, according to Proposition
3.14
,
Z\models^{r\ast}_{\mathbf{M}_{i}}\alpha
for each
\mathbf{M}_{i}\in\mathcal{M}
, that is,
Z\models^{r\ast}_{\mathcal{M}}\alpha
.
∎

Corollary 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a finite family of finite matrices.
Then
\mathcal{M}
-r
\ast
-consequence is strongly finitary.

4.
Substitution

According to Proposition
3.1
, any
\mathbf{Cn}_{\mathbf{M}}
operator is structural. In terms of consequence relations, this means that, given a matrix
\mathbf{M}
, for any substitution
\sigma
,

X\models_{\mathbf{M}}\alpha~\Longrightarrow~\sigma(X)\models_{\mathbf{M}}\sigma(\alpha).

The structurality property can hardly be expected for
\models^{r}_{\mathbf{M}}
or
\models^{r\ast}_{\mathbf{M}}
relations. However, the following rule can be considered for these relations, although not without some restriction.

X\models^{r}_{\mathbf{M}}\sigma(\alpha)~\Longrightarrow~X\models^{r}_{\mathbf{M}}\alpha,

(23)

providing that
\mathcal{V}(X)\cap\mathcal{V}(\alpha)=\varnothing
. (In Section
5.2
this property is formulated as the
rule of reverse substitution
.)

It is obvious that (
23
) is true.
Indeed, the restriction ‘
\mathcal{V}(X)\cap\mathcal{V}(\alpha)=\varnothing
’ allows us to reduce (
23
) to the implication: for any valuation
v
in
\mathbf{M}
, if
v
validates
\sigma(\alpha)
, then
v\circ\sigma
validates
\alpha
. On the other hand, if we drop the restriction, we face with the problem that when a valuation
v
validates
X
and its extension
w
validates
\sigma(\alpha)
, the valuation
w\circ\sigma
, which validates
\alpha
, is not necessarily an extension of
v
.

It is also true that (
23
) can be extended to the following:

X\models^{r}_{\mathcal{M}}\sigma(\alpha)~\Longrightarrow~X\models^{r}_{\mathcal{M}}\alpha

and

X\models^{r\ast}_{\mathcal{M}}\sigma(\alpha)~\Longrightarrow~X\models^{r\ast}_{\mathcal{M}}\alpha,

providing that
\mathcal{V}(X)\cap\mathcal{V}(\alpha)=\varnothing
.

The last implication can obviously be reduced to the case when
\mathcal{M}
consists of a single matrix:

X\models^{r\ast}_{\mathbf{M}}\sigma(\alpha)~\Longrightarrow~X\models^{r\ast}_{\mathbf{M}}\alpha,

where
\mathcal{V}(X)\cap\mathcal{V}(\alpha)=\varnothing
.

Indeed, according to (
22
), the last implication is equivalent to the following:

X_{\mathbf{c}_{\mathbf{M}}}\models^{r}_{\mathbf{M}}\sigma(\alpha)~\Longleftrightarrow~X_{\mathbf{c}_{\mathbf{M}}}\models^{r}_{\mathbf{M}}\alpha.

It remains to notice that
\mathcal{V}(X_{\mathbf{c}_{\mathbf{M}}})\cap\mathcal{V}(\alpha)=\varnothing
whenever
\mathcal{V}(X)\cap\mathcal{V}(\alpha)=\varnothing
.

5.
Logical friendliness relation and beyond

So far, the specifics of objective language have not been used in our discussion to achieve positive results, except that the language was sentential. But to get negative results, we had to turn to specific languages. However, specifying an objective language can also open up more possibilities for defining nonmonotonic consequence relations.

For the rest of this paper, we will use the language of Example
3.1
, that is a language with the logical connectives
\land
,
\lor
,
\rightarrow
,
\neg
and the constant
\top
. We denote this formal language by
\mathcal{L}^{\circ}
. Unspecified formulas of
\mathcal{L}^{\circ}
we denote by letters
A
,
B
, etc., and sets of such formulas by letters
\Gamma
,
\Delta
, etc. We continue using the same notation for substitutions and valuations. Since all our matrices will be Heyting algebras with a greatest element
\bm{1}
, for each valuation
v
, we require the condition:
v[\top]=\bm{1}
.

5.1.
Logical friendliness

Our interest to nonmonotonic relation was inspired by
[
Makinson, 2005b
]
and
[
Makinson, 2007
]
, where the relation of
logical friendliness
was introduced.
20
20

20

Makinson has proved that logical friendliness is strongly finitary with some additional property for monotonicity, which is however weaker than the one formulated in Proposition 5.1 of
[
Muravitsky, 2021
]
.
To define logical friendliness, we use the matrix
\mathbf{B}_{2}
which is a two-element Boolean algebra with the filter
\{\bm{1}\}
; namely

\Gamma\mid\!\sim_{F}A~\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{df}}}{{\Longleftrightarrow}}~\Gamma\models_{\mathbf{B}_{2}}^{r}A.

(24)

Further, we define:

\Gamma\mid\!\sim_{F^{\ast}}A~\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{df}}}{{\Longleftrightarrow}}~\Gamma\models_{\mathbf{B}_{2}}^{r\ast}A.

(25)

We denote the operators corresponding to
\mid\!\sim_{F}
and
\mid\!\sim_{F^{\ast}}
by
\mathbf{C}_{F}
and
\mathbf{C}_{F^{\ast}}
, respectively.

In view of Example
3.1
and Example
3.2
, we observe:

\mathbf{Cn}_{\mathbf{B}_{2}}<\mathbf{C}_{F}<\mathbf{C}_{F^{\ast}}.

(26)

The following proposition later will play the role of a navigator, but it is convenient to discuss this property here. It reads that for defining logical friendliness, we can use any nontrivial Boolean algebra.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\mathbf{B}
be a nontrivial Boolean algebra that is in combination with a logical filter
\{\bm{1}\}
forms a logical matrix
\mathbf{B}
. Then

\Gamma\models_{\mathbf{B}}^{r}A~\Longleftrightarrow~\Gamma\models_{\mathbf{B}_{2}}^{r}A.

(27)

Proof.

We recall that
\mathbf{B}
(understood as an algebra, not matrix) is a subdirect product
\mathbf{B}_{2}^{I}
(the Cartesian product of copies of
\mathbf{B}_{2}
), where
\mathbf{B}_{2}
is also regarded here as an algebra. This means that
\mathbf{B}
is embedded to
\mathbf{B}_{2}^{I}
so that each projection,
g_{i}
, is an epimorphism. We also recall that each valuation in an algebra is a homomorphism from the corresponding formula algebra to the former.

Now assume that
\Gamma\models_{\mathbf{B}}^{r}A
and consider a valuation
v
in
\mathbf{B}_{2}
such that
v[\Gamma]\subseteq\{\bm{1}\}
and
\mathbf{dom}(v)=\mathcal{V}(\Gamma)
.

We define a valuation
\hat{v}
in
\mathbf{B}
as follows.

\hat{v}[p]:=\begin{cases}\begin{array}[]{cl}\bm{0}&\text{if $v[p]=\bm{0}$},\\
\bm{1}&\text{if $v[p]=\bm{1}$},~\text{for every $p\in\mathcal{V}(\Gamma)$}.\end{array}\end{cases}

It is clear that
\hat{v}[\Gamma]\subseteq\{(\bm{1})_{I}\}
. Therefore, by premise, there is an extension
w\geq\hat{v}
such that

w[\Gamma\cup\{\alpha\}]\subseteq\{(\bm{1})_{I}\}
. Now,
v^{\ast}:=g_{i}\circ w
is a valuation in
\mathbf{B}_{2}
such that
v^{\ast}\geq v
and
v^{\ast}[\Gamma\cup\{A\}]\subseteq\{\bm{1}\}
.

Next assume that
\Gamma\models_{\mathbf{B}_{2}}A
and that a valuation
w
in
\mathbf{B}
validates
\Gamma
, that is,
w[\Gamma]\subseteq\{(\bm{1})_{I}\}
. Then, obviously, valuation
v:=g_{i}\circ w
validates
\Gamma
in
\mathbf{B}_{2}
, that is,
v[\Gamma]\subseteq\{\bm{1}\}
. Then there is an extension
v^{\prime}\geq v
such that
v^{\prime}[\Gamma\cup\{A\}]\subseteq\{\bm{1}\}
. Now we define a valuation
w^{\prime}
in
\mathbf{B}
(regarded as a subalgebra of
\mathbf{B}_{2}^{I}
) according to the rule:
(w^{\prime}[p])_{i}:=v^{\prime}[p]
. It is clear that
w^{\prime}\geq w
and
w^{\prime}[\Gamma\cup\{A\}]\subseteq\{(\bm{1})_{I}\}
.
∎

5.2.
Beyond logical friendliness

We aim to make logical friendliness the starting point for getting more logical nonmonotonic consequences. This time we choose the method of proof theory.

Let
Int
and
Cl
denote the classes of intuitionistic and classical propositional tautologies, respectively. An
intermediate logic
is a set
L
of formulas, satisfying the following conditions:
\textsf{Int}\subseteq L\subseteq\textsf{Cl}
,
L
is closed under uniform substitution and under modus ponens. The class of all intermediate logics is denoted by
\mathbf{ExtInt}
.

Let
L\in\mathbf{ExtInt}
. A finite sequence

A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{n}

is called an
L
-
derivation from a set

\Gamma
if for each
A_{i}
, either
A_{i}\in L
or
A_{i}\in\Gamma
or
A_{i}
can be obtained by modus ponens from two preceding formulas of the sequence. In addition, we say that the sequence is an
L
-derivation of the last formula of the sequence, that is, of
A_{n}
.

Next, we define:

\Gamma\vdash_{L}A~\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{df}}}{{\Longleftrightarrow}}~\text{there is an $L$-derivation of $A$ from $\Gamma$}.

Each relation
\vdash_{L}
obviously is a monotonic consequence relations. We denote the corresponding consequence operator by
\mathbf{Cn}_{L}
.

We call a set
\Gamma
a
\mathbf{Cn}_{L}
-
theory
if
\mathbf{Cn}_{L}(\Gamma)=\Gamma
. (This concept will be employed later.)

We call any expression of the form
\Gamma\!\Rightarrow\!A
a
sequent
.

Next, we provide the list of
sequential

L
-
axioms
(or
sL
-
axioms
for short) and that of
sequential

L
-
rules
(or
sL
-
rules
for short).

s
L
-
Axioms
:

axiom 1:

\!\Rightarrow\!p
, for any
p\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{L}^{\circ}}
;

axiom 2:

\Gamma\!\Rightarrow\!\top
;

axiom 3:

\Gamma\!\Rightarrow\!\bigwedge\Delta
if
\Delta\Subset\Gamma
(
\bigwedge\Delta:=\top
if
\Delta=\varnothing
);

axiom 4:

\Gamma\!\Rightarrow\!A
whenever
\Gamma\vdash_{L}A
.

s
L
-
Rules
:

rule 1:

\dfrac{\!\Rightarrow\!A}{\Gamma\!\Rightarrow\!A}
, providing that

\mathcal{V}(\Gamma)\cap\mathcal{V}(A)=\varnothing
;

rule 2:

\dfrac{\Gamma,A\!\Rightarrow\!C~\text{and}~\Delta,B\!\Rightarrow\!C}{\Gamma\cup\Delta,A\lor B\!\Rightarrow\!C}
,
providing that
\mathcal{V}(\Gamma\cup\{A\})=\mathcal{V}(\Delta\cup\{B\})
;

rule 3:

\dfrac{\Gamma\!\Rightarrow\!\sigma(A)}{\Gamma\!\Rightarrow\!A},

providing that
\mathcal{V}(\Gamma)\cap\mathcal{V}(A)=\varnothing
; (Reverse substitution)

rule 4:

\dfrac{\Gamma\!\Rightarrow\!A,~\text{and}~A\!\Rightarrow\!B}{\Gamma\!\Rightarrow\!B},

providing that either
\mathcal{V}(\Gamma)\subseteq\mathcal{V}(A)
or

\mathcal{V}(\Gamma)\cap\mathcal{V}(B)\subseteq\mathcal{V}(A)\subseteq\mathcal{V}(\Gamma)
; (Cut)

rule 5:

\dfrac{\Gamma,A\!\Rightarrow\!B~\text{and}~C\!\Rightarrow\!A}{\Gamma,C\!\Rightarrow\!B},

providing that
\mathcal{V}(C)\subseteq\mathcal{V}(\Gamma\cup\{A\})
;

(Deductive replacement in antecedent)

rule 6:

\dfrac{\Gamma\!\Rightarrow\!A~\text{and}~A\!\Rightarrow\!B}{\Gamma\!\Rightarrow\!B}
. (Deductive replacement in consequent)

A finite nonempty list

\Gamma_{1}\!\Rightarrow\!A_{1},\ldots,\Gamma_{n}\!\Rightarrow\!A_{n}

is called an
sL
-
derivation
(of the last sequent on the list, i.
\Gamma_{n}\!\Rightarrow\!A_{n}
) if each
\Gamma_{k}\!\Rightarrow\!A_{k}
is either a s
L
-axiom or can be obtained from preceding sequents by one of the rules 1–6. We say that

\Gamma\!\Rightarrow\!A

holds
in s
L
-calculus
if there is an s
L
-derivation ending with
\Gamma\!\Rightarrow\!A
.

This leads to the following definition: for each
l\in\mathbf{ExtInt}
,

\Gamma\Vdash_{L}A~\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{df}}}{{\Longleftrightarrow}}~\Gamma\!\Rightarrow_{L}\!A~\text{holds in s$l$-calculus}.

(28)

The motivation for formulating the s
L
-calculi is based on the following observation.

\Gamma\mid\!\sim_{F}A~\Longleftrightarrow~\Gamma\Vdash_{\textsf{Cl}}A~(\text{cf.~\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{muravitsky2009}{}{}]}, theorems 4.5 and 4.8}).

Reformulating the last equivalence, we obtain completeness theorem for the nonmonotonic consequence
\Vdash_{\textsf{Cl}}
:

\Gamma\Vdash_{\textsf{Cl}}A~\Longleftrightarrow~\Gamma\models_{\mathbf{B}_{2}}^{r}A.

This suggests that there may be similar completeness theorems for other
\Vdash_{L}
relations. We will come back to this later, but now we will discuss what is interesting about the
\Vdash_{L}
relations.

According to
[
Muravitsky, 2021
]
, proposition 7.3, each relation
\Vdash_{L}
is reflexive and nonmonotonic. Further, for any nonempty set
\Gamma
and any formula
A
with
\mathcal{V}(\Gamma)\cap\mathcal{V}(A)=\varnothing
, if
\Gamma\Vdash_{L}A
, then there is a nonempty set
\Delta\Subset\Gamma
such that

\Delta\Vdash_{L}A
. To this, we add the following.

Proposition 5.2
.

Each
\Vdash_{L}
is logical relative to
\vdash_{L}
.

Proof.

Indeed, suppose
\Gamma\vdash_{L}A
. Then, by axiom 4,
\Gamma\!\Rightarrow_{L}\!A
holds, that is,
\Gamma\Vdash_{L}A
.

Next, assume that
\Gamma\Vdash_{L}A
and
A\vdash_{L}B
. The first assumption implies that there is an s
L
-derivation of the s
L
-sequent
\Gamma\!\Rightarrow_{L}\!A
.
Then we apply rule 6. Thus there is an s
L
-derivation of the s
L
-sequent
\Gamma\!\Rightarrow_{L}\!B
, that is,
\Gamma\Vdash_{L}B
.
∎

Now let us return to the question of the possibility of the completeness theorem for each relation
\Vdash_{L}
.

We recall that, in the terminology of
[
Citkin and Muravitsky, 2022
]
, each
\vdash_{L}
is a

.

For definitions, the reader is offered to consult

For a fixed

\mathbf{LT}_{L}[D]

In the case

By proposition 6.3.5 of

This allows us to say that each

-consequence, where

\mathcal{M}_{L}:=\{\mathbf{LT}_{L}[D]~:~D\in\Sigma_{L}\}.

In the case

\Gamma\vdash_{\textsf{Cl}}A~\Longleftrightarrow~\Gamma\models_{\mathbf{LT}_{\textsf{Cl}}[D]}A~\text{for every $D\in\Sigma_{\textsf{Cl}}$}.

Now, when we move from
\mathcal{M}_{\textsf{Cl}}
consequence to

\mathcal{M}_{\textsf{Cl}}
-r-consequence, in virtue of Proposition

, we obtain the conclusion: relation
\mid\!\sim_{F}
is the
\mathcal{M}_{\textsf{Cl}}
-r-consequence. This suggests the following.

Conjecture 1
.

Each
\Vdash_{L}
is the
\mathcal{M}_{L}
-r-consequence.

Of course, from
\mathcal{M}_{L}
-r-consequence, we can move to
\mathcal{M}_{L}
-r
\ast

\Gamma\Vdash_{L^{\ast}}A~\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{df}}}{{\Longleftrightarrow}}~\Gamma_{\mathbf{c}}\Vdash_{L}A,

\Gamma_{\mathbf{c}}:=\{B_{\mathbf{c}_{\mathbf{LT}_{L}[X]}}~:~B\in\Gamma\}

and each

Conjecture 2

\Gamma\Vdash_{L^{\ast}}A

6.
What has been achieved?

We started our discussion about the concept of nonmonotonic reasoning from a philosophical point of view. Popular belief says that time can render the final verdict.
But what is meant by saying this? We can suggest two possible answers.

The first is contained in the following quote from R. von Mises.

I aim at the construction of a rational theory, based on the simplest possible concepts, one which, although admittedly inadequate to represent the complexity of the real processes, is able to reproduce satisfactorily some of their essential properties. (
[
von Mises, 1981
]
, The inadequacy of theories)

The second opinion is more difficult to express.

The success of the proposed approach is often provided by unexplored space for its further development. The larger this space, the more questions left unanswered, the more tools available to answer these questions, the more likely it is that this approach will become the main focus of research. Such an approach could be termed
open
, that is, open for further development.

This view of mine echoes another statement by von Mises.

[\ldots]
the value of a concept is not gauged by its correspondence with some usual group of notions, but only by its usefulness for further scientific development
[\ldots]
(
[
von Mises, 1981
]
, Synthetic definitions)

To be open in the above sense means not to be sterile in the understanding of G. Kreisel, who wrote,

Aristotle gives much attention to the matter of
choice

of abstractions. This indicates a shift of emphasis away from matters of principle (e.g. mere validity of analogies) to a focus (on, of course, valid cases) which provides safeguards against
sterility
(not merely against straight error). (
[
Kreisel, 2019
]
, Choice of abstractions)

Whether a (consistent) approach is open enough to avoid sterility can only be judged by time.

In a broader context, concerning the
what
and
how
questions, the difficulty of the task should not overshadow the more important level associated with the act of knowing. This thought is not mine, but a paraphrase of the thought of Thomas Aquinas, who wrote,

When something is more difficult, it is not for that reason necessarily more worthwhile, but it must be more difficult in such a way, as also to be at a higher level of goodness. [Read: “the act of knowing” instead of “goodness”] (Quoted from
[
Pieper, 2009
]
, chapter II.)
