Tatsuya Tate

Abstract
. The purpose of this paper is to give a direct proof of an eigenfunction expansion formula for one-dimensional 2-state quantum walks, which is an analog of that for Sturm-Liouville operators due to Weyl, Stone, Titchmarsh and Kodaira. In the context of the theory of CMV matrix it had been already established by Gesztesy-Zinchenko. Our approach is restricted to the class of quantum walks mentioned above whereas it is direct and it gives some important properties of Green functions. The properties given here enable us to give a concrete formula for a positive-matrix-valued measure, which gives directly the spectral measure, in a simplest case of the so-called two-phase model.

\begin{split}R_{\lambda}(x,x)&=-\lambda^{-1}\mathrm{z}_{L}(x)+F_{\lambda}(x)[\mathrm{x}_{0}(\lambda)+\lambda^{-1}\mathrm{z}_{L}(0)]F_{\lambda^{*}}(x)^{*}\\

&=-\lambda^{-1}\mathrm{z}_{R}(x)+F_{\lambda}(x)[\mathrm{x}_{0}(\lambda)+\lambda^{-1}\mathrm{z}_{R}(0)]F_{\lambda^{*}}(x)^{*},\end{split}

which shows

and this completes the proof.

\square

Lemma
3.2
.

Let

. Then we have

\sum_{x\geq 1}\|F_{\lambda}(x)\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}=+\infty,\quad\sum_{x\leq-1}\|F_{\lambda}(x)\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}=+\infty.

(3.11)

The Hilbert-Schmidt norm of the
2\times 2
matrix

c&d\end{bmatrix}

is given as
\|A\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}=|a|^{2}+|b|^{2}+|c|^{2}+|d|^{2}
. Lemma
3.2
could be proved
by a method in
[
16
]
. See Appendix in the paper. But, we give here another proof.

Proof.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}\setminus S^{1}
. We have the following formula.

\mathcal{C}(x)F_{\lambda}(x)=\lambda\pi_{L}F_{\lambda}(x-1)+\lambda\pi_{R}F_{\lambda}(x+1).

(3.12)

The formula
(
3.12
)
is used to prove Theorem
1.1
and is proved by using (1), (2) in Lemma
2.2
.
Since the proof of assertions for two sums in
(
3.11
)
is identical, we prove that the sum

D:=\sum_{x\geq 1}\|F_{\lambda}(x)\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}

is infinity. To prove this we assume
D<+\infty
and deduce a contradiction.
In general, if
C\in\mathrm{U}(2)
then for any
A\in\mathrm{M}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
, we have

\|CA\|_{\mathrm{HS}}=\|CA\|_{\mathrm{HS}},\quad\|A\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}=\|\pi_{L}A\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}+\|\pi_{R}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}.

(3.13)

The equation
(
3.12
)
and the first of the identities
(
3.13
)
give

\|F_{\lambda}(x)\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}=\|\mathcal{C}(x)F_{\lambda}(x)\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}=|\lambda|^{2}\|\pi_{L}F_{\lambda}(x-1)\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}+|\lambda|^{2}\|\pi_{R}F_{\lambda}(x+1)\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}.

Therefore, we have

\begin{split}D&=|\lambda|^{2}\sum_{x\geq 1}\|\pi_{L}F_{\lambda}(x-1)\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}+\|\lambda|^{2}\sum_{x\geq 1}\|\pi_{R}F_{\lambda}(x+1)\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}\\
&=|\lambda|^{2}\sum_{x\geq 0}\|\pi_{L}F_{\lambda}(x)\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}+|\lambda|^{2}\sum_{x\geq 2}\|\pi_{R}F_{\lambda}(x)\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}\\
&=|\lambda|^{2}\|\pi_{L}F_{\lambda}(0)\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}-|\lambda|^{2}\|\pi_{R}F_{\lambda}(1)\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}+|\lambda|^{2}\sum_{x\geq 1}\left(\|\pi_{L}F_{\lambda}(x)\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}+\|\pi_{R}F_{\lambda}(x)\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}\right)\\
&=|\lambda|^{2}\|\pi_{L}F_{\lambda}(0)\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}-|\lambda|^{2}\|\pi_{R}F_{\lambda}(1)\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}+|\lambda|^{2}D.\end{split}

By the facts
F_{\lambda}(0)=I
,
F_{\lambda}(1)=T_{\lambda}(0)
and the definition of

show

\|\pi_{L}F_{\lambda}(0)\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}=1,\quad\|\pi_{R}F_{\lambda}(1)\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}=|\lambda|^{-2}(|c_{0}|^{2}+|d_{0}|^{2})=|\lambda|^{-2}.

By substituting this into the above equation and by noting
|\lambda|\neq 1
by the assumption
we conclude

D=|\lambda|^{2}-1+|\lambda|^{2}D,\quad\text{hence}\quad D=-1,

which is a contradiction.
∎

\begin{split}\langle\,\mathrm{x}(\lambda)^{*}u,v\,\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}&=\langle\,u,\mathrm{x}(\lambda)v\,\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}=\overline{\langle\,\mathrm{x}(\lambda)v,u\,\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}}=\overline{\langle\,K(\lambda)\delta_{0}\otimes v,\delta_{0}\otimes u\,\rangle}=\langle\,\delta_{0}\otimes u,K(\lambda)\delta_{0}\otimes v\,\rangle\\

&\hskip 20.0pt+2|F_{L}(r\zeta)|^{2}\langle\,[{\rm Re}\,\mathrm{x}(r\zeta)]e_{L},e_{L}\,\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}.\end{split}

.
First of all, let us consider the fundamental example, namely the case where

For simplicity we assume

Thus we have to consider the zeros of

f(\zeta)=1-s-J(\zeta)Z_{-}(\zeta_{(\pm)})

(5.17)

as a function in
\zeta\in S^{1}
, where, in what follows, we sometimes write

F(\zeta_{(-)})=\lim_{r\uparrow 1}f(r\zeta),\quad F(\zeta_{(+)})=\lim_{r\uparrow 1}f(r^{-1}\zeta)\quad(\zeta\in S^{1})

for a function
F
on
\mathbb{C}\setminus S^{1}
, if the limit exists.
We set
\zeta=z+iy\in S^{1}
. For
\zeta\in S
, we see
Z_{-}=x+iw
with
w=\pm\sqrt{\rho^{2}-x^{2}}
and
thus
f(\zeta)=1-s-x^{2}-ixw
which is zero if and only if
x=\pm\rho
and
\rho^{2}=1-s
.
When
\rho^{2}=1-s
, the singularity coming from
f(\zeta)
is
1/\sqrt{\rho^{2}-x^{2}}
as will be seen below.
But this is integrable on the interval
[-\rho,\rho]
. Therefore, we take the pointwise limit
of
{\rm Re}\,\mathrm{x}(r\zeta)
to compute the matrix-valued measure
d\Sigma(\zeta)|_{S}
restricted to
S
.
When
\zeta\not\in S
, we see
Z_{-}=x+w
with
w=\pm\sqrt{x^{2}-\rho^{2}}
.
In this case
f(\zeta)
vanishes if and only if
\zeta
is the following four points,

\zeta_{*}=e^{i\theta_{*}}:=x_{*}+i\sqrt{1-x_{*}^{2}},\quad-\overline{\zeta_{*}},\quad-\zeta_{*},\quad\overline{\zeta_{*}},

(5.18)

where a positive real number
x_{*}
is given by

x_{*}=\frac{1-s}{\sqrt{2-2s-\rho^{2}}}.

It is well-known (see
[
12
]
) that these points are actually eigenvalues of
U
,
which will also be shown as a point mass of
\Sigma
later.
We have

\begin{split}\langle\,\mathrm{z}_{L}(0)e_{L},\mathrm{v}_{+}(\lambda)^{\perp}\,\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}&=\frac{\overline{\beta}\lambda}{D_{+}},\\
\langle\,\mathrm{z}_{R}(0)e_{R},\mathrm{v}_{-}(\lambda)^{\perp}\,\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}&=\frac{1}{D_{-}}(\lambda Z_{-}-\rho^{2})=\frac{Z_{-}}{D_{-}}(Z_{-}-\lambda^{-1})\end{split}

These formulas lead us to obtain

\begin{split}\lambda\mathrm{x}_{0}(\lambda)e_{L}&=\frac{1}{2[1-s-J(\lambda)Z_{-}(\lambda)]}\begin{bmatrix}\beta_{0}\overline{\beta}-1+\lambda Z_{-}(\lambda)\\[7.0pt]
-\alpha_{0}\overline{\beta}\end{bmatrix}\\[7.0pt]
\lambda\mathrm{x}_{0}(\lambda)e_{R}&=\frac{1}{2[1-s-J(\lambda)Z_{-}(\lambda)]}\begin{bmatrix}\overline{\alpha_{0}}\beta\\[7.0pt]
\overline{\beta_{0}}\beta-1+\lambda Z_{-}(\lambda)\end{bmatrix}\end{split}

(5.19)

Then we see

\begin{split}\zeta\mathrm{x}_{0}(\zeta)e_{L}&=\frac{1}{2[1-s-x^{2}-ixw]}\begin{bmatrix}s-y^{2}-yw+i(xy+xw+t)\\
-\alpha_{0}\overline{\beta}\end{bmatrix}\\[7.0pt]
\zeta\mathrm{x}_{0}(\zeta)e_{R}&=\frac{1}{2[1-s-x^{2}-ixw]}\begin{bmatrix}\overline{\alpha_{0}}\beta\\
s-y^{2}-yw+i(xy+xw-t)\end{bmatrix}\end{split}

These formulas make us to obtain the following.

d\Sigma(\zeta)|_{S}=\frac{-w}{(1-s)^{2}-[2(1-s)-\rho^{2}]x^{2}}\begin{bmatrix}[(1-s)y+tx]&-i\overline{\alpha_{0}}\beta x\\
i\alpha_{0}\overline{\beta}x&[(1-s)y-tx]\end{bmatrix}

(5.20)

Next we consider
\Sigma
on
S^{1}\setminus S
. On the
S^{1}\setminus S
, we have

\lim_{r\uparrow 1}(Z_{-}(r\zeta)-Z_{-}(r^{-1}\zeta))=0\quad(\zeta\in S^{1}\setminus S\cup\{\pm\zeta_{*},\pm\overline{\zeta_{*}}\}).

On the domains

\Omega_{+}:=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\mid{\rm Re}\,(\lambda)>0,\ \lambda\not\in S\cup i\mathbb{R}\},\quad\Omega_{-}:=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\mid{\rm Re}\,(\lambda)<0,\ \lambda\not\in S\cup i\mathbb{R}\},

the function
Z_{-}(\lambda)
is then given as

Z_{-}(\lambda)=\begin{cases}J(\lambda)+\sqrt{J(\lambda)^{2}-\rho^{2}}&\lambda\in\Omega_{+},\\
J(\lambda)-\sqrt{J(\lambda)^{2}-\rho^{2}}&\lambda\in\Omega_{-}.\end{cases}

We denote
\zeta_{o}=x_{o}+iy_{o}
one of the eigenvalues
\{\pm\zeta_{*},\pm\overline{\zeta_{*}}\}

of
U
and write
e^{i\theta_{o}}=\zeta_{o}
. For any
r\in(1/2,1)
and small
\epsilon>0
, we define

J_{r,\epsilon}=\{te^{i\theta}\mid r\leq t\leq r^{-1},\,|\theta-\theta_{o}|\leq\epsilon\},\quad A_{\epsilon}=J_{r,\epsilon}\cap S^{1}=\{e^{i\theta}\mid|\theta-\theta_{o}|\leq\epsilon\},

where
\epsilon>0
is chosen so that
J_{r,\epsilon}
is contained in one of
\Omega_{\pm}
which contains
\zeta_{o}

and
J_{r,\epsilon}
contains only
\zeta_{o}
among
\{\pm\zeta_{*},\pm\overline{\zeta_{*}}\}
.
For simplicity we set

f(\lambda)=1-s-J(\lambda)Z_{-}(\lambda).

Then
\zeta_{o}
is a zero of
f
of order
1
and there are no zeros of
f
on a neighborhood of
J_{r,\epsilon}
.
Thus, for any holomorphic function
h(\lambda)
near
J_{r,\epsilon}
, Cauchy’s formula gives

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial J_{r,\epsilon}}\frac{h(\lambda)}{f(\lambda)}\,\frac{d\lambda}{\lambda}=\frac{h(\zeta_{o})}{\zeta_{o}f^{\prime}(\zeta_{o})}=-\frac{h(\zeta_{o})}{\zeta_{o}Z_{-}(\zeta_{o})Z_{-}^{\prime}(\zeta_{o})},

(5.21)

where
\partial J_{r,\epsilon}
is the boundary of
J_{r,\epsilon}
with the counterclockwise direction.
By computing the contour integral in the right-hand side of
(
5.21
)
, we see

\lim_{r\uparrow 1}\left[\int_{A_{\epsilon}}\frac{h(r\zeta)}{f(r\zeta)}\,d\hskip-1.0pt\bar{}\,\zeta-\int_{A_{\epsilon}}\frac{h(r^{-1}\zeta)}{f(r^{-1}\zeta)}\,d\hskip-1.0pt\bar{}\,\zeta\right]=\frac{h(\zeta_{o})}{\zeta_{o}Z_{-}(\zeta_{o})Z_{-}^{\prime}(\zeta_{o})}.

(5.22)

The set of all Laurent polynomials is dense in the space of continuous functions on
A_{\epsilon}
.
Thus, the formula
(
5.22
)
still holds for any continuous function
h
on
A_{\epsilon}
.
From
(
5.22
)
we obtain, for
{\rm Re}\,(\zeta_{o})>0
,

\begin{split}\Sigma(\{\zeta_{o}\})&=\frac{1}{2\zeta_{o}Z_{-}(\zeta_{o})Z_{-}^{\prime}(\zeta_{o})}\begin{bmatrix}[\beta_{o}\overline{\beta}-1+\zeta_{o}Z_{-}(\zeta_{o})]\delta_{\zeta_{o}}&\overline{\alpha_{0}}\beta\delta_{\zeta_{o}}\\
-\alpha_{0}\overline{\beta}\delta_{\zeta_{o}}&[\overline{\beta_{o}}\beta-1+\zeta_{o}Z_{-}(\zeta_{o})]\delta_{\zeta_{o}}\end{bmatrix}\\
&=\frac{x_{o}\sqrt{x_{o}^{2}-\rho^{2}}}{2y_{o}(1-s)^{2}}\begin{bmatrix}[(1-s)y_{o}+tx_{o}]\delta_{\zeta_{o}}&-i\overline{\alpha_{0}}\beta x_{o}\delta_{\zeta_{o}}\\
i\alpha_{0}\overline{\beta}x_{o}&[(1-s)y_{o}-tx_{o}]\delta_{\zeta_{o}}\end{bmatrix}\end{split}

(5.23)

where
\delta_{\zeta_{o}}
is the delta measure at the point
\zeta_{o}
, and for
{\rm Re}\,(\zeta_{o})<0
,

\Sigma(\{\zeta_{o}\})=-\frac{x_{o}\sqrt{x_{o}^{2}-\rho^{2}}}{2y_{o}(1-s)^{2}}\begin{bmatrix}[(1-s)y_{o}+tx_{o}]\delta_{\zeta_{o}}&-i\overline{\alpha_{0}}\beta x_{o}\delta_{\zeta_{o}}\\
i\alpha_{0}\overline{\beta}x_{o}&[(1-s)y_{o}-tx_{o}]\delta_{\zeta_{o}}\end{bmatrix}.

(5.24)

From this we conclude the following.

Theorem
5.2
.

The positive-matrix-valued measure
\Sigma
for the two-phase model
U(\mathcal{C})
with
the assumption
(
5.10
)
is given by the following.

\begin{split}d\Sigma(\zeta)&=\frac{\sqrt{\rho^{2}-x^{2}}}{(1-s)^{2}-(2-2s-\rho^{2})x^{2}}\begin{bmatrix}(1-s)|y|+\operatorname{sgn}\nolimits(y)tx&-\operatorname{sgn}\nolimits(y)i\overline{\alpha_{0}}\beta x\\
i\operatorname{sgn}\nolimits(y)\overline{\alpha_{0}}\beta x&(1-s)|y|-\operatorname{sgn}\nolimits(y)tx\end{bmatrix}\chi_{S}d\hskip-1.0pt\bar{}\,\zeta\\
&\hskip 10.0pt+\frac{x_{*}\sqrt{x_{*}^{2}-\rho^{2}}}{2y_{*}(1-s)^{2}}\begin{bmatrix}[(1-s)y_{*}+tx_{*}]&-i\overline{\alpha_{0}}\beta x_{*}\\
i\alpha_{0}\overline{\beta}x_{*}&[(1-s)y_{*}-tx_{*}]\end{bmatrix}\left(\delta_{\zeta_{*}}+\delta_{-\zeta_{*}}\right)\\
&\hskip 10.0pt+\frac{x_{*}\sqrt{x_{*}^{2}-\rho^{2}}}{2y_{*}(1-s)^{2}}\begin{bmatrix}[(1-s)y_{*}-tx_{*}]&i\overline{\alpha_{0}}\beta x_{*}\\
-i\alpha_{0}\overline{\beta}x_{*}&[(1-s)y_{*}+tx_{*}]\end{bmatrix}\left(\delta_{\overline{\zeta_{*}}}+\delta_{-\overline{\zeta_{*}}}\right),\end{split}

where we set
\zeta=x+iy\in S^{1}
with
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
,

\operatorname{sgn}\nolimits(y)=\begin{cases}+1&(y>0)\\
-1&(y<0)\end{cases}

and
\zeta_{*}=x^{*}+iy_{*}
is given as

x_{*}=\frac{1-s}{\sqrt{2-2s-\rho^{2}}},\quad y_{*}=\sqrt{1-x_{*}^{2}}=\sqrt{\frac{1-\rho^{2}-s^{2}}{2-2s-\rho^{2}}}.

Appendix A
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Theorem
1.1
is proved in
[
10
]
,
[
12
]
,
[
16
]
.
But the way of presentation here is somehow different from them.
Thus, we give its proof for completeness.
Suppose that
f\in\mathrm{Map}\,(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{2})
satisfies
U(\mathcal{C})f=\lambda f
with
\lambda\in S^{1}
.
Then by definition of
U(\mathcal{C})
, we have

\pi_{L}\mathcal{C}(x+1)f(x+1)=\lambda\pi_{L}f(x),\quad\pi_{R}\mathcal{C}(x-1)f(x-1)=\lambda\pi_{R}f(x)

(A.1)

Shifting the variable
x
, we get

\pi_{L}\mathcal{C}(x)f(x)=\lambda\pi_{L}f(x-1),\quad\pi_{R}\mathcal{C}(x)f(x)=\lambda\pi_{R}f(x+1).

(A.2)

The second equation of
(
A.2
)
gives

\pi_{R}f(x+1)=\lambda^{-1}\pi_{R}\mathcal{C}(x)f(x).

(A.3)

The first equation of
(
A.1
)
gives

a_{x+1}\pi_{L}f(x+1)+\pi_{L}\mathcal{C}(x+1)\pi_{R}f(x+1)=\lambda\pi_{L}f(x).

Substituting
(
A.3
)
into the above and dividing both side by
a_{x+1}
show

\pi_{L}f(x+1)=\frac{1}{a_{x+1}}\left(\lambda\pi_{L}-\lambda^{-1}\pi_{L}\mathcal{C}(x+1)\pi_{R}\mathcal{C}(x)\right)f(x)

(A.4)

Summing
(
A.3
)
and
(
A.4
)
then gives

f(x+1)=T_{\lambda}(x)f(x)\quad(x\in\mathbb{Z}),

(A.5)

where
T_{\lambda}(x)
is given in
(
1.5
)
. From
(
A.5
)
,
it is easy to show that
f(x)=F_{\lambda}(x)f(0)
.
Conversely, a direct computation using Lemma
2.2
shows that
the function defined in
(
1.7
)
is an eigenfunction of
U(\mathcal{C})
with the eigenvalue
\lambda
.
Therefore
\mathcal{M}^{\lambda}=\Phi_{\lambda}(\mathbb{C}^{2})
.
Since
\Phi_{\lambda}\colon\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathrm{Map}\,(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{2})
is obviously injective,
we have
\dim\mathcal{M}^{\lambda}=2
. Next, we introduce a map

J\colon\mathrm{Map}\,(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{2})\to\mathrm{Map}\,(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{2})

defined as

(Jf)(x)=\begin{bmatrix}f_{L}(x-1)\\
f_{R}(x)\end{bmatrix},\quad\text{where}\quad f(x)=\begin{bmatrix}f_{L}(x)\\
f_{R}(x)\end{bmatrix}.

Let
f=\Phi_{\lambda}(w)
with
w\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Using
(
A.2
)
we see

a_{x}\pi_{L}f(x)=\lambda\pi_{L}f(x-1)-\pi_{L}\mathcal{C}(x)\pi_{R}f(x),\quad\pi_{R}\mathcal{C}(x)\pi_{L}f(x)-\lambda\pi_{R}f(x+1)=-d_{x}\pi_{R}f(x).

This is equivalent to

Jf(x+1)=S_{\lambda}(x)Jf(x),\quad S_{\lambda}(x)=\frac{1}{a_{x}}\begin{bmatrix}\lambda&-b_{x}\\
c_{x}&\lambda^{-1}\triangle_{x}\end{bmatrix}.

(A.6)

The matrix
S_{\lambda}(x)
is also called the
transfer matrix
(
[
12
]
,
[
16
]
).
Compared with
T_{\lambda}(x)
which we used throughout the paper,

S_{\lambda}(x)
has a nice property
\det S_{\lambda}(x)=d_{x}/a_{x}

and hence
|\det S_{\lambda}(x)|=1
whereas
\det T_{\lambda}(x)=d_{x}/a_{x+1}

whose absolute value is, in general, not the unity.
Let
v,w\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
and suppose, as in
[
16
]
, that
\Phi_{\lambda}(v)\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{2})

and that
\|\Phi_{\lambda}(w)(x)\|_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}
is bounded.
By the definition of
J
, it is easy to show that
\|(J\Phi_{\lambda}(w))(x)\|_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}
is bounded.
Since
J
is unitary,
J\Phi_{\lambda}(w)\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C})
.
Thus we can take a sequence
\{x_{n}\}
of positive integers such that
\|(J\Phi_{\lambda}(w))(x_{n})\|_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}\to 0

as
n\to\infty
.
But, by
(
A.6
)
, we see that the function

W(x)=|\det[(J\Phi_{\lambda}(v))(x),\ (J\Phi_{\lambda}(w))(x)]|

in
x\in\mathbb{Z}
is constant. We have
W(x_{n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
and hence
W(x)
is identically zero.
This shows that
J\Phi_{\lambda}(v)(x)
and
J\Phi_{\lambda}(w)(x)
are linearly dependent.
Let
s_{0},t_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
satisfy

s_{0}J\Phi_{\lambda}(v)(0)+t_{0}J\Phi_{\lambda}(w)(0)=0.

Then for
x\geq 1
, we see

s_{0}J\Phi_{\lambda}(v)(x)+t_{0}J\Phi_{\lambda}(w)(x)=S_{\lambda}(x-1)\cdots S_{\lambda}(0)\left(s_{0}J\Phi_{\lambda}(v)(0)+t_{0}J\Phi_{\lambda}(w)(0)\right)=0.

Similar computation for
x\leq-1
shows that
s_{0}J\Phi_{\lambda}(v)(x)+t_{0}J\Phi_{\lambda}(w)(x)=0

for any
x\in\mathbb{Z}
. Therefore, we conclude
\dim\mathcal{M}^{\lambda}\cap\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{2})\leq 1
.

\square
