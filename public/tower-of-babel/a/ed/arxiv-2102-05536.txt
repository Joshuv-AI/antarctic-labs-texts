Slicing the hypercube is not easy
Gal Yehuda, Amir Yehudayoff

Abstract
. We prove that at least Ω ⁡ ( n 0.51 ) \Omega(n^{0.51}) hyperplanes are needed to slice all edges of the n n -dimensional hypercube. We provide a couple of applications: lower bounds on the computational complexity of parity, and a lower bound on the cover number of the hypercube by skew hyperplanes.



1.
Introduction

The Boolean hypercube has a natural geometric representation in Euclidean space.
Its vertices are the
2^{n}
points
\{\pm 1\}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Its edges are the
n2^{n-1}
line segments

[x,y]\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
connecting
every two adjacent vertices
(i.e.,
x
and
y
differ in a single coordinate).
This representation is important and useful.
It leads to many applications
in algorithm design, optimization, machine learning, and more.

The question we address is

How many hyperplanes are needed to slice all edges?

A hyperplane
h=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\left\langle z,v\right\rangle=\mu\}
slices
the edge
[x,y]
if
x
and
y
lie on two different sides of
h
; that is,
(\left\langle x,v\right\rangle-\mu)(\left\langle y,v\right\rangle-\mu)<0
.

This question has attracted a lot of attention.
It was first studied in the 1970s by O’Neil
with motivation from machine learning
[
18
]
,
and by Grünbaum with motivations from convex geometry
[
11
]
.
Additional motivation comes from computational complexity theory (see e.g.
[
19
]
).
For an excellent introduction to this and related topics,
see the survey of Saks
[
21
]
.

It is tempting to guess that
n
hyperplanes are needed to slice all edges.
This, however, was refuted by M. Paterson in an unpublished
example (see
[
21
]
).
Paterson showed that only
5
hyperplanes suffice to
slice the
6
-dimensional cube. By sub-additivity,

\lceil\tfrac{5n}{6}\rceil
hyperplanes suffice to slice the
n
-dimensional cube.
This is the best upper bound known even today.

In terms of lower bounds, O’Neil proved that the minimum number of slicing hyperplanes
is
\geq\Omega(n^{0.5})
. The reason is that a single hyperplane
can slice a fraction of
\leq O(n^{-0.5})
edges,
and this is sharp
[
4
]
.
In several special cases, better lower bounds are known.
In dimension
n\leq 4
, Emamy-Khansary
[
8
]
proved
that at least
n
hyperplanes are needed.
If all entries in all the normal vectors
v_{i}
are non-negative
then at least
n
hyperplanes are needed
[
1
]
.
If all normal vectors have coefficients in
\{\pm 1\}

then at least
\frac{n}{2}
hyperplanes are needed
[
3
,
21
]
.

We provide the first improvement
1
1

1

We did not attempt
to optimize the constant in the exponent.
in fifty years.

Theorem
.

Slicing all edges in
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
requires

\Omega(n^{0.51})
hyperplanes.

Before discussing the proof,
we provide a couple of applications.

1.1.
Applications

The theorem immediately implies new lower
bounds in computational complexity theory;
specifically, for computing parity with threshold circuits.
The high-level reason is that the
k
gates in the first (closest
to the inputs) layer of a threshold
circuit for parity always yield
k
slicing hyperplanes.
The best known lower bounds were proved
about thirty years ago.
Paturi and Saks proved an
\Omega(n^{1.5})

lower bound on the number of wires in any depth-two
threshold circuit computing parity
[
19
]
.
Impagliazzo, Paturi, and Saks
proved a super-linear lower bound on the number
of wires in any constant-depth threshold circuit for parity
[
12
]
.
In special cases, better lower bounds are known.
The main result of Paturi and Saks is, in fact, that if the coefficients
in the depth-two circuit are integers with bit-complexity
O(\log(n))
,
then a stronger
\Omega(n^{2}/\log^{2}(n))

lower bound on the wire complexity holds
[
19
]
.

The theorem implies the first
\omega(n^{0.5})
lower bound on the number
of gates in the first layer of any threshold circuit computing parity, no matter how deep it is.
It also implies the first
\omega(n^{1.5})

lower bound on the number of wires in any depth-two
threshold circuit for parity.
An extension to other constant depths can be obtained using the ideas
in
[
12
]
.

Corollary
.

The number of wires in a depth-two threshold circuit
for parity is
\Omega(n^{1.51})
.

Proof.

Use the theorem and Lemma 1 in
[
19
]
.
∎

The second application is more geometric.
Instead of slicing edges, we can ask to cover vertices.
A hyperplane
\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\left\langle z,v\right\rangle=\mu\}
covers the
vertex
x
if
\left\langle x,v\right\rangle=\mu
.
How many hyperplanes are needed to cover all vertices?
Just two.
A natural and standard way to make the question
more interesting is to ask the hyperplanes to be skewed
(i.e., all entries in all normal vectors are nonzero).

For the slicing problem, the skew restriction is not severe.
A small perturbation of a slicing family is also a slicing family.
So, we can assume that all the hyperplanes are skewed.

For the covering problem, it creates a huge difference.
The minimum number of skewed hyperplanes needed to cover
all vertices is known to be at least
\Omega(n^{0.5})
; follows from
[
14
,
9
]
.
Again, better results are known in special cases (see
[
21
,
3
]
).

We prove the first improvement on skew cover numbers as well.

Corollary
.

Covering all vertices in
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
requires

\Omega(n^{0.51})
skew hyperplanes.

Proof.

Let
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be skewed and let
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
.
If the
k
hyperplanes defined by
v_{i}
and
\mu_{i}

are covering, then duplicating each
v_{i}
with two thresholds
\mu_{i}\pm\beta
for some small
\beta>0
gives
2k

hyperplanes that slice all edges.
∎

1.2.
Outline

The proof of our main result
brings together ideas from several fields (geometry,
probability theory, partially ordered sets, linear algebra,
and some specialized structural arguments).
We now sketch some of the main ideas.

Let us assume that
k\leq n^{0.51}
hyperplanes slice all edges of the cube.
Our task is to locate the missing edge, an edge that is not sliced.
Where should we look for it?
We can try to use randomness.
This is indeed helpful and proves the
\Omega(n^{0.5})

lower bound, but it is not clear how to get a better lower bound
using just randomness.
We can try to use algebra. This is also fruitful in some cases
[
3
,
21
]
.
But we are dealing with general real numbers
and with the notion of slicing, so it is not clear how algebra can help.
We can try to use topology or geometry.

Our opening move identifies a high-level connection
between the slicing problem and Tarski’s plank problem
[
23
]
.
The plank problem asks about the minimum total width
of planks (regions between parallel hyperplanes)
needed to cover a convex body.
It was solved by Bang in his celebrated work
[
6
]
.
We use a lemma that Ball isolated from Bang’s solution
[
5
]
.

Lemma 1

(Bang)
.

Let
M
be a
k\times k
real symmetric matrix with ones on the diagonal.
Let
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\theta\geq 0
.
Then, there is
\epsilon\in\{\pm 1\}^{k}
so that
for every
i\in[k]
,

\big|\theta(M\epsilon)_{i}-\gamma_{i}\big|\geq\theta.

The proof of Bang’s lemma is elegant and surprisingly short.
The lemma is relevant to the slicing problem because
it states the
existence
of a vector.
This vector is the starting point of our search.

But before we are able to use Bang’s lemma
we need to understand where to focus our attention.
We identify a useful partition of the
k
normal vectors
to two parts with different behaviors (Section
7
).
To utilize this partition,
we develop a couple of general anti-concentration results.
One strong anti-concentration result for vectors with many scales
(Section
6
).
The second set of results is general and
extends several well-known theorems.

An antichain in
\{0,1\}^{n}

is a family of sets with no pairwise strict inclusions.
Sperner’s lemma provides a sharp upper bound on the size of antichains
[
22
]
.
The lemma states that if
A\subset\{0,1\}^{n}
is an antichain
then

|A|\leq\max_{\ell\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}}{n\choose\ell}.

It is fundamental and has many applications
(specifically, in the context of anti-concentration; see
[
9
]
).
There are several extensions of Sperner’s lemma
to various settings.
We need a version of the
lemma for product measures.

Let
P
be a product distribution on
\{0,1\}^{n}
.
We say that
P
is non trivial if
p_{j}(1-p_{j})>0
for all
j\in[n]
,
where
p_{j}=\Pr_{z\sim P}[z_{j}=1]
.
Aizenman et al.
[
2
]
proved
a version of Sperner’s lemma for product distributions.
They showed that the maximum measure of an antichain is

\leq O(\tfrac{1}{\alpha\sqrt{n}})
where

\alpha=\min\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n},1-p_{1},\ldots,1-p_{n}\}
.
Their proof uses Bernoulli decompositions of random variables.
Their bound, however, is not strong enough for our purposes.
We obtain the sharpest possible bound,
and develop some theory in the process (see Section
2
).

Theorem 2
.

For every non trivial product distribution
P

on
\{0,1\}^{n}
, and for every antichain
A\subset\{0,1\}^{n}
,

(1)

\displaystyle\Pr_{z\sim P}[z\in A]\leq\max_{\ell\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}}\Pr_{z\sim P}[|z|=\ell].

The Lubell–Yamamoto–Meshalkin inequality is stronger than
Sperner’s lemma
[
24
,
16
,
15
]
. It states that if
A\subset\{0,1\}^{n}
is an antichain then

\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\frac{|A_{\ell}|}{{n\choose\ell}}\leq 1,

where
A_{\ell}=\{a\in A:|a|=\ell\}
.
The LYM inequality was also generalized in several ways (see e.g.
[
10
]
),
and is sometimes more useful than Sperner’s lemma (see e.g.
[
2
]
).
The following lemma is a strict generalization of the LYM inequality,
and puts the inequality in context.

Theorem 3
.

For every non trivial product distribution
P

on
\{0,1\}^{n}
, and for every antichain
A\subset\{0,1\}^{n}
,

\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\Pr_{z\sim P}[z\in A||z|=\ell]\leq 1.

What underlying property of product measures allows to control
the measure of antichains?
We identify the following mechanism.
There is a way to sample a full chain
in a way that respects the measure.

Lemma 4
.

Let
P
be a non trivial product distribution
P

on
\{0,1\}^{n}
.
For
\ell\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
,
let
P_{\ell}
be the distribution of
z\sim P
conditioned on the event that

|z|=\ell
.
Then, there is a distribution on maximal chains

\emptyset=c_{0}\subset c_{1}\subset\ldots\subset c_{n}=[n]

so that for every
\ell\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
,
the set
c_{\ell}
is distributed according to
P_{\ell}
.

To make use of Theorem
2
,
we need to control the right hand side of (
1
).
Namely, we need to prove an anti-concentration result
for general product measures.
This is done in Section
3
.

Theorem 5
.

There is a constant
C_{1}>0
so that the following holds.
Let
P
be a product distribution on
\{0,1\}^{n}
.
Denote the variance of
|z|
for
z\sim P
by

\sigma^{2}_{P}=\sum_{j}p_{j}(1-p_{j})
.
Then,

\max_{\ell\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}}\Pr_{z\sim P}[|z|=\ell]\leq\frac{C_{1}}{\sigma_{P}}.

What we really care about is not antichains of vertices
but antichains of edges,
because we are interested in the edges that are sliced by a hyperplane.
Edge antichains are somehow more complicated than vertex antichains.
A specific difficulty we need to deal with is orientation.
In the hypercube with the uniform distribution,
all directions “look the same”.
For general product distributions, this is no longer true.
There are special directions.
This becomes problematic now
because by choosing an edge we must commit to some direction.
The chosen direction might not agree with the (unknown) orientation.
All this is explained in Section
4
.

After discovering all these general results,
we identified a second approach for proving what we need
about antichains.
We can replace
general
oriented antichains of edges
by antichains of edges of a specific type.
Antichains that are generated
by oriented monotone Boolean functions.
This naturally leads us to
Fourier analysis over the hypercube; see e.g.
[
13
,
7
,
17
]

and references within.
For the details, see Section
5
.

Remark
.

To summarize,
there are two avenues for proving the “antichain part” of our main result.
One wide avenue that extends Sperner’s lemma
and the LYM inequality (Sections
2
,
3

and
4
).
The second avenue is more specific, quicker and relies on Fourier analysis
(Section
5
).

2.
Antichains of vertices

Here we extend Sperner’s lemma and the LYM inequality to
general product measures.
The main proposition is natural,
but the proof we found is technical.
Recall that
P_{\ell}
denotes the distribution of

z\sim P
conditioned on
|z|=\ell
.

Proposition 6
.

Let
P
be a non trivial product distribution on
\{0,1\}^{n}
.
Let
\ell\in\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
.
Then, there is a probability distribution on pairs
(c_{\ell},c_{\ell+1})\in{[n]\choose\ell}\times{[n]\choose\ell+1}
so that the following hold:

(1)

c_{\ell}\subset c_{\ell+1}
almost surely.

(2)

c_{\ell}
is distributed like
P_{\ell}
.

(3)

c_{\ell+1}
is distributed like
P_{\ell+1}
.

Proof.

Choose
c_{\ell}
according to
P_{\ell}
.
For
s\subset[n]
of size
|s|=\ell
and
j\not\in s
,
we need to decide what is the probability of
c_{\ell+1}=s\cup\{j\}
conditioned on
c_{\ell}=s
.

Let
q\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be so that for all
j\in[n]
,

q_{j}=\frac{p_{j}}{1-p_{j}}>0.

Consider the symmetric polynomial

g_{\ell}(q)=\sum_{t\subseteq[n]:|t|=\ell}\prod_{j\in t}q_{j},

where
g_{0}(q)=1
.
For
s\subset[n]
of size
\ell
,

\displaystyle\Pr[z=s]

\displaystyle=\prod_{a\in s}p_{a}\cdot\prod_{j\not\in s}(1-p_{j})=\prod_{j\in[n]}(1-p_{j})\cdot\prod_{a\in s}q_{a}.

It follows that

\displaystyle P_{\ell}(s)=\Pr_{z\sim P}[z=s||z|=\ell]

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{a\in s}q_{a}}{g_{\ell}(q)}.

Let

h_{s,j}=\sum_{t\subset[n]:|t|=\ell,j\not\in t}\frac{1}{|(s\cup\{j\})\setminus t|}\prod_{a\in t}q_{a}>0.

When
\ell=0
set
h_{s,j}=1
.
For every
s
,

\displaystyle\sum_{j\not\in s}q_{j}h_{s,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\not\in s}q_{j}\sum_{t:|t|=\ell,j\not\in t}\frac{1}{|(s\cup\{j\})\setminus t|}\prod_{a\in t}q_{a}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t:|t|=\ell}\sum_{j\not\in s\cup t}\frac{1}{|(s\cup\{j\})\setminus t|}\prod_{a\in t\cup\{j\}}q_{a}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t:|t|=\ell}\sum_{j\not\in s\cup t}\frac{1}{1+|s\setminus t|}\prod_{a\in t\cup\{j\}}q_{a}

(
t^{\prime}=t\cup\{j\}
)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t^{\prime}:|t^{\prime}|=\ell+1}\prod_{a\in t^{\prime}}q_{a}\sum_{j\in t^{\prime},j\not\in s}\frac{1}{1+|s\setminus t^{\prime}|}.

Because
|s|=\ell
and
|t^{\prime}|=\ell+1
,

|t^{\prime}\setminus s|+|t^{\prime}\cap s|=\ell+1=|s\setminus t^{\prime}|+|s\cap t^{\prime}|+1.

We can conclude

\displaystyle\sum_{j\not\in s}q_{j}h_{s,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t^{\prime}:|t^{\prime}|=\ell+1}\prod_{a\in t^{\prime}}q_{a}\sum_{j\in t^{\prime},j\not\in s}\frac{1}{|t^{\prime}\setminus s|}=g_{\ell+1}(q).

This is also true for
\ell=0
.

Finally, define

\Pr[c_{\ell+1}=s\cup\{j\}|c_{\ell}=s]=\frac{q_{j}h_{s,j}}{g_{\ell+1}(q)}.

We need to show that
c_{\ell+1}
is properly distributed.
For fixed
s^{\prime}
of size
\ell+1
,

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in s^{\prime}}h_{s^{\prime}\setminus\{j\},j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in s^{\prime}}\sum_{t:|t|=\ell,j\not\in t}\frac{1}{|s^{\prime}\setminus t|}\prod_{a\in t}q_{a}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t:|t|=\ell}\prod_{a\in t}q_{a}\sum_{j\in s^{\prime}\setminus t}\frac{1}{|s^{\prime}\setminus t|}=g_{\ell}(q).

This is also true for
\ell=0
.
So,

\displaystyle\Pr[c_{\ell+1}=s^{\prime}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in s^{\prime}}\Pr[c_{\ell}=s^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}]\frac{q_{j}\cdot h_{s^{\prime}\setminus\{j\},j}}{g_{\ell+1}(q)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{a\in s^{\prime}}q_{a}}{g_{\ell}(q){g_{\ell+1}(q)}}\sum_{j\in s^{\prime}}h_{s^{\prime}\setminus\{j\},j}=P_{\ell+1}(s^{\prime}).

∎

The set
c_{0}

For
\ell<n
, define
c_{\ell+1}
from
c_{\ell}

3.
Anti-concentration

Here we prove a general anti-concentration
result for product measures.
The simple proof is inspired by
[
20
]
.

Proof of Theorem
5
.

Think of
z
as taking values in
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
;
this just simplifies the calculations.
Let
\theta
be uniformly distributed in
[0,1]
.
For every integer
t
,

\displaystyle\Pr\Big[\sum_{j=1}^{n}z_{j}=t\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}_{\theta}\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}_{z}\exp\Big(2\pi i\theta\Big(\sum_{j}z_{j}-t\Big)\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}_{\theta}\Big|\prod_{j}\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}_{z_{j}}\exp(2\pi i\theta z_{j})\Big|.

For each
j
,

\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}_{z_{j}}\exp({2\pi i\theta z_{j}})=p_{j}\exp({2\pi i\theta})+(1-p_{j})\exp(-2\pi i\theta)
.
So,

\displaystyle|\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}_{z_{j}}\exp(2\pi i\theta z_{j})|^{2}

\displaystyle=p_{j}^{2}+(1-p_{j})^{2}+2p_{j}(1-p_{j})\cos(2\pi\theta)

\displaystyle=1-2p_{j}(1-p_{j})(1-\cos(2\pi\theta))

\displaystyle=1-4p_{j}(1-p_{j})\sin^{2}(\pi\theta)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-4p_{j}(1-p_{j})\sin^{2}(\pi\theta)).

It follows that

\displaystyle\Pr\Big[\sum_{j=1}^{n}z_{j}=t\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}_{\theta}\exp(-2\sigma^{2}_{P}\sin^{2}(\pi\theta))

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{1/2}\exp(-2\sigma^{2}_{P}\sin^{2}(\pi\theta))d\theta

(
\sin(\xi)\geq\xi/2
)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp(-\sigma^{2}_{P}\theta^{2})d\theta\leq\frac{C_{1}}{\sigma_{P}}.

∎

4.
Antichains of edges

The following section explains how to bound from above the measure
of
oriented
antichains of
edges
in general product distributions.
Oriented antichains of edges are more difficult to handle than
antichains of vertices.
The arguments in this section are, consequently, more technical
and less accurate than in the previous sections.
Nevertheless, the arguments are inspired by
and rely on Section
2
.

The cube can be oriented so that
u\in\{0,1\}^{n}
is its new origin.
This orientation allows to stratify the cube according to
the Hamming distance from
u
.
A
u
-chain of edges is a set

C=\{(c_{0},c_{1}),(c_{1},c_{2}),\ldots,(c_{n-1},c_{n})\}

of edges in the cube so that the Hamming distance of
c_{\ell}
from

u
is
\ell
, for all
\ell\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
.
A
u
-antichain of edges is a set of edges
A
so that
every
u
-chain of edges intersects
A
in at most a single edge.

The set of edges
A_{h}
that are sliced by a hyperplane
h
is an oriented
antichain of edges
[
18
]
.
The origin
u
is defined by the sign pattern
of the normal vector
v
via
u_{j}=1
iff
v_{j}>0
.
The set
A_{h}
is a
u
-antichain of edges.

Theorem 7
.

There is a constant
C_{2}>0
so that the following holds.
Let
P
be a non trivial product distribution on
\{0,1\}^{n}
.
There is a distribution on edges
[x,y]
in the cube
so that
x\sim P
with the following property.
Let
u\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and let
A
be a
u
-antichain of edges.
Then,

\Pr[[x,y]\in A]\leq\frac{C_{2}n^{4}}{\sigma^{9}_{P}}.

Remark
.

It is important that the distribution on
[x,y]

does not depend on
u
.

Proof.

We start by partitioning
[n]
according to
P
.
For
j\in[n]
, let

p_{j}=\Pr_{z\sim P}[z_{j}=1].

Let

J_{*}=\{j\in[n]:p_{j}(1-p_{j})\geq p_{*}\},

where

p_{*}=\frac{\sigma_{P}^{2}}{2n}.

Let
n_{*}=|J_{*}|
and let

P_{*}
be the restriction of
P
to coordinates in
J_{*}
.
Because
\sigma_{P}^{2}=\sum_{j\in[n]}p_{j}(1-p_{j})
,
we know that

(3)

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{P_{*}}=\sum_{j\in J_{*}}p_{j}(1-p_{j})\geq\frac{\sigma_{P}^{2}}{2}.

The distribution on the edge
[x,y]
is defined as follows.
Let
x\sim P
.
Denote by
N_{*}(x)
the set of neighbors of
x

that differ from
x
only in coordinates in
J_{*}
.
Let
y
be chosen uniformly at random in
N_{*}(x)
.
The vertices
x,y
agree in all coordinates not in
J_{*}
.
For the rest of the proof, we ignore coordinates not in
J_{*}
,
and think about them as fixed.

The orientation of the cube is determined by
u\in\{0,1\}^{n}
.
We claim that we can assume without loss of generality that
u=0
.
Why?
Because we can replace
P
by
the product distribution
P_{u}
on
\{0,1\}^{n}

so that
z+u\sim P_{u}
where
z\sim P
.
And we can replace the
u
-antichain
A

by the
\emptyset
-antichain
A+u
,
obtained by replacing each edge
[a,b]
in

by the edge
[a+u,b+u]
.
The distribution

yields exactly the same set

as

does. Consequently,

is distributed like
P_{u}
,
and the distribution of

conditioned on

remains the same.
In addition, the edge
[x+u,y+u]
belongs to

iff the edge
[x,y]
belongs to
A
.

Proposition
6
allows to sample
a vertex at level
\ell+1
from a vertex at level
\ell
.
By repeatedly applying the proposition,
we can grow a chain from any starting vertex
either towards
\emptyset
or in the opposite direction.
A crucial property of this construction is its reversibility.
For example, if
z\sim P_{*}
and we grow a chain from

towards

, then we can generate the same distribution
by growing a chain from
\emptyset
outwards,
and stop at a random time distributed like

.
This reversibility is instrumental in the proof of Lemma

below.

To handle the unknown orientation, we generate three random chains of edges

C,C_{+}
and
C_{-}
:

(1)

Let
C
be a random chain of edges that contains the edge
[x,y]

as follows.
Use Proposition

with
P_{*}

in two opposite directions.
From the minimum between

grow a chain towards
\emptyset
.
From the maximum between
x,y
grow a chain towards
J_{*}
.
The chain
C
is obtained by gluing these two chains
via the edge
[x,y]
.

(2)

The chain
C_{+}
is generated as follows.
Let
y_{+}
be chosen uniformly at random in
N_{*}(x)
conditioned on

|y_{+}\cap J_{*}|=|x\cap J_{*}|+1
; when there is no such option,
set
C_{+}
to be empty.
As in the construction of
C
,
the chain
C_{+}
is obtained by
growing a chain that contains the edge
[x,y_{+}]
.

(3)

The chain
C_{-}
is generated similarly.
Let
y_{-}
be uniformly random in
N_{*}(x)
conditioned on

|y_{-}\cap J_{*}|=|x\cap J_{*}|-1
; when there is no such option,

C_{-}
to be empty.
As in the construction of

and
C_{+}
,
the chain

is obtained by
growing a chain that contains the edge
[y_{-},x]
.

There is a coupling of the three chains
C,C_{+}
and
C_{-}

so that each chain separately has the correct distribution,
and the edge
[x,y]
is always equal to one of
[x,y_{+}]
or
[y_{-},x]
.
First, choose
x\sim P
.
Then choose a neighbor
y_{+}
of
x

uniformly at random on the level above
x
,
and choose a neighbor
y_{-}
of
x

uniformly at random on the level below

.
If one of the two sets of neighbors is empty

To generate
C_{+}
extend the edge
[x,y_{+}]

to a chain.
To generate
C_{-}
extend the edge
[y_{-},x]

to a chain as well.
Finally, choose

to be one of
C_{+}

or
C_{-}
with the appropriate chance
so that

is a uniform neighbor of

.

The key idea is to control the distribution of

conditioned on

.

Lemma 8
.

There is a universal constant
K>0
so that the following holds.
Denote by
c_{0},c_{1},\ldots,c_{n_{*}}

the
{n_{*}}+1
vertices that appear in some fixed chain
c_{+}
of edges.
Then, for all
\ell\in\{0,1,\ldots,{n_{*}}\}
, we have
\Pr[x=c_{\ell}|C_{+}=c_{+}]\leq\frac{Kn^{4}}{\sigma^{9}_{P}}
.

Proof of lemma.

For every
\ell
,

\displaystyle\Pr[x=c_{\ell}|C_{+}=c_{+}]

\displaystyle=\frac{\Pr[x=c_{\ell}]\Pr[C_{+}=c_{+}|x=c_{\ell}]}{\Pr[C_{+}=c_{+}]}.

Let
Q(s,j)=Q(s,\{j\})
be the probability to move from
s
to
s\cup\{j\}

according to the construction in Proposition
6
;
here
j
is assumed to be not in
s
.
Let
Q_{j,j+1}=Q(c_{j},c_{j+1}\setminus c_{j})
.
Similarly, denote by
Q_{a+1,a}
the probability to move
from
c_{a+1}
to
c_{a}
.
By construction, for every
j<n_{*}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\Pr[x=c_{j}]}{\Pr[|x\cap J_{*}|=j]}Q_{j,j+1}

\displaystyle=\Pr[x=c_{j}||x\cap J_{*}|=j]Q_{j,j+1}

\displaystyle=\Pr[x=c_{j+1}||x\cap J_{*}|=j+1]Q_{j+1,j}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Pr[x=c_{j+1}]}{\Pr[|x\cap J_{*}|=j+1]}Q_{j+1,j}.

So,

\displaystyle Q_{j+1,j}=\frac{\Pr[x=c_{j}]}{\Pr[|x\cap J_{*}|=j]}\frac{\Pr[|x\cap J_{*}|=j+1]}{\Pr[x=c_{j+1}]}Q_{j,j+1}.

Focus on (for
\ell<n_{*}
)

\displaystyle\Pr[C_{+}=c_{+}|x=c_{\ell}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{*}-\ell}\prod_{j>\ell}Q_{j,j+1}\prod_{a<\ell}Q_{a+1,a}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{*}-\ell}\prod_{j>\ell}Q_{j,j+1}\prod_{a<\ell}Q_{a,a+1}\frac{\Pr[x=c_{a}]}{\Pr[|x\cap J_{*}|=a]}\frac{\Pr[|x\cap J_{*}|=a+1]}{\Pr[x=c_{a+1}]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(n_{*}-\ell)Q_{\ell,\ell+1}}\frac{\Pr[|x\cap J_{*}|=\ell]}{\Pr[|x\cap J_{*}|=0]}\frac{\Pr[x=c_{0}]}{\Pr[x=c_{\ell}]}\prod_{j}Q_{j,j+1}.

Hence, for every
\ell\neq\ell^{\prime}
that are less than
n_{*}
,

(4)

\displaystyle\frac{\Pr[x=c_{\ell}|C_{+}=c_{+}]}{\Pr[x=c_{\ell^{\prime}}|C_{+}=c_{+}]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Pr[x=c_{\ell}]\Pr[C_{+}=c_{+}|x=c_{\ell}]}{\Pr[x=c_{\ell^{\prime}}]\Pr[C_{+}=c_{+}|x=c_{\ell^{\prime}}]}

\displaystyle=\frac{(n_{*}-\ell^{\prime})Q_{\ell^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}+1}\Pr[|x\cap J_{*}|=\ell]}{(n_{*}-\ell)Q_{\ell,\ell+1}\Pr[|x\cap J_{*}|=\ell^{\prime}]}.

We need to understand this ratio.

In Proposition
6
, we used the notation

q_{j}=\frac{p_{j}}{1-p_{j}}
.
By construction, for every
s
of size
\ell
and
j
not in
s
,

\displaystyle Q(s,j)=\frac{q_{j}}{g_{\ell+1}(q)}\sum_{t:|t|=\ell,j\not\in t}\frac{1}{|(s\cup\{j\})\setminus t|}\prod_{a\in t}q_{a}.

Let
j^{\prime}
be not in
s\cup\{j\}
.
There is a one-to-one correspondence between
sets
t
of size
\ell
that do not contain
j
,
and sets
t^{\prime}
of size
\ell
that do not contain
j^{\prime}
;
if
t
does not contain
j^{\prime}
then
t^{\prime}=t
,
and if
t
contains
j^{\prime}
then
t^{\prime}=(t\setminus\{j^{\prime}\})\cup\{j\}
.
For every
t
and the corresponding
t^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle\frac{q_{j}}{|(s\cup\{j\})\setminus t|}\prod_{a\in t}q_{a}

\displaystyle\leq\max\Big\{1,\frac{q_{j}}{q_{j^{\prime}}}\Big\}\frac{q_{j^{\prime}}}{|(s\cup\{j^{\prime}\})\setminus t^{\prime}|}\prod_{a\in t^{\prime}}q_{a}.

We can always bound

\displaystyle\frac{q_{j}}{q_{j^{\prime}}}\leq\frac{\frac{1}{p_{j}(1-p_{j})}}{p_{j^{\prime}}(1-p_{j^{\prime}})}\leq\frac{1}{p_{*}^{2}}.

It follows that for all
j,j^{\prime}
we have
\frac{Q(s,j)}{Q(s,j^{\prime})}\leq\frac{1}{p_{*}^{2}}
.
Because
j\mapsto Q(s,j)
is a probability distribution
on a universe of size
n_{*}-|s|
,
we can conclude that for all
j
,

p_{*}^{2}\leq(n_{*}-|s|)Q(s,j)\leq\frac{1}{p_{*}^{2}}.

It now follows that for all
\ell\neq\ell^{\prime}
that are less than
n_{*}
,

\displaystyle p_{*}^{4}\leq\frac{(n_{*}-\ell^{\prime})Q_{\ell^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}+1}}{(n_{*}-\ell)Q_{\ell,\ell+1}}\leq\frac{1}{p_{*}^{4}}.

Together with (
4
) we get that
for every
\ell<n
,

(5)

\displaystyle\Pr[x=c_{\ell}|C_{+}=c_{+}]\leq\frac{2}{p_{*}^{4}}\Pr[|x\cap J_{*}|=\ell].

Indeed, if for some
\ell_{0}
the above does not hold then
for all
\ell<n_{*}
,

\Pr[x=c_{\ell}|C_{+}=c_{+}]>2\Pr[|x\cap J_{*}|=\ell]

(we can assume that
\frac{C_{1}}{\sigma_{P}}<\frac{1}{2}
).
Summing over
\ell<n_{*}
,
Theorem
5
yields a contradiction:

1>2(1-\Pr[|x\cap J_{*}|=n_{*}])>1.

Finally, (
5
), the choice of
p_{*}
, and
another application of Theorem
5

complete the proof of the lemma.
∎

The lemma allows to control the behavior of
[x,y_{+}]
:

\displaystyle\Pr[[x,y_{+}]\in A]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}_{C_{+}}\Pr[[x,y_{+}]\in A|C_{+}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}_{C_{+}}\Pr[|x|=h(C_{+})|C_{+}]\leq\frac{Kn^{4}}{\sigma^{9}_{P}},

where
h(C_{+})
is the height of the unique edge in

C_{+}\cap A
(if there is such an edge).
A similar bound holds for
\Pr[[y_{-},x]\in A]
.
By the coupling described above,

\Pr[[x,y]\in A]\leq\Pr[[x,y_{+}]\in A]+\Pr[[y_{-},x]\in A].

∎

5.
Oriented monotone functions

The following section explains how to bound from above the
measure of oriented antichains of edges of a specific type.
Antichains that are generated by oriented monotone functions.
A function
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
is (locally) monotone
if for every
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and for every
j\in[n]

so that
x_{j}=0
it holds that
f(x+e_{j})\geq f(x)
,
where addition is coordinate-wise modulo two
and
e_{j}
is the unit vector with a one in the
j
’th coordinate.
Again, the hypercube can be oriented so that

u\in\{0,1\}^{n}
is its new origin.
A function
f
is
u
-monotone if the map

x\mapsto f(x+u)
is monotone.

The hyperplane
\left\langle z,v\right\rangle=\mu
defines the linear threshold function

f(z)=\mathsf{sign}(\left\langle z,v\right\rangle-\mu)
.
The set of edges
A
that are sliced by this hyperplane is not just an oriented antichain. It has additional structure.
The map
f
is
u
-monotone with
u

so that
u_{j}=1
iff
v_{j}>0
.
The set of sliced edges
A
is deeply related to the sensitivity
of
f
.
Fourier analysis is a standard tool for controlling sensitivity.
The specific implementation for general product measures
we use appears in the work of Bshouty and Tamon
[
7
]
.

The sensitivity of oriented monotone functions, however, is not
our main concern. We need to find distributions
on edges that typically produce unsliced edges.

Theorem 9
.

Let
P
be a non trivial product distribution on
\{0,1\}^{n}
.
There is a distribution on edges
[x,y]

so that
x\sim P
with the following property.
Let
u\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and let

f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
be
u
-monotone.
Then,

\Pr[f(x)\neq f(y)]\leq\frac{1}{\sigma_{P}}.

Remark
.

It is important that the distribution of
[x,y]

does not depend on
u
.

Remark
.

Theorem
9
is less general
but quantitively stronger than Theorem
7
.

Proof.

Choose
x\sim P
.
The distribution of
y
conditioned on
x
is as follows.
For
j\in[n]
, let
\sigma_{j}=p_{j}(1-p_{j})
.
For each
j\in[n]
, choose
y=x+e_{j}
with probability

\frac{\sigma^{2}_{j}}{\sigma_{P}^{2}}
.
It remains to bound the probability that
f(x)\neq f(y)
.

Let
F
be the map defined by
F(z)=f(u+z)
.
By assumption, the map
F
is monotone.
Denote by
Q
the distribution of
x+u
.
Let
z\sim Q
and let
w
be a neighbor of
z

distributed so that
w=z+e_{j}
with probability

\frac{\sigma^{2}_{j}}{\sigma_{P}^{2}}
.
The distribution of
[x+u,y+u]
is identical to that of
[z,w]
so that

\Pr[f(x)\neq f(y)]=\Pr[F(z)\neq F(w)].

We briefly mention the essentials of Fourier analysis
over the hypercube.
For
j\in[n]
, let
q_{j}=\Pr[x_{j}+u_{j}=1]
.
The
n
random variables
\frac{z_{1}-q_{1}}{\sigma_{1}},\ldots,\frac{z_{n}-q_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}
are independent
variables with expectation zero and variance one.
The
2^{n}
maps

\chi_{S}(z)=\prod_{j\in S}\frac{z_{j}-q_{j}}{\sigma_{j}}

form an orthonormal basis for the space of functions
from
\{0,1\}^{n}
to
\mathbb{R}
with respect to the inner product

\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}_{z\sim Q}h(z)g(z)
.
Write
F
as

F(z)=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\hat{F}(S)\chi_{S}(z).

Because
F
is Boolean, we know
\sum_{S}(\hat{f}(S))^{2}\leq 1
.

The sensitivity of a monotone function is known
to have a nice Fourier-theoretic representation.
For
j\in[n]
and
z\in\{0,1\}^{n}
,
let
z^{j\to 1}
be the vector that is the same as
z

except that it always has a one in the
j
’th coordinate,
and let
z^{j\to 0}
be defined similarly.
Because
F
is monotone,

\displaystyle\Pr[F(z)\neq F(w)]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}_{z}\sum_{j}\frac{\sigma_{j}^{2}}{\sigma_{P}^{2}}1_{F(z)\neq F(z+e_{j})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\frac{\sigma_{j}}{\sigma_{P}^{2}}\sigma_{j}\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}_{z}(F(z^{j\to 1})-F(z^{j\to 0})).

For fixed
j\in[n]
,
denote by
z_{-j}
the vector
z
after deleting the
j
’th
coordinate so that

\displaystyle\sigma_{j}\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}_{z}(F(z^{j\to 1})-F(z^{j\to 0}))

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}_{z}\frac{q_{j}(1-q_{j})}{\sigma_{j}}(F(z^{j\to 1})-F(z^{j\to 0}))

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}_{z}q_{j}\frac{1-q_{j}}{\sigma_{j}}F(z^{j\to 1})+(1-q_{j})\frac{-q_{j}}{\sigma_{j}}F(z^{j\to 0})

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}_{z_{-j}}\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}_{z_{j}}\chi_{\{j\}}(z)F(z)

\displaystyle=\hat{F}(\{j\}).

By Cauchy-Schwartz, we can deduce

\displaystyle\Pr[F(z)\neq F(w)]=\frac{1}{\sigma_{P}^{2}}\sum_{j}\sigma_{j}\hat{F}(\{j\})\leq\frac{1}{\sigma_{P}^{2}}\sigma_{P}\sqrt{\sum_{j}(\hat{F}(\{j\}))^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\sigma_{P}}.

∎

6.
Vectors with many scales

In this section we prove a strong anti-concentration bound
for vectors with many scales
(see definition below).
We start with a simple claim.

Claim 10
.

There is a constant
C_{0}>1
so that the following holds.
If
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
has norm
\|u\|_{2}=1
then

\Pr_{x\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}}[\tfrac{1}{C_{0}}\leq|\left\langle x,u\right\rangle|\leq C_{0}]\geq\frac{1}{C_{0}},

where
x\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}
means that
x
is uniformly distributed
in the cube.

Proof.

Let
Z=(\left\langle x,u\right\rangle)^{2}
. So,

\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}Z=1

and

\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}Z^{2}\leq 6\|u\|_{2}^{4}+\|u\|_{4}^{4}\leq 7.

By the Payley-Zygmond inequality,

\displaystyle\Pr[Z\geq C_{0}^{-2}]\geq\frac{(1-C_{0}^{-2})^{2}}{7}.

By Markov’s inequality,

\Pr[Z\geq C_{0}^{2}]\leq\frac{1}{C_{0}^{2}}.

∎

Definition
.

The vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
has
S
scales if

v
can be partitioned to
S
vectors

v^{(1)},v^{(2)},\ldots,v^{(S)}

so that

\|v^{(s)}\|_{2}\geq 4C_{0}^{2}\|v^{(s+1)}\|_{2}

for all
s<S
, where
C_{0}
is
from Claim
10
.
The smallest scale of
v
is defined to be
\|v^{(S)}\|_{2}
.

Lemma 11
.

There is a constant
C_{3}>1
so that the following holds.
If
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
has
S
scales and its smallest scale is
\delta\geq 0
,
then for every
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
b\geq 2
we have

\Pr_{x\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}}[|\left\langle x,v\right\rangle-a|<b\delta]<C_{3}\exp(-\tfrac{S}{C_{3}}+C_{3}\log(b)).

Proof.

For
s\in[S]
let
z_{s}=|\left\langle x^{(s)},v^{(s)}\right\rangle|
,
where we partitioned
x
according to the partition of
v
.
Let
C>0
be a large enough constant to be determined.
By Chernoff’s inequality and Claim
10
, with probability at least

1-C\exp(-S/C)
, there are at least
S/C

values of
r\in[S]
so that

(6)

\displaystyle\frac{\|v^{(r)}\|_{2}}{C_{0}}\leq z_{r}\leq C_{0}\|v^{(r)}\|_{2}.

Condition on this very likely event.
For every such
r<r^{\prime}
,

z_{r}\geq\frac{\|v^{(r)}\|_{2}}{C_{0}}\geq 4C_{0}\|v^{(r^{\prime})}\|_{2}\geq 4z_{r^{\prime}}.

In particular, there are at least
\tfrac{S}{C}-C\log(b)
such
r
’s so that

(7)

\displaystyle z_{r}>4b\delta.

Denote by
R
the set of
r\in[S]
so that both
(
6
) and (
7
) hold.
Let
z=\sum_{s\not\in R}z_{s}
.
Conditioned on
R
, on the values of
z_{r}
for
r\in R
,
and on
z
,
the event that
|\left\langle x,v\right\rangle-a|<b\delta

is the same as

(8)

\displaystyle\Big|z-a+\sum_{r\in R}\epsilon_{r}z_{r}\Big|<b\delta,

where
\epsilon\sim\{\pm 1\}^{R}
.
We claim that there is at most a single

\epsilon
so that (
8
) holds.
Indeed, if
\epsilon\neq\epsilon^{\prime}
both satisfy (
8
)
then

\displaystyle 2b\delta

\displaystyle>\Big|\sum_{r\in R}(\epsilon_{r}-\epsilon^{\prime}_{r})z_{r}\Big|

\displaystyle=\Big|(\epsilon_{r_{0}}-\epsilon^{\prime}_{r_{0}})z_{r_{0}}+\sum_{r\in R:r>r_{0}}(\epsilon_{r}-\epsilon^{\prime}_{r})z_{r}\Big|,

where
r_{0}
is the minimum index in which
\epsilon
and
\epsilon^{\prime}

differ (so
|\epsilon_{r_{0}}-\epsilon^{\prime}_{r_{0}}|=2
).
This is a contradiction because

\displaystyle z_{r_{0}}>b\delta+\sum_{r\in R:r>r_{0}}z_{r}.

∎

7.
Decomposing a matrix

The following lemma helps to identify where we need to focus our attention.

Lemma 12
.

Let
V
be the matrix whose rows are the skewed
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

with
k\leq n^{0.51}
and
n
large enough.
We can re-order the rows and columns of
V
as follows.
Let
V^{\prime}=(v^{\prime}_{ij})
be the matrix obtained by looking at the first

k^{\prime}\leq k
rows of
V
,
the first
n^{\prime}\geq n/2
columns of
V
,
and renormalizing each row of
V^{\prime}
to have
\ell_{2}
-norm one.
The following hold:

(1)

Every row
i>k^{\prime}
in
V
that is not in
V^{\prime}
has
S:=\lfloor n^{0.001}\rfloor
scales, and the position of the smallest scale of row
i
contains the first
n^{\prime}
columns.

(2)

For every column
j\leq n^{\prime}
in
V^{\prime}
,

(9)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\leq k^{\prime}}{v^{\prime}}_{ij}^{2}<n^{-0.487}.

In particular,

(10)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\leq k^{\prime}}|v^{\prime}_{ij}|<\sqrt{kn^{-0.487}}\leq n^{0.0115}.

Remark
.

The value of
k^{\prime}
can be zero.
In this case, all rows of
V
have
S
scales.

Proof.

It is convenient to use the following terminology.
The mass of a vector
u
is
\|u\|_{2}^{2}
.
If there is no column
j
in
V
with mass

\sum_{i}v_{ij}^{2}\geq n^{-0.488},

then we are done.
Otherwise, there are such columns.
Start moving them one-by-one to the end of
V
,
and removing them from
V^{\prime}
as described below.

When we remove a column from
V^{\prime}
,
the norm of each row changes, but we do not immediately renormalize it.
Let
\tau>0
be so that

\sqrt{\frac{1-\tau}{\tau}}=4C_{0}^{2},

where
C_{0}
is from Lemma
4
.
If for a given row
i
, after the removal of a column,
the total current mass of the part
that remained in
V^{\prime}
drops below
\tau
, then mark a “drop” for row
i
,
and renormalize its norm in
V^{\prime}
to be one.
When a drop occurs, we get one more scale for
v_{i}
,
by the choice of
\tau
.
If a row is dropped more than
S
times, then move it to the end of
V
and remove it from
V^{\prime}
. Each removed row has
S
scales
with the position of the minimum scale containing all columns of
V^{\prime}
.
This explains the first item.

Let

\sigma_{t}
be the mass of the column
that was removed at time
t
, with the normalization at time
t
.
So, for all
t
,

\sigma_{t}\geq n^{-0.488}.

If there are
T
iterations, then

\sum_{t}\sigma_{t}\geq n^{-0.488}T.

Let
\sigma_{t,i}
be the contribution of row
i
to

\sigma_{t}
; it is zero if the row was removed before time
t
.
Because every row is dropped at most
S
times, for each row
i
, we have

\sum_{t}\sigma_{t,i}<S
.
So,

\sum_{t}\sigma_{t}<Sk\leq n^{0.511}.

It follows that after
T<n^{0.999}\leq\frac{n}{2}

steps, we get a matrix
V^{\prime}
so that the mass of every column
j
in it is

\sum_{i\leq k^{\prime}}{v^{\prime}}_{ij}^{2}<n^{-0.488}.

The rows of this matrix are not yet normalized.
The norm of each row is at least
\sqrt{\tau}
.
The renormalization can not increase the norm of the columns
by more than
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tau}}
.
After the final renormalization we get

\displaystyle\sum_{i\leq k^{\prime}}{v^{\prime}}_{ij}^{2}<\frac{n^{-0.488}}{\sqrt{\tau}}<n^{-0.487}.

∎

8.
Finding the missing edge

We are ready to prove the main result.
Assume towards a contradiction that the
k\leq n^{0.51}
hyperplanes
defined by the skewed
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
and by
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k}
slice all edges of the hypercube
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
for large

.
