Mendata: A Framework to Purify Manipulated Training Data
Zonghao Huang, Neil Gong, Michael K. Reiter

Abstract
Untrusted data used to train a model might have been manipulated to endow the learned model with hidden properties that the data contributor might later exploit. Data purification aims to remove such manipulations prior to training the model. We propose Mendata, a novel framework to purify manipulated training data. Starting from a small reference dataset in which a large majority of the inputs are clean, Mendata perturbs the training inputs so that they retain their utility but are distributed similarly (as measured by Wasserstein distance) to the reference data, thereby eliminating hidden properties from the learned model. A key challenge is how to find such perturbations, which we address by formulating a min-max optimization problem and developing a two-step method to iteratively solve it. We demonstrate the effectiveness of Mendata by applying it to defeat state-of-the-art data poisoning and data tracing techniques.



1
Introduction

A machine learning (ML) system often harvests training data from
unverified
data contributors
, e.g., users/webpages on the
Internet. In such cases, an ML system is fundamentally vulnerable to

data manipulation
, i.e., changes introduced to the training
data to endow the learned model with certain hidden,
data-contributor-desired
properties
. Such data manipulation
techniques can be used as an attack to compromise the integrity of an
ML system or as a defense to protect data contributors. For instance,
when a malicious data contributor
poisons
the training
data
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
,
the property is that the learned model will misclassify (perhaps in a
targeted way) a certain
target input
or any input patched
with a
trigger
, pre-chosen by the data contributor. When a
data contributor instead marks its data for the purpose of

data tracing

[
6
]
, the
property is that the data contributor can detect that her data was
used for model training (perhaps without authorization).

Most existing defenses against data manipulation focus on

prevention

[
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
or
detection

[
16
,
17
,
18
]
. Specifically, a
prevention mechanism aims to redesign the ML training algorithm so
that the learned model does not inherit the hidden property even if
some training inputs are manipulated. For instance, ensemble methods,
which train multiple models on different subsets of the training data,
can guarantee that the target input is correctly classified, despite
some training data having been poisoned
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
. However, these
prevention methods 1) require modifying the ML training algorithm, 2)
can only tolerate a tiny fraction of poisoned training inputs, and 3)
often sacrifice the utility of the models. A detection mechanism aims
to detect clean training data in a manipulated training dataset, and
only the training data detected to be clean is subsequently used for
model training. These detection methods can effectively make the
learned model free of hidden properties, but they sacrifice utility of
the learned model by discarding the training data that are (perhaps
falsely) detected as manipulated.

Data purification
aims to complement prevention and
detection. For instance, when a subset of the manipulated training
dataset is detected as clean by a detector, instead of throwing away
the remaining training data, data purification can be used to purify
them. We refer to the training data detected to be clean as

reference data
and the remaining training data as

untrusted data
. Then, the
purified training data
, which
consists of the reference data and the purified untrusted data, is
used for model training. Note that the reference dataset may include
poisoned training data while the untrusted dataset may include clean
training data, due to the imperfection of the detector.
Unfortunately, data purification is largely unexplored, and indeed, we
are aware of only one instance: Liu et al.
[
19
]
proposed a data
purification method called
Friendly Noise
, which adds as large
perturbations to the untrusted dataset as possible while maintaining
the predictions made by a model pre-trained on the original
manipulated training dataset.

We propose
Mendata
, a novel and general framework to purify
(or “mend”) manipulated training data. Given a manipulated training
dataset, a subset of which is detected as a reference dataset by an
existing detector, Mendata aims to purify the inputs in the remaining
untrusted dataset by adding carefully crafted perturbations to
them. Specifically, Mendata aims to achieve three goals: 1) a

property goal
, which is that a model learned on the purified
training data is free of the data-contributor-desired property, 2) a

utility goal
, which is that a model learned on the purified
training data is accurate, and 3) a
generality goal
, which is
that Mendata is applicable to a wide range of data manipulation
algorithms and properties.

To achieve the property goal, our key observation is that a model
trained on the reference dataset is unlikely to have the property
since an overwhelming majority of the reference data are clean
(assuming the detector is reasonably accurate). Therefore, Mendata perturbs each input in the untrusted dataset so that they follow a
similar distribution to the reference dataset. We quantify the
distribution similarity between the two datasets using the well-known

Wasserstein distance

[
20
,
21
]
, due to its intuitive
interpretation (the minimum cost of moving a pile of earth in the
shape of one distribution to achieve the shape of the other). To
achieve the utility goal, Mendata aims to bound the perturbations
added to the untrusted inputs. Formally, we formulate finding the
perturbations as a minimization optimization problem, whose objective
function is a weighted sum of the Wasserstein distance between the two
dataset distributions (quantifying the property goal) and the
magnitudes of the perturbations (quantifying the utility goal).
Mendata achieves the generality goal by not depending on any specific
data manipulation algorithm or property it might be intended to
embed in the learned model, when formulating and solving the
optimization problem.

However, it is hard to solve the optimization problem due to the
challenge of computing the Wasserstein distance. To address the
challenge, we leverage the Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality
[
22
]
to
approximate the Wasserstein distance as the
maximum
difference
between the average function value of the untrusted inputs and that of
the reference inputs, where the function value of an input is
calculated using a neural network that implements a
1
-Lipschitz
function. Then, we refine our optimization problem as a min-max one,
where the inner max problem aims to find the
1
-Lipschitz neural
network that approximates the Wasserstein distance and the outer min
problem aims to find the perturbations. We detail a two-step method
to iteratively solve the min-max optimization problem. One step
starts from current perturbations and solves the inner max problem to
update the neural network used to approximate the Wasserstein
distance. The second step starts from the current neural network
approximating Wasserstein distance and solves the outer min problem to
update the perturbations.

We evaluate Mendata on two representative use cases. The first use
case is to mitigate data poisoning
attacks
[
3
,
4
,
23
]
,
while the second is to avoid data
tracing
[
6
]
. Our results on multiple
image benchmark datasets show that Mendata can effectively eliminate
the hidden property from the model learned on the purified training
data while still achieving high accuracy, even if the reference
dataset includes a small fraction of poisoned data. Moreover, we show
that Mendata outperforms existing data purification methods, notably
Friendly Noise
[
19
]
, and state-of-the-art
prevention methods. For instance, when the models learned on the
purified training data achieve similar accuracy, the models learned on
the training data purified by Mendata are more likely to be
property-free.

To summarize, our key contributions are as follows:

•

We propose Mendata, a general framework to purify manipulated
training data.

•

We formulate data purification as an optimization problem and
leverage a two-step method to approximately solve it.

•

We show the effectiveness of Mendata by applying it to two
representative use cases, namely to defeat state-of-the-art data
poisoning and data tracing schemes.

2
Related Work

2.1
Training-Data Manipulation

Clean-label vs. dirty-label training-data manipulation
:
Training-data manipulation generally refers to tampering with the
training data to endow the learned model with certain hidden
properties. Some training-data manipulation methods
[
3
,
4
,
24
,
5
,
6
]
only
tamper with the training inputs (known as
clean-label
), while
some
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
also tamper with the labels (known as
dirty-label
). For
instance, dirty-label backdoor attacks
[
28
,
29
]
, which embed an attacker-chosen
trigger into some training inputs and re-label them as an
attacker-chosen, incorrect label, are representative dirty-label
training-data manipulation methods. However, labels are often manually
analyzed and assigned by a data curator depending on the application
context of the data. For instance, a face image could be labeled as
the person name when used for face recognition
[
30
]
, or labeled as
“happy” when used for emotion recognition
[
31
]
. In such cases, it is hard
for a data contributor to tamper with the labels. Therefore, we focus
on
clean-label
training-data manipulation in this work.

Training-data manipulation as attacks
: Training data
manipulation can be used to compromise the integrity of an ML
system. Such attacks are known as
data poisoning attacks
and
can be categorized into
untargeted attacks

[
32
,
33
,
34
]
,

subpopulation attacks

[
35
]
,

targeted attacks

[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
15
]
,
and
backdoor attacks

[
36
,
37
,
23
]
. Untargeted
attacks decrease the testing accuracy of the learned model for many
inputs, while subpopulation (or targeted) attacks cause the learned
model to misclassify an attacker-chosen group of inputs (or a single

target input
) as an attacker-chosen
target label
.
Clean-label backdoor attacks mislead the trained model to classify inputs
patched with an attack-chosen
trigger
as a
target label
by inserting perturbations
into the training data without necessarily changing their labels.
Targeted attacks (or backdoor attacks) do not affect the testing accuracy of the learned
model for the non-target inputs (or normal inputs), which makes it harder to be detected
than the other two categories of attacks. We show in

Sec.
5
that Mendata can be applied to defend against
targeted attacks and backdoor attacks.

Training-data manipulation as defenses
: Training data
manipulation can also be used as defenses to protect data
contributors
[
38
,
6
,
39
]
.
Data tracing

[
6
]
, which proactively detects
(unauthorized) data use in model training, is one such representative
use case. In particular, a data contributor carefully
marks/manipulates her data so that a model learned using her marked
data (perhaps without her authorization) can be detected as such. For
instance,
Radioactive Data

[
6
]
, the state-of-the-art data
tracing method, carefully marks a subset of the training inputs such
that the parameters of a model learned on the marked training data
have a certain bias, which can be detected by hypothesis testing with
statistical guarantees. In
Sec.
6
, we apply our Mendata as an attack to defeat data tracing.

2.2
Countering Training-Data Manipulation

Prevention
: A prevention countermeasure against
training-data manipulation aims to redesign the ML training algorithm
so that the learned model is guaranteed to be free of the hidden
properties even if some training examples are arbitrarily
manipulated. For instance, the learned model is guaranteed to not
misclassify the target input as the target label to prevent targeted
data poisoning attacks. Examples of prevention methods include

randomized smoothing

[
7
,
8
,
9
]
,
differential
privacy

[
40
,
10
,
11
]
,

majority vote

[
12
,
13
,
14
]
,

and reweighting

[
41
,
15
,
42
,
43
,
44
]
. Randomized
smoothing adds random noise to the training inputs when learning a
model; differential privacy adds random noise to the gradients used to
update the model during training; and majority vote trains multiple
models on different subsets of the training data
[
12
,
13
]
or leverages
the intrinsic majority vote mechanism in the nearest neighbor
algorithm
[
14
]
. However, these prevention methods require modifying
the ML training algorithm and often sacrifice model accuracy.
Reweighting methods dynamically reweight training samples during training but most of them
[
41
,
42
,
43
,
44
]
are
only effective against dirty-label backdoor attacks
[
29
,
26
,
45
]
and some simple
clean-label backdoor attacks
[
36
,
46
]
.

Detection
: A detection mechanism
[
16
,
47
]
aims to detect the clean
training data in the manipulated dataset and only use those data for
model training. For instance, Peri et al.
[
16
]
proposed a

k
-Nearest Neighbors (
k
-NN) based method,
which is state-of-the-art detection method against clean-label
training-data manipulation. Specifically,
k
-NN detects a
training example as clean if its label is the same as the majority of
its
k
nearest neighbors. Other studies
[
18
,
17
,
48
,
49
]
proposed
detection methods for dirty-label training-data manipulation such as
backdoor attacks. These detection
methods are not applicable for clean-label training-data manipulation,
which is the focus of this work, because they rely on the incorrect
labels of the manipulated training inputs.

Purification
: Purification aims to purify the training
data detected as manipulated, so the training data detected as clean
by a detector and the purified version of those detected as
manipulated can be used together for model training. Unfortunately,
purifying manipulated training data is largely unexplored. To the best
of our knowledge,
Friendly Noise

[
19
]
is the only
training-data purification method. Specifically, Friendly Noise aims
to add as large perturbations to the training inputs as possible
without changing their predictions made by a model pre-trained on the
original manipulated training data. However, the model learned on the
original manipulated training data is different from the one learned
on the purified training data. As a result, Friendly Noise achieves a
suboptimal trade-off between model accuracy and effectiveness at
eliminating the hidden properties from the learned model, as shown in
our experimental results.

Some methods
[
50
,
51
]
were proposed to purify
testing
inputs,
e.g., adversarial examples. These methods require a large amount of

clean
training data to learn generative models (e.g., diffusion
models
[
52
]
), which are subsequently used to purify testing
inputs. They are not applicable in our scenario where the training
data is manipulated.

3
Problem Formulation

3.1
System Model

We consider a general machine-learning pipeline with three
entities: a
data contributor
, a
data curator
, and a

model trainer
.
We describe these three entities as follows.

Data contributor
: A data contributor holds a clean
dataset. The goal of the data contributor is to turn it into a
manipulated dataset using a
data manipulation algorithm
, such
that a model trained on the manipulated data has a certain hidden

property
. For instance, in data poisoning attacks
[
4
,
23
]
, the
property is that the trained model predicts an attacker-chosen label
for an attacker-chosen target input or inputs
with an attacker-chosen trigger; and in data tracing
[
6
]
, the
property is that the parameters of the trained model are biased in a
particular way such that the data contributor can detect her data was
used to train the model. We will describe more details about the data
manipulation algorithms and their desired properties for the trained
model in
Sec.
5.1
and
Sec.
6.1

when applying our technique to mitigate data poisoning attacks and
counter data tracing, respectively. The data contributor publishes her
manipulated dataset, e.g., posts it on the public Internet such as
social media websites. Note that the data manipulation algorithm
often preserves utility of the data, e.g., by adding
human-imperceptible perturbation to the data. Moreover, we assume the

data manipulation algorithm does not modify labels of the data
. This
is because the labels are often assigned by a data curator, which we
discuss next.

Data curator
: A data curator collects data from various
sources, e.g., the public Internet. We call the collected dataset

manipulated training dataset
and denote it as

\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{tr}}
. In particular, we assume the data contributor’s
manipulated dataset is collected by the data curator as a part of

\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{tr}}
. Moreover, the data curator pre-processes

\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{tr}}
as a
purified training dataset

\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{pf}}
and manually labels it if needed. The data
curator then sells
\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{pf}}
to model
trainers. Assuming such a data curator is realistic as there are many
of them in the real world, e.g., Argilla (
https://argilla.io
),
Cleanlab (
https://cleanlab.ai
), and Lightly
(
https://www.lightly.ai
).

We assume the data curator has a feature extractor
f
, which
outputs a feature vector for an input. For instance, the feature
extractor could be pre-trained on ImageNet
[
53
]
or a publicly available one
pre-trained using self-supervised learning; e.g., OpenAI makes its
CLIP feature extractor
[
54
]
pre-trained on 400 million image-text pairs
publicly available (
https://openai.com/research/clip
). Moreover,
we assume the data curator has access to a
reference dataset

\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{rf}}
. The data curator could obtain the
reference dataset
\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{rf}}
from a trustworthy source;
e.g., its employees could generate
\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{rf}}

themselves. Alternatively, the data curator can automatically detect a
subset of his
\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{tr}}
as
\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{rf}}
using
an existing detector.
\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{rf}}
does not need to be
completely clean due to the imperfection of the detector. However, we
assume that the fraction of manipulated inputs in

\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{rf}}
is much smaller than in

\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{tr}}
. Thus, a model trained based on

\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{rf}}
alone is less likely to have the property.
The data curator uses
\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{rf}}
as a reference to
purify the remaining untrusted dataset

\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{tr}}\setminus\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{rf}}
.

Model trainer
: A model trainer aims to train a model,
e.g., to deploy it as a cloud service or end-user application. Data
collection, labeling, and purification often require a large amount of
data, computation, and human resources, which may not be available to
a model trainer. Thus, we assume the model trainer obtains the
purified training data
\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{pf}}
from a data curator
and trains a model based on it, e.g., using a standard supervised
learning algorithm. In this work, we assume the model is a classifier,
which outputs a discrete label in the label set
\mathcal{Y}=\{1,2,\dots,\mathrm{Y}\}
for an input.

Though we distinguish between a data curator and a model trainer to
make our system setup more general, one party could play both roles.

3.2
Design Goals

We aim to design a
data purification framework
for the data
curator to purify his
\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{tr}}
as

\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{pf}}
. In particular, we aim to
achieve the following three design goals:

•

Property goal.
Recall that a data contributor
manipulates her data such that a model trained on her
manipulated data has some hidden property. Specifically, if the
data curator shares his manipulated training dataset

\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{tr}}
instead of the purified one

\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{pf}}
with a model trainer, the trained model
would have the property that the data contributor
desires. Therefore, the
property goal
means that a model
trained on the purified training dataset
\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{pf}}
does

not
have the data-contributor-desired property. For
instance, in the context of mitigating data poisoning attacks
performed by a malicious data contributor, the model trained on
the purified dataset should not misclassify the
data-contributor-chosen target input; and in the context of
countering data tracing, the data contributor cannot detect the
unauthorized use of her data in training the model.

•

Utility goal.
The purified training dataset is used to train
models by model trainers. A model trainer desires a more
accurate model. Therefore, the
utility goal
means that
the purified training dataset has good utility, so that a model trained
on it is as accurate as possible.

•

Generality goal.
A data contributor may use different
data manipulation algorithms to manipulate her data to achieve
different properties for the trained model; e.g., data poisoning
and data tracing represent two popular examples of data
manipulation algorithms and properties. Moreover, the data
manipulation algorithms and properties may be unknown to the
data curator and model trainer. Therefore, the
generality
goal
means that the data purification framework should be
agnostic to the data manipulation algorithm and
data-contributor-desired property.

In this work, we aim to design a data purification framework that
achieves these three goals. We only focus on image purification and
leave purification of other types of data as future work.

4
The Mendata Framework

To achieve the property goal, our data purification framework Mendata aims to perturb the unpurified data to overwhelm the perturbations
added by the data manipulation algorithm of the data contributor, so a
model trained on the purified data does not have the
data-contributor-desired property. A key challenge is how to perturb
the unpurified data to achieve both the property and utility
goals. For instance, one naive method is to add large perturbations to
the unpurified data such that they become random noise. This method
can achieve the property goal, but it substantially sacrifices the
utility of the purified data. To address this challenge, our key
observation is that the reference dataset achieves the property goal;
i.e., a model trained on the reference dataset is unlikely to have the
data-contributor-desired property, and so a dataset that is
distributed similarly to the reference dataset is likely to achieve
the property goal. Based on this observation, Mendata adds a bounded
perturbation (achieving the utility goal) to the unpurified data such
that the purified data has a feature distribution similar to the
reference data (achieving the property goal), where the features of an
input are extracted by the data curator’s feature extractor

f
. We consider feature distribution instead of the raw input
distribution because features are high-level summaries of an input and
are more likely to be relevant to the data-contributor-desired
property of a model.

Formally, we use the well-known Wasserstein distance to quantify the
similarity between two distributions and formulate finding such
perturbations to the unpurified data as an optimization
problem. However, it is challenging to solve the optimization problem
due to the complexity of the Wasserstein distance. To address this
challenge, we propose a two-step method to approximately solve the
optimization problem, which iteratively alternates between optimizing
the perturbations added to the unpurified data and approximating the
Wasserstein distance between the feature distributions of the current
purified data and reference data. Mendata achieves the generality
goal by not depending on any specific data manipulation algorithm and
data-contributor-desired property when formulating the optimization
problem and solving it.

4.1
Formulating an Optimization Problem

Formulating an optimization problem
: Given a manipulated
training dataset
\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{tr}}
, a reference dataset

\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{rf}}\subset\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{tr}}
, and a feature
extractor
f

untrusted
dataset

\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{ut}}=\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{tr}}\setminus\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{rf}}=\{x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n}\}
. Our goal is to add a perturbation

\delta_{j}
to each
x_{j}
such
that the purified untrusted dataset

\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{pe}}=\{x_{1}+\delta_{1},x_{2}+\delta_{2},\cdots,x_{n}+\delta_{n}\}
achieves
the property and utility goals. The purified training dataset

\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{pf}}
then consists of both

\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{rf}}
and
\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{pe}}
. We formulate
finding the perturbations

\{\delta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}

as the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\{\delta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}}

\displaystyle\quad\mathrlap{\mathsf{d}(\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{pe}},\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{rf}})+\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left\|{\delta_{j}}\right\|_{2}^{2}}

(1)

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad x_{j}+\delta_{j}\in[0,1]^{w}

\displaystyle\forall{j}=1,2,\cdots,n

(2)

where
\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{pe}}
and

\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{rf}}
are respectively the distributions of
the purified data and reference data in the feature space defined by
the feature extractor
f
;
\mathsf{d}
is a
distance metric between two distributions;
w
is the
number of dimensions of an input/perturbation; and

[0,1]^{w}
is the domain of an input, e.g., the
image domain with pixel values normalized in the range

[0,1]
. Both distributions
\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{pe}}
and

\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{rf}}
are defined in the feature space

\mathcal{V}
. Specifically, if we sample an input
x
from the
purified dataset (or the reference dataset) uniformly at random, its
feature vector
f(x)
has a probability

\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{pe}}(f(x))
(or

\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{rf}}(f(x))
) under the
distribution
\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{pe}}
(or

\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{rf}}
).

The first term aims to achieve the property goal, while the second
term aims to achieve the utility goal. Specifically, the first term
aims to perturb the untrusted data such that the purified data has a
similar feature distribution with the reference data. Since a model
trained on the reference data is likely to achieve the property goal,
the purified data, whose features follow a similar distribution with
the reference data, is also likely to achieve the property goal. The
second term aims to minimize the perturbations added to the untrusted
data to preserve its utility. Our optimization problem essentially
achieves a trade-off between the property and utility goals, which can
be tuned by the hyperparameter
\lambda
.

Using Wasserstein distance as

\mathsf{d}(\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{pe}},\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{rf}})
: The distance

\mathsf{d}(\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{pe}},\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{rf}})
is a key component of Mendata.
Wasserstein distance offers an interpretable and smooth representation
of the distance between two distributions
[
21
]
. In particular,
Wasserstein distance between two distributions can be interpreted as
the minimum cost of moving a pile of earth in the shape of one
distribution to the other. Therefore, we choose Wasserstein distance
as
\mathsf{d}(\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{pe}},\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{rf}})
. Formally, the Wasserstein distance

\mathsf{d}(\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{pe}},\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{rf}})
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mathsf{d}(\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{pe}},\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{rf}})\triangleq\inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{pe}},\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{rf}})}\mathbb{E}_{(v^{\mathsf{pe}},v^{\mathsf{rf}})\sim\gamma}\left({\left\|{v^{\mathsf{pe}}-v^{\mathsf{rf}}}\right\|_{2}}\right),

(3)

where
\Pi(\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{pe}},\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{rf}})
is the set of all joint distributions
for a pair of random variables
(v^{\mathsf{pe}},v^{\mathsf{rf}})
in the feature space, whose
marginals are
\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{pe}}
and

\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{rf}}
, respectively;
\mathbb{E}\left({}\right)
means
expectation; and

(v^{\mathsf{pe}},v^{\mathsf{rf}})\sim\gamma

means that

(v^{\mathsf{pe}},v^{\mathsf{rf}})

follows the joint distribution
\gamma
.

Approximating the Wasserstein distance

\mathsf{d}(\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{pe}},\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{rf}})
: It is a well-known challenge to
compute the Wasserstein distance

\mathsf{d}(\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{pe}},\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{rf}})
exactly. In particular, in our problem,
the two distributions do not have analytical forms, making it even
more challenging. To address the challenge, we approximate the
Wasserstein distance in a computationally efficient way. First,
according to the Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality
[
22
]
, the
Wasserstein distance can be represented as follows:

\displaystyle\mathsf{d}(\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{pe}},\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{rf}})

\displaystyle\quad\quad=\sup_{{h}\in H}\mathbb{E}_{v^{\mathsf{pe}}\sim\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{pe}}}\left({h(v^{\mathsf{pe}})}\right)-\mathbb{E}_{v^{\mathsf{rf}}\sim\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{rf}}}\left({h(v^{\mathsf{rf}})}\right),

(4)

where
H
is the set of all
1
-Lipschitz functions in the
feature space. A function
h:\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
a
1
-Lipschitz function
[
55
]
if it satisfies the following:

\displaystyle\left\|{h(v)-h(v^{\prime})}\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|{v-v^{\prime}}\right\|_{2}

for all
v,v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}
.
Note that
H
includes all possible
1
-Lipschitz
functions, which makes it still hard to compute

\mathsf{d}(\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{pe}},\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{rf}})
using

Eq. (
4
)
. To address the challenge, we
parameterize a
1
-Lipschitz function as a neural network (we use a
two-layer neural network in our experiments) and we approximate

H
as
H_{\vartheta}
that consists of all

1
-Lipschitz two-layer neural networks. We use a simple two-layer
neural network because the function is defined in the feature space
and to make our method more efficient. Then, we approximate

\mathsf{d}(\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{pe}},\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{rf}})
as follows using

H_{\vartheta}
:

\displaystyle\mathsf{d}(\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{pe}},\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{rf}})\approx\max_{h\in H_{\vartheta}}\mathbb{E}_{v^{\mathsf{pe}}\sim\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{pe}}}\left({h(v^{\mathsf{pe}})}\right)-\mathbb{E}_{v^{\mathsf{rf}}\sim\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{rf}}}\left({h(v^{\mathsf{rf}})}\right).

The expectation
\mathbb{E}_{v^{\mathsf{pe}}\sim\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{pe}}}\left({h(v^{\mathsf{pe}})}\right)
can be further
approximated by the average function value of
h
for the
perturbed data, i.e.,

\frac{1}{\left|{\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{pe}}}\right|}\sum_{x\in\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{pe}}}h(f(x))
, which we denote
as
\mathsf{Avg}(h,\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{pe}})
.
Similarly,
\mathbb{E}_{v^{\mathsf{rf}}\sim\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{rf}}}\left({h(v^{\mathsf{rf}})}\right)
can be approximated as

\mathsf{Avg}(h,\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{rf}})
. Thus, we have

\mathsf{d}(\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{pe}},\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{rf}})\approx\max_{h\in H_{\vartheta}}\mathsf{Avg}(h,\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{pe}})-\mathsf{Avg}(h,\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{rf}})
.

Refining the optimization problem
: After approximating

\mathsf{d}(\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{pe}},\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{rf}})
, we can reformulate

Eq. (
1
)
as the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\{\delta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}}

\displaystyle\max_{h\in H_{\vartheta}}\mathsf{Avg}(h,\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{pe}})-\mathsf{Avg}(h,\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{rf}})+\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left\|{\delta_{j}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

(5)

s.t.

\displaystyle x_{j}+\delta_{j}\in[0,1]^{w}\quad\quad\forall j=1,2,\cdots,n.

(6)

Note that the reformulated optimization problem is a min-max one with
an inner maximization optimization and an outer min optimization.

4.2
Solving the Optimization Problem

It is challenging to exactly solve the min-max optimization problem in

Eqns. (
5
)–(
6
)
due to the
complexity of the neural network
h
. To address this challenge,
we propose an iterative two-step solution to approximately solve it,
which is shown in
Alg. (
1
)
. Specifically, we first
initialize each perturbation
\delta_{j}
as

0^{w}
and
h
as a random neural network. Then,
we iteratively alternate between Step I and Step II, where Step I aims
to solve the inner max problem to update
h
and Step II aims to
solve the outer min problem to update the perturbations.

Step I: Solve the inner max problem
: Given the current
perturbations, Step I solves the inner max problem to update

h
. Formally, the inner max problem is as follows:

\max_{h\in H_{\vartheta}}\ell(h)\triangleq\mathsf{Avg}(h,\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{pe}})-\mathsf{Avg}(h,\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{rf}})
. We use a gradient ascent based method to
iteratively solve the max problem. Specifically, in each iteration, we
sample a mini-batch of
\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{pe}}
and a mini-batch of

\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{rf}}
to estimate
\ell(h)
; and
then we use the Adam optimizer
[
56
]
to update

h
. However, a key challenge is that
h
should be a

1
-Lipschitz function, i.e.,

h\in H_{\vartheta}
. The updated
h
in the
above iterative process does not necessarily guarantee that
h

is a
1
-Lipschitz function. To address this challenge, we add a
penalty term to the loss function
\ell(h)
, which is
inspired by prior work on approximating Wasserstein
distance
[
21
]
. The key idea of the penalty
term is to reduce the magnitude of the gradient of the function

h
with respect to its input, so the updated
h
is more
likely to be a
1
-Lipschitz
function.
Lines
11
–
16

in
Alg. (
1
)
correspond to calculating the penalty
term, where
\nu
is a penalty coefficient
used to balance
\ell(h)
and the penalty term.

We repeat the iterative process until the loss function

\ell(h)
converges. Specifically, we save the value of

\ell(h)
calculated using the entire

\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{pe}}
and
\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{rf}}
as a
checkpoint in every
t
steps. If the relative difference
between
\ell(h)
in the current checkpoint and the
average
\ell(h)
in the latest
5
checkpoints is less
than
1\%
, we stop the iterative process.

Step II: Solve the outer min problem
: Given the current

h
, Step II solves the outer min problem to update the
perturbations. Instead of updating each perturbation

\delta_{j}
, we only update those whose
corresponding purified inputs are far away from the centroid of the
distribution of the reference data, i.e., have high function values
under the current
h
. We do this to enhance the utility of the
purified data. Specifically, we select the
\rho
fraction
of inputs in the purified dataset that have the largest function
values under
h
.
For each selected purified input

x_{j}+\delta_{j}
, we update
its
\delta_{j}
. Specifically, for a
perturbation
\delta_{j}
, the inner min
problem becomes:
\min_{\delta_{j}}h(f(x_{j}+\delta_{j})).
We use gradient
descent to update
\delta_{j}
for one
iteration (
Line
24

in
Alg. (
1
)
).

Stopping criteria, perturbation amplification, and
projection
: We alternate between Step I and Step II until a stopping
criteria is satisfied. Specifically, we stop the iterative process
when the number of rounds reaches a predefined maximum number of
rounds (we use 30 in experiments) or the objective function values of
the min-max problem in the latest 5 rounds are all larger than the
minimal objective function value seen so far. After stopping the
iterative process, we amplify each perturbation
(
Line
29
) to better
achieve the property goal
[
57
]
. Finally, we clip the perturbation such that
each purified input is within the legitimate domain

[0,1]^{w}

(
Line
30
).

5
As a Defense Against Data Poisoning

5.1
Clean-Label Data Poisoning

Generally, clean-label data
poisoning
[
3
,
4
,
23
]
aims to
perturb a subset of training inputs without changing their semantics
such that a model trained on a correctly labeled dataset containing
the perturbed inputs would misclassify an attacker-chosen input
(called the
target input
) or any input
with a attacker-chosen patch (called
trigger
) into an attacker-chosen, incorrect
label (called the
target label
). A data manipulation algorithm
in this context takes a labeled training dataset, a target input

x^{\prime}
(or a trigger
z
), and a target label
y^{\prime}
as input and outputs a poisoned training dataset. The data-contributor-desired
property is that a model trained on the poisoned training dataset
should predict
y^{\prime}
for
x^{\prime}
(
targeted attack
) or
predict any input with the trigger
z
for
y^{\prime}
(
backdoor attack
).
We consider both
targeted attack
and
backdoor attack
in this work.

In particular, a bounded perturbation is added to a subset of training
inputs to preserve their semantics. The key challenge of data
poisoning is what perturbations should be added to the training
inputs. This challenge is often addressed by formulating finding the
perturbations as a bi-level optimization problem
[
32
,
58
]
, where the
inner optimization problem is to minimize the training loss (i.e.,
inner objective function) of a model on the poisoned training dataset,
while the outer optimization problem is to minimize the loss (i.e.,
outer objective function) of the model for the
(
x^{\prime}
,
y^{\prime}
) pair in
targeted attack

(or (
z
,
y^{\prime}
) pair in
backdoor attack
).
Different data poisoning attacks use different heuristics to approximately solve the
bi-level optimization problem. For instance,
Gradient
Matching

[
4
,
23
]
, the state-of-the-art clean-label data poisoning attack,
uses a gradient based method to iteratively solve the bi-level
optimization problem; and in each iteration, it aims to update the
perturbations to maximize the alignment between the gradient of the
inner objective function and that of the outer objective
function.
Gradient Matching
adds perturbations within a ball
of radius
\epsilon
in the infinity norm to a subset of
training inputs. In our experiments, we use

Gradient Matching

[
4
,
23
]
to manipulate data.

Selecting reference data
: Mendata requires a detector to detect a reference dataset

\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{rf}}
from a poisoned training dataset

\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{tr}}
. We consider both
simulated detectors

and a
real-world detector
for a comprehensive evaluation of
Mendata in different scenarios. A simulated detector enables us to
explicitly control the performance of the detector and thus the
quality of the reference dataset, which makes it possible to
understand the performance of Mendata in different scenarios. A
real-world detector is a state-of-the-art detector that is currently
available to a data curator, which enables us to understand the
performance of Mendata as of now.

•

Simulated detectors.
We characterize a simulated detector
using its
precision

q
and
recall

r
. Precision is the
fraction of inputs in the detected reference dataset

\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{rf}}
that are unmanipulated, while recall is the
fraction of unmanipulated inputs in the poisoned training dataset

\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{tr}}
that are detected to be in

\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{rf}}
. Given a precision
q
and recall

r
, we uniformly sampled a subset of size

\lfloor{\left|{\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{tr}}}\right|\times 0.99\times r\times\frac{1-q}{q}}\rfloor

from the poisoned/manipulated inputs and a subset of size

\lfloor{\left|{\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{tr}}}\right|\times 0.99\times r}\rfloor
from the
unmanipulated inputs, and merged them to create

\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{rf}}
. Note that 0.99 appears in these
calculations because the fraction of poisoned/manipulated inputs is
1%. When the precision of this detector is
1.0
, this simulates the
scenario where reference data is collected from trusted sources.
Note that 1% of the training data is poisoned. Therefore, we consider
the precision
q>0.99
so that the simulated detector is better
than the random detector that selects the reference data from the poisoned
training dataset uniformly at random.

•

Real-world detectors.
This type of detector represents an

6
As an Attack on Data Tracing

6.1
Radioactive Data

Generally, data tracing is a technique used to modify (“mark”) a
subset of training inputs such that a data contributor can detect
whether her marked training inputs were used to train a given
model. The modifications are carefully crafted such that the
parameters of a model trained on the marked training data have
property that can be detected by the data contributor. We take

Radioactive Data

[
6
]
, the state-of-the-art data tracing
method, as an example to illustrate the key idea of data
tracing.

Manipulating data
: Radioactive Data assumes the data
contributor marks a subset of the training inputs based on a feature
extractor
f^{{\mathsf{mk}}}
called the
marking feature
extractor
. Specifically, the data contributor selects a random unit
vector
u_{i}
for each class

i=1,2,\cdots,\mathrm{Y}
. The unit vector
u_{i}

has the same number of dimensions as the feature vector output by

f^{{\mathsf{mk}}}
. The unit vectors

\{u_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\mathrm{Y}}
are called
“marks”. These marks are used to guide how to modify the training
inputs. In particular, for each selected training input
x
to be
marked in class
i
, Radioactive Data aims to add a
modification
\zeta
to
x
such that 1) the feature
vector of
x+\zeta
, i.e.,

f^{{\mathsf{mk}}}(x+\zeta)
, is similar to
the mark
u_{i}
; 2)
x+\zeta
and

x
have similar feature vectors; 3)
x+\zeta

is close to
x
with respect to their pixel values; and 4)

\left\|{\zeta}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon
. (2),
(3), and (4) aim to preserve the utility of the marked training
inputs, while (1) aims to make a model trained on the marked training
data have a property that the data contributor can detect with
a statistical guarantee, which we discuss next.

Detecting property
: Suppose a model is trained on a
training dataset, a subset of which was marked by Radioactive Data. To
illustrate the data-contributor-detectable property of the model and
how to detect it, we assume for simplicity that the model uses the
same marking feature extractor and adds a linear layer on top of it.
When a model does not use the marking feature extractor (this is the
case in our experiments), the data contributor can decompose it into a
feature extractor and a linear layer, align the two feature extractors
by applying a transformation of the second feature extractor, and then
detect the property after alignment
[
6
]
.

We denote the parameters of the added linear layer for class

{i}
as
\theta_{i}
. If the model does

not
use the marked data for training, then

\theta_{i}
is not statistically related to the mark

u_{i}
. Since a mark
u_{i}
is picked
uniformly at random, the cosine similarity between

\theta_{i}
and
u_{i}
follows a prior
distribution, in particular an incomplete beta distribution
[
62
]
.
When the model uses the marked data for training, then

\theta_{i}
is statistically related to the mark

u_{i}
. In particular,
\theta_{i}
would
be similar to
u_{i}
since many training inputs (i.e., the
marked ones) in class
i
have feature vectors
similar to
u_{i}
. As a result, the cosine similarity
between
\theta_{i}
and
u_{i}
would
significantly deviate from the prior incomplete beta distribution.

Formally, Radioactive Data uses hypothesis testing to detect whether
this cosine similarity significantly deviates from the prior
distribution and combines hypothesis testing for the
\mathrm{Y}

classes to obtain a result. If the testing favors the hypothesis that
the cosine similarity is not from the prior incomplete beta
distribution under a significance level (e.g., a
p
-value with

p<0.05
used in both Radioactive Data and our experiments),
then the data contributor concludes that her marked data was used to
train the model. When applying our Mendata to attack data tracing,
the goal is to purify the marked training data such that the data
contributor cannot detect the use of her data, i.e., the

p
-value in the hypothesis testing is no smaller than 0.05.

7
Discussion

7.1
Adaptive Training-Data manipulation

Mendata minimizes the distance between the distributions of the
untrusted data and that of the reference data. In response, a data
contributor might constrain the manipulation such that the
distribution of the manipulated data is closer to that of the
unmanipulated data, causing Mendata to minimally perturb the training
inputs since the distribution distance is already small. To test such
a strategy, we considered an adaptive data contributor that tunes
the bound of the manipulation (
\epsilon
). A smaller
manipulation bound (
\epsilon
) makes the distribution of
the manipulated data more similar to the one of the reference data. To
implement such adaptive strategies, we adapted data poisoning
(described in
Sec.
5
) by changing

\epsilon
from
16
(the default) to
4
, and data tracing
(described in
Sec.
6
) by changing
\epsilon
from
10
(the default) to
2
. Our experimental results (see

Table
24
in
App.
G
) show that when
\epsilon
was
tuned down, Mendata was slightly degraded but was still effective to
remove the hidden properties without significantly affecting the
utility of the data. However, at the same time, the decrease of

\epsilon
also reduced effectiveness of training-data
manipulation. The study demonstrates the robustness of Mendata to
the adaptive training-data manipulation. Details of this study are shown in
App.
G
.

7.2
Ethical Statement

Mendata can be used as an attack on data tracing methods such as

Radioactive Data
. We believe that our work will benefit the
security community in this area by providing a tool to test the
robustness of data tracing methods in the future.

8
Conclusion

In this work, we propose Mendata, a general framework to purify
manipulated data. We find that, via adding carefully crafted
perturbations to the potentially manipulated training inputs, we can
make the model learned on the purified training data free of hidden
properties intended by the data contributor, while retaining the
accuracy of the learned model. Given a reference dataset, finding
such perturbations can be formulated as a min-max optimization
problem, which can be approximately solved by a two-step method.
Mendata achieves good performance as long as the detector used to
detect the reference data provides high precision, though it need not
be perfect. We demonstrated the generality of Mendata using two
representative use cases, namely defending against data poisoning and
data tracing. Interesting future work includes applying Mendata into other
types of data as well as
developing purification methods with formal guarantees.

Appendix C
Baselines and Their Implementation Details

Random Noise (
\textsc{Rand}(\sigma)
)
: This method constructs perturbations that are sampled from a
Gaussian distribution.
\textsc{Rand}(\sigma)

adds perturbation into the images from the poisoned training dataset
excluding the reference data (i.e.,
\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{ut}}=\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{tr}}\setminus\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{rf}}
).
Here
\sigma
denotes the
standard deviation of a Gaussian distribution. Specifically, when
adding perturbation to an image, this method adds a random noise
sampled from a Gaussian distribution with mean 0 and standard
deviation
\sigma
to each pixel value of the image. We
explored
\sigma\in[16,\infty)
.

Friendly Noise (
\textsc{Frien}(\alpha)
)
[
19
]
: This method constructs a perturbation that is maximized
within a ball of radius
\alpha
in the infinity norm
but that, when added to the data, does not substantially change the
model’s output. Specifically, the method first trains a model

g{}
on
\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{tr}}
.
Then, for an input

x
,
Friendly Noise
purifies it by adding a perturbation

\xi
to it, where
\xi
is constructed by solving the following
optimization problem:
\min_{\xi:\left\|{\xi}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\alpha}D_{KL}\big({g{(}x+\xi)}\parallel{g{(}x)}\big)-\mu\left\|{\xi}\right\|_{2}
,
where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and

\mu\in\mathbb{R}
are two hyperparameters
controlling the magnitude of the perturbation, and

D_{KL}\big({g{(x+\xi)}}\parallel{g{(}x)}\big)
denotes the KL-divergence
between
g{(x+\xi)}
and

g{(}x)
, which are model outputs for
x+\xi
and
x
, respectively.

We denote the
Friendly Noise
method as

\textsc{Frien}(\alpha)
that purifies the inputs in

\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{ut}}
.
We followed the previous
work
6
6

6

https://github.com/tianyu139/friendly-noise

in the implementation of
\textsc{Frien}(\alpha)
. We set

\mu=1
and
explored different options of
\alpha\in[16,\infty)
.

k
-NN
[
16
]
:
See the description of
k
-NN detection
[
16
]
in
App.
B
.
In the implementation of
k
-NN detection, we set
k
as
5000
for
CIFAR-10 and
500
for CIFAR-100 or TinyImageNet.

FrienB

[
19
]
:

FrienB
adds friendly noises to all the trainining data before model training and introduces
Bernoulli noises
\xi\in\{-\varkappa,\varkappa\}
to
augment training samples during model training. As such,
FrienB
is a
combination of purification and prevention.
FrienB
is considered as the state-of-the-art defense method against
clean-label data poisoning attacks
[
4
,
23
]
. The construction of friendly noise is described in
the method
\textsc{Frien}(\alpha)
. The Bernoulli noises are sampled from
a Bernoulli distribution and are added to each training batch in model training. We followed
the previous work
[
19
]
to implement this method,
where we set
\alpha=16
,
\varkappa=16
, and the other hyperparameters to the default values.

Epic

[
15
]
:

Epic
is a robust learning algorithm where the training set is dynamically updated by removing samples located
in low-density gradient space during the learning process. Specifically, the basic idea of
Epic
is designed as follows:
after
\varpi
epochs of model training, for every
\digamma
epochs, it searches for
\varsigma\times\left|{\mathrm{D}^{\mathsf{tr}}}\right|
most centrally located
training samples, known as
medoids
, in the gradient domain, assigns the other samples into these medoids, and then removes
the medoids that no other sample is assigned to, from the training set. As such, the training set keeps being updated during model training
where the effective poisoned data is removed.
Epic
has been demonstrated to be effective against
Gradient Matching
-based
clean-label targeted poisoning attack and clean-label backdoor attack
[
15
,
19
]
. We followed the previous
work
7
7

7

https://github.com/YuYang0901/EPIC/tree/master
to implement
Epic
. As suggested by the authors of
Epic
, we set

\varpi=1
,
\digamma=5
, and
\varsigma=0.2
. We set the other hyperparameters to the default values.

ASD

[
44
]
:

ASD
is a robust learning algorithm that dynamically splits samples from the training set to learn. It includes
3
steps:
at the first step when the number of epochs is less than
\digamma_{1}
, starting from
10
clean samples per class as the clean set,
for every
5
epochs, it moves
10
samples with the lowest symmetric cross-entropy loss per class from the remaining training set to the clean set and leaves
the remaining as the polluted set;
at the second step when the number of epochs is less than
\digamma_{2}
, it adds
50\%
samples of the entire training set with the lowest loss
into the clean set and leaves the remaining as the polluted set; at the third step when the number of epochs is less than
\digamma_{3}
,
it applies a meta-split method to detect the hard clean samples from the polluted set. While the clean set and polluted set are
dynamically changed, the model is updated by being supervisedly trained on the clean set and semi-supervisedly trained on the polluted set.
ASD
has been demonstrated
as a powerful defense against dirty-label backdoor attacks. We followed the previous work
8
8

8

https://github.com/KuofengGao/ASD/tree/main
to implement it,
where
\digamma_{1}=60
,
\digamma_{2}=90
, and
\digamma_{3}=120
. We set the other hyperparameters to the default values.

CBD

[
43
]
:

CBD
is a learning algorithm inspired by causal inference
[
64
]
. It firstly trained a backdoored model on the poisoned dataset for
5
epochs.
Then a clean model is trained for
100
epochs by reweighting the training samples such that the clean model is independent of the backdoored model in the hidden space.

CBD
is a recently proposed powerful defense against dirty-label backdoor attacks. We followed the previous work
9
9

9

https://github.com/zaixizhang/CBD
to
implement
CBD
, where we set the hyperparameters to the default values.

Appendix G
Adaptive Training Data Manipulation

We considered a setting where the data contributor knows the deployment of Mendata.
As such, the data contributor applies adaptive training data manipulation strategies by
tuning the perturbation bound (
\epsilon
). Specifically,
the data contributor tunes down
\epsilon
such that the manipulated data is similar
to the unmanipulated one to degrade the performance of Mendata. In this study, we investigate the effectiveness
of Mendata on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 when
\epsilon
of the manipulation is changed from
16
to
4
for data poisoning
and from
10
to
2
for data tracing. To implement Mendata, we used a simulated detector with
q=1.0
and

r=0.1
to select reference data, and set the same hyperparameters as those used in
Sec.
5
and
Sec.
6
.

The experimental results from adaptive manipulation techniques are shown
in
Table
24
, where we compared the performances of Mendata with those from no purification applied. When
\epsilon
is tuned down from
16
to
8

in adaptive data poisoning, our proposed method is degraded slightly by the adaptive strategies
but still effective to defend against the attacks. When
\epsilon
continues to decrease to

4
, the effectiveness of the attacks is diminished and thus our proposed method can easily
remove the manipulation from data poisoning. We have similar observations from the results
of adaptive data tracing. These results demonstrate the robustness of Mendata to the adaptive
strategies.
