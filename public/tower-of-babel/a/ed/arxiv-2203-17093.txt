Electron scattering of light new particles from evaporating primordial black holes
Roberta Calabrese, Marco Chianese, Damiano F.G. Fiorillo, Ninetta Saviano

Abstract
Primordial black holes are a possible component of dark matter, and a most promising way of investigating them is through the product of their Hawking evaporation. As a result of this process, any species lighter than the Hawking temperature is emitted, including possible new particles beyond the Standard Model. These can then be detected in laboratory-based experiments via their interaction with the Standard Model particles. In a previous work, we have first proposed and studied this scenario in the presence of an interaction between the light new species and nucleons. Here we extend this discussion to include the case of interaction with electrons. We show that the simultaneous presence of primordial black holes and species lighter than about 100 100 MeV can be constrained by the measurements of direct detection experiments, such as XENON1T, and water Cherenkov neutrino detectors, such as Super-Kamiokande. Our results provide a complementary and alternative way of investigation with respect to cosmological and collider searches.



I
Introduction

Primordial black holes (PBHs) are hypothetical black holes formed due to gravitational collapse of over-density fluctuations in the very early Universe
Zel’dovich and Novikov 1967
;
Harrison 1970
. Different from astrophysical black holes, which must have sufficiently large masses in order to encounter the instability leading to their formation, PBHs can be produced with any mass larger than about 0.1 g
Carr et al. 2021
, because of the different equation of state involved. From the cosmological point of view, PBHs behave as a component of dark matter (DM). The possibility of observing PBHs with masses between
5\times 10^{14}
and
10^{18}
g is mainly connected with the phenomenon of Hawking evaporation
Hawking 1974
;
Hawking 1975
;
Zel’dovich 1976
;
Carr 1976
;
Page 1976a
;
Page 1976b
;
Page 1977
;
MacGibbon and Webber 1990
;
MacGibbon 1991
, namely the emission of particles induced by the PBHs gravitational field. The nonobservation of any production that could be connected with Hawking evaporation has allowed to severely constrain the contribution of PBHs with masses
M_{\rm PBH}\lesssim 10^{17}
g to the dark matter content of the Universe
Calabrese et al. 2021
;
Coogan et al. 2021
;
Dasgupta et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2021
;
Iguaz et al. 2021
. In different mass ranges, the amount of PBHs can further be constrained by different means, see Ref.
Carr et al. 2021
for a review.

Hawking evaporation produces any species that is gravitationally interacting. This includes also any elementary particle beyond the Standard Model with a mass below the Hawking temperature. In particular, if DM is dominated by a sufficiently light particle
\chi
(with
m_{\chi}\lesssim 100
MeV), it can be emitted by PBHs. In Ref.
Calabrese et al. 2022
, we proposed this scenario as a possible source of experimental signatures in direct detection experiments
Agnes et al. 2015
;
Aalseth et al. 2018
;
Aprile et al. 2012
;
Aprile et al. 2019a
;
Meng et al. 2021
;
Akerib et al. 2020
;
Bueno 2006
;
Fiorillo et al. 2008
;
Marchionni et al. 2011
;
Amaudruz et al. 2016
;
Angloher et al. 2005
;
Agnes et al. 2015
;
Angloher et al. 2016
;
Aalbers et al. 2016
;
Agnese et al. 2017
;
Aprile et al. 2018
;
Akerib et al. 2020
;
Aprile et al. 2019b
;
Angloher et al. 2017
;
Abdelhameed et al. 2019
;
Aprile et al. 2020a
;
Adhikari et al. 2021
, due to the DM-nucleon interaction. We showed that the DM emitted by PBHs would provide a signal qualitatively similar to the DM boosted by cosmic-ray interactions
Cappiello et al. 2019
;

;
Ema et al. 2019
;

Given our ignorance of the interactions of DM with the Standard Model, a natural question is what are the corresponding constraints if DM interacts only with electrons
Essig et al. 2012a
;
Essig et al. 2012b
;
Essig et al. 2017
;
Crisler et al. 2018
;
Agnes et al. 2018
;
Aguilar-Arevalo et al. 2019
;
Andersson et al. 2020
. Here we aim to answer this question. Recently, the same subject has been discussed in Ref.
Li and Liao 2022
, where the authors apply our treatment discussed in Ref.
Calabrese et al. 2022
to the DM-electron interaction and obtain constraints from Super-Kamiokande
Kachulis et al. 2018
and XENON1T
Aprile et al. 2020b
measurements. However, the authors do not correctly account for the ionization of atoms that results from the DM-electron scattering. Rather, they assume electrons to be free. While this may be a reasonable approximation for Super-Kamiokande
Kachulis et al. 2018
;
Ema et al. 2019
;
Cappiello and Beacom 2019
, it is not the correct treatment for XENON1T
Lee et al. 2015
. For the same reason, the discussion of energy loss in the Earth is also not applicable to the DM-electron interaction, since the DM particle does not just lose energy, but rather performs a random walk traversing the Earth for sufficiently large cross sections
Cappiello and Beacom 2019
. Finally, the authors of Ref.
Li and Liao 2022
model the interaction between
\chi
and electrons with a differential cross section flat in the electron recoil energy, which may not be easily realized in a realistic particle model setting.

In the present work, which was developed independently at the same time as Ref.
Li and Liao 2022
, we approach the determination of the constraints with a correct treatment of the atom ionization for Xenon nuclei. We adopt an effective field theory for the DM-electron interaction, and we use it to deduce the constraints from Super-Kamiokande and XENON1T. Remarkably, the parameter space of the model is also affected by additional constraints, such as cosmological
Henning and Murayama 2012
;
Sabti et al. 2020
;
Sabti et al. 2021
and collider
Liang et al. 2021
ones, which are completely absent in Ref.
Li and Liao 2022
. The constraints that we draw are not necessarily connected with the assumption that the species emitted by PBHs is dark matter. Indeed, any light species is emitted by Hawking evaporation and can subsequently be detected if it has a coupling with electrons. We emphasize that, in order to reach Earth from very far distances, such a species should be stable on cosmological scales. Therefore, throughout the paper, we will refer to this species as a generic light new particle
\chi
, and will derive the constraints with no further assumptions.

We structure the discussion as follows. In Sec.
II
we compute the flux of the new fermionic particles
\chi
from the evaporation of the primordial black holes. In Sec.
III
we discuss the possible detection of
\chi
particles in two different categories of experiments: XENON1T and Super-Kamiokande. Then, we report in Sec.
IV
the constraints we obtain for the combined parameter space of primordial black holes and light new species. Finally, we draw our conclusions in Sec
V
.

II
Flux of light particles from Primordial Black Holes

In this section we derive the flux of a fermionic species
\chi
emitted from evaporating PBHs. PBHs are fully characterized by mass, charge, and spin. However, evaporating, they lose mass slower than they lose charge
Page 1976b
;
Zaumen 1974
;
Carter 1974
;
Gibbons 1975
;
Page 1976a
;
Page 1977
. For this reason, it is reasonable today to consider neutral PBHs that formed in the very early Universe. Moreover, we consider spinless PBHs since rotating PBHs evaporate faster causing a higher flux on Earth and leading to more stringent constraints. The radiation emitted by a single PBH of a mass
{\rm M}_{\rm PBH}
is characterized by a thermal blackbodylike spectrum defined by the Hawking temperature
\mathcal{T}_{\rm PBH}
. In the case of nonrotating and neutral PBHs, it takes the following expression
Hawking 1974
;
Hawking 1975

k_{B}\mathcal{T}_{\rm PBH}=\frac{\hbar c^{3}}{8\pi G_{\rm N}{\rm M}_{\rm PBH}}\approx 1.06\left[\frac{10^{16}~{\rm g}}{{\rm M}_{\rm PBH}}\right]\mathrm{MeV}\,,

(1)

where
k_{B}
,
G_{N}
and
\hbar
are the Boltzmann, the gravitational and the Planck constants, respectively and
c
is the speed of light. The differential spectrum of a single species
\chi
from an evaporating PBH is given by
Hawking 1974
;
Hawking 1975

\frac{{\rm d}N}{{\rm d}t{\rm d}T}=\frac{n_{\mathrm{dof}}^{\chi}\Gamma^{\chi}(T,\mathcal{T}_{\rm PBH})}{2\pi(e^{(T+m_{\chi})/\mathcal{T}_{\rm PBH}}+1)},\,

(2)

where
T
is kinetic energy of
\chi
,
m_{\chi}
is the mass and
n_{\mathrm{dof}}^{\chi}=4
is the number of degrees of freedom. The grey-body factor
\Gamma^{\chi}
takes into account the distortions in the black-body spectrum and it is provided by the
BlackHawk
code
Arbey and Auffinger 2019
;
Arbey and Auffinger 2021
. This spectrum is peaked at an energy approximately corresponding to
\sim 5
times the Hawking temperature and is kinematically suppressed if its mass is greater than such a value.

For clarity of exposition, we assume the PBH mass distribution to be monochromatic. However, the method can be easily generalized to mass distributions. We are able then to compute the
\chi
flux from the spectrum of primordial black holes. Two components contribute to the flux, one resulting from the total emission of Extra-Galactic PBHs (
\Phi_{\mathrm{EG}}
) and the other from Galactic (
\Phi_{\mathrm{G}}
) ones.
The Extra-Galactic contribution can be written as

\frac{{\rm d}\Phi_{\chi}^{\mathrm{EG}}}{{\rm d}T}=\int_{t_{\mathrm{min}}}^{t_{\rm max}}\differential{t}\left.\frac{\differential N}{\differential t\differential T}\right|_{E_{s}}\frac{f_{\rm PBH}\,\Omega_{\rm DM}\rho_{\mathrm{cr}}}{{\rm M}_{\rm PBH}}[1+z(t)],

(3)

where
z(t)
is the redshift and
E_{s}=\sqrt{(E^{2}_{\chi}-m_{\rm DM}^{2})(1+z(t))^{2}+m_{\rm DM}^{2}}
is the redshifted energy of
\chi
particles. The quantity
\Omega_{\rm DM}
is the cosmological dark matter density and
\rho_{\mathrm{cr}}
is the critical density. Indeed, since PBHs are dark matter candidates, it is convenient to refer to the PBHs abundance as the fraction of the dark matter content of the Universe:

f_{\rm PBH}=\frac{\rho_{\rm PBH}}{\rho_{\mathrm{DM}}}=\frac{\Omega_{\rm PBH}}{\Omega_{\mathrm{DM}}}\,,

(4)

where
f_{\rm PBH}\leq 1
. The integral in Eq. (
3
) is performed from the time of matter-radiation equality (
t_{\mathrm{min}}
) and the age of the Universe (
t_{\rm max}
) assuming that PBHs are not fully evaporated today. We emphasize that considering times smaller than
t_{\rm min}
would not affect the flux in a appreciable way. The Extra-Galactic component is assumed to be isotropic.

Concerning the Galactic component, this can be written as

\frac{\differential\Phi_{\chi}^{\mathrm{G}}}{\differential T}=\int\frac{\differential{\Omega}}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{l_{\rm max}}\differential{l}\frac{\differential N}{\differential t\differential T}\frac{f_{\rm PBH}\,\rho_{\rm NFW}(r(l,\phi))}{{\rm M}_{\rm PBH}},

(5)

where
\rho_{\mathrm{NFW}}(r)
is the standard Navarro-Frenk-White profile defined as
Navarro et al. 1997

\rho_{\mathrm{NFW}}(r)=\rho_{\odot}\,\left(\frac{r_{\odot}}{r}\right)\left(\frac{1+r_{\odot}/r_{s}}{1+r/r_{s}}\right)^{2}

(6)

where
\rho_{\odot}=0.4~{\rm GeV\,cm^{-3}}
is the local DM density,
r_{\odot}=8.5~{\rm kpc}
is the distance between the Sun and the Milky Way center, and
r_{s}=20~{\rm kpc}
is the scale radius. The galactocentric distance
r
is

r(l,\phi)=\sqrt{r_{\odot}^{2}-2lr_{\odot}\cos\phi+l^{2}}\,,\hskip 28.45274ptl_{\rm max}=\sqrt{r_{h}^{2}-r_{\odot}^{2}\sin^{2}\phi}+r_{\odot}\cos\phi\,.

(7)

where
r_{h}=200~{\rm kpc}
is the halo radius. We mention that since our analysis takes into account the
\chi
flux from the whole sky (with a sizeable contribution from Extra-Galactic PBHs), the exact choice of the galactic profile is not so relevant.

According to the previous Eq.s (
3
) and (
5
), the total flux of
\chi
particles is proportional to
f_{\rm PBH}/{\rm M}_{\rm PBH}
. Therefore, the lower the PBH mass, the higher the
\chi
flux. However, at lower masses the fraction
f_{\rm PBH}
is typically constrained to a very small value (
<<1
) as given in Ref.
Carr et al. 2021
, thus suppressing the
\chi
flux.

III
Detection of light particles

The possibility of detecting the light particle
\chi
is crucially dependent on its interactions with the Standard Model. In Ref.
Calabrese et al. 2022
we assumed that
\chi
interacts with nucleons, whereas here we focus on its interaction with electrons. For the sake of definiteness, we consider the coupling Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}_{\chi}=\frac{1}{\Lambda^{2}}\bar{\chi}\chi\,\bar{\ell}\ell\,.

(8)

This is a non-renormalizable effective field theory (EFT) coupling characterized by the energy scale
\Lambda
, and
\ell
refers to the Standard Model lepton fields. Such an interaction arises at the effective level from a scalar mediator with mass higher than the energies involved in the process, which in our case is at most
\sim 10
MeV. With this condition, the scalar mediator can be integrated out, leaving the effective four-fermions interaction of Eq. (
8
).

With this coupling, the
\chi
particles emitted by PBHs can be looked for in mainly two classes of experiments:

•

double phase dark matter direct detection experiments based on noble liquid technology
Agnes et al. 2015
;
Aalseth et al. 2018
;
Aprile et al. 2012
;
Aprile et al. 2019a
;
Meng et al. 2021
;
Akerib et al. 2020
;
Bueno 2006
;
Fiorillo et al. 2008
;
Marchionni et al. 2011
;
Amaudruz et al. 2016
;

•

neutrino experiments based on water Cherenkov
Aardsma et al. 1987
;
Aslanides et al. 1999
;
Bazarko 2001
;
Ahrens et al. 2002
;
Fukuda et al. 2003
.

In this paper, we consider XENON1T and Super-Kamiokande as representatives of the two classes, respectively. For both of them, we use the measured data to constrain the scenario of
\chi
particles emitted from evaporating PBHs. In this section, we first present the general framework for
\chi
interacting with the electrons bound to the atoms in the detector, and then specialize our discussion to each of the two experiments separately.

The particles
\chi
hit detector’s atoms interacting with electrons through the effective interaction of Eq. (
8
). The
\chi
-
e
scatterings would give rise to atom ionization with an outgoing non-relativistic free electron as

\chi+A\to\chi+A^{*}+e^{-},

(9)

where
A
denotes the atom, and
A^{*}
is the ionized atom. The key quantity characterizing the detection of the signal is the differential event rate
{\rm d}R_{\chi}/{\rm d}E_{r}
per unit recoil energy
E_{r}
, which corresponds to the free electron kinetic energy. It can be computed as:

\frac{\differential R_{\chi}}{\differential E_{r}}=n_{t}\,\eta(E_{r})\,F(E_{r})\,\int\differential{T}\,\frac{\differential\Phi_{\chi}}{\differential T}\sum_{n,\,l}\frac{\differential\sigma^{n,\,l}}{\differential E_{r}}(E_{r},m_{\chi},T).

(10)

Here, the quantity
n_{t}
is the number of detector’s targets per tonne and
\eta
is the detector’s efficiency. The quantity
F(E_{r})
is the Fermi factor that takes into account the distortion of the scattered electron wavefunction by the presence of the atom. In the non-relativistic limit, we have

F(E_{r})=\frac{2\pi\nu}{1-e^{-2\pi\nu}},

(11)

where
\nu=Z_{\rm eff}(\alpha m_{e}/\sqrt{2\,m_{e}\,E_{r}})
and
Z_{\rm eff}
is the effective charge that is felt by the scattered electron. In our analysis, we conservatively set
Z_{\rm eff}=1
.

1

In general
Z_{\rm eff}
is greater than one since the shielding of the escaping electron by the remaining bounded electrons is imperfect. In Ref.

Finally,
\differential\Phi_{\chi}/\differential T
is the differential
\chi

\differential\sigma^{n,l}/\differential E_{r}
is the differential cross section for scattering of a
\chi
particle on a bound electron with principal quantum number
n

l

E_{r}
. Denoting by
E_{b}^{n,\,l}
the electron binding energy of the atomic orbital
(n,l)

E_{\chi}-|E_{b}^{n,\,l}|=E_{\chi}^{\prime}+E_{r}

(12)

where
E_{\chi}=T+m_{\chi}
and
E_{\chi}^{\prime}
are the initial and final energy of
\chi

The strength of the
\chi
-
e
interaction can be parameterized in terms of the coupling at the Lagrangian level. However, it is more common to express it in terms of the cross section on a free electron at a fixed momentum transfer
\alpha m_{e}

\overline{\sigma}_{\chi e}=\frac{\mu_{\chi e}^{2}}{\pi\,\Lambda^{4}}\left(1+\frac{\alpha^{2}\,m_{e}^{2}}{4\,m_{\chi}^{2}}\right)\,

(13)

with
\alpha
being the fine-structure constant. This is the quantity that we aim to constrain. Before discussing how the analysis proceeds for the two experiments separately, it is worth noticing that the same
\chi
-
e
interaction would cause an attenuation effect of the
\chi
flux due to the propagation in the atmosphere and the Earth
Starkman et al. 1990
;
Mack et al. 2007
;
Kavanagh et al. 2017
;
Emken and Kouvaris 2018
. However, as shown by Ref.
Ema et al. 2019
;
Cappiello and Beacom 2019
the attenuation is negligible for
\overline{\sigma}_{\chi e}\lesssim 10^{-31}~{\rm cm^{2}}
. For this reason, we restrict our analysis to smaller cross sections only.

III.2
Super-Kamiokande’s event rate

Super-Kamiokande is a water Cherenkov detector realized by a
cylindrical tank filled with 50kt of water.
Charged particles in the water produce Cherenkov radiation that is recorded by the photo-multipliers.
The light particle
\chi
is also expected to yield Cherenkov radiation when scattering inside the detector. Super-Kamiokande has observed
N_{\rm SK}=4042
events in the recoil energy range between
[0.1-1.33]~{\rm GeV}

Kachulis et al. 2018
.

In the case of Super-Kamiokande, previous analyses assume the electrons to be free and at rest in the observer frame
Necib et al. 2017
;
Ema et al. 2019
;
Cho et al. 2020
;
Granelli et al. 2022
. In this work, we follow the same approach and assume the number of targets to be
n_{t}=3.34\times 10^{28}~{\rm ton^{-1}}
and the detector efficiency to be equal to 0.93 in the energy range considered
Kachulis et al. 2018
. In the limit of free electrons, we have
F(E_{r})=1
, and the differential cross section is simply given by

\frac{\differential\sigma}{\differential E_{r}}=\frac{\overline{\sigma}_{\chi e}\,\Theta(E_{\rm max}-E_{r})}{8\,\mu^{2}_{\chi e}\,\tilde{p}^{2}\left(1+\frac{\alpha^{2}\,m_{e}^{2}}{4\,m_{\chi}^{2}}\right)}(2m_{e}+E_{r})(2m_{\chi}^{2}+m_{e}E_{r})

(21)

where
E_{\rm max}
is the maximum allowed recoil energy equal to

E_{\rm max}=\frac{2\,m_{e}\,T\,(T+2\,m_{\chi})}{\left(\left(m_{e}+m_{\chi}\right)^{2}+2\,m_{e}\,T\right)}\,.

(22)

Plugging this equation into Eq. (
10
) we can compute the expected number of events in Super-Kamiokande provided by

V
Conclusions

In this paper, we have extended the work
Calabrese et al. 2022
where we proposed to test the potential existence of new light particles beyond the Standard Model through the evaporation of primordial black holes. In particular, we have focused on the possible interactions of such new particles with the electrons through an effective coupling mediated by a heavy scalar. Hence, we have investigated the consequent detection of
\chi
particles from evaporating primordial black holes in dark matter direct detection experiments, such as XENON1T, and neutrino detectors, such as Super-Kamiokande. The nonobservation of the expected signal has been used to set constraints on the combined parameter space of primordial black holes and light new particles. In the case of XENON1T, we have performed a binned likelihood analysis comparing the observed event rate with the one predicted in our scenario. We have properly taken into account the ionization process of Xe atoms due to
\chi
-
e
scattering. On the other hand, for Super-Kamiokande we have performed a more conservative analysis based on the total number of events detected. We have found that XENON1T (Super-Kamiokande) is able to constrain the
\chi
-
e
cross section down to
10^{-32}~{\rm cm^{2}}
(
10^{-35}~{\rm cm^{2}}
) for
m_{\chi}=1~{\rm MeV}
in case of PBH with masses from
5\times 10^{14}
to
10^{18}
g. These limits are complementary to cosmological and collider constraints and do not require the
\chi
particles to be dark matter.
