A Context-Aware Cutting Plane Selection Algorithm for Mixed-Integer Programming
Mark Turner, Timo Berthold, Mathieu Besançon

Abstract
The current cut selection algorithm used in mixed-integer programming solvers has remained largely unchanged since its creation. In this paper, we propose a set of new cut scoring measures, cut filtering techniques, and stopping criteria, extending the current state-of-the-art algorithm and obtaining a 5% performance improvement for SCIP over the MIPLIB 2017 benchmark set.



1
Introduction and Related Work

We focus on this paper on the selection of cutting planes, an essential component of the branch-and-cut framework, which is the main algorithmic paradigm to solve Mixed-Integer Linear Programs (MILP) classically defined as:

\displaystyle\underset{\mathbf{x}}{\mathrm{argmin}}\{\mathbf{c}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}\;\;|\;\;\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{b},\;\;\mathbf{l}\leq\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{u},\;\;\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{|\mathcal{J}|}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-|\mathcal{J}|}\}

(1)

A cut, parameterised by
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\beta)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, is an inequality
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}\leq\beta
that is violated by at least one solution of the LP relaxation but that does not increase the optimal value of the problem when added i.e. it is valid for (
1
).
It is used to tighten the Linear Programming (LP) relaxation of (
1
).
Cuts are generated in rounds, where we refer to the process of computing cuts, applying a subset of the computed cuts, and resolving the LP as a
separation round
. In general, cuts are cheap to compute, and more are computed than actually applied to the LP. The process of deciding which cuts to add to the LP is called
cut selection
, and is the focus of this paper, in which we introduce a new cut selection algorithm.

Cut selection was considered largely “solved", following computational results on a variety of MILP solvers
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
, in that cheap heuristic selection rules were sufficient for good performance. Recently this conclusion has been challenged, with largely machine learning-driven research attempting to extract further performance, see
[
4
]
for an overview.
Such research, however, is often limited in how it can be deployed in a MILP solver due to the complexity of deploying black-box predictors and their brittle generalisation.
In this paper we introduce a new cut selection algorithm, which revises the three major aspects of cut selection, namely cut scoring measures, cut filtering techniques, and stopping criteria.
We determine default parameter values of our algorithm by leveraging methodology
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
and software
[
8
]
from the algorithm configuration community.
Our algorithm and the accompanying default values will be integrated in the next release of SCIP
[
9
]
.

2
New Cut Selection Techniques

Our new cut selection algorithm expands and generalises the existing one from SCIP
[
9
]
in three aspects, namely, cut scoring, cut filtering, and stopping criteria.
For all three aspects, we incorporate additional information from the cut and from the current context in which the separation takes place.

2.1
Cut Scoring

The most studied aspect of cut selection is the scoring of cuts, which subsumes their ranking. This is both due to the large variety of scoring measures that exist, see
[
3
,
10
]
, and the common assumption of a fixed selection rule outside of scoring
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
. We introduce a new set of cut scoring measures that incorporate information from other non-cutting plane aspects of the MILP solving process.

2.1.1
Pseudo-cost Scoring

Pseudo-costs
[
14
]
, the dominant decision-maker in state-of-the-art MILP branching rules
[
15
]
, estimate scores for branching candidates based on the historical objective value improvement observed when branching on the candidates. It has recently been shown in
[
16
]
that cut scoring measures can complement pseudo-cost-based branching rules for improved solver performance. We address the reverse direction here, and use pseudo-costs to augment cut scores, where we let
\boldsymbol{\psi}(x_{i})
be the pseudo-cost of variable
x_{i}
.
We define the pseudo-cost scoring of a cut,
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\beta)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, as the expected objective change as predicted by the pseudo-costs and express it as follows:

\displaystyle\mathtt{psc}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\beta,\mathbf{x}^{LP}):=\sum\limits_{{i,\alpha_{i}\neq 0}}\boldsymbol{\psi}(x_{i})\left(|x^{LP}_{i}-\alpha_{i}\frac{\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}^{LP}-\beta}{\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|^{2}}|\right)

(2)

We normalise the result by the maximum pseudo-cost score of a cut in the separation round, so all pseudo-cost scores are in the range
[0,1]
.

2.1.2
Lock Scoring

Down-locks and up-locks, introduced in
[
1
]
, can be interpreted for a variable
x_{i}
, as the number of constraints that “block" the shifting of
x_{i}
in the direction of the variable’s lower or upper bound. Let
\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{-}_{i}
be the number of down-locks and
\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{+}_{i}
be the number of up-locks for variable
x_{i}
. We define the lock scoring of a cut,
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\beta)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, as:

\displaystyle\mathtt{loc}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i,\alpha_{i}\neq 0}({\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{-}_{i}+\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{+}_{i}})

(3)

As with the pseudo-cost measure, we normalise the result by the maximum among all cuts in the separation round so the scores are in the range
[0,1]
. Additionally, as it is initially unclear if we want to reward a cut featuring variables with many locks, i.e. further restrict heavily restricted variables, we also introduce the complement of this measure, i.e. promote cuts that restrict variables with few restrictions.

2.1.3
Sparsity Scoring

Sparsity refers to the fraction of zero coefficients in a cut. In general, dense cuts slow down LP solves
[
17
]
. We therefore introduce a score that promotes sparse cuts, which, given a cut
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\beta)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, and the parameters
\texttt{maxsps}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and
\texttt{endsps}\in[0,1]
, is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathtt{sps}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}):=\max\{\texttt{maxsps}-\frac{\texttt{maxsps}}{\texttt{endsps}}\cdot\;\|\alpha\|_{0},\;\;0\}

(4)

2.2
Cut Filtering

The standard approach in the literature for cut filtering is the parallelism-based approach
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. The approach selects the highest-scoring cut, removes any remaining cut from consideration that is too parallel to the selected cut, and then repeats until some stopping criteria is met or no more non-filtered cuts are left to be selected. Such a procedure was shown to be absolutely necessary for good performance in
[
1
,
2
]
, however that is only w.r.t. trivial selection approaches. We therefore present and discuss two other approaches for cut filtering.

2.2.1
Density Filtering

Density-based filtering, unlike parallelism-based filtering, is performed at the start of the selection process. It immediately removes any generated cut above a given density threshold. Preliminary results for such an approach already exist, see
[
10
,
18
]
, and suggest that
40\%
density, i.e. a cut with at most
40\%
of its entries being non-zero, is a potentially desirable setting.

2.2.2
Parallelism-Based Penalties

It is possible to simply impose a penalty on any cut parallel to previously-added ones as opposed to outright removing it from consideration. In our algorithm, this involves reducing the score of all remaining cuts considered parallel to a selected cut.
If the score falls below some threshold, which we set to 0, then the cut is removed from consideration.

2.3
Stopping Criteria

In general for computational studies, and MILP solvers by extension, there is a hard-coded limit on the number of cuts that can be added per round. Typically, this limit is not reached, with the standard parallelism filtering algorithm removing enough cuts by itself as detailed in Subsection
2.2
. As we are employing alternate filtering algorithms, however, we believe it is necessary to have an instance-dependent limit. We therefore place a limit on the number of
non-zeros
added to the LP at each separation round, where the limit is a multiple of the number of columns in the LP.

Experiments and Computational Results

From the remaining instances, we create a mapping to a feature space, where the features are the proportion of each constraint and variable type, the average row density, the maximum row density, and the objective vector density. The runs generated largely different parameter choices, with all choices resulting in an 8-15% improvement w.r.t. solve time over SCIP default on the training set. The generated parameter choices differed mostly in how to score a cut, where we could find no common parameter choices. 4
Conclusion

In this paper we introduced a new cut selection algorithm, which combines new context-aware scoring measures, filtering methods, and stopping criteria. We incorporate the algorithm into SCIP, and obtain a 5% improvement in solve time over the MIPLIB 2017 benchmark set.
