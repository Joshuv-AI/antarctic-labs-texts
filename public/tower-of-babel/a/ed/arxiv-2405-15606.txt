Chiral-Gain Photonics
Sylvain Lannebère 1, David E. Fernandes 2, Tiago A. Morgado 1, Mário G. Silveirinha 2

Abstract
Here, we present an exploratory study of the potential applications of electrically biased materials that possess a nonreciprocal and non-Hermitian electromagnetic response analogous to the electronic response of field-effect transistors. The most distinctive feature of such materials is their chiral-gain, meaning that their response can be active or dissipative depending on the handedness of the wave polarization. Here, we show how the chiral-gain can be harnessed to develop novel electromagnetic devices with unique properties such as chiral lasers, polarization-dependent mirrors, and coherent-perfect-absorber lasers. Furthermore, it is demonstrated that materials with chiral-gain can bypass a reciprocity constraint that typically limits the external coupling strength, thus facilitating the excitation of cavities with extremely large quality factors.



I
Introduction

Photonic integrated circuits have the potential to revolutionize information processing devices by enhancing their speed, bandwidth, and power efficiency
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. However, their development has been slowed down by several challenges, particularly the absence of integrated light sources and amplifiers suitable for large scale production
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
, as well as the lack of efficient integrated nonreciprocal components
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
.

In a recent article
[
11
]
, we introduced a nonlinear (meta-)material that mimics the response of semiconductor transistors. Such an idealized platform, which we designated by MOSFET-metamaterial, is characterized by a linearized optical response that is both nonreciprocal and non-Hermitian. These effects arise from the the interplay between material nonlinearities with a static electric bias. The most remarkable property of the metamaterial is that its non-Hermitian response is controlled by an
indefinite
matrix (see below). Consequently, the interactions of a wave with the material can be either dissipative or exhibit gain, depending on the field-polarization.
In a series of subsequent articles, we demonstrated that transistor-like electromagnetic responses may be implemented in different ways, using transmission lines coupled with FETs
[
12
]
, in 2D engineered materials (strained twisted bilayer graphene)
[
13
]
, and in 3D low symmetry natural materials with a large Berry curvature dipole
[
14
]
. Remarkably, the gain response of such platforms has chiral-properties, such that the material exhibits gain for a particular handedness of the optical field, while it is dissipative for the opposite polarization handedness
[
11
,
13
,
15
,
14
]
.

The objective of this article is to present an exploratory study of the potential applications of such “chiral-gain” materials in the realm of nanophotonics.
Here, similar to
[
11
]
, we restrict our attention to the case of an idealized nonlinear material characterized by the constitutive relation
\mathbf{P}=\varepsilon_{0}\overline{\chi}(\mathbf{E})\cdot\mathbf{E}
with a nonlinear susceptibility of the form

\displaystyle\overline{\chi}(\mathbf{E})

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\chi_{xx}(E_{z})&0&0\\
0&\chi_{yy}&0\\
0&0&\chi_{zz}\end{pmatrix}

(1)

where
\mathbf{E}
is the electric field and
\mathbf{P}
is the polarization vector. As shown in
[
11
]
, under a static electric bias with a nonzero
x
component, the linearization of
\mathbf{P}
leads to a (linearized) permittivity tensor of the form

\displaystyle\overline{\varepsilon}=\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{xx}&0&\varepsilon_{xz}\\
0&\varepsilon_{yy}&0\\
0&0&\varepsilon_{zz}\end{pmatrix}.

(2)

Remarkably, this permittivity tensor describes a nonreciprocal (
\overline{\varepsilon}^{T}\neq\overline{\varepsilon}
)
[
16
]
and non-Hermitian (
\overline{\varepsilon}^{\dagger}\neq\overline{\varepsilon}
) material response.
Additionally, in the special case where the permittivity tensor is real-valued and frequency independent, the linearized response is also time-reversal (
\mathcal{T}
) invariant (
\overline{\varepsilon}^{\ast}=\overline{\varepsilon}
).
Here,
T
,
\ast
and
\dagger
represent the transpose, the complex conjugate and the Hermitian-conjugate operations, respectively.

The non-Hermitian response of the material is ruled by the matrix
\overline{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}\equiv\frac{\overline{\varepsilon}-\overline{\varepsilon}^{\dagger}}{2i}

[
11
]
. For the model of Eq. (
2
), supposing for simplicity that the diagonal elements are real-valued, the matrix
\overline{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}
is given by:

\displaystyle\overline{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2i}\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\varepsilon_{xz}\\
0&0&0\\
-\varepsilon_{xz}^{\ast}&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

(3)

As the matrix trace vanishes, the matrix is indefinite. This means that some of its eigenvalues are positive, corresponding to a dissipative response, whereas other eigenvalues are negative corresponding to a gain response. It was shown in
[
11
]
that when
\varepsilon_{xz}
is real-valued the relevant eigenvectors of the matrix
\overline{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}
are
\hat{\mathbf{x}}\pm i\hat{\mathbf{z}}
. Thus, the polarization states associated with the dissipative/gain responses are circular and have opposite handedness. The polarization that activates the gain (dissipation) is controlled by the orientation of the electric field static bias.

In our previous work, we showed that a material with chiral-gain can be used to combine optical isolation with amplification within the same device
[
11
]
.
Here, we analyze how the chiral-gain can be harnessed for several potential nanophotonic applications.
First, in section
II
we characterize the spectrum of generalized indefinite-gain materials and discuss the conditions for their stability and the role of parity-time (
\mathcal{PT}
) symmetry.
In section
III
, we investigate the application of chiral-gain materials in lasing. In section
IV
, we theoretically demonstrate that chiral-gain materials can be used as polarization-dependent amplifying mirrors. Next, in section
V
, it is shown that open cavities formed by chiral-gain media may be engineered to exhibit an arbitrarily high quality factor.
Finally, in section
VI
, we demonstrate that such cavities are ideal for light-storage and that their operation is not limited by the reciprocity constraint.
This distinctive behavior is traced back to the unique analytical structure of the scattering matrix, which simultaneously accommodates a zero and a pole at the same frequency. This property delineates a regime where coherent perfect absorption (CPA or anti-lasing)
[
17
,
18
]
and “quasi-lasing” are seamlessly integrated within the same resonator, similar to the behavior of “CPA-lasers”
[
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
]
and their nonlinear counterparts
[
23
,
24
]
.
We find that, under optimal conditions, the energy retention within the cavity is primarily influenced by the excitation duration rather than by the incoming wave amplitude.

II
Bulk stability and
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetry

Indefinite-gain materials are characterized by a matrix
\overline{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}=\frac{\overline{\varepsilon}-\overline{\varepsilon}^{\dagger}}{2i}
that is indefinite, meaning that the interactions between the material and a wave can be either dissipative or exhibit gain.
Next, we study the stability of a bulk material described by the relative permittivity tensor

\displaystyle\overline{\varepsilon}=\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{xx}&0&\varepsilon_{xz}\\
0&\varepsilon_{yy}&0\\
\varepsilon_{zx}&0&\varepsilon_{zz}\end{pmatrix}.

(4)

This corresponds to a generalization of the MOSFET-metamaterial model introduced in
[
11
]
.
When
\varepsilon_{xz}\neq\varepsilon_{zx}
(
\varepsilon_{xz}\neq\varepsilon_{zx}^{\ast}
) the response is nonreciprocal (non-Hermitian). Furthermore, when the diagonal-elements are real-valued (as assumed below) the matrix
\overline{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}
is indefinite. For simplicity, in this article we neglect the frequency dispersion.

The natural modes of an indefinite-gain medium can be obtained by solving Maxwell equations in the frequency domain

\displaystyle\nabla\times\mathbf{E}

\displaystyle=i\omega\mu_{0}\mathbf{H}

(5a)

\displaystyle\nabla\times\mathbf{H}

\displaystyle=-i\omega\varepsilon_{0}\overline{\varepsilon}\cdot\mathbf{E}

(5b)

where
\omega
is the oscillation frequency. A time variation of the type
\text{e}^{-i\omega t}
is implicit.
For continuous material invariant under arbitrary space translations, the bulk normal modes are plane waves with a spatial variation of the form
\text{e}^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}
, where
\mathbf{k}
is the wave vector and
\mathbf{r}
is the position vector.
To assess the material stability we characterize the plane waves with a real-valued wave vector and complex frequency
\omega=\omega^{\prime}+i\omega^{\prime\prime}
.
The real part of
\omega
determines the time period
T=2\pi/\omega^{\prime}
of the natural oscillations and the imaginary part determines the amplitude variation with time. Since
\text{e}^{-i\omega t}=\text{e}^{-i\omega^{\prime}t}\text{e}^{\omega^{\prime\prime}t}
, it follows that
\omega^{\prime\prime}>0
corresponds to a wave growing exponentially with time (unstable response) while
\omega^{\prime\prime}<0
to a wave decaying with time (stable response).
The plane wave solutions satisfy the wave equation
\mathbf{k}\times(\mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{E})+\left(\omega^{2}/c^{2}\right)\overline{\varepsilon}\cdot\mathbf{E}=0
where
c
is the speed of light in vacuum.

The non-Hermitian physics is ruled by the nontrivial eigenvectors of
\overline{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}
, which are in the
xoz
plane. Thereby, we focus our attention in the propagation along the
y
direction, as it maximizes the gain or loss interactions.
For
\mathbf{k}=k\hat{\mathbf{y}}
, there are two transverse modes characterized by

\displaystyle\omega

\displaystyle=\omega_{\pm}\equiv\frac{kc}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\pm}}}

(6a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}

\displaystyle\sim\mathbf{E}_{\pm}\equiv\varepsilon_{xz}\hat{\mathbf{x}}+\left(\varepsilon_{\pm}-\varepsilon_{xx}\right)\hat{\mathbf{z}}

(6b)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\pm}

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon_{xx}+\varepsilon_{zz}\pm\sqrt{\gamma}}{2}

(6c)

where
\gamma=(\varepsilon_{xx}-\varepsilon_{zz})^{2}+4\varepsilon_{xz}\varepsilon_{zx}
.

Consider first that the permittivity tensor (
4
) is real-valued so that the response is invariant under a time-reversal.
In this situation,
\gamma
is also real-valued.
When
\gamma>0
, both
\varepsilon_{\pm}
[Eq. (
6c
)] and the natural frequencies
\omega_{\pm}
[Eq.(
6a
)] are real-valued and non-degenerate. In this scenario
\omega^{\prime\prime}=0
and the bulk material is stable. On the other hand, when
\gamma<0
, both
\varepsilon_{\pm}
and
\omega_{\pm}
become pairs of complex conjugates numbers, meaning that one mode is attenuated (
\omega^{\prime\prime}<0
) while the other is amplified (
\omega^{\prime\prime}>0
). In this case, which arises when
\varepsilon_{xz}
and
\varepsilon_{zx}
have opposite signs and their product satisfies
|\varepsilon_{xz}\varepsilon_{zx}|>\frac{1}{4}(\varepsilon_{xx}-\varepsilon_{zz})^{2}
, the bulk material is unstable. The transition from the stable to the unstable occurs when
\gamma=0
, which corresponds to an exceptional point where
\varepsilon_{\pm}
, the eigenfrequencies [Eq. (
6a
)] and the eigenvectors [Eq. (
6b
)] coalesce and become degenerate. It is worth noting that when
\varepsilon_{zz}=\varepsilon_{xx}
, the analysis presented here reduces to the case discussed in
[
25
]
.

The characteristics of the natural modes can be understood by noting that the material is invariant under a parity transformation
\mathcal{P}
that flips the
y
spatial coordinate
\mathcal{P}:(x,y,z)\to(x,-y,z)
. The permittivity tensor of a nonmagnetic and non-bianisotropic material invariant under
\mathcal{P}
must satisfy
\overline{\varepsilon}(y)=\overline{V}\cdot\overline{\varepsilon}(-y)\cdot\overline{V}
, where
\overline{V}=\hat{\mathbf{x}}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{x}}-\hat{\mathbf{y}}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{y}}+\hat{\mathbf{z}}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{z}}
. This condition is satisfied by the permittivity tensor (
4
). Therefore, since the material is invariant under both
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{T}
transformations, it is also invariant under a
\mathcal{PT}
transformation, the composition of the parity and time-reversal transformations.

\mathcal{PT}
symmetric systems are known to possess a spectrum with mirror symmetry with respect to the real-frequency axis and to exhibit stable and unstable phases separated by an exceptional point
[
26
,
27
]
.
When
\gamma>0
, the system is in the so-called unbroken
\mathcal{PT}
symmetric phase, where all the eigenmodes are real-valued and the bulk material is stable. When
\gamma<0
, the system is in the broken
\mathcal{PT}
symmetric phase, where the eigenmodes appear in complex conjugated pairs, corresponding to an unstable phase.

This behavior is illustrated in Fig.
1
where the locus of the normalized natural frequencies
\omega_{\pm}/(kc)
given by (
6a
) is represented in the complex plane as a function of
\varepsilon_{zx}
.

Figure 1:
Locus in the complex plane of the normalized bulk natural frequencies
\omega_{\pm}/(kc)
, when
\varepsilon_{zx}
varies in the range
0<\varepsilon_{zx}<0.1
. The insets indicate the value of
\varepsilon_{zx}
at the points marked with crosses. The remaining parameters used in the simulation are
\varepsilon_{xx}=1
,
\varepsilon_{zz}=1.1
and
\varepsilon_{xz}=-0.05
. The exceptional point (
\gamma=0
) is reached when
\varepsilon_{zx}=-\varepsilon_{xz}=0.05
.

For the considered range of parameters, it is seen that when
0<\varepsilon_{zx}<0.05
the parameter
\gamma
is positive and both frequencies
\omega_{\pm}
lie on the real axis, implying that the system is stable and is in the unbroken
\mathcal{PT}
symmetric phase. As
\varepsilon_{zx}
increases from 0 to 0.05, it can be observed that the eigenmodes gradually converge towards each other until they coalesce at the exceptional point associated with
\varepsilon_{zx}=0.05
. For larger values of
\varepsilon_{zx}
, the eigenmodes leave the real axis and become complex conjugates. In this case, the system enters the broken
\mathcal{PT}
symmetric phase, where it becomes unstable, with waves growing exponentially in time until some nonlinear saturation mechanism limits the gain.

Both for the stable (
\gamma>0
) and unstable (
\gamma<0
) situations, the eigenvectors of the indefinite matrix
\overline{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}
that controls the non-Hermitian interactions are
\hat{\mathbf{x}}\pm i\hat{\mathbf{z}}
. Thus, when
\overline{\varepsilon}
is real-valued, the wave polarizations that activate the gain/loss are always circularly polarized (chiral-gain). On the other hand, if either
\varepsilon_{xz}
or
\varepsilon_{zx}
are allowed to be complex-valued the eigenpolarizations that control the non-Hermitian interactions are in general elliptically polarized, corresponding to a more general form of indefinite-gain. For example, when
\varepsilon_{zx}=0
and
\varepsilon_{xz}
is pure-imaginary (as considered later in the article) the relevant eigenpolarizations are linearly polarized and are of the form
\hat{\mathbf{x}}\pm\hat{\mathbf{z}}
.

In the rest of the article, we focus on the permittivity tensor given by (
2
) with
\varepsilon_{zx}=0
.
From the previous stability analysis, it is evident that such a response is always stable (
\gamma\geq 0
), even when
\varepsilon_{xz}
is allowed to be complex-valued. Furthermore, the exceptional point (
\gamma=0
), where the eigenvectors of the two transverse natural modes coalesce into a single linear polarization, is reached when
\varepsilon_{xx}=\varepsilon_{zz}
. In this regime the indefinite-gain medium behaves as an ideal travelling wave amplifier, as discussed in detail in
[
12
]
.
Throughout the remainder of the paper, we avoid this scenario by assuming that
\varepsilon_{xx}\neq\varepsilon_{zz}
, so that the material response is “bounded”.

III
Lasing

In this section, we illustrate how the non-Hermitian response provided by chiral-gain media can be harnessed for the creation of an electromagnetic oscillator, i.e. a laser. A related study based on a stack of 2D materials was recently put forward in
[
15
]
. Next, we study laser-configurations based on 3D materials.

A laser is an oscillator that supports exponentially growing natural modes (
\omega^{\prime\prime}>0
) before it reaches the saturation (nonlinear) regime. Here, we consider a one dimensional closed cavity consisting of a chiral-gain medium slab positioned in between two mirrors. We investigate the natural modes of such a cavity for wave propagation along the
y
direction.

The complex eigenmodes of the cavity can be determined by solving Maxwell equations (
5
). For propagation along the
y
direction, we have
\nabla=\partial/\partial y\,\hat{\mathbf{y}}
, and these equations can be written in a matrix form as

\displaystyle i\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\mathbf{f}(y)=\overline{\mathbf{M}}\cdot\mathbf{f}(y)

(7)

where the four-component state vector
\mathbf{f}
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}(y)=\begin{pmatrix}E_{x}(y)&E_{z}(y)&H_{x}(y)&H_{z}(y)\end{pmatrix}^{T}

(8)

with
E_{x}
,
E_{z}
,
H_{x}
,
H_{z}
the
x
and
z
components of the electric and magnetic fields at position
y
. The
4\times 4
matrix
\overline{\mathbf{M}}
is given by

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{M}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&\omega\mu_{0}\\
0&0&-\omega\mu_{0}&0\\
0&-\omega\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{zz}&0&0\\
\omega\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{xx}&\omega\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{xz}&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

(9)

Similar to a Schrödinger equation, the solution of Eq. (
7
) is

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}(y)=\text{e}^{-iy\overline{\mathbf{M}}}\cdot\mathbf{f}(0),

(10)

where the exponential
\text{e}^{-iy\overline{\mathbf{M}}}
is a
4\times 4
matrix that plays the role of a transfer matrix. The matrix exponential can be evaluated analytically (not shown here), or alternatively it can be numerically evaluated. Thus, the fields at a generic position
y
within the material depend only on the fields calculated at
y=0
.

III.1
Cavity closed by two PECs

To begin, we consider the scenario where the two reflectors are perfect electric conducting (PEC) walls as represented in Fig.
2
.

Indefinite-gain medium cavity closed with two PEC walls at
y=0
and

In this case, the fields at each boundary of the cavity satisfy

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}(0)

\displaystyle=H_{x}(0)\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{3}+H_{z}(0)\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{4}

(11a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}(d)

\displaystyle=H_{x}(d)\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{3}+H_{z}(d)\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{4}

(11b)

where
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{i}=(\delta_{i1}\;\delta_{i2}\;\delta_{i3}\;\delta_{i4})^{T}
with
\delta_{ij}
the Kronecker delta symbol, is a vector with the entry ”1” at the
i
-th column. Taking into account that
\mathbf{f}(d)=\text{e}^{-id\overline{\mathbf{M}}}\cdot\mathbf{f}(0)
[Eq.(
10
)] and that
\mathbf{f}(d)\cdot\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}=\mathbf{f}(d)\cdot\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{2}=0
, we find after straightforward manipulations the following system of equations:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}\cdot\text{e}^{-id\overline{\mathbf{M}}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{3}&&\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}\cdot\text{e}^{-id\overline{\mathbf{M}}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{4}\\
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{2}\cdot\text{e}^{-id\overline{\mathbf{M}}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{3}&&\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{2}\cdot\text{e}^{-id\overline{\mathbf{M}}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{4}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}H_{x}(0)\\
H_{z}(0)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\end{pmatrix}

(12)

The dispersion equation is found by setting the determinant of the matrix in Eq. (
12
) equal to zero.
After simplifications, we obtain the following explicit result

\displaystyle\sin\left(\frac{\omega}{c}d\sqrt{\varepsilon_{xx}}\right)\sin\left(\frac{\omega}{c}d\sqrt{\varepsilon_{zz}}\right)=0.

(13)

Clearly, the natural frequencies of the cavity of Fig.
2
are
\omega=\omega_{n}^{x}
and
\omega=\omega_{n}^{z}
with

\displaystyle\omega_{n}^{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{n\pi c}{d\sqrt{\varepsilon_{xx}}}

(14a)

\displaystyle\omega_{n}^{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{n\pi c}{d\sqrt{\varepsilon_{zz}}}

(14b)

and
n
is an integer. Curiously, even though the bulk material can provide optical gain
[
11
]
, the cavity eigenmodes are real-valued and consequently it is not possible to generate lasing relying simply on PEC walls. To understand why, let us examine the time averaged net power exchanged between the wave and the material. For time-harmonic fields
\mathbf{E}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{E}_{\omega}\text{e}^{-i\omega t}+\mathbf{E}_{\omega}^{\ast}\text{e}^{i\omega t}\right)
it is given by
\left\langle Q\right\rangle=\frac{\omega\varepsilon_{0}}{2}\int\mathbf{E}_{\omega}^{\ast}\cdot\overline{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}\cdot\mathbf{E}_{\omega}~dV
(see the supplemental material of
[
11
]
). For a chiral-gain medium described by the permittivity tensor (
2
) with purely real diagonal elements, one has

\displaystyle\left\langle Q\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega\varepsilon_{0}}{2}\int\mathrm{Im}\left\{\varepsilon_{xz}E_{\omega,x}^{\ast}E_{\omega,z}\right\}~dV.

(15)

From Eqs. (
10
), (
11a
) and (
12
) the eigenvectors
\mathbf{E}_{n}^{x}
and
\mathbf{E}_{n}^{z}
associated with the natural modes
\omega_{n}^{x}
and
\omega_{n}^{z}
, respectively, are:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{n}^{x}

\displaystyle=A^{x}\sin\left(\frac{n\pi y}{d}\right)\hat{\mathbf{x}}

(16a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{n}^{z}

\displaystyle=A^{z}\sin\left(\frac{n\pi y}{d}\right)\left(\hat{\mathbf{x}}+\frac{\varepsilon_{zz}-\varepsilon_{xx}}{\varepsilon_{xz}}\hat{\mathbf{z}}\right)

(16b)

where
A^{x}
and
A^{z}
are normalization constants. As seen, the eigenmodes are linearly polarized
everywhere in the cavity when
\varepsilon_{xz}
is real-valued. When
\varepsilon_{xz}
is complex-valued, the eigenmode associated with
\omega_{n}^{z}
becomes elliptically polarized.
In either case, by substituting the explicit formulas for the fields into Eq. (
15
) (
\mathbf{E}_{\omega}\to\mathbf{E}_{n}^{i}
) one finds that
\left\langle Q\right\rangle=0
for arbitrary
\varepsilon_{xz}
. Thus, in this configuration the polarization of the eigenmodes is unable to unlock the gain and consequently there is no net transfer of energy from the material to the wave.

It is interesting to point out that the PEC mirrors preserve the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetry of the bulk material, and thereby for a real-valued
\overline{\varepsilon}
the spectrum must remain real-valued for an unbroken
\mathcal{PT}
-phase.

III.2
Cavity closed by a PEC and a soft-hard boundary

To extract energy from the medium we need to engineer the polarization of the cavity eigenmodes so that it “overlaps” the eigenvector of
\overline{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}
that triggers the gain response. This can be done by replacing one of the PEC walls by a soft-hard

\displaystyle\beta_{-}\sin\left(\frac{\omega d}{c}\left[\sqrt{\varepsilon_{zz}}-\sqrt{\varepsilon_{xx}}\right]\right)-\beta_{+}\sin\left(\frac{\omega d}{c}\left[\sqrt{\varepsilon_{zz}}+\sqrt{\varepsilon_{xx}}\right]\right)=0

\left(\sqrt{\varepsilon_{zz}}\mp\sqrt{\varepsilon_{xx}}\right).

For
\varphi=0

As the sum (or the difference) of two sine-functions can be written as the product of a sine function and a cosine function, it is clear that for
\varphi=0

\varepsilon_{xz}

For the reasons discussed above, the modal equation (

Solving the equations

IV
Chiral-gain Mirrors

In this section, we analyze another application of chiral-gain materials, namely the realization of non-Hermitian mirrors that provide either amplification or attenuation, depending on the wave polarization handedness. To this end, we consider the plane wave excitation of a chiral-gain medium slab backed by a mirror, as illustrated in Fig.
5
(a). We focus on the case where
\varepsilon_{xz}
is real-valued, so that the system is time-reversal symmetric.

The mirror is taken as a corrugated wall described by soft-hard boundary conditions, while the surrounding medium is a dielectric of permittivity
\varepsilon_{d}
, for example air. As illustrated in Fig.
5
(b)–(c), the non-Hermitian mirror can be used to selectively amplify or attenuate the incoming wave, depending on its polarization state. This can be useful, for instance, to enhance the back-scattering of some object with a specific polarization signature.

It is assumed that the plane wave propagates along the
+y
-direction and impinges on the slab along the normal direction. Let
\mathbf{E}_{t}^{\text{inc}}=E_{x}^{\text{inc}}\hat{\mathbf{x}}+E_{z}^{\text{inc}}\hat{\mathbf{z}}
be the complex amplitude of the (transverse) incident field and
\mathbf{E}_{t}^{\text{ref}}
the complex amplitude of the (transverse) reflected field. At the
y=0
interface these fields are related by

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{t}^{\text{ref}}(y=0)

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathbf{R}}\cdot\mathbf{E}_{t}^{\text{inc}}(y=0)

(25)

where
\overline{\mathbf{R}}
is the 2
\times
2 reflection matrix, whose expression is derived in appendix
A
[Eq. (
42
)].
The power reflected by the chiral-gain slab is proportional to

\displaystyle|\mathbf{E}_{t}^{\text{ref}}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{t}^{\text{inc},\ast}\cdot\overline{\mathbf{R}}^{\dagger}\cdot\overline{\mathbf{R}}\cdot\mathbf{E}_{t}^{\text{inc}}

(26)

The reflectance matrix
\overline{\mathcal{R}}=\overline{\mathbf{R}}^{\dagger}\cdot\overline{\mathbf{R}}
plays a crucial role in our analysis. It is a positive definite Hermitian matrix, meaning that its eigenvectors
\mathbf{E}_{\text{eig},1}
and
\mathbf{E}_{\text{eig},2}
form an orthogonal basis, while the eigenvalues
|\rho_{1}|^{2}
and
|\rho_{2}|^{2}
are positive numbers.
It is assumed, without loss of generality, that
|\rho_{1}|^{2}\leq|\rho_{2}|^{2}
. Clearly, the reflectance
|\rho|^{2}\equiv|\mathbf{E}_{t}^{\text{ref}}|^{2}/|\mathbf{E}_{t}^{\text{inc}}|^{2}
is bounded by the eigenvalues of
\overline{\mathcal{R}}
, namely
|\rho_{1}|^{2}\leq|\rho|^{2}\leq|\rho_{2}|^{2}
. Additionally, the eigenvectors
\mathbf{E}_{\text{eig},1}
and
\mathbf{E}_{\text{eig},2}
determine the polarizations of the incident field that minimize and maximize the reflected signal power, respectively.
It is important to note that
\mathbf{E}_{\text{eig},i}
are not eigenvectors of the reflection matrix, which means that the reflected wave may have a polarization different from that of the incident wave when
\mathbf{E}_{t}^{\text{inc}}=\mathbf{E}_{\text{eig},i}
.

In a lossless and passive system (two-port microwave network), energy conservation imposes
that the power reflected by the network must be identical to the incident power. Thus, in the conservative case one has
|\rho_{1}|^{2}=|\rho_{2}|^{2}=1
. On the other hand, non-Hermitian systems are not constrained by energy conservation, and hence it is possible to have

|\rho_{i}|^{2}>1
(
|\rho_{i}|^{2}<1
), which corresponds to amplification (attenuation) of the incident wave.

for a (

). As seen, the eigenvalues are different from unity and have values that oscillate periodically with the frequency or with the slab thickness.
As illustrated in Fig.
5
(b)–(c), in a wide range of frequencies, polarization 1 (red lines) can be strongly absorbed by the slab whereas in the same frequency range (the orthogonal) polarization 2 (blue lines) is strongly amplified leading to a reflected wave with amplitude larger than the incoming wave. This behavior is possible as soon as
\varepsilon_{xz}
is different from zero (no threshold). Furthermore, it can be checked that the orientation for the Kildal’s mirror corrugations that provides the strongest amplification is the same as the orientation that gives the largest
\omega^{\prime\prime}
in the closed PEC/Kildal cavity. The maximum power gain is 18.7 for the example of Fig.
6
(corresponding to point B of the plot) with
\varphi=45
, whereas it can reach 37.5 when
\varphi=67.5
(not shown).

Quite remarkably the product
|\rho_{1}|^{2}|\rho_{2}|^{2}
is equal to the unity meaning that when one of the eigenvectors is amplified the corresponding orthogonal polarization is attenuated in the same proportion. The property
|\rho_{1}|^{2}|\rho_{2}|^{2}=1
is a consequence of time-reversal symmetry. Indeed, under a time-reversal the roles of the incident and reflected waves are swapped [see Fig.
5
(b) and (d)]
[
31
,
32
]
. Thereby, the maxima and the minima of the reflectance are necessarily interchanged under a time-reversal
|\rho|^{2}\to 1/|\rho|^{2}
, implying that
|\rho_{1}|^{2}=1/|\rho_{2}|^{2}
. Note that this result demonstrates that
\det\overline{\mathcal{R}}=1=\left|\det\overline{\mathbf{R}}\right|
.

Furthermore, the previous argument also implies that if the field reflected by wave 1,
\overline{\mathbf{R}}\cdot{{\mathbf{E}}_{{\rm{eig,1}}}}
, (i..e, by the wave
{\mathbf{E}}_{{\rm{eig,1}}}
that activates maximum attenuation) is sent back to the material slab then it it will generate maximum gain.
Thereby, it follows that
{\left(\overline{\mathbf{R}}\cdot{\mathbf{E}_{\rm{eig,1}}}\right)^{\ast}}\sim{{\mathbf{E}}_{{\rm{eig,2}}}}
.
A similar identity holds true with the indices 1 and 2 interchanged. We took into account that under a time-reversal the electric field is conjugated.
Thus, we have shown that the field reflected by one of the eigenvectors is proportional to the time-reversed version of the other eigenvector. Formally, this can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{R}}\cdot\mathbf{E}_{\text{eig},j}=|\rho_{j}|\text{e}^{i\theta_{j}}\mathbf{E}_{\text{eig},l}^{\ast},\qquad j\neq l

(27)

with
\theta_{j}
some phase. It is implicit in this discussion that
\omega
is real-valued and that the system is non-Hermitian (
|\rho_{1}|\neq|\rho_{2}|
). The eigenvectors amplitude is normalized to the unity.

The polarization curves of the eigenvectors
\mathbf{E}_{\text{eig},1}
,
\mathbf{E}_{\text{eig},2}
are depicted in Fig.
6
(b)–(d) for different values of the normalized frequency
\omega d/(\pi c)
.
The incident fields that maximize the amplification/absorption are generally elliptically polarized [see Fig.
6
(c)], except at some special high-symmetry points. Due to the orthogonality of the eigenvectors, the handedness of the wave that activates the dissipation,
\mathbf{E}_{\text{eig},1}
, is always the opposite of the handedness of the wave that activates the gain,
\mathbf{E}_{\text{eig},2}
.
From Eq. (
27
), the polarization curve of the field reflected by wave 1 is coincident (apart from a scale factor and the absolute sense of rotation) with the polarization curve of wave 2. In particular, the polarization curves of an incident wave (1 or 2) and of the corresponding reflected wave always differ by a 90 rotation.

Counter-intuitively, the frequency for which the mirror amplification/attenuation is maximized [points B, B', D and D' in Fig.
6
(a)], corresponds to linearly polarized incident wave [Fig.
6
(b)], with the electric field forming an angle of approximately
30
with respect to the coordinate axes. This is possible because the field polarization inside the material slab is controlled not only by the polarization of the incident wave, but also by the anisotropy of the material and by the anisotropy of the Kildal-mirror. Consequently, there is no strict correlation between the polarization of the incident wave that triggers the maximum gain and the eigenvector of the matrix
\overline{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}
that maximizes the gain response of the medium. Thereby, an incident linearly polarized wave can unlock the chiral-gain within the material. It is also important to note that any incident polarization can be decomposed into the basis vectors
\mathbf{E}_{\text{eig},1}
and
\mathbf{E}_{\text{eig},2}
. In cases of very strong gain, the component associated with
\mathbf{E}_{\text{eig},2}
typically dominates the scattered field, even if it is weak in the incident wave. Therefore, there is no universal relationship between the polarization handedness of the incident field and the type of non-Hermitian (dissipative or amplifying) mirror response.

While the configuration of Fig.
5
provides the strongest non-Hermitian effects, it is also feasible to achieve chiral-amplification when the Kildal's mirror is replaced by a PEC mirror. For the parameters used in Fig.
6
, the chiral-gain slab backed by a PEC mirror yields a maximum power gain of 6 (not shown), which is roughly six times smaller than the maximum amplification achievable with the Kildal's mirror. The fact that the slab backed by the PEC wall can provide amplification while a cavity enclosed by two PEC mirrors [Fig.
2
] cannot produce lasing does not result in any contradiction. This difference of behavior can be attributed to the polarization of the field inside the material slab. In the closed cavity, the polarization is controlled only by the boundary conditions. On the other hand, in the open system, the polarization of the incident field becomes a free parameter that can be harnessed to achieve either amplification or absorption.

In summary, it was demonstrated that a chiral-gain medium can be used to realize polarization-controlled non-Hermitian mirrors. The degree of amplification/absorption is highly dependent on the polarization and frequency of the incident wave as well as on the slab thickness.

Modes of the open cavity

(a)].
For a pure-imaginary
\varepsilon_{xz}
there is no threshold, and consequently the modes always split into two branches [blues crosses in Fig.
7
(a)], similar to the closed cavity.
Interestingly, for a sufficiently large
\varepsilon_{xz}
, some modes exhibit a negative imaginary part very close to zero, meaning that their lifetime in the cavity is very long [Fig.
7
(b)].
Thus, the chiral-gain provided by the material can indeed compensate the radiation loss effects.
By finely adjusting the resonator parameters, it is possible to achieve a state where the cavity gain compensates almost exactly the cavity leakage for the eigenmode with the largest lifetime.
This is the scenario represented in Fig.
7
(b), where the mode frequency is only slightly below the real-frequency axis. This can be done either by tuning the corrugation orientation (green cross) of the Kildal's mirror or the strength of the gain parameter (purple triangle).
In this peculiar situation, that we will refer to as the “perfectly tuned” case, the field inside the cavity oscillates in time without any noticeable decay. It corresponds to a system that can store light for a long period of time, despite being open to the exterior.
Such systems may have potential applications in optical memories
[
35
]
or in sensing.
By further increasing the gain coefficient
\varepsilon_{xz}
beyond the perfectly tuned case, the imaginary part of the eigenfrequency of at least one mode becomes positive, causing the open cavity to become an oscillator (laser). It is relevant to note that in the perfectly tuned case (lasing threshold) the field in the dielectric region corresponds to an outgoing propagating plane wave, implying that the non-Hermitian material continuously radiates energy towards the dielectric.

VI

Lasing-Anti-Lasing Operation and the reciprocity constraint

In the following, we show that time-reversal invariant open-resonators relying on materials with chiral-gain can be operated in a regime where they behave as a CPA-laser
[
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
.
A CPA-laser simultaneously behaves as an anti-laser – signifying that it can fully absorb an incoming signal – and as ``quasi-laser'' signifying that it can interact resonantly with an incoming wave and store efficiently its energy with no sensible relaxation in time. Previous implementations of linear CPA-lasers
[
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
]
were based on combining different gain and lossy materials. In contrast, our system integrates both gain and loss within the same material. We show that unlike conventional passive resonators
[
36
]
, the external coupling in our resonator is not limited by reciprocity. Thereby our system can efficiently harvest a significant amount of energy, even for incoming pulses with relatively short-duration.

VI.1

Time-reversal symmetry and anti-lasing

In recent years, there has been significant interest in the analytical properties of the scattering matrix of time-reversal invariant systems (e.g, formed by lossless dielectrics)
[
17
,
20
,
37
,
38
]
. In such systems, the scattering matrix is constrained by the symmetry
\overline{\mathbf{S}}\left(\omega\right)=\left[\overline{\mathbf{S}}^{-1}\left(\omega^{\ast}\right)\right]^{\ast}

[
31
]
. Thereby, the poles and zeros of
\overline{\mathbf{S}}
are related by complex conjugation. Note that the poles of
\overline{\mathbf{S}}
determine the natural frequencies of the open-system, and thereby for a passive system lie in the lower half-plane. On the other hand, the zeros of
\overline{\mathbf{S}}
determine the frequencies for which the system does not generate scattering. The ``zeros'' are the frequencies associated with a nontrivial null space of the scattering matrix. For a time-reversal invariant passive platform, these zeros lie in the upper-half frequency plane. We underline that the ``zeros'' do not refer to the individual elements of the scattering matrix, but rather to the zeros of the matrix determinant.

Several works have developed interesting strategies to harvest, absorb, and store light by leveraging these analytical properties. Notably, it has been suggested that introducing a suitable amount of loss into a system may enable the displacement of a zero of
\overline{\mathbf{S}}
to the real-frequency axis, thereby allowing the full absorption of an incoming wave at that specific frequency with no back-scattering. This phenomenon is known as ``coherent perfect absorption'' (CPA) or ``anti-lasing''
[
17
,
18
]
.
Moreover, it has been demonstrated that the complex zeros of the scattering matrix can be utilized to store and release light in an ideally lossless cavity for an arbitrarily long time period. This mechanism, known as ``coherent virtual absorption''
[
38
]
, relies on exciting the system with a signal that exponentially grows over time.

Conversely, by judiciously inserting gain into an open-resonator it is possible to push one of the poles to the real-frequency axis, so that the resonator can support oscillations that do not decay in time. We refer to this mode of operation as a ``quasi-laser''.

Furthermore, by suitably incorporating loss and gain – such as in
\mathcal{PT}
symmetric systems – it is feasible to align a pole and a zero at the same real-frequency, enabling the system to function both as an anti-laser and a quasi-laser, depending on the excitation. This dual functionality defines what is known as a ``CPA-laser''
[
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
.
In the next subsections, we demonstrate that the perfectly tuned resonator introduced in Sect.
V
can behave as a CPA-laser.

VI.2

Analytical structure of
\overline{\mathbf{R}}
near a resonance

The geometry of our cavity is the same as in Sect.
V
. We suppose that the permittivity tensor is real-valued to ensure that the system is time-reversal symmetric. In order to understand how the chiral-gain affects the excitation of the open cavity, next we study the analytical properties of the scattering matrix, which in our system corresponds to the reflection matrix
\overline{\mathbf{R}}
.

In Appendix
B
, it is demonstrated that in the vicinity of a resonance (
\omega_{0}=\omega^{\prime}_{0}+i\omega^{\prime\prime}_{0}
) of a open resonator with high-quality factor, the reflection matrix has the following structure:

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{R}}\left(\omega\right)\approx\mathbf{U}^{\ast}\left(\omega_{0}^{\prime}\right)\cdot\begin{pmatrix}0&\frac{A}{\left(\omega-\omega_{0}\right)}\\
\frac{1}{A^{\ast}}\left(\omega-\omega_{0}^{\ast}\right)&0\end{pmatrix}\cdot\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\left(\omega_{0}^{\prime}\right),

(30)

where
A
is some constant and
\mathbf{U}
is a unitary matrix.

Consistent with the principles of time-reversal symmetry, the pole of the reflection matrix is accompanied by a complex conjugated zero, so that
|\det(\overline{\mathbf{R}})|=1
in the real-frequency axis, as it should be. Notably, the pole and zero pertain to
distinct
elements within the middle matrix in the right-hand side of Eq. (
30
). This aspect becomes particularly significant when the system gain is adjusted in such a way that
\omega_{0}
approaches the real-axis. In fact, the structure of the reflection matrix preserves the influences of both the pole and zero (associated with a nontrivial nullspace), allowing them to coexist without cancellation for a perfectly tuned resonator. Note that one of the eigenvalues of the reflection matrix is controlled by the pole, whereas the second eigenvalue is controlled by the zero.
This remarkable property is rooted on the non-Hermitian response of the material. Indeed, it is well known from the study of bound states in the continuum (BIC), that the influence of a system pole approaching the real-frequency axis through precise tuning is often completely canceled by a corresponding zero. This phenomenon renders the excitation of the bound state from an external region unfeasible, as highlighted in various studies
[
33
,
39
,
40
,
41
,
42
,
43
]
. This underscores the exceptional nature of the chiral-gain response in our context, where the non-Hermitian characteristics fundamentally alter the expected analytical properties of the reflection matrix.

the magnitude of the elements of the reflection coefficient for the perfectly tuned cavity corresponding to the purple triangle in Fig.

VI.3

Time excitation of the open cavity and the reciprocity constraint

Let us now analyze the excitation of a high-quality factor open cavity. Consider first a time harmonic excitation with the incident field polarized as
\mathbf{E}_{\text{eig},1}
. In the limit (
\omega^{\prime\prime}_{0}\to 0^{-}
), the reflected wave is vanishingly weak because
|\rho_{1}|\to 0
. This field polarization is thereby completely absorbed by the resonator, corresponding to a form of coherent perfect absorption (anti-lasing).
This effect is a consequence of the ``zero'' of the scattering matrix (
|\rho_{1}|\to 0
) on the real frequency axis. It is relevant to note that under a time-reversal, the scattering problem just discussed, maps into a scenario where the resonator radiates away a plane wave with frequency
\omega^{\prime}_{0}
without any external excitation. This is precisely the perfectly tuned eigenstate discussed in Sect.
V
, corresponding to the lasing threshold.

Consider now, a time harmonic excitation with the incident polarization determined by
\mathbf{E}_{\text{eig},2}

|\rho_{2}|\to\infty
, indicating that there is no time-harmonic steady-state (quasi-laser resonance). This divergence is caused by the fact that in the limit
\omega^{\prime\prime}_{0}\to 0^{-}
the excitation frequency matches a pole of the scattering matrix.

In summary, we showed that a chiral-gain cavity can be operated in a peculiar regime corresponding to the threshold of lasing/anti-lasing, similar to a CPA-laser. For an excitation polarized as

The cavity is capable of efficiently collecting and storing the energy of the incoming wave within it, even for short-duration pulses, without being limited by a reciprocity constraint on the external coupling strength.
The field trapped inside the cavity does not decay over time. The amount of energy stored in the cavity is proportional to the duration of the excitation.

VII

Conclusions

We investigated various applications facilitated by chiral-gain media, revealing their potential in the development of electrically pumped chiral lasers, polarization-controlled non-Hermitian mirrors, and light storage. In particular, we demonstrated that the non-Hermitian properties of chiral-gain media lay the groundwork for creating CPA-lasers using a single material that integrates both gain and loss responses.

This capability sets our approach apart from traditional resonators, such as those supporting BICs, which typically suffer from inefficient excitation due to pole-zero cancellation. By contrast, the unique analytical features of our system scattering matrix allow a zero and a pole to coexist at the same frequency. Additionally, we established that our system transcends the limitations imposed by reciprocity. As a result, our CPA-laser can be efficiently excited with signals of relatively short duration and can retain the captured energy without significant decay. Thus, chiral-gain materials emerge as a promising solution for advancing nanophotonic technologies.

(43)

The matrix
\overline{\mathbf{Z}}^{-1}
is typically well defined at points where
\overline{\mathbf{Z}}
is not.

Appendix B

Derivation of Eq. (
30
)

To characterize the analytical structure of the reflection matrix, first we note that Eq. (
27
) can be rewritten as:

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{R}}\cdot\mathbf{U}\left(\omega\right)=\mathbf{U}^{\ast}\left(\omega\right)\cdot\begin{pmatrix}0&a_{2}\left(\omega\right)\\
a_{1}\left(\omega\right)&0\end{pmatrix}.

(44)

The identity holds true in the real-frequency axis.
Here,
\mathbf{U}
is a matrix whose columns are the normalized eigenvectors (
\mathbf{E}_{\text{eig},1}
and
\mathbf{E}_{\text{eig},2}
) of the reflectance matrix (
\overline{\mathcal{R}}
) and
{a_{j}}\left(\omega\right)=\left|{{\rho_{j}}}\right|{e^{i{\theta_{j}}}}
. Since
\mathbf{U}
is a unitary matrix and varies little near the resonance, for a high-quality resonator it is possible to write:

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{R}}\left(\omega\right)\approx\mathbf{U}^{\ast}\left(\omega_{0}^{\prime}\right)\cdot\begin{pmatrix}0&a_{2}\left(\omega\right)\\
a_{1}\left(\omega\right)&0\end{pmatrix}\cdot\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\left(\omega_{0}^{\prime}\right).

(45)

The resonance corresponds to the pole of
a_{2}
, which thereby may be approximated by
a_{2}\approx A/(\omega-\omega_{0})
, where
A
is some constant.

In order that our theory is applicable away from the real-frequency axis, we enforce
that the
reflection matrix satisfies the time-reversal symmetry condition:
\overline{\mathbf{R}}\left(\omega\right)={\left[{{{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}^{-1}}\left({{\omega^{\ast}}}\right)}\right]^{\ast}}
. It is easy to check that this possible only if
{a_{1}}\left(\omega\right)={1\mathord{\left/{\vphantom{1{{{\left[{{a_{2}}\left({{\omega^{\ast}}}\right)}\right]}^{\ast}}}}}\right.\kern-1.2pt}{{{\left[{{a_{2}}\left({{\omega^{\ast}}}\right)}\right]}^{\ast}}}}
. These approximations for
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
yield Eq.
(
30
).

Appendix C

Time domain formalism

Here, we describe the formalism used to characterize the time domain electric field
\mathbf{E}^{\text{tot}}(y,t)
in the resonator problem.

We rely on Fourier theory. The Fourier transform
\psi(\mathbf{r},\omega)
of a time signal
\psi(\mathbf{r},t)
is defined as
\psi(\mathbf{r},\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\psi(\mathbf{r},t)\text{e}^{i\omega t}
. Then, it follows that the complex amplitude of the incident field [Eq. (
31
)] in the frequency domain is

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}^{\text{inc}}(y,\omega)

\displaystyle=\sigma\sqrt{\pi}\mathbf{E}_{0}^{\text{inc}}\text{e}^{ik_{d}y}\text{e}^{i(\omega-\omega_{0})t_{0}}\text{e}^{-\left(\frac{\sigma[\omega-\omega_{0}]}{2}\right)^{2}}

(46)

where
k_{d}=\sqrt{\varepsilon_{d}}\omega/c
. Using Eqs. (
10
), (
25
) and (
39
) it can be shown that in the frequency domain the total field
\mathbf{E}^{\text{tot}}
is:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}^{\text{tot}}(y,\omega)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mathbf{E}^{\text{inc}}(y,\omega)+\text{e}^{-ik_{d}y}\overline{\mathbf{R}}\cdot\mathbf{E}^{\text{inc}}(0,\omega)&y\leq 0\\
\left(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}+\hat{\mathbf{z}}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{2}\right)\cdot\text{e}^{-iy\mathbf{M}}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\left(\mathbf{1}_{2\times 2}+\overline{\mathbf{R}}\right)\cdot\mathbf{E}^{\text{inc}}(0,\omega)\\
\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{d}}}{\eta_{0}}\mathbf{J}\cdot\left(\mathbf{1}_{2\times 2}-\overline{\mathbf{R}}\right)\cdot\mathbf{E}^{\text{inc}}(0,\omega)\end{pmatrix}&y\geq 0\\
\end{cases}

(47)

Note that the region
y<0
corresponds to the dielectric region and
0<y<d
is the chiral-gain resonator.
The field in the time domain is obtained with an inverse Fourier transformation:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}^{\text{tot}}(y,t)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Re}\left\{\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega\mathbf{E}^{\text{tot}}(y,\omega)\text{e}^{-i\omega t}\right\}.

(48)

For systems with spectrum not confined to the lower-half frequency plane, the Fourier transform is ill-defined and one should use instead

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}^{\text{tot}}(y,t)=\mathrm{Re}\left\{\frac{1}{2\pi}\text{e}^{\omega^{\prime\prime}t}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega^{\prime}\mathbf{E}^{\text{tot}}(y,\omega^{\prime}+i\omega^{\prime\prime})\text{e}^{-i\omega^{\prime}t}\right\}.

(49)

where

\omega^{\prime\prime}
is chosen above all the poles of
\mathbf{E}^{\text{tot}}(y,\omega^{\prime}+i\omega^{\prime\prime})
.

Appendix D

Constraint on the coupling strength of time-reversal symmetric and non-conservative resonators

We consider a single mode resonator coupled to
n
ports. The complex amplitude
a(t)
of the resonator's mode is governed by
[
36
,
48
,
44
]
:

\displaystyle\frac{da}{dt}

\displaystyle=-i\omega_{0}a+\mathbf{K}\cdot\mathbf{s}_{+}

(50)

where
\omega_{0}
is the complex resonant frequency,
\mathbf{s}_{+}=\begin{pmatrix}s_{+1}&s_{+2}&\dots&s_{+n}\end{pmatrix}^{T}
is the vector describing the complex amplitude of the input waves and
\mathbf{K}
the vector describing the input coupling at each port.
The complex amplitude of the outgoing waves
\mathbf{s}_{-}
is ruled by

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}_{-}

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathbf{c}}\cdot\mathbf{s}_{+}+\mathbf{d}a

(51)

where
\overline{\mathbf{c}}
is the direct scattering matrix and
\mathbf{d}
the vector describing the output coupling to the ports. The different amplitudes are normalized in such a way that the stored energy is
{\left|a\right|^{2}}
, and the incoming and outgoing power fluxes are controlled by
{\left|{{s_{+i}}}\right|^{2}}
and
{\left|{{s_{-i}}}\right|^{2}}
, respectively.

For a natural mode (
\mathbf{s}_{+}=0
):

\displaystyle\frac{da}{dt}

\displaystyle=-i\omega_{0}a

(52a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}_{-}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{d}a

(52b)

and it follows that the complex amplitude of a natural mode is given by
a(t)=a_{0}\text{e}^{-i\omega_{0}t}
with
a_{0}
a complex number.
Under a time-reversal transformation
\mathcal{T}
,
a(t)
is transformed as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\left[a(t)\right]\xrightarrow{~\mathcal{T}~}\mathrm{Re}\left[a(-t)\right]=\mathrm{Re}\left[a_{0}^{\ast}\text{e}^{-i\omega_{0}^{\ast}t}\right],

(53)

meaning that under a
\mathcal{T}
transformation
a(t)\xrightarrow{~\mathcal{T}~}a^{\ast}(-t)
. If the system exhibits time-reversal symmetry, then the time-reversed natural mode field
a^{\ast}(-t)=a_{0}^{\ast}\text{e}^{-i\omega_{0}^{\ast}t}
is also solution of equations (
52
) with the incoming and outgoing waves time-reversed. Under a time-reversal the incoming and outgoing waves are interchanged
[
31
,
49
]
. Thus, there is no outgoing wave and the input wave is the time-reversal of
\mathbf{s}_{-}(t)
, given by
\mathbf{s}_{-}^{\ast}(-t)
. It follows that:

\displaystyle\frac{da^{\ast}(-t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=-i\omega_{0}a^{\ast}(-t)+\mathbf{K}\cdot\mathbf{s}_{-}^{\ast}(-t)

(54)

\displaystyle=-i\omega_{0}a^{\ast}(-t)+\mathbf{K}\cdot\mathbf{d}^{\ast}a^{\ast}(-t)

(55)

where we used Eq. (
52b
). This is equivalent to

\displaystyle i\left(\omega_{0}-\omega_{0}^{\ast}\right)a^{\ast}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{K}\cdot\mathbf{d}^{\ast}a^{\ast}

(56)

So, we have proven that for systems invariant under time-reversal symmetry:

\displaystyle-2\omega_{0}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{K}\cdot\mathbf{d}^{\ast}

(57)

From the definition of the radiative decay rate
\omega_{\text{rad}}^{\prime\prime}
, vector
\bf{d}
satisfies
[
36
,
48
,
44
]
:

\displaystyle-2\omega_{\text{rad}}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{d}^{\ast}

(58)

For one-port systems, the combination of Eqs. (
57
) and (
58
) implies that the magnitude of the input coupling is:

\displaystyle|K|

\displaystyle=\frac{-2\omega_{0}^{\prime\prime}}{\sqrt{-2\omega_{\text{rad}}^{\prime\prime}}}

(59)

For conservative systems,
\omega_{0}^{\prime\prime}=\omega_{\text{rad}}^{\prime\prime}
, and the previous equation reduces to the well-known result of Haus
[
36
]
,

\displaystyle|K|=\sqrt{-2\omega_{\text{rad}}^{\prime\prime}},

(60)

which constraints the strength of the input coupling. It can be shown that the above result remains valid for arbitrary (possibly dissipative) reciprocal systems (such systems are constrained by
\bf{K}=\bf{d}
).

In contrast, our (two-port) non-Hermitian system is only constrained by Eqs. (
57
) and (
58
). Applying the first equation to the CPA-laser of section
VI
(
\omega_{0}^{\prime\prime}\to 0
), it follows that:

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}\cdot\mathbf{d}^{\ast}

\displaystyle=0

(61)

\displaystyle-2\omega_{\text{rad}}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{d}^{\ast}

(62)

Since the radiative decay rate does not vanish (
\mathbf{d}\neq 0
), it follows that
\mathbf{K}
is perpendicular to
\mathbf{d}
. For the problem of section
VI.3
,
\mathbf{d}\sim\mathbf{E}_{\text{eig},1}^{\ast}
, which corresponds to the field radiated by the natural mode of the cavity (time-reversal of the mode that is fully absorbed). This means that
\mathbf{K}\sim\mathbf{E}_{\text{eig},2}^{\ast}
, as expected, so that the optimal incident wave to excite the cavity is
\mathbf{s}_{+}\sim\mathbf{E}_{\text{eig},2}
. The amplitude of
\mathbf{K}
is not directly constrained by time-reversal symmetry.
