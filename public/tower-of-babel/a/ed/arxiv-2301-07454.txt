On the analytical continuation of lattice Liouville theory
Xiangyu Cao, Raoul Santachiara, Romain Usciati

Abstract
The path integral of Liouville theory is well understood only when the central charge c ∈ [ 25 , ∞ ) c\in[25,\infty) . Here, we study the analytical continuation the lattice Liouville path integral to generic values of c c , with a particular focus on the vicinity of c ∈ ( − ∞ , 1 ] c\in(-\infty,1] . We show that the c ∈ [ 25 , ∞ ) c\in[25,\infty) lattice path integral can be continued to one over a new integration cycle of complex field configurations. We give an explicit formula for the new integration cycle in terms of a discrete sum over elementary cycles, which are a direct generalization of the inverse Gamma function contour. Possible statistical interpretations are discussed. We also compare our approach to the one focused on Lefschetz thimbles, by solving a two-site toy model in detail. As the parameter equivalent to c c varies from [ 25 , ∞ ) [25,\infty) to ( − ∞ , 1 ] (-\infty,1] , we find an infinite number of Stokes walls (where the thimbles undergo topological rearrangements), accumulating at the destination point c ∈ ( − ∞ , 1 ] c\in(-\infty,1] , where the thimbles become equivalent to the elementary cycles.



1
Introduction

Liouville theory is a class of two-dimensional conformal field theories parametrized by the central charge
c
. The extent to which the theory is understood depends on the value of
c
. In the domain
c\in\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,1]
, i.e., everywhere in the complex plane except a half-line, the theory is exactly solved by conformal bootstrap. One has a non-compact theory with a continuous spectrum, and correlation functions can be consistently determined from the Dorn-Otto-Zamalodchikov-Zamalodchikov (DOZZ) structure constants
do94
;
zz95
and conformal blocks (for a pedagogical account, see
ribault2014conformal
); in particular, correlation functions are analytical in
c\in\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,1]
. For
c\in[25,\infty)
, the interval relevant to 2D quantum gravity
polya81
, one can do even better: it is possible to make rigorous sense of the path integral defining the theory, and confirm key aspects of the bootstrap solution from bottom up
david16
;
vargas2019
;
vargas2020
. (The path integral representation underlies also the connection between
c\geq 25
Liouville theory and random energy models
lft
;
lft2
.)

The situation is more confused on the half-line
c\in(-\infty,1]
. From the bootstrap perspective, the DOZZ structure constants cannot be analytically continued to generic values of
c\in(-\infty,1]
. Instead, another set of structure constants were proposed, independently by Schomerus
Schomerus_2003
, Al. Zamalodchikov
Zam05
, and Petkova-Kostov
kp05
, as an alternative solution to a set of degenerate crossing symmetry equations
Te95
. These
c\leq 1
structure constants were considered non-physical for a long time, until a number of recent concurrent results put them back on the table. Among these, the most clear-cut came arguably from conformal bootstrap: Ribault and Santachiara
RiSa015
showed that the
c\leq 1
structure constants define a consistent conformal field theory with a continuous and diagonal spectrum, by numerical checks of the crossing symmetry. However, the bootstrap does not inform of a path integral or microscopic realization of the theory.

The study of
c\leq 1
Liouville theory has long been motivated by critical lattice models in two dimensions, such as the
O(n)
loop models and the related Potts models. Critical exponents in these models can be exactly determined by arguments involving the Liouville path integral
kondev
. A series of works in the last decade established the statistical interpretation of the
c\leq 1
structure constants in terms of three-point correlation functions in Potts
Delfino_2011
;
Ziff_2011
;
PICCO13
and loop models
IJS
;
ang2021integrability
. However, exhaustive recent scrutiny of the four-point correlation functions of Potts and loop models
Ribault:2022qwq
;
Grans-Samuelsson:2021uor
;
Jacobsen:2022nxs
;
He:2020mhb
;
He:2020rfk
;
Picco:2016ilr
;
LykkeJacobsen:2018cbt
has revealed an operator content that is distinct from — and considerably more involved than — the aforementioned bootstrap solution
RiSa015
: Liouville theory and loop models are not as related as one might have believed.

Independently, motivated by applications to holography and four-dimensional gauge theory, Harlow, Maltz and Witten
Harlow_2011
undertook an extensive semiclassical analysis of Liouville path integral (see Section
4
for more detailed discussion). Their results indicate that, to define the Liouville path integral for generic complex values of
c
(and in particular for
c\in(-\infty,1]
) requires to understand what the symbol

\int[\mathcal{D}\varphi]

actually means, in particular, on which integration cycle the path integral should be carried out. The continuum path integral being a highly nontrivial mathematical object, we shall be more modest and consider lattice regularizations. To obtain the
c\geq 25
Liouville, the integration cycle and the corresponding lattice regularization are rather straightforward to specify:

\int[\mathcal{D}\varphi]=\int_{\varphi\in\mathbb{R}}[\mathcal{D}\varphi]\longrightarrow\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\prod_{r=1}^{N}\mathrm{d}\varphi(x_{r})\quad\quad(c\geq 25)\,.

(1)

Note that this equation is not as innocent as it may appear. It implies in particular that one should integrate over the global shift of the
\varphi
field (also known as the zero mode), as well as its fluctuations. The importance of the zero mode was noticed early on in the physics literature
seiberg90
;
goulianli
, and re-emphasized in the recent rigorous construction of the path integral using probability theory
david16
(see also
lft
for application to disordered systems). For other values of
c
, and in particular for
c\leq 1
, a precise prescription analogous to (
1
) is unknown, besides the general expectation that one should integrate over a cycle of middle dimension in the space of complexified field configurations
Harlow_2011
, i.e., a submanifold of
N
real dimensions in
\mathbb{C}^{N}
in a lattice with
N
sites.

In this work, we show how to analytically continue the
lattice
Liouville path integral from
c\geq 25
to all values of
c\in\mathbb{C}
. In particular, under mild conditions, we provide an explicit integration cycle
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{C}^{N}
(in terms of a discrete sum over “elementary cycles”), which allows to extend the lattice Liouville path integral to parameter regions where the cycle
\mathbb{R}^{N}
is no longer viable, including the vicinity of
c\in(-\infty,1]
. We will argue that the extended lattice path integral we propose has a continuum limit described by the Liouville bootstrap for
c\notin(-\infty,1]
. Meanwhile, for
c\leq 1
, we expect our proposal to display singularities in the continuum limit. Hence, the definition of a continuum path integral for
c\leq 1
Liouville remains an open question.

2
Minisuperspace approximation

The issue of choosing an integration cycle can be already illustrated in a minisuperspace approximation, where we only integrate over constant field configurations
Harlow_2011
. Equivalently, we can think of a lattice Liouville theory on a single site. This gives rise to a one-variable integral

\int_{\mathcal{C}}\exp(-n\varphi-e^{\varphi})\mathrm{d}\varphi\,.

(2)

This integral is convergent on the
c\geq 25
contour
\mathcal{C}=\mathbb{R}
if and only if
\mathrm{Re}(n)<0
(this is known as a Seiberg bound
seiberg90
in the context of Liouville), in which case it can be related to the usual gamma function integral by a change of variable
t=e^{\varphi}
:

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp(-n\varphi-e^{\varphi})=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{-n-1}e^{-t}\mathrm{d}t=\Gamma(-n)\,.

(3)

Thus, the result of the integral can be analytically continued beyond the Seiberg bound to a meromorphic function of
n\in\mathbb{C}
, with poles at
n=0,1,2,3\dots
.

Figure 1:
Integration cycles of the Gamma function integral (
2
) in the complex plane. The integral converges on the black solid line
\varphi\in\mathbb{R}
when
\mathrm{Re}(n)<0
. In that case
\mathbb{R}
is also a Lefschetz thimble. The blue dashed curves are the elementary cycles, indexed by
h\in\mathbb{Z}
. The integral is always convergent on the elementary cycles, and gives essentially an inverse Gamma function (
5
). These are equivalent to the thimbles for
\mathrm{Re}(n)>0
. Finally the red curve is a thimble when
n=e^{-\mathbf{i}\theta}
for
\theta=\pi/2-0.01
, at the vicinity of a Stokes wall.

Now, there is a class of cycles, dubbed “elementary cycles”, on which the integral is convergent for any
n\in\mathbb{C}
. These are indexed by an integer
h\in\mathbb{Z}
, and have a shape of the letter “U” rotated by
90
degrees clockwise. We can describe them as a parametric curve
\varphi(s),s\in(-\infty,+\infty)
, with the following limiting behavior

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{h}:\varphi(s)\longrightarrow\begin{cases}+\infty+2\pi\mathbf{i}h&s\to+\infty\\
+\infty+2\pi\mathbf{i}(h+1)&s\to-\infty\end{cases}

(4)

Such cycles are depicted by blue dashed curves in Figure
1
.
By Cauchy’s theorem, the precise geometric shape of curve does not affect the integral. In both limits
s\to\pm\infty
,
-n\varphi-e^{\varphi}\sim e^{\varphi}\to-\infty
, so the integral (
2
) converges rapidly, for any value of
n
. Hence, the integral over
\mathcal{U}_{h}
is an entire function of
n
. In fact one can show that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{U}_{h}}\exp(-n\varphi-e^{\varphi})\mathrm{d}\varphi

\displaystyle=\Gamma(-n)(e^{-2\pi hn\mathbf{i}}-e^{-2n\pi(h+1)\mathbf{i}})

(5)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi\mathbf{i}}{\Gamma(1+n)}\,e^{-(2h+1)n\pi\mathbf{i}}\,.

(6)

To see this, we can restrict to
\mathrm{Re}(n)<0
(thanks to analyticity) and deform the contour into the difference between two horizontal lines
\mathbb{R}+2\pi h\mathbf{i}
and
\mathbb{R}+2\pi(h+1)\mathbf{i}
, evaluate the integral using (
3
) and use reflection formula
\Gamma(-n)\Gamma(1+n)=\pi/\sin(-\pi n)
to obtain the expression (
6
), which is manifestly analytical in the whole complex plane.

Comparing (
3
) and (
5
), we see that the integral on the
c\geq 25
cycle can be written as an infinite sum of the elementary cycles:

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\omega=\begin{cases}\sum_{h=0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathcal{U}_{h}}\omega&\mathrm{Im}(n)<0\\
-\sum_{h=-1}^{-\infty}\int_{\mathcal{U}_{h}}\omega&\mathrm{Im}(n)>0\end{cases}\,,\,

(7)

where
\omega=\exp(-n\varphi-e^{\varphi})\mathrm{d}\varphi
. Note that we must choose the infinite series depending on the sign of
\mathrm{Im}(n)
as above, since it is the only convergent one, by (
6
). On the other hand, the convergence of the series does not depend on the sign of
\mathrm{Re}(n)
, so the right hand side (
7
) allows to extend the left hand side beyond its region of convergence. We have here a (rather trivial) example of the following phenomenon: the analytical continuation of the
c\geq 25
lattice path integral (
3
) gives rise to a discrete sum over elementary cycles. The main point of this paper is that this happens to the lattice Liouville theory beyond the minisuperspace approximation.

The example of Gamma function integral is often discussed
pasquetti
;
berry91
;
boyd
;
Harlow_2011
;
witten2011analytic
with an emphasis on the steepest descent contours (also known as Lefschetz thimbles) and the associated Stokes phenomena (see Section
3.4
below for a definitions and a novel example). When
n<0
,
\mathbb{R}
is the steepest descent contour associated with the saddle point
\varphi_{*}=\ln(-n)\in\mathbb{R}
of the “action”
S(\varphi)=n\varphi+e^{\varphi}
. Since
S^{\prime}(\varphi)
is a periodic function in
\varphi
with period
2\pi\mathbf{i}
, there are a sequence of saddle points (corresponding to different branches of
\ln(-n)
), and their thimble is always a horinzontal line. As we move
n
around in
\mathbb{C}
, the steepest descent contours smoothly deform, as long as
\mathrm{Re}(n)<0
. When
n
hits the imaginary axis, the steepest descent contours undergo a topological rearrangement known as the Stokes phenomenon, such that when
\mathrm{Re}(n)>0
, the thimbles all become equivalent to the elementary cycles
\mathcal{U}_{h}
. In order to analytically continue the
c\geq 25
integral, one must also switch abruptly the integration contour from a single thimble to a sum of thimbles as in (
7
) to compensate for the Stokes phenomenon. This can be visualized in Figure
1
, where the red curve is the steepest descent contour just before
n
hits the Stokes wall. With a larger number of degrees of freedom, characterizing thimbles and the Stokes phenomena can become highly involved
Alexandru:2020wrj
. Our point of view here is that this difficult task can be avoided by focusing instead on the elementary cycles.

3
Liouville theory on two sites

To further compare the approaches based on thimbles and elementary cycles, it is helpful to consider the simplest toy model of Liouville path integral that involves a “kinetic term”, which connects two lattice sites. This toy model is simple enough so that the Lefschetz thimbles and the Stokes phenomena can be reasonably understood. They turn out to be considerably more involved than the one-site example above, whereas the elementary-cycle approach yields a more transparent picture.

We consider the integral

Z({\mathcal{C}})=\int_{\mathcal{C}}\exp\left(-S(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2})\right)\mathrm{d}\varphi_{1}\mathrm{d}\varphi_{2}

(8)

where the action

S(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2})=\frac{1}{2b^{2}}(\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2})^{2}+\sum_{i=1,2}\left(e^{\varphi_{i}}-\frac{\eta_{i}}{b^{2}}\varphi_{i}\right)

(9)

depends on the parameters
b
and
\eta_{i},i=1,2
. We can associate
b
with the central charge
c
in a way such that

\begin{cases}c\geq 25&b\in\mathbb{R}\,,\,b^{2}>0\\
c\leq 1&b\in\mathbf{i}\mathbb{R}\,,\,b^{2}<0\,.\end{cases}\,.

(10)

Indeed, the kinetic term in the action has the usual (“space-like”) sign when
b^{2}>0
, and the “wrong” (“time-like”) sign as
b^{2}<0
. Of course, in the case of a two sites model, the notion of conformal symmetry, and therefore of central charge, is meaningless. However, changing
c
from
[25,\infty)
to
(-\infty,1]
does correspond to flipping the sign of the kinetic term in the full-fledged Liouville path integral, see (
56
) below. Similarly, anticipating (
60
) below, we associate
\eta_{i}
with operator insertions in Liouville theory.

When
b^{2}>0
, the path integral on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
converges provided
\eta_{i}
satisfy the Seiberg bound:

\sum_{i}\mathrm{Re}(\eta_{i})>0\,.

(11)

We will be interested in the continuation of the
c\geq 25
path integral to the vicinity of
c\in(-\infty,1]
. This corresponds to rotating
b^{2}
from the positive real axis to the negative one. For definitiveness, we choose to do it
clock-wise
:

b^{2}=|b^{2}|e^{-\mathbf{i}\theta}\,,\,\theta=0\to\pi\,.

(12)

For simplicity, we fix
\eta_{i}
to be equal and positive:

\eta_{1}=\eta_{2}=\eta>0\,.

(13)

This restriction will be relaxed in a more general setting in Section
4
below.

3.1
Elementary cycle decomposition

A useful way to analyze (
8
) is to decouple the kinetic term in (
9
) by introducing an auxiliary variable
\chi
:

\displaystyle\exp\left(-S(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2})\right)=\int_{\chi}\frac{e^{b^{2}\chi^{2}/2}\mathrm{d}\chi}{\mathbf{i}\sqrt{2\pi/b^{2}}}\prod_{i=1,2}\exp\left(-n_{i}\varphi_{i}-e^{\varphi_{i}}\right)

(14)

\displaystyle n_{1}=-\frac{\eta}{b^{2}}+\chi\,,\,n_{2}=-\frac{\eta}{b^{2}}-\chi

(15)

The contour of
\chi
can be chosen arbitrarily, so long as the Gaussian integral
\int_{\chi}e^{b^{2}\chi^{2}/2}
converges. For example, a vertical (horizontal) contour is admissible whenever
\mathrm{Re}(b^{2})>0
(
<0
, respectively). For any fixed
\chi

14

2

\varphi_{i}

\varphi_{i}

Z(\mathbb{R}^{2})=\int_{\chi}\frac{e^{b^{2}\chi^{2}/2}\mathrm{d}\chi}{\mathbf{i}\sqrt{2\pi/b^{2}}}\prod_{i=1,2}\Gamma(-n_{i})\,.

(16)

It follows that the left hand side can be analytically continued beyond where the
\varphi_{i}
-integrals converge, as long as one can smoothly deform the
\chi
-contour to keep the
\chi
integral convergent, while avoiding the poles of the Gamma functions. One can check that this can be done for all
0<\theta<\pi
(recall
b^{2}=|b^{2}|e^{-\mathbf{i}\theta}

to

We now consider the elementary cycles in the two-site model. They are indexed by
(h_{1},h_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, and are defined as a product of the one-site elementary cycles

\mathcal{U}_{h_{1},h_{2}}:=\prod_{i=1,2}\mathcal{U}_{h_{i}}(\varphi_{i})\,,

(17)

where
\mathcal{U}_{h}
is the one-site elementary cycle defined in (

). It is not hard to convince oneself that the path integral is convergent in any

for any value of

and

.
Indeed, as
\mathrm{Re}(\varphi_{i})\to\infty
and
\mathrm{Im}(\varphi_{i})\to 2\pi h_{i}
,
h_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
, that is, along the elementary cycle

, the action (

, and its real part diverges rapidly to

Z(\mathcal{U}_{h_{1},h_{2}})=\int_{\chi}\frac{e^{b^{2}\chi^{2}/2}\mathrm{d}\chi}{\mathbf{i}\sqrt{2\pi/b^{2}}}\prod_{i=1,2}\frac{2\pi\mathbf{i}e^{-(2h_{i}+1)n_{i}\pi\mathbf{i}}}{\Gamma(1+n_{i})}

(18)

We turn to discuss the decomposition of
\mathbb{Z}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, or rather the analytical continuation thereof, into a sum of elementary cycles.
For any fixed
\chi
, the one-site decomposition formula (

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\prod_{i=1,2}\exp\left(-n_{i}\varphi_{i}-e^{\varphi_{i}}\right)\mathrm{d}\varphi_{i}

. Therefore, we can decompose the whole path integral into a sum of elementary cycles
if

\chi\in\mathbb{R}+\mathbf{i}c

\mathrm{Re}(b^{2})<0
, which is satisfied for
\theta\in(\pi/2,\pi)

Z({\mathbb{R}^{2}})=\sum_{h_{1}=0}^{\infty}\sum_{h_{2}=0}^{\infty}Z({\mathcal{U}_{h_{1},h_{2}}})\,,\,\theta\in(\pi/2,\pi)\,.

, where

\displaystyle Z({\mathbb{R}^{2}})=\widehat{Z}\sum_{h_{0}=0}^{\infty}{e^{4\pi\mathbf{i}h_{0}\eta/b^{2}}}=\frac{\widehat{Z}}{1-e^{4\pi\mathbf{i}\eta/b^{2}}}\,,\,\theta>\pi/2\,,

\widehat{Z}:=\sum_{\min(h_{1},h_{2})=0}Z(\mathcal{U}_{h_{1},h_{2}})=Z(\mathcal{U}_{0,0})+\sum_{h=1}^{\infty}(Z(\mathcal{U}_{h,0})+Z(\mathcal{U}_{0,h}))\,.

\widehat{Z}

. Indeed, for

\mathrm{Im}(\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2})\sim\pm 2\pi\mathbf{i}h
for all
(\varphi_{i})\in\mathcal{U}_{h,0}
or
\mathcal{U}_{0,h}
, the integral over these cycles are suppressed by the “time-like” kinetic term in (

, which dominates over the time-like kinetic term). Therefore, (

) isolates the singularities of

as we approach

: we have a series of poles at

b^{2}=-2\eta/n\,,\,n=1,2,3,\dots\,.

(23)

The upshot of the above analysis is the following. The

) with nonnegative indices

. The singularities as we approach the
c\leq 1

\min h_{i}=0
.

3.2
The zero mode approach

The goal of this section and the next one is to understand the above result in light of the zero mode approach, which is an important step in the analysis of the
c\geq 25
Liouville path integral
goulianli
;
david16
. The idea is to write the Liouville field as a sum of the zero mode
\varphi_{0}
and the fluctuating ones
{\tilde{\varphi}}

goulianli
;
david16
. In our toy model, there is only one fluctuating mode, and we can write:

\varphi_{0}=\frac{1}{2}(\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{2})\,,\,{\tilde{\varphi}}=\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}\;\Leftrightarrow\;\varphi_{1}=\varphi_{0}+\frac{{\tilde{\varphi}}}{2}\,,\,\varphi_{2}=\varphi_{0}-\frac{{\tilde{\varphi}}}{2}\,.

(24)

In terms of these variables the action is

S=\frac{{\tilde{\varphi}}}{2b^{2}}+2\cosh\left(\frac{{\tilde{\varphi}}}{2}\right)e^{\varphi_{0}}-\frac{2\eta\varphi_{0}}{b^{2}}

(25)

Now, for any fixed
{\tilde{\varphi}}
, upon a shift,

\varphi_{0}=\varphi-\ln\left(2\cosh\left(\frac{{\tilde{\varphi}}}{2}\right)\right)\,,

(26)

we have a one-site action (
2
) for
\varphi
, with
n=-2\eta/b^{2}
. Therefore, to calculate
Z(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, we can integrate first over
\varphi_{0}

(29)

\widehat{Z}=\frac{2\pi\mathbf{i}e^{2\pi\mathbf{i}\eta/b^{2}}}{\Gamma\left(1-\frac{2\eta}{b^{2}}\right)}\int_{(\mathbf{i}-\epsilon)\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}{\tilde{\varphi}}\,\exp(-S_{\text{eff}}({\tilde{\varphi}}))\,.

3.3
Elementary cycles of the Coulomb gas integral

How are the results of the zero approach (
29
) and (
30
) related to the elementary cycle decompositions (
19
) and (
22
)? To address this question, we examine the Coulomb gas integral on a general cycle:

\widetilde{Z}(\mathcal{C}):=\int_{\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{d}{\tilde{\varphi}}\,\exp(-S_{\text{eff}}({\tilde{\varphi}}))\,.

(33)

Throughout this section, we shall restrict ourselves to the near time-like region
\mathrm{Re}(b^{2})<0
. Since
\eta>0
, the real part of the effective action goes to
+\infty
at the zeros of
\cosh({\tilde{\varphi}}/2)
:

\mathrm{Re}(S_{\text{eff}})\to+\infty\,,\,{\tilde{\varphi}}\to(2\tilde{h}+1)\pi\mathbf{i}\,,\,\tilde{h}\in\mathbb{Z}\,.

(34)

Therefore, for any
\tilde{h}
, the interval

\mathcal{I}_{\tilde{h}}=((2\tilde{h}-1)\pi\mathbf{i},(2\tilde{h}+1)\pi\mathbf{i})

(35)

is a closed integration cycle on which (
33
) converges. However, due to the multi-valuedness of (
28
), the cycles should be defined on a cover space of
\mathbb{C}-\{(2\tilde{h}+1)\pi\mathbf{i}:\tilde{h}\in\mathbb{Z}\}
on which
S_{\text{eff}}
is single-valued. In practice, we can specify the deck by the value of the log, say at the mid-point of
\mathcal{I}_{\tilde{h}}
:

\mathcal{I}_{\tilde{h}}^{h_{0}}=((2\tilde{h}-1)\pi\mathbf{i},(2\tilde{h}+1)\pi\mathbf{i})\,,\,\ln\cosh(\mathbf{i}\pi\tilde{h})=-2\pi h_{0}\mathbf{i}\,.

(36)

Here,
h_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}/2
such that

h_{1}=h_{0}+\frac{\tilde{h}}{2}\,,\,h_{2}=h_{0}-\frac{\tilde{h}}{2}

(37)

are both integers (compare to (
24
)). Hence, we have again a lattice of cycles

\mathcal{I}_{h_{1},h_{2}}:=\mathcal{I}_{\tilde{h}}^{h_{0}}\,,\,\tilde{h}=h_{1}-h_{2}\,,\,h_{0}=\frac{1}{2}(h_{1}+h_{2})

(38)

indexed by
(h_{1},h_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, which we call the elementary cycles of the Coulomb gas integrand. Now, it is not hard to verify that the Coulomb gas integral in (
30
) admits the following decomposition

\displaystyle\int_{(\mathbf{i}-\epsilon)\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}{\tilde{\varphi}}\,\exp(-S_{\text{eff}}({\tilde{\varphi}}))=

\displaystyle\widetilde{Z}(\mathcal{I}_{0}^{0})+\sum_{\tilde{h}=1}^{\infty}\left(\widetilde{Z}\left(\mathcal{I}^{\tilde{h}/2}_{\tilde{h}}\right)+\widetilde{Z}\left(\mathcal{I}^{\tilde{h}/2}_{-\tilde{h}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\min(h_{1},h_{2})=0}\widetilde{Z}(\mathcal{I}_{h_{1},h_{2}})\,.

(39)

Note that
h_{0}
increases as
\tilde{h}\to\pm\infty
precisely because, as we go from
0
to
\pm(\mathbf{i}-\epsilon)\infty
on the contour of (
30
), we always half-encircle the zeros of
\cosh
clock-wise, in both directions; see Figure
3
. Eq. (
39
) is clearly reminiscent of (
22
). This is no coincidence. In fact, we can prove the following relation between the elementary cycles (see Appendix
A
):

Z(\mathcal{U}_{h_{1},h_{2}})=\frac{2\pi\mathbf{i}e^{2\pi\mathbf{i}\eta/b^{2}}}{\Gamma\left(1-\frac{2\eta}{b^{2}}\right)}\widetilde{Z}(\mathcal{I}_{h_{1},h_{2}})\,.

(40)

Combining equations (
30
), (
39
) and (
40
), we recover the elementary cycle decomposition (
22
).

Let us summarize what we learned so far. The elementary cycles of the Coulomb gas integral are in one-to-one correspondence with those of the two-site path integral. The continuation of the Coulomb gas integral to the time-like region is an infinite sum of elementary cycles. This sum is qualitatively distinct from that of the one-site decomposition (or the Gamma function integral) as no two cycles are simply related.

Since essential feature of the two-site Liouville integral can be captured by the one-variable Coulomb gas integral, we can use the latter as a proxy to understand the Stoked phenomena of the (two-site) Liouville action as we continue it from
c\geq 25
to
c\leq 1
. This is the subject of the next section.

3.4
Stokes phenomema of the Coulomb gas integral

In this section we study the saddle points, thimbles and Stokes phenomena of the Coulomb gas effective action, which we recall here:

S_{\text{eff}}({\tilde{\varphi}})=\frac{{\tilde{\varphi}}^{2}}{2b^{2}}+\frac{2\eta}{b^{2}}\ln\left(2\cosh\left(\frac{{\tilde{\varphi}}}{2}\right)\right)

(41)

The saddle points are by definition where the derivative of the action vanishes,
\partial_{{\tilde{\varphi}}}S_{\text{eff}}=0
. Here, they are independent of
b
, and can be found as the solutions to a familiar transcendental equation:

{\tilde{\varphi}}=\mathbf{i}y\,,\,y=-\eta\tan\left(\frac{y}{2}\right)\,.

(42)

As
\eta>0
, there is exactly one saddle point in each interval
\mathcal{I}_{\tilde{h}}
(
35
) connecting two neighboring zeros of the
\cosh
; so we shall call that saddle point
{\tilde{\varphi}}_{\tilde{h}}=\mathbf{i}y_{\tilde{h}}
. Strictly speaking, because of the multivaluedness of
S_{\text{eff}}
, each saddle point
{\tilde{\varphi}}_{\tilde{h}}
is the projection of a series of identical copies
{\tilde{\varphi}}^{h_{0}}_{\tilde{h}}
where
h_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}/2
is such that
h_{0}\pm\tilde{h}/2
are integers, see (
37
) above. The same can be said about their thimbles. However, to keep the notations light we shall suppress the dependence on
h_{0}
unless when it is absolutely necessary to display it.

The thimble (usually known as the steepest descent contour) of
{\tilde{\varphi}}_{\tilde{h}}
, which we denote as
\mathcal{T}_{\tilde{h}}
, is defined by the solutions to the “upward flow” equation
Alexandru:2020wrj
;
witten2010new
that emanate from the saddle point:

\overline{\partial_{s}{\tilde{\varphi}}(s)}=\partial_{{\tilde{\varphi}}}S_{\text{eff}}({\tilde{\varphi}}(s))\,,\,\lim_{s\to-\infty}{\tilde{\varphi}}={\tilde{\varphi}}_{\tilde{h}}\,.

(43)

It is well-known that, as
{\tilde{\varphi}}
flows along the thimble and away from the saddle point, the real part of the action increases while the imaginary part is a constant:

\mathrm{Re}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}S_{\text{eff}}}{\mathrm{d}s}\right]>0\,,\,\mathrm{Im}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}S_{\text{eff}}}{\mathrm{d}s}\right]=0\,.

(44)

As a result, as
s\to+\infty
, one of the followings happen: (i)
S_{\text{eff}}\to\infty
; this implies that
{\tilde{\varphi}}\to\infty
or
{\tilde{\varphi}}
tends to a zero of
\cosh
in (
41
) (the latter can happen only if
\mathrm{Re}(b^{2})<0
, since
\eta>0
); (ii) the thimble converges to a saddle point (which can be the same as
{\tilde{\varphi}}_{\tilde{h}}
):
{\tilde{\varphi}}(s)\to{\tilde{\varphi}}_{\tilde{h}^{\prime}}
. The latter can happen only if the action has the same imaginary part on both saddles:

\mathrm{Im}(S_{\text{eff}}({\tilde{\varphi}}_{\tilde{h}^{\prime}}))-\mathrm{Im}(S_{\text{eff}}({\tilde{\varphi}}_{\tilde{h}}))=0\,.

(45)

As the left hand side of this equation smoothly depends on the parameters of the action, the possibility (ii) cannot happen generically and only does so when the parameters are fine-tuned, i.e., when they hit a “Stokes wall”. As the parameters vary across a Stokes wall, the topology of the thimbles goes through an abrupt transition known as a Stokes phenomenon.

\tilde{h}

\eta=3

4
Lattice Liouville path integral

The Liouville action on a connected compact 2D surface
\Sigma
without boundary is defined as
2
2

2

Often, one parametrizes the action a bit differently, with an interaction term
e^{b\phi}
and a
b
-independent kinetic term. This parametrization is related to the ours by a change of variable
\varphi=b\phi
.

S=\int_{\Sigma}\left(\frac{1}{16\pi b^{2}}(\nabla\varphi)^{2}+\frac{1}{8\pi}(1+b^{-2})R\varphi+\mu e^{\varphi}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{A}\,.

(55)

Here,
\mathrm{d}\mathcal{A}
is the area element of the surface,
R
its scalar curvature, and
b
is related to the central charge
c
by

c=1+6\left(b+b^{-1}\right)^{2}\,.

(56)

\mu
is known as the cosmological constant, and affects correlation functions in a simple way. The standard observables are correlation functions between “vertex operators”, which are exponential of the field. So the general form of a Liouville path integral is the following:

\int[\mathcal{D}\varphi]\,e^{-S[\varphi]+a_{1}\varphi(z_{1})+\dots+a_{n}\varphi(z_{n})}

(57)

Here, the meaning of
\int[\mathcal{D}\varphi]
is well-understood only when
b^{2}>0
, or
c\geq 25
. We shall consider a naive regularization of this action on a 2D planar lattice. The field is defined on the faces of the lattice:

\varphi(z)\to\varphi_{r}\,.

The kinetic term will be replaced by a sum over lattice bonds:

\int_{\Sigma}\frac{1}{16\pi b^{2}}(\nabla\varphi)^{2}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{A}\to\sum_{\left<r,r^{\prime}\right>}\frac{K_{rr^{\prime}}}{2b^{2}}(\varphi_{r}-\varphi_{r^{\prime}})^{2}\,.

(58)

Here and below we denote a bond by the two faces
r,r^{\prime}
it separates. The coefficients
K_{rr^{\prime}}>0
are to be chosen appropriately as a function of the lattice to reproduce the continuum kinetic term in the scaling limit. For example, on a square lattice we may choose
K_{rr^{\prime}}=1/(8\pi)
(note that in 2D, the Gaussian free field has scaling dimension
0
, so
K
does not diverge with the lattice spacing in the continuum limit) The interaction term
\propto\mu
becomes

\mu\int_{\Sigma}e^{\varphi}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{A}\to\sum_{r}\mu_{r}e^{\varphi_{r}}\,,

(59)

where
\mu_{r}=\mu A_{r}
and
A_{r}
is the area of the face
r
. Finally, both the coupling to curvature and the vertex operator insertion can be accounted for by a position-dependent source
\eta_{r}
:

-\sum_{i}a_{i}\varphi(z_{i})+\int_{\Sigma}\frac{1}{8\pi}(1+b^{-2})R\varphi\mathrm{d}\mathcal{A}\to-\sum_{r}\frac{\eta_{r}}{b^{2}}\varphi_{r}\,.

(60)

In terms of
\eta_{r}
, the Seiberg bound at
c\geq 25
is again given by

\sum_{r}\eta_{r}>0\,.

(61)

In summary, we have a lattice Liouville action:

S[\varphi_{r}]=\sum_{\left<r,r^{\prime}\right>}\frac{K_{rr^{\prime}}}{2b^{2}}(\varphi_{r}-\varphi_{r^{\prime}})^{2}+\sum_{r}\left[\mu_{r}e^{\varphi_{r}}-\frac{\eta_{r}}{b^{2}}\varphi_{r}\right]\,.

(62)

When the Seiberg bound is satisfied, the
c\geq 25
Liouville theory can be defined by the continuum limit of a lattice path integral where every
\varphi_{r}
is integrated over the real line:

Z(\mathbb{R}^{N})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\prod_{r}\mathrm{d}\varphi_{r}e^{-S[\varphi_{r}]}\,,

(63)

where
N
is the number of faces of the planar lattice. Based on rigorous results
david16
and numerical tests
lft
, we expect the lattice path integral has a continuum limit described by the
c\geq 25
Liouville theory provided the Seiberg bounds
seiberg90
and also
b<1
. Our goal here is to calculate the analytical continuation of the lattice path integral as
b^{2}
rotates clockwise from
\mathbb{R}_{+}
to
\mathbb{R}_{-}
, that is, we let

b^{2}=|b^{2}|e^{-\mathbf{i}\theta}\,,\,\theta:0\to\pi\,,

while the
\eta_{r}
’s are kept fixed. In the continuum theory, this amounts to fixing the surface geometry (hence
R
), and rotating
\eta_{r}
along with
b^{2}
so that
\eta_{r}/b^{2}
is kept fixed; in particular,
\eta_{r}
will change sign as does
b^{2}
.

4.1
The zero mode approach

It is helpful to review first the zero mode approach, which is well-understood when
c\geq 25
, and appreciate the issues involved in its analytical continuation. Like in the two-site model, we write the Liouville field as a sum of a zero mode
\varphi_{0}
and a fluctuating field
{\tilde{\varphi}}_{r}
that has vanishing sum:

\varphi_{r}=\varphi_{0}+{\tilde{\varphi}}_{r}\,,\,\sum_{r}{\tilde{\varphi}}_{r}=0\,.

(64)

To evaluate the
c\geq 25
path integral, we can integrate over the zero mode: for any fixed
{\tilde{\varphi}}
, the zero-mode integral one-site Gamma function integral (
3
). Introducing the notations
3
3

3

A[{\tilde{\varphi}}]

\displaystyle n:=-\sum_{r}\frac{\eta_{r}}{b^{2}}\,,\,

\displaystyle A[{\tilde{\varphi}}]:=\sum_{r}\mu_{r}e^{{\tilde{\varphi}}_{r}}\,,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}\varphi_{0}\;e^{-S[\varphi]}

It remains to consider the continuation of the fluctuation mode integral in the right hand side of (

68

A[{\tilde{\varphi}}]^{n}

{\tilde{\varphi}}

\mathbb{C}^{N}

is multi-valued and we need to specify the integration cycle with respect to the branch cuts. We have encountered a particularly mild version of this problem in the two-site toy model above. In that model,
{\tilde{\varphi}}

One exception is when

\widehat{Z}/c=\frac{1}{n!}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left[\mathcal{D}\psi\right]\exp(-S_{0}[\psi])e^{-\mathbf{i}\sum_{r}{\eta_{r}\psi_{r}}/{\beta}}A[\mathbf{i}\beta\psi]^{n}\,,\,b^{2}<0\,,

(71)

where

S_{0}[\psi]=\frac{K_{rr^{\prime}}}{2}(\psi_{r}-\psi_{r^{\prime}})^{2}\,.

Hence, we can view

. Then, expanding the “observable”

\widehat{Z}/c=Z_{\text{GFF}}\sum_{\sum_{r}n_{r}=n}\frac{1}{n_{r}!}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{rr^{\prime}}\left(n_{r}\beta-{\eta_{r}}/{\beta}\right)G_{rr^{\prime}}\left(n_{r^{\prime}}\beta-{\eta_{r}}/{\beta}\right)\right]

(73)

where the sum is over configurations with

55
), and

is the partition function of the Gaussian free field. In the continuum limit, (

n
positions on the surface
\Sigma

In summary, the
c\geq 25\to c\leq 1
continuation of the Liouville path integral gives rise to the Coulomb gas integral at special values of the parameters, which are known as satisfying a “charge neutrality” condition. Yet, for generic parameters, the zero-mode approach does not seem to lead anything tractable.

4.2
Elementary cycle decomposition

In this section, we apply the methods of Section
3.1
to the general lattice Liouville path integral. We shall assume that the charges of the vertex operators are real,
a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
, and that the Seiberg bound is satisfied. Equivalently, in terms of
\eta_{r}

(74)

In particular, some
\eta_{r}
can be negative.

We first decouple the kinetic term in (

\chi_{rr^{\prime}}
for each edge

\displaystyle=\int\prod_{\left<rr^{\prime}\right>}\frac{e^{\frac{b^{2}\chi_{rr^{\prime}}^{2}}{2K_{rr^{\prime}}}}\mathrm{d}\chi_{rr^{\prime}}}{\mathbf{i}\sqrt{2\pi K_{rr^{\prime}}/b^{2}}}\prod_{r}\exp(-n_{r}\varphi_{r}-\mu_{r}e^{\varphi_{r}})

One equivalent way of (

;
fredenhagen-shomerus

The discrete sum over the height function in (
84
) and (
85
) suggests a statistical interpretation in terms of loop models. An
O(n)
lattice loop model can be defined by a partition sum over non-intersecting loop ensembles on a planar lattice, with a fugacity
n
coupled to the number of loops. (The fugacity coupled to the total loop length is adjusted so that the system is critical.) It is common practice to orient the loops, and assign a complex weight
e^{\pm\mathbf{i}\pi e_{0}}
to each of the orientations, such that
n=2\cos(\pi e_{0})
. Then, the oriented loop configuration defines a discrete height field
h_{\mathbf{r}}
on the faces,
up to an overall shift (zero mode)
, such that the oriented loops are the isotopic lines of
h_{\mathbf{r}}
: whenever one crosses a loop from its left to its right,
h_{\mathbf{r}}
increments by
1
. Well-known arguments
nienhuis
;
kondev
;
estienne2015correlation
show that this height field is described by the
c\leq 1
Liouville action such that
c=1-6(b-1/b)^{2}
and

e_{0}=1-b^{-2}\,,\,b\in[1/\sqrt{2},\sqrt{2}]\,.

In particular, each
n\in[-2,2)
correspond to two values of
c
, which describe the dilute and dense fixed points, respectively. This identification allowed to obtain critical exponents by simple free field calculations. These calculations are feasible partly because the correlation functions in question depend only on the difference between
h_{\mathbf{r}}
evaluated at two points, and one does not need to specify the zero mode. This is not the case for generic correlation functions beyond two points: in addition to summing over the loop configurations, we need to specify the zero mode so that the vertex operators
e^{\alpha\varphi_{\mathbf{r}}}\to e^{\alpha 2\pi\mathbf{i}h_{\mathbf{r}}}
can be evaluated without ambiguity. For instance, the loop model interpretation of the
c\leq 1
Liouville structure constant put forward in Ref.
IJS
involves a nontrivial prescription of the zero mode. If the three-point function is evaluated on
z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}
, then
h_{\mathbf{r}}=0
for
\mathbf{r}\in G
, where
G
is the unique region such that such that for any
i\in\{1,2,3\}
,
G
is adjacent to a loop that surrounds
z_{i}
. It is unclear how to generalize this geometric prescription to four point functions.

Now, our result (
84
) provides an interesting new prescription if we identify the integer-valued height function of the loop model with that indexing the elementary cycles. We should sum over all zero modes such that
\min_{\mathbf{r}}h_{\mathbf{r}}\geq 0
in order to calculate the continuation of the
c\geq 25
path integral, or fix the zero mode such that
\min_{\mathbf{r}}h_{\mathbf{r}}=0
to calculate the reduced continuation
\widehat{Z}
.
We note that our prescription is different from that of
IJS
on the lattice, and we expect this difference to persist in the continuum limit. Indeed, our prescription is intended to reproduce the analytical continuation of the
c\geq 25
Liouville correlation functions, while the latter gives rise to
c\leq 1
Liouville correlation functions.
Identifying the height functions is reasonable
in the continuum limit
. On each individual elementary cycle, fluctuation of the field
\varphi
is suppressed:
\mathrm{Im}(\varphi_{r})
is confined in an interval of length
2\pi
, and
\mathrm{Re}(\varphi_{r})\to+\infty
along the elementary cycle is prohibited energetically. As a result, critical fluctuations can only result from the sum over
h_{{r}}
’s, weighted by an elastic energy
\propto\sum_{rr^{\prime}}(h_{{r}}-h_{{r}^{\prime}})^{2}
plus source terms
\propto\eta_{r}h_{r}
(in other words, we approximate
\varphi_{r}\approx(2h_{r}+1)\pi\mathbf{i}
in an elementary cycle). Such a partition sum is equivalent to that of the loop-model height functions, up to irrelevant perturbations, e.g., those restricting the height difference between neighboring faces
nienhuis
;
kondev
.

5
Conclusion

We considered the problem of analytically continuing the lattice Liouville path integral (
63
) from
c\geq 25
to generic values of
c
, focusing on the vicinity of
c\in(-\infty,1]
. We showed that the result (
84
) can be written as an discrete sum over elementary cycles, defined in (
81
). These elementary cycles are a straightforward generalization of the inverse Gamma function contours (
4
), appear to be a useful basis of the integration cycles of the Liouville action. We illustrated the convenience of this basis, compared to the Lefshetz thimbles, with a detailed analysis of the two-site toy model (Section

Appendix B
Thimble numerics

In this appendix we describe how to construct numerically the thimble of an action with two degrees of freedom,
S(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2})
,
(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
, associated with a critical point. Without loss of generality we can assume that the critical point is
\varphi_{1}=0,\varphi_{2}=0
. We also assume that the Hessian is non-degenerate, that is,

S(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2})=S_{c}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}\varphi_{i}H_{ij}\varphi_{j}+\mathcal{O}(\varphi^{3})\,,\,

(91)

and
H
is an invertible complex symmetric matrix.

In principle, the basic idea is very simple: the steepest descent contours that end at the saddle point (as
s\to-\infty
) can be parametrized by one parameter, say the angle
\theta
at which it approaches the saddle point. Hence, we can integrate the upward flow for a dense lattice of
\theta
’s, and obtain a good approximation of the thimble parametrized by the polar coordinate
(s,\theta)
. To integrate over the thimble, it suffices to know the Jacobian
\partial(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2})/\partial(s,\theta)
; indeed,

\int_{\mathcal{T}}e^{-S}\mathrm{d}\varphi_{1}\mathrm{d}\varphi_{2}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\theta\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}s\,e^{-S(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2})}\,\det\left[\frac{\partial(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2})}{\partial(s,\theta)}\right]\,.

(92)

The Jacobian can be obtained by integrating the “adjoint flow”
Alexandru:2020wrj
, see (
98
) below.

There is however a practical issue which renders the above naive approach numerically unfeasible. The upward flow, linearized near the saddle point:

\partial_{s}\varphi_{i}=\sum_{j}\overline{H_{ij}\varphi_{j}}+\mathcal{O}(\varphi^{2})

(93)

will have two different local Lyapunov exponents. As a result, the polar coordinate is a singular parametrization of the thimble, which is problematic for numerical integration. To resolve this issue, we shall find a linear change of variables

\begin{pmatrix}\varphi_{1}\\
\varphi_{2}\end{pmatrix}=U\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{1}\\
\phi_{2}\end{pmatrix}

(94)

such that

U^{T}HU=I\,.

(95)

Then, the upward flow in the coordinate system
\phi
,

\partial_{s}\phi_{i}=\overline{\frac{\partial S}{\partial\phi_{i}}}\,,

(96)

will have a linearization with equal Lyapunov exponents. Now, the upward flow is
not
invariant under a change of variable. Rather, (
96
) transforms to

\partial_{s}\varphi_{i}=g_{ij}\overline{\frac{\partial S}{\partial\varphi_{j}}}\,,\,\text{where }g={U}U^{\dagger}\,.

(97)

Note that
g
is an invertible Hermitian matrix, and thus can be viewed as a metric. The modified flow (
97
) will change the geometric shape of the thimble, but not its topology, so the path integral will have the same value.

H

v^{-1}Hv=w
, where
w
is a diagonal matrix. The symmetry of
H
ensures that
