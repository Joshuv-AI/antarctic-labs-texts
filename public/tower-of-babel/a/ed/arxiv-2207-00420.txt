Cactus Mechanisms: Optimal Differential Privacy Mechanisms in the Large-Composition Regime
Wael Alghamdi, Shahab Asoodeh, Flavio P. Calmon, Oliver Kosut, Lalitha Sankar, Fei Wei

Abstract
Most differential privacy mechanisms are applied (i.e., composed) numerous times on sensitive data. We study the design of optimal differential privacy mechanisms in the limit of a large number of compositions. As a consequence of the law of large numbers, in this regime the best privacy mechanism is the one that minimizes the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the conditional output distributions of the mechanism given two different inputs. We formulate an optimization problem to minimize this divergence subject to a cost constraint on the noise. We first prove that additive mechanisms are optimal. Since the optimization problem is infinite dimensional, it cannot be solved directly; nevertheless, we quantize the problem to derive near-optimal additive mechanisms that we call “cactus mechanisms” due to their shape. We show that our quantization approach can be arbitrarily close to an optimal mechanism. Surprisingly, for quadratic cost, the Gaussian mechanism is strictly sub-optimal compared to this cactus mechanism. Finally, we provide numerical results which indicate that cactus mechanism outperforms the Gaussian mechanism for a finite number of compositions.

Introduction

Likelihood ratios are at the heart of most privacy metrics. Consider the problem of quantifying the privacy loss suffered by a sensitive variable
X
given an observation of a disclosed variable
Y
. For example,
X
may represent a dataset and
Y
a randomized function computed over
X
. Privacy can be measured in terms of properties of the
privacy loss random variable
, defined as

L_{x,x^{\prime}}\coloneqq\log\frac{dP_{Y|X=x}}{dP_{Y|X=x^{\prime}}}(Y),

(1)

where
Y\sim P_{Y|X=x}
and
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}\coloneqq\mathsf{supp}(X).
The channel
P_{Y|X}
is often referred to as a
privacy mechanism
.

Today, the most popular privacy definition (including, in practice
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
) is
differential privacy
(DP), which quantifies privacy in terms of
L_{x,x^{\prime}}
when
x,x^{\prime}
are close or “neighboring.” Thus, given a metric
d:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
,
P_{Y|X}
is said to be
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differentially private (
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP)
[
5
]
if

\sup_{d(x,x^{\prime})\leq s}\ \sup_{A\subset\mathcal{Y}}\left[P_{Y|X=x}(A)-e^{\varepsilon}P_{Y|X=x^{\prime}}(A)\right]\leq\delta,

(2)

where
s
determines when inputs
x
and
x^{\prime}
are neighboring, and
\mathcal{Y}:=\mathsf{supp}(Y)
. Intuitively, if a mechanism is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differentially private for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
and
\delta
, then an adversary observing
Y
cannot accurately distinguish between small changes in
X
.

Most privacy mechanisms are applied several times on sensitive data. Quantifying privacy guarantees under multiple
compositions
of a mechanism is a challenging problem. In the simple case where the same mechanism
P_{Y|X}
is independently applied
n
times on data
X
generating output
Y^{n}
, i.e.,
P_{Y^{n}|X}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}P_{Y_{i}|X}
, the privacy loss random variable is given by

L_{x,x^{\prime}}^{n}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\frac{dP_{Y_{i}|X=x}}{dP_{Y_{i}|X=x^{\prime}}}(Y_{i}),

(3)

where
Y_{i}\sim P_{Y_{i}|X=x}.
Differential privacy can be cast in terms of the privacy loss random variable. The reader can directly verify that
n
independent applications of a mechanism
P_{Y|X}
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP if

\sup_{d(x,x^{\prime})\leq s}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(1-e^{-(L_{x,x^{\prime}}^{n}-\varepsilon)}\right)^{+}\right]\leq\delta.

(4)

From the law of large numbers, the distribution of
L_{x,x^{\prime}}^{n}/n
will concentrate around its mean, the KL-divergence, as

\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[L_{x,x^{\prime}}^{n}\right]=D\left(P_{Y|X=x}\|P_{Y|X=x^{\prime}}\right).

(5)

Since the function
f(u)\coloneqq(1-e^{-nu+\varepsilon})^{+}
is non-decreasing, in the limit of large compositions, privacy mechanisms with lower values of
D(P_{Y|X=x}\|P_{Y|X=x^{\prime}})
will enjoy stronger
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP guarantees. Thus, regardless of the exact distribution of the privacy loss random variable, its mean (
5
) plays a central role in the privacy guarantees offered after many compositions. In applications such as privacy-ensuring machine learning, the number of compositions frequently exceeds
n=10^{3}
.

We study the design of privacy mechanisms with favorable
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP guarantees under a large number of compositions. Our approach departs from previous work in that we focus on the large-composition regime instead of optimizing (
2
). Since after many compositions, privacy will be mostly determined by the mean of the privacy loss random variable (
5
), we solve the optimization problem

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\inf_{P_{Y|X}\in\mathscr{R}}&\displaystyle\displaystyle\sup_{|x-x^{\prime}|\leq s}D(P_{Y|X=x}\|P_{Y|X=x^{\prime}})\\[8.61108pt]
\text{subject to}&\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}~\mathbb{E}[c(Y-x)\mid X=x]\leq C,\end{array}

(6)

where
c:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
is a pre-specified cost function,
s,C>0
are constants, and
\mathscr{R}
is the set of all Markov kernels on
\mathbb{R}
. Note that the cost function is critical: without the constraint, (
6
) can be trivially solved by any mechanism that is independent of
X
.

Our main contributions are as follows:

1.

We show (Thm.
1
) that additive mechanisms—i.e., where
Y=X+Z
for a noise variable
Z
independent of
X
—suffice to minimize (
6
).

2.

Even restricting to additive mechanisms, (
6
) is an infinite-dimensional optimization problem, so it cannot be solved directly. Instead, we formulate an approximate problem that is finite dimensional and can be solved efficiently. We prove (Thm.
3
) that this approximate problem can get arbitrary close to optimal.

3.

We solve the approximate problem to derive (near) optimal mechanisms for the quadratic cost function, i.e.,
c(x)=x^{2}
. We dub the resulting mechanism the “cactus mechanism” due to the shape of the distribution (see Fig.
1
). Surprisingly, the Gaussian distribution is strictly sub-optimal for (
6
), as the cactus mechanism achieves a smaller KL divergence for the same variance.

4.

We bound the
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP for the cactus mechanism in the context of sub-sampled stochastic gradient descent using the moments accountant method. Compared to the same analysis applied to a Gaussian mechanism, our approach does better for a reasonable number of compositions.

1.1
Related Work

Identifying optimal mechanisms is a fundamental and challenging problem in the domain of differential privacy. There have been several works in the literature that have attempted to address this problem. For instance, within the class of additive noise mechanisms and under the single shot setting (i.e., no composition), Ghosh et al.
[
6
]

showed that the geometric mechanism is universally optimal for
(\varepsilon,0)
-DP in a Bayesian framework, and Gupte and
Sundararajan
[
7
]
derived the optimal noise distribution in a minimax cost framework. For a rather general cost function, the optimal noise distribution was shown to have a staircase-shaped density function
[
8
,
9
,
10

.

Geng and Viswanath
[
11

showed that for
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP and integer-valued query functions, in the single-shot setting, the discrete uniform noise distribution and the discrete Laplacian noise distribution are asymptotically optimal (for

and

\varepsilon

[
12

studied the same setting except for real-valued query functions and identified truncated Laplace distribution is asymptotically optimal in various high privacy regimes. Finally, Geng et al.
[
13
]
showed that the optimal noise distribution for real-valued query and
(0,\delta)
-DP is uniform with probability
mass at the origin. Our work differs from these works in that we focus on the optimal mechanisms under a large number of compositions, rather than the single shot setting.

When considering a composition of
n
mechanisms, an important line of research has been to derive tighter composition results: relationships between the DP parameters of the composed mechanism and the parameters of each constituent mechanism. There are several composition results in the literature, such as
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
. More recently, Dong et al.
[
20
]
have proposed a composition result for large

and for a new variant of DP, called Gaussian-DP, that leverages the central limit theorem. These results can be sub-optimal (see, for example,
[
21
, Fig. 1]
). Consequently, numerical composition results have gained increasing traction as they lead to easier, yet powerful, methods for accounting the privacy loss in composition
[
22
,
21
,
23
,
24
]
. In particular,
Koskela et al.
[
22
]
obtained a numerical composition result based on a numerical approximation of an integral that gives the DP parameters of the composed mechanism. The approximation is carried out by discretizing the integral and by evaluating discrete convolutions via the fast Fourier transform algorithm. The running time and memory needed for this approximation were subsequently improved
[
21

. While our work shares the focus on the large composition regime, we are primarily interested in synthesizing optimal mechanisms rather than analyzing existing mechanisms.

2
Optimality of Additive Continuous Channels

We start by deriving characterizations of solutions to the optimization problem (
6
). The difficulty of this problem lies in the fact that we are optimizing over all conditional distributions. This not only makes the problem infinite-dimensional, but it also renders direct approaches ineffective. The main result of this section, shown in Theorem
1
, is that it suffices to consider continuous additive channels. In other words, the optimization in (
6
) may be restricted to conditional distributions of the form
P_{Y|X=x}=T_{x}P
for some Borel probability measure
P
on
\mathbb{R}
that is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. Equipped with this reduction, we build in the next section an explicit family of finitely-parametrized distributions that are also optimal in (
6
).

2.1
Assumptions and Definitions

Throughout the paper, we require the cost function to satisfy the following properties.

Assumption 1
.

The cost function
c:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies:

•

Positivity:

c(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R},
and
c(0)=0.

•

Symmetry:

c(x)=c(-x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

•

Monotonicity:

c(x)\leq c(x^{\prime})
if
|x|\leq|x^{\prime}|
.

•

Continuity:

c
is continuous over
\mathbb{R}.

•

Tail regularity:
There exist
\alpha,\beta>0
such that
c(x)\sim\beta x^{\alpha}
as
x\to\infty.

A natural choice of cost function is the quadratic cost
c(x)=x^{2}
, but we allow
c(x)
to be any function that satisfies the above assumptions. For example,
c(x)=|x|^{\alpha}
for any positive
\alpha
is a natural family of cost functions.

Let
\mathscr{P}\subset\mathscr{R}
be the set of conditional distributions
P_{Y|X}
satisfying the cost constraint in (
6
), i.e., set

\mathscr{P}:=\left\{P_{Y|X}\in\mathscr{R}~;~\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}~\mathbb{E}[c(Y-x)\mid X=x]\leq C\right\}.

(7)

The infimal value in (
6
) is then

\text{KL}^{\star}:=\inf_{P_{Y|X}\in\mathscr{P}}\ \sup_{x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}:|x-x^{\prime}|\leq s}\ D(P_{Y|X=x}\|P_{Y|X=x^{\prime}}).

(8)

We are interested in computing
\text{KL}^{\star},
as well as mechanisms
P_{Y|X}
that approach this optimal value. Note that, for clarity of presentation, we suppress the dependence on
(s,c,C)
in the notations
\mathscr{P}
and
\text{KL}^{\star}.

In the main problem (
6
), we allow
P_{Y|X}
to be any mechanism that produces
Y
given
X
. A more restrictive but natural and easy-to-implement class of mechanisms is the
additive
mechanism class. An additive mechanism is given by
P_{Y|X=x}(B)=T_{x}P(B)
where
P
is a Borel probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
. In other words, an additive mechanism
P_{Y|X}
has
Y
of the form
Y=X+Z
for some noise random variable
Z\sim P\in\mathscr{B}
that is independent of the input
X.
Let
\mathscr{P}_{\text{add}}\subset\mathscr{B}
be the set of additive mechanisms satisfying the cost constraint in (
6
),

\mathscr{P}_{\text{add}}:=\left\{P\in\mathscr{B}~;~\mathbb{E}_{P}[c]\leq C\right\}.

(9)

Since the KL-divergence is shift-invariant, restricting the optimization (
6
) to additive mechanisms amounts to considering the simplified optimization problem

\text{KL}_{\text{add}}^{\star}:=\inf_{P\in\mathscr{P}_{\text{add}}}\ \sup_{a\in\mathbb{R}:|a|\leq s}\ D(P\|T_{a}P).

(10)

Of course, it is immediate that
\text{KL}^{\star}\leq\text{KL}_{\text{add}}^{\star}
. In fact, we will show below that these quantities are the same, meaning that there is no loss in restricting to additive mechanisms.

2.2
Optimality of Continuous Additive Mechanisms

The optimization problem in (
6
) is a convex problem, but the fact that the feasible set
\mathscr{P}
is of infinite dimension means it cannot be solved directly, nor do the tractable properties one expects of a convex optimization problem necessarily follow. For example, in any finite dimensional convex optimization problem, a symmetry in the problem leads to the same symmetry in the solution. In this problem, one can see that shifting the mechanism—i.e., given
P_{Y|X}
, construct
Q_{Y|X=x}(B)=P_{Y|X=x+z}(B+z)
for some
z
—does not change the cost constraint nor the objective value in (
6
). Thus, one might be inclined to conclude that the optimal mechanism is invariant to a shift (i.e., is an additive mechanism). Unfortunately, the infinite-dimensional nature of the problem means that this conclusion is not immediate. We resolve this issue in the following theorem which states that additive mechanisms are in fact optimal in (
6
).

Theorem 1
.

We have that

\textup{KL}^{\star}=\textup{KL}_{\textup{add}}^{\star},

(11)

and there exists a
P^{\star}\in\mathscr{P}_{\textup{add}}
achieving this value. Further, any such
P^{\star}
is necessarily absolutely continuous.

Proof sketch.

The proof is given in Appendix
A
.
We give here only a high level description of the approach. Let
P_{Y|X}^{(k)}
be a sequence achieving
\text{KL}^{\star}
. We make these mechanisms increasingly closer to being additive, while sacrificing neither feasibility nor utility, by considering the convex combinations

\overline{P}_{Y|X=x}^{(k)}(B):=\mathbb{E}\left[P_{Y|X=x+Z_{k}}^{(k)}(B+Z_{k})\right]

(12)

where
Z_{k}\sim\mathrm{Unif}([-k,k])
. Specifically, one can invoke Prokhorov’s theorem on the
\overline{P}_{Y|X}^{(k)}
, thereby extracting a probability measure
P^{\star}
such that
\overline{P}_{Y|X=x}^{(k)}\to T_{x}P^{\star}
weakly for each fixed
x
. Finally, we show that the mechanism
P^{\star}
is optimal by invoking joint convexity and lower-semicontinuity of the KL-divergence.
∎

Remark 1
.

The proof of
P^{\star}\ll\lambda
only relies on the property that
P^{\star}\ll T_{a}P^{\star}
for every
|a|\leq s
, which holds in view of
\text{KL}^{\star}<\infty
. Therefore, any
feasible
additive mechanism must be absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, i.e., if
\mu\in\mathscr{B}
satisfies
\sup_{|a|\leq s}D(\mu\|T_{a}\mu)<\infty
then we necessarily have
\mu\ll\lambda
.

Numerical Approximation: The Cactus Distribution

The optimization problem over additive mechanisms in (
10
) is infinite-dimensional, so it cannot be solved numerically as-is, and it appears to have no closed-form solution for non-trivial cost functions. The lack of closed-form solution is true even for the simple case of
c(x)=x^{2}
: to our surprise, as will be illustrated later, the Gaussian mechanism is not optimal!
2
2

2

Of course, simply because Gaussian is not optimal does not imply that there is no closed-form solution. It is possible to write a set of KKT conditions for (
10
), which we have omitted from this paper in the interest of space. This set of KKT conditions cannot be solved in closed-form.
In our companion paper
[
1
]
, we explore the regime where
s\to 0^{+}

is
Gaussian—although for other costs the optimal distribution is much more surprising.

In the regime of fixed positive

, to find practically achievable near-optimal mechanisms, we resort to numerical approximation of (
10

s=1
. We can do this without loss of generality simply by scaling: that is, the optimization problem in (

and cost function

is equivalent to the same problem with sensitivity
1

To approximate (
10

Definition 1
.

Fix two positive integers
n
and
N
, and a constant
r\in(0,1)
. Consider the partition of
\mathbb{R}
by intervals
\{\mathcal{J}_{n,i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
defined by:
\mathcal{J}_{n,0}:=[-1/(2n),1/(2n)]
and

\mathcal{J}_{n,i}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\left(\frac{i-1/2}{n},\frac{i+1/2}{n}\right],&\text{if }i>0,\\
\left[\frac{i-1/2}{n},\frac{i+1/2}{n}\right),&\text{if }i<0.\end{array}\right.

(13)

We associate to each vector
\bm{p}=(p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{N})\in[0,1]^{N+1}
a piecewise constant function that is defined by

f_{n,r,\bm{p}}(x)=\begin{cases}np_{|i|},&\text{if }x\in\mathcal{J}_{n,i},\text{with }|i|<N,\\

(14)

We also associate with

the Borel measure

P_{n,r,\bm{p}}(B):=\int_{B}f_{n,r,\bm{p}}(x)\,dx.

(15)

Remark 2
.

Note that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{n,r,\bm{p}}(x)\,dx=p_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}2p_{i}+\frac{2p_{N}}{1-r}=:S_{r,\bm{p}}.

(16)

If

Appendix A
Proof of Theorem
1
: Optimality of Additive Continuous Channels

Let
F:\mathscr{R}\to[0,\infty]
denote the objective function in (
6
), i.e.,

F(P_{Y|X}):=\sup_{|u-v|\leq s}~D(P_{Y|X=u}\|P_{Y|X=v}).

(22)

Thus,

\text{KL}^{\star}=\inf_{P_{Y|X}\in\mathscr{P}}~F(P_{Y|X}).

(23)

Fix a sequence of conditional distributions

\left\{P_{Y|X}^{(k)}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathscr{P}

(24)

satisfying

\text{KL}^{\star}=\lim_{k\to\infty}F\left(P_{Y|X}^{(k)}\right).

(25)

Recall that by assumption, the version of each conditional distribution
P_{Y|X}^{(k)}
we choose is regular, i.e.,
x\mapsto P_{Y|X=x}^{(k)}(B)
is a Borel function for each Borel set
B\subset\mathbb{R}
. Note that
\text{KL}^{\star}<\infty
since, e.g., the Gaussian mechanism is feasible. Throwing away the first few elements in the sequence, we assume that
F\left(P_{Y|X}^{(k)}\right)<\infty
for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

We break the proof down into several steps:

1.

Introduce Markov kernels
\overline{P}_{Y|X}^{(k)}
as “continuous” convex combinations of the
P_{Y|X}^{(k)}
.

2.

The
\overline{P}_{Y|X}^{(k)}
also satisfy the cost constraint.

3.

The
\overline{P}_{Y|X}^{(k)}
asymptotically achieve
\text{KL}^{\star}
.

4.

The
\overline{P}_{Y|X=x}^{(k)}
are asymptotically shifted versions
T_{x}P^{\star}
of a fixed
P^{\star}\in\mathscr{B}
.

5.

P^{\star}
achieves
\text{KL}^{\star}
.

\bullet

Step 1
: Averaging the
P_{Y|X}^{(k)}
.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we will define the Markov kernel
\overline{P}_{Y|X}^{(k)}\in\mathscr{R}
by

\overline{P}_{Y|X=x}^{(k)}(B):=\frac{1}{2k}\int_{-k}^{k}P^{(k)}_{Y|X=x+z}(B+z)\,dz.

(26)

Of course, we need to check that (
26
) indeed yields a Markov kernel
\overline{P}_{Y|X}^{(k)}
. In view of Fubini’s theorem, it suffices to check that the map
(x,z)\mapsto P^{(k)}_{Y|X=x+z}(B+z)
is jointly Borel (for every fixed Borel set
B\subset\mathbb{R}
). This joint measurability is not self-evident, so we check next that it indeed holds.

Let the transition probability kernel
L^{(k)}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\to[0,1]
be defined by

L^{(k)}((x,z),A):=P_{Y|X=x+z}^{(k)}(A).

(27)

Let
N^{(k)}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\to[0,1]
denote the map

N^{(k)}((x,z),B):=P^{(k)}_{Y|X=x+z}(B+z).

(28)

For each
(x,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and Borel set
B\subset\mathbb{R}
, we may write
N^{(k)}((x,z),B)
as the integral of a nonnegative Borel function against
L((x,z),dy)
, namely,

N^{(k)}((x,z),B)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}1_{B}(y-z)\,L((x,z),dy).

(29)

Hence (see, e.g.,
[
25
, Chapter 1, Proposition 6.9]
)
(x,z)\mapsto N^{(k)}((x,z),B)
is a Borel function. Hence,
\overline{P}_{Y|X}^{(k)}
as given by (
26
) is indeed a well-defined Markov kernel on
\mathbb{R}
.

For the next steps, we will use the following notation

\displaystyle R^{(k,x)}_{Y|X=z}(B)

\displaystyle:=P_{Y|X=x+z}^{(k)}(B+z),

(30)

\displaystyle P^{(k,x)}(B)

\displaystyle:=\overline{P}_{Y|X=x}^{(k)}(B),

(31)

\displaystyle U^{(k)}(B)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2k}\cdot\lambda(B\cap[-k,k]).

(32)

Note that
R^{(k,x)}_{Y|X}\in\mathscr{R}
and
P^{(k,x)}\in\mathscr{B}
for each fixed
(k,x)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{R}
, and (
26
) may be rewritten as

P^{(k,x)}=R_{Y|X}^{(k,x)}\circ U^{(k)}.

(33)

\bullet

Step 2
: The
\overline{P}_{Y|X}^{(k)}
satisfy the cost constraint.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and we will show next that
\overline{P}_{Y|X}^{(k)}\in\mathscr{P}
, i.e., that
\overline{P}_{Y|X}^{(k)}
satisfies the cost constraint. Recall that a Markov kernel
P_{Y|X}\in\mathscr{R}
belongs to
\mathscr{P}
if and only if it satisfies

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}~\mathbb{E}_{P_{Y|X=x}}\left[T_{x}c\right]\leq C.

(34)

By the assumption that
P_{Y|X}^{(k)}\in\mathscr{P}
, we have that

\mathbb{E}_{P_{Y|X=x}^{(k)}}\left[T_{x}c\right]\leq C

(35)

for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Shifting the variable of integration in (
35
) by a fixed constant
-z
, we obtain that

\mathbb{E}_{T_{-z}P_{Y|X=x}^{(k)}}\left[T_{x-z}c\right]\leq C

(36)

for every
(x,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Replacing
x
by
x+z
in (
36
), we conclude that (see (
30
))

\mathbb{E}_{R^{(k,x)}_{Y|X=z}}\left[T_{x}c\right]\leq C

(37)

for every
(x,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. We proceed via the following standard approximation by simple functions argument.

Fix
x\in\mathbb{R}
, and let
\sum_{j}a_{j}1_{B_{j}}(y)
be a nonnegative simple function upper bounded by
(T_{x}c)(y)
. Integrating against
R^{(k,x)}_{Y|X=z}(dy)
we deduce from (
37
) that

\sum_{j}a_{j}R^{(k,x)}_{Y|X=z}(B_{j})\leq C

(38)

for every
z\in\mathbb{R}
. Integrating (
38
) against
U^{(k)}(dz)
, and noting that
P^{(k,x)}=R^{(k,x)}_{Y|X}\circ U^{(k)}
(see (
33
)), we deduce that

\sum_{j}a_{j}P^{(k,x)}(B_{j})\leq C.

(39)

Now, as (
39
) holds for all nonnegative simple functions below
T_{x}c
, taking an increasing sequence of nonnegative simple function converging pointwise to
T_{x}c
we conclude that

\mathbb{E}_{P^{(k,x)}}[T_{x}c]\leq C.

(40)

In other words (see (
31
)),

\mathbb{E}_{\overline{P}_{Y|X=x}^{(k)}}[T_{x}c]\leq C.

(41)

As (
41
) holds for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we have shown that
\overline{P}_{Y|X}^{(k)}\in\mathscr{P}
.

\bullet

Step 3
: The
\overline{P}_{Y|X}^{(k)}
are asymptotically optimal.

Next, we use monotonicity of the KL-divergence under conditioning (see Lemma
1
) to show the limit

\text{KL}^{\star}=\lim_{k\to\infty}F\left(\overline{P}_{Y|X}^{(k)}\right).

(42)

Shift-invariance of the KL-divergence implies that, for each
x,x^{\prime},z\in\mathbb{R}
,

D\left(R_{Y|X=z}^{(k,x)}\|R_{Y|X=z}^{(k,x^{\prime})}\right)=D\left(P_{Y|X=x+z}^{(k)}\|P_{Y|X=x^{\prime}+z}^{(k)}\right).

(43)

Thus, as
(x+z)-(x^{\prime}+z)=x-x^{\prime}
, we conclude that

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}|x-x^{\prime}|\leq s\\
z\in\mathbb{R}\end{subarray}}D\left(R_{Y|X=z}^{(k,x)}\|R_{Y|X=z}^{(k,x^{\prime})}\right)=F\left(P_{Y|X}^{(k)}\right)

(44)

By assumption of optimality of the
P_{Y|X}^{(k)}
(see (
25
)), there exists a
k_{0}
such that for all
k\geq k_{0}
,

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}|x-x^{\prime}|\leq s\\
z\in\mathbb{R}\end{subarray}}D\left(R_{Y|X=z}^{(k,x)}\|R_{Y|X=z}^{(k,x^{\prime})}\right)\leq\text{KL}^{\star}+\delta.

(45)

By definition of KL-divergence, we infer
R_{Y|X=z}^{(k,x)}\ll R_{Y|X=z}^{(k,x^{\prime})}
for all
z\in\mathbb{R}
and
|x-x^{\prime}|\leq s
. Also, (
45
) shows in particular that

\sup_{|x-x^{\prime}|\leq s}\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim U^{(k)}}\left[D\left(R_{Y|X=\xi}^{(k,x)}\|R_{Y|X=\xi}^{(k,x^{\prime})}\right)\right]\leq\text{KL}^{\star}+\delta.

(46)

Using (
33
), Lemma
1
yields that

\sup_{|x-x^{\prime}|\leq s}D\left(P^{(k,x)}\|P^{(k,x^{\prime})}\right)\leq\text{KL}^{\star}+\delta.

(47)

Taking
\delta\to 0^{+}
, we see that (
42
) holds.

\bullet

Step 4
:
P^{(k,x)}
is asymptotically
T_{x}P^{\star}
for a fixed
P^{\star}
.

Next, we show that there is a measure
P^{\star}\in\mathscr{B}
such that, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we have the weak convergence

P^{(k,x)}\to T_{x}P^{\star}

(48)

as
k\to\infty
.

First, for each fixed
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we establish the total-variation distance convergence

\lim_{k\to\infty}\left\|P^{(k,x)}-T_{x}P^{(k,0)}\right\|_{\text{TV}}=0.

(49)

We may write

\left(T_{x}P^{(k,0)}\right)(B)=\frac{1}{2k}\int_{-k-x}^{k-x}R_{Y|X=z}^{(k,x)}(B)\,dz.

(50)

Therefore, for any Borel set
B\subset\mathbb{R}
we have that

\displaystyle\left|P^{(k,x)}(B)-T_{x}P^{(k,0)}(B)\right|\leq\frac{1}{2k}\int_{[-k,k]\Delta[-k-x,k-x]}R_{Y|X=z}^{(k,x)}(B)\,dz\leq\frac{|x|}{k},

(51)

where
\Delta
denotes the symmetric difference. As the bound (
51
) is uniform in
B
, we conclude that the total-variation limit in (
49
) holds.

The next ingredient we need is that the set
\{P^{(k,0)}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathscr{B}
is tight, i.e., that for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists an
n>0
such that

\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}~P^{(k,0)}(\mathbb{R}\setminus[-n,n])\leq\varepsilon.

(52)

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. By the assumption that
c(x)\sim\beta|x|^{\alpha}
where
\alpha,\beta>0
, we have
\lim_{|x|\to\infty}c(x)=\infty
. Thus there exists an integer
n
such that
c(x)\geq C/\varepsilon
whenever
|x|\geq n.
Then, for each
(z,k)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle R_{Y|X=z}^{(k,0)}\left(\mathbb{R}\setminus[-n,n]\right)

\displaystyle=P_{Y|X=z}^{(k)}\left(\mathbb{R}\setminus[-n+z,n+z]\right)

(53)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-n+z,n+z]}\frac{c(y-z)}{c(n)}\,dP_{Y|X=z}^{(k)}(y)

(54)

\displaystyle\leq c(n)^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{P_{Y|X=z}^{(k)}}[T_{z}c]

(55)

\displaystyle\leq c(n)^{-1}\cdot C

(56)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon,

(57)

where (
54
) follows by monotonicity of
c
, (
55
) by nonnegativity of
c
, and (
56
) since
P_{Y|X}^{(k)}\in\mathscr{P}
. Hence,

\sup_{(z,k)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{N}}~R_{Y|X=z}^{(k,0)}\left(\mathbb{R}\setminus[-n,n]\right)\leq\varepsilon.

(58)

Averaging over
z
, we deduce that (
52
) holds, i.e., that
\{P^{(k,0)}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is tight.

By tightness of
\{P^{(k,0)}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, we conclude via Prokhorov’s theorem
[
25
, Chapter 3, Theorem 5.13]
after passing to a subsequence that there is a
P^{\star}\in\mathscr{B}
such that
P^{(k,0)}\to P^{\star}
weakly as
k\to\infty
, i.e., for every continuous and bounded function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
we have

\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{P^{(k,0)}}[f]=\mathbb{E}_{P^{\star}}[f].

(59)

This immediately implies that, for each
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we also have

T_{x}P^{(k,0)}\to T_{x}P^{\star}

(60)

weakly as
k\to\infty
. As convergence in total variation is stronger than weak convergence, we conclude from (
49
) and (
60
) that for every
x\in\mathbb{R}

P^{(k,x)}\to T_{x}P^{\star}

(61)

weakly as
k\to\infty
.

\bullet

Step 5
: The additive mechanism
P^{\star}
is optimal.

The final step is showing that
P^{\star}
attains
\text{KL}^{\star}
and satisfies the cost constraint. By joint lower-semicontinuity of the KL-divergence
[
27
, Theorem 1]
, we deduce from (
61
) that for each
x\in\mathbb{R}

D(P^{\star}\|T_{x}P^{\star})\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}D\left(P^{(k,0)}\|P^{(k,x)}\right).

(62)

But we also have

\sup_{|x|\leq s}D\left(P^{(k,0)}\|P^{(k,x)}\right)\leq F\left(\overline{P}_{Y|X}^{(k)}\right).

(63)

Therefore, taking the supremum over
|x|\leq s
in (
62
), we infer from (
42
) that

\sup_{|x|\leq s}D(P^{\star}\|T_{x}P^{\star})\leq\text{KL}^{\star}.

(64)

Hence, it only remains to check that
P^{\star}\in\mathscr{P}_{\text{add}}
for us to conclude that equality holds in (
64
).

For every
A>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
, the function
1_{[-A,A]}\cdot T_{x}c
is continuous and bounded. Hence, the weak convergence
P^{(k,x)}\to T_{x}P^{\star}
yields

\mathbb{E}_{T_{x}P^{\star}}\left[1_{[-A,A]}\cdot T_{x}c\right]=\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{P^{(k,x)}}\left[1_{[-A,A]}\cdot T_{x}c\right].

(65)

As
\overline{P}_{Y|X}^{(k)}\in\mathscr{P}
, nonnegativity of
c
implies in view of (
65
) that

\mathbb{E}_{T_{x}P^{\star}}\left[1_{[-A,A]}\cdot T_{x}c\right]\leq C.

(66)

By the monotone convergence theorem, taking
A\to\infty
yields

\mathbb{E}_{T_{x}P^{\star}}\left[T_{x}c\right]\leq C,

(67)

In other words,
P^{\star}\in\mathscr{P}_{\text{add}}
. Therefore, we must have

\text{KL}^{\star}\leq\text{KL}_{\text{add}}^{\star}\leq\sup_{|x|\leq s}~D(P^{\star}\|T_{x}P^{\star}).

(68)

Combining this inequality with (
64
), we conclude that

\text{KL}^{\star}=\text{KL}_{\text{add}}^{\star}=\sup_{|x|\leq s}~D(P^{\star}\|T_{x}P^{\star}).

(69)

This completes the proof of the first statement of the theorem.

For the last statement of the theorem, we show that the relation
\mu\ll T_{x}\mu
for every
|x|\leq s
(which holds for
P^{\star}
by (
69
) and
\text{KL}^{\star}<\infty
) is enough to conclude that
\mu\ll\lambda
. Fix a Borel set
B\subset\mathbb{R}
such that
\lambda(B)=0
, and we will show that
\mu(B)=0
. Note that the function
x\mapsto(T_{x}\mu)(B)
is Borel as it is given by the convolution
1_{B}\ast\eta
where
\eta(A):=\mu(-A)
. Then, by Tonelli’s theorem and translation-invariance of the Lebesgue measure,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}(T_{x}\mu)(B)\,d\lambda(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}1_{B-x}(b)\,d\mu(b)\,d\lambda(x)

(70)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}1_{B-x}(b)\,d\lambda(x)\,d\mu(b)

(71)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}1_{B-b}(x)\,d\lambda(x)\,d\mu(b)

(72)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(T_{b}\lambda)(B)\,d\mu(b)

(73)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lambda(B)\,d\mu(b)=0.

(74)

Thus,
(T_{x}\mu)(B)=0
for
\lambda
-almost every
x
. In particular,
(T_{x}\mu)(B)=0
for at least one
x\in[-s,s]
. Thus,
\mu\ll T_{x}\mu
implies
\mu(B)=0
, and the proof is complete.

Remark 4
.

The lemma stated below, showing that conditioning increases divergence, is a well-known fact. It is shown in the literature under various assumptions on the underlying distributions (see, e.g.,
[
28
, Theorem 2.2 and Section 2.6]
). We use it in the proof of Theorem
1
in the specific situation where one of the conditional distributions is absolutely continuous with respect to the other for each individual input. As in
[
28
, Remark 2.4]
, Doob’s version of the Radon-Nikodym theorem can be used to derive that conditioning increases divergence in our case. For completeness, we add a proof of this lemma here.

Lemma 1

(Conditioning increases divergence)
.

Let
P_{Y|X},P_{Y|X}^{\prime}
be Markov kernels on
\mathbb{R}
such that
P_{Y|X=x}\ll P_{Y|X=x}^{\prime}
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Then, denoting the marginalizations of
P_{X,Y}:=P_{Y|X}\otimes P_{X},P_{X,Y}^{\prime}:=P_{Y|X}^{\prime}\otimes P_{X}
in the second coordinate by
P_{Y},P_{Y}^{\prime}
, we have that

D\left(P_{Y}\|P_{Y}^{\prime}\right)\leq\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim P_{X}}\left[D\left(P_{Y|X=\xi}\|P_{Y|X=\xi}^{\prime}\right)\right].

(75)

Proof.

Since by assumption
P_{Y|X=x}\ll P_{Y|X=x}^{\prime}
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
, a generalization of the Radon-Nikodym theorem by Doob (see
[
25
, Chapter 5, Theorem 4.44]
) yields the existence of a version of the Radon-Nikodym derivatives
dP_{Y|X=x}/dP_{Y|X=x}^{\prime}
such that the function

(x,y)\mapsto\frac{dP_{Y|X=x}}{dP_{Y|X=x}^{\prime}}(y)

(76)

is jointly measurable. We show that this function is a version of
dP_{X,Y}/dP_{X,Y}^{\prime}
. First, note that
P_{X,Y}\ll P_{X,Y}^{\prime}
are equivalent. Indeed, for any Borel set
E\subset\mathbb{R}
, denoting the sections by
E_{x}:=\{y\in\mathbb{R}~;~(x,y)\in E\}
, we have that
P_{X,Y}(E)=0
if and only if
P_{Y|X=x}(E_{x})=0
for
P_{X}
-a.e.
x
, and a similar statement holds for
P_{X,Y}^{\prime}
. By assumption,
P_{Y|X=x}\ll P_{Y|X=x}^{\prime}
for each
x
, so we obtain
P_{X,Y}\ll P_{X,Y}^{\prime}
. By joint measurability and nonnegativity, using the disintegration theorem (see, e.g.,
[
25
, Chapter 1, Theorem 6.11]
) we obtain that for any Borel
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}

\displaystyle\int_{E}\frac{dP_{Y|X=x}}{dP_{Y|X=x}^{\prime}}(y)\,dP_{X,Y}^{\prime}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{E_{x}}\frac{dP_{Y|X=x}}{dP_{Y|X=x}^{\prime}}(y)\,dP_{Y|X=x}^{\prime}(y)\,dP_{X}(x)

(77)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{E_{x}}dP_{Y|X=x}(y)\,dP_{X}(x)

(78)

\displaystyle=P_{X,Y}(E).

(79)

Thus, we have the equality

\frac{dP_{X,Y}}{dP_{X,Y}^{\prime}}(x,y)=\frac{dP_{Y|X=x}}{dP_{Y|X=x}^{\prime}}(y)

(80)

for
P_{X,Y}^{\prime}
-a.e.
(x,y)
.

Define
f:[0,\infty)\to[-1/e,\infty)
by
f(0)=0
and
f(t)=t\log t
for
t>0
. By the disintegration theorem and (
80
), we have the equality

\displaystyle D\left(P_{X,Y}\|P_{X,Y}^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f\left(\frac{dP_{X,Y}}{dP_{X,Y}^{\prime}}\right)\,dP_{X,Y}^{\prime}

(81)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f\left(\frac{dP_{Y|X=x}}{dP_{Y|X=x}^{\prime}}(y)\right)\,dP_{Y|X=x}^{\prime}\,dP_{X}(x)

(82)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim P_{X}}\left[D\left(P_{Y|X=\xi}\|P_{Y|X=\xi}^{\prime}\right)\right].

(83)

On the other hand, disintegration with respect to
Y
yields the following bound. Denote by
P_{X|Y},P_{X|Y}^{\prime}
the disintegrations of
P_{X,Y},P_{X,Y}^{\prime}
with respect to
P_{Y},P_{Y}^{\prime}
. In particular,
P_{X|Y}
and
P_{X|Y}^{\prime}
are Markov kernels on
\mathbb{R}
. By the disintegration theorem and Jensen’s inequality,

\displaystyle D\left(P_{X,Y}\|P_{X,Y}^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f\left(\frac{dP_{X,Y}}{dP_{X,Y}^{\prime}}\right)\,dP_{X,Y}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f\left(\frac{dP_{X,Y}}{dP_{X,Y}^{\prime}}(x,y)\right)\,dP_{X|Y=x}^{\prime}(x)\,dP_{Y}^{\prime}(y)

(85)

(86)

(87)

is finite
P_{Y}^{\prime}
-a.e. In fact, we show that

. Note that

implies that
P_{Y}\ll P_{Y}^{\prime}
. Now, for any Borel

, the disintegration theorem yields that

\displaystyle\int_{B}g\,dP_{Y}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{B}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{dP_{X,Y}}{dP_{X,Y}^{\prime}}(x,y)\,dP_{X|Y=x}^{\prime}(x)\,dP_{Y}^{\prime}(y)

(88)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}\times B}\frac{dP_{X,Y}}{dP_{X,Y}^{\prime}}\,dP_{X,Y}^{\prime}

(89)

\displaystyle=P_{X,Y}(\mathbb{R}\times B)=P_{Y}(B).

(90)

Thus, we have that

(91)

for
P_{Y}^{\prime}
-a.e.

(92)

Combining inequality (

\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-z_{n},z_{n}]}q_{\sigma}\left|\log\frac{q_{\sigma}}{T_{a_{n}}q_{\sigma}}\right|\geq\varepsilon/2.

(165)

Since
[-1,1]
is a compact set, there exists a convergent subsequence
\{a_{n_{k}}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, say
a_{n_{k}}\to a
where
a\in[-1,1]
. Moreover, for any
z>0
, for sufficiently large
k
we have
z_{n_{k}}\geq z
, which implies

\limsup_{k\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-z,z]}q_{\sigma}\left|\log\frac{q_{\sigma}}{T_{a_{n_{k}}}q_{\sigma}}\right|\geq\varepsilon/2.

(166)

Recall that
\psi
is as defined in (
138
) and that, as shown in (
140
), it satisfies the inequality

\frac{\psi(x+y)}{\psi(x)}\leq\mathrm{exp}\left(|y|^{\gamma}\right)

(167)

for every
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
. Thus, for any
a,b,z\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle(T_{a}q_{\sigma})(z)

\displaystyle=q_{\sigma}(z-a)

(168)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}q(x)\frac{1}{\sigma}\psi\left(\frac{z-a-x}{\sigma}\right)dx

(169)

\displaystyle\leq e^{|a-b|^{\gamma}/\sigma^{\gamma}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}q(x)\frac{1}{\sigma}\psi\left(\frac{z-b-x}{\sigma}\right)dx

(170)

\displaystyle=e^{|a-b|^{\gamma}/\sigma^{\gamma}}(T_{b}q_{\sigma})(z).

(171)

Thus, for any
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, we have the uniform bound

\left\|\log\frac{T_{a}q_{\sigma}}{T_{b}q_{\sigma}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\left(\frac{|a-b|}{\sigma}\right)^{\gamma}.

(172)

Applying this bound to the integral in (
166
) gives

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-z,z]}q_{\sigma}\cdot\left|\log\frac{q_{\sigma}}{T_{a_{n_{k}}}q_{\sigma}}\right|

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-z,z]}q_{\sigma}\cdot\left|\log\frac{q_{\sigma}}{T_{a}q_{\sigma}}+\log\frac{T_{a}q_{\sigma}}{T_{a_{n_{k}}}q_{\sigma}}\right|

(173)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-z,z]}q_{\sigma}\cdot\left(\left|\log\frac{q_{\sigma}}{T_{a}q_{\sigma}}\right|+\left(\frac{|a_{n_{k}}-a|}{\sigma}\right)^{\gamma}\right)

(174)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{|a_{n_{k}}-a|}{\sigma}\right)^{\gamma}+\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-z,z]}q_{\sigma}\cdot\left|\log\frac{q_{\sigma}}{T_{a}q_{\sigma}}\right|.

(175)

Recalling inequality (
166
) and that
a_{n_{k}}\to a
as
k\to\infty
, we have, for any
z>0
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-z,z]}q_{\sigma}\left|\log\frac{q_{\sigma}}{T_{a}q_{\sigma}}\right|\geq\varepsilon/2.

(176)

Finally, note that by finiteness of the KL-divergence
D(q_{\sigma}\|T_{a}q_{\sigma})
(see (
163
)), we also have that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}q_{\sigma}\left|\log\frac{q_{\sigma}}{T_{a}q_{\sigma}}\right|<\infty.

(177)

Indeed, the function
f(t):=t\log t
over
(0,\infty)
is lower bounded by
-1/e
, so dividing the integration region over the two regions where
f
is positive or negative we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}q_{\sigma}\left|\log\frac{q_{\sigma}}{T_{a}q_{\sigma}}\right|

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{T_{a}q_{\sigma}}\left[\left|f\circ\frac{q_{\sigma}}{T_{a}q_{\sigma}}\right|\right]\leq D\left(q_{\sigma}\|T_{a}q_{\sigma}\right)+\frac{2}{e}<\infty.

(178)

Thus, by the monotone convergence theorem, we must have

\lim_{z\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-z,z]}q_{\sigma}\left|\log\frac{q_{\sigma}}{T_{a}q_{\sigma}}\right|=0.

(179)

As this contradicts (
176
), the lemma is proved.
∎

The following lemma gives an
\mathrm{exp}(-O(w^{\gamma}))
lower bound on the minimum value of
q_{\sigma}
over
[-w,w]
and on the probability that
Z_{\sigma}\sim q_{\sigma}
exceeds
w
, both as
w\to\infty
.

Lemma 4
.

For a PDF
q
and a constant
\sigma>0
, we have that

\int_{[w,\infty)}q_{\sigma}=\mathrm{exp}\left(-O(w^{\gamma})\right)

(180)

and

\min_{|x|\leq w}q_{\sigma}(x)=\mathrm{exp}\left(-O(w^{\gamma})\right),

(181)

both as
w\to\infty
.

Proof.

First, we show that there is a bounded Borel set
B
with
\lambda(B)>0
such that

\mu:=\inf_{x\in B}q(x)>0.

(182)

Note that we may remove the boundedness condition on
B
. Indeed, if the Borel set
B
satisfies
\lambda(B)>0
and
\inf_{x\in B}q(x)>0
, then the bounded Borel sets
A_{m}:=B\cap[-m,m]
also satisfy
\lambda(A_{m})>0
and
\inf_{x\in A_{m}}q(x)>0
for all large
m
by continuity of
\lambda
and the definition of the infimum. Now, to see that such a
B
exists, consider the Borel sets
B_{n}:=q^{-1}([1/n,\infty))
for integers
n\geq 1
. For each
n\geq 1
, we have that
\inf_{x\in B_{n}}q(x)\geq 1/n
. Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that
\lambda(B_{n})=0
for each
n
. Then we would have

\displaystyle\lambda(q^{-1}((0,\infty)))

\displaystyle=\lambda\left(q^{-1}\left(\bigcup_{n\geq 1}[1/n,\infty)\right)\right)=\lambda\left(\bigcup_{n\geq 1}B_{n}\right)=0.

(183)

Hence,
q=0
a.e. However, this would contradict that
q
is a PDF. Thus, we conclude that
\lambda(B_{n})>0
for some
n
. In short, there must exist a bounded Borel set
B
with
\lambda(B)>0
and
\inf_{x\in B}q(x)>0
. Fix such a
B
, and let
x_{0}>0
be such that
B\subset[-x_{0},x_{0}]
.

Recall that we define
q_{\sigma}=q\ast\psi^{\sigma}
(see equations (
137
)–(
142
)). For each
w\in\mathbb{R}
, Tonelli’s theorem implies that

\int_{[w,\infty)}q_{\sigma}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}q(x)\int_{w}^{\infty}\psi\left(\frac{y-x}{\sigma}\right)\frac{1}{\sigma}\,dy\,dx.

(184)

Performing a change of variable, we have for every
x,w\in\mathbb{R}

\int_{w}^{\infty}\psi\left(\frac{y-x}{\sigma}\right)\frac{1}{\sigma}\,dy=\int_{[(w-x)/\sigma,\infty)}\psi.

(185)

Further, for any
z\geq 0
, by definition of
\psi
, we have the bound

\int_{[z,\infty)}\psi\geq\int_{[z,z+1]}\psi\geq\mathrm{exp}\left(-\left(z+1\right)^{\gamma}\right)\cdot\chi^{-1},

(186)

where
\chi=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{exp}(-|u|^{\gamma})\,du
is the normalization constant for
\psi
. Therefore, whenever
w\geq x
we have

\int_{[(w-x)/\sigma,\infty)}\psi\geq\mathrm{exp}\left(-\left(\frac{w-x+\sigma}{\sigma}\right)^{\gamma}\right)\cdot\chi^{-1}.

(187)

Now, combining (
184
) and (
185
), nonnegativity of the PDFs
q
and
\psi
implies the bound

\int_{[w,\infty)}q_{\sigma}\geq\int_{B}q(x)\int_{[(w-x)/\sigma,\infty)}\psi(u)\,du\,dx.

(188)

Since
B\subset[-x_{0},x_{0}]
, we conclude from (
187
) that for every
w\geq x_{0}

\displaystyle\int_{[w,\infty)}q_{\sigma}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{B}\mu\cdot\mathrm{exp}\left(-\left(\frac{w-x+\sigma}{\sigma}\right)^{\gamma}\right)\cdot\chi^{-1}\,dx

(189)

\displaystyle\geq\lambda(B)\mu\chi^{-1}\cdot\mathrm{exp}\left(-\left(\frac{w+x_{0}+\sigma}{\sigma}\right)^{\gamma}\right).

(190)

The estimate in (
180
) follows by taking
w\to\infty
.

Finally, we show that (
181
) holds. Let
w_{0}>0
be such that
\int_{[-w,w]}q\geq 1/2
for every
w\geq w_{0}
. Then, for any
w\geq w_{0}
and
x\in[-w,w]
,

\displaystyle q_{\sigma}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}q(u)\psi^{\sigma}(x-u)\,du

(191)

\displaystyle=(\sigma\chi)^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}q(u)~\mathrm{exp}\left(-|x-u|^{\gamma}/\sigma^{\gamma}\right)\,du

(192)

\displaystyle\geq(\sigma\chi)^{-1}\int_{-w}^{w}q(u)~\mathrm{exp}\left(-|x-u|^{\gamma}/\sigma^{\gamma}\right)\,du

(193)

\displaystyle\geq(\sigma\chi)^{-1}~\mathrm{exp}\left(-(2/\sigma^{\gamma})w^{\gamma}\right)\int_{[-w,w]}q

(194)

\displaystyle\geq(2\sigma\chi)^{-1}~\mathrm{exp}\left(-(2/\sigma^{\gamma})w^{\gamma}\right).

(195)

The estimate (
181
) follows by taking
w\to\infty
.

Conversely, the following lemma gives an upper bound on the tail of any distribution that satisfies the cost constraint.

Lemma 5
.

For any
P\in\mathscr{B}
, if
\mathbb{E}_{P}[c]<\infty
then

P(\mathbb{R}\setminus[-x,x])=o\left(c(x)^{-1}\right)

(196)

as
x\to\infty.

Proof.

We start by showing that

\lim_{t\to\infty}P\left(\left\{c>t\right\}\right)\cdot t=0.

(197)

Denote
f(t):=P\left(\left\{c>t\right\}\right)
for
t>0
. Note that
f
is a decreasing nonnegative function over
(0,\infty)
. Further,
f
is integrable by nonnegativity of
c
and by the assumption in the lemma since

\int_{0}^{\infty}P\left(\left\{c>t\right\}\right)\,dt=\mathbb{E}_{P}[c]<\infty.

(198)

We show that these three properties of
f
yield that
f(t)=o(t^{-1})
.

Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that there is an
\varepsilon>0
and an increasing sequence
t_{n}\nearrow\infty
of strictly positive numbers such that

f(t_{n})\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{t_{n}}

(199)

for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
f
is decreasing, we infer from (
199
) that

f(t)\geq\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\varepsilon}{t_{n+1}}\cdot 1_{(t_{n},t_{n+1}]}(t)

(200)

for every
t>t_{1}
. Integrating both sides in (
200
), integrability of
f
implies that

\infty>\int_{(t_{1},\infty)}f\geq\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\varepsilon\left(1-\frac{t_{n}}{t_{n+1}}\right).

(201)

By convergence of the series in (
201
), we conclude that
t_{n}/t_{n+1}\sim 1
as
n\to\infty
. In particular, the constant

\tau:=\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{t_{n}}{t_{n+1}}

(202)

satisfies
\tau\in(0,1)
. Set
\delta=\varepsilon\tau
, and note that
\delta>0
. Then, from (
200
) we obtain

\displaystyle f(t)\cdot 1_{(t_{1},\infty)}(t)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\delta}{\tau\cdot t_{n+1}}\cdot 1_{(t_{n},t_{n+1}]}(t)

(203)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\delta}{t_{n}}\cdot 1_{(t_{n},t_{n+1}]}(t)

(204)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\delta}{t}\cdot 1_{(t_{n},t_{n+1}]}(t)=\frac{\delta}{t}\cdot 1_{(t_{1},\infty)}(t).

(205)

However, (
205
) contradicts the integrability of
f
. Thus, we conclude that it must be the case that

f(t)=o\left(t^{-1}\right)

(206)

as
t\to\infty
.

To finish the proof of the lemma, recall by the tail-regularity assumption on
c
that we have

\lim_{|x|\to\infty}\frac{c(x)}{|x|^{\alpha}}=\beta

(207)

for some
\alpha,\beta>0
. Thus there are
0<\beta_{1}<\beta<\beta_{2}
such that for sufficiently large
|x|
,
\beta_{1}|x|^{\alpha}\leq c(x)\leq\beta_{2}|x|^{\alpha}
. Then, for all large enough
t
, we have that

\displaystyle P\left(\left\{c>t\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\geq P\left(\mathbb{R}\setminus\left[-(t/\beta_{1})^{1/\alpha},(t/\beta_{1})^{1/\alpha}\right]\right).

(208)

Writing
x=(t/\beta_{1})^{1/\alpha}
, we conclude that for all large
x

\displaystyle c(x)P\left(\mathbb{R}\setminus[-x,x]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{2}x^{\alpha}P\left(\mathbb{R}\setminus[-x,x]\right)

(209)

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta_{2}}{\beta_{1}}tP\left(\mathbb{R}\setminus\left[-(t/\beta_{1})^{1/\alpha},(t/\beta_{1})^{1/\alpha}\right]\right)

(210)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\beta_{2}}{\beta_{1}}tP\left(\left\{c>t\right\}\right).

(211)

Taking
t\to\infty
, we obtain from (
206
) that

c(x)P\left(\mathbb{R}\setminus[-x,x]\right)\to 0,

(212)

as desired.
∎

The final auxiliary lemma gives an upper bound on the tail of the cost constraint incurred by a cactus distribution.

Lemma 6
.

Fix
r\in(0,1)
and integers
N>n\geq 1
, and set
w=(N-1/2)/n
. Assume that
c(x)\leq\beta_{1}x^{\alpha}
for
x\geq w
. Then, we have the bound

\sum_{i\geq N}c_{n,i}r^{i-N}\leq\beta_{1}\ell_{\alpha}\left(\frac{w^{\alpha}}{1-r}+\frac{2\left(\frac{\alpha}{e}\right)^{\alpha}\log\frac{1}{r}+\Gamma(\alpha+1)}{rn^{\alpha}\left(\log\frac{1}{r}\right)^{\alpha+1}}\right),

(213)

where
\ell_{\alpha}:=\max(1,2^{\alpha-1})
.

Proof.

By monotonicity of
c
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i\geq N}c_{n,i}r^{i-N}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\geq N}\int_{(i-1/2)/n}^{(i+1/2)/n}ncr^{i-N}

(214)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\geq N}\beta_{1}\left(\frac{i+1/2}{n}\right)^{\alpha}r^{i-N}

(215)

\displaystyle=\beta_{1}\sum_{i\geq 0}\left(w+\frac{i+1}{n}\right)^{\alpha}r^{i}

(216)

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{1}\ell_{\alpha}\left(\frac{w^{\alpha}}{1-r}+\frac{\text{Li}_{-\alpha}(r)}{rn^{\alpha}}\right),

(217)

where

\text{Li}_{-\alpha}(r):=\sum_{k\geq 1}k^{\alpha}r^{k}

(218)

is the polylogarithm function. To finish the proof of the lemma, we show next that

\text{Li}_{-\alpha}(r)\leq 2\left(\frac{\alpha}{e\log\frac{1}{r}}\right)^{\alpha}+\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}{\left(\log\frac{1}{r}\right)^{\alpha+1}}.

(219)

Now, consider the function
g:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
defined by

g(x):=x^{\alpha}r^{x}.

(220)

We have that

g^{\prime}(x)=\left(\alpha+x\log r\right)x^{\alpha-1}r^{x}.

(221)

Thus,
g
increases until it reaches a maximum at
x_{0}=\alpha/\log\frac{1}{r}
then it decreases. Thus,

\text{Li}_{-\alpha}(r)\leq g(\lfloor x_{0}\rfloor)+g(\lceil x_{0}\rceil)+\int_{(0,\infty)}g.

(222)

We have

g(\lfloor x_{0}\rfloor)+g(\lceil x_{0}\rceil)\leq 2g(x_{0})=2\left(\frac{\alpha}{e\log\frac{1}{r}}\right)^{\alpha},

(223)

and

\int_{(0,\infty)}g=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}{\left(\log\frac{1}{r}\right)^{\alpha+1}}.

(224)

The proof is thus complete.
∎

We show next that

By construction of

(283)

Next, we show that

satisfies the KL bound (

). We begin by splitting the integration at the points
\pm z
. By finiteness of the considered KL-divergences, we have for each

\displaystyle D(f_{n,r,\bm{p}}\|T_{-a}f_{n,r,\bm{p}})-D(q\|T_{-a}q)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{[-z,z]}\left(f_{n,r,\bm{p}}\log\frac{f_{n,r,\bm{p}}}{T_{-a}f_{n,r,\bm{p}}}-q\log\frac{q}{T_{-a}q}\right)

We already have a uniform bound for the last integral in (

We proceed to bounding the first integral in (

We do this via deriving an upper bound on the integrand that is uniform in both

(287)

Define the function

(288)

That the range of

follows since
f_{n,r,\bm{p}}
approximates

to within
\varepsilon

(289)

We note that, over
[-z,z]
, the inequality

\displaystyle f_{n,r,\bm{p}}\log\frac{f_{n,r,\bm{p}}}{T_{-a}f_{n,r,\bm{p}}}-q\log\frac{q}{T_{-a}q}\leq-q\log\left(1-T_{-a}\frac{g}{q}\right)-g\log\left(1-\frac{g}{q}\right)+g\log\frac{T_{-a}q}{q}

[-w,w]
. Now, we bound each of the terms in (

(292)

Now, we show that

over

over

(294)

It is also clear that over

(295)

Plugging in inequalities (

)–(

) into (

(296)

over
[-z,z]
. Integrating, we deduce

\displaystyle\int_{[-z,z]}f_{n,r,\bm{p}}\log\frac{f_{n,r,\bm{p}}}{T_{-a}f_{n,r,\bm{p}}}-q\log\frac{q}{T_{-a}q}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{6zM\varepsilon}{\mu_{w}}<\frac{\delta}{3},

(297)

where the last inequality follows by (

It remains to upper bound the middle integral in (

\sup_{|a|\leq 1}\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-z,z]}f_{n,r,\bm{p}}\log\frac{f_{n,r,\bm{p}}}{T_{-a}f_{n,r,\bm{p}}}\leq\frac{\delta}{3}.

(298)

We will further split the integration at the points

. By evenness of

, we have that this integral depends only on

Thus, it suffices for (

Consider first the integral

(301)

a\in(0,1]

2

p_{i}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}p_{|i|},&\text{if }-N\leq i\leq-1,\\
p_{N}r^{|i|-N},&\text{if }|i|>N.\end{array}\right.

(302)

For each
i\in\mathbb{Z}

with

f_{n,r,\bm{p}}\log\frac{f_{n,r,\bm{p}}}{T_{-a}f_{n,r,\bm{p}}}=np_{i}\log\frac{p_{i}}{p_{i+j}}

\displaystyle=np_{N}r^{|i|-N}(|i|-|i+j|)\log r\leq n^{2}p_{N}r^{|i|-N}\log\frac{1}{r}.

(305)

Summing over

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-(w+1),w+1]}f_{n,r,\bm{p}}\log\frac{f_{n,r,\bm{p}}}{T_{-a}f_{n,r,\bm{p}}}

(309)

Thus, we have shown that

\sup_{a\in(0,1]}\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-(w+1),w+1]}f_{n,r,\bm{p}}\log\frac{f_{n,r,\bm{p}}}{T_{-a}f_{n,r,\bm{p}}}\leq\frac{\delta}{6}.

(310)

The final integral bound we need is the following:

\sup_{0<a\leq 1}\int_{w-1<|x|\leq w+1}f_{n,r,\bm{p}}(x)\log\frac{f_{n,r,\bm{p}}(x)}{T_{-a}f_{n,r,\bm{p}}(x)}\,dx\leq\frac{\delta}{6}.

(311)

By evenness of
f_{n,r,\bm{p}}
, we have that

\displaystyle\int_{w-1<|x|\leq w+1}f_{n,r,\bm{p}}(x)\log\frac{f_{n,r,\bm{p}}(x)}{T_{-a}f_{n,r,\bm{p}}(x)}\,dx=\int_{(w-1,w+1]}f_{n,r,\bm{p}}\log\frac{f_{n,r,\bm{p}}^{2}}{(T_{-a}f_{n,r,\bm{p}})\cdot(T_{a}f_{n,r,\bm{p}})}.

(312)

Consider the function inside the logarithm in the integrand:

\rho(x;a):=\frac{f_{n,r,\bm{p}}(x)^{2}}{f_{n,r,\bm{p}}(x+a)f_{n,r,\bm{p}}(x-a)}.

We will prove the uniform upper bound

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in(w-1,w+1]\\

For each

Thus, it suffices to show that

\eta\leq 24\beta_{1}\ell_{\alpha}\cdot\chi^{-1}\cdot(\theta_{\alpha}^{\prime})^{-2}

(alternatively, we may increase the size of
w

P_{n,r,\bm{p}}((w-1,w+1])\cdot 2w^{\gamma^{\prime}}.

\displaystyle P_{n,r,\bm{p}}((w-1,w+1])]

(337)

Recall that we showed in (

\mathbb{E}_{f_{n,r,\bm{p}}}[c]\leq C+\eta.

To sum up, we make the dependence on

\textup{KL}_{n,N,r}^{\star}(C)
