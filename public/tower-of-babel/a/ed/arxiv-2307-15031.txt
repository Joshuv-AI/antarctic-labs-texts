Galaxy bias renormalization group
Fabian Schmidt

Abstract
The effective field theory of large-scale structure allows for a consistent perturbative bias expansion of the rest-frame galaxy density field. In this work, we present a systematic approach to renormalize galaxy bias and stochastic parameters using a finite cutoff scale Λ \Lambda . We derive the differential equations of the Wilson-Polchinski renormalization group that describe the evolution of the finite-scale bias parameters with Λ \Lambda , analogous to the β \beta -function running in QFT. We further provide the connection between the finite-cutoff scheme and the renormalization procedure for n n -point functions that has been used as standard in the literature so far; some inconsistencies in the treatment of renormalized bias in current EFT analyses are pointed out as well. The fixed-cutoff scheme allows us to predict, in a principled way, the finite part of loop contributions which is due to perturbative modes and which, in the standard renormalization approach, is absorbed into counterterms. We expect that this will allow for the robust extraction of (a yet-to-be-determined amount of) additional cosmological information from galaxy clustering, both when using field-level techniques and n n -point functions.

Introduction

The large-scale structure of the universe (LSS), as probed via the three-dimensional distribution of galaxies, is known to be a rich source of information on gravity, dark matter, dark energy, and the initial conditions of structure formation. To unlock this information, however, one has to marginalize over the significant uncertainties in our understanding of galaxy formation. What is required in particular is a reliable prediction for the conditional probability of forming a galaxy that passes observational luminosity and color selections, at a given location. The effective field theory (EFT) approach
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
, built upon cosmological perturbation theory, allows for a consistent expansion of the galaxy density field into operators
O
, ranked in terms of perturbative order, and corresponding bias coefficients
b_{O}
as well as stochastic contributions described by a field
\epsilon
and coefficients
c_{\epsilon,O}
(see
[
5
]
for a review):

\delta_{g}(\bm{x},\tau)\equiv\frac{n_{g}(\bm{x},\tau)}{\bar{n}_{g}(\tau)}-1=\sum_{O}\left[b_{O}(\tau)+c_{\epsilon,O}(\tau)\epsilon(\bm{x},\tau)\right]O(\bm{x},\tau)+\epsilon(\bm{x},\tau).

(1.1)

The bias parameters and stochastic coefficients are only functions of time
\tau
, while the stochastic field does not correlate with the operators
O
and is characterized completely by its moments
[
6
]
. The bias operators are constructed out of the matter density field
\delta(\bm{x},\tau)=\rho_{\rm m}(\bm{x},\tau)/\bar{\rho}_{\rm m}(\tau)-1
. It is a nontrivial result that a local-in-time relation such as Eq. (
1.1
) can also capture the long time scales over which galaxies form
[
6
,
7
]
(see also
[
8
]
), i.e. despite the fact the theory is fundamentally nonlocal in time. This is a consequence of the fact that, at any given order in perturbation theory, the solutions to the equation of motion for matter and gravitational potential are a sum over a finite number of terms which each are factorizable functions of time and space
[
9
,
10
]
.

When computing observable statistics such as
n
-point correlation functions based on Eq. (
1.1
) (technically these involve expectation values of products of composite operators), loop integrals arise which have to be regularized. The standard approach has so far been to work at the level of
n
-point correlation functions, and to remove the UV-sensitive loop integrals via counterterms
[
11
]
; for recent work on different regularization schemes for the galaxy bias, see
[
12
]
. In this paper, our aim is instead to follow the renormalization-group (RG) approach of Wilson
[
13
]
and Polchinski
[
14
]
, which proceeds by integrating out small-scale modes in the partition function.
1
1

1

Two main different RG approaches are used in the context of field theory: the Wilsonian RG and the continuum RG
[
15
]
. The Wilsonian RG makes use of the fact that the physics at energy scales
E\ll\Lambda
has to be independent of the finite cutoff
\Lambda
(although the couplings may change with
\Lambda
). Later, Polchinski derived the Wilsonian equations in terms of differential increments in the path integral formalism
[
14
]
, an approach similar to the one taken in this work. The continuum case instead makes direct use of the invariance of an observable with respect to any arbitrary scale, e.g. via dimensional regularization
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
. It is more frequently used in the field theory context since it usually allows for more straightforward calculations than the Wilson/Polchinski approach. We will see here however that the latter can be readily applied in the EFT of LSS context.
Ref.
[
3
]
was the first to point out how this approach can be adapted to the EFT of LSS. Ref.
[
20
]
used it to derive the EFT prediction for the conditional probability density of the galaxy field given the matter density field; that is, the crucial ingredient needed for making predictions for galaxy clustering, as noted above.

The partition function is usually formulated in terms of a Lagrangian for the fields under consideration. However, the nonlinear dynamics of collisionless matter under gravity cannot be derived from an interaction Lagrangian written in terms of three-dimensional fields. One can reverse-engineer a Lagrangian that gives rise to the fluid equations
[
21
,
22
]
. This Lagrangian will however not give rise to the corrections to a perfect fluid, such as effective pressure and stochastic fluctuations, which are expected in the EFT. It is even less applicable to generate the EFT for biased tracers who do not obey mass and momentum conservation. Instead, our partition function will be based directly on the bias expansion Eq. (
1.1
), and the free propagator for the linear density field, that is the linear power spectrum
P_{\rm L}(k)
.

The Wilson-Polchinski approach to renormalization proceeds by integrating out modes
in the free field
above a momentum cutoff
\Lambda
. In the LSS case, this corresponds to integrating modes above the cutoff
in the linear density field
.
This is in fact essential in order to keep predictions for the galaxy density field under perturbative control, as noted in
[
23
]
.
In this paper, we continue to work along these lines by deriving how the bias coefficients
b_{O}^{\Lambda}
(and stochastic amplitudes), which now depend explicitly on the finite scale
\Lambda
, evolve under a change in the cutoff. Following Wilson’s foundational work on this topic, this is known as “renormalization group flow” in the field theory context.

Our results can be useful in several ways:

•

Observational measurements of bias coefficients
b_{O}^{\Lambda}
at different
\Lambda
can be compared with the EFT prediction for the running, providing an additional cross-check of EFT-based cosmology inferences based on the same data.

•

Simulation measurements of bias coefficients are typically performed using
n
-point correlation functions on large scales (e.g.,
[
24
]
) or the separate-universe technique
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
, both of which return bias coefficients in the low-scale limit
\Lambda\to 0
. If one wants to use these measurements to put prior information on finite-scale bias parameters, one needs to run the former to the desired finite
\Lambda
(see Sec.
2
and Sec.
3
).

•

We point out that the finite-scale renormalization scheme allows us to predict finite contributions to loop integrals that are completely absorbed by counterterms in the approach used so far (Sec.
4
). This indicates that there might be more cosmological information to be gained when adopting the finite-scale renormalization instead.

The outline of the paper is as follows. We first introduce our notation and conventions. In Sec.
2
we derive the RG equations for the galaxy bias and stochastic parameters, discussing particular solutions for the bias evolution in
\Lambda
. We connect the finite-
\Lambda
renormalization scheme to the
n
-point renormalized bias in Sec.
3
and discuss in Sec.
4
how the two schemes treat loop contributions. We conclude in Sec.
5
. The appendices contain details on the more technical calculations. We use a Planck 2018 Euclidean
\Lambda
CDM cosmology
[
28
]
for all numerical results.

Dictionary and Notation

We provide here a brief explanation of the terms and notations used in this paper.

Fourier conventions.

We keep the letters
\bm{k},\bm{p}
and
\bm{q}
for momenta variables. We use bold letters for three-vectors and subscripts to refer to sum of vectors, e.g.
\bm{p}_{1\dots n}=\bm{p}_{1}+\dots+\bm{p}_{n}
. We use the following notation for the Fourier-space integrals

\int_{\bm{p}_{1},\dots,\bm{p}_{n}}=\int\frac{d^{3}p_{1}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\dots\int\frac{d^{3}p_{n}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,.

(1.2)

The smoothing of a field

on a length scale
1/\Lambda
with a filter
W
is done in real space via

f_{\Lambda}(\bm{x})=\int d^{3}x^{\prime}\,W_{\Lambda}(\bm{x}^{\prime}-\bm{x})f(\bm{x}^{\prime})\,,

(1.3)

such that in Fourier space

f_{\Lambda}(\bm{k})=\,W_{\Lambda}(\bm{k})f(\bm{k})\,.

(1.4)

Throughout this work we assume the filter
W_{\Lambda}
to be a sharp filter in Fourier space.

Scales.

•

\Lambda
: denotes the smoothing scale when not fixed to any value;

•

\Lambda_{\ast}
: is the
fixed
reference value of the smoothing scale, i.e. the fixed cutoff used in a given analysis;

•

k_{\rm NL}
: is the range of validity of the theory, where perturbation theory breaks down;

•

k_{\rm max}
: is the highest wavenumber considered in a given analysis.

Within the perturbative RG flow context, the hierarchy
k_{\rm max}<\Lambda,\Lambda_{\ast}<k_{\rm NL}
has to be fulfilled.

Operators.

•

O[\delta]
: General operator, which is constructed as a convolution of density fields

(1.5)

We refer to the number of density fields appearing in the definition as “order” of the operator. Hence in case of Eq. (
1.5
), the order is
n
, and we also denote this as
O^{[n]}
.

•

We work with the following basis of operators in Eq. (
1.1

First order:

\displaystyle\quad\delta\,;

Second order:

\displaystyle\quad\delta^{2},\ \mathcal{G}_{2}\,;

Third order:

\displaystyle\quad\delta^{3},\ \delta\,\mathcal{G}_{2},\ \Gamma_{3},\ \mathcal{G}_{3}\,;

(1.6)

\displaystyle(\nabla_{i}\nabla_{j}\Phi_{g})^{2}-(\nabla^{2}\Phi_{g})^{2}\,,

(1.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{3}(\Phi_{g})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\left[2\nabla_{i}\nabla_{j}\Phi_{g}\nabla^{j}\nabla_{k}\Phi_{g}\nabla^{k}\nabla^{i}\Phi_{g}+(\nabla^{2}\Phi_{g})^{3}-3(\nabla_{i}\nabla_{j}\Phi_{g})^{2}\nabla^{2}\Phi_{g}\right]\,.

(1.8)

•

\mathcal{P}[\varphi]

RG flow via the partition function

In this section we compute the RG running of the bias parameters using the partition function formalism.
The partition function for matter can be written as
[
3
,
20
]

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}^{\rm m}[J_{\Lambda}]

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{P}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}]\exp\left(\int_{\bm{k}}J_{\Lambda}(\bm{k})\delta_{\rm fwd}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}](-\bm{k})\right)\,,\,

(2.1)

where
\mathcal{P}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}]
is defined in Eq. (
1.15
) and
\delta_{\rm fwd}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}]
denotes the perturbative (forward-evolved) solution to the equations of motion for the matter density field.
As discussed in Sec.
1
, we have to insert the solution of the equations of motion in the coupling to the source current
J_{\Lambda}
, which, as indicated by the subscript, only has support over wavenumbers below
\Lambda
. Moreover, we only keep the fastest-growing mode, allowing us to reduce the initial conditions to a single field
\delta^{(1)}
. Notice that we already normalized the partition function in
\mathcal{P}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}]
[see Eq. (
1.15
)], i.e.
\mathcal{Z}^{\rm m}[0]=1
.

The partition function generates all
n
-point correlation functions of the matter density field via functional derivatives with respect to
J_{\Lambda}

[
3
]
.
The finite cutoff ensures that all observables derived from
\mathcal{Z}
are finite, but of course accurate only below
\Lambda
, which is why we consider a source current cut at
\Lambda
. However, since the cutoff is imposed artificially as a calculational tool, observables should not depend on
\Lambda
. In order to satisfy this
requirement in general, one has to introduce counterterms whose
\Lambda
-dependent coefficients absorb the cutoff dependence. Physically, the counterterms capture the effect of small-scale modes that have been integrated out to obtain Eq. (
2.1
). In the case of the matter density, the leading such counterterm is
c_{\nabla^{2}\delta}(\Lambda)\nabla^{2}\delta

[
1
]
.

We now turn to the corresponding partition function for biased tracers. It can be similarly guessed from the form of the bias expansion Eq. (
1.1
) to be
[
20
]

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}[J_{\Lambda}]=\int\mathcal{D}\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{P}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}]\exp

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\bm{k}}J_{\Lambda}(\bm{k})\left[\sum_{O}b_{O}^{\Lambda}O[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}](-\bm{k})\right]\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.+\frac{1}{2}P_{\epsilon}^{\Lambda}\int_{\bm{k}}J_{\Lambda}(\bm{k})J_{\Lambda}(-\bm{k})+\mathcal{O}[J_{\Lambda}^{2}\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda},\ J_{\Lambda}^{3}]\right)\,.

(2.2)

There are thus two differences to Eq. (
2.1
). First, we have included the counterterms, including their
\Lambda
-dependent bias coefficients
b_{O}^{\Lambda}
, since they already appear at linear order and lowest order in derivatives for biased tracers.
Second, we have a term
\propto J_{\Lambda}^{2}
, which captures stochasticity (noise) in the tracer field
[
20
]
. Specifically, we include the leading Gaussian part
P_{\epsilon}^{\Lambda}\equiv P_{\epsilon}^{\{0\},\Lambda}
[cf. Eq. (
1.14
)]; this is proportional to the variance of the field
\epsilon(\bm{x},\tau)
in Eq. (
1.1
)]. On the other hand, we have not written the stochastic terms that incorporate the coupling with the operators
O[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}]
, corresponding to the
c_{\epsilon,O}
in Eq. (
1.1
). We also have not explicitly written
non-Gaussian
stochastic terms which are of order
J_{\Lambda}^{3}
and higher
[
30
]
, relevant, for instance, for the bispectrum. We will neglect these terms throughout the paper as they are not relevant for the running of the bias parameters.
Their investigation is left for future work.
Note that a noise contribution exists also for the matter density, which we have not written in Eq. (
2.1
), as it is suppressed by
k^{4}
, and hence only relevant at fairly high order in perturbation theory.

Similarly to Eq. (
2.1
), Eq. (
2.2
) generates all
n
-point correlation functions of the galaxy density field
\delta_{g}
.
In addition, it can be used to derive the probability density functional (or “likelihood”) of the tracer field
[
20
]
.

2.1
General RG equations

We start by reviewing the results from
[
3
]
, who derived the running of the matter counterterms using the partition function. They expand the action in Eq. (
2.1
) as a general series in
\delta^{(1)}
and
J
, introducing index notation for momentum vectors and Einstein summation convention:

S_{\rm int}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda},J^{\Lambda}]=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!m!}K^{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}_{i_{1}\ldots i_{m}}(\Lambda)(\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda})^{i_{1}}\cdots(\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda})^{i_{m}}\;J^{\Lambda}_{j_{1}}\ldots J^{\Lambda}_{j_{n}}.

(2.3)

We have already assumed only the fastest growing mode, allowing us to reduce the degrees of freedom from
\phi=(\delta,\theta)
to
\delta
.
Here, the terms with
n=1
correspond to the “deterministic” (mean-field) prediction for
\delta_{g}
, i.e. the first line of Eq. (
2.2
), while contributions with
n>1
, i.e. those nonlinear in the current
J_{\Lambda}
, encode stochasticity as noted above. Each of the kernels
K
is a sum of several terms involving contributions from several operators at different orders in perturbations.
In fact, this expansion of the effective action and the RG equations derived from it are directly applicable to the case of biased tracers [Eq. (
2.2
)] as well.
Thus, the kernels include the bias and stochastic parameters from the large-scale bias expansion, but defined at the scale
\Lambda
.

Ref.
[
3
]
then derive RG equations for the coefficients

K^{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}_{i_{1}\ldots i_{m}}(\Lambda)
[Eq. (4.22) there]. For a kernel of order
n
(upper indices),
the source terms in the RG equation
only involve orders
\leq n
(this justifies why we can drop non-Gaussian stochastic terms in this paper). Since kernels with
n=0
vanish (for Gaussian initial conditions, at least), the RG equations for the
n=1
(bias) and
n=2
(Gaussian stochastic) kernels become

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\Lambda}K^{j}_{i_{1}\ldots i_{m}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{d(P_{\rm L}^{\Lambda})^{kl}}{d\Lambda}K^{j}_{kli_{1}\ldots i_{m}}

(2.4)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\Lambda}K^{j_{1}j_{2}}_{i_{1}\ldots i_{m}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{d(P_{\rm L}^{\Lambda})^{kl}}{d\Lambda}K^{j_{1}j_{2}}_{kli_{1}\ldots i_{m}}+2\frac{d(P_{\rm L}^{\Lambda})^{kl}}{d\Lambda}\sum_{k=0}^{m}{m\choose k}K^{j_{1}}_{ki_{1}\cdots i_{k}}K^{j_{2}}_{li_{k+1}i_{m}},

(2.5)

where
d(P_{\rm L}^{\Lambda})^{kl}/d\Lambda=\delta_{\rm K}^{kl}P_{\rm shell}
is the shell propagator written in index notation.
Note that, if and only if the bias expansion is complete at each order in perturbations, the kernels
K
depend on
\Lambda
only through the coefficients
b_{O}^{\Lambda}
.
In the next section, we will derive the RG equations explicitly at the level of bias operators, show that indeed the
b_{O}^{\Lambda}
completely absorbs the running of the kernels, and connect the results with the general RG equation above.

2.2
RG equations for biased tracers

The partition function
\mathcal{Z}[J]
defined at some scale
\Lambda^{\prime}
predicts the running of the bias coefficients
b_{O}^{\Lambda^{\prime}}
to any lower scale
\Lambda<\Lambda^{\prime}
. To compute this, we
consider the running over an infinitesimal distance from
\Lambda^{\prime}
to
\Lambda=\Lambda^{\prime}-\lambda
. This corresponds to integrating out modes in a
shell
of width
\lambda
in momentum space. In RG flow computations, it is common to do this by computing the derivative of
\mathcal{Z}
with respect to
\Lambda
; here we begin by considering a finite cutoff shift
\lambda
, which is assumed to be infinitesimal afterwards.

The computation of the running proceeds by integrating out the modes of the linear density field within the shell
|\bm{k}|\in(\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime}]
. Correspondingly, we separate
\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda^{\prime}}
into two parts:

\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda^{\prime}}(\bm{k})=\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}(\bm{k})+\delta^{(1)}_{\rm shell}(\bm{k}),

(2.6)

where
\delta^{(1)}_{\rm shell}(\bm{k})
has support
|\bm{k}|\in(\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime}]
, and its power spectrum
P_{\rm shell}
is defined in Eq. (
1.19
).
The path integral in Eq. (
2.2
) factorizes, leading to

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}[J_{\Lambda}]=\int\mathcal{D}\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{P}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}]\int\mathcal{D}\delta^{(1)}_{\rm shell}\mathcal{P}[\delta^{(1)}_{\rm shell}]

(2.7)

\displaystyle\quad\times\exp\left(\int_{\bm{k}}J_{\Lambda}(\bm{k})\left[\sum_{O}b_{O}^{\Lambda^{\prime}}O[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}+\delta^{(1)}_{\rm shell}](-\bm{k})\right]+\frac{1}{2}P_{\epsilon}^{\Lambda^{\prime}}\int_{\bm{k}}J_{\Lambda}(\bm{k})J_{\Lambda}(-\bm{k})+\mathcal{O}[J_{\Lambda}^{2}\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda},\ J_{\Lambda}^{3}]\right)\,.

We emphasize again that
J_{\Lambda}
has support only at scales below
\Lambda
, while the right-hand side integrates over all modes
\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda^{\prime}}=\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda+\lambda}
. Thus,
J_{\Lambda}
has no support in the shell, which guarantees that the product
P_{\rm shell}(k)J_{\Lambda}(k)
is zero (in other words,
J_{\Lambda}
and
\delta^{(1)}_{\rm shell}
are orthogonal). If we are interested in computing observables up to a maximum scale
k_{\rm max}
, we can in fact choose
J
to have support only up to
k_{\rm max}
, and choosing
\Lambda^{\prime}>\Lambda>k_{\rm max}
is sufficient to fulfill this condition (where all of these scales are below
k_{\rm NL}
).
In perturbation theory, every operator
O
is evaluated up to some finite maximum order. Let us consider the
n
-th order contribution
O^{(n)}
, which involves
n
powers of the linear density field (including of course the contributions from the nonlinear evolution of matter itself). Thus, we can write

\displaystyle O^{(n)}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}+\delta^{(1)}_{\rm shell}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O^{(n)}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}]+O^{(n),{(1)_{\rm shell}}}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda},\delta^{(1)}_{\rm shell}]+O^{(n),{(2)_{\rm shell}}}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda},\delta^{(1)}_{\rm shell}]

(2.8)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\ldots+O^{(n),{(n-1)_{\rm shell}}}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda},\delta^{(1)}_{\rm shell}]+O^{(n)}[\delta^{(1)}_{\rm shell}],

where the
O^{(n),{(m)_{\rm shell}}}
involve
m
powers of
\delta^{(1)}_{\rm shell}
and
n-m
powers of
\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}
. Trivially in this notation
O^{(n),{(0)_{\rm shell}}}=O^{(n)}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}]
and
O^{(n),{(n)_{\rm shell}}}=O^{(n)}[\delta^{(1)}_{\rm shell}]
. We refer the reader to App.
A.1
for examples on how this expansion works for the basis of operators considered in this paper.

Note that in the limit
\lambda\to 0
,
\delta^{(1)}_{\rm shell}
is parametrically smaller than
\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}
; to see this, note that
\langle\delta^{(1)}_{\rm shell}(\bm{x})^{2}\rangle=(d\sigma_{\Lambda}^{2}/d\Lambda)\lambda
[see Eq. (
2.18
) below], which in the limit
\lambda\to 0
is much smaller than
\langle\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}(\bm{x})^{2}\rangle=\sigma_{\Lambda}^{2}
.
Using this, we can expand the exponential in Eq. (
2.7
) in powers of
\delta^{(1)}_{\rm shell}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}[J_{\Lambda}]

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{P}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}]\exp\left(\int_{\bm{k}}J_{\Lambda}(\bm{k})\left[\sum_{O}b_{O}^{\Lambda^{\prime}}O[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}](-\bm{k})\right]\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left.+\frac{1}{2}P_{\epsilon}^{\Lambda^{\prime}}\int_{\bm{k}}J_{\Lambda}(\bm{k})J_{\Lambda}(-\bm{k})+\mathcal{O}[J_{\Lambda}^{2}\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda},\ J_{\Lambda}^{3}]\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\bigg(1+\int_{\bm{k}}J_{\Lambda}(\bm{k})\left[\sum_{O}b_{O}^{\Lambda^{\prime}}\left(\mathcal{S}_{O}^{1}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}](-\bm{k})+\mathcal{S}_{O}^{2}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}](-\bm{k})+\ldots\right)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}J_{\Lambda}(\bm{k})J_{\Lambda}(\bm{k}^{\prime})\sum_{O,O^{\prime}}b_{O}^{\Lambda^{\prime}}b_{O^{\prime}}^{\Lambda^{\prime}}\left[\mathcal{S}_{OO^{\prime}}^{11}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}](\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime})+\ldots\right]+\mathcal{O}[J_{\Lambda}^{2}\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda},\ J_{\Lambda}^{3}]\bigg),

(2.9)

where we introduce the operator expectation values

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{O}^{2}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 2}\int\mathcal{D}\delta^{(1)}_{\rm shell}\mathcal{P}[\delta^{(1)}_{\rm shell}]\>O^{(n),(2)_{\rm shell}}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda},\delta^{(1)}_{\rm shell}](\bm{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 2}\left\langle O^{(n),(2)_{\rm shell}}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda},\delta^{(1)}_{\rm shell}](\bm{k})\right\rangle_{\rm shell}

(2.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{OO^{\prime}}^{11}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}](\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,n^{\prime}\geq 1}\int\mathcal{D}\delta^{(1)}_{\rm shell}\mathcal{P}[\delta^{(1)}_{\rm shell}]\>O^{(n),(1)_{\rm shell}}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda},\delta^{(1)}_{\rm shell}](\bm{k})\>O^{\prime(n^{\prime}),(1)_{\rm shell}}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda},\delta^{(1)}_{\rm shell}](\bm{k}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,n^{\prime}\geq 1}\left\langle O^{(n),(1)_{\rm shell}}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda},\delta^{(1)}_{\rm shell}](\bm{k})\>O^{\prime(n^{\prime}),(1)_{\rm shell}}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda},\delta^{(1)}_{\rm shell}](\bm{k}^{\prime})\right\rangle_{\rm shell}\,,

where the sums run over the contributions to
O
and
O^{\prime}
in perturbation theory. On the other hand,

\mathcal{S}_{O}^{1}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}]=\sum_{n\geq 1}\int\mathcal{D}\delta^{(1)}_{\rm shell}\mathcal{P}[\delta^{(1)}_{\rm shell}]\>O^{(n),(1)_{\rm shell}}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda},\delta^{(1)}_{\rm shell}](\bm{k})=0

(2.11)

vanishes, since the integrand is linear in
\delta^{(1)}_{\rm shell}
by definition.
We also emphasize that these expectation values are functionals of
\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}
; that is, the long-wavelength modes are

integrated out.
Terms that are proportional to more than two powers of

) by dots, are suppressed such that we drop them hereafter. Thus, only

and

We can then reconstruct the exponential from its series and compare it to the partition function for the bias and stochastic parameters at the scale

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}[J_{\Lambda}]

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{P}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}]\exp\left[\int_{\bm{k}}J_{\Lambda}(\bm{k})\left(\sum_{O}b_{O}^{\Lambda^{\prime}}\left[O[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}](-\bm{k})+\mathcal{S}_{O}^{2}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}](-\bm{k})\right]\right)\right.

(2.12)

\displaystyle\left.\qquad+\frac{1}{2}P_{\epsilon}^{\Lambda^{\prime}}\int_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}J_{\Lambda}(\bm{k})J_{\Lambda}(\bm{k}^{\prime})\left((2\pi)^{3}\delta_{\rm D}(\bm{k}+\bm{k}^{\prime})+\sum_{O,O^{\prime}}b_{O}^{\Lambda^{\prime}}b_{O^{\prime}}^{\Lambda^{\prime}}\mathcal{S}_{OO^{\prime}}^{11}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}](\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime})\right)\right]\,.

corresponds to the contribution of the operator

(2.13)

and correspondingly
\mathcal{S}_{OO^{\prime}}^{11}(\bm{k}_{1},\ldots\bm{k}_{m})
is the contribution of operators

to

\frac{d(P_{\rm L}^{\Lambda})^{kl}}{d\Lambda}K^{j_{1}}_{ki_{1}\cdots i_{k}}\Big|_{O}K^{j_{2}}_{li_{k+1}i_{m}}\Big|_{O^{\prime}}\,.

(2.14)

Note that
S_{OO^{\prime}}^{11}
multiplies two powers of the current
J_{\Lambda}
. This means that it contributes to the stochastic part of the partition function, in particular to the coupling between
\mathcal{O}(J_{\Lambda}^{2})
with long-wavelength operators
O[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}]
. Since we disregard these higher-order stochastic contributions in this paper, we do not need to consider
S_{OO^{\prime}}^{11}
further. We note though that it is ultimately these stochastic terms which also produce the leading stochastic term
P_{\epsilon}^{\Lambda}\int_{\bm{k}}J_{\Lambda}(\bm{k})J_{\Lambda}(-\bm{k})

Let us then focus on the terms linear in
J_{\Lambda}
.
By comparing Eq. (
2.12
) with the partition function Eq. (
2.2
) at the scale
\Lambda
, we can read off

\sum_{O}b_{O}^{\Lambda}O[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}]=\sum_{O}b_{O}^{\Lambda^{\prime}}\left(O[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}]+\mathcal{S}_{O}^{2}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}]\right).

(2.15)

If (and only if) the set of bias operators is closed under renormalization, the shell expectation value
\mathcal{S}_{O}^{2}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}]
can depend on
\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}
only through the bias operators themselves. That is, we should be able to write

\mathcal{S}_{O}^{2}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}]=\lambda\sum_{O^{\prime}}\Theta_{O,O^{\prime}}(\Lambda)O^{\prime}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}],

(2.16)

where we have used the fact that
\mathcal{S}_{O}^{2}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}]
is first order in the shell width
\lambda
. We can anticipate that

\mathcal{S}_{O}^{2}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}]\propto\int_{\bm{p}}P_{\rm shell}(p)\,,

(2.17)

and we will show this explicitly in the next section. Since [cf. Eq. (
1.21
)]

\int_{\bm{p}}P_{\rm shell}(p)=\int_{\Lambda}^{\Lambda+\lambda}\frac{p^{2}dp}{2\pi^{2}}P_{\rm L}(p)=\frac{d\sigma^{2}_{\Lambda}}{d\Lambda}\Big|_{\Lambda}\lambda+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{2})\,,

(2.18)

we can isolate the leading
\Lambda
dependence in
\Theta
as

\Theta_{O,O^{\prime}}(\Lambda^{\prime})=c_{O,O^{\prime}}(\Lambda)\frac{d\sigma^{2}_{\Lambda}}{d\Lambda}\Big|_{\Lambda}\,,

(2.19)

where
c_{O,O^{\prime}}(\Lambda)
are coefficients which will be determined below.
For operators
O
that are leading-order in derivatives,
c_{O,O^{\prime}}
turn out to be
\Lambda
-independent coefficients of order unity. For higher-derivative operators
O
, they are
\Lambda
-dependent, with the powers of
\Lambda
determined by the dimensions of the operator.

Inserting Eq. (
2.16
) into Eq. (
2.15
), and reordering the sum, we obtain

\sum_{O}b_{O}^{\Lambda}O[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}]=\sum_{O}\left[b_{O}^{\Lambda^{\prime}}+\lambda\sum_{O^{\prime}}\Theta_{O^{\prime}O}(\Lambda)b_{O^{\prime}}^{\Lambda^{\prime}}\right]O[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}]\,.

(2.20)

Now we can identify coefficients and read off

b_{O}^{\Lambda}=b_{O}^{\Lambda^{\prime}}+\lambda\sum_{O^{\prime}}\Theta_{O^{\prime}O}(\Lambda)b_{O^{\prime}}^{\Lambda^{\prime}}\,,

(2.21)

which via Eq. (
2.19
) and since
\Lambda=\Lambda^{\prime}-\lambda
becomes

\frac{d}{d\Lambda}b_{O}^{\Lambda}=-\frac{d}{d\lambda}b_{O}^{\Lambda}=-\sum_{O^{\prime}}\Theta_{O^{\prime}O}(\Lambda)b_{O^{\prime}}^{\Lambda}=-\frac{d\sigma^{2}_{\Lambda}}{d\Lambda}\sum_{O^{\prime}}c_{O^{\prime}O}\,b_{O^{\prime}}^{\Lambda}\,.

(2.22)

In order to derive the RG equations for the
b_{O}^{\Lambda}
, we thus only need to determine the coefficients
c_{O^{\prime}O}
.

2.3
Running of the bias parameters

We now compute the running of the bias parameters, evaluating the ensemble average of correlators over the initial modes in the shell described by Eq. (
2.10
),
keeping the modes
|\bm{k}|<\Lambda
fixed
.
This is a central difference to the
n
-point-function-based renormalization from
[
11
]
: our approach keeps
all linear modes below the cutoff explicit
, whereas the
n
-point-function-based renormalization marginalizes over all modes, including large scales.

We evaluate the running of linear and second-order bias parameters considering up to third-order corrections to those operators.
As argued above, the only non-trivial contributions are the
\mathcal{S}^{2}_{O}
terms

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{O}^{2}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}](\bm{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle O^{{(2)_{\rm shell}}}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda},\delta^{(1)}_{\rm shell}](\bm{k}_{1})\rangle_{\rm shell}\,,\,

(2.23)

with contributions
\mathcal{S}_{O}^{\geq 3}
being suppressed by
\delta^{(1)}_{\rm shell}
. We now proceed to calculate those shell ensemble averages for operators that are up to the third order in the bias expansion, with the goal of computing the coefficients in the expansion Eq. (
2.16
). We refer to App.
A.2
for the complete calculation, and highlight in the main text the most relevant results.
We also comment later on how fourth-order operators affect the RG running of second-order bias operators.

The shell integrals for
\mathcal{S}^{2}_{\delta}
leads to terms that are proportional to
\int_{\bm{p}}F_{\ell}(\bm{p},-\bm{p},\dots)P_{\rm shell}(p)
; specifically,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{2}_{\delta}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}](\bm{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 3\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}(\bm{k})\int_{\bm{p}}F_{3}(\bm{p},-\bm{p},\bm{k})P_{\rm shell}(p)

(2.24)

\displaystyle\quad+\,6\int_{\bm{p},\bm{p}_{1},\bm{p}_{2}}\delta_{\rm D}(\bm{k}-\bm{p}_{12})F_{4}(\bm{p},-\bm{p},\bm{p}_{1},\bm{p}_{2})P_{\rm shell}(p)\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}(\bm{p}_{1})\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}(\bm{p}_{2})

\displaystyle\quad+\,\mathcal{O}\left[\left(\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}\right)^{3}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k\ll p}}{{=}}

\displaystyle-\frac{61}{315}k^{2}\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}(\bm{k})\int\frac{p^{2}dp}{2\pi^{2}}\,\frac{P_{\rm shell}(p)}{p^{2}}+\,\mathcal{O}\left[\nabla^{2}\left(\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}\right)^{2}\right]\,,

\mathcal{O}[\nabla^{2}(\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda})^{2}]

\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}

\delta
thus only contributes a correction to the
\nabla^{2}\delta

[

In the case of
\delta

\delta
can only source higher-derivative terms.

The shell contraction for the operator
\delta^{2}
leads to

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{2}_{\delta^{2}}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}](\bm{k})

\displaystyle=4\,\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}(\bm{k})\int_{\bm{p}}F_{2}(\bm{k},\bm{p})P_{\rm shell}(p)

(2.25)

\displaystyle+6\int_{\bm{p},\bm{p}_{1},\bm{p}_{2}}\delta_{\rm D}(\bm{k}-\bm{p}_{12})\left[F_{3}(\bm{p},-\bm{p},\bm{p}_{1})+F_{3}(\bm{p},\bm{p}_{1},\bm{p}_{2})\right]P_{\rm shell}(p)\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}(\bm{p}_{1})\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}(\bm{p}_{2})

\displaystyle+4\int_{\bm{p},\bm{p}_{1},\bm{p}_{2}}\delta_{\rm D}(\bm{k}-\bm{p}_{12})F_{2}(\bm{p},\bm{p}_{1})F_{2}(-\bm{p},\bm{p}_{2})P_{\rm shell}(p)\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}(\bm{p}_{1})\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}(\bm{p}_{2})

Here and throughout we assumed
\bm{k}\neq 0

(2\pi)^{3}\delta_{\rm D}(\bm{k})\int_{\bm{p}}P_{\rm shell}(p)

The first term leads to a contribution to
\delta
and the term proportional to
F_{3}(\bm{p},-\bm{p},\bm{p}_{1})
leads to corrections to
\nabla^{2}\delta
.
The terms proportional to
F_{2}(\bm{p},\bm{p}_{1})F_{2}(-\bm{p},\bm{p}_{2})

(\bm{p}_{1}\cdot\bm{p}_{2})/(p_{1}^{2}p_{2}^{2})

\partial_{i}\Phi_{g}\partial_{j}\Phi_{g}

. The sum of all contributions in Eq. (

\displaystyle\quad+\mbox{higher derivative (h.d.)}+\mathcal{O}\left[\left(\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}\right)^{3}\right]\,,

(2.26)

where we omitted higher-derivative (h.d.) and higher-order operators. Notice that Eq. (

) has the same structure and coefficients as Eq. (2.44) of
[

]
[once one converts
\mathcal{G}_{2}

)]

2

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{2}_{\delta^{4}}[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}](\bm{k})

\displaystyle\frac{db_{\delta}}{d\Lambda}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left[\frac{68}{21}b_{\delta^{2}}+3b_{\delta^{3}}^{\ast}-\frac{4}{3}b_{\mathcal{G}_{2}\delta}^{\ast}\right]\frac{d\sigma^{2}_{\Lambda}}{d\Lambda}\,,

\displaystyle-\left[\frac{8126}{2205}b_{\delta^{2}}+\frac{17}{7}b_{\delta^{3}}^{\ast}-\frac{376}{105}b_{\mathcal{G}_{2}\delta}^{\ast}+b_{n=4}^{\ast(\delta^{2})}\right]\frac{d\sigma^{2}_{\Lambda}}{d\Lambda}\,,

(2.31)

\displaystyle\frac{db_{\mathcal{G}_{2}}}{d\Lambda}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left[\frac{254}{2205}b_{\delta^{2}}+\frac{116}{105}b_{\mathcal{G}_{2}\delta}^{\ast}+b_{n=4}^{\ast(\mathcal{G}_{2})}\right]\frac{d\sigma^{2}_{\Lambda}}{d\Lambda}\,.

(2.32)

The system of equations above is the analogue of the
\beta
-function running for QFT, also known as the Callan-Symanzik equation
[
18
,
19
]
. We use the notation

to account for the fourth order contributions to
\delta^{2}
and similarly for
b_{n=4}^{\ast(\mathcal{G}_{2})}
, which, as noted above, we have not derived.
We use the superscript

to indicate bias parameters that do not evolve in
\Lambda
and are fixed at some scale
\Lambda_{\ast}
. Note that we already approximated the third and fourth-order bias parameters as constants; computing the running of those parameters would demand calculating the shell contractions for sixth-order operators. We comment on this approximation and how to go beyond it below.

We also find that the sourced higher-derivative terms have a different running compared to the other bias parameters, proportional to

\int_{\bm{p}}\frac{P_{\rm shell}(p)}{p^{2}}=\int_{\Lambda}^{\Lambda+\lambda}\frac{dp}{2\pi^{2}}P_{\rm L}(p)\,.

(2.33)

Higher-derivative terms only source higher-derivative terms, and hence do not affect the running of the other bias parameters (while being sourced by them, however).

2.4
Quantitative results and closure of RG hierarchy

We highlight that the RG equations form a linear ODE system
3
3

3

This is the case solely for Gaussian initial conditions. Non-Gaussian initial conditions will introduce nonlinear running of the bias and stochastic parameters
[
3
]
.
for the bias parameters in which, in general, the bias coefficients of order
n
contribute to bias parameters at order

Running higher-order bias and truncation of RG hierarchy.

Finally, we discuss the truncation of the hierarchy of bias RG equations. As mentioned before, the complete running of the second-order bias parameters demands calculating the fourth-order bias terms and those, in turn, also depend on the running of sixth-order bias parameters. One may then wonder if there is a systematic way to truncate this ODE system. Here we argue that, in order to calculate the complete evolution of operators of order
n

n+2

\Lambda_{*}

Proving that this holds at all orders is beyond the scope of this paper, but since the bias expansion guarantees that (at least for large scales) the system is perturbative, we take the heuristic approach of considering

\displaystyle b_{n=3+4}^{(O)}(\sigma^{2})=b^{*(O)}_{n=3+4}\,e^{-c^{(O)}(\sigma^{2}-\sigma^{2}_{\ast})}

3
Connection to bias parameters defined via
\bm{n}
-point functions

The (so far) standard approach to comparing theory with data is to use
n
-point correlation functions, where the theory prediction is usually calculated semi-analytically as the ensemble mean of a given correlation function in perturbation theory. These correlation functions have leading and next-to-leading order contributions; the latter in general need to be regularized. In this context, Ref.
[
11
]
derived renormalization conditions [Eq. (
1.12
)] which determine what counterterms need to be subtracted from a given bias operator in order to regularize loop integrals [e.g., Eq. (
1.13
)]. For this, they introduced a cutoff, but merely as a calculational tool; in practice, this cutoff is usually sent to infinity when numerically evaluating loop integrals, as they converge to finite values at low loop orders (at least in
\Lambda
CDM). Note the key difference to the cutoff used so far in this paper, which is kept finite and explicit throughout.
According to the Wilsonian prescription, and as we comment later, the observables (i.e. the
n
-point functions) in the end have to be independent of this artificial (finite or infinite) cutoff while the
free coefficients
(coupling constants and masses, or bias parameters) in general depend on
\Lambda
. The information absorbed in the bias coefficients is different in both approaches, however, since the finite cutoff allows us to keep more perturbative modes in the loop integrals which are removed in the standard renormalized bias scheme.

In this section, we derive the connection between the
finite-scale
operators, where
O=O[\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda}]
is constructed from the filtered density field at the scale
\Lambda
,

5

.

\llbracket O\rrbracket

[

(see also Sec. 2.10 of

The goal of this section is to prove this relation. The reason we look at the infrared limit is that first, this is what the definition of renormalized operators refers to, and second, the calculation becomes much more tractable and transparent in this limit. At this point, however, we should emphasize that in current EFT analyses the UV-sensitive contributions in Eq. (

\Lambda

This procedure is fine as long as all counterterm coefficients, i.e. bias and stochastic parameters, are left completely free. 4.4
) is controlled by
(b_{\delta^{2}}^{\rm n-pt})^{2}
, but its UV-sensitivity has to be absorbed by
P_{\epsilon}^{\{2\},\rm n-pt}
. This means that these two parameters are correlated. Priors on the latter parameter that do not consistently take into account this UV sensitivity could bias the inferred value of
b_{\delta^{2}}^{\rm n-pt}
. Similar
arguments apply to higher-order statistics, such as the 1-loop galaxy bispectrum, however involving now a substantially larger bias and stochastic parameter space and even more complex interdependencies.

To reiterate, while priors for a given set of bias and stochastic parameters are usually derived from physical arguments which don’t make reference to the details of the UV-sensitive implementation of loop integrals, this is not consistent with the renormalization scheme as currently employed, as these parameters in fact have to absorb UV-sensitive loop contributions, which moreover correlate different parameters.
The non-negligible effect of priors in EFTofLSS analyses has indeed been the focus of recent work
[
41
,
42
,
43
]
.

To summarize, the proper
n
-point-function-baed renormalization demands a meticulous (and error-prone) treatment of the basis of operators and loop integrals. For the one-loop bispectrum, for example, it involves subtracting many terms from fourth-order operators. Subleading analytic and higher-derivative contributions (i.e. those scaling as
k^{2}/p^{2}
)
have to be removed from loop integrals.
We emphasize again that all of these subtractions are unnecessary, and all UV sensitivity is absent in the finite-scale renormalization scheme presented in this work. The importance of consistent and physically motivated priors on bias parameters (now at a given scale
\Lambda_{*}
) applies equally in this case, however.

5
Discussion and conclusion

In this work, we have constructed the renormalization group equations for the galaxy bias and stochastic parameters, Eqs. (
2.30
)-(
2.32
). We used the partition function formalism of
[
3
]
, generalized to include galaxy bias and stochasticity following
[
20
]
to derive the running of those parameters as a function of the smoothing scale
\Lambda
, as modes above
\Lambda
in the initial conditions are integrated out. We have discussed solutions to this ODE system, indicating that in order to solve the RG evolution at a given order
n
in the bias expansion, one has to include bias operators up to order
n+2
. We provide solutions for a given set of initial conditions and comment on the stability of the joint first- and second-order bias parameter space under RG flow.
We also point out
how the running of the bias parameters generates higher-order stochastic contributions. A more detailed investigation of stochasticity is relegated to future work.

We then connect the finite-cutoff bias parameters
b_{O}^{\Lambda}
to their counterparts
b_{O^{\prime}}^{\rm n-pt}
defined using the standard
n
-point-function-based renormalization procedure
[
11
]
. Specifically, we show that the bias parameters of both schemes coincide in the limit
\Lambda\to 0
. The finite cutoff bias parameters, however, allow us to predict additional perturbative contributions to the galaxy
n
-point functions which so far have usually been absorbed, partially or completely, via counterterms (see Sec.
4
), in particular into the effective shot noise amplitudes
P_{\epsilon}^{\{j\},\rm n-pt}
. This indicates that the finite-cutoff renormalization scheme in principle allows to extract more information from the clustering of biased tracers than has been used in EFT-based analyses so far (see also
[
40
]
). This information is available both at the field level and at the level of summary statistics, as we comment on in Sec.
4
using the contribution of
b_{\delta^{2}}^{\Lambda}
to the power spectrum (at 1-loop level) and the bispectrum (at tree level) as an example.
A key difference to the standard,
n
-point-function-based renormalization is that the renormalization done here keeps
all linear modes below the cutoff explicit
; subsequently, they can be either marginalized over (for
n
-point functions) or determined by the data (in case of field-level inference). In contrast, the
n
-point-function-based renormalization is done
after marginalizing over all modes, including large scales.

The difference in the effective shot noise treatment in the standard renormalization approach and the bias expansion at a finite scale (see Sec.
4
) can have a particularly large impact for tracers with low “intrinsic” shot noise
[
44
,
45
]
. Intriguingly, the effective low-
k
shot noise covariance matrix of dark matter halos appears to have a large eigenvalue associated with
b_{\delta^{2}}

[
46
]
; it is possible that this contribution can be predicted perturbatively when adopting the finite-scale bias expansion.
The actual information gained in the latter over the standard approach will thus depend on the details of the tracer sample, volume, and data vector chosen. This is clearly an issue worthy of further investigation.

Finally, while the finite-scale bias expansion is currently mostly used in field-level forward models
[
23
,
10
,
47
]
, it is worth noting that it can equally be used in standard semi-analytical calculations of
n
-point functions. The presence of the cutoff will however complicate the use of numerical techniques such as
FFTlog

[
48
]
. This deserves further investigation, as does the choice of smoother cutoff functions than the Heaviside form adopted here (though a compact support in momentum space is essential to ensure UV insensitivity).
