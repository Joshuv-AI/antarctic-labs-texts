q q -Stirling numbers in type B B
Bruce E. Sagan

Abstract
Stirling numbers, which count partitions of a set and permutations in the symmetric group, have found extensive application in combinatorics, geometry, and algebra. We study analogues and q q -analogues of these numbers corresponding to the Coxeter group of type B B . In particular, we show how they are related to complete homogeneous and elementary symmetric polynomials; demonstrate how they q q -count signed partitions and permutations; compute their ordinary, exponential, and q q -exponential generating functions; and prove various identities about them. Ordered analogues of the q q -Stirling numbers of the second kind have recently appeared in conjectures of Zabrocki and of Swanson–Wallach concerning the Hilbert series of certain super coinvariant algebras. We provide conjectural bases for these algebras and show that they have the correct Hilbert series.

Introduction

Let
{\mathbb{Z}}
and
{\mathbb{N}}
be the integers and nonnegative integers, respectively. If
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. then we will be interested in two intervals of integers, namely

[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\langle{n}\rangle=\{-n,-n+1,\ldots,n-1,n\}.

If
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and

\displaystyle[n]_{q}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-q^{n}}{1-q},

\displaystyle[n]_{q}!

\displaystyle=[n]_{q}[n-1]_{q}\cdots[1]_{q},

\displaystyle[n]_{q}!!

\displaystyle=[n]_{q}[n-2]_{q}[n-4]_{q}\cdots,

where the double factorial ends at
[1]_{q}
or

Note that if

because both are the empty product.
Note also that if

then

[n]_{q}=1+q+q^{2}+\cdots+q^{n-1}.

We will often drop the subscript

in such notation if there can be no confusion about whether

denotes the polynomial or the set.

1.1
Classical

-Stirling numbers

The purpose of the present work is to give a comprehensive treatment of the Stirling numbers in type

and their

-analogues. We begin by defining the various classical Stirling numbers in type

-analogues recursively. Each recursion in this paper will always have

and

with the same boundary condition when

Definition 1.1
.

The
(type

) Stirling numbers of the second kind
are

for

and

defined by the initial condition

It is well known that

in the intersection lattice of the type

We will be primarily interested in
q
-analogues of Stirling numbers. There are in fact

q
-analogues of the Stirling numbers of the second kind, which differ by a
q
-shift. We will call the
(type
A
)
q
-Stirling numbers of the second kind
the polynomials
S[n,k]
in the variable

obtained by replacing (
1
) with

S[n,k]=S[n-1,k-1]+[k]S[n-1,k].

(2)

Sometimes

is also encountered, which replaces (
1

\overline{S}[n,k]=q^{k-1}\overline{S}[n-1,k-1]+[k]\overline{S}[n-1,k].

(3)

Some of the work related to
q
-Stirling numbers concerns the following ordered analogue.

Definition 1.2
.

The
(type
A
) ordered
q
-Stirling numbers of the second kind
are

S^{o}[n,k]=[k]!S[n,k],

and
\overline{S}^{o}[n,k]=[k]!\overline{S}[n,k]
.

When

counts the number of

We now recall the Stirling numbers of the first kind and their

-analogue.

Definition 1.3
.

The
(signless, type
A
) Stirling numbers of the first kind
are
c(n,k)
for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
defined by the initial condition
c(0,k)=\delta_{0,k}
and for
n\geq 1
,

c(n,k)=c(n-1,k-1)+(n-1)c(n-1,k).

(4)

Their signed counterparts are

s(n,k)=(-1)^{n-k}c(n,k).

Combinatorially,
c(n,k)
counts the number of elements in the symmetric group of permutations of
[n]
which have
k
cycles in their disjoint cycle decomposition. The
(signless, type
A
)
q
-Stirling numbers of the first kind
are the polynomials
c[n,k]
obtained by replacing (
4
) with

c[n,k]=c[n-1,k-1]+[n-1]c[n-1,k].

(5)

Their signed counterparts are
s[n,k]=(-1)^{n-k}c[n,k]
.

Stirling numbers of both kinds have been extensively studied in combinatorics and have interesting applications in algebra and geometry. See the texts of Sagan
[
Sag20
]
or Stanley
[
Sta97
]
for more information. We next review the literature on
q
-Stirling numbers.

Existing

The

Wachs and White

Ehrenborg and Readdy
[

interpreted the

Other work on

-Stirling numbers of the second kind has been done by Garsia and Remmel
[

]
and by Leroux
[

]
.

In fact, in Carlitz’s original paper
[

]
, the sum which arises in the context of Stirling numbers is actually for

Zeng and Zhang
[

]
used analytic means to prove a formula relating the

and a

-analogue of the

-exponential numbers.
A connection between the ordered polynomials and ordered set partitions was obtained by Steingrímsson in a 2001 preprint which was finally published in 2020
[
Ste20
]
. There he gave eight statistics analogous to the ones of Wachs and White. He also made a number of conjectures and posed an open problem (to find a combinatorial proof of Zeng and Zhang’s identity) which spurred a number of other authors to work on ordered set partitions
[
IKZ06
,
IKZ08b
,
KZ09
,
KZ02

. See

Haglund, Rhoades, and Shimozono

showed that there is a connection between ordered set partitions, generalized coinvariant algebras, and the Delta Conjecture (see the recent proof of the rise version
[
DM21
]
). In related work, Zabrocki

]
conjectured that

Swanson and Wallach

The Stirling numbers in type

Symmetric polynomials

Certain properties of the Stirling numbers follow from the fact that they can be expressed in terms of elementary and complete homogeneous symmetric polynomials. We will derive some of them, including their ordinary generating functions, in this section. Further information about symmetric polynomials can be found in the books of Macdonald
[

]

[

]

[

2.1
Ordinary generating functions

Let
{\bf x}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
be a set of commuting variables. The
degree
k
elementary symmetric polynomial
, denoted
e_{k}(n)=e_{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
, is the sum of all degree
k
square-free monomials in
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
.
The
degree
k
complete homogeneous symmetric polynomial

h_{k}(n)=h_{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
is the sum of all degree
k
monomials in these variables. For example

e_{2}(3)=x_{1}x_{2}+x_{1}x_{3}+x_{2}x_{3}

and

h_{2}(3)=x_{1}x_{2}+x_{1}x_{3}+x_{2}x_{3}+x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}.

By considering which monomials contain the variable
x_{n}
and which do not, it is easy to derive the following recursions for
n\geq 1
:

e_{k}(n)=e_{k}(n-1)+x_{n}e_{k-1}(n-1)

(10)

and

h_{k}(n)=h_{k}(n-1)+x_{n}h_{k-1}(n).

(11)

Let
t
be a variable. The following generating functions are well known and easy to prove directly from the definitions above:

E_{n}(t):=\sum_{k=0}^{n}e_{k}(n)t^{k}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+x_{i}t)

(12)

and

H_{n}(t):=\sum_{k\geq 0}h_{k}(n)t^{k}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{1-x_{i}t}.

(13)

The next result is a type
B
analogue of the following facts from the literature in type
A

(a)

c[n,k]=e_{n-k}([1],[2],\ldots,[n-1])
.

(b)

S[n,k]=h_{n-k}([1],[2],\ldots,[k])
.

(c)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}c[n,k]t^{k}=t(t+[1])(t+[2])\cdots(t+[n-1])
.

(d)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq k}S[n,k]t^{n}=\frac{t^{k}}{(1-[1]t)(1-[2]t)\cdots(1-[k]t)}
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

(a)

c_{B}[n,k]=e_{n-k}([1],[3],\ldots,[2n-1])
.

(b)

S_{B}[n,k]=h_{n-k}([1],[3],\ldots,[2k+1])
.

(c)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{B}[n,k]t^{k}=(t+[1])(t+[3])\cdots(t+[2n-1])
.

(d)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq k}S_{B}[n,k]t^{n}=\frac{t^{k}}{(1-[1]t)(1-[3]t)\cdots(1-[2k+1]t)}
.

Proof.

Parts (a) and (b) follow easily by comparing the recursions for the symmetric polynomials with those for the Stirlings and using induction. Parts (c) and (d) are obtained by combining the first two parts with the generating functions (
12
) and (
13

t\hskip-2.0pt\downarrow_{k}^{\bf x}=(t-x_{1})(t-x_{2})\cdots(t-x_{k}).

Theorem 2.2
.

For

2.3
Inverse matrices

For our matrix identity, we need a new result about symmetric polynomials.
Given a generating function
f(t)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}t^{n}
we define the coefficient extraction function to be

[t^{n}]f(t)=a_{n}.

A special case of the following can be found in the book of

[

.

Theorem 2.5
.

We have

\sum_{a+b=N}(-1)^{a}e_{a}(n)h_{b}(m)=\begin{cases}(-1)^{N}e_{N}(x_{m+1},\ldots,x_{n})&n\geq m,\\
h_{N}(x_{n+1},\ldots,x_{m})&n\leq m.\end{cases}

Proof.

From the generating functions in (
12

13

\displaystyle\sum_{a+b=N}(-1)^{a}e_{a}(n)h_{b}(m)

\displaystyle=[t^{N}]E_{n}(-t)H_{m}(t)

\displaystyle=[t^{N}]\frac{(1-x_{1}t)(1-x_{2}t)\cdots(1-x_{n}t)}{(1-x_{1}t)(1-x_{2}t)\cdots(1-x_{m}t)}\rule{0.0pt}{15.0pt}.

If
n\geq m
then we are finding the coefficient of
t^{N}
in
(1-x_{m+1}t)(1-x_{m+2}t)\cdots(1-x_{n}t)
which is the desired signed elementary symmetric polynomial. The case
n\leq m
is similar.
∎

and

and

Note that both

and

are lower uni-triangular. Let

be the
{\mathbb{N}}\times{\mathbb{N}}

Theorem 2.6
.

We have

EH=I

Proof.

Since
E
and
H
are both lower uni-triangular, so is their product. Therefore we only need to evaluate the entry

(EH)_{n,k}=\sum_{i\geq 0}(-1)^{n-i}e_{n-i}(n)h_{i-k}(k+1)

which is the sum in the previous theorem with

Specialization of
x_{i}=[2i-1]
immediately gives the following result.

Corollary 2.7
.

If
s_{B}=[s_{B}[n,k]]_{n,k\geq 0}
and
S_{B}=[S_{B}[n,k]]_{n,k\geq 0}
then
s_{B}S_{B}=I
. ∎

Note that setting
x_{i}=i-1
in the previous theorem gives the well-known result that
sS=I
where

s=[s(n,k)]_{n,k\geq 0}
and
S=[S(n,k)]_{n,k\geq 0}
.

3
Combinatorial interpretations

We will now give combinatorial interpretations of the
S_{B}[n,k]
and
c_{B}[n,k]
(and hence also the case
q=1
) in terms of certain statistics on signed partitions and permutations, respectively. The lattice of signed partitions ordered by refinement is isomorphic to the intersection lattice
{\cal L}_{B_{n}}
for the hyperplane arrangement of the Coxeter group
B_{n}
. We will rederive the fact that
s_{B}(n,k)
and
S_{B}(n,k)
are the Whitney numbers of the first and second kind, respectively, for
{\cal L}_{B_{n}}
, as well as proving a new result expressing the Möbius function of
{\cal L}_{B_{n}}
in terms signed permutations.

3.1
Signed partitions

Definition 3.1
.

Let
S
be a set and
\rho=\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\}
be a set partition of
S
, so the
S_{i}
are nonempty subsets whose disjoint union in
S
. We call the
S_{i}

blocks
and write
\rho=S_{1}/\ldots/S_{k}\vdash S
, removing the set braces from the
S_{i}
themselves.

Definition 3.2
.

A
signed
or
type
B
partition
is a partition of the set

\langle{n}\rangle

of the form

\rho=S_{0}/S_{1}/S_{2}/\dots/S_{2k}

satisfying

1.

0\in S_{0}
and if
i\in S_{0}
then
-i\in S_{0}
, and

2.

for
i\geq 1
we have
S_{2i}=-S_{2i-1}
,

where
-S=\{-s\ :\ s\in S\}
.
Call the blocks
S_{2i}
and
S_{2i-1}

paired
.
We write
\rho\vdash_{B}\langle{n}\rangle
and let

S_{B}(\langle{n}\rangle,k)
denote the set of all type
B
partitions of
\langle{n}\rangle
with
2k+1
blocks.

For readability, we will sometimes use an overline to represent a negative sign and group the paired blocks together separated by a forward slash, while vertical slashes separate pairs. Finally, set braces and commas may be removed.

Example 3.3
.

To illustrate, an element of
S_{B}(\langle{7}\rangle,2)
is

\rho=0\overline{1}1\overline{3}3\overline{6}6\mid\overline{4}/4\mid 2\overline{5}7/\overline{2}5\overline{7}.

Note that we may write the elements of any block in any order, reverse the order of any pair, and rearrange the pairs amongst themselves without changing the type
B
partition.

Let
|S|=\{|s|\ :\ s\in S\}
, so that
|S_{2i}|=|S_{2i-1}|
for
i\geq 1
.
For all
i
we let

m_{i}=\min|S_{i}|.

We will always write signed partitions in
standard form
which means that

1.

m_{2i}\in S_{2i}
for all
i
, and

2.

0=m_{0}<m_{2}<m_{4}<\dots<m_{2k}
.

Example 3.4
.

The standard form of our example type
B
partition above is

\rho=0\overline{1}1\overline{3}3\overline{6}6\mid\overline{2}5\overline{7}/2\overline{5}7\mid\overline{4}/4

with
m_{0}=0
,
m_{1}=m_{2}=2
, and
m_{3}=m_{4}=4
.

Definition 3.5
.

An
inversion
of
\rho\vdash_{B}\langle{n}\rangle

written in standard form is a pair

(s,S_{j})
satisfying

1.

s\in S_{i}
for some
i<j
, and

2.

s\geq m_{j}
.

Let
\Inv\rho
be set of inversions of
\rho
and
\inv\rho=\#\Inv\rho
where the hash tag denotes cardinality.

While we have not specified the order of elements within each block, this does not affect which pairs form inversions.
Note that the second condition implies that any
s
causing an inversion must be positive. Note also that in this condition it is not actually possible for
s=m_{i}
because of the partition being in standard form. However, we include it because when considering ordered set partitions equality will be possible and such cases will need to be counted.

Example 3.6
.

Continuing our example,

\Inv\rho=\{(3,S_{1}),(3,S_{2}),(6,S_{1}),(6,S_{2}),(6,S_{3}),(6,S_{4}),(5,S_{2}),(5,S_{3}),(5,S_{4}),(7,S_{3}),(7,S_{4})\}

so that
\inv\rho=11
.

The
S_{B}[n,k]
count signed partitions by inversions. The next theorem is a type
B
analogue of the result of Milne
[
Mil82
]
cited in the introduction for
S[n,k]
. See also
Section

6.2
for a strongly related interpretation involving the Hilbert series of a conjectural monomial basis.

Theorem 3.7
.

We have

S_{B}[n,k]=\sum_{\rho\in S_{B}(\langle{n}\rangle,k)}q^{\inv\rho}.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
n
where the base case is trivial.
Given
\rho\in S_{B}(\langle{n}\rangle,k)
we can remove
n
and
-n
to obtain a new partition
\rho^{\prime}
.

If
n
(and thus
-n
) is in a singleton block then
\rho^{\prime}\in S_{B}(\langle{n-1}\rangle,k-1)
and there is only one way to construct
\rho
from
\rho^{\prime}
.
Furthermore, in this case the standardization condition forces
S_{2k-1}=\{-n\}
and
S_{2k}=\{n\}
in
\rho
. It follows that

\inv\rho=\inv\rho^{\prime}
. So, by induction, such
\rho
contribute
S_{B}[n-1,k-1]
to the sum.

If
n
(and thus
-n
) is in a block with other elements, then
\rho^{\prime}\in S_{B}(\langle{n-1}\rangle,k)
. The possible
\rho
giving rise to a fixed
\rho^{\prime}
are obtained by inserting
n
in one of the
2k+1
blocks of
\rho^{\prime}
.
And if
n
is put in block
S_{i}
then this adds inversions of the form
(n,S_{j})
for all
j>i
.
Furthermore the placement of
-n
, wherever it is forced by that of
n
, does not contribute any inversions.
So

\inv\rho=2k-i+\inv\rho^{\prime}
where
0\leq i\leq 2k
.
Thus the contribution of these
\rho
is
[2k+1]S_{B}[n-1,k]
and we are done.
∎

Definition 3.8
.

The
descent set
of
\rho\in S_{B}(\langle{n}\rangle,k)
is the multiset

\Des\rho=\{\!\{1^{n_{1}},2^{n_{2}},\dots,(2k)^{n_{2k}}\}\!\}

where
n_{i}
is the number of elements of
S_{i-1}
which are greater than
m_{i}
. Define the
major index
of
\rho
to be

\maj\rho=1\cdot n_{1}+2\cdot n_{2}+\cdots+2k\cdot n_{2k}.

Note that this convention differs from the one for descents in a permutation in the symmetric group since
i
is the index of the block containing the smaller integer. This could be fixed by renumbering the blocks, but then the conventions above for
S_{0}
would become less natural.

Example 3.9
.

In our perennial example, descents are caused by the
3
and
6
in
S_{0}
, the
5
in
S_{1}
, and the
7
in
S_{2}
. Hence
\Des\rho=\{\!\{1^{2},2^{1},3^{1}\}\!\}
and

\maj\rho=1\cdot 2+2\cdot 1+3\cdot 1=7
.

Just as with permutations in the symmetric group,
\inv
and
\maj
have the same distribution over signed partitions.

Theorem 3.10
.

We have

S_{B}[n,k]=\sum_{\rho\in S_{B}(\langle{n}\rangle,k)}q^{\maj\rho}.

Proof.

We proceed as in the previous proof, keeping the same notation. When
n
and
-n
are in singleton blocks, the same reasoning applies to show
\maj\rho=\maj\rho^{\prime}
. So, as before, such
\rho
contribute
S_{B}[n-1,k-1]
to the sum.
Now assume that
n
is inserted into a block of
\rho^{\prime}
and
-n
into the companion block.
If
n
is in
S_{i}
then

\maj\rho=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\maj\rho^{\prime}&\mbox{if $i=2k$,}\\
i+1+\maj\rho^{\prime}&\mbox{if $0\leq i<2k$.}\end{array}\right.

Thus the contribution of these
\rho
is
[2k+1]S_{B}[n-1,k]
which finishes the proof.
∎

It is possible to give a combinatorial proof of equation (
14
) by showing that when
t
is a positive integer both sides count the set of functions
f:[n]\rightarrow[t]
. We will now give a combinatorial proof of
2.4
when
q=1
using similar ideas.

Definition 3.11
.

A
type
B
function
is any function
f:\langle{n}\rangle\rightarrow\langle{p}\rangle
satisfying

f(-i)=-f(i)

(15)

for all
i\in\langle{n}\rangle
. The
kernel of
f
is the partition
\ker f
of
\langle{n}\rangle
whose blocks are the nonempty fibers
f^{-1}(j)
for
j\in\langle{p}\rangle
.

In particular, a type
B
function satisfies
f(0)=0
, and if
f(i)=0
, then
f(-i)=0
. Hence the definition of a type
B
function ensures that
\ker f
is a type
B
partition.

Define the
type
B
falling factorial
to be

t\hskip-2.0pt\downarrow_{k}^{B}=(t;1)\hskip-2.0pt\downarrow_{k}^{B}=(t-1)(t-3)\cdots(t-2k+1).

The next result is the special case
q=1
of
2.4
. But here we give a combinatorial proof rather than relying on symmetric functions.
We note that this result with essentially the same proof below was also given in a paper of Bagno and Garber
[
BG22
]
, but we include it for completeness.

Theorem 3.12
.

For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

t^{n}=\sum_{k}S_{B}(n,k)t\hskip-2.0pt\downarrow^{B}_{k}.

Proof.

Since this is a polynomial identity, it suffices show that it holds when
t
is an odd positive integer, say
t=2p+1
. We claim that in this case both sides of the identity count the number of type
B
functions
f:\langle{n}\rangle\rightarrow\langle{p}\rangle
.

On the one hand, we can determine
f
by first choosing
f(1),\dots,f(n)
, in which case
f(-1),\dots,f(-n)
are determined by (
15
).
And we know
f(0)=0
.
Since
\#\langle{p}\rangle=t
, there are
t
ways to choose each of the necessary values, for a total count of
t^{n}
.

Alternatively, we can construct
f
by first picking a type
B
partition
\rho=S_{0}/S_{1}/\dots/S_{2k}
of
\langle{n}\rangle
to be
\ker f
and then injectively mapping the blocks of
\rho
into
\langle{p}\rangle
. Since
0\in S_{0}
we must have
f(S_{0})=0
.
This leaves
t-1
choices for
f(S_{1})
. Now
f(S_{2})
is determined by (
15
). There remains
t-3
choices for
f(S_{3})
, and so forth.
∎

3.2
Signed permutations

We now turn to the Stirling numbers of the first kind. A
permutation
of a finite set
S
is a bijection
\pi:S\rightarrow S
. As usual,
\pi
can be factored into
cycles

c=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{k})
where
\pi(a_{i})=a_{i+1}
with subscripts taken modulo
k
. Let
\langle{n}\rangle^{\prime}=\langle{n}\rangle\setminus\{0\}
.

Definition 3.13
.

A
signed
or
type
B
permutation
is a permutation
\pi
of
\langle{n}\rangle^{\prime}
satisfying

\pi(-i)=-\pi(i)

(16)

for all
i\in\langle{n}\rangle^{\prime}
.

This condition implies that any cycle
c
of
\pi
is of one of two types.

1.

If
c=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{k})
does not contain both
i
and
-i
for any
i\in\langle{n}\rangle^{\prime}
then
\pi
also contains
-c=(-a_{1},-a_{2},\ldots,-a_{k})
. We say that
c
and
-c
are
paired
.

2.

If
c
contains both
i
and
-i
for some
i\in\langle{n}\rangle^{\prime}
then
c
must have the form

c=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{k},-a_{1},-a_{2},\ldots,-a_{k}).

We call such a cycle
unpaired
.

Finally, let
c_{B}(\langle{n}\rangle^{\prime},k)
be the set of all
B_{n}
permutations with
2k
paired cycles.

We use the same conventions when writing signed cycle decomposition as for partitions, using bars instead of negative signs and keeping paired cycles closer together than others.

Example 3.14
.

In the permutation

\pi=(1,\overline{3},\overline{1},3)\ (\overline{4})(4)\ (2,\overline{5},7)(\overline{2},5,\overline{7})\ (\overline{6},6)

the four cycles
(\overline{4})
,
(4)
,
(2,\overline{5},7)
, and
(\overline{2},5,\overline{7})
are paired while the cycles
(1,\overline{3},\overline{1},3)
and
(\overline{6},6)
are not. Since there are
k=2
cycle pairs,
\pi\in c_{B}(\langle{7}\rangle^{\prime},2)
.

We will use the absolute value and minimum notation for permutations exactly as we did for partitions. Our
standard form
for a
B_{n}
permutation
\pi=c_{1}c_{2}\ldots c_{\ell}
will be to list the cycles so that the minima
m_{i}=\min|c_{i}|
satisfy

1.

m_{1}\leq m_{2}\leq\ldots\leq m_{\ell}
,

2.

if
m_{i}=m_{i+1}
then
-m_{i}\in c_{i}
and

m_{i+1}\in c_{i+1}
,

3.

each
c_{i}
is listed with its
\pm m_{i}
last, with unpaired cycles ending in
-m_{i}
.

Putting our example permutation is standard form gives

\pi=(3,1,\overline{3},\overline{1})\ (5,\overline{7},\overline{2})(\overline{5},7,2)\ (\overline{4})(4)\ (6,\overline{6}).

Definition 3.15
.

Given
\pi\in c_{B}(\langle{n}\rangle^{\prime},k)
in standard form, we let
w=w_{1}w_{2}\ldots w_{2n}
be the word obtained by removing the parentheses from
\pi
. Define the
set of inversions of
\pi
to be

\Inv\pi=\{(i,j)\ :\ \text{$i<j$ and $w_{i}>|w_{j}|$}\}

with corresponding
inversion number

\inv\pi=\#\Inv\pi.

Example 3.16
.

For our running example permutation, we have

w=3,1,\overline{3},\overline{1},5,\overline{7},\overline{2},\overline{5},7,2,\overline{4},4,6,\overline{6}.

Hence
\Inv\pi
is the set of pairs

(1,2),(1,4),(1,7),(1,10),(5,7),(5,10),(5,11),(5,12),(9,10),(9,11),(9,12),(9,13),(9,14)

and
\inv\pi=13
.

Theorem 3.17
.

We have

c_{B}[n,k]=\sum_{\pi\in c_{B}(\langle{n}\rangle^{\prime},k)}q^{\inv\pi}.

Proof.

As usual, we induct on
n
and only give details for the induction step. Take
\pi\in c_{B}(\langle{n}\rangle^{\prime},k)
and remove
n
and
-n
to form
\pi^{\prime}
.

If
n
and
-n
were both fixed points, then

\pi^{\prime}\in c_{B}(\langle{n-1}\rangle^{\prime},k-1)
. Furthermore, standard form forces the last two cycles of
\pi
to be
(-n)(n)
so that

\inv\pi=\inv\pi^{\prime}
. Thus permutations in this case give a contribution of
c_{B}[n-1,k-1]
to the sum.

The other possibility is that
n
and
-n
are both in cycles of length at least two. If these cycles are paired, then they remain paired cycles after these elements are removed. If both elements are in the same unpaired cycle then, even if that cycle contains no other elements, upon removal
\pi^{\prime}
still has the same number of paired cycles. So in either case
\pi^{\prime}\in c_{B}(\langle{n-1}\rangle^{\prime},k)
.

Now consider all the ways
\pm n
can be inserted in a given
\pi^{\prime}
in this case. Note that the position of
n
determines the position of
-n
, and that
-n
can never cause any inversions.
One possibility is to adjoin the cycle
(n,-n)
to
\pi
which must be at the right end to be in standard form. Now
n
causes no inversions either so
\inv\pi=\inv\pi^{\prime}
. The other possibility is that
n
is inserted just before, and in the same cycle as, any of the
2n-2
elements of
\pi^{\prime}
. (One must use the space before since using the space after a final element in a cycle would make the result nonstandard.) If this element is the
i
th from the right then
\inv\pi=i+\inv\pi^{\prime}
where
1\leq i\leq 2n-2
. It follows that the total contribution of this case is

(q^{0}+q^{1}+\cdots+q^{2n-2})c_{B}[n-1,k]=[2n-1]c_{B}[n-1,k]

which finishes the proof.
∎

Letting
q=1
in the previous theorem gives the following result.

Corollary 3.18
.

The number of
B_{n}
permutations with
2k
paired cycles is
c_{B}(n,k)
.

3.3
The lattice of signed partitions

We now connect the
S_{B}(n,k)
and
s_{B}(n,k)
with the intersection lattice for the Coxeter group
B_{n}
.

Definition 3.19
.

Let
P
be a finite poset (partially ordered set) with a unique minimal element
\hat{0}
. Call
P

ranked
if, for every
x\in P
, all maximal chains from
\hat{0}
to
x
have the same length. This length is called the
rank of
x
and denoted
\rk x
. The
k
th rank of
P
is

\Rk(P,k)=\{x\in P\ :\ \rk x=k\}

with corresponding
Whitney number of the second kind

W(P,k)=\#\Rk(P,k).

The
(one variable) Möbius function
of
P
is the function
\mu:P\rightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}
defined recursively by

\sum_{x\leq y}\mu(x)=\delta_{\hat{0},y}.

This is a far-reaching generalization of the Möbius function in number theory. See
[
Sag20
]
or
[
Sta97
]
for more details. The
Whitney numbers of the first kind
for
P
are

w(P,k)=\sum_{x\in\Rk(P,k)}\mu(x).

If
P
has a unique maximal element
\hat{1}
then we will use the notation

\mu(P)=\mu(\hat{1}).

Definition 3.20
.

For any finite set
S
we denote by
\Pi_{S}
the lattice of all set partitions of
S
ordered by
refinement
so that
\rho\leq\sigma
if every block of
\rho
is contained in some block of
\sigma
.
Let
\Pi_{B_{n}}
denote the subposet of
\Pi_{\langle{n}\rangle}
obtained by restricting the partial order to the type
B
partitions. We call
\Pi_{B_{n}}
the
B_{n}
partition lattice
.

The Coxeter group
B_{n}
has reflecting hyperplanes
x_{i}=0
and
x_{i}=\pm x_{j}
where
i,j\in[n]
and
x_{i}
is the
i
th coordinate function. The corresponding
intersection lattice
,
{\cal L}_{B_{n}}
, is the set of all subspaces which are intersections of these hyperplanes ordered by reverse inclusion. Zaslavsky
[
Zas81
]
showed that these subspaces are in bijective correspondence with certain signed graphs which are clearly in bijection with signed set partitions of
\langle{n}\rangle
. It easily follows that
{\cal L}_{B_{n}}
and
\Pi_{B_{n}}
are isomorphic.

It will be useful to connect type
B
partitions and permutations. Given a signed permutation
\pi
, its
underlying signed set partition
is
\rho
obtained by replacing every paired cycle by its underlying subset, and taking the union of the sets underlying all paired cycles together with
\{0\}
to be the zero block. The reader can verify that our recurring example
\pi
and
\rho
satisfy this relation. We let
B(\rho)
be the set of type
B
permutations with underlying set
\rho
.
The first and third parts of the next result also follow from
[
Zas81
]
.

Theorem 3.21
.

Fix
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and let
\mu
be the Möbius function of
\Pi_{B_{n}}
.

(a)

For
0\leq k\leq n
, we have
W(\Pi_{B_{n}},k)=S_{B}(n,n-k)
.

(b)

If
\rho=S_{0}/S_{1}/S_{2}/\ldots/S_{2k}\in S_{B}(\langle{n}\rangle,k)
then

\displaystyle\#B(\rho)

\displaystyle=(\#S_{0}-2)!!\prod_{i=1}^{k}(\#S_{2i}-1)!

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n-k}\mu(\rho).

(c)

For
0\leq k\leq n
we have
w(\Pi_{B_{n}},k)=s_{B}(n,n-k)
.

Proof.

To prove (a), if
\rho
is covered by
\sigma
in
\Pi_{B_{n}}
, then there are two possibilities. One is that there were two pairs of blocks
S_{2i-1},S_{2i}
and
S_{2j-1},S_{2j}
in
\rho
which were replaced in
\sigma
by a pair whose component blocks are unions of one block from each of the given pairs. The other is that a pair
S_{2i-1},S_{2i}
was absorbed into the zero block.
Note that in either case, the total number of blocks decreases by two in passing from
\rho
to
\sigma
.
Using this fact, the desired result now follows from an easy induction on
k
.

For the first equality in (b), note that, for
i\geq 1
, the number of ways to turn
S_{2i}
into a cycle is
(\#S_{2i}-1)!
.
And once a cycle is put on
S_{2i}
, the cycle on
S_{2i-1}
is fixed.
So, letting
\#S_{0}=2m+1
, it suffices to show that the number of ways to decompose
S_{0}\setminus\{0\}
into unpaired cycles is

(2m-1)!!
. But the count we seek is the number of signed permutations of
\langle{m}\rangle^{\prime}
which have no paired cycles. By
3.18
and
2.1
(a) with
q=1
, this is
c_{B}(m,0)=e_{m}(1,3,\ldots,2m-1)=(2m-1)!!
.

We now prove (c) and the second equality in (b) simultaneously by induction on
n
. From the description of the covering relations in part (a) we see that
the interval
[\hat{0},\rho]
in
\Pi_{B_{n}}
is isomorphic to a product of posets, with one poset for
S_{0}
and one for each pair
S_{2i-1},S_{2i}
for
1\leq i\leq k
. For
i\geq 1
, the partitions contained in
S_{2i}
form the lattice
\Pi_{S_{2i}}
. And once a partition
\sigma
of
S_{2i}
is chosen, then
S_{2i-1}
must be partitioned so that each block is the negative of some block of
\sigma
. For the zero block, the partitions contained in
S_{0}
contribute the lattice
\Pi_{B_{m}}
. Thus we have the isomorphism

[\hat{0},\rho]\cong\Pi_{B_{m}}\times\Pi_{S_{2}}\times\Pi_{S_{4}}\times\cdots\times\Pi_{S_{2k}}

and, since
\mu(P\times Q)=\mu(P)\mu(Q)
,

\mu(\rho)=\mu(\Pi_{B_{m}})\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mu(\Pi_{S_{2i}}).

(17)

It is well known that
\mu(\Pi_{S})=(-1)^{\#S-1}(\#S-1)!
. And we can assume by induction that for
m<n
we have
\mu(\Pi_{B_{m}})=(-1)^{m}(2m-1)!!
. Plugging these values into (
17
) proves the second equality in (b) as long as
\rho<\hat{1}
. Furthermore, from the proof of (a), the
\rho
at rank
k
of
\Pi_{B_{n}}
are exactly those signed partitions of
\langle{n}\rangle
with
2(n-k)+1
blocks. It follows that for
\rho<\hat{1}
, the sum of the
\mu
values of these partitions is exactly
(-1)^{k}c_{B}(n,n-k)=s_{B}(n,n-k)
.

To handle the case
\rho=\hat{1}
, we use the definition of
\mu
and the previous paragraph to give

\mu(\hat{1})=-\sum_{\rho<\hat{1}}\mu(\rho)=-\sum_{k<n}s_{B}(n,n-k).

On the other hand, plugging in
q=1
and
t=-1
to
2.1
(c) and multiplying both sides by
(-1)^{n}
gives

\sum_{k=0}^{n}s_{B}(n,k)=0.

Comparing the last two displayed equations and using
2.1
(a) when

q=1
and
k=0
yields

\mu(\hat{1})=s_{B}(n,0)=(-1)^{n}e_{n}(1,3,\ldots,2n-1)=(-1)^{n}(2n-1)!!

which finishes this case and the proof.
∎

4
Exponential and
q
-exponential generating functions

We now derive exponential and
q
-exponential generating functions for the Stirling numbers and
q
-Stirling numbers of types
A
and
B
. We give combinatorial proofs for the former using the theory of species. See the book of Bergeron, Labelle, and Leroux
[
BLL98
]
or
[
Sag20
, Chapter 4]
for more information. For the latter, we use the theory of
q
-difference equations.
Existence and uniqueness of solutions of linear
q
-difference equations with constant coefficients is given explicitly in
[
AM12
, Thm. 2.11]
. A general existence and uniqueness result for
q
-difference equations is given in
[
AM12
, Thm. 2.1]
. Basic properties of
q
-derivatives such as their definition and the
q
-product rule are summarized in
[
AM12
, §1.3]
.

Our
q
-exponential generating function identities do not seem to be known for the Stirling numbers even for
S[n,k]
and
s[n,k]
. Consequently, we will provide full proofs of these results in type
A
and then just sketch whatever changes are needed for type
B
.

4.1
Exponential generating functions

The following identities are well known; see e.g.
[
DLMF
, §26.8(ii)]
,
[
OEI22
, A008275]
, and
[
OEI22
, A008277]
.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}S(n,k)\frac{x^{n}}{n!}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k!}(e^{x}-1)^{k},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{n,k\geq 0}S(n,k)t^{k}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}

\displaystyle=e^{t(e^{x}-1)},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}c(n,k)\frac{x^{n}}{n!}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k!}\left(\ln\frac{1}{1-x}\right)^{k},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{n,k\geq 0}c(n,k)t^{k}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-x)^{t}}.

The type
B
analogues are as follows. The first is stated (without proof) in
[
OEI22
, A039755]
. The last two are stated (again, without proof) in
[
OEI22
, A028338]
, though effectively using
2.1
(c) as a definition.

Theorem 4.1
.

We have

(a)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}S_{B}(n,k)\frac{x^{n}}{n!}=\frac{1}{2^{k}k!}e^{x}(e^{2x}-1)^{k}
.

(b)

\displaystyle\sum_{k,n\geq 0}S_{B}(n,k)t^{k}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}=e^{x}\sqrt{e^{t(e^{2x}-1)}}
.

(c)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}c_{B}(n,k)\frac{x^{n}}{n!}=\frac{1}{k!\sqrt{1-2x}}\left(\log\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-2x}}\right)^{k}
.

(d)

\displaystyle\sum_{k,n\geq 0}c_{B}(n,k)t^{k}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}=\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-2x}}\right)^{1+t}
.

Proof.

We will only prove (a) and (c) since then (b) and (d) follow by summing on
k
.

For (a) note there is a bijection between type
B
partitions
S_{0}/\ldots/S_{2k}

and ordered pairs of the form

(T_{0},S_{2}/S_{4}/\ldots/S_{2k})
where
T_{0}
is the (possibly empty) set of positive integers in
S_{0}
. This is because the other elements of
S_{0}
are
-S_{0}\uplus\{0\}
and
S_{2i-1}=-S_{2i}
for all
i
.

By the conventions for the
S_{2i}
, its smallest element is positive and the others can be signed arbitrarily. Consider the species
{\cal S}
such that
{\cal S}(L)
is all sets obtained from a set of nonempty integers
L
by arbitrarily signing every element of
L
except the smallest. The number of such sets is
2^{\#L-1}
with corresponding exponential generating function

\sum_{n\geq 1}2^{n-1}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}=\frac{1}{2}(e^{2x}-1).

So, by the Product Rule for exponential generating functions
[
Sag20
, Theorem 4.4.2(b)]
, the exponential generating function for ordered
k
-tuples of such sets is
(e^{2x}-1)^{k}/2^{k}
. We must divide by
k!
to remove the order. For
T_{0}
, we use the species
{\cal T}(L)=\{L\}
which has exponential generating function
e^{x}
since
\#{\cal T}(L)=1
for all
L
. Now using the Product Rule again completes the proof of (a).

To prove (c), consider a type
B
permutation
\pi
and its underlying partition
\rho
. As in part (a), we can reconstruct
\rho
from a pair
(T_{0},S_{2}/S_{4}/\ldots/S_{2k})
. To recover the possible
\pi
associated with
\rho
we must sign all but the smallest element of
S_{2i}
and then put a cycle on these elements, where the latter can be done in
(n-1)!
ways if
\#S_{2i}=n
. This gives the exponential generating function

\sum_{n\geq 1}(n-1)!2^{n-1}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{(2x)^{n}}{n}=\log\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-2x}}.

On the other hand, if
\#T_{0}=n
then the elements in
T_{0}\uplus(-T_{0})
need to be turned into unpaired cycles. By
2.1
(a), this can be done in

c_{B}(n,0)=(2n-1)!!
ways which gives an exponential generating function of

\sum_{n\geq 0}(2n-1)!!\frac{x^{n}}{n!}=\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1/2)\hskip-2.0pt\downarrow_{n}(-2)^{n}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\binom{-1/2}{n}(-2x)^{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-2x}}.

Using the Product Rule and dividing by
k!
to remove the order finishes the proof.
∎

4.2
q
-exponential generating functions

For our
q
-exponential generating functions, we will need the
q
-binomial coefficients

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}n\\
k\end{array}\right]=\frac{[n]!}{[k]![n-k]!},

the
q
-exponential function

\exp_{q}(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{x^{n}}{[n]!},

the
q
-logarithm

-\log_{q}(1-x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{x^{n}}{[n]},

as well as the
q
-derivative

D_{q}f(x)=\frac{f(qx)-f(x)}{qx-x}.

See
[
AM12
, §1.3]
for a summary of
q
-calculus. It will also be convenient to find the
q
-exponential generating functions for the ordered versions of the Stirling numbers of the second kind.

Theorem 4.2
.

We have

\sum_{n\geq 0}S^{o}[n,k]\frac{x^{n}}{[n]!}=\frac{1}{q^{\binom{k}{2}}}\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^{k-i}q^{\binom{k-i}{2}}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}k\\
i\end{array}\right]\exp_{q}([i]x),

(18)

and

\sum_{n,k\geq 0}S^{o}[n,k]t^{k}\frac{x^{n}}{[n]!}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{i}t^{i}}{(1+t)(q+t)\cdots(q^{i}+t)}\exp_{q}([i]x).

Proof.

To simplify the proof of the first equality, let

E_{k}=\sum_{n\geq 0}S^{o}[n,k]\frac{x^{n}}{[n]!}=\sum_{n\geq 0}[k]!S[n,k]\frac{x^{n}}{[n]!}.

From the recursion (
2
) we get that

S^{o}[n,k]=[k](S^{o}[n-1,k-1]+S^{o}[n-1,k])

(19)

for
n\geq 1
.
Combining this with the
q
-derivative

D_{q}x^{n}=[n]x^{n-1}

implies that

D_{q}E_{k}=[k](E_{k}+E_{k-1})

(20)

for all
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
with
E_{k}=0
for
k<0
. This equation and induction on
k
give

\left(\prod_{i=0}^{k}(D_{q}-[i])\right)E_{k}=0.

Since
D_{q}\exp_{q}([i]x)=[i]\exp_{q}([i]x)
, the theory of linear, constant-coefficient
q
-difference equations now implies that

E_{k}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}c_{i}^{k}\exp_{q}([i]x)

(21)

for certain
c_{i}^{k}
which are constant with respect to
x
. So it suffices to show that

c_{i}^{k}=\frac{(-1)^{k-i}q^{\binom{k-i}{2}}}{q^{\binom{k}{2}}}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}k\\
i\end{array}\right].

(22)

Substituting (
21
) into (
20
) and using the linear independence of
\{\exp_{q}([i]x)\}_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
gives

c_{i}^{k}=\frac{-[k]}{q^{i}[k-i]}c_{i}^{k-1}

for
0\leq i\leq k-1
which, after iteration, results in

c_{i}^{k}=\frac{(-1)^{k-i}}{q^{i(k-i)}}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}k\\
k-i\end{array}\right]c_{i}^{i}.

(23)

Comparing this with (
22
) and using the identity

i(k-i)+\binom{k-i}{2}=\binom{k}{2}-\binom{i}{2},

(24)

we see that we will be done if we can show

c_{i}^{i}=q^{-\binom{i}{2}}

for
i\geq 0
. Let us rewrite this as
c_{k}^{k}=q^{-\binom{k}{2}}
and induct on
k
. Since
E_{k}(0)=\delta_{0,k}
, we see from (
21
) that
c_{0}^{0}=1=q^{-\binom{0}{2}}
and,
for
k\geq 1
,

\sum_{i=0}^{k}c_{i}^{k}=0.

This equation determines
c_{k}^{k}
in terms of the
c_{i}^{k}
for
i<k
. And the latter are known to have the desired form by equation (
23
) and induction. So it suffices to prove that

0=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\frac{(-1)^{k-i}}{q^{i(k-i)+\binom{i}{2}}}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}k\\
k-i\end{array}\right]=\frac{1}{q^{\binom{k}{2}}}\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^{k-i}q^{\binom{k-i}{2}}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}k\\
k-i\end{array}\right]=\frac{1}{q^{\binom{k}{2}}}\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^{i}q^{\binom{i}{2}}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}k\\
i\end{array}\right].

But this last sum is seen to be zero by substituting
t=-1
into the
q
-Binomial Theorem

\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}(1+q^{i}t)=\sum_{i=0}^{k}q^{\binom{i}{2}}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}k\\
i\end{array}\right]t^{i}.

(25)

For the second equality in the statement of the theorem, we multiply (
18
) by
t^{k}
and sum to get

\displaystyle\sum_{n,k\geq 0}[k]!S[n,k]t^{k}\frac{x^{n}}{[n]!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{k}}{q^{\binom{k}{2}}}\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^{k-i}q^{\binom{k-i}{2}}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}k\\
i\end{array}\right]\exp_{q}([i]x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{q^{\binom{i}{2}}}\left(\sum_{k=i}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k-i}}{q^{i(k-i)}}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}k\\
i\end{array}\right]t^{k}\right)\exp_{q}([i]x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{i}t^{i}}{(1+t)(q+t)\cdots(q^{i}+t)}\exp_{q}([i]x),

where the second equality uses equation (
24
), and the third is a form of the Negative
q
-Binomial Theorem as in Exercise 8(b) from Chapter 3 of
[
Sag20
]
substituting
-t
for
t
and
1/q
for
q
.
∎

The proof of the next result is similar to the one just given. So we will only mention the highlights.

Theorem 4.3
.

We have

\sum_{n\geq 0}S_{B}^{o}[n,k]\frac{x^{n}}{[n]!}=\frac{1}{q^{k^{2}}}\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^{k-i}q^{2\binom{k-i}{2}}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}k\\
i\end{array}\right]_{q^{2}}\exp_{q}([2i+1]x),

and

\sum_{n,k\geq 0}S_{B}^{o}[n,k]t^{k}\frac{x^{n}}{[n]!}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{2i+1}t^{i}}{(q+t)(q^{3}+t)\cdots(q^{2i+1}+t)}\exp_{q}([2i+1]x).

Proof.

Let
F_{k}
be the first sum. Using the recursion (
8
) and the fact that

S_{B}^{o}[n,k]=[2k]!!S_{B}[n,k]
we see that

S_{B}^{o}[n,k]=[2k]S_{B}^{o}[n-1,k-1]+[2k+1]S_{B}^{o}[n-1,k]

for
n\geq 1
, This in turn implies that for
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}

D_{q}F_{k}=[2k+1]F_{k}+[2k]F_{k-1}

(26)

and
F_{k}=0
for
k<0
. Thus

F_{k}
satisfies the linear
q
-difference equation

\left(\prod_{i=0}^{k}(D_{q}-[2i+1])\right)F_{k}=0.

So we can write

F_{k}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}d_{i}^{k}\exp_{q}([2i+1]x)

for certain constants
d_{i}^{k}
. To show that the
d_{i}^{k}
have the correct form, one uses (
26
) and iteration to get

d_{i}^{k}=\frac{(-1)^{k-i}}{q^{(2i+1)(k-i)}}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}k\\
k-i\end{array}\right]_{q^{2}}d_{i}^{i}.

The analogue of (
24
) is

(2i+1)(k-i)+i^{2}=k^{2}-2\binom{k-i}{2},

(27)

which implies that we must show
d_{k}^{k}=q^{-k^{2}}
. This is accomplished recursively using the
q
-Binomial Theorem (
25
) as before with the substitutions
t=-1
and
q^{2}
for
q
.

The bivariate generating function is now obtained by summing over
k
and using the previous version of the Negative
q
-Binomial Theorem with substitutions
-t/q
for
t
and
1/q^{2}
for
q
.
∎

For the
q
-Stirling numbers of the first kind we will need to use a version of the chain rule. Unfortunately, no such analogue exists for
D_{q}(g(f))
. But Gessel
[
Ges82
]
defined a
q
-analogue of composition which does obey a
q
-chain rule. Given an
q
-exponential generating function
f(x)
with
f(0)=1
, define its
k
th symbolic power
recursively by
f^{[0]}=1
and

D_{q}f^{[k]}=[k]f^{[k-1]}D_{q}f.

Note that
x^{[k]}=x^{k}
, so that when
q=1
we have
f^{[k]}=f^{k}

for
k\geq 0
. Given
g=\sum_{n\geq 0}g_{n}x^{n}/[n]!
, Gessel then defines a
q
-analogue of functional composition
to be

g[f]=\sum_{n\geq 0}g_{n}\frac{f^{[n]}}{[n]!}.

Again, when
q=1
we have
g[f]=g(f)
. Gessel’s
q
-analogue of the chain rule states that

D_{q}(g[f])=(D_{q}g)[f]D_{q}f.

(28)

The first result of the next theorem was also obtained by Johnson
[
Joh96
, (4.12)]
.

Theorem 4.4
.

We have

\sum_{n\geq 0}c[n,k]\frac{x^{n}}{[n]!}=\frac{(-\log_{q}(1-x))^{[k]}}{[k]!},

and

\sum_{n,k\geq 0}c[n,k]t^{k}\frac{x^{n}}{[n]!}=\exp_{q}[-t\log_{q}(1-x)].

Proof.

Let
C_{k}=\sum_{n\geq 0}c[n,k]x^{n}/[n]!
.
The usual manipulations and the recursion (
5
) give the
q
-difference equation

D_{q}C_{k}=\frac{C_{k-1}}{1-x}

for
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
with
C_{k}=0
for
k<0
. The formula for
C_{k}
now follows from a simple induction on
k
using the definition of symbolic power. And the bivariate generating function is a consequence of the definition of
q
-composition.
∎

Unfortunately, for the
c_{B}[n,k]
we were only able to derive a differential equation for the desired
q
-exponential generating function. We will have more to say about this in
Section

7.3
.

5
Ordered analogues and identities

In this section we will prove alternating sum identities as well as divisiblity results for the ordered
q
-Stirling numbers of the second kind. The former will prove two conjectures of Swanson and Wallach
[
SW21
]
. Our main tools will be the use of sign-reversing involutions. These results and their demonstrations are new even in type
A
so, as in the previous section, the type
B
proofs will only be sketched.

5.1
Alternating sums

We first need a combinatorial interpretation for the
S^{o}[n,k]
. These polynomials count
ordered set partitions of
[n]
into
k
blocks
which are sequences of nonempty sets
\omega=(S_{1}/S_{2}/\ldots/S_{k})
such that
\uplus_{i}S_{i}=[n]
.
Note the use of parentheses to denote a sequence rather than a family of sets.
The set of these sequences is denoted
S^{o}([n],k)
. We define the inversion statistic exactly the same as for unordered signed partitions using
3.5
, letting

\Inv\omega=\{(s,S_{j})\ :\ \text{$s\in S_{i}$ for some $i<j$ and $s\geq\min S_{j}$}\}

(29)

and
\inv\omega=\#\Inv\omega
.
Using a similar proof to that of Theorem
3.7
, one can show the following.

Theorem 5.1
.

For
n,k\geq 0
we have

\rule{4.30554pt}{0.0pt}\hfill{\displaystyle S^{o}[n,k]=\sum_{\omega\in S^{o}([n],k)}q^{\inv\omega}.}\hfill\qed

Definition 5.2
.

We now define the maps which will make up our involution in type
A
. Given an ordered partition
\omega=(S_{1}/S_{2}/\ldots/S_{k})
, suppose
M=\max S_{i}
.
Say that
\omega
is
splittable at
M
if
\#S_{i}\geq 2
. In that case the
splitting map

\sigma_{M}
is defined by

\sigma_{M}(\omega)=(S_{1}/\ldots/S_{i-1}/\{M\}/S_{i}-\{M\}/S_{i+1}/\ldots/S_{k}).

We define
\omega
to be
mergeable at
M
if

1.

S_{i}=\{M\}
, and

2.

M>\max S_{i+1}
.

If these conditions hold then one can apply the
merging map

\mu_{M}
where

\mu_{M}(\omega)=(S_{i}/\ldots/S_{i-1}/S_{i}\uplus S_{i+1}/S_{i+2}/\ldots/S_{k}).

Example 5.3
.

The ordered set partition
\omega=(246/8/35/1/7)
is splittable for
M=6
and
5
, and

\sigma_{6}(\omega)=(6/24/8/35/1/7).

On the other hand,
\omega
is only mergeable for
M=8
and

\mu_{8}(\omega)=(246/358/1/7).

Note that if
\omega
is splittable at
M
, then
\sigma_{M}(\omega)
is mergeable at
M
and
\mu_{M}\sigma_{M}(\omega)=\omega
. The same statement holds with the roles of
\sigma_{M}
and
\mu_{M}
reversed. Merge and split maps have been useful in a number of areas, including the computation of antipodes in Hopf algebras as shown by Benedetti and Sagan
[
BS17
]
. We can now define the involution we will use for our first alternating sum.

Definition 5.4
.

Define
\phi:\uplus_{k}S^{o}([n],k)\rightarrow\uplus_{k}S^{o}([n],k)
as follows. Given
\omega\in S^{o}([n],k)
,
find the largest
M
(if any) such that
\omega
is either splitable or mergeable at
M=\max S_{i}
. Note that, because of the restriction on
\#S_{i}
for these two operations, it can not be both. Let

\phi(\omega)=\begin{cases}\sigma_{M}(\omega)&\text{if $\omega$ is splittable at $M$,}\\
\mu_{M}(\omega)&\text{if $\omega$ is mergeable at $M$,}\\
\omega&\text{if no such $M$ exists.}\end{cases}

We see that
\phi
is an involution because of the remarks at the end of the last paragraph and the fact that the largest splittable or mergeable
M
is preserved by splitting or merging at this value.

Theorem 5.5
.

For
n\geq 0
, we have

\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-q)^{n-k}S^{o}[n,k]=1.

Proof.

Define the sign of
\omega\in S^{o}([n],k)
to be

\sgn\omega=(-1)^{n-k}.

(30)

The involution
\phi
is sign-reversing on partitions which are not fixed since both
\sigma_{M}
and
\mu_{M}
change the number of blocks of
\omega
by
1
.

By
5.1
, we can write

\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-q)^{n-k}S^{o}[n,k]=\sum_{\omega\in\uplus_{k}S^{o}([n],k)}(\sgn\omega)q^{n-k+\inv\omega}.

(31)

We claim that the terms for
\omega
and
\phi(\omega)
in equation (
31
) will cancel each other since splitting adds one inversion and one block whereas merging removes one inversion and one block, hence
n-k+\inv
is preserved by
\phi
.

To see that the claim holds when
\phi
applies
\sigma_{M}
, note that
since
M
is not the minimum of its block all the inversions of
\omega
will still be inversions of
\phi(\omega)
. Furthermore, if
M=\max S_{i}
then splitting off
M
will cause a new inversion
(M,S_{i}-\{M\})
. Thus
\inv\phi(\omega)=\inv\omega+1
.
Since
\phi
is an involution, it also follows that

\inv\phi(\omega)=\inv\omega-1
.

To complete the proof, it suffices to show that
\omega_{0}=(1,2,\ldots,n)
is a fixed point of
\phi
and the only one since its contribution to (
31
) is
1
. Clearly
\omega_{0}
is fixed since it has no blocks of size at least
2
and its block maxima are in increasing order. Conversely, if
\omega
is a fixed point, then it can have no blocks of size
2
since then one could apply
\sigma_{M}
. So
\omega
is a sequence of singleton blocks with increasing elements since otherwise
\mu_{M}
could be applied. The sequence
\omega_{0}
is the only ordered partition with these two properties. This finishes the proof.
∎

To prove the type
B
analogue of the previous theorem, we proceed in a similar manner.
An
ordered signed partition
of
\langle{n}\rangle
is a sequence

\omega=(S_{0}/S_{1}/S_{2}/\ldots/S_{2k})
satisfying conditions (1) and (2) in
3.2
. Note that no assumption is made about standard form.
The set of such partitions with
2k+1
blocks is denoted
S_{B}^{o}(\langle{n}\rangle,k)
.

The definition of inversion in
3.5
remains unchanged. But now it is possible to have an inversion where
s=m_{j}

if
m_{j}\in S_{j-1}
and
-m_{j}\in S_{j}
. The usual arguments give us the following result.

Theorem 5.6
.

For
n,k\geq 0
we have

\rule{4.30554pt}{0.0pt}\hfill{\displaystyle S_{B}^{o}[n,k]=\sum_{\omega\in S_{B}^{o}(\langle{n}\rangle,k)}q^{\inv\omega}.}\hfill\qed

Definition 5.7
.

The

and

t^{n-k}
for these
T
completes the proof.
∎

For the description of
{\cal S}{\cal A}{\cal B}
in terms of ordered set partitions
\omega
we will use the same notation as in type
A
. This will cause no confusion because it will be clear from context whether the necessary functions are being applied to a partition which is type
A
or type
B
. We write
\omega\models\langle{n}\rangle
if
\omega=(S_{0}/S_{1}/S_{2}/\ldots)
is an ordered set partition of
\langle{n}\rangle
. The definitions (
33
), (
34
), and (
35
) carry over to type
B
without change. For example, if we have

\omega=(0\overline{1}1\mid 4/\overline{4}\mid\overline{2}3/2\overline{3})
then

\eta(\omega)=(0,0,1,3)
and
T(\omega)=\{1,3\}
.

Proposition 6.19
.

We have

{\cal S}{\cal A}{\cal B}_{n}=\{{\bf x}^{\eta(\omega)}\theta_{T(\omega)}\ :\ \omega\models\langle{n}\rangle\}.

Proof.

As in type
A
,
it suffices to define a weight-preserving bijection from the pairs
(T,\alpha)
appearing in
6.16
to the
\omega\models\langle{n}\rangle
. Given
(T,\alpha)
we construct
\omega
by starting with
\omega=(0)
and inserting the numbers
\pm 1,\pm 2,\ldots,\pm n
in order according to the following rules. Note that the position of
k
forces the position of
-k
so that either both are in the zero block or
-k
is in the block paired to the one containing
k
.

1.

If
k\in T
then put
k
in the existing block of
\omega
so that exactly
\alpha_{k}
new inversions result. (The forced insertion of
-k
will not cause any new inversions in this case.)

2.

If
k\not\in T
then make
k
and
-k
a new pair of blocks of
\omega
so that exactly
\alpha_{k}
new inversions result.

As before, details that this map is well defined and invertible are straightforward and so omitted.
∎

Example 6.20
.

Suppose that
n=3
,
T=\{2,3\}
, and
\alpha=(0,1,2)
. We start with
\omega=(0)
. Since
1\not\in T
, we must add
1
and
\overline{1}
as separate blocks. And
\alpha_{1}=0
means that no inversions are to be created so that now

\omega=(0\mid\overline{1}/1).

We have
2\in T
so it must be placed in a block with other elements. And it must create
\alpha_{2}=1
new inversion. This forces
2
into the block with
\overline{1}
and also
\overline{2}
into the block with
1
resulting in

\omega=(0\mid\overline{1}2/1\overline{2}).

Finally,
3\in T
so
T
will go into one of the existing blocks. It must create
\alpha_{3}=2
inversions and so must reside in the zero block. It follows that
\overline{3}
is also in
S_{0}
and we finally have

\omega=(0\overline{3}3\mid\overline{1}2/1\overline{2}).

7
Comments and open questions

In this section we collect various comments and open questions raised by the present work.

7.1
Complex reflection groups

If
G
is any complex reflection group, then one can define Stirling numbers of the first and second kind for
G
using the Whitney numbers of the first and second kind, respectively, for
G
’s intersection lattice analogous to
3.21
in type
B
.
It follows from the work of Shephard and Todd
[
ST54
]
that the Stirling numbers of the first kind can be expressed as elementary symmetric polynomials in the coexponents of
G
. For the Stirling numbers of the second kind, the situation is more complicated and this approach is explored in
[
SS
]
.

7.2
Major index for signed permutations and super coinvariant bases

We showed in
3.2
that
S_{B}[n,k]
can be viewed as the generating function for both an inversion and a major index statistic on signed partitions. By contrast, only an inversion generating function was given for
c_{B}[n,k]
in
3.17
. It would be interesting to find a major index analogue for permutations in type
B
.

In another direction, the Artin basis in type
A
has a well-known “major index analogue,” the Garsia–Stanton basis

\left\{\prod_{i\in\Des(\pi)}x_{\pi(1)}\cdots x_{\pi(i)}\ :\ \sigma\in S_{n}\right\}.

See
[
GS84
]
.
Adin–Brenti–Roichman
[
ABR05
]
gave a type
B
analogue of the Garsia–Stanton basis using the
flag-major index
of Adin–Roichman
[
AR01
]
. It would be interesting to give conjectured super coinvariant extensions of these bases in types
A
and
B
.

7.3
q
-difference equations

The generating function

C=\sum_{n,k\geq 0}c[n,k]t^{k}\frac{x^{n}}{[n]!}

in

4.4
satisfies the first-order linear
q
-difference equation

D_{q}C-\frac{tC}{1-x}=0

(36)

where
C(0,t)=1
.
The classical solution of
y^{\prime}+p(x)y=0
given by
y=\exp\left(-\int p(x)\,dx\right)
generalizes to
q
-difference equations using Gessel’s
q
-composition and
q
-chain rule. In particular, if
D_{q}Y+P(x)Y=0
, then
Y=\exp\left[\int P(x)\,d_{q}x\right]
, where we have used the
q
-antiderivative. From (
36
),
C=\exp[-y\log_{q}(1-x)]
.

Now consider

C_{B}=\sum_{n,k\geq 0}c_{B}[n,k]t^{k}\frac{x^{n}}{[n]!}.

Using manipulations similar to those in the proof of
4.4
, one may show
C_{B}
obeys the following second-order linear
q
-difference equation.

Lemma 7.1
.

We have

x^{2}q^{2}(1-q)D_{q}^{2}C_{B}+(1-q(1+q)x)D_{q}C_{B}-(1+t)C_{B}=0

(37)

where
C_{B}(0,t)=1
.

When
q=1
, this becomes a first order differential equation with solution
(1-2x)^{-(1+t)/2}
, giving an alternate proof of
4.1
(d).

We are unaware of techniques which allow us to solve general second-order linear q-differential equations in terms of well-known operations. However, we may rewrite (
37
) as a first order equation using the
q
-pre-composition operator

Q(F(x))=F(qx)
:

D_{q}C_{B}-qxD_{q}(1+Q)C_{B}-(1+t)C_{B}=0.

If we replace
Q
with
q
(i.e. post-multiply by
q
rather than pre-multiply by
q
), we may solve the resulting
q
-differential equation using the methods of the previous paragraph, which may be considered as a “first approximation” of
C_{B}
. More explicitly, we have

(1-q(1+q)x)D_{q}\widetilde{C}_{B}-(1+t)\widetilde{C}_{B}=0

where

\widetilde{C}_{B}=\exp_{q}\left[-\frac{1+t}{q(1+q)}\log_{q}[1-q(1+q)x]\right].

When
q=1
, we again recover
4.1
(d). Since a wide variety of special functions satisfy second order linear differential equations, solving (
37
) in a recognizable way would be interesting.

7.4
Sign-reversing involutions

In
Section

5.1
we gave combinatorial proofs of the alternating sums involving
S^{o}[n,k]
and
S_{B}^{o}[n,k]
using sign-reversing involutions. We also showed how these equations were special cases of the symmetric function function identity in
2.2
, where that result was demonstrated by algebraic manipulation. Can
2.2
itself be proved by sign-reversing involution?

7.5
Log concavity

Partially order
{\mathbb{R}}[{\bf x}]
by letting
f({\bf x})\leq g({\bf x})
if
g({\bf x})-f({\bf x})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}[{\bf x}]
where
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
is the nonnegative reals. A sequence of polynomials
(f_{k}({\bf x}))_{k\geq 0}=f_{0}({\bf x}),f_{1}({\bf x}),f_{2}({\bf x}),\ldots
is said to be
{\bf x}
-log-concave
if

f_{k}({\bf x})^{2}\geq f_{k-1}({\bf x})f_{k+1}({\bf x})

for all
k>0
. Call the sequence
strongly
{\bf x}
-log-concave
if

f_{k}({\bf x})f_{l}({\bf x})\geq f_{k-1}({\bf x})f_{l+1}({\bf x})

for all
l\geq k>0
. Clearly strong
{\bf x}
-log concavity implies
{\bf x}
-log concavity. The converse is not true in general, although it is well known that it does hold if the polynomials are all positive constants. And in the case of a sequence of constants we say that it is just

log-concave
.

The following is a corollary of Theorem 2.6 in
[
Sag92
]
.

Theorem 7.2
.

If the sequence
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},\dots
is strongly
{\bf x}
-log-concave then so are the following sequences

(e_{k}(n))_{n\geq 0}\quad\mbox{and}\quad(h_{k}(n))_{n\geq 0}

where
k
is fixed, as well as

(e_{k-j}(n+j))_{j\geq 0}\quad\mbox{and}\quad(e_{k-j}(n+j))_{j\geq 0}

where both
k
and
n
are fixed. ∎

All the definitions in the previous paragraph apply when there is a single variable
q
.
It is easy to see that the sequence
[1]_{q},[2]_{q},[3]_{q},\ldots
is strongly
q
-log-concave. So any subsequence will be as well. Combining this fact, the previous theorem, and parts (a) and (b) of
2.1
give the following result.

Corollary 7.3
.

For fixed
n
, the sequences
(S_{B}[n,k])_{k\geq 0}
and
(c_{B}[n,k])_{k\geq 0}
are strongly
q
-log-concave. ∎

A condition that is implied by log concavity for positive integer sequences
a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
is
unimodality
which means that there is some index
m
such that

a_{0}\leq a_{1}\leq\ldots\leq a_{m}\geq a_{m+1}\geq\ldots\geq a_{n}.

So, one may ask if the sequences of coefficients of the polynomials
S[n,k]
,

S_{B}[n,k]
,
c[n,k]
, or
c_{B}[n,k]
are unimodal or even log-concave for each particular choice of
n,k
. Using a brute-force computation for
n,k\leq 50
, we have the following conjecture.

Conjecture 7.4
.

For each
n,k
, the coefficients of
S[n,k]
and
c[n,k]
are log-concave and positive, hence unimodal.

In type
B
, the coefficients are not necessarily even unimodal. The first counterexamples are as follows:

\displaystyle S_{B}[6,4]

\displaystyle=15+24q+34q^{2}+38q^{3}+43q^{4}+42q^{5}+43q^{6}+38q^{7}+35q^{8}

\displaystyle+26q^{9}+20q^{10}+14q^{11}+10q^{12}+6q^{13}+4q^{14}+2q^{15}+q^{16}

\displaystyle c_{B}[7,5]

\displaystyle=21+36q+51q^{2}+60q^{3}+70q^{4}+74q^{5}+79q^{6}+78q^{7}+79q^{8}

\displaystyle+74q^{9}+71q^{10}+62q^{11}+56q^{12}+44q^{13}+35q^{14}+26q^{15}+20q^{16}

\displaystyle+14q^{17}+10q^{18}+6q^{19}+4q^{20}+2q^{21}+q^{22}

A sequence
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},\ldots
is
parity-unimodal
if
a_{0},a_{2},a_{4},\ldots
and
a_{1},a_{3},a_{5},\ldots
are each unimodal. See the article of Billey, Konvalinka, and Swanson
[
BKS20
, §9]
for additional instances of this notion. Inspired by this definition, we say that such a sequence is
parity log-concave
if
a_{0},a_{2},a_{4},\ldots
and
a_{1},a_{3},a_{5},\ldots
are each log-concave and similarly for parity unimodal.

Conjecture 7.5
.

For each
n,k
, the coefficients of
S_{B}[n,k]
and
c_{B}[n,k]
are parity log-concave and positive, hence parity unimodal.

Another common property of a sequence
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}
is that it is
symmetric
meaning that
a_{k}=a_{n-k}
for all
k\in[n]
. From the examples above, it is clear that the Stirling polynomials do not have symmetric coefficients, but there is a related condition that they seem to enjoy.
A sequence is
bottom heavy
if
a_{k}\geq a_{n-k}
for
k<n/2
. See the article of McConville, Sagan, and Smyth
[
MSS21
]
as well as the references therein for more about the bottom heavy condition.

Conjecture 7.6
.

For each
n,k
, the coefficients of
S[n,k]
,
c[n,k]
,
S_{B}[n,k]
, and
c_{B}[n,k]
are all bottom heavy.

Both of the previous conjectures have been verified by computer for
n,k\leq 50
.
A property which implies both bottom heaviness and unimodality is being
bottom interlacing
which means that

a_{n}\leq a_{0}\leq a_{n-1}\leq a_{1}\leq\ldots\leq a_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}

where
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
is the floor function.
Again, see
[
MSS21
]
for more information about this property.
The coefficients of
c[n,k]
,
S[n,k]
,
c_{B}[n,k]
, and
S_{B}[n,k]
are not in general bottom interlacing. For example,

S[4,3]=3+2q+q^{2}

does not have this property.

7.6
Asymptotics

A sequence of real-valued random variables
\mathcal{X}_{1},\mathcal{X}_{2},\ldots
is
asymptotically normal
if the sequence of standardized random variables
\mathcal{X}_{1}^{*},\mathcal{X}_{2}^{*},\ldots
converges in distribution to the standard normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. More explicitly, this means that for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{\mathcal{X}_{n}-\mu_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}\leq t\right]=\int_{-\infty}^{t}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp(-x^{2}/2)\,dx.

In order for
\mathcal{X}_{i}^{*}
to be well-defined, we must assume
\mathcal{X}_{i}
does not have the degenerate distribution with variance
0
supported at a single point. This is only a minor inconvenience, since degenerate distributions are normal with variance
0
and are “morally” if not technically asymptotically normal.

We say that a sequence of non-zero polynomials
P_{1}(q),P_{2}(q),\ldots
with non-negative real coefficients
P_{n}(q)=\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{n}(k)q^{k}
is
asymptotically normal
if the sequence of random variables defined by
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{X}_{n}=k]=a_{n}(k)/P_{n}(1)
is asymptotically normal. Informally, plotting the list of coefficients of
P_{n}(q)
for large
n
must give a bell-shaped curve. See e.g.
[
BKS20
, §2.4, §4.1]
for further discussion and an example.

The polynomials
p_{n}(q)=\sum_{k\geq 0}c(n,k)q^{k}
and

P_{n}(q)=\sum_{k\geq 0}S(n,k)q^{k}
were shown to be asymptotically normal by Bender
[
Ben73
]
and by Harper
[
Har67
]
, respectively. It is natural to ask for asymptotic estimates of the coefficients of
q
-Stirling numbers analogous to these classic results, which we have been unable to find in the existing literature. We content ourselves with the following simple case whose proof is an easy application of Bender’s well-known result involving bivariate generating functions.

Theorem 7.7
.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. The coefficients of
S[n,k]
when
k\geq 2
, and of
S_{B}[n,k]
when
k\geq 1
are asymptotically normal as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

As we saw in
Section

2.1
, the generating functions are

\sum_{n\geq 0}S[n,k]x^{n}=\frac{x^{k}}{(1-[1]x)(1-[2]x)\cdots(1-[k]x)}

and

\sum_{n\geq 0}S_{B}[n,k]x^{n}=\frac{x^{k}}{(1-[1]x)(1-[3]x)\cdots(1-[2k+1]x)}.

Each is of the form
g(x,q)/P(x,q)
where

1.

P(x,q)
is a polynomial in
x
with coefficients continuous in
q
,

2.

P(x,1)
has a simple root at
r=1/k
or
r=1/(2k+1)
with all other roots having larger absolute value,

3.

g(x,q)=x^{k}
is entire, and

4.

g(r,1)\neq 0
.

The distributions are non-degenerate for
n
large when
k\geq 2
in type
A
and when
k\geq 1
in type
B
. The result follows from
[
Ben73
, Ex. 3.1, p.95]
(which inadvertently neglects the
\sigma\neq 0
condition).
∎

A direct analogue of
7.7
for the
q
-Stirling numbers of the first kind would require explicit expressions for
\sum_{n\geq 0}c[n,k]x^{n}/n!
or
\sum_{n\geq 0}c_{B}[n,k]x^{n}/n!
, which we do not have.
In a complementary direction, the distributions of the coefficients of
S[n,n-k]
,
S_{B}[n,n-k]
,
c[n,n-k]
, and
c_{B}[n,n-k]
for fixed
k
as
n\to\infty
appear to be non-normal, e.g. when
k=1
the limiting standardized disribution in each case is the triangle with density
(2\sqrt{2}-x)/9
for
-\sqrt{2}\leq x\leq 2\sqrt{2}
.

Based on computational data, the coefficients of the
q
-Stirling numbers of both kinds and in both types all appear to be “generically asymptotically normal.” The following conjecture is one way to make this intuition precise.

Conjecture 7.8
.

Suppose
n,k\to\infty
in such a way that
k/n\to\alpha
for some
0<\alpha<1
. Then the coefficients of
S[n,k]
,

S_{B}[n,k]
,
c[n,k]
, and
c_{B}[n,k]
are all asymptotically normal.

More strongly, the coefficients of
S[n,k],S_{B}[n,k],c[n,k],c_{B}[n,k]
appear to tend towards a “limit shape.” See
Figure

5
for an example with
S[n,k]
.
Note that the slices for fixed
k
appear to be parabolic near their maximum, consistent with asymptotic normality, at least for
k
not close to
0
or
n
.
It would be interesting to find the limit shape precisely in each case. It would also be very interesting to develop tools for proving limit shapes of the coefficients arising from recursions similar to (
2
), perhaps by exploiting their

-difference equations.
