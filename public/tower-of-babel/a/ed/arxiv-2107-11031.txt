Critical Galton-Watson processes with overlapping generations
Serik Sagitov

Abstract
A properly scaled critical Galton-Watson process converges to a continuous state critical branching process ξ ⁡ ( ⋅ ) \xi(\cdot) as the number of initial individuals tends to infinity. We extend this classical result by allowing for overlapping generations and considering a wide class of population counts. The main result of the paper establishes a convergence of the finite dimensional distributions for a scaled vector of multiple population counts. The set of the limiting distributions is conveniently represented in terms of integrals ( ∫ 0 y ξ ⁡ ( y − u ) ​ d ​ u γ , y ≥ 0 ) (\int_{0}^{y}\xi(y-u)du^{\gamma},y\geq 0) with a pertinent γ ≥ 0 \gamma\geq 0 .

Introduction

One of the basic stochastic population model of a self-reproducing system is build upon two assumptions

(A) different individuals live independently from each other according to the same individual life law described in (B),

(B) an individual dies at age one and at the moment of death gives birth to a random number
N
of offspring.

Within this model, the numbers of individuals
Z_{0},Z_{1},\ldots
, born at times
t=0,1,\ldots
, form a Markov chain, whose transition probabilities are fully described by the distribution of the offspring number
N
. The Markov chain
\{Z_{t},t\geq 0\}
is usually called a Galton-Watson process, or
GW-process
for short.
A GW-process is classified as subcritical, critical, or supercritical, depending on whether the mean offspring number
\mathrm{E}(N)
is less than, equal to, or larger than the critical value 1.

It is known, that in the critical case, with

\mathrm{E}(N)=1,\quad\mathrm{Var\hskip 0.56905pt}(N)=2b,\quad b<\infty,

(1.1)

the finite dimensional distributions (fdd’s) of a properly scaled GW-process converge

\{n^{-1}Z_{nu},\ u\geq 0\,|\,Z_{0}=n\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm fdd\ }}{{\longrightarrow}}\{\xi(u),\ u\geq 0\,|\,\xi(0)=1\},\ n\to\infty,

(1.2)

and the limiting fdd’s are represented by a continuous state branching process
\xi(\cdot)
, which is a continuous time Markov process with a transition law determined by

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\big(e^{-\lambda\xi(v+u)}\,|\,\xi(v)=x\big)=e^{-\frac{\lambda x}{1+\lambda bu}}\,,\quad v,u,x,\lambda\geq 0.

(1.3)

Note how the parameter
b
acts as a time scale: the larger is the variance of
N
, the faster is changing the population size.

In this paper, we study
\{Z(t),t\geq 0\}
, a Galton-Watson process with overlapping generations, or
GWO-process
for short, where
Z(t)
is the number of individuals alive at time
t
in a reproduction system satisfying the following two assumptions

(A
∗
) different individuals live independently from each other according to the same individual life law described in (B
∗
),

(B
∗
) an individual lives
L
units of time and gives
N
births at random ages
\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{N}
, satisfying

1\leq\tau_{1}\leq\ldots\leq\tau_{N}\leq L.

(1.4)

Assumption (B
∗
) allows for overlapping generations, when mothers may coexist with their daughters. We focus on the critical case (
1.1
) and aim at an extension of (
1.2
) to the GWO-processes.

The process
\{Z(t),t\geq 0\}
, being non-Markov in general, is studied with help of an associated renewal process, introduced in Section
2
. The mean inter-arrival time

\displaystyle a:=\mathrm{E}(\tau_{1}+\ldots+\tau_{N})

(1.5)

of this renewal process gives us the average generation length.
It is important to
distinguish between the average generation length
a
, which in this paper will be assumed finite, and the average life length

\mu:=\mathrm{E}(L)
, allowed to be infinite.

With a more sophisticated reproduction mechanism (
1.4
), there are many interesting population counts to study, alongside the number of newborns
Z_{t}
and the number of individual alive
Z(t)
at the time
t
. (For GW-processes,
a=1
and
Z(t)
equals
Z_{t}
, since all alive individuals are newborn.) An interesting case of population counts is treated by Theorem
4
dealing with decomposable multitype GW-processes. Theorem
4
is obtained as an application of the main results of the paper, Theorems
1
,
2
,
3
, stated and proven in Section
5
. The following three statements are straightforward corollaries of our Theorems
1
,
2
, and
3
respectively. In these theorems, it is always assume that the GWO-process stems from a large number
Z_{0}=n
of progenitors born at time zero.

Corollary 1
.

Consider a GWO-process satisfying (
1.1
) and
a<\infty
. If
\mu<\infty
, then

\{n^{-1}Z(nu),\ u>0\,|\,Z_{0}=n\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm fdd\ }}{{\longrightarrow}}\{\mu a^{-1}\xi(ua^{-1}),\ u>0\,|\,\xi(0)=1\},\quad n\to\infty.

Corollary 2
.

Consider a GWO-process satisfying (
1.1
) and
a<\infty
. If
\mu=\infty
, and for some slowly varying function at infinity

\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
,

\sum\nolimits_{j=0}^{t}\mathrm{P}(L>j)=t^{\gamma}\mathcal{L}(t),\quad 0\leq\gamma\leq 1,\quad t\to\infty,

(1.6)

then, as

n\to\infty
,

\{n^{-1-\gamma}\mathcal{L}^{-1}(n)Z(nu),\ u>0\,|\,Z_{0}=n\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm fdd\ }}{{\longrightarrow}}\{a^{\gamma-1}\xi_{\gamma}(ua^{-1}),\ u>0\,|\,\xi(0)=1\}.

Corollary 3
.

Consider a GWO-process satisfying (
1.1
),
a<\infty
, and (
1.6
). Then, as
n\to\infty
,

\{(n^{-1-\gamma}\mathcal{L}^{-1}(n)Z(nu),n^{-1}Z_{nu}),\ u>0\,|\,Z_{0}=n\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm fdd\ }}{{\longrightarrow}}\{(a^{\gamma-1}\xi_{\gamma}(ua^{-1}),a^{-1}\xi(ua^{-1})),\ u>0\,|\,\xi(0)=1\}.

Notice that condition (
1.6
) holds even in the case
\mu<\infty
, with
\gamma=0
and
\mathcal{L}(t)\to\mu
as
t\to\infty
. The family of processes
\{\xi_{\gamma}(\cdot)\}_{\gamma\geq 0}
emerging in our limit theorems can be expressed in the integral form

\displaystyle\xi_{0}(u):=\xi(u),\text{ for }\gamma=0,\text{ and }\xi_{\gamma}(u):=\int_{0}^{u}\xi(u-v)dv^{\gamma}\text{ for }\gamma>0,\quad u\geq 0,

(1.7)

which is treated as a convenient representation of the limiting fdd’s, see Section
4
.

2
Population counts

The number of individuals alive at time
t
can be counted as the sum of individual scores

Z(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{t}\sum_{k=1}^{Z_{j}}1_{\{j\leq t<j+L_{jk}\}}=\sum_{j=0}^{t}\sum_{k=1}^{Z_{j}}\chi_{jk}(t-j),

where
L_{jk}
is the life length of an individual born at time
j
, and
\chi_{jk}(t)=1_{\{0\leq t<L_{jk}\}}
is its individual score. In this case, the individual score is 1, if the individual is alive at time
t
, and 0 otherwise. This representation leads to the next definition of a population count.

Definition 2.1
.

For a progenitor of the GWO-process, define its individual score as a vector
(\chi(t))_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
with non-negative, possibly dependent components, such that
\chi(t)=0
for all
t<0
. This random vector is allowed to depend on the individual characteristics (
1.4
), but it is assumed to be independent from such characteristics of other individuals,

Define a population count
X(t)=X^{[\chi]}(t)
as the sum of time shifted individual scores

X(t):=\sum_{j=0}^{t}\sum_{k=1}^{Z_{j}}\chi_{jk}(t-j),\quad t\in\mathbb{Z},

(2.1)

assuming that the individual scores
(\chi_{jk}(t))_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
are
independent copies of
(\chi(t))_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
.

2.1
The litter sizes

In terms of (
1.4
), the litter sizes of a generic individual are defined by

\nu(t):=\sum_{j=1}^{N}1_{\{\tau_{j}=t\}}
,
t\geq 1
, so that
\nu(1)+\ldots+\nu(L)=N
. On the other hand, given a random infinite dimensional vector

(L,\nu(1),\nu(2),\ldots),\quad L\geq 1,\ \nu(t)\geq 0,\ t\geq 1,

(2.2)

where
\nu(t)
is treated the litter size at age
t
for an individual with the life length
L
, the consecutive ages at childbearing can be found as

\tau_{j}=\sum\nolimits_{t=1}^{L}t1_{\{N(t-1)<\tau_{j}\leq N(t)\}},\quad N(t):=(\nu(1)+\ldots+\nu(t))1_{\{L\geq t\}},

where
N(t)
is the number of daughters produced by a mother of age
t
.

In the critical case, the probabilities

A(t):=\mathrm{E}(\nu(t)1_{\{L\geq t\}}),\quad t\geq 1,

sum up to one, since

\sum_{t\geq 1}A(t)=\mathrm{E}(\nu(1)+\ldots+\nu(L))=\mathrm{E}(N)=1.

A renewal process with inter-arrival times having distribution
A(1),(A(2),\ldots
plays a crucial role in the analysis of the critical GWO-processes. Observe that the corresponding mean inter-arrival time is indeed given by (
1.5
):

\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}tA(t)=\mathrm{E}\Big(\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}t\nu(t)1_{\{L\geq t\}}\Big)=\mathrm{E}\Big(\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}t\sum_{j=1}^{N}1_{\{\tau_{j}=t\}}\Big)=\mathrm{E}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}t1_{\{\tau_{j}=t\}}\Big)=\mathrm{E}(\tau_{1}+\ldots+\tau_{N})=a.

2.2
Associated renewal process

In the GWO setting with
Z_{0}=1
, the process
Z_{t}
conditioned on
\{N(t)=k\}
, can be viewed as the sum of
k
independent daughter copies

Z_{t}=Z^{(1)}_{t-\tau_{j}}+\ldots+Z^{(k)}_{t-\tau_{k}}
.
This branching property implies that the expected number of newborns
U(t):=\mathrm{E}_{1}(Z_{t})
satisfies a recursive relation

U(t)=\mathrm{E}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{N(t)}U(t-\tau_{j})\Big)=\mathrm{E}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{t}U(t-k)\nu(k)1_{\{L\geq k\}}\Big)=U*A(t),\quad t\geq 1,

where the
*
symbol stands for a discrete convolution

A_{1}*A_{2}(t):=\sum\nolimits_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}A_{1}(t-j)A_{2}(j),\quad t\in\mathbb{Z}.

Resolving the obtained recursion
U(t)=1_{\{t=0\}}+U*A(t),

we find a familiar expression for the renewal function

U(t)=1_{\{t=0\}}+\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{t}A^{*k}(t),\quad A^{*1}(t):=A(t),\ \ A^{*(k+1)}(t):=A^{*k}*A(t),

(2.3)

so that by the elementary renewal theorem,

U(t)\to 1/a,\quad t\to\infty.

(2.4)

This says that in the long run, the underlying reproduction process produces one birth per
a
units of time. In this sense,
a
can be treated as the average generation length.

Later on, we will need the following facts concerning the distribution of
W_{t}
, the waiting time to the next renewal event

R_{t}(j):=\mathrm{P}(W_{t}=j),\quad j\geq 1,\ t\geq 0.

These probabilities satisfy the renewal equation

R_{t}(j)=A(t+j)+R_{t}*A(t),

which yields

R_{t}(j)=\sum\nolimits_{k=0}^{t}A(t+j-k)U(k),\ j\geq 1,\ t\geq 0.

(2.5)

By the key renewal theorem, there exists a stable distribution of the residual time
W_{t}
, in that

R_{t}(j)\to R(j),\quad t\to\infty,\quad R(j):=a^{-1}\sum\nolimits_{k=j}^{\infty}A(k),\quad j\geq 1.

(2.6)

Lemma 2.2
.

Assume (
1.1
),
a<\infty
, and suppose a family of non-negative functions
r^{(n)}(t)
is such that

\sup_{n\geq 1,t\geq 1}r^{(n)}(t)<\infty,\qquad r^{(n)}(ny)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle y\,}}{{\Rightarrow}}r(y),\quad n\to\infty.

If
r(y)\to r(0)
as
y\to 0
, then

\sum\nolimits_{t=1}^{\infty}r^{(n)}(t)R_{ny}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle y}}{{\to}}r(0),\ n\to\infty.

Proof.

Observe that

\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}r^{(n)}(t)R_{ny}(t)-r(0)=\sum_{t=1}^{t_{0}}(r^{(n)}(t)-r(0))R_{ny}(t)+\sum_{t=t_{0}+1}^{\infty}(r^{(n)}(t)-r(0))R_{ny}(t)

for any
t_{0}>0
. From

\sum_{t=1}^{t_{0}}(r^{(n)}(t)-r(0))R_{ny}(t)=\sum_{t=1}^{t_{0}}(r^{(n)}(t)-r(tn^{-1}))R_{ny}(t)+\sum_{t=1}^{t_{0}}(r(tn^{-1})-r(0))R_{ny}(t),

we deduce

\sum\nolimits_{t=1}^{t_{0}}(r^{(n)}(t)-r(0))R_{ny}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle y}}{{\to}}0,\ n\to\infty,

using the assumptions on
r^{(n)}(\cdot)
and
r(\cdot)
. It remains to notice that

\sum_{t=t_{0}+1}^{\infty}|r^{(n)}(t)-r(0)|R_{ny}(t)\leq C\sum_{t=t_{0}+1}^{\infty}R_{ny}(t),

and

\sum_{t=t_{0}+1}^{\infty}R_{ny}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle y}}{{\to}}\sum_{t=t_{0}+1}^{\infty}R(t)\to 0
as first
t\to\infty
and then
t_{0}\to\infty
.

2.3
Expected population counts

If
Z_{0}=1
, then
X(t)
, defined by (
2.1
), can be represented as

X(t)=\chi(t)+\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{N(t)}X^{(j)}(t-\tau_{j})

(2.7)

in terms of the independent daughter processes
X^{(j)}(\cdot)
.
Taking expectations,
we arrive at a recursion

M(t)=m(t)+\mathrm{E}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{N(t)}M(t-\tau_{j})\Big)=m(t)+\sum_{j=1}^{t}M(t-j)A(j),

where

M(t):=\mathrm{E}_{1}(X(t))
,
m(t):=\mathrm{E}(\chi(t))
.
This renewal equation

M(t)=m(t)+M*A(t)
yields

M(t)=m*U(t)=\sum\nolimits_{j=0}^{t}m(t-j)U(j),

and applying the key renewal theorem, we conclude

\mathrm{E}_{1}(X(t))\to m_{\chi},\quad t\to\infty,\quad m_{\chi}:=a^{-1}\sum\nolimits_{t=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{E}(\chi(t)).

(2.8)

The obtained parameter
m_{\chi}
can be viewed as the average
\chi
-score for the population with overlapping generations.
The next result goes further than (
2.8
) by giving a useful asymptotical relation in the case
m_{\chi}=\infty
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Consider a critical GWO-process with
a<\infty
. If for some slowly varying at infinity function
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
,

\sum\nolimits_{j=0}^{t}\mathrm{E}(\chi(j))=t^{\gamma}\mathcal{L}(t),\ t\to\infty,\ 0\leq\gamma<\infty,

(2.9)

then

\mathrm{E}_{1}(X(t))\sim a^{-1}t^{\gamma}\mathcal{L}(t)
as
t\to\infty
.

Proof.

We have to show that (
2.9
) implies

M(t)-a^{-1}M_{t}=o(M_{t})
as
t\to\infty,

where
M_{t}:=\sum_{j=0}^{t}m(j)
.
To this end, observe that the difference

\displaystyle M(t)-a^{-1}\sum_{j=0}^{t}m(t-j)=\sum_{j=0}^{t}m(t-j)(U(j)-a^{-1})

is estimated from above by

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{t}m(t-j)\,|\,U(j)-a^{-1}\,|\,

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{j=0}^{t_{\epsilon}-1}m(t-j)+\epsilon\sum_{j=t_{\epsilon}}^{t}m(t-j)\leq C(M_{t}-M_{t-t_{\epsilon}})+\epsilon M_{t},\quad t\geq t_{\epsilon},

for an arbitrarily small
\epsilon>0
and some finite constants
C
,
t_{\epsilon}
. It remains to apply the property of the regularly varying function
M_{t}
, saying that
M_{t}-M_{t-c}=o(M_{t})
as
t\to\infty
for any fixed
c\geq 0
.

Turning to
X(t)=Z(t)
, the number of individuals alive at time
t
, observe that with
\chi(t)=1_{\{0\leq t<L\}}
,

\sum\nolimits_{t\geq 0}\mathrm{E}(\chi(t))=\sum\nolimits_{t\geq 0}\mathrm{P}(L>t)=\mu.

Therefore, given
\mathrm{E}(N)=1
,

\mathrm{E}_{1}(Z(t))\to\mu a^{-1},\quad t\to\infty.

In this case, the parameter
m_{\chi}=\mu a^{-1}
can be treated as the degree of generation overlap. For example,
m_{\chi}=2
means that on average, the life length
L
covers two generation lengths.

3
Branching renewal equations

A useful extension of Definition
2.1
broadens the range of individual scores by replacing (
2.1
) with

X(t):=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{Z_{j}}\chi_{jk}(t-j),\quad t\in\mathbb{Z}.

(3.1)

Relation (
3.1
) takes into account even those individuals who are born after time
t
, allowing
\chi(t)>0
for
t<0
. In this paper, we refer to this extension only to deal with the finite dimensional distributions of the population counts defined by (
2.1
), see Lemma
3.2
below.

Definition 3.1
.

For the population count
X(t)=X^{[\chi]}(t)
given by (
3.1
), define a log-Laplace transform
\Lambda(t)=\Lambda^{[\chi]}(t)
via

e^{-\Lambda(t)}:=\mathrm{E}_{1}(e^{-X(t)}),\quad t\in\mathbb{Z}.

The purpose of this section is to introduce a branching renewal equation for
\Lambda(\cdot)
and establish Proposition
3.5
, which will play a key role in the proofs of the main results of this paper.

Lemma 3.2
.

For a given vector
(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p})
with non-negative integer components, consider the log-Laplace transform

\Lambda(t)=-\ln\mathrm{E}_{1}\Big(\exp\Big\{-\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{i}X(t_{i}+t)\Big\}\Big)

of the
p
-dimensional distribution of the population sum
X(\cdot)
defined by (
2.1
).
Then, in accordance with Definition
3.1
,

\Lambda(t)=\Lambda^{[\psi]}(t),\qquad\psi(t):=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{i}\chi(t_{i}+t),\quad t\in\mathbb{Z}.

Proof.

It suffices to observe that

\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{i}X(t_{i}+t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{X}}}{{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sum_{j=0}^{t}\sum_{k=1}^{Z_{j}}\lambda_{i}\chi_{jk}(t_{i}+t-j)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{Z_{j}}\psi_{jk}(t-j)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{X+}}}{{=}}X^{[\psi]}(t).

3.1
Derivation of the branching renewal equation

Here we show that Definition
3.1
leads to what we call a branching renewal equation:

\Lambda(t)=B(t)-\Psi[\Lambda]*U(t),\quad t\geq 0,

(3.2)

where the operator

\Psi[f](t):=\mathrm{E}\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{L}e^{-\nu(j)f(t-j)}\Big)-\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}e^{-f(t-j)}A(j),\quad t\geq 0

(3.3)

is defined on the set of non-negative sequences
(f(t))_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
, see more on it in Section
3.2
. The convolution term
\Psi[\Lambda]*U(t)
represents the non-linear part of the branching renewal equation. A seemingly free term
B(\cdot)
of the equation (
3.2
) is a non-negative function specified below by (
3.4
) and (
3.6
). It also depends on the function
\Lambda(\cdot)
in a non-linear way, however, asymptotically it acts as a truly free term.

The derivation of (
3.2
) is based on the following extended version of decomposition (
2.7
)

X(t)=\chi(t)+\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{N}X^{(j)}(t-\tau_{j}),\quad t\in\mathbb{Z},

where
X^{(j)}(\cdot)
are independent daughter copies of
(X(\cdot)|Z_{0}=1)
.
It entails

e^{\chi(t)-X(t)}=\prod_{j=1}^{N}e^{-X^{(j)}(t-\tau_{j})}
,
and taking expectations, we obtain

\mathrm{E}_{1}(e^{\chi(t)-X(t)})=\mathrm{E}(e^{-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Lambda(t-\tau_{j})})=\mathrm{E}(e^{-\sum_{j=1}^{L}\nu(j)\Lambda(t-j)}).

On the other hand (recall
e_{1}^{x}:=1-e^{-x}
),

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{1}(e^{\chi(t)-X(t)})-e^{-\Lambda(t)}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}_{1}(e^{\chi(t)-X(t)}-e^{-X(t)})=\mathrm{E}_{1}\Big(e_{1}^{\chi(t)}e^{\chi(t)-X(t)}\Big).

Denoting the last expectation
D(t)
, we can write

D(t)=\mathrm{E}\Big(e_{1}^{\chi(t)}e^{-\sum_{j=1}^{L}\nu(j)\Lambda(t-j)}\Big),

(3.4)

due to independence between the progenitor score
\chi(t)
and the GWO-processes stemming from progenitor’s daughters.
Combing the previous relations, we find

3.2
Laplace transform of the reproduction law

The Laplace transform of the reproduction law

is a positive functional defined on the set of non-negative sequences
(f(t))_{t\geq 1}
. The higher than first moments of the joint distribution of

\sum_{i=1}^{k}(a_{i}-b_{j})-\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}+\prod_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(a_{i}-b_{j})(1-a_{1}\ldots a_{j-1}b_{j+1}\ldots b_{k}).

\displaystyle\Psi(f_{t})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{Psif})}}{{=}}\mathrm{E}\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{N}e^{-f_{t}(\tau_{j})}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}e^{-f_{t}(\tau_{j})}\Big)=\mathrm{E}\Big(\prod_{k=1}^{L}e^{-f_{t}(k)\nu(k)}-\sum_{k=1}^{L}e^{-f_{t}(k)}\nu(k)\Big)

5.5
Proof of Theorem
4

Adapting the setting of Theorem
4
to Theorem
3
, we treat the process
(Z_{t}^{1},\ldots,Z_{t}^{q})
as a vector of population counts for a single type GW-process. This is achieved by focussing on the type 1 individuals and introducing
q
individual scores for a generic individual of type 1 born at time 0 by setting

\chi_{1}(t):=1_{\{t=0\}}
,

\chi_{j}(t):=
the number of descendants of the generic individual, which (a) have no other intermediate ancestors of type 1, (b) are born at time
t
, and (c) have type
i
,

for
j=2,\ldots,q
. Having this, our task is to check that conditions (
2.9
) and (
5.2
) hold with

\gamma:=j-1
and
\mathcal{L}(t):=\tfrac{1}{(j-1)!}m_{1,2}\cdots m_{j-1,j}
for all
t\geq 1
and
j=2,\ldots,q
.

To check condition (
2.9
) with
\chi(\cdot):=\chi_{j}(\cdot)
for a given
j=2,\ldots,q
, we use a representation

\chi_{j}(t+1)=\sum_{i=2}^{j}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{1i}}Z^{i}_{t}(j,k),

(5.34)

where
Z^{i}_{t}(j,k)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm d}}{{=}}Z^{i}_{t}(j)
stands for the number of type
j
individuals born at time
t+1
and descending from a type
i
individual born at time 1.
This gives

\mathrm{E}(\chi_{j}(t+1))=\sum\nolimits_{i=2}^{j}m_{1j}M_{ij}(t),\quad M_{ij}(t):=\mathrm{E}(Z^{i}_{t}(j)|Z^{i}_{0}=1).

Furthermore, due to the decomposable branching property, we have

Z^{i}_{t+1}(j)=\sum\nolimits_{l=i}^{j}\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{N_{il}}Z^{l}_{t}(j,k),

(5.35)

implying the recursion

M_{ij}(t+1)=\sum\nolimits_{l=i}^{j}m_{il}M_{lj}(t)=M_{ij}(t)+\sum\nolimits_{l=i+1}^{j-1}m_{il}M_{lj}(t)+m_{ij}.

Putting here
j=i+1
, we get
M_{i,i+1}(t)=tm_{i,i+1}
. From

M_{i,i+2}(t+1)=M_{i,i+2}(t)+m_{i,i+1}M_{i+1,i+2}(t)+m_{i,i+2}=M_{i,i+2}(t)+tm_{i,i+1}m_{i+1,i+2}+m_{i,i+2},

we find

M_{i,i+2}(t)\sim\tfrac{1}{2}t^{2}m_{i,i+1}m_{i+1,i+2}
as
t\to\infty.

Thus, by iteration, we derive

M_{ij}(t)\sim\tfrac{1}{(i-j)!}t^{j-i}m_{i,i+1}\cdots m_{j-1,j},\quad t\to\infty,

which allows us to conclude that condition (
2.9
) holds in the desired form, because

\mathrm{E}(\chi_{j}(t))\sim\tfrac{1}{(j-2)!}t^{j-2}m_{1,2}\cdots m_{j-1,j},\quad t\to\infty.

Finally, to verify condition (
5.2
) with
\gamma=j-1
, it suffices to show that

\mathrm{E}(\chi_{j}^{2}(t))\leq Ct^{2j-3},\quad j=2,\ldots,q,

(5.36)

using the following corollary of (
5.34
)

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}(\chi_{j}^{2}(t+1))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}\Big(\Big(\sum_{i=2}^{j}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{1i}}Z^{i}_{t}(j,k)\Big)^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=2}^{j}m_{1i}V_{ij}(t)+2\sum_{i=2}^{j}\mathrm{E}(N_{1i}(N_{1i}-1))M^{2}_{ij}(t)+2\sum_{2\leq i<l\leq j}\mathrm{E}(N_{1i}N_{1l})M_{ij}(t)M_{lj}(t),

where
V_{ij}(t):=\mathrm{E}((Z^{i}_{t}(j))^{2}|Z^{i}_{0}=1)
. From here, relation (
5.36
) is obtained from

\sum_{i=2}^{j}\mathrm{E}(N_{1i}(N_{1i}-1))M^{2}_{ij}(t)+\sum_{2\leq i<l\leq j}\mathrm{E}(N_{1i}N_{1l})M_{ij}(t)M_{lj}(t))\leq C_{1}\sum_{2\leq i\leq l\leq j}t^{j-i}t^{j-l}\leq C_{2}t^{2j-4}

and the upper bound
V_{ij}(t)\leq Ct^{2j-2i+1}
, derived next.
Using (
5.35
) and applying similar estimates, we find

V_{ij}(t+1)=\mathrm{E}\Big(\Big(\sum_{l=i}^{j}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{il}}Z^{l}_{t}(j,k)\Big)^{2}\Big)\leq\sum_{l=i}^{j}m_{il}V_{lj}(t)+Ct^{2j-2i}.

In particular,
V_{jj}(t+1)\leq V_{jj}(t)+C

implies
V_{jj}(t)\leq Ct
. This in turn, gives

V_{j-1,j}(t+1)\leq V_{j-1,j}(t)+C_{1}t+C_{2}t^{2},

and
V_{j-1,j}(t)\leq Ct^{3}
. Reiterating this argument, we find
V_{ij}(t)\leq Ct^{2j-2i+1}
, which ends the proof of (
5.36
) and Theorem
4
as a whole.
