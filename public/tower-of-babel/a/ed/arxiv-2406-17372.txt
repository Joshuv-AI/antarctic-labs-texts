Non-commutative error correcting codes and proper subgroup testing
Michael Chapman, Irit Dinur, Alexander Lubotzky

Abstract
. Property testing has been a major area of research in computer science in the last three decades. By property testing we refer to an ensemble of problems, results and algorithms which enable to deduce global information about some data by only reading small random parts of it. In recent years, this theory found its way into group theory (see [ 5 , 10 , 8 ] and the references therein), mainly via group stability . In this paper, we study the following problem: Devise a randomized algorithm that given a subgroup H H of G G , decides whether H H is the whole group or a proper subgroup, by checking whether a single (random) element of G G is in H H . The search for such an algorithm boils down to the following purely group theoretic problem: For G G of rank k k , find a small as possible test subset A ⊆ G A\subseteq G such that for every proper subgroup H H , | H ∩ A | ≤ ( 1 − δ ) ​ | A | |H\cap A|\leq(1-\delta)|A| for some absolute constant δ > 0 \delta>0 , which we call the detection probability of A A . It turns out that the search for sets A A of size linear in k k and constant detection probability is a non-commutative analogue of the classical search for families of good error correcting codes. This paper is devoted to proving that such test subsets exist , which implies good universal error correcting codes exist — providing a far reaching generalization of the classical result of Shannon [ 32 ] . In addition, we study this problem in certain subclasses of groups — such as abelian, nilpotent, and finite solvable groups — providing different constructions of test subsets for these subclasses with various qualities. Finally, this generalized theory of non-commutative error correcting codes suggests a plethora of interesting problems and research directions.

Let

Fact 2.1
.

Let

be a group and

(1)

The detection probability

(2)

If

is an epimporphism, then

\delta(\pi(A);G^{\prime})\geq\delta(A;G).

(3)

An epimporphism

\delta(\pi(A);G^{\prime})=\delta(A;G)
.

Proof sketch.

(1)

Note that
\langle A\rangle
is a subgroup of
G
.

(2)

By the correspondence theorem (fourth isomorphism theorem), for every
H^{\prime}\lneq G^{\prime}
there is a
\ker\pi\leq H\lneq G
such that
\pi(H)=H^{\prime}
. Furthermore, as we use multi-sets,

and
|A\cap H|=|\pi(A\cap H)|\leq|\pi(A)\cap H^{\prime}|
. Since this goes over all subgroups of

(but not necessarily all subgroups of

(3)

Note again by the correspondence theorem, that if

is a maximal subgroup, and
\ker\pi\subseteq M
, then
\pi(M)\lneq G^{\prime}
. Also, as
\ker\pi\subseteq M

\pi(A\cap M)=\pi(A)\cap\pi(M)
. This provides the reverse inequality.

Let

Let

Define a bilinear

\displaystyle\forall v,w

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{k}\ \colon\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle v,w}}\right\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}v_{i}w_{i}.

Given
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{k},
define the functional
\varphi_{\alpha}\colon\mathbb{F}_{p}^{k}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}

by
\varphi_{\alpha}(v)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\alpha,v}}\right\rangle,
and
let
\alpha^{\perp}={\rm ker}\varphi_{\alpha}.
Also, for every functional

\varphi\colon\mathbb{F}_{p}^{k}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}
, there exists an

\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{k}
such that
\varphi=\varphi_{\alpha}.

Since
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{{\texttt{enc}}_{i}}}\right\}_{i\in[n]}
are functionals,
there exists a collection
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{v_{i}}}\right\}_{i\in n}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}^{k}

such that
{\texttt{enc}}_{i}=\varphi_{v_{i}}
.

Assume
A\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{k}
is the set guaranteed by Theorem
1.1
, where
\mathcal{F}_{k}
is the free group on basis
B=\{x_{1},...,x_{k}\}
, and recall that
\delta_{0}
is the lower bound on its detection probability. By applying on
\mathcal{F}_{k}
abelianization mod
p
,
\Phi_{p}\colon\mathcal{F}_{k}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}^{k}
, the (multi-)set
Z=\Phi(A)
satisfies both
|Z|=|A|\leq C_{0}\cdot k
and
\delta(Z;\mathbb{F}_{p}^{k})\geq\delta_{0}
(by Fact
2.1
).
As we remarked when defining the detection probability (
1.1
), we may assume the proper linear subspace
H
is maximal, i.e.,
it is of co-dimension
1
. Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{k}
be a vector such that
H=\alpha^{\perp}
.
I.e., if
h
is the indicator function of
H
, then
h(v)=1
if and only if
\langle\alpha,v\rangle=0
.
Therefore, the fact the detection probability of
Z
is bounded from below by
\delta_{0}
is equivalent to the condition

\forall\vec{0}\neq\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{k}\ \colon\ \ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{v\in Z\mid\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\alpha,v}}\right\rangle\neq 0}}\right\}}}\right|\geq\delta_{0}|Z|.

Let
|Z|=n
,
\texttt{enc}\colon\mathbb{F}_{p}^{k}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
be
\texttt{enc}(\alpha)=(\langle\alpha,v\rangle)_{v\in Z}
, and
\mathcal{C}=\textrm{Im}(\texttt{enc})
. Then
\mathcal{C}
is an
[n,k,\delta_{0}n]_{p}
-code. Thus:

Corollary 2.3
.

A collection of sets as in Theorem
1.1
induces, in a uniform way, a collection of good codes on
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{k}
for every
p
.

The observation of Corollary
2.3
is the key to most of our results — we use constructions and methods from the theory of error correcting codes as inspiration to try to resolve the opposite direction, i.e., to build
test subsets
out of codes, or phrased differently —
universal codes
using classical coding theory techniques.

If the above perspective on our problem as non-commutative error correction was not motivating enough, we provide the test case of
finite
abelian groups, where the connection between the problems is quite tight (Section
4
resolves the general infinite abelian groups case).

2.3.
Finite abelian groups

As we have shown above, if we want to find a subset
A
of
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{k}
of size
n
with detection probability
\delta>0
, then it is equivalent to finding an error correcting code
[n,k,\delta n]_{p}
. In particular, we can use the Gilbert–Varshamov bound, and find sets of size
n\approx\frac{k}{1-H_{p}(\delta)}
with detection probability
\delta>0
, where
H_{p}
is the
p
-ary entropy function
H_{p}(x)=x\log_{p}(p-1)-x\log_{p}x-(1-x)\log_{p}(1-x)
.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group. Every such group is isomorphic to a (finite) product of cyclic groups of prime power order
\nicefrac{{\mathbb{Z}}}{{p^{k}\mathbb{Z}}}
. The Frattini subgroup in this case is
\nicefrac{{p\mathbb{Z}}}{{p^{k}\mathbb{Z}}}
. Hence, by item
(3)
of Fact
2.1
, we can assume
G
is a (finite) product of finite vector spaces
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{r(G,p)}
.

Now, let
G
and
G^{\prime}
be two finite abelian groups such that
\gcd(|G|,|G^{\prime}|)=1
, and let
A=\{a_{1},...,a_{n}\}\subseteq G,B=\{b_{1},...,b_{n}\}\subseteq G^{\prime}
be ordered (multi-)subsets the same size with detection probability
\delta
. Let
C\subseteq G\times G^{\prime}
be the diagonal (multi-)subset associated with
A
and
B
,

\forall i\in[n]\ \colon\ \ c_{i}=(a_{i},b_{i})\in C.

Then,
C
has detection probability
\delta
. This is because for every maximal subgroup
H\leq G\times G^{\prime}
,
\nicefrac{{G\times G^{\prime}}}{{H}}
is a group of prime order, which is either co-prime to
|G|
or to
|G^{\prime}|
. Therefore, either
\{{\rm Id}\}\times G^{\prime}
or
G\times\{{\rm Id}\}
is contained in
H
. This in turn implies that either
\pi_{G}(H)
is a maximal subgroup of
G
, or that
\pi_{G^{\prime}}(H)
is a maximal subgroup of
G^{\prime}
, where
\pi_{G}
(respectively
\pi_{G^{\prime}}
) is the projection to the left (respectively right) coordinate. All in all, either
|C\cap H|=|A\cap\pi_{G}(H)|\leq(1-\delta)|A|=(1-\delta)|C|
, or
|C\cap H|=|B\cap\pi_{G^{\prime}}(H)|\leq(1-\delta)|B|=(1-\delta)|C|
.

Combining the above observations, we deduce a Gilbert–Varshamov type bound for finite abeilan groups:

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group. Modulo its Frattini subgroup,
G
is a product of vector spaces
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{r(G,p)}
. Then, for every
0<\delta<1-\frac{1}{\min\{p\mid r(G,p)\neq 0\}}
, we can find a subset of
G
of size (approximately)
\displaystyle{\max_{p\ \textrm{prime}}}\frac{r(G,p)}{1-H_{p}(\delta)}
and detection probability
\delta
.
Since the rank of
G
is
k=\max(r(G,p))
, and for every
0<\delta<\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
we have
H_{2}(\delta)\geq H_{p}(\delta)
,
G
has a subset of size (approximately)
\frac{k}{1-H_{2}(\delta)}
with detection probability
\delta
.

3.
Universal codes

As mentioned in Remark
1.9
in the Introduction, the following observation is repeatedly used in this section:

Observation 3.1
.

Let
B=\{x_{1},...,x_{t}\}
be a subset of
G
, and let
w=\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}x_{i_{j}}^{\varepsilon_{j}}
be a word in
B
, namely
i_{j}\in[t]
and
\varepsilon_{j}\in\{\pm 1\}
for every
1\leq j\leq\ell
. Let
H
be a subgroup of

. Assume there is an
1\leq r\leq\ell
such that
x_{i_{r}}\notin H
while
x_{i_{j}}\in H
for every
j\neq r
. Then
w\notin H
.

Remark 3.2
.

We often want to think of finite multi-sets
A
of
G
both as sets (which allows us to take intersections of them with other subsets) and with a certain order (which makes them into tuples). This creates situations where we use notations as
\vec{g}\cap H
for a tuple of elements from
G
and a subgroup
H
. By this we mean that we forget about the order of
\vec{g}
, which leaves us with a finite multi-set
A
of elements in
G
, and then the intersection is the multi-set of all elements from
A
that are also in
H
.

3.1.
An exponential relaxation.

At first glance, it may not be clear that there are finite sets of
\mathcal{F}_{k}
with detection probability bounded by
\delta>0
which is independent of
k
. It turns out that sets of size exponential in
k
suffice.

Theorem 3.3
.

There is a set
A\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{k}
of size
|A|=2^{k},
such that

\delta(A;\mathcal{F}_{k})\geq\frac{1}{2}
.

Remark 3.4
.

The following construction is a non-commutative version of the Hadamard code. Namely, if you run the sets constructed in the following proof through the process described in Section
2
, you get (classical) codes; choosing
p=2
will result with the Hadamard code.

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

Let
B=\{x_{1},...,x_{k}\}
be a basis for
\mathcal{F}_{k}
.
For every subset
S\subseteq B
, let
s
be its indicator
function, and let

(3.1)

x_{S}=x_{1}^{s(x_{1})}\cdot x_{2}^{s(x_{2})}\cdot...\cdot x_{k}^{s(x_{k})}.

Namely, for every subset
S
of
B
we take
x_{S}
to be the product
of the elements in
S
according to the order defined by their index.
Now, let
A=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x_{S}}}\right\}_{S\subseteq B}.
Clearly
|A|=2^{k}.

Let
H
be a proper subgroup of
\mathcal{F}_{k}
. Let
x_{i}

be the generator with the smallest index not belonging to
H
, namely

x_{i}\notin H
but
x_{j}\in H
for every
j<i
. For every subset
S

of
B
not containing
x_{i},
let
S^{\prime}=S\cup\{x_{i}\}
.
Then, at most one of
x_{S}
and
x_{S^{\prime}}
can be in
H
. This is
because
x_{S^{\prime}}x_{S}^{-1}
is a conjugate of
x_{i}\notin H
by
an element of
H
— the product
x_{1}^{s(x_{1})}\cdot...\cdot x_{i-1}^{s(x_{i-1})}

is in
H
regardless of what
S
is — and in particular cannot be
in
H
(this is a simple version of Observation
3.1
). Since
S\leftrightarrow S^{\prime}
is a perfect matching of the subsets of
B
, we deduce that
\frac{|A\cap H|}{2^{k}}\leq\frac{1}{2}
, and thus
\delta(A;\mathcal{F}_{k})\geq\frac{1}{2}
.
∎

.

Avi Wigderson has mentioned to us that the technique used in Theorem

— namely, ordering the generators and taking subwords in this order — was utilized in various works, and specifically in the following two influential papers by Babai–Szemerédi

3.2.
A randomized construction

In the theory of classical error correcting codes, one of the first observations is that random codes are good
12
12

12

This was mentioned both in the Introduction as Shannon’s theorem, as well as in Section
2.2
as the Gilbert–Varshamov
lower
bound.
. We spell out the main proof ideas of this fact for the case of
\mathbb{F}_{2}
: If we sample a vector in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
uniformly at random, it has a probability of
\frac{1}{2}
to be out of a given co-dimension
1
subspace
H\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
. Hence, by standard concentration of measure techniques (see Lemma
3.7
), if we sample
n
vectors from
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
, the probability that less than
\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}
of them are not in
H
decreases exponentially in
n
. Since there are only
2^{k}-1
different co-dimension
1
subspaces, one can apply a union bound to deduce that a collection of

random vectors will provide an encoding map
\texttt{enc}\colon\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, as in Section
2.2
, whose image is a code with normalized distance of at least
\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}
almost surely.

One can hope to mimic the above argument by sampling
O(k)
elements of the set
\widetilde{B}=\{x_{S}\}_{S\subseteq B}
from Theorem
3.3
to produce the required universal codes a la Definition
1.3
. The issue with this approach is in the last step of the analysis: While
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
has
2^{k}-1
maximal subgroups,
\mathcal{F}_{k}
has infinitely many. We overcome this problem by clustering subgroups of
\mathcal{F}_{k}
into finitely many bins, actually,
2^{k}-1
many bins. This clustering of subgroups is according to their

in the basis of the free group. The resulting clusters are amenable to a probabilistic argument, though not with respect to the uniform distribution over
\widetilde{B}
, as is explained soon.

Let us spell out the above plan with more details, beginning with our clustering mechanism. For every subgroup
H
and set
B=\{x_{1},...,x_{k}\}\subset G
, the
syndrome
of
H
in
B
is

(3.2)

Synd(H;B)=B-H=\{x_{i}\in B\mid x_{i}\notin H\}.

The syndrome with respect to the basis
B
of
\mathcal{F}_{k}
distributes its infinitely many subgroups into finitely many bins: For every
C\subseteq B
, let

(3.3)

\Sigma_{C}=\bigcup_{Synd(H^{\prime};B)=C}{H^{\prime}}.

Now, for every
H\lneq\mathcal{F}_{k}
, we have
Synd(H;B)\neq\emptyset
, and thus every proper subgroup is in some
\Sigma_{C}
for
\emptyset\neq C\subseteq B
. There are only

such non-trivial syndromes, and thus we are back to familiar grounds — if we are able to find an
A
such that for every
\emptyset\neq C\subseteq B
, a constant fraction of
A
avoids
\Sigma_{C}
, then we are done.

To that end, we need the following observation: As in (
3.1
), for every subset
S\subseteq B
with characteristic function
s
, let
x_{S}=\prod x_{i}^{s(x_{i})}\in\widetilde{B}
. Note that

(3.4)

|S\cap Synd(H;B)|=1\implies x_{S}\notin H,

which is another special case of Observation
3.1
.
Therefore, one seeks a sampling procedure on subsets
S
of
B
with a high chance that the resulting subset intersects a specific syndrome
C
exactly
once
, and thus
x_{S}\notin\Sigma_{C}
. This leads us to the final key observation:
Let
H
be a proper subgroup with syndrome
C
, and let
\sigma=|C|>0
. Let
0<p<1
. If we sample
S\subseteq B
such that each
x_{i}
is included in
S
with probability
p
independently of the others, then

(3.5)

\Pro[|S\cap C|=1]=\sigma p(1-p)^{\sigma-1}.

In particular, if
p=\frac{1}{\sigma}
, then
\sigma p(1-p)^{\sigma-1}\geq e^{-1}
. This calculation is actually quite robust, namely, even for
\frac{1}{2\sigma}\leq p\leq\frac{2}{\sigma}
we get a constant lower bound on
\Pro[|S\cap C|=1]
. Therefore, we sample in the following way:
For every
\ell\leq\log k
, we sample
O(k)
many subsets

of size

uniformly and independently. That way, for every
\emptyset\neq C\subseteq B
there exists an

such that a constant fraction of the sampled sets

of size
2^{\ell}
that were sampled satisfy

. Thus, the detection probability of this sampled set is
\Omega(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\log k}})
. This is the content of Theorem
3.8
.
But the goal is to have a constant detection probability. To resolve that, in Proposition
3.10
, we provide an amplification trick that replaces the guaranteed
A
with a not much larger

, with the benefit that

is
\Omega(1)
. This amplification trick is analogous to composing the random code defined in Theorem
3.8
with the Hadamard code of Theorem
3.3
. In Section
3.3
, we show that more iterations of code compositions lead to somewhat better parameters, from which we deduce Theorem
1.7
.

Remark 3.6
.

Our sampling scheme may resonate with a reader which is familiar with the proof of Bourgain’s embedding theorem

Let

Theorem 3.8
.

There is a set
A
in
\mathcal{F}_{k}
of size

,

(3.8)

\frac{|A\cap\Sigma_{C}|}{|A|}\leq 1-\frac{1}{12\log k}.

Remark 3.9
.

(1)

Since every

, (

\delta(A;\mathcal{F}_{k})\geq\frac{1}{12\log k}

(2)

The parameter

As

Proof of Theorem
3.8
.

The proof idea is as follows: We choose for every

a collection of
O(k)
random words from
\widetilde{B}=\{x_{S}\}_{S\subseteq B}

For every

of

independently as follows: Each

\begin{split}\Pro[x_{S(\ell,j)}\notin\Sigma_{C}]&\geq\Pro[|S(\ell,j)\cap C|=1]\\
&=\underbrace{\frac{\sigma}{2^{\ell}}}_{\geq\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\underbrace{1-\frac{1}{2^{\ell}}}_{\geq 1-\frac{1}{\sigma}}}}\right)^{\sigma-1}\\
&\geq\nicefrac{{1}}{{2e}}.\end{split}

Let

\Pro\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\exists\emptyset\neq C\subseteq B\colon|A_{\ell}\cap\Sigma_{C}|\geq\frac{11}{12}\cdot 432k}}\right]<2^{k}\cdot e^{-k}<1.

Hence, there is a choice of

\begin{split}\frac{|A\cap\Sigma_{C}|}{|A|}\leq 1-\frac{|A_{\ell}-\Sigma_{C}|}{432k\log k}\leq 1-\frac{1}{12\log k},\end{split}

(Amplification to constant by sub-sampling)

Proof.

We first spell out the main ideas: If one samples a set of size

3.3.
Iterative composition

We now suggest a more gradual amplification, which allows us to find smaller test subsets (which are not yet linear in
k
) with constant detection probability. The idea is similar to the code composition used in the proof of Proposition

5.
Profinite groups with polynomial maximal subgroup growth

Let us start with a few sentences about profinite groups, for more see
[
36
,
31
,
30
,
14
]
. A profinite group
G
is a topological group which is Hausdorff, compact and totally disconnected. Equivalently,
G
is isomorphic (as a topological group) to an inverse limit of finite groups. A topological group
G
is topologically generated by a set
A
, if the subgroup generated by
A
is dense in
G
. The topological rank of
G
in this case is the size of its smallest topological generating set. We keep using the notation
\textrm{rank}(G)
for the topological rank if
G
is topological.
Since a profinite group
G
is compact, it is equipped with a unique Haar measure
\mu
satisfying
\mu(G)=1
. It therefore follows that an open, an thus finite index, subgroup
H
of index
n
has Haar measure
\mu(H)=\frac{1}{n}
.

In what follows we will study the proper subgroup testing problem for profinite groups. From that, we will deduce results about certain collections of finite groups. We use the notation
\delta(A,G)
as before, quantifying only over proper
closed
subgroups
H
. Unlike discrete groups, in the category of profinite groups, every proper closed subgroup is contained in a proper open subgroup, and we can deduce that

\delta(A;G)=\inf\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1-\frac{|A\cap H|}{|A|}\ \middle|\ H\lneq_{open}G}}\right\}.

We mention in passing that for a (topologically) finitely generated profinite group
G
, a deep result of Nikolov–Segal
[
27
]
asserts that every finite index subgroup of
G
is open, but we will not use this result.

A (profinite) group
G
is said to have
polynomial maximal subgroup growth
(PMSG) if there exists
E=E(G)>0
such that
m_{N}(G)\leq N^{E}
for every
N\in\mathbb{N}
, where
m_{N}(G)
is the number of maximal open subgroups of index
N
in
G
.
A family of groups
\mathfrak{G}
is said to have
uniform PMSG
if there exists
E^{\prime}>0
, such that for every
G\in\mathfrak{G}
,
m_{N}(G)\leq N^{E^{\prime}\cdot\textrm{rank}(G)}
.
In
[
25
]
, Mann shows that for the free prosolvable group on
k
generators
\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{k}^{\textrm{sol}}
,
m_{N}(\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{k}^{\textrm{sol}})\leq N^{\frac{13}{4}k+2}
. This implies that the family of prosolvable groups (and, in particular the finite solvable groups) are uniformly PMSG. More generally, in
[
6
]
, Borovik–Pyber–Shalev showed the following: Let
L
be a finite group. Then there exists a constant
E(L)
such that if
G
is any profinite group of rank
k
without a section
L
, then
m_{N}(G)\leq N^{E(L)\textrm{rank}(G)}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Recall that
L
is a section of
G
if
G
has an open subgroup which is mapped onto
L
. This implies that the family

\mathfrak{G}_{\neq L}=\{G\hbox{\ profinite\ without\ }L-\hbox{section}\}

is uniformly PMSG.
Mann and Shalev showed in
[
24
]
that a profinite group
G
is PMSG if and only if it is
positively finitely generated
(PFG), i.e. there exists a positive integer
r
, such that

\Pro_{x_{1},...,x_{r}\sim G}[\overline{\langle x_{1},...,x_{r}\rangle}=G]>0,

where
x\sim G
is sampling an element according to the Haar measure of
G
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
0<\delta<\frac{1}{3},
and
\mathfrak{G}
a family of profinite groups which are uniformly PMSG with constant
E^{\prime}>0
. Then, there exists a constant
C=C(E^{\prime},\delta)
such that every
G\in\mathfrak{G}
has a subset
A
of size
|A|\leq C\cdot\textrm{rank}(G)
with
\delta(A;G)\geq\delta
.

Proof.

Let
G\in\mathfrak{G}
with
\textrm{rank}(G)=k
. The idea of the proof is as follows: If
x\sim G
and
M
is a subgroup of
G
of index
N
, then
\Pro[x\in M]=\nicefrac{{1}}{{N}}
. Hence, the probability
M
avoids less than a
\delta
-fraction of
n
independently sampled elements of
G
decreases exponentially. Since we have a polynomial bound on the number of maximal subgroups of each index, we can use a union bound and choose
n
so that there is a positive probability for the sampled
n
elements to have a detection probability of
\delta
.

Let
A
be a set of
n
independently sampled elements of
G
.
Then, for a maximal subgroup
M
of
G
of index
N
, we have

\begin{split}\Pro[|A\cap M|\geq(1-\delta)n]\leq\binom{n}{(1-\delta)n}\cdot\frac{1}{N^{(1-\delta)n}}\leq\frac{2^{H(1-\delta)n}}{N^{(1-\delta)n}}\leq N^{(H_{2}(1-\delta)-(1-\delta))n},\end{split}

where
H_{2}(1-\delta)=-\delta\log\delta-(1-\delta)\log(1-\delta)
is the binary entropy function. Therefore, by a union bound,

\begin{split}\Pro[\exists M\leq_{max}G\colon|A\cap M|\geq(1-\delta)n]&\leq\sum_{N=2}^{\infty}\underbrace{m_{N}(G)}_{\leq N^{E^{\prime}k}}N^{(H_{2}(1-\delta)-(1-\delta))n}\\
&\leq\sum_{N=2}^{\infty}N^{E^{\prime}k+{(H_{2}(1-\delta)-(1-\delta))n}}.\end{split}

In particular, if we choose
n
such that
E^{\prime}k+{(H_{2}(1-\delta)-(1-\delta))n}\leq-2
, then this probability is smaller than
1
and we are done.
Solving the inequality, and as
H_{2}(1-\delta)<1-\delta
for
\delta<\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}
, we deduce that

n\geq\frac{-2-E^{\prime}k}{H_{2}(1-\delta)-(1-\delta)}\geq\frac{-2E^{\prime}}{H_{2}(1-\delta)-(1-\delta)}\cdot k.

Therefore, we can choose
C=\frac{-2E^{\prime}}{H_{2}(1-\delta)-(1-\delta)}
, which finishes the proof.
∎

Since finite solvable groups are uniformly PMSG, Theorem
1.13
is deduced from the above. For finite solvable groups, by Mann’s result,
E^{\prime}\leq\frac{17}{4}
, and if we take
\delta=\nicefrac{{1}}{{10}}
, then
H(1-\delta)-(1-\delta)\leq-\nicefrac{{2}}{{5}}
and
C\leq 17\cdot 5=85
.

6.
Running time and lower bounds on the length

The running time of the tester
\mathcal{T}
which we described in the introduction is composed of its sampling phase and verification phase. In the sampling phase,
\mathcal{T}
uses as much time steps as random bits, with some constant overhead, to choose the index
i
. Then, the tester needs to calculate
w_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{k}
out of the sampled index
i
, write down
w_{i}
, and send it to the oracle of the function
h
. Lastly, after
h
replies with a bit, it takes a constant time to decide whether to accept or reject. Specifically, the time resources needed for running
\mathcal{T}
is at least the length of (the encoding of)
w_{i}
, which is
|w_{i}|\cdot\log k
.
19
19

19

We use
|w|
to denote the length of
w\in\mathcal{F}_{k}
as a word in the fixed basis
B=\{x_{1},...,x_{k}\}
of
\mathcal{F}_{k}
.

We now prove that to achieve detection probability of at least
\delta>0
, we need
\Ex_{w_{i}\in A}[|w_{i}|]\geq\delta k
, which in turn means that just to write down an average
w_{i}
we need
\omega(\textrm{poly}(\log k))
time steps. This means that such a tester cannot be time efficient.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{k}
be a finite set, and
\delta>0
, such that

\forall H\lneq\mathcal{F}_{k}\ \colon\ \ \frac{|A\cap H|}{|A|}\leq 1-\delta.

Then,

\Ex_{w\in A}[|w|]\geq\delta k.

Proof.

Let
B=\{x_{1},...,x_{k}\}
be a fixed basis of
\mathcal{F}_{k}
. Let
H_{i}
be the subgroup of
\mathcal{F}_{k}
generated by all of the
x_{j}
’s except for
x_{i}
. Then,
w\in H_{i}
if and only if when writing it down as a reduced word in
B
,
x_{i}
and its inverse do not appear. Therefore,
the condition
\frac{|A\cap H_{i}|}{|A|}\leq 1-\delta
implies that
x_{i}
or its inverse
appear
in at least
\delta|A|
of the words in
A
. Since
|w|\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\bf 1}_{x_{i}\in w}
, where
x_{i}\in w
means that
x_{i}
or its inverse appear in the word
w
, we have

\Ex_{w\in A}[|w|]=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{w\in A}|w|\geq\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{w\in A}\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\bf 1}_{x_{i}\in w}=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\underbrace{\sum_{w\in A}{\bf 1}_{x_{i}\in w}}_{\geq\delta|A|}\geq\delta k.

∎
