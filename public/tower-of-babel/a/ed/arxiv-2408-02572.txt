Non-commutative optimization problems with differential constraints
Mateus Araújo, Andrew J. P. Garner, Miguel Navascués

Abstract
Non-commutative polynomial optimization (NPO) problems seek to minimize the state average of a polynomial of some operator variables, subject to polynomial constraints, over all states and operators, as well as the Hilbert spaces where those might be defined. Many of these problems are known to admit a complete hierarchy of semidefinite programming (SDP) relaxations. In this work, we consider a variant of NPO problems where a subset of the operator variables satisfies a system of ordinary differential equations. We prove that, under mild conditions of operator boundedness, for every such problem one can construct a standard NPO problem with the same solution. This allows us to define a complete hierarchy of SDPs to tackle the original differential problem. We apply this method to bound averages of local observables in quantum spin systems subject to a Hamiltonian evolution (i.e., a quench). We find that, even in the thermodynamic limit of infinitely many sites, low levels of the hierarchy provide very good approximations for reasonably long evolution times.



I
Introduction

The goal of non-commutative polynomial optimization (NPO) is to minimize the state average of a polynomial of operator variables, with the condition that a number of polynomials, evaluated on said variables, result in positive semidefinite operators. The minimization is understood to take place both over all states and tuples of operators and the Hilbert spaces that those inhabit
[
1
]
. Many important problems in quantum physics can be cast as an NPO problem, e.g.: bounding the maximum quantum value of a Bell inequality
[
2
]
, estimating the ground state energy of a local Hamiltonian
[
3
]
and, more recently, bounding local observables of many-body systems at zero
[
4
]
or finite temperature
[
5
]
or in a stationary state
[
6
,
7
]
. Nowadays NPO theory is a standard tool in quantum information theory
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
.

There exist, however, important physical problems that cannot be formulated within the framework of NPO theory. Note that the time evolution of a quantum system, open or closed, is modeled by means of differential equations on non-commuting variables. Since one cannot express them as polynomial inequalities, NPO theory cannot enforce such ‘differential constraints’. Thus, several tasks in quantum mechanics, such as predicting the future behavior of a many-body system
[
11
,
12
]
, or mitigating the readout error of a Noisy Intermediate Scale Quantum (NISQ) device
[
13
]
can only be accomplished in very special scenarios (e.g.: one-dimensional many-body systems)
[
14
]
.

To tackle these challenges, we introduce the notion of
differential NPO (DNPO) problems
. Such are optimization problems over non-commuting variables where a subset of the variables, indexed by a continuous number
t
, satisfies a system of ordinary differential equations. We show that each DNPO problem can be relaxed to an auxiliary NPO problem, which can therefore be approached with the usual semidefinite programming hierarchies. Moreover, if the resulting NPO problem is Archimedean (has bounded variables, so to speak), then its solution equals that of the original DNPO. Since Archimedeanity is also a sufficient condition for the NPO SDP hierarchies to converge to the solution of the auxiliary NPO problem, it follows that the proposed hierarchy of SDP relaxations is also complete for the original DNPO problem.

To illustrate the power of our method, we apply it to solve a very natural problem in statistical physics: the determination of the local properties of quenched many-body systems. More specifically, picture a finite spin system, initially in the ground or Gibbs state of some known local Hamiltonian
H
, and let
O
be any local operator. We switch on the local Hamiltonian
H^{\prime}
for a time
\tau
. What is the new value of
\langle O\rangle
? We show that this problem can be cast as a DNPO. Its
\kappa^{th}
SDP relaxation outputs the quantities
u^{\kappa},l^{\kappa}
, which satisfy
l^{\kappa}\leq\langle O\rangle\leq u^{\kappa}
, with
\lim_{\kappa\to\infty}u^{\kappa}=\lim_{\kappa\to\infty}l^{\kappa}=\langle O\rangle
, if the initial state is unique. This problem can be generalized to the thermodynamic limit of infinitely many particles, where one would demand the initial state and Hamiltonians
H,H^{\prime}
to be translation-invariant. As in the case of finite size, we provide a complete hierarchy of SDP relaxations. We test low levels of the two hierarchies and find that the resulting upper and lower bounds are very close to each other for reasonably long times.

This paper is structured as follows: in section
II
, we will define the basic DNPO problem and motivate it by formulating a recent quantum information task as such. In section
III
we will provide an NPO relaxation for the general DNPO problem, and derive a hierarchy of semidefinite programming
[
15
]
relaxations to tackle the latter. We will also state a sufficient criterion for this hierarchy to converge to the solution of the original DNPO problem. The performance of the hierarchy of SDPs will be tested with two numerical examples in section
IV
. In section
V
we show how our method can be adapted to tackle the problem of characterizing the average of many-body local observables after a quench. Finally, in section
VI
we will present our conclusions.

II
Differential non-commutative polynomial problems

Let
Y\equiv(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N})
be a vector of non-commutative Hermitian variables and let
X(t):=(X_{1}(t),\ldots,X_{M}(t))
be a collection of vectors of non-commutative variables parameterized by
t\in[0,1]
. Since the boundary conditions
X(0),X(1)
will play a privileged role and do not depend on
t
, we include them among the variables
Y
. That is,
Y_{k}:=X_{k}(0)
, for
k=1,\ldots,M
, and
Y_{k}:=X_{k-M}(1)
, for
k=M+1,\ldots,2M
. Given the polynomials
f(t,X(t),Y)
,
\{g_{i}(t,X(t),Y)\}_{i=1}^{M}
,
\{p_{k}(t,X(t),Y)\}_{k=1}^{K}
, and
\{q_{j}(t,X(t),Y)\}_{j=1}^{J}
we consider the following optimization problem
\mathbf{P}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}:\qquad\begin{array}[]{rl}p^{\star}:=\min&\psi\left(\int_{[0,1]}dtf(t,X(t),Y)\right),\\[3.0pt]
\mbox{such that }&p_{k}(t,X(t),Y)\geq 0,\quad\mbox{for }k=1,\ldots,K,\;t\in[0,1],\\[3.0pt]
&\int_{[0,1]}dt\psi(q_{j}(t,X(t),Y))\geq 0,\quad\mbox{for }j=1,\ldots,J,\\[3.0pt]
&\frac{d}{dt}X_{i}(t)=g_{i}(t,X(t),Y),\quad\mbox{for }i=1,\ldots,M,\;t\in[0,1]\end{array}

where the optimization takes place over all Hilbert spaces
{\cal H}
, all operators
Y_{i},X_{j}(t)\in B({\cal H})
and all normalized states
\psi:B({\cal H})\to{\mathbb{C}}
. We call problem (
II
) a
differential non-commutative polynomial optimization (DNPO) problem
. If
f(t,X(t),Y)=f(Y)
, the integral over
t
is superfluous, and the objective function is simply the average of a polynomial on the operator variables
Y
. The same holds for the polynomials
\{q_{j}\}_{j}
. If
M=0
, i.e., if there are no
t
-dependent operators
X(t)
, this problem is a non-commutative polynomial optimization (NPO) problem
[
1
]
.

Throughout the whole article, we will always assume that Problem (
II
) has feasible points, i.e., that the polynomials
\{p_{k}\}_{k}\cup\{q_{j}\}\cup\{g_{i}\}_{i}
are such that there exist a Hilbert space
{\cal H}
, operators
\{X_{i}(t),Y_{j}\}_{ij}\subset B({\cal H})
and a state
\psi:B({\cal H})\to{\mathbb{C}}
satisfying the problem constraints.

To motivate the study of DNPO problems, we next show that a recently proposed task in quantum information theory can be formulated as such. The authors of
[
16
]
consider the problem of characterizing the correlations generated by measuring a quantum system of known spin number
J\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}
with a device that allows rotating the system. Namely, the observed measurement statistics is of the form

P(a|\vec{\theta})=\mbox{tr}\{M_{a}U(\vec{\theta})\rho U(-\vec{\theta})\},

(5)

where
(M_{a})_{a=1}^{A}
is a projective measurement,
\rho\in B({\cal H})
is a quantum state and
U(\vec{\theta}):=e^{-i\sum_{k=1}^{3}\theta_{k}S_{k}}
, for some self-adjoint operators
S_{1},S_{2},S_{3}
satisfying the relations:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{3}S_{k}^{2}=J(J+1){\mathbb{I}},

\displaystyle[S_{1},S_{2}]=iS_{3},[S_{2},S_{3}]=iS_{1},[S_{3},S_{1}]=iS_{2}.

(6)

Neither the state nor the projective measurement nor the Hilbert space where they are defined are fixed. The set of all such “rotation boxes” is dubbed
Q_{J}
. For finitely-many vectors of angles
\zeta=\{\vec{\theta}^{j}\}_{j}
, deciding whether a finite collection of observed measurement statistics
\{P(a|\vec{\theta}^{j})\}_{j=1}^{m}
admits a representation of the form (
5
) is an arduous task. In fact, the authors of
[
16
]
only manage to solve this problem for
A=2
and two-angle scenarios
\zeta=\{(0,0,0),(0,0,\theta)\}
.

Characterizing
Q_{J}
is dual to conducting linear optimizations over this set. For some finite set of angles
\zeta=\{\vec{\theta}^{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
, consider therefore the following problem:

\displaystyle\min_{P}\sum_{a=1}^{A}\sum_{j=1}^{m}f_{a,j}P(a|\vec{\theta}^{j})

such that

\displaystyle P(a|\vec{\theta})\in Q_{J}.

(7)

This problem can be reformulated as:

\displaystyle\min\psi\left(\sum_{a=1}^{A}\sum_{j=1}^{m}f_{a,j}U_{j}(1)^{\dagger}M_{a}U_{j}(1)\right)

such that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{3}S_{k}^{2}=J(J+1){\mathbb{I}},

\displaystyle M_{a}M_{b}-M_{a}\delta_{ab}=0,a=1,...,A,\sum_{a}M_{a}={\mathbb{I}},

\displaystyle[S_{1},S_{2}]=iS_{3},[S_{2},S_{3}]=iS_{1},[S_{3},S_{1}]=iS_{2},

\displaystyle U_{j}(t)U_{j}(t)^{\dagger}=U_{j}(t)^{\dagger}U_{j}(t)={\mathbb{I}},j=1,...,m,

\displaystyle[U_{j}(t),\vec{\theta}^{j}\cdot\vec{S}]=0,j=1,...,m,

\displaystyle\frac{dU_{j}(t)}{dt}=iU_{j}(t)(\vec{\theta}^{j}\cdot\vec{S}),U_{j}(0)={\mathbb{I}},j=1,...,m.

(8)

Defining

\displaystyle X_{j}(t)

\displaystyle:=\frac{U_{j}(t)+U_{j}(t)^{\dagger}}{2},\mbox{ for }j=1,...,m,

\displaystyle X_{j}(t)

\displaystyle:=i\frac{U_{j}(t)-U_{j}(t)^{\dagger}}{2},\mbox{ for }j=m+1,...,2m,

\displaystyle Y_{4m+a}

\displaystyle:=M_{a},\mbox{ for }a=1,...,A,

\displaystyle Y_{4m+A+k}

\displaystyle:=S_{k},\mbox{ for }k=1,2,3,

(9)

the above problem is revealed to be a DNPO. If we could solve it or at least provide a good enough relaxation thereof, then we could use this knowledge to certify the randomness of the measurement outcome
a
, as the authors of
[
16
]
do in two-angle scenarios.

Notice also that

\displaystyle 1+J(J+1)+3m-\sum_{j=1}^{2m}\left(X_{j}(t)^{2}+X_{j}(0)^{2}+X_{j}(1)^{2}\right)-\sum_{a}M_{a}^{2}-\sum_{k}S_{k}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left\{\left(1-U_{j}(t)U_{j}(t)^{\dagger}\right)+\left(1-U_{j}(t)^{\dagger}U_{j}(t)\right)+\left(1-U_{j}(0)U_{j}(0)^{\dagger}\right)+\left(1-U_{j}(0)^{\dagger}U_{j}(0)\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\left(1-U_{j}(1)U_{j}(1)^{\dagger}\right)+\left(1-U_{j}(1)^{\dagger}U_{j}(1)\right)\right\}+\left(1-\sum_{a}M_{a}\right)+\left(\sum_{a}\left(M_{a}-M_{a}^{2}\right)\right)+\left(J(J+1){\mathbb{I}}-\sum_{k}S_{k}^{2}\right).

(10)

Due to the operator constraints, for any feasible point of Problem (
8
) the right-hand side of the equation above vanishes. This implies, by the left-hand side, that any tuple of feasible operators
(X(t),Y)
must be bounded. In the next section we will see that, for any DNPO with non-commuting variables whose norm can be bounded in this way, we can provide a complete hierarchy of SDP relaxations.

III
A hierarchy of SDP relaxations for DNPO problems

Problem (
II
) cannot be attacked by brute force. Note, indeed, that the optimization in (
II
) is over all Hilbert spaces
{\cal H}
, finite- or infinite-dimensional: a full parametrization of each operator appearing in (
II
) would therefore lead us to a problem with infinitely many real variables. In fact, some undecidable tasks can be reduced to the approximate resolution of the smaller class of NPO problems
[
17
]
. Since there exist complete sets of relaxations for some such problems, it follows that there are no general and complete
variational
algorithms to solve NPO problems and thus DNPO problems.

The previous argument does not preclude, however, the existence of complete sets of
relaxations
for DNPO problems: finding these is the goal of this section. To achieve it, we take inspiration from the work of Lasserre et al.
[
18
]
. In this paper, it is shown that classical control problems can be reduced to polynomial optimization problems, which, in turn, can be tackled through hierarchies of convex relaxations
[
19
,
20
]
; more concretely, semidefinite programs (SDP)
[
15
]
.

Correspondingly, in the following lines we show how to relax problem
\mathbf{P}
(
section

II
) to an NPO problem, for which there also exists a hierarchy of semidefinite programming (SDP) relaxations
[
15
]
. This hierarchy is complete under the assumption that all the relevant operators are bounded
[
1
]
. This covers very interesting problems in quantum mechanics, such as the characterization of the set of rotation boxes (see problem (
8
) in the previous section), the extrapolation of quantum measurement data (section
IV.2
), and the computation of averages of local observables in many-body systems subject to a quench (section
V
).

Let us then derive the promised NPO relaxation. Suppose, for some Hilbert space
{\cal H}
, that the operators
\{X_{1}(t),\ldots,X_{M}(t),Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N}\}\subset B({\cal H})
satisfy the conditions of problem
\mathbf{P}
. On the extended Hilbert space
\tilde{{\cal H}}:={\cal H}\otimes{\cal T}
, where
{\cal T}:={\cal L}^{2}[0,1]
is spanned by the generalized vectors
1
1

1

Those are assumed to be orthogonal, i.e.,
\langle t|u\rangle=\delta(t-u)
.

\{|t\rangle:t\in[0,1]\}
, we define the operators:

\displaystyle\tilde{T}

\displaystyle:={\mathbb{I}}_{{\cal H}}\otimes\int_{[0,1]}dt\;t\,|t\rangle\!\langle t|_{{\cal T}},

\displaystyle\tilde{X}_{i}

\displaystyle:=\int_{[0,1]}dt\,X_{i}(t)\otimes|t\rangle\!\langle t|_{{\cal T}},

\displaystyle\tilde{Y}_{j}

\displaystyle:=Y_{j}\otimes{\mathbb{I}}_{{\cal T}}\qquad\mathrm{for~}j=1,\ldots,N.

(11)

We further define the vectors
\tilde{X}:=(\tilde{X}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{X}_{M})
and
\tilde{Y}:=(\tilde{Y}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{Y}_{N})

Note that, for any pair of functions
s_{1},s_{2}:[0,1]\to B({\cal H})

\left(\int_{[0,1]}dts_{1}(t)\otimes|t\rangle\!\langle t|\right)\times\left(\int_{[0,1]}dt^{\prime}s_{2}(t^{\prime})\otimes|t^{\prime}\rangle\!\langle t^{\prime}|\right)=\int_{[0,1]}dts_{1}(t)s_{2}(t)\otimes|t\rangle\!\langle t|.

(12)

For any polynomial
h(\tilde{T},\tilde{X},\tilde{Y})
, we thus have that

h(\tilde{T},\tilde{X},\tilde{Y})=\int_{[0,1]}dt\;h(t,X(t),Y)\otimes|t\rangle\!\langle t|.

(13)

For
k=1,\ldots,K
, the condition
p_{k}(t,X(t),Y)\geq 0
, for
t\in[0,1]
is hence equivalent to the condition

p_{k}(\tilde{T},\tilde{X},\tilde{Y})\geq 0.

(14)

Now, define the state
\omega\equiv\psi_{{\cal H}}\otimes\beta_{{\cal T}}
, where
\beta_{\cal T}\in B({\cal T})
is any state assigning a uniform probability distribution over

\beta_{{\cal T}}(\bullet):=\int_{[0,1]^{2}}dtdt^{\prime}\langle t|\bullet|t^{\prime}\rangle

From

\omega\left(h(\tilde{T},\tilde{X},\tilde{Y})\right)=\int_{[0,1]}dt\;\psi\left(h(t,X(t),Y)\right).

(15)

Thus, we can re-express the objective function of problem
\mathbf{P}
as

\omega\left(f(\tilde{T},\tilde{X},\tilde{Y})\right)=\psi\left(\int_{[0,1]}dtf(t,X(t),Y)\right),

(16)

and all constraints of the form
\int_{[0,1]}dt\psi\left(q_{j}(t,X(t),Y)\right)\geq 0
as

\omega(q_{j}(\tilde{T},\tilde{X},\tilde{Y}))\geq 0.

(17)

From the definition of
\tilde{T}
, it follows that
\mbox{spec}(\tilde{T})=[0,1]
, which implies the extra operator inequality:

\tilde{T}-\tilde{T}^{2}\geq 0.

(18)

It remains to enforce, by constraints on the new variables
\tilde{T}
,
\tilde{X}
,
\tilde{Y}
and
\omega

is the solution of the differential equation in problem

.
To do so, it will be convenient introduce a new set of asbtract, non-commutative variables:
\tilde{t},\tilde{x},\tilde{y}
. Those must not be interpreted as operators acting in a specific Hilbert space, but as the generators of an abstract
*

\tilde{\mathbb{P}}

Within the set of polynomials
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}
we next define the ‘differentiation’ linear map
{\cal D}:\tilde{\mathbb{P}}\to\tilde{\mathbb{P}}
by induction on the degree of the monomial to which it is applied:

Definition 1
.

The
differentiation
linear map
{\cal D}:\tilde{\mathbb{P}}\to\tilde{\mathbb{P}}
satisfies

\displaystyle{\cal D}(\tilde{t})

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle{\cal D}(1)

\displaystyle={\cal D}(\tilde{y}_{k})=0\quad\forall k,

\displaystyle{\cal D}(\tilde{x}_{i})

\displaystyle=g_{i}(\tilde{t},\tilde{x},\tilde{y})\quad\forall i,

\displaystyle{\cal D}(hh^{\prime})

\displaystyle={\cal D}(h)h^{\prime}+h{\cal D}(h^{\prime})\quad\forall h,h^{\prime}\in\tilde{{\cal P}}.

(19)

Note that the differentiation map depends on the tuple of polynomials
g_{1},...,g_{M}
defining the differential equation in (
II
).

Example 1
.

Let
h_{1}=\tilde{t}^{2}\tilde{y}_{8}\tilde{x}_{2}\tilde{y}_{9}\tilde{x}_{1}+\tilde{x}_{2}(1)
.
Then,

\displaystyle{\cal D}h_{1}=2\tilde{t}\tilde{y}_{8}\tilde{x}_{2}\tilde{y}_{9}\tilde{x}_{1}+\tilde{t}^{2}\tilde{y}_{8}g_{2}(\tilde{t},\tilde{x},\tilde{y})\tilde{y}_{9}\tilde{x}_{1}+\tilde{t}^{2}\tilde{y}_{8}\tilde{x}_{2}\tilde{y}_{9}g_{1}(\tilde{t},\tilde{x},\tilde{y}).

(20)

If
\frac{d}{dt}X_{i}(t)=g_{i}(t,X(t),Y)
for all

, then, for all polynomials

, it can be verified by induction on eq. (

) that:

\displaystyle({\cal D}h)(\tilde{T},\tilde{X},\tilde{Y})=\int_{[0,1]}dt\,\frac{d}{dt}h(t,X(t),Y)\otimes|t\rangle\!\langle t|.

(21)

By eq. (
15
), if we average the expression above on state
\omega
, we arrive at

\displaystyle\omega\left({\cal D}(h)(T,\tilde{X},\tilde{Y})\right)

\displaystyle=\psi\left(\int_{[0,1]}dt\frac{d}{dt}h(t,X(t),Y)\right)=\psi\left(h(1,X(1),Y)\right)-\psi\left(h(0,X(0),Y)\right)

\displaystyle=\omega\left(h(1,\tilde{X}(1),\tilde{Y})\right)-\;\omega\left(h(0,\tilde{X}(0),\tilde{Y})\right),

where in the second equality we have invoked the fundamental theorem of calculus and the last equality follows from eq. (

) and the fact that the last two polynomials do not depend on
\tilde{T}
or
\tilde{X}
.

Eqs. (
14
), (

), (

), (

), and (

\mathbf{Q}
of problem

(

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}:\qquad\begin{array}[]{rl}q^{\star}:=\min&\;\omega\left(f(\tilde{T},\tilde{X},\tilde{Y})\right),\\[2.5pt]
\mbox{such that }&[\tilde{T},\tilde{X}_{i}]=[\tilde{T},\tilde{Y}_{j}]=0\quad\forall i,j,\\[2.5pt]
&\tilde{T}-\tilde{T}^{2}\geq 0,\\[2.5pt]
&p_{k}(\tilde{T},\tilde{X},\tilde{Y})\geq 0\quad\mbox{for }k=1,\ldots,K,\\[2.5pt]
&\omega\left(q_{j}(\tilde{T},\tilde{X},\tilde{Y})\right)\geq 0\quad\mbox{for }j=1,\ldots,J,\\
&\omega\left({\cal D}(h)(\tilde{T},\tilde{X},\tilde{Y})\right)=\omega\left(h({\mathbb{I}},\tilde{X}(1),\tilde{Y})\right)-\;\omega\left(h(0,\tilde{X}(0),\tilde{Y})\right)\;\forall\,h\in\tilde{\mathbb{P}},\end{array}

where the minimization is over the operators
(\tilde{T},\tilde{X},\tilde{Y})
, the Hilbert spaces on which they act, and the normalized states

in those spaces.

This is a non-commutative polynomial optimization (NPO) problem, as defined in
Pironio et al. [1]
, but with a countable (rather than finite) number of state constraints. Following
[
1
]
, we next sketch how to derive a hierarchy of semidefinite programming (SDP) relaxations of problem (
III
).

The starting point to arrive at an SDP relaxation of (
III
) is replacing the optimization over
\omega,\tilde{T}
, and
\tilde{Z}:=(\tilde{X},\tilde{Y})
in Problem (
III
) by an optimization over linear functionals
\tilde{\omega}:\tilde{\mathbb{P}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
. Intuitively, we wish
\tilde{\omega}
to satisfy the relation

\tilde{\omega}(p(\tilde{t},\tilde{z}))=\omega(p(\tilde{T},\tilde{Z}))

(29)

for all polynomials
p
.

How does relation (
29
) constrain the functional
\tilde{\omega}
? For once,
\omega
is normalized, so the relation above implies
\tilde{\omega}(1)=1
. Furthermore,
\omega
is a state. Hence,

\tilde{\omega}(\mu\mu^{\dagger})\geq 0\quad\forall\mu\in\tilde{\mathbb{P}}.

(30)

Operator positivity constraints of the form
p(\tilde{T},\tilde{Z})\geq 0
are modeled by demanding that

\tilde{\omega}(\mu p\mu^{\dagger})\geq 0\quad\forall\mu\in\tilde{\mathbb{P}}.

(31)

Equality constraints of the form
h(\tilde{T},\tilde{Z})=0
are similarly enforced by demanding
\tilde{\omega}
to satisfy

\tilde{\omega}(\mu h\nu)=0\quad\forall\mu,\nu\in\tilde{\mathbb{P}}.

(32)

Putting all together, we arrive at the problem:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rl}\min&\;\tilde{\omega}\left(f(\tilde{t},\tilde{x},\tilde{y})\right),\\[2.5pt]
\mbox{such that }&\tilde{\omega}(1)=1,\tilde{\omega}(\mu\mu^{\dagger})\geq 0,\forall\mu\in\tilde{\mathbb{P}},\\
&\tilde{\omega}(\mu[\tilde{t},\tilde{x}_{i}]\nu)=\tilde{\omega}(\mu[\tilde{t},\tilde{y}_{j}]\nu)=0\quad\forall i,j,\forall\mu,\nu\in\tilde{\mathbb{P}},\\[2.5pt]
&\tilde{\omega}(\mu(\tilde{t}-\tilde{t}^{2})\mu^{\dagger})\geq 0,\forall\mu\in\tilde{\mathbb{P}},\\[2.5pt]
&\tilde{\omega}(\mu p_{k}(\tilde{t},\tilde{x},\tilde{y})\mu^{\dagger})\geq 0\quad\mbox{for }k=1,\ldots,K,\\[2.5pt]
&\tilde{\omega}\left(q_{j}(\tilde{t},\tilde{x},\tilde{y})\right)\geq 0\quad\mbox{for }j=1,\ldots,J,\\
&\tilde{\omega}\left({\cal D}(h)(\tilde{t},\tilde{x},\tilde{y})\right)=\tilde{\omega}\left(h(1,\tilde{x}(1),\tilde{y})\right)-\;\tilde{\omega}\left(h(0,\tilde{x}(0),\tilde{y})\right)\;\forall\,h\in\tilde{\mathbb{\mathbb{P}}}.\end{array}

III
). One can relax it further by placing limits on the degrees of the polynomials appearing above. More explicitly, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, a sound relaxation of (
III
) is given by:

\displaystyle q^{k}:=\min

\displaystyle\;\tilde{\omega}\left(f(\tilde{t},\tilde{x},\tilde{y})\right),

(40a)

such that

\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}(1)=1,\tilde{\omega}(\mu\mu^{\dagger})\geq 0,\forall\mu\in\tilde{\mathbb{P}},\mbox{deg}(\mu)\leq k,

(40b)

\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}(\mu[\tilde{t},\tilde{x}_{i}]\nu)=\tilde{\omega}(\mu[\tilde{t},\tilde{y}_{j}]\nu)=0\quad\forall i,j,\forall\mu,\nu\in\tilde{\mathbb{P}},\deg(\mu)+\deg(\nu)\leq 2k-2,

(40c)

\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}(\mu(\tilde{t}-\tilde{t}^{2})\mu^{\dagger})\geq 0,\forall\mu\in\tilde{\mathbb{P}},\deg(\mu)\leq k-1,

(40d)

\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}(\mu p_{l}(\tilde{t},\tilde{x},\tilde{y})\mu^{\dagger})\geq 0\quad\mbox{for }l=1,\ldots,K,\forall\mu\in\tilde{\mathbb{P}},\deg(\mu)\leq k-\left\lceil\frac{\deg(p_{k})}{2}\right\rceil,

(40e)

Remark 1

V
Application: Modeling quenches in 2-Reduced Density Matrix Theory

V.1
The quantum many-body scenario and 2-Reduced Density Matrix Theory

Consider a system of
n
spins, let them be qubits for ease of exposition. Let
\sigma_{c}^{(j)}
, with
c\in\{1,2,3\}
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
denote the
c^{th}
Pauli matrix acting on qubit
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Normally, it is assumed that the Hamiltonian
H
that the system is subject to consists of sums of two-particle interactions, i.e.,

H=\sum_{j<k,a,b}h^{ab}_{jk}\sigma^{(j)}_{a}\sigma_{b}^{(k)}+\sum_{j,a}h_{j}^{a}\sigma^{(j)}_{a},

(102)

where
\{h_{jk}^{ab}\}\subset\mathbb{R}
.
When interactions between every pair of particles are allowed, we speak of a
spin glass
. In some physical scenarios, though, the spins lie on the sites of a regular lattice. In that case, only interactions between near neighbors are significant. For instance, in a 1D material with nearest-neighbor interactions, a general Hamiltonian would look like this:

H=\sum_{j,a,b}h^{a,b}_{j}\sigma^{(j)}_{a}\sigma_{b}^{(j+1)}+\sum_{j,a}h_{j}^{a}\sigma^{(j)}_{a}.

(103)

In quantum spin systems with a local (or few-particle) Hamiltonian, one seeks to answer questions like “what is the ground state energy?” Or, “what is the magnetization of the sample at temperature
T
?”.

The first question can be cast as an NPO problem. It reduces to

\displaystyle\min\psi(H)

such that

\displaystyle\{\sigma^{(j)}_{a},\sigma^{(j)}_{b}\}=2\delta_{ab}{\mathbb{I}},\forall a,b,j,

\displaystyle[\sigma^{(j)}_{a},\sigma^{(k)}_{b}]=2i\delta_{jk}\sum_{c}\varepsilon^{abc}\sigma^{(j)}_{c},\forall a,b,j,k,

(104)

where
\varepsilon^{ijk}
is the Levi-Civita symbol. Indeed, the only irreducible representation of the above operator constraints is unitarily equivalent to the set of Pauli matrices acting on
n
qubits.

Since the above is an NPO, one can use the method in
[
1
]
to derive a complete hierarchy of SDP relaxations. This hierarchy, which can be shown to converge at level
k=n
to the exact solution of the problem, is the basis of 2-Reduced Density Matrix Theory (2-RDMT)
[
3
,
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
,
30
]
.

Due to the commutation and anticommutation relations of the Pauli operators, polynomials of the non-commuting variables
\{\tilde{\sigma}^{(k)}_{a}\}_{a,k}
can always be brought to a unique
normal form
. More specifically, they can always be expressed as linear combinations of monomials of the form:

\mathbb{M}:=\left\{\prod_{j=1}^{s}\tilde{\sigma}^{(k_{j})}_{a_{j}}:k_{j}\not=k_{l},\mbox{ for }j\not=l\right\}.

(105)

Note that all these monomials are self-adjoint. From now on, for any Pauli polynomial
p
, we denote by
[p]
its normal form.

In principle, we could relax problem (
104
) by optimizing over functionals
\tilde{\omega}
defined on the span of
\{p\in\mathbb{M}:\mbox{deg}(p)\leq 2k\}
. For spin glasses, that would make perfect sense. For 1D, 2D or 3D materials, it is computationally preferable to tailor the considered polynomials to fit the neighboring relations between the
n
qubits
[
28
]
.

So, given a Hamiltonian
H
like in (
102
), let
{\cal G}(H)
be a graph with vertices
\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and edges

\{(j,k):h^{ab}_{jk}\not=0,\mbox{ for some }a,b\}.

(106)

This graph represents the neighboring relations among the
n
qubits, e.g.: for a spin glass, the edges of
{\cal G}(H)
would be
\{(j,k):k,j=1,\ldots,n\}
; for a 1D material,
\{(j,j+1):j=1,\ldots,n-1\}
.

Next, define

\mathbb{M}({\cal G},k):=\left\{\prod_{j=1}^{k^{\prime}}\tilde{\sigma}^{(s_{j})}_{a_{j}}:s_{j}\not=s_{l},\mbox{ for }j\not=l,\mbox{dist}_{{\cal G}}(s_{j},s_{l})<k,k^{\prime}\leq k\right\}.

(107)

With this notation, the
k^{th}
SDP relaxation of problem (
104
) is usually presented as:

\displaystyle\min\tilde{\omega}(H)

such that

\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}(1)=1,

\displaystyle M\geq 0,

\displaystyle M_{o,o^{\prime}}:=\tilde{\omega}([oo^{\prime}]),o,o^{\prime}\in\mathbb{M}({\cal G}(H),k),

(108)

where the optimization takes place among linear functionals
\tilde{\omega}:\mathbb{M}({\cal G}(H),k)^{2}\to{\mathbb{C}}
.

For several decades, the only rigorous quantities that 2-RDMT could provide were lower bounds on the ground state energy. In
[
31
]
, it is noted that, given an upper bound on the ground state energy (obtained, e.g., through variational methods), one can define an NPO to bound the value of the average of an arbitrary local operator within the set of ground states. More recently, it was discovered that, adding extra
H
-dependent convex constraints to the linear functional
\omega
, one can restrict the optimization to ground states of
H

[
4
,
5
]
, Gibbs states at any given temperature
[
5
]
and steady states of a local Lindbladian
[
6
,
7
]
. For any set of monomials
{\cal M}
, all such constraints have the form

(\tilde{\omega}(o))_{o\in{\cal M}}\in G({\cal M}),

(109)

which we abbreviate to
\tilde{\omega}({\cal M})\in G({\cal M})
. As the set
{\cal M}
grows, the constraints become tighter, and
G({\cal M})
exactly characterizes the desired set of states. In that regime, we simply write
\tilde{\omega}\in G
.

V.2
Quenches in finite systems

In the following lines, we detail how our results in Section
III
imply that we can add a new functionality to the 2-RDMT toolbox, namely, a technique to model
quenches
, or time-independent Hamiltonian evolutions. Suppose that the state of our quantum spin system initially belongs to some set of states
G
(say, the ground state/Gibbs state of some local Hamiltonian
H
or a steady state). We switch on Hamiltonian
H
for time
\tau
, and consider the problem of lower bounding the value of the local operator
f(\sigma)
on the new state. The problem to solve is therefore:

\displaystyle\min\psi(e^{iH(\sigma)\tau}f(\sigma)e^{-iH(\sigma)\tau})

such that

\displaystyle\{\sigma^{(j)}_{a},\sigma^{(j)}_{b}\}=2\delta_{ab}{\mathbb{I}},\forall a,b,j,

\displaystyle[\sigma^{(j)}_{a},\sigma^{(k)}_{b}]=2i\delta_{jk}\sum_{c}\varepsilon^{abc}\sigma^{(j)}_{c},\forall a,b,j,k,

\displaystyle\psi\in G.

(110)

This problem can be tackled by modeling the operator
e^{iH(\sigma)\tau}
, as we did in Section
IV.2
). There is, however, a better way to deal with Hamiltonian evolution, which can be generalized to the thermodynamic limit of infinitely many spins.

First, we move to the Heisenberg picture. Namely, we introduce the time-dependent operator variables

\displaystyle\sigma^{(j)}_{a}(t):=e^{iHt\tau}\sigma^{(j)}_{a}e^{-iHt\tau},

(111)

\displaystyle H(t):=e^{iHt\tau}He^{-iHt\tau}.

(112)

Then one finds that
H(t)=e^{iHt\tau}He^{-iHt\tau}=H
, with

\displaystyle H(t)=\sum_{j<k}\sum_{a,b}h_{jk}^{a,b}\sigma_{a}^{(j)}(t)\sigma_{b}^{(k)}(t)+\sum_{j,a}h_{j}^{a}\sigma^{(j)}_{a}(t).

(113)

Thus, the variables
\sigma^{(j)}_{a}(t)
follow the differential equation:

\displaystyle\frac{d\sigma^{(l)}_{c}(t)}{dt}=i\tau[H,\sigma^{(l)}_{c}(t)]=i\tau[H(t),\sigma^{(l)}_{c}(t)]=

\displaystyle-2\tau\left(\sum_{k>l}\sum_{a,b,d}h_{lk}^{a,b}\varepsilon^{acd}\sigma_{d}^{(l)}(t)\sigma_{b}^{(k)}(t)+\sum_{j<l}\sum_{a,b,d}h_{jl}^{a,b}\varepsilon^{bcd}\sigma_{a}^{(j)}(t)\sigma_{d}^{(l)}(t)+\sum_{a,d}h_{l}^{a}\varepsilon^{acd}\sigma_{d}^{(l)}(t)\right).

(114)

We arrive at the following problem:

\displaystyle\min\;

\displaystyle\psi\left(f(\sigma)\right),

such that

\displaystyle\{\sigma^{(j)}_{a}(t),\sigma^{(j)}_{b}(t)\}=2\delta_{ab}{\mathbb{I}},

\displaystyle[\sigma^{(j)}_{a}(t),\sigma^{(k)}_{b}(t)]=2i\delta_{jk}\sum_{c}\varepsilon^{abc}\sigma^{(j)}_{c}(t),

(115)

\displaystyle\frac{d\sigma^{(l)}_{c}(t)}{dt}=-2\tau\left(\sum_{k>l}\sum_{a,b,d}h_{lk}^{a,b}\varepsilon^{acd}\sigma_{d}^{(l)}(t)\sigma_{b}^{(k)}(t)+\sum_{j<l}\sum_{a,b,d}h_{jl}^{a,b}\varepsilon^{bcd}\sigma_{a}^{(j)}(t)\sigma_{d}^{(l)}(t)+\sum_{a,d}h_{l}^{a}\varepsilon^{acd}\sigma_{d}^{(l)}(t)\right),

\displaystyle\psi\in G.

(116)

This is a fairly general Hamiltonian simulation problem, which is widely believed to be intractable for classical computers. However, a relaxation of this problem would provide us with a lower bound
\mu_{-}
on
\langle f(\sigma)\rangle_{\psi^{\prime}}
, where
\psi^{\prime}
denotes the time-evolved state. By changing the sign of the objective function, one arrives at a problem whose relaxation would provide an upper bound
\mu_{+}
. Provided that
\mu_{+}-\mu_{-}\ll 1
, the problem can be considered solved for all practical purposes.

Problem (
116
) is a Markovian, independently Archimedean DNPO with the extra state constraints
\psi\in G
. The proof of Theorem
2
trivially extends to cover this case, so we can use the results of Section
III.1
to reduce Problem (
116
) to an optimization over three linear functionals, namely,
\tilde{\omega}_{[0,1]}
,
\tilde{b}_{0}
and
\tilde{b}_{1}
. Functional
\tilde{\omega}_{[0,1]}
will only act on the set
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{[0,1]}
of polynomials of
\tilde{t},\tilde{\sigma}
, while
\tilde{b}_{0}
(
\tilde{b}_{1}
) will act on the set
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{0}
(
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
) of polynomial expressions of
\tilde{\sigma}(0)
(
\tilde{\sigma}(1)
).

To simplify the presentation of the SDP relaxations, we note that, due to the Pauli relations and the commutation with
\tilde{t}
, the sets of polynomials
\mathbb{P}_{[0,1]},\mathbb{P}_{0},\mathbb{P}_{1}
respectively admit a normal form. Namely, for
k_{\sigma},k_{t}
large enough, any polynomial can be expressed as linear combinations of the self-adjoint monomials:

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}_{[0,1]}({\cal G},k_{\sigma},k_{t}):=\left\{\tilde{t}^{r}\prod_{j=1}^{s}\tilde{\sigma}^{(k_{j})}_{a_{j}}:k_{j}\not=k_{l},\mbox{ for }j\not=l,\mbox{dist}_{{\cal G}}(k_{j},k_{l})<k_{\sigma},s\leq k_{\sigma},r\leq k_{t}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}_{0}({\cal G},k_{\sigma}):=\left\{\prod_{j=1}^{s}\tilde{\sigma}^{(k_{j})}_{a_{j}}(0):k_{j}\not=k_{l},\mbox{ for }j\not=l,\mbox{dist}_{{\cal G}}(k_{j},k_{l})<k_{\sigma},s\leq k_{\sigma}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}_{1}({\cal G},k_{\sigma}):=\left\{\prod_{j=1}^{s}\tilde{\sigma}^{(k_{j})}_{a_{j}}(1):k_{j}\not=k_{l},\mbox{ for }j\not=l,\mbox{dist}_{{\cal G}}(k_{j},k_{l})<k_{\sigma},s\leq k_{\sigma}\right\}.

(117)

Given a polynomial
p
, we will be dealing with moment and localizing matrices of the form

\Lambda_{\alpha}(p,\vec{k}):=\{(\Lambda_{\alpha})_{o,o^{\prime}}:=\omega_{\alpha}([opo^{\prime}]),o,o^{\prime}\in\mathbb{M}_{\alpha}({\cal G}(H),\vec{k}\},

(118)

for
\alpha\in\{[0,1],0,1\}
.

Let
\kappa:=(k_{\sigma},k_{t})
. With this notation, the
\kappa^{th}
SDP relaxation of Problem (
116
) is:

\displaystyle q^{\kappa}:=\min

\displaystyle\;\tilde{b}_{1}\left(f(\tilde{\sigma}(1))\right),

such that

\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}_{[0,1]}(1)=1,\Lambda_{[0,1]}(1,k_{\sigma},k_{t})\geq 0,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{[0,1]}(\tilde{t}-\tilde{t}^{2},k_{\sigma},k_{t}-1)\geq 0,

\displaystyle\tilde{b}_{0}(1)=1,\Lambda_{0}(1,k_{\sigma})\geq 0,

\displaystyle\tilde{b}_{0}\in G\left(\left[\mathbb{M}_{0}({\cal G}(H),k_{\sigma})^{2}\right]\right),

\displaystyle\tilde{b}_{1}(1)=1,\Lambda_{1}(1,k_{\sigma})\geq 0,

\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}_{[0,1]}\left(\left[j\tilde{t}^{j-1}o(\tilde{\sigma})+i\tau\tilde{t}^{j}[H(\tilde{\sigma}),o(\tilde{\sigma})]\right]\right)=\tilde{b}_{1}\left(o(\tilde{\sigma}(1))\right)-\delta_{j,0}\tilde{b}_{0}\left(o(\tilde{\sigma}(0))\right),

\displaystyle\forall o\in[\mathbb{M}_{0}({\cal G}(H),k_{\sigma})^{2}],\left[j\tilde{t}^{j-1}o(\tilde{\sigma})+i\tau\tilde{t}^{j}[H(\tilde{\sigma}),o(\tilde{\sigma})]\right]\in\mbox{span}\left(\left[\mathbb{M}_{[0,1]}({\cal G}(H),k_{\sigma},k_{t})^{2}\right]\right).

(119)

Note that, if we restrict the optimization to functionals
\{\tilde{\omega}_{j}\}_{j=0,1}
, respectively defined in
\{\mathbb{M}_{j}({\cal G}(H),k_{\sigma},k_{t})^{2}\}_{j=0,1}
, and functionals
\tilde{\omega}_{[0,1]}
, defined in
\mathbb{M}_{[0,1]}({\cal G}(H),k_{\sigma},k_{t})^{2}
, then the size of the greatest positive semidefinite (PSD) constraint is
1+k_{t}
times greater than the only PSD constraint needed to run 2-RDMT relaxations of order
k_{\sigma}
.

To illustrate the performance of the hierarchy of SDPs (
119
), we consider a quenching scenario where the initial many-body state is an element of the computational basis
|a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\rangle
, with
\vec{a}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. The corresponding set of constraints on local momenta is in this case very simple, namely:

G_{|\vec{a}\rangle}=\{\langle\tilde{\sigma}_{3}^{(j)}\rangle=(-1)^{a_{j}},\forall j\}.

(120)

As a local observable, we choose
f(\sigma)=\sigma_{3}^{(n)}
, the spin of the last particle of the chain in the direction
\hat{z}
. We assume that the Hamiltonian
H
is the Heisenberg Hamiltonian:

H=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}\sum_{a=1,2,3}\sigma_{a}^{(i)}\sigma_{a}^{(i+1)}.

(121)

n=18

V.3
Quenches in the thermodynamic limit

We next consider a scenario where there are infinitely many spins in a regular lattice. We will assume, for simplicity, that the lattice is 1D, i.e., an infinite chain.

Systems in the thermodynamic limit of infinitely many particles are typically studied under the assumption of translation invariance. This means that both the initial state of the system and the Hamiltonian are invariant under the action of the
translation operator

\mathbb{T}
, an invertible homomorphism defined as:

\mathbb{T}(\sigma^{(j)}_{a}):=\sigma^{(j+1)}_{a}.

(122)

In this context, a general translation-invariant Hamiltonian with nearest-neighbors interactions is written as:

H=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\sum_{a,b}h^{a,b}\sigma^{(j)}_{a}\sigma_{b}^{(j+1)}+\sum_{a}h^{a}\sigma^{(j)}_{a}\right).

(123)

Note that the coefficients
h^{a,b},h^{a}
are site-independent.

The problem we want to solve is therefore:

\displaystyle\min\;

\displaystyle\psi\left(f(\sigma(1))\right),

such that

\displaystyle\{\sigma^{(j)}_{a}(t),\sigma^{(j)}_{b}(t)\}=2\delta_{ab}{\mathbb{I}},j\in\mathbb{Z},a,b=1,2,3,

\displaystyle[\sigma^{(j)}_{a}(t),\sigma^{(k)}_{b}(t)]=2i\delta_{jk}\sum_{c}\varepsilon^{abc}\sigma^{(j)}_{c}(t),j,k\in\mathbb{Z},a,b=1,2,3,

(124)

\displaystyle\frac{d\sigma^{(l)}_{c}(t)}{dt}=i\tau[H(\sigma(t)),\sigma_{c}^{(l)}(t)],

\displaystyle\psi\in G,

\displaystyle\psi\circ\mathbb{T}=\psi,

(125)

Note that this time the index
j
of the variables
\sigma^{(j)}_{a}(t)\}
ranges from
-\infty
to
\infty
. In addition, we are enforcing the initial state to be translation-invariant (TI). Notice as well that, despite the fact that
H(\tilde{\sigma})
is not a polynomial, expressions of the form

[H(\tilde{\sigma}),p(\tilde{\sigma})]

(126)

are. Namely, for any polynomial
p
, the above equation represents a local observable.

If
\psi
is TI, then so will be any state of the form
\psi_{t}(\bullet):=\psi(e^{iHt}\bullet e^{-iHt})
. In the Heisenberg picture, this implies that

\displaystyle\psi(p(\mathbb{T}(\sigma(0))))=\psi(p(\sigma(0))),\forall p\in\tilde{{\cal P}}_{0},

\displaystyle\psi(p(\mathbb{T}(\sigma(1))))=\psi(p(\sigma(1))),\forall p\in\tilde{{\cal P}}_{1}.

(127)

Following the derivation in Section
III
of the NPO formulation of Problem (
II
), we find that the functionals
\tilde{\omega}_{[0,1]},\tilde{b}_{0},\tilde{b}_{1}
satisfy

\displaystyle\tilde{b}_{0}(p(\tilde{\mathbb{T}}(\tilde{\sigma}(0))))=\tilde{b}_{0}(p(\tilde{\sigma}(0))),\forall p\in\tilde{{\cal P}}_{0},

\displaystyle\tilde{b}_{1}(p(\tilde{\mathbb{T}}(\tilde{\sigma}(1))))=\tilde{b}_{1}(p(\tilde{\sigma}(1))),\forall p\in\tilde{{\cal P}}_{1},

\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}_{[0,1]}(p(\tilde{t},\tilde{\mathbb{T}}(\tilde{\sigma})))=\tilde{\omega}_{[0,1]}(p(\tilde{t},\tilde{\sigma})),\forall p\in\tilde{{\cal P}}_{[0,1]},

(128)

where
\tilde{\mathbb{T}}
is a linear map from polynomials to polynomials, defined by the relation

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbb{T}}(p(\tilde{t},(\tilde{\sigma}^{(j)}_{a})_{a,j},(\tilde{\sigma}^{(j)}_{a}(0))_{a,j},(\tilde{\sigma}^{(j)}_{a}(1))_{a,j})=p(\tilde{t},(\tilde{\sigma}^{(j+1)}_{a})_{a,j},(\tilde{\sigma}^{(j+1)}_{a}(0))_{a,j},(\tilde{\sigma}^{(j+1)}_{a}(1))_{a,j}).

(129)

The last line of eq. (
128
) is proven as follows:

\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}(\tilde{t}^{k}p(\tilde{\sigma}))=\int_{[0,1]}t^{k}dt\psi(e^{-iHt}p(\sigma(0))e^{iHt})=\hat{\psi}(p(\sigma(0))),

(130)

with
\hat{\psi}=\int dtt^{k}\psi_{t}
. Since
\psi_{t}
is TI, so is
\hat{\psi}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}(p(\sigma(0)))=\hat{\psi}\circ(p(\mathbb{T}(\sigma(0))))

\displaystyle=\tilde{\omega}(\tilde{t}^{k}p(\tilde{\mathbb{T}}(\tilde{\sigma})))=\tilde{\omega}\circ\tilde{\mathbb{T}}(\tilde{t}^{k}p).

(131)

Identifying the right-hand side of this equation with the left-hand side of eq. (
130
) we arrive at the third line of eq. (
128
).

To model a thermodynamic state
\omega
with finite computational resources, we consider its action on a finite set
{\cal O}
of local operators and enforce that
\omega(o)=\omega(o^{\prime})
,
\forall o,o^{\prime}\in{\cal O}
such that
o^{\prime}=\mathbb{T}(o)
(such ‘local’ conditions are termed
local translation invariance
(LTI) in the statistical physics literature
[
35
]
).

To handle the corresponding linear functionals, it will be useful to define the following sets of monomials:

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}_{[0,1]}^{1D}(k_{\sigma},k_{t},n)

V.4
Higher local and spatial dimensions

In the last sections, we have been assuming that our many-body system is made of
n
(or infinitely many) qubits. Our results, however, easily extend to condensed matter systems with higher local dimensions. Suppose, indeed, that we wished to deal with condensed matter systems of local dimension
D
. The first step would be finding a basis of Hermitian operators in
B({\mathbb{C}}^{D})
. One can take, e.g., the generators of
SU(D)
in dimension
D
. A simpler alternative is using the operators

\displaystyle{\mathbb{I}},

\displaystyle\bar{P}_{j}:=|k\rangle\!\langle k|,k=1,\ldots,n-1,

\displaystyle\bar{X}_{jk}:=|k\rangle\langle j|+|j\rangle\langle k|,j,k\in\{1,\ldots,n\},j>k,

\displaystyle\bar{Y}_{jk}:=i(|k\rangle\langle j|-|j\rangle\langle k|),j,k\in\{1,\ldots,n\},j>k,

(139)

with
j>k
,
j,k=1,\ldots,D
. One can uniquely express any product of operators in
{\cal O}_{D}:=\{\bar{X}_{jk},\bar{Y}_{jk},\bar{P}_{j}\}_{j,k}
as a linear combination of elements of
{\cal O}_{D}
. If we define one such set
{\cal O}^{l}_{D}:=\{\bar{X}^{(l)}_{jk},\bar{Y}^{(l)}_{jk},\bar{P}^{(l)}_{j}\}_{j,k}
of variables for each subsystem
l
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\bar{P}^{(l)}_{j}:={\mathbb{I}}^{\otimes(l-1)}\otimes\bar{P}_{k}\otimes{\mathbb{I}}^{\otimes(n-l)},k=1,\ldots,n-1,

\displaystyle\bar{X}^{(l)}_{jk}:={\mathbb{I}}^{\otimes(l-1)}\otimes X_{jk}\otimes{\mathbb{I}}^{\otimes(n-l)},,j,k\in\{1,\ldots,n\},j>k,

\displaystyle\bar{Y}^{(l)}_{jk}:={\mathbb{I}}^{\otimes(l-1)}\otimes Y_{jk}\otimes{\mathbb{I}}^{\otimes(n-l)},j,k\in\{1,\ldots,n\},j>k,

(140)

then again any product of those can be expressed as linear combinations of tensor products of operators in
{\cal O}_{D}
. Thus, like in the qubit case, any polynomial of the corresponding abstract, non-commuting variables
\{x^{(l)}_{jk},y^{(l)}_{jk},p^{(l)}_{j}\}
admits a unique normal form. Crucially, for any irreducible representation
\pi
satisfying the constraint

\mu(\pi(x),\pi(y),\pi(z))=[\mu](\pi(x),\pi(y),\pi(z)),\forall\mu,

(141)

it holds that

\displaystyle\pi(\tilde{p}^{(l)}_{j})=U\bar{P}^{(l)}_{j}U^{\dagger},

\displaystyle\pi(\tilde{x}^{(l)}_{ij})=U\bar{X}^{(l)}_{jk}U^{\dagger},

\displaystyle\pi(\tilde{y}^{(l)}_{ij})=U\bar{Y}^{(l)}_{jk}U^{\dagger},

for some unitary
U
.

It is then trivial to tackle the
D
-dimensional analogs of (
116
) or (
125
) through hierarchies of SDP relaxations similar to (
119
) and (
134
). For finite systems, the higher-dimensional hierarchy will be complete by virtue of Theorem
2
. For infinite systems, the proof of Theorem
3
trivially extends to cover higher local dimensions.

When considering thermodynamical systems, we stuck to 1D systems. Dealing with cubic lattices of higher spatial dimension is, however, easy. Take, e.g., a 2D material, in which case each site is labeled by a pair of integers
(j,k)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, which denote its coordinates in the square lattice. The only changes we need to enforce on the hierarchy (
134
) to cover this case involve defining
two
translation operators
\mathbb{T}_{H},\mathbb{T}_{V}
, respectively for horizontal and vertical translations, demand the functionals
\tilde{\omega}_{[0,1]}
,
\tilde{b}_{0}
,
\tilde{b}_{1}
to be invariant under the action of both, and replace the sets of monomials
\mathbb{M}_{\alpha}^{1D}
with
\mathbb{M}_{\alpha}^{2D}(k_{\sigma},k_{t},N):=\mathbb{M}_{\alpha}({\cal G}_{2D},k_{\sigma},k_{t},N)
, with
{\cal G}_{2D}=\{((i,j),(i+1,j))\}\cup\{((i,j),(i,j+1))\}
. The proof of Theorem
3
does not rely on the 1D geometry, so it immediately generalizes to 2D.

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we have introduced the notion of differential non-commutative polynomial optimization problems. We have shown that each such problem can be relaxed into an NPO problem. If the latter happens to be Archimedean, then it is equivalent to the original problem. Since all Archimedean NPO problems admit a complete hierarchy of SDP relaxations, so do all such differential non-commutative polynomial optimization problems.

We tested the speed of convergence of the SDP hierarchy with a number of examples, obtaining reasonably good results with reasonable computational resources. Notably, our hierarchy solves the problem of extrapolating or interpolating a quantum time series, a basic quantum information task for which no general algorithm existed previously.

Most importantly, our reformulation of DNPO problems into NPOs leads to a complete hierarchy of tractable relaxations for the time-evolved local observables of condensed matter systems, be they finite or infinite. Together with the results in
[
5
,
4
,
6
,
7
]
, they imply that one can model the effect of a quench on ground and Gibbs states of (some other) local Hamiltonians, as well as steady states of local Lindbladians. Our numerical tests suggest that the first levels of the hierarchy already provide very accurate approximations to the local properties of the quenched system, even in the thermodynamic limit. We therefore expect that our algorithm finds application in the study of quenched 2D models, for which the usual approximation methods, e.g., via tensor network states
[
38
]
fail.

Appendix D
Spin systems in the thermodynamic limit

In the following lines, we explain why Theorem
3
holds. Our starting point is the net
(\tilde{\omega}^{\kappa})_{\kappa}
, where
\tilde{\omega}^{\kappa}:=(\tilde{\omega}^{\kappa}_{[0,1]},\tilde{b}_{0}^{\kappa},\tilde{b}_{1}^{\kappa})
denotes a solution of the SDP (
134
), with
\kappa=(k_{\sigma},k_{t},n)
. We need to show that this net implies the existence of a Hilbert space
{\cal H}
, operators
\{\sigma_{a}^{(k)}:a=1,2,3\}_{k\in{\cal Z}}
and a TI state
\psi
such that conditions (
136
) and (
137
) hold.

The proof is essentially identical to that of Theorem
2
, so we just focus on solving the theoretical hurdles that appear when one tries to adapt the methods of Appendix
C
to systems with an infinite number of operator variables.

There are four such hurdles:

•

The Archimedean condition is not independently satisfied, because
\tilde{\sigma}
,
\tilde{\sigma}(0)
,
\tilde{\sigma}(1)
represent infinite sets of variables. This is not a real problem: it suffices when, for each non-commuting variable
\tilde{x}_{i}
, we have that the polynomial

K_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i}^{2}

(239)

is SOS (with respect to the set of operator constraints of the set of variables to which
\tilde{x}_{i}
belongs) for some
K_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. This is the case in our problem, since

1-(\tilde{\sigma}_{a}^{(k)})^{2},1-\tilde{\sigma}_{a}^{(k)}(0)^{2},1-\tilde{\sigma}_{a}^{(k)}(1)^{2}

(240)

are operator identities and

1-\tilde{t}^{2}=\sqrt{2}(\tilde{t}-\tilde{t}^{2})\sqrt{2}+(1-\tilde{t})^{2}.

(241)

Condition (
239
) is, indeed, enough to bound the values of
\tilde{\omega}^{\kappa}
, see the proof of
[
1
, Theorem 1]
. Like their finite-variable counterparts, these functionals are defined by their (bounded) values on a countable set of monomials. Thus, one can invoke the Banach-Alaoglu theorem as usual and deduce the existence of a subnet
(h(\kappa^{\prime}))_{\kappa^{\prime}}
such that the limit
\lim_{\kappa^{\prime}\to\infty}\tilde{\omega}^{h(\kappa^{\prime})}
exists.

•

The limiting functionals
\tilde{\omega}_{[0,1]},\tilde{\omega}_{0},\tilde{\omega}_{1}
can only evaluate polynomials spanned by products of operator variables corresponding to the qubits (
1,2,\ldots
) (and also products of
\tilde{t}
, when we deal with
\tilde{\omega}_{[0,1]}
). To arrive at translation-invariant functionals, respectively defined for all polynomials in
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{[0,1]},\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{0},\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
, we thus extend the original functionals in this form:

\tilde{\omega}_{\alpha}(p):=\tilde{\omega}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{k(p)}(p)),

(242)

where
k(p)
is any natural number
k
such that
\mathbb{T}^{k}(p)
only contains operators pertaining qubits
1,2,\ldots
. This definition is consistent due to LTI.

•

Following the proof of Theorem
2
would require applying the GNS construction to the functionals
\tilde{\omega}_{[0,1]},\tilde{\omega}_{0},\tilde{\omega}_{1}
on polynomials with infinitely many variables. This is not problematic. Moreover, since the three sets of variables are countable, the constructed Hilbert space will be separable in each case.

•

Define the polynomial

g_{a}(\tilde{\sigma}^{(k-1)},\tilde{\sigma}^{(k)},\tilde{\sigma}^{(k+1)}):=i\tau[H(\tilde{\sigma}),\tilde{\sigma}_{a}^{(k)}].

(243)

The proof of Theorem
2
relies on Lemma
4
, which in turn requires that the (classical) ODE

\displaystyle\frac{d\xi_{a}^{(k)}(t)}{dt}=g^{+}_{a}(\xi^{(k-1)}(t),\xi^{(k)}(t),\xi^{(k+1)}(t)),

\displaystyle\xi_{a}^{(k)}(\tau)=\gamma>0,

(244)

admits an analytic solution for some open ball around
\tau=0
in the complex plane. In Appendix
A
, this was dealt with by invoking the Cauchy-Kovalevskaya theorem
[
39
]
. However, this theorem does not apply here, since the vector of variables
(\xi_{a}^{(k)})_{a,k}
is infinite.

Nonetheless, we next show that eq. (
244
) does admit an analytic solution around
t=0
. Consider the simpler ODE:

\displaystyle\frac{d\xi_{a}(t)}{dt}=g^{+}_{a}(\xi(t),\xi(t),\xi(t)),a=1,2,3,

\displaystyle\xi_{a}(0)=\gamma>0,a=1,2,3.

(245)

This system of ODEs is finite dimensional, so Cauchy-Kovalevskaya applies: for some
R>0
, there exists a solution
\hat{\xi}(t)
of eq. (
245
), analytic in
|t|\leq R
. Define
\hat{\xi}_{a}^{(k)}(t):=\hat{\xi}_{a}(t)
. Then,
(\hat{\xi}_{a}^{(k)}(t))_{a,k}
is an analytic solution of (
244

\hat{\xi}_{a}^{(k)}(0)=\hat{\xi}_{a}(0)=\gamma
and

\frac{d\hat{\xi}^{(k)}_{a}(t)}{dt}=\frac{d\hat{\xi}_{a}(t)}{dt}=g^{+}_{a}(\hat{\xi}(t),\hat{\xi}(t),\hat{\xi}(t))=g^{+}_{a}(\hat{\xi}^{(k-1)}(t),\hat{\xi}^{(k)}(t),\hat{\xi}^{(k+1}(t)).

(246)

From this point on, one can follow the proof of Theorem
2
without further obstructions.
