Scattering matrix approach to dynamical Sauter-Schwinger process: Spin- and helicity-resolved momentum distributions
M. M. Majczak, K. Krajewska, J. Z. Kamiński, A. Bechler

Abstract
Dynamical Sauter-Schwinger mechanism of electron-positron pair creation by a time-dependent electric field pulses is considered using the S S -matrix approach and reduction formulas. They lead to the development of framework based on the solutions of the Dirac equation with the Feynman- or anti-Feynman boundary conditions. Their asymptotic properties are linked to the spin-resolved probability amplitudes of created pairs. The same concerns the helicity-resolved amplitudes. Most importantly, the aforementioned spin- or helicity-resolved amplitudes, when summed over spin or helicity configurations, reproduce the momentum distributions of created particles calculated with other methods that are typically used in this context. This does validate the current approach. It also allows us to investigate the vortex structures in momentum distributions of produced particles, as the method provides an access to the phase of the probability amplitude. As we also illustrate numerically, the method is applicable to arbitrary time-dependent electric fields with, in general, elliptical polarization. This proves its great flexibility.

Introduction

Control of physical processes by external electromagnetic fields is at the core of interests of modern physics. Note that, initially, these problems
were analyzed using perturbative methods. However, with technological developments that allow to generate intense fields (e.g., in laser
physics

;

clear. This has led to the development of nonperturbative methods. The most prominent example is the generation of high-order harmonics resulting
in the synthesis of short in atomic-scale and intense light pulses, which in turn has given rise to a new field of knowledge called
attoscience

(note that the generation of zeptosecond light pulses in Thomson or Compton scattering has also been
investigated

Macchi et al. 2013

Accelerator physics, in turn, allows one to build generators of coherent radiation (Free-Electron Lasers, FELs) of frequencies in the ultraviolet
and beyond

that permit, on one hand, to more accurately describe and interpret the already available observational data, and on other hand, to predict new
physical effects that could be expected in such complex situations.

One of the topics of such activity is to study the fundamental processes of Quantum Electrodynamics (QED) in the presence of strong
electromagnetic fields

The aim of this paper is to provide the method that allows to fully investigate the spin (helicity) effects in
e^{-}e^{+}

the spin-resolved (helicity-resolved) momentum distributions. To achieve this goal, our starting point is the QED scattering

;
Dyson 1949b

formula

;
Lehmann et al. 1957
;

electromagnetic field is derived. It connects the elements of the
S
-matrix with the propagator (or the Green’s function) satisfying Feynman
boundary conditions (Sec.
II
). With the help of the reduction formula and the propagator, we then determine the so-called
Feynman and anti-Feynman solutions of the Dirac equation and show how to relate their asymptotic properties in the far future and past with
the spin-resolved momentum distributions of created pairs (Sec.
III
). This is done for an arbitrary electromagnetic field,
provided that its vector potential vanishes in the future and the past. Although this condition
excludes the very often used Sauter pulse from further analysis, it is consistent with the form of the electric field generated in laser pulses.
Let us mention, however, that our theoretical description can be generalized to the case when the electromagnetic potential takes
different values in the past and in the future.

Theoretical and numerical analysis of the dynamical Sauter-Schwinger effect in a general situation
when the electric field changes rapidly in space and time (i.e., for distances and times comparable to the Compton wavelength
\lambda_{C}
and
the Compton time
\lambda_{C}/c
, respectively) is difficult and only recently some attempts have been made in this direction. The aim of our
general analysis of the scattering matrix is to show that the problems of pair creation by an external electromagnetic field cannot be reduced
to solving the Dirac equation with an arbitrarily chosen initial condition, because its form is correlated with the final states of the particles
through the asymptotic Feynman conditions (if we determine electron distributions) or anti-Feynman conditions (if we determine positron distributions).
In particular, their spin-momentum distributions significantly depend on the choice of the initial spin state. Therefore, to simplify further analysis,
the process of
e^{-}e^{+}
pair creation in a uniform in space electric pulse is discussed (Sec.
IV
). Additionally, in
order to compare the results with those obtained by other methods, we define the momentum distributions summed over electron and positron spins,
and introduce the additional normalization (Sec.
V
). The latter, however, does not follow from the scattering matrix and
reduction formulas, but is present in the method based on the DHW function (Sec.
VI
) or in the spinorial approach
(Sec.
VII
). Only after these additional steps, our results fully agree with the results obtained using other methods described
in this work. However, it is the analysis based on the scattering matrix and reduction formulas with appropriate boundary conditions imposed on
the solutions of the Dirac equation that allows to fully describe the spin (helicity) and momentum distributions of created particles. Numerical
illustrations of the spin-resolved (helicity-resolved) momentum distributions are presented in Sec.
VIII
both for linearly
(Sec.
VIII.1
) and circularly (Sec.
VIII.2
) polarized electric field pulses. For both polarizations we explore the existence of quantized vortex
structures in the momentum distributions by analyzing the phase of the corresponding probability amplitudes. Finally, in Sec.
IX

II
Reduction formulas for electron-positron pair creation

Consider the process of electron-positron pair creation from vacuum by a strong time-dependent external electromagnetic field, that
is described by the vector potential
A_{\mu}(x)
, where
x=(x^{0},{\bm{x}})=(ct,\bm{x})

The amplitude of pair creation can be written as

(2)

and

and

\lambda_{-}
and

Here, we have introduced the in-vacuum and the out-vacuum states,
|0;{\rm in}\rangle

|0;{\rm out}\rangle
, respectively.
Note that the vacuum-to-vacuum probability is not equal to unity since the time-dependent external field can create pairs, so that
|0;{\rm in}\rangle\neq|0;{\rm out}\rangle

In the language of Feynman diagrams, dividing by the vacuum-to-vacuum amplitude in Eq. (
1

S_{\mathrm{F}}(x-y)
is equal to
Itzykson and Zuber 1980
;
Bjorken and Drell 1964

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{F}}(x-y)=-\int\frac{d^{4}p}{(2\pi)^{4}}\frac{\not{p}+m_{\bf e}c}{p^{2}-(m_{\rm e}c)^{2}+i\epsilon}e^{-ip\cdot(x-y)},

(34)

with infinitesimally small and positive
\epsilon
. Note the minus sign in front of the integral, which is due to the definition of the Feynman propagator (
27
) (cf., Ref.
Białynicki-Birula and Białynicka-Birula 1975
).

The boundary conditions satisfied by
\psi^{(-)}_{\mathrm{F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(x)
look as follows: in the remote past, i.e., for
x^{0}\rightarrow-\infty
, it contains only negative frequencies, whereas in the remote future, i.e., for
x^{0}\rightarrow\infty
, it contains both the negative and positive frequencies with the negative frequency contribution given exactly by
\chi^{(-)}_{\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(x)
. Writing it explicitly, the so-called Feynman state
Białynicki-Birula and Białynicka-Birula 1975
,
\psi^{(-)}_{\mathrm{F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(x)
, fulfills the following boundary conditions,

\displaystyle\lim_{x^{0}\rightarrow-\infty}\psi^{(-)}_{\mathrm{F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(x)=\psi^{(-)}_{\mathrm{in};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(x)

(35)

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{\lambda=\pm}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}C^{(-)}_{\mathrm{F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(\bm{p},\lambda)\chi^{(-)}_{\bm{p},\lambda}(x)

and

\displaystyle\lim_{x^{0}\rightarrow\infty}\psi^{(-)}_{\mathrm{F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(x)=\chi^{(-)}_{\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(x)

(36)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\lambda=\pm}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}C^{(+)}_{\mathrm{F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(\bm{p},\lambda)\chi^{(+)}_{\bm{p},\lambda}(x),

with some complex functions,
C^{(\pm)}_{\mathrm{F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(\bm{p},\lambda)
, that determine the momentum and spin distributions
of created pairs. To show this, let us note that the Feynman state satisfies the Dirac equation (
3
), meaning that

\displaystyle-e\not{A}(x)\psi^{(-)}_{\mathrm{F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(x)=D_{x}\psi^{(-)}_{\mathrm{F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(x).

(37)

Substituting the right-hand-side of this equation into Eq. (
III
), performing integration by parts, and taking into account that

\bar{\chi}^{(+)}_{\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(x)\overleftarrow{D}_{x}=0
, one arrives at

\displaystyle{\cal A}(\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+})

(38)

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx^{0}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{0}}\int d^{3}x\bar{\chi}^{(+)}_{\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(x)\gamma^{0}\psi^{(-)}_{\mathrm{F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(x)

\displaystyle\quad=\lim_{x^{0}\rightarrow\infty}\int d^{3}x\,\bar{\chi}^{(+)}_{\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(x)\gamma^{0}\psi^{(-)}_{\mathrm{F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(x).

Note that, due to boundary condition imposed on
\psi^{(-)}_{\mathrm{F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(x)
, the lower integration limit
x^{0}\rightarrow-\infty
does not
contribute here. Finally, applying the orthogonality condition for free-particle solutions of the Dirac equation, we obtain

\displaystyle{\cal A}(\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+})=C^{(+)}_{\mathrm{F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}).

(39)

Therefore, in this formulation, we interpret the amplitude introduced in Eq. (
1
) as the conditional probability amplitude of detecting
electron of the momentum
\bm{p}_{-}
and spin polarization
\lambda_{-}
provided that positron has the momentum
\bm{p}_{+}
and spin polarization

\lambda_{+}
. Moreover, by normalizing the Feynman state in the remote past to unity we obtain the momentum and spin distribution of created
electrons,

\displaystyle F^{(+)}_{\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-})=|A^{(+)}_{\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-})|^{2},

(40)

where

\displaystyle A^{(+)}_{\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-})=\frac{C^{(+)}_{\mathrm{F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-})}{\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}}

(41)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{2}_{\mathrm{F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}=\sum_{\lambda=\pm}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}|C^{(-)}_{\mathrm{F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(\bm{p},\lambda)|^{2}.

(42)

Note that the electron distribution (
40
) is defined provided that positrons are detected with momenta
\bm{p}_{+}
and spins

\lambda_{+}
, similarly to the famous Bothe’s coincidence experiment, originally performed for Compton scattering
(see, e.g., Refs.
Bothe 1926
;
Garrison and Chiao 2008
). Hence, in order to define the electron momentum and spin distribution
irrespective of the positron parameters, we have to integrate over momenta and sum over spins of created positrons such that

\displaystyle F^{(+)}(\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-})=\sum_{\lambda_{+}=\pm}\int\frac{d^{3}{p}_{+}}{(2\pi)^{3}}F^{(+)}_{\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}).

(43)

Additionally,
C^{(-)}_{\mathrm{F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(\bm{p},\lambda)
fixes the initial profile of the positron wave packet that leads to the Feynman asymptotic conditions. This profile is
a priori
not known as it is determined by the solution of the Dirac equation in the future.

It is well-known that in quantum theory the scattering amplitude can be represented in two equivalent forms in which the exact scattering state
appears either with the out-going or in-coming spherical waves
Goldberger and Watson 1964
;
Newton 1982
. We meet a similar situation
in the case of pair production. Indeed, by rearranging Eq. (
III
) we arrive at

\displaystyle{\cal A}(\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+})

(44)

\displaystyle\quad=-i\int d^{4}x\bar{\psi}^{(+)}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(x)e\not{A}(x)\chi^{(-)}_{\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(x).

This expression for the probability amplitude contains Dirac conjugation of the so-called anti-Feynman wave function
Białynicki-Birula and Białynicka-Birula 1975

\psi^{(+)}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(x)
given by

\displaystyle\psi^{(+)}_{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(x)

\displaystyle=\chi^{(+)}_{\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(x)

(45)

\displaystyle-\int d^{4}y\,K_{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}}(x,y)e\not{A}(y)\chi^{(+)}_{\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(y).

Here, the anti-Feynman propagator of the Dirac equation,
K_{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}}(x,y)
, is defined as

\displaystyle K_{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}}(x,y)=\gamma^{0}K^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{F}}(y,x)\gamma^{0},

(46)

or

\displaystyle K_{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}}(x,y)=-i\frac{\langle 0;{\rm in}|\hat{\bar{T}}[\hat{\Psi}(x)\bar{\hat{\Psi}}(y)]|0;{\rm out}\rangle}{\langle 0;{\rm in}|0;{\rm out}\rangle},

(47)

with
\hat{\bar{T}}
denoting anti-chronological ordering. Now, the anti-Feynman wave function fulfills the integral equation
[here,
S_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}}}(x-y)=\gamma^{0}S^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{F}}(y-x)\gamma^{0}
, similarly to Eq. (
46
)],

\displaystyle\psi^{(+)}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(x)

\displaystyle=\chi^{(+)}_{\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(x)

(48)

\displaystyle-\int d^{4}y\,S_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}}}(x-y)e\not{A}(y)\psi^{(+)}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(y),

along with the boundary conditions: in the remote past it contains only solutions of the Dirac equation with positive frequencies,
whereas in the remote future it contains both positive and negative frequencies with the positive frequency part exactly equal to
\chi^{(+)}_{\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(x)
. More explicitly,

\displaystyle\lim_{x^{0}\rightarrow-\infty}\psi^{(+)}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(x)=\psi^{(+)}_{\mathrm{in};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(x)

(49)

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{\lambda=\pm}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}C^{(+)}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(\bm{p},\lambda)\chi^{(+)}_{\bm{p},\lambda}(x)

and

\displaystyle\lim_{x^{0}\rightarrow\infty}\psi^{(+)}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(x)=\chi^{(+)}_{\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(x)

(50)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\lambda=\pm}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}C^{(-)}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(\bm{p},\lambda)\chi^{(-)}_{\bm{p},\lambda}(x).

Next, using the relation

\displaystyle-\bar{\psi}^{(+)}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(x)e\not{A}(x)=\bar{\psi}^{(+)}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(x)\overleftarrow{D}_{x},

(51)

and performing similar steps as above for the Feynman state, we obtain another equivalent expression for the amplitude of pair creation in the form of a scalar product in remote future,

\displaystyle{\cal A}(\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+})

(52)

\displaystyle\quad=-\lim_{x^{0}\rightarrow\infty}\int d^{3}x\,\bar{\psi}^{(+)}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(x)\gamma^{0}\chi^{(-)}_{\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(x)

\displaystyle\quad=-C^{(-)}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}).

This time, the amplitude
{\cal A}(\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+})
can be interpreted as the conditional probability amplitude
for the creation of positrons of the momentum
\bm{p}_{+}
and spin
\lambda_{+}
provided that electrons are created with the momentum
\bm{p}_{-}

and spin
\lambda_{-}
. Similarly, by normalizing to unity the initial state
\psi^{(+)}_{\mathrm{in};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(x)
in Eq. (
49
),
we define the conditional momentum and spin distribution for created positrons,

\displaystyle F^{(-)}_{\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+})=|A^{(-)}_{\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+})|^{2},

(53)

where

\displaystyle A^{(-)}_{\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+})=-\frac{C^{(-)}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+})}{\mathcal{N}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}}

(54)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{2}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}=\sum_{\lambda=\pm}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}|C^{(+)}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(\bm{p},\lambda)|^{2}.

(55)

Similar to Eq. (
43
), we can also define the positron distribution,

\displaystyle F^{(-)}(\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+})=\sum_{\lambda_{-}=\pm}\int\frac{d^{3}{p}_{-}}{(2\pi)^{3}}F^{(-)}_{\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}),

(56)

that is irrespective of the electron spin and momentum characteristics. Moreover,
C^{(+)}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(\bm{p},\lambda)
fixes
the profile of the initial electron wave packet, which is also
a priori
unknown, as it has to be chosen such that the solution evolves
to the anti-Feynman asymptotic conditions in the future.

While the above considerations are very general, in the following sections we focus on the case when the
e^{-}e^{+}
pair creation occurs in a spatially homogeneous electric field.

IV
Integral equations

In this section, we analyze in detail the integral equations (
33
) and (
48
) in the case of a spatially homogeneous
electromagnetic field, i.e., assuming that the vector potential depends only on time,
A(x^{0})=(0,\bm{A}(x^{0}))
.
For the spatially homogeneous vector potential, the wave function
\psi^{(-)}_{\mathrm{F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(x)
can be written as

\psi^{(-)}_{\mathrm{F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(x)=e^{-i\bm{p}_{+}\cdot\bm{x}}\Phi^{(-)}_{\mathrm{F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(x^{0}).

(57)

Using further Eq. (
14
) one can write Eq. (
33
) as

\displaystyle\Phi^{(-)}_{\mathrm{F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(x^{0})=e^{i{p}_{+}^{0}x^{0}}u^{(-)}_{\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}

(58)

\displaystyle\quad-\int d^{4}yS_{\mathrm{F}}(x-y)e\not{A}(y^{0})e^{i\bm{p}_{+}\cdot(\bm{x}-\bm{y})}\Phi^{(-)}_{\mathrm{F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(y^{0}).

Substituting here the integral representation of the free Feynman propagator,

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{F}}(x-y)

(59)

\displaystyle\quad=i\theta(x^{0}-y^{0})\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{\not{k}+m_{\rm e}c}{2k^{0}}e^{-ik\cdot(x-y)}

\displaystyle\quad-i\theta(y^{0}-x^{0})\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{\not{k}-m_{\rm e}c}{2k^{0}}e^{ik\cdot(x-y)},

where
k=(k^{0},\,\bm{k})
with
k^{0}=\sqrt{\bm{k}^{2}+(m_{\rm e}c)^{2}}
, we obtain the integral equation for the Feynman-type function

\displaystyle\Phi^{(-)}_{\mathrm{F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(x^{0})=e^{i{p}_{+}^{0}x^{0}}u^{(-)}_{\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}

(60)

\displaystyle\quad-ie^{-i{p}_{+}^{0}x^{0}}\frac{\not{p}_{+}^{\prime}+m_{\rm e}c}{2{p}_{+}^{0}}\int_{-\infty}^{x^{0}}dy^{0}e^{i{p}_{+}^{0}y^{0}}e\not{A}(y^{0})\Phi^{(-)}_{\mathrm{F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(y^{0})

\displaystyle\quad+ie^{i{p}_{+}^{0}x^{0}}\frac{\not{p}_{+}-m_{\rm e}x}{2{p}_{+}^{0}}\int_{x^{0}}^{\infty}dy^{0}e^{-i{p}_{+}^{0}y^{0}}e\not{A}(y^{0})\Phi^{(-)}_{\mathrm{F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(y^{0}),

where
{p}_{+}=({p}_{+}^{0},\bm{p}_{+})
and
{p}_{+}^{\prime}=({p}_{+}^{0},-\bm{p}_{+})
.

For the anti-Feynman wave function
\psi^{(+)}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(x)
, we take

\psi^{(+)}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(x)=e^{i\bm{p}_{-}\cdot\bm{x}}\Phi^{(+)}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(x^{0}),

(61)

where
\bm{p}_{-}
is now interpreted as the electron momentum. Moreover, in the integral equation (
48
) we use

\displaystyle S_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}}}(x-y)=\gamma^{0}S^{\dagger}_{\textrm{F}}(y-x)\gamma^{0}

(62)

\displaystyle\quad=i\theta(x^{0}-y^{0})\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{\not{k}-m_{\rm e}c}{2k^{0}}e^{ik\cdot(x-y)}

\displaystyle\quad-i\theta(y^{0}-x^{0})\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{\not{k}+m_{\rm e}c}{2k^{0}}e^{-ik\cdot(x-y)}.

After performing in Eq. (
48
) the integrals over
\bm{y}
and
\bm{k}
, for the anti-Feynman function we obtain that

\displaystyle\Phi^{(+)}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(x^{0})=e^{-ip^{0}_{-}x^{0}}u^{(+)}_{\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}

(63)

\displaystyle\quad-ie^{i{p}_{-}^{0}x^{0}}\frac{\not{p}_{-}^{\prime}-m_{\rm e}c}{2{p}_{-}^{0}}\int_{-\infty}^{x^{0}}dy^{0}e^{-i{p}_{-}^{0}y^{0}}e\not{A}(y^{0})\Phi^{(+)}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(y^{0})

\displaystyle\quad+ie^{-i{p}_{-}^{0}x^{0}}\frac{\not{p}_{-}+m_{\rm e}c}{2{p}_{-}^{0}}\int_{x^{0}}^{\infty}dy^{0}e^{i{p}_{-}^{0}y^{0}}e\not{A}(y^{0})\Phi^{(+)}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(y^{0}),

where
{p}_{-}=({p}_{-}^{0},\bm{p}_{-})
and
{p}_{-}^{\prime}=({p}_{-}^{0},-\bm{p}_{-})
.

Note that the boundary conditions satisfied by the Feynman and anti-Feynman wave functions can be deduced from Eqs. (
60
) and (
63
).
The Feynman-type wave function
\Phi_{{\rm F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}^{(-)}(x^{0})
in the remote past takes the form,

\displaystyle\Phi^{(-)}_{{\rm F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(x^{0})\big\lvert_{x^{0}\rightarrow-\infty}\sim e^{i{p}_{+}^{0}x^{0}}u^{(-)}_{\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}

(64)

\displaystyle\quad+i\mathrm{e}^{i{p}_{+}^{0}x^{0}}\frac{\not{p}_{+}-m_{\rm e}c}{2{p}_{+}^{0}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy^{0}{\rm e}^{-i{p}_{+}^{0}y^{0}}e\not{A}(y^{0})\Phi^{(-)}_{{\rm F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(y^{0}),

i.e., it contains only solutions of the free Dirac equation with negative frequencies. On the other hand, in the far future, we have

\displaystyle\Phi^{(-)}_{{\rm F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(x^{0})\big\lvert_{x^{0}\rightarrow\infty}\sim e^{i{p}_{+}^{0}x^{0}}u^{(-)}_{\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}

(65)

\displaystyle\quad-ie^{-i{p}_{+}^{0}x^{0}}\frac{\not{p}_{+}^{\prime}+m_{\rm e}c}{2{p}_{+}^{0}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy^{0}e^{i{p}_{+}^{0}y^{0}}e\not{A}(y^{0})\Phi^{(-)}_{{\rm F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(y^{0}).

Thus, the Feynman-type wave function is a combination of the solutions of the free Dirac equation with negative and positive frequencies and with prescribed negative
frequency part given by
e^{i{p}_{+}^{0}x^{0}}u^{(-)}_{\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}
; the latter corresponding to the created positron with the given momentum and spin.
The anti-Feynman wave function
\Phi^{(+)}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(x^{0})
in the remote past contains only positive frequency part,

\displaystyle\Phi^{(+)}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(x^{0})\big|_{x^{0}\rightarrow-\infty}\sim e^{-i{p}_{-}^{0}x^{0}}u^{(+)}_{\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}

(66)

\displaystyle\quad+ie^{-i{p}_{-}^{0}x^{0}}\frac{\not{p}_{-}+m_{\rm e}c}{2{p}_{-}^{0}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy^{0}e^{i{p}_{-}^{0}y^{0}}e\not{A}(y^{0})\Phi^{(+)}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(y^{0}).

In the remote future, it contains both positive and negative frequency parts with the positive frequency contribution given by

e^{-i{p}_{-}^{0}x^{0}}u^{(+)}_{\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}
, corresponding to the given momentum and spin of the created electron,

\displaystyle\Phi^{(+)}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(x^{0})\big|_{x^{0}\rightarrow\infty}\sim e^{-i{p}_{-}^{0}x^{0}}u^{(+)}_{\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}

(67)

\displaystyle\quad-ie^{i{p}_{-}^{0}x^{0}}\frac{\not{p}_{-}^{\prime}-m_{\rm e}c}{2{p}_{-}^{0}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy^{0}e^{-i{p}_{-}^{0}y^{0}}e\not{A}(y^{0})\Phi^{(+)}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(y^{0}).

As we will demonstrate next, the above asymptotic conditions for the Feynman and anti-Feynman states allow us to define
the momentum and spin distributions of created particles.

V
Pair creation amplitudes and momentum distributions

The Feynman-type wave function
\Phi^{(-)}_{{\rm F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(x^{0})
fulfills the Dirac equation in the form obtained after separating
the coordinate dependent factor
e^{-i\bm{p}_{+}\cdot\bm{x}}
,

\displaystyle[-i\gamma^{0}\partial_{0}-\bm{\gamma}\cdot\bm{p}_{+}+e\not{A}(x^{0})+m_{\rm e}c]\Phi^{(-)}_{F;\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(x^{0})=0.

(68)

For the purpose of detailed analysis of the time evolution, the wave function is decomposed into free solutions of the Dirac equation,

\displaystyle\Phi^{(-)}_{{\rm F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(x^{0})=\sum_{\lambda=\pm}

\displaystyle\bigl[C^{(+)}_{{\rm F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+},\lambda}(x^{0})u^{(+)}_{-\bm{p}_{+},\lambda}e^{-i{p}_{+}^{0}x^{0}}

(69)

\displaystyle+C^{(-)}_{{\rm F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+},\lambda}(x^{0})u^{(-)}_{\bm{p}_{+},\lambda}e^{i{p}_{+}^{0}x^{0}}\bigr].

Momentum of the Dirac bispinors corresponding to components with positive frequency has been chosen as
-\bm{p}_{+}
since
\bm{p}_{+}
is interpreted as the positron momentum so that the electron momentum is equal to
\bm{p}_{-}=-\bm{p}_{+}
. It follows from the asymptotic conditions analyzed in the previous section, Eqs. (
64
) and (
65
), that

\displaystyle C^{(+)}_{{\rm F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+},\lambda}(-\infty)=0,\,C^{(-)}_{{\rm F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+},\lambda}(\infty)=\delta_{\lambda_{+}\lambda}.

(70)

Pair creation amplitude can be now obtained from the formula (
38
) by substituting Eq. (
57
) and using the explicit form of the free solution
\chi^{(+)}_{\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(x)
. This gives

\displaystyle{\cal A}(\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+})=(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(\bm{p}_{-}+\bm{p}_{+})

(71)

\displaystyle\quad\times e^{i{p}_{-}^{0}x^{0}}\bar{u}^{(+)}_{\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}\gamma^{0}\Phi^{(-)}_{{\rm F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(x^{0})\big\lvert_{x^{0}\rightarrow\infty},

where the momentum conservation delta is due to spatial integration in Eq. (
38
). In this version of the amplitude, momentum and polarization of the positron are fixed and we determine polarization of the electron (its momentum is opposite to the positron momentum). From the asymptotic condition in remote future (
70
) we have

\displaystyle\Phi^{(-)}_{{\rm F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}(x^{0})\big\lvert_{x^{0}\rightarrow\infty}=u^{(-)}_{\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}e^{i{p}_{+}^{0}x^{0}}

(72)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\lambda=\pm}C^{(+)}_{{\rm F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+},\lambda}(x^{0})\big\lvert_{x^{0}\rightarrow\infty}u^{(+)}_{-\bm{p}_{+},\lambda}{\rm e}^{-i{p}_{+}^{0}x^{0}}.

After substituting Eq. (
72
) into Eq. (
71
), we obtain

\displaystyle{\cal A}(\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+})=(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(\bm{p}_{-}+\bm{p}_{+})C^{(+)}_{{\rm F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+},\lambda_{-}}(\infty).

(73)

This is the analogue of Eq. (
39
) for the time-dependent electric field. Further, the time-evolution of the coefficients
C^{(+)}_{{\rm F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+},\lambda}(x^{0})
and
C^{(-)}_{{\rm F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+},\lambda}(x^{0})
is analyzed by substituting Eq. (
69
) into Eq. (
68
) and solving the resulting system of equations with the asymptotic conditions (
70
).

In order to define the momentum distributions of created electrons per unit volume let us introduce the norm of the initial positron state,

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{N}}^{2}_{{\rm F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}=\sum_{\lambda=\pm}|C^{(-)}_{{\rm F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+},\lambda}(-\infty)|^{2}.

(74)

Introducing now the ‘renormalized’ amplitude,

\displaystyle A^{(+)}_{\lambda_{+}}(\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-})=\frac{1}{\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{{\rm F};-\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{+}}}C^{(+)}_{{\rm F};-\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{+},\lambda_{-}}(\infty),

(75)

with the replacement
\bm{p}_{+}=-\bm{p}_{-}
, we obtain the momentum and spin distribution for created electrons per unit volume,
f^{(+)}_{\lambda_{+}}(\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-})
, provided that positrons are created with the spin
\lambda_{+}
,

\displaystyle f^{(+)}_{\lambda_{+}}(\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-})

\displaystyle=|A^{(+)}_{\lambda_{+}}(\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-})|^{2}

(76)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{3}{p}_{+}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{|{\cal A}(\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+})|^{2}}{V\tilde{\mathcal{N}}^{2}_{{\rm F};\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}}}.

Here, the volume
V
has been introduced by applying the standard procedure,

\displaystyle[(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(\bm{p}_{+}+\bm{p}_{-})]^{2}\longrightarrow V(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(\bm{p}_{+}+\bm{p}_{-}),

(77)

which is well-known in scattering theory. In addition, we introduce the spin-independent momentum distribution for created electrons,

\displaystyle f^{(+)}(\bm{p}_{-})=\sum_{\lambda_{+},\lambda_{-}=\pm}f^{(+)}_{\lambda_{+}}(\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}).

(78)

Note that the above normalization of probability distributions does not follow from the
S
-matrix theory. We have introduced it exclusively
to provide a meaningful comparison with the distributions calculated by other methods, which will be presented in the next sections.

For the anti-Feynman states we proceed in the same way. The function
\Phi^{(+)}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(x^{0})

is presented in the form

\displaystyle\Phi^{(+)}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}(x^{0})=\sum_{\lambda=\pm}

\displaystyle\bigl[C^{(+)}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-},\lambda}(x^{0})u^{(+)}_{\bm{p}_{-},\lambda}e^{-i{p}_{-}^{0}x^{0}}

(79)

\displaystyle+C^{(-)}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-},\lambda}(x^{0})u^{(-)}_{-\bm{p}_{-},\lambda}e^{i{p}_{-}^{0}x^{0}}\bigr],

but now with the asymptotic conditions,

\displaystyle C^{(-)}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-},\lambda}(-\infty)=0,\,C^{(+)}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-},\lambda}(\infty)=\delta_{\lambda_{-}\lambda}.

(80)

Introducing the normalization constant for the state in the remote past,

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{N}}^{2}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-}}=\sum_{\lambda=\pm}|C^{(+)}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};\bm{p}_{-},\lambda_{-},\lambda}(-\infty)|^{2},

(81)

we define the ‘renormalized’ amplitude,

\displaystyle A^{(-)}_{\lambda_{-}}(\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+})=-\frac{1}{\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};-\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{-}}}C^{(-)}_{{\raisebox{3.98611pt}{\rotatebox{180.0}{{\scriptsize F}}}};-\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{-},\lambda_{+}}(\infty).

(82)

Hence, we arrive at the momentum and spin distribution for created positrons per unit volume,
f^{(-)}_{\lambda_{-}}(\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+})
, provided that electrons are created with the spin
\lambda_{-}
,

\displaystyle f^{(-)}_{\lambda_{-}}(\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+})=|A^{(-)}_{\lambda_{-}}(\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+})|^{2}.

(83)

Further, by summing up over the final spin states of electrons and positrons we obtain the momentum distribution of created positrons,

\displaystyle f^{(-)}(\bm{p}_{+})=\sum_{\lambda_{-},\lambda_{+}=\pm}f^{(-)}_{\lambda_{-}}(\bm{p}_{+},\lambda_{+}).

(84)

Note that for space-homogeneous electric fields it holds that
f^{(-)}(\bm{p})=f^{(+)}(-\bm{p})
.

In summary of theoretical analysis presented in Secs.
II
-
V
, we note that, although the momentum
and spin distributions of created
e^{-}e^{+}
pairs are determined by the solutions of the Dirac equation, they are not related to the
‘
initial value problem
’. In the considered case, they have to satisfy the asymptotic conditions both in the remote past and future,
that are imposed either on electrons in the past and positrons in the future (Feynman states), or on positrons in the past and electrons
in the future (anti-Feynman states). Therefore, determination of the momentum and spin distributions in the dynamical Sauter-Schwinger
process cannot be reduced to the solution of the Dirac equation with some initial conditions. In particular, the analysis carried out in these
sections shows that, if in the future we have positrons with the precisely defined momentum and spin state, this determines unambiguously the
positron state in the past. It also means that the initial positron state cannot be chosen arbitrarily (the same is true for electrons). Therefore, it can be concluded that in the dynamical Sauter-Schwinger process ’
the future determines the past
’ (see also the discussion presented in the book
Białynicki-Birula and Białynicka-Birula 1975
, cf. Sec. 17, and in the original Feynman’s papers
Feynman 1949a
;
Feynman 1949b
). This severely complicates the numerical analysis of the pair creation by an arbitrary space- and time-dependent electromagnetic fields. For this reason other approaches have been developed that try to surpass these difficulties, and which so far have been successfully applied only for time-dependent and homogeneous electric fields. We mean here the Dirac-Heisenberg-Wigner (DHW) function approach (or, equivalently, the bispinorial one
Bechler et al. 2023
), originally introduced in Ref.
Białynicki-Birula et al. 1991
for the Dirac equation (for the nonrelativistic Schrödinger equation, see Ref.
Białynicki-Birula et al. 1992
). Or, the earlier developed method that we call the spinorial method (see, e.g., Refs.
Białynicki-Birula and Rudnicki 2011
;
Kamiński et al. 2018
), which is equivalent to the one based on the quantum Vlasov equation
Blaschke et al. 2013
;
Krajewska and Kamiński 2019a
. Both are shortly discussed in the following two sections.

VI
The DHW-function approach

The DHW-function for the fermion field
\hat{\Psi}(t,{\bm{x}})
is defined as

\displaystyle W_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle(t,\bm{x},\bm{p})=-\frac{1}{2}\int d^{3}s\,e^{-i\bm{p}\cdot\bm{s}}

(85)

\displaystyle\times\langle 0|\mathcal{U}(t,\bm{s},\bm{x})[\hat{\Psi}_{\mu}(t,\bm{x}+\bm{s}/2),\hat{\Psi}^{\dagger}_{\nu}(t,\bm{x}-\bm{s}/2)]|0\rangle,

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(t,\bm{s},\bm{x})=\exp\Bigl[-ie\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}d\xi\,\bm{s}\cdot\bm{A}(t,\bm{x}+\xi\bm{s})\Bigr].

(86)

It can be decomposed in terms of the Dirac matrices (for the definition of the Dirac matrices
\gamma^{\mu}
and the related ones which are used here,
see Ref.
Bechler et al. 2023
),

\displaystyle W(t,\bm{x},\bm{p})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}(f_{0}+\gamma_{5}f_{1}-i\gamma^{0}\gamma_{5}f_{2}+\gamma^{0}f_{3}

(87)

\displaystyle+\bm{\Sigma}\cdot\bm{g}_{0}+\bm{\alpha}\cdot\bm{g}_{1}-i\bm{\gamma}\cdot\bm{g}_{2}+\gamma^{0}\bm{\Sigma}\cdot\bm{g}_{3}),

with sixteen dimensionless expansion coefficients. For a spatially homogeneous electric field only ten functions:

f_{3},\,\bm{g}_{0},\,\bm{g}_{1},\,\bm{g}_{2}
survive. Denoting by
W
the ten-dimensional column vector (the superscript
T
below means the transposition),

\displaystyle W(t,\bm{p})=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}f_{3}(t,\bm{p}),\,\bm{g}_{0}(t,\bm{p}),\,\bm{g}_{1}(t,\bm{p}),\,\bm{g}_{2}(t,\bm{p})\end{array}\right]^{T},

we can write equations fulfilled by the expansion coefficients in the compact matrix form

\displaystyle\dot{W}(t,\bm{p}(t))=M(\bm{p}(t))W(t,\bm{p}(t)),

(89)

where
\bm{p}(t)=\bm{p}-e\bm{A}(t)
and dot denotes complete time derivative. The matrix
M
has the following block structure,

M(\bm{p})=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}0&\bm{0}^{T}&\bm{0}^{T}&2c\bm{p}^{T}\\
\bm{0}&\mathds{O}_{3}&2c\bm{p}\times&\mathds{O}_{3}\\
\bm{0}&2c\bm{p}\times&\mathds{O}_{3}&-2m_{\mathrm{e}}c^{2}\mathds{I}_{3}\\
-2c\bm{p}&\mathds{O}_{3}&2m_{\mathrm{e}}c^{2}\mathds{I}_{3}&\mathds{O}_{3}\end{array}\right],

(90)

where the symbol
\bm{p}\times
means the vector product with the corresponding
\bm{g}_{0}
or
\bm{g}_{1}
vector functions.

Note that the ten-dimensional vector
W
[Eq. (
VI
)] can be also constructed by means of so-called bispinorial representation of DHW function
Bechler et al. 2023

\displaystyle{\cal V}(x^{0},\bm{p})=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathrm{Tr}(\gamma^{0}A_{\bm{p}}(x^{0}))\\
\mathrm{Tr}(\bm{\Sigma}A_{\bm{p}}(x^{0}))\\
\mathrm{Tr}(\bm{\alpha}A_{\bm{p}}(x^{0}))\\
\mathrm{Tr}(-i\bm{\gamma}A_{\bm{p}}(x^{0}))\end{array}\right],

where
A_{\bm{p}}
is the following
4\times 4
matrix,

\displaystyle A_{\bm{p}}(x^{0})=\sum_{\lambda}\Phi_{\bm{p},\lambda}(x^{0})\Phi^{\dagger}_{\bm{p},\lambda}(x^{0}),

(95)

and
\Phi_{\bm{p},\lambda}(x^{0})
is implicitly defined in Eq. (
57
) for the Feynman- and in Eq. (
61
) for the anti-Feynman boundary condition.
It has been shown in Ref.
Bechler et al. 2023
that the vector
{\cal V}
fulfills the same system of differential equations as (
89
). Moreover,
its form for
x^{0}\rightarrow-\infty
is the same as the vacuum value of
W

Białynicki-Birula et al. 1991
,

\displaystyle W^{\rm vac}(\bm{p})=\frac{2}{p^{0}}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}-m_{e}c\\
\bm{0}\\
-\bm{p}\\
\bm{0}\end{array}\right],

provided that

\displaystyle A_{\bm{p}}(x^{0})|_{x^{0}\rightarrow-\infty}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}\Phi^{(-)}_{F;\bm{p},\lambda}(x^{0})\Phi^{(-){\dagger}}_{F;\bm{p},\lambda}(x^{0})\bigr|_{x^{0}\rightarrow-\infty}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}u^{(-)}_{-\bm{p},\lambda}u^{(-){\dagger}}_{-\bm{p},\lambda}.

(100)

The latter corresponding to the Feynman-type boundary condition in the past. Since
W
and
{\cal V}
fulfill the same system of differential equations and,
with the Feynman-type boundary condition in the past, these two vectors have the same initial values, therefore
{\cal V}=W
. This shows explicitly the connection
of the DHW-function approach with the boundary conditions exploited in this paper.

Basically, Eq. (
89
) could be used for numerical analysis, e.g., in the case of circularly or elliptically polarized field. However, it has been argued in Ref.
Blinne and Gies 2014
that there is a serious precision problem and reformulation of the equations is required. The idea is to decompose
W
in the following manner

\displaystyle W=(e_{1}\cdot W)e_{1}+\mathcal{P}W,

(101)

where
e_{1}
is the four-vector proportional to the vacuum value of
W
,

\displaystyle e_{1}=-\frac{1}{2}W^{\mathrm{vac}}=\dfrac{1}{p^{0}}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}m_{\mathrm{e}}c,\,\bm{0},\,\bm{p},\,\bm{0}\end{array}\right]^{T},

\mathcal{P}
is the projection matrix onto the nine-dimensional subspace orthogonal to
e_{1}
, and
p^{0}=(\bm{p}^{2}+m_{\mathrm{e}}^{2}c^{2})^{1/2}
. Next, we express the scalar product in Eq. (
101
) by one-particle distribution function per unit volume,
f^{(+)}_{\rm W}(t,\bm{p})
,

\displaystyle e_{1}\cdot W=\frac{m_{\mathrm{e}}cf_{3}}{p^{0}}+\frac{\bm{p}\cdot\bm{g}_{1}}{p^{0}}=2(f_{\rm W}^{(+)}-1).

(103)

Also, following Ref.
Blinne and Gies 2014
, we write part of
W
orthogonal to
e_{1}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}W=\mathcal{T}w_{9},

(104)

where
\mathcal{T}
is the
10\times 9
matrix such that
e_{1}^{T}\mathcal{T}=0_{9}^{T}
(
0_{9}
denotes nine-dimensional column null vector).
Here, the vector
w_{9}
has nine components,

\displaystyle w_{9}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\bm{v}_{1},\,\bm{v}_{2},\,\bm{v}_{3}\end{array}\right]^{T}.

As a result, Eq. (
101
) becomes

\displaystyle W=2(f_{\mathrm{W}}^{(+)}-1)e_{1}+\mathcal{T}w_{9}.

(106)

Note that the choice of matrix
\mathcal{T}
fulfilling condition
e_{1}^{T}\mathcal{T}=0_{9}^{T}
is not unique. Here, we use its form in analogy with Ref.
Blinne and Gies 2014
, adjusted to our ordering of the DHW-coefficients,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}-\dfrac{\bm{p}^{T}}{m_{\mathrm{e}}c}&\bm{0}^{T}&\bm{0}^{T}\\
\mathds{O}_{3}&\mathds{I}_{3}&\mathds{O}_{3}\\
\mathds{I}_{3}&\mathds{O}_{3}&\mathds{O}_{3}\\
\mathds{O}_{3}&\mathds{O}_{3}&\mathds{I}_{3}\end{array}\right],

where
\mathds{O}_{3}
and
\mathds{I}_{3}
denote respectively the
3\times 3
null and identity matrices. After some algebra it leads to the following system of differential equations,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\dot{f}_{\rm W}^{(+)}=\frac{e\bm{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\bm{v}_{1}}{2p^{0}},\\
&\dot{\bm{v}}_{1}=\frac{2e}{(p^{0})^{3}}[\bm{p}(\bm{p}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{E}})-(p^{0})^{2}\bm{\mathcal{E}}](f_{\mathrm{W}}^{(+)}-1)\\
&\quad-\frac{e\bm{p}(\bm{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\bm{v}_{1})}{(p^{0})^{2}}+2c\bm{p}\times\bm{v}_{2}-2m_{\mathrm{e}}c^{2}\bm{v}_{3},\\
&\dot{\bm{v}}_{2}=2c\bm{p}\times\bm{v}_{1},\\
&\dot{\bm{v}}_{3}=\frac{2}{m_{\mathrm{e}}}[\bm{p}(\bm{p}\cdot\bm{v}_{1})+(m_{\mathrm{e}}c)^{2}\bm{v}_{1}],\end{split}

(111)

where
\bm{p}
has to be replaced by
\bm{p}(t)
and
\dot{\bm{p}}(t)=-e\dot{\bm{A}}(t)=e\bm{\mathcal{E}}(t)
was used. The initial conditions for
t\rightarrow-\infty
are

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{W}}^{\mathrm{in}}=0,\quad\bm{v}_{1}^{\mathrm{in}}=\bm{v}_{2}^{\mathrm{in}}=\bm{v}_{3}^{\mathrm{in}}=\bm{0},

(112)

where the vector potential vanishes and
\bm{p}(t)\rightarrow\bm{p}
, with
\bm{p}
being the momentum of the created electron. These equations are the same as those derived and discussed in Refs.
Blinne and Gies 2014
;
Blinne and Strobel 2016
;
Li et al. 2017
;
Hu et al. 2023
.

It has to be stressed that Eqs. (
111
) have been derived under the assumption of Feynman-type boundary conditions. For anti-Feynman boundary conditions, the Dirac wave function in the past contains only positive frequencies, i.e.,

\displaystyle\psi(t,\bm{x})|_{t\rightarrow-\infty}=e^{-icp^{0}t+i\bm{p}\cdot\bm{x}}u^{(+)}_{\bm{p},\lambda}.

(113)

Hence,

\displaystyle W^{\mathrm{vac}}=\sum_{\lambda}u^{(+){\dagger}}_{\bm{p},\lambda}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\gamma^{0}\\
\bm{\Sigma}\\
\bm{\alpha}\\
-i\bm{\gamma}\end{array}\right]u^{(+)}_{\bm{p},\lambda}=\frac{2}{p^{0}}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}m_{\mathrm{e}}c\\
\bm{0}\\
\bm{p}\\
\bm{0}\end{array}\right],

which is a negative ”Feynman-type” vacuum condition. The one-particle distribution function per unit volume,
f_{\mathrm{W}}^{(-)}(t,\bm{p})
, is now given by

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{W}}^{(-)}=\frac{1}{2}(e_{1}\cdot W-e_{1}\cdot W^{\mathrm{vac}})=\frac{m_{\mathrm{e}}cf_{3}+\bm{p}\cdot\bm{g}_{1}}{2p^{0}}-1,

(122)

whereas for the ”Feynman-type” vacuum conditions we had
+1
, since in that case
(1/2)e_{1}\cdot W^{\mathrm{vac}}=-1
. Due to this change of sign, Eq. (
106
) has to be replaced by

\displaystyle W=2(f_{\mathrm{W}}^{(-)}+1)e_{1}+\mathcal{T}w_{9}.

(123)

This leads to the system of differential equations,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\dot{f}_{\rm W}^{(-)}=\frac{e\bm{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\bm{v}_{1}}{2p^{0}},\\
&\dot{\bm{v}}_{1}=\frac{2e}{(p^{0})^{3}}[\bm{p}(\bm{p}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{E}})-(p^{0})^{2}\bm{\mathcal{E}}](f_{\mathrm{W}}^{(-)}+1)\\
&\quad-\frac{e\bm{p}(\bm{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\bm{v}_{1})}{(p^{0})^{2}}+2c\bm{p}\times\bm{v}_{2}-2m_{\mathrm{e}}c^{2}\bm{v}_{3},\\
&\dot{\bm{v}}_{2}=2c\bm{p}\times\bm{v}_{1},\\
&\dot{\bm{v}}_{3}=\frac{2}{m_{\mathrm{e}}}[\bm{p}(\bm{p}\cdot\bm{v}_{1})+(m_{\mathrm{e}}c)^{2}\bm{v}_{1}].\end{split}

(124)

with the same initial conditions (
112
), but now
-\bm{p}
is the momentum of the created positron.

The functions
f_{\mathrm{W}}^{(\pm)}(t,\bm{p})
and
f^{(\pm)}(\bm{p}_{\mp})
, the latter defined by Eqs. (
78
) and (
84
), are related with each other. Namely,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}f_{\mathrm{W}}^{(\pm)}(t,\pm\bm{p})=f^{(\pm)}(\bm{p}),

(125)

which is going to be the topic of our numerical analysis. Moreover, identical equations can be derived by using the bispinorial approach
Bechler et al. 2023
.

Concluding this section, we note that only for the space homogeneous electric field the determination of the spin summed up momentum distributions of electrons or positrons can be reduced to the initial value problem, provided that extra normalizations (
75
) or (
82
) are applied. However, already for the spin-resolved distributions one has to account for the Feynman or anti-Feynman asymptotic conditions that couple ‘the future to the past’, i.e., depending on spins and momenta of created pairs the initial state has to be prepared in a particular well-defined spin state.

VII
The spinorial approach

Eqs. (
111
) or (
124
) can be further simplified for the linearly polarized electric field,
\bm{\mathcal{E}}(t)=\mathcal{E}(t)\bm{n}
,
where
\bm{n}
is a constant unit vector. Then, one can show that instead of solving the system of ten differential equations the problem can be
reduced to the solution of two equations for complex amplitudes. This system of equations has the form similar to the precession of spin 1/2
in a time-dependent magnetic field,

\displaystyle i\frac{d}{dt}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}c_{\bm{p}}^{(+)}(t)\\
c_{\bm{p}}^{(-)}(t)\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\omega_{\bm{p}}(t)&i\Omega_{\bm{p}}(t)\\
-i\Omega_{\bm{p}}(t)&-\omega_{\bm{p}}(t)\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}c_{\bm{p}}^{(+)}(t)\\
c_{\bm{p}}^{(-)}(t)\end{array}\right],

where

\displaystyle\omega_{\bm{p}}(t)=c\sqrt{\bm{p}_{\perp}^{2}+[p_{\|}-eA(t)]^{2}+m_{\mathrm{e}}^{2}c^{2}}

(132)

and

\displaystyle\Omega_{\bm{p}}(t)=\frac{c\epsilon_{\perp}e\mathcal{E}(t)}{2[\omega_{\bm{p}}(t)]^{2}},

(133)

with
\epsilon_{\perp}=c\sqrt{\bm{p}_{\perp}^{2}+m^{2}c^{2}}
,
p_{\|}=\bm{p}\cdot\bm{n}
, and
\bm{p}_{\perp}=\bm{p}-p_{\|}\bm{n}
.

By solving the system of equations (
VII
) with the initial conditions
c^{(+)}_{\bm{p}}(-\infty)=1
and
c^{(-)}_{\bm{p}}(-\infty)=0
(analogue of the anti-Feynman boundary conditions) we obtain the momentum distribution for created positrons,

\displaystyle f^{(-)}(\bm{p})=2|c^{(-)}_{-\bm{p}}(\infty)|^{2}.

(134)

On the other hand, by choosing the initial conditions as
c^{(+)}_{\bm{p}}(-\infty)=0
and
c^{(-)}_{\bm{p}}(-\infty)=1
(analogue of the Feynman boundary conditions) we arrive at the momentum distribution for created electrons,

\displaystyle f^{(+)}(\bm{p})=2|c^{(+)}_{\bm{p}}(\infty)|^{2}.

(135)

In closing this section, we recall that the spinorial approach has been developed much earlier than the DHW-function method (see, Refs.
Grib et al. 1972
;
Mostepanenko and Frolov 1974
;
Bagrov et al. 1975
;
Grib et al. 1994
;
Gavrilov and Gitman 1996
). Moreover, a similar system of equations can be also obtained for the Klein-Gordon equation, for which the system is pseudo-hermitian
Krajewska and Kamiński 2019b
. Finally, let us remark that the equivalent approach to the one presented in this section has been developed by Avetissian and co-workers
Avetissian et al. 2002
and extensively used in Refs.
Jansen and Müller 2013
;
Villalba-Chávez and Müller 2019b
;
Folkerts et al. 2023
.

VIII
Numerical illustrations

The aim of a numerical analysis presented in this section is, among others, to compare three approaches discussed above. We start our discussion with linearly polarized electric field pulses, for which all three methods can be applied.
Next, we shall consider circularly polarized pulses, for which only the S-matrix and the DHW-function approaches can be used. In both cases, we have to define the time-dependent pulses of the electric field,
\bm{\mathcal{E}}(t)
, that satisfy the condition

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\,\bm{\mathcal{E}}(t)=\bm{0}.

(136)

Due to this equation, the vector potential

\bm{A}(t)=\int_{t}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\,\bm{\mathcal{E}}(t^{\prime}),

(137)

fulfills the conditions
\bm{A}(\pm\infty)=\bm{0}
, under which our theoretical analysis has been carried out.

VIII.1
Linear polarization

VIII.1.1
Sauter-like electric field pulse

For the Sauter-Schwinger process in a linearly polarized electric field, the standard choice of the pulse shape is the Sauter one. Its time dependence
is given by the function
1/\cosh^{2}(t/\tau_{0})
, where the parameter
\tau_{0}
determines the pulse duration. Although exact analytical solutions
of the spinorial equations (
VII
) can be constructed in this case, the pulse itself does not meet the condition (
136
). For this
reason, we introduce a function
F(t)
,

F(t)=\frac{\tau_{0}}{\cosh(t/\tau(t))},\,\tau(t)=\tau_{0}[1+\sigma\tanh(t/t_{0})],

(138)

and define the electric field and vector potential as

\bm{\mathcal{E}}(t)={\cal E}(t)\bm{\varepsilon}=-\mathcal{E}_{0}F^{\prime}(t)\bm{\varepsilon},\quad\bm{A}(t)=A(t)\bm{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{E}_{0}F(t)\bm{\varepsilon}.

(139)

Here,
\bm{\varepsilon}
is a constant unit vector determining the polarization of the electric field and
\mathcal{E}_{0}
fixes its strength.

{\cal E}(t)
and
A(t)
are referred to as shape functions for the electric field and the vector potential, respectively, and are defined
by Eqs. (
138
) and (
139
). The pulse additionally depends on three parameters,
\tau_{0}>0
,
-1<\sigma<1
, and
t_{0}>0
,
of which
\tau_{0}
controls the duration of the pulse, whereas
\sigma
relates to the rate of its growth and decay. In particular, if
\sigma
is
close to unity, the pulse builds up quickly and decays slowly. The opposite situation takes place when
\sigma
is close to
-1
. The third parameter,
t_{0}
, plays a minor role and in our numerical analysis is put equal to
\tau_{0}
.

VIII.1.2
Helicity-resolved distributions

In Sec.
VIII.1.1

in the

quantization axis the situation becomes more complex and, in general, all spin-resolved distributions are nonzero. Still, the total spin summed up
distribution stays unchanged. To illustrate this, we consider now the case when the pairs are created in their helicity states,
for which the free bispinors have the form [cf., Eq. (
II

\displaystyle u^{(+)}_{\bm{p},\lambda}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{p^{0}+m_{\rm e}c}{2p^{0}}}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\chi_{\lambda}(\bm{p})\\
\frac{\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{p}}{p^{0}+m_{\rm e}c}\chi_{\lambda}(\bm{p})\\
\end{array}\right],

\displaystyle u^{(-)}_{-\bm{p},\lambda}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{p^{0}+m_{\rm e}c}{2p^{0}}}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}-\frac{\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{p}}{p^{0}+m_{\rm e}c}\chi_{\lambda}(\bm{p})\\
\chi_{\lambda}(\bm{p})\end{array}\right],

for
\lambda=\pm
, and

\displaystyle\chi_{+}(\bm{p})=

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{c}e^{-i\varphi_{\bm{p}}/2}\cos(\theta_{\bm{p}}/2)\\
e^{i\varphi_{\bm{p}}/2}\sin(\theta_{\bm{p}}/2)\end{array}\right],\,

\displaystyle\chi_{-}(\bm{p})=

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{c}-e^{-i\varphi_{\bm{p}}/2}\sin(\theta_{\bm{p}}/2)\\
e^{i\varphi_{\bm{p}}/2}\cos(\theta_{\bm{p}}/2)\end{array}\right].

Here,
\theta_{\bm{p}}
and
\varphi_{\bm{p}}
denote the polar and azimuthal angles of the particle momentum
\bm{p}
, respectively,

\frac{\bm{p}}{|\bm{p}|}=\bm{n}_{\bm{p}}=[\sin\theta_{\bm{p}}\cos\varphi_{\bm{p}},\sin\theta_{\bm{p}}\sin\varphi_{\bm{p}},\cos\theta_{\bm{p}}]^{T},

(150)

and the spinors
\chi_{\lambda}(\bm{p})
fulfill the eigenvalue equation,

(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{n}_{\bm{p}})\chi_{\lambda}(\bm{p})=\lambda\chi_{\lambda}(\bm{p}).

(151)

Hence, the helicity states are the eigenstates of both the free Dirac Hamiltonian and the helicity operator (see, e.g., Ref.
Białynicki-Birula and Białynicka-Birula 1975

The inspection of Fig.

check if these are the zeros of
f^{(+)}_{+}(\bm{p},-)
, we have to analyze the phase of the amplitude
A^{(+)}_{+}(\bm{p},-)
. The reason being
that, at these points, the phase is not defined whereas in their vicinity it acquires all possible values (see, e.g., the original discussion of
this problem presented in Ref.
Dirac 1931
). However, the total phase of
A^{(+)}_{+}(\bm{p},-)
makes such analysis difficult, as it is
the sum of the regular part (called the dynamical phase), that is continuous for all momenta, and the singular part (called the geometrical
or Berry phase), that is not defined for zeros of the amplitude. Unfortunately, the regular phase can change rapidly with momentum, which makes it
difficult to trace the singular points from the phase image. The solution to this problem could be to extract from the amplitude the regular
phase factor, so that the remaining phase is a slowly changing function of momentum beyond singularities. Therefore, we consider the modified amplitude

e^{i\phi(\bm{p})}A^{(+)}_{+}(\bm{p},-)
, where
\phi(\bm{p})
is a regular function of
{\bm{p}}

A priori

it is not uniquely defined. One can only guess its approximate form by analyzing the phase image. In doing so, we have found that its possible form,

\phi(\bm{p})=\eta c\sqrt{\bm{p}^{2}+(m_{\mathrm{e}}c)^{2}}(t_{\mathrm{f}}-t_{\mathrm{i}}),\,\eta=1.8,

(153)

where

and for
\sigma=0.8

t_{\mathrm{i}}=-20t_{C}
and
t_{\mathrm{f}}=200t_{C}
. Of course, by changing a little the factor

significantly modify the phase image, but such that the positions of the singular points are not changed. We have applied this form of the phase factor in all numerical illustrations presented in this paper.

IX
Conclusions

The main motivation of this work was to present an analysis of the dynamical Sauter-Schwinger process based on the standard QED formalism, i.e.,
on the scattering matrix and reduction formulas. We have demonstrated that this formalism provides a way to determine the spin-momentum distributions
of created
e^{-}e^{+}
pairs and the corresponding probability amplitudes. Knowledge of the amplitudes and their phases allows us to study the interference
effects occurring in this process, in particular the vortex structures. These distributions result from certain solutions of the Dirac equation that
satisfy either the Feynman asymptotic conditions (for the distributions of particles, i.e., electrons) or the anti-Feynman ones (for the distributions
of anti-particles, i.e., positrons). Both are not the initial conditions for the Dirac equation, but rather the boundary ones. However, the numerical
analysis of the Sauter-Schwinger effect presented here shows that for the situation when the electric field pulse depends only on time and for
the momentum distributions summed over the electron and positron spins, the approach presented in this paper gives results identical to those obtained
by methods using initial conditions, such as the DHW function formalism. We need to stress that this agreement is achieved only after additional normalization
of the Dirac solution in the far past. The normalizing factor appearing here is close to unity for electric field strengths not approaching
the Schwinger value
\mathcal{E}_{S}
, as in this case the maximum values of momentum distributions are much smaller than one. However, for stronger fields the results obtained by using the formalism presented here differ from those that follow, for example, from the DHW function, if additional normalization is not applied.

We have shown that the Sauter-Schwinger process can be studied by solving the Dirac equation with the appropriate boundary conditions.
It seems that the discussed in this paper approach has a significant advantage over other approaches used in this context, as it allows one
to determine the spin or helicity distributions of probability amplitudes. The issue of creating pairs by space- and time-dependent electric
field pulses remains, however, an open problem. In such situations, the formalism of the DHW function leads to a system of nonlocal equations
(i.e., containing spatial derivatives of arbitrarily high order), the numerical analysis of which has only begun to be developed
recently
Hebenstreit et al. 2010
;
Kohlfürst 2020
;
Al-Naseri et al. 2021
;
Kohlfürst et al. 2022
. It turns out that the approach presented
here is equally complex, as it requires to solve the Dirac equation with appropriate boundary conditions, imposed both in the past and the future.
