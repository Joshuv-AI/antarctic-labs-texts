Ipelets for the Convex Polygonal Geometry
Nithin Parepally, Ainesh Chatterjee, Auguste H. Gezalyan, Hongyang Du, Sukrit Mangla, Kenny Wu, Sarah Hwang, David M. Mount

Abstract
There are many structures, both classical and modern, involving convex polygonal geometries whose deeper understanding would be facilitated through interactive visualizations. The Ipe extensible drawing editor, developed by Otfried Cheong, is a widely used software system for generating geometric figures. One of its features is the capability to extend its functionality through programs called Ipelets. In this media submission, we showcase a collection of new Ipelets that construct a variety of geometric objects based on polygonal geometries. These include Macbeath regions, metric balls in the forward and reverse Funk distance, metric balls in the Hilbert metric, polar bodies, the minimum enclosing ball of a point set, and minimum spanning trees in both the Funk and Hilbert metrics. We also include a number of utilities on convex polygons, including union, intersection, subtraction, and Minkowski sum (previously implemented as a CGAL Ipelet). All of our Ipelets are programmed in Lua and are freely available.

1.1
Macbeath Regions

Given a point
x
in a convex polygon
\Omega
, the Macbeath region
[
38
]
around
x
, denoted
M_{\Omega}(x)
, is a useful tool in convex approximation
[
4
,
5
,
1
,
6
]
, approximate range searching
[
7
,
14
]
, smooth distance approximation
[
2
]
. Additionally, it has useful relations with cap coverings
[
9
,
8
]
and Hilbert balls
[
1
]
. Macbeath regions around a point are constructed by taking the original polygon and intersecting it with its reflection around the point (see Figure
1
).

Definition 1

(Macbeath Region)
.

Given a convex polygon
\Omega
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a point
x\in\interior(\Omega)

M_{\Omega}(x)~=~x+((\Omega-x)\cap(x-\Omega)).

1.2
Funk and Hilbert Balls

The Funk metric is an asymmetric metric used in the analysis of Finsler metrics. Its importance for Finsler geometry and differential geometry is the collection of the following three properties: it is non-reversible, complete, and projectively flat
[
50
,
56
,
46
]
. The Funk metric also has uses in relation to Hilbert geometries
[
45
,
46
]
, Weil-Petersson spaces
[
29
]
, and hyperbolic billiards
[
27
]
. Since it is non-symmetric to define balls, we present Ipelets for both the forward and reverse Funk metric. The Funk and reverse Funk ball around a point
p
in a polygonal geometry
\Omega
with
m
sides is a polygon with
O(m)
sides.

Note that in the following definitions, let
\|\cdot\|
denote the Euclidean norm.

Definition 2

((Forward) Funk Metric)
.

Given an open convex body
\Omega
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and two distinct points
p,q\in\interior(\Omega)
, let
q^{\prime}
be the intersection of the ray
p
through
q
with
\partial\kern 1.0pt\Omega
(Figure
2
(a)):

F_{\Omega}(p,q)~=~\ln\frac{\|p-q^{\prime}\|}{\|q-q^{\prime}\|},~~\text{and}~~F_{\Omega}(p,p)=0.

Definition 3

(Reverse Funk Metric)
.

Given an open convex body
\Omega
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and two distinct points
p,q\in\interior(\Omega)
, let
p^{\prime}
be the intersection of the ray
q
through
p
with
\partial\kern 1.0pt\Omega
(see Figure
2
(b)):

{}^{r}\kern-1.0ptF_{\Omega}(p,q)~=~\ln\frac{\|p^{\prime}-q\|}{\|p^{\prime}-p\|},~~\text{and}~~{}^{r}\kern-1.0ptF_{\Omega(p,p)}=0.

Figure 2:
(a) The Funk metric, (b) the reverse Funk metric, and (c) the Hilbert metric.

The Hilbert polygonal geometry is a generalization of the Cayley-Klein model of hyperbolic geometry to arbitrary convex bodies
[
19
]
and the average of the forward and reverse Funk metrics. It has become increasingly studied due to its applications in convex approximation
[
1
,
53
]
, clustering
[
43
]
, and graph embeddings
[
44
]
as well as its relation to flags
[
53
,
52
]
. Several classical algorithms have been developed for the polygonal metric space including Voronoi diagrams
[
32
,
15
]
and Delaunay triangulations
[
31
]
. The Hilbert ball around a point
p
in a polygonal geometry
\Omega
with
m
sides will be a polygon with
O(m)
sides
[
42
]
. Like Funk balls, Hilbert balls are constructed with the use of spokes representing the intersection of lines through the center of the ball and the vertices of
\Omega
with
\partial\kern 1.0pt\Omega
(see Figure
3
).

Definition 4

(Hilbert metric)
.

Given an open convex body
\Omega
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and two distinct points
p,q\in\Omega
, let
p^{\prime}
and
q^{\prime}
be the intersection of line
pq
with
\partial\kern 1.0pt\Omega
such that the points lie in order
\langle p^{\prime},p,q,q^{\prime}\rangle
(see Figure
2
(c)) then

H_{\Omega}(p,q)~=~\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(p^{\prime},p;q,q^{\prime}\right)=\frac{1}{2}(F_{\Omega}(p,q)+{}^{r}\kern-1.0ptF_{\Omega}(p,q)),~~\text{and}~~H_{\Omega}(p,p)=0.

Where
(p^{\prime},p;q,q^{\prime})
is the cross ratio of the four points.

Figure 3:
(a) Funk, (b) reverse Funk, and (c) Hilbert balls (with and without spokes).

1.3
Polar Bodies

The polar body of a convex polygon is a classical duality with modern applications in Minkowski geometry
[
33
]
, quantum mechanics and information theory
[
24
,
22
,
21
,
23
]
, and convex approximation
[
41
,
3
,
6
]
. The key quality of the polar body of a convex polygon is that pointed corners in the primal space become flatter in the dual (see Figure
4
).

Definition 5

(Polar Body)
.

Given a convex body
\Omega
containing the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
its polar dual
\Omega^{\circ}
defined to be:

\Omega^{\circ}~=~\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,:\,\langle x,y\rangle\leq 1,\forall x\in\Omega\}.

If
\Omega
is in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with vertices
\{(a_{i},b_{i},\dots)\}_{i=1}^{m}
, its polar dual
\Omega^{\circ}
is the polygon defined as the intersection of the half-spaces
\{a_{i}x+b_{i}y+\dots\leq 1\}_{i=1}^{m}
.

The polar body.

2.2
Minkowski Sum

The Minkowski sum of two polygons is used extensively in many modern fields of research for collision detection
[
54
,
49
,
55
]
, solid modeling
[
26
,
51
]
, and motion planning
[
34
,
36
]
others. It is defined as the pairwise addition of all points in both polygons (see Figure
7
(a)). The Minkowski sum of two polygons can be thought of as the region traced out by the centroid of one polygon moving along the boundary of the other (see Figure
7
(b)).

Definition 6

(Minkowski Sum)
.

Given two polygons
\Omega
and
\Psi
the Minkowski sum of the two polygons is defined to be:

\Omega\oplus\Psi~=~\{a+b\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,:\,a\in\Omega,b\in\Psi\}.

(a) Minkowski sum of two polygons, (b) collision detection using the two polygons

16
