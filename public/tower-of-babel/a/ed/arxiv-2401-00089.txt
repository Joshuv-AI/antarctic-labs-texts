Conditions for eigenvalue configurations of two real symmetric matrices: a symmetric function approach
Hoon Hong, Daniel Profili, J. Rafael Sendra

Abstract
For two real symmetric matrices, their eigenvalue configuration is the arrangement of their eigenvalues on the real line. We study the problem of determining a quantifier-free necessary and sufficient condition for two real symmetric matrices to realize a given eigenvalue configuration as a generalization of Descartes’ rule of signs. We exploit the combinatorial properties of our definition for eigenvalue configuration to reduce a two-polynomial root counting problem into several single-polynomial root counting problems of symmetric polynomials. We then leverage the fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials to derive a final quantifier-free necessary and sufficient condition for two real symmetric matrices to realize a given eigenvalue configuration.

The main drawback to applied these techniques is that the general algorithms in quantifier elimination have still doubly exponential time complexity, and furthermore their outputs grow very quickly toward being incomprehensible for even moderately sized inputs. As a consequence of these limitations, it seems natural to address the problem avoiding the use of general quantifier elimination techniques. This paper is framed in that context.

In this paper we analyze the eigenvalue configuration of two real symmetric matrices; that is, the arrangement of their eigenvalues on the real line. In particular, the problem can be stated as follows: given an eigenvalue configuration provide necessary and sufficient conditions on the entries of two generic real symmetric matrices so that their eigenvalue configuration is the prescribed one.

The main contribution of this paper
is to provide a quantifier-free method to solve the problem above. For this purpose, we exploit the combinatorial structure of our definition of an eigenvalue configuration in order to construct symmetric polynomials in the eigenvalues of the two matrices
F
and
G
and then apply the Fundamental Theorem of Symmetric Polynomials to transform these polynomials in the eigenvalues to polynomials in the entries of
F
and
G

all

Theorem 8

(Main Result)
.

Let
c=(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})
be an eigenvalue
configuration and let
F,G\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be symmetric.. Then

EC(F,G)=c\iff\bigwedge_{r=1}^{n}v(p_{r}(-x))=(T_{n}c)_{r},

where

\displaystyle p_{r}

\displaystyle=\text{polynomial such that }h_{r}(x,\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n},\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n})=p_{r}(x,a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b_{1},\dots,b_{n}).

Remark 9
.

The key idea of Theorem
8
is that in the logical equivalence

\underbrace{EC(F,G)=c}_{\text{involves quantifiers (eigenvalues of }F,G)}\iff\underbrace{\bigwedge_{r=1}^{n}v(p_{r}(-x))=(T_{n}c)_{r}}_{\text{involves only polynomial operations, }c,\,\,\wedge,\,\,\vee,\text{ entries of }F,G},

the left-hand side, when fully expanded in terms of the entries of
F
and

G
, involves quantifiers, since eigenvalue configurations are defined in
terms of the eigenvalues of
F
and
G
. In contrast, the right-hand side
consists of a disjunction of conjunctions of polynomial equations and
inequalities involving the entries of
F
and
G
, with
no reference

to the eigenvalues of
F
and
G
and therefore
no quantifiers
.

4
Derivation of the Main Result

We present the proof of Theorem
8
in the style of a derivation. This
approach will allow us to better illustrate the key ideas behind this
approach. To do this, we split up the derivation into several lemmas, each of
which illustrates one key idea.

Our overall goal is to somehow express the information encoded in the
eigenvalue configuration in a way which does not refer directly to the
eigenvalues of
F
and
G
. In order to do this, we will first define a vector

q=(q_{1},\dots,q_{n})
which encodes the information contained in the
eigenvalue configuration vector in a way that allows us to compute it via
counting the roots of a certain polynomial. We accomplish this via an
invertible linear transformation
T_{n}
whose entries are defined
combinatorially. But first, we show that such a transformation exists.

Lemma 10

(Transform)
.

We have

c=EC\left(F,G\right)\ \ \ \ \Longrightarrow\ \ \ \ q=T_{n}c

where

q=\begin{pmatrix}q_{1}\\
\vdots\\
q_{n}\end{pmatrix}\ \ \text{and }c=\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}\\
\vdots\\
c_{n}\end{pmatrix}\ \ \text{and\ \ }T_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}(T_{n})_{11}&\cdots&(T_{n})_{1n}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
(T_{n})_{n1}&\cdots&(T_{n})_{nn}\end{pmatrix}

and where

(T_{n})_{rs}=\#\left\{\left(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\right):1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq n\ \ \wedge\ \ \#\left\{p:i_{p}\leq s\right\}=odd\right\}.

Proof.

Assume that
c=EC\left(F,G\right)
. It suffices to show
q=T_{n}c
. Recall
from Notation
6
that

Q_{r}=\left\{\left(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r},j\right):1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i\,_{r}\leq n\ \ \wedge\ \ 1\leq j\leq n\right\},

and

q_{r}=\#\left\{\left(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r},j\right)\in Q_{r}:\prod_{p=1}^{r}\left(\alpha_{i_{p}}-\beta_{j}\right)<0\right\}.

We proceed by repeatedly rewriting the definition of
q_{r}
, with the goal of
expressing it in terms of the eigenvalue configuration vector. We begin with
the definition of
q_{r}
.

q_{r}=\#\left\{\left(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r},j\right)\in Q_{r}:\prod_{p=1}^{r}\left(\alpha_{i_{p}}-\beta_{j}\right)<0\right\}

Note that for each
(i_{1},\dots,i_{r})
satisfying
1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq n
, we also have
(i_{1},\dots,i_{r},j)\in Q_{r}
simply by
appending each
j=1,\dots,n
. Hence, we can rewrite this action of counting
over the set
Q_{r}
as a summation over all such tuples
(i_{1},\dots,i_{r})
. Thus we obtain

q_{r}=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq n}\ \#\left\{j:\prod_{p=1}^{r}\left(\alpha_{i_{p}}-\beta_{j}\right)<0\right\}

We can then introduce another summation by partitioning the set on the
right-hand side depending on which interval
I_{s}
each
\beta_{j}
belongs
to.

q_{r}=\sum_{1\leq s\leq n}\ \sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq n}\ \#\left\{j:\beta_{j}\in I_{s}\ \ \wedge\ \ \prod_{p=1}^{r}\left(\alpha_{i_{p}}-\beta_{j}\right)<0\right\}

Then, we eliminate the product symbol by observing that the product

\prod_{p=1}^{r}(\alpha_{i_{p}}-\beta_{j})
is negative if and only if there
are an odd number of
p
’s such that
\alpha_{i_{p}}-\beta_{j}<0
, or
equivalently
\alpha_{i_{p}}<\beta_{j}
.

\displaystyle q_{r}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq s\leq n}\ \sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq n}\ \#\left\{j:\beta_{j}\in I_{s}\ \ \wedge\ \ \#\left\{p:\alpha_{i_{p}}<\beta_{j}\right\}=odd\right\}

Next, we use the fact that the
\alpha
’s are indexed in ascending order, and
we note that
\alpha_{i_{p}}<\beta_{j}
if and only if
i_{p}\leq s
, since

\beta_{j}\in I_{s}
. Thus

\displaystyle q_{r}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq s\leq n}\ \sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{s}\leq n}\ \#\left\{j:\beta_{j}\in I_{s}\ \ \wedge\ \ \#\left\{p:i_{p}\leq s\right\}=odd\right\}

Now, note that if
\#\{p:i_{p}\leq s\}
is even, then the size of the set
in the summand is zero. If
\#\{p:i_{p}\leq s\}
is odd, then the size of
the set in the summand equals
\#\{j:\beta_{j}\in I_{s}\}
, which is
exactly
c_{s}
. Hence

\displaystyle q_{s}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq s\leq n}\ \sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq n}\left\{\begin{array}[c]{ll}0&\text{if\ \ }\#\left\{p:i_{p}\leq s\right\}=even\\
c_{s}&\text{if\ \ }\#\left\{p:i_{p}\leq s\right\}=odd\end{array}\right.

Next, we factor out the
c_{s}
and move it outside the innermost summation.

\displaystyle q_{r}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq s\leq n}\ \sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq n}\left(\left\{\begin{array}[c]{ll}0&\text{if\ \ }\#\left\{p:i_{p}\leq s\right\}=even\\
1&\text{if\ \ }\#\left\{p:i_{p}\leq s\right\}=odd\end{array}\right.c_{s}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq s\leq n}\ \left(\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq n}\left\{\begin{array}[c]{ll}0&\text{if\ \ }\#\left\{p:i_{p}\leq r\right\}=even\\
1&\text{if\ \ }\#\left\{p:i_{p}\leq r\right\}=odd\end{array}\right.\right)c_{s}

Then, we fold the conditional into the summation.

q_{r}=\sum_{1\leq s\leq n}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq n\\
\#\left\{p:i_{p}\leq s\right\}=odd\end{subarray}}1\right)c_{s}

Since the innermost summand is just a summation of 1’s, we can view it as
counting the elements of the set
\{(i_{1},\dots,i_{r}):1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq n\,\wedge\,\#\{p:i_{p}\leq s\}=\text{odd}\}
.

q_{r}=\sum_{1\leq s\leq n}(T_{n})_{rs}c_{s}

where

(T_{n})_{rs}=\#\left\{\left(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\right):1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq n\ \ \wedge\ \ \#\left\{p:i_{p}\leq s\right\}=odd\right\}.

By the definition of matrix multiplication, this is just the multiplication of
the vector
c=(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})^{T}
by the matrix
T_{n}
whose entries are
defined as above. Thus

\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}q_{1}\\
\vdots\\
q_{n}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[c]{ccc}(T_{n})_{11}&\cdots&(T_{n})_{1n}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
(T_{n})_{n1}&\cdots&(T_{n})_{nn}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}c_{1}\\
\vdots\\
c_{n}\end{array}\right)

that is

q=T_{n}c.

∎

Lemma 11
.

T_{n}
is invertible.

Proof.

It suffices to prove that
\left|T_{n}\right|\neq 0
. We prove it in
several stages. First, we establish a recurrence relation for the entries of

T_{n}
, and then apply that to decompose
T_{n}
into a product of triangular
matrices to more easily compute the determinant.

Claim 1:
We have

(T_{n})_{rs}=\left\{\begin{array}[c]{ll}s&\text{if }\left(r,s\right)\in\left\{1\right\}\times\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}\\
\left\{\begin{array}[c]{ll}1&\text{if }s\ \text{is odd}\\
0&\text{if }s\ \text{is even}\end{array}\right.&\text{if }\left(r,s\right)\in\left\{n\right\}\times\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}\\
\left\{\begin{array}[c]{ll}\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}n\\
r\end{array}\right)&\text{if }r\ \text{is odd}\\
0&\text{if }r\ \text{is even}\end{array}\right.&\text{if }\left(r,s\right)\in\left\{2,\ldots,n-1\right\}\par\times\left\{n\right\}\\
(T_{n-1})_{r-1,s}+(T_{n-1})_{r,s}&\text{if }\left(r,s\right)\in\left\{2,\ldots,n-1\right\}\times\left\{1,\ldots,n-1\right.\}.\end{array}\right.

Proof of the claim: There are four cases in the above. We will prove them one
by one.

1.

\left(r,s\right)\in\left\{1\right\}\times\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}
.

By definition, we have

(T_{n})_{1s}=\#\left\{\left(i_{1}\right):1\leq i_{1}\leq n\ \ \wedge\ \ \#\left\{p:i_{p}\leq s\right\}=odd\right\}.

Since we are only considering
i_{1}
, then
\#\{p:i_{p}\leq s\}
can only be
zero or one. If that quantity is zero, then
(T_{n})_{1,s}
is zero, since
zero is even. If instead
\#\{p:i_{p}\leq s\}=1
, then
1\leq i_{1}\leq s
, so
there are
s
choices for
i_{1}
. Thus
(T_{n})_{1,s}=s
.

2.

\left(r,s\right)\in\left\{n\right\}\times\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}
.

By definition, we have

\displaystyle(T_{n})_{ns}

\displaystyle=\#\left\{\left(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}\right):1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{n}\leq n\ \ \wedge\ \ \#\left\{p:i_{p}\leq s\right\}=odd\right\}

\displaystyle=\#\left\{\left(1,\ldots,n\right):\#\left\{p:p\leq s\right\}=odd\right\}

\displaystyle=\#\left\{\left(1,\ldots,n\right):s=odd\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[c]{ll}1&\text{if }s\ \text{is odd}\\
0&\text{if }s\ \text{is even}\end{array}\right.

3.

\left(r,s\right)\in\left\{2,\ldots,n-1\right\}\times\left\{n\right\}
.

By definition of
(T_{n})_{rs}
we have

(T_{n})_{rn}=\#\left\{\left(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\right):1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq n\ \ \ \wedge\ \ \ \#\left\{p:1\leq p\leq r\ \ \wedge\ \ i_{p}\leq n\right\}=\text{odd}\right\}.

But
i_{p}\leq n
trivially, so
\#\{p:1\leq p\leq r\ \wedge\ i_{p}\leq n\}=\#\{p:1\leq p\leq r\}
. Therefore

\displaystyle(T_{n})_{rn}

\displaystyle=\#\left\{\left(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\right):1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq n\ \ \ \wedge\ \ \ \#\left\{1,\ldots,r\right\}=\text{odd}\right\}

\displaystyle=\#\left\{\left(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\right):1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq n\ \ \ \wedge\ \ \ r=\text{odd}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[c]{ll}\#\left\{\left(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\right):1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq n\right\}&\text{if }r\ \text{is odd}\\
\#\left\{{}\right\}&\text{if }r\ \text{is even}\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[c]{ll}\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}n\\
r\end{array}\right)&\text{if }r\ \text{is odd}\\
0&\text{if }r\ \text{is even}\end{array}\right.

4.

\left(r,s\right)\in\left\{2,\ldots,n-1\right\}\times\left\{1,\ldots,n-1\right\}
.

By definition, we have

(T_{n})_{rs}=\#\left\{\left(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\right):1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq n\ \ \wedge\ \ \#\left\{p:i_{p}\leq s\right\}=odd\right\}.

We can partition the set whose elements we are counting into elements with

i_{r}<n
and those with
i_{r}=n
.

\displaystyle(T_{n})_{rs}

\displaystyle=\#\left\{\left(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\right):1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}=n\ \ \wedge\ \ \#\left\{p:i_{p}\leq s\right\}=odd\right\}+

\displaystyle\#\left\{\left(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\right):1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}<n\ \ \wedge\ \ \#\left\{p:i_{p}\leq s\right\}=odd\right\}.

If
i_{r}=n
, then
(i_{1},\dots,i_{r-1})
is a tuple such that
1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq n-1
. In addition, we still have that
\#\{p:i_{p}\leq s\}
is odd, because
s<n
. These are exactly the tuples that are counted by

(T_{n-1})_{r-1,s}
; thus

\displaystyle\#\left\{\left(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\right):1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}=n\ \ \wedge\ \ \#\left\{p:i_{p}\leq s\right\}=odd\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\#\left\{\left(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r-1}\right):1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r-1}\leq n-1\ \ \wedge\ \ \#\left\{p:i_{p}\leq s\right\}=odd\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(T_{n-1})_{r-1,s}.

On the other hand, if
i_{r}<n
, then
(i_{1},\dots,i_{r})
is a tuple
satisfying
1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq n-1
and again
\#\{p:i_{p}\leq s\}

remains odd. These tuples are counted by
(T_{n})_{r,s}
, and so

\displaystyle\#\left\{\left(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\right):1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}<n\ \ \wedge\ \ \#\left\{p:i_{p}\leq s\right\}=odd\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\#\left\{\left(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\right):1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq n-1\ \ \wedge\ \ \#\left\{p:i_{p}\leq s\right\}=odd\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(T_{n-1})_{r,s}.

Putting those together, we get that

(T_{n})_{rs}=(T_{n-1})_{r-1,s}+(T_{n-1})_{r,s}.

We have proved Claim 1.

Claim 2:
We have

T_{n}=L_{n}U_{n}

where

(L_{n})_{rs}=\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}n-s\\
r-s\end{array}\right)\ \ \ \,\text{and \ }\ (U_{n})_{rs}=\left(-2\right)^{r-1}\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}s\\
r\end{array}\right)

Proof of the claim. It suffices to show

(T_{n})_{rs}=\sum_{t=1}^{n}(L_{n})_{rt}(U_{n})_{ts}

We proceed by induction on
n
. Note that when
n=1
, we have

1=(T_{1})_{11}=\binom{1-1}{1-1}(-2)^{1-1}\binom{1}{1}=L_{1}U_{1}.

For the induction hypothesis, suppose the claim is true for some
n-1
. We
will prove the claim for
n
. There are four cases in the above. We will prove
them one by one.

1.

\left(r,s\right)\in\left\{1\right\}\times\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}
.

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n}(L_{n})_{1t}(U_{n})_{ts}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\binom{n-t}{1-t}(-2)^{t-1}\binom{s}{t}

\displaystyle=\binom{n-1}{1-1}(-2)^{1-1}\binom{s}{1}\ \ \ \ \text{since }\binom{n-t}{1-t}=0\ \text{for }t>1

\displaystyle=s

\displaystyle=(T_{n})_{1s}

2.

\left(r,s\right)\in\left\{n\right\}\times\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}
.

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n}(L_{n})_{nt}(U_{n})_{ts}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}n-t\\
n-t\end{array}\right)\left(-2\right)^{t-1}\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}s\\
t\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{s}\left(-2\right)^{t-1}\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}s\\
t\end{array}\right)\ \ \,\,\,\text{since }\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}s\\
t\end{array}\right)=0\ \,\text{for }t>s

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{-2}\left(-1+\sum_{t=0}^{s}\left(1\right)^{s-t}\left(-2\right)^{t}\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}s\\
t\end{array}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{-2}\left(-1+\left(1-2\right)^{s}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[c]{ll}1&\text{if }s\ \text{is odd}\\
0&\text{if }s\ \text{is even}\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle=\left(T_{n}\right)_{ns}

3.

\left(r,s\right)\in\left\{2,\ldots,n-1\right\}\times\left\{n\right\}
.

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n}(L_{n})_{rt}(U_{n})_{tn}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\binom{n-t}{r-t}(-2)^{t-1}\binom{n}{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{\left(n-t\right)!}{\left(n-r\right)!\left(r-t\right)!}(-2)^{t-1}\frac{n!}{\left(n-t\right)!t!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{n!}{\left(n-r\right)!}\frac{1}{\left(r-t\right)!t!}(-2)^{t-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{n!}{\left(n-r\right)!r!}\frac{r!}{\left(r-t\right)!t!}(-2)^{t-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}n\\
r\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}r\\
t\end{array}\right)(-2)^{t-1}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}n\\
r\end{array}\right)\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}r\\
t\end{array}\right)(-2)^{t-1}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}n\\
r\end{array}\right)\left(\frac{1}{-2}\left(-1+\sum_{t=0}^{n}\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}r\\
t\end{array}\right)\left(1\right)^{r-t}(-2)^{t}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}n\\
r\end{array}\right)\left(\frac{1}{-2}\left(-1+\left(1-2\right)^{r}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[c]{ll}\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}n\\
r\end{array}\right)&\text{if }r\ \text{is odd}\\
0&\text{if }r\ \text{is even}\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle=\left(T_{n}\right)_{rn}

4.

\left(r,s\right)\in\left\{2,\ldots,n-1\right\}\times\left\{1,\ldots,n-1\right\}
.

Recall that we have

(T_{n})_{rs}=(T_{n-1})_{r-1,s}+(T_{n-1})_{r,s}.

By the induction hypothesis, we have

\displaystyle(T_{n})_{rs}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}(L_{n-1})_{r-1,t}(U_{n-1})_{t,s}+\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}(L_{n-1})_{r,t}(U_{n-1})_{t,s}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}(L_{n-1})_{r-1,t}(U_{n-1})_{t,s}+(L_{n-1})_{r,t}(U_{n-1})_{t,s}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}((L_{n-1})_{r-1,t}+(L_{n-1})_{r,t})(U_{n-1})_{t,s}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}\left(\binom{n-1-t}{r-1-t}+\binom{n-1-t}{r-t}\right)(U_{n-1})_{t,s}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}\binom{n-t}{r-t}(U_{n-1})_{t,s}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}(L_{n})_{r,t}(U_{n-1})_{t,s}.

But
(U_{n-1})_{t,s}
does not depend on
n
. Thus
(U_{n-1})_{t,s}=(U_{n})_{t,s}
. Hence

\displaystyle(T_{n})_{rs}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}(L_{n})_{r,t}(U_{n})_{t,s}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}(L_{n})_{r,t}(U_{n})_{t,s}+\underbrace{\binom{n-n}{r-n}(-2)^{r-1}\binom{s}{n}}_{=0\text{ since }s<n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{n}(L_{n})_{r,t}(U_{n})_{t,s}.

We have proved Claim 2.

Now note that
L_{n}
is lower triangular, since if
s>r
then

r-s<0
and so
\binom{n-s}{r-s}=0
. Similarly,
U_{n}
is upper triangular,
since if
r>s
then
\binom{s}{r}=0
. Further, we have

|L_{n}|=\prod_{t=1}^{n}(L_{n})_{tt}=\prod_{t=1}^{n}\binom{n-t}{t-t}=\prod_{t=1}^{n}\binom{n-t}{0}=\prod_{t=1}^{n}1=1,

and

|U_{n}|=\prod_{t=1}^{n}(U_{n})_{tt}=\prod_{t=1}^{n}(-2)^{t-1}\binom{t}{t}=\prod_{t=1}^{n}(-2)^{t-1}=(-2)^{0+1+\dots+n-1}=(-2)^{\binom{n}{2}}.

Thus we have

|T_{n}|=|L_{n}||U_{n}|=(-2)^{\binom{n}{2}}\neq 0\text{.}

Hence
T_{n}
is invertible.
∎

Remark 12
.

In the previous proof, as a stepping stone toward proving that
T_{n}
is
invertible we showed that
T_{n}
has the decomposition
T_{n}=L_{n}U_{n}

where

(L_{n})_{rs}=\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}n-s\\
r-s\end{array}\right)\ \ \text{and\ \ }(U_{n})_{rs}=\left(-2\right)^{r-1}\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}s\\
r\end{array}\right).

These are combinatorially beautiful matrices for which we provided no
combinatorial interpretation. This is one focus of our future work in this
area, as the matrix
T_{n}
has many interesting combinatorial aspects which
we did not have time or space to address in this paper.

Now, we can summarize Lemmas
10
and
11
into the following lemma.

Lemma 13
.

We have

EC(F,G)=c\ \ \iff\ \ q=T_{n}c.

Proof.

Lemma
10
shows that
EC(F,G)=c\implies q=T_{n}c
. Lemma

11
shows that
T_{n}
is invertible, hence
T_{n}
is a 1-1 linear map.
Therefore, we have that
q=T_{n}c\implies EC(F,G)=c.

∎

At this point, we have found an equivalent condition for
EC(F,G)=c
. However,
this condition still contains quantifiers, as it references the eigenvalues of

F
and
G
. The remainder of the derivation will focus on solving this issue,
by introducing symmetric polynomials
h_{r}
whose roots can be counted to
produce an equivalent condition in terms of the entries of
F
and
G
only,
and not their eigenvalues.

Lemma 14
.

We have

q_{r}=\#\text{ of negative roots of }h_{r}.

Proof.

Recall that by definition we have

h_{r}:=\prod\limits_{\left(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r},j\right)\in Q_{r}}\left(x-\prod_{p=1}^{r}\left(\alpha_{i_{p}}-\beta_{j}\right)\right).

The roots of
h_{r}
are, by construction, exactly the products
\prod_{p=1}^{r}(\alpha_{i_{p}}-\beta_{j})
. Hence the negative roots of
h_{r}

are exactly what is counted by
q_{r}
.
∎

Notice that
h_{r}
is a symmetric polynomial with real roots. This is the
crucial fact which allows us to construct another polynomial
p_{r}
involving
the
entries
of
F
and
G
rather than their
eigenvalues
.
This will eliminate the quantifiers.

Proposition 15
.

For each
r=1,\dots,n
, there exists
p_{r}\in\mathbb{R}\left[x,a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\right]
such that

h_{r}=p_{r}(x,a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b_{1},\dots,b_{n}).

Proof.

For the purposes of this proof, we view
h_{r}
as being a polynomial in
x,\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n},\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}
, with real coefficients.

Note that by construction
h_{r}
is symmetric in
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
. Recall that by the Fundamental Theorem of Symmetric Polynomials, this
means that there exists a polynomial
p_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}[x,y_{1},\dots,y_{n}]
such that

h_{r}(x,\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n},\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n})=p_{\alpha}(x,\sigma_{1}(\alpha),\dots,\sigma_{n}(\alpha)).

Further note
h_{r}
is also symmetric in
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}
; hence

p_{\alpha}
is symmetric in
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}
. Thus by again
applying the Fundamental Theorem of Symmetric Polynomials, there exists a
polynomial
p_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}[x,y_{1},\dots,y_{n}][z_{1},\dots,z_{n}]
such
that

p_{\alpha}(x,y_{1},\dots,y_{n})=p_{r}(x,\sigma_{1}(\alpha),\dots,\sigma_{n}(\alpha),\sigma_{1}(\beta),\dots,\sigma_{n}(\beta)).

Now, combining these together, we have that

\displaystyle p_{r}(x,\sigma_{1}(\alpha),\dots,\sigma_{n}(\alpha),\sigma_{1}(\beta),\dots,\sigma_{1}(\beta))

\displaystyle=p_{\alpha}(x,\sigma_{1}(\alpha),\dots,\sigma_{n}(\alpha))

\displaystyle=h_{r}(x,\alpha_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n},\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}).

Finally, note that

\displaystyle a_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}\leq n}\alpha_{i_{1}}=\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}=\sigma_{1}(\alpha)

\displaystyle a_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}\leq n}\alpha_{i_{1}}\alpha_{i_{2}}=\sigma_{2}(\alpha)

\displaystyle a_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{n}\leq n}\alpha_{i_{1}}\cdots\alpha_{i_{n}}=\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{n}=\sigma_{n}(\alpha).

Similarly

\displaystyle b_{1}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{1}(\beta)

\displaystyle b_{n}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{n}(\beta).

Hence, we have that

\displaystyle h_{r}(x,\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n},\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n})

\displaystyle=p_{r}(x,\underbrace{\sigma_{1}(\alpha)}_{a_{1}},\dots,\underbrace{\sigma_{n}(\alpha)}_{a_{n}},\underbrace{\sigma_{1}(\beta)}_{b_{1}},\dots,\underbrace{\sigma_{n}(\beta)}_{b_{n}})

\displaystyle=p_{r}(x,a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b_{1},\dots,b_{n})

∎

With that, we are ready to prove the main result (Theorem
8
).

Proof of Main Result (Theorem
8
)
.

Let
q=\begin{pmatrix}q_{1}\\
\vdots\\
q_{n}\end{pmatrix}=T_{n}c
.
For each
r=1,\dots,n
, by Proposition
15
there exists
p_{r}\in\mathbb{R}[x,a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b_{1},\dots,b_{n}]
such that

h_{r}(x,\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n},\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n})=p_{r}(x,a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b_{1},\dots,b_{n}).

Note by Lemma
14

\displaystyle q_{r}

\displaystyle=\#\text{ negative roots of }h_{r}(x)

\displaystyle=\#\text{ negative roots of }p_{r}(x)

\displaystyle=\#\text{ positive roots of }p_{r}(-x)

\displaystyle=v(p_{r}(-x))\,\text{by Descartes' rule of signs, since all roots of }p_{r}\text{ are real}.

Therefore, we have that

q=T_{n}c\,\,\iff\,\,\bigwedge_{r=1}^{n}v(p_{r}(-x))=(T_{n}c)_{r}.

Finally, by Lemmas
10
and
11
, we have

EC(F,G)=c\,\,\iff\,\,q=T_{n}c\,\,\iff\,\,\underbrace{\bigwedge_{r=1}^{n}v(p_{r}(-x))=(T_{n}c)_{r}}_{P}.

Note that each
v(p_{r}(-x))=(T_{n}c)_{r}
is a logical disjunction (see Algorithm
19
and Remark
20
) involving the
coefficients of
p_{r}
as viewed as a polynomial in
x
, whose coefficients
are themselves polynomials in
a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b_{1},\dots,b_{n}
. Hence the
expression
P
is a quantifier-free condition which is equivalent to

EC(F,G)=c
.
∎
