The Rate of Convergence for Selberg’s Central Limit Theorem under the Riemann Hypothesis
Asher Roberts

Abstract
We assume the Riemann hypothesis to improve upon the rate of convergence of ( log ⁡ log ⁡ log ⁡ T ) 2 / log ⁡ log ⁡ T (\log\log\log T)^{2}/\sqrt{\log\log T} in Selberg’s central limit theorem for log ⁡ | ζ ⁡ ( 1 / 2 + i ​ t ) | \log|\zeta(1/2+it)| given by the author in roberts . We achieve a rate of convergence of log ⁡ log ​ log ⁡ log ​ T / log ⁡ log ⁡ T \sqrt{\log\log\log\log T}/\sqrt{\log\log T} in the Dudley distance. The proof is an adaptation of the techniques used by the author in roberts , based on the work of Radziwiłł and Soundararajan in rands and Arguin et al. in maxzeta , combined with a lemma of Selberg selbergmollifier that provides for a mollifier close to the critical line Re ⁡ ( s ) = 1 / 2 \re(s)=1/2 under the Riemann hypothesis.

Introduction

Let
\tau
be a random point uniformly distributed on
[T,2T]
. Selberg’s central limit theorem states that as
T\to\infty
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\log|\zeta(\tfrac{1}{2}+i\tau)|>V\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\log\log T}\right)\to\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{V}^{\infty}e^{-u^{2}/2}du,\quad\forall V\in\mathbb{R}.

In this paper, we determine the rate of convergence to a Gaussian random variable in Selberg’s theorem using the Dudley (also called bounded Wasserstein, e.g. see Gaunt and Li
dudley
) distance between two random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
given by

d_{\mathcal{D}}(X,Y)=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{L}}|\mathbb{E}[f(X)]-\mathbb{E}[f(Y)]|

where
\mathcal{L}=\{f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\mid f
is Lipschitz,
\|f\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, and
\|f\|_{\text{Lip}}\leq 1\}
. Here and throughout the paper
\|f\|_{\infty}
denotes the supremum norm of
f
and
\|f\|_{\text{Lip}}=\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{\|x-y\|_{1}}
with
\|\cdot\|_{1}
denoting the
L^{1}
norm. We use the Dudley distance since the boundedness property allows us to use
Lemma

4
in
Proposition

5
, while the Lipschitz property assists us with the remaining propositions in the paper.

1.1
Main Result

Theorem 1.1

Assume the Riemann hypothesis and let
\tau
be a random point distributed uniformly on
[T,2T]
. Then for
T
large enough,

d_{\mathcal{D}}\left(\frac{\log|\zeta(\tfrac{1}{2}+i\tau)|}{\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\log\log T}},\mathcal{Z}\right)\ll\frac{\sqrt{\log\log\log\log T}}{\sqrt{\log\log T}},

where
\mathcal{Z}
is a standard normal random variable.

Theorem

1.1
is an improvement of the rate of convergence

d_{\mathcal{D}}\left(\frac{\log|\zeta(\tfrac{1}{2}+i\tau)|}{\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\log\log T}},\mathcal{Z}\right)\ll\frac{(\log\log\log T)^{2}}{\sqrt{\log\log T}}

given by the author in
roberts
using the Dudley distance. We modify the techniques of the author in
roberts
to prove
Theorem

1.1
by mollifying
\log|\zeta(1/2+it)|
closer to the critical line
\re(s)=1/2
. This allows us to approximate
\log|\zeta(1/2+it)|
by an “almost” Gaussian prime sum using fewer steps, yielding a smaller error.

1.2
Relations to Previous Results

In 1946 Selberg presented a proof of his central limit theorem for the Riemann zeta function in
selberg
. Later, Radziwiłł and Soundararajan used simpler techniques to prove Selberg’s theorem for
\log|\zeta(\frac{1}{2}+it)|
in
rands
. It is challenging to apply these techniques to the imaginary case, due to the difficulty in mollifying the imaginary part of zeta. Nevertheless, the mollifier used in
Proposition

2
of this paper can be applied to the imaginary part of zeta, but this would require a different way of moving the imaginary part of zeta off axis than we present in the proof of
Proposition

1
. However, our work be extended to obtain analogous results for Dirichlet
L
-functions, as Hsu and Wong did in
wongandhsu
to prove the central limit theorem using the techniques of Radziwiłł and Soundararajan.

Our paper mainly uses the techniques employed by the author in
roberts
, although some modifications are needed. Here the rate of convergence was obtained for zeta at two points, while our result is presented for zeta at one point for simplicity but can be similarly extended. The main difference lies in the mollification step
Proposition

2
. Without the Riemann hypothesis, the mollifier Selberg offers in
selbergmollifier
presents a sum over zeta zeros that is difficult to estimate. By assuming the Riemann hypothesis and moving off axis by
1/\log T
(instead of by
(\log\log\log T)^{2}/\log T
in
roberts
), we can estimate this sum to be as close to order
1
as we would like by further iterating the log in the exponent of the parameter
X
used.

In
selbergamalfi
Selberg claims a rate of convergence of
(\log\log\log T)^{2}/\sqrt{\log\log T}
unconditionally for the real part of zeta using the Kolmogorov distance, a proof of which is given by Tsang in
tsang
. The author reproduces this rate using the techniques of Radziwiłł and Soundararajan as well as Arguin et al. in
roberts
. Selberg also claims a rate of
\log\log\log T/\sqrt{\log\log T}
for the imaginary case. An explicit proof of this result is given by Wahl in
wahl
in the context of mod-gaussian convergence (more information is given in Kowalski and Nikeghbali
modgaussian
).

While the Riemann hypothesis allows us to improve upon Selberg’s rate of convergence in
selbergamalfi
, there exist barriers towards further improvement. Specifically, although we can push the parameter
X
further in
Proposition

2
as stated, we are still limited in the choice of parameters
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
used in the Dirichlet polynomials we compare to Gaussian distributions in
Proposition

5
. That is, iterating the log further in those parameters results in Dirichlet polynomials that align less closely with Gaussian distributions, worsening the errors presented in
Lemmas

5
to
6
. Improving our overall stated rate of convergence therefore requires more sophisticated techniques in handling parameters with log iterations that approach order
1
.

1.3
Structure of the Proofs

The proof of
Theorem

1.1
follows by five successive propositions. All proofs are given in
Section

2
, and the distances between the random variables are explicit at each step of the proof. The trickiest step is
Proposition

2
, mollifying zeta. In particular, handling the sum over zeta zeros. However, the step that incurs the greatest error is
Proposition

4
, truncating the prime sum. Although we could improve the error obtained in mollifying by iterating the logarithm further in the definition of the parameter
X
used in
Proposition

2
, we would still be required to perform the same truncation in
Proposition

4
, in order to effectively compare moments with the Fourier transforms in
Proposition

5
. That is, an improvement to the rate of convergence we obtain requires an improvement in the way we truncate using
Proposition

4
.

To connect zeta with the aforementioned “almost” Gaussian prime sum, we first move off axis using
Proposition

1
, since the Riemann hypothesis will then ensure that we do not encounter a zero when mollifying zeta in
Proposition

2
. To simplify notation, we set

\sigma_{0}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{\log T},\qquad s_{0}=\sigma_{0}+i\tau,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathfrak{s}=\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\log\log T}.

(1)

The contribution towards the rate of convergence in
Theorem

1.1
by moving off axis is then given by

Proposition 1
(Moving off axis)

With the notation above, we have

d_{\mathcal{D}}\left(\frac{\log|\zeta(\tfrac{1}{2}+i\tau)|}{\mathfrak{s}},\frac{\log|\zeta(s_{0})|}{\mathfrak{s}}\right)\ll\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log\log T}}.

Off axis, we are now able to approximate zeta with a mollifier in the form of a Dirichlet polynomial
\mathcal{P}(s)
given in
Proposition

2
. Note that
Proposition

2
is the only proposition conditional on the Riemann hypothesis. The contribution towards the rate of convergence in
Theorem

1.1
by mollifying is then given by

Proposition 2
(Mollifying)

Assume the Riemann hypothesis. Let
X=T^{1/(\log_{4}T)^{1/4}}
where
\log_{\ell}
stands for the logarithm iterated
\ell
times, and

\Lambda_{X}(n)=\begin{cases}\Lambda(n)&\text{for }1\leq n\leq X,\\
\Lambda(n)\dfrac{\log\dfrac{X^{2}}{n}}{\log X}&\text{for }X\leq n\leq X^{2}.\end{cases}

Set

\mathcal{P}(s_{0})=\sum_{n<X^{2}}\frac{\Lambda_{X}(n)}{n^{s_{0}}\log n}.

Then

d_{\mathcal{D}}\left(\frac{\log|\zeta(s_{0})|}{\mathfrak{s}},\frac{\re\mathcal{P}(s_{0})}{\mathfrak{s}}\right)\ll\frac{\sqrt{\log_{4}T}}{\sqrt{\log\log T}}.

We then discard the higher order primes in the sum
\mathcal{P}(s)
, whose contribution towards the rate of convergence in
Theorem

1.1
is given by

Proposition 3
(Discarding higher order primes)

Set

\mathcal{P}(s_{0})=\sum_{n<X^{2}}\frac{\Lambda_{X}(n)}{n^{s_{0}}\log n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad P(s_{0})=\re\sum_{p\leq X}\frac{1}{p^{s_{0}}}.

With the notation above, we have

d_{\mathcal{D}}\left(\frac{\re\mathcal{P}(s_{0})}{\mathfrak{s}},\frac{P(s_{0})}{\mathfrak{s}}\right)\ll\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log\log T}}.

In order to compare moments in
Proposition

5
, we truncate the prime sum. The more we truncate the sum, the faster the sum tends toward a Gaussian random variable. This truncation bears a contribution towards the rate of convergence in
Theorem

1.1
given by

Proposition 4
(Truncating the prime sum)

Set

\displaystyle P_{1}(s_{0})

\displaystyle=\re\sum_{p\leq X_{1}}\frac{1}{p^{s_{0}}},

\displaystyle P_{2}(s_{0})

\displaystyle=\re\sum_{X_{1}<p\leq X_{2}}\frac{1}{p^{s_{0}}}\text{, and}

\displaystyle P_{3}(s_{0})

\displaystyle=\re\sum_{X_{2}<p\leq X}\frac{1}{p^{s_{0}}},

so that
P(s_{0})=P_{1}(s_{0})+P_{2}(s_{0})+P_{3}(s_{0})
where
X_{1}=T^{1/\log\log T}
and
X_{2}=T^{1/\log\log\log T}
. With the notation above, we have

d_{\mathcal{D}}\left(\frac{P(s_{0})}{\mathfrak{s}},\frac{P_{1}(s_{0})+P_{2}(s_{0})}{\mathfrak{s}}\right)\ll\frac{\sqrt{\log_{4}T}}{\sqrt{\log\log T}}.

Finally, we compare our truncated sum to a Gaussian random variable, generating a contribution towards the rate of convergence in
Theorem

1.1
given by

Proposition 5

With the notation above, we have

d_{\mathcal{D}}\left(\frac{P_{1}(s_{0})+P_{2}(s_{0})}{\mathfrak{s}},\mathcal{Z}\right)\ll\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log\log T}},

where
\mathcal{Z}
is a standard normal random variable.

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}f\,d\mu-\int_{\mathbb{R}}f\,d\nu\right|\leq\frac{1}{F}+\|f\|_{\infty}\left\{(RF)\|(\hat{\mu}-\hat{\nu})\mathbbm{1}_{(-F,F)}\|_{\infty}+\mu((-R,R)^{c})+\nu((-R,R)^{c})\right\}.

To use
Lemma

4
, we introduce a lemma that shows that the difference in expectations between the Fourier transforms of the prime sum and that of a Gaussian random variable is small.

Lemma 5

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{i\xi P_{1}(s_{0})}{\mathfrak{s}}\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}[\exp(i\xi\mathcal{Z})]\right|

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{(\log\log T)^{2}}.

Proof

We first truncate the Fourier transform of
P_{1}(s_{0})
for
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
by writing

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{i\xi P_{1}(s_{0})}{\mathfrak{s}}\right)\cdot\mathbbm{1}(|P_{1}(s_{0})|\leq\log\log T)\right]

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{n\leq N}\frac{1}{n!}i^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{\xi P_{1}(s_{0})}{\mathfrak{s}}\right)^{n}\cdot\mathbbm{1}(|P_{1}(s_{0})|\leq\log\log T)\right]+(\log T)^{-C}

for a constant
C
, where the error term
(\log T)^{-C}
is estimated in
roberts
using Stirling’s formula. The event
\{|P_{1}(s_{0})|\leq\log\log T\}
is introduced to assist with the truncation, and is subsequently removed by inserting the complement of this event,
\{|P_{1}(s_{0})|>\log\log T\}
, and showing that the probability of the complement is small. After truncating, the moment estimate for
P_{1}
in
Lemma

3
is used to provide the error in comparing the truncated moments to a Gaussian. Full details of the proof are given in Lemma 9 of
roberts
.

Next we introduce
Lemma

6
as an analog of
Lemma

5
for
P_{2}(s_{0})
.

Lemma 6

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{i\xi P_{2}(s_{0})}{\mathfrak{s}}\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}[\exp(i\xi\mathcal{Z})]\right|

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{(\log\log T)^{2}}.

Proof

We truncate the Fourier transform of
P_{2}(s_{0})
for
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
by writing

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{i\xi P_{2}(s_{0})}{\mathfrak{s}}\right)\cdot\mathbbm{1}(|P_{2}(s_{0})|\leq 2\log\log\log T)\right]

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{n\leq N}\frac{1}{n!}i^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{\xi P_{2}(s_{0})}{\mathfrak{s}}\right)^{n}\cdot\mathbbm{1}(|P_{2}(s_{0})|\leq 2\log\log\log T)\right]+(\log T)^{-D}

for a constant
D
, similar to the way we truncated the Fourier transform of
P_{1}(s_{0})
. Note that we take
2\log\log\log T
in the event in order to achieve an error of
1/(\log\log T)^{2}
, since
the proof requires an estimate for
\mathbb{P}(|P_{2}(s_{0})|>2\log\log\log T)
instead of for
\mathbb{P}(|P_{1}(s_{0})|>\log\log T)
. As such,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|P_{2}(s_{0})|>2\log\log\log T)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(2\log\log\log T)^{2k}}\mathbb{E}\left[|P_{2}(s_{0})|^{2k}\right]

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{(2\log\log\log T)^{2k}}\left[\frac{(2k)!}{k!2^{k}}\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\log\log\log T}\right)^{2k}\right]

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{(2\log\log\log T)^{2k}}\left[\frac{(2k)^{2k}}{k^{k}2^{k}}\frac{\exp(-2k)}{\exp(-k)}\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\log\log\log T}\right)^{2k}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\log\log\log T)^{2k}}\left[(2k)^{k}\exp(-k)2^{-k}(\log\log\log T)^{k}\right],

so we can set
k=\lfloor\log\log\log T\rfloor
to get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|P_{2}(s_{0})|>2\log\log\log T)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{(2k)^{2k}}[(2k)^{k}\exp(-k)2^{-k}k^{k}]

\displaystyle=2^{-2k}\exp(-k)

\displaystyle\ll\exp(-2k)=\frac{1}{(\log\log T)^{2}}.

The rest of the proof is the same as for
Lemma

5
.

Now, using
Lemmas

4
to
6
we have

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\left(\frac{P_{1}(s_{0})+P_{2}(s_{0})}{\mathfrak{s}}\right),\mathcal{Z}\right]\leq d_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\left(\frac{P_{1}(s_{0})}{\mathfrak{s}}\right),\mathcal{Z}\right]+d_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\left(\frac{P_{2}(s_{0})}{\mathfrak{s}}\right),\mathcal{Z}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{F}+\left\{(R_{1}F)\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{i\xi P_{1}(s_{0})}{\mathfrak{s}}\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}[\exp(i\xi\mathcal{Z})]\right|\right.

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq}\left.+\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{P_{1}(s_{0})}{\mathfrak{s}}\right|>R_{1}\right)+\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{Z}|>R_{1})\right\}

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq}+\frac{1}{F}+\left\{(R_{2}F)\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{i\xi P_{2}(s_{0})}{\mathfrak{s}}\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}[\exp(i\xi\mathcal{Z})]\right|\right.

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq}\left.+\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{P_{2}(s_{0})}{\mathfrak{s}}\right|>R_{2}\right)+\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{Z}|>R_{2})\right\}

for any
R_{1},R_{2},F>0
. Since

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|P_{2}(s_{0})|>\sqrt{\log\log\log T}\sqrt{\log\log T}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(\log\log\log T)(\log\log T)}\mathbb{E}[|P_{2}(s_{0})|^{2}]=\frac{1}{\log\log T}

we can take
R_{1}=\sqrt{\log\log T}
,
R_{2}=\sqrt{\log\log\log T}
and
F=C\sqrt{\log\log T}
to get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log\log T}}+\left[C\log\log T\left(\frac{1}{(\log\log T)^{2}}\right)+\frac{1}{\log T}+\frac{1}{(\log T)^{D}}\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log\log T}}+\left[C\sqrt{\log\log\log T}\sqrt{\log\log T}\left(\frac{1}{(\log\log T)^{2}}\right)+\frac{1}{\log\log T}+\frac{1}{(\log\log T)^{D}}\right]

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log\log T}}.
