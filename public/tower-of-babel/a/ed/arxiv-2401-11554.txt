Transfer Learning under Covariate Shift: Local k k -Nearest Neighbours Regression with Heavy-Tailed Design
Petr Zamolodtchikov, Hanyuan Hang

Abstract
Covariate shift is a common transfer learning scenario where the marginal distributions of input variables vary between source and target data while the conditional distribution of the output variable remains consistent. The existing notions describing differences between marginal distributions face limitations in handling scenarios with unbounded support, particularly when the target distribution has a heavier tail. To overcome these challenges, we introduce a new concept called density ratio exponent to quantify the relative decay rates of marginal distributions’ tails under covariate shift. Furthermore, we propose the local k-nearest neighbour regressor for transfer learning, which adapts the number of nearest neighbours based on the marginal likelihood of each test sample. From a theoretical perspective, convergence rates with and without supervision information on the target domain are established. Those rates indicate that our estimator achieves faster convergence rates when the density ratio exponent satisfies certain conditions, highlighting the benefits of using density estimation for determining different numbers of nearest neighbours for each test sample. Our contributions enhance the understanding and applicability of transfer learning under covariate shift, especially in scenarios with unbounded support and heavy-tailed distributions.



1
Introduction

Transfer learning aims to leverage the knowledge gained from the
source
distribution
\operatorname{P}
to improve the performance on the
target
distribution
\operatorname{Q}
, especially when limited data is available from the target distribution.
In contrast with traditional machine learning approaches, where models are trained and evaluated on the same distribution, transfer learning allows models to generalize knowledge across source and target distributions, potentially improving performance on the target distribution with limited data.
Transfer learning has been successfully applied in various domains, including computer vision
[
10
,
18
]
, natural language processing
[
13
]
, healthcare
[
1
,
6
]
, speech recognition
[
16
]
and so on.
By adapting the knowledge of the source distribution to the target distribution, transfer learning helps to improve the performance of the learning algorithms in these fields.

In this article, we focus on one of the most common settings in transfer learning, i.e.,
covariate shift

[
15
]
.
Under this setting, the marginal distributions of the input variables may differ from the source to target data, whereas the conditional distribution of the output variable, given the input variables, remains the same across the source and the target distribution. Covariate shift indicates that there has been a change in the distribution of input features across domains. This change may be caused by factors such as different periods, different devices, and diverse environments, leading to variations in the data distribution. For example, consider a computer vision scenario where source data consists of images captured during the day, while target data comprises images taken at night. Due to differences in lighting conditions and other factors, a covariate shift exists between the two sets, potentially leading to a decline in the performance of a model trained on nighttime images when applied to daytime images. In addition to this example, covariate shift is also conceivable in a variety of real-world fields, including semantic segmentation
[
12
,
5
]
, speech recognition
[
14
]
, sentiment analysis
[
3
]
, and reinforcement learning
[
4
]
.

In the theoretical studies of algorithms under covariate shift,
various notions have been proposed to describe differences in feature probabilities between the source and target domains.
However, these notions typically focus on scenarios with bounded support and do not account for unbounded support situations.
For instance,
[
9
]
introduces the concept called transfer-exponent that parameterizes ball-mass ratios
\operatorname{Q}\{\operatorname{B}(x,r)\}/\operatorname{P}\{\operatorname{B}(x,r)\}
to encode the singularity of
\operatorname{Q}
with respect to
\operatorname{P}
.
However, if
\operatorname{Q}
has heavier tail than
\operatorname{P}
, the transfer-exponent
\gamma
is infinite. Consequently, according to the convergence rates established in
[
9
]
, no matter how much data is provided, it cannot improve the convergence rates of classifiers in the target domain, implying that source domain data does not assist in predicting the target domain.
More recently,
[
11
]
introduces a new similarity measure between two probability measures. However, the similarity measure becomes infinite when
\operatorname{Q}
has heavier tails than
\operatorname{P}
.
In this case, the theoretical results established by
[
11
]
are no longer valid.
Notice that under the covariate shift assumption, where the source domain and the target domain share the same conditional distribution of labels given features, the relationship learned between features and labels on
\operatorname{P}
may still be effective on
\operatorname{Q}
, indicating an improvement of predictions on the target domain with the usage of the data from
\operatorname{P}
.
However, existing notions and the corresponding theoretical results cannot prove this point, indicating that in some cases, the exponent cannot effectively describe the relationship between
\operatorname{P}
and
\operatorname{Q}
with unbounded support, especially when
\operatorname{Q}
has heavier tails than
\operatorname{P}
. This reflects the limitation of these notions.

To address these challenges, we first introduce a new concept called
density ratio exponent
to describe the relationship between the marginal distributions of
\operatorname{P}
and
\operatorname{Q}
under covariate shift, which can quantify the relative decay rates of the tails of the source and target marginal distributions. Furthermore, based on the information from the marginal distributions of
\operatorname{P}
and
\operatorname{Q}
,
we propose a
local

k
-nearest neighbour regressor (
k
-NN) for transfer learning under covariate shift, which assigns different numbers of nearest neighbours to test samples. Compared to the standard
k
-NN regressor, the local choice of
k
depends on the relevance of test instances to the source distribution.
Specifically, if a test instance belongs to a high-probability region of the source distribution, more neighbours of this instance are used for prediction. By contrast, if a test instance is unlikely to appear in the source domain, only a few neighbours are used for prediction.

The contributions of this article can be summarised as follows.

(i)

We introduce the concept of the
density ratio exponent
to characterise the relationship between the marginal density functions of the source and target distributions. In comparison to existing notions under the covariate shift assumption, the density ratio exponent can quantify the relative decay rates between the tails of
\operatorname{P}
and
\operatorname{Q}
.

(ii)

We propose a
local

k
-NN for transfer learning under covariate shift. From a theoretical perspective, convergence rates are established for both the supervised and unsupervised cases when
k
is allowed to vary for each test sample based on its marginal distribution.

(iii)

Our theoretical results indicate that, compared to the standard
k
-NN regressor, our estimator can achieve faster convergence rates when the density ratio exponent satisfies certain conditions. This demonstrates the advantages of utilising density estimation to determine varying numbers of nearest neighbours for each test sample from the perspective of convergence rates.

Nonparametric regression model under covariate shift.
We consider the standard nonparametric regression model. Let
(X,Y)\sim\operatorname{Q}_{\mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}}
be a couple of random variables with values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}.
We assume the existence of a function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying

\displaystyle Y=f(X)+\varepsilon,

(1)

where
\varepsilon
is a centered random variable, independent from
X,
and which satisfies the uniform exponential condition

Assumption 1

(Uniform exponential condition)
.

There exists a couple of constant
\lambda_{0},\sigma_{0}>0
such that

\displaystyle\operatorname{E}\big[\exp(\lambda_{0}|\varepsilon|)\big]\leq\sigma_{0}.

(2)

In particular, any sub-Gaussian and any subexponential random variables satisfy Assumption

. Moreover, we assume that

lies in a Hölder ball defined below.

Definition 2

(Hölder Class of Functions)
.

Let
\beta\in(0,1],\ F>0,

We denote by
\mathcal{H}_{\beta}(F,L)

\displaystyle|f(x)-f(y)|\leq L\|x-y\|^{\beta}.

(3)

Given an i.i.d.

(X_{1},Y_{1}),\dots,(X_{n},Y_{n})
following a probability distribution
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}}
whose marginals satisfy (
1
), the goal is to construct an estimator
\widehat{f}(\cdot,\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n})
that minimizes the Mean Squared Error (MSE)
\ell(\widehat{f})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\operatorname{E}[(\widehat{f}(X)-Y)^{2}].
It is well known that under Model (
1
), the MSE is minimised by the Bayes’ least squares estimate,
x\mapsto\operatorname{E}[Y|X=x]=f(x).
It is then standard to shift
\ell_{\operatorname{Q}}(\widehat{f})
by the MSE at the Bayes’ estimate, which leads to the
L^{2}(\operatorname{Q}_{\mathrm{X}})
-norm
\mathcal{E}_{\operatorname{Q}_{\mathrm{X}}}(\widehat{f},f)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\ell_{\operatorname{Q}}(\widehat{f})-\ell_{\operatorname{Q}}(f)=\operatorname{E}[(\widehat{f}(X)-f(X))^{2}].
The latter is a random quantity since it implicitly depends on the sample. In this article, we study bounds in probability for
\mathcal{E}_{\operatorname{Q}_{\mathrm{X}}}(\widehat{f},f).
The setting of transfer learning marks a departure from the standard risk analysis for nonparametric regression in which the data
\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
follows the same distribution as the testing pair
(X,Y).
Instead, we consider that the two distributions only share weaker structural characteristics, which we model by the standard Covariate Shift (CS) assumption.

(Covariate Shift)
.

Let

\operatorname{Q}_{\mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}}

We say that
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}}

\operatorname{Q}_{\mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}}

\displaystyle\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}=\operatorname{Q}_{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}.

(CS)

Under (

), the unknown function

in (
1
) is the same under
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}}
and
\operatorname{Q}_{\mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}}.
The mismatch takes effect on the level of marginal distributions

and
\operatorname{Q}_{\mathrm{X}}.
Additionally, this means that the noise variable’s distribution is invariant across distributions satisfying (

Since the distribution of

the unknown function

and the noise variable

Density ratio exponent.
Intuitively, the prediction risk depends on the source-target pair
(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}},\operatorname{Q}_{\mathrm{X}}).
On one hand, the transferless case, mentioned before, refers to the situation
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}=\operatorname{Q}_{\mathrm{X}}.
In our setup, this extreme case leads to the well-known minimax optimal rate
n^{-2\beta/(2\beta+d)}.
Another extreme is reached whenever
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}
and
\operatorname{Q}_{\mathrm{X}}
have disjoint supports. In this case, it is easy to see that the prediction risk for data
\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}
sampled solely from
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}},
is lower bounded by a constant, rendering transfer learning useless for large datasets. Therefore, the interest lies in characterising the behaviour of regression estimates for source-target pairs that realise a compromise between those two extremes. This compromise is quantified by a similarity measure, which, in turn, appears as a modulating parameter in the rates obtained under (
CS
). One challenge in the recent literature on transfer learning under covariate shift is the design of a similarity measure that induces a meaningful class of source-target pairs. Two of those similarity measures have been mentioned in Section
1
. This article defines a new similarity measure called
density ratio exponent
.

Definition 4

(Density Ratio Exponent)
.

Let

we say that the couple
(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}},\operatorname{Q}_{\mathrm{X}})
has a density ratio exponent

if

\displaystyle\int\frac{q(x)}{p(x)^{\gamma}}\,dx<\infty,

(DRE)

where
p
and

are the respective densities of
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}
and
\operatorname{Q}_{\mathrm{X}}.

We give examples of source-target pairs that admit a density ratio exponent in Example
7
. We are now ready to define the class of probability distributions considered in this article.

Local Nearest Neighbours.
To address the covariate shift problem, we need to estimate the regression function
f.
We compare two approaches: the standard

-NN and the local
k
-NN. Let

and a neighbour function

We define the local

-NN estimator as

\displaystyle\widehat{f}(x)=\frac{1}{k(x)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}(x)\mathds{1}(i\leq k(x)).

(4)

In particular, taking

as a constant independent of

leads to the standard

-NN estimator.
Additionally, we define the two-sample local

-NN. Let
\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\{(X^{\prime}_{i},Y^{\prime}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m}
be, respectively, two sets of

and

\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}}
and
\operatorname{Q}_{\mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}}.
Given two neighbour functions
k_{\operatorname{P}}
and

the two-sample local
(k_{\operatorname{P}},k_{\operatorname{Q}})
-nearest neighbours estimator is defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{f}(x)=\frac{1}{k_{\operatorname{P}}(x)+k_{\operatorname{Q}}(x)}\Bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}(x)\mathds{1}(i\leq k_{\operatorname{P}}(x))+\sum_{j=1}^{m}Y^{\prime}_{j}(x)\mathds{1}(j\leq k_{\operatorname{Q}}(x))\Bigg].

(5)

The standard

-NN is obtained by choosing constant neighbour functions
k_{\operatorname{P}}
and

Local one- and two-sample nearest neighbours.
We now consider the local nearest neighbours regressor. The aim here is to improve the prediction risk by choosing a neighbour function that balances bias and variance in a pointwise manner. In this context, our choice of neighbours depends on the density of the data. Hence, a good selection of
k
involves estimating the covariates’ densities. We introduce the
\ell
-nearest neighbours density estimator.

Definition 10
.

Let
\operatorname{P}
be an absolutely continuous distribution on
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
and denote by
p
its density. Let
\ell\in\{1,\dots,n\}.
We denote by
\widehat{p}
the
\ell
-nearest neighbours density estimator of
p
, which expression is given, at

by

\displaystyle\widehat{p}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{\operatorname{P}_{n}(\operatorname{B}(x,R_{\ell}(x)))}{R_{\ell}(x)^{d}}=\frac{\ell}{nR_{\ell}(x)^{d}},

(6)

where
\operatorname{P}_{n}
denotes the empirical distribution associated with the sample
(X_{1},\dots,X_{n}).

We show, in the appendix, that the
\ell
-NN density estimator achieves
\widehat{p}(x)\asymp p(x)

4
Outline of the proof

The proof relies on a uniform pointwise analysis of the error associated with the local nearest neighbours regressor
\widehat{f}.
Considering
\overline{f}\colon x\mapsto\operatorname{E}[\widehat{f}(x)|X^{n}],
the following bias-variance decomposition holds for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

\displaystyle\big|\widehat{f}(x)-f(x)\big|

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\big|\widehat{f}(x)-\overline{f}(x)\big|}_{\text{Variance}}+\underbrace{\big|\overline{f}(x)-f(x)\big|}_{\text{Bias}}.

Using the smoothness and the boundedness of
f\in\mathcal{H}_{\beta}(L,F),
the bias term is bounded by
LR_{k(x)}(x)^{\beta}\wedge 2F.
The nearest neighbour radius
R_{k(x)}(x)
is then related to a deterministic quantity that depends only on the design distribution. Let
h>0
and define the function
\zeta_{h}\colon x\mapsto\inf\{t>0\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\operatorname{P}\{\operatorname{B}(x,t)\}\geq h\}.
Picking
h=h(x)\asymp k(x)/n,\ k\colon x\mapsto\{\lceil\log n\rceil,\dots,n\},
and exploiting the fact that the set of closed balls in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is Vapnik-Chervonenkis (VC) subgraph, Proposition
20
shows that

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{R_{k(x)}(x)}{\zeta_{h(x)}(x)}\leq 1

holds with high probability. The minimal mass property of
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}
implies that
\zeta_{h}(x)\lesssim(k(x)/np(x))^{1/d},
for all
x
in
\Delta_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}p(x)\gtrsim k(x)/n\}.
Hence, with high probability, for
x\in\Delta_{n},

\displaystyle|f(x)-\overline{f}(x)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\bigg(\frac{k(x)}{np(x)}\bigg)^{\beta/d},

Next, we deal with the variance term, which reads

\displaystyle|\widehat{f}(x)-\overline{f}(x)|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k(x)}\sum_{i=1}^{k(x)}\big|Y_{i}(x)-f(X_{i}(x))\big|.

(7)

Given
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a realisation of
X^{n}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n}),
the uniform exponential assumption (
2
) together with Theorem 12.2 in
[
2
]
provides an exponential bound on the tail of the sum in (
7
). Conditionally on the sample
X^{n},
any given
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be associated with a permutation
\sigma_{x}
of
\{1,\dots,n\}
that satisfies
(X_{1}(x),\dots,X_{n}(x))=(X_{\sigma_{x}(1)},\dots,X_{\sigma_{x}(n)}).
Let the
\mathcal{W}=\{\sigma_{x}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
be the set of permutations that are obtainable when
x
describes
\mathbb{R}^{d}.
The Milnor-Thom theorem (see Theorem 12.2 in
[
2
]
) shows that the cardinality of
\mathcal{W}
grows polynomially in
n.
Upon applying the union bound and taking the expectation with respect to the sample, we are left with

\displaystyle\big|\widehat{f}(x)-\overline{f}(x)\big|\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{k(x)}},

uniformly over
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and with high probability.

Bounding the prediction error of the two-sample estimator
Consider now
\widehat{f}
to be a two-sample
(k_{\operatorname{P}},k_{\operatorname{Q}})
-NN. One can rewrite

\displaystyle|\widehat{f}(x)-f(x)|

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{\operatorname{P}}(x)}{k_{\operatorname{P}}(x)+k_{\operatorname{Q}}(x)}|\widehat{f}_{\operatorname{P}}(x)-f(x)|+\frac{k_{\operatorname{Q}}(x)}{k_{\operatorname{P}}(x)+k_{\operatorname{Q}}(x)}|\widehat{f}_{\operatorname{Q}}(x)-f(x)|,

where
f_{\operatorname{P}}
and
f_{\operatorname{Q}}
are, respectively, a
k_{\operatorname{P}}
and a
k_{\operatorname{Q}}
one-sample nearest neighbour regressors associated with an
n
-sample from
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}}
and an
m
-sample from
\operatorname{Q}_{\mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}}.
We apply the previously derived high-probability bounds to both terms in the above decomposition. To do so, we define two sets
\Delta_{\operatorname{P}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{x\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}p(x)\gtrsim\log(n+m)/n\}
and
\Delta_{\operatorname{Q}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{x\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}q(x)\gtrsim\log(n+m)/m\},
and we bound the prediction error as follows

\mathcal{E}_{\operatorname{Q}_{\mathrm{X}}}(\widehat{f},f)\lesssim\int_{\Delta_{\operatorname{P}}\cap\Delta_{\operatorname{Q}}}\frac{\log(n+m)}{k_{\operatorname{P}}(x)+k_{\operatorname{Q}}(x)}\,d\operatorname{Q}_{\mathrm{X}}(x)\\
+\int_{\Delta_{\operatorname{P}}\cap\Delta_{\operatorname{Q}}}\frac{1}{k_{\operatorname{P}}(x)+k_{\operatorname{Q}}(x)}\Bigg[k_{\operatorname{P}}(x)\bigg(\frac{k_{\operatorname{P}}(x)}{np(x)}\bigg)^{2\beta/d}+k_{\operatorname{Q}}(x)\bigg(\frac{k_{\operatorname{Q}}(x)}{mq(x)}\bigg)^{2\beta/d}\Bigg]\,d\operatorname{Q}_{\mathrm{X}}(x)\\
+\int_{\Delta_{\operatorname{P}}^{c}\cap\Delta_{\operatorname{Q}}}\frac{k_{\operatorname{P}}(x)}{k_{\operatorname{P}}(x)+k_{\operatorname{Q}}(x)}\,d\operatorname{Q}_{\mathrm{X}}(x)+\int_{\Delta_{\operatorname{P}}\cap\Delta_{\operatorname{Q}}^{c}}\frac{k_{\operatorname{Q}}(x)}{k_{\operatorname{P}}(x)+k_{\operatorname{Q}}(x)}\,d\operatorname{Q}_{\mathrm{X}}(x)\\
+\operatorname{Q}_{\mathrm{X}}\{\Delta_{\operatorname{P}}^{c}\cap\Delta_{\operatorname{Q}}^{c}\}.

(8)

We now take
k_{\operatorname{P}}
and
k_{\operatorname{Q}}
as in Theorem
11
. It can be shown that in the regime
n\wedge m\gtrsim\log(n+m),
for
\ell_{\operatorname{P}}\asymp\log(n+m),
the maximal mass property implies the existence of two constants

such that

for all

A similar result holds for
\widehat{q}
and
\ell_{\operatorname{Q}}\asymp\log(n+m).
This, and some algebra, shows that the first two terms in (

\log(n+m)/(np(x)+mq(x)).
Integrating the latter over
\Delta_{\operatorname{P}}\cap\Delta_{\operatorname{Q}}
leads to

\int_{\Delta_{\operatorname{P}}\cap\Delta_{\operatorname{Q}}}\bigg(\frac{\log(n+m)}{np(x)+mq(x)}\bigg)^{2\beta/(2\beta+d)}\\
\lesssim\int_{\Delta_{\operatorname{P}}}\bigg(\frac{\log(n+m)}{np(x)}\bigg)^{2\beta/(2\beta+d)}q(x)\,dx\wedge\int_{\Delta_{\operatorname{Q}}}\bigg(\frac{\log(n+m)}{mq(x)}\bigg)^{2\beta/(2\beta+d)}q(x)\,dx.

Denote by

the
\ell_{\operatorname{P}}
nearest neighbour’s distance to

among the data sampled from
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}}
and by

the

among the data sampled from
\operatorname{Q}_{\mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}}.
The maximal mass property enables us to show that

is lower-bounded by a positive constant for all

Hence, using
\ell_{\operatorname{P}}\asymp\ell_{\operatorname{Q}}\asymp\log(n+m),
in the regime

\displaystyle\int_{\Delta_{\operatorname{P}}^{c}\cap\Delta_{\operatorname{Q}}}\frac{k_{\operatorname{P}}(x)}{k_{\operatorname{P}}(x)+k_{\operatorname{Q}}(x)}\,d\operatorname{Q}_{\mathrm{X}}(x)

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\Delta_{\operatorname{P}}^{c}\cap\Delta_{\operatorname{Q}}}1\wedge\Bigg(\frac{R^{\operatorname{Q}}_{\ell_{\operatorname{Q}}}(x)^{d}}{R^{\operatorname{P}}_{\ell_{\operatorname{P}}}(x)^{d}}\Bigg)^{2\beta/(2\beta+d)}\,d\operatorname{Q}_{\mathrm{X}}(x)

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\Delta_{\operatorname{P}}^{c}\cap\Delta_{\operatorname{Q}}}1\wedge\bigg(\frac{\log(n+m)}{mq(x)}\bigg)^{2\beta/(2\beta+d)}\,d\operatorname{Q}_{\mathrm{X}}(x)

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\Delta_{\operatorname{P}}^{c}}q(x)\,dx\wedge\int_{\Delta_{\operatorname{Q}}}\bigg(\frac{\log(n+m)}{mq(x)}\bigg)^{2\beta/(2\beta+d)}q(x)\,dx.

A similar analysis leads to

\displaystyle\int_{\Delta_{\operatorname{P}}\cap\Delta_{\operatorname{Q}}^{c}}\frac{k_{\operatorname{Q}}(x)}{k_{\operatorname{P}}(x)+k_{\operatorname{Q}}(x)}\,d\operatorname{Q}_{\mathrm{X}}(x)

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\Delta_{\operatorname{Q}}^{c}}q(x)\,dx\wedge\int_{\Delta_{\operatorname{P}}}\bigg(\frac{\log(n+m)}{np(x)}\bigg)^{2\beta/(2\beta+d)}q(x)\,dx.

Finally, the result of Theorem
11

(ii)
are obtained by bounding each of the terms, including
\operatorname{Q}_{\mathrm{X}}\{\Delta_{\operatorname{P}}^{c}\cap\Delta_{\operatorname{Q}}^{c}\},
using that, for non-negative
f,
if
\int f(x)^{b}\,dx<\infty
and

then

\displaystyle\int_{\{x\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}f(x)\geq\delta\}}f(x)^{a}\,dx\leq\delta^{a-b}\int f(x)^{b}\,dx,\text{ and }\int_{\{x\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}f(x)<\delta\}}f(x)\,dx\leq\delta^{1-b}\int f(x)^{b}\,dx,

together with (

) and (

).

5
Conclusion

We have introduced a novel similarity measure for transfer learning under covariate shift called density ratio exponent. In particular, the density ratio exponent quantifies the relative tail decays of the source and target distributions. We have also introduced the local
k
-nearest neighbours regressor and derived its convergence rates under smoothness assumption and covariate shift. Our results indicate that the standard
k
-NN regressor is outperformed by its local counterpart, demonstrating the advantages of a local choice of
k.
Our rates account for the tails of both source and target distributions and are optimal if the target admits moments of order
\rho>2\beta
and the density ratio exponent is larger than
2\beta/(2\beta+d).
Existing literature shows that optimal rates are unobtainable for
\rho\leq 2\beta
and
\gamma<2\beta/(2\beta+d).
It remains unclear whether the rates we obtain in those regimes are minimax optimal. Interestingly, minimax optimal rates can be attained even when the likelihood ratio
q(x)/p(x)
explodes. Our results are obtained under the strong maximal mass assumption. A weakening of the latter will reveal more intricate dynamics for the convergence rates under covariate shift. The authors stress the interest of a weakening that would extend the analysis to distributions that admit zeroes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}.
Such weakening and derivations of corresponding minimax lower bounds are left for future research.

Lemma 42
.

Let
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}=\mathcal{E}(\lambda),
then,
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}\in\mathcal{P}(\lambda,\exp(-\lambda),1,2\sinh(\lambda)/\lambda,1)
and
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}
satisfies (
PM
) for any
\rho\geq 0.
Additionally, if
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}=\mathcal{E}(\lambda_{\operatorname{P}}),\ \operatorname{Q}_{\mathrm{X}}=\mathcal{E}(\lambda_{\operatorname{Q}}),
and
\lambda=\lambda_{\operatorname{P}}\vee\lambda_{\operatorname{Q}},
then,
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}
and
\operatorname{Q}_{\mathrm{X}}
are both in
\mathcal{P}(\lambda,\exp(-\lambda),1,2\sinh(\lambda)/\lambda,1),
and
\operatorname{Q}_{\mathrm{X}}\in\mathcal{Q}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}},\gamma,\rho)
for all
\gamma<\lambda_{\operatorname{Q}}/\lambda_{\operatorname{P}}
and all
\rho\geq 0.

Proof.

We first check the assumptions in Definition
5
. By definition, the density of
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}
is upper bounded by
\lambda.
Next, for any
x>0
and
0<t\leq 1,

\displaystyle\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}\{\operatorname{B}(x,t)\}=\int_{x-t}^{x+t}\lambda\exp(-\lambda u)\mathds{1}(u\geq 0)\,du

\displaystyle\geq\int_{x}^{x+t}\lambda\exp(-\lambda(x+t))\,du

\displaystyle=t\exp(-\lambda t)p(x)\geq a_{-}tp(x),

where
a_{-}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\exp(-\lambda).
This proves that the minimal mass property is satisfied for any
0<t\leq 1
and
a_{-}
defined previously. Next, we prove the maximal mass property. Let
t\in(0,1].
If
0\leq x\leq t,
then
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}\{\operatorname{B}(x,t)\}=1-\exp(-\lambda(x+t)),
and

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}\{\operatorname{B}(x,t)\}}{tp(x)}=\frac{1-\exp(-\lambda(x+t))}{t\lambda\exp(-\lambda x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp(\lambda x)-\exp(-\lambda t)}{t\lambda}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\exp(\lambda t)-\exp(-\lambda t)}{t\lambda}=\frac{2\sinh(\lambda t)}{\lambda t}.

The function
g\colon t\mapsto 2\sinh(\lambda t)/(\lambda t)
continuously increases on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}.
Moreover,
g
admits a limit as
t\to 0,
which is equal to
2.
Let then
a_{+}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=g(1)=\sinh(\lambda)/\lambda,
we have proven that for
0<t\leq 1
and
0\leq x\leq t,
it holds that
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}\{\operatorname{B}(x,t)\}\leq a_{+}p(x)t.
We now consider
t<x,
in which case we have

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}\{\operatorname{B}(x,t)\}}{tp(x)}=\frac{\exp(-\lambda(x-t))-\exp(-\lambda(x+t))}{\lambda t\exp(-\lambda x)}=\frac{2\sinh(\lambda t)}{\lambda t}\leq a_{+},

as previously, for all
0<t\leq 1.
We have shown that
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}\in\mathcal{P}(\lambda,\exp(-\lambda),1,2\sinh(\lambda)/\lambda,1).
Next, we prove the pseudo moment assumption (
PM
). Let
\rho>0
arbitrary. We have

\displaystyle\int p(x)^{d/(\rho+d)}\,dx=\int\lambda^{d/(\rho+d)}\exp\bigg(-\frac{\lambda xd}{\rho+d}\bigg)\,dx<\infty.

Hence,
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}
satisfies (
PM
) for all
\rho>0.
Finally, we show the second claim. Let
\gamma<\lambda_{\operatorname{Q}}/\lambda_{\operatorname{P}}.
Then,

\displaystyle\int\frac{q(x)}{p(x)^{\gamma}}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{\operatorname{Q}}}{\lambda_{\operatorname{P}}^{\gamma}}\int\exp((\gamma\lambda_{\operatorname{P}}-\lambda_{\operatorname{Q}})x)\,dx<\infty,

which finishes the proof.
∎

Lemma 43
.

Let
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}
and
\operatorname{Q}_{\mathrm{X}}
be two Pareto distributions with parameters
\alpha_{\operatorname{P}}
and
\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}
respectively, that is,
p(x)=x^{-(\alpha_{\operatorname{P}}+1)}\mathds{1}(x\geq 1)
and
q(x)=x^{-(\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}+1)}\mathds{1}(x\geq 1).
Then, the distribution
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}
belongs to
\mathcal{P}(1,2,1/2,a(\alpha_{\operatorname{P}}),1/2)
with
a\colon\alpha\mapsto 3^{\alpha+1}/2^{\alpha+2}.
Additionally,
\operatorname{Q}_{\mathrm{X}}\in\mathcal{P}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}},\gamma,\rho)
for all
\gamma<\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}/(1+\alpha_{\operatorname{P}})
and all
\rho>\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}.
Consequently,
(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}},\operatorname{Q}_{\mathrm{X}})\in\mathcal{P}(1,2,1/2,a(\alpha_{\operatorname{P}}\vee\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}),1/2).

Proof.

Let
p
be the density of
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}
and
r_{+}=r_{-}=1/2.
We have, for all
0<r\leq 1/2,

\displaystyle\frac{2(p(x-r)\wedge 1)}{p(x)}

\displaystyle=x^{\alpha_{\operatorname{P}}+1}\mathds{1}(x\leq 1+r)+\bigg(\frac{x}{x-r}\bigg)^{\alpha_{\operatorname{P}}+1}\mathds{1}(x>1+r)

\displaystyle\leq(1+r)^{\alpha_{\operatorname{P}}+1}\leq(1+r_{+})^{\alpha_{\operatorname{P}}+1}=\bigg(\frac{3}{2}\bigg)^{\alpha_{\operatorname{P}}+1}=a(\alpha_{\operatorname{P}}).

This implies the maximal mass property since

\displaystyle\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}\{\operatorname{B}(x,r)\}=\int_{(x-r)\vee 1}^{x+r}p(x)\,dx\leq 2rp((x-r)\wedge 1)\leq a(\alpha_{\operatorname{P}})p(x)r.

On the other hand, we have, for all
0<r\leq 1/2,

\displaystyle 2\frac{p(x+r)}{p(x)}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{x+r}{x}\bigg)^{\alpha_{\operatorname{P}}+1}\geq 2,

which shows that the minimal mass property holds since,

\displaystyle\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}\{\operatorname{B}(x,r)\}=\int_{(x-r)\vee 1}^{x+r}p(x)\,dx\geq rp(x+r)\geq 2rp(x).

We now show the second claim. We can check that

\displaystyle\int x^{-(\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}+1)/(\rho+1)}\mathds{1}(x\geq 1)\,dx

converges if and only if
\rho>\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}.
Finally,

\displaystyle\int\frac{q(x)}{p(x)^{\gamma}}\,dx=\int x^{\gamma(\alpha_{\operatorname{P}}+1)-\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}-1}\mathds{1}(x\geq 1)\,dx

converges if and only if
\gamma<\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}/(\alpha_{\operatorname{P}}+1).
This finishes the proof.
∎
