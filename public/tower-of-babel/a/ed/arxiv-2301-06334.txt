Abstract
We investigate local properties of weak solutions to nonlocal and nonlinear kinetic equations whose prototype is given by ∂ t u + v ⋅ ∇ x u + ( − Δ v ) p s ​ u = f ⁡ ( u ) . \partial_{t}u+v\cdot\nabla_{x}u+(-\Delta_{v})^{s}_{p}u=f(u). We consider equations whose diffusion part is a (possibly degenerate) integro-differential operator of differentiability order s ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) s\in(0,1) and summability exponent p ∈ ( 1 , ∞ ) p\in(1,\infty) . Amongst other results, we provide an explicit local boundedness estimate by combining together a suitable Sobolev embedding theorem and a fractional Caccioppoli-type inequality with tail. For this, we introduce in the kinetic framework a new definition of nonlocal tail of a function and of its related tail spaces, also by establishing some useful estimates for the tail of weak solutions. Armed with the aforementioned results we give a precise control of the long-range interactions arising from the nonlocal behaviour of the involved diffusion operator.

Introduction

In this paper we deal with a wide class of kinetic Kolmogorov-Fokker-Planck equations, whose diffusion part is an integro-differential operator of differentiability order
s\in(0,1)
and summability order
p\in(1,\infty)
. We investigate local properties of weak solutions to the following equation

\mathcal{T}u(v,x,t)+\mathcal{L}u(v,x,t)=f(v,x,t,u),\qquad\mbox{in}~\mathds{R}^{n}\times\mathds{R}^{n}\times\mathds{R},

(1.1)

where the transport term
\mathcal{T}
is given by

\mathcal{T}:=\partial_{t}+v\cdot\nabla_{x},

(1.2)

and the diffusion term
\mathcal{L}
is defined as follows

\mathcal{L}u(v,x,t):=P.~\!V.\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}\mathcal{A}u(v,w,x,t)\,{\rm d}w,

(1.3)

where, for the sake of brevity, we have denoted by

\mathcal{A}u(v,w,x,t):=\mathcal{K}(v,w)\lvert u(v,x,t)-u(w,x,t)\rvert^{p-2}(u(v,x,t)-u(w,x,t)).

Here,
P.~\!V.
stands for “in the principal value sense” and
\mathcal{K}:\mathds{R}^{n}\times\mathds{R}^{n}\rightarrow[0,+\infty)
is a measurable kernel satisfying

\frac{\lambda}{\lvert v-w\rvert^{n+sp}}\leq\mathcal{K}(v,w)\leq\frac{\Lambda}{\lvert v-w\rvert^{n+sp}},\qquad\mbox{for a.~\!e.}~v,w\in\mathds{R}^{n},\,\Lambda\geq\lambda\geq 1.

(1.4)

Regarding the diffusion part
\mathcal{L}
, we remark that we consider a wide class of integro-differential operators driven by nonsymmetric kernels having only measurable coefficients. For this we have to deal with both the transport operator
\mathcal{T}
in (
1.2
) and the nonlinear and nonlocal behaviour of the involved diffusion
\mathcal{L}
.

As prototype for equation (
1.1
), even though in this scenario the difficulty
arising when dealing with measurable coefficients vanishes, one can consider the simpler case when
\mathcal{K}
does coincide with the classical Gagliardo kernel
\mathcal{K}\equiv\lvert v-w\rvert^{-n-sp}
. In this setting, equation (
1.1
) reduces to

\partial_{t}u+v\cdot\nabla_{x}u+(-\Delta_{v})_{p}^{s}u=f(u),

(1.5)

where
(-\Delta_{v})_{p}^{s}
is the
(s,p)
-Laplacian with respect to the
v
-variable

(-\Delta_{v})_{p}^{s}u(v,x,t):=P.~\!V.\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}\frac{\lvert u(v,x,t)-u(w,x,t)\rvert^{p-2}(u(v,x,t)-u(w,x,t))}{\lvert v-w\rvert^{n+sp}}\,{\rm d}w.

Finally, we assume the source term
f:\mathds{R}^{2n+1}\times\mathds{R}\to\mathds{R}
being measurable with respect to the
(v,x,t)
-variables and continuous with respect to the
u
-variable.

Moreover, we further suppose that
f
satisfies the following growth conditions

|f(v,x,t,u)|\leq\texttt{c}_{\rm o}\lvert u\rvert^{\gamma-1}+\texttt{h}(v,x,t),\qquad\mbox{for a.e.}\ \ (v,x,t,u)\in\mathds{R}^{2n+1}\times\mathds{R},

(1.6)

where:
\textup{{c}}_{\rm o}
is a positive constant;
\textup{{h}}\in L^{\infty}_{\rm loc}(\mathds{R}^{2n+1})
;
\gamma
is a positive parameter such that

1<\gamma\leq p.

(1.7)

1.2
Background and recent results

In recent years, great attention has been focused on the study of nonlocal operators
and their related fractional Sobolev spaces. For this reason, the literature is too wide to
attempt any precise treatment. However, we still mention the pioneering papers
DKP14
;
DKP16
for integro-differential operators whose prototype is the
(s,p)
-Laplacian.
There, the authors proved various regularity results in the same spirit as the De Giorgi-Nash-Moser theory and, among other things, they introduced a new quantity to measure the long-range interactions naturally arising in the study of nonlocal problems, i. ​e.
the nonlocal tail of a function
.
Such quantity has been subsequently used to derived fine properties of solutions to nonlocal equations; see the survey
Pal18
and the references therein for a more exhaustive presentation.

The definition of nonlocal tail was successfully extended at some extent
to the parabolic framework, as e. ​g. in
Strom19
,
where it is used to prove the local boundedness from above of weak subsolutions to fractional nonlinear parabolic operators in the superlinear case, when
p\geq 2
.
See also
DZZ21
where a boundedness estimate is proved for any values of the growth exponent
p\in(1,\infty)
together with a local Hölder continuity in the superlinear case. We also recall
BLS21
for a very interesting proof of a similar result for Hölder continuity of solutions. Hölder regularity for weak solutions to the parabolic
(s,p)
-Laplacian has been very recently extended to any value of
p\in(1,\infty)
in
Liao22
.

As for the
fractional panorama for Fokker-Planck equations
, the theory is far from being complete and, to the best of our knowledge, the known regularity properties are only available in the linear case when
p=2
, e. ​g. Hölder regularity
Sto19
,
L^{p}
-estimates
CZ18
;
HMP19
, hypoelliptic regularity
Li14
and Hölder continuity together with Harnack inequalities
Loh22
. Moreover, we also mention
WD17
for the existence of weak solutions and
Imb05
for existence, uniqueness and regularity of solutions in the viscosity sense.

For what concern the more general setting when a
p
-growth exponent is involved our contribution would be the first. In this regard the forthcoming Theorem
1.2

serves as first step in proving that weak solutions to (
1.1
) enjoy some expected local properties. Also, our result is new even in the linear case when
p=2
, where boundedness is usually assumed as assumption; see for example the case of Hölder continuity and Harnack inequality in
Loh22
. Moreover, our estimates are the nonlocal counterpart of the same results obtained in
AR-harnack
;
GIMV
;
GI
;
GM
. Also, we precisely quantify the
long-range contribution introducing in our functional setting a new definition of kinetic tail; see Definition
1.3
below.

Apart from the novelty of the result, the boundedness estimates obtained are the necessary step, as well known in literature, in order to study local regularity properties of weak solutions to the class of equations we are dealing with. In particular, the problem of the validity of Harnack-type inequalities for weak solutions to (
1.1
) can be addressed.
On one hand, even for the case of parabolic nonlocal operators, a general version of the Harnack inequality for any value of the integrability exponent
p\in(1,\infty)
is not available yet. Indeed, the only results at our disposal in this setting are contained in
Strom19-2
;
Kim19
, where the linear case
p=2
is investigated. On the other hand, a Harnack-type estimate for
nonnegative
solutions is proved in
Loh22
for the case of kinetic nonlocal operators when
p=2
.
Clearly, it can be expected that weak solutions to (
1.1
) satisfy some nonlocal version of the classical Harnack inequalities such as the ones proposed in
DKP14
for the elliptic scenario and in
Strom19-2
;
Kim19
for the parabolic setting. This kind of results will be the object of our forthcoming paper
AP2
, where we aim at establishing Harnack-type estimates by dropping the nonnegativity assumption on solutions made in
(
Loh22
, Theorem 1.1)
and taking in consideration the presence of the nonlocal tail on the righthand side of the inequality.

Furthermore, the study of Dirichlet-type boundary value problems associated to (
1.1
) as well as boundary regularity, in the same spirit of the recent papers
S
;
Z
, are still open problems that we believe are topics of great interest for the community.

1.4
Physical motivations

Equations such as (
1.1
) are interesting because of their many applications to problems arising in
Physics. In particular, the main setting where equations of the type in (
1.1
) do appear is Gas Dynamics.
For instance, when
p=2
, the integrability condition (
1.4
) is compatible
with the assumptions considered in
IS-harnack
for the Boltzmann collision
kernel in the non-cutoff case. The Boltzmann equation

\mathcal{T}u(v,x,t)=\mathcal{Q}(u,u),\quad\text{for }\,(v,x,t)\in\mathds{R}^{2n}\times(0,T),

(1.17)

is a model to describe the dynamics of a dilute gas.
As very well explained in
IS-harnack
, the transport term on the lefthnd side of (
1.17
)
describes the fact that particles travel in straight lines when no external force is applied. On the contrary,
the diffusion term on the righthand side expresses the fluctuations in velocity resulting from particles interactions.
In this setting, the solution
u
represents the density of particles with position
x
and velocity
v
at time
t
, and hence characterizes the state of the gas in a statistical way.
Indeed, this type of physical description of gas dynamics stands in between the trajectory of each individual particle (microscopic scale) and
the macroscopic scale associated to fluid mechanics.

The collision operator
\mathcal{Q}(\cdot,\cdot)
appearing on the righthand side of (
1.17
) is always an integro-differential operator with respect to the velocity acting
on the solution
u(\cdot,x,t)
at any fixed
x
and
t
. Usually, it takes the following form

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(u,u):=\iint_{\mathds{R}^{n}\times\mathds{S}^{n-1}}\left(u(v_{*}^{{}^{\prime}})u(v^{{}^{\prime}})-u(v_{*})u(v)\right)\texttt{B}\left(\lvert v-v_{*}\rvert,\cos\theta\right)\,{\rm d}v_{*}{\rm d}\sigma,

where
v_{*}^{{}^{\prime}}
and
v^{{}^{\prime}}
are computed in terms of
v
,
v_{*}
and
\sigma
by the formula

\displaystyle v^{\prime}=\frac{v+v_{*}}{2}+\frac{\lvert v-v_{*}\rvert}{2}\sigma\quad\text{and}\quad v^{\prime}_{*}=\frac{v+v_{*}}{2}-\frac{\lvert v-v_{*}\rvert}{2}\sigma,

and
\theta
is the angle measuring the deviation between
v
and
v^{\prime}
, whose cosinus is defined as

\displaystyle\cos\theta:=\frac{v+v_{*}}{\lvert v-v_{*}\rvert}\sigma.

In this setting, the couples
v
,
v_{*}
, and
v^{{}^{\prime}}
,
v^{{}^{\prime}}_{*}
represent pre and post collisional velocities of two distinct particles, respectively.
The rate by which particles switch from initial velocities
v
,
v_{*}
to
v^{{}^{\prime}}
,
v^{{}^{\prime}}_{*}
after the collision is given by
B
, which is usually know as
collision kernel. Depending on the shape of the kernel
B
, we are able to model different types of collisions between particles
(see
IS-harnack
for some examples).

More specifically, our assumptions are compatible with the case where the Grad’s cutoff assumption
(ensuring the non singularity of the kernel
B
) is not considered. Thus, when we are allowed to consider a
family of standard non-cutoff collision kernels

\displaystyle\texttt{B}(r,\cos\theta)=r^{\alpha}b(\cos\theta),\quad\text{with }\,b(\cos\theta)=\lvert\sin(\theta/2)\rvert^{-(n-1)-2s},

with
\alpha>-n
and
s\in(0,1)
.
Indeed, by
IS
, (
1.4
) is satisfied by the Boltzmann collision kernel if the macroscopical
physical quantities involved in the statement of
(
IS
, Assumption 1.1)
are bounded.
Hence, our studies can be seen as a generalization of the regularity results on the
Boltzmann equation in the non cut-off case, for which we refer the reader to
IS-harnack
;
IS
and the references therein.

We point out that in our setting
we consider sign changing solutions, as classically done in the nonlinear nonlocal literature. Nevertheless, our estimates
are compatible with the ones obtained in the classical Boltzmann literature, since the nonlocal tail of the solution appearing in every main result
listed in the present work simply disappears when considering nonnegative and essentially bounded functions; see the forthcoming Section
3
.
