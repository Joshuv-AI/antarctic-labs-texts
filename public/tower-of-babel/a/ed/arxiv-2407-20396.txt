Mutual information chain rules for security proofs robust against device imperfections
Amir Arqand, Tony Metger, Ernest Y.-Z. Tan

Abstract
In this work we derive a number of chain rules for mutual information quantities, suitable for analyzing quantum cryptography with imperfect devices that leak additional information to an adversary. First, we derive a chain rule between smooth min-entropy and smooth max-information, which improves over previous chain rules for characterizing one-shot information leakage caused by an additional conditioning register. Second, we derive an “information bounding theorem” that bounds the Rényi mutual information of a state produced by a sequence of channels, in terms of the Rényi mutual information of the individual channel outputs, similar to entropy accumulation theorems. In particular, this yields simple bounds on the smooth max-information in the preceding chain rule. Third, we derive chain rules between Rényi entropies and Rényi mutual information, which can be used to modify the entropy accumulation theorem to accommodate leakage registers sent to the adversary in each round of a protocol. We show that these results can be used to handle some device imperfections in a variety of device-dependent and device-independent protocols, such as randomness generation and quantum key distribution.



1
Introduction

Standard security proofs for quantum cryptography protocols (such as quantum key distribution (QKD) or randomness generation) typically assume that after a measurement is performed, the resulting raw key data remains secret and independent of various processes in subsequent rounds (apart from standard techniques to handle publicly announced classical information)
[
Ren05
]
. However, when moving towards practical device implementations, such an assumption may no longer be a proper description of the actual device,
due to issues such as photon leakage from photodetectors
[
KZM+01
,
PCS+18
]
, or source imperfections and correlations
[
CLPK+23
,
PCLN+23
,
MD24
,
CLNL+23
]
(in which the states generated by a source in a prepare-and-measure protocol are not exactly the desired ones, or have correlations to previous setting choices).
It would hence be more realistic to take into account possible imperfections of the device in security proofs.
Thus, having a method to handle security proofs with imperfect devices is crucial. Such a method should also be robust against a small amount of imperfection. While such a property might sound intuitive, we highlight that phenomena such as information locking
[
KRB+07
,
DHL+04
,
Win17
]
demonstrate that there are situations where even a small amount of leaked information can have drastic effects.

To handle this issue, in this work we propose a variety of chain rules involving mutual information quantities, which can quantify the amount of information leakage from imperfect devices. While this is most intuitively applied to photon leakage or similar mechanisms, we show that it can also be applied to other imperfections such as source correlations as well. From an information-theoretic perspective, our bounds are “natural” in the sense that they involve mutual-information quantities, quantifying the amount of correlation between the leakage and the secret data.
They should also be simple to apply in practice, as our final results simply consist of directly subtracting off some easily computed corrections from the keyrates obtained from existing entropic proof techniques.

We start by proving a chain rule that connects smooth min-entropy and smooth max-information, to characterize the information leakage caused by an extra conditioning register in a one-shot setting. (The smooth min-entropy is the quantity that characterizes extractable secure key length under a composable security definition, hence avoiding issues such as information locking.) Previously, one approach to remove a conditioning leakage register in smooth min-entropy was to use a fairly crude chain rule that just subtracts off the log-dimension of the leakage register. In
[
VDT+13
]
, a tighter bound was proved, in which the leakage information was measured by smooth max-entropy. However, even though that bound is tighter than just the log-dimension, it is still not the tightest bound. Intuitively, when leakage happens, what should matter is the amount of its correlation to the secret data.
This observation is supported by the chain rules for von Neumann entropies, where the effect of removing a conditioning register is characterized by conditional quantum mutual information (CQMI)
[
CA98
]
.

Unfortunately, there has not yet been a unique well-behaved definition of smooth max-CQMI, and in this paper we do not aim to introduce a new definition for this quantity. However, it is well known that an upper bound on CQMI in the von Neumann regime is the mutual information between appropriately chosen registers, which is also a measure of correlation
[
KW20
,
DH11
,
DHW13
]
; furthermore, unlike smooth max-CQMI, there are well-behaved definitions of smooth max-information
[
CBR14
]
. Motivated by these results, we show that one can at least bound the effect of leakage via a smooth max-information between the leakage register and all the other registers; specifically, we prove a chain rule of the form

\displaystyle\text{(informal summary)}\quad H_{\mathrm{min}}^{\epsilon}(S|LE)_{\rho}\geq H_{\mathrm{min}}^{\epsilon^{\prime}}(S|E)_{\rho}-I_{\mathrm{max}}^{\epsilon^{\prime\prime}}(SE;L)_{\rho}-\text{[small corrections]},

(1)

where the registers can be interpreted as
S
being the secret data,
L
being the leakage registers, and
E
being some other side-information. We furthermore argue that in fact the leakage term can be sharpened to something roughly like a smooth max-CQMI, though the resulting quantity may be difficult to analyze and we do not attempt to do so here.

The smooth max-information in the above context would usually involve a large number of registers leaked over the course of a protocol. To obtain a simple bound on it, we next derive an “information bounding theorem”. Informally, suppose we have a bipartite state that is produced by applying a sequence of channels to some initial state, and we are interested in bounding the Rényi mutual information between the registers in the final state. Then, our information bounding theorem provides us with a simple bound based on Rényi mutual information of the individual rounds. Specifically, we derive a bound of the following form:

\displaystyle I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(Z;L_{1}^{n})_{\rho}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sup_{\omega_{\widetilde{Z}R_{i-1}}}I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(\widetilde{Z};L_{i})_{\mathcal{M}_{i}(\omega)},

(2)

where
\{\mathcal{M}_{i}:R_{i-1}\rightarrow R_{i}L_{i}\}_{i}
are a sequence of quantum channels such that
\rho=\mathcal{M}_{n}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{M}_{1}(\rho^{0})
for some initial state
\rho^{0}
, and
Z
is some overall “reference” register not acted on by the channels, while
\widetilde{Z}
is a purifying register for any individual channel.
With this in hand, and existing results that connect smooth max-information to the Rényi mutual information, we can bound the smooth max-information via a bound of the form

\displaystyle\text{(informal summary)}\quad I_{\mathrm{max}}^{\epsilon}(Z;L_{1}^{n})_{\rho}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sup_{\omega_{\widetilde{Z}R_{i-1}}}I(\widetilde{Z};L_{i})_{\mathcal{M}_{i}(\omega)}+O(\sqrt{n}),

(3)

connecting the smooth max-information to the von Neumann mutual information. This can then be applied in the chain rule (
1
) for some protocols, under some assumptions about their structure.

This result is similar to a form of the entropy accumulation theorem (EAT)
[
DFR20
]
or its generalized version (GEAT)
[
MFS+22
]
, except that here the quantity of interest is mutual information, instead of conditional entropy or divergence.

We note that the information bounding theorem does not seem to be a straightforward consequence of the Rényi divergence chain rules in the GEAT
[
MFS+22
]
(although our proof techniques are instead somewhat similar to those for the original EAT
[
DFR20
]
). This is because the GEAT chain rules are formulated in terms of channel divergences, i.e. optimizations over all possible input states to a channel, and it does not seem straightforward to construct a channel that outputs states with the correct “product structure” to yield a Rényi mutual information in the sense of
D_{\alpha}(\rho_{AB}||\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B})
or
\inf_{\sigma_{B}}D_{\alpha}(\rho_{AB}||\rho_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B})
or
\inf_{\omega_{A},\sigma_{B}}D_{\alpha}(\rho_{AB}||\omega_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B})
(see Def.
2.6
).
(Also, since here we are
upper
-bounding the final quantity of interest, we refer to it as a “bounding” theorem rather than “accumulation”; see also Remark
4.1
.) It should be noted that this information bounding theorem is a stand-alone statement that can bound the Rényi mutual information in an
n
-round process, and thus might have other applications in information theory, in scenarios where one might be interested in breaking down the
n
-round Rényi mutual information into its individual rounds.

The preceding results have some limitations on the way one can handle leakage processes with them, in that they essentially analyze the leakage registers as a ‘‘separate’’ process from the main protocol. This is restrictive, since for some device imperfections a more realistic description of the protocol would be to allow the leakage to happen during the protocol itself, and it does not seem straightforward to apply the above tools in various such cases.
2
2

2

However, for some tasks such as randomness generation or expansion, where one sometimes assumes that an adversary does not interact with the devices apart from holding some initial side-information register, the preceding results would indeed already suffice.
For this purpose, we derive yet another chain rule that relates Rényi conditional entropies to Rényi mutual information, in the sense that for states satisfying a suitable channel structure, we can write

\displaystyle\text{(informal summary)}\quad H_{\alpha}^{\uparrow}(A|BC)_{\rho}\geq H_{\alpha}^{\uparrow}(A|B)_{\rho}-\xi_{\alpha},

(4)

where
\xi_{\alpha}
is essentially a Rényi mutual information involving
C
, after taking the “worst-case” value over possible channel outputs.
We apply this chain rule iteratively over sequences of channels to develop variations of entropy accumulation, where leakage is allowed to happen during the protocol and an adversary can interact with it adaptively, and we compensate for it by simply subtracting the sum of
\xi_{\alpha}
values across rounds.

\mathcal{L}_{i}

\mathcal{N}_{i}

A
.)

This result allows us to analyze protocols where a leakage process happens in each round, and the adversary is allowed to adapt their attacks arbitrarily using the leaked information (Fig.
1
). Furthermore, our results should be very simple to apply in practice, since they consist entirely of just subtracting some easily-computed value from the existing keyrate calculations. We describe some simple examples of device imperfections we can handle with our approach, though we leave a detailed analysis for future work. As our analysis is compatible with the entropy accumulation framework, it applies to both device-dependent and device-independent (DI) protocols, with devices that have imperfections outside of the “standard assumptions” in those scenarios.

1.1
Prior work

A Rényi chain rule with a somewhat similar form to our one-shot chain rule in Eq. (
1
) was derived in
[
MT20
]
. Specifically, from their results, we can write the following (or some variants depending on which Rényi conditional entropy or mutual information definitions are used) for suitable Rényi parameters
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime\prime}
:

\displaystyle H_{\alpha^{\prime\prime}}(S|LE)_{\rho}\geq H_{\alpha^{\prime}}(S)_{\rho}-I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(LE;S)_{\rho}\geq H_{\alpha^{\prime}}(S|E)_{\rho}-I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(LE;S)_{\rho},

(5)

where the second inequality holds by data-processing. Alternatively, by applying another instance of their chain rule for some other
\alpha^{\prime\prime\prime},\alpha^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}
, we could instead get

\displaystyle H_{\alpha^{\prime\prime}}(S|LE)_{\rho}\geq H_{\alpha^{\prime}}(S)_{\rho}-I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(LE;S)_{\rho}\geq H_{\alpha^{\prime\prime\prime}}(S|E)_{\rho}+I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}}(E;S)_{\rho}-I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(LE;S)_{\rho}.

(6)

However, inspecting the grouping of registers in the above “leakage” terms shows that they differ nontrivially from our chain rule (
1
), in a way that is harder to use in our intended applications.

Furthermore,
the question of quantum cryptography with leaky devices has been considered in various works:
[
KZM+01
,
PCS+18
,
TZCW+22
,
TPW+21
,
PPW+22
]
considered various forms of leakage behaviour in IID scenarios,
[
JK22
]
considered a leakage model accounting for a bounded number of qubits, and
[
Tan23
]
considered various forms of “probabilistic” constrained leakage. Our results should cover a more general class of models than those works.
Also, various source imperfection models were studied in
[
CLPK+23
,
PCLN+23
,
CLNL+23
]
, while approximate versions of entropy accumulation (which could handle various device imperfections) were developed in
[
MD23
]
, and a related approach was used in
[
MD24
]
to analyze some forms of source imperfection. The class of possible imperfections we consider is not straightforwardly comparable to theirs: for instance,
[
CLPK+23
,
PCLN+23
,
CLNL+23
]
consider the closeness of the generated states to some target states,
[
MD23
]
uses a diamond-norm constraint together with a dimension bound, while
[
MD24
]
uses a form of “testing” on the source. In contrast, we choose to quantify the leakage by bounding the Rényi mutual information. The constraints in our approach could perhaps be obtained from theirs or vice versa in some scenarios, but the conversions would be complicated and likely to be highly suboptimal (with the exception of the imperfections considered in
[
CLPK+23
,
PCLN+23
,
CLNL+23
]
, which we believe could also be incorporated into our approach in principle; see Sec.
6
later). However, we believe that our results should at least yield simpler bounds in the contexts where they apply.

Definition 2.4
.

(Rényi divergence)
For any
\rho,\sigma\in\operatorname{Pos}(A)
with
\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[\rho\right]\neq 0
, and
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty)
, the (sandwiched) Rényi divergence between
\rho
,
\sigma
is defined as:

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(\rho||\sigma)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\frac{\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[\left(\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}\rho\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}\right)^{\alpha}\right]}{\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[\rho\right]}&\left(\alpha<1\ \wedge\ \rho\not\perp\sigma\right)\vee\left(\operatorname{supp}(\rho)\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(\sigma)\right)\\
+\infty&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(14)

where for
\alpha>1
the
\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}
terms are defined via the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse if
\sigma
is not full-support
[
Tom16
]
.
The above definition is extended to
\alpha\in\{0,1,\infty\}
by taking the respective limits, and
the
\alpha=\infty
case is usually referred to as the
max-divergence
.
For the
\alpha=1
case, it reduces to the Umegaki divergence:

\displaystyle D(\rho||\sigma)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[\rho\log\rho-\rho\log\sigma\right]}{\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[\rho\right]}&\operatorname{supp}(\rho)\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(\sigma)\\
+\infty&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(15)

For any two classical probability distributions
\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}
on a common alphabet, the Rényi divergence
D_{\alpha}(\mathbf{p}||\mathbf{q})
is defined analogously, e.g. by viewing the distributions as diagonal density matrices in the above formulas; in the
\alpha=1
case this gives the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence.

Definition 2.5
.

(Stabilized Rényi channel divergence)
For
\mathcal{M}:A\rightarrow B
,
\mathcal{N}:A\rightarrow B
, and
\alpha\in[\frac{1}{2},1)\cup(1,\infty)
, the stabilized Rényi channel divergence is defined as

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(\mathcal{M}||\mathcal{N})\coloneqq\sup_{\omega\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(AR)}D_{\alpha}\left(\mathcal{M}(\omega_{AR})\middle|\middle|\mathcal{N}(\omega_{AR})\right),

(16)

where
R
is isomorphic to
A
.

While we state the following definitions of Rényi conditional entropies and mutual information for subnormalized states, we will in fact not need the subnormalized cases for any

Definition 2.6
.

(Rényi conditional entropies and mutual information)
For any bipartite state
\rho\in\operatorname{S}_{\leq}(AB)
with
\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[\rho\right]\neq 0
, and
\alpha\in[0,\infty]
, we define the following (sandwiched) Rényi conditional entropies and mutual information:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &H_{\alpha}(A|B)_{\rho}\coloneqq-D_{\alpha}(\rho_{AB}||\mathbb{I}_{A}\otimes\rho_{B})\\
&H_{\alpha}^{\uparrow}(A|B)_{\rho}\coloneqq-\inf_{\sigma_{B}\in\operatorname{S}_{\leq}(B)}D_{\alpha}(\rho_{AB}||\mathbb{I}_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B}),\\
&I_{\alpha}(A:B)_{\rho}\coloneqq D_{\alpha}(\rho_{AB}||\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B}),\\
&I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(A;B)_{\rho}\coloneqq\inf_{\sigma_{B}\in\operatorname{S}_{\leq}(B)}D_{\alpha}(\rho_{AB}||\rho_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B}),\\
&I^{\downarrow\downarrow}_{\alpha}(A:B)_{\rho}\coloneqq\inf_{\omega_{A}\in\operatorname{S}_{\leq}(A),\sigma_{B}\in\operatorname{S}_{\leq}(B)}D_{\alpha}(\rho_{AB}||\omega_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B}).\end{aligned}

(17)

All the above optimizations can instead be restricted to normalized states without changing the optimal values.
For
\alpha=1
and
\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[\rho\right]=1
, we have
H_{\alpha}(A|B)=H^{\uparrow}_{\alpha}(A|B)
and their values are equal to the von Neumann conditional entropy; similarly,
I_{\alpha}(A:B)=I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(A;B)=I^{\downarrow\downarrow}_{\alpha}(A:B)
and their values are equal to the von Neumann mutual information.
Note that while
I_{\alpha}
and
I^{\downarrow\downarrow}_{\alpha}
are symmetric with respect to partial registers,
I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}
is not (i.e.,
I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(A;B)_{\rho}\neq I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(B;A)_{\rho}
).

From the definitions, it is clear that
H_{\alpha}\leq H^{\uparrow}_{\alpha}
and
I_{\alpha}\geq I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}\geq I^{\downarrow\downarrow}_{\alpha}
, as suggested by the notation. The fact that the optimizations can be restricted to normalized states follows by observing that the definition of
D_{\alpha}
implies
D_{\alpha}(\rho||t\sigma)=D_{\alpha}(\rho||\sigma)-\log t>D_{\alpha}(\rho||\sigma)
for any
t\in(0,1)
,
so given any subnormalized feasible point, one can always obtain a “better” feasible point by normalizing it.

We use the quantity
I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}
to state the following choice of definition for Rényi conditional mutual information, as a difference between Rényi mutual information terms. However, we note that this definition may not be as “well-behaved” as its von Neumann analogue; for instance, it is unclear whether it satisfies data-processing on
A
. Still, we introduce it because some of our results are most cleanly stated in terms of this definition.

Definition 2.7
.

(Rényi conditional mutual information)
For any tripartite state
\rho\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(ABC)
, and
\alpha\in[0,\infty]
, we define the following (sandwiched) Rényi conditional mutual information

\displaystyle I^{\downarrow,\mathrm{diff}}_{\alpha}(A;B|C)_{\rho}\coloneqq I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(A;BC)_{\rho}-I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(A;C)_{\rho}

(18)

For
\alpha=1
, this definition is equal to the von Neumann conditional mutual information. Note that this definition is not symmetric with respect to partial registers(i.e.,
I^{\downarrow,\mathrm{diff}}_{\alpha}(A;B|C)_{\rho}\neq I^{\downarrow,\mathrm{diff}}_{\alpha}(B;A|C)_{\rho}
).

\displaystyle D_{\infty}^{\epsilon}(\rho||\sigma)\coloneqq\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tilde{\rho}\in\operatorname{S}_{\leq}(A)\text{ s.t.}\\
P(\tilde{\rho},\rho)\leq\epsilon\end{subarray}}D_{\infty}(\tilde{\rho}||\sigma).

(20)

Note that the infimum is attained in the above definition, but it may not necessarily be attained by a normalized state, even if
\rho
is normalized
[
Tom16
]
.

Similarly, we can define various versions of smoothed min-entropy or smoothed max-information, corresponding to the assorted possible definitions of Rényi entropies or mutual information for
\alpha=\infty
. In this work, for all our formal definitions and results we use the notations
H_{\infty}^{\uparrow,\epsilon}
,
I_{\infty}^{\epsilon}
,
I_{\infty}^{\downarrow,\epsilon}
,
I_{\infty}^{\downarrow\downarrow,\epsilon}
(rather than e.g.
H_{\mathrm{min}}^{\epsilon}
or
I_{\mathrm{max}}^{\epsilon}
) for greater clarity in terms of their relations to the above definitions of Rényi conditional entropy and mutual information.
We remark that for smoothed min-entropy, we only introduce the
H^{\uparrow}_{\infty}
version because we do not need the
H_{\infty}
version; in any case the term “min-entropy” is more commonly used to refer to the former version.

Definition 2.10
.

(Smoothed min-entropy and smoothed max-information)
For
\rho\in\operatorname{S}_{\leq}(AB)
with
\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[\rho\right]\neq 0
, and any
\epsilon\in\left[0,\sqrt{\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[\rho_{AB}\right]}\right)
, let

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &H_{\infty}^{\uparrow,\epsilon}(A|B)_{\rho}\coloneqq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tilde{\rho}\in\operatorname{S}_{\leq}(AB)\text{ s.t.}\\
P(\tilde{\rho},\rho)\leq\epsilon\end{subarray}}H_{\infty}^{\uparrow}(A|B)_{\tilde{\rho}}=-\inf_{\sigma_{B}\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(B)}D_{\infty}^{\epsilon}(\rho_{AB}||\mathbb{I}_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B}),\\
&I_{\infty}^{\epsilon}(A:B)_{\rho}\coloneqq\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tilde{\rho}\in\operatorname{S}_{\leq}(AB)\text{ s.t.}\\
P(\tilde{\rho},\rho)\leq\epsilon\end{subarray}}I_{\infty}(A:B)_{\tilde{\rho}},\\
&I_{\infty}^{\downarrow,\epsilon}(A;B)_{\rho}\coloneqq\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tilde{\rho}\in\operatorname{S}_{\leq}(AB)\text{ s.t.}\\
P(\tilde{\rho},\rho)\leq\epsilon\end{subarray}}I_{\infty}^{\downarrow}(A;B)_{\tilde{\rho}},\\
&I_{\infty}^{\downarrow\downarrow,\epsilon}(A:B)_{\rho}\coloneqq\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tilde{\rho}\in\operatorname{S}_{\leq}(AB)\text{ s.t.}\\
P(\tilde{\rho},\rho)\leq\epsilon\end{subarray}}I_{\infty}^{\downarrow\downarrow}(A:B)_{\tilde{\rho}}=\inf_{\omega_{A}\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(A),\sigma_{B}\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(B)}D_{\infty}^{\epsilon}(\rho_{AB}||\omega_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B}).\end{aligned}

(21)

Again, it may not be the case that the optimum in the above definitions is attained by a normalized
\tilde{\rho}
, though we know of two special cases. First, for
H_{\infty}^{\uparrow,\epsilon}(A|B)_{\rho}
with normalized
\rho
, the optimum is attainable in some sense by a normalized
\tilde{\rho}
embedded in a larger Hilbert space
[
Tom16
, Lemma 6.5]
. Second, for
I_{\infty}^{\downarrow,\epsilon}(A;B)_{\rho}
with normalized
\rho
, we observe that the optimum is always attainable by a normalized
\tilde{\rho}
, because the objective function
I_{\infty}^{\downarrow}(A;B)_{\tilde{\rho}}
is “scaling-invariant”: we have
D_{\infty}(t\tilde{\rho}_{AB}||t\tilde{\rho}_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B})=D_{\infty}(\tilde{\rho}_{AB}||\tilde{\rho}_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B})
for any

(and any

\tilde{\rho}
attaining the optimum (also noting that since
\rho
is normalized, this normalization will only increase the value of

according to Def.

). In summary, we have

\displaystyle I_{\infty}^{\downarrow,\epsilon}(A;B)_{\rho}=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tilde{\rho}\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(AB)\text{ s.t.}\\
P(\tilde{\rho},\rho)\leq\epsilon\end{subarray}}I_{\infty}^{\downarrow}(A;B)_{\tilde{\rho}}.

(22)

We will use this property of
I_{\infty}^{\downarrow,\epsilon}(A;B)_{\rho}
in one of our later proofs.

We also remark that in the above definitions, we were able to rewrite

and

3
One-shot chain rule

In this section, we derive a chain rule between smooth min-entropy and smooth max-information, which can be used for one-shot characterization of information leakage caused by an additional conditioning register.
This can be tighter than previous chain rules which were either dimension-based
[
Tom16
]
, or were based on smooth max-entropy
[
VDT+13
]
.

We do so via an “operational” argument, by drawing a connection to state redistribution protocols. Informally, the idea is that if we can “reversibly” compress a quantum register down to some smaller dimension, while in some sense maintaining its correlation with other registers, then the information it contains about other registers should be characterized by only the smaller compressed dimension. To formalize this better, we first define state redistribution protocols:

Definition 3.1
.

(
\delta
-Quantum state redistribution; see
[
DY08
,
ADJ17
]
)
Consider any state
\rho\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(ABCD)
.
The protocol consists of three parties, Alice, Bob and a reference party, where Alice begins with registers
AC
, Bob has
B
, and the reference party has
D
. In addition, Alice and Bob have access to any shared entangled state
\rho_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}
. The goal is for Alice to transmit the
C
register to Bob, possibly assisted by the shared entanglement, such that the final state on
ABCD
after the protocol is
\delta
-close in purified distance to the initial state. More formally, let
Q
denote a quantum register that Alice sends to Bob in the protocol. A
\delta
-quantum state redistribution protocol for
\rho
consists of a pair of encoding and decoding channels
\mathcal{E}:AA^{\prime}C\rightarrow AQ
and
\mathcal{D}:QBB^{\prime}\rightarrow BC
, such that

\displaystyle P\left(\rho_{ABCD},\left(\mathcal{D}\circ\mathcal{E}\right)(\rho_{ABCD}\otimes\rho_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}})\right)\leq\delta.

(33)

We refer to
\log|Q|
as the
quantum communication cost
of the protocol.

Note that in the above definition, we have not imposed a restriction that
\rho_{ABCD}
is pure; however, various existing results for state redistribution are only valid for pure states, so some care is needed when applying those results.
Also, while we have allowed the “message” register
Q
to be quantum, it is a standard result that one can restrict to classical messages by simply doubling the communication cost, because it is an entanglement-assisted scenario and hence the pre-shared entanglement can be used to teleport any one qubit with two classical bits. (For classical
Q
, our chain rule below would just have
\log|Q|
instead of
2\log|Q|
, consistent with this notion that the classical communication cost is twice the quantum communication cost.)

We now use this concept to derive a one-shot chain rule. While the register ordering for the protocol described in this theorem might appear unintuitive at first, we discuss below how in principle it gives the “correct” register ordering for a CQMI-like term to quantify the leakage, even if that term is not necessarily easy to analyze.

Theorem 3.1
.

For any
\rho\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(SLE)
, consider a purification
\left|\rho\right>\!\!\left<\rho\right|\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(SLEP)
.
Suppose there exists a
\delta
-quantum state redistribution protocol for
\rho
(Def.
3.1
) in which Alice holds
PL
, Bob’s initial register is
E
, the reference holds
S
, and the register to be transmitted is
L
. Let the quantum communication cost of the protocol be
\log|Q|
with
Q
being the encoded register as mentioned in that definition. Then for any
\epsilon\in[0,1)
, the following holds:

\displaystyle H_{\infty}^{\uparrow,\epsilon+\delta}(S|LE)_{\rho}\geq H_{\infty}^{\uparrow,\epsilon}(S|E)_{\rho}-2\log|Q|.

(34)

If the register
Q
in the state redistribution protocol is classical, then in the above bound,
2\log|Q|
can be replaced with
\log|Q|
.

Proof.

Consider a
\delta
-quantum state redistribution protocol where Alice, Bob and the reference hold onto the registers indicated in the statement of the theorem. We write the initial state of the protocol as
\rho_{SLEPXY}=\left|\rho\right>\!\!\left<\rho\right|_{SLEP}\otimes\rho_{XY}
, where
\rho_{XY}
is the shared entanglement between Alice and Bob. Then, by Def.
3.1
there exist an encoding
\mathcal{E}:LPX\rightarrow PQ
, and decoding
\mathcal{D}:QEY\rightarrow LE
, such that

\displaystyle P\left(\rho^{\prime\prime}_{SLEP},\rho_{SLEP}\right)\leq\delta

(35)

where (leaving identity maps implicit)
\rho^{\prime\prime}_{SLEP}=\left(\mathcal{D}\circ\mathcal{E}\right)(\rho_{SLEPXY})
. Denoting the state after the encoding map as
\rho^{\prime}_{SEPQY}=\mathcal{E}(\rho_{SLEPXY})
, we have

\displaystyle H_{\infty}^{\uparrow,\epsilon+\delta}(S|LE)_{\rho}

\displaystyle\geq H_{\infty}^{\uparrow,\epsilon}(S|LE)_{\mathcal{D}\circ\mathcal{E}\left[\rho\right]}

\displaystyle\geq H_{\infty}^{\uparrow,\epsilon}(S|QEY)_{\mathcal{E}\left[\rho\right]}

\displaystyle\geq H_{\infty}^{\uparrow,\epsilon}(S|EY)_{\mathcal{E}\left[\rho\right]}-2\log|Q|

\displaystyle=H_{\infty}^{\uparrow,\epsilon}(S|EY)_{\rho}-2\log|Q|

\displaystyle\geq H_{\infty}^{\uparrow,\epsilon}(S|E)_{\rho}-2\log|Q|,

(36)

where the first line holds by Eq. (
35
), the second line is an application of data-processing (Fact
2.1
), the third line follows from Prop. 3.3.4 in
[
Led16
]
, the fourth line follows from the fact that the encoding channel
\mathcal{E}
acts as identity on
SEY

\rho_{SEY}=\rho_{SE}\otimes\rho_{Y}
. Note that by isometric equivalence of purifications and the isometric invariance property of min-entropies, the above proof holds for all purifications of
\rho_{SLE}
.

The version for classical
Q
holds by simply noting that we instead have
H_{\infty}^{\uparrow,\epsilon}(S|QEY)_{\mathcal{E}\left[\rho\right]}\geq H_{\infty}^{\uparrow,\epsilon}(S|EY)_{\mathcal{E}\left[\rho\right]}-\log|Q|
in that case, or alternatively by noting that any such state redistribution protocol can be converted to one where
Q
is fully quantum but halved in size, via dense coding (since we are allowing arbitrary entanglement assistance).
∎

Proof.

]
. For compactness of notation, let us write
B_{\epsilon}(\rho)\subset\operatorname{S}_{\leq}(SLE)
to denote the set of subnormalised states
\epsilon
-close to
\rho
in purified distance, and
L(R)
to denote the set of linear operators on some register
R
.
The proof follows from a sequence of lower bounds on
H_{\infty}^{\uparrow,\epsilon}(X|BC)_{\rho}
. First, from Theorem
3.2
we have

\displaystyle H_{\infty}^{\uparrow,\epsilon}(S|LE)_{\rho}

\displaystyle\geq\max_{\omega\in B_{\epsilon}(\rho)}\left(H_{\infty}^{\uparrow}(S|E)_{\omega}-I_{\infty}(L:SE)_{\omega}\right)

(49)

We can lower bound the maximization by splitting it into two parts as follows:

\displaystyle\text{\eqref{eqn:smoothed1}}\geq\max_{\hat{\omega}\in B_{\epsilon/3}(\rho)}\quad\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}T_{SE}\in L(SE){\text{~s.t.~}}\\
T_{SE}\hat{\omega}_{SLE}T_{SE}^{\dagger}\in B_{2\epsilon/3}(\hat{\omega})\end{subarray}}\left(H_{\infty}^{\uparrow}(S|E)_{T_{SE}\hat{\omega}_{SLE}T_{SE}^{\dagger}}-I_{\infty}(L:SE)_{T_{SE}\hat{\omega}_{SLE}T_{SE}^{\dagger}}\right)

(50)

The above is a lower bound because the maximisation in Eq. (
50
) is over a subset of the maximisation in Eq. (
49
).
We now observe that since
D_{\infty}
satisfies data-processing for any completely positive map
[
Tom16
]
, it follows that
I_{\infty}(L:SE)_{T_{SE}\hat{\omega}_{SLE}T_{SE}^{\dagger}}\leq I_{\infty}(L:SE)_{\hat{\omega}_{SLE}}
. Therefore, it is in turn lower bounded by:

\displaystyle\text{\eqref{eqn:smoothed2}}\geq\max_{\hat{\omega}\in B_{\epsilon/3}(\rho)}\quad\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}T_{SE}\in L(SE){\text{~s.t.~}}\\
T_{SE}\hat{\omega}_{SLE}T_{SE}^{\dagger}\in B_{2\epsilon/3}(\hat{\omega})\end{subarray}}\left(H_{\infty}^{\uparrow}(S|E)_{T_{SE}\hat{\omega}_{SLE}T_{SE}^{\dagger}}-I_{\infty}(L:SE)_{\hat{\omega}_{SLE}}\right)

(51)

We now pick
\hat{\omega}\in B_{\epsilon/3}(\rho)
such that
I_{\infty}(L:SE)_{\hat{\omega}_{SLE}}=I_{\infty}^{\epsilon/3}(L:SE)_{\rho}
, obtaining another lower bound:

\displaystyle\text{\eqref{eqn:smoothed3}}\geq\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}T_{SE}\in L(SE){\text{~s.t.~}}\\
T_{SE}\hat{\omega}_{SLE}T_{SE}^{\dagger}\in B_{2\epsilon/3}(\hat{\omega})\end{subarray}}\left(H_{\infty}^{\uparrow}(S|E)_{T_{SE}\hat{\omega}_{SLE}T_{SE}^{\dagger}}\right)-I_{\infty}^{\epsilon/3}(L:SE)_{\rho}

(52)

Let
\tilde{\rho}_{SE}\in B_{\epsilon/3}(\rho_{SE})
be such that
H_{\infty}^{\uparrow,\epsilon/3}(S|E)_{\rho}=H_{\infty}^{\uparrow}(S|E)_{\tilde{\rho}}
.
By
[
DBW+14
, Lemma B.3]
there exists a
\hat{T}_{SE}
such that
\hat{T}_{SE}\hat{\omega}_{SLE}\hat{T}_{SE}^{\dagger}\in\operatorname{S}_{\leq}(SLE)
is an extension of
\tilde{\rho}_{SE}
and
P(\hat{\omega}_{SLE},\hat{T}_{SE}\hat{\omega}_{SLE}\hat{T}_{SE}^{\dagger})=P(\hat{\omega}_{SE},\tilde{\rho}_{SE})
.
We can bound the latter quantity using the triangle inequality for the purified distance, obtaining
P(\hat{\omega}_{SE},\tilde{\rho}_{SE})\leq P(\hat{\omega}_{SE},\rho_{SE})+P(\rho_{SE},\tilde{\rho}_{SE})\leq\epsilon/3+\epsilon/3=2\epsilon/3
.
Therefore, this
\hat{T}_{SE}
lies inside the set of
T_{SE}
over which we optimise, so we can pick
\hat{T}_{SE}
to get a final lower bound:

\displaystyle H_{\infty}^{\uparrow}(S|E)_{\tilde{\rho}}-I_{\infty}^{\epsilon/3}(L:SE)_{\rho}=H_{\infty}^{\uparrow,\epsilon/3}(S|E)_{\rho}-I_{\infty}^{\epsilon/3}(L:SE)_{\rho}\,,

(53)

as claimed.
∎

4
Information bounding theorem

We now derive a theorem that bounds the Rényi mutual information produced by a sequence of channels, in a similar sense to the entropy accumulation theorem
[
DFR20
,
MFS+22
]
, except that here we instead have an upper bound (which in some sense cannot have a “matching” lower bound; see Remark
4.1
later).
To achieve this, we start with the following lemma which can be used to establish a chain rule for Rényi mutual information.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\rho\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(ABC)
,
\sigma\in\operatorname{Pos}(C)
,
\omega^{\prime}\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(A)
, and
\omega^{\prime\prime}\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(B)
. Then, for any
\alpha\in[0,\infty)
we have

\displaystyle\inf_{\omega_{AB}\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(AB)}D_{\alpha}(\rho_{ABC}||\omega_{AB}\otimes\sigma_{C})\leq D_{\alpha}(\rho_{AC}||\omega^{\prime}_{A}\otimes\sigma_{C})+D_{\alpha}(\nu_{ABC}||\omega^{\prime\prime}_{B}\otimes\nu_{AC}),

(54)

where
\nu_{ABC}=\nu_{AC}^{\frac{1}{2}}\rho_{B|AC}\nu_{AC}^{\frac{1}{2}}
, with

\displaystyle\nu_{AC}=\frac{\left(\rho_{AC}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\omega^{\prime}_{A}\otimes\sigma_{C}\right)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\rho_{AC}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{\alpha}}{\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(\rho_{AC}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\omega^{\prime}_{A}\otimes\sigma_{C}\right)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\rho_{AC}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{\alpha}\right]}.

(55)

In particular

\displaystyle I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(C;AB)_{\rho}\leq I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(C;A)_{\rho}+I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(AC;B)_{\nu}

(56)

Proof.

The proof follows from the following crucial relation:

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(\rho_{ABC}||\omega^{\prime}_{A}\otimes\omega^{\prime\prime}_{B}\otimes\sigma_{C})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(\rho_{ABC}\left(\omega^{\prime}_{A}\otimes\omega^{\prime\prime}_{B}\otimes\sigma_{C}\right)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\right)^{\alpha}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(\rho_{ABC}\rho_{AC}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\rho_{AC}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\omega^{\prime}_{A}\otimes\omega^{\prime\prime}_{B}\otimes\sigma_{C}\right)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\rho_{AC}^{\frac{1}{2}}\rho_{AC}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{\alpha}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(\rho_{ABC}\rho_{AC}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\rho_{AC}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\omega^{\prime}_{A}\otimes\sigma_{C}\right)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\rho_{AC}^{\frac{1}{2}}\rho_{AC}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\otimes\omega^{\prime\prime\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}_{B}\right)^{\alpha}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(\rho_{ABC}\rho_{AC}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\nu_{AC}^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\rho_{AC}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\otimes\omega^{\prime\prime\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}_{B}\right)^{\alpha}\right]+\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(\rho_{AC}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\omega^{\prime}_{A}\otimes\sigma_{C}\right)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\rho_{AC}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{\alpha}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(\rho_{B|AC}\nu_{AC}^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\otimes\omega^{\prime\prime\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}_{B}\right)^{\alpha}\right]+D_{\alpha}(\rho_{AC}||\omega^{\prime}_{A}\otimes\sigma_{C})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(\nu_{ABC}\left(\nu_{AC}\otimes\omega^{\prime\prime}_{B}\right)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\right)^{\alpha}\right]+D_{\alpha}(\rho_{AC}||\omega^{\prime}_{A}\otimes\sigma_{C})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(\nu_{ABC}||\omega^{\prime\prime}_{B}\otimes\nu_{AC})+D_{\alpha}(\rho_{AC}||\omega^{\prime}_{A}\otimes\sigma_{C}),

(57)

where the second line follows from the cyclic properties of trace, fourth line is just a reshuffling, fifth line follows from substituting Eq. (
55
), the sixth line is a reshuffling and using the definition of conditional state in Def.
2.3
, and the penultimate line is from definition of
\nu_{ABC}
, and reshuffling terms.

From Eq. (
57
) we immediately obtain Eq. (
54
) as a consequence,
by simply picking
\omega_{AB}=\omega^{\prime}_{A}\otimes\omega^{\prime\prime}_{B}
as a feasible point in the infimum over
\omega_{AB}
.
Now to prove Eq. (
56
), we pick
\sigma_{C}=\rho_{C}
, and
\omega^{\prime\prime}_{B}=\omega^{*}_{B},\omega^{\prime}_{A}=\omega^{*}_{A}
such that the following relations hold:

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(\nu_{ABC}||\omega^{*}_{B}\otimes\nu_{AC})=\inf_{\sigma_{B}\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(B)}D_{\alpha}(\nu_{ABC}||\sigma_{B}\otimes\nu_{AC})

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(\rho_{AC}||\omega^{*}_{A}\otimes\rho_{C})=\inf_{\sigma_{A}\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(A)}D_{\alpha}(\rho_{AC}||\sigma_{A}\otimes\rho_{C}).

(58)

With these choices, Eq. (
54
) states:

\displaystyle I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(C;AB)_{\rho}

\displaystyle=\inf_{\sigma_{AB}\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(AB)}D_{\alpha}(\rho_{ABC}||\sigma_{AB}\otimes\rho_{C})

\displaystyle\leq D_{\alpha}(\rho_{AC}||\omega^{*}_{A}\otimes\rho_{C})+D_{\alpha}(\nu_{ABC}||\omega^{*}_{B}\otimes\nu_{AC})

\displaystyle=I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(C;A)_{\rho}+I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(AC;B)_{\nu},

(59)

where the second line follows from definition
2.6
.
∎

Remark 4.1
.

Eq. (
57
) in the above proof (which is an
equality
, not an inequality) can be viewed as a Rényi analogue of the following relation for von Neumann mutual information:

\displaystyle D(\rho_{ABC}||\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B}\otimes\rho_{C})=D(\rho_{AC}||\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{C})+D(\rho_{ABC}||\rho_{B}\otimes\rho_{AC})=I(A:C)+I(AC:B),

(60)

where the quantity on the left-hand-side is sometimes termed the
multipartite mutual information

I(A:B:C)\coloneqq D(\rho_{ABC}||\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B}\otimes\rho_{C})
, and is an upper bound on
I(AB:C)
. Hence qualitatively speaking, our above bound is loose up to the difference between the Rényi analogues of
I(A:B:C)
and
I(AB:C)
(putting aside the fact that one of the terms in our bound is evaluated on a new state
\nu
). Alternatively, we can view our bound as being a loosened version of the chain rule for von Neumann mutual information:

\displaystyle I(AB:C)=I(A:C)+I(A:B|C),

(61)

in which the
I(A:B|C)
term has been relaxed to its upper bound
I(AC:B)
.

Due to this, it is not straightforward to find a converse to our above bound that would instead prove the Rényi mutual information “accumulates”, since our bound is inherently somewhat loose compared to the von Neumann versions.
However, despite this looseness, our bounds still suffice for various applications, as we describe later.

The drawback of Eq. (
56
) is that
I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(AC;B)_{\nu}
has to be evaluated over a complicated state; this difficulty can be avoided in that term by replacing it with an optimization over input states to some channel.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\rho^{\prime}\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(ARC)
and
\mathcal{N}:R\rightarrow B
be a channel. Let
\rho_{ABC}=\mathcal{N}(\rho^{\prime}_{ARC})
, then for any
\alpha\in[0,\infty)
we have:

\displaystyle I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(C;AB)_{\mathcal{N}(\rho^{\prime})}\leq I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(C;A)_{\rho^{\prime}}+\sup_{\omega\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(ARC)}I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(AC;B)_{\mathcal{N}(\omega)}.

(62)

Proof.

Defining
S_{\rho}\coloneqq\left\{\sigma\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(ABC)\middle|\ \sigma_{B|AC}=\rho_{B|AC}\right\}
, we have

\displaystyle I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(C;AB)_{\rho}\leq I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(C;A)_{\rho}+\sup_{\sigma\in S_{\rho}}I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(AC;B)_{\sigma},

(63)

where the inequality follows from Eq. (
56
), and by noting that
\nu_{ABC}
in that equation is a feasible solution to the above optimization. Since by definition
\rho^{\prime}_{AC}=\rho_{AC}
, it is now only needed to show that the optimization in Eq. (
62
) contains the set
S_{\rho}
defined above (i.e.,
\nu_{ABC}\in S_{\rho}
). In order to do so let
\rho_{ABC}=\mathcal{N}(\rho^{\prime}_{ARC})
, Defining
\omega\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(ARC)
such that

\displaystyle\omega_{ARC}=\nu_{AC}^{\frac{1}{2}}\rho_{AC}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\rho^{\prime}_{ARC}\rho_{AC}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\nu_{AC}^{\frac{1}{2}},

(64)

then we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\omega_{ARC})

\displaystyle=\nu_{AC}^{\frac{1}{2}}\rho_{AC}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{N}(\rho^{\prime}_{ARC})\rho_{AC}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\nu_{AC}^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\nu_{AC}^{\frac{1}{2}}\rho_{B|AC}\nu_{AC}^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\nu_{ABC}.

(65)

where the last line follows from definition of
\nu_{ABC}
in lemma
4.1
.
∎

Having proven the above chain rule, we can iteratively apply it to get the information bounding theorem in Eq. (
2
), which bounds the Rényi mutual information of a state that is produced by a sequence of channels, in terms of the Rényi mutual information of the individual channel outputs. We can also connect it to the smooth max-information of the final state, for application in the Corollary
3.1
–
3.2
chain rules we described above (after converting to
I_{\infty}^{\downarrow,\epsilon}
or
I_{\infty}^{\epsilon}
via
[
CBR14
, Theorem 3]
):

Theorem 4.1

(Information bounding theorem)
.

Consider a sequence of channels
\left\{\mathcal{M}_{i}:R_{i-1}\rightarrow R_{i}L_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
. Let
\epsilon\in[0,1)
. Then, for any
\alpha>1
, and any
\rho\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(R_{0}SE)
we have:

\displaystyle I_{\infty}^{\downarrow\downarrow,\epsilon}(SE:L_{1}^{n})_{\mathcal{M}_{n}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{M}_{1}(\rho_{R_{0}SE})}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sup_{\sigma\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(R_{i-1}SE)}I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(L_{1}^{i-1}SE;L_{i})_{\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma_{R_{i-1}SE})}-\frac{g(\epsilon)}{\alpha-1},

(66)

where
g(\epsilon)=\log\left(1-\sqrt{1-\epsilon^{2}}\right)
.

Proof.

First note that by combining Fact
2.3
with the generic inequality
I^{\downarrow\downarrow}_{\alpha}\leq I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}
, we have

\displaystyle I_{\infty}^{\downarrow\downarrow,\epsilon}(SE:L_{1}^{n})_{\mathcal{M}_{n}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{M}_{1}(\rho_{R_{0}SE})}

\displaystyle\leq I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(SE;L_{1}^{n})_{\mathcal{M}_{n}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{M}_{1}(\rho_{R_{0}SE})}-\frac{g(\epsilon)}{\alpha-1}.

(67)

Then to bound the Rényi mutual information term, we employ Lemma
4.2
:

\displaystyle I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(SE;L_{1}^{n})_{\mathcal{M}_{n}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{M}_{1}(\rho_{R_{0}SE})}\leq I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(SE;L_{1}^{n-1})_{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{M}_{1}(\rho_{R_{0}SE})}+\sup_{\sigma\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(R_{n-1}SE)}I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(L_{1}^{n-1}SE;L_{n})_{\mathcal{M}_{n}(\sigma_{R_{n-1}SE})}.

(68)

Repeating this step
n
times we get:

\displaystyle I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(SE;L_{1}^{n})_{\mathcal{M}_{n}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{M}_{1}(\rho_{R_{0}SE})}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sup_{\sigma\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(R_{i-1}SE)}I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(L_{1}^{i-1}SE;L_{i})_{\mathcal{M}_{i}(\sigma_{R_{i-1}SE})}.

(69)

Combining Eq. (
69
) with (
67
) proves the Theorem.
∎

In principle (assuming the registers have bounded dimension), one could bound the single-round Rényi mutual information terms in terms of von Neumann mutual information via continuity bounds of the form
I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(L_{1}^{i-1}SE;L_{i})\leq I(L_{1}^{i-1}SE;L_{i})+O(\alpha-1)
(see
[
DFR20
,
DF19
]
; the bounds in the latter may be sharper in some contexts). By choosing
\alpha=1+\Theta(1/\sqrt{n})
, one would then obtain a bound relating smooth max-information to von Neumann mutual information in the form described in Eq. (
3
). However, we note later in Sec.
6
that tighter bounds can be usually obtained by analyzing the Rényi mutual information directly. We also defer the discussion of how to get explicit bounds on these quantities to that section.

Remark 4.2
.

Note that another work
[
CMW16
]
has also analyzed Rényi mutual information quantities in some “sequential” processes, though mainly those focused on contexts such as quantum hypothesis testing and quantum-feedback-assisted channel capacities. We believe that the proof techniques in that work (specifically, Sec. 5) can be used to obtain a bound with a somewhat similar form to Theorem
4.1
, but where the global state is instead
\mathcal{M}_{n}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathcal{M}_{1}(\rho_{R_{1}^{n}SE})
for some channels
\left\{\mathcal{M}_{i}:R_{i}\rightarrow L_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
; in other words, it is produced by a tensor product of channels rather than a sequence of channels. However, we leave a detailed investigation of this point for future work. We thank M. Wilde for discussions regarding this work.

While the approach of combining Corollary
3.1
or
3.2
with Theorem
4.1
is fairly straightforward to implement, it has a drawback in that it requires the registers
L_{1}^{n}
to be handled “separately” from the other side-information. This implies for instance that an adversary Eve in a protocol cannot “adapt” her attack using the leakage register
L_{i}
in each round (see the “restricted adaptiveness” condition described in
[
Tan23
]
). Also, there may be some subtle conditions in ensuring that the channels describing leakage in the protocol satisfy the conditions of Theorem
4.1
; for instance, it seems we would require all of the secret raw data to be generated first, and then keep a separate register
R_{0}
from which the leakage registers are produced.
7
7

7

One simple approach would be just to have
R_{0}
be a classical copy of
S
, but there may be some leakage models in which such a channel structure does not quite work.
In the next section, we describe a different approach that can handle this issue, by having the leakage processes “built in” to an entropy accumulation framework.

5
Entropy accumulation with leakage

One drawback of the entropy accumulation framework is that it cannot directly handle some forms of device imperfections, such as photon leakage from the measurement devices, or source correlations.
The reason for this is that to analyze such scenarios, one essentially needs to introduce a leakage register that sends information to the adversary in each round. However, such a construction violates the non-signalling (NS) condition in
[
MFS+22
]
, or the Markov condition in
[
DFR20
]
. In this section we derive a chain rule which can be used to modify the entropy accumulation theorem in a way that allows us to handle device imperfections.

5.1
Relating Rényi conditional entropies to Rényi mutual information

We begin with the following corollary that states a somewhat similar result to Corollaries
3.1
and
3.2
, but instead relates Rényi conditional entropies to Rényi mutual information.

Corollary 5.1
.

Let
\rho\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(ABC)
and
\nu_{ABC}=\nu_{AC}^{\frac{1}{2}}\rho_{B|AC}\nu_{AC}^{\frac{1}{2}}
, where
\nu_{AC}
is defined in (
55
). Then, for any
\alpha\in[0,\infty)
we have

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}^{\uparrow}(C|AB)_{\rho}\geq H_{\alpha}^{\uparrow}(C|A)_{\rho}-I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(AC;B)_{\nu}.

(70)

Moreover, let
\rho^{\prime}\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(RAC)
, and
\mathcal{M}:R\rightarrow B
be a channel such that
\rho_{ABC}=\mathcal{M}(\rho^{\prime}_{RAC})
. Then, the above relation can be relaxed to the following

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}^{\uparrow}(C|AB)_{\mathcal{M}(\rho^{\prime})}\geq H_{\alpha}^{\uparrow}(C|A)_{\rho^{\prime}}-\sup_{\omega\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(RAC)}I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(AC;B)_{\mathcal{M}(\omega)}.

(71)

Proof.

The proof of Eq.(
70
) follows directly by applying Lemma
4.1
, setting the states in that lemma to be
\sigma_{C}=\mathbb{I}_{C}
and
\omega^{\prime}_{A},\ \omega^{\prime\prime}_{B}
to be such that

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(\rho_{AC}||\omega^{\prime}_{A}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{C})=\inf_{\sigma_{A}\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(A)}D_{\alpha}(\rho_{AC}||\sigma_{A}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{C}),

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(\nu_{ABC}||\omega^{\prime\prime}_{B}\otimes\nu_{AC})=\inf_{\sigma_{B}\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(B)}D_{\alpha}(\nu_{ABC}||\sigma_{B}\otimes\nu_{AC}).

(72)

Then we have

\displaystyle H^{\uparrow}_{\alpha}(C|AB)_{\rho}

\displaystyle=-\inf_{\sigma_{AB}\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(AB)}D_{\alpha}(\rho_{ABC}||\sigma_{AB}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{C})

\displaystyle\geq-D_{\alpha}(\rho_{AC}||\omega^{\prime}_{A}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{C})-D_{\alpha}(\nu_{ABC}||\omega^{\prime\prime}_{B}\otimes\nu_{AC})

\displaystyle=H^{\uparrow}_{\alpha}(C|A)_{\rho}-I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(AC;B)_{\nu},

(73)

where the second line follows by applying Lemma
4.1
, and the last line holds by definition. To prove Eq. (
71
), let us define
S_{\rho}=\left\{\sigma\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(ABC)\middle|\ \sigma_{B|AC}=\rho_{B|AC}\right\}
. Since by definition
\nu_{B|AC}=\rho_{B|AC}
, we have
\nu_{ABC}\in S_{\rho}
, and thus we can relax Eq. (
70
) to the following:

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}^{\uparrow}(C|AB)_{\rho}\geq H_{\alpha}^{\uparrow}(C|A)_{\rho}-\sup_{\sigma\in S_{\rho}}I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(AC;B)_{\sigma}.

(74)

It is now suffices to show that the optimization in Eq. (
70
) contains the set
S_{\rho}
. Defining
\omega\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(RAC)
as

\displaystyle\omega_{RAC}=\nu_{AC}^{\frac{1}{2}}\rho_{AC}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\rho^{\prime}_{RAC}\rho_{AC}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\nu_{AC}^{\frac{1}{2}}

(75)

Then we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\omega_{RAC})

\displaystyle=\nu_{AC}^{\frac{1}{2}}\rho_{AC}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{M}(\rho^{\prime}_{RAC})\rho_{AC}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\nu_{AC}^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\nu_{ABC}.

(76)

It is clear that
\mathcal{M}(\omega_{RAC})\in S_{\rho}
, and thus the lemma is proven.
∎

The result in Corollary
5.1
can be tightened further in a certain sense. Intuitively, at least for the limit of
\alpha\rightarrow 1
(i.e., the von Neumann limit), one would expect that when removing a conditional register from conditional entropy, the penalty term should be a conditional mutual information as opposed to just the unconditional mutual information. In the following, we derive a chain rule with a Rényi version of conditional mutual information. We begin by proving a statement without any conditioning registers on the right-hand-side:

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\rho\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(AB)
,
\sigma\in\operatorname{Pos}(B)
, and
\omega\in\operatorname{Pos}(A)
. Then, for any
\alpha\in[0,\infty)
we have

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(\rho_{AB}||\omega_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B})=D_{\alpha}(\rho_{A}||\omega_{A})+D_{\alpha}(\eta_{AB}||\eta_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B}),

(77)

where
\eta_{AB}=\eta_{A}^{\frac{1}{2}}\rho_{B|A}\eta_{A}^{\frac{1}{2}}
, with

\displaystyle\eta_{A}=\frac{\left(\rho_{A}^{\frac{1}{2}}\omega_{A}^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\rho_{A}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{\alpha}}{\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(\rho_{A}^{\frac{1}{2}}\omega_{A}^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\rho_{A}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{\alpha}\right]}.

(78)

In particular

\displaystyle H^{\uparrow}_{\alpha}(A|B)_{\rho}=H_{\alpha}(A)_{\rho}-I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(A;B)_{\eta}.

(79)

Proof.

The proof holds by the following chain of equalities:

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(\rho_{AB}||\omega_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(\rho_{AB}\left(\omega_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B}\right)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\right)^{\alpha}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(\rho_{AB}\rho_{A}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\rho_{A}^{\frac{1}{2}}\omega_{A}^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\rho_{A}^{\frac{1}{2}}\rho_{A}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\otimes\sigma_{B}^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\right)^{\alpha}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(\rho_{B|A}\eta^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\otimes\sigma_{B}^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\right)^{\alpha}\right]+\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(\rho_{A}^{\frac{1}{2}}\omega_{A}^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\rho_{A}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{\alpha}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(\nu_{AB}\left(\eta_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B}\right)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\right)^{\alpha}\right]+D_{\alpha}(\rho_{A}||\omega_{A})

\displaystyle=D_{\alpha}(\eta_{AB}||\eta_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B})+D_{\alpha}(\rho_{A}||\omega_{A}),

(80)

where the first line is due to the cyclic property of trace, the third line follows from Def.
2.3
and substituting Eq. (
78
), and the fourth line follows from reshuffling terms and the definition of
\eta_{AB}
. To prove Eq. (
79
), we take
\omega_{A}=\mathbb{I}_{A}
in (
77
), and take an infimum from both sides. Thus we have:

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}^{\uparrow}(A|B)_{\rho}

\displaystyle=-\inf_{\sigma_{B}\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(B)}D_{\alpha}(\rho_{AB}||\omega_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B})

\displaystyle=-D_{\alpha}(\rho_{A}||\mathbb{I}_{A})-\inf_{\sigma_{B}\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(B)}D_{\alpha}(\eta_{AB}||\eta_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B})

\displaystyle=H_{\alpha}(A)_{\rho}-I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(A;B)_{\eta},

(81)

which completes the proofs.
∎

With this, we can obtain a bound involving a form of Rényi conditional mutual information:

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
\rho\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(ABC)
, and set
\eta_{ABC}\coloneqq\eta_{A}^{\frac{1}{2}}\rho_{BC|A}\eta_{A}^{\frac{1}{2}}
where
\eta_{A}
is the state in Eq. (
78
). Then, for any
\alpha\in[0,\infty)
:

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}^{\uparrow}(A|BC)_{\rho}=H_{\alpha}^{\uparrow}(A|B)_{\rho}-I^{\downarrow,\mathrm{diff}}_{\alpha}(A;C|B)_{\eta}.

(82)

Moreover, let
\rho^{\prime}\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(RA)
, and
\mathcal{N}:R\rightarrow BC
be a channel such that
\rho_{ABC}=\mathcal{N}(\rho^{\prime}_{RA})
. Then, Eq. (
82
) can be relaxed to

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}^{\uparrow}(A|B)_{\mathcal{N}(\rho^{\prime})}-\sup_{\omega\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(RA)}I^{\downarrow,\mathrm{diff}}_{\alpha}(A;C|B)_{\mathcal{N}(\omega)}\leq H_{\alpha}^{\uparrow}(A|BC)_{\mathcal{N}(\rho^{\prime})}\leq H_{\alpha}^{\uparrow}(A|B)_{\mathcal{N}(\rho^{\prime})}-\inf_{\omega\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(RA)}I^{\downarrow,\mathrm{diff}}_{\alpha}(A;C|B)_{\mathcal{N}(\omega)}.

(83)

Proof.

We start by applying Lemma
5.1
to the left side of the above equality:

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}^{\uparrow}(A|BC)_{\rho}

\displaystyle=H_{\alpha}(A)_{\rho}-I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(A;BC)_{\eta}

\displaystyle=H_{\alpha}^{\uparrow}(A|B)_{\rho}+I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(A;B)_{\eta}-I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(A;BC)_{\eta}

\displaystyle=H_{\alpha}^{\uparrow}(A|B)_{\rho}-I^{\downarrow,\mathrm{diff}}_{\alpha}(A;C|B)_{\eta},

(84)

where the second line follows from applying Eq. (
78
) and noting
\eta_{AB}=\operatorname{Tr}_{C}\eta_{ABC}=\eta_{A}^{\frac{1}{2}}\rho_{B|A}\eta_{A}^{\frac{1}{2}}
, and the last line follows from the definition of Rényi conditional mutual information in Def.
2.7
.
To prove the relaxed version let us first define
S_{\rho}=\left\{\sigma\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(ABC)\middle|\ \sigma_{BC|A}=\rho_{BC|A}\right\}
. Then, we have:

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}^{\uparrow}(A|B)_{\rho}-\sup_{\sigma\in S_{\rho}}I^{\downarrow,\mathrm{diff}}_{\alpha}(A;C|B)_{\sigma}\leq H_{\alpha}^{\uparrow}(A|BC)_{\rho}\leq H_{\alpha}^{\uparrow}(A|B)_{\rho}-\inf_{\sigma\in S_{\rho}}I^{\downarrow,\mathrm{diff}}_{\alpha}(A;C|B)_{\sigma},

(85)

where the inequalities hold by noting that by definition,
\eta_{BC|A}=\rho_{BC|A}
, therefore,
\eta_{ABC}\in S_{\rho}
. Thus, we only need to show that the optimizations in Eq. (
83
) contain the set
S_{\rho}
. Let
\rho_{ABC}=\mathcal{N}(\omega_{RA})
and define
\omega\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(RA)
such that the following holds:

\displaystyle\omega_{RA}=\eta_{A}^{\frac{1}{2}}\rho_{A}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\rho^{\prime}_{RA}\rho_{A}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\eta_{A}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(86)

Then,

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\omega_{RA})

\displaystyle=\eta_{A}^{\frac{1}{2}}\rho_{A}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{N}(\rho^{\prime}_{RA})\rho_{A}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\eta_{A}^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\eta_{ABC}.

(87)

It is then clear that
\mathcal{N}(\omega_{RA})\in S_{\rho}
, which completes the proof.
∎

As compared to Corollary
5.1
, the above chain rule in Corollary
5.2
has the advantage that as
\alpha\to 1

5.1

being potentially a less well-behaved quantity. (Note that we also cannot view Corollary

directly as a relaxation of Corollary

shows that the registers are ordered in a way that does not yield Corollary

Example applications

Qualitatively, our results in the preceding section (and Appendix

\displaystyle\sup_{\omega\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(\widetilde{Z}R)}I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(\widetilde{Z};L)_{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\omega)}.

(104)

In other words, we simply need to upper bound the Rényi mutual information between the channel output and some arbitrary purification of its input. Given a bound on the above quantity

We now outline some device-imperfection scenarios in which such a model can be considered, before explaining possible methods to explicitly bound the above quantity.

6.1
Types of device imperfections

The most straightforward way to apply this model would be if there is an actual physical system
L_{j}
that leaks to the adversary Eve in each round, and we can constrain its correlations to the secret data. For instance, this could be a photonic system that we constrain by requiring it to have a large vacuum component, as considered in
[
KZM+01
,
PCS+18
]
for device-dependent protocols and in
[
Tan23
]
for device-independent protocols. In particular, this resolves an issue regarding a “restricted adaptiveness” condition imposed on Eve in
[
Tan23
]
— under the model we consider here, Eve is free to modify her attack in every round based on the leakage registers gathered from previous rounds. Hence our result allows us to handle such leakage mechanisms even against the most general adaptive attacks.

However, our method can also apply to less obviously related forms of device imperfection, such as source correlation. To sketch out the ideas, a typical prepare-and-measure protocol consists of Alice generating some secret classical value
S_{j}
in each round, and then using some source device to prepare a quantum state (to be sent to Bob) that depends only on the value of
S_{j}
. Such a source can be handled using the GEAT in a PM protocol, as it satisfies the NS condition. The notion of source correlation refers to the possibility that a realistic source device may be imperfect, in that the state it generates might depend not only on the
S_{j}
value in that round, but also the values used in previous rounds (or even more generally, it might even be entangled with the quantum states it prepared in previous rounds).

While it may not be immediately apparent that our results can be used to study such a scenario, we now argue that in fact they can be, for some classes of source correlations.
Specifically, our results can be applied whenever the source correlation can be modelled as follows (again leaving out the
j
subscripts for brevity): after each round, the source retains a memory register
R
for the next round. Then each time Alice uses it to prepare a state, the source first prepares the ideal specified state (on some register, say,
A^{\prime}
), then applies a channel of the form
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}:R\to L
, and finally applies some channel
A^{\prime}L\to A^{\prime}
to modify the state it prepared on
A^{\prime}
.
9
9

9

Strictly speaking, for full accuracy in this model, various channels should have additional output registers to allow memory to be transferred onwards to future rounds. However, these are not pertinent to our discussion here and so we omit them for brevity.

A concrete example of a simple scenario we can capture with this would be for instance if the source behaves as follows in each round:

•

With some probability
1-\delta_{\mathrm{leak}}
the source perfectly prepares the ideal state on
A^{\prime}
.

•

Otherwise (with probability
\delta_{\mathrm{leak}}
), it modifies the prepared state on
A^{\prime}
via some arbitrary quantum channel
A^{\prime}R\to A^{\prime}
.

This can be captured within our model as follows. Let
L
be a quantum register isomorphic to
R
except with one additional dimension given by a basis state we denote as
\left|\perp\right>_{L}
. We describe the source device behaviour as:

•

It first applies a channel
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}:R\to L
, which with probability
1-\delta_{\mathrm{leak}}
completely ignores the input and outputs
\left|\perp\right>\!\!\left<\perp\right|_{L}
, and otherwise embeds the input state on
R
into the output register
L
.

•

It then perfectly prepares the ideal state on
A^{\prime}
.

•

It then performs a two-outcome projective measurement on
L
, onto the subspace spanned by
\left|\perp\right>_{L}
and the orthogonal subspace. Conditioned on the outcome, it either simply leaves the state on
A^{\prime}

6.2
Bounding the mutual information

We now turn to the question of bounding (
104
). If we consider the simple example described above, a straightforward bound can be obtained as follows. (This analysis also applies in the context of photonic leakage as described earlier, if it follows the “classical-probabilistic leakage” model described in
[
Tan23
]
; refer to that work for details of that model.)

First note that in the described model, we can write
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
as
(1-\delta_{\mathrm{leak}})\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{\perp}+\delta_{\mathrm{leak}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{\mathrm{leak}}
for some channels
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{\perp}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{\mathrm{leak}}
, where the former just outputs a fixed state
\left|\perp\right>\!\!\left<\perp\right|_{L}
.
Therefore, for any possible output state
\nu_{RL}\coloneqq\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\omega_{\widetilde{Z}R})
of the channel (with a purifying system
\widetilde{Z}
), by writing
\nu^{\prime}_{\widetilde{Z}L}\coloneqq\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{\mathrm{leak}}(\omega_{\widetilde{Z}R})
, we have

\displaystyle\nu_{\widetilde{Z}L}=(1-\delta_{\mathrm{leak}})\nu_{\widetilde{Z}}\otimes\left|\perp\right>\!\!\left<\perp\right|_{L}+\delta_{\mathrm{leak}}\nu^{\prime}_{\widetilde{Z}L},\text{ where $\left<\perp\right|\nu^{\prime}_{L}\left|\perp\right>=0$ and $\nu^{\prime}_{\widetilde{Z}}=\nu_{\widetilde{Z}}$},

(105)

where the
\nu^{\prime}_{\widetilde{Z}}=\nu_{\widetilde{Z}}
property follows from the fact that all these channels did not act on
\widetilde{Z}
. With this we have a simple bound:

\displaystyle I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(\widetilde{Z};L)_{\nu}

\displaystyle\leq I_{\alpha}(\widetilde{Z}:L)_{\nu}

\displaystyle=D_{\alpha}((1-\delta_{\mathrm{leak}})\nu_{\widetilde{Z}}\otimes\left|\perp\right>\!\!\left<\perp\right|_{L}+\delta_{\mathrm{leak}}\nu^{\prime}_{\widetilde{Z}L}||\nu_{\widetilde{Z}}\otimes((1-\delta_{\mathrm{leak}})\left|\perp\right>\!\!\left<\perp\right|_{L}+\delta_{\mathrm{leak}}\nu^{\prime}_{L}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\left((1-\delta_{\mathrm{leak}})2^{(\alpha-1)(0)}+\delta_{\mathrm{leak}}2^{(\alpha-1)D_{\alpha}(\nu^{\prime}_{\widetilde{Z}L}||\nu_{\widetilde{Z}}\otimes\nu^{\prime}_{L})}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\left((1-\delta_{\mathrm{leak}})+\delta_{\mathrm{leak}}2^{(\alpha-1)D_{\alpha}(\nu^{\prime}_{\widetilde{Z}L}||\nu^{\prime}_{\widetilde{Z}}\otimes\nu^{\prime}_{L})}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\left((1-\delta_{\mathrm{leak}})+\delta_{\mathrm{leak}}2^{(\alpha-1)\zeta_{\mathrm{cq}}\log\min\{\dim(\widetilde{Z}),\dim(L)\}}\right)\quad\forall\alpha\in(1,3/2),

(106)

where
\zeta_{\mathrm{cq}}\coloneqq 1
if either
\widetilde{Z}
or
L
is classical and
\zeta_{\mathrm{cq}}\coloneqq 2
otherwise. In the above, the third line holds by (
27
) (and the fact that all the terms with
\nu^{\prime}
have disjoint support from the terms with
\left|\perp\right>\!\!\left<\perp\right|_{L}
), the fourth line uses the critical property
\nu^{\prime}_{\widetilde{Z}}=\nu_{\widetilde{Z}}
, and the last line is from
[
DF19
, proof of Corollary III.5]
. (In our specific example we always have
\dim(\widetilde{Z})<\dim(L)
so the minimum in the last line is quite redundant, but we leave it in this form for other potential applications.) While the dimension dependence might appear inconvenient, we note that if for instance the memory
R
only stores memory about a bounded number of previous rounds, then its dimension (and thus also that of the purifying system
\widetilde{Z}
) can be bounded. Alternatively, as observed in works such as
[
Win16
,
Tan23
]
, it should be possible to replace it with weaker conditions (such as an energy bound); we leave this for future work.

While the above bound might look like a slightly complicated formula, it is easy to compute, and straightforwardly verified to go to zero as
\delta_{\mathrm{leak}}\to 0
, as we would expect. Slightly less straightforwardly, it is also decreasing as
\alpha\to 1
, and in that limit it simply converges to

\displaystyle\delta_{\mathrm{leak}}\zeta_{\mathrm{cq}}\log\min\{\dim(\widetilde{Z}),\dim(L)\},

(107)

which is the upper bound we would get if we had considered the von Neumann mutual information
I(\widetilde{Z}:L)_{\nu}
.

11

Interestingly, this bound is linear in
\delta_{\mathrm{leak}}
, in contrast to previous bounds computed in
[

(which applied to the “classical-probabilistic leakage” scenario), which reduced to

Remark 6.1

In the above bounds, we defined

12

Strictly speaking, this formulation is needed anyway to rigorously formalize the constraint that

We now take any arbitrary

attaining the supremum (this always exists, by compactness and continuity), and perform the following sequence of transformations to find another optimizer
\widetilde{\omega}_{\widetilde{Z}R}
that is fully classical. First note that
\mathcal{P}_{R}[\omega_{\widetilde{Z}R}]
also attains the supremum, since
\mathcal{P}_{R}\circ\mathcal{P}_{R}=\mathcal{P}_{R}

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}

also attains the supremum, and it is fully classical as desired (here we exploited the fact that for a state of the form

Remark 6.2
.

A natural question regarding the above analysis is whether the dimension dependence can be removed. However, a fundamental obstacle that must be addressed in such contexts is the issue of the “random full-leakage attack” described in
[
Tan23
, Sec. 2.2]
(or
[
MD23
, Appendix E]
), which inherently renders a protocol completely insecure despite each round only “behaving badly” with probability
\delta_{\mathrm{leak}}
. Any imperfection-robust security proof
necessarily
has to impose enough assumptions to rule out that attack. In this case we chose to do so by assuming the dimensions of
L
or
\widetilde{Z}
are bounded (so that (
106
) is a nontrivial bound), but our general results highlight that in principle, any other reasonably plausible assumption that bounds the correlation between the single-round leakage register and other registers (specifically, as quantified by
I^{\downarrow}_{\alpha}(\widetilde{Z};L)
) would suffice. As mentioned above, another alternative to dimension bounds would be energy bounds, given suitable Hamiltonians; see
[
Tan23
,
Win16
]
.

7
Conclusion

Overall, in this work we have derived a variety of chain rules involving mutual information quantities, including both one-shot chain rules and Rényi chain rules suitable for repeated application over a seequence of channels. In particular, the latter yield modifications of entropy accumulation that can accommodate some leakage, which are fairly straightforward to incorporate into security proofs. These results should be useful in rigorously quantifying the effects of device imperfections in protocol implementations.

We also remark that the one-shot chain rules in Sec.
3
may potentially be relevant in the context of composable security
[
PR21
]
. A full discussion of this topic is well beyond the scope of this work, but to informally summarize, the composable security of QKD protocols may encounter issues if additional information
L
about the raw key is leaked after the protocol has completed
[
BCK13
]
. Our one-shot chain rules suggest that it might theoretically be possible to compensate for such additional leakage by shortening the output of the privacy amplification step in QKD
[
Ren05
]
by approximately
I_{\infty}^{\downarrow,\delta}(SE;L)
(or the potentially tighter quantity in Theorem
3.1
), if that value can be bounded. In particular, this bound would likely be better than simply subtracting the log-dimension or smooth max-entropy of
L
, since
I_{\infty}^{\downarrow,\delta}(SE;L)
does at least reduce to zero in trivial scenarios where
L
is in product with
SE
.
However, we leave a detailed analysis of whether such an approach truly yields composable security to future work. (For now we simply highlight that in particular, it likely does not work if the leakage “directly depends on” the
final
key rather than the
raw
key, e.g. if the leakage is simply an exact copy of some bits in the final key.)

Definition A.2
.

(Measure-and-prepare or read-and-prepare channels)
A (projective)

\mathcal{E}:Q\to QQ^{\prime}
of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}[\rho_{Q}]=\sum_{j}(P_{j}\rho_{Q}P_{j})\otimes\sigma_{Q^{\prime}|j},

for some projective measurement

Definition A.3
.

\{\mathcal{M}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
is called a
sequence of GEAT-with-testing-and-leakage (GEATTL) channels
if each
\mathcal{M}_{j}
is a channel
R_{j-1}E_{j-1}\rightarrow S_{j}R_{j}E_{j}\overline{C}_{j}\widehat{C}_{j}
such that the output registers
\overline{C}_{j}\widehat{C}_{j}

\mathcal{M}_{j}=\mathcal{N}_{j}\circ\mathcal{L}_{j}

\mathcal{L}_{j}:R_{j-1}\rightarrow\widehat{R}_{j-1}L_{j}

\displaystyle\exists\text{ a channel }\mathcal{R}_{j}:L_{j}E_{j-1}\rightarrow E_{j}\widehat{C}_{j}\text{ such that }\operatorname{Tr}_{S_{j}R_{j}\overline{C}_{j}}\circ\mathcal{N}_{j}=\mathcal{R}_{j}\circ\operatorname{Tr}_{\widehat{R}_{j-1}}.

(110)

\mathcal{L}_{j}

\rho\in\operatorname{S}_{=}(S_{1}^{n}\overline{C}_{1}^{n}\widehat{C}_{1}^{n}E_{n}R_{n})

(leaving some identity channels implicit)

Qualitatively,

\overline{C}_{j}
