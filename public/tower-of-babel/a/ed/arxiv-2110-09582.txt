Stability of the chemostat system with a mutation factor
T. Bayen, H. Cazenave-Lacroutz, J. Coville

Abstract
In this paper, we consider a resource-consumer model taking into account a mutation effect between species (with constant mutation rate). The corresponding mutation operator is a discretization of the Laplacian in such a way that the resulting dynamical system can be viewed as a regular perturbation of the classical chemostat system. We prove the existence of a unique locally stable steady-state for every value of the mutation rate and every value of the dilution rate not exceeding a critical value. In addition, we give an expansion of the steady-state in terms of the mutation rate and we prove a uniform persistence property of the dynamics related to each species. Finally, we show that this equilibrium is globally asymptotically stable for every value of the mutation rate provided that the dilution rate is with small enough values.



Keywords
: chemostat system, population dynamics, dynamical system, regular perturbation, global stability.

The next Proposition (point (ii) only) extends the analysis of
[
13
]
showing that, depending on the values of
(\varepsilon,u)
,
(
2.1
) has a unique locally stable equilibrium.
The notation
\|\cdot\|
stands for the euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

(i)
If
(\varepsilon,u)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
is such that
\lambda(B(1,u,\varepsilon))\leq 0
, then, the washout steady-state
E_{wo}
is the only equilibrium of (
2.1
) and it is stable. If
\lambda(B(1,u,\varepsilon))<0
, it is globally asymptotically stable.

(ii)
If
(\varepsilon,u)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
is such that
\lambda(B(1,u,\varepsilon))>0
, then, (
2.1
) admits a unique locally stable equilibrium

E_{\varepsilon,u}:=(x^{\varepsilon,u},s^{\varepsilon,u})\in(0,+\infty)^{n}\times(0,1)
called coexistence steady-state and
E_{wo}
is unstable.

Proof.

For sake of completeness, we give the proof of (i) which can also be found in
[
13
]
. If
(x,s)
is a steady-state of (
2.1
), then

{}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}B(s,u,\varepsilon)x&=0,\\
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}(s)x_{j}&=u(1-s).\end{array}\right.

(3.1)

The equation
B(s,u,\varepsilon)x=0
with
x\not=0
implies that
0 is an eigenvalue, hence
\lambda(B(s,u,\varepsilon))\geq 0
. It is possible only if
s=1
. Indeed, otherwise, since
s<1\;\Rightarrow\;\lambda(B(s,u,\varepsilon))<\lambda(B(1,u,\varepsilon))
, we would have
\lambda(B(s,u,\varepsilon))<0
and a contradiction. It follows from (
3.1
) that any equilibrium verifies
x=0
, so, the only equilibrium point is the washout.
The Jacobian of (
2.1
) at
E_{wo}
is the block matrix

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}B(1,u,\varepsilon)&0\\
-\mu_{1}(1)\cdots-\mu_{n}(1)&-u\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+1)\times(n+1)}.

If
\lambda(B(1,u,\varepsilon))\leq 0
, then,

\dot{x}=B(s,u,\varepsilon)x\leq B(1,u,\varepsilon)x
, thus there is
C_{1}>0
such that
\|x(t)\|\leq C_{1}\|x(0)\|
for every
t\geq 0
. Using
that
\Delta
is attractive for (
2.1
) (recall Lemma
2.1
), we deduce that
E_{wo}
is stable. If, in addition,
\lambda(B(1,u,\varepsilon))<0
,
B(1,u,\varepsilon)
is a Hurwitz matrix, and, thanks to the inequality
\dot{x}\leq B(1,u,\varepsilon)x
, we deduce that
x(t)\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow+\infty
which proves the desired property using Lemma
2.1
.

In case (ii), we find two equilibria depending if
s=1
or
s<1
. If
s=1
, then
x=0
and the corresponding steady-state is the washout that is unstable since
\lambda(B(1,u,\varepsilon))>0
.
The other possible steady-states satisfy (
3.1
) with
s<1
, so,
x\in\mathrm{Ker}(B(s,u,\varepsilon))\backslash\{0\}
.
But, the largest eigenvalue of
B(s,u,\varepsilon)
is the only one with a positive eigenvector (thanks to the Perron-Frobenius Theorem). So, we necessarily have
\lambda(B(s,u,\varepsilon))=0
which has a unique solution
s^{\varepsilon,u}

(because of the monotonicity of
\lambda(B(\cdot,u,\varepsilon))
and the fact that
\lambda(B(0,u,\varepsilon))\lambda(B(1,u,\varepsilon))<0
). Hence, zero is the Perron root of
B(s^{\varepsilon,u},u,\varepsilon)
(it is a simple eigenvalue) and we denote by
a^{\varepsilon,u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
its Perron vector. We deduce that
x
necessarily satisfies

x=\nu a^{\varepsilon,u}\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}+s^{\varepsilon,u}=1,

where
\nu\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
. These two equalities define a unique point
x^{\varepsilon,u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
x^{\varepsilon,u}_{i}>0
for every
1\leq i\leq n
.
Let us now turn to the local asymptotic stability property. Observe that (
2.1
) is equivalent to

\left|\begin{array}[]{cl}\dot{x}&=B(b-\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j},u,\varepsilon)x,\\
\dot{b}&=u(1-b),\end{array}\right.

(recall that
b=s+\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}
). The Jacobian matrix of the preceding system at
(x^{\varepsilon,u},1)
is

J_{\varepsilon,u}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}A_{\varepsilon,u}&d_{\varepsilon,u}\\
0&-u\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+1)\times(n+1)},

where
d_{\varepsilon,u}:=M^{\prime}(s^{\varepsilon,u})x^{\varepsilon,u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
A_{\varepsilon,u}:=B(s^{\varepsilon,u},u,\varepsilon)-d_{\varepsilon,u}a^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a rank-one perturbation of
B(s^{\varepsilon,u},u,\varepsilon)
.
For proving our claim, it is then enough to show that
A_{\varepsilon,u}
is a Hurwitz matrix. Observe that one has

-B(s^{\varepsilon,u},u,\varepsilon)=\rho(H)I_{n}-H
where
H:=\rho(B(s^{\varepsilon,u},u,\varepsilon))I_{n}+B(s^{\varepsilon,u},u,\varepsilon)
. Hence,
-B(s^{\varepsilon,u},u,\varepsilon)
can be written as a singular
M
-matrix which is thus non-negative.
We can now apply Theorem 2.7 (v) of
[
9
]
with the matrix
H
(for this, note that
d_{\varepsilon,u}
and
a
have positive coefficients) and deduce that

-A_{\varepsilon,u}
is strictly positive stable (which means that all eigenvalues of
-A_{\varepsilon,u}
are with positive real parts). We can thus conclude that

J_{\varepsilon,u}
is a Hurwitz matrix which ends the proof.
∎

In the rest of the paper, we keep the notation
a^{\varepsilon,u}
for the Perron vector associated with the
0
eigenvalue of the matrix
B(s^{\varepsilon,u},u,\varepsilon)
.

3.2
Occurrence of the washout and coexistence steady-states

In this part, we make more explicit the condition about
\lambda(B(1,u,\varepsilon))
which separates washout and coexistence equilibria in Proposition
3.1
. It is convenient to introduce the functions

\bar{\mu}(s):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}(s)\;;\;\hat{\mu}(s):=\max(\mu_{1}(s),...,\mu_{n}(s)),

which are increasing over
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Also, we set
m:=\hat{\mu}(1)
.

Proposition 3.2
.

For every
(\varepsilon,u)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
such that
\lambda(B(1,u,\varepsilon))>0
, one has:

{}\bar{\mu}(s^{\varepsilon,u})\leq u\leq\hat{\mu}(s^{\varepsilon,u})\leq u+2\varepsilon.

(3.2)

In addition, for every
(\varepsilon,u)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
, the quantity
\lambda(B(1,u,\varepsilon))
satisfies the following inequalities:

{}\max\Big(\hat{\mu}(1)-u-2\varepsilon,\bar{\mu}(1)-u\Big)\leq\lambda(B(1,u,\varepsilon))\leq\hat{\mu}(1)-u.

(3.3)

Proof.

First, observe that
B(s,u,\varepsilon)\leq\hat{B}(s)
(componentwise) where
\hat{B}(s):=\max_{1\leq j\leq n}(\mu_{j}(s)-u)I_{n}+\varepsilon T\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, and that both matrices are quasi-positive and irreducible. We can thus deduce that
\lambda(B(s,u,\varepsilon))\leq\lambda(\hat{B}(s))=\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\mu_{j}(s)-u
. This gives the second inequality in (
3.3
).

Now, for
s\in[0,1]
, we set
\zeta:=\lambda(B(s,u,\varepsilon))
and let
x
be an eigenvector of
B(s,u,\varepsilon)
with eigenvalue
\zeta
. Recall from Perron-Frobenius’s Theorem that
x>0
.
The equality
B(s,u,\varepsilon)x=\zeta x
rewrites

{}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(\mu_{1}(s)-u-\varepsilon)x_{1}+\varepsilon x_{2}&=\zeta x_{1},\\
(\mu_{i}(s)-u-2\varepsilon)x_{i}+\varepsilon(x_{i+1}+x_{i-1})&=\zeta x_{i},\quad 2\leq i\leq n-1,\\
(\mu_{n}(s)-u-\varepsilon)x_{n}+\varepsilon x_{n-1}&=\zeta x_{n},\end{array}\right.

(3.4)

Summing those
n
equalities with
s=s^{\varepsilon,u}
and
x=x^{\varepsilon,u}
gives

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}(s^{\varepsilon,u})x_{j}^{\varepsilon,u}=u\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}^{\varepsilon,u},

thus, one obtains
u=\sum_{j=1}^{n}p_{j}^{\varepsilon,u}\mu_{j}(s^{\varepsilon,u})
(where for
1\leq i\leq n
,
p_{i}^{\varepsilon,u}:=\frac{x_{i}^{\varepsilon,u}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}^{\varepsilon,u}}
). Because
p_{i}^{\varepsilon,u}\leq 1
, we deduce that

u\leq\hat{\mu}(s^{\varepsilon,u})
, which gives the second inequality in (
3.2
).
From (
3.4
) with
s\in[0,1]
, we also get

{}\zeta=\mu_{i}(s)-u-a_{i}\varepsilon+\varepsilon\frac{x_{i+1}+x_{i-1}}{x_{i}},

(3.5)

with
a_{i}=2
for
2\leq i\leq n-1
,
a_{1}=a_{n}=1
, and the convention that
x_{0}=x_{n+1}=0
. Since
x_{i}>0
for
1\leq i\leq n
,
this equality entails

\zeta\geq\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\mu_{j}(s)-u-2\varepsilon.

From the preceding inequality, we can deduce the last inequality in (
3.2
) (taking
s=s^{\varepsilon,u})
and also the inequality

\lambda(B(1,u,\varepsilon))\geq\hat{\mu}(1)-u-2\varepsilon
in (
3.3
) (taking
s=1
).

To conclude, we need to prove the two inequalities in (
3.2
)-(
3.3
) involving the mean values of the kinetics. Doing so, we sum equalities (
3.5
) which gives

\zeta=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}(s)-u-\frac{2(n-1)}{n}\varepsilon+\frac{\varepsilon}{n}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{x_{j+1}}{x_{j}}+\sum_{j=2}^{n}\frac{x_{j-1}}{x_{j}}\right].

Applying the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality yields

\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{x_{j+1}}{x_{j}}\geq\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{x_{j+1}}{x_{j}}\right)^{\frac{1}{n-1}}=\left(\frac{x_{n}}{x_{1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{n-1}}\;;\;\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{x_{j-1}}{x_{j}}\geq\left(\frac{x_{1}}{x_{n}}\right)^{\frac{1}{n-1}},

which implies

\zeta\geq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}(s)-u-\frac{2(n-1)}{n}\varepsilon+\frac{(n-1)\varepsilon}{n}\left[\left(\frac{x_{n}}{x_{1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{n-1}}+\left(\frac{x_{1}}{x_{n}}\right)^{\frac{1}{n-1}}\right].

Using that
y^{\frac{1}{n-1}}+y^{-\frac{1}{n-1}}\geq 2
for every
y>0
, we obtain the inequality
\zeta\geq\bar{\mu}(s)-u
.
Specializing this inequality with
s=s^{\varepsilon,u}
and
s=1
gives us the left inequalities in (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) which concludes the proof.
∎

Thanks to this proposition, we can make the following observations:

\bullet

From (
3.2
)-(
3.3
), the steady-state
E_{\varepsilon,u}
occurs whenever
\bar{\mu}(1)>u
and this condition does not depend on the parameter
\varepsilon
.

\bullet

Observe also that, thanks to those inequalities, we recover the fact that if
u\geq m
, then, only washout occurs.

For every
\varepsilon\geq 0
, we can also uniquely define a critical value for the dilution rate

u_{c}(\varepsilon):=\lambda(M(1)+\varepsilon T),

which is such that only washout occurs if the dilution rate is such that
u\geq u_{c}(\varepsilon)
(according to Proposition
3.1
(i)).
From (
3.3
) and the previous remarks, the value
u_{c}(\varepsilon)
satisfies:

{}\forall\varepsilon\geq 0,\;u_{c}(\varepsilon)\in[\hat{u}(\varepsilon),m],

(3.6)

where
\hat{u}(\varepsilon):=\max(m-2\varepsilon,\bar{\mu}(1))
. Interestingly, the presence of mutation in the system implies occurrence of the washout for values of the dilution rate in the interval
[m-2\varepsilon,m)

for which the species with the least break-even concentration would survive (without mutation).
In addition, we can observe that the larger the mutation rate is, the lower the dilution need to be to avoid washout.

We now recall a result related to the differentiability of
\lambda(\cdot)
that will be applied several times in this paper.
Given a symmetric quasi-positive matrix
A
, the largest eigenvalue of
A
is simple and thus
\lambda(\cdot)
is analytic as a function of its
n^{2}
coefficients in some neighborhood of
A
in the symmetric matrices space (see,
e.g.
,
[
47
,
15
]
).
In addition, the first derivative of
\lambda(\cdot)
(
i.e.
, the matrix whose
(i,j)
entry is
\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial a_{i,j}}(A))
is given by

{}D_{1}\lambda(A)=ww^{\top},

(3.7)

where
w
denotes the Perron vector associated with
\lambda(A)
(see
[
15
,
24
]
).

Proposition 3.3
.

The function
\varepsilon\mapsto u_{c}(\varepsilon)
is non-increasing over
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. In addition, one has
u_{c}(0)=m
, and
u_{c}(\varepsilon)\rightarrow\bar{\mu}(1)
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow+\infty
.

Proof.

Applying the previous property with the symmetric quasi-positive matrix

B(1,0,\varepsilon)=M(1)+\varepsilon T
gives

u_{c}^{\prime}(\varepsilon)=\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\frac{\partial\lambda(B(1,0,\varepsilon))}{\partial b_{i,j}}\frac{\partial b_{i,j}(1,0,\varepsilon)}{\partial\varepsilon}=(v^{\varepsilon})^{\top}Tv^{\varepsilon}\leq 0,

where
v^{\varepsilon}
is the Perron vector associated with
B(1,0,\varepsilon)
and
b_{i,j}(1,0,\varepsilon)
denote the
n^{2}
entries of
B(1,0,\varepsilon)
.
This shows that
u_{c}(\cdot)
is non-increasing over
\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
Now, from the CEP, we have immediately
u_{c}(0)=m
. Finally,
using (
3.7
), we have the expansion

{}u_{c}(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon\lambda\left(T+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}M(1)\right)=\varepsilon\left[\lambda(T)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\frac{a^{\top}M(1)a}{a^{\top}a}+o\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\right]=\bar{\mu}(1)+o(1),

(3.8)

as
\varepsilon\rightarrow+\infty
, which concludes the proof.
∎

3.3
Global stability in the two species case

In this part, we prove that
E_{\varepsilon,u}
is GAS for (
2.1
) when
n=2
and we also give explicit expressions for the steady-state and the critical value of the dilution rate. We start by addressing the global stability property.

Proposition 3.4
.

For
n=2
and
(\varepsilon,u)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\times(0,u_{c}(\varepsilon))
,
E_{\varepsilon,u}
is globally asymptotically stable in
\mathcal{D}
.

Proof.

For those initial conditions in the set
\Delta
, (
2.1
) is equivalent to

{}\dot{x}=h(x),

(3.9)

where
h:\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
is given
by (recall (
2.10
))

h(x):=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}(\mu_{1}(1-x_{1}-x_{2})-u)x_{1}+\varepsilon(x_{2}-x_{1})\\
(\mu_{2}(1-x_{1}-x_{2})-u)x_{2}+\varepsilon(x_{1}-x_{2})\end{array}\right).

If we set
\varphi(x_{1},x_{2}):=\frac{1}{x_{1}x_{2}}
for
x_{1},x_{2}>0
, a direct computation shows that the quantity

\frac{\partial(\varphi h_{1})}{\partial x_{1}}+\frac{\partial(\varphi h_{2})}{\partial x_{1}}=-\varepsilon\frac{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}}{x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{2}}-\frac{\mu^{\prime}_{1}(1-x_{1}-x_{2})}{x_{2}}-\frac{\mu^{\prime}_{2}(1-x_{1}-x_{2})}{x_{2}},

is negative in the interior of
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
. It follows from the Bendixson-Dulac Theorem that no periodic orbits occurs
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
for (
3.9
).
Now, Proposition
3.1
implies that only two equilibria occur, namely the washout
(0,0)
which is unstable and the point
x^{\varepsilon,u}
in the interior of
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
which is locally asymptotically stable. Since there are no periodic orbits, we deduce that
x^{\varepsilon,u}
is globally asymptotically stable for (
2.1
) restricted to
\Delta
.
Now, coming back to (
2.1
) for
n=2
, the sub-system satisfied by
x
reads

{}\dot{x}=\tilde{h}(t,x),

(3.10)

where
\tilde{h}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
is defined as

\tilde{h}(t,x):=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}(\mu_{1}(b(t)-x_{1}-x_{2})-u)x_{1}+\varepsilon(x_{2}-x_{1})\\
(\mu_{2}(b(t)-x_{1}-x_{2})-u)x_{2}+\varepsilon(x_{1}-x_{2})\end{array}\right).

Clearly,
\tilde{h}(t,x)\rightarrow h(t,x)
uniformly locally w.r.t.
x
, thus (
3.10
) is a non-autonomous perturbation of (
3.9
).
Since every solution to (
3.9
) converges to
x^{\varepsilon,u}
,
we deduce from
[
43
]
that every solution to (
3.10
) also converges to this point. To conclude, let us given a solution
(x(\cdot),s(\cdot))
of (
2.1
). We have proved that

x(t)\rightarrow x^{\varepsilon,u}
when
t
goes to infinity. Since
s(t)+x_{1}(t)+x_{2}(t)\rightarrow 1
as
t\rightarrow+\infty
, we deduce that
s(t)\rightarrow s^{\varepsilon,u}
as
t
goes to infinity which ends the proof.
∎

We now turn to explicit expressions involving the steady-state. The sub-system
(x_{1},x_{2})
of (
2.1
) rewrites

\dot{x}=B(s,u,\varepsilon)x\quad\mathrm{with}\quad B(s,u,\varepsilon)=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mu_{1}(s)-(u+\varepsilon)&\varepsilon\\
\varepsilon&\mu_{2}(s)-(u+\varepsilon)\end{array}\right].

The largest eigenvalue of
B(s,u,\varepsilon)
can thus be explicitly computed:

{}\lambda(B(s,u,\varepsilon))=-\varepsilon-u+\frac{\mu_{1}(s)+\mu_{2}(s)}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{(\mu_{1}(s)-\mu_{2}(s))^{2}+4\varepsilon^{2}}.

(3.11)

Hence, the coexistence steady-state
(x^{\varepsilon,u},s^{\varepsilon,u})
exists provided that
\lambda(B(1,u,\varepsilon))>0
(see Proposition
3.1
) which amounts to saying that the dilution rate fulfills the inequality

u<u_{c}(1)=-\varepsilon+\frac{\mu_{1}(1)+\mu_{2}(1)}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{(\mu_{1}(1)-\mu_{2}(1))^{2}+4\varepsilon^{2}}.

For a given
u
satisfying the previous inequality, we can compute
s^{\varepsilon,u}
numerically solving
\lambda(B(s,u,\varepsilon))=0
w.r.t.
s
, thanks to (
3.11
) (see Fig.
1
).
It follows that the dilution rate
u
is related to
s^{\varepsilon,u}
via the equality

u=-\varepsilon+\frac{\mu_{1}(s^{\varepsilon,u})+\mu_{2}(s^{\varepsilon,u})}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{(\mu_{1}(s^{\varepsilon,u})-\mu_{2}(s^{\varepsilon,u}))^{2}+4\varepsilon^{2}}.

Using that
x^{\varepsilon,u}_{1}+x^{\varepsilon,u}_{2}+s^{\varepsilon,u}=1
, one also obtains

x^{\varepsilon,u}_{1}=\frac{(1-s^{\varepsilon,u})(u-\mu_{2}(s^{\varepsilon,u}))}{\mu_{1}(s^{\varepsilon,u})-\mu_{2}(s^{\varepsilon,u})}\;;\;x^{\varepsilon,u}_{2}=\frac{(1-s^{\varepsilon,u})(u-\mu_{1}(s^{\varepsilon,u}))}{\mu_{2}(s^{\varepsilon,u})-\mu_{1}(s^{\varepsilon,u})}.

Remark 3.1
.

The previous expressions of
x^{\varepsilon,u}_{i}
are valid if the kinetics do not intersect. If there is a (unique)
\bar{s}\in(0,1)

such that
\mu_{1}(\bar{s})=\mu_{2}(\bar{s})
, then, these expressions are valid only if
u\not=\mu_{1}(\bar{s})
. If
u=\bar{u}:=\mu_{1}(\bar{s})
, then, one has
s^{\varepsilon,\bar{u}}=\bar{s}
and

x^{\varepsilon,\bar{u}}=\big(\frac{1-\bar{s}}{2},\frac{1-\bar{s}}{2}\big)
.

depicts
\varepsilon\mapsto x^{\varepsilon,u}

and

) and that

)).

4
Behavior of the coexistence steady state

In this part, we study the behavior of the coexistence equilibrium w.r.t. the parameter
\varepsilon
. Based on the implicit function theorem, we give an expansion of
E_{\varepsilon,u}
up to the first order as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
(for a fixed dilution rate
u
) and we also study its limit as
\varepsilon\rightarrow+\infty
. Recall that
m=\hat{\mu}(1)
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Suppose that
u\in(0,m)
and that there is a unique
1\leq i_{0}\leq n
such that

Lemma 6.1
.

For every
(\varepsilon,u_{1})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\times(0,u_{c}(\varepsilon))
, there is
\beta_{\varepsilon}>0

such that for every

and every initial condition in

, the unique solution of (

associated with this initial condition satisfies

\liminf_{t\rightarrow+\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}(t)\geq\beta_{\varepsilon}.

Proof.

Let
(\varepsilon,u_{1})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\times(0,u_{c}(\varepsilon))
. Given
u\in[0,u_{1}]
, the Perron vector
v^{\varepsilon,u}
associated with the greatest eigenvalue of the matrix
B(1,u,\varepsilon)
is the unique solution to the system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}B(1,u,\varepsilon)w-\lambda(B(1,u,\varepsilon))w&=0,\\
\|w\|-1&=0.\end{array}\right.

Now, consider the
C^{1}
-mapping

\tilde{\psi}:(u,w)\in[0,u_{1}]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\mapsto\tilde{\psi}(u,w):=\Big(B(1,u,\varepsilon)w-\lambda(B(1,u,\varepsilon))w,\frac{\|w\|^{2}-1}{2}\Big)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}.

Its partial derivative w.r.t.
w
at the point
(u,v^{\varepsilon,u})\in[0,u_{1}]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
is given by:

\frac{\partial\tilde{\psi}}{\partial w}(u,v^{\varepsilon,u})=\Big(B(1,u,\varepsilon)-\lambda(B(1,u,\varepsilon))I_{n},v^{\varepsilon,u}\Big).

Hence, if
w
is in the kernel of
\frac{\partial\tilde{\psi}}{\partial w}(u,v^{\varepsilon,u})
, it satisfies

B(1,u,\varepsilon)w=\lambda(B(1,u,\varepsilon))w
and
v^{\varepsilon,u}\cdot w=0
(here,
\cdot
is the scalar product in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
). The first equality implies that there is

\nu\in\mathbb{R}
such that
w=\nu v^{\varepsilon,u}
. Using the second equality, we find that
\nu=0
, thus
w=0
and
\frac{\partial\tilde{\psi}}{\partial w}(u,v^{\varepsilon,u})

is invertible. Thanks to the implicit function theorem, we obtain that way that

is locally continuous around every

, thus it is continuous over
[0,u_{1}]
.
Now, Proposition
5.1
of

implies that

\liminf_{t\rightarrow+\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}(t)\geq c\min_{1\leq j\leq n}v_{j}^{u,\varepsilon}.

Since the mapping

is continuous over
[0,u_{1}]
, so is

\liminf_{t\rightarrow+\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}(t)\geq\beta_{\varepsilon}:=c\min_{u\in[0,u_{1}]}\min_{1\leq j\leq n}v^{u,\varepsilon}_{j}.

Because

is positive and continuous over

, we get that
\beta_{\varepsilon}>0
which ends the proof.

7
Conclusion and perspectives

In this paper, we could extend some results of
[
13
]
showing that the coexistence steady-state of (
2.1
) is always LAS and in particular GAS
provided that the dilution rate is small enough (assuming only that kinetics are of Monod type).
Let us emphasize that in contrast with the chemostat system, mutation implies coexistence,
i.e.
, each species is present asymptotically.
Future works could investigate global stability via a Lyapunov approach at least for
\varepsilon>0
small enough taking into account the knowledge of a Lyapunov function for
\varepsilon=0
.
Asymptotic stability properties could be also addressed with more complicated mutation terms such as in
[
1
,
29
]
. As well, most properties proved in this paper are still valid if the kinetics are only increasing, hence, one can wonder if such properties remain valid with more sophisticated growth functions such as Haldane’s kinetics.
Finally, it could be also interesting to study continuous models describing the growth of a population structured by a phenotypical trait living in a limited substrate environment (see
[
36
]
).
