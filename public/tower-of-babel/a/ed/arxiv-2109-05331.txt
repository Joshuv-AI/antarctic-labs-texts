Extremal { p , q } \{p,q\} -Animals
Greg Malen, Érika Roldán, Rosemberg Toalá-Enríquez

Abstract
. An animal is a planar shape formed by attaching congruent regular polygons, known as tiles, along their edges. In this paper, we study extremal animals defined on regular tessellations of the plane. In 1976, Harary and Harborth studied animals in the Euclidean cases, finding extremal values for their vertices, edges, and tiles, when any one of these parameters is fixed. Here, we generalize their results to hyperbolic animals. For each hyperbolic tessellation, we exhibit a sequence of spiral animals and prove that they attain the minimal numbers of edges and vertices within the class of animals with n n tiles. In their conclusions, Harary and Harborth also proposed the question of enumerating extremal animals with a fixed number of tiles. This question has previously only been considered for Euclidean animals. As a first step in solving this problem, we find special sequences of extremal animals that are unique extremal animals, in the sense that any animal with the same number of tiles which is distinct up to isometries can’t be extremal.

1.
Introduction

An
animal
is a planar shape with connected interior formed by gluing together a finite number of congruent regular polygons along their edges. Here, we study animals that have extremal combinatorial properties such as having maximally many shared (or interior) edges for a given number of polygons. We refer to animals attaining these optimal values as
extremal animals
. In the Euclidean plane, extremal animals have been well studied and it is known that they exhibit interesting geometric and combinatorial properties such as being isoperimetric
[
7
]
, giving contact numbers for optimal disk packing configurations
[
8
]
, and providing the right shape to produce animals with maximally many holes
[
12
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
.

In the hyperbolic plane, combinatorial and geometric properties of extremal animals have not been examined as closely. However, the relevance of extremal animals in this setting to other extremal combinatorial problems has already been established. These include optimal disk packing problems
[
4
]
, the calculation of Cheeger constants
[
9
]
, the establishment of the exponential growth constant of regular hyperbolic tessellations
[
13
]
, and the implementation of algorithms to sample certain hyperbolic animals to approximate hyperbolic percolation thresholds
[
22
]
.

Recently, extremal hyperbolic animals have been used to model and study structures in applied mathematics, such as in crystallography theory for materials with hyperbolic symmetries
[
3
]
, and in the architecture of circuit quantum electrodynamics
[
14
]
. More generally,
models based on hyperbolic tessellations have also been used in areas such as Bloch band theory in physics
[
16
]
, and in quantum error correcting and quantum storage codes
[
5
,
10
]
, among others. Therefore, having a better understanding of extremal hyperbolic animals might lead to interesting insights, results, techniques, and new research questions in these areas.

The families of animals that we study in this paper are formed by finite subsets of tiles of regular Euclidean and hyperbolic tessellations. These tessellations can be parameterized using the Schläfli symbol
\{p,q\}
, where
p
denotes the number of sides of the regular polygon forming the tessellation and
q
is the number of edges or tiles meeting at each vertex. It is well known that if
(p-2)(q-2)>4
,
=4
, or
<4
, then the tessellation corresponds to the geometry of the hyperbolic plane, the Euclidean plane, or the sphere, respectively. We call an animal living in a
\{p,q\}
-tessellation a
\{p,q\}
-animal. The most well-known families of animals are those living in the Euclidean tessellations, namely for
\{p,q\}
either
\{3,6\}
,
\{4,4\}
, or
\{6,3\}
. Formed by triangles, squares, or hexagons, respectively, these animals are also more commonly known as polyiamonds, polyominoes, and polyhexes in the literature.

In 1976
[
7
]
, Harary and Harborth studied extremal animals in the three Euclidean cases. More specifically, they gave algebraic expressions and bounds for the number of interior, exterior, and total numbers of both vertices and edges that an animal with a fixed number of tiles can have. Then they constructed a family of
spiral
animals which attain optimal values. Here, we extend Harary and Harborth’s analysis to extremal hyperbolic animals. First, we define and analyze analogous sequences of
spiral

\{p,q\}
-animals for
(p-2)(q-2)\geq 4
and provide algebraic expressions for the number of vertices and edges in terms of a particular recursive sequence of integers. Then we prove that these
spiral

\{p,q\}
-animals are indeed extremal. As a corollary of our results, we recover the formulas derived in
[
7
]
for the Euclidean cases.

We also explore enumeration of extremal hyperbolic animals. Exact enumeration of
\{p,q\}
-animals is only known for a few
\{p,q\}
pairs, and even then only for animals with a very small number of tiles. For instance, the number of fixed
\{4,4\}
-animals with
n
tiles is only known for
n\leq 56

[
11
]
; and otherwise very little is known (see, for example, the entries of the OEIS A001207, A001420, A000228, A000577, A119611
[
24
]
for values in the
\{3,6\}
-,
\{6,3\}
-, and
\{4,5\}
-tessellations). In the particular case of Euclidean animals, there are also asymptotic results and computational approximations that give a better understanding of the problem of animal enumeration
[
1
,
2
,
21
,
23
]
. In their closing thoughts in
[
7
]
, Harary and Harborth raised the question of enumeration of extremal animals with
n
tiles. Surprisingly, this problem has remained largely unexplored, and it has been completely solved only for
\{4,4\}
-animals
[
15
]
. Before now, nothing was known about the enumeration of extremal hyperbolic animals. As a consequence of our main results, for all
\{p,q\}
we find special sequences of extremal animals that are
unique
extremal animals, in the sense that any animal with the same number of tiles which is distinct up to isometries can’t be extremal.

1.1.
Main Results

We begin by analyzing a sequence of layered animals, which we use as benchmarks for computing extremal values. These layered animals will attain extremal values, but more significantly they provide context for keeping track of how each subsequent tile affects all of the graph parameters in play.

Remark 1.1
.

Note that in a
\{p,q\}
-animal, a vertex can have up to
q
incident tiles. If it has fewer than
q
incident tiles, it is necessarily a perimeter vertex. If it has a full set of
q
incident tiles, it is an interior vertex, and we say that it is
saturated
.

Definition 1.2
.

We denote by
A_{p,q}(1)
the animal with only one
p
-gon. We construct
A_{p,q}(k)
from
A_{p,q}(k-1)
by adding precisely the tiles needed to saturate all perimeter vertices of
A_{p,q}(k-1)
. The new tiles are called the
k
-th layer
of the structure. We call
A_{p,q}(k)
the
complete
k
-layered
\{p,q\}
-animal
.

A_{p,q}(3)
for
\{p,q\}=\{4,4\}
.
Right:

A_{p,q}(3)
for
\{p,q\}=\{4,5\}

\{4,5\}

Theorem 1.3
.

Denote by
\check{v}(k)
,
\check{e}(k)
, and
\check{n}(k)
the number of vertices, edges, and tiles, respectively, of the complete
k
-layered
\{p,q\}
-animal
A_{p,q}(k)
. Let
t=(p-2)(q-2)-2
and
\alpha=\frac{t+\sqrt{t^{2}-4}}{2}
, then

(1)

For t=2, we have Euclidean animals, and they satisfy

\displaystyle\check{v}(k)

\displaystyle=pk^{2},

\displaystyle\check{e}(k)

\displaystyle=pk\left(\frac{q}{2}k-\frac{q}{2}+1\right),

\displaystyle\check{n}(k)

\displaystyle=1+\frac{p(q-2)}{2}k^{2}-\frac{p(q-2)}{2}k.

(2)

For
t>2
, we have hyperbolic animals, and they satisfy

\displaystyle\check{v}(k)

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{t-2}\left(\alpha^{k}+\frac{1}{\alpha^{k}}-2\right),

\displaystyle\check{e}(k)

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{(\alpha-1)\sqrt{t^{2}-4}}\left((\alpha+q-1)\alpha^{k}+\frac{\alpha q-\alpha+1}{\alpha^{k}}-q(\alpha+1)\right),

\displaystyle\check{n}(k)

\displaystyle=1+\frac{p(q-2)}{(\alpha-1)\sqrt{t^{2}-4}}\left(\alpha^{k}+\frac{1}{\alpha^{k-1}}-\alpha-1\right).

We also find algebraic expressions in Equation (
3.5
) for the following graph parameters: the number of interior vertices, perimeter edges, and interior edges of
A_{p,q}(k)
, denoted respectively by
\check{v}_{int}(k)
,
\check{e}_{1}(k)
,
\check{e}_{2}(k)
.

For
t>2
, we get as a corollary that the parameters of
A_{p,q}(k)
grow exponentially; as expected from the properties of hyperbolic geometry. The exponential growth rate of these parameters is governed by the constant
\alpha>1
.

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
t=(p-2)(q-2)-2\geq 2
and
\alpha=\frac{t+\sqrt{t^{2}-4}}{2}
. Then

\lim_{k\to\infty}\check{v}(k+1)/\check{v}(k)=\alpha,

and the same limit is true for the rest of the graph parameters listed in Theorem
1.3
.

Some of the recursive formulas for the complete layered sequence
A_{p,q}(k)
in Theorem
1.3
were independently computed and used as a tool for understanding geometric and combinatorial properties of the
\{p,q\}
-tessellations. However, the extremality of these values was not previously examined.

For instance, in
[
9
]
, Higuchi and Shirai use the layered
A_{p,q}(k)
structures to compute the Cheeger constant of the infinite graph of the
\{p,q\}
-tessellations. Specifically, they give recursive computations for
\check{v}(k)
and a value equivalent to the number of tiles in a layer, i.e.,
\check{n}(k)-\check{n}(k-1)
. Using these values they proved that the Chegeer constant of the
\{p,q\}
-tessellation, defined by

\inf\left\{\frac{|E(\partial_{v}W)|}{\text{vol}(W)}:W\text{ is a finite subgraph}\right\},

is equal to the limit of the corresponding values for
A_{p,q}(k)
as
k\to\infty
. Here,
|E(\partial_{v}W)|
is the number of edges connecting
W
with its complement, and vol
(W)=\sum_{x\in W}deg(x)
.

The
A_{p,q}(k)
serve as a discrete analogue of balls of radius
k
centered at
A_{p,q}(1)
, where we consider two faces to be adjacent if their intersection is non-empty. More commonly, proper combinatorial balls with respect to edge-distance in a graph with vertex set
V
are defined as
\mathcal{B}_{k}(x)=\{y\in V:d(x,y)\leq k\}
. Then viewing the
\mathcal{B}_{k}(x)
as a structure on the dual graph of an animal, the definitions of
A_{p,q}(k)
and
\mathcal{B}_{k}(x)
coincide when
q=3
, since every face in the
k
-th layer is necessarily glued along an edge to a face of
A_{p,q}(k-1)
. However, when
q\geq 4
there will always be new faces only glued to the previous layer at a vertex, and
\mathcal{B}_{k}(x)
will correspond to a sub-animal of
A_{p,q}(k)
.

In
[
13
]
, Keller and Peyerimhoff compute the perimeter of these combinatorial balls
\mathcal{B}_{k}(x)
. These values are then used to find the
exponential growth
of the graph, defined as,

\mu=\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{\log\text{vol}(\mathcal{B}_{k}(x))}{k}.

They obtain
\mu=\tau+\sqrt{\tau^{2}-1}
for the
\{p,q\}
-tessellation, where
\tau=q-\frac{2}{p-2}
. This value is related to our
t=(p-2)(q-2)-2
by
\tau=\frac{t}{p-2}+2
. Moreover, Theorem
1.3
can be stated in terms of the combinatorial curvature at a vertex of a
\{p,q\}
-tessellation, given by
\kappa=1-\frac{q}{2}+\frac{q}{p}=\frac{2-t}{2p}
.

In
[
22
]
, Mertens and Moore find recursive formulas for the number of vertices and tiles on the sequence
A_{p,q}(k)
in order to sample a certain type of random hyperbolic
\{p,q\}
-animals that allows them to give computational estimates of hyperbolic percolation thresholds on
\{p,q\}
-tessellations.

Instead of relying on these independent and related works, we give a complete proof of Theorem
1.3
to keep the discussion self-contained. In addition, our analysis is aimed at a more detailed description of the perimeter structure of
A_{p,q}(k)
, which we use to compute the graph parameters of all extremal
n
-tile
\{p,q\}
-animals.

After proving Theorem
1.3
, we construct and analyze a sequence of spiral animals with
n
tiles, of which the complete
k
-layered animals are a subsequence. These
\{p,q\}
-spirals are denoted by
S_{p,q}(n)
and are constructed in roughly the following way: find the maximum
k
such that
\check{n}(k)\leq n
, then attach
n-\check{n}(k)
adjacent tiles in one direction along the boundary of
A_{p,q}(k)
, saturating perimeter vertices one a time along the way. The definition of the sequence
S_{p,q}(n)
is made more precise in Definition
4.1
(see Figure
2
).

Before discussing the spirals in more detail, we pause here for a minute to point out the subtleties in the deluge of notation and bookkeeping we employ to keep track of all the parameters we are interested in. In particular, the same set of graph parameters are discussed in three distinct settings, and we use variations on the notation
v
,
e
, and
n
for each. First, we have the parameters of
A_{p,q}(k)
, which use the adorned letters presented in Theorem
1.3
with the layer value as the input. For the spiral
S_{p,q}(n)
we use unadorned letters and the tile number as the input, e.g.,
v(n)=v(S_{p,q}(n)),e(n)=e(S_{p,q}(n))
, etc. For a
\{p,q\}
-animal
A
or it’s underlying planar graph, we use unadorned notation and input the animal’s name, e.g.,
v(A)
,
e(A)
, etc.

For the spiral
S_{p,q}(n)
, we compute its parameters in terms of the sequence of vertex degrees on the perimeter of the previous layer of the spiral. To accomplish this, it is useful to label the vertices on the perimeter of
A_{p,q}(k)
by
x_{k,i}
, and to denote by
d_{k,i}
the degree of
x_{k,i}
. The sequences

are computed recursively at the end of Section 3.

Theorem 1.5
.

Fix
\{p,q\}
such that
(p-2)(q-2)\geq 4
. Let
S_{p,q}(n)
be the
\{p,q\}
-spiral with
n

k

\check{n}(k)\leq n<\check{n}(k+1)

A_{p,q}(k)

S_{p,q}(n)

\displaystyle e(n)

\displaystyle=(p-1)(n-\check{n}(k))+\check{e}(k)-m,

\displaystyle v(n)

\displaystyle=(p-2)(n-\check{n}(k))+\check{v}(k)-m.

\displaystyle e_{1}(n)

\displaystyle=(p-2)(n-\check{n}(k))+\check{e}_{1}(k)-2m,

Furthermore,
m
can be computed as follows: If
n-\check{n}(k)\leq q-d_{k,1}
, then
m=0
. And otherwise

takes the unique value
\geq 1
such that

(1.6)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}(q-d_{k,i})\leq n-\check{n}(k)-1<\sum_{i=1}^{m+1}(q-d_{k,i}).

The next theorem states that the spirals in fact attain extremal values in the graph parameters listed above. It is natural to imagine that this should be the case, as the spiral configuration intuitively has the effect of wrapping up as many vertices and edges in the interior as possible, where they can be shared by the most tiles. Thus, interior parameters are maximized while perimeter and overall counts are minimized.

Theorem 1.7
.

Fix
\{p,q\}
such that
(p-2)(q-2)\geq 4
, and let
A
be a
\{p,q\}
-animal with
n
tiles. Then
e_{2}(A)
and
v_{int}(A)
are maximized, and
e_{1}(A)
,
e(A)
, and
v(A)
are minimized when
A=S_{p,q}(n)
.

Spiral-like
animals have also been used by Buchholz and de Launey
[
6
]
for exploring the extremal combinatorial problem of edge-minimization for families of
\{p,q\}
-animals that do not tessellate the Euclidean plane. They use spiral-like arrangements to obtain asymptotic results for extremal values on the number of internal edges. In that setting, it remains open as to whether these spiral-like arrangements are actually extremal, that is, if they attain and provide the exact minimal optimal values for the number of internal edges.

Theorem
1.7
shows that the minimum perimeter attainable by a
\{p,q\}
-animal with
n
tiles, which we denote by
\mathcal{P}_{p,q}(n)
, is equal to
e_{1}(n)
. Plugging the Euclidean
\{p,q\}
values into Theorem
1.5
, we recover Harary and Harborth’s formulas for extremal values of animals on regular tessellations of the Euclidean plane
[
7
]
.

Corollary 1.8
.

For
\{p,q\}=\{3,6\}
,
\{4,4\}
, and
\{6,3\}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{3,6}(n)

\displaystyle=2\left\lceil\frac{1}{2}(n+\sqrt{6n})\right\rceil-n,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{4,4}(n)

\displaystyle=2\left\lceil 2\sqrt{n}\right\rceil,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{6,3}(n)

\displaystyle=2\left\lceil\sqrt{12n-3}\right\rceil.

While the Euclidean cases immediately yield closed formulas, in the hyperbolic case it remains open to find a closed formula for
\mathcal{P}_{p,q}
in terms of

.

Finally, we address the problem of enumerating extremal
n
-tile animals for a fixed

, exhibiting several sequences which give unique extremal animals up to isometries. The question of counting extremal animals was first posed by Harary and Harborth in
[

\{4,4\}

[

, Kurz proved that squares and pronic rectangles, i.e. polyominoes with

\{p,q\}
pairs in Theorems
6.3
and

.

2.
Definitions and Preliminary Results

To begin, we restrict ourselves to animals with no holes, where a hole is defined to be a finite connected component of the complement. We then extend results on extremality to animals with holes as the last piece in proving Theorem
1.7
.

Consider a
\{p,q\}
-animal
A
with no holes. The edges and vertices of the
p
-gons of
A
define a planar graph in which all vertices have degree at least 2 and at most
q
, and its bounded faces are all regular
p
-gons. Here, and throughout this paper we use the term faces only to refer to the
bounded
faces of a planar graph. So the number of tiles of
A
is equal to the number of faces of its underlying planar graph, which we also refer to simply as
A
, conflating the notation for convenience.

There is also a dual graph
G^{\prime}
associated with any planar graph
G
, constructed by placing a vertex at each face and connecting two vertices if and only if their corresponding faces share an edge. For an animal
A
, the dual graph
A^{\prime}
may not be an animal, as some edges may not be contained in any faces. Thus we define a larger class of planar graphs to work in, which contains all graphs defined by animals with no holes.

Definition 2.1
.

We call
G
a
\{p,q\}
-graph if
G
is a planar graph in which each vertex of
G
has degree at most
q
, and each face of
G
has exactly
p
edges.

Then a
\{p,q\}
-animal with no holes is necessarily a
\{p,q\}
-graph. However, not every
\{p,q\}
-graph is the graph of an animal or even a subgraph of the regular
\{p,q\}
-tessellation. For instance, we can build a rooted
\{p,q\}
-tree starting with a single vertex, the root
R
, which has
q
adjacent vertices. Then we give each of
R
’s neighbors
q-1
distinct additional neighbors, and then each of those new vertices get
q-1
distinct additional neighbors, and so on. After more than
p/2
iterations of this, such a graph can no longer live in the
\{p,q\}
-tessellation, as these strings starting from
R
would necessarily form
p
-gons.

For the rest of this paper, we always assume that
(p-2)(q-2)\geq 4
for the pair
\{p,q\}
. We denote the total number of vertices, edges and faces of a fixed graph
G
in the same manner as for a
\{p,q\}
-animal, using
v(G)
,
e(G)
and
n(G)
, respectively. Furthermore, we partition the vertices and edges by the number of incident edges and faces, respectively:

\displaystyle v_{i}(G)

\displaystyle=\#\text{ vertices of degree }i,\text{ for }1\leq i\leq q-1,

\displaystyle v_{q}(G)

\displaystyle=\#\text{ vertices of degree $q$ incident to only $q-1$ faces,}

\displaystyle v_{int}(G)

\displaystyle=\#\text{ interior vertices, i.e., vertices of degree $q$ incident to $q$ faces,}

\displaystyle e_{i}(G)

\displaystyle=\#\text{ edges incident to $i$ faces, for }i=0,1,2.

All non-interior vertices are called
perimeter
vertices, and these are partitioned by the
v_{i}
according to their degrees.

When a fixed
G
is understood, we write simply
v
,
e
,
n
, etc, and for its dual graph
G^{\prime}
we write
v^{\prime}
,
e^{\prime}
,
n^{\prime}
, etc. It is easily seen that when
G
is a
\{p,q\}
-graph,
p
and
q
switch roles for
G^{\prime}
, which is a
\{q,p\}
-graph. In general,
v^{\prime}
,
e^{\prime}
and
n^{\prime}
can be counted directly by various parameters of
G
. We know by definition that
v^{\prime}=n
; the edges of
G^{\prime}
correspond to edges of
G
which are incident to two faces of
G
, and hence
e^{\prime}=e_{2}
; and a face of
G^{\prime}
corresponds to an interior vertex of
G
, so
n^{\prime}=v_{int}
. We further partition the edges of the dual graph and count its connected components:

\displaystyle e_{2,i}(G)

\displaystyle=\#\text{ edges in $G^{\prime}$ incident to $i$ faces, for }i=0,1,2,

\displaystyle c^{\prime}(G)

\displaystyle=\#\text{ connected components of $G^{\prime}$.}

A
\{5,5\}
-animal and its dual. Its parameters are

When
G
is an animal,
c^{\prime}
is always 1 by definition. But here we will obtain more general bounds for all
\{p,q\}
-graphs. Using Euler’s formula for
G^{\prime}
, and also counting its edges with respect to incident faces, we have that:

\displaystyle n-e_{2}+v_{int}

\displaystyle=c^{\prime},

\displaystyle q\cdot v_{int}

\displaystyle=e_{2,1}+2e_{2,2}=e_{2}-e_{2,0}+e_{2,2}.

Combining these equations to eliminate
v_{int}
yields

(2.2)

\displaystyle e_{2}=\frac{q(n-c^{\prime})-e_{2,0}+e_{2,2}}{q-1}.

This formula will allow us to use induction to prove that
e_{2}(G)\leq e_{2}(S_{p,q}(n))
for any
\{p,q\}
-graph with exactly
n
faces by translating the inequality to the dual graphs.

We will also need the following lemma which asserts that the number of tiles decreases when we perform the dual operation. Observe that if a
\{p,q\}
-graph is a
tree
, that is a connected graph with no faces, then
v_{int}=0
and the dual graph of that component is the empty graph. Thus, it is sufficient to only consider components with faces.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
G
be a
\{p,q\}
-graph with
q\geq 4
such that every connected component has at least one face. Then
v_{int}\leq n-1
. Moreover, if
q=3
then
v_{int}\leq 2n-2
, and if
q\geq 6
then
v_{int}<\frac{n}{2}
.

Proof.

The Handshake Lemma and Euler’s formula for
G
are:

\displaystyle q\cdot v_{int}+\sum_{i=1}^{q}i\cdot v_{i}

\displaystyle=2e,

\displaystyle v-e+n

\displaystyle=c.

Using these equations to eliminate
e
we obtain:

(q-2)v_{int}+\sum_{i=1}^{q}(i-2)\cdot v_{i}=2n-2c.

The sum of
(i-2)\cdot v_{i}
can be negative if
v_{1}>0
, and to avoid this we iteratively prune all degree 1 vertices, one at a time, until there are none left. Cycles in a graph cannot be deleted via this process, therefore since each component is assumed to have at least one face, the resulting graph has the same number of faces, interior vertices, and connected components as
G
. Let
\bar{v}_{i}
denote the corresponding parameters in the reduced graph, with
\bar{v}_{1}=0
. Then

(q-2)v_{int}\leq(q-2)v_{int}+\sum_{i=2}^{q}(i-2)\cdot\bar{v}_{i}=2n-2c\leq 2n-2.

This gives the desired bounds, choosing the appropriate
q
in each case.
∎

are denoted by
\check{v}(k)
,
\check{e}(k)

\check{n}(k)

As each new layer is added, these parameters can be tracked by characterizing the geometry of how individual tiles are attached. When adding a tile
T

\deg_{A}(T)
to denote the number of edges

\epsilon

for every tile

in the
k

One key observation is that the intersection of a tile in the
k
-th layer with

is a connected path, which may be only a single vertex, in which case the tile is
0-glued
. Then a given perimeter edge gets covered with a single tile, and we must consider if that tile can cover any adjacent edges on the perimeter as well. For instance, a tile that is 2-glued will completely cover one shared vertex with
A_{p,q}(k-1)
, which is possible if and only if that vertex already has degree
q
in
A_{p,q}(k-1)
. Otherwise 0-glued tiles must be filled in next to a 1-glued tile until the degree of the common perimeter vertex is

(see Figure

Remark 3.1

If

Remark 3.2

If

, then

4.
\{p,q\}
-spiral animals

In this section, we turn our attention to
\{p,q\}
-spiral animals, counting their graph parameters, and proving Theorem
1.5
.

The
\{p,q\}
-spiral with
n
tiles will be denoted by
S_{p,q}(n)
. Recall that we refer to its graph parameters as
e(n),v(n),e_{1}(n)
, etc. The following procedure for constructing
S_{p,q}(n)
gives the spiral in the counter-clockwise direction, as seen in Figure
2
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
n
be a positive integer. Then
S_{p,q}(n)
is constructed by adding tiles
T_{1},T_{2},\ldots,T_{n}
one at a time such that
T_{n}
is the unique tile added to
S_{p,q}(n-1)
to obtain
S_{p,q}(n)
. If
n=\check{n}(k)
, then
S_{p,q}(n)=A_{p,q}(k)
. Otherwise, for
\check{n}(k)<n<\check{n}(k+1)
, first
T_{\check{n}(k)+1}
is attached to
A_{p,q}(k)
by gluing it along the edge
\{x_{k,1},x_{k,\check{e}_{1}(k)}\}
. Then subsequent tiles
T_{i}
are added by gluing along the perimeter edge of
T_{i-1}
which contains
x_{k,1}
, until
x_{k,1}
is saturated. Next, we move on to the vertex
x_{k,2}
, and we repeat this process until
n-\check{n}(k)
tiles have been added to
A_{p,q}(k)
.

For the rest of this section, we always assume that
\check{n}(k)<n\leq\check{n}(k+1)
, and thus
T_{n}
is a tile being added in the
(k+1)
-th layer. Similar to
A_{p,q}(k)
, we use the gluing parameter of each subsequent tile to chart the jumps in the sequences
e_{1}(n)
and
e_{2}(n)
. Here,
T_{n}
is added to
S_{p,q}(n-1)
, and so we say
T_{n}
is
\epsilon
-glued if
\deg_{S_{p,q}(n-1)}(T_{n})=\epsilon
, with
\epsilon\geq 1
since
T_{n}
always shares an edge with
T_{n-1}
.

Remark 4.2
.

If
T_{n}
is
\epsilon
-glued to
S_{p,q}(n-1)
, then

\displaystyle e_{1}(n)-e_{1}(n-1)

\displaystyle=p-2\epsilon,

\displaystyle e_{2}(n)-e_{2}(n-1)

\displaystyle=\epsilon.

This is a simple observation, as when
T_{n}
is
\epsilon
-glued it adds
p-\epsilon
perimeter edges while covering
\epsilon
edges of
S_{p,q}(n-1)
, and each covered edge is added to the count for
e_{2}
.

With
T_{n}
in the
(k+1)
-th layer, note that
\deg_{S_{p,q}(n-1)}(T_{n})
is also easily determined from
\deg_{A_{p,q}(k)}(T_{n})
, differing by either 0, 1, or 2 additional edges. The first tile
T_{\check{n}(k)+1}
has no additional shared edges with other tiles in the
(k+1)
-th layer, and is 1-glued for
q\geq 4
and 2-glued for
q=3
(the latter for
k\geq 2
). Meanwhile, the last tile
T_{\check{n}(k+1)}
has two additional shared edges; one with
T_{\check{n}(k+1)-1}
and one with the first tile in this layer,
T_{\check{n}(k)+1}
. Thus, it is 2-glued for
q\geq 4
or 3-glued for
q=3
. For any other value of
n
,
T_{n}
only shares one additional edge with
T_{n-1}
, and so
\deg_{S_{p,q}(n-1)}(T_{n})=\deg_{A_{p,q}(k)}(T_{n})+1
. Combining this with the analysis in Proposition
3.3
and Remarks
3.1
and
3.2
, we can then determine the exact values of these jumps.

Lemma 4.3
.

When
q>3
and for
n>1
,

\displaystyle e_{1}(n)-e_{1}(n-1)=p-2\text{ or }p-4,

\displaystyle e_{2}(n)-e_{2}(n-1)=1\text{ or 2, respectively}.

When
q=3
and for
n>2
,

\displaystyle e_{1}(n)-e_{1}(n-1)=p-4\text{ or }p-6,

\displaystyle e_{2}(n)-e_{2}(n-1)=2\text{ or 3, respectively}.

Corollary 4.4
.

For
n\geq 1
and
l\geq 1
, we have the bounds

\displaystyle e_{2}(n+l)-e_{2}(n)\leq 2l\text{ when }q>3,

\displaystyle e_{2}(n+l)-e_{2}(n)\leq 3l\text{ when }q=3.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Recall that
e_{1}(n)
is the number of perimeter edges of
S_{p,q}(n)
, and
m
is the number of perimeter vertices of
A_{p,q}(k)
which are saturated in
S_{p,q}(n)
.

When
T_{n}
is
\epsilon
-glued, it saturates
\epsilon-1
perimeter vertices of
A_{p,q}(k)
. So an
\epsilon
-glued tile increments
m
by
\epsilon-1
, and
e_{1}
by
p-2\epsilon=(p-2)-2
(increment of
m
). Then, for any
q
, we get

e_{1}(n)=(p-2)(n-\check{n}(k))+\check{e}_{1}(k)-2m,

which gives the expression in Theorem
1.5
. Similarly, we have

\displaystyle e_{2}(n)

\displaystyle=\check{e}_{2}(k)+n-\check{n}(k)+m,

\displaystyle e(n)

\displaystyle=(p-1)(n-\check{n}(k))+\check{e}(k)-m,

\displaystyle v(n)

\displaystyle=(p-2)(n-\check{n}(k))+\check{v}(k)-m.

Now, we compute
m
with the help of the sequence
d_{k,i}
. The vertex
x_{k,1}
has degree
d_{k,1}
in
A_{p,q}(k)
, hence it needs
q-d_{k,i}+1
tiles to become saturated. So, if
n-\check{n}(k)\leq q-d_{k,1}
then
m=0
. Now, for
i>1
, the vertex
x_{k,i}
has degree
d_{k,i}
in
A_{p,q}(k)
, but in the spiral there is already a tile attached to both
x_{k,i-1}
and
x_{k,i}
; so
x_{k,i}
needs
q-d_{k,i}
tiles to become saturated. This analysis, together with the fact that
n-\check{n}(k)
must be between the number of tiles needed to saturate
m
vertices and the number of tiles needed to saturate
m+1
vertices, gives us the inequality,

1+\sum_{i=1}^{m}(q-d_{k,i})\leq n-\check{n}(k)<1+\sum_{i=1}^{m+1}(q-d_{k,i}).

∎

We now turn our attention to the properties of the dual graph of
S_{p,q}(n)
. Recall that
n^{\prime}
is the number of faces in the dual graph, and we use
T_{n^{\prime}}
and
T_{n^{\prime}+1}
to denote the
n^{\prime}
-th and
(n^{\prime}+1)
-th tiles of the dual
\{q,p\}
-spiral. Additionally, recall that
e_{2,i}
is the number of edges that are incident to
i
faces in the dual graph, with
e_{2,0}
tracking how far off
S^{\prime}_{p,q}(n)
is from being exactly a
\{q,p\}
-spiral.

Lemma 4.5
.

The dual graph of
S_{p,q}(n)
consists of the
\{q,p\}
-spiral animal
S_{q,p}(n^{\prime})
with a possibly trivial path of
e_{2,0}
-edges attached. Moreover, if
T_{n^{\prime}+1}
is
\epsilon
-glued to
S_{q,p}(n^{\prime})
, then
e_{2,0}(n)\leq q-2-\epsilon
.

Proof.

Recall that dual faces, i.e., tiles of the dual animal, are in 1-to-1 correspondence with interior vertices of the original. Then by Definition
4.1
, we want to show that if
x_{k,i}
is an interior vertex that corresponds to tile
T_{n^{\prime}}
in the dual, then vertex
x_{k,i+1}
(or
x_{k+1,1}
when
i=\check{e}_{1}(k)
) corresponds to the tile
T_{n^{\prime}+1}
in the dual. To briefly avoid confusion caused by the index, we write
x_{*}
to denote whichever of
x_{k,i+1}
or
x_{k+1,1}
is the next vertex.

For
n\leq q
, this is trivially true, and covering the vertex
x_{1,1}
of
T_{1}
gives
S^{\prime}_{p,q}(q)=S_{q,p}(1)
. For larger
n
, this follows from Definition
4.1
, since tiles are added in the same direction as the increments in the vertex labels. For instance, it is clear that the tiles represented by
x_{k,i}
and
x_{*}
will be adjacent, and the tile
T
of
A_{p,q}(k)
containing the edge
\{x_{k,i},x_{*}\}
corresponds to the next open perimeter vertex in the dual, to which
T_{n^{\prime}+1}
is being glued. Furthermore, when
q>3
, 1-glued tiles correspond to adding a path of
e_{2,0}
-edges in the dual. This path gets closed at the next 2-glued tile, which saturates
x_{*}
and completes
T_{n^{\prime}+1}
.

The bound follows from counting the extant edges of
T_{n^{\prime}+1}
in the dual. There are
\epsilon
edges already present in
S_{q,p}(n^{\prime})
, where it is going to be attached. The vertex representing the 2-glued tile in the original
\{p,q\}
-spiral completes the tile
T_{n^{\prime}+1}
, adding two edges simultaneously. So these
\epsilon+2
edges cannot be in the path tracing out the
q
-gon
T_{n^{\prime}+1}
.
∎

5.
Proof of Theorem
1.7

We now work towards proving Theorem
1.7
and the extremality of the graph properties of
S_{p,q}(n)
. Recall that an animal
A
with
n
tiles is said to be
extremal
if
e_{2}(A)
and
v_{int}(A)
are maximal, and
e(A),e_{1}(A)
or
v(A)
are all minimal in the set of animals with
n
tiles.

First, we show the maximality of
e_{2}(n)
within the class of
\{p,q\}
-graphs with
n
faces. This requires the following lemma, which asserts that if this holds for the dual structure, then it can be lifted to the original graph.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
G
be a
\{p,q\}
-graph with
n
faces and
v_{int}
interior vertices. If
e_{2}\left(G^{\prime}\right)\leq e_{2}\left(S_{q,p}\left(v_{int}\right)\right)
, then
e_{2}(G)\leq e_{2}(n)
.

Proof.

Recall that
e_{2,2}(G)=e_{2}\left(G^{\prime}\right)
, which by assumption is at most
e_{2}\left(S_{q,p}\left(v_{int}\right)\right)
. Then, we can plug this into Equation (
2.2
),

\displaystyle e_{2}(G)

\displaystyle=\frac{q\left(n-c^{\prime}\right)-e_{2,0}(G)+e_{2,2}(G)}{q-1},

\displaystyle\leq\frac{q\left(n-c^{\prime}\right)+e_{2,2}(G)}{q-1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{q\left(n-c^{\prime}\right)+e_{2}\left(S_{q,p}\left(v_{int}\right)\right)}{q-1}.

To avoid a cacophony of indices and parentheses, we abbreviate our notation when referring to the dual
\{q,p\}
-spiral and write
\tilde{e}_{2}(i)=e_{2}\left(S_{q,p}(i)\right)
. Then applying Euler’s identity for
G^{\prime}
to replace
v_{int}
, we have

e_{2}(G)\leq\frac{q\left(n-c^{\prime}\right)+\tilde{e}_{2}\left(v_{int}\right)}{q-1}=\frac{q\left(n-c^{\prime}\right)+\tilde{e}_{2}\left(e_{2}(G)-n+c^{\prime}\right)}{q-1}.

We can reduce this to the case
c^{\prime}=1
by using Corollary
4.4
to obtain

\tilde{e}_{2}\left(e_{2}(G)-n+c^{\prime}\right)=\tilde{e}_{2}\left(e_{2}(G)-n+1+c^{\prime}-1\right)\leq\tilde{e}_{2}\left(e_{2}(G)-n+1\right)+3\left(c^{\prime}-1\right).

And similarly adding and subtracting 1 to
n-c^{\prime}
in the first term, we get

(5.2)

\begin{split}e_{2}(G)&\leq\frac{q\left(n-c^{\prime}\right)+\tilde{e}_{2}\left(e_{2}(G)-n+c^{\prime}\right)}{q-1},\\
&\leq\frac{q(n-1)-q\left(c^{\prime}-1\right)+\tilde{e}_{2}\left(e_{2}(G)-n+1\right)+3\left(c^{\prime}-1\right)}{q-1},\\
&\leq\frac{q(n-1)+\tilde{e}_{2}\left(e_{2}(G)-n+1\right)}{q-1}.\end{split}

The last inequality follows from the fact that
q\geq 3
, so the coefficient of
\left(c^{\prime}-1\right)
is at most 0.

Now we define the sequence

g_{n}(j)=\tilde{e}_{2}(j-n+1)-(q-1)j+q(n-1),

noting that a simple rearrangement of the inequality in Equation (
5.2
) yields
g_{n}(e_{2}(G))\geq 0
. To prove
e_{2}(G)\leq e_{2}(n)
, we will show that
g_{n}
is non-increasing, and
j=e_{2}(n)
is the last value where it is non-negative.

That
g_{n}
is non-increasing is primarily a consequence of Corollary
4.4
applied to the
\{q,p\}
-spiral
S_{q,p}(j-n+1)
. Using
\delta
as the multiplicative factor, which here depends on
p
instead of
q
,

\displaystyle g_{n}(j+1)

\displaystyle=\tilde{e}_{2}(j-n+1+1)-(q-1)(j+1)+q(n-1),

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{e}_{2}(j-n+1)+\delta-(q-1)j-(q-1)+q(n-1),

\displaystyle=g_{n}(j)-q+\delta+1.

Recall that
\delta=2
if
p>3
and
\delta=3
if
p=3
. In the first case,
q\geq 3
, and in the second
q\geq 6
, so we always get
g_{n}(j+1)\leq g_{n}(j)
.

Then
g_{n}(e_{2}(n))=g_{n}\left(e_{2}\left(S_{p,q}(n)\right)\right)\geq 0
follows from
g_{n}\left(e_{2}(G)\right)\geq 0
for any
\{p,q\}
-graph
G
with
n
faces, and it only remains to show that
g_{n}\left(e_{2}(n)+1\right)<0
. Recall that the dual of
S_{p,q}(n)
is connected by definition, and is a
\{q,p\}
-spiral with
n^{\prime}
faces and possibly some additional edges. Hence
c^{\prime}=1
, and by Euler’s formula the number of faces in the dual is
n^{\prime}=1-n+e_{2}(n)
, with
\tilde{e}\left(n^{\prime}\right)=e_{2}(n)
. Now, we can apply Remark
4.2
to
\tilde{e}_{2}\left(1-n+e_{2}(n)+1\right)=\tilde{e}_{2}\left(n^{\prime}+1\right)
. For
\epsilon
the gluing parameter of the tile
T_{n^{\prime}+1}
in the
\{q,p\}
-spiral, we get that

\displaystyle g_{n}\left(e_{2}(n)+1\right)

\displaystyle=\tilde{e}_{2}\left(n^{\prime}+1\right)-(q-1)\left(e_{2}(n)+1\right)+q(n-1),

\displaystyle=\tilde{e}_{2}\left(n^{\prime}\right)+\epsilon-(q-1)e_{2}(n)-(q-1)+q(n-1).

And Equation (
2.2
) for
G=S_{p,q}(n)
yields that

\displaystyle e_{2,0}(n)

\displaystyle=e_{2,2}(n)-(q-1)e_{2}(n)+q(n-1),

\displaystyle=\tilde{e}_{2}\left(n^{\prime}\right)-(q-1)e_{2}(n)+q(n-1).

So we have

\displaystyle g_{n}\left(e_{2}(n)+1\right)=e_{2,0}(n)+\epsilon-(q-1).

Finally, Lemma
4.5
tell us that
e_{2,0}(n)\leq q-2-\epsilon
, and hence
g_{n}\left(e_{2}(n)+1\right)\leq-1
. Therefore, since
g_{n}\left(e_{2}(G)\right)\geq 0
, it must be that
e_{2}(G)\leq e_{2}(n)
.
∎

We use induction and the bounds from Lemma
2.3
to show that this lifting can always be done, and
e_{2}(n)
is in fact always maximal in the class of
\{p,q\}
-graphs.

Lemma 5.3
.

Fix a pair
\{p,q\}
and
n\geq 0
. If
G
is a
\{p,q\}
-graph with
n
faces, then
e_{2}(G)\leq e_{2}(n)
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction, leveraging the dual structure and Lemma
4.5
. Let
\mathcal{I}(p,q,n)
be the statement:

“If
G
is a
\{p,q\}
-graph with exactly
n
faces, then
e_{2}(G)\leq e_{2}(n)
.”

Our initial base case will be that
\mathcal{I}(p,q,n)
holds for all
p,q
and
n
such that
n\leq q
. When
n<q
, there cannot be any interior vertices. So the dual graph of
G
is either a tree, a disjoint union of trees, or the empty graph on
n
vertices, and
e_{2}(G)=e(G^{\prime})\leq n-1=e_{2}(n)
. Similarly, when
n=q
, the optimal value is obtained when the dual graph is a
q
-cycle, then
e_{2}(G)\leq q=e_{2}(q)
.

Next, we observe that Lemma
5.1
asserts that
\mathcal{I}(p,q,n)
follows from
\mathcal{I}(q,p,v_{int})
, where we slightly abuse notation and allow
v_{int}
to represent any viable value for
v_{int}
given a
\{p,q\}
-graph with
n
faces. To make this more rigorous, we combine this with the bounds in Lemma
2.3
for
v_{int}
in terms of
n
to get the following three facts:

(1)

For
p,q\geq 4
,
\mathcal{I}(p,q,n)
follows from the set
\{\mathcal{I}(q,p,i):i\leq n-1\}
.

(2)

For
p=3
,
\mathcal{I}(3,q,n)
follows from the set
\{\mathcal{I}(q,3,i):i\leq n/2\}
.

(3)

For
q=3
,
\mathcal{I}(p,3,n)
follows from the set
\{\mathcal{I}(3,p,i):i\leq 2n-2\}
.

First, we show
\mathcal{I}(p,q,n)
holds for all
n
in the case that both
p,q\geq 4
. Fix
q\geq 4
. Then, for any
p>q
and any
q<n\leq p
, we have that
v_{int}<p
by Lemma
2.3
. Hence
\mathcal{I}(q,p,v_{int})
is satisfied by the base cases, and for any pair
p,q\geq 4
, we conclude
\mathcal{I}(p,q,n)
holds for all
n\leq\max(p,q)
.

Now suppose that for all pairs
p,q\geq 4
, the statement
\mathcal{I}(p,q,i)
holds for all
i\leq\max(p,q)+j
for some fixed integer
j\geq 0
. Then, for any fixed
p
and
q
, let
n=\max(p,q)+j+1
. Thus
\mathcal{I}(p,q,n)
follows by statement (1) and the induction assumption, and applying this to each ordered pair
(p,q)
allows us to increment
j
by 1 in the assumption. Therefore
\mathcal{I}(p,q,n)
holds for all
n
and all
p,q\geq 4
.

Next, fix one parameter at 3, and denote the other by
r\geq 6
. Then
\mathcal{I}(r,3,n)
follows from
\mathcal{I}(3,r,v_{int})
if we set
2n-2\leq r
, and hence
n\leq(r+2)/2
. Conversely, for
\mathcal{I}(3,r,n)
we need up to
\mathcal{I}(r,3,n/2)
. By the previous statement for the
q=3
case, we know
\mathcal{I}(r,3,n/2)
holds if
n/2\leq(r+2)/2\Rightarrow n\leq r+2
. Hence for every
r\geq 6
, we have that
\mathcal{I}(r,3,n)
holds for
n\leq(r+2)/2
, and
\mathcal{I}(3,r,n)
holds for
n\leq r+2
. Using these as the base cases, we zipper them together to perform the induction.

Fix any
r\geq 6
, and suppose that for a fixed
j\geq 0
,
\mathcal{I}(r,3,i)
holds for all
i\leq(r+2)/2+j
, and additionally
\mathcal{I}(3,r,i)
holds for all
i\leq r+2+2j
. Let
n=(r+2)/2+j+1
. Then a graph considered in
\mathcal{I}(r,3,n)
has
v_{int}\leq 2n-2=r+2+2j
. Similarly, for
n=r+2+2j+1
, a graph considered in
\mathcal{I}(3,r,n)
has
v_{int}\leq n/2=(r+2)/2+j+1/2
. For
n=r+2+2j+2
, the bound is
v_{int}\leq n/2=(r+2)/2+j+1
. Hence, adding 1 to
n
in the
q=3
case is covered by the assumption in the
p=3
case, and adding either 1 or 2 in the
p=3
case is covered by the assumption in the
q=3
case. Thus, we can increment
j
by 1 for every
r\geq 6
in both cases, and by induction
\mathcal{I}(r,3,n)
and
\mathcal{I}(3,r,n)
hold for all
r\geq 6
and all
n\geq 0
.

∎

The maximality of
e_{2}(n)
can immediately be churned into the full litany of extremal properties of spiral animals in the class of
\{p,q\}
-graphs. As a corollary, we obtain a proof of the statement of Theorem
1.7
in the particular case for animals with no holes.

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
A
be a
\{p,q\}
-animal with
n
tiles and no holes. Then
e_{2}(A)\leq e_{2}(n)
,
v_{int}(A)\leq v_{int}(n)
,
e_{1}(A)\geq e_{1}(n)
,
e(A)\geq e(n)
, and
v(A)\geq v(n)
.

Proof.

Any
\{p,q\}
-animal
A
with no holes can be regarded as a
\{p,q\}
-graph. Lemma
5.3
implies that
e_{2}(A)\leq e_{2}(n)
, and the remaining inequalities are obtained from the relations between the graph parameters:
e=e_{1}+e_{2}=p\cdot n-e_{2}
;
e_{1}=p\cdot n-2e_{2}
;
v_{int}=1+e_{2}-n
; and
v=1-n+e
.
∎

To prove Theorem
1.7
, it remains to show that this corollary also holds for animals with holes. We will use induction on the number of holes to complete the proof, but first we require the following lemma stating that the number of interior edges in
S_{p,q}(n)
plus the total number of edges in
S_{p,q}(l)
is at least the number of interior edges in
S_{p,q}(n+l)
.

Lemma 5.5
.

For any fixed
n>1
and fixed
l\geq 1
,

e_{2}(n)+e(l)\geq e_{2}(n+l).

Proof.

Note that
e(l)=p\cdot l-e_{2}(l)
. By Corollary
4.4
along with the base case
e_{2}(1)=0
, we know that
e_{2}(l)\leq\delta\cdot l
, where
\delta=2
for
q>3
, and
\delta=3
for
q=3
.
Now consider the case
p\geq 4
, in which
p\geq 2\delta
(if
\delta=2
then
q>3
and
p\geq 4
, if
\delta=3
then
q=3
and
p\geq 6
). Then we have that

\displaystyle e_{2}(n)+e(l)

\displaystyle=e_{2}(n)+p\cdot l-e_{2}(l),

\displaystyle\geq e_{2}(n)+p\cdot l-\delta\cdot l,

\displaystyle\geq e_{2}(n)+\delta\cdot l,

\displaystyle\geq e_{2}(n+l).

The last inequality is again Corollary
4.4
, this time applied to
S_{p,q}(n)
.

When
p=3
, we need a sharper analysis. Note that
p=3
forces
q\geq 6
, and so all tiles are either 1- or 2-glued. Therefore, the increments of the sequence
e_{2}(l)-l
are either 0 or 1, respectively. Furthermore, up to a small offset due to the initial value being
e_{2}(1)-1=-1
, this sequence counts the number tiles that are 2-glued. Recall that these 2-glued tiles are exactly those which completely surround a perimeter vertex, forcing it to the interior. Since for
p=3
the perimeter vertices of
A_{p,q}(k)
have degree
\leq 4
, we need at least
q-3
tiles to surround a perimeter vertex, the first and last of which are always 2-glued. Thus the increments of 1 are separated by at least
q-5
increments of 0.
On the other hand, for fixed
n
the sequence
e_{2}(n)+2l-e_{2}(n+l)
counts the number of 1-glued tiles added when constructing
S_{p,q}(n+l)
starting from
S_{p,q}(n)
. The increments of this sequence are again 0 or 1, but now the increments by 0 are the 2-glued tiles. Since 1-glued tiles are added at a consistent ratio of at least
q-5
to 1, and
e_{2}(l)-l
actually starts at -1, we conclude that

\displaystyle e_{2}(n)+2l-e_{2}(n+l)

\displaystyle\geq e_{2}(l)-l,

\displaystyle=3l-e(l)-l.

The equality here follows from the edge partition formula
e(l)=3l-e_{2}(l)
, and then a simple rearrangement of the inequality gives the desired result.

∎

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

Suppose that
e_{2}(n)
is maximal in the class of
\{p,q\}
-animals with
h

\displaystyle e_{2}(n)+e(l)

\displaystyle\geq e_{2}(n+l),

\displaystyle\geq e_{2}\left(\bar{A}\right),

\displaystyle=e_{2}(A)+e(H),

\displaystyle\geq e_{2}(A)+e(l).

∎

6.
Enumeration of extremal
\{p,q\}
-animals

In 2008
[
15
]
, Kurz enumerated polyominoes, i.e.
\{4,4\}
-animals, that attain minimum perimeter for any fixed number of tiles. Moreover, he proved that for all
l\geq 1
there exists a unique polyomino with
l^{2}
tiles which is extremal, and also a unique polyomino with
l(l+1)
tiles. The former are the square shape polyominoes with side length
l
, and the latter are pronic rectangles with side lengths
l
and
l+1
. In the general
\{p,q\}
setting, we find that the sequence
A_{p,q}(k)
corresponds precisely to squares with odd side lengths, and we define three more sequences corresponding to even squares and the two types of pronic rectangles distinguished by the parity of the shorter side. We are then able to generalize Kurz’s result and show that each of these sequences yields unique extremal
\{p,q\}
-animals for any
p
and
q
.

First we define the sequence
B_{p,q}(k)
analogously to
A_{p,q}(k)
; but start with
B_{p,q}(1)=x_{0}
, a single vertex. Then
B_{p,q}(2)
is the set of
q
tiles incident to
x_{0}
, and
B_{p,q}(3)
is the set of all tiles incident to the perimeter of
B_{p,q}(2)
, and so on. These form another distinct subsequence of spirals that are related to the
A_{p,q}(k)
through the dual graph. Observe that the
B_{p,q}(k)
sequence corresponds directly to the square shaped polyominoes with even side length in the
\{4,4\}
case.

Remark 6.1
.

The dual of
A_{p,q}(k)
is
B_{q,p}(k)
, and thus
B_{p,q}(k)=S_{p,q}(n)
for
n=v_{int}\left(A_{q,p}(k)\right)
. Similarly, the dual of
B_{p,q}(k)
is
A_{q,p}(k-1)
.

We will use the duality of these two structures to build an inductive proof of their uniqueness, but first, we require a short proposition establishing a condition under which, up to isometries, an animal and its dual graph are in 1-to-1 correspondence.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
A
be a
\{p,q\}
-animal such that
e_{2,0}(A)=0
. If
M
is a
\{p,q\}
-animal with
M^{\prime}=A^{\prime}
, then
M=A
.

Proof.

The faces of
A^{\prime}
always represent a fixed set of vertices in the
\{p,q\}
-lattice which are saturated in
A
. By definition, a vertex is saturated if and only if all
q
incident tiles are present in
A
. But if
e_{2,0}(A)=0
, then every vertex of
A^{\prime}
is incident to a face of
A^{\prime}
, and hence every tile of
A
is incident to a saturated vertex of
A
. So a tile is in
A
if and only if it is incident to a saturated vertex. But then
M^{\prime}=A^{\prime}
implies that also
e_{2,0}(M)=0
, so by the same reasoning a tile is in
M
if and only if it is incident to a saturated vertex. Therefore, since
A
and
M
have the same set of saturated vertices, it must be that
M=A
.
∎

Theorem 6.3
.

For any
k\geq 1
,
A_{p,q}(k)
is the unique extremal
\{p,q\}
-animal with
\check{n}(k)
tiles. Similarly, the same is true for
B_{p,q}(k)
for
k\geq 2
, where the number of tiles is computed from
S_{p,q}(n)
for
n=v_{int}\left(A_{q,p}(k)\right)
.

Proof of Theorem
6.3
.

The statement is trivially true for
A_{p,q}(1)
for all
p
and
q
. The induction will proceed by zippering the two sequences, with
B_{p,q}(k)
following from
A_{q,p}(k-1)
and then
A_{p,q}(k)
following from
B_{q,p}(k)
.

Suppose that
A_{p,q}(k-1)
is a unique extremal animal for all
p
and
q
. Then for any fixed
p
and
q
, let
B
be a
\{p,q\}
-animal with
n(B)=n\left(B_{p,q}(k)\right)
and perimeter
e_{1}(B)=e_{1}\left(B_{p,q}(k)\right)
. By the relations in the proof of Corollary
5.4
, we then have that
B
has the same number of interior edges and interior vertices as
B_{p,q}(k)
. Thus
B^{\prime}
has the same number of edges and faces as the dual of
B_{p,q}(k)
, which is
A_{q,p}(k-1)
.

Since
A_{q,p}(k-1)
is itself a spiral, it’s number of edges
e_{2,2}\left(B_{p,q}(k)\right)
is maximal for its number of faces. Hence
e_{2,2}(B)\leq e_{2,2}\left(B_{p,q}(k)\right)
. Then by Equation (
2.2
) with
c^{\prime}=1
in both cases, it must be that
e_{2,0}(B)=0
, and thus
e_{2,2}(A)=e_{2,2}\left(B_{p,q}(k)\right)
. So the dual of
B
has the same number of faces, edges, and interior edges as
A_{q,p}(k-1)
. And by our inductive assumption
A_{q,p}(k-1)
is the unique animal with these parameters, and it must be that
B^{\prime}=A_{q,p}(k-1)
. Hence, since
e_{2,0}=0
, by Proposition
6.2
we can lift this equality to get
B=B_{p,q}(k)
, which is therefore the unique extremal animal with
n=v_{int}\left(A_{q,p}(k)\right)
tiles.

The exact same argument suffices to show that the uniqueness of
A_{p,q}(k)
follows from knowing uniqueness for
B_{q,p}(k)
. Therefore, by induction we have uniqueness of
A_{p,q}(k)
for all
k\geq 1
, and for
B_{p,q}(k)
for all
k\geq 2
.
∎

To extend now the pronic case, we define two more sequences in a similar fashion to the
A_{p,q}(k)
and
B_{p,q}(k)
. Let
C_{p,q}(1)
be the
\{p,q\}
-animal which has two tiles glued along a single adjoining edge, and let
D_{p,q}(1)
be the
\{p,q\}
-graph which is simply a single edge connecting two vertices. Then the sequences
C_{p,q}(k)
and
D_{p,q}(k)
are defined analogously, with all possible tiles incident to the perimeter added in each subsequent step.

Theorem 6.4
.

For any
k\geq 1
,
C_{p,q}(k)
is a unique extremal animal. Similarly, for any
k\geq 2
,
D_{p,q}(k)
is a unique extremal animal.

Proof.

Note that as in the previous case, both of these sequences are subsequences of the spiral
S_{p,q}(n)
, and thus are extremal. Furthermore, the dual of
C_{p,q}(k)
is
D_{q,p}(k)
, and the dual of
D_{p,q}(k)
is
C_{q,p}(k-1)
. It is clear that up to isometries that there is only one
\{p,q\}
-animal on two tiles, and hence
C_{p,q}(1)
is unique. The proof then follows by the same inductive argument as in the proof of Theorem

.

As shown in Figure

naturally appear in pairs in the corners. Then let
A
be an animal obtained by removing two of these corner pairs. It is simple to compute that both

, and since they also have the same number of tiles, all other graph parameters will also be equal. Hence

7.
Conclusions and open problems

Recall that Theorem
1.7
asserts that
\mathcal{P}_{p,q}(n)
, the minimum perimeter that an
n
-tile
\{p,q\}
-animal can attain, is equal to
e_{1}(n)
. Although here we give an algorithm to iteratively find the values of
\mathcal{P}_{p,q}(n)
, it remains as an open problem to find closed formulas for
\mathcal{P}_{p,q}(n)
in the hyperbolic cases. The substitution rules seen in Section
3
hint at the theory of Sturmian words as a promising approach to finding such formulas. Nevertheless, we can derive some interesting properties of
\mathcal{P}_{p,q}
as a direct consequence of Corollary
4.4
and Theorem
1.7
. For instance, we have that
\mathcal{P}_{3,q}(n)-\mathcal{P}_{3,q}(n)=\pm 1
for
q\geq 6
,
\mathcal{P}_{p,q}(n)
is non-decreasing when
p=4
or
\{p,q\}=\{6,3\}
, and
\mathcal{P}_{p,q}(n)
is strictly increasing otherwise. These properties are summarized in the following Corollary.

Corollary 7.1
.

For any
\{p,q\}
with
(p-2)(q-2)\geq 4
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{p,q}(n+1)-\mathcal{P}_{p,q}(n)=p-2\text{ or $p-4$, when $q>3$.}

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{p,3}(n+1)-\mathcal{P}_{p,3}(n)=p-4\text{ or $p-6$, for $n>1$.}

In the hyperbolic case, the minimum perimeter is of the order of the number of tiles. Bounds for
\mathcal{P}_{p,q}(n)
for sufficiently large
n
can be obtained from the following corollary to Theorem
1.5
bounding
e_{2}(n)
.

Corollary 7.2
.

For
p\geq 4
and
n>\check{n}(3)
, we have

\displaystyle n+\frac{n-1}{q-2}\leq e_{2}(n)\leq n+\frac{n-1}{q-3}.

For
p=3
and
n>\check{n}(4)
, we have

\displaystyle n+\frac{n-1}{q-3}\leq e_{2}(n)\leq n+\frac{n-1}{q-4}.

Proof.

For
p\geq 4
, it can be checked that
\check{n}(k)+\dfrac{\check{n}(k)}{q-2}+1\leq\check{e}_{2}(k)\leq\check{n}(k)+\dfrac{\check{n}(k)}{q-3}
, for
k\geq 3
. As usual, given
n
choose
k
such that
\check{n}(k)<n<\check{n}(k+1)
. In this case, each
d_{k,i}=2
or
d_{k,i}=3
so, Formula
(\ref{eqn:formula-m})
gives the following bound

\displaystyle m(q-3)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}(q-d_{k,i})\leq n-\check{n}(k)-1<\sum_{i=1}^{m+1}(q-d_{k,i})\leq(m+1)(q-2),

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\hskip 28.45274pt

\displaystyle\frac{n-\check{n}(k)-1}{q-2}-1\leq m\leq\frac{n-\check{n}(k)-1}{q-3}

Then, the bounds for
\check{e}_{2}(k)
and
m
, together with the formula
e_{2}(n)=n-\check{n}(k)+\check{e}_{2}(k)+m
gives the result. The analysis for
p=3
is analogous.
∎

We further conjecture a sharp limit for this ratio, based on the ratio for the
A_{p,q}(k)
sequence.

Conjecture 7.3
.

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{e_{2}(n)}{n}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\check{e}_{2}(k)}{\check{n}(k)}=\frac{q-1}{q-2}+\frac{1}{\alpha(q-2)}

Recall that
e_{2}(n)
increments by the gluing parameter of each tile. So when
q=3
, all tiles are either 2-glued or 3-glued, and thus

\dfrac{e_{2}(n)}{n}=\dfrac{2n+(\#\text{ of 3-glued tiles})}{n}=2+\dfrac{\#\text{ of 3-glued tiles}}{n}.

And similarly when
q\geq 4
all tiles are either 1-glued or 2-glued, and the ratio is

\frac{e_{2}(n)}{n}=1+\dfrac{\#\text{ of 2-glued tiles}}{n}.

So this limit is capturing the ratio of these maximally glued tiles in each case. If this limit holds, then all such ratios for the graph parameters of
S_{p,q}(n)
will also have limits given by the corresponding subsequences for
A_{p,q}(k)
.

One application of finding exact formulas for
\mathcal{P}_{p,q}(n)
is to explore extremal topological animals. Euclidean animals with minimum perimeter have provided the right shape to produce animals with maximally many holes. We expect that the same will hold true for hyperbolic animals. Represent the maximum number of holes that a
\{p,q\}
-animal with
n
tiles can have by
f_{p,q}(n)
. Then, using techniques introduced in
[
17
]
, the following topological isoperimetric inequality holds

(7.4)

\displaystyle f_{p,q}(n)\leq\frac{np-2(n-1)-\mathcal{P}_{p,q}(n+f_{p,q}(n))}{p}.

This also provides a measure of certain geometric efficiencies, which are optimized when
f_{p,q}(n)
attains this upper bound. In the Euclidean case, much of the analysis in solving the question of how many holes an
n
-tile animal can have stemmed from understanding these efficiencies, and analyzing the gap between
f_{p,q}(n)
and this bound
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
.

Finally, it is also natural to ask about algebraic characterizations of combinatorial parameters of higher dimensional extremal animals. For example, one can consider face-minimization properties of structures that are built by gluing together
d
-dimensional polytopes along codimension-1 faces in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
or
\mathbb{H}^{d}
. In these settings, the analogs of the complete layered animals are straightforward to construct, but it is not immediately clear, for instance, how to generalize the 2-dimensional spirals that interpolate between them.
