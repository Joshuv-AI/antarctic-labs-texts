Nonlinear optical response of Type-II Weyl fermions in two dimensions
Yaraslau Tamashevich, Leone Di Mauro Villari, Marco Ornigotti

Abstract
We present a theoretical model to study the nonlinear optical response of materials with a low-energy spectrum characterised by strongly tilted, type-II, Weyl cones in two dimensions, which we call 2D Weyl materials. Our findings reveal that the tilted nature of the Weyl cones is responsible for the appearance of even harmonics in the nonlinear signal, as well as its strong polarisation dependence. We discuss how it is possible to control such nonlinear response and envision how 2D Weyl materials can be used to realise novel photonic devices for sensing applications.



I
Introduction

Relativistic massless and massive fermions can be probed with high-energy physics experiments, but also appear as low-energy quasi-particle excitations in condensed matter systems, where their massless character is typically protected by crystal symmetries. The low energy dispersion of such excitations usually contain diabolical points and conical intersections, that are frequently referred to as Dirac, or Weyl, points. A prime example of that is certainly graphene. Since its discovery in 2004 by Novoselov and Geim
Novoselov et al. 2004
;
Novoselov and Geim 2005
, in fact, condensed matter physics has been witnessing a rapid expansion in the fabrication and engineering of a wide variety of materials with pseudo-relativistic quasiparticle excitations: the so-called Dirac materials
Wehling et al. 2013
. This class of materials encompasses, amongst others, semimetals
Thakur et al. 2018
, transition-metal-dichalcogenides (TMDs)
Xu et al. 2013
, and topological insulators
Hasan and Kane 2010
.
A considerable amount of effort has then been made in the last decade to understand the electronic and optical properties of such materials, and their underlying fundamental physical principles, both at the linear
Jia 2019
and nonlinear
Guo et al. 2019a
;
You et al. 2018
level. The research in this field, for example, has paved the way for breakthrough in spintronics and valleytronics
Schaibley et al. 2016
, generation of strong harmonics
Kumar et al. 2013
;
Wang et al. 2014
;
Ornigotti et al. 2021
, unconventional exciton states
Brotons-Gisbert et al. 2020
;
Brotons-Gisbert et al. 2019
, superconductivity
Cao et al. 2018
, but also the discovery of new topological states of matter
Hasan and Kane 2010
.The universality of these concepts, moreover, very rapidly contaminated many others fields of physics, leading very quickly to, e.g., the fields of topological mechanics
Beenakker and Kouwenhoven 2016
, topological photonics
Lu et al. 2014
;
Ozawa et al. 2019
, and topological atomic physics
Goldman et al. 2016
;
Dalibard et al. 2011
.

To understand what are the properties and peculiarities of the various Dirac materials, one could first try to classify them based on the nature of their low-energy dispersion, namely if they admit Dirac or Weyl (DW) cones.
Dirac materials contain both time reversal and spatial inversion symmetry. When one of these symmetries is broken, the Dirac points are split into two constituent Weyl points, and the medium becomes a Weyl material. In bulk materials both Dirac and Weyl points have been observed
Armitage et al. 2018
. In two dimensions,
the amount of research on Dirac and Weyl fermions has been remarkable
Wehling et al. 2013
;
Castro Neto et al. 2009
. However, in contrast to Dirac fermions, which are easy to observe and engineer in 2D materials, two dimensional Weyl fermions have been only explored theoretically and, although several materials have been suggested, they are still eluding experimental verification
Guo et al. 2019b
;
You et al. 2019
;
Lin et al. 2016
.

DW cones (fermions) can be further classified into
Type I and Type II cones. While the former present DW cones that are straight, and preserve Lorentz invariance, the latter are tilted and with broken Lorentz invariance.
Type II cones typically occur when Type I DW nodes are tilted enough, along some specific direction, that a Lifschitz transition occurs and the system acquires a finite density of states at the DW node. The first proposal of Type II Weyl fermions
Soluyanov and et
al. 2015
has inspired intensive investigations of the counterpart Type-II Dirac fermions, to the point that during the last few years a good deal of research has been done to characterise and find materials showing tilted DW cones. Type II fermions have been found in a variety of materials such as semimetals, TMDs (PtTe
2
, WTe
2
)
Yan and et al. 2017
, LaAlO
3

3

and PdTe

While the electronic properties of such media are fairly well studied, understanding the nonlinear optical processes, and harnessing their potential applications for photonics, constitutes a less explored territory. This work envisions the characterisation of the nonlinear optical properties of materials with a low energy spectrum described by strongly tilted (Type-II) Weyl cones in two dimensions, which we call 2D Weyl materials (2D-WMs). In particular we are interested in systems with band-crossing nodal points (massless fermions) and broken inversion symmetry such as Cr
2
C and XP
2

;

This work is organised as follows: in Sect. II we present the generalised model for 2D-WMs in the low-energy approximation. Section III is then dedicated to analysing the temporal and spatial symmetries possessed by such materials, and we show explicitly, how the inversion symmetry is broken. Section IV is then dedicated to obtaining Dirac-Bloch equations for 2D-WMs, which are then used in Sect. V to construct the nonlinear induced current. In Sect. VI, we discuss the properties of the harmonics generated by an electromagnetic pulse interacting with 2D-WMs, and, in particular, we point out the role the tilting od DW cones plays in said nonlinear response. Finally, conclusions are drawn in Sect. VII.

II
Hamiltonian for a 2D Weyl Material

To start our analysis, we consider the following low-energy, generalised, two-band Weyl Hamiltonian in
d
dimensions

H_{\xi}(\mathbb{q})=\sum_{\mu=0}^{3}c^{\xi}_{\mu,\mathbb{q}}\sigma_{\mu},

(1)

where
\sigma_{\mu}
are the Pauli matrices (with
\sigma_{0}=\mathbb{I}
),

\xi
indicates a nodal point with opposite chirality (valley degree of freedom), and the coefficients
c_{\mathbf{q},\mu}^{\xi}
are linked to the velocity and tilting parameters of the 2D-WM as follows:

\displaystyle c_{\mathbf{q},0}^{\xi}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{q}_{\xi},

(2a)

\displaystyle\left(c_{\mathbf{q},1}^{\xi}\,c_{\mathbf{q},2}^{\xi}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\hat{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\mathbf{q}_{\xi}\right)^{T},

(2b)

\displaystyle c_{\mathbf{q},3}^{\xi}

\displaystyle=\xi v\,q_{x}+\frac{\Delta}{2},

(2c)

where
\mathbf{a}=(a_{x},a_{y})
is the tilting vector, describing the orientation and magnitude of the tilted cone,
\mathbf{q}_{\xi}=(\xi q_{x},q_{y})
is the valley-dependent momentum,
\hat{\mathbf{v}}
is carrier velocity tensor, whose diagonal elements
v_{ii}
represent the carrier velocity along the direction
x_{i}
, and
v
and
\Delta
are, respectively, the inversion symmetry breaking parameter and a staggered potential accounting for an eventual gap in the band structure. If we introduce the following set of generalised polar coordinates

\displaystyle g_{\mathbf{q}}^{\xi}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\left(c_{\mathbf{q},1}^{\xi}\right)^{2}+\left(c_{\mathbf{q},2}^{\xi}\right)^{2}},

(3a)

\displaystyle\varphi_{\mathbf{q}}^{\xi}

\displaystyle=\arctan\left(\frac{c_{\mathbf{q},2}^{\xi}}{c_{\mathbf{q},1}^{\xi}}\right),

(3b)

the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
1
) can be written in matrix form as follows

H_{\xi}(\mathbb{q})=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}c^{\xi}_{\mathbb{q},0}+c^{\xi}_{\mathbb{q},3}&g^{\xi}_{\mathbb{q}}e^{-i\varphi^{\xi}_{\mathbb{q}}}\\
\ g^{\xi}_{\mathbb{q}}e^{i\varphi^{\xi}_{\mathbb{q}}}&c^{\xi}_{\mathbb{q},0}-c^{\xi}_{\mathbb{q},3}\end{array}\right).

(4)

The form of the above Hamiltonian is particularly convenient and is readily diagonalised, with eigenstates and eigenvalues given by

\displaystyle\epsilon^{\xi}_{\lambda,\mathbb{q}}=c^{\xi}_{\mathbb{q},0}+\lambda f^{\xi}_{\mathbb{q}},

(5)

\displaystyle\vec{u}^{\,\xi}_{\lambda,\mathbb{q}}=\frac{g^{\xi}_{\mathbb{q}}}{f^{\xi}_{\mathbb{q}}\sqrt{2w^{\xi}_{\mathbb{q},\lambda}}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{f^{\xi}_{\mathbb{q}}\,w^{\xi}_{\mathbb{q},\lambda}}{g^{\xi}_{\mathbb{q}}}e^{-i/2\varphi^{\xi}_{\mathbb{q}}}\\
e^{i/2\varphi^{\xi}_{\mathbb{q}}}\end{array}\right),

where
f^{\xi}_{\mathbb{q}}=\sqrt{\left(g^{\xi}_{\mathbb{q}}\right)^{2}+\left(c^{\xi}_{\mathbb{q},3}\right)^{2}}
,
w^{\xi}_{\mathbb{q},\lambda}=1+\lambda\left(c^{\xi}_{\mathbb{q},3}/f^{\xi}_{\mathbb{q}}\right)
(with
\lambda=\pm 1
being the band index, identifying the valence (
\lambda=-1
) or conduction (
\lambda=+1
) band of the material).

Notice, that this form of eigenvalue problem is fairly general, and it applies to every material (or physical process), whose low energy Hamiltonian can be parametrised with a
2\times 2
matrix.

III
Temporal and Spatial Inversion Symmetries of 2D Weyl Materials

In this section we discuss the behaviour of the Hamiltonian defined above under time reversal and spatial inversion symmetry. In particular, we show how the former is preserved, while the latter is broken, due to the presence of the term
c_{\mathbf{q},3}^{\xi}
.

III.1
Time Reversal Symmetry

Let us start with time reversal symmetry. To prove that the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
1
) preserves time reversal symmetry, we need to prove that, under the action of the time reversal operator
\hat{T}=\mathcal{K}
(which, essentially, amounts to complex conjugation), the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
1
) transforms as
\hat{T}H_{\xi}(\mathbf{q})=H_{-\xi}(\mathbf{q})
. An explicit calculation using Eqs.(
1
), and (
2
) gives

\displaystyle\hat{T}H_{\xi}(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle H^{*}_{\xi}(-\mathbf{q})

(6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\xi\sigma_{0}\left(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{q}\right)-\xi\sigma_{x}\left(\hat{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\mathbf{q}\right)_{1}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sigma_{y}\left(\hat{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\mathbf{q}\right)_{2}+\sigma_{z}\left(-\xi v\,q_{x}+\frac{\Delta}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H_{-\xi}(\mathbf{q}),

where
(\hat{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\mathbf{q})_{k}
indicates the
k
-th element of the vector
\hat{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\mathbf{q}
, and we have made use of the relation
-\sigma_{y}^{*}=\sigma_{y}
. Notice, how to go from the third to the fourth line, we only made the substitution
\xi\rightarrow-\xi
, as it is expected from time reversal symmetry to hold.

III.2
Spatial Inversion Symmetry

For the case of spatial inversion symmetry, the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
1
) should transform, under the action of the inversion operator
\hat{I}=\sigma_{x}
, as follows

\hat{I}\,H_{\xi}(-\mathbf{q})\,\hat{I}^{\dagger}=H_{-\xi}(\mathbf{q}).

(7)

Again, using Eqs. (
1
) and (
2
), we can show that this symmetry is broken by calculating explicitly the left- and right-hand side of the above equality and show they give a different result. In partcular, we have that

\displaystyle\hat{I}\,H_{\xi}(-\mathbf{q})\,\hat{I}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\xi\,\sigma_{0}\,\left(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{q}\right)

(8)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\xi\,\sigma_{x}\,\left(\hat{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\mathbf{q}\right)_{1}+\sigma_{y}\,\left(\hat{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\mathbf{q}\right)_{2}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sigma_{z}\,\left(\xi\,v\,q_{x}-\frac{\Delta}{2}\right).

A direct comparison of the result above with Eq. (
6
) allows us to immediately conclude, that
\hat{I}\,H_{\xi}(-\mathbf{q})\,\hat{I}^{\dagger}\neq\,H_{-\xi}(\mathbf{q})
, and therefore that spatial inversion symmetry is broken. Notice, how this symmetry is broken because of the presence of the last term, proportional to
\sigma_{z}
, which corresponds to the
c_{\mathbf{q},3}^{\xi}
term in Eq. (
1
).

IV
Dirac-Bloch Equations for 2D Weyl materials

We can now use the formalism developed so far to derive the Dirac-Bloch equations (DBEs) for a generalised 2D Weyl material. To do so we introduce the minimal substitution
\mathbb{q}\to\bm{\pi}(t)=\mathbb{q}+e\,\mathbb{A}(t)
, where
\mathbb{A}(t)
is an external electromagnetic field. The Hamiltonian is now explicitly time dependent, but it can be still diagonalised in term of instantaneous band eigenstates and eigenvalues.

For the purposes of this work, we employ a linearly polarised, spatially homogeneous, Gaussian electromagnetic pulse, whose vector potential has the following explicit form

\mathbf{A}(t)=E_{0}\,\tau\,e^{-t^{2}/\tau^{2}}\cos(\omega_{L}t)\,\hat{\mathbf{f}}

(9)

where
E_{0}
is the electric field amplitude,
\tau
the duration (FWHM) of the pulse,
\omega_{L}
its carrier frequency, anf
\hat{\mathbf{f}}=\{\hat{\mathbf{x}},\hat{\mathbf{y}}\}
indicates the pulse’s polarisation, which we choose to be either oriented along the
x
- or
y
-axis.

Next, we can represent the time dependent, momentum space coefficients
c_{\bm{\pi},\mu}^{\xi}(t)
, in direct space by means of the formal substitution (i.e., inverse Fourier transform)
\mathbf{q}\,\rightarrow\,-i\hbar\,\nabla
, to obtain the following time-dependent
Type-II Weyl equation

i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\vec{\psi}^{\,\xi}(\mathbf{r},t)=\sum_{\mu}\sigma_{\mu}\mathcal{D}_{\mu}\,\vec{\psi}^{\,\xi}(\mathbf{r},t),

(10)

where the differential operators
\mathcal{D}_{\mu}
have the following explicit expression

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{0}

\displaystyle=a_{x}D_{x}+a_{y}D_{y},

(11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{1}

\displaystyle=v_{xx}D_{x}+v_{xy}D_{y},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{2}

\displaystyle=v_{yx}D_{x}+v_{yy}D_{y},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{3}

\displaystyle=vD_{x}+\frac{\Delta}{2},

where
D_{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}+eA_{\mu}(t)/\hbar c
is the standard covariant derivative
Maggiore
. Note, that while
D_{\mu}
transforms as a three-vector under the Lorentz group,
\mathcal{D}_{\mu}
does not. To prove this, we can define a general form of
\mathcal{D}_{\mu}
from the relations above, as linear combination of covariant derivatives along
x
and
y
with some constant coefficients, i.e.,
\mathcal{D}_{\mu}=a_{\mu}\,D_{x}+b_{\mu}\,D_{y}
, where
a_{\mu},b_{\mu}
can be determined by using the relations above. Applying a Lorentz transformation
\Lambda^{\nu}_{\mu}
to
\mathcal{D}_{\mu}
then gives the following result

\displaystyle\mathcal{\tilde{D}}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{\mu}\,\tilde{D}_{x}+b_{\mu}\,\tilde{D}_{y}

(12)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{\mu}\,\left(\Lambda^{-1}\right)^{\nu}_{x}D_{\nu}+b_{\mu}\,\left(\Lambda^{-1}\right)^{\nu}_{y}\,D_{\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[a_{\mu}\,\left(\Lambda^{-1}\right)^{x}_{x}+b_{\mu}\,\left(\Lambda^{-1}\right)^{x}_{y}\right]\,D_{x}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left[a_{\mu}\left(\Lambda^{-1}\right)^{y}_{x}+b_{\mu}\,\left(\Lambda^{-1}\right)^{y}_{y}\right]D_{y}

\displaystyle\neq

\displaystyle\left(\Lambda^{-1}\right)^{\nu}_{\mu}\mathcal{D}_{\nu},

Hence, the equation above is not Lorentz covariant.

The general spinor solution can be then expressed in term of instantaneous band eigenstates as

\vec{\psi}_{\xi}(\mathbf{r},t)=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\mathbb{q},\lambda}\,\hat{a}_{\mathbb{q},\lambda}\vec{u}^{\xi}_{\lambda,\mathbb{q}}e^{-i(\tilde{\omega}_{\mathbb{q},\lambda}(t)+\gamma_{\mathbb{q},\lambda}(t)-\mathbb{q}\cdot\mathbf{r})},

(13)

where
\hat{a}_{\mathbb{q},\lambda}
are the band-electrons ladder operators for valence (
\lambda=-1
) and conduction (
\lambda=+1
) bands, respectively,

\tilde{\omega}_{\mathbb{q}}(t)=\frac{\lambda}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}\,d\tau\,\epsilon_{\mathbb{q}}(\tau)

(14)

is the dynamical phase, and

\gamma_{\mathbb{q},\lambda}(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}\,d\tau\,\Omega_{\lambda\lambda}(\tau)

(15)

is the Berry phase, with
\Omega_{\lambda\mu}(t)
being the intraband (if
\lambda=\mu
) or interband (if
\lambda\neq\mu
) Rabi frequency, defined as
\Omega_{\lambda\mu}(t)=\left(\vec{u}^{\,\xi}_{\mu,\mathbb{q}}\right)^{\dagger}\cdot\vec{u}^{\,\xi}_{\lambda,\mathbb{q}}
.

Inserting the Ansatz in Eq. (
13
) into the field Hamiltonian

H=\int d\mathbf{r}\,\vec{\psi}_{\xi}^{\,\dagger}(\mathbf{r},t)\sigma_{\mu}\mathcal{D}^{\mu}\vec{\psi}_{\xi}(\mathbf{r},t),

(16)

and upon introducing the electron-hole ladder operators
\hat{a}_{\mathbb{q},1}=\hat{a}_{\mathbb{q}}
and
\hat{a}_{\mathbb{q},-1}=\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{-\mathbb{q}}
, and from the Heisenberg equations of motion for these ladder operators, we arrive, after a lengthy but straightforward calculation, at the following set of DBEs for the population and inversion variables
n^{e}_{\mathbb{q}}=\braket{\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\mathbb{q}}\hat{a}_{\mathbb{q}}}
,
n^{h}_{\mathbf{k}}=\braket{\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{-\mathbb{q}}\hat{b}_{-\mathbb{q}}}
and
p_{\mathbb{q}}=\braket{b_{-\mathbb{q}}a_{\mathbb{q}}}
, i.e.,

\begin{cases}\hbar\dot{p}_{\mathbb{q}}^{\xi}&=-2if_{\mathbb{q}}^{\xi}(t)p_{\mathbb{q}}^{\xi}-i\hbar\Omega_{\mathbb{q}}^{\xi}(t)\,e^{-2i\gamma_{\mathbb{q}}^{\xi}(t)}\Delta n_{\mathbf{q},\xi},\\
\\
\dot{n}^{e,h}_{\mathbb{q}}&=-4\operatorname{Re}\left\{\left(\Omega_{\mathbb{q}}^{\xi}(t)\right)^{*}\,e^{2i\gamma_{\mathbb{q}}^{\xi}(t)}p_{\mathbb{q}}^{\xi}\right\},\end{cases}

(17)

where
\Delta n_{\mathbf{q},\xi}=(n_{\mathbb{q},\xi}^{e}+n_{\mathbb{q},\xi}^{h}-1)
, and
\Omega_{\mathbb{q}}^{\xi}(t)\equiv\Omega_{\lambda,-\lambda}(t)
. An important aspect of the DBEs is that while describing the interband transitions they encapsulate the intraband dynamisc trough the instantaneous energy and dynamical angle
\varphi_{\mathbb{q}}^{\xi}(t)
, as well as the Berry phase
\gamma_{\mathbb{q}}(t)
. In the following section we compute explicitly the interband and intraband contributions to the nonlinear current.

V
Generalised Nonlinear current

We now compute explicitly the time dependent current that we will use to study the nonlinear response of Type-II 2D-WMs. The quantised current density can be written as

\bm{\mathcal{J}}=-e\sum_{\xi}\vec{\psi}^{\,\dagger}_{\xi}\,\nabla_{\pi}H^{\xi}(\mathbb{q})\,\vec{\psi}_{\xi},

(18)

J_{\mu}=-e\,\sum_{\xi,\mu}\,\psi^{\dagger}_{\xi}\,\sigma_{\mu}\partial_{\pi_{\mu}}c_{\mathbf{q},\mu}^{\xi}\vec{\psi}_{\xi}.

(19)

VI
Nonlinear Optical response of 2D Weyl Materials

The nonlinear optical response of material presenting either a Dirac or Weyl low energy dispersion has been the focus of relevant research in recent years. In particular, many studies focused on the role of the nodal point, both gapped or gapless, in the interaction with an external electromagnetic field
Ishikawa 2010
;
Carvalho et al. 2018
;
Carvalho et al. 2017
;
Luu and Wörner 2018
;
Ghimire et al. 2011
;
Lv et al. 2021
;
Drueke et al. 2021
;
Ikeda 2020
. Remarkably it has been pointed out that the spike-like Berry curvature, in type-I and type-II bulk Weyl materials, plays an important role in the generation of efficient high order harmonic generation
Luu and Wörner 2018
;
Ghimire et al. 2011
;
Lv et al. 2021
;
Drueke et al. 2021
.
2D-WMs have not been observed in nature yet, nor have they been fabricated. Specifying a set of parameters
\{\mathbf{a},\hat{\mathbf{v}}\}
that would correspond to a specific 2D-WM, therefore, is not possible. Tilted DW cones, however, have been observed in several bulk and layered materials, such as Mo
\text{Te}_{2}
, TaIr
\text{Te}_{4}
, and W
\text{Te}_{2}

Zhuo et al. 2021
, the latter also showing signatures of tilted Weyl cones in 2D monolayers
Xu et al. 2018
, or layered organic conductors, which possesses Dirac, rather than Weyl, tilted cones
Hirata et al. 2016
. Following the discussion presented in these references, however, allows us to make reasonable assumptions on the shape and values of the material parameters.

In our simulations, therefore, for the Weyl material we assume a diagonal form of the velocity tensor with
v_{xx}=6.70\,v_{0}
,
v_{yy}=6.86\,v_{0}
, and a tilting vector
\mathbf{a}=(5.06,0.75)v_{0}
, where
v_{0}=0.01\,v_{F}
and

is the Fermi velocity in graphene
Katsnelson

For the impinging electromagnetic field, on the other hand, we assume an amplitude of
E_{0}=10^{7}

V/m
, a carrier frequency of
\omega_{L}=12

\tau=50

To characterise the nonlinear optical response we solve Eqs. (
17

, and then we compute the nonlinear electrical current from Eq. (

I(\omega)\thicksim|\omega\,\mathbf{J}(\omega)|^{2}.

This is the main result of our work. The presence of tilted cones in 2D-WMs leads to a polarisation-dependent harmonic spectrum, which allows, on one hand, the generation of even harmonics and, on the other hand, presents an unbalanced response for different impinging polarisation states, which ultimately, is due to the orientation of the tilting vector
\mathbf{a}
. The great sensitivity of the nonlinear response of 2D-WMs to polarisation could be employed, for example, to realise efficient nanoscale nonlinear polarisation sensors. Interestingly, the strong anisotropy shown by the harmonic response of type-II Weyl fermions has been studied, for bulk materials, in two very recent experimental papers
Lv et al. 2021
;
Drueke et al. 2021

and T

;
Carvalho et al. 2017

Tuning the nonlinear response of 2D-WMs -
An important aspect to look at, when considering the potential impact 2D-WMs could have for the realisation of different linear and nonlinear photonic devices, is to understand how their different degrees of freedom, such as anisotropy and tilting, can be engineered to optimise the onset of desiderable features, like the polarisation-dependent SHS amplification described above. To this aim, in Fig.
3
we show how changing the tilting vector
\mathbf{a}
along the
x
-direction (while maintaining it constant in the
y
-direction) affects the intensity of the SHS. As it can be seen, varying the tilting parameter
a_{x}
corresponds to a nonlinear modulation of the intensity of the SHS, which saturates to approx. 60 dB for tilting values
a_{x}\geq 5
. This, moreover, is not limited to SHS, and the same kind of analysis could be applied, in principle, to optimise the material to amplify a specific harmonic signal. Suitably engineering the tilting vector
\mathbf{a}
of 2D-WMs could then lead to very efficient, polarisation-sensitive frequency converters. Nonlinear frequency conversion is a useful tool for the manipulation of laser light. Optical non-linearity is utilised for converting part of the optical power of the input light to output light in a different wavelength region
Liu et al. 2018
;
Binder and Koch 1995
. In this perspective the anisotropy of the sample introduces further possibilities. It would allow to select the optimal polarisation of the input light for the efficiency of the conversion.

VII
Conclusions

In this work, we have introduced a low-energy model for Type-II 2D-WMs, and studied its nonlinear optical response. In particular, we have show how the tilted nature of Type-II DW cones results in a natural polarisation-dependent anisotropy of the nonlinear signal, and in a significant polarisation-dependent amplification (see Fig.
1
). Moreover, we have also discussed how it is possible to control the nonlinear response of such materials, by suitably engineering its properties, in terms of velocity tensor and tilting vector. The results presented here suggest that 2D-WMs possess a significant potential for disruptive photonic devices, such as, for example, very efficient nonlinear polarisation sensors. In addition to that, our work could be impactful in the topological photonics community as well, where Type-II Dirac cones have recently made their appearance in photonic lattices
Pyrialakos et al. 2020
. Understanding the formation and dynamics of Type-II tilted Weyl cones in such materials could, in fact, open new possibilities for the control of light in photonic nanostructures.
