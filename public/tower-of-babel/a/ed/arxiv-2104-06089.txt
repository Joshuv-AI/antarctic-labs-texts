Exponential convergence to a steady-state for a population genetics model with sexual reproduction and selection
Gaël Raoul

Abstract
. We are interested in the dynamics of a population structured by a phenotypic trait. Individuals reproduce sexually, which is represented by a non-linear integral operator. This operator is combined to a multiplicative operator representing selection. When the strength of selection is small, we show that the dynamics of the population is governed by a simple macroscopic differential equation, and that solutions converge exponentially to steady-states that are locally unique. The analysis is based on Wasserstein distance inequalities using a uniform lower bound on distributions. These inequalities are coupled to tail estimates to show the stability of the steady-states.

1.
Introduction

In this paper, we are interested in the dynamics of following kinetic equation that describes a population that is structured by a phenotypic trait and reproduces sexually:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}n(t,x)

\displaystyle=\int\int\Gamma_{\sigma^{2}}\left(x-\frac{y_{*}+y_{*}^{\prime}}{2}\right)(1+\alpha a(y_{*}))n(t,y_{*})(1+\alpha a(y_{*}^{\prime}))n(t,y_{*}^{\prime})\,dy_{*}\,dy_{*}^{\prime}

(1)

\displaystyle\quad-\left(1+\alpha\int a(x)n(t,x)\,dx\right)^{2}n(t,x).

In this model,
t\in[0,\infty)
is the time variable,
x\in\mathbb{R}
is a phenotypic trait. We assume that
\int n(t,x)\,dx=1
for
t\geq 0
, so that
n(t,\cdot)
is the density of individuals along the phenotypic trait
x
. The function
a=a(x)
represents selection: the trait
x
of an individual affects the number of reproductions it takes part in, and this is modulated by a parameter
\alpha>0
(the
strength of selection
) that we will assume small in this study. Throughout this manuscript,
\Gamma_{\sigma^{2}}
is a Gaussian distribution:

(2)

\Gamma_{\sigma^{2}}(x):=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}.

The first term on the right hand side of (
1
) is the birth term: each individual of trait
y
contributes to the production of gametes at a rate
(1+\alpha a(y))
. Two parents, with traits
y_{*}
and
y_{*}^{\prime}
, are necessary for a reproduction to occur, and the offspring’s trait is then drawn from a Gaussian distribution centred in
\frac{y_{*}+y_{*}^{\prime}}{2}
, following Fisher’s
Infinitesimal Model

[
Fisher, 1919
,
Barton et al., 2017
]
. The last term of (
1
) is a death term, with a death rate that is independent of
x
, and that keeps the population size constant. For a discussion of the biological aspects of this model, we refer to Section
1.3
.

Selection models for asexual populations have been thoroughly studied in the last decades (see e.g.
[
Diekmann et al., 2005
,
Desvillettes et al., 2008
]
). A fruitful approach has been to use a Hopf-Cole transform to relate the asexual population models to
constrained Hamilton-Jacobi equations
when the mutation rate is small
[
Diekmann et al., 2005
,
Lorz et al., 2011
]
. In
[
Calvez et al., 2019
]
, this idea was extended to a sexual selection model (very close to (
1
)), through
U(t,x):=-\sigma\log n(t,x)
and the asymptotics of
\sigma>0
is small. The authors are then able to show the local uniqueness of steady-states (the existence of which is proven in
[
Bourgeron et al., 2017
]
). The idea is then to take advantage of the contractive effect of the reproduction operator on derivatives of
U
, that is
\partial_{x}^{i}U(t,x)
for
i\in\{1,2,3\}
. A combination of these derivatives is employed to build a functional spaces where the reproduction operator is contractive close to a steady-state of the equation. In this manuscript, we will try to describe the dynamics of (
1
), that is closely related to the model considered in
[
Calvez et al., 2019
]
. We will however use a different analysis approach, based on Wasserstein distances rather than regularity estimates.

The
2-
Wasserstein distance is defined on
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, the set of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
with a bounded second moment: for
n,m\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
,

W_{2}(n,m)=\left(\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(n,m)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|x-y|^{2}\,d\pi(x,y)\right),

where
\Pi(n,m)
is the set of measures on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with marginals
n
and
m
. Wasserstein distances have proven useful in probability and in analysis and we review below some of existing studies that are close to the analysis done in this manuscript.

Equation (
1
) is closely related to models that describe the alignment of either biological individuals (fish, birds, etc.) or physical rods
[
Bertin et al., 2006
,
Vicsek et al., 1995
,
Degond et al., 2014
]
. More broadly, it is related to inelastic Boltzmann equations
[
Villani, 2006
]
. In this context, the wasserstein distances have been used to describe the long time dynamics of solutions
[
Pulvirenti and Toscani, 2004
,
Bolley and Carrillo, 2007
]
, following the seminal work of Tanaka
[
Tanaka, 1978
]
. Note that a related set of work uses Fourier-based distances that have related properties
[
Bobylev, 1976
,
Carrillo and Toscani, 2007
]
.

Wasserstein distance methods have also been used to analyse Fokker-Planck equations with non-local interaction potentials. They have proven very useful to show the existence and stability of steady-states for these models. We refer to
[
Carrillo and Toscani, 2007
]
for dimension
1
and to
[
Malrieu et al., 2003
]
for the general case. An interesting development was proposed in
[
Carrillo et al., 2003
,
Carrillo et al., 2006
]
, where the authors considered interaction potentials between particles that are (slightly) non-convex. The non-convexity of the interaction potentials is detrimental to the contraction of solutions towards the steady-state: the equation having a gradient-flow structure, the stability of steady-states is related to the convexity of an energy along some geodesics. It can however be shown that these detrimental effects can be balanced out by the contraction effects from other operators present in the equation, provided the non-convexity only occurs in parts of the domain where numerous particles are present. We refer to
[
Cattiaux et al., 2008
,
Bolley et al., 2012
,
Bolley et al., 2013
]
for the development of this idea into more quantitative results.

In this manuscript, we are interested in the stability of the steady-states of (
1
) when
\alpha>0
is small. This hypothesis on
\alpha
is similar to the asymptotics considered in
[
Calvez et al., 2019
]
(but it is not exactly the same). We will however develop a different idea to describe the dynamics of solutions: we will rely on the Wasserstein contraction implied by the reproduction operator (see (
13
) and
[
Raoul, 2017
]
). The main difficulty is then to balance the detrimental effects of the selection term. We restrict our analysis to a situation where the selection function
a(\cdot)
is constant outside of a compact set, so that
n(t,\cdot)
can be bounded from below on that compact set. This allows us to use an argument inspired by the developments made on Fokker-Planck equations with non-convex interaction potentials and that have been discussed above.

In
[
Mirrahimi and Raoul, 2013
]
, a model close to (
1
) with an additional spatial variable has been considered. Heuristic arguments and numerical simulations were used to describe the macroscopic quantities of that model and show that their dynamics can be approximated by the Kirkpatrick-Barton model
[
Kirkpatrick and Barton, 1997
]
. In
[
Raoul, 2017
]
, this idea was made rigorous thanks to Wasserstein estimates combined to parabolic estimates. For the present study, this approach is not sufficient: we want to prove that the distribution
n(t,\cdot)
of the population converges to a unique steady state. To do so, one needs to show that the contraction effect of the reproduction operator
T
can dominate the influence of the multiplicative operator
n\mapsto(1+\alpha a(\cdot))n
. We mention finally the on-going work
[
Poyato and Calvez,
]
that could provide some interesting estimates on the effect of more general selection functions
a
, and could provide ideas to push the present asymptotic study further.

1.1.
Main results and structure of the paper

Our goal is to prove the existence and uniqueness of a steady state of (
1
) in a neighbourhood of
\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-\bar{Z})
, provided
\alpha>0
is small enough. Furthermore, we show that this steady-state is stable: under some conditions on the initial condition, solutions converge exponentially to that steady-state. We may the following assumptions, on the coefficients of the model (Assumption 1) and the initial condition (Assumption 2):

Assumption 1
.

We assume that
a\in W^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}_{+})
is compactly supported, and that for
\bar{Z}\in\mathbb{R}
,

F(\bar{Z})=0,\quad F^{\prime}(\bar{Z})<0,

where

(3)

F(Y):=\int xa(x)\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(x-Y)\,dx-Y\int a(x)\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(x-Y)\,dx.

Assumption 2
.

n^{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\cap C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
,
R>0
and
\rho>0
satisfy

\forall x\in[R,+\infty),\quad\partial_{x}n^{0}(-x)>n^{0}(-x),\quad\partial_{x}n^{0}(x)<-n^{0}(x).

We assume that
W_{2}\left(n^{0},\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}\left(\cdot-Z^{0}\right)\right)\leq\rho
and
n^{0}\geq\frac{1}{R}\Gamma_{1/R}
. Moreover, if
Z^{0}:=\int x\,n^{0}(x)\,dx
, we assume that
F(x)>0
on
[Z^{0},\bar{Z}]
if
Z^{0}<\bar{Z}
(resp.
F(x)<0
on
[\bar{Z},Z^{0}]
if
\bar{Z}<Z^{0}
).

We can now state our main result:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
R>0
and
a\in W^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R})
,
\bar{Z}\in\mathbb{R}
that satisfy Assumption 1.

There exists
\bar{\alpha}>0
,
\rho>0
,
C>0
such that for
\alpha\in(0,\bar{\alpha})
, there exists a steady-state
\bar{n}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
of (
1
) that satisfies
W_{2}(\bar{n},\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-\bar{Z}))\leq C\alpha
and the following properties hold.

If
n^{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\cap C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
satisfies Assumption 2 and
n\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
is the solution of (
1
) with initial data
n^{0}
, then for
t\in[0,\infty)
,

(4)

W_{2}(n(t,\cdot),\bar{n})\leq Ce^{\left(F^{\prime}(\bar{Z})+C\sqrt{\alpha}\right)\alpha t},

and

(5)

\left|\int xn(t,x)\,dx-Y(t)\right|\leq\frac{C}{-\ln\alpha},\quad W_{2}\big(n(t,\cdot),\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(Y(t)-\cdot)\big)\leq\frac{C}{-\ln\alpha}+C\rho e^{-t/8},

where
Y
is the solution of

(6)

Y^{\prime}(t)=F(Y(t)).

with initial data
Y(0)=\int x\,n^{0}(x)\,dx
.

The driving idea of this manuscript is contained in Section
3
: inspired by
[
Bolley et al., 2012
]
, we show that the fact that distributions are bounded away from
0
gives way to improved Wasserstein estimates on the effect of a multiplicative operator. Using explicit examples, we show in Section
3.4
that a lower bound on solutions is indeed necessary to obtain the estimates we introduce. The Wasserstein estimates requiring a lower bound on distributions can only be used on a compact set (since the distributions cannot be bounded away from
0
uniformly on
\mathbb{R}
). They should then be combined to tail estimates. This turned out to be a technical aspect of this study and to obtain these estimates, we had to assume that
a
is compactly supported. Note that combining Wasserstein estimates to tail estimates is also present in the Fokker-Planck manuscripts that have inspired our approach (see
[
Bolley et al., 2012
,
Bolley et al., 2013
]
): the tail estimates are then based on the convexity of potentials outside of a compact set. The analysis of the tails is however made more complex here by the highly non-local effect of selection: the fact that the population size is constant implies that each death corresponds to a birth, but these two events can occur for very different traits
x\in\mathbb{R}
. These birth/death events can thus be seen as ”jumps” in the trait space, and such events were not present in aforementioned works. The main difficulty raised by these events concern the tails of the distributions, and we will need to develop technical estimates to control them. In this manuscript, we only consider the one-dimensional case (
x\in\mathbb{R}
). One benefit is the use of pseudo-inverse distributions (see (
7
)) which provide convenient tools to develop Wasserstein estimates. It would be interesting to try to generalize these Wasserstein estimates to the case of a multi-dimensional phenotypic trait, but new tail estimates would then also be necessary.

In Section
1.2
, we introduce notations and basic properties, before discussing the biological aspects of this study in Section
1.3
, and illustrating the result with a few numerical simulations.
In Section 2, we show that the macroscopic quantity
Z(t)=\int x\,n(t,x)\,dx
(the mean phenotypic trait of the population) approximatively satisfies an ODE. We take advantage of this first result to prove the existence of a steady-state and to obtain uniform tail estimates on
n(t,\cdot)
.
In Section 3, we develop Wasserstein distance estimates that will enable us to control the effects of the multiplicative selection operator
n\mapsto\frac{(1+\alpha a(\cdot)}{\int(1+\alpha a(y)n(y)\,dy}
. We discuss the necessity of a lower bound assumption to obtain these estimates in Section
3.4
. Finally, in Section 4, we combine the results obtained so far to show that the flow of (
1
) contracts solutions around the steady-state, concluding the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

1.2.
Definitions and notations

Wasserstein distance and pseudo-inverse functions

Since we consider measures on
\mathbb{R}
, we can take advantage of the pseudo-inverse of the distributions (see
[
Carrillo and Toscani, 2007
]
for a review on the one-dimensional Wasserstein distance): if
u,v:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
are defined for
z\in[0,1]
by

(7)

z=\int_{-\infty}^{u(z)}n(x)\,dx=\int_{-\infty}^{v(z)}m(x)\,dx,

then
W_{2}(n,m)^{2}=\int_{0}^{1}|u(z)-v(z)|^{2}\,dz
. If
n
is continuous, the derivation of (
7
) provides the following useful property:

(8)

u^{\prime}(z)=\frac{1}{n(u(z))}.

In our analysis, we will use the following quantity to describe the distance between two distributions:

w(n,m)=\min_{\zeta}W_{2}\left(n,m(\cdot-\zeta)\right).

The idea behind this definition is to consider the
W_{2}
distance parallelly to the macroscopic quantity that is the center of mass of the distribution (in (
15
), we will see that the center of mass is an invariant of reproduction operator). Using the pseudo-inverse of
n
and
m
, the quantity
w(\cdot,\cdot)
can be written

w(n,m)^{2}=\min_{\zeta\in\mathbb{R}}\int_{0}^{1}|u(z)-v(z)+\zeta|^{2}\,dz.

If we differentiate this expression, we get that
\zeta=\int_{0}^{1}v(z)-u(z)\,dz
. This provides a more explicit expression of
w(\cdot,\cdot)
:

(9)

w(n,m)=W_{2}\left(n\left(\cdot-Z_{n}\right),m\left(\cdot-Z_{m}\right)\right),

where

(10)

Z_{n}=\int xn(x)\,dx,\quad Z_{m}=\int xm(x)\,dx.

Finally, we will use the following coupling inequality satisfied by
W_{2}
: for
n1,n_{2},m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
and
\theta\in(0,1)
,

(11)

W_{2}(\theta n_{1}+(1-\theta)n_{2},\theta m_{1}+(1-\theta)m_{2})\leq\theta W_{2}(n_{1},m_{1})+(1-\theta)W_{2}(n_{2},m_{2}).

Reproduction operator

For
n,m\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
and
a\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, we define

(12)

I_{n}=\int a(x)n(x)\,dx,\quad I_{m}=\int a(x)m(x)\,dx.

If
n,m\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
have the same center of mass, ie such that
\int xn(x)\,dx=\int xm(x)\,dx
, then (see
[
Raoul, 2017
]
),

(13)

\displaystyle W_{2}\left(T(n),T(m)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}W_{2}(n,m).

We define the operator
T(n,m)
, for
n,m\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
as follows:

(14)

T(n,m)(x)=\int\int\Gamma\left(x-\frac{y_{*}+y_{*}^{\prime}}{2}\right)n(y_{*})m(y_{*}^{\prime})\,dy_{*}\,dy_{*}^{\prime},

and use the notation
T(n):=T(n,n)
. Note that the center of mass is conserved by
T
: if
n\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, then

(15)

\int xT(n)(x)\,dx=\int xn(x)\,dx.

Another property of
T
that will be important for the analysis is that for any
Z\in\mathbb{R}
,

(16)

\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(x-Z)=\int\int\Gamma_{\sigma^{2}}\left(x-\frac{y_{*}+y_{*}^{\prime}}{2}\right)\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(y_{*}-Z)\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(y_{*}^{\prime}-Z)\,dy_{*}\,dy_{*}^{\prime},

that is
T\big(\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-Z)\big)=\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-Z)
(see
[
Turelli and Barton, 1994
,
Raoul, 2017
]
). We introduce the notation
I_{n}(t):=\int a(x)n(t,x)\,dx
, for
n\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+},L^{1}(\mathbb{R}))
, and notice that the solutions of (
1
) can be written in the following integral form

\displaystyle n(t,x)

\displaystyle=n(0,x)e^{-\int_{0}^{t}\left(1+\alpha I_{n}(s)\right)^{2}\,ds}

(17)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\left(1+\alpha I_{n}(s)\right)^{2}T\left(\frac{(1+\alpha a)n(s,\cdot)}{1+\alpha I_{n}(s)}\right)(x)e^{-\int_{s}^{t}\left(1+\alpha I_{n}(\tau)\right)^{2}\,d\tau}\,ds.

1.3.
Biological setting and numerical simulations

In natural populations, phenotypic traits can be measured on individuals of a given species: size of a bacteria, number of eggs laid by a geese, etc. From these observations, biologists have noticed that the phenotypic traits of offspring are often normally distributed around a mean. A theoretical framework has been developed for such populations by Fisher in 1919: the
Infinitesimal Model
(see
[
Fisher, 1919
,
Turelli and Barton, 1994
,
Barton et al., 2017
]
). This model considers a situation where the phenotype is obtained as a sum of the allelic effects of a large number of loci. Under a
weak selection
assumption (see
[
Hartl et al., 1997
,
Bürger, 2000
]
), that is if the phenotype has a limited impact on the survival of the species (this is similar to the assumption that
\alpha>0
is small in our study), the allelic values on different loci decorrelate. This decorrelation property is called
Linkage Equilibrium
and it leads to the Gaussian kernel appearing in (
1
). It also implies that the phenotypic traits of the population are normally distributed (this property is related to the relation (
16
)), and the variance of that Gaussian is the
Variance at Linkage Equilibrium
(denoted by
V_{LE}
). This quantity is related to our parameter
\sigma^{2}
(
\sigma^{2}
which is sometimes called
segregational variance
):
V_{LE}=2\sigma^{2}
.

The fact that the phenotypic traits of a population are normally distributed is a central assumption in Population Genetics (see e.g.
[
Haldane, 1990
,
Wright, 1935
]
). Note that normal distribution can also be observed in asexual populations for very different reasons (see
[
Kimura, 1965
]
), so that the assumption that a population is normally distributed is not directly linked to the infinitesimal model. Typically, a biological problem as depicted by (
1
) would be analysed as follows: provided the selection is weak (that is
\alpha>0
is small), one may assume that the population is a Gaussian distribution around its mean, that is
n(t,x)\sim\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(x-Z(t))
. The dynamics of
Z
can then be approximated by

\displaystyle Z^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\int xn(t,x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\alpha I_{n}(t)\left(1+\alpha I_{n}(t)\right)\left(\int xa(x)n(t,x)\,dx-I_{n}(t)\int xn(t,x)\,dx\right)

(18)

\displaystyle\sim\alpha F(Z(t)),

where
F
is defined by (
3
). This argument is directly related to the so-called Fundamental Theorem of natural selection introduced by Fisher
[
Fisher, 1930
]
. It provides a convenient description of the evolutionary dynamics of populations. One outcome of this argument would be that the mean phenotypic trait of the population converges to a steady-state
\bar{Z}
, and the population would then be normally distributed around
\bar{Z}
with a variance
V_{LE}=2\sigma^{2}
. An interesting aspect of our analysis is that it is connected on this approximation argument: The proof of our main result relies on quantitative estimates around (
18
) and (
16
). We show that the
2-
Wasserstein distances is an convenient quantity to obtain these quantitative estimates.

The model (
1
) describes the dynamics of a constant size population:
\int n(t,x)\,dx\equiv 1
. This assumption is classical in Population genetics. Our analysis can however be used for models that do not satisfy this assumption. We may for instance consider a more ecological model, with a logistic regulation of the population size:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f(t,x)

\displaystyle=\int\int\Gamma_{\sigma^{2}}\left(x-\frac{y_{*}+y_{*}^{\prime}}{2}\right)(1+\alpha a(y_{*}))f(t,y_{*})(1+\alpha a(y_{*}^{\prime}))\frac{f(t,y_{*}^{\prime})}{\int f(t,y)\,dy}\,dy_{*}\,dy_{*}^{\prime}

(19)

\displaystyle\quad-\left(\int K(y)f(t,y)\,dy\right)f(t,x).

Then
n(t,x):=\frac{f(t,x)}{\int f(t,y)\,dy}
satisfies (
1
), and the convergence of
f
to a steady-state
\bar{f}
can be deduced from Theorem
1.1
.

2.
Preliminary results

2.1.
Wasserstein distance to a Gaussian distribution

In this section, we show that the solutions of (
1
) are close to Gaussian distributions in
x
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
R>0
,
\rho>0
and
a\in W^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R})
,
\bar{Z}\in\mathbb{R}
satisfying Assumption 1.

There exists
\bar{\alpha}>0
, and
C>0
such that for
\alpha\in(0,\bar{\alpha})
and
n^{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\cap C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
satisfying Assumption 2, the solution
n\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
of (
1
) with initial data
n^{0}
satisfies

(20)

\forall t\in\left[-C\ln\alpha/\alpha,\infty\right),\quad W_{2}\left(n(t,\cdot),\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\leq C\alpha,

and

(21)

\forall t\in[0,\infty),\quad|Z_{n}(t)-Y(t/\alpha)|\leq\frac{C}{-\ln\alpha},

where
Y
is the solution of (
6
) with initial data
Z^{0}=\int xn^{0}(x)\,dx
. Moreover,

(22)

\forall t\in[0,\infty),\quad\int x^{2}n(t,x)\,dx\leq C.

(23)

\forall t\in[0,\infty),\quad|Z_{n}(t)|\leq\max\left\{|Z^{0}|,\max\{|x|;\,x\in\supp a\}\right\},

(24)

\forall t\in[0,\infty),\quad W_{2}(n(t,\cdot),\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(Z_{n}(t)-\cdot))\leq C\alpha+C\rho e^{-t/8}

Proof of Lemma
2.1
.

Step 1: We derive uniform estimates on moments of
n(t,\cdot)

Let us first notice that Assumption 2 implies a bound on the second moment of
n^{0}
. Indeed,
n^{0}
is decreasing on
[R,R+1]
, thus
n(R+1)\leq\int_{R}^{R+1}n\leq 1
. Since
\partial_{x}n^{0}<-n^{0}
on
[R+1,\infty)
,
\int_{[R+1,\infty)}x^{2}\,n^{0}(x)\,dx\leq\int_{[R+1,\infty)}x^{2}\,n^{0}(R+1)e^{(R+1)-x}\,dx\leq C
for some constant
C>0
. The same argument can be repeated for
x\leq-(R+1)
, and proves the existence of an upper bound on
\int x^{2}\,n^{0}(x)\,dx
that depends on
R
only. This bound also implies a bound on
Z^{0}
:
|Z^{0}|\leq\int\frac{1+x^{2}}{2}n(x)\,dx\leq C
.

We consider now
Z_{n}(t)=\int xn(t,x)\,dx
and notice it satisfies

\displaystyle Z_{n}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=(1+\alpha I_{n}(t))\int y(1+\alpha a(y))n(t,y)\,dy-(1+\alpha I_{n}(t))^{2}Z_{n}(t)

(25)

\displaystyle=\alpha I_{n}(t)(1+\alpha I_{n}(t))\left[\int y\frac{a(y)n(t,y)}{I_{n}(t)}\,dy-Z_{n}(t)\right].

Since
a(\cdot)\geq 0
, the quantity
\int y\frac{a(y)}{I_{n}(t)}n(t,y)\,dy
belongs to the convex envelop of the support of
a
, and (
25
) then implies the uniform bound (
23
). If we multiply (
1
) by
x^{2}
and integrate, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int x^{2}n(t,x)\,dx\leq(1+\alpha I_{n}(t))^{2}\sigma^{2}+\alpha\frac{1+\alpha I_{n}(t)}{2}\int x^{2}a(x)n(t,x)\,dx

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\left(Z_{n}(t)+\alpha\int xa(x)n(t,x)\,dx\right)^{2}-\frac{1+2\alpha I_{n}(t)}{2}\left(1+\alpha I_{n}(t)\right)\int x^{2}n(t,x)\,dx

\displaystyle\quad\leq C-\left(\frac{1}{2}+\mathcal{O}(\alpha)\right)\int x^{2}n(t,x)\,dx,

which, combined to the upper bound already established on
\int x^{2}n^{0}(x)\,dx
, implies (
22
).

Step 2: We show that
n(t,\cdot)
is close to
\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-Z_{n}(t))
when
\alpha>0
is small and
t\geq-\ln\alpha
.

We use (
17
), which, thanks to (
11
), implies

\displaystyle W_{2}(n(t,\cdot),\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-Z_{n}(t)))\leq e^{-\int_{0}^{t}(1+\alpha I_{n}(s))^{2}\,ds}W_{2}(n(0,\cdot),\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-Z_{n}(t)))

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\int_{s}^{t}(1+\alpha I_{n}(\tau))^{2}\,d\tau}(1+\alpha I_{n}(s))^{2}W_{2}\left(T\left(\frac{(1+\alpha a)n(s,\cdot)}{1+\alpha I_{n}(s)}\right),\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-Z_{n}(t))\right)\,ds.

Thanks to (
16
) and if
\alpha\|a\|_{\infty}\leq 1-\frac{3^{1/4}}{\sqrt{2}}
,

\displaystyle W_{2}(n(t,\cdot),\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-Z_{n}(t)))\leq e^{-\int_{0}^{t}(1+\alpha I_{n}(s))^{2}\,ds}W_{2}(n(0,\cdot),\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-Z_{n}(t)))

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\int_{s}^{t}(1+\alpha I_{n}(\tau))^{2}\,d\tau}(1+\alpha I_{n}(s))^{2}W_{2}\left(T\left(\frac{(1+\alpha a)n(s,\cdot)}{1+\alpha I_{n}(s)}\right),T\Big(\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-Z_{n}(t))\Big)\right)\,ds

\displaystyle\quad\leq W_{2}(n(0,\cdot),\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-Z_{n}(t)))e^{-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}t}

\displaystyle\qquad+\left(1+\alpha\|a\|_{\infty}\right)^{2}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}(t-s)}\bigg[W_{2}\left(T\left(\frac{(1+\alpha a)n(s,\cdot)}{1+\alpha I_{n}(s)}\right),T\left(\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-\hat{Z}_{n}(s))\right)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\phantom{dsgsrfghfghjgfdhfgfgegz}+W_{2}\left(T\left(\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-\hat{Z}_{n}(s))\right),T\left(\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-Z_{n}(t))\right)\right)\bigg]\,ds

\displaystyle\quad\leq W_{2}(n(0,\cdot),\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-Z_{n}(t)))e^{-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}t}

\displaystyle\qquad+\left(1+\alpha\|a\|_{\infty}\right)^{2}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}(t-s)}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}W_{2}\left(\frac{(1+\alpha a)n(s,\cdot)}{1+\alpha I_{n}(s)},\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}\left(\cdot-\hat{Z}_{n}(s)\right)\right)+\left|Z_{n}(t)-\hat{Z}_{n}(s)\right|\right]\,ds,

where we have used the notation
\hat{Z}_{n}(s):=\int x\frac{(1+\alpha a)n(s,\cdot)}{1+\alpha I_{n}(s)}\,dx
and the
W_{2}-
contraction implied by
T
on probability measures with the same center of mass (see (
13
)). This definition of
\hat{Z}_{n}
implies in particular that
\hat{Z}_{n}(s)=Z_{n}(s)+\mathcal{O}(\alpha)
, and since
\|Z_{n}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}=\mathcal{O}(\alpha)
(see (
25
)),

\hat{Z}_{n}(s)=Z_{n}(s)+\mathcal{O}(\alpha)=Z_{n}(t)+\mathcal{O}(\alpha)(1+|t-s|).

We may moreover use the following convex decomposition:

\displaystyle\frac{(1+\alpha a)n(s,\cdot)}{1+\alpha I_{n}(s)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+\alpha I_{n}(s)}n(s,\cdot)+\frac{\alpha I_{n}(s)}{1+\alpha I_{n}(s)}\frac{a\,n(s,\cdot)}{I_{n}(s)}

to show that

\displaystyle W_{2}\big(n(t,\cdot),\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-Z_{n}(t))\big)\leq W_{2}\big(n(0,\cdot),\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-Z_{n}(t))\big)e^{-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}t}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1+\mathcal{O}(\alpha)}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}(t-s)}\left[W_{2}(n(s,\cdot),\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-Z_{n}(s)))+\mathcal{O}(\alpha)(1+|t-s|)\right]\,ds

\displaystyle\quad\leq W_{2}\big(n(0,\cdot),\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-Z_{n}(t))\big)e^{-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}t}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1+\mathcal{O}(\alpha)}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}(t-s)}W_{2}(n(s,\cdot),\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-Z_{n}(s)))\,ds+\mathcal{O}(\alpha).

The quantity
\phi(t)=e^{\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}t}W_{2}(n(t,\cdot),\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-Z_{n}(t)))
then satisfies

\phi(t)\leq W_{2}(n(0,\cdot),\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-Z_{n}(t)))+\mathcal{O}(\alpha)e^{\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}t}+\frac{1+\mathcal{O}(\alpha)}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\phi(s)\,ds.

Thanks to a Grönwall’s inequality,

\displaystyle\phi(t)

\displaystyle\leq W_{2}(n(0,\cdot),\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-Z_{n}(t)))+\mathcal{O}(\alpha)e^{\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}t}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\left(W_{2}(n(0,\cdot),\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-Z_{n}(t)))+\mathcal{O}(\alpha)e^{\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}s}\right)\frac{1+\mathcal{O}(\alpha)}{\sqrt{2}}e^{\frac{1+\mathcal{O}(\alpha)}{\sqrt{2}}(t-s)}\,ds

\displaystyle\leq CW_{2}(n(0,\cdot),\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-Z_{n}(t)))e^{\frac{1+\mathcal{O}(\alpha)}{\sqrt{2}}t}+\mathcal{O}(\alpha)e^{\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}t}+\mathcal{O}(\alpha)\int_{0}^{t}e^{\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}s}e^{\frac{1+\mathcal{O}(\alpha)}{\sqrt{2}}(t-s)}\,ds

\displaystyle\leq CW_{2}(n(0,\cdot),\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-Z_{n}(t)))e^{\frac{1+\mathcal{O}(\alpha)}{\sqrt{2}}t}+\mathcal{O}(\alpha)e^{\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}t}.

Then

(26)

W_{2}(n(t,\cdot),\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(Z_{n}(t)-\cdot))\leq CW_{2}(n(0,\cdot),\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-Z_{n}(t)))e^{-\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}-\frac{1+\mathcal{O}(\alpha)}{\sqrt{2}}\right)t}+\mathcal{O}(\alpha),

which proves (
24
), and in particular, if
\alpha>0
is small enough,

(27)

\forall t\geq-8\ln\alpha,\quad W_{2}\left(n(t,\cdot),\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(Z_{n}(t)-\cdot)\right)\leq C\alpha.

Step 3: We show that
Z_{n}(t)\sim\bar{Z}
for
t\geq\frac{-C\ln\alpha}{\alpha}
, provided
\alpha>0
is small enough.

Thanks to the Kantorovich-Rubinstein formula, (
25
) implies

\displaystyle Z_{n}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\alpha I_{n}(t)(1+\alpha I_{n}(t))\left[\int y\frac{a(y)}{I_{n}(t)}\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(Z_{n}(t)-y)\,dy-Z_{n}(t)+\frac{\mathcal{O}(1)}{I_{n}(t)}W_{1}(n(t,\cdot),\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(Z_{n}(t)-\cdot))\right].

Thanks to (
27
) (note that the
1-
Wasserstein distance is dominated by the
2-
Wasserstein distance), for
\alpha>0
small enough and
t\geq-8\ln\alpha
,

(28)

\displaystyle Z_{n}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\alpha(1+\alpha I_{n}(t))\left[F(Z_{n}(t))+\mathcal{O}\left(\alpha\right)\right].

We notice that for
t\in[0,-8\ln\alpha)
,
Z_{n}(t)
remains close to
Z^{0}
:

(29)

|Z_{n}(t)-Z^{0}|\leq\|Z^{\prime}\|_{\infty}(-8\ln\alpha)=\mathcal{O}(\alpha)(-8\ln\alpha)\xrightarrow[\alpha\to 0]{}0.

We recall Assumption 1, Assumption 2, and consider three cases:

Case 1:
Z^{0}
close to
\bar{Z}

Since
F
is continuously differentiable, there exists
\eta>0
such that
F^{\prime}(Z)\leq\frac{F^{\prime}(\bar{Z})}{2}<0
for
Z\in[\bar{Z}-\eta,\bar{Z}+\eta]
. If
|Z^{0}-\bar{Z}|\leq\frac{\eta}{2}
, then (
29
) implies
|Z_{n}(-8\ln\alpha)-\bar{Z}|\leq\eta
provided
\alpha>0
is small enough. For
t\geq-8\ln\alpha
, (
28
) implies

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}|Z_{n}-\bar{Z}|^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\alpha(1+\alpha I_{n}(t))\left[\left(F(Z_{n}(t))-F(\bar{Z})\right)\textrm{sgn }\left(Z_{n}(t)-\bar{Z}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\alpha\right)\right]

(30)

\displaystyle=\alpha\left[F^{\prime}(\theta)\left|Z_{n}(t)-\bar{Z}\right|+\mathcal{O}\left(\alpha\right)\right],

for some
\theta\in[\bar{Z}-\eta,\bar{Z}+\eta]
. Then if
y(t):=\left|Z_{n}(t)-\bar{Z}\right|-C\alpha
(for some constant
C>0
), we have
y^{\prime}\leq\alpha F^{\prime}(\theta)y
when
y\geq 0
. Then
y(t)\leq\eta e^{\alpha\frac{F^{\prime}(\bar{Z})}{2}t}
and as soon as
\alpha>0
is small enough, for some constant
C>0
,

(31)

\displaystyle\forall t\geq\frac{C\ln(-\ln\alpha)}{\alpha},\quad\left|Z_{n}(t)-\bar{Z}\right|\leq\frac{C}{-\ln\alpha},

and for longer times, we have the improved estimate

(32)

\displaystyle\forall t\geq\frac{-C\ln\alpha}{\alpha},\quad\left|Z_{n}(t)-\bar{Z}\right|\leq C\alpha.

Case 2:
Z^{0}<\bar{Z}

Since
F
is a continuous function, Assumption 1 implies that for some
\varepsilon>0
,

\forall Z\in(Z^{0}-\varepsilon,\bar{Z}-\eta/2),\quad\int xa(x)\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(Z-x)\,dx-Z\int a(x)\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}\left(Z-x\right)\,dx\geq\varepsilon.

Then, provided
\alpha>0
is small enough, (
28
) implies
Z_{n}^{\prime}(t)>\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
when
Z_{n}(t)\in(Z^{0}-\varepsilon,\bar{Z}-\eta)
and
t\geq-8\ln\alpha
. This estimate and (
29
) imply
Z_{n}(t)\geq\bar{Z}-\eta/2
for some
t\geq-8\ln\alpha+\frac{|\bar{Z}-Z^{0}|+\varepsilon}{\alpha\varepsilon}
. We can then apply the argument for Case 1 for
t\geq-8\ln\alpha+\frac{|\bar{Z}-Z^{0}|+\varepsilon}{\alpha\varepsilon}
and prove (
31
) and (
32
), for
\alpha>0
small enough, in this case also.

Case 3:
Z^{0}<\bar{Z}

This case is similar to Case 2, and we have thus proven (
31
) and (
32
) (for
\alpha>0
small enough) in all three cases.

Step 4: We prove that
Z_{n}(t)
is close to
Y(\alpha t)
when
\alpha>0
is small.

Thanks to (
29
) and the Lipschitz continuity of
Y
,

\forall t\in[0,-8\ln\alpha],\quad|Z_{n}(t)-Y(\alpha t)|\leq|Z_{n}(t)-Z^{0}|+|Y(\alpha t)-Y(0)|\leq\mathcal{O}(\alpha)(-\ln\alpha).

We can then use (
28
) to show that for
t\geq-8\ln\alpha
,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}|Z_{n}(t)-Y(\alpha t)|

\displaystyle\leq|Z_{n}^{\prime}(t)-\alpha F(Z_{n}(t))|+|\alpha F(Z_{n}(t))-\alpha Y^{\prime}(\alpha t)|

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{2})+\mathcal{O}(\alpha)|Z_{n}(t)-Y(\alpha t)|\leq C\alpha\left(\alpha+|Z_{n}(t)-Y(\alpha t)|\right),

where
C>0
is a constant. Then, thanks to a Grönwall inequality,

|Z_{n}(t)-Y(\alpha t)|\leq\left(|Z_{n}(-8\ln\alpha)-Y(-8\alpha\ln\alpha)|+\alpha\right)e^{C\alpha(t+8\alpha\ln\alpha)}-\alpha.

For
t\in[-8\ln\alpha,C\ln(-\ln\alpha)/\alpha]
, we use (
29
) to show

(33)

\displaystyle|Z_{n}(t)-Y(\alpha t)|\leq C\alpha(-\ln\alpha)e^{-C\ln\alpha}=C\alpha(-\ln\alpha)^{C+1}.

Combining (
29
), (
31
) and (
33
), we obtain (
21
). Finally, (
27
) and (
32
) imply (
20
), which concludes the proof of Lemma
2.1
.

∎

2.2.
Existence of a steady-state

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
R>0
and
a\in W^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R})
,
\bar{Z}\in\mathbb{R}
satisfying Assumption 1. Let
\rho>0
.

There exists
\bar{\alpha}>0
and
C>0
such that for
0<\alpha<\bar{\alpha}
, there is a steady-state
\bar{n}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
for (
1
) that satisfies

W_{2}(\bar{n},\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-\bar{Z}))\leq C\alpha.

Proof of Lemma
2.2
.

Let
n\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
satisfying
W_{2}(n,\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-\bar{Z}))\leq 1
.
We recall the definition (
14
) of
T
and let
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}(n):=T\left(\frac{(1+\alpha a(\cdot))n}{1+\alpha I_{n}}\right)
, where
I_{n}
is defined from
n
through (
12
). We also define
Z_{n}
from
n
thanks to (
10
). Thanks to (
15
),

\displaystyle\hat{Z}

\displaystyle:=\int y\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}(n)(y)\,dy=\int y\frac{(1+\alpha a(y))n(y)}{1+\alpha I_{n}}\,dy=\frac{Z_{n}(1+\alpha I_{n})-\alpha I_{n}Z_{n}+\alpha\int ya(y)n(y)\,dy}{1+\alpha I_{n}}

\displaystyle=Z_{n}+\alpha\frac{F(Z_{n})+\left(\int ya(y)n(y)\,dy-\int ya(y)\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(y-Z_{n})\,dy\right)-\left(\int a(y)n(y)\,dy-\int a(y)\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(y-Z_{n})\,dy\right)Z_{n}}{1+\alpha I_{n}}

(34)

\displaystyle=Z_{n}+\alpha\frac{F^{\prime}(\theta)(Z_{n}-\bar{Z})+\mathcal{O}\left(W_{2}(n,\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-Z_{n}))\right)}{1+\alpha I_{n}},

with
\theta\in[\bar{Z},Z_{n}]
(or
\theta\in[Z_{n},\bar{Z}]
if
Z_{n}<\bar{Z}
), and thanks to the Kantorovich-Rubinstein formula.
We use the contraction (
13
) implied by
T
and inequality (
16
) to show

\displaystyle W_{2}\left(\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}(n),\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}\left(\cdot-\hat{Z}\right)\right)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}W_{2}\left(\frac{(1+\alpha a(\cdot))n}{1+\alpha I_{n}},\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}\left(\cdot-\hat{Z}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\frac{1-2\alpha\|a\|_{\infty}}{1+\alpha I_{n}}W_{2}\left(n,\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}\left(\cdot-\hat{Z}\right)\right)+\alpha\frac{2\|a\|_{\infty}+I_{n}}{1+\alpha I_{n}}W_{2}\left(\frac{2\|a\|_{\infty}+a}{2\|a\|_{\infty}+I_{n}}n,\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}\left(\cdot-\hat{Z}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{1+\mathcal{O}(\alpha)}{\sqrt{2}}\left(W_{2}\left(n,\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}\left(\cdot-Z_{n}\right)\right)+\left|Z_{n}-\hat{Z}\right|\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathcal{O}(\alpha)\left(W_{2}\left(\frac{2\|a\|_{\infty}+a}{2\|a\|_{\infty}+I_{n}}n,\delta_{0}\right)+W_{2}\left(\delta_{0},\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}\left(\cdot-\hat{Z}\right)\right)\right)

(35)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{1+\mathcal{O}(\alpha)}{\sqrt{2}}W_{2}\left(n,\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}\left(\cdot-Z_{n}\right)\right)+\mathcal{O}(\alpha)\left(\left|Z_{n}-\bar{Z}\right|+W_{2}(n,\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-Z_{n}))\right)+\mathcal{O}(\alpha).

We use this inequality and (
34
) to estimate

\displaystyle\left|\hat{Z}-\bar{Z}\right|+\sqrt{\alpha}\,W_{2}\left(\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}(n),\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-\hat{Z})\right)

(36)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left(1+\alpha F^{\prime}(\theta)+\mathcal{O}\left(\alpha^{3/2}\right)\right)|Z_{n}-\bar{Z}|+\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\alpha}\right)\right)\sqrt{\alpha}\,W_{2}\left(n,\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-Z_{n})\right)+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{3/2}).

Let
L(m):=\left|\int y\,m(y)\,dy-\bar{Z}\right|+\sqrt{\alpha}\,W_{2}\left(m,\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}\left(\cdot-\int y\,m(y)\,dy\right)\right)
for
m\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. There exist
\delta>0
such that if
|Z_{n}-\bar{Z}|\leq\delta
(which is implied by
L(n)\leq\delta)
) and
\alpha>0
, then

\displaystyle L(\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}(n))

\displaystyle\leq L(n)+\left(\alpha F^{\prime}(\bar{Z})+\mathcal{O}\left(\alpha^{3/2}\right)\right)|Z_{n}-\bar{Z}|+\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}-1+\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\alpha}\right)\right)\sqrt{\alpha}\,W_{2}\left(n,\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-Z_{n})\right)+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{3/2})

\displaystyle\leq L(n)+\alpha\frac{F^{\prime}(\bar{Z})}{2}L(n)+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{3/2}).

Notice that for
\alpha>0
small,
1+\alpha\frac{F^{\prime}(\bar{Z})}{2}\geq 0
, and
L(n)\leq\alpha^{1/3}
then implies
L(\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}(n))\leq\alpha^{1/3}+\frac{F^{\prime}(\bar{Z})}{2}\alpha^{4/3}+\bar{C}\alpha^{3/2}<\alpha^{1/3}
.
The operator
T_{\alpha}
then satisfies
T_{\alpha}(\Omega)\subset\Omega
, where

\Omega:=\left\{n\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R});L(n)\leq\alpha^{1/3}\right\}\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R}).

The set
\Omega
is a non-empty convex set and
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
is a continuous and compact operator on
\Omega
: the compactness is a consequence of the Ascoli Theorem, since
T(n)(\cdot)
is uniformly Lipschitz continuous for
n\in\Omega
. We can thus apply the Schauder fixed point Theorem to show that there exists a steady-state
\bar{n}
of
T_{\alpha}
within
\Omega
. This steady-state satisfies
0\leq\alpha\frac{F^{\prime}(\bar{Z})}{2}L(n)+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{3/2})
, that is
L(n)\leq C\sqrt{\alpha}
and thus
\left|\bar{Z}-\int x\bar{n}(x)\,dx\right|\leq C\sqrt{\alpha}
. One can check that
\bar{n}
and
R:=\max\{|x|;\,x\in\supp a\}+1
satisfy Assumption 2 if
\alpha>0
is small, and we may thus apply Lemma
2.1
to obtain a more precise estimate:
W_{2}(\bar{n},\Gamma_{\sigma^{2}}(\cdot-\bar{Z}))\leq C\alpha
for some constant
C>0
.
∎

2.3.
Tail estimates

In this section, derive properties of solutions of (
1
) from estimate (
20
). The first property we are interested in is a lower bound of solutions around
x=\bar{Z}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
R>0
and
a\in W^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R})
,
\bar{Z}\in\mathbb{R}
satisfying Assumption 1. Let
\rho>0
.

There exists
\bar{\alpha}>0
and
C>0
such that for
\alpha\in(0,\bar{\alpha})
and
n^{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\cap C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
satisfying Assumption 2, the solution
n\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
of (
1
) with initial data
n^{0}
satisfies

(37)

\forall(t,x)\in[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R},\quad n(t,x)\geq\frac{1}{C}\Gamma_{1/R\wedge\sigma^{2}/2}(\bar{Z}-x).

Proof of Lemma
2.3
.

Thanks to Lemma
2.1
(see (
22
)), for
x_{0}>0
,

\int_{-x_{0}}^{x_{0}}n(t,x)\,dx\geq 1-\int_{(-x_{0},x_{0})^{c}}n(t,x)\,dx\geq 1-\frac{1}{x_{0}^{2}}\int x^{2}n(t,x)\,dx\geq\frac{1}{2},

provided
x_{0}
is chosen large enough. For
\alpha>0
small enough, thanks to (
17
)),

\displaystyle n(t,x)

3.1.
Effect of a multiplicative operator acting on the bulk of the distribution

Lemma 3.1

Step 1: Preliminaries

\tilde{m}

\displaystyle\int_{r}^{1-r}\left|v(\phi_{u}(s))-v(\phi_{v}(s))\right|^{2}\,ds

For

3.2.
Effect of a multiplicative factor on the tails of a distribution

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\nu>0
,
R>0
,
\bar{Z}
and
M>0
. There exists
C>0
such that if
n\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
satisfies
\int x^{2}n(x)\,dx\leq M
,

(51)

\forall x\in(-\infty,-R],\quad n^{\prime}(x)>n(x),\quad\forall x\in[R,\infty),\quad n^{\prime}(x)<-n(x),

(52)

\forall x\in\mathbb{R},\quad n\geq\nu\Gamma_{\nu}(\bar{Z}-\cdot),

and if
p\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
satisfies
\supp p\subset[-R,R]
, then, for any
\alpha,\tilde{\alpha}\in[0,1/4]
,

W_{2}\big((1-\alpha)n+\alpha p,(1-\tilde{\alpha})n+\tilde{\alpha}p\big)\leq C|\alpha-\tilde{\alpha}|.

Remark 3.3
.

Notice that the convexity of
W_{2}
provides a related estimate that holds for any
n,p\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
:

W_{2}\big((1-\alpha)n+\alpha p,(1-\alpha^{\prime})n+\alpha^{\prime}p\big)\leq C\sqrt{|\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}|},

but we need a stronger estimate, linear in
\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}
. This improved estimate derives from the assumptions made on the tails of the distribution
n
.

Proof of Lemma
3.2
.

W.l.o.g.,
\tilde{\alpha}\leq\alpha
. Let
u
the pseudo-inverse of the cumulative distribution of
n
, and
u_{\alpha}
the one of
(1-\alpha)n+\alpha p
. We define
r:=\frac{\nu}{2}\int_{|Z|+R+1}^{\infty}\Gamma_{\nu}
, which implies in particular that
u_{\alpha}(s)<-R-1
for
s\in(0,r)
. For
s\in(0,r]
and
h\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{u_{\tilde{\alpha}}(s)+h}(1-\alpha)n(x)+\alpha p(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{u_{\tilde{\alpha}}(s)+h}(1-\alpha)n(x)\,dx

\displaystyle\geq\int_{-\infty}^{u_{\tilde{\alpha}}(s)}(1-\alpha)n(x)\,dx+\int_{u_{\tilde{\alpha}}(s)}^{u_{\tilde{\alpha}}(s)+h}(1-\alpha)\left(\int_{-\infty}^{x}n(y)\,dy\right)\,dx,

thanks to (
51
). Then,

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{u_{\tilde{\alpha}}(s)+h}(1-\alpha)n(x)+\alpha p(x)\,dx\geq(1+h)\int_{-\infty}^{u_{\tilde{\alpha}}(s)}(1-\alpha)n(x)\,dx

\displaystyle\quad\geq(1+h)\frac{1-\alpha}{1-\tilde{\alpha}}\int_{-\infty}^{u_{\tilde{\alpha}}(s)}(1-\tilde{\alpha})\,n(x)\,dx=(1+h)\,\frac{1-\alpha}{1-\tilde{\alpha}}\,s,

and if we choose
h:=\frac{\alpha-\tilde{\alpha}}{(1-\alpha)}
, we get

\int_{-\infty}^{u_{\tilde{\alpha}}(s)+\frac{\alpha-\tilde{\alpha}}{(1-\alpha)}}(1-\alpha)n(x)+\alpha p(x)\,dx\geq s,

and then
u_{\alpha}(s)\leq u_{\tilde{\alpha}}(s)+\frac{\alpha-\tilde{\alpha}}{1-\alpha}
.

The opposite relationship between
u_{\alpha}(s)
and
u_{\tilde{\alpha}}(s)
(we still consider
s\in(0,r)
here) is easier to obtain: since
\tilde{\alpha}\leq\alpha
and
p(x)=0
for
x\in(-\infty,u_{\tilde{\alpha}}(s)]
,

\int_{-\infty}^{u_{\tilde{\alpha}}(s)}(1-\alpha)n(x)+\alpha p(x)\,dx\leq\int_{-\infty}^{u_{\tilde{\alpha}}(s)}(1-\tilde{\alpha})n(x)+\tilde{\alpha}p(x)\,dx=s,

and then
u_{\tilde{\alpha}}(s)\leq u_{\alpha}(s)
. This argument for
s\in(0,r)
can be repeated for
s\in(1-r,1)
and we get

(53)

\forall s\in(0,r)\cup(1-r,1),\quad\left|u_{\tilde{\alpha}}(s)-u_{\alpha}(s)\right|\leq\frac{\alpha-\tilde{\alpha}}{1-\alpha}.

For
s\in(r,1-r)
, we notice that

\int_{-\infty}^{u_{\alpha}(s)}(1-\alpha)n(x)+\alpha p(x)\,dx=s=\int_{-\infty}^{u_{\tilde{\alpha}}(s)}(1-\tilde{\alpha})n(x)+\tilde{\alpha}p(x)\,dx

and thus

(54)

\left|\int_{-\infty}^{u_{\alpha}(s)}(\tilde{\alpha}-\alpha)n(x)+(\alpha-\tilde{\alpha})p(x)\,dx\right|\geq(1-\tilde{\alpha})|u_{\alpha}(s)-u_{\tilde{\alpha}}(s)|\inf_{[-\chi,\chi]}n,

where
\chi=\max\left(|u_{\alpha}(r)|,|u_{\tilde{\alpha}}(r)|,|u_{\alpha}(1-r)|,u_{\tilde{\alpha}}(1-r)|\right)
. Since
u_{\alpha}(r)<-R
, we have

(55)

\displaystyle r=\int_{-\infty}^{u_{\alpha}(r)}(1-\alpha)n+\alpha p=\int_{-\infty}^{u_{\alpha}(r)}(1-\alpha)n\leq\frac{1-\alpha}{u_{\alpha}(r)^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{u_{\alpha}(r)}x^{2}n(x)\,dx\leq\frac{M}{u_{\alpha}(r)^{2}},

and then
|u_{\alpha}(r)|\leq\sqrt{\frac{M}{r}}
. We may repeat the argument to show that
\chi\leq\sqrt{\frac{M}{r}}
, and then
\inf_{[-\chi,\chi]}n\geq\nu\Gamma_{\nu}\left(|\bar{Z}|+\sqrt{M/r}\right)
, so that (
54
) implies

(56)

|u_{\alpha}(s)-u_{\alpha^{\prime}}(s)|\leq\frac{3}{\nu\Gamma_{\sigma^{2}/2}\left(|\bar{Z}|+\sqrt{M/r}\right)}|\alpha-\tilde{\alpha}|.

This estimate for
s\in[r,1-r]
and (
53
) for
(0,r)\cup(1-r,1)
can be used to conclude the proof:

W_{2}^{2}\Big((1-\alpha)n+\alpha p,(1-\tilde{\alpha})n+\tilde{\alpha}p\Big)=\int_{0}^{1}|u_{\alpha^{\prime}}(s)-u_{\alpha}(s)|^{2}\,ds\leq C|\alpha-\tilde{\alpha}|^{2}.

∎

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}(\alpha)\Big(|Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(s)-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(s)|+w(\mathcal{N}(s,\cdot),\mathcal{M}(s,\cdot))\Big),

and the definition of
w(\cdot,\cdot)
(see (
9
)) implies

\displaystyle W_{2}\Big(\mathcal{N}(s,\cdot),\mathcal{M}\big(s,\cdot-(\tilde{Z}_{m}(s)-\tilde{Z}_{n}(s))\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=w(\mathcal{N}(s,\cdot),\mathcal{M}(s,\cdot))

\displaystyle\quad+\left|(Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(s)-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(s))-(\tilde{Z}_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(s)-\tilde{Z}_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(s))\right|.

These estimates and (
75
) lead to

\displaystyle J_{2}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\alpha\right)|Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(s)-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(s)|+\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\alpha}\right)\right)w(\mathcal{N}(s,\cdot),\mathcal{M}(s,\cdot)),

and then, using again (
75
), estimate (
67
) becomes

\displaystyle J_{1}\leq\mathcal{O}(\alpha)|Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(s)-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(s)|+\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\alpha}\right)\right)w(\mathcal{N}(s,\cdot),\mathcal{M}(s,\cdot)).

Thanks to Assumption 2, the second moments of both
n(0,\cdot)
and
m(0,\cdot)
are bounded and since
Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}
is also bounded thanks to Lemma
2.1
,

\displaystyle w(\mathcal{N}(t,\cdot),\mathcal{M}(t,\cdot))\leq Ce^{-t}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-s)}\left(\mathcal{O}(\alpha)|Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(s)-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(s)|+\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}+\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\alpha})\right)w(\mathcal{N}(s,\cdot),\mathcal{M}(s,\cdot))\right)\,ds,

that is

\displaystyle w(\mathcal{N}(t,\cdot),\mathcal{M}(t,\cdot))\leq Ce^{-t}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-s)}\left(C\alpha|Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(s)-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(s)|+\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}+C\sqrt{\alpha}\right)w(\mathcal{N}(s,\cdot),\mathcal{M}(s,\cdot))\right)\,ds,

for some
C>0
. We define
X:t\in[0,\infty)\mapsto X(t)
by
X(0)=2C
, and

(69)

X^{\prime}(t)=-\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}+C\sqrt{\alpha}\right)X(t)+C\alpha|Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)|.

Then
X
satisfies

\displaystyle X(t)=Ce^{-t}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-s)}C\alpha|Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(s)-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(s)|+\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}+C\sqrt{\alpha}X(s)\right)\,ds,

and thanks to the comparison principle,

(70)

\forall t\in[0,\infty),\quad w(\mathcal{N}(t,\cdot),\mathcal{M}(t,\cdot))\leq X(t).

We notice finally that
|Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}|
is uniformly bounded thanks to (
23
), and then
X^{\prime}(t)\leq 0
as soon as
X(t)\geq C^{\prime}\alpha
(for some constant
C^{\prime}>0
). We have then the following rough estimate:

(71)

X(t)\leq 2C+C\alpha.

4.2.
Contraction estimate for the macroscopic quantity
Z(t)

We recall the notations (
62
) and notations
\mathcal{N}
,
\mathcal{M}
introduced in Section
4.1
. The goal of this section is to prove a contraction result on the difference
Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}
. If we multiply (
1
) by
x
and integrate it, we obtain (see (
25
) for a similar calculation):

\displaystyle(Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m})^{\prime}(t)=\alpha\left(\frac{1}{1+\alpha I_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)}-\frac{1}{1+\alpha I_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)}\right)\left(\int ya(y)\mathcal{N}(t,y)\,dy-I_{n}(t)Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha I_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)}\left(\int ya(y)\mathcal{N}(t,y)\,dy-\int ya(y)\mathcal{M}(t,y)\,dy\right)

(72)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha I_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)}\big(I_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)-I_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)\big).

The first term can be estimated as follows, thanks to (
74
):

\displaystyle\alpha\left(\frac{1}{1+\alpha I_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)}-\frac{1}{1+\alpha I_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)}\right)\left(\int ya(y)n(t,y)\,dy-I_{n}(t)Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{2})\left|I_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)-I_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)\right|=\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{2})|Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)|+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{2})w(\mathcal{N}(t,\cdot),\mathcal{M}(t,\cdot)).

Next we decompose the following factor of the second term on the right hand side of (
72
):

\displaystyle\int ya(y)\mathcal{N}(t,y)\,dy-\int ya(y)\mathcal{M}(t,y)\,dy

\displaystyle\quad=\int(y-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t))a(y-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t))\left(\mathcal{N}(t,y-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t))-\mathcal{M}(t,y-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t))\right)\,dy

\displaystyle\qquad+\int\left((y-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t))a(y-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t))-(y-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t))a(y-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t))\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\hskip 28.45274pt\left(\mathcal{N}(t,y-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t))-\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(y)\right)\,dy

\displaystyle\qquad+\int\left((y-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t))a(y-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t))-(y-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t))a(y-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t))\right)\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(y)\,dy.

We notice that
g(y):=(y-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t))a(y-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t))-(y-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t))a(y-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t))
satisfies

g^{\prime}(y)=a(y-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t))+(y-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t))a^{\prime}(y-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t))-a(y-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t))-(y-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t))a^{\prime}(y-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)),

and then
\|g^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(|Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)|\right)\left(\|a^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+\|a^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}\right)
(note that
a\in W^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R})
thanks to Assumption 1). If
\pi
is a measure on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with marginals
\mathcal{N}(t,\cdot-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t))
and
\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}
, then

\displaystyle\int g(y)\left(\mathcal{N}(t,y-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t))-\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(y)\right)\,dy=\int g(x)-g(y)\,d\pi(x,y)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\|g^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\int|x-y|\,d\pi(x,y)\leq\|g^{\prime}\|_{\infty}W_{2}\left(\mathcal{N}(t,\cdot-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)),\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}\right).

We can use this estimate and (
9
) to obtain

\displaystyle\int ya(y)\mathcal{N}(t,y)\,dy-\int ya(y)\mathcal{M}(t,y)\,dy=\mathcal{O}\big(w(\mathcal{N}(t,\cdot),\mathcal{M}(t,\cdot))\big)

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathcal{O}\left(|Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)|\right)W_{2}(\mathcal{N}(t,\cdot-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)),\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}})

\displaystyle\qquad+\int\big[(y-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t))a(y-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t))-(y-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t))a(y-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t))\big]\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(y)\,dy.

A similar argument can be used to estimate the last term of (
72
):

\displaystyle I_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)-I_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)=\int Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)a(y-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t))\mathcal{M}(t,y-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t))\,dy

\displaystyle\qquad-\int Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)a(y-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t))\mathcal{N}(t,y-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t))\,dy

\displaystyle\quad=\int Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)a(y-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t))\big(\mathcal{M}(t,y-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t))-\mathcal{N}(t,y-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t))\big)\,dy

\displaystyle\qquad+\int\big(Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)a(y-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t))-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)a(y-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t))\big)\left(\mathcal{N}(t,y-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t))-\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(y)\right)\,dy

\displaystyle\qquad+\int\big(Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)a(y-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t))-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)a(y-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t))\big)\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(y)\,dy

and if we define
h(y):=Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)a(y-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t))-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)a(y-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t))
, then
h^{\prime}(y)=Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)a^{\prime}(y-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t))-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)a^{\prime}(y-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t))
and thus
\|h^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq\left(\|a^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+\|a^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}\right)\mathcal{O}(|Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)|)
. We have therefore

\displaystyle I_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)-I_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)=\mathcal{O}\big(w(\mathcal{N}(t,\cdot),\mathcal{M}(t,\cdot))\big)

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathcal{O}(|Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)|)W_{2}\big(\mathcal{N}(t,\cdot-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)),\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad+\int\big[Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)a(y-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t))-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)a(y-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t))\big]\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(y)\,dy.

Thanks to the estimates we have established, (
72
) becomes

\displaystyle(Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m})^{\prime}(t)=\alpha^{2}\mathcal{O}(|Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)|)

\displaystyle\qquad+\alpha\mathcal{O}\big(w(\mathcal{N}(t,\cdot),\mathcal{M}(t,\cdot))\big)+\alpha W_{2}\big(\mathcal{N}(t,\cdot-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)),\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}\big)\mathcal{O}(|Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)|)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha I_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)}\bigg[\int ya(y)\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(y+Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t))\,dy-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)\int a(y)\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(y+Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t))\,dy\bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha I_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)}\bigg[\int ya(y)\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(y+Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t))\,dy-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)\int a(y)\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}(y+Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t))\,dy\bigg]

\displaystyle\quad=\alpha^{2}\mathcal{O}(|Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)|)

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathcal{O}(\alpha)X(t)+\alpha W_{2}\big(\mathcal{N}(t,\cdot-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)),\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}\big)\mathcal{O}(|Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)|)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha I_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)}\left[F(Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t))-F(Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t))\right],

thanks to (
70
). For
t\geq t_{0}=-C\ln\alpha/\alpha
, Lemma
2.1
implies
|Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)-\bar{Z}|\leq C\alpha
,
|Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)-\bar{Z}|\leq C\alpha
, and
W_{2}\big(\mathcal{N}(t,\cdot-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)),\Gamma_{2\sigma^{2}}\big)\leq C\alpha
. For
\alpha>0
small enough, since
F^{\prime}(\bar{Z})<0
(see Assumption 1), we then get

(73)

\frac{d}{dt}|Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)|\leq\left(F^{\prime}(\bar{Z})+\mathcal{O}(\alpha)\right)\alpha|Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)|+\mathcal{O}(\alpha)X(t),

for
t\geq t_{0}=-C\ln\alpha/\alpha
.

4.3.
Conclusion of the proof of Theorem
1.1

Thanks to (
69
) and (
73
), for
t\geq t_{0}=-C\ln\alpha/\alpha
,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\sqrt{\alpha}\,X(t)+|Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)|\right)

\displaystyle\quad\leq-\sqrt{\alpha}\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}+C\sqrt{\alpha}\right)X(t)+C\alpha^{3/2}|Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(s)-Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(s)|

\displaystyle\qquad+\left(F^{\prime}(\bar{Z})+\mathcal{O}(\alpha)\right)\alpha|Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)|+\mathcal{O}(\alpha)X(t)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left(F^{\prime}(\bar{Z})+\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\alpha})\right)\alpha\left(\sqrt{\alpha}\,X(t)+|Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)|\right).

Then (
70
) and (
71
) imply

\displaystyle\sqrt{\alpha}w(\mathcal{N}(t,\cdot),\mathcal{M}(t,\cdot))+|Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\alpha}\,X(t)+|Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)|

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{\left(F^{\prime}(\bar{Z})+\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\alpha})\right)\alpha(t-t_{0})},

and thanks to the definition of
w
(see (
9
)), for
t\geq t_{0}
,

\displaystyle W_{2}(\mathcal{N}(t,\cdot),\mathcal{M}(t,\cdot))

\displaystyle\leq w(\mathcal{N}(t,\cdot),\mathcal{M}(t,\cdot))+|Z_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}(t)-Z_{m}\circ\varphi_{m}(t)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{\alpha}}e^{\left(F^{\prime}(\bar{Z})+\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\alpha})\right)\alpha(t-t_{0})},

and thus, for some constant
C>0
,

W_{2}(\mathcal{N}(t,\cdot),\mathcal{M}(t,\cdot))\leq Ce^{\left(F^{\prime}(\bar{Z})+C\sqrt{\alpha}\right)\alpha t}.

If we apply this estimate with
m(t,\cdot)\equiv\bar{n}
, where
\bar{n}
is the steady-state provided by Lemma
2.2
, then (
63
) implies (
4
). The first inequality in (
5
) is provided by (
21
), while the second is implied by (
21
) and (
24
). We have then completed the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Appendix A
Appendix: Estimates on macroscopic quantities

We derive here technical estimates on
I_{n}-I_{m}
and the centres of mass of
Z_{n}
and
Z_{m}
.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
a\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R})
compactly supported, and
\kappa>0
. There exists
C>0
such that if
m,n\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
satisfy
I_{m},\,I_{n}\geq\kappa>0
(where
I_{m}
,
I_{n}
are defined from
m
,
n
thanks to (
12
)), then for
\alpha\in(0,1)
,

(74)

|I_{n}-I_{m}|\leq C\left(|Z_{n}-Z_{m}|+w(n,m)\right),

(75)

\big|(\tilde{Z}_{n}-\tilde{Z}_{m})-(Z_{n}-Z_{m})\big|\leq C\alpha\left(|Z_{n}-Z_{m}|+w(n,m)\right),

with
Z_{n},\,Z_{m}
defined from
m
,
n
by (
10
) and
\tilde{Z}_{n},\,\tilde{Z}_{m}
are

(76)

\tilde{Z}_{n}:=\int y\left(1-\alpha+\alpha\frac{a(y)}{I_{n}}\right)n(y)\,dy,\quad\tilde{Z}_{m}:=\int y\left(1-\alpha+\alpha\frac{a(y)}{I_{m}}\right)m(y)\,dy.

Proof of Lemma
A.1
.

We estimate:

\displaystyle I_{n}-I_{m}

\displaystyle=\int a(y)n(y)\,dy-\int a(y)m(y)\,dy

\displaystyle=\int a(y-Z_{n})n(y-Z_{n})\,dy-\int a(y-Z_{m})n(y-Z_{n})\,dy

\displaystyle\quad+\int a(y-Z_{m})\left(n(y-Z_{n})-m(y-Z_{m})\right)\,dy

\displaystyle\leq\|a^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\mathcal{O}(|Z_{n}-Z_{m}|)+\|a^{\prime}\|_{\infty}W_{2}(n(\cdot-Z_{n}),m(\cdot-Z_{m}))

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}(|Z_{n}-Z_{m}|)+\mathcal{O}(1)w(n,m),

where we have the property (
9
) satisfied by
w(\cdot,\cdot)
. To obtain the second inequality, we proceed as follows:

\displaystyle\big|(\tilde{Z}_{n}-\tilde{Z}_{m})-(Z_{n}-Z_{m})\big|=\left|\int y\left(\alpha-\alpha\frac{a(y)}{I_{m}}\right)m(y)\,dy-\int y\left(\alpha-\alpha\frac{a(y)}{I_{n}(t)}\right)n(y)\,dy\right|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\alpha\big|Z_{n}-Z_{m}\big|+\alpha\left|\int a(y)\left(\frac{n(y)}{I_{n}}-\frac{m(y)}{I_{m}}\right)\,dy\right|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\alpha\big|Z_{n}-Z_{m}\big|+\alpha\left|\int a(y-Z_{m})\left(\frac{n(y-Z_{n})}{I_{n}}-\frac{m(y-Z_{m})}{I_{m}}\right)\,dy\right|

\displaystyle\qquad+\alpha\left|\int a(y)\left(\frac{n(y)}{I_{n}}-\frac{n(y-Z_{n}+Z_{m})}{I_{n}}\right)\,dy\right|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\alpha\big|Z_{n}-Z_{m}\big|+\mathcal{O}(\alpha)w(n,m)+\mathcal{O}(\alpha)\left(\frac{1}{I_{n}}-\frac{1}{I_{m}}\right)+\alpha\left|\int\Big(a(y)-a\big(y+Z_{n}-Z_{m}\big)\Big)\frac{n(y)}{I_{n}}\,dy\right|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\mathcal{O}(\alpha)\Big(\big|Z_{n}-Z_{m}\big|+w(n,m)\Big),

which concludes the proof of the lemma.
