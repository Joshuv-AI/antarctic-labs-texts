Robust changepoint detection in the variability of multivariate functional data
Kelly Ramsay, Shoja’eddin Chenouri

Abstract
We consider the problem of robustly detecting changepoints in the variability of a sequence of independent multivariate functions. We develop a novel changepoint procedure, called the functional Kruskal–Wallis for covariance (FKWC) changepoint procedure, based on rank statistics and multivariate functional data depth. The FKWC changepoint procedure allows the user to test for at most one changepoint (AMOC) or an epidemic period, or to estimate the number and locations of an unknown amount of changepoints in the data. We show that when the “signal-to-noise” ratio is bounded below, the changepoint estimates produced by the FKWC procedure attain the minimax localization rate for detecting general changes in distribution in the univariate setting (Theorem 3.1 ). We also provide the behavior of the proposed test statistics for the AMOC and epidemic setting under the null hypothesis (Theorem 3.2 ) and, as a simple consequence of our main result, these tests are consistent (Corollary 3.1 ). In simulation, we show that our method is particularly robust when compared to similar changepoint methods. We present an application of the FKWC procedure to intraday asset returns and f-MRI scans. As a by-product of Theorem 3.1 , we provide a concentration result for integrated functional depth functions (Lemma B.2 ), which may be of general interest.



Keywords—
Depth function, Multiple changepoint, Covariance operator, Rank statistics

Introduction

Motivated by
Stoehr et al., 2019
, who expressed the need for a robust method for detecting changepoints in the covariance operator of f-MRI data, we study the problem of robustly and nonparametrically detecting changepoints in the variability of a sequence of multivariate functional data.
Detecting the presence and location of changepoints in the covariance operator of a sequence of observed functions has received some recent interest in the statistics literature, see, e.g.,
(
Jarušková, 2013
;
Sharipov and Wendler, 2020
;
Jiao et al., 2020
;
Dette and Kokot, 2020
;
Harris et al., 2021
)
.
However, most previous works have not considered the robustness of their procedure.
For example, many previous works require fourth moment assumptions
(
Stoehr et al., 2019
;
Dette and Kokot, 2020
;
Sharipov and Wendler, 2020
)
, are based on CUSUM statistics and/or long-run covariance estimators which are not robust
(
Dette and Kokot, 2020
;
Sharipov and Wendler, 2020
;
Jiao et al., 2020
)
and/or rely on bootstrapping or other data-driven methods to estimate the null distribution of the test statistic, which are vulnerable to corruption
(
Sharipov and Wendler, 2020
;
Dette and Kokot, 2020
;
Jiao et al., 2020
)
.
Recently,
Harris et al., 2021
began the study of robust changepoint detection in the mean and/or covariance operator of an observed sequence of functions.
They used simulation to test the performance of a fused Lasso and a CUSUM statistic for robustly detecting multiple changepoints in the mean and/or covariance operator of an observed sequence of functions.

We continue the study of robust changepoint detection in multivariate functional data by providing a comprehensive changepoint detection procedure which detects changes in the variability of such data.
In particular, we leverage the recent results of
Ramsay and Chenouri, 2023a
, who show that a large class of differences in the covariance kernel between samples imply differences in the median of multivariate functional data depth values between samples.
We call this class of differences “differences in variability”.
Motivated by the results of
Ramsay and Chenouri, 2023a
, we study depth-based ranking methods in the setting of changepoint detection in variability.

Our contributions are as follows: We introduce a nonparametric, robust procedure for detecting changes in the variability of multivariate functional data. We may call this procedure the Functional Kruskal–Wallis Covariance operator changepoint procedure, following the naming convention of
Ramsay and Chenouri, 2023a
. The FKWC changepoint procedure includes a hypothesis test for the presence of at most one changepoint (AMOC), a hypothesis test for the presence of an “epidemic period” and an algorithm to estimate the locations of multiple changepoints when the number of changepoints is not known.

Our main result says that when the “signal-to-noise” ratio is bounded below, the estimated changepoints resulting from the FKWC procedure attain the minimax localization rate for detecting general changes in distribution in the univariate setting
(
Padilla et al., 2021
)
(Theorem
3.1
).
We also derive the asymptotic distribution of the proposed test statistics under the null hypothesis for the AMOC and epidemic setting (Theorem
3.2
) and, as a simple consequence of our main result, these tests are consistent (Corollary
3.1
).
As a by-product of Theorem
3.1
, we prove a concentration result for integrated functional depth functions (Lemma
B.2
), which may be of general interest.
In addition, another by-product of Theorem
3.1
is an improved rate of convergence for the multivariate changepoint estimator given by
Ramsay and Chenouri, 2023b
, see Remark
3.3
.
A simulation study and a real data analysis are also included, which highlight the robustness of the proposed procedure (see Section
4
and Section
5
).

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
Section

Section

Section

Methodology

2.1
Preliminary assumptions

We suppose that we have observed a sequence of mutually independent multivariate random functions
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
defined on some probability space
(\Omega^{\prime},\mathscr{A}^{\prime},P)
.
For each
\omega\in\Omega^{\prime}
, each
X_{i}\coloneqq X_{i,\omega}
is a map from
[0,1]^{d}
to
\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
We assume that for some integers
k_{0}=0<k_{1}<\dots<k_{\ell}<k_{\ell+1}=n
, it holds that
X_{k_{i-1}+1},\dots,X_{k_{i}}
have a common law
\nu^{i}
.
For
m\in\mathbb{N}
, let
[m]=\{1,\ldots,m\}
.
It is assumed that for all
i\in[\ell]
, we have that
\nu^{i}\neq\nu^{i+1}
. Furthermore, we assume that
\nu^{i}\neq\nu^{j}
in such a way so that the shape and/or magnitude of functions drawn from
\nu^{i}
differ from that of
\nu^{j}
, on average.
We call this a change in variability.

Formally, our procedure can detect changes in the sequence of (population) functional depth values, to be defined in Section
2.2
.

Define the covariance kernel and covariance operator of
\nu^{i}
, respectively, as follows

\mathcal{K}_{i}(s,t)={\rm{E}}\left[X_{k_{i}}(s)X_{k_{i}}^{\top}(t)\right]-{\rm{E}}\left[X_{k_{i}}(s)\right]{\rm{E}}\left[X_{k_{i}}(t)\right]^{\top}\hskip 18.49988pt\text{and}\hskip 18.49988pt(\mathscr{K}_{i}f)(t):=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f(s)\mathcal{K}_{i}(s,t)ds.

Functional depth values are designed to differentiate between functions with differing shape and/or magnitude
(
Sun and Genton, 2011
;
Dai et al., 2020
;
Ramsay and Chenouri, 2023a
)
, and so it is natural to use them in this setting.
We will consider two cases, the first of which is the case when
\ell
is known. Here, the goal is to test if there are changepoints in the sequence of data.
If the test is significant, the goal is then to estimate each
k_{i}
, the locations of the changepoints.
The second case is where
\ell
is unknown, and the goal is to estimate each
k_{i}
and
\ell
, both the locations and number of changepoints.

It is necessary to clarify what is meant by the “function” in the preceding paragraph.
We assume that the sequence of observations satisfies the following condition.

Condition 1
.

For
d,p\in\mathbb{N}
and all
i,j\in[n]
with
i\neq j
it holds that

•

{\rm{E}}\left[X_{i}\right]=0
and
X_{i}
is independent of
X_{j}
,

•

X_{i}
is a continuous function,

•

X_{i}
is a mean square continuous stochastic process,

•

each component of
X_{i}
is differentiable on
(0,1)^{d}
and each of its partial derivatives are mean square continuous stochastic processes and continuous functions.

Let
L^{2}\coloneqq L^{2}[0,1]^{d}
be the space of square integrable functions over
[0,1]^{d}
and let
(L^{2})^{p}
denote the
pth
Cartesian product of
L^{2}
with itself.
Condition
1
implies that
X_{i}\in(L^{2})^{p}
.
In addition, Condition
1
ensures that we can view the observed functions and their derivatives as both stochastic processes over
[0,1]^{d}
and random elements drawn from some probability measure over
(L^{2})^{p}
, see
(
Hsing and Eubank, 2015
)
for more details on representations of functional observations.
When
p>1
, this constitutes the case known as multivariate functional data and when
p=1
this constitutes the case known as functional data.
Note that the assumption
{\rm{E}}\left[X_{i}\right]=0
is not particularly restrictive.
When the observed functions do not have zero mean, they can be centered by a robust measure of location to satisfy the requirement.
In addition, if one suspects changepoints in the mean of the sequence, a robust changepoint algorithm for detecting such changepoints can be run first.
One can then center the data within each segment.

2.2
Multivariate functional depth functions

We now cover the essential background on multivariate functional depth functions, on which our proposed methodology relies.
Denote the empirical measure of the observed functions by
\hat{\nu}
and, given a space,
\mathscr{S},
let
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathscr{S})
denote the set of probability measures on
\mathscr{S}
.
As the name suggests, a multivariate functional depth function measures the “depth” of an observed function with respect to a given probability measure on
(L^{2})^{p}
, i.e.,
\ID\colon(L^{2})^{p}\times\mathcal{M}_{1}((L^{2})^{p})\rightarrow[0,1]
.
Therefore, the sample depth value of an observed function
X_{i}
, i.e.,
\ID(X_{i},\hat{\nu})
, describes the centrality of
X_{i}
with respect to
\hat{\nu}
.
An observation will have high depth (with respect to the sample
\hat{\nu}
) when it is nested inside the sample and is similar in shape to the sample.
This fact makes depth a natural choice when detecting changes in variability.

There are many definitions of multivariate functional depth.
We focus on a general class of integrated depths, or integrated dual depths
(
Cuevas and Fraiman, 2009
)
, which we define below.
Let
(\Omega,\mathscr{A},Q)
be a probability space, where
(\Omega,\mathscr{A})
is a separable measure space.
Now, let
g\colon(L^{2})^{p}\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a measurable function.
Next, for
u\in\Omega
, define
\nu_{u}
to be the law of
g(X,u)
where
X\sim\nu
.
Lastly, for a given multivariate depth function
{\rm D}\colon\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{p})\to[0,1]
, (which is assumed to be a Borel function) define the integrated multivariate functional depth as

\ID(x,\nu)=\int_{\Omega}{\rm D}(g(x,u),\nu_{u})dQ(u).

(2.1)

Typically in practice, a finite number, say
M
, values from
\Omega
are selected (either deterministically or randomly), and we use the following approximation

\ID(x,\hat{\nu})\approx\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}{\rm D}(g(x,u_{i}),\hat{\nu}_{u_{i}}).

Two popular notions of multivariate functional depth can be recovered from the above definition.
Namely, the multivariate functional halfspace depth
(
Fraiman and Muniz, 2001
;
Slaets, 2011
;
Claeskens et al., 2014
)
and the random projection depth
(
Cuevas et al., 2007
)
.
The multivariate functional halfspace depth, denoted
\MFHD
, can be recovered by taking
\Omega=[0,1]^{d}
,
g(x,u)=x(u)
,
Q
equal to the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]^{d}
and
{\rm D}=\HD
, the multivariate halfspace depth.
Let
S
be the unit sphere in
L^{2}
and, for
x,y\in L^{2}
, let
\langle x,y\rangle=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}x(t)y(s)dtds
.
The random projection depth, denoted
\RP
, can be recovered by taking
\Omega=S
,
Q\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(S)
,
g(x,u)=(\langle x_{1},u\rangle,\ldots,\langle x_{p},u\rangle)
and
{\rm D}
to be any multivariate depth.
Explicit definitions for these instances of the integrated depth can be seen in Appendix
A.1
.
Our theoretical results, see Section
3
, cover the general class of integrated depths given by (
2.1
), provided there are no issues with measurability.

2.3
Changepoint methodology

The backbone of our changepoint methodology is a form of
sample depth-based ranks
, which incorporate the derivatives of the observed functions.
For a given function,
x\in(L^{2})^{p}
, let
x_{k}
be the
k
th component of
x
and suppose that
x_{k}
is differentiable on
(0,1)^{d}
for all
k\in[p]
.
Denote the vector of partial derivatives of each component
k
of
x
by
\nabla x_{k}
and let
h(x)=(x,\nabla x_{1},\ldots,\nabla x_{k})
.
That is
h(x)
is the
p(d+1)
-dimensional vector of
x
and each of its
p
-components’ partial derivatives.

Next, let
h\circ\hat{\nu}
be the empirical measure of
h(X_{1}),\ldots,h(X_{n})
.
For a given integrated depth function, we will consider the depth values
\ID(h(X_{i}),h\circ\hat{\nu})
.
Now, given that depth values are univariate, one can rank the observed functions based on their depth values.
These are defined as the
sample depth-based ranks
, viz. for
i\in[n]
, we have that

\widehat{R}_{i}=\#\left\{X_{j}\colon\ID(h(X_{j}),h\circ\hat{\nu})\leq\ID(h(X_{i}),h\circ\hat{\nu}),\ j\in[n]\right\}\ .

It is shown in
Ramsay and Chenouri, 2023a
that when
p=1
, the median of
\ID(X_{i},\hat{\nu})
is influenced by the covariance kernel of an observation
X_{i}
, which, along with the fact that conceptually, a function’s depth is meant to differentiate between functions of different magnitude and shape
(
Sun and Genton, 2011
;
Dai et al., 2020
)
, is the motivation for the use of depth in our methodology.

The motivation for incorporating the derivative information stems from
Hubert et al., 2012
, who showed that one can improve inference procedures for functional data by incorporating the derivatives of the observed functions into the depth values.
This was employed also by
Ramsay and Chenouri, 2023a
, which improved the performance of their hypothesis testing procedure.
After computing the sample depth-based ranks
\widehat{R}_{1},\ldots\widehat{R}_{n}
, the next phase of our procedure is to look for changes in the mean of these ranks.

In order to detect changes in these ranks, we employ the
classical Kruskal–Wallis test statistic.
Consider a candidate set of changepoints
\mathbf{r}=\{r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}\}
, which we will always assume to be ordered by their indices, i.e.,
r_{0}=0<r_{1}<\ldots<r_{m}<r_{m+1}=n
.
Then the Kruskal–Wallis test statistic, where the “treatment groups” are the candidate segments
\{\widehat{R}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{R}_{r_{1}}\}
,
\{\widehat{R}_{r_{1}+1},\ldots,\widehat{R}_{r_{2}}\}
, etc., is:

\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{12}{n(n+1)}\sum_{j=1}^{m+1}(r_{j}-r_{j-1})\overline{\widehat{R}}_{j}^{2}-3(n+1),\hskip 18.49988pt\text{where}\hskip 18.49988pt\overline{\widehat{R}}_{j}=\frac{1}{r_{j}-r_{j-1}}\sum_{i=r_{j-1}+1}^{r_{j}}\widehat{R}_{i}.

(2.2)

Recall that in the Kruskal–Wallis ANOVA procedure, a large test statistic signals that there are differences between the groups.
It follows that, in this setting, the bigger the differences in variability between segments, the bigger (
2.2
) will be.
Therefore, maximizing a version of (
2.2
) over candidate changepoint sets
\mathbf{r}
should give a set of time intervals in which the functions differ in variability.

If the number of true changepoints
\ell
is known, we can directly maximize
\mathcal{W}
, which results in the estimate

\hat{\mathbf{k}}_{\ell}=\argmax_{1\leq r_{1}<\ldots<r_{\ell}<n}\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{r}).

(2.3)

When
\ell
is known, it is often of interest to perform a hypothesis test for significance of the changepoints.
In that case, we propose
\mathcal{W}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}_{\ell})=\sup_{1\leq r_{1}<\ldots<r_{\ell}<n}\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{r})
as the test statistic, whose asymptotic distribution under the null hypothesis is a simple transformation of a standard Brownian bridge, see Theorem
3.2
below.

If the number of true changepoints
\ell
is unknown, then maximizing the objective function
\mathcal{W}
over all possible candidate sets of changepoints is a degenerate problem.
Therefore, we must add a penalty term,
\kappa_{n}>0
, on the number of changepoints, that is,

\hat{\mathbf{k}}=\argmax_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<r_{1}<\ldots<r_{\ell}<n\end{subarray}}[\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{r})-\ell\kappa_{n}].

(2.4)

We call the estimates
\hat{\mathbf{k}}
and
\hat{\mathbf{k}}_{\ell}
the functional Kruskal–Wallis for covariance (FKWC) changepoint estimators.
The estimate
\hat{\mathbf{k}}
can be computed with the PELT algorithm
(
Killick et al., 2012
)
, which, given the sample depth-based ranks, allows the changepoint estimates to be computed in linear time.
The running time of our method then depends on our choice of
g
and
{\rm D}
in (
2.1
).
For instance, if we let
N
represent the number of points in the grid on which the observed functions are discretized when computing the depth values, and we take
{\rm D}
to be the integrated dual depth or integrated-rank-weighted depth
(
Cuevas et al., 2007
;
Ramsay et al., 2019
)
, then computing the set of random projection depth-based ranks takes
O(MN^{2}+M^{2}npd+n\log n)
time.
On the other hand, setting
{\rm D}
to be the halfspace or simplicial depth exponential time in
d\times p
.
Practically, it seems as though the majority of the computational burden comes in the form of computing the sample depth values.
For example, 1 million univariate observations can be ranked in
R
(Version 4.4.0) in 0.37 seconds.

using an Intel(R) Core(TM) i7-8700K CPU @ 3.70GHz microchip and 32 Gb of RAM.

By contrast, computing the
\RP
depth values of one million observations would take considerably longer with existing implementations in

(Version 4.4.0).

3
Theoretical results

In this section, we present the theoretical results for the FKWC changepoint procedure.
We first consider the setting where the number of changepoints is unknown, from which the setting where the number of changepoints is known is a simple consequence.

3.1
The number of changepoints is unknown

Before presenting our main result, we first introduce a regularity condition on the multivariate depth function

Remark 3.1
.

The type of change captured by the procedure is entirely encapsulated in the following condition:
If there exists a changepoint at time
k_{i}
, then
\Pr\left(\ID(h(X_{k_{i}}),h\circ\nu)\leq\ID(h(X_{k_{i}+1}),h\circ\nu)\right)\neq 1/2
.
Therefore, we must have that the change in the distribution at
k_{i}+1
must imply a location change in the depth value sequence.
Definitionally, depth functions distinguish between functions of differing magnitudes and/or shapes
(
Sun and Genton, 2011
;
Dai et al., 2020
)
, so we say this is equivalent to a change in variability.
In addition, if
\ID
is taken to be the random projection depth, a fairly large class of changes in the covariance kernel imply changes in the mean of the sequence
\ID(X_{i},\nu)
for a large class of distributions
\nu

(
Ramsay and Chenouri, 2023a
)
.
We expect such changes to also change the sequence
\ID(h(X_{i}),h\circ\nu)
, which is confirmed by our simulation study (see Section
4
) and the simulation study of
Ramsay and Chenouri, 2023a
.

Remark 3.2
.

Condition
1
implies that we have access to the observed functions
X_{i}
in full. In reality, we often only have access to
X_{i}
, observed noisily at a finite number of points, say
m_{i}
.
These discretized functions are then smoothed prior to analysis.
Convergence rates of the integrated depths (and other depths) computed on noisy discretized functions were characterized by
Nagy and Ferraty, 2019
.
Essentially, under some broad, technical assumptions on the curves, in the setting of
m_{i}>>n
, which we can describe as “dense” functional data, where the uniform convergence rate of an integrated depth function to the population depth function is not degraded
(
Nagy and Ferraty, 2019
, see Theorem 6 and Section 5)
.
Therefore, if the curves are densely observed and are sufficiently smooth, we need not be concerned about this point.
On the other hand, for sparse functional data, we expect the rate of convergence of the FKWC changepoint estimates to be slower than that as given in Theorem
3.1
.

Remark 3.3
.

The algorithm described is similar to that of Algorithm 2 of
Ramsay and Chenouri, 2023a
for multivariate data. It is natural to ask if the theoretical analysis of
Ramsay and Chenouri, 2023a
applies here.
However, the rate given in Theorem 2 of
Ramsay and Chenouri, 2023a
is slower than that given in Theorem
3.1
.
Therefore, applying their analysis would produce a weaker result.
Furthermore, Theorem
3.1
offers an improvement over Theorem 2 of
Ramsay and Chenouri, 2023a
.
It is straightforward to show that the result of Theorem
3.1
extends to the multivariate case, which means Theorem
3.1
implies a faster rate of convergence for the multivariate changepoint estimator of
Ramsay and Chenouri, 2023a
.

3.2
The number of changepoints is known

We now consider the setting where the number of changepoints is known.
The fact that the hypothesis tests and changepoint estimates
\hat{\mathbf{k}}_{\ell}
are consistent follows from the analysis of Theorem
3.1
.
Let
B(t)
be a standard Brownian bridge.

Corollary 3.1
.

If Conditions

–
2

c_{1},c_{2}>0
and there exists an integer

\Pr\left(\max_{\hat{k}_{j}\in\hat{\mathbf{k}}_{\ell}}\min_{k_{i}\in\mathbf{k}}|k_{i}-\hat{k}_{j}|\leq c_{1}\frac{\lambda_{n,\mathscr{F},K}}{\tau_{n}^{2}}\right)\geq 1-c_{2}/n.

4.1
Simulation setup

In order to test the empirical performance of our methodology and compare to existing methods, we simulated observations from several changepoint models.
For brevity, we restricted the simulation to the case where
d=p=1
.
In each simulation run,
\nu^{1},\ldots,\nu^{\ell+1}
were such that all observed functions were either Gaussian processes, denoted
\mathcal{G}
, Student
t
processes with degrees of freedom equal to three, denoted
t_{3}
, or Skewed Gaussian processes denoted
\mathcal{SG}
.
Each
\nu^{i}
had a squared exponential covariance kernel

\mathcal{K}_{i}(s,t;\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})=\beta_{i}\ e^{\frac{-(s-t)^{2}}{2\alpha_{i}^{2}}}.

At each changepoint, either
\alpha_{i}\neq\alpha_{i+1}
(shape change) or
\beta_{i}\neq\beta_{i+1}
(scale change).
Changes in
\alpha
correspond to a ‘shape’ change in the data, while changes in
\beta
correspond to a scale, i.e. a magnitude, change in the data.
We considered the following scenarios:

•

AMOC: We simulated data with zero changepoints as well as with one changepoint in the middle of the sample. Sample sizes of 100, 200, and 500 were used.

•

Epidemic: We simulated data with two uniformly random changepoints, where we required that the changepoints were at least 10% of the sample size apart. Sample sizes of 100, 200, and 500 were used.

•

Multiple: We simulated data with five randomly placed changepoints which also had to be at least 10% of the sample size apart. Sample sizes of of 200, 500, 1000 and 2500 were used. We ran four different simulation scenarios, two where the changepoints were shape-type and two where the changes were magnitude-type.
Within these groups, the set of changepoints was either “ascending”, i.e.,
\alpha
or
\beta
was increasing with each change or “alternating”, i.e.,
\alpha
or
\beta
was oscillating between a high and low value with each change.

We ran all of the above cases once where the changepoints were shape changes and once where they were scale changes.

In terms of comparing the FKWC procedure under the two depths
\RP
and
\MFHD
, the performance of each was very similar, with
\MFHD
being slightly better for magnitude differences and
\RP
being slightly better for shape differences.
Therefore, we only present the results with
\ID=\RP
.

To evaluate the accuracy of the algorithms, we also look at the energy distance between the estimated changepoint set and true changepoint set for each method.
The energy distance between the estimated and the true changepoint set can be written as

\frac{2}{\hat{\ell}\ell}\sum_{i=1}^{\hat{\ell}}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}|\hat{k}_{i}-k_{j}|-\frac{1}{\hat{\ell}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{\hat{\ell}}\sum_{j=1}^{\hat{\ell}}|\hat{k}_{i}-\hat{k}_{j}|-\frac{1}{\ell^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}|k_{i}-k_{j}|.

We use this distance because, as discussed in Appendix B.3 of
Harris et al., 2021

One criticism is that if the algorithm fails to identify any changepoints, then the energy distance to a set of true changepoints will not be defined.

The numbers along the top of the graph indicate the proportion of simulation runs in which the algorithm failed to identify any changepoints.

However, the FMCI method can perform poorly in the heavy-tailed scenario.
For instance, for heavy-tailed processes with scale changes, the FMCI failed to detect a changepoint in the majority of the simulation runs.
We conclude that the FMCI method performs better when the data are not heavy-tailed, but the FKWC performs better when the data are heavy-tailed.
In other words, the FKWC procedure sacrifices some of the accuracy of the FMCI method for robustness.

5.2
Resting state f-MRI pre-processing

Functional magnetic resonance imaging, or f-MRI, is a type of imaging for brain activity.
f-MRI uses magnetic fields to determine oxygen levels of blood in the brain in order to produce 3-dimensional images of the brain.
Many of these images are taken over a period of time, which results in a time series of 3-dimensional images.
Note that each MRI in a given subject’s f-MRI can be viewed as a function on
[0,1]^{3}
.
Resting state f-MRI is a type of f-MRI data where no intervention is applied to the subject during the scanning process.
f-MRI scans go through extensive pre-processing before being analysed.

One assumption commonly made is that, after several pre-processing steps, each subject’s resulting functional time series is stationary.
It is therefore important to check the scans at an individual level in order to ensure that each time series is stationary.
For subjects whose time series is not stationary, we must make the necessary corrections or exclusions from the ensuing data analysis.
The covariance kernel of an f-MRI time series is a 6-dimensional function.
Existing methods make a separability assumption on the covariance kernel
(
Stoehr et al., 2019
)
, which we do not make here.
Additionally,
Stoehr et al., 2019
mentions the need for a robust method of detecting non-stationarities in f-MRI data, which leads us to apply the FKWC procedure to this data.
We analyse several scans from the Beijing dataset, which were retrieved from
www.nitrc.org
.
These scans were also analysed by
Stoehr et al., 2019
.
Following instruction provided by
https://johnmuschelli.com/
, we performed the following pre-processing steps to the data.
We trimmed the first 10 seconds from the beginning of the scan, in order to have a stable signal.
We then performed rigid motion correction using
antsMotionCalculation
function in the
ANTsR R
package.
A 0.1 Hz high-pass Butterworth filter of order 2 was applied voxel-wise to remove drift and trend from the data.
We then removed 15 observations from either end of the time series in order to remove the edge effects of the filter.
The gradient of each scan was then estimated using the
numDeriv
package, which resulted in four time series of functional data, where each function is a three-dimensional image.

We then computed the
\RP
sample depth values as follows.
First we projected each of the four time series’ onto 50 unit functions.
Then, for each of the 50 projected time series, we computed the halfspace depth values of each four-dimensional observation.
We then averaged these depths over the 50 unit vectors.
We use the half-space depth since it is faster to compute than the simplicial depth for four-dimensional data.
We then applied the FKWC hypothesis tests and the FKWC multiple changepoint algorithm to the resulting depth values.
In addition, we restricted estimated changepoints to be at least 10 observations away from either boundary.
Code to run the FKWC procedure on three-dimensional functional data can be retrieved from Github
(
Ramsay, 2021
)
.

Comments on multivariate functional depth functions

This section contains additional details on multivariate functional depth functions.

A.1
Explicit definitions of multivariate functional depth functions

We consider the random projection depth and the multivariate halfspace depth.
We first consider the random projection depth.
Here, define
\nu_{u}
to be the law of
(\langle X_{1},u\rangle,\ldots,\langle X_{p},u\rangle)
if
X\sim\nu
and let
S=\{u\in L^{2}\colon\ \left\lVert u\right\rVert=1\}
, where
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
is the
L^{2}
-norm with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]^{d}
.
As stated in Section
2.2
, the random projection depth takes
\Omega=S
,
{\rm D}
any multivariate depth function,
Q\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(S)
and
g(x,u)=(\langle x_{1},u\rangle,\ldots,\langle x_{p},u\rangle)
.
In simulation, we take
{\rm D}
to be the simplicial depth
(
Liu, 1988
)
.
The Simplicial depth is
(p+1,\mathscr{F})
-regular with
\operatorname{VC}(\mathscr{F})=p^{2}\log(p)

(
Ramsay et al., 2022
)
.
Let
\Delta(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d+1})
be the
d
-dimensional simplex with vertices
x_{1},\ldots,x_{d+1}
.
Suppose that
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{d+1}
are i.i.d. from some
\mu~\in~\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The simplicial depth of a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with respect to
\mu
is

\SD(x,\mu)=\Pr\left(x\in\Delta(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{d+1})\right).

The random projection depth
(
Cuevas et al., 2007
)
is defined as follows:

Definition A.1
.

Let
u_{1},\ldots,u_{M}\sim\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(S)
.
The random projection depth, with base probability measure
\mu
, of a
p
-dimensional vector of functions
x\in(L^{2})^{p}
with respect to
\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}((L^{2})^{p})
is

\RP(x,M,\nu,\mu)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\SD(\langle x,u_{m}\rangle,\nu_{u_{m}}).

The random projection depth of a point
x
is the average projected, multivariate depth of
x
with respect to some base probability measure
\mu
.
Here, we take
\mu
to be such that
u_{1},\ldots,u_{M}
are zero mean Gaussian processes with exponential variogram
\gamma(s,t)=\exp(-5|s-t|)
, standardized such that they have unit norm.

Define the multivariate halfspace depth of a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
with respect to
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as

\HD(x,\mu)=\inf_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\ \left\lVert u\right\rVert=1}\mu(X^{\top}u\leq x^{\top}u).

Let
\nu_{t}
be the law of
X(t)
if
X\sim\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}((L^{2})^{p})
.
The multivariate functional halfspace depth is defined as follows.

Definition A.2
.

The multivariate functional halfspace depth of a
p
-dimensional vector of continuous functions
x\in(L^{2})^{p}
with respect to
\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}((L^{2})^{p})
is

\MFHD(x,\nu)=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\HD(x(t),\nu_{t})dt.

The multivariate functional halfspace depth is the average over
[0,1]^{d}
of the multivariate depth of
x(t)
with respect to
\nu_{t}
.
Note that the multivariate functional halfspace depth was introduced in a more general form
(
Slaets, 2011
;
Hubert et al., 2012
;
Claeskens et al., 2014
)
.
Furthermore, when
p=1
, this depth function is also equivalent to the depth of
Fraiman and Muniz, 2001
.
As in the case of the random projection depth, one can replace the halfspace depth in Definition
A.2
with another multivariate depth function, such as simplicial depth
(
Fraiman and Muniz, 2001
)
.

A.2
Connections between depth and covariance

We provide a brief summary of the connection between the distribution of
\ID(X_{k_{i}+1},\nu)-\ID(X_{k_{i}},\nu)
and the covariance operators
\mathscr{K}_{i}
,
\mathscr{K}_{i+1}
.
To this end, we must first define the
symmetry weight function
.
Consider some univariate probability measure
P\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
which possesses the property
\int_{\mathbb{R}}Xd\mu=0
.
Suppose that
P
also possesses a twice differentiable cumulative distribution function
F_{P}
, by which we denote the first and second derivative of
F_{P}
by
f_{P}
and
f_{P}^{\prime}
, respectively.
The symmetry weight function of
P
is

\SW(P)=\left(\frac{1}{2}-F_{P}(0)\right)f_{P}^{\prime}(0)+f_{P}(0)^{2}.

The first term is a signed measure of skew, whereas the second term is large when there is high density at the mean.
For the
\RP
depth function, as defined in Appendix
A.1
, the connection can be summarized as follows:
Suppose that
\nu
is such that

{\rm{E}}\left[\ID(X_{k_{1}+1},\nu)-\ID(X_{k_{1}},\nu)\right]\neq 0\implies\med(\ID(X_{k_{1}+1},\nu)-\ID(X_{k_{1}},\nu))\neq 0.

(A.1)

Then if
\ID=\RP
,
\SW(\nu_{u})
is well-defined for all
u\in S
and
{\rm{E}}_{\nu}\left[\left\lVert X\right\rVert^{3}\right]<\infty,
a sufficient condition for the jump sizes to be non-zero, i.e.,
\Pr\left(\ID(X_{k_{1}},\nu)\leq\ID(X_{k_{1}+1},\nu)\right)\neq 1/2
, is

\int_{S}\SW(\nu_{u})\langle\mathscr{K}_{2}u,u\rangle dQ+\mathcal{R}_{1}\neq\int_{S}\SW(\nu_{u})\langle\mathscr{K}_{1}u,u\rangle dQ+\mathcal{R}_{2}.

Here
\mathcal{R}_{1},\mathcal{R}_{2}
are remainder terms which are expected to be small see
(
Ramsay and Chenouri, 2023a
)
.
Then, if
\ID=\MFHD
and (
A.1
) holds, a sufficient condition for
\Pr\left(\ID(X_{k_{1}},\nu)\leq\ID(X_{k_{1}+1},\nu)\right)\neq 1/2
to hold is

\int_{[0,1]^{d}}{\rm{E}}|1/2-F_{\nu_{t}}(X_{k_{1}}(t))|dt\neq\int_{[0,1]^{d}}{\rm{E}}|1/2-F_{\nu_{t}}(X_{k_{1}+1}(t))|dt.

Now, assuming
F_{\nu_{t}}
is twice differentiable and the following notion of symmetry
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{d}}}{{=}}-X
for
X\sim\nu
.
Taking a first order Taylor expansion of
F_{\nu_{t}}
yields

\int_{[0,1]^{d}}{\rm{E}}|f_{\mu_{t}}(0)X_{k_{1}}(t)+\mathcal{R}_{3}(t)|dt\neq\int_{[0,1]^{d}}{\rm{E}}|f_{\mu_{t}}(0)X_{k_{1}+1}(t)+\mathcal{R}_{4}(t)|dt,

(A.2)

where
\mathcal{R}_{3},\mathcal{R}_{4}
are the remainder terms resulting from the second order Taylor expansion of
F_{\nu_{t}}
.
Notice that the quantity (
A.2
) is roughly a functional data analogue of the median absolute deviation.

We first prove a bound on the expectation of

Lemma B.1
.

If Conditions

2

\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}

{\rm E}_{U}

Then, we have that by Jensen’s inequality

\displaystyle{\rm E}\sup_{x\in L^{2}[0,1]}|\ID(x,\hat{\nu})-\ID(x,\nu)|

\displaystyle={\rm E}\sup_{x\in L^{2}[0,1]}\left|\int_{\Omega}{\rm D}(g(x,u),\hat{\nu}_{u})dQ(u)-\int_{\Omega}{\rm D}(g(x,u),\nu_{u})dQ(u)\right|

\displaystyle\leq{\rm E}\int_{\Omega}\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}|{\rm D}(y,\hat{\nu}_{u})-{\rm D}(y,\nu_{u})|dQ(u)

Now, using Condition
2

{\rm E}\int_{\Omega}\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}|{\rm D}(y,\hat{\nu}_{u})-{\rm D}(y,\nu_{u})|dQ(u)\leq{\rm E}_{\mathbf{X}}{\rm E}_{U}Kd_{\mathscr{F}}(\hat{\nu}_{U},\nu_{U})=K\cdot{\rm E}_{U}{\rm E}_{\mathbf{X}}(d_{\mathscr{F}}(\hat{\nu}_{U},\nu_{U})).

(B.1)

Lastly, applying equation (85) of
(

\displaystyle K\cdot{\rm E}_{U}{\rm E}_{\mathbf{X}}(d_{\mathscr{F}}(\hat{\nu}_{U},\nu_{U}))

\displaystyle\lesssim\ell K\cdot{\rm E}_{U}\sqrt{\operatorname{VC}(\mathscr{F})/n}=\ell K\sqrt{\operatorname{VC}(\mathscr{F})/n}.\qed

We now prove a concentration inequality for the integrated depths.

Lemma B.2
.

If Conditions
1
–
2
then, there exists universal constant
c,c^{\prime}>0

\Pr\left(\sup_{x\in(L^{2})^{p}}|\ID(x,\hat{\nu})-\ID(x,\nu)|\geq t+cK\ell\sqrt{\operatorname{VC}(\mathscr{F})/n}\right)\leq 2\exp\left(c^{\prime}\frac{-nt^{2}}{K\ell\sqrt{\operatorname{VC}(\mathscr{F})/n}+1}\right).

Proof.

Directly from (
B.1
), we have that

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in(L^{2})^{p}}|\ID(x,\hat{\nu})-\ID(x,\nu)|

\displaystyle\leq K\cdot{\rm E}_{U}(d_{\mathscr{F}}(\hat{\nu}_{U},\nu_{U}))=K\cdot{\rm E}_{U}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{G}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(X_{i},U)\right|,

where

\mathcal{G}=\left\{h\colon h(x,u)=f(g(x,u))-\int f(g(x,u))d\nu_{u}(x),\ f\in\mathscr{F}\right\}.

Next, for all
s>0
, we have that

\displaystyle\Pr\left({\rm E}_{U}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{G}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(X_{i},U)\right|\geq s\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\left({\rm E}_{U}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{G}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(X_{i},U)\geq s\right)+\Pr\left({\rm E}_{U}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{G}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}-f(X_{i},U)\geq s\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\left(\sup_{f\in\mathcal{G}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\rm E}_{U}f(X_{i},U)\geq s\right)+\Pr\left(\sup_{f\in\mathcal{G}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}-{\rm E}_{U}f(X_{i},U)\geq s\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\Pr\left(\sup_{f\in\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(X_{i})\geq s\right),

where

\mathcal{G}^{\prime}=\left\{\pm h\colon\ \ h(x,u)=\int_{\Omega}\left[f(g(x,u))-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}f(g(x,u))d\nu_{u}\right]dQ,\ f\in\mathscr{F}\right\}.

Now, observe the following three facts:

1.

For any

\int_{(L^{2})^{p}}f(x)d\nu=0
.

2.

A direct application of Lemma

gives that
{\rm E}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(X_{i})\lesssim K\ell\sqrt{\operatorname{VC}(\mathscr{F})/n}

3.

By assumption,
\sup_{f\in\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1
.

Letting

Bousquet, 2003

This application yields that there exists a universal constant

\displaystyle\Pr\left(Z\geq t+{\rm E}Z\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\left(Z\geq t+cK\ell\sqrt{\operatorname{VC}(\mathscr{F})/n}\right)\leq\exp\left(\frac{-nt^{2}}{4cK\ell\sqrt{\operatorname{VC}(\mathscr{F})/n}+2+2t/3}\right).

Next, using the fact that

\displaystyle\Pr\left(Z\geq t+cK\ell\sqrt{\operatorname{VC}(\mathscr{F})/n}\right)\leq\exp\left(\frac{-nt^{2}}{4cK\ell\sqrt{\operatorname{VC}(\mathscr{F})/n}+5}\right)\leq\exp\left(c^{\prime}\frac{-nt^{2}}{K\ell\sqrt{\operatorname{VC}(\mathscr{F})/n}+1}\right)

\displaystyle=a_{n}b_{n,i^{*}}^{2}+a_{n}b_{n,i^{*}+1}^{2}-\frac{\Delta_{n}}{n^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\Delta_{n}}\sum_{j=k_{i^{*}}-a_{n}}^{k_{i^{*}}+a_{n}}\sum_{m=1}^{\ell+1}n_{m}(p_{j^{\prime}m}-1/2)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=a_{n}b_{n,i^{*}}^{2}/2+a_{n}b_{n,i^{*}+1}^{2}/2-a_{n}b_{n,i^{*}}b_{n,i^{*}+1}

\displaystyle=a_{n}(b_{n,i^{*}}-b_{n,i^{*}+1})^{2}/2

\displaystyle\geq\Delta_{n}\tau_{n}^{2}/4.

We have now shown that there exists universal constants
c,c^{\prime}>0
such that
\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{k})-\mathcal{W}(\hat{\mathbf{k}})\gtrsim c^{\prime}\Delta_{n}\tau_{n}^{2}-c\ell\kappa_{n}.

Therefore, if
\kappa_{n}\leq c^{\prime}\Delta_{n}\tau_{n}^{2}/\ell
for all
n\geq 1
, then this yields a contradiction.

Next, assume that
\hat{\ell}>\ell
.
First, given
\hat{\mathbf{k}}\subset\hat{\mathbf{k}}\cup\mathbf{k}
, we have that
f(\hat{\mathbf{k}})\leq f(\hat{\mathbf{k}}\cup\mathbf{k})
.
Using this fact and the assumption that
\hat{\ell}>\ell
, on
A_{1}(C)^{c}\cap A_{2}(C)^{c}
, we have

\widehat{\mathcal{W}}(\mathbf{k})-\widehat{\mathcal{W}}(\hat{\mathbf{k}})\geq f(\mathbf{k})-f(\hat{\mathbf{k}})-2C\hat{\ell}\lambda_{n,\mathscr{F},K}+(\hat{\ell}-\ell)\kappa_{n}\geq f(\mathbf{k})-f(\hat{\mathbf{k}}\cup\mathbf{k})-2C\hat{\ell}\lambda_{n,\mathscr{F},K}+(\hat{\ell}-\ell)\kappa_{n}.

Next, similar to the analysis of
f(\mathbf{r}_{1})
and
f(\mathbf{r}_{2})
, we have that
f(\mathbf{k})=f(\hat{\mathbf{k}}\cup\mathbf{k})
.
Therefore, it holds that

\widehat{\mathcal{W}}(\mathbf{k})-\widehat{\mathcal{W}}(\hat{\mathbf{k}})\geq(\hat{\ell}-\ell)\kappa_{n}-2C\hat{\ell}\lambda_{n,\mathscr{F},K}\geq\hat{\ell}(\kappa_{n}-2C\lambda_{n,\mathscr{F},K})-\ell\kappa_{n}.

The assumed bounds on
\kappa_{n}
yield that
\hat{\ell}(\kappa_{n}-2C\lambda_{n,\mathscr{F},K})-\ell\kappa_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, giving a contradiction.

Finally, assume that for some
c>0
there exists
k_{i^{*}}\in\mathbf{k}
such that
\min_{k\in\mathbf{k}}|\hat{k}-k_{i^{*}}|>c\lambda_{n,\mathscr{F},K}/\tau_{n}^{2}
.
Define
\mathbf{r}^{\prime}_{1}
in the same way as
\mathbf{r}_{1}
but replace
\Delta_{n}
with
c\lambda_{n,\mathscr{F},K}/\tau_{n}^{2}
:

\mathbf{r}^{\prime}_{1}=\{k_{i^{*}}-c\lambda_{n,\mathscr{F},K}/2\tau_{n}^{2},k_{i^{*}}+c\lambda_{n,\mathscr{F},K}/2\tau_{n}^{2}\}\cup\mathbf{k}\backslash k_{i^{*}}\hskip 18.49988pt\text{and}\hskip 18.49988pt\mathbf{r}^{\prime}_{2}=\mathbf{r}^{\prime}_{1}\cup\hat{\mathbf{k}}.

Similar to the analysis of
\{\hat{\ell}<\ell\}
, we can write

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{k})-\mathcal{W}(\hat{\mathbf{k}})=f(\mathbf{k})-f(\hat{\mathbf{k}})-2C\ell\lambda_{n,\mathscr{F},K}\gtrsim f(\mathbf{k})-f(\mathbf{r}_{1}^{\prime})-2C\ell\lambda_{n,\mathscr{F},K}\geq c\lambda_{n,\mathscr{F},K}/4-2C\ell\lambda_{n,\mathscr{F},K}.

Therefore, taking
c
large enough yields that
c\lambda_{n,\mathscr{F},K}/4-2C\ell\lambda_{n,\mathscr{F},K}>0
, which yields a contradiction.
∎

In addition, for any
1<k<n
, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n-k}\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}\widehat{R}_{i}-\frac{n+1}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n-k}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{R}_{i}-\frac{1}{n-k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widehat{R}_{i}-\frac{n+1}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{n-k}\frac{n+1}{2}-\frac{1}{n-k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widehat{R}_{i}-\frac{n+1}{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{n}{n-k}-1\right)\frac{n+1}{2}-\frac{k}{n-k}\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widehat{R}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{n-k}\left(\frac{n+1}{2}-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widehat{R}_{i}\right).

Now, let
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}(t)=\mathcal{W}(\{\lfloor tn\rfloor\})
.
Now, observe that for any
t\in(0,1)
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{\prime}(t)=\mathcal{W}(\{\lfloor tn\rfloor\})

\displaystyle=\sigma^{-2}_{n}\left[\lfloor tn\rfloor\left(\frac{1}{\lfloor tn\rfloor}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor tn\rfloor}\widehat{R}_{i}-\frac{n+1}{2}\right)^{2}+(n-\lfloor tn\rfloor)\left(\frac{1}{n-\lfloor tn\rfloor}\sum_{i=\lfloor tn\rfloor+1}^{n}\widehat{R}_{i}-\frac{n+1}{2}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sigma^{-2}_{n}\left[\lfloor tn\rfloor\left(\frac{1}{\lfloor tn\rfloor}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor tn\rfloor}\widehat{R}_{i}-\frac{n+1}{2}\right)^{2}+(n-\lfloor tn\rfloor)\left(\frac{\lfloor tn\rfloor}{n-\lfloor tn\rfloor}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{1}{\lfloor tn\rfloor}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor tn\rfloor}\widehat{R}_{i}-\frac{n+1}{2}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sigma^{-2}_{n}\left[\lfloor tn\rfloor+(n-\lfloor tn\rfloor)\left(\frac{\lfloor tn\rfloor}{n-\lfloor tn\rfloor}\right)^{2}\right]\left(\frac{1}{\lfloor tn\rfloor}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor tn\rfloor}\widehat{R}_{i}-\frac{n+1}{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{-2}_{n}\lfloor tn\rfloor\left[1+\frac{\lfloor tn\rfloor}{n-\lfloor tn\rfloor}\right]\left(\frac{1}{\lfloor tn\rfloor}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor tn\rfloor}\widehat{R}_{i}-\frac{n+1}{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{-2}_{n}\frac{\lfloor tn\rfloor}{n}\left[\frac{n}{n-\lfloor tn\rfloor}\right]\left(\sqrt{n}\left(\frac{1}{\lfloor tn\rfloor}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor tn\rfloor}\widehat{R}_{i}-\frac{n+1}{2}\right)\right)^{2}

It follows from continuous mapping theorem and Slutsky’s theorem that

\sup_{1<r\leq n}\mathcal{W}(r)=\sup_{0<t\leq 1}\mathcal{W}^{\prime}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{d}}}{{\rightarrow}}\sup_{0<t<1}t(1-t)|B(t)|^{2}.

We now prove the second claim in the theorem.
Note that

(\hat{k}_{1},\hat{k}_{2})=\argmax_{r_{1},r_{2}}\frac{12}{n(n+1)}\left(\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i<r_{1}\\
r_{2}\leq i\leq n\end{subarray}}^{n}\frac{\widehat{R}_{i}}{\sqrt{n-r_{2}+r_{1}}}\right)^{2}+\left(\sum_{i=r_{1}}^{r_{2}-1}\frac{\widehat{R}_{i}}{\sqrt{r_{2}-r_{1}}}\right)^{2}\right)-3(n+1).

(B.8)

Let
q_{n}=\frac{(n^{2}-1)}{n(n+1)}
, clearly
q_{n}\rightarrow 1
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.
We write
\mathcal{W}
as a function of the partial sums of similar form of that of
\widehat{Z}_{n}(t)
.
Consider the first term in (
B.8
). We have that

\displaystyle\left(\frac{12}{n(n+1)}\right)^{1/2}\sum_{1\leq j<k_{1}\atop k_{2}\leq j\leq n}^{n}\frac{\widehat{R}_{j}-(n+1)/2}{\sqrt{n-k_{2}+k_{1}}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{q_{n}}{1-k_{2}/n+k_{1}/n}\right)^{1/2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k_{1}-1}\frac{\widehat{R}_{j}-(n+1)/2}{(n^{2}-1)/12}+\sum_{j=k_{2}}^{n}\frac{\widehat{R}_{j}-(n+1)/2}{(n^{2}-1)/12}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{q_{n}}{1-k_{2}/n+k_{1}/n}\right)^{1/2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k_{1}-1}\frac{\widehat{R}_{j}-(n+1)/2}{(n^{2}-1)/12}-\sum_{j=1}^{k_{2}-1}\frac{\widehat{R}_{j}-(n+1)/2}{(n^{2}-1)/12}\right)

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{q_{n}}{1-k_{2}/n+k_{1}/n}\right)^{1/2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\sum_{j=k_{1}}^{k_{2}-1}\frac{\widehat{R}_{j}-(n+1)/2}{(n^{2}-1)/12}\right).

For the second term in (
B.8
), we have that

\displaystyle\left(\frac{12}{n(n+1)}\right)^{1/2}\sum_{j=k_{1}}^{k_{2}-1}\frac{\widehat{R}_{j}-(n+1)/2}{\sqrt{k_{2}-k_{1}}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{q_{n}}{k_{2}/n-k_{1}/n}\right)^{1/2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\sum_{j=k_{1}}^{k_{2}-1}\frac{\widehat{R}_{j}-(n+1)/2}{(n^{2}-1)/12}\right).

So, it follows that

\mathcal{W}(k_{1},k_{2})=\frac{q_{n}}{(k_{2}/n-k_{1}/n)(1-k_{2}/n+k_{1}/n)}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=k_{1}}^{k_{2}-1}\frac{\widehat{R}_{j}-(n+1)/2}{(n^{2}-1)/12}\right)^{2}.

We can write
\mathcal{W}
as a function of

\mathcal{W}(t_{1},t_{2})
, where
k_{1},k_{2}

We can then write

\mathcal{W}(t_{1},t_{2})\coloneqq g_{n}(t_{1},t_{2})\mathcal{W}^{\prime}(t_{1},t_{2}),

where

g_{n}(t_{1},t_{2})=\frac{1}{(t_{2}-t_{1})(1-t_{2}+t_{1})}+o(1)\hskip 18.49988pt\text{ and }\hskip 18.49988pt\mathcal{W}^{\prime}(t_{1},t_{2})=\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\sum_{j=\lfloor nt_{1}\rfloor}^{\lfloor nt_{2}\rfloor-1}\frac{\widehat{R}_{j}-(n+1)/2}{(n^{2}-1)/12}\right)\right)^{2}.

Recall that
\widehat{Z}_{n}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{d}}}{{\rightarrow}}B(t)

\mathcal{W}^{\prime}(t_{1},t_{2})=(\widehat{Z}_{n}(t_{1})-\widehat{Z}_{n}(t_{2}))^{2}
.

\mathcal{W}^{\prime}(t_{1},t_{2})

\mathcal{W}^{\prime}(t_{1},t_{2})=\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\sum_{j=\lfloor nt_{1}\rfloor}^{\lfloor nt_{2}\rfloor-1}\frac{\widehat{R}_{j}-(n+1)/2}{(n^{2}-1)/12}\right)\right)^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{d}}}{{\rightarrow}}(B(t_{2})-B(t_{1}))^{2}.

and

\displaystyle\sup_{t_{1}<t_{2}}\mathcal{W}((\lfloor nt_{1}\rfloor,\lfloor nt_{2}\rfloor))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{d}}}{{\rightarrow}}\sup_{t_{1}<t_{2}}\left(\frac{1}{(t_{2}-t_{1})(1-t_{2}+t_{1})}\right)(B(t_{2})-B(t_{1}))^{2}.
