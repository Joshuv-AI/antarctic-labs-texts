Direct searches for general dark matter-electron interactions with graphene detectors: Part I. Electronic structure calculations
Riccardo Catena, Timon Emken, Marek Matas, Nicola A. Spaldin, Einar Urdshals

Abstract
We develop a formalism to describe electron ejections from graphene-like targets by dark matter (DM) scattering for general forms of scalar and spin 1/2 DM-electron interactions and compare their applicability and accuracy within the density functional theory (DFT) and tight binding (TB) approaches. This formalism allows for accurate prediction of the daily modulation signal expected from DM in upcoming direct detection experiments employing graphene sheets as the target material. A key result is that the physics of the graphene sheet and that of the DM and the ejected electron factorise, allowing for the rate of ejections from all forms of DM to be obtained with a single graphene response function. We perform a comparison between the TB and DFT approaches to modeling the initial state electronic wavefunction within this framework, with DFT emerging as the more self-consistent and reliable choice due to the challenges in the embedding of an appropriate atomic contribution into the TB approach.



I
Introduction

Dark Matter (DM) plays a key role in simultaneously explaining otherwise anomalous physical phenomena that occur on extremely different astronomical length scales
Bertone and Hooper 2018
. For example,
it provides the initial density fluctuations that trigger the formation of cosmic structures and generate the anisotropy pattern observed in the cosmic microwave background temperature and polarisation maps
Aghanim et al. 2020
.
It bends the light emitted by distant astrophysical sources, giving rise to spectacular gravitational lensing events
Clowe et al. 2006
and, furthermore, it provides the mass required to support the flat rotation curves of spiral galaxies
Persic et al. 1996
.

Despite these remarkable observations, we still do not know what DM is made of. The leading hypothesis in astroparticle physics is that DM is made of unidentified, yet-to-be-discovered particles
Bertone and Hooper 2018
. While this simple assumption can collectively explain all phenomena listed above, the hypothetical particles forming our universe’s DM component have so far escaped detection. This dichotomy between solid gravitational evidence and lack of microscopic description makes the search for the “DM particle” a top priority.

A prominent class of experiments searching for the DM particle relies on the direct detection technique
Drukier and Stodolsky 1984
;
Goodman and Witten 1985
. This technique seeks for rare interactions between DM particles from the Milky Way and detector materials located deep underground in low background environments. As far as the DM-material interaction is concerned, DM direct detection experiments have until recently focused on the search for nuclear recoil events induced by the scattering of Weakly Interacting Massive Particles (WIMPs) in crystals, or liquid noble gases
Schumann 2019
. Consequently, direct detection experiments have so far only probed DM particles of mass above about 1 GeV, as lighter particles would not be able to cause an observable nuclear recoil. However, the lack of detection of WIMPs has recently motivated the exploration of alternative experimental approaches that are better suited to probe DM particles of sub-GeV mass
Battaglieri et al. 2017
. It is in this context that DM direct detection experiments sensitive to DM-induced electronic transitions or electron ejections in materials play a central role.

Materials that have been proposed to search for sub-GeV DM particles via DM-electron interactions include liquid argon
Agnes et al. 2018
and xenon
Essig et al. 2012a
;
Essig et al. 2017
;
Aprile et al. 2019
, semiconductor crystals
Essig et al. 2012b
;
Graham et al. 2012
;
Lee et al. 2015
;
Essig et al. 2016
;
Crisler et al. 2018
;
Agnese et al. 2018
;
Abramoff et al. 2019
;
Aguilar-Arevalo et al. 2019
;
Amaral et al. 2020
;
Andersson et al. 2020
, 3D Dirac materials
Hochberg et al. 2018
;
Geilhufe et al. 2020
, graphene
Hochberg et al. 2017
;
Geilhufe et al. 2018
and carbon nanotubes
Capparelli et al. 2015
;
Cavoto et al. 2016
;
Cavoto et al. 2018
;
Cavoto et al. 2020
, to name a few. In this context, anisotropic media, particularly materials with anisotropic Fermi velocities such as graphene and carbon nanotubes, are interesting, as the associated rate of DM-induced electron ejections exhibits an enhanced daily modulation.
This enhancement is caused by the structural anisotropy of the target material in combination with its relative orientation to the DM wind. Given that such a daily modulation is not present in typical experimental backgrounds, it would thus be a smoking gun for DM signal. A proposed experiment to search for DM-induced electron ejections from graphene sheets or arrays of carbon nanotubes, which is currently in the conceptual design stage, is the Princeton Tritium Observatory for Light, Early-Universe, Massive-Neutrino Yield, or PTOLEMY
Betts et al. 2013
;
Baracchini et al. 2018
;
Apponi et al. 2022

PTOLEMY’s experimental design employs a large area surface-deposition tritium target coupled to a graphene substrate to detect the cosmic neutrino background via the observation of single electrons produced in the neutrino absorption by tritium atoms
Betts et al. 2013
. The coupling between tritium and graphene reduces the energy dispersion of the final state electrons by about an order of magnitude compared to the case of molecular tritium
Baracchini et al. 2018
. A small electron energy dispersion allows for better discrimination between electrons produced by neutrino absorption and electrons populating the tail of the tritium
\beta
-decay spectrum
Baracchini et al. 2018
. For this experimental setup to work, it is crucial to experimentally validate the use of graphene as a substrate by accurately measuring the electron-graphene interaction properties. In this intermediate stage of the PTOLEMY experimental program, when tritium target and graphene substrate are still decoupled, PTOLEMY can also operate as a directional MeV-scale DM detector. Specifically, two experimental configurations have been proposed. In the first one, a sample of stacked graphene sheets is considered (PTOLEMY-G
3
)
Hochberg et al. 2017
. Once an electron is ejected from one of the graphene sheets, it drifts in an external electric field until it reaches a calorimeter at the edge of the detector volume. This configuration allows for a full reconstruction of the final state electron kinematics. In a second experimental configuration (PTOLEMY-CNT)
Capparelli et al. 2015
;
Cavoto et al. 2016
;
Cavoto et al. 2018
;
Cavoto et al. 2020
;
Apponi et al. 2022
, an array of single- or multi-wall metallic carbon nanotubes is positioned in vacuum. When an electron is ejected from one of the nanotubes, it is driven by an electric field to the detection region, and recorded by a single electron sensor. The idea of adopting graphene sheets and carbon nanotubes as targets for directional, light DM detection has recently been further developed by the “Graphene-FET” and “dark-PMT” projects, respectively
Apponi et al. 2021
.

The possibility of using graphene or carbon nanotubes as directional detectors sensitive to DM-induced electron ejections motivates an accurate and comprehensive modelling of DM scattering by electrons bound in this class of anisotropic materials. In contrast, the rate of DM-induced electron ejections from graphene sheets
Hochberg et al. 2017
and from carbon nanotubes
Capparelli et al. 2015
;
Cavoto et al. 2016
;
Cavoto et al. 2018
;
Cavoto et al. 2020
has so far been computed assuming that the amplitude for DM-electron interactions depends on the momentum transfer only, and that the DM-electron interaction is spin-independent. This is a rather restrictive assumption, which can easily be violated, e.g. in models where DM has a non negligible magnetic or anapole moment
Catena et al. 2020
. Furthermore, current estimates rely on the tight-binding approximation and have not been validated against first-principles calculations.

The purpose of this work is to extend and improve the formalism currently used to model the scattering of DM particles by electrons bound to graphene sheets. First, we extend the current formalism to virtually arbitrary DM-electron interactions by using the non-relativistic effective theory framework we developed in
Catena et al. 2020
;
Catena et al. 2022
, and recently applied by the XENON group in an analysis of the electron recoil data reported in
Aprile et al. 2022
. Second, we improve the existing formalism by performing state-of-the art density functional theory (DFT) calculations in order to accurately model the electronic properties of graphene.

We expect that the formalism and findings we present here will be useful in the design of the PTOLEMY detector, as well as for the development of the Graphene-FET and dark-PMT projects. However, the relevance of our formalism goes beyond its application to these experimental concepts, as it can also be straightforwardly used to study the ejection of electrons in other experimental settings, where the final state is a free electron that can be described by a plane wave. We leave this exploration for future work.

This paper is the first of a two part series studying DM-electron scatterings in graphene targets. In this paper (Paper I), we lay the theoretical foundations. In the companion Paper II, we will focus on more explicit experimental setups and sensitivity studies
Catena et al. 2023
.
In addition, the software tools
Darphene
and
QEdark-EFT
developed for TB and DFT calculations respectively are publicly available
Emken 2023
;
Urdshals and Matas 2021
.

This article is organized as follows. In Sec.

we introduce our general formalism for modeling the ejection of electrons by the scattering of DM particles in two- and three-dimensional periodic systems. In Sec.
III
, we describe the detailed electronic structure calculations we performed for graphene, both within the tight-binding-approximation and within DFT. We apply these results to study the daily modulation of the DM-induced electron ejection rate for a hypothetical graphene detector in Sec.

II
Rate of electron ejection caused by general dark matter-electron interactions

In this section, we derive an expression for the rate of electron ejection caused by general DM-electron interactions in periodic systems. In Sec.
III
, we will perform the detailed electronic structure calculations that will enable us in Sec.
IV
to apply this general expression to the specific, experimentally relevant case of graphene.

II.1
General formalism

We are interested in processes in which a DM particle
\chi
of mass
m_{\chi}
, initial velocity in the detector rest frame
\mathbf{v}
, and momentum
\mathbf{p}=m_{\chi}\mathbf{v}
is scattered by an electron in initial state
\left|\mathbf{e}_{1}\right\rangle
. During the interaction, the DM particle transfers momentum
\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{p}^{\prime}
to the electron, where
\mathbf{p}^{\prime}
is the final DM momentum, and causes an electronic transition from
\left|\mathbf{e}_{1}\right\rangle
to the final state
\left|\mathbf{e}_{2}\right\rangle

;
Catena et al. 2021

\displaystyle R_{1\rightarrow 2}

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{\chi}}{16m^{2}_{\chi}m^{2}_{e}}\,\int\frac{{\rm d}^{3}\mathbf{q}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int{\rm d}^{3}\mathbf{v}\,f_{\chi}(\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle\times(2\pi)\delta(E_{f}-E_{i})\overline{\left|\mathcal{M}_{1\rightarrow 2}\right|^{2}}\,,

(1)

where

is the electron mass,
n_{\chi}=\rho_{\chi}/m_{\chi}
is the local DM number density,
\rho_{\chi}=0.4\,\text{GeV cm}^{-3}
is the local DM mass density, and

is the local DM velocity distribution boosted to the detector rest frame. For

\displaystyle f_{\chi}(\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\rm esc}\pi^{3/2}v_{0}^{3}}\exp\left[-\frac{(\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{v}_{e})^{2}}{v_{0}^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\times\Theta\left(v_{\rm esc}-|\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{v}_{e}|\right)\,,

\mathbf{v}_{\odot}

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{e}=v_{e}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\sin\alpha_{e}\sin\beta\\

The total initial (final) energy

(

\displaystyle E_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{|\mathbf{p}|^{2}}{2m_{\chi}}+E_{1}\,,\quad E_{f}=\frac{|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}|^{2}}{2m_{\chi}}+E_{2}\,,

(5)

where

(

) is the energy eigenvalue of the electronic state

. We denote the corresponding wave functions by

and

These electron wave functions enter the electron transition amplitude

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{1\rightarrow 2}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{{\rm d}^{3}\boldsymbol{\ell}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\widetilde{\psi}_{2}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\ell}+\mathbf{q})\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{\ell},\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})\widetilde{\psi}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\ell})\,,

(6)

where

the initial state electron momentum. Here, we use momentum conservation to eliminate explicit dependence on the final state electron momentum from

. Furthermore, since the scattering of Milky Way DM particles by free electrons is expected to be non-relativistic, we use the Galilean invariance of

\mathbf{v}^{\perp}_{\rm el}=\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{q}/(2\mu_{\chi e})-\boldsymbol{\ell}/m_{e}

is the DM-electron reduced mass. Finally, we expand

at linear order in

, and write it as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{v}^{\perp}_{\rm el})\approx\left.\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{v}^{\perp}_{\rm el})\right|_{\boldsymbol{\ell}=\mathbf{0}}+\boldsymbol{\ell}\cdot\left.\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{v}^{\perp}_{\rm el})\right|_{\boldsymbol{\ell}=\mathbf{0}}\,.

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{1\rightarrow 2}

\displaystyle=\left.\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{v}^{\perp}_{\rm el})\right|_{\boldsymbol{\ell}=\mathbf{0}}f_{1\rightarrow 2}(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle+m_{e}\left.\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{v}^{\perp}_{\rm el})\right|_{\boldsymbol{\ell}=\mathbf{0}}\cdot\mathbf{f}_{1\rightarrow 2}(\mathbf{q})\,,

\displaystyle f_{1\rightarrow 2}(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle\equiv\int\mathrm{d}^{3}\textbf{x}\,\psi_{2}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\,e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\,\psi_{1}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\,,

(9)

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{1\rightarrow 2}(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle f_{1\rightarrow 2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{V}}\widetilde{\psi}_{1}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime}-\mathbf{q})\,,

(12)

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{1\rightarrow 2}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{V}}\frac{\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}{m_{e}}\,\widetilde{\psi}_{1}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime}-\mathbf{q})\,.

(13)

1

\displaystyle\overline{\left|\mathcal{M}_{1\rightarrow 2}\right|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\bigg\{\overline{\left|\mathcal{M}\right|^{2}}+2\;\overline{\textrm{Re}\left[\mathcal{M}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\mathcal{M}\cdot(\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\right]}+\overline{\left|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\mathcal{M}\cdot(\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\right|^{2}}\bigg\}\times\frac{1}{V}\left|\widetilde{\psi}_{1}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime}-\mathbf{q})\right|^{2}

(14)

where we introduced the free particle response function
R_{\mathrm{free}}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{v})

A
. In order to understand the physical meaning of

\psi_{1}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\overline{\left|\mathcal{M}_{1\rightarrow 2}\right|^{2}}=R_{\mathrm{free}}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{v})\times(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{3}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\ell}-\mathbf{q})\,,

(15)

where all information, besides momentum conservation, is contained in

14

In this latter case, it encodes all relevant material properties via the Fourier transform of the initial state electron wave function. As we will see in the next section, this factorization allows us to express the rate of DM-induced electron ejection from materials in terms of a single material response function. This is in contrast with our previous findings for the cases of atomic ionizations

and excitations in crystals

Catena et al. 2020
;
Catena et al. 2021
neglect the directional information of the event rate and assume a simplified treatment of the velocity integral in the transition-rate formula. By performing this integral exactly, as we do here via Monte Carlo integration (see Sec.

Catena et al. 2021

II.4
Application to periodic systems

We continue the development of our formalism by specifying the wave function for the initial state electrons, which we assume to be bound within a crystal consisting of periodically repeating atoms. In this case, the electron eigenfunction is characterized by an energy band,
i
, and a lattice momentum,
\mathbf{k}
,

\displaystyle\psi_{1}(\mathbf{x})\rightarrow\psi_{i\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x})\,,

(27)

and has the form of a Bloch state, in which

\displaystyle\psi_{i\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{a})=e^{i\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{k}}\,\psi_{i\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x})\,,

(28)

with
\mathbf{a}
being an arbitrary lattice vector. We refer to App.
C.1
for further details on Bloch’s theorem and Bloch states. In this work, we consider two different bases to form the Bloch states of the initial state electrons. For our tight-binding approximation calculations (Sec.
II.4.1

II.4.2
) we use linear combinations of plane waves.

Assuming a Bloch state of crystal momentum
\mathbf{k}
for the initial electron wave function and a plane wave of linear momentum
\mathbf{k}^{\prime}
for the final state electron, we can combine Eq. (
14
) with Eq. (

R_{i\mathbf{k}\rightarrow\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}
, that is the rate of electron ejections by DM scattering when the initial electron is in energy band
i
with crystal momentum
\mathbf{k}
and the outgoing electron has linear momentum
\mathbf{k}^{\prime}
. The energy difference in Eq. (
1

\displaystyle E_{f}-E_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{k^{\prime 2}}{2m_{e}}+\frac{q^{2}}{2m_{\chi}}-\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{q}+\Phi-E_{i}(\mathbf{k})\,,

(29)

where
E_{i}(\mathbf{k})
is the energy of an electron in energy band

with wavevector
\mathbf{k}
, which is negative for bound electrons.
\Phi
is the (positive) work function, which corresponds to the energy difference between the highest occupied electronic state and the zero-energy unbound free-electron plane-wave state; in this work we take the measured value for graphene of
\Phi=
4.3 eV
Puschnig and Lüftner 2015
;
Hochberg et al. 2017

Adding the contributions from all occupied initial states yields

R_{\textrm{any}\rightarrow\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\equiv 2\sum_{i}\int_{\text{BZ}}\frac{N_{\text{cell}}V_{\text{cell}}\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}R_{i\mathbf{k}\rightarrow\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\,,

(30)

where the factor of 2 accounts for the double occupation of each electronic state due to spin degeneracy. We then sum the contributions from all final plane-wave states to obtain the total rate of electron ejections by DM scattering,

R\equiv\int\frac{V\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{3}}R_{\textrm{any}\rightarrow\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\,.

(31)

Finally, we express the total ejection rate,
R
, as follows

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{\chi}N_{\text{cell}}}{32\pi^{2}m_{\chi}^{2}m_{e}^{2}}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\int\mathrm{d}\,E_{e}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{q}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{v}\,f_{\chi}(\mathbf{v})\delta\left(\Delta E_{e}+\frac{q^{2}}{2m_{\chi}}-\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{q}\right)R_{\mathrm{free}}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{v})\;W(\mathbf{k}^{\prime}-\mathbf{q},E_{e})\,,

(32)

where we define the electron’s energy change as
\Delta E_{e}\equiv\frac{k^{\prime 2}}{2m_{e}}+\Phi-E_{e}
, and introduce the material-specific response function

\displaystyle W\left(\boldsymbol{\ell},E_{e}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{V_{\mathrm{cell}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\sum_{i}\int_{\text{BZ}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\delta\left(E_{e}-E_{i}(\mathbf{k})\right)

\displaystyle\times\left|\widetilde{\psi}_{i\mathbf{k}}(\boldsymbol{\ell})\right|^{2}\,.

(33)

As implied by the above
\delta
-function,
E_{e}
takes the value of the initial state electron energy where
E_{e}=0

E_{e}<0
eV for other bound states. Eq. (

The response function is normalized to

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}E_{e}\mathrm{d}^{3}\boldsymbol{\ell}\;W(\boldsymbol{\ell},E_{e})=N_{\mathrm{bands}}\,,

(34)

where
N_{\mathrm{bands}}
is the number of occupied initial-state electronic bands and we use the normalization of
\widetilde{\psi}_{i\mathbf{k}}(\boldsymbol{\ell})
(see Eq. (

V_{\mathrm{cell}}V_{\mathrm{1BZ}}=(2\pi)^{3}

In the next two subsections we express the response function

II.4.1
Atomic orbital basis

Let us now derive a compact expression for the response function

by expanding the initial state electron wave function,

\displaystyle\psi_{i\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}C_{ij}(\mathbf{k})\Phi_{j\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x})\,,

(35a)

\displaystyle\Phi_{j\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}}\sum_{r=1}^{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{jr}}\varphi_{j}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{R}_{jr})\,.

(35b)

Here
\varphi_{j}
is an atomic wave function on an atom at position
\mathbf{R}_{jr}

is the number of unit cells and

is a normalisation constant defined in App.

To evaluate the material response function defined in Eq. (

\displaystyle\widetilde{\psi}_{i\mathbf{k}}(\boldsymbol{\ell})

\displaystyle={\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{k}}\over\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}C_{ij}(\mathbf{k})\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\ell})\sum_{r=1}^{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}e^{i(\mathbf{k}-\boldsymbol{\ell})\cdot\mathbf{R}_{jr}}\,.

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{jr}}=\frac{1}{V_{\mathrm{cell}}}\sum_{\mathbf{G}}(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}\left(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G}\right)\,e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{\delta}_{j}}\,,

where
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{j}

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{3}\Big|\sum_{\mathbf{G}}\delta^{(3)}\left(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G}\right)\Big|^{2}=N_{\mathrm{cell}}V_{\mathrm{cell}}\sum_{\mathbf{G}}\delta^{(3)}\left(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G}\right)\,,

(38)

where
N_{\mathrm{cell}}V_{\mathrm{cell}}=(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(0)

37

\displaystyle|\widetilde{\psi}_{i\mathbf{k}}(\boldsymbol{\ell})|^{2}

\displaystyle={\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}\over V_{\mathrm{cell}}}\Big|\sum_{j=1}^{n}C_{ij}(\mathbf{k})\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\ell})e^{i(\mathbf{k}-\boldsymbol{\ell})\cdot\boldsymbol{\delta}_{j}}\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{\mathbf{G}}(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}\left(\mathbf{k}-\boldsymbol{\ell}+\mathbf{G}\right)\,,

(39)

and by combining Eq. (

\displaystyle W\left(\boldsymbol{\ell},E_{e}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\sum_{i}\Big|\sum_{j=1}^{n}C_{ij}(\mathbf{k})\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\ell})e^{-i\mathbf{G}^{*}\cdot\boldsymbol{\delta}_{j}}\Big|^{2}\delta\left(E_{e}-E_{i}(\mathbf{k})\right)\Big|_{\mathbf{k}=\boldsymbol{\ell}-\mathbf{G}^{*}}\,.

(40)

Here, we performed the integral over the lattice momentum
\mathbf{k}

Plane wave basis

III
Electronic structure calculations for electron ejections in graphene detectors

The equations derived in the previous section refer to three-dimensional periodic systems. We are now interested in applying them to the specific case of graphene, which is a single-layer material that is periodic in two dimensions. The “dimensional reduction” can be performed straightforwardly by means of the replacement specified below,

\displaystyle V_{\rm cell}\int_{\rm BZ}\frac{{\rm d}^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\longrightarrow A_{\rm cell}\int_{\rm BZ}\frac{{\rm d}^{2}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,,

(45)

where
A_{\rm cell}
is the two-dimensional unit cell of graphene, while
\mathbf{k}
in the right-(left-)hand-side is a two-dimensional (three-dimensional) lattice vector in the first Brillouin zone.

With a general formalism for electron ejections by DM scattering in graphene detectors in place, we can now focus on the evaluation of the predicted electron ejection rate. This crucially depends on the response function
W
, which is in turn a function of the initial state electron wave functions. As a result, numerical evaluation of the predicted electron ejection rate requires detailed electronic structure calculations for graphene. In the following, we perform such electronic structure calculations using two methods: the tight binding approximation, and DFT. From this analysis, DFT will emerge as our recommended framework for electronic structure calculations for DM-electron scattering in graphene-based DM detectors.

III.1.4
Limitations

As described in greater detail in App.
C
, we can reproduce the measured band structure of graphene by adjusting the overlap and transfer parameters of the TB approximation.
However, in particular the overlap parameter, e.g. the parameter
s
in Eq. (
73
), is given by the overlap integrals of atomic wavefunctions at neighboring atomic sites.
For a given choice of atomic wavefunctions, it is therefore possible to compute
s
independently of the band structures.
This gives rise to the issue of self-consistency of this approach.

In
Hochberg et al. 2017
, the authors use hydrogenic wavefunctions to approximate the atomic orbitals of carbon.
As described in detail in App.
D.1
, they ensure consistency between the two independent values of the overlap parameters, by re-scaling the effective charge factor
Z_{\mathrm{eff}}
.
However, the resulting wavefunctions do not resemble the atomic wavefunctions of carbon, as we describe in App.
D
, which is why we chose to use RHF wavefunctions.
While it is possible to perform a similar re-scaling of the RHF wavefunctions to establish consistency with the overlap parameters listed in Tab.
2
, this generally modifies the wavefunctions to the extent that they no longer describe electrons in carbon atoms.
Therefore, it seems to be a limitation of the TB approximation to reconcile the use of realistic atomic wavefunctions with the overlap parameters that reproduce the material’s band structure in a fully self-consistent manner.
This issue is not a characteristic of our specific treatment but rather a general feature of the TB approximation itself reflecting the phenomenological nature of this approximation.

III.2
Density Functional Theory

Having carefully described the features and limitations of the tight-binding approach, we now report on our DFT electronic structure calculations. We start with a brief review of the main assumptions underlying DFT in Sec.
III.2.1
. In Sec.
III.2.2
, we provide a general argument supporting DFT as a framework for electronic structure calculations in the case of DM-induced electron ejections by graphene targets. In Sec.
III.2.3

III.2.1
Assumptions

Density functional theory (DFT)
Hohenberg and Kohn 1964
;
Kohn and Sham 1965
(for a review see
Martin 2004
) is a widely used method for calculating the ground-state electronic properties of materials. It allows for explicit treatment of the chemistry and crystal structure without the need for empirically determined input parameters, and provides well-tested and computationally affordable approximations for the many-body electron-electron interactions. In addition, it has been implemented in numerous publically or commercially available computer codes that are convenient to use.

The theory is based on the Hohenberg-Kohn theorem
Hohenberg and Kohn 1964
which states that, for electrons in an external potential (in this case provided by the charged atomic nuclei), the total energy is a unique functional of the electron density, with the ground-state density being the one that minimizes the value of this functional.
The electronic ground state charge density can therefore be obtained variationally.

In practice the charge density,
n_{e}(\mathbf{x})
, is written as a sum over the so-called Kohn-Sham wave functions,
\psi_{i}(\mathbf{x})

Kohn and Sham 1965
of a fictitious auxiliary system in which the electrons are non-interacting. This mapping enables convenient computational solution of the many-body Schrödinger equation at the expense of an inexact description of the quantum mechanical exchange and correlation terms. These have been obtained numerically using Quantum Monte Carlo for the homogeneous interacting electron gas
Ceperley and Alder 1980
, and a number of well-tested approximations exist that are appropriate for different material systems. An additional widely used and well-established approximation divides the electrons into valence electrons, which are treated explicitly within the DFT calculation, and low-energy core electrons, which are combined with the nuclei in the external potential. This pseudopotential approximation drastically reduces the computational expense and is chemically well founded, since the core electrons are not involved in chemical bonding and are only minimally modified in the solid.
The choice of pseudopotential, and the numerical and implementational details of the DFT calculation for graphene performed here, are discussed further in Sec.

We note that, while the Kohn-Sham wave functions and energies do not formally correspond to true single-electron wave functions and energies (except for the highest occupied level, which provides the ionization energy), in practice their dispersion is usually in remarkable agreement with measured photoelectron spectra. The Kohn-Sham band structure is therefore often treated as a proxy for an effective single-particle band structure in a periodic solid.
Since the Hohenberg-Kohn theorem describes only the ground-state electron density, however, this is particularly ill-founded for unoccupied conduction-band states. The methodology and results we present here, however, do not rely on any physical interpretation of the Kohn-Sham wavefunctions, since our final electron states are unbound plane-wave states, and, as we show next, our response function is primarily determined by the ground-state charge density rather than the ground-state wavefunctions.

V
Summary and conclusion

In this paper, we have investigated two solid-state-physics approaches to modeling DM interactions with graphene-like targets, TB and DFT.
Below, we summarise the main features of the two methods and the arguments that led us to identify DFT as the preferred framework for modeling the scattering of DM particles by electrons bound in graphene.

Both DFT and TB capture the main characteristics of the band structure of graphene, such as the Dirac cone at the K-symmetry point of the Brillouin zone and valence band energy distributions (Fig.
5
), and predict a response function that reflects the symmetry of the reciprocal space underlying the graphene lattice (Fig.
1
). The two computational frameworks also predict a qualitatively similar daily modulation pattern in the total rate of electron ejections.
However, the two methods generally predict electron ejection rates that differ by an
\mathcal{O}(1)
factor with TB (DFT) predicting higher rates for high (low) DM masses.

The two approaches employ a different set of approximations that limit their predictivity and region of validity. In order to effectively perform DFT calculations, one usually chooses a radial cutoff to the pseudo-potential and smoothens the core-electron wavefunctions closer to the atomic nucleus. This approximates the electronic structure below that cutoff and suppresses some of the high-momentum contributions to the electronic wavefunction. The total event rate is therefore suppressed when higher energy excitations (of tens of eV) are considered
Griffin et al. 2021
.

On the other hand, DFT is able to provide a self-consistent calculation of the ground-state electron density, which is the indirect quantity of interest for the DM interactions considered in this work. Indeed, an important result of this work is our demonstration that in cases where the outgoing electrons can be treated as a plane wave, the DM and electronic contributions to the DM-induced electron ejection rate factorize. The five crystal response functions identified previously in our work
Catena et al. 2021
then simplify into a single crystal response that is directly proportional to “diagonal part” of the Fourier transform of the ground-state electron density.

The TB approach has the advantage of easier implementability and computational affordability.
In its usual low-energy applications, the detailed form of the atomic basis wavefunctions is not explicitly considered. However, since for the case of DM-electron scattering, we are interested in the explicit form of the electronic wavefunctions, one needs to embed the atomic wavefunctions into this framework.

This proves problematic since these atomic wavefunctions are required to satisfy the overlap integrals of TB that reproduce the experimentally observed band structure. The TB approach assumes that a wavefunction satisfying these relations exists, but does not allow us to calculate its form directly. One possible approach for modeling it is to use the hydrogenic wavefunctions and adjust their parameters such that they would satisfy the imposed overlap integrals (as employed in
Hochberg et al. 2017
). These modified hydrogenic wavefunctions, however, differ significantly from those of real carbon atoms bound within the graphene lattice which is limiting their predictive powers and makes the atomic contribution to the total electronic momenta unreliable.

Another approach is to use the Roothaan-Hartree-Fock wavefunctions fit to describe unbound carbon atoms instead of hydrogenic wavefunctions as the atomic basis. While this atomic basis does describe the individual carbon atoms better than the hydrogenic wavefunctions, they do not satisfy the overlap integrals given by the structure of the graphene lattice, creating an inconsistency in the implementation of the theory. In order to satisfy these relations, one would have to significantly distort the shape of the atomic orbitals, spoiling the original fit describing the carbon atom.

The problem underlying the form of the atomic orbitals within TB, together with the robust predictive powers of the ground-state electron density of DFT have led us to recommend DFT as the framework of choice
for graphene-like DM detector modeling. We will further expand this topic and use DFT to obtain predictions for various possible detector setups and DM candidates in the associated Paper II.

\displaystyle+c_{15}^{2}\left(\frac{\mathbf{q}}{m_{e}}\right)^{4}\Bigg]+\left(\frac{\mathbf{q}}{m_{e}}\cdot\frac{\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}{m_{e}}\right)^{2}\left[-4c_{5}^{2}-c_{15}^{2}\left(\frac{\mathbf{q}}{m_{e}}\right)^{2}+2c_{12}c_{15}+2c_{13}c_{14})\right]\Bigg\}\,.

(57)

Furthermore, to rewrite the above equations one can use the following relations

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\mathbf{q}}{m_{e}}\times\mathbf{v}_{\rm el}^{\perp}\right)^{2}=

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}^{2}+\frac{\mathbf{q}^{2}}{4\mu_{\chi e}^{2}}\frac{m_{\chi}-m_{e}}{m_{e}+m_{\chi}}-\frac{\Delta E_{e}}{\mu_{\chi e}}\,,

(59)

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{v}_{\rm el}^{\perp}\cdot\mathbf{q}\right)|_{\boldsymbol{\ell}=0}=

\displaystyle\Delta E_{e}-\frac{\mathbf{q}^{2}}{2m_{e}}\,,

(60)

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{\rm el}^{\perp}|_{\boldsymbol{\ell}=0}=

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}-\frac{\mathbf{q}}{2\mu_{\chi e}}\,,

(61)

where

Appendix C
The tight-binding approximation

C.1
General review

In this section, we review the tight-binding approximation.
In particular, we emphasize how the energy dispersion
E_{i}(\mathbf{k})
, appearing in Eq. (
29
), and wave function coefficients
C_{ij}(\mathbf{k})
of Eq. (
35
) are evaluated in general using this technique
Saito et al. 1998
.
The translational symmetry of a lattice should also be reflected in the wave functions. In particular, the electron wave function
\Psi(\mathbf{x})
has to satisfy
Bloch’s theorem
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{a}}\Psi(\mathbf{x})\equiv\Psi(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{a})=e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{a}}\Psi(\mathbf{x})\,.

(67)

Here, we introduced the translation operator
\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{a}}
along one of the lattice vectors
\mathbf{a}
, and the lattice momentum
\mathbf{k}
.

One way to write down a generic wave function satisfying Bloch’s theorem can be obtained using the
tight binding
approximation. A tight-binding Bloch function
\Phi_{j\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x})
is an approximation to the system’s wave functions which is defined by summing up the wave functions of the
j^{\rm th}
atomic orbital of isolated atoms at their respective lattice site,

\displaystyle\Phi_{j\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{k}}\varphi_{j}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{R}_{k})\,,\quad j=1,\dots,n\,.

(68)

This way, Bloch functions sum up the wave functions
\varphi_{j}(\mathbf{x})
of
N
unit cells weighted with a lattice site dependent phase which ensures that Eq. (
67
) is satisfied.
Note that, even if the isolated atomic wave functions
\varphi_{j}(\mathbf{x})
are normalized, the overlaps between neighboring wave functions generally render the Bloch functions as non-normalized.

The actual electron wave functions of the material are linear combinations of the Bloch functions, mixing the different atomic orbitals (but not different lattice momenta),

\displaystyle\Psi_{i\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}C_{ij}(\mathbf{k})\Phi_{j\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x})\,,

(69)

where the constant
\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{k}}
ensures that the wave function
\Psi_{i\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x})
is normalized.

Using Schrödinger’s equation, the energy values of these
n
states are

\displaystyle E_{i}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{\left\langle\Psi_{i}\middle|\mathcal{H}\middle|\Psi_{i}\right\rangle}{\left\langle\Psi_{i}\middle|\Psi_{i}\right\rangle}

(70a)

\displaystyle=\frac{\int\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{x}\;\Psi^{*}_{i\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x})\;\mathcal{H}\;\Psi_{i\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x})}{\int\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{x}\;\Psi^{*}_{i\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x})\Psi_{i\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x})}

(70b)

Substituting Eq. (
69
), the energy eigenvalues can be expressed as

\displaystyle E_{i}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{n}C^{*}_{ij}(\mathbf{k})C_{ij^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k})\left\langle\Phi_{j}\middle|\mathcal{H}\middle|\Phi_{j^{\prime}}\right\rangle}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{n}C^{*}_{ij}(\mathbf{k})C_{ij^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k})\left\langle\Phi_{j}\middle|\Phi_{j^{\prime}}\right\rangle}

(71a)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\mathbf{C}^{\dagger}_{i}(\mathbf{k})\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}(\mathbf{k})\cdot\mathbf{C}_{i}(\mathbf{k})}{\mathbf{C}^{\dagger}_{i}(\mathbf{k})\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{k})\cdot\mathbf{C}_{i}(\mathbf{k})}\,.

(71b)

Here, we defined the coefficient vectors

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{i}(\mathbf{k})=\begin{pmatrix}C_{i1}(\mathbf{k})\\[5.69054pt]
\vdots\\[5.69054pt]
C_{in}(\mathbf{k})\end{pmatrix}\,,

(72)

as well as the transfer integral matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}(\mathbf{k})
, and the overlap integral matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{k})
.
Their components are defined as

\displaystyle\left[\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}(\mathbf{k})\right]_{ij}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\Phi_{i}\middle|\mathcal{H}\middle|\Phi_{j}\right\rangle\,,

(73)

\displaystyle\left[\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{k})\right]_{ij}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\Phi_{i}\middle|\Phi_{j}\right\rangle\,.

(74)

As already seen in Eq. (
71
), the normalization of
\Psi_{i\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x})
can be written in terms of the coefficient vector
\mathbf{C}_{i}(\mathbf{k})
and the overlap matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{k})
,

\displaystyle\left\langle\Psi_{i}\middle|\Psi_{i}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}\;\mathbf{C}^{\dagger}_{i}(\mathbf{k})\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{k})\cdot\mathbf{C}_{i}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=\left[\mathbf{C}^{\dagger}_{i}(\mathbf{k})\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{k})\cdot\mathbf{C}_{i}(\mathbf{k})\right]^{-1/2}\,.

(75)

The entries of the coefficient vector
\mathbf{C}_{i}(\mathbf{k})
are obtained using the variational principle by minimizing the energy eigenvalues
E_{i}(\mathbf{k})
, i.e.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial E_{i}(\mathbf{k})}{\partial C_{ij}^{*}}

\displaystyle=0\,.

(76)

This equation is equivalent to the following general eigenvalue problem,

\displaystyle\left[\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}-E_{i}(\mathbf{k})\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}\right]\cdot\mathbf{C}_{i}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=0\,.

(77)

Non-vanishing eigenvectors can only be found, if the
secular equation
applies,

\displaystyle\text{det}\left[\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}-E_{i}(\mathbf{k})\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}\right]=0\,.

(78)

For a fixed
\mathbf{k}
, this polynomial of degree
n
can be solved for the
n
eigenvalues, i.e. the energy dispersion
E_{i}(\mathbf{k})
.
Furthermore, the eigenvectors determine the electron wave functions
\Psi_{i\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x})
.

Finally, the entries of the transfer integral matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}(\mathbf{k})
, and the overlap integral matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{k})
are often fixed to specific values, which ensure that the correct band structure of the material of interest is reproduced. These values are obtained either experimentally or from first-principles calculations.

C.2
Tight-binding approximation for graphene

We will apply the results of the previous section to the case of graphene.
As illustrated in Fig.
8
, the unit cell of graphene consists of two carbon atoms, denoted
A
and
B
.
The relevant atomic orbitals of the carbon atoms on these locations are
2s
,
2p_{x}
,
2p_{y}
, and
2p_{z}
(the
1s
orbitals form a low energy core state and do not contribute to the valence band levels).
In graphene, the first three orbitals combine or hybridize and form the so-called
\sigma
-bands and the
2p_{z}
orbitals hybridize to form the
\pi
-bands.
As a result, there are
n=2(6)

\pi(\sigma)
-bonding atomic orbitals in each unit cell, and Eq. (
78
) constitutes a two-(six-) dimensional eigenvalue problem for the
\pi(\sigma)
band.
As we will describe in detail, the energy dispersion and wave function for the
\pi
electrons can be expressed analytically, whereas the
\sigma
electrons require numerical methods to solve the six-dimensional secular equation.

The
\pi
-electrons

Figure 9:
Energy bands of the
\pi
-electrons in graphene evaluated in the tight-binding approximation, see Eq. (
88
).

The
\pi
-electrons are a hybridization of the atomic
2p_{z}
orbitals of carbon.
In order to compute the corresponding energy bands by solving Eq. (
78
), the main step is to evaluate the transfer integral and overlap integral matrices
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}(\mathbf{k})
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{k})
, which we defined in Eqs. (
73
) and (
74
).
In this case, they are
2\times 2
matrices.
Starting with the former, we substitute Eq. (
68
) into Eq. (
73
).
The diagonal entries read

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}_{AA}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\Phi_{A}\middle|\mathcal{H}\middle|\Phi_{A}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}\sum_{k,k^{\prime}=1}^{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{R}_{k}-\mathbf{R}_{k^{\prime}})}\left\langle\varphi_{A}(\mathbf{R}_{k^{\prime}})\middle|\mathcal{H}\middle|\varphi_{A}(\mathbf{R}_{k})\right\rangle\,.

(79)

If we only take the terms into account for which
\mathbf{R}_{k}=\mathbf{R}_{k^{\prime}}
and neglect sub-dominant contributions with
\mathbf{R}_{k}\neq\mathbf{R}_{k^{\prime}}
, we find

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}_{AA}

\displaystyle\approx\varepsilon_{2p}\,,\quad\text{with }\varepsilon_{2p}\equiv\left\langle\varphi_{A,k}\middle|\mathcal{H}\middle|\varphi_{A,k}\right\rangle\,.

(80)

By analogy,
\mathcal{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}}_{BB}=\varepsilon_{2p}
.
For the off-diagonal components, we can do a similar (nearest-neighbor) approximation.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}_{AB}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\Phi_{A}\middle|\mathcal{H}\middle|\Phi_{B}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}\sum_{k,k^{\prime}=1}^{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{R}_{k}-\mathbf{R}_{k^{\prime}})}\left\langle\varphi_{A}(\mathbf{R}_{k^{\prime}})\middle|\mathcal{H}\middle|\varphi_{B}(\mathbf{R}_{k})\right\rangle\,.

(81)

Next, we only involve the contributions of the three nearest neighbors of each atom A,

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}\sum_{k=1}^{3}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot((\mathbf{R}_{k^{\prime}}+\mathbf{N}_{k})-\mathbf{R}_{k^{\prime}})}

\displaystyle\times\left\langle\varphi_{A}(\mathbf{R}_{k^{\prime}})\middle|\mathcal{H}\middle|\varphi_{B}(\mathbf{R}_{k^{\prime}}+\mathbf{N}_{k})\right\rangle

(82)

where the three vectors
\mathbf{N}_{k}
are given by Eq. (
64
). We define the parameter
t\equiv\left\langle\varphi_{A}(\mathbf{R}_{k^{\prime}})\middle|\mathcal{H}\middle|\varphi_{B}(\mathbf{R}_{k^{\prime}}+\mathbf{N}_{k})\right\rangle
, which is identical for all
k
due to the rotational symmetry of the
2p_{z}
wave function.
This leaves us with

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}_{AB}

\displaystyle=t\times\sum_{k=1}^{3}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{N}_{k}}\,.

(83)

The other off-diagonal is simply
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}_{BA}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}_{AB}^{*}
.
In summary, the full transfer integral matrix for the
\pi
-electrons in the nearest-neighbor approximation is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}}

\displaystyle\approx\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{2p}&tf(\mathbf{k})\\
tf(\mathbf{k})^{*}&\varepsilon_{2p}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(84)

The function
f(\mathbf{k})
is defined as

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{k=1}^{3}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{N}_{k}}\,.

(85)

The square of this function can be evaluated as

\displaystyle|f(\mathbf{k})|^{2}=3+2\sum_{k=1}^{3}\cos(\mathbf{a}_{k}\cdot\mathbf{k})\,,\text{ with }\mathbf{a}_{3}\equiv\mathbf{a}_{2}-\mathbf{a}_{1}\,.

(86)

For the overlap matrix, the previous steps can essentially be repeated to find

\displaystyle\mathcal{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}}

\displaystyle\approx\begin{pmatrix}1&sf(\mathbf{k})\\
sf(\mathbf{k})^{*}&1\end{pmatrix}\,,

(87)

where
s\equiv\left\langle\varphi_{A}(\mathbf{R}_{j})\middle|\varphi_{B}(\mathbf{R}_{j}+\mathbf{N}_{k})\right\rangle
.

Energy bands of
\pi
-electrons

Given the explicit form of the two matrices, we can show that the corresponding eigenvalues solving the secular equation in Eq. (
78
) are given by

\displaystyle E_{\pi}(\mathbf{k})=\frac{\varepsilon_{2p}\pm t|f(\mathbf{k})|}{1\pm s|f(\mathbf{k})|}\,.

(88)

This energy dispersion is visualized in Fig.
9
for
\mathbf{k}\in\text{BZ}
, as well as along the path between the high-symmetry points given by Eq. (
66
), which is also indicated in the figure.
In order to reproduce the band structure of graphene to a good degree, we used the parameters
\varepsilon_{2p_{z}}=0
(by convention),
s=0.129
and
t=-3.033
eV
Saito et al. 1998
.

Since
t
is negative, the
`+^{\prime}
solution is lower energy and corresponds to the bonding or valence
\pi
-band, whereas the
`-^{\prime}
solution is the anti-bonding or conduction
\pi^{*}
-band.
The bands are degenerate at the high-symmetry point
\mathbf{K}
.

Next, we turn our attention towards the
\mathbf{C}_{i}(\mathbf{k})
coefficients.
The normalized eigenvectors corresponding to the eigenvalues of Eq. (
88
) are obtained by solving Eq. (
77
). For the
\pi
-electrons we find

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{\pi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\[5.69054pt]
\pm e^{i\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(89a)

\displaystyle\text{with }\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=-\arctan{\frac{\text{Im}f(\mathbf{k})}{\text{Re}f(\mathbf{k})}}\,.

(89b)

Hence, the
\pi
-electron wave functions can be written as

\displaystyle\Psi_{\pi\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{k}}}{\sqrt{2N_{\mathrm{cell}}}}\times

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}\bigg[e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{k}^{A}}\varphi_{2p_{z}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{R}_{k}^{A})

\displaystyle+e^{i\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}+i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{k}^{B}}\varphi_{2p_{z}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{R}_{k}^{B})\bigg]\,.

(90)

As opposed to the treatment in
Hochberg et al. 2017
, we do not perform a nearest-neighbor approximation on this level, as it is not well-defined here.
Instead it is necessary to sum over all
N
unit cells.
However, the nearest-neighbor approximation can be applied when computing the norm of
\Psi_{\pi\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x})
,

\displaystyle\left\langle\Psi_{\pi}\middle|\Psi_{\pi}\right\rangle=\frac{\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}}{2N_{\mathrm{cell}}}\sum_{k,{k^{\prime}}=1}^{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}\bigg[

\displaystyle e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{R}_{k^{\prime}}^{A}-\mathbf{R}_{k}^{A})}\left\langle\varphi_{2p_{z}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{R}_{k}^{A})\middle|\varphi_{2p_{z}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{R}_{k^{\prime}}^{A})\right\rangle

\displaystyle+e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{R}_{k^{\prime}}^{B}-\mathbf{R}_{k}^{A})}\left\langle\varphi_{2p_{z}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{R}_{k}^{A})\middle|\varphi_{2p_{z}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{R}_{k^{\prime}}^{B})\right\rangle e^{i\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}}

\displaystyle+e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{R}_{k}^{B}-\mathbf{R}_{k^{\prime}}^{A})}\left\langle\varphi_{2p_{z}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{R}_{k}^{B})\middle|\varphi_{2p_{z}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{R}_{k^{\prime}}^{A})\right\rangle e^{-i\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}}

\displaystyle+e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{R}_{k}^{B}-\mathbf{R}_{k^{\prime}}^{B})}\left\langle\varphi_{2p_{z}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{R}_{k}^{B})\middle|\varphi_{2p_{z}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{R}_{k^{\prime}}^{B})\right\rangle\bigg]

Next, we perform the sum over
{k^{\prime}}
using the nearest-neighbor approximation (but also taking next-nearest neighbors into account).
In addition to the neighboring contributions, the first and last lines contain terms with
\mathrm{R}_{k}=\mathrm{R}_{k^{\prime}}
, each contributing with

to the final sum.
Hence, we find

\displaystyle\left\langle\Psi_{\pi}\middle|\Psi_{\pi}\right\rangle\approx\frac{\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}}{2N_{\mathrm{cell}}}\Bigg\{2N_{\mathrm{cell}}+\sum_{{k^{\prime}}=1}^{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}\sum_{k=1}^{3}\bigg[

\displaystyle+e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{R}_{k^{\prime}}^{A}+\mathbf{N}_{k}-\mathbf{R}_{k^{\prime}}^{A})}se^{-i\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}}+e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{R}_{k^{\prime}}^{B}+\mathbf{a}_{k}-\mathbf{R}_{k^{\prime}}^{B})}s^{\prime}\bigg]

(92)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}\bigg[1+\sum_{k=1}^{3}\left(s\cos(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{N}_{k}+\varphi_{\mathbf{k}})+s^{\prime}\cos(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{a}_{k})\right)\bigg]\,.

(93)

Here,

denotes the overlap integral of the atomic orbitals at next-to-nearest neighboring sites.
We find that the two leading terms are consistent with the general expression of Eq. (

\displaystyle\left\langle\Psi_{\pi}\middle|\Psi_{\pi}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\approx\mathbf{C}^{\dagger}_{\pi}(\mathbf{k})\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{k})\cdot\mathbf{C}_{\pi}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=1+s\sum_{k=1}^{3}\cos(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{N}_{k}+\varphi_{\mathbf{k}})\,,

(94)

and hence

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=\left[1+s\sum_{k=1}^{3}\cos(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{N}_{k}+\varphi_{\mathbf{k}})\right]^{-1/2}\,.

(95)

Next, we shift our attention from position space to momentum space.
The Fourier-transformed Bloch wave functions are

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Phi}_{\mathbf{k}}(\boldsymbol{\ell})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}e^{i(\boldsymbol{\ell}+\mathbf{k})\cdot\mathbf{R}_{k}}\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\ell})\,,

(96)

where

is the conjugate momentum to

. Therefore, the

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{k}}}{\sqrt{2N_{\mathrm{cell}}}}\widetilde{\varphi}_{2p_{z}}(\boldsymbol{\ell})\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}\bigg[e^{i(\boldsymbol{\ell}+\mathbf{k})\cdot\mathbf{R}_{k}^{A}}+e^{i(\boldsymbol{\ell}+\mathbf{k})\cdot\mathbf{R}_{k}^{B}+i\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}\bigg]\,.

Using

(98)

For the evaluation of the exponential sum, we can follow the steps outlined in Sec.

and use the identity in Eq. (

).
In the end, we find

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Psi}_{\pi\mathbf{k}}(\boldsymbol{\ell})

\displaystyle\times\left\{1+e^{i\left[\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}+(\boldsymbol{\ell}+\mathbf{k})\cdot\boldsymbol{\delta}\right]}\right\}\,.

(99)

The
\sigma
-electrons

The
\sigma

2\text{p}_{x}
, and

Appendix D
Atomic wavefunctions

The evaluation of the graphene response function requires a specific form of the atomic wavefunctions of carbon
\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{x})
(and its Fourier transform
\widetilde{\varphi}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\ell})
).
We present results for two particular choices.
As proposed by Hochberg et al.
Hochberg et al. 2017
, we start by approximating the wavefunctions of carbon with hydrogenic wave functions with a re-scaled
Z_{\mathrm{eff}}
factor.
We improve upon this choice by using Roothaan-Hartree-Fock (RHF) wavefunctions for the ground states of carbon
Bunge et al. 1993
.
In this appendix, we summarize the explicit wave functions both in position and momentum space and present a comparison.

The wave function of the atomic state
(n,\ell,m)
in position space is given by

\displaystyle\varphi_{nlm}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=R_{nl}(r)Y_{l}^{m}(\hat{\mathbf{x}})\,,

(105)

where
Y_{l}^{m}(\hat{\mathbf{x}})
are spherical harmonics, and
R_{nl}(r)
is the radial component of the wave function.

Regardless of the choice of form for the radial component, the corresponding Fourier-transformed wave function in momentum space
\widetilde{\varphi}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\ell})
for a given position space wave function
\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{x})
is defined as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\varphi}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\ell})=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{x}\,\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{x})e^{-i\boldsymbol{\ell}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\,,

(106)

which fixes the normalization of the wave function to

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{x}\;|\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{x})|^{2}

\displaystyle=1\,,\quad\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}\boldsymbol{\ell}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\;|\widetilde{\varphi}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\ell})|^{2}=1\,.

(107)

For the evaluation of the graphene response function using the TB approximation, the required wavefunctions are those of the atomic orbitals
2s
and
2p
in the environment of a carbon atom.
Additionally for the
2p
orbitals, the crystal structure of graphene gives rise to the
2p_{x}
,
2p_{y}
, and
2p_{z}
orbitals.
The corresponding wavefunctions are given by

\displaystyle\varphi_{2p_{i}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=R_{2p}(r)Y_{i}(\hat{\mathbf{x}})\,,\;\text{with}

(108a)

\displaystyle Y_{i}(\hat{\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle\equiv\sqrt{\frac{3}{4\pi}}\frac{x_{i}}{r}\,.

(108b)

Similarly, the relevant momentum wave functions can be written as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\varphi}_{nlm}(\boldsymbol{\ell})

\displaystyle=\chi_{nl}(\ell)Y_{l}^{m}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\ell}})\,,

(109)

and hence for the
2p_{i}
states we find

\displaystyle\widetilde{\varphi}_{2p_{i}}(\boldsymbol{\ell})

\displaystyle=\chi_{2p}(\ell)Y_{i}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\ell}})\,.

(110)

Appendix E
Comparison of our TB treatment to Hochberg et al. (2017)

The first study of graphene targets for sub-GeV DM searches was published by Hochberg et al.
Hochberg et al. 2017
.
Therein, the authors chose a semi-analytic approach to describe the electron wavefunctions in graphene based on the tight-binding (TB) approximation.
In reproducing their results, we noticed a number of deviations to our TB treatment of the electron wavefunctions in graphene.

•

The modeling of the Bloch wavefunctions in the above-mentioned work does not satisfy the Bloch’s theorem given in Eq. (
67
). This also gives rise to a different normalization factor
\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{k}}
.

•

While the hydrogenic wavefunctions proposed in
Hochberg et al. 2017
give overlap integrals in agreement with the TB theory, they differ significantly from more accurate RHF atomic wavefunctions of carbon atoms, as shown in App.
D
.

•

After a careful evaluation of the formula for the DM induced electron ejection rate, we find an extra factor of 1/2 coming from normalizing to the number of unit cells instead of the number of carbon atoms in the system.

In this appendix, we review how we improved the TB treatment of graphene wavefunctions and how the updated electron ejection rate compares to the one presented by Hochberg et al.

4
4

4

We note that we were able to reproduce the signal energy spectra reported in
Hochberg et al. 2017
by accounting for all differences.

E.1
Bloch states and their normalization

The “Bloch states” proposed in
Hochberg et al. 2017
are given by

\displaystyle\Phi_{A\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\varphi_{A}(\mathbf{x})\,,

(117a)

\displaystyle\Phi_{B\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{3}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{N}_{k}}\varphi_{B}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{N}_{k})\,.

(117b)

Compared to the expression of Eq. (
68
), these states, while capturing the nearest-neighbour approximation, do not formally correspond to Bloch wavefunctions.
Consequently, they do not satisfy Bloch’s theorem or rigorously describe a periodic system, see Eq. (
67
).

One consequence of this choice of Bloch states is a deviating normalization factor
\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{k}}
.
As an example, using the Bloch states by Hochberg et al., it is possible to compute the
exact
normalization factor for the
\pi
-electrons,

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}

\displaystyle=\left[2+s\sum_{k=1}^{3}\cos(\boldsymbol{\ell}\cdot\mathbf{N}_{k}+\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\ell}})+s^{\prime}\sum_{k=1}^{3}\cos(\boldsymbol{\ell}\cdot\mathbf{a}_{k})\right]^{-1/2}\,.

(118)

While this factor correctly normalizes the electron wavefunctions involving the Bloch states of (
117
), it deviates from our respective expression for the
\pi
-electrons in Eq. (
95
).
Furthermore, our normalization constant is consistent with Eq. (
75
), i.e. the general expression for the normalization constant for Bloch wavefunctions as given by Eq. (
68
).
Similar arguments hold for the
\sigma
-electrons.

E.2
Atomic wavefunctions

In
Hochberg et al. 2017
, Hochberg et al. propose to describe the atomic wavefunctions of carbon by using hydrogenic wavefunctions with a re-scaled
Z_{\mathrm{eff}}
factor.
The re-scaling ensures that the overlap integrals of the wavefunctions are consistent with the TB parameters that reproduce the band structure of graphene listed in Tab.
2
.
For completeness, we list these wavefunctions in App.
D.1
.

In contrast, we model the carbon wavefunctions using Roothaan-Hartree-Fock (RHF) wavefunctions
Bunge et al. 1993
, which we summarize in App.
D.2
.
By comparison, we find that the re-scaled hydrogenic wavefunctions are a poor approximation for the required ground state wavefunctions of atomic electrons in carbon, as can be seen in Fig.
11
.

While the RHF wavefunctions are a better description of carbon electrons, it should be noted that their overlap integrals are not consistent with the overlap parameters for graphene in Tab.
2
.
A re-scaling of the RHF wavefunctions similar to the approach by Hochberg et al. is possible, but spoils the accurate description of electrons in atomic carbon.
However, this seems to be a general feature of evaluating electron wavefunctions in the TB approximation.
