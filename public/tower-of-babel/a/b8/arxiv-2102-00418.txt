Abstract
. Multi-degree Tchebycheffian splines are splines with pieces drawn from extended (complete) Tchebycheff spaces, which may differ from interval to interval, and possibly of different dimensions. These are a natural extension of multi-degree polynomial splines. Under quite mild assumptions, they can be represented in terms of a so-called MDTB-spline basis; such basis possesses all the characterizing properties of the classical polynomial B-spline basis. We present a practical framework to compute MDTB-splines, and provide an object-oriented implementation in Matlab . The implementation supports the construction, differentiation, and visualization of MDTB-splines whose pieces belong to Tchebycheff spaces that are null-spaces of constant-coefficient linear differential operators. The construction relies on an extraction operator that maps local Tchebycheffian Bernstein functions to the MDTB-spline basis of interest.



1.
Introduction

Splines are undoubted an important tool in several branches of the sciences including geometric modeling, signal processing, data analysis, visualization, and numerical simulation, just to mention a few
(
Cohen
et al. 2001
;
Cottrell
et al. 2009
)
.
The term
splines
usually refers to univariate piecewise (algebraic) polynomial functions with certain smoothness, whose popularity can be mainly attributed to their representation in terms of the so-called
B-splines
. The B-splines enjoy properties as local linear independence, minimal support, non-negativity and partition of unity; they can be computed through a stable recurrence relation; and they can even be seen as the geometrically optimal basis for piecewise polynomial spaces.

This raises the following natural question: is there a more general class of piecewise functions with similar properties? The answer is the class of Tchebycheffian splines.

Tchebycheff refers to the famous Russian mathematician and can be alternatively transliterated from the Russian writing of the name as Chebysheff, Chebyshev, Chebychov, Chebyshov, Tchebychev, Tschebyschev, Tschebyschef, Tschebyscheff, etc. We follow the traditional French transcription, in the footsteps of the monograph by
Schumaker 2007
.
Besides algebraic polynomial splines, it contains exponential and trigonometric splines, and is closely related to null-spaces of linear differential operators
(
Schumaker 2007
)
.

The term
Tchebycheffian splines
was coined by
Karlin and
Ziegler 1966
. The authors considered functions belonging piecewise to a
(p+1)
-dimensional linear space
{\mathbb{T}_{p}}
, spanned by an extended complete Tchebycheff system (ECT-system; see Section
2.1
), and discussed their smoothness and approximation properties.
We will refer to
p
as the degree, in analogy with the polynomial splines.
Tchebycheffian B-splines, among other properties, were established by
Karlin 1968
.
It is impossible to give a complete account of the numerous articles related to this topic, so we do not want to make any attempt.
Noteworthy techniques for their construction and analysis are generalized divided differences
(
Lyche 1985
)
, generalized polar forms
(
Pottmann 1993
)
, generalized de Boor–Fix dual functionals
(
Barry 1996
)
and repeated integration
(
Bister and
Prautzsch 1997
)
.
Tchebycheffian B-splines possess all the characterizing properties of the classical polynomial B-splines.
We refer the reader to the monograph by
Schumaker 2007
and the survey article by
Lyche
et al. 2019
for more details on Tchebycheffian splines and Tchebycheffian B-splines.

Tchebycheffian splines find applications in data approximation/interpolation
(
Koch and Lyche 1993
)
,
geometric modeling
(
Beccari
et al. 2019
;
Mazure 2011b
)
and
signal processing
(
Unser 2005
;
Unser and Blu 2005
)
. Because of their relation to null-spaces of differential operators, they also offer a lot of opportunity in the context of isogeometric analysis, a spline paradigm for the numerical solution of differential problems
(
Cottrell
et al. 2009
)
. Thanks to their structural similarities, Tchebycheffian B-splines are
plug-to-plug compatible with classical polynomial B-splines, so they can be potentially easily incorporated in any software library supporting polynomial B-splines to enrich its capability.

A particularly interesting subclass of Tchebycheffian B-splines are the so-called
generalized polynomial B-splines
, introduced by
Kvasov and
Sattayatham 1999
.
They can be seen as the minimal extension of (algebraic) polynomial B-splines towards the wide variety of Tchebycheffian B-splines, with a small selection of shape parameters. The fine-tuning of these parameters generally results in a gain from the accuracy point of view, compared with polynomial B-splines. In addition, suitable choices of such spaces — including algebraic polynomial and exponential/trigonometric functions — allow for an exact representation through (almost) arc-length parameterization of profiles of salient interest in applications, such as conic sections and helices.
These are prominent features for geometric modeling
(
Fang
et al. 2010
;
Wang and Fang 2008
)
and isogeometric analysis
(
Aimi
et al. 2017
;
Manni
et al. 2011
;
Manni
et al. 2015
;
Manni
et al. 2017
)
.
A stable but costly method (based on convolution) to approximately evaluate generalized polynomial B-splines on uniform knots was proposed by

Roman
et al. 2017
.

It was shown by
Nürnberger et al. 1983
;
Nürnberger et al. 1984
that many properties of ordinary Tchebycheffian splines carry over to certain generalized Tchebycheffian splines, in the sense that pieces can be drawn from different ECT-spaces of different dimensions. Under quite mild assumptions, such splines can be represented in terms of a B-spline-like basis. This basis is called
generalized Tchebycheffian B-spline basis
or also
multi-degree Tchebycheffian B-spline basis
to reflect better the analogy with polynomial splines. We will follow the latter terminology and refer to these basis functions as MDTB-splines. Their properties were studied more recently by
Buchwald and
Mühlbach 2003
and
Hiemstra et al. 2020
.
The (algebraic) polynomial subclass of such multi-degree B-splines were explored in the context of geometric modeling
(
Beccari
et al. 2017
)
and isogeometric analysis
(
Toshniwal et al. 2017
)
; these splines are called
polynomial MDB-splines
or just
MDB-splines
.

Unfortunately, despite their theoretical interest and applicative potential, MDTB-splines have not gained much attention in practice. The reason behind this is that MDTB-splines are generally difficult to compute. Classical approaches based on generalized divided differences, Hermite interpolation or repeated integration are computationally expensive and/or numerically unstable. An important step forward was recently made by
Hiemstra et al. 2020
; the authors proposed a construction based on a so-called
multi-degree spline extraction operator
that represents MDTB-splines as linear combinations of local Tchebycheffian Bernstein functions. The local Tchebycheffian Bernstein functions form a basis of the local ECT-spaces involved in the definition of the MDTB-splines. In the polynomial case, these are nothing but the classical Bernstein polynomial basis functions.
The same type of extraction operator was already earlier investigated by
Toshniwal et al. 2017
;
Toshniwal et al. 2020
and
Speleers 2019
for dealing with the subclass of polynomial MDB-splines. A similar idea has also been pursued by
Beccari and
Casciola 2021
for computing polynomial MDB-splines.

Here, we present an object-oriented
Matlab
toolbox to construct and manipulate MDTB-splines whenever they exist. The key ingredient is the extraction operator discussed above. The toolbox is a continuation and extension of the
Matlab
toolbox developed by
Speleers 2019
for dealing with polynomial MDB-splines. The toolbox supports MDTB-splines whose pieces belong to ECT-spaces that are null-spaces of constant-coefficient linear differential operators. The computation of the corresponding Tchebycheffian Bernstein functions is inspired by the state-of-the-art implementation from the C++ library of
Róth 2019
for the general ECT-space setting, but also relies on more efficient and more robust routines for certain specialized ECT-spaces (polynomial and generalized polynomial spaces of exponential and trigonometric type).

To the best of our knowledge, no general-purpose software library is nowadays available to work with Tchebycheffian splines, also considering the more restricted case where the local ECT-spaces have all the same degree or even where these local spaces are taken all the same. Tchebycheffian Bernstein functions and curves have been addressed by
Róth 2019
, but spline curves are preferred in practice as they combine more local control of the shapes with built-in higher smoothness globally. Note that splines of lower degrees are usually employed for geometric modeling (so avoiding a source of ECT-space instabilities; see Section
6.2
). In this perspective, the
Matlab
toolbox may unlock Tchebycheffian splines for a wide audience, and help pushing them from an elegant theoretical extension of polynomial splines towards a mainstream practical tool.

The remainder of the article is organized as follows. In Section
2
we introduce the notion of ECT-space and show how to define Tchebycheffian Bernstein functions in such space. We detail in particular the important large class of ECT-spaces that are null-spaces of constant-coefficient linear differential operators. In Section
3
we focus on multi-degree Tchebycheffian spline spaces, and give a (theoretical) recursive definition of MDTB-splines. We also describe a knot insertion procedure that represents a set of MDTB-splines in terms of another set of MDTB-splines of lower smoothness. This procedure will form the foundation of the practical computation of MDTB-splines elaborated in Section
4
; it gives rise to an extraction operation that maps local Tchebycheffian Bernstein functions to the MDTB-spline basis of interest. In Section
5
we discuss some practical implementation aspects and review the general structure of the object-oriented
Matlab
toolbox.
Section
6
illustrates the
Matlab
toolbox with a selection of numerical examples, and we highlight certain pitfalls of working with ECT-spaces. We end in Section
7
with some concluding remarks.

2.
Extended Complete Tchebycheff Spaces

In this section, we define notation for ECT-spaces and recall some of their main properties. We also discuss an important basis for such spaces, the so-called Tchebycheffian Bernstein basis. We refer the reader to the survey works of
Schumaker 2007
and
Lyche
et al. 2019
for more details.

2.1.
ET-Spaces and ECT-Spaces

We start by defining two important classes of Tchebycheff spaces on a real interval
J
.

Definition 0 (Extended Tchebycheff Space).

Given an interval
J
, a space
{\mathbb{T}_{p}}(J)\subset C^{p}(J)
of dimension
p+1
(
p\geq 0
) is an extended Tchebycheff
(ET-)
space on
J
if any Hermite interpolation problem with
p+1
data on
J
has a unique solution in
{\mathbb{T}_{p}}(J)
.
In other words, for any positive integer
m
,
let
{\bar{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\bar{x}}_{m}
be distinct points in
J
and
let
d_{1},\ldots,d_{m}
be non-negative integers such that
p+1=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(d_{i}+1)
. Then,
for any set
\{f_{i,j}\in{\mathbb{R}}\}_{i=1,\ldots,m,\,j=0,\ldots,d_{i}}

there exists a unique
g\in{\mathbb{T}_{p}}(J)
such that

D^{j}g({\bar{x}}_{i})=f_{i,j},\quad i=1,\ldots,m,\quad j=0,\ldots,d_{i}.

Definition 0 (Extended Complete Tchebycheff Space).

Given an interval
J
, a space
{\mathbb{T}_{p}}(J)\subset C^{p}(J)
of dimension
p+1
is an extended complete Tchebycheff (ECT-) space if there exists a basis
\{g_{0},\ldots,g_{p}\}
of
{\mathbb{T}_{p}}(J)
such that every subspace
\langle g_{0},\ldots,g_{k}\rangle
is an ET-space on
J
for
k=0,\ldots,p
. The basis
\{g_{0},\ldots,g_{p}\}
is called an ECT-system.

A
(p+1)
-dimensional subspace of
C^{p}(J)
is an ECT-space on
J
if and only if there exists a basis
\{g_{0},\ldots,g_{p}\}
such that their
Wronskian determinants
are positive:

W[g_{0},\ldots,g_{k}](x):=\det\begin{bmatrix}g_{0}(x)&g_{1}(x)&\cdots&g_{k}(x)\\
Dg_{0}(x)&Dg_{1}(x)&\cdots&Dg_{k}(x)\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
D^{k}g_{0}(x)&D^{k}g_{1}(x)&\cdots&D^{k}g_{k}(x)\\
\end{bmatrix}>0,\quad k=0,\ldots,p,

for all
x\in J
.
This basis forms an ECT-system and gives rise to a set of positive
weight functions
defined by

(1)

w_{j}(x):=\frac{W[g_{0},\ldots,g_{j}](x)W[g_{0},\ldots,g_{j-2}](x)}{\bigl(W[g_{0},\ldots,g_{j-1}](x)\bigr)^{2}},\quad j=0,\ldots,p,

with the convention that
W[\emptyset]:=1
.
Conversely, any set of positive functions
w_{j}\in C^{p-j}(J)
,
j=0,1,\ldots,p
, generates the following ECT-system:

(2)

\begin{cases}g_{0}(x):=w_{0}(x),\\
g_{1}(x):=w_{0}(x)\int_{z}^{x}w_{1}(y_{1}){\,\mathrm{d}}y_{1},\\
\hskip 27.03003pt\vdots\\
g_{p}(x):=w_{0}(x)\int_{z}^{x}w_{1}(y_{1})\int_{z}^{y_{1}}\cdots\int_{z}^{y_{p-1}}w_{p}(y_{p}){\,\mathrm{d}}y_{p}\cdots{\,\mathrm{d}}y_{1},\end{cases}

for any fixed point
z\in J
. The functions
g_{0},\ldots,g_{p}
in (
2
) are called
generalized powers
.
From a practical point of view, it is often desired that the space
{\mathbb{T}_{p}}(J)
contains constants. This is achieved with the choice
w_{0}=1
.

Example 0.

The space
{\mathbb{P}}_{p}:=\langle 1,x,\ldots,x^{p}\rangle
of algebraic polynomials is an ECT-space on any interval of the real line.
It can be regarded as the span of the ECT-system

(3)

\left\{1,{x-z},\frac{(x-z)^{2}}{2},\ldots,\frac{(x-z)^{p}}{p!}\right\},

for any fixed point
z\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Indeed, the Wronskian determinants of this system are all equal to one. The functions in (
3
) form the classical Taylor basis for algebraic polynomials. They can be generated by the weight functions
w_{0}=\cdots=w_{p}=1
according to (
2
).

Remark 1.

A given ECT-space can be identified by different sets of weight functions; see
Lyche
et al. 2019
for details and examples. In particular, it is easy to see that
the two weight systems

w_{0},\ldots,w_{p}\quad\text{and}\quad K_{0}w_{0},\ldots,K_{p}w_{p},

where
K_{0},\ldots,K_{p}
are positive constants, identify the same ECT-space. A constructive procedure for finding all weight systems associated with a given ECT-space on a bounded closed interval is described by
Mazure 2011a
.

Remark 2.

From Definition
2
it is clear that an ECT-space of dimension
p+1
on
J
is an ET-space of dimension
p+1
on
J
. The converse is not true in general. However, if
J
is a bounded closed interval, then any ET-space of dimension
p+1
on
J
is an ECT-space on
J
; see
Mazure 2007
. In the context of Tchebycheffian spline spaces, the scope of this article, we are only interested in bounded closed intervals, so both notions are interchangeable. Further on, we use the notion ECT even if it can be weakened to ET.

2.2.
Tchebycheffian Bernstein Functions

We now set
J:=[x_{0},x_{1}]
with
x_{0}<x_{1}
. Instead of working with the generalized power basis, an alternative basis is formed by the so-called
Tchebycheffian Bernstein functions
associated with the ECT-space
{\mathbb{T}_{p}}(J)
. They are denoted with
B_{j,p}
,
j=0,\ldots,p
and can be defined recursively as follows. Let
w_{j}
,
j=0,\ldots,p
be positive weight functions generating
{\mathbb{T}_{p}}(J)
and we assume
w_{0}=1
. For
q=0,\ldots,p
and
j=0,\ldots,q
, the function
B_{j,q}
is defined at
x\in[x_{0},x_{1}]
as

(4)

B_{0,0}(x):=w_{p}(x),

and

(5)

B_{j,q}(x):=w_{p-q}(x)\cdot\begin{cases}\displaystyle 1-\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\dfrac{B_{0,q-1}(y)}{b_{0,q-1}}{\,\mathrm{d}}y,&j=0,\\[9.95863pt]
\displaystyle\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\biggl[\dfrac{B_{j-1,q-1}(y)}{b_{j-1,q-1}}-\frac{B_{j,q-1}(y)}{b_{j,q-1}}\biggr]{\,\mathrm{d}}y,&0<j<q,\\[9.95863pt]
\displaystyle\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\dfrac{B_{q-1,q-1}(y)}{b_{q-1,q-1}}{\,\mathrm{d}}y,&j=q,\end{cases}\quad q>0,

where

b_{j,q-1}:=\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{1}}B_{j,q-1}(y){\,\mathrm{d}}y.

The Bernstein functions
B_{0,p},\ldots,B_{p,p}
are non-negative, form a partition of unity, and enjoy the following end-point conditions:

(6)

\displaystyle B_{0,p}(x_{0})

\displaystyle=1,\quad D^{k}B_{j,p}(x_{0})=0,\quad k=0,\ldots,j-1,

\displaystyle B_{p,p}(x_{1})

\displaystyle=1,\quad D^{k}B_{j,p}(x_{1})=0,\quad k=0,\ldots,p-j-1.

Moreover, they are a basis of the space
{\mathbb{T}_{p}}(J)
.

Example 0.

When dealing with algebraic polynomials, see Example
3
, the Tchebycheffian Bernstein functions are nothing but the classical Bernstein polynomials, which can be explicitly expressed as

B_{j,p}(x)=\frac{p!}{j!(p-j)!}\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{x_{1}-x_{0}}\right)^{j}\left(\frac{x_{1}-x}{x_{1}-x_{0}}\right)^{p-j},\quad j=0,\ldots,p.

Remark 3.

The definition of the Tchebycheffian Bernstein basis in a given ECT-space
{\mathbb{T}_{p}}(J)
requires the existence of a set of positive weight functions
w_{j}
,
j=0,\ldots,p
, generating
{\mathbb{T}_{p}}(J)
such that
w_{0}=1
. This is guaranteed if and only if the derivative space of
{\mathbb{T}_{p}}(J)
is an ECT-space.

Remark 4.

Instead of using the recurrence relation (
4
)–(
5
), each Bernstein function
B_{j,p}
can also be computed by solving the following Hermite interpolation problem in the space
{\mathbb{T}_{p}}(J)
: for
j=0
,

B_{0,p}(x_{0})=1,\quad D^{k}B_{0,p}(x_{1})=0,\quad k=0,\ldots,p-1,

and for
j=1,\ldots,p
,

(7)

\displaystyle D^{k}B_{j,p}(x_{0})

\displaystyle=0,\quad k=0,\ldots,j-1,\quad D^{k}B_{j,p}(x_{1})=0,\quad k=0,\ldots,p-j-1,

\displaystyle D^{j}B_{j,p}(x_{0})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}D^{j}B_{k,p}(x_{0}).

Since
{\mathbb{T}_{p}}(J)
is an ECT-space, this interpolation problem has a unique solution; see Definition
1
. Note that the conditions in (
7
) require that the Bernstein functions
B_{j,p}
are computed sequentially from
j=0
to
j=p
. The order can be reversed by employing the alternative conditions

B_{p,p}(x_{1})=1,\quad D^{p-j}B_{j,p}(x_{1})=-\sum_{k=j+1}^{p}D^{p-j}B_{k,p}(x_{1}),\quad j=0,\ldots,p-1.

Any convenient basis of
{\mathbb{T}_{p}}(J)
can be used to represent the Bernstein functions.

2.3.
A Large Class of ECT-Spaces

Let
\mathcal{L}_{p}
be the linear differential operator defined by

(8)

\mathcal{L}_{p}f:=D^{p+1}f+\sum_{j=0}^{p}a_{j}D^{j}f,\quad f\in C^{p+1}(J),

with constant coefficients
a_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
J:=[x_{0},x_{1}]
.
Any operator of the form (
8
) is uniquely identified by its null-space
{\mathbb{N}}_{p}
. A fundamental set of solutions, forming a basis of
{\mathbb{N}}_{p}
, can be generated through the (higher-order) roots of the characteristic polynomial

(9)

\omega^{p+1}+\sum_{j=0}^{p}a_{j}\omega^{j},\quad\omega\in{\mathbb{C}},

associated with the differential operator in (
8
). Let
\omega=\alpha+{\mathrm{i}}\beta
be a root of order
\mu
(
\mu\geq 1
) of the polynomial in (
9
) for some
\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
{\mathrm{i}}:=\sqrt{-1}
. Then, this root generates the following fundamental subspace:

•

if
\beta=0
, then

\bigl\langle x^{i}{\mathrm{e}}^{\alpha x}:i=0,\ldots,\mu-1\bigr\rangle\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}_{p};

•

if
\beta\neq 0
, then the complex conjugate of
\omega
is also a root of order
\mu
, and

\bigl\langle x^{i}{\mathrm{e}}^{\alpha x}\cos(\beta x),x^{i}{\mathrm{e}}^{\alpha x}\sin(\beta x):i=0,\ldots,\mu-1\bigr\rangle\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}_{p}.

The fundamental subspaces related to different (non-conjugate) roots are disjoint, and all together they span the full null-space.
Note that
{\mathbb{N}}_{p}
is translation-invariant. In order to ensure that constants belong to
{\mathbb{N}}_{p}
, we have to assume that
\omega=0
is at least a first-order root of the characteristic polynomial (
9
).

The null-space
{\mathbb{N}}_{p}
is an ECT-space on
J
if and only if there exist positive weight functions
w_{j}\in C^{p-j+1}(J)
,
j=0,\ldots,p
such that

(10)

\mathcal{L}_{p}f=w_{0}\cdots w_{p}D_{p}\cdots D_{0}f,

where

D_{j}f:=D\left(\frac{f}{w_{j}}\right),\quad j=0,\ldots,p;

see
Coppel 1971
. Furthermore,
{\mathbb{N}}_{p}
is always an ECT-space on intervals of sufficiently small length.
The so-called
critical length
,
\ell_{p}>0
, is the supremum of the range of lengths of the intervals on which the space is ECT.
The critical length can be bounded from below as

\ell_{p}\geq\pi/M\in(0,+\infty],

where
M\geq 0
is the maximum of the imaginary parts of all roots of the characteristic polynomial. In view of Remark
3
, the existence of a Tchebycheffian Bernstein basis in
{\mathbb{N}}_{p}
is guaranteed if and only if the derivative space of
{\mathbb{N}}_{p}
is ECT, and hence the critical length of the derivative space is also of practical importance. This length is denoted with
\ell^{\prime}_{p}
and often called
critical length for design
.
We refer the reader to
Carnicer
et al. 2003
for a theoretical study on critical lengths of such ECT-spaces and to
Beccari
et al. 2020
for a numerical study.

Example 0.

From Example
3
we know that the polynomial space
{\mathbb{P}}_{p}
is an ECT-space generated by the weight functions
w_{0}=\cdots=w_{p}=1
. This space is the null-space of the linear differential operator
\mathcal{L}_{p}f=D^{p+1}f
, in agreement with (
10
), and its critical length is
\ell_{p}=+\infty
.

Example 0.

Consider the linear differential operator

\mathcal{L}_{p}f=D^{\mu_{0}}(D-\alpha_{1})^{\mu_{1}}\cdots(D-\alpha_{m})^{\mu_{m}}f,

with distinct real values
\alpha_{k}\neq 0
,
k=1,\ldots,m
, and positive integer values
\mu_{k}
,
k=0,\ldots,m
such that
\sum_{k=0}^{m}\mu_{k}=p+1
. Its null-space can be described by means of the fundamental set of solutions as

(11)

{\mathbb{N}}_{p}=\bigl\langle 1,x,\ldots,x^{\mu_{0}-1},{\mathrm{e}}^{\alpha_{1}x},x{\mathrm{e}}^{\alpha_{1}x},\ldots,x^{\mu_{1}-1}{\mathrm{e}}^{\alpha_{1}x},\ldots,{\mathrm{e}}^{\alpha_{m}x},x{\mathrm{e}}^{\alpha_{m}x},\ldots,x^{\mu_{m}-1}{\mathrm{e}}^{\alpha_{m}x}\bigr\rangle.

It is easily verified that

W[{\mathrm{e}}^{\alpha x},x{\mathrm{e}}^{\alpha x},\ldots,x^{\mu-1}{\mathrm{e}}^{\alpha x}](x)={\mathrm{e}}^{\alpha\mu x}\prod_{k=0}^{\mu-1}k!>0,

for any
\alpha,x\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
\mu\in{\mathbb{N}}
. More generally, the set of functions in (
11
) forms an ECT-system on any interval in
{\mathbb{R}}
, and can be used to compute a set of positive weight functions according to (
1
). This implies that the space in (
11
) is an ECT-space and its critical length is
\ell_{p}=+\infty
. The derivative space of
{\mathbb{N}}_{p}
in (
11
) is of the same form as
{\mathbb{N}}_{p}
, so its critical length is
\ell^{\prime}_{p}=+\infty
.

Example 0.

Consider the linear differential operator

\mathcal{L}_{p}f=D^{p-1}(D^{2}+\beta^{2})f,

for some real value
\beta\neq 0
and
p\geq 1
. Its null-space can be described by means of the fundamental set of solutions as

(12)

{\mathbb{N}}_{1}=\bigl\langle\cos(\beta x),\sin(\beta x)\bigr\rangle,\quad{\mathbb{N}}_{p}=\bigl\langle 1,x,\ldots,x^{p-2},\cos(\beta x),\sin(\beta x)\bigr\rangle,\quad p\geq 2.

Such space is sometimes called cycloidal space. It is an ECT-space on finite intervals of sufficiently small length. Assuming
\beta=1
, it has been shown by
Carnicer
et al. 2003
that
\ell_{p}\leq 2\pi\lfloor p/2\rfloor
for
p\geq 2
and in particular that
\ell_{1}=\pi
,
\ell_{2}=\ell_{3}=2\pi
, and
\ell_{4}=\ell_{5}\approx 8.9868
. More generally, the critical lengths are connected to roots of Bessel functions
(
Carnicer
et al. 2017
)
.
The derivative space of
{\mathbb{N}}_{p}
in (
12
) is of the same form as
{\mathbb{N}}_{p}
and its critical length is found to be
\ell^{\prime}_{p}=\ell_{p-1}
for
p\geq 2
.

3.
Multi-Degree Tchebycheffian Spline Spaces

We are interested in spaces of piecewise functions, whose pieces belong to ECT-spaces and are glued together in a certain smooth way. We show how a B-spline-like basis can be defined for such spaces, the so-called MDTB-spline basis, with similar characteristics to the classical polynomial B-spline basis. We summarize the main properties and follow the notation from
Hiemstra et al. 2020
; see also
Buchwald and
Mühlbach 2003
and
Nürnberger et al. 1984
.

3.1.
Multi-Degree Tchebycheffian Splines

Pieces of our splines shall be drawn from arbitrary ECT-spaces of possibly different dimensions. Consider a partitioning of the interval
[a,b]\subset{\mathbb{R}}
into a sequence of break points,

\Delta:=\{a=:x_{0}<x_{1}<\cdots<x_{m-1}<x_{m}:=b\}.

Furthermore, we set
J_{i}:=[x_{i-1},x_{i})
,
i=1,\ldots,m-1
, and
J_{m}:=[x_{m-1},x_{m}]
. We also define an ECT-space of dimension
p_{i}+1
on each closed interval
[x_{i-1},x_{i}]
,
i=1,\ldots,m
:

{\mathbb{T}_{p_{i}}^{(i)}}:=\bigl\langle g^{(i)}_{0},\ldots,g^{(i)}_{p_{i}}\bigl\rangle,\quad g^{(i)}_{j}\in C^{p_{i}}([x_{i-1},x_{i}]),\quad j=0,\ldots,p_{i},

where
g^{(i)}_{0},\ldots,g^{(i)}_{p_{i}}
are generalized powers defined in terms of positive weight functions
w_{j}^{(i)}\in C^{p_{i}-j}([x_{i-1},x_{i}])
,
j=0,\ldots,p_{i}
as in (
2
).
Collectively, these local ECT-spaces span the following global space:

(13)

{\mathbb{S}_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}}(\Delta):=\bigl\{{s:[a,b]\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}:}\left.s\right|_{J_{i}}\in{\mathbb{T}_{p_{i}}^{(i)}},\;i=1,\ldots,m\bigr\}.

Then, by prescribing the smoothness at the break points we can define the corresponding space of Tchebycheffian splines as follows.

Definition 0 (MDT-Spline Space).

Given the sets of integers
{\boldsymbol{p}}:=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}\}
and

(14)

{\boldsymbol{r}}:=\bigl\{r_{i}\in{\mathbb{Z}}:-1\leq r_{i}\leq\min\{p_{i},p_{i+1}\},\;i=1,\ldots,m-1,\;r_{0}=r_{m}=-1\bigr\},

we define

{\mathbb{S}_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{{\boldsymbol{r}}}}(\Delta):=\bigl\{s\in{\mathbb{S}_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}}(\Delta):D^{j}_{-}s(x_{i})=D^{j}_{+}s(x_{i}),\;j=0,\ldots,r_{i}\text{ and }i=1,\ldots,m-1\bigr\}.

This space is called multi-degree Tchebycheffian spline (MDT-spline) space.

The value
r_{i}
represents the smoothness at break point
x_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,m-1
. All smoothness conditions are linearly independent because the functions
\{g^{(i)}_{0},\ldots,g^{(i)}_{p_{i}}\}
on each interval
J_{i}
form an ECT-system.
Hence, the dimension of
{\mathbb{S}_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{{\boldsymbol{r}}}}(\Delta)
is given by

(15)

n:=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}{(p_{i+1}-r_{i})}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(p_{i}-r_{i}).

When considering different ECT-spaces on different intervals, the construction of spline spaces equipped with the same properties as classical polynomial splines (including a B-spline-like basis) requires constraints on the various ECT-spaces.

Definition 0 (Admissible Weights).

The weight systems
\{w_{j}^{(i)}:j=0,\ldots,p_{i}\}
generating the ECT-spaces
{\mathbb{T}_{p_{i}}^{(i)}}
,
i=1,\ldots,m
, are admissible for the space
{\mathbb{S}_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{{\boldsymbol{r}}}}(\Delta)
if

D_{-}^{l}w_{j}^{(i)}(x_{i})=D_{+}^{l}w_{j}^{(i+1)}(x_{i}),\quad l=0,\ldots,r_{i}-j,

for
i=1,\ldots,m-1
and
j=0,\ldots,r_{i}
.
Moreover, it is assumed that
w_{0}^{(i)}=1
for
i=1,\ldots,m
.

Remark 5.

Dealing with admissible weights gives only a sufficient condition for obtaining Tchebycheffian splines equipped with a B-spline-like basis; see
Buchwald and
Mühlbach 2003
. The simplicity of this condition and the fact that it embraces relevant classes of Tchebycheffian splines motivate our choice.
We refer the reader to
Mazure 2018
for explicit necessary and sufficient conditions for smoothly gluing together ECT-spaces of dimension
5
.

Remark 6.

Definition
1
can be easily extended to incorporate periodicity. In this case, periodic continuity constraints need to be imposed and we set
r_{0}=r_{m}=r_{{\rm per}}
for some value of
r_{{\rm per}}\geq 0
. For simplicity of exposition, we do not consider this extension in the following.

3.2.
Multi-Degree Tchebycheffian B-Splines

We now introduce basis functions for the MDT-spline space
{\mathbb{S}_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{{\boldsymbol{r}}}}(\Delta)
that possess all the characterizing properties of classical polynomial B-splines. We call the corresponding functions
multi-degree Tchebycheffian B-splines (MDTB-splines)
to stress the fact that ECT-spaces of different dimensions can be employed on different intervals, in analogy with the polynomial MDB-splines considered in
Speleers 2019
and
Toshniwal et al. 2020
.

The construction and analysis of MDTB-splines can be eased by considering two knot vectors,

(16)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{u}}{}

\displaystyle:=(u_{k})_{k=1}^{n}:=(\;\underbrace{x_{0},\;\ldots,\;x_{0}}_{p_{1}-r_{0}\text{ times}},\;\ldots,\;\underbrace{x_{i},\;\ldots,\;x_{i}}_{p_{i+1}-r_{i}\text{ times}},\;\ldots,\;\underbrace{x_{m-1},\;\ldots,\;x_{m-1}}_{p_{m}-r_{m-1}\text{ times}}\;),

(17)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{v}}{}

\displaystyle:=(v_{k})_{k=1}^{n}:=(\;\underbrace{x_{1},\;\ldots,\;x_{1}}_{p_{1}-r_{1}\text{ times}},\;\ldots,\;\underbrace{x_{i},\;\ldots,\;x_{i}}_{p_{i}-r_{i}\text{ times}},\;\ldots,\;\underbrace{x_{m},\;\ldots,\;x_{m}}_{p_{m}-r_{m}\text{ times}}\;).

Assume there exist admissible weights for the space
{\mathbb{S}_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{{\boldsymbol{r}}}}(\Delta)
. The set of MDTB-splines
\{N_{k}:k=1,\ldots,n\}
can be computed through an integral recurrence relation that is very similar to the one of the Tchebycheffian Bernstein functions in (
4
)–(
5
). To this end, we set
p:=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}p_{i}
, and we define a global set of weight functions
\{w_{j}:j=0,\ldots,p\}
by

w_{j}(x):=\begin{cases}w_{j}^{(i)}(x),&j\leq p_{i},\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\quad x\in[x_{i-1},x_{i}),\quad i=1,\ldots,m.

Then, the MDTB-splines
N_{k}=N_{k,p}
,
k=1,\ldots,n
, can be defined recursively as follows.
For
q=0,\ldots,p
and
k=p-q+1,\ldots,n
, the spline
N_{k,q}
is supported on the interval
[u_{k},v_{k-p+q}]
, and is defined at
x\in[a,b)
as

(18)

N_{k,0}(x):=\begin{cases}w_{p}(x),&x\in[x_{i-1},x_{i}),\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

and

(19)

N_{k,q}(x):=w_{p-q}(x)\int_{a}^{x}\biggl[\dfrac{N_{k,q-1}(y)}{d_{k,q-1}}-\dfrac{N_{k+1,q-1}(y)}{d_{k+1,q-1}}\biggr]{\,\mathrm{d}}y,\quad q>0,

where

d_{j,q-1}:=\int_{a}^{b}N_{j,q-1}(y){\,\mathrm{d}}y.

In the above we assumed that any undefined
N_{j,q-1}
with
j<p-q+2
or
j>n
must be regarded as the zero function,
and we used the convention that if
d_{j,q-1}=0
then

\int_{a}^{x}\dfrac{N_{j,q-1}(y)}{d_{j,q-1}}{\,\mathrm{d}}y:=\begin{cases}1,&x\geq u_{j}\text{~and~}j\leq n,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

At the right end point
b
, the spline
N_{k,q}
is defined by taking the limit from the left, that is
N_{k,q}(b):=\lim_{x\rightarrow b,x<b}N_{k,q}(x)
. We refer the reader to
Hiemstra et al. 2020
for alternative definitions.

Example 0.

Consider the discontinuous MDT-spline space
{\mathbb{S}_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}}(\Delta)
in (
13
). Each of the corresponding MDTB-splines is supported on a single interval
[x_{i-1},x_{i}]
for some
i
. Moreover, the non-zero MDTB-splines on
[x_{i-1},x_{i})
coincide on this interval with the Tchebycheffian Bernstein functions associated with the ECT-space
{\mathbb{T}_{p_{i}}^{(i)}}
.
Hence, the similarity between the definitions in (
4
)–(
5
) and (
18
)–(
19
) is not a coincidence.

The MDTB-spline basis enjoys several nice properties.

Proposition 0.

Assume there exist admissible weights for the space
{\mathbb{S}_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{{\boldsymbol{r}}}}(\Delta)
. Then, the set
\{N_{k}:k=1,\ldots,n\}
is a basis of the space
{\mathbb{S}_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{{\boldsymbol{r}}}}(\Delta)
, with the following properties:

•

local support:

\mathrm{supp}(N_{k})=[u_{k},v_{k}],\quad k=1,\ldots,n;

•

non-negative partition of unity:

N_{k}(x)\geq 0,\quad k=1,\ldots,n,\quad\sum_{k=1}^{n}N_{k}(x)=1,\quad x\in[a,b];

•

interpolation at the end points:

\displaystyle N_{1}(a)=1,\quad N_{k}(a)=0,\quad k=2,\ldots,n,

\displaystyle N_{n}(b)=1,\quad N_{k}(b)=0,\quad k=1,\ldots,n-1.

These properties are of interest in both geometric modeling and isogeometric analysis; they make the set of MDTB-splines
\{N_{k}:k=1,\ldots,n\}
a very appealing basis for the space
{\mathbb{S}_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{{\boldsymbol{r}}}}(\Delta)
in those applications. However, using the recurrence relation in (
18
)–(
19
) for their construction is a computational nightmare! Not only one has to cope with a repeated calculation of integrals, but also one has to find a proper set of weight functions that is admissible for the MDT-spline space.
Given the complexity of finding such weight functions in general (see, e.g.,
Lyche
et al. 2019
and references therein), it is wishful to avoid them in the computation at all.
An alternative way of constructing MDTB-splines is based on knot insertion, where a new set of basis functions is computed from another set of basis functions. The idea is outlined in the following section.

3.3.
Knot Insertion

Let us first observe that MDTB-splines possess super-smoothness at the knots, that is higher smoothness than the space
{\mathbb{S}_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{{\boldsymbol{r}}}}(\Delta)
requires. More precisely, if
r_{i}<\min\{p_{i},p_{i+1}\}
, then
there are only
r_{i}+3
successive MDTB-splines
N_{k}
that have a jump in their
(r_{i}+1)
-th order derivative at
x=x_{i}
, namely for
k=\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}(i),\ldots,\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}(i)+1
,
where

\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}(i):=\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}(p_{j+1}-r_{j}),\quad\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}(i):=\sum_{j=1}^{i}(p_{j}-r_{j}),\quad i=1,\ldots,m-1.

Suppose now that the knot vectors
{\boldsymbol{u}}{}
and
{\boldsymbol{v}}{}
defined in (
16
)–(
17
) are obtained from other knot vectors
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}{}}
and
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}{}}
by inserting a single knot
u=v=x_{i}\in(a,b)
, respectively, for some
0<i<m
.
The related smoothness vector is easily deduced to be
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{r}}}=(r_{0},\ldots,r_{i-1},r_{i}+1,r_{i+1},\ldots,r_{m})
; it is assumed to satisfy the same restrictions as in (
14
), and so
r_{i}+1\leq\min\{p_{i},p_{i+1}\}
. Consequently, the spline space
{\mathbb{S}_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\tilde{{\boldsymbol{r}}}}}(\Delta)
is a subspace of
{\mathbb{S}_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{{\boldsymbol{r}}}}(\Delta)
with one additional continuous derivative at
x=x_{i}
. Let
\{\tilde{N}_{k}:k=1,\ldots n-1\}
be the set of MDTB-splines of
{\mathbb{S}_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\tilde{{\boldsymbol{r}}}}}(\Delta)
.
Then,

(20)

\tilde{N}_{k}(x)=\alpha_{k}N_{k}(x)+\beta_{k+1}N_{k+1}(x),\quad k=1,\ldots n-1,

where

(i)

\alpha_{k}=1
and
\beta_{k+1}=0
if
1\leq k<\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}(i)
;

(ii)

\alpha_{k}>0
and
\beta_{k+1}=-\alpha_{k}\dfrac{l_{k}}{l_{k+1}}>0
if
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}(i)\leq k\leq\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}(i)
;

(iii)

\alpha_{k}=0
and
\beta_{k+1}=1
if
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}(i)<k<n
,

and

l_{k}:={D^{r_{i}+1}_{-}N_{k}(x_{i})-D^{r_{i}+1}_{+}N_{k}(x_{i})}.

Moreover,
\alpha_{k}+\beta_{k}=1
for
k=1,\ldots,n
. This property implies that the coefficients in item (ii) can be computed in succession as follows:

(21)

\displaystyle\alpha_{\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}(i)}=1\quad\rightarrow\quad\beta_{\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}(i)+1}

\displaystyle=-\alpha_{\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}(i)}\frac{l_{\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}(i)}}{l_{\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}(i)+1}}

\displaystyle\downarrow

\displaystyle\alpha_{\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}(i)+1}

\displaystyle=1-\beta_{\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}(i)+1}\quad\rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\beta_{\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}(i)+2}

\displaystyle=-\alpha_{\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}(i)+1}\frac{l_{\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}(i)+1}}{l_{\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}(i)+2}}

\displaystyle\downarrow

\displaystyle\ddots\quad

\displaystyle\rightarrow\quad\beta_{\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}(i)+1}=1.

The relation in (
20
) allows us to write a set of MDTB-splines in terms of another set of MDTB-splines of lower smoothness. Hence, low-smooth spaces can be used as a step-up to deal with high-smooth spaces. In this perspective, the discontinuous space
{\mathbb{S}_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}}(\Delta)
in (
13
) is useful as starting point since the corresponding MDTB-splines can be locally computed as Tchebycheffian Bernstein functions; see Example
3
.

4.
Computational Aspects

As already discussed before, the computation of MDTB-splines through the integral recurrence relation in (
18
)–(
19
) is numerical challenging. Therefore, in this section, we describe an alternative, practical construction based on knot insertion
(
Hiemstra et al. 2020
)
. The construction relies on an extraction operator that represents all MDTB-splines as linear combinations of local Tchebycheffian Bernstein functions. For its practical implementation, we can closely follow the algorithmic procedure by
Speleers 2019
developed for the specific case of polynomial MDB-splines.

4.1.
Computation of MDTB-Splines

On the
i
-th interval
J_{i}
, we have
p_{i}+1
Bernstein functions
B_{0,p_{i}}^{(i)},\ldots,B_{p_{i},p_{i}}^{(i)}
that span the local spline space
{\mathbb{T}_{p_{i}}^{(i)}}
.
In the first step, we extend them on the entire interval
[a,b]
by defining them to be zero outside
J_{i}
.
More precisely, setting

\mu(0):=0,\quad\mu(i):=\sum_{j=1}^{i}(p_{j}+1),\quad i=1,\ldots,m,

we define for
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
,
j\in\{0,\ldots,p_{i}\}
,

B_{\mu(i-1)+j+1}(x):=\begin{cases}B_{j,p_{i}}^{(i)}(x),&\text{if }x\in J_{i},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

For the sake of simplicity, we dropped the reference to the (local) degree in the notation.
From the properties of B-splines, it is clear that the functions
B_{1},\ldots,B_{\mu(m)}
are linearly independent,
form a non-negative partition of unity, and span the space
{\mathbb{S}_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}}(\Delta)
.
We arrange these basis functions in a column vector
{\boldsymbol{B}}
of length
\mu(m)
.

Now, we are looking for the set of MDTB-spline basis functions
\{N_{1},\ldots,N_{n}\}
that span the smoother space
{\mathbb{S}_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{{\boldsymbol{r}}}}(\Delta)
.
We arrange these basis functions in a column vector
{\boldsymbol{N}}
of length
n
.
Since
{\mathbb{S}_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{{\boldsymbol{r}}}}(\Delta)\subseteq{\mathbb{S}_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}}(\Delta)
, we aim to construct a matrix
{\boldsymbol{H}}
of size
n\times\mu(m)
such that

(22)

{\boldsymbol{N}}={\boldsymbol{H}}\,{\boldsymbol{B}}.

To this end, we build continuity constraints at all break points corresponding to
{\boldsymbol{r}}
and construct
{\boldsymbol{H}}
as their (left) null-space. For the computation of
{\boldsymbol{H}}
, we can apply exactly the same algorithm as described by
Speleers 2019
in the specific case of polynomial splines, thanks to the structural similarity between MDTB-splines and MDB-splines
(
Hiemstra et al. 2020
)
. For the sake of completeness and comprehension in our Tchebycheffian spline setting, we revisit the algorithm in the following.

Consider the
i
-th break point
x_{i}
for some
i\in\{1,\ldots,m-1\}
.
Let
{\boldsymbol{K}}^{(i,1)}
be a matrix of size
(p_{i}+1)\times(r_{i}+1)
,
whose
k
-th column is given by

(23)

\begin{bmatrix}0&\cdots&0&D_{-}^{k-1}B_{\mu(i)-k+1}(x_{i})&\cdots&D_{-}^{k-1}B_{\mu(i)}(x_{i})\end{bmatrix}^{T},

and let
{\boldsymbol{K}}^{(i,2)}
be a matrix of size
(p_{i+1}+1)\times(r_{i}+1)
, whose
k
-th column is given by

(24)

\begin{bmatrix}-D_{+}^{k-1}B_{\mu(i)+1}(x_{i})&\cdots&-D_{+}^{k-1}B_{\mu(i)+k}(x_{i})&0&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix}^{T}.

Note that the derivatives of the basis functions in the above matrices can be computed by evaluating the derivatives of the corresponding local Bernstein functions at the end points of their basic interval. This explains the triangular structure of both matrices; see (
6
).

{\boldsymbol{K}}^{(i)}

C^{r_{i}}

•

{\boldsymbol{f}}

{\boldsymbol{f}}\,{\boldsymbol{B}}
is going to be
C^{r_{i}}
across
x_{i}
. Therefore, once all the matrices
{\boldsymbol{K}}^{(i)}
have been assembled, the only remaining step is the construction of
{\boldsymbol{H}}
such that it spans their left null-spaces. The matrix
{\boldsymbol{H}}
is called
multi-degree spline extraction operator
, and we employ the algorithm in Figure

for its construction.
The algorithm addresses a single continuity constraint at a time, so this increases the smoothness of the basis functions (obtained by (
22
)) gradually.
The matrix
{\boldsymbol{L}}
keeps track of the remaining continuity constraints for the basis functions built so far.

We now focus on the left null-space computation of a column of the continuity constraint matrix
{\boldsymbol{L}}
.
Any basis of the null-space would lead to a valid basis of the space
{\mathbb{S}_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{{\boldsymbol{r}}}}(\Delta)
using the previously described procedure. However, we are not just interested in any basis, but are looking for the MDTB-spline basis. We employ the algorithm in Figure

for its construction.
It strives to build the sparsest possible left null-space of column vector
{\boldsymbol{l}}
, containing the next continuity constraint in the matrix
{\boldsymbol{L}}
. This is equivalent to building the conversion matrix between two MDTB-spline bases of different smoothness as in (
20
). Hence, the algorithm can follow the pattern described in (

).

Remark 7.

The described extraction mechanism can be efficiently encoded by exploiting the sparsity of the involved matrices, similar to the polynomial multi-degree spline case
(
Speleers 2019
, Remark 7)
. Furthermore, imposing periodicity can be easily built into the procedure by circularly shifting the rows of the extraction matrix such that the periodic continuity constraints behave like continuity constraints at an interior segment join
(
Speleers 2019
, Remark 9)
.

4.2.
Computation of Bernstein Functions

The only missing aspect in the computation of the extraction matrix
{\boldsymbol{H}}
described in Section
4.1
is the explicit construction of the matrices
{\boldsymbol{K}}^{(i,1)}
and
{\boldsymbol{K}}^{(i,2)}
in (
23
)–(
24
).
This requires the computation of derivatives of Tchebycheffian Bernstein functions at the end points of their basic interval. Furthermore, once
{\boldsymbol{H}}
has been computed, evaluation and manipulation of MDTB-splines boils down to the equivalent operations on Tchebycheffian Bernstein functions. Hence, we now focus on the practical computation of Bernstein functions.

Consider the ECT-space
{\mathbb{T}_{p}}(J)
on the interval
J:=[x_{0},x_{1}]
. Suppose we know a set of functions
\{\phi_{0},\ldots,\phi_{p}\}
that forms a basis of
{\mathbb{T}_{p}}(J)
. We arrange these basis functions in a column vector
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
of length
p+1
. We are looking for the set of Bernstein functions
\{B_{0,p},\ldots,B_{p,p}\}
that span the same space, and we arrange these basis functions in a column vector
{\boldsymbol{B}}
of length
p+1
. Then, we aim to construct a conversion matrix
{\boldsymbol{C}}
of size
(p+1)\times(p+1)
such that

(25)

{\boldsymbol{B}}={\boldsymbol{C}}\,{\boldsymbol{\phi}}.

For the computation of
{\boldsymbol{C}}
, we can rely on the end-point interpolation properties of Bernstein functions in (
6
) and solve a specific Hermite interpolation problem in
{\mathbb{T}_{p}}(J)
for each Bernstein function; see Remark
4
. Let
{\boldsymbol{M}}_{0}
and
{\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}
be the transposed Wronskian matrix of the basis
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
evaluated at the end points
x_{0}
and
x_{1}
, respectively, so

{\boldsymbol{M}}_{0}:=({\boldsymbol{W}}(x_{0}))^{T},\quad{\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}:=({\boldsymbol{W}}(x_{1}))^{T},

where

{\boldsymbol{W}}(x):=\begin{bmatrix}\phi_{0}(x)&\phi_{1}(x)&\cdots&\phi_{p}(x)\\
D\phi_{0}(x)&D\phi_{1}(x)&\cdots&D\phi_{p}(x)\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
D^{p}\phi_{0}(x)&D^{p}\phi_{1}(x)&\cdots&D^{p}\phi_{p}(x)\\
\end{bmatrix}.

Then, we can employ the algorithm in Figure
3
for the construction of
{\boldsymbol{C}}
. Note that the use of matrix inverses is just for notational convenience, and they should not be computed explicitly! Instead of inverting a matrix, a linear system should be solved by means of standard numerical linear algebra routines. These linear systems are unisolvent by the properties of the Bernstein functions and ECT-spaces.

end-point derivative matrices
{\boldsymbol{M}}_{0}
and
{\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}
(size:
(p+1)\times(p+1)
)

output :
conversion matrix
{\boldsymbol{C}}

{\boldsymbol{C}}\leftarrow\mbox{zero matrix}
(size:
(p+1)\times(p+1)
);

{\boldsymbol{s}}\leftarrow\mbox{zero row vector}
(size:
1\times(p+1)
);

{\boldsymbol{e}}\leftarrow\mbox{first canonical row vector}
(size:
1\times(p+1)
);

{\boldsymbol{C}}(p+1,:)\leftarrow{\boldsymbol{e}}\,[{\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}(:,1),{\boldsymbol{M}}_{0}(:,1\,\mbox{:}\,p)]^{-1}
;

for

i\leftarrow 2

to

p

do

{\boldsymbol{s}}\leftarrow{\boldsymbol{s}}+{\boldsymbol{C}}(p-i+3,:)
;

{\boldsymbol{c}}\leftarrow\mbox{zero row vector}
(size:
1\times(p+1)
);

{\boldsymbol{c}}(i)\leftarrow-{\boldsymbol{s}}\,{\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}(:,i)
;

{\boldsymbol{C}}(p-i+2,:)\leftarrow{\boldsymbol{c}}\,[{\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}(:,1\,\mbox{:}\,i),{\boldsymbol{M}}_{0}(:,1\,\mbox{:}\,(p-i+1))]^{-1}
;

end for

{\boldsymbol{C}}(1,:)\leftarrow{\boldsymbol{e}}\,[{\boldsymbol{M}}_{0}(:,1),{\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}(:,1\,\mbox{:}\,p)]^{-1}
;

end

The choice of the basis

The construction and use of MDTB-splines through the previously described extraction procedure has been implemented in a small object-oriented
Matlab
toolbox that is available through the CALGO library. In this section, we give an overview of its class structure.
Full details of the facilities available from the
Matlab
toolbox may be found in the user manual that accompanies the software.

5.1.
Object-Oriented Implementation

The class diagram of the MDTB-spline toolbox is shown in Figure
4
. The central class in the toolbox is the class
MDTB_patch
, which provides functionality for computing the MDTB-spline extraction matrix, both in the periodic and non-periodic spline setting. Furthermore, it allows for evaluating, differentiating and visualizing the obtained MDTB-spline basis functions and any MDT-spline function represented in such basis.

The class
MDTB_patch
is built upon the class
TB_patch
, which mainly identifies a local ECT-space. According to the definition of MDT-spline spaces (see Definition
1
), each object of type
MDTB_patch
contains a (heterogeneous) array of objects of type
TB_patch
. In order to reflect the heterogeneous nature of ECT-spaces, the class
TB_patch
is
abstract
. The functionality of evaluation and differentiation of the Bernstein basis functions is delegated to (specialized) child classes, as well as the option to provide a separate implementation for end-point derivatives because of their importance in the MDTB-spline framework.
Manipulation of functions represented in the Bernstein basis and their visualization is handled in the class
TB_patch
itself.

As indicated in the class diagram in Figure
4
, there are several child classes of the class
TB_patch
available in the toolbox. They provide functionality to work with general ECT-spaces based on constant-coefficient linear differential operators (class
TB_patch_tcheb
), algebraic polynomial spaces (classes
TB_patch_poly
and
TB_patch_spline
), other polynomial-type spaces (classes
TB_patch_pexp
and
TB_patch_ptrig
), and generalized polynomial spaces (classes
TB_patch_gexp
and
TB_patch_gtrig
). In Section
5.2
we discuss these example spaces and their implementations in more detail.
Thanks to the object-oriented structure of the toolbox, other ECT-spaces and/or specialized implementations can be easily incorporated by adding new child classes of
TB_patch
— they need to implement the three methods
TB_evaluation_all
,
TB_differentiation_all
, and
TB_diffend_all
).

The purpose of the class
TB_patch_multi
is to encapsulate an object of the class
MDTB_patch
and a non-periodic extraction matrix such that the corresponding multi-degree spline space can be treated as if it is an instance of type
TB_patch
. In this way, already constructed multi-degree spline spaces can be embedded into larger multi-degree spline spaces without the need for recomputing them.

The classes
MDTB_patch_tcheb
,
MDTB_patch_poly
,
MDTB_patch_ppoly
, and
MDTB_patch_gpoly
are factory classes for
MDTB_patch
. They provide simplified functionality to initialize objects of type
MDTB_patch
consisting of local ECT-spaces based on constant-coefficient linear differential operators, algebraic polynomial spaces, other polynomial-type spaces, and generalized polynomial spaces, respectively.

Remark 9.

This MDTB-spline toolbox is a redesigned, object-oriented and extended version of the MDB-spline toolbox provided by
Speleers 2019
, also implemented in
Matlab
. The latter toolbox only deals with polynomial MDB-splines, and its functionality is essentially covered by the classes
TB_patch
,
TB_patch_spline
,
MDTB_patch
and
MDTB_patch_poly
. Even though the MDB-spline toolbox is not object-oriented, the syntax of the new MDTB-spline toolbox is (almost fully) compatible with it and all its function calls are still available (under the minor restriction that the names start now with “
(MD)TB_*
” instead of “
(MD)B_*
”, so as to emphasize the Tchebycheffian nature of the extension).

5.2.
Implementation of ECT-Spaces

Here we focus on the large class of ECT-spaces described in Section
2.3
.
We refer the reader to
Róth 2019
for its practical relevance.
Consider the null-space
{\mathbb{N}}_{p}
of a constant-coefficient linear differential operator
\mathcal{L}_{p}
as in (
8
) with
p\geq 1
. We assume that
{\mathbb{N}}_{p}
is an ECT-space (so the interval
J:=[x_{0},x_{1}]
is chosen sufficiently small) and that
\omega=0
is at least a first-order root of the characteristic polynomial (
9
). This ECT-space can be uniquely identified by means of the triples

(26)

(0,0,\mu_{0}),\quad(\alpha_{1},\beta_{1},\mu_{1}),\quad\ldots,\quad(\alpha_{m},\beta_{m},\mu_{m}),

representing the different roots
\omega_{k}=\alpha_{k}+{\mathrm{i}}\beta_{k}
of order
\mu_{k}
(
\mu_{k}\geq 1
) of the polynomial (
9
).
Such space is translation-invariant. When we want to emphasize the specific parameters in (
26
), the space will be denoted with
{\mathbb{N}}_{p}^{(\alpha_{1},\beta_{1},\mu_{1}),\ldots,(\alpha_{m},\beta_{m},\mu_{m})}
. For simplicity of notation, we assume that the complex conjugate roots are excluded from (
26
).

The basis
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
in (
25
) depends on the type of roots in (
26
) and is chosen as follows. We differentiate between four types of roots for implementational efficiency. A given root
\omega=\alpha+{\mathrm{i}}\beta
of order
\mu
gives rise to the following basis functions for
i=0,\ldots,\mu-1
:

•

if
\alpha=0
and
\beta=0
, then

\phi_{i}(x)=\frac{(x-x_{0})^{i}}{i!},\quad D\phi_{i}(x)=\phi_{i-1}(x);

•

if
\alpha\neq 0
and
\beta=0
, then

\phi_{i}(x)=\frac{(x-x_{0})^{i}}{i!}{\mathrm{e}}^{\alpha(x-x_{0})},\quad D\phi_{i}(x)=\phi_{i-1}(x)+\alpha\phi_{i}(x);

•

if
\alpha=0
and
\beta\neq 0
, then

\phi_{2i}(x)=\frac{(x-x_{0})^{i}}{i!}\cos(\beta(x-x_{0})),\quad\phi_{2i+1}(x)=\frac{(x-x_{0})^{i}}{i!}\sin(\beta(x-x_{0})),

and their derivatives

D\phi_{2i}(x)=\phi_{2i-2}(x)-\beta\phi_{2i+1}(x),\quad D\phi_{2i+1}(x)=\phi_{2i-1}(x)+\beta\phi_{2i}(x);

•

if
\alpha\neq 0
and
\beta\neq 0
, then

\ \ \ \phi_{2i}(x)=\frac{(x-x_{0})^{i}}{i!}{\mathrm{e}}^{\alpha(x-x_{0})}\cos(\beta(x-x_{0})),\quad\phi_{2i+1}(x)=\frac{(x-x_{0})^{i}}{i!}{\mathrm{e}}^{\alpha(x-x_{0})}\sin(\beta(x-x_{0})),

and their derivatives

\ \ \ D\phi_{2i}(x)=\phi_{2i-2}(x)+\alpha\phi_{2i}(x)-\beta\phi_{2i+1}(x),\quad D\phi_{2i+1}(x)=\phi_{2i-1}(x)+\alpha\phi_{2i+1}(x)+\beta\phi_{2i}(x).

In the expressions of the derivatives it is assumed that
\phi_{i}(x)=0
for
i<0
.
These basis functions are chosen so that the conversion matrix
{\boldsymbol{C}}
will be invariant under translations of the interval
J
. Moreover, when evaluating all basis functions (and their derivatives) simultaneously, a lot of arithmetic calculations can be shared due to their particular structure.

This class of ECT-spaces is extremely flexible, as there are plenty of (shape) parameters to be chosen in (
26
). However, this flexibility comes at a risk because it is very easy to select a combination of shape parameters that leads to numerical instabilities, e.g., when choosing two roots that are different but very close to each other. Such instabilities will lead to linear systems that are ill-conditioned, and hence their numerical solution might not be accurate anymore; see Section
6.2
for a discussion about instabilities.
In the
Matlab
toolbox, the class
TB_patch_tcheb
deals with such general ECT-spaces.

In the following three subsections, we discuss subclasses of ECT-spaces of particular interest, for which an improved implementation has been provided that is more efficient and/or more robust. In the
Matlab
toolbox, they have been addressed in separate
Matlab
classes (see the class diagram in Figure
4
) and should be selected whenever possible.

Remark 10.

The same Bernstein functionality as in the
Matlab
class
TB_patch_tcheb
has also been implemented in the C++ library by
Róth 2019
. However, the implementation of the class
TB_patch_tcheb
differs at the following points:

•

The Bernstein basis is directly computed from the basis
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
in (
25
), instead of first building an intermediate bicanonical basis and then obtaining the Bernstein functions via a particular LU-decomposition of the corresponding Wronskian matrix.

•

The (high-order) derivatives of the basis functions in
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
are computed by means of the above recurrence relations instead of application of the general Leibniz rule.

•

Since all Bernstein basis functions are treated simultaneously (for their construction, evaluation, and differentiation), a lot of arithmetic calculations can be shared. Moreover, all parts of the
Matlab
code are highly vectorized. Finally, because of their importance in the MDTB-spline framework, end-point derivatives are implemented separately, resulting in additional computational speed and stability.

5.2.1.
Algebraic Polynomial Spaces

Algebraic polynomial spaces are the most established ECT-spaces (see Examples
3
and
5
). In this case, the Tchebycheffian Bernstein functions are the classical Bernstein polynomials (see Example
4
). Since we know a simple explicit expression for them, it is not necessary to use the conversion procedure described in Section
4.2
. Alternatively, they can be evaluated at
x\in[x_{0},x_{1}]
through the following stable recurrence relation:

B_{j,p}(x)=\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{x_{1}-x_{0}}\right)B_{j-1,p-1}(x)+\left(\frac{x_{1}-x}{x_{1}-x_{0}}\right)B_{j,p-1}(x),\quad p\geq 1,

with
B_{j,p}(x)=0
for
j<0
,
j>p
and
B_{0,0}(x)=1
.
Their derivatives can be easily computed as

DB_{j,p}(x)=\frac{p}{x_{1}-x_{0}}\bigl(B_{j-1,p-1}(x)-B_{j,p-1}(x)\bigr).

This has been implemented in the
Matlab
class
TB_patch_poly
.

When dealing with spline spaces consisting of local algebraic polynomial spaces of the same degree
p
, then the MDTB-splines are nothing but the classical polynomial B-splines. In this case, instead of applying the extraction process described in Section
4.1
, one can also use the following stable recurrence relation for their computation. Given an
open knot vector
of the form

(27)

{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}:=(\xi_{k})_{k=1}^{n+p+1}:=(\;\underbrace{x_{0},\;\ldots,\;x_{0}}_{p-r_{0}\text{ times}},\;\ldots,\;\underbrace{x_{i},\;\ldots,\;x_{i}}_{p-r_{i}\text{ times}},\;\ldots,\;\underbrace{x_{m},\;\ldots,\;x_{m}}_{p-r_{m}\text{ times}}),

the polynomial B-splines
N_{k,p}
,
k=1,\ldots,n
can be evaluated at
x\in[x_{0},x_{m})
as

N_{k,p}(x)=\frac{x-\xi_{k}}{\xi_{k+p}-\xi_{k}}\,N_{k,p-1}(x)+\frac{\xi_{k+p+1}-x}{\xi_{k+p+1}-\xi_{k+1}}\,N_{k+1,p-1}(x),

starting from

N_{k,0}(x)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }\xi_{k}\leq x<\xi_{k+1},\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

and under the convention that fractions with zero denominator have value zero.
Their derivatives can be computed as

D_{+}N_{k,p}(x)=p\left(\frac{N_{k,p-1}(x)}{\xi_{k+p}-\xi_{k}}-\frac{N_{k+1,p-1}(x)}{\xi_{k+p+1}-\xi_{k+1}}\right).

Such spline space can be used as local space in the MDTB-spline setting, and will be more efficient than working with separate polynomial spaces of the same degree
(
Speleers 2019
, Remark 3)
. This has been implemented in the
Matlab
class
TB_patch_spline
; its implementation has been borrowed from the MDB-spline toolbox developed by
Speleers 2019
.

Remark 11.

The MDTB-spline knot vectors in (
16
)–(
17
) relate to the classical B-spline knot vector in (
27
) as
u_{k}=\xi_{k}
and
v_{k}=\xi_{k+p+1}
,
k=1,\ldots,n
.

5.2.2.
Other ECT-spaces with stable recurrence relations

Besides algebraic polynomial spaces, there are few other classes of ECT-spaces for which simple and stable recurrence relations are known for the evaluation of the Tchebycheffian Bernstein functions and their derivatives.
In particular, in the
Matlab
toolbox we consider two classes of polynomial-type spaces of the form

{\mathbb{P}}_{p}^{\mathfrak{u},\mathfrak{v}}:=\bigl\langle 1,\mathfrak{u}(x),\mathfrak{v}(x),\mathfrak{u}(2x),\mathfrak{v}(2x),\ldots,\mathfrak{u}(qx),\mathfrak{v}(qx)\bigr\rangle,\quad p=2q\geq 2,

where
\mathfrak{u},\mathfrak{v}
are chosen to be exponential or trigonometric functions.
The first type of space corresponds to the null-space in Example
6
with
p=m=2q
,
\mu_{k}=1
,
k=0,\ldots,2q
, and
\alpha_{2k-1}=-\alpha_{2k}=k\alpha
,
k=1,\ldots,q
, and we denote it with

\displaystyle{\mathbb{PE}}_{p}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle:={\mathbb{N}}_{p}^{(\alpha,0,1),(-\alpha,0,1),\ldots,(q\alpha,0,1),(-q\alpha,0,1)}=\bigl\langle 1,{\mathrm{e}}^{\alpha x},{\mathrm{e}}^{-\alpha x},{\mathrm{e}}^{2\alpha x},{\mathrm{e}}^{-2\alpha x},\ldots,{\mathrm{e}}^{q\alpha x},{\mathrm{e}}^{-q\alpha x}\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle\;=\bigl\langle 1,\cosh(\alpha x),\sinh(\alpha x),\cosh(2\alpha x),\sinh(2\alpha x),\ldots,\cosh(q\alpha x),\sinh(q\alpha x)\bigr\rangle.

The second type of space is the null-space

{\mathbb{PT}}_{p}^{\beta}:={\mathbb{N}}_{p}^{(0,\beta,1),\ldots,(0,q\beta,1)}=\bigl\langle 1,\cos(\beta x),\sin(\beta x),\cos(2\beta x),\sin(2\beta x),\ldots,\cos(q\beta x),\sin(q\beta x)\bigr\rangle.

They are both invariant under translations and reflections.

To address these two spaces, we set

\mathfrak{u}(x)=\cosh(\alpha x),\quad\mathfrak{v}(x)=\sinh(\alpha x)

and

\mathfrak{u}(x)=\cos(\beta x),\quad\mathfrak{v}(x)=\sin(\beta x),

respectively.
Then, we can evaluate the Tchebycheffian Bernstein functions at

x\in[x_{0},x_{1}]
through the following stable recurrence relation:

B_{j,p}(x)=B_{2,2}(x)\,B_{j-2,p-2}(x)+B_{1,2}(x)\,B_{j-1,p-2}(x)+B_{0,2}(x)\,B_{j,p-2}(x),\quad p\geq 4,

with
B_{j,p}(x)=0
for
j<0
,
j>p
. Moreover, we have

B_{0,2}(x)=\sigma_{0,2}\,\mathfrak{v}^{2}\left(\frac{x_{1}-x}{2}\right),\quad B_{1,2}(x)=\sigma_{1,2}\,\mathfrak{v}\left(\frac{x_{1}-x}{2}\right)\mathfrak{v}\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{2}\right),\quad B_{2,2}(x)=\sigma_{2,2}\,\mathfrak{v}^{2}\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{2}\right),

and

\sigma_{0,2}=\sigma_{2,2}=1\Big/\mathfrak{v}^{2}\left(\frac{x_{1}-x_{0}}{2}\right),\quad\sigma_{1,2}=2\,\mathfrak{u}\left(\frac{x_{1}-x_{0}}{2}\right)\Big/\mathfrak{v}^{2}\left(\frac{x_{1}-x_{0}}{2}\right).

Their derivatives can be computed as

DB_{j,p}(x)=\tau\left(j\frac{\sigma_{2,2}\sigma_{j,p}}{\sigma_{j-1,p}}\,B_{j-1,p}(x)-(q-j)\sigma_{1,2}\,B_{j,p}(x)-(p-j)\frac{\sigma_{0,2}\sigma_{j,p}}{\sigma_{j+1,p}}\,B_{j+1,p}(x)\right),

where

\tau=\frac{D\mathfrak{v}(0)}{2}\,\mathfrak{v}\left(\frac{x_{1}-x_{0}}{2}\right),

and

\sigma_{j,p}=\sigma_{2,2}\,\sigma_{j-2,p-2}+\sigma_{1,2}\,\sigma_{j-1,p-2}+\sigma_{0,2}\,\sigma_{j,p-2},\quad p\geq 4,

with
\sigma_{j,p}=0
for
j<0
,
j>p
. More details can be found in
Sánchez-Reyes 1998
and
Shen and Wang 2005
.
These recurrence relations have been implemented in the
Matlab
classes
TB_patch_pexp
and
TB_patch_ptrig
for the exponential and trigonometric cases, respectively.

5.2.3.
Generalized Polynomial Spaces

Generalized polynomial spaces are an important class of ECT-spaces. They can be seen as the minimal extension of algebraic polynomial spaces still offering a wide variety of flexibility. They are defined as

(28)

{\mathbb{G}}_{p}^{\mathfrak{u},\mathfrak{v}}:=\bigl\langle 1,x,\ldots,x^{p-2},\mathfrak{u}(x),\mathfrak{v}(x)\bigr\rangle,\quad p\geq 2,

for given functions
\mathfrak{u},\mathfrak{v}\in C^{p}(J)
such that
\langle D^{p-1}\mathfrak{u},D^{p-1}\mathfrak{v}\rangle
is an ECT-space on
J:=[x_{0},x_{1}]
.
We refer the reader to
Costantini
et al. 2005
and
Lyche
et al. 2019
for more details.
Two practically relevant instances of (
28
) are obtained by choosing
\mathfrak{u},\mathfrak{v}
to be exponential or trigonometric functions. They are implemented in the
Matlab
classes
TB_patch_gexp
and
TB_patch_gtrig
, respectively.

First, we consider the null-space in Example
6
with
p\geq 2
,
m=2
,
\mu_{0}=p-1
,
\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}=1
, and
\alpha_{1}=-\alpha_{2}=\alpha
, and denote it with

{\mathbb{GE}}_{p}^{\alpha}:={\mathbb{N}}_{p}^{(\alpha,0,1),(-\alpha,0,1)}=\bigl\langle 1,x,\ldots,x^{p-2},{\mathrm{e}}^{\alpha x},{\mathrm{e}}^{-\alpha x}\bigr\rangle=\bigl\langle 1,x,\ldots,x^{p-2},\cosh(\alpha x),\sinh(\alpha x)\bigr\rangle.

This is the algebraic polynomial space of degree
p-2
, enriched with two exponential functions. It is invariant under translations and reflections. For values of
\alpha
away from zero, we take the basis
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
in (
25
) as

\phi_{i}(x)=\alpha^{i}\frac{(x-x_{0})^{i}}{i!},\quad i=0,\ldots,p-2,\quad\phi_{p-1}(x)={\mathrm{e}}^{\alpha(x-x_{0})},\quad\phi_{p}(x)={\mathrm{e}}^{-\alpha(x-x_{0})}.

Note that
D\phi_{0}=0
,
D\phi_{i}=\alpha\phi_{i-1}
,
i=1,\ldots,p-2
,
D\phi_{p-1}=\alpha\phi_{p-1}
and
D\phi_{p}=-\alpha\phi_{p}
. This allows us to scale the
i
-th column of both matrices
{\boldsymbol{M}}_{0}
and
{\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}
in Figure
3
by the factor
\alpha^{-i}
to balance better the overall magnitude of the entries when solving the involved linear systems.
Unfortunately, the above choice of basis becomes numerically unstable when
\alpha
is close to zero. Therefore, taking into account that
\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 0}{\mathbb{GE}}_{p}^{\alpha}={\mathbb{P}}_{p}
, we employ the following basis for values of
\alpha
close to zero:

\phi_{i}(x)=\frac{(x-x_{0})^{i}}{i!},\quad i=0,\ldots,p-2,\quad\phi_{i}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{+\infty}\alpha^{2j}\frac{(x-x_{0})^{i+2j}}{({i+2j})!},\quad i=p-1,p.

The choice of
\phi_{p-1}
and
\phi_{p}
is based on the Taylor expansion of
\cosh(\alpha(x-x_{0}))
and
\sinh(\alpha(x-x_{0}))
around
x=x_{0}
. Of course, in practice, we need to truncate these series; the order of truncation should be based on the required level of accuracy and the value of
\alpha
. This dual choice of basis has been implemented in the
Matlab
class
TB_patch_gexp
.

Second, we consider the null-space in Example
7
with
p\geq 2
, and denote it with

{\mathbb{GT}}_{p}^{\beta}:={\mathbb{N}}_{p}^{(0,\beta,1)}=\bigl\langle 1,x,\ldots,x^{p-2},\cos(\beta x),\sin(\beta x)\bigr\rangle.

This is the algebraic polynomial space of degree
p-2
, enriched with two trigonometric functions. It is invariant under translations and reflections. The implementation of the corresponding Bernstein basis functions can be done in a manner that is very similar to the exponential case. For values of
\beta
away from zero, we take

\phi_{i}(x)=\beta^{i}\frac{(x-x_{0})^{i}}{i!},\quad i=0,\ldots,p-2,\quad\phi_{p-1}(x)=\cos(\beta(x-x_{0})),\quad\phi_{p}(x)=\sin(\beta(x-x_{0})),

while for values of
\beta
close to zero, we take

\phi_{i}(x)=\frac{(x-x_{0})^{i}}{i!},\quad i=0,\ldots,p-2,\quad\phi_{i}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{+\infty}(-1)^{j}\alpha^{2j}\frac{(x-x_{0})^{i+2j}}{({i+2j})!},\quad i=p-1,p.

This dual choice of basis has been implemented in the
Matlab
class
TB_patch_gtrig
.

6.2.
Instabilities of ECT-Spaces

As mentioned in Section
5.2
, the user of the
Matlab
toolbox has to be aware of numerical instabilities in certain choices of ECT-spaces. We refer the reader to
Róth 2019
for a detailed description and illustration of instabilities in the class of ECT-spaces described in Section
2.3
.

Possible sources of numerical instabilities are:

•

too small intervals
[x_{0},x_{1}]
, so
x_{1}\in(x_{0},x_{0}+\varepsilon)
;

•

roots close to each other, so
\omega_{k}=\alpha_{k}+{\mathrm{i}}\beta_{k}
and
\omega_{k+1}\in(\alpha_{k}-\varepsilon,\alpha_{k}+\varepsilon)+{\mathrm{i}}(\beta_{k}-\varepsilon,\beta_{k}+\varepsilon)
;

•

large exponential shape parameters, so
\alpha_{k}(x_{1}-x_{0})\gg 0
;

•

high-dimensional spaces, so
p\gg 0
.

The first two bullets give rise to linear systems to be solved with similar (or almost identical) rows or columns in the conversion procedure described in Section
4.2
, so these are (severely) ill-conditioned. The third bullet implies very high values of the function
{\mathrm{e}}^{\alpha_{k}x}
and its derivatives. This leads to highly unbalanced entries in the involved linear systems, implying also ill-conditionings, and/or turns into numerical overflow.
The last bullet indicates that very high (end-point) derivatives are involved in the computation, and these will amplify the negative impact of the previous three sources.

Whenever a nearly singular linear system has to be solved in the
Matlab
toolbox, a warning will be thrown. Then, it is up to the user to ignore the result or not as it may be inaccurate.
The following checks are simple to perform and might give a numerical validation of the obtained result:

•

Check the non-negative partition of unity property of the computed Tchebycheffian Bernstein basis functions in a set of points distributed over the interval
[x_{0},x_{1}]
. The basis functions can be simultaneously evaluated through the
Matlab
method
TB_evaluation_all
. The resulting matrix should have non-negative entries and its column sum should give values (approximately) equal to one.

•

Do a visual inspection of the smoothness of the Tchebycheffian Bernstein basis functions; instabilities result in “noisy” functions. The basis functions can be simultaneously visualized through the
Matlab
method
TB_visualization_all
.

As pointed out by
Róth 2019
, given the infinitely many ECT-spaces with a vast possibility of inner structure, there is no general recipe for the range of shape parameters for which the outputs of the proposed algorithms are guaranteed to be accurate enough — this should be determined empirically by the user on a case-by-case basis. Nevertheless, a rule of thumb is to avoid high degrees and very small intervals.

6.3.
Critical Lengths for Design of ECT-Spaces

The choice of the interval
J:=[x_{0},x_{1}]
needs to be done in conjunction with the shape parameters in (
26
), in order to ensure that the null-space
{\mathbb{N}}_{p}
is an ECT-space possessing a Tchebycheffian Bernstein basis
\{B_{0,p},\ldots,B_{p,p}\}
.
The critical length for design
\ell^{\prime}_{p}
guarantees the existence of such a basis, and so one has to choose
0<x_{1}-x_{0}<\ell^{\prime}_{p}
. The
Matlab
toolbox works under the assumption that the interval of an ECT-space is always chosen within the critical length for design.

In general, the determination of the critical length for design is theoretically challenging
(
Brilleaud and
Mazure 2012
;
Carnicer
et al. 2003
;
Carnicer
et al. 2017
)
and one may need to rely on numerical techniques for its investigation
(
Beccari
et al. 2020
)
. Our
Matlab
toolbox could be of some help for this purpose as well. In the search for
\ell^{\prime}_{p}
, one could use the evaluation routines for the Bernstein basis to estimate the minimum positive value
\ell
such that

(29)

\min_{0\leq j\leq p}\min_{x\in[0,\ell]}B_{j,p}(x)

becomes negative. This condition can be approximately verified by tabulating the values of all Bernstein basis functions over a fine grid over
[0,\ell]
for a sequence of increasing values of
\ell
. Once the minimum function value becomes negative (taking into account a negative tolerance), the previous value of
\ell
in the sequence could be a possible candidate for the critical length for design. Of course, this approach assumes that there do not occur numerical instabilities in the computation of the Bernstein basis functions (see Section
6.2
). Moreover,
it cannot give a conclusive result because the condition in (
29
) only provides an upper bound for
\ell^{\prime}_{p}
. Hence, the obtained numerical guess should still undergo a theoretical validation to be certain of the correct value of the critical length for design.

7.
Conclusion

In this article, we have presented a practical framework to deal with Tchebycheffian splines. These are splines with pieces drawn from different ECT-spaces (of possibly different dimensions) that are glued together smoothly. Under quite mild assumptions, they can be represented in terms of a so-called MDTB-spline basis, which enjoys properties similar to the classical polynomial B-spline basis. Thanks to the wide variety of ECT-spaces, such splines allow for an extraordinary flexibility in shape that may be optimally exploited in applications such as geometric modeling and isogeometric analysis.

We have detailed a simple procedure to built an extraction operator that represents all MDTB-splines as a linear combination of Tchebycheffian Bernstein functions related to each of the local ECT-spaces. With this procedure in hand, the complexity of computing and manipulating MDTB-splines is reduced to the same operations on Tchebycheffian Bernstein functions. The latter are, of course, easier to implement, but still require careful treatment in general as numerical instabilities are often lurking for certain (classes of) ECT-spaces; see Section
6.2
.

Following this procedure, we have implemented an object-oriented
Matlab
toolbox for dealing with MDTB-splines — it is available through the CALGO library.
This is a redesigned and extended version of the MDB-spline toolbox by
Speleers 2019
, developed for (algebraic) polynomial splines. The new toolbox can handle splines with pieces drawn from the large class of ECT-spaces that are null-spaces of constant-coefficient linear differential operators. This class covers most of the practically relevant ECT-spaces in geometric modeling
(
Róth 2019
)
and isogeometric analysis
(
Manni
et al. 2017
)
. Each of these spaces can be identified by means of the roots of the characteristic polynomial of the corresponding linear differential operator. Once all local ECT-spaces of the MDT-spline space of interest have been specified by the user, the toolbox allows for an easy computation and manipulation of the MDTB-splines through the
Matlab
class
MDTB_patch
.

At the same time, the treatment of ECT-spaces in the toolbox is valuable on its own, with the computation and manipulation of Tchebycheffian Bernstein functions. The general
Matlab
class
TB_patch_tcheb
gives comparable results to the state-of-the-art C++ library recently developed by
Róth 2019
— the
Matlab
implementation is slightly different however; see Remark
10
. In addition, the toolbox offers specialized (more efficient and/or more robust) implementations for algebraic polynomial spaces, other polynomial-type spaces, and generalized polynomial spaces — important spaces in practical applications
(
Manni
et al. 2017
)
. Thanks to the object-oriented structure of the toolbox, other implementations of ECT-spaces can be easily incorporated as well; one just needs to add new child classes of the abstract class
TB_patch
.

The
Matlab
toolbox assumes that the input parameters specified by the user lead to valid ECT-spaces and MDT-spline spaces. For ECT-spaces, this means that the length of the basic interval should be smaller than its critical length for design. A sufficient condition for MDT-spline spaces is the existence of admissible weights in the sense of Definition
2
.
However, a full theoretical characterization of such spaces is not yet available in general. The development of practical recipes for selection of valid ranges of the input parameters is an important topic of further investigation.
The toolbox could assist in this investigation; see Section
6.3
.

The extraction procedure described in Section
4.1

is applicable to any kind of spline space that is equipped with a B-spline-like basis (in the sense of Remark 6.5 from
Hiemstra et al. 2020
), also beyond our Tchebycheffian setting. The
Matlab
toolbox could thus be beneficial in this more general context as well. As long as child classes of the abstract class
TB_patch
are provided, one can profit of the extraction procedure and the spline environment created by the toolbox.

Tchebycheffian splines are a beautiful theoretical tool, with a huge potential in applications such as geometric modeling and isogeometric analysis. However, as argued in
(
Hiemstra et al. 2020
)
, their full exploitation in practice has been limited so far by the lack of stable algorithms and implementations. We hope that the presented
Matlab
toolbox may contribute towards the flourishing of Tchebycheffian splines, both in theory and practice.
