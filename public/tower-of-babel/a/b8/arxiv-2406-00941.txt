A Robust Residual-Based Test for Structural Changes in Factor Models
Bin Peng, Liangjun Su ,, Yayi Yan

Abstract
In this paper, we propose an easy-to-implement residual-based specification testing procedure for detecting structural changes in factor models, which is powerful against both smooth and abrupt structural changes with unknown break dates. The proposed test is robust to the over-specified number of factors, and serially and cross-sectionally correlated error processes. A new central limit theorem is given for the quadratic forms of panel data with dependence over both dimensions, thereby filling a gap in the literature. We establish the asymptotic properties of the proposed test statistic, and accordingly develop a simulation-based scheme to select critical value in order to improve finite sample performance. Through extensive simulations and a real-world application, we confirm our theoretical results and demonstrate that the proposed test exhibits desirable size and power in practice. JEL Classification: C14, C23, C33. Keywords: Factor model; structural change; residual-based test; serial correlation; cross-sectional dependence.

Introduction

As we are embracing the era of data rich environment, factor models have
been very popular in the past three decades or so. See
Jolliffe (2002)
and

Fan et al. (2021)
for comprehensive reviews on a variety of applications of
factor models. One of the most attractive features is possibly the low rank
representation, which usually imposes that a set of high dimensional
observations can be driven by a series of low dimensional vectors. More
often than not, the low dimensional vectors are the foundation of many
factor augmented analyses (see, e.g.,
Stock and Watson, 2016
, and references
therein).

To obtain these low dimensional vectors properly, extensive efforts have
been devoted to estimating the number of factors, viz., the length of
aforementioned low dimensional vectors. See, e.g.,
Bai and Ng (2002)
,
Onatski (2010)
,
Negahban and Wainwright (2011)
,
Lam and Yao (2012)
,
Ahn and Horenstein (2013)
, among
others. Notably, all of these works are based on the conventional factor
models where the loadings are assumed to remain constant over time. As
economists realize that the relationships between economic and financial
variables may suffer from structural changes over time, the factor loadings,
which capture the relationships between these variables and the latent
common factors, could be varying over time or some state variables. For this
reason, various methods have been proposed to test for the structural
changes in factor models or to estimate the factor models with breaks. For
example,
Breitung and Eickmeier (2011)
,
Chen et al. (2014)
,
Han and Inoue (2015)
,
Yamamoto and Tanaka (2015)
,
Su and Wang (2017)
;
Su and Wang (2020)
,
Baltagi et al. (2021)
,
Fu et al. (2023)
, and
Bai et al. (2024)
consider various
methods to detect structural changes in large dimensional factor models.
Except
Su and Wang (2017)
;
Su and Wang (2020)
and
Fu et al. (2023)
who allow
for smooth changes, almost all the other papers in the literature consider
one or multiple abrupt changes. We provide a detailed summary of these works
in Section
A.1
for the purpose of comparison. In terms of
estimation,
Cheng et al. (2016)
study the estimation of a factor
models with a one-time break;
Ma and Su (2018)
and
Baltagi et al. (2021)
consider
the estimation of factor models with multiple abrupt breaks;
Su and Wang (2017)
and
Pelger and Xiong (2022)
consider the kernel estimation of
factor models with loadings changing over time and stated variables,
respectively;
Fu et al. (2024)
study time-varying factor-augmented
regression models.

In this paper, we are also interested in testing whether the loadings change
over time or not. We aim to propose a new test statistic that has a superb
power in detecting smaller local alternatives than almost all existing tests
by utilizing richer information along both cross-sectional and time
dimensions, and remains robust against both cross-sectional and serial
dependence. One popular approach of detecting an abrupt change is via the
use of a Lagrange multiplier (LM) type of test statistic. For example,
Chen et al. (2014)
,
Baltagi et al. (2021)
and
Bai et al. (2024)
all consider LM-type
of tests based on the information of the unknown factors. When doing so, the
test statistic can only detect local alternatives that converge to the null
at a slow rate (e.g.,
T^{-1/2}
) associated with the time dimension
\left(T\right)
alone, and it is generally not robust to misspecified factor
numbers (e.g., see p. 359 in
Baltagi et al., 2021
). Another popular approach
aims at detecting smooth structural changes and remains powerful against
abrupt breaks too. The tests of
Su and Wang (2017)
;
Su and Wang (2020)
and
Fu et al. (2023)
are capable of examining the parametric loadings against the
nonparametric generalization via a smooth function. In general, such tests
are able to utilize the information along both dimensions of the residuals,
thereby detecting local alternatives converging to the null at a rate faster
than
T^{-1/2}.
In particular,
Su and Wang (2017)
;
Su and Wang (2020)
consider a
kernel-based smoothing nonparametric test for structure changes that can
detect local alternatives converging to the null at the rate
N^{-1/4}T^{-1/2}h^{-1/4},
where
N
and
T
denote cross-sectional and time
dimensions, respectively, and
h
is a bandwidth parameter;
Fu et al. (2023)
consider a nonsmoothing test

for the structural changes in factor
models based on the discrete Fourier transform and their test can detect
local alternatives at a rate
(NT)^{-1/2}
. But to work out the asymptotic
distributional theory, early research typically assumes either cross
sectional independence or serial uncorrelation for the error terms
\left\{\varepsilon_{it}\right\}
in the factor model. The reason is that one
often has to deal with the following terms:

\varepsilon_{it}\varepsilon_{js}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with}\hskip 10.00002pti,j\in[N],\hskip 10.00002ptt,s\in[T],

(1)

where
\left[L\right]\coloneqq\left\{1,\ldots,L\right\}
for a given

cross-sectional and time dimensions, early literature needs to balance the
rate of convergence yielded from the aggregation of the terms in (
1
) and the independence conditions imposed on
\left\{\varepsilon_{it}\right\}
. To the best of our knowledge, no attempt has
been made to construct a test statistic using (
1
) and to study
its limit null distribution while allowing for dependence along both
dimensions of
\left\{\varepsilon_{it}\right\}
. Despite the technical
challenge in dosing so, the issue is a realistic one. As

remark, the weak dependence along both dimensions of
\left\{\varepsilon_{it}\right\}
is likely to be the rule rather than the exception

In this paper, we focus on a residual-based test to examine whether one
should go beyond a traditional type of factor model practically while
accounting for dependence over both dimensions of
\left\{\varepsilon_{it}\right\}
. We shall derive the asymptotic properties of the newly
proposed test statistic, and conduct extensive numerical studies to confirm
our theoretical findings. To proceed, we briefly review the literature on
residual-based model specification tests. The basic idea is to use the
estimated residuals (typically from a parametric or semiparametric model
under the null) to construct a test statistic, in which

\text{estimated residuals}=\text{observations}-\text{fitted values}.

Under the null, the estimated residuals are typically sufficiently close to
the true error terms so that the limiting distribution of a suitably
constructed residual-based test statistic can be established. Under the
alternative, the estimated residuals contain a term that reflects the
deviation from the null hypothesis and thus contribute to the power of the
test statistic. Along this line of research, early works focus on examining
different types of time series models (e.g.,
Dickey and Fuller, 1979
;
Phillips and Perron, 1988
).
Gradually, the idea is extended to justify different model specifications
for a variety of data types such as cross-sectional data (
Li, 1994
;
Zheng, 1996
), time series data (
Gao et al., 2009
), and panel data (
Su et al., 2015
;
Wu et al., 2022
).

To sum up, our main contributions are as follows. First, assuming the number
of factors is correctly specified, we establish the asymptotic distribution
of a residual-based test statistic under the null by allowing for dependence
along both dimensions of
\left\{\varepsilon_{it}\right\}
. As mentioned
above, this is a highly technically challenging issue, and thus a new
central limit theorem is given for the quadratic forms of panel data with
dependence over both dimensions, thereby filling a gap in the literature.
This result is of independent interest and can be used in various
kernel-based specification tests (e.g.,
Su et al., 2015
;
Chen and Huang, 2018
). Second, we explore the
local power of the proposed test and show that it can detect local
alternatives converging to the null at a rate
(NT)^{-1/2}h^{-1/4},
faster
than almost all existing tests in the literature but
Fu et al. (2023)

who consider a nonsmoothing test. It is well known a nonsmoothing test (like
that of
Fu et al. (2023)
) tends to be more powerful than a smoothing
test (like ours) under low frequency local alternatives whereas a smoothing
test can be more powerful than a nonsmoothing test under high frequency
local alternatives as studied in
Rosenblatt (1975)
. See the
discussion on p. 400 of
Li and Racine (2007)
. Third, we show the
robustness of our test by proving the limiting distributions under the null
and local alternatives continue to hold true when the number of factors is
over-specified. Fourth, we also study the global power behavior of the
proposed test and our investigation helps to explain where the
residual-based tests gain their power. Finally, we conduct numerical studies
to examine the finite sample performance of the test and the results
corroborate the theoretical findings.

2
The Null Hypothesis and the Test Statistic

In this section we introduce the null hypothesis and the test statistic.

2.1
Hypotheses

Consider a series of high dimensional vectors
\{\mathbf{x}_{t}:t\in\left[T\right]\}
, and assume that
\{\mathbf{x}_{t}\}
is generated by the
following factor model that is potentially time-varying:

\mathbf{x}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{t}\mathbf{f}_{t}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t},

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}=(\varepsilon_{1t},\ldots,\varepsilon_{Nt})^{\top}
is a vector of zero-mean error terms,
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{t}=(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1t},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{Nt})^{\top}
is an
N\times r

time-varying loading matrix, and
\mathbf{f}_{t}
is an
r\times 1
latent
factor. We focus on the case where
r
is fixed, and
\left(N,T\right)\rightarrow\infty
. To examine whether
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{t}
’s evolve over
time, the null hypothesis is specified as follows:

\mathbb{H}_{0}:\ \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\ \text{for all}\ t\in[T],

(3)

where
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
is a conformable constant matrix. The alternative
hypothesis
\mathbb{H}_{1}
is the negation of
\mathbb{H}_{0}.
Obviously,
under
\mathbb{H}_{0},
the factor model in (
2
) degenerates to the
conventional time-invariant factor model that has been widely studied in the
literature. Under
\mathbb{H}_{1},
the loadings
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{it}
must
change over time for some
i\in\left[N\right]
.

2.2
The Test Statistic

Under
\mathbb{H}_{0}
, we have

\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{F}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\top}+\mathcal{E},

(4)

where
\mathbf{X=(\mathbf{x}}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{\mathbf{x}}_{T}\mathbf{)}^{\top}\mathbf{,}

\mathbf{F}=(\mathbf{f}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{f}_{T})^{\top},
and
\mathcal{E}
is defined accordingly. Following the lead
of
Bai and Ng (2002)
, we impose the identification condition
\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{F}^{\top}\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{I}_{r}
to conduct the standard principal
component analysis (PCA):

\widehat{\mathbf{F}}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{F}},

(5)

where
\widehat{\mathbf{V}}
is an
r\times r
diagonal matrix including the
first
r
largest eigenvalues of
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}
on the main
diagonal, and
\frac{1}{T}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}=\mathbf{I}_{r}
. Accordingly, we have the residual matrix
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}=\mathbf{X}-\widehat{\mathbf{F}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\top}=\mathbf{M}_{\widehat{\mathbf{F}}}\mathbf{X},
where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}=\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}
. Having
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}=(\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{N})=\{\widehat{\varepsilon}_{it}\}_{T\times N}
in hand, we construct the residual-based
test statistic as follows:

L_{NT}=\frac{1}{T^{2}N^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{i}^{\top}\,\mathbf{K}_{h}\,\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{j},

(6)

where
\mathbf{K}_{h}=\{\frac{1}{h}K(\frac{t-s}{Th})\}_{T\times T}
with
t,s\in[T]
and
h\coloneqq h_{T}
is a bandwidth parameter. The test
statistic in (
6
) has a typical form of the residual-based test,
and the expression further concurs with (
1
) that one has to
account for dependence yielded by
\varepsilon_{it}\varepsilon_{js}

appearing in the leading term in the expansion of
L_{NT}.

3
The Asymptotic Properties of the Test Statistic

In this section, we establish the limiting distribution of
L_{NT}
under
the null, and study its asymptotic local and global power properties. To
facilitate the presentation, we first provide the basic assumptions used for
our asymptotic analyses in Section
3.1
. In Section
3.2
we
establish the asymptotic distribution of the test statistic under the null,
and then present the result with the number of factors over-specified. In
Sections
3.3
and
3.4
, we study the power properties of our
test under different scenarios, of which one is also helpful to explain
where the residual-based tests gain their global power. Finally, we point
out that the newly proposed test can also be used to select the number of
factors practically.

3.1
Basic Assumptions

Note that we can write
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{N})^{\top}
under
\mathbb{H}_{0}.
As in
Su and Wang (2017)
,
we will assume that the loadings are nonrandom without loss of generality.
To facilitate the asymptotic analyses, we make the following assumptions.

Assumption 1
.

(a)
K(\cdot)
is a continuous and symmetric function with
bounded support
[-1,1]
, and
K(0)=1
.

(b) As
(N,T)\rightarrow\infty,

h\rightarrow 0,

Th\rightarrow\infty,

hT^{2}/N^{2}\rightarrow 0
and
hN^{2}/T^{2}\rightarrow 0
.

Assumption 2
.

Suppose that

Assumption 3
.

(a) Let

be a stationary process such that
\mathbf{f}_{t}=\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{e}_{t},\mathbf{e}_{t-1},\ldots)

\mathbf{e}_{t}
being independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.)

\mathbf{G}(\cdot)=[G_{1}(\cdot),\ldots,G_{r}(\cdot)]^{\top}

(b) Let
|\mathbf{f}_{m}-\mathbf{f}_{m}^{\ast}|_{4}\eqqcolon\lambda_{4}^{f}(m)=O(m^{-\alpha})

\alpha>3
, where
\mathbf{f}_{t}^{\ast}=\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{e}_{t},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{0}^{\prime},\mathbf{e}_{-1},\ldots)
is the coupled version of
\mathbf{f}_{t}
with
\mathbf{e}_{0}^{\prime}
being an independent copy of
\{\mathbf{e}_{t}\}
.

Assumption 4
.

(a) Let
\{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}\}
be a stationary process such

\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}=\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{e}_{t},\mathbf{e}_{t-1},\ldots)
,

,
E[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}^{\top}]=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}
,
\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{2}\leq c_{3}
for some constant
c_{3}>0,
and
\mathbf{g}(\cdot)=[g_{1}(\cdot),\ldots,g_{N}(\cdot)]^{\top}
is a vector of measurable
functions.

(b) Let
\sup_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\|\mathbf{v}\|=1}|\overline{\varepsilon}_{m,\mathbf{v}}-\overline{\varepsilon}_{m,\mathbf{v}}^{\ast}|_{4}\eqqcolon\lambda_{4}^{\varepsilon}(m)=O(m^{-\alpha})
for some
\alpha>3
, where
\overline{\varepsilon}_{t,\mathbf{v}}=\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{v}
,
\overline{\varepsilon}_{t,\mathbf{v}}^{\ast}=\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}^{\ast\top}\mathbf{v}
with
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}^{\ast}=\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{e}_{t},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{0}^{\prime},\mathbf{e}_{-1},\ldots)
, and
\mathbf{e}_{0}^{\prime}
is as defined in Assumption
3
(b).

(c)
|\mathcal{P}_{t-m}(\mathbf{f}_{t}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{it}\varepsilon_{is})|_{2}=O(m^{-\beta})
for
some
\beta>1
, where
\mathcal{P}_{t}[\cdot]=E[\cdot\,|\,\mathscr{E}_{t}]

-E[\cdot\,|\,\mathscr{E}_{t-1}]
and
\mathscr{E}_{t}=(\mathbf{e}_{t},\mathbf{e}_{t-1},\ldots)
.

Assumption
1
imposes some standard conditions on the kernel
function
K\left(\cdot\right)
and the bandwidth parameter
h.
In
addition, we allow for
N
and
T
diverging to infinity at different or
same rates. Without loss of generality, Assumption
2
assumes
that the loadings under
\mathbb{H}_{0}
are non-random; see, e.g.,
Su and Wang (2017)
and
Fu et al. (2023)
and the references therein. It is easy
to see that the asymptotic analyses remain valid when the loadings are
random and independent of the factors and error terms, and satisfy some
moment conditions as in
Bai and Ng (2002)
.

Assumption
3
imposes weak dependence conditions on
\{\mathbf{f}_{t}\}
, allowing for serial correlations and conditional
heteroscedasticity of unknown forms. The strength of temporal dependence is
defined by the functional dependence measure of
Wu (2005)
,
which nests many stationary processes as special cases (e.g.,
Tong, 1990
, p.204
;
Wu, 2005
). Here, we only
require an algebraic decay rate of the temporal dependence. Intuitively, the
physical dependence coefficients
\{\lambda_{4}^{f}(m)\}
quantify the
dependence of outputs
\{\mathbf{f}_{m}\}
on
\mathbf{e}_{0}
.

Assumption
4
regulates the decay rate of temporal dependence
after taking weighted average cross-sectionally. By doing so,
\varepsilon_{it}
’s are allowed to exhibit cross-sectional dependence of various
unknown forms. The constants “
\alpha
” and “
\beta
” play a role similar to the

\alpha
-mixing or
\beta
-mixing coefficients in the time series
literature and we only require an algebraic decay rate of the temporal
dependence. Assumption
4
(c) requires the underlying data
generating process to satisfy the following moment restriction (see Lemma
A.1
(c) for details)

\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{TN}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{f}_{t}\sum_{i=1}^{N}[\varepsilon_{it}\varepsilon_{is}-E(\varepsilon_{it}\varepsilon_{is})]\right|_{2}=O(1).

As a high level assumption, the above condition has been widely adopted in
the literature on factor models (see, e.g., Assumption F.1 in
Bai, 2003
, Assumption A.1 (vii) in
Su and Wang, 2017

and Assumption A.7 in
Fu et al., 2023
). Intuitively, Assumption
4
(c) requires that
\mathbf{f}_{t}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{it}\varepsilon_{is}
and
\mathbf{e}_{t-m}
are
asymptotically independent as

for any fixed

In
Section
B.2
of the online supplement, we provide an example to show
that Assumption

is fulfilled by a well known data
generating process.

3.2
Asymptotic Null Distribution

Define

\sigma_{\varepsilon,a}^{2}=\lim_{(N,T)\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{Th}\sum_{t,s=1}^{T}E(\overline{\varepsilon}_{t,a}\overline{\varepsilon}_{s,a})K\left(\frac{t-s}{Th}\right),

where
\overline{\varepsilon}_{t,a}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-a_{i})\varepsilon_{it}
,
a_{i}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{i}^{\top}(\frac{1}{N}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda})^{-1}\overline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
, and
\overline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{i}
. Clearly,
\sigma_{\varepsilon,a}^{2}
denotes a kernel-weighted version
of the long-run variance of
{\varepsilon}_{it}.
We are now
ready to present the first main result of this paper.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
–
4
hold, and
\sigma_{\varepsilon,a}^{2}>0
. As
(N,T)\rightarrow\infty
,

TN\sqrt{h}[L_{NT}-(TNh)^{-1}\sigma_{\varepsilon,a}^{2}]\rightarrow_{D}N(0,2\nu_{0}\sigma_{\varepsilon,a}^{4})\text{ under }\mathbb{H}_{0},

where
\nu_{0}=\int_{-1}^{1}K^{2}(u)\mathrm{d}u
.

As indicated in Theorem
1
, in order to achieve an
asymptotically nondegenerate normal distribution, the long-run variance
\sigma_{\varepsilon,a}^{2}
cannot be 0. This immediately rules out the
following special case:

\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{1}_{N}f_{t}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t},

where
f_{t}
is a common trend function with loadings given identically by
1. In this case, we have
1-a_{i}=0
for all
i\in[N]
. Notably, the
term
1-a_{i}
is equivalent to
B_{i}
of
Fu et al. (2023, p. 307)
,
which also suffers the issue raised here. The reason of having a structure
as in
1-a_{i}
is due to the use of projection matrix involved in the PCA.
Our test has the usual asymptotic normal distribution when one has
idiosyncratic trends as follows

\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{\Lambda}f_{t}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t},\text{ }

or equivalently in scalar notations:
x_{it}=\lambda_{i}f_{t}+\varepsilon_{it},
where
\lambda_{i}
is not a constant across
i.
Interestingly,
the common trend issue has been addressed in another literature. Various
tests have been proposed to test for common trend in the literature; see
e.g.,
Zhang et al. (2012)
and
Wu et al. (2022)
. As discussed in
Zhang et al. (2012)
, high-dimensional time series rarely share a common trend
in the real world and idiosyncratic trending behavior is a norm rather than
the exception. For the factor model, if one wishes, one can first eliminate
the possibility of having a common time trend in the dataset, say, by using
the test of
Wu et al. (2022)
. But this is subject to the usual pretesting
issue. For this reason we do not recommend testing the presence of common
trends before using our test.

Building on Theorem
1
, we investigate the case when the number
of factors is over-specified. That said, suppose that when conducting PCA,
\widetilde{r}\ (\geq r)
is specified as the number of factors. Then the

5

\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{F}},

where
\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}
is an
\widetilde{r}\times\widetilde{r}

diagonal matrix including the first
\widetilde{r}
largest eigenvalues of
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}
on the main diagonal, and
\frac{1}{T}\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}=\mathbf{I}_{\widetilde{r}}
. Accordingly, we have

\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}=\mathbf{X}-\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\top}=\mathbf{M}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}}\mathbf{X},

where
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}=\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}=(\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{N})=\{\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{it}\}_{T\times N}
. With a bit abuse of notation, we continue to use
L_{NT}
to denote the test statistic:

L_{NT}=\frac{1}{T^{2}N^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{i}^{\top}\,\mathbf{K}_{h}\,\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{j}.

(7)

We summarize the asymptotic property of
L_{NT}
under the null in the
following theorem.

Theorem 2
.

Let Assumptions
1
–
4
hold
and
L_{NT}
be defined in (
7
) with
\widetilde{r}\geq r
being
fixed. Then as
(N,T)\rightarrow\infty
,

TN\sqrt{h}[L_{NT}-(TNh)^{-1}\sigma_{\varepsilon,a}^{2}]\rightarrow_{D}N(0,2\nu_{0}\sigma_{\varepsilon,a}^{4})\text{ under }\mathbb{H}_{0}.

Theorem
2
infers that when the number of factors is
over-specified, we are still able to achieve the same limit null
distribution as in Theorem
1
.

To implement our test, one needs to estimate the long-run variance
\sigma_{\varepsilon,a}^{2}
. Define a panel heteroskedasticity and
autocorrelation consistent (HAC) variance estimator by

\widehat{\sigma}_{\varepsilon,a}^{2}=\sum_{k=-l}^{l}\widehat{\sigma}_{\varepsilon,a,k}^{2}a(k/l),

(8)

where
\widehat{\sigma}_{\varepsilon,a,k}^{2}\coloneqq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T-|k|}\widehat{\overline{\varepsilon}}_{t}\widehat{\overline{\varepsilon}}_{t+|k|}
,
\widehat{\overline{\varepsilon}}_{t}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{it}
,
a(\cdot)

is a kernel function, and
l\coloneqq l_{T}
is a bandwidth parameter for
the HAC estimation. We further impose the following assumption.

Assumption 5
.

(a) Suppose that
a(\cdot)
is a symmetric positive kernel
function that is Lipschitz continuous on
[-1,1]
. For
q\in\{1,2\}
,
\lim_{|x|\rightarrow 0}\frac{1-a(x)}{|x|^{q}}=\bar{c}_{q}
for some constant

\bar{c}_{q}\in(0,\infty)
.

(b) As
\left(N,T\right)\rightarrow\infty,

1/l+l/T\rightarrow 0
,
Tl/N^{2}\rightarrow 0
and
lN^{2}/T^{3}\rightarrow 0
.

Assumption
5
includes a set of typical conditions for the
kernel function
a(\cdot)
and the bandwidth
l
, and we omit the discussion and refer interested readers
to
Andrews (1991)
for details. Then the following
result holds.

Proposition 1
.

Let Assumptions
1
–
5
hold.
Under the null hypothesis in (
3
), for any fixed
\widetilde{r}\geq r
,

\widehat{\sigma}_{\varepsilon,a}^{2}=\sigma_{\varepsilon,a}^{2}+O_{P}(l^{-q}+\sqrt{l/T}).

In addition, if
l\simeq T^{1/(2q+1)}
and
hT^{2q/(2q+1)}\rightarrow\infty
, then

\widehat{L}_{T}=\frac{TN\sqrt{h}[L_{NT}-(TNh)^{-1}\widehat{\sigma}_{\varepsilon,a}^{2}]}{\sqrt{2\nu_{0}}\widehat{\sigma}_{\varepsilon,a}^{2}}\rightarrow_{D}N(0,1).

(9)

Proposition
1
infers that
\widehat{L}_{T}
is asymptotically
pivotal under the null. By the first part of Proposition
1
,
l\simeq T^{1/(2q+1)}
corresponds to the optimal rate of bandwidth in terms
of minimizing the asymptotic mean squared error of
\widehat{\sigma}_{\varepsilon,a}^{2}.

3.3
Asymptotic Local Power

To study the asymptotic local power property of our test, we consider a
sequence of local alternatives:

\mathbb{H}_{1}(a_{TN}):\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{it}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{i}+a_{TN}\mathbf{g}_{it}\ \ \text{for all}\ \ i\in[N],

(10)

where
a_{TN}\rightarrow 0
,
\mathbf{g}_{it}=\mathbf{g}_{i}(t/T)
, and
\int_{0}^{1}\mathbf{g}_{i}(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau=\mathbf{0}_{r}
for the
purpose of normalization. The term
a_{TN}\mathbf{g}_{it}
characterizes the
departure of
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{it}
from the constant
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{i}
, and
our setup allows for both abrupt and smooth structural changes. For example,
if we have only one break at
T_{1}
with
\tau_{1}=T_{1}/T:

\mathbf{\lambda}_{it}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{\lambda}_{i\left(1\right)}&\text{ for }t<T_{1}\\
\mathbf{\lambda}_{i\left(2\right)}&\text{for }t\geq T_{1}\end{array}\right.,

then we can define

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}_{i}(\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{g}_{i\left(1\right)}&\text{ for }\tau<T_{1}/T\\
\mathbf{g}_{i\left(2\right)}&\text{for }\tau\geq T_{1}/T\end{array}\right.

with
\mathbf{g}_{i\left(1\right)}=-\frac{\mathbf{\lambda}_{i\left(2\right)}-\mathbf{\lambda}_{i\left(1\right)}}{a_{TN}}\tau_{2}
,

\mathbf{g}_{i\left(2\right)}=\frac{\mathbf{\lambda}_{i\left(2\right)}-\mathbf{\lambda}_{i\left(1\right)}}{a_{TN}}\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}=1-\tau_{1}
to ensure
\int_{0}^{1}\mathbf{g}_{i}(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau=\tau_{1}\mathbf{g}_{i\left(1\right)}+\tau_{2}\mathbf{g}_{i\left(2\right)}=0.
Here,
a_{TN}
controls the speed at which
\mathbf{\lambda}_{i\left(2\right)}-\mathbf{\lambda}_{i\left(1\right)}
shrinks to 0.

The following theorem indicates that our test statistic can detect a class
of Pitman local alternatives at a departure rate of
(TN)^{-1/2}h^{-1/4}
.
In addition, our test is robust to the over-specified number of factors
under the local alternatives.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
–
4
hold, and
Th^{3/2}\rightarrow\infty
. Then under
\mathbb{H}_{1}(a_{TN})
with
a_{TN}=(TN)^{-1/2}h^{-1/4}
and for any fixed
\widetilde{r}\geq r
, we have

TN\sqrt{h}[L_{NT}-(TNh)^{-1}\sigma_{\varepsilon,a}^{2}]\rightarrow_{D}N(\mu_{1},2\nu_{0}\sigma_{\varepsilon,a}^{4}),

where
\mu_{1}=\int_{-1}^{1}K(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau\int_{0}^{1}[\overline{\mathbf{g}}_{a}(\tau)^{\top}E(\mathbf{f}_{t})]^{2}\mathrm{d}\tau
, and
\overline{\mathbf{g}}_{a}(\tau)=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-a_{i})\mathbf{g}_{i}(\tau)
.

Theorem
3
indicates that
L_{NT}
has asymptotically
non-negligible power in detecting local alternatives converging to the null
at the rate
(TN)^{-1/2}h^{-1/4}
provided
\mu_{1}>0.
This rate is much
faster than almost all existing tests in the literature by noticing that
Nh^{1/2}\rightarrow\infty
as
\left(N,T\right)\rightarrow\infty
. As
mentioned in the introduction, existing time-series-based tests, such as

Breitung and Eickmeier (2011)
,
Chen et al. (2014)
,
Han and Inoue (2015)
,

Yamamoto and Tanaka (2015)
,
Baltagi et al. (2021)
, and
Bai et al. (2024)
, have power
in detecting local alternatives at rate
T^{-1/2}.
Similarly, the
kernel-based smoothing tests of
Su and Wang (2017)
and
Su and Wang (2020)

can detect local alternatives at the rate
N^{-1/4}T^{-1/2}h^{-1/4}.
The
only test that can detect local alternatives converging to the null faster
than ours is the non-smoothing test of
Fu et al. (2023)
, which is based
on a discrete Fourier transform and can detect local alternatives at rate
(NT)^{-1/2}.
Again, neither the smoothing kernel test of
Su and Wang (2017)

and
Su and Wang (2020)
nor the nonsmoothing test of
Fu et al. (2023)

allows for serial dependence in the error terms.

It is worth mentioning that the result in Theorem
3
holds for
any fixed
\widetilde{r}\geq r.
That is, the over-specification of the
number of factors does not affect the local power property of our test.
Intuitively, this is due to the fact that the local alternatives converge to
the null at a very fast rate (viz.,
(TN)^{-1/2}h^{-1/4}
), and the
over-specified
\widetilde{r}-r
factors are essentially very weak factors
so that they cannot be identified by the PCA and their effect is still
present through the residuals in the PCA.

Note that the requirement that
\mu_{1}>0
rules out the case where
E(\mathbf{f}_{t})=0
. We believe that this requirement is not restrictive for
practical purpose. For example, in the case of asset pricing (e.g.,
Lettau and Pelger, 2020
), the risk factors, say, in the Fama-French
three-factor model (and also the estimated latent pricing factors) usually
do not have zero mean due to the risk premium required by investors. The
inclusion of an intercept may not address the issue of non-zero mean
factors. As a matter of factor, when an idiosyncratic intercept term
\alpha_{i}
is included in the regression, it can be absorbed into the vector of
factor loadings which has the associated factor
\mathbf{1}_{T}
. In this
case, the population mean of
\mathbf{f}_{t}
is surely nonzero.

Below we show that when
E(\mathbf{f}_{t})=0
, our test statistic is able to
detect a class of local alternatives at a departure rate of
N^{-1/2}h^{1/4}.
This rate converges to zero slower than
(TN)^{-1/2}h^{-1/4}
, indicating the price we have to pay when all the
factors in
\mathbf{f}_{t}
have zero mean.

Proposition 2
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
–
4

hold with
E(\mathbf{f}_{t})=0
and
E(\mathbf{f}_{t}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{s}^{\top})=0
for any
1\leq t,s\leq T
. Then under
\mathbb{H}_{1}(a_{TN})

with
a_{TN}=N^{-1/2}h^{1/4}
and for any fixed
\widehat{r}\geq r
, we have

TN\sqrt{h}[L_{NT}-(TNh)^{-1}\sigma_{\varepsilon,a}^{2}]\rightarrow_{D}N(\mu_{2},2\nu_{0}\sigma_{\varepsilon,a}^{4}),

where
\mu_{2}=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t,s=1}^{T}E\left(\overline{\mathbf{g}}_{a}(t/T)^{\top}\mathbf{f}_{t}\mathbf{f}_{s}^{\top}\overline{\mathbf{g}}_{a}(s/T)\right)K\left(\frac{t-s}{Th}\right)
.

3.4
Global Power

In this section, we explain the source of power of our test when data
include global structural breaks. Additionally, we point out that the newly
proposed test can be used to select the number of factors practically.

To accommodate some typical settings of structural breaks such as those in

Breitung and Eickmeier (2011)
, we write the loadings as
\mathbb{A}=[\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{T}],
where
\mathbb{A}
is a
N\times Tr
matrix. Let
R\coloneqq(Tr)\wedge N
and consider the singular
value decomposition (SVD):
\mathbb{A}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{V}^{\top}
, where
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{V}
are
N\times R
and
Tr\times R

unitary matrices, and
\mathbf{S}
denotes an
R\times R
diagonal matrix
with singular values
s_{TN,1},\ldots,s_{TN,R}
along the diagonal in
descending order. Then we can rewrite (
2
) in a compact form:

\mathbf{X}=\mathbb{F}\mathbb{A}^{\top}+\mathcal{E},

(12)

where
\mathbb{F}=\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{f}_{1}^{\top},\ldots,\mathbf{f}_{T}^{\top})
is a
T\times Tr
block diagonal matrix.

To facilitate the development, we add the following assumption.

Assumption 6
.

(a)
\max_{i,t}\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{it}\|\leq c_{0}
for some
constant
c_{0}>0
, where
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{it}
is the
i^{th}
column of
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{t}^{\top}
.

(b) The first
J
singular values of
\mathbf{S}
is of order
\sqrt{TN}
, while the remaining singular values satisfy
(TN)^{-1}\sum_{j=J+1}^{R}s_{TN,j}^{2}=o(1)
, where
J
is fixed.

This assumption encompasses many existing works as special cases. For
example, the model (
12
) with a single structural break as in
Breitung and Eickmeier (2011)
has
r<\mathrm{rank}(\mathbb{A})=J\leq 2r.
Assumption
6
(a) regulates the factor loadings such that
\|\mathbb{A}\|/\sqrt{TN}=O(1)
. Thus, we have
\mathrm{tr}(\mathbb{A}^{\top}\mathbb{A}/(TN))=(TN)^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{R}s_{TN,j}^{2}=O(1)
. Assumption
6
(b) suggests that the first
J
singular values diverge to
infinity at the rate of
\sqrt{TN}
.

Now, we rewrite the model in (
12
) as

\mathbf{X}=\mathbb{F}\mathbb{A}^{(J),\top}+\mathbb{F}\mathbb{A}^{(-J),\top}+\mathcal{E}=\mathbb{F}\mathbb{A}^{(J),\top}+\mathcal{E}^{\dagger},

(13)

where
\mathbb{A}^{(J)}=\mathbf{U}^{(J)}\mathbf{S}^{(J)}(\mathbf{V}^{(J)}\mathbf{)}^{\top}
,
\mathbb{A}^{(-J)}=\mathbf{U}^{(-J)}\mathbf{S}^{(-J)}(\mathbf{V}^{(-J)})^{\top}
,
\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}=\mathcal{E}+\mathbb{F(}\mathbb{A}^{(-J)})^{\top}
,
\mathbf{U}^{(J)}
(resp.
\mathbf{V}^{(J)}
)
and
\mathbf{U}^{(-J)}
(resp.
\mathbf{V}^{(-J)}
) contain the first
J

and the last
R-J
columns of
\mathbf{U}
(resp.
\mathbf{V}
)
respectively, and
\mathbf{S}^{(J)}
(resp.
\mathbf{S}^{(-J)}
) contains
the first
J
(resp. the remaining
R-J
) singular values of
\mathbf{S}
.
Therefore, (
12
) has a representation involving the time-invariant
factor loadings only:

\mathbf{X}=\mathcal{F}\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{\top}+\mathcal{E}^{\dagger},

where
\mathcal{F}=\sqrt{T}\mathbb{F}\mathbf{V}^{(J)}
with
\|\mathcal{F}^{\top}\mathcal{F}/T\|=O_{P}(1)
, and
\boldsymbol{\Theta}=\mathbf{U}^{(J)}\mathbf{S}^{(J)}/\sqrt{T}
. Hence, the above analysis also explains where
the power of LM-type tests come from under a general setup since these tests
(e.g.,
Chen et al., 2014
) try to test if the covariance matrix of
the larger finite-dimensional set of estimated factors is stable.

Define the
J\times J
symmetric positive definite matrix:

\boldsymbol{\Delta}=\plim(TN)^{-1}\mathbf{S}^{(J)}(\mathbf{V}^{(J)})^{\top}\mathbb{F}^{\top}\mathbb{F}\mathbf{V}^{(J)}\mathbf{S}^{(J)}.

Let
\mathbb{V}
be an
\widetilde{r}\times\widetilde{r}
diagonal matrix
containing the
\widetilde{r}
largest eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
in
descending order and
\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}
be a
J\times\widetilde{r}

eigenvector matrix of
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
corresponding to the
\widetilde{r}

eigenvalues in
\mathbb{V}
. The following theorem studies the asymptotic
behavior of
L_{NT}
under the global alternative.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
,
3
,
4
and
6
hold. For
\widetilde{r}<J
, there exists a positive constant
c
such that

L_{NT}=\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}_{J}-\mathbf{S}_{J}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{J})\boldsymbol{\Omega}(\mathbf{I}_{J}-\mathbf{S}_{J}\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{J}^{-1})\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+o_{P}(1)\geq c+o_{P}(1),

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{J}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{(N,T)\rightarrow\infty}\mathrm{diag}(s_{TN,1}/\sqrt{TN},\ldots,s_{TN,J}/\sqrt{TN}),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\plim\frac{1}{T^{2}h}\sum_{t,s=1}^{T}\mathcal{F}_{t}\mathcal{F}_{s}^{\top}K((t-s)/(Th)),

\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}
with
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}
being the
i^{th}
column of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{\top}
, and
\mathcal{F}_{t}
is the
t^{th}
column of
\mathcal{F}^{\top}
.

We make three comments on Theorem
4
. First, Theorem
4
implies that
\widehat{L}_{T}
defined in (
9
)
generally diverges to infinity at a rate
NTh^{1/2}
if one selects
\widetilde{r}<J
under the alternative. Second, Theorem
4

explains where the power of residual-based tests comes from. In the presence
of
k
breaks,
J\coloneqq
rank
\left(\mathbb{A}\right)\leq k\cdot r.

Therefore as long as one specifies a factor model with
\widetilde{r}<J
(so
that the true rank of the represented model is under-specified), the
residual-based tests would have power in detecting the global alternative.
Third, in connection with the results in Section
3.2
, Theorem
4
also indicates that we can propose sequential procedure to test
the composite null hypotheses

\mathbb{H}_{0}^{\prime}:r=j\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for}\hskip 10.00002ptj\in[r_{\max}]

to identify the number of factors practically if a low rank representation
is believed to be true. The estimated value of factor number,
\widehat{r}
,
will be the value of
j
when we fail to reject the null at the first time.
Of course, in the presence of abrupt structural changes,
\widehat{r}
would
over-estimate the underlying number of factors,
r
, for the original factor
model.

To proceed, we emphasize that as explained in

Fu et al. (2023, pp.
310–311)
, the information criterion of
Su and Wang (2017)

still offers a consistent estimation of
r
rather than
J
. We therefore
suggest that one always turns to the information criterion of
Su and Wang (2017)
when estimating
r
to avoid any power loss.

3.5
Simulating the Critical Value

Note that
\widehat{L}_{T}
is asymptotically pivotal under
\mathbb{H}_{0}

defined in (
3
). It is well known that kernel-based nonparametric
tests may not perform well in finite samples if one uses the normal critical
values and they can be sensitive to the choice of bandwidth. To improve the
finite sample performance of the test, we propose a simulation-based scheme
for the selection of the critical value. A similar procedure has also been
adopted by
Zhang and Wu (2012)
for the same purpose in the context of
testing univariate time-varying regression models.

The algorithm of a simulation-assisted testing procedure is as follows.

Step 1:

Use
\{\mathbf{x}_{t}\}
to estimate the constant factor
model, and compute
\widehat{L}_{T}
based on Proposition
1
.

Step 2:

Generate i.i.d.
\widetilde{r}
-dimensional standard normal
random vectors
\{\mathbf{f}_{t}^{\ast}\}_{t=1}^{T}
and
\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{i}^{\ast}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, and i.i.d.
N
-dimensional standard normal random
vectors
\{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}^{\ast}\}
, and generate
2
2

2

In our own experiments, we also generate i.i.d.
N
-dimensional standard
normal random vectors
\{\mathbf{x}_{t}^{*}\}_{t=1}^{T}
to compute the
simulated test statistics, which yields similar simulation results.

x_{it}^{\ast}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{i}^{\ast,\top}\mathbf{f}_{t}^{\ast}+\varepsilon_{it}^{\ast}
.

Step 3:

Compute the bootstrap statistic
\widehat{L}_{T}^{b}
in the
same way as
\widehat{L}_{T}
using the simulated sample
\{x_{it}^{\ast}\}
.

Step 4:

Repeat Steps 2-3
B
times to obtain
B
test statistics
\{\widehat{L}_{T}^{b}\}_{b=1}^{B}
, as well as its empirical quantile
\widehat{q}_{1-\alpha}
. We reject the null hypothesis (
3
) at the
significance level
\alpha
if
\widehat{L}_{T}>\widehat{q}_{1-\alpha}
.

By Theorems
1
and
2
,
\widehat{L}_{T}^{b}
and
\widehat{L}_{T}
have the same asymptotic distribution under the null. This
suggests that instead of using the normal critical value, we can obtain the
simulated critical value
\widehat{q}_{1-\alpha}
for
\widehat{L}_{T}
.
Under the global alternatives,
\widehat{L}_{T}^{b}
remains to be
asymptotically normal whereas
\widehat{L}_{T}
diverges to infinity in
probability.

Note that in the above simulation procedure, we do not need to use the
estimated factors and loadings under the null to generate
x_{it}^{\ast}.

This is similar in spirit to the fixed-regressor wild bootstrap of
Hansen (2000)
, where one does not need to mimic the exact dependence
structure in the bootstrap world and may still obtain a valid bootstrap test
that shares the same limit null distribution as the original test statistic.
For our test, one may theoretically provide the approximation rate for the
simulation procedure by establishing a deep Gaussian approximation theory
for a quadratic form of panel data with complex dependence structure, based
on which one can judge whether the simulation can provide a better
approximation to the finite sample distribution than the asymptotic normal.
But this certainly goes beyond the scope of the current paper, and has to be
left for future research.

4
Numerical Studies

In this section, we conduct Monte Carlo simulations and consider a real data

4.1
Simulation

In this section, we examine the finite sample performance of the proposed
testing procedure via extensive simulation experiments. We also compare our
test with the parametric tests of
Chen et al. (2014)
and
Han and Inoue (2015)

Su and Wang (2020)

Throughout the numerical studies, the Bartlett kernel is adopted. When

Stock and Watson (2020)

and set the bandwidth
\ell

\lceil 0.75T^{1/3}\rceil

of

\widetilde{r}=2,\ldots,5

in Sections

–

. For comparison, we also examine the

.

We first examine the size performance of our test (referred to as PSY).

1
reports the empirical rejection rates based on 1000

from these tables. First, PSY tends to be slightly oversized (undersized)

size
T

nominal levels. Second, when
\widetilde{r}

. Third,

Conclusion

In this paper, we propose an easy-to-implement residual-based specification
testing procedure to detect structural changes in factor models, which is
powerful against both smooth and abrupt structural changes with unknown
break dates. The proposed test is robust against the over-specified number
of factors under the null and local alternatives, and serially and
cross-sectionally correlated error processes. A new central limit theorem is
derived for the quadratic forms of panel data with dependence over both
dimensions, thereby filling a gap in the literature. We establish the
asymptotic properties of the proposed test statistic under the null and a
sequence of Pitman local alternative, and accordingly develop a
simulation-based scheme to select critical value in order to improve finite
sample performance. Through extensive simulations and a real-world
application, we confirm our theoretical results and demonstrate that the
proposed test exhibits desirable size and power in practice.

Some extensions are possible. First, despite the superb asymptotic power
properties of the proposed test among all existing kernel-based
nonparametric smooth test, its power decreases in the case when all factors
have zero mean in the model. It is worthwhile to consider a remedy to avoid
such a power loss. Second, we only consider the case of pervasive factors in
the paper. Given the recent burgeoning literature on weak factors (
Bai and Ng, 2023
), it is interesting to extend the current work
to allow for the presence of weak factors. We leave these topics for future
research.

Proof of Lemma
A.5
.

We will prove this lemma by using the martingale central limit theorem
(CLT); see, e.g.,
Hall and Heyde (1980)
.

Note that
H_{t}
and
H_{s}
are mutually independent if
|t-s|\geq m+1
. Also, since
\{H_{t}\sum_{s=t-m+1}^{t-1}H_{s}w_{s,t}\}_{t}
is
an m.d.s., by Burkholder inequality and Minkowski inequality, if
m/(Th)\rightarrow 0
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{t=2}^{T}H_{t}\sum_{s=t-m+1}^{t-1}H_{s}w_{s,t}\right|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle O(1)\sum_{t=2}^{T}\left|H_{t}\sum_{s=t-m+1}^{t-1}H_{s}w_{s,t}\right|_{2}^{2}\leq O(1)\sum_{t=2}^{T}\left|H_{t}\right|_{4}^{2}\left|\sum_{s=t-m+1}^{t-1}H_{s}w_{s,t}\right|_{4}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle O(1)\sum_{t=2}^{T}\left|H_{t}\right|_{4}^{2}\sum_{s=t-m+1}^{t-1}\left|H_{s}w_{s,t}\right|_{4}^{2}\leq O(1)\sum_{t=2}^{T}\sum_{s=t-m+1}^{t-1}w_{s,t}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O(Tm/(T^{2}h))=o(1).

Then we can easily verify the Lindeberg condition since

\sum_{t=1+m}^{T}\left|H_{t}\sum_{s=t-m}^{t-1}H_{s}w_{s,t}\right|_{4}^{4}\leq\sum_{t=1+m}^{T}\left|H_{t}\right|_{4}^{4}\left|\sum_{s=t-m}^{t-1}H_{s}w_{s,t}\right|_{4}^{4}\leq\sum_{t=1+m}^{T}\left|H_{t}\right|_{4}^{4}\left(\sum_{s=t-m}^{t-1}\left|H_{s}w_{s,t}\right|_{4}^{2}\right)^{2}=o(1).

We next verify the convergence of conditional variance, i.e.,

\sum_{t=m+1}^{T}E[(H_{t}\sum_{s=1}^{t-m}H_{s}w_{s,t})^{2}\,|\,\mathscr{E}_{t-1}]\rightarrow_{P}\sum_{t=m+1}^{T}E[(H_{t}\sum_{s=1}^{t-m}H_{s}w_{s,t})^{2}],

then the result follows from the martingale CLT.

Let
W_{t-1}=\sum_{s=1}^{t-m}H_{s}w_{s,t}
. We will prove this convergence
result by showing that

\left|\sum_{t=m+1}^{T}\left[E(H_{t}^{2}\,|\,\mathscr{E}_{t-1})-E(H_{t}^{2})\right]W_{t-1}^{2}\right|_{1}=o(1)

and

\left|\sum_{t=m+1}^{T}E(H_{t}^{2})\left[W_{t-1}^{2}-E(W_{t-1}^{2})\right]\right|_{1}=o(1),

respectively.

First, we consider
\left|\sum_{t=m+1}^{T}\left[E(H_{t}^{2}\,|\,\mathscr{E}_{t-1})-E(H_{t}^{2})\right]W_{t-1}^{2}\right|_{1}
. Let
H_{t}^{\ast}=E(H_{t}^{2}\,|\,\mathscr{E}_{t-1})-E(H_{t}^{2})
and thus
E(H_{t}^{\ast})=0
. For
0\leq j\leq m-1
, let
U(j)=\sum_{t=m+1}^{T}\left(\mathcal{P}_{t-j}H_{t}^{\ast}\right){W}_{t-1}^{2}
, then

\sum_{t=m+1}^{T}\left[E(H_{t}^{2}\,|\,\mathscr{E}_{t-1})-E(H_{t}^{2})\right]W_{t-1}^{2}=\sum_{t=m+1}^{T}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\mathcal{P}_{t-j}H_{t}^{\ast}\right)W_{t-1}^{2}=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}U(j).

Note that
\left\{\left(\mathcal{P}_{t-j}H_{t}^{\ast}\right)W_{t-1}^{2}\right\}_{t}
forms a martingale difference sequence since

E\left\{\left(\mathcal{P}_{t-j}H_{t}^{\ast}\right)W_{t-1}^{2}|\mathscr{E}_{t-j-1}\right\}=\left[E(H_{t}^{\ast}|\mathscr{E}_{t-j-1})-E(H_{t}^{\ast}|\mathscr{E}_{t-j-1})\right]W_{t-1}^{2}=0.

By using Burkholder inequality and the fact that
|\mathcal{P}_{t-j}H_{t}^{\ast}|_{2}\leq 2|H_{t}^{\ast}|_{2}<\infty
,

\displaystyle|U(j)|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle O(1)\sum_{t=m+1}^{T}|\left(\mathcal{P}_{t-j}H_{t}^{\ast}\right)W_{t-1}^{2}|_{2}^{2}\leq O(1)\sum_{t=m+1}^{T}|W_{t-1}|_{4}^{4}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle O(1)\sum_{t=m+1}^{T}(\sum_{s=1}^{t-m}w_{s,t}^{2})^{2}=O\left(T^{-1}\right).

It follows that

\left|\sum_{t=m+1}^{T}\left[E(H_{t}^{2}\,|\,\mathscr{E}_{t-1})-E(H_{t}^{2})\right]W_{t-1}^{2}\right|_{1}=o(1).

Now, we consider
\left|\sum_{t=2}^{T}E({H}_{t}^{2})\left[W_{t-1}^{2}-E(W_{t-1}^{2})\right]\right|_{1}
. For notational
simplicity, we omit the constant
E(H_{t}^{2})
and write

\displaystyle\sum_{t=m+1}^{T}[W_{t-1}^{2}-E\left(W_{t-1}^{2}\right)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{t=m+1}^{T}\sum_{s=1}^{t-m}\left(H_{s}^{2}-E\left(H_{s}^{2}\right)\right)w_{s,t}^{2}+2\sum_{t=m+2}^{T}\sum_{s_{1}=2}^{t-m}\sum_{s_{2}=1}^{s_{1}-1}\left[H_{s_{1}}H_{s_{2}}w_{s_{1},t}w_{s_{2},t}\right]

\displaystyle\eqqcolon

\displaystyle I_{3}+2I_{4}.

\displaystyle I_{3}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{s=1}^{T-m}\left(H_{s}^{2}-E(H_{s}^{2})\right)\left(T\sum_{t=s+m}^{T}w_{s,t}^{2}\right)=O_{P}(1/\sqrt{T}).

\displaystyle|I_{4}|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle O(1)\sum_{s_{1}=2}^{T-m}\left|H_{s_{1}}\sum_{s_{2}=1}^{s_{1}-1}H_{s_{2}}\sum_{t=s_{1}+m}^{T}w_{s_{1},t}w_{s_{2},t}\right|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle O(1)\sum_{s_{1}=2}^{T-m}\left|H_{s_{1}}\right|_{4}^{2}\left|\sum_{s_{2}=1}^{s_{1}-1}H_{s_{2}}\sum_{t=s_{1}+m}^{T}w_{s_{1},t}w_{s_{2},t}\right|_{4}^{2}

\displaystyle\|\frac{1}{TN}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-a_{i})\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{F}\|+a_{TN}\|\frac{1}{TN}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-a_{i})\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{g}_{it}^{\top}\mathbf{f}_{t}\mathbf{f}_{t}^{\top}\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O_{P}(1/\sqrt{TN})+a_{TN}O_{P}(1/\sqrt{T})=O_{P}(1/\sqrt{TN}).

(f). Write

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{F}}\cdot\frac{1}{TN}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{TN}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}=\frac{1}{TN}(\mathbf{F}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\top}+\mathcal{E}^{\ast})(\mathbf{F}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\top}+\mathcal{E})^{\ast,\top}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{TN}\mathbf{F}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{F}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}+\frac{1}{TN}\mathbf{F}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\top}\mathcal{E}^{\ast,\top}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}+\frac{1}{TN}\mathcal{E}^{\ast}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{F}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}+\frac{1}{TN}\mathcal{E}^{\ast}\mathcal{E}^{\ast,\top}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}.

Note that

\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\|\frac{1}{TN}\mathbf{F}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\top}\mathcal{E}^{\ast,\top}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}\|=O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\right)\ \ \text{ and }\ \ \frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\|\frac{1}{TN}\mathcal{E}^{\ast}\mathcal{E}^{\ast,\top}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}\|=O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T\wedge N}}\right)

by Lemma
A.10
(c) and
A.10
(a) respectively. Then part (f)
follows.

(g). Write

\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{F}^{\top}(\widehat{\mathbf{F}}-\mathbf{F}\mathbf{H})\frac{1}{TN}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}=\frac{1}{T^{2}N}\mathbf{F}^{\top}\mathbf{F}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\top}\mathcal{E}^{\ast,\top}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}+\frac{1}{T^{2}N}\mathbf{F}^{\top}\mathcal{E}^{\ast}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{F}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}+\frac{1}{T^{2}N}\mathbf{F}^{\top}\mathcal{E}^{\ast}\mathcal{E}^{\ast,\top}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}.

For the first term on the r.h.s., by Lemmas
A.10
(b) and (f), we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T^{2}N}\mathbf{F}^{\top}\mathbf{F}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\top}\mathcal{E}^{\ast,\top}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T^{2}N}\mathbf{F}^{\top}\mathbf{F}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\top}\mathcal{E}^{\ast,\top}\mathbf{F}\mathbf{H}+\frac{1}{T^{2}N}\mathbf{F}^{\top}\mathbf{F}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\top}\mathcal{E}^{\ast,\top}(\widehat{\mathbf{F}}-\mathbf{F}\mathbf{H})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O_{P}(1/\sqrt{TN})+O_{P}(1/\sqrt{N(T\wedge N)}).

In the same fashion, by Lemmas
A.10
(b) and (d), we obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T^{2}N}\mathbf{F}^{\top}\mathcal{E}^{\ast}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{F}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}=O_{P}(1/\sqrt{TN}),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T^{2}N}\mathbf{F}^{\top}\mathcal{E}^{\ast}\mathcal{E}^{\ast,\top}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}=O_{P}(1/\sqrt{T(T\wedge N)}).

Then part (g) follows.

(h). Write

\displaystyle\frac{1}{TN}\mathcal{E}^{\ast}\mathcal{E}^{\ast,\top}(\widehat{\mathbf{F}}-\mathbf{F}\mathbf{H})\widehat{\mathbf{V}}/(TN)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T^{2}N^{2}}\mathcal{E}^{\ast}\mathcal{E}^{\ast,\top}\mathbf{F}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\top}\mathcal{E}^{\ast,\top}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}+\frac{1}{T^{2}N^{2}}\mathcal{E}^{\ast}\mathcal{E}^{\ast,\top}\mathcal{E}^{\ast}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{F}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{T^{2}N^{2}}\mathcal{E}^{\ast}\mathcal{E}^{\ast,\top}\mathcal{E}^{\ast}\mathcal{E}^{\ast,\top}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}.

For the first term on the r.h.s.,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T^{2}N^{2}}\mathcal{E}^{\ast}\mathcal{E}^{\ast,\top}\mathbf{F}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\top}\mathcal{E}^{\ast,\top}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T^{2}N^{2}}\mathcal{E}^{\ast}\mathcal{E}^{\ast,\top}\mathbf{F}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\top}\mathcal{E}^{\ast,\top}\mathbf{F}\mathbf{H}+\frac{1}{T^{2}N^{2}}\mathcal{E}^{\ast}\mathcal{E}^{\ast,\top}\mathbf{F}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\top}\mathcal{E}^{\ast,\top}(\widehat{\mathbf{F}}-\mathbf{F}\mathbf{H})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{TN(T\wedge N)}}\right)+O_{P}\left(\frac{\sqrt{T}}{\sqrt{TN}(T\wedge N)}\right)

by Lemmas
A.10
(b)–(d) and (f). Similarly, by Lemmas
A.10

(c), (d) and (f) we can show that the second term is
O_{P}\left(\sqrt{T}/\sqrt{N(T\wedge N)}\right)
and the third term is
O_{P}(\sqrt{T}/(T\wedge N)^{3/2})
. It follows that
\|\frac{1}{TN}\mathcal{E}^{\ast}\mathcal{E}^{\ast,\top}(\widehat{\mathbf{F}}-\mathbf{F}\mathbf{H})\|=O_{P}(\sqrt{T}/(T\wedge N)^{3/2}).

(i). Let
\mathbf{G}_{i}
denote the
i^{th}
column of
\mathbf{G}
in which
\mathbf{G}=\{\mathbf{g}_{it}^{\top}\mathbf{f}_{t}\}_{T\times N}
. Write

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-a_{i})\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\ast,\top}(\widehat{\mathbf{F}}-\mathbf{F}\mathbf{H})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-a_{i})\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\ast,\top}\left(\frac{1}{TN}\mathbf{F}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\top}\mathcal{E}^{\ast,\top}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}+\frac{1}{TN}\mathcal{E}^{\ast}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{F}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}+\frac{1}{TN}\mathcal{E}^{\ast}\mathcal{E}^{\ast,\top}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}\right)(\frac{1}{TN}\widehat{\mathbf{V}})^{-1}.

For the first term on the r.h.s., we apply Lemmas
A.10
(b) and
(e)–(f) to obtain

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{T^{2}N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-a_{i})\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\ast,\top}\mathbf{F}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\top}\mathcal{E}^{\ast,\top}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{T^{2}N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-a_{i})\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\ast,\top}\mathbf{F}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\top}\mathcal{E}^{\ast,\top}\mathbf{F}\mathbf{H}\right\|+\left\|\frac{1}{T^{2}N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-a_{i})\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\ast,\top}\mathbf{F}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\top}\mathcal{E}^{\ast,\top}(\widehat{\mathbf{F}}-\mathbf{F}\mathbf{H})\right\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O_{P}(1/(TN))+O_{P}\left(\sqrt{T}/(TN\sqrt{(T\wedge N)})\right).

For the second term, by Lemmas
A.2
(g) and
A.10
(c), we have

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{T^{2}N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-a_{i})\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\ast,\top}\mathcal{E}^{\ast}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{F}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle a_{TN}\left\|\frac{1}{T^{2}N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-a_{i})\mathbf{G}_{i}^{\top}\mathcal{E}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{F}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}\right\|+a_{TN}^{2}\left\|\frac{1}{T^{2}N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-a_{i})\mathbf{G}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{G}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{F}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}\right\|

\displaystyle+\left\|\frac{1}{T^{2}N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-a_{i})\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\top}\mathcal{E}^{\ast}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{F}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}\right\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{TN}\left\|\frac{1}{T^{2}N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-a_{i})\mathbf{G}_{i}^{\top}\mathcal{E}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{F}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}\right\|+O_{P}(a_{TN}^{2})+O_{P}(1/N).

In addition, since
|\mathbf{G}_{i}^{\top}\mathcal{E}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}|_{2}=|\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{g}_{it}^{\top}\mathbf{f}_{t}\varepsilon_{jt}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{j}^{\top}|_{2}=O(\sqrt{TN})
by using
Lemma
A.1
(b), we then have
a_{TN}\left\|\frac{1}{T^{2}N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-a_{i})\mathbf{G}_{i}^{\top}\mathcal{E}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{F}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}\right\|=O_{P}(a_{TN}/\sqrt{TN})
.
Similarly, for the third term, we have

\left\|\frac{1}{T^{2}N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-a_{i})\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\ast,\top}\mathcal{E}^{\ast}\mathcal{E}^{\ast,\top}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}\right\|=O_{P}(a_{TN}/\sqrt{T\wedge N})+O_{P}(a_{TN}\sqrt{T}/(T\wedge N)).

This completes the proof.

∎

B.2
Verification of Assumption
4

In this section we verify Assumption
4
via a given
example. Consider a high-dimensional MA(
\infty
) process
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{B}_{j}\mathbf{e}_{t-j},
where
\mathbf{B}_{j}
’s are
N\times N
matrices. We make the following primitive conditions
on
\left\{\mathbf{B}_{j}\right\},

\{\mathbf{e}_{t-j}\}
and
\{\mathbf{f}_{t}\}.

Assumption B.1
.

(a)
\|\mathbf{B}_{j}\|_{2}=O(j^{-\alpha})
for some
\alpha>3
;

(b)
\{e_{it}\}
is independent over
(i,t)
with mean 0 and
variance 1, and
E|e_{it}|^{4}<\infty
, where
e_{it}
is the
i^{th}

element of
\mathbf{e}_{t}
.

Assumption B.2
.

\{\mathbf{f}_{t}\}
satisfy Assumption
3
,
\{\mathbf{f}_{t}\}
is independent of
\{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}\}
,
\|\mathbf{B}_{j}\|_{1}=O(j^{-\alpha})
and
\|\mathbf{B}_{j}\|_{\infty}=O(j^{-\alpha})
.

Below we verify that
\{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}\}
fulfills Assumption
4
(a)–(b) under Assumption
B.1
(a)–(b).
Similarly, Assumption
4
(c) is satisfied under Assumptions
B.1
–
B.2
.

B.2.1
Verification of Assumption
4
(a)–(b)

By Assumption
B.1
(a)-(b) and the independence between

\{\mathbf{e}_{t-j}\}
and
\{\mathbf{f}_{t}\}
,
E(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}|\mathbf{f}_{t})=E(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t})=0
and
E[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}^{\top}]=
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{B}_{j}\mathbf{B}_{j}^{\prime}\eqqcolon\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
. By the
triangle inequality and the submultiplicative property of the spectral norm,
we have

\left\|\Sigma_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{2}=\left\|\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{B}_{j}\mathbf{B}_{j}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left\|\mathbf{B}_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}j^{-2\alpha}=O(1).

This verifies Assumption
4
(a).

Let
\mathbf{B}_{j}=\{B_{j,kl}\}_{k,l\in[N]}
and
\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{B}_{j}=(B_{j,\,\centerdot 1},\ldots,B_{j,\,\centerdot N})

for any
\|\mathbf{v}\|=1
. As
\{e_{it}\}
are independent over
i
, we can write

\displaystyle E\left|\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{B}_{j}(\mathbf{e}_{0}-\mathbf{e}_{0}^{\prime})\right|^{4}=E\left|\sum_{l=1}^{N}B_{j,\,\centerdot l}^{2}(e_{l,0}-e_{l,0}^{\prime})^{2}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle+4E\left|\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}\sum_{k=l+1}^{N}B_{j,\,\centerdot l}B_{j,\,\centerdot k}(e_{l,0}-e_{l,0}^{\prime})(e_{k,0}-e_{k,0}^{\prime})\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle O(1)\left(\sum_{l=1}^{N}B_{j,\,\centerdot l}^{2}\right)^{2}+O(1)\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}\sum_{k=l+1}^{N}B_{j,\,\centerdot l}^{2}B_{j,\,\centerdot k}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle O(1)\left(\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{B}_{j}\mathbf{B}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{v}\right)^{2}=O(\|\mathbf{B}_{j}\|_{2}^{4}),

where the first inequality follows from some direct calculations, and the
second inequality follows from the fact that
\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}\sum_{k=l+1}^{N}B_{j,\,\centerdot l}^{2}B_{j,\,\centerdot k}^{2}\leq(\sum_{l=1}^{N}B_{j,\,\centerdot l}^{2})^{2}
. Based on the above
development, we have

\sup_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\|\mathbf{v}\|=1}\left|\mathbf{v}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{j}^{\ast})\right|_{4}=\sup_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\|\mathbf{v}\|=1}\left|\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{B}_{j}(\mathbf{e}_{0}-\mathbf{e}_{0}^{\prime})\right|_{4}=O(|\mathbf{B}_{j}|_{2})=O(j^{-\alpha}).

This verifies Assumption
4
(b).

B.2.2
Verification of Assumption
4
(c)

Let
\mathbf{f}_{t,t-m}
and
\varepsilon_{it,t-m}
be the coupled
version of
\mathbf{f}_{t}
and
\varepsilon_{it}
with
\mathbf{e}_{t-m}

replaced by
\mathbf{e}_{t-m}^{\prime}
. Since
\{\mathbf{f}_{t}\}
is
independent of
\{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}\}
, by Jensen inequality, we have

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{P}_{t-m}\left(\mathbf{f}_{t}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{it}\varepsilon_{is}\right)\right|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|E(\mathbf{f}_{t}\,|\,\mathscr{E}_{t-m})-E(\mathbf{f}_{t}\,|\,\mathscr{E}_{t-m-1})|_{2}\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{it}\varepsilon_{is}\right|_{2}

\displaystyle+|\mathbf{f}_{t}|_{2}\left|E\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{it}\varepsilon_{is}\,|\,\mathscr{E}_{t-m}\right)-E\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{it}\varepsilon_{is}\,|\,\mathscr{E}_{t-m-1}\right)\right|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|E(\mathbf{f}_{t}-\mathbf{f}_{t,t-m}\,|\,\mathscr{E}_{t-m})|_{2}\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{it}\varepsilon_{is}\right|_{2}

\displaystyle+|\mathbf{f}_{t}|_{2}\left|E\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\varepsilon_{it}\varepsilon_{is}-\varepsilon_{it,t-m}\varepsilon_{is,t-m})\,|\,\mathscr{E}_{t-m}\right)\right|_{2}.

Noting that
|E(\mathbf{f}_{t}-\mathbf{f}_{t,t-m}\,|\,\mathscr{E}_{t-m})|_{2}\leq|\mathbf{f}_{m}-\mathbf{f}_{m}^{\ast}|_{2}=O(m^{-\alpha})

and
|\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{it}\varepsilon_{is}|_{2}=O(1)
, it is sufficient to show the order of

\left|E\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\varepsilon_{it}\varepsilon_{is}-\varepsilon_{it,t-m}\varepsilon_{is,t-m})\,|\,\mathscr{E}_{t-m}\right)\right|_{2}.

Let
\mathbf{B}_{j,i\cdot}
denote the
i^{th}
column of
\mathbf{B}_{j}^{\top}
. Hence, we have
\varepsilon_{it}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{B}_{j,i\cdot}^{\top}\mathbf{e}_{t-j}
and
\varepsilon_{it,t-m}=\sum_{j=0,\neq m}^{\infty}\mathbf{B}_{j,i\cdot}^{\top}\mathbf{e}_{t-j}+\mathbf{B}_{m,i\cdot}^{\top}\mathbf{e}_{t-m}^{\prime}
. Then write

\displaystyle\left|E\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\varepsilon_{it}\varepsilon_{is}-\varepsilon_{it,t-m}\varepsilon_{is,t-m})\,|\,\mathscr{E}_{t-m}\right)\right|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|E\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\mathbf{B}_{m,i\cdot}^{\top}\mathbf{e}_{t-m}^{\prime}\mathbf{B}_{s+m-t,i\cdot}^{\top}\mathbf{e}_{t-m}^{\prime}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\mathbf{B}_{m,i\cdot}^{\top}\mathbf{e}_{t-m}\mathbf{B}_{s+m-t,i\cdot}^{\top}\mathbf{e}_{t-m})\,|\,\mathscr{E}_{t-m}\right)\right|_{2}

\displaystyle+\left|E\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{B}_{m,i\cdot}^{\top}(\mathbf{e}_{t-m}^{\prime}-\mathbf{e}_{t-m})(\sum_{j=0,\neq s+m-t}^{\infty}\mathbf{B}_{j,i\cdot}^{\top}\mathbf{e}_{s-j})\,|\,\mathscr{E}_{t-m}\right)\right|_{2}

\displaystyle+\left|E\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{B}_{s+m-t,i\cdot}^{\top}(\mathbf{e}_{t-m}^{\prime}-\mathbf{e}_{t-m})(\sum_{j=0,\neq m}^{\infty}\mathbf{B}_{j,i\cdot}^{\top}\mathbf{e}_{t-j})\,|\,\mathscr{E}_{t-m}\right)\right|_{2}

\displaystyle\eqqcolon

\displaystyle E_{1}+E_{2}+E_{3}.

For
E_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle E_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{l=1}^{N}B_{m,il}B_{s+m-t,il}(e_{l,t-m}^{2}-1)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1,\neq l}^{N}B_{m,il}B_{s+m-t,il}e_{l,t-m}e_{k,t-m}\right|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{l=1}^{N}B_{m,il}B_{s+m-t,il}(e_{l,t-m}^{2}-1)\right|_{2}

\displaystyle+\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1,\neq l}^{N}B_{m,il}B_{s+m-t,ik}e_{l,t-m}e_{k,t-m}\right|_{2}

\displaystyle\eqqcolon

\displaystyle E_{11}+E_{12}.

For
E_{11}
, by using Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{l,i=1}^{N}B_{m,il}^{2}\leq\|\mathbf{B}_{m}\|_{2}^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle E_{11}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{l=1}^{N}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}B_{m,il}B_{s+m-t,il}\right)^{2}E\left((e_{l,t-m}^{2}-1)^{2}\right)\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle O(1)\left(\max_{1\leq l\leq N}|\sum_{i=1}^{N}B_{s+m-t,il}^{2}|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{l,i=1}^{N}B_{m,il}^{2}\right)^{1/2}=O(\|\mathbf{B}_{m}\|_{2})=O(m^{-\alpha}).

Next, for
E_{12}
, we have

\displaystyle E_{12}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{l=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1,\neq l}^{N}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}B_{m,il}B_{s+m-t,ik}\right)^{2}E\left(e_{l,t-m}^{2}e_{k,t-m}^{2}\right)\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle O(1)\left(\sum_{l=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1,\neq l}^{N}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}B_{m,il}B_{s+m-t,ik}\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle O(1)\left(\max_{k}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|B_{s+m-k,ik}|\max_{k}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|B_{s+m-t,jk}|\max_{l}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|B_{m,jl}|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{l=1}^{N}|B_{m,il}|\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O(\sqrt{\|\mathbf{B}_{m}\|_{1}\|\mathbf{B}_{m}\|_{\infty}})=O(m^{-\alpha}).

Similarly, we can show that
E_{2}
is also
O(\|\mathbf{B}_{m}\|_{2})=O(m^{-\alpha})
for some
\alpha>3
.

Finally, we consider the third term
E_{3}
. By using arguments as
used in the study of
E_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle\left|E\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{B}_{s+m-t,i\cdot}^{\top}(\mathbf{e}_{t-m}^{\prime}-\mathbf{e}_{t-m})(\sum_{j=0,\neq m}^{\infty}\mathbf{B}_{j,i\cdot}^{\top}\mathbf{e}_{t-j})\,|\,\mathscr{E}_{t-m}\right)\right|_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{B}_{s+m-t,i\cdot}^{\top}\mathbf{e}_{t-m}\sum_{j=m+1}^{\infty}\mathbf{B}_{j,i\cdot}^{\top}\mathbf{e}_{t-j}\right|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j=m+1}^{\infty}\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{B}_{s+m-t,i\cdot}^{\top}\mathbf{e}_{t-m}\mathbf{B}_{j,i\cdot}^{\top}\mathbf{e}_{t-j}\right|_{2}=\sum_{j=m+1}^{\infty}O(m^{-\alpha})=O(m^{-\alpha+1}).

This completes the verification of Assumption
4
(c).

B.3.1

simulation studies. The details of the moving block bootstrap (MBB)
procedure can be found in Appendix C.6 of
Fu et al. (2023)
, in which
they suggest a MBB procedure in order to deal with serially correlated
errors.

serial and cross-sectional correlations. The simulation results for the 5%

. Overall, we can see that the MBB

T
. This
result is similar to those in Appendix C.6 of
Fu et al. (2023)
, in which
they find that SW and FHW tests tend to over-reject but their sizes decrease
with the increasing sample size.
