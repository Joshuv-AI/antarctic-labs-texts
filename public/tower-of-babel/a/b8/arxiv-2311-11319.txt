GeoSAM: Fine-tuning SAM with Multi-Modal Prompts for Mobility Infrastructure Segmentation Journal: arxiv
Rafi Ibn Sultan, Chengyin Li, Hui Zhu, Prashant Khanduri, Marco Brocanelli, Dongxiao Zhu

Abstract
In geographical image segmentation, performance is often constrained by the limited availability of training data and a lack of generalizability, particularly for segmenting mobility infrastructure such as roads, sidewalks, and crosswalks. Vision foundation models like the Segment Anything Model (SAM), pre-trained on millions of natural images, have demonstrated impressive zero-shot segmentation performance, providing a potential solution. However, SAM struggles with geographical images, such as aerial and satellite imagery, due to its training being confined to natural images and the narrow features and textures of these objects blending into their surroundings. To address these challenges, we propose Geographical SAM (GeoSAM), a SAM-based framework that fine-tunes SAM with automatically generated multi-modal prompts, combining point prompts from a pre-trained task-specific model as primary visual guidance and text prompts from a large language model as secondary semantic guidance to enhance model comprehension. GeoSAM outperforms existing approaches for mobility infrastructure segmentation in both familiar and completely unseen regions by at least 5% in mIoU, representing a significant leap in leveraging foundation models to segment mobility infrastructure, including both road and pedestrian infrastructure in geographical images. The source code is available in this GitHub Repository .

Foundation Model-Based Geographical Image Segmentation

Task-agnostic Vision foundation models, in their essence, aim to address the shortcomings of traditional segmentation models by being readily adaptable through prompts to previously unknown classes for various downstream tasks.
While the application of foundation models e.g. SAM in the field of geographical imagery is not as extensive as in other domains, it has been utilized in several research endeavors. Considering the zero-shot SAM approaches, several works, including

In these instances, SAM does not require detailed domain-specific knowledge; sensible prompts alone suffice.

Method

3.1
Problem Definition

Given a geographical/ remote sensing image i.e. aerial or satellite imagery dataset
D
containing
n
sample images, where each image
I\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times 3}
represents a standard high-resolution RGB image with height
H
, width
W
, and 3 color channels. We implement GeoSAM, a semantic segmentation model to obtain the multi-class segmentation maps
S
for each image, where
S\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W}
and each pixel in
S
corresponds to one of the three classes: background, pedestrian infrastructure (sidewalk/crosswalk), and road infrastructure.

3.2
SAM: Background

SAM comprises three components: an image encoder (
\operatorname{Enc_{I}}
), a prompt encoder (
\operatorname{Enc_{P}}
), and a mask decoder, (
\operatorname{Dec_{M}}
). SAM, designed as a model that can be prompted, accepts an image, denoted as
I\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times 3}
, and a collection of prompts, denoted as
P
(e.g., a point, a box, a dense mask, and text prompts). In its operation, SAM initially employs
\operatorname{Enc_{I}}
to extract features from the input image. It then utilizes
\operatorname{Enc_{P}}
to transform the human-provided prompts, which have a length of
k
, into prompt tokens. Specifically:
F_{I}=\operatorname{Enc_{I}}(I),\quad T_{P}=\operatorname{Enc_{P}}(P)
, where
F_{I}
is the feature embedding of the input image where
F_{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times C}
,
H
and
W
represent the resolution of the image feature map, and
C
denotes the feature dimension. Likewise,
T_{P}
is the joint feature embedding of both of the point and text prompts where
T_{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times C}
,
K
is the combined length of the prompts. Following this, the encoded image features (
F_{I}
) and prompt tokens (
T_{P}
) are supplied to the decoder (
\operatorname{Dec_{M}}
), which employs attention-based mechanisms, including cross-attention and self-attention, for feature interaction. The decoder dynamically updates the prompt tokens based on the input image features and combines them with the image embedding to predict the segmentation mask. The mask output is defined as:

S=\operatorname{Dec_{M}}\left(F_{I},T_{P}\right),

where
S
represents the output segmentation mask predicted by SAM. The prompt tokens (
T_{P}
) play a crucial role in guiding the decoder during mask generation by incorporating both spatial and semantic information.

Multi-Modal Prompt Generation

Point Prompts
GeoSAM employs an automated mechanism (illustrated in Figure
3
) to generate point prompts by leveraging a task-specific pre-trained traditional model
f_{\text{pre}}
. Given an input image
I\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times 3}
, the model produces a pseudo-label segmentation map
M_{\text{pseudo}}
:

M_{\text{pseudo}}=f_{\text{pre}}(I),\quad M_{\text{pseudo}}\in\{0,1,\dots,C_{\text{pre}}-1\}^{H\times W},

(1)

where
C_{\text{pre}}
denotes the number of classes in the pre-trained model. From
M_{\text{pseudo}}
, we randomly sample a set of point prompts
x_{p}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
, where each point
x_{i}
is a coordinate in the image domain
\Omega_{I}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The set
x_{p}
is partitioned into foreground and background points for each of the classes:

\displaystyle x_{p}^{\text{fg}}

\displaystyle=\{x_{i}\in\Omega_{I}\mid M_{\text{pseudo}}(x_{i})\in\mathcal{C}_{\text{fg}}\},\quad|x_{p}^{\text{fg}}|=k_{1},

(2)

\displaystyle x_{p}^{\text{bg}}

\displaystyle=\{x_{i}\in\Omega_{I}\mid M_{\text{pseudo}}(x_{i})\in\mathcal{C}_{\text{bg}}\},\quad|x_{p}^{\text{bg}}|=k_{2},

\displaystyle x_{p}

\displaystyle=x_{p}^{\text{fg}}\cup x_{p}^{\text{bg}},\quad k=k_{1}+k_{2},

where
\mathcal{C}_{\text{fg}}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\text{bg}}
are the sets of class labels corresponding to foreground and background, respectively. These point prompts are transformed by the prompt encoder
\operatorname{Enc_{P}}
into point embeddings of
C
dimension:

T_{P_{x_{p}}}=\operatorname{Enc_{P}}(x_{p})\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times C},

(3)

Here, the accuracy of
M_{\text{pseudo}}
is not critical; as long as the generated points are approximately within the foreground or background regions of the class, they can effectively guide the focus of the segmentation process.

For our experiments, we utilize nnU-Net
[
9
]
as the pre-trained traditional model
f_{\text{pre}}
, which outputs a multi-class segmentation map with classes for roads and sidewalks/crosswalks, and background. We define the class sets as:

\mathcal{C}_{\text{fg}}^{\text{road}}=\{\text{road}\},\quad\mathcal{C}_{\text{fg}}^{\text{pedestrian}}=\{\text{sidewalk},\text{crosswalk}\},\quad\mathcal{C}_{\text{bg}}=\{\text{background}\}.

Each binary task utilizes its respective foreground and background point prompts to provide GeoSAM with class-specific information.

Text Prompts
In addition to geometric guidance from point prompts, GeoSAM incorporates semantic context through text prompts
x_{t}
, enriching the model’s understanding of the objects to be segmented. Oftentimes a single pixel can belong to multiple objects, such as both a road and a crosswalk, the text prompt provides a description of the specific class, enhancing the model’s ability to distinguish between overlapping classes. This semantic information strengthens the prompting mechanism by removing ambiguity. Text prompts are crafted as a description related to the mobility infrastructure class, formatted as,
x_{t}
: “[CLS]: Description.", where [CLS] is the designated class i.e. roads or sidewalk/crosswalk. An example text prompt might be “Roads: paved surfaces, vehicle lanes.", as illustrated in Figure
2
. These text prompts are dynamically generated for every image using a large language model, specifically the “Chat Completions API” of GPT-4
[
48
]
. This approach provides the model with diverse semantic meanings of the classes, enabling more robust class definitions throughout. The following prompt is provided to GPT-4 to create these class definitions:

“role: system, content: You are a creative assistant, skilled in providing detailed visual descriptions of objects as seen in aerial imagery.”

“role: user, content: Print out a visual description (don’t mention their names) that can be seen from aerial images of [CLS] (in one line, 4 to 5 words, not more, not less).”

Following the default configuration of the pre-trained CLIP
[
18
]
text encoder, the generated descriptions are converted into class-based embeddings. This embeddings are denoted as
T_{P_{x_{t}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k^{\prime}\times C}
, where
k^{\prime}
is the number of sentences. CLIP’s text encoder processes tokenized text sequences through a transformer model to generate fixed-dimensional vector representations, which are then L2-normalized along the feature dimension. In CLIP, the embedding dimension
C
of
T_{Px_{t}}
is 512, while SAM operates with
C=256
. To bridge this, a trainable linear projection layer is used during training to map between these dimensions.

3.4
Fine-Tuning

The pre-learned position embeddings from SAM’s pre-training (indicating whether a point is in the foreground or background) are appended with the point prompt embeddings,
T_{Px_{p}}
. To process the text prompt embeddings,
T_{Px_{t}}
, we leverage CLIP’s inherent capability to align text and image embeddings in the same space. To form the joint embeddings
T_{P}
, the text prompt embeddings
T_{P_{x_{t}}}
of each class are concatenated with the corresponding foreground point embeddings of that class along the feature dimension. Hence, the
T_{Px_{p}}
contributes a geometrical position sense, while
T_{Px_{t}}
provides a rich semantic understanding of the classes, enhancing the model’s ability to comprehend them. Then,
T_{P}
along with
F_{I}
are concatenated and supplied to the decoder.

The decoder utilizes a bidirectional transformer, where image embeddings are updated through repeated Self Attention (SA) and Cross Attention (CA) with prompts.

\hat{T_{P}}=\text{MLP}(\text{CA}(\text{SA}(T_{P}),F_{I})),\vskip-14.22636pt

(4)

\hat{F_{I}}=\text{CA}(F_{I},\hat{T_{P}}),

(5)

where
\hat{T_{P}}
represents the refined set of prompt embeddings, and
\hat{F_{I}}
, denotes the updated set of visual embeddings after refining the embeddings by attending to the positional and semantic information of the prompts, enabling context-aware representations that ultimately produce the segmentation map.

SAM is a binary-class segmentation model, and the generated segmentation map
S
is a binary-class map that includes only foreground and background information. Hence, GeoSAM adapts this to multiple channels in the segmentation map for road and pedestrian infrastructure segmentation respectively throughout the whole pipeline and processes both information together and outputs a multi-channel segmentation map. The model calculates the loss by comparing the output segmentation maps to the multi-channel ground truth images, produced through one-hot encoding.

GeoSAM then begins updating the decoder’s parameters in this phase while keeping the other parameters frozen, using the Decoder-Only Fine-Tuning technique of PEFT, a popular fine-tuning strategy of foundation models where a small portion of the foundation models is updated to make it compatible with a specific task
[
42
]
. It is a standard PEFT technique where the encoder’s general ability to understand or encode information is already satisfactory, and the emphasis is on improving how the model generates or manipulates outputs
[
49
]
.

Loss Function
We opt for Dice Focal Loss, combining Dice and Focal Loss. As mobility infrastructure occupies only a small fraction of high-resolution remote sensing images, this combined Loss puts focus on both overlapping accuracy and severe class imbalance.

The Dice Loss, based on the Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC), can be expressed as

\mathcal{L}_{\text{Dice}}=1-\frac{2\sum_{c=1}^{C}\sum_{i=1}^{N}S_{i}^{c}G_{i}^{c}}{\sum_{c=1}^{C}\sum_{i=1}^{N}S_{i}^{c}+\sum_{c=1}^{C}\sum_{i=1}^{N}G_{i}^{c}},

(6)

where
G_{i}^{c}
represents the ground truth binary indicator of class label
c
for the pixel
i
, and
S_{i}^{c}
denotes the corresponding predicted segmentation probability.

Focal Loss
[
50
]
is a weighted Cross Entropy Loss designed to focus on hard-to-classify examples while down-weighting easy-to-classify examples. Focal Loss also introduces
\alpha
and
\gamma
as the balancing and focusing parameters, respectively. The balancing factor
\alpha
assigns different weights to different classes to provide more importance to the minority class whereas the focusing parameter
\gamma
affects how much the loss is focused on hard-to-classify examples
[
51
]
. Parameters
\alpha
and
\gamma
are combined with the basic Cross Entropy Loss to get the Equation of Focal Loss:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\text{Focal}}&=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\alpha_{c}\left[\left(1-S_{i}^{c}\right)^{\gamma}G_{i}^{c}\log(S_{i}^{c})\right].\\
\end{split}

(7)

Finally, both losses are combined uniformly to get the overall Dice Focal Loss used for fine-tuning the GeoSAM.

\mathcal{L}_{\text{DiceFocal}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{Dice}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{Focal}}.

(8)

Experiments Setup
We adopted ViT-H
[
13
]
as the encoder version of SAM and initialized the model with pre-trained weights from SAM’s ViT-H version. Following the original SAM paper settings
[
17
]
, the choice of optimizer was the AdamW (
\beta 1=0.9
,
\beta 2=0.999
), with an initial learning rate set at
e^{-5}
and weight decay of 0.1 and no data augmentation techniques were applied.
To have an adaptable learning rate, a cosine annealing learning rate scheduler was employed with a maximum learning rate decaying smoothly to a minimum value (
e^{-7}
) over the course of training. A pre-trained nnU-Net
[
9
]
model (trained on the training dataset), has been selected as the pre-trained traditional model for point prompt generation. We adopted CLIP’s ViT-B version
[
18
]
as the text encoder. Finally, for point prompt generation, we selected 2000 foreground and 1000 background points. All the experiments were conducted on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU with 24 GB of memory and Python 3.10.9. We use a total of 100 epochs to train GeoSAM as well as the other baseline models. While doing the training for GeoSAM, we followed PEFT techniques, where we kept the parameters of the encoder part (both image and prompt encoder) frozen and only updated the gradients of the decoder.

Postprocessing
GeoSAM implements some postprocessing techniques to improve cosmetically the initially generated segmentation maps from the mask decoder. Techniques like morphological erosion and dilation
[
55
]
are used to enhance the cosmetics of the output. As our priority is path creation over pixel classification, we focus more on the connectivity of the routes. In segmentation maps representing real-world pedestrian paths, scattered isolated regions indicate inaccuracies. We use erosion to eliminate these isolated regions. Abrupt interruptions or gaps in connected paths indicate segmentation failures, addressed by dilation to connect disjointed regions, aligning the map closer to the ground truth. We apply a (10
\times
10) filter across a (1024
\times
1024) resolution segmentation map to perform erosion and dilation on covered regions. These operations are repeated for 10 iterations to refine the map effectively. Figure
5
shows the application of these techniques. Visualizations of each class separately reveal the removal of isolated regions and established connections closer to the ground truth. We compare the initial and postprocessing outputs to highlight quality differences.

Generalization Performance of GeoSAM (
Q2
)
When evaluating the performance of GeoSAM on the generalizability dataset from Cambridge, MA, a performance decrease is observed across all models compared to the results from Washington, D.C., due to the anticipated data shift from differing geological regions. Despite this, GeoSAM significantly outperforms other SOTA models in generalization, achieving at least twice the performance of the second-best model. This underscores the struggles of traditional models with generalizability, as their capabilities are constrained by the training data, particularly in regions like Cambridge with distinct visual characteristics. However, foundation models like SAM, with their extensive segmentation capabilities, adapt more effectively to diverse and unknown scenarios. With the addition of automated guided prompts, GeoSAM further enhances this adaptability, demonstrating superior performance (5% and 9% increase over the second-best model in mIoU and mAP) in such challenging settings.
This supports our assertion that GeoSAM is scalable and possesses superior generalizability capabilities due to being based on a foundation model compared to traditional models.

Necessity of Auto Prompt Generation (
Q3
)
Table
3
highlights the impact of prompts on GeoSAM’s performance in the test dataset, specifically analyzing the effect of the ratio and number of point prompts. Since point prompts emulate user clicks on images, we examine the optimal number of clicks generated by the automated point prompt generation system. From the results, we determine that a 2:1 foreground-to-background click ratio with 2000:1000 points (a total of 3000 points) provides optimal performance, establishing this configuration as the default. This result emphasizes that objects like roads and sidewalks, being sparse and dispersed across the image, necessitate numerous distributed points for effective segmentation, a task that would be exceedingly laborious if done manually. Even with 150 total points (first row), pedestrian infrastructure performance drops by 8%, highlighting the sensitivity of the model to the number of prompts. Additionally, if foreground points are fewer than or equal to background points, performance deteriorates, as excessive background points overly constrain the flexibility of foreground segmentation.
These findings underscore the necessity of automated prompt generation, as manually crafting such a large number of prompts is practically infeasible.

Ablation Study
The first ablation study (referred to in Table
4
), assessed the influence of key components, including incorporating point prompts, text prompts, and fine-tuning the decoder, on GeoSAM’s effectiveness. It is evident that relying solely on point prompts results in a significant performance drop, with an 8% decrease in pedestrian infrastructure segmentation. This underscores the importance of text prompts and their role in incorporating semantic understanding through natural language to resolve ambiguities. However, testing GeoSAM with only text prompts also yielded inferior results, with a 22% decrease from the optimal results in pedestrian infrastructure segmentation. This supports the hypothesis that text prompts alone are insufficient for such a nuanced task, as they are limited by the semantic interpretation capabilities of the text encoder. Nevertheless, as secondary prompts, they effectively enhance the model’s focus by providing additional context to the decoder during segmentation. Moreover, removing the fine-tuned decoder caused a notable performance drop (last row of the table). Even with auto-generated point prompts, zero-shot SAM produced significantly inferior results, with a 26% decrease in pedestrian infrastructure segmentation. This indicates that although the original SAM’s encoder possesses strong segmentation abilities, the decoder is inadequate in producing segmentation maps for this domain, and through fine-tuning it gets familiar and adapts to the unique challenges of geographical images.

The second ablation study supports our claim that the performance of a pre-trained traditional model used to generate point prompts does not need to be highly critical, as long as the prompts are relatively well-positioned. As shown in Table
5
, replacing nnU-Net (our preferred choice) with UNet or Swin UNETR for generating prompts results in only a minor performance drop in the test dataset. Even with alternative backbones, GeoSAM continues to outperform other SOTA models listed in Table
2
.

5
Conclusion

GeoSAM leverages SAM’s capabilities for mobility infrastructure segmentation within geographical images, offering significant social impact, particularly for pedestrians. It employs multi-modal prompts, including point and text prompts, along with fine-tuning the foundation segmentation model SAM. Unlike existing methods, our developed training and end-to-end inference pipeline is transferable to other geo-locations and multiple classes using any pre-trained traditional model with fine-tuned decoders on different datasets. We believe this methodology is generic, reproducible, and adaptable for various domain segmentation tasks.

Limitation
Future work aims to extend the application of GeoSAM to additional objects like stairs, islands/bridges, potholes, and even more imaging modalities, as well as to various other cities for a more comprehensive analysis.
