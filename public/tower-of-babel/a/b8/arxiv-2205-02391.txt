Orbital integrals and normalizations of measures
Julia Gordon

Abstract
. This note provides an informal introduction, with examples, to some technical aspects of the re-normalization of measures on orbital integrals used in the work of Langlands, Frenkel-Langlands-Ngô, and Altug on Beyond Endoscopy. In particular, we survey different relevant measures on algebraic tori and explain the connection with the Tamagawa numbers. We work out the example of GL 2 \operatorname{GL}_{2} in complete detail. The Appendix by Matthew Koster illustrates, for the Lie algebras 𝔰 ​ 𝔩 2 \mathfrak{sl}_{2} and 𝔰 ​ 𝔬 3 \mathfrak{so}_{3} , the relation between the so-called geometric measure on the orbits and Kirillov’s measure on co-adjoint orbits in the linear dual of the Lie algebra.



1.
Introduction

Haar measure on a locally compact topological group is unique up to a constant. In many situations the normalization matters (as we shall see below). In particular, it seems that some measures are more convenient than others in the approach to Beyond Endoscopy by Frenkel-Langlands-Ngô, Arthur, and Altug. The main goal of this note is to track some of the normalizations of the measures that arise in the literature on orbital integrals on reductive groups over non-Archimedean local fields, and provide an introduction to this aspect of Altug’s lectures.
In particular, our first goal is to give an exposition of the formula (3.31) in
[
FLN10
]
, which is the first technical step towards Poisson summation.
The second goal is to summarize the relationship between measures on
p
-adic manifolds, point-counts over the residue field, and local
L
-functions. These relations are scattered over the literature, and the aim here is to collect the references in one place, and provide some examples.

Fundamentally, there are two approaches to choosing a normalization of a Haar measure on the set of
F
-points of an algebraic group
for a local field
F
: one can consider a measure associated with a specified differential form; or one can choose a specific compact subgroup and prescribe the volume of that subgroup. As we shall see, both approaches have certain advantages, and converting between these two kinds of normalizations can be surprisingly tricky. All the objects we consider here will be affine algebraic varieties, and we will only consider algebraic differential forms. To avoid confusion, we will try to consistently denote varieties by bold letters, while various sets of rational points will be denotes by letters in the usual font.

In this context, we start, in §
2
, with a quick survey of A.Weil’s definition of the measure on the set
\mathbf{V}(F)
of
F
-points of an affine variety
\mathbf{V}

associated with an algebraic volume form on
\mathbf{V}
. We discuss the relation between this measure and counting rational points of
\mathbf{V}
over the residue field, and the results
on the various measures on
\mathbf{T}(F)
for an algebraic torus
\mathbf{T}
that follow. Next in §
3
, we compare
two natural measures on the orbits of the adjoint action of an algebraic group
\mathbf{G}(F)
on itself. This comparison is the main reason for writing this note. More specifically, we introduce Steinberg map and derive the relationship between two measures on the orbits:
the so-called
geometric
measure, obtained by considering the stable orbits as fibres of Steinberg map, and the measure obtained as a quotient of two natural measures coming from volume forms. In §
4
, we combine the outcome with the results
of §
2
to obtain the relationship between the geometric measure and the so-called
canonical
measure (which is the one most frequently used to define orbital integrals). We do the
\operatorname{GL}_{2}
example in detail.
Finally, in §
5
, we assemble the local results into a global calculation, first, in the context of the analytic class number formula, and then in the case of Eichler-Selberg Trace Formula.

2.
Volume forms and point-counting

Everywhere in this note,
F
stands for a non-Archimedean local field (of characteristic zero or positive characteristic), with the ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{F}
and residue field
k_{F}
of cardinality
q
. We denote its
uniformizing element
(a
uniformizer
for short)
by
\varpi
; by definition,
\varpi
is a generator of the maximal ideal of
\mathcal{O}_{F}
. We denote the normalized valuation on
F
by
\operatorname{ord}
; thus,
\operatorname{ord}(\varpi)=1
.

2.1.
The measure on the affine line

We start with choosing, once and for all, an additive Haar measure on the affine line.
For a non-Archimedean local field
F
, we normalize the additive Haar measure on
F
so that
\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{O}_{F})=1
.
For an affine line over
F
, given a choice of the coordinate
x
, there is an invariant differential form
dx
; we declare that the associated measure
|dx|
on
\mathbb{A}^{1}(F)
also gives volume
1
to the ring of integers (this choice is analogous
to setting up the ‘unit interval’ on the
x
-axis over the reals and declaring that the interval
[0,1]
has ‘volume’ (
i.e.
, length)
1
with respect to the measure
|dx|
).
Now that this choice is made, any non-vanishing top degree differential form
\omega
(defined over
F
or any finite extension of
F
)
on a
d
-dimensional
F
-variety
\mathbf{V}
determines a
measure
|\omega|
on the set
\mathbf{V}(F)
of its
F
-points, where
|\cdot|
stands for the absolute value on
F

(respectively, its unique extension to the field of definition of
\omega
). Thus, our definition of the measure associated with a volume form is such that for the additive group

\mathbb{G}_{a}
both approaches to normalizing the measure give the same natural measure: the measure that gives
\mathbb{G}_{a}(\mathcal{O}_{F})
volume
1
.
We shall see that such a measure is closely related to counting points over the residue field.

Here we will only need to consider the case of reductive algebraic groups. We start with algebraic tori, where the volumes already carry interesting arithmetic information.

2.2.
Tori

Let
\mathbf{T}
be an algebraic torus defined over
F
. Let
F^{\operatorname{sep}}
be the separable closure of
F
.
As discussed above, to define a measure on

T:=\mathbf{T}(F)
one can start with a differential form or with a compact subgroup. To define either, we first need a choice of coordinates on
\mathbf{T}
. One natural choice, albeit not defined over
F
unless
\mathbf{T}
is
F
-split, comes from any basis of the character lattice of
\mathbf{T}
.
Let
\chi_{1},\dots,\chi_{r}
, where
r
is the rank of
\mathbf{T}
, be any set of generators of
X^{\ast}(\mathbf{T})
over
\mathbb{Z}
(the characters a priori are defined over
F^{\operatorname{sep}}
). We note that this choice is equivalent to a choice of an isomorphism
\mathbf{T}\simeq\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}
over
F^{\operatorname{sep}}
.
Then we can define a volume form (defined over
F^{\operatorname{sep}}
but
not over
F
, unless
\mathbf{T}
is split over
F
):

(2)

\omega_{T}=\frac{d\chi_{1}}{\chi_{1}}\wedge\ldots\wedge\frac{d{\chi_{r}}}{\chi_{r}}.

The group of
F
-points
\mathbf{T}(F)
has a unique maximal compact subgroup in the
p
-adic topology;
we denote it by
T^{c}
, following the notation of
[
Shy77
]
. Ono gave the description of this subgroup in terms of
characters:

T^{c}=\{t\in\mathbf{T}(F):|\chi(t)|=1\text{ for }\chi\in X^{\ast}(\mathbf{T})_{F}\},

where
X^{\ast}(\mathbf{T})_{F}
is the sublattice of
X^{\ast}(\mathbf{T})
consisting of the characters defined over
F
.

Question 1:

What is the volume of
T^{c}
with respect to the measure
|\omega_{T}|
?

The question is well-defined because any other
\mathbb{Z}
-basis of
X^{\ast}(\mathbf{T})
would differ from
\{\chi_{i}\}
by a
\mathbb{Z}
-matrix of determinant
\pm 1
, and hence the resulting volume form would give rise to the same measure.

The complete answer to this question is quite involved and requires machinery beyond the scope of this note.
Here we show some basic examples illustrating the easy part and the difficulty, and provide further references in §
2.2.3
.

Example 2.2
.

\mathbf{T}
is
F
-split.
For
\mathbb{G}_{m}
, we have: the invariant form as above is
dx/x
, where
x=\chi_{1}:\mathbb{G}_{m}\to\mathbb{G}_{m}
is the identity character and the natural coordinate, and

\mathbb{G}_{m}^{c}=\mathcal{O}_{F}^{\times}
.
The volume calculation gives:

\displaystyle\operatorname{vol}_{\left|\frac{dx}{x}\right|}(\mathcal{O}_{F}^{\times})=\int_{\mathcal{O}_{F}^{\times}}\frac{1}{|x|}|dx|=\int_{\mathcal{O}_{F}^{\times}}|dx|=\operatorname{vol}_{|dx|}(\mathcal{O}_{F})-\operatorname{vol}_{|dx|}(\varpi\mathcal{O}_{F})

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{q}=\frac{\#k_{F}^{\times}}{q},

as predicted by (
1
).
Then for an
F
-split torus of rank
r
,

\operatorname{vol}_{|\omega_{T}|}(T^{c})=\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)^{r}.

The next easiest case is Weil restriction of scalars.

Example 2.3
.

Let
E/F
be a quadratic extension, and
\mathbf{T}:=\operatorname{Res}_{E/F}\mathbb{G}_{m}
.
Assume that the residue characteristic
p\neq 2
.

We write
E=F(\sqrt{\epsilon})
, where
\epsilon
is any non-square in
F
, and we can choose it to be in
\mathcal{O}_{F}
without loss of generality.
By definition,
\mathbf{T}
has two characters defined over
E
, call them
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
and think of them as
E
-coordinates on
\mathbf{T}(E)
.
Then the volume form
\omega_{T}
is simply

\omega_{T}=\frac{dz_{1}}{z_{1}}\wedge\frac{dz_{2}}{z_{2}}
. Note that it is defined over
E
but not over
F
.
We can try to rewrite it in
F
-coordinates:
we write
z_{1}=x+\sqrt{\epsilon}y,z_{2}=x-\sqrt{\epsilon}y
, and get:

(3)

\displaystyle\omega_{T}=\frac{d(x+\sqrt{\epsilon}y)}{x+\sqrt{\epsilon}y}\wedge\frac{d(x-\sqrt{\epsilon}y)}{x-\sqrt{\epsilon}y}

\displaystyle=\frac{(dx+\sqrt{\epsilon}dy)\wedge(dx-\sqrt{\epsilon}dy)}{x^{2}-\epsilon y^{2}}=\frac{-2\sqrt{\epsilon}}{N_{E/F}(x+\sqrt{\epsilon}y)}dx\wedge dy,

where
N_{E/F}
is the norm map.

We note that
\mathbf{T}(F)=E^{\times}
as a set, and the norm map is the generator of the group of
F
-characters
of
\mathbf{T}
.
Thus the subgroup
T^{c}
of
\mathbf{T}(F)
is

T^{c}=\{x+\sqrt{\epsilon}y\in E^{\times}:|x^{2}-{\epsilon}y^{2}|_{F}=1\}.

Its volume with respect to the measure
|\omega_{T}|
is:

(4)

\operatorname{vol}_{|\omega_{T}|}(T^{c})=|2\sqrt{\epsilon}|\int_{\{(x,y)\in F^{2}:|x^{2}-\epsilon y^{2}|=1\}}dxdy.

Thus we have reduced the computation of the volume of
T^{c}
with respect to the volume form
\omega_{T}
to the computation of the volume of the subset of
\mathbb{A}^{2}(F)
, which we denote by
C
, defined by

C:=\{(x,y)\in F^{2}:|x^{2}-\epsilon y^{2}|=1\}

with respect to the usual measure on the plane
|dx\wedge dy|
(note that
C
is open in
\mathbb{A}^{2}(F)
in the
p
-adic topology).
The computation of this volume illustrates the way to use point-counting over the residue field, and for this reason we do it in detail.
There are two cases:

\epsilon
is a unit (i.e.,
E/F
is unramified), and
\epsilon
is not a unit.

First, consider the unramified case. Note that
\operatorname{ord}(x^{2}-\epsilon y^{2})=\min(\operatorname{ord}(x^{2}),\operatorname{ord}(y^{2}))
since
\epsilon
is a non-square unit.
Then our set can be decomposed as:

C=\{(x,y):x\in\mathcal{O}_{F}^{\times},y\in\mathcal{O}_{F}\}\sqcup\{(x,y):x\in\mathcal{O}_{F}\setminus\mathcal{O}_{F}^{\times},y\in\mathcal{O}_{F}^{\times}\},

and its volume with respect to the affine plane measure
|dx\wedge dy|
is, therefore:

\operatorname{vol}_{|dx\wedge dy|}(C)=\frac{(q-1)q}{q^{2}}+\frac{1}{q}\frac{q-1}{q}=\frac{q-1}{q}\left(1+\frac{1}{q}\right).

There is an alternative (and more insightful) way to do this calculation:
first, as above, observe that necessarily the set
C
is contained in
\mathcal{O}_{F}^{2}
. Then
consider the reduction mod
\varpi
map
(x,y)\mapsto(\bar{x},\bar{y})
from
\mathcal{O}_{F}^{2}
to
k_{F}^{2}
, where
\varpi
is the uniformizer of the valuation of
F
. Each fibre of this map is a translate of the set

(\varpi)\times(\varpi)\subset\mathcal{O}_{F}^{2}
, thus each fibre has volume
q^{-2}
with respect to the measure that we have denoted by
|dx\wedge dy|
. Therefore we just need to compute the number of these fibres to complete the calculation. There are two ways to do it: one is to proceed by hand, which in this case is easy enough.
Another is to appeal to a generalization of Hensel’s Lemma: the affine
\mathcal{O}_{F}
-scheme defined by
x^{2}-\epsilon y^{2}\neq 0
is smooth; this implies that
the reduction map from the set of its
\mathcal{O}_{F}
-points is surjective onto the set of
k_{F}
-points of its special fibre (see, e.g.,
[
Ser81
, §3]
and

[
Bou85
, III.4.5, Corollaire 3, p.271]
). The set
C
can be written as:

C=\{(x,y)\in\mathcal{O}_{F}^{2}:\overline{(x^{2}-{\epsilon}y^{2})}\neq 0\}.

Since the reduction map is surjective in this case, we just need to find the number of points

(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in k_{F}^{2}
such that
\bar{x}^{2}-\bar{\epsilon}\bar{y}^{2}\neq 0
. The set of points satisfying this condition is in bijection with
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{\times}
, where
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
is the quadratic extension of our residue field
k=\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and we get the same result as above for the volume of
C
.

If the extension is ramified, the calculation changes.
In this case
\operatorname{ord}(\epsilon)=1
, hence for
x^{2}-\epsilon y^{2}
to be a unit,
x
has to be a unit and there is no condition on
y
other than it has to be an integer. Again consider the reduction modulo the uniformizer map. Its image in this case is
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\times\mathbb{F}_{q}
(again, this can be checked by hand in this specific case), and thus the volume of the set
C
is

\frac{(q-1)q}{q^{2}}
.

We summarize (cf.
[
Lan13
]
):

(5)

\operatorname{vol}_{|\omega_{T}|}(T^{c})=\begin{cases}&(\frac{q-1}{q})^{2}\quad{\mathbf{T}}\text{ split }\\
&\frac{q-1}{q}\frac{q+1}{q}\quad{\mathbf{T}}\text{ non-split unramifed }\\
&\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\frac{q-1}{q}\quad{\mathbf{T}}\text{ non-split ramified. }\\
\end{cases}

Note the factor
|2|
from (
4
) disappears (i.e.,
|2|_{v}=1
) since we are assuming that
p\neq 2
. The factor

\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}
in the ramified case
comes from the factor
|\sqrt{\epsilon}|
in (
4
) (in this case,
\epsilon=\varpi
up to a unit since
p\neq 2
).

For
p=2
, everything in the calculation is slightly different (and a lot longer) but the answers are similar.
Not to interrupt the flow of the exposition, we postpone the discussion of
p=2
till §
5.2
below.

2.2.1.
Norm-1 torus of a quadratic extension

Finally, to illustrate general difficulties of this volume computation, we consider the example of the norm-1 torus of a quadratic extension.

There is an exact sequence of algebraic
F
-tori:

(6)

1\to\operatorname{Res}_{E/F}^{(1)}\mathbb{G}_{m}\to\operatorname{Res}_{E/F}\mathbb{G}_{m}\to\mathbb{G}_{m}\to 1,

where the last map is the norm map; its kernel is an algebraic torus
\mathbf{T}_{1}:=\operatorname{Res}_{E/F}^{(1)}\mathbb{G}_{m}
over
F
, called the
norm-1
torus.

It is tempting to try to use this exact sequence to compute the volume of
T_{1}^{c}
with respect to
\omega_{T_{1}}
, but that is not the right way to proceed. The standard way to do this calculation is to consider an isogeny between
\mathbf{T}_{1}\times\mathbb{G}_{m}
and
\mathbf{T}
and use the results of Ono on the behaviour of various invariants attached to tori under isogenies; see also
[
Shy77
]
. However, this would take us too far afield; instead we proceed with an elementary calculation.
Before we do this calculation for a
p
-adic field, consider for a moment the situation when
F=\mathbb{R}
and
E=\mathbb{C}
, in order to get some geometric intuition.

Example 2.4
.

Let
\mathbf{T}:=\operatorname{Res}_{\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{G}_{m}(\mathbb{R})=\mathbb{C}^{\times}
; then the norm-1 torus is the unit circle
S^{1}
.

The same calculation as in Example
2.3
shows that the volume form
\omega_{T}
gives the measure

|\omega_{T}|=|\frac{2idx\wedge dy}{x^{2}+y^{2}}|=\frac{2}{x^{2}+y^{2}}|dx\wedge dy|
on
\mathbb{C}^{\times}
.

Similarly, if we go by the definition of the volume form
\omega_{S^{1}}
on
S^{1}
, we obtain the following.
The generator of the character group
X^{\ast}(S^{1})
(over
\mathbb{C}
) is simply the identity character
z\mapsto z=x+iy
.
We get that
\omega_{S^{1}}=\frac{dz}{z}
by definition, but intuitively it is not yet clear what is the measure defined by this form.
We write
dz=dx+idy
, and note that
\frac{1}{z}=\bar{z}=x-iy
when
z\in S^{1}
.
We obtain:

(7)

\omega_{S^{1}}=(x-iy)(dx+idy)=(x-iy)dx+(y+ix)dy.

It is still not obvious what measure this form gives; it would be convenient to rewrite it
using the local coordinate of some chart on the circle.
Here we can use the fact that we are working over
\mathbb{R}
and take
\theta
to be the arc length; then
x=\cos\theta
,
y=\sin\theta
,

0\leq\theta<2\pi

is the familiar (transcendental) parametrization.
We get:

(8)

\displaystyle\omega_{S^{1}}=(x-iy)dx+(y+ix)dy

\displaystyle=(\cos\theta-i\sin\theta)d(\cos\theta)+(\sin\theta+i\cos\theta)d(\sin\theta)

\displaystyle=-\cos\theta\sin\theta d\theta+\sin\theta\cos\theta d\theta+i(\cos^{2}\theta d\theta+\sin^{2}\theta d\theta)=id\theta.

Since
|i|=1
, we see that the measure
|\omega_{S^{1}}|
coincides with the arc length.

The exact sequence (
6
) gives a relation between this measure and the measures on
S^{1}
and
\mathbb{G}_{m}
, which is the same as rewriting the measure
dx\wedge dy
in polar coordinates.
Indeed, we have (from calculus)
dx\wedge dy=rdr\wedge d\theta
, so

\frac{dx\wedge dy}{x^{2}+y^{2}}=\frac{dr}{r}\wedge d\theta
.
We obtain the relation between
\omega_{S^{1}}
and the form
\omega_{T}
on
\mathbf{T}=\operatorname{Res}_{\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{G}_{m}
:

(9)

\omega_{T}=2\omega_{S^{1}}\wedge\omega_{\mathbb{G}_{m}}.

The appearance of the factor
2
in this relation, combined with the fact the norm map to
\mathbb{G}_{m}
is not surjective on
\mathbb{R}
-points
and is
2:1
illustrates that the relation between the measures on
\mathbf{T}
and
\mathbf{T}_{1}
is not straightforward (if one cares for a power of
2
). Armed with this caution, we move on to the
p
-adic fields.

Example 2.5
.

Let
\mathbf{T}_{1}:=\operatorname{Res}_{E/F}^{(1)}\mathbb{G}_{m}
be the norm-1 torus of a quadratic extension as above, but with
E=F(\sqrt{\epsilon})
an extension of non-Archimedean local fields as in Example
2.3
. As before, we assume
p\neq 2
(the case
p=2
is treated below in §
5.2
).

As above, we would like to understand the form
\omega_{\mathbf{T}_{1}}
. We observe that the relation (
7
) can be easily adapted to this case (essentially, replacing
i
with
\sqrt{\epsilon}
). What we need is an algebraic parametrization of the conic
x^{2}-\epsilon y^{2}=1
.
Such a parametrization is given in projective coordinates
(x:y:z)
by:

(10)

x=\epsilon t^{2}+1,\quad y=2t,\quad z=1-\epsilon t^{2},\quad t\in F.

Then a calculation similar to (
8
) shows that in the affine chart
z\neq 0
,

\omega_{\mathbf{T}_{1}}=-\frac{2\sqrt{\epsilon}}{1-{\epsilon}t^{2}}dt.

We note here that we could have used the same rational parametrization for the unit circle in the example above; then at this point we would have obtained the same answer: if we plug in
\epsilon=-1
, we
get that
the “volume” of the circle with respect to
|\omega_{S^{1}}|
is

2\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{t^{2}+1}\,dt=2\pi
, as expected.

Continuing with the
p
-adic calculation, we can discard
|2|
since
p\neq 2
.
Next, we observe that
T_{1}^{c}=\mathbf{T}_{1}(F)
(our torus is
anisotropic
; it has no non-trivial
F
-characters, and hence the condition defining
T_{1}^{c}
is vacuous).
Therefore, the volume of
T_{1}^{c}
with respect to
\omega_{\mathbf{T}_{1}}
is

(11)

\operatorname{vol}_{|\omega_{\mathbf{T}_{1}}|}(T_{1}^{c})=\int_{F}\left|\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon}}{{1-\epsilon}t^{2}}\right|\,|dt|.

Now we need to consider two cases.

Case 1.
The extension is unramified, i.e.,
\epsilon
is a non-square unit.
Then
(
11
) becomes (using the fact that
the volume of the
p
-adic annulus
\{t:\operatorname{ord}(t)=n\}
with respect to the measure
|dt|
equals
q^{-n}-q^{-(n+1)}
) :

(12)

\displaystyle\operatorname{vol}_{|\omega_{\mathbf{T}_{1}}|}(T_{1}^{c})=

\displaystyle\int_{F}\frac{1}{|1-\epsilon t^{2}|}\,|dt|=\int_{\mathcal{O}_{F}}|dt|+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{q^{2n}}(q^{n}-q^{(n-1)})

\displaystyle=1+\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{q^{n}}=1+\frac{1}{q}.

Case 2.
The extension is ramified, i.e.,
\operatorname{ord}(\epsilon)=1
.
Then
|1-\epsilon t^{2}|=1
if
t\in\mathcal{O}_{F}
and
|1-\epsilon t^{2}|=q^{2n-1}
if
\operatorname{ord}(t)=n<0
.
Thus, the integral computing the volume of
T_{1}^{c}
again breaks down as a sum:

(13)

\displaystyle\operatorname{vol}_{|\omega_{\mathbf{T}_{1}}|}(T_{1}^{c})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\int_{F}\frac{1}{|1-\epsilon t^{2}|}\,|dt|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\left(\int_{\mathcal{O}_{F}}|dt|+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{q^{2n-1}}(q^{n}-q^{(n-1)})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\left(1+q\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{q^{n}}\right)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{q}}.

Let us compare the results of this calculation with the approach to volumes via point-counting.
If we take the equation
x^{2}-\epsilon y^{2}=1
and reduce it modulo the uniformizer, we get an equation of a conic over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
In the unramified case, this conic is in bijection with
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
via (
10
); thus we expect the volume to equal

\frac{q+1}{q}
, which agrees with (
12
).

In the ramified case, when
\epsilon
is not a unit, the reduction of the same equation modulo the uniformizer gives
a disjoint union of two lines over the finite field: it is the subvariety of the affine plane defined by
x^{2}=1
.
The point-count over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
gives us
2q
, thus the volume we obtain is

\frac{2q}{q}=2
, which agrees with (
13
) once we make the correction for the fact that our volume form had a factor of
\sqrt{\epsilon}
and thus was not defined over
F
(this again illustrates why in
[
Wei82
]
the disciminant factor appears in the definition of the volume form).
We note that when
p\neq 2
, the affine scheme
\operatorname{Spec}F[x,y]/(x^{2}-\epsilon y^{2}-1)
is smooth over
\mathcal{O}_{F}
(in both the ramified and unramified cases, which can be checked by the Jacobi criterion,
[
BLR80
, §2.2]
), and this justifies the fact that the point-count on the reduction
\mod\varpi
does give us the correct answer.

2.2.2.
The Néron model

How do the above calculations generalize to an arbitrary algebraic torus?
The issue is that for a torus that is not
F
-split, it is not a priori obvious how to choose ‘coordinates’ defined over
\mathcal{O}_{F}
; more precisely, one first needs to define an
integral model
for
\mathbf{T}
, i.e., a scheme over
\mathcal{O}_{F}
such that its generic fibre is
\mathbf{T}
. In order to use the formula
(
1
), this model would also need to be a
smooth
scheme over
\mathcal{O}_{F}
.
There is a canonical way to define such a smooth integral model for
\mathbf{T}
, namely, the
weak Néron model
,
[
BLR80
, Chapter 4]
, which we shall denote by
\mathcal{T}
.
In general it is a scheme not of finite type (it can have infinitely many connected components).
The
\mathcal{O}_{F}
-points of its identity component
T^{0}:={\mathcal{T}}^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{F})
provide another canonical compact subgroup of
\mathbf{T}(F)
. This subgroup is traditionally used in the literature to normalize the Haar measures on tori (and plays a role in normalization of measures on general reductive groups, as we shall see below), but it plays no explicit role in this note, hence we do not discuss any details of its definition.

Moreover, once we have the
\mathcal{O}_{F}
-model for
\mathbf{T}
, we can use the local coordinates associated with this model to define a volume form on
\mathbf{T}(F)
. Unlike the volume form defined above by using the characters, this form actually has coefficients in
F
; it is called the
canonical
volume form in the literature, following the article
[
Gro97
]
; we call it
\omega^{{\operatorname{can}}}
, but we shall not use any explicit information about it in this note.

In general,
T^{0}
is a subgroup of finite index in
T^{c}
(this index is an interesting arithmetic invariant, see
[
Bit11
]
for a detailed study),
and the relationship between the form
\omega_{T}
defined above and

\omega^{{\operatorname{can}}}
is discussed in
[
GG99
]
.
The subgroup
T^{c}
is the set of
\mathcal{O}_{F}
-points of the so-called
standard
integral model of
\mathbf{T}
, which is not smooth in general; roughly speaking, the coordinates on the standard model come from the characters
\chi_{i}
as above in the examples (see
[
Bit11
, §1.1]
).

If
\mathbf{T}
splits over an unramified extension of
F
, the situation is simple (see
[
Bit11
]
and references therein for details):

Theorem 2.6
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{T}
splits over an unramified extension of
F
. Then

(1)

T^{0}=T^{c}
, and the special fibre
{\mathcal{T}}^{0}_{\kappa}
of
{\mathcal{T}}^{0}
is an algebraic torus over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

(2)

\operatorname{vol}_{|\omega_{T}|}(T^{c})=\operatorname{vol}_{|\omega^{{\operatorname{can}}}|}(T^{c})=\frac{\#{\mathcal{T}}^{0}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{F}_{q})}{q^{\dim(\mathbf{T})}}
.

We give one illustrative example without any details, and summarize the known general results below in §
2.2.3
.

Example 2.7
.

Consider again Example
2.5
, where
\mathbf{T}
is the norm-1 torus of a quadratic
extension. It is anisotropic over
F
, and consequently its Néron model is a scheme of finite type over
F
.
If
E/F
is unramified, the ‘standard model’ (see
[
Bit11
]
) coincides with the Néron model, and is simply defined by the equation

x^{2}-\epsilon y^{2}=1
over
\mathcal{O}_{F}
.
It is connected and its special fibre is an irreducible conic over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, which has
q+1

rational points over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, as discussed above.

If
E/F
is ramified, e.g.
E=F[\sqrt{\varpi}]
, then
|T^{c}/{T}^{0}|=2
(the special fibre of
\mathcal{T}
has two connected components, each isomorphic to an affine line as we saw above – note that it is not an algebraic torus!)
And in this case we have

\operatorname{vol}_{|\omega^{{\operatorname{can}}}|}({T}^{0})=\frac{\#{\mathcal{T}}^{0}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{F}_{q})}{q^{\dim(\mathbf{T})}}=\frac{q}{q}=1,

and also
\omega_{T}=|\sqrt{\varpi}|_{E}\omega^{{\operatorname{can}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\omega^{{\operatorname{can}}}
.

Example 2.8
.

For an arbitrary finite extension
E/F
and

\mathbf{T}=\operatorname{Res}_{E/F}\mathbb{G}_{m}
, we have
\mathbf{T}(F)=E^{\times}
, and
T^{c}=\mathcal{O}_{E}^{\times}
.
If
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r}
are elements of
\mathcal{O}_{E}
that form a basis for
\mathcal{O}_{E}
over
\mathcal{O}_{F}
,
then
\chi_{i}(x):=\operatorname{Tr}_{E/F}(\alpha_{i}x)
form a basis of
X^{\ast}(\mathbf{T})
over
\mathbb{Z}
.
Then by definition of the discriminant (as the norm of the different
{\mathfrak{d}}_{E/F}
),
the measure
|\omega_{T}|=|\wedge d\chi_{i}|
equals

|\det(\operatorname{Tr}_{E/F}(\alpha_{i}))|_{F}\omega^{{\operatorname{can}}}
, i.e. the conversion factor is
the square root of the
F
-absolute value of the discriminant of
E
:

\omega_{T}=\sqrt{|\Delta_{E/F}|}\omega^{\operatorname{can}}.

This calculation is generalized to an arbitrary reductive group (not just an arbitrary torus) in
[
GG99
]
.

2.2.4.
The local
L
-functions

There is yet another way to express the number of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-points of

{\mathcal{T}}^{0}_{\kappa}
, and hence the volume of
T^{0}
, entirely in terms of the representation of the Galois group on the character lattice, using Artin L-factor.

Indeed, an algebraic torus
\mathbf{T}
over
F
is uniquely determined by the action of the Galois group of its splitting field
E
on
X^{\ast}(\mathbf{T})
. Let
\Gamma=\operatorname{Gal}(E/F)
and let
I
be the inertia subgroup and let
\operatorname{Fr}
be the Frobenius automorphism of
k_{E}
over
k_{F}
. Recall that we have the exact sequence of groups

1\to I\to\Gamma\to\langle\operatorname{Fr}\rangle\to 1,

and the cyclic group
\langle\operatorname{Fr}\rangle
is isomorphic to the Galois group of the residue field
\operatorname{Gal}(k_{E}/k_{F})
.
Thus we get a natural action of
\operatorname{Gal}(k_{E}/k_{F})
on the set of inertia invariants
X^{\ast}(\mathbf{T})^{I}
.
Let us denote this integral representation by

\sigma_{T}:\operatorname{Gal}(k_{E}/k_{F})\to{\operatorname{Aut}}_{\mathbb{Z}}(X^{\ast}(\mathbf{T})^{I})\simeq\operatorname{GL}_{d_{I}}(\mathbb{Z}),

where
d_{I}=\operatorname{rank}(X^{\ast}(\mathbf{T})^{I})
.
The Artin
L
-factor associated with this representation is, by definition,

(15)

L(s,\sigma_{T})=\det\left(I_{d_{I}}-\frac{\sigma_{T}(\operatorname{Fr})}{q^{s}}\right)^{-1}\quad\text{ for }s\in\mathbb{C},

where
I_{d_{I}}
is the identity matrix of size
d_{I}
.
Then the following relation holds (we are quoting it from
[
Bit11
, Propostion 2.14]
):

Theorem 2.9
.

\omega^{{\operatorname{can}}}(T^{0})=\frac{\#{\mathcal{T}}^{0}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{F}_{q})}{q^{\dim(\mathbf{T})}}=L(1,\sigma_{T})^{-1}
.

Note that if
E/F
is unramified, the inertia is trivial, so
d_{I}=\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{T})
.

Thus, to summarize, we have defined a natural invariant form
\omega_{T}
on
\mathbf{T}
and described the maximal compact subgroup
T^{c}
of
\mathbf{T}(F)
in terms of the characters of
\mathbf{T}
.
If
\mathbf{T}
splits over an unramified extension, the volume of
T^{c}
with respect to this differential form equals

(16)

\operatorname{vol}_{|\omega_{T}|}(T^{c})=\frac{\#{\mathcal{T}}^{0}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{F}_{q})}{q^{\dim(\mathbf{T})}}=L(1,\sigma_{T})^{-1},

where the second equality holds for any
\mathbf{T}
, not necessarily unramified.
In general, the volume of
T^{c}
with respect to this differential form contains two more factors – the index of

T^{0}
in
T^{c}
and the ratio between the differential forms
\omega_{T}
and
\omega^{{\operatorname{can}}}
.

2.3.
Reductive groups

Similarly to the case of tori discussed above, for a general reductive group
\mathbf{G}
over a local field
F
,
the choice of a normalization of Haar measure is linked with a choice of a ‘canonical’ differential form or a ‘canonical’ compact subgroup of
\mathbf{G}(F)
(unlike an algebraic torus, the set of
F
-points of a general reductive group can have more than one conjugacy class of maximal compact subgroups, and this choice matters for the normalization of measure).
Luckily, for the questions studied in
[
FLN10
]
the choice of the normalization of measure on
\mathbf{G}(F)
does not matter – it only contributes some global constant.

However, for completeness, we record that a ‘canonical’ choice of a compact subgroup
G^{0}
and an associated volume form
\omega_{G}
is
described by B. Gross in
[
Gro97
]
, using Bruhat-Tits theory.
The group
G^{0}
is the set of
\mathcal{O}_{F}
-points of a smooth scheme
\underline{\mathbf{G}}
over
\mathcal{O}_{F}
whose generic fibre is
\mathbf{G}
.
Hence, by Weil’s general argument (since
\underline{\mathbf{G}}
is smooth over
\mathcal{O}_{F}
), the volume of
G^{0}
is obtained by counting points on the special fibre of
\underline{\mathbf{G}}
(see (
[
Gro97
, Proposition 4.7]
):

(17)

\operatorname{vol}_{|\omega_{G}|}(G^{0})=\frac{\#\underline{\mathbf{G}}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{F}_{q})}{q^{\dim\mathbf{G}}}.

Moreover, Gross defines an Artin-Tate motive
M
associated with
\mathbf{G}
such that the volume of the canonical compact subroup with respect to the canonical form is given by the value of the Artin
L
-function associated with this motive at
1
. (If
\mathbf{G}
is an algebraic torus, the associated motive is precisely the representation of the Galois group on its character lattice as above, and Gross’ result amounts precisely to the statement of Theorem
2.9
above).

3.
Orbital integrals: the geometric measure

3.1.
The two normalizations

Let
\mathbf{G}
be a connected reductive algebraic group over
F
.
We denote the sets of regular semisimple elements in
G:=\mathbf{G}(F)
by
G^{\operatorname{rss}}

(respectively,
\mathfrak{g}^{\operatorname{rss}}
for the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
of
\mathbf{G}
).
2
2
footnotetext:
The simplest way to characterize the set of regular semisimple elements is to use any faithful representation of

G
to think of its elements as matrices;
then an element

\gamma\in\mathbf{G}(F)
(respectively,
X\in\mathfrak{g}
) is regular and semisimple if and only if its eigenvalues (in an algebraic closure of
F
) are distinct; we will also give a precise definition below in §
3.3.1
.

Let
\gamma\in G:=\mathbf{G}(F)^{\operatorname{rss}}

(in this note we are only interested in this setting).
The adjoint orbit (or simply, ‘orbit’, or sometimes, ‘rational orbit’ when we want to emphasize that it is the group of
F
-points of

\mathbf{G}
that is acting on it) of
\gamma
is the set

\mathcal{O}(\gamma):=\{g\gamma g^{-1}\mid g\in\mathbf{G}(F)\}.

The centralizer of
\gamma
is by definition the group
C_{G}(\gamma)=\{g\in\mathbf{G}(F)\mid g\gamma g^{-1}=\gamma\}
.
We will also briefly refer to the notion of a
stable
orbit of
\gamma
. It is a finite union of rational orbits;
as a first approximation, it can be thought of as the set

\mathcal{O}(\gamma)^{\mathrm{stable}}:=\{g\gamma g^{-1}\mid g\in\mathbf{G}(F^{\operatorname{sep}})\}\cap\mathbf{G}(F).

However, this is not the correct definition in general; see
[
Kot82
]
.
We will not need a precise definition in this note.
If
\mathbf{G}=\operatorname{GL}_{n}
, then for
\gamma\in G^{\operatorname{rss}}
, the stable orbit and rational orbit are the same.

When
\gamma\in G^{\operatorname{rss}}
, the identity component of the centralizer of
\gamma
is a maximal torus
T\subset G
; it can be thought of as a set of
F
-points
T=\mathbf{T}(F)
of an algebraic torus
\mathbf{T}
defined over
F
.
This leads to
two natural approaches to normalizing the measure on the orbit of
\gamma
:

(1)

Normalize the measures on
G
and
T
according to one of the methods discussed above and consider the quotient measure.

(2)

Describe the space of all (stable) orbits, and derive a measure on each orbit as a quotient measure with respect to the measure on the space of orbits.
3
3

3

In fact, there is a third natural approach if we are working with the orbital integrals on the Lie algebra rather than the group: namely, to identify
\mathfrak{g}
with
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
and consider the differential form on the orbit itself which comes from Kirillov’s symplectic form on co-adjoint orbits, see
[
Kot05
]
. We will not discuss this approach here as it is not related to the main subject of the note. However, the example of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
where one can clearly see the relation of this measure to what one would expect from calculus is provided in the Appendix by Matthew Koster.

Since the
G
-invariant measure on each orbit is unique up to a constant multiple, the two orbital integrals defined with respect to these measures will of course differ by a constant; however, this constant can, and does, depend on the orbit.
The goal of this section is to give a detailed explanation for the formula that relates the two orbital integrals; this is equation (3.31) in
[
FLN10
]
.
More specifically, we start with a review of the construction of Steinberg map

\mathfrak{c}:\mathbf{G}\to\mathbb{A}_{G}
, in §
3.2
below.
The set
\mathbb{A}_{G}(F)
has an open dense subset whose points parametrize the stable orbits of regular semisimple elements in
G
– each fibre of the map
\mathfrak{c}
over a point of this subset is such a stable orbit.
The relation (3.31) in
[
FLN10
]
we aim to explain is:

(18)

\int_{\mathfrak{c}^{-1}(a)}f(g)d|\omega_{a}|=|\Delta(t)|L(1,\sigma_{T\backslash G})O^{\mathrm{stable}}(t,f),

where
a=\mathfrak{c}(t)
.
We start by defining all the ingredients of this formula (as we shall see, this formula is not really about orbital integrals; it is simply a statement about the relationship between two invariant measures on an orbit).
We also simultaneously treat the orbital integrals on Lie algebras.

3.2.
The space
\mathbb{A}_{G}
; Chevalley and Steinberg maps

We start with the Lie algebra, where the situation is simpler.
We recall that
\mathbf{G}
acts on
\mathfrak{g}
via adjoint action, denoted by
\operatorname{Ad}:\mathbf{G}\to\operatorname{GL}(\mathfrak{g})
(for the classical groups and their Lie algebras,

\operatorname{Ad}(g)
is simply matrix conjugation by an element
g\in\mathbf{G}(F)
).
When we talk about orbits in
\mathfrak{g}
, it is the orbits under the adjoint action.
For
X\in\mathfrak{g}
, its
centralizer

C_{G}(X)
is, by definition, its stabilizer (in
\mathbf{G}(F)
) under the adjoint action.
If
X\in\mathfrak{g}^{\operatorname{rss}}
, then
C_{G}(X)
is a maximal torus in
\mathbf{G}(F)
.

3.2.1.
Reductive Lie algebra; algebraically closed field

4
4

4

This section is entirely based on

[
Kot05
, §14]
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be the Lie algebra of
\mathbf{G}
. For the moment let us work over an algebraically closed field
k
of characteristic
0
(in fact, assuming sufficiently large characteristic is sufficient here but we will not pursue this direction).
Let
\mathfrak{t}=\mathrm{Lie}(\mathbf{T})
be a maximal Cartan subalgebra.
Then the ring of polynomial functions on
\mathfrak{t}
is the symmetric algebra
S=S(\mathfrak{t}^{\ast})
. The Weyl group acts on
S
, and the ring of invariants
S^{W}
is the ring of regular functions on the quotient
\mathfrak{t}/W
.
In other words,
\mathfrak{t}/W
is a variety over
k
, isomorphic to
\operatorname{Spec}S^{W}
.
Furthermore, in fact
S^{W}
is itself a polynomial ring, and so
\mathfrak{t}/W
is isomorphic to the affine space
\mathbb{A}^{r}
, where
r=\operatorname{Rank}(\mathbf{T})
.

Example 3.1
.

Let
\mathbf{G}=\operatorname{GL}_{n}
, and let
\mathfrak{t}\subset\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
be the Cartan subalgebra consisting of diagonal matrices. Then
W=S_{n}
,
S=k[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
, and
S^{W}
is the algebra of symmetric polynomials.
As we know, it is generated by the elementary symmetric polynomials. Thus, the map
\mathfrak{t}\to\mathbb{A}^{r}
is given by:

t=\operatorname{diag}(t_{1},\dots,t_{r})\mapsto(\sigma_{1}(\bar{t}),\dots,\sigma_{r}(\bar{t}))
, where
\bar{t}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{r})
and

\sigma_{k}(\bar{t})=\sum_{\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}\subset\{1,\dots,r\}}t_{i_{1}}\dots t_{i_{k}}
is the
k
-th elementary symmetric polynomial.
Note that in particular, for
n=2
, we get the map
t\mapsto(\operatorname{Tr}(t),\det(t))
.

Let
k[\mathfrak{g}]
be the
F
-algebra of polynomial functions on
\mathfrak{g}
, and let
k[\mathfrak{g}]^{G}
be the subalgebra of
the polynomials invariant under the adjoint action of
G
.
We quote from
[
Kot05
, §14.2]
: Chevalley’s restriction theorem can be stated as:

k[\mathfrak{g}]^{G}\cong S^{W},

where the isomorphism is given by restricting the polynomial functions from
\mathfrak{g}
to
\mathfrak{t}
.
Dually to the inclusion
k[\mathfrak{g}]^{G}\hookrightarrow k[\mathfrak{g}]
, we get the surjection (which we will refer to as
Chevalley map
)

\mathfrak{c}_{\mathfrak{g}}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathbb{A}_{G}=\mathfrak{t}/W,

which maps
X\in\mathfrak{g}
to the unique
W
-orbit in
\mathfrak{t}
consisting of elements conjugate to the semisimple part of
X
.
An important observation (which is not used in these notes but is very relevant for the subject) is that the nilpotent cone in
\mathfrak{g}
is
\mathfrak{c}_{\mathfrak{g}}^{-1}(0)
.

In general, the role of ‘elementary symmetric polynomials’ is played by the traces of irreducible representations of
\mathfrak{g}
determined by the
fundamental weights
. Namely, let
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
be the fundamental weights determined by a choice of simple roots for
\mathfrak{g}

(i.e., the weights of
\mathfrak{g}
defined by
\langle\mu_{i},\alpha_{j}\rangle=\delta_{ij}
, where
\Delta=\{\alpha_{j}\}_{j=1}^{r}
is a base of the root system of
\mathfrak{g}
).
Let
\rho_{i}
be the representation of
\mathfrak{g}
of highest weight
\mu_{i}
.
Then
S^{W}
, as an algebra, is generated by
\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{i})
(see, e.g.,
[
Hum72
, 23.1]
).

For the type
A_{n}
, one recovers the elementary symmetric polynomials from this construction.
Namely, for
\mathfrak{sl}_{n}
, it happens that the exterior powers of the standard representation are irreducible, and they give all the fundamental representations:

\rho_{i}=\wedge^{i}\rho_{1}
, for
i=1,\dots,{n-1}
, where
\rho_{1}
is the standard representation, which has highest weight
\mu_{1}
.
Consequently, since the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial of a matrix are (up to sign) the traces of its exterior powers, we obtain:

Example 3.2
.

For
\mathfrak{sl}_{n}
,
Chevalley map can be realized explicitly as

X\mapsto(a_{i})
, where
a_{i}
are the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial of
X
.

3.2.2.
Reductive Lie algebra, non-algebraically closed field

When the field
F
is not algebraically closed, the space
\mathbb{A}_{G}
can be defined as
\operatorname{Spec}F[\mathfrak{g}]^{G}
, avoiding the need to choose a maximal torus; it turns out that the morphism
\mathfrak{c}
is still defined over
F
(see
[
Kot05
, §14.3]
).
However, in this note we are only considering the case of
\mathbf{G}
split over

Namely, if
\mathbf{G}
is split over
F
, we can choose the split maximal torus

in

3.3.
Weyl discriminant

We recall the definition and the basic properties of the Weyl discriminant (for the Lie algebra, the main source is
[
Kot05
, §§7, 14]
).

3.3.1.
Weyl disriminant on the Lie algebra

Definition 3.5
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a reductive Lie algebra, let
X\in\mathfrak{g}
be a regular semisimple element, and let
T=C_{G}(X)
be its centralizer with the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{t}=\operatorname{Lie}(T)
. Then

D(X)=\det(\operatorname{ad}(X)|_{\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{t}})

is called the Weyl discriminant of
X
.

The discriminant is, in fact, a polynomial function on
\mathfrak{g}
(and thus extends to all of
\mathfrak{g}
from the dense subset of regular semisimple elements):
D(X)
is the lowest non-vanishing coefficient of the characteristic polynomial of
\operatorname{ad}(X)
(see
[
Kot05
, 7.5]
).
This interpretation allows us to give an intrinsic characterization of the set of regular semisimple elements: in fact,

X\in\mathfrak{g}
is regular semisimple if and only if
D(X)\neq 0
; thus it can be taken as a definition of
regular semisimple
.

We also recall the expression for
D(X)
in terms of roots:

(20)

D(X)=\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi}\alpha(X)=(-1)^{\frac{\dim\mathfrak{g}-\operatorname{rank}\mathfrak{g}}{2}}\left(\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}\alpha(X)\right)^{2},

where
\Phi
is the set of all roots and
\Phi^{+}
is any set of positive roots.

Example 3.6
.

We compute the explicit expressions for the Weyl discriminant in terms of the eigenvalues of
X
, in the cases

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sl}_{n}
and
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sp}_{2n}
, for use in future examples.

Ler
X\in\mathfrak{g}
have eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}\in\bar{F}
.

For
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sl}_{n}
, the roots are
\alpha_{ij}(X)=\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}
,
1\leq i,j\leq n\text{ and }i\neq j
.
Then the Weyl discriminant of
X
coincides with the polynomial discriminant of the characteristic polynomial of
X
:

D(X)=\prod_{{1\leq i,j\leq n}\atop{i\neq j}}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}).

(We observe that the eigenvalues satisfy the relation
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}=\operatorname{Tr}(X)=0
).

For
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sp}_{n}
, the explicit expression for the roots depends on the choice of the coordinates for the standard representation (though of course the answer does not).
We define
\operatorname{Sp}_{2n}
and
\mathfrak{sp}_{2n}
explicitly as:

\operatorname{Sp}_{2n}(F)=\{g\in\operatorname{GL}_{2n}(F):g^{t}Jg=J\},\quad\mathfrak{sp}_{2n}(F)=\{X\in\mathfrak{gl}_{2n}(F):X^{t}J+JX=0\},

where
J=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&I_{n}\\
-I_{n}&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]
and
I_{n}
stands for the
n\times n
-identity matrix.
Then the eigenvalues of any element
X\in\mathfrak{sp}_{2n}
satisfy

\lambda_{n+i}=-\lambda_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq n
, and
the set of values of the roots at
X
is (cf.
[
Hum72
, §12.1]
):

\{\pm(\lambda_{i}\pm\lambda_{j}),1\leq i,j\leq n,i\neq j\}\cup\{\pm 2\lambda_{i},1\leq i\leq n\}
.
Then we get:

D(X)=(-1)^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}2^{n}\prod_{{1\leq i,j\leq n}\atop{i\neq j}}(\lambda_{i}^{2}-\lambda_{j}^{2})\prod_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}.

We also observe that in a reductive Lie algebra, the Weyl discriminant of any element is computed entirely via the derived subalgebra
\mathfrak{g}^{\operatorname{der}}
, by definition (since
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{t}=\mathfrak{g}^{\operatorname{der}}/\mathfrak{t}^{\operatorname{der}}
).

3.3.2.
Weyl discriminant on the group

On the group, the definition is obtained essentially by reducing to the Lie algebra:

Definition 3.7
.

Let
\gamma\in\mathbf{G}(F)
be a regular semisimple element, and let
T=C_{G}(X)
be its
centralizer with the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{t}=\operatorname{Lie}(T)
. Then the Weyl discriminant of
\gamma
is

D(\gamma)=\det(1-\operatorname{Ad}(\gamma)|_{\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{t}}).

Similarly to the Lie algebra case, the Weyl discriminant has an expression in terms of the (multiplicative) roots:

(21)

D(\gamma)=\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi}(1-\alpha(\gamma))=(-1)^{\frac{\dim\mathfrak{g}-\operatorname{rank}\mathfrak{g}}{2}}\rho^{2}(\gamma)\left(\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}(1-\alpha(\gamma))\right)^{2},

where
\rho
is half the sum of positive roots, so
2\rho
is the sum of positive roots (in the above formula,

\rho^{2}(\gamma)
is the value of the character
2\rho
at
\gamma
).
Note that the second part of the formula expressing the Weyl discriminant as a product over
positive
roots now has an extra factor that did not arise in the Lie algebra case (the examples below illustrate this).

We again show the calculation for the general linear and symplectic groups.
Note that the final expressions are a lot simpler when restricted to
\mathbf{G}^{\operatorname{der}}
.

Example 3.8

\displaystyle=\left(1-\lambda_{i}^{2}\right)\left(1-1/\lambda_{i}^{2}\right).

(4)

If the element

3.4.
Orbital integrals: the Lie algebra case

We start with a prototype case of a Lie algebra.

3.4.1.
Definitions: Lie algebra

Let
\mathbf{G}
be a connected reductive group defined over a local field
F
, as above. The orbital integrals (for regular semisimple elements) on the Lie algebra
are distributions on the space
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathfrak{g})
of the locally constant compactly supported functions on
\mathfrak{g}
, defined as follows.

Let
X\in\mathfrak{g}
be a regular semisimple element, and let
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathfrak{g})
.
Since
X
is regular semisimple, its centralizer is a torus
T=C_{G}(X)=\mathbf{T}(F)
, as discussed above, and thus the adjoint orbit of
X
can be identified
with the quotient
\mathbf{T}(F)\backslash\mathbf{G}(F)
.
Both
\mathbf{T}(F)
and
\mathbf{G}(F)
can be endowed with any of the natural measures discussed above in
§
3.1
. Once the measures on
G
and
T
are fixed, there is a unique quotient measure on
T\backslash G
, which
we will denote by
\mu_{T\backslash G}
(see e.g.,
[
Kot05
, §2.4]
for the definition of the quotient measure in this context).
The orbital integral with respect to this measure is

(22)

O_{X}(f):=\int_{T\backslash G}f(\operatorname{Ad}(g^{-1})X)d\mu_{T\backslash G}.

We observe that there are finitely many
F
-conjugacy classes of tori in
G
; thus there are finitely many choices of measures that we need to make on the representatives of these conjugacy classes, and these choices endow the orbit
of every regular semisimple element with a measure. If the canonical measures (in the sense of
[
Gro97
]
, discussed above in
2.2.2
) are chosen on the tori, the resulting orbital integrals are called
canonical
.
This approach to the normalization of measures on the orbits is the one typically used in the literature.

On the other hand, one can use Chevalley map defined above to normalize the measures on orbits.
For a general reductive group
\mathbf{G}
and
X\in\mathfrak{g}
regular semisimple, the fibre
\mathfrak{c}^{-1}(\mathfrak{c}(X))

over the point
a:=\mathfrak{c}(X)\in\mathbb{A}_{G}(F)

is the stable orbit of
X

F
-rational orbits. Thus, every
F
-rational orbit is an open subset of
\mathfrak{c}^{-1}(a)
for some

, and if we define a measure on this fibre, we get a measure on every
F
-rational orbit contained in it by restriction.

In the Introduction, we have fixed measures on affine spaces
with a choice of a basis
.
The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is an affine space; it does not come with a canonical choice of a basis, and this choice would not matter much in the discussion below; we can choose an arbitrary
F

\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
of
\mathfrak{g}
for our purposes.
This basis then gives rise to a differential form
\omega_{\mathfrak{g}}=\wedge_{i=1}^{n}dx_{i}
on
\mathfrak{g}

|\omega_{\mathfrak{g}}|
as in the Introduction.
The space
\mathbb{A}_{G}
is also an affine space under our assumptions (since at the moment we are working with the Lie algebra); and in our construction it comes with a choice of basis
\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
as in §

. We let
\omega_{\mathbb{A}_{G}}
be the differential form associated with this basis.

Thus we get the quotient measure on each fibre
\mathfrak{c}^{-1}(\mathfrak{c}(X))
: it is the measure associated with the differential form
\omega_{\mathfrak{c}(X)}^{\operatorname{geom}}
such that

(23)

\omega_{\mathfrak{g}}=\omega^{{\operatorname{geom}}}_{\mathfrak{c}(X)}\wedge\omega_{\mathbb{A}_{G}}.

That is, by definition of
\omega_{\mathfrak{c}(X)}^{{\operatorname{geom}}}
, for any

,

(24)

\int_{\mathfrak{g}}f(X)\,d|\omega_{\mathfrak{g}}|=\int_{\mathbb{A}_{G}}\int_{\mathfrak{c}^{-1}(\mathfrak{c}(X))}f(X)\,d|\omega^{{\operatorname{geom}}}_{\mathfrak{c}(X)}|\,d|\omega_{\mathbb{A}_{G}}|.

Our immediate goal is to
derive the relationship between these two measures on the orbit:
\mu_{T\backslash G}
and
|d\omega_{a}^{{\operatorname{geom}}}|
, where
a=\mathfrak{c}(X)
.
First we observe that since both measures are quotient measures of a chosen Haar measure on
G
, their ratio does not depend on the choice of the measure on
G
, as long as it is compatible with the choice of the measure on the Lie algebra; thus at this point, the choice of the measure on
G
is determined by our choice of the form

\omega_{\mathfrak{g}}
. (Conversely, one often chooses a measure on
G
first, and this determines
\omega_{\mathfrak{g}}

At the same time, the choice of the measures on the representatives of conjugacy classes of tori affects the measure

\mu_{T\backslash G}
but not the measure
|\omega_{a}^{{\operatorname{geom}}}|
.
Here we address two natural choices of such measures:

(i)

Let
\omega_{G}
be a volume form on
G
, and on each algebraic torus
T
, define the form
\omega_{T}
using the characters of the torus as in (
2
).
Then we get the quotient measure
\omega_{T\backslash G}
on each orbit.
This is the measure discussed in
[
FLN10
]
.
We discuss this measure in this section.

(ii)

Use the measure denoted above by
|\omega^{\operatorname{can}}|
, associated with the Néron model, on each torus. This measure on the orbits is discussed in the next section.

Thus our first goal is to determine the ratio of
\omega_{T\backslash G}
to
\omega_{a}^{\operatorname{geom}}
, for each

(which determines
T
and

). It turns out that the conversion between these two measures is based on exactly the same calculation as the Weyl integration formula, which we now review.

3.4.2.
Weyl integration formula, revisited

We follow
[
Kot05
, §7, §14.1.1]

For a torus

[

Weyl integration formula (which we quote in this form from

for an arbitrary Schwartz-Bruhat function

(25)

\int_{\mathfrak{g}}f(Y)d|\omega_{\mathfrak{g}}(Y)|=\sum_{T}\frac{1}{|W_{T}|}\int_{\mathfrak{t}}|D(X)|\int_{T\backslash G}f(\operatorname{Ad}(g^{-1})X)d|\omega_{T\backslash G}|\,d|\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}(X)|,

where the sum on the right-hand side is over the representatives of the conjugacy classes of tori in

The proof of this formula relies on a computation of the Jacobian of the map

(26)

\displaystyle(T\backslash G)\times\mathfrak{g}

\displaystyle\to\mathfrak{g}

\displaystyle(g,X)

\displaystyle\mapsto\operatorname{Ad}(g^{-1})X.

This map is
|W_{T}|:1

|D(X)|
(see
[

The simplest group case

•

Remark 3.12

3.5.1.
Sketch of the proof.

As the measures are defined by differential forms, the calculation is carried out in the exterior power of the cotangent space, and hence it is essentially the same calculation as for the Lie algebra above. The only ingredients that needs to be treated slightly differently are the discriminant and the Jacobian of the map from
T
to

T/W
.
Indeed, for
\gamma\in G^{\operatorname{rss}}(F^{\operatorname{sep}})
, we still have the exact sequence of tangent spaces (see
[
FLN10
, Lemma 26]
)

0\to Tan_{\gamma}(\mathfrak{c}^{-1}(a))\to Tan_{\gamma}\mathbf{G}\to Tan_{a}(\mathbb{A}_{G})\to 0,

and by definition,

\omega_{\mathfrak{c}(\gamma)}^{\operatorname{geom}}\wedge\omega_{\mathbb{A}_{G}}=\omega_{G}
;
\omega_{G}=\omega_{T}\wedge\omega_{T\backslash G}
.
The proof proceeds exactly as for Lie algebras, except the map (
26
) needs to be replaced with the map

(31)

\displaystyle(T\backslash G)\times G

\displaystyle\to G

\displaystyle(g,\gamma)

\displaystyle\mapsto g^{-1}\gamma g,

and the map
\mathfrak{t}\to\mathbb{A}_{G}=T/W
is replaced with the map
T\to T/W
.
The Jacobian of the first map is the group version of the Weyl discriminant (and fits into the group version of Weyl integration formula in the exact same way as it did for the Lie algebra):

|W_{T}|^{-1}|D(\gamma)|\omega_{T\backslash G}\wedge\omega_{T}=\omega_{G}.

Next, we need to relate
\omega_{T}
with
\omega_{\mathbb{A}_{G}}
.

Lemma 3.14
.

(
[
FLN10
, Proposition 3.29]
.) Let
\mathbf{G}
be a split, semi-simple, simply connected group, and let

T\subset G
be a maximal torus.
Let

\omega_{T}
be defined by (
2
).
Then

|W_{T}|^{-1}\omega_{T}(\gamma)=|\Delta(\gamma)|\omega_{\mathbb{A}_{G}}(\mathfrak{c}(\gamma)).

Proof.

For
T=T^{\operatorname{spl}}
, this is proved in
[
FLN10
]
, as well as in
[
Bou02
]
(where the field is assumed algebraically closed, but the proof works verbatim for the split torus).
Now it remains to consider the restriction of
\mathfrak{c}
to an arbitrary (not necessarily split) maximal torus.
The map
\mathfrak{c}
on
T
can be defined as a composition

\mathbf{T}\to\mathbf{T}^{\operatorname{spl}}\to\mathbb{A}_{G}=\mathbf{T}^{\operatorname{spl}}/W,

where the first map is an isomorphism over the algebraic closure of
F
.
The pullback of the form
\omega_{T^{\operatorname{spl}}}
on
T^{\operatorname{spl}}
is precisely the form
\omega_{T}
on
T
, and thus the equality remains true.
∎

The theorem follows, precisely as in the Lie algebra case.
To conclude this section, we compute some examples illustrating the above Lemma (which show that it is substantially non-trivial even for the split torus).

3.5.2.
Examples of Jacobians and discriminants on the group

Example 3.15

We again start with

Example 3.16

\displaystyle dt_{1}\wedge dt_{2}=\pm\prod_{\alpha<0}(1-\alpha(\gamma_{t_{1},t_{2}}))^{-1}t_{1}^{-1}da\wedge db

4.2.
The next step

In
[

Roughly speaking, Poisson summation formula is a relation between the sum of the values of a smooth function over a lattice in a vector space, and the sum of the values of its Fourier transform over a dual lattice.
Here the space is the set of adèlic points of the Steinberg-Hitchin base for

The lattice in it is the image of the diagonal embedding of the base field (we can take

The geometric side of the Trace Formula contains a sum over the conjugacy classes of elliptic elements

However, for that the function needs to satisfy some smoothness assumption.
Now we can at least make some preliminary remarks about how far our function is from being smooth, at least at every finite place.

If we take

5.1.1.
Tamagawa measure

We briefly recall the definition of the Tamagawa measure, just for the special case of our torus

11

We note that superficially, it differs from the definition that A. Weil uses in

1.
Let

We have the exact sequence

1\to(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}}^{\times})^{1}\to\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}}^{\times}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{\times}\to 1,

where the first map is the inclusion and the second map is the product of absolute values over all places,

Moreover, the exact sequence splits and we have a canonical decomposition

(in the sense of the metric topology) of the group

We note that the image of the diagonal embedding of

into

, and

2.
To define the Tamagawa measure on

Fortunately, in our special case, the differential form

2.3

12

12

GG99

[

Recall the local Artin

Let

We make a note of some subtle features of this definition:

(1)

The definition does not depend on the choice of a volume form (as long as

is defined over
\mathbb{Q}
), since any two choices differ by a constant in
\mathbb{Q}
, which does not matter globally thanks to the product formula.

(2)

Without the

does not define a measure on

, since (as one can easily see in our example) the maximal compact subgroup

There is some choice involved in the definition of the convergence factors (for example, in Weil’s definition in
[

As Ono explains in §3.5 of
[

.

For future use, we define by

the measure

on
\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
.

3.
The Tamagawa number of
\mathbf{T}
is, by definition, the volume (with respect to the Tamagawa measure on the quotient, discussed below),
of
\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{Q})\backslash\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})^{1}
, where

\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})^{1}=\{(x_{v})\in\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}}):\prod_{v}|\chi(x_{v})|_{v}=1\text{ for all }\chi\in X^{\ast}(\mathbf{T})\text{ that are defined over }\mathbb{Q}\}.

The group of
\mathbb{Q}
-characters of
\mathbf{T}
has rank 1, and is generated by the norm map.
Thus,

\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})^{1}=\{(x_{v})\in\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}}):\prod_{v}|N_{K_{v}/\mathbb{Q}_{v}}(x_{v})|_{v}=1\},

where the product is over the places of
\mathbb{Q}
.
We note that
\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})=\mathbb{A}_{K}^{\times}
, and we have the exact sequence

(49)

1\to\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})^{1}\to\mathbb{A}_{K}^{\times}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{\times}\to 1,

where the map to
\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{\times}
is the product of the local norm maps.

Let

(using the notation and terminology of
[
Shy77
]
) be the measure on

\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})^{1}
that ‘matches topologically’ in this exact sequence with the measure

.
That is,

is the measure on
\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})^{1}
such that

Since

is a discrete subgroup of
\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})^{1}

with respect to this measure is, by definition, the Tamagawa number

.

We note that the exact sequence (

5.1.2.
The proof of the analytic class number formula

Now we are ready to go from (

Step1. The finite places.

Rewriting the relation (
5

We will see below in §

Thus,

, and we see explicitly in this example, that our measure

Step 2. The infinite places and putting it together.

We also have the exact sequence (where

where the first map is the inclusion into

that maps

to the adèle

Since the image of the diagonal embedding of

intersects the image of

(52)

1\to S^{1}\to K^{\times}\backslash\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})^{1}\to K^{\times}\backslash\mathbb{A}_{K}^{\operatorname{fin}}\to 1.

Now we need to carefully find the component at infinity

(which is a measure on
S^{1}
) of the measure

defined via the exact sequence (
49
). First, we choose a convenient basis for the character lattice of
\mathbf{T}
, in view of this exact sequence: we use the characters

\chi_{1}(z,\bar{z})=z
and

. These two characters, which can be thought of as the vectors
(1,0)
and

in Example

, still form a
\mathbb{Z}

, and hence we can write

\mathbf{T}
for the rest of this calculation.
We write every element of
\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{A})
as

, where
a_{f}
has the infinity component

and
a_{\infty}=(1,(z,\eta))

has all the components at the finite places equal to
1
.
In this notation,
\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{A})^{1}
is defined by the condition

.
We write

\mu^{Tama}=\mu_{{\operatorname{fin}}}^{Tama}\mu_{\infty}^{Tama},

where
\mu_{{\operatorname{fin}}}^{Tama}=\rho_{T}^{-1}\prod_{p}\mu_{p}
, and
\mu_{\infty}^{Tama}=|\omega|
.
We recall that by definition,
\omega=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\Delta_{K}|}}\omega_{T}
. Then by the definition of

We have computed above in (

Thus, the volume of

with respect to

is

Finally, we get from (

(53)

\displaystyle 1=\operatorname{vol}^{Tama}(\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{Q})\backslash\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})^{1})=\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{|\Delta_{K}|}}\operatorname{vol}^{Tama}(\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{Q})\backslash\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}}^{\operatorname{fin}}))

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{|\Delta_{K}|}}\rho_{T}^{-1}\operatorname{vol}_{\nu_{T}}(K^{\times}\big\backslash\left(\mathbb{A}_{K}^{\operatorname{fin}})^{\times}\right)=\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{|\Delta_{K}|}}L(1,\chi_{K})^{-1}\frac{h_{K}}{w_{K}},

K
.
We note that the product
L(1,\chi_{K})
converges only conditionally.

5.3.
Global orbital integrals in
\operatorname{GL}_{2}

Let us put together the information we have so far about the orbital integrals with respect to the canonical vs. geometric measures, and the information about the volume of
K^{\times}\big\backslash\left(\mathbb{A}_{K}^{\operatorname{fin}}\right)^{\times}
that we just obtained.

Let
\gamma\in\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Q})
be a regular semisimple element, such that its centralizer
T
is non-split over
\mathbb{R}
.
Let
K
be the quadratic extension of
\mathbb{Q}
generated by the eigenvalues of
\gamma
. Then

T=\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{Q})
with

\mathbf{T}=\operatorname{Res}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{G}_{m}
, as in §

.
Let
f^{\operatorname{fin}}=\otimes_{p}f_{p}
, with

equal to
{\bf 1}_{\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{p})}
,
the characteristic function of
\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{p})

be a test function on
G(\mathbb{A}^{\operatorname{fin}})

Taking the product of the relations (
36

, and applying the product formula to
the absolute values
\prod_{p}|D(\gamma)|_{p}

O_{\gamma}^{{\operatorname{geom}}}(f^{\operatorname{fin}})=|D(\gamma)|^{-1/2}|\Delta_{K}|^{1/2}L(1,\sigma_{G/T})O_{\gamma}^{{\operatorname{can}}}(f^{\operatorname{fin}}).

We observe that the canonical measure on the centralizer of
\gamma
(or any measure coinciding with it at almost all places) is convenient for defining a global orbital integral because with such a measure, all but finitely many factors
O_{\gamma_{p}}(f_{p})
are equal to

and the primes where
f_{p}\neq{\bf 1}_{G(\mathbb{Z}_{p})}

In the Trace Formula, the orbital integrals are weighted by volumes; and the
volume has to be taken with respect to the same measure on the centralizer that was used to define the orbital integral.
Comparing (
59
) with (
41
), we see explicitly that for
\operatorname{GL}_{2}
, the volume term contains some of the factors that also appear when we pass from the canonical measure to the geometric measure on the orbit.
Now we are ready to explicate an observation (that is implicit in the work of Langlands) that switching to the geometric measure makes the volume term disappear in the case

\mathbf{G}=\operatorname{GL}_{2}
, in addition to making the orbital integrals at finite places better-behaved. This comes at the cost of now having the orbital integral expressed as only a
conditionally convergent
infinite product.
This theorem can be thought of as the main point of this note.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\gamma
be a regular elliptic element of
\operatorname{GL}_{2}
as above, that splits over a quadratic extension

.
Let

\operatorname{vol}^{\operatorname{can}}(\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{Q})\backslash\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{A}^{\operatorname{fin}}))O_{\gamma}^{\operatorname{can}}(f^{\operatorname{fin}})=\frac{|D(\gamma)|^{1/2}}{2\pi}O_{\gamma}^{\operatorname{geom}}(f^{\operatorname{fin}}).

Proof.

Combining the relation (
59
) with the calculation in (
53
), we see that the factors

and

cancel out, and
we obtain:

\displaystyle\operatorname{vol}^{\operatorname{can}}(\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{Q})\backslash\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{A}^{\operatorname{fin}}))O_{\gamma}^{\operatorname{can}}(f^{\operatorname{fin}})=\frac{\sqrt{|\Delta_{K}|}}{2\pi}L(1,\chi_{T})\operatorname{vol}^{Tama}(\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{Q})\backslash\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{A})^{1})O_{\gamma}^{\operatorname{can}}(f^{\operatorname{fin}})

\displaystyle=\frac{|D(\gamma)|^{1/2}}{2\pi}\tau(\mathbf{T})O_{\gamma}^{\operatorname{geom}}(f^{\operatorname{fin}}).

Since
\mathbf{T}

5.4.
Eichler-Selberg Trace Formula for
\operatorname{GL}_{2}

The Eichler-Selberg Trace Formula expresses the trace of a Hecke operator
T_{n}
on the space
S_{k}(N)
of cusp forms of weight
k
and level
N
.
For simplicity of exposition, we set
N=1
in this note; in this case the central character is also trivial.
In this setting, the Eichler-Selberg Trace Formula states:

(60)

\displaystyle n^{1-k/2}\operatorname{Tr}T_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{k-1}{12}\begin{cases}&1,n\text{ is a perfect square}\\
&0\text{ otherwise }\end{cases}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t^{2}<4n}\frac{\rho^{k-1}-\bar{\rho}^{k-1}}{\rho-\bar{\rho}}\sum_{m}h_{w}\left(\frac{t^{2}-4n}{m^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{d\mid n}\min(d,\frac{n}{d})^{k-1},

where in the middle line
\rho
and
\bar{\rho}
are roots of the polynomial
X^{2}-tX+n
, and

h_{w}\left(\frac{t^{2}-4n}{m^{2}}\right)
is the weighted class number of the order in
\mathbb{Q}[\rho]
that has discriminant
\frac{t^{2}-4n}{m^{2}}
.
The sum over
m
runs over the integers
m\geq 1
such that
m^{2}
divides
t^{2}-4n
, and
\frac{t^{2}-4n}{m^{2}}
is
0
or
1

\mod 4
.

The goal of this section is to sketch, without any detail, a connection between this formula and the (geometric side of) Arthur-Selberg Trace formula.

5.4.1.
The test function

We start with a very brief recall of the connection between modular forms and automorphic forms on
\operatorname{GL}_{2}
. We refer to e.g., Knightly and Li
[
KL06
]
for all the details.

Let
\mathbf{G}=\operatorname{GL}_{2}
for the rest of this section.
A cusp form of weight
k
generates (as a representation of
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})
under the action by right translations) a closed subspace
(\pi,V)
of
L^{2}_{0}(\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Q})\backslash\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{A}))
; for a level
1
cusp form (which is our assumption here, so in particular we should assume
k>2
), the central character of
\pi
is trivial.
By Flath’s theorem, the representation
\pi
factors as a restricted tensor product

\pi=\pi_{\operatorname{fin}}\otimes\pi_{\infty}=\otimes^{\prime}_{p}\pi_{p}\otimes\pi_{\infty}.

Since
(\pi,V)
came from a cusp form of weight
k
, we have
\pi_{\infty}=\pi_{k}
– the discrete series representation of highest weight
k
.

Every function
f\in C_{c}(\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{A}))
gives rise to a linear operator
R(f)
on
L^{2}(\mathbf{Z}(\mathbb{A})\backslash\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{A}))
defined by

(R(f)\phi)(g):=\int_{(\mathbf{Z}\backslash\mathbf{G})(\mathbb{A})}f(x)\phi(gx)dx.

In this language, the Hecke operator
T_{n}
on
S_{k}
is precisely
n^{k/2-1}R(f_{n,k})
, where
f_{n,k}
is a specific test function in the space
L^{2}(\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Q})\backslash\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{A})^{1})
. We quote the definition of this test function from
[
KL06
]
.

•

Let
f_{\infty}
be a matrix coefficient of the representation
\pi_{k}
.
By orthogonality of matrix coefficients, this ensures that the image of
R(f)
is contained in
(\pi,V)
.
(Since
(\pi,V)
is irreducible, this means
R(f)
projects onto
V
).

•

For
p\nmid n
, let
f_{p}
be the characteristic function of
\mathbf{Z}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Z}_{p})
,

•

For
p\mid n
, let
f_{p}
be the characteristic function of
\mathbf{Z}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})M_{n,p}
, where
M_{n,p}
is the set of matrices of determinant
n
in
M_{2}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
(we prefer to think of it as the characteristic function of the union of the double cosets of the Cartan decomposition for
\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
of determinant
n
).

5.4.2.
The transition to Arthur Trace Formula

We plug
f:=f_{n,k}
into Arthur’s Trace Formula for
\operatorname{GL}_{2}
, and examine the geometric side.
Since for our test function
f
, the continuous and residual parts of the spectral side vanish, the geometric side in fact equals

\operatorname{Tr}R(f)
(see
[
KL06
, §22]
).
Finally, Knightly and Li show that:

•

The first line of (
60
) matches
the contribution of the trivial conjugacy class;

•

The last line matches the contribution of the unipotent and hyperbolic conjugacy classes.

•

The middle line matches the contribution of the elliptic conjugacy classes.

We discuss why the last claim is plausible.
By definition of the test function
f
, its orbital integrals vanish on all elements
\gamma
such that
\det(\gamma)\neq n
; hence, in the geometric side of Arthur’s Trace Formula, we are left with the sum over
\gamma\in\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Q})
satisfying
\det(\gamma)=n
.
The conjugacy classes in
\operatorname{GL}_{2}
are parametrized by characteristic polynomials, and the elliptic ones correspond to the polynomials with negative discriminants, so at least superficially, we recognize the sum over the integers

such that

as a sum over the rational elliptic conjugacy classes.

Next, note that the expression
\frac{\rho^{k-1}-\bar{\rho}^{k-1}}{\rho-\bar{\rho}}
is the value of the character of
\pi_{k}
on the corresponding conjugacy class; thus, we recognize it as the orbital integral of
f_{\infty}
(see e.g.
[
Kot05
, §1.11]
for the discussion of characters as orbital integrals of matrix coefficients; see also
[
GGPS16
, Ch.I, §5.2]
).

Knightly and Li show (in our notation):

(61)

O_{\gamma}^{\operatorname{can}}(f^{\operatorname{fin}})=\sum_{m}h_{w}\left(\frac{t^{2}-4n}{m^{2}}\right),

where the sum over
m
is as in (
60
).
While the appearance of class numbers in our earlier calculations is suggestive, and proves this relation in the trivial case when
t^{2}-4n
is square-free, it appears that our arguments are insufficient for getting a simpler proof of this claim in general (other than by essentially direct computation of the both sides, or matching the computation of the right-hand side in
[
KL06
]
with the calculation on the building in
[
Kot05
]
, the results of which we already quoted above).
A similar statement for
\operatorname{GL}_{n}
, relating orbital integrals to sums of class numbers of orders,
is proved by Zhiwei Yun,
[

]
.

In this appendix we illustrate Kirillov’s construction of a volume form on co-adjoint orbits in a Lie algebra.
Here we work over

in order to be able to use the intuition from calculus. In these examples, we also relate this form to the geometric measure discussed in the article.

6.1.
The coadjoint orbits

A large part of this section is quoted from
[
Kot05
, §17.3]
for the reader’s convenience and to set up notation.
Let
G
denote a semisimple Lie group,
\mathfrak{g}
its Lie algebra, and
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
the linear dual space of
\mathfrak{g}
.
We denote elements of
\mathfrak{g}
by capital letters, e.g.
X
, and use starts to denote elements of
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
;
unless explicitly stated there is no a priori relationship between
X
and
X^{*}
.

G
acts on
\mathfrak{g}
by Ad and acts on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
by Ad
∗
, where

\langle\operatorname{Ad}^{*}(g)(X^{*}),X\rangle=\langle X^{*},\operatorname{Ad}(g^{-1})(X)\rangle.

Let
\mathcal{O}(X^{*})\subset\mathfrak{g}^{*}
denote the orbit of
X^{*}
under this action (called a co-adjoint orbit).

We recall that the differential of the adjoint action
\operatorname{Ad}
of
G
is the action of
\mathfrak{g}

on itself by
\operatorname{ad}
where
\operatorname{ad}_{X}(Z)=[X,Z]
.
The co-adjoint action of
\mathfrak{g}

on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
is
the differential of
\operatorname{Ad}^{*}
;
we denote it
by
\operatorname{ad}^{*}
; explicitly, this action is defined by

\langle\operatorname{ad}_{X}^{*}(Y^{\ast}),Z\rangle=\langle Y^{\ast},[Z,X]\rangle
.

A choice of an element
X^{\ast}\in\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
defines a map

\varphi_{X^{\ast}}:G\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}

by
\varphi_{X^{\ast}}(g)=\operatorname{Ad}^{*}_{g}(X^{*})
.
The differential of this map at the identity
e\in G
gives an identification of
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{c}(X^{*})
with the tangent space
T_{X^{*}}\mathcal{O}(X^{*})
at
X^{\ast}
,
defined by
X\mapsto\operatorname{ad}^{*}_{X}(X^{*})
. Here

\mathfrak{c}(X^{\ast})
is the stabilizer of
X^{\ast}
under
\operatorname{ad}^{\ast}
, and we are viewing
T_{X^{*}}\mathcal{O}(X^{*})
as a subspace of
T_{X^{*}}\mathfrak{g}^{*}\cong\mathfrak{g}^{*}
. We denote this identification by
\Phi_{X^{\ast}}:\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{c}(X^{\ast})\to T_{X^{\ast}}\mathcal{O}(X^{\ast})\hookrightarrow\mathfrak{g}^{*}
.
The element
X^{\ast}
gives an alternating form
\omega_{X^{\ast}}
on
\mathfrak{g}
, defined by

(62)

\omega^{\prime}_{X^{\ast}}(X,Y):=\langle X^{\ast},[X,Y]\rangle=-\langle\operatorname{ad}^{\ast}(X)X^{\ast},Y\rangle.

This form clearly vanishes on
\mathfrak{c}(X^{\ast})
, and gives a non-degenerate bilinear form on
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{c}(X^{\ast})
, which we have just identified with
T_{X^{\ast}}\mathcal{O}(X^{\ast})
.
Thus, given a co-adjoint orbit
\mathcal{O}
, we get a symplectic
2
-form
\omega^{\prime}
on it by letting the value of
\omega^{\prime}
at
X^{\ast}\in\mathcal{O}
equal
\omega^{\prime}_{X^{\ast}}
. In particular, as a manifold,
\mathcal{O}
has to have even dimension; if its dimension is
2k
, then
the
k
-fold wedge product of the form
\omega^{\prime}
gives a volume form on
\mathcal{O}
.

Over a field of characteristic zero, we can identify a semisimple Lie algebra with its dual; we will use the Killing form for this.
Then the adjoint orbits in
\mathfrak{g}

get identified with the co-adjoint orbits in
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
, and thus we get a very natural algebraic volume form on each adjoint orbit in
\mathfrak{g}
.
Here our goal is to compute this form explicitly in two examples: the regular nilpotent orbit in
\mathfrak{sl_{2}}(\mathbb{R})
and a semisimple

\mathrm{SO}_{3}(\mathbb{R})
-orbit in
\mathfrak{so}_{3}(\mathbb{R})
(we do not use the accidental isomorphism in this calculation). In both cases the orbit will be two-dimensional, so we are just computing the form denoted by
\omega^{\prime}
above.

6.2.
Rewriting the form as a form on an orbit in
\mathfrak{g}

Given
X_{0}^{\ast}\in\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
, we compute the form
\omega
on the orbit of an element
X_{0}\in\mathfrak{g}
that corresponds to

X_{0}^{\ast}\in\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
under the isomorphism defined by Killing form, in three steps:

(1)

Compute the map
\Phi_{X_{0}^{\ast}}
.

(2)

For
\widetilde{v_{1}},\widetilde{v_{2}}\in T_{X_{0}^{*}}\mathcal{O}(X_{0}^{*})
find
v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathfrak{g}
with
\Phi_{X_{0}^{\ast}}(v_{i})=\widetilde{v_{i}}

for
i=1,2
, and then evaluate
\omega_{X_{0}^{*}}(\widetilde{v_{1}},\widetilde{v_{2}})=\langle X_{0}^{*},[v_{1},v_{2}]\rangle
.

(3)

Using Killing form, identify
\mathfrak{g}
with
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
, which identifies a co-adjoint orbit of
X_{0}^{\ast}
in
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
with an adjoint
orbit of an element
X_{0}\in\mathfrak{g}
.
Then use the adjoint action to explicitly define the volume form
\omega_{X}
on
T_{X}\mathcal{O}
at a point
X
in this orbit by pulling back the form
\omega_{X_{0}}
.

6.3.
Example I: a regular nilpotent orbit in
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})

Let
G=\operatorname{SL}(2;\mathbb{R})
,
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sl}(2;\mathbb{R})
, and consider the standard basis

\{\mathbf{e},\mathbf{f},\mathbf{h}\}

for
\mathfrak{g}
given by:

\displaystyle{\bf e}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{bmatrix},\ \ \ \ {\bf f}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{bmatrix},\ \ \ \ {\bf h}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{bmatrix}.

Let
\{\mathbf{e}^{*},\mathbf{f}^{*},\mathbf{h}^{*}\}
be the basis for
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
dual to
\{\mathbf{e},\mathbf{f},\mathbf{h}\}
under the Killing form. Explicitly this means that
\mathbf{e}^{*}(\mathbf{f})=4
,
\mathbf{f}^{*}(\mathbf{e})=4
, and
\mathbf{h}^{*}(\mathbf{h})=8
.
We will compute Kirillov form on the co-adjoint orbit
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{f}^{\ast}}

of
\mathbf{f}^{*}
, which we identify with the adjoint orbit of
\mathbf{e}
in
\mathfrak{g}
.

When we refer to coordinates
x,y,z
on
\mathfrak{g}
, it is with respect to our chosen basis
\{\mathbf{e},\mathbf{f},\mathbf{h}\}
. Given this choice of coordinates, we
have the basis of the space of
1
-forms on
\mathfrak{g}
given by
dx
,
dy
,
dz
.

Under the isomorphism
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}\simeq\mathfrak{g}
defined by Killing form,
a point
(x,y,z)\in\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
is mapped to

\left[\begin{smallmatrix}z/2&x\\
y&-z/2\end{smallmatrix}\right]\in\mathfrak{g}
.
We can describe the orbit of
\mathbf{e}
very explicitly in these coordinates.

6.3.1.
The nilpotent cone

If we are working over
\mathbb{R}
, then the set of nilpotent elements in
\mathfrak{g}
forms a cone: indeed, for a nilpotent matrix we have

\det\left[\begin{smallmatrix}z/2&x\\
y&-z/2\end{smallmatrix}\right]=0
, i.e.,
z^{2}+4xy=0
.
(One can easily see that it is, indeed, a cone by the change of coordinates
u=x+y
,
v=x-y
: in these coordinates, the equation of the orbit becomes
z^{2}+u^{2}=v^{2}
). See e.g.
[
DeB05
, §2.3]
for more detail of this picture.

The nilpotent cone consists of 3 orbits of
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
:
\{0\}
, the half-cone with
v>0
(which is the orbit of
\mathbf{e}
), and the half-cone with
v<0
(the orbit of
\mathbf{f}
).
One can explicitly compute that given a matrix
X_{0}:=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}z_{0}/2&x_{0}\\
y_{0}&-z_{0}/2\end{smallmatrix}\right]\in\mathfrak{g}
satisfying

z_{0}^{2}=-4x_{0}y_{0}
(which forces
x_{0}y_{0}<0
if we are working over
\mathbb{R}
), the element
g_{0}
below provides the conjugation so that

X_{0}=Ad^{*}_{g_{0}}(\mathbf{e})
(it is convenient for us to write
g_{0}
as a product of a diagonal and a unipotent matrix with a view toward further calculations):

(63)

g_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{x_{0}}&0\\
\sqrt{-y_{0}}&\frac{1}{\sqrt{x_{0}}}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{x_{0}}&0\\
0&\frac{1}{\sqrt{x_{0}}}\\
\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
\sqrt{-{x_{0}}{y_{0}}}&1\\
\end{bmatrix}.

Note that since
v=x_{0}-y_{0}>0
, and
x_{0}y_{0}<0
, we have
x_{0}>0,y_{0}<0
, which explains our choice of signs inside the square roots.

6.3.2.
A measure from calculus

Given that our orbit is an open half-cone, we can write down a natural measure on it as a parametrized surface.
In fact, as we think of a parametrization for this cone, we can be guided by the fact that we are looking for an
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
-invariant measure. We recall Cartan decompositon:
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})=UK
, where
U
is the group of lower-triangular unipotent matrices and
K=\mathrm{SO}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\simeq S^{1}
.
The adjoint action of
\mathrm{SO}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
is given by a fairly complicated formula (see
[
DeB05
, §2.3]
), but at the same time one has the obvious action of
S^{1}
on the cone by rotations; thus it is reasonable to make a rotation-invariant measure on our cone.
Therefore, we use cylindrical coordinates to parametrize the cone
z^{2}+u^{2}=v^{2}
and arrive at
z=t\cos(\theta)
,
u=t\sin(\theta)
,
v=t
, which translates to the parametrization

\rho:(0,\infty)\times[0,2\pi)\to\mathfrak{g}
given by:

(64)

\rho(t,\theta)=(t(\cos\theta+1),t(\cos\theta-1),t\sin\theta).

The natural volume form on the cone is then
dt\wedge d\theta
; below we see how it compares to Kirillov’s volume form.
Note: now that we made this guess at a form, we could just express the actions of
U
and
K=\mathrm{SO}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
on the cone in the
(t,\theta)
coordinates, and check if this form is invariant. However, we prefer to compute the Kirillov’s form directly and derive the comparison this way.

6.3.3.
Computing Kirillov’s form

Step 1. The calculation at
\mathbf{f}^{\ast}
.

We compute the map
\Phi_{\mathbf{f}^{\ast}}
defined in §
6.1
.
It is a map from
\mathfrak{g}
to
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
, so given
(x,y,z)=x\mathbf{e}+y\mathbf{f}+z\mathbf{h}\in\mathfrak{g}
, its image under
\Phi_{\mathbf{f}^{\ast}}
is a linear functional on
\mathfrak{g}
. Thus it makes sense to write
\langle\Phi_{\mathbf{f}^{\ast}}(x,y,z),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\rangle
, where
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\in\mathfrak{g}
. We evaluate:

\displaystyle\langle\Phi_{\mathbf{f}^{\ast}}(x\mathbf{e}+y\mathbf{f}+z\mathbf{h}),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\mathbf{f}^{*},[x^{\prime}\mathbf{e}+y^{\prime}\mathbf{f}+z^{\prime}\mathbf{h},x\mathbf{e}+y\mathbf{f}+z\mathbf{h}]\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\mathbf{f}^{*},2(z^{\prime}x-x^{\prime}z)\mathbf{e}+2(y^{\prime}z-z^{\prime}y)\mathbf{f}+(x^{\prime}y-y^{\prime}x)\mathbf{h}\rangle

\displaystyle=8(z^{\prime}x-x^{\prime}z)

\displaystyle=\langle-(2z\mathbf{f}^{*}-x\mathbf{h}^{*}),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\rangle.

Thus

(65)

\Phi_{\mathbf{f}^{\ast}}(x\mathbf{e}+y\mathbf{f}+z\mathbf{h})=-(2z\mathbf{f}^{*}-x\mathbf{h}^{*}).

Step 2.

We need to find a preimage under
\Phi_{\mathbf{f}^{\ast}}
for a vector
\tilde{v}\in T_{\mathbf{f}^{\ast}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{f}^{\ast}}
.
We recall that this tangent space is identified with a subspace of
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
, which we later plan to identify with a subspace of
\mathfrak{g}
. Because of this latter anticipated identification, we write
\tilde{v}=x\mathbf{f}^{\ast}+y\mathbf{e}^{\ast}+z\mathbf{h}^{\ast}
. We see directly from (
65
)
that for
\tilde{v}
to be in the image of
\Phi_{\mathbf{f}^{\ast}}
it has to satisfy
y=0
, and then
v=z\mathbf{e}-\frac{x}{2}\mathbf{h}
satisfies

\Phi_{\mathbf{f}^{\ast}}(v)=\tilde{v}
.

We are now ready to compute the form
\omega_{\mathbf{f}^{\ast}}
. Let

\widetilde{v_{i}}=x_{i}\mathbf{f}^{*}+y_{i}\mathbf{e}^{*}+z_{i}\mathbf{h}^{*}
for
i=1,2
; then as discussed above, we can take
v_{i}=z_{i}\mathbf{e}-\frac{x_{i}}{2}\mathbf{h}
.
Then
[v_{1},v_{2}]=(z_{1}x_{2}-x_{1}z_{2})\mathbf{e}
, and
finally we have that:

(66)

\omega_{\mathbf{f}^{*}}(\tilde{v}_{1},\tilde{v}_{2})=\langle\mathbf{f}^{*},[v_{1},v_{2}]\rangle=4(z_{1}x_{2}-x_{1}z_{2}).

Under the identification of the differential
2
-forms on a vector space with alternating
2
-tensors, we recognize this form as

-4dx^{*}\wedge dz^{*}
, which we identify with the form
\omega_{\mathbf{e}}:=-4dx\wedge dz
on the adjoint orbit of
\mathbf{e}\in\mathfrak{g}
.

Step 3. Pullback of
\omega_{\mathbf{e}}
under the adjoint action.
We compute the operator
\operatorname{Ad}_{g_{0}}
for the element
g_{0}
from (
63
) in our coordinates, in order to use it to pull back the form
\omega_{\mathbf{e}}
.
By the right-hand side of (
63
), the matrix of
\operatorname{Ad}_{g_{0}}
in the basis
\{\mathbf{e},\mathbf{f},\mathbf{h}\}
is

(67)

\operatorname{Ad}_{g_{0}}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{0}&0&0\\
0&\frac{1}{x_{0}}&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
-{x_{0}}{y_{0}}&1&2\sqrt{-{x_{0}}{y_{0}}}\\
\sqrt{-{x_{0}}{y_{0}}}&0&1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{0}&0&0\\
-{y_{0}}&\frac{1}{x_{0}}&2\sqrt{-\frac{y_{0}}{x_{0}}}\\
\sqrt{-{x_{0}}{y_{0}}}&0&1\end{bmatrix}.

Thus,

(68)

\displaystyle(\operatorname{Ad}_{g_{0}})^{\ast}(dx\wedge dz)=\left|\begin{matrix}x_{0}&0\\
\sqrt{-x_{0}y_{0}}&0\end{matrix}\right|dx\wedge dy-\left|\begin{matrix}x_{0}&0\\
\sqrt{-x_{0}y_{0}}&1\end{matrix}\right|dx\wedge dz+\left|\begin{matrix}0&0\\
0&1\end{matrix}\right|dy\wedge dz

\displaystyle=x_{0}dx\wedge dz.

By definition, the volume form at
X_{0}\in\mathfrak{g}
is
(\operatorname{Ad}_{g_{0}^{-1}})^{\ast}(\omega_{\mathbf{e}})
, and thus we get

\omega_{X_{0}}=\frac{1}{x_{0}}dx\wedge dz.

Coverting to
(t,\theta)
-coordinates, we get:

\displaystyle\rho^{*}(dx\wedge dz)

\displaystyle=((\cos\theta+1)dt-t\sin\theta\ d\theta)\wedge(\sin\theta\ dt+t\cos\theta\ d\theta)

\displaystyle=t\cos\theta(\cos\theta+1)dt\wedge d\theta-t\sin^{2}\theta d\theta\wedge dt

\displaystyle=(t\cos^{2}\theta+t\cos\theta+t\sin^{2}\theta)dt\wedge d\theta

\displaystyle=t(1+\cos\theta)dt\wedge d\theta

and therefore

\displaystyle\rho^{*}\omega

\displaystyle=\rho^{*}\left(\frac{4dx\wedge dz}{x}\right)=\frac{4t(1+\cos\theta)}{t(1+\cos\theta)}dt\wedge d\theta

\displaystyle=4\ dt\wedge d\theta.

6.3.4.
Semisimple orbits in
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})

The orbits of split semisimple elements in
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
are hyperboloids of one sheet asymptotically approaching the nilpotent cone on the outside; the orbits of elliptic elements are the individual sheets of hyperboloids of two sheets that lie inside the same asymptotic cone (see e.g.,
[
DeB05
, §2.3.3]
for detail).
Measures on them can be computed in a similar way (we return to this calculation below).
For now we compute another example,
a semisimple orbit in
\mathfrak{so}_{3}(\mathbb{R})
.

6.4.
Another example: a semisimple (elliptic) element in
\mathfrak{so}_{3}(\mathbb{R})

Let
G=\mathrm{SO}(3)
,
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{so}(3)
, and let
\{X,H,Y\}
be the basis for
\mathfrak{g}
given by:

X=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&0&-1\\
0&1&0\end{pmatrix}\ \ H=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\
0&0&0\\
-1&0&0\end{pmatrix}\ \ Y=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}

Let
\{X^{\ast},H^{\ast},Y^{\ast}\}
be dual basis for
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
under the Killing form.
Explicitly this means
X^{*}(X)=-2
,
Y^{*}(Y)=-2
, and
H^{\ast}(H)=-2
. Denote by
\mathcal{O}_{H^{\ast}}
the co-adoint orbit of
H^{\ast}
. Then a brief calculation shows that
\mathcal{O}_{H^{\ast}}
is the unit sphere:

\mathcal{O}_{H^{\ast}}=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}\ |\ x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}=1\}

We compute Kirillov’s form on this sphere, using a slightly different method from the above (to illustrate various approaches to such computations). Namely, rather than computing the form at one fixed base point on the orbit and then using the group action to compute it at all points, we do the computation directly for each point
X_{0}
of our orbit
\mathcal{O}_{H^{\ast}}
.

As above, given

X_{0}\in\mathcal{O}_{H^{\ast}}
,
we have
\varphi_{X_{0}}:G\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}
given by
\varphi_{X_{0}}(g)=Ad^{*}_{g}(X_{0})
.
We write
X_{0}=(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
in
\{X^{\ast},H^{\ast},Y^{\ast}\}
-coordinates.
As above, the differential of
\varphi_{X_{0}}
at
e\in G
gives an identification
\Phi_{X_{0}}:\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{c}(X)\to T_{X_{0}}\mathcal{O}_{H^{\ast}}\hookrightarrow{}T_{X_{0}}\mathfrak{g}^{*}\cong\mathfrak{g}^{*}
. This can be computed either by recalling that
\langle\Phi_{X_{0}}(X),Y\rangle=\langle X_{0},[Y,X]\rangle
or via the exponential map:

\displaystyle\Phi_{X_{0}}(X)=\frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0}(\varphi_{X_{0}}\circ\exp)(tX).

The result of this computation is that with respect to
our coordinates, the matrix representation for
\Phi_{X_{0}}
is given by:

\begin{pmatrix}0&z_{0}&-y_{0}\\
-z_{0}&0&x_{0}\\
y_{0}&-x_{0}&0\end{pmatrix}.

Write
\omega=f_{1}dX^{*}\wedge dH^{*}+f_{2}dX^{*}\wedge dY^{*}+f_{3}dH^{*}\wedge dY^{*}
.
We have:

\displaystyle f_{1}(X_{0})=\omega_{X_{0}}(\frac{\partial}{\partial X^{*}},\frac{\partial}{\partial H^{*}})=\langle X_{0},[(0,z_{0},-y_{0}),(-z_{0},0,x_{0})]\rangle=-2(x_{0}^{2}z_{0}+y_{0}^{2}z_{0}+z_{0}^{3})=-2z_{0}

\displaystyle f_{2}(X_{0})=\omega_{X_{0}}(\frac{\partial}{\partial X^{*}},\frac{\partial}{\partial Y^{*}})=\langle X_{0},[(0,z_{0},-y_{0}),(y_{0},-x_{0},0)]\rangle=2(x_{0}^{2}y_{0}+y_{0}^{3}+y_{0}z_{0}^{2})=2y_{0}

\displaystyle f_{3}(X_{0})=\omega_{X_{0}}(\frac{\partial}{\partial H^{*}},\frac{\partial}{\partial Y^{*}})=\langle X_{0},[(-z_{0},0,x_{0}),(y_{0},-x_{0},0)]\rangle=-2(x_{0}^{3}+x_{0}y_{0}^{2}+x_{0}z_{0}^{2})=-2x_{0}.

Therefore,

(69)

\displaystyle\omega(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=-2x\ dH^{*}\wedge dY^{*}+2y\ dX^{*}\wedge dY^{*}-2z\ dX^{*}\wedge dH^{*}

\displaystyle=-2(x\ dH^{*}\wedge dY^{*}-y\ dX^{*}\wedge dY^{*}+z\ dX^{*}\wedge dH^{*}).

It is easy to check that if we parametrize the sphere using the spherical coordinates, this form is rewritten as twice the usual surface area element:

\omega(\varphi,\theta)=2\sin\varphi
. We leave this check as an exercise.

6.4.1.
General semi-simple orbits in
{\mathfrak{so}}_{3}(\mathbb{R})
and
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})

Since the group
\mathrm{SO}_{3}(\mathbb{R})
is compact, all its maximal tori are conjugate; consequently, every semisimple element in
{\mathfrak{so}}_{3}(\mathbb{R})
is conjugate to
rH
for some
r\in\mathbb{R}
(and all semisimple orbits are spheres). It is clear that if we replace
H^{\ast}
with
rH^{\ast}
, the form in (
69
) gets scaled by
r
:

\omega_{rH^{\ast}}=2rdx\wedge dz
, so it is again the natural area element on a sphere of radius
r
.

We also note that all our calculations for these algebraic volume forms are valid over any field of characteristic different from
2
(the only reason we were working over the reals is the nice geometric picture and the intuitive parametric equations for the orbits as surfaces; note that despite our use of these
transcendental parametrizations, in the end all the differential forms are algebraic).

Returning to semi-simple orbits in
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, we can use the accidental isomorphism: over
\mathbb{C}
,
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
and
\mathfrak{s0}_{3}
are isomorphic. Thus, the same calculation as above shows also that the value at the element
t\mathbf{h}
of the Kirillov form on the orbit
of
t{\mathbf{h}}\in\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
is
2tdx\wedge dy
(note that the
y
- and
z
-coordinates are swapped in §
6.3
and
§
6.4
); this uniquely determines the invariant form on the orbit.

How does this form relate to the volume form
\omega_{c}^{\operatorname{geom}}
defined by (
23
) in §
3.4
?
Using the coordinates of
6.3
, Chevalley map is given by

\left[\begin{smallmatrix}z/2&x\\
y&-z/2\end{smallmatrix}\right]\mapsto-\frac{z^{2}}{4}-xy.

The geometric measure is defined as a quotient:

dx\wedge dy\wedge{\frac{1}{2}dz}=\omega_{c}^{\operatorname{geom}}\wedge dc
, where
c=-\frac{z^{2}}{4}-xy
.
Evaluating all the forms at the point
t\mathbf{h}
(which corresponds to
x=y=0
,
z=2t
, and thus
c=-t^{2}
), we see that

\omega_{c}^{\operatorname{geom}}
must satisfy

(\omega_{c}^{\operatorname{geom}})_{t\mathbf{h}}\wedge(-2tdt)=dx\wedge dy\wedge dt,

and therefore,

(\omega_{c}^{\operatorname{geom}})_{t\mathbf{h}}=-\frac{1}{2t}dx\wedge dy
.
We obtain the conversion coefficient between Kirillov’s form and the geometric form: on the orbit of a split semi-simple element
t\mathbf{h}
it is
-\frac{1}{4t^{2}}=-D(t\mathbf{h})^{-1}
.
It would be interesting to find this coefficient for a general reductive Lie algebra.
